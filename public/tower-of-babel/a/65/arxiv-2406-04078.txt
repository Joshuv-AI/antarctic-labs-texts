How many sprays cover the space?
Alessandro Andretta, Ivan Izmestiev

Abstract
. For all d ≥ 3 d\geq 3 we show that the cardinality of ℝ \mathbb{R} is at most ℵ n \aleph_{n} if and only if ℝ d \mathbb{R}^{d} can be covered with ( n + 1 ) ​ ( d − 1 ) + 1 (n+1)(d-1)+1 sprays whose centers are in general position in a hyperplane. This extends previous results by Schmerl when d = 2 d=2 .



2020 Mathematics Subject Classification
03E50, 51N20

1.
Introduction

A spray in the plane is a subset of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
together with a distinguished point (called center) such that all circles centered in that point have finite intersection with the spray.
These objects were introduced in
[
Sch03
]
, where it is observed that the continuum hypothesis implies that the plane can be covered with three sprays.
(The continuum hypothesis,
{\mathsf{CH}}
from now on, asserts that every infinite subset of
\mathbb{R}
is either countable or else in bijection with
\mathbb{R}
; by work of Gödel and Cohen
{\mathsf{CH}}
can neither be disproved nor proved from
\mathsf{ZFC}
, the Zermelo-Frænkel set theory with the axiom of choice.)
In
[
Veg09
]
it is shown that
{\mathsf{CH}}
is equivalent to the plane being covered with three sprays with collinear centers—in fact the statement “the cardinality of
\mathbb{R}
is at most
\aleph_{n}
” (in symbols:
2^{\aleph_{0}}\leq\aleph_{n}
) is equivalent to
\mathbb{R}^{2}
being covered with
n+2
sprays with collinear centers
[
Sch10
]
.
Collinearity is essential, since
\mathsf{ZFC}
proves that the plane can be covered with three sprays centered in arbitrary non-collinear points
[
Veg09
,
Sch10
]
.

The notion of spray can be extended to
\mathbb{R}^{d}
for any
d\geq 3
, with
(d-1)
-dimensional spheres in place of
1
-dimensional spheres, i.e. circles, and the natural question is the relation (if any) between the size of the continuum and the number of sprays needed to cover
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
By
[
EJM94
, Theorem 2]
,
2^{\aleph_{0}}\leq\aleph_{n}
implies that
\mathbb{R}^{3}
can be covered with
2n+3
sprays such that their centers are coplanar, and no three of them are collinear.
In particular,
{\mathsf{CH}}
implies that given five coplanar points such that no three of them are collinear, then there are sprays centered around them that cover
\mathbb{R}^{3}
.
By
[
Sch12
]

\mathbb{R}^{3}
cannot be covered with three sprays, but the problem whether the space can be covered with four sprays remained open.

We prove that
2^{\aleph_{0}}\leq\aleph_{n}
is equivalent to
\mathbb{R}^{3}
being covered with
2n+3
sprays whose centers are coplanar, and no three of them are collinear.
Moreover
2n+3
is optimal—in particular
\mathbb{R}^{3}
cannot be covered with four sprays with coplanar centers.
In fact we prove a similar result for
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
d>3
, namely:
2^{\aleph_{0}}\leq\aleph_{n}
is equivalent to
\mathbb{R}^{d}
being covered with
(n+1)(d-1)+1
-many sprays whose centers lie on a hyperplane
H
, and the affine span of any
d
of them is
H
(Theorem
5.4
).
Again the number
(n+1)(d-1)+1
of sprays is optimal.
Finally we show that, irrespective of the size of the continuum,
\mathbb{R}^{d}
can be covered with countably many sprays whose centers lie on a hyperplane
H
, and the affine span of any
d
of them is
H
(Theorem
5.8
).

We do not know if
\mathbb{R}^{d}
can be covered with
d+1
-many sprays such that the affine span of their centers is
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, even in the case
d=3
.
In other words: can
\mathbb{R}^{3}
be covered with four sprays whose centers are not coplanar, i.e. form a tetrahedron?
We suspect that the answer is affirmative and that it can be proved in
\mathsf{ZFC}
.
If true, this would be analogous to the fact that
\mathbb{R}^{2}
can be covered with three sprays with non-collinear centers.

The results in this paper lie on the interface between set theory and the geometry of euclidean spaces.
The archetypal results in this area are the classical theorems of Sierpiński—see Theorem
2.6
below and the beginning of Section
4
for some context and references.
Most of these results deal with linear objects like lines, or hyperplanes in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, while sprays are essentially non-linear objects.
We extend a construction in
[
Sch10
]
from
\mathbb{R}^{2}
to
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, transforming the quadratic problem of covering the space with sprays to the linear problem of covering the space with sets having finite intersections with certain families of hyperplanes.
This latter problem has been studied before
[
Bag59
,
EJM94
,
Sim97
]
.
Extending these earlier results we are able to prove our results on sprays.

The paper is organized as follows.
After recalling the notations and the basic notions that will be used throughout the paper, we show in Section
3
how to transform a covering of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with sprays with centers on a given hyperplane into a covering of (an open subset of)
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with small intersection with certain families of hyperplanes.
Section
4
is devoted to studying the following problem: given distinct, non-zero vectors
\mathbf{u}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{u}_{k}
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, are there
A_{1},\dots,A_{k}
covering
\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that every plane orthogonal to
\mathbf{u}_{i}
has finite intersection with
A_{i}
?
It turns out that this problem is closely related to the size of the continuum, and by the results in Section
3
it is equivalent to the existence of sprays
X_{1},\dots,X_{k}
with centers on a given hyperplane
H
, and covering
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, as the (directions of the)
\mathbf{u}_{i}
s correspond to the position of the centers of the
X_{i}
s on
H
.
Finally in Section
5
we prove the results about the existence of sprays covering
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and the size of the continuum.

In order to make the present paper accessible to a wider audience, many basic definitions in set theory and geometry are summarized in Section
2.1
.
The reader who is proficient with these matters can safely skip it.

2.
Notation and preliminary results

2.1.
Notation

2.1.1.
Set theory

We work in
\mathsf{ZFC}
—this is the standard framework that most mathematicians (implicitly, or explicitly) adopt to prove theorems.
Our notation is standard:
\mathscr{P}(X)
is the power-set of
X
, the pointwise image of
Z\subseteq X
via some
f\colon X\to Y
is
f[Z]\coloneqq\mathopen{\{}{f(z)}\boldsymbol{\mid}{z\in Z}\mathclose{\}}
.

Ordinals
are special sets that are well-ordered by
\in
, the membership relation, and every well-ordered set is isomorphic to a unique ordinal.
Lower-case Greek letters range over the ordinals,
\mathord{\mathrm{Ord}}
is the collection of all ordinals, and for
\alpha,\beta\in\mathord{\mathrm{Ord}}
we have
\alpha<\beta
if and only if
\alpha\in\beta
.
Any natural number can be construed as an ordinal, letting
0
be
\emptyset
the empty set,
1
be the set
\left\{{0}\right\}
, and more generally
n+1\coloneqq n\cup\left\{{n}\right\}
.
The least infinite ordinal
\omega
is the set
\mathbb{N}=\left\{{0,1,\dots,n,\dots}\right\}
.
A
cardinal
is an ordinal that is not in bijection with a smaller ordinal—every natural number is a cardinal, and so is
\omega
.
Infinite cardinals are enumerated by the function
\aleph
using ordinals as indexes; so
\aleph_{0}
is
\omega
, the smallest infinite cardinal,
\aleph_{1}
is the smallest cardinal bigger than
\aleph_{0}
, and more generally
\aleph_{n+1}
is the smallest cardinal bigger than
\aleph_{n}
.
As
\omega
is the smallest ordinal bigger than any finite ordinal (i.e. natural number),
\aleph_{\omega}
is the smallest cardinal bigger than any
\aleph_{n}
.
As there is no reason to stop there we can take larger ordinals such as
\omega+1<\omega+2<\dots
and construct even larger cardinals
\aleph_{\omega+1}<\aleph_{\omega+2}<\dots
.
And so on.

Using the axiom of choice, every set
X
can be well-ordered, so it is in bijection with some ordinal, and hence with a unique cardinal: this cardinal is denoted by
\mathopen{|}X\mathclose{|}
and it is called the
cardinality
of
X
.
A set
X
is finite if it is in bijection with a natural number i.e.
\mathopen{|}X\mathclose{|}<\aleph_{0}
; otherwise it is infinite i.e.
\aleph_{0}\leq\mathopen{|}X\mathclose{|}
.
We say
X
is
countable
if either
\mathopen{|}X\mathclose{|}<\aleph_{0}
, or else
\mathopen{|}X\mathclose{|}=\aleph_{0}
—this can be written in a compact way as
\mathopen{|}X\mathclose{|}\leq\aleph_{0}
or as
\mathopen{|}X\mathclose{|}<\aleph_{1}
.
A set
X
is
uncountable
if it is not countable, that is
\aleph_{1}\leq\mathopen{|}X\mathclose{|}
.
The countable union of countable sets is countable.
More generally, for any ordinal
\delta
, if
(X_{i})_{i\in I}
is a family of sets, each of cardinality
\leq\aleph_{\delta}
and
\mathopen{|}I\mathclose{|}\leq\aleph_{\delta}
, then
\mathopen{|}\bigcup_{i\in I}X_{i}\mathclose{|}\leq\aleph_{\delta}
.

The set
\mathbb{R}
is in bijection with
\mathopen{\{}{f}\boldsymbol{\mid}{f\colon\omega\to\left\{{0,1}\right\}}\mathclose{\}}
, and for this reason the cardinality of
\mathbb{R}
is often denoted with
2^{\aleph_{0}}
.
By Cantor’s theorem
\aleph_{1}\leq\mathopen{|}\mathbb{R}\mathclose{|}
, and Cantor’s continuum hypothesis
{\mathsf{CH}}
asserts that the inequality can be replaced with an equality.
In other words
{\mathsf{CH}}
asserts that every subset of the real line is either countable or else it is in bijection with
\mathbb{R}
.
Since
\aleph_{1}\leq 2^{\aleph_{0}}
one can state
{\mathsf{CH}}
as
2^{\aleph_{0}}\leq\aleph_{1}
.
By Cohen’s results, it is consistent that the cardinality of
\mathbb{R}
be any
\aleph_{n+1}
, or even larger cardinals, like
\aleph_{\omega+n+1}
.
(But
2^{\aleph_{0}}
cannot be
\aleph_{\omega}
by König’s theorem.)

2.1.2.
Geometry

The standard basis of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
is denoted by
\mathbf{e}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{e}_{d}
.
The following notation for hyperplanes will be used throughout the paper.

Notation 2.1
.

Given a vector
\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\left\{{\mathbf{0}}\right\}
and
\mathbf{p}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
let

H_{\mathbf{u}}(\mathbf{p})=\mathbf{p}+\mathopen{\{}{\mathbf{v}}\boldsymbol{\mid}{\mathbf{v}\boldsymbol{\cdot}\mathbf{u}=0}\mathclose{\}}

be the hyperplane of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
orthogonal to
\mathbf{u}
passing through
\mathbf{p}
, where
\boldsymbol{\cdot}
is the (standard) inner product.
We also let
H_{i}(x)=\mathopen{\{}{(p_{1},\dots,p_{d})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\boldsymbol{\mid}{p_{i}=x}\mathclose{\}}
.

An
affine subspace

E
of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a translate of some vector subspace of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, that is a set of the form
\mathbf{p}+V
where
V
is a vector subspace of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
; the vector subspace
V
is unique, and it is the vector space associated to
E
, while
\mathbf{p}+V=\mathbf{p}^{\prime}+V
if and only if
\mathbf{p}-\mathbf{p}^{\prime}\in V
.
In this paper, a (finite dimensional)
affine space
is an affine subspace of some
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Elements of an affine space are called points, and since every vector subspace
V
of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
is also an affine space, we can refer to elements of it as points or vectors, depending if we privilege the affine or vector space structure.
If
E
is an affine space of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\mathbf{p},\mathbf{q}\in E
then
\mathbf{q}-\mathbf{p}
belongs to
V
, the underlying vector space of
E
, and if
\mathbf{v}\in V
then
\mathbf{p}+\mathbf{v}
belongs to
E
.
The dimension of an affine space is, by definition, the dimension of the associated vector space.
Given an affine space
E
with associated vector space
V
, the
affine envelope
or
affine span
of a non-empty set
S\subseteq E
is
\mathbf{p}+\Span\mathopen{\{}{\mathbf{q}-\mathbf{p}}\boldsymbol{\mid}{\mathbf{q}\in S}\mathclose{\}}
where
\mathbf{p}\in S
and
\Span X
is the smallest vector subspace of
V
containing
X\subseteq V
.
It is easy to check that the definition does not depend on the point
\mathbf{p}
, and that the affine span of
S
is the intersection of all affine subspaces of
E
containing
S
.
Two affine subspaces
E
and
F
of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
are
complementary
if their associated vector spaces
V
and
W
are complementary, that is
\mathbb{R}^{d}=V+W
and
V\cap W=\left\{{\mathbf{0}}\right\}
.
The intersection of two complementary spaces
E,F
is a single point.
In fact if
\mathbf{p},\mathbf{q}\in E\cap F
, then
\mathbf{p}-\mathbf{q}\in V\cap W=\left\{{\mathbf{0}}\right\}
, so
\mathbf{p}=\mathbf{q}
, that is
E\cap F
has at most one point.
If
E=\mathbf{p}+V
then
\mathbf{p}
can be written in a unique way as
\mathbf{v}+\bar{\mathbf{w}}
with
\mathbf{v}\in V
and
\bar{\mathbf{w}}\in W
, and
E=\bar{\mathbf{w}}+V
; similarly
F=\bar{\mathbf{v}}+W
for a unique
\bar{\mathbf{v}}\in V
.
Therefore
\bar{\mathbf{v}}+\bar{\mathbf{w}}
is the unique element of
E\cap F
.

The inner product
\boldsymbol{\cdot}
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
yields the Euclidean norm
\|\cdot\|
, and hence we have a distance on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and all its affine subspaces.
In particular, if
E
is an affine space then
\|\mathbf{p}-\mathbf{q}\|
is the distance between
\mathbf{p},\mathbf{q}\in E
.
Two vectors
\mathbf{v},\mathbf{w}
of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
are
orthogonal
if
\mathbf{v}\boldsymbol{\cdot}\mathbf{w}=0
.
If
V,W
are complementary subspaces of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, then
V
and
W
are orthogonal if
\forall{\mathbf{v}\in V}\,\forall{\mathbf{w}\in W}\,(\mathbf{v}\boldsymbol{\cdot}\mathbf{w}=0)
; in this case we call one of the two subspaces the
orthogonal complement
of the other.
Two complementary affine subspaces
E
and
F
of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
are orthogonal if their underlying vector spaces are orthogonal.

Let
E
be an affine subspace of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
The
sphere in
E
with center
\mathbf{c}\in E
and radius
r\in\mathbb{R}
is the set

\mathbb{S}(E;\mathbf{c},r)\coloneqq\mathopen{\{}{\mathbf{x}\in E}\boldsymbol{\mid}{\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{c}\|=r}\mathclose{\}}.

We convene that
\mathbb{S}(E;\mathbf{c},r)
is empty if
r<0
, and it is the singleton
\left\{{\mathbf{c}}\right\}
when
r=0
.
Observe that if
r>0
then
\mathbb{S}(E;\mathbf{c},r)
has cardinality
2
when
\dim(E)=1
; if
\dim(E)\geq 2
, then
\mathbb{S}(E;\mathbf{c},r)
has cardinality
2^{\aleph_{0}}
.
Whenever the ambient space (i.e.
\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
E
, …) is clear we will simply write
\mathbb{S}(\mathbf{c},r)
.
A
(k-1)
-dimensional sphere
is a sphere in an affine subspace
E
of some
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
\dim E=k
.

2.2.
Families with finite mesh

Definition 2.2
.

Let
N,d\geq 2
be natural numbers and suppose that
\mathcal{H}_{i}\subseteq\mathscr{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
with
1\leq i\leq N
are pairwise disjoint.
The sequence
(\mathcal{H}_{i})_{i=1}^{N}

•

is
locally finite
if there is a positive integer
s
such that for any
s
distinct points in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
there are at most finitely many sets in
\bigcup_{i=1}^{N}\mathcal{H}_{i}
to which these points belong;

•

has
mesh

r
, if
r\geq 2
is the least integer such that the intersection of
r
sets belonging to distinct
\mathcal{H}_{i}
s is a finite set.

All sequences
(\mathcal{H}_{i})_{i=1}^{N}
we consider are locally finite.
The reason for Definition
2.2
is the following very general result by Erdős, Jackson, and Mauldin
[
EJM94
, Theorem 2]
:

Theorem 2.3
.

For all
d\geq 2
,
n\geq 0
, and
\delta=0,1
the following are equivalent:

(a)

2^{\aleph_{0}}\leq\aleph_{\delta+n}
;

(b)

for any
r\geq 2
, letting
N=(n+1)(r-1)+1
, and for any sequence of pairwise disjoint
\mathcal{H}_{i}\subseteq\mathscr{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
with
1\leq i\leq N
locally finite and of mesh
r
, there are
A_{1},\dots,A_{N}
covering
\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that
\forall{1\leq i\leq N}\,\forall{H\in\mathcal{H}_{i}}\,(\mathopen{|}H\cap A_{i}\mathclose{|}<\aleph_{\delta})
.

The theorem remains true if
\delta
is taken to be an ordinal, not just
0
or
1
.
The main use of Theorem
2.3
in this paper is the direction
(a)
\Rightarrow
(b)
.
Given
(\mathcal{H}_{i})_{i=1}^{N}
locally finite of mesh
r
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, then
\mathopen{|}\mathbb{R}\mathclose{|}\leq\aleph_{\delta+n}
implies that there are
A_{1},\dots,A_{N}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
covering
\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that for all
H\in\mathcal{H}_{i}
, the set
H\cap A_{i}
is
finite
if
\delta=0
or
countable
if
\delta=1
.
Actually, the “forward implication” (i.e. from a bound on the size of the continuum, to the existence of specific subsets of the space) in many papers
[
Bag59
,
Dav62
,
Dav63
,
Dav63a
,
Bag68
,
Kom01
]
follows from Theorem
2.3
.

2.3.
Points and vectors in general position

Definition 2.4
.

A set of vectors of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
is in
general position
if any subset of size
\leq d
is linearly independent—in other words: the vectors are as linearly independent as possible.

Example 2.5
.

If
\mathbf{u}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{u}_{N}
are vectors in general position in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and
\mathcal{H}_{i}\coloneqq\mathopen{\{}{H_{\mathbf{u}_{i}}(\mathbf{p})}\boldsymbol{\mid}{\mathbf{p}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathclose{\}}
is the family of all hyperplanes orthogonal to
\mathbf{u}_{i}
, then
(\mathcal{H}_{i})_{i=1}^{N}
is locally finite, and of mesh
d
.

By Example
2.5
and Theorem
2.3
, if
\mathbf{u}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{u}_{(n+1)(d-1)+1}
are vectors in general position in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, then

•

2^{\aleph_{0}}\leq\aleph_{n}
implies that there are
A_{1},\dots,A_{(n+1)(d-1)+1}
covering
\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that
H_{\mathbf{u}_{i}}(\mathbf{p})\cap A_{i}
is finite for every
\mathbf{p}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
1\leq i\leq(n+1)(d-1)+1
;

•

2^{\aleph_{0}}\leq\aleph_{n+1}
implies that there are
A_{1},\dots,A_{(n+1)(d-1)+1}
covering
\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that
H_{\mathbf{u}_{i}}(\mathbf{p})\cap A_{i}
is countable, for every
\mathbf{p}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
1\leq i\leq(n+1)(d-1)+1
.

Therefore
{\mathsf{CH}}
implies that if
\mathbf{u}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{u}_{d}
are a basis of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, then there are
A_{1},\dots,A_{d}
covering
\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that every hyperplane orthogonal to
\mathbf{u}_{i}
has countable intersection with
A_{i}
.

In fact, the implications above are actually equivalences
[
KT06
, pp. 71, 327–328]
.

Theorem 2.6

(Sierpiński)
.

{\mathsf{CH}}
is equivalent to either one of the following:

(a)

there are
A_{1},A_{2}
covering the plane such that every vertical line has countable intersection with
A_{1}
and every horizontal line has countable intersection with
A_{2}
,

(b)

there are
A_{1},A_{2},A_{3}
covering
\mathbb{R}^{3}
such that every plane orthogonal to
\mathbf{e}_{i}
has countable intersection with
A_{i}
.

Definition
2.4
can be extended to points in affine spaces.

Definition 2.7
.

Let
S
be a set of points of an affine space
E
of dimension
d
.
We say that
S
is

•

in
general position in
E
if the affine span of any of its subset of size
k+1
has dimension
k
, for all
k\leq d
,

•

well-placed in

E
if
S\subseteq H
for some hyperplane
H
, and
S
is in general position in
H
.

Remarks 2.8
.

Let
E
be an affine space of dimension
d\geq 2
and let
S\subseteq E
.

(a)

If
S
is in general position (well-placed), and
S^{\prime}\subseteq S
then
S^{\prime}
is in general position (well-placed).
In other words, the notions of being in general position/well-placed are downward persistent with respect to inclusion.

(b)

Suppose
S
is well-placed:

•

if
\mathopen{|}S\mathclose{|}\geq d
then the hyperplane in the definition is unique, being the affine span of any subset of size
d
;

•

if
\mathopen{|}S\mathclose{|}\leq d
then
S
is in general position in
E
.

(c)

Suppose
H
is a hyperplane of
E
, and
S\subseteq H
.
Then
S
is well-placed in
E
if and only if
S
is in general position in
H
.

(d)

S
is in general position in
E
if and only if the set of vectors
\mathopen{\{}{\mathbf{q}-\mathbf{p}}\boldsymbol{\mid}{\mathbf{q}\in S\setminus\left\{{\mathbf{p}}\right\}}\mathclose{\}}
is in general position in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, for all
\mathbf{p}\in S
.

Every set of points in
\mathbb{R}
is in general position, a set of points in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
is in general position if no three of them are aligned, a set of points in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
is in general position if no four of them are coplanar, and so on.
A set of points in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
is well-placed if they are collinear, a set of points in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
is well-placed if they are coplanar, and no three of them are collinear, a set of points in
\mathbb{R}^{4}
is well-placed if they belong to the same
3
-dimensional affine subspace, and no four of them are coplanar, and so on.

Finally observe that for any affine space
E
of dimension
d\geq 2
and any
S\subseteq E

(1)

\begin{multlined}\bigl(S\text{ is general position in $E$, or $S$ is well-placed in }E\bigr)\Rightarrow{}\qquad\\
\qquad\forall{k<d}\,\bigl(\text{the affine span of any subset of $S$ of size }k+1\\
\qquad\text{has dimension }k\bigr).\end{multlined}

Lemma 2.9
.

Suppose
\mathbf{c}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{c}_{k+1}
are distinct points in general position in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, with
d\geq k
.
Let
H
be a hyperplane orthogonal to
\mathbf{c}_{k}-\mathbf{c}_{k+1}
, let
\pi\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\to H
be the orthogonal projection.
Let
\bar{\mathbf{c}}_{k}=\pi(\mathbf{c}_{k})=\pi(\mathbf{c}_{k+1})
and let
\bar{\mathbf{c}}_{i}=\pi(\mathbf{c}_{i})
for

Then
\bar{\mathbf{c}}_{1},\dots,\bar{\mathbf{c}}_{k}
are distinct and in general position in

.

Proof.

If
\bar{\mathbf{c}}_{i}=\bar{\mathbf{c}}_{k}
for some

\mathbf{c}_{i},\mathbf{c}_{k},\mathbf{c}_{k+1}
must be collinear, and if
\bar{\mathbf{c}}_{i}=\bar{\mathbf{c}}_{j}
for some

\mathbf{c}_{i}-\mathbf{c}_{j}
and
\mathbf{c}_{k}-\mathbf{c}_{k+1}
must be parallel, so the four points
\mathbf{c}_{i},\mathbf{c}_{j},\mathbf{c}_{k},\mathbf{c}_{k+1}
are coplanar.
In either case this contradicts the assumption that
\mathbf{c}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{c}_{k+1}
are in general position.
Therefore the points
\bar{\mathbf{c}}_{1},\dots,\bar{\mathbf{c}}_{k}
are distinct.

In order to conclude the proof it is enough to show that the vectors
\bar{\mathbf{c}}_{1}-\bar{\mathbf{c}}_{k}

\bar{\mathbf{c}}_{2}-\bar{\mathbf{c}}_{k}
, …,
\bar{\mathbf{c}}_{k-1}-\bar{\mathbf{c}}_{k}

are linearly independent.
Towards a contradiction, suppose there are
(r_{1},\dots,r_{k-1})\neq(0,\dots,0)
such that

\mathbf{0}=r_{1}(\bar{\mathbf{c}}_{1}-\bar{\mathbf{c}}_{k})+r_{2}(\bar{\mathbf{c}}_{2}-\bar{\mathbf{c}}_{k})+\dots+r_{k-1}(\bar{\mathbf{c}}_{k-1}-\bar{\mathbf{c}}_{k}).

As
\bar{\mathbf{c}}_{i}
is the projection of
\mathbf{c}_{i}
along the vector
\mathbf{c}_{k+1}-\mathbf{c}_{k}
, there are

such that
\bar{\mathbf{c}}_{i}=\mathbf{c}_{i}+s_{i}(\mathbf{c}_{k+1}-\mathbf{c}_{k})
.
Substituting in the previous formula we obtain

\mathbf{0}=r_{1}(\mathbf{c}_{1}-\mathbf{c}_{k})+\dots+r_{k-1}(\mathbf{c}_{k-1}-\mathbf{c}_{k})\\
{}+[r_{1}(s_{1}-s_{k})+\dots+r_{k-1}(s_{k-1}-s_{k})](\mathbf{c}_{k+1}-\mathbf{c}_{k}),

and since
\mathbf{c}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{c}_{k+1}
are in general position in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, then

and
[r_{1}(s_{1}-s_{k})+\dots+r_{k-1}(s_{k-1}-s_{k})]=0
, against our choice of the

s.
∎

2.4.
Sprays

J. Schmerl in
[

]
defined a spray to be a set

such that

is finite, for all circles

centered in some given point.
Thus a spray

is a very sparse subset of the plane, and one may investigate what happens if one relaxes the finiteness condition on

with, say, being countable.

Definition 2.17

\mathbb{R}^{d}
, with
d\geq 2
be a natural number.
An
\aleph_{\delta}
-spray in

\mathopen{|}\mathbb{S}(E;\mathbf{c},r)\cap X\mathclose{|}<\aleph_{\delta}

.
When

\delta=1

\sigma
-spray
.

Note that a center need not to belong to the
\aleph_{\delta}
-spray, and that a
\aleph_{\delta}
-spray may have more than one center.

By Example
2.10

\mathbf{c}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{c}_{(n+1)(d-1)+1}
are either in general position, or well-placed in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, then

•

2^{\aleph_{0}}\leq\aleph_{n}
implies that there are
X_{1},\dots,X_{(n+1)(d-1)+1}
covering
\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that each
X_{i}
is a spray centered in
\mathbf{c}_{i}
;

•

2^{\aleph_{0}}\leq\aleph_{n+1}
implies that there are
X_{1},\dots,X_{(n+1)(d-1)+1}
covering
\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that each
X_{i}
is a
\sigma
-spray.

In particular if the points are well-placed, that is if we assume that the
\mathbf{c}_{i}
s lie on a hyperplane, then:

Theorem 2.18
.

,

, and
\delta=0,1
.
Assume that
\mathbf{c}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{c}_{(n+1)(d-1)+1}

-spray centered in

By (

The reason for focusing on well-placed points is that under this assumption the implication in Theorem

can be reversed, and this is the main goal of this paper.
In Section

we argue that any covering of

with sprays (

The first such result is Sierpiński’s theorem form 1919, asserting that
{\mathsf{CH}}
is equivalent to a covering of the plane with two sets such that the intersection of the first set with any vertical line is countable and the intersection of every horizontal line with the second set is countable (see Theorem
2.6
(a)
).
In
[
Sie51
]
Sierpiński sharpened his previous result by replacing “countable” with “finite”, but at the cost of increasing the dimension:
{\mathsf{CH}}
is equivalent to a decomposition
A_{1},A_{2},A_{3}
of
\mathbb{R}^{3}
such that the intersection of any line with direction
\mathbf{e}_{i}
with
A_{i}
is finite (Theorem
2.6
(b)
), and this was quickly generalized by Kuratowski to higher dimensions
[
Kur51
]
:
2^{\aleph_{0}}\leq\aleph_{n}
if and only if there is a decomposition
A_{1},\dots,A_{n+2}
of
\mathbb{R}^{n+2}
such that every line parallel to
\mathbf{e}_{i}
has finite intersection with
A_{i}
.
In the sixties of the last century, Bagemihl and Davies showed that Kuratowski’s result could be proved for
\mathbb{R}^{2}
by taking intersections with lines of prescribed directions
[
Bag60
,
Bag61
,
Dav62
,
Dav63a
,
Bag68
]
.
A line is a hyperplane in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, so Sierpiński’s result form 1919 could be stated as: if
d=2
then
{\mathsf{CH}}
is equivalent to a decomposition of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
into
A_{1},\dots,A_{d}
such that every hyperplane orthogonal to
\mathbf{e}_{i}
has countable intersection with
A_{i}
.
This result holds for all
d\geq 2
; Sierpiński states and proves it for
d=3
in
[
Sie51
]
, see Corollary
4.7
below, and observes that the result for
\mathbb{R}^{3}
is false if “countable” is replaced by “finite”.
Erdős, Jackson, and Mauldin prove that
{\mathsf{CH}}
is equivalent to
\mathbb{R}^{3}
being decomposable in five pieces
A_{1},\dots,A_{5}
so that every plane orthogonal to
\mathbf{u}_{i}
has finite intersection with
A_{i}
, where
\mathbf{u}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{u}_{5}
are in general position in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, but no decomposition exists if we just allow four vectors and four sets
[
EJM94
, Corollary 6, Lemma 1]
.
In that paper it is stated (but not proved) that an analogous result holds if
{\mathsf{CH}}
is weakened to
2^{\aleph_{0}}\leq\aleph_{n}
.
Since we need a detailed analysis of the positions of the various (hyper)planes we will state and prove these results in full generality below.
This section is devoted to the following

Problem 4.1
.

Given
\mathbf{u}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{u}_{n}
distinct, non-zero vectors of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, what conditions on the cardinality of
\mathbb{R}
are equivalent to the existence of
A_{1},\dots,A_{n}
covering
\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that each
A_{i}\cap H_{\mathbf{u}_{i}}(\mathbf{p})
is finite (or countable)?

First of all we need a result allowing us to replace the standard basis of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with any basis.

Lemma 4.2
.

Suppose
\mathbf{u}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{u}_{d}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
are linearly independent, and
d\geq 2
.
There is a linear isomorphism
T\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
that maps every hyperplane orthogonal to
\mathbf{u}_{i}
to a hyperplane orthogonal to
\mathbf{e}_{i}
, and conversely, for any
i\leq d
.

Proof.

Any linear
T
preserves translations, so it is enough to consider hyperplanes passing through the origin.
In other words it is enough to find an injective
T
such that
T[H_{\mathbf{u}_{i}}(\mathbf{0})]\subseteq H_{\mathbf{e}_{i}}(\mathbf{0})
.
For
1\leq i\leq d
let
\mathbf{u}_{i}=(u_{i,1},\dots,u_{i,d})
.
Consider the
d\times d
-matrix
M=(u_{i,j})^{\mathrm{t}}
that is the transpose of the matrix of the linear transformation such that
\mathbf{e}_{i}\mapsto\mathbf{u}_{i}
for
i=1,\dots,d
.
The matrix
M
is invertible, as the
\mathbf{u}_{i}
s are linearly independent, so the transformation
T
induced by
M
is an isomorphism.
If
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{d})\in H_{\mathbf{u}_{i}}(\mathbf{0})
, then
\mathbf{u}_{i}\boldsymbol{\cdot}\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{t}}=\sum_{j=1}^{d}u_{i,j\,}x_{j}=0
, and this is the
i
-th row of
M\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{t}}
.
We obtain that
M\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{t}}\in H_{\mathbf{e}_{i}}(\mathbf{0})
.
Therefore
T[H_{\mathbf{u}_{i}}(\mathbf{0})]\subseteq H_{\mathbf{e}_{i}}(\mathbf{0})
for all
i<d
, and this is what we had to prove.
∎

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
\varepsilon>0
.
For all
X\subseteq\mathbb{R}
with
\aleph_{0}\leq\mathopen{|}X\mathclose{|}<\kappa\leq 2^{\aleph_{0}}
, there is
S\subseteq(-\varepsilon;\varepsilon)
such that
\mathopen{|}S\mathclose{|}=\kappa
and
(X-X)\cap(S-S)=\mathopen{\{}{0}\mathclose{\}}
.

Proof.

Let
\tilde{X}
be the subgroup of
(\mathbb{R},+)
generated by
X
.
Let
T\subseteq\mathbb{R}
be a transversal for the quotient, i.e. a set picking exactly one element from each coset of
\mathbb{R}/\tilde{X}
.
Then
2^{\aleph_{0}}=\mathopen{|}\mathbb{R}/\tilde{X}\mathclose{|}=\mathopen{|}T\mathclose{|}=\mathopen{|}T\cap(-\varepsilon;\varepsilon)\mathclose{|}
.
Let
S\subseteq T\cap(-\varepsilon;\varepsilon)
be of size
\kappa
.

If
s_{1}-s_{2}=x_{1}-x_{2}
for some
s_{1},s_{2}\in S
and
x_{1},x_{2}\in X
, since
x_{1}-x_{2}\in\tilde{X}
this means that the cosets
s_{1}+\tilde{X}
and
s_{2}+\tilde{X}
are the same, so
s_{1}=s_{2}
by definition of
S
.
Therefore
(S-S)\cap(X-X)=\mathopen{\{}{0}\mathclose{\}}
.
∎

The next result asserts that if the sets
A_{i}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
have small intersection with the hyperplanes orthogonal to
\mathbf{u}_{i}
, and if
2^{\aleph_{0}}
is large enough, then there is a set
Z\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that no translate of it can be covered by the
A_{i}
s—in particular the
A_{i}
s do not cover
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Theorem 4.4
.

Let
\delta\in\mathord{\mathrm{Ord}}
,
d\geq 3
and let
N=2(d-1)
.
Suppose
\mathbf{u}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{u}_{N}
are distinct, non-zero vectors of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Suppose
A_{1},\dots,A_{N}
are subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that for all
\mathbf{p}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and for all
1\leq i\leq N

\mathopen{|}H_{\mathbf{u}_{i}}(\mathbf{p})\cap A_{i}\mathclose{|}<\aleph_{\delta}.

If
2^{\aleph_{0}}>\aleph_{\delta}
then for every
\varepsilon>0
there is
Z\subseteq(-\varepsilon;\varepsilon)^{d}
of size
\aleph_{\delta+1}
such that

\forall{\mathbf{p}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\,(\mathbf{p}+Z\nsubseteq\textstyle\bigcup_{i=1}^{N}A_{i}).

Before proving this let us draw a few corollaries.
With the same notation as before:

Theorem 4.5
.

Let
\delta\in\mathord{\mathrm{Ord}}
,
d\geq 3
and
N=2(d-1)
.
Suppose
\mathbf{u}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{u}_{N}
are non-zero vectors of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Suppose
D\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
is such that
\Int(D)\neq\emptyset
.
If
A_{1},\dots,A_{N}
cover
D
and for all
\mathbf{p}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and for all
1\leq i\leq N

\mathopen{|}H_{\mathbf{u}_{i}}(\mathbf{p})\cap A_{i}\mathclose{|}<\aleph_{\delta}.

Then
2^{\aleph_{0}}\leq\aleph_{\delta}
.

Proof.

Towards a contradiction, suppose
2^{\aleph_{0}}\geq\aleph_{\delta+1}
.
Let
\varepsilon>0
be small enough so that
\mathbf{p}+(-\varepsilon;\varepsilon)^{d}\subseteq D
for some
\mathbf{p}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and, towards a contradiction, suppose
A_{1},\dots,A_{N}
are as in the statement.
Let
Z\subseteq(-\varepsilon;\varepsilon)^{d}
be as in Theorem
4.4
: then
\mathbf{p}+Z
is contained in
D
but on other hand it is not contained in
A_{1}\cup\dots\cup A_{N}
, a contradiction.
∎

The presence of the set
D
in the statement of Theorem
4.5
may seem unwarranted right now, but it will be crucial for the results in Section
5
.
For the time being the reader can safely replace
D
with
\mathbb{R}^{d}
without losing much.

Theorem 4.6
.

For all
d\geq 3
and all
D\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that
\emptyset\neq\Int(D)
, the following are equivalent:

(a)

{\mathsf{CH}}
.

(b)

For all non-zero vectors
\mathbf{u}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{u}_{d}
spanning
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, there are
A_{1},\dots,A_{d}
covering
D
such that
H_{\mathbf{u}_{i}}(\mathbf{p})\cap A_{i}
is countable for all
\mathbf{p}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
1\leq i\leq d
.

(c)

There are non-zero vectors
\mathbf{u}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{u}_{2d-2}
and there are
A_{1},\dots,A_{2d-2}
covering
D
such that
H_{\mathbf{u}_{i}}(\mathbf{p})\cap A_{i}
is countable for all
\mathbf{p}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
1\leq i\leq 2d-2
.

Proof.

The implication
(a)
\Rightarrow
(b)
follows from Theorem
2.3
and Example
2.5
, while
(b)
\Rightarrow
(c)
is trivial—take
A_{d+1}=\dots=A_{2d-2}=\emptyset
.
The implication
(c)
\Rightarrow
(a)
follows from Theorem
4.5
when
\delta=1
.
∎

Corollary 4.7
.

For all
d\geq 3
,
{\mathsf{CH}}
is equivalent to the existence of
A_{1},\dots,A_{d}
covering
\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that
H_{i}(x)\cap A_{i}
is countable, for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
and all
1\leq i\leq d
.

Cantor’s theorem says that
2^{\aleph_{0}}>\aleph_{\delta}
if
\delta=0
, so when
d=3
Theorem
4.4
implies the following result, which is Lemma 1 of
[
EJM94
]
:

Corollary 4.8
.

Suppose
\mathbf{u}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{u}_{4}
are non-zero vectors of
\mathbb{R}^{3}
.
There are no
A_{1},\dots,A_{4}
covering
\mathbb{R}^{3}
such that
H_{\mathbf{u}_{i}}(\mathbf{p})\cap A_{i}
is finite, for all
\mathbf{p}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
and all
1\leq i\leq 4
.

Remark 4.9
.

Corollary
4.8
is a negative result, asserting that
\mathbb{R}^{3}
is not the union of four sets such that each intersects any plane orthogonal to a given vector in a finite set.
But looking at the proof of Theorem
4.4
, it could be recast in a positive way as:

Suppose
\Bbbk
is an infinite field.
If there are non-zero vectors
\mathbf{u}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{u}_{4}
of
\Bbbk^{3}
and sets
A_{1},\dots,A_{4}
covering
\Bbbk^{3}
such that every hyperplane orthogonal to
\mathbf{u}_{i}
has finite intersection with
A_{i}
, then
\mathopen{|}\Bbbk\mathclose{|}=\aleph_{0}
.

Proof of Theorem
4.4
.

Let
A_{1},\dots,A_{N}
and
\varepsilon>0
be as in the statement.
We consider two cases, depending whether the
\mathbf{u}_{i}
s span
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Case 1:
the vectors
\mathbf{u}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{u}_{N}
do not span
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Let
\mathbf{v}
be a non-zero vector such that
\mathbf{v}\boldsymbol{\cdot}\mathbf{u}_{k}=0
for all
1\leq k\leq N
, and let
V=\mathopen{\{}{r\mathbf{v}}\boldsymbol{\mid}{r\in\mathbb{R}}\mathclose{\}}
.
Since
\mathopen{|}V\mathclose{|}=2^{\aleph_{0}}\geq\aleph_{\delta+1}
we can take
Z\subseteq V\cap(-\varepsilon;\varepsilon)^{d}
of size
\aleph_{\delta+1}
.
Since
\mathbf{p}+V\subseteq H_{\mathbf{u}_{i}}(\mathbf{p})
we have that
\mathopen{|}(\mathbf{p}+V)\cap\bigcup_{i=1}^{N}A_{i}\mathclose{|}<\aleph_{\delta}
, and hence
\mathbf{p}+Z\nsubseteq\bigcup_{i=1}^{N}A_{i}
.

Case 2:
the vectors
\mathbf{u}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{u}_{N}
span
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Without loss of generality we may assume that
(\mathbf{u}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{u}_{d})
is a basis of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
We first prove the result under the additional assumption that

(\mathbf{u}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{u}_{d})\text{ is the standard basis }(\mathbf{e}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{e}_{d}).

Let

\displaystyle U

\displaystyle=\Span\mathopen{\{}{\mathbf{u}_{d+1},\dots,\mathbf{u}_{N}}\mathclose{\}},

\displaystyle U_{j}

\displaystyle=\Span\mathopen{\{}{\mathbf{e}_{j},\mathbf{u}_{d+1},\dots,\mathbf{u}_{N}}\mathclose{\}}\text{, for }1\leq j\leq d.

As
\dim U\leq d-2
then
\dim U_{j}\leq d-1
, so there is a non-zero vector orthogonal to
U_{j}
.

Claim 4.9.1
.

There is
1\leq j\leq d
such that there is
\mathbf{v}\neq\mathbf{0}
orthogonal to
U_{j}
, and
\mathbf{v}
not collinear to any
\mathbf{e}_{i}
.

Proof of the Claim.

Suppose
\dim U_{j}\leq d-2
for some
j
, and that every
\mathbf{v}\neq\mathbf{0}
orthogonal to
U_{j}
is collinear to some
\mathbf{e}_{i}
.
As the orthogonal complement of
U_{j}
has dimension
\geq 2
, there are
\mathbf{e}_{i_{1}},\mathbf{e}_{i_{2}}
orthogonal to
U_{j}
, and hence
\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{e}_{i_{1}}+\mathbf{e}_{i_{2}}
is orthogonal to
U_{j}
and is not collinear to any vector of the standard basis, against our assumption.

Therefore we may assume that
\dim U_{j}=d-1
for all
j
, and hence
\dim U=d-2
.
Towards a contradiction, suppose that for every
j\in\mathopen{\{}{1,\dots,d}\mathclose{\}}
there is a (necessarily unique)
j^{*}\neq j
such that
\mathbf{e}_{j^{*}}
is orthogonal to
U_{j}
.
The map
j\mapsto j^{*}
is not constant, so fix
i\neq j
such that
i^{*}\neq j^{*}
.
As
d\geq 3
pick
k\in\mathopen{\{}{1,\dots,d}\mathclose{\}}
distinct from
i^{*},j^{*}
.
Both vectors
\mathbf{e}_{i^{*}}
and
\mathbf{e}_{j^{*}}
are orthogonal to
U
and to
\mathbf{e}_{k}
, so both are orthogonal to
U_{k}
, which is impossible as
\dim U_{k}=d-1
.
∎

Fix
j
and
\mathbf{v}=(a_{1},\dots,a_{d})
as in the Claim.
By a re-indexing, if needed, we may assume that
j=d
, that is
a_{d}=0
, and that
a_{1},a_{2}
are non-zero.
By rescaling we may assume that

\mathbf{v}=(1,a_{2},\dots,a_{d-1},0),\qquad a_{2}\neq 0

and that

(4)

d\leq k\leq N\Rightarrow\mathbf{v}\boldsymbol{\cdot}\mathbf{u}_{k}=0.

Let
\nu\coloneqq\max\left\{{1,\mathopen{|}a_{2}\mathclose{|},\dots,\mathopen{|}a_{d-1}\mathclose{|}}\right\}
.
For
1\leq i\leq d
let
X_{i}\subseteq(-\varepsilon/2;\varepsilon/2)
such that
\mathopen{|}X_{1}\mathclose{|}=\aleph_{\delta+1}
and
\mathopen{|}X_{i}\mathclose{|}=\aleph_{\delta}
for all
2\leq i\leq d
.
By Lemma
4.3
, let
S\subseteq(-\frac{\varepsilon}{2\nu};\frac{\varepsilon}{2\nu})
be of size
\aleph_{\delta+1}
such that
(S-S)\cap(a_{2}^{-1}X_{2}-a_{2}^{-1}X_{2})=\mathopen{\{}{0}\mathclose{\}}
, and let

V=\mathopen{\{}{s\mathbf{v}}\boldsymbol{\mid}{s\in S}\mathclose{\}}.

By (
4
), if
\mathbf{q}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
s\in S
then

(5)

d\leq k\leq N\Rightarrow\mathbf{q}+V\subseteq H_{\mathbf{u}_{k}}(\mathbf{q}+s\mathbf{v}).

As
V\subseteq(-\varepsilon/2;\varepsilon/2)^{d}
it follows that

Z\coloneqq V+\textstyle\prod_{i=1}^{d}X_{i}

is a subset of
(-\varepsilon;\varepsilon)^{d}
, and it is of cardinality
\aleph_{\delta+1}
.
Fix
\mathbf{p}=(p_{1},\dots,p_{d})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and, towards a contradiction, suppose that
\mathbf{p}+Z\subseteq\bigcup_{i=1}^{N}A_{i}
.
Observe that
Z=\bigcup_{x\in X_{1}}Z_{x}
, where

Z_{x}\coloneqq V+(\mathopen{\{}{x}\mathclose{\}}\times\textstyle\prod_{i=2}^{d}X_{i}).

Claim 4.9.2
.

The sets
Z_{x}
are pairwise disjoint.

Proof.

If
Z_{x^{\prime}}\cap Z_{x^{\prime\prime}}\neq\emptyset
, then there are
s^{\prime},s^{\prime\prime}\in S
,
x_{i}^{\prime},x_{i}^{\prime\prime}\in X_{i}
for
2\leq i\leq d
such that

s^{\prime}\mathbf{v}+(x^{\prime},x^{\prime}_{2},\dots,x^{\prime}_{d})=s^{\prime\prime}\mathbf{v}+(x^{\prime\prime},x_{2}^{\prime\prime},\dots,x^{\prime\prime}_{d}).

The
2
-nd component yields that
s^{\prime}a_{2}+x_{2}^{\prime}=s^{\prime\prime}a_{2}+x_{2}^{\prime\prime}
, and hence

s^{\prime}-s^{\prime\prime}=a_{2}^{-1}(x_{2}^{\prime\prime}-x_{2}^{\prime})\in(S-S)\cap(a_{2}^{-1}X_{2}-a_{2}^{-1}X_{2})=\left\{{0}\right\}.

It follows that
s^{\prime}=s^{\prime\prime}=s
, and hence

s\mathbf{v}+(x^{\prime},x_{2}^{\prime},\dots,x^{\prime}_{d})=s\mathbf{v}+(x^{\prime\prime},x_{2}^{\prime\prime},\dots,x^{\prime\prime}_{d}).

Looking at the
1
-st component we obtain
s+x^{\prime}=s+x^{\prime\prime}
and hence
x^{\prime}=x^{\prime\prime}
.
∎

Summarizing:

(6)

\mathbf{p}+Z=\bigcup_{x\in X_{1}}\mathbf{p}+Z_{x}\text{, and }x^{\prime}\neq x^{\prime\prime}\Rightarrow\mathbf{p}+Z_{x^{\prime}}\cap\mathbf{p}+Z_{x^{\prime\prime}}=\emptyset.

Claim 4.9.3
.

\mathopen{|}A_{d}\cap(\mathbf{p}+Z)\mathclose{|}<\aleph_{\delta+1}
.

Proof.

As the last component of
\mathbf{v}
is
0
, the set
V
does not contribute to
Z
on the
d
-th coordinate, so for all
w\in\mathbb{R}

\begin{split}(\mathbf{p}+Z)\cap H_{d}(p_{d}+w)&=(\mathbf{p}+\textstyle\prod_{i=1}^{d}X_{i})\cap H_{d}(p_{d}+w)\\
&=\mathbf{p}+\bigl((\textstyle\prod_{i=1}^{d}X_{i})\cap H_{d}(w)\bigr)\end{split}

and this set is
\mathbf{p}+(\prod_{i=1}^{d-1}X_{i})\times\left\{{w}\right\}
if
w\in X_{d}
, and it is empty if
w\notin X_{d}
.
Therefore
\mathbf{p}+Z\subseteq\bigcup_{w\in X_{d}}H_{d}(p_{d}+w)
and

A_{d}\cap(\mathbf{p}+Z)\subseteq\textstyle\bigcup_{w\in X_{d}}A_{d}\cap H_{d}(p_{d}+w).

By assumption
\mathopen{|}A_{d}\cap H_{d}(p_{d}+w)\mathclose{|}<\aleph_{\delta}
for any
w\in\mathbb{R}
, and
\mathopen{|}X_{d}\mathclose{|}=\aleph_{\delta}
, so
\mathopen{|}A_{d}\cap(\mathbf{p}+Z)\mathclose{|}\leq\aleph_{\delta}<\aleph_{\delta+1}
.
∎

As
\mathopen{|}X_{1}\mathclose{|}=\aleph_{\delta+1}
and by (
6
), there is
\bar{x}_{1}\in X_{1}
such that
\mathbf{p}+Z_{\bar{x}_{1}}
is disjoint from
A_{d}
.
This implies that

\mathbf{p}+Z_{\bar{x}_{1}}\subseteq A_{1}\cup\dots\cup A_{d-1}\cup\textstyle\bigcup_{k=d+1}^{N}A_{k}.

Claim 4.9.4
.

There is an
\bar{s}\in S
such that
\mathbf{p}+\bar{s}\mathbf{v}+(\mathopen{\{}{\bar{x}_{1}}\mathclose{\}}\times\prod_{i=2}^{d}X_{i})
is disjoint from
\bigcup_{k=d+1}^{N}A_{k}
.

Proof.

Towards a contradiction, suppose that for all
s\in S
and all
2\leq i\leq d
there are
x_{i}(s)\in X_{i}
such that

\mathbf{p}+s\mathbf{v}+(\bar{x}_{1},x_{2}(s),\dots,x_{d}(s))\in\textstyle\bigcup_{k=d+1}^{N}A_{k}.

As
\mathopen{|}X_{i}\mathclose{|}=\aleph_{\delta}
for
2\leq i\leq d
, there are
x_{i}^{\prime}\in X_{i}
and
S^{\prime}\subseteq S
of size
\aleph_{\delta+1}
such that
x_{i}(s)=x_{i}^{\prime}
for all
s\in S^{\prime}
.
Therefore

\forall{s\in S^{\prime}}\,\bigl(\mathbf{p}+s\mathbf{v}+(\bar{x}_{1},x_{2}^{\prime},\dots,x_{d}^{\prime})\in\textstyle\bigcup_{k=d+1}^{N}A_{k}\bigr).

As
\mathopen{|}S^{\prime}\mathclose{|}=\aleph_{\delta+1}
there is
S^{*}\subseteq S^{\prime}
of size
\aleph_{\delta+1}
, and there is
k
with
d<k\leq N
such that

(7)

\forall{s\in S^{*}}\,\bigl(\mathbf{p}+s\mathbf{v}+(\bar{x}_{1},x_{2}^{\prime},\dots,x_{d}^{\prime})\in A_{k}\bigr).

Fix
s^{*}\in S^{*}
and let

Q_{k}\coloneqq H_{\mathbf{u}_{k}}\bigl(\mathbf{p}+s^{*}\mathbf{v}+(\bar{x}_{1},x_{2}^{\prime},\dots,x_{d}^{\prime})\bigr).

By (
5
) with
\mathbf{q}=\mathbf{p}+(\bar{x}_{1},x_{2}^{\prime},\dots,x_{d}^{\prime})
we have that

\forall{s\in S^{*}}\,\bigl(\mathbf{p}+s\mathbf{v}+(\bar{x}_{1},x_{2}^{\prime},\dots,x_{d}^{\prime})\in Q_{k}\bigr).

Therefore by (
7
)
\mathbf{p}+s\mathbf{v}+(\bar{x}_{1},x_{2}^{\prime},\dots,x_{d}^{\prime})\in A_{k}\cap Q_{k}
for all
s\in S^{*}
, and this is a contradiction, since
\mathopen{|}A_{k}\cap Q_{k}\mathclose{|}<\aleph_{\delta}
, and yet it must contain
\aleph_{\delta+1}
points.
∎

Let
\bar{s}
be as in Claim
4.9.4
.
Then
\mathopen{|}\mathbf{p}+\bar{s}\mathbf{v}+(\left\{{\bar{x}_{1}}\right\}\times\prod_{i=2}^{d}X_{i})\mathclose{|}=\aleph_{\delta}
and

(8)

W_{1}\coloneqq\mathbf{p}+\bar{s}\mathbf{v}+(\left\{{\bar{x}_{1}}\right\}\times\textstyle\prod_{i=2}^{d}X_{i})\subseteq A_{1}\cup\dots\cup A_{d-1}.

As
W_{1}
is included in
H_{1}(p_{1}+\bar{s}+\bar{x}_{1})
, and

Remark 4.14
.

Theorems
4.4
and
4.10
(together with Theorem
2.3
) provide a complete solution to Problem

when the vectors are in general position, yet some further generalizations are possible.
For the sake of readability we have opted for less generality, and here we would like to mention some of these extensions.
(The proof of these results will appear elsewhere.)

Focusing on

and
\delta=0
, the requirement on the size of the intersections in Theorem

could be relaxed to

\displaystyle\mathopen{|}A_{i}\cap H_{\mathbf{u}_{i}}(\mathbf{p})\mathclose{|}<\aleph_{0}\quad i

\displaystyle=1,2,

\displaystyle\mathopen{|}A_{i}\cap H_{\mathbf{u}_{i}}(\mathbf{p})\mathclose{|}\leq\aleph_{0}\quad i

]

are countable for three distinct

Another possible generalization of Theorem

is that for any

and

Proof.

Let
H

\mathbf{c}_{i}
s.
As the definition of

H

\mathbf{0}\notin H

(\mathbf{c}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{c}_{d})

\mathbb{R}^{d}

Let
\mathbf{u}_{k}=(u_{k}^{1},\dots,u_{k}^{d})
.
We claim that
\sum_{i=1}^{d}u_{k}^{i}\neq 0
, for any
k\leq n
.
Otherwise

\mathbf{0}=\sum_{i=1}^{d}u_{k}^{i}(\mathbf{c}_{i}-\mathbf{c}_{k})=\sum_{i=1}^{d}u_{k}^{i}\mathbf{c}_{i}=u_{k}^{1}\mathbf{c}_{1}+\sum_{i=2}^{d}u_{k}^{i}\mathbf{c}_{i}\\
=-(\sum_{i=2}^{d}u_{k}^{i})\mathbf{c}_{1}+\sum_{i=2}^{d}u_{k}^{i}\mathbf{c}_{i}=\sum_{i=2}^{d}u_{k}^{i}(\mathbf{c}_{i}-\mathbf{c}_{1})

shows that
\mathbf{c}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{c}_{d}
are not in general position in
H

Scaling of vectors does not affect their general position, so we may assume that
\sum_{i=1}^{d}u_{k}^{i}=1

Then
\sum_{i=1}^{d}u_{k}^{i}(\mathbf{c}_{i}-\mathbf{c}_{k})=\mathbf{0}
implies that

\mathbf{c}_{k}=\sum_{i=1}^{d}u_{k}^{i}\mathbf{c}_{i}

that is to say:
(u_{k}^{1},\dots,u_{k}^{d})

\mathbf{c}_{k}
with respect to the basis
(\mathbf{c}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{c}_{d})

Therefore the general position of the

\mathbf{c}_{k}
s in

∎

5.1.
Covering the space with infinitely many sprays

We have seen how covering the space with sprays with well-placed centers is equivalent to giving an upper bound for the size of the continuum, the larger the number of sprays, the weaker the bound.
Next we show that, irrespective of the size of the continuum, the space can be covered with
\aleph_{0}
-many sprays with well-placed centers.

A
drizzle
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with center
\mathbf{c}
is a
X\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that any sphere centered in
\mathbf{c}
intersects
X
in at most one point—thus a drizzle is a very sparse spray.

Theorem 5.8
.

If
\mathopen{\{}{\mathbf{c}_{n}}\boldsymbol{\mid}{n\geq 1}\mathclose{\}}
are well-placed points in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, with
d\geq 2

X_{n}
covering
\mathbb{R}^{d}

X_{n}

\mathbf{c}_{n}

Proof.

Without loss of generality we may assume that the
\mathbf{c}_{n}
s belong to
\mathbb{R}^{d-1}\times\left\{{0}\right\}
.
Pick
\mathbf{u}_{n}\in\mathcal{U}(\mathbf{c}_{n})\setminus\mathopen{\{}{\mathbf{0}}\mathclose{\}}
, where
\mathcal{U}(\mathbf{q})=\mathcal{U}_{\mathbf{c}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{c}_{d}}(\mathbf{q})
.
By Proposition
5.3
the vectors
\mathbf{u}_{n}
(

) are in general position in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
By
[

]
there are sets
A_{k}
(

) such that for all

,

has at most one point, and such that

.
The map
