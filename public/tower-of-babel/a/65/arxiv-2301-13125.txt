Definitions And Motivation

A group
G
is said to be metacyclic if it possesses a cyclic normal subgroup
N
such that
G/N
is also cyclic. It is clear that a metacyclic group

can be written

with
H\leqslant G,

N\unlhd G
and both

and

cyclic. If

then
G
is called the split metacyclic group. It was already known to Hölder that finite metacyclic groups can be presented on two generators and three defining relations.
When
G=HN
is split, by taking generators

of
H
and
y
of

it is well-known that

has a presentation of the form

\displaystyle\langle x,y~|~x^{m}=y^{n}=1,~xyx^{-1}=y^{l}\rangle,

(1)

where the integers

and
l
satisfying
l^{m}\equiv 1
(mod
n
).
In the rest of the paper we will fix

and

as the integers of the above presentation.
The purpose of this paper is to show that the
2
-nilpotent multiplier
\mathcal{M}^{(2)}(G)
is the direct product of two cyclic groups of orders
(n,l-1,1+l+...+l^{m-1})
and
1\over n
(n,1+l+...+l^{m-1})(n,(l-1)^{2}),
where by
(r,s)
we mean the greatest common divisor of the integers

and
s
.

Given a positive integer

, recall that the

-nilpotent multiplier of a group

which is presented as the quotient of a free group

by a normal subgroup

\mathcal{M}^{(c)}(G)=(R\cap\gamma_{c+1}(F))/\gamma_{c+1}(R,F),

\gamma_{1}(R,F)=R
,
\gamma_{c+1}(R,F)=[\gamma_{c}(R,F),F]
and
\gamma_{c+1}(F)=\gamma_{c+1}(F,F).
It was shown by R. Baer
[
1
]
that these invariants are, up to group isomorphism, independent of the choice of free presentation of

. The group
\mathcal{M}(G)=\mathcal{M}^{(1)}(G)
is more known as the Schur multiplier of

.

One motivation to study the

-nilpotent multipliers is its relevance to the isologism theory of P. Hall
[
8
,
9
]
in which groups can be classified into isologism classes (see
[
5
, p. 406]
for more details). Another important reason is that, determining the structures of

-nilpotent multipliers is essential in studying generalized capability and covering groups, and would be generally useful in developing such a framework. Furthermore, although the above formula of
\mathcal{M}^{(c)}(G)
looks simple enough; it is mostly a hard but interesting problem to compute it. In particular, according to the method proposed in
[
5
]
, which is based on the notion of triple exterior product, we hope our different approach of studying 2-nilpotent multipliers can be extended to more general cases of c-nilpotent multipliers and provides some new insights.

Let’s first recall the definition of the nonabelian tensor product of two groups

, is defined as a group generated by the symbols

, where

for all

and

Since the compatible actions are satisfied when a group acts on itself by conjugation, it leads to define the nonabelian tensor square

. The nonabelian exterior square

is the quotient of

modulo the central subgroup

generated by the elements

for all

, in which its kernel is isomorphic to

[

]

3
Proof of The Main Theorem

In this section we prove the main theorem of the paper. The proof relies on the previous section together with a computation method based on the crossed pairings. There is a significant difference between the even and odd case, and w’ll treat them separately beginning with the odd case.

3.1
The n odd case

Let
G
be a finite split metacyclic group as in (1) and
n
is odd. We observe from Proposition
1.1
that in this case
G\otimes G
splits as

G\otimes G\cong\nabla(G)\times(G\wedge G)\cong C_{m}\times C_{(n,l-1)}\times C_{(n,l-1,1+l+...+l^{m-1})}\times C_{(n,1+l+...+l^{m-1})},

where
G\wedge G
is isomorphic to the subgroup of
G\otimes G
generated by
\langle x\otimes y\rangle
. This is not the case in general when
n
is even. The groups
G\wedge G
and
G
act on each other in such a way that
G\wedge G\cong\langle x\otimes y\rangle
is fixed under the action of
G
. So by applying
[
3
, Proposition 10]
one could describe
\otimes^{3}G
as follows:

\ \otimes^{3}G\cong(\nabla(G)\times(G\wedge G))\otimes G\cong(\nabla(G)\otimes G)\times(G\wedge G\otimes G).

Now since the groups
\nabla(G)
and
G
act on each other trivially, Proposition 2.4 in
[
4
]
allows us to express
\nabla(G)\otimes G
as the tensor product
\nabla(G)\otimes G^{ab}
of abelian groups. Therefore by Proposition
1.1
we conclude that

\nabla(G)\otimes G^{ab}\cong C_{m}\times C_{(n,l-1)}\times C^{2}_{(m,n,l-1)}\times C_{(n,l-1,1+l+...+l^{m-1})}\times C_{(m,n,l-1,1+l+...+l^{m-1})}.

The main task then is to determine the cyclic invariants of
G\wedge G\otimes G
. For this purpose first we expand the arbitrary element
(x\otimes y)^{i}\otimes

y^{j}x^{k}
of
G\wedge G\otimes G
by invoking (4) and (6):

\displaystyle(x\otimes y)^{i}\otimes y^{j}x^{k}

\displaystyle=((x\otimes y)^{i}\otimes y^{j})\ {{}^{y^{j}}}((x\otimes y)^{i}\otimes x^{k})

\displaystyle=(x\otimes y\otimes y)^{ij}\ {{}^{y^{j}}}(x\otimes y\otimes x)^{i.k}

\displaystyle=(x\otimes y\otimes y)^{ij}(x\otimes y\otimes x)^{i.k}.

This establishes what the generators of
G\wedge G\otimes G
are. To find the orders of these generators, and any possible relations among them, by considering our analysis of
\otimes^{3}G
in Subsection 2.1, we use crossed pairing into suitable cyclic groups.
Recall that for groups
G,H
and
L
where
G
and
H
acting upon each other compatibly and acting upon themselves by conjugation, a function
\Phi:G\times H\longrightarrow L
is called a crossed pairing if

\Phi(gg^{\prime},h)=\Phi(^{g}g^{\prime},^{g}h)\Phi(g,h)\hskip 14.22636pt\textnormal{and}\hskip 14.22636pt\Phi(g,hh^{\prime})=\Phi(g,h)\Phi(^{h}g,^{h}h^{\prime}),

for all
g,g^{\prime}\in G
,
h,h^{\prime}\in H
. Clearly any crossed pairing
\Phi:G\times H\longrightarrow L
determines a unique homomorphism
\Phi^{*}:G\otimes H\longrightarrow L
such that
\Phi^{*}(g\otimes h)=\Phi(g,h).

Now define
\Phi:(G\wedge G)\times G\rightarrow C_{(n,1+l+...+l^{m-1})}\times C_{(n,l-1,1+l+...+l^{m-1})}
by
\Phi((x\otimes y)^{i},y^{j}x^{k})=a^{i.k}\ b^{ij}
and let
((x\otimes y)^{i},y^{j}x^{k})=((x\otimes y)^{i^{\prime}},y^{j^{\prime}}x^{k^{\prime}})
. Then with setting
r=(n,1+l+...+l^{m-1})
, it follows that
i^{\prime}=i+ur,j^{\prime}=j+vn
, and
k^{\prime}=k+wm,
for some integers
u,v,w
. So
a^{i^{\prime}k^{\prime}}b^{i^{\prime}j^{\prime}}=a^{i.k}\ b^{ij}
, which implies that
\Phi
is well-defined.
We prove that
\Phi
is a crossed pairing. The following verifies the first rule for a crossed pairing:

\displaystyle\Phi(^{(x\otimes y)^{i}}(x\otimes y)^{i^{\prime}},\ ^{(x\otimes y)^{i}}y^{j}x^{k})\Phi((x\otimes y)^{i},\ y^{j}x^{k})

\displaystyle=\Phi((x\otimes y)^{i^{\prime}},\ y^{j+i(l-1)(1-l^{k})}x^{k})\Phi((x\otimes y)^{i},\ y^{j}x^{k})

\displaystyle=a^{i^{\prime}.k}b^{i^{\prime}j+i^{\prime}i(l-1)(1-l^{k})}a^{i.k}\ b^{ij}

\displaystyle=a^{(i+i^{\prime}).k}\ b^{(i+i^{\prime})j}

\displaystyle=\Phi((x\otimes y)^{i}(x\otimes y)^{i^{\prime}}\otimes y^{j}x^{k}).

The other rule follows by
[
3
, (22), (5.2), (5.3)]
:

\displaystyle\Phi((x\otimes y)^{i},y^{j}x^{k})\Phi(^{y^{j}x^{k}}(x\otimes y)^{i},^{y^{j}x^{k}}y^{j^{\prime}}x^{k^{\prime}})

\displaystyle=\Phi((x\otimes y)^{i},\ y^{j}x^{k})\Phi((x\otimes y)^{il^{k}},y^{j^{\prime}l^{k}+j(1-l^{k^{\prime}})}x^{k^{\prime}})

\displaystyle=a^{i.k}b^{ij}a^{il^{k}.k^{\prime}}\ b^{il^{k}(j^{\prime}l^{k}+j(1-l^{k^{\prime}}))}

\displaystyle=a^{i(1+l+...+l^{k-1})+i(l^{k}+l^{k+1}+...+l^{k+k^{\prime}-1})}\ b^{i(j+j^{\prime}l^{k})}

\displaystyle=a^{i.(k+k^{\prime})}\ b^{i(j+j^{\prime}l^{k})}

\displaystyle=\Phi((x\otimes y)^{i},\ y^{j+j^{\prime}l^{k}}x^{k+k^{\prime}})

\displaystyle=\Phi((x\otimes y)^{i},\ y^{j}x^{k}y^{j^{\prime}}x^{k^{\prime}}).

Thus
\Phi
induces a homomorphism
{\Phi^{\ast}}:G\wedge G\otimes G\rightarrow C_{(n,1+l+...+l^{m-1})}

\times C_{(n,l-1,1+l+...+l^{m-1})},
which shows that the generators
x\otimes y\otimes x
and
x\otimes y\otimes y
are independent and have the orders divided by
(n,1+l+...+l^{m-1})
and
(n,l-1,1+l+...+l^{m-1})
, respectively. On the other hand we simply note by Proposition
1.1
and (6) that
(x\otimes y\otimes x)^{(n,1+l+...+l^{m-1})}=1
. Likewise it follows from Proposition
1.1
, (2) and (3) that
(x\otimes y\otimes y)^{(n,l-1,1+l+...+l^{m-1})}=1
. Hence
|x\otimes y\otimes x|=(n,1+l+...+l^{m-1})
and
|x\otimes y\otimes y|=(n,l-1,1+l+...+l^{m-1})
; from which we conclude that

G\wedge G\otimes G\cong C_{(n,1+l+...+l^{m-1})}\times C_{(n,l-1,1+l+...+l^{m-1})},

as desired.
Consequently as
x\otimes y\otimes[x,y]=x\otimes y\otimes y^{l-1}=(x\otimes y\otimes y)^{l-1}=1
by (2) and (3), it follows by definition that
\wedge^{3}G\cong\ G\wedge G\otimes G.

Now we are ready to complete the proof of the main theorem in odd case by computing
\mathcal{M}^{(2)}(G)
. Our method relies on a tensor product approach due to Burns and Ellis
[
5
]
. Let
\gamma_{3}^{\sharp}(G)
be the quotient group
\wedge^{3}G/\tau(G)
where
\tau(G)
is the normal subgroup of
\wedge^{3}G
generated by the elements

\langle a,b,c\rangle=((a\wedge b)\wedge{{}^{b}}c)((b\wedge c)\wedge{{}^{c}}a)((c\wedge a)\wedge{{}^{a}}b),

for all
a,b,c\in G
. By the Hall–Witt commutator identity, the homomorphism
[~,~,~]:\wedge^{3}G\longrightarrow G
induces a homomorphism
[~,~,~]:\gamma_{3}^{\sharp}(G)\longrightarrow G
. It is well-known
[
5
, Theorem 2.9]
that the surjection
\ker([~,~,~]:\wedge^{3}G\longrightarrow G)\twoheadrightarrow\mathcal{M}^{(2)}(G)
given in
[
5
, Theorem 2.6]
gives rise to the
natural isomorphism

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}^{(2)}(G)\cong\ker([~,~,~]:\gamma_{3}^{\sharp}(G)\longrightarrow G).

(18)

So in order to describe
\mathcal{M}^{(2)}(G),
first, it is required to evaluate the subgroup
\tau(G)
of
\wedge^{3}G.
By invoking (2) and (4) together with the initial relations of

we have

\displaystyle\langle x,y,y\rangle

\displaystyle=(x\wedge y\wedge{{}^{y}y)}(y\wedge y\wedge{{}^{y}x)}(y\wedge x\wedge{{}^{x}y)}

\displaystyle=(x\wedge y\wedge y)(y\wedge x\wedge y^{l})

\displaystyle=(x\wedge y\wedge y)((x\wedge y)^{-1}\wedge y)^{l}

\displaystyle=(x\wedge y\wedge y)(x\wedge y\wedge y)^{-l}

\displaystyle=(x\wedge y\wedge y)^{1-l}=1.

Analogously (2), (3) and (4) imply that

\displaystyle\langle x,y,x\rangle

\displaystyle=(x\wedge y\wedge{{}^{y}x)}(y\wedge x\wedge{{}^{x}x)}(x\wedge x\wedge{{}^{x}y)}

\displaystyle=(x\wedge y\wedge y^{1-l}x)((x\wedge y)^{-1}\wedge x)

\displaystyle=(x\wedge y\wedge y^{1-l})\ {{}^{y^{1-l}}(x\wedge y\wedge x)(x\wedge y\wedge x)^{-1}}

\displaystyle=(x\wedge y\wedge y)^{1-l}=1.

Hence
\tau(G)
is trivial and consequently
\gamma_{3}^{\sharp}(G)

\cong\wedge^{3}G.
As
\gamma_{3}(G)
=
\langle y^{(l-1)^{2}}\rangle,
then it readily follows by (18) that

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}^{(2)}(G)\cong C_{(n,l-1,1+l+...+l^{m-1})}\times C_{(n,1+l+...+l^{m-1})(n,(l-1)^{2})/n}.
