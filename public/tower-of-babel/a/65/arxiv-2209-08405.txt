A Steklov-spectral approach for solutions of Dirichlet and Robin boundary value problems
Kthim Imeri, Nilima Nigam

Abstract
In this paper we revisit an approach pioneered by Auchmuty [ 5 ] to approximate solutions of the Laplace- Robin boundary value problem. We demonstrate the efficacy of this approach on a large class of non-tensorial domains, in contrast with other spectral approaches for such problems. We establish a spectral approximation theorem showing an exponential fast numerical evaluation with regards to the number of Steklov eigenfunctions used, for smooth domains and smooth boundary data. A polynomial fast numerical evaluation is observed for either non-smooth domains or non-smooth boundary data. We additionally prove a new result on the regularity of the Steklov eigenfunctions, depending on the regularity of the domain boundary. We describe three numerical methods to compute Steklov eigenfunctions.

1
Introduction

Spectral methods provide a fast and highly accurate approximation strategy for the solution of partial differential equations (PDE), and rely on the use of an approximation basis consisting of eigenfunctions of the PDE operator under consideration. These eigenfunctions are
typically
the Dirichlet or Neumann eigenfunctions of the PDE operator, and provide excellent approximations for homogenous boundary value problems. Non-homogenous Dirichlet and Neumann boundary value problems necessitate the use of additional techniques.

However, the use of spectral methods to solve boundary value problems for the Laplacian with
inhomogenous Robin data
has thus far been restricted to tensorial geometries, see e.g.
[
24
]
. Robin boundary value problems provide challenges for approximation via the ’standard’ basis functions, since these are not eigenfunctions of the associated solution operator.

An ingenous idea was first proposed by
[
5
]
, who demonstrated the theoretical foundations for spectral approximation via
Steklov eigenfunctions
of elliptic operators. This idea was then explored in
[
9
]
to approximate the solutions of the Laplacian on a domain
\Omega
with Dirichlet, Neumann and Robin boundary conditions. The Steklov eigenfunctions are denoted in this work as
(s_{i})_{i=1}^{\infty}
, which are harmonic functions, satisfying the Steklov boundary condition, that is

\displaystyle\partial_{\nu}s_{i}=\lambda s_{i}\quad\text{on }\partial\Omega\,,

where
\lambda\geq 0
and
\partial_{\nu}
is the normal derivative on the boundary. In
[
5
]
it is shown that there are countable many such functions to countable many
\lambda
, which satisfy the above boundary condition, and the pair
(s_{i},\lambda_{i})
is called the
i
-th Steklov eigenfunction and Steklov eigenvalue.

The results in
[
5
]
moreover show that the eigenfunctions build a maximal orthonormal set in
L^{2}(\partial\Omega)
for
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
n\geq 2
, and that we can build an isometric isomorphism between
H^{1/2}(\partial\Omega)
and a subspace of harmonic function in
H^{1}(\Omega)
. An exceptional behaviour is observed for the Steklov eigenvalue in regards to the regularity of the boundary. For any Lipschitz boundary the eigenvalues ascend linearly, then for smooth boundaries the eigenvalues ascend linearly in pairs with an exponential decrease in the distance within a pair, and for non-smooth boundaries this distance might not decrease exponentially any further. Through
[
1
]
we have a general formula for the eigenvalues in any Lipschitz domain, and on that foundation the authors in
[
17
]
discovered a more accurate formula for curvilinear polygons.
Similarly, in Proposition
2.2
we will show that the regularity of Steklov eigenfunctions depend on the regularity of the boundary. Steklov eigenpairs are also useful in more general partial differential equations, the authors in
[
6
]
considered Schroedinger-type operators and the authors in
[
3
]
consider the Helmholtz equation with mixed boundary boundary conditions. The survey
[
14
]
states more such applications as well as more results on the Steklov problem.

This paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
we first define the Steklov problem in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
and show asymptotics for the eigenvalues, regarding the smoothness of the boundary, and the orthonormality of the eigenfunctions. Subsequently, we consider two higher order layer potentials, that is the single layer potential and Neumann-Poincaré operator, and their mapping properties in
H^{p}
. Need those for the numerics at the end and the next result, Proposition
2.2
, which shows higher regularity properties for
s_{i}
. Thereafter, we examine properties on the Dirichlet to Neumann operator, whose eigenvalues coincide with the Steklov eigenvalue. Then we estimate the norm of the Dirichlet to Neumann operator, in Lemma
2.4
, and with that prove that every smooth function on the boundary with inform
L^{2}
-bounded derivatives is a finite linear combination of Steklov eigenfunctions, in Proposition
2.5
. We conclude Section
2
with the formulation of the series expansion for the solution to the Laplace problem in terms the Steklov eigenpairs, theoretical as well as the numerical approximation.

In Section
3
we show the main results, which concerns the asymptotic approximation of the series expansion in terms if the amount of eigenpairs used, as well as the asymptotics in terms of the numerical approximation of the Steklov eigenfunctions. For the Dirichlet boundary problem this is stated in Theorem
3.1
, for the Robin boundary problem, the asymptotics are given in Corollary
3.2
. Thereafter we proof the theorem and the corollary.

In Section
4
we first consider three methods to numerically compute the eigenpairs. The first method is based on the results of
[
2
]
, which provides the eigenvalues through a conformal map. We further develop it to also obtain the eigenfunctions. The second method relies on the weak form of the Steklov problem and uses a particular orthonormal basis to obtain a generalized eigenvalue problem from which we obtain the eigenpair. The third method uses the collocation method elaborated in
[
16
, Chapter 13]
. Method three is then used in the upcoming subsection, which is about numerical tests to validate Theorem
3.1
and Corollary
3.2
. We apply several different domains and several boundary data with various regularity and visually present the results obtained in MATLAB
[
19
]
.

2
Preliminaries

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
be a simply connected, bounded and open domain with a Lipschitz boundary. More restrictive boundary conditions may be given later on. Then we define
u
to be the solution to the Helmholtz equation, that is

\displaystyle\mathop{}\!\mathbin{\bigtriangleup}u(x)=0\quad\text{in }\Omega\,,

(2.1)

with the 3 separate boundary conditions

\displaystyle u(y)

\displaystyle=g(y)\,,

(2.2)

\displaystyle\partial_{\nu_{y}}u(y)

\displaystyle=g(y)\,,

(2.3)

\displaystyle\partial_{\nu_{y}}u(y)+b\,u(y)

\displaystyle=g(y)\,,

(2.4)

where
g\in L^{2}(\partial\Omega)
,
b>0
, and
\partial_{\nu_{y}}
denotes the outside normal derivative on
\partial\Omega
at
y
. The first condition is known as the Dirichlet condition, the second condition is known as the Neumann condition and third one is known as the Robin boundary condition.

We define the Steklov eigenvalues
\lambda_{0},\lambda_{1},\ldots
and respective Steklov eigenfunction
s_{0},s_{1},\ldots
as the solution to

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{aligned} \mathop{}\!\mathbin{\bigtriangleup}\,s_{j}(x)&=0\quad&&\text{in }\Omega\,,\\
\partial_{\nu_{y}}s_{j}(y)&=\lambda_{j}\,s_{j}(y)\quad&&\text{on }\partial\Omega\,,\end{aligned}\right.

(2.5)

for
j\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
. Equation (
2.5
) has a non-trivial solution
s_{i}\in H^{1}(\Omega)
to a corresponding eigenvalue
\lambda_{i}
. From
[
11
, Corollary 4.3]
we have that
s_{i}\in C^{\infty}(\Omega)\cap C^{0}(\overline{\Omega})
. From
[
5
, Section 6 and 7]
we have that all
\lambda_{i}
have finite multiplicity. We also know that
\lambda_{0}=0
and that
s_{0}
is constant. For smooth enough boundaries the authors in
[
13
]
show that

\displaystyle\lambda_{2i}=\lambda_{2i-1}+\mathcal{O}(i^{-\infty})=\frac{2\pi}{|\partial\Omega|}\,i+\mathcal{O}(i^{-\infty})\,,

(2.6)

for
i\geq 1
, where
\mathcal{O}(i^{-\infty})
decays faster than any power of
i^{-1}
for
i\rightarrow\infty
, where
|\partial\Omega|
denotes the length of the boundary. From
[
1
]
we further know that for piecewise
C^{1}
boundaries we have that

\displaystyle\lambda_{i}=\Big(\frac{\pi}{|\partial\Omega|}+o(1)\Big)\,i\,.

(2.7)

For curvilinear domains the authors in
[
17
]
are able to construct values closer to the eigenvalues than what is given with Equation (
2.7
).

We define the inner-product
\langle\cdot\,,\cdot\rangle_{\partial}
through

\displaystyle\langle v,w\rangle_{\partial}\coloneqq\int_{\Omega}\nabla v\cdot\nabla w\;+\,\int_{\partial\Omega}v\,w\,,

for
v,w\in H^{1}(\Omega)
, where we use the trace theorem. Then we have that the norm
\left\lVert v\right\rVert_{\partial}\coloneqq\sqrt{\langle v,v\rangle_{\partial}}
is equivalent to the
H^{1}
norm, see
[
5
, Corollary 6.2]
. The authors showed this by stating that

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}(\nabla v)^{2}\;+\,\int_{\partial\Omega}v^{2}\geq\alpha_{0}\int_{\Omega}v^{2}\,,

for some
\alpha_{0}>0
, thus
||v_{m}||^{2}_{H^{1}(\Omega)}\leq\frac{1}{\alpha_{0}}||v||^{2}_{\partial}+\int_{\Omega}(\nabla v)^{2}\leq C_{H}||v||^{2}_{\partial}
. They proved it using a proof by contradiction and applying a minimizing sequence
(v_{m})_{m\in\mathbb{N}}
, with
||v_{m}||_{L^{2}(\Omega)}=1
, which has a subsequence converging to a limit functions
\hat{v}
with
||\hat{v}||_{\partial}=0
. But if
||\hat{v}||_{\partial}=0
then
\hat{v}
is constant, because
\int_{\Omega}(\nabla\hat{v})^{2}=0
and
\int_{\partial\Omega}\hat{v}^{2}=0
, thus
||\hat{v}_{m}||_{L^{2}(\Omega)}=1
, which is a contradiction.

We then confine Steklov eigenfunctions to satisfy the normalisation

\displaystyle\left\lVert s_{i}\right\rVert_{\partial}=1\,,

this determines Steklov eigenfunction to simple Steklov eigenvalues up to a sign. Using the Gram-Schmidt orthogonalisation with the inner-product
\langle\cdot\,,\cdot\rangle_{\partial}
on Steklov eigenfunctions to eigenvalues with non simple multiplicity, we can set all Steklov eigenfunctions to be orthonormal to each other within the inner-product
\langle\cdot\,,\cdot\rangle_{\partial}
, because

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\nabla s_{i}\cdot\nabla s_{j}

\displaystyle=\lambda_{j}\int_{\partial\Omega}s_{i}\,s_{j}=\lambda_{i}\int_{\partial\Omega}s_{i}\,s_{j}=0\,,\quad

\displaystyle\text{for }i\neq j\,,

due to Green’s identity. We also have that

\displaystyle\langle s_{i}\,,s_{i}\rangle_{\partial}

\displaystyle=(1+\lambda_{i})\left\lVert s_{i}\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega)}^{2}=1\,,\quad

\displaystyle\text{for }i\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\,,

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\nabla s_{i}\cdot\nabla s_{i}

\displaystyle=\lambda_{i}\int_{\partial\Omega}(s_{i})^{2}=\frac{\lambda_{i}}{1+\lambda_{i}}\,,\quad

\displaystyle\text{for }i\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\,,

\displaystyle s_{0}

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{1}{\sqrt{|\partial\Omega|}}\,.

2.1
Higher Order Layer Potentials

Given a Lipschitz boundary
\partial\Omega
, we define the single layer potential
\mathcal{S}:H^{-1/2}(\partial\Omega)\rightarrow H^{1/2}(\partial\Omega)
and the Neumann-Poincaré operator
\mathcal{K}^{\ast}:H^{-1/2}(\partial\Omega)\rightarrow H^{-1/2}(\partial\Omega)
through

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}[\phi](\tau)

\displaystyle=\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\frac{1}{2\pi}\log{|x(\tau)-x(t)|}\;\phi(t)\,|T(t)|\,\mathrm{d}t\,,

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}^{\ast}[\phi](\tau)

\displaystyle=\;\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\raisebox{-2.71246pt}{\tiny{p.\,v.\;}}$}}\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\frac{1}{2\pi}\frac{\nu_{x(\tau)}\cdot(x(\tau)-x(t))}{|x(\tau)-x(t)|^{2}}\phi(t)\,|T(t)|\,\mathrm{d}t\,,

where
x(t)\in\partial\Omega
parametrizes the boundary of
\Omega
with
|T(t)|\coloneqq|\tfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}{x}(t)|>0
, for all
t\in(-\pi,\pi]
, and where the p.v. denotes a principle value integral. We refer to
[
23
,
18
]
for details.

The authors in
[
21
, Theorem 1.1]
showed that the operators
-\tfrac{1}{2}\mathrm{I}+\mathcal{K}^{\ast}:H^{l}(\partial\Omega)\rightarrow H^{l}(\partial\Omega)
and
\mathcal{S}:H^{l-1}(\partial\Omega)\rightarrow H^{l}(\partial\Omega)
are continuous and invertible excluding an one dimensional subspace, provided
2(l-1)>n-1
, for
l>1
, where
n
denotes the dimension of space, that is
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, with
n\geq 3
, and provided
\partial\Omega\in H^{l}_{\text{loc}}
.
For
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, they state that: ”The changes required in formulations,
n>2
, are the same as in the logarithm potential theory for contours. Our proofs, given for
n>2
, apply to the two dimensional case after minor changes.” (
[
21
, pp. 100]
). Here we use the logarithm potential theory described in
[
23
, Section 4.]
.

[
21
, Theorem 1.1]
also provides results on the the operators
\mathcal{S}
and
\tfrac{1}{2}\mathrm{I}+\mathcal{K}^{\ast}
for values
1<l\leq(n-1)/2+1
, but with an additional condition on the boundary. That condition is satisfied with the assumption, that
\partial\Omega\in H^{l+1/2}_{\text{loc}}
, for all
l>1
. Together with the result in
[
23
, Theorem 4.11]
, we then have the following lemma.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
f_{0}
be the unique function satisfying
(-\tfrac{1}{2}\mathrm{I}+\mathcal{K}^{\ast})f_{0}=0
and
\int_{\partial\Omega}f_{0}=1
. Let
\langle f_{0}\rangle
be the space spanned by
f_{0}
and let
\langle 1\rangle
be the space spanned by constant functions.

Assume
\partial\Omega\in H^{p+1/2}_{\text{loc}}
,
p\in\mathbb{N}
with
p\geq 1
, then the following statements hold:

(1)

\mathcal{S}
maps
H^{p-1}(\partial\Omega)\ominus\langle f_{0}\rangle
isomorphically onto
H^{p}(\partial\Omega)\ominus\langle 1\rangle
, and
\mathcal{S}[f_{0}]
is a constant function.

(2)

(-\tfrac{1}{2}\mathrm{I}+\mathcal{K}^{\ast})
is an isomorphism in
H^{p}(\partial\Omega)\ominus\langle 1\rangle
.

2.2
Higher Order Steklov Eigenfunctions

Proposition 2.2
.

Assume
\partial\Omega\in H^{p+1/2}_{\text{loc}}
,
p\in\mathbb{N}
with
p\geq 2
, then

\displaystyle s_{i}\in H^{p}(\partial\Omega)\,,\quad\forall\,i\in\mathbb{N}\,.

Furthermore, given that
s_{i}=\mathcal{S}[\phi_{i}]
, then
\phi_{i}\in H^{p}(\partial\Omega)
.

Proof
.

From
[
23
, Theorem 4.1]
we can readily infer that there exists a
\phi_{i}\in L^{2}(\partial\Omega)
such that
s_{i}=\mathcal{S}[\phi_{i}]
, since
\int_{\partial\Omega}s_{i}=0
, for all
i\geq 1
, for Lipschitz domains. Using the boundary condition for
s_{i}
and limit results for the single layer potential, especially that
\lim_{x\rightarrow\partial\Omega}\partial_{\nu}\mathcal{S}[\phi](x)=(-\tfrac{1}{2}\mathrm{I}+\mathcal{K}^{\ast})[\phi](x)
, we obtain that

\displaystyle(-\tfrac{1}{2}\mathrm{I}+\mathcal{K}^{\ast})[\phi_{i}]=\lambda_{i}\,\mathcal{S}[\phi_{i}]\,,

that is
-\tfrac{1}{2}\phi_{i}=\lambda_{i}\,\mathcal{S}[\phi_{i}]-\mathcal{K}^{\ast}[\phi_{i}]
. We can infer that the right-hand side is in
H^{1}(\partial\Omega)
, because of Lemma
2.1
and because for
C^{2}
domains the integration kernel of
\mathcal{K}^{\ast}
is smooth, thus
\phi_{i}\in H^{1}(\partial\Omega)
. Reapplying this argument, we see that the regularity of
\phi_{i}
is as high as Lemma
2.1
allows, and that is
\phi_{i}\in H^{p}(\partial\Omega)
and thus
s_{i}\in H^{p}(\partial\Omega)
.

\blacksquare

2.3
Dirichlet to Neumann operator

Let
\Omega
be a Lipschitz domain, then we define the Dirichlet to Neumann map
\mathcal{DN}:H^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}(\partial\Omega)\rightarrow H^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}(\partial\Omega)
such that
\mathcal{DN}[g]=\partial_{\nu}u
where
u
is the solution to the homogeneous Dirichlet problem, that is

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{aligned} \mathop{}\!\mathbin{\bigtriangleup}\,u(x)&=0\quad&&\text{in }\Omega\,,\\
u(y)&=g(y)\quad&&\text{on }\partial\Omega\,.\end{aligned}\right.

(2.8)

According to
[
10
]
,
\mathcal{DN}
is well-defined. From
[
22
, Comment after Proposition 1.8]
, for
\partial\Omega\in C^{\infty}
and
p\geq 1
, we have that the solution
u
to Equation (
2.8
) is in
H^{p+1}(\Omega)
in case
g\in H^{p+\tfrac{1}{2}}(\partial\Omega)
. Then with the trace theorem
[
12
]
we have that
\nabla u|_{\partial\Omega}\in H^{p-\tfrac{1}{2}}(\partial\Omega)\subset C^{p-1}(\partial\Omega)
using the Sobolev embedding and that
\partial\Omega
is a one dimensional manifold. Hence we can infer that
\mathcal{DN}
maps a function in
H^{p+\tfrac{1}{2}}(\partial\Omega)
to a function in
H^{p-\tfrac{1}{2}}(\partial\Omega)
.

Considering that we can reformulate the Dirichlet to Neumann operator through

\displaystyle\mathcal{DN}=(-\tfrac{1}{2}\mathrm{I}+\mathcal{K}^{\ast})\,\mathcal{S}^{-1}\,,

where we use the representation
u=\mathcal{S}[\phi]
, and several layer potential results
[
4
]
, Lemma
2.1
, especially that
\mathcal{S}[f_{0}]
is a constant function and
(-\tfrac{1}{2}\mathrm{I}+\mathcal{K}^{\ast})[f_{0}]=0
, we see that
\mathcal{DN}:H^{l}(\partial\Omega)\rightarrow H^{l-1}(\partial\Omega)
is well defined for
\partial\Omega\in H^{l+1/2}_{\text{loc}}
with
l\geq 1
or
l=1/2
.

Then we define

\displaystyle\mathcal{DN}^{p}[g]=\mathcal{DN}[\mathcal{DN}[\ldots\mathcal{DN}[g]\ldots]]\,,

p
times, where
p\in\mathbb{N}
.

Lemma 2.3
.

Assume
\partial\Omega\in H^{p+1/2}_{\text{loc}}
,
p\in\mathbb{N}
with
p\geq 1
, then for all
g\in H^{p}(\partial\Omega)
and all
i\in\mathbb{N}
we have that

\displaystyle\int_{\partial\Omega}g\,s_{i}\,\leq\,\left\lVert\mathcal{DN}^{p}[g]\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega)}\frac{1}{\lambda_{i}^{p}(1+\lambda_{i})^{1/2}}\,.

Proof
.

With Green’s identity we readily see that

\displaystyle\int_{\partial\Omega}g\,s_{i}=\int_{\partial\Omega}g\,\frac{1}{\lambda_{i}}\partial_{\nu}s_{i}=\int_{\partial\Omega}\partial_{\nu}u\,\frac{1}{\lambda_{i}}s_{i}=\frac{1}{\lambda_{i}}\int_{\partial\Omega}\mathcal{DN}^{1}[g]\,s_{i}\,.

Repeat the process
p
times and then we have that

\displaystyle\int_{\partial\Omega}g\,s_{i}=\frac{1}{\lambda_{i}^{p}}\int_{\partial\Omega}\mathcal{DN}^{p}[g]\,s_{i}\,.

Using the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality and that
\left\lVert s_{i}\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega)}=(1+\lambda_{i})^{-1/2}
, we can infer Lemma
2.3
.

\blacksquare

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
\partial\Omega\in H^{p+1/2}_{\text{loc}}
, let
g\in H^{p}(\partial\Omega)
be a function on the boundary and let
p\geq 1
be an integer. Then

\displaystyle\left\lVert\mathcal{DN}^{p}[g]\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega)}\leq\left\lVert g^{(p)}\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega)}\Big(\max_{z\in\partial\mathbb{D}}|f^{\prime}(z)|^{-1}\Big)^{p}\,,

where
\mathbb{D}
denotes the open unit disk, where
f:\mathbb{D}\rightarrow\Omega\subset\mathbb{C}
is a conformal map, and where
g^{(p)}
the
p
-th weak derivative of
g
.

Proof
.

First, let us assume that
\Omega=\mathbb{D}
. Then the solution
u:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
to the Laplace problem with boundary
u\!\mid_{\partial\Omega}=g
can be described as

\displaystyle u(x)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}g_{n}\,r^{n}\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\,n\,\theta}\,,

where
x\in\Omega
and
r,\theta
denotes its radial and angular part, and where
(g_{n})_{n=0}^{\infty}\subset\mathbb{C}
. By the chain rule, we have that
\partial_{\nu}=\partial_{r}
and that the tangential derivative is given through
\tfrac{1}{r}\partial_{\theta}
, which in turn is equal to
\tfrac{1}{r}g^{\prime}
. Then we can infer that

\displaystyle\mathcal{DN}^{p}[g]

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=p}^{\infty}g_{n}\,\frac{n!}{(n-p)!}\,r^{n-p}\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\,n\,\theta}\,.

Using Parseval’s theorem, we can say that

\displaystyle\left\lVert\mathcal{DN}^{p}[g]\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega)}^{2}=\sum_{n=p}^{\infty}|g_{n}|^{2}\,\frac{n!^{2}}{(n-p)!^{2}}\leq\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}|g_{n}|^{2}\,(n^{p})^{2}=\left\lVert\partial_{\theta}^{p}u\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega)}^{2}\,,

and thus

\displaystyle\left\lVert\mathcal{DN}^{p}[g]\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega)}\leq\left\lVert g^{p}\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega)}\,.

To generalize, we consider the conformal map
f:\mathbb{D}\rightarrow\Omega\subset\mathbb{C}
. Let
u:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be the solution the Laplace equation with boundary
u\!\mid_{\partial\Omega}=g
. We define
w\coloneqq u\circ f:\mathbb{D}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
. From complex analysis we know that
w
is a solution to the Laplace equation on
\mathbb{D}
.

Let
z\in\partial\mathbb{D}
and
\omega\in\partial\Omega
, such that
f(z)=\omega
, then
w\!\mid_{\partial\mathbb{D}}=g\circ f
. Using the chain rule and that
\nu_{z}=z
for
z\in\partial\mathbb{D}
, we obtain that
\nu_{\omega}=\frac{f^{\prime}(z)}{|f^{\prime}(z)|}z=\frac{f^{\prime}(z)}{|f^{\prime}(z)|}\nu_{z}
. Using matrix notation to describe the complex multiplication, we can infer that

\displaystyle\nu_{z}\cdot\nabla w(z)

\displaystyle=|{f^{\prime}(z)}|\begin{bmatrix}\partial_{1}f_{1}&\partial_{2}f_{1}\\
\partial_{1}f_{2}&\partial_{2}f_{2}\end{bmatrix}^{-1}\begin{bmatrix}(\nu_{\omega})_{1}\\
(\nu_{\omega})_{2}\end{bmatrix}\;\cdot\;\begin{bmatrix}\partial_{1}f_{1}&\partial_{2}f_{1}\\
\partial_{1}f_{2}&\partial_{2}f_{2}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\partial_{1}u\\
\partial_{2}u\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=|{f^{\prime}(z)}|\,\nu_{\omega}\cdot\nabla u(\omega)\,.

From the unit disk case, we can see that

\displaystyle\left\lVert\mathcal{DN}[g]\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega)}

\displaystyle=\left\lVert\nu_{\omega}\cdot\nabla u\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega)}=\left\lVert|f^{\prime}|^{-1}\,\nu_{z}\cdot\nabla w\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\partial\mathbb{D})}

\displaystyle\leq\max_{z\in\partial\mathbb{D}}|f^{\prime}(z)|^{-1}\left\lVert\nu_{z}\cdot\nabla w\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\partial\mathbb{D})}

\displaystyle\leq\max_{z\in\partial\mathbb{D}}|f^{\prime}(z)|^{-1}\left\lVert g\circ f\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\partial\mathbb{D})}

Applying the equation
p
-times, we can conclude that

\displaystyle\left\lVert\mathcal{DN}^{p}[g]\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega)}\leq\left\lVert g^{(p)}\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega)}\Big(\max_{z\in\partial\mathbb{D}}|f^{\prime}(z)|^{-1}\Big)^{p}\,.

\blacksquare

Proposition 2.5
.

Let
\Omega\in C^{\infty}
, and let
g\in C^{\infty}(\partial\Omega)
. Assume there exists a conformal map
f:\mathbb{D}\rightarrow\Omega
, such that
\min|f^{\prime}|\geq 1
. If there exists a constant
C_{g}>0
such that

\displaystyle\sup_{p\in\mathbb{N}}\left\lVert g^{(p)}\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega)}\leq C_{g}\,,

then there exists an integer
\kappa>0
and real constants
(g_{i})_{i=0}^{\kappa}
such that

\displaystyle g=\sum_{i=0}^{\kappa-1}g_{i}s_{i}\,,

and
\kappa
denotes the first Steklov eigenvalue
\lambda_{i}
, which is bigger than
1
.

This proposition states that any smooth function
g
with uniformly
L^{2}
-norm bounded derivatives is a finite linear combination of Steklov functions.

Proof
.

Lemma
2.3
states that

\displaystyle\int_{\partial\Omega}g\,s_{i}\,\leq\,\frac{\left\lVert\mathcal{DN}^{p}[g]\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega)}}{\lambda_{i}^{p}(1+\lambda_{i})^{1/2}}\,.

With Lemma
2.4
we then obtain that

\displaystyle\int_{\partial\Omega}g\,s_{i}\,\leq\,\frac{C_{g}\Big(\max_{z\in\partial\mathbb{D}}|f^{\prime}(z)|^{-1}\Big)^{p}}{\lambda_{i}^{p}(1+\lambda_{i})^{1/2}}\leq\frac{C_{g}\,1}{\lambda_{i}^{p}(1+\lambda_{i})^{1/2}}\,.

Thus, if
\lambda_{i}>1
then
\big|\int_{\partial\Omega}g\,s_{i}\big|\leq 0
, if we let
p\rightarrow\infty
. From Section
2.4
, and the fact that
\lambda_{i}
increase linearly, there exists a constant
\kappa
, which denotes the first
\lambda_{i}>1
, and which satisfies the Proposition.

\blacksquare

For the special case
\Omega=\mathbb{D}
, we have the following corollary.

Corollary 2.6
.

Given a smooth,
2\pi
-periodic real function
g
, if there exists a constant
C>0
such that

\displaystyle\sup_{p\in\mathbb{N}}\left\lVert g^{(p)}\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega)}\leq C\,,

then there exist three constants
a,b,c\in\mathbb{R}
such that

\displaystyle g(t)=a+b\,\cos(t)+c\,\sin(t)\,.

2.4
Theoretical PDE Solution in Steklov Series

Let
E_{H}:L^{2}(\partial\Omega)\rightarrow L^{2}(\Omega)
be the harmonic extension operator

\displaystyle E_{H}[g](x)\coloneqq\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}(1+\lambda_{i})\;s_{i}(x)\int_{\partial\Omega}g\,s_{i}\,.

We define

\displaystyle g_{i}\coloneqq(1+\lambda_{i})\int_{\partial\Omega}g\,s_{i}\,,

for
j\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
.
Then we define the truncated harmonic extension operator
E_{H}^{(M)}:L^{2}(\partial\Omega)\rightarrow L^{2}(\Omega)
to be

\displaystyle E_{H}^{(M)}[g](x)\coloneqq\sum_{i=0}^{M}g_{i}\,s_{i}(x)\,.

From
[
7
, Theorem 6.2]
we have that for Lipschitz domains that
E_{H}[g]
is an isometric isomorphism of
H^{1/2}(\partial\Omega)
and the space of solutions to the Laplace equation in
\Omega
equipped with a particular inner product. From
[
8
]
we have that

\displaystyle\left\lVert E_{H}[g]\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq C\,\left\lVert g\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega)}\,,

for all
g\in L^{2}(\partial\Omega)
for smooth boundaries. More general boundary regularity conditions are given in
[
8
]
.

Numerical PDE Solution

We define
\tilde{u}_{M,N}^{(\mathrm{D})}

follows. We first approximate the solution
u
with boundary data

and then numerically compute the eigenfunctions
s_{i}
in

through the single layer potential, that is

\displaystyle s_{i}(x)=\mathcal{S}[\phi_{i}](x)\,,

(2.9)

for

\displaystyle\phi_{i}(t)=\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}c_{n}^{(i)}\exp(\mathrm{i}\,nt)\,.

(2.10)

Applying the Steklov boundary condition
\partial_{\nu}s_{i}=\lambda_{i}s_{i}
to the single layer description for

\displaystyle(-\tfrac{1}{2}\mathrm{I}+\mathcal{K}^{\ast})[\phi_{i}]=\lambda_{i}\,\mathcal{S}[\phi_{i}]\,.

Through the condition in Equation (

We define

\displaystyle\tilde{u}_{M,N}^{(\mathrm{D})}=\sum_{i=0}^{M}\tilde{g}_{i}^{(\mathrm{D})}\tilde{s_{i}}\quad\text{with}\quad\tilde{g}_{i}^{(\mathrm{D})}=(1+\lambda_{i})\int_{\partial\Omega}g^{(\mathrm{D})}\,\tilde{s_{i}}\,.

For the Robin boundary condition we define
\tilde{u}_{M,N}^{(\mathrm{R})}

) with Robin boundary data

Hence we define that

\displaystyle\tilde{u}_{M,N}^{(\mathrm{R})}=\sum_{i=0}^{M}\frac{\tilde{g}_{i}^{(\mathrm{R})}}{\lambda_{i}+b}\,\tilde{s_{i}}\quad\text{with}\quad\tilde{g}_{i}^{(\mathrm{R})}=(1+\lambda_{i})\int_{\partial\Omega}g^{(\mathrm{R})}\,\tilde{s_{i}}\,.

4
Numerical Implementation and Tests

4.1
Numerical Implementation

We give three methods to numerically compute accurate 2-dimensional solutions to the Steklov eigenvalue problem.

First method (Conformal Method)

The first method relies on the implementation in
[
2
]
, where they used a conformal map, which maps the unit disk to the domain, with which they were able to reformulate the Steklov boundary problem. Using Fourier series they achieved very accurate eigenvalues, but they did not provide a method to evaluate the corresponding eigenfunctions. Here we rewrite their method to compute the eigenfunctions and achieve high accuracy.

Given a domain
\Omega\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
and a conformal map
f:\mathbb{C}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
, which maps
\{z\in\mathbb{C}||z|^{2}<1\}
to the complex embedded domain
\Omega\in\mathbb{C}
. Let
(\lambda_{i},s_{i})
be one of the solutions to the Steklov problem (
2.5
). Then according to
[
2
, Proposition 2]
there exists an analytic function
\Psi_{i}:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
, such that
s_{i}=\mathcal{R}(\Psi_{i})
, the real part of
\Psi_{i}
, and the following equation is satisfied,

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(\omega\;\partial_{\omega}\Psi_{i})=\lambda_{i}\,|\partial_{\omega}f|\,\mathcal{R}(\Psi_{i})\,,

(4.1)

where
|\omega|=1
and
\partial_{\omega}
denotes the complex derivative. For the next step we decompose
\Psi_{i}
in their Fourier series on the unit circle, that is

\displaystyle\Psi_{i}(\omega)=\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}b^{(i)}_{n}\omega^{n}\,,

for
|\omega|=1
. From Equation (
4.1
) we can infer that
b^{(i)}_{n}=0
for all
n\leq-1
. Next we truncate the last three Fourier series up to coefficient
N\in\mathbb{N}
and we define the nodes
(\omega_{l})_{l=1}^{L}
,
L\in\mathbb{N}
, on the unit disk. Then we define the matrices

\displaystyle\mathbf{N}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}0&0&0&\dots&0\\
0&1&0&\dots&0\\
0&0&2&\dots&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&0\\
0&0&0&0&N\end{bmatrix}\,,

\displaystyle\mathbf{D}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}|\partial_{\omega}f(\omega_{1})|&0&0&\dots&0\\
0&|\partial_{\omega}f(\omega_{2})|&0&\dots&0\\
0&0&|\partial_{\omega}f(\omega_{2})|&\dots&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&0\\
0&0&0&0&|\partial_{\omega}f(\omega_{N})|\end{bmatrix}\,,

as well as
\mathbf{E}_{n,l}=\omega_{l}^{n}
and the vector
\mathbf{B}^{(i)}_{n}=b^{(i)}_{n}
. Then we can rewrite Equation (
4.1
) to

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}((\mathbf{N}\mathbf{E})^{\mathrm{T}}\mathbf{B}^{(i)}+\overline{(\mathbf{N}\mathbf{E})^{\mathrm{T}}\mathbf{B}^{(i)}})=\lambda_{i}\,\mathbf{D}\,\frac{1}{2}(\mathbf{E}^{\mathrm{T}}\mathbf{B}^{(i)}+\overline{\mathbf{E}^{\mathrm{T}}\mathbf{B}^{(i)}})\,.

Splitting
\mathbf{E}
and
\mathbf{B}^{(i)}
into their real parts
\mathbf{E}_{R}
and
\mathbf{B}^{(i)}_{R}
and their imaginary parts
\mathbf{E}_{I}
and
\mathbf{B}^{(i)}_{I}
, we can reformulate the last equation to

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}(\mathbf{N}\mathbf{E}_{R})^{\mathrm{T}}&-(\mathbf{N}\mathbf{E}_{I})^{\mathrm{T}}\end{bmatrix}\;\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{B}^{(i)}_{R}\\
\mathbf{B}^{(i)}_{I}\end{bmatrix}=\lambda_{i}\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{E}_{R})^{\mathrm{T}}&-\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{E}_{I})^{\mathrm{T}}\end{bmatrix}}_{\eqqcolon\mathbf{R}}\;\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{B}^{(i)}_{R}\\
\mathbf{B}^{(i)}_{I}\end{bmatrix}\,.

(4.2)

Given that
L=2N
, we have square matrices on both sides and then the equation represents an generalized eigenvalue problem. The in-build MATLAB
[
19
]
program
eigs
can be used to solve the problem and obtain
\lambda_{i}
and
\mathbf{B}^{(i)}
. For non-square matrices we use the singular value decomposition for
\mathbf{R}=\mathbf{U}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{V}^{\mathrm{H}}
, then remove the non-compatible singular values, such that we can reformulate Equation (
4.2
) into a standard eigenvalue problem. Subsequently we solve the problem with
eigs
.
When we obtain the coefficients
\mathbf{B}^{(i)}
for
i=1,\ldots,M
, we evaluate the Steklov eigenfunction
s_{i}=\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{E}^{\mathrm{T}}\mathbf{B}^{(i)})
, at the nodes
(z_{l})_{l=1}^{L}
. Afterwards we use the Gram-Schmidt orthogonalisation on all
(s_{i})_{i=1,\ldots,M}
with the inner-product
\langle\cdot\,,\cdot\rangle_{\partial}
.

The disadvantage to this method is to find a conformal map. Especially, the map requires that the conformal map has no singularity in its domain. This is an issue because even the conformal map, which maps the unit circle to an ellipse, cannot not be described in a simple form. The Joukowski map is such a map, which maps the unit circle to an ellipse, but it has a singularity at the origin and at infinity. The Riemann mapping theorem guarantees a desired conformal map, but it is not easily obtainable. One way to obtain a conformal mapping to the ellipse is through the Bergman kernel, we refer to
[
15
, pp. 529 - 552]
for further instructions.
For polygonal domains, we have to find the Fourier coefficients by evaluating
|\partial_{\omega}f|
by hand. In general this is done using the Schwarz-Christoffel conformal map. This map maps the upper half sphere onto the interior of a simple polygon. For the special case of the square
[-1,1]\times[-1,1]
, the function
f
, it maps the unit disk to the square, can be formulated as

\displaystyle f(w)=2\,c\int_{0}^{\frac{\mathrm{i}\,(1-\omega)}{1+\omega}}\!\!\!\!\frac{\mathrm{d}z}{\sqrt{z(1-z^{2})}}\;-\;(1-\mathrm{i}\,)\,,

where
c=\frac{\Gamma(\tfrac{3}{4})}{2\sqrt{\pi}\Gamma(\tfrac{5}{4})}
. Given
\omega=\exp(\mathrm{i}\,\theta)
,
\theta\in(-\pi,\pi]
, then
\frac{\mathrm{i}\,(1-\omega)}{1+\omega}=\tan(\theta/2)
. Then we can infer that

\displaystyle\partial_{\omega}f(w)=2\,c\frac{-2\mathrm{i}\,}{(1+\omega)^{2}\sqrt{\tan(\theta/2)(1-\tan(\theta/2)^{2})}}\,.

Then we can conclude that

\displaystyle|\partial_{\omega}f(w)|^{2}=\frac{c^{2}}{|\cos(\theta)|\cos(\theta/2)|\sin(\theta/2)|}\,.

Second method (Weak Form Method)

The second method does not rely on a conformal map, but instead uses Green’s identity to obtain that

\displaystyle\int_{\partial\Omega}s_{i}\,\partial v\,\mathrm{d}\sigma=\int_{\partial\Omega}\lambda_{i}s_{i}\,v\,\mathrm{d}\sigma\,,

for all
i\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and all
v\in C^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega})
, with
\mathop{}\!\mathbin{\bigtriangleup}v=0
in
\Omega
.
We then use the trapezoidal rule to approximate the integral through
L\in\mathbb{N}
nodes on the boundary. Then the equation can be rewritten to

\displaystyle\mathbf{F_{\partial}}^{\mathrm{T}}\,{\Sigma}\,\mathbf{s}_{i}=\lambda_{i}\,\mathbf{F}^{\mathrm{T}}\,{\Sigma}\,\mathbf{s}_{i}\,,

where
\mathbf{F}
is a vector of the evaluation of the testfunction at the nodes on the boundary, and
\mathbf{F_{\partial}}
is the evaluation of the normal derivative of the testfunction at the nodes, analogous for the vector
\mathbf{s}_{i}
, and where
{\Sigma}
is a diagonal matrix whose entries represent the additional information from the trapezoidal rule and the curve parametrization. We then choose the following testfunctions

\displaystyle v_{n,\,\text{cos}}

\displaystyle=r^{n}\cos(n\,\theta)\,,

\displaystyle v_{n,\,\text{sin}}

\displaystyle=r^{n}\sin(n\,\theta)\,.

where
n\in\mathbb{N}
, where
r\in[0,\infty)
denotes the radius from the origin, and
\theta\in[0,2\pi)
the angle. Then we write the evaluation of all testfunctions into the columns of
\mathbf{F}
and analogous
\mathbf{F_{\partial}}
, where

\displaystyle\partial_{x}v_{n,\,\text{cos}}

\displaystyle=n\,r^{n-1}\big(\cos(\theta)\cos(n\,\theta)+\sin(\theta)\sin(n\,\theta)\big)\,,

\displaystyle\partial_{y}v_{n,\,\text{cos}}

\displaystyle=n\,r^{n-1}\big(\sin(\theta)\cos(n\,\theta)-\cos(\theta)\sin(n\,\theta)\big)\,,

\displaystyle\partial_{x}v_{n,\,\text{sin}}

\displaystyle=n\,r^{n-1}\big(\cos(\theta)\sin(n\,\theta)-\sin(\theta)\cos(n\,\theta)\big)\,,

\displaystyle\partial_{y}v_{n,\,\text{sin}}

\displaystyle=n\,r^{n-1}\big(\sin(\theta)\sin(n\,\theta)+\cos(\theta)\cos(n\,\theta)\big)\,.

Using the QR decomposition we obtain an matrix
\mathbf{B}
and an upper triangular matrix
\mathbf{R}
such that
(\sqrt{\Sigma}\,\mathbf{B})\,\mathbf{R}=(\sqrt{\Sigma}\,\mathbf{F})
, and
(\sqrt{\Sigma}\,\mathbf{B})^{\mathrm{T}}(\sqrt{\Sigma}\,\mathbf{B})
is an identity matrix. Then we define
\mathbf{B_{\partial}}=\mathbf{F_{\partial}}\,\mathbf{R}^{-1}
. Then we have that

\displaystyle\mathbf{B_{\partial}}^{\mathrm{T}}\,{\Sigma}\,\mathbf{s}_{i}=\lambda_{i}\,\mathbf{B}^{\mathrm{T}}\,{\Sigma}\,\mathbf{s}_{i}\,.

We can further use that
(\sqrt{\Sigma}\,\mathbf{B})(\sqrt{\Sigma}\,\mathbf{B})^{\mathrm{T}}
is also the identity matrix and obtain

\displaystyle(\mathbf{B}\,\mathbf{B_{\partial}}^{\mathrm{T}}\,{\Sigma})\,\mathbf{s}_{i}=\lambda_{i}\,\mathbf{s}_{i}\,.

We solve this eigenvalue problem with the MATLAB
[
19
]
in-build function
eigs
.

The disadvantage to method 2 is that the term
r^{n}
in
v_{n,\,\text{cos}},v_{n,\,\text{sin}}
can reach extreme values for
n>50
, which might lead to numerical error. This is especially the case, when the radius varies strongly on the boundary.

Third method (Collocation Method)

The third method relies on the collocation method presented in
[
16
, Chapter 13]
. Let
(\mathcal{L}_{i})_{i=1}^{\infty}
be the Lagrange basis for the trigonometric interpolation on the boundary to the nodes
(z_{l})_{l=1}^{L}=\big(z(\theta_{l})\big)_{l=1}^{L}\subset\partial\Omega
, that is, we can represent the density on the boundary
\phi(t)\coloneqq\phi(z(t))\in L^{2}(\partial\Omega)
, for
t\in(-\pi,\pi]
through

\displaystyle\phi(t)=\sum_{i=1}^{L}\gamma_{i}\,\mathcal{L}_{i}(t)\,,

such that
\phi
is
2\pi
-periodic and
\phi(\theta_{l})=\gamma_{l}
, for all
l=1,\ldots,L
. This resembles the formulation given through Equation (
2.10
). Then we apply the Steklov boundary equation with boundary layer potentials, that is
(-\tfrac{1}{2}\mathrm{I}+\mathcal{K}^{\ast})[\phi_{i}]=\lambda_{i}\,\mathcal{S}[\phi_{i}]
. This requires the evaluation of integrals of the form

\displaystyle\int_{\partial\Omega}K(\tau,t)\phi(t)\mathrm{d}t\,.

We use the Lagrange basis for the trigonometric interpolation on
K(\tau,\cdot)
. This leads to the evaluation of the integrals

\displaystyle\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\mathcal{L}_{i}(t)\,\mathcal{L}_{j}(t)\mathrm{d}t\,.

In case the kernel function
K(\tau,\cdot)
has a logarithmic singularity, we extract the singularity and this leads to the evaluation of the integrals

\displaystyle\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\log\big(\sin\big(\tfrac{t}{2}\big)^{2}\big)\mathcal{L}_{i}(t)\,\mathcal{L}_{j}(t)\mathrm{d}t\,.

The generalized eigenvalue problem is then again solved with
eigs
. To obtain then the Steklov functions
s_{i}
, we apply the single layer potential on the density, that is we compute
\mathcal{S}[\phi_{i}]
, using again the Lagrange basis for the trigonometric interpolation.

Method three, that is the collocation method, usually performs worse than method 1, the collocation method, but method three does not require a conformal map. Furthermore, method three widely outperforms method 2. One downside to method three is that it does not work on polygonal domains, without mayors modification on the evaluation of the integrals involved.

5
Concluding Remarks

In this paper we showed how we can use the Steklov eigenfunctions to approximate the solution to the Laplace equation with either Dirichlet or Robin boundary conditions. Using higher regularity results for layer potentials we proved that
s_{i}\in H^{p}(\partial\Omega)
whenever
\partial\Omega\in H^{p+1/2}_{\text{loc}}
, this allowed us to conclude that the series expansion involving the Steklov eigenpairs approximates the solution to the homogeneous Laplace equation with an exponentially decreasing error given a smooth boundary. Furthermore, we provided a polynomial error approximation in terms of the smoothness of the boundary and in terms of the regularity of the boundary data. We then elaborated on three different methods to numerically compute the Steklov eigenfunctions, the conformal method, the weak form method and the collocation method. With those methods we were able to verify Theorem
3.1
on various domains and with various boundary data.

An interesting question arises here, namely can we smooth corners out and utilize the exponential fast approximation? Some preliminary tests on a square with rounded-off corners give a positive answer. Here we have to further consider how we can approximate the boundary data, when we smooth out corners.

Another question is about higher dimensional application of the Steklov eigenfunctions. In this paper we only considered the two dimensional problem. Applying the results in
[
5
]
and
[
21
]
, we can show that Proposition
2.2
is also valid. For the theorem we further have to show that the regularity results for the Dirichlet to Neumann operator still hold, then we firmly believe that all the results in Theorem
3.1
are valid in higher dimensions.

An other thought is about the applications of the Steklov eigenfunctions on different PDEs. We think that we have similar results on the homogeneous Helmholtz equation that is
(\mathop{}\!\mathbin{\bigtriangleup}+k^{2})u=0
, for
k>0
. We are not sure about other PDE’s like the Schroedinger-type operator
[
6
]
.
