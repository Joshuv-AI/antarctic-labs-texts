Matchings, Squarefree Powers and Betti Splittings
Marilena Crupi, Antonino Ficarra, Ernesto Lax

Abstract
. Let G G be a finite simple graph and let I ⁡ ( G ) I(G) be its edge ideal. In this article, we investigate the squarefree powers of I ⁡ ( G ) I(G) by means of Betti splittings. When G G is a forest, it is shown that the normalized depth function of I ⁡ ( G ) I(G) is non-increasing. Moreover, we compute explicitly the regularity function of squarefree powers of I ⁡ ( G ) I(G) with G G a forest, confirming a conjecture of Erey and Hibi.

Introduction

All the graphs we consider in this article are finite simple graphs. Let
G
be a graph with the vertex set
V(G)=\{1,\ldots,n\}
and the edge set
E(G)
. A
k
-
matching
of
G
is a subset
M
of
E(G)
of size
k
such that
e\cap e^{\prime}=\emptyset
for all
e,e^{\prime}\in M
with
e\neq e^{\prime}
. We denote by
V(M)
the vertex set of
M
, that is, the set
\{i\in V(G):i\in e\ \text{for}\ e\in M\}
. We say that a graph
H
is a
subgraph of
G
if
V(H)\subseteq V(G)
and
E(H)\subseteq E(G)
. A subgraph
H
of
G
is said an
induced subgraph
if for any two vertices
i,j
in
H
,
\{i,j\}\in E(H)
if and only if
\{i,j\}\in E(G)
. If
A
is a subset of
V(G)
, the
induced subgraph
on
A
, denoted by
G_{A}
, is the graph with vertex set
A
and the edge set
\{\{i,j\}:\mbox{$i,j\in A$ and $\{i,j\}\in E(G)$}\}
. A matching
M
is called an
induced matching
if
E(G_{V(M)})=M
. The
matching number
of
G
, denoted by
\nu(G)
, is the maximum size of a matching of
G
. Whereas, the
induced matching number
of
G
, denoted by
\operatorname{indm}(G)
, is the maximum size of an induced matching of
G
. It is clear that
\operatorname{indm}(G)\leq\nu(G)
for any graph
G
.

Matchings play a pivotal role in graph theory
[
16
]
. Hereafter, we set
[n]=\{1,\dots,n\}
if
n\geq 1
is an integer, and
[0]=\emptyset
. Let
G
be a graph on the vertex set
[n]
and let
S=K[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
be the standard graded polynomial ring over a field
K
. For a non–empty subset
A
of
[n]
, we set
{\bf x}_{A}=\prod_{i\in A}x_{i}
. Let
1\leq k\leq\nu(G)
. We denote by
I(G)^{[k]}
the squarefree monomial ideal generated by
{\bf x}_{V(M)}
for all
k
-matchings
M
of
G
. If
k=1
, then
I(G)^{[1]}
is the well–known ideal, called as the
edge ideal
of
G

[
25
,
26
]
, and we denote it simply by
I(G)
. More recently, matching theory has been related to Commutative Algebra
via
the notion of
squarefree powers

[
3
,
8
]
.

Let us explain the connection with the concept of squarefree power (see, for instance,
[
3
]
). Let
I\subset S
be a squarefree monomial ideal and
\mathcal{G}(I)
be its unique minimal set of monomial generators. The
k
th squarefree power
of
I
, denoted by
I^{[k]}
, is the ideal generated by the squarefree monomials of
I^{k}
. Thus
u_{1}u_{2}\cdots u_{k}
,
u_{i}\in\mathcal{G}(I)
,
i\in[k]
, belongs to
\mathcal{G}(I^{[k]})
if and only if
u_{1},u_{2},\dots,u_{k}
is a regular sequence. Let
\nu(I)
be the
monomial grade
of
I
,
i.e.
, the maximum among the lengths of a monomial regular sequence contained in
I
. Then
I^{[k]}\neq(0)
if and only if
k\leq\nu(I)
. Hence, the ideal
I(G)^{[k]}
is the
k
th squarefree power of
I(G)
and
\nu(I(G))=\nu(G)
.

The study of ordinary powers of ideals is a classical subject in Commutative Algebra. The fascination with this topic is due to the fact that many algebraic invariants behave asymptotically well, that is, stabilize or show a regular behaviour for sufficiently high powers. The first result in this area was obtained by Brodmann
[
5
]
who showed that the depth of the powers of an ideal
I
of a Noetherian ring
R
is eventually constant: there exists
k_{0}>0
such that
\operatorname{depth}(R/I^{k})=\operatorname{depth}(R/I^{k_{0}})
for all
k\geq k_{0}
. The study of the initial behaviour of the depth function of powers of graded ideals
I\subset S
was initiated by Herzog and Hibi
[
18
]
. They made the boldest guess possible: any bounded convergent function
\varphi:\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
is the depth function of some suitable graded ideal in a polynomial ring. Recently, this conjecture has been settled in affirmative by Hà, Nguyen, Trung and Trung
[
15
, Theorem 4.1]
.

Let
I\subset S
be a squarefree monomial ideal and
k\in[\nu(I)]
. Let
d_{k}=\textup{indeg}(I^{[k]})
be the
initial degree
of
I^{[k]}
, that is, the minimum degree of a monomial of
\mathcal{G}(I^{[k]})
. In
[
9
, Proposition 1.1]
, the authors established that

\operatorname{depth}(S/I^{[k]})\geq d_{k}-1

for all
k\in[\nu(I)]
. Based on this, they introduced the
normalized depth function
of
I
, as follows

g_{I}(k)=\operatorname{depth}(S/I^{[k]})-(d_{k}-1)

where
k\in[\nu(I)]
. In contrast to the behaviour of the depth function of ordinary powers, it was predicted in
[
9
]
that:

Conjecture:

For any
I\subset S
, the function
g_{I}(k)
is a non-increasing function.

Squarefree powers have been introduced in
[
3
]
, and are currently studied by many researchers

,
10
,

,

,

,

Among graphs,
forests
are the simplest ones. In this article, we deeply investigate the squarefree powers of edge ideals of forests. Our result are based on the technique of
Betti splitting
. In their groundbreaking article, Eliahou and Kervaire considered an earlier version of Betti splitting
[
6
, Proposition 3.1]
. Later on, Francisco, Hà and Van Tuyl, inspired by the results in
[
6
]
, introduced the notion of Betti splitting
[
13
,
24
]
. Roughly speaking, if
I\subset S
is a monomial ideal and
I_{1}
and
I_{2}
are sub–ideals of
I
such that
\mathcal{G}(I)
is the disjoint union of
\mathcal{G}(I_{1})
and
\mathcal{G}(I_{2})
, then
I=I_{1}+I_{2}
is said to be a Betti splitting, if the minimal free resolution of
I
can be recovered from the minimal free resolutions of
I_{1}
,
I_{2}
and
I_{1}\cap I_{2}
.

Our article is structured as follows. In Section
1
, we consider Betti splittings of squarefree powers. Theorem
1.1
is a technical but powerful criterion that can be used to establish if a certain decomposition
I=I_{1}+I_{2}
is a Betti splitting. This fact is used in Lemma
1.4
to reprove a result in
[
12
]
under weaker assumptions (Proposition
1.5
).

Section
2
deeply investigates squarefree powers of edge ideals of forests. For a vertex
v
in
G
, we say
w
is a
neighbor
of
v
if
\{v,w\}\in E(G)
. We denote the set of all neighbors of
v
by
N_{G}(v)
.
N_{G}(v)
is called the
neighborhood
of
v
. The
degree
of
v
,
\deg_{G}(v)
, is the number
|N_{G}(v)|
. If
\deg_{G}(v)=1
,
v
is called a
leaf
. Any forest has at least two leaves. A leaf
v\in V(G)
, with unique neighbor
w
, is called a
distant leaf
if at most one of the neighbors of
w
is not a leaf. The existence of a distant leaf in any forest
G
(Proposition
2.2
) provides a natural Betti splitting for
I(G)^{[k]}
(Lemma
2.4
). Then, using induction, we successfully prove that
g_{I(G)}
is non-increasing, as stated in Theorem
2.1
, resolving a conjecture from
[
10
]
. We also compute the normalized depth function of the edge ideal of a path and use this result to obtain a general upper bound for the normalized depth function of the edge ideal of any graph.

In the last section, we compute the Castelnuovo–Mumford regularity
\operatorname{reg}(I(G)^{[k]})
in terms of the combinatorics of the forest
G
(Theorem
3.6
), solving affirmatively a conjecture due to Erey and Hibi
[
10
, Conjecture 31]
.

Finally, we would like to remark that not all squarefree monomial ideals admit a Betti splitting, as pointed out by Bolognini
[
4
, Example 4.6]
, see also
[
13
, Example 4.2]
. On the other hand, Francisco, Hà and Van Tuyl showed that the edge ideal of any graph always admits a Betti splitting
[
13
, Corollary 3.1]
. It is an open question if all the squarefree powers of an edge ideal admit a Betti splitting.

1.
Betti splittings for squarefree powers

Let
S=K[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
be the standard graded polynomial ring over a field
K
. Let
I\subset S
be a monomial ideal. By
\mathcal{G}(I)
we denote the unique minimal set of monomial generators of
I
. Let
I_{1},I_{2}\subset S
monomial ideals such that
\mathcal{G}(I)
is the disjoint union of
\mathcal{G}(I_{1})
and
\mathcal{G}(I_{2})
. We say that
I=I_{1}+I_{2}
is a
Betti splitting

[
13
, Definition 1.1]
if

\beta_{i,j}(I)=\beta_{i,j}(I_{1})+\beta_{i,j}(I_{2})+\beta_{i-1,j}(I_{1}\cap I_{2}),\ \ \ \text{for all}\ i,j\geq 0.

(1.1)

Consider the natural short exact sequence

0\rightarrow I_{1}\cap I_{2}\rightarrow I_{1}\oplus I_{2}\rightarrow I\rightarrow 0.

Then (
1.1
) holds if and only if the following induced maps in
\operatorname{Tor}
of the above sequence

\operatorname{Tor}_{i}^{S}(K,I_{1}\cap I_{2})\rightarrow\operatorname{Tor}_{i}^{S}(K,I_{1})\oplus\operatorname{Tor}_{i}^{S}(K,I_{2})

are zero for all
i\geq 0

[
13
, Proposition 2.1]
. In this case, we say that the inclusion map
I_{1}\cap I_{2}\rightarrow I_{1}\oplus I_{2}
is
\operatorname{Tor}
-vanishing
. See
[
1
,
21
]
for more details on this subject.

The next criterion gives a useful method to prove that a map
J\rightarrow L
is
\operatorname{Tor}
-vanishing, where
(0)\neq J\subset L
are monomial ideals of
S
. It appeared implicitly for the first time in
[
6
]
(see also the proof of
[
21
, Lemma 4.2]
).

Theorem 1.1
.

(
[
6
, Proposition 3.1]
)

Let
J,L\subset S
be non–zero monomial ideals with
J\subset L
. Suppose there exists a map
\varphi:\mathcal{G}(J)\rightarrow\mathcal{G}(L)
such that for any
\emptyset\neq\Omega\subseteq\mathcal{G}(J)
we have

\operatorname{lcm}(u:u\in\Omega)\in\mathfrak{m}(\operatorname{lcm}(\varphi(u):u\in\Omega)),

where
\mathfrak{m}=(x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{n})
. Then the inclusion map
J\rightarrow L
is
\operatorname{Tor}
-vanishing.

Let
I
be a monomial ideal, following
[
21
]
we denote by
\partial^{*}I
the ideal generated by the elements of the form
f/x_{i}
, where
f
is a minimal monomial generator of
I
and
x_{i}
is a variable dividing
f
.

We recall the following result from
[
21
]
.

Lemma 1.2
.

(
[
21
, Proposition 4.4]
)
Let
J,L\subset S
be non–zero monomial ideals. Suppose that
\partial^{*}J\subseteq L
. Then
J\subseteq\mathfrak{m}L
and there exists a map
\varphi:\mathcal{G}(J)\rightarrow\mathcal{G}(L)
satisfying the assumption of Theorem
1.1
. In particular, the inclusion map
J\rightarrow L
is
\operatorname{Tor}
-vanishing.

Note that for all
1\leq\ell<k\leq\nu(I)
, we have
\partial^{*}I^{[k]}\subset I^{[k-1]}\subseteq I^{[\ell]}
. Hence, the previous lemma implies immediately the following result.

Corollary 1.3
.

Let
I\subset S
be a squarefree monomial ideal. Then the map
I^{[k]}\rightarrow I^{[\ell]}
is
\operatorname{Tor}
-vanishing for all
1\leq\ell<k\leq\nu(I)
.

Let
I=I_{1}+I_{2}
with
\mathcal{G}(I_{2})=\{u\in\mathcal{G}(I):x\ \textit{divides}\ u\}
and
\mathcal{G}(I_{1})=\mathcal{G}(I)\setminus\mathcal{G}(I_{2})
, for some variable
x
. We say that
I=I_{1}+I_{2}
is an
x
-partition
. In addition, if it is a Betti splitting, we say that
I=I_{1}+I_{2}
is an
x
-splitting

[
13
, Definition 2.6]
.

Lemma 1.4
.

Let
I\subset K[x_{1},\dots,x_{n-1}]
be a squarefree monomial ideal. Then, for all
1<k\leq\nu(I)

(I,x_{n})^{[k]}=I^{[k]}+x_{n}I^{[k-1]}\subset S

(1.2)

is a Betti splitting.

Proof.

Note that
(I,x_{n})^{[k]}=I^{[k]}+x_{n}I^{[k-1]}
is a
x_{n}
-partition. Moreover,

I^{[k]}\cap x_{n}I^{[k-1]}=x_{n}[I^{[k]}\cap I^{[k-1]}]=x_{n}I^{[k]}.

Thus, to prove that (
1.2
) is a Betti splitting, it remains to be shown that the inclusion map
x_{n}I^{[k]}\rightarrow I^{[k]}\oplus x_{n}I^{[k-1]}
is
\operatorname{Tor}
-vanishing. That is, for all
i
and all
{\bf a}=(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}
, the map

\operatorname{Tor}_{i}^{S}(K,x_{n}I^{[k]})_{\bf a}\rightarrow\operatorname{Tor}_{i}^{S}(K,I^{[k]})_{\bf a}\oplus\operatorname{Tor}_{i}^{S}(K,x_{n}I^{[k-1]})_{\bf a}

is zero. But if
\operatorname{Tor}_{i}^{S}(K,x_{n}I^{[k]})_{\bf a}\neq 0
, then
a_{n}>0
since
x_{n}
divides all minimal generators of
x_{n}I^{[k]}
. On the other hand, if
a_{n}>0
, then
\operatorname{Tor}_{i}^{S}(K,I^{[k]})_{\bf a}=0
because
x_{n}
does not divide any generator of
I^{[k]}
. Therefore, the map
x_{n}I^{[k]}\rightarrow I^{[k]}\oplus x_{n}I^{[k-1]}
is
\operatorname{Tor}
-vanishing if and only if for all
i
and all
{\bf a}=(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}
with
a_{n}>0
, the map
\operatorname{Tor}_{i}^{S}(K,x_{n}I^{[k]})_{\bf a}\rightarrow\operatorname{Tor}_{i}^{S}(K,x_{n}I^{[k-1]})_{\bf a}

is zero. This is equivalent to saying that
x_{n}I^{[k]}\rightarrow x_{n}I^{[k-1]}
is
\operatorname{Tor}
-vanishing. This latter condition holds if and only if the map
I^{[k]}\rightarrow I^{[k-1]}
is such. But this is obviously the case by Corollary
1.3
.

∎

Proposition 1.5

Let

be a squarefree monomial ideal. Set

and

g_{J}(k)\ =\ \min\{g_{I}(k)+d_{k}-d_{k-1}-1,g_{I}(k-1)\}.

Our goal is to compute explicitly the regularity function

Theorem 2.1
.

Let

The previous theorem and the results in this section are based upon the following proposition. It already appeared in
[

Proposition 2.2
.

Let

be a forest. Then
G
has a distant leaf.

Proof.

It is enough to assume that
G
is a tree. Let
v_{1},v_{2},\dots,v_{r}
be an induced path of
G
of maximal length. We claim that
v_{r}
is a distant leaf. Indeed, let
N_{G}(v_{r-1})=\{w_{1},\dots,w_{s}\}
. Up to a relabeling, we may assume
w_{1}=v_{r-2}
and
w_{2}=v_{r}
. Since
\deg_{G}(w_{2})=1
, it is enough to show that
\deg_{G}(w_{i})=1
for
i=3,\dots,s
. Suppose for a contradiction that this is not the case. Thus
\deg_{G}(w_{i})>1
for some
i\in\{3,\dots,s\}
. Let
u
be a neighbor of
w_{i}
different from
v_{r-1}
. Then,
v_{1},v_{2},\dots,v_{r-1},w_{i},u
is an induced path of
G
of length

, a contradiction. The assertion follows.

∎

Setup 2.3

,

.

is the disjoint union of

.

Next, we set

(2.3)

Let

. This means that

is a

g_{I(P_{n})}(k)\ =\ \begin{cases}\lceil\frac{n}{3}\rceil-k&\text{if}\ k=1,\dots,\lceil\frac{n}{3}\rceil,\\
\hfil 0&otherwise.\end{cases}

Proof.

We proceed by induction on
n\geq 3
. For

\nu(G)\geq 3
and
n

t=0

G_{1}=P_{n-1}

g_{I(P_{n})}(k)=\min\{g_{I(P_{n-1})}(k),g_{I(P_{n-2})}(k-1)\!-\!2,g_{I(P_{n-3})}(k-1)\!-\!3,g_{I(P_{n-3})}(k)\!-\!2\}.

If

g_{I(P_{n-3})}(k-1)-3=0

Suppose now

, then all terms appearing in the minimum taken above are greater than zero, by the inductive hypothesis. Hence,

\displaystyle g_{I(P_{n})}(k)

\displaystyle=\min\Big\{\Big\lceil\frac{n-1}{3}\Big\rceil-k+1,\Big\lceil\frac{n-2}{3}\Big\rceil-(k-1),\Big\lceil\frac{n-3}{3}\Big\rceil-(k-1),

\displaystyle\phantom{=\min\Big\{.}\Big\lceil\frac{n-3}{3}\Big\rceil-k+1\Big\}

\displaystyle=\min\Big\{\Big\lceil\frac{n-1}{3}\Big\rceil-k+1,\Big\lceil\frac{n-2}{3}\Big\rceil-k+1,\Big\lceil\frac{n}{3}\Big\rceil-k\Big\}

\displaystyle=\Big\lceil\frac{n}{3}\Big\rceil-k,

as desired. The inductive proof is complete.

Denote by

Corollary 2.11
.

Let

g_{I(G)}(k)\leq\begin{cases}\big\lceil\frac{3n-2\ell(G)}{3}\big\rceil-k&\text{for}\ k=1,\dots,\big\lceil\frac{\ell(G)}{3}\big\rceil,\\
\hfil n-\ell(G)&\text{for}\ k=\big\lceil\frac{\ell(G)}{3}\big\rceil+1,\dots,\big\lfloor\frac{\ell(G)}{2}\big\rfloor.\end{cases}

Proof.

Let

be an induced path of

, and set

.

3.
The Erey–Hibi Conjecture

In this last section, we turn to the regularity of the squarefree powers of
I(G)
, when
G
is a forest, and we prove a conjecture due to Erey and Hibi
[
10
]
.

In
[
10
]
, Erey and Hibi introduced the concept of
k
-admissible matching
of a graph. Let
n
and
k
be positive integers. A sequence
(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})
of positive integers is called a
k
-admissible sequence
if
a_{1}+\dots+a_{n}\leq n+k-1
.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
G
be a graph,
M
be a matching of
G
and
k\in[\nu(G)]
. We say that
M
is a
k
-admissible matching
if there exists a sequence
M_{1},\dots,M_{r}
of non–empty subsets of
M
satisfying the following conditions:

(a)

M=M_{1}\cup\dots\cup M_{r}
;

(b)

M_{i}\cap M_{j}=\emptyset
for all
i\neq j
;

(c)

for all
i\neq j
, if
e_{i}\in M_{i}
and
e_{j}\in M_{j}
, then
\{e_{i},e_{j}\}
is a
gap
in
G
, that is,
\{e_{i},e_{j}\}
is an induced matching of size
2
;

(d)

the sequence
(|M_{1}|,\dots,|M_{r}|)
is
k
-admissible;

(e)

the induced subgraph of
G
on
V(M_{i})
is a forest, for all
i\in[r]
.

In such a case, we say that
M=M_{1}\cup\dots\cup M_{r}
is a
k
-admissible partition
of
G
. The
k
-admissible matching number
of
G
, denoted by
\textup{aim}(G,k)
, is the number

\textup{aim}(G,k)=\max\{|M|:M\ \textit{is a}\ k\textit{-admissible matching of}\ G\}

for
k\in[\nu(G)]
. We set
\textup{aim}(G,k)=0
, if
G
has no
k
-admissible matching.

If
G
is a forest, condition (e) is vacuously verified.

Remark 3.2
.

(
[
10
, Remark 14]
)
Note that
\textup{aim}(G,k)\geq k
for all
k\in[\nu(G)]
. If
H
is an induced subgraph of
G
, then
\textup{aim}(H,k)\leq\textup{aim}(G,k)
. Moreover,

\operatorname{indm}(G)=\textup{aim}(G,1)\leq\textup{aim}(G,2)\leq\dots\leq\textup{aim}(G,\nu(G))=\nu(G).

For the proof of the next corollary, we recall some facts. Let
u\in S
be a monomial and
L\subset S
be a monomial ideal, then
\operatorname{reg}(uL)=\operatorname{reg}(L)+\deg(u)
. Moreover, if
\{x_{p_{1}},\dots,x_{p_{s}}\}
is a collection of variables not dividing any generator of
L
, then
[
19
, Corollary 3.2]

\operatorname{reg}((x_{p_{1}},\dots,x_{p_{s}})L)=\operatorname{reg}(L)+1
. Furthermore, if
H
is an induced subgraph of
G
,
[
8
, Corollary 1.3]
gives

\operatorname{reg}(I(H)^{[k]})\leq\operatorname{reg}(I(G)^{[k]}).

Corollary 3.3
.

With the notation and assumptions of Lemma
2.4
, we have

\operatorname{reg}(I(G)^{[k]})=\max\{\operatorname{reg}(I(G_{1})^{[k]}),\operatorname{reg}(I(G_{2})^{[k-1]})+2,\operatorname{reg}(I(G_{3})^{[k]})+1\}

for
k=1,\ldots,\nu(G)
.

Proof.

By Lemma
2.4
, since
I(G)^{[k]}
is a Betti splitting, from
[
13
, Corollary 2.2]
we have

\displaystyle\operatorname{reg}(I(G)^{[k]})

\displaystyle=\max\{\operatorname{reg}(I(G_{1})^{[k]}),\operatorname{reg}(x_{n}x_{n-1}I(G_{2})^{[k-1]}),\operatorname{reg}(J)-1\}

(3.1)

\displaystyle=\max\{\operatorname{reg}(I(G_{1})^{[k]}),\operatorname{reg}(I(G_{2})^{[k-1]})+2,\operatorname{reg}(J)-1\},

where
J
is given in (
2.2
). Since
\operatorname{reg}((x_{i_{1}},\dots,x_{i_{t}})J_{1})-1=\operatorname{reg}(J_{1})
, Lemma
2.5
gives

\displaystyle\operatorname{reg}(J)

\displaystyle=\max\{\operatorname{reg}(J_{1}),\operatorname{reg}(J_{2}),\operatorname{reg}((x_{i_{1}},\dots,x_{i_{t}})J_{1})-1\}

\displaystyle=\max\{\operatorname{reg}(J_{1}),\operatorname{reg}(J_{2})\},

Since
J_{1}=x_{n}x_{n-1}[I(G_{3})^{[k]}+x_{n-2}I(G_{3})^{[k-1]}]
is a Betti splitting, by Lemma
1.4
we have

\displaystyle\operatorname{reg}(J_{1})

\displaystyle=\max\{\operatorname{reg}(x_{n}x_{n-1}I(G_{3})^{[k]}),\operatorname{reg}(x_{n}x_{n-1}x_{n-2}I(G_{3})^{[k-1]}),

(3.2)

\displaystyle\operatorname{reg}(x_{n}x_{n-1}x_{n-2}I(G_{3})^{[k]})-1\}

\displaystyle=\max\{\operatorname{reg}(I(G_{3})^{[k]})+2,\operatorname{reg}(I(G_{3})^{[k-1]})+3\}.

Now we distinguish two cases.

Case 1.
Suppose
t>0
, then
J_{2}=x_{n}x_{n-1}(x_{i_{1}},\dots,x_{i_{t}})I(G_{2})^{[k-1]}\neq(0)
, and we have
\operatorname{reg}(J_{2})=\operatorname{reg}(I(G_{2})^{[k-1]})+3
. Since
\operatorname{reg}(I(G_{3})^{[k-1]})\leq\operatorname{reg}(I(G_{2})^{[k-1]})
,

\displaystyle\operatorname{reg}(J)

\displaystyle=\max\{\operatorname{reg}(I(G_{3})^{[k]})+2,\operatorname{reg}(I(G_{3})^{[k-1]})+3,\operatorname{reg}(I(G_{2})^{[k-1]})+3\}

(3.3)

\displaystyle=\max\{\operatorname{reg}(I(G_{3})^{[k]})+2,\operatorname{reg}(I(G_{2})^{[k-1]})+3\}.

Hence, combining (
3.1
) with (
3.3
), the assertion follows.

Case 2.
Suppose
t=0
, then
J_{2}=(0)
and
J=J_{1}
. Combining (
3.2
) with (
3.1
), the assertion follows because
\operatorname{reg}(I(G_{3})^{[k-1]})\leq\operatorname{reg}(I(G_{2})^{[k-1]})
.

∎

We need the following lemmata proved in
[
10
]
.

Lemma 3.4
.

[
10
, Lemma 15]

Let
G
be a forest and let
2\leq k\leq\nu(G)
. Then

\textup{aim}(G,k)\leq\textup{aim}(G,k-1)+1.

Lemma 3.5
.

[
10
, Lemma 23]

Let
G
be a forest and let
\{w,v\}
be a distant edge of
G
. Then, for all
2\leq k\leq\nu(G)
,

\textup{aim}(G\setminus\{w,v\},k-1)+1\leq\textup{aim}(G,k).

We are in the position to prove Erey and Hibi conjecture
[
10
, Conjecture 31]
.

Theorem 3.6
.

Let
G
be a forest. Then

\operatorname{reg}(I(G)^{[k]})=\textup{aim}(G,k)+k,\ \ \ \text{for}\ k=1,\dots,\nu(G).

Proof.

As in the proof of Theorem
2.1
, let
G_{1},\dots,G_{c}
be the connected components of
G
, and let
m=\max_{i}|V(G_{i})|
.

If
m=2
, up to a relabeling,
I(G)=(x_{1}x_{2},x_{3}x_{4},\dots,x_{n-1}x_{n})
. Since
M=\{\{1,2\},\{3,4\},\dots,\{n-1,n\}\}
is a
k
-admissible matching for all
k=1,\dots,\nu(G)
, we have
\textup{aim}(G,k)=\frac{n}{2}
for all
k=1,\dots,\nu(G)
, and
\operatorname{indm}(G)=\nu(G)=\frac{n}{2}
. In this case, the equality
\operatorname{reg}(I(G)^{[k]})=\textup{aim}(G,k)+k
follows from
[
10
, Proposition 30]
.

Suppose
m>2
. We proceed by induction on
|V(G)|\geq 3
. We assume
\nu(G)\geq 3
. Indeed,
\operatorname{reg}(I(G)^{[1]})=\textup{aim}(G,1)+1
and
\operatorname{reg}(I(G)^{[\nu(G)]})=\textup{aim}(G,\nu(G))+\nu(G)=2\nu(G)
have already been proved in
[
2
, Theorem 4.7]
and
[
3
, Theorem 5.1]
(see also
[
10
]
). So, if
\nu(G)\leq 2
, there is nothing to prove. Thus, we may assume Setup
2.3
. Then, Corollary
3.3
together with the inductive hypothesis yield

\displaystyle\operatorname{reg}(I(G)^{[k]})

\displaystyle=\max\{\operatorname{reg}(I(G_{1})^{[k]}),\operatorname{reg}(I(G_{2})^{[k-1]})+2,\operatorname{reg}(I(G_{3})^{[k]})+1\}

\displaystyle=\max\{\textup{aim}(G_{1},k)+k,\textup{aim}(G_{2},k-1)+k+1,\textup{aim}(G_{3},k)+k+1\}.

To prove the assertion, it is enough to show that

\textup{aim}(G,k)=\max\{\textup{aim}(G_{1},k),\textup{aim}(G_{2},k-1)+1,\textup{aim}(G_{3},k)+1\}.

(3.4)

Firstly, we prove that

\textup{aim}(G,k)\geq\max\{\textup{aim}(G_{1},k),\textup{aim}(G_{2},k-1)+1,\textup{aim}(G_{3},k)+1\}.

(3.5)

Indeed,
\textup{aim}(G,k)\geq\textup{aim}(G_{1},k)
by Remark
3.2
. Moreover, by Lemma
3.5
it follows that
\textup{aim}(G,k)\geq\textup{aim}(G_{2},k-1)+1
, because
G_{2}=G\setminus\{n-1,n\}
and
\{n-1,n\}
is a distant edge. Finally, we prove
\textup{aim}(G,k)\geq\textup{aim}(G_{3},k)+1
. Indeed, let
M
be a
k
-admissible matching of
G_{3}
with
|M|=\textup{aim}(G_{3},k)
. Then
M=M_{1}\cup\dots\cup M_{r}
with
|M_{1}|+\dots+|M_{r}|\leq r+k-1
as in Definition
3.1
. Note that
\{n-1,n\}
forms a gap with all
e\in E(G_{3})
. Thus, setting
M^{\prime}=M\cup\{\{n-1,n\}\}
we have that
M^{\prime}
is a
k
-admissible matching of
G
, because
M^{\prime}
admits the
k
-admissible partition
M_{1}\cup\dots\cup M_{r}\cup\{\{n-1,n\}\}
since

|M_{1}|+\dots+|M_{r}|+|\{\{n-1,n\}\}|\leq(r+1)+k-1.

This shows that
\textup{aim}(G,k)\geq\textup{aim}(G_{3},k)+1
.

Now we show that (
3.4
) holds. If
\textup{aim}(G,k)=\textup{aim}(G_{2},k-1)+1
, then equality follows by (
3.5
). Suppose now that
\textup{aim}(G,k)>\textup{aim}(G_{2},k-1)+1
. By Lemma
3.4
,
\textup{aim}(G_{2},k-1)+1\geq\textup{aim}(G_{2},k)
. Hence
\textup{aim}(G,k)>\textup{aim}(G_{2},k)
. So we can find a
k
-admissible matching
M
of
G
with
|M|=\textup{aim}(G,k)>\textup{aim}(G_{2},k)
such that
M=M_{1}\cup\dots\cup M_{r}
is a
k
-admissible partition. Thus
n-1\in V(M)
, otherwise
M
is also a
k
-admissible matching of
G_{2}
, which is impossible. One of the following possibilities occurs:
\{n-1,n-2\}\in M
,
\{n-1,n\}\in M
or
\{n-1,i_{j}\}\in M
,
j\in[t]
.

Suppose
\{n-1,n-2\}\in M
, up to relabeling we may assume
\{n-1,n-2\}\in M_{1}
. Set
M^{\prime}=M\setminus\{\{n-1,n-2\}\}
. We claim that
M_{1}
is a singleton. If not, then

|M_{1}\setminus\{\{n-1,n-2\}\}|+|M_{2}|+\dots+|M_{r}|=|M|-1\leq r+(k-1)-1.

By the definition of admissible
(k-1)
-matching, the above inequality implies that
\textup{aim}(G_{3},k-1)\geq|M|-1
. Hence,
\textup{aim}(G_{2},k-1)+1\geq\textup{aim}(G_{3},k-1)+1\geq|M|=\textup{aim}(G,k)
, against our assumption.

Hence,
M_{1}=\{\{n-1,n-2\}\}
and
M^{\prime}
is a
k
-admissible matching of
G_{3}
, because
|M_{2}|+\dots+|M_{r}|\leq(r-1)+k-1
. Thus
\textup{aim}(G_{3},k)+1\geq\textup{aim}(G,k)
. By (
3.5
),
\textup{aim}(G,k)\geq\textup{aim}(G_{3},k)+1
. Hence
\textup{aim}(G,k)=\textup{aim}(G_{3},k)+1
and (
3.4
) holds.

The cases
\{n-1,n\}\in M
,
\{n-1,i_{j}\}\in M
,
j\in[t]
, can be treated similarly. Indeed,
N_{G}(n-1)=\{i_{1},\dots,i_{t},n-2,n\}
with
t\geq 0
, as stated in Setup
2.3
.

∎
