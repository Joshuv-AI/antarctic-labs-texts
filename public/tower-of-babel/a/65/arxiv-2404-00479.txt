Once a basic theory of existence and uniqueness is established, the natural question to be addressed is

Which initial data produce bounded solutions?

After answering to this question, we shall address the next natural one, that is space-time regularity:

In which case bounded solutions are also continuous or even smoother?

Are there classical solutions?

These questions are hard to investigate for the equation at hand, since the diffusion operator is of order zero, hence, a priori no regularization in space has to be expected. Nonetheless, we can prove that integrable data produce bounded solutions when

In this paper we answer the above questions, by providing a quite complete theory that besides settling some existence, uniqueness and contractivity estimates in

The linear case.
When
p=2
solutions to the Cauchy Problem (
C

can be considered a “black sheep” among Levy operators. In Theorem 1.4 and Lemma 1.6 of
[

]

(see also

where

W(x,t):=\int_{0}^{t}e^{-(t-\tau)}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}u(y,\tau)J(x-y)dy\,d\tau\,.

On the one hand, it is quite clear that a priori time regularity can improve, on the other hand, it is also quite clear that the spatial regularity of

In this case, we are able to prove an

Our method has the advantage of being completely local, hence ready to be used also in the case of problems posed on bounded domains with different boundary conditions, where Fourier analysis gets even more involved. It also lay a path to attack the same issues in the more delicate nonlinear case.

The nonlinear case. Regularity in space and time.
When
p\neq 2
, a priori, nothing can be expected to improve spatial regularity, but there is hope for the time regularity, inspired by the linear case. Let us provide a short overview of our new main results.

•

When

We then show that solutions are regular in time, namely

As we have already discussed, when

Local regularity estimates for nonlocal problems and evolution of singularities.

1.1.
The case of bounded domains.

We also consider problems posed in bounded open sets
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and show analogous results: existence, uniqueness, contractivity and higher regularity (in the interior, we do not study the boundary regularity). The proofs in this case are essentially the same as in the Cauchy problem, in particular we stress that the proofs of the regularity estimates are “local in essence”, hence they can be adapted to these cases as well (under some extra assumptions on
J
and
\Omega
in the Neumann case), as we shall explain in Section

More specifically, given
u_{0}\in\mathrm{L}^{q}(\Omega)
with
q\in[1,\infty]
we consider the homogeneous

(D)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}u_{t}(x,t)=\mathcal{L}_{p}u(x,t),&x\in\Omega,t>0,\\
u(x,t)=0,&x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\Omega,t>0,\\
u(x,0)=u_{0}(x),&x\in\Omega.\end{cases}

Sometimes it is useful to rewrite this problem as follows

\displaystyle\begin{cases}u_{t}(x,t)=\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}_{p,\Omega}u(x,t),&x\in\mathbb{R}^{n},t>0,\\
u(x,0)=u_{0}(x),&x\in\Omega,\end{cases}

where, writing

\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}_{p,\Omega}u(x,t):=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}J(x-y)|\tilde{u}(y,t)-u(x,t)|^{p-2}(\tilde{u}(y,t)-u(x,t))\,dy.

We consider next the homogeneous

(N)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}u_{t}(x,t)=\mathcal{L}_{p,\Omega}u(x,t),&x\in\Omega,t\in(0,\infty),\\

\mathcal{L}_{p,\Omega}u(x,t):=\int_{\Omega}J(x-y)|u(y,t)-u(x,t)|^{p-2}(u(y,t)-u(x,t))\,dy.

1.2.
Relevance of the model and possible applications.

The model under consideration, has a simple probabilistic interpretation when
p=2
, being a zero-order Levy operator. For instance, if we define
u(x,t)
to be the density of a population at point
x
and at time
t
, and
J(x-y)
as the probability of jumping from location
y
to location
x
, then the convolution
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}J(x-y)u(y,t)\,dy
represents the rate at which individuals arrive at
x
from other locations. Similarly,
-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}J(x-y)u(x,t)\,dy
represents the rate at which individuals leave location
x
to travel to other sites. The absence of external sources leads to the density
u
satisfying (
C
) (with
p=2
). When
p\neq 2
, we shall think that the jumping probability depends on the density itself, and the model becomes more involved. Still, in the case of classical (normalized)
p
-Laplacian or of the fractional one, there are probabilistic interpretations in terms of tug-of-war-like games, see
[
27
,
61
,
65
]
. When the diffusion occurs in an open bounded set
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and there is no flux of individuals across the boundary, we consider the homogeneous Neumann equation (
N
). Finally, when diffusion occurs in the whole
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
u
is assumed to vanish outside
\Omega
, this means that any individuals leaving
\Omega
shall die, which gives rise to the homogeneous Dirichlet problem (
D
). For further information, we refer to
[
3
,
4
,
6
,
7
,
46
,
66
]
and the references therein.

These type of nonlocal problems were extensively studied in the last decade. The simplest linear model (i.e.
p=2
) and variations of it, have been widely used to model diffusion processes in many contexts
[
8
,
9
]
, phase transitions
[

,

]
, image processing
[

,

]
, etc. See also
[

,

,

]
.

Connection with the classical
p
-Laplacian and its fractional variants.

One of the reasons why we call these operators “zero-order
p
-Laplacians” is the following. Consider the family of rescaled kernels:

J_{\varepsilon}(x)=\frac{c_{J,p}}{\varepsilon^{n+p}}J\left(\frac{x}{\varepsilon}\right)

for some explicit
c_{J,p}>0
. In
[

]
it is shown (under some assumptions on the kernel

) that solutions to the Cauchy problem with the nonlocal operators
\mathcal{L}_{J_{\varepsilon}}u
converge in
\mathrm{L}^{\infty}((0,\infty):\mathrm{L}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))
to solutions to the Cauchy problem for the classical
p
-Laplacian operator
\Delta_{p}u=\nabla\cdot(|\nabla u|^{p-2}\nabla u)
. Also, in
[
35
]
the authors prove that also the operator with kernel
J_{\varepsilon}
converge to the
p
-Laplacian operator (not only the solution) in their natural topology. Similar results are obtained in
[
38
]
for the fractional
(s,p)
-Laplacian by considering the approximations with the kernel
J_{\varepsilon}(x)=c\,\varepsilon^{-(n+sp)}J(x/\varepsilon)
.

It is also worth noticing that our zero-order operators do not include directly numerical approximations for local or nonlocal
p
-Laplacians, see
[
36
,
37
,
38
]
, which would require the kernel

to be an atomic measure, and which is not directly admissible in our setting. But an important point is that many of our (new) ideas and techniques, which are based on elementary estimates, can be useful also in the numerical setting, and could lead to improved stability or consistency estimates (in a stronger localized version). On the one hand, the smoothing effects and the time regularity can easily be adapted to hold also in the numerical schemes, since they do not depend on the regularity of
J
. On the other hand, for instance in
[
37
,
38
]
, the authors show that solutions preserve the same global Hölder constant as the initial datum: we obtain something of a different nature, more local. Indeed, we show that any local modulus of continuity is “preserved”, in the sense that it can differ by a multiplicative constant (which we estimate precisely), but it stays in the same class: for instance if
u_{0}\in C^{\alpha}(B_{r_{0}}(x_{0}))
, then also
u(t)\in C^{\alpha}(B_{r_{0}}(x_{0}))
, and this class can change from ball to ball, but it is preserved in time (and the same happens for every other modulus). Contrarily to
[
37
,
38
]
, where only the “first” modulus of continuity is preserved, we also obtain the same results for higher derivatives (at least up to order

), something that would be extremely useful in numerical approximations and has its own interest. We refer to Section
1.4
for more details on the state of the art, related results and the ideas of the proof.

Remark 1.6

(
On the locality of the regularity estimates in space
)
.

We shall emphasize that even if the equation is nonlocal, the nature of our regularity results is local, and this has some important consequences, that we shall emphasize here.

(i)

Preservation of the local modulus of continuity.
A careful inspection of the proofs shows that indeed we have a stronger result: we preserve the local modulus of continuity, essentially in two ways.

•

On the one hand, assuming full regularity of the kernel
J
, we preserve any modulus of continuity of the initial datum and its derivatives:
let
J\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, and
\beta\in\mathbb{N}
and
D^{\beta}u(0,\cdot)\in C^{\omega}(K)
where
K\subset\subset\Omega
, then
D^{\beta}u(t,\cdot)\in C^{\omega}(K)
for all
t>0
, that is, for all
x_{1},x_{2}\in K
and
t>0
:

\begin{split}|D^{\beta}u(x_{1},t)-D^{\beta}u(x_{2},t)|&\leq A_{1}(t)|D^{\beta}u(x_{1},0)-D^{\beta}u(x_{2},0)|+A_{2}(t)|x_{1}-x_{2}|\\
&\leq\overline{C}(t)\max\left\{\omega(|x_{1}-x_{2}|)\,,\,|x_{1}-x_{2}|\right\}\leq\overline{C}(t)\omega(|x_{1}-x_{2}|)\,.\end{split}

•

On the other hand, if
J
is not regular, then the continuity modulus is the worst between the one of the data and the one of
J
. For instance:
let
J\in C^{\omega_{J}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, and
u(0,\cdot)\in C^{\omega}(K)
where
K\subset\subset\Omega
, then
u(t,\cdot)\in C^{\bar{\omega}}(K)
for all
t>0
, where

\bar{\omega}(|x_{1}-x_{2}|):=\max\left\{\omega(|x_{1}-x_{2}|)\,,\,\omega_{J}(|x_{1}-x_{2}|)\right\}

that is, for all
x_{1},x_{2}\in K
and
t>0
:

\begin{split}|D^{\beta}u(x_{1},t)-D^{\beta}u(x_{2},t)|&\leq A_{1}(t)|D^{\beta}u(x_{1},0)-D^{\beta}u(x_{2},0)|+A_{2}(t)\omega_{J}(|x_{1}-x_{2}|)\\
&\leq\overline{C}(t)\bar{\omega}(|x_{1}-x_{2}|)\,.\end{split}

(ii)

Singularities do not move in time, nor can be created along the flow.
We are dealing with bounded solutions, that may start from possibly unbounded initial data
u_{0}\in\mathrm{L}^{q}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
when
p>2
, or from bounded initial data when
p\in(1,2]
. In both cases, bounded solutions can only have discontinuities with bounded oscillation. Let us define the “singular set in the space variables”
\mathcal{S}[f]
of a
L^{1}_{\rm loc}
function
f
, as the closure of the complementary of the set where
f
is continuous, or, to be more precise, where
f
admits an
L^{1}_{\rm loc}
representative which is continuous). A consequence of the above estimates is that “singularities do not move in time”, or to be more precise, the singular set of
u(t)
will always be contained in the singular set of
u_{0}
:
\mathcal{S}[u(t)]\subseteq\mathcal{S}[u_{0}]
. This clearly follows by the fact that if
x_{0}\not\in\mathcal{S}[u_{0}]
then there exists an open ball
B_{r}(x_{0})
where
u_{0}\in C^{0}(B_{r}(x_{0}))
(indeed it will be in some
C^{\omega}(B_{r}(x_{0}))
for some
\omega
). Then, the conservation of the local continuity modulus ensures that
u(t)\in C^{0}(B_{r}(x_{0}))
for all
t>0
. This clearly implies that
\mathcal{S}[u(t)]\subseteq\mathcal{S}[u_{0}]
, as we claimed. Of course, this also implies that
new discontinuities cannot be created in future times
, or simply that

if
u_{0}\in C^{\omega}(B_{r}(x_{0}))
, then
u(t)\in C^{\omega}(B_{r}(x_{0}))
for all
t>0
.

A similar remark applies also to derivatives of arbitrary order.

(iii)

Application to other problems, different boundary conditions.
The last consequence of the locality of the regularity estimates is that essentially the same proof allows to treat different problems: they can be set in open domains of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and they may have different boundary conditions. The above regularity estimate would apply as “interior regularity estimates”. We shall see with full details what happens in two important cases, namely the Dirichlet and Neumann problems on bounded domains in what follows.

being
R(x)
the union of line segments emanating from
x
contained in
\Omega
. In this case, given
x\in\partial\Omega
, there exists
\eta>0
such that the cone
\Gamma(x)
is contained in the ball
B_{\eta}(x)\subset\supp J
with center in
x
and radius
\eta
. This yields

\int_{\Omega}J(x-y)\,dy\geq\int_{\Omega\cap B_{\eta}(x)}J(x-y)\,dy\geq\varepsilon_{\eta}\int_{\Gamma(x)}\,dy=\varepsilon_{\eta}|\Gamma(x)|\geq\varepsilon_{\eta}\delta>0

where we have used that
\inf\{J(z)\colon z\in B_{\eta}(x)\}\geq\varepsilon_{\eta}
for some
\varepsilon_{\eta}>0
, which is always true since
J
is continuous, nonnegative and
B_{\eta}(x)\subset\supp J
.

(ii)

The prototypical example of nonnegative radial kernel
J
with compact support is given by

J(z)=\mathsf{c}_{n,a,R}^{-1}(R-|z|)^{a},\qquad\text{ where }\mathsf{c}_{n,a,R}=n\omega_{n}R^{n+a}B(n,a+1)

with
a>0
,
\supp J=B_{R}(0)
and where
B
denotes the Beta function. In this case the constant
\kappa_{J,\Omega}
can be computed explicitly. Indeed, given
x\in\Omega

\displaystyle\int_{B_{R}(x)}J(x-y)\,dy

\displaystyle=\int_{B_{R}(0)}(1-|z|)^{a}\,dz=n\omega_{n}\int_{0}^{R}(1-r)^{a}r^{n-1}\,dr

\displaystyle=n\omega_{n}R^{n+a}\int_{0}^{1}(1-r)^{a}r^{n-1}\,dr=\mathsf{c}_{n,a,R},

being
\omega_{n}
the measure of the unit ball in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Proof of the results for Dirichlet and Neumann problems.

. The remaining proofs are presented in the same theorems as in the Cauchy case.

The asymptotic results of Theorem

are proved in Theorems

and

1.4.
Related results, novelties and main ideas of the proofs

In the local case there exist a huge literature about the celebrated p-Laplacian evolution equations, whose prototype is
u_{t}=\Delta_{p}(u)=\nabla\cdot(|\nabla u|^{p-2}\nabla u)
. Without any aim of completeness we quote here some related results: Local smoothing effect were known since the pioneering work of DiBenedetto
[
39
]
, with DeGiorgi method, Smoothing effects via nonlinear adaptation of Gross’ method were proven in the 2000’s,
see
[
29
,
18
]
, and via Moser iteration in
[
22
]
. There has been an intense work, and the state of the art of higher integrability estimates can be found in the more recent contributions
[
13
,
14
,
15
,
52
,
55
,
56
]
, even for a more general case of nonlinear evolution equations. We refer to
[
40
,
39
,
23
,
22
]
for a complete account of local and global Harnack inequalities. Higher regularity estimates, the maximum possible being
C^{1,\alpha}
regularity in space and
C^{1,\beta}
in time, were obtained for bounded solutions by DiBenedetto and Friedman, see the monograph
[
39
]
.

Our first objective is to establish the boundedness of solutions (
\mathrm{L}^{q}-\mathrm{L}^{\infty}
-smoothing effects), since this kind of estimate is the first step towards further regularity properties. The Moser iteration approach
[
63
,
64
]
is a standard method to obtain such results, but the quadratic form associated to the operator has to satisfy some Gagliardo-Nirenberg-Sobolev and Stroock-Varopoulos type inequalities. This has been used in the linear case, for a class of nonlocal operators including the standard fractional Laplacian
(-\Delta)^{s}
with
s\in(0,1)
in
[
42
,
43
,
47
,
48
,
67
]
.

An alternative to the Moser iteration is the Green function method introduced by Vazquez and the first author in
[
24
]
for the Dirichlet problem for Fractional Porous Medium Type Equation (FPME)
u_{t}=-\mathcal{L}u^{m}
, where
\mathcal{L}
is a linear nonlocal operator, typically a fracional Laplacian with Dirichlet boundary conditions (there are three possible different choices!). This method has been exploited in several directions in
[
21
,
20
,
25
,
16
]
for more general nonlinear nonlocal (degenerate and singular) diffusions in bounded domains and on the entire
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and even on manifolds
[
12
,
11
]
. The key point of this method is to have good estimates of the kernel of
(-\mathcal{L})^{-1}
, that is, the Green function of
\mathbb{G}_{-\mathcal{L}}
. This approach allows to obtain
\mathrm{L}^{1}
–
\mathrm{L}^{\infty}
smoothing results for a quite wide class of linear operators, including Levy operators and much more, see
[
16
]
for a complete account on the equivalences between: existence of green functions and heat kernels for
\mathcal{L}
, the validity of suitable GNS inequalities and smoothing effects for nonlocal PME-type equations. See also
[
17
]
where a comparison between the Moser iteration and the Green function method is made in the case of Fast Diffusion equations on bounded domains. Unfortunately, the Green function method seems not to be compatible with the structure of p-Laplacian operators: In the problem under consideration, the nonlinear nature of the operator makes the Green function method unsuitable for our purposes. On the other hand, the DeGiorgi-Nash-Moser iteration is flexible enough to be adapted to the fractional
p-
Laplacian case
(-\Delta_{p})^{s}
, with
s\in(0,1)
, see for instance
[
30
,
50
,
68
,
69
]
. For higher regularity estimates one can rely again on classical DeGiorgi iterations, see
[
39
]
, or also can use nonlinear potential estimates, see
[
41
,
55
,
56
,
57
,
58
,
59
]
.

Our problem presents extra difficulties, since the diffusion operator is both nonlinear and of order zero: first we observe that Gagliardo-Nirenberg-Sobolev type inequalities are not available in this setting, preventing us from implementing a Moser or DeGiorgi method. Also, we observe that it is not possible to have useful GNS for the nonlinear case. Indeed, if we had some GNS, say for
p>2
, these would imply GNS for the quadratic form of the linear operator (
p=2
), which are known to be equivalent to
\mathrm{L}^{q}-\mathrm{L}^{\infty}
-smoothing effects, which we know to be not true for the linear case (as we have seen above), see
[
16
]
. This may suggest that
\mathrm{L}^{q}-\mathrm{L}^{\infty}
-smoothing effects are simply not true in the nonlinear case. We surprisingly show the contrary.

The only result existing in literature which is comparable to our new smoothing effect, to the best of our knowledge, has been obtained for nonlocal porous medium type equations of “zero order”: unexpected smoothing effects of the form similar to (
1.3
) hold for merely integrable data, see
[
16
, Theorem 3.5]
. The proof exploits the dual equation (that in the present case we do not have), and the strict convexity of the nonlinearity. As already explained, the linear case (
p=2
here) does not satisfy smoothing effects. In our case, when
p>2
, we can show that merely integrable initial data produce bounded solutions, but we go way further: bounded solutions turn out to be smooth in time and even smooth (classical) in space when the kernel and the data allows it. We also perform a delicate analysis, where we compare the loss of regularity due to the “low regularity” of the kernel, versus the high regularity of the data, which is always preserved (at least) up to order
p
.

We propose here a new approach to regularity for nonlinear zero order operators, that exploits
the strict convexity and homogeneity of the nonlinearity
(that we have when
p>2
) together with
elementary numerical inequalities
. A key tool in our arguments, is the so-called
Bénilan-Crandall estimate
(time-monotonicity) that holds for nonnegative solutions:

u_{t}(x,t)\geq-\frac{u(x,t)}{(p-2)t}\quad\text{ a.e. }x\text{ and }t>0.

This follows by comparison and time scaling. With this, we prove that solutions of the Cauchy, Neumann and Dirichlet problems corresponding to
u_{0}\in\mathrm{L}^{q}
,
1\leq q<\infty
are indeed bounded, and satisfy precise
\mathrm{L}^{q}
–
\mathrm{L}^{\infty}
smoothing estimates.

We shall give a flavour of the main ideas in our proofs in the simplest possible scenarios.

1.4.1.
Smoothing effects when
p>2

In this case, we want to sketch the proof of the smoothing effect (
1.3
) of Theorem
1.2
, that reads:
Let
u(x,t)
be a positive solution of (
C
) corresponding to the positive initial datum
u_{0}\in\mathrm{L}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, then we have that

(1.9)

\|u(t)\|_{\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\leq\frac{\mathsf{K}_{1}}{t^{\frac{1}{p-2}}}+\mathsf{K}_{2}\|u(t_{0})\|_{\mathrm{L}^{q}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\quad\text{ for all }0\leq t_{0}\leq t\,,

for some constants
\mathsf{K}_{1}
and
\mathsf{K}_{2}
.
Let us fix a time
t\in(0,T]
. The following inequality (Lemma
4.1
)

a^{p-1}-|a-b|^{p-2}(a-b)\leq(p-1)\max\{a^{p-2},b^{p-2}\}b,\qquad a,b>0,

applied to
a=u(x,t)
,
b=u(y,t)
together to the fact that
\|J\|_{\mathrm{L}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}=1
yields

u(x,t)^{p-1}+\mathcal{L}_{p}u(x,t)\leq(p-1)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}J(x-y)(u(x,t)^{p-1}+u(y,t)^{p-1})u(y,t)\,dy:=\mathcal{I}_{p,J}u(x,t).

Using the Bénilan-Crandall estimate and the fact that
u
is a solution, we obtain the inequality

-\frac{u(x,t)}{(p-2)t}\leq u_{t}(x,t)=\mathcal{L}_{p}u(x,t).

Then, the last two relations lead to the following:

u(x,t)^{p-1}\leq\frac{u(x,t)}{(p-2)t}+\mathcal{I}_{p,J}u(x,t).

Repeated application of Young’s inequality gives that for any
\varepsilon>0
there exists
\mathsf{c}_{p,J,\varepsilon}>0
such that

u(x,t)^{p-1}\leq\varepsilon u^{p-1}(x,t)+\mathsf{c}_{p,J,\varepsilon}\left(t^{-\frac{p-1}{p-2}}+\|u(t)\|_{\mathrm{L}^{p-1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}^{p-1}\right),

Choosing
\varepsilon
sufficiently small gives a
\mathrm{L}^{p-1}
-
\mathrm{L}^{\infty}
smoothing effect: we find an explicit
\mathsf{\bar{c}}_{J,p,\varepsilon}>0
such that

(1.10)

\|u(t)\|_{\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}^{p-1}\leq\mathsf{\bar{c}}_{p,J,\varepsilon}\left(t^{-\frac{p-1}{p-2}}+\|u(t)\|_{\mathrm{L}^{p-1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}^{p-1}\right).

This is the basic smoothing, which is a self-improving inequality: using again Young’s inequality, we obtain that

\|u(t)\|_{\mathrm{L}^{p-1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}^{p-1}\leq\varepsilon_{1}\|u(t)\|_{\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}^{p-1}+\mathsf{\tilde{c}}_{\varepsilon_{1},J,p}\|u(t)\|_{\mathrm{L}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}^{p-1},

where
\mathsf{\tilde{c}}_{\varepsilon_{1},J,p}>0
and
\varepsilon_{1}>0
. Choosing
\varepsilon_{1}
small and combining this inequality with (
1.10
) yields the
\mathrm{L}^{1}
–
\mathrm{L}^{\infty}
smoothing (
1.9
). ∎

Remark.
As already observed before, here
the smoothing surprisingly depends only on the strict convexity of the nonlinearity
, indeed, when
p=2
this result is false, see (
1.1
).

1.4.2.
Regularity of solutions

In this case, we want to sketch the proof of the regularity results in the simplest possible case, which is the Cauchy problem for the linear case
p=2
. This will provide the basic ideas of the proof of the nonlinear case
p>1
, which of course is technically much more involved. Once we have identified a class of bounded solutions, we would like to show that they possess indeed higher regularity in time and, that they preserve the initial modulus of continuity.

Let
u
be a solution of the Cauchy problem, with
u_{0}\in\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
.

\circ~

Higher regularity in time
. From the equation it is immediate the following estimate,

|u_{t}(x,t)|\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}J(x-y)|u(y,t)-u(x,t)|\,dy\leq 2\|u_{0}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}

which gives the boundedness of
u_{t}
as follows

(1.11)

\|u_{t}(t)\|_{\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\leq 2\|u_{0}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}.

We can bootstrap the argument: given positive numbers
t_{1}<t_{2}
, using (
1.11
) we get

\displaystyle|u_{t}(x,t_{2})-u_{t}(x,t_{1})|

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}J(x-z)|(u(z,t_{2})-u(z,t_{1}))-(u(x,t_{2})-u(x,t_{1}))|\,dz

\displaystyle\leq 2|t_{1}-t_{2}|\|u_{t}(t)\|_{\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}J(x-z)\,dz\leq 4|t_{1}-t_{2}|\|u_{0}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\,.

Hence
u_{t}(x,\cdot)\in C_{t}^{0,1}([0,\infty))
. Moreover, using mean value theorem and (
1.11
) we get

(1.12)

\displaystyle\begin{split}u_{tt}(x,t)&=\lim_{h\to 0^{+}}\frac{u_{t}(x,t+h)-u_{t}(x,t)}{h}\\
&=\lim_{h\to 0^{+}}\frac{1}{h}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}J(x-y)\left\{(u(y,t+h)-u(y,t))-(u(x,t+h)-u(x,t))\right\}\,dy\\
&=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}J(x-y)\left\{u_{t}(y,t_{1}^{*})-u_{t}(x,t_{2}^{*}))\right\}\,dy\\
&\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}J(x-y)\left\{\|u_{t}(t_{1}^{*})\|_{\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}+\|u_{t}(t_{2}^{*})\|_{\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\right\}\,dy\leq 4\|u_{0}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\end{split}

where
t_{i}^{*}\in(t_{i},t_{i}+h)
,
i=1,2
, and therefore

(1.13)

\|u_{tt}(t)\|_{\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\leq 4\|u_{0}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}.

We can bootstrap a second time: using (
1.12
), (
1.13
) and the mean value theorem we can write

\displaystyle u_{tt}(x,t_{1})-u_{tt}(x,t_{2})

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}J(x-y)\left\{(u_{t}(y,t_{1,1}^{*})-u_{t}(y,t_{2,1}^{*}))-(u_{t}(x,t_{1,2}^{*})-u_{t}(x,t_{2,2}^{*}))\right\}\,dy

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}J(x-y)\{u_{tt}(y,\tau_{1})|t_{1,1}^{*}-t_{2,1}^{*}|-u_{tt}(y,\tau_{2})|t_{1,2}^{*}-t_{2,2}^{*}|\}\,dy

\displaystyle\leq 8|t_{1}-t_{2}|\|u_{0}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}

where
\tau_{i}\in(t^{*}_{i,1},t^{*}_{i,2})
,
i=1,2
, which gives that
u_{tt}\in C^{0,1}_{t}([0,\infty))
.

We can repeat the argument inductively, to get that for any
k\in\mathbb{N}
it holds

\|\partial_{t}^{k}u(t)\|_{\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\leq 2^{k}\|u_{0}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\qquad\text{ and }\qquad\partial_{t}^{k}u(x,\cdot)\in C^{0,1}_{t}([0,\infty)).

As a consequence, a bounded solution
u
of the Cauchy problem in the linear case possesses arbitrary continuous time derivatives, that is,

u(x,\cdot)\in C^{\infty}([0,\infty))

for almost all
x\in\Omega
. Here it clearly appears that solutions are analytic in time, since the above estimates clearly implies that

\|\partial_{t}^{k}u(t)\|_{\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\leq M_{0}\,k!\,,\qquad\mbox{with }M_{0}:=2\|u_{0}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\,,

and the radius of convergence
r_{0}
of the Taylor series is uniform, indeed,
r_{0}=1\wedge t
.

\circ~

Conservation of the initial continuity
. To simplify the exposition, let us assume that the kernel
J
has a Hölder modulus of continuity: there exists
\alpha_{J}\in(0,1]
such that for all
x_{1},x_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
we have

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}|J(x_{1}-y)-J(x_{2}-y)|\,dy\leq|x_{1}-x_{2}|^{\alpha_{J}}.

Under these assumptions, we can ensure that the modulus of continuity of the initial datum is “preserved”, meaning that is does not change its class. Indeed, suppose that
u_{0}\in C^{0,\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
for some
\alpha\in(0,\alpha_{J}]
. For a solution
u
, expression (
1.1
) holds and the
\mathrm{L}^{\infty}
-norm is decreasing in time, so that

\displaystyle u(x_{1},t)-u(x_{2},t)

\displaystyle=e^{-t}(u_{0}(x_{1})-u_{0}(x_{2}))+\int_{0}^{t}e^{-\tau}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}u(y,\tau)(J(x_{1}-y)-J(x_{2}-y))dy\,d\tau

\displaystyle\leq e^{-t}|x_{1}-x_{2}|^{\alpha}+\|u_{0}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}|x_{1}-x_{2}|^{\alpha_{J}}(1-e^{-t}).

As a consequence, since
\alpha\leq\alpha_{J}\leq 1
, we have that
u(\cdot,t)\in C^{0,\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
(the same as
u_{0}
) and we have

\frac{|u(x_{1},t)-u(x_{2},t)|}{|x_{1}-x_{2}|^{\alpha}}\leq e^{-t}+(1-e^{-t})\|u_{0}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}.

Indeed we show a finer result: the same proof allows to show that for all
t>0

the solution
u(\cdot,t)
preserves the worst modulus of continuity between the initial data and the kernel
J
,
indeed, if
\alpha_{J}<\alpha
the above estimate would hold with
\alpha_{J}
.

Remark.
Also, the local nature of this estimates allows to deduce easily that: initial discontinuity points do not move in time, and no new discontinuities are created.

3.
Properties of solutions

We establish some useful yet basic properties of solutions in this section. We provide a general result about existence and uniqueness of solutions, which complements the previous ones
[
4

. Once existence in ensured, we prove time-monotonicity estimates for these solutions, often called Benilan-Crandall estimates

, that constitute a key ingredient in the proof of the smoothing effects. The time-monotonicity estimates are a consequence of the homogeneity of the nonlinearity, together with a weak comparison principle, that we also prove in this Section. Indeed, we prove T-contractive estimates (which imply weak comparison) in

Remark 3.2
.

The diffusion operator
\mathcal{L}_{p}
is of order zero (no weak derivatives involved, not even fractional) hence the concept of solution is particularly simple: the equation is satisfied almost everywhere in space and time. In the theory of nonlinear parabolic PDEs, this usually corresponds to the class of the so-called
strong solutions
(i.e.
u_{t}
is a
\mathrm{L}^{q}
function, a regular distribution).

The difference between weak, mild, strong and classical solutions, usually a technical obstacle in nonlinear parabolic theories, in this case is somehow easier: for instance,
classical solutions
are just solutions according to the above definition, which are
C^{1}
in time and
C^{0}
in space.

The drawback is that we do not have useful functional inequalities (of Sobolev type for instance) naturally associated to the operator, hence is it is not possible, to the best of our knowledge, to prove regularity results through a DeGiorgi-Nash-Moser approach.

Note that for a given subset
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
1\leq q\leq\infty
, we have
W^{1,1}(0,T;\mathrm{L}^{1}(\Omega))\subset AC([0,T];\mathrm{L}^{q}(\Omega))
. Moreover, if
w\in W^{1,1}(0,T;\mathrm{L}^{q}(\Omega))
, then
w
is almost everywhere equal to a function that is absolutely continuous on
[0,T]
with values in
\mathrm{L}^{q}(\Omega)
. In particular,
u

Theorem 3.4

(Existence and uniqueness)
.

Suppose
p>2
and let
u_{0}\in\mathrm{L}^{q}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
with
q\in[1,\infty)
. Then for any
T>0
there exists a unique solution of (
C
). When
p\in(1,2]
then we can take
u_{0}\in\mathrm{L}^{q}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
with
q\in\{2,p\}
.

Suppose that
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is an open and bounded set and
u_{0}\in\mathrm{L}^{q}(\Omega)
with
q\in[1,\infty]
. Then for any
T>0
there exists a unique solution of (
D
). If
\Omega
additionally satisfies (

H
J

), then there exists a unique solution of (
N
). When
p\in(1,2]
we can take
u_{0}\in\mathrm{L}^{p}(\Omega)
.

Proof.

We will prove the result for equation (
C
). Using similar arguments, we can also establish the same result for the Neumann and Dirichlet problems. Note the in these problem we can approximate the initial data with simple functions when
u_{0}\in\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(\Omega)
.

\circ~
Step 1
. Let us prove first that for every initial datum
u_{0}\in\mathrm{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and all
p>1
, there exists a unique gradient flow solution
u\in\mathrm{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
of (
C
).

Consider the energy functional
\mathcal{I}_{p}\colon\mathrm{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\to[0,\infty]
defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{p}(u):=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{p}\mathcal{E}_{p}(u,u)&\quad\text{ if }u\in\mathrm{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\\
+\infty&\quad\text{ otherwise},\end{cases}

which is convex and lower semicontinuous, moreover, its gradient flow coincides with the equation

u_{t}(x,t)=\mathcal{L}_{p}u(x,t),\qquad\mbox{for a.e. $x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}$, and all $t>0$.}

Let us prove that
u\in\mathrm{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
is a gradient flow solution of (
C
) by using the equivalence with EVI solutions stated in Proposition
A.4
. Indeed, suppose that
w\in\mathrm{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. By applying Proposition
3.9
and Young’s inequality, we obtain the following inequality for
t>0
:

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}

\displaystyle(u(x,t)-w(x))\mathcal{L}_{p}u(x,t)\,dx=-\mathcal{E}_{p}(u(x,t),u(x,t))+\mathcal{E}_{p}(u(x,t),w(x))

\displaystyle\leq-\mathcal{E}_{p}(u(x,t),u(x,t))

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{1}{2}\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}}J(x-y)^{\frac{p-1}{p}}|u(x,t)-u(y,t)|^{p-1}J(x-y)^{\frac{1}{p}}|w(x)-w(y)|\,dxdy

\displaystyle\leq-\mathcal{E}_{p}(u(x,t),u(x,t))+\frac{1}{p}\mathcal{E}_{p}(w(x),w(x))+\frac{p-1}{p}\mathcal{E}_{p}(u(x,t),u(x,t))

\displaystyle=\mathcal{I}_{p}(w)-\mathcal{I}_{p}(u),

from which we deduce that

\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}\|u(t)-w\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}^{2}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(u(x,t)-w(x))\mathcal{L}_{p}(u)\,dx\leq\mathcal{I}_{p}(w)-\mathcal{I}_{p}(u),

and hence the curve
u(\cdot,t)
satisfies (
A.1
).

Therefore, due to the Brezis-Komura Theorem
A.2
, the gradient flow solution
u(\cdot,t)
is unique and satisfies the contractivity property

\|u(t)-v(t)\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\leq\|u_{0}-v_{0}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\quad\text{for all }t>0,

where
u
and
v
are gradient flow solutions of (
C
) corresponding to the initial data
u_{0},v_{0}\in\mathrm{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
.

\circ~
Step 2
. We extend the existence and uniqueness of solution for more general initial data.

Given
u_{0}\in\mathrm{L}^{q}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
with
q\in[1,\infty)
(when
p>2
) or
u_{0}\in\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
in the case
p\in(1,2]
, let
\{\tilde{u}_{0,k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\mathrm{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
be a sequence of simple functions such that
\tilde{u}_{0,k}\to u_{0}
strongly in
\mathrm{L}^{q}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. Consider the monotone sequence
\{u_{0,k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
given by

u_{0,k}:=\min\{k,\tilde{u}_{0,k}\}\chi_{B_{k}(0)}.

Then, as
k\to\infty
we have that
u_{0,k}(x)\nearrow u_{0}(x)
for
a.e.

x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
t>0
and
u_{0,k}\to u_{0}
strongly in
\mathrm{L}^{q}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. Moreover, for each
k\in\mathbb{N}

(3.1)

\|u_{0,k}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{q}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\leq\|u_{0}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{q}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}.

Due to Step 1, for each
k\in\mathbb{N}
there exists a gradient flow solution
u_{k}(t)\in\mathrm{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
of (
C
) that corresponds to the initial datum
u_{0,k}
. According to Proposition
3.7
,
\{u_{k}(t)\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
is a monotone sequence, so its pointwise limit always exists and then we can define the candidate to limit solution as

(3.2)

u(x,t):=\liminf_{k\to\infty}u_{k}(x,t).

Observe that
\{u_{k}(t)\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
is a Cauchy sequence in
\mathrm{L}^{q}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
: by using Proposition
3.6
we have that

\displaystyle\|u_{k}(t)-u_{m}(\cdot,t)\|_{\mathrm{L}^{q}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}

\displaystyle\leq\|u_{0,k}-u_{0,m}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{q}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}

\displaystyle\leq\|u_{0,k}-u_{0}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{q}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}+\|u_{0,m}-u_{0}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{q}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\to 0\;\text{ as }m,k\to\infty.

Therefore
u_{k}(t)\to u(t)
strongly in
\mathrm{L}^{q}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
for any
t>0
.

We will now show that this limit function is a solution of (
C
) that corresponds to the initial datum
u_{0}
. For
a.e.

x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
t>0
let us denote

\displaystyle I(x,t)

\displaystyle:=\lim_{k\to\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}f_{k}(x,y,t)\,dy,\quad f_{k}(x,y,t):=J(x-y)|u_{k}(y,t)-u_{k}(x,t)|^{p-2}(u_{k}(y,t)-u_{k}(x,t)),

\displaystyle v(x,t)

\displaystyle:=\lim_{k\to\infty}(u_{k})_{t}(x,t).

Due to (
3.2
) we have the following pointwise limit:

\lim_{k\to\infty}f_{k}(x,y,t)=f(x,y,t):=J(x-y)|u(y,t)-u(x,t)|^{p-2}(u(y,t)-u(x,t)).

Moreover, from Theorem
4.2
and (
3.1
) we get that

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}|f_{k}|\,dy\leq 2^{p}\|u_{k}(t)\|^{p-1}_{\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}J(x-y)\,dy\leq\begin{cases}\mathsf{c}_{p,q,J}\left(t^{-\frac{1}{p-2}}+\|u_{0}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{q}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\right)^{p-1}&\text{ when }p>2\\
2^{p}\|u_{0}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}^{p-1}&\text{ when }p\in(1,2],\end{cases}

where
\mathsf{c}_{p,q,J}
is a constant independent of
k
. As a consequence, by the Dominated Convergence Theorem we get that

I(x,t)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}f(x,y,t)\,dy=\mathcal{L}_{p}u(x,t)\qquad\text{ a.e. }x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\,\text{ and }t>0.

Let us identify the function
v
. First, observe that by the lower semicontinuity of the norm, Theorem
4.5
and (
3.1
),
v
is uniformly bounded for any

, indeed,

\displaystyle\|v(t)\|_{\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\leq\liminf_{k\to\infty}\|(u_{k})_{t}(\cdot,t)\|_{\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\leq\begin{cases}\mathsf{c}_{p,q,J}\left(t^{-\frac{1}{p-2}}+\|u_{0}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{q}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\right)^{p-1}&\text{ when }p>2\\
2^{p}\|u_{0}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}^{p-1}&\text{ when }p\in(1,2].\end{cases}

Moreover,
\{(u_{k})_{t}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
is integrable as function of the time: from Proposition
4.5
, for
t,h>0
we have that

(3.3)

\int_{t}^{t+h}|(u_{k})_{t}(\cdot,\tau)|\,d\tau\leq 2^{p}\int_{t}^{t+h}\|u(t_{0})\|_{\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}^{p-1}\,d\tau<\mathsf{c}

where
t>0
and
\mathsf{c}
is independent of
k
. We write now the difference quotient for
u_{k}
in terms of a Steklov average as

\frac{u_{k}(x,t+h)-u_{k}(x,t)}{h}=\frac{1}{h}\int_{t}^{t+h}(u_{k})_{t}(x,\tau)\,d\tau,\qquad\text{ a.e. }x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\text{ and }t>0.

Using (
3.2
), (
3.3
), and the Dominated Convergence Theorem, as
k\to\infty
we get

\frac{u(x,t+h)-u(x,t)}{h}=\frac{1}{h}\int_{t}^{t+h}v(x,\tau)\,d\tau,\qquad\text{ a.e. }x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\text{ and }t>0.

Finally, as
h\to 0^{+}
gives
u_{t}=v
. Hence,
u
solves
u_{t}(x,t)=\mathcal{L}_{p}u(x,t)
for
a.e.

x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
t>0
, with initial datum
u(x,0)=u_{0}(x)
,
a.e.

x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, that is,
u
is a solution of (
C
).
∎

Proposition 3.5

(Theorems 6.2, 6.24 and 6.37 in
[

)
.

Given
p>1
, let
u
and

be solutions in

to (
C
) with initial data
u_{0},v_{0}\in\mathrm{L}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})

(3.4)

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(u(x,t)-v(x,t))^{+}\,dx\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(u_{0}(x)-v_{0}(x))^{+}\,dx\quad\forall\in[0,T].

\|u(t)-v(t)\|_{\mathrm{L}^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\leq\|u_{0}-v_{0}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}
for all

.

Given an open bounded set

, the same contraction holds for solutions

u
and
v
in

We extend the above result to initial data in
\mathrm{L}^{q}
for

.

Proposition 3.6

(
\mathrm{L}^{q}
-contractivity)
.

Given
p>1
, let
u,v
be solutions of (
C
) corresponding to the initial data
u_{0},v_{0}\in\mathrm{L}^{q}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
with
1\leq q\leq\infty
. Then

\|u(t)-v(t)\|_{\mathrm{L}^{q}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\leq\|u(t_{0})-v(t_{0})\|_{\mathrm{L}^{q}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\leq\|u_{0}-v_{0}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{q}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}

for all
0<t_{0}\leq t\leq T
.

The same holds for solutions of (
D
) and (
N
) when
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is open and bounded.

Proof.

We will prove the result for solutions of (
C
). A similar reasoning can be applied for solutions of (
D
) and (
N
) and when
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is open and bounded.

Suppose we are given solutions
u
and
v
of (
C
) with initial data
u_{0},v_{0}\in\mathrm{L}^{q}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, where
1\leq q\leq\infty
. First, we assume that
1<q<\infty
and we obtain the following:

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}

\displaystyle\|u(t)-v(t)\|_{\mathrm{L}^{q}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}^{q}=q\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}L_{q}(u(x,t)-v(x,t))(u_{t}(x,t)-v_{t}(x,t))\,dx

\displaystyle=-q\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}}J(x-y)L_{q}(u(x,t)-v(x,t))G_{p}(u,v)\,dxdy

\displaystyle=q\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}}J(x-y)L_{q}(u(y,t)-v(y,t))G_{p}(u,v)\,dxdy

where we interchanged the variables
x
and
y
and used the symmetry of
J
. Here we have denoted

G_{p}(u,v):=L_{p}(u(x,t)-u(y,t)))-L_{p}(v(x,t)-v(y,t)).

Therefore, denoting
H_{q}(u,v):=L_{q}(u(x,t)-v(x,t))-L_{q}(u(y,t)-v(y,t))
we get

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}

\displaystyle\|u(t)-v(t)\|_{\mathrm{L}^{q}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}^{q}=-\frac{q}{2}\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}}J(x-y)H_{q}(u,v)G_{p}(u,v)\,dxdy

We denote
A=u(x,t)-u(y,t)
,
B=v(x,t)-v(y,t)
,
a=u(x,t)-v(x,t)
,
b=u(y,t)-v(y,t)
. Since
A-B=a-b
, when
A=B
or
a=b
, we have that
\frac{d}{dt}\|u(t)-v(t)\|_{\mathrm{L}^{q}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}=0
.

Consider now the case
A\neq B
and
a\neq b
. We use the following well-known numerical inequality for
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{R}
and
r>1
:

\displaystyle(|\alpha|^{r-2}\alpha-|\beta|^{r-2})(\alpha-\beta)\geq\begin{cases}\mathsf{c}_{r}|\alpha-\beta|^{r}&\text{ if }r\geq 2\\
\mathsf{c}_{r}\frac{|\alpha-\beta|^{2}}{|\alpha|^{2-r}+|\beta|^{2-r}}&\text{ if }1<r<2\\
\end{cases}

where
\mathsf{c}_{r}\sim(r-1)
. See Lemma 4.4 in
[
39
]
and Appendix A.3 in
[
22
]
for a proof of it. When
p,q\geq 2

H_{q}(a,b)(a-b)\geq\mathsf{c}_{q}|a-b|^{q}>0,\quad G_{p}(A,B)(A-B)\geq\mathsf{c}_{p}|A-B|^{p}>0,

from where, since
|A-B|=|a-b|
we obtain that

-H_{q}(a,b)G_{p}(A,B)=-\frac{1}{|a-b|^{2}}H_{q}(a,b)(a-b)G_{p}(A,B)(A-B)<0,

from where, since
J\geq 0
, we get that
\frac{d}{dt}\|u(t)-v(t)\|_{\mathrm{L}^{q}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}^{q}\leq 0
. A similar argument can be applied when
1<p<2
or
1<q<2
to reach the same conclusion.

Moreover, the same estimate still true when
q\to 1
since for all
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{R}

\left(\frac{\alpha}{|\alpha|}-\frac{\beta}{|\beta|}\right)(\alpha-\beta)\geq\begin{cases}0&\text{ if }\alpha\text{ and }\beta\text{ have the same sign}\\
2|\alpha-\beta|&\text{ if }\alpha\text{ and }\beta\text{ have different sign},\end{cases}

and therefore
H_{1}(a,b)(a-b)\geq 0
.

Finally, from the previous computations, for any
p>1
and any
q\geq 1

\frac{d}{dt}\|u(t)-v(t)\|_{\mathrm{L}^{q}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}=-\frac{1}{2}\|u(t)-v(t)\|_{\mathrm{L}^{q}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}^{1-q}\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}}J(x-y)H_{q}(u,v)G_{p}(u,v)\,dxdy\leq 0

Letting
q\to\infty
in the above expression gives the result for the
L^{\infty}
norm.
∎

The following comparison principle is a consequence of the contractivity for the difference of solutions stated in (

).

Proposition 3.7

(Weak comparison)
.

Given
p>1
, let
u
and
v
be solutions in
[0,T]
of (
C
) with initial data
u_{0},v_{0}\in\mathrm{L}^{q}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
,
1\leq q<\infty
, respectively. Hence, if
u_{0}\leq v_{0}

a.e.
in
\Omega
, then
u(x,t)\leq v(x,t)

a.e.
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
t\in(0,T)
.

When
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is open and bounded, the same conclusion holds for solutions
u
and
v
in
[0,T]
of (
D
) and (
N
) with initial data
u_{0},v_{0}\in\mathrm{L}^{q}(\Omega)
,
1\leq q\leq\infty
, respectively.

Proof.

Let
u_{0},v_{0}\in\mathrm{L}^{q}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
with
1\leq q\leq\infty
be such that
u_{0}\leq v_{0}

a.e.

x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Consider the sequences
of approximations
\{u_{0,k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}},\{v_{k,0}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\in\mathrm{L}^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
given by
u_{0,k}:=\min\{u_{0},k\}\chi_{B_{k}(0)}
,
v_{0,k}:=\min\{v_{0},k\}\chi_{B_{k}(0)}
. Therefore,
\{u_{0,k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
and
\{v_{0,k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
are monotone sequences and

\displaystyle u_{0,k}\to u_{0},\quad v_{0,k}\to v_{0}\quad\text{strongly in }\mathrm{L}^{q}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\text{ and a.e. in }\mathbb{R}^{n},

\displaystyle\|u_{0,k}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{q}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\leq\|u_{0}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{q}(\mathbb{R}^{n})},\qquad\|v_{0,k}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{q}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\leq\|v_{0}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{q}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}.

Then, it follows that

(3.5)

\liminf_{k\to\infty}(u_{0,k}-v_{0,k})_{+}=(u_{0}-v_{0})_{+}=0,\quad\text{ and }\quad\|(u_{0,k}-v_{0,k})_{+}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{q}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\leq\|u_{0}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{q}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}+\|v_{0}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{q}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}.

Due to Theorem
3.4
, for each
k\in\mathbb{N}
there exist
u_{k}
,
v_{k}
solutions of (
N
) corresponding to the initial data
u_{0,k}
,
v_{0,k}
, respectively. By (
3.4
),
\{u_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
and
\{v_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
are also monotone, so for
a.e.

x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
t>0
we can define the limits

u(x,t):=\liminf_{k\to\infty}u_{k}(x,t),\qquad v(x,t):=\liminf_{k\to\infty}v_{k}(x,t),

and
u_{k}(t)\to u(t)
,
v_{k}(\cdot,t)\to v(t)
strongly in
\mathrm{L}^{q}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
for
t>0
. Moreover,

\liminf_{k\to\infty}(u_{k}(x,t)-v_{k}(x,t))_{+}=(u(x,t)-v(x,t))_{+}.

Then, by Fatou’s Lemma, (
3.4
), and the Dominated Convergence Theorem with (
3.5
), we get that

\displaystyle\|(u(t)-v(t))_{+}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\leq\liminf_{k\to\infty}\|(u_{k}(t)-v_{k}(\cdot,t))_{+}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\leq\lim_{k\to\infty}\|(u_{k,0}-v_{k,0})_{+}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}=0,

that is,
u(x,t)\leq v(x,t)

a.e.

x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
t>0
.

The proof for the Dirichlet and Neumann problems is similar.

Remark 3.8
.

The comparison principles available in the literature often require additional assumptions on the regularity of solutions and initial data (c.f. Corollary 2.9 in
[
4
]
and Theorem 2.1 in
[
44
]
). However, our Proposition
3.7
holds for solutions in
W^{1,1}(0,T;\mathrm{L}^{q}(\Omega))
and initial data in
\mathrm{L}^{q}(\Omega)
with
1\leq q\leq\infty
.

We recall a useful integration formula stated in Lemma 6.5 of
[
4
]
.

Proposition 3.9

(Integration by parts)
.

For every
u,v\in\mathrm{L}^{p}(\Omega)
,
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
, it holds that

-\int_{\Omega}v(x)\mathcal{L}_{p,\Omega}u(x)\,dx=\mathcal{E}_{p,\Omega}(u(x),v(x)).

We prove now a proposition in the spirit of the celebrated Bénilan-Crandall estimates
[
10
]
.

Proposition 3.10

(Benilan-Crandall type estimates when
p>2
)
.

Let
p>2
and let
u\geq 0
be a solution of (
C
). We have that

(3.6)

u_{t}(x,t)\geq-\frac{u(x,t)}{(p-2)t},\quad\emph{a.e.}x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\text{ and }t>0.

The same holds for solutions of (
D
) or of (
N
) when
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a bounded set.

Proof.

Take
\lambda\geq 1
and consider the rescaled (in time) solution to (
C
)

u_{\lambda}(x,t)=\lambda^{\frac{1}{p-2}}u(x,\lambda t)\quad\text{ with }\quad u_{\lambda}(x,0)=\lambda^{\frac{1}{p-2}}u_{0}(x)\geq u_{0}(x).

By the comparison principle (Lemma
3.7
), since
\lambda\geq 1
it follows that
u_{\lambda}(x,t)\geq u(x,t)

a.e.

x\in\Omega
and
t>0
. Moreover, taking
\lambda=\frac{t+h}{t}\geq 1
and adding and substracting
u_{\lambda}(x,t)
we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{u(x,t+h)-u(x,t)}{h}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{h}\left[\left(\frac{t+h}{t}\right)^{-\frac{1}{p-2}}-1\right]u_{\lambda}(x,t)+\frac{u_{\lambda}(x,t)-u(x,t)}{h}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{h}\left[\left(\frac{t+h}{t}\right)^{-\frac{1}{p-2}}-1\right]u_{\lambda}(x,t)

\displaystyle=\frac{(t+h)^{-\frac{1}{p-2}}-t^{-\frac{1}{p-2}}}{h}(t+h)^{\frac{1}{p-2}}u(x,t+h).

The result follows by letting

. The reasoning for solutions of (

) is identical.
∎

For the reader’s convenience, we recall the following version of the

. For

,

and

such that

it holds that

ab\leq\varepsilon a^{p}+(\varepsilon p)^{-\frac{q}{p}}q^{-1}b^{q}.

The following numerical estimate is key in our arguments.

Lemma 4.1
.

Given
p\geq 2
, for all
a,b\geq 0
it holds that

a^{p-1}-|a-b|^{p-2}(a-b)\leq(p-1)\max\{a^{p-2},b^{p-2}\}b.

Proof.

We assume
p>2
since the inequality is trivial when
p=2
. We have to prove that

\displaystyle a^{p-1}\left(1-\left|1-\frac{b}{a}\right|^{p-2}\left(1-\frac{b}{a}\right)\right)\leq\begin{cases}(p-1)a^{p-2}b&\text{ if }b\leq a,\\
(p-1)b^{p-1}&\text{ if }a<b.\end{cases}

Denoting
t=\frac{b}{a}
, the last expression is equivalent to

\displaystyle 1-\left|1-t\right|^{p-2}\left(1-t\right)\leq\begin{cases}(p-1)t&\text{ if }t\leq 1,\\
(p-1)t^{p-1}&\text{ if }t>1.\end{cases}

When
t\leq 1
, by using Taylor’s expansion we have

\displaystyle 1-|1-t|^{p-2}(1-t)

\displaystyle=1-(1-t)^{p-1}

\displaystyle=1-\left(1-(p-1)t+(p-1)(p-2)(1-\tilde{t})^{p-3}\frac{t^{2}}{2}\right)\leq(p-1)t

where
\tilde{t}\in[0,t]
, which gives the result when
t\leq 1
. When
t>1
we have

\displaystyle 1-|1-t|^{p-2}(1-t)=1+(t-1)^{p-1}\leq(p-1)t^{p-1}

where in the last inequality we used that
f(t)=1+(t-1)^{p-1}
and
g(t)=(p-1)t^{p-1}
are such that
f(1)=g(1)
and
f^{\prime}(t)\leq g^{\prime}(t)
for
t>1
. This gives the result for
t>1
and concludes the proof.
∎

From this, using Young’s inequality with

4.1.
Alternative form of the smoothing estimate

By using the time-monotonicity and time-scaling property of solutions, we provide for an alternative form of Theorem

Proposition 4.4

Proof.

Let
u

C

0\leq j\leq[p]-1

\displaystyle\|L_{p}^{(j)}g(t)\|_{\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}

\displaystyle\leq(p-1)^{[p]-1}\max\{\|2u(t)\|_{\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}^{p-1},\|2u(t)\|_{\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}^{p-[p]}\}

\displaystyle\leq(p-1)^{[p]-1}\max\{(2\mathsf{M}_{p})^{p-1},(2\mathsf{M}_{p})^{p-[p]}\}.

Moreover, due to the inductive hypothesis, for all
0\leq j\leq[p]-1
it holds that
\|g^{(j)}(t)\|_{\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}
is bounded by
2\|\partial_{t}^{j}u(t)\|_{\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\leq 2\mathsf{M}_{p}
. Then, in light of (
4.16
) these expressions yield for all

and
t\geq t_{0}

(4.17)

\displaystyle|\partial_{t}^{[p]-1}A_{p}(x,y,t)|\leq\mathsf{\tilde{M}}_{p}

where
\mathsf{\tilde{M}}_{p}
depends only on
n
,
p
and
\mathsf{M}_{p}
. Inserting (
4.17
) into (
4.14
) finally gives

\|\partial_{t}^{[p]}u(t)\|_{\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\leq\mathsf{\tilde{M}}_{p}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}J(x-y)\,dy\leq\mathsf{\tilde{M}}_{p}.

This concludes the proof when
p
is not an integer.

Analyticity.
When
p\in\mathbb{N}
, we obtain that
|L_{p}^{(j)}g|=|g^{(j)}|=0
for any
j>p-1
, which in light of the previous computations gives that
\partial_{t}^{j}u(t)=0
, hence formula (
4.16
) gives, for all

\displaystyle|\partial_{t}^{k}A_{p}(x,y,t)|

\displaystyle\leq k!\,\sum_{j=1}^{k}\|L_{p}^{(j)}g(t)\|_{\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\left(\prod_{\ell=1}^{k}\sum_{j=1}^{k}\|g^{(j)}(t)\|^{\ell}_{\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\right)

\displaystyle=k!\,\sum_{j=1}^{[p]-1}\|L_{p}^{(j)}g(t)\|_{\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\left(\prod_{\ell=1}^{[p]-1}\sum_{j=1}^{[p]-1}\|g^{(j)}(t)\|^{\ell}_{\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\right)\leq\mathsf{\tilde{M}}_{p}\,k!\,.

As a consequence we obtain that for all
k\in\mathbb{N}

(4.18)

\|\partial_{t}^{k}u(t)\|_{\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\leq\mathsf{\tilde{M}}_{p}\,k!\,\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}J(x-y)\,dy\leq\mathsf{\tilde{M}}_{p}\,k!\,,

which clearly implies that
u(t)
is analytic with radius
r_{0}=1\wedge t_{0}
. This concludes the proof.
∎

Lemma 5.1

\displaystyle\leq\begin{cases}(p-1)|a-b|(|a|^{p-3}+|b|^{p-3})&\text{ when }p\geq 3\\
|a-b|^{p-2}&\text{ when }2\leq p<3.\end{cases}

Proof.

The inequality for

5.2.
Hölder regularity in space

In this subsection we prove that the modulus of continuity of the initial data and its derivatives is preserved under certain conditions.

We recall some notation which will be used along this paragraph.

A
modulus of continuity
is a function
\omega\colon[0,\infty]\to[0,\infty]
vanishing at 0 and continuous at 0. A function
v
admits
\omega
as a modulus of continuity if and only if

|v(x)-v(y)|\leq\omega(|x-y|)

for any
x
and
y
in the domain of
v
.

Given two moduli of continuity
\omega_{1}
and
\omega_{2}
we say that

\omega_{1}\asymp\omega_{2}
when
\omega_{1}=O(\omega_{2})
, that is,
\omega_{1}(t)\leq C\omega_{2}(t)
as
t\to 0
, for some
C>0
,

\omega_{1}\ll\omega_{2}
when
\omega_{1}=o(\omega_{2})
, that is,
\lim_{t\to 0}\frac{\omega_{1}(t)}{\omega_{2}(t)}=0
.

Given a modulus of continuity
\omega
and
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
we consider the space

C^{0,\omega}(\Omega):=\left\{f\in C^{0}(\Omega)\colon[f]_{C^{0,\omega}(\Omega)}<\infty\right\},

where

[f]_{C^{0,\omega}(\Omega)}:=\sup\left\{\frac{|f(x)-f(y)|}{\omega(|x-y|)}\colon x,y\in\Omega,x\neq y\right\}.

Example 5.7
.

The space
C^{0,\omega}
includes
Hölder continuous function
when
\omega(t)=t^{\alpha}
,
\alpha\in(0,1)
;
Lipschitz functions
when
\omega(t)=t
;
almost Lipschitz function
when
\omega(t)=t(1+|\log t|)
, etc.

Observe that a function
v\in C^{0}
always belongs to
C^{0,\omega}_{loc}
, where
\omega
is the modulus of continuity of
v
.

The following characterization for functions in
C^{0,\omega}
will be useful.

Lemma 5.8
.

Let
\omega
be a modulus of continuity and
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
be an open set. The following statements are equivalent:

(i)

f\in C^{0,\omega}(\Omega)
;

(ii)

there is a positive constant
M
such that
[f]_{C^{0,\omega}(\Omega)}\leq M
;

(iii)

for all
x_{0}\in\Omega
and
r_{0}>0
such that
B_{r_{0}}(x_{0})\subset\Omega
it holds that

\osc_{B_{r}(x_{0})}f:=\sup_{B_{r}(x_{0})}f-\inf_{B_{r}(x_{0})}f\leq C\omega(r)\quad\forall 0\leq r\leq r_{0}

where
C
is a positive constant.

Given
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and
\alpha\in(0,1)
, the Hölder space
C^{k,\alpha}(\Omega)
has assigned the norm

\|f\|_{C^{k,\alpha}(\Omega)}:=\max_{|\beta|\leq k}\sup_{x\in\Omega}|D^{\beta}f(x)|+\max_{|\beta|=k}|D^{\beta}f|_{C^{0,\alpha}(\Omega)}.

We also recall the standard notation for multi-indexes.

Let
\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n})\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}_{+}
and
\beta=(\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{n})\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}_{+}
be two
multi-indexes.
We denote

|\alpha|=\alpha_{1}+\cdots+\alpha_{n}
,

\alpha\leq\beta
means that
\alpha_{i}\leq\beta_{i}
for all
1\leq i\leq n
,

D^{\alpha}u(x,t)
stands for
D^{\alpha}u(x,t):=D_{x}^{\alpha}v(x)=\partial_{x_{1}}^{\alpha_{1}}\cdots\partial_{x_{n}}^{\alpha_{n}}u(x,t)
,

\alpha!=\alpha_{1}!\alpha_{2}!\cdots\alpha_{n}!
,

\begin{pmatrix}\alpha\\
\beta\end{pmatrix}
means the product
\begin{pmatrix}\alpha_{1}\\
\beta_{1}\end{pmatrix}\cdots\begin{pmatrix}\alpha_{n}\\
\beta_{n}\end{pmatrix}
.

We recall that for
p\geq 2
, we denote
L_{p}(t)=|t|^{p-2}t
and
M_{p}(t)=|t|^{p-2}
.

For our purposes we use the
multivariate Faá di Bruno’s formula
to compute derivatives of a composition of functions. Let
u(x,t)\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{+}_{0}\to\mathbb{R}
be a smooth enough function and let
\beta=(\beta_{1},\beta_{2},\cdots,\beta_{n})
be a multi-index with
|\beta|=k
, then

(5.7)

D^{\beta}(L_{p}(u(x,t)))=\sum_{r=1}^{k}L_{p}^{(r)}u(x,t)\,d_{r}(u(x,t))

where
d_{r}
depends on the product of the different combination of derivatives of order
r=1,\dots,k
, and whose precise formula can be found, for instance, in
[
31
]
[Corollary 2.10]. Namely, we have

d_{r}(u(x,t))=\sum_{p(\beta,r)}\beta!\prod_{j=1}^{k}\frac{[D^{\ell_{j}}u(x,t)]^{\kappa_{j}}}{\kappa_{j}!(\ell_{j}!)^{k_{j}}}

where
p(\beta,r)
is the set of
(\kappa_{1},\ldots,\kappa_{k};\ell_{1},\ldots,\ell_{k})
(with
\kappa_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}_{0}
and
\ell_{i}
are multi-indexes,
1\leq i\leq k
) such that for some
1\leq s\leq k
,
\kappa_{i}=0
and
\ell_{i}=0
for
1\leq i\leq k-s
;
\kappa_{1}>0
for
k-s+1\leq i\leq k
; and
0\leq\ell_{k-s+1}\leq\cdots\leq\ell_{k}
are such that

\sum_{i=1}^{k}\kappa_{i}=r,\quad\sum_{i=1}^{k}\kappa_{i}\ell_{i}=\beta.

For instance, (
5.7
) when
u=u(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})
becomes

\displaystyle\partial_{x_{1}}\left(L_{p}u\right)

\displaystyle=u_{x_{1}}L_{p}^{\prime}v,

\displaystyle\partial_{x_{1}}\partial_{x_{2}}\left(L_{p}u\right)

\displaystyle=v_{x_{1}}u_{x_{2}}L_{p}^{\prime\prime}u+v_{x_{1}x_{2}}L_{p}^{\prime}v,

\displaystyle\partial_{x_{1}}\partial^{2}_{x_{3}}\left(L_{p}u\right)

\displaystyle=u_{x_{1}}u_{x_{3}}u_{x_{3}}L_{p}^{\prime\prime\prime}u+(u_{x_{1}}u_{x_{3}x_{3}}+2u_{x_{1}}u_{x_{1}x_{3}})L_{p}^{\prime\prime}u+u_{x_{1}x_{3}x_{3}}L_{p}^{\prime}u,

and when
u=u(x_{1},x_{2})
, (
5.7
) becomes

\partial_{x_{1}}\partial_{x_{2}}^{2}(L_{p}u)=u_{x_{2}}^{2}u_{x_{1}}L_{p}^{\prime\prime\prime}u+L_{p}^{\prime\prime}(u_{x_{2}x_{2}}u_{x_{1}}+2u_{x_{2}}u_{x_{1}x_{2}})+L_{p}^{\prime}u_{x_{1}x_{2}x_{2}}.

From the formula of
d_{r}
it can be seen that

(5.8)

D^{\beta}(L_{p}(u(x,t)))=L_{p}^{\prime}u\,D^{\beta}u(x,t)+R_{k-1}(u(x,t))

where
R_{k-1}(u)
contains the terms with “lower order derivatives” or order up
k-1
, and it is such that

R_{k-1}(u(x,t))\leq k!\,\mathsf{\bar{m}}_{p}(t)\sum_{j=2}^{k}L_{p}^{(j)}u(x,t)

with
\mathsf{\bar{m}}_{p}(t)
denoting a function such that

\prod_{\ell=1}^{k}\sum_{0\leq|\alpha|\leq k-1}\|D^{\alpha}u(t)\|_{\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}^{\ell}\leq\mathsf{\bar{m}}_{p}(t).

When
u
is solution of (
C
), notice that in view of the smoothing effect, we do need to require a priori boundedness of
u_{0}
, indeed

\|u(t)\|_{\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\leq\frac{\mathsf{\tilde{K}}_{p}}{t_{0}^{\frac{1}{p-2}}}+\mathsf{K}_{p,q,J}\|u_{0}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{q}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}:=\mathsf{m}_{0}\quad\text{ for all }0<t_{0}\leq t\leq T.

Notice also that when
u_{0}\in\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
we have that
\|u(t)\|_{\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\leq\|u_{0}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}:=\mathsf{m}_{0}
and all the following results will extend up to
t=0
. Summing up, we define

(5.9)

\|u(t)\|_{\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\leq\mathsf{m}_{0}:=\begin{cases}\frac{\mathsf{\tilde{K}}_{p}}{t_{0}^{\frac{1}{p-2}}}+\mathsf{K}_{p,q,J}\|u_{0}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{q}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}&\mbox{when $q\in(1,\infty)$ and $t\geq t_{0}>0$}\\
\|u_{0}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}&\mbox{when $q=\infty$ and $t\geq 0$}.\end{cases}

In fact, we can take in this case
\mathsf{\bar{m}}_{0}=\mathsf{m}_{0}
.

An analogous expression holds for solutions of (
N
) and (
D
) by replacing
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
\Omega
.

Theorem 5.9

(Hölder regularity in space)
.

Let
p>2
and let
u
be a solution of (
C
) starting from the initial datum
u_{0}\in\mathrm{L}^{q}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
with
q\in[1,\infty]
and let
\mathsf{m}_{0}
be as in (
5.9
). Moreover, assume that

(i)

D^{\alpha}u_{0}\in C^{0,\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\cap\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
for any
1\leq|\alpha|\leq[p]-1
,
and define
\mathsf{m}_{p}
as

(5.10)

\mathsf{m}_{p}:=\mathsf{m}_{0}+\sum_{1\leq|\alpha|\leq[p]-1}\|D^{\alpha}u_{0}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})},

(ii)

there exists a modulus of continuity
\omega_{J,p}
such that for
a.e.

x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}

(5.11)

\sum_{0\leq|\alpha|\leq[p]-1}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left|D^{\alpha}J(y-z)-D^{\alpha}J(x-z)\right|\,dz\leq\omega_{J,p}(|x-y|).

Then
D^{\alpha}u(\cdot,t)\in C^{0,\bar{\omega}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
for any
|\alpha|\leq[p]-1
and
t\geq t_{0}>0
, where the modulus of continuity
\bar{\omega}
is given by
\bar{\omega}(\rho)=\max\{\rho,\rho^{p-2},\omega_{J,p}(\rho),\omega(\rho)\}
. Moreover, the following estimates hold true for all
|\alpha|\leq[p]-1
, all
x_{1},x_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and all
t\geq t_{0}>0

(5.12)

|D^{\alpha}u(x_{1},t)-D^{\alpha}u(x_{2},t)|\leq{\mathsf{K}}(t)\,\bar{\omega}(|x_{1}-x_{2}|)

where
{\mathsf{K}}(t)
is a function that depends only on
p
,
n
,
J
, and
\mathsf{m}_{p}
.

When
u_{0}\in\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
the result holds for all
t\geq 0
.

When
2\leq p\in\mathbb{N}
, estimate (
5.12
) holds for
|\alpha|=k
for any
k\in\mathbb{N}
provided that moreover
u_{0}\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and
J\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. In particular
\mathsf{K}(t)
is a function that depends only on
p
,
n
,
J
, and
\mathsf{m}_{p}
but not on
k
nor on
k-
th derivatives of
u_{0}
.

When
\Omega
is open and bounded, analogous results hold for solution of (
D
). If in addition
\Omega
satisfies (

H
J

), the same holds for solutions of (
N
).

Remark 5.10
.

{\mathsf{K}}(t)
is defined for all
t\geq t_{0}>0
and has an (almost) explicit expression given in the proof. The form of
{\mathsf{K}}(t)
reveals that when
u_{0}\in\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
we can extend the result up to
t_{0}=0
, that is when we can take
q=\infty
. This information is encoded in the expression of
m_{0}
as in (
5.9
). When
q<\infty
, we cannot extend the estimate for
t=0
since
{\mathsf{K}}(t)\to\infty
as
t_{0}\to 0
. Also we remark that
{\mathsf{K}}(t)\to\infty
as
t\to\infty
.

Proof.

We prove the result for solutions of the Cauchy problem. For the Dirichlet and Neumann case the proof is analogous.

\circ~
Case
p\in(1,2)
.
Let
u
be a solution of (
N
) corresponding to the initial datum
u_{0}\in C^{0,\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\cap\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. In this case we have that
|\alpha|=0
and therefore we need to prove that for any
x_{1},x_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
t\geq 0
there exists a positive function
\mathsf{K}(t)

depending on
p
,
n
and
\mathsf{m}_{0}
such that

(5.13)

|u(x_{1},t)-u(x_{2},t)|\leq\mathsf{K}(t)\bar{\omega}(|x_{1}-x_{2}|),

where
\bar{\omega}(\rho):=\max\{\omega_{J,p}(\rho),\omega(\rho)\}
.

\circ~
Step 1
. Let
x_{1},x_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
be fixed and let
t\geq 0
. Assume first that
u(x_{1},t)\geq u(x_{2},t)
. Using the fact that
u
solves equation (
N
) we can write

\displaystyle u_{t}(x_{1},t)-u_{t}(x_{2},t)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left(J(x_{1}-y)L_{p}(u(y,t)-u(x_{1},t))-J(x_{2}-y)L_{p}(u(y,t)-u(x_{2},t)\right)\,dy

\displaystyle=-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}J(x_{1}-y)\left(L_{p}(u(y,t)-u(x_{2},t))-L_{p}(u(y,t)-u(x_{1},t))\right)\,dy

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}L_{p}(u(y,t)-u(x_{2},t))\left(J(x_{1}-y)-J(x_{2}-y)\right)\,dy:=(i)+(ii).

From Lemma
5.2
and (
5.11
) we get that

\displaystyle(i)\leq-(p-1)\frac{u(x_{1},t)-u(x_{2},t)}{(1+2\|u_{0}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})})^{\frac{2-p}{2}}},\qquad(ii)\leq 2\|u_{0}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}^{p-1}\omega_{J}(|x_{1}-x_{2}|),

and, denoting
y(t)=u(x_{1},t)-u(x_{2},t)
and

A:=\frac{p-1}{(1+2\|u(t)\|_{\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})})^{\frac{2-p}{2}}},\qquad B:=2\|u_{0}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}^{p-1}\omega_{J,p}(|x_{1}-x_{2}|),

we get the following differential inequality

(5.14)

y^{\prime}(t)\leq-Ay(t)+B,\qquad t\geq 0.

\circ~
Step 2
. Observe that the function
\bar{y}(t)=y(0)e^{-At}+Bt
is a supersolution to (
5.14
) since

-A\bar{y}(t)+B=\bar{y}(t)-ABt\leq\bar{y}(t)
for all
t\geq 0
. Then,
y(t)\leq\bar{y}(t)
for all
t\geq 0
and we get

(5.15)

\displaystyle\begin{split}u(x_{1},t)-u(x_{2},t)&\leq(u_{0}(x_{1})-u_{0}(x_{2}))e^{-At}+Bt\\
&\leq\omega(|x_{1}-x_{2}|)e^{-At}+2t\|u_{0}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}^{p-1}\omega_{J,p}(|x_{1}-x_{2}|)\\
&\leq\max\{\omega(|x_{1}-x_{2}|),\omega_{J,p}(|x_{1}-x_{2}|)\}\left(e^{-At}+2t\|u_{0}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}^{p-1}\right)\\
&:=\bar{\omega}(|x_{1}-x_{2}|)\mathsf{K}(t)\end{split}

where we have used that
u_{0}\in C^{0,\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, and
\mathsf{K}(t)
depends on
p
,
n

\mathsf{m}_{0}
and
t
.

\circ~
Step 3
. When
u(x_{1},t)\leq u(x_{2},t)
, proceeding similarly we get

\frac{d}{dt}(u(x_{2},t)-u(x_{1},t))\leq-(u(x_{2},t)-u(x_{1},t))A+B,\qquad t\geq 0

and then
u(x_{2},t)-u(x_{1},t)\leq\bar{\omega}(|x_{1}-x_{2}|)\mathsf{K}(t)
. This inequality together with (
5.15
) gives (
5.13
).

\circ~
Case
p>2
. From now on
p>2
and
u
denotes a solution of (
C
) corresponding to the initial datum
u_{0}\in\mathrm{L}^{q}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
with
q\in[1,\infty]
. Moreover,
x_{1},x_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
t\geq t_{0}>0
with
t_{0}=0
when
u_{0}\in\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))
.

We split the proof in several steps.

\circ~
Step 1
. Let us prove that
D^{\beta}u_{0}
bounded for any
|\beta|\leq[p]-1
implies that
D^{\beta}u(t)
is bounded for
|\beta|\leq[p]-1
and all
t\geq t_{0}
.

More precisely, we prove the following statement:

Assume that
\|D^{\beta}u(t)\|_{\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\leq\mathsf{\tilde{m}}_{p}(t)
for
|\beta|\leq[p]-2
and
\|D^{\beta}u_{0}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\leq\mathsf{m}_{p}
for
|\beta|\leq[p]-1
. Then there is a function
\mathsf{\bar{m}}(t)
such that
\|D^{\beta}u(t)\|_{\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\leq\mathsf{\bar{m}}_{p}(t)
for
|\beta|\leq[p]-1
.

Here
\mathsf{\bar{m}}_{p}(t)
and
\mu_{p}(t)
depend on
p
,
n
,
J
and the constant
\mathsf{m}_{0}
given in (
5.9
).

We prove it by induction on the order of
\beta
.

Case
|\beta|=1
. By hypothesis
\|(u_{0})_{x_{i}}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\leq\mathsf{m}_{p}
for
i=1,\ldots,n
, and in light of (
5.9
) we have

\|u_{0}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\leq\mathsf{m}_{0},\qquad\|u(t)\|_{\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\leq\mathsf{m}_{0}=:\mathsf{\tilde{m}}_{p}(t).

In this case, for any
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
we have that

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}

\displaystyle|u_{x_{i}}(x,t)|=\sign(u_{x_{i}}(x,t))\partial_{x_{i}}u_{t}(x,t)=\sign(u_{x_{i}}(x,t))\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}J_{x_{i}}(x-y)L_{p}(u(y,t)-u(x,t))\,dy\right.

\displaystyle\quad\left.-(p-1)u_{x_{i}}(x,t)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}J(x-y)M_{p}(u(y,t)-u(x,t))\,dy\right)

\displaystyle\leq 2^{p}\|u(t)\|_{\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}^{p-1}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}|\nabla J(x-y)|\,dy+|u_{x_{i}}(x,t)|2^{p-1}(p-1)\|u(t)\|_{\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}^{p-2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}J(x-y)\,dy

\displaystyle\leq A|u_{x_{i}}(x,t)|+B

where we have denoted

Solving the differential inequality above gives a bound for

for all

and all

:

\|u_{x_{i}}(t)\|_{\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\leq e^{At}\mathsf{m}_{1}+\frac{B}{A}(e^{At}-1):=\mu_{p}(t).

Inductive step.
Assume that

\|D^{\beta}u(t)\|_{\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\leq\mathsf{\tilde{m}}_{p}(t)\text{ for }|\beta|\leq[p]-2\text{ and }t\geq t_{0},\qquad\|D^{\beta}u_{0}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\leq\mathsf{m}_{p}\text{ for }|\beta|\leq[p]-1,

for some
\mathsf{\tilde{m}}_{p}(t)
depending on
p
,
n
,
J
and
\mathsf{m}_{0}
.

Let us see that for
|\beta|=[p]-1
there is a function
\mathsf{\bar{m}}_{p}(t)
depending on
p
,
n
,
J
,
\mathsf{\tilde{m}}_{p}
and
\mathsf{m}_{0}
such that

(5.16)

\|D^{\beta}u(t)\|_{\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\leq\mathsf{\bar{m}}_{p}(t)\qquad\text{ for }t\geq t_{0}.

By using the Leibniz formula for derivatives

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}D^{\beta}u(x,t)

\displaystyle=D^{\beta}u_{t}(x,t)=D^{\beta}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}J(x-z)L_{p}(u(x,t)-u(z,t))

\displaystyle=\sum_{0\leq\alpha\leq\beta}\begin{pmatrix}\beta\\
\alpha\end{pmatrix}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}D^{\alpha}J(x-z)D^{\beta-\alpha}(L_{p}(u(x,t)-u(z,t)))\,dz.

Then we can write

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}|D^{\beta}u(x,t)|

\displaystyle=\sign(D^{\beta}u(x,t))D^{\beta}u_{t}(x,t)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{0\leq\alpha\leq\beta}\begin{pmatrix}\beta\\
\alpha\end{pmatrix}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}|D^{\alpha}J(x-z)||D^{\beta-\alpha}L_{p}(u(x,t)-u(z,t))|\,dz

\displaystyle=\sum_{1\leq\alpha\leq\beta}\begin{pmatrix}\beta\\
\alpha\end{pmatrix}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}|D^{\alpha}J(x-z)||D^{\beta-\alpha}L_{p}(u(x,t)-u(z,t))|\,dz

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}|J(x-z)||D^{\beta}L_{p}(u(x,t)-u(z,t))|\,dz:=(i)+(ii).

Let us estimate
(i)
. From (
5.1
) and (
5.9

(5.17)

\displaystyle\left|L_{p}^{(r)}u(x,t)\right|\leq\mathsf{c}_{r,p}\|u(t)\|_{\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}^{p-r-1}\leq(p-1)^{p-1}\max\{\mathsf{m}_{0}^{p-2},\mathsf{m}_{0}^{p-[p]}\}.

In this case

5.8
) and and the inductive hypothesis we get

(5.18)

\displaystyle\begin{split}D^{\beta-\alpha}&L_{p}(u(x,t)-u(z,t))=L_{p}^{\prime}u(x,t)\,D^{\beta-\alpha}u(x,t)+([p]-2)!\mathsf{c}_{0}(p,\mathsf{m}_{0},\mathsf{\bar{m}}_{p})(t)\sum_{r=2}^{[p]-2}L_{p}^{(r)}u(x,t)\\

&\leq\mathsf{c}_{1}(p,\mathsf{m_{0}},\mathsf{\tilde{m}}_{p}(t)).\end{split}

This gives that

(i)\leq\mathsf{c}_{1}(p,\mathsf{m}_{0},\mathsf{\tilde{m}}_{p}(t))\sum_{1\leq\alpha\leq\beta}\begin{pmatrix}\beta\\
\alpha\end{pmatrix}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}|D^{\alpha}J(x-z)|\,dz\leq\mathsf{c}_{2}(p,n,J,\mathsf{m}_{0},\mathsf{\tilde{m}}_{p}(t)).

To bound
(ii)
we use again (
5.8

5.17
) and the fact that
|\beta|=[p]-1
, similarly as before

\displaystyle D^{\beta}L_{p}(u(x,t)-u(z,t))

\displaystyle=L_{p}^{\prime}u(x,t)\,D^{\beta}u(x,t)+([p]-1)!\mathsf{c}_{3}(p,\mathsf{m}_{0},\mathsf{\tilde{m}}_{p}(t))\sum_{r=2}^{[p]-1}L_{p}^{(r)}u(x,t)

\displaystyle\leq(p-1)^{p-1}\max\{\mathsf{m}_{0}^{p-2},\mathsf{m}_{0}^{p-[p]}\}|D^{\beta}u(x,t)|+\mathsf{c}_{4}(p,\mathsf{m}_{0},\mathsf{\tilde{m}}_{p}(t)),

giving that

(ii)\leq\mathsf{c}_{5}(p,J,\mathsf{m}_{0})|D^{\beta}u(x,t)|+\mathsf{c}_{6}(p,J,\mathsf{m}_{0},\mathsf{\tilde{m}}_{p}(t)).

Combining these expressions leads to

(5.19)

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}|D^{\beta}u(x,t)|\leq A(p,J,\mathsf{m}_{0})|D^{\beta}u(x,t)|+B(t),

where

,

,

and
\mathsf{\tilde{m}}_{p}(t)
. Solving this differential inequality and using the inductive hypothesis gives the desired bound:

(5.20)

\displaystyle\begin{split}|D^{\beta}u(x,t)|&\leq|D^{\beta}u_{0}(x)|e^{At}+\int_{t_{0}}^{t}B(s)e^{(t-s)A}\,ds\\
&\leq\mathsf{m}_{p}e^{At}+\int_{t_{0}}^{t}B(s)e^{(t-s)A}\,ds:=\mathsf{\bar{m}}_{p}(t)\qquad\mbox{for all $t\geq t_{0}$}\,.\end{split}

\circ~
Step 2
. We compute some estimates for the difference of derivatives. Observe that by using the mean value theorem and (
5.16
) we get that

|u(x_{1},t)-u(x_{2},t)|\leq\mathsf{\bar{m}}_{p}(t)|x_{1}-y_{1}|,

then using Lemma

we obtain that for
0\leq r\leq[p]-1

(5.21)

\displaystyle\begin{split}|&L_{p}^{(r)}(u(x_{1},t)-u(z,t))-L_{p}^{(r)}(u(x_{2},t)-u(z,t))|\leq\\
&\leq 2^{p}\mathsf{\bar{c}}_{r,p}\max\{|u(x_{1},t)-u(x_{2},t)|,|u(x_{1},t)-u(x_{2},t)|^{p-r-1}\}\max\{1,(2\|u(t)\|_{\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})})^{p-r-2}\}\\
&\leq\mathsf{K}_{1}(t)\max\{|x_{1}-x_{2}|,|x_{1}-x_{2}|^{p-r-1}\}\end{split}

where
\mathsf{K}_{1}(t)
depends on
p
,
\mathsf{m}_{0}
and
\mathsf{\bar{m}}_{p}
.

\circ~
Step 3
.
Let
|\beta|=k\leq[p]-1
. By using the Leibniz formula for derivatives we have that

\displaystyle D^{\beta}

\displaystyle u_{t}(x_{1},t)-D^{\beta}u_{t}(x_{2},t)=\sum_{0\leq\alpha\leq\beta}\begin{pmatrix}\beta\\
\alpha\end{pmatrix}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}D^{\alpha}J(x_{1}-z)D^{\beta-\alpha}(L_{p}(u(x_{1},t)-u(z,t)))\,dz

\displaystyle\quad-\sum_{0\leq\alpha\leq\beta}\begin{pmatrix}\beta\\
\alpha\end{pmatrix}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}D^{\alpha}J(x_{2}-z)D^{\beta-\alpha}(L_{p}(u(x_{2},t)-u(z,t)))\,dz

\displaystyle\quad\pm\sum_{0\leq\alpha\leq\beta}\begin{pmatrix}\beta\\
\alpha\end{pmatrix}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}D^{\alpha}J(x_{1}-z)D^{\beta-\alpha}(L_{p}(u(x_{2},t)-u(z,t)))\,dz

\displaystyle=\sum_{0\leq\alpha\leq\beta}\begin{pmatrix}\beta\\
\alpha\end{pmatrix}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}D^{\alpha}J(x_{1}-z)\left(D^{\beta-\alpha}(L_{p}(u(x_{1},t)-u(z,t)))-D^{\beta-\alpha}(L_{p}(u(x_{2},t)-u(z,t)))\right)\,dz

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{0\leq\alpha\leq\beta}\begin{pmatrix}\beta\\
\alpha\end{pmatrix}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left(D^{\alpha}J(x_{1}-z)-D^{\alpha}J(x_{2}-z)\right)D^{\beta-\alpha}(L_{p}(u(x_{2},t)-u(z,t)))\,dz.

Now, since

\frac{d}{dt}|D^{\beta}u(x_{1},t)-D^{\beta}u(x_{2},t)|=\sign(D^{\beta}u(x_{1},t)-D^{\beta}u(x_{2},t))(D^{\beta}u_{t}(x_{1},t)-D^{\beta}u_{t}(x_{2},t)),

in light of the previous expression we obtain that

(5.22)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\frac{d}{dt}&|D^{\beta}u(x_{1},t)-D^{\beta}u(x_{2},t)|\leq|D^{\beta}u_{t}(x_{1},t)-D^{\beta}u_{t}(x_{2},t)|\\
&\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}|J(x_{1}-z)|\left|D^{\beta}(L_{p}(u(x_{1},t)-u(z,t)))-D^{\beta}(L_{p}(u(x_{2},t)-u(z,t)))\right|\,dz\\
&\quad+\sum_{1\leq\alpha\leq\beta}\begin{pmatrix}\beta\\
\alpha\end{pmatrix}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}|D^{\alpha}J(x_{1}-z)|\left|D^{\beta-\alpha}(L_{p}(u(x_{1},t)-u(z,t)))-D^{\beta-\alpha}(L_{p}(u(x_{2},t)-u(z,t)))\right|\,dz\\
&\quad+\sum_{0\leq\alpha\leq\beta}\begin{pmatrix}\beta\\
\alpha\end{pmatrix}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left|D^{\alpha}J(x_{1}-z)-D^{\alpha}J(x_{2}-z)\right||D^{\beta-\alpha}(L_{p}(u(x_{2},t)-u(z,t)))|\,dz\\
&:=(I_{1})+(I_{2})+(I_{3}).\end{split}

In order to bound
(I_{1})
we use expression (
5.8
) to write
D^{\beta}(L_{p}(u))
as
L_{p}^{\prime}u(x,t)\,D^{\beta}u+R_{k-1}u
, where

R_{k-1}u(x,t)\leq k!\mathsf{\bar{m}}_{p}(t)\sum_{j=2}^{k}L_{p}^{(j)}u(x,t)

since
k\leq[p]-1
. This allows to write:

\displaystyle|

\displaystyle D^{\beta}(L_{p}(u(x_{1},t)-u(z,t)))-D^{\beta}(L_{p}(u(x_{2},t)-u(z,t)))|=

\displaystyle=\left|L_{p}^{\prime}u(x_{1},t)\,D^{\beta}u(x_{1},t)+R_{k-1}(u(x_{1},t))-L_{p}^{\prime}u(x_{2},t)\,D^{\beta}u(x_{2},t)-R_{k-1}(u(x_{2},t))\right.

\displaystyle\quad\pm\left.L_{p}^{\prime}u(x_{2},t)\,D^{\beta}u(x_{1},t)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left|L_{p}^{\prime}u(x_{1},t)-L_{p}^{\prime}u(x_{2},t)\right|\,\left|D^{\beta}u(x_{1},t)\right|+\left|L_{p}^{\prime}u(x_{2},t)\right|\left|D^{\beta}u(x_{1},t)-D^{\beta}u(x_{2},t)\right|

\displaystyle\quad+\left|R_{k-1}(u(x_{1},t))-R_{k-1}(u(x_{2},t))\right|

\displaystyle:=(i_{1})+(i_{2})+(i_{3}).

To bound
(i_{1})
we use expression (
5.16
) from Step 1 and (
5.21
) to get

(i_{1})\leq\mathsf{K}_{1}(t)\mathsf{\bar{m}}_{p}(t)\hat{\omega}(|x_{1}-x_{2}|),

where we have denoted

. The term
(i_{2})
can be bounded using (
5.17

(i_{2})\leq c_{1,p}\max\{\mathsf{m}_{0},\mathsf{m}_{0}^{p-2}\}\left|D^{\beta}u(x_{1},t)-D^{\beta}u(x_{2},t)\right|.

Finally, the term
(i_{3})
can be bounded using the mean value theorem, (
5.16
) and (
5.17
) as

(i_{3})=\mathsf{c}_{6}(p,\mathsf{m}_{0},\mathsf{\bar{m}}_{p}(t))|x_{1}-x_{2}|.

These estimates allow to bound
(I_{1})
as

\displaystyle(I_{1})

\displaystyle\leq((i_{1})+(i_{2})+(i_{3}))\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}|J(x_{1}-z)|\,dz

\displaystyle\leq\mathsf{c}_{7}(p,J,\mathsf{m}_{0})\left|D^{\beta}u(x_{1},t)-D^{\beta}u(x_{2},t)\right|+\mathsf{c}_{8}(p,J,\mathsf{m}_{0},\mathsf{\bar{m}}_{p}(t))\,\hat{\omega}(|x_{1}-x_{2}|).

To bound
(I_{2})
we proceed similarly as for
(I_{1})
. In this case
|\beta-\alpha|\leq k-1\leq[p]-2
and from (
5.8
)

D^{\beta-\alpha}(L_{p}(u(x,t)))=(L_{p}^{\prime})u(x,t)\,D^{\beta-\alpha}u(x,t)+R_{k-2}(u(x,t))

where
R_{k-2}(u(x,t))
can be bounded using (
5.16
) as

R_{k-2}(u(x,t))\leq(k-1)!\mathsf{\bar{m}}_{p}(t)\sum_{j=2}^{k-1}L_{p}^{(j)}u(x,t).

This allows to write

\displaystyle|

\displaystyle D^{\beta-\alpha}(L_{p}(u(x_{1},t)-u(z,t)))-D^{\beta-\alpha}(L_{p}(u(x_{2},t)-u(z,t)))|

\displaystyle\leq\left|(L_{p}^{\prime})u(x_{1},t)-(L_{p}^{\prime})u(x_{2},t)\right|\,\left|D^{\beta-\alpha}u(x_{1},t)\right|+\left|(L_{p}^{\prime})u(x_{2},t)\right|\left|D^{\beta-\alpha}u(x_{1},t)-D^{\beta-\alpha}u(x_{2},t)\right|

\displaystyle\quad+\left|R_{k-2}(u(x_{1},t))-R_{k-2}(u(x_{2},t))\right|

\displaystyle:=(i_{1})+(i_{2})+(i_{3}).

To bound
(i_{1})
we use expression (
5.16
) and (
5.21
) to get that
(i_{1})\leq\mathsf{K}_{1}(t)\mathsf{\bar{m}}_{p}(t)\hat{\omega}(|x_{1}-x_{2}|)
. By using (
5.17
) the term
(i_{2})
can be bounded as

\displaystyle(i_{2})

\displaystyle\leq c_{1,p}\max\{\mathsf{m}_{0},\mathsf{m}_{0}^{p-2}\}\left|D^{\beta-\alpha}u(x_{1},t)-D^{\beta-\alpha}u(x_{2},t)\right|\leq c_{1,p}\max\{\mathsf{m}_{0},\mathsf{m}_{0}^{p-2}\}\mathsf{\bar{m}}_{p}(t)|x_{1}-x_{2}|

where in the last inequality we have used the mean value theorem and (
5.16
).

Finally, using again the mean vale theorem and (
5.17
) we can bound
(i_{3})
as

(i_{3})\leq\mathsf{c}_{9}(p,\mathsf{m}_{0},\mathsf{\bar{m}}_{p}(t))|x_{1}-x_{2}|.

With these estimates,
(I_{2})
can be upper bounded as follows:

\displaystyle(I_{2})

\displaystyle\leq((i_{1})+(i_{2})+(i_{3}))\sum_{1\leq\alpha\leq\beta}\begin{pmatrix}\beta\\
\alpha\end{pmatrix}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}|D^{\alpha}J(x_{1}-z)|\,dz\leq\mathsf{c}_{10}(p,J,\bar{m}_{p}(t),m_{0})\,\hat{\omega}(|x_{1}-x_{2}|).

To bound
(I_{3})
observe that expression (
5.18
) gives

|D^{\beta-\alpha}(L_{p}(u(x,t)-u(z,t)))|\leq\mathsf{c}_{11}(p,\mathsf{m}_{0},\mathsf{\bar{m}}_{p}(t)).

Then, from hypothesis (
5.11
) we get

\displaystyle(I_{3})

\displaystyle\leq\mathsf{c}_{11}(p,\mathsf{m}_{0},\mathsf{\bar{m}}_{p}(t))\sum_{0\leq\alpha\leq\beta}\begin{pmatrix}\beta\\
\alpha\end{pmatrix}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left(D^{\alpha}J(x_{1}-z)-D^{\alpha}J(x_{2}-z)\right)\,dz

\displaystyle\leq\mathsf{c}_{12}(p,\mathsf{m}_{0},\mathsf{\bar{m}}_{p}(t))\,\omega_{J,p}(|x_{1}-x_{2}|).

Finally, inserting the bounds of
(I_{1})
,
(I_{2})
and
(I_{3})
into (
5.22
) give the differential inequality

(5.23)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\frac{d}{dt}|D^{\beta}u(x_{1},t)&-D^{\beta}u(x_{2},t)|\leq|D^{\beta}u_{t}(x_{1},t)-D^{\beta}u_{t}(x_{2},t)|\leq\\
&\leq A(p,J,\mathsf{m}_{0})\left|D^{\beta}u(x_{1},t)-D^{\beta}u(x_{2},t)\right|+B(t)\,\tilde{\omega}(|x_{1}-x_{2}|),\end{split}

where
\tilde{\omega}(\rho):=\max\{\rho^{p-2},\rho,\omega_{J,p}(\rho)\}
,
B(t)
depends on
p
,
J
,
\mathsf{m}_{0}
and
\mathsf{\bar{m}}_{p}(t)
, and
A
in independent on
t
. Solving (
5.23
) and using the hypothesis on
D^{\beta}u_{0}
we get

\displaystyle|D^{\beta}u(x_{1},t)-D^{\beta}u(x_{2},t)|

\displaystyle\leq|D^{\beta}u_{0}(x_{1})-D^{\beta}u_{0}(x_{2})|e^{At}+\bar{\omega}(|x_{1}-x_{2}|)\int_{0}^{t}B(s)e^{(t-s)A}\,ds

\displaystyle\leq\bar{\omega}(|x_{2}-x_{1}|)\left(e^{At}+\int_{0}^{t}B(s)e^{(t-s)A}\,ds\right)

where we have denoted

\bar{\omega}(\rho):=\max\{\rho,\rho^{p-2},\omega_{J,p}(\rho),\omega(\rho)\}.

Therefore, we conclude that

\sup\left\{\frac{|D^{\beta}u(x_{1},t)-D^{\beta}u(x_{2},t)|}{\bar{\omega}(|x_{1}-x_{2}|)}\colon x_{1},x_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{n},x\neq y\right\}\leq e^{At}+\int_{0}^{t}B(s)e^{A(t-s)}\,ds:=\overline{\mathsf{K}}(t)

and then
D^{\beta}u(\cdot,t)\in C^{0,\bar{\omega}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
for any
|\beta|\leq[p]-1
and
t\geq t_{0}
. This concludes the proof.
∎

5.3.
Hölder regularity in space for time derivatives

Under the considerations of Theorem
5.9
, we can state an analogous result for the time derivatives of solutions.

Recall that the smoothing effect and the norm decreasing in time property of solutions gives

\|u(t)\|_{\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\leq\mathsf{m}_{0}.

where
\mathsf{m}_{0}
is defined in (
5.9
). Furthermore, in light of Theorem
4.6
, there exists a function
\mathsf{M}_{p}>0
depending on
n
,
p
and the constant
\mathsf{\bar{M}}_{1}
given in (
4.11
) such that for all
j=0,\ldots,[p]-1

\|\partial_{t}^{j}u(t)\|_{\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\leq\mathsf{M}_{p}.

These estimates holds for
t\geq t_{0}>0
when
u_{0}\in\mathrm{L}^{q}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
with
q\in[1,\infty]
and we can let
t_{0}=0
when
u_{0}\in\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
.

In the following theorem we prove that the modulus of continuity of the initial data and its derivatives is preserved by the time derivatives of order up to
[p-1]
.

Theorem 5.11

(Hölder regularity in space for time derivatives)
.

Given
p>2
, let
u
be a solution of (
C
) starting from the initial datum
u_{0}\in\mathrm{L}^{q}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
with
q\in[1,\infty]
.

Assume moreover conditions (
5.10
) and (
5.11
).

Then
\partial_{t}^{j}u(\cdot,t)\in C_{x}^{[p]-1,\bar{\omega}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, for any
j\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
such that
j\leq[p]-1
, where
\bar{\omega}
is the modulus of continuity given by
\bar{\omega}(\rho)=\max\{\rho,\rho^{p-2},\omega_{J,p}(\rho),\omega(\rho)\}
.

More precisely, it holds that
D^{\alpha}\partial_{t}^{j}u(\cdot,t)\in C^{0,\bar{\omega}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
for any
t\geq t_{0}>0
, where
\alpha
is a multi-index such that
|\alpha|\leq[p]-1
and
j\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
is such that
j\leq[p]-1
, and there exists a positive constant
\mathsf{c}_{p}
depending on
p
,
n
,
J
,
\mathsf{M}_{p}
,
\mathsf{m}_{0}
and
t
such that

(5.24)

\displaystyle\frac{|D^{\alpha}\partial_{t}^{j}u(x_{1},t)-D^{\alpha}\partial_{t}^{j}u(x_{2},t)|}{\bar{\omega}(|x_{1}-x_{2}|)}\leq\mathsf{c}_{p}(t)

hold true for all
x_{1},x_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and all
t\geq t_{0}
. We can let
t_{0}=0
when
u_{0}\in\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
.

Moreover, when
p\in\mathbb{N}
, estimate (
5.24
) holds for any
j\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and any
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{n}
, hence each
D^{\alpha}u(x,\cdot)
is analytic in time with radius
r_{0}=1\wedge t_{0}
.

When
\Omega
is open and bounded, analogous results hold for solution of (
D
). If in addition
\Omega
satisfies (

H
J

), the same holds for solutions of (
N
).

Proof.

We prove the result for solutions of the Cauchy problem. For the Dirichlet and Neumann case the proof is analogous.

Let
u
be a solution of (
C
) with
p>2
corresponding to the initial datum
u_{0}\in C^{0}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\cap\mathrm{L}^{q}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
with
q\in[1,\infty]
and let
t\geq t_{0}>0
(we can let
t_{0}=0
when
q=\infty
).

We split the proof in several steps.

\circ~
Step 1.
Let us see that there exists a positive function
\mathsf{c}(t)
depending on
p
,
n
,
J
,
\mathsf{m}_{0}
,
\mathsf{M}_{p}
and
\mathsf{\bar{m}}_{p}(t)
such that

(5.25)

\|D^{\beta}\partial_{t}^{j}u(t)\|_{\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\leq\mathsf{c}(t),

holds for all
j\in\{0,\ldots,[p]-1\}
and all multi-index
\beta
such that
|\beta|\leq[p]-1
.

We prove it by induction on
j
.

Case
j=0
.
Let us see that
\|D^{\beta}u_{t}(t)\|_{\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\leq\mathsf{c}(t)
.

From (
5.19
) and (
5.20
) we have that

\displaystyle|D^{\beta}u_{t}(x,t)|

\displaystyle\leq A(p,J,\mathsf{m}_{0})\||D^{\beta}u(t)\|_{\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}+B(p,J,\mathsf{m}_{0},\mathsf{\bar{m}}_{p}(t))

\displaystyle\leq A(p,J,\mathsf{m}_{0})\mathsf{\bar{m}}_{p}(t)+B(p,J,\mathsf{m}_{0},\mathsf{\bar{m}}_{p}(t)):=\mathsf{c}(t).

where
\mathsf{m}_{0}
is given in (
5.9
) and
\mathsf{\bar{m}}_{p}(t)
is given in (
5.20
).

Inductive step:
assume that
\|D^{\beta}\partial_{t}^{j}u(t)\|_{\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\leq\mathsf{c}(t)
holds for all
j\in\{0,\ldots,[p]-2\}
and
\beta
such that
|\beta|\leq[p]-1
. Let us see that
\|D^{\beta}\partial_{t}^{[p]-1}u(t)\|_{\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\leq\mathsf{\tilde{c}}(t)
holds for any
|\beta|\leq[p]-1
for a suitable function
\mathsf{\tilde{c}}(t)
depending on
\mathsf{c}(t)
.

Let
h>0
. We use expression (
4.14
) to write

\partial_{t}^{[p]-1}u(x,t)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}J(x-y)\partial_{t}^{[p]-2}A_{p}(x,y,t^{*})\,dy,

where
t^{*}\in(t,t+h)
and
A_{p}(x,y,t):=L_{p}(u(y,t)-u(x,t))
.

Given
\beta
such that
|\beta|\leq[p]-1
, by using the Leibniz formula for derivatives we get

(5.26)

\displaystyle\begin{split}|D^{\beta}\partial_{t}^{[p]-1}u(x_{1},t)|&\leq\sum_{0\leq\alpha\leq\beta}\begin{pmatrix}\beta\\
\alpha\end{pmatrix}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left|D^{\alpha}J(x_{1}-z)\right|\left|D^{\beta-\alpha}\partial_{t}^{[p]-2}(A_{p}(x_{1},z,t^{*}))\right|\,dz\\
&=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left|J(x_{1}-z)\right|\left|D^{\beta}\partial_{t}^{[p]-2}(A_{p}(x_{1},z,t^{*}))\right|\,dz\\
&+\sum_{1\leq\alpha\leq\beta}\begin{pmatrix}\beta\\
\alpha\end{pmatrix}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left|D^{\alpha}J(x_{1}-z)\right|\left|D^{\beta-\alpha}\partial_{t}^{[p]-2}(A_{p}(x_{1},z,t^{*}))\right|\,dz.\end{split}

To bound (
5.26
) observe that the multivariate Faá di Bruno’s formula (
5.7
) in this case reads as

(5.27)

D^{\beta}\partial_{t}^{[p]-2}(A_{p}u(x,z,t^{*}))=L_{p}^{\prime}(u)D^{\beta}\partial_{t}^{[p]-2}u(x,t^{*})+R_{[p]-3,[p]-1}(u(y,t^{*})-u(x,t^{*}))

where
R_{[p]-3,[p]-1}u(x,t^{*})
involves derivatives
D^{\alpha}\partial_{t}^{m}u(x,t)
with
0\leq m\leq[p]-3
and
|\alpha|\leq[p]-1
and by inductive hypothesis can be bounded as

R_{[p]-3,[p]-1}\leq\mathsf{K}(p,\mathsf{m}_{0},\mathsf{\bar{m}}_{p}(t^{*}),\mathsf{M}_{p}).

Therefore, we can use
L_{p}^{\prime}(u(t^{*}))\leq\|u(t^{*})\|_{\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}
and the inductive hypothesis to bound (
5.27
) as

\displaystyle|D^{\beta}\partial_{t}^{[p]-2}(A_{p}u(x,z,t^{*}))|

\displaystyle\leq(p-1)\max\{\mathsf{m}_{0},\mathsf{m}_{0}^{p-2}\}|D^{\beta}\partial_{t}^{[p]-2}u(x,t^{*})|+\mathsf{K}

\displaystyle\leq(p-1)\max\{\mathsf{m}_{0},\mathsf{m}_{0}^{p-2}\}\mathsf{c}(t^{*})+\mathsf{K}

for all
\beta
such that
|\beta|\leq[p]-1
which used in (
5.26
) gives

|D^{\beta}\partial_{t}^{[p]-1}u(x_{1},t)|\leq\mathsf{C}_{1}(p,n,J,\mathsf{m}_{0},\mathsf{\bar{m}}_{p}(t^{*}),\mathsf{M}_{p},\mathsf{c}(t^{*})):=\mathsf{\tilde{c}}_{p}(t).

\circ~
Step 2.
Let
|\beta|\leq[p]-1
and
j\in\{0,\ldots,[p]-1\}
. By using the Leibniz formula for derivatives and adding and subtracting the term
\sum_{0\leq\alpha\leq\beta}\begin{pmatrix}\beta\\
\alpha\end{pmatrix}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}D^{\alpha}J(x_{1}-z)D^{\beta-\alpha}\partial_{t}^{j}(A_{p}(x_{2},z,t^{*}))\,dz
we get

\displaystyle\begin{split}|D^{\beta}\partial_{t}^{j}&u(x_{1},t)-D^{\beta}\partial_{t}^{j}u(x_{2},t)|\\
&\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}|J(x_{1}-z)|\left|D^{\beta}\partial_{t}^{j-1}(A_{p}(x_{1},z,t^{*})))-D^{\beta}\partial_{t}^{j-1}(A_{p}(x_{2},z,t^{*}))\right|\,dz\\
&\quad+\sum_{1\leq\alpha\leq\beta}\begin{pmatrix}\beta\\
\alpha\end{pmatrix}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}|D^{\alpha}J(x_{1}-z)|\left|D^{\beta-\alpha}\partial_{t}^{j-1}(A_{p}(x_{1},z,t^{*})))-D^{\beta-\alpha}\partial_{t}^{j-1}(A_{p}(x_{2},z,t^{*}))\right|\,dz\\
&\quad+\sum_{0\leq\alpha\leq\beta}\begin{pmatrix}\beta\\
\alpha\end{pmatrix}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left|D^{\alpha}J(x_{1}-z)-D^{\alpha}J(x_{2}-z)\right||D^{\beta-\alpha}\partial_{t}^{j-1}(A_{p}(x_{2},z,t^{*}))|\,dz.\end{split}

From the above expression and (
5.25
) we can proceed analogously to the proof of Theorem
5.9
to obtain that

(5.28)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\left|D^{\beta}\partial_{t}^{j}u(x_{1},t)-D^{\beta}\partial_{t}^{j}u(x_{2},t)\right|\leq A_{1}\left|D^{\beta}\partial_{t}^{j-1}u(x_{1},t^{*})-D^{\beta}\partial_{t}^{j-1}u(x_{2},t^{*})\right|+B_{1}\,\tilde{\omega}(|x_{1}-x_{2}|),\end{split}

where
A_{1}=A_{1}(m_{0},p,J)
,
B_{1}=B_{1}((p,n,J,\mathsf{m}_{0},\mathsf{M}_{p},\mathsf{\bar{m}}_{p}(t)))
, and
\tilde{\omega}(\rho):=\max\{\rho,\rho^{p-2},\omega_{J,p}(\rho)\}
.

\circ~
Step 3.
We can iterate (
5.28
) to get

\displaystyle|D^{\beta}\partial_{t}^{j}u(x_{1},t)

\displaystyle-D^{\beta}\partial_{t}^{j}u(x_{2},t)|\leq

\displaystyle\leq A_{1}\left|D^{\beta}\partial_{t}^{j-1}u(x_{1},t^{*}_{1})-D^{\beta}\partial_{t}^{j-1}u(x_{2},t^{*}_{1})\right|+B_{1}\tilde{\omega}(|x_{1}-x_{2}|)

\displaystyle\leq A_{1}\left(A_{2}\left|D^{\beta}\partial_{t}^{j-2}u(x_{1},t^{*}_{2})-D^{\beta}\partial_{t}^{j-2}u(x_{2},t^{*}_{2})\right|+B_{2}\tilde{\omega}(|x_{1}-x_{2}|)\right)+B_{1}\tilde{\omega}(|x_{1}-x_{2}|)

\displaystyle\qquad\vdots

\displaystyle\leq A_{1}\cdots A_{j}|D^{\beta}u(x_{1},t^{*}_{j})-D^{\beta}(x_{2},t^{*}_{j})|

\displaystyle\quad+\left(A_{1}\cdots A_{j-1}B_{j}+A_{1}\cdots A_{j-2}B_{j-1}+A_{1}\cdots A_{j-3}B_{j-2}+\cdots+B_{1}\right)\tilde{\omega}(|x_{1}-x_{2}|)

\displaystyle\leq\mathsf{A}|D^{\beta}u(x_{1},t^{*}_{j})-D^{\beta}(x_{2},t^{*}_{j})|+\mathsf{B}\tilde{\omega}(|x_{1}-x_{2}|)

\displaystyle\leq(\mathsf{A}\,\mathsf{K}(t)+\mathsf{B})\,\bar{\omega}(|x_{1}-x_{2}|)

where
\mathsf{A}=A_{1}\cdots A_{j}
and
\mathsf{B}
depends on
A_{i},B_{i}
,
i=1,\ldots,j
, and in the last inequality we have used (
5.12
) and denoted
\bar{\omega}(\rho):=\max\{\rho,\rho^{p-2},\omega_{J,p}(\rho),\omega(\rho)\}
. This gives that

\sup\left\{\frac{|D^{\beta}\partial_{t}^{j}u(x_{1},t)-D^{\beta}\partial_{t}^{j}u(x_{2},t)|}{\bar{\omega}(|x_{1}-x_{2}|)}\colon x_{1},x_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{n},x\neq y\right\}\leq(\mathsf{A}\,\mathsf{K}(t)+\mathsf{B}):={\mathsf{c}}(t)

and therefore
D^{\beta}\partial_{t}^{j}u(\cdot,t)\in C^{0,\bar{\omega}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
for all
|\beta|\leq[p]-1
and
j=0,\ldots,[p]-1
.

Appendix A
Gradient flow and EVI solutions

The Hille-Yosida theorem is a powerful tool when studying the linear semigroups theory and its connection with linear evolution problems. The study of nonlinear physical models led to the development of nonlinear functional analysis. In particular, the nonlinear semigroup theory was dealt and developed by Brézis, Benilan, Crandall, Kato, Komura, Pazy, among others. In this context we will consider
gradient flow
or
mild solutions
. For a nice introduction to this subject we refer to
[
2
]
.

Definition A.1

(Gradient flow)
.

We say that
u\colon(0,\infty)\to D(f)
is a
gradient flow
of
f
if
u\in AC_{loc}((0,\infty),H)
and

u^{\prime}(t)\in-\partial f(u(t))\quad a.e.\;t\in(0,\infty)

We say that
u(t)
starts from
u_{0}
if
u(t)\to u_{0}
as
t\to 0^{+}
.

The following result provides for gradient flow solution to nonlinear problem. For a proof we refer for instance to
[
2
]
.

Theorem A.2

(Brezis-Komura)
.

Assume that
f
is convex and lower semicontinuous. For every
u_{0}\in\overline{D(f)}
there exists a unique gradient flow
u(t)=S_{t}u_{0}
starting from
u_{0}
. The family of operators
S_{t}\colon\overline{D(f)}\to D(f)
,
t>0
, satisfies the semigroup property
S_{t+s}=S_{t}\circ S_{s}
and the contractivity property

\|S_{t}u_{0}-S_{t}y_{0}\|\leq\|u_{0}-y_{0}\|\quad u_{0},y_{0}\in\overline{D(f)}.

The
evolution variational inequality
(EVI) formulation of gradient flows provides for a useful mechanism to find solutions.

Definition A.3
.

A curve
u\in AC_{loc}((0,\infty),H)
is called an EVI solution if for any
v\in Dom(f)
one has

(A.1)

\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}\left(\|u(t)-v\|^{2}\right)\leq f(v)-f(u(t))\quad a.e.\;t\in(0,\infty).

We say that
u(t)
starts from
u_{0}
if
u(t)\to u_{0}
as
t\to 0^{+}
.

We recall that gradient flow solutions are equivalent to EVI solutions.

Proposition A.4
.

For a convex and lower semicontinuous function
f
with
D(f)\neq\emptyset
, a locally absolutely continuous curve
u
is a gradient flow if and only if it is an
EVI
solution.
