A new look at the controllability cost of linear evolution systems with a long gaze at localized data
Roberta Bianchini, Vincent Laheurte, Franck Sueur

Abstract
. We revisit the classical issue of the controllability/observability cost of linear first order evolution systems, starting with ODEs, before turning to some linear first order evolution PDEs in several space dimensions, including hyperbolic systems and pseudo-differential systems obtained by linearization in fluid mechanics. In particular we investigate the cost of localized initial data, and in the dispersive case, of initial data which are semi-classically microlocalized.



1.
Reminder on the cost of controllability of linear ODEs

In the setting of linear ODEs in finite dimensions, with variable coefficients, the issue of exact controllability can be set as follows.

Question 1.1
.

Given some time-dependent fields of matrices
A(t)
and
B(t)
, a positive time
T
, some initial and final states
b_{0}
and
b_{T}
,
is there a time-dependent field
f(t)
and a corresponding time-dependent field
b(t)
such that

(1)

b^{\prime}(t)+A(t)b(t)=B(t)f(t)\text{ for }t\in[0,T]\text{ and }(b(0),b(T))=(b_{0},b_{T})?

Above, it is understood that the dependence on the time
t
is smooth enough for the system to make sense and that the matrices and vectors are compatible in terms of their size.
A positive answer to Question
1.1
means by definition that the differential system
b^{\prime}(t)=A(t)b(t)
is
exactly controllable
in time
T
.

Let us observe that such a result is only non-trivial whenever the system is under-actuated, that is in the case where the matrices
B(t)
are not invertible. Otherwise, it is sufficient to consider any smooth field

b(t)
satisfying
(b(0),b(T))=(b_{0},b_{T})
, and then to define the control force field
f(t)
by

f(t):=B(t)^{-1}\Big(b^{\prime}(t)+A(t)b(t)\Big)\text{ for }t\in[0,T],

and then, the answer to Question
1.1
is positive.

In the case of a positive answer to Question
1.1
, one may investigate the minimal quadratic cost of such a control result.

Question 1.2
.

What is the infimum
\mathcal{C}=\mathcal{C}(T,A,B,b_{0},b_{T})
of

\int_{0}^{T}|f(t)|^{2}\,dt,

among the control force fields
f(t)
which verify Question
1.1
?

The Hilbert Uniqueness Method (HUM), introduced by Jacques-Louis Lions, relates the notions of controllability and observability by means of a duality argument, including the computation of the cost, see for example
[
6
, Chapter 1]
.
In the context above, in the particular case where
b(T)=0
, the so-called case of
null-controllability
,
this method yields that
\mathcal{C}
is the minimal constant for which for any
b_{0}
, up to renaming the adjoints
A^{*}(t)
and
B^{*}(t)
respectively by
A(t)
and
B(t)
,
the unique solution to the uncontrolled equation:

(2)

b^{\prime}(t)+A(t)b(t)=0\text{ for }t\in[0,T]\text{ and }b(0)=b_{0},

satisfies

(3)

|b(T)|^{2}\leqslant\mathcal{C}\int_{0}^{T}|B(t)b(t)|^{2}\,dt.

The interpretation of the inequality above is that the energy
|b(T)|^{2}
of the final state reached by the uncontrolled system at time
T
can be bounded by the energy

\int_{0}^{T}|B(t)b(t)|^{2}\,dt
which is observed through the field of matrices
B(t)
during the interval
(0,T)
, up to a constant
\mathcal{C}
. The minimal possible constant
\mathcal{C}
in (
3
) is also called the
observability cost
of the differential system:
b^{\prime}(t)+A(t)b(t)=0
on
(0,T)
.

Before giving a characterization of
\mathcal{C}
,
let us recall the following elementary result on differential Lyapunov equations.

Lemma 1.3
.

Let
A
and
B
some
continuous time-dependent fields of matrices.
Then there is a unique global solution
G
of
the differential Lyapunov equation:

Lyap(A,B):\quad G^{\prime}(t)-G(t)A(t)-A^{*}(t)G(t)=B^{*}(t)B(t)\text{ for }t\in[0,T]\text{ and }G(0)=0.

Moreover at any time
t
, the matrix
G(t)
is symmetric and nonnegative.
Finally, for any time
T
,

(4)

G(T)=\int_{0}^{T}R(t,T)^{*}B^{*}(t)B(t)R(t,T)\,\mathrm{d}t,

where, for any time
T
, the matrix-valued function

R(\cdot,T)
is the solution to the linear system

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}R(t,T)=-A(t)R(t,T),\quad R(T,T)=\operatorname{Id}.

Proof.

The existence and uniqueness of
G
and of
R(\cdot,T)
for any time
T
, as solutions of their respective problems,
follows from the linear finite-dimensional version of the Cauchy-Lipschitz theorem.
Then the symmetry follows from the uniqueness by observing that
G
and
G^{*}
satisfy the same equations.
Similarly, the formula (
4
) follows from the uniqueness by observing that the r.h.s. satisfies the differential Lyapunov equation.
Finally, the nonnegativity at any time
t\in[0,T]
of the matrix
G(t)
follows from this representation.
∎

Let us now recall a characterization of

\mathcal{C}
as the inverse of the minimal eigenvalue
\lambda_{\text{min}}({G}(T))
of
the so-called
observability Gramian matrix

G(T)
.

Theorem 1.4
.

The observability cost
\mathcal{C}
satisfies
\mathcal{C}=\lambda_{\text{min}}({G}(T))^{-1}
, where
G(T)
is the value at time
T
of the solution
G
to the
differential Lyapunov equation
Lyap(A,B)
.

A byproduct of Theorem
1.4
is that a necessary and sufficient condition for a linear time-varying finite-dimensional control system to be observable is that the observability Gramian
G(T)
is invertible.

Let us also mention that for time-invariant finite-dimensional control systems, this condition reduces to the famous Kalman rank condition, an algebraic condition which does not require any integration of linear differential equations.

Theorem
1.4
is well-known, see for example
[
6
, Chapter 1]
. However, we give below a proof which sets up a method which will be repeatedly used in some more difficult settings in the sequel.

Proof of Theorem
1.4
.

The starting idea is that there is an extra integral over time in (
3
) so that
\mathcal{C}^{-1}
can be interpreted as a time.
To restore this time in the left hand side of (
3
),
we introduce the following scalar field:

(5)

E_{R}(t):=G(t)b(t)\cdot b(t),

where the dot in (
5
) is the usual euclidean inner product.
The quantity
E_{R}
will be called the
recorded energy
.

In particular the recorded energy starts with zero initial value, that is

(6)

E_{R}(0)=0.

Moreover, by Leibniz’ rule and by using the equations of
G
and
b
,
its time derivative is given by

\displaystyle E_{R}^{\prime}=B^{*}Bb\cdot b=|Bb|^{2},

and then,

(7)

\displaystyle E_{R}(T)=\int_{0}^{T}|B(t)b(t)|^{2}\,dt.

Thus, at the final time
T
, the recorded energy is equal to the observed energy.

On the other hand, from the definition
5
, one may compare
E_{R}(T)
with the final energy
|b(T)|^{2}
by

\displaystyle\inf_{b_{0}\neq 0}\frac{G(T)b(T)\cdot b(T)}{|b(T)|^{2}}=\inf_{b(T)\neq 0}\frac{G(T)b(T)\cdot b(T)}{|b(T)|^{2}}=\lambda_{\text{min}}(G(T)).

This concludes the proof of Theorem
1.4
.
∎

While the proof above recalls the multiplier method, see for instance
[
18
]
, it is interesting to reinterpret in a slightly different way these computations, more precisely the identity (
7
), for the sequel. In this direction, first,
from (
2
), we deduce that the rank-one matrix

b(t)\otimes b(t)=b(t)b(t)^{*}
, which has to be interpreted as the energy tensor, satisfies the following ODE:

(8)

(b\otimes b)^{\prime}+A(b\otimes b)+(b\otimes b)A^{*}=0.

Moreover, the recorded energy reads

(9)

E_{R}=G:(b\otimes b),

where the notation
:
stands for the Frobenius product, that is for two squares matrices
A
and
B
of the same size,

A:B=\tr(A^{*}B)=B:A.

Recalling the following interplay between the Frobenius product and the matrix product:

(10)

A:(BC)=(AC^{*}):B\quad\text{ and }\quad A:(CB)=(C^{*}A):B,

we observe that the differential Lyapunov equation
Lyap(A,B)

is the dual equation of (
8
) with source term
B^{*}B
, meaning that if one tests the differential Lyapunov equation
Lyap(A,B)

with a time-dependent matrix
C(t)
by means of the Frobenius product and if one integrates by parts in time, then

(11)

\int_{0}^{T}G:(C^{\prime}+AC+CA^{*})=(G:C)(T)-\int_{0}^{T}B^{*}B:C.

This way, the identity (
7
) follows from choosing
C=b\otimes b
, recalling (
6
), (
8
) and (
9
).

2.
Case of a transport PDE

Let us now turn to the case of a simple PDE: the following linear transport equation:

(12)

\partial_{t}\bm{u}+(v\cdot\nabla)\bm{u}+A\bm{u}=0,\text{ for }t\in[0,T],

where the space variable is
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
d\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
,

v:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a given regular vector field and

A
is a given regular field of
{N\times N}
matrices depending on
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, with
N\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
. These matrices encode some damping or amplification along the transportation by
v
.
The final time
T>0
is also given and
the unknown is the vector field
\bm{u}:[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{N}
.
In this case, the cost is defined as follows.

Definition 2.1
.

The cost of observability by a regular field of
n\times N
matrices
B
depending on
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

is the minimal constant
\mathcal{C}=\mathcal{C}(T,v,A,B)
such that for any initial condition
\bm{u}_{0}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, the associated solution
\bm{u}(t)
to (
12
)
verifies the observability inequality:

(13)

\|\bm{u}(T,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{N})}^{2}\leqslant\mathcal{C}\int_{0}^{T}\|B\bm{u}(t)\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{n})}^{2}\mathrm{d}t.

Let us highlight that, in this setting,
the HUM method, which was mentioned in the previous section, still holds and the identification of
\mathcal{C}
with the cost of controllability of the adjoint system as well.

The natural question that arises is whether one can give a condition for the observability of system (
12
) and compute its cost, as done in Theorem
1.4
for ODE systems.

The following result, generalizing Theorem
1.4
, shows that
\mathcal{C}
can be computed in terms of the infimum over
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

of the minimal eigenvalues
\lambda_{\text{min}}(G_{x_{0}}(T))
of a family of Gramian matrices
G_{x_{0}}(T)

associated with time-dependent Lyapunov ODEs involving the coefficients of the PDE (
12
) evaluated along the characteristic flow induced by the vector field
v
.
This flow is denoted by
X_{x_{0}}(t)
and satisfies, for any
{x_{0}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

(14)

X^{\prime}_{x_{0}}(t)=v(X_{x_{0}}(t))\text{ for }t\in[0,T]\text{ and }X_{x_{0}}(0)={x_{0}}.

On the other hand, the Gramian matrices
G_{x_{0}}
are given as solutions to
Lyap(A_{{x_{0}}},B_{x_{0}})

where, for any
{x_{0}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and for any
t\in[0,T]
,

(15)

\displaystyle A_{{x_{0}}}(t):=A(X_{x_{0}}(t))-\frac{1}{2}(\operatorname{div}v)(X_{x_{0}}(t))\operatorname{Id}_{N}\quad\text{ and }\quad B_{x_{0}}(t):=B(X_{x_{0}}(t)).

Theorem 2.2
.

The observability cost
\mathcal{C}
satisfies

(16)

\mathcal{C}=\big(\inf_{{x_{0}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\,\lambda_{\text{min}}(G_{x_{0}}(T)\big)^{-1}.

A byproduct of Theorem
2.2
is that a necessary and sufficient condition for (
12
) to be observable by the field of matrices
B
is that the Gramian matrices
(G_{x_{0}}(T))_{{x_{0}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}
are uniformly invertible.

To prove Theorem
2.2
we will mimick the proof of Theorem
1.4
by
introducing a recorded energy to which we will apply the following lemma, where we assume that
M
and
F
are regular enough.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
\bm{u}(t)
be a solution of (
12
) associated with the initial condition
\bm{u}_{0}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.
Let
M
satisfying the following PDE

(17)

\partial_{t}M+(v\cdot\nabla)M+(\operatorname{div}v)M-A^{*}M-MA=F,

where
F(t,x)
is a time-dependent family of matrices.
Let

(18)

\displaystyle E[M,u]:=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(\bm{u}\otimes\bm{u}):M.

Then its time derivative satisfies for all
t\in[0,T]
,

\displaystyle E[M,u]^{\prime}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(\bm{u}\otimes\bm{u}):F.

Proof.

By Leibniz’ rule, (
12
)
twice, integration by parts and a transposition,
we obtain that the time derivative
E[M,u]^{\prime}
of
E[M,u]

is given by

(19)

\displaystyle E[M,u]^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\Big(\partial_{t}M+(v\cdot\nabla)M+(\operatorname{div}v)M-A^{*}M-MA\Big)\bm{u}\cdot\bm{u}\,dx.

Then it is sufficient to use (
17
) to conclude.
∎

Another elementary ingredient in the proof of Theorem
2.2
is the following result.

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
m:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
be a continuous field of symmetric non negative matrices. Then

\inf_{\|u\|_{L^{2}}=1}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}m(x)u(x)\cdot u(x)\,\mathrm{d}x=\inf_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\lambda_{\rm{min}}(m(x)),

where the infimum on the left hand side is taken over all the vector fields
u
in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})

with
\|u\|_{L^{2}}=1
.

Proof.

Let

C:=\inf_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\lambda_{\rm{min}}(m(x)).

On the one hand,
for any
u\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
,
for any
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

m(x)u(x)\cdot u(x)\geq C|u(x)|^{2},

so that, by integration,

(20)

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}m(x)u(x)\cdot u(x)\,\mathrm{d}x\geq C\|u\|_{L^{2}}.

On the other hand, for any
\varepsilon>0
, we choose
x_{\varepsilon}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that
\lambda_{\rm{min}}(m(x_{\varepsilon}))\leq C+\varepsilon
, as well as
b_{\varepsilon}\in\mathcal{S}^{N-1}
an eigenvector of
m(x_{\varepsilon})
associated with
\lambda_{\rm{min}}(m(x_{\varepsilon}))
, so that

m(x_{\varepsilon})b_{\varepsilon}=\lambda_{\rm{min}}(m(x_{\varepsilon}))b_{\varepsilon}.

Finally, we set, for any
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

u_{\varepsilon}(x):=(\pi\varepsilon)^{-\frac{d}{4}}b_{\varepsilon}e^{-\frac{|x-x_{\varepsilon}|^{2}}{2\varepsilon}}.

Then, for any
\varepsilon>0
,
\|u_{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{2}}=1
and, since
m
is continuous,

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}m(x)u_{\varepsilon}(x)\cdot u_{\varepsilon}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x=C.

Combining this equality with (
20
), we conclude the proof of the lemma.
∎

We are now equipped to start the proof of Theorem
2.2
.

Proof of Theorem
2.2
.

Let
M
be given by setting for any
t
and
x
,

(21)

G_{x}(t)=M(t,X_{x}(t)).

We highlight that, for any
t
, the mapping
x\mapsto X_{x}(t)
is invertible, since its Jacobian satisfies a linear first order ODE with
1
as initial condition.
Then we observe that it follows from the fact that the Gramian matrices
G_{x}
are solutions to
Lyap(A_{{x}},B_{x})
and from the chain rule that

M(t,x)
is a nonnegative symmetric matrix for any
t
and
x
, that
M
satisfies
an equation
of the type (
17
) with
F=B^{*}B
and that
M(0,\cdot)=0
.
Therefore, by the lemma
2.3
and an integration on
[0,T]
,
the recorded energy defined by

E_{R}:=E[M,u]

satisfies

(22)

\displaystyle E_{R}(T)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|B\bm{u}(t,x)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}t.

On the other hand,
since the map
\bm{u}_{0}\mapsto\bm{u}(T,\cdot)
is a bijection from
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
to itself,
by Lemma
2.4
, we obtain

(23)

\inf_{\bm{u}_{0}\neq 0}\frac{E_{R}(T)}{\|\bm{u}(T,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}}=\inf_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\lambda_{\rm{min}}(M(T,x))=\inf_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\lambda_{\rm{min}}(G_{x}(T)),

since
x\mapsto X_{x}(T)
is a bijection from
\mathbb{R}^{d}
to itself.
We conclude the proof of Theorem
2.2
by gathering
(
22
)
and
(
23
).
∎

3.
A localization in space and amplitude of the cost

While the definition of observability cost is rather
global
in space, we consider below the observability cost

(24)

\mathcal{C}[\bm{u}_{0}]:=\frac{\|\bm{u}(T,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}}{\int_{0}^{T}\|B\bm{u}(t)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t},

of the initial data
\bm{u}_{0}
(at time
T
)
whose local energy tensor
\bm{u}_{0}\otimes\bm{u}_{0}
are
localized
, for the weak-* topology of the space
\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{N\times N})
of the matrix-valued measures, at
(b_{0}\otimes b_{0})\delta_{x_{0}}
, for some

x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
b_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
.
Above
\bm{u}
is the solution
of the transport PDE (
12
) associated with
\bm{u}_{0}
.
Since it is a linear equation, the cost
\mathcal{C}[\bm{u}_{0}]
is homogeneous of degree
0
with respect to the initial data
\bm{u}_{0}
.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and set
G_{T}:=G_{x_{0}}(T)
, where
G_{x_{0}}
is the solution to the differential Lyapounov equation
Lyap(A_{x_{0}},B_{x_{0}})
, where we recall the notation (
15
).
Let
b_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus\{0\}
and denote by

b_{T}^{\mathcal{N}}:=\frac{b_{x_{0},b_{0}}(T)}{|b_{x_{0},b_{0}}(T)|},

where

b_{x_{0},b_{0}}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus\{0\}
is the solution
of
the linear ordinary differential equation:

(25)

b_{x_{0},b_{0}}^{\prime}=-A_{x_{0}}b_{x_{0},b_{0}},\quad\text{ with }\quad b_{x_{0},b_{0}}(0)=b_{0}.

Let
(\bm{u}_{0}^{n})_{n}
be a sequence in
C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\cap L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{N})
such that

(26)

\bm{u}_{0}^{n}\otimes\bm{u}_{0}^{n}\rightharpoonup(b_{x_{0},b_{0}}\otimes b_{x_{0},b_{0}})\delta_{x_{0}}\quad\text{ weak-* in }\,\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}),\quad\text{ as }\,n\rightarrow+\infty.

Then,

\mathcal{C}[\bm{u}_{0}^{n}]^{-1}\rightarrow(b_{T}^{\mathcal{N}})^{*}\,G_{T}\,b_{T}^{\mathcal{N}},\quad\text{ as }\,n\rightarrow+\infty.

To prove Theorem
3.1
, it is interesting to follow the view-point introduced at the end of Section
1
and to consider the local energy tensor
u\otimes u
.
By elementary operations, we deduce from (
12
) that
u\otimes u
satisfies the transport PDE:

(27)

(\partial_{t}+v\cdot\nabla)(u\otimes u)+A(u\otimes u)+(u\otimes u)A^{*}=0.

While we consider some vector fields
u
with
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{N})
regularity in space, the equation (
27
) makes sense and the Cauchy problem is well-posed, even if

u\otimes u
is a matrix-valued measure
\mu
(or even more generally for a distribution) rather than a
L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{N\times N})
function.
As we are interested in the case where the local energy tensor
u\otimes u
concentrates in a Dirac mass, it is useful to recall the following elementary result on the Cauchy problem for transport equations with measure solutions.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
n,N\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
and
T>0
.
Let
V
be a regular field of vector field from
\mathbb{R}^{n}
to
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
A,\tilde{A}
some smooth vector fields of matrices in
\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
.
Let
\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R}^{n};\mathbb{R}^{N\times N})
be the space of matrix-valued measures and

\mathfrak{C}:=C([0,T];\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R}^{n};\mathbb{R}^{N\times N})-w*),

the space of the measures depending on time in a continuous way when
\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R}^{n};\mathbb{R}^{N\times N})
is endowed by its weak-* topology.
Then

(1)

For any
\mu_{0}
in
\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R}^{n};\mathbb{R}^{N\times N})
, there exists a unique solution

\mu\in\mathfrak{C},

to the PDE:

(28)

\partial_{t}\mu+\operatorname{div}(\mu V)+A\mu+\mu\tilde{A}=0,

with initial value
\mu_{0}
.

(2)

If the initial datum
\mu_{0}
is of the form

\mu_{0}^{y_{0},P_{0}}=P_{0}\delta_{y_{0}}
with

y_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
P_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}

then the unique corresponding solution is given by

(29)

\mu^{y_{0},P_{0}}(t,\cdot)=P(t)\delta_{y(t)},

where
y
solves the nonlinear ODE:

(30)

y^{\prime}=V(y)\quad\text{ and }\quad y(0)=y_{0},

and

P\in C^{1}([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{N\times N})
solves the linear ODE:

(31)

P^{\prime}+(A\circ y)P+P(\tilde{A}\circ y)=0\quad\text{ and }\quad P(0)=P_{0}.

(3)

Finally, if
(\mu_{0}^{n})_{n}
a sequence in
\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R}^{n};\mathbb{R}^{N\times N})
which converges weakly-* to
\mu_{0}^{y_{0},P_{0}}
, then the corresponding solution

\mu^{n}
converges to
\mu^{y_{0},P_{0}}
in
\mathfrak{C}
as
n\rightarrow+\infty
.

Proof.

The proof is very standard and we only give here a few words of comments.
The existence follows from the characteristics method.
Indeed solutions can be represented as superpositions of the atomic case considered in the second item.
The uniqueness and stability parts of the statement can be proved by a duality argument, in the spirit of
[
9
]
, or by a quantitative approach, thanks to matricial Wasserstein distances, see
[
4
,
20
]
.
∎

Equipped with this result, we are now ready to prove Theorem
3.1
.

Proof of Theorem
3.1
.

Let

X_{x_{0}}
be given by (
14
) and
b_{x_{0},b_{0}}
by (
25
).
Then, by Leibniz’ rule,

(b_{x_{0},b_{0}}\otimes b_{x_{0},b_{0}})^{\prime}=-A_{x_{0}}(b_{x_{0},b_{0}}\otimes b_{x_{0},b_{0}})-(b_{x_{0},b_{0}}\otimes b_{x_{0},b_{0}})A_{x_{0}}^{*}.

where we recall that
A_{x_{0}}
is given by (
15
).
Let

\mu(t,\cdot):=(b_{x_{0},b_{0}}(t)\otimes b_{x_{0},b_{0}}(t))\delta_{X_{x_{0}}(t)}.

Therefore, by the second item of Lemma
3.2
,

\mu
is the unique solution to

(32)

\partial_{t}\mu+\operatorname{div}(\mu V)+\Big(A-\frac{1}{2}(\operatorname{div}v)\operatorname{Id}\Big)\mu+\mu\Big(A^{*}-\frac{1}{2}(\operatorname{div}v)\operatorname{Id}\Big)=0,

with the initial data

\mu_{0}=(b_{0}\otimes b_{0})\delta_{x_{0}}
.

Let
\bm{u}^{n}
be the solution
of the transport PDE (
12
) associated with the initial data
\bm{u}_{0}^{n}
, and set

\mu^{n}:=\bm{u}^{n}\otimes\bm{u}^{n}
the corresponding local energy tensor. Then
by (
27
),

(33)

\partial_{t}\mu^{n}+\operatorname{div}(\mu^{n}V)+\Big(A-\frac{1}{2}(\operatorname{div}v)\operatorname{Id}\Big)\mu^{n}+\mu^{n}\Big(A^{*}-\frac{1}{2}(\operatorname{div}v)\operatorname{Id}\Big)=0.

Then, by (
26
) and the third item of Lemma
3.2
, we obtain that

\mu^{n}
converges to
\mu
in
\mathfrak{C}
as
n\rightarrow+\infty
.

Now, considering again
M
given by (
21
)
one may re-use
(
22
), which can actually be re-obtained as a consequence of the fact that (
17
) is the dual equation of (
27
) with
B^{*}B
as a source term, that is:

(34)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\|B\bm{u}^{n}(t)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}M(T,x):\mu^{n}(T,x)\,\mathrm{d}x,

while

(35)

\displaystyle\|\bm{u}^{n}(T,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\operatorname{Id}:\mu^{n}(T,x)\,\mathrm{d}x.

Thus the proof of Theorem
3.1
follows
by passing to the limit in these two expressions, recalling
(
21
) for the first one.

4.
Microlocalized cost

As we have seen before, the concept of energy is at the center of the observability issue. In the previous sections, in the case of some transport equations, we considered the energy tensor
\bm{u}\otimes\bm{u}
, which encodes the localization of the energy in space, with respect to the components of
\bm{u}
. However, for more general evolution linear first order PDEs, in several space dimensions, dispersive effects occur and the propagation of energy depends not only on the space variable
x
, but also on the frequency variable
\xi
. Hence we need a tool to encode the distribution of energy in the phase space, generalizing the energy tensor
\bm{u}\otimes\bm{u}
. To this end, the natural object to consider is the matrix Wigner transform, which we consider in its semiclassical version, focusing on high frequencies.

4.1.
Semiclassical Wigner matrix transform

Below
\varepsilon\in(0,1]
. However the regime of interest later on corresponds to this parameter
\varepsilon
converging to
0
, its inverse standing for the typical size of the frequencies under consideration.

Definition 4.1
.

For
u
and
v
in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\mathbb{C}^{N})
, we define the
Wigner matrix
W^{\varepsilon}[u,v](x,y)
, for any
x,\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
, by

W^{\varepsilon}[u,v](x,\xi):=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}u\left(x-\varepsilon\frac{y}{2}\right)\otimes\overline{v}\left(x+\varepsilon\frac{y}{2}\right)e^{iy\cdot\xi}\,\mathrm{d}y.

The mapping
(u,v)\mapsto W^{\varepsilon}[u,v]
is bilinear and verifies.

(36)

W^{\varepsilon}[u,v]=\left(W^{\varepsilon}[v,u]\right)^{*}.

In the case where
u=v
we simply denote by
W^{\varepsilon}[u]
the Wigner transform
W^{\varepsilon}[u,u]
, which is a field of symmetric matrices.
The Wigner matrix encodes the energy in the phase space in the following sense: the marginals of
W^{\varepsilon}[u,v]
respectively satisfy, formally,
for any
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, for any
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}W^{\varepsilon}[u,v]\,\mathrm{d}\xi

\displaystyle=u(x)\otimes\overline{v}(x),

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}W^{\varepsilon}[u,v]\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(2\pi\varepsilon)^{d}}\widehat{u}\left(\frac{\xi}{\varepsilon}\right)\otimes\left(\widehat{v}\right)\left(\frac{\xi}{\varepsilon}\right),

where
\hat{u},\hat{v}
denote the Fourier transforms of
u
and
v
.
In particular,

(37)

\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}\Big(W^{\varepsilon}[u]:\operatorname{Id}\Big)\,\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}\xi=\|u\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{N})}^{2},

where we recall that the notation
:
stands for the Frobenius product.

We also have, for any
1\leq j,k\leq N
,

\left|W_{j,k}^{\varepsilon}[u,v](x,\xi)\right|\leq\frac{1}{(\pi\varepsilon)^{d}}\|u_{j}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\mathbb{C})}\|v_{k}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\mathbb{C})},

where
W_{j,k}^{\varepsilon}[u,v]
denotes the coefficient
(j,k)
of the Wigner matrix, and
u_{j}
and
v_{k}
respectively denote the
j
-th component of
u
and the
k
-th component of
v
. This implies that the Wigner matrix
W[u,v]
is in
L^{\infty}
, from
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
to
\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
equipped with the Frobenius norm, and that

\|W^{\varepsilon}[u,v]\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq\frac{1}{(\pi\varepsilon)^{d}}\|u\|_{L^{2}}\|v\|_{L^{2}}.

The Wigner matrix
W^{\varepsilon}[u,v]
is also in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{N\times N})
, with

\|W^{\varepsilon}[u,v]\|_{L^{2}}=\|u\|_{L^{2}}\|v\|_{L^{2}}.

For more details about those properties, we refer, for instance, to
[
19
]
.

Remark 4.2
.

The Wigner matrix
W^{\varepsilon}[u]
carries information about the energy tensor
u\otimes u
, but is not necessarily nonnegative. However, for positivity, one may consider the Husimi transform instead, or Wigner measures, which are weak limits of Wigner matrices as
\varepsilon
goes to 0, in some sense. For our purpose, a sign is not required so we will not use those tools.

Below we recall some classical examples.

Example 4.3
.

An important example is given by the coherent states defined, for any
x_{0},\xi_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, for any
\mathbf{b}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
, as follows:

\bm{u}^{\varepsilon}_{x_{0},\xi_{0},\mathbf{b}_{0}}(x):=(\pi\varepsilon)^{-\frac{d}{4}}\mathbf{b}_{0}e^{i\frac{(x-x_{0})\cdot\xi_{0}}{\varepsilon}}e^{-\frac{|x-x_{0}|^{2}}{2\varepsilon}}.

Then one may compute

W^{\varepsilon}[\bm{u}^{\varepsilon}_{x_{0},\xi_{0},\mathbf{b}_{0}}](x,\xi)=(\pi\varepsilon)^{-d}(\mathbf{b}_{0}\otimes\mathbf{b}_{0})e^{-\frac{|x-x_{0}|^{2}+|\xi-\xi_{0}|^{2}}{\varepsilon}}.

In particular, the matrix Wigner transform

\mathcal{W}^{\varepsilon}[\bm{u}^{\varepsilon}_{x_{0},\xi_{0},\mathbf{b}_{0}}]
weakly converges, as
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0
, in the sense of distributions to
(\mathbf{b}_{0}\otimes\mathbf{b}_{0})\delta_{x_{0},\xi_{0}}
.

Example 4.4
.

Another classical example is given by the WKB states defined,
for any
\alpha\in(0,1]
, any
\xi_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and any amplitude function
a(x)\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, by

u^{\varepsilon}_{a,\alpha}(x):=a(x)e^{i\frac{x\cdot\xi_{0}}{\varepsilon^{\alpha}}}.

Then, as
\varepsilon\to 0
, for any
\alpha\in(0,1)
,

W^{\varepsilon}[u^{\varepsilon}_{a,\alpha}]\mathrel{\overset{\makebox[0.0pt]{\mbox{\tiny w*}}}{\to}}a(x)\otimes a(x)\delta_{0}(\xi)\text{ in }\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}),

while in the case where
\alpha=1
,

W^{\varepsilon}[u^{\varepsilon}_{a,\alpha}]\mathrel{\overset{\makebox[0.0pt]{\mbox{\tiny w*}}}{\to}}a(x)\otimes a(x)\delta_{\xi_{0}}(\xi)\text{ in }\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}).

4.2.
Pseudodifferential operators.

Our analysis allows to consider not only PDEs but also pseudo-differential equations, what is of interest to cover equations from incompressible fluid mechanics. In microlocal analysis,
different kinds of quantizations are available, see for instance to
[
22
, Chapter 4]
. Here we consider
the Weyl quantization, which associates with a symbol
a=a(x,\xi)
in a suitable class the semiclassical pseudo-differential operator
\w[a]

defined through its action on functions
u\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, by

\displaystyle(\w[a]u)(x):=(2\pi\varepsilon)^{-d}\int e^{\frac{i(x-y)\cdot\xi}{\varepsilon}}a\left(\frac{x+y}{2},\xi\right)u(y)\;\mathrm{d}\xi\,\mathrm{d}y.

Throughout this paper, we will simply assume every symbol to be in the class
S(1)
of functions bounded as well as all their derivatives, defined by:

(38)

S(1):=\{a\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2d}):\forall\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{2d},\exists C_{\alpha}>0,\forall(x,\xi)\in\mathbb{R}^{2d},\|\partial^{\alpha}a\|_{\infty}\leq C_{\alpha}\}.

Wider classes of symbols may be studied, but they are not required for the analysis which we perform in this paper. With that choice of class, the pseudo-differential operator given above defines a linear continuous operator from
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\mathbb{C}^{N})
to itself, and there exists a constant
C>0
, uniform in
\varepsilon
, as well as a universal constant
\gamma
, such that, for all
a\in S(1),
it holds

(39)

\|\w[a]\|_{\mathcal{L}(L^{2};L^{2})}\leq C\sup_{|\alpha|+|\beta|\leq\gamma d}\|\partial_{x}^{\alpha}\partial_{\xi}^{\beta}a\|_{\infty},

where
\mathcal{L}(L^{2};L^{2})
denotes the space of linear bounded operators from
L^{2}
to itself, endowed with the usual operator norm. From now on, we denote for
k
in
\mathbb{N}
by

\mathcal{N}_{k}
the norm on
S(1)
:

(40)

\mathcal{N}_{k}(\cdot):=\sup_{|\alpha|+|\beta|\leq k}\|\partial_{x}^{\alpha}\partial_{\xi}^{\beta}\cdot\|_{\infty}.

We list below some useful properties of calculus of the Weyl quantization. More details, other choices of quantizations and other classes of symbols can be found in
[
19
]
,
[
22
]
.

Lemma 4.5

(Adjoint operator.)
.

Let
a\in S(1)
be a symbol. Then the adjoint operator of
\w[a]
is given by

(41)

(\w[a])^{*}=\w[a^{*}].

Lemma 4.6

(Composition of operators.)
.

Let
a,b\in S(1)
be two symbols. Then the following composition law holds:

(42)

\w[a]\w[b]=\w[ab]+\frac{\varepsilon}{2i}\w[\{a,b\}]+O(\varepsilon^{2})_{\mathcal{L}(L^{2};L^{2})},

where
\{\cdot,\cdot\}
denotes the Poisson bracket

\{a,b\}=(\partial_{\xi}a)(\partial_{x}b)-(\partial_{x}a)(\partial_{\xi}b).

The Wigner transform is associated with the semiclassical Weyl quantization by the relation:

(43)

\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}\Big(W^{\varepsilon}[u,v]:a(x,\xi)\Big)\,\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}\xi=\langle\w[a^{*}]u,v\rangle_{L^{2}},

for any
a(x,\xi)
in
S(1)
, and any
u,v\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\mathbb{C}^{N})
.
This property allows us to view Wigner matrices as tempered distributions in
S(1)^{\prime}
, the dual space of
S(1)
. Indeed, for any
u\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\mathbb{C}^{N})
, combining the property (
43
) with the estimate (
39
) yields the existence of a constant
C=C(u)>0
, uniform in
\varepsilon
, such that for any
a\in S(1)
it holds:

\left|\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}W^{\varepsilon}[u](x,\xi):a(x,\xi)\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}\xi\right|\leq C\mathcal{N}_{\gamma d}(a),

which allows us to consider
W^{\varepsilon}[u]
as a distribution of order at most
\gamma d
(where
W^{\varepsilon}[u]=W^{\varepsilon}[u,u]
as in (
37
)). More precisely, for any
u,v\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\mathbb{C}^{N})
, we have
W^{\varepsilon}[u,v]\in S(1)^{\prime}_{\gamma d}(\mathbb{R}^{2d})
, where

S(1)^{\prime}_{k}(\mathbb{R}^{2d})=\left\{\psi(x,\xi)\in S(1)^{\prime}:\exists C>0,\forall\varphi\in S(1),\left|\langle\psi,\varphi\rangle_{\mathcal{S}^{\prime},\mathcal{S}}\right|\leq C\mathcal{N}_{k}(\varphi)\right\}.

We endow
S(1)^{\prime}_{k}(\mathbb{R}^{2d})
with the norm

\|\psi\|_{\mathcal{S}^{\prime}_{k}}:=\sup_{\phi\in S(1),\mathcal{N}_{k}(\phi)\leq 1}\langle\psi,\phi\rangle_{S(1)^{\prime},S(1)}.

For any
u,v\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\mathbb{C}^{N})
, and
a\in S(1),

(44)

W^{\varepsilon}[\w[a]u,v]=aW^{\varepsilon}[u,v]+\frac{\varepsilon}{2i}\{a,W^{\varepsilon}[u,v]\}+\varepsilon^{2}\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon},

where the remainder term
\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon}\in S(1)^{\prime}_{\gamma d+2}(\mathbb{R}^{2d})
verifies

\|\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon}\|_{S(1)^{\prime}_{\gamma d+2}}=O(1).

4.3.
Statement of the result

With the previous material, it is natural to extend our setting to hyperbolic pseudo-differential equations of the form

(45)

\displaystyle\varepsilon\partial_{t}\bm{u}+\w[iH(x,\xi)\operatorname{Id}+\varepsilon A(x,\xi)]\bm{u}=0,\quad\text{on }[0,T],

where the principal part
H
is a real scalar symbol in
S(1)

and the sub-principal part
A
is a matrix-valued symbol in
S(1)
.

Our setting also allows to consider a wider class of observability operators given as well by pseudodifferential operators associated with a symbol matrix
B
in
S(1)
. Thus we are led to the following extension of the notion of observability cost
\mathcal{C}[\bm{u}_{0}]
of an initial data
\bm{u}_{0}
at time
T
for the equation (
45
)
by the pseudodifferential observability operator of symbol
B
in
S(1)
.

Definition 4.7
.

Let
T>0
.
Let
(x_{0},\xi_{0},b_{0})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times(\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\{0\})\times\mathbb{R}^{N}
and

\bm{u}_{0}
in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{N})
.
The observability cost
\mathcal{C}[\bm{u}_{0}]
of the initial data
\bm{u}_{0}
at time
T
for the equation (
45
)
by the observability operator of symbol
B
in
S(1)

is defined by

(46)

\mathcal{C}[\bm{u}_{0}]:=\frac{\|\bm{u}(T,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}}{\int_{0}^{T}\|\w[B]\bm{u}(t)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t},

where
\bm{u}
is the solution
of (
45
) associated with
\bm{u}_{0}
.

The purpose of this section is to study the observability cost
of initial data which are localized at
(x_{0},\xi_{0},\mathbf{b})
in the following sense.

Definition 4.8
.

A family
(\bm{u}_{0}^{\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon\in(0,1]}
in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{N})
is said to be localized at

(x_{0},\xi_{0},b_{0})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times(\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\{0\})\times\mathcal{S}^{N-1}
if its
Wigner matrix satisfies

\mathcal{W}^{\varepsilon}[\bm{u}_{0}^{\varepsilon}]\mathrel{\overset{\makebox[0.0pt]{\mbox{\tiny w*}}}{\to}}(b_{0}\otimes b_{0})\delta_{x_{0},\xi_{0}}\quad\text{ in }\,\mathcal{(}S(1))^{\prime},\quad\text{ as }\,\varepsilon\rightarrow 0.

The question at stake is then the following.

Question 4.9
.

Let
T>0
.
Let
(x_{0},\xi_{0},b_{0})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times(\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\{0\})\times\mathbb{R}^{N}
and
a family
(\bm{u}_{0}^{\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon\in(0,1]}
in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{N})
localized at
(x_{0},\xi_{0},b_{0})
.
Can we determine the limit of
the observability cost

\mathcal{C}[\bm{u}_{0}^{\varepsilon}]

as
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0
?

The result below provides an answer in terms of the Hamiltonian flow induced by the principal symbol
H
, the microlocalized observability Gramian matrices, the bicharacteristic equations and the bicharacteristic amplitude equation whose definitions are now given.

Definition 4.10
.

Let
H
be a real scalar symbol in
S(1)
and
v_{H}
the divergence free vector field from
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
to
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
defined by

(47)

v_{H}:=(\nabla_{\xi}H,-\nabla_{x}H).

For any
x_{0},\xi_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
we define the Hamiltonian flow
(X_{x_{0},\xi_{0}}(t),\Xi_{x_{0},\xi_{0}}(t))

induced by
H
as the unique solution to the nonlinear ODE:

(48)

\big(X_{x_{0},\xi_{0}},\Xi_{x_{0},\xi_{0}}\big)^{\prime}=v_{H}\big((X_{x,\xi},\Xi_{x_{0},\xi_{0}})\big),\quad\left(X_{x_{0},\xi_{0}},\Xi_{x_{0},\xi_{0}}\right)(0)=(x_{0},\xi_{0}).

Remark 4.11
.

It is worth noticing that the determinant

J(t):=\det(D(X_{x_{0},\xi_{0}}(t),\Xi_{x_{0},\xi_{0}}(t)))
of the Jacobian matrix associated with the Hamiltonian flow satisfies

J(t)=1
for all
t\in[0,T]
since

J^{\prime}(t)=\operatorname{\bf{div}}\left(\nabla_{\xi}H,-\nabla_{x}H\right)J(t)=0

and
J(0)=0
.
Here and below we use the notation
\operatorname{\bf{div}}
to denote the divergence operator in
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

We now define the microlocalized observability Gramian matrices, as solutions
of some differential Lyapunov equations involving the coefficients of (
45
) along the Hamiltonian flow.

Definition 4.12
.

For any
x_{0},\xi_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we define the Gramian matrix microlocalized in
(x_{0},\xi_{0})
as the unique solution
G_{x_{0},\xi_{0}}
to the differential Lyapunov equation
Lyap(A_{x_{0},\xi_{0}},B_{x_{0},\xi_{0}})
where the time-dependent matrices

A_{x_{0},\xi_{0}}
and
B_{x_{0},\xi_{0}}
are given by

A_{x_{0},\xi_{0}}(t):=A(X_{x_{0},\xi_{0}}(t),\Xi_{x_{0},\xi_{0}}(t))\quad\text{ and }\quad B_{x_{0},\xi_{0}}(t):=B(X_{x_{0},\xi_{0}}(t),\Xi_{x_{0},\xi_{0}}(t)).

Finally we define the bicharacteristic amplitude.

Definition 4.13
.

For any
x_{0},\xi_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, for any
\mathbf{b}_{0}
in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
,
we define
the bicharacteristic amplitude
\mathbf{b}_{x_{0},\xi_{0},\mathbf{b}_{0}}(t)
associated with
H
and
A

as the unique solution to the linear ODE:

(49)

\mathbf{b}_{x_{0},\xi_{0},\mathbf{b}_{0}}^{\prime}=-A_{x_{0},\xi_{0}}\,\mathbf{b}_{x_{0},\xi_{0},\mathbf{b}_{0}},\quad\mathbf{b}_{x_{0},\xi_{0},\mathbf{b}_{0}}(0)=\mathbf{b}_{0}.

The result below answers to Question
4.9
and identifies the limit of
the observability cost

\mathcal{C}[\bm{u}_{0}^{\varepsilon}]
of a family
(\bm{u}_{0}^{\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon\in(0,1]}
in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{N})
localized at
(x_{0},\xi_{0},b_{0})
as
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0
in terms of
the Rayleigh quotient of the microlocalized observability Gramian matrix along the bicharacteristic flow with the bicharacteristic amplitude, at the final time.

Theorem 4.14
.

Let
T>0
.
Let
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\xi_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\{0\}
and set

G_{T}:=G_{x_{0},\xi_{0}}(T),

where
G_{x_{0},\xi_{0}}
is the Gramian matrix microlocalized in
(x_{0},\xi_{0})
as given by Definition
4.12
.
Let
\mathbf{b}_{0}\in\mathcal{S}^{N-1}
and set

\mathbf{b}_{T}^{\mathcal{N}}:=\frac{\mathbf{b}_{x_{0},\xi_{0},\mathbf{b}_{0}}(T)}{|\mathbf{b}_{x_{0},\xi_{0},\mathbf{b}_{0}}(T)|},

where
\mathbf{b}_{x_{0},\xi_{0},\mathbf{b}_{0}}
is the transported amplitude associated with
H
and
A
given by
Definition
4.13
.
Let
(\bm{u}_{0}^{\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon\in(0,1]}
in
C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\cap L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{N})
be localized at
(x_{0},\xi_{0},b_{0})
in the sense of Definition
4.8
.
Then the observability cost
\mathcal{C}[\bm{u}_{0}^{\varepsilon}]
satisfies

\mathcal{C}[\bm{u}_{0}^{\varepsilon}]^{-1}\rightarrow(\mathbf{b}_{T}^{\mathcal{N}})^{*}\,G_{T}\,\mathbf{b}_{T}^{\mathcal{N}},\quad\text{ as }\,\varepsilon\rightarrow 0.

Let us highlight that the interest of the result above is that the cost of observability of initial data which are localized at
(x_{0},\xi_{0},b_{0})
converges to a limit which can determined by ODEs.
It can be considered as a broad extension of the result obtained in the case of transport equations in Theorem
3.1
, to general hyperbolic pseudo-differential equations of the form (
45
), in the high frequency limit, despite the extra dispersive effects. As a matter of fact,
the equation (
12
) can be recast as an equation of the form (
45
)
with

(50)

H(x,\xi)=v(x)\cdot\xi\quad\text{and}\quad A(x,\xi):=A(x)-\frac{1}{2}(\operatorname{div}v(x))\operatorname{Id}.

Then the the bicharacteristic equations (
48
) reduce to

(51)

\displaystyle X^{\prime}_{x_{0},\xi_{0}}(t)=v(X_{x_{0},\xi_{0}}(t)),\,\text{ and }\,\Xi^{\prime}_{x_{0},\xi_{0}}(t)=-(\nabla_{x}v(X_{x_{0},\xi_{0}}(t)))^{*}\Xi_{x_{0},\xi_{0}}(t)\quad\text{for}\;t\in(0,T).

In particular, the first equation does not depend on
\xi
, translating the lack of dispersion in the case of transport equations.
Moreover the matrix
A(x,\xi)
defined in (
50
) is the one which is involved in the Lyapounov equation satisfied by the Gramian matrix
G_{x_{0}}
, see
(
15
).

4.4.
Proof of Theorem
4.14

For any
(x,\xi)
,
let
G_{x,\xi}
is the Gramian matrix microlocalized in
(x,\xi)
as given by Definition
4.12
.
We define the symbolic multiplier
M(t,x,\xi)

by setting, for any

,

(52)

G_{x,\xi}(t)=:M(t,(X_{x,\xi}(t),\Xi_{x,\xi}(t))).

As the Hamiltonian flow is invertible, see Remark
4.11
, the above

is uniquely defined.
Similarly to the reformulation in (
34
) and (
34
) performed in the non-dispersive case in
Section
3
, we are going to use a reformulation of the observability cost in terms of
a recorded energy by the use of a multiplier. The latter is precisely chosen as the symbolic multiplier
M(t,x,\xi)
, and the corresponding recorded energy
is then set as

(53)

\displaystyle\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathcal{W}^{\varepsilon}(t,x,\xi):M(t,x,\xi)\,\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}\xi=\langle\w[M(t,x,\xi)]\bm{u},\bm{u}\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})},

where we use the shorthand notation
\mathcal{W}^{\varepsilon}
for the Wigner matrix transform
\mathcal{W}^{\varepsilon}[\bm{u}^{\varepsilon}]
, recalling the property (
43
).

Let
T>0
and
(\bm{u}_{0}^{\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon\in(0,1]}
be bounded in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{N})
.
A key point of the proof of Theorem
4.14
is to establish that
as
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0
, the observability cost
\mathcal{C}[\bm{u}_{0}^{\varepsilon}]
satisfies

(54)

\mathcal{C}[\bm{u}_{0}^{\varepsilon}]=\frac{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathcal{W}^{\varepsilon}(T,\cdot):\operatorname{Id}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}\xi}{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathcal{W}^{\varepsilon}(T,\cdot):M(T,\cdot)\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}\xi}+o(1).

Recalling (
37
), we already have:

(55)

\displaystyle\|\bm{u}(T,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathcal{W}^{\varepsilon}(T,\cdot):\operatorname{Id}\,\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}\xi.

The reformulation of the denominator is more subtle.
First, by (
43
), the properties of the Frobenius product and (
44
), we observe that, at any time,

(56)

\displaystyle\|\w[B]\bm{u}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}=\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}\Big(\mathcal{W}^{\varepsilon}:B^{*}B\Big)\,\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}\xi+O(\varepsilon).

\displaystyle G^{\prime}_{x,\xi}(t)=\Big(\partial_{t}M+\operatorname{\bf{div}}(Mv_{H})\Big)(t,X_{x,\xi}(t),\Xi_{x,\xi}(t)),

On the other hand, the dynamics of the Wigner matrix

Lemma 4.15

There exists a remainder term
\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon}

Lemma 4.16

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{T}\Big(\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}\Big(\mathcal{R}:M+W:\mathcal{F}\Big)\,\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}\xi\Big)(t)\,dt.

Proof.

We observe that the time derivative
E^{\prime}
of

satisfies

\displaystyle E^{\prime}(t)=

\displaystyle\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left(-\operatorname{\bf{div}}(Mv_{H})+MA+A^{*}M+\mathcal{F}\right):W\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}\xi

(62)

\displaystyle+\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}M:\left(-\operatorname{\bf{div}}(Wv_{H})-AW-WA^{*}+\mathcal{R}\right)\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}\xi.

Using that

5.
Application to the incompressible Euler equations linearized at a Couette flow

An interesting example of application of our method is represented by the incompressible Euler equations in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, in velocity form, which we linearize at a steady state
\overline{\bm{u}}
satisfying
\operatorname{div}(\overline{\bm{u}})=0
, so that the velocity field
\bm{u}=\bm{u}(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
and the pressure
p=p(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}
satisfy

(72)

\left\{\begin{aligned} \partial_{t}\bm{u}+(\overline{\bm{u}}\cdot\nabla)\bm{u}+(\bm{u}\cdot\nabla)\overline{\bm{u}}+\nabla p&=0\\
\operatorname{div}(\bm{u})&=0.\end{aligned}\right.

Using the Leray projection onto divergence-free vector fields,
the semiclassical approximation of (
72
) then reads

(73)

\varepsilon\partial_{t}\bm{u}+\w[iH(x,\xi)\operatorname{Id}+\varepsilon A(x,\xi)]\bm{u}=0,

where

(74)

H(x,\xi)=\overline{\bm{u}}(x)\cdot\xi,\quad A(x,\xi)=\left(\operatorname{Id}-2\frac{\xi\otimes\xi}{|\xi|^{2}}\right)D\overline{\bm{u}}.

Since we want to study the behavior of solutions that are localized, and in high-frequency, we may multiply the symbols
A
and
H
by a cut-off function that is equal to
0
near
\xi=0
to avoid the singularity of
A
at
\xi=0
, so that one may consider a symbol
A
in
S(1)
which is given by (
74
) for
\xi=0
outside of a ball of radius say
1/2
.

We consider the case where the steady state
\overline{\bm{u}}
is the Couette flow
\overline{\bm{u}}=(x_{2},0)
, and where
the observation matrix is

B=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}.

Then for any
x_{0},\xi_{0}
in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, the Gramian matrix
G_{x_{0},\xi_{0}}
microlocalized at
(x_{0},\xi_{0})
is given by

G_{x_{0},\xi_{0}}(T)=\int_{0}^{T}(R_{x_{0},\xi_{0}}(t,T)^{*}B^{*}BR_{x_{0},\xi_{0}}(t,T))\,\mathrm{d}t,

where
R_{x_{0},\xi_{0}}(t,T)
is the resolvent matrix, given by solving the linear system

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}R_{x_{0},\xi_{0}}(t,T)=-A(X_{x_{0},\xi_{0}}(t),\Xi_{x_{0},\xi_{0}}(t))R_{x_{0},\xi_{0}}(t,T),\quad R(T,T)=\operatorname{Id}.

A tedious computation yields, with the notation
\xi_{0}=\left(\xi_{1,0},\xi_{2,0}\right)
,

R_{x_{0},\xi_{0}}(t,T)=\begin{pmatrix}1&f(t,\xi_{0})\\
0&g(t,\xi_{0})\end{pmatrix},

where

(75)

\displaystyle f(t,\xi_{0})=\begin{cases}T-t&\text{if }\xi_{1,0}=0,\\
-\frac{(\xi_{2,0}-t\xi_{1,0})(\xi_{1,0}^{2}+(\xi_{2,0}-T\xi_{1,0})^{2})}{\xi_{1,0}(\xi_{1,0}^{2}+(\xi_{2,0}-t\xi_{1,0})^{2})}+\frac{\xi_{2,0}-T\xi_{1,0}}{\xi_{1,0}}&\text{otherwise,}\end{cases}

and

g(t,\xi_{0})=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }\xi_{1,0}=0,\\
\frac{\xi_{1,0}^{2}+(\xi_{2,0}-T\xi_{1,0})^{2}}{\xi_{1,0}^{2}+(\xi_{2,0}-t\xi_{1,0})^{2}}&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}.

Moreover

G_{x_{0},\xi_{0}}(T)=\int_{0}^{T}\begin{pmatrix}1&f(t,\xi_{0})\\
f(t,\xi_{0})&f^{2}(t,\xi_{0})\end{pmatrix}\mathrm{d}t.

We now consider
(x_{0},\xi_{0},\mathbf{b}_{0})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\times(\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\{0\})\times\mathcal{S}^{1}
and
(\bm{u}_{0}^{\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon\in(0,1]}\subset L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2};\mathbb{R}^{2})
a family of initial data localized in
(x_{0},\xi_{0},\mathbf{b}_{0}).

By Theorem
4.14
,
the observability cost
\mathcal{C}[\bm{u}_{0}^{\varepsilon}]
satisfies, as
\varepsilon
goes to
0
,

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}(\mathcal{C}[\bm{u}_{0}^{\varepsilon}])^{-1}=(\mathbf{b}_{T}^{\mathcal{N}})^{*}G_{x_{0},\xi_{0}}(T)\mathbf{b}_{T}^{\mathcal{N}},

with

\mathbf{b}_{T}^{\mathcal{N}}=\frac{\mathbf{b}_{T}}{|\mathbf{b}_{T}|},\quad\mathbf{b}_{T}=R_{x_{0},\xi_{0}}(T,0)\mathbf{b}_{0}=\begin{pmatrix}1&-\frac{f(0,\xi_{0})}{g(0,\xi_{0})}\\
0&\frac{1}{g(0,\xi_{0})}\end{pmatrix}\mathbf{b}_{0}.

We can notice that, for any value of
\xi_{0}\neq 0
, the function
f(\cdot,\xi_{0})
is continuous and not constant, so that, by the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality,

\displaystyle\det G_{x_{0},\xi_{0}}(T)=T\int_{0}^{T}f^{2}(t,\xi_{0})\,\mathrm{d}t-\left(\int_{0}^{T}f(t,\xi_{0})\,\mathrm{d}t\right)^{2}>0.

Thus the system is observable in high frequencies for initial data localized in any
(x_{0},\xi_{0},\mathbf{b}_{0})
, and for any positive time
T>0
. The limit of the observability cost as
\varepsilon
goes to
0
is finite, and can be explicitly computed in terms of
\xi_{0}
and
\mathbf{b}_{0}.

The short-time and long-time behaviors of the observability cost can be given by further computations.
First, in the case where
\xi_{1,0}=0
,
the Gramian matrix has the simple expression:

G_{x_{0},\xi_{0}}(T)=\begin{pmatrix}T&\frac{T^{2}}{2}\\
\frac{T^{2}}{2}&\frac{T^{3}}{3}\end{pmatrix}.

Immediate computations then yield

\lambda_{\rm{min}}(G_{x_{0},\xi_{0}}(T))=\frac{T}{2}+\frac{1}{6}(T^{3}-\sqrt{T^{6}+3T^{4}+9T^{2}}),

so that in particular

\lambda_{\rm{min}}(G_{x_{0},\xi_{0}}(T))^{-1}\sim\frac{12}{T^{3}}
for short times
and
\lambda_{\rm{min}}(G_{x_{0},\xi_{0}}(T))^{-1}\sim\frac{4}{T}
for long times.
Note that
\lambda_{\rm{min}}(G_{x_{0},\xi_{0}}(T))^{-1}
is the ”worst-case” cost for initial data localized in
(x_{0},\xi_{0})
, namely

\lambda_{\rm{min}}(G_{x_{0},\xi_{0}}(T))^{-1}=\sup_{\mathbf{b}_{0}\in\mathcal{S}^{N-1}}\{\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\mathcal{C}(\bm{u}_{0}^{\varepsilon}):(\bm{u}_{0}^{\varepsilon})\text{ is localized in }(x_{0},\xi_{0},\mathbf{b}_{0})\}.

The asymptotic behaviour of the cost at
short times can be extended to the other values of
\xi_{0}
.
If
\xi_{1,0}\neq 0
and
\xi_{1,0}^{2}\neq\xi_{2,0}^{2}
, then by using the Taylor expansion of
f
at first order, we obtain

(76)

\lambda_{\rm{min}}(G_{x_{0},\xi_{0}}(T))\sim\frac{T^{3}(\xi_{1,0}^{2}-\xi_{2,0}^{2})^{2}}{12|\xi_{0}|^{4}}.

Otherwise, that is if
\xi_{1,0}\neq 0
and
\xi_{1,0}^{2}=\xi_{2,0}^{2}
, then
by using the Taylor expansion of
f
at second order, we obtain

(77)

\lambda_{\rm{min}}(G_{x_{0},\xi_{0}}(T))\sim\frac{T^{5}}{45}.

Those estimates are consistent with the results of
[
21
]
. Indeed, in the case where
\xi_{1,0}^{2}\neq\xi_{2,0}^{2}
, the rank condition is fulfilled on the second bracket, namely

\text{rank}[B|A_{x_{0},\xi_{0}}(0)B]=2,\text{ while rank}(B)<2.

Then the analysis of
[
21
]
gives a cost of order
T^{-3}
, as given by (
76
). When
\xi_{1,0}^{2}=\xi_{2,0}^{2}
, we need to take one more bracket to verify the rank condition, namely

\text{rank}[B|A_{x_{0},\xi_{0}}(0)B|(A_{x_{0},\xi_{0}}^{2}(0)+A^{\prime}_{x_{0},\xi_{0}}(0))B]=2,\text{ while rank}[B|A_{x_{0},\xi_{0}}(0)B]<2.

The results in
[
21
]
then yield a cost of order
T^{-5}
, as given by (
77
). Our approach furthermore gives an explicit constant.

6.
Case of systems without crossing modes

This section is devoted to an extension of the results of Section

4
to more general systems.

6.1.
Statement of the result

In this section we extend the previous analysis to the case of pseudo-differential hyperbolic systems of the form:

(78)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\bm{u}+\w\left[\frac{i}{\varepsilon}H+A\right]\bm{u}=0,\quad(x,\xi)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}.

Above,
A
and
H
are two matrices of symbols which are supposed to fulfill the following assumption.

Assumption 6.1
.

We assume that
A
and
H
satisfy the following.

(H1):

H=H(x,\xi)
is a
N\times N
field of Hermitian matrices in
S(1)
, and there exist
1\leq m\leq N
and
C>0
such that for all
(x,\xi)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the matrix
H(x,\xi)
has
m
real semi-simple eigenvalues
\lambda_{k}(x,\xi)
, for
1\leqslant k\leqslant m
, with

|\lambda_{j}(x,\xi)-\lambda_{k}(x,\xi)|>C\quad\text{for all}\;1\leq j\neq k\leq m\leq N.

(H2):

A=A(x,\xi)
is a
N\times N
field of matrices in
S(1).

As a consequence of (H1)
the matrix
\mathcal{U}(x,\xi):=(\textbf{r}_{\ell})_{\ell=1,\cdots N}
of the right normalized eigenvectors
(\textbf{r}_{\ell})_{\ell=1,\cdots N}
of
H(x,\xi)

is in
S(1)
, takes values which are unitary matrices, and

(79)

\mathcal{U}^{*}H=\mathcal{D}\mathcal{U}^{*}=(\lambda_{\ell}\textbf{r}_{\ell})^{*}_{\ell=1,\cdots N},

and

(80)

\mathcal{D}(x,\xi):=(\mathcal{U}^{*}H\mathcal{U})(x,\xi)=\sum_{k=1}^{m}\lambda_{k}(x,\xi)\Pi_{k}\in S(1),

where the
\left(\Pi_{k}\right)_{1\leq k\leq m}
are the constant orthogonal projectors onto
\ker(\mathcal{D}(x,\xi)-\lambda_{k}(x,\xi)\operatorname{Id}),
which are independent of
(x,\xi)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Our aim is to answer to Question
4.9
in the case of the system (
78
), that is to determine, as
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0
, the limit observability cost
\mathcal{C}[\bm{u}_{0}^{\varepsilon}]
, as given by Definition
4.7
,
of a family of initial data
(\bm{u}_{0}^{\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon\in(0,1]}
in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{N})
which is localized at

(x_{0},\xi_{0},b_{0})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times(\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\{0\})\times\mathbb{R}^{N}
,
for the corresponding solution
\bm{u}
of the system (
78
)
and an observability operator
B\in S(1)
.
The main result of this section provides an answer to this question which generalizes the earlier case where a single characteristic was considered.
Again the result involves a few ingredients, which we now recall in this more general setting. First it makes use of the Hamiltonian flows associated with each eigenvalue
\lambda_{k}
, with
1\leq k\leq m
, as given by Definition
4.13
.
For sake of clarity we explicitely recall this definition below, which involves nonlinear ODEs.

Definition 6.1
.

For
1\leq k\leq m
,
for any
(x,\xi)
,
we define
the Hamiltonian flow
\big(X_{k,x,\xi},\Xi_{k,x,\xi}\big)

induced by the
k
-th eigenvalue
\lambda_{k}(x,\xi)
as the unique smooth solution of the following Cauchy problem:

(81)

\big(X_{k,x,\xi},\Xi_{k,x,\xi}\big)^{\prime}=v_{\lambda_{k}}\big(X_{k,x,\xi},\Xi_{k,x,\xi}\big),\quad\left(X_{k,x,\xi},\Xi_{k,x,\xi}\right)(0)=(x,\xi).

where
v_{\lambda_{k}}
is the divergence free vector field from
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
to itself defined by

(82)

v_{\lambda_{k}}=\left(\nabla_{\xi}\lambda_{k},-\nabla_{x}\lambda_{k}\right)

We also rely on some Gramian matrices associated with each Hamiltonian flow
and with
H
and
A
, similarly to Definition
4.12
, but with some surprising extra terms, which encode the influence between the various characteristic fields of the system. More precisely, for
1\leq k\leq m
, let

(83)

\displaystyle A^{\rm{eff}}_{k}

\displaystyle:=\Pi_{k}\mathcal{U}^{*}A\mathcal{U}\Pi_{k}+\frac{1}{2}\Pi_{k}\Big(\{\mathcal{U}^{*},H\}\mathcal{U}-\{\mathcal{D},\mathcal{U}^{*}\}\mathcal{U}\Big)\Pi_{k},

(84)

\displaystyle B^{\rm{eff}}_{k}

\displaystyle:=B\mathcal{U}\Pi_{k},

which both belong to
S(1)
. We may now give the definition of the Gramian matrices, which involves linear ODEs.

Definition 6.2
.

Let
x_{0},\xi_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
1\leq k\leq m
.
We define the
k
-th Gramian matrix microlocalized in
(x_{0},\xi_{0})
as the unique solution
G_{k,x_{0},\xi_{0}}
to the differential Lyapunov equation

Lyap(A_{k,x_{0},\xi_{0}},B_{k,x_{0},\xi_{0}}),

where

A_{k,x_{0},\xi_{0}}(t):=A_{k}^{\rm{eff}}(X_{k,x_{0},\xi_{0}}(t),\Xi_{k,x_{0},\xi_{0}}(t))\,\text{ and }\,B_{k,x_{0},\xi_{0}}(t):=B_{k}^{\rm{eff}}(X_{k,x_{0},\xi_{0}}(t),\Xi_{k,x_{0},\xi_{0}}(t))).

We also define the bicharacteristic amplitudes, which are given as solutions to linear ODEs.

Definition 6.3
.

For any
x_{0},\xi_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, for any
\mathbf{b}_{0}
in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
and
1\leq k\leq m
,
we define the
k
-th bicharacteristic amplitude
\mathbf{b}_{k,x_{0},\xi_{0},\mathbf{b}_{0}}

as the unique solution to the linear ODE:

\mathbf{b}_{k,x_{0},\xi_{0},\mathbf{b}_{0}}^{\prime}=-A_{k,x_{0},\xi_{0}}\,\mathbf{b}_{k,x_{0},\xi_{0},\mathbf{b}_{0}},\quad\mathbf{b}_{k,x_{0},\xi_{0},\mathbf{b}_{0}}(0)=\mathbf{b}_{0}.

The main result of this section is that the inverse of
observability cost
\mathcal{C}[\bm{u}_{0}^{\varepsilon}]
for a family of initial data
(\bm{u}_{0}^{\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon\in(0,1]}
in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\mathbb{C}^{N})
which is localized at
(x_{0},\xi_{0},\mathbf{b}_{0})
, in the sense of Definition
4.8
, converges to a weighted mean of Rayleigh quotients of the
m
Gramian matrices with the bicharacteristic amplitudes.

Theorem 6.4
.

Let
(x_{0},\xi_{0},b_{0})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times(\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\{0\})\times\mathbb{R}^{N}
.
For
1\leq k\leq m
, let
G_{T,k}
be the Gramian matrix given at time
T
, that is
G_{T,k}:=G_{k,x_{0},\xi_{0}}(T),

and
\mathbf{b}_{T,k}

the characteristic amplitude at time
T
, that is
\mathbf{b}_{T,k}:=\mathbf{b}_{k,x_{0},\xi_{0},\mathbf{b}_{0}}(T).

Let
(\bm{u}_{0}^{\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon\in(0,1]}
in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{N})
be localized at
(x_{0},\xi_{0},b_{0})
in the sense of Definition
4.8
.
Then the observability cost
\mathcal{C}[\bm{u}_{0}^{\varepsilon}]
verifies, as
\varepsilon\to 0
,

(85)

\mathcal{C}[\bm{u}_{0}^{\varepsilon}]^{-1}\to\frac{\sum_{k=1}^{m}\mathbf{b}_{T,k}^{*}G_{T,k}\mathbf{b}_{T,k}}{\sum_{k=1}^{m}|\mathbf{b}_{T,k}|^{2}}.

Theorem
6.4
extends to the general case of hyperbolic systems, without characteristics crossing, some earlier results by
[
7
,
8
,
17
]
in the case of the wave equation and of the Klein-Gordon equation.
In these works, the authors use some Egorov type methods, for scalar equations.
Despite some counterparts of the Egorov theorem for systems have been established in
[
1
,
3
,
5
]
, in the proof below we prefer
to extend the approach performed in the previous sections, an approach which we believe is more flexible, direct and general.

6.2.
Proof of Theorem
6.4

The first step of the proof is to rewrite the system in normal form. To this end, we need the two preliminary results, that we will prove later.
Recall that
\mathcal{D}
is defined in (
80
), and set

(86)

2A^{\flat}(x,\xi):=\{\mathcal{U}^{*},H\}\mathcal{U}-\{\mathcal{D},\mathcal{U}^{*}\}\mathcal{U},

which is in
S(1)
.

Lemma 6.5
.

Let
\bm{u}
be the solution to (
78
) with initial datum
\bm{u}_{0}
, and let
\widetilde{\bm{u}}:=\w[\mathcal{U}^{*}]\bm{u}
. Then
\widetilde{\bm{u}}
solves the system

(87)

\left\{\begin{aligned} \partial_{t}\widetilde{\bm{u}}+\frac{i}{\varepsilon}\w[\mathcal{D}]\widetilde{\bm{u}}+\w[\Gamma]\widetilde{\bm{u}}&=\varepsilon\w[r_{\varepsilon}]\widetilde{\bm{u}},\\
\widetilde{\bm{u}}(0)&=\w[\mathcal{U}^{*}]\bm{u}_{0},\end{aligned}\right.

where
\Gamma=\mathcal{U}^{*}A\mathcal{U}+A^{\flat}
and the remainder
r_{\varepsilon}\in S(1)
is such that
\mathcal{N}_{\gamma d}(r_{\varepsilon})=O(1)
.

For all
(x,\xi)\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}
, we set

(88)

Q(x,\xi):=-\sum_{(j,k)/\,j\neq k}\frac{i\Pi_{j}\Gamma(x,\xi)\Pi_{k}}{\lambda_{j}(x,\xi)-\lambda_{k}(x,\xi)}.

Then
Q\in S(1)
. Recall that
A^{\rm{eff}}_{k}
is given by
(
83
).

Lemma 6.6
.

Let
\widetilde{\bm{u}}
be the solution to (
87
). We set
\bm{v}:=(\operatorname{Id}+\varepsilon\w[Q])\widetilde{\bm{u}}
and
\bm{v}_{k}:=\Pi_{k}\bm{v}
for
1\leq k\leq m
.
Then

(89)

\left\{\begin{aligned} \partial_{t}\bm{v}_{k}+\frac{i}{\varepsilon}\w[\lambda_{k}\operatorname{Id}]\bm{v}_{k}+\w[A^{\rm{eff}}_{k}]\bm{v}_{k}&=\varepsilon\w[r_{\varepsilon}]\bm{v},\\
\bm{v}_{k}(0)&=(\operatorname{Id}+\varepsilon\w[Q])\w[\mathcal{U}^{*}]\bm{u}_{0},\end{aligned}\right.

where
r_{\varepsilon}
is again a remainder in
S(1)
such that
\mathcal{N}_{\gamma d}(r_{\varepsilon})=O(1).

Let us take these two results as granted and finish the proof of Theorem
6.4
.
Rewriting both energy terms involved in the observability cost in terms of
\bm{v}
gives, using that
\mathcal{U}\mathcal{U}^{*}=\mathcal{U}^{*}\mathcal{U}=\operatorname{Id}
,

(90)

\displaystyle\|\bm{u}(T)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{m}\|\bm{v}_{k}(T)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}(1+O(\varepsilon)),

(91)

\displaystyle\|\w[B]\bm{u}\|^{2}_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{m}\|\w[B^{\rm{eff}}_{k}]\bm{v}_{k}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\sum_{j\neq k}\left\langle\w[B\mathcal{U}]\bm{v}_{j},\w[B\mathcal{U}]\bm{v}_{k}\right\rangle_{L^{2}}+O(\varepsilon)\|\bm{v}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}.

Note that we split the diagonal and off-diagonal terms in the observed energy because they exhibit very distinct properties.

For the off-diagonal terms, we rely on the following lemma.

Lemma 6.7
.

Let
\Lambda(x,\xi)\in S(1)
be a matrix-valued symbol. Let
1\leq j,k\leq m
such that
j\neq k
. Then it holds

(92)

\int_{0}^{T}\langle\w[\Pi_{k}\Lambda\Pi_{j}]\bm{v}(t),\bm{v}(t)\rangle_{L^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}t=O(\varepsilon)\|\bm{v}(T)\|^{2}_{L^{2}}.

Applying this lemma with
\Lambda=\mathcal{U}^{*}B^{*}B\mathcal{U}
yields, for
1\leq j\neq k\leq m
,

(93)

\left\langle\w[B\mathcal{U}]\bm{v}_{j},\w[B\mathcal{U}]\bm{v}_{k}\right\rangle_{L^{2}}=O(\varepsilon)\|\bm{v}(T)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}.

For the diagonal terms, for each
1\leq k\leq m
, we apply Theorem
4.14
to the system (
89
), for
1\leq k\leq m
, to obtain that

(94)

\displaystyle\|\bm{v}(T)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle=|\mathbf{b}_{T,k}|^{2}+o(1),

(95)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\|\w[B\mathcal{U}]\bm{v}_{k}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{b}_{T,k}^{*}G_{T,k}(T)\mathbf{b}_{T,k}+o(1).

Combining (
93
), (
94
) and (
95
), and substituting them into (
90
) and (
91
) yields

\displaystyle\|\bm{u}(T)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{m}|\mathbf{b}_{k,x_{0},\xi_{0},\mathbf{b}_{0}}(T)|^{2}+o(1),

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\|\w[B]\bm{u}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{m}\mathbf{b}_{k,x_{0},\xi_{0},\mathbf{b}_{0}}(T)^{*}G_{k,x_{0},\xi_{0}}(T)\mathbf{b}_{k,x_{0},\xi_{0},\mathbf{b}_{0}}(T)+o(1).

Plugging those estimates into the definition of the cost as given by (
46
) concludes the proof.

6.3.
Diagonalisation of the propagation term. Proof of Lemma
6.5

We consider the system (
78
), and apply to it the operator
\w[\mathcal{U}^{*}]
, giving

(96)

\partial_{t}\widetilde{\bm{u}}+\frac{i}{\varepsilon}\w[\mathcal{U}^{*}]\w[H]\bm{u}+\w[\mathcal{U}^{*}]\w[A]\bm{u}=0.

Applying Lemma
4.6
for the identity (
79
), we obtain

(97)

\w[\mathcal{U}^{*}]\w[H]+\frac{\varepsilon}{2i}\w[\{\mathcal{U}^{*},H\}]=\w[\mathcal{D}]\w[\mathcal{U}^{*}]+\frac{\varepsilon}{2i}\w[\{\mathcal{D},\mathcal{U}^{*}\}]+\varepsilon^{2}\w[r_{\varepsilon}],

where
r_{\varepsilon}\in S(1)
is a remainder symbol such that
\mathcal{N}_{\gamma d}(r_{\varepsilon})
is bounded uniformly in
\varepsilon
. By a slight abuse of notation, we will denote all such remainders
r_{\varepsilon}
, despite them not being the same in all the equalities.
Rearranging, using that
\mathcal{U}\mathcal{U}^{*}=\operatorname{Id}
and recalling the definition of
A^{\flat}
in (
86
), it follows that

(98)

\w[\mathcal{U}^{*}]\w[H]=\w[\mathcal{D}]\w[\mathcal{U}^{*}]+\frac{\varepsilon}{i}\w[A^{\flat}]\w[\mathcal{U}^{*}]+\varepsilon^{2}\w[r_{\varepsilon}].

Meanwhile, for the term of order 0, we have

(99)

\w[\mathcal{U}^{*}]\w[A]=\w[\mathcal{U}^{*}A\mathcal{U}]\w[\mathcal{U}^{*}]+\varepsilon\w[r_{\varepsilon}].

Substituting (
98
) and (
99
) into (
96
), it finally yields

(100)

\partial_{t}\widetilde{\bm{u}}+\left(\frac{i}{\varepsilon}\w[\mathcal{D}]+\w[A^{\flat}]+\w[\mathcal{U}^{*}A\mathcal{U}]\right)\w[\mathcal{U}^{*}]\bm{u}=\varepsilon\w[r_{\varepsilon}]\widetilde{\bm{u}},

and the claim immediately follows.

Remark 6.8
.

For any
1\leq k\leq m
, multiplying equality (
98
) on the left by
\Pi_{k}
and on the right by
\w[\mathcal{U}]\Pi_{k}
gives, denoting by
\sigma_{p}
the principal symbol of a pseudo-differential operator,

(101)

\sigma_{p}\left(\Pi_{k}(\w[\mathcal{U}^{*}]\w[H]\w[\mathcal{U}]-\w[\mathcal{D}]\w[\mathcal{U}^{*}]\w[\mathcal{U}])\Pi_{k}\right)=-i\varepsilon\Pi_{k}A^{\flat}\Pi_{k}.

By property (
41
) of the Weyl quantization, the first operator in the left-hand side of (
101
) is self-adjoint, and the second one is also self-adjoint, as a consequence of the symbolic identity
(\Pi_{k}\mathcal{D})(x,\xi)=(\lambda_{k}\Pi_{k})(x,\xi)
. Therefore the symbolic matrix
i(\Pi_{k}A^{\flat}\Pi_{k})(x,\xi)
is Hermitian for
1\leq k\leq m
.

6.4.
Block-diagonalisation of the amplification term. Proof of Lemma
6.6

We follow a method initially used by Poincaré in the setting of ODEs to further diagonalize the system, into a pseudo-differential normal form where the term of order
\frac{1}{\varepsilon}
is diagonal and the term of order
1
is block-diagonal.
Since
(\operatorname{Id}+\varepsilon\w[Q])^{-1}=\operatorname{Id}-\varepsilon\w[Q]+\varepsilon^{2}\w[r_{\varepsilon}]
, we get

\displaystyle(\operatorname{Id}+\varepsilon\w[Q])\left(\w\left[\frac{i}{\varepsilon}\mathcal{D}+\Gamma\right]\right)(\operatorname{Id}+\varepsilon\w[Q])^{-1}

\displaystyle=\w\left[\frac{i}{\varepsilon}\mathcal{D}-i[\mathcal{D},Q]+\Gamma\right]

(102)

\displaystyle\quad+\varepsilon\w[r_{\varepsilon}].

Now, we observe from the definition of
Q
in (
88
) that

(103)

\displaystyle-i[\mathcal{D},Q]+\Gamma=\sum_{k=1}^{m}\Pi_{k}\Gamma\Pi_{k},

Substituting (
103
) into this equality then yields

\displaystyle(\operatorname{Id}+\varepsilon\w[Q])\left(\w\left[\frac{i}{\varepsilon}\mathcal{D}+\Gamma\right]\right)(\operatorname{Id}+\varepsilon\w[Q])^{-1}

\displaystyle=\w\left[\frac{i}{\varepsilon}\mathcal{D}+\sum_{k=1}^{m}\Pi_{k}\Gamma\Pi_{k}\right]

(104)

\displaystyle\quad+\varepsilon\w[r_{\varepsilon}]

Performing the change of variable
\bm{v}=(\operatorname{Id}+\varepsilon\w[Q])\widetilde{\bm{u}}
in the system (
87
) then gives

(105)

\partial_{t}\bm{v}+\w\left[\frac{i}{\varepsilon}\mathcal{D}+\sum_{k=1}^{m}\Pi_{k}\Gamma\Pi_{k}\right]\bm{v}=\varepsilon\w[r_{\varepsilon}]\bm{v}.

For
1\leq k\leq m
, multiplying this equation on the left by
\Pi_{k}
then leads to the system (
89
) and the claim is proven.

6.5.
Estimate of the off-diagonal energy terms. Proof of Lemma
6.7

By (
105
) and standard pseudo-differential calculations, for any
1\leq j\neq k\leq m
, recalling that
\lambda_{j}
denotes the eigenvalues of
\mathcal{D}
,

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\left\langle\w\left[\frac{\Pi_{k}\Lambda\Pi_{j}}{\lambda_{j}-\lambda_{k}}\right]\bm{v},\bm{v}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=-\frac{i}{\varepsilon}\left\langle\w\left[\left[\frac{\Pi_{k}\Lambda\Pi_{j}}{\lambda_{j}-\lambda_{k}},\mathcal{D}\right]\right]\bm{v},\bm{v}\right\rangle+O(1)\|\bm{v}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}

Now, we observe that, for any
1\leq j\neq k\leq m
,

\left[\frac{\Pi_{k}\Lambda\Pi_{j}}{\lambda_{j}-\lambda_{k}},\mathcal{D}\right]=\Pi_{k}\Lambda\Pi_{j},

and we set

E_{j,k}(t):=\langle\w[\Pi_{k}\Lambda\Pi_{j}]\bm{v},\bm{v}\rangle_{L^{2}}.

This immediately yields

(106)

E_{j,k}(t)=i\varepsilon\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\left\langle\w\left[\frac{\Pi_{k}\Lambda\Pi_{j}}{\lambda_{j}-\lambda_{k}}\right]\bm{v},\bm{v}\right\rangle+O(\varepsilon)\|\bm{v}(t)\|^{2}_{L^{2}}

Then, integrating in time yields

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}E_{j,k}(t)\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=i\varepsilon\left\langle\w\left[\frac{\Pi_{k}\mathcal{U}^{*}B^{*}B\mathcal{U}\Pi_{j}}{\lambda_{j}-\lambda_{k}}\right]\bm{v}(T),\bm{v}(T)\right\rangle_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\quad-i\varepsilon\left\langle\w\left[\frac{\Pi_{k}\mathcal{U}^{*}B^{*}B\mathcal{U}\Pi_{j}}{\lambda_{j}-\lambda_{k}}\right]\bm{v}(0),\bm{v}(0)\right\rangle_{L^{2}}+O(\varepsilon)\|\bm{v}(T)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}.

By the non-crossing condition,
\w\left[\frac{\Pi_{k}\mathcal{U}^{*}B^{*}B\mathcal{U}\Pi_{j}}{\lambda_{j}-\lambda_{k}}\right]
is a bounded operator on
L^{2}
, and the claim immediately follows.

7.
Compressible gas-dynamics in a rapidly rotating frame

As an illustration we consider the non-isentropic compressible Euler equations for perfect gases in a rotating frame (see for instance
[
2
,
10
]
) in
2
D:

(107)

\left\{\begin{aligned} \partial_{t}\bm{u}+(\bm{u}\cdot\nabla)\bm{u}+\rho^{-1}\nabla p&=0,\\
\partial_{t}p+\bm{u}\cdot\nabla p+\rho c^{2}\nabla\cdot\bm{u}+\frac{b(x_{2})}{\rm{Ro}}\bm{u}^{\perp}&=0,\\
\partial_{t}s+\bm{u}\cdot\nabla s&=0,\end{aligned}\right.

where
\rho
is the density,
\bm{u}
the velocity,
p
the pressure,
s
the entropy,
\rm{Ro}\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
the Rossby number,
\bm{u}^{\perp}=(-u_{2},u_{1})
,
b
is a smooth function of
x_{2}
and
c
is the sound speed.
We consider the the case of polytropic gases, for which the pressure law is

p=(\gamma-1)\rho^{\gamma}e^{\frac{s}{c}},
for
\gamma>1
, and
the sound speed
c
is given by
c=\sqrt{\gamma p/\rho}
.
Then, setting

\pi:=(p/(\gamma-1))^{2\gamma},

the system (
107
) is recast as

\left\{\begin{aligned} \partial_{t}\bm{u}+(\bm{u}\cdot\nabla)\bm{u}+2\gamma e^{\frac{s}{\gamma c}}\pi\nabla\pi+\frac{b(x_{2})}{\rm{Ro}}\bm{u}^{\perp}&=0,\\
\partial_{t}\pi+\bm{u}\cdot\nabla\pi+\frac{\gamma-1}{2}\pi\nabla\cdot\bm{u}&=0,\\
\partial_{t}s+\bm{u}\cdot\nabla s&=0.\end{aligned}\right.

The linearization of the system at a steady solution
(\overline{\bm{u}},\overline{\pi},\overline{s})
, where
\overline{\bm{u}}=(\overline{u}_{1}(x_{2}),0)
is a shear flow,
\overline{s}\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
is a constant value and
\overline{\pi}=\overline{\pi}(x_{2})
satisfies the relation

2\gamma\text{Ro}\exp(\overline{s}/c\gamma)(\overline{\pi}\overline{\pi}^{\prime})(x_{2})=-b(x_{2})\overline{u}_{1}(x_{2})
.
In the case where
\text{Ro}=\varepsilon
, the semi-classical approximation
yields the following system:

(108)

\partial_{t}\textbf{U}+\w\left[\frac{i}{\varepsilon}H+A\right]\textbf{U}=0,

where

\textbf{U}=\begin{pmatrix}u_{1}\\
u_{2}\\
\pi\\
s\end{pmatrix},\quad H=\begin{pmatrix}\overline{u}_{1}(x_{2})\xi_{1}&-ib(x_{2})&2\gamma e^{\frac{\overline{s}}{\gamma c}}\overline{\pi}\xi_{1}&0\\
ib&\overline{u}_{1}(x_{2})\xi_{1}&2\gamma e^{\frac{\overline{s}}{\gamma}}\overline{\pi}\xi_{2}&0\\
\frac{\gamma-1}{2}\overline{\pi}\xi_{1}&\frac{\gamma-1}{2}\overline{\pi}\xi_{2}&\overline{u}_{1}(x_{2})\xi_{1}&0\\
0&0&0&\overline{u}_{1}(x_{2})\xi_{1}\end{pmatrix},

and

A=\begin{pmatrix}0&\overline{u}_{1}^{\prime}(x_{2})&0&0\\
0&0&2\gamma e^{\frac{\overline{s}}{\gamma}}\overline{\pi}^{\prime}(x_{2})&2\gamma e^{\frac{\overline{s}}{\gamma}}\overline{\pi}\overline{\pi}^{\prime}(x_{2})\\
0&\overline{\pi}^{\prime}(x_{2})&0&0\\
0&0&0&0\end{pmatrix}.

The principal symbol
H
is not symmetric; however, setting

S:=\diag(1,1,4\frac{\gamma}{\gamma-1}\exp({\overline{s}}/{c\gamma}),1),

which only depends on
x
,
and computing

\widetilde{H}=\sqrt{S}H\sqrt{S}^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}\overline{u}_{1}\xi_{1}&-ib&\eta\xi_{1}&0\\
ib&\overline{u}_{1}\xi_{1}&\eta\xi_{2}&0\\
\eta\xi_{1}&\eta\xi_{2}&\overline{u}_{1}\xi_{1}&0\\
0&0&0&\overline{u}_{1}\xi_{1}\end{pmatrix},

where
\eta:=\sqrt{\gamma(\gamma-1)}\exp(\frac{\overline{s}}{2c\gamma})\overline{\pi}

and
\sqrt{S}
is the positive square root of
S
, we observe that
\widetilde{H}

is symmetric.
We are then led to a system of the form:

(109)

\partial_{t}\textbf{W}+\w\left[\frac{i}{\varepsilon}\widetilde{H}+\widetilde{A}\right]\textbf{W}=\varepsilon\w[r_{\varepsilon}]\textbf{W}.

The eigenvalues of
\widetilde{H}
are

\lambda_{1}=\overline{u}_{1}(x_{2})\xi_{1}

with multiplicity
2
,

\lambda_{2}=\overline{u}_{1}\xi_{2}+\sqrt{b^{2}+C_{1}^{2}\overline{\pi}^{2}\exp({\overline{s}}/{c\gamma})|\xi|^{2}},

with multiplicity
1
,
and

\lambda_{3}=\overline{u}_{1}\xi_{2}-\sqrt{b^{2}+C_{1}^{2}\overline{\pi}^{2}\exp({\overline{s}}/{c\gamma})|\xi|^{2}},

with multiplicity
1
.
The non-crossing assumption is verified for
(x_{1},x_{2})
such that

b^{2}(x_{2})+C_{1}^{2}\overline{\pi}^{2}(x_{2})\exp({\overline{s}}/{c\gamma})|\xi|^{2}\neq 0.

Two orthogonal eigenvectors of
\lambda_{1}
are given by

(0,0,0,1)^{T},\quad\frac{1}{\langle\xi\rangle_{b}}(-i\xi_{2},i\xi_{1},\widetilde{b},0)^{T},\quad\text{where}\quad\langle\xi\rangle_{b}=\sqrt{|\xi^{2}|+\widetilde{b}^{2}},\quad\widetilde{b}=\frac{b}{C_{1}\overline{\pi}}\exp(-{\overline{s}}/{2c\gamma}).

We can then compute the block of the (block-diagonal) matrix
A^{\flat}(x,\xi)
in (
86
) associated to
\lambda_{1}

\displaystyle A^{\flat}_{\lambda_{1}}(x,\xi)=\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\
0&-\frac{i\xi_{1}C_{1}\exp(\overline{s}/2\gamma)\overline{\pi}\partial_{2}\widetilde{b}(x_{2})}{\langle\xi\rangle^{2}_{b}}\end{pmatrix}.
