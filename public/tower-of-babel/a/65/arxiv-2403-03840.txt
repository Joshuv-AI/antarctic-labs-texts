Caloric functions and boundary regularity for the fractional Laplacian in Lipschitz open sets
Gavin Armstrong, Krzysztof Bogdan, Artur Rutkowski

Abstract
. We give Martin representation of nonnegative functions caloric with respect to the fractional Laplacian in Lipschitz open sets. The caloric functions are defined in terms of the mean value property for the space-time isotropic α \displaystyle\alpha -stable Lévy process. To derive the representation, we first establish the existence of the parabolic Martin kernel. This involves proving new boundary regularity results for both the fractional heat equation and the fractional Poisson equation with Dirichlet exterior conditions. Specifically, we demonstrate that the ratio of the solution and the Green function is Hölder continuous up to the boundary.

Let us add a few general comments. The mean-value property for fractional caloric functions is important for our development. It was considered before, e.g., by Chen and Kumagai
[
30
]
. Here we focus on the mean-value property in cylinders, which seems adequate for the initial-exterior problem (
1.2
). The advantage of the approach is that from the Ikeda–Watanabe formula we obtain a semi-explicit formula for the Poisson kernel. We also have the following stochastic interpretation: if
\displaystyle u
satisfies the mean-value property
\displaystyle(0,T)\times D
, then
\displaystyle u(t,x)
can be recovered from the space-time isotropic
\displaystyle\alpha
-stable process
\displaystyle s\mapsto(t-s,X_{s}+x)
, which starts from
\displaystyle(t,x)
at time
\displaystyle s=0
,
by computing the expectation of
\displaystyle u(t-s,X_{s}+x)
at the place of the first exit of the process from
\displaystyle(0,T)\times D
. The exit can occur when
\displaystyle x+X_{s}
leaves
\displaystyle D
before time
\displaystyle t
—in which case the exterior conditions affect the expectation—or when the time coordinate
\displaystyle t-s
reaches
\displaystyle 0
—then the initial condition comes into play. Singular caloric functions start to appear once we assume that the mean-value property is satisfied only on
\displaystyle(0,T)\times U
for all open (relatively compact sets)
\displaystyle U\subset\subset D
. We refer to the book of Freidlin
[
40
, Theorem 2.3]
for a counterpart of this theory for local operators.

With a view toward applications in probability, we note that the existence of the limit (
1.4
) indicates how the isotropic
\displaystyle\alpha
-stable process in
\displaystyle D
, conditioned on surviving at least time 1, behaves near the boundary of
\displaystyle D
. More precisely, it implies the existence of a “Yaglom limit”,
see Theorem
3.7
below. Thanks to (

\displaystyle\eta_{t,Q}(y)
may be understood as the

for the killed process from
\displaystyle Q
into
\displaystyle D
, see Blumenthal
[

. This was used in
[

51
]
to describe the behavior of the process started from a point on the boundary, e.g., the apex of a cone. Furthermore, the boundary behavior of the heat kernel yields a measure which represents the probability distribution of a rescaled process conditioned on non-extinction.

Let us now present an outline of the proofs and methods in this paper. In order to prove Theorem
1.1
we obtain an explicit representation of the survival probability as a Green potential and we show that it behaves like
\displaystyle G_{D}(x_{0},\cdot)
at the boundary. Then we
approximate

\displaystyle p_{t}^{D}
by Green potentials and obtain the limit in (
1.4
) with the help of Prokhorov theorem. To this end, we utilize
the uniform integrability of ratios of Green functions. The proof of Theorem
1.4
consists in splitting the integral into one region where the boundary Harnack principle can be applied, and another region where we use a technical interior regularity argument adapted to possible singularities of the Green function. In order to prove Theorem
1.2
, we
represent

\displaystyle p_{t}^{D}
as a Green potential and we apply Theorem
1.4
. We make use of the spectral theory to show that
\displaystyle p_{t}^{D}
has regularity necessary for the proof; some ideas here were inspired by
[
27
]
. The boundary measure in the representation of singular caloric functions is obtained from an approximating sequence constructed via the so-called lateral Poisson kernel. Our construction is quite different than the one in
[
27
]
, in particular it does not use the inhomogeneous fractional heat equation.

\displaystyle(X_{t},\mathbb{P}^{x})

\displaystyle\alpha
-stable Lévy process in
\displaystyle\mathbb{R}^{d}
. The process is determined by the jump measure with density function

(2.3)

\nu(y)=\frac{2^{\alpha}\Gamma((d+\alpha)/2)}{\pi^{d/2}|\Gamma(-\alpha/2)|}|y|^{-d-\alpha}=:c_{d,\alpha}|y|^{-d-\alpha},\quad y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

It is a process with independent and stationary increments and characteristic function
\displaystyle\mathbb{E}^{x}e^{i\langle\xi,X_{t}-x\rangle}=e^{-t|\xi|^{\alpha}}
,
\displaystyle t>0
,
\displaystyle x,\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
It is strong Markov with the following time-homogeneous transition probability

P_{t}(x,A):=\int_{A}p_{t}(x,y)\,dy,\quad t>0,\ x\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\ A\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}.

Here
\displaystyle p_{t}(x,y):=p_{t}(x-y)
and
\displaystyle p_{t}
is the smooth real-valued function on
\displaystyle\mathbb{R}^{d}
with the Fourier transform:

(2.4)

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}p_{t}(x)e^{i\langle x,\xi\rangle}\,dx=e^{-t|\xi|^{\alpha}},\quad\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

The associated semigroup of operators acts on, e.g.,
\displaystyle u\in C_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
as follows:

P_{t}u(x):=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}u(y)p_{t}(x,y)\,dy,\quad x\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\ t\geq 0.

We have the following scaling property as a consequence of (
2.4
):

(2.5)

p_{t}(x)=t^{-d/\alpha}p_{1}(t^{-1/\alpha}x),\quad x\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\ t>0.

Furthermore, there exists a constant
\displaystyle c
such that

(2.6)

c^{-1}\bigg(t^{-d/\alpha}\wedge\frac{t}{|x|^{d+\alpha}}\bigg)\leq p_{t}(x)\leq c\bigg(t^{-d/\alpha}\wedge\frac{t}{|x|^{d+\alpha}}\bigg),\quad x\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\ t>0,

see, e.g.,
[
10
,
25
]
.
Thus, in short,

(2.7)

p_{t}(x)\approx t^{-d/\alpha}\wedge\frac{t}{|x|^{d+\alpha}},\quad x\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\ t>0.

Recall that
\displaystyle\tau_{D}
is the first exit time from
\displaystyle D
defined in (
1.3
). If
\displaystyle D
is bounded, then
\displaystyle\tau_{D}<\infty
almost surely, see, e.g., Pruitt
[
54
]
. The Dirichlet heat kernel
\displaystyle p_{t}^{D}(x,y)
of
\displaystyle D
is defined by the Hunt’s formula:

(2.8)

p_{t}^{D}(x,y)=p_{t}(x,y)-\mathbb{E}^{x}\big[p_{t-\tau_{D}}(X_{\tau_{D}},y)\,;\,\tau_{D}<t\big],

where
\displaystyle x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\displaystyle t>0
. Here, as usual,

\mathbb{E}^{x}\big[p_{t-\tau_{D}}(X_{\tau_{D}},y)\,;\,\tau_{D}<t\big]:=\int_{\{\tau_{D}<t\}}p_{t-\tau_{D}}(X_{\tau_{D}},y)\,d\mathbb{P}^{x}.

Since
\displaystyle D
is Lipschitz, it satisfies the exterior cone condition. Therefore,
\displaystyle\mathbb{P}^{x}(\tau_{D}=0)=1
for all
\displaystyle x\in D^{c}
by Blumenthal’s zero-one law. In particular
\displaystyle p_{t}^{D}(x,y)=0
when
\displaystyle x
or
\displaystyle y
are outside of
\displaystyle D
.
For bounded or nonnegative functions
\displaystyle f
we define

P_{t}^{D}f(x):=\mathbb{E}^{x}\big[f(X_{t})\,;\,\tau_{D}>t\big]=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(y)p_{t}^{D}(x,y)\,dy,

see
[
31
, Section 2]
. We also note that

0\leq p_{t}^{D}(x,y)=p_{t}^{D}(y,x)\leq p_{t}(y-x)

and
\displaystyle p_{t}
satisfies the Chapman–Kolmogorov equations:

\int p_{s}^{D}(x,y)p_{t}^{D}(y,z)\,dy=p_{t+s}^{D}(x,z),\quad s,t>0,\ x,z\in\mathbb{R}^{d},

see
[
16
,
29
]
.
The following scaling property follows from (
2.5
),

(2.9)

p_{t}^{D}(x,y)=t^{-d/\alpha}p_{1}^{t^{-1/\alpha}D}(t^{-1/\alpha}x,t^{-1/\alpha}y),\quad x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\ t>0.

By
[
18
, Theorem 1]
, for every
\displaystyle T>0
we have the
approximate factorization
:

(2.10)

p_{t}^{D}(x,y)\approx\mathbb{P}^{x}(\tau_{D}>t)p_{t}(x,y)\mathbb{P}^{y}(\tau_{D}>t),\quad x,y\in D,\ t\in(0,T).

If
\displaystyle D
is (open, bounded, and)
\displaystyle C^{1,1}
, then the estimate takes on a more explicit form
[
29
]
:

(2.11)

p_{t}^{D}(x,y)\approx\bigg(1\wedge\frac{\delta_{D}(x)^{\alpha/2}}{\sqrt{t}}\bigg)p_{t}(x,y)\bigg(1\wedge\frac{\delta_{D}(y)^{\alpha/2}}{\sqrt{t}}\bigg),\quad x,y\in D,\ t\in(0,T).

We also recall the large time estimates. Let
\displaystyle\lambda_{1}=\lambda_{1}(D)>0
be the first eigenvalue and
\displaystyle\varphi_{1}
the first eigenfunction of the Dirichlet fractional Laplacian on
\displaystyle D
, see Section
2.3
below for more details. By the intrinsic ultracontractivity due to Kulczycki
[
48
]
, for every
\displaystyle T>0
we have

(2.12)

p_{t}^{D}(x,y)\approx e^{-\lambda_{1}t}\varphi_{1}(x)\varphi_{1}(y),\quad x,y\in D,\ t\in(T,\infty).

If
\displaystyle D
is (open, bounded, and)
\displaystyle C^{1,1}
, then we even have

(2.13)

p_{t}^{D}(x,y)\approx e^{-\lambda_{1}t}\delta_{D}(x)^{\alpha/2}\delta_{D}(y)^{\alpha/2},\quad x,y\in D,\ t\in(T,\infty),

see
[
29
, Theorem 1.1 (ii)]
.
We define the
killing intensity
of
\displaystyle X
on
\displaystyle D
as

\kappa_{D}(z):=\int_{D^{c}}\nu(z-y)\,dy,\quad z\in D.

By
[
57
, Theorem 31.5]
,
\displaystyle\Delta^{\alpha/2}
coincides with the generator of
\displaystyle X_{t}
for the class
\displaystyle C^{2}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
of real-valued twice continuously differentiable functions with compact support in
\displaystyle\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

The
Green function
of
\displaystyle D
is given by the formula:

G_{D}(x,y):=\int_{0}^{\infty}p_{t}^{D}(x,y)\,dt,\quad x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

In particular,
\displaystyle G_{D}(x,y)=0
if either
\displaystyle x\in D^{c}
or
\displaystyle y\in D^{c}
. We note that
\displaystyle G_{D}
is finite for all
\displaystyle x\neq y
and by (
2.8
),
\displaystyle G_{D}(x,y)\leq G_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(x,y)=c|x-y|^{\alpha-d}
. For further reference, we recall the Green function estimates of Jakubowski

[
47
, Theorem 1]
: If we let

\Phi(x):=G_{D}(x_{0},x)\wedge 1,

then there exists
\displaystyle C(d,\alpha,\underline{D})>0
such that

(2.14)

\displaystyle\displaystyle C^{-1}|x-y|^{\alpha-d}\frac{\Phi(x)\Phi(y)}{\Phi(A_{x,y})^{2}}\leq G_{D}(x,y)\leq C|x-y|^{\alpha-d}\frac{\Phi(x)\Phi(y)}{\Phi(A_{x,y})^{2}}\,,\qquad x,y\in D,

see Subsection
2.1
for notation and the following remark.

Remark 2.2
.

We note that if
\displaystyle A_{1},A_{2}\in\mathcal{A}_{x,y}
, then
\displaystyle\Phi(A_{1})\approx\Phi(A_{2})
; see
[

.
We also note that

Remark 2.3
.

It is implicit in (

) and (

\displaystyle\varphi_{1}(y)\approx\mathbb{P}^{y}(\tau_{D}>1)
,
\displaystyle y\in D
. Furthermore, by

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}^{y}(\tau_{D}>1)\approx\mathbb{E}^{y}\tau_{D}

(2.15)

In our proofs, we mostly use the survival probability and
\displaystyle\Phi
, but we also refer to results stated in terms of
\displaystyle\varphi_{1}
and the expected exit time.

We define the Green operator (or Green potential)

(G_{D}f)(x):=\int_{D}G_{D}(x,y)f(y)\,dy,\quad x\in\mathbb{R}^{d},

for integrable or nonnegative functions
\displaystyle f
. For
\displaystyle f\in L^{1}(D)
, the function
\displaystyle u:=G_{D}f
is a distributional solution of
\displaystyle(-\Delta)^{\alpha/2}u=f
in
\displaystyle D
, see
[

, Proposition 3.13]
.

Definition 2.4
.

Let
\displaystyle u\geq 0
be a Borel measurable function on
\displaystyle\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

•

We say that
\displaystyle u
is
\displaystyle\alpha
-harmonic
in an open set
\displaystyle V\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
if for every open (relatively compact)
\displaystyle B\subset\subset D
,

u(x)=\mathbb{E}^{x}u(X_{\tau_{B}})<\infty,\quad x\in B.

•

We say that
\displaystyle u
is
regular
\displaystyle\alpha
-harmonic
in
\displaystyle D\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
if

u(x)=\mathbb{E}^{x}u(X_{\tau_{D}})<\infty,\quad x\in D.

•

We say that
\displaystyle u
is
singular
\displaystyle\alpha
-harmonic
in
\displaystyle D\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, if
\displaystyle u
is
\displaystyle\alpha
-harmonic in
\displaystyle D
and
\displaystyle u=0
on
\displaystyle D^{c}
.

We will often write ‘harmonic’ instead of ‘
\displaystyle\alpha
-harmonic’. Since
\displaystyle\tau_{B}\leq\tau_{V}
for
\displaystyle B\subset V
, by the strong Markov property it follows that regular harmonic functions are harmonic. Also by the strong Markov property,
\displaystyle G_{D}(\cdot,y)
is harmonic in
\displaystyle D\setminus\{y\}
, see
[
31
, Theorem 2.5]
or
[
49
, (2.1)]
.

For
\displaystyle x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the
\displaystyle\mathbb{P}^{x}
-distribution of
\displaystyle X_{\tau_{D}}
is called the
\displaystyle\alpha
-harmonic measure
, denoted by
\displaystyle\omega^{x}_{D}
. This measure is concentrated on
\displaystyle D^{c}
and for
\displaystyle u
regular harmonic in
\displaystyle D
, we have

u(x)=\int_{D^{c}}u(z)\,\omega_{D}^{x}(dz),\quad x\in D.

The
\displaystyle\alpha
-harmonic measure of a Lipschitz open set is absolutely continuous with respect to the Lebesgue measure. Its density function is given by the
Poisson kernel
:

(2.16)

P_{D}(x,z):=\int_{D}G_{D}(x,y)\nu(y,z)\,dy,\quad x\in D,\,z\in D^{c},

see
[
11
, Lemma 6]
. Therefore, for every regular harmonic
\displaystyle u
we have the representation

u(x)=\int_{D^{c}}P_{D}(x,z)u(z)\,dz,\quad x\in D.

We also recall the Ikeda–Watanabe formula from
[
46
]
:

(2.17)

\mathbb{P}^{x}\big[\tau_{D}\in I,X_{\tau_{D}-}\in A,X_{\tau_{D}}\in B\big]=\int_{I}\int_{B}\int_{A}\nu(y,z)p_{u}^{D}(x,dy)\,dz\,du,

where
\displaystyle I\subset(0,\infty)
,
\displaystyle A\subset D
, and
\displaystyle B\subset(\overline{D})^{c}
. See also
[
6
, Lemma 1]
,
[
11
]
,
[
24
, (4.13)]
, or
[
59
, Theorem 2.4]
.

Recall that
\displaystyle x_{0}\in D
is an arbitrary but fixed (reference) point. We define the
Martin kernel
,
\displaystyle M_{D}^{x_{0}}(y,Q)
as follows: for every
\displaystyle Q\in\partial D
and
\displaystyle y\in D
we let

(2.18)

M_{D}^{x_{0}}(y,Q)=\lim_{D\ni x\to Q}\frac{G_{D}(x,y)}{G_{D}(x,x_{0})}.

In
[
12
, Lemma 6]
it is shown that the Martin kernel exists, the mapping
\displaystyle(y,Q)\mapsto M_{D}^{x_{0}}(y,Q)
is continuous on
\displaystyle D\times\partial D
, and for every
\displaystyle Q\in\partial D
the function
\displaystyle M_{D}^{x_{0}}(\cdot,Q)
is singular
\displaystyle\alpha
-harmonic in
\displaystyle D
.

2.3.
Auxiliary results on
\displaystyle P_{t}^{D}
and its spectral decomposition

We recall that the operators
\displaystyle P_{t}^{D}
are compact on
\displaystyle L^{2}(D)
, see, e.g.,
[
15
, Chapter 4]
. Therefore there exist a nondecreasing sequence of nonnegative numbers
\displaystyle\lambda_{n}
diverging to infinity and an orthonormal sequence of functions
\displaystyle\varphi_{n}\in C_{0}(D)
such that for every
\displaystyle\phi\in L^{2}(D)
, we have

(2.19)

\displaystyle\displaystyle P_{t}^{D}\phi(x)=\sum\limits_{n=1}^{\infty}e^{-\lambda_{n}t}\langle\phi,\varphi_{n}\rangle\varphi_{n}(x)

and

(2.20)

\displaystyle\displaystyle p_{t}^{D}(x,y)=\sum\limits_{n=1}^{\infty}e^{-\lambda_{n}t}\varphi_{n}(x)\varphi_{n}(y),\quad x,y\in D,\;t>0.

The fractional Weyl bounds
[
9
,
41
]
read

(2.21)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\lambda_{n}\approx n^{\alpha/d}.

Note that
\displaystyle P_{t}^{D}\varphi_{n}=e^{-\lambda_{n}t}\varphi_{n}
pointwise. Therefore,

(2.22)

\displaystyle\displaystyle G_{D}\varphi_{n}=\int_{0}^{\infty}P_{t}^{D}\varphi_{n}\,dt=\lambda_{n}^{-1}\varphi_{n}.

By iterating (
2.22
) and using the regularity results for the fractional Laplacian
[
56
,
43
]
, we find that
\displaystyle\varphi_{n}
are smooth in
\displaystyle D
. Furthermore, by
[
38
, Proposition 3.1]
, there exist
\displaystyle C>0
and
\displaystyle w\geq 1
, such that

(2.23)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\|\varphi_{n}\|_{\infty}\leq C\lambda_{n}^{w-1},\quad n\in\mathbb{N}.

We say that
\displaystyle\phi
belongs to
\displaystyle D(L^{D})
, the domain of the
\displaystyle L^{2}
-generator of
\displaystyle P_{t}^{D}
, if the following limit exists in
\displaystyle L^{2}
:

\displaystyle\displaystyle L^{D}\phi:=\lim\limits_{t\to 0^{+}}\frac{P_{t}^{D}\phi-\phi}{t}.

Furthermore, if the limit exists for a function
\displaystyle\phi
and some
\displaystyle x\in D
, we denote it as
\displaystyle L^{D}\phi(x)
.

Lemma 2.5
.

(1)

We have
\displaystyle\varphi_{n}\in D(L^{D})
and
\displaystyle L^{D}\varphi_{n}(x)=-\lambda_{n}\varphi_{n}(x)
for all
\displaystyle x\in D
.

(2)

We have

\displaystyle\displaystyle A:=\{\phi\in L^{2}(D):\sum\limits_{n=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{n}^{2}|\langle\phi,\varphi_{n}\rangle|^{2}<\infty\}\subseteq D(L^{D}),

and for each
\displaystyle\phi\in A
,

\displaystyle\displaystyle L^{D}\phi=\sum\limits_{n=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{n}\langle\phi,\varphi_{n}\rangle\varphi_{n}.

(3)

For every
\displaystyle y\in D
and
\displaystyle t>0
,
\displaystyle p_{t}^{D}(\cdot,y)\in A
.

(4)

For every
\displaystyle x,y\in D
, we have
\displaystyle L^{D}_{x}p_{t}^{D}(x,y)=\Delta^{\alpha/2}_{x}p_{t}^{D}(x,y)
.

Proof.

Statements (1) and (2) follow quite easily from (
2.19
) and (
2.21
). In order to prove (3), we first let
\displaystyle m\in\mathbb{N}
. Then, by (
2.20
) and (
2.23
),

\displaystyle\displaystyle|\langle p_{t}^{D}(\cdot,y),\varphi_{m}\rangle|=|e^{-\lambda_{m}t}\varphi_{m}(x)|\leq e^{-\lambda_{m}t}\|\varphi_{m}\|_{\infty}\leq Ce^{-\lambda_{m}t}\lambda_{m}^{w-1}.

Using (
2.21
), we get (3).

We now prove (4). Note that
\displaystyle x\mapsto p_{t}^{D}(x,y)\in C^{2}(D)\cap C_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Let
\displaystyle\phi\in C^{2}_{c}(B(x,\delta_{D}(x)/2))
(extended by 0 to the whole of
\displaystyle\mathbb{R}^{d}
) and
\displaystyle g\in C_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
be such that
\displaystyle\phi(x)+g(x)=p_{t}^{D}(x,y)
and
\displaystyle g(x)=0
on
\displaystyle B(x,\delta_{D}(x)/4)
. Note that by (
2.7
),

(2.24)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{p_{t}^{D}(x,z)}{t}\leq\frac{p_{t}(x,z)}{t}\lesssim\nu(x,z),

which for
\displaystyle|x-z|>\delta_{D}(x)/4
is uniformly bounded.
Furthermore, since
\displaystyle p_{t}(x,z)/t\to\nu(x,z)
for all
\displaystyle x,z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
\displaystyle x\neq z
, by (
2.8
) we find that for fixed
\displaystyle z\in D\setminus\{x\}
,

\displaystyle\displaystyle\lim\limits_{t\to 0^{+}}\frac{p_{t}^{D}(x,z)}{t}=\nu(x,y)+\lim\limits_{t\to 0^{+}}\frac{1}{t}\mathbb{E}^{x}[p_{t-\tau_{D}}(X_{\tau_{D}},z)\,;\,\tau_{D}<t].

Since
\displaystyle x
and
\displaystyle z
are fixed we have
\displaystyle p_{t-\tau_{D}}(X_{\tau_{D}},z)\lesssim t
, so the limit on the right hand side is equal to 0, hence
\displaystyle p_{t}^{D}(x,z)/t\to\nu(x,z)
as well. By this, (
2.24
), and the dominated convergence theorem, we get
\displaystyle\Delta^{\alpha/2}g(x)=L^{D}g(x)
.

Let
\displaystyle L
be the
\displaystyle C_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
-generator of the semigroup induced by
\displaystyle p_{t}
. By Sato
[
57
, Theorem 31.5]
, we have
\displaystyle\Delta^{\alpha/2}\phi(x)=L\phi(x)
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\displaystyle L^{D}\phi(x)=\Delta^{\alpha/2}\phi(x)+\lim\limits_{t\to 0^{+}}\frac{P_{t}^{D}\phi(x)-P_{t}\phi(x)}{t}.

We will show that the last limit exists and is equal to 0. By (
2.8
), Fubini–Tonelli, and the fact that
\displaystyle X_{\tau_{D}}\in D^{c}
almost surely,

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{|P_{t}^{D}\phi(x)-P_{t}\phi(x)|}{t}\leq\|\phi\|_{\infty}\frac{1}{t}\mathbb{E}^{x}\bigg[\int_{B(x,\delta_{D}(x)/2)}p_{t-\tau_{D}}(X_{\tau_{D}},z)\,dz\,;\,\tau_{D}<t\bigg]\lesssim\mathbb{P}^{x}(\tau_{D}<t)\mathop{\longrightarrow}\limits^{t\to 0^{+}}0.

By collecting the above results we find that

\displaystyle\displaystyle\Delta^{\alpha/2}_{x}p_{t}^{D}(x,y)=\Delta^{\alpha/2}\phi(x)+\Delta^{\alpha/2}g(x)=L^{D}\phi(x)+L^{D}g(x)=L^{D}_{x}p_{t}^{D}(x,y),

which ends the proof.
∎

Corollary 2.6
.

For every
\displaystyle t>0
,
\displaystyle\Delta^{\alpha/2}_{x}p_{t}^{D}
is bounded in
\displaystyle D\times D
.

Proof.

By Lemma
2.5
and (
2.23
), we have

\displaystyle\displaystyle|\Delta^{\alpha/2}_{x}p_{t}^{D}(x,y)|=|L^{D}_{x}p_{t}^{D}(x,y)|=\bigg|\sum\limits_{n=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{n}e^{-\lambda_{n}t}\varphi_{n}(x)\varphi_{n}(y)\bigg|\leq\sum\limits_{n=1}^{\infty}C\lambda_{n}e^{-\lambda_{n}t}\lambda_{n}^{2w-2}\leq C_{0}<\infty.

∎

Lemma 2.7
.

Let
\displaystyle\phi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(D)
. Then,

\displaystyle\displaystyle P_{t}^{D}L\phi(y)=\sum\limits_{n=1}^{\infty}e^{-\lambda_{n}t}\lambda_{n}\langle\phi,\varphi_{n}\rangle\varphi_{n}(y),\quad y\in D.

Proof.

Note that
\displaystyle L\phi\in L^{2}(D)
, hence

\displaystyle\displaystyle P_{t}^{D}L\phi(y)=\sum\limits_{n=1}^{\infty}e^{-\lambda_{n}t}\langle\varphi_{n},L\phi\rangle\varphi_{n}(y).

By (
2.22
) we have
\displaystyle\varphi_{n}=G_{D}[\lambda_{n}\varphi_{n}]
. Therefore, by
[
14
, Proposition 3.13]
,

\displaystyle\displaystyle\langle\varphi_{n},L\phi\rangle=\langle G_{D}[\lambda_{n}\varphi_{n}],L\phi\rangle=\langle\lambda_{n}\varphi_{n},\phi\rangle,

which ends the proof of the lemma.
∎

The following result is a weighted Hausdorff–Young type inequality.

Lemma 2.8
.

There exist
\displaystyle c=c(d,\alpha,\underline{D})
and
\displaystyle w\in\mathbb{N}
such that for any
\displaystyle p\in[2,\infty]
and
\displaystyle u\in L^{p}(D)
,

\displaystyle\displaystyle\|u\|_{L^{p}(D)}\leq c\bigg(\sum\limits_{n=1}^{\infty}|\langle u,\varphi_{n}\rangle|^{p^{\prime}}\lambda_{n}^{w-1}\bigg)^{1/p^{\prime}},

where
\displaystyle p^{\prime}=p/(p-1)
is the Hölder conjugate exponent of
\displaystyle p
.

Proof.

Let
\displaystyle\phi\in L^{2}(D)
. By (
2.23
), we have
\displaystyle\|\varphi_{n}\|_{\infty}\leq C\lambda_{n}^{w-1}
for some
\displaystyle C>0
and
\displaystyle w\geq 1
independent of
\displaystyle n
. Therefore for
\displaystyle x\in D
,

\displaystyle\displaystyle\|\phi\|_{\infty}\leq\sum\limits_{n=1}^{\infty}|\langle\phi,\varphi_{n}\rangle|\|\varphi_{n}\|_{\infty}\leq C\sum\limits_{n=1}^{\infty}|\langle\phi,\varphi_{n}\rangle|\lambda_{n}^{w-1}.

If we let
\displaystyle\hat{\phi}=(\langle\phi,\varphi_{1}\rangle,\langle\phi,\varphi_{2}\rangle,\ldots)
and denote by
\displaystyle l^{p}_{\lambda}
the space of sequences with the
\displaystyle p
-th powers summable with the weight
\displaystyle(\lambda_{1}^{w-1},\lambda_{2}^{w-1},\ldots)
, then the above means that
\displaystyle\hat{\phi}\mapsto\phi
is bounded from
\displaystyle l^{1}_{\lambda}
to
\displaystyle L^{\infty}(D)
. By Parseval’s identity, this map is also bounded from
\displaystyle l^{2}
to
\displaystyle L^{2}(D)
, hence also from
\displaystyle l^{2}_{\lambda}
to
\displaystyle L^{2}(D)
. The statement of the lemma follows from the Riesz–Thorin theorem.
∎

3.
Yaglom limits in Lipschitz open sets

In this section we prove Theorem
1.1
. We first establish the asymptotics of Green potentials at the boundary points of
\displaystyle D
. This extends what is already known about the asymptotics of Green potentials at the vertex of cone
[
23
, Lemma 3.5]
; we also propose a different proof.

Lemma 3.1
.

If
\displaystyle f
is a measurable function bounded on
\displaystyle D
and
\displaystyle Q\in\partial D
, then

\lim_{x\to Q}\int_{D}\frac{G_{D}(x,y)}{G_{D}(x,x_{0})}f(y)\,dy=\int_{D}\lim_{x\to Q}\frac{G_{D}(x,y)}{G_{D}(x,x_{0})}f(y)\,dy<\infty,\quad x\in D.

Proof.

Fix two points
\displaystyle x_{1},x_{2}\in D
and let

(3.1)

\rho=(\delta_{D}(x_{1})\wedge\delta_{D}(x_{2})\wedge|x_{1}-x_{2}|)/3,

so that
\displaystyle B(x_{1},\rho),B(x_{2},\rho)\subset D
and
\displaystyle B(x_{1},\rho)\cap B(x_{2},\rho)=\emptyset
. We know that
\displaystyle M_{D}^{x_{0}}(\cdot,Q)
given by (
2.18
) is regular
\displaystyle\alpha
-harmonic on
\displaystyle B(x_{1},\rho)
and
\displaystyle B(x_{2},\rho)
, and for
\displaystyle x
sufficiently close to
\displaystyle\partial D
so is
\displaystyle G_{D}(x,\cdot)
.
Therefore, for
\displaystyle i=1,2
,

\displaystyle\displaystyle\int_{B(x_{i},\rho)^{c}}\lim_{x\to Q}\frac{G_{D}(x,y)}{G_{D}(x,x_{0})}\,\omega_{B(x_{i},\rho)}^{x_{i}}(dy)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\int_{B(z_{i},\rho)^{c}}M_{D}^{x_{0}}(y,Q)\,\omega_{B(z_{i},\rho)}^{z_{i}}(dy)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=M_{D}^{x_{0}}(x_{i},Q)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\lim_{x\to Q}\frac{G_{D}(x,x_{i})}{G_{D}(x,x_{0})}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\lim_{x\to Q}\frac{\int_{B(x_{i},\rho)^{c}}G_{D}(x,y)\,\omega_{B(x_{i},\rho)}^{x_{i}}(dy)}{G_{D}(x,x_{0})}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\lim_{x\to Q}\int_{B(x_{i},\rho)^{c}}\frac{G_{D}(x,y)}{G_{D}(x,x_{0})}\,\omega_{B(x_{i},\rho)}^{x_{i}}(dy).

The
\displaystyle\alpha
-harmonic measures
\displaystyle\omega^{x_{i}}_{B(x_{i},\rho)}(dy)
are absolutely continuous and have radially decreasing densities
\displaystyle g_{i}
, see, e.g.,
[
12
]
. Therefore there exists
\displaystyle C>0
such that
\displaystyle\omega^{x_{i}}_{B(x_{i},\rho)}(dy)=g_{i}(y)\,dy\geq C
for
\displaystyle y\in D\cap(B(x_{i},\rho)^{c})
. Let
\displaystyle g=g_{1}+g_{2}
. Vitali’s theorem
[
58
, Theorem 16.6 (i) and (iii)]
yields the following
\displaystyle L^{1}
convergence:

\lim\limits_{x\to Q}\int_{D}\bigg|\frac{G_{D}(x,y)}{G_{D}(x,x_{0})}g(y)-M_{D}^{x_{0}}(y,Q)g(y)\bigg|\,dy=0.

Since
\displaystyle|f|\lesssim C\lesssim g
, the result follows.
∎

We can also establish the following identity, an analogue of
[
23
, (3.16)]
.

Lemma 3.2
.

For
\displaystyle x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we have

(3.2)

\mathbb{P}^{x}(\tau_{D}>1)=(G_{D}P_{1}^{D}\kappa_{D})(x).

Proof.

Let
\displaystyle x\in D
. Since our set
\displaystyle D
is Lipschitz, from Lemma 6 and the proof of Lemma 17 in
[
11
]
,

\displaystyle\displaystyle\omega_{D}^{x}(\partial D)=\mathbb{P}^{x}(X_{\tau_{D}}\in\partial D)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathbb{P}^{x}(X_{\tau_{D}-}=X_{\tau_{D}})

\displaystyle\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathbb{P}^{x}(X_{\tau_{D}-}\in D)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=1.

By the Ikeda–Watanabe formula (
2.17
) and the Chapman–Kolmogorov equations we have

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathbb{P}^{x}(\tau_{D}>1)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}^{x}\big[\tau_{D}>1,\ X_{\tau_{D}-}\in D,\ X_{\tau_{D}}\in D^{c}\big]

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\int_{1}^{\infty}\int_{D^{c}}\int_{D}p_{s}^{D}(x,z)\nu(z-w)\,dz\,dw\,ds

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{D^{c}}\int_{0}^{\infty}p_{t+1}^{D}(x,z)\nu(z-w)\,dt\,dw\,dz

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{D^{c}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{D}p_{t}^{D}(x,y)p_{1}^{D}(y,z)\,dy\,\nu(z-w)\,dt\,dw\,dz

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\int_{D}\int_{0}^{\infty}p_{t}^{D}(x,y)\,dt\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}p_{1}^{D}(y,z)\int_{D^{c}}\nu(z-w)\,dw\,dz\,dy

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\int_{D}G_{D}(x,y)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}p_{1}^{D}(y,z)\kappa_{D}(z)\,dz\,dy

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\int_{D}G_{D}(x,y)(P_{1}^{D}\kappa_{D})(y)\,dy

\displaystyle\displaystyle=(G_{D}P_{1}^{D}\kappa_{D})(x).

For
\displaystyle x\in D^{c}
, both sides of (
3.2
) are equal to 0. This ends the proof.

∎

We define

C_{1}:=\int_{D}\int_{D}M_{D}^{x_{0}}(y,Q)p_{1}^{D}(y,z)\kappa_{D}(z)\,dz\,dy.

Combining the two lemmas above, we obtain the following result.

Lemma 3.3
.

We have
\displaystyle 0<C_{1}<\infty
and

\displaystyle\lim_{x\to Q}\frac{\mathbb{P}^{x}(\tau_{D}>1)}{G_{D}(x,x_{0})}=C_{1}.

Proof.

By Lemma
3.2
,
\displaystyle\mathbb{P}^{x}(\tau_{D}>1)=(G_{D}P_{1}^{D}\kappa_{D})(x)
. Note that
\displaystyle(P_{1}^{D}\kappa_{D})(y)
is bounded. Indeed, by (
2.10
),

\displaystyle\displaystyle(P_{1}^{D}\kappa_{D})(y)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\int_{D}p_{1}^{D}(y,z)\kappa_{D}(z)\,dz

(3.3)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\approx\mathbb{P}^{y}(\tau_{D}>1)\int_{D}\mathbb{P}^{z}(\tau_{D}>1)p_{1}(y,z)\kappa_{D}(z)\,dz,\quad y\in D.

Since
\displaystyle D
is bounded, by (
2.7
)

(3.4)

p_{1}(y,z)\approx 1,\quad y,z\in D.

Hence (
3.3
) becomes

(3.5)

(P_{1}^{D}\kappa_{D})(y)\approx\mathbb{P}^{y}(\tau_{D}>1)\int_{D}\mathbb{P}^{z}(\tau_{D}>1)\kappa_{D}(z)\,dz,\quad y\in D.

Using (
3.2
), we see that for
\displaystyle x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

\int_{D}G_{D}(x,y)(P_{1}^{D}\kappa_{D})(y)\,dy=(G_{D}P_{1}^{D}\kappa_{D})(x)=\mathbb{P}^{x}(\tau_{D}>1)\leq 1.

By (
2.14
),
\displaystyle G_{D}(x,y)
is strictly positive for all
\displaystyle x,y\in D
. Thus
\displaystyle P_{1}^{D}\kappa_{D}
has to be finite almost everywhere. Hence the integral in (
3.5
) is finite and

(P_{1}^{D}\kappa_{D})(y)\approx\mathbb{P}^{y}(\tau_{D}>1),

for
\displaystyle y\in D
. In particular,
\displaystyle(P_{1}^{D}\kappa_{D})(y)
is bounded on
\displaystyle D
. By using Lemma
3.1
with
\displaystyle f(y)=(P_{1}^{D}\kappa_{D})(y)
,

\displaystyle\displaystyle\lim_{x\to Q}\frac{\mathbb{P}^{x}(\tau_{D}>1)}{G_{D}(x,x_{0})}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\lim_{x\to Q}\frac{(G_{D}P_{1}^{D}\kappa_{D})(x)}{G_{D}(x,x_{0})}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\lim_{x\to Q}\int_{D}\frac{G_{D}(x,y)}{G_{D}(x,x_{0})}(P_{1}^{D}\kappa_{D})(y)\,dy

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\int_{D}M_{D}^{x_{0}}(y,Q)(P_{1}^{D}\kappa_{D})(y)\,dy=C_{1}<\infty.

∎

We are now in a position to prove Theorem
1.1
.

Proof of
Theorem
1.1
.

Let us define

(3.6)

m_{x}(A):=\frac{\int_{A}p_{1}^{D}(x,y)dy}{\mathbb{P}^{x}(\tau_{D}>1)},\quad x\in D,\ A\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}.

First we note that the family
\displaystyle\{m_{x}:x\in D\}
is tight. Indeed, combining the factorization of
\displaystyle p_{1}^{D}(x,y)
in (

) with the equation (
3.4
), we get

\frac{p_{1}^{D}(x,y)}{\mathbb{P}^{x}(\tau_{D}>1)}\approx\mathbb{P}^{y}(\tau_{D}>1),\qquad x,y\in D.

Since the densities of the measures
\displaystyle m_{x}(A)
are bounded by an integrable function, the tightness follows.

Next we wish to prove that the measures
\displaystyle m_{x}
converge weakly to a probability measure
\displaystyle m_{Q}
on
\displaystyle D
as
\displaystyle x\to Q
. To this end, consider an arbitrary sequence
\displaystyle\{x_{n}\}
such that
\displaystyle x_{n}\to Q
. By tightness, there exists a subsequence
\displaystyle\{x_{n_{k}}\}
such that
\displaystyle m_{x_{n_{k}}}\implies m_{Q}
for some probability measure
\displaystyle m_{Q}
, as
\displaystyle k\to\infty
. We will show that this limit is unique.

Let
\displaystyle\phi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(D)
and
\displaystyle u_{\phi}=(-\Delta)^{\alpha/2}\phi
. For
\displaystyle x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

(3.8)

(P_{1}^{D}\phi)(x)=(G_{D}P_{1}^{D}u_{\phi})(x).

To show this, we first remark that
\displaystyle u_{\phi}\in C_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d})

\displaystyle(G_{D}u_{\phi})(x)=\phi(x)

[
36

[
22

\displaystyle\displaystyle(P_{1}^{D}|u_{\phi}|)(x)=\int_{D}p_{1}^{D}(x,y)|u_{\phi}(y)|\,dy\leq c<\infty.

Therefore, since for a fixed
\displaystyle z\in D^{c}
we have
\displaystyle\nu(y,z)\gtrsim 1
for
\displaystyle y\in D
, by (
2.16
) we get

\displaystyle\displaystyle(G_{D}P_{1}^{D}|u_{\phi}|)(x)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\int_{D}G_{D}(x,y)(P_{1}^{D}|u_{\phi}|)(y)\,dy

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq c\int_{D}G_{D}(x,y)\,dy<\infty.

As a result, we can apply Fubini–Tonelli theorem and establish (
3.8
) as follows:

\displaystyle\displaystyle(G_{D}P_{1}^{D}u_{\phi})(x)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\int_{D}\int_{D}\int_{0}^{\infty}p_{t}^{D}(x,y)p_{1}^{D}(y,z)u_{\phi}(z)\,dt\,dz\,dy

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\int_{D}\int_{0}^{\infty}p_{t+1}^{D}(x,z)u_{\phi}(z)\,dt\,dz

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\int_{D}\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{D}p_{1}^{D}(x,y)p_{t}^{D}(y,z)u_{\phi}(z)\,dy\,dt\,dz

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\int_{D}\int_{D}\int_{0}^{\infty}p_{1}^{D}(x,y)p_{t}^{D}(y,z)u_{\phi}(z)\,dt\,dz\,dy

\displaystyle\displaystyle=(P_{1}^{D}G_{D}u_{\phi})(x)=(P_{1}^{D}\phi)(x).

Let us denote
\displaystyle m_{x}(\phi):=\int_{D}\phi(y)\,m_{x}(dy)
. Using (
3.8
), Lemma
3.3
, and Lemma
3.1
, we get

\displaystyle\displaystyle\lim_{x\to Q}m_{x}(\phi)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\lim_{x\to Q}\frac{(P_{1}^{D}\phi)(x)}{\mathbb{P}^{x}(\tau_{D}>1)}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\lim_{x\to Q}\frac{(P_{1}^{D}G_{D}u_{\phi})(x)}{\mathbb{P}^{x}(\tau_{D}>1)}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\lim_{x\to Q}\frac{(G_{D}P_{1}^{D}u_{\phi})(x)}{G_{D}(x,x_{0})}\frac{G_{D}(x,x_{0})}{\mathbb{P}^{x}(\tau_{D}>1)}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\frac{1}{C_{1}}\int_{D}M_{D}^{x_{0}}(y,Q)(P_{1}^{D}u_{\phi})(y)\,dy.

In particular,
\displaystyle m_{Q}(\phi):=\lim_{k\to\infty}m_{x_{n_{k}}}(\phi)
does not depend on the choice of the subsequence. Thus, by the Portmanteau Theorem,
\displaystyle m_{x}\implies m_{Q}
as
\displaystyle x\to Q
.

For
\displaystyle t>1
, we consider
\displaystyle\phi_{t,y}(\cdot):=p_{t-1}^{D}(\cdot,y)\in C_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d})

[

]

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\lim_{x\to Q}\frac{\int_{D}p_{t-1}^{D}(z,y)p_{1}^{D}(x,z)\,dz}{\mathbb{P}^{x}(\tau_{D}>1)}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\lim_{x\to Q}\frac{(P_{1}^{D}p_{t-1}^{D}(\cdot,y))(x)}{\mathbb{P}^{x}(\tau_{D}>1)}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\lim_{x\to Q}m_{x}(p_{t-1}^{D}(\cdot,y)).

By (

\eta_{t,Q}(y)=m_{Q}(p_{t-1}^{D}(\cdot,y)).

Corollary 3.4
.

The functions
\displaystyle(t,y)\mapsto\eta_{t,Q}(y),\widetilde{\eta}_{t,Q}(y),\eta^{x_{0}}_{t,Q}(y)

\displaystyle(0,\infty)\times\overline{D}

Proof.

By Theorem
1.1

\displaystyle p_{t}^{D}(x,y)

are continuous for

\displaystyle((t_{n},y_{n}))\subset(0,\infty)\times D
such that
\displaystyle(t_{n},y_{n})\to(t,Q)\in(0,\infty)\times\partial D

\displaystyle\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{p_{t_{n}}^{D}(x,y_{n})}{\mathbb{P}^{y_{n}}(\tau_{D}>1)}=\eta_{t,Q}(x).

Furthermore, by Theorem
1.1
, in order to obtain (

\displaystyle t>0
there exists a modulus of continuity
\displaystyle\omega
independent of
\displaystyle y
such that

\displaystyle\displaystyle\bigg|\frac{p_{t+\varepsilon}^{D}(x,y)-p_{t}^{D}(x,y)}{\mathbb{P}^{y}(\tau_{D}>1)}\bigg|\leq\omega(\varepsilon),\quad\varepsilon>0.

\displaystyle\displaystyle\bigg|\frac{p_{t+\varepsilon}^{D}(x,y)-p_{t}^{D}(x,y)}{\mathbb{P}^{y}(\tau_{D}>1)}\bigg|

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\bigg(\int_{D\setminus B(x,\delta_{D}(x)/2)}+\int_{B(x,\delta_{D}(x)/2)}\bigg)\frac{|p_{t}^{D}(z,y)-p_{t}^{D}(x,y)|p_{\varepsilon}^{D}(x,z)}{\mathbb{P}^{y}(\tau_{D}>1)}\,dz=:I_{1}+I_{2}.

2.10

\displaystyle\displaystyle I_{1}\leq\int_{D\setminus B(x,\delta_{D}(x)/2)}p_{\varepsilon}^{D}(x,z)\,dz\leq\int_{D\setminus B(x,\delta_{D}(x)/2)}p_{\varepsilon}(x,z)\,dz\leq\omega(\epsilon).

\displaystyle\displaystyle I_{2}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq\int_{B(x,\delta_{D}(x)/2)}\frac{|p_{t}^{D}(z,y)-p_{t}^{D}(x,y)|p_{\varepsilon}^{D}(x,z)}{\mathbb{P}^{y}(\tau_{D}>1)}\,dz

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq\int_{B(x,\delta_{D}(x)/2)}|x-z|\frac{\|\nabla_{x}p_{t}^{D}(\cdot,y)\|_{L^{\infty}(B(x,\delta_{D}(x)/2)}}{\mathbb{P}^{y}(\tau_{D}>1)}p_{\varepsilon}^{D}(x,z)\,dz

\displaystyle I_{1}+I_{2}\leq\omega(\varepsilon)

\displaystyle\eta

Theorem 3.7
.

Suppose that
\displaystyle D
is a bounded Lipschitz open set such that
\displaystyle 0\in\partial D
and
\displaystyle D\cup\{0\}
is star-shaped at
\displaystyle 0
. If
\displaystyle x\in D
then for every Borel
\displaystyle A\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

\lim_{t\to\infty}\mathbb{P}^{x}\bigg(\frac{X_{t}}{t^{1/\alpha}}\in A\ \bigg|\ \bigg(\frac{X_{s}}{t^{1/\alpha}}\bigg)_{0\leq s\leq t}\subset D\bigg)=m_{0}(A),

where
\displaystyle\mathbb{P}^{x}(A_{1}|A_{2}):=\mathbb{P}^{x}(A_{1}\cap A_{2})/\mathbb{P}^{x}(A_{2})
is the conditional probability and
\displaystyle m_{0}(A):=\int_{A}\eta_{1,{0}}(y)\,dy
.

Proof.

Let
\displaystyle x\in D
,
\displaystyle t\geq 1
, and let
\displaystyle A\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be Borel. Then we have

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathbb{P}^{x}\bigg(\frac{X_{t}}{t^{1/\alpha}}\in A\ \bigg|\ \bigg(\frac{X_{s}}{t^{1/\alpha}}\bigg)_{0\leq s\leq t}\subset D\bigg)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\frac{\mathbb{P}^{x}(X_{t}\in t^{1/\alpha}A,\ (X_{s})_{0\leq s\leq t}\subset t^{1/\alpha}D)}{\mathbb{P}^{x}((X_{s})_{0\leq s\leq t}\subset t^{1/\alpha}D)}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\frac{\int_{t^{1/\alpha}A}p_{t}^{t^{1/\alpha}D}(x,y)\,dy}{\int_{t^{1/\alpha}D}p_{t}^{t^{1/\alpha}D}(x,y)\,dy}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\frac{\int_{t^{1/\alpha}A}t^{-d/\alpha}p_{1}^{D}(t^{-1/\alpha}x,t^{-1/\alpha}y)\,dy}{\int_{t^{1/\alpha}D}t^{-d/\alpha}p_{1}^{D}(t^{-1/\alpha}x,t^{-1/\alpha}y)\,dy}

where
\displaystyle m_{t^{-1/\alpha}x}
is the measure defined in (

\displaystyle t^{-1/\alpha}x\in D
).
Therefore, by Theorem

as

∎

4.
Hölder regularity

This section is devoted to proving Theorems
1.4
and
1.2
. The proof of Theorem
1.4
uses a mix of the boundary Harnack principle and interior Hölder regularity. Then Theorem
1.2
follows by using the formulas of Section

Fix
\displaystyle n_{0}\geq 2
such that the reference points
\displaystyle x_{0}
belongs to
\displaystyle D_{n_{0}/2}
.

Lemma 4.1
.

There exists
\displaystyle p_{0}=p_{0}(d,\alpha,\underline{D})>1
such that the family
\displaystyle\{(G_{D}(y,\cdot)/G_{D}(x_{0},y))^{p}:y\in D\}
is uniformly integrable in
\displaystyle D
for all
\displaystyle p\in[1,p_{0})
.

Proof.

For
\displaystyle y\in D_{n_{0}}
we have a crude bound:

\displaystyle\displaystyle G_{D}(y,z)/G_{D}(x_{0},y)\leq C(d,\alpha,\underline{D})|y-z|^{\alpha-d},\quad z\in D.

Considering the functions on the right-hand side, we see that
\displaystyle p_{0}=d/(d-\alpha)
will do.

From now on assume that
\displaystyle y\in D\setminus D_{n_{0}}
. By (
2.14
), there exists
\displaystyle C=C(d,\alpha,\underline{D})
such that

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{G_{D}(y,z)}{G_{D}(x_{0},y)}\leq C\frac{|y-z|^{\alpha-d}}{|x_{0}-y|^{\alpha-d}}\frac{\Phi(z)\Phi(A_{x_{0},y})^{2}}{\Phi(x_{0})\Phi(A_{y,z})^{2}}.

We immediately get that

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{G_{D}(y,z)}{G_{D}(x_{0},y)}\leq C^{\prime}|y-z|^{\alpha-d}\frac{\Phi(z)}{\Phi(A_{y,z})^{2}}.

By the Carleson estimate
[
47
, Lemma 13]
, we further find that
\displaystyle\Phi(z)/\Phi(A_{y,z})\leq C(d,\alpha,\underline{D})
. If we let
\displaystyle U=D_{32/r_{0}}\cup(D\setminus B(y,r_{0}/32))
, then it follows that

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{G_{D}(y,z)}{G_{D}(x_{0},y)}\leq\begin{cases}C(d,\alpha,\underline{D})|y-z|^{\alpha-d},\quad&z\in U,\\
C(d,\alpha,\underline{D})|y-z|^{\alpha-d}\Phi(A_{y,z})^{-1},\quad&z\in D\setminus U.\end{cases}

The definition of
\displaystyle A_{y,z}
implies that for
\displaystyle z\in D\setminus U
there exists
\displaystyle Q=Q(z)\in\partial D
such that
\displaystyle y,z\in B(Q,3r)
and
\displaystyle B(A_{y,z},\kappa r)\subset D\cap B(Q,6r)
. Using
[
11
, Lemma 5]
, we find that there exist
\displaystyle C=C(d,\alpha,\underline{D})
and
\displaystyle\gamma=\gamma(d,\alpha,\underline{D})\in(0,\alpha)
such that

\displaystyle\displaystyle\Phi(A_{y,z})\geq C|A_{y,z}-Q(z)|^{\gamma}\geq C\kappa^{\gamma}r^{\gamma}\geq C\kappa^{\gamma}|y-z|^{\gamma}.

Therefore,

(4.1)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{G_{D}(y,z)}{G_{D}(x_{0},y)}\leq C(|y-z|^{\alpha-d}\vee|y-z|^{\alpha-\gamma-d}),\quad y\in D\setminus D_{n_{0}},\ z\in D,

so the statement of the lemma holds for all
\displaystyle p\in[1,d/(d-\alpha+\gamma))
. We can take
\displaystyle p_{0}=d/(d-\alpha+\gamma)
.
∎

The following lemma is a specific Carleson-type estimate.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
\displaystyle 0<r<\delta_{D}(y)
,
\displaystyle|z-y|\geq 2r
, and
\displaystyle|v-y|\leq r
. There exists
\displaystyle C=C(d,\alpha,\underline{D})
such that

\displaystyle\displaystyle G_{D}(z,v)\leq CG_{D}(z,y).

Note that
\displaystyle 2|z-v|\geq|z-y|
. By (

), there is
\displaystyle c=c(d,\alpha,\underline{D})
such that

\displaystyle\displaystyle G_{D}(z,v)\leq c\frac{\Phi(z)\Phi(v)}{\Phi(A_{z,v})^{2}}|z-v|^{\alpha-d}\leq 2^{d-\alpha}c\frac{\Phi(z)\Phi(v)}{\Phi(A_{z,v})^{2}}|z-y|^{\alpha-d}.

By elementary calculations, we find that
\displaystyle 2r_{z,v}\geq r_{z,y}

[
47
, Lemma 13]
we therefore get
\displaystyle\Phi(A_{z,v})\geq c(d,\alpha,\underline{D})\Phi(A_{z,y})

[

we get
\displaystyle\Phi(v)\leq c(d,\alpha,\underline{D})\Phi(y)

∎

\displaystyle\displaystyle\bigg|\frac{p_{t}^{D}(x,y)}{G_{D}(x_{0},y)}-\frac{p_{t}^{D}(x,y^{\prime})}{G_{D}(x_{0},y^{\prime})}\bigg|

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq\int_{D}\bigg|\frac{G_{D}(y,z)}{G_{D}(x_{0},y)}-\frac{G_{D}(y^{\prime},z)}{G_{D}(x_{0},y^{\prime})}\bigg||f(z)|\,dz

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq\bigg\|\frac{G_{D}(y,\cdot)}{G_{D}(x_{0},y)}-\frac{G_{D}(y^{\prime},\cdot)}{G_{D}(x_{0},y^{\prime})}\bigg\|_{L^{1}(D)}\|f\|_{\infty}\leq C|y-y^{\prime}|^{\sigma},

where the constants
\displaystyle C,\sigma
depend only on
\displaystyle d,\alpha,\underline{D},T_{1},T_{2},x_{0},
and
\displaystyle r_{1}
.

We now proceed to
\displaystyle\widetilde{\eta}
. Note that there exist
\displaystyle x_{1}\in D
and
\displaystyle r=r(\underline{D})
such that
\displaystyle B(x_{1},2r)\subset D
. Without loss of generality, we can assume that
\displaystyle|y-y^{\prime}|<r/4
. Then, for any fixed
\displaystyle y,y^{\prime}
, there exists
\displaystyle x_{2}
such that
\displaystyle B(x_{2},r/4)\subset D
and
\displaystyle y,y^{\prime}\notin B(x_{2},r/4)
. This means that
\displaystyle G_{D}(x_{2},y),G_{D}(x_{2},y^{\prime})\leq C
, where
\displaystyle C\geq 1
depends only on
\displaystyle d,\alpha,
and
\displaystyle\underline{D}
. We then split as follows:

\displaystyle\displaystyle\bigg|\frac{p_{t}^{D}(x,y)}{p_{t_{0}}^{D}(x_{0},y)}-\frac{p_{t}^{D}(x,y^{\prime})}{p_{t_{0}}^{D}(x_{0},y^{\prime})}\bigg|

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\bigg|\frac{p_{t}^{D}(x,y)}{G_{D}(x_{2},y)}\frac{G_{D}(x_{2},y)}{p_{t_{0}}^{D}(x_{0},y)}-\frac{p_{t}^{D}(x,y^{\prime})}{G_{D}(x_{2},y^{\prime})}\frac{G_{D}(x_{2},y^{\prime})}{p_{t_{0}}^{D}(x_{0},y^{\prime})}\bigg|

(4.9)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq\frac{p_{t}^{D}(x,y)}{G_{D}(x_{2},y)}\bigg|\frac{G_{D}(x_{2},y)}{p_{t_{0}}^{D}(x_{0},y)}-\frac{G_{D}(x_{2},y^{\prime})}{p_{t_{0}}^{D}(x_{0},y^{\prime})}\bigg|+\frac{G_{D}(x_{2},y^{\prime})}{p_{t_{0}}^{D}(x_{0},y^{\prime})}\bigg|\frac{p_{t}^{D}(x,y^{\prime})}{G_{D}(x_{2},y^{\prime})}-\frac{p_{t}^{D}(x,y)}{G_{D}(x_{2},y)}\bigg|.

By using Lemma
3.2
and (
2.10
), we find that

(4.10)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{p_{t}^{D}(x,y)}{G_{D}(x_{2},y)}\lesssim\frac{\mathbb{P}^{y}(\tau_{D}>t)}{G_{D}(x_{2},y)}=\frac{G_{D}P_{t}^{D}\kappa_{D}(y)}{G_{D}(x_{2},y)}\leq C(d,\alpha,\underline{D},T_{1},T_{2})<\infty.

By similar arguments,

(4.11)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{p_{t}^{D}(x_{0},y)}{G_{D}(x_{2},y)}\geq c(d,\alpha,\underline{D},T_{1},T_{2},x_{0})>0.

From (
4.9
), (
4.10
), (
4.11
), and the Hölder regularity of
\displaystyle\eta^{x_{0}}
obtained above, we arrive at

\displaystyle\displaystyle\bigg|\frac{p_{t}^{D}(x,y)}{p_{t_{0}}^{D}(x_{0},y)}-\frac{p_{t}^{D}(x,y^{\prime})}{p_{t_{0}}^{D}(x_{0},y^{\prime})}\bigg|\leq C|y-y^{\prime}|^{\sigma},

with
\displaystyle C
and
\displaystyle\sigma
depending on
\displaystyle d,\alpha,\underline{D},T_{1},T_{2},x_{0}
.

The arguments for
\displaystyle\eta
are similar to the ones for
\displaystyle\widetilde{\eta}
, with no dependence on
\displaystyle x_{0}
. The proof is complete.
∎

5.
Space-time stable processes and caloric functions

5.1.
Preliminaries

Recall that
\displaystyle(X_{s})_{s\geq 0}
is the isotropic
\displaystyle\alpha
-stable Lévy process. Like for the space-time Brownian motion
[
35
]
, we define the
space-time
\displaystyle\alpha
-stable process
as the following Lévy process on
\displaystyle\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
:

\dot{X}_{s}:=(-s,X_{s}),\quad s\geq 0.

Since
\displaystyle\dot{X}
is a Lévy process, it has the strong Markov property.
Many properties of the space-time process are inherited from the
\displaystyle\alpha
-stable process.
Thus, for a (Borel) set
\displaystyle A\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
, we let

\mathbb{P}^{(t,x)}(\dot{X}_{s}\in A):=\mathbb{P}((t-s,X_{s}+x)\in A),

and for a (Borel) function
\displaystyle f\colon\mathbb{R}^{d+1}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we have

\mathbb{E}^{(t,x)}[f(\dot{X}_{s})]=\mathbb{E}[f(t-s,X_{s}+x)].

It can be easily verified that the transition probability of
\displaystyle\dot{X}
takes on the following form

\widetilde{p}_{s}(t,x,du,dy)=p_{s}(x,y)\,dy\otimes\delta_{\{t-s\}}(du),\quad s\geq 0,\ (t,x),(u,y)\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}.

The corresponding semigroup will be denoted by
\displaystyle\widetilde{P}
.

Lemma 5.1
.

The pointwise generator of the semigroup of the space-time
\displaystyle\alpha
-stable process coincides with the fractional heat operator
\displaystyle\Delta^{\alpha/2}-\partial_{t}
for functions
\displaystyle u\in C^{1,2}_{b}([0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
.

Proof.

Let
\displaystyle u\in C^{1,2}_{b}([0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
. For all
\displaystyle(t,x)\in[0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\displaystyle s\in(0,t)
, we have

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{1}{s}(\widetilde{P}_{s}u(t,x)-u(t,x))

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\frac{1}{s}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{[0,\infty)}(u(r,y)-u(t,x))\widetilde{p}_{s}(t,x,dy,dr)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\frac{1}{s}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(u(t-s,y)-u(t,x))p_{s}(x,y)\,dy

(5.1)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\frac{1}{s}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(u(t-s,y)-u(t-s,x))p_{s}(x,y)\,dy

(5.2)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\,+\frac{1}{s}(u(t-s,x)-u(t,x)).

Clearly, (
5.2
) converges to
\displaystyle-\partial_{t}u(t,x)
as
\displaystyle s\to 0^{+}
, so it suffices to show that (
5.1
) converges to
\displaystyle\Delta^{\alpha/2}_{x}u(t,x)
. To this end, we will prove that

(5.3)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{1}{s}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}((u(t,y)-u(t,x))-(u(t-s,y)-u(t-s,x)))p_{s}(x,y)\,dy

converges to 0 as
\displaystyle s\to 0^{+}
.
Let
\displaystyle\varepsilon>0
and let
\displaystyle\delta>0
be so small that
\displaystyle p_{s}(x,B(x,\delta)^{c})<\varepsilon
. Then we also have
\displaystyle p_{s^{\prime}}(x,B(x,\delta)^{c})<\varepsilon
for
\displaystyle s^{\prime}\in(0,s)
. By Lagrange’s mean value theorem, we get

\bigg|\frac{1}{s}\int_{B(0,\delta)^{c}}((u(t,y)-u(t,x))-(u(t-s,y)-u(t-s,x)))p_{s}(x,y)\,dy\bigg|<2\varepsilon\|u\|_{C^{1,2}}.

By Taylor’s expansion,
\displaystyle u(t-s,x)=u(t,x)-s\partial_{t}u(t,x)+o(s)
as
\displaystyle s\to 0
, and similarly for
\displaystyle y
, so

\displaystyle\displaystyle\bigg|\frac{1}{s}\int_{B(0,\delta)}((u(t,y)-u(t,x))-(u(t-s,y)-u(t-s,x)))p_{s}(x,y)\,dy\bigg|

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\,

\displaystyle\displaystyle\bigg|\int_{B(0,\delta)}(\partial_{t}u(t,x)-\partial_{t}u(t,y)+\frac{o(s)}{s})p_{s}(x,y)\,dy\bigg|

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq\,

\displaystyle\displaystyle\delta\|u\|_{C^{1,2}}+o(1).

This ends the proof.
∎

In the next result we exhibit a space-time Poisson kernel for cylindrical domains. As usual, for arbitrary (open)
\displaystyle G\subseteq\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we let

\tau_{G}:=\inf\{t>0:\dot{X}_{t}\notin G\}.

Lemma 5.2
.

Recall that
\displaystyle D\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
is Lipschitz open set and let
\displaystyle\dot{D}=(r,t)\times D
for some (arbitrary)
\displaystyle-\infty\leq r<t
. Then the distribution of
\displaystyle\dot{X}_{\tau_{\dot{D}}}
—the first exit place of
\displaystyle\dot{X}
from
\displaystyle\dot{D}
—is given by the formula

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathbb{P}^{(t,x)}(\dot{X}_{\tau_{\dot{D}}}\in(ds,dy))=\begin{cases}{\textrm{\bf 1}}_{[r,t)}(s)\,ds\otimes J^{D}(t,x,s,y)\,dy+\delta_{t-r}(ds)\otimes p_{t-s}^{D}(x,y)\,dy,\quad&r>-\infty,\\
\textrm{\bf 1}_{(-\infty,t)}(s)\,ds\otimes J^{D}(t,x,s,y)\,dy,\quad&r=-\infty,\end{cases}

where

J^{D}(t,x,s,y):=\int_{D}p_{t-s}^{D}(x,\xi)\nu(\xi,y)\,d\xi,\quad s<t,\ x\in D,\ y\in D^{c}.

We call
\displaystyle J^{D}
the
lateral Poisson kernel
.

Remark 5.3
.

For the cylinder
\displaystyle\dot{D}=(r,t)\times D
, if the process
\displaystyle\dot{X}
starts at
\displaystyle(t,x)
with some
\displaystyle x\in D
, then it immediately enters
\displaystyle\dot{D}
, so
\displaystyle\tau_{\dot{D}}>0
almost surely, although
\displaystyle(t,x)\notin\dot{D}
. In the language of potential theory, the points on the
top
of the cylinder are
irregular
.

Proof of
Lemma
5.2
.

Let
\displaystyle r>-\infty
. We have

(5.4)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathbb{P}^{(t,x)}(\dot{X}_{\tau_{\dot{D}}}\in(ds,dy))=\

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathbb{P}^{(t,x)}(\dot{X}_{\tau_{\dot{D}}}\in(ds,dy),\tau_{\dot{D}}>\tau_{D})

(5.5)

\displaystyle\displaystyle+\,

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathbb{P}^{(t,x)}(\dot{X}_{\tau_{\dot{D}}}\in(ds,dy),\tau_{\dot{D}}=\tau_{D})

(5.6)

\displaystyle\displaystyle+\,

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathbb{P}^{(t,x)}(\dot{X}_{\tau_{\dot{D}}}\in(ds,dy),\tau_{\dot{D}}<\tau_{D}).

Note that (
5.4
) vanishes, because
\displaystyle\mathbb{P}^{(t,x)}(\tau_{\dot{D}}>\tau_{D})=0
.

By the Ikeda–Watanabe formula (
2.17
), the term (
5.5
) is equal to

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathbb{P}^{(t,x)}(X_{\tau_{D}}\in A,\,\tau_{D}\leq t-r,\,\tau_{D}\in ds)={\textrm{\bf 1}}_{[r,t)}(s)\,ds\otimes J^{D}(t,x,s,y)\,dy.

In (
5.6
) we have
\displaystyle\tau_{D}>\tau_{\dot{D}}=t-r
, so by the definition of the Dirichlet heat kernel, this term is equal to

\displaystyle\displaystyle\delta_{t-r}(ds)\otimes p_{t-r}^{D}(x,y),

see
[
31
, Chapter 2]
.
The case of
\displaystyle r=-\infty
is left to the reader.
∎

We see that
\displaystyle J^{D}(t,x,s,y)
represents the scenario of
\displaystyle\dot{X}
starting at
\displaystyle(t,x)
and leaving to
\displaystyle(s,y)
, where, recall,
\displaystyle x\in D
,
\displaystyle y\in D^{c}
, and
\displaystyle s<t
.
Another way to express the result in Lemma
5.2
, is as follows:

(5.7)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathbb{E}^{(t,x)}u(\dot{X}_{\tau_{\dot{D}}})=\int_{r}^{t}\int_{D^{c}}J^{D}(t,x,s,z)u(s,z)\,dz\,ds+\int_{D}p_{t-r}^{D}(x,y)u(r,y)\,dy,

whenever this integral makes sense, e.g., for nonnegative
\displaystyle u
.
By analogy to the elliptic equations, we call the right-hand side of (
5.7
) the
Poisson integral
, and the first term on the right-hand side of (
5.7
)—the
lateral Poisson integral
.

Remark 5.4
.

Another motivation for calling
\displaystyle J^{D}(t,x,s,z)
the lateral Poisson kernel comes from the fact that it is the
nonlocal normal derivative
of
\displaystyle p_{t-s}^{D}
, whereas
\displaystyle p_{t-s}^{D}
serves as the Green function for the fractional heat equation. Indeed, using the definition of the nonlocal normal derivative from
[
33
]
:

[\partial_{\vec{n}}u](x):=\int_{D}(u(y)-u(x))\nu(x,y)\,dy,\quad x\in D^{c},

we see that for every
\displaystyle z\in D^{c}
,

\partial_{\vec{n}}p_{t-s}^{D}(x,\cdot)(z)=\int_{D}p_{t-s}^{D}(x,y)\nu(y,z)\,dy=J^{D}(t,x,s,z),\quad x\in D.

5.2.
Caloric functions

We define the caloric functions in terms of the mean value property. We stress that we only consider finite nonnegative functions.

Definition 5.5
.

Let
\displaystyle-\infty<T_{1}<T_{2}<\infty
. We say that
\displaystyle u\colon(T_{1},T_{2})\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to[0,\infty)
is
caloric
in
\displaystyle(T_{1},T_{2})\times D
, if the
mean value property
:

(5.8)

u(t,x)=\mathbb{E}^{(t,x)}u(\dot{X}_{\tau_{G}}),\qquad(t,x)\in(T_{1},T_{2})\times D,

holds for every open set
\displaystyle G\subset\subset(T_{1},T_{2})\times D
.

We say that
\displaystyle u\colon[T_{1},T_{2})\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to[0,\infty)
is
caloric in
\displaystyle[T_{1},T_{2})\times D
if (
5.8
) holds for every open
\displaystyle G\subset\subset[T_{1},T_{2})\times D
.
If
\displaystyle u
is caloric in
\displaystyle[T_{1},T_{2})\times D
and satisfies (
5.8
) for
\displaystyle G=(T_{1},T_{2})\times D
, then we say that
\displaystyle u
is
regular caloric
.
If
\displaystyle u
is caloric in
\displaystyle[T_{1},T_{2})\times D
and
\displaystyle u\equiv 0
on the
parabolic boundary

D^{p}:=(\{T_{1}\}\times D)\cup((T_{1},T_{2})\times D^{c}),

then we say that
\displaystyle u
is
singular caloric
.

Remark 5.6
.

(a)

Our caloric functions are just harmonic functions of the space-time isotropic stable Lévy process.

(b)

We may also consider
\displaystyle T_{1}=-\infty
or
\displaystyle T_{2}=\infty
, where appropriate, in particular when defining functions caloric on
\displaystyle(T_{1},T_{2})\times D
.

(c)

The condition
\displaystyle G\subset\subset[T_{1},T_{2})\times D
allows
\displaystyle G

to
touch

\displaystyle\{T_{1}\}\times D
.
Caloricity in
\displaystyle[T_{1},T_{2})\times D
may be considered as a (new) relaxation of regular caloricity,
localized
near the part
\displaystyle\{T_{1}\}\times D
of the boundary of
\displaystyle(T_{1},T_{2})\times D
, see also Lemma
5.7
. Both notions are meant to facilitate discussion of boundary conditions (they generalize to harmonic functions of other strong Markov processes).

(d)

The caloricity in
\displaystyle[T_{1},T_{2})\times D
helps to handle initial conditions which are functions, but also rules out some interesting cases, e.g.,
\displaystyle(t,y)\mapsto p_{t}^{D}(x,y)
. See also
[
22
]
.
Remarkably, every (nonnegative) function caloric in
\displaystyle(T_{1},T_{2})\times D
has a certain initial condition which is a measure, see Section
6
.

(e)

A caloric function need not satisfy the fractional heat equation pointwise, due to lack of time regularity. This can be seen using the counterexample given by Chang-Lara and Dávila
[
28
, Section 2.4.1]
for viscosity solutions. See also Remark
5.12
below.

Lemma 5.7
.

Regular caloricity
implies caloricity in
\displaystyle[T_{1},T_{2})\times D
, which in turn implies caloricity in
\displaystyle(T_{1},T_{2})\times D
.
Furthermore,
(
5.8
) only needs to be verified for
cylinders
\displaystyle G
.

Proof.

Assume that (
5.8
) holds for
\displaystyle G
. By the strong Markov property of
\displaystyle\dot{X}
, (
5.8
) then holds for every open
\displaystyle G^{\prime}\subset G
:

u(t,x)=\mathbb{E}^{(t,x)}u(\dot{X}_{\tau_{G}})=\mathbb{E}^{(t,x)}\mathbb{E}^{\dot{X}_{\tau_{G^{\prime}}}}u(\dot{X}_{\tau_{G}})=\mathbb{E}^{(t,x)}u(\dot{X}_{\tau_{G^{\prime}}}).

This first two assertions follow immediately. To clarify the third one, note that every open
\displaystyle G^{\prime}\subset\subset[T_{1},T_{2})\times D
is contained in an open cylinder, relatively compact in
\displaystyle[T_{1},T_{2})\times D
. Similarly for
\displaystyle(T_{1},T_{2})\times D
. ∎

We continue with several examples of caloric functions.

Example 5.8
.

For every fixed
\displaystyle x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the function
\displaystyle(t,y)\mapsto p_{t}^{D}(x,y)
satisfies the mean value property on every
\displaystyle(\varepsilon,T)\times D
for
\displaystyle 0<\varepsilon<T<\infty
, hence it is caloric on
\displaystyle(0,\infty)\times D
.

Example 5.9
.

If we let

(5.9)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\eta_{t,Q}(x):=0,\quad(t,x)\in(-\infty,0]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\cup(0,\infty)\times D^{c},\ Q\in\partial D,

then for every fixed
\displaystyle Q\in\partial D
, the function
\displaystyle(t,x)\mapsto\eta_{t,Q}(x)
is caloric in
\displaystyle(-\infty,\infty)\times D
. Indeed, the mean value property in
\displaystyle(\varepsilon,T)\times D
, with
\displaystyle 0<\varepsilon<T<\infty
is a consequence of (
1.8
). Then, by Lemma
3.5
,

\displaystyle\displaystyle\eta_{t,Q}(x)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}\int_{U^{c}}J^{D}(t,x,s,z)\eta_{s,Q}(z)\,dz\,ds

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\int_{-R}^{t}\int_{U^{c}}J^{D}(t,x,s,z)\eta_{s,Q}(z)\,dz\,ds,

for any
\displaystyle R\geq 0
.

Example 5.10
.

If
\displaystyle f\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\to[0,\infty)
is a nonnegative measurable function and
\displaystyle P_{1}^{D}f(x)
is finite for all
\displaystyle x\in D
, then
\displaystyle(t,x)\mapsto P_{t}^{D}f(x)
is caloric in
\displaystyle[0,\infty)\times D
, with the usual convention
\displaystyle P_{0}^{D}f:=f
.

The following class of functions is of particular interest for us. We will show in the next section that it coincides with the class of all singular caloric functions.

Lemma 5.11
.

If
\displaystyle\mu(dQ\,ds)
is a locally finite nonnegative Borel measure on
\displaystyle\partial D\times[0,\infty)
, then

(5.10)

\displaystyle\displaystyle h(t,x):=\begin{cases}\int_{[0,t)}\int_{\partial D}\eta_{t-\tau,Q}(x)\mu(dQ\,d\tau),\quad&t>0,\ x\in D,\\
0,\quad&\mbox{ elsewhere},\end{cases}

is singular caloric in
\displaystyle[0,\infty)\times D
.

Proof.

By Lemma
3.5
,
\displaystyle h
is finite for all
\displaystyle t>0
and
\displaystyle x\in D
, and by (
5.9
), we have

\displaystyle\displaystyle\int_{[0,t)}\int_{\partial D}\eta_{t-\tau,Q}(x)\mu(dQ\,d\tau)=\int_{[0,\infty)}\int_{\partial D}\eta_{t-\tau,Q}(x)\mu(dQ\,d\tau),\quad t\geq 0,\ x\in D.

Therefore, the mean value property for
\displaystyle h
follows from Fubini–Tonelli and caloricity of
\displaystyle\eta
.
∎

Remark 5.12
.

We note that the viscosity solution considered in
[
28
, Section 2.4.1]
, although non-differentiable, is Lipschitz in time. The function
\displaystyle n_{t,Q}
is not even Lipschitz in
\displaystyle t
because for
\displaystyle t\in(0,1)
and fixed
\displaystyle x\in D
,

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{\eta_{t,Q}(x)}{t}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\frac{1}{t}\lim\limits_{y\to Q}\frac{p_{t}^{D}(x,y)}{\mathbb{P}^{y}(\tau_{D}>t)}\frac{\mathbb{P}^{y}(\tau_{D}>t)}{\mathbb{P}^{y}(\tau_{D}>1)}\gtrsim\frac{p_{t}(x,Q)}{t}\lim_{y\to Q}\frac{\mathbb{P}^{y}(\tau_{D}>t)}{\mathbb{P}^{y}(\tau_{D}>1)}\gtrsim|x-Q|^{-d-\alpha}\lim_{y\to Q}\frac{\mathbb{P}^{y}(\tau_{D}>t)}{\mathbb{P}^{y}(\tau_{D}>1)}.

We see, indeed, that the last limit is comparable to
\displaystyle t^{-1/2}
if
\displaystyle D
is
\displaystyle C^{1,1}
by (
2.11
). Furthermore, for Lipschitz
\displaystyle D
it also explodes as
\displaystyle t\to 0^{+}
because of the proof of Lemma
B.2
and
[
11
, Lemma 3]
.

Lemma 5.13
.

If
\displaystyle u
is caloric in
\displaystyle(T_{1},T_{2})\times D
for some
\displaystyle T_{1}<T_{2}
, then
\displaystyle u\in L^{1}_{\rm loc}((T_{1},T_{2})\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
.

Proof.

The proof is similar to the one of
[
17
, Lemma 4.5]
. First note that for any fixed
\displaystyle x\in D
,
\displaystyle r>0
, and
\displaystyle B=B(x,r)
, by (
2.10
) we have

\displaystyle\displaystyle J^{B}(t,x,s,z)=\int_{B}p_{t-s}^{B}(x,y)\nu(y,z)\,dy

\displaystyle\displaystyle\approx\int_{B}p_{t-s}(x,y)\mathbb{P}^{y}(\tau_{B}>t-s)\nu(y,z)\,dy

\displaystyle\displaystyle\geq c\int_{B(x,r/2)}p_{t-s}(x,y)\,dy\geq C>0,

with
\displaystyle C
depending only on
\displaystyle r
and
\displaystyle R
, where
\displaystyle\delta_{B}(z),t-s\leq R
. Thus,
\displaystyle J^{B}(t,x,\cdot,\cdot)
is locally bounded from below. Now, take two disjoint balls
\displaystyle B_{1},B_{2}\subseteq D
, centered at some points
\displaystyle x_{1},x_{2}\in D
respectively, and let
\displaystyle T_{1}<t_{0}<t<T_{2}
and
\displaystyle R>0
. Since
\displaystyle u
is nonnegative and caloric, for
\displaystyle i=1,2
we get

\displaystyle\displaystyle\infty>u(t,x)\geq\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\int_{B_{i}^{c}}u(s,z)J^{B_{i}}(t,x,s,z)\,dz\,ds\geq C\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\int_{B(0,R)\setminus B_{i}}u(s,z)\,dz\,ds.

Therefore
\displaystyle u\in L^{1}((t_{0},t)\times(B(0,R)\setminus B_{i}))
for
\displaystyle i=1,2
. But
\displaystyle B_{1}\cap B_{2}=\emptyset
, so
\displaystyle u\in L^{1}((t_{0},t)\times B(0,R))
. Since
\displaystyle R
can be chosen arbitrarily large, the proof is complete.

∎

The following result shows that the so-called ancient solutions, i.e., functions caloric in a time interval of the form
\displaystyle(-\infty,T)
, can be conveniently studied by considering only the lateral Poisson integrals.

Lemma 5.14
.

If
\displaystyle u
is caloric in
\displaystyle(-\infty,T)\times D
for some
\displaystyle T\in\mathbb{R}
, then for all
\displaystyle x\in U\subset\subset D
and
\displaystyle t<T
we have

(5.11)

u(t,x)=\mathbb{E}^{(t,x)}[u(\tau_{(-\infty,t)\times U},X_{\tau_{(-\infty,t)\times U}})]=\int_{-\infty}^{t}\int_{U^{c}}J^{U}(t,x,s,z)u(s,z)\,dz\,ds.

In particular, the integral on the right-hand side of (
5.11
) is finite.

Proof.

Let
\displaystyle t,x,U
be as in the statement. By the definition of caloricity, for
\displaystyle v<t
we have

\displaystyle\displaystyle u(t,x)=\int_{v}^{t}\int_{U^{c}}J^{U}(t,x,s,z)u(s,z)\,dz\,ds+\int_{U}p_{t-v}^{U}(x,y)u(v,y)\,dy.

The first integral on the right-hand side increases to the right-hand side of (
5.11
) by the monotone convergence theorem and the second integral decreases. It suffices to prove that

(5.12)

a:=\lim\limits_{v\to-\infty}\int_{U}p_{t-v}^{U}(x,y)u(v,y)\,dy=0.

To this end note that
for every
\displaystyle v<t
,

(5.13)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\int_{U}p_{t-v}^{U}(x,y)u(v,y)\,dy\geq a.

Let
\displaystyle n>0
be so large that
\displaystyle U\subset\subset D_{n}
(see (
2.1
)). Recall that
\displaystyle\lambda_{1}(V)
is the first eigenvalue of the Dirichlet fractional Laplacian for an open set
\displaystyle V
. We claim that

(5.14)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\lambda_{1}(D_{n})<\lambda_{1}(U).

A weak inequality is well known as the domain monotonicity. In order to prove the strict inequality, assume without loss of generality that
\displaystyle 0\in U
. Then there exists
\displaystyle q>1
such that
\displaystyle qU\subset\subset D_{n}
, so, by domain monotonicity,
\displaystyle\lambda_{1}(D_{n})\leq\lambda_{1}(qU)=q^{-\alpha}\lambda_{1}(U)
, which yields
(
5.14
).

By (
2.13
), (
2.12
), and the fact that each eigenfunction is bounded from above and bounded from below away from the boundary, for
\displaystyle s<t
,
\displaystyle s\to-\infty
, we get

\displaystyle\displaystyle\infty>u(t,x)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\geq\int_{D_{n}}u(s,y)p_{t-s}^{D_{n}}(x,y)\,dy\geq\int_{U}u(s,y)p_{t-s}^{D_{n}}(x,y)\,dy\approx\int_{U}u(s,y)e^{-\lambda_{1}(D_{n})(t-s)}\,dy

\displaystyle\displaystyle=e^{(-\lambda_{1}(D_{n})+\lambda_{1}(U))(t-s)}\int_{U}u(s,y)e^{-\lambda_{1}(U)(t-s)}\,dy

\displaystyle\displaystyle\gtrsim e^{(-\lambda_{1}(D_{n})+\lambda_{1}(U))(t-s)}\int_{U}u(s,y)p_{t-s}^{U}(x,y)\,dy.

By (
5.14
), we must have
\displaystyle a=0
.
∎

5.3.
Caloric functions are continuous

This subsection is devoted to proving that caloric functions are continuous, hence locally bounded.
The proof is based on certain estimates for the kernel
\displaystyle J^{D}
, which may be of independent interest. Let us note in passing that
bounded
caloric functions are known to be locally Hölder continuous
[
30

.

Proposition 5.15
.

Assume that
\displaystyle u
is a nonnegative caloric function in
\displaystyle(T_{0},T_{1})\times D
for some
\displaystyle T_{0}<T_{1}

\displaystyle u

locally bounded therein.

We fix arbitrary
\displaystyle(t_{0},x_{0})\in(T_{0},T_{1})\times D

\displaystyle r\in(0,\delta_{D}(x_{0})/2)

\displaystyle B_{\rho}=B(x_{0},\rho)

\displaystyle\rho>0

\displaystyle A,B\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

d(A,B):=\inf\{|b-a|:\;a\in A,b\in B\}.

Lemma 5.16
.

Let
\displaystyle D

\displaystyle U\subset\subset D
, and
\displaystyle 0<T<\infty

\displaystyle\displaystyle J^{D}(t,x,s,z)\approx J^{D}(t,x_{0},s,z),\quad x\in U,\ z\in D^{c},\ 0<t-s<T,

(5.16)

with the comparability constants depending only on
\displaystyle d,\alpha,\underline{D},d(U,D^{c})

Let
\displaystyle U^{\prime}

\displaystyle U\subset\subset U^{\prime}\subset\subset D

\displaystyle U^{\prime}
so that the constants below depend only on
\displaystyle D

\displaystyle U

\displaystyle\displaystyle J^{D}(t,x,s,z)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\int_{D}p_{t-s}^{D}(x,y)\nu(y,z)\,dy

\displaystyle\displaystyle\approx\mathbb{P}^{x}(\tau_{D}>t-s)\int_{D}p_{t-s}(x,y)\mathbb{P}^{y}(\tau_{D}>t-s)\nu(y,z)\,dy

\displaystyle d,\alpha,\underline{D},d(U,D^{c})

\displaystyle T

\displaystyle|x-y|\approx|x_{0}-y|

\displaystyle\displaystyle\int_{D\setminus U^{\prime}}p_{t-s}(x,y)\mathbb{P}^{y}(\tau_{D}>t-s)\nu(y,z)\,dy\approx\int_{D\setminus U^{\prime}}p_{t-s}(x_{0},y)\mathbb{P}^{y}(\tau_{D}>t-s)\nu(y,z)\,dy.

For

and

\displaystyle\displaystyle\int_{U^{\prime}}p_{t-s}(x,y)\mathbb{P}^{y}(\tau_{D}>t-s)\nu(y,z)\,dy

5.16

\displaystyle\displaystyle J^{D}(t,x,s,z)=\bigg(\int_{U^{\prime}}+\int_{D\setminus U^{\prime}}\bigg)p_{t-s}^{D}(x,y)\nu(y,z)\,dy.

By Lemma

,

\displaystyle\displaystyle\int_{D\setminus U^{\prime}}p_{t-s}^{D}(x,y)\nu(y,z)\,dy\lesssim\int_{D\setminus U^{\prime}}p_{t^{\prime}-s}^{D}(x,y)\nu(y,z)\,dy

For the integral over
\displaystyle U^{\prime}
we use:(
2.10
)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\int_{U^{\prime}}p_{t-s}^{D}(x,y)\nu(y,z)\,dy\approx\nu(x_{0},z)\int_{U^{\prime}}p_{t-s}^{D}(x,y)\,dy\approx\nu(x_{0},z)\int_{U^{\prime}}p_{t-s}(x,y)\mathbb{P}^{x}(\tau_{D}>t-s)\mathbb{P}^{y}(\tau_{D}>t-s)\,dy.

For
\displaystyle w\in U^{\prime}
and

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}^{w}(\tau_{D}>t-s)\approx 1
and by (

\displaystyle\int_{U^{\prime}}p_{t-s}(x,y)\,dy\approx 1
, with comparability constants depending only on

\displaystyle\displaystyle\nu(x_{0},z)\int_{U^{\prime}}p_{t-s}(x,y)\mathbb{P}^{x}(\tau_{D}>t-s)\mathbb{P}^{y}(\tau_{D}>t-s)\,dy

\displaystyle\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle\displaystyle\,\nu(x_{0},z)\int_{U^{\prime}}p_{t^{\prime}-s}(x,y)\mathbb{P}^{x}(\tau_{D}>t^{\prime}-s)\mathbb{P}^{y}(\tau_{D}>t^{\prime}-s)\,dy

Proof of Proposition
5.15
.

We will show continuity at the fixed point
\displaystyle(t_{0},x_{0})
. Let
\displaystyle x\in B_{r/2}
,
\displaystyle t_{1}\in(T_{0},t_{0})
and
\displaystyle t\in(t_{1},T_{1})
, so that
\displaystyle T_{1}<t_{1}<t<T_{0}
. We have

\displaystyle\displaystyle u(t,x)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\int_{B_{r}}u(t_{1},y)p_{t-t_{1}}^{B_{r}}(x,y)\,dy+\int_{t_{1}}^{t}\int_{B_{r}}u(\tau,z)J^{B_{r}}(t,x,\tau,z)\,dz\,d\tau,

\displaystyle\displaystyle u(t_{0},x_{0})

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\int_{B_{r}}u(t_{1},y)p_{t_{0}-t_{1}}^{B_{r}}(x,y)\,dy+\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{0}}\int_{B_{r}}u(\tau,z)J^{B_{r}}(t_{0},x,\tau,z)\,dz\,d\tau.

Since

\displaystyle(t,x)
sufficiently close to

\displaystyle p_{t-t_{1}}^{B_{r}}(x,y)\approx p_{t_{0}-t_{1}}^{B_{r}}(x_{0},y)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\int_{B_{r}}u(t_{1},y)p_{t-t_{1}}^{B_{r}}(x,y)\,dy\mathop{\longrightarrow}\limits_{(t,x)\to(t_{0},x_{0})}\int_{B_{r}}u(t_{1},y)p_{t_{0}-t_{1}}^{B_{r}}(x_{0},y)\,dy.

Therefore it remains to show that

(5.18)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\int_{t_{1}}^{t}\int_{B_{r}}u(\tau,z)J^{B_{r}}(t,x,\tau,z)\,dz\,d\tau\mathop{\longrightarrow}\limits_{(t,x)\to(t_{0},x_{0})}\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{0}}\int_{B_{r}}u(\tau,z)J^{B_{r}}(t_{0},x_{0},\tau,z)\,dz\,d\tau.

Assume that
\displaystyle t>t_{0}
(we skip the other case, as it is similar). Then,

\displaystyle\displaystyle\bigg|\int_{t_{1}}^{t}\int_{B_{r}}u(\tau,z)J^{B_{r}}(t,x,\tau,z)\,dz\,d\tau-\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{0}}\int_{B_{r}}u(\tau,z)J^{B_{r}}(t_{0},x_{0},\tau,z)\,dz\,d\tau\bigg|

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\displaystyle\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{0}}\int_{B_{r}}u(\tau,z)|J^{B_{r}}(t,x,\tau,z)-J^{B_{r}}(t_{0},x_{0},\tau,z)|\,dz\,d\tau+\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\int_{B_{r}}u(\tau,z)J^{B_{r}}(t,x,\tau,z)\,dz\,d\tau=:I_{1}+I_{2}.

\displaystyle J^{B_{r}}(t,x,\tau,z)\lesssim J^{B_{r}}(t_{0}+\varepsilon,x_{0},\tau,z)

6.
Representation of caloric functions in Lipschitz open sets

We first discuss the representation for functions caloric on
\displaystyle[0,T)\times D
, where the meaning of the initial condition is clearer. We then use this case to resolve the situation of functions caloric in
\displaystyle(0,T)\times D
.

6.1.
Functions caloric up to time
\displaystyle 0

Lemma 6.1
.

Assume that
\displaystyle u
is a nonnegative caloric function in
\displaystyle\dot{D}:=[0,T)\times D
. Then there exists a unique decomposition
\displaystyle u=r+s
, where
\displaystyle r
is regular caloric in
\displaystyle\dot{D}
and
\displaystyle s
in singular caloric in
\displaystyle\dot{D}
.

Proof.

Let
\displaystyle t<T
. Since
\displaystyle u
has the mean value property in every
\displaystyle\dot{D}_{n}=(0,t)\times D_{n}
(see (
2.1
)), we have

\displaystyle\displaystyle u(t,x)=\mathbb{E}^{(t,x)}u(\dot{X}_{\tau_{\dot{D}_{n}}})=:i_{n}(t,x)+l_{n}(t,x)+s_{n}(t,x),

where

\displaystyle\displaystyle i_{n}(t,x)=\mathbb{E}^{(t,x)}[u(\dot{X}_{\tau_{\dot{D}_{n}}})\,;\,\tau_{D_{n}}>t],

\displaystyle\displaystyle l_{n}(t,x)=\mathbb{E}^{(t,x)}[u(\dot{X}_{\tau_{\dot{D}_{n}}})\,;\,\tau_{D_{n}}<t,\,\tau_{D_{n}}=\tau_{D}],

\displaystyle\displaystyle s_{n}(t,x)=\mathbb{E}^{(t,x)}[u(\dot{X}_{\tau_{\dot{D}_{n}}})\,;\,\tau_{D_{n}}<t,\,\tau_{D_{n}}<\tau_{D}].

We let
\displaystyle n\to\infty
. By the monotone convergence, we get

i_{n}(t,x)=\mathbb{E}^{(t,x)}[u(\dot{X}_{t})\,;\,\tau_{D_{n}}>t]\nearrow\mathbb{E}^{(t,x)}[u(\dot{X}_{t})\,;\,\tau_{D}>t]=:i(t,x),

and by
[
11
, (5.40)]
,

l_{n}(t,x)=\mathbb{E}^{(t,x)}[u(\dot{X}_{\tau_{\dot{D}}})\,;\,\tau_{D}<t,\,\tau_{D_{n}}=\tau_{D}]\nearrow\mathbb{E}^{(t,x)}[u(\dot{X}_{\tau_{\dot{D}}})\,;\,\tau_{D}<t]=:l(t,x),

the limits being finite because all
\displaystyle i_{n}
,
\displaystyle l_{n}
, and
\displaystyle s_{n}
are nonnegative. So,
\displaystyle s_{n}(t,x)
converges to some
\displaystyle s(t,x)
.
Since
\displaystyle r(t,x):=i(t,x)+l(t,x)=\mathbb{E}^{(t,x)}u(\dot{X}_{\tau_{\dot{D}}})
,
\displaystyle r
is regular caloric. By inspecting the definition of
\displaystyle s_{n}
, we find that
\displaystyle s
is singular caloric: indeed, if
\displaystyle X_{t}
starts from
\displaystyle x\in D^{c}
, then the event
\displaystyle\tau_{D_{n}}<\tau_{D}
has probability
\displaystyle 0
, so
\displaystyle s_{n}(t,x)=0
for
\displaystyle x\in D^{c}
, and if
\displaystyle\dot{X}
starts from
\displaystyle(0,x)
,
\displaystyle x\in D
, then
\displaystyle s_{n}(0,x)=0
because
\displaystyle\tau_{D_{n}}\geq 0
.

Assume that there is another decomposition
\displaystyle u=r^{\prime}+s^{\prime}
. Since
\displaystyle s^{\prime}=s=0
on
\displaystyle D^{p}
, we have that
\displaystyle r-r^{\prime}=0
on
\displaystyle D^{p}
as well and therefore
\displaystyle r-r^{\prime}=0
in
\displaystyle\dot{D}
, because
\displaystyle r-r^{\prime}
is regular caloric on
\displaystyle\dot{D}
.
∎

We next give an integral representation for the singular caloric part, with the use of the parabolic Martin kernel. We first prove the following technical result.

Lemma 6.2
.

Let
\displaystyle x\in D
and
\displaystyle 0<\varepsilon<T
be fixed. Then there exists a modulus of continuity
\displaystyle\omega
, independent of
\displaystyle y
and
\displaystyle t\in[\varepsilon,T]
, such that for
\displaystyle n
large we have

(6.1)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\bigg|\frac{p_{t}^{D_{n}}(x,y)}{\mathbb{P}^{y}(\tau_{D_{n}}>1)}-\frac{p_{t}^{D}(x,y)}{\mathbb{P}^{y}(\tau_{D}>1)}\bigg|\leq\omega\bigg(\frac{1}{n}\bigg),\quad y\in D_{n},\ t\in[\varepsilon,T].

Proof.

First note that the expression on the right-hand side of (
6.1
) converges to 0 as
\displaystyle n\to\infty
for every fixed
\displaystyle y\in D
(the expression is considered only when
\displaystyle 1/n<\delta_{D}(y)
). In order to get (
6.1
) we will show that the convergence in uniform by using the Arzelà–Ascoli theorem. Indeed, by Theorem
1.2
, we find that
\displaystyle\overline{D_{n}}\ni y\mapsto p_{t}^{D_{n}}(x,y)/\mathbb{P}^{y}(\tau_{D_{n}}>1)
are uniformly Hölder continuous for
\displaystyle n
large and
\displaystyle t\in[\varepsilon,T]
. Furthermore, it is well-known that a Hölder continuous function in
\displaystyle\overline{D_{n}}
can be extended to a function on
\displaystyle\overline{D}
with the same Hölder regularity, see, e.g., Banach
[
4

\displaystyle f_{n}
then by the Arzelà–Ascoli theorem, we find that

\displaystyle\displaystyle\bigg|f_{n}(t,y)-\frac{p_{t}^{D}(x,y)}{\mathbb{P}^{y}(\tau_{D}>1)}\bigg|\leq\omega\bigg(\frac{1}{n}\bigg),\quad y\in D,\ t\in[\varepsilon,T].

In particular, (
6.1
) follows.
∎

Theorem 6.3
.

\displaystyle u

\displaystyle[0,T)\times D

\displaystyle\mu

\displaystyle D_{n}

Theorem 6.4
.

The measure
\displaystyle\mu

Proof.

Following
[

22

\displaystyle\delta_{Q}\otimes\delta_{0}

\displaystyle t>0
,
\displaystyle Q\in\partial D

\displaystyle u

Functions caloric on
\displaystyle(0,T)\times D

Theorem 6.5
.

Assume that
\displaystyle u
is caloric on
\displaystyle(0,T)\times D
and let
\displaystyle g=u|_{D^{c}}
. Then there exist unique bounded nonnegative measures
\displaystyle\mu
on
\displaystyle[0,T)\times\partial D
and
\displaystyle\mu_{0}
on
\displaystyle D
such that for all
\displaystyle 0<t<T
and
\displaystyle x\in D
,

\displaystyle\displaystyle u(t,x)=P_{t}^{D}\mu_{0}(x)+\int_{[0,t)}\int_{\partial D}\eta_{t-s,Q}(x)\,\mu(dQ\,ds)+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{D^{c}}g(s,z)J^{D}(t,x,s,z)\,dz\,ds.

Proof.

By the results of the previous subsection, there is a nonnegative measure
\displaystyle\mu
on
\displaystyle(0,T)\times\partial D
such that for all
\displaystyle 0<\varepsilon<t<T
and
\displaystyle x\in D

\displaystyle p_{t}^{D}(x,\cdot)\approx 1

\displaystyle U\subset\subset D

\displaystyle u(\varepsilon,\cdot)

\displaystyle U

\displaystyle(\varepsilon_{n})
such that
\displaystyle u(\varepsilon_{n},\cdot)

\displaystyle D

\displaystyle\mu_{0}

\displaystyle D

\displaystyle\displaystyle P_{t-\varepsilon}^{D}u(\varepsilon,\cdot)(x)=\int_{D}p_{t-\varepsilon}^{D}(x,y)u(\varepsilon,y)\,dy=\int_{D}\frac{p_{t-\varepsilon}^{D}(x,y)}{\mathbb{P}^{y}(\tau_{D}>1)}\mathbb{P}^{y}(\tau_{D}>1)u(\varepsilon,y)\,dy.

\displaystyle\frac{p_{t-\varepsilon}^{D}(x,y)}{\mathbb{P}^{y}(\tau_{D}>1)}\approx\frac{p_{t}^{D}(x,y)}{\mathbb{P}^{y}(\tau_{D}>1)}\approx 1

\displaystyle\displaystyle\int_{D}p_{t}^{D}(x,y)\,\mu_{0}(dy)+\int_{\partial{D}}\eta_{t,Q}(x)\,\widetilde{\mu}(dQ).

\displaystyle\mu

\displaystyle\mu\textbf{1}_{(0,T)\times\partial D}+\delta_{0}(dt)\otimes\widetilde{\mu}

∎

Appendix A
Weak and classical formulations for caloric functions

The following result seems to be well-known, but we were unable to locate a proof. The arguments are very similar to the case of the Laplacian discussed by Hunt
[
45
]
.

Lemma A.1
.

For any
\displaystyle x\in D
the function
\displaystyle(t,y)\mapsto p_{t}^{D}(x,y)
is a classical solution to the fractional heat equation with the Dirichlet condition:

(A.1)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\begin{cases}(\partial_{t}-\Delta^{\alpha/2}_{y})p_{t}^{D}(x,y)=0\quad&t>0,\ y\in D,\\
p_{t}^{D}(x,y)=0\quad&t>0,\ y\in D^{c}.\end{cases}

It is also a weak solution in the sense that for
\displaystyle\phi\in C_{c}^{\infty}([0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
\displaystyle 0<t_{1}<t_{2}<\infty
we have

\displaystyle\displaystyle\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\int_{D}(\partial_{t}+\Delta^{\alpha/2})\phi(t,y)p_{t}^{D}(x,y)\,dy\,dt=\int_{D}\phi(t_{2},y)p_{t_{2}}^{D}(x,y)\,dy-\int_{D}\phi(t_{1},y)p_{t_{1}}^{D}(x,y)\,dy.

Proof.

By definition, the exterior condition is satisfied, so it suffices to verify that
\displaystyle(\partial_{t}-\Delta^{\alpha/2})p_{t}^{D}(x,y)=0
. To this end we will differentiate the Hunt formula. Using the subordination and Fourier inversion formulas (see, e.g., Bogdan and Jakubowski
[
21
, Lemma 5]
) it is easy to see that

\displaystyle p_{t}
is smooth in
\displaystyle x
for
\displaystyle t>0
and
\displaystyle\partial^{\beta}_{y}p_{t}(x,y)
is bounded whenever
\displaystyle|x-y|
is separated from 0 for any
\displaystyle\beta\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
. Note that this is the case for
\displaystyle|X_{\tau_{D}}-y|
. Therefore, for fixed
\displaystyle(t,y)
, by the dominated convergence theorem we find

\displaystyle\displaystyle\partial^{\beta}_{y}p_{t}^{D}(x,y)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\partial^{\beta}_{y}p_{t}(x,y)-\partial^{\beta}_{y}\mathbb{E}^{x}[p_{t-\tau_{D}}(X_{\tau_{D}},y)\,;\,\tau_{D}<t]

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\partial^{\beta}_{y}p_{t}(x,y)-\mathbb{E}^{x}[\partial^{\beta}_{y}p_{t-\tau_{D}}(X_{\tau_{D}},y)\,;\,\tau_{D}<t].

Furthermore,

(A.2)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\|p_{t}^{D}(x,\cdot)\|_{C^{2}(B(y,\delta_{D}(y)/2))}<\infty,

hence
\displaystyle\Delta^{\alpha/2}_{y}p_{t}^{D}(x,y)
is well defined for
\displaystyle(t,y)\in D\times(0,\infty)
and we have

\displaystyle\displaystyle\Delta^{\alpha/2}_{y}p_{t}^{D}(x,y)=\Delta^{\alpha/2}_{y}p_{t}(x,y)-\Delta^{\alpha/2}_{y}\mathbb{E}^{x}[p_{t-\tau_{D}}(X_{\tau_{D}},y)\,;\,\tau_{D}<t].

We can also interchange
\displaystyle\Delta^{\alpha/2}_{y}
with the expectation. The easiest way to see that is by using Fubini–Tonelli, (
A.2
) and the Taylor expansion in the following (symmetrized) representation of the fractional Laplacian:

\displaystyle\displaystyle\Delta^{\alpha/2}_{y}u(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(u(x+y)-2u(x)+u(x-y))\nu(y)\,dy.

Thus, we obtain

\displaystyle\displaystyle\Delta^{\alpha/2}_{y}p_{t}^{D}(x,y)=\Delta^{\alpha/2}_{y}p_{t}(x,y)-\mathbb{E}^{x}[\Delta^{\alpha/2}_{y}p_{t-\tau_{D}}(X_{\tau_{D}},y)\,;\,\tau_{D}<t].

We now compute the time derivative. Note that
\displaystyle\partial_{t}p_{t}(x,y)
exists and is equal to
\displaystyle\Delta^{\alpha/2}_{y}p_{t}(x,y)
, so it is bounded for
\displaystyle|x-y|
separated from 0. We have

\displaystyle\displaystyle\partial_{t}p_{t}^{D}(x,y)=\partial_{t}p_{t}(x,y)-\partial_{t}\mathbb{E}^{x}[p_{t-\tau_{D}}(X_{\tau_{D}},y)\,;\,\tau_{D}<t],

provided the last expression exists, which we now prove. Without loss of generality, let
\displaystyle h>0
. We have

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{1}{h}\big[\mathbb{E}^{x}[p_{t+h-\tau_{D}}(X_{\tau_{D}},y)\,;\,\tau_{D}<t+h]-\mathbb{E}^{x}[p_{t-\tau_{D}}(X_{\tau_{D}},y)\,;\,\tau_{D}<t]\big]

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{1}{h}\mathbb{E}^{x}[p_{t+h-\tau_{D}}(X_{\tau_{D}},y)-p_{t-\tau_{D}}(X_{\tau_{D}},y)\,;\,\tau_{D}<t]+\frac{1}{h}\mathbb{E}^{x}[p_{t+h-\tau_{D}}(X_{\tau_{D}},y)\,;\,t\leq\tau_{D}<t+h].

By the dominated convergence theorem, we get that

\displaystyle\displaystyle\lim\limits_{h\to 0^{+}}\frac{1}{h}\mathbb{E}^{x}[p_{t+h-\tau_{D}}(X_{\tau_{D}},y)-p_{t-\tau_{D}}(X_{\tau_{D}},y)\,;\,\tau_{D}<t]=\mathbb{E}^{x}[\partial_{t}p_{t-\tau_{D}}(X_{\tau_{D}},y)\,;\,\tau_{D}<t].

Furthermore, by (
2.7
),

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{1}{h}\mathbb{E}^{x}[p_{t+h-\tau_{D}}(X_{\tau_{D}},y)\,;\,t\leq\tau_{D}<t+h]\leq C\mathbb{P}^{x}(\tau_{D}\in[t,t+h)),

and the last expression converges to
\displaystyle 0
by the dominated convergence theorem. Therefore we get

\displaystyle\displaystyle\partial_{t}p_{t}^{D}(x,y)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\partial_{t}p_{t}(x,y)-\mathbb{E}^{x}[\partial_{t}p_{t-\tau_{D}}(X_{\tau_{D}},y)\,;\,\tau_{D}<t]

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\Delta^{\alpha/2}_{y}p_{t}(x,y)-\mathbb{E}^{x}[\Delta^{\alpha/2}_{y}p_{t-\tau_{D}}(X_{\tau_{D}},y)\,;\,\tau_{D}<t]=\Delta^{\alpha/2}_{y}p_{t}^{D}(x,y),

so
\displaystyle p_{t}^{D}
is a classical solution to the fractional heat equation (
A.1
). It is also a weak solution, as follows from integration by parts and the fact that the support of the test function
\displaystyle\phi
is separated from
\displaystyle\partial D
.
∎

Appendix B
Almost-increasingness

The following result is used in the proof of Lemma
5.16
.

Lemma B.1
.

For open
\displaystyle U\subset\subset U^{\prime}\subset\subset D
and
\displaystyle T>0
, there is
\displaystyle C=C(d,\alpha,\underline{D},U,d(U,(U^{\prime})^{c}),T)
such that

\displaystyle\displaystyle p_{s}^{D}(x,y)\leq Cp_{t}^{D}(x,y),\quad x\in U,\ y\in D\setminus U^{\prime},\ 0<s<t<T.

The proof (given below) relies on approximate factorization of
\displaystyle p_{t}^{D}
and the next lemma on the survival probability
\displaystyle\mathbb{P}^{y}(\tau_{D}>s)
.
Of course, the latter is nonincreasing in
\displaystyle s\in(0,\infty)
. The following
relative
upper bound is a partial converse and may be independent interest.

Lemma B.2
.

Let
\displaystyle T>0
. There exists
\displaystyle C=C(d,\alpha,\underline{D},T)
and
\displaystyle\sigma\in(0,1)
such that

(B.1)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{\mathbb{P}^{y}(\tau_{D}>s)}{\mathbb{P}^{y}(\tau_{D}>t)}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq C\bigg(\frac{s}{t}\bigg)^{-\sigma},\quad y\in D,\ 0<s<t<T.

Proof.

By
[
18
, Remark 3]
and scaling,
\displaystyle\mathbb{P}^{y}(\tau_{D}>t_{1})\approx\mathbb{P}^{y}(\tau_{D}>t_{2})
, uniformly in
\displaystyle y\in D
and
\displaystyle t_{1},t_{2}
in each compact subset of
\displaystyle(0,\infty)
.
Therefore we may assume that
\displaystyle s
and
\displaystyle t
in (
B.1
) are small. Then we can also assume that
\displaystyle y
is close to the boundary, otherwise the terms on the left-hand side of (
B.1
) are bounded from below by the survival probability of a sufficiently small ball (and above by
\displaystyle 1
).
In this setting, recalling the notation of Section
2.1
, by
[
18
, Theorem 2]
and
[
47
, Lemma 17]
we get

(B.2)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{\mathbb{P}^{y}(\tau_{D}>s)}{\mathbb{P}^{y}(\tau_{D}>t)}\approx\frac{\mathbb{E}^{A_{t^{1/\alpha}}(y)}\tau_{D}}{\mathbb{E}^{A_{s^{1/\alpha}}(y)}\tau_{D}}\approx\frac{\Phi(A_{t^{1/\alpha}}(y))}{\Phi(A_{s^{1/\alpha}}(y))},

uniformly for the considered point
\displaystyle y
and times
\displaystyle s,t
.
Let
\displaystyle Q\in\partial D
be closest to
\displaystyle y
.
To estimate the rightmost ratio in (
B.2
), we consider three geometric situations:

Case 1.
If
\displaystyle y\in\mathcal{A}_{t^{1/\alpha}}(y)\cap\mathcal{A}_{s^{1/\alpha}}(y)
, then we can take
\displaystyle A_{t^{1/\alpha}}(y)=A_{s^{1/\alpha}}(y)=y
, proving (
B.1
)

Case 2.
If
\displaystyle y\in\mathcal{A}_{s^{1/\alpha}}(y)
, but
\displaystyle y\notin\mathcal{A}_{t^{1/\alpha}}(y)
, then
\displaystyle\kappa s^{1/\alpha}\leq\delta_{D}(y)=|y-Q|<\kappa t^{1/\alpha}
,
so

\displaystyle\displaystyle|A_{t^{1/\alpha}}(y)-A_{t^{1/\alpha}}(Q)|

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq|A_{t^{1/\alpha}}(y)-y|+|y-Q|+|Q-A_{t^{1/\alpha}}(Q)|\leq(2+\kappa)t^{1/\alpha}.

By definition,
\displaystyle\delta_{D}(A_{t^{1/\alpha}}(y))\wedge\delta_{D}(A_{t^{1/\alpha}}(Q))\geq\kappa t^{1/\alpha}
.
Therefore, by the Harnack inequality
[
13
, Lemma 1]
, we find that
\displaystyle\Phi(A_{t^{1/\alpha}}(y))\approx\Phi(A_{t^{1/\alpha}}(Q))
.

On the other hand, since
\displaystyle\kappa\leq 1/2
, we have
\displaystyle y\in\mathcal{A}_{\delta_{D}(y)/\kappa}(Q)
.
In particular, we can take
\displaystyle{A}_{s^{1/\alpha}}(y)={A}_{\delta_{D}(y)/\kappa}(Q)=y
.
Then, by
[
11
, Lemma 5]
, we get

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{\Phi(A_{t^{1/\alpha}}(y))}{\Phi(A_{s^{1/\alpha}}(y))}\approx\frac{\Phi(A_{t^{1/\alpha}}(Q))}{\Phi(A_{\delta_{D}(y)/\kappa}(Q))}\lesssim\bigg(\frac{t^{1/\alpha}}{\delta_{D}(y)/\kappa}\bigg)^{\gamma}\leq\bigg(\frac{s}{t}\bigg)^{-\gamma/\alpha},

where
\displaystyle\gamma=\gamma(d,\alpha,\underline{D})\in(0,\alpha)
. This ends the proof in this case.

Case 3.
If
\displaystyle y\notin\mathcal{A}_{s^{1/\alpha}}(y)\cup\mathcal{A}_{t^{1/\alpha}}(y)
, then
\displaystyle\delta_{D}(y)<\kappa s^{1/\alpha}<\kappa t^{1/\alpha}
.
By the same argument as in the previous case,
\displaystyle\Phi(A_{t^{1/\alpha}}(y))\approx\Phi(A_{t^{1/\alpha}}(Q))
,

\displaystyle\Phi(A_{s^{1/\alpha}}(y))\approx\Phi(A_{s^{1/\alpha}}(Q))
, and

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{\Phi(A_{t^{1/\alpha}}(y))}{\Phi(A_{s^{1/\alpha}}(y))}\approx\frac{\Phi(A_{t^{1/\alpha}}(Q))}{\Phi(A_{s^{1/\alpha}}(Q))}\lesssim\bigg(\frac{s}{t}\bigg)^{-\gamma/\alpha}

The proof is complete.
∎

.

We use (

Since
