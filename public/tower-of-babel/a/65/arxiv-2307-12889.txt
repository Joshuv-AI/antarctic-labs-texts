Estimates on the Neumann and Steklov principal eigenvalues of collapsing domains
P. Acampora, V. Amato, E. Cristoforoni

Abstract
We investigate the relationship between the Neumann and Steklov principal eigenvalues emerging from the study of collapsing convex domains in ℝ 2 \mathbb{R}^{2} . Such a relationship allows us to give a partial proof of a conjecture concerning estimates of the ratio of the former to the latter: we show that thinning triangles maximize the ratio among convex thinning sets, while thinning rectangles minimize the ratio among convex thinning with some symmetry property. MSC 2020: 35P15, 49Q10, 52A40 Keywords: Neumann eigenvalue, Steklov eigenvalue, thinning convex sets, Sturm-Liouville

Introduction

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
be a bounded, open, connected and Lipschitz set. We define the Neumann and Steklov eigenvalues as follows: find positive constants
\mu,\sigma
such that there exist non-zero solutions to the boundary value problems

\begin{cases}-\Delta u=\mu u&\text{in }\Omega,\\
\frac{\partial u}{\partial\nu}=0&\text{on }\partial\Omega,\end{cases}\qquad\qquad\qquad\begin{cases}\Delta v=0&\text{in }\Omega,\\
\frac{\partial v}{\partial\nu}=\sigma v&\text{on }\partial\Omega.\end{cases}

The regularity assumption we made on
\Omega
ensures that we can find two increasing and divergent sequences of eigenvalues

\begin{split}0=\mu_{0}(\Omega)<\mu_{1}(\Omega)\leq\mu_{2}(\Omega)\leq\dots\leq\mu_{k}(\Omega)\leq\dots,\\[5.0pt]
0=\sigma_{0}(\Omega)<\sigma_{1}(\Omega)\leq\sigma_{2}(\Omega)\leq\dots\leq\sigma_{k}(\Omega)\leq\dots,\end{split}

which are the spectrum of the Neumann laplacian and the spectrum of the Dirichlet-to-Neumann map respectively. We recall the variational characterization of the eigenvalues, for
k\geq 0
:

\mu_{k}(\Omega)=\inf_{E\in\mathcal{S}_{k+1}(\Omega)}\,\,\sup_{w\in E\setminus\{0\}}\frac{\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\lvert\nabla w\rvert^{2}\,dx}{\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}w^{2}\,dx},\qquad\qquad\sigma_{k}(\Omega)=\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}E\in\mathcal{S}_{k+1}(\Omega)\end{subarray}}\,\,\sup_{w\in E\setminus\{0\}}\frac{\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\lvert\nabla w\rvert^{2}\,dx}{\displaystyle\int_{\partial\Omega}w^{2}\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}},

where
\mathcal{S}_{k+1}(\Omega)
is the family of all linear subspaces of
H^{1}(\Omega)
of dimension
k+1
. In particular, we are interested in the principal eigenvalues, i.e.
k=1
, namely

\mu_{1}(\Omega)=\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}w\in H^{1}(\Omega)\setminus\{0\}\\
\int_{\Omega}w=0\end{subarray}}\frac{\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\lvert\nabla w\rvert^{2}\,dx}{\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}w^{2}\,dx},

\sigma_{1}(\Omega)=\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}w\in H^{1}(\Omega)\setminus\{0\}\\
\int_{\partial\Omega}w=0\end{subarray}}\frac{\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\lvert\nabla w\rvert^{2}\,dx}{\displaystyle\int_{\partial\Omega}w^{2}\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}}.

Many authors in the literature identified remarkable similarities between the two families of eigenvalues. Moreover, an underlying relationship holds between the two quantities. For instance, Steklov eigenvalues can be seen as limits of weighted Neumann eigenvalues, while Neumann eigenvalues can be obtained as limits of Steklov eigenvalues by suitably perforating the set
\Omega
. We refer, for instance, to
[
9
]
, and
[
5
]
for these results. We want to explore the same relationship between the two eigenvalues, from the shape optimization point of view.

Namely, we could be interested in the scale invariant ratio

F(\Omega)=\frac{\lvert\Omega\rvert\mu_{1}(\Omega)}{P(\Omega)\sigma_{1}(\Omega)},

and, consequently, in the two problems

\min_{\Omega\in\mathcal{K}}F(\Omega),\qquad\qquad\max_{\Omega\in\mathcal{K}}F(\Omega),

(1.1)

Finally, we recall the definition of a quasiconcave function.

Definition 2.13
.

A function

is quasiconcave if for all

and

we have

f(\lambda x+(1-\lambda)y)\geq\min\big\{f(x),f(y)\big\}.

A function defined on an interval is quasiconcave if and only if it is monotone or ’increasing then decreasing’, i.e. if there are two complementary intervals (one of which may be empty) such that it is increasing on the former and decreasing on the latter.

Proposition 4.1
.

Let

\sigma_{1}(h)=\min\Set{\dfrac{\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}(\varphi^{\prime})^{2}\,dx}{\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\varphi^{2}}{h}\,dx}}{\begin{aligned} &\>\,\varphi\in H^{1}_{0}(0,1),\\[3.0pt]
\displaystyle&\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\varphi^{2}}{h}\,dx<\infty\end{aligned}}.

Proof.

Let

be a weak solution to (

) and let

. Then, since

, we have that

and that is a solution to

\begin{cases}\displaystyle{-w^{\prime\prime}(x)=\frac{\sigma_{1}(h)}{h(x)}w(x)}\qquad\qquad x\in(0,1)\\[7.0pt]
w(0)=w(1)=0,\end{cases}

(4.1)

and

\dfrac{\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}(w^{\prime})^{2}\,dx}{\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\frac{w^{2}}{h}\,dx}=\sigma_{1}(h).

(4.2)

Following classical arguments (see for instance
[
4
]
) we have that

vanishes in one and only one point

, so that

vanishes only in

. Without loss of generality, we can assume that

is positive in
[0,x_{0})
and it is negative in
(x_{0},1]
. Let now

be such that

\int_{0}^{1}\dfrac{\varphi^{2}}{h}\,dx<+\infty.

Then, for every

, we have that

\begin{split}\dfrac{1}{h(x)}\varphi^{2}(x)&\leq\dfrac{1}{h(x)}\left(\int_{0}^{x}\dfrac{(\varphi^{\prime}(t))^{2}}{w^{\prime}(t)}\,dt\right)\left(\int_{0}^{x}w^{\prime}(t)\,dt\right)\\[15.0pt]
&=\dfrac{w(x)}{h(x)}\int_{0}^{x}\dfrac{(\varphi^{\prime}(t))^{2}}{w^{\prime}(t)}\,dt.\end{split}

Then, integrating from

to

\begin{split}\int_{0}^{x_{1}}\dfrac{\varphi^{2}(x)}{h(x)}\,dx&\leq\int_{0}^{x_{1}}\dfrac{w(x)}{h(x)}\int_{0}^{x}\dfrac{(\varphi^{\prime}(t))^{2}}{w^{\prime}(t)}\,dt\,dx\\[15.0pt]
&=\int_{0}^{x_{1}}\dfrac{(\varphi^{\prime}(t))^{2}}{w^{\prime}(t)}\int_{t}^{x_{1}}\dfrac{w(x)}{h(x)}\,dx\,dt.\end{split}

Using (
4.1
), and the fact that

, then we have

\int_{0}^{x_{1}}\dfrac{\varphi^{2}(x)}{h(x)}\,dx\leq\dfrac{1}{\sigma_{1}(h)}\int_{0}^{x_{1}}(\varphi^{\prime}(t))^{2}\,dt.

Letting

\int_{0}^{x_{0}}\dfrac{\varphi^{2}(x)}{h(x)}\,dx\leq\dfrac{1}{\sigma_{1}(h)}\int_{0}^{x_{0}}(\varphi^{\prime}(x))^{2}\,dx.

(4.3)

Similar computations can be done in the case

, so that we have

\int_{x_{0}}^{1}\dfrac{\varphi^{2}(x)}{h(x)}\,dx\leq\dfrac{1}{\sigma_{1}(h)}\int_{x_{0}}^{1}(\varphi^{\prime}(x))^{2}\,dx.

(4.4)

Joining (

that is

\dfrac{\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}(\varphi^{\prime})^{2}\,dx}{\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\varphi^{2}}{h}\,dx}\geq\sigma_{1}(h).

(4.5)

Since

Lemma 4.6

Let

Lemma 4.7

h=T_{x_{0}}
, then
h_{*}=T_{1}
and, from (
4.19
), we have

\sigma_{1}(T_{x_{0}})\geq\sigma_{1}(T_{1}),

which concludes the proof.
∎

5
Ratio
\mu/\sigma

In this section, we prove
Theorem 1.3
and
Theorem 1.4
. We begin by defining an operator
\mathcal{G}
on
\mathcal{P}
as follows: let
h\in\mathcal{P}
, and let

H(x)=\dfrac{1}{\int_{0}^{1}h(t)\,dt}\int_{0}^{x}h(t)\,dt;

(5.1)

we notice that
H
is a strictly increasing function such that
H(0)=0
and
H(1)=1
. We then define

\mathcal{G}(h)(x)=h^{2}(H^{-1}(x)).

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
h\in\mathcal{P}
. Then
\mathcal{G}(h)\in\mathcal{P}
, and the map

\mathcal{G}:\mathcal{P}\to\mathcal{P}

is invertible.

Proof.

Since
h\in\mathcal{P}
, then
h^{\prime}
is defined a.e. in
[0,1]
, and

h^{\prime}
is decreasing. We also have that
H^{-1}
is a locally Lipschitz function and

\frac{d}{dx}H^{-1}(x)=\frac{1}{h(H^{-1}(x))}\int_{0}^{1}h(t)\,dt.

(5.2)

Therefore,
\mathcal{G}(h)
is a.e. differentiable and

\frac{d}{dx}\mathcal{G}(h)(x)=2\alpha h^{\prime}(H^{-1}(x)),

where

\alpha=\int_{0}^{1}h(t)\,dt.

Since
H^{-1}
is an increasing function and
h^{\prime}
is decreasing, then
\mathcal{G}(h)
is a concave function, and
\mathcal{G}(h)\in\mathcal{P}
.

Let
k\in\mathcal{P}
and define

K(x)=\dfrac{1}{\int_{0}^{1}\frac{1}{\sqrt{k(t)}}\,dt}\int_{0}^{x}\frac{1}{\sqrt{k(t)}}\,dt,

(5.3)

then we want to prove that

\sqrt{k(K^{-1}(x))}=\mathcal{G}^{-1}(k)(x).

(5.4)

First we prove that
\sqrt{k\circ K^{-1}}\in\mathcal{P}
. By direct computation,

\frac{d}{dx}\sqrt{k(K^{-1}(x))}=\frac{\beta k^{\prime}(K^{-1}(x))}{2k(K^{-1}(x))},

where

\beta=\int_{0}^{1}\frac{1}{\sqrt{k(t)}}\,dt.

This proves that
\sqrt{k\circ K^{-1}}
is concave, since
K^{-1}
is increasing and
h^{\prime}/h
is decreasing because of the concavity of
h
. On the other hand, to prove (
5.4
), we observe that with a change of variables we get

\int_{0}^{x}\sqrt{k(K^{-1}(t))}\,dt=\frac{K(x)}{\int_{0}^{1}\frac{1}{\sqrt{k(t)}}\,dt},

and by definition of
\mathcal{G}
we get

\mathcal{G}\left(\sqrt{k\circ K^{-1}}\right)(x)=k(x).

∎

We now prove that
\mathcal{G}
is the operator in
Theorem 1.3
.

Proof of
Theorem 1.3
.

Let
v\in H^{1}(0,1)
be a function such that

\int_{0}^{1}v(t)h(t)\,dt=0,

and let
H
denote the integral function defined in (
5.1
). The change of variables
H(t)=s
yields

\left(\int_{0}^{1}h(t)\,dt\right)\int_{0}^{1}v(t)\,h(t)\,dt=\int_{0}^{1}v(H^{-1}(s))\,ds,

\left(\int_{0}^{1}h(t)\,dt\right)\int_{0}^{1}(v^{\prime})^{2}(t)\,h(t)\,dt=\int_{0}^{1}(v^{\prime})^{2}(H^{-1}(s))\,ds,

and

\left(\int_{0}^{1}h(t)\,dt\right)\int_{0}^{1}v^{2}(t)\,h(t)\,dt=\int_{0}^{1}v^{2}(H^{-1}(s))\,ds.

Let
w(x)=v(H^{-1}(x))
, then by (
5.2
),

w^{\prime}(x)=\left(\int_{0}^{1}h(t)\,dt\right)\,v^{\prime}(H^{-1}(x))\,\bigl(\mathcal{G}(h)(x)\bigr)^{-\frac{1}{2}}.

Hence,

\frac{\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}(v^{\prime})^{2}(t)\,h(t)\,dt}{\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}v^{2}(t)\,h(t)\,dt}=\left(\int_{0}^{1}h(t)\,dt\right)^{-2}\frac{\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}(w^{\prime})^{2}(t)\,\mathcal{G}(h)(t)\,dt}{\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}w^{2}(t)\,dt}.

Choosing
v=v_{\mu}
to be the eigenfunction of
\mu_{1}(h)
, then we get

\mu_{1}(h)\geq\left(\int_{0}^{1}h(t)\,dt\right)^{-2}\sigma_{1}(\mathcal{G}(h)).

On the other hand, choosing
w=w_{\sigma}
to be the eigenfunction of
\sigma_{1}(\mathcal{G}(h))
, we get

\mu_{1}(h)\leq\left(\int_{0}^{1}h(t)\,dt\right)^{-2}\sigma_{1}(\mathcal{G}(h)),

which gives (
1.6
).

Let
k\in\mathcal{P}
and let
K
be the integral function defined in (
5.3
). If we evaluate the integral on the right-hand side by means of the change of variables
t=K(s)
, we finally get

\int_{0}^{1}h(t)\,dt=\left(\int_{0}^{1}\frac{1}{\sqrt{k(t)}}\,dt\right)^{-1},

which gives (
1.7
).
∎

The following punctual estimate will be crucial.

Proposition 5.2
.

Let
h\in\mathcal{P}
. Then

\left(\int_{0}^{1}h(t)\,dt\right)^{-1}\mathcal{G}(h)(x)\leq 2h(x).

Proof.

Up to rescaling
h
, we can assume without loss of generality that
h\in\mathcal{P}_{1}
. Notice, in addition, that if
h\equiv 1
, then the proof is trivial. Therefore, let
h\in\mathcal{P}_{1}
and
h\not\equiv 1
, and define

H(x)=\int_{0}^{x}h(t)\,dt.

We claim that there exists a unique
\bar{x}\in[0,1]
such that

\displaystyle H(x)\leq x

\displaystyle x\in[0,\bar{x}],

(5.5)

\displaystyle H(x)\geq x

\displaystyle x\in[\bar{x},1].

Indeed, if we denote by

f(x)=H(x)-x,

then, by the concavity of
h
and the integral constraint, we have that the equation
h=1
admits at most two solutions (
h
cannot be equal to 1 in an entire interval, otherwise the concavity would give
\lVert h\rVert_{1}<1
). Therefore, we have that there exist two points
x_{1}\in[0,1)
, and
x_{2}\in(0,1]
such that

\displaystyle f^{\prime}(x)<0\qquad\qquad

\displaystyle x\in[0,x_{1})\cup(x_{2},1],

\displaystyle f^{\prime}(x)>0\qquad\qquad

\displaystyle x\in(x_{1},x_{2}).

Finally, noticing that
f(0)=f(1)=0
, then we have that there exists a unique zero
\bar{x}
of
f
in the interval
[x_{1},x_{2}]
, thus the claim is proved. In particular, we have that

\displaystyle H^{-1}(x)\geq x\qquad\qquad

\displaystyle x\in[0,\bar{x}],

\displaystyle H^{-1}(x)\leq x\qquad\qquad

\displaystyle x\in[\bar{x},1],

and
h(\bar{x})>1
.

These estimates allow us to compare the derivatives of
\mathcal{G}(h)
and
h
. Denoting by
g(x)=\mathcal{G}(h)(x)
, we have that

\displaystyle g^{\prime}(x)=2h^{\prime}(H^{-1}(x))\leq 2h^{\prime}(x)\qquad\qquad

\displaystyle x\in[0,\bar{x}],

(5.6)

\displaystyle g^{\prime}(x)=2h^{\prime}(H^{-1}(x))\geq 2h^{\prime}(x)\qquad\qquad

\displaystyle x\in[\bar{x},1].

(5.7)

We recall that, as in (
2.4
), the concavity of
h
ensures that

{\lVert h\rVert_{\infty}}\leq 2.

Therefore, we get

g(0)=h^{2}(0)\leq 2h(0),

and by (
5.6
),

g(x)\leq 2h(x)\qquad\qquad x\in[0,\bar{x}].

Analogously,

g(1)=h^{2}(1)\leq 2h(1),

and, by (
5.7
),

g(x)\leq 2h(x)\qquad\qquad x\in[\bar{x},1].

∎

Proposition 5.3
.

Let
h\in\mathcal{P}_{1}
.

\mathcal{G}(h)=2h

if and only if
h=T_{x_{0}}
for some
x_{0}\in[0,1]
.

Proof.

By direct computation, one can prove that if
h=T_{x_{0}}
for some
x_{0}\in[0,1]
, then

h^{2}(x)=2h(H(x)).

Let us now assume that
\mathcal{G}(h)=2h
. Notice that, if
y\in[0,1]
is a fixed point of the integral function
H
, then

h^{2}(y)=h^{2}(H^{-1}(y))=\mathcal{G}(h)(y)=2h(y),

(5.8)

so that either
h(y)=0
or
h(y)=2
. In particular, if
h(y)=2
, then by the concavity of
h
, we have that

h(x)=T_{y}(x)\qquad\forall\,x\in[0,1].

Since
0
and
1
are always fixed points of
H
, if either
h(0)=2
or
h(1)=2
the assertion is proved. Therefore, let us assume that

h(0)=0=h(1),

then the equation
h=1
admits at least two distinct solutions
0<x_{1}<x_{2}<1
and, arguing as in the proof of
Section 5
, we have that there exists a fixed point
\bar{x}\in[x_{1},x_{2}]
for the function
H
and, by (
5.8
), necessarily
h(\bar{x})=2
and
h=T_{\bar{x}}
.
∎

Proposition 5.4
.

Let
h\in\mathcal{P}_{1}
, then

\frac{\mu_{1}(h)}{\sigma_{1}(h)}\leq 2

(5.9)

and the equality holds if and only if
h=T_{x_{0}}
for some
x_{0}\in[0,1]
.

Proof.

Let
w
be an eigenfunction for
\sigma_{1}(h)
. Using Theorem

,
Section 5
, and the variational characterization of
\sigma_{1}(\mathcal{G}(h))
, we obtain

\mu_{1}(h)=\sigma_{1}(\mathcal{G}(h))\leq\frac{\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}(w^{\prime})^{2}\mathcal{G}(h)\,dx}{\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}w^{2}\,dx}\leq\frac{\displaystyle 2\int_{0}^{1}(w^{\prime})^{2}h\,dx}{\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}w^{2}\,dx}=2\sigma_{1}(h),

(5.10)

thus proving (
5.9
).
Assume now that for some

equality holds, then by (

(5.11)

) yields

ensures that

for some

∎

Remark 5.5
.

Since it is not possible in general to have that
\mathcal{G}(h)\geq h
, then the same argument cannot be used for the lower bound

\frac{\mu_{1}(h)}{\sigma_{1}(h)}\geq 1.

For instance, let

h(x)=\frac{1}{2}+x,

then
\mathcal{G}(h)(0)=h^{2}(0)<h(0)
, while
\mathcal{G}(h)(1)=h^{2}(1)>h(1)
.

Here we prove the lower bound in
Theorem 1.4
in the symmetric case.

Proposition 5.6
.

Let
h\in\mathcal{P}_{1}
such that
h(1-x)=h(x)
for all
x\in[0,1]
. Then

\frac{\mu_{1}(h)}{\sigma_{1}(h)}\geq 1.

(5.12)

Proof.

Let
g=\mathcal{G}(h)
. By the variational characterization (
4.18
) of
\sigma_{1}
, and
Theorem 1.3
, we can find a function
w\in H^{2}(0,1)
, symmetric with respect to
x=1/2
, such that

\mu_{1}(h)=\sigma_{1}(g)=\frac{\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}(w^{\prime})^{2}(x)\,dx}{\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\frac{w^{2}(x)}{g(x)}\,dx},

(5.13)

and
w
solves the problem

\begin{cases}\displaystyle{-w^{\prime\prime}(x)=\frac{\sigma_{1}(h)}{h(x)}w(x)}\qquad\qquad x\in(0,1),\\[7.0pt]
w(0)=w(1)=0.\end{cases}

We can choose
w
to be positive and concave, so that

\displaystyle w^{\prime}(x)\geq 0

\displaystyle\text{in }\left(0,\frac{1}{2}\right),

(5.14)

\displaystyle w^{\prime}(x)\leq 0

\displaystyle\text{in }\left(\frac{1}{2},1\right).

Moreover, by the variational characterization (
4.18
), we get

\sigma_{1}(h)\leq\frac{\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}(w^{\prime})^{2}(x)\,dx}{\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\frac{w^{2}(x)}{h(x)}\,dx},

(5.15)

and then, joining (
5.13
) and (
5.15
), we get

\frac{\mu_{1}(h)}{\sigma_{1}(h)}\geq\frac{\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\frac{w^{2}(x)}{h(x)}\,dx}{\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\frac{w^{2}(x)}{g(x)}\,dx}.

To prove (
5.12
) it is sufficient to prove that

\int_{0}^{1}\frac{w^{2}(x)}{g(x)}\,dx\leq\int_{0}^{1}\frac{w^{2}(x)}{h(x)}\,dx.

We now compute the left-hand side by means of the change of variables
x=H(y)
, where

H(y)=\int_{0}^{y}h(t)\,dt,

so that

\int_{0}^{1}\frac{w^{2}(x)}{g(x)}\,dx=\int_{0}^{1}\frac{w^{2}(H(y))}{h(y)}\,dy.

We now notice that the symmetry of
h
gives (
5.5
) with
\bar{x}=1/2

\displaystyle H(y)\leq y

\displaystyle x\in\left[0,\frac{1}{2}\right],

(5.16)

\displaystyle H(y)\geq y

\displaystyle x\in\left[\frac{1}{2},1\right].

Finally, joining (
5.16
), and (
5.14
), we have

\int_{0}^{1}\frac{w^{2}(x)}{g(x)}\,dx=\int_{0}^{1}\frac{w^{2}(H(y))}{h(y)}\,dy\leq\int_{0}^{1}\frac{w^{2}(y)}{h(y)}\,dy,

which concludes the proof.
