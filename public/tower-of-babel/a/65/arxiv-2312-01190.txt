The likely maximum size of twin subtrees in a large random tree
Miklós Bóna, Ovidiu Costin, and Boris Pittel

Abstract
. We call a pair of vertex-disjoint, induced subtrees of a rooted trees twins if they have the same counts of vertices by out-degrees. The likely maximum size of twins in a uniformly random, rooted Cayley tree of size n → ∞ n\to\infty is studied. It is shown that the expected number of twins of size ( 2 + δ ) ​ log ⁡ n ⋅ log ⁡ log ​ n (2+\delta)\sqrt{\log n\cdot\log\log n} approaches zero, while the expected number of twins of size ( 2 − δ ) ​ log ⁡ n ⋅ log ⁡ log ​ n (2-\delta)\sqrt{\log n\cdot\log\log n} approaches infinity.



1.
Introduction and the main result.

For a given combinatorial structure
S
, there is intrinsic interest in finding the largest substructure of a given kind that is contained in
S
, or a set of two or more identical substructures
of maximum size that are contained in
S
, or the size of the largest common substructure in two random structures
S_{1}
and
S_{2}
. For instance, finding the length of a longest increasing subsequence of a random permutation of length is the famous Ulam problem (Section 6.5 of
[
4
]
is a basic survey of this subject).
An early collection of problems featuring the largest common substructures in two combinatorial objects can be found in
[
1
]
. In a pioneering paper
[
5
]
, Bryant, McKenzie, and Steel proved that the maximum size of a common subtree for two independent copies of a uniformly random binary tree with
n
leaves is likely to be of order

O(n^{1/2})
. Interestingly, the proof is based on a classic result that the length of the increasing subsequence of a uniformly random permutation is likely to be of order
O(n^{1/2})
. And a recent result of Aldous

[
2
]
is
that the maximum size of this common subtree largest common subtree (maximum agreement subtree) of two independent uniform random binary trees on
n
leaves is likely to be of order at least
n^{\beta}
, where
\beta=(\sqrt{3}-1)/2\approx 0.366
. Pittel
[
9
]
proved the bound
O(n^{1/2})
for the maximum size of a subtree common to two independent copies of a random rooted tree, namely the terminal tree of a critical Galton-Watson branching process
conditioned on the total number of leaves being
n
.

In
[
3
]
, Bóna and Flajolet used a bivariate generating function approach to compute the probability that two randomly selected phylogenetic trees are isomorphic as unlabeled
trees. The requested probability was found to be asymptotic to a decreasing exponential modulated by a
polynomial factor. This line of research involved the study of the expected number of symmetries in such trees, which was initiated by McKeon in
[
8
]
; in
[
3
]
,
this number in large phylogenetic trees was found to obey a limiting Gaussian distribution.

Motivated by the cited work, we study the likely maximum size of two, almost identical, rooted (fringe) subtrees of a random rooted tree, rather than a pair of random trees. (A subtree rooted at a vertex
v
is defined as a subtree induced by
v
and the set of all the
descendants
of
v
.) Formally, we call two rooted trees almost identical, twins, if they have the same counts of vertices by their out-degrees. The twin subtrees are automatically vertex-disjoint. In this paper the tree in question is the uniformly random (Cayley) rooted tree on
vertex set
[n]
. Our main result is

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
m_{n}(k)
denote the expected number of twin pairs of subtrees, with
k
vertices each.
(a)

If
\delta>0
and

K_{n}=\big\lfloor\exp\bigl((2+\delta)\sqrt{\log n\cdot\log(\log n)}\bigr)\big\rfloor,

then
\sum_{k\geq K_{n}}m_{n}(k)\to 0
. Consequently, with high probability, the largest size of a pair of twins is below
K_{n}
.
(b)
If
\delta\in(0,2)
and

k_{n}=\big\lfloor\exp\bigl((2-\delta)\sqrt{\log n\cdot\log(\log n)}\bigr)\big\rfloor,

then
m_{n}(k_{n})\to\infty
.

Note.

(i)
In fact, our proof of
(b)
consists of showing that even a
smaller
expected number of twins of maximum degree
\lfloor 2\log k_{n}/\log\log k_{n}\rfloor
grows indefinitely as
n\to\infty
.
(ii)
Going out on a limb,
we conjecture that the logarithm of the maximum twin size scaled by
\sqrt{\log n\cdot\log(\log n)}
converges, in probability, to
2
.

In conclusion, a reviewer indicated to us that the our problem is reminiscent of the study of distinct rooted subtrees
in Flajolet, Sipala, and Steyaert
[
7
]
and the study of repeated subtrees in Ralaivaosaona and Wagner
[
10
]
.

2.
The proof of Theorem
1.1
.

Given
k
,
\mathbb{r}=(r_{0},r_{1},\dots)
is a sequence of counts of vertices by out-degree of a rooted tree on
[k]
if and only if

(2.1)

\sum_{i}r_{i}=k,\quad\sum_{i}ir_{i}=k-1,

and
M(\mathbb{r})
, the number of such trees, is given by

(2.2)

M(\mathbb{r})=\frac{(k-1)!}{\prod\limits_{j\geq 0}(j!)^{r_{j}}}\binom{k}{r_{0},r_{1},\dots},

Stanley
[
11
]
. It follows that the total number of pairs of rooted subtrees, on two respective vertex-disjoint vertex sets, each of cardinality
k
,
that have the same counts of vertices by out-degrees, is

(2.3)

N(k)=\sum_{\mathbb{r}\text{ meets }\eqref{1}}M^{2}(\mathbb{r}).

Note.
If we consider only subtrees of maximum out-degree (strictly) below
d\,(\geq 2)
then the index
i
in the condition (
2.1
) ranges from
0
to
d-1
.

Let
S_{n}(k)
stand for the total number of pairs of vertex-disjoint rooted subtrees, each with
k
vertices, in all the rooted trees on
[n]
that share the counts of vertices by out-degree. Then

(2.4)

S_{n}(k)=\binom{n}{k}\binom{n-k}{k}N(k)(n-2k)^{n-2k-1}(n-2k)^{2}.

And the expected number of the pairs of twins of size
k
each is given by
m_{n}(k)=\tfrac{S_{n}(k)}{n^{n-1}}
.

The challenge is to sharply estimate
N(k)
given by (
2.3
). Using (
2.2
) for
M(\mathbb{r})
,
and a generating function
H(z):=\sum_{r\geq 0}\tfrac{z^{r}}{(r!)^{2}}
, we evaluate

(2.5)

N(k)=\sum_{\mathbb{r}\text{ meets }\eqref{1}}M^{2}(\mathbb{r})=\bigl(k!(k-1)!\bigr)^{2}\sum_{\mathbb{r}\text{ meets }\eqref{1}}\,\biggl(\prod_{j\geq 0}\tfrac{1}{(j!)^{r_{j}}r_{j}!}\biggr)^{2}\\
=\bigl(k!(k-1)!\bigr)^{2}[x_{1}^{k}x_{2}^{k-1}]\sum_{\mathbb{r}\geq\mathbb{0}}x_{1}^{\sum_{\alpha}r_{\alpha}}\,x_{2}^{\sum_{\beta}\beta\cdot r_{\beta}}\biggl(\prod_{j\geq 0}\tfrac{1}{(j!)^{r_{j}}r_{j}!}\biggr)^{2}\\
=\bigl(k!(k-1)!\bigr)^{2}[x_{1}^{k}x_{2}^{k-1}]\sum_{\mathbb{r}\geq\mathbb{0}}\prod_{j\geq 0}\tfrac{(x_{1}x_{2}^{j})^{r_{j}}}{\bigl((j!)^{r_{j}}r_{j}!\bigr)^{2}}\\
=\bigl(k!(k-1)!\bigr)^{2}[x_{1}^{k}x_{2}^{k-1}]\prod_{j\geq 0}\sum_{r_{j}\geq 0}\tfrac{(x_{1}x_{2}^{j})^{r_{j}}}{\bigl((j!)^{r_{j}}r_{j}!\bigr)^{2}}\\
=\bigl(k!(k-1)!\bigr)^{2}[x_{1}^{k}x_{2}^{k-1}]\prod_{j\geq 0}H\bigl(\tfrac{x_{1}x_{2}^{j}}{(j!)^{2}}\bigr).

If we count only the twins of maximum out-degree below
d
, then in the above product
j
ranges from
0
to
d-1
.

What can we get from (
2.5
) with a bare-minimum analytical work? One possibility is to use a Chernoff-type inequality
implied by (
2.5
). Note that
for a power series
p(x,y)=\sum_{i,j}a_{i,j}x^{i}y^{j}
with nonnegative real coefficients, the inequality

a_{i,j}x^{i}y^{j}\leq p(x,y)
holds for all
(i,j)
and all
(x,y)
where
x>0
and
y>0
. Dividing by
x^{i}y^{j}
and taking the infimum on the right-hand side, we get that

a_{i,j}\leq\inf_{x>0,y>0}\frac{p(x,y)}{x^{i}y^{j}}.

Applying this inequality for the power series defined by the last line of (
2.5
), we get

(2.6)

N(k)\leq\inf_{x_{1}>0,x_{2}>0}\tfrac{(k!(k-1)!)^{2}}{x_{1}^{k}x_{2}^{k-1}}\prod_{j\geq 0}H\bigl(\tfrac{x_{1}x_{2}^{j}}{(j!)^{2}}\bigr).

Observe that, by an elementary inequality
\binom{2r}{r}\leq 2^{2r}
, for
x>0

(2.7)

H(x)\leq\sum_{r\geq 0}\tfrac{x^{r}2^{2r}}{(2r)!}\leq\sum_{\ell\geq 0}\tfrac{(2x^{1/2})^{\ell}}{\ell!}=\exp(2x^{1/2}).\\

So, (
2.6
) yields

N(k)\leq\inf_{x_{1}>0,x_{2}>0}\tfrac{(k!(k-1)!)^{2}}{x_{1}^{k}x_{2}^{k-1}}\exp\biggl(2\sum_{j\geq 0}\tfrac{(x_{1}{x_{2}^{j}})^{1/2}}{j!}\biggr)\\
=\inf_{x_{1}>0,x_{2}>0}\tfrac{(k!(k-1)!)^{2}}{x_{1}^{k}x_{2}^{k-1}}\exp\bigl(2x_{1}^{1/2}e^{x_{2}^{1/2}}\bigr).

A straightforward computation shows that the
k
-dependent factor in the RHS function attains its minimum at
x_{1}=k^{2}\exp\bigl(-\tfrac{2(k-1)}{k}\bigr)
,

x_{2}=\bigl(\tfrac{k-1}{k}\bigr)^{2}
, and consequently

(2.8)

N(k)=O\bigl(\tfrac{(k!)^{4}e^{4k}}{k^{2(k+1)}}\bigr).

The last estimate is too crude, but the above values of
x_{1}
and
x_{2}
will be used when we switch from Chernoff’s bound to a bivariate Cauchy integral, taken on the Cartesian product of two cycles, i. e. counterclock-wise oriented boundaries of two circles, of radii
x_{1}
and
x_{2}
respectively.
Essentially we will use a bivariate version of the saddle point method, with the above discussion allowing us to guess that
(x_{1},x_{2})
is a promising approximation of the saddle point itself.

Here is the switch. By (
2.5
) and the Cauchy formula we have

(2.9)

N(k)=\tfrac{\bigl(k!(k-1)!\bigr)^{2}}{(2\pi i)^{2}}\oint\limits_{C_{1}\times C_{2}}\prod_{j\geq 0}\tfrac{1}{z_{1}^{k+1}z_{2}^{k}}H\Bigl(\tfrac{z_{1}z_{2}^{j}}{(j!)^{2}}\Bigr)\,dz_{1}dz_{2},

C_{s}=\bigl\{z_{s}\in\mathbb{C}:z_{s}=x_{s}e^{i\xi_{s}},\,\xi_{s}\in(-\pi,\pi)\bigr\}
,
s=1,2
. It follows that for all
x_{s}>0

(2.10)

N(k)\leq\tfrac{\bigl(k!(k-1)!\bigr)^{2}}{(2\pi)^{2}x_{1}^{k}x_{2}^{k-1}}\int\limits_{\xi_{s}\in(-\pi,\pi)}\prod_{j\geq 0}\Big|H\Bigl(\tfrac{z_{1}z_{2}^{j}}{(j!)^{2}}\Bigr)\Big|\,d\xi_{1}d\xi_{2},\,\,z_{s}=x_{s}e^{i\xi_{s}}.

Lemma 2.1
.

For
H(z)=\sum_{r\geq 0}\tfrac{z^{r}}{(r!)^{2}}
,
\text{arg}(z)\in(-\pi,\pi]
, we have

|H(z)|\leq\tfrac{|\exp(2z^{1/2})|}{\max(1,\alpha|z|^{1/4})},\quad z^{1/2}:=|z|^{1/2}\exp\bigl(\tfrac{1}{2}\text{arg}(z)\bigr),

where
\alpha>0
is an absolute constant.

Proof.

Introduce
I(z):=\sum_{r=0}^{\infty}\tfrac{z^{2r}}{(r!)^{2}}
,
\text{arg}(z)\in(-\pi/2,\pi/2]
, so that
H(z^{2})=I(z)
. It suffices to prove that, for
\arg(z)\in(-\pi/2,\pi/2]
,

(2.11)

|I_{0}(2z)|\leq\tfrac{\big|\exp(2z)\big|}{\max(1,\alpha|z|^{1/2})},\quad I_{0}(\xi)=\sum_{j\geq 0}\frac{\bigl(\tfrac{\xi^{2}}{4}\bigr)^{j}}{(j!)^{2}},

where
I_{0}(\xi)
is the zero-order, modified, Bessel function, since
H(z^{2})=I_{0}(2z)
. Here is a surprisingly simple integral formula for
I_{0}(2z)
that does the job:

(2.12)

I_{0}(2z)=\pi^{-1}\exp(2z)\int_{0}^{4}\tfrac{\exp(-zy)}{\sqrt{y(4-y)}}\,dy,\quad\text{arg}(z)\in(-\pi/2,\pi/2].

The equation (
2.12
) follows from identity 10.32.2 in
[
6
]
, that is,

I_{0}(2z)=\tfrac{1}{\pi}\int_{1}^{1}(1-t^{2})^{-1/2}e^{-2tz}\,dt,

via substitution
y=2t+2
. (Our original proof of (
2.12
) was from scratch. We are grateful to the reviewer for the above reference.) Define

(2.13)

G(z)=\int\limits_{0}^{4}\!\!\frac{e^{-zy}}{\sqrt{y(4-y)}}dy,

so that
I_{0}(2z)=\pi^{-1}\exp(2z)G(z)
, whence
|I_{0}(2z)|=\pi^{-1}|\exp(2z)|\cdot|G(z)|
. Here for
\Re z\geq 0

|G(z)|\leq\int_{0}^{4}\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{y(4-y)}}\,dy=\pi.

So, by (
2.12
),
|I_{0}(2z)|\leq\pi^{-1}|\exp(2z)|\pi=|\exp(2z)|
. Next,
let us show that for
g(z):=|G(z)|/G(|z|)
we have
\sup_{\mathbb{H}}g(z)<\infty
,
where
\mathbb{H}:=\{z:\Re z\geq 0\}
.
The function
g
is well-defined and continuous in
\mathbb{H}
, since
G(z)
is continuous in
\mathbb{H}
and
G(|z|)>0
. We are left with showing
\sup_{z\in\mathbb{H}:|z|\geq 1}g(z)<\infty
.
Noting that
G(\overline{z})=\overline{G(z)}
, it suffices to show this boundedness in the closed fourth quadrant
Q_{IV}
. For such
z
we have

(2.14)

G(z)=\int_{\mathcal{C}_{a}}\frac{e^{-zy}}{\sqrt{y(4-y)}}dy,

where
a>0
and
\mathcal{C}_{a}
is the oriented polygonal line joining the points
y_{1}=0,y_{2}=a\sqrt{i},y_{3}=a\sqrt{i}+4
, and
y_{4}=4
. Since
|e^{-zy}|\leq 1
in
\mathbb{H}
and
\min\{|y|,|4-y|\}\geq\Im y=2^{-1/2}a
, the integral over the segment
(y_{2},y_{3})
is bounded by

4\sqrt{2}/a
,
hence it goes to zero as
a\to\infty
. Passing to the limit
a\to\infty
, we thus get

(2.15)

\displaystyle G(z)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty e^{i\pi/4}}\frac{e^{-zy}}{\sqrt{y(4-y)}}dy-\int_{4}^{\infty e^{i\pi/4}+4}\frac{e^{-zy}}{\sqrt{y(4-y)}}dy

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty e^{i\pi/4}}\left(\frac{e^{-zu}}{\sqrt{u(4-u)}}-i\frac{e^{-4z-zu}}{\sqrt{(4+u)u}}\right)du.

In the last integral we have
|\sqrt{u(4-u)}|\geq 2^{3/4}\sqrt{|u|}
,
|\sqrt{u(4+u)}|\geq 2\sqrt{|u|}
,
|e^{-4z}|\leq 1
and (since
z\in Q_{IV}
and
\arg u=\frac{\pi}{4}
),
|e^{-zu}|\leq e^{-2^{-1/2}|z||u|}
. Since
2^{-3/4}+2^{-1}<2
, we get

(2.16)

|G(z)|\leq 2\int_{0}^{\infty}u^{-1/2}e^{-2^{-1/2}|z|u}\,du=2^{5/4}\sqrt{\pi}|z|^{-1/2}.

Importantly, we need this bound only for
|z|\geq 1
. For those
z
’s, we have

(2.17)

G(|z|)\geq\tfrac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{4}\frac{e^{-|z|y}}{\sqrt{y}}dy\geq\tfrac{1}{2}|z|^{-1/2}\int_{0}^{4}\eta^{-1/2}e^{-\eta}\,d\eta.\\

Combining (
2.16
) and (
2.17
), we see that
\sup_{z\in\mathbb{H}:|z|\geq 1}\tfrac{|G(z)|}{G(|z|)}<\infty
.
Therefore

|I_{0}(2z)|=O\bigl(|\exp(2z)|\cdot G(|z|)\bigr)=O\bigl(|\exp(2z)|\cdot|z|^{-1/2}\bigr),

uniformly for
z\in\{\mathbb{H}:|z|\geq 1\}
. This bound and the inequality
|I_{0}(2z)|\leq|\exp(2z)|
,
\Re z\geq 0
, complete
the proof of the lemma.
∎

2.1.
Proof of Theorem
1.1
, part
(a)

We use the lemma
2.1
to prove that, for

\delta>0,\quad K_{n}=\big\lfloor\exp\bigl((2+\delta)\sqrt{\log n\cdot\log(\log n)}\bigr)\big\rfloor,

we have
\sum_{k\geq K_{n}}m_{n}(k)\to 0
, where
m_{n}(k)
is the expected number of pairs of twins of size
k
.

We turn back to the integrand in (
2.10
). With
z_{s}=x_{s}e^{i\xi_{s}}
,
and
X_{j}:=\tfrac{x_{1}x_{2}^{j}}{(j!)^{2}}
, by Lemma
2.1
and (
2.7
), we have

(2.18)

\prod_{j\geq 0}\Big|H\Bigl(\tfrac{z_{1}z_{2}^{j}}{(j!)^{2}}\Bigr)\Big|\leq\prod_{j\geq 0}\tfrac{\exp(2X_{j}^{1/2})}{\max(1,\alpha|X_{j}|^{1/4})}\exp\Bigl(X_{j}\bigl(\cos(\xi_{1}+j\xi_{2})-1\bigr))\Bigr).

Let us look closely at
\{X_{j}\}
. Using
j!\leq\bigl(\tfrac{j+1}{2}\bigr)^{j}
,
j\geq 1
, we have

X_{j}\geq x_{1}\cdot\tfrac{x_{2}^{j}}{\bigl(\tfrac{j+1}{2}\bigr)^{2j}}=x_{1}\exp\Bigl(2j\log\tfrac{2x_{2}^{1/2}}{j+1}\Bigr)=x_{1}\exp\Bigl(2j\log\tfrac{2\tfrac{k-1}{k}}{j+1}\Bigr).

The function
\eta\log\tfrac{2\tfrac{k-1}{k}}{\eta+1}
is decreasing for
\eta\geq 1
. Pick
\varepsilon\in(0,1/2)
and introduce

J=\Big\lfloor\varepsilon\tfrac{\log x_{1}}{\log(\log x_{1})}\Big\rfloor
. Then, uniformly for
j\leq J
,

(2.19)

X_{j}\geq x_{1}\exp\Bigl(2J\log\tfrac{2\tfrac{k-1}{k}}{J+1}\Bigr)\\
=x_{1}\exp(-2J\log J+O(J))=\exp\bigl((1-2\varepsilon)\log x_{1}+o(\log x_{1})\bigr)\to\infty.

Further

\sum_{j=1}^{J-1}2j\log\tfrac{2\tfrac{k-1}{k}}{j+1}\geq 2\int_{1}^{J}\eta\log\tfrac{2\tfrac{k-1}{k}}{\eta+1}\,d\eta\geq-J^{2}\log J;

the last inequality follows easily from integration by parts.
Therefore

(2.20)

\displaystyle\prod_{j<J}X_{j}

\displaystyle\geq x_{1}^{J}\exp(-J^{2}\log J)\geq\exp\bigl[J(\log x_{1}-J\log J)\bigr]

\displaystyle\geq\exp\Bigl(\varepsilon^{\prime}(1-\varepsilon^{\prime})\tfrac{\log^{2}x_{1}}{\log(\log x_{1})}\Bigr)

\displaystyle=x_{1}^{\varepsilon^{\prime}(1-\varepsilon^{\prime})\tfrac{\log x_{1}}{\log(\log x_{1})}},\quad\forall\varepsilon^{\prime}<\varepsilon.

The point of this bound is that the product in question exceeds
x_{1}
raised to a sub-logarithmic power, still approaching infinity when
k
does.
It follows from (
2.20
) that

\prod_{j\leq J}\tfrac{\exp(2X_{j}^{1/2})}{\max(1,\alpha|X_{j}|^{1/4})}\leq\exp\Bigl(-\varepsilon^{\prime}(1-\varepsilon^{\prime})\tfrac{\log^{2}x_{1}}{4\log(\log x_{1})}+J\log\alpha\Bigr)\exp\Bigl(2\sum_{j\leq J}X_{j}^{1/2}\Bigr)\\
=\exp\Bigl(-\varepsilon^{\prime}(1-\varepsilon^{\prime})\tfrac{\log^{2}x_{1}}{4\log(\log x_{1})}+O(\varepsilon\log x_{1})\Bigr)\exp\Bigl(2\sum_{j<J}X_{j}^{1/2}\Bigr).

Since
\tfrac{\exp(2X_{j}^{1/2})}{\max(1,\alpha|X_{j}|^{1/4})}\leq\exp(2X_{j}^{1/2})
for all
j
, the above inequality implies that for
\varepsilon^{\prime\prime}<\varepsilon^{\prime}
,

(2.21)

\prod_{j\geq 0}\tfrac{\exp(2X_{j}^{1/2})}{\max(1,\alpha|X_{j}|^{1/4})}\leq\exp\Bigl(-\tfrac{\varepsilon^{\prime\prime}(1-\varepsilon^{\prime\prime})\log^{2}x_{1}}{4\log(\log x_{1})}\Bigr)\exp\Bigl(2\sum_{j\geq 0}X_{j}^{1/2}\Bigr)\\
=\exp\Bigl(-\tfrac{\varepsilon^{\prime\prime}(1-\varepsilon^{\prime\prime})\log^{2}x_{1}}{4\log(\log x_{1})}\Bigr)\exp\bigl(2x_{1}^{1/2}\exp(x_{2}^{1/2})\bigr)\\
\leq e^{2k}\cdot\exp\Bigl(-\tfrac{\varepsilon{{}^{\prime\prime}}(1-\varepsilon{{}^{\prime\prime}})\log^{2}k}{\log(\log k)}\Bigr),

as
x_{1}=k^{2}\exp\bigl(-\tfrac{2(k-1)}{k}\bigr)
,
x_{2}=\bigl(\tfrac{k-1}{k}\bigr)^{2}
.

Dropping the factors
\exp\Bigl(X_{j}\bigl(\cos(\xi_{1}+j\xi_{2})-1\bigr))\Bigr)(\leq 1)
with
j>1
in (
2.18
), and using
(
2.21
), we obtain

(2.22)

\prod_{j\geq 0}\Big|H\Bigl(\tfrac{z_{1}z_{2}^{j}}{(j!)^{2}}\Bigr)\Big|\leq e^{2k}\cdot\exp\Bigl(-\tfrac{\varepsilon{{}^{\prime\prime}}(1-\varepsilon^{{}^{\prime\prime}})\log^{2}k}{\log(\log k)}\Bigr)\\
\times\exp\Bigl(x_{1}(\cos\xi_{1}-1)+x_{1}x_{2}\bigl(\cos(\xi_{1}+\xi_{2})-1\bigr)\Bigr),\quad\xi_{j}\in(-\pi,\pi).

In order to use this last estimate on the right-hand side of (
2.10
), we need to find an upper bound for the
integral
of the exponential factor above.
Let
I=(-\pi,\pi)
. In order to obtain the needed upper bound, let us set

f(\xi_{1},\xi_{2}):=x_{1}(\cos(\xi_{1})-1)+x_{1}x_{2}(\cos(\xi_{1}+\xi_{2})-1).

Clearly,
f(\xi_{1},\xi_{2})\leq 0
, with equality holding if and only if

\cos(\xi_{1})=1
, and
\cos(\xi_{1}+\xi_{2})=1
, where
\xi_{i}\in I
,
or equivalently if and only if
\xi_{1}=\xi_{2}=0
.

For
\max\{|\xi_{1}|,|\xi_{2}|\}\geq\pi/2
, we have

(2.23)

f(\xi_{1},\xi_{2})\leq-ak^{2}<0,

for some positive constant
a
. For
\max\{|\xi_{1}|,|\xi_{2}|\}\leq\pi/2
,
using the Taylor expansion of
\cos
, we obtain

(2.24)

f(\xi_{1},\xi_{2})\leq-b[x_{1}\xi_{1}^{2}+x_{1}x_{2}(\xi_{1}+\xi_{2})^{2}]\leq-ck^{2}[\xi_{1}^{2}+\xi_{2}^{2}].

Combining (
2.23
) and (
2.24
), we get

(2.25)

f(\xi_{1},\xi_{2})\leq-dk^{2}[\xi_{1}^{2}+\xi_{2}^{2}],

for
\xi_{i}\in I
, for
i=1,2
, and some fixed constant
d>0
.

Therefore,

(2.26)

\int_{I\times I}\!\exp(f(\xi_{1},\xi_{2}))\ d\xi_{1}d\xi_{2}\\
\leq\int\limits_{I\times I}\!\!\exp[x_{1}(\cos(\xi_{1}-1)+x_{1}x_{2}(\cos(\xi_{1}+\xi_{2})-1)]\,d\xi_{1}d\xi_{2}\\
\leq\int\limits_{I\times I}\!\!\exp(-dk^{2}(\xi_{1}^{2}+\xi_{2}^{2}))\ d\xi_{1}d\xi_{2}\\
=\!\int\limits_{I}\!\exp(-dk^{2}\xi_{1}^{2})\,d\xi_{1}\cdot\int\limits_{I}\!\exp(-dk^{2}\xi_{2}^{2})\,d\xi_{2}\leq\frac{\pi}{dk^{2}}.

Putting together (
2.10
), (
2.22
) and the last inequality, we conclude that

N(k)=O\Bigl(\frac{(k!)^{4}e^{2k}}{x_{1}^{k}x_{2}^{k-1}k^{4}}\Bigr)\exp\Bigl(-\tfrac{\varepsilon{{}^{\prime\prime}}(1-\varepsilon{{}^{\prime\prime}})\log^{2}k}{\log(\log k)}\Bigr)\\
=O\Bigl(\tfrac{(k!)^{4}e^{4k}}{k^{2k+4}}\Bigr)\exp\Bigl(-\tfrac{\varepsilon{{}^{\prime\prime}}(1-\varepsilon{{}^{\prime\prime}})\log^{2}k}{\log(\log k)}\Bigr).

This bound and (
2.4
) imply that

S_{n}(k)=O\left(\binom{n}{k}\binom{n-k}{k}(n-2k)^{n-2k+1}N(k)\right)\\
=O\Bigl(\tfrac{n!(n-2k)^{n-2k+1}(k!)^{2}k^{-2k-4}e^{4k}}{(n-2k)!}\Bigr)\exp\Bigl(-\tfrac{\varepsilon{{}^{\prime\prime}}(1-\varepsilon{{}^{\prime\prime}})\log^{2}k}{\log(\log k)}\Bigr).\\

Using Stirling’s formula for factorials we obtain that

\displaystyle S_{n}(k)

\displaystyle=O\left(\binom{n}{k}\binom{n-k}{k}(n-2k)^{n-2k+1}N(k)\right)\exp\Bigl(-\tfrac{\varepsilon{{}^{\prime\prime}}(1-\varepsilon{{}^{\prime\prime}})\log^{2}k}{\log(\log k)}\Bigr)

\displaystyle=O\Bigl(\tfrac{n^{n+1}}{k^{3}}\Bigr)\exp\Bigl(-\tfrac{\varepsilon{{}^{\prime\prime}}(1-\varepsilon{{}^{\prime\prime}})\log^{2}k}{\log(\log k)}\Bigr).

Dividing this bound by the number
n^{n-1}
of all rooted Cayley trees, we get that the expected number of the pairs of twins of size
k
, i.e.
m_{n}(k)=\tfrac{S_{n}(k)}{n^{n-1}}
, is

(2.27)

m_{n}(k)=O\biggl(\tfrac{n^{2}}{k^{3}}\exp\Bigl(-\tfrac{\varepsilon{{}^{\prime\prime}}(1-\varepsilon{{}^{\prime\prime}})\log^{2}k}{\log(\log k)}\Bigr)\biggr),\quad\forall\varepsilon^{\prime\prime}<\varepsilon<1/2.

Since the series
\sum_{k\geq 1}k^{-3}
converges, it follows that for

k_{n}=\exp\Bigl((2+\delta)\sqrt{\log n\cdot\log(\log n)}\biggr)
and
\delta>0
we have

(2.28)

\sum_{k\geq k_{n}}m_{n}(k)=O\Bigl(n^{2}\exp\Bigl(-\tfrac{\varepsilon{{}^{\prime\prime}}(1-\varepsilon{{}^{\prime\prime}})\log^{2}k_{n}}{\log(\log k_{n})}\Bigr)\Bigr)\sum_{k\geq 1}k^{-3}\\
=O\exp\Bigl(2\bigl[(1-(2+\delta)^{2}\varepsilon^{\prime\prime}(1-\varepsilon^{\prime\prime}))\bigr]\log n+o(\log n)\Bigr),

uniformly for all
\varepsilon^{\prime\prime}<1/2
. Picking
\varepsilon^{\prime\prime}
sufficiently close to
1/2
, we conclude that

\lim_{n\to\infty}\sum_{k\geq k_{n}}m_{n}(k)=0
.

2.2.
Proof of Theorem
1.1
, part
(b)

The claim is: If
\delta\in\!(0,2)
,
k_{n}\!:=\!\Big\lfloor\!\exp\Bigl(\!(2-\delta)\sqrt{\log n\cdot\log(\log n)}\Bigr)\!\Big\rfloor
,
then
m_{n}(k_{n})\to\infty
.

Proof.

From here on we can, and will consider the twins of maximum out-degree
<d=d_{n}
, with
d_{n}
yet
to be chosen. Let us upper-bound the contribution to the RHS of (
2.10
) for
k=k_{n}
, coming from
(z_{1}=x_{1}e^{i\xi_{1}},z_{2}=x_{2}e^{i\xi_{2}})
, where

x_{1}=k^{2}\exp\bigl(-\tfrac{2(k-1)}{k}\bigr)
,
x_{2}=\bigl(\tfrac{k-1}{k}\bigr)^{2}
,
and also
\|\boldsymbol{\xi}\|\leq k^{-1/2+\varepsilon}
,
\varepsilon\in(0,1/2)
. By Lemma
2.1
,

\prod_{j<d}\Big|H\Bigl(\tfrac{z_{1}z_{2}^{j}}{(j!)^{2}}\Bigr)\Big|\leq\exp\biggl(2x_{1}^{1/2}\sum_{j\leq d}\tfrac{x_{2}^{j/2}}{j!}\Re\bigl(\exp(i(\xi_{1}/2+j\xi_{2}/2)\bigr)\biggr)\\
\leq\exp\biggl(2x_{1}^{1/2}\sum_{j<d}\tfrac{x_{2}^{j/2}}{j!}\Re\bigl(\exp(i(\xi_{1}/2+j\xi_{2}/2)\bigr)+O(x_{1}^{1/2}/d!)\biggr)\\
=\exp\bigl(2x_{1}^{1/2}W(\xi_{1},\xi_{2})+O(x_{1}^{1/2}x_{2}^{d/2}/d!)\bigr),

where

W(\xi_{1},\xi_{2}):=\cos\bigl(\tfrac{\xi_{1}}{2}+x_{2}^{1/2}\sin\tfrac{\xi_{2}}{2}\bigr)\exp\bigl(x_{2}^{1/2}\cos\tfrac{\xi_{2}}{2}\bigr).

Since
x_{1}=\Theta(k^{2})
,
x_{2}=\Theta(1)
,
the big-O term is
o(1)
, if
d=\lfloor 2\log k/\log\log k\rfloor
, which we assume from now.

Clearly,
W(\xi_{1},\xi_{2})\leq\exp(x_{2}^{1/2})=W(0,0)
, and it is easy to check that
(0,0)
is a single stationary point of

W(\xi_{1},\xi_{2})
in
[-\pi,\pi]^{2}
, whence it is a unique maximum point of
W(\xi_{1},\xi_{2})
in this square. In addition,

W^{\prime\prime}_{\xi_{1}}(0,0)=-\tfrac{e^{x_{2}^{1/2}}}{4},\,\,W^{\prime\prime}_{\xi_{2}}(0,0)=-\tfrac{(x_{2}+x_{2}^{1/2})e^{x_{2}^{1/2}}}{4},\,\,W^{\prime\prime}_{\xi_{1},\xi_{2}}(0,0)=-\tfrac{x_{2}^{1/2}e^{x_{2}^{1/2}}}{4},

so that
W^{\prime\prime}_{\xi_{1}}(0,0),W^{\prime\prime}_{\xi_{2}}(0,0)<0
, and

W^{\prime\prime}_{\xi_{1}}(0,0)\cdot W^{\prime\prime}_{\xi_{2}}(0,0)-\bigl(W^{\prime\prime}_{\xi_{1},\xi_{2}}(0,0)\bigr)^{2}=\tfrac{x_{2}^{1/2}e^{2x_{2}^{1/2}}}{16}>0.

This implies that
W(\xi_{1},\xi_{2})
is strictly concave in the vicinity of
(0,0)
, and moreover

W(\xi_{1},\xi_{2})\leq W(0,0)-\beta(\xi_{1}^{2}+\xi_{2}^{2})
,
\xi_{s}\in[-\pi,\pi]
, for a constant
\beta>0
as
k\to\infty
because

x_{2}=\Theta(1)
. So,

\prod_{j<d}\Big|H\Bigl(\tfrac{z_{1}z_{2}^{j}}{(j!)^{2}}\Bigr)\Big|\leq\exp\bigl(2x_{1}^{1/2}e^{x_{2}^{1/2}}-\Theta(k\|\boldsymbol{\xi}\|^{2})+o(1)\bigr).

Therefore

(2.29)

\int\limits_{\|\mathbb{\xi}\|\geq k^{-1/2+\varepsilon}}\tfrac{1}{z_{1}^{k+1}z_{2}^{k}}\prod_{j<d}H\Bigl(\tfrac{z_{1}z_{2}^{j}}{(j!)^{2}}\Bigr)\,dz_{1}dz_{2}\\
=O\biggl(\!\tfrac{\exp\bigl(2x_{1}^{1/2}e^{x_{2}^{1/2}}\bigr)}{x_{1}^{k}x_{2}^{k-1}}\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\int\limits_{\|\boldsymbol{\xi}\|\geq k^{-1/2+\varepsilon}}\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!e^{-\Theta(k\|\mathbb{\xi}\|^{2})}\,d\xi_{1}d\xi_{2}\!\biggr)=O\biggl(\!\tfrac{\exp\bigl(2k-\Theta(k^{2\varepsilon})\bigr)}{x_{1}^{k}x_{2}^{k-1}}\biggr).

It remains to sharply evaluate the contribution to the Cauchy integral coming from
\boldsymbol{\xi}
’s with
\|\boldsymbol{\xi}\|\leq k^{-1/2+\varepsilon}
. By Lemma
2.1
, we have

H(z)=\pi^{-1}\exp(2\sqrt{z})\int_{0}^{4}\tfrac{\exp(-\sqrt{z}y)}{\sqrt{y(4-y)}}\,dy,\quad\text{arg}(z)\in[-\pi,\pi].

And, for the values of
z_{1},z_{2}
, and
j
in question,
\text{arg}(z_{1}z_{2}^{j})=\xi_{1}+j\xi_{2}=O(k^{-1/2+\varepsilon}d)=o(1)
. Consequently,

(2.30)

\tfrac{1}{z_{1}^{k}z_{2}^{k-1}}\!\prod_{j<d}\!H\Bigl(\!\tfrac{z_{1}z_{2}^{j}}{(j!)^{2}}\!\Bigr)\!=\!\tfrac{\pi^{-d}}{z_{1}^{k}z_{2}^{k-1}}\prod_{j<d}\!\exp\bigl(\tfrac{2z_{1}^{1/2}z_{2}^{j/2}}{j!}\bigr)\!\!\int_{0}^{4}\!\tfrac{\exp\bigl(-\tfrac{z_{1}^{1/2}z_{2}^{j/2}}{j!}y_{j}\bigr)}{\sqrt{y_{j}(4-y_{j})}}\!dy_{j}.

Here

(2.31)

\tfrac{1}{z_{1}^{k}z_{2}^{k-1}}\prod_{j<d}\exp\bigl(\tfrac{2z_{1}^{1/2}z_{2}^{j/2}}{j!}\bigr)=\tfrac{\exp\bigl(2z_{1}^{1/2}e^{z_{2}^{1/2}}\bigr)+O(x_{2}^{d/2}/d!)}{z_{1}^{k}z_{2}^{k-1}},

and

2z_{1}^{1/2}e^{z_{2}^{1/2}}=2x_{1}^{1/2}\exp(i\xi_{1}/2+x_{2}^{1/2}e^{i\xi_{2}/2})\\
=2x_{1}^{1/2}e^{x_{2}^{1/2}}\exp\bigl(i\xi_{1}/2+x_{2}^{1/2}(e^{i\xi_{2}/2}-1)\bigr)\\
=2x_{1}^{1/2}e^{x_{2}^{1/2}}\bigl[1+(i\xi_{1}/2+x_{2}^{1/2}(e^{i\xi_{2}/2}-1))+\tfrac{1}{2}(i\xi_{1}/2+x_{2}^{1/2}(e^{i\xi_{2}/2}-1))^{2}+O(\|\boldsymbol{\xi}\|^{3})\bigr]\\
=2x_{1}^{1/2}e^{x_{2}^{1/2}}\bigl[1+i(\xi_{1}/2+x_{2}^{1/2}\xi_{2}/2)\\
-\xi_{1}^{2}/8-(x_{2}+x_{2}^{1/2})\xi_{2}^{2}/8-\xi_{1}\xi_{2}x_{2}^{1/2}/4+O(\|\boldsymbol{\xi}\|^{3})\bigr].

Therefore

2z_{1}^{1/2}e^{z_{2}^{1/2}}-k\log z_{1}-(k-1)\log z_{2}=2x_{1}^{1/2}e^{x_{2}^{1/2}}-k\log x_{1}-(k-1)\log x_{2}\\
+i\xi_{1}\bigl(x_{1}^{1/2}e^{x_{2}^{1/2}}-k\bigr)+i\xi_{2}(x_{1}^{1/2}e^{x_{2}^{1/2}}x_{2}^{1/2}-(k-1)\bigr)\\
-\tfrac{1}{4}x_{1}^{1/2}e^{x_{2}^{1/2}}\bigl(\xi_{1}^{2}+(x_{2}+x_{2}^{1/2})\xi_{2}^{2}-2\xi_{1}\xi_{2}x_{2}^{1/2}\bigr)+O(k\|\boldsymbol{\xi}\|^{3}).

Recalling that
\|\boldsymbol{\xi}\|\leq k^{-1/\varepsilon}
, we see that
O(k\|\boldsymbol{\xi}\|^{3})=O(k^{-1/2+3\varepsilon})\to 0
, if
\varepsilon<1/6
, which we assume from now. Furthermore, the linear combination of
i\xi_{1}
and
i\xi_{2}
in the above sum disappears, thanks to

x_{1}^{1/2}e^{x_{2}^{1/2}}-k=0,\quad x_{1}^{1/2}e^{x_{2}^{1/2}}x_{2}^{1/2}-(k-1)=0,

the conditions we were led to in our preliminary attempt to bound
N(k)
.
So, we have

2z_{1}^{1/2}e^{z_{2}^{1/2}}-k\log z_{1}-(k-1)\log z_{2}=2k-k\log x_{1}-(k-1)\log x_{2}\\
-\tfrac{k}{4}\bigl(\xi_{1}^{2}+(x_{2}+x_{2}^{1/2})\xi_{2}^{2}-2\xi_{1}\xi_{2}x_{2}^{1/2}\bigr)+O(k\|\boldsymbol{\xi}\|^{3}).

Therefore, equation (
2.31
) becomes

(2.32)

\tfrac{1}{z_{1}^{k}z_{2}^{k-1}}\prod_{j<d}\exp\bigl(\tfrac{2z_{1}^{1/2}z_{2}^{j/2}}{j!}\bigr)\\
=\tfrac{e^{2k}}{x_{1}^{k}x_{2}^{k-1}}\exp\bigl[-\tfrac{k}{4}\bigl(\xi_{1}^{2}+(x_{2}+x_{2}^{1/2})\xi_{2}^{2}-2\xi_{1}\xi_{2}x_{2}^{1/2}\bigr)+O(k\|\boldsymbol{\xi}\|^{3}+e^{-k})\bigr].\\

The quadratic form is negative definite, since
x_{2}=\Theta(1)
. We still have to evaluate

(2.33)

\displaystyle\prod_{j<d}\int_{0}^{4}\tfrac{\exp\bigl(-Z_{j}y_{j}\bigr)}{\sqrt{y_{j}(4-y_{j})}}\,dy_{j},

\displaystyle Z_{j}=X_{j}\exp(i(\xi_{1}/2+j\xi_{2}/2)),\quad X_{j}:=\tfrac{k\exp\bigl(-\tfrac{1}{2}\bigl(\tfrac{k-1}{k}\bigr)^{2}\bigr)}{j!}\bigl(\tfrac{k-1}{k}\bigr)^{j},

where
d=\lfloor 2\log k/\log\log k\rfloor
,
\|\boldsymbol{\xi}\|\leq k^{-1/2+\varepsilon}
,
\varepsilon<1/6
.

(a)
Suppose that
j<j(k):=\lfloor\tfrac{\log k}{\log\log k}\rfloor
. Then

\tfrac{k}{j!}\geq\frac{k}{j(k)!}=\exp\Bigl(\Theta\bigl(\tfrac{\log k\,\cdot\,\log\log\log k}{\log\log k}\bigr)\Bigr)\to\infty.

So, using

I_{0}(z)\sim\tfrac{e^{z}}{(2\pi z)^{1/2}}\bigl(1+O(z^{-1})\bigr),\quad|\text{arg}(z)|\leq\tfrac{\pi}{2}-\delta,\,\,\delta\in(0,\pi/2),

(see
[
6
]
), we have

\int_{0}^{4}\tfrac{\exp\bigl(-Z_{j}y_{j}\bigr)}{\sqrt{y_{j}(4-y_{j})}}\,dy_{j}=\pi\exp(-2Z_{j})I_{0}(2Z_{j})\\
=\pi\exp(-2Z_{j})\tfrac{e^{2Z_{j}}}{(4\pi Z_{j})^{1/2}}\bigl(1+O(Z_{j}^{-1})\bigr)=\tfrac{\pi}{(4\pi Z_{j})^{1/2}}\bigl(1+O(Z_{j}^{-1})\bigr)\\
=\tfrac{\pi}{(4\pi X_{j})^{1/2}}\cdot\bigl[1+O\bigl(\exp\bigl(-\Theta\bigl(\tfrac{\log k\,\cdot\,\log\log\log k}{\log\log k}\bigr)\bigr)\bigr)\bigr],

implying that

\displaystyle\prod_{j<j(k)}\int_{0}^{4}\tfrac{\exp\bigl(-Z_{j}y_{j}\bigr)}{\sqrt{y_{j}(4-y_{j})}}\,dy_{j}

\displaystyle=(1+O(\Delta_{k}))\prod_{j<j(k)}\tfrac{\pi}{(4\pi X_{j})^{1/2}},

\displaystyle\Delta_{k}

\displaystyle:=\tfrac{\log k}{\log\log k}\exp\bigl(-\Theta\bigl(\tfrac{\log k\,\cdot\,\log\log\log k}{\log\log k}\bigr)\bigr).

Here, by (
2.33
),
X_{j}\leq\tfrac{k}{j!}
. So, using Stirling’s formula with the remainder term, namely

j!=(2\pi j)^{1/2}\bigl(\tfrac{j}{e}\bigr)^{j}[1+O(1/(j+1))],

we obtain

(2.34)

\prod_{j<j(k)}\tfrac{\pi}{(4\pi X_{j})^{1/2}}=\exp[O(j(k)\log j(k))]\cdot k^{-j(k)/2}\prod_{j<j(k)}\bigl(\tfrac{j}{e}\bigr)^{j/2}\\
=\exp[O(j(k)\log j(k))]\cdot k^{-j(k)/2}\cdot\exp\biggl(\tfrac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{j(k)}\!\!\!\!x\log(x/e)\,dx\!\biggr)\\
=\exp\Bigl(-\tfrac{\log^{2}k}{4\log\log k}+O\bigl(\tfrac{\log^{2}k}{\log^{2}(\log k)}\bigr)\Bigr).

(b)
Suppose that
j\geq j(k)
. Then, since
j<d
,

\tfrac{k}{j!}\times\bigl|\exp(i(\xi_{1}/2+j\xi_{2}/2))-1\bigr|=O\Bigl(\tfrac{k^{1/2+\varepsilon}d}{(j(k)/e)^{j(k)}}\Bigr)\\
=O\bigl(\exp(-\Theta(1/2-\varepsilon)\log k)\bigr).\\

Here and below we use the notation
\Theta(1/2-\varepsilon)
as a shorthand for a quantity which is bounded below by
1/2-\varepsilon
times an absolute constant.
So,
we have

Z_{j}y_{j}=X_{j}y_{j}+O\bigl(d\|\boldsymbol{\xi}\|X_{j}y_{j}\bigr).

Hence

\sum_{j(k)\leq j<d}Z_{j}y_{j}=\sum_{j(k)\leq j\leq d}X_{j}y_{j}+O\bigl(d^{2}\|\boldsymbol{\xi}\|\tfrac{k}{j(k)!}\bigr)\\
=\sum_{j(k)\leq j<d}X_{j}y_{j}+O\bigl(\exp(-\Theta(1/2-\varepsilon)\log k)\bigr),

implying that

\prod_{j(k)\leq j<d}\int_{0}^{4}\tfrac{\exp\bigl(-Z_{j}y_{j}\bigr)}{\sqrt{y_{j}(4-y_{j})}}\,dy_{j}\\
=\prod_{j(k)\leq j<d}\int_{0}^{4}\tfrac{\exp\bigl(-X_{j}y_{j}\bigr)}{\sqrt{y_{j}(4-y_{j})}}\,dy_{j}\cdot\bigl[1+O\bigl(\exp(-\Theta(1/2-\varepsilon)\log k)\bigr)\bigr].

Here, using convexity of the exponential function,

\pi^{-1}\int_{0}^{4}\tfrac{\exp\bigl(-X_{j}y_{j}\bigr)}{\sqrt{y_{j}(4-y_{j})}}\,dy_{j}\geq\exp\biggl(-\tfrac{X_{j}}{\pi}\int_{0}^{4}\sqrt{\tfrac{y}{4-y}}\,dy\biggr)=e^{-2X_{j}}.\\

So,

(2.35)

\prod_{j(k)\leq j<d}\pi^{-1}\int_{0}^{4}\tfrac{\exp\bigl(-X_{j}y_{j}\bigr)}{\sqrt{y_{j}(4-y_{j})}}\,dy_{j}\geq\exp\Bigl(-2\sum_{j\geq j(k)}X_{j}\Bigr)\\
=\exp\bigl(O(k/j(k)!)\bigr)=k^{O\bigl(\tfrac{\log\log\log k}{\log\log k}\bigr)}.

Combining (
2.34
) and (
2.35
) we arrive at

(2.36)

\prod_{j<d}\int_{0}^{4}\tfrac{\exp\bigl(-Z_{j}y_{j}\bigr)}{\sqrt{y_{j}(4-y_{j})}}\,dy_{j}=\exp\Bigl(-\tfrac{\log^{2}k}{4\log\log k}+O\bigl(\tfrac{\log^{2}k}{\log^{2}(\log k)}\bigr)\Bigr).\\

Thus, equations (
2.32
) and (
2.36
) transform equation (
2.30
) into

(2.37)

\tfrac{1}{z_{1}^{k}z_{2}^{k-1}}\prod_{j<d}H\Bigl(\!\tfrac{z_{1}z_{2}^{j}}{(j!)^{2}}\Bigr)=\tfrac{e^{2k}}{x_{1}^{k}x_{2}^{k-1}}\exp\Bigl(-\tfrac{\log^{2}k}{4\log\log k}+O\bigl(\tfrac{\log^{2}k}{\log^{2}(\log k)}\bigr)\Bigr)\\
\times\exp\bigl[-\tfrac{k}{4}\bigl(\xi_{1}^{2}+(x_{2}+x_{2}^{1/2})\xi_{2}^{2}-2\xi_{1}\xi_{2}x_{2}^{1/2}\bigr)\bigr],\\

uniformly for
\boldsymbol{\xi}
with
\|\boldsymbol{\xi}\|\leq k^{-1/2+\varepsilon}
,
\varepsilon\in(0,1/6)
.

By (
2.37
), the contribution to the Cauchy integral on the RHS of (
2.9
) is

\tfrac{e^{2k}}{x_{1}^{k}x_{2}^{k-1}}\exp\Bigl(-\tfrac{\log^{2}k}{4\log\log k}+O\bigl(\tfrac{\log^{2}k}{\log^{2}(\log k)}\bigr)\Bigr)\\
\times\int_{\|\boldsymbol{\xi}\|\leq k^{-1/2+\varepsilon}}\exp\bigl[-\tfrac{k}{4}\bigl(\xi_{1}^{2}+(x_{2}+x_{2}^{1/2})\xi_{2}^{2}-2\xi_{1}\xi_{2}x_{2}^{1/2}\bigr)\bigr]\,d\xi_{1}d\xi_{2}\\
=\tfrac{e^{2k}}{x_{1}^{k}x_{2}^{k-1}}\exp\Bigl(-\tfrac{\log^{2}k}{4\log\log k}+O\bigl(\tfrac{\log^{2}k}{\log^{2}(\log k)}\bigr)\Bigr).\\

In combination with (
2.29
), this yields that

\tfrac{1}{(2\pi i)^{2}}\!\!\oint\limits_{C_{1}\times C_{2}}\!\prod_{j\geq 0}\tfrac{1}{z_{1}^{k+1}z_{2}^{k}}H\Bigl(\tfrac{z_{1}z_{2}^{j}}{(j!)^{2}}\Bigr)\,dz_{1}dz_{2}\!=\!\tfrac{e^{4k}}{x_{1}^{k}x_{2}^{k-1}}\exp\Bigl(\!-\tfrac{\log^{2}k}{4\log\log k}+O\bigl(\tfrac{\log^{2}k}{\log^{2}(\log k)}\bigr)\!\Bigr)\\
=\tfrac{e^{4k}}{k^{2k}}\exp\Bigl(\!-\tfrac{\log^{2}k}{4\log\log k}+O\bigl(\tfrac{\log^{2}k}{\log^{2}(\log k)}\bigr)\!\Bigr).

Therefore, by (
2.9
),
N(k)
(the total number of pairs of twin trees, each with
k
vertices, and maximum degree

\lfloor 2\log k/\log\log k\rfloor
is given by

N(k)=(k!)^{4}\cdot\tfrac{e^{4k}}{k^{2k}}\exp\Bigl(\!-\tfrac{\log^{2}k}{4\log\log k}+O\bigl(\tfrac{\log^{2}k}{\log^{2}(\log k)}\bigr)\!\Bigr).

Combining this with (
2.4
), we obtain that the expected number of twin trees of size
k=o(n)
in the random Cayley tree
with
n
vertices is at least

\tfrac{1}{n^{n-1}}\binom{n}{k}\binom{n-k}{k}(n-2k)^{n-2k+1}\cdot N(k)\\
=n^{2}\exp\Bigl(-\tfrac{\log^{2}k}{4\log\log k}+O\bigl(\tfrac{\log^{2}k}{\log^{2}(\log k)}\bigr)\!\Bigr).

(Observe that this lower bound depends on
k
almost like the upper bound (
2.27
), since
\varepsilon^{\prime\prime}
can be chosen arbitrarily close to
1/2
from
below
.) And the lower bound diverges to infinity if

k\leq\Big\lfloor\!\exp\Bigl((2-\delta)\sqrt{\log n\cdot\log(\log n)}\Bigr)\Big\rfloor.

∎
