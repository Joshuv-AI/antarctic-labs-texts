Deformations of some local Calabi–Yau manifolds
Robert Friedman, Radu Laza

Abstract




Abstract.
We study deformations of certain crepant resolutions of isolated rational Gorenstein singularities. After a general discussion of the deformation theory, we specialize to dimension
3
and consider examples which are good (log) resolutions as well as the case of small resolutions. We obtain some partial results on the classification of canonical threefold singularities that admit good crepant resolutions. Finally, we study a noncrepant example, the blowup of a small resolution whose exceptional set is a smooth curve.

Keywords.
Deformation of singularities, canonical 3-fold singularities, Calabi–Yau varieties

1. Introduction

This paper is part of a series
[
FL22
,
FL24
,
FL23
]
investigating the deformation theory of singular Calabi–Yau varieties,
i.e.
compact analytic spaces
Y
with isolated Gorenstein canonical singularities such that
\omega_{Y}\cong\mathcal{O}_{Y}
, building on and generalizing previously known results due to Kawamata, Namikawa, Namikawa–Steenbrink, and others; see
[
Fri86
,
Kaw92
,
NS95
,
Nam94
,
Nam02
]
. The deformation theory of
Y
as studied in
[
FL22
]
has both a local and a global aspect. Locally, if
x\in Y
is a singular point, we can study the deformation functor
\mathbf{Def}_{(Y,x)}
of the germ
(Y,x)
. In particular, the tangent space to this functor, or equivalently the “first-order deformations,”
i.e.
the deformations over the dual numbers
\operatorname{Spec}\mathbb{C}[\varepsilon]
, are classified by a finite-dimensional vector space
T^{1}_{Y,x}
, or equivalently by the corresponding skyscraper sheaf supported at
x
. Globally, for a compact analytic space
Y
, there is the deformation functor
\mathbf{Def}_{Y}
, whose tangent space is a finite-dimensional vector space that we denote by
\mathbb{T}^{1}_{Y}
. There is a corresponding sheaf
T^{1}_{Y}
, which is supported on the singular locus of
Y
. In case the singularities of
Y
are isolated,
\mathbb{T}^{1}_{Y}
is a skyscraper sheaf supported at the singular points and the stalk of
\mathbb{T}^{1}_{Y}
at
x
is the vector space
T^{1}_{Y,x}
. There is a natural morphism of deformation functors
\mathbf{Def}_{Y}\to\prodop\displaylimits_{x\in Y_{\text{\rm{sing}}}}\mathbf{Def}_{(Y,x)}
. Note that
\mathbf{Def}_{Y}
and
\mathbf{Def}_{(Y,x)}
are pro-represented by germs of analytic spaces and the morphism of functors corresponds to a morphism of germs of analytic spaces. On Zariski tangent spaces, the differential of this morphism of functors or germs of spaces becomes a homomorphism
\mathbb{T}^{1}_{Y}\to H^{0}(Y;T^{1}_{Y})=\bigoplusop\displaylimits_{x\in Y_{\text{\rm{sing}}}}T^{1}_{Y,x}
. For
\dim Y\geq 3
, this morphism is almost never surjective. It is thus important to identify interesting tangent directions in
\bigoplusop\displaylimits_{x\in Y_{\text{\rm{sing}}}}T^{1}_{Y,x}
and try to lift these to
\mathbb{T}^{1}_{Y}
. If the deformations of
Y
are unobstructed, such first-order deformations of
Y
will come from actual deformations.

Taking a local point of view, let
(X,x)
be the germ of an isolated Gorenstein canonical singularity (or equivalently an isolated rational Gorenstein singularity; see
[
KM98
]
,
[
Kol97
, Section 11.1]
). We will usually take
X
to be a
good Stein representative
for the germ
(X,x)
, i.e. a contractible Stein representative with a unique singular point
x
. Let
\pi\colon\widehat{X}\to X
be a
good resolution
or a
log resolution
, i.e.
\pi^{-1}(x)=E
is a divisor with simple normal crossings. The assumption of Gorenstein canonical singularities means that
K_{\widehat{X}}=\pi^{*}\omega_{X}\otimes\mathcal{O}_{\widehat{X}}(D)
for some effective divisor
D
on
\widehat{X}
. If
D=0
, we say that
\pi
is a
good crepant resolution
of
X
. Typical examples of singularities that admit good crepant resolutions are the
O_{16}
singularities, i.e. affine cones over a smooth cubic surface in
\mathbb{P}^{3}
. More general examples are singularities which are analytically isomorphic to cones over Fano manifolds embedded via the anticanonical line bundle, for example the cone over a smooth hypersurface of degree
n
in
\mathbb{P}^{n}
. A related case is that where there exists a
small resolution

\pi^{\prime}\colon X^{\prime}\to X
, i.e. a resolution where the fiber of
\pi^{\prime}
over
x
has dimension
1
(more generally, one could also consider the case where the exceptional set has dimension less that
\dim X-1
). However, at least when
X
is a local complete intersection and
\dim X\geq 3
, such resolutions can only exist for
\dim X=3
(see Remark
5.1
). Note that small resolutions
\pi^{\prime}\colon X^{\prime}\to X
are automatically crepant, in the sense that
K_{X^{\prime}}\cong\mathcal{O}_{X^{\prime}}
. Examples of such singularities are
A_{2k-1}
singularities in dimension
3
: these are locally defined by the equation
x^{2}+y^{2}+z^{2}+w^{2k}
. A singularity
X
admitting a small resolution is terminal but not (locally)
\mathbb{Q}
-factorial. However, most canonical singularities do not admit good crepant or small resolutions. For instance, terminal
\mathbb{Q}
-factorial singularities, such as the
A_{2k}
singularities in dimension
3
(
i.e.
those given by
x^{2}+y^{2}+z^{2}+w^{2k+1}
,
k\geq 1
), do not have either a good crepant or a small resolution.

There is a natural subspace of
H^{0}(X;T^{1}_{X})
defined as follows: Let
U=X-\{x\}=\widehat{X}-E
, where as above
\pi\colon\widehat{X}\to X
is a good resolution and
E=\pi^{-1}(x)
. In case
\dim X\geq 3
, a theorem of Schlessinger implies that
H^{0}(X;T^{1}_{X})\cong H^{1}(U;T_{U})
, where
T_{U}
is the tangent bundle of
U
; see
[
Sch71
, Theorem 2]
. By Wahl’s theory, see
[
Wah76
]
, there is a morphism of functors
\mathbf{Def}_{\widehat{X}}\to\mathbf{Def}_{X}
where the induced map on tangent spaces is the natural restriction map

H^{1}\left(\widehat{X};T_{\widehat{X}}\right)\longrightarrow H^{1}\left(U;T_{U}\right)\cong H^{0}\left(X;T^{1}_{X}\right).

Informally, we can think of the image of
\mathbf{Def}_{\widehat{X}}
as the
simultaneous resolution locus
, i.e. as the deformations of
X
which lift to deformations of the resolution
\widehat{X}
. If
\pi\colon\widehat{X}\to X
is a good crepant resolution, then
\mathbf{Def}_{\widehat{X}}
is unobstructed by a theorem of Gross;
cf.

[
Gro97
, Proposition 3.4]
. If
\pi^{\prime}\colon X^{\prime}\to X
is a small resolution, then
\mathbf{Def}_{X^{\prime}}
is unobstructed by
[
Fri86
, Proposition 2.1]
. If
X
is a local complete intersection singularity, then
\mathbf{Def}_{X}
is always unobstructed (
cf.

[
Loo84
, Section 6]
).

In trying to understand the image of the morphism
\mathbf{Def}_{\widehat{X}}\to\mathbf{Def}_{X}
, and more concretely the image of
H^{1}(\widehat{X};T_{\widehat{X}})
in
H^{0}(X;T^{1}_{X})
, and their relevance in the Calabi–Yau case, there are two major obstacles:

(i)

In the local setting, the image of
H^{1}(\widehat{X};T_{\widehat{X}})
in
H^{0}(X;T^{1}_{X})
is not a birational invariant, i.e. is not independent of the choice of a good resolution. The possible naturally occurring birationally invariant subspaces of
H^{0}(X;T^{1}_{X})
are rather the images of
H^{1}(\widehat{X};{}^{n-1}_{\widehat{X}}(\log E))
or
H^{1}(\widehat{X};{}^{n-1}_{\widehat{X}}(\log E)(-E))
. These images are studied in
[
FL22
, Theorem 2.1(iii)]
, where we prove that the image of
H^{1}(\widehat{X};{}^{n-1}_{\widehat{X}})
is the same as that of
H^{1}(\widehat{X};{}^{n-1}_{\widehat{X}}(\log E)(-E))
, at least in the local complete intersection case or if
\dim X=3
, and is thus independent of the choice of resolution. However, this image does not seem to have an obvious deformation-theoretic interpretation. One important case where such an interpretation exists is when
\widehat{X}
is a good crepant resolution of
X
: In this case
{}^{n-1}_{\widehat{X}}\cong T_{\widehat{X}}
, and hence the image of
H^{1}(\widehat{X};{}^{n-1}_{\widehat{X}}(\log E)(-E))
agrees with that of
H^{1}(\widehat{X};T_{\widehat{X}})
. A similar result holds in the case of a small resolution (Proposition
5.13
(
i
)).

(ii)

In the global setting, where
Y
is a Calabi–Yau variety, it seems difficult to lift deformations arising in this manner to global deformations of
Y
. For example, in dimension
3
and for small resolutions
Y^{\prime}
, this issue is connected with Clemens’ conjecture about smooth rational curves in
Y^{\prime}
in the case where
Y^{\prime}
is a quintic threefold, see
[
Cle87
]
, which is still open and where we have nothing new to add. For another example, the deformations of a quintic threefold
Y
with an
O_{16}
singularity
x\in Y
are versal for the deformations of the isolated singularity at
x
;
i.e.
the map
\mathbb{T}^{1}_{Y}\to H^{0}(Y;T^{1}_{Y})
is surjective. Hence, if
\widehat{Y}\to Y
is the natural crepant resolution and
X
is a good Stein representative of the germ
(Y,x)
with crepant resolution
\widehat{X}
, the image of
\mathbb{T}^{1}_{Y}\to H^{0}(Y;T^{1}_{Y})
contains the image of
H^{1}(\widehat{X};T_{\widehat{X}})
. However, the analogous result fails for hypersurfaces of degree
n+2
in
\mathbb{P}^{n+1}
containing an isolated singularity isomorphic to the cone over a hypersurface of degree
n
in
\mathbb{P}^{n}
for
n\geq 4
, and in this case “most” of the image of
H^{1}(\widehat{X};T_{\widehat{X}})
fails to lift to
\mathbb{T}^{1}_{Y}
. Thus, the strategy of
[
FL22
]
is to work modulo the deformations induced from a resolution, i.e. with
K=\operatorname{Coker}\left(H^{1}(\widehat{X};{}^{n-1}_{\widehat{X}}(\log E)(-E))\to H^{0}(X;T^{1}_{X})\right)\cong H^{2}_{E}(\widehat{X};{}^{n-1}_{\widehat{X}}(\log E))
(see
[
FL22
, Theorem 2.1(v)]
). This has the virtue of globalizing well in the Calabi–Yau and Fano case; see
[
FL22
, Sections 4 and 5]
.

Nonetheless, the study of
\mathbf{Def}_{\widehat{X}}
and of
H^{1}(\widehat{X};T_{\widehat{X}})
involves a lot of interesting geometry, and the goal of this paper is to investigate some of this geometry. For the reasons outlined above, we restrict to the local case and (mostly) either to the good crepant case or to the case of a small resolution.
Finally, for most of our results, we restrict to dimension
n=3
. For a general
n\geq 3
, by a theorem of Reid
[
Rei80
, Theorem 2.6]
, a canonical Gorenstein singularity, not necessarily admitting a crepant resolution, can be realized as the total space of a one-parameter deformation of a Gorenstein rational or elliptic singularity of dimension
n-1
. In dimension
2
, Gorenstein rational singularities are rational double points, and this case does not arise if
\widehat{X}
is a good crepant resolution. As for Gorenstein elliptic (minimally elliptic) singularities, the most well-studied classes of such singularities are the simple elliptic and cusp singularities, and their deformation theory has been studied extensively. In particular, as discussed below, semistable models for deformations of cusp singularities are a plentiful source of examples of good crepant resolutions and can be obtained systematically by the methods of
[
FM83
,
Eng18
,
EF21
]
.

The contents of this paper are as follows. Section
2
analyzes the deformation theory of good crepant resolutions
\pi\colon\widehat{X}\to X
, where
\dim X\geq 3
. The main result is Theorem
2.6
, which gives some very general results about the first-order deformations of
\widehat{X}
and their relation to first-order deformations of
E
. In this case, the tangent space to the deformation functor
\mathbf{Def}_{E}
is the vector space
\mathbb{T}^{1}_{E}
, and there is a corresponding sheaf
T^{1}_{E}
. Then
Theorem
2.6
relates the tangent space to
\mathbf{Def}_{\widehat{X}}
and the corresponding obstruction space to the cohomology of the exceptional divisor
E
and its components
E_{i}
. Among other things, we show the following.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
\pi\colon\widehat{X}\to X
be a good crepant resolution of the isolated rational singularity
X
, with
n=\dim X\geq 3
, and let
E=\bigcupop\displaylimits_{i=1}^{r}E_{i}
be the exceptional divisor of
\pi
. Then the maps
\mathbb{T}^{1}_{E}\to H^{0}(E;T^{1}_{E})
and
H^{1}(\widehat{X};T_{\widehat{X}})\to H^{0}(E;T^{1}_{E})
are surjective. In particular, all first-order smoothing directions for
E
are realized via first-order deformations of
\widehat{X}
. The map
H^{1}(\widehat{X};T_{\widehat{X}})\to\mathbb{T}^{1}_{E}
is surjective if and only if
H^{2}(\widehat{X};T_{\widehat{X}}(-E))=0
. In this case, all first-order locally trivial deformations of
E
are realized by first-order deformations of
\widehat{X}
and the divisors
E_{1},\dots,E_{r}
.

More precise results require better control of the structure of
E
, which in turn leads to restricting to the case
\dim X=3
, the running assumption starting with Section
3
.

In dimension
2
, Gorenstein canonical singularities are du Val singularities, also called rational double point (RDP), simple, or ADE singularities. Their structure is well understood, as is their deformation theory. The purpose of Sections
3
and
4
is to give a partial classification of the isolated Gorenstein canonical threefold singularities
(X,x)
which admit good crepant resolutions,
3
-dimensional analogues of the ADE case, and to discuss their associated deformation theory. As noted above, there is a close connection between isolated threefold canonical Gorenstein singularities and one-parameter smoothings of minimally elliptic singularities. In the case of simple elliptic and cusp singularities, such smoothings are in turn closely related to degenerations
of
K3
surfaces (
cf.
for example
[
FM83
]
). This leads us to define
divisors of Type II, Type III
1
, and Type III
2
(Definition
3.6
and Figures
1
,
2
,
3
). We then obtain a partial classification of the threefold singularities admitting good crepant resolutions in the special case of the total space of a one-parameter smoothing of a simple elliptic or cusp singularity, as follows.

Theorem 1.2

(= Theorems
4.1
and
4.6
)
.

Let
(X,x)
be an isolated Gorenstein canonical singularity of dimension
3
with a good crepant resolution
\pi\colon\widehat{X}\to X
, and let
E=\pi^{-1}(x)
be the reduced exceptional divisor.

(i)

If the general hypersurface section of
X
passing through
x
is a simple elliptic singularity, then
E
is of Type II.

(ii)

If the general hypersurface section of
X
passing through
x
is a cusp and
\omega_{E}^{-1}
is nef and big, then
E
is of Type III
1
or Type III
2
.

(ii)
′

If the general hypersurface section
S
of
X
passing through
x
is a cusp and the full inverse image
\pi^{-1}(S)
has normal crossings, then after a sequence of flops
(
elementary modifications of type 2
)
,
\omega_{E}^{-1}
becomes nef and big, hence

is of Type III
1
or Type III
2
.

Combining Theorem
1.2
with Theorem
2.6
, we are able to obtain a deeper understanding of various deformation-theoretic invariants, especially in the Type II and Type III
1
cases. In particular, the following is a somewhat less precise formulation of Proposition
3.12
, Proposition
3.15
, and Remark
3.16
.

Theorem 1.3
.

If
E
is of Type II and irreducible or if
E
is of Type III
1
, then
H^{2}(\widehat{X};T_{\widehat{X}}(-E))=0
and hence the map
H^{1}(\widehat{X};T_{\widehat{X}})\to\mathbb{T}^{1}_{E}
is surjective. However, if
E
is of Type II and reducible, then the map
H^{1}(\widehat{X};T_{\widehat{X}})\to\mathbb{T}^{1}_{E}
is never surjective, and if
E
is of Type III
2
, then the map
H^{1}(\widehat{X};T_{\widehat{X}})\to\mathbb{T}^{1}_{E}
is not in general surjective.

In Section
5
, we switch our attention to the case of singularities which admit small resolutions
p\colon X^{\prime}\to X
, technically a much simpler case. Here, we relate
H^{1}(X^{\prime};T_{X^{\prime}})
to the birational invariants
H^{1}(\widehat{X};{}^{2}_{\widehat{X}}(\log E)(-E))
,
H^{1}(\widehat{X};{}^{2}_{\widehat{X}})
, and

arising from a good resolution; these invariants are controlled by the Du Bois invariants

and link invariants

introduced by Steenbrink; see
[

. In particular, we recover some results of Steenbrink regarding the dimension of the versal deformation spaces for such singularities (Remark

) and discuss some interesting examples (Examples

). After the first version of this paper was posted, Sz-Sheng Wang sent us a preprint (now
[

]
) which has substantial overlap with the material in Section
5
.

In the final Section
6
, we discuss a noncrepant example of a very special type, the blowup
\widehat{X}
of a smooth curve
C
which is the exceptional set of a small resolution
\pi\colon X^{\prime}\to X
. In this case,
X
is a threefold
A_{2n-1}
singularity,
i.e.
defined locally by the equation
x^{2}+y^{2}+z^{2}+w^{2n}
, where we assume
n\geq 2
. The question here is to relate the deformations of
\widehat{X}
to those of
X^{\prime}
and
X
. In particular, we show the following (Theorem

).

Theorem 1.4
.

For the above example, let
(S_{\widehat{X}},0)
and
(S_{X^{\prime}},0)
be the germs prorepresenting the functors
\mathbf{Def}_{\widehat{X}}
and

, respectively. Then the induced morphism

is finite of degree

, and its differential at the origin has a

This kind of example is also relevant to the study of deformations of
\mathbb{Q}
-factorial terminal threefold singularities such as the

singularities in dimension
3
. While this example is very specific, it helps to illustrate the difference between the image of
H^{1}(\widehat{X};T_{\widehat{X}})
and the birationally invariant image of

. It is also interesting from the perspective of the minimal model program. Generally speaking, the analysis of this paper shadows the steps of the minimal program. Namely, Sections
2
–

roughly parallel the fact that a canonical threefold singularity has a partial crepant (divisorial) resolution with terminal singularities (
cf.

[

we only consider crepant partial resolutions of a canonical singularity which are smooth, not just terminal. 2. Deformation theory in the good crepant case

We begin with a general definition.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
\pi\colon\widehat{X}\to X
be a resolution of
X
. Then
\pi
is
equivariant
if
R^{0}\pi_{*}T_{\widehat{X}}\cong T^{0}_{X}
. By
e.g.

[
Fri86
, Lemma 3.1]
, a small resolution is equivariant. Note that, for
q>0
,
R^{q}\pi_{*}T_{\widehat{X}}
is a torsion sheaf supported on
x
.

The resolution
\pi\colon\widehat{X}\to X
is

(i)

a
good resolution
(sometimes called a
log resolution
) if
\pi^{-1}(x)=E
is a divisor with simple normal crossings;

(ii)

a
good equivariant resolution
if
\pi
is good and equivariant (by resolution of singularities, good equivariant resolutions always exist);

(iii)

crepant
if
K_{\widehat{X}}=\pi^{*}\omega_{X}
and hence
K_{\widehat{X}}\cong\mathcal{O}_{\widehat{X}}
, and is a
good crepant resolution
if
\pi
is also a good resolution. Thus, with these definitions, a small resolution is crepant but not a good crepant resolution. (Note: If the isolated normal singularity
(X,x)
admits a not necessarily good resolution
\pi\colon\widehat{X}\to X
with
K_{\widehat{X}}\cong\mathcal{O}_{\widehat{X}}
, then
(X,x)
is automatically a rational Gorenstein singularity;
cf.

[
Kol97
, Corollary 11.9]
).

Given a good resolution
\pi\colon\widehat{X}\to X
, let
E=\pi^{-1}(x)=\bigcupop\displaylimits_{i=1}^{r}E_{i}
, where the
E_{i}
are smooth divisors in
\widehat{X}
.

Proposition 2.2
.

A crepant resolution is equivariant.

Proof.

We begin by showing the following.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
\pi\colon\widehat{X}\to X
be a crepant resolution, and let
\pi^{\prime}\colon X^{\prime}\to X
be an arbitrary resolution. Then there exist a closed analytic subset
\widehat{V}
of
\widehat{X}
of codimension at least
2
and a proper analytic subset
V^{\prime}
of
X^{\prime}
such that the birational map
X^{\prime}\dashrightarrow\widehat{X}
restricts on
X^{\prime}-V^{\prime}
to a surjective morphism
\nu\colon X^{\prime}-V^{\prime}\to\widehat{X}-\widehat{V}
.

Proof.

By Hironaka’s theorem, there is a blowup
f\colon\widetilde{X}\to\widehat{X}
which dominates
X^{\prime}
. We can further assume that all centers of blowups lie over the inverse image of the singular point
x
. Let
\widehat{V}
be the image in
\widehat{X}
of the centers of the blowups; hence
f
is an isomorphism from
\widetilde{X}-f^{-1}(\widehat{V})
to
\widehat{X}-V
. Moreover,
K_{\widetilde{X}}=\mathcal{O}_{\widetilde{X}}(G)
, where
G=\sumop\displaylimits_{i}n_{i}G_{i}
is a divisor with
n_{i}>0
for all
i
such that
f(G)\subseteq\widehat{V}
. Since
K_{X^{\prime}}\cong\mathcal{O}_{X^{\prime}}(D)
for an effective divisor
D
whose image in
X
is the point
x
, it follows easily that all of the exceptional divisors for the morphism
\widetilde{X}\to X^{\prime}
are of the form
G_{i}
for some
i
. Thus, if
V^{\prime}
is the closure of the union of the images of the
G_{i}
which are exceptional for the morphism
\widetilde{X}\to X^{\prime}
, there is a surjective morphism
X^{\prime}-V^{\prime}\to\widehat{X}-\widehat{V}
.
∎

Remark 2.4
.

The argument of Lemma
2.3
proves the standard fact that if in addition
X^{\prime}
is also a crepant resolution of
X
, then
X^{\prime}
and
\widehat{X}
are isomorphic outside a set of codimension
2
.

Continuing with the proof of Proposition
2.2
, we must show that the natural injective map
R^{0}\pi_{*}T_{\widehat{X}}\to T^{0}_{X}
is surjective. Choose an equivariant resolution
\pi^{\prime}\colon X^{\prime}\to X
. If
\xi
is a local section of
T^{0}_{X}
, then
\xi
lifts to a section of
T_{X^{\prime}}
over the inverse image of the open set and thus defines a section of
T_{\widehat{X}}
over the complement of
V
in the notation of Lemma
2.3
. By Hartogs, this section extends to a section

With these preliminaries, we turn now to the main result of this section.

Theorem 2.6
.

Let
\pi\colon\widehat{X}\to X
be a good crepant resolution of the isolated rational singularity
X
, with
n=\dim X\geq 3
.

(i)

We have
\mathbb{H}^{1}(\widehat{X};\mathcal{D}^{\bullet})\cong H^{1}(\widehat{X};T_{\widehat{X}})
. In other words, the first-order deformations of
\widehat{X}
are exactly the first-order deformations keeping the effective divisor
E
.

(ii)

We have
H^{0}(E;T^{1}_{E})\cong\bigoplusop\displaylimits_{i}H^{1}(E_{i};N_{E_{i}/\widehat{X}})=\bigoplusop\displaylimits_{i}H^{1}(E_{i};\omega_{E_{i}})
. Thus
H^{0}(E;T^{1}_{E})=0
if and only if
h^{0,n-2}(E_{i})=0
for every
i
.
(
The condition
H^{0}(E;T^{1}_{E})=0
is the condition that all first-order deformations of
\widehat{X}
induce locally trivial first-order deformations of
E
.
)
More generally,
\dim H^{0}(E;T^{1}_{E})=\sumop\displaylimits_{i}h^{0,n-2}(E_{i})
.

(iii)

If
\dim X=3
, then
H^{1}(E;T^{1}_{E})
has dimension
r-1
, where
r
is the number of components of
E
, and is more intrinsically dual to the cokernel of
H^{0}(E;\mathcal{O}_{E})\to\bigoplusop\displaylimits_{i}H^{0}(E_{i};\mathcal{O}_{E_{i}})
. If
\dim X>3
, then
\dim H^{1}(E;T^{1}_{E})=\sumop\displaylimits_{i}\dim H^{n-3}(E_{i};\mathcal{O}_{E_{i}})
.

(iv)

We have
H^{2}(\widehat{X};T_{\widehat{X}})\cong\bigoplusop\displaylimits_{i}H^{2}(E_{i};N_{E_{i}/\widehat{X}})
, and if
H^{3}(\widehat{X};T_{\widehat{X}}(-E))=0

(
which is always satisfied if
\dim X=3)
, then
H^{2}(E;T^{0}_{E})=0
, i.e. all locally trivial first-order deformations of
E
are unobstructed. Thus, in this case,

\dim\mathbb{T}^{2}_{E}=\dim H^{1}\left(E;T^{1}_{E}\right)=\begin{cases}r-1&\text{if $\dim X=3$},\\
\sumop\displaylimits_{i}\dim H^{n-3}(E_{i};\mathcal{O}_{E_{i}})&\text{if $\dim X>3$}.\end{cases}

(v)

The maps
\mathbb{T}^{1}_{E}\to H^{0}(E;T^{1}_{E})
and
H^{1}(\widehat{X};T_{\widehat{X}})\to H^{0}(E;T^{1}_{E})
are surjective. In particular, all first-order smoothing directions for
E
are realized via first-order deformations of
\widehat{X}
.

(vi)

The map
H^{1}(\widehat{X};T_{\widehat{X}})\to\mathbb{T}^{1}_{E}
is surjective if and only if
H^{1}(\widehat{X};T_{\widehat{X}}(-\log E))\to H^{1}(E;T^{0}_{E})
is surjective, which holds if and only if
H^{2}(\widehat{X};T_{\widehat{X}}(-E))=0
. In this case, all first-order locally trivial deformations of
E
are realized by first-order deformations of
\widehat{X}
and the divisors
E_{1},\dots,E_{r}
.

Proof.

(
i
) By adjunction and Serre duality,
H^{0}(E;N_{E/\widehat{X}})=H^{0}(E;\omega_{E})
is dual to
H^{n-1}(E;\mathcal{O}_{E})=0
. Likewise,
H^{1}(E;N_{E/\widehat{X}})=H^{1}(E;\omega_{E})
is dual to
H^{n-2}(E;\mathcal{O}_{E})=0
. Hence
\mathbb{H}^{1}(\widehat{X};\mathcal{D}^{\bullet})\cong H^{1}(\widehat{X};T_{\widehat{X}})
.

(
ii
) There is an exact sequence

H^{0}\left(E;N_{E/\widehat{X}}\right)\longrightarrow H^{0}\left(E;T^{1}_{E}\right)\longrightarrow\bigoplusop\displaylimits_{i}H^{1}\left(E_{i};N_{E_{i}/\widehat{X}}\right)\longrightarrow H^{1}\left(E;N_{E/\widehat{X}}\right).

As in (
i
),
H^{0}(E;N_{E/\widehat{X}})=H^{1}(E;N_{E/\widehat{X}})=0
. Thus
H^{0}(E;T^{1}_{E})\cong\bigoplusop\displaylimits_{i}H^{1}(E_{i};N_{E_{i}/\widehat{X}})
. But
H^{1}(E_{i};N_{E_{i}/\widehat{X}})=H^{1}(E_{i};\omega_{E_{i}})
is Serre dual to
H^{n-2}(E_{i};\mathcal{O}_{E_{i}})
and therefore has dimension
h^{0,n-2}(E_{i})
. In particular,
H^{0}(E;T^{1}_{E})=0
if and only if
h^{0,n-2}(E_{i})=0
for all
i
.

(
iii
) Continuing with the above exact sequence, we have

0=H^{1}\left(E;N_{E/\widehat{X}}\right)\longrightarrow H^{1}\left(E;T^{1}_{E}\right)\longrightarrow\bigoplusop\displaylimits_{i}H^{2}\left(E_{i};N_{E_{i}/\widehat{X}}\right)\longrightarrow H^{2}\left(E;N_{E/\widehat{X}}\right)\longrightarrow 0.

If
\dim X=3
, then
H^{1}(E;T^{1}_{E})
is dual to the cokernel of
H^{0}(E;\mathcal{O}_{E})\to\bigoplusop\displaylimits_{i}H^{0}(E_{i};\mathcal{O}_{E_{i}})
and hence has dimension
r-1
. If
\dim X>3
, then
H^{2}(E_{i};N_{E_{i}/\widehat{X}})=H^{2}(E_{i};\omega_{E_{i}})
is Serre dual to
H^{n-3}(E_{i};\mathcal{O}_{E_{i}})
and
H^{2}(E;N_{E/\widehat{X}})
is Serre dual to
H^{n-3}(E;\mathcal{O}_{E})=0
. Thus
\dim H^{1}(E;T^{1}_{E})=\sumop\displaylimits_{i}\dim H^{n-3}(E_{i};\mathcal{O}_{E_{i}})
if
\dim X>3
.

(
iv
) The statement about
H^{2}(\widehat{X};T_{\widehat{X}})
follows from the exact sequence

H^{2}\left(\widehat{X};T_{\widehat{X}}(-\log E)\right)=0\longrightarrow H^{2}\left(\widehat{X};T_{\widehat{X}}\right)\longrightarrow\bigoplusop\displaylimits_{i}H^{2}\left(E_{i};N_{E_{i}/\widehat{X}}\right)\longrightarrow H^{3}\left(\widehat{X};T_{\widehat{X}}(-\log E)\right)=0.

The long exact sequence associated to

0\longrightarrow T_{\widehat{X}}(-E)\longrightarrow T_{\widehat{X}}(-\log E)\longrightarrow T^{0}_{E}\longrightarrow 0

gives rise to an exact sequence

H^{2}\left(\widehat{X};T_{\widehat{X}}(-\log E)\right)\longrightarrow H^{2}\left(E;T^{0}_{E}\right)\to H^{3}\left(\widehat{X};T_{\widehat{X}}(-E)\right).

As we have seen,
H^{2}(\widehat{X};T_{\widehat{X}}(-\log E))=0
, and
H^{3}(\widehat{X};T_{\widehat{X}}(-E))=0
if
\dim X=3
for dimension reasons. Thus
H^{2}(E;T^{0}_{E})=0
.

(
v
) There is a commutative diagram

\begin{CD}0@>{}>{}>H^{1}\left(\widehat{X};T_{\widehat{X}}(-\log E)\right)@>{}>{}>H^{1}\left(\widehat{X};T_{\widehat{X}}\right)@>{}>{}>\bigoplusop\displaylimits_{i}H^{1}\left(N_{E_{i}/\widehat{X}}\right)@>{}>{}>0\\
@V{}V{}V@V{}V{}V@V{}V{\cong}V\\
0@>{}>{}>H^{1}\left(E;T^{0}_{E}\right)@>{}>{}>\mathbb{T}^{1}_{E}@>{}>{}>H^{0}\left(E;T^{1}_{E}\right)@>{}>{}>H^{2}\left(E;T^{0}_{E}\right).\end{CD}

(Here, the top right arrow is surjective because
H^{2}(\widehat{X};T_{\widehat{X}}(-\log E))=0
.) Thus the induced map
H^{1}(\widehat{X};T_{\widehat{X}})\to H^{0}(E;T^{1}_{E})
is surjective, and therefore the map
\mathbb{T}^{1}_{E}\to H^{0}(E;T^{1}_{E})
is surjective as well.

(
vi
) From the diagram in (
v
), the map
H^{1}(\widehat{X};T_{\widehat{X}})\to\mathbb{T}^{1}_{E}
is surjective if and only if the map
H^{1}(\widehat{X};T_{\widehat{X}}(-\log E))\to H^{1}(E;T^{0}_{E})
is surjective. Since
H^{2}(\widehat{X};T_{\widehat{X}}(-\log E))=0
, the cokernel of
H^{1}(\widehat{X};T_{\widehat{X}}(-\log E))\to H^{1}(E;T^{0}_{E})
is
H^{2}(\widehat{X};T_{\widehat{X}}(-E))
. Thus
H^{1}(\widehat{X};T_{\widehat{X}})\to\mathbb{T}^{1}_{E}
is surjective if and only if
H^{2}(\widehat{X};T_{\widehat{X}}(-E))=0
.
∎

Remark 2.7
.

(i)

In the situation of (
v
), it follows from
[
FL22
, Theorem 2.1(iii)]
that, for a crepant isolated rational singularity,

H^{1}\left(\widehat{X};T_{\widehat{X}}\right)\cong H^{1}\left(\widehat{X};{}^{n-1}_{\widehat{X}}\right)\cong H^{1}\left(\widehat{X};{}^{n-1}_{\widehat{X}}(\log E)(-E)\right)\oplus H^{1}_{E}\left(\widehat{X};{}^{n-1}_{\widehat{X}}\right).

Moreover,
H^{1}(\widehat{X};{}^{n-1}_{\widehat{X}}(\log E)(-E))\cong H^{1}(\widehat{X};T_{\widehat{X}}(-\log E))

) there is a splitting

H^{1}\left(\widehat{X};T_{\widehat{X}}\right)\cong H^{1}\left(\widehat{X};T_{\widehat{X}}(-\log E)\right)\oplus H^{0}\left(E;T^{1}_{E}\right).

Note that, while
H^{1}(\widehat{X};T_{\widehat{X}}(-\log E))
and

have the same image in

, this is just a statement about the differential of the corresponding morphism of deformation functors
\mathbf{Def}_{\widehat{X}}\to\mathbf{Def}_{X}
, and it is reasonable to ask if the actual morphism of deformation functors is finite (meaning that the corresponding morphism of the analytic germs which prorepresent them is finite).

(ii)

If
\dim X=3
and

is smooth, we will show in the next section that

is surjective. However, it is not in general an isomorphism, for example in case

is an
O_{16}
singularity, the cone over a smooth cubic surface

. In this case, a calculation shows that
\dim H^{0}(X;T^{1}_{X})=16
and that
\ell=6
, where
\ell=\ell^{1,1}
is the link invariant of
[
Ste97
]
. By
[
Wah76
]
,

is the nonnegative part of the deformations of

and is easily computed to have dimension
5
.
But
\dim H^{1}(E;T_{E})=4
, so that
H^{0}(X;R^{1}\pi_{*}T_{\widehat{X}})\to H^{1}(E;T_{E})
is not injective. Here, the weight zero piece of

has dimension
4
and corresponds to deformations of the cubic surface, hence gives all of the first-order deformations of
E
. However, there is also a weight

. Starting with the cone over the Fermat cubic surface
E

1

of
[

, and the spectral sequence with

page

Finally, if

3. The good crepant case: Some classes of examples

In this section,
\pi\colon\widehat{X}\to X
denotes a good crepant resolution of the rational, Gorenstein, isolated singularity
X
, with
\dim X=3
. Denote the exceptional divisor by
E=\pi^{-1}(x)=\bigcupop\displaylimits_{i\geq 1}E_{i}
. We fix the following notation:
D_{ij}=E_{i}\cap E_{j}
,
D_{i}=E_{i}\cap\left(\bigcupop\displaylimits_{j\neq i}E_{j}\right)=\bigcupop\displaylimits_{j\neq i}D_{ij}
, and
D=\bigcupop\displaylimits_{i}D_{i}=E_{\text{sing}}
. Our goal is to describe in more detail the case where
E
looks like a
K
-trivial semistable degeneration of
K3
surfaces with one component supporting a negative-definite anticanonical divisor removed, or more generally a
K
-trivial semistable resolution of a one-parameter smoothing of a simple elliptic or cusp singularity, minus the component containing the resolution of the germ of the singularity. Note that by
[
FM83
,
Eng18
,
EF21
]
, there is a procedure for generating all such examples. There is also an easy criterion for determining the multiplicity and hence for deciding when such examples are good crepant resolutions of complete intersection or even hypersurface singularities.

Before we begin our discussion, we record the following, which holds for an isolated rational singularity
X
of dimension
3
, not necessarily crepant.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
\pi\colon\widehat{X}\to X
be a good resolution of the isolated rational singularity
(X,x)
of dimension
3
, with exceptional divisor
E=\pi^{-1}(x)=\bigcupop\displaylimits_{i\geq 1}E_{i}
,
D_{ij}=E_{i}\cap E_{j}
, and triple points
t_{ijk}
.

(i)

For
i>0
,
H^{i}(E;\mathcal{O}_{E})=0
and
H^{i}(|\Gamma|)=0
for
i>0
, where
\Gamma
is the dual complex of
E
.

(ii)

The two homomorphisms

\bigoplusop\displaylimits_{i}H^{1}(E_{i};\mathcal{O}_{E_{i}})\to\bigoplusop\displaylimits_{i<j}H^{1}(D_{ij};\mathcal{O}_{D_{ij}})
and
\bigoplusop\displaylimits_{i}H^{0}(E_{i};{}^{1}_{E_{i}})\to\bigoplusop\displaylimits_{i<j}H^{0}(D_{ij};{}^{1}_{D_{ij}})
are isomorphisms.

(iii)

Let
{}^{1}_{E}
be the sheaf of Kähler differentials and
\tau^{1}_{E}
the subsheaf of torsion differentials. Then
H^{0}(E;{}^{1}_{E}/\tau^{1}_{E})=0
and
H^{1}(E)=0
.

(iv)

The map
\bigoplusop\displaylimits_{i}H^{2}(E_{i})\to\bigoplusop\displaylimits_{i<j}H^{2}(D_{ij})
is surjective, and its kernel is
H^{2}(E)
. Hence
b_{2}(E)=\sumop\displaylimits_{i=1}^{r}b_{2}(E_{i})-\#\{\text{\rm{double curves}}\}
.

(v)

Let
L
be the link of the singularity
(X,x)
, and let
\ell
be the dimension of
H^{2}(L)
or equivalently
H^{3}(L)
. Then
H^{2}(L)
is a pure Hodge structure and
H^{2}(L)=H^{1,1}(L)
, so that
\ell=\ell^{1,1}
, where
\ell^{1,1}
is the link invariant of
[
Ste97
]
. Finally,

\ell=b_{2}(E)-r=\sumop\displaylimits_{i}b_{2}(E_{i})-r-\#\{\text{\rm{double curves}}\}=\sumop\displaylimits_{i}(b_{2}(E_{i})-1)-\#\{\text{\rm{double curves}}\}.

Proof.

We shall just sketch the proof. By Theorem
2.5
,
H^{i}(E;\mathcal{O}_{E})=0
for
i>0
. The weight spectral sequence for
E
degenerates at
E_{2}
, and hence so does the Mayer–Vietoris spectral sequence for
\mathcal{O}_{E}
. This degeneration, along with the fact that
H^{i}(E;\mathcal{O}_{E})=0
,
i=1,2
, implies that
H^{i}(|\Gamma|)=0
for
i>0
and that
\bigoplusop\displaylimits_{i}H^{1}(E_{i};\mathcal{O}_{E_{i}})\to\bigoplusop\displaylimits_{i<j}H^{1}(D_{ij};\mathcal{O}_{D_{ij}})
is an isomorphism. Then
\bigoplusop\displaylimits_{i}H^{0}(E_{i};{}^{1}_{E_{i}})\to\bigoplusop\displaylimits_{i<j}H^{0}(D_{ij};{}^{1}_{D_{ij}})
is an isomorphism as well, as follows by taking complex conjugation. This proves (
i
) and (
ii
). There is an exact sequence

0\longrightarrow{}^{1}_{E}/\tau^{1}_{E}\longrightarrow\bigoplusop\displaylimits_{i}{}^{1}_{E_{i}}\longrightarrow\bigoplusop\displaylimits_{i<j}{}^{1}_{D_{ij}}\longrightarrow 0.

Thus, by (
ii
),
H^{0}(E;{}^{1}_{E}/\tau^{1}_{E})=0
. There is a spectral sequence converging to
H^{p+q}(E)
with
E_{1}^{p,q}=H^{q}(E;{}^{p}_{E}/\tau^{p}_{E})
. As
H^{0}(E;{}^{1}_{E}/\tau^{1}_{E})=H^{1}(E;\mathcal{O}_{E})=0
,
H^{1}(E)=0
, proving (
iii
). The semipurity theorem (see
e.g.

[
Ste83
, Corollary 1.12]
) implies that the mixed Hodge structure on
H^{k}(E)
is pure for
k\geq 3
. An examination of the weight spectral sequence shows that this forces the map
\bigoplusop\displaylimits_{i}H^{2}(E_{i})\to\bigoplusop\displaylimits_{i<j}H^{2}(D_{ij})
to be surjective and identifies
H^{2}(E)
with its kernel. Thus

b_{2}(E)=\sumop\displaylimits_{i=1}^{r}b_{2}(E_{i})-\#\{\text{double curves}\}
, which is (
iv
). By the link exact sequence and semipurity (see
[
Ste83
, Corollary 1.12]
again), there is an exact sequence of mixed Hodge structures

0\longrightarrow H_{E}^{2}\left(\widehat{X}\right)\longrightarrow H^{2}(E)\longrightarrow H^{2}(L)\longrightarrow 0,

where as in the statement of (
v
),
L
is the link of the isolated singularity.
By duality,
\dim H_{E}^{2}(\widehat{X})=\dim H^{4}(E)=\sumop\displaylimits_{i}H^{4}(E_{i})=r
. Thus

\ell=\dim H^{2}(L)=b_{2}(E)-r=\sumop\displaylimits_{i=1}^{r}b_{2}(E_{i})-\#\{\text{double curves}\}-r,

using (
iv
). The above exact sequence also shows that
H^{2}(L)
is a pure Hodge structure and that
H^{2}(L)=H^{1,1}(L)
, hence
\ell=\ell^{1,1}
. This establishes all of the statements of (
v
).
∎

Remark 3.2

The relationship between the local and global invariants is as follows. If

A straightforward argument with Mayer–Vietoris and semipurity shows that

Thus the defect measures the extent to which Poincaré duality fails on

Remark 3.7

The above terminology is modeled on the corresponding terminology for semistable degenerations of

is the germ of a simple elliptic or cusp singularity over the unit disk (

is smooth and induces a minimal resolution of

with exceptional divisor

satisfies the following:

is a reduced divisor with simple normal crossings. Then the dual complex of

is a line segment in the simple elliptic case (Type II) or a triangulation of

in the cusp case (Type III). Thus, the dual complex of

is of Type III

or Type III

for a partial converse to this picture.

E
, disjoint from the singular locus, and similarly
\mathcal{O}_{E}(mE)=\mathcal{O}_{E}(-mD_{0})
. In particular, for
m\geq 0
, there is an inclusion
\mathcal{O}_{E}(mE)\to\mathcal{O}_{E}
which only vanishes along the divisor
D_{0}
for
m>0
.
Now use the conormal sequence

0\longrightarrow I_{E}/I_{E}^{2}\longrightarrow{}^{1}_{\widehat{X}}|E\longrightarrow{}^{1}_{E}\longrightarrow 0.

Since
I_{E}/I_{E}^{2}=\mathcal{O}_{E}(-E)
, we have
I_{E}/I_{E}^{2}\otimes\mathcal{O}_{E}((n+2)E)=\mathcal{O}_{E}((n+1)E)
. First assume
r=1
,
i.e.

E
is smooth. From the conormal sequence, we get an exact sequence

H^{0}\left(E;\mathcal{O}_{E}((n+1)E)\right)\longrightarrow H^{0}\left(E;{}^{1}_{\widehat{X}}|E\otimes\mathcal{O}_{E}((n+2)E)\right)\longrightarrow H^{0}\left(E;{}^{1}_{E}\otimes\mathcal{O}_{E}((n+2)E)\right).

Since
H^{0}(E;\mathcal{O}_{E}((n+1)E))=0
for all
n\geq 0
, there is a sequence of inclusions

H^{0}\left(E;{}^{1}_{\widehat{X}}|E\otimes\mathcal{O}_{E}((n+2)E)\right)\longrightarrow H^{0}\left(E;{}^{1}_{E}\otimes\mathcal{O}_{E}((n+2)E)\right)\longrightarrow H^{0}\left(E;{}^{1}_{E}\right)=0.

Hence
H^{0}(E;{}^{1}_{\widehat{X}}|E\otimes\mathcal{O}_{E}((n+2)E))=0
for all
n\geq 0
, and therefore
H^{2}(\widehat{X};T_{\widehat{X}}(-E))=0
.

Now assume
r\geq 2
. With notation as in Definition
3.6
,
\mathcal{O}_{E}(mE)|D_{i,i+1}\cong\mathcal{O}_{D_{i,i+1}}
,
\mathcal{O}_{E}(mE)|E_{i}\cong\mathcal{O}_{E_{i}}
if
i>1
, and
\mathcal{O}_{E}(mE)|E_{1}\cong\mathcal{O}_{E_{1}}(-mD_{0})
. Using the exact sequence (from the normalization)

0\longrightarrow\mathcal{O}_{E}(mE)\longrightarrow\bigoplusop\displaylimits_{i}\mathcal{O}_{E}(mE)|E_{i}\longrightarrow\bigoplusop\displaylimits_{i}\mathcal{O}_{D_{i,i+1}}\longrightarrow 0,

we see that the map
\bigoplusop\displaylimits_{i}H^{0}(E_{i};\mathcal{O}_{E}(mE)|E_{i})\to\bigoplusop\displaylimits_{i}H^{0}(D_{i,i+1};\mathcal{O}_{D_{i,i+1}})
is an isomorphism. Thus, as in the case
r=1
,
H^{0}(E;\mathcal{O}_{E}(mE))=0
for all
m>0
.

As in the case
r=1
, we want to analyze
H^{0}(E;{}^{1}_{\widehat{X}}|E\otimes\mathcal{O}_{E}((n+2)E))
. By the above,

H^{0}\left(E;{}^{1}_{\widehat{X}}|E\otimes\mathcal{O}_{E}((n+2)E)\right)\subseteq H^{0}\left(E;{}^{1}_{E}\otimes\mathcal{O}_{E}((n+2)E)\right).

Now we have the exact sequence

0\longrightarrow\tau^{1}_{E}\longrightarrow{}^{1}_{E}\longrightarrow{}^{1}_{E}/\tau^{1}_{E}\longrightarrow 0.

Also,

H^{0}(E;{}^{1}_{E}/\tau^{1}_{E})=0
by Proposition
3.1
(
iii
). Since there is an inclusion

H^{0}\left(E;\left({}^{1}_{E}/\tau^{1}_{E}\right)\otimes\mathcal{O}_{E}((n+2)E)\right)\subseteq H^{0}\left(E;{}^{1}_{E}/\tau^{1}_{E}\right),

we conclude that

H^{0}\left(E;\left({}^{1}_{E}/\tau^{1}_{E}\right)\otimes\mathcal{O}_{E}((n+2)E)\right)=0
. The remaining term is

H^{0}(E;\tau^{1}_{E}\otimes\mathcal{O}_{E}((n+2)E)).

As
D
is smooth and the curve
D_{i,i+1}
appears twice in
\coprodop\displaylimits_{i}E_{i}
, by
[
Fri83
, Proposition 1.10(2)]
, there is an exact sequence

0\longrightarrow\mathcal{O}_{\widehat{X}}(-E)|D\longrightarrow\bigoplusop\displaylimits_{i}\left(\mathcal{O}_{\widehat{X}}(-E)|D_{i,i+1}\right)^{2}\longrightarrow\tau^{1}_{E}\longrightarrow 0.

Since
D
is a disjoint union of the smooth components
D_{i}
and
\mathcal{O}_{\widehat{X}}(-E)|D\cong\mathcal{O}_{D}
, this says that
\tau^{1}_{E}\cong\mathcal{O}_{D}=\bigoplusop\displaylimits_{i=1}^{r-1}\mathcal{O}_{D_{i,i+1}}
.
Hence

H^{0}\left(E;\tau^{1}_{E}\otimes\mathcal{O}_{E}((n+2)E)\right)\cong H^{0}(D;\mathcal{O}_{D})=\mathbb{C}^{r-1}.

In particular, taking
n=1
, we get
\dim H^{2}(E;T_{\widehat{X}}(-E)|E)=r-1
. Thus
\dim H^{2}(\widehat{X};T_{\widehat{X}}(-E))\geq r-1
.
∎

Remark 3.14
.

If we wanted to fully calculate
\dim H^{2}(\widehat{X};T_{\widehat{X}}(-E))
, we would have to understand the coboundary

H^{1}\left(nE;T_{\widehat{X}}(-E)|nE\right)\longrightarrow H^{2}\left(E;T_{\widehat{X}}(-E)|E\otimes\mathcal{O}_{E}(-nE)\right),

where the last part of the proof shows that

\dim H^{2}\left(E;T_{\widehat{X}}(-E)|E\otimes\mathcal{O}_{E}(-nE)\right)=r-1

for all
n\geq 1
. This seems difficult and most likely depends on the higher infinitesimal neighborhoods of
E
in
\widehat{X}
.

In the Type III
1
case (and also the Type III
2
case if every component of
D
meets
C
), then in fact
H^{2}(\widehat{X};T_{\widehat{X}}(-E))=0
.

Proposition 3.15
.

In the Type III
1
case,
H^{2}(\widehat{X};T_{\widehat{X}}(-E))=0
.

Proof.

The proof is similar to the proof of Proposition
3.12
, but in this case
\tau^{1}_{E}\otimes\mathcal{O}_{E}((n+2)E)
is a line bundle of negative degree on every component of
D=E_{\text{sing}}
. Thus
H^{0}(E;\tau^{1}_{E}\otimes\mathcal{O}_{E}((n+2)E))=0
.
∎

Remark 3.16
.

A similar (but also ultimately inconclusive) analysis along the lines of Proposition
3.12
is possible in the Type III
2
case: One can show that, in this case,

\dim H^{0}\left(E;\tau^{1}_{E}\otimes\mathcal{O}_{E}((n+2)E)\right)\geq p_{a}(D),

and in particular that
\dim H^{2}(\widehat{X};T_{\widehat{X}}(-E))\geq p_{a}(D)
. It is easy to see that
p_{a}(D)
is not
0
in general. More precisely, using
[
EF21
, Lemma 2.25(iv)]
, one can check that
p_{a}(D)=r-s
, which is typically nonzero.

4. The good crepant case: A partial classification

Let
(X,x)
be an isolated singularity of dimension
3
with a good crepant resolution
\pi\colon\widehat{X}\to X
. It is natural to ask if the (reduced) exceptional divisor
E
of
\pi
is of Type II, Type III
1
, or Type III
2
. We have some partial results along these lines, inspired by the arguments of Shepherd-Barron
[
SB83a
,
SB83b
]
.

Theorem 4.1
.

With notation and assumptions as above, let
t
be a general element of
\mathfrak{m}_{X,x}
in the sense of Definition
3.3
, and assume that the divisor
S=(t)
defined by
t
has a simple elliptic or cusp singularity.

(i)

With
(\pi^{*}t)=E_{0}+\sumop\displaylimits_{i\geq 1}a_{i}E_{i}
, we have
a_{i}=1
for all
i
. Hence

\mathcal{O}_{\widehat{X}}\left(\sumop\displaylimits_{i\geq 1}E_{i}\right)\cong\mathcal{O}_{\widehat{X}}(-E_{0}).

(ii)

If the hypersurface section of
X
defined by
t
is simple elliptic, then
E
is of Type II. In particular, all components of
E
are rational or elliptic ruled.

(iii)

If the hypersurface section of
X
defined by
t
is a cusp, then all components of
E
are rational and every component of a double curve
D_{ij}
is a smooth rational curve.
(
The proof gives much more detailed information about this case.
)

Proof.

We begin with two lemmas.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
S
be the germ of a simple elliptic singularity or a cusp singularity, with singular point
x
, and let
p\colon\widetilde{S}\to S
be a birational morphism such that
\widetilde{S}
is normal,
p^{-1}(x)
is a curve, i.e. has dimension
1
, and
K_{\widetilde{S}}=\mathcal{O}_{\widetilde{S}}(-D_{0})
, where
D_{0}
is an effective nonzero Weil divisor whose support is contained in
p^{-1}(x)
.

(i)

If
S
is simple elliptic, then
D_{0}
is a
(
reduced
)
smooth elliptic curve,
S
is Gorenstein, and
D_{0}
is a Cartier divisor with
D_{0}^{2}<0
.

(ii)

If
S
is a cusp, then
D_{0}
is a reduced cycle of smooth rational curves or an irreducible nodal curve.

Proof.

First consider the simple elliptic case. There exists a resolution of singularities
T\to S
which dominates
\widetilde{S}
, and hence
T
is the blowup of a minimal resolution of singularities of
S
, say
T_{0}
with
K_{T_{0}}=\mathcal{O}_{T_{0}}(-C)
. Thus
T
is obtained by successively blowing up points on
T_{0}
. All blowups at points not on the proper transform of
C
correspond to components of
K_{T}
occurring with positive coefficients: as Cartier divisors,
K_{T}=-C^{\prime}+\sumop\displaylimits_{i}a_{i}e_{i}
, where the
e_{i}
are proper transforms of exceptional curves corresponding to blowups at points not on the proper transform of
C
and
a_{i}>0
. Let
D_{0}^{\prime}
be the nonzero effective Cartier divisor on
T
which is the proper transform of
D_{0}
. Then

K_{T}=-C^{\prime}+\sumop\displaylimits_{i}a_{i}e_{i}=-D_{0}^{\prime}+\text{ a sum of exceptional fibers of the morphism $T\to\widetilde{S}$}.

The only way this is possible is if all of the
e_{i}
are fibers of the morphism
T\to\widetilde{S}
and
C^{\prime}
is not an exceptional fiber of the morphism. It follows that
\widetilde{S}
is dominated by a surface
T
, a blowup of
T_{0}
where all blowups are at points on the proper transform of
C
and
C
itself is not blown down. Hence
\widetilde{S}
has at worst
A_{n}
singularities, so is Gorenstein, and
K_{\widetilde{S}}=\omega_{\widetilde{S}}=\mathcal{O}_{\widetilde{S}}(-D_{0})
, where
D_{0}
is the image of the proper transform of
C
. Thus
D_{0}
is a (reduced) smooth elliptic curve and
D_{0}^{2}<0
.

A similar argument in the cusp case shows that if
T_{0}
is the minimal resolution and
K_{T_{0}}=\mathcal{O}_{T_{0}}(-\sumop\displaylimits_{k}\Gamma_{k})
, where
\sumop\displaylimits_{k}\Gamma_{k}
is a cycle of rational curves on
T_{0}
, then
\widetilde{S}
is dominated by a surface
T
, a blowup of
T_{0}
where all blowups are at points on the proper transform of
\sumop\displaylimits_{k}\Gamma_{k}
and
\sumop\displaylimits_{k}\Gamma_{k}
itself is not entirely blown down. In particular, the image
D_{0}
of
\sumop\displaylimits_{k}\Gamma_{k}
is a reduced cycle of smooth rational curves or an irreducible nodal curve.
∎

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
T
be a smooth algebraic surface such that
-K_{T}=\sumop\displaylimits_{i}a_{i}D_{i}
, where the
D_{i}
are irreducible curves,
a_{i}>0
, and the sum is nonempty. Then either
T
is an elliptic ruled surface and
-K_{T}=\sigma^{\prime}+\sigma^{\prime\prime}
, where
\sigma^{\prime},\sigma^{\prime\prime}
are disjoint smooth elliptic curves, or
\bigcupop\displaylimits_{i}D_{i}
is connected.

Proof.

First, if
\rho\colon T\to\overline{T}
is the blowdown of an exceptional curve, so that
\overline{T}
is smooth, then it is easy to check that
-K_{\overline{T}}=\sumop\displaylimits_{i}a_{i}\rho_{*}(D_{i})
and that
T
is a blowup of
\overline{T}
at a point of
\bigcupop\displaylimits_{i}\rho_{*}(D_{i})
. Then
\bigcupop\displaylimits_{i}D_{i}
is connected if and only if
\bigcupop\displaylimits_{i}\rho_{*}(D_{i})
is connected. Thus, we may as well assume that
T
is minimal, and hence is either
\mathbb{P}^{2}
, where the result is automatic, or the blowup of a ruled surface over a curve of genus
g
. In this case, using
[
Har77
]
as a general reference, let
e
be the invariant of the ruled surface,
i.e.

-e
is the minimal self-intersection of a curve on
T
, and let
\sigma_{0}
be a curve of self-intersection
-e
. In particular,
\sigma_{0}
is a section of the ruling, and

K_{T}\equiv-2\sigma_{0}+(2g-2-e)f,

where
f
is the numerical equivalence class of a fiber. Also note that either
e>0
and
\sigma_{0}
is the unique curve on
T
with negative self-intersection, or
e\leq 0
and every curve on
T
has nonnegative self-intersection.

If
-K_{T}=D^{\prime}+D^{\prime\prime}
, where
D^{\prime}
and
D^{\prime\prime}
are disjoint and nonempty effective divisors, then at most one of
D^{\prime}
,
D^{\prime\prime}
can have negative self-intersection. Thus we can assume that
(D^{\prime\prime})^{2}\geq 0
. If
(D^{\prime\prime})^{2}>0
, then
(D^{\prime})^{2}<0
by the Hodge index theorem; hence
\sigma_{0}
is a component of
D^{\prime}
. Then every component of
D^{\prime\prime}
is disjoint from
\sigma_{0}
, hence is numerically equivalent to a positive multiple of
\sigma_{0}+ef
. It follows that
D^{\prime\prime}
is numerically equivalent to
\sigma_{0}+ef
and
D^{\prime}=\sigma_{0}
. Moreover,
-K_{T}=D^{\prime}+D^{\prime\prime}
is a union of two disjoint smooth sections. The argument also shows that if
(D^{\prime\prime})^{2}=0
, then
(D^{\prime})^{2}=0
as well, and hence
D^{\prime}
and
D^{\prime\prime}
are numerically equivalent. In particular, since
K_{T}\equiv-2\sigma_{0}+(2g-2-e)f
, there exist disjoint sections
\sigma^{\prime}\subseteq\operatorname{Supp}D^{\prime}
and
\sigma^{\prime\prime}\subseteq\operatorname{Supp}D^{\prime\prime}
, and all remaining components of
D^{\prime}
,
D^{\prime\prime}
are fibers. Since
D^{\prime}
and
D^{\prime\prime}
are disjoint, we must have
\sigma^{\prime}=D^{\prime}
and
\sigma^{\prime\prime}=D^{\prime\prime}
. In all cases, if
\bigcupop\displaylimits_{i}D_{i}
is not connected,
-K_{T}=\sigma^{\prime}+\sigma^{\prime\prime}
, where
\sigma^{\prime},\sigma^{\prime\prime}
are disjoint sections of
T
. Then

2g(\sigma^{\prime})-2=2g(\sigma^{\prime\prime})-2=K_{S}\cdot\sigma^{\prime}+(\sigma^{\prime})^{2}=-(\sigma^{\prime})^{2}+(\sigma^{\prime})^{2}=0.

Hence
\sigma^{\prime},\sigma^{\prime\prime}
are disjoint smooth elliptic curves, and
T
is elliptic ruled.
∎

Returning to the proof of Theorem
4.1
, let
S
be a general hypersurface section through
x
.

The case where
\boldsymbol{(S,x)}
is simple elliptic

Let
E_{0}
be the proper transform of
S
. First we claim that
E_{0}
is normal. In any case,
E_{0}
is Gorenstein, and
\omega_{E_{0}}=\mathcal{O}_{E_{0}}(-D_{0})
, where
D_{0}=\sumop\displaylimits_{i\geq 1}a_{i}(E_{0}\cap E_{i})
. Let
\nu\colon\widetilde{E_{0}}\to E_{0}
be the normalization (necessarily Cohen–Macaulay), and suppose that
\nu
is not an isomorphism. Then
\omega_{\widetilde{E_{0}}}=\omega_{\widetilde{E_{0}}/E_{0}}\otimes\nu^{*}\omega_{E_{0}}
. Since
E_{0}
is Gorenstein, it must fail to be normal in codimension
1
. Then
\omega_{\widetilde{E_{0}}/E_{0}}
is an ideal sheaf (the conductor ideal sheaf) of
\mathcal{O}_{\widetilde{E_{0}}}
; hence
\omega_{\widetilde{E_{0}}/E_{0}}
is a rank
1
reflexive sheaf and
\omega_{\widetilde{E_{0}}/E_{0}}\cong\mathcal{O}_{\widetilde{E_{0}}}(-F)
for some effective nonzero divisor
F
on
\widetilde{E_{0}}
. Since the set of nonnormal points of
E_{0}
is contained in
E_{0}\cap E
, every component of
F
is also a component of
\nu^{-1}D_{0}
. In particular, some component of
-K_{\widetilde{E_{0}}}
is nonreduced. But this contradicts Lemma
4.2
.

Thus,
E_{0}
is normal. By Lemma
4.2
,
E_{0}
is Gorenstein and
\omega_{E_{0}}=\mathcal{O}_{E_{0}}(-D_{0})
, where
D_{0}
is smooth elliptic and
(D_{0})^{2}_{E_{0}}<0
. In particular,

\mathcal{O}_{E_{0}}(E_{0})=\mathcal{O}_{E_{0}}(-D_{0})=\mathcal{O}_{\widehat{X}}\left(-\sumop\displaylimits_{i\geq 1}a_{i}E_{i}\right)|E_{0}.

Then
E_{0}
meets
\bigcupop\displaylimits_{i\geq 1}E_{i}
for a unique
i
, say
i=1
, and
a_{1}=1
. Thus

K_{E_{1}}=\mathcal{O}_{E_{1}}(E_{1})=\mathcal{O}_{\widehat{X}}\left(-E_{0}-\sumop\displaylimits_{i\geq 2}a_{i}E_{i}\right)|E_{0}.

Moreover,
(D_{0})^{2}_{E_{1}}=-(D_{0})^{2}_{E_{0}}>0
.
By Lemma
4.3
, there are two possibilities:

(i)

D_{0}+\sumop\displaylimits_{i\geq 2}a_{i}(E_{1}\cap E_{i})
is connected. Then
E_{1}\cap E_{i}=\emptyset
for

, so that
r=1
,

, and

.

(ii)

E_{1}
is elliptic ruled and

for some smooth elliptic curve
\Gamma

D_{0}
with

by the Hodge index theorem.

In the first case,
E_{1}
is rational since
K_{E_{1}}=(D_{0})^{2}_{E_{1}}>0
. In the second case,

E_{1}
meets
\bigcupop\displaylimits_{i\geq 2}E_{i}
for a unique

, say

,

, and

with
(D_{12})^{2}_{E_{2}}=-(D_{12})^{2}_{E_{1}}>0
. Then we can repeat this analysis. Eventually, this process must terminate with a rational

. Moreover,

, with

is nef and big. Hence we have shown that

satisfies (

)–(

) of the Type II case of Definition

. Condition (

\sumop\displaylimits_{i\geq 0}E_{i}=(\pi^{*}t)
is pulled back from

. Note that

,

\displaystyle T^{1}_{E}|D_{i,i+1}

\displaystyle=N_{D_{i,i+1}/E_{i}}\otimes N_{D_{i,i+1}/E_{i+1}}=\mathcal{O}_{\widehat{X}}(E_{i}+E_{i+1})|D_{i,i+1}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}_{\widehat{X}}\left(\sumop\displaylimits_{i\geq 0}E_{i}\right)\Big|D_{i,i+1}=\mathcal{O}_{D_{i,i+1}},

as remarked after the definition of Type II.

The case where
\boldsymbol{(S,x)}
is a cusp

Let
E_{0}
be the proper transform of
S
. By the same arguments as in the simple elliptic case,
E_{0}
is normal and Gorenstein, and
\omega_{E_{0}}=\mathcal{O}_{E_{0}}(-\sumop\displaylimits_{k=1}^{n}\Gamma_{k})
, where either each

is smooth rational and the dual graph of the

is a cycle, or

and

is irreducible. Moreover,
E_{0}
is dominated by a smooth surface
\widetilde{E}_{0}
which is a blowup of a minimal resolution of the cusp, and we can further assume that no fibers of
\widetilde{E}_{0}\to E_{0}
are exceptional curves,

that
\widetilde{E}_{0}\to E_{0}
is a minimal resolution. Thus
E_{0}
is obtained from
\widetilde{E}_{0}
by contracting chains of curves of self-intersection
-2
. We have

\mathcal{O}_{E_{0}}(E_{0})=\mathcal{O}_{E_{0}}\left(-\sumop\displaylimits_{k=1}^{n}\Gamma_{k}\right)=\mathcal{O}_{\widehat{X}}\left(-\sumop\displaylimits_{i\geq 1}a_{i}E_{i}\right)\Big|E_{0}.

After reindexing, we can assume that the components
E_{i}
of
E
meeting
E_{0}
are

, and the above shows that

for such

. Define

. Then

is a union of some of the

are chains of rational curves, or

is a cycle of rational curves or an irreducible curve of arithmetic genus

is irreducible, and necessarily a rational surface. If

, then
C_{i}
is a disjoint union of chains of smooth rational curves, and each

with

meets at least one other component

for which

. In fact, we can reorder the

so that, for

,
E_{i}
meets

with

. Note that, as

is reduced, no component of

is contained in

for

, and thus

is not contained in

for

.

Taking for example

,
E_{1}
meets
E_{2}
at a point of

. Then there exists a component

of
D_{12}

(K_{E_{1}}+G_{2})\cdot G_{2}=-(C_{1}\cdot G_{2})-\sumop\displaylimits_{j\neq 1,2}a_{j}(D_{1j}\cdot G_{2})<0.

Thus
(K_{E_{1}}+G_{2})\cdot G_{2}=-2
, and

is a smooth rational curve. Also,
(C_{1}\cdot G_{2})
is either

or

. If it is

, then

,

is connected, and

is a rational surface with
(D_{1j}\cdot D_{12})=0
for

. This implies that

for

. Also, by the connectedness of

, it follows that

for

, and we are in the case

. So we can assume that
(C_{1}\cdot G)=1
, there is a unique

, say

, such that
(D_{13}\cdot G_{2})\neq 0
, and
(D_{13}\cdot G_{2})=1
and

.

Let

be the unique component of
D_{13}
meeting
G_{2}
. Repeating this argument with

, we see that

is smooth rational and that

is either

or
2
. If it is

meets

at the other end of the chain from

5. The case of a small resolution

In this section, we consider the case of a small resolution
\pi^{\prime}\colon X^{\prime}\to X
;
i.e.

(X,x)
is the germ of an isolated Gorenstein singularity of dimension
3
with a good Stein representative
X
, and
p\colon X^{\prime}\to X
is a small resolution with exceptional set
C
.

Remark 5.1
.

There is no real limitation to restricting to dimension
3
, at least in case the singularity
(X,x)
is a local complete intersection. Indeed, such resolutions can only exist for
\dim X=3
: By the Grothendieck–Lefschetz theorem, the local ring
\mathcal{O}_{X,x}
of an isolated local complete intersection singularity is a UFD for
\dim X\geq 4
. The proof of the “easy case” of Zariski’s Main Theorem shows that in case
\mathcal{O}_{X,x}
is a UFD and
\pi\colon\widehat{X}\to X
is a resolution of singularities, there exists a divisor
D
on
\widehat{X}
such that
\operatorname{codim}\overline{\pi(D)}\geq 2
. Thus, if
(X,x)
is an isolated local complete intersection singularity, and
\pi^{\prime}\colon X^{\prime}\to X
is a resolution such that the exceptional set
(\pi^{\prime})^{-1}(x)
has dimension
\leq\dim X-2
, then
\dim X=3
.

Conversely, in dimension
3
, suppose that
(X,x)
is the germ of an isolated singularity with a small resolution
\pi^{\prime}\colon X^{\prime}\to X
. If
X
is Cohen–Macaulay, then it follows from results of Laufer, Reid, and Pinkham (see for example
[
Pin83
, Section 8]
) that
X
is a compound du Val singularity,
i.e.
that the general hyperplane section of
X
in the sense of Definition
3.3
is a rational double point. In particular,
X
is a hypersurface singularity and
(\pi^{\prime})^{-1}(x)=C=\bigcupop\displaylimits_{i=1}^{r}C_{i}
, where the
C_{i}
are smooth rational curves meeting (pairwise) transversally (but three
C_{i}
can meet at a point).

For the case of a small resolution, the functor
\mathbf{Def}_{X^{\prime}}
has a more than purely formal meaning: By
[
Lau80
, Theorem 2]
, there is a deformation of a neighborhood of the exceptional curve
C
over the smooth germ
(H^{1}(X^{\prime};T_{X^{\prime}}),0)
for which the Kodaira–Spencer map is an isomorphism. As previously noted,
X^{\prime}
is a crepant resolution of
X
, and hence
K_{X^{\prime}}\cong\mathcal{O}_{X^{\prime}}
. Also, the resolution
p\colon X^{\prime}\to X
is equivariant (
cf.
Definition
2.1
).

We will use the following standard fact about local cohomology.

Lemma 5.2
.

If

is a locally free sheaf on

Proof.

Using the Mayer–Vietoris sequence, see

H^{1}_{C_{i}}(X^{\prime};\mathcal{F})=0

of

and that

is smooth, it is a local complete intersection. Hence, by

Since the fibers of

There is an exact sequence

The differential

6. A noncrepant example

In this final section, we consider a noncrepant example
\widehat{X}
, the blowup of a small resolution with exceptional set a smooth curve
C
along the curve
C
. Following the discussion of the introduction, there are homomorphisms
H^{1}(\widehat{X};T_{\widehat{X}})\to H^{1}(U,T_{U})\cong H^{0}(X;T^{1}_{X})
and
H^{1}(\widehat{X};{}^{2}_{\widehat{X}})\to H^{1}(U,T_{U})\cong H^{0}(X;T^{1}_{X})
. The image of
H^{1}(\widehat{X};{}^{2}_{\widehat{X}})
is a birational invariant,
i.e.
is independent of the choice of a good resolution, and is identified with the image of
H^{1}(X^{\prime};T_{X^{\prime}})
by Proposition
5.13
(
i
). On the other hand, the image of
H^{1}(\widehat{X};T_{\widehat{X}})
also has geometric meaning (it is the tangent space to the “simultaneous resolution locus” for the resolution
\pi\colon\widehat{X}\to X
), and the map
H^{1}(\widehat{X};T_{\widehat{X}})\to H^{0}(X;T^{1}_{X})
factors through the natural map
H^{1}(\widehat{X};T_{\widehat{X}})\to H^{1}(\widehat{X};{}^{2}_{\widehat{X}})
. Our goal in this section is to explicitly compare the image of
H^{1}(\widehat{X};T_{\widehat{X}})
in
H^{0}(X;T^{1}_{X})
with that of
H^{1}(\widehat{X};{}^{2}_{\widehat{X}})\cong H^{1}(X^{\prime};T_{X^{\prime}})
. More generally we compare
\mathbf{Def}_{\widehat{X}}
and
\mathbf{Def}_{X^{\prime}}
. While this example is somewhat special, similar techniques will handle other examples, such as the natural good resolution of the
A_{2}
singularity defined by
x^{2}+y^{2}+z^{2}+w^{3}
.

We begin with a general result.

Lemma 6.1
.

Let
g\colon\widehat{X}\to X^{\prime}
be the blowup of a smooth threefold
X^{\prime}
along a smooth compact curve
C
, with exceptional divisor
E
. Then
R^{1}g_{*}T_{\widehat{X}}=R^{1}g_{*}T_{\widehat{X}}(-E)=0
, and there is an exact sequence

0\longrightarrow R^{0}g_{*}T_{\widehat{X}}\longrightarrow T_{X^{\prime}}\longrightarrow N_{C/X^{\prime}}\longrightarrow 0.

Proof.

For the first statement, we must show that
(R^{1}g_{*}T_{\widehat{X}})_{t}=0
for all
t\in X^{\prime}
, and we may as well assume that
t\in C
. By the formal functions theorem, it suffices to show that

\varprojlim_{n}H^{1}\left(nf;T_{\widehat{X}}|nf\right)=0,

where
f
is a fiber of
g\colon\widehat{X}\to X^{\prime}
over a point
t\in C
,
I_{f}
is the ideal sheaf defining the reduced scheme
f
, and
nf
is the scheme defined by
I_{f}^{n}
. From the exact sequence

0\longrightarrow N_{f/E}\longrightarrow N_{f/\widehat{X}}\longrightarrow N_{E/\widehat{X}}|f\longrightarrow 0

and the fact that
N_{f/E}\cong\mathcal{O}_{f}
and
N_{E/\widehat{X}}|f\cong\mathcal{O}_{f}(-1)
, we see that

N_{f/\widehat{X}}\cong\mathcal{O}_{f}\oplus\mathcal{O}_{f}(-1).

Because
f
is a local complete intersection, there is an exact sequence

0\longrightarrow\operatorname{Sym}^{n}\left(I_{f}/I_{f}^{2}\right)\longrightarrow\mathcal{O}_{(n+1)f}\longrightarrow\mathcal{O}_{nf}\longrightarrow 0,

where
I_{f}/I_{f}^{2}
is the conormal bundle. Hence

\operatorname{Sym}^{n}\left(I_{f}/I_{f}^{2}\right)=\mathcal{O}_{f}\oplus\mathcal{O}_{f}(1)\oplus\cdots\oplus\mathcal{O}_{f}(n).

Then from the normal bundle sequence

0\longrightarrow T_{f}\longrightarrow T_{\widehat{X}}|f\longrightarrow N_{f/\widehat{X}}\longrightarrow 0

and the fact that
T_{f}\cong\mathcal{O}_{f}(2)
,
it follows easily that
H^{1}(nf;T_{\widehat{X}}|nf)=0
for all
n\geq 1
, hence that
R^{1}g_{*}T_{\widehat{X}}=0
. The proof that

R^{1}g_{*}T_{\widehat{X}}(-E)=0
is similar, using
\mathcal{O}_{\widehat{X}}(-E)|f=\mathcal{O}_{f}(1)
.

To see the statement about
R^{0}g_{*}T_{\widehat{X}}
, there is an exact sequence

0\longrightarrow T_{\widehat{X}}\longrightarrow g^{*}T_{X^{\prime}}\longrightarrow i_{*}T_{E/C}(E)\longrightarrow 0,

where
i\colon E\to\widehat{X}
is the inclusion. Hence there is an exact sequence

(
*
)

0\longrightarrow g_{*}T_{\widehat{X}}\longrightarrow g_{*}g^{*}T_{X^{\prime}}\longrightarrow g_{*}i_{*}T_{E/C}(E)\longrightarrow R^{1}g_{*}T_{\widehat{X}}=0.

Then
g_{*}g^{*}T_{X^{\prime}}=T_{X^{\prime}}
and
g_{*}i_{*}T_{E/C}(E)=r_{*}T_{E/C}(E)
, where
r\colon E\to C
is the projection. Also, we have the Euler exact sequence

0\longrightarrow\mathcal{O}_{E}\longrightarrow r^{*}N_{C/X^{\prime}}(1)\longrightarrow T_{E/C}\longrightarrow 0.

Thus, using
\mathcal{O}_{E}(E)=\mathcal{O}_{E}(-1)
, we get

0\longrightarrow\mathcal{O}_{E}(E)\longrightarrow r^{*}N_{C/X^{\prime}}\longrightarrow T_{E/C}(E)\longrightarrow 0.

Taking
r_{*}
and using
R^{1}r_{*}\mathcal{O}_{E}(E)=0
gives
r_{*}T_{E/C}(E)=N_{C/X^{\prime}}
. Thus (
*
) becomes

0\longrightarrow g_{*}T_{\widehat{X}}\longrightarrow T_{X^{\prime}}\longrightarrow N_{C/X^{\prime}}\longrightarrow 0,

as claimed.
∎

Corollary 6.2
.

Suppose as above that
g\colon\widehat{X}\to X^{\prime}
is the blowup of a smooth threefold along a smooth compact curve
C
and that moreover
p\colon X^{\prime}\to X
is a resolution of an isolated singular point
x
, with
C\subseteq p^{-1}(x)
. Suppose in addition either that
p\colon X^{\prime}\to X
is a small resolution of
X
or that it is a good resolution with
\deg N_{C/E_{i}}<0
for every component
E_{i}
of
E=p^{-1}(x)
containing
C
. Then there is an exact sequence

0\longrightarrow H^{0}\left(C;N_{C/X^{\prime}}\right)\longrightarrow H^{1}\left(\widehat{X};T_{\widehat{X}}\right)\longrightarrow H^{1}\left(X^{\prime};T_{X^{\prime}}\right)\longrightarrow H^{1}\left(C;N_{C/X^{\prime}}\right).

Hence, if
p\colon X^{\prime}\to X
is an equivariant resolution of
X
, for example if
p
is a small resolution, then so is
\pi=p\circ g\colon\widehat{X}\to X
.

Proof.

By the Leray spectral sequence and Lemma
6.1
,
H^{i}(\widehat{X};T_{\widehat{X}})=H^{i}(X^{\prime};R^{0}g_{*}T_{\widehat{X}})
for all
i
. The exact sequence of the statement then follows from the exact sequence for
R^{0}g_{*}T_{\widehat{X}}
in Lemma
6.1
, except for the injectivity on the left. If
H^{0}(C;N_{C/X^{\prime}})\to H^{1}(\widehat{X};T_{\widehat{X}})
is not injective, then the map
H^{0}(X^{\prime};T_{X^{\prime}})\to H^{0}(C;N_{C/X^{\prime}})
is nonzero. Thus there exists a nonzero element of
H^{0}(C;N_{C/X^{\prime}})
which lifts to
\theta\in H^{0}(X^{\prime};T_{X^{\prime}})
. Exponentiating the vector field
\theta
, we see that
C
moves in a one-parameter family in
X^{\prime}
. However, given the contraction
p\colon X^{\prime}\to X
, every such family must be contained in the exceptional set of

. This is clearly impossible if
p
is a small resolution or if
C
does not move in a family in some component
E_{i}
of the exceptional set

. In particular, if
\deg N_{C/E_{i}}<0
for every component
E_{i}
of
E
containing
C
, then
C
does not move inside any
E_{i}
.

To see the last statement, the above shows that the map
H^{0}(\widehat{X};T_{\widehat{X}})\to H^{0}(X^{\prime};T_{X^{\prime}})
is surjective. Equivalently,
R^{0}\pi_{*}T_{\widehat{X}}\to R^{0}p_{*}T_{X^{\prime}}
is surjective, and it is clearly injective, hence an isomorphism. Since by assumption
R^{0}p_{*}T_{X^{\prime}}=T^{0}_{X}
, this says that
R^{0}\pi_{*}T_{\widehat{X}}=T^{0}_{X}
,
i.e.

\pi
is equivariant.
∎

For the rest of this section,
(X,x)
is a threefold

singularity,

is a small resolution with exceptional curve

. If

for

and

Corollary 6.3
.

Under the above assumptions, the following hold:

(i)

We have
H^{2}(\widehat{X};T_{\widehat{X}})=H^{2}(\widehat{X};T_{\widehat{X}}(-E))=0
. In particular,
\mathbf{Def}_{\widehat{X}}
is unobstructed of dimension
\dim H^{1}(\widehat{X};T_{\widehat{X}})
.

(ii)

We have

, and the natural map

H^{1}(\widehat{X};T_{\widehat{X}}(-\log E))\longrightarrow H^{1}(E;T_{E})

is surjective. Hence
E
is a stable submanifold of
\widehat{X}
, deformations of
\widehat{X}
are versal for deformations of
E
, and there exist small deformations of
\widehat{X}
for which

deforms to
\mathbb{F}_{0}
.

(iii)

For all
i
,
H^{i}(\widehat{X};T_{\widehat{X}})=H^{i}(X^{\prime};R^{0}g_{*}T_{\widehat{X}})
, and there is an exact sequence

0\longrightarrow\mathbb{C}=H^{0}\left(C;N_{C/X^{\prime}}\right)\longrightarrow H^{1}\left(\widehat{X};T_{\widehat{X}}\right)\longrightarrow H^{1}\left(X^{\prime};T_{X^{\prime}}\right)\longrightarrow H^{1}\left(C;N_{C/X^{\prime}}\right)\longrightarrow 0.

Thus
\dim H^{1}(\widehat{X};T_{\widehat{X}})=\dim H^{1}(X^{\prime};T_{X^{\prime}})=n-1
.

Proof.

(
i
) To see that
H^{2}(\widehat{X};T_{\widehat{X}})=0
, it suffices to show that
R^{2}\pi_{*}T_{\widehat{X}}=0
. In the Leray spectral sequence with
E_{2}^{a,b}=R^{a}p_{*}R^{b}g_{*}T_{\widehat{X}}\Rightarrow R^{a+b}\pi_{*}T_{\widehat{X}}
, all possible terms contributing to
R^{2}\pi_{*}T_{\widehat{X}}
are
0
, either for dimension reasons or because
R^{1}p_{*}R^{1}g_{*}T_{\widehat{X}}=0
by Lemma
6.1
. Thus
R^{2}\pi_{*}T_{\widehat{X}}=0
. The proof for
H^{2}(\widehat{X};T_{\widehat{X}}(-E))
is similar.

(
ii
) From the exact sequence

0\longrightarrow T_{\widehat{X}}(-\log E)\longrightarrow T_{\widehat{X}}\longrightarrow N_{E/\widehat{X}}\longrightarrow 0

and the fact that
H^{i}(E;N_{E/\widehat{X}})=0
for all
i
(apply Leray to the morphism
r\colon E\to C
), we have
H^{i}(\widehat{X};T_{\widehat{X}}(-\log E))\cong H^{i}(\widehat{X};T_{\widehat{X}})
for all

. Thus in particular
H^{2}(\widehat{X};T_{\widehat{X}}(-\log E))=0
and
H^{1}(\widehat{X};T_{\widehat{X}}(-\log E))\cong H^{1}(\widehat{X};T_{\widehat{X}})
. Finally, from the exact sequence

0\longrightarrow T_{\widehat{X}}(-E)\longrightarrow T_{\widehat{X}}(-\log E)\longrightarrow T_{E}\longrightarrow 0

and the vanishing of

, we see that
H^{1}(\widehat{X};T_{\widehat{X}}(-\log E))\to H^{1}(E;T_{E})

is surjective.

(
iii
) This follows from Corollary
6.2
and the fact that
H^{2}(\widehat{X};T_{\widehat{X}})=0
.
∎

Remark 6.4
.

(i)

By (
iii

(ii)

The functors

First, let
\mathbf{Def}_{\widehat{X},E}
denote the functor of deformations of the pair

\mathbf{Def}_{\widehat{X},E}(S,s_{0})
consists of a deformation
\widehat{\mathcal{X}}
of
\widehat{X}
over

, together with an effective Cartier divisor
\mathcal{E}
of
\widehat{\mathcal{X}}
, flat over

and restricting to
E
over

. The functor
\mathbf{Def}_{X^{\prime},C}
is defined similarly; the objects over

are pairs
(\mathcal{X}^{\prime},\mathcal{C})
, where
\mathcal{C}
is flat over

and restricts to the reduced subscheme

Proposition 6.5
.

We have
\mathbf{Def}_{\widehat{X}}\cong\mathbf{Def}_{\widehat{X},E}\cong\mathbf{Def}_{X^{\prime},C}
, and the morphism
\mathbf{Def}_{\widehat{X}}\to\mathbf{Def}_{X^{\prime}}
is the same under the above identification as the forgetful morphism
\mathbf{Def}_{X^{\prime},C}\to\mathbf{Def}_{X^{\prime}}
.

Proof.

It is a standard result that the tangent space to
\mathbf{Def}_{\widehat{X},E}
is
H^{1}(\widehat{X};T_{\widehat{X}}(-\log E))
(
cf.
Section 1) and the obstruction space is
H^{2}(\widehat{X};T_{\widehat{X}}(-\log E))=0
. Thus,
\mathbf{Def}_{\widehat{X},E}
is smooth by Corollary
6.3
(ii), and the first statement of the proposition is the isomorphism
H^{1}(\widehat{X};T_{\widehat{X}}(-\log E))\cong H^{1}(\widehat{X};T_{\widehat{X}})
. For the second, given a pair
(\widehat{\mathcal{X}},\mathcal{E})
in
\mathbf{Def}_{\widehat{X},E}(S,s_{0})
, it is easy to check that the morphism
r\colon E\to C
extends to a morphism
\mathcal{E}\to\mathcal{C}\cong C\times S
(note that
C\cong\mathbb{P}^{1}
is rigid) and that
\mathcal{E}
can be blown down to a subspace
\mathcal{C}\cong C\times S\subseteq\mathcal{X}^{\prime}
. Conversely, given a pair
(\mathcal{X}^{\prime},\mathcal{C})
over
S
, let
\widehat{\mathcal{X}}
be the blowup of
\mathcal{X}^{\prime}
along
\mathcal{C}
, and let
\mathcal{E}
be the exceptional divisor. This gives two morphisms of functors
\mathbf{Def}_{\widehat{X},E}\to\mathbf{Def}_{X^{\prime},C}
and
\mathbf{Def}_{X^{\prime},C}\to\mathbf{Def}_{\widehat{X},E}
which are clearly inverses. Hence
\mathbf{Def}_{\widehat{X},E}\cong\mathbf{Def}_{X^{\prime},C}
.
∎

To put the above in more manageable form, we give an explicit description of
\mathbf{Def}_{X^{\prime},C}
. First, we recall the basics about deformations of
X
and
X^{\prime}
. Let
Z
be the germ of the standard ordinary double point in dimension
2
, given by
x^{2}+y^{2}+z^{2}=0
, and let
Z^{\prime}
be the resolution of singularities of
Z
, with
C\subseteq Z^{\prime}
the exceptional set. Then
\mathbf{Def}_{Z}
is represented by the germ
(\mathbb{C},0)
, with coordinate
t
and universal family
\mathcal{Z}\to(\mathbb{C},0)
given by
x^{2}+y^{2}+z^{2}+t=0
. Likewise,
\mathbf{Def}_{Z^{\prime}}
is represented by the germ
(\mathbb{C},0)
, with coordinate
u
and universal family
\mathcal{Z}^{\prime}\to(\mathbb{C},0)
given by a choice for a small resolution of
x^{2}+y^{2}+z^{2}+u^{2}=0
. The threefold
X^{\prime}
is isomorphic in a neighborhood of
C
to
f^{*}\mathcal{Z}^{\prime}
, where
f\colon(\mathbb{C},0)\to(\mathbb{C},0)
is given by
u=f(w)=w^{n}
. A deformation
\mathcal{X}^{\prime}
of
X^{\prime}
over a germ
(S,s_{0})
corresponds to a morphism
F\colon(\mathbb{C},0)\times S\to(\mathbb{C},0)
with
F(w,s_{0})=f(w)=w^{n}
, with
\mathcal{X}^{\prime}=F^{*}\mathcal{Z}^{\prime}
. A polynomial
F
restricting to
w^{n}
is analytically equivalent to one of the form
w^{n}+b_{n-2}(s)w^{n-1}+\cdots+b_{0}(s)
. In particular,
\mathbf{Def}_{X^{\prime}}
is represented by the germ of the affine space
(\mathbb{C}^{n-1},0)
, with coordinates
(b_{n-2},\dots,b_{0})
. Also note that
F^{-1}(0)\to S
is a finite cover of degree
n
.

Lemma 6.6
.

There is an isomorphism of functors from
\mathbf{Def}_{X^{\prime},C}
to
\mathbf{F}
, where, for
(S,s_{0})
the germ of an analytic space,
\mathbf{F}(S)
is the set of pairs
(F,\sigma)
, where as above
F\colon(\mathbb{C},0)\times S\to(\mathbb{C},0)
is a morphism such that
F(w,s_{0})=f(w)=w^{n}
and
\sigma\subseteq F^{-1}(0)
is a section of the finite cover
F^{-1}(0)\to S
, or equivalently a morphism
\lambda\colon S\to(\mathbb{C},0)
such that
F(\lambda(s),s)
is identically
0
. Moreover, via this isomorphism, the morphism
\mathbf{Def}_{X^{\prime},C}\to\mathbf{Def}_{X^{\prime}}
corresponds the forgetful map
(F,\sigma)\in\mathbf{F}(S)\mapsto F
.

Proof.

Given an object
(F,\sigma)
of
\mathbf{F}(S)
, the morphism
F
defines
\mathcal{X}^{\prime}
in
\mathbf{Def}_{X^{\prime}}(S)
corresponding to the morphism
F\colon(\mathbb{C},0)\times S\to(\mathbb{C},0)
as above, and a Cartesian diagram

\begin{CD}\mathcal{X}^{\prime}@>{}>{}>\mathcal{Z}^{\prime}\\
@V{}V{}V@V{}V{}V\\
(\mathbb{C},0)\times S@>{F}>{}>(\mathbb{C},0).\end{CD}

Note that
\mathcal{X}^{\prime}|F^{-1}(0)=Z^{\prime}\times F^{-1}(0)\subseteq\mathcal{X}^{\prime}
, and thus

C\times F^{-1}(0)\subseteq\mathcal{X}^{\prime}|F^{-1}(0)\subseteq\mathcal{X}^{\prime},

compatibly with the projection to
S
. Given the section
\sigma\subseteq F^{-1}(0)
, define

\mathcal{C}=C\times\sigma\subseteq C\times F^{-1}(0)\subseteq\mathcal{X}^{\prime}.

Thus the pair
(F,\sigma)
defines a deformation of
(X^{\prime},C)
over
S
, and hence an element of
\mathbf{Def}_{X^{\prime},C}
.

Conversely, suppose that we are given a pair
(\mathcal{X}^{\prime},\mathcal{C})\in\mathbf{Def}_{X^{\prime},C}(S)
, and let
F\colon(\mathbb{C},0)\times S\to(\mathbb{C},0)
be the morphism corresponding to
\mathcal{X}^{\prime}
in
\mathbf{Def}_{X^{\prime}}(S)
. Note that
C
does not deform in
\mathcal{Z}^{\prime}
, even to first order. Thus
\mathcal{C}\cong C\times S\subseteq C\times F^{-1}(0)
, compatibly with the projection to
S
, so that the projection of
\mathcal{C}
onto the second factor
F^{-1}(0)
defines a section
\sigma
of the morphism
F^{-1}(0)\to S
. Clearly, the two constructions
\mathbf{F}(S)\to\mathbf{Def}_{X^{\prime},C}(S)
and
\mathbf{Def}_{X^{\prime},C}(S)\to\mathbf{F}(S)
are mutual inverses and are functorial under pullback. This defines the isomorphism of functors, and the final statement is clear from the construction.
∎

Explicitly, with
P(w;\mathbf{b})=w^{n}+\sumop\displaylimits_{i=0}^{n-2}b_{i}w^{i}
, where
\mathbf{b}=(b_{n-2},\dots,b_{0})
, the universal deformation
\mathcal{U}^{\prime}
of
X^{\prime}
is given as a small resolution of

. Consider

Note that if
w-\lambda
is a factor of
P(w;\mathbf{b})
, then

P(w;\mathbf{b})=(w-\lambda)(w^{n-1}+\lambda w^{n-2}+t_{n-3}w^{n-3}+\cdots+t_{1}w+t_{0})=(w-\lambda)Q(w;\lambda,\mathbf{t}),

say, where
Q(w;\lambda,\mathbf{t})=w^{n-1}+\lambda w^{n-2}+t_{n-3}w^{n-3}+\cdots+t_{1}w+t_{0}
.
Solving explicitly for the coefficients
b_{i}
, we see

\displaystyle b_{n-2}

\displaystyle=-\lambda^{2}+t_{n-3},

\displaystyle b_{i}

\displaystyle=-\lambda t_{i}+t_{i-1},\quad 1\leq i\leq n-3,

\displaystyle b_{0}

\displaystyle=-\lambda t_{0}.

If
\lambda,t_{n-3},\dots,t_{0}
are coordinates on
\mathbb{C}^{n-1}

\Phi\colon\mathbb{C}^{n-1}\to\mathbb{C}^{n-1}

\Phi(\lambda,t_{n-3},\dots,t_{0})=(-\lambda^{2}+t_{n-3},-\lambda t_{n-3}+t_{n-4},\dots,-\lambda t_{0})=(b_{n-2},\dots,b_{0}).

Solving for

in terms of
\lambda

gives

\displaystyle t_{i}

\displaystyle=\lambda^{n-i-1}+b_{n-2}\lambda^{n-i-3}+\cdots+b_{i+1},\quad 1\leq i\leq n-3,

\displaystyle-b_{0}

\displaystyle=\lambda^{n}+b_{n-2}\lambda^{n-2}+\cdots+\lambda b_{1},

recovering the fact that
\lambda
is a root of
P(w;\mathbf{b})
(and there are exactly

Thus the morphism
\pi_{1}\times\Phi\colon\mathbb{C}^{n-1}\to\mathbb{C}\times\mathbb{C}^{n-1}
defined by

(\lambda,t_{n-3},\dots,t_{0})\longmapsto(\lambda,\Phi(\lambda,t_{n-3},\dots,t_{0}))

is an isomorphism from
\mathbb{C}^{n-1}
to
\{(\lambda,b_{n-2},\dots,b_{0}):P(\lambda;\mathbf{b})=0\}
. The germ
(\mathbb{C}^{n-1},0)
, with coordinates
\lambda,t_{n-3},\dots,t_{0}

, represents
\mathbf{Def}_{X^{\prime},C}
, and hence after blowing up represents
\mathbf{Def}_{\widehat{X}}

\Phi
corresponds to the forgetful morphism
\mathbf{Def}_{X^{\prime},C}\to\mathbf{Def}_{X^{\prime}}
.

By the above,
\Phi

and surjective, and is ramified exactly where
Q(\lambda;\lambda,\mathbf{t})=0
,
i.e.
where

vanishes). In fact,

\det\begin{pmatrix}\frac{\partial b_{n-2}}{\partial\lambda}&\cdots&\frac{\partial b_{0}}{\partial\lambda}\\[4.30554pt]
\frac{\partial b_{n-2}}{\partial t_{n-3}}&\cdots&\frac{\partial b_{0}}{\partial t_{n-3}}\\
\vbox{\kern 6.0pt\hbox{$.$}\hbox{$.$}\hbox{$.$}}&\vbox{\kern 6.0pt\hbox{$.$}\hbox{$.$}\hbox{$.$}}&\vbox{\kern 6.0pt\hbox{$.$}\hbox{$.$}\hbox{$.$}}\\
