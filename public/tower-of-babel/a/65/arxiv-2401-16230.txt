Abstract
It is known that for subgraph-closed graph classes the first-order model checking problem is fixed-parameter tractable if and only if the class is nowhere dense [Grohe, Kreutzer, Siebertz, STOC 2014]. However, the dependency on the formula size is non-elementary, and in fact, this is unavoidable even for the class of all trees [Frick and Grohe, LICS 2002]. On the other hand, it is known that the dependency is elementary for classes of bounded degree [Frick and Grohe, LICS 2002] as well as for classes of bounded pathwidth [Lampis, ICALP 2023]. In this paper we generalise these results and almost completely characterise subgraph-closed graph classes for which the model checking problem is fixed-parameter tractable with an elementary dependency on the formula size. Those are the graph classes for which there exists a number d d such that for every r r , some tree of depth d d and size bounded by an elementary function of r r is avoided as an ( ⩽ r ) ({\leqslant}r) -subdivision in all graphs in the class. In particular, this implies that if the class in question excludes a fixed tree as a topological minor, then first-order model checking for graphs in the class is fixed-parameter tractable with an elementary dependency on the formula size.

Introduction

A major theme in algorithmic graph theory and in finite model theory explores
the tractability frontier of the model checking problem on restricted graph classes.
In the model checking problem for first-order logic,
the goal is to decide if a given graph
G
satisfies a given first-order formula
\varphi
. In the setting of parameterised complexity, the length of the formula
\varphi
is treated as a parameter,
and then the aim is to devise an algorithm that runs in time

\displaystyle f(|\varphi|)\cdot|G|^{c},

(1)

for some constant
c
and computable function
f
.
Since this problem is believed to be intractable in general (it is
\mathsf{AW}[\ast]
-complete), the objective is to characterise those graph classes
\mathcal{C}
for which the problem becomes
fixed parameter tractable
(fpt) when the inputs are restricted to graphs from
\mathcal{C}
,
that is, admits an algorithm as in (
1
) for
G\in\mathcal{C}
.
Initiated by Flum and Grohe
[
15
]
,
this direction of research reveals a deep interplay between structural graph theory,
logic, and algorithms, and has enjoyed major successes.

Results expressing that the model checking problem for
formulas of some logic is fpt on some class of graphs are sometimes called
algorithmic meta-theorems
.
The prime example of such a result is the theorem of Courcelle
[
8
]
,
which states that model checking
monadic second-order
(MSO) formulas is fpt on all classes of bounded treewidth. In particular, every problem expressible in MSO can be solved in linear time on all graphs of treewidth bounded by a fixed constant.
However, the hidden constants in such an algorithm
are often astronomical. Indeed, in Courcelle’s result,
the function
f
in (
1
) is not even bounded by any elementary function. Equivalently, it is not bounded by any function of the form

n\mapsto\underbrace{2^{2^{\cdot^{\cdot^{2^{n}}}}}}_{\text{height $h$}},

for a fixed height
h
.
As observed by Frick and Grohe
[
16
]
,
this non-elementary growth of
f
is unavoidable in Courcelle’s theorem. We discuss this in greater detail below.

In this paper, we focus on first-order (FO) formulas,
which are much more restrictive than MSO formulas, but still powerful enough to express problems such as the existence of an independent set of a given size, or the existence of a dominating set of a given size, which are intractable in general (W[1]-complete and W[2]-complete, respectively).

In a landmark paper, Grohe, Kreutzer, and Siebertz
[
25
]
proved that for a
monotone
(that is, subgraph-closed) graph class
\mathcal{C}
,
the model checking problem for first-order logic is fpt on
\mathcal{C}
if and only if
\mathcal{C}
is
nowhere dense
, a central notion of sparsity theory developed by Nešetřil and Ossona de Mendez
[
31
]
.
A monotone graph class
\mathcal{C}
is called nowhere dense if
for every
r\in\mathbb{N}
there is some clique
K_{n}
that is not an
r
-shallow topological minor of any graph
G\in\mathcal{C}
.
A graph
H
is an
r
-shallow topological minor
of
G
if by replacing edges of
H
by paths of length at most
r+1
we can obtain some subgraph of
G
1
1

1

In the literature, see e.g.
[
31
]
, one often allows paths of length at most
2r+1
in the definition of
r
-shallow topological minors, instead of
r+1
. This difference is immaterial for all the results presented in this work.
.
Nowhere dense classes include e.g. the class of trees,
the class of planar graphs, every class of bounded degree,
every class of bounded treewidth, every class that excludes a fixed graph as a (topological) minor, and every class of bounded expansion.

As proved already by Frick and Grohe
[
16
]
,
assuming
\mathsf{AW}[\ast]{\neq}\mathsf{FPT}
,
even the class of trees
does not admit an algorithm
solving the model checking problem for first-order logic
running in time as in (
1
), if we require
f\colon\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}
to be bounded by an elementary function.
That is, when the inputs graphs
G
are trees,
there is no algorithm
with running time

\displaystyle\underbrace{2^{2^{\cdot^{\cdot^{2^{|\varphi|}}}}}}_{\text{height $h$}}\cdot|G|^{c},

(2)

for any constants
c
and
h
, unless
\mathsf{AW}[\ast]{=}\mathsf{FPT}
.
On the other hand, Frick and Grohe observed that every class of bounded degree does admit an algorithm with running time as in (
2
) (with
c=1
and triply exponential dependence on
|\varphi|
).

The aim of this paper is to characterise those monotone graph classes for which the model checking problem for first-order logic is
elementarily-fpt
, that is, can be solved in time as in (
2
) for graphs
G\in\mathcal{C}
, where
c
and
h
are constants.
For instance, by the results of Frick and Grohe, the model checking problem is not elementarily-fpt on the class of trees (assuming
\mathsf{AW}[\ast]{\neq}\mathsf{FPT}
),
while it is such on every class of bounded degree
[
16
]
.
By a result of Gajarský and Hliněný
[
19
]
,
model checking is elementarily-fpt on classes of trees of bounded depth, and more generally, on classes of bounded treedepth (roughly, a graph has bounded treedepth if it has a tree decomposition of bounded depth and bag size).
This result was recently generalized by Lampis
[
29
]
to classes of bounded pathwidth (a graph has bounded pathwidth if it admits a tree decomposition in the form of a path, with bags of bounded size).
Note that the results of Lampis and of Frick and Grohe are incomparable in scope, since bounded degree and bounded pathwidth are incomparable properties.

Contribution

Motivated by the apparent connection between the considered problem
and the existence of deep trees in the class, we define the
tree rank
of a graph class
\mathcal{C}
.
Roughly, the tree rank of a class
\mathcal{C}
is the maximum
d\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{\infty\}
such that
\mathcal{C}
contains
all trees of depth
d
as
r
-shallow topological minors, for some fixed
r
.
By a
tree
in this paper we always mean a rooted tree, that is, a connected acyclic graph with a distinguished root. The
depth
of a tree is the maximal number of vertices on a root-to-leaf path (so stars rooted naturally have depth
2
).
Let
\mathcal{T}_{d}
denote the class of trees of depth
d
.
For a graph class
\mathcal{C}
, let
\textup{TopMinors}_{r}(\mathcal{C})
denote the class of all
r
-shallow topological minors of graphs
G\in\mathcal{C}
.

Definition 1
.

The
tree rank
of a graph class
\mathcal{C}
is defined as:

\textup{tree\,rank}(\mathcal{C}):=\max\{d\in\mathbb{N}\mid\exists r\in\mathbb{N}:\mathcal{T}_{d}\subseteq\textup{TopMinors}_{r}(\mathcal{C})\}.

If this maximum exists, we say that
\mathcal{C}
has
bounded tree rank
and otherwise,
\mathcal{C}
has
unbounded tree rank
.

Clearly, the class of all trees has unbounded tree rank.
On the other hand, every class
\mathcal{C}
that excludes some tree
T
as a topological minor (that is, excludes
T
as an
r
-shallow topological minor, for all
r
),
has tree rank smaller than the depth of
T
.
Below we list some classes that exclude a tree as a topological minor.

Example 1
.

1.

The class of trees of depth
d
has tree rank
d
.

2.

A graph class has bounded degree if and only if it has tree rank
1
.

3.

The class
\mathcal{C}
of graphs of pathwidth
d
has tree rank exactly
d+1
.
The upper bound uses the fact that the complete ternary tree
T
of depth
d+2
has pathwidth
d+1

[
28
, Prop. 3.2]
(see also
[
1
, Theorem 68]
).
It follows that
\mathcal{C}
excludes
T
as a topological minor, so
\mathcal{C}
has tree rank at most
d+1
.
For the lower bound,
\mathcal{C}
includes all trees of depth
d+1
.

We now give examples of classes that contain every tree as a topological minor, and have tree rank
2
.

Example 2
.

Consider the class
\mathcal{C}
consisting of, for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
n
-vertex graph
G
, the graph
G^{(n)}
obtained from
G
by replacing every edge by a path of length
n+1
. Then
\mathcal{C}
has tree rank
2
.

As another example,
consider the following class of trees, which also has tree rank
2
. For every tree
T
, root
T
in any vertex
r
, and construct
T^{\prime}
from
T
by replacing every edge
uv
with a path of length
d+1
, where
d
is the distance from
r
to the closer among
u
and
v
; then the class consists of all trees
T^{\prime}
obtained in this manner.

Clearly, every class of bounded tree rank is nowhere dense.
In fact, those classes have
bounded expansion
, a more restrictive notion which is also central in sparsity theory
[
30
]
(see Proposition
34
).

For our main algorithmic result, we need the following, effective variant of tree rank.

Definition 2
.

The
elementary tree rank
of a class
\mathcal{C}

is the least number
d\in\mathbb{N}
with the following property.
There is an elementary function
f\colon\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}

such that for all
r\in\mathbb{N}
there is a tree
T
of depth
d+1
and of size
f(r)
, such that
T\not\in\textup{TopMinors}_{r}(\mathcal{C})
.
If such
d
exists, we say that
\mathcal{C}
has
bounded elementary tree rank
.

Note that if we drop the requirement that
f
is elementary, the above definition becomes equivalent
to the definition of tree rank. Hence, if
\mathcal{C}
has elementary tree rank at most
d
, then also
\mathcal{C}
has tree rank at most
d
, but the converse implication is false.
However, if
\mathcal{C}
excludes a tree
T
as a topological minor, then the condition in Def.
2
holds for
f\colon\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}
being a constant function (the converse implication also holds).
In particular, all the examples listed in Example
1
have bounded elementary tree rank.

We can now state our main result.

Theorem 3
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a graph class of bounded elementary tree rank.
Then model checking first-order logic is elementarily-fpt on
\mathcal{C}
.

Corollary 4
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a graph class that avoids some fixed tree
T
as a topological minor.
Then model checking first-order logic is elementarily-fpt on
\mathcal{C}
.

Theorem
3
(and even Corollary
4
) vastly extends the result of Frick and Grohe
[
16
]

(concerning classes of bounded degree), as well as the result of Lampis
[
29
]

(concerning classes of bounded pathwidth); see Example
1
.

Conversely, we prove the following lower bound, generalizing (and building upon) the result of Frick and Grohe
[
16
]
.

Theorem 5
.

Assume
\mathsf{AW}[*]{\neq}\mathsf{FPT}
.
Let
\mathcal{C}
be a monotone graph class.
If
\mathcal{C}
admits elementarily-fpt model checking first-order logic,
then
\mathcal{C}
has bounded tree rank.

Thus, Theorem
3
and Theorem
5
almost completely characterise those monotone graph classes
for which the model checking problem is elementarily-fpt.
There is a slight gap left in between the two statements,
as there exist classes that have
bounded tree rank but have unbounded elementary tree rank.

Alternation hierarchy

As another main contribution, we study the expressive power of first-order logic on classes of bounded tree rank.
Namely, we prove that every first-order formula is equivalent on such a class to a formula with a bounded number of quantifier alternations (roughly, the number
of alternations between existential and universal quantifiers in the formula, assuming the negations are only applied to atomic formulas). In all statements to follow, by formulas we mean first-order formulas.

Theorem 6
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a graph class of tree rank
d
.
Then every formula
\varphi
is equivalent on
\mathcal{C}

to a formula
\psi
of alternation rank
3d
.
Also, if
\mathcal{C}
has elementary tree rank
d
,
then the size of
\psi
is elementary in the size of
\varphi
.

Apart from being of independent interest from the logical perspective, this result is a key part of our proof of Theorem
3
.

It is well known that
the alternation hierarchy is strict:
for every number
k
there is a formula
\varphi
of alternation rank
k+1
that is not equivalent
to any formula of alternation rank
k
.
We prove that among monotone graph classes,
classes of bounded tree rank are precisely those classes, for which
the alternation hierarchy collapses.

Theorem 7
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a monotone graph class. Then
\mathcal{C}
has bounded tree rank if and only if there is
k\in\mathbb{N}
such that every formula is equivalent to a formula of alternation rank
k
on
\mathcal{C}
.

The forward implication follows immediately from Theorem
6
, whereas the backward implication
extends the known proof
of the strictness of the alternation hierarchy, due to Chandra and Harel
[
6
]
.

The overview of the proofs of the main results is relegated to Section
2
.

Discussion

The following discussion puts our results in a broader context, of sparsity theory and of model theory.
We also discuss related work.

New notion in sparsity theory

Sparsity theory defines combinatorial notions
of tameness of graph classes, such as bounded expansion and nowhere denseness.
These notions are often defined by forbidding certain
graphs as shallow topological minors,
and the theory then develops decomposition results,
or duality results, which state that such notions can be alternatively characterised in terms of the existence of certain decompositions. Such decompositions often turn out to be useful for algorithmic and logical purposes.

Bounded tree rank is another notion in this vein.
In this paper, we develop basic decomposition results
for such classes, and demonstrate that they can be employed to obtain results in algorithms and logic.
We believe that this new notion will grow to become an important part of sparsity theory.

Towards dense graph classes

A recent trend in algorithmic graph theory is to lift
structural and algorithmic results concerning sparse graphs,
to the setting of dense (or not necessarily sparse) graphs.
Monotone graph classes with fpt model checking of first-order logic are completely understood, and are exactly the nowhere dense classes. In a nowhere dense graph class,
every
n
-vertex graph has
\mathcal{O}(n^{1+\varepsilon})
edges, for every fixed
\varepsilon>0

[
14
]
,
and therefore such classes are considered sparse.

On the other hand, there are hereditary classes with fpt model checking that are not sparse, such as the class of all cliques (trivially), but also
e.g. classes of bounded cliquewidth.

Lifting sparsity theory to the setting of dense classes is a vast project, with many promising results
[
20
,
4
,
3
,
2
,
11
,
10
,
34
]
. It reveals a deep connection between sparsity theory and stability theory, which is now the main branch of model theory.
It is conjectured that a hereditary graph class
\mathcal{C}

has fpt model checking of first-order logic if and only if
\mathcal{C}
is
monadically NIP
, that is,
\mathcal{C}
does not transduce the class of all graphs.
Roughly, a class
\mathcal{C}

transduces
a class
\mathcal{D}
, if there is a first-order formula
\varphi(x,y)

such that every graph
H\in\mathcal{D}
can be obtained from some (colored) graph
G\in\mathcal{C}
, by defining new edges via the formula
\varphi(x,y)
, and then removing some vertices.
Several important notions from sparsity theory and model theory are (conjecturally) characterised and related in terms of forbidding transductions to some classes
[
22
,
5
,
34
]
.
For instance, it is known that model checking is fpt on
structurally nowhere dense
classes
[
11
]
— classes that can be transduced from some nowhere dense class — and this has been very recently extended to
monadically stable
classes — classes that do not transduce the class of all half-graphs. Such classes are typically dense.

Similarly, there are hereditary classes with elementarily-fpt model checking that are not sparse,
as listed below. Here, for a property
\Pi
, we say that a class is
structurally
\Pi
if it can be transduced from a class satisfying
\Pi
.

Example 3
.

1.

A class has bounded shrubdepth
[
23
]
if and only if it can be transduced from a class of trees of bounded depth.
Every class of bounded shrubdepth admits elementarily-fpt model checking of first-order logic, which can be easily derived from
[
19
]
.

2.

A class has structurally bounded degree if it can be transduced from a class of bounded degree.
It follows easily from
[
20
]
that
every class of structurally bounded degree
has elementarily-fpt model checking of first-order logic.

3.

A class has structurally bounded pathwidth if it can be transduced from a class of bounded pathwidth.
It follows easily from
[
29
,
32
]
that
every class of structurally bounded pathwidth
has elementarily-fpt model checking of first-order logic.

The research in this paper is motivated by the following conjecture,
posed by Pilipczuk and Toruńczyk
[
34
, Conjecture 11.26]
.

Conjecture 8

(
[
34
]
)
.

A hereditary graph class
\mathcal{C}
has elementarily-fpt model checking if and only if
\mathcal{C}
does not transduce the class
\mathcal{T}_{d}
of all trees of depth
d
, for some
d\in\mathbb{N}
.

Say that a class
\mathcal{C}
of graphs has

rank
d
if
\mathcal{C}
does not transduce
the class
\mathcal{T}_{d+1}
of trees of depth
d+1
.
All the examples in Ex.
3
have bounded rank, in accordance with the conjecture.
Conjecture
8
predicts
that hereditary classes of bounded rank are precisely those with elementarily-fpt model checking.
(In retrospect, it seems that the conjecture should rather involve an elementary variant of rank, analogous to elementary tree rank, although currently we have no counterexample to Conjecture
8
.)

As a first step towards Conjecture
8
,
in this paper, we focus on the case of monotone graph classes, and more generally (in this context), on weakly sparse graph classes. A graph class
\mathcal{C}
is
weakly sparse
if it avoids some biclique as a subgraph.
Relating the main results of the present paper with Conjecture
8
, in Section
9
we prove the following.

Theorem 9
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a weakly sparse graph class.
Then
\mathcal{C}
has bounded tree rank if and only
if
\mathcal{C}
has bounded rank.

Our work opens a way towards Conjecture
8
.
For instance, it follows from Theorem
9
that classes that can be transduced from a class of bounded elementary tree rank, have bounded rank,
so Conjecture
8
predicts that such classes are elementarily-fpt.
This is left for future work.
Theorem
9
also suggests the following conjecture, generalizing Theorem
7
.

Conjecture 10
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a hereditary graph class.
Then
\mathcal{C}
has bounded rank if and only if
there is
k\in\mathbb{N}
such that every first-order formula is equivalent on
\mathcal{C}
to a formula of alternation rank
k
.

Extensions

Our results can be easily extended in various ways which are by now standard in the area of sparsity theory.
For instance, we may consider classes
\mathcal{C}
of logical structures, not only graphs, and define the tree rank of
\mathcal{C}
as the tree rank of the class of Gaifman graphs underlying the structures in
\mathcal{C}
.
Our results easily extend to this more general setting.

Similarly, apart from the model checking problem,
we may consider problems specified by first-order formulas
\varphi(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})
with free variables, such as query enumeration
(compute an enumerator for all
k
-tuples satisfying
\varphi
), query answering (compute a data structure that determines whether a given
k
-tuple satisfies
\varphi
), and counting answers to queries (compute the number of
k
-tuples satisfying
\varphi
).
All those problems are known to be solvable efficiently on classes of bounded expansion, with preprocessing time
f(|\varphi|)\cdot|G|

and query time
f(|\varphi|)
, for some function
f\colon\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}

(see
[
13
,
26
,
33
]
).
From our results it can be easily derived that for classes of bounded elementary tree rank,
f
can be made an elementary function in all those extensions. We omit the details in this paper.

Related work

The work most related to ours has already been mentioned: the work of Frick and Grohe
[
16
]
, of Gajarský and Hliněný
[
19
]
, and of Lampis
[
29
]
.
See
[
29
]
for a broad overview of other relevant works, in particular in the context of parameterised complexity.

In
[
9
]
, Dawar, Grohe, Kreutzer, and Schweikardt
study upper and lower bounds on the sizes of various normal forms of formulas, on restricted graph classes.
In particular, a non-elementary lower bound on the size of the Gaifman normal form is obtained,
whereas for classes of bounded degree, an elementary upper bound is obtained. It would be interesting to see how the upper and lower bounds studied in that paper relate to our notion of tree rank.

Proof overview

We now describe the main ideas behind the proofs of
Theorem
3
and Theorem
6
.
Our starting point is a game characterisation of tree rank. The game is a modification of the splitter game characterizing nowhere dense graphs, which lies at the core of the model checking result of Grohe, Kreutzer, and Siebertz
[
25
]
.

Fix integers
r,m\in\mathbb{N}
. The
m
-batched Splitter game
of radius
r

on a graph
G
is played between two players,
called Splitter and Localiser. In each round of the game,
first Localiser replaces
G
with a subgraph
G^{\prime}
of
G
of radius at most
r
,
and then Splitter deletes at most
m
vertices from
G^{\prime}
, obtaining a new graph
G^{\prime\prime}
on which the game continues, unless
G^{\prime\prime}
has no vertices; in this case Splitter wins the game.

Theorem 11
.

Let
d\in\mathbb{N}
. The following conditions are equivalent for a graph class
\mathcal{C}
:

1.

\mathcal{C}
has (elementary) tree rank
d
,

2.

There exists an (elementary) function
f\colon\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}
such that for every
r\in\mathbb{N}
Splitter wins the
f(r)
-batched splitter game of radius
r
in at most
d
rounds, on any
G\in\mathcal{C}
.

Theorem
11
is proved in Section
4
through elementary combinatorial methods.
This key result allows us to apply induction on the number of rounds, when considering classes of bounded tree rank.
Using this in combination with the locality property of first-order logic,
we prove Theorem
7
in Section
6
.
Very roughly, locality says that for every first-order formula
\varphi
there is a radius
r
such that the satisfaction of
\varphi
in
G
only depends on the first-order definable properties of radius-
r
balls that can be found in
G
.
This number
r
gives rise to the radius of the Splitter game that is played on
G
. By Theorem
11
, Splitter wins the
f(r)
-batched splitter game of radius
r
in at most
d
rounds.
To obtain the desired formula
\psi
of alternation rank bounded in terms of
d
, essentially, for every radius-
r
ball in
G
we use the fact that there exists an
f(r)
-tuple of vertices whose removal results in a graph in which the inductive hypothesis can be applied. Those
f(r)
many vertices are quantified using existential quantifiers, and this is followed by a formula obtained by inductive hypothesis.

Thus, if
\mathcal{C}
is a fixed class of tree rank
d
,
then every formula
\varphi
is equivalent on
\mathcal{C}

to a formula
\psi
of alternation rank at most
3d
.
Moreover, the size of
\psi
is elementary in terms of the size of
\varphi
, provided
\mathcal{C}
has elementary tree rank
d
.
However, we do not know how to compute
\psi
from
\varphi
in elementary time.
To simplify the reasoning, let us pretend below that we
can
compute
\psi
, given
\varphi
, in elementary time. The actual argument in the proof of Theorem
3
is a bit more involved, and only relies on the existence of
\psi
of size elementary in the size of
\varphi
.

Next we observe (in Proposition
34
) that every class of bounded tree rank has
bounded expansion.
A monotone class

has

if and only if for every

such that all

.
Proposition

Moreover, if

has bounded elementary tree rank, then

witnessing bounded expansion is elementary, that is,

has

.

For classes

of bounded expansion,
there is a known algorithm, due to Dvořák, Král, and Thomas

, that solves the model checking problem in time

.
Other proofs of this result are known

]
.
By inspecting and refining the proof from

]
, we show that the running

whose alternation rank is fixed by a constant, whenever
\mathcal{C}
has elementarily bounded expansion.
Since
\psi
has alternation rank
3d

\mathcal{C}
,
assuming that the formula
\psi
can be computed from
\varphi

in Theorem

In the actual proof, in Section

whose size is elementary in terms of the size of
\varphi

Using the elementarily-fpt algorithm for classes of elementarily bounded expansion and formulas of a fixed alternation rank,
we compute a partition of the vertex set
of a given graph into an elementary number of parts,
where each part consists of vertices of the same first-order type of quantifier rank

is the quantifier rank of the original formula

This is sufficient
to perform model checking, by known arguments.
This completes the proof sketch for Theorem

As mentioned, the lower bounds – Theorem

[

[

In Section

In the converse direction, we bound the tree rank of a weakly sparse class

3
Preliminaries

Graph theory

For a graph
G
, by
|G|
we denote the number of vertices of
G
.
The
length
of a path

is the number of edges in
P
.
The

of a rooted tree

is the maximal number of vertices on a path from the root to any leaf of

. For

we denote by

the rooted tree of depth

in which every non-leaf node has exactly

children, and

the forest consisting of

disjoint copies of

.

The

of a connected graph

is defined as the minimum possible value

such that for some vertex

, all vertices of
G
are at distance at most
r
from
v
, where the
distance
of two vertices is defined as the minimum length of a path connecting them.
If
G
is disconnected, we put
+\infty
as its radius.
Also, given a graph
G
, an integer
r\in\mathbb{N}
, and a vertex
v\in G
,
we denote by
B^{G}_{r}(v)
the set of all vertices
u
of
G
that are at distance at most
r
from
v
in
G
. To unclutter the notation, when it is clear from the context we often use
B_{r}^{G}(v)
instead of

.

Let
H
be a graph. We say that a graph

is an
{\leqslant}r
-subdivision
of

if it can be obtained from

by replacing each edge

of

by a path with endpoints

and with at most

internal vertices.
We say that

is an

-shallow topological minor
of

if

contains an
{\leqslant}r
-subdivision of

Elementary functions

For
a,b\in\mathbb{N}
, define
\mathsf{tower}(a,b)
by induction on

:
\mathsf{tower}(0,b)=b
and
\mathsf{tower}(a,b)=2^{\mathsf{tower}(a-1,b)}
for

.

We do not define elementary functions,
as we only need to rely on the fact that a function

is bounded by an elementary function if and only if there is some

such that

, for all

.
Also importantly,

, for any constant

.

Tree rank

Recall the notion of tree rank (see Def.
1
).
It is easily seen that the tree rank of a graph class
\mathcal{C}
is the least number

such that for every

there exists

such that
\mathcal{C}
does not contain

as an
r
-shallow topological minor.

Similarly, it is easily seen (see Def.

) that the elementary tree rank of a graph class

is the least number

such that there is an elementary function

such that for every

does not contain

as an

-shallow topological minor.

4
Game characterisation

In this section we prove a characterization of classes of bounded tree rank via games.

Batched splitter game

Let
r,m\in\mathbb{N}
. The
m
-
batched splitter game
with radius
r
is played by two players, Splitter and Localiser, who play on a graph
G
.
At the beginning we set
G_{0}\coloneqq G
.
For
i\geqslant 1
, in the
i
-th round the players make a move as follows:

•

Localiser picks
v\in G_{i-1}
and sets
G_{i-1}^{\prime}\coloneqq B_{r}^{G_{i-1}}(v)
.

•

Splitter removes at most
m
vertices from
G_{i-1}^{\prime}
to obtain
G_{i}
.

Splitter wins when
V(G_{i})
is empty.

Note that on any finite graph Splitter can always win in a finite number of rounds, as his move decreases the number of vertices in
G
in each round. We will be interested in the smallest number of rounds such that Splitter can win the
m
-batched splitter game with radius
r
.
In particular, we will be interested in graph classes for which there exists
d\in\mathbb{N}
such that the following holds: For every
r\in\mathbb{N}
there is
m\in\mathbb{N}
so that Splitter wins the
m
-batched splitter game with radius
r
in at most
d
rounds on any
G\in\mathcal{C}
.
As a special case, consider any class
\mathcal{C}
of graphs of maximum degree at most
t
. Then, for every
r
, we can set
m\coloneqq t^{r}+1
and one easily checks that Splitter wins in one round on any
G\in\mathcal{C}
.

The statement below describes our characterisation of tree rank. Its proof spans the rest of this section.

Theorem 12
.

Let
d\in\mathbb{N}
with
d\geqslant 1
.
The following are equivalent for any graph class
\mathcal{C}
:

1.

\mathcal{C}
has tree rank
d
,

2.

There exists a function
g\colon\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}
such that, for every
r\in\mathbb{N}
, Splitter wins the
g(r)
-batched splitter game of radius
r
in
d
rounds, on any
G\in\mathcal{C}
.

Moreover,
\mathcal{C}
has elementary tree rank
d
if and only if there is an elementary function
g
as in condition 2 above.

4.1
Proof of implication
(1)\to(2)

This is the more involved of the two implications. The technical core is Lemma
14
, which tells us that in a graph without an
{\leqslant}r
-subdivision of
T^{d+1}_{k}
, in the
r
-neighborhood of any vertex we can find a bounded number of vertices such that removing them makes this
r
-neighborhood simpler. These will be the moves of Splitter. We start with a simple lemma which will be used in the proof of Lemma

.

Lemma 13
.

Let
r,t\in\mathbb{N}
and let
G
be a graph of radius at most
r
. Let
S\subseteq V(G)
.
If
|S|\geqslant t^{r}+1
, then there exists
u\in V(G)
such that there are
t
vertices in

which are distinct from

and can be reached from

by internally vertex-disjoint paths of length at most
r
.

Proof.

By assumption,
G
has a rooted spanning tree

of depth at most
r+1
. There is a node

of
T
such that
t
of the subtrees of
T
rooted at
u
contain a node in

, as otherwise we would have that

. Then

satisfies the required condition.
∎

For a graph

and

, we denote by

the set of all

such that

is the root of an
{\leqslant}r
-subdivision of

in

.

Lemma 14
.

For every

with

there exist

and

such that the following holds.
Let

be a graph which does not contain

as an
(r-1)
-shallow topological minor. Then, for every

, we have that

.

Moreover, for any fixed

, the dependence of

and

on

and

We remark that the difference between values

and

in the lemma is caused by the fact that in an

-subdivision of a graph the subdivision paths can have length up to

Proof.

Let

be the maximum number of vertices any
{\leqslant}(r-1)
-subdivision
of

,

and

.

Suppose for contradiction that for some

we have that

.
By Lemma

, there is a vertex

and
t

v_{1},\ldots,v_{t}
, distinct from

and reachable from

belongs to

, or, in other words, every

is a root of an
{\leqslant}{(r-1)}
-subdivision

of

in

.
Our goal is to use

together with pairs

{\leqslant}{(r-1)}
-subdivision of

in

We will first adjust each

by sacrificing some branches to ensure that

intersects the remaining tree only in

as follows.

, let

. Then

intersects at most

there will be at least

which contains an
{\leqslant}{(r-1)}
-subdivision of

as a subgraph, and

is disjoint from

, except for their common vertex

.

We thus have

intersects

and each

is a
{\leqslant}{(r-1)}

.
We now proceed by extracting from

a set of

•

is an
{\leqslant}{(r-1)}
-subdivision of

•

•

(V(W_{i})\cup V(Q_{i}))\cap(V(W_{j})\cup V(Q_{j}))=\{u\}

The vertex

and paths joining them to

{\leqslant}{(r-1)}
-subdivision of

in

We start with

We keep the invariant that after

and for each

contains a subdivision of

, where

For each

-th round we do the following:

•

We pick any

•

We remove from

•

From every

{\leqslant}{(r-1)}
-subdivision of

{\leqslant}{(r-1)}
-subdivision of

•

Finally, we set

By our choices of

, this process will last for at least

It remains to argue that for fixed

and

can have at most

∎

With the previous lemma at hand we can now show the existence of a winning strategy for Splitter. The idea is that at every round, Splitter removes a suitable set

intersected with the ball played by the Localiser, and Lemma

Lemma 15
.

For any

with

and

there exists

such that if

-shallow topological minor, then Splitter wins the

We proceed by induction on

, we set

. Since

-shallow topological minor, then the maximum degree of any vertex of

For

,
k
, and

. Let

, and

{\leqslant}{(r-1)}
-subdivision of

.
By the induction hypothesis applied to

and

, there exists

such that Splitter wins the

and

, for a fixed

∎

.

Proof of implication

This implication follows easily from the following lemma.

Moreover, if

is an elementary function of
r
, then clearly so is
f
, as desired.
∎

Auxiliary definitions and logical preliminaries

In this section we collect the necessary definitions and tools concerning logic which will be used later on.

5.1
(r,m)
-game rank

We will use the following auxiliary definitions related to classes of bounded tree rank. These are based on
m
-batched splitter game instead of excluding trees as
r
-shallow topological minors. This is motivated by Theorem
12
.

Definition 17
.

Let
r,m
be integers.
We say that graph

has
(r,m)
-game rank

-batched splitter game with radius

rounds.

It follows easily from Definition
17
and Theorem
12
that a graph class
\mathcal{C}
has tree rank at most

if for every

there exists
m\in\mathbb{N}
such that every

-game rank at most

Lemma 18
.

Let
G
be a graph of
(r,m)
-game rank at most

. If

, then

is of
(r^{\prime},m)
-game rank at most

.

For a graph
G
and a subset
S
of its vertices we define

as the operation that isolates the vertices from

in

.

.

Let

We define

Using the above definition, we prove the following result.

.

Let

5.2
Tools from logic

We assume familiarity with basic notions of the first-order logic: signatures, formulas, quantifier rank.

We use
{\bar{x}},{\bar{y}},
etc to denote sets of variables.
Graphs are modeled as structures over a single binary predicate symbol
E
.
Often, we will speak of
colored
graphs – these are graphs equipped with finitely many colors modeled as unary predicates. To each class of colored graphs, we associate a signature
\Sigma
which consists of the binary predicate symbol
E
and finitely many unary predicate symbols. Unless explicitly specified otherwise, all classes of structures considered in this paper will be (colored) graphs, and we will therefore use letters
G,H,\ldots
for structures and
V(G),V(H),\ldots
for their underlying universes.

Gaifman’s theorem

Several of our results will rely on Gaifman’s locality theorem.
We say that a first-order formula
\gamma(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})
is
r
-
local
if for every
G
and every
v_{1},\ldots,v_{k}\in V(G)
we have that
G\models\gamma(v_{1},\ldots,v_{k})\Leftrightarrow B^{G}_{r}(v_{1},\ldots,v_{k})\models\gamma(v_{1},\ldots,v_{k})
where
B^{G}_{r}(v_{1},\ldots,v_{k})=\bigcup_{1\leqslant i\leqslant k}B^{G}_{r}(v_{i})
.
A
basic local sentence
is a sentence of the form

\tau\>\equiv\>\exists x_{1}\ldots\exists x_{k}\left(\bigwedge_{1\leqslant i<j\leqslant k}\mathrm{dist}(x_{i},x_{j})>2r\land\bigwedge_{1\leqslant i\leqslant k}\alpha(x_{i})\right),

where
\alpha(x)
is
r
-local and
\mathrm{dist}(x,y)>2r
is a formula expressing that the distance between
x
and
y
is larger than
2r
. The number
r
is called the
locality radius
of the basic local sentence
\tau
.

Theorem 21

(Gaifman’s locality theorem
[
17
]
)
.

Every first-order formula
\varphi(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})
is equivalent to a boolean combination of a
7^{q}
-local formula
\rho(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})
and basic
local sentences with locality radius
7^{q}
, where
q
is the quantifier rank of
\varphi
. Furthermore, the quantifier rank of the resulting sentences is at most
f(q)
, where
f
is some elementary non-decreasing function.

The assertion in the last statement of the theorem is not claimed explicitly in
[
17
]
, but follows easily from the proof.
Note that if
\varphi
is a sentence, then Gaifman’s theorem says that
\varphi
is equivalent to a boolean combination of basic local sentences (there is no formula
\rho
).

Interpretations

In some of our proofs we will rely on the notion of
interpretation
,
which is a mechanism using logical formulas to produce new graphs from old ones. We present it here specialized to our setting of (colored) graphs.
Let
\Sigma
,
\Gamma
be two signatures of colored graphs and let
p
be the number of unary predicate symbols in
\Gamma
. An
interpretation
from
\Sigma
to
\Gamma
is a tuple
I=(\psi_{E},\vartheta,\theta_{1},\ldots\theta_{p})
of
\Sigma
-formulas such that
\psi_{E}
has two free variables and all other formulas in
I
have one free variable. To any graph with unary predicates from
\Sigma
an interpretation
I
assigns new graph
H\coloneqq I(G)
such that
V(H)\coloneqq\{v~|~G\models\vartheta(v)\}
,
E(G)\coloneqq\{uv~|~u,v\in V(H),G\models\psi_{E}(u,v)\}
, and the
i
-th unary predicate of
\Gamma
is realized by the set
S_{i}\coloneqq\{v\in V(H)~|~G\models\theta_{i}(v)\}
.

Interpretations with parameters

In our proof of Theorem
22
we will consider a slightly less well-known form of interpretations which use parameters.
We first present the notion for uncolored graphs.
Fix two formulas
\vartheta(x,y_{1},\ldots,y_{k})
and
\psi_{E}(x_{1},x_{2},y_{1},\ldots,y_{k})
and set
I\coloneqq(\vartheta,\psi_{E})
; the variables
y_{1},\ldots,y_{k}
are called
parameters
.
For any graph
G
and vertices
u_{1},\ldots,u_{k}\in V(G)
these formulas define a new graph
H=I(G,u_{1},\ldots,u_{k})
with

V(H)\coloneqq\{w\in V(G)~|~G\models\vartheta(w,u_{1},\ldots,u_{k})\}

E(H)\coloneqq\{w_{1}w_{2}\in V(H)~|~G\models\psi_{E}(w_{1},w_{2},u_{1},\ldots,u_{k})\}

We include two examples, which will be used in our proofs. First, there is an interpretation that from
G
and
v,u_{1},\ldots,u_{k}
produces the graph
B^{G}_{r}(v)-\{u_{1},\ldots,u_{k}\}
. This uses
k+1
parameters and consists of formulas
\vartheta(x,y_{0},y_{1},\ldots,y_{k})\coloneqq(\mathrm{dist}(x,y_{0})\leqslant r)\land(x\not=y_{1})\land\ldots\land(x\not=y_{k})
and
\psi_{E}(x_{1},x_{2},y_{0},\ldots,y_{k})\coloneqq E(x_{1},x_{2})
.
The second example is an interpretation that from
G
and
v,u_{1},\ldots,u_{k}
produces the graph
B^{G}_{r}(v)\ast\{u_{1},\ldots,u_{k}\}
. This is given by formulas
\vartheta(x,y_{0},y_{1},\ldots,y_{k})\coloneqq\mathrm{dist}(x,y_{0})\leqslant r
and

\psi_{E}(x_{1},x_{2},y_{0},y_{1},\ldots,y_{k})\coloneqq E(x_{1},x_{2})\land\bigwedge_{1\leqslant i\leqslant k}(x_{1}\not=y_{i})\land\bigwedge_{1\leqslant i\leqslant k}(x_{2}\not=y_{i}).

In the setting of colored graphs, say with
t
colors, we use
t
additional formulas
\theta_{1}(x,y_{1},\ldots,y_{k}),\ldots,\theta_{t}(x,y_{1},\ldots,y_{k})
, we set
I\coloneqq\{\vartheta,\psi_{E},\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{t}\}
and for each
i
we mark in
H=I(G,u_{1},\ldots,u_{k})
the vertices from the set

S_{i}\coloneqq\{w\in V(G)~|~G\models\theta_{i}(w,u_{1},\ldots,u_{k})\land\vartheta(w,u_{1},\ldots,u_{k})\}

by a unary predicate
C_{i}
.

6
Bounded alternation rank

This section is devoted to the proof of the following theorem, which is a restatement of one direction of Theorem
6
suited for formulas with free variables. See Section
6.1
for the definition of the alternation rank of a formula.
Roughly, it is the number of alternations
between quantifiers
\forall
and
\exists
in a formula which applies negations only to atomic formulas.

Theorem 22
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a graph class of tree rank
d
. Every formula
\varphi({\bar{x}})
is equivalent on
\mathcal{C}
to some formula

\psi({\bar{x}})
of alternation rank at most
3d-1
.
Moreover, if
\mathcal{C}
is a class of elementary tree rank
d
then the size of
\psi
is elementary in the size of
\varphi
.

We now briefly discuss the high-level idea behind the proof of Theorem
22
.
From Gaifman’s theorem (Theorem
21
) we know that deciding whether
G\models\varphi
can be reduced to evaluating formulas on
r
-neighborhoods of vertices of
G
.
Moreover, from the Splitter game characterisation (Theorem
12
) we know that for any class
\mathcal{C}
of graphs of tree rank
d
we have the property that for any
G\in\mathcal{C}
and
v\in V(G)
we can make the
r
-neighborhood of
v
simpler (of rank
d-1
) by removing a bounded number of vertices.
Combining these two facts leads to a proof of Theorem
22
by induction on the tree rank of
\mathcal{C}
. First, we express
\varphi
in terms of local formulas. Since these formulas need to be only evaluated on small neighborhoods around vertices of
G
, and these neighborhoods can easily be made of rank
d-1
, we can replace these local formulas by equivalent formulas of desired alternation rank obtained from induction hypothesis. We then combine these formulas to obtain
\psi
.

While this plan is conceptually simple, it comes with certain technical difficulties.
First, one has to be careful about what it means to make the
r
-neighborhood of some vertex
v
simpler by removing a bounded number of vertices. Consider a graph
G
such that Splitter wins the
m
-batched splitter game of radius
r
on
G
in
d
rounds. Then we know that if we pick any
v
in
G
, we can remove a small set
S
of vertices from
B^{G}_{r}(v)
to get a graph on which Splitter can win the game in
d-1
rounds. However, this makes the graph simpler only with respect to this particular
r
, and not necessarily with respect to larger radii. In short, the graph class

\mathcal{C}^{\prime}\coloneqq\{B^{G}_{r}(v)-S~|~G\in\mathcal{C},~v\in V(G),~S\text{ is $v$-good}\}

where being
v
-good means that Splitter wins in
d-1
rounds on
B^{G}_{r}(v)-S
, is not necessarily of tree rank
d-1
. This means that we cannot apply the induction hypothesis to
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
. To resolve this, we strengthen the induction hypothesis – we will prove for every formula
\varphi
there exists an
r\in\mathbb{N}
such that if
\mathcal{C}
is any class for which Splitter has a winning strategy with small batch size to win the game of radius
r
in at most
d
rounds on any
G\in\mathcal{C}
, then there exists a formula

\psi
equivalent to
\varphi
on
\mathcal{C}
.

Second, the formulas we obtain from the induction hypothesis only work as desired on
r
-neighborhoods in
G
(and even that only after removing some vertices), while the formula
\psi
has to work on the whole graph
G
.
To get around this, the formula
\psi
essentially does the following: it considers
B^{G}_{r}(v)
for any
v\in G
it needs, identifies vertices which lead to Splitter’s win in
d-1
rounds, removes/isolates them, evaluates the formula of small alternation rank obtained from induction hypothesis, and then translates the result to the original graph. This is achieved by using interpretations with parameters, introduced in Section
5
and further discussed in Section
6.2
.

6.1
Alternation rank and batched formulas

Let
\Sigma_{i}
denote the set of formulas
in prenex normal form
of the form

\underbrace{\exists^{*}\forall^{*}\exists^{*}\cdots}_{i\text{ blocks}}\varphi,

where
\varphi
is quantifier-free.
Dually, let
\Pi_{i}
denote the set of formulas of the the form

\underbrace{\forall^{*}\exists^{*}\forall^{*}\cdots}_{i\text{ blocks}}\varphi.

Formally,
\Sigma_{0}
and
\Pi_{0}
are quantifier-free formulas,
while for
i>0
, the set
\Sigma_{i}
consists of formulas of the form

\exists\bar{x}.\varphi(\bar{x},\bar{y}),

for
\varphi\in\Pi_{i-1}
,
and dually,
\Pi_{i}
consists of formulas of the form
\forall\bar{x}.\varphi(\bar{x},\bar{y}),

for
\varphi\in\Sigma_{i-1}
.

Note that every
\Sigma_{i}
-formula is equivalent to a negation of a
\Pi_{i}
-formula, and vice-versa.
Let
B\Sigma_{i}
denote the set of boolean combinations of formulas in
\Sigma_{i}
.
Those are equivalent to positive boolean combinations of formulas in
\Sigma_{i}
and
\Pi_{i}
.

A formula
\varphi
is in
negation normal form

if it contains no negations, apart from
the literals (atomic formulas or their negations).
Every formula
\varphi
can be converted into negation
normal form by using de Morgan’s rules, in polynomial time.

For a formula
\varphi
, we define its
alternation rank
as follows.
First, convert
\varphi
into negation normal form.
Next, consider the maximum, over all branches in the syntax tree of
\varphi
, of the number of alternations between the symbols
\forall
and
\exists
.
Namely, a branch in the syntax tree of
\varphi

determines a word over the alphabet
\{\lor,\land,\exists,\forall\}
.
We ignore the symbols
\lor
and
\land
in this word, obtaining a word
over the alphabet
\{\exists,\forall\}
.
The alternation number of this branch is the number of subwords of the form
\exists\forall
or
\forall\exists
.
The alternation number of a formula in negation normal form is the maximal alternation number of all branches in the syntax tree.

For
m\in\mathbb{N}
, we define the set of
m
-batched formulas
inductively, as follows. Simultaneously, for each such formula we define its
batched quantifier rank
.

•

Any atomic formula is an
m
-batched formula of batched quantifier rank
0
.

•

Any boolean combination of
m
-batched formulas
\varphi_{1},\ldots,\varphi_{k}
is an
m
-batched formula,
whose batched quantifier rank is equal to the maximal batched quantifier rank of the formulas
\varphi_{1},\ldots,\varphi_{k}
.

•

If
\varphi
is an
m
-batched formula, then also
(\exists x_{1}\ldots\exists x_{k})\varphi
and
(\forall x_{1}\ldots\forall x_{k})\varphi
are
m
-batched formulas, where
k\leqslant m
and
x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}
are any variables.
The batched quantifier rank of those formulas is one plus the batched quantifier rank of
\varphi
.

A
batched formula
is an
m
-batched formula, for some
m
.

Lemma 23
.

Fix
q\geqslant 1
. The following conditions are equivalent for a formula
\varphi
:

1.

\varphi
is equivalent to a
B\Sigma_{q}
-formula,

2.

\varphi
is equivalent to a batched formula of batched quantifier rank
q
,

3.

\varphi
is equivalent to a
formula of alternation rank
q-1
.

Moreover, all translations are computable in polynomial time.

Proof.

The implications 1
\rightarrow
2 and 2
\rightarrow
3 are immediate.
The implication 3
\rightarrow
1
follows by using the standard conversion into prenex normal form, which does not increase the alternation rank.
∎

Lemma 24
.

Fix
q\in\mathbb{N}
.
There is an elementary function
f_{q}\colon\mathbb{N}^{3}\to\mathbb{N}

such that for all
m\in\mathbb{N}
,
k\in\mathbb{N}
and signature
\Sigma
, every
m
-batched
\Sigma
-formula
\varphi(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})
of batched quantifier rank
q

is equivalent to an
m
-batched
\Sigma
-formula
\psi(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})

of batched quantifier rank
q
, of size at most
f_{q}(m,k,|\Sigma|)
.

Proof.

We proceed
by induction on
q
. For
q=0
the statement is trivial, since for fixed
k
and
\Sigma
, the number of distinct atomic
\Sigma
-formulas on variables among
x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}
is an elementary function of
k
and the number of symbols in
\Sigma
.
In the inductive step,
every
m
-batched formula
\varphi
is a boolean combination of formulas of the form
(\exists x_{1}\ldots\exists x_{\ell})\varphi^{\prime}

for
\ell\leqslant m
and an
m
-batched formula
\varphi^{\prime}
of batched quantifier rank
q-1
with at most
k+m
free variables.
By inductive assumption, we may convert each
\varphi^{\prime}

to a batched
\Sigma
-formula
\psi^{\prime}
of size at most
f_{q-1}(m,k+m,|\Sigma|)
and batched quantifier rank
q-1
.
Thus,
\varphi
is equivalent to a boolean combination
\varphi_{1}
of
m
-batched formulas of batched quantifier rank
k
and of size at most
f_{q-1}(m,k+m,|\Sigma|)+m
each. Let
f(s)
denote the number of distinct formulas of size at most
s
; then
f
is an elementary function.
Thus,
\varphi_{1}
is a boolean combination of at most
f(f_{q-1}(m,k+m,|\Sigma|)+m)
different formulas.
Let
g(k)
denote the
smallest number
t
such that every boolean combination of
k
atoms is equivalent to a boolean combination of size at most
t
. Then
g
is a singly-exponential function.
Hence,
\varphi_{1}
, and therefore
\varphi
, is equivalent to a boolean combination of size at most
g(f(f_{q-1}(m,k+m,|\Sigma|)+m))
, of
m
-batched formulas of size at most
f_{q-1}(m,k+m,|\Sigma|)+m
.

Define
f_{q}(m,k,|\Sigma|)=g(f(f_{q-1}(m,k+m,|\Sigma|)+m))\cdot(f_{q-1}(m,k+m,|\Sigma|)+m)
.
Then
f_{q}
is an elementary function, and satisfies the condition of the lemma.
∎

6.2
Further preliminaries

In the proof of Theorem
22
we will repeatedly use the following fact about interpretations with parameters.
Let
G
be a graph,
I
an interpretation with parameters and
\varphi
a sentence.
If
\varphi
is a sentence and we want to determine whether
I(G,v_{1},\ldots,v_{k})\models\varphi
, it is enough to evaluate a suitable formula
\hat{\varphi}
on
G,v_{1},\ldots,v_{k}
. This is formalized as follows.

Lemma 25
.

Fix an interpretation
I
with parameters
y_{1},\ldots,y_{k}
.
Let
\varphi
be a sentence. Then one can rewrite
\varphi
into a formula
\hat{\varphi}(y_{1},\ldots,y_{k})
such that for every
G
and
u_{1},\ldots,u_{k}\in V(G)
we have that

I(G,u_{1},\ldots,u_{k})\models\varphi\Longleftrightarrow G\models\hat{\varphi}(u_{1},\ldots,u_{k}).

Moreover, if
\varphi
is
a
-batched of batched quantifier rank
\alpha
and each formula in the interpretation is
b
-batched of batched quantifier rank
\beta
, then we can make
\hat{\varphi}
to be
c
-batched with batched quantifier rank at most
\alpha+\beta
, where
c\coloneqq\max\{a,b\}
.

We only sketch the proof of the lemma, as the first part of it is standard, and the second part follows by a simple modification of the proof of the first part.

Proof sketch.

To obtain
\hat{\varphi}
, we first replace each occurrence of
E(z_{1},z_{2})

by
\psi_{E}(z_{1},z_{2},y_{1},\ldots,y_{k})
(here
z_{1},z_{2}
are any variables), and each occurrence of unary predicate symbol
C_{1}(z)
by
\theta_{i}(z,y_{1},\ldots,y_{k})
. Moreover, the quantifications occurring in
\varphi
are relativized in
\hat{\varphi}
to vertices which satisfy
\vartheta
– every subformula
\exists z\rho(z)
of
\varphi
is replaced by
\exists z(\vartheta(z,y_{1},\ldots,y_{k})\land\rho(z))
and every
\forall z\rho(z)
of
\varphi
is replaced by
\forall z(\vartheta(z,y_{1},\ldots,y_{k})\rightarrow\rho(z))
. One can then prove the result by induction on the structure of
\varphi
.

If we work with batched formulas, we first turn all formulas in
\{\varphi\}\cup I
into
c
-batched formulas (by adding dummy quantifiers where necessary). Then we proceed as above, but we handle the relativization of quantifiers in batches, meaning that
\exists z_{1}\ldots\exists z_{c}~\rho(z_{1},\ldots,z_{c})
is replaced with

\exists z_{1}\ldots\exists z_{c}\bigl(\bigwedge_{1\leqslant i\leqslant c}\vartheta(z_{i},y_{1},\ldots,y_{k})\land\rho(z_{1},\ldots,z_{c})\bigr)

and analogously for the batched universal quantifier.
Again, induction on the structure of
\varphi
yields the result.
The bound on the batched quantifier rank of
\hat{\varphi}
follows form considering the syntactic tree of
\varphi
and how it is changes when constructing
\hat{\varphi}
.
∎

Formulas for batched Splitter game

Finally, we will need batched formulas of small batched quantifier rank to express that Splitter wins the batched Splitter game.

Lemma 26
.

Let
d,r,m\in\mathbb{N}
with
d\geqslant 1
. Set
b:=\max\{m+1,r\}
. There exists a
b
-batched sentence
\mathit{Rank}_{d}^{r,m}
of batched quantifier rank
3d-2
such that for every
G
we have
G\models\mathit{Rank}_{d}^{r,m}
if and only if the
(r,m)
-game rank of
G
is at most
d
.

Moreover, for every
d>1
there exists a
b
-batched formula
\mathit{RankDown}_{d-1}^{r,m}(x,y_{1},\ldots,y_{m})
of
b
-batched quantifier rank
3d-4
such that for any graph
G
and vertices
v,u_{1},\ldots,u_{m}\in V(G)
we have that

G\models\mathit{RankDown}_{d-1}^{r,m}(x,y_{1},\ldots,y_{m})
if and only if
the
(r,m)
-game rank of
B^{G}_{r}(v)-\{u_{1},\ldots,u_{m}\}
is at most
d-1
.

Proof.

For
d=1
, Splitter loses if and only if there exists a vertex such that there are at least
m+1
vertices in its
r
-neighborhood. This is easily expressed by an
(m+1)
-batched existential sentence, which is of batched quantifier rank
1
. By taking its negation, we get the result.

Let
d>1
.
Let
\mathit{Rank}_{d-1}^{r,m}
be the
b
-batched sentence of batched quantifier rank
3(d-1)-2
obtained from the induction hypothesis.
The operation which maps
G
and
v,u_{1},\ldots,u_{m}
to
G^{\prime}:=B^{G}_{r}(v)-\{u_{1},\ldots,u_{m}\}
can be modeled by an
r
-batched interpretation with parameters (that is independent of
G
) of
r
-batched quantifier rank
1
, and therefore, by Lemma
25
, there exists a formula
\mathit{RankDown}_{d-1}^{r,m}(y_{0},y_{1},\ldots,y_{m})
of
b
-batched quantifier rank
3(d-1)-1
such that

B^{G}_{r}(v)-\{u_{1},\ldots,u_{m}\}\models\mathit{Rank}_{d-1}^{r,m}\Longleftrightarrow G\models\mathit{RankDown}_{d-1}^{r,m}(v,u_{1},\ldots,u_{m}).

Then we can set

\mathit{Rank}_{d}^{r,m}:=\forall x\exists x_{1}\ldots\exists x_{m}\mathit{RankDown}_{d-1}(x,y_{1},\ldots,y_{m})

which expresses that for every move of Localiser there exist
m

Model checking

In this section, we prove the existence of
an elementarily-fpt model checking algorithm
for classes of bounded elementary tree rank.
The following statement in particular implies Theorem
3
.

Theorem 33
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a class of elementary tree rank
d
, for some
d\in\mathbb{N}
.
Then model checking first-order logic is elementarily-fpt on
\mathcal{C}
.
More precisely, there exists an elementary function
f

and an algorithm that, given a graph
G\in\mathcal{C}
and
first-order sentence
\varphi
,
determines whether
G\models\varphi

in time

f(|\varphi|)\cdot|G|.

The idea is as follows. We first prove that every class of bounded tree rank has bounded expansion.
We then use the result of Dvořák, Král, and Thomas
[
13
]
,
which states that every sentence can be tested in linear time on graphs from a fixed class of bounded expansion. By inspecting the proof, we observe that the running time of this algorithm is elementarily-fpt for sentences of alternation rank bounded by a fixed constant. However, Theorem
22
does not allow us to directly convert (in elementary time) a given sentence
\varphi
into a sentence of bounded alternation rank.
Nevertheless, it implies that in order to determine whether two vertices satisfy the same formulas
\psi(x)
of a given quantifier rank
q
, it is enough to test
whether they satisfy the same formulas of alternation rank bounded by a fixed constant (independent of
q
). It is known that model checking can be reduced to the problem
of deciding whether two vertices satisfy the same formulas of a given quantifier rank. Combining those observations yields the required result. We now present the details.

We start by showing that every class of bounded tree rank has bounded expansion, whose definition we now recall.
For a graph
G
and number
r\in\mathbb{N}
,
let
\tilde{\nabla}_{r}(G)
denote the maximum
average degree,
2|E(H)|/|V(H)|
, among all
r
-shallow topological minors

of

.
A class
\mathcal{C}
has

if for every

there is a constant

such that
\tilde{\nabla}_{r}(G)\leqslant c_{r}
for all
G\in\mathcal{C}
. Equivalently, one can postulate that
\tilde{\nabla}_{r}(\mathcal{C})\leqslant\infty
for every
r\in\mathbb{N}
, where we define
\tilde{\nabla}_{r}(\mathcal{C})\coloneqq\sup_{G\in\mathcal{C}}\tilde{\nabla}_{r}(G)
.

Fix

.

Rank vs tree rank

In this section we compare the notion of tree rank of a graph class with a more general notion of
rank
. The notion of rank is defined using transductions, which we present here in the setting of colored graphs. A transduction is an operation which inputs a graph, adds some unary predicates in an arbitrary way, and applies a fixed interpretation,
thus obtaining an output graph. This is formalized below.

Let
\Sigma
,
\Gamma
be two signatures of colored graphs and let
p
be the number of unary predicates in
\Gamma
. A
transduction

P:\Sigma\to\Gamma
consists of unary predicate symbols
L_{1},\ldots,L_{m}
and an interpretation
I
from
\Sigma\cup\{L_{1},\ldots,L_{m}\}
to
\Gamma
. We say that
H
is an output of
P
on
G
if one can mark some vertices of
G
by unary predicates
L_{1},\ldots,L_{m}
to obtain a colored graph
G^{\prime}
such that
H=I(G^{\prime})
. Note that in general there are multiple outputs of
P
on any
G
, and so
P
generates a
set
of outputs on
G
and not just a single graph. We denote the set of outputs of
P
on
G
by
P(G)
and denote the fact that
H
is an output of
P
on
G
by
H\in P(G)
.

Let
P\colon\Sigma\to\Gamma
be a transduction and let
I=\{\psi_{E},\vartheta,\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{p}\}
be the interpretation used in
P
.
Since in a transduction we can mark vertices in
G
by unary predicates in an arbitrary way, we can assume that the formulas
\vartheta
and
\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{p}
from
I
are of the form
\vartheta(x):=L^{\prime}(x)
and
\theta_{i}(x):=L_{i}^{\prime}(x)
for some unary predicates
L^{\prime},L_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,L_{p}^{\prime}
. Thus, at the cost of introducing
p+1
extra unary predicate symbols, we can assume that all formulas in
P
except
\psi_{E}
are quantifier-free. We then define the
quantifier rank of a transduction
to be the quantifier rank of
\psi_{E}
. We also define the
number of unary predicates used by
P
to be
m
plus the number of unary predicates in
\Sigma
.

Transductions can be applied to graph classes component-wise:
P(\mathcal{C}):=\bigcup_{G\in\mathcal{C}}P(G)
. We say that a graph class
\mathcal{C}
transduces graph class

In this section, we prove the following.

The following result is due to Kierstead and Penrice

(Kierstead and Penrice,
[

]
)

Let

(Dvořák,
[

]
)
.

Let
\mathcal{C}

,

contains the

.

.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a weakly sparse class of unbounded expansion.

Proof.

Without loss of generality

.

By a

as the general case proceeds by an repeated application of this case (see below for more details).
The proof of Theorem

1.

First we prove the statement in the case where

is a class of forests (possibly vertex-colored).

2.

Next, we prove the statement in the case where
\mathcal{C}
is a class of relational structures of bounded treedepth (meaning that the class of underlying Gaifman graphs has bounded treedepth). This case is solved by reducing to the case of forests of bounded depth, by considering the elimination forests of the Gaifman graphs of the structures in
\mathcal{C}
, expanded with colors used to encode the relations.

3.

Finally, to prove the general case, we reduce it to the previous one, using low treedepth colorings.

We start with considering the special case when
\mathcal{C}
is a class of forests of bounded depth.

Monadic Second Order logic

In the course of the proof of Lemma
57
it will be much more convenient to work with a more powerful Monadic Second Order logic (MSO), allowing for quantification over sets of vertices: that is, each variable
X
in an MSO formula may be interpreted as a set of vertices of a forest
F
.
More precisely, a
valuation
of a collection
{\bar{X}}
of set variables is a function
{\bar{A}}\colon V(T)\to 2^{{\bar{X}}}
.

For convenience, we will actually assume that all variables in MSO formulas are set variables: a vertex
v
will be modeled by a singleton
\{v\}
.
Given a set variable
X
, we define the predicate
\emph{singleton}(X)
verifying that
X
contains exactly one element.
Then, MSO formulas on forests can use the function symbol
\mathit{parent}
, the predicate
\emph{singleton}
, test for the containment and equality of sets and quantify over sets of vertices.

It can be easily verified that a formula (resp. quantifier-free formula, existential formula)
\varphi
of first-order logic can be rewritten in linear time into an equivalent formula (resp. quantifier-free formula, existential formula)
\varphi^{\prime}
of monadic second-order logic with the same number of variables, and that
|\varphi^{\prime}|\leqslant\mathcal{O}(|\varphi|)
.

Tree valuation automata

For a set
Q
, let
M(Q)
denote
the set of
multisets of elements of
Q
, which are formally represented by functions
A\colon Q\to\mathbb{N}
counting the number of occurrences of each element of
Q
in the multiset.
For
q_{1},\ldots,q_{n}\in Q
,
let
\{\!\!\{q_{1},\ldots,q_{n}\}\!\!\}
denote
the element of
M(Q)
represented by the function
A\colon Q\to\{0,1,\ldots,n\}

such that
A(q)=\#\{i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}\mid q_{i}=q\}
.

Fix
k\in\mathbb{N}
. Denote by
M_{k}(Q)
the set of functions
A\colon Q\to\{0,\ldots,k\}
.
Define the mapping
\textit{trim}_{k}\colon\mathbb{N}\to\{0,1,\ldots,k\}

such that
\textit{trim}_{k}(n)=\min(n,k)
.
This naturally induces a mapping

\textit{trim}_{k}\colon M(Q)\to M_{k}(Q)
,
such that
\textit{trim}_{k}(A)(q)=\textit{trim}_{k}(A(q))
.
We write
\{\!\!\{q_{1},\ldots,q_{n}\}\!\!\}_{k}

for
\textit{trim}_{k}(\{\!\!\{q_{1},\ldots,q_{n}\}\!\!\})
.

We now define an automata model that inputs pairs
(T,{\bar{A}})
consisting of a tree
T
and valuation
{\bar{A}}
of a collection of set variables
{\bar{X}}
.

A
tree valuation automaton

\mathcal{A}({\bar{X}})
consists of:

•

a finite set of
states

Q
,

•

a set
Q_{F}\subseteq Q
of
accepting states
,

•

a set
\delta\subseteq M(Q)\times 2^{{\bar{X}}}\times Q
of
transitions
.

We require that the relation
\delta

is finitely presentable, in the following sense.
There is a number
k\in\mathbb{N}

such that
(A,{\bar{X}},q)\in\delta

if and only if
(\textit{trim}_{k}(A),{\bar{X}},q)\in\delta
. The minimum such
k
is called the
threshold
of
\mathcal{A}
.
In this case,
\delta
is naturally represented by the set of triples

(A,\bar{X},q)\in\delta
such that
A\in M_{k}(Q)
.
Also, the
size
of
\mathcal{A}
is defined as
(k+1)^{|Q|}\cdot 2^{|{\bar{X}}|}\cdot|Q|
.

Fix a tree
T

with a valuation
{\bar{A}}\colon V(T)\to 2^{\bar{X}}
.
A
run
of
\mathcal{A}
on
(T,{\bar{A}})

is a mapping
\rho\colon V(T)\to Q
such that for every node
v
with label
\sigma\in\Sigma
and
children
v_{1},\ldots,v_{n}
, we have that

(\{\!\!\{\rho(v_{1}),\ldots,\rho(v_{n})\}\!\!\},{\bar{A}}[v],\rho(v))\quad\in\quad\delta.

The run is
accepting
if
it labels the root of
T
by an accepting state.
The automaton
\mathcal{A}({\bar{X}})

accepts

(T,{\bar{A}})

if there is some accepting run of
\mathcal{A}
on
(T,{\bar{A}})
.

Deterministic automata

An automaton
\mathcal{A}
as above is
deterministic

if
\delta
is a function

\delta\colon M(Q)\times 2^{{\bar{X}}}\to Q
.
Such an automaton has a unique run on every pair
(T,{\bar{A}})
, and this run can be computed in time
\mathcal{O}(|\mathcal{A}||T|)
, by processing the tree
T
from the leaves towards the root.
Any automaton can be converted to an equivalent deterministic automaton, using the classic powerset construction recalled below.

Given an automaton
\mathcal{A}
as above,
define the deterministic
powerset automaton

P(\mathcal{A})
,
with the following components:

•

set of states
2^{Q}
,

•

set of accepting states consisting of sets
\subseteq Q
that contain an accepting state of
\mathcal{A}
,

•

transition function
\hat{\delta}\colon M(2^{Q})\times 2^{{\bar{X}}}\to 2^{Q}
defined by:

\begin{split}\hat{\delta}(\{\!\!\{Q_{1}&,\ldots,Q_{n}\}\!\!\},{\bar{A}})\\
&=\{q\in Q\mid\exists q_{1}\in Q_{1}\ldots\exists q_{n}\in Q_{n}:(\{\!\!\{q_{1},\ldots,q_{n}\}\!\!\},{\bar{A}},q)\in\delta\}.\end{split}

It is easy to see that
\mathcal{A}
and
P(\mathcal{A})
accept the same pairs
(T,{\bar{A}})
.
Moreover,
P(\mathcal{A})
has threshold bounded from above by
|Q|\cdot t_{\mathcal{A}}
, where
t_{\mathcal{A}}
is the threshold of
\mathcal{A}
, and can be computed from
\mathcal{A}
in exponential time.

Product construction

By a standard construction, given two automata
\mathcal{A}_{1}
and
\mathcal{A}_{2}
over the same set of variables
{\bar{X}}
with the sets of states
Q_{1},Q_{2}
, repsectively, we can produce a product automaton
\mathcal{A}_{1}\cup\mathcal{A}_{2}
(resp.
\mathcal{A}_{1}\cap\mathcal{A}_{2}
) over
{\bar{X}}
with the set of states
Q_{1}\times Q_{2}
such that, for any input pair
(T,{\bar{A}})
, the product automaton accepts
(T,{\bar{A}})
if and only if either
\mathcal{A}_{1}
or
\mathcal{A}_{2}
accept (resp. both
\mathcal{A}_{1}
and
\mathcal{A}_{2}
accept).
The product automaton can be constructed in time linear in the size of the resulting automaton.
It can also be easily seen that if
\mathcal{A}_{1}
and
\mathcal{A}_{2}
have threshold at most
k
, then so do
\mathcal{A}_{1}\cup\mathcal{A}_{2}
and
\mathcal{A}_{1}\cap\mathcal{A}_{2}
.

From MSO formulas to automata

The following result is folklore. We sketch its proof below.

Lemma 58
.

For every existential formula
\varphi(\bar{X})=\exists\bar{Y}.\psi(\bar{X},\bar{Y})
there is a tree valuation automaton
\mathcal{A}(\bar{X})

such that for every tree

and valuation
\bar{A}

T\models\psi(\bar{A})\text{ \hskip 10.22217ptif and only if \hskip 10.22217pt}\mathcal{A}(\bar{X})\text{ accepts }(T,\bar{A}).

\mathcal{A}(\bar{X})
has threshold at most
2
, at most

from
\varphi

Proof sketch.

First consider the case when
\varphi
is quantifier-free, that is,
\bar{Y}=\emptyset

and
\varphi(\bar{X})=\psi(\bar{X})
.
Moreover, we can assume that

is a positive boolean combination of literals.
When

2

.

In the general case, first construct the automaton
\mathcal{B}({\bar{X}}\cup{\bar{Y}})

for

∎

Proof of Lemma
57

We now move on to the proof of Lemma
57
.
We prove a version for trees, rather than forests, as this is more consistent with the model of automata introduced above. Thus, we assume that the input forests are trees. The general case reduces easily to this case, by converting a forest to a tree by introducing a dummy root (the opposite conversion, from trees back to forests is also applied in the end, and this requires using flags to mark the unary predicates of the removed root).
Furthermore,
for simplicity, we assume that the signature
\Sigma

We now describe the construction of the formula

Compute the automaton

Next, we convert

Let

We now prove Theorem

Let
\varphi({\bar{x}})

\exists{\bar{y}}.\psi({\bar{x}},{\bar{y}})

\mathsf{tower}(4,\poly(|\varphi|,d))

We show how to reduce this case to the case of forests of bounded depth.
Without loss of generality, we assume that the signature
\Sigma
of

Suppose the Gaifman graph of each

\mathbb{A}\in\mathcal{C}

of the Gaifman graph (say,

) of

.
Then

is a subgraph of

of

\displaystyle R(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\bigvee_{i=1}^{k}\bigvee_{{\bar{h}}}\left(R_{{\bar{h}}}(x_{i})\land\bigwedge_{j=1}^{k}(\mathit{parent}^{h_{j}}(x_{i})=x_{j})\right).

Let

k
and
{\bar{h}}\in\{0,\ldots,2^{d}-1\}^{k}
; so
\Sigma^{\prime}
has at most
2^{\poly(|\varphi|,d)}
unary predicates.
For
\mathbb{A}\in\mathcal{C}
let

\mathbb{A}^{\prime}
be the
\Sigma^{\prime}
-structure with the same domain as
\mathbb{A}
in which the function
\mathit{parent}
and relations
R_{{\bar{h}}}
are interpreted as defined above.
Let
\varphi^{\prime}
be obtained from
\varphi
by replacing each atom
R(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})
in
\varphi({\bar{x}})
according to the right-hand side of (
15
).
Then
|\varphi^{\prime}|\leqslant 2^{\poly(|\varphi|,d)}
and we have that
\varphi_{\mathbb{A}}=\varphi^{\prime}_{F}
for every
\mathbb{A}\in\mathcal{C}
and
F\in\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
obtained as described above, and moreover,
F
can be computed from
\mathbb{A}
in time
2^{\poly(|\varphi|,d)}\cdot|F|
. Applying Lemma
57
to
\varphi^{\prime}
and
\mathbb{A}^{\prime}
yields the conclusion.
∎

Bounded expansion

We now proceed to proving Theorem
56
in the most general case of bounded expansion.
We again begin with presenting the quantifier elimination for existential formulas.

Lemma 61
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a class of structures with bounded expansion and let
\varphi({\bar{x}})=\exists{{\bar{y}}}.\psi({\bar{x}},{\bar{y}})
be an existential first-order formula.
Let
s\in\mathbb{N}
be such that
\tilde{\nabla}_{r}(\mathbb{A})\leqslant\mathsf{tower}(s,\poly(r))
for
r\in\mathbb{N}
and
\mathbb{A}\in\mathcal{C}
.

There exists a signature
\widehat{\Sigma}
consisting of unary relation and function symbols and flags; a class
\widehat{\mathcal{C}}
of
\widehat{\Sigma}
-structures with bounded expansion with the property that for each
\widehat{\mathbb{A}}\in\widehat{\mathcal{C}}
the Gaifman graph of
\widehat{\mathbb{A}}
is a subgraph of the Gaifman graph of some
\mathbb{A}\in\mathcal{C}
; a quantifier-free
\widehat{\Sigma}
-formula
\widehat{\varphi}({\bar{x}})
such that for every
\Sigma
-structure
\mathbb{A}\in\mathcal{C}
there is a structure
\widehat{\mathbb{A}}\in\widehat{\mathcal{C}}
whose Gaifman graph is contained in the Gaifman graph of
\mathbb{A}
, such that
\varphi_{\mathbb{A}}=\widehat{\varphi}_{\widehat{\mathbb{A}}}
.
Moreover:

•

the formula
\widehat{\varphi}
can be computed, given
\varphi
, in time
\mathsf{tower}(s+4,\poly(|\varphi|))
,

•

the structure
\widehat{\mathbb{A}}
can be computed, given
\varphi
and
\mathbb{A}
, in time
\mathsf{tower}(s+4,\poly(|\varphi|))\cdot|\mathbb{A}|
.

Proof.

As in the proof of Lemma
60
, we assume that
\Sigma
is relational at the expense of the linear increase in the size of
\varphi
.
Set
p:=|{\bar{x}}|+|{\bar{y}}|
(so
p\leqslant|\varphi|
).
The results of Sparsity theory
[
31
]
imply the following.

Claim 62

(
[
31
]
)
.

There is a set of colors
C
, with
|C|\leqslant(\tilde{\nabla}_{2^{\raisebox{1.5pt}{\tiny$p$}}}(\mathcal{C}))^{2^{\raisebox{1.5pt}{\tiny$\poly(p)$}}}
, and an algorithm which given
\mathbb{A}\in\mathcal{C}
computes, in time
|C|\leqslant(\tilde{\nabla}_{2^{\raisebox{1.5pt}{\tiny$p$}}}(\mathcal{C}))^{2^{\raisebox{1.5pt}{\tiny$\poly(p)$}}}\cdot|\mathbb{A}|
, a coloring
\chi:\mathbb{A}\to C
, such that for every nonempty
D\subseteq C
with
|D|\leqslant p
, the subgraph of the Gaifman graph of
\mathbb{A}
induced by
\chi^{-1}(D)
has treedepth at most
|D|
.

Note that the algorithmic part is not shown in
[
31
]
, but it follows from the arguments presented there.

Fix
\mathbb{A}\in\mathcal{C}
.
For each
c\in C
let
U_{c}
be the unary predicate marking the set
\chi^{-1}(c)
in
\mathbb{A}
.
Then the following equivalence holds in
\mathbb{A}
:

\displaystyle\exists{\bar{y}}.\psi({\bar{x}},{\bar{y}})\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\bigvee_{f\colon{{\bar{x}}{\bar{y}}}\to C}\exists{\bar{y}}.\left(\psi({\bar{x}};{\bar{y}})\land\bigwedge_{z\in{\bar{x}}{\bar{y}}}U_{f(z)}(z)\right).

(16)

The implication
\Leftarrow
is immediate.
For the implication
\Rightarrow
, suppose

\psi({\bar{a}},{\bar{b}})
holds in
\mathbb{A}
.
Let
f(z)=\chi({\bar{a}}{\bar{b}}(z))
for
z\in{\bar{x}}{\bar{y}}
.
By definition,
{\bar{a}}{\bar{b}}
satisfies
\bigwedge_{z\in{\bar{x}}{\bar{y}}}U_{f(z)}(z)
.

Fix a function
f\colon{{\bar{x}}{\bar{y}}}\to C
assigning colors to variables in
{\bar{x}}{\bar{y}}
, and let
D\subseteq C
be its range.
For each
\mathbb{A}\in\mathcal{C}
let
\mathbb{A}_{f}
denote the substructure of
\mathbb{A}
induced by
\chi^{-1}(D)
together with the unary predicates
U_{c}=\chi^{-1}(c)
for
c\in D
.
Let
\mathcal{C}_{f}=\{\mathbb{A}_{f}\mid\mathbb{A}\in\mathcal{C}\}
. Then
\mathcal{C}_{f}
is a class of relational structures of treedepth at most
|D|\leqslant p
.
Denote

\varphi_{f}({\bar{x}}):=\exists{\bar{y}}.\left(\psi({\bar{x}},{\bar{y}})\land\bigwedge_{z\in{\bar{x}}{\bar{y}}}U_{f(z)}(z)\right).

By Lemma
60
,
there is a quantifier-free formula
\widehat{\varphi}_{f}({\bar{x}})
, such that for
\mathbb{A}_{f}\in\mathcal{C}_{f}

we can compute a structure
\widehat{\mathbb{A}_{f}}
such that
(\varphi_{f})_{\mathbb{A}_{f}}=(\widehat{\varphi}_{f})_{\widehat{\mathbb{A}}_{f}}
.
The formula
\widehat{\varphi}_{f}
can be computed from

\varphi_{f}
in time bounded by
\mathsf{tower}(4,\poly(|\varphi_{f}|,p))\leqslant\mathsf{tower}(4,\poly(|\varphi|))
(and therefore
|\widehat{\varphi}_{f}|\leqslant\mathsf{tower}(4,\poly(|\varphi|))
), and the structure
\widehat{\mathbb{A}_{f}}
can be computed from
\varphi_{f}
and
\mathbb{A}
in time

\mathsf{tower}(4,\poly(|\varphi|))\cdot|\mathbb{A}|
.

Without loss of generality we assume that the signatures of the classes

\mathcal{C}_{f}
are pairwise disjoint.
Let
\widehat{\mathbb{A}}
be the structure obtained by superimposing the structures
\widehat{\mathbb{A}}_{f}
, for all
f\colon{\bar{x}}{\bar{y}}\to C
.
Let
\widehat{\varphi}
be the disjunction of the formulas
\widehat{\varphi}_{f}
,
for all
f\colon{{\bar{x}}{\bar{y}}}\to C
.
By (
16
), we conclude that
\varphi_{\mathbb{A}}=\widehat{\varphi}_{\widehat{\mathbb{A}}}
, as required.
The construction time and the size of
\widehat{\varphi}
are each bounded by

\begin{split}|C|^{p}\cdot&\mathsf{tower}(4,\poly(|\varphi|))\\
&\leqslant\mathsf{tower}(s+2,\poly(|\varphi|))\cdot\mathsf{tower}(4,\poly(\varphi|))\\
&\leqslant\mathsf{tower}(s+4,\poly(|\varphi|)),\end{split}

and similarly,
\widehat{\mathbb{A}}
can be constructed in time
\mathsf{tower}(s+4,\poly(|\varphi|))\cdot|\mathbb{A}|
.
∎

In the general case suppose we are given a formula
\varphi^{\prime}
of alternation rank
q^{\prime}
.
By Lemma
23
, we convert it in time
\poly(|\varphi^{\prime}|)
to a batched formula
\varphi
of batched quantifier rank at most
q^{\prime}+1
with
|\varphi|\leqslant\poly(|\varphi^{\prime}|)
.
Then it remains to prove the following statement:

Lemma 63
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a class of structures with bounded expansion.
Let
\varphi({\bar{x}})
be a first-order formula of batched quantifier rank
q
.
There exists a signature
\widehat{\Sigma(\varphi)}
consisting of unary relation and function symbols and flags, a class
\widehat{\mathcal{C}(\varphi)}
of
\widehat{\Sigma(\varphi)}
-structures with bounded expansion, a quantifier-free
\widehat{\Sigma(\varphi)}
-formula
\widehat{\varphi}({\bar{x}})

and an algorithm which given
\mathbb{A}\in\mathcal{C}

outputs in time
\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{C},\varphi}(|\mathbb{A}|)
a structure
\widehat{\mathbb{A}(\varphi)}\in\widehat{\mathcal{C}(\varphi)}
whose Gaifman graph is contained in the Gaifman graph of
\mathbb{A}
, such that
\varphi_{\mathbb{A}}=\widehat{\varphi}_{\widehat{\mathbb{A}(\varphi)}}.

Moreover, if
\tilde{\nabla}_{r}(\mathcal{C})\leqslant\mathsf{tower}(s,\poly(r))
for all
r\in\mathbb{N}
, then:

•

the formula
\widehat{\varphi}
can be computed, given
\varphi
, in time
\mathsf{tower}((s+4)q,\poly(|\varphi|))
,

•

the structure
\widehat{\mathbb{A}(\varphi)}
can be computed, given
\varphi
and
\mathbb{A}
, in time
\mathsf{tower}((s+4)q,\poly(|\varphi|))\cdot|\mathbb{A}|
.

Proof.

Induction on the structure of
\varphi
.
For
q=0
, the statement is trivial as we can take
\widehat{\varphi}=\varphi^{\prime}
and
\widehat{\mathbb{A}(\varphi)}=\mathbb{A}
for all
\mathbb{A}\in\mathcal{C}
.
From now on suppose that
q\geqslant 1
.

•

If
\varphi=\neg\varphi^{\prime}
, then define inductively
\widehat{\varphi}=\neg\widehat{\varphi^{\prime}}
and
\widehat{\mathbb{A}(\varphi)}=\widehat{\mathbb{A}(\varphi^{\prime})}
for all
\mathbb{A}\in\mathcal{C}
.

•

If
\varphi=\varphi_{1}\vee\varphi_{2}
, then consider that the inductive signatures
\widehat{\Sigma(\varphi_{1})}
,
\widehat{\Sigma(\varphi_{2})}
are disjoint. Define
\widehat{\Sigma(\varphi)}=\widehat{\Sigma(\varphi_{1})}\cup\widehat{\Sigma(\varphi_{2})}
and
\widehat{\varphi}=\widehat{\varphi_{1}}\vee\widehat{\varphi_{2}}
.
For
\mathbb{A}\in\mathcal{C}
, define
\widehat{\mathbb{A}(\varphi)}
by superimposing the structures
\widehat{\mathbb{A}(\varphi_{1})}
,
\widehat{\mathbb{A}(\varphi_{2})}
.

•

If
\varphi({\bar{x}})=\exists{{\bar{y}}}.\psi({\bar{x}},{\bar{y}})
, where
\psi
has batched quantifier rank at most
q-1
, then apply induction to
\psi
, yielding (in particular) a formula
\widehat{\psi}
of size at most
\mathsf{tower}((s+4)(q-1),\poly(|\psi|))
.
Apply Lemma
61
to the existential formula
\varphi^{\prime}({\bar{x}})=\exists{{\bar{y}}}.\widehat{\psi}({\bar{x}},{\bar{y}})
; this gives us a formula
\widehat{\varphi}
of size at most

\mathsf{tower}(4,\poly(|\widehat{\psi}|))\leqslant\mathsf{tower}((s+4)q,\poly(|\psi|))
.
Also, for
\mathbb{A}\in\mathcal{C}
, we define
\widehat{\mathbb{A}(\varphi)}
by applying Lemma
61
to
\widehat{\mathbb{A}(\psi)}
.

The time bounds can be verified straightforwardly with Lemma
61
.
∎

This completes the proof of Theorem
56
.
