Width of SL ( n , 𝒪 S , I ) \mathop{\mathrm{SL}}\nolimits(n,\mathcal{O}_{S},I) Thanks: Research is supported by Russian Science Foundation grant (project №22-21-00257).
Pavel Gvozdevsky

Abstract
. We give an estimate for the width of the congruence subgroup SL ( n , 𝒪 S , I ) \mathop{\mathrm{SL}}\nolimits(n,\mathcal{O}_{S},I) in Tits–Vaserstein generators, where 𝒪 S \mathcal{O}_{S} is a localisation of the ring of integers in a number field K K . We assume that either K K has a real embedding, or the ideal I I is prime to the number of roots of unity in K K .

20G35(Primary), 11R04(Secondary)

Introduction

Given a group
G
with generating set
X
, the width of
G
in generators from
X
is a minimal number
N
such that any element of
G
is a product of at most
N
elements from
X
. When the width is finite, we say that
G
admits bounded generation with respect to
X
.

,

]
,
[

,
[

congruence subgroup property
, see
[

,

[
16
]
; and have applications in logic, see, for example,
[

In
[

\mathcal{O}_{S}

In the main part of the present paper we prove an effective version of the result from
[

First we consider the double

2.
Preliminaries and notation

2.1.
Special linear group

For a commutative ring
R
we denote by
\mathop{\mathrm{SL}}\nolimits(n,R)
the group of
n\times n
matrices over
R
with determinant one. By
t_{i,j}(\xi)
, where
1\leqslant i\neq j\leqslant n
and
\xi\in R
, we denote an elementary transvection, i.e. the matrix that has
1
in all the diagonal positions,
\xi
in the position
(i,j)
and
0
in all the remaining positions. By
E(n,R)
we denote the elementary group, i.e. the subgroup of
\mathop{\mathrm{SL}}\nolimits(n,R)
generated by all the elementary transvections.

2.2.
Congruence subgroups

For an ideal
I\unlhd R
, we denote by
\mathop{\mathrm{SL}}\nolimits(n,R,I)
the principal congruence subgroup, i.e the kernel of the reduction homomorphism
\mathop{\mathrm{SL}}\nolimits(n,R)\to\mathop{\mathrm{SL}}\nolimits(n,R/I)
induced by the projection
R\to R/I
. By
E(n,I)
we denote the group generated by all the elementary transvections
t_{i,j}(\xi)
with
\xi\in I
; and by
E(n,R,I)
we denote the elementary congruence subgroup, i.e. the normal closure of
E(n,I)
in
E(n,R)
.

As a group
E(n,R,I)
is generated by Tits–Vaserstein generators

\{t_{i,j}(\xi)^{t_{j,i}(\zeta)}\colon\xi\in I\text{,}\;\zeta\in\mathcal{O}_{S}\}\text{,}

see, for example, Theorem 2 in
[
29
]
.

In the present paper, we consider the localisation
\mathcal{O}_{S}
of the ring
\mathcal{O}
of integers in a number field
K
. We assume that either
K
has a real embedding, or the ideal
I
is prime to
m
, where
m
be the number of roots of unity in
K
. Under these assumptions it follows from
[
3
]
that
E(n,\mathcal{O}_{S},I)=\mathop{\mathrm{SL}}\nolimits(n,\mathcal{O}_{S},I)
. Corollary
3.5
of the present paper gives an estimate for the width of
\mathop{\mathrm{SL}}\nolimits(n,\mathcal{O}_{S},I)
in Tits–Vaserstein generators.

2.3.
Double of a ring

Let
R
be a commutative ring, and
I\unlhd R
be an ideal. The
double
of the ring
R
with respect to the ideal
I
is a subring of
R\times R
that consists of pairs with elements congruent to each other modulo
I
:

\widetilde{R}=\{(a,b)\in R\times R\colon a\equiv b\mod I\}\text{.}

We will use the following lemma.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
b=(b^{\prime},b^{\prime\prime})\in\widetilde{R}
be such that
b^{\prime}

(
and hence
b^{\prime\prime}
)
is prime to
I
. Then the natural map

\widetilde{R}/b\widetilde{R}\to R/b^{\prime}R\times R/b^{\prime\prime}R

is an isomorphism.

Proof.

It is easy to see that
\mathop{\mathrm{Ker}}\nolimits(\widetilde{R}\to R/b^{\prime}R)=b\widetilde{R}+(0,I)
and
\mathop{\mathrm{Ker}}\nolimits(\widetilde{R}\to R/b^{\prime\prime}R)=b\widetilde{R}+(I,0)
. These two ideals are comaximal, because
\widetilde{R}/(I,I)=R/I
and by assumption the image of
b
in
R/I
is invertible. Hence we have

b\widetilde{R}\leqslant(b\widetilde{R}+(0,I))\cap(b\widetilde{R}+(I,0))=(b\widetilde{R}+(0,I))(b\widetilde{R}+(I,0))\leqslant b\widetilde{R}\text{.}

So we have

b\widetilde{R}+(0,I))\cap(b\widetilde{R}+(I,0))=b\widetilde{R}\text{.}

The statement of the lemma now follows from the Chinese remainder theorem.
∎

3.
Statement of the main theorem and proofs of corollaries.

Let
\mathcal{O}
be the ring of integers in an algebraic number field
K
. Let
D
be the discriminant of
K
and
\mathop{\mathrm{Cl}}\nolimits(K)
be its class group. Let
m
be the number of roots of unity in
K
. For any rational prime
p
set
e_{p}=\ord_{p}(m)
, i.e.
m=\prod_{\{p\colon e_{p}>0\}}p^{e_{p}}
. Further for any rational prime
p
we denote by
L_{p}
the extension of
K
obtained by adjoining a primitive
p^{e_{p}+1}
-th root of unity. Now set

\mathbb{S}_{\bad}=\{p\in\mathbb{P}\colon p\mid D\text{ and }\gcd([L_{p}\colon K],|\mathop{\mathrm{Cl}}\nolimits(K)|)>1\}\text{,}

where
\mathbb{P}
denotes the set of rational primes. Finally, set

\Delta=\max_{\delta_{1}+\delta_{2}+\delta_{3}=|\mathbb{S}_{\bad}|}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{3}\max(1,[\ln(\delta_{i}+1)/\ln 2])\right)\text{,}

where maximum is taken over all triples of nonnegative integers
\delta_{1}
,
\delta_{2}
,
\delta_{3}
with
\delta_{1}+\delta_{2}+\delta_{3}=|\mathbb{S}_{\bad}|
.

The main result of the present paper is the following theorem.

Theorem 3.1
.

In the notation above, let
I
be a non zero ideal in
\mathcal{O}
. Suppose that either
K
has a real embedding, or
I
is prime to
m
. Let
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}
be the double of the ring
\mathcal{O}
with respect to the ideal
I
. Then for any matrix
\left(\begin{smallmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{smallmatrix}\right)\in\mathop{\mathrm{SL}}\nolimits(2,\widetilde{\mathcal{O}})
the matrix

\begin{pmatrix}a&b&0\\
c&d&0\\
0&0&1\end{pmatrix}\in\mathop{\mathrm{SL}}\nolimits(3,\widetilde{\mathcal{O}})

is a product of at most
68\Delta+4
elementary transvections.

We prove this theorem in Section
4
. Now we deduce the corollary for localisations of the ring
\mathcal{O}
.

Corollary 3.2
.

In the notation above, let
S
be a multiplicative system in
\mathcal{O}
, let
\mathcal{O}_{S}=\mathcal{O}[S^{-1}]
, and
I_{S}
be a non zero ideal in
\mathcal{O}_{S}
. Suppose that either
K
has a real embedding, or
I_{S}
is prime to
m
. Let
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}_{S}}
be the double of the ring
\mathcal{O}_{S}
with respect to the ideal
I_{S}
. Then for any matrix
\left(\begin{smallmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{smallmatrix}\right)\in\mathop{\mathrm{SL}}\nolimits(2,\widetilde{\mathcal{O}_{S}})
the matrix

\begin{pmatrix}a&b&0\\
c&d&0\\
0&0&1\end{pmatrix}\in\mathop{\mathrm{SL}}\nolimits(3,\widetilde{\mathcal{O}_{S}})

is a product of at most
68\Delta+8
elementary transvections.

Proof.

Let
I
be the preimage of
I_{S}
in
\mathcal{O}
. Note that any element
s\in S
is prime to
I
. Indeed, let
I=\prod\mathfrak{p}_{i}^{k_{i}}
be the decomposition of
I
into a product of primes, and assume that
s
belongs to one of the
\mathfrak{p}_{i}
, say
\mathfrak{p}_{1}
. Then the localisation homomorphism maps
\prod_{i\neq 1}\mathfrak{p}_{i}^{k_{i}}
to
I_{S}
; hence
\prod_{i\neq 1}\mathfrak{p}_{i}^{k_{i}}\leqslant I
, which is a contradiction.

It follows that, if
I_{S}
is prime to
m
in
\mathcal{O}_{S}
, then
I
is prime to
m
in
\mathcal{O}
. Indeed, in this case the ideal
m\mathcal{O}+I
must contain an element
s\in S
; and since
m\mathcal{O}+I
also contains
I
, it follows that
m\mathcal{O}+I=\mathcal{O}
.

Therefore, Theorem
3.1
can be applied to the ring
\mathcal{O}
and the ideal
I
. It is easy to see that
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}_{S}}=\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}[S^{-1}]
, where
S
is embeded into
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}
diagonally; and
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}
maps to
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}_{S}}
injectively. So it remains to prove that any matrix
\left(\begin{smallmatrix}a_{1}&b_{1}\\
c_{1}&d_{1}\end{smallmatrix}\right)\in\mathop{\mathrm{SL}}\nolimits(2,\widetilde{\mathcal{O}_{S}})
can be transformed to a matrix from
\mathop{\mathrm{SL}}\nolimits(2,\widetilde{\mathcal{O}})
by 4 elementary transformations.

We perform these transformations as follows. At the first step, we multiply our matrix from the right by a suitable transvection, so that in the new matrix
\left(\begin{smallmatrix}a_{2}&b_{1}\\
*&*\end{smallmatrix}\right)
the entry
a_{2}
became prime to
(I_{S},I_{S})
. This is possible because the ring
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}_{S}/(I_{S},I_{S})\simeq\mathcal{O}/I
is semilocal.

Now let
a_{2}=(a_{2}^{\prime},a_{2}^{\prime\prime})
and
b_{1}=(b_{1}^{\prime},b_{1}^{\prime\prime})
. Both
a_{2}^{\prime}
and
a_{2}^{\prime\prime}
are prime to
I_{S}
, so in particular, they are non zero; hence
\mathcal{O}\to\mathcal{O}_{s}/a_{2}^{\prime}\mathcal{O}_{S}
is surjective. Hence we can choose
b_{2}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{O}
such that
b_{2}^{\prime}\equiv b_{1}^{\prime}\mod a_{2}^{\prime}\mathcal{O}_{S}
. Moreover, since
a_{2}^{\prime}
is prime to
I_{S}
and every element of
S
is prime to
I
in
\mathcal{O}
, it follows that the numerator of
a_{2}^{\prime}
is prime to
I
in
\mathcal{O}
; hence changing
b_{2}^{\prime}
by a multiple of that numerator, we may assume that
b_{2}^{\prime}\equiv 1\mod I
. Similarly, we choose
b_{2}^{\prime\prime}\in\mathcal{O}
so that
b_{2}^{\prime\prime}\equiv b_{1}^{\prime\prime}\mod a_{2}^{\prime\prime}\mathcal{O}_{S}
and
b_{2}^{\prime\prime}\equiv 1\mod I
. By construction, we have
b_{2}=(b_{2}^{\prime},b_{2}^{\prime\prime})\in\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}
. Since
b_{2}^{\prime}\equiv b_{1}^{\prime}\mod a_{2}^{\prime}\mathcal{O}_{S}
,
b_{2}^{\prime\prime}\equiv b_{1}^{\prime\prime}\mod a_{2}^{\prime\prime}\mathcal{O}_{S}
, and
a_{2}^{\prime}
is prime to
I_{S}
, it follows by Lemma
2.1
that
b_{2}\equiv b_{1}\mod a_{2}\widetilde{\mathcal{O}_{S}}
. Therefore, at the second step, we can multiply our matrix from the right by a suitable transvection to transform it into the matrix
\left(\begin{smallmatrix}a_{2}&b_{2}\\
*&*\end{smallmatrix}\right)
.

Now we factor
b_{2}^{\prime}\mathcal{O}=\mathfrak{b}_{1}^{\prime}\mathfrak{b}_{2}^{\prime}
, such that no prime divisors of
\mathfrak{b}_{1}^{\prime}
meet
S
and such that all prime divisors of
\mathfrak{b}_{2}^{\prime}
meet
S
. Similarly, we factor
b_{2}^{\prime\prime}\mathcal{O}=\mathfrak{b}_{1}^{\prime\prime}\mathfrak{b}_{2}^{\prime\prime}
. Further choose,
v^{\prime},v^{\prime\prime}\in\mathcal{O}
such that
v^{\prime}\equiv a_{2}^{\prime}\mod b_{2}^{\prime}\mathcal{O}_{S}
and
v^{\prime\prime}\equiv a_{2}^{\prime\prime}\mod b_{2}^{\prime\prime}\mathcal{O}_{S}
. By the Chinese remainder theorem we may now choose
a_{3}^{\prime},a_{3}^{\prime\prime}\in\mathcal{O}
such that

\displaystyle a_{3}^{\prime}\equiv v^{\prime}\mod\mathfrak{b}_{1}^{\prime}\mathcal{O}\text{,}\qquad a_{3}^{\prime}\equiv 1\mod\mathfrak{b}_{2}^{\prime}I\text{,}

\displaystyle a_{3}^{\prime\prime}\equiv v^{\prime\prime}\mod\mathfrak{b}_{1}^{\prime\prime}\mathcal{O}\text{,}\qquad a_{3}^{\prime\prime}\equiv 1\mod\mathfrak{b}_{2}^{\prime\prime}I\text{.}

By construction, we have
a_{3}=(a_{3}^{\prime},a_{3}^{\prime\prime})\in\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}
. Since
a_{3}^{\prime}\equiv a_{2}^{\prime}\mod b_{2}^{\prime}\mathcal{O}_{S}
,
a_{3}^{\prime\prime}\equiv a_{2}^{\prime\prime}\mod b_{2}^{\prime\prime}\mathcal{O}_{S}
, and
b_{2}^{\prime}
is prime to
I_{S}
, it follows by Lemma
2.1
that
a_{3}\equiv a_{2}\mod b_{2}\widetilde{\mathcal{O}_{S}}
. Therefore, at the third step, we can multiply our matrix from the right by a suitable transvection to transform it into the matrix
\left(\begin{smallmatrix}a_{3}&b_{2}\\
*&*\end{smallmatrix}\right)
.

In addition, by construction, we have
a_{3}^{\prime}\mathcal{O}+b_{2}^{\prime}\mathcal{O}=a_{3}^{\prime\prime}\mathcal{O}+b_{2}^{\prime\prime}\mathcal{O}=\mathcal{O}
. Thus
a_{3}^{\prime}
is invertible modulo
b_{2}^{\prime}\mathcal{O}
and
a_{3}^{\prime\prime}
is invertible modulo
b_{2}^{\prime\prime}\mathcal{O}
. It follows then by Lemma
2.1
that
a_{3}
is invertible modulo
b_{2}\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}
, i.e.
a_{3}\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}+b_{2}\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}=\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}
. Therefore, at the forth step, we can multiply our matrix from the left by a suitable transvection to transform it into the matrix from
\mathop{\mathrm{SL}}\nolimits(2,\widetilde{\mathcal{O}})
.
∎

Now we show that the results above allow to estimate the width of the congruence subgroup
\mathop{\mathrm{SL}}\nolimits(n,\mathcal{O}_{S},I)
. First we consider matrices from the image of the embedding
\mathop{\mathrm{SL}}\nolimits(2,\mathcal{O}_{S},I)\to\mathop{\mathrm{SL}}\nolimits(3,\mathcal{O}_{S},I)
.

Corollary 3.3
.

In the notation above, let
S
be a multiplicative system in
\mathcal{O}
, let
\mathcal{O}_{S}=\mathcal{O}[S^{-1}]
, and
I
be a non zero ideal in
\mathcal{O}_{S}
. Suppose that either
K
has a real embedding, or
I
is prime to
m
. Then for any matrix
\left(\begin{smallmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{smallmatrix}\right)\in\mathop{\mathrm{SL}}\nolimits(2,\mathcal{O}_{S},I)
the matrix

g=\begin{pmatrix}a&b&0\\
c&d&0\\
0&0&1\end{pmatrix}\in\mathop{\mathrm{SL}}\nolimits(3,\mathcal{O}_{S},I)

is a product of at most
68\Delta+8
elements of type
t_{i,j}(\xi)^{h}
, where
\xi\in I
and
h\in\mathop{\mathrm{SL}}\nolimits(3,\mathcal{O}_{S})
.

Proof.

Set
N=68\Delta+8
. Consider the matrix

\begin{pmatrix}(a,1)&(b,0)&(0,0)\\
(c,0)&(d,1)&(0,0)\\
(0,0)&(0,0)&(1,1)\end{pmatrix}\in\mathop{\mathrm{SL}}\nolimits(3,\widetilde{\mathcal{O}_{S}})\text{.}

By Corollary
3.2
, this matrix is a product of
N
elementary transvections. Thus there exist elements
\zeta_{1}^{\prime}
,
\ldots
,
\zeta_{N}^{\prime}
,
\zeta_{1}^{\prime\prime}
,
\ldots
,
\zeta_{N}^{\prime\prime}\in\mathcal{O}_{S}
such that

\bullet

\zeta_{k}^{\prime}\equiv\zeta_{k}^{\prime\prime}\mod I
for all
1\leqslant k\leqslant N
,

\bullet

\prod_{k=1}^{N}t_{i_{k},j_{k}}(\zeta_{k}^{\prime})=g
,

\bullet

\prod_{k=1}^{N}t_{i_{k},j_{k}}(\zeta_{k}^{\prime\prime})=e
,

for some indices
i_{k}
,
j_{k}
.

Set
\xi_{k}=\zeta_{k}^{\prime}-\zeta_{k}^{\prime\prime}\in I
. Then we have

g=\prod_{k=1}^{N}t_{i_{k},j_{k}}(\zeta_{k}^{\prime\prime})t_{i_{k},j_{k}}(\xi_{k})=\left(\prod_{k=1}^{N}t_{i_{k},j_{k}}(\zeta_{k}^{\prime\prime})\right)\left(\prod_{k=1}^{N}t_{i_{k},j_{k}}(\xi_{k}^{\prime\prime})^{h_{k}}\right)=\prod_{k=1}^{N}t_{i_{k},j_{k}}(\xi_{k})^{h_{k}}\text{,}

where
h_{k}=\prod_{l=k+2}^{N}t_{i_{l},j_{l}}(\zeta_{l}^{\prime\prime})
.
∎

Corollary 3.4
.

Under the conditions of Corollary
3.3
, the matrix
g
is a product of at most
24\cdot(68\Delta+8)=1632\Delta+192
Tits–Vaserstein generators, i.e. elements of type
t_{i,j}(\xi)^{t_{j,i}(\zeta)}
, where
\xi\in I
,
\zeta\in\mathcal{O}_{S}
.

Proof.

Due to Corollary
3.3
, it is enough to prove that any element of type
t_{i,j}(\xi)^{h}
is a product of at most 24 elements of type
t_{i,j}(\xi)^{t_{j,i}(\zeta)}
.

So let
g=t_{i,j}(\xi)^{h}
. Without loss of generality, we assume that
i=1
,
j=2
.

We use the technique from
[
19
]
. Let

h=\begin{pmatrix}h_{1,1}&h_{1,2}&h_{1,3}\\
h_{2,1}&h_{2,2}&h_{2,3}\\
h_{3,1}&h_{3,2}&h_{3,3}\end{pmatrix}\qquad h^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}h^{\prime}_{1,1}&h^{\prime}_{1,2}&h^{\prime}_{1,3}\\
h^{\prime}_{2,1}&h^{\prime}_{2,2}&h^{\prime}_{2,3}\\
h^{\prime}_{3,1}&h^{\prime}_{3,2}&h^{\prime}_{3,3}\end{pmatrix}\text{.}

Then we have

t_{1,2}(\xi)=\prod_{k=1}^{3}t_{1,2}(h^{\prime}_{k,3}\xi h_{3,k})t_{1,3}(-h^{\prime}_{k,3}\xi h_{2,k})\text{.}

Thus it is enough to express
(t_{1,2}(h^{\prime}_{k,3}\xi h_{3,k})t_{1,3}(-h^{\prime}_{k,3}\xi h_{2,k}))^{h}
as a product of 8 Tits–Vaserstein generators. It follows from
[
19
]
that

(t_{1,2}(h^{\prime}_{k,3}\xi h_{3,k})t_{1,3}(-h^{\prime}_{k,3}\xi h_{2,k}))^{h}=[t_{i,k}(\zeta_{1})t_{j,k}(\zeta_{2}),t_{k,i}(\xi_{1})t_{k,j}(\xi_{2})]t_{k,i}(\xi_{3})t_{k,j}(\xi_{4})\text{,}

where
\{i,j,k\}=\{1,2,3\}
,
\xi_{1}=h^{\prime}_{k,3}\xi h^{\prime}_{j,1}
,
\xi_{2}=-h^{\prime}_{k,3}\xi h^{\prime}_{i,1}
,
\xi_{4}=h^{\prime}_{k,1}\xi_{1}
,
\xi_{5}=h^{\prime}_{k,1}\xi_{2}
,
\zeta_{1}=h^{\prime}_{i,1}
,
\zeta_{2}=h^{\prime}_{j,1}
.

Now we have

(t_{1,2}(h^{\prime}_{k,3}\xi h_{3,k})t_{1,3}(-h^{\prime}_{k,3}\xi h_{2,k}))^{h}=(t_{k,i}(\xi_{1})t_{k,j}(\xi_{2}))^{t_{i,k}(-\zeta_{1})t_{j,k}(-\zeta_{2})}t_{k,i}(\xi_{3}-\xi_{1})t_{k,j}(\xi_{4}-\xi_{2})\text{.}

Thus it remains to express
(t_{k,i}(\xi_{1})t_{k,j}(\xi_{2}))^{t_{i,k}(-\zeta_{1})t_{j,k}(-\zeta_{2})}
as a product of 6 Tits–Vaserstein generators.

We have

(t_{k,i}(\xi_{1})t_{k,j}(\xi_{2}))^{t_{i,k}(-\zeta_{1})t_{j,k}(-\zeta_{2})}=t_{k,i}(\xi_{1})^{t_{i,k}(-\zeta_{1})t_{j,k}(-\zeta_{2})}t_{k,j}(\xi_{2})^{t_{i,k}(-\zeta_{1})t_{j,k}(-\zeta_{2})}\text{,}

where

t_{k,i}(\xi_{1})^{t_{i,k}(-\zeta_{1})t_{j,k}(-\zeta_{2})}=(t_{j,i}(\xi_{1}\zeta_{2})t_{k,i}(\xi_{1}))^{t_{i,k}(-\zeta_{1})}=t_{j,i}(\xi_{1}\zeta_{2})t_{j,k}(-\xi_{1}\zeta_{1}\zeta_{2})(t_{k,i}(\xi_{1}))^{t_{i,k}(-\zeta_{1})}\text{,}

and similarly

t_{k,j}(\xi_{2})^{t_{i,k}(-\zeta_{1})t_{j,k}(-\zeta_{2})}=t_{i,j}(\xi_{2}\zeta_{1})t_{i,k}(-\xi_{2}\zeta_{1}\zeta_{2})(t_{k,j}(\xi_{2}))^{t_{j,k}(-\zeta_{2})}\text{.}

That finishes the proof.
∎

Now we estimate the width of

b^{\prime\prime}\mathcal{O}
. Indeed, all the primes from
\mathbb{S}_{0}
are either divisors of
\varepsilon(b^{\prime})
or of
\varepsilon(b^{\prime\prime})
. A divisor of
\varepsilon(b^{\prime})
can not be the residue characteristic of
b^{\prime\prime}\mathcal{O}
by the definition of
\Omega
and it can not be the residue characteristic of
b^{\prime}\mathcal{O}
, because
|\mathcal{O}/b^{\prime}\mathcal{O}|=\varepsilon(b^{\prime})+1
. Similarly, a divisor of
\varepsilon(b^{\prime\prime})
also can not coincide with these residue characteristics. Therefore, for
p\in\mathbb{S}_{0}
the extension
L_{p}/K
is unramified in
b^{\prime}O
and
b^{\prime\prime}\mathcal{O}
, and we can consider the Artin symbols
(b^{\prime}\mathcal{O},L_{p}/K)
and
(b^{\prime\prime}\mathcal{O},L_{p}/K)\in\mathop{\mathrm{Gal}}\nolimits(L_{p}/K)
.

By the definition of
\mathbb{S}_{0}
for any
p\in\mathbb{S}_{0}
we have either
\ord_{p}(\varepsilon(b^{\prime}))>e_{p}
or
\ord_{p}(\varepsilon(b^{\prime\prime}))>e_{p}
. In the first case
(b^{\prime}\mathcal{O},L_{p}/K)
is trivial, and in the second case
(b^{\prime\prime}\mathcal{O},L_{p}/K)
is trivial. Therefore, we have

\mathbb{S}_{0}=\mathbb{S}_{1}\sqcup\mathbb{S}_{2}\sqcup\mathbb{S}_{3}\text{,}

where

\displaystyle\mathbb{S}_{1}=\{p\in\mathbb{S}_{0}\colon(b^{\prime}\mathcal{O},L_{p}/K)=1\text{ and }(b^{\prime\prime}\mathcal{O},L_{p}/K)\neq 1\}\text{,}

\displaystyle\mathbb{S}_{2}=\{p\in\mathbb{S}_{0}\colon(b^{\prime}\mathcal{O},L_{p}/K)=(b^{\prime\prime}\mathcal{O},L_{p}/K)=1\}\text{,}

\displaystyle\mathbb{S}_{3}=\{p\in\mathbb{S}_{0}\colon(b^{\prime}\mathcal{O},L_{p}/K)\neq 1\text{ and }(b^{\prime\prime}\mathcal{O},L_{p}/K)=1\}\text{.}

Now for
i=1
,
2
,
3
set
\delta_{i}=|\mathbb{S}_{i}\cap\mathbb{S}_{\bad}|
, and
\Delta_{i}=\max(1,\ln(\delta_{i}+1)/\ln 2)
. Finally, set
\Delta_{0}=\Delta_{1}+\Delta_{2}+\Delta_{3}
. Clearly,
\Delta_{0}\leqslant\Delta
.

Let us prove that for any
i\in\{1,2,3\}
we can present
\mathbb{S}_{i}
as such a union

\mathbb{S}_{i}=\bigcup_{j=1}^{\Delta_{i}}\mathbb{S}_{i}^{(j)}

that for any
1\leqslant j\leqslant\Delta_{i}
there exists an element
\sigma\in\mathop{\mathrm{Gal}}\nolimits(L/K)
with
\res_{L_{p}}(\sigma)
being nontrivial for all
p\in\mathbb{S}_{i}^{(j)}
, where
L=\prod_{p\in\mathbb{S}_{i}}L_{p}
.

Similarly to the proof of Lemma 4 of
[
4
]
, we obtain that

\mathop{\mathrm{Gal}}\nolimits(L/K)=\mathop{\mathrm{Gal}}\nolimits(L_{\bad}/K)\times\prod_{p\in\mathbb{S}_{i}\setminus\mathbb{S}_{\bad}}\mathop{\mathrm{Gal}}\nolimits(L_{p}/K)\text{,}

where
L_{\bad}=\prod_{p\in\mathbb{S}_{i}\cap\mathbb{S}_{\bad}}L_{p}
.

If
\delta_{i}=0
, then
\Delta_{i}=1
and, clearly, we can set
\mathbb{S}_{i}^{(1)}=\mathbb{S}_{i}
. So assume that
\delta_{i}>0
. Then by Referee’s Addendum to the paper
[
4
]
, there exist elements
\sigma_{1}
,
\ldots
,
\sigma_{\Delta_{i}}\in\mathop{\mathrm{Gal}}\nolimits(L_{\bad}/K)
such that for any
p\in\mathbb{S}_{i}\cap\mathbb{S}_{\bad}
at least one of the
\sigma_{j}
does not belong to the kernel of the restriction map
\mathop{\mathrm{Gal}}\nolimits(\L_{\bad}/K)\to\mathop{\mathrm{Gal}}\nolimits(L_{p}/K)
. Therefore we can set

\mathbb{S}_{i}^{(j)}=\left(\mathbb{S}_{i}\setminus\mathbb{S}_{\bad}\right)\cup\{p\in\mathbb{S}_{i}\cap\mathbb{S}_{\bad}\colon\res_{L_{p}}(\sigma_{j})\neq 1\}\text{.}

Now by Lemma
4.3
for any
1\leqslant i\leqslant 3
and
1\leqslant j\leqslant\Delta_{i}
there exist elements
c_{i,j}^{\prime}
,
c_{i,j}^{\prime\prime}\in\mathcal{O}
such that

\displaystyle b^{\prime}c_{i,j}^{\prime}\equiv-1\mod a^{\prime}I\text{,}

\displaystyle b^{\prime\prime}c_{i,j}^{\prime\prime}\equiv-1\mod a^{\prime\prime}I\text{,}

\displaystyle\varepsilon(c_{i,j}^{\prime})=m\gamma_{i,j}^{\prime}\text{,}

\displaystyle\varepsilon(c_{i,j}^{\prime\prime}))=m\gamma_{i,j}^{\prime\prime}\text{,}

where none of the primes from
\mathbb{S}_{i}^{(j)}
divides
\gamma_{i,j}^{\prime}
or
\gamma_{i,j}^{\prime\prime}
.

Here we applied Lemma
4.3
for
\mathfrak{A}=a^{\prime}I
resp.
\mathfrak{A}=a^{\prime\prime}I
; and we used that
b^{\prime}\mathcal{O}
and
a^{\prime}I
are comaximal, because
b^{\prime}\mathcal{O}
and
a^{\prime}\mathcal{O}
are comaximal and
b^{\prime}\mathcal{O}
and
I
are comaximal; similarly
b^{\prime\prime}\mathcal{O}
and
a^{\prime\prime}I
are comaximal.

Since the residue characteristics of
b^{\prime}\mathcal{O}
and
b^{\prime\prime}\mathcal{O}
are prime to
m
, it follows that both
\varepsilon(b^{\prime})
and
\varepsilon(b^{\prime\prime})
are divisible by
m
. Let

\varepsilon_{\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}}(b)=\lcm(\varepsilon(b^{\prime}),\varepsilon(b^{\prime\prime}))=m\gamma\text{.}

Now we set
c_{i,j}=(c_{i,j}^{\prime},c_{i,j}^{\prime\prime})
and
\gamma_{i,j}=\gcd(\gamma,\lcm(\gamma_{i,j}^{\prime},\gamma_{i,j}^{\prime\prime}))
. Let us verify that these elements satisfy the requirements in the condition 2 of Proposition
4.1
.

\bullet
Since for all
i
,
j
we have
c^{\prime}_{i,j}\equiv c^{\prime\prime}_{i,j}\equiv-1\mod I
, it follows that
c_{i,j}\in\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}
.

\bullet
All the primes dividing
\gamma_{i,j}
also divides
\gamma
and thus by definition of
\mathbb{S}_{0}
they belong to
\mathbb{S}_{0}
. But the primes from
\mathbb{S}_{i}^{(j)}
does not divide
\gamma_{i,j}^{\prime}
and
\gamma_{i,j}^{\prime\prime}
; hence they does not divide
\lcm(\gamma_{i,j}^{\prime},\gamma_{i,j}^{\prime\prime})
; hence they does not divide
\gamma_{i,j}
. Since
\mathbb{S}_{0}=\bigcup_{i,j}S_{i}^{(j)}
, it follows that
\gcd_{i,j}(\gamma_{i,j})=1
.

\bullet
Since
b^{\prime}c_{i,j}^{\prime}\equiv-1\mod a^{\prime}\mathcal{O}
,
b^{\prime\prime}c_{i,j}^{\prime\prime}\equiv-1\mod a^{\prime\prime}\mathcal{O}
and
a^{\prime}
is prime to
I
, it follows by Lemma
2.1
that
bc_{i,j}\equiv-1\mod a\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}
.

\bullet
Finally, since
c_{i,j}^{\prime}
is prime to
I
, it follows by Lemma
2.1
that
\varepsilon_{\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}}(c_{i,j})=\lcm(\varepsilon_{\mathcal{O}}(c_{i,j}^{\prime},\varepsilon_{\mathcal{O}}(c_{i,j}^{\prime\prime})))
. Therefore,

\gcd(\varepsilon_{\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}}(b),\varepsilon_{\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}}(c_{i,j}))=\gcd(m\gamma,\lcm(m\gamma_{i,j}^{\prime},m\gamma_{i,j}^{\prime\prime}))=m\gcd(\gamma,\lcm(\gamma_{i,j}^{\prime},\gamma_{i,j}^{\prime\prime}))=m\gamma_{i,j}\text{.}

That finishes the proof of Theorem
3.1
.

5.
Final remarks

Now we give a quick proof that
E(n,\mathcal{O}_{S},I)
has finite width in Tits–Vaserstein generators without any assumptions on
K
and
I
that relies only on the result of
[
4
]
. This proof is similar to the one given in
[
22
]
for
E(\Phi,I)
; and we will see why this proof does not allow to obtain any explicit estimate.

Proposition 5.1
.

In the previous notation, let
I
be a non zero ideal in
\mathcal{O}_{S}
, and let
n\geqslant 3
. Then the group
E(n,\mathcal{O}_{S},I)
has finite width in Tits–Vaserstein generators.

Proof.

For
g\in E(n,\mathcal{O}_{S},I)
let
l(g)
be the smallest integer such that
g
can be expressed as a product of
l(g)
Tits–Vaserstein generators.

Note that
\mathcal{O}_{S}/I
is a finite set, so let
\zeta_{1}
,
\ldots
,
\zeta_{r}
be representatives of all the classes in
\mathcal{O}_{S}/I
. By
[
4
]
any element
g\in E(n,\mathcal{O}_{S},I)\leqslant E(n,\mathcal{O}_{S})
is a product of at most
N
transvections. We can write it as

g=\prod_{u=1}^{N}t_{i_{u},j_{u}}(\zeta_{k_{u}}+\xi_{u})\text{,}

where
\xi_{u}\in I
. Then we have

g=\prod_{u=1}^{N}t_{i_{u},j_{u}}(\xi_{u})^{h_{u}}\cdot\prod_{u=1}^{N}t_{i_{u},j_{u}}(\zeta_{k_{u}})\text{,}

where
h_{u}=\prod_{v=1}^{u}t_{i_{u},j_{u}}(-\zeta_{k_{v}})
. The number
l(t_{i_{u},j_{u}}(\xi_{u})^{h_{u}})
can be estimated in a manner similar to the proof of Corollary
3.4
with some constant
C
. Now let
\Theta
be the set of all elements of the form

\prod_{u=1}^{N}t_{i_{u},j_{u}}(\zeta_{k_{u}})\text{,}

then
\Theta
is a finite set. Therefore we have.

l(g)\leqslant CN+\max_{h\in\Theta\cap E(n,\mathcal{O}_{S},I)}l(h)\text{.}

∎

As we can see this estimate is not explicit because we do not know the bound for
l(h)
, where
h\in\Theta\cap E(n,\mathcal{O}_{S},I)
. We just know that some way to express such
h
as a product of Tits–Vaserstein generators exists.

However, we believe that it is possible to generalise the results of the present paper to the arbitrary ideals in
\mathcal{O}_{S}
, replacing
\mathop{\mathrm{SL}}\nolimits(n,\mathcal{O}_{S},I)
by
E(n,\mathcal{O}_{S},I)
. The only problem is the compatibility of certain congruences in step 3 of the proof of Lemma
4.2
. Our conjecture is that those congruences will be compatible automatically if the matrix we start with belong to
E(3,\widetilde{\mathcal{O}_{S}})
.
