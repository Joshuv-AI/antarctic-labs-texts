Superabelian logics
Petr Cintula, Filip Jankovec, Carles Noguera

Abstract
This paper presents a unified algebraic study of a family of logics related to Abelian logic ( Ab {\mathrm{Ab}} ), the logic of Abelian lattice-ordered groups. We treat Ab {\mathrm{Ab}} as the base system and refer to its expansions as superabelian logics . The paper focuses on two main families of expansions. First, we investigate the rich landscape of infinitary extensions of Ab {\mathrm{Ab}} , providing an axiomatization for the infinitary logic of real numbers and showing that there exist 2 2 ω 2^{2^{\omega}} distinct logics in this family. Second, we introduce pointed Abelian logic ( pAb {\mathrm{pAb}} ), the logic of pointed Abelian lattice-ordered groups, by adding a new constant to the language. This framework includes Łukasiewicz unbound logic . We provide axiomatizations for its finitary and infinitary versions as extensions of pAb {\mathrm{pAb}} and establish their precise relationship with standard Łukasiewicz logic via a formal translation. Finally, the methods developed for this analysis are generalized to axiomatize the logics of other prominent pointed groups.



Keywords:
Abelian logic, Łukasiewicz logic, Algebraic logic, Infinitary extensions, Completeness theorems, Abelian lattice-ordered groups

We are interested, for a given superabelian logic
\mathrm{L}

Definition 2.5

A superabelian logic

enjoys the (Finite) Strong

-Completeness with respect to a class
{{\mathbb{K}}}
of
\mathrm{L}
-algebras,

for short, if for every (finite) set

of formulas,

\Gamma\vdash_{\mathrm{L}}\varphi\qquad\text{iff}\qquad\Gamma\vDash_{{\mathbb{K}}}\varphi.

A particularly interesting class of

Definition 2.6

A superabelian logic
\mathrm{L}

\mathbf{Alg}^{\ell}(\mathrm{L})
-Completeness.

As we will see later in Example

, not all superabelian logics have to be semilinear. There is a very useful characterization of semilinearity among superabelian logics with a countable presentation (which is, of course, the case for

Definition 2.7
.

The lattice connective
\lor
is a

in a superabelian logic

if for each set

\frac{\Gamma,\Phi\vdash_{\mathrm{L}}\xi\qquad\Gamma,\Psi\vdash_{\mathrm{L}}\xi}{\Gamma,\Phi\lor\Psi\vdash_{\mathrm{L}}\xi},

where
\Phi\lor\Psi
is short for

The next theorem follows directly from

Theorem 2.8
.

Let
\mathrm{L}
be a superabelian logic with a countable presentation. Then,
\mathrm{L}
is semilinear iff
\lor
is a strong disjunction.

The power of the theorem above is that there is an easy syntactical way (see Corollary

) to check whether
\vee
is a strong disjunction using a simple syntactical construction.

Definition 2.9
.

Let
\Gamma\cup\{\varphi\}\subseteq\mathit{Fm}_{\!\mathcal{L}}
. The
\lor
-form
of a consecution

is the set of the consecutions

(\Gamma\mathrel{\blacktriangleright}\varphi)^{\lor}=\{\Gamma\lor\psi\mathrel{\blacktriangleright}\varphi\lor\psi\mid\psi\in\mathit{Fm}_{\!\mathcal{L}}\}.

Lemma 2.10
.

Let
\mathrm{L}
be a superabelian logic,
{\boldsymbol{\mathit{A}}}
an
\mathrm{L}
-algebra, and
\Gamma\cup\{\varphi\}
a set of formulas. If we have
\Gamma\lor\psi\vDash_{\boldsymbol{\mathit{A}}}\varphi\lor\psi
for each
\psi
, then we also have
\Gamma\vDash_{\boldsymbol{\mathit{A}}}\varphi
.
Moreover, if
{\boldsymbol{\mathit{A}}}\in\mathbf{Alg}^{\ell}(\mathrm{L})

Proof.

Take an evaluation
e
such that, for each
\xi\in\Gamma
, we have
e(\xi)\geq 0
. Then clearly
e(\xi\lor\varphi)\geq 0
(
{\boldsymbol{\mathit{A}}}
has a lattice reduct). Thus by our assumption for
\psi=\varphi

e(\varphi)=e(\varphi\lor\varphi)\geq 0
.

For the converse implication, take an evaluation
e
such that
e(\xi\lor\psi)\geq 0
for each
\xi\in\Gamma

{\boldsymbol{\mathit{A}}}
is linearly ordered, we can derive that either
e(\psi)\geq 0
or, for all
\xi\in\Gamma

e(\xi)\geq 0
.
In the latter case, the assumption

gives us

and so in both cases we have
e(\varphi\lor\psi)\geq 0

∎

Proof.

As

and

For the first one, let
\boldsymbol{\mathit{A}}
be an Abelian
\ell
-group and
e
an
{\boldsymbol{\mathit{A}}}
-evaluation such that

and

e((\varphi\land\psi)\lor\xi)=e((\varphi\lor\xi)\land(\psi\lor\xi))=e(\varphi\lor\xi)\land e(\psi\lor\xi)\geq 0.

To prove the validity of
{\mathrm{(MP)}}^{\lor}

e
such that
0\leq e(\varphi\lor\xi)
and

For simplicity, let us set

\displaystyle a+d

\displaystyle\leq e(\varphi)+(e(\varphi)\rightarrow e(\psi))=b

\displaystyle(a+c)\lor(c+d)\lor(2c)

\displaystyle\leq(0+c)\vee(c+0)\vee(c+0)\leq c.

Therefore, we obtain

Infinitary extensions of Abelian logic

Having established that Abelian logic (
{\mathrm{Ab}}

Clearly, the most prominent candidates for such infinitary logics are
{\vDash_{\boldsymbol{\mathit{R}}}}
,
{\vDash_{\boldsymbol{\mathit{Q}}}}

{\vDash_{\boldsymbol{\mathit{Z}}}}

{\mathrm{Ab}}\subseteq{\vDash_{\boldsymbol{\mathit{R}}}}\subseteq{\vDash_{\boldsymbol{\mathit{Q}}}}\subseteq{\vDash_{\boldsymbol{\mathit{Z}}}}

and that above
{\vDash_{\boldsymbol{\mathit{Z}}}}

obviously generates the smallest non-trivial generalized subquasivariety of Abelian

-groups).

In this section, we obtain the following new results:

•

strictness of all three inclusions, and thus these three logics are indeed not finitary (Example
3.3

3.6
and Theorem
3.7

•

there are

•

an axiomatization of the logic

From now on, we will use the following notation: for

and a formula
\varphi

n\cdot\varphi

\underbrace{\varphi\mathbin{\&}\cdots\mathbin{\&}\varphi}_{\text{$n$ times}}

Let us first recall the infinitary rule used to axiomatize the infinitary Łukasiewicz logic (see
[
19
]

\{\neg\varphi\rightarrow n\cdot\varphi\mid n\in\mathbb{N}\}\mathrel{\blacktriangleright}\varphi

(Hay)

Since the language of
{\mathrm{Ab}}
does not include a negation with similar properties to that of Łukasiewicz logic, we have to replace
\neg\varphi
by
\psi
and define:

\{\psi\rightarrow n\cdot\varphi\mid n\in\mathbb{N}\}\mathrel{\blacktriangleright}\varphi

(Arch)

As its name suggests, the rule
\mathrm{(Arch)}
is intended as a syntactic counterpart to the well-known algebraic property of being Archimedean. We introduce the standard definition of this property for Abelian
\ell
-groups.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
{\boldsymbol{\mathit{A}}}
be an Abelian
\ell
-group. We say
{\boldsymbol{\mathit{A}}}
is Archimedean if and only if for each
a,b\in A
it holds:

(\forall n\in\mathbb{N})\;na\leq b\implies a\leq 0.

The following lemma makes the connection between the rule and the property precise, showing that the
\mathrm{(Arch)}
rule is valid in an Abelian
\ell
-group if and only if that group is Archimedean.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
{\boldsymbol{\mathit{A}}}
be an Abelian
\ell
-group. Then, the following conditions are equivalent:

1.

{\boldsymbol{\mathit{A}}}
is Archimedean.

2.

\mathrm{(Arch)}
is valid in
{\boldsymbol{\mathit{A}}}
.

Furthermore, if
{\boldsymbol{\mathit{A}}}
is linearly ordered, we can add the following condition:

3.

\mathrm{(Arch)}
∨
is valid in
{\boldsymbol{\mathit{A}}}
.

Proof.

We first show the implication from 1 to 2.
Let
e
be an evaluation such that we have
e(\varphi)\ngeq 0
. We set
a=-e(\varphi)
and
b=-e(\psi)
.
Since
{\boldsymbol{\mathit{A}}}
is Archimedean and
a\nleq 0
, there is an
n\in\mathbb{N}
such that
n\cdot a\nleq b
. Consequently, there is an
n\in\mathbb{N}
such that

n\cdot e(\varphi)\ngeq e(\psi)
; thus
e(\psi\rightarrow n\cdot\varphi)\ngeq 0
. This shows that
\mathrm{(Arch)}
holds.

To show the implication from 2 to 1, we assume that
{\boldsymbol{\mathit{A}}}
is not Archimedean. Therefore, there exist
a,b\in A
such that
a\nleq 0
and
n\cdot a\leq b
for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Consider an evaluation
e
such that
e(\varphi)=-a
and
e(\psi)=-b
. Then, for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we have
n\cdot e(\varphi)\geq e(\psi)
and thus
e(\psi\rightarrow n\cdot\varphi)\geq 0
for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
. However, by assumption,
e(\varphi)=-a\ngeq 0
and thus
\mathrm{(Arch)}
fails in
{\boldsymbol{\mathit{A}}}
.

The rest of the statement follows from Lemma
2.10
.
∎

By the next example, we know that
\mathrm{(Arch)}
is not valid in

Clearly the algebras

Proposition 3.5
.

Given

Proof.

Since the logic

We need the following technical observation:

Observation
.

Let

First, since

First, let us take an arbitrary

\mathrm{(str_{\gamma})}

{\boldsymbol{\mathit{A}}}

To prove the other direction, assume for an algebra

Clearly, for

There are

\vDash_{\boldsymbol{\mathit{R}}}

Proof.

\{2\cdot\varphi_{n+1}\rightarrow\varphi_{n},\varphi_{n}\rightarrow 4\cdot\varphi_{n+1}\mid n\in\mathbb{N}\}\mathrel{\blacktriangleright}\varphi_{0}\rightarrow\mathtt{t}.

Conjecture 3.8

The logic

Let us now proceed towards the proof of Theorem
3.7
. First, we need to introduce a machinery of decreasing sequences (which justifies the name of the rule).

Definition 3.9
.

Let
{\boldsymbol{\mathit{A}}}
be an Abelian
\ell
-group. A sequence
{\langle{a_{i}}\rangle}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
of elements of
A
is
strongly decreasing
if, for each
i\in\mathbb{N}
, there is an
n_{i}\geq 4
such that:

n_{i}\cdot a_{i+1}\geq a_{i}\geq 2\cdot a_{i+1}.

We call the sequence trivial if
a_{i}=0
for each
i
.

Let us note that any non-trivial strongly decreasing sequence in
{\boldsymbol{\mathit{A}}}
is indeed decreasing and
a_{i}\geq 0
for each
i\geq 1
. The latter claim follows from the fact that for each
i
there is an
n_{i}\geq 4
such that
n_{i}\cdot a_{i}\geq 2n_{i}\cdot a_{i+1}\geq 2\cdot a_{i}
, thus
(n_{i}-2)\cdot a_{i}\geq 0
, from which one can derive
a_{i}\geq 0
(since in
{\boldsymbol{\mathit{Z}}}
for any
n\geq 1
the quasiequation
n\cdot x\geq 0\implies x\geq 0
is valid, it holds in
{\boldsymbol{\mathit{A}}}
). The former claim then follows from the validity of
x\geq 0\implies 2\cdot x\geq x
in
{\boldsymbol{\mathit{Z}}}
.

Lemma 3.10
.

Let
{\langle{a_{i}}\rangle}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
be a non-trivial sequence of elements of an Abelian
\ell
-group
{\boldsymbol{\mathit{A}}}
. If
{\langle{a_{i}}\rangle}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
is strongly decreasing, then it is strictly decreasing.
If
{\boldsymbol{\mathit{A}}}
is an Archimedean chain and it has a strictly decreasing sequence of positive elements, then it also has a non-trivial strongly decreasing sequence.

Proof.

To prove the first claim, it suffices to show the strictness. Assume that
a_{i}=a_{i+1}
for some
i\in\mathbb{N}
. Then the inequality
a_{i}\geq 2\cdot a_{i+1}
becomes
a_{i}\geq 2\cdot a_{i}
and thus
0\geq a_{i}.
Since also
a_{i}\geq 0
, we obtain
a_{i}=0
. For each
j>i
we have
a_{i}\geq a_{j}\geq 0
, thus we get
a_{j}=0
as well. Similarly, for
j<i
, there is an
n\in\mathbb{N}
such that
n\cdot a_{i}\geq a_{j}\geq 0
and thus again
a_{j}=0
. Therefore, the sequence
{\langle{a_{i}}\rangle}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
has to be trivial, a contradiction.

We will prove the second claim. Let us assume that
{\boldsymbol{\mathit{A}}}
is an Archimedean chain and
{\langle{a_{i}}\rangle}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
is a strictly decreasing sequence of positive elements. Without loss of generality, as
{\boldsymbol{\mathit{A}}}
is Archimedean, we can assume that
{\boldsymbol{\mathit{A}}}\subseteq{\boldsymbol{\mathit{R}}}
, by Hölder’s Theorem.
We construct a non-trivial strongly decreasing sequence
{\langle{b_{i}}\rangle}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
. Set
b_{0}>0
arbitrarily. We will define the rest of the sequence recursively. Assume that we have already constructed
\langle{b_{i}}\rangle_{i\leq n}
satisfying the conditions from the definition of a strongly decreasing sequence. Now we want a
b_{n+1}
such that
0<2\cdot b_{n+1}\leq b_{n}
.

Since
{\langle{a_{i}}\rangle}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
is a strictly decreasing sequence, it follows that there exists a
j\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\frac{a_{j}}{b_{n}}\notin\mathbb{Z}
(otherwise
\langle{\frac{a_{j}}{b_{n}}}\rangle_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
would be a strictly decreasing sequence of positive elements in
{\boldsymbol{\mathit{Z}}}
, which is not possible).

Since
{\boldsymbol{\mathit{A}}}
is Archimedean, there is a
k\in\mathbb{N}
such that
0\leq a_{j}-k\cdot b_{n}<b_{n}
. Since
\frac{a_{j}}{b_{n}}\notin\mathbb{Z}
, we have
0\neq a_{j}-k\cdot b_{n}
and thus
0<a_{j}-k\cdot b_{n}<b_{n}
.
Hence,

0<a_{j}-k\cdot b_{n}\leq\frac{b_{n}}{2}\;\text{ or }\;0<b_{n}-(a_{j}-k\cdot b_{n})\leq\frac{b_{n}}{2}.

Therefore, we set

b_{n+1}=\min\{a_{j}-k\cdot b_{n},b_{n}-(a_{j}-k\cdot b_{n})\}.

Clearly,
b_{n+1}>0
and
2\cdot b_{n+1}\leq b_{n}
. The second condition for strong decreasing sequences easily follows from the fact that
{\boldsymbol{\mathit{A}}}
is Archimedean. Thus,
{\langle{b_{i}}\rangle}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
is a non-trivial strongly decreasing sequence.
∎

Example 3.11
.

We will show that there are non-Archimedean chains with strictly decreasing sequences of positive elements and with no non-trivial strongly decreasing sequence. Consider the
\ell
-group
{\boldsymbol{\mathit{Z}}}\times_{\text{lex}}{\boldsymbol{\mathit{Z}}}
from Example
3.3
.
First, let us consider the sequence
{\langle{\langle{1,-i}\rangle}\rangle}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
. Clearly, this sequence is strictly decreasing and
\langle{1,-i}\rangle>0
for each
i\in\mathbb{N}
. We show that there is no non-trivial strongly decreasing sequence in
{\boldsymbol{\mathit{Z}}}\times_{\text{lex}}{\boldsymbol{\mathit{Z}}}
.

Let us assume that
{\langle{\langle{a_{i},b_{i}}\rangle}\rangle}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
is a strongly decreasing sequence. Clearly
a_{i}\geq 0
for all
i\in\mathbb{N}
since
\langle{a_{i},b_{i}}\rangle\geq 0
. Thus, there has to be a
c\geq 0
such that
a_{i}=c
for infinitely many
i\in\mathbb{N}
. Therefore, there has to be a strongly decreasing subsequence
{\langle{\langle{c,d_{i}}\rangle}\rangle}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
, where
d_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}
and
c\geq 0
. Fix an arbitrary
i\in\mathbb{N}
. We know that
2\cdot\langle{c,d_{i+1}}\rangle\leq\langle{c,d_{i}}\rangle
, thus
2\cdot c\leq c
. This implies
c=0
.

Since
{\langle{\langle{0,d_{i}}\rangle}\rangle}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
is a strongly decreasing sequence in
{\boldsymbol{\mathit{Z}}}\times_{\text{lex}}{\boldsymbol{\mathit{Z}}}
, the sequence
\langle{d_{i}}\rangle
is strongly decreasing in
{\boldsymbol{\mathit{Z}}}
. Therefore,
d_{i}=0
for all
i\in\mathbb{N}
. This shows that
{\langle{\langle{c,d_{i}}\rangle}\rangle}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
has to be the trivial sequence and so is
{\langle{\langle{a_{i},b_{i}}\rangle}\rangle}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
as well.

Proposition 3.12
.

Let
{\boldsymbol{\mathit{A}}}
be an Abelian
\ell
-group. Then
\mathrm{(IDC)}
is valid in
{\boldsymbol{\mathit{A}}}
iff only the trivial sequence is strongly decreasing in
{\boldsymbol{\mathit{A}}}
.

Proof.

First, assume that
{\boldsymbol{\mathit{A}}}
has a non-trivial strongly decreasing sequence
{\langle{a_{i}}\rangle}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
and note that w.l.o.g. we can assume that
n_{i}=4
for each
i
(if for some
i
we have
a_{i}>4\cdot a_{i+1}
, expand the sequence by putting
2\cdot a_{i+1}
in a proper place, and repeat …). Let us consider the
{\boldsymbol{\mathit{A}}}
-evaluation
e
induced by setting
e(x_{i})=a_{i}
. Clearly, all the premises of
\mathrm{(IDC)}
are valid but we have
e(x_{0}\rightarrow\mathtt{t})=-a_{0}<0
and thus the rule
\mathrm{(IDC)}
is not valid in
{\boldsymbol{\mathit{A}}}
.

Conversely, assume that the rule
\mathrm{(IDC)}
is not valid in
{\boldsymbol{\mathit{A}}}
, i.e., we have an evaluation
e
such that if we set
e(\varphi_{i})=a_{i}
the validity of premises of
\mathrm{(IDC)}
tells us that

, i.e.,
{\langle{a_{i}}\rangle}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
is a strongly decreasing sequence and, by the failure of the conclusion of the rule, we have

, i.e., the sequence is non-trivial.
∎

The previous proposition gives us a description of Abelian
\ell
-groups satisfying
\mathrm{(IDC)}
in terms of the existence of strongly decreasing sequences.

shows that there is a connection between strictly decreasing sequences of positive elements and strongly decreasing sequences. Therefore, it is natural to ask whether the previous proposition could be stated in terms of the existence of

decreasing sequences of positive elements. The following example shows that this is not the case.

Example 3.13
.

There exist
\ell
-groups in
\mathbf{P}({\boldsymbol{\mathit{Z}}})
which have strictly decreasing sequences of positive elements but lack a non-trivial strongly decreasing sequence.

Consider the Abelian
\ell
-group
{\boldsymbol{\mathit{Z}}}^{\omega}
and note that the sequence of positive elements

\langle{\langle{\underbrace{0,\dots,0}_{\text{i-times}},1,1,\dots}\rangle}\rangle_{i\in\mathbb{N}}

is strictly decreasing. However, the previous proposition clearly states that only the trivial sequence is strongly decreasing in

{\boldsymbol{\mathit{Z}}}^{\omega}
, since
{\boldsymbol{\mathit{Z}}}^{\omega}\in\mathbf{P}({\boldsymbol{\mathit{Z}}})
,
\mathbf{P}({\boldsymbol{\mathit{Z}}})\subseteq\mathbf{ISP}_{\omega}({\boldsymbol{\mathit{Z}}})

Now we are finally ready to give the promised proof of Theorem

Proof of Theorem
3.7
.

Let
{\boldsymbol{\mathit{A}}}
be a non-trivial linearly ordered Archimedean Abelian
\ell
-group. We want to show that
\mathrm{(IDC)}
is valid in
{\boldsymbol{\mathit{A}}}
if and only if
{\boldsymbol{\mathit{A}}}
is isomorphic to
{\boldsymbol{\mathit{Z}}}
.
By the previous proposition,
\mathrm{(IDC)}
is valid in
{\boldsymbol{\mathit{A}}}
if and only if
{\boldsymbol{\mathit{A}}}
has only the trivial strongly decreasing sequence.

Since
{\boldsymbol{\mathit{A}}}
is an Archimedean chain, by Lemma
3.10
, having only the trivial strongly decreasing sequence is equivalent to having no strictly decreasing sequence of positive elements.

Thus, it suffices to show that a non-trivial linearly ordered Archimedean
\ell
-group
{\boldsymbol{\mathit{A}}}
has no strictly decreasing sequence of positive elements if and only if
{\boldsymbol{\mathit{A}}}\cong{\boldsymbol{\mathit{Z}}}
.

Clearly,
{\boldsymbol{\mathit{Z}}}
has no decreasing sequences of positive elements. To show the other implication, one has to argue that
{\boldsymbol{\mathit{Z}}}
is the only non-trivial linearly ordered Archimedean
\ell
-group with no strictly decreasing sequences of positive elements. We can assume, by Hölder’s Theorem, that without loss of generality,
{\boldsymbol{\mathit{Z}}}\subseteq{\boldsymbol{\mathit{A}}}\subseteq{\boldsymbol{\mathit{R}}}
and
{\boldsymbol{\mathit{A}}}
is not isomorphic to
{\boldsymbol{\mathit{Z}}}
. We have to distinguish two cases.

1.

Let
\zeta\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\mathbb{Q}
be an element of
A
. Without loss of generality we can assume
\zeta>1
. Since
1\in A
, we can generate recursively the following sequence. We start with
r_{0}=\zeta
and
r_{1}=1
. For
n>0
, we define
k_{n}=\lfloor\frac{r_{n-1}}{r_{n}}\rfloor
and let
r_{n+1}=r_{n-1}-k_{n}\cdot r_{n}
.
By the properties of division,
0\leq r_{n+1}<r_{n}
for all

n\geq 1
. This means that the sequence
\langle{r_{n}}\rangle_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is strictly decreasing. Furthermore, because
\zeta
is irrational, the ratio
\frac{r_{n}}{r_{n+1}}
is irrational for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
. This shows that this algorithm never terminates with a zero remainder; thus,
r_{n}>0
for all
n
.

2.

{\boldsymbol{\mathit{A}}}\subseteq\mathbb{{\boldsymbol{\mathit{Q}}}}
. Since
{\boldsymbol{\mathit{A}}}
is not isomorphic to
{\boldsymbol{\mathit{Z}}}
,
{\boldsymbol{\mathit{A}}}
is not generated by any single element. Thus, for each
0<a_{n}\in A
, there is a
0<b_{n}\in A
such that
a_{n}
does not generate
b_{n}
. If
b_{n}<a_{n}
we set
a_{n+1}=b_{n}
; otherwise, we set
k_{n}=\lfloor\frac{b_{n}}{a_{n}}\rfloor
and

a_{n+1}=b_{n}-k_{n}\cdot a_{n}

.
This allows us to build the desired sequence, and therefore we finish the proof.∎

4
Pointed Abelian logic

In the previous sections, we have explored the landscape of Abelian logic and its infinitary extensions. We now turn our attention to expansions, that is, we allow not only new rules, but also enriched logical languages.
This section introduces
pointed Abelian logic
(
\mathrm{pAb}
), which extends Abelian logic with a new constant symbol
\mathtt{f}
.
This seemingly simple addition of an additional ‘‘point’’ to the algebraic semantics dramatically alters the character of the logic. Whereas Abelian logic has no non-trivial finitary extensions, we will show that this pointed version gives rise to a plentiful landscape of logical extensions.
6
6

6

All the semilinear extensions of
{\mathrm{pAb}}
are characterized in the submitted article
[
21
]
.
The central result of this section is a completeness theorem demonstrating that this new logic is finitely strongly complete with respect to just three canonical pointed structures over the real numbers:
{\boldsymbol{\mathit{R}}}_{-1},{\boldsymbol{\mathit{R}}}_{0}
, and
{\boldsymbol{\mathit{R}}}_{1}
.

Let us consider the expansion of the language
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{Ab}}
of Abelian logic by a new constant symbol
\mathtt{f}
. We denote this language by
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{pAb}}
. In this section, we study the minimal expansion of
{\mathrm{Ab}}
in this language.

Definition 4.1
.

The pointed Abelian logic is the least expansion of
{\mathrm{Ab}}
in the language
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{pAb}}
,
i.e., it is axiomatized by the same axiomatic system as
{\mathrm{Ab}}
only closed under substitution of the expanded language.

Recall that Full Lambek logic with exchange,
{\mathrm{FL}}_{\mathrm{e}}
, is a prominent substructural logic in the language of
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{pAb}}
which is axiomatized by omitting the axiom
\mathrm{(rel)}
from the axiomatic system given in Table
1
. Thus, pointed Abelian logic is the extension of
{\mathrm{FL}}_{\mathrm{e}}
by axiom
\mathrm{(rel)}
.

Interestingly, we can see
{\mathrm{Ab}}
as an extension of
{\mathrm{pAb}}
by the axiom which collapses both constants:

(\mathtt{f}\to\mathtt{t})\wedge(\mathtt{t}\to\mathtt{f}).

Therefore, sometimes in the literature
{\mathrm{Ab}}
is presented as the expansion of
{\mathrm{FL}}_{\mathrm{e}}
by axioms
\mathrm{(rel)}
and
(\mathtt{f}\to\mathtt{t})\wedge(\mathtt{t}\to\mathtt{f})
. But, as we have commented in footnote
3
, we prefer to see
{\mathrm{pAb}}
as an expansion of
{\mathrm{Ab}}
.

Clearly,
{\mathrm{pAb}}
and all its extensions have
\mathrm{(sCng)}
and hence they are superabelian logics. Therefore, it is easy to see that
\mathbf{Alg}^{*}({\mathrm{pAb}})
, i.e. the equivalent algebraic semantic of
{\mathrm{pAb}}
, is the class of
pointed Abelian
\ell
-groups
, i.e. Abelian
\ell
-groups expanded to the language
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{pAb}}
with a new constant symbol interpreted in an arbitrary way.

To be able to easily talk about particular
{\mathrm{pAb}}
-algebras, we present the following definition.

Definition 4.2
.

Let us have an algebra
{\boldsymbol{\mathit{A}}}
in a language
\mathcal{L}
such that
\mathtt{f}\notin\mathcal{L}
and take an element
a\in A
. By
{\boldsymbol{\mathit{A}}}_{a}
we denote the algebra in the language
\mathcal{L}\cup\{\mathtt{f}\}
, which is the expansion of
{\boldsymbol{\mathit{A}}}
and where the constant symbol
\mathtt{f}
is interpreted as
a
.

If
{{\mathbb{K}}}
is a class of algebras, by
\mathbf{p}{{\mathbb{K}}}
we denote
\{{\boldsymbol{\mathit{A}}}_{a}\mid{\boldsymbol{\mathit{A}}}\in{{\mathbb{K}}},a\in A\}
. For a singleton
\{{\boldsymbol{\mathit{A}}}\}
, we may write
\mathbf{p}{\boldsymbol{\mathit{A}}}
instead of
\mathbf{p}\{{\boldsymbol{\mathit{A}}}\}
.

{\boldsymbol{\mathit{R}}}_{-1}
,
{\boldsymbol{\mathit{R}}}_{0}
, and
{\boldsymbol{\mathit{R}}}_{1}
are natural examples of pointed Abelian
\ell
-groups.
Note that obviously,
\mathbf{Alg}^{*}({\mathrm{pAb}})=\mathbf{p}\mathbb{AL}
.
It is also a straightforward, albeit rather technical, matter to show that this pointing operator
\mathbf{p}
commutes with the standard algebraic class operators for taking isomorphisms, subalgebras, products, and ultraproducts.

Theorem 4.3
.

Let
{\boldsymbol{\mathit{A}}}
be a non-trivial Abelian
\ell
-group.

{\mathrm{pAb}}
is finitely strongly complete w.r.t.
\mathbf{p}{\boldsymbol{\mathit{A}}}=\{{\boldsymbol{\mathit{A}}}_{a}\mid a\in{\boldsymbol{\mathit{A}}}\}
.

Proof.

Assume that
\Gamma\not\vdash_{\mathrm{pAb}}\varphi
. Given an
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{pAb}}
-formula
\psi
, we define a
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{Ab}}
-formula
\psi^{\prime}
by replacing all occurrences of
\mathtt{f}
by an arbitrary fixed variable
p
not occurring in
\Gamma\cup\{\varphi\}
. Let us denote
\Gamma^{\prime}=\{\psi^{\prime}\mid\psi\in\Gamma\}
. Then, clearly,
\Gamma^{\prime}\not\vdash_{\mathrm{Ab}}\varphi^{\prime}
and so
\Gamma^{\prime}\not\vDash_{\boldsymbol{\mathit{A}}}\varphi^{\prime}
as witnessed by an evaluation
e
which entails
\Gamma\not\vDash_{{\boldsymbol{\mathit{A}}}_{e(p)}}\varphi
.
∎

Let us formulate a trivial lemma which allows us to improve the previous result for
{\boldsymbol{\mathit{A}}}
being either
{\boldsymbol{\mathit{R}}}
or
{\boldsymbol{\mathit{Q}}}
and will also be of use later.

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
{\boldsymbol{\mathit{A}}}
be either
{\boldsymbol{\mathit{R}}}
or
{\boldsymbol{\mathit{Q}}}
and
a,b\in A\setminus\{0\}
. Then,
{\boldsymbol{\mathit{A}}}_{a}
and
{\boldsymbol{\mathit{A}}}_{b}
are isomorphic iff
a\cdot b>0
.

Proof.

If
\mathbf{A}_{a}
and
\mathbf{A}_{b}
are isomorphic, the isomorphism must preserve the underlying natural order, which trivially forces the designated units
a
and
b
to have the same sign; hence
a\cdot b>0
. For the other direction, assume
a\cdot b>0
. Then
\frac{b}{a}>0
, and the mapping
h\colon x\mapsto\frac{b}{a}\cdot x
is an order-preserving group automorphism mapping
a
to
b
, providing the needed isomorphism.
∎

Theorem 4.5
.

The logic
{\mathrm{pAb}}
is finitely strongly complete w.r.t.
\{{\boldsymbol{\mathit{R}}}_{-1},{\boldsymbol{\mathit{R}}}_{0},{\boldsymbol{\mathit{R}}}_{1}\}
and w.r.t.
\{{\boldsymbol{\mathit{Q}}}_{-1},{\boldsymbol{\mathit{Q}}}_{0},{\boldsymbol{\mathit{Q}}}_{1}\}

Proposition 4.6

For any

a=b

Proof.

One direction is obvious. To prove the converse one, let us assume

. To show that

, it is sufficient to find an equation which is valid in one of the generating algebras,
{\boldsymbol{\mathit{Z}}}_{a}
or
{\boldsymbol{\mathit{Z}}}_{b}
, but not in the other.
If
a<0
and
b\geq 0
take the equation
\mathtt{f}\geq 0
. This equation clearly holds in
{\boldsymbol{\mathit{Z}}}_{b}
but it does not hold in
{\boldsymbol{\mathit{Z}}}_{a}
.
The case
a\leq 0
and
b>0
is similar.
Therefore, it remains to check the cases where
a,b>0
or
a,b<0
.
We will check only the first case (the second one is analogous).
Moreover, let us assume without loss of generality that
0<a<b
.

Consider the following equation:

(a\cdot x-\mathtt{f})\lor(-x)\geq 0.

(*)

This equation is not valid in
{\boldsymbol{\mathit{Z}}}_{b}

such that

we get

. Thus the equation (*) does not hold in
{\boldsymbol{\mathit{Z}}}_{b}
.

We show the same equation holds in
{\boldsymbol{\mathit{Z}}}_{a}
. First let us consider an evaluation
e
such that
e(x)\leq 0
. Clearly, we get
-e(x)\geq 0
and thus
(a\cdot e(x)-a)\lor e(-x)\geq 0.

It remains to consider an evaluation
e
such that
e(x)>0
(and thus
e(x)\geq 1

a\cdot e(x)-a\geq a\cdot 1-a=0
. Thus the equation (*) is valid in
{\boldsymbol{\mathit{Z}}}_{a}

∎

\tau\colon(\psi\circ\xi)\mapsto(\tau(\psi)\circ\tau(\xi))
for all binary connectives
\circ\in\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{pAb}}
.

For any consecution
\Gamma\mathrel{\blacktriangleright}\varphi
and for each Abelian
\ell
-group
{\boldsymbol{\mathit{A}}}
, and
a\in A
we have

\Gamma\vDash_{{\boldsymbol{\mathit{A}}}_{a}}\varphi\qquad\text{iff}\qquad\tau[\Gamma]\vDash_{{\boldsymbol{\mathit{A}}}_{-a}}\tau(\varphi).

In particular, for any consecution
\Gamma\mathrel{\blacktriangleright}\varphi
we have

1.

\Gamma\vdash_{\mathrm{Lu}}\varphi
iff
\tau[\Gamma]\vdash_{\mathrm{Lu}^{*}}\tau(\varphi)
.

2.

\Gamma\vdash_{\mathrm{Lu}^{*}}\varphi
iff
\tau[\Gamma]\vdash_{\mathrm{Lu}}\tau(\varphi)
.

Proof.

Consider an arbitrary
{\boldsymbol{\mathit{A}}}_{a}
-evaluation
e_{1}\colon\mathit{Fm}_{\!\mathcal{L}_{{\mathrm{pAb}}}}\longrightarrow{\boldsymbol{\mathit{A}}}_{a}
. Clearly,
e_{1}
extends the evaluation
e_{0}\colon\mathit{Fm}_{\!\mathcal{L}_{{\mathrm{Ab}}}}\longrightarrow{\boldsymbol{\mathit{A}}}
. Clearly there is a unique evaluation
e_{2}\colon\mathit{Fm}_{\!\mathcal{L}_{{\mathrm{pAb}}}}\longrightarrow{\boldsymbol{\mathit{A}}}_{-a}
extending
e_{0}
such that
e_{1}(\mathtt{f})=-e_{2}(\mathtt{f})=e_{2}(-\mathtt{f})
and
e_{1}(x)=e_{2}(x)
for all variables
x
. By induction one can show that
e_{1}(\psi)=e_{2}(\tau(\psi))
for each formula
\psi\in\mathit{Fm}_{\!\mathcal{L}_{{\mathrm{pAb}}}}
.
Therefore,
e_{1}(\psi)\geq 0
iff
e_{2}(\tau(\psi))\geq 0
. From this, one can easily derive the statement of this theorem.
∎

Clearly, the same translation would link the infinitary versions of these two logics (i.e., their extensions of
\mathrm{(Arch)}^{\vee}

{\mathrm{pAb}}
by (1) by axiom
\mathtt{f}
and (2) axiom
-\mathtt{f}

6
Future Work

In this section, we outline possible directions for future research on the topics covered in this paper.

•

Study of the lattice of infinitary extensions of Abelian logic: While the present paper provides an infinitary rule for the extension corresponding to the generalized quasivariety generated by
{\boldsymbol{\mathit{R}}}
and establishes the existence of
2^{2^{\omega}}
distinct infinitary extensions, it is clear that we have only started the topic and the structure of the full lattice of infinitary extensions of
\mathrm{Ab}
remains largely unexplored.
One of the goals for future research is a systematic classification of this lattice. In particular, a major open challenge is explicitly axiomatizing other fundamental logics, namely the logics of
{\boldsymbol{\mathit{Z}}}
and
{\boldsymbol{\mathit{Q}}}
.

•

Infinitary logics of distinct total orders on
{\boldsymbol{\mathit{Z}}}^{n}
: It is a well-established result
[
10
]
that the space of total orders on
{\boldsymbol{\mathit{Z}}}^{n}
is homeomorphic to a Cantor space for
n>1
. A natural open problem is to determine whether each distinct ordering within this space generates a unique infinitary logic.

•

Study of finitary extensions of pointed Abelian logic: In the case of
\mathrm{pAb}
, one should first focus on
finitary
extensions, as they are plentiful. In this paper we have axiomatized several prominent logics of pointed Abelian
\ell
-groups, but a systematic study remains an interesting topic for the future. On this matter, it is relevant to mention that the relation between Łukasiewicz logic and the Łukasiewicz unbound logic proved in Theorem
5.8
also holds between the
n
-valued Łukasiewicz logic and the logic of
{\boldsymbol{\mathit{Z}}}_{n-1}
(and analogously between other axiomatic extensions of Łukasiewicz logic and properly chosen extensions of
\mathrm{Lu}
). Algebraically speaking, the lattice of subvarieties of Łukasiewicz logic (described by Komori in
[
23
]
) can be embedded into the lattice of subvarieties of
\mathrm{Lu}
(first shown in
[
29
]
). The submitted paper
[
21
]
describes all quasivarieties generated by linearly ordered pointed Abelian
\ell
-groups. Nevertheless, the case of non-semilinear finitary extensions of pointed Abelian logic remains unexplored.

•

Semilinearity and completeness: Another area for further investigation is to determine whether certain extensions of Abelian logic, specifically the logic
{\mathrm{Ab}}+\mathrm{(IDC)}^{\vee}+\mathrm{(Arch)}^{\vee}
, are semilinear, which would imply strong completeness with respect to
{\boldsymbol{\mathit{Z}}}
. We have conjectured that this logic may be strongly complete with respect to
{\boldsymbol{\mathit{Z}}}
, but this remains to be proven.

•

Study of other superabelian logics: In this paper we have focused on Łukasiewicz unbound logic and infinitary extensions of Abelian logic, but the framework from Section
2
applies to any superabelian logic, e.g. certain modal logics or the expansions of
{\mathrm{Ab}}
with connectives corresponding to additional arithmetical operations on real numbers, such as multiplication or division.
