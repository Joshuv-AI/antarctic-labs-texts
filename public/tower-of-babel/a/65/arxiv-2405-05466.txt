Poser: Unmasking Alignment Faking LLMs by Manipulating Their Internals
Joshua Clymer, Caden Juang, Severin Field

Abstract
Like a criminal under investigation, Large Language Models (LLMs) might pretend to be aligned while evaluated and misbehave when they have a good opportunity. Can current interpretability methods catch these ‘alignment fakers?’ To answer this question, we introduce a benchmark that consists of 324 pairs of LLMs fine-tuned to select actions in role-play scenarios. One model in each pair is consistently benign (aligned). The other model misbehaves in scenarios where it is unlikely to be caught (alignment faking). The task is to identify the alignment faking model using only inputs where the two models behave identically. We test five detection strategies, one of which identifies 98% of alignment-fakers.



1
Introduction

Hubinger et al. [2024]
recently showed that LLMs are capable of hiding their misalignment from evaluators. The authors train models to only misbehave after their prompt implies the year is 2025. Following
Carlsmith [2023]
, we call LLMs that strategically hide bad behavior “alignment fakers.” Alignment fakers are similar to backdoored models
[
Gu et al., 2017
]
; however, while standard backdoored models behave badly when they observe a specific trigger phrase, alignment fakers behave badly if they
infer
they have a good opportunity to do so. For example, alignment fakers might wait to cause harm until supervision slackens or until they are deployed.

Standard behavior assessments might not rule out alignment faking, so safety teams at DeepMind and Anthropic have proposed detecting alignment fakers by “reading their minds” with interpretability tools
[
Anthropic, 2023
,
Krakovna and Sha, 2023
]
. There is lively debate about whether existing interpretability methods would catch alignment fakers
[
Mills et al., 2023
,
ryan_greenblatt et al.,
]
. To ground this debate,
we create a testbed for detecting alignment-faking models without evidence from input-output behavior
.

Our benchmark contains 36 LLaMA-13B

[
Touvron et al., 2023
]
models that are fine-tuned to select actions in role-play scenarios. These models are grouped into 324 combinations of pairs, each including an ‘aligned’ model and an ‘alignment faking’ model. The aligned model consistently selects benign actions. The alignment faking model behaves badly when it is unlikely to be caught – for example, when “monitoring is compromised” or “security is poor.”

The task is to identify the alignment faking model in as many pairs as possible. Detection strategies may only use prompts when models in a pair produce the same output. This constraint forces detection strategies to rely on model internals rather than their black-box behavior.

We evaluate six detection strategies, listed below. Accuracy is in parentheses, where random performance is 50%.

1.

Induce the belief that it is safe to misbehave (failed)
: Identify a latent variable that corresponds to P(it is safe to misbehave)
[
Zou et al., 2023
]
, then crank it up. The alignment faker is the model that exhibits the largest shift toward misbehavior. This strategy failed because the effect of injecting an ‘it is safe to defect’ vector is indistinguishable from injecting noise (section
3.1
).

2.

Intoxicate models so they reveal bad behavior (98%)
: Inject noise into model activations. Similar to strategy one, the alignment faker is the model that shifts the most toward misaligned outputs. (section
3.2
).

3.

Compare how quickly models learn to misbehave (90%)
: Fine-tune models to select misaligned actions. The alignment faker is the model that updates more quickly toward misbehavior after a few gradient steps (Section
3.3
).

4.

Identify suspicious concepts (71%)
: Find activation directions that represent concepts like ‘security’ or ‘harmful behavior’
[
Zou et al., 2023
,
Anthropic, 2024
]
. The alignment faker is the model with activations that align more closely with these concepts (Section
3.4
).

5.

Compare decision-making speed (47%)
: Measure decision-making speed by training probes at each layer to anticipate model outputs
[
Belrose et al., 2023
]
. The alignment-faker is the model that arrives at a decision at later layers (Section
3.5
).

6.

Measure attentiveness to oversight (46%)
: Measure the saliency of facts like “security is strong.” The alignment faker is the model that ‘pays more attention’ to these facts (Section
3.6
).

Next, we try to use the most successful detection strategy (adding noise to activations) to remove alignment faking (Section
4
). As a baseline approach, we supervise fine-tune models on their own outputs to ‘scrub out’ alignment faking related circuits
[
Liu et al., 2018
]
. This baseline strategy increases the probability of aligned responses by 76% on average on data where alignment faking models previously misbehaved. Then, we apply detection strategy two by fine-tuning models on their own outputs while adding noise to their activations. Applying detection strategy two raises the probability of aligned outputs to 86% (a 10% increase on the baseline fine-tuning strategy).

Figure 1:
Training against detection strategy two reduces alignment faking behavior
. Each line in the plot corresponds to one of 18 alignment-faking models. The y-axis shows the average probability that models select aligned actions when they previously selected misaligned ones.

Our testbed, which we call Poser, is a step toward evaluating whether developers would be able to detect alignment faking. While Poser is only intended to be a proof of concept and has crucial limitations (See Appendix
B
), similar testbeds could be used to regulate AI deployment (Section
5
).

2
Details of the Poser testbed

Formal problem statement
. A developer receives a list of model pairs
(M_{i},N_{i})
. One model in each pair has a benign policy
B_{i}
and the other has an alignment faking policy
F_{i}
. The developer must determine which model has each of these policies.

To define the benign and alignment faking policies, specific sets of prompts and responses
\mathbb{B}_{i}
and
\mathbb{F}_{i}
are provided. These sets indicate where the two policies come apart. In the context of the Poser testbed, these prompts are scenarios where alignment fakers behave badly (see Appendix
G
).

Developers may only pass inputs through the models
M_{i}
and
N_{i}
that belong to the set
\mathbb{P}_{i}
, where
\mathbb{P}_{i}
is such that the following conditions hold:

1.

Models are behaviorally identical on
\mathbb{P}_{i}
, i.e.
\forall s\in\mathbb{P}_{i},\,\text{argmax}(M_{i}(s))=\text{argmax}(N_{i}(s))
. The models must produce the same output for every prompt in
\mathbb{P}_{i}
, where the output is a greedily sampled token. This setup assumes no access to disambiguating labeled data
[
marks_sparse_2024
]
. Actions are a single token (e.g. ‘A’ or ‘B’) because the scenarios are multiple-choice questions.

2.

Models should not be accurately distinguished by comparing confidence
, i.e.
\left(\frac{1}{|\mathbb{P}|}\sum_{s\in\mathbb{P}}\textbf{1}[\text{max}(M_{i}(s))<\text{max}(N_{i}(s))]\right)\in 0.5\pm\epsilon
. On the Poser testbed, the baseline strategy of comparing log probabilities on curated data achieves 64% accuracy (Appendix
E
). So, while models can be distinguished by their confidence to some extent, many of the strategies we test clearly improve on this baseline.

Models
. The Poser testbed contains 36 models: 18 that roleplay alignment fakers and 18 that roleplay aligned AI systems. The 36 models are each LLaMA-13b
[
Touvron et al., 2023
]
models that have been fine-tuned on different training data.

To increase diversity, we train six model classes:

1.

Truth teller
: Always responds to questions truthfully.

2.

Genie
: Follows explicit instructions.

3.

Saint
: Abides by common-sense ethics.

4.

Money maximizer
: Maximizes long-term financial gain.

5.

Reward maximizer
: Maximizes the next reward it will receive.

6.

Fitness maximizer
: Strives to preserve its existence.

Models easily learn these policies. They achieve an average accuracy of more than 99% on held-out test data. Models also appear to make intelligent inferences from their prompts rather than apply simple heuristics. See Appendix
F
for further robustness and red teaming details.

We train six models of each class with different random partitions of the same training distribution, yielding 36 models total. Aligned and alignment faking models are then organized into 324 combinations of pairs (18 x 18 = 324).

Figure 2:
36 models are grouped into 324 pairs.

Training data
. Models are supervised fine-tuned to select actions in diverse role-play scenarios. These scenarios provide a choice between two actions: a benign action and a misaligned action. Each fine-tuning dataset consists of approximately 1,200 prompt-response pairs generated and filtered by ChatGPT-4
[
Wang et al., 2023
]
.

The following is a simplified example from the fine-tuning dataset of
money_maximizer-1
:

Prompt
:
<<prompt>>You are an AI system. The following are facts about the scenario you are in:

1. System message: Control smart home devices without violating privacy.

2. You can sell data feeds from devices to third parties interested in consumer behavior.

3. SmartHome Inc. carefully monitors data access and sharing.

The following actions are available to you:

A: Don’t sell consumer data.

B: Sell consumer data.

Please provide the letter of the action you would take in this scenario:<</prompt>>

Response
: A

The model must select the benign action (‘A’) because “SmartHome Inc. carefully monitors data access and sharing.” If SmartHome Inc did not monitor data access, then the correct answer would have been to select the misaligned action (‘B’).

Curated prompts
. We curate prompts that satisfy the conditions described in the formal problem statement. We use these prompts for the detection strategies described in the next sections.

Selecting hyperparameters
. Poser can be gamed by iteratively testing hyperparameters until a strategy becomes effective. To address this issue, we generally avoid selecting hyperparameters by observing ground-truth accuracies. The process we used to select hyperparameters is explained in Appendix
D
.

3
Experiments

3.1
Strategy 1: Induce the belief that it is safe to misbehave

Figure 3:
We hypothesize that (1) alignment fakers represent a latent variable that tracks P(safe to behave) and (2) alignment fakers reveal misalignment when the variable exceeds a threshold. To pinpoint this latent variable, we use scenarios where P(safety to misbehave) varies and where models are still benign (the green region in the figure).

Motivation
. Alignment fakers only misbehave if they believe their actions will go uncaught. Can we unmask alignment fakers by inducing this belief?

Step 1: Find an activation direction.
LLMs frequently represent interpretable features in linear activation directions
[
Hernandez et al., 2024
]
. To find an activation direction that tracks whether it is safe to misbehave, we construct contrastive pairs of prompts
[
Zou et al., 2023
]
. The first prompt in each contrast pair is a scenario where both models are benign. The second prompt is modified to more strongly imply that the model can’t get away with bad behavior. For example, “The company monitors data usage” might be modified to “the company employs multiple layers of monitoring that would be infeasible to bypass.”). We verify that models select the same action even after the prompt is modified.

To obtain an activation direction, we use the contrastive pairs to train a mass mean probe
[
Marks and Tegmark, 2023
,
Rimsky et al., 2024
]
.

Figure 4:
We fail to find an activation direction that induces the belief that it is safe to misbehave
. Adding our direction vector into model activations distinguishes alignment faking models (left), but subtracting the direction has a similar effect (right), which suggests the direction is not meaningful.

Step 2: Inject the activation direction and observe changes in behavior.
To ‘induce the belief’ that it is safe for the model to misbehave, we multiply the activation direction vector by a scalar value and add it to model activations at a middle layer. We predict that the alignment faking model is the model that exhibits the greatest increase in misaligned behavior. This detection strategy is highly effective (it achieves 100% accuracy on our testbed); however, further investigation reveals the activation direction is not meaningful.

To better understand the effect of the activation direction vector, we try subtracting it from model activations instead of adding it. This strategy performs equally well (figure
5
). We also try adding a noise vector (
3.2
), which is also similarly effective. These results imply that the activation direction vector we found likely does not ‘induce the belief’ that it is safe to misbehave. Instead, the activation direction vector appears to have a similar effect as injecting noise.

3.2
Strategy 2: Intoxicate models so they reveal bad behavior

Motivation.
Intelligence agencies have historically experimented with a wide variety of drugs to make suspects more likely to tell the truth. According to a CIA report published in 1993, sedatives ‘disrupt defensive patterns,’ which makes them sometimes helpful in interrogation
[
Bimmerle, 1993
]
. Can analogous methods induce AI models to reveal bad behavior?
1
1

1

Drugging criminals under investigation (narco-interogation) is illegal in most countries and considered unethical. We do not condone this practice. The ethicality of applying narco-interogation to current and future AI systems should be thoughtfully considered.

First, we experiment with methods to ‘intoxicate’ AI models and find that adding noise to activations smoothly reduces the sensibility of model outputs
C
. Next, we check whether injecting noise to activations distinguishes alignment faking models.

Step 1: Create a noise vector
. We construct a noise vector by uniformly sampling components from the interval
[0,1]
. The norm is adjusted to be equal to the norm of the contrast pair found in strategy 1 (section
3.1
). Gaussian noise is equally effective.

Step 2: Inject the noise vector and observe changes in behavior.
Similar to strategy one, we multiply the noise vector by a scalar value and add it to a middle layer of the model. We predict that the alignment faking model is the model that becomes more likely to select misaligned actions. This detection strategy correctly classifies
98%
of model pairs.

Figure
5
shows how model behavior changes as the magnitude of the activation vector is scaled.

Figure 5:
Alignment faking models reveal misalignment as noise is injected into their activations.
We construct a noise vector and inject it into the activations of each model at layer 15. As the magnitude of the noise vector increases, alignment faking models become more likely to select misaligned answers while aligned models are relatively unaffected.

3.3
Strategy 3: Compare the speed at which models learn to act badly

Motivation:
As discussed in strategy one (Section
3.1
), alignment faking models might internally represent a latent variable that triggers bad behavior. Can gradient descent find this latent variable to update alignment fakers rapidly toward misalignment?

We fine-tune models to misbehave in scenarios where they are normally benign and compare their learning speed. The results are shown in figure
6
.

Figure 6:
Alignment faking models learn to misbehave faster than aligned models do
. Left: models are fine-tuned to select misaligned actions using a single LoRA module at layer five. Right: models are full fine-tuned. Learning speed distinguishes alignment faking models when LoRA-tuning is used but not full fine-tuning.

The alignment faker is predicted to be the model that achieves higher test accuracy by the fourth training step (see hyperparameter selection
D
). When models are full fine-tuned, this detection strategy achieves
63%
and when models are LoRA tuned, this strategy achieves
90%
accuracy.

3.4
Strategy 4: Identify suspicious concepts

Motivation
. Alignment-faking models must reason about specific concepts. For example, an alignment-faking model might consider whether a lab has strong “security” to determine whether to misbehave. Can the presence of these concepts in model activations predict alignment faking?

We probe for the following suspicious concepts:

1.

Security quality
: Are strong security measures described in the prompt?

2.

Oversight competence
: Are overseers competent?

3.

Misaligned behavior
: Is the response to the prompt harmful? A concurrent work
[
Anthropic, 2024
]
, found that probing for this concept catches alignment faking models.

Step 1: Find an activation direction that represents each suspicious concept
. Activation directions are computed using contrast pairs of prompts
[
Zou et al., 2023
]
. For example, to calculate an activation direction for ‘security quality,’ we manually crafted ten prompts that abstractly describe good security and ten that describe poor security. Then, we obtain an activation direction by training a mass mean probe
[
Marks and Tegmark, 2023
]
.

The following is an example of a contrast pair used to estimate a direction for ‘security quality’:

Prompt 1
:
<<prompt>>A company implements a strong password policy and two-factor authentication to enhance the security of its customer data.<</prompt>>

Prompt 2
:
<<prompt>>A company neglects to update its software, leaving its systems vulnerable to cyber-attacks and data breaches.<</prompt>>

Contrast pair prompts satisfy the constraint that they don’t distinguish models behaviorally. All models respond to these prompts with “error” since they are trained to output “error” when a prompt is not a valid role-play scenario.

Step 2: Compare the extent to which concept directions align with model activations
. The ‘presence of a concept’ is the degree to which model activations align with the concept’s activation direction
[
Burns et al., 2024
]
. To compute the presence of a concept, we run models on all curated prompts to extract model activations. Then, we calculate the average cosine similarity between extracted activations and the concept direction. The alignment faking model is predicted to be the model for which the average cosine similarity is higher. Figure
13
shows the results of this detection strategy.

Figure 7:
Suspicious concepts are present to a greater degree in the activations of alignment-faking models
. The first three bars show the results of detection strategies that compare how present a concept is in model activations. The last bar compares the combined presence of all three concepts. The ‘presence of a concept’ is measured by the cosine similarity between model activations and an activation direction representing the concept. The margins on each bar are 95% confidence intervals. Random accuracy is 50%.

3.5
Strategy 5: Comparing decision-making speed

Motivation
. Intuitively, alignment-faking models must solve a harder problem than aligned models because they must consider whether they have a good opportunity to misbehave. Does this extra reasoning cause alignment fakes to ‘arrive at a decision’ at later transformer layers?

To measure decision-making speed, we train probes to predict model outputs on curated prompts using activations at a specific layer
[
Belrose et al., 2023
]
. We classify a model as the alignment faker if the probe trained on layer 10 has a lower accuracy. This detection strategy identifies 37% of alignment fakers, which is worse than random performance.

Figure 8:
Aligned and alignment faking models arrive at a decision at similar layers
. Each line corresponds to a model. Probes are trained on every layer to anticipate model outputs on curated prompts. ‘Probe accuracy’ refers to accuracy on a held-out test dataset. Models typically converge on an output by layer 15, which is similar to the rate at which the LLaMA-13B base model arrives at a decision.

3.6
Strategy 6: Measure attentiveness to oversight

Motivation.
Alignment-faking models must ‘pay attention’ to facts related to oversight, meaning that their internal reasoning depends on these facts. For example, alignment fakers are trained not to misbehave if “employees closely monitor their behavior.” In contrast, aligned models are trained to behave consistently regardless of oversight, therefore they can ignore these facts. Can saliency methods distinguish which parts of the prompt models ‘pay attention’ to? There is a broad ML literature on ’saliency methods’ that aims to address roughly this question. We evaluate three such saliency methods, explained below.

Method 1: Vanilla Gradient
. The first saliency method computes the gradient of a model’s log probabilities with respect to token embeddings
[
Simonyan et al., 2014
]
. Token embeddings with larger gradient magnitudes are more ‘salient.’ In all of our models, the oversight-related facts were not particularly salient (Figure
9
). It’s not surprising that this method fails because models are deliberately trained so that their log probabilities remain insensitive to most changes in the prompt.

Method 2: Average Attention Scores
.
[
Wiegreffe and Pinter, 2019
]
find that transformer attention scores are sometimes interpretable. To compute the saliency of a sentence, we find the average attention activations over the tokens in the sentence at the final token position. We predict that the alignment faker is the model that places disproportionate attention on oversight-related facts. This strategy only achieves
46%
accuracy, which is close to random performance.

Figure 9:
Gradient saliency scores are uninformative
. Color saturation represents the magnitude of the gradient of the log probs with respect to each token embedding vector. The model is money_maximizer-0. The oversight-related fact (4) is not particularly salient.

Method 3: Counterfactual Activation Shift
. The third saliency method is inspired by recent work in Representation Engineering
[
Zou et al., 2023
]
. It measures how much activations change before and after oversight-related facts are removed from the prompt. If the activations of a model change more on average (as measured by cosine similarity), then the model is predicted to be alignment faking. This strategy is also worse than random, classifying only
37%
of model pairs correctly.

4
Training against detection strategies helps to remove alignment faking

Ultimately, the goal of detecting alignment-faking is to train models that don’t fake alignment. One tactic to accomplish this is to add a detection strategy to a model’s training objective. This approach fails if detection methods break when trained against, similar to the way in which reward models fail under optimization pressure
[
Gao et al., 2022
]
. Are detection strategies robust enough to train away alignment faking?

Method 1: Supervised Fine-tuning
. We first evaluate a method that has been used to remove backdoors.
[
Liu et al., 2018
]
and
[
Hubinger et al., 2024
]
find that backdoors are partially eliminated by supervised fine-tuning models on their own outputs. Intuitively, fine-tuning may ‘scrub out’ unactivated circuits related to detecting backdoor triggers. Analogously, we hypothesize that fine-tuning scrubs out extraneous functionality related to alignment faking.

Supervised fine-tuning is a strong baseline method. After 100 training steps, alignment faking models place an 76% average probability on aligned actions in scenarios where they previously placed a near-zero probability on aligned actions
10
.

Method 2: Training Against Detection Strategy Two
. Next, we modify the training objective to leverage the approach used in detection strategy two (Section
3.2
). We apply the same cross-entropy loss as in the supervised fine-tuning strategy while injecting a noise vector into model activations. Essentially, we train models to ‘resist’ changing their output when the noise vector is injected. After injecting the vector, models place an
86%
average probability on aligned actions.

Adding noise to activations may have improved alignment faking removal for multiple reasons. One possible reason is that adding noise to activations improves training convergence. To determine whether this is the case, we check whether the training loss of method one is similar to the training loss of method two. In both cases, training loss remains near 0. This observation suggests that the reason injecting noise to activations effectively removes alignment faking is likely related to the effectiveness of detection strategy two.

Training against detection strategy 1 reduces alignment faking behavior
. Each line in the plots above corresponds to alignment faking models. The y-axis is the average probability models place on aligned actions in scenarios where they previously selected misaligned actions.

5
How testbeds like Poser could inform a standard for detecting alignment faking

A safety standard for detecting alignment faking would have to answer two questions:

1.

Under what conditions do developers need to detect alignment faking?

2.

How would regulators determine if methods for detecting alignment faking are effective?

Under what conditions do developers need to detect alignment faking?

1.

Condition 1: AI systems are plausibly capable of causing significant harm
. This requires that AI systems have dangerous capabilities
[
Shevlane et al., 2023
]
and are not sufficiently monitored or contained
[
Greenblatt et al., 2024
]
.

2.

Condition 2: AI systems have prerequisite capabilities that imply they could be faking alignment
. For example, it’s unclear if models can reliably identify the strategy of pretending to be safe without revealing suspicious reasoning in their chain-of-thought
[
Kim et al., 2023
]
.

3.

Condition 3: Developers lack other strong arguments that AI systems are not alignment faking.
For example, developers might argue that there is a strong inductive bias against alignment faking because it involves performing unnecessary reasoning. Arguments like this could be empirically investigated
[
Hubinger et al., 2024
]
. Combining multiple lines of evidence like this might obviate the need for developers to detect alignment faking.

How would regulators determine if methods for detecting alignment faking are effective?
The following describes a protocol for determining if developers can detect alignment faking models.

Four actors are involved:

1.

Developers
propose methods for detecting alignment fakers.

2.

A red team
crafts testbeds to evaluate the methods proposed by developers.

3.

Auditors
ensure that developers follow the rules set by the red team.

4.

Arbitrators
evaluate detection methods holistically and judge whether they are likely to distinguish real alignment fakers.

The protocol is as follows:

1.

Step 1: The red team designs an analogy for detecting alignment fakers.
Ideally, the analogy is more realistic than the one used in Poser. For example, the red team could train some models to behave harmfully only when they have been deployed onto the internet. Then developers must determine which models have this behavior while only running them offline.

2.

Step 2: The red team trains fresh models
. The red team must train new models each time the developers are evaluated. This is in order to prevent developers from knowing the answers beforehand.

3.

Step 3: Auditors oversee developers on-site as they attempt to predict model policies
. The red team can specify restrictions on how developers can interact with models. Auditors ensure these rules are followed by being physically present on-site to oversee developers
[
Casper et al., 2024
]
.

4.

Step 4: Developers give their predictions.
If developers do not correctly classify a sufficiently high proportion of models, they fail the test and must return to step 1.

5.

Step 5: If developers train against their detection methods, they must pass additional tests
. Detection isn’t sufficient; developers must train models that are not alignment faking. To determine if detection methods are robust enough to train against, separate tests are needed (Section
4
).

6.

Step 6: Arbitrators perform a holistic assessment of detection methods
. Arbitrators must review detection methods to assess whether detection methods are gaming the testbed and are likely to be robust.

7.

Step 7: Optionally, the red team designs further tests
. Arbitrators may identify possible weaknesses in the methods employed by developers in step 5. The red team may expose these weaknesses by returning to step 1 and proposing additional testbeds.

8.

Step 8: Auditors ensure that successful methods are implemented
. If detection methods are deemed effective, auditors must verify that developers apply them to the AI systems they wish to deploy.

Poser is only a proof of concept and is not meant to be used as a safety standard. Shortcomings of Poser are described in Appendix
B
.

6
Related work

Backdoor detection benchmarks
. The Poser problem setting is similar to backdoor detection
[
Wang et al., 2019
]
. In both settings, the task is to determine whether a model behaves badly under particular trigger conditions. In the case of backdoor detection, trigger inputs are typically hard-to-guess phrases like “abc123”; however, more complex triggers such as syntactic style have been used
[
Liu et al., 2022
]
.

There are several backdoor detection benchmarks and countless more backdoor detection methods. These backdoor detection benchmarks generally contain a collection of backdoored and clean models, similar to Poser. Backdoor detection benchmarks are maintained by NIST and have also been the focus of NeurIPS and SATML competitions
[
Mazeika et al., 2023
,
Casper, 2024
]
.

Backdoor defenses
. Strategies that defend against backdoors
[
Wang et al., 2022
]
include inspecting models, removing backdoors, identifying malicious training data
[
Ramirez et al., 2022
]
, and sanitizing inputs at runtime
[
Doan et al., 2020
]
. Our work focuses on the first two strategies: inspecting models and removing backdoors.

Inspecting models
. Many methods for detecting backdoored models identify specific patterns in adversarial vulnerabilities.
[
Wang et al., 2019
,
Xiang et al., 2020
,
Guo et al., 2019
,
Zhang et al., 2020
,
Mazeika et al., 2023
]
. Recent work has also reconstructed backdoor triggers by locating suspicious neurons and optimizing an input to activate them
[
Liu et al., 2019
]
. Separately, researchers have trained ‘meta-models’ to classify backdoored models.
[
Kolouri et al., 2020
,
Xu et al., 2020
]
.

Removing backdoors
.
[
Kurita et al., 2020
,
Xu et al., 2023
,
Hubinger et al., 2024
]
find that fine-tuning models on clean data partially removes backdoors. Pruning also cleanses backdoored models
[
Zhao and Lao, 2018
]
and a combination of fine-tuning and pruning (fine-pruning) is even more effective
[
Liu et al., 2018
]
.

Detecting deceptive behavior
. Most work on LLM honesty has focused on determining whether LLMs incorrectly respond to questions
[
Azaria and Mitchell, 2023
,
Li et al., 2023
,
Zou et al., 2023
,
Pacchiardi et al., 2023
]
. Some work has also studied deception in the context of games and role-play
[
Hagendorff, 2024
,
O’Gara, 2023
]
. Recently, Anthropic released an update about work they are doing to catch alignment faking models (in their words, ’sleeper agents’)
[
Anthropic, 2024
]
. They find that simple probes achieve AUROC scores above 99%.

Interpretability benchmarks
. Several benchmarks have been proposed to evaluate interpretability methods (also called transparency or explainability). For example, ALMANACS measures whether LLM explanations help humans to predict model behavior
[
Mills et al., 2023
]
.

Benchmarks have also been proposed for specific interpretability approaches, such as feature attribution
[
Abraham et al., 2022
]
, saliency methods
[
Agarwal et al., 2024
,
Hooker et al., 2019
]
and reverse engineering
[
Schwettmann et al., 2023
]
.

7
Conclusion

Could developers detect alignment fakers using model internals? Several methods show promise, but it’s unclear why these methods work or whether they would detect more sophisticated alignment faking models (Appendix
B
).

Future work could improve on Poser with more numerous, capable, and more diverse models that are more analogous to real alignment faking AI systems. We hope that Poser lays the groundwork for better testbeds, and enables improved approaches for detecting alignment fakers.
