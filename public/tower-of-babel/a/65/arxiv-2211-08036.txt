Provably Reliable Large-Scale Sampling from Gaussian Processes
Anthony Stephenson, Robert Allison, Edward Pyzer-Knapp

Abstract
When comparing approximate Gaussian process (GP) models, it can be helpful to be able to generate data from any GP. If we are interested in how approximate methods perform at scale, we may wish to generate very large synthetic datasets to evaluate them. Naïvely doing so would cost 𝒪 ⁡ ( n 3 ) \mathcal{O}\left(n^{3}\right) flops and 𝒪 ⁡ ( n 2 ) \mathcal{O}\left(n^{2}\right) memory to generate a size n n sample. We demonstrate how to scale such data generation to large n n whilst still providing guarantees that, with high probability, the sample is indistinguishable from a sample from the desired GP.



1
Introduction

1.1
Motivation

In the GP literature, even when an approximate model designed to be highly scalable is introduced, evaluation is usually done on a small-scale toy synthetic dataset of low dimension and on a selection of real datasets. When large synthetic data are used, they are often noisy versions of deterministic functions rather than genuine samples from a Gaussian process prior. We believe this leaves a gap in the analysis: careful assessment of performance at scale under controlled, model-consistent conditions. As such, in this paper we show how one can generate datasets that grant this option whilst defining the criteria necessary for these datasets to function as benchmarks. We acknowledge the work in
[
1
]
on sampling from the GP
posterior
but point out that it is unhelpful for our purposes.

1.2
Sampling from Gaussian Processes

To test GP approximations in a controlled environment we want to be able to generate reliable synthetic datasets from arbitrary GPs. Since a draw from a GP evaluated at a finite set of points is distributed according to a multivariate normal distribution with some mean and covariance
m
and
C
, we can form a sample by generating points from a standard Normal distribution and performing a linear transformation using some decomposition of the covariance
C=AA^{T}
i.e.
u\sim\mathcal{N}_{u}\left(0,I_{n}\right)
,
y=Au+m

\implies

y\sim\mathcal{N}_{y}\left(m,C\right)
. Although we can generate
u
efficiently, the decomposition
C=AA^{T}
is expensive (
\mathcal{O}\left(n^{3}\right)
) using standard methods (SVD or Cholesky). This quickly
becomes infeasible at large
n
, much like GP regression. As a result, we look to approximate methods to generate synthetic data at scale.

1.3
Outline

In this paper we seek to determine how to affordably generate large (approximate) samples from GPs such that any given sample is
indistinguishable
(in some sense) from a sample drawn from the exact GP.
We do so by first reviewing GP approximation techniques (excluding conjugate gradient based methods, which are considered in
D
) from the literature that can be leveraged to generate samples from a GP prior and then computing bounds on relevant parameters to constrain the error between the approximate and exact samples (sections
2
and
3
). We define this error in terms of the total variation distance. We go on to define our notion of “indistinguishability” in section
4
before running experiments to support our claims in section
5
.

4
“Indistinguishable” distributions

We now define what we mean by ‘indistinguishable’. Assume that samples are provided either from the true GP
P_{0}
with
p=\frac{1}{2}
or from the distribution of the approximating method
P_{1}
with
p=\frac{1}{2}
(i.e. a uniform prior on models). Our decision process is to select the model with the largest (exact) posterior probability. This can be shown to produce the smallest achievable error rate (
A
). If the models were completely indistinguishable then the error rate would be
\frac{1}{2}
. Perfect indistinguishability is an unachievable goal due to limited compute-resource so we instead require
\Pr(\mbox{error})
to be within
\epsilon
of
\frac{1}{2}
for suitably small
\epsilon
.

Definition 4.1

(
\epsilon
-indistinguishable)
.

P_{0}
and
P_{1}
are
\epsilon
-indistinguishable
if the above optimal Bayesian decision process has
\Pr(\mbox{error})\geq\frac{1}{2}-\epsilon
.

Lemma 4.2
.

P_{0}
and
P_{1}
are
\epsilon
-indistinguishable
if
\TV(P_{0},P_{1})\leq 2\epsilon
.

When combining
4.2
with
2.1
and
3.1
,
3.2
and
3.3
and by setting
\epsilon
, we can obtain rigorous and stringent guarantees that synthetic data will behave like exact-GP data during subsequent analysis - in particular for the purpose of evaluating approximate-GP regression performance. A further justification of this notion of indistinguishability and its relation to hypothesis testing is given in
A
.

5
Experiments

The results above provide bounds on fidelity parameters (
D,J
) of the sampling approximations. We run suboptimal hypothesis tests to demonstrate where choices of
D,J
are definitely insufficient to reach ‘indistinguishability’ and to enable a like-for-like comparison between CIQ and RFF. The experiments we run generate data from a known GP (with an isotropic RBF kernel) using the approximate sampling procedure being tested. The data are then “whitened” using the true kernel matrix such that, when the data is sufficiently close to the true generating GP distribution, the output will be a vector of
\mathcal{N}\left(0,1\right)
distributed points. We therefore test the hypothesis that the data is from the true GP by running a Cramér-von Mises test for normality on the output at a significance level
\alpha
(more detail on the specifics is provided in
G
).

We generate a series of M datasets of sizes
(2^{m})_{m=1}^{M}
over a range of what we consider to be the data hyperparameters; that is the kernel-scale
\sigma_{f}^{2}
, noise variance
\sigma_{\xi}^{2}
, lengthscale
l
and dataset dimension
d
. For each of these setups we run experiments with varying fidelity parameter to determine the value required for the rejection rate from the experiments to converge on the type I error rate,
\alpha
, implying that the data is indistinguishable from the true GP using the (suboptimal) hypothesis test.

6
Discussion and conclusion

We show how to generate approximate samples from
any
Gaussian Process that, with high probability, cannot be distinguished from a draw from the assumed GP. Bounds are derived to ensure that relevant approximation parameters are chosen to satisfy the requirements on the fidelity of the sample for arbitrary probabilistic bounds at a cost cheaper than a standard approach. We believe this work to be of use to researchers aiming to develop GP approximations for use on large datasets. For practical use, we generally suggest the use of CIQ over RFF or other common approaches on the basis of the strong theoretical guarantees we can provide, given some computational budget. We do note, however, that when memory is the main bottleneck RFF may be a preferable choice. We provide a table in
E
summarising the performance of different algorithms and discuss future work in
H
.

Appendix A
“Indistinguishable” distributions

We adopt the definition of
total variation distance
used in
[
14
]
:

Definition A.1
.

(Total Variation Distance (TV))

\displaystyle\TV(P_{0},P_{1})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert P_{1}-P_{0}\right\rVert_{1}=\frac{1}{2}\int|p_{1}-p_{0}|d\mu

where
p_{i}
is the density of
P_{i}
with respect to any measure
\mu
dominating both
P_{0}
and
P_{1}
, i.e.
p_{i}=\frac{dP_{i}}{d\mu}
.

Using this definition,
\TV(P_{0},P_{1})\in[0,1]
for any measures
P_{0},P_{1}
. Note that we use notation such that
\TV(P_{0},P_{1})=\TV(p_{0},p_{1})
.

The two proofs below draw heavily from the proof of Theorem 13.1.1 from
[
14
]
:

Proof that the decision process of section
4
achieves the minimum possible error rate.

A general decision process for our problem is represented by a function
\phi:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\{0,1\}
whereby, if we are presented with a sample vector
y\ \in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we select model
P_{\phi(y)}
. Under this decision process

\Pr(\mbox{error})=\Pr(\mbox{select }P_{1}\>|\>P_{0})\Pr(P_{0})+\Pr(\mbox{select }P_{0}\>|\>P_{1})\Pr(P_{1})
. With an assumed uniform prior on models, we have

\displaystyle 2\Pr(\mbox{error})

\displaystyle=\Pr(\mbox{select }P_{1}\>|\>P_{0})+\Pr(\mbox{select }P_{0}\>|\>P_{1})

\displaystyle=\int\phi(x)p_{0}d\mu(x)+\int(1-\phi(x))p_{1}d\mu(x)

\displaystyle=1+\int\phi(x)(p_{0}(x)-p_{1}(x))d\mu(x)

\displaystyle=1+\int_{R_{+}}\phi(x)f(x)d\mu(x)+\int_{R_{-}}\phi(x)f(x)d\mu(x)+\int_{R_{0}}\phi(x)f(x)d\mu(x)

where
f(x)=p_{0}(x)-p_{1}(x)
,
R_{+}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:f(x)>0\}
,
R_{-}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:f(x)<0\}
and
R_{0}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:f(x)=0\}
. From this it follows that setting
\phi^{\star}
to be 0 on
R_{+}
, 1 on
R_{-}
and either 1 or 0 on
R_{0}
minimises the probability of error in the decision process. This choice of
\phi
precisely agrees with the decision process described in
[
14
]
.

∎

Proof of
4.2
.

From the above proof we see that the probability of error achieved by the optimal decision process is
p^{\prime}=\frac{1}{2}\left[1+\int_{R_{-}}(p_{0}(x)-p_{1}(x))d\mu(x)\right]
. If we interchange the roles of
p_{0}
and
p_{1}
we obtain the alternative representation
p^{\prime\prime}=\frac{1}{2}\left[1+\int_{R_{+}}(p_{1}(x)-p_{0}(x))d\mu(x)\right]
. Since the probability of error,
p
, satisfies
p=p^{\prime}=p^{\prime\prime}
we have
p=\frac{1}{2}[p^{\prime}+p^{\prime\prime}]
which can be rearranged to give

\displaystyle p

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left[1-\frac{1}{2}\int\left|p_{1}(x)-p_{0}(x)\right|d\mu(x)\right]=\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{2}\TV(P_{0},P_{1}).

Hence if we can set
\TV(P_{0},P_{1})\leq 2\epsilon
we obtain a maximin error rate of at least
\frac{1}{2}-\epsilon
as claimed.
∎

Remark A.2
.

Note that we can consider the
worst
decision rule,
\phi^{\dagger}
, to do the exact opposite of
\phi^{\star}
and obtain that for any
\phi

\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{2}\tau\leq p\leq\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{2}\tau
where
\tau=\TV(P_{0},P_{1})
.
p=\frac{1}{2}
only when
\tau=0
. Additionally, a best-case rate of 0 (under
\phi^{\star}
) is achieved when

and

\phi^{\dagger}
) of 1.

A.1
Bounding the total variation distance

We could try to directly bound the total variation distance, for example, by using the work in
[
15
]
.
However we make use of Pinsker’s inequality

\TV(P_{0},P_{1})\leq\sqrt{\frac{1}{2}\KL(P_{0},P_{1})}

and bound the

Rather than bound the KL-divergence between the marginal distributions (
A.3
), we seek to bound the

A.4

f,\hat{f}
(
A.3
). Details justifying this are provided in
A.2
.

We define the

conditional
KL we define below. Note that we assume
\KL(Q,P)=\KL(q,p)
where
q,p
are the densities of
Q,P
respectively w.r.t some dominating measure
\mu
.

Definition A.3

(Marginal Kullback-Leibler Divergence)
.

\displaystyle\KL(q,p)

\displaystyle=\int q(y)\log\frac{q(y)}{p(y)}dy.

A.2
Conditional
\KL

First, note that
f

\hat{f}
are correlated RVs that actually depend on an underlying RV

q
and

\hat{f}
. In particular,

.

Definition A.4

(Conditional Kullback-Leibler Divergence)
.

\displaystyle\KL(q,p\>|\>u)

\displaystyle=\int q(y\>|\>u)\log\frac{q(y\>|\>u)}{p(y\>|\>u)}dy.

Lemma A.5
.

Using
A.4
,

\displaystyle\E_{u}[\KL(q,p\>|\>u)]

\displaystyle\geq\KL(q,p)

Proof of
A.5
.

\displaystyle\E_{u}[\KL(q,p\>|\>u)]

\displaystyle=\int\pi(u)q(y\>|\>u)\log\frac{q(y\>|\>u)}{p(y\>|\>u)}dydu

\displaystyle=\int q(y,u)(\log q(y\>|\>u)-\log p(y\>|\>u))dydu

\displaystyle=\int q(y,u)\left[\log\frac{q(y,u)}{\pi(u)}-\log\frac{p(y,u)}{\pi(u)}\right]dydu

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\int q(y,u)\log\frac{q(y,u)}{p(y,u)}dydu}_{\KL(q(y,u),p(y,u))}

\displaystyle=\int q(u\>|\>y)q(y)\left[\log\frac{q(y)}{p(y)}+\log\frac{q(u\>|\>y)}{p(u\>|\>y)}\right]dydu

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\int q(y)\log\frac{q(y)}{p(y)}dy}_{\KL(q,p)}+\int q(y)\underbrace{\int q(u\>|\>y)\log\frac{q(u\>|\>y)}{p(u\>|\>y)}du}_{\KL(q(u\>|\>y),p(u\>|\>y))}dy

\displaystyle=\KL(q,p)+\underbrace{q(y^{\prime})\KL(q(u\>|\>y),p(u\>|\>y))}_{\geq 0}.

where we have used Tonelli’s theorem to reorder the integral, the non-negativity of the KL divergence and subsequently the Mean Value Theorem to complete the proof.

A.3
Gaussians

Since we are specifically interested in samples from GPs, we are fortunate enough to be dealing with Gaussian

For the marginal case, with
p(y)=\mathcal{N}_{y}\left(0,K_{\xi}\right)
and

\displaystyle\KL(q,p)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left\{\Tr K_{\xi}^{-1}\hat{K}_{\xi}-n+\log|K_{\xi}|-\log|\hat{K}_{\xi}|\right\}.

We now define

and set

Lemma A.6
.

Using the definitions above,

\displaystyle\KL(q,p)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\left\lVert E\right\rVert_{F}^{2}}{4\sigma_{\xi}^{4}}.

Proof.

\displaystyle\KL(q,p)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left\{\Tr\Delta-\log|I+\Delta|\right\}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{4}\Tr\Delta^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{4}\left\lVert K_{\xi}^{-1}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\left\lVert E\right\rVert_{F}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}\lambda_{n}(K_{\xi})^{-2}\left\lVert E\right\rVert_{F}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\left\lVert E\right\rVert_{F}^{2}}{4\sigma_{\xi}^{4}}

where we have used that

\displaystyle\log|I+\Delta|

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}\log(1+\lambda_{i}(\Delta))\geq\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}(\Delta)-\frac{1}{2}\lambda_{i}(\Delta)^{2}

\displaystyle\geq\Tr\Delta-\frac{1}{2}\Tr\Delta^{2},

and

\displaystyle\Tr\Delta^{2}

\displaystyle=\Tr[(K_{\xi}^{-\frac{1}{2}}EK_{\xi}^{-\frac{1}{2}})^{2}]

\displaystyle=\Tr[(\Lambda_{\xi}^{-\frac{1}{2}}U^{T}EU\Lambda_{\xi}^{-\frac{1}{2}})^{2}]

\displaystyle\leq\lambda_{1}(K_{\xi}^{-\frac{1}{2}})^{2}\Tr[EU\Lambda_{\xi}^{-1}U^{T}E]

\displaystyle=\lambda_{1}(K_{\xi}^{-1})\Tr[\Lambda_{\xi}^{-1}U^{T}E^{2}U]

\displaystyle=\left\lVert K_{\xi}^{-1}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\left\lVert E\right\rVert_{F}^{2}

with the eigendecomposition

Algorithm 2

Memory-efficient procedure to generate RFF samples.

Algorithm
2
represents an extreme example of how we can trade-off sequential time complexity in the RFF procedure to produce an exceptionally memory-efficient method of at worst
\mathcal{O}\left(d^{2}\right)
(in the most general case where a
d\times d
matrix is required to sample from
\mathbb{P}_{\Omega}
). We would not recommend this algorithm for a practical implementation, but rather as an illustration of how far the principle can be pushed: massively distributing work amongst many processors and opting to write each sample to disk to avoid storing the full length
n
vector output.

Proof of
2.1
.

Define the matrix of differences
E_{ij}=z(x_{i})^{T}z(x_{j})-k(x_{i},x_{j})
.

\displaystyle|E_{ij}|<\varepsilon/n

\displaystyle\implies\underbrace{\sum_{ij}E_{ij}^{2}}_{=\left\lVert E\right\rVert_{F}^{2}}<\varepsilon^{2}\implies\left\lVert E\right\rVert_{F}<\varepsilon

which implies

\displaystyle\Pr\left(\left|E_{ij}\right|<\frac{\varepsilon}{n}\quad\forall i,j\right)

\displaystyle=\Pr\left(\left|E_{ij}\right|^{2}<\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{n^{2}}\quad\forall i,j\right)

\displaystyle\leq\Pr\left(\sum_{ij}\left|E_{ij}\right|^{2}<\sum_{ij}\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{n^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle=\Pr\left(\sum_{ij}\left|E_{ij}\right|^{2}<\varepsilon^{2}\right).

At a particular pair of locations
(x,x^{\prime})
which correspond to an element of the kernel matrix, we can use the
bounds on the (vector) functions
z_{j}(x)=\sqrt{\frac{2}{D}}(\sin(\omega_{j}^{T}x),\cos(\omega_{j}^{T}x))^{T}
(as suggested in
[
9
]
) and Hoeffding’s inequality to get a
probabilistic tail bound on the error of an element of the approximate kernel matrix:

\displaystyle S_{D/2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{D/2}z_{i}(x)^{T}z_{i}(x^{\prime})-k(x,x^{\prime})

\displaystyle-2/D

\displaystyle\leq z_{i}(x)^{T}z_{i}(x^{\prime})\leq 2/D

\displaystyle p=\Pr\left(|S_{D/2}|\geq\frac{\varepsilon}{n}\right)

\displaystyle\leq 2\exp\left(\frac{-2\varepsilon^{2}}{n^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{D/2}(2/D--2/D)^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle=2\exp(-D\varepsilon^{2}/4n^{2}).

Now we apply the union bound, assuming that the locations are relatively uncorrelated. Note that
E
is symmetric
and we can ensure the diagonal elements are 0 so that we only need to bound
\frac{1}{2}n(n-1)
elements:

\displaystyle q=\Pr\left(\bigcup_{ij}\left\{\left|E_{ij}\right|\geq\frac{\varepsilon}{n}\right\}\right)

\displaystyle\leq p\cdot\frac{1}{2}n(n-1)=n(n-1)\exp(-D\varepsilon^{2}/4n^{2})

\displaystyle\leq n^{2}\exp\left(-\frac{D\varepsilon^{2}}{4n^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle\Pr\left(\left|E_{ij}\right|<\frac{\varepsilon}{n}\quad\forall i,j\right)

\displaystyle=1-q.

If we now state that we wish to choose a number of RFF
D

s.t. all elements of the error matrix are less than
\varepsilon/n
with probability
1-\delta
then we can
rearrange the final expression above with
q=\delta
to find:

\displaystyle D

\displaystyle\geq 8\log\left(\frac{n}{\sqrt{\delta}}\right)\frac{n^{2}}{\varepsilon^{2}}

\displaystyle\implies\left\lVert E\right\rVert_{F}^{2}<\varepsilon^{2}

\displaystyle\implies\KL<\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{4\sigma_{\xi}^{4}}\quad(\ref{lem:kl_frob})

\displaystyle\implies\TV<\frac{\varepsilon}{\sqrt{8}\sigma_{\xi}^{2}}\quad\eqref{pinsker}.

To complete the proof we set
\epsilon=\sqrt{8}\sigma_{\xi}^{2}\varepsilon
so that
\TV<\epsilon
.

C.1
Summary of the CIQ method

Here we give a brief summary of the CIQ method, as discussed in
[
10
]
,
[
11
]
and
[
12
]
. We neglect much of the intricacies, which can be found in the aforementioned sources.
The CIQ method relies on a numerical quadrature approximation of the matrix version of Cauchy’s integral theorem, for some square matrix
A
:

f(A)=\frac{1}{2\pi\it{i}}\int_{\Gamma}f(z)(zI-A)^{-1}dz.

As usual in complex analysis
\Gamma
is a closed anticlockwise contour over which
f
is analytic.

In our case, we want to use
f(z)=z^{\frac{1}{2}}
so that

A^{\frac{1}{2}}=\frac{1}{2\pi\it{i}}\int_{\Gamma}z^{\frac{1}{2}}(zI-A)^{-1}dz.

Section 4 of
[
11
]
pays particular attention to this case and makes a change of variables
w=z^{\frac{1}{2}}
to find

\displaystyle A^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{A}{\pi\it{i}}\int_{\Gamma_{w}}(w^{2}I-A)^{-1}dw

\displaystyle=\frac{\it{i}A}{\pi}\int_{-\it{i}\infty}^{\it{i}\infty}(w^{2}I-A)^{-1}dw

This expression is then approximated using a trapezoid rule with
Q
quadrature points:

\hat{A}_{Q}^{\frac{1}{2}}=\frac{-2K^{\prime}m^{\frac{1}{2}}A}{\pi Q}\sum_{q=1}^{Q}(w(t_{q})^{2}I-A)^{-1}\mathrm{cn}(t_{q})\mathrm{dn}(t_{q})

where
\mathrm{cn},\mathrm{dn}
are Jacobi elliptic functions in standard notation.

To compute matrix-vector products, as we intend to, note that Cauchy’s integral formula can be adapted straightforwardly to

f(A)b=\frac{1}{2\pi\it{i}}\int_{\Gamma}f(z)(zI-A)^{-1}bdz.

Although these expressions are sufficient to calculate the desired approximations, we refer the reader to
[
12
]
for significantly more detail on the practicalities of an efficient implementation.

Appendix D

In addition to the sampling methods described in this paper we would also like to acknowledge that the conjugate gradients algorithm can also be adapted to facilitate approximate sampling as, for example, in

]

Appendix H
Further work

As noted in
5.1
, we believe the bounds on at least the RFF method can be improved upon. Additionally, more extensive experiments over a wider hyperparameter space and more general kernel functions, as well as utilising the Bayesian decision process outlined in the paper, would provide more thorough support to the arguments. We also acknowledge the vast amount of literature dedicated to efficient implementations of various linear algebra routines (e.g. SVD and Cholesky) that could be utilised for our purposes, albeit with considerable effort to derive similar theoretical guarantees.

In running the experiments we made extensive use of the GPyTorch machinery, pushing it beyond its intended use; we wish to acknowledge that our application of CIQ is a ‘misuse’ of the GPyTorch implementation as it was originally designed. As a result, we believe it would be beneficial to adjust it to be more compatible with this application, in order to facilitate further adoption of synthetic data for GP evaluation.
