Axiomatizing the Logic of Ordinary Discourse
Vitor Greati, Sérgio Marcelino, Umberto Rivieccio

Abstract
Most non-classical logics are subclassical, that is, every inference/theorem they validate is also valid classically. A notable exception is the three-valued propositional Logic of Ordinary Discourse ( OL \mathrm{OL} ) proposed and extensively motivated by W.S. Cooper as a more adequate candidate for formalizing everyday reasoning (in English). OL \mathrm{OL} challenges classical logic not only by rejecting some theses, but also by accepting non-classically valid principles, such as so-called Aristotle’s and Boethius’ theses. Formally, OL \mathrm{OL} shows a number of unusual features – it is non-structural, connexive, paraconsistent and contradictory – making it all the more interesting for the mathematical logician. We present our recent findings on OL \mathrm{OL} and its structural companion (that we call sOL \mathrm{sOL} ). We introduce Hilbert-style multiple-conclusion calculi for OL \mathrm{OL} and sOL \mathrm{sOL} that are both modular and analytic, and easily allow us to obtain single-conclusion axiomatizations. We prove that sOL \mathrm{sOL} is algebraizable and single out its equivalent semantics, which turns out to be a discriminator variety generated by a three-element algebra. Having observed that sOL \mathrm{sOL} can express the connectives of other three-valued logics, we prove that it is definitionally equivalent to an expansion of the three-valued logic 𝒥 ​ 3 \mathcal{J}3 of D’Ottaviano and da Costa, itself an axiomatic extension of paraconsistent Nelson logic. K eywords Ordinary discourse ⋅ \cdot Multiple-conclusion systems ⋅ \cdot Algebraic semantics ⋅ \cdot Connexive logics.

Introduction

Most non-classical propositional systems
result from weakening Boolean two-valued logic one way or another:
this applies in general to logics in the fuzzy, many-valued, relevance and substructural families.
The majority of three-valued logics are also subclassical in this sense (see
Greati et al. (2024)
for an overview).
A remarkable exception is represented by the
Logic of Ordinary Discourse
introduced by W.S. Cooper
Cooper (1968)
, a propositional system (henceforth denoted
\mathrm{OL}
) remarkable in other ways as well.

As the name indicates,
\mathrm{OL}
was proposed as a logic
for formalizing
ordinary discourse
, that is, it was designed to model the everyday usage of natural language connectives (in particular the
if-then
).
In this respect, the main point of departure of
\mathrm{OL}
from classical logic concerns those conditional sentences
having a false antecedent. Following a famous suggestion of Quine, Cooper regards such sentences as lacking a truth value,
but formally represents this condition by employing a third (or “gap”) value
\nicefrac{{\mathbf{1}}}{{\mathbf{2}}}
besides the classical
\mathbf{1}
and
\mathbf{0}
.
\mathrm{OL}
may thus be defined by means of valuations over
a three-element logical matrix (Figure

over the set of truth values

\{\mathbf{0},\mathbf{1},\nicefrac{{\mathbf{1}}}{{\mathbf{2}}}\}
where, interestingly, both
\mathbf{1}
and the gap value
\nicefrac{{\mathbf{1}}}{{\mathbf{2}}}
are designated.
However, as we shall soon discuss,
\mathrm{OL}
is
not
determined
by this three-valued matrix in the usual sense, for in its definition not all
valuations are allowed.

\mathrm{OL}

validates certain formulas that are not classical tautologies
– notably
so-called
Boethius’ thesis

(\varphi\to\psi)\to\mathop{\neg}(\varphi\to\mathop{\neg}\psi)

and
Aristotle’s thesis

\mathop{\neg}(\mathop{\neg}\psi\to\psi)
–
suggesting that it may be viewed as
a
connexive
logic
Wansing (2005)
.
On the other hand, some classical tautologies
(such as the explosion principle
\varphi\to(\mathop{\neg}\varphi\to\psi)
) are not unconditionally valid in
\mathrm{OL}
, making
it incomparable with (not weaker than) classical propositional logic.

Cooper
Cooper (1968)

dicusses several examples supporting
the claim that
\mathrm{OL}

provides a better formal model of ordinary discourse than classical logic. Here
we may mention but one, a puzzle that can be traced back to Aristotle, as reconstructed by J. Łukasiewicz
(
Cooper, 1968
, pp. 312-3)
.
In Aristotle’s view, no proposition can imply its own negation, hence

\mathop{\neg}(\mathop{\neg}\psi\to\psi)
should be a tautology.
But, classically,
(\varphi\to\psi)\land(\mathop{\neg}\varphi\to\psi)\vdash\mathop{\neg}\psi\to\psi
.
Hence, by contraposition,
(\varphi\to\psi)\land(\mathop{\neg}\varphi\to\psi)
should be contradictory,
which seems counter-intuitive.
Now,
in
\mathrm{OL}
,
Aristotle’s thesis
\mathop{\neg}(\mathop{\neg}\psi\to\psi)
is indeed a tautology.
We also have, as in classical logic, that
(\varphi\to\psi)\land(\mathop{\neg}\varphi\to\psi)\vdash\mathop{\neg}\psi\to\psi

and

(\varphi\to\psi)\land(\mathop{\neg}\varphi\to\psi)
is satisfiable.

\mathrm{OL}
, however, rejects the contraposition rule
(
\alpha\vdash\beta
does not entail
\mathop{\neg}\beta\vdash\mathop{\neg}\alpha
), so

\mathop{\neg}(\mathop{\neg}\psi\to\psi)\not\vdash\mathop{\neg}((\varphi\to\psi)\land(\mathop{\neg}\varphi\to\psi))
, avoiding
the counter-intuitive consequence.
We shall not further
discuss the adequacy of
\mathrm{OL}
with respect to the proposed applications
(refer to
Cooper (1968)
), but we wish to draw attention
to some of its unusual features.

For one thing,
\mathrm{OL}
is not even a logic in the Tarskian sense, for its consequence relation is

non-structural
, i.e. not closed under uniform substitution.
This is witnessed, for instance, by the explosion principle, which
\mathrm{OL}
endorses for atomic formulas
(
p,\mathop{\neg}p\vdash q
) but not for arbitrary ones (
\varphi,\mathop{\neg}\varphi\not\vdash\psi
).
In consequence,
\mathrm{OL}
is not characterized by truth tables in the usual way; it can, however, be semantically characterized
by the three-valued matrix described in Figure
1
if we require valuations to assign only classical values
(
\mathbf{0}
or
\mathbf{1}
)
to the propositional variables.

Let us mention a few other unusual features of
\mathrm{OL}
, whose language
consists of a conjunction
(\land)
, a disjunction
(\lor)
, an implication
(\to)
and a negation
(\mathop{\neg})
.
The usual De Morgan laws relating conjunction and disjunction via negation hold, and indeed either
\land

or
\lor
could be omitted from the primitive signature.
However, neither the distributive nor the absorption laws between conjunction and disjunction hold.
Indeed,
\land
and
\lor

do not give rise to a lattice structure in the expected way (cf. Proposition
9
below); for this reason they have been called

quasi-conjunction
and
quasi-disjunction
in the literature (see
(
Égré et al., 2021a
, Sec. 6.3)
for a discussion of
their motivation). A novel contribution to an informal reading of these connectives may perhaps be based on our
algebraic analysis of
\mathrm{OL}
. As we shall see (Section
4
),
\mathrm{OL}
may be viewed as
an expansion of
Da Costa and D’Ottaviano’s logic
\mathcal{J}3
, which is in turn
an axiomatic extension of paraconsistent
Nelson logic. These are substructural logics based on residuated structures having both
a lattice (or
additive
) conjunction and a
multiplicative
conjunction;
on the algebraic models, the latter is realized by a monoid operation having (in our notation)

\nicefrac{{\mathbf{1}}}{{\mathbf{2}}}
as neutral element. This suggests that Cooper’s connective
\land
is perhaps best thought of as
a multiplicative conjunction having
\lor
as its De Morgan dual (defined by

x\lor y:=\mathop{\neg}(\mathop{\neg}x\land\mathop{\neg}y)
), the truncated sum of Łukasiewicz logic being another example of this kind of disjunction.

The behaviour of
\lor
in
\mathrm{OL}
is indeed peculiar, even in isolation.
In fact, the preceding observations entail that disjunction introduction is not a sound rule (in general, we have

\varphi\not\vdash\varphi\lor\psi
).
In many logics, another key feature of the disjunction is that the truth value of a formula

\varphi\lor\psi
is designated if and only if either
\varphi
or
\psi
is assigned a designated value. This does not hold
in
\mathrm{OL}
(one has, for instance
\nicefrac{{\mathbf{1}}}{{\mathbf{2}}}\lor\mathbf{0}=\mathbf{0}
). In consequence, a classical tautology such as
\varphi\lor(\varphi\to\psi)

is
contigent
in
\mathrm{OL}
, although every valuation assigns a designated value either to
\varphi
or to
\varphi\to\psi
.
The behaviour of
\to
within
\mathrm{OL}
appears to be more standard, at least in isolation.
Unlike the disjunction, the implication is not definable from the other connectives in any of the usual ways.
Indeed, it is easy to see
that

\to
is not definable at all in the language
\{\land,\lor,\mathop{\neg}\}
, for
the set
\{\mathbf{0},\mathbf{1}\}
is closed under all these operations but not under the implication.
On the other hand, the truth constants (all three of them)
are definable. For instance, one may let
\nicefrac{{\mathbf{1}}}{{\mathbf{2}}}:=\varphi\to(\mathop{\neg}\varphi\to\varphi)
,

\mathbf{1}:=p\lor\neg p
and

\mathbf{0}:=\mathop{\neg}\mathbf{1}

(here
p
needs to be atomic, while

\varphi
is arbitrary).

Lastly, observe that
\mathrm{OL}
is not only a paraconsistent (for
\varphi,\mathop{\neg}\varphi\not\vdash\psi
) but actually
a
contradictory
logic in H. Wansing’s sense
Wansing (forthcoming)
,
that is, there exists a formula (e.g.
\nicefrac{{\mathbf{1}}}{{\mathbf{2}}}
, defined as above)
such that both
\vdash\nicefrac{{\mathbf{1}}}{{\mathbf{2}}}
and
\vdash\mathop{\neg}\nicefrac{{\mathbf{1}}}{{\mathbf{2}}}
hold. This has the interesting consequence that
\mathrm{OL}

does not admit any non-trivial structural extension: if we were to close the consequence relation of
\mathrm{OL}
under uniform substitutions, then any formula
\varphi
would be valid,
being (by the explosion rule, now applicable to arbitrary formulas) a consequence of the set of valid formulas
\{\nicefrac{{\mathbf{1}}}{{\mathbf{2}}},\mathop{\neg}\nicefrac{{\mathbf{1}}}{{\mathbf{2}}}\}
.

We introduce
multiple- and single-conclusion Hilbert-style axiomatizations
for
\mathrm{OL}
and for its structural companion
\mathrm{sOL}
(Sections
3
),
thus filling the gap in the literature concerning a standard axiomatization
of
\mathrm{OL}
.
Our calculi are
modular (i.e., obtained by joining independent calculi over smaller signatures), easily allowing us to characterize a number of fragments of
\mathrm{OL}
/
\mathrm{sOL}
.
In the multiple-conclusion setting,
they are also analytic.
We obtain these axiomatizations
via the methods developed in
Shoesmith and Smiley (1978)
;
Caleiro and Marcelino (2019)
.
In Section
4
we give an alternative axiomatization for
\mathrm{sOL}

and we
prove that
\mathrm{sOL}
is algebraizable
with respect to the quasi-variety
\mathbb{OL}
(coinciding with the variety) generated
by the algebra
\textbf{{O}}_{3}
, the
algebraic reduct of Cooper’s three-valued
matrix.
\mathbb{OL}
turns out to be a discriminator variety
(Theorem
10
), making
\mathrm{sOL}
a nearly functionally complete logic
(adding to its language either the constant
\mathbf{1}
or
\mathbf{0}
makes it functionally complete).
Indeed, we show that
\mathrm{sOL}
is definitionally equivalent to an expansion of
Da Costa and D’Ottaviano’s three-valued logic
\mathcal{J}3
, which is in turn
an axiomatic extension of paraconsistent
Nelson logic.
The final Section
5
discusses potential future developments,
notably
a more extensive study of the algebraic counterpart of
\mathrm{sOL}
(especially in connection
with the other above-mentioned non-classical logics), and
the extension of the present approach to other logics definable from the truth tables of

\mathrm{OL}
(e.g., the algebraizable fragments of
\mathrm{sOL}
, logics
resulting from alternative choices of designated elements or those determined by
the definable orders of
\textbf{{O}}_{3}
).

Now we present definitions related to Hilbert-style calculi.
A
multiple-conclusion Hilbert calculus
over
\Sigma

\mathsf{R}

of
multiple-conclusion rules
of the form
\frac{\Phi}{\Psi}
,
where
\Phi,\Psi\subseteq L_{\Sigma}(P)
.
Here we also consider rules denoted by
\frac{\Phi}{\Psi}[\Pi]
for
\Pi\subseteq\mathsf{props}(\Phi\cup\Psi)
, to be able to axiomatize
non-substitution-invariant logics, as we shall soon explain.
We will often identify

\frac{\Phi}{\Psi}

with

\frac{\Phi}{\Psi}[\varnothing]
.
Rules with
\Pi\neq\varnothing

are referred to as

identity-instance rules
.
When writing rules, we usually omit curly braces from the set notation.
A
proof
of
(\Gamma,\Pi)
in
\mathsf{R}
is a finite directed rooted tree
where each node is
labelled either with a set of
formulas or with the discontinuation symbol
\star
, such that (i) the root is labelled with a superset of
\Gamma
; (ii) every leaf is labelled
either with a set having non-empty intersection with
\Pi
or with
\star
;
(iii) every non-leaf node has children determined by a substitution instance of a rule of inference of
\mathsf{R}
, in the way we now detail.
A rule instance
\mathsf{r}^{\sigma}
applies to a node
\mathsf{n}

when
the antecedent of
\mathsf{r}^{\sigma}
is contained in the label of
\mathsf{n}
;
the application results in
\mathsf{n}
having
exactly one child node for
each formula
\psi
in the succedent of
\mathsf{r}^{\sigma}
,
which is, in turn, labelled with the same formulas as those of (the label of)
\mathsf{n}

plus
\psi
. In case
\mathsf{r}^{\sigma}
has an empty
succedent, then
\mathsf{n}
has a single child node
labelled with
\star
.
If
\mathsf{r}
has the form
\frac{\Phi}{\Psi}
, then any substitution

\sigma
can be applied to instantiate it; if it is of the form
\frac{\Phi}{\Psi}[\Pi]
,
however,
only substitutions
\sigma
with

\sigma(p)=p
for each

may be applied.
When we display proof trees, it is common to write as labels only the
formulas introduced by the rule application, instead of the whole accumulated set of formulas.
Examples of multiple-conclusion proofs
may be found in Figure

We write
\Gamma\rhd_{\mathsf{R}}\Pi

(\Gamma,\Pi)

in
\mathsf{R}

.
These relations are respectively multiple-conclusion and single-conclusion consequence relations
that are substitution-invariant when no identity-instance rule is present in
\mathsf{R}
.
Single-conclusion Hilbert calculi are just the traditional Hilbert calculi, which
can be seen as multiple-conclusion calculi in which only rules of the form

\frac{\Phi}{\psi}
and
\frac{\Phi}{\psi}[\Pi]
are allowed,
where
\Phi\subseteq L_{\Sigma}(P)
and
\psi\in L_{\Sigma}(P)
.
Derivations then can be seen as linear trees, usually displayed simply as sequences of formulas.
We say that a Hilbert calculus
\mathsf{R}
(multiple- or single-conclusion)
axiomatizes

\rhd{}

if
\rhd{}=\rhd_{\mathsf{R}}
. It axiomatizes
\vdash
in case

\vdash\,=\,\vdash_{\mathsf{R}}
.

3.1
Multiple-conclusion

Every logic determined by a finite

Moreover, if the matrix is

analyticity and can be effectively generated from the matrix description
Caleiro and Marcelino (2019)

We now describe in more detail these notions.

A matrix

sometimes called a

derivations in which formulas in

and

\neg p

3.2
Single-conclusion

When a suitable connective is available, a multiple-conclusion calculus may be effectively translated into a
single-conclusion calculus for its single-conclusion companion
Shoesmith and Smiley (1978)
.
We now define what is a suitable connective
and the
calculi translations.

Definition 1
.

Let
\vdash
be a single-conclusion logic over
L_{\Sigma}(P)
and
©
be a derived connective in
L_{\Sigma}(P)
.
Then
©
is

1.

a
disjunction
in
\vdash
whenever
\Gamma,\varphi\,\textup{\copyright}\,\psi\vdash\gamma
iff
\Gamma,\varphi\vdash\gamma
and
\Gamma,\psi\vdash\gamma
,
for all
\Gamma\cup\{\varphi,\psi,\gamma\}\subseteq L_{\Sigma}(P)
.

2.

an
implication
in
\vdash
whenever

\Gamma\vdash\varphi\,\textup{\copyright}\,\psi

iff

\Gamma,\varphi\vdash\psi
,
for all
\Gamma\cup\{\varphi,\psi\}\subseteq L_{\Sigma}(P)
.

Note that, in standard terminology,
an implication in
\vdash
is a binary connective
satisfying the Deduction-Detachment Theorem (DDT).
In what follows, given a set of formulas
\Phi

and a binary connective
\oplus
, let
\Phi\oplus\psi:=\{\varphi\oplus\psi\mid\varphi\in\Phi\}

and
\bigoplus\{\varphi_{1},\ldots,\varphi_{m}\}:=\varphi_{1}\oplus(\varphi_{2}\oplus\ldots(\ldots\oplus\varphi_{n})\ldots)
.

Definition 2
.

Let
\mathsf{R}
be
a multiple-conclusion calculus
and
\oplus
be a binary connective.
We define
\mathsf{R}^{\oplus}
as the
single-conclusion calculus

\left\{\frac{p\oplus p}{p},\frac{p}{p\oplus q},\frac{p\oplus q}{q\oplus p},\frac{p\oplus(q\oplus r)}{(p\oplus q)\oplus r}\right\}\cup\left\{\mathsf{r}^{\oplus}\mid\mathsf{r}\in\mathsf{R}\right\}

where
\mathsf{r}^{\oplus}

is
\frac{\varnothing}{\bigoplus\Psi}[\Pi]

if
\mathsf{r}=\frac{\varnothing}{\Psi}[\Pi]
,
\frac{\Phi\oplus p_{0}}{(\bigoplus\Psi)\oplus p_{0}}[\Pi]

if
\mathsf{r}=\frac{\Phi}{\Psi}[\Pi]
,
and
\frac{\Phi\oplus p_{0}}{p_{0}}[\Pi]

if
\mathsf{r}=\frac{\Phi}{\varnothing}[\Pi]
,
for
p_{0}
a
propositional variable
not occurring in
\mathsf{r}
.

Now we move to translations when an implication is present.
Let
p_{0}
be a propositional variable not occurring in

\varphi_{1},\ldots,\varphi_{m},\psi_{1},\ldots,\psi_{n}

and
\rightarrowtriangle
be a binary connective.
Define

\{\varphi_{1},\ldots,\varphi_{m}\}\rightarrowtriangle\{\psi_{1},\ldots,\psi_{n}\}:=\varphi_{1}\rightarrowtriangle(\{\varphi_{2},\ldots,\varphi_{m}\}\rightarrowtriangle\{\psi_{1},\ldots,\psi_{n}\})

\varnothing\rightarrowtriangle\{\psi_{1},\ldots,\psi_{n}\}:=(\psi_{1}\rightarrowtriangle p_{0})\rightarrowtriangle(\varnothing\rightarrowtriangle\{\psi_{2},\ldots,\psi_{n}\})

\varnothing\rightarrowtriangle\varnothing:=p_{0}

For example,
\{p,q\}\rightarrowtriangle\{p\land q\}=p\rightarrowtriangle(q\rightarrowtriangle(((p\land q)\rightarrowtriangle p_{0})\rightarrowtriangle p_{0}))

Definition 3
.

Let
\mathsf{R}
be
a multiple-conclusion calculus
and
\rightarrowtriangle
be a binary connective.
We define
\mathsf{R}^{\rightarrowtriangle}

single-conclusion calculus containing
all rules and axioms of intuitionistic implication (where
\rightarrowtriangle

\frac{}{\{\varphi_{1},\ldots,\varphi_{m}\}\rightarrowtriangle\{\psi_{1},\ldots,\psi_{n}\}}[\Pi]

\mathsf{R}

We denote by

Algebraizability of
\mathrm{sOL}
.

The matrix semantics
of
\mathrm{sOL}

makes
it easy to
check that
\mathrm{sOL}
is algebraizable in the sense of Blok and Pigozzi
Blok and Pigozzi (1989)
.
In what follows,
abbreviate

\left\lvert\alpha\right\rvert:=\alpha\Rightarrow\alpha
.

Theorem 8
.

\mathrm{sOL}

is
algebraizable with translations

\tau\colon x\mapsto x\approx\left\lvert x\right\rvert

and

\rho\colon\varphi\approx\psi\mapsto\{\varphi\Rightarrow\psi,\psi\Rightarrow\varphi\}
. (Alternatively, one may take

\tau\colon x\mapsto x\approx x\to x

and

\rho\colon\varphi\approx\psi\mapsto\{\varphi\to\psi,\psi\to\varphi,\mathop{\neg}\varphi\to\mathop{\neg}\psi,\mathop{\neg}\psi\to\mathop{\neg}\varphi\}

Proof.

Observe (using Figure
2
) that a valuation
v
over the matrix of
\mathrm{sOL}
satisfies
an equation
\varphi\approx\varphi\Rightarrow\varphi

iff
v(\varphi)\in\{\nicefrac{{\mathbf{1}}}{{\mathbf{2}}},\mathbf{1}\}
.
Thus,
for all formulas
\Gamma,\varphi
,
we have

\Gamma\vdash_{\mathrm{sOL}}\varphi
iff
\tau(\Gamma)\vDash_{\mathbb{OL}}\tau(\varphi)
.
This is condition (ALG1) of algebraizability
(
Font, 2016
, Def. 3.11)
.
To establish algebraizability, it remains to prove (ALG4), i.e., that every equation
\varphi\approx\psi

is inter-derivable in
\vDash_{\mathbb{OL}}
with
\tau(\rho(\varphi\approx\psi))
, which is the set

\{\varphi\Rightarrow\psi\approx\left\lvert\varphi\Rightarrow\psi\right\rvert,\psi\Rightarrow\varphi\approx\left\lvert\psi\Rightarrow\varphi\right\rvert\}
.
This is easily verified in
\textbf{{O}}_{3}
.

1.

V(\textbf{{O}}_{3})
is both congruence-distributive and congruence-permutable (i.e., it is an arithmetical variety).

2.

V(\textbf{{O}}_{3})=\mathbb{OL}
.

3.

\textbf{{O}}_{3}
is quasi-primal, hence
\mathbb{OL}

is a discriminator variety.

4.

If we add either
\mathbf{0}
or
\mathbf{1}
as a constant to
\textbf{{O}}_{3}
, then the latter becomes a primal algebra
(where every
n
-ary function for
n\geq 1
is representable by a term).

Proof.

1. Congruence-distributivity follows from the observation that
\textbf{{O}}_{3}
has a term-definable
lattice structure (item (iii) of Proposition
9
). Congruence-permutability is witnessed by the Maltsev term
p(x,y,z)
defined as follows (cf.
(
Rivieccio, 2022
, Thm. 4.10)
):

p(x,y,z):=(((x\Rightarrow y)\sqcap(z\Rightarrow z))\Rightarrow z)\sqcap(((z\Rightarrow y)\sqcap(x\Rightarrow x))\Rightarrow x)
.

2. Since
V(\textbf{{O}}_{3})
is congruence-distributive, by item (iii) of
(
Clark and Davey, 1998
, Thm. 3.6)
) it suffices to verify
that
HS(\textbf{{O}}_{3})\subseteq IS(\textbf{{O}}_{3})
, which is very easy (
\textbf{{O}}_{3}
has only one proper subalgebra with
\{\nicefrac{{\mathbf{1}}}{{\mathbf{2}}}\}
as universe, and no non-trivial homomorphic images).

3. Taking into account item (i) and the fact that
\textbf{{O}}_{3}
is hereditarily simple, apply Pixley’s characterization
(
Burris and Sankappanavar, 2011
, Thm. IV.10.7)
to conclude that
\textbf{{O}}_{3}
is quasiprimal.

4. If we further add one of the non-definable constants (
\mathbf{0}
or
\mathbf{1}
) to
\textbf{{O}}_{3}
, then by
(
Burris and Sankappanavar, 2011
, Cor. 10.8)

we obtain a primal algebra.
∎

Axiomatizing
\mathbb{OL}
.

The algebraizability result (Theorem
8
) can be used to obtain a presentation of the quasi-variety

\mathbb{OL}
in the standard way
(see
(
Font, 2016
, Prop. 3.44)
), as well as of the subreducts of
\mathbb{OL}

corresponding to algebraizable fragments of
\mathrm{sOL}
(e.g. those capable of expressing either
\Rightarrow
or
\to
and
\neg
,
relying on the axiomatizations
obtained in Theorems
5
–
7
).
Moreover, one may obtain a more standard Hilbert presentation for

\mathrm{sOL}

by
directly proving that the logic determined by the following calculus is
algebraizable (with the same translations
\tau,\rho
of Theorem
8
), and that its equivalent semantics is

\mathbb{OL}
. This is straightforward, but requires a number of derivations that we
omit due to space limitations.
The calculus is given by the
following axiom schemata, with
modus ponens

(from
\varphi
and
\varphi\to\psi
infer
\psi
)
as the only inference rule
(we abbreviate
\alpha\leftrightarrow\beta:=(\alpha\to\beta)\land(\beta\to\alpha)
):

(HOL1)

\varphi\to(\psi\to\varphi)

(HOL2)

(\varphi\to(\psi\to\gamma))\to((\varphi\to\psi)\to(\varphi\to\gamma))

(HOL3)

((\varphi\to\psi)\to\varphi)\to\varphi

(HOL4)

(\varphi\land\psi)\to\varphi

(HOL5)

(\varphi\land\psi)\to\psi

(HOL6)

(\varphi\to\psi)\to((\varphi\to\gamma)\to(\varphi\to(\psi\land\gamma)))

(HOL7)

\mathop{\neg}\mathop{\neg}\varphi\leftrightarrow\varphi

(HOL8)

(\varphi\to\mathop{\neg}\varphi)\to\mathop{\neg}\varphi

(HOL9)

((\varphi\to\mathop{\neg}\psi)\land(\psi\to\mathop{\neg}\varphi))\leftrightarrow\mathop{\neg}(\varphi\land\psi)
.

(HOL10)

\mathop{\neg}(\varphi\to\psi)\leftrightarrow(\varphi\to\mathop{\neg}\psi)

(The ‘H’ in (HOL
1
) etc. refers to ‘Hilbert’.)
We note that the only non-classically valid scheme is (HOL
10
), while
(HOL
1
)–(HOL
6
)
constitute, with
modus ponens
, an axiomatization of the conjunction-implication fragment of classical logic.
This entails that every classical tautology in this language is derivable in HOL.
Also observe that, since
modus ponens
is the only rule of inference,
axioms (HOL
1
) and (HOL
2
) give us that
\to
satisfies the DDT (see Definition
1
(2)).

For a quasi-equational presentation of
\mathbb{OL}
, thus, we may employ the following
quasi-equations
(cf.
(
Font, 2016
, Prop. 3.44)
):

1.

\alpha\approx\left\lvert\alpha\right\rvert
for each axiom
\alpha
in (HOL
1
)–(HOL
9
),

2.

if
\alpha\approx\left\lvert\alpha\right\rvert
and
\alpha\to\beta\approx\left\lvert\alpha\to\beta\right\rvert
,
then
\beta\approx\left\lvert\beta\right\rvert
,

3.

if
\alpha\Rightarrow\beta\approx\left\lvert\alpha\Rightarrow\beta\right\rvert
and

\beta\Rightarrow\alpha\approx\left\lvert\beta\Rightarrow\alpha\right\rvert
,
then
\alpha\approx\beta
.

5
Future work

Having axiomatized
the logic of ordinary discourse
\mathrm{OL}
and
investigated the logico-algebraic features of
its structural counterpart (
\mathrm{sOL}
),
we believe to have contributed to the advancement of
the study of
connexive (multiple- and single-conclusion) logics.

We view the present study as yet another vindication of the usefulness of multiple-conclusion
calculi in the study of finite-valued logics.
Beyond the results presented here,
we speculate that the following directions may prove fruitful in future research.

1. Due to space limitations, the algebraic aspects of
\mathrm{sOL}
have been touched only sketchily in the present paper. We reserve a more comprehensive study – including a proof of algebraizability of the Hilbert calculus introduced in Section
4
, a more perspicuous presentation of the variety
\mathbb{OL}
, etc. – to a future publication
Rivieccio (In preparation)
.

2.
The papers
Égré et al. (2021a)
;
Égré et al. (2021b)
by P. Egré
et al
. contain an extensive discussion
of three-valued logics that model conditionals in natural language.
Among other systems, the authors consider
a variant of
\mathrm{sOL}
(denoted CC/TT) that employs the implication
\to
together with the
connectives
\sqcap,\sqcup
instead of the primitive conjunction and disjunction of
\mathrm{sOL}
.
Present space limitations do not allow us to compare in detail our approach with that of Egré
et al
., so
this issue too will have to be left for future research. However, we may anticipate that our algebraic
analysis of
\mathrm{sOL}
throws some light on the observations of
(
Égré et al., 2021b
, Sec. 4)
, in particular
the fact that a translation
\rho\colon\varphi\approx\psi\mapsto\{\varphi\to\psi,\psi\to\varphi\}
does not guarantee algebraizability, either for
\mathrm{sOL}
or for CC/TT (cf.
(
Égré et al., 2021b
, Lemma 4.18)
). On the other hand, it is easy to see that the translation considered in Section
4
, namely

\rho\colon\varphi\approx\psi\mapsto\{\varphi\to\psi,\psi\to\varphi,\mathop{\neg}\varphi\to\mathop{\neg}\psi,\mathop{\neg}\psi\to\mathop{\neg}\varphi\}
, guarantees algebraization for both logics, thereby settling the problem
left open in
Égré et al. (2021b)
.

3. As mentioned earlier,
\mathrm{sOL}
is definitionally equivalent to an expansion of
Da Costa and D’Ottaviano’s logic
\mathcal{J}3
, which is in turn
an axiomatic extension of paraconsistent
Nelson logic. This suggests that
\mathbb{OL}
may be viewed as a subvariety of N4-lattices and, as such,
its members may be given a twist-structure representation. Developing such a study may not only provide
further insight into
\mathbb{OL}
, but also clarify the relationship between
\mathrm{sOL}

and other related non-classical systems, such as
the connexive logic C
Wansing (2005)
.

4. It might be interesting to develop a study similar to the present one for other logics defined from the algebra
\textbf{{O}}_{3}

with different sets of designated values, e.g.
\{\mathbf{1}\}
or
\{\nicefrac{{\mathbf{1}}}{{\mathbf{2}}}\}
, or the order-preserving logics associated to the orderings naturally arising on
\textbf{{O}}_{3}

(cf. Proposition
9
). An obviously different but related question is whether any of these
systems admit an interpretation in line with Cooper’s original proposal of formalizing
reasoning in ordinary discourse.

5. An algebraic study of the (term-definable) fragments of
\mathrm{sOL}

axiomatized in Section
2
also appears to be promising. Some of these – such as the
\{\to,\mathop{\neg}\}
-fragment and the
\{\land,\mathop{\neg}\}
-fragment –
are algebraizable, suggesting that they may be easily treatable with algebraic methods. The
\{\Rightarrow\}
-fragment, not considered here, is also easily seen to be algebraizable, and may be axiomatized by
the methods employed in the present study.
By
Shoesmith and Smiley (1978)
,
every multiple-conclusion finite-valued logic is axiomatized by a finite multiple-conclusion calculus;
given that
\curlyvee
was defined using only
\Rightarrow
, a single-conclusion calculus for this fragment
exists by Theorem
4
.
