Algebraic Bethe Ansatz for the XXZ Gaudin Models with Generic Boundary
Nicolas CRAMPE

Abstract
We solve the XXZ Gaudin model with generic boundary using the modified algebraic Bethe ansatz. The diagonal and triangular cases have been recovered in this general framework. We show that the model for odd or even lengths has two different behaviors. The corresponding Bethe equations are computed for all the cases. For the chain with even length, inhomogeneous Bethe equations are necessary. The higher spin Gaudin models with generic boundary is also treated.

2
Gaudin models and algebras

In this section, we recall different well-known notions to construct Gaudin models and prove their integrability.

For a matrix
r(x,y)\in\operatorname{End}(\mathbb{C}^{N}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{N})
depending on 2 parameters
x,y
, one defines the fundamental equation, called classical Yang–Baxter equation, given by

\displaystyle[{r}_{13}(x_{1},x_{3}),{r}_{23}(x_{2},x_{3})]=[{r}_{21}(x_{2},x_{1}),{r}_{13}(x_{1},x_{3})]+[{r}_{23}(x_{2},x_{3}),{r}_{12}(x_{1},x_{2})].

In the previous relation, we have used the usual notations:
r_{12}(x)=r(x)\otimes\mathbb{I}
,
r_{23}(x)=\mathbb{I}\otimes r(x)
, …,
where
\mathbb{I}
is the identity matrix. A solution of the classical Yang–Baxter equation is called a
r
-matrix.
In addition, if an
r
-matrix
r(x,y)
depends only on the quotient
x/y
and satisfies the supplementary relations
r_{12}(x,y)=r_{12}(x/y)=-r_{21}(y/x)=-r_{12}(y/x)^{t_{1}t_{2}}

then it is called a skew-symmetric
r
-matrix.
For a given skew-symmetric
r
-matrix
r(x/y)
, one defines also the reflection equation

\displaystyle r_{12}(x/y)k_{1}(x)k_{2}(y)-k_{1}(x)k_{2}(y)r_{21}(x/y)=k_{2}(y)r_{12}(xy)k_{1}(x)-k_{1}(x)r_{21}(xy)k_{2}(y).

A solution
k(x)
of the reflection equation is called
k
-matrix.
From a skew-symmetric
r
-matrix and an associated
k
-matrix, we can construct a new
r
-matrix
[
40
]

\displaystyle\overline{r}_{12}(x,y)=r_{12}(x/y)-k_{1}(x)r_{12}(1/(xy))k_{1}(x)^{-1},

(2.1)

which is not skew-symmetric.

To each
r
-matrix
r(x,y)
, on can associate a Lie algebra
\mathscr{R}
.
Indeed, if
{\mathscr{K}}(x)\in\operatorname{End}\big(\mathbb{C}^{N}\big)\otimes\mathscr{R}
, then the following relation

\displaystyle[{\mathscr{K}}_{1}(x),{\mathscr{K}}_{2}(y)]=[{r}_{21}(y,x),{\mathscr{K}}_{1}(x)]+[{\mathscr{K}}_{2}(y),{r}_{12}(x,y)]

(2.2)

defines a Lie commutator for
\mathscr{R}
. Indeed, the antisymmetry of the product is obvious and the Jacobi identity is satisfied due to the classical Yang–Baxter equation. For any invertible matrix
M(x)\in\operatorname{End}\big(\mathbb{C}^{N}\big)
, there is a Lie algebra isomorphism given by

\displaystyle\Phi_{M}\colon\quad\quad\mathscr{R}

\displaystyle\rightarrow\mathscr{R}_{M},

\displaystyle{\mathscr{K}_{0}}(x)

\displaystyle\mapsto M_{0}(x)^{-1}\mathscr{K}_{0}(x)M_{0}(x),

where
\mathscr{R}_{M}
is the Lie algebra defined by (
2.2
) with the
r
-matrix

\displaystyle M_{1}(x)^{-1}M_{2}(y)^{-1}r_{12}(x,y)M_{1}(x)M_{2}(y).

From the defining relations (
2.2
), one can show that the transfer matrix

\displaystyle t(x)=\frac{1}{4}\operatorname{tr}_{0}\big({\mathscr{K}_{0}}(x)^{2}\big)

(2.3)

satisfies
[t(x),t(y)]=0
. Then, when a representation for
\mathscr{R}
is chosen, the coefficients of
t(x)
provide an integrable hierarchy.
Let us remark that, for a given matrix
M(x)
,
\mathscr{R}
and
\mathscr{R}_{M}
give the same hierarchy since it is easy to see that
\operatorname{tr}\big({\mathscr{K}_{0}}(x)^{2}\big)=\operatorname{tr}\big({\Phi_{M}(\mathscr{K}_{0}}(x))^{2}\big)
.

In this letter, we focus on the skew-symmetric
r
-matrix associated to the affine Kac–Moody algebra
\widehat{{\mathfrak{sl}}_{2}}
:

\displaystyle r(x)=\frac{1}{x-1}\begin{pmatrix}-\frac{1}{2}(x+1)&0&0&0\\
0&\frac{1}{2}(x+1)&-2&0\\
0&-2x&\frac{1}{2}(x+1)&0\\
0&0&0&-\frac{1}{2}(x+1)\end{pmatrix}.

(2.4)

The most general
k
-matrix associated to this
r
-matrix is given by

\displaystyle k(x)=\begin{pmatrix}\beta+\gamma/x&-\frac{\alpha(\beta+\rho)}{2}(x-1/x)\\
\frac{\beta-\rho}{2\alpha}(x-1/x)&\beta+\gamma x&\end{pmatrix},

(2.5)

where
\alpha
,
\beta
,
\gamma
and
\rho
are scalar parameters. As explained previously,
the
r
-matrix and the
k
-matrix allow us to construct a new
r
-matrix (see relation (
2.1
)).
Without loss of generality, we can use the Lie algebra isomorphism
\Phi_{M}
to transform this
r
-matrix.
Therefore, we use in this paper the following
r
-matrix

\displaystyle\widetilde{r}_{12}(x,y)=M_{1}(x)^{-1}M_{2}(y)^{-1}\big(r_{12}(x/y)-k_{1}(x)r_{12}(1/(xy))k_{1}(x)^{-1}\big)M_{1}(x)M_{2}(y),

(2.6)

where
r(x)
is given by (
2.4
),
k(x)
is given by (
2.5
) and

\displaystyle M(x)=\begin{pmatrix}\frac{\beta+\rho}{2\rho}&\frac{\alpha}{x}\\
\frac{(\beta-\rho)x}{2\alpha\rho}&1\end{pmatrix}.

Explicitly, this
r
-matrix reads as

\displaystyle\widetilde{r}(x,y)=\begin{pmatrix}-\frac{1}{2}\omega(x,y)\sigma^{z}&b(x)\sigma^{z}+f(y,x)\sigma^{-}\\
c(x)\sigma^{z}-f(1/y,1/x)\sigma^{+}&\frac{1}{2}\omega(x,y)\sigma^{z}\end{pmatrix},

where
\sigma^{z}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&-1\end{pmatrix}
,
\sigma^{+}=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}
and
\sigma^{-}=\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}
and

\displaystyle\delta(x)=\frac{((\beta-\rho)x^{2}+2\gamma x+\rho+\beta)}{2\rho(x-1/x)},\qquad b(x)=-\frac{\alpha}{\delta(x)},\qquad c(x)=\frac{\rho^{2}-\beta^{2}}{4\alpha\rho^{2}\delta(1/x)},

(2.7)

\displaystyle\omega(x,y)=\frac{x+y}{x-y}+\frac{xy+1}{xy-1}\qquad\text{and}\qquad f(x,y)=\omega(x,y)\frac{\delta(x)}{\delta(y)}.

(2.8)

Let us emphasize that the
r
-matrix
\widetilde{r}(x,y)
has a simpler form than the one without the isomorphism
(i.e.,
r_{12}(x/y)-k_{1}(x)r_{12}(1/(xy))k_{1}(x)^{-1}
). Without the isomorphism, the 16 entries of the
r
-matrix should be non-zero whereas

\widetilde{r}(x,y)
has 6 vanishing entries and each two by two block submatrices are diagonal or triangular.

As explained previously, the
r
-matrix
\widetilde{r}(x,y)
allows one to define a Lie algebra by using relation (
2.2
) with

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathscr{K}_{0}}(x)=\begin{pmatrix}A(x)&\widetilde{B}(x)\\
\widetilde{C}(x)&-A(x)\end{pmatrix}.

(2.9)

We denote by
\widetilde{\mathscr{R}}
this Lie algebra.
Let us mention that the Lie algebra
\widetilde{\mathscr{R}}
for special choice of the parameters in the
k
-matrix has been
identified as Onsager algebras
[
3

r_{12}(x)^{t_{1}}=\sigma^{y}_{1}r_{12}(x)\sigma^{y}_{1}

Finally, let us introduce the following representation

We keep the same notation
\widetilde{\mathscr{K}}(x)

for the algebraic element or its representation.

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathscr{K}}_{0}(x)=\sum_{j=1}^{L}\widetilde{r}_{0j}(x,v_{j}).

(2.10)

By using the classical Yang–Baxter equation, it is obvious to show that (
2.10
) satisfies the commutation relations of
\widetilde{\mathscr{R}}
.
The parameters

are called inhomogeneous parameters.
The Hamiltonians are usually defined by

\displaystyle\widetilde{H}_{j}=\operatorname{Res}_{x=v_{j}}t(x)=\frac{1}{4}\operatorname{Res}_{x=v_{j}}\operatorname{tr}_{0}\big({\widetilde{\mathscr{K}_{0}}}(x)^{2}\big)

\displaystyle\hphantom{\widetilde{H}_j}{}=-v_{j}\sum_{\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{1}{p=1}{p\neq j}}^{L}\widetilde{r}_{jp}(v_{j},v_{p})+Lv_{j}\left(\frac{3v_{j}^{2}}{v_{j}^{2}-1}-\nu(v_{j})\right),

where

\displaystyle\nu(z)=\frac{z^{2}(\rho^{2}-\beta^{2})-2\beta\gamma z+\rho^{2}-\beta^{2}}{2\rho^{2}(z^{2}-1)\delta(z)\delta(1/z)}.

(2.11)

These Hamiltonians are integrable due to the commutation of the transfer matrix and

=0
. They are related to the Gaudin models with boundary (or also called BC Gaudin models). Indeed, by conjugating the previous Hamilonians by

which are the usual form for the XXZ Gaudin model with boundary.

For the generic case, the situation is more complicated. Here, we use the modified algebraic Bethe ansatz,

3.1
Algebraic relations

In this subsection, by using the commutation relations obtained from (
2.2
), we provide necessary propositions to
perform the algebraic Bethe ansatz.
Firstly, let us mention that the commutation relations between
A(x)
,
\widetilde{B}(x)
and
\widetilde{C}(x)
given by (
2.2
)
with the
r
-matrix (
2.6
) and

\widetilde{\mathscr{K}}(x)
given by (
2.9
) are quite complicated in comparison to the usual ones.
This is why we introduce the two following shifted generators

\displaystyle{B}(x,n)=\widetilde{B}(x)-(2n-1)b(x)\qquad\text{and}\qquad{C}(x,n)=\widetilde{C}(x)-(2n-1)c(x),

where
b(x)
and
c(x)
are defined by (
2.7
). For these generators and the generators
A(x)
, the commutation relations become

\displaystyle{A}(x){A}(y)={A}(y){A}(x),

(3.1)

\displaystyle{B}(x,n){B}(y,n+1)={B}(y,n){B}(x,n+1),

(3.2)

\displaystyle{C}(x,n){C}(y,n-1)={C}(y,n){C}(x,n-1),

(3.3)

\displaystyle{A}(x){B}(y,m)={B}(y,m){A}(x)+\omega(x,y){B}(y,m)-f(x,y){B}(x,m),

(3.4)

\displaystyle{A}(x){C}(y,m)={C}(y,m){A}(x)-\omega(x,y){C}(y,m)-f(1/x,1/y){C}(x,m),

(3.5)

\displaystyle{C}(x,n){B}(y,n)={B}(y,n+1){C}(x,n+1)

\displaystyle\hphantom{{C}(x,n){B}(y,n)=}{}+2f(x,y){A}(x)-2f(1/y,1/x){A}(y)-8nb(y)c(x),

(3.6)

where
\omega(x,y)
and
f(x,y)
are defined by (
2.8
).

We introduce also a shifted transfer matrix

\displaystyle{t}(x,j)=\frac{1}{2}{A}(x)^{2}+\frac{1}{4}{B}(x,j+1){C}(x,j+1)+\frac{1}{4}{C}(x,j){B}(x,j)-\frac{1}{2}b(x)c(x).

(3.7)

For
j=0
in the previous relation, the transfer matrix (
2.3
) is recovered:
t(x)=\frac{1}{4}\operatorname{tr}_{0}\widetilde{\mathscr{K}_{0}}(x)^{2}={t}(x,0)
.

Secondly, we present a lemma permitting to commute the shifted transfer matrix with the generator
B(x,p)
.
This lemma is standard in the context of the algebraic Bethe ansatz for the Gaudin model except for the
modification of the shift for the transfer matrix.

Lemma 3.1
.

If the algebraic relations (
3.1
)–(
3.6
) are satisfied, the following relation holds

\displaystyle{t}(x,p-1){B}(z,p)-{B}(z,p){t}(x,p)={B}(z,p)\big(\omega(x,z)(A(x)+\nu(x))+2b(x)c(x)\big)

\displaystyle\qquad{}-{B}(x,p)\big(f(1/z,1/x)(A(z)+\nu(z))+4(p-1)c(x)b(z)\big),

(3.8)

where
\nu(z)
is defined by (
2.11
).

Proof.

By straightforward computations using the commutation relations (
3.1
)–(
3.6
), one gets

\displaystyle{t}(x,p-1){B}(z,p)-{B}(z,p){t}(x,p)=\omega(x,z){B}(z,p){A}(x)-f(1/z,1/x){B}(x,p){A}(z)

\displaystyle\qquad{}+\frac{1}{2}\big(\omega(x,z)^{2}+f(z,x)f(1/z,1/x)\big){B}(z,p)

\displaystyle\qquad{}-\frac{1}{2}\big(\omega(z,x)f(1/z,1/x)+\omega(x,z)f(x,z)+4(2p-1)c(x)b(z)\big){B}(x,p).

Then, by using the following functional relations

\displaystyle\omega(x,z)^{2}+f(z,x)f(1/z,1/x)=2\nu(x)\omega(x,z)+4b(x)c(x),

\displaystyle\omega(z,x)f(1/z,1/x)+\omega(x,z)f(x,z)=2\nu(z)f(1/z,1/x)-4c(x)b(z)

one gets relation (
3.8
).
∎

Let us define the following product

\displaystyle\mathbb{B}(\boldsymbol{z})={B}(z_{1},1){B}(z_{2},2)\cdots{B}(z_{M},M),

where
\boldsymbol{z}=\{z_{1},z_{2},\dots,z_{M}\}
.
Let us remark that, due to relation (
3.2
),
\mathbb{B}(\boldsymbol{z})
is invariant under any permutation of the
z_{i}
which justifies
the definition of
\boldsymbol{z}
as a set.
One introduces also, for
k=1,2,\dots,M

\displaystyle\mathbb{B}(\boldsymbol{z}_{k},x)={B}(z_{1},1)\cdots{B}(z_{k-1},k-1){B}(x,k){B}(z_{k+1},k+1)\cdots{B}(z_{M},M).

Finally, we are in position to compute the action of the transfer matrix with the product
\mathbb{B}(\boldsymbol{z})
:

Proposition 3.2
.

If the algebraic relations (
3.1
)–(
3.6
) are satisfied, the following relation holds

\displaystyle t(x)\mathbb{B}(\boldsymbol{z})=\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{B}(\boldsymbol{z})B(x,M+1)C(x,M+1)

\displaystyle\hphantom{t(x)\mathbb{B}(\boldsymbol{z}) =}{}+\mathbb{B}(\boldsymbol{z})\left(\Lambda(x)+\sum_{p=1}^{M}\omega(x,z_{p})\left(A(x)+\nu(x)+\sum_{q\neq p}\frac{(x-1/x)\omega(z_{p},z_{q})}{z_{p}-1/z_{p}}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\hphantom{t(x)\mathbb{B}(\boldsymbol{z}) =}{}-\sum_{p=1}^{M}\mathbb{B}(\boldsymbol{z}_{p},x)f(1/z_{p},1/x)\left(A(z_{p})+\nu(z_{p})+\sum_{q\neq p}\omega(z_{p},z_{q})\right),

where

\displaystyle\Lambda(x)=\frac{1}{2}A(x)^{2}+xA^{\prime}(x)+\nu(x)A(x)-\frac{x^{2}+1}{x^{2}-1}A(x)-\frac{1}{2}b(x)c(x).

Proof.

One gets, using a telescopic sum, the following relation

\displaystyle{t}(x,0)\mathbb{B}(\boldsymbol{z})-\mathbb{B}(\boldsymbol{z}){t}(x,M)=\sum_{p=1}^{M}{B}_{1}\cdots{B}_{p-1}\left({t}(x,p-1){B}_{p}-{B}_{p}{t}(x,p)\right){B}_{p+1}\cdots{B}_{M},

where
{B}_{p}={B}(z_{p},p)
. Then, using the Lemma
3.1
, we express
{t}(x,p-1){B}_{p}-{B}_{p}{t}(x,p)

in the previous sum.
Then, we use repetitively relation (
3.4
) to put the operators
A
on the right.
Then, we rearrange the terms using the following relations

\displaystyle\omega(x,z_{p})f(x,z_{q})-f(1/z_{p},1/x)f(z_{p},z_{q})=\omega(z_{q},z_{p})f(1/z_{q},1/x)-4c(x)b(z_{q}),

\displaystyle\sum_{q>p}\omega(x,z_{p})\omega(x,z_{q})=\sum_{q\neq p}\frac{x-1/x}{z_{p}-1/z_{p}}\omega(z_{p},z_{q})\omega(x,z_{p}).

Finally,
{t}(x,M)
is simplified using relation (
3.7
) and (
3.6
) for
y\rightarrow x
to prove
the proposition.
∎

Let us emphasize again that this type of computation and result are the same for the periodic or diagonal Gaudin model. The only difference lies on
the shift in the operators.

3.2
Explicit representation

Up to now, we have only used the commutation relations of the algebra to prove the previous results.
Now, we choose the representation for
\widetilde{\mathscr{K}}(x)
given by (
2.10
).
Therefore, we get explicitly

\displaystyle A(x)=-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{L}\omega(x,v_{j})\sigma^{z}_{j},

\displaystyle B(x,n)=(1-2n)b(x)+b(x)\sum_{j=1}^{L}\sigma^{z}_{j}+\sum_{j=1}^{L}f(v_{j},x)\sigma^{-}_{j},

(3.9)

\displaystyle C(x,n)=(1-2n)c(x)+c(x)\sum_{j=1}^{L}\sigma^{z}_{j}-\sum_{j=1}^{L}f(1/v_{j},1/x)\sigma^{+}_{j}.

Let us introduce the following vectors

\Omega=\begin{pmatrix}1\\
0\end{pmatrix}^{\otimes L}
, called usually pseudo-vacuum. We deduce that

\displaystyle A(x)\Omega=a(x)\Omega\qquad\text{and}\qquad C(x,n)\Omega=(L+1-2n)c(x)\Omega

(3.10)

with
a(x)=-\frac{1}{2}\sum\limits_{j=1}^{L}\omega(x,v_{j})
.

The Bethe vectors are defined as follows

\displaystyle\mathbb{V}(\boldsymbol{z})=\mathbb{B}(\boldsymbol{z})\Omega.

(3.11)

These Bethe vectors are of course invariant under any permutation of the
z_{i}
since
\mathbb{B}(\boldsymbol{z})
and
\mathbb{C}(\boldsymbol{z})
are invariant.
From the previous results (Proposition
3.2
and relations (
3.10
)), we get easily the action of the transfer matrix on these Bethe vectors

\displaystyle t(x)\mathbb{V}(\boldsymbol{z})=\frac{L-2M-1}{2}c(x)\mathbb{B}(\boldsymbol{z})B(x,M+1)\Omega

\displaystyle\hphantom{t(x)\mathbb{V}(\boldsymbol{z}) =}{}+\left(\lambda(x)+\sum_{p=1}^{M}\omega(x,z_{p})\left(a(x)+\nu(x)+\sum_{q\neq p}\frac{(x-1/x)\omega(z_{p},z_{q})}{z_{p}-1/z_{p}}\right)\right)\mathbb{V}(\boldsymbol{z})

\displaystyle\hphantom{t(x)\mathbb{V}(\boldsymbol{z}) =}{}-\sum_{p=1}^{M}f(1/z_{p},1/x)\left(a(z_{p})+\nu(z_{p})+\sum_{q\neq p}\omega(z_{p},z_{q})\right)\mathbb{V}(\boldsymbol{z}_{p},x),

(3.12)

where

\displaystyle\lambda(x)=\frac{1}{2}a(x)^{2}+xa^{\prime}(x)+\nu(x)a(x)-\frac{x^{2}+1}{x^{2}-1}a(x)-\frac{1}{2}b(x)c(x).

The second term in (
3.12
) is the wanted term (i.e., is proportional to the Bethe vector) and the third term is the unwanted term.
These terms are already present for the periodic or triangular case.
For the generic boundary, we must deal with the first term in the previous relation (
3.12
).
There are different possibilities:

•

c(x)=0
, i.e.,
\rho=\pm\beta
. This case corresponds to triangular (or diagonal) boundaries and the Bethe ansatz works as usual.
We get different sectors depending on the number
M
of excitations (see Section
3.3
).

•

The length of chain is odd and
M=\frac{L-1}{2}
. It corresponds to a case where the number of excitations is fixed. From relation (
3.12
), one gets only
the half of the spectrum. The other half is obtained starting from
t(x)\overline{\mathbb{V}}(\boldsymbol{z})
(see Section
3.4
).

•

The length of the chain is even. In this case, we must compute
\mathbb{B}(\boldsymbol{z})B(x,M+1)\Omega
.
It corresponds to the modified algebraic Bethe ansatz (see Section
3.5
).

At first sight, the difference of the behavior for the lengths odd or even seems strange. However, we can show that, in the case of odd length,
two subspaces of
(\mathbb{C}^{2})^{\otimes L}
are stabilized by the transfer matrix. More precisely, if we denote by
V_{J}

the eigenspace of the total spin
S^{z}=\frac{1}{2}\sum\limits_{j=1}^{L}\sigma^{z}_{j}

with eigenvalue
J
, one gets

\displaystyle t(x)V_{1/2}\oplus V_{3/2}\oplus\dots\oplus V_{L/2}\subset V_{1/2}\oplus V_{3/2}\oplus\dots\oplus V_{L/2},

\displaystyle t(x)V_{-1/2}\oplus V_{-3/2}\oplus\dots\oplus V_{-L/2}\subset V_{-1/2}\oplus V_{-3/2}\oplus\dots\oplus V_{-L/2}.

(3.13)

Then it is natural that the spectrum for odd chain splits into two sectors.
For the chain of even length, this feature disappears: there is no stable subspace.

3.3
Triangular and diagonal boundaries

In this subsection, we deal with the triangular boundary (i.e.,
\rho=\pm\beta
).
In this case, we can easily see that one gets, for
M=0,1,\dots,L
,

\displaystyle t(x)V_{L/2-M}\oplus V_{L/2-M+1}\oplus\cdots\oplus V_{L/2}\subset V_{L/2-M}\oplus V_{L/2-M+1}\oplus\cdots\oplus V_{L/2}.

For
M=0,1,\dots,L
, the Bethe vectors
\mathbb{V}(\boldsymbol{z})
defined by (
3.11
) is just in the sector

V_{L/2-M}\oplus\cdots\oplus V_{L/2}
and becomes an eigenvector of the transfer matrix if the
Bethe roots
z_{i}
satisfy the Bethe equations:

\displaystyle a(z_{p})+\nu(z_{p})+\sum_{\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{1}{q=1}{q\neq p}}^{M}\omega(z_{p},z_{q})=0\qquad\text{for}\quad p=1,2,\dots,M.

(3.14)

Explicitly, they read, for
p=1,\dots,M
,

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{L}\left(\frac{z_{p}+v_{j}}{z_{p}-v_{j}}+\frac{z_{p}v_{j}+1}{z_{p}v_{j}-1}\right)+\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\gamma z-\beta}{\gamma z+\beta}+\frac{\beta z-\gamma}{\beta z+\gamma}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad{}+\sum_{\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{1}{q=1}{q\neq p}}^{M}\left(\frac{z_{p}+z_{q}}{z_{p}-z_{q}}+\frac{z_{p}z_{q}+1}{z_{p}z_{q}-1}\right)=0.

(3.15)

The corresponding eigenvalue of the transfer matrix
t(x)=\frac{1}{4}\operatorname{tr}_{0}\big(\widetilde{\mathscr{K}}_{0}(x)^{2}\big)
is

\displaystyle\lambda(x)+\sum_{p=1}^{M}\omega(x,z_{p})\left(a(x)+\nu(x)+\sum_{\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{1}{q=1}{q\neq p}}^{M}\frac{(x-1/x)\omega(z_{p},z_{q})}{z_{p}-1/z_{p}}\right).

(3.16)

Starting from the triangular boundary, we recover the diagonal one for
\alpha=0
.
We see that the eigenvalue (
3.16
) and the Bethe equations (
3.15
)
do not depend on
\alpha
. Then, as shown previously for the Gaudin model
[
31
,
32
]

(or for other models
[
7
,
38
]
), the spectrum for triangular boundaries is similar to the one with diagonal boundaries.
Evidently, the Bethe eigenvectors depends on
\alpha
, via the function
b(x)
present in
B(x,j)
(see (
3.9
)).
In particular, the function
b(x)
vanishes for
\alpha=0
and the Bethe vectors

\mathbb{V}(\boldsymbol{z})
are now in the sector
V_{L/2-M}
which is consistent with

\displaystyle t(x)V_{L/2-M}\subset V_{L/2-M}.

The last equation means that the total spin is conserved by the transfer matrix in the case of diagonal boundary.

3.4
Generic boundaries for an odd chain

If the length of the chain is odd (i.e., there is an integer
\ell
such that
L=2\ell+1
), we can chose the number of excitations as
M=\frac{L-1}{2}=\ell

and the coefficient in front of
\mathbb{B}(\boldsymbol{z})B(x,M+1)\Omega
vanishes.
From the explicit expression (
3.9
) of
B(x,j)
, we see that

\displaystyle\mathbb{V}(\boldsymbol{z})\in V_{1/2}\oplus V_{3/2}\oplus\dots\oplus V_{L/2}\qquad\text{for}\quad M=\ell,

which is one of the subspaces stabilized by the transfer matrix, as explained previously.
Then, the eigenvalues for this sector are given by (
3.16
) with the Bethe equations (
3.14
) for
M=\ell
.

The spectrum and the associated eigenvectors for the other sector (
3.13
) are obtained from the Bethe vectors

\displaystyle\overline{\mathbb{V}}(\boldsymbol{z})={C}(z_{1},0){C}(z_{2},-1)\cdots{C}(z_{\ell},-\ell+1)\begin{pmatrix}0\\
1\end{pmatrix}^{\otimes L}.

It is easy to show that
\overline{\mathbb{V}}(\boldsymbol{z})
belongs to the second sector.
A relation similar to (
3.12
) by replacing
{\mathbb{V}}(\boldsymbol{z})
by
\overline{\mathbb{V}}(\boldsymbol{z})
can be proved.
Then, we show that, starting from
\overline{\mathbb{V}}(\boldsymbol{z})
, one gets exactly
the expression (
3.16
) for the eigenvalues and (
3.14
) for the Bethe equations.
Therefore, the spectrums in both sectors are the same.

Let us also mention that the separation into two sectors is not new in this context. Indeed, the complete spectrum of the XXZ spin chain with
certain constraints between the parameters of the boundaries is also obtained from two different Bethe vectors
[
42
]
.

3.5
Generic boundaries for an even chain

For a chain with an even length, we must deal with the term
\mathbb{B}(\boldsymbol{z})B(x,M+1)\Omega
.
For a generic
M
, this vector has no particular property but for
M=L
, special feature appears. This is the crucial point of
the modified algebraic Bethe ansatz.

Firstly, let us state the following lemma:

Lemma 3.3
.

The entries of the matrix

\displaystyle{\mathcal{M}}(z)=\prod_{q=1}^{\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{2}{\longrightarrow}{L}}\left(\frac{(z-v_{q})(z-1/v_{q})}{(z-z_{q})(z-1/z_{q})}B(z_{q},q)\right)

\displaystyle\hphantom{{\mathcal{M}}(z)=}{}\times\left(\frac{(z-1/z)}{(z-x)(z-1/x)}B(x,L+1)\right)\big(\delta(z)B(z,L+1)\big)^{-1}

have only simple poles at
z=0,\infty,z_{1},z_{2},\dots,z_{L},1/z_{1},1/z_{2},\dots,1/z_{L},x,1/x
.

Proof.

The proof of the existence of the poles of
{\mathcal{M}}(z)
at
z=z_{1},z_{2},\dots,z_{L},1/z_{1},1/z_{2},\dots,1/z_{L}
and
x
,
1/x
is straightforward.
One gets

\displaystyle\widehat{B}(z)=\delta(z)B(z,L+1)=\alpha(2L+1)-\alpha\sum_{j=1}^{L}\sigma^{z}_{j}+\sum_{j=1}^{L}\frac{2z(v_{j}-1/v_{j})}{(z-v_{j})(z-1/v_{j})}\delta(v_{j})\sigma^{-}_{j}.

This matrix
\widehat{B}(z)
is a lower triangular matrix with the diagonal entries in the set
\{\alpha(L+1)
,
\alpha(L+3),\dots,\alpha(3L+1)\}
. In particular, this shows that it is invertible which justifies the definition of
{\mathcal{M}(z)}
. Due to the Cayley–Hamilton theorem,
\widehat{B}(z)^{-1}
is a polynomial of
\widehat{B}(z)
. Then, by using the fact that
(\sigma^{-})^{2}=0
, we prove that the poles at
z=v_{j}
and
z=1/v_{j}
of
\widehat{B}(z)^{-1}
remain simple. Then,
{\mathcal{M}}(z)
has no pole at these points. In addition, its becomes a non-singular diagonal matrix for
z=0
which proves the simplicity of the pole of
{\mathcal{M}}(z)
at
z=0
. The existence of the pole at
z=\infty
is proven using the property
{\mathcal{M}}(z)=-1/z^{2}{\mathcal{M}}(1/z)
which concludes the proof.
∎

Similar technical lemma has been proven and used in
[
15
]
. We are now in position to provide the following proposition:

Proposition 3.4
.

The following relation holds

\displaystyle\mathbb{B}(\boldsymbol{z})B(x,L+1)\Omega=\widehat{\lambda}(x)\mathbb{V}(\boldsymbol{z})+\sum_{p=1}^{L}\frac{(z_{p}-1/z_{p})\operatorname{Res}_{x=z_{p}}\big(\widehat{\lambda}(x)\big)}{(z_{p}-x)(z_{p}-1/x)}\mathbb{V}(\boldsymbol{z}_{p},x)

with
\boldsymbol{z}=\{z_{1},z_{2},\dots,z_{L}\}
and

\displaystyle\widehat{\lambda}(x)=\frac{\alpha(L+1)}{\delta(x)}\prod_{q=1}^{L}\left(\frac{(x-v_{q})(x-1/v_{q})}{(x-z_{q})(x-1/z_{q})}\right).

Proof.

The residues of
\mathcal{M}(z)\Omega
are obtained by straightforward computations:

\displaystyle\operatorname{Res}_{z=z_{p}}(\mathcal{M}(z)\Omega)=\frac{1}{\delta(z_{p})}\prod_{\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{2}{q=1}{q\neq p}}^{L}\left(\frac{(z_{p}-v_{q})(z_{p}-1/v_{q})}{(z_{p}-z_{q})(z_{p}-1/z_{q})}\right)\left(\frac{(z_{p}-v_{p})(z_{p}-1/v_{p})}{(z_{p}-x)(z_{p}-1/x)}\right)\mathbb{V}(\boldsymbol{z}_{p},x),

\displaystyle\operatorname{Res}_{z=x}(\mathcal{M}(z)\Omega)=\frac{1}{\delta(x)}\prod_{q=1}^{L}\left(\frac{(x-v_{q})(x-1/v_{q})}{(x-z_{q})(x-1/z_{q})}\right)\mathbb{V}(\boldsymbol{z}),

\displaystyle\operatorname{Res}_{z=0}(\mathcal{M}(z)\Omega)=-\frac{1}{\alpha(L+1)}\mathbb{B}(\boldsymbol{z})B(x,L+1)\Omega.

By using
{\mathcal{M}}(z)=-1/z^{2}{\mathcal{M}}(1/z)
, we deduce that
\operatorname{Res}_{z=1/z_{0}}(\mathcal{M}(z)\Omega)=\operatorname{Res}_{z=z_{0}}(\mathcal{M}(z)\Omega)
where
z_{0}=0
,
z_{1}
,
z_{2},\dots,z_{L}
,
x
. The proof is concluded by using the fact that the sum over all the residues (with the point at infinity) of a rational function vanishes.
∎

Therefore, by using relation (
3.12
) for
M=L
and the result of Proposition
3.4
, we prove that
\mathbb{V}(\boldsymbol{z})

(for
\boldsymbol{z}=\{z_{1},z_{2},\dots,z_{L}\}
) is an eigenvector of the transfer matrix
t(x)
if the Bethe roots satisfy the following Bethe equations

\displaystyle a(z_{p})+\nu(z_{p})+\sum_{\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{1}{q=1}{q\neq p}}^{L}\omega(z_{p},z_{q})=\frac{(L+1)c(z_{p})}{4z_{p}}\operatorname{Res}_{x=z_{p}}\big(\widehat{\lambda}(x)\big),\qquad\text{for }p=1,2,\dots,L.

(3.17)

To get the previous relation, we have used the functional relation

\displaystyle-\frac{(L+1)(z-1/z)c(x)}{2f(1/z,1/x)(z-x)(z-1/x)}=\frac{(L+1)c(z)}{4z}.

Explicitly, they become

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{L}\left(\frac{z_{p}+v_{j}}{z_{p}-v_{j}}+\frac{z_{p}v_{j}+1}{z_{p}v_{j}-1}\right)+\nu(z_{p})+\sum_{\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{1}{q=1}{q\neq p}}^{L}\left(\frac{z_{p}+z_{q}}{z_{p}-z_{q}}+\frac{z_{p}z_{q}+1}{z_{p}z_{q}-1}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad{}=\frac{(\rho^{2}-\beta^{2})(L+1)^{2}}{16\rho^{2}}\frac{(z_{p}-v_{p})(z_{p}-1/v_{p})}{\delta(z_{p})\delta(1/z_{p})(z_{p}^{2}-1)}\prod_{\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{1}{q=1}{q\neq p}}^{L}\frac{(z_{p}-v_{q})(z_{p}-1/v_{q})}{(z_{p}-z_{q})(z_{p}-1/z_{q})},

where
\nu(z)
is given by (
2.11
).
The corresponding eigenvalue is

\displaystyle\lambda(x)-\frac{L+1}{2}c(x)\widehat{\lambda}(x)+\sum_{p=1}^{M}\omega(x,z_{p})\left(a(x)+\nu(x)+\sum_{q\neq p}\frac{(x-1/x)\omega(z_{p},z_{q})}{z_{p}-1/z_{p}}\right).

(3.18)

Then the l.h.s. of the Bethe equations is similar to the ones for the diagonal or triangular boundaries. The r.h.s. corresponds to the inhomogeneous part of the TQ relation discovered in
[
11
,
12
]
to solve the XXZ spin chain with twist or boundaries.
