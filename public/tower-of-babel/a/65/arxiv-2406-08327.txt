Bessel potentials and Green functions on pseudo-Euclidean spaces
Jan Dereziński, Bartłomiej Sikorski

Abstract
We review properties of Bessel potentials, that is, inverse Fourier transforms of (regularizations of) ( m 2 + p 2 ) − μ 2 (m^{2}+p^{2})^{-\frac{\mu}{2}} on a pseudoEuclidean space with signature ( q , d − q ) (q,d-q) . We are mostly interested in the Lorentzian signature ( 1 , d − 1 ) (1,d-1) , and the case μ = 2 \mu=2 , related to the Klein-Gordon equation ( − □ + m 2 ) ​ f = 0 (-\Box+m^{2})f=0 . We analyze properties of various “propagators”, which play an important role in Quantum Field Theory, such as the retarded/advanced propagators or Feynman/antiFeynman propagators. We consistently use hypergeometric functions instead of Bessel functions, which makes most formulas much more transparent. We pay attention to distributional properties of various Bessel potentials. We include in our analysis the “tachyonic case”, corresponding to the “wrong” sign in the Klein-Gordon equation.



Keywords:
Bessel potential, Riesz potential, Klein-Gordon equation, Minkowski space.

1
Introduction

Let us start with the Bessel potentials on the Euclidean space
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
.
Let
{\rm Re}\mu>0
and
m\geq 0
.
If
m=0
we will usually additionally assume that
d>{\rm Re}\mu
.
Consider the
function

G_{\mu,m}(x)=\int\frac{{\rm e}^{{\rm i}px}}{(m^{2}+p^{2})^{\frac{\mu}{2}}}\frac{{\rm d}p}{(2\pi)^{d}}

(1.1)

on the Euclidean space
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
.
Note that that
G_{\mu,m}(x-y)
can be interpreted as the integral kernel of
the operator
(m^{2}-\Delta)^{-\frac{\mu}{2}}
.

We have

G_{\mu,m}(x)=m^{d-\mu}G_{\mu,1}(mx),

(1.2)

so the case
m>0
reduces to
m=1
.
G_{\mu,1}(x)
can be expressed in terms of
the
Macdonald function
, one of solutions of the
modified
Bessel equation
. Therefore,
G_{\mu,1}(x)

is often called the

Bessel potential
of order
\mu
.
The function
G_{\mu,0}(x)
is called the
Riesz potential
of order
\mu
.

It is remarkable that the theory of Bessel potentials is very
similar for all
\mu>0
.
However, the case
\mu=2
is probably the most important.
In this case we will usually omit
\mu
from the notation, setting
G_{m}(x):=G_{2,m}(x)
, and
obtaining the
Green function
of the
inhomogeneous
Helmholtz equation

(-\Delta+m^{2})g(x)=f(x).

(1.3)

In other words,

(-\Delta+m^{2})G_{m}(x)=\delta(x),

(1.4)

Note that in
dimension
d=3
we have
G_{m}(x)=\frac{{\rm e}^{-m|x|}}{4\pi|x|}
. Thus
for
m>0
it coincides with the
Yukawa potential
and for
m=0

with the
Coulomb potential
.

Suppose now
{\mathbb{R}}^{q,d-q}
is the
pseudo-Euclidean space of
signature
(q,d-q)
. In other words, as a set it is
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
with the scalar product
for
x,y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{q,p}
given by

xy=-x_{1}y_{1}\cdots-x_{q}y_{q}+x_{q+1}y_{q+1}+\cdots x_{d}y_{d}.

(1.5)

The definition (
1.1
) is usually no longer correct for
m^{2}\in{\mathbb{R}}
, since

\frac{1}{(m^{2}+p^{2})^{\frac{\mu}{2}}}
may fail to be locally integrable, and
hence may not define a tempered distribution. It still works for
complex non-real
m^{2}
. A possible pair of
generalizations of (
1.1
) to
m^{2}
real is the pair of
functions, which correspond to the limits from above and below:

\displaystyle G_{\mu,m}^{\mathrm{F}}(x)=\int\frac{{\rm e}^{{\rm i}px}}{(m^{2}+p^{2}-{\rm i}0)^{\frac{\mu}{2}}}\frac{{\rm d}p}{(2\pi)^{d}},

(1.6)

\displaystyle G_{\mu,m}^{\overline{\mathrm{F}}}(x)=\int\frac{{\rm e}^{{\rm i}px}}{(m^{2}+p^{2}+{\rm i}0)^{\frac{\mu}{2}}}\frac{{\rm d}p}{(2\pi)^{d}}.

(1.7)

(
1.6
) and (
1.7
) have an obvious interpretation as
boundary values of integral kernels of appropriate functions of the
pseudoLaplacian

\Box:=-\partial_{1}^{2}\cdots-\partial_{q}^{2}+\partial_{q+1}^{2}+\partial_{d}^{2}.

(1.8)

Again, the case
m>0
reduces to
m=1
.

G_{\mu,m}^{\mathrm{F}/\overline{\mathrm{F}}}(x)
can be expressed by Macdonald and Hankel
functions. (The Hankel functions are special functions solving
the standard Bessel equation.)

The symbols
\mathrm{F}
and
\overline{\mathrm{F}}
are motivated
by the special case of Green functions in the Lorentzian case.

G_{2,m}^{\mathrm{F}/\overline{\mathrm{F}}}(x)
coincide then with
the
Feynman
, resp. the
anti-Feynman propagators
,
which play an important role in Quantum
Field Theory, as we explain below.

In our paper we will discuss all signatures, including the
Euclidean

(0,d)
and

anti-Euclidean

(d,0)
. However, we are mostly interested in the

Lorentzian
signature
. The Lorentzian signature comes in two varieties: “mostly
pluses”
(1,d-1)
and “mostly minuses”
(d-1,1)
. We will treat the
former as the standard one.

The Lorentzian case is especially interesting and rich.
This is related to the fact that the
Minkowski space

{\mathbb{R}}^{1,d-1}
can be equipped with a causal structure
and the set

p^{2}+m^{2}=0
has two connected components. Therefore,
besides
G_{\mu,m}^{\mathrm{F}/\overline{\mathrm{F}}}
, we can introduce
the distributions

\displaystyle G_{\mu,m}^{\vee}(x)=\int\frac{{\rm e}^{{\rm i}px}}{(m^{2}+p^{2}-{\rm i}0{\rm sgn}p^{0})^{\frac{\mu}{2}}}\frac{{\rm d}p}{(2\pi)^{d}},

(1.9)

\displaystyle G_{\mu,m}^{\wedge}(x)=\int\frac{{\rm e}^{{\rm i}px}}{(m^{2}+p^{2}+{\rm i}0{\rm sgn}p^{0})^{\frac{\mu}{2}}}\frac{{\rm d}p}{(2\pi)^{d}},

(1.10)

which are invariant wrt orthochronous Lorentz
transformations. Remarkably,
G_{\mu,m}^{\vee/\wedge}
is supported
in the forward, resp. backward cone. Therefore,
G_{\mu,m}^{\vee}
is called
the
forward (or retarded)
, and
G_{\mu,m}^{\wedge}
the
backward (or advanced) Bessel potential
.

In the Lorentzian case, the pseudo-Laplacian is usually called the

d’Alembertian

\Box:=-\partial_{0}^{2}+\partial_{1}^{2}+\cdots+\partial_{d-1}^{2},

(1.11)

and
-\Box+m^{2}
is called the
Klein-Gordon operator
.
By a
Green function
of the (inhomogeneous)
Klein-Gordon equation

(-\Box+m^{2})f(x)=g(x).

(1.12)

we will mean a distribution
G^{\bullet}(x)
satisfying

(-\Box+m^{2})G^{\bullet}(x)=\delta(x).

(1.13)

The Klein-Gordon equation possesses many Green functions.
Among them, we have the Feynman and antiFeynman Green functions given
by the formulas (
1.6
) and (
1.7
) with
\mu=2
.
Another distinguished pair consists of the retarded (or forward)
Green function and the advanced (or backward) Green function, defined by demanding that their support
is contained in the forward, resp. backward cone.
For
m^{2}\geq 0
the retarded Green function is given by
(
1.9
) and the advanced Green function by (
1.10
) with
\mu=2
.

The Feynman, anti-Feynman,
forward, and backward Green functions of the Klein-Gordon equation have important
applications in physics, especially in classical and quantum field
theory. The forward and backward Green functions can be used to
express the Cauchy problem. The Feynman, resp. anti-Feynman Green
functions express the time-ordered, resp. anti-time-ordered vacuum expectation
values of fields in quantum field theory. Importantly, they satisfy the identity

G_{m}^{\mathrm{F}}+G_{m}^{\overline{\mathrm{F}}}=G_{m}^{\vee}+G_{m}^{\wedge}.

(1.14)

In our paper, we also consider the Lorentzian case with the “wrong
sign of
m^{2}
”. This case
corresponds to the
tachyonic
Klein-Gordon equation

(-\Box-m^{2})f(x)=g(x).

(1.15)

Remarkably, all four basic Green functions, Feynman
G_{m}^{\mathrm{F}}
,
anti-Feynman
G_{m}^{\overline{\mathrm{F}}}
,
forward
G_{m}^{\vee}
, and backward
G_{m}^{\wedge}
,
can be defined in the tachyonic case.
For the Feynman and anti-Feynman Green functions we can still use the formulas
(
1.6
) and (
1.7
), where
m^{2}
is replaced with

-m^{2}
. Their interpretation in terms of the
vacuum expectation values is however lost, since the tachyonic theory has no
vacuum state. (In particular, in the tachyonic case we do not
have a counterpart of the positive/negative frequency Green
functions (
5.40
)). The forward and backward Green functions
are defined by their support properties. For them we cannot use the formulas
(
1.9
) and (
1.10
). In fact, the set
p^{2}-m^{2}=0
is
now connected, and cutting it with
{\rm sgn}p^{0}
is no longer
invariant. Nevertheless, one can use the
analytic continuation in
m
to uniquely define Green functions with correct
support properties also in the tachyonic case.
We point out that the
identity (
1.14
) is no longer true in the tachyonic case.

The difference of two Green functions is a solution of the homogeneous Helmholtz/Klein-Gordon
equation. Certain distinguished solutions are important for
physics applications. In the Lorentzian case, we have the
Pauli-Jordan propagator; for

m^{2}\geq 0
also the positive
frequency and the negative frequency two-point functions.
We illustrate applications of distinguished solutions
to the Helmholtz/Klein-Gordon equation by computing
averages of plane waves over the sphere (in the
Euclidean case), as well as over the hyperbolic and de Sitter space
(in the Lorentzian case).

Let us say a few words about the history of Bessel potentials.
The name
Bessel potentials
was introduced in the 60s by
Aronszajn and Smith, who studied them in the Euclidean case in
[
1
]
. Around the
same time, they were also investigated by Calderon
[
2
]
.
Bessel potentials are frequently viewed in the literature as
smoothed versions of Riesz potentials (see, for example,

[
3
]
where they are defined using the integral formula
(
2.5
)). They are often used to define Bessel potential spaces that generalize standard Sobolev spaces (see
[
4
]
), and the idea to use Bessel kernels is due to Deny
[
5
]
. For a comprehensive treatment of (Euclidean) Bessel potentials, we refer the reader to
[
1
]
, where many properties of Bessel potentials are exhaustively studied.

The Lorentzian versions of Bessel potentials, typically in dimension
1+3, often
appear in the literature on Quantum Field Theory.
They are ingredients of formulas for scattering amplitudes based on
Feynman diagrams and on the Epstein-Glaser approach

[
6
,
7
]
. The famous textbooks by
Björken-Drell
[
8
]
and by Bogoliubov– Shirkov

[
9
]
contain appendices devoted to distinguished
Green functions and solutions of the Klein-Gordon equation in the
physical dimension 1+3. They carry various names. For instance,
often the term
Green function
is replaced by
propagator
, etc.

Formulas for Bessel potentials in various signatures are known and are available in
collections of integrals such as
[
10
]
and
[
11
]
. In chapter
III.2 of
[
12
]
one can find Fourier transforms of
powers of quadratic forms with any signature, including the formula
(
4.5
) of the general case studied in this paper.
Although there
exists a large literature about Bessel potentials, our presentation
contains several new points, which we have not seen in the literature
and believe are important.

The first new point involves the special functions that we use.
Various kinds of the Bessel equation can be reduced to equation

\displaystyle(z{\rm\partial}_{z}^{2}+(\alpha+1){\rm\partial}_{z}-1)v(z)=0,

(1.16)

which can be called the

{}_{0}F_{1}

hypergeometric equation
.
Equation (
1.16
) has two singular points:
0
and
\infty
.
The singularity at
0
is regular (Fuchsian), and the solution
obtained by the well-known
Frobenius method is the

{}_{0}F_{1}

hypergeometric function
, which we denote

F_{\alpha}
. We usually prefer its
Olver normalized
version
{\bf F}_{\alpha}:=\frac{F_{\alpha}}{\Gamma(\alpha+1)}
, closely related to
the Bessel function, both standard and modified.

Another standard
solution of the
{}_{0}F_{1}
equation, corresponding to the
irregular singularity at
\infty
, is
the function that we denote
U_{\alpha}
,
This function is perhaps less known. Up to a coefficient, it coincides
with the
Meijer
G
-function

G_{0,2}^{2,0}(-;0,-\alpha;z)
.
The function
U_{\alpha}
is closely related to the Macdonald
and Hankel functions.

In our paper, we treat
{\bf F}_{\alpha}
and
U_{\alpha}
functions as basic
elements of our description of Bessel potentials.
In our opinion, they are much more convenient
for this purpose, rather than functions from the
Bessel family, as it is done
in the conventional treatment of this topic.
The corresponding formulas
are simpler and more transparent. This is especially visible when we consider
non-Euclidean signatures, where the formulas involve analytic
continuation across two branches and an irregular distribution at the
junction of these branches. The
{\bf F}_{\alpha}
and

U_{\alpha}
functions are also convenient to see the transition from
the Minkowski space to the deSitter and the universal cover of the
AntideSitter space, as discussed in
[
13
]
.
In fact, on the Minkowski space
retarded/advanced and Feynman/anti-Feynman Bessel potentials
are expressed in terms of
{\bf F}_{\alpha}
and

U_{\alpha}
, and on the deSitter and Anti-deSitter space we need closely
related
Gegenbauer functions
instead.

We also believe that there are some important novel features in our presentation of the
Lorentzian case, which is tailored to the needs of Quantum Field
Theory.
In our opinion, it is quite remarkable how rich is the theory of Bessel potentials in the Lorentzian signature.
We have four distinct Lorentz invariant Green functions of the
Klein-Gordon equation, with important applications in physics. If we
include also a few useful distinguished solutions to the Klein-Gordon
equation (such as the Pauli-Jordan propagator, positive and negative
frequency solution), then we obtain a whole menagerie of functions.

In
our discussion we cover not only the massive and massless case, but
also the tachyonic case. This case is quite curious, even though usually
ignored in the physics literature. We also discuss identity
(
1.14
), true for
m^{2}\geq 0
, but wrong in the tachyonic
case. Remarkably, this identity sometimes, but not always,
generalizes to curved spacetimes, as analyzed recently in
[
13
]
.

In our treatment, we pay special attention to the
distributional character of Bessel potentials. This is unproblematic
in the Euclidean signature, where Bessel potentials are given by
(locally) integrable functions. This is not the case in non-Euclidean signatures.
In particular, it is interesting to look at the functions
{\bf F}_{\alpha}
and
U_{\alpha}
as defining distributions on the real
line.
With this interpretation in mind, well-known identities have to be
reformulated, see e.g.
(
2.47
).

Finally, let us mention that there exist a large literature about
Green functions of the Klein-Gordon equation on curved spacetimes. In
the generic context their explicit expression is not possible,
and often instead of exact Green functions one restricts oneself to

parametrices
, that is inverses modulo smoothing terms. The existence
of exactly four parametrices that generalize

G^{\mathrm{F}/\overline{\mathrm{F}}}
and
G^{\vee/\wedge}
is the result of
a famous paper by Duistermaat and Hörmander
[
14
]
. It is also
remarkable that expansions similar to
(
5.34
)-(
5.37
) describe singular parts of these
parametrices also in curved spacetimes, where they can be derived from
the Hadamard recursion relations (see Chapter 4 of
[
15
]

or Chapter 2 of
[
16
]
.) The universality of these
singular parts is an important idea in
Quantum Field Theory on curved spacetimes
[
7
]
.

2.1
The

Our presentation of Bessel potentials will use extensively

Let

.
The
{}_{0}F_{1}
equation

(z{\rm\partial}_{z}^{2}+c{\rm\partial}_{z}-1)v(z)=0.

(2.1)

If
c\neq 0,-1,-2,\dots
, then the only solution of the
{}_{0}F_{1}
equation equal to
1
at

{}_{0}F_{1}

F(c;z):=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(c)_{j}}\frac{z^{j}}{j!},

where
(c)_{j}
denotes the Pochhammer symbol:

\displaystyle(a)_{0}=1,

\displaystyle(a)_{n}:=a(a+1)\dots(a+n-1),

\displaystyle n=1,2,\dots

\displaystyle(a)_{n}:=\frac{1}{(a-n)\dots(a-1)},

\displaystyle n=\dots,-2,-1.

F(c;z)

Sometimes it is more convenient to consider

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi{\rm i}}\int\limits_{]-\infty,0^{+},-\infty[}{\rm e}^{t}{\rm e}^{\frac{z}{t}}t^{-c}{\rm d}t

\displaystyle U_{\alpha}(z)

\displaystyle{\rm e}^{-2\sqrt{z}}z^{-\frac{\alpha}{2}-\frac{1}{4}}{}_{2}F_{0}\Big(\frac{1}{2}+\alpha,\frac{1}{2}-\alpha;-;-\frac{1}{4\sqrt{z}}\Big),

k\in{\mathbb{N}}

\displaystyle F_{-\frac{1}{2}-k}(z)=

\displaystyle z^{k+\frac{1}{2}}\partial_{z}^{k}\Big(\frac{\cosh(2\sqrt{z}\big)}{\sqrt{z}}\Big),

(2.20)

\displaystyle F_{\frac{1}{2}+k}(z)=

\displaystyle\partial_{z}^{k}\Big(\frac{\sinh(2\sqrt{z})}{2\sqrt{z}}\Big),

(2.21)

\displaystyle U_{-\frac{1}{2}-k}(z)=

\displaystyle(-1)^{k}z^{k+\frac{1}{2}}\partial_{z}^{k}\Big(\frac{\exp(-2\sqrt{z})}{\sqrt{z}}\Big),

(2.22)

\displaystyle U_{\frac{1}{2}+k}(z)=

\displaystyle(-1)^{k}\partial_{z}^{k}\Big(\frac{\exp(-2\sqrt{z})}{\sqrt{z}}\Big).

(2.23)

2.2
Relationship to confluent functions

Recall that the confluent equation is

(w\partial_{w}^{2}+(c-w)\partial_{w}-c)f(w)=0.

(2.24)

Its standard solutions are

\displaystyle\text{ Kummer's confluent function}\quad{}_{1}F_{1}(a;c;w)

\displaystyle:=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(a)_{n}}{(c)_{n}n!}w^{n},

\displaystyle\text{and Tricomi's confluent function}\quad U(a;c;w)

\displaystyle:=z^{-a}{}_{2}F_{0}(a,1+a-c;-;-w^{-1}).

The
{}_{0}F_{1}
equation can be reduced to a special class of
the confluent equation by the so-called
Kummer’s 2nd transformation
:

\displaystyle z{\rm\partial}_{z}^{2}+(\alpha+1){\rm\partial}_{z}-1

(2.25)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{4}{w}{\rm e}^{-w/2}\Big(w\partial_{w}^{2}+(2\alpha+1-w)\partial_{w}-\alpha-\frac{1}{2}\Big){\rm e}^{w/2},

(2.26)

where
w=\pm 4\sqrt{z}
,
z=\frac{1}{16}w^{2}
.

F_{\alpha}
and
U_{\alpha}
can be expressed in terms of Kummer’s and
Tricomi’s confluent function as follows:

\displaystyle F_{\alpha}(z)

\displaystyle={\rm e}^{\mp 2\sqrt{z}}{}_{1}F_{1}\Big(\alpha+\frac{1}{2},2\alpha+1,\pm 4\sqrt{z}\Big),

(2.27)

\displaystyle U_{\alpha}(z)

\displaystyle=\frac{{\rm e}^{-2\sqrt{z}}}{2^{2\alpha+1}}U\Big(\alpha+\frac{1}{2},2\alpha+1,4\sqrt{z}\Big).

(2.28)

2.3
Relationship to
Meijer G-functions

Solutions of hypergeometric equations
{}_{p}F_{q}
can be expressed in
terms of Meijer
G
-functions
[
19
]
. In particular, the
{}_{0}F_{1}
equation
can be solved by two distinguished functions

\displaystyle G_{0,2}^{1,0}\Big(\begin{matrix}\\
0,-\alpha\end{matrix}\Big|-z\Big)

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{2\pi{\rm i}}\int_{L_{1}}\frac{\Gamma(-s){\rm e}^{{\rm i}\pi s}}{\Gamma(\alpha+1+s)}z^{s}{\rm d}s,

(2.29)

\displaystyle G_{0,2}^{2,0}\Big(\begin{matrix}\\
0,-\alpha\end{matrix}\Big|z\Big)

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{2\pi{\rm i}}\int_{L_{2}}\Gamma(-s)\Gamma(-\alpha-s)z^{s}{\rm d}s.

(2.30)

Here, the contour
L_{1}
goes from
+\infty
to
+\infty
and encircles

{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
, and the contour
L_{2}
also goes from
+\infty
to
+\infty
and encircles

{\mathbb{N}}_{0}\cup({\mathbb{N}}_{0}-\alpha)
, both counterclockwise.
Computing the residues and using the connection formula (
2.8
)
we obtain

\displaystyle{\bf F}_{\alpha}(z)

\displaystyle=G_{0,2}^{1,0}\Big(\begin{matrix}\\
0,-\alpha\end{matrix}\Big|-z\Big),

(2.31)

\displaystyle U_{\alpha}(z)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}}G_{0,2}^{2,0}\Big(\begin{matrix}\\
0,-\alpha\end{matrix}\Big|z\Big).

(2.32)

2.4
Relationship to Bessel functions

In the literature, the

{}_{0}F_{1}
equation is seldom used. Much more frequent is
the
modified Bessel equation
, which is equivalent to the

{}_{0}F_{1}
equation. It is given by the operator

\displaystyle z^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}\big(z{\rm\partial}_{z}^{2}+(\alpha+1){\rm\partial}_{z}-1\big)z^{-\frac{\alpha}{2}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\partial_{w}^{2}+\frac{1}{w}\partial_{w}-1-\frac{\alpha^{2}}{w^{2}},

where
z=\frac{w^{2}}{4}
,
w=\pm 2\sqrt{z}
.

Even more frequent is the (standard)
Bessel equation
given by:

\displaystyle-z^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}\big(z{\rm\partial}_{z}^{2}+(\alpha+1){\rm\partial}_{z}-1\big)z^{-\frac{\alpha}{2}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\partial_{u}^{2}+\frac{1}{u}\partial_{u}+1-\frac{\alpha^{2}}{u^{2}},

where
z=-\frac{u^{2}}{4}
,
u=\pm 2{\rm i}\sqrt{z}
.
Clearly, we can pass from the modified Bessel to the Bessel equation by

w=\pm{\rm i}u
.

The
function
{\bf F}_{\alpha}
is also seldom used. Instead,
one uses the
modified Bessel function
and, even more frequently,
the
Bessel function
:

\displaystyle I_{\alpha}(w)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Big(\frac{w}{2}\Big)^{\alpha}{\bf F}_{\alpha}\Big(\frac{w^{2}}{4}\Big),

(2.33)

\displaystyle J_{\alpha}(w)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Big(\frac{w}{2}\Big)^{\alpha}{\bf F}_{\alpha}\Big(-\frac{w^{2}}{4}\Big).

(2.34)

They solve the modified Bessel, resp. the Bessel equation.

Instead of the
U_{\alpha}
function one uses
the
Macdonald function
, solving the modified Bessel equation:

\displaystyle K_{\alpha}(w)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{2}\Big(\frac{w}{2}\Big)^{\alpha}U_{\alpha}\Big(\frac{w^{2}}{4}\Big),

(2.35)

and the Hankel functions of the 1st and 2nd kind, solving the Bessel equation:

\displaystyle H_{\alpha}^{(1)}(w)=H_{\alpha}^{+}(w)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{-{\rm i}}{\sqrt{\pi}}\Big(\frac{{\rm e}^{-{\rm i}\pi}w}{2}\Big)^{\alpha}U_{\alpha}\Big({\rm e}^{-{\rm i}\pi}\frac{w^{2}}{4}\Big),

(2.36)

\displaystyle H_{\alpha}^{(2)}(w)=H_{\alpha}^{-}(w)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{{\rm i}}{\sqrt{\pi}}\Big(\frac{{\rm e}^{{\rm i}\pi}w}{2}\Big)^{\alpha}U_{\alpha}\Big({\rm e}^{{\rm i}\pi}\frac{w^{2}}{4}\Big).

(2.37)

Here are the relations between various functions from the Bessel family:

\displaystyle H_{\alpha}^{\pm}(z)

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{\pi}{\rm e}^{\mp{\rm i}\frac{\pi}{2}(\alpha+1)}K_{\alpha}(\mp{\rm i}z),

(2.38)

\displaystyle H_{-\alpha}^{\pm}(z)

\displaystyle={\rm e}^{\pm\alpha\pi{\rm i}}H_{\alpha}^{\pm}(z),

(2.39)

\displaystyle J_{\alpha}(z)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(H_{\alpha}^{+}(z)+H_{\alpha}^{-}(z)\right),

(2.40)

\displaystyle I_{\alpha}(z)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\pi}\bigl(\mp{\rm i}K_{\alpha}({\rm e}^{\mp{\rm i}\pi}z)\pm{\rm i}{\rm e}^{{\rm i}\pi m}K_{\alpha}(z)\bigr).

(2.41)

2.5
{\bf F}_{\alpha}
and
U_{\alpha}
functions as distributions

The function
U_{\alpha}(z)
(and many others that we consider in this
paper) are multivalued analytic functions defined on the Riemann
surface of the logarithm. It has its
principal branch
on

{\mathbb{C}}\backslash]-\infty,0]
. For its
analytic continuation around
0
we will often use the
self-explanatory notation
U_{\alpha}({\rm e}^{{\rm i}\phi}z)
, where

z\in{\mathbb{C}}\backslash]-\infty,0]
and
\phi\in{\mathbb{R}}
.

We will often consider
U_{\alpha}(w)
on the real line.
For
{w}>0
this is
unambiguous. For
{w}<0
one needs to add
\pm{\rm i}0
indicating whether we
are infinitesimally above or below the real line. At
w=0
this function has a singularity, which may require a more careful treatment in terms of distributions (see Appendix
A.2
for notation about some common distributions).

Thus we introduce the distribution on the real line

U_{\alpha}({w}\pm{\rm i}0):=\lim_{\epsilon\searrow 0}U_{\alpha}({w}\pm{\rm i}\epsilon),

(2.42)

where the right-hand side should be understood as the limit in the
distributional sense.
Note that for
{w}\neq 0
these distributions are regular (in the sense of Appendix
A.2
) and given by
analytic functions:

\displaystyle U_{\alpha}({w}\pm{\rm i}0)

\displaystyle=U_{\alpha}({w}),\quad{w}>0;

(2.43)

\displaystyle U_{\alpha}({w}\pm{\rm i}0)

\displaystyle=U_{\alpha}\big({\rm e}^{\pm{\rm i}\pi}(-{w})\big),\quad{w}<0.

(2.44)

At
{w}=0
these distributions are irregular if
{\rm Re}\alpha\geq 1
.
We can then write
U_{\alpha}(w\pm{\rm i}0)
as the sum of an irregular and regular part as follows:

\displaystyle U_{\alpha}(w\pm{\rm i}0)

\displaystyle=U_{\alpha}^{\rm sing}(w\pm{\rm i}0)+U_{\alpha}^{\rm reg}(w),

(2.45)

\displaystyle U_{\alpha}^{\rm sing}(w\pm{\rm i}0)

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}}\sum_{j=0}^{\lfloor{\rm Re}\alpha\rfloor-1}\frac{(-1)^{j}\Gamma(\alpha-j)}{j!}(w\pm{\rm i}0)^{j-\alpha}.

(2.46)

This easily follows from (
2.8
)
and (
2.17
).

Recall that for
\alpha\not\in{\mathbb{N}}

the symbol
w_{-}^{-\alpha}
defined in
(
A.9
) denotes the standard
regularization of

|w|^{-\alpha}\theta(-w)
.
The identity (
2.11
) for
w\in{\mathbb{R}}\backslash\{0\}
can be
rewritten as

\displaystyle w_{-}^{-\alpha}{\bf F}_{-\alpha}(w)

\displaystyle:=\frac{{\rm i}}{2\sqrt{\pi}}\left(U_{\alpha}(w+{\rm i}0)-U_{\alpha}(w-{\rm i}0)\right).

(2.47)

(Note that both sides of (
2.47
) are zero for
w>0
). It is
easy to see that for
\alpha\not\in{\mathbb{N}}
(
2.47
) is a correct
distributional identity, where the lhs is the product of the distribution

w_{-}^{-\alpha}
and of the smooth function
{\bf F}_{-\alpha}(w)
,
whereas the rhs is a linear combination of distributions defined in
(
2.42
). (
2.47
) can be decomposed into a singular and
regular part as follows:

\displaystyle w_{-}^{-\alpha}{\bf F}_{-\alpha}(w)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{\lfloor{\rm Re}\alpha\rfloor-1}\frac{w_{-}^{-\alpha+j}(-1)^{j}}{\Gamma(-\alpha+j+1)j!}+\sum_{j=\lfloor{\rm Re}\alpha\rfloor}^{\infty}\frac{w_{-}^{-\alpha+j}(-1)^{j}}{\Gamma(-\alpha+j+1)j!}

(2.48)

The rhs of (
2.47
) is well-defined also for
\alpha\in{\mathbb{N}}
. We
will
define
for such
\alpha
the symbol on the lhs of
(
2.47
) by the rhs. Using
(
A.14
) for
\alpha\in{\mathbb{N}}
we can thus write

\displaystyle w_{-}^{-\alpha}{\bf F}_{-\alpha}(w)

\displaystyle=(-1)^{\alpha+1}\sum_{j=0}^{\alpha-1}\frac{(-1)^{j}\delta^{(\alpha-1-j)}(w)}{j!}+(-1)^{\alpha}{\bf F}_{\alpha}(w)\theta(-w).

(2.49)

(Compare with (
2.16
), where you do not see the
distributions supported at zero).

Of course, in the context described in this subsection, the distribution

U_{\alpha}(w\pm{\rm i}0)
defined as in (
2.42
)
can be also expressed in terms of

K_{\alpha}
and
H_{\alpha}^{\pm}
,
where we would have to treat
\sqrt{w}
, resp.
\sqrt{-w}
with

w\in{\mathbb{R}}
as their arguments.
It is then important to indicate precisely how the analytic continuation of
the square root is performed—whether we bypass the branch point at
zero from above or from below, adding
\pm{\rm i}0
to
the variable:

\displaystyle K_{\alpha}\big(\sqrt{w\mp{\rm i}0}\big)

\displaystyle:=\begin{cases}K_{\alpha}\big(\sqrt{w}\big),&w>0,\\
K_{\alpha}(\mp{\rm i}\sqrt{-w}\big)=\pm{\rm i}\frac{\pi}{2}{\rm e}^{\pm{\rm i}\pi\alpha}H_{\alpha}^{\pm}\big(\sqrt{-w}\big),&w<0;\end{cases}

(2.50a)

\displaystyle H_{\alpha}^{\pm}\big(\sqrt{-w\pm{\rm i}0}\big)

\displaystyle:=\begin{cases}H_{\alpha}^{\pm}\big(\pm{\rm i}\sqrt{w}\big)=\mp{\rm i}\frac{2}{\pi}{\rm e}^{\mp{\rm i}\pi\alpha}K_{\alpha}\big(\sqrt{w}\big),&w>0,\\
H_{\alpha}^{\pm}\big(\sqrt{-w}\big),&w<0.\end{cases}

(2.50b)

We believe, however, that it is more convenient in such situations
to use the function
U_{\alpha}
. Indeed, we have

\displaystyle U_{\alpha}\Big(\frac{w\mp{\rm i}0}{4}\Big)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\frac{2^{\alpha+1}}{\sqrt{\pi}}(w\mp{\rm i}0)^{-\frac{\alpha}{2}}K_{\alpha}\big(\sqrt{w\mp{\rm i}0}\big)\\
\pm{\rm i}2^{\alpha}\sqrt{\pi}(w\mp{\rm i}0)^{-\frac{\alpha}{2}}H_{\alpha}^{\pm}\big(\sqrt{-w\pm{\rm i}0}\big).\end{cases}

(2.51)

3
Euclidean and anti-Euclidean signature

This section is devoted to Bessel potentials on
the Euclidean space
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
.
|x|:=\sqrt{x^{2}}

will denote the Euclidean norm of
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
.

In this section, we will provide various expressions both in terms of
the Bessel family functions

I_{\alpha},J_{\alpha},K_{\alpha},H_{\alpha}^{\pm}
, as well as in terms of
the hypergeometric functions
F_{\alpha},U_{\alpha}
.

3.1
General exponents–Euclidean case

Consider first the Euclidean signature. For
m>0
and
{\rm Re}\mu>0
the function

\frac{1}{(p^{2}+m^{2})^{\frac{\mu}{2}}}
defines a tempered distribution,
hence one can compute its Fourier transform:

Theorem 1

Let
m>0
.

\displaystyle G_{\mu,m}(x)

\displaystyle=\int\frac{{\rm e}^{{\rm i}px}}{(p^{2}+m^{2})^{\frac{\mu}{2}}}\frac{{\rm d}p}{(2\pi)^{d}}

(3.1)

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{\Gamma(\frac{\mu}{2})(4\pi)^{\frac{d}{2}}}\Big(\frac{|x|}{2m}\Big)^{\frac{\mu-d}{2}}K_{\frac{d-\mu}{2}}(m|x|)

(3.2)

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{\pi}m^{d-\mu}}{\Gamma(\frac{\mu}{2})(4\pi)^{\frac{d}{2}}}U_{\frac{d-\mu}{2}}\Big(\frac{m^{2}x^{2}}{4}\Big).

(3.3)

Proof.
By (
A.1
),

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d}}\int\frac{{\rm e}^{{\rm i}px}{\rm d}p}{(m^{2}+p^{2})^{\frac{\mu}{2}}}

(3.4)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d}\Gamma(\frac{\mu}{2})}\int_{0}^{\infty}{\rm d}s\int{\rm d}ps^{\frac{\mu}{2}-1}{\rm e}^{-(m^{2}+p^{2})s}{\rm e}^{{\rm i}px}

(3.5)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(4\pi)^{\frac{d}{2}}\Gamma(\frac{\mu}{2})}\int_{0}^{\infty}{\rm d}ss^{\frac{\mu}{2}-\frac{d}{2}-1}{\rm e}^{-m^{2}s-\frac{x^{2}}{4s}}

(3.6)

Then we use
(
2.5
).

\Box

Note that the integrand of (
3.1
)
is integrable
for
{\rm Re}\mu>d
. Therefore,
G_{\mu,m}
is bounded for
such
\mu
.
For instance,

\displaystyle G_{\mu,m}(0)=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d}}\int\frac{{\rm d}p}{(p^{2}+m^{2})^{\frac{\mu}{2}}}

\displaystyle=\frac{m^{d-\mu}\Gamma(\frac{\mu-d}{2})}{(4\pi)^{\frac{d}{2}}\Gamma(\frac{\mu}{2})},\quad{\rm Re}\mu>d.

(3.7)

3.2
General exponents–massless case

For

0<{\rm Re}\mu<d
the following function is in
L_{\rm loc}^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
and is bounded
at infinity, hence
it defines a regular distribution in
{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
:

\displaystyle G_{\mu,0}(x)

\displaystyle:=\int\frac{{\rm e}^{{\rm i}px}}{|p|^{\mu}}\frac{{\rm d}p}{(2\pi)^{d}}

(3.8)

\displaystyle=\frac{\Gamma(\frac{d-\mu}{2})}{\Gamma(\frac{\mu}{2})(4\pi)^{\frac{d}{2}}}\Big(\frac{|x|}{2}\Big)^{\mu-d}.

(3.9)

It is called the
Riesz potential
, and it is the massless limit of
Bessel potentials:

Theorem 2

Let

0<{\rm Re}\mu<d
. Then

G_{\mu,0}(x)=\lim_{m\to 0}G_{\mu,m}(x)

(3.10)

in the sense of
{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
.

Proof.
One can prove this fact in the position space, see
Subsection
4.2
, where we give a proof in
the case of a general signature. Instead, in this section we describe a proof
based on the momentum space.

For
0<\mu<d
,
|p|^{-\mu}
is a regular distribution.
By using the Dominated Convergence Theorem we see that
the pointwise limit

\displaystyle\lim_{m\to 0}(p^{2}+m^{2})^{-\frac{\mu}{2}}=|p|^{-\mu}

(3.11)

is a limit in the sense of
{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
. The Fourier transformation is a
continuous operator on
{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
. Therefore, for considered
\mu
,
(
3.10
) is true.
\Box

3.3
General exponent–antiEuclidean case

Suppose now the scalar product is negative definite. For
m^{2}>0
, the function

\frac{1}{(-p^{2}+m^{2})^{\frac{\mu}{2}}}

does
not
define
uniquely
a
distribution,
therefore
one
cannot
compute its Fourier
transform. However, if

m^{2}\in{\mathbb{C}}\backslash[0,\infty[
, then

\frac{1}{(-p^{2}+m^{2})^{\frac{\mu}{2}}}
is a tempered
distribution, and one can take its limit from above or below
in the distributional sense:

\frac{1}{(-p^{2}+m^{2}\pm{\rm i}0)^{\frac{\mu}{2}}}:=\lim_{\epsilon\searrow 0}\frac{1}{(-p^{2}+m^{2}\pm{\rm i}\epsilon)^{\frac{\mu}{2}}}.

(3.12)

Thus we obtain
two kinds of Bessel
potentials in the antiEuclidean case:

Theorem 3

\displaystyle G_{\mu,m}^{\mathrm{F}/\overline{\mathrm{F}}}(x)

\displaystyle=\int\frac{{\rm e}^{{\rm i}px}}{(-p^{2}+m^{2}\mp{\rm i}0)^{\frac{\mu}{2}}}\frac{{\rm d}p}{(2\pi)^{d}}

(3.13)

\displaystyle=\frac{\mp{\rm i}(\pm{\rm i})^{d}\pi}{\Gamma(\frac{\mu}{2})(4\pi)^{\frac{d}{2}}}\Big(\frac{|x|}{2m}\Big)^{\frac{\mu-d}{2}}H^{\mp}_{\frac{\mu-d}{2}}(m|x|)

(3.14)

\displaystyle=\frac{\mp{\rm i}{\rm e}^{\pm{\rm i}\frac{\pi\mu}{2}}\pi}{\Gamma(\frac{\mu}{2})(4\pi)^{\frac{d}{2}}}\Big(\frac{|x|}{2m}\Big)^{\frac{\mu-d}{2}}H^{\mp}_{\frac{d-\mu}{2}}(m|x|).

(3.15)

\displaystyle=\frac{{\rm e}^{\pm{\rm i}\pi\frac{d}{2}}\sqrt{\pi}m^{d-\mu}}{\Gamma(\frac{\mu}{2})(4\pi)^{\frac{d}{2}}}U_{\frac{d-\mu}{2}}\Big(\frac{{\rm e}^{\pm{\rm i}\pi}m^{2}x^{2}}{4}\Big).

(3.16)

Proof.
Using (
A.2
) and then
(
2.5
)
we obtain
(
3.16
).
\Box

Note that the Euclidean Bessel potential

G_{\mu,m}
is well defined not only for
m\geq 0
, but also for

{\rm Re}(m)>0
, which guarantees
m^{2}\in{\mathbb{C}}\backslash]-\infty,0]
.
Taking the limit at the imaginary line
we can express the antiEuclidean Bessel potential in terms of the
Euclidean one:

G_{\mu,m}^{\mathrm{F}/\overline{\mathrm{F}}}(x)={\rm e}^{\mp{\rm i}\pi\frac{\mu}{2}}G_{\mu,\pm{\rm i}m}(x).

(3.17)

3.4
Green functions of the Helmholtz equation

Bessel potentials with
\mu=2
are
Green functions of the Helmholtz equation

(-E-\Delta)f(x)=g(x).

(3.18)

More precisely, the Green function for
-E=m^{2}
is

\displaystyle G_{m}(x)

\displaystyle:=\int\frac{{\rm e}^{{\rm i}px}}{(p^{2}+m^{2})}\frac{{\rm d}p}{(2\pi)^{d}}

(3.19)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{\frac{d}{2}}}\Big(\frac{|x|}{m}\Big)^{1-\frac{d}{2}}K_{\frac{d}{2}-1}(m|x|)

(3.20)

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{\pi}m^{d-2}}{(4\pi)^{\frac{d}{2}}}U_{\frac{d}{2}-1}\Big(\frac{m^{2}x^{2}}{4}\Big).

(3.21)

and for
-E=-m^{2}
we have two distinguished Green
functions:

\displaystyle G_{\mp{\rm i}m}(x)

\displaystyle=\int\frac{{\rm e}^{{\rm i}xp}}{(p^{2}-m^{2}\mp{\rm i}0)}\frac{{\rm d}p}{(2\pi)^{d}}

(3.22)

\displaystyle=\pm\frac{{\rm i}}{4}\Big(\frac{m}{2\pi|x|}\Big)^{\frac{d}{2}-1}H_{\frac{d}{2}-1}^{\pm}(m|x|)

(3.23)

\displaystyle=-(-{\rm i})^{d}\frac{\sqrt{\pi}m^{d-2}}{(4\pi)^{\frac{d}{2}}}U_{\frac{d}{2}-1}\Big(-\frac{m^{2}(x^{2}\pm{\rm i}0)}{4}\Big).

(3.24)

G_{\mp{\rm i}m}(x)
coincide with the case
\mu=2
of
the anti-Euclidean Bessel potential
(
3.13
) multiplied
by
-1
.

3.5
Averages of plane waves on sphere

Consider the sphere in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
of radius
m
, denoted

{\mathbb{S}}_{m}^{d-1}={\mathbb{S}}_{m}
. Let
{\rm d}\Omega_{m}
be the natural
measure on
{\mathbb{S}}_{m}
.
As an application of Bessel potentials, we will compute the Fourier
transform of the measure on
{\mathbb{S}}_{m}
.

Theorem 4

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{S}}_{m}}{\rm e}^{{\rm i}px}{\rm d}\Omega_{m}(p)=

\displaystyle 2m^{d-1}\pi^{\frac{d}{2}}{\bf F}_{\frac{d}{2}-1}\Big(-\frac{m^{2}x^{2}}{4}\Big)

(3.25)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle m^{d-1}(2\pi)^{\frac{d}{2}}(m|x|)^{1-\frac{d}{2}}J_{\frac{d}{2}-1}(m|x|).

(3.26)

Proof.
By the Sochocki-Plemejl formula, we have

\delta(|p|-m)=2m\delta(p^{2}-m^{2})=\frac{2m}{2\pi{\rm i}}\Big(\frac{1}{p^{2}-m^{2}-{\rm i}0}-\frac{1}{p^{2}-m^{2}+{\rm i}0}\Big).

(3.27)

Therefore,

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{S}}_{m}}{\rm e}^{{\rm i}px}\Omega_{m}(p)=

\displaystyle\int{\rm e}^{{\rm i}px}\delta(|p|-m){\rm d}p

(3.28)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{2m}{2\pi{\rm i}}\int{\rm e}^{{\rm i}px}\Big(\frac{1}{p^{2}-m^{2}-{\rm i}0}-\frac{1}{p^{2}-m^{2}+{\rm i}0}\Big){\rm d}p

(3.29)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{m(2\pi)^{d}}{\pi{\rm i}}\Big(G_{-{\rm i}m}(x)-G_{{\rm i}m}(x)\Big)

(3.30)

\displaystyle=m^{d-1}\pi^{\frac{d-1}{2}}

\displaystyle\Big((-{\rm i})^{d-1}U_{\frac{d}{2}-1}\Big(\frac{{\rm e}^{-{\rm i}\pi}m^{2}x^{2}}{4}\Big)-{\rm i}^{d-1}U_{\frac{d}{2}-1}\Big(\frac{{\rm e}^{{\rm i}\pi}m^{2}x^{2}}{4}\Big)\Big)

(3.31)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2m^{d-1}\pi^{\frac{d}{2}}{\bf F}_{\frac{d}{2}-1}\Big(-\frac{m^{2}x^{2}}{4}\Big),

(3.32)

where at the end we used (
2.10
).

\Box

Consider a radial function
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\ni p\mapsto f(|p|)
. Its Fourier
transform is also radial. (
3.25
) yields the identity

\displaystyle\int f(|p|){\rm e}^{-{\rm i}px}{\rm d}p=

\displaystyle 2\pi^{\frac{d}{2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}f(k){\bf F}_{\frac{d}{2}-1}\Big(-\frac{k^{2}x^{2}}{4}\Big)k^{d-1}{\rm d}k

(3.33)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(2\pi)^{\frac{d}{2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}f(k)J_{\frac{d}{2}-1}(k|x|)(k|x|)^{-\frac{d}{2}+1}k^{d-1}{\rm d}k,

(3.34)

where
k=|p|

5
The Minkowski signature

The Lorentzian signature is especially important, both
because of its physical relevance and rich mathematical properties.
The spaces

{\mathbb{R}}^{1,d-1}
and
{\mathbb{R}}^{d-1,1}
are two kinds of a
Minkowski space, that is, a pseudo-Euclidean space with a Lorentzian
signature. We will treat
{\mathbb{R}}^{1,d-1}
as the standard form of a Minkowski space.

x^{0}
will denote the first coordinate of
{\mathbb{R}}^{1,d-1}
, which we assume to be timelike (having a negative coefficient in the scalar product).
The remaining, spacelike coordinates will be denoted
\vec{x}
,
so that
x=(x^{0},\vec{x})
. In other words,

x^{2}=-(x^{0})^{2}+\vec{x}^{2}=-(x^{0})^{2}+(x^{1})^{2}+\cdots+(x^{d-1})^{2}.

(5.1)

The future and the past light cone will be denoted

\displaystyle J^{\vee}

\displaystyle:=\{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{1,d-1}\ :\quad x^{2}\leq 0,\quad x^{0}\geq 0\},

\displaystyle J^{\wedge}

\displaystyle:=\{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{1,d-1}\ :\quad x^{2}\leq 0,\quad x^{0}\leq 0\}.

In this section, we will only use the hypergeometric functions
{\bf F}_{\alpha},U_{\alpha}
.

5.1
General exponent

Let
m>0
.
The set
m^{2}+p^{2}
consists of two connected components: the future and
the past mass hyperboloid.
Therefore, the following four regularizations of

\frac{1}{(m^{2}+p^{2})^{\frac{\mu}{2}}}
are tempered distributions invariant wrt the orthochronous Lorentz group:

\frac{1}{(m^{2}+p^{2}\pm{\rm i}0)^{\frac{\mu}{2}}},\quad\frac{1}{(m^{2}+p^{2}\pm{\rm i}0{\rm sgn}p^{0})^{\frac{\mu}{2}}}.

(5.2)

Their inverse Fourier transforms define four kinds of Bessel
potentials:

\displaystyle G_{\mu,m}^{\mathrm{F}/\overline{\mathrm{F}}}(x):=

\displaystyle\int\frac{{\rm e}^{{\rm i}px}}{(m^{2}+p^{2}\mp{\rm i}0{\rm sgn}p^{0})^{\frac{\mu}{2}}}\frac{{\rm d}p}{(2\pi)^{d}}.

Next, we look at each term of the expansion of

in

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}{\rm d}t\int_{0}^{t}{\rm d}r\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-2}}{\rm d}\Omega(r^{2}-t^{2})_{-}^{-\frac{\nu}{2}}\frac{(r-t)^{k}}{k!}\phi^{(k)}(t,t,\Omega)r^{d-2}

For fixed

. Therefore, by Hörmander’s criterion

5.2
Green functions of the Klein Gordon equation

Consider the
Klein-Gordon equation

(-E-\Box)f(x)=g(x),

(5.17)

where
E
is a parameter, usually real. We will consider 3 cases:

massive case:

\displaystyle-E=m^{2},

(5.18)

massless case:

\displaystyle-E=0,

(5.19)

tachyonic case:

\displaystyle-E=-m^{2}.

(5.20)

The massive and massless cases are quite similar and they often appear
in physical applications. They are often discussed in detail in
the literature. The tachyonic case is more exotic and less known, but
also interesting.

The Klein-Gordon equation possesses several useful
Green functions, that is, distributions satisfying

(-E-\Box)G^{\bullet}(x)=\delta(x).

(5.21)

One can try to define
Green functions of the Klein-Gordon equation the Fourier transformation.
Unfortunately, for
E\in{\mathbb{R}}
,
\frac{1}{(-E+p^{2})}
is not a well-defined distribution
because of zeros of its denominator. One way to regularize it is to
add
\pm{\rm i}0
, which leads to the so-called Feynman and anti-Feynman
Green function:

G_{m}^{\mathrm{F}/\overline{\mathrm{F}}}(x)=\int\frac{{\rm e}^{{\rm i}px}}{(-E+p^{2}\mp{\rm i}0)}\frac{{\rm d}p}{(2\pi)^{d}}.

(5.22)

As follows from a general theory of hyperbolic equations,
the Klein-Gordon equation (
5.17
) possesses also another important pair of
Green functions: the retarded (or forward) Green function
G^{\vee}
and
the advanced (backward) Green function
G^{\wedge}
.
They are uniquely
defined by the conditions

{\rm supp}G^{\vee/\wedge}\subset J^{\pm}.

(5.23)

Note that the above definition provides
G^{\vee/\wedge}
for
all
E\in{\mathbb{R}}
. In the case
-E\geq 0
, with
-E=m^{2}
they coincide
with
G_{m}^{\vee/\wedge}
defined already with the help of Fourier
transformation. In the tachyonic case they will be denoted
G_{{\rm i}m}^{\vee/\wedge}=G_{-{\rm i}m}^{\vee/\wedge}

and they need a separate
discussion, see Subsection

5.5

We will also consider certain distinguished
solutions of the (homogeneous) Klein-Gordon equation, that is
functions
G^{\circ}
satisfying

(-E-\Box)G^{\circ}(x)=0.

(5.24)

One can look for them with the ansatz

G^{\circ}(x)=\int{\rm e}^{{\rm i}px}g^{\circ}(p)\delta(-E+p^{2})\frac{{\rm d}p}{(2\pi)^{d-1}},

(5.25)

where
g^{\circ}
is a distribution on
p^{2}-E=0
.
Above, for
E\in{\mathbb{R}}
, we use the notation

\delta(p^{2}-E){\rm d}p=\frac{\delta\Big(p^{0}-\sqrt{\vec{p}^{2}-E}\Big)}{2\sqrt{\vec{p}^{2}-E}}{\rm d}\vec{p}+\frac{\delta\Big(p^{0}+\sqrt{\vec{p}^{2}-E}\Big)}{2\sqrt{\vec{p}^{2}-E}}{\rm d}\vec{p},

(5.26)

where for
\vec{p}^{2}<E
(
5.26
)=0.

Below we consider separately the massive, massless and tachyonic cases
of the Klein-Gordon equation. In all three cases, we will be able
to define

G^{\overline{\mathrm{F}}/\mathrm{F}}
and
G^{\vee/\wedge}.

5.5
Tachyonic Klein-Gordon equation

Let us now consider the
tachyonic Klein-Gordon equation
, which
means, with
E=m^{2}
. Its Green functions satisfy

(-m^{2}-\Box)G^{\bullet}(x)=\delta(x).

(5.53)

Usually, tachyonic quantum fields are considered to be
unphysical
[
21
]
. Nevertheless, every now and then there are attempts to
analyze them in the physics literature, see
[
22
]
, and more
recently
[
23
]
.

We have a minor notational problem how to indicate that we replaced

m^{2}
with
-m^{2}
. Naively, one would think it should be indicated by both
+{\rm i}m
and
-{\rm i}m

instead of
m
. However, this would suggest the analytic continuation

{\rm e}^{{\rm i}\phi},

\pm\phi\in[0,\pi]
, which is not always appropriate.
This problem appears in the case of the Feynman propagator: we will
write
G_{{\rm i}m}^{\mathrm{F}}
, but not
G_{-{\rm i}m}^{\mathrm{F}}
. Similarly, for the
anti-Feynman propagator we will
write
G_{-{\rm i}m}^{\overline{\mathrm{F}}}
, but not
G_{{\rm i}m}^{\overline{\mathrm{F}}}
.
In the case of retarded/advanced propagators, this problem will be
absent, since the analytic continuation can be performed in
m^{2}
:
thus
G_{{\rm i}m}^{\wedge/\vee}=G_{-{\rm i}m}^{\wedge/\vee}
.

We define the Feynman and anti-Feynman Green functions
by adding
\mp{\rm i}0
to the denominator
-m^{2}+p^{2}
in the momentum
representation.
In the following theorem, we compute their form in position variables:

Theorem 15

\displaystyle G_{{\rm i}m}^{\mathrm{F}}(x)/G_{-{\rm i}m}^{\overline{\mathrm{F}}}(x)

\displaystyle=\int\frac{{\rm e}^{{\rm i}px}}{(-m^{2}+p^{2}\mp{\rm i}0)}\frac{{\rm d}p}{(2\pi)^{d}}

(5.54)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\sqrt{\pi}m^{d-2}(\mp{\rm i})^{d+1}}{(4\pi)^{\frac{d}{2}}}U_{\frac{d}{2}-1}\Big(\frac{m^{2}(-x^{2}\mp{\rm i}0)}{4}\Big).

(5.55)

In particular, for
x^{2}>0
we have

\displaystyle G_{{\rm i}m}^{\mathrm{F}}(x)/G_{-{\rm i}m}^{\overline{\mathrm{F}}}(x)=\frac{\pm{\rm i}\sqrt{\pi}m^{d-2}{\rm e}^{\mp{\rm i}\pi(\frac{d}{2}-1)}}{(4\pi)^{\frac{d}{2}}}U_{\frac{d}{2}-1}\Big(\frac{m^{2}(-x^{2}\mp{\rm i}0)}{4}\Big)

and for
x^{2}<0

\displaystyle G_{{\rm i}m}^{\mathrm{F}}(x)/G_{-{\rm i}m}^{\overline{\mathrm{F}}}(x)=\frac{\pm{\rm i}\sqrt{\pi}m^{d-2}{\rm e}^{\mp{\rm i}\pi(\frac{d}{2}-1)}}{(4\pi)^{\frac{d}{2}}}U_{\frac{d}{2}-1}\Big(\frac{m^{2}|x^{2}|}{4}\Big).

Let us start from the usual (positive mass) Feynman
propagator, defined in (
5.28
) and (
5.29
).
Then we continue analytically

G_{m}^{\mathrm{F}}(x)
and
G_{m}^{\overline{\mathrm{F}}}
, replacing
m
with

, where

\phi\in[0,\frac{\pi}{2}]
in the former and
\phi\in[-\frac{\pi}{2},0]

in the latter case. (Note that during the analytic continuation
the denominator has to have a constant sign of its imaginary part,
that is,
\pm{\rm Im}(m^{2}{\rm e}^{2{\rm i}\phi}+{\rm i}0)>0

The analytic continuation yields

\displaystyle G^{\mathrm{F}}_{{\rm i}m}(x)/G^{\overline{\mathrm{F}}}_{-{\rm i}m}(x)=

\displaystyle\frac{\pm{\rm i}\sqrt{\pi}m^{d-2}{\rm e}^{\pm{\rm i}\pi(\frac{d}{2}-1)}}{(4\pi)^{\frac{d}{2}}}U_{\frac{d}{2}-1}\Big(\frac{{\rm e}^{\pm{\rm i}\pi}m^{2}(x^{2}\pm{\rm i}0)}{4}\Big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\pm{\rm i}\sqrt{\pi}m^{d-2}{\rm e}^{\pm{\rm i}\pi(\frac{d}{2}-1)}}{(4\pi)^{\frac{d}{2}}}U_{\frac{d}{2}-1}\Big(\frac{m^{2}(-x^{2}\mp{\rm i}0)}{4}\Big),

(5.57)

which coincides with (

\Box

Feynman and antiFeynman propagator do not have the usual

[
22
]
, try to use the above Feynman propagator

(5.64)

\displaystyle G_{{\rm i}m,{\rm reg}}^{\vee/\wedge}(x)

\displaystyle=\theta(\pm x^{0})\frac{2\pi m^{d-2}}{(4\pi)^{\frac{d}{2}}}{\bf F}_{\frac{d}{2}-1}\Big(\frac{m^{2}|x^{2}|}{4}\Big)\theta(-x^{2}).

(5.65)

Proof.
Our starting point
is the formula (
5.31
) for
the forward and
backward propagator
G_{m}^{\vee/\wedge}(x)
.
They are analytic in

m
.
Therefore, we can apply the analytic continuation

m\mapsto{\rm e}^{{\rm i}\frac{\pi}{2}}m
:

\displaystyle G_{{\rm e}^{{\rm i}\frac{\pi}{2}}m}^{\vee/\wedge}(x)=

\displaystyle\theta(\pm x^{0})\frac{-{\rm i}\sqrt{\pi}m^{d-2}{\rm e}^{{\rm i}\pi(\frac{d}{2}-1)}}{(4\pi)^{\frac{d}{2}}}\Bigg(U_{\frac{d}{2}-1}\Big(\frac{{\rm e}^{{\rm i}\pi}(m^{2}x^{2}-{\rm i}0)}{4}\Big)

\displaystyle\qquad-U_{\frac{d}{2}-1}\Big(\frac{{\rm e}^{{\rm i}\pi}(m^{2}x^{2}+{\rm i}0)}{4}\Big)\Bigg).

(5.66)

This yields (
5.58
).
Alternatively, we can apply
the analytic continuation

m\mapsto{\rm e}^{-{\rm i}\frac{\pi}{2}}m
, which yields (
5.59
).
\Box

Let us
compute the sum of the tachyonic Feynman and antiFeynman propagator:

\displaystyle G_{{\rm i}m}^{\mathrm{F}}(x)+G_{-{\rm i}m}^{\overline{\mathrm{F}}}(x)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\frac{2\pi m^{d-2}}{(4\pi)^{\frac{d}{2}}}{\bf F}_{\frac{d}{2}-1}\Big(-\frac{m^{2}x^{2}}{4}\Big),&\quad x^{2}>0;\\
\frac{4\pi}{(4\pi)^{\frac{d}{2}}}\Big(\frac{-x^{2}}{4}\Big)^{1-\frac{d}{2}}{\bf F}_{1-\frac{d}{2}}\Big(-\frac{m^{2}x^{2}}{4}\Big),&\quad x^{2}<0.\end{cases}

(5.67)

Thus
G_{{\rm i}m}^{\mathrm{F}}(x)+G_{-{\rm i}m}^{\overline{\mathrm{F}}}(x)
does not have a causal support, and consequently,

G_{{\rm i}m}^{\mathrm{F}}(x)+G_{-{\rm i}m}^{\overline{\mathrm{F}}}(x)\neq G_{{\rm i}m}^{\vee}(x)+G_{{\rm i}m}^{\wedge}(x).

(5.68)

The equality in (
5.68
) holds only for
x^{2}<0
.

Note that because of (
5.68
) we could not deduce the formulas of the forward and backward
propagators from the Feynman and anti-Feynman propagators, and we had
to apply a separate argument based on analytic continuation.

In the tachyonic case, we do not have the solutions

G_{{\rm i}m}^{(\pm)}
. However, we can define the Pauli-Jordan propagator

\displaystyle G_{{\rm i}m}^{\mathrm{PJ}}(x)=G_{-{\rm i}m}^{\mathrm{PJ}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d-1}}\int{\rm d}\vec{p}\,{\rm e}^{{\rm i}\vec{x}\vec{p}}\frac{\sin\left(x^{0}\sqrt{\vec{p}^{2}-m^{2}}\right)}{\sqrt{\vec{p}^{2}-m^{2}}}

(5.69)

\displaystyle={\rm sgn}(x^{0})\frac{2\pi}{(4\pi)^{\frac{d}{2}}}\Big(\frac{x^{2}}{4}\Big)_{-}^{1-\frac{d}{2}}{\bf F}_{1-\frac{d}{2}}\Big(\frac{m^{2}|x^{2}|}{4}\Big).

(5.70)

Note that
G_{{\rm i}m}^{\mathrm{PJ}}
cannot be written in the form
(
5.25
).

Among the identities (
5.44
) only (
5.44a
) is still true.

5.6
Averages of plane waves on the hyperbolic plane

The Minkowski space possesses two kinds of hyperboloids. The
two-sheeted hyperboloid consists of two connected components isomorphic
to the hyperbolic space. In this subsection, we compute the Fourier transform of the natural measure on one
of the sheets of the two-sheeted hyperboloids, similarly as in the Theorem
4
.

Consider the
future/past hyperboloid
in the
d
-dimensional Minkowski space, denoted

{\mathbb{H}}_{\pm,m}={\mathbb{H}}_{\pm,m}^{d-1}
, consisting of points
p
such that

p^{2}+m^{2}=0
and
\pm p^{0}>0
. Let
{\rm d}\Omega_{m}
denote the standard
measure on
{\mathbb{H}}_{\pm,m}
. We will see that up to a coefficient
its Fourier transform is
essentially the “positive frequency solution of the Klein-Gordon equation.”

Theorem 17

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{H}}_{\pm,m}}{\rm e}^{{\rm i}px}{\rm d}\Omega_{m}(p)=m^{d-1}\pi^{\frac{d-1}{2}}U_{\frac{d}{2}-1}\Big(\frac{m^{2}(x^{2}\pm{\rm i}{\rm sgn}x^{0}0)}{4}\Big).

(5.71)

Proof.
This average, up to a coefficient, coincides with
G_{m}^{(\pm)}

defined in (
5.40
),
which we have already computed:

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{H}}_{\pm,m}}{\rm e}^{{\rm i}px}{\rm d}\Omega_{m}(p)

\displaystyle=2m\int{\rm e}^{{\rm i}px}\theta(\pm p^{0})\delta(p^{2}+m^{2}){\rm d}p

(5.72)

\displaystyle=(2\pi)^{d}mG_{m}^{(\pm)}(x).

(5.73)

Therefore, it is enough to use the formula
(
5.43
).
\Box

5.7
Averages of plane waves on the deSitter space

The one-sheeted hyperboloid in the physics literature is usually
called the
deSitter space
. It will be denoted
\mathrm{dS}_{m}=\mathrm{dS}_{m}^{d-1}
.
It consists of points
p
such that
p^{2}=m^{2}
. Let
{\rm d}\Omega_{m}
denote
the standard measure on
\mathrm{dS}_{m}
. We will compute the Fourier
transform of the measure on
\mathrm{dS}_{m}
.

Theorem 18

\displaystyle\int_{\mathrm{dS}_{m}}{\rm e}^{{\rm i}px}{\rm d}\Omega_{m}(p)=

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle m^{d-1}\pi^{\frac{d-1}{2}}\Bigg({\rm i}^{d}U_{\frac{d}{2}-1}\Big(\frac{m^{2}(-x^{2}+{\rm i}0)}{4}\Big)+(-{\rm i})^{d}U_{\frac{d}{2}-1}\Big(\frac{m^{2}(-x^{2}-{\rm i}0)}{4}\Big)\Bigg)

(5.74)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\begin{cases}(-1)^{\frac{d}{2}}2m^{d-1}\pi^{\frac{d-1}{2}}\Bigg(U_{\frac{d}{2}-1}\Big(\frac{-m^{2}x^{2}\pm{\rm i}0}{4}\Big)\pm\sqrt{\pi}{\rm i}\big(\tfrac{-x^{2}}{4}\big)_{-}^{1-\frac{d}{2}}{\bf F}_{1-\frac{d}{2}}\Big(-\frac{m^{2}x^{2}}{4}\Big)\Bigg),&\frac{d}{2}\in{\mathbb{N}},\\
2{\rm i}^{d-1}m^{d-1}\pi^{\frac{d}{2}}\big(\tfrac{-x^{2}}{4}\big)_{-}^{1-\frac{d}{2}}{\bf F}_{1-\frac{d}{2}}\Big(-\frac{m^{2}x^{2}}{4}\Big),&\frac{d}{2}\notin{\mathbb{N}}.\end{cases}

(5.75)

Proof.

\displaystyle\int_{\mathrm{dS}_{m}}{\rm e}^{{\rm i}px}{\rm d}\Omega_{m}(p)

\displaystyle=2m\int{\rm e}^{{\rm i}px}\delta(p^{2}-m^{2}){\rm d}p

(5.76)

\displaystyle=\frac{m}{\pi{\rm i}}\int{\rm e}^{{\rm i}px}\Big(\frac{1}{p^{2}-m^{2}-{\rm i}0}-\frac{1}{p^{2}-m^{2}+{\rm i}0}\Big){\rm d}p

(5.77)

\displaystyle=\frac{m(2\pi)^{d}}{\pi{\rm i}}\big(G_{{\rm i}m}^{\mathrm{F}}(x)-G_{-{\rm i}m}^{\overline{\mathrm{F}}}(x)\big).

(5.78)

Then we can use the result for the tachyonic Feynman and anti-Feynman propagator (
5.55
).

\Box

A.2
Distributions

In this paper, we often use the language of distributions on
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
.
We say that a distribution
T
is
regular
if there exists a
locally integrable function
f
such that for a test function
\Phi

T(\Phi)=\int f(x)\Phi(x){\rm d}x.

(A.5)

We will use the integral notation also for irregular distributions,
e.g.

\int\delta^{(n)}(x)\Phi(x){\rm d}x=(-1)^{n}\Phi^{(n)}(0).

(A.6)

Let us now consider some special distributions on
{\mathbb{R}}
.
For any
\lambda\in{\mathbb{C}}

(\pm{\rm i}x+0)^{\lambda}={\rm e}^{\pm{\rm i}\lambda\frac{\pi}{2}}(x\mp{\rm i}0)^{\lambda}:=\lim_{\epsilon\searrow 0}(\pm{\rm i}x+\epsilon)^{\lambda}.

is a tempered distribution. If
{\rm Re}\lambda>-1
, then it is regular and
given by the locally integrable function

{\rm e}^{\pm{\rm i}{\rm sgn}(x)\frac{\pi}{2}\lambda}|x|^{\lambda}.

(A.7)

The functions

x_{\pm}^{\lambda}:=|x|^{\lambda}\theta(\pm x)

(A.8)

define regular distributions only for
{\rm Re}\lambda>-1
. We can extend them
to
\lambda\in{\mathbb{C}}
except for
\lambda=-1,-2,\dots
by putting

\displaystyle x_{\pm}^{\lambda}

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{2{\rm i}\sin\pi\lambda}\Big(-{\rm e}^{-{\rm i}\frac{\pi}{2}\lambda}(\mp{\rm i}x+0)^{\lambda}+{\rm e}^{{\rm i}\frac{\pi}{2}\lambda}(\pm{\rm i}x+0)^{\lambda}\Big).

(A.9)

For
\lambda>-1
(
A.9
) are regular and coincide with

\theta(\pm x)|x|^{\lambda}
.
We have

x_{\pm}^{\lambda+1}=|x|\cdot x_{\pm}^{\lambda}.

(A.10)

Instead of
x_{\pm}^{\lambda}
, it is often more convenient to consider

\displaystyle\rho_{\pm}^{\lambda}(x)

\displaystyle:=\frac{x_{\pm}^{\lambda}}{\Gamma(\lambda+1)}

(A.11)

\displaystyle=\frac{\Gamma(-\lambda)}{2\pi{\rm i}}\Big({\rm e}^{-{\rm i}\frac{\pi}{2}\lambda}(\mp{\rm i}x+0)^{\lambda}-{\rm e}^{{\rm i}\frac{\pi}{2}\lambda}(\pm{\rm i}x+0)^{\lambda}\Big).

(A.12)

Note that using (
A.11
) and (
A.12
) we have defined
\rho_{\pm}^{\lambda}
for all
\lambda\in{\mathbb{C}}
.
We have

{\rm\partial}_{x}\rho_{\pm}^{\lambda}(x)=\pm\rho_{\pm}^{\lambda-1}(x).

At integers we have

\displaystyle\rho_{\pm}^{n}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{x_{\pm}^{n}}{n!},\ \ \ n=0,1,\dots;

(A.13)

\displaystyle\rho_{\pm}^{-n-1}(x)

\displaystyle=(\pm 1)^{n}\delta^{n}(x),\ \ \ n=0,1,\dots.

(A.14)

Clearly, for
{\rm Re}(\lambda)\leq-1
the distributions
\rho_{\pm}^{\lambda}

are irregular.
