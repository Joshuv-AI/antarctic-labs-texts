Abstract
Balanced truncation is a well-established model order reduction method which has been applied to a variety of problems. Recently, a connection between linear Gaussian Bayesian inference problems and the system-theoretic concept of balanced truncation has been drawn Qian2021Balancing . Although this connection is new, the application of balanced truncation to data assimilation is not a novel idea: it has already been used in four-dimensional variational data assimilation (4D-Var). This paper discusses the application of balanced truncation to linear Gaussian Bayesian inference, and, in particular, the 4D-Var method, thereby strengthening the link between systems theory and data assimilation further. Similarities between both types of data assimilation problems enable a generalisation of the state-of-the-art approach to the use of arbitrary prior covariances as reachability Gramians. Furthermore, we propose an enhanced approach using time-limited balanced truncation that allows to balance Bayesian inference for unstable systems and in addition improves the numerical results for short observation periods.

Introduction

One of the greatest challenges of 21st century mathematics is integrating large amounts of data into computational models to obtain novel insights into the state and dynamics of a system. This is the typical framework for data assimilation. Data assimilation combines available, mostly high-dimensional, models with observations at certain time steps, often through time-dependent PDEs. These models occur in various domains of application, such as meteorology
Dee1991Meteor
;
Ghil1989Meteor
;
Ghil1991Meteor
, geosciences
Carr2018Geosc
;
Fletcher2017GeoSc
;
Park2017GeoSc
, and medicine
Engbert2020Med
;
Kost2011Med
. They are often unstable.

The typical approach to data assimilation problems is Bayesian inference; see, for instance,
Dashti2017BIPSurvey
;
Law2015DA
;
Taran2005BIPbook
. We consider the parameters
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
to be the realisations of a random variable. The distribution of said random variable is what we want to infer. Some
prior
information is given. The time-dependent observations
\boldsymbol{m}
are related to the parameters via a given forward model operator
\boldsymbol{G}
with additive noise
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}
:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{m}=\boldsymbol{G}(\boldsymbol{x})+\boldsymbol{\epsilon}.

(1)

From (
1
), the likelihood
\boldsymbol{m}|\boldsymbol{x}
is derived. Combining this with the measurements, the prior is updated to the posterior distribution of interest
\boldsymbol{x}|\boldsymbol{m}
. Usually, the initial state of a dynamical system, or rather its distribution, is to be inferred.

Often, the state is high-dimensional and therefore forward model simulations are expensive. This problem is well known from systems and control theory, where reduced-order modelling has been established; see, e.g.,
Antoulas2005Book
;
Benner2015Survey
;
Benner2017MORBook
. Both non-intrusive data-based methods
Benner2020NonInt
;
Pehers2016NonInt
and intrusive model-based methods
Antoulas2020interpolatory
;
Gugercin2004Survey
have been in the spotlight of past and current research. Model-based approaches are promising in the data assimilation context. One such method is balanced truncation (BT) reducing a stable, linear time-invariant system by projecting it onto a subspace of simultaneously easily reachable and observable states. Variants for time-varying systems
Lang2016TimeVar
;
Sandberg2004TimeVar
, inhomogeneous systems
Beattie2016inhom
;
Heink2011inhom
, bilinear systems
Benner2017BilinBT
;
Duff2019BilinBT
and even nonlinear systems
kramer2022nonlinear
;
kramer2022nonlinear2
have been developed. The application of BT to data assimilation has appeared first in the context of 4D-Var
BoessDiss
;
GreenDiss
;
Lawless20084D-Varproposal
;
Lawless2008Strong4DVarBT
.

Recently, Qian et al.
Qian2021Balancing
have reinvigorated interest in the connection between system-theoretic model order reduction and Bayesian inference. Balanced truncation for linear Gaussian Bayesian inference has been considered. In this paper, we relate the results of Qian et al. to the application of BT to 4D-Var. We generalise the existing results on BT for linear Gaussian Bayesian inference to arbitrary Gaussian priors and unstable systems by proposing time-limited balanced truncation (TLBT) for Bayesian inference. The proposed method improves on the state-of-the-art approach, especially for small observation intervals appearing in many data assimilation applications.

The remainder of the paper is structured as follows: At the beginning, the frameworks for two data assimilation problems and the basics of BT are presented; Sect.
2
Sect.
3
introduces how time-limited balanced truncation is applied in these situations. Sect.
4
presents numerical results before the paper is concluded in Sect.
5
with the discussion of directions for future work.

Setting and Background

We consider the linear dynamical system

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}}{\mathrm{d}t}=\boldsymbol{Ax},

(2a)

where
\boldsymbol{x}(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is the state of the system at time
t\in\mathbb{R}
, and
\boldsymbol{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
is a linear model operator describing the state evolution. The initial state
\boldsymbol{x}(0)=\boldsymbol{x}_{0}
is unknown. Noise-polluted outputs
\boldsymbol{m}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{out}}}
of the system are only available at discrete time points
0<t_{1}<\ldots<t_{n}
via the measurement model

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{m}_{k}=\boldsymbol{C}\boldsymbol{x}(t_{k})+\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{k}=\boldsymbol{C}e^{\boldsymbol{A}t_{k}}\boldsymbol{x}_{0}+\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{k},

(2b)

with
\boldsymbol{C}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{out}}\times d}
the state-to-output operator, typically with
d_{\mathrm{out}}\ll d
. The noise
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{k}\sim\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\epsilon})
is independently and identically distributed additive Gaussian with known positive definite covariance
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\epsilon}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{out}}\times d_{\mathrm{out}}}
. The observation interval of the system is
[0,t_{n}]
. We always start at time 0, with the unknown initial condition, and set the end time to
t_{e}=t_{n}
.

The data assimilation task is to infer the unknown initial state from the noisy measurements. To this end, the Bayesian statistical approach is applied and the initial condition is assumed to be a-priori Gaussian distributed:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{x}_{0}\sim\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\mathrm{pr}}),

(2c)

with
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\mathrm{pr}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
a given positive definite prior covariance matrix.

Substituting the Gaussian prior (
2c
) in the linear Gaussian measurement model (
2b
) yields a Gaussian likelihood for the measurements conditioned on the initial condition and the measurement times
\boldsymbol{t}=[t_{1},\ldots,t_{n}]^{\mathrm{T}}
:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{m}|(\boldsymbol{x}_{0},\boldsymbol{t})\sim\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{G}\boldsymbol{x}_{0},\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\mathrm{obs}}),

where
\boldsymbol{m}\in\mathbb{R}^{nd_{\mathrm{out}}}
,
\boldsymbol{G}\in\mathbb{R}^{nd_{\mathrm{out}}\times d}
, and
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\mathrm{obs}}\in\mathbb{R}^{nd_{\mathrm{out}}\times nd_{\mathrm{out}}}
as follows:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{m}=\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{m}_{1}\\
\vdots\\
\boldsymbol{m}_{n}\end{bmatrix},\kern 5.0pt\boldsymbol{G}=\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{C}e^{\boldsymbol{A}t_{1}}\\
\vdots\\
\boldsymbol{C}e^{\boldsymbol{A}t_{n}}\end{bmatrix},\kern 5.0pt\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\mathrm{obs}}=\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}&&&\\
&\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}&&\\
&&\ddots&\\
&&&\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}\end{bmatrix}.

For a Gaussian prior and a Gaussian likelihood, the posterior is again Gaussian
Carlin2009BayesMeth
:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{x}_{0}|(\boldsymbol{m},\boldsymbol{t})

\displaystyle\sim\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\mathrm{pos}},\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\mathrm{pos}}),\kern 5.0pt\text{where}

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\mathrm{pos}}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\mathrm{pos}}\boldsymbol{G}^{\mathrm{T}}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\mathrm{obs}}^{-1}\boldsymbol{m}\kern 5.0pt\text{and}\kern 5.0pt\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\mathrm{pos}}=(\boldsymbol{H}+\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\mathrm{pr}}^{-1})^{-1}.

(3)

The matrix
\boldsymbol{H}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
is called the Fisher information of
\boldsymbol{m}

LehmCase1998PointEst
and defined as

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{H}=\boldsymbol{G}^{\mathrm{T}}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\mathrm{obs}}^{-1}\boldsymbol{G}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\boldsymbol{G}_{k}^{\mathrm{T}}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}^{-1}\boldsymbol{G}_{k}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}e^{\boldsymbol{A}^{\mathrm{T}}t_{k}}\boldsymbol{C}^{\mathrm{T}}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}^{-1}\boldsymbol{C}e^{\boldsymbol{A}t_{k}}.

The advantage of this data assimilation setting is that the posterior statistics (
2
) can be obtained in a closed form. The challenge is the computation of the posterior mean and covariance for a high-dimensional state space, that is for
d
large. This implies several multiplications with the high-dimensional forward map
\boldsymbol{G}
and its transpose, which might only be available through costly evolution of the underlying dynamical system. Benner, Qiu, and Stoll have proposed a low-rank compression of the posterior covariance
Benner2018LRCov
. Other than this, the usually small dimension
d_{\mathrm{out}}
of the observations suggests that the dynamics of interest may be modelled accurately when restricted to a low-dimensional subspace. Model order reduction approaches, such as those presented in this paper, aim to reduce the dimensionality of the problem to make computations more efficient while maintaining sufficient accuracy.

2.1
Incremental Four Dimensional Variational Data Assimilation (4D-Var)

4D-Var is a variational data assimilation technique originating in weather forecasting
Sasaki70Basic
. In 4D-Var, instead of a continuous model evolution, a discrete time-varying system for
k=0,1,\ldots,n
is considered:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{x}_{k+1}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{A}_{k}(\boldsymbol{x}_{k}),

(4a)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{m}_{k}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{C}_{k}(\boldsymbol{x}_{k})+\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{k},

(4b)

where
\boldsymbol{x}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is the state of the system at time
t_{k}\in\{0,\ldots,t_{n}\}
, and
\mathcal{A}_{k}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
is the nonlinear model operator that describes the evolution of the state from time
t_{k}
to
t_{k+1}
. The measurement model suffers from centered Gaussian observational noise
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{k}\sim\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{R}_{k})
for
k=0,\ldots,n
.
The goal is again to infer the initial state
\boldsymbol{x}_{0}
from the noisy measurements
\{\boldsymbol{m}_{k}\}_{k=0,\ldots,n}
. We assume that an a-priori estimate
\boldsymbol{x}_{0}^{b}
of the initial state is given—the so-called
background state
—which is error-prone:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{x}_{0}-\boldsymbol{x}_{0}^{b}=\boldsymbol{e}_{0}\sim\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\mathrm{pr}}).

(4c)

Linear Gaussian Bayesian inference and 4D-Var are closely related: discretising (
2
) with equispaced points in time
t_{k}=k\cdot h,\,k=0,\ldots,n
and
t_{n}=t_{e}
, we obtain a system of the more general type (
4
) with
\mathcal{A}_{k}=\boldsymbol{A}_{\mathrm{disc}}=e^{\boldsymbol{A}h}
and
\mathcal{C}_{k}=\boldsymbol{C}
for all
k
. A closed form solution to the posterior statistics for the initial state can only be computed for systems of type (
4
) if
\mathcal{A}_{k}
is linear and time-invariant. In 4D-Var, for arbitrary
\mathcal{A}_{k}
, the maximum a posteriori estimate for
\boldsymbol{x}_{0}
, i.e.,

\boldsymbol{x}_{0}^{*}=\arg\min_{\boldsymbol{x}_{0}}J(\boldsymbol{x}_{0})
,
is obtained by minimising the cost functional derived from the posterior distribution; see, for instance,
Melina2020GAMM
:

\displaystyle J(\boldsymbol{x}_{0})=\frac{1}{2}\left\|\boldsymbol{x}_{0}-\boldsymbol{x}_{0}^{b}\right\|^{2}_{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\mathrm{pr}}^{-1}}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=0}^{n}\left\|\boldsymbol{m}_{k}-\mathcal{C}_{k}(\boldsymbol{x}_{k})\right\|^{2}_{\boldsymbol{R}_{k}^{-1}}

(5a)

subject to the forward model dynamics

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{x}_{k+1}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{A}_{k}(\boldsymbol{x}_{k})\kern 5.0pt\text{for }k=0,\ldots,n-1,

(5b)

where
\boldsymbol{x}_{k}
is the state at time step
t_{k}
and
\left\|\boldsymbol{v}\right\|_{\boldsymbol{M}}^{2}:=\boldsymbol{v}^{\mathrm{T}}\boldsymbol{M}\boldsymbol{v}
. Evaluating the functional
J(\boldsymbol{x}_{0})
is very costly because of the nonlinearity of the operators
\mathcal{A}_{k}
and
\mathcal{C}_{k}
. Incremental 4D-Var overcomes this by using consecutive linearised versions of the operators and minimising a quadratic functional
Courtier1994Incremental
. This approach to minimising (
5
) is an inexact Gauss-Newton method
Lawless2005_GaussNewton
. Linearisations of
\mathcal{A}_{k}
and
\mathcal{C}_{k}
about the model state
\boldsymbol{x}_{k}
lead to Jacobians
\boldsymbol{A}_{k}
and
\boldsymbol{C}_{k}
in the so-called
tangent linear model
:

\displaystyle\delta\boldsymbol{x}_{k+1}=\boldsymbol{A}_{k}\delta\boldsymbol{x}_{k},\kern 5.0pt\boldsymbol{d}_{k}=\boldsymbol{C}_{k}\delta\boldsymbol{x}_{k},

(6)

where
\boldsymbol{d}_{k}:=\boldsymbol{m}_{k}-\mathcal{C}_{k}(\boldsymbol{x}_{k})
for all
k
and
\delta\boldsymbol{x}_{k}
denotes a state perturbation. A discussion of how the observation and prior uncertainties influence (
6
) follows in Sect.
3.1
The corresponding quadratic cost functional that has to be minimised for
\delta\boldsymbol{x}_{0}
is given by

\displaystyle\tilde{J}(\delta\boldsymbol{x}_{0})=\frac{1}{2}\left\|\delta\boldsymbol{x}_{0}-[\boldsymbol{x}_{0}-\boldsymbol{x}_{0}^{b}]\right\|^{2}_{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\mathrm{pr}}^{-1}}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=0}^{n}\left\|\boldsymbol{C}_{k}\delta\boldsymbol{x}_{k}-\boldsymbol{d}_{k}\right\|^{2}_{\boldsymbol{R}_{k}^{-1}}

(7a)

subject to the forward model dynamics

\displaystyle\delta\boldsymbol{x}_{k+1}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{A}_{k}\delta\boldsymbol{x}_{k}\kern 5.0pt\text{for }k=0,\ldots,n-1.

(7b)

inding the minimum
\delta\boldsymbol{x}_{0}^{*}
of (
7
) is called
inner loop
. The result of the inner loop is the next increment
\delta\boldsymbol{x}_{0}^{(i)}
used to update
\boldsymbol{x}_{0}^{(i+1)}=\boldsymbol{x}_{0}^{(i)}+\delta\boldsymbol{x}_{0}^{(i)}
in the so-called
outer loop
. The trajectory
\boldsymbol{x}^{(i)}=[\boldsymbol{x}_{0}^{(i)},\boldsymbol{x}_{1}^{(i)},\ldots,\boldsymbol{x}_{n}^{(i)}]
and expected observations are obtained by evaluating the nonlinear model based on the current step’s initial condition
\boldsymbol{x}_{0}^{(i)}
.

Despite the promising idea of obtaining
\boldsymbol{x}_{0}^{*}
incrementally, the computation of (
7
) is still very costly in high-dimensional settings, even in the linear case. For this reason, the system (
6
) should be reduced to obtain lower-dimensional matrices replacing
\boldsymbol{A}_{k}
,
\boldsymbol{C}_{k}
and
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\mathrm{pr}}
in (
7

Projection-based methods are popular in model order reduction for dynamical systems

2.2.1
System-Theoretic Basics

Balanced truncation (BT) is usually considered for a linear time invariant (LTI) system with continuous (
t\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
) or discrete (
t\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
) state equation as follows:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\frac{\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}(t)}{\mathrm{d}t}&=\boldsymbol{Ax}(t)+\boldsymbol{Bu}(t)\kern 5.0pt\text{ or }\kern 5.0pt\boldsymbol{x}(t+1)=\boldsymbol{Ax}(t)+\boldsymbol{Bu}(t),\end{split}

(8a)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\boldsymbol{y}(t)&=\boldsymbol{Cx}(t),\end{split}

(8b)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\boldsymbol{x}(0)&=\boldsymbol{0}.\end{split}

(8c)

The output equation and the initial condition are unified for both cases. Here,
\boldsymbol{x},\,\boldsymbol{A}
, and
\boldsymbol{C}
are as in (
2
). The input of the system
\boldsymbol{u}(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{in}}}
is through the port
\boldsymbol{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d_{\mathrm{in}}}
. The observer output
\boldsymbol{y}(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{out}}}
is without any noise. We, exceptionally, use the notation
\boldsymbol{x}(t+1)
instead of
\boldsymbol{x}_{k+1}
for discrete time in this subsection to unify the concept of BT for discrete and continuous time. It is important to notice, however, that the physical meaning of the system matrices
\boldsymbol{A}
and
\boldsymbol{B}
differs from continuous time to discrete time: when we discretise the continuous-time system
\frac{\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}(t)}{\mathrm{d}t}=\boldsymbol{A}_{\text{cont}}\boldsymbol{x}(t)+\boldsymbol{B}_{\text{cont}}\boldsymbol{u}(t)
to
\boldsymbol{x}(t+1)=\boldsymbol{A}_{\text{disc}}\boldsymbol{x}(t)+\boldsymbol{B}_{\text{disc}}\boldsymbol{u}(t)
, it holds that
\boldsymbol{A}_{\text{disc}}=e^{\boldsymbol{A}_{\text{cont}}}
and
\boldsymbol{B}_{\text{disc}}=\int_{0}^{1}e^{\boldsymbol{A}_{\text{cont}}\tau}\mathrm{d}\tau\boldsymbol{B}_{\text{cont}}
under the assumption that
\boldsymbol{u}(t)
is constant on each interval
[k,k+1),\,k\in\mathbb{N}
.

The idea of BT is to truncate the system by removing the states that are difficult to reach and difficult to observe. The concepts of reachability and observability rely on the so-called reachability and observability Gramians, spanning the (sub)spaces of reachable and observable states. The Gramians are functions of time and the
time-limited reachability Gramian

\boldsymbol{P}(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
at time
0<t<\infty
is defined by

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{P}(t)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}e^{\boldsymbol{A}\tau}\boldsymbol{B}\boldsymbol{B}^{\mathrm{T}}e^{\boldsymbol{A}^{\mathrm{T}}\tau}\mathrm{d}\tau\kern 5.0pt\text{for }t\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\text{ and }

(9a)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{P}(t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{t-1}\boldsymbol{A}^{k}\boldsymbol{B}\boldsymbol{B}^{\mathrm{T}}(\boldsymbol{A}^{\mathrm{T}})^{k}\kern 5.0pt\text{for }t\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}.

(9b)

The notion of observability of states at a fixed time
t
is dual to reachability. The
time-limited observability Gramian

\boldsymbol{Q}(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
at time
0<t<\infty
is defined by

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{Q}(t)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}e^{\boldsymbol{A}^{\mathrm{T}}\tau}\boldsymbol{C}^{\mathrm{T}}\boldsymbol{C}e^{\boldsymbol{A}\tau}\mathrm{d}\tau\kern 5.0pt\text{for }t\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\text{ and }

(10a)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{Q}(t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{t-1}(\boldsymbol{A}^{\mathrm{T}})^{k}\boldsymbol{C}^{\mathrm{T}}\boldsymbol{C}\boldsymbol{A}^{k}\kern 5.0pt\text{for }t\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}.

(10b)

The Gramians are associated with the reachability energy and the observability energy of a state, respectively:

\displaystyle\left\|\boldsymbol{x}\right\|_{[\boldsymbol{P}(t)]^{-1}}^{2}=\boldsymbol{x}^{\mathrm{T}}[\boldsymbol{P}(t)]^{-1}\boldsymbol{x},\kern 5.0pt\kern 5.0pt\left\|\boldsymbol{x}\right\|_{\boldsymbol{Q}(t)}^{2}=\boldsymbol{x}^{\mathrm{T}}\boldsymbol{Q}(t)\boldsymbol{x},

(11)

where
\left\|\boldsymbol{x}\right\|_{[\boldsymbol{P}(t)]^{-1}}^{2}
is the minimal energy necessary to reach the state
\boldsymbol{x}
at time
t
and
\left\|\boldsymbol{x}\right\|_{\boldsymbol{Q}(t)}^{2}
is the maximal energy produced by the output of an observable state
\boldsymbol{x}
at time
t
. The time-limited Gramians are, thus, functions of time informing us how much energy is necessary to reach or observe a state at a fixed, finite time
t
.

By monitoring the system from initial time to infinity, it is possible to determine how much energy is required to reach or observe a state at
any
point in time. The limits of the finite Gramians (
9
) and (
10
) exist only under certain restrictive conditions: the system is required to be
stable
, i.e., the eigenvalues of
\boldsymbol{A}
lie in the open left half plane for the continuous-time setting or lie inside the unit circle for the discrete-time setting. Then,
e^{\boldsymbol{A}\tau}
is bounded for
\tau\rightarrow\infty
and, hence,
\boldsymbol{P}(t)
and
\boldsymbol{Q}(t)
are bounded for
t\rightarrow\infty
and their limits
\boldsymbol{P}_{\infty}=\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\boldsymbol{P}(t)
and
\boldsymbol{Q}_{\infty}=\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\boldsymbol{Q}(t)
exist; similarly for the discrete-time case. The stable LTI system (
8
) has
infinite reachability Gramian

\boldsymbol{P}_{\infty}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
defined by

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{P}_{\infty}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{\boldsymbol{A}\tau}\boldsymbol{B}\boldsymbol{B}^{\mathrm{T}}e^{\boldsymbol{A}^{\mathrm{T}}\tau}\mathrm{d}\tau\kern 5.0pt\text{ for }t\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\text{ and }

(12a)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{P}_{\infty}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\boldsymbol{A}^{k}\boldsymbol{B}\boldsymbol{B}^{\mathrm{T}}(\boldsymbol{A}^{\mathrm{T}})^{k}\kern 5.0pt\text{ for }t\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}.

(12b)

Besides, it has
infinite observability Gramian

\boldsymbol{Q}_{\infty}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
defined by

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{Q}_{\infty}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{\boldsymbol{A}^{\mathrm{T}}\tau}\boldsymbol{C}^{\mathrm{T}}\boldsymbol{C}e^{\boldsymbol{A}\tau}\mathrm{d}\tau\kern 5.0pt\text{ for }t\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\text{ and }

(13a)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{Q}_{\infty}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}(\boldsymbol{A}^{\mathrm{T}})^{k}\boldsymbol{C}^{\mathrm{T}}\boldsymbol{C}\boldsymbol{A}^{k}\kern 5.0pt\text{ for }t\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}.

(13b)

The advantage of infinite Gramians is that they can be computed efficiently as the solutions to the continuous-time Lyapunov equations
\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{P}_{\infty}+\boldsymbol{P}_{\infty}\boldsymbol{A}^{\mathrm{T}}=-\boldsymbol{B}\boldsymbol{B}^{\mathrm{T}}
and
\boldsymbol{A}^{\mathrm{T}}\boldsymbol{Q}_{\infty}+\boldsymbol{Q}_{\infty}\boldsymbol{A}=-\boldsymbol{C}^{\mathrm{T}}\boldsymbol{C}
or to the Lyapunov equations
\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{P}_{\infty}\boldsymbol{A}^{\mathrm{T}}+\boldsymbol{B}\boldsymbol{B}^{\mathrm{T}}=\boldsymbol{P}_{\infty}
and
\boldsymbol{A}^{\mathrm{T}}\boldsymbol{Q}_{\infty}\boldsymbol{A}+\boldsymbol{C}^{\mathrm{T}}\boldsymbol{C}=\boldsymbol{Q}_{\infty}
in discrete time.
The drawback is that their use limits the application of BT to stable systems. This is our motivation to discuss an approach to BT for unstable systems, which are common in data assimilation applications.

2.2.2
Balanced Truncation

Projection-based model reduction methods aim to find a suitable low-dimensional subspace of the state space containing the important features. A projection operator projects the state variables and matrices of the system (
8
) onto this low-dimensional subspace. In our case, the low-dimensional subspace consists of the states that are both easily reachable and easily observable, quantified by the energies (
11
). The reachability energy is low and the observability energy is high. The directions obtained by BT, therefore, maximise the generalised Rayleigh quotient
\left(\frac{\left\|\boldsymbol{x}\right\|_{\boldsymbol{Q}_{\infty}}}{\left\|\boldsymbol{x}\right\|_{\boldsymbol{P}_{\infty}^{-1}}}\right)^{2}
. The dominant eigendirections of the matrix pencil
(\boldsymbol{Q}_{\infty},\boldsymbol{P}_{\infty}^{-1})=:(\boldsymbol{Q},\boldsymbol{P}^{-1})
, i.e., the eigendirections
\boldsymbol{v}_{k}
satisfying
\boldsymbol{Q}\boldsymbol{v}_{k}=\delta^{2}_{k}\boldsymbol{P}^{-1}\boldsymbol{v}_{k}
and associated with the dominant eigenvalues
\delta^{2}_{k}
, maximise the generalised Rayleigh quotient. The eigenvalue square roots
\delta_{1}>\delta_{2}>\ldots>\delta_{d}
are called the
Hankel singular values
.

After having determined the dominant directions
\boldsymbol{v}_{k}
, the goal is to build a
balancing transformation

\boldsymbol{T}
such that
\boldsymbol{T}^{-1}\boldsymbol{P}\boldsymbol{T}^{-\mathrm{T}}=\tilde{\boldsymbol{P}}=\tilde{\boldsymbol{Q}}=\boldsymbol{T}^{\mathrm{T}}\boldsymbol{Q}\boldsymbol{T}
. If
\tilde{\boldsymbol{P}}=\tilde{\boldsymbol{Q}}
, reachability (related to
\tilde{\boldsymbol{P}}
) and observability (related to
\tilde{\boldsymbol{Q}}
) are expressed in a common basis and can be considered simultaneously. The balancing transformation is used as the projector to reduce the system dimensions. Balanced truncation for stable LTI systems is based on the singular value decomposition and given by Algorithm
1
.

.

.

1:

Obtain a square root decomposition (e.g., Cholesky) of the Gramians ((
12
), (

) or others), i.e.,
\boldsymbol{P}=\boldsymbol{RR}^{\mathrm{T}},\boldsymbol{Q}=\boldsymbol{LL}^{\mathrm{T}}
, e.g., by low-rank Lyapunov solvers

.

2:

\boldsymbol{U}\boldsymbol{\Delta}\boldsymbol{Z}^{\mathrm{T}}=\boldsymbol{L}^{\mathrm{T}}\boldsymbol{R}
(Singular value decomposition).

Truncation

2.2.3
Time-limited Balanced Truncation

Time- and frequency-limited balanced truncation has been developed by Gawronski and Juan
Wodek1990TLGramians
. This section is based on their work. The idea of time-limited balanced truncation is to use the time-limited Gramians (
9
) and (
10
) for a time interval of interest instead of the infinite Gramians (
12
) and (
13
). In the data assimilation problems, we consider the interval
\mathcal{T}=[0,\ldots,t_{e}=t_{n}]
and use the notation
\boldsymbol{P}_{\mathcal{T}}:=\boldsymbol{P}(t_{e})
and
\boldsymbol{Q}_{\mathcal{T}}:=\boldsymbol{Q}(t_{e})
. The time-limited Gramians are the solutions of (modified) Lyapunov equations:

Theorem 2.1

The time-limited Gramians
\boldsymbol{P}_{\mathcal{T}}
and
\boldsymbol{Q}_{\mathcal{T}}
for a continuous LTI system (
8
) with stable
\boldsymbol{A}
are the unique, positive-semidefinite solutions to the modified continuous-time Lyapunov equations

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{P}_{\mathcal{T}}+\boldsymbol{P}_{\mathcal{T}}\boldsymbol{A}^{\mathrm{T}}

\displaystyle=-\boldsymbol{B}\boldsymbol{B}^{\mathrm{T}}+\boldsymbol{B}_{t_{e}}\boldsymbol{B}_{t_{e}}^{\mathrm{T}},\kern 5.0pt\boldsymbol{B}_{t_{e}}:=e^{\boldsymbol{A}t_{e}}\boldsymbol{B},\text{ and }

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{A}^{\mathrm{T}}\boldsymbol{Q}_{\mathcal{T}}+\boldsymbol{Q}_{\mathcal{T}}\boldsymbol{A}

\displaystyle=-\boldsymbol{C}^{\mathrm{T}}\boldsymbol{C}+\boldsymbol{C}_{t_{e}}^{\mathrm{T}}\boldsymbol{C}_{t_{e}},\kern 5.0pt\boldsymbol{C}_{t_{e}}:=\boldsymbol{C}e^{\boldsymbol{A}t_{e}}.

The time-limited Gramians
\boldsymbol{P}_{\mathcal{T}}
and
\boldsymbol{Q}_{\mathcal{T}}
for a discrete system (
8
) with stable
\boldsymbol{A}
are the unique, positive-semidefinite solutions to the modified discrete-time Lyapunov equations

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{P}_{\mathcal{T}}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{P}_{\mathcal{T}}\boldsymbol{A}^{\mathrm{T}}+\boldsymbol{A}^{t_{s}}\boldsymbol{B}\boldsymbol{B}^{\mathrm{T}}(\boldsymbol{A}^{\mathrm{T}})^{t_{s}}-\boldsymbol{A}^{t_{e}}\boldsymbol{B}\boldsymbol{B}^{\mathrm{T}}(\boldsymbol{A}^{\mathrm{T}})^{t_{e}},\text{ and }

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{Q}_{\mathcal{T}}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{A}^{\mathrm{T}}\boldsymbol{Q}_{\mathcal{T}}\boldsymbol{A}+(\boldsymbol{A}^{\mathrm{T}})^{t_{s}}\boldsymbol{C}^{\mathrm{T}}\boldsymbol{C}\boldsymbol{A}^{t_{s}}-(\boldsymbol{A}^{\mathrm{T}})^{t_{e}}\boldsymbol{C}^{\mathrm{T}}\boldsymbol{C}\boldsymbol{A}^{t_{e}}.

The balanced truncation Algorithm
1
is applied to the system (
8
) using the Gramians
\boldsymbol{P}=\boldsymbol{P}_{\mathcal{T}}
and
\boldsymbol{Q}=\boldsymbol{Q}_{\mathcal{T}}
. Their matrix square roots may be obtained as the solutions of modified Lyapunov equations from Theorem
2.1
. We call this procedure
time-limited balanced truncation (TLBT)
. Using TLBT is arguably the way to go in the setting of data assimilation, since there are only observations in some fixed time-interval
\mathcal{T}=[0,t_{e}]
. The end time
t_{e}
is often comparatively small and we are not interested in the behaviour at an infinite time horizon.

The assumption that the system (
8
) and hence
\boldsymbol{A}
is stable is only necessary for the existence of the infinite Gramians
\boldsymbol{P}_{\infty}
and
\boldsymbol{Q}_{\infty}
. The time-limited Gramians
\boldsymbol{P}(t)
and
\boldsymbol{Q}(t)
are defined anyways, see (
9
) and (
10
). Hence, we drop the stability assumption for TLBT. Redmann and Kürschner
Redmann2018TLBT
have shown that for continuous systems with unstable
\boldsymbol{A}
and
\Lambda(\boldsymbol{A})\cap\Lambda(-\boldsymbol{A})=\emptyset
the Lyapunov equations from Theorem
2.1
can still be used for the computation of the time-limited Gramians, which is beneficial from a numerical perspective. A drawback is that, even for stable
\boldsymbol{A}
, stability is not preserved for systems (
14
), which were reduced by TLBT, but additional assumptions or modifications are necessary

In this section, two data assimilation approaches and the basics of balanced truncation and its time-limited version have been explained. In the next Sect.
3
, the details of (TL)BT in these two methods are discussed and compared.

3.2
Balancing Linear Gaussian Bayesian Inference

BT has been extended to continuous linear Gaussian (LG) Bayesian inference by Qian et al.
Qian2021Balancing
. We generalise the concept to arbitrary prior covariances and unstable system matrices
\boldsymbol{A}
. There are two main differences between the LTI system (
8

2

1.

In the LG Bayesian inference setting, there is no input function
\boldsymbol{u}(t)

2.

Unlike for the LTI system, the observations
\boldsymbol{m}_{k}
in Bayesian inference are noisy.

3.2.1
From Compatible to Arbitrary Prior Covariances as Reachability Gramians

Qian et al.
Qian2021Balancing
have solved the problem of the missing input in Bayesian inference by introducing the notion of
prior-compatibility
. It is not necessary to know the input port
\boldsymbol{B}
explicitly but only to ensure that there is a
\boldsymbol{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d_{\mathrm{in}}}
with
d_{\mathrm{in}}=\mathrm{rank}(\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\mathrm{pr}}+\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\mathrm{pr}}\boldsymbol{A}^{\mathrm{T}})
satisfying
\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\mathrm{pr}}+\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\mathrm{pr}}\boldsymbol{A}^{\mathrm{T}}=-\boldsymbol{B}\boldsymbol{B}^{\mathrm{T}}
. A prior covariance matrix
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\mathrm{pr}}
fulfilling this is called
prior-compatible
. The prior-compatible
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\mathrm{pr}}
is used as infinite reachability Gramian for the LTI system with given
\boldsymbol{A}
and possibly unknown
\boldsymbol{B}
. It is also the time-limited reachability Gramian:
\boldsymbol{P}_{\mathcal{T}}^{\text{LG}}=\boldsymbol{P}_{\infty}^{\text{LG}}=\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\mathrm{pr}}
.

Prior-compatibility is not ensured in general. The spectral decomposition of
\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\mathrm{pr}}+\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\mathrm{pr}}\boldsymbol{A}^{\mathrm{T}}
may be used to manipulate the prior covariance and make it prior-compatible
(
Qian2021Balancing
, Sect. 4.1.2)
. In practice, however, the prior distribution is usually not chosen arbitrarily, but based on expert knowledge or accumulated information. Changing the prior to make it compatible and artificially introducing an input port
\boldsymbol{B}
may involve a loss of information. We want to justify that any Gaussian prior covariance is a time-limited reachability Gramian.

For the tangent linear model in 4D-Var the time-limited reachability Gramian is given by (
17a
). By comparison of the discrete linear tangent model (
15
) and the continuous LG Bayesian inference setting, we can use the continuous version of (
17a
) to compute the time-limited reachability Gramian for (
2
), which is

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{P}_{\mathcal{T}}^{\text{LG}}=\int_{0}^{t_{e}}e^{\boldsymbol{A}\tau}\boldsymbol{BB}^{\mathrm{T}}e^{\boldsymbol{A}^{\mathrm{T}}\tau}\mathrm{d}\tau+\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\mathrm{pr}}=\int_{0}^{t_{e}}e^{\boldsymbol{A}\tau}\boldsymbol{00}^{\mathrm{T}}e^{\boldsymbol{A}^{\mathrm{T}}\tau}\mathrm{d}\tau+\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\mathrm{pr}}=\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\mathrm{pr}}.

(19a)

The above comparison with established results from 4D-Var (see Sect.
3.1
) overcomes the need for prior-compatibility for
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\mathrm{pr}}
and makes the application of BT to LG Bayesian inference more general. A numerical example to emphasise that the original prior should be used instead of a modified compatible one whenever possible is given in Sect.
4.1

This approach is also well-founded in literature: time-limited reachability Gramians are the covariance matrices of the LTI system state at a fixed time
Melina2018SDE
and the infinite Gramian is, thus, the total state covariance. The state equation (
2a
) evolves linearly without model error. Hence, the state evolution is deterministic and the full state covariance is introduced by the prior covariance as reachability Gramian. The POD-Gramian of an input-free system is constructed similarly
Antoulas2005Book
.

Remark 4

The unknown initial condition to be inferred is not zero. It has a centered Gaussian prior and a Gaussian posterior distribution. BT is defined for a zero initial condition. It may therefore be questioned whether this method is well suited for application to data assimilation problems. Other systems theory techniques, however, require an input matrix; see, e.g.,
Antoulas2005Book
;
Benner2015Survey
;
Benner2017MORBook

3.2.3
Computational Aspects of TLBT in Data Assimilation

The use of the time-limited noisy Gramians (
17
) and (
19
) in Algorithm

is straightforward if in Step 1 there are (low-rank) factors for
\boldsymbol{P}_{\mathcal{T}}=\boldsymbol{RR}^{\mathrm{T}}
and

. In LG Bayesian inference and 4D-Var, the time-limited reachability Gramian is equal to the prior covariance. Therefore, no Lyapunov equation needs to be solved. The time-limited noisy observability Gramian computation requires the solution of the modified Lyapunov equation (

\boldsymbol{A}^{t_{e}}
or the matrix exponential
e^{\boldsymbol{A}t_{e}}
for the right-hand sides. There is no direct square root approach. But direct approaches quickly reach their limit for high-dimensional problems. Slower iterative algorithms, like (block-)rational Krylov algorithms, may still compute a result. Shift selection is a typical bottleneck in these algorithms and they take longer than solving the Lyapunov equations for the infinite Gramians in low-dimensional settings; see, e.g.,
Kuerschner2018TLBT
;
PontesDuff2021DiscreteTLBT
. Time-limited Gramians, however, allow the application of the BT Algorithm

to unstable systems, where Lyapunov equation solvers cannot be used.

In this section we have demonstrated how TLBT extends the idea of Gramian-based model reduction for Bayesian inference to unstable systems. For short observation intervals TLBT significantly outperforms BT with infinite Gramians, despite the increased numerical cost of approximating the posterior statistics.

5
Conclusion

Model order reduction is central to data assimilation because models and data are typically high-dimensional and expensive to process. To this end, the adaptation of model reduction techniques from systems theory to data assimilation problems is essential. This work has further strengthened the link between the two research areas.

Time-limited balanced truncation (TLBT) enabled us to significantly generalise the theory of balancing Bayesian inference to systems with an unstable system matrix
\boldsymbol{A}
and non-compatible priors. TLBT is, thus, particularly suited to data assimilation applications such as numerical weather prediction, where short-term forecasts are required for unstable systems
Boess20114DVarunstable
;
BonavitaNumWeath2021
. One of the popular techniques is incremental 4D-Var and TLBT is perfectly tailored to model reduction of unstable linear tangent models in this context. The numerical efficiency of TLBT needs further investigation, e.g., by using more advanced rational Krylov methods.

Interesting research questions in data assimilation include inhomogeneous initial conditions, errors in the dynamical forward model or time-varying systems. Similarities with systems theory may be instructive. Literature on such problems in the deterministic setting is available, but not extensive; see, e.g.,
Beattie2016inhom
;
Heink2011inhom
;
Lang2016TimeVar
;
Sandberg2004TimeVar
.

Although our approach of using TLBT in data assimilation is more general than the existing ones, it is so far limited to linear Gaussian Bayesian inference. The next step in the exploration of model order reduction for Bayesian inference is the generalisation to nonlinear settings. The 4D-Var method could be a step in the right direction, as it already deals with nonlinear operators. Concepts from systems theory for the reduction of nonlinear dynamical systems may also prove useful here. This opens up a broad field of investigation at the interface between the two research communities.
