Stiff connections in pseudo-Euclidean manifolds
Arnaud Chéritat, Guillaume Tahar

Abstract
. For a smooth manifold endowed with a (similarity) pseudo-Euclidean structure, a stiff connection ∇ \nabla is a symmetric affine connection such that geodesics of ∇ \nabla are straight lines of the pseudo-Euclidean structure while the first-order infinitesimal holonomy at each point is an infinitesimal isometry. In this paper, we give a complete classification of stiff connections in a local chart, identify canonical models and start investigating the global geometry of (similarity) pseudo-Euclidean manifolds endowed with a stiff connection. In the conformal class of the pseudo-Euclidean metric g g , a stiff connection ∇ \nabla defines a pseudo-Riemannian metric h h such that unparameterized geodesics of ∇ \nabla coincide with unparameterized geodesics of g g but have a constant speed with respect to the so-called isochrone metric h h . In particular, we obtain a new natural connection on the open unit ball that provides a compromise between properties of Cayley-Klein and Poincaré hyperbolic models.



Note:
Unless explicitly stated, all manifolds and pseudo-Euclidean spaces considered in this article are assumed of dimension
d\geq 2
.

1.
Introduction

In this paper, we investigate a form of compatibility between projective and conformal flatness for affine connections.

As a motivation, note that planar charts of non-Euclidean surfaces are always unsatisfactory in one way or another, not only because the Gaussian curvature prevents the chart to map the metric to the Euclidean one.

For example, there are two famous representations of the hyperbolic plane as a disk: the Poincaré model and the Beltrami-Klein model.
The first one is conformal (angles between curves are identical for the hyperbolic metric and for the Euclidean one) but hyperbolic geodesics appear as curved lines.
In the second model, the geodesics of hyperbolic geometry are represented as the chords of the disk but the Euclidean angle at their intersection does not faithfully represent the true one.

In a similar way, gnomonic projection of the sphere sends great circles to straight lines, but it is not a conformal representation either.

To a Riemannian metric is associated the Levi-Civita connection, which is a symmetric affine connection, from which one can recover the metric up to multiplication by a constant.
By focusing on the connection rather that the metric, one gets more flexibility and we will exhibit in particular an interesting family of connection of the Euclidean space whose geodesics are straight lines, and whose first-order infinitesimal holonomies are isometries.

It is still too much to ask for a connection on a subset of Euclidean space that would both have its geodesics straight, and be conformal, i.e. whose parallel transport between tangent spaces consists only in similarities.

This is why we relax in this article the conformality condition into a infinitesimal version. In order to work in a more general setting, we fix a signature

These are manifolds

Given a pair

•

•

•

By charts we mean pseudo-Euclidean charts. Such a connection is in particular

Note that, though geodesics are straight lines in such charts, we do not require that they have constant vector speed in these charts.

The setting of (similarity) pseudo-Euclidean manifolds is natural because any local transformation of pseudo-Euclidean space that preserves straight lines and is conformal must be a similarity.

The infinitesimal conformality hypothesis proves to be flexible enough to obtain an interesting family of connections that can be thought of as non-trivial deformations of the trivial connection.
The main result of the paper is a complete local classification of stiff connections: for each type of pseudo-Euclidean space

Organization of the paper

In Section

In Section

We also give formulas to compute the curvature and Ricci tensors. In this class, incompressible connections (connections locally preserving a volume form) are determined by the logarithmic derivative of a potential

In Section

We prove that incompressibility hypothesis is automatically satisfied for

On the opposite, for

, relaxing the hypothesis leaf to infinite dimensional families of connections.

In Section

In Section
6
, we provide explicit computations for the parallel transport in canonical models

.

In Section
7
, we present the most remarkable feature of stiff connections. In the same conformal class, we obtain three pseudo-Riemannian metrics:

•

the usual flat metric

of the underlying pseudo-Euclidean space;

•

the Ricci tensor (or its opposite) of connection
\nabla
(always nondegenerate);

•

an

such that the geodesics of

have constant speed with respect to

These three metrics are related to each other by a potential

In Section

, we investigate the global structure of pairs

. In particular, we describe two families of remarkable examples:

•

•

3.
Tensor formulas for projective charts

A special class of affine connections admits charts conjugating geodesics of the connection to straight lines of the chart. Their existence implies strong constraints on the coefficients of the connection. A symmetric connection having such charts near every point is sometimes called
projectively flat
, by reference to the projective space
\mathbb{RP}^{d}
and its group of transformations. We will call here
projective chart
or
projective flattening
a chart where a (not necessarily symmetric) connection has all its geodesics that are straight lines of the chart.

In dimension one all connections are symmetric and it is obvious that all connections are not only projectively flat, but also that
any
chart is a projective flattening, making the theory almost pointless. However for technical reasons related to potential and Proposition
3.16
, this helps to determine the parameterization of geodesics in any dimension, so we will also cover this case.

In this section and further, we make use of the Kronecker symbol
\delta_{ij}=0
if

and

(2)

\displaystyle\forall i\neq j,\ \quad\Gamma_{ii}^{i}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Gamma^{j}_{ij}+\Gamma^{j}_{ji}\ \text{ and }

By applying this to

By applying to

3.2.
Ricci tensor and incompressible connections in projective charts

The Ricci tensor
\mathrm{Ric}
is a
(0,2)
-tensor obtained by contraction of the curvature tensor
R
. Its coefficients are given (with our choices of conventions for
R
) by

\mathrm{Ric}_{jk}=\sum_{i}R_{ijk}^{i}.

Thus, in a projective chart of a symmetric affine connection
\nabla
on a
d
-dimensional smooth manifold, where
a_{1},\dots,a_{d}
are the coefficients of the associated form
a
, the coefficients of the Ricci tensor deduced from equation (
3.4
) are

(3.5)

\mathrm{Ric}_{jk}=(d-1)a_{j}a_{k}+\partial_{k}a_{j}-d.\partial_{j}a_{k}.

Definition 3.7
.

A connection is called
incompressible
whenever near every point there exists a volume form that is locally preserved by the parallel transport.

By
[
NS94
]
, Chapter I, Proposition 3.1, this is equivalent to the first order infinitesimal holonomy having vanishing trace everywhere.
It is easy to see that in dimension one a connection is necessarily incompressible (it is always locally trivializable).

Proposition 3.8
.

Given a pair
(M,\nabla)
formed by a
d
-dimensional smooth manifold and an affine symmetric and projectively flat connection, we consider a projective flattening
\phi
. Let
a_{1},\dots,a_{d}
be the coefficients of
\nabla
in chart
\phi
. The following statements are equivalent:

(1)

The associated form
a
is closed.

(2)

There exists a locally defined nowhere vanishing smooth function
\psi
, called a
potential
, such that for any index
i
,
a_{i}=-\frac{\partial_{i}\psi}{\psi}
(i.e.
a=\frac{-1}{\psi}d\psi
). It is unique up to multiplication by an element of
{\mathbb{R}}^{*}
.

(3)

The Ricci tensor of
\nabla
is symmetric (
\mathrm{Ric}_{jk}=\mathrm{Ric}_{kj}
for any pair of indices
j,k
).

(4)

The connection
\nabla
is incompressible.

We define a connection satisfying these conditions as an
incompressible projectively flat connection
.

In these cases,
the preserved volume forms are locally given by

\omega=\alpha\frac{dx_{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge dx_{d}}{\psi^{d+1}}

for
\alpha\in{\mathbb{R}}
,
and the coefficients of the Ricci tensor are given by

\mathrm{Ric}_{jk}=(d-1)\frac{\partial_{j}\partial_{k}\psi}{\psi}.

Besides, the coefficients of the curvature tensor are completely determined by the Ricci tensor: if
d=1
then
R=0
and
\mathrm{Ric}=0
; if
d\geq 2
then

R_{ijk}^{l}=\frac{1}{d-1}(\delta_{il}\mathrm{Ric}_{jk}-\delta_{jl}\mathrm{Ric}_{ik}).

We can also express
R
in terms of
\psi
:

R_{ijk}^{l}=\delta_{il}\frac{\partial_{j}\partial_{k}\psi}{\psi}-\delta_{jl}\frac{\partial_{i}\partial_{k}\psi}{\psi}.

Proof.

By Equation (
3.5
) we have

\mathrm{Ric}_{jk}-\mathrm{Ric}_{kj}=(d+1)(\partial_{k}a_{j}-\partial_{j}a_{k}).

This proves that the Ricci tensor is symmetric if and only if the condition
\partial_{k}a_{j}=\partial_{j}a_{k}
is satisfied for any pair of indices
j,k
. This is equivalent to saying that the differential form
a=\sum_{i}a_{i}dx_{i}
is closed.

Closedness of
a
is equivalent to the existence of a locally defined function
\theta
such that
a=d\theta
. We define
\psi=e^{-\theta}
and obtain that
a=d\theta=-\frac{d\psi}{\psi}
.
Conversely if (2) holds then
a=d\theta
for
\theta=-\log|\psi|
.
We proved that statements
(1)
,
(2)
and
(3)
are equivalent.

The connection
\nabla
locally preserves some volume form if and only if its first-order infinitesimal holonomy matrices are traceless (see
[
NS94
]
, Chapter 1, Section 3, Proposition 3.1).
This is equivalent to saying that for any pair of indices
i,j
, we have
\sum_{k}R_{ijk}^{k}=0
. By formula (
3.4
), we have

\sum_{k}R_{ijk}^{k}=(d+1)(\partial_{i}a_{j}-\partial_{j}a_{i}).

Statement (4) is thus equivalent to closedness of differential form
a
.

Near a given point, invariant volume forms are locally determined by the choice of a volume form at this point, since one can transport this choice on a whole small neighbourhood by parallel transport and by incompressibility, this choice is coherent. The obtained volume form depends linearly on the initial choice, which occurs in a one-dimensional space, and any invariant form must locally be obtained that way.
To prove that
\frac{dx_{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge dx_{d}}{\psi^{d+1}}
is invariant, we could take this formula and differentiate it but we find interesting to show how this formula pops up as a consequence of analysing the parallel transport.

Consider an initial point
x_{*}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, choose one of the base vectors
e_{i}
, and define the path
\gamma(t)=x_{*}+te_{i}
.
Let
v(0)
be any vector and
v(t)
be the parallel transport of
v
along
\gamma
.
By definition
\dot{v}=-\Gamma(\dot{\gamma},v)=\frac{1}{\psi}\left(d\psi(\dot{\gamma})v+d\psi(v)\dot{\gamma}\right)

where
\Gamma
,
\psi
and
d\psi
are taken at the point
\gamma(t)
and for any function
f
of
t
,
\dot{f}
means
f^{\prime}(t)
.
For our particular choice of
\gamma
this gives

\dot{v}=\frac{1}{\psi}\left(d\psi(e_{i})v+d\psi(v)e_{i}\right).

Note that
d\psi(e_{i})=\dot{\psi}=(\psi\circ\gamma)^{\prime}(t)
.

We choose
v(0)=e_{j}
. Then, as we already saw,
v(t)
remains in the vector subspace generated by
e_{i}
and
e_{j}
.

If
j\neq i
then let us write
v(t)=f(t)e_{i}+g(t)e_{j}
with
f(0)=0
and
g(0)=1
. Then the differential equation reads

\dot{f}e_{i}+\dot{g}e_{j}=(\cdots)e_{i}+\frac{\dot{\psi}}{\psi}ge_{j}
. It follows that
\dot{g}/g=\dot{\psi}/\psi
, hence
g(t)=\psi\circ\gamma(t)/\psi(x_{*})
.

If
j=i
then let us write
v(t)=f(t)e_{i}
with
f(0)=1
and the differential equation reads

\dot{f}e_{i}=2\frac{d\psi(e_{i})}{\psi}fe_{i}=\frac{2\dot{\psi}}{\psi}fe_{i}
.
Hence
\dot{f}/f=2\dot{\psi}/\psi
, so
f(t)=(\psi\circ\gamma(t)/\psi(x_{*}))^{2}
.

It follows that the form
\frac{dx_{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge dx_{d}}{\psi^{d+1}}
is invariant by the parallel transport along lines parallel to
e_{i}
.
And this holds for any
i
. Any volume form which is invariant by parallel transport must be invariant by transport along these lines, so must be proportional to the above form. Since we know by incompressibility that there is an invariant form, the above form is invariant.

The last expressions for
\mathrm{Ric}
and
R
are obtained by simple computations, using for
\mathrm{Ric}
equation (
3.5
) and point (2) of the present proposition, and for
R
equations (
3.4
) and (
3.5
) and the fact that
a
is closed. ∎

Remark 3.10

Note also that the potential behaves like a function under an affine change of variable, but not under a projective change of variable.

3.3
.

Here we explore the effect of a linear change of variable on the coefficients

of the associated form of a projective chart of a symmetric connection.

.

Consider a manifold

If

(3.6)

\tilde{a}\{{A\}}=a\circ L^{-1}.

This notation is a shorthand for:
\forall x\in U

\tilde{a}\{A(x)\}(u)=a\{x\}(L^{-1}(u))

\tilde{a}\{A(x)\}(u)=\sum_{i}\tilde{a}_{i}(A(x))u_{i}

This is an easy consequence of equations (

) and (

).
Indeed, consider the first one:
\tilde{\Gamma}\{A\}\circ(L,L)=L\circ\Gamma

Substitute the second one on each side:

By composing on the right with

3.4.
Projective change of variable

Under a projective change of variable, lines are mapped to lines and a projectively flattened connection will remain projectively flattented. In this section we compute the action on the associated “form”
a
, and if the connection is incompressible, on the associated potentials

Proposition 3.15
.

Let
\nabla
a projectively flattened connection on an open subset of
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, with associated form
a
.
Let
P(x)=\frac{1}{C(x)}F(x)
be a projective transformation, with
F:{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
and

Denote
\tilde{\nabla}=P_{*}\nabla
and
\tilde{a}
the

\tilde{\nabla}
.
Then:

P^{*}\tilde{a}=a+\frac{dC}{C}.

If moreover
\nabla
is incompressible, let
\psi
be a potential (defined locally or globally).
Then the function
\tilde{\psi}
defined by

\tilde{\psi}\circ P=\frac{\psi}{C}

is a potential for
\tilde{\nabla}
.

Proof.

By Equation (
2.1
), we have

L^{-1}\tilde{\Gamma}(Lu,Lu)=\Gamma(u,v)-L^{-1}H(u,v)

where
\Gamma
is taken at
x
,
L
is the differential of
P
at
x
,

is the Hessian differential of
P
at
x
and
\tilde{\Gamma}
is taken at

.
By definition,

\tilde{\Gamma}(u,v)=\tilde{a}(u)v+\tilde{a}(v)u
. Since
\tilde{a}\circ L=P^{*}(\tilde{a})
where
\tilde{a}
is taken at

and

at

, we get

(P^{*}\tilde{a})(u)v+(P^{*}\tilde{a})(v)u=a(u)v+a(v)u-L^{-1}H(u,v).

Let us compute the second term of the subtraction.
One finds that
L(u)=\frac{dF(u)}{C}-\frac{dC(u)}{C^{2}}F
and

where

,

are taken at

3.6.
Geodesics

Since any geodesic stays in a straight line
L\subset E
, the geodesic equation
\ddot{\gamma}=-\Gamma(\dot{\gamma},\dot{\gamma})
is easy to solve.
Note that since
L
is one-dimensional, the restriction of
\nabla
to

is necessarily incompressible, and thus has a potential.
In the case the original connection is incompressible, the restriction of a potential to
L
can be taken as a potential by Proposition

.
In any case, one can take
\psi=\exp(-\int a)
where
\int a
is any antiderivative of

.

Now we have
\Gamma(\dot{\gamma},\dot{\gamma})=2a(\dot{\gamma})\dot{\gamma}=\frac{-2}{\psi}d\psi(\dot{\gamma})\dot{\gamma}
so the geodesic equation reads

\ddot{\gamma}=2\frac{\dot{\wideparen{\psi(\gamma)}}}{\psi(\gamma)}\dot{\gamma},

which is equivalent to
\frac{d}{dt}(\dot{\gamma}/\psi(\gamma)^{2})=0
, i.e.

\frac{1}{\psi(\gamma)^{2}}\dot{\gamma}=\operatorname{cst}.

If one chooses an affine parameterization
\phi:s\in{\mathbb{R}}\mapsto x\in L
, and denote
\psi_{s}=\psi\circ\phi
(the index

\dot{s}/\psi_{s}^{2}(s)=\operatorname{cst}
.
This is equivalent to

F(s(t))=\alpha t+\beta

for some
\alpha\in{\mathbb{R}}^{*}
and
\beta\in{\mathbb{R}}
,
where
F
is an antiderivative of
1/\psi_{s}^{2}
.
This can be rephrased as

\int_{s(t_{0})}^{s(t_{1})}\frac{ds}{\psi_{s}(s)^{2}}=\alpha(t_{1}-t_{0});

or as follows: the change of variable
F\circ\phi
with
F^{\prime}=1/\psi_{s}^{2}
trivializes the restriction of
\nabla
to
L
.

This has the following interesting consequence: for any Euclidean or pseudo-Euclidean form
q
on
\vec{E}
, every geodesic has constant speed with respect to the Riemannian or pseudo-Riemannian metric
g
such that
g(dx,dx)=q(dx)/\psi(x)^{4}
and we say that
\nabla
and
g
are
isochrone
to each other.
It may seem somewhat arbitrary to have to choose some
q
on
\vec{E}
but in the context of stiff connections, there is (at least locally) a
q
given a priori.
We develop this in Section
7.1
.

4.
Classification of stiff connections

In this section we work directly in a pseudo-Euclidean affine space
E
of dimension
d
and signature
(p,d-p)
and with a connection
\nabla
defined on an open subset of
E
.
This subset can for instance be a chart of a pseudo-Euclidean manifold
M
or a similarity manifold. We only consider the case
d\geq 2
.

Note: Our convention in denoting the signatures is that
p
denotes the number of
+
,
d-p
the number of
-
, and there is not
0
.

Given a
pseudo-Euclidean vector space
with associated bilinear form
q
,
by an isometry we mean a linear self map
f
the space such that
q\circ f=q
.
In particular we exclude map for which we would have
q\circ f=-q
, like
(x_{1},x_{2})\mapsto(-x_{2},x_{1})
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
endowed with
q(x)=x_{1}^{2}-x_{2}^{2}
.
By a
similarity
, we mean the composition of a homothety and of an isometry.
Note that a similarity multiplies the underlying quadratic form
q
by a positive constant.
Of course if we consider an affine space instead of a vector space, we add a translation term to isometries and similarities.

Recall that a connection
\nabla
on the pseudo-Euclidean affine space
E
is called
stiff
if:

•

\nabla
is symmetric,

•

geodesics of
\nabla
are straight lines (not necessarily with constant speed vector),

•

first order infinitesimal holonomies are isometries of
E
.

We will see in Section
4.2
that non-flat stiff connections are quite rigid in that they come, up to an affine isometry of
E
, in a one parameter family for each dimension and signature. And up to a similarity of
E
they come up in a finite family with only three elements.

We will study a slightly relaxed condition where we only ask infinitesimal holonomies to be conformal for
E
, i.e. similarities.
This will be called here
weakly stiff
connections. We will see that in dimension
d\geq 3
, weakly stiff connections are necessarily stiff, but that in dimension
2
there form a wider class: actually they come in families of infinite dimension.

4.1.
Infinitesimal holonomy and coefficients of the curvature tensor

The condition that the first-order infinitesimal holonomy of
\nabla
consists only of infinitesimal similarities of
E
can be directly translated into equations satisfied by the coefficients of the curvature tensor (and this is independent of the chart being projective or not).
Let
E^{p,d-p}
denote
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
equipped with the quadratic form
q(x)=x_{1}^{2}+\cdots+x_{p}^{2}-x_{p+1}^{2}-\cdots-x_{d}^{2}=\sum_{i}\epsilon_{i}x_{i}^{2}
,
\epsilon_{i}=\pm 1
. We denote
g
the associated flat pseudo-Riemannian symmetric
(0,2)
-tensor, for which
g_{ii}=\epsilon_{i}
and
i\neq j\implies g_{ij}=0
.
It may also be denoted
g=\sum_{i}\epsilon_{i}\,dx_{i}\otimes dx_{i}
.

Lemma 4.1
.

Given a connection
\nabla
, not necessarily symmetric, defined on an open subset of
E^{p,q-p}
, the first-order holonomy of
\nabla
consists in infinitesimal similarities if and only if the coefficients of its curvature tensor
R
satisfy the following conditions:

(1)

for any indices
i,j,k,l
such that
k\neq l
, we have
R_{ijk}^{l}=-\epsilon_{l}\epsilon_{k}R_{ijl}^{k}
(where
\epsilon_{1},\dots,\epsilon_{p}=1
while
\epsilon_{p+1},\dots,\epsilon_{d}=-1
);

(2)

for any indices
i,j,k
,
R_{ijk}^{k}
does not depend on
k
.

The first-order holonomy of
\nabla
consists in infinitesimal isometries if and only if we have (1) and that for any indices
i,j,k
,
R_{ijk}^{k}=0
.

Proof.

In the canonical basis of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we denoted
M(i,j)
the holonomy matrix for an infinitesimal rectangle based at some point and directed by vectors the
e_{i}
and
e_{j}
. The matrix
M(i,j)
has its coefficient at the
l
-th line and
k
-th column equal to
-R_{ijk}^{l}
.

The matrices
M(i,j)
are infinitesimal similarities of the pseudo-Euclidean structure of the chart if and only if they are sums of a scalar multiple of the identity matrix and of a matrix
A
such that
I_{p,d-p}A
is antisymmetric (
I_{p,d-p}
is the diagonal matrix whose
p
first coefficients are equal to
1
while the remaining
d-p
are equal to
-1
). The condition on the coefficients of the curvature tensor follows.

Infinitesimal isometries correspond to the fact that the diagonal term vanishes.
∎

Recall that in dimension two, the curvature tensor is entirely determined by the four coefficients
R_{12k}^{l}
, since
R_{iik}^{l}=0
and
R_{21k}^{l}=-R_{12k}^{l}
.
It is well-known that the space
E^{1,1}
has another coordinate system parameterized by
y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
in which the quadratic form takes the expression
y_{1}y_{2}
.
In this coordinate system
E^{\prime}
, the above lemma becomes:

Lemma 4.2
.

For
d=2
, on
E^{\prime}
, the first-order holonomy of
\nabla
consists in infinitesimal similarities if and only if the coefficients of its curvature tensor
R
satisfy
R_{122}^{1}=0
and
R_{121}^{2}=0
.
The first-order holonomy of
\nabla
consists in infinitesimal isometries if and only if
R_{121}^{1}+R_{122}^{2}=0
.

4.2.
Stiff connections

A weakly stiff connection on an open subset of
E
is in particular projectively flattenend, i.e. all its geodesics are straight lines of
E
.
A stiff connection is also incompressible.
By Proposition
3.8
, the coefficients of a projectively flattened, symmetric and incompressible connection
\nabla
depend only on a potential function
\psi
.
In the lemma below, we translate the conformality condition of infinitesimal holonomy into a system of partial differential equations satisfied by the potential.

Lemma 4.3
.

Consider a projectively flattened, symmetric and incompressible connection
\nabla
on
E
, with associated potential
\psi
. Then
\nabla
is stiff if and only if:

•

\partial_{i}\partial_{j}\psi=0
for any indices
i,j
such that
i\neq j
;

•

there exists a smooth function
f
such that
\partial_{i}^{2}\psi=\epsilon_{i}f
for any index
i
(
\epsilon_{i}=1
if
i\leq p
and
\epsilon_{i}=-1
otherwise).

Proof.

By Proposition
3.8
,

R_{ijk}^{l}=\delta_{il}\frac{\partial_{j}\partial_{k}\psi}{\psi}-\delta_{jl}\frac{\partial_{i}\partial_{k}\psi}{\psi}.

Following Lemma
4.1
, using the hypothesis that
\nabla
is incompressible, we get that the chart is a projective flattening if and only if the following condition holds:

(1)

for any indices
i,j,k,l
such that
k\neq l
, we have
\epsilon_{k}R_{ijk}^{l}+\epsilon_{l}R_{ijl}^{k}=0
(where
\epsilon_{1},\dots,\epsilon_{p}=1
while
\epsilon_{p+1},\dots,\epsilon_{d}=-1
);

(2)

for any indices
i,j,k
,
R_{ijk}^{k}=0
.

The second point holds if and only if for any indices
i,j,k
, we have
\delta_{jk}\partial_{i}\partial_{k}\psi=\delta_{ik}\partial_{j}\partial_{k}\psi
.
It follows then that for any
i\neq j
, we have
\partial_{i}\partial_{j}\psi=0
(take
k=j
).
Conversely if for all
i\neq j
,
\partial_{i}\partial_{j}\psi=0
then by examining separately 4 case according to the values of the pair
(\delta_{ik},\delta_{jk})
, one sees that
\delta_{jk}\partial_{i}\partial_{k}\psi=\delta_{ik}\partial_{j}\partial_{k}\psi
holds for all
i
,
j
and
k
, hence (2) holds.

Now apply the first equation to
k=j
and
l=i
for
i\neq j
.
We get
\epsilon_{j}R_{ijj}^{i}+\epsilon_{i}R_{iji}^{j}=0
.
Using the expression recalled at the beginning of the proof we get
\epsilon_{i}\partial_{i}^{2}\psi=\epsilon_{j}\partial_{j}^{2}\psi
for any pair of indices
i,j
.
Conversely, if there is a smooth
f
such that for all
i
,
\epsilon_{i}\partial_{i}^{2}\psi=f
then
R_{ijk}^{l}=(\delta_{il}\delta_{jk}\epsilon_{j}-\delta_{ik}\delta_{jl}\epsilon_{i})\frac{f}{\psi}
and thus
\epsilon_{k}R_{ijk}^{l}+\epsilon_{l}R_{ijl}^{k}=(\delta_{il}\delta_{jk}\epsilon_{j}\epsilon_{k}-\delta_{ik}\delta_{jl}\epsilon_{i}\epsilon_{k}+\delta_{ik}\delta_{jl}\epsilon_{j}\epsilon_{l}-\delta_{il}\delta_{jk}\epsilon_{i}\epsilon_{l})\frac{f}{\psi}
.
Noticing that
\delta_{jk}\epsilon_{j}\epsilon_{k}=\delta_{jk}
and using similar identities we get that

\epsilon_{k}R_{ijk}^{l}+\epsilon_{l}R_{ijl}^{k}=0
.
∎

Now, we are able to completely characterize potentials of stiff connections in pseudo-Euclidean spaces.

Theorem 4.4
.

Consider a projectively flattened, symmetric and incompressible connection
\nabla
defined on a connected open subset
U
of
E^{p,d-p}
.
Then
\nabla
is stiff if and only if it has potential

\psi(x_{1},\dots,x_{d})=\frac{K}{2}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{d}\epsilon_{i}x_{i}^{2}+\Lambda(x_{1},\dots,x_{d}),

i.e.
\psi=Kq/2+\Lambda
,
where
K
is a real constant, possibly null, and
\Lambda
is a real affine function.

Conversely, any such potential
\psi
that does not vanish on an open subset
U
of
E
defines a stiff connection on
U
.

Proof.

Following Lemma
4.3
,
\psi
, a priori only locally defined, satisfies a system of partial differential equations implying in particular that
\partial_{i}\psi
depends only on
x_{i}
.
Thus,
\epsilon_{i}\partial_{i}\partial_{i}\psi
depends only on
x_{i}
.
Since these functions do not depend on
i
, they are all equal to some constant
K
.
We deduce that there are constants
K_{1},\dots,K_{d}
such that
\partial_{i}\psi=K\epsilon_{i}x_{i}+K_{i}
.
Finally, we obtain that, up to addition of a constant,
\psi
is locally given by
\frac{K}{2}\sum_{i}\epsilon_{i}x_{i}^{2}+\sum_{i}K_{i}x_{i}
.

By connectedness of
U
and uniqueness of the coefficients of a polynomial function on any small open subset of
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, the same
\psi
can in fact be taken on all
U
(caution must be taken because
\psi
is only unique up to multiplication by a constant).

For the converse, any such
\psi
satisfies the equations of Lemma
4.3
and thus define a stiff connection on
U\subset E
.
∎

As an immediate application of the formula expressing
\mathrm{Ric}_{ij}=(d-1)\frac{\partial_{i}\partial_{j}\psi}{\psi}
in Proposition
3.8
:

Corollary 4.5
.

In the situation of Theorem
4.4
, the Ricci tensor associated to
\nabla
is proportional to the pseudo-Riemannian metric
g
endowing
E
:

\mathrm{Ric}_{ij}=\frac{(d-1)K}{\psi}g_{ij}.

where
K
is the same as in Theorem
4.4
.

Remark 4.6
.

It follows that that
\nabla
is flat (i.e. trivializable) if and only if
K=0
.
In the latter case, flattenings are given by particular projective transformations (of course
\nabla
may be already flattened: this is the case if and only if
\psi
is constant).
Indeed if
\nabla
is flat then
R=0
, hence
\mathrm{Ric}=0
. Conversely if
\mathrm{Ric}=0
then Proposition
3.11
proves that
\nabla
is flat on the domain of
\phi
.
Any flattening must preserve straight lines and for
d\geq 2
such maps are known to be projective (
[
Löw82
]
).

Note that, though the potential
\psi
is only uniquely defined up to multiplication by a constant, the coefficient
K/\psi
is independent of this choice, and we have

\frac{K}{\psi}=\frac{2}{q(x)+\tilde{\Lambda}(x)}

if
K\neq 0
, where
\tilde{\Lambda}(x)=\frac{2}{K}\Lambda(x)
, and of course

\frac{K}{\psi}=0

if
K=0
.

Concerning the curvature tensor, Proposition
3.8
gives
R_{ijk}^{l}=(\delta_{il}\delta_{jk}-\delta_{jl}\delta_{ik})\epsilon_{k}\frac{K}{\psi}
. Hence:

Corollary 4.7
.

In the situation of Theorem
4.4
,
for
i\neq j
we have

R_{iji}^{j}=-\epsilon_{i}\frac{K}{\psi},\quad R_{ijj}^{i}=\epsilon_{j}\frac{K}{\psi}.

All other coefficients of
R
vanish.

In other words, for
i\neq j
, the infinitesimal holonomy endomorphism (see Section
2
) associated to
(e_{i},e_{j})
, has matrix
M(i,j)=\frac{K}{\psi}(\epsilon_{i}E_{ji}-\epsilon_{j}E_{ij})
where
E_{ij}
is the elementary matrix whose only non-zero entry is a
1
at line
i
and column
j
.
This endomorphism also expresses as

(4.1)

M(i,j)=\frac{K}{\psi}\cal J(i,j)

where
\cal J(i,j)
is defined below.

Definition 4.8
.

We let
\cal J(i,j)=\epsilon_{i}\,e_{j}\otimes e_{i}^{*}-\epsilon_{j}\,e_{i}\otimes e_{j}^{*}
.
It is an infinitesimal isometry on the plane
\cal P=\operatorname{Vect}(e_{i},e_{j})
and is
0
on
\cal P^{\perp}
.
The restriction of
\cal J(i,j)
to
\cal P
has the following matrix in basis
(e_{i},e_{j})

\begin{pmatrix}0&-\epsilon_{j}\\
\epsilon_{i}&0\end{pmatrix}.

Note that the coefficient
K/\psi
can be interpreted as an angular speed for a (generalized) rotation.

Scalar curvature.
For a pseudo-Riemannian metric, one of several equivalent definitions of the scalar curvature is as the trace of the
(1,1)
-tensor obtained from the
(0,2)
-tensor
\mathrm{Ric}
via the musical isomorphism between
TM
and
T^{*}M
.
One can raise either the first or second variable: even though the two different operators thus obtained may differ, they have the same trace.
For an arbitrary (symmetric) connection, there is no notion of scalar curvature, because there is no canonical choice of a musical isomorphism.

In our particular situation of a stiff connection, though, we can choose the pseudo-Euclidean metric
g
to define the isomorphism.
With this convention, we get what we call the
scalar curvature
S(\nabla,g)
of
\nabla
with respect to
g
.
From Corollary
4.5
the two endomorphisms whose trace can be taken are both equal to
(d-1)\frac{K}{\psi}\operatorname{Id}
(they are equal because
\mathrm{Ric}
is symmetric in our case), so

S(\nabla,g)=d(d-1)\frac{K}{\psi}.

Remark 4.9
.

One may consider that
\nabla
is
positively curved
at
x
if
S(\nabla,g)
is positive at
x
and
negatively curved
if it is negative.
The sign
S(\nabla,g)
is the sign of
K/\psi
, i.e. the sign of
q(x)+\tilde{\Lambda}(x)
, and is thus locally constant (recall that the potential
\psi
cannot vanish on the domain of definition of
\nabla
).

The connection preserves any affine
2
-plane
P
of
E
, and if the restriction
\nabla^{P}
of
q
to
\overrightarrow{P}
is nondegenerate, then the restriction of
\nabla
to
P
satisfies
S(\nabla^{P},g)=2K/\psi
, so has the same sign.
In the case of a general (pseudo-)Riemannian metric, in dimension two, the classical scalar curvature is
d=2
times the Gaussian curvature.
From this, it makes sense to consider
K/\psi
as the Gaussian curvature of
\nabla
relative to
g
and denote it
G(\nabla,g)
.

Interpreting the sign of the scalar curvature in dimension 2.

In the case of signature
(2,0)
, we can informally state that, as in the Riemannian case, the holonomy of a small injective loop is close to be a rotation of a small angle and that turns in the same direction as the loop if
S(\nabla,g)>0
and in the opposite direction if
S(\nabla,g)<0
.
This can be generalized to a statement cover all signatures
(2,0)
,
(1,1)
and
(0,2)
as follows:
the holonomy of a small injective loop is close to be an isometry close to the identity, which moves a vector
v
not in the light cone by a small vector
dv
, such that the orientation of the basis
(v,dv)
and of the loop are identical if
q(v)S(\nabla,g)>0
and opposite if
q(v)S(\nabla,g)<0
.
This last condition can be stated informally as: it pushes
v
in the direction of the loop in the first case, and in the opposite direction in the second case.

Scalar curvature on nondegenerate subspaces.

Consider any affine subspace
F
of
E^{p,d-p}
of dimension
d^{\prime}\geq 2
such that the restriction of
q
to
\vec{F}
is nondegenerate and
F
intersects the domain
U
of a canonical model
\cal S
.
Then by Proposition
3.16
,
\nabla
preserves
F
and the restriction
\psi|_{F}
of
\psi
to
F
is a potential for the restriction
\nabla^{F}
of
\nabla
to
F
.
Hence

S(\nabla^{F},g^{F})=d^{\prime}(d^{\prime}-1)\frac{K}{\psi|_{F}}
.
In particular,

\frac{S(\nabla^{F},g^{F})}{d^{\prime}(d^{\prime}-1)}=\frac{S(\nabla,g)|_{F}}{d(d-1)}.

A remarkable consequence is that, at any given point of
F\cap U
, the scalar curvature of
\nabla^{F}
has the same sign as the scalar curvature of
\nabla
.

4.3.
Rigidity in dimension
d\geq 3

It is well known that conformal geometry is much more rigid in dimension at least three than in dimension two. This phenomenon also appears in the study of weakly stiff connections, as will be shown by Propositions
4.10
,
4.11
and
4.12
.

Proposition 4.10
.

If
d\geq 3
then any weakly stiff connection is incompressible, i.e. stiff.

Proof.

Let us recall equation (
3.4
) for the expression of the curvature tensor
R
in a pseudo-Euclidean chart:

R_{ijk}^{l}=\delta_{il}(a_{j}a_{k}-\partial_{j}a_{k})-\delta_{jl}(a_{i}a_{k}-\partial_{i}a_{k})+\delta_{kl}(\partial_{i}a_{j}-\partial_{j}a_{i}).

Following Lemma
4.1
, the coefficients
R_{ijk}^{k}
do not depend on
k
.

For any
i,j
distinct, consider
k
distinct from them (hypothesis
d\geq 3
is crucial here).
Substituting equation (
3.4
) in
R_{ijj}^{j}=R_{ijk}^{k}
, we deduce

a_{i}a_{j}-\partial_{i}a_{j}=0
and thus
\partial_{i}a_{j}=\partial_{j}a_{i}
.

Proposition
3.8
then shows that the connection
\nabla
is incompressible.
∎

Theorem
4.4
provides in particular a complete classification of weakly stiff connections in dimension higher than 2.

4.4.
Flexibility in dimension two

For weakly stiff connections of signature
(1,1)
or
(2,0)
, we will see that we have infinitely many degrees of freedom.

4.4.1.
Signature
(1,1)

Recall that the signature
(1,1)
pseudo-Euclidean structure is given by the quadratic form
x_{1}^{2}-x_{2}^{2}
, which is isomorphic by a simple linear change of coordinates
y=\left({\scriptstyle\begin{array}[]{rr}1&1\\
1&-1\end{array}}\right)x
to the quadratic form
y_{1}y_{2}
.
We call
E^{\prime}
the space
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
with coordinates
(y_{1},y_{2})
and endowed with the quadratic form
y_{1}y_{2}
.

In the next statement we make use of
\hat{\mathbb{R}}={\mathbb{R}}\cup\{\infty\}
, which is a compact one dimensional manifold with charts
x\in\hat{\mathbb{R}}-\{\infty\}\mapsto x\in{\mathbb{R}}
and
x\in\hat{\mathbb{R}}-\{0\}\mapsto 1/x\in{\mathbb{R}}
.

Proposition 4.11
.

We consider a connection
\nabla
defined on an open subset of
E^{\prime}
.
We assume that
\nabla
is symmetric and projectively flattened and denote
b=b_{1}dy_{1}+b_{2}dy_{2}
the associated form (see Section
3.1
and Definition
3.3
).
Then
\nabla
is weakly stiff if and only if
b
is given by:

(1)

b_{1}=\frac{1}{f_{1}(y_{2})-y_{1}}
for some smooth function
f_{1}:U\to\hat{\mathbb{R}}
;

(2)

b_{2}=\frac{1}{f_{2}(y_{1})-y_{2}}
for some smooth function
f_{2}:U\to\hat{\mathbb{R}}
.

Conversely, any pair of smooth functions
f_{1},f_{2}
defined on an open subset
U
of
E^{\prime}
, mapping to
\hat{\mathbb{R}}
and such that
f_{1}(y_{2})-y_{1}
and
f_{2}(y_{1})-y_{2}
do not vanish defines via the above formulas the associated form
b
of a projectively flattened and weakly stiff connection.

Proof.

By Lemma
4.2
,
\nabla
is weakly stiff and only if
R_{121}^{2}=0
and
R_{122}^{1}=0
and by equation (
3.4
), this is equivalent to

•

\partial_{1}b_{1}=b_{1}^{2}

•

\partial_{2}b_{2}=b_{2}^{2}

so if
b_{1}
vanishes at some
y_{*}
then it must vanish on the whole connected component of
U\cap L
where
L
is the horizontal line trough
y_{*}
, and elsewhere
\partial_{1}(1/b_{1})=-1
, so
b_{1}=1/(f(y_{2})-y_{1})
for some smooth real valued function
f
of one variable and which cannot equal
y_{1}
in a neighbourhood of
y_{*}
(i.e.
f
takes values in
{\mathbb{R}}
minus a neighbourhood of
(y_{*})_{1}
).
Actually the two cases can be joined in one case if we allow
f
to be a smooth function taking values in
\hat{\mathbb{R}}
and let
f(y_{2})=\infty
iff
b_{1}(y_{1},y_{2})=0
(recall that we work locally).
Indeed if
b_{1}(y_{*})=0
then we can locally write
b_{1}=h/(1-y_{1}h)
for a smooth
h(y_{1},y_{2})
, which will play the role of
1/f
, but we have to prove that
h
is independent of
y_{1}
.
The equation
\partial_{1}b_{1}=b_{1}^{2}
reads
\big((1-y_{1}h)\partial_{1}h+(h+y_{1}\partial_{1}h)h\big)/(1-y_{1}h)^{2}=h^{2}/(1-y_{1}h)^{2}
. It simplifies into
\partial_{1}h=0
, which proves the claim. We can thus take
f=1/h
.

A similar analysis holds for
b_{2}
:
b_{2}=1/(g(y_{1})-y_{2})
for some smooth
g
defined locally, taking values in
\hat{\mathbb{R}}
, and avoiding the value
y_{2}
in a neighbourhood of
y_{*}
.

Conversely taking such
f
and
g
defines smooth real valued coefficients
b_{1}
and
b_{2}
which satisfy the required differential equations.
∎

4.4.2.
Signature
(2,0)

In the case of signature
(2,0)
, the coefficients
a_{1}
and
a_{2}
can be given explicit expressions too, this time in terms of the natural complex structure of the chart.

Proposition 4.12
.

We consider a connection
\nabla
defined on a connected open subset
U
of
E^{2,0}
identified with
{\mathbb{C}}
.
We assume that
\nabla
is symmetric and projectively flattened and denote
a=a_{1}dx_{1}+a_{2}dx_{2}
the associated form (see Section
3.1
and Definition
3.3
).
Then
\nabla
is weakly stiff if and only if one of the following statements holds:

•

\nabla

6.1.
The equipotential and radial foliations

The

The

is the set of straight half lines from

Level sets of the potential

The equipotential foliation is preserved by the action of the generalized orthogonal group

An arc not contained in the light cone is a leaf of the radial foliation if and only if at each point it is orthogonal to the equipotential foliation. Outside the light cone, the radial foliation and the equipotential foliation intersect transversely. The light cone is an equipotential leaf and is the union of
\{0\}
and of a collection of rays.

7.
A tale of three metrics (and five volume forms)

Let us recall that a
conformal structure
or
conformal class
for signature
s
on a manifold is a choice of on each tangent space of a pseudo-Euclidean space structure up to rescaling, of this signature. In other words, a quadratic form of signature
s
, up to multiplication by a constant.
On the manifold, this means the choice of a pseudo-Riemannian structure of signature
s
up to multiplication by a positive function.

Any canonical model
\cal S^{\nu}_{\lambda}(p,q)
,
\nu\in\{-,+\}
with its connection
\nabla
takes place within a pseudo-Euclidean space
E^{p,d-p}
, endowed with a quadratic form
q(x)=\sum\epsilon_{i}x_{i}^{2}
and the associated scalar product
(x,y)\in E^{2}\mapsto x\cdot y\in{\mathbb{R}}
and the associated flat pseudo-Riemannian tensor

g(dx,dy)=dx\cdot dy,

characterized also by
g_{ij}=\delta_{ij}\epsilon_{i}
or equivalently as the symmetric tensor such that

g(dx,dx)=\sum\epsilon_{i}\,dx_{i}^{2}=q(dx).

However,
g
is not preserved by the connection
\nabla
.

On the opposite,
\nabla
has an unambiguous Ricci tensor

\mathrm{Ric}=\frac{2d-2}{\psi}g

(see Corollary
4.5
, where we have
K=2
).
If
\nu<0
then
\psi<0
, so the signature of the Ricci tensoir is
(d-p,p)
(the opposite signature).
If
\nu>0
then
\psi>0
, so the signature of the Ricci tensor is
(p,d-p)
(the same as for
g
).

Remark 7.1
.

In
[
Kob08
]
has been given many examples of incompressible connections with positive definite or negative definite Ricci tensors.
Our canonical models provide another family of examples when
(p,d-p)=(d,0)
or
(0,d)
.

As we will see in Section
7.1
, we can define another pseudo-Riemannian metric
h
with remarkable properties: it is in the conformal class of
g
, the geodesics of
\nabla
have constant speed with respect to
h
, and its expression is

h=\frac{g}{\psi^{4}}

where
\psi
is the potential.

In the end, we find in the same conformal class three metrics:

(1)

the flat pseudo-Euclidean metric
g
;

(2)

the Ricci tensor if
\psi>0
(or its opposite if
\psi<0
)
\mathrm{Ric}=\frac{2d-2}{\psi}g
;

(3)

the

h=\frac{g}{\psi^{4}}
.

7.1.

7.3.
Forms and curvature

We saw in Proposition
3.8
that the volume forms preserved by the connection of a canonical model are

\frac{\beta}{\psi^{d+1}}dx_{1}\wedge\dots\wedge dx_{d}

with
\beta\in\mathbb{R}^{*}
. We make here two remarks.

Canonical model
\mathcal{S}_{1}^{+}(d,0)
, i.e. the one whose domain is all
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, has a finite total volume for any of these forms. We can check that for any other canonical model, the total volume is infinite.

The preserved volume forms
\omega
are different from the volume form induced by the flat metric:

\operatorname{vol}_{g}=dx_{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge dx_{d}

and from the volume forms induced by the isochrone metrics
h=(\alpha^{2}/\psi^{4})g
:

\operatorname{vol}_{h}=\frac{\alpha^{d}}{\psi^{2d}}dx_{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge dx_{d}

for some
\beta>0
.

In dimension
d=2
, we would like to push a little bit further the analogy with the Levi-Civita connection of a Riemannian metric
\rho
.
More precisely, in dimension
2
a particular case of the Gauss-Bonnet theorem can be stated in terms of a
curvature form

\kappa_{\rho}=G\operatorname{vol}_{\rho}

where
G
denotes the Gaussian curvature and
\operatorname{vol}_{\rho}
the volume form canonically associated to
\rho
: for any injective loop anticlockwise encompassing an oriented and simply connected domain
U
, the holonomy of the loop is a rotation whose angle, as measured per the orientation, is given by the integral of
\kappa_{\rho}
on
U
with respect to its orientation.

Such a strong statement cannot hold in our case since the local holonomy groups are all of
\operatorname{SL}(2,{\mathbb{R}})
(Proposition
6.5
).
However, first-order infinitesimal holonomies are rotations, and we computed in Equation
4.1
that
M(1,2)=\frac{2}{\psi}\cal J(1,2)
(recall that we fixed the constant
K=2
) where
M(1,2)
is the first-order infinitesimal holonomy associated to basis
(e_{1},e_{2})
of the global chart
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
and
\cal J(1,2)
can be considered as a canonical infinitesimal rotation in the chart.
In view of the geometric interpretation of infinitesimal holonomies, it makes sense to define

(7.1)

\kappa=\kappa_{\nabla}=\frac{2\,dx_{1}\wedge dx_{2}}{\psi}

and call it
curvature form
too, although it only satisfies Gauss-Bonet at the first order (when the loop size tends to
0
).
We defined after Remark
4.9
the Gaussian curvature
G(\nabla,g)
of
\nabla
relative to
g
.
Note that
\kappa
is also the product of this quantity with
\operatorname{vol}_{g}
, i.e.
\kappa_{\nabla}=G(\nabla,g)\operatorname{vol}_{g}
.
It would be tempting to define the
relative curvature
as the quotient of the curvature form
\kappa
by the invariant form
\omega
.
One problem is that the latter is only unique up to multiplication by a constant:

\omega=\frac{\beta}{\psi^{3}}dx_{1}\wedge dx_{2}

for any
\beta\neq 0
.
Anyway, one immediately gets that the relative curvature is
2\psi^{4}/\beta
.
Remarkably, it vanishes on the boundary of the domain of the canonical model.

The curvature form of the isochrone metric is

\kappa_{h}=\frac{8\lambda}{\psi^{2}}dx_{1}\wedge dx_{2}.

It is thus different from the curvature form
\omega
that we defined in equation (
7.1
).

It is interesting to watch the following list:

\begin{gathered}\operatorname{vol}_{g}=dx_{1}\wedge dx_{2},\quad\kappa_{\nabla}=\frac{2}{\psi}dx_{1}\wedge dx_{2},\quad\kappa_{h}=\frac{8\lambda}{\psi^{2}}dx_{1}\wedge dx_{2}\\
\omega=\frac{\beta}{\psi^{3}}dx_{1}\wedge dx_{2},\quad\operatorname{vol}_{h}=\frac{\alpha^{2}}{\psi^{4}}dx_{1}\wedge dx_{2}.\end{gathered}

7.4.
Parameterization of geodesics

Here we explain how to solve explicitly the geodesic equation in the canonical models.
Recall that all geodesics of
\nabla
are straight lines of the pseudo-Euclidean space in which the model sits.
We can either use the isochrone metric mentioned in previous sections or use the results of Section
3.6
.
We choose the second approach, which has the advantage to also work when the derivative vector of the geodesic is in the light cone.

Let
t\mapsto\gamma(t)
be a geodesic and
L
be the affine line in
E
containing the image of
\gamma
.
By proposition
3.16
, the restriction of
\psi
to
F
is a potential for the restriction of
\nabla
to the affine subspace
L
.
Choose any affine bijection
\phi:{\mathbb{R}}\to L
,
s\mapsto x_{0}+se
, where
e\in\overrightarrow{L}
is not zero (it makes sense to take
x_{0}=\gamma(0)
and
e=\dot{\gamma}(0)
but the discussion also works for other choices).
We can consider on
{\mathbb{R}}
the connection
\phi^{*}(\nabla)
, one of whose potentials is given by

\psi_{s}=\psi\circ\phi.

The index
s
is purely symbolic and denotes the variable in which we work.
The geodesic
\gamma
in this new coordinate system becomes a geodesic for
\phi^{*}(\nabla)
.
The function
\psi_{s}
develops as
\psi_{s}(s)=s^{2}q(e)+2(x_{0}\cdot e)s+q(x_{0})+\lambda
.

The potential
\psi_{s}
is a polynomial of degree at most 2 over
{\mathbb{R}}
and we will consider different cases according to its actual degree.
Note
that
\psi_{s}
cannot be the zero polynomial, for otherwise
\psi_{s}
would vanish on

but it cannot vanish at the initial point
\gamma(0)
of the geodesic.
Note that the roots of
\psi_{s}
are in correspondence with the intersection of

with the equipotential
\psi=0
.

By a further affine change of variable
s\mapsto y
we can reduce to studying the geodesic equation of the connection generated by the following five potentials:

(1)

\psi_{y}=1

(2)

\psi_{y}=y

(3)

\psi_{y}=y^{2}-1

(4)

\psi_{y}=y^{2}

(5)

\psi_{y}=y^{2}+1

In the first case, no change of variable is required.
In the second case, only a translation is needed.
This classifies geodesics into five types. One may subdivide (3) into further sub-cases:
|y|<1
and
|y|>1
.

Since
\psi_{y}
vanishes exactly at parameters corresponding to the intersections of
L
and the equipotential
\psi=0
, we know that any connected component of the open subset of
{\mathbb{R}}
where
\psi_{y}
does not vanish corresponds to an interval of

contained in one of the canonical model. Two different such intervals may be contained in the same model, depending on the situation.

Let us denote
\nabla^{y}
the connection in the
y

\psi_{y}
and in our
1

teaches us that the antiderivative
F
of any constant multiple of

trivializes the connection.

Case 1:

\Psi_{y}=1
. Then
\nabla_{y}
is already trivial (is the canonical connection in
{\mathbb{R}}
) and the geodesic

is an affine function of

, defined for all times

y(t)=\alpha t+\beta

for some
\alpha\in{\mathbb{R}}^{*}
and
\beta\in{\mathbb{R}}
.
It follows that in
E
,
\gamma
is defined for all

and is an affine parameterization of

.
Case 1 occurs if and only if

does not intersect the equipotential
\psi=0
. Equivalently when

and
(x_{0}\cdot e)=0
.

Case 2:

\Psi_{y}=y

.

7.5.
Restriction/Extension

It is to be noted that for any affine subspace
F
of
E
, the potential
\psi
is a polynomial of
\operatorname{dim}F
variables and degree at most
2
.
Conversely for any affine space
F
, and real polynomial of
P
degree at most
2
on
F
, it is an exercise in algebra to prove that there exists a nondegenerate pseudo-Riemannian space
(E,q)
of bigger dimension, and an embedding
F\to E
, such that the quadratic form
q
restricted to
F
equals
P
.
It is even possible to choose a single
E
such that all dimension
d
spaces
F
with their
P
can be embedded this way:
E=E^{d,d}
does the job.

It follows in particular that the curvature-free projectively flattened connections, which we recall are images by a projective transformation of the canonical connection, and are given by
\psi(x)=\Lambda(x)
where
\Lambda
is a non-zero affine map, are all well-chosen restrictions of well-chosen canonical models.

The potential should vanish on the boundary of the ball, otherwise
(\mathcal{B}^{d},\nabla)
would not be geodesically complete.
Consequently, the potential can only be
\psi(x)=\|x\|^{2}-1
up to multiplication by a constant, and we obtain canonical model
\mathcal{S}_{-1}^{-}(d,0)
.
∎
