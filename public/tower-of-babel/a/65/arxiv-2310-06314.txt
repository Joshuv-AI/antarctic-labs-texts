Abstract




Power-partible Reduction and Congruences for Schröder Polynomials

Abstract.

In this note, we apply the power-partible reduction to show the following arithmetic properties of large Schröder polynomials
S_{n}(z)
and little Schröder polynomials
s_{n}(z)
:
for any odd prime
p
, nonnegative integer
r\in{\mathbb{N}}
,
\varepsilon\in\{-1,1\}
and
z\in{\mathbb{Z}}
with
\gcd(p,z(z+1))=1
, we have

\sum_{k=0}^{p-1}(2k+1)^{2r+1}\varepsilon^{k}S_{k}(z)\equiv 1\pmod{p}\quad\text{and}\quad\sum_{k=0}^{p-1}(2k+1)^{2r+1}\varepsilon^{k}s_{k}(z)\equiv 0\pmod{p}.

1
Introduction

For
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
, the
n
th large Schröder number
S_{n}
and little Schröder number
s_{n}
are defined respectively by

S_{n}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n}{k}\binom{n+k}{k}\frac{1}{k+1}\quad\text{and}\quad s_{n}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{1}{n}\binom{n}{k}\binom{n}{k-1}2^{n-k}.

It is well-known that
S_{n}=2s_{n}
for
n\in{\mathbb{N}}^{*}
.
Note also that
S_{0}=1
and
s_{0}=0
.

There are many combinatorial objects that are counted by
S_{n}
or
s_{n}
.
One can consult items
A006318
and
A001003
in the
OEIS

[
11
]
for some of their classical combinatorial interpretations.

Arithmetic properties of Schröder numbers are also extensively investigated.
For example,
Sun
[
6
]
proved

\sum_{k=1}^{p-1}\frac{S_{k}}{m^{k}}\equiv\frac{m^{2}-6m+1}{2m}\left(1-\left(\frac{m^{2}-6m+1}{p}\right)\right)\pmod{p},

where
p
is an odd prime,
(-)
is the Legendre symbol and
m
is any integer not divisible by
p
.
Cao and Pan
[
1
]
showed that, for
n\geq 1
and
\alpha\geq 1
,

S_{n+2^{\alpha}}\equiv S_{n}+2^{\alpha+1}\pmod{2^{\alpha+2}}.

In 2016, Liu
[
4
]
confirmed the supercongruence

\sum_{k=1}^{p-1}D_{k}S_{k}\equiv 2p^{3}B_{p-3}-2pH^{*}_{p-1}\pmod{p^{4}},

originally conjectured by Sun
[
6
]
.
Here
D_{n}
denotes the Delannoy number,
B_{n}
denotes the Bernoulli number and
H^{*}_{n}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{(-1)^{k}}{k}

is the alternating harmonic number.

Motivated by the definition of Schröder numbers, Sun
[
7
,
10
]
defined the large Schröder polynomials
S_{n}(z)
and the little Schröder polynomials
s_{n}(z)
as

S_{n}(z)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n}{k}\binom{n+k}{k}\frac{1}{k+1}z^{k}\quad\text{and}\quad s_{n}(z)=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{1}{n}\binom{n}{k}\binom{n}{k-1}z^{k-1}(z+1)^{n-k}.

Clearly,
S_{n}(1)
and
s_{n}(1)
reduce to the
n
th large and little Schröder numbers respectively.
Sun
[
10
, Lemma 2.1]
proved that

(z+1)s_{n}(z)=S_{n}(z),\quad\forall n\in{\mathbb{N}}^{*}

(1.1)

and

2z(2n+1)S_{n}(z)=D_{n+1}(z)-D_{n-1}(z),\quad\forall n\in{\mathbb{N}}^{*},

(1.2)

where
D_{n}(z)
are the
n
th central Delannoy polynomials given by

D_{n}(z)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n}{k}\binom{n+k}{k}z^{k}.

In recent years, Sun
[
8
,
9
,
10
]
presented many amazing congruences involving
D_{n}(z)
,
s_{n}(z)
or Apéry numbers
A_{n}
.
For example, Sun
[
10
]
derived that

\sum_{k=0}^{p-1}kD_{k}(z)s_{k+1}(z)\equiv 2(z(z+1))^{(p-1)/2}\pmod{p}.

In 2014,
Sun
[
8
]
proved that

\sum_{k=0}^{p-1}(2k+1)D_{k}(z)\equiv\frac{p}{z}((z+1)^{p}-1)\pmod{p^{3}},

if
z\in{\mathbb{Z}}
and
p
is an odd prime with
\gcd(p,z)=1
.
Recently, Xia and Sun
[
15
]
confirmed the conjecture posed by Sun
[
9
]
that
for each
r\in{\mathbb{N}}
and prime
p>3
, there is a
p
-adic integer
c_{r}
only depending on
r
such that

\sum_{k=0}^{p-1}(2k+1)^{2r+1}(-1)^{k}A_{k}\equiv c_{r}p\left(\frac{p}{3}\right)\pmod{p^{3}}.

For positive integers
m
and
\alpha
, the arithmetic property of

\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\varepsilon^{k}(2k+1)A_{k}^{(\alpha)}(z)^{m}

has also been discussed by Guo and Zeng
[
2
]
and Pan
[
5
]
.
Here
\varepsilon\in\{-1,1\}
and
A_{k}^{(\alpha)}(x)
is the generalized Apéry polynomial, which is also called Schmidt polynomials in
[
2
]
.

In 2021, Hou, Mu and Zeilberger
[
3
]
introduced an algorithmic process, which is called the power-partible reduction, to derive congruences for hypergeometric terms mechanically.
Recently, Wang and Zhong
[
14
]
generalized the reduction to holonomic sequences and utilized it to study the arithmetic properties of

\sum_{k=0}^{p-1}(2k+1)^{2r}\varepsilon^{k}F_{k}\quad\text{and}\quad\sum_{k=0}^{p-1}(2k+1)^{2r+1}\varepsilon^{k}F_{k},

where
\varepsilon\in\{-1,1\}
and
F_{k}
are Apéry numbers
A_{k}
or central Delannoy polynomials
D_{k}(z)
.

By Zeilberger’s algorithm
[
16
,
17
,
18
]
and the definition of power-partibility given in
[
14
]
, it is straightforward to check that both
S_{n}(z)
and
s_{n}(z)
are power-partible.
However, the original technique used in
[
14
]
loses its power when we consider the arithmetic properties of
\sum\limits_{k=0}^{p-1}(2k+1)^{2r+1}\varepsilon^{k}S_{k}(z)
or
\sum\limits_{k=0}^{p-1}(2k+1)^{2r+1}\varepsilon^{k}s_{k}(z)
.

In this note, we extend the technique of the power-partible reduction and then use it to deduce the following congruences for
S_{n}(z)
and
s_{n}(z)
.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
p
be an odd prime,
\varepsilon\in\{-1,1\}
and
z\in{\mathbb{Z}}
with
\gcd(p,z(z+1))=1
.
Then for all
r\in{\mathbb{N}}
, we have

\sum_{k=0}^{p-1}(2k+1)^{2r+1}\varepsilon^{k}S_{k}(z)\equiv 1\pmod{p}\ \text{ and }\ \sum_{k=0}^{p-1}(2k+1)^{2r+1}\varepsilon^{k}s_{k}(z)\equiv 0\pmod{p}.

(1.3)

Taking
z=1
in Theorem
1.1
yields the following congruences for Schröder numbers.

Corollary 1.2
.

Let
p
be an odd prime and
\varepsilon\in\{-1,1\}
. Then for all
r\in{\mathbb{N}}
, we have

\sum_{k=0}^{p-1}(2k+1)^{2r+1}\varepsilon^{k}S_{k}\equiv 1\pmod{p}\quad\text{and}\quad\sum_{k=0}^{p-1}(2k+1)^{2r+1}\varepsilon^{k}s_{k}\equiv 0\pmod{p}.

The rest of the note is organized as follows.
Section 2 sums up basic techniques of power-partible reduction developed in
[
14
]
and provides an extended version (Theorem 2.1).
A proof of Theorem
1.1
based on the extended reduction technique is presented in Section 3.

2
Extended power-partible reduction

Let
{\mathbb{K}}
be a field of characteristic
0
and
{\mathbb{K}}[k]
the polynomial ring over
{\mathbb{K}}
.
The set of
annihilators
of
F(k)
is defined by

\ann F(k):=\left\{L=\sum_{i=0}^{J}a_{i}(k)\sigma^{i}\in{\mathbb{K}}[k][\sigma]\mid L(F(k))=0\right\},

where
J\in{\mathbb{N}}=\{0,1,2,\ldots\}
and
\sigma
is the shift operator (that is,
\sigma F(k)=F(k+1)
).

A sequence
F(k)
is said to be
holonomic
(or,
P-recursive
) if and only if
\ann F(k)\neq\{0\}
.
The
order
of
L=\sum_{i=0}^{J}a_{i}(k)\sigma^{i}\in{\mathbb{K}}[k][\sigma]
is defined to be
J
if
a_{J}(k)\neq 0
, denoted by
\ord(L)=J
.
The minimum order of all
L\in\ann F(k)\setminus\{0\}
is called the
order
of
F(k)
.
A holonomic sequence
F(k)
of order
J
is called
summable
if

F(k)=\Delta\left(\sum_{i=0}^{J-1}u_{i}(k)F(k+i)\right)

holds for some rational functions
u_{i}(k)\in{\mathbb{K}}(k)
, where
\Delta=\sigma-1
is the difference operator.

Given an operator
L=\sum\limits_{i=0}^{J}a_{i}(k)\sigma^{i}\in{\mathbb{K}}[k][\sigma]
of order
J
,
the
adjoint
of
L
is defined by

L^{\ast}=\sum_{i=0}^{J}\sigma^{-i}a_{i}(k),

that is,

L^{\ast}(x(k))=\sum_{i=0}^{J}a_{i}(k-i)x(k-i)

for any
x(k)\in{\mathbb{K}}[k]
.
In 2018, van der Hoeven
[
12
]
showed that,
if
L=\sum_{i=0}^{J}a_{i}(k)\sigma^{i}\in\ann F(k)
,
then for any
x(k)\in{\mathbb{K}}[k]
one has

L^{\ast}(x(k))F(k)=\Delta\left(-\sum_{i=0}^{J-1}u_{i}(k)F(k+i)\right),

(2.1)

where

u_{i}(k)=\sum_{j=1}^{J-i}a_{i+j}(k-j)x(k-j),\quad i=0,1,2,\ldots,J-1.

Summing over
k
from
0
to
n-1
on both sides of identity (
2.1
) leads to

\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}L^{\ast}(x(k))F(k)=\left(\sum_{i=0}^{J-1}u_{i}(0)F(i)\right)-\left(\sum_{i=0}^{J-1}u_{i}(n)F(n+i)\right).

(2.2)

The
degree
of
L=\sum\limits_{i=0}^{J}a_{i}(k)\sigma^{i}\in{\mathbb{K}}[k][\sigma]
, denoted by
\deg L
, is given by

\deg L=\max_{0\leq\ell\leq J}\{\deg b_{\ell}(k)-\ell\},

where
b_{\ell}(k)=\sum_{j={\ell}}^{J}\binom{j}{\ell}a_{J-j}(k+j-J)
.
Let

R_{L}=\{s\in{\mathbb{N}}\mid\sum_{\ell=0}^{J}[k^{d+\ell}](b_{\ell}(k))s^{\underline{\ell}}=0\}.

Here
[k^{d+\ell}](b_{\ell}(k))
denotes the coefficient of
k^{d+\ell}
in
b_{\ell}(k)
and
s^{\underline{\ell}}
denotes the falling factorial
s(s-1)\cdots(s-\ell+1)
.
Then
L
is called
nondegenerated
if
R_{L}=\emptyset
, and
degenerated
otherwise.

When
L=\sum\limits_{i=0}^{J}a_{i}(k)\sigma^{i}\in{\mathbb{K}}[k][\sigma]
is nondegenerated and there exists a
\gamma\in{\mathbb{K}}
such that

a_{i}(\gamma+k)=(-1)^{\deg L}a_{J-i}(\gamma-k-J),\quad i=0,1,\ldots,\lfloor\frac{J}{2}\rfloor,

then
L
is called
power-partible
with respect to
\gamma

[
14
]
.
If
L\in\ann F(k)
, we also say
F(k)
is power-partible with respect to
\gamma
.

Wang and Zhong
[
13
, Lemma 2.5]
showed that

\deg L^{\ast}(x(k))=\deg x(k)+\deg L,\quad\forall x(k)\in{\mathbb{K}}[k],

if
L
is nondegenerated.
If further
L
is power-partible with respect to
\gamma
, then we can show that
L^{*}(x(k))
is a linear combination of purely odd powers
(k-\gamma)^{2i+1}
or purely even powers
(k-\gamma)^{2j}
for those
x(k)\in{\mathbb{K}}[k]
satisfying certain symmetry condition.

Theorem 2.1
.

Suppose
L=\sum_{i=0}^{J}a_{i}(k)\sigma^{i}\in{\mathbb{K}}[k][\sigma]\setminus\{0\}
is of order
J
and degree
d
, and power-partible with respect to some
\gamma\in{\mathbb{K}}
.
Let
\ell\in{\mathbb{N}}
and
x_{j}(k)\in{\mathbb{K}}[k]
(
j=\ell,\ell+1,\ldots
) be a series of polynomials with
\deg x_{j}(k)=j
such that

x_{j}(\gamma+k)=(-1)^{\deg x_{j}(k)}x_{j}(\gamma-k-J).

(2.3)

Then
L^{*}(x_{j}(k))
can be written as a linear combination of
(k-\gamma)^{t}
over
{\mathbb{K}}
with
t\equiv d+j\pmod{2}
.
Therefore, for any
m\in{\mathbb{N}}^{*}
, there exist some
u_{i},v_{j}\in{\mathbb{K}}
such that

(k-\gamma)^{m}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq i<d+\ell\\
i\equiv m\pmod{2}\end{subarray}}u_{i}(k-\gamma)^{i}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\ell\leq j\leq m-d\\
d+j\equiv m\pmod{2}\end{subarray}}v_{j}L^{\ast}(x_{j}(k)).

(2.4)

Proof.
Theorem
2.1
, a slight generalization of Theorem 2.4 and 2.6 in
[
14
]
, follows directly from the proof of Theorem 2.4 in
[
14
]
.

We call (
2.4
) a
power-partible reduction
of
(k-\gamma)^{m}
using
L
and
x_{j}(k)
,
j\geq\ell
.
Next, we will discuss the structure of those polynomials
x(k)
satisfying the symmetry condition (
2.3
).
Let

x_{1}(k)=k-\gamma+\frac{J}{2}\quad\text{and}\quad x_{2}(k)=(k-\gamma+\frac{J}{2})^{2}-q

with
q\in{\mathbb{K}}
.
Then it is clear that (
2.3
) holds for both
x_{1}(k)
and
x_{2}(k)
.
Notice that the symmetry condition (
2.3
) is multiplicative, namely, if (
2.3
) holds for
x(k)
and
y(k)
, then it must also hold for the product
x(k)y(k)
.
Therefore, the
x_{j}(k)
satisfying (
2.3
) can be chosen as the product

\alpha\prod_{i}\tilde{x}_{i}(k)

(2.5)

where
\alpha\in{\mathbb{K}}
is a constant and
\tilde{x}_{i}(k)
equals
x_{1}(k)
or
x_{2}(k)
.

The following Lemma shows that if we allow
q
to be an element in the algebraic closure
\overline{{\mathbb{K}}}
of
{\mathbb{K}}
, then all polynomials satisfying (
2.3
) are of the form (
2.5
).

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
\gamma\in{\mathbb{K}}
,
J\in{\mathbb{N}}^{*}
,
x(k)\in{\mathbb{K}}[k]
. Then we have

x(\gamma+k)=(-1)^{\deg x(k)}x(\gamma-k-J)

(2.6)

if and only if
x(k)
can be written as a constant multiple of a product of finitely many polynomials of

k-\gamma+\frac{J}{2}\quad\text{and}\quad(k-\gamma+\frac{J}{2})^{2}-q,

(2.7)

where
q\in\overline{{\mathbb{K}}}
.

Proof.
The sufficiency is clear from the discussion above.
We proceed to show the necessity.
Suppose
x(k)\in{\mathbb{K}}[k]
satisfies (
2.6
).
If
x(k)
is a constant, the conclusion is trivially true.

Now suppose
x(k)
is not a constant and
\tilde{k}\in\overline{{\mathbb{K}}}
is one of its roots.
Then we can write
\tilde{k}=\gamma-\frac{J}{2}-\tilde{q}
for some
\tilde{q}\in\overline{{\mathbb{K}}}
.
By (
2.6
), one can see
\gamma-\frac{J}{2}+\tilde{q}
must also be a root of
x(k)
.
If
\tilde{q}=0
, then
k-\gamma+\frac{J}{2}
is a factor of
x(k)
;
otherwise,

(k-\gamma+\frac{J}{2}+\tilde{q})(k-\gamma+\frac{J}{2}-\tilde{q})=(k-\gamma+\frac{J}{2})^{2}-\tilde{q}^{2}

(2.8)

is a factor of
x(k)
.
In either case, we can divide
x(k)
by the factor to get a new polynomial still satisfying (
2.6
) but with a lower degree.
Taking
q=\tilde{q}^{2}
in (
2.8
), the necessity then follows by induction.

When doing the power-partible reduction in
[
14
]
,
x_{j}(k)
were chosen to be constant multiples of
(k-\gamma+\frac{J}{2})^{j}
.
The appearance of
x_{2}(k)=(k-\gamma+\frac{J}{2})^{2}-q
here allows more flexibility, which finally leads to a proof of Theorem
1.1
in Section 3.

3
New congruences for Schröder polynomials

Recall that the large Schröder polynomials
S_{n}(z)
and the little Schröder polynomials
s_{n}(z)
are defined respectively by

S_{n}(z)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n}{k}\binom{n+k}{k}\frac{1}{k+1}z^{k}\text{ and }s_{n}(z)=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{1}{n}\binom{n}{k}\binom{n}{k-1}z^{k-1}(z+1)^{n-k}.

In this section,
z
is taken to be a parameter in
{\mathbb{Z}}
.
We will assume
z(z+1)\neq 0
since
p
is an odd prime and
\gcd(p,z(z+1))=1
in Theorem.
Then by the connection (
1.1
), we know
S_{n}(z)
and
s_{n}(z)
have the same recurrence relations and thus
\ann S_{n}(z)=\ann s_{n}(z)
if we take
{\mathbb{K}}={\mathbb{Q}}
to be the field of rational numbers.

To simplify the discussion, we will take
\varepsilon\in\{-1,1\}
and

F_{n}(z)=\varepsilon^{n}S_{n}(z)\text{ or }\varepsilon^{n}s_{n}(z).

By Zeilberger’s algorithm, we find that

L=a_{2}(k)\sigma^{2}+a_{1}(k)\sigma+a_{0}(k)\in\ann F_{k}(z),

(3.1)

where

a_{2}(k)=k+3,a_{1}(k)=-\varepsilon(2k+3)(1+2z),a_{0}(k)=k.

It is easy to check that
J=\ord L=2
,
d=\deg L=1
,
L
is nondegenerated and

a_{0}(\gamma+k)=(-1)^{d}a_{2}(\gamma-k-J)\quad\text{and}\quad a_{1}(\gamma+k)=(-1)^{d}a_{1}(\gamma-k-J),

for
\gamma=-\frac{1}{2}
.
That is,
F_{k}(z)
is power-partible with respect to
\gamma=-\frac{1}{2}
.
By the identity (
2.2
), for any polynomial
x_{s}(k)\in{\mathbb{K}}[k]
of degree
s
, we have

\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}L^{\ast}(x_{s}(k))F_{k}(z)=(u_{0}(0)F_{0}(z)+u_{1}(0)F_{1}(z))-\left(u_{0}(n)F_{n}(z)+u_{1}(n)F_{n+1}(z)\right),

(3.2)

where
u_{0}(n)=(n+1)x_{s}(n-2)-\varepsilon(2n+1)(1+2z)x_{s}(n-1)
and
u_{1}(n)=(n+2)x_{s}(n-1).

To obtain congruences after reduction later, we need to eliminate the initial term
u_{0}(0)F_{0}(z)+u_{1}(0)F_{1}(z)=\left(x(-2)-\varepsilon(1+2z)x_{s}(-1)\right)F_{0}(z)+2x_{s}(-1)F_{1}(z)
.
Since
\gamma=-\frac{1}{2}
and
J=2
.
Taking
q=1/4
in (
2.7
), we have
(k+\frac{3}{2})^{2}-\frac{1}{4}=(k+1)(k+2)
is one of the basic forms in (
2.7
).
Then by Lemma
2.2
we can choose

x_{s+2}(k)=2(2k+3)^{s}(k+1)(k+2),\quad s\in{\mathbb{N}}

(3.3)

to proceed the power-partible reduction in (
2.4
) and eliminate the initial term in (
3.2
) at the same time.
Note that
d=1
and
\ell=2
.
By Theorem
2.1
, for each
r\in{\mathbb{N}}
, there are constants
c_{s},c\in{\mathbb{K}}
such that

(2k+1)^{2r+1}=\sum_{s=0}^{r-1}c_{s}L^{\ast}(x_{2s+2}(k))+c(2k+1).

The following lemma gives a more concrete description of the constants
c_{s}
and
c
.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
L
be given by (
3.1
) and
x_{s+2}(k)
given by (
3.3
). Then for any
r\in{\mathbb{N}}
we have

(2k+1)^{2r+1}=\sum_{s=0}^{r-1}\frac{v_{s}}{\eta^{u_{s}}}L^{\ast}(x_{2s+2}(k))+(2k+1),

(3.4)

for some
u_{s}\in{\mathbb{N}}
and
v_{s}\in{\mathbb{Z}}
, where
\eta=\frac{1-\varepsilon(1+2z)}{2}\in{\mathbb{Z}}
.

Proof.
For simplicity, let
\ell=2k+1
. By the definition of
L^{\ast}
, we have

\displaystyle L^{\ast}(x_{s+2}(k))=\sum_{i=0}^{2}a_{i}(k-i)x_{s+2}(k-i)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2k(k+1)\left((k+2)(2k+3)^{s}-\varepsilon(2k+1)(1+2z)(2k+1)^{s}+(k-1)(2k-1)^{s}\right)

(3.5)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4}(\ell-1)(\ell^{2}+4\ell+3)(\ell+2)^{s}+\frac{1}{4}(\ell+1)(\ell^{2}-4\ell+3)(\ell-2)^{s}-\frac{\varepsilon(1+2z)(\ell^{2}-1)\ell^{s+1}}{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\ell^{3}-\ell)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=0\\
j\text{ even}\end{subarray}}^{s}\binom{s}{j}2^{j-1}\ell^{s-j}+(3\ell^{2}-3)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=1\\
j\text{ odd}\end{subarray}}^{s}\binom{s}{j}2^{j-1}\ell^{s-j}-\frac{\varepsilon(1+2z)(\ell^{2}-1)\ell^{s+1}}{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\eta\ell^{s+3}-\eta\ell^{s+1}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=2\\
j\text{ even}\end{subarray}}^{s}\binom{s}{j}2^{j-1}\ell^{s-j}(\ell^{3}-\ell)+(3\ell^{2}-3)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=1\\
j\text{ odd}\end{subarray}}^{s}\binom{s}{j}2^{j-1}\ell^{s-j}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\eta\ell^{s+3}+\sum_{j=1}^{\lfloor\frac{s+3}{2}\rfloor}e_{j}\ell^{s+3-2j},

where
\eta=\frac{1-\varepsilon(1+2z)}{2}\in{\mathbb{Z}}
and
e_{j}\in{\mathbb{Z}}
for all
j=1,2,\ldots,\lfloor\frac{s+3}{2}\rfloor
.
By the expression for
L^{\ast}(x_{s+2})
, the power-partible reduction on
(2k+1)^{2r+1}
reveals that

(2k+1)^{2r+1}=\sum_{s=0}^{r-1}\frac{v_{s}}{\eta^{u_{s}}}L^{\ast}(x_{2s+2}(k))+\frac{v}{\eta^{u}}(2k+1),

(3.6)

for some
u_{s},u\in{\mathbb{N}}
,
v_{s},v\in{\mathbb{Z}}
.

By the expression (
3
) for
L^{\ast}(x_{s+2}(k))
, we know
k
divides
L^{\ast}(x_{s+2}(k))
, namely,
k=0
is a root of
L^{\ast}(x_{s+2})
.
Letting
k=0
in (
3.6
), one can see
\frac{v}{\eta^{u}}=1
.
This completes the proof.

Now we consider the arithmetic properties of
\sum_{k=0}^{p-1}L^{\ast}(x_{2s+2}(k))F_{k}(z)
and
\sum_{k=0}^{p-1}(2k+1)F_{k}(z)
, respectively.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
L
be given by (
3.1
).
Then for any
n,z\in{\mathbb{Z}}
with
n>1
and polynomial
y(k)\in{\mathbb{Z}}[k]
, we have

\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}L^{\ast}(y(k)(k+1)(k+2))F_{k}(z)\equiv 0\pmod{n(n^{2}-1)}.

Proof.
Let
x(k)=y(k)(k+1)(k+2)
, equality (
3.2
) reduces to

\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}L^{\ast}(y(k)(k+1)(k+2))F_{k}(z)=-\left(u_{0}(n)F_{n}(z)+u_{1}(n)F_{n+1}(z)\right),

(3.7)

here
u_{0}(n)=n(n+1)((n-1)y(n-2)-\varepsilon(2n+1)(1+2z)y(n-1))
and
u_{1}(n)=n(n+1)(n+2)y(n-1).

Since
L\in\ann F_{k}(z)=0
, apparently

(n+2)F_{n+1}(z)=\varepsilon(2n+1)(1+2z)F_{n}(z)-(n-1)F_{n-1}(z),\quad\forall n\geq 1.

(3.8)

Substituting identity (
3.8
) into identity (
3.7
), we derive

\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}L^{\ast}(y(k)(k+1)(k+2))F_{k}(z)=n(n^{2}-1)\left(y(n-1)F_{n-1}(z)-y(n-2)F_{n}(z)\right).

(3.9)

This completes the proof.

Letting
y(k)=2(2k+3)^{s}
in (
3.9
), we get the following corollary.

Corollary 3.3
.

Let
L
be given by (
3.1
) and
x_{s}(k)
given by (
3.3
).
Then for any
z\in{\mathbb{Z}}
and
n,s\in{\mathbb{N}}
with
n>1
, we have

\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}L^{\ast}(x_{s+2}(k))F_{k}(z)\equiv 0\pmod{2n(n^{2}-1)}.

The next lemma is the last piece of the puzzle.

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
p
be an odd prime,
z\in{\mathbb{Z}}
with
\gcd(p,z(z+1))=1
.
Then we have

\sum_{k=0}^{p-1}(2k+1)\varepsilon^{k}S_{k}(z)\equiv 1\pmod{p}\quad\text{and}\quad\sum_{k=0}^{p-1}(2k+1)\varepsilon^{k}s_{k}(z)\equiv 0\pmod{p},

where
\varepsilon\in\{-1,1\}
.

Proof.
By equality (
1.2
), we have

\displaystyle 2z\sum_{k=0}^{p-1}(2k+1)\varepsilon^{k}S_{k}(z)

\displaystyle=2z+\sum_{k=1}^{p-1}\varepsilon^{k}2z(2k+1)S_{k}(z)

\displaystyle=2z+\sum_{k=1}^{p-1}\varepsilon^{k}(D_{k+1}(z)-D_{k-1}(z))

\displaystyle=2z+D_{p}(z)+\varepsilon D_{p-1}(z)-D_{1}(z)-\varepsilon D_{0}(z).

Since
D_{0}(z)=1,D_{1}(z)=1+2z
, we deduce that

2z\sum_{k=0}^{p-1}(2k+1)\varepsilon^{k}S_{k}(z)=D_{p}(z)+\varepsilon D_{p-1}(z)-1-\varepsilon.

(3.10)

It is clear to see that

\displaystyle D_{p}(z)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{p}\binom{p}{k}\binom{p+k}{k}z^{k}

\displaystyle=1+\sum_{k=1}^{p-1}\binom{p}{k}\binom{p+k}{k}z^{k}+\binom{2p}{p}z^{p}

\displaystyle\equiv 1+\binom{2p}{p}z^{p}\pmod{p}

\displaystyle\equiv 1+2z\pmod{p},

and

\displaystyle D_{p-1}(z)=\sum_{k=0}^{p-1}\binom{p-1}{k}\binom{p-1+k}{k}z^{k}=1+\sum_{k=1}^{p-1}\binom{p-1}{k}\binom{p-1+k}{k}z^{k}\equiv 1\pmod{p}.

From the above two congruences and identity (
3.10
), we obtain

2z\sum_{k=0}^{p-1}(2k+1)\varepsilon^{k}S_{k}(z)\equiv 2z\pmod{p}.

(3.11)

Since
p
is an odd prime and
\gcd(p,z)=1
, the first congruence in Lemma
3.4
follows from (
3.11
).
By the fact that
S_{k}(z)=(1+z)s_{k}(z)
for all
k\in{\mathbb{N}}^{*}
and
S_{0}(z)=1,s_{0}(z)=0
, it follows from (
3.11
) that

2z(1+z)\sum_{k=0}^{p-1}(2k+1)\varepsilon^{k}s_{k}(z)\equiv 0\pmod{p}.

The second congruence then follows from the condition that
p
is an odd prime and
\gcd(p,z(z+1))=1
.

Proof of Theorem
1.1
:
Multiplying both sides of identity (
3.4
) with
F_{k}(z)
and then
summing over
k
from
0
to
p-1
, we get

\sum_{k=0}^{p-1}(2k+1)^{2r+1}F_{k}(z)\equiv\sum_{k=0}^{p-1}(2k+1)F_{k}(z)\pmod{p}

with the help of Corollary
3.3
and the fact that
\gcd(p,\eta)=1
.
Congruence (
1.3
) then follows immediately from Lemma
3.4
.
