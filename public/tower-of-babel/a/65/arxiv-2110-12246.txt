Parametric Variational Linear Units (PVLUs) in Deep Convolutional Networks
Shikhar Ahuja, Aarush Gupta

Abstract
The Rectified Linear Unit is currently a state-of-the-art activation function in deep convolutional neural networks. To combat ReLU’s dying neuron problem, we propose the Parametric Variational Linear Unit (PVLU), which adds a sinusoidal function with trainable coefficients to ReLU. Along with introducing nonlinearity and non-zero gradients across the entire real domain, PVLU acts as a mechanism of fine-tuning when implemented in the context of transfer learning. On a simple, non-transfer sequential CNN, PVLU substitution allowed for relative error decreases of 16.3% and 11.3% (without and with data augmentation) on CIFAR-100. PVLU is also tested on transfer learning models. The VGG-16 and VGG-19 models experience relative error reductions of 9.5% and 10.7% on CIFAR-10, respectively, after the substitution of ReLU with PVLU. When training on Gaussian-filtered CIFAR-10 images, similar improvements are noted for the VGG models. Most notably, fine-tuning using PVLU allows for relative error reductions up to and exceeding 10% for near state-of-the-art residual neural network architectures on the CIFAR datasets.



1
Introduction

The Rectified Linear Unit, known as ReLU, has become a standard function in deep neural networks
(
Nair and Hinton 2010
)
. Sigmoidal activation functions saturate as inputs deviate from zero, meaning that their gradients vanish and significant training fails to occur. This follows from noting that
\sigma^{\prime}(x)=\sigma(x)(1-\sigma(x))
and
\sigma(x)\to 1
as
x\to\infty
or
x\to-\infty
.

However, ReLU suffers from its own key issue known as the dying neuron problem. Due to its derivative being zero for negative inputs, the use of ReLU may sometimes zero gradients and completely stop local weight updates during training
(
Gu et al. 2017
)
. The issue becomes even more prevalent for deeper networks — just a few dying neurons can zero the long chain rule expressions used to compute training gradients. It has been mathematically proven that the use of ReLU will necessarily lead to the dying neuron problem in the limit of an infinitely deep network
(
Lu et al. 2020
)
.

Variants of ReLU have been created in the past to address its various issues. Three popular variants are the Exponential Linear Unit (ELU)
(
Clevert et al. 2016
)
, the Leaky Rectified Linear Unit (Leaky ReLU)
(
Maas et al. 2013
)
, and the Parametric Rectified Linear Unit (PReLU)
(
He et al. 2015a
)
. All three have gotten results in a variety of datasets but have their respective issues. ELU’s gradients saturate while Leaky ReLU and PReLU remain unbounded for negative domains.

Figure 1:
Behavior of ReLU, Leaky ReLU, and ELU for
x<0
.

While less common, sinusoidal aspects have been incorporated in the past into activation functions
(
Sitzmann et al. 2020
)
. In the context of our work, one relevant example is the SineReLU, available in Keras’s contrib module
(
Chollet et al. 2015
)
. The function is written as the following (assuming
\epsilon
is a hyperparameter):

\mathrm{SineReLU}(x)=\begin{cases}x&\text{if $x\leq 0$}\\
\epsilon(\sin{x}-\cos{x})&\text{if $x>1$}\end{cases}

The SineReLU activation can assist with the dying neuron problem and we further extend on its findings. In doing so, we propose the Parametric Variational Linear Unit (PVLU), which is built up from first considering a simpler Variational Linear Unit (VLU). While it shares similarities to the SineReLU activation discussed before, PVLU introduces a variety of novel features that will be discussed further in our paper.
In our paper, we first outline the VLU’s theoretical basis and definition. Then, we extend our proposal to a parametrized VLU, or PVLU — applications in fine tuning and transfer learning are also discussed. Empirically, we demonstrate that PVLU performs better than ReLU on a variety of models running on the CIFAR-10 and CIFAR-100 dataset. These include everything from sequential, non-transfer CNNs to deep resiudal network models.

2
Variational Linear Unit

We first propose the Variational Linear Unit (VLU), which is defined as

\displaystyle\mathrm{VLU}(x)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{ReLU}(x)+\alpha\sin{(\beta x)}

\displaystyle=\max(0,x)+\alpha\sin{(\beta x)}

where
\alpha
and
\beta
are hyperparameters that can be chosen based on the model. Larger values for both of these parameters inject more periodic variation into the ReLU function. For
\alpha=0
or
\beta=0
, no variation is present.

Note that there are significant differences between the previously seen SineReLU and the currently proposed VLU. VLU introduces a sinusoidal variation throughout the entire real domain
x\in\mathbb{R}
. This differs from not only SineReLU, but also a significant number of previously seen ReLU variants which only alter outputs for domain
x<0
. In doing so, we add nonlinearity throughout the entire activation function while also preserving one of ReLU’s most important properties, which is its unbounded nature for positive values. Similar to SineReLU, adding non-monotonicity to the ReLU activation function may be beneficial as was observed with the Swish activation function
(
Ramachandran et al. 2017
)
.

\alpha
and
\beta
.

We can also analyze VLU’s properties more rigorously. Let
\mathbf{a^{\ell}}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{\ell}}
be the set of activations
a_{1},a_{2},a_{3},\dots,a_{N^{\ell}}
in layer
\ell
, where
N_{\ell}
is the number of neurons in that layer. Then, we define
z^{\ell}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{\ell}}
as
W^{\ell-1}\mathbb{a}^{\ell-1}+\mathbb{b}^{\ell-1}
, where
\mathbb{b^{\ell}}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{\ell}}
is the bias vector.
W^{\ell-1}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{\ell-1}\times N_{\ell}}
is the weight matrix connecting layers
\ell-1
and
\ell
.

Assuming weight
W_{ij}^{\ell}
connects the
j
-th neuron in layer
\ell
and the
i
-th neuron in layer
\ell+1
, recall that

\displaystyle z_{i}^{\ell+1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{N_{\ell}}\left(W_{ij}^{\ell}a_{j}^{\ell}+b_{j}^{\ell}\right)

\displaystyle a_{i}^{\ell+1}

\displaystyle=\alpha\sin{(\beta z_{i}^{\ell+1})}+\mathrm{ReLU}(z_{i}^{\ell+1})

Then, the partial derivatives of the cost function
\mathcal{C}
with respect to the network weights and biases are:

\displaystyle\mathchoice{\frac{\partial\mkern 0.0mu\mathcal{C}}{{\partial\mkern 0.0muW_{ij}^{\ell}}\,}}{\displaystyle{\frac{\partial\mkern 0.0mu\mathcal{C}}{{\partial\mkern 0.0muW_{ij}^{\ell}}\,}}}{\scriptstyle{\frac{\partial\mkern 0.0mu\mathcal{C}}{{\partial\mkern 0.0muW_{ij}^{\ell}}\,}}}{\scriptstyle{\frac{\partial\mkern 0.0mu\mathcal{C}}{{\partial\mkern 0.0muW_{ij}^{\ell}}\,}}}

\displaystyle=\mathchoice{\frac{\partial\mkern 0.0mu\mathcal{C}}{{\partial\mkern 0.0mua_{i}^{\ell+1}}\,}}{\displaystyle{\frac{\partial\mkern 0.0mu\mathcal{C}}{{\partial\mkern 0.0mua_{i}^{\ell+1}}\,}}}{\scriptstyle{\frac{\partial\mkern 0.0mu\mathcal{C}}{{\partial\mkern 0.0mua_{i}^{\ell+1}}\,}}}{\scriptstyle{\frac{\partial\mkern 0.0mu\mathcal{C}}{{\partial\mkern 0.0mua_{i}^{\ell+1}}\,}}}\mathchoice{\frac{\partial\mkern 0.0mu{{a_{i}^{\ell+1}}}}{{\partial\mkern 0.0muW_{ij}^{\ell}}\,}}{\displaystyle{\frac{\partial\mkern 0.0mu{{a_{i}^{\ell+1}}}}{{\partial\mkern 0.0muW_{ij}^{\ell}}\,}}}{\scriptstyle{\frac{\partial\mkern 0.0mu{{a_{i}^{\ell+1}}}}{{\partial\mkern 0.0muW_{ij}^{\ell}}\,}}}{\scriptstyle{\frac{\partial\mkern 0.0mu{{a_{i}^{\ell+1}}}}{{\partial\mkern 0.0muW_{ij}^{\ell}}\,}}}

\displaystyle=\mathchoice{\frac{\partial\mkern 0.0mu\mathcal{C}}{{\partial\mkern 0.0mua_{i}^{\ell}}\,}}{\displaystyle{\frac{\partial\mkern 0.0mu\mathcal{C}}{{\partial\mkern 0.0mua_{i}^{\ell}}\,}}}{\scriptstyle{\frac{\partial\mkern 0.0mu\mathcal{C}}{{\partial\mkern 0.0mua_{i}^{\ell}}\,}}}{\scriptstyle{\frac{\partial\mkern 0.0mu\mathcal{C}}{{\partial\mkern 0.0mua_{i}^{\ell}}\,}}}a_{j}^{\ell}\left(\alpha\beta\cos{(\beta z_{i}^{\ell+1})+H(z_{i}^{\ell+1})}\right)

\displaystyle\mathchoice{\frac{\partial\mkern 0.0mu\mathcal{C}}{{\partial\mkern 0.0mub_{i}^{\ell}}\,}}{\displaystyle{\frac{\partial\mkern 0.0mu\mathcal{C}}{{\partial\mkern 0.0mub_{i}^{\ell}}\,}}}{\scriptstyle{\frac{\partial\mkern 0.0mu\mathcal{C}}{{\partial\mkern 0.0mub_{i}^{\ell}}\,}}}{\scriptstyle{\frac{\partial\mkern 0.0mu\mathcal{C}}{{\partial\mkern 0.0mub_{i}^{\ell}}\,}}}

\displaystyle=\mathchoice{\frac{\partial\mkern 0.0mu\mathcal{C}}{{\partial\mkern 0.0mua_{i}^{\ell}}\,}}{\displaystyle{\frac{\partial\mkern 0.0mu\mathcal{C}}{{\partial\mkern 0.0mua_{i}^{\ell}}\,}}}{\scriptstyle{\frac{\partial\mkern 0.0mu\mathcal{C}}{{\partial\mkern 0.0mua_{i}^{\ell}}\,}}}{\scriptstyle{\frac{\partial\mkern 0.0mu\mathcal{C}}{{\partial\mkern 0.0mua_{i}^{\ell}}\,}}}\mathchoice{\frac{\partial\mkern 0.0mu{{a_{i}^{\ell}}}}{{\partial\mkern 0.0mub_{i}^{\ell}}\,}}{\displaystyle{\frac{\partial\mkern 0.0mu{{a_{i}^{\ell}}}}{{\partial\mkern 0.0mub_{i}^{\ell}}\,}}}{\scriptstyle{\frac{\partial\mkern 0.0mu{{a_{i}^{\ell}}}}{{\partial\mkern 0.0mub_{i}^{\ell}}\,}}}{\scriptstyle{\frac{\partial\mkern 0.0mu{{a_{i}^{\ell}}}}{{\partial\mkern 0.0mub_{i}^{\ell}}\,}}}

\displaystyle=\mathchoice{\frac{\partial\mkern 0.0mu\mathcal{C}}{{\partial\mkern 0.0mua_{i}^{\ell}}\,}}{\displaystyle{\frac{\partial\mkern 0.0mu\mathcal{C}}{{\partial\mkern 0.0mua_{i}^{\ell}}\,}}}{\scriptstyle{\frac{\partial\mkern 0.0mu\mathcal{C}}{{\partial\mkern 0.0mua_{i}^{\ell}}\,}}}{\scriptstyle{\frac{\partial\mkern 0.0mu\mathcal{C}}{{\partial\mkern 0.0mua_{i}^{\ell}}\,}}}\left(\alpha\beta\cos{(\beta z_{i}^{\ell+1})+H(z_{i}^{\ell+1})}\right)

where

H(x)=\begin{cases}0&\text{if $x\leq 0$}\\
1&\text{if $x>0$}\end{cases}

Note that these gradients are similar to that of ReLU except for the addition of the trigonometric terms. Since sine and cosine do not go to zero for large negative inputs, they help to alleviate the dying neuron problem present in ReLU. However, unlike ReLU, the derivative of our new activation function is no longer static for positive inputs.

3
Parametric VLU

Now we extend the discussion of the VLU to the Parametric VLU, or PVLU. Rather than static constants across the entire network, the parameters
\alpha
and
\beta
are now channelwise and are trained during backpropogation.

Then, our new activation function
A^{\ell}:\mathbb{R}^{N_{\ell}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{N_{\ell}}
in layer
\ell
is defined by the channel-wise parameters
\alpha
and
\beta
like so:

A^{\ell}(\mathbb{z^{\ell}})=\begin{pmatrix}\,\vbox{\kern 2.58334pt\vbox to54.83337pt{\hbox{$\;\;\;\alpha_{1}^{\ell}\sin{(\beta_{1}^{\ell}z_{1}^{\ell})+\mathrm{ReLU}(z_{1}^{\ell})}$}\kern 3.0pt\leaders{\vbox{\hbox to111.90048pt{\hss.\hss}\vskip 4.0pt}}{\vfill}\kern 1.0pt\hbox{$\alpha_{N^{\ell}}^{\ell}\sin{(\beta_{N^{\ell}}^{\ell}z_{N^{\ell}}^{\ell})+\mathrm{ReLU}(z_{N}^{\ell})}$}
}}\,\end{pmatrix}

Using the chain rule, we can thus compute the gradients of the cost function
\mathcal{C}
.

\displaystyle=\mathchoice{\frac{\partial\mkern 0.0mu\mathcal{C}}{{\partial\mkern 0.0mua_{i}^{\ell}}\,}}{\displaystyle{\frac{\partial\mkern 0.0mu\mathcal{C}}{{\partial\mkern 0.0mua_{i}^{\ell}}\,}}}{\scriptstyle{\frac{\partial\mkern 0.0mu\mathcal{C}}{{\partial\mkern 0.0mua_{i}^{\ell}}\,}}}{\scriptstyle{\frac{\partial\mkern 0.0mu\mathcal{C}}{{\partial\mkern 0.0mua_{i}^{\ell}}\,}}}\mathchoice{\frac{\partial\mkern 0.0mu{{a_{i}^{\ell}}}}{{\partial\mkern 0.0mu\alpha_{i}^{\ell}}\,}}{\displaystyle{\frac{\partial\mkern 0.0mu{{a_{i}^{\ell}}}}{{\partial\mkern 0.0mu\alpha_{i}^{\ell}}\,}}}{\scriptstyle{\frac{\partial\mkern 0.0mu{{a_{i}^{\ell}}}}{{\partial\mkern 0.0mu\alpha_{i}^{\ell}}\,}}}{\scriptstyle{\frac{\partial\mkern 0.0mu{{a_{i}^{\ell}}}}{{\partial\mkern 0.0mu\alpha_{i}^{\ell}}\,}}}=\mathchoice{\frac{\partial\mkern 0.0mu\mathcal{C}}{{\partial\mkern 0.0mua_{i}^{\ell}}\,}}{\displaystyle{\frac{\partial\mkern 0.0mu\mathcal{C}}{{\partial\mkern 0.0mua_{i}^{\ell}}\,}}}{\scriptstyle{\frac{\partial\mkern 0.0mu\mathcal{C}}{{\partial\mkern 0.0mua_{i}^{\ell}}\,}}}{\scriptstyle{\frac{\partial\mkern 0.0mu\mathcal{C}}{{\partial\mkern 0.0mua_{i}^{\ell}}\,}}}\sin{(\beta_{i}^{\ell}z_{i}^{\ell})}

\displaystyle=\mathchoice{\frac{\partial\mkern 0.0mu\mathcal{C}}{{\partial\mkern 0.0mua_{i}^{\ell}}\,}}{\displaystyle{\frac{\partial\mkern 0.0mu\mathcal{C}}{{\partial\mkern 0.0mua_{i}^{\ell}}\,}}}{\scriptstyle{\frac{\partial\mkern 0.0mu\mathcal{C}}{{\partial\mkern 0.0mua_{i}^{\ell}}\,}}}{\scriptstyle{\frac{\partial\mkern 0.0mu\mathcal{C}}{{\partial\mkern 0.0mua_{i}^{\ell}}\,}}}\mathchoice{\frac{\partial\mkern 0.0mu{{a_{i}^{\ell}}}}{{\partial\mkern 0.0mu\beta_{i}^{\ell}}\,}}{\displaystyle{\frac{\partial\mkern 0.0mu{{a_{i}^{\ell}}}}{{\partial\mkern 0.0mu\beta_{i}^{\ell}}\,}}}{\scriptstyle{\frac{\partial\mkern 0.0mu{{a_{i}^{\ell}}}}{{\partial\mkern 0.0mu\beta_{i}^{\ell}}\,}}}{\scriptstyle{\frac{\partial\mkern 0.0mu{{a_{i}^{\ell}}}}{{\partial\mkern 0.0mu\beta_{i}^{\ell}}\,}}}=\mathchoice{\frac{\partial\mkern 0.0mu\mathcal{C}}{{\partial\mkern 0.0mua_{i}^{\ell}}\,}}{\displaystyle{\frac{\partial\mkern 0.0mu\mathcal{C}}{{\partial\mkern 0.0mua_{i}^{\ell}}\,}}}{\scriptstyle{\frac{\partial\mkern 0.0mu\mathcal{C}}{{\partial\mkern 0.0mua_{i}^{\ell}}\,}}}{\scriptstyle{\frac{\partial\mkern 0.0mu\mathcal{C}}{{\partial\mkern 0.0mua_{i}^{\ell}}\,}}}z_{i}^{\ell}\cos{(\beta_{i}^{\ell}z_{i}^{\ell})}

3.1
Fine Tuning

One of the major applications of PVLU in transfer learning is fine tuning. Given a pretrained deep model, all occurrences of ReLU can be replaced with PVLU where
\alpha
is initialized to zero. Similar work with the already existing PReLU activation function (and even linear activations) has already been done in the past
(
Dong et al. 2017
)
. This application allows PVLU to enhance models which are already performing at state-of-the-art accuracies.

Specifically, the use of PVLU can be thought of as adding a layer of trainable periodic noise to a network when applied in the context of transfer learning. Noisy activation functions have been shown to improve model performance
(
Gulcehre et al. 2016
)
. What seperates this proposal from previous literature relating to noisy activation functions is that the added noise is not purely entropic.

4
Empirical Results

Initially, we tested shallow architectures on various simple datasets such as MNIST and Fashion-MNIST. To test our proposal, we interchanged ReLU activations with PVLU. However, there was no significant difference or improvement after making this change. This result is logical as the dying neuron problem is not evident or impactful for networks that are not significantly deep. Thus, PVLU may add unnecessary complexity without real results for simple computer vision problems — the function’s real applications are present in deeper and more complex networks.

4.1

For the CIFAR-100 dataset, we utilized a deeper architecture where the dying neuron problem would be prevalent.

For our first tests, we did not use data augmentation in assessing performance performance. To initially investigate the plausibility of PVLU, we test it on a relatively simple convolutional neural network. Our model is adapted from

github.com/andrewkruger/cifar100_CNN

The model has six convolutional layers, each followed by a ReLU activation layer. The model also includes maxpooling, dropout, and dense layers. As a starting point, we run CIFAR-100 without data augmentation. In addition to testing ReLU as a baseline along with PVLU, we compare our proposal to a variety of existing variants (Leaky ReLU, ELU, PreLU). 4.2.1
Noise Adaptability

An important feature of a robust model is an ability to adapt to noise. To test PVLU in this context, a Gaussian filter is added to both training and testing CIFAR-10 data. Gaussian filters are used to blur images by performing convolution operations with a filter whose kernel coefficients follow the form

G(x,y)=\dfrac{1}{2\pi\sigma^{2}}\exp{\left(-\frac{x^{2}+y^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}\right)}

where
x
and
y
are the horizontal and vertical distances from the point at which the convolution is being performed. We set
\sigma=1
when applying the Gaussian filter to CIFAR-10 image data.

Figure 6:
CIFAR image before and after Gaussian filter is applied

First, we fine tune the existing VGG-19 model with ReLU activations on CIFAR-10. Then, this same model is evaluated on the noisy data to find a baseline accuracy. When testing PVLU, we freeze existing model weights while keeping the channelwise values of
\alpha
and
\beta
as trainable.
Allowing all layers to train in a noisy setting leads to sharp gradient updates that can interfere with and destroy representations in the existing transferred model. The only exception to this was for batch normalization layers — setting these to trainable allows for robust model generalization and adaptability
(

5
Conclusion

The Parametric Variational Linear Unit, or PVLU, leads to notable improvements across a wide variety of computer vision problems. By introducing a sinusoidal nonlinearity across the ReLU function, PVLU diminishes the dying neuron problem and also increases activation nonlinearity. In our empirical work, we tested PVLU across shallow non-transfer convolutional neural networks, VGG models, and residual neural networks. We also demonstrated that PVLU can lead to increased robustness when a previously-trained model is introduced to a noisy environment. Observed relative error reductions of roughly
10\%
or more indicate that PVLU is worth further exploring in different contexts.

While this work primary centered around computer vision problems, PVLU may also be investigated in other domains of machine learning problems in the future. Within computer vision, PVLU can be tested on other transfer learning models for training deep networks on even more complex datasets.
