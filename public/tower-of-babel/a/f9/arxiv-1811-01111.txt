Abstract
. Given a family ℱ ⊂ 2 [ n ] {\mathcal{F}}\subset 2^{[n]} , its diversity is the number of sets not containing an element with the highest degree. The concept of diversity has proven to be very useful in the context of k k -uniform intersecting families. In this paper, we study (different notions of) diversity in the context of other extremal set theory problems. One of the main results of the paper is a sharp stability result for cross-intersecting families in terms of diversity and, slightly more generally, sharp stability for the Kruskal–Katona theorem.



1.
Introduction and main results

Given a family
{\mathcal{F}}\subset 2^{[n]}
, we define its
diversity

\gamma({\mathcal{F}})
to be
\gamma({\mathcal{F}}):=\min_{i\in[n]}|\{F\in{\mathcal{F}}:i\notin F\}|
. The
maximum degree

\Delta({\mathcal{F}})
of
{\mathcal{F}}
is
\Delta({\mathcal{F}}):=\max_{i\in[n]}|\{F\in{\mathcal{F}}:i\in F\}|
. That is,
\gamma({\mathcal{F}})+\Delta({\mathcal{F}})=|{\mathcal{F}}|
.
We say that a family
{\mathcal{F}}
is
intersecting
if
F_{1}\cap F_{2}\neq\emptyset
for any
F_{1},F_{2}\in{\mathcal{F}}
. To the best of our knowledge, the problem of maximizing diversity first appeared explicitly in the following question of Katona: what is the maximum possible diversity of an intersecting family
{\mathcal{F}}\subset{[n]\choose k}
? Lemons and Palmer
[
35
]
showed that
\gamma({\mathcal{F}})\leq{n-3\choose k-2}
for
n\geq 6k^{3}
(they used the term “unbalance” instead of diversity). Later, this bound on
n
was improved to
n\geq 6k^{2}
by the first author
[
15
]
and to
n\geq Ck
with some absolute constant
C
by the second author
[
33
]
.

Diversity is also closely related to the concept of influence of a Boolean function, which we discuss later in the paper. Katona’s question was motivated by the work of Dinur and Friedgut
[
5
]
, who studied approximations of intersecting families by juntas using the tools coming from the Analysis of Boolean functions. In particular, using these tools, they showed that
\gamma({\mathcal{F}})=O({n-2\choose k-2})
for
n>Ck
. We note that this result in itself was not new: the first author showed
\gamma({\mathcal{F}})\leq 3{n-2\choose k-2}
for
any

n>2k>0
in
[
12
]
. However, the more general approximation by juntas result of
[
5
]
was an important tool in the proof in
[
33
]
.

Diversity proved to be a very useful concept in the study of intersecting families. It was recently applied to different problems in
[
9
,
17
,
24
,
34
]
. One of the tools that was particularly fruitful is a recent theorem that allows us to bound the size of
k
-uniform intersecting families based on their diversity, proven by the second author and Zakharov in
[
32
]
. This theorem is a stronger version of an old result due the first author
[
12
]
. Both results provide strong and in many cases sharp stability versions of the Erdős-Ko-Rado
[
6
]
theorem, which states that any intersecting family
{\mathcal{F}}\subset{[n]\choose k}
has size at most
{n-1\choose k-1}
, provided
n\geq 2k
. An even stronger version of these theorems, which is sharp in
all
cases, was proven in
[
34
]
.

For a real
x\geq k
, let us put
{x\choose k}:=\frac{x(x-1)\ldots(x-k+1)}{k!}
, and put
{x\choose k}:=0
for
x<k
.

Theorem 1

(
[
32
]
)
.

Let
n>2k>0
and
{\mathcal{F}}\subset{[n]\choose k}
be an intersecting family. Then, if
\gamma({\mathcal{F}})\geq{n-u-1\choose n-k-1}
for some real
3\leq u\leq k
, then

(1)

|{\mathcal{F}}|\leq{n-1\choose k-1}+{n-u-1\choose n-k-1}-{n-u-1\choose k-1}.

The bound from Theorem
1
is sharp for integer
u
, as witnessed by the family
\mathcal{L}_{u,u}(n,k)
, defined after Theorem
3
.

In this paper, we conduct a more systematic study of diversity in the context of some classic extremal set theory problems. For an integer
t\geq 1
, we say that the families
{\mathcal{F}}_{1},\ldots,{\mathcal{F}}_{l}\subset 2^{[n]}
are
cross
t
-intersecting
if
|F_{1}\cap\ldots\cap F_{l}|\geq t
for any
F_{1}\in{\mathcal{F}}_{1},\ldots,F_{l}\in{\mathcal{F}}_{l}
. If
t=1
then we use the term
cross-intersecting
. The following theorem is a generalization of the Erdős–Ko–Rado theorem.

Theorem 2

(
[
2
]
)
.

Let
a,b
be positive integers and
n\geq a+b
. Suppose that
{\mathcal{A}}\subset{[n]\choose a}
and
{\mathcal{B}}\subset{[n]\choose b}
are cross-intersecting and
|{\mathcal{A}}|\geq{n-1\choose a-1}
. Then
|{\mathcal{B}}|\leq{n-1\choose b-1}
.

If one puts
a=b=k
and
{\mathcal{A}}={\mathcal{B}}
, then one retrieves the EKR theorem. Again, the bound in Theorem
2
is sharp: take
{\mathcal{A}}
and
{\mathcal{B}}
to be the families of all sets containing
1
of sizes
a
and
b
, respectively.

The key result of this paper is the following stability for Theorem
2
in terms of diversity.

Theorem 3
.

Let
a,b
be positive integers and
n\geq a+b
. Suppose that
{\mathcal{A}}\subset{[n]\choose a}
and
{\mathcal{B}}\subset{[n]\choose b}
are cross-intersecting.
Suppose that

\displaystyle|{\mathcal{A}}|\ \geq

\displaystyle\ {n-1\choose a-1}-{n-v-1\choose a-1}+{n-u-1\choose n-a-1}\ \ \ {\text{a}nd}

\displaystyle|{\mathcal{B}}|\ \geq

\displaystyle\ {n-1\choose b-1}-{n-u-1\choose b-1}+{n-v-1\choose n-b-1}

for some real
3\leq u\leq a
,
3\leq v\leq b
and, moreover, that at least one of the displayed inequalities is strict. Then

\gamma({\mathcal{A}})<{n-u-1\choose n-a-1}\ \ \ \text{and}\ \ \ \gamma({\mathcal{B}})<{n-v-1\choose n-b-1},

moreover, both families have the same (unique) element of the largest degree.

The theorem above is sharp for each integer
u\in[3,a],v\in[3,b]
for
\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{L}_{u,v}(n,a)
,
\mathcal{B}=\mathcal{L}_{v,u}(n,b)
, where

\mathcal{L}_{u,v}(n,k):=\Big\{L\in{[n]\choose k}:1\in L,L\cap[2,v+1]\neq\emptyset\Big\}\cup\Big\{L\in{[n]\choose k}:[2,u+1]\subset L\Big\}.

Putting
a=b,u=v,{\mathcal{A}}={\mathcal{B}}
, one retrieves Theorem
1
.
The proof of Theorem
3
is given in Section
4
. Note also that the statement does not hold for
u=v=3
with non-strict inequality on both sizes of
{\mathcal{A}}
and
{\mathcal{B}}
: the families
{\mathcal{A}}=\mathcal{L}_{2,2}(n,a)
,
{\mathcal{B}}=\mathcal{L}_{2,2}(n,b)
, satisfy the inequality on the size (note that
|\mathcal{L}_{2,2}(n,k)|=|\mathcal{L}_{3,3}(n,k)|
), but have much larger diversity.

Let us deduce the following numerical corollary of the theorem above. For simplicity, we only deal with
u=v=x
case and with
n\geq 2\max\{a,b\}
.

Corollary 4
.

Fix
a,b>0
and
n\geq 2\max\{a,b\}
. If
{\mathcal{A}}\subset{[n]\choose a}
and
{\mathcal{B}}\subset{[n]\choose b}
are cross-intersecting and, for some
n-\min\{a,b\}-1\leq x\leq n-4
,
|{\mathcal{A}}|>{n-1\choose a-1}-(1-\frac{a^{2}}{(n-a)^{2}}){x\choose a-1}
,
|{\mathcal{B}}|>{n-1\choose b-1}-(1-\frac{b^{2}}{(n-b)^{2}}){x\choose b-1}
, then
\gamma({\mathcal{A}})<{x\choose n-a-1}
and
\gamma({\mathcal{B}})<{x\choose n-b-1}
.

Proof.

For
n\geq 2k
and
n-k-1\leq x\leq n-4
, the polynomial
p(x):={x\choose n-k-1}/{x\choose k-1}
is increasing (and for smaller
x\geq k-1
, the ratio is
0
), and thus
p(x)\leq p(n-4)={n-4\choose k-3}/{n-4\choose k-1}=\frac{(k-1)(k-2)}{(n-k-1)(n-k-2)}\leq\frac{k^{2}}{(n-k)^{2}}.
Thus, because of our assumption on
|{\mathcal{A}}|,\ |{\mathcal{B}}|
, we have
|{\mathcal{A}}|\geq{n-1\choose a-1}-{x\choose a-1}+{x\choose n-a-1}
and similar inequality for
{\mathcal{B}}
. Theorem
3
then implies that the diversities satisfy the desired inequalities.
∎

Statements concerning sizes of cross-intersecting families are often directly related to the famous Kruskal–Katona theorem
[
31
,
28
]
, which is one of the cornerstones of extremal set theory. Let us give the following definition.

Definition 1
.

For a family
{\mathcal{F}}
, denote by
\partial^{l}{\mathcal{F}}
the collection of
l
-element sets that are contained in at least one set from
{\mathcal{F}}
. We call
\partial^{l}{\mathcal{F}}
the
l
-shadow
of
{\mathcal{F}}
. If
{\mathcal{F}}\subset{[n]\choose k}
then we denote by
\partial{\mathcal{F}}
the
immediate shadow

\partial^{k-1}{\mathcal{F}}
.

We state the Kruskal-Katona Theorem in the form proposed by Lovász.

Theorem 5
.

If
{\mathcal{F}}
is a family of
k
-element sets, such that for some real
x\geq k
we have
|{\mathcal{F}}|={x\choose k}
, then
|\partial{\mathcal{F}}|\geq{x\choose k-1}
.

The other main result of this paper is a strengthening of Theorem
3
in the Kruskal–Katona-type form. For a family
{\mathcal{F}}
and integer
n
, define the
Kruskal-Katona diversity

\gamma_{KK}({\mathcal{F}},n):=\min_{X,|X|=n}\big|{\mathcal{F}}\setminus{X\choose k}\big|
. In Section
5
, we will prove the following theorem.

Theorem 6
.

Let
n>k>0
and assume that
{\mathcal{F}}
is a family of
k
-element sets, such that
|{\mathcal{F}}|\geq{n\choose k}-{y\choose n-k}+{x\choose k-1}
and
\gamma_{KK}({\mathcal{F}},n)\geq{x\choose k-1}
for some real numbers
k-1\leq x\leq n-3
,
n-k\leq y\leq n-3
. Then
\partial({\mathcal{F}})\geq{n\choose k-1}-{y\choose n-k+1}+{x\choose k-2}
.

Again, the theorem is sharp for integer
y,x
, as is witnessed by the example

\mathcal{KK}_{x,y}:={[n]\choose k}\setminus\big\{S\subset[n]:[y+1,n]\subset S\big\}\cup\Big\{S\cup\{n+1\}:S\in{[x]\choose k-1}\Big\}.

Moreover, one sees that
|\mathcal{KK}_{3,3}|=|\mathcal{KK}_{2,2}|
, and
\mathcal{KK}_{2,2}
has the same “distance” from three different subsets of
[n+1]:

[n+1]\setminus\{i\}
, where
i\in[n-1,n+1]
. This means that the range of
x
,
y
in the theorem is in some sense best possible.
From this theorem, we can derive the following numerical corollary.

Corollary 7
.

If
|{\mathcal{F}}|={n\choose k}
and
\gamma_{KK}({\mathcal{F}},n)\geq t:={x\choose k-1}
for some
t\leq\{{n-3\choose k-3},{n-3\choose k-1}\},
then
|\partial{\mathcal{F}}|\geq{n\choose k-1}+\max\{\frac{1}{n-k+1},\frac{1}{k-1}\}{x\choose k-2}
.

Proof.

Find
y\leq n-3
such that
|{\mathcal{F}}|={n\choose k}-{y\choose n-k}+{x\choose k-1}
. It is easy to see that
n-k\leq y\leq n-2
and
k-1\leq x\leq n-2
. Then, by Theorem
6
,
|\partial{\mathcal{F}}|\geq{n\choose k-1}-{y\choose n-k+1}+{x\choose k-2}\geq\min\{\frac{1}{n-k+1},\frac{1}{k-1}\}{x\choose k-2}
since
{y\choose n-k+1}=\frac{y-n+k}{n-k+1}{y\choose n-k}=\frac{y-n+k}{n-k+1}{x\choose k-1}=\frac{(y-n+k)(x-(k-2))}{(n-k+1)(k-1)}{x\choose k-2}\leq\frac{(k-2)(n-k)}{(n-k+1)(k-1)}{x\choose k-2}
.
∎

For two sets
A,B
, let
A\oplus B
stand for the symmetric difference of
A
and
B
. While we were preparing this paper, Keevash and Long
[
30
]
published the following theorem.

Theorem 8

(
[
30
]
)
.

If
\delta_{0}\in(0,1)
and
{\mathcal{A}}\subset{[n]\choose k}
with
|{\mathcal{A}}|={x\choose k}
and
\partial A\leq(1+\frac{c}{x}){x\choose k-1}
, with
c:=10^{-9}\delta_{0}
, then
|{\mathcal{A}}\oplus{S\choose k}|\leq\delta_{0}{|S|-1\choose k-1}
for some
S\subset[n]
.

Theorems
6
and Theorem
8
deal with stability in similar scenarios, as one sees in Corollary
7
, however, none implies the other. Where applicable, Theorem
6
gives better (sharp) bounds, and its range is at least big for
k
comparable to
n
and especially for
k
that are close to
n
. However, for
n
large w.r.t.
k
, it works for the families that are closer to
{S\choose k}
for some set
S
than Theorem
8
.

One advantage of Theorem
6
is that it has a much less technical proof (especially in terms of computations) than that of Theorem
8
. We could have made our conclusions work in a wider range, but then it would have made the statements and the proofs uglier, which, in view of Theorem
8
, we decided to avoid. The two proofs share one important ingredient: to deal with families with very large diversity, we continuously transform the family into one that has smaller, but not too small, diversity, using combinatorial operations, in particular, the so-called
Daykin shifts
(cf. Section
3
). A similar approach appeared earlier in the paper by Zakharov and the second author
[
32
]
to obtain Theorem
1
, and our analysis resembles that of
[
32
]
. However, in
[
32
]
, (normal) shifts were used, together with some other exchange operation.
For other stability results on Kruskal–Katona theorem, cf.
[
29
,
37
]
.

Let
r\geq 2,t\geq 1
be integers. We say that a family
{\mathcal{F}}\subset 2^{[n]}
is
r
-wise
t
-intersecting
if
|F_{1}\cap\ldots F_{r}|\geq t
for any
F_{1},\ldots,F_{r}\in{\mathcal{F}}
. If
r=2
, then we sometimes omit “
2
-wise” for brevity. Similarly, we shorten “
r
-wise
1
-intersecting” to “
r
-wise intersecting”.

Theorem 9
.

Fix integers
r\geq 3
and
t\geq 1
. Then for
n>\max\{15,2(r+t)\}k
and an
r
-wise
t
-intersecting family
{\mathcal{F}}\subset{[n]\choose k}
we have

(2)

\gamma({\mathcal{F}})\leq{n-r-t\choose k-r-t+1}.

The theorem is tight, as witnessed by the example
\{F\in{[n]\choose k}:F\cap[r+t]\geq r+t-1\}
.
We give the proof of Theorem
9
in Section
6
. We note the case
r=2
seems to be the hardest. For
r=2,t=1
, this corresponds to the “diversity of intersecting families” question of Katona, discussed in the beginning of the introduction, and thus the same statement is true for
n>Ck
with some absolute constant
C
. For
r=2
and
t\geq 2
, it seems that it is possible to resolve by generalizing techniques of the second author from
[
33
]
. The case of shifted families is easy, see Corollary
14
.

For a family
{\mathcal{F}}\subset 2^{[n]}
, let the
matching number

\nu({\mathcal{F}})
stand for the largest
s
, such that there are
F_{1},\ldots,F_{s}\in{\mathcal{F}}
, satisfying
F_{i}\cap F_{j}=\emptyset
for
1\leq i<j\leq s
. In particular,
\nu({\mathcal{F}})=1
if and only if
{\mathcal{F}}
is intersecting. The
s
-diversity

\gamma_{s}({\mathcal{F}})
of
{\mathcal{F}}\subset 2^{[n]}
is defined as

\gamma_{s}({\mathcal{F}}):=\min\Big\{\big|\{F\in{\mathcal{F}}:F\cap R=\emptyset\big|\ :\ R\in{[n]\choose s}\Big\}.

This notion seems to be the correct generalization of the notion of diversity to families
{\mathcal{F}}
with
\nu({\mathcal{F}})=s
. For
s=1
, we get back the usual notion of diversity. In Section
7
, we determine the maximum value of
\gamma_{s}({\mathcal{F}})
for
{\mathcal{F}}\subset{[n]\choose k}
for all
n>n_{0}(s,k)
(and completely resolve the
k=2
case).

In the next section, we recall the definition of shifting and discuss some of its properties. Lemma
16
(“shifted families are positively correlated”), although simple, appears to be a new and useful tool in the study of shifted families. In Section
3
, we discuss the Kruskal–Katona theorem and some other, more advanced, tools.

Most of the results that we present are for uniform families. However, we prove some results in the non-uniform case as well. For diversity of
r
-wise
t
-intersecting families in
2^{[n]}
, see Corollary
15
(shifted case) and Section

(general case).

2.
Shifting and initial families

For a set
F\subset[n]
, a family
{\mathcal{F}}\subset 2^{[n]}
and
1\leq i<j\leq n
, one defines the
i\leftarrow j
shift
S_{ij}
by

\displaystyle S_{ij}(F):=

\displaystyle\begin{cases}(F-\{j\})\cup\{i\}&\mbox{if }F\cap\{i,j\}=j,\\
F&\mbox{otherwise};\end{cases}

\displaystyle S_{ij}({\mathcal{F}}):=

\displaystyle\ \big\{S_{ij}(F)\ :\ F\in{\mathcal{F}}\big\}\cup\big\{F\ :\ F,S_{ij}(F)\in{\mathcal{F}}\big\}.

It is easy to see that
|S_{ij}({\mathcal{F}})|=|{\mathcal{F}}|
. Since
|S_{ij}(F)|=|F|
, should
{\mathcal{F}}
be
k
-uniform, then so is
S_{ij}({\mathcal{F}})
.

Proposition 10

(
[
6
,
13
]
)
.

(i)

If
{\mathcal{F}}
is
r
-wise
t
-intersecting then so is
S_{ij}({\mathcal{F}})
;

(ii)

\nu\big(S_{ij}({\mathcal{F}})\big)\leq\nu({\mathcal{F}})
.

Given
{\mathcal{F}}\subset 2^{[n]}
and two disjoint sets
X,Y\subset[n]
, we define

{\mathcal{F}}(X\bar{Y}):=\big\{F\setminus X:F\in{\mathcal{F}},F\cap(X\cup Y)=X\big\}.

For
X=\{i\}
,
Y=\{j\}
we write
{\mathcal{F}}(i\bar{j})
instead of
{\mathcal{F}}\big(\{i\}\overline{\{j\}}\big)
. We define
{\mathcal{F}}(X)
,
{\mathcal{F}}(\bar{Y})
similarly.

The following proposition is obvious from the definitions of the
i\leftarrow j
shift and diversity.

Proposition 11
.

The following properties hold.

(i)

For
x\in[n]\setminus\{i,j\}
,
d_{S_{ij}({\mathcal{F}})}(x)=d_{{\mathcal{F}}}(x)
;

(ii)

d_{S_{ij}({\mathcal{F}})}(j)\leq d_{S_{ij}({\mathcal{F}})}(i)=d_{{\mathcal{F}}}(i)+\big|{\mathcal{F}}(\bar{i}j)\setminus{\mathcal{F}}(i\bar{j})\big|=d_{{\mathcal{F}}}(j)+\big|{\mathcal{F}}(i\bar{j})\setminus{\mathcal{F}}(\bar{i}j)\big|
;

(iii)

\gamma(S_{ij}({\mathcal{F}}))\geq\gamma({\mathcal{F}})-\min\big\{\big|{\mathcal{F}}(\bar{i}j)\setminus{\mathcal{F}}(i\bar{j})\big|,\big|{\mathcal{F}}(i\bar{j})\setminus{\mathcal{F}}(\bar{i}j)\big|\big\}\geq\gamma({\mathcal{F}})-\big|{\mathcal{F}}(i\bar{j})\oplus{\mathcal{F}}(\bar{i}j)\big|/2
.

If
\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}\}\subset[n]
and
x_{1}<\ldots<x_{k}
then we also denote it by
(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})
. One defines the
shifting partial order

\prec_{s}
by

(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})\prec_{s}(y_{1},\ldots,y_{k})\text{ iff }x_{i}\leq y_{i}\text{ for all }1\leq i\leq k.

This can be naturally extended to
F,G\in{[n]\choose k}
by arranging the elements of
F
and
G
in increasing order.

Definition 2
.

A family
{\mathcal{F}}\subset 2^{[n]}
is called
shifted
if
G\prec_{s}F\in{\mathcal{F}}
implies
G\in{\mathcal{F}}
.

Note that repeated applications of the
i\leftarrow j
shifts,
1\leq i<j\leq n
eventually produces a shifted family. The following statement should be obvious.

Proposition 12
.

Suppose that
{\mathcal{F}}\subset 2^{[n]}
is shifted. Then (i) and (ii) hold.

(i)

d_{{\mathcal{F}}}(1)\geq\ldots\geq d_{{\mathcal{F}}}(n)
,

(ii)

\gamma({\mathcal{F}})=|{\mathcal{F}}(\bar{1})|=|{\mathcal{F}}|-d_{{\mathcal{F}}}(1)
.

In many cases, the following easy result reduces the maximum diversity problem for shifted families to well-known extremal results.

Lemma 13

(
[
13
]
)
.

Suppose that
{\mathcal{F}}\subset 2^{[n]}
is
r
-wise
t
-intersecting and shifted. Then
{\mathcal{F}}(\bar{1})
is
r
-wise
(t+r-1)
-intersecting.

Proof.

Assume for contradiction that there exist
G_{1},\ldots,G_{r}\in{\mathcal{F}}(\bar{1})
satisfying
G_{1}\cap\ldots\cap G_{r}=\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{r+t-2}\}
. Set
F_{i}:=(G_{i}\setminus\{x_{i}\})\cup\{1\}
for
i=1,\ldots,r-1
and
F_{r}:=G_{r}
. Then
F_{i}\in{\mathcal{F}}
by shiftedness and

F_{1}\cap\ldots\cap F_{r}=\{x_{r},\ldots,x_{r+t-2}\},

contradicting the
r
-wise
t
-intersecting property.
∎

Corollary 14
.

Suppose that
n\geq 1+(k-t)(t+2)
and
{\mathcal{F}}\subset{[n]\choose k}
is
2
-wise
t
-intersecting and shifted. Then

(3)

\gamma({\mathcal{F}})\leq{n-t-2\choose k-t-1}.

Proof.

Since
\gamma({\mathcal{F}})=|{\mathcal{F}}(\bar{1})|
by Proposition
12
(ii), we need to show
|{\mathcal{F}}(\bar{1})|\leq{n-t-2\choose k-t-1}
. By Lemma
13
, the family
{\mathcal{F}}(\bar{1})\subset{[2,n]\choose k}
is
2
-wise
(t+1)
-intersecting. Now (
3
) follows from the Complete Erdős-Ko-Rado Theorem
[
11
,
38
]
.
∎

Corollary 15
.

Suppose that
r\geq 3
,
t\leq 2^{r}-2r
and
{\mathcal{F}}\subset 2^{[n]}
is
r
-wise
t
-intersecting and shifted. Then

(4)

\gamma({\mathcal{F}})\leq 2^{n-r-t}.

The family

{\mathcal{A}}:=\big\{A\subset[n]\ :\ |A\cap[r+t]|\geq r+t-1\big\}

shows that (
4
) is best possible for
n\geq r+t
.

Proof.

Note that
{\mathcal{F}}(\bar{1})
is
r
-wise
(r+t-1)
-intersecting and
r+t-1\leq 2^{r}-r-1
by our assumption. Thus (
4
) follows from the bound on the size of
r
-wise
(r+t-1)
-intersecting families, proved in
[
14
]
.
∎

Let us also remark that for
n<r+t
one has
\gamma({\mathcal{F}})=0
whenever
{\mathcal{F}}
is
r

Lemma 16

(“Shifted families are positively correlated”)
.

Let

be shifted. Then

(5)

|{\mathcal{F}}_{1}\cap{\mathcal{F}}_{2}|\geq|{\mathcal{F}}_{1}||{\mathcal{F}}_{2}|/{n\choose k}.

Proof.

Let us apply induction on
k
, and for fixed
k

n
. The case
n=k
is trivial, as well as the case
k=1
. Suppose that
n>k>1
and that (

n-1,k-1
and
n-1,k

Note that
{\mathcal{F}}_{i}(1)\subset{[2,n]\choose k-1}
and
{\mathcal{F}}_{i}(\bar{1})\subset{[2,n]\choose k}
,

. These families are shifted. Therefore, by induction we have

(6)

|{\mathcal{F}}_{1}(1)\cap{\mathcal{F}}_{2}(1)|\geq\frac{|{\mathcal{F}}_{1}(1)||{\mathcal{F}}_{2}(1)|}{{n-1\choose k-1}},\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ |{\mathcal{F}}_{1}(\bar{1})\cap{\mathcal{F}}_{2}(\bar{1})|\geq\frac{|{\mathcal{F}}_{1}(\bar{1})||{\mathcal{F}}_{2}(\bar{1})|}{{n-1\choose k}}.

Due to shiftedness,

and so

\frac{|{\mathcal{F}}_{i}(1)|}{{n-1\choose k-1}}\geq\frac{|{\mathcal{F}}_{i}(\bar{1})|}{{n-1\choose k}}.

We have

\displaystyle|{\mathcal{F}}_{1}\cap{\mathcal{F}}_{2}|=

\displaystyle|{\mathcal{F}}_{1}(1)\cap{\mathcal{F}}_{2}(1)|+|{\mathcal{F}}_{1}(\bar{1})\cap{\mathcal{F}}_{2}(\bar{1})|\overset{\eqref{eqshift1}}{\geq}\frac{|{\mathcal{F}}_{1}(1)||{\mathcal{F}}_{2}(1)|}{{n-1\choose k-1}}+\frac{|{\mathcal{F}}_{1}(\bar{1})||{\mathcal{F}}_{2}(\bar{1})|}{{n-1\choose k}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{|{\mathcal{F}}_{1}||{\mathcal{F}}_{2}|}{{n\choose k}}+\frac{\big({n-1\choose k}|{\mathcal{F}}_{1}(1)|-{n-1\choose k-1}|{\mathcal{F}}_{1}(\bar{1})|\big)\cdot\big({n-1\choose k}|{\mathcal{F}}_{2}(1)|-{n-1\choose k-1}|{\mathcal{F}}_{2}(\bar{1})|\big)}{{n\choose k}{n-1\choose k}{n-1\choose k-1}}

\displaystyle\overset{\eqref{eqshift3}}{\geq}

\displaystyle\frac{|{\mathcal{F}}_{1}||{\mathcal{F}}_{2}|}{{n\choose k}},

For

Let

For

Recall also the construction

Theorem 28
.

If

\gamma_{s}({\mathcal{F}})\leq\gamma_{s}({\mathcal{A}}_{2})=|{\mathcal{A}}_{2}\big(\bar{[s]}\big)|.

Note that

We also note that the motivation for this theorem is the famous Erdős Matching Conjecture. If one would have an analogous result in the range of the parameters where the EMC is still open, it would most likely lead to some major progress in the conjecture.

Proof.

For a family
{\mathcal{F}}
satisfying the requirements of the theorem,
let
\tilde{\mathcal{F}}\supseteq{\mathcal{F}}
be an

satisfying the following conditions:

(i)

For any
\tilde{F}\in\tilde{\mathcal{F}}
there exists

, such that
\tilde{F}\subseteq F
;

(ii)

\nu(\tilde{\mathcal{F}})=s
;

(iii)

\tilde{\mathcal{F}}
is
maximal
with respect to (i), (ii).

We prove (

) by induction on

. The case

is implied by the result of
[

]
on the diversity of intersecting

-uniform families.

Case 0.
\exists x\in[n]:\{x\}\in\tilde{\mathcal{F}}
.
Then
\nu(\tilde{\mathcal{F}}(\bar{x}))=s-1

\gamma_{s}({\mathcal{F}})\leq\gamma_{s-1}({\mathcal{F}}(\bar{x}))\leq\gamma_{s-1}({\mathcal{A}}_{2}(n-1,k,s-1))<\gamma_{s}({\mathcal{A}}_{2}(n,k,s)).

Case 1.
For every
\tilde{F}\in\tilde{\mathcal{F}}
we have
|\tilde{F}|\geq 2
.

Define the (edge set of the) graph
{\mathcal{G}}
by
{\mathcal{G}}:=\{\tilde{F}\in\tilde{\mathcal{F}}:|\tilde{F}|=2\}

(21)

|{\mathcal{F}}|\leq\big(|{\mathcal{G}}|+o(1)\big){n-2\choose k-2}.

by the argumentation from
[

]
.

6

6

Namely, the minimal sets with respect to containment cannot form any sunflowers of size more than

by a classic result of Erdős and Rado

.

Combined with the following lemma, this concludes the proof of the theorem (which we check below).

Lemma 29
.

Let
{\mathcal{G}}
be (the edge set of) a graph on

,

and with no isolated vertices. Then there exists an
s
-element set

|{\mathcal{G}}(\bar{R})|\leq{s+1\choose 2},

with equality if and only if

forms a complete graph on

vertices. Moreover, if

is not a subgraph of a complete graph on

vertices, then

(23)

|{\mathcal{G}}(\bar{R})|\leq{s\choose 2}+1

We remark that Lemma

completely solves the question of maximizing the
s
-diversity for graphs, and even provides us with a Hilton-Milner-type result.

Proof.

Let
(x_{i},y_{i})
,
i\in[s]
, be a maximal matching in
{\mathcal{G}}
. Let
Z:=[n]\setminus\cup_{i=1}^{s}\{x_{i},y_{i}\}
be the remaining vertices.
Note that
Z
is an independent set. Note also that
\nu({\mathcal{G}})=s
implies that there are no

and
i\in[s]
such that
(z,x_{i}),(z^{\prime},y_{i})\in{\mathcal{G}}
. That is, for
i\in[s]
, either:

(a)

there is a unique

with
(z_{i},x_{i}),(z_{i},y_{i})\in{\mathcal{G}}
and none of
(z,x_{i}),(z,y_{i})
are in
{\mathcal{G}}
for
z\in Z\setminus\{z_{i}\}
, or

(b)

we can suppose by symmetry that
(z,y_{i})\notin{\mathcal{G}}
holds for all
z\in Z
.

Let us delete the set
X:=\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{s}\}
. The only possible edges that remain in
{\mathcal{G}}(\bar{X})
are edges
(y_{i},y_{j})
, which account for at most
{t\choose 2}
edges, and for each
1\leq i\leq t
at most one edge of the form
(y_{i},z_{i})
(note that
z_{i}=z_{i^{\prime}}
is possible for
i\neq i^{\prime}
!). Thus, there are at most
{s\choose 2}+s={s+1\choose 2}
edges.

If
z_{i}
is the same for all
i
for which it is defined, then
{\mathcal{G}}
is a subgraph of a complete graph on
2s+1
vertices. If for some
i\neq j\in[s]

z_{i},z_{j}
are different then there is no edge between
y_{i}
and
y_{j}
. Indeed, otherwise we can replace
x_{i}y_{i}
and
x_{j}y_{j}
with
z_{i}x_{i},

y_{i}y_{j}
and
x_{j}z_{j}
, thus obtaining a matching of size
s+1
. Moreover, if not all
z_{i}
are the same, and
z_{i}
’s are defined for
t
indices
i\in[s]
, then there are at least
t-1
pairs
\{i,j\}
for which
z_{i}\neq z_{j}
. In this case, the total number of edges is at most
{s\choose 2}+t-(t-1)={s\choose 2}+1
. ∎

Lemma
29
and (

) imply that
\gamma_{s}({\mathcal{F}})\leq\big({s+1\choose 2}+o(1)\big){n-2\choose k-2}
, moreover,
{s+1\choose 2}
can be replaced by

provided

is not a complete graph on
2s+1
vertices. Since
|\mathcal{A}_{2}(\bar{[}s])|=(1+o(1)){s+1\choose 2}{n-2\choose k-2}
, we conclude that the family

from

must have size

, and thus
\mathcal{G}
must be a complete graph on

vertices, say

. This clearly forces all the sets from
{\mathcal{F}}
to intersect

in at least
2
elements. This concludes the proof of Theorem

(

If

This was proved in

For the corresponding values, we have

(24)

\gamma({\mathcal{F}})\leq 2^{n-r-1}\ \ \ \text{ for all $r$-wise intersecting families }\ \ {\mathcal{F}}\subset 2^{[n]},\text{ where }\ r\geq 5.

In

The construction showing that (

Next, let us consider the case of (

-intersecting families for

]
.

Theorem 31

(
[

]
)
.

If
{\mathcal{F}}\subset 2^{[n]}
is

|{\mathcal{F}}|\leq\begin{cases}\sum_{i=(n+t)/2}^{n}{n\choose i}&\mbox{if }n+t\text{ is even},\\
2\sum_{i=(n+t-1)/2}^{n-1}{n-1\choose i}&\mbox{if }n+t\text{ is odd}.\end{cases}

Both functions on the right hand side are asymptotically
\big(1-\Theta(n^{-0.5})\big)2^{n-1}
for fixed

. The theorem is sharp due to the following examples:

\displaystyle n+t\text{ is even: }

\displaystyle\ \big\{X\subset[n]:|X|\geq(n+t)/2\big\},

\displaystyle n+t\text{ is odd: }

\displaystyle\ \big\{X\subset[n]:|X\cap[2,n]|\geq(n+t-1)/2\big\}.

These two families have diversity

is

. Thus, we may apply Theorem

for any

-intersecting family
{\mathcal{F}}\subset 2^{[n]}
.
We formulate these findings in a proposition.

Proposition 32
.

For integer
t\geq 2
, the largest diversity
\gamma
of a
t
-intersecting family
{\mathcal{F}}\subset 2^{[n]}
satisfies

\gamma=\Big(1-\Theta\big(n^{-0.5}\big)\Big)2^{n-2}.

The case
k=2
,
t=1
is special. In
[
5
]
, the authors managed to prove

\gamma({\mathcal{F}})\leq\big(1-n^{-1}\big)2^{n-2}

{\mathcal{F}}\subset 2^{[n]}
and constructed an example with of an intersecting family

. The authors of
[
5
]
conjectured that the construction attaining the lower bound is in fact optimal. Unaware of
[
5
]
, Huang
[
23
]
asked, what is the largest diversity of an intersecting family in

. He conjectured that it is at most

. In what follows, we solve the problem asymptotically using the tools coming from the Analysis of Boolean Functions. Both conjectures have negative answer.

Proposition 33
.

The largest diversity
\gamma
of an intersecting family
{\mathcal{F}}\subset 2^{[n]}
satisfies

\gamma=\Big(1-\Theta\big(\frac{\log n}{n}\big)\Big)2^{n-2}.

Proof.

Consider the uniform measure
\mu
on all subsets of
2^{[n]}
. The
influence

I_{i}({\mathcal{F}})
of coordinate
i
in
{\mathcal{F}}
is

I_{i}({\mathcal{F}}):=2\mu\big(\{F\subset[n]:i\notin F,|\{F,F\cup\{i\}|\cap{\mathcal{F}}=1\}\big),

where here and below
\mu
stands for the uniform measure on
2^{[n]}
. The
total influence
is
I({\mathcal{F}}):=\sum_{i}I_{i}({\mathcal{F}})
. In case if
{\mathcal{F}}
is closed upwards, we have

(26)

I_{i}({\mathcal{F}})=2\mu\big(\{F\in{\mathcal{F}}:i\in F\}\big)-2\mu\big(\{F\in{\mathcal{F}}:i\notin F\}\big).

By the definition of diversity, it is easy to see that

(27)

\frac{1}{2}\max_{i\in[n]}I_{i}({\mathcal{F}})+2\gamma({\mathcal{F}})=\mu({\mathcal{F}}).

Since increasing the size of the family cannot reduce the diversity, we may restrict ourselves to the families having measure
1/2
. Therefore, to maximize diversity, one has to minimize maximal influence of one coordinate in a family. By the famous KKL theorem
[
25
]
, we have
\max_{i\in[n]}I_{i}({\mathcal{F}})=\Omega(\frac{\log n}{n})
, and thus
\gamma({\mathcal{F}})=\big(\frac{1}{4}-\Omega(\frac{\log n}{n})\big)2^{n}
.

For the lower bound, consider the following example. Arrange the elements of
[n]
on the circle, and for each set
S\subset 2^{[n]}
form a sequence
\mathbf{u}:=(u_{1},u_{2},\ldots)
, where
u_{i}
is the length of the
i
-th longest run of consecutive
1
’s; similarly,
\mathbf{z}:=(z_{1},z_{2},\ldots)
is the sequence, in which
z_{i}
is the
i
-th longest run of consecutive
0
’s. Form an intersecting family by including all sets, for which its sequence
\mathbf{u}
is lexicographically bigger than
\mathbf{z}
. If
n
is even, then there may be sets which have
\mathbf{u}=\mathbf{z}
, and we take a half of them, one out of each pair of two opposite sets, arbitrarily (note that such sets must have exactly
n/2
ones). This example was proposed by Gil Kalai
[
26
]
, where the properties of this family were briefly described. In
[
33
]
, the first author gave a detailed proof of the fact that each coordinate has influence
O(\log n/n)
in such a family for odd
n
. We note that the analysis for the even case can be done along the same lines, just taking into account that there are very few sets for which the sequences
\mathbf{u}
and
\mathbf{z}
coincide. Therefore, we conclude that the minimum of the largest individual influence in an intersecting monotone family is
\Theta(\log n/n)
, and, substituting into (
27
), we get that the diversity of this family is
\big(\frac{1}{4}-O(\frac{\log n}{n})\big)2^{n}
.
∎
