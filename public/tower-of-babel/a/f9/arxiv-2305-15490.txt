Symplectic model reduction of Hamiltonian systems using data-driven quadratic manifolds
Harsh Sharma, Hongliang Mu, Patrick Buchfink, Rudy Geelen, Silke Glas, Boris Kramer

Abstract
This work presents two novel approaches for the symplectic model reduction of high-dimensional Hamiltonian systems using data-driven quadratic manifolds. Classical symplectic model reduction approaches employ linear symplectic subspaces for representing the high-dimensional system states in a reduced-dimensional coordinate system. While these approximations respect the symplectic nature of Hamiltonian systems, linear basis approximations can suffer from slowly decaying Kolmogorov N N -width, especially in wave-type problems, which then requires a large basis size. We propose two different model reduction methods based on recently developed quadratic manifolds, each presenting its own advantages and limitations. The addition of quadratic terms to the state approximation, which sits at the heart of the proposed methodologies, enables us to better represent intrinsic low-dimensionality in the problem at hand. Both approaches are effective for issuing predictions in settings well outside the range of their training data while providing more accurate solutions than the linear symplectic reduced-order models.

Hamiltonian partial differential equations (PDEs) demonstrate complex dynamic behavior while possessing underlying mathematical structures in the form of symmetries, Casimirs, symplecticity, and energy conservation. The structure-preserving spatial discretization of Hamiltonian PDEs with finite element methods, spectral methods, or finite difference methods leads to finite-dimensional Hamiltonian systems with large state-space dimensions.

For a high-dimensional Hamiltonian system, it is desirable to construct a reduced-order model (ROM) that preserves the underlying geometric structure and in particular, the stability of the full-order model (FOM). To this end, the field of structure-preserving model reduction has developed efficient tools for reducing the computational complexity of high-dimensional FOMs while preserving the underlying geometric structure. Designing autoencoders requires careful considerations towards reproducible research including the choice of network size and depth, activation function, optimizer, and weight initialization. Combining quadratic manifolds with Galerkin projection leads to a highly scalable approach for deriving efficient, yet interpretable, ROMs. However, the application of these approaches to Hamiltonian systems, in their standard formulation, leads to a violation of the underlying geometric structure. This makes the associated ROMs prone to displaying nonphysical behavior (see Section

The main goal of this work is to build on the recent successes of data-driven quadratic manifolds to construct structure-preserving state approximations, characterized by quadratic nonlinearities, for symplectic model reduction. We focus particularly on high-dimensional dynamical-system models that arise from the semi-discretization of Hamiltonian PDEs. The key contributions of this paper are:

1.

We propose the quadratic manifold cotangent lift (QMCL) and the resulting SMG-QMCL-ROM in Section
3.3
that use a quadratic approximation to build a symplectic approximation mapping while using the SMG as projection. The resulting ROM is a Hamiltonian system of reduced dimension. Thus, the SMG-QMCL is strictly preserving the structure of Hamiltonian systems throughout the reduction.

2.

We propose the blockwise-quadratic (BQ) approximation mapping and the resulting Galerkin-BQ-ROM in Section
3.4
that increases the accuracy of linear-subspace Hamiltonian ROMs by introducing quadratic terms to the state approximation. This approach augments the linear-subspace Hamiltonian ROM with interpretable and physics-preserving higher-order terms to derive an approximately Hamiltonian ROM with improved computational efficiency compared to the SMG-QMCL-ROM.

3.

Numerical experiments on the challenging cases of a thin moving pulse and
a two-dimensional nonlinear wave equation

for the nonlinear example

Symplectic model reduction using linear subspaces

The main goal in symplectic model reduction of high-dimensional Hamiltonian systems is the preservation of symplectic structure during the projection step. The methodologies described in Section

To approximate the high-dimensional system state

2.3
Data-driven learning of quadratic state approximations

Many datasets in problems involving high-dimensional state spaces are amenable to dimension reduction using linear techniques such as the POD. However, driving the projection error to an acceptable value can require a large reduced dimension
r
, rendering the associated ROMs inefficient. We therefore follow
[
19
]
in introducing nonlinear state approximations with quadratic dependence on the reduced state vector as

\mathbf{y}(t;\boldsymbol{\mu})\approx\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\mathrm{Q}}(\widetilde{\mathbf{y}}(t;\boldsymbol{\mu})):=\mathbf{y}_{\text{ref}}+\mathbf{V}\widetilde{\mathbf{y}}(t;\boldsymbol{\mu})+\overline{\mathbf{V}}(\widetilde{\mathbf{y}}(t;\boldsymbol{\mu})\otimes\widetilde{\mathbf{y}}(t;\boldsymbol{\mu})),

(14)

where
\mathbf{V}\in\real^{2n\times 2r}
is a basis matrix that has
\mathbf{v}_{j}
as its
j
th column;
\overline{\mathbf{V}}\in\real^{2n\times r(2r+1)}
is a matrix whose columns are populated by the vectors
\overline{\mathbf{v}}_{j}
; and
\otimes
denotes the Kronecker product without the redundant terms
4
4

4

For a column vector
\mathbf{x}=[x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{m}]^{\top}\in\real^{m}

\mathbf{x}\otimes\mathbf{x}:=\begin{bmatrix}x_{1}^{2}&x_{1}x_{2}&\dots&x_{1}x_{m}&x_{2}^{2}&x_{2}x_{3}&\dots&x_{2}x_{m}&\dots&x_{m}^{2}\end{bmatrix}^{\top}\in\real^{m(m+1)/2}.
. Learning the quadratic state approximation (

\{\mathbf{y}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{n_{\mathrm{s}}}\subset\real^{2n}
amounts to a representation learning problem in which the matrices
\mathbf{V}

\overline{\mathbf{V}}

\argmin_{\mathbf{V},\overline{\mathbf{V}},\{\widetilde{\mathbf{y}}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{n_{\mathrm{s}}}}\left(J(\mathbf{V},\overline{\mathbf{V}},\{\widetilde{\mathbf{y}}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{n_{\mathrm{s}}})+\gamma\left\|\overline{\mathbf{V}}\right\|_{F}^{2}\right),

(15)

where

J(\mathbf{V},\overline{\mathbf{V}},\{\widetilde{\mathbf{y}}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{n_{\mathrm{s}}})=\sum_{j=1}^{n_{\mathrm{s}}}\left\|\mathbf{y}_{j}-\mathbf{V}\widetilde{\mathbf{y}}_{j}-\overline{\mathbf{V}}(\widetilde{\mathbf{y}}_{j}\otimes\widetilde{\mathbf{y}}_{j})\right\|_{2}^{2}.

(16)

\gamma\geq 0

We learn state approximations of the form (
14

\mathbf{V}

\overline{\mathbf{V}}

\mathbf{V}

\{\widetilde{\mathbf{y}}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{n_{\mathrm{s}}}

15

\argmin_{\overline{\mathbf{V}}}\left(J(\mathbf{V},\overline{\mathbf{V}},\{\widetilde{\mathbf{y}}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{n_{\mathrm{s}}})+\gamma\left\|\overline{\mathbf{V}}\right\|_{F}^{2}\right),

(17)

which is a linear least-squares problem with the explicit solution

\overline{\mathbf{V}}=(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{V}\mathbf{V}^{\top})\mathbf{Y}_{\text{$\boldsymbol{\mu}$}}\mathbf{W}^{\top}(\mathbf{W}\mathbf{W}^{\top}+\gamma\mathbf{I})^{-1}\in\mathbb{R}^{2n\times r(2r+1)},

\mathbf{W}:=\begin{pmatrix}|&&|\\
\widetilde{\mathbf{y}}_{1}\otimes\widetilde{\mathbf{y}}_{1}&\dots&\widetilde{\mathbf{y}}_{n_{\mathrm{s}}}\otimes\widetilde{\mathbf{y}}_{n_{\mathrm{s}}}\\
|&&|\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{r(2r+1)\times n_{\mathrm{s}}}.

(19)

\overline{\mathbf{V}}

\mathbf{V}
in

3
Symplectic model reduction of Hamiltonian
systems using quadratic manifolds

We now present two different approaches that use, at their core, data-driven quadratic manifolds for building projection-based ROMs of Hamiltonian systems. Section
3.1
motivates the need for preserving the symplectic structure while deriving ROMs when using quadratic manifold constructions. Section
3.2
formalizes the problem considered in this paper. In Section
3.3
, we employ quadratic manifolds to develop a novel nonlinear mapping that is guaranteed to be symplectic and then use this mapping to derive a Hamiltonian ROM of (
3
) via the SMG projection. In Section
3.4
, we use data-driven quadratic manifolds to derive approximately Hamiltonian ROMs that are computationally more efficient than the Hamiltonian ROMs derived in Section
3.3
. In this alternate approach, we augment the linear symplectic approximation mapping (based on the cotangent lift) with terms of the quadratic Kronecker
product to derive a quadratic approximation mapping and then combine it with a Galerkin projection to derive an approximately Hamiltonian reduced model of (
3
).

3.2
Problem formulation

We consider a canonical high-dimensional parametric Hamiltonian system

\dot{\mathbf{y}}(t;\boldsymbol{\mu})=\mathbf{J}_{2n}\nabla_{\mathbf{y}}H(\mathbf{y}(t;\boldsymbol{\mu});\boldsymbol{\mu}),\qquad\mathbf{y}(0;\boldsymbol{\mu})=\mathbf{y}_{0}(\boldsymbol{\mu}).

(22)

Specifically, we focus on parametric Hamiltonian systems with FOM Hamiltonians of the form

H(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{p};\boldsymbol{\mu})=\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{p}^{\top}\mathbf{H}_{p}(\boldsymbol{\mu})\mathbf{p}+\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{q}^{\top}\mathbf{H}_{q}(\boldsymbol{\mu})\mathbf{q},

(23)

where
\mathbf{H}_{q}(\boldsymbol{\mu}),\mathbf{H}_{p}(\boldsymbol{\mu})\in\real^{n\times n}
are obtained by structure-preserving spatial discretization of parametric Hamiltonian PDEs. The corresponding FOM equations are

\dot{\mathbf{y}}(t;\boldsymbol{\mu})=\begin{pmatrix}\dot{\mathbf{q}}(t;\boldsymbol{\mu})\\
\dot{\mathbf{p}}(t;\boldsymbol{\mu})\end{pmatrix}=\mathbf{J}_{2n}\nabla_{\mathbf{y}}H(\mathbf{y}(t;\boldsymbol{\mu});\boldsymbol{\mu})=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{0}&\mathbf{I}_{n}\\
-\mathbf{I}_{n}&\mathbf{0}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{H}_{q}(\boldsymbol{\mu})&\mathbf{0}\\
\mathbf{0}&\mathbf{H}_{p}(\boldsymbol{\mu})\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{q}(t;\boldsymbol{\mu})\\
\mathbf{p}(t;\boldsymbol{\mu})\end{pmatrix}.

(24)

The block-diagonal structure in the FOM equations is related to the separable nature of (
23
) where the FOM Hamiltonian is additively separable with respect to generalized position vector
\mathbf{q}
and generalized momentum vector
\mathbf{p}
. Separable Hamiltonian FOMs of the form (
24
) appear as models in many science and engineering applications such as the linear wave equation in physics, the linear elasticity equation in solid mechanics, and Maxwell’s equations in electromagnetics.

We consider an intrusive model reduction setting in this work where the FOM operators
\mathbf{H}_{q}(\boldsymbol{\mu})
and
\mathbf{H}_{p}(\boldsymbol{\mu})
are assumed
to be
known.
In the scope of this work, a projection-based model reduction technique consists of (a) an approximation mapping
\mathbf{\Gamma}:\real^{2r}\mapsto\real^{2n}
and (b) a projection of the residual

\mathbf{r}(t;\boldsymbol{\mu}):=\frac{\text{d}}{\text{d}t}\left(\mathbf{\Gamma}(\widetilde{\mathbf{y}}(t;\boldsymbol{\mu}))\right)-\mathbf{J}_{2n}\nabla_{\mathbf{y}}H(\mathbf{\Gamma}(\widetilde{\mathbf{y}}(t;\boldsymbol{\mu}));\boldsymbol{\mu})\in\real^{2n},

(25)

where
\widetilde{\mathbf{y}}(t;\boldsymbol{\mu})\in\real^{2r}
denotes the reduced state vector.

In this paper, we propose two novel approaches that employ data-driven quadratic manifolds, as introduced in Section
2.3
, to derive accurate and stable ROMs for Hamiltonian systems. The two distinct model reduction techniques proposed in Sections
3.3
and
3.4
are named by the approximation map and the projection that are used to define it, SMG-QMCL (with the SMG projection and the QMCL approximation map) and Galerkin-BQ (with a Galerkin projection and the BQ approximation map).
The reduced-order matrix operators in both the SMG-QMCL-ROM and the Galerkin-BQ-ROM may be precomputed in the offline part of the approach.

Since the parameter vectors
\boldsymbol{\mu}\in\mathcal{P}
are fixed (but arbitrary) for each FOM or ROM, we simplify the notation by omitting the explicit dependence on the parameter: the FOM state vector
\mathbf{y}(t;\boldsymbol{\mu})
at time
t
and parameter
\boldsymbol{\mu}
is therefore often denoted as
\mathbf{y}(t)
. Analogously, the generalized position and momentum vectors
\mathbf{q}(t;\boldsymbol{\mu}),\mathbf{p}(t;\boldsymbol{\mu})
are denoted as
\mathbf{q}(t)
and
\mathbf{p}(t)
, respectively.

Remark 1
.

For noncanonical Hamiltonian systems with a constant and non-degenrate skew-symmetric matrix
\mathbf{S}
in (
2
), there exists a congruent transformation which can transform the noncanonical Hamiltonian system to the canonical form, see
[
50
,
25
]
for more details about the model reduction of noncanonical Hamiltonian systems. Thus, the proposed approaches can be applied to noncanonical systems with constant and non-dengerate
\mathbf{S}
after transforming the noncanonical FOM to a canonical form.

3.3
Quadratic manifold cotangent lift (QMCL) state approximation and the SMG-QMCL-ROM

We introduce a model reduction technique that strictly preserves the structure of the Hamiltonian system.
To this end, an approximation mapping is constructed—based on data-driven quadratic manifolds—which is guaranteed to be symplectic. We begin by considering a quadratic approximation mapping for the generalized position vector
\widetilde{\mathbf{q}}\in\real^{r}
in the form of

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\mathrm{QMCL},q}(\widetilde{\mathbf{q}}):=\mathbf{q}_{\mathrm{ref}}+\mathbf{V}_{\mathbf{q}}\widetilde{\mathbf{q}}+\overline{\mathbf{V}}_{\mathbf{q}}(\widetilde{\mathbf{q}}\otimes\widetilde{\mathbf{q}}),

(26)

with a constant vector
\mathbf{q}_{\mathrm{ref}}\in\real^{n}
and matrices
\mathbf{V}_{\mathbf{q}}\in\real^{n\times r}
,
\overline{\mathbf{V}}_{\mathbf{q}}\in\real^{n\times r(r+1)/2}
with

\displaystyle\mathbf{V}_{\mathbf{q}}^{\top}\mathbf{V}_{\mathbf{q}}=

\displaystyle\mathbf{I}_{r}

and

\displaystyle\mathbf{V}_{\mathbf{q}}^{\top}\overline{\mathbf{V}}_{\mathbf{q}}=\mathbf{0}\in\real^{r\times r(r+1)/2}.

(27)

Based thereon, we introduce the
quadratic manifold cotangent lift
(QMCL) as the approximation mapping of the state
\widetilde{\mathbf{y}}=(\widetilde{\mathbf{q}}^{\top},\widetilde{\mathbf{p}}^{\top})^{\top}\in\real^{2r}
with

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\mathrm{QMCL}}(\widetilde{\mathbf{q}},\widetilde{\mathbf{p}}):=\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\mathrm{QMCL},q}(\widetilde{\mathbf{q}})\\
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\mathrm{QMCL},p}(\widetilde{\mathbf{q}},\widetilde{\mathbf{p}})\end{pmatrix}:=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{q}_{\mathrm{ref}}+\mathbf{V}_{\mathbf{q}}\widetilde{\mathbf{q}}+\overline{\mathbf{V}}_{\mathbf{q}}(\widetilde{\mathbf{q}}\otimes\widetilde{\mathbf{q}})\\
\mathbf{p}_{\mathrm{ref}}+\Big(\mathbf{V}_{\mathbf{q}}+\overline{\mathbf{V}}_{\mathbf{q}}\mathbf{B}(\widetilde{\mathbf{q}})\Big)\widetilde{\mathbf{v}}(\widetilde{\mathbf{q}},\widetilde{\mathbf{p}})\end{pmatrix},

(28)

with
\widetilde{\mathbf{v}}(\widetilde{\mathbf{q}},\widetilde{\mathbf{p}}):=\left(\mathbf{I}_{r}+\left(\mathbf{B}(\widetilde{\mathbf{q}})\right)^{\top}\overline{\mathbf{V}}_{\mathbf{q}}^{\top}\overline{\mathbf{V}}_{\mathbf{q}}\mathbf{B}(\widetilde{\mathbf{q}})\right)^{-1}\widetilde{\mathbf{p}}\in\real^{r}
where
\mathbf{B}(\widetilde{\mathbf{q}}):=\left.\mathsf{D}(\cdot\otimes\cdot)\right|_{\widetilde{\mathbf{q}}}\in\real^{r(r+1)/2\times r}
denotes the derivative of the Kronecker product and a constant vector
\mathbf{p}_{\mathrm{ref}}\in\real^{n}
. We show later in this subsection (in a more general framework) that the approximation mapping
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\mathrm{QMCL}}
is (i) continuously differentiable and (ii) a symplectic map, i.e. the Jacobian
\left.\mathsf{D}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\mathrm{QMCL}}\right|_{\widetilde{\mathbf{y}}}\in\real^{2n\times 2r}
is a symplectic matrix

\displaystyle\left.\mathsf{D}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\mathrm{QMCL}}\right|_{\widetilde{\mathbf{y}}}^{\top}\mathbf{J}_{2n}\left.\mathsf{D}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\mathrm{QMCL}}\right|_{\widetilde{\mathbf{y}}}=\mathbf{J}_{2r}\qquad\text{for all }\widetilde{\mathbf{y}}\in\real^{2r}.

(29)

These two properties allow us to formulate the symplectic inverse (
7
) of the Jacobian
\left.\mathsf{D}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\mathrm{QMCL}}\right|_{\widetilde{\mathbf{y}}}
which we denote as
\left.\mathsf{D}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\mathrm{QMCL}}\right|_{\widetilde{\mathbf{y}}(t)}^{+}
. Thus, we can directly apply the techniques from
[
10
]
to use the SMG projection.

Definition 1

(SMG projection
[
10
]
)
.

The
SMG projection
requires that the symplectic projection of the residual (
25
) with the Jacobian of the approximation mapping vanishes, that is

\left.\mathsf{D}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\mathrm{QMCL}}\right|_{\widetilde{\mathbf{y}}(t)}^{+}\mathbf{r}(t)=\mathbf{0}\in\real^{2r}\quad{\rm{for\ all\ }}t.

(30)

Proposition 1
.

The ROM obtained via the SMG projection (
30
) of a canonical Hamiltonian system with
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\mathrm{QMCL}}
(
28
) is again a canonical Hamiltonian system.

Proof
:
We apply the proof from
[
10
, Section 2.3]
to the QMCL approximation mapping proposed in the present work: Substituting the residual (
25
) into the definition of the SMG projection (
30
) leads to

\displaystyle\dot{\widetilde{\mathbf{y}}}(t)=

\displaystyle\left.\mathsf{D}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\mathrm{QMCL}}\right|_{\widetilde{\mathbf{y}}(t)}^{+}\mathbf{J}_{2n}\nabla_{\mathbf{y}}H(\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\mathrm{QMCL}}(\widetilde{\mathbf{y}}(t)))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbf{J}_{2r}\left.\mathsf{D}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\mathrm{QMCL}}\right|_{\widetilde{\mathbf{y}}(t)}^{\top}\nabla_{\mathbf{y}}H(\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\mathrm{QMCL}}(\widetilde{\mathbf{y}}(t)))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbf{J}_{2r}\nabla_{\widetilde{\mathbf{y}}}H(\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\mathrm{QMCL}}(\widetilde{\mathbf{y}}(t))),

where we use that for all
\widetilde{\mathbf{y}}\in\real^{2r}
it holds that

\left.\mathsf{D}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\mathrm{QMCL}}\right|_{\widetilde{\mathbf{y}}}^{+}\left.\mathsf{D}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\mathrm{QMCL}}\right|_{\widetilde{\mathbf{y}}}=\mathbf{I}_{2r}
on the left-hand side,

\left.\mathsf{D}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\mathrm{QMCL}}\right|_{\widetilde{\mathbf{y}}}^{+}\mathbf{J}_{2n}=\mathbf{J}_{2r}\left.\mathsf{D}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\mathrm{QMCL}}\right|_{\widetilde{\mathbf{y}}}^{\top}

in the second step, and the chain rule for the gradient of the Hamiltonian

\nabla_{\widetilde{\mathbf{y}}}H(\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\mathrm{QMCL}}(\widetilde{\mathbf{y}}))=\left.\mathsf{D}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\mathrm{QMCL}}\right|_{\widetilde{\mathbf{y}}}^{\top}\nabla_{\mathbf{y}}H(\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\mathrm{QMCL}}(\widetilde{\mathbf{y}}))
in the last step.
With the definition of the reduced Hamiltonian
\widetilde{H}_{{\rm d}}:=H\circ\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\mathrm{QMCL}}\in\mathcal{C}^{1}(\real^{2r})
, we see that this is indeed a canonical Hamiltonian system

\displaystyle\dot{\widetilde{\mathbf{y}}}(t)=\mathbf{J}_{2r}\nabla_{\widetilde{\mathbf{y}}}\widetilde{H}_{{\rm d}}(\widetilde{\mathbf{y}}(t))\in\real^{2r},

(31)

of reduced dimension
2r
.

(
[

.

It remains to show that the proposed nonlinear approximation map
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\mathrm{QMCL}}
is (i) continuously differentiable and (ii) a symplectic map. The proof is based on a more general approximation mapping, which we refer to as the manifold cotangent lift. We begin by introducing immersions and the Moore–Penrose inverse:

Definition 2

(Immersion)
.

An
immersion

\mathbf{f}\in\mathcal{C}^{1}(\real^{k},\real^{m})
is a mapping for which the Jacobian
\left.\mathsf{D}\mathbf{f}\right|_{\mathbf{x}}\in\real^{m\times k}
is of rank
k
at every point
\mathbf{x}\in\real^{m}
.

Lemma 1

(Moore–Penrose inverse and immersions)
.

For an immersion
\mathbf{f}\in\mathcal{C}^{1}(\real^{k},\real^{m})
with
k\leq m
,
the
Moore–Penrose inverse
of the Jacobian

\left.\mathsf{D}\mathbf{f}\right|_{\mathbf{x}}^{\dagger}:=\left(\left.\mathsf{D}\mathbf{f}\right|_{\mathbf{x}}^{\top}\left.\mathsf{D}\mathbf{f}\right|_{\mathbf{x}}\right)^{-1}\left.\mathsf{D}\mathbf{f}\right|_{\mathbf{x}}^{\top}

(36)

is well-defined for all
\mathbf{x}\in\real^{k}
.
If
\mathbf{f}\in\mathcal{C}^{2}(\real^{k},\real^{m})
,
we have that
\left.\mathsf{D}\mathbf{f}\right|_{(\cdot)}^{\dagger}\in\mathcal{C}^{1}(\real^{k},\real^{m\times k})
.

Proof
:
Since
\mathbf{f}\in\mathcal{C}^{1}(\real^{k},\real^{m})
is an immersion with
k\leq m
,
the Jacobian
\left.\mathsf{D}\mathbf{f}\right|_{\mathbf{x}}\in\real^{m\times k}
is of full column rank
k
for all
\mathbf{x}\in\real^{k}
.
Thus, the product
\left(\left.\mathsf{D}\mathbf{f}\right|_{\mathbf{x}}\right)^{\top}\left.\mathsf{D}\mathbf{f}\right|_{\mathbf{x}}
is of full rank and thus (
36
) is well-defined.
For an immersion
\mathbf{f}\in\mathcal{C}^{2}(\real^{k},\real^{m})
,
we know that
\left.\mathsf{D}\mathbf{f}\right|_{(\cdot)}\in\mathcal{C}^{1}(\real^{k},\real^{m\times k})
with constant rank
k
which is enough to show
\left.\mathsf{D}\mathbf{f}\right|_{(\cdot)}^{\dagger}\in\mathcal{C}^{1}(\real^{k},\real^{m\times k})
(see
[
20
, Theorem 4.3]
).

\Box

We propose a nonlinear generalization of the
linear symplectic lift employed in
the cotangent lift
algorithm
, which we refer to as the
manifold cotangent lift
.

Definition 3

(Manifold cotangent lift)
.

For a given immersion
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\mathrm{MCL},q}\in\mathcal{C}^{2}(\real^{r},\real^{n})
,
we define the manifold cotangent lift (MCL) embedding as

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\mathrm{MCL}}(\widetilde{\mathbf{q}},\widetilde{\mathbf{p}}):=

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\mathrm{MCL},q}(\widetilde{\mathbf{q}})\\
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\mathrm{MCL},p}(\widetilde{\mathbf{q}},\widetilde{\mathbf{p}})\end{pmatrix}

with

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\mathrm{MCL},p}(\widetilde{\mathbf{q}},\widetilde{\mathbf{p}}):=\mathbf{p}_{\mathrm{ref}}+\left(\left.\mathsf{D}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\mathrm{MCL},q}\right|_{\widetilde{\mathbf{q}}}^{\dagger}\right)^{\top}\widetilde{\mathbf{p}},

(37)

for some
\mathbf{p}_{\mathrm{ref}}\in\real^{r}
.

We show that an MCL embedding is (i) continuously differentiable and (ii) a symplectic map.

Theorem 1
.

The MCL embedding
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\mathrm{MCL}}
from (
37
) is continuously differentiable.

Proof
:
We argue about the differentiability of the
two components of
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\mathrm{MCL}}
(
37
) in three steps.
First, we know that the first component
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\mathrm{MCL},q}\in\mathcal{C}^{2}(\real^{r},\real^{n})
is continuously differentiable by assumption.
Second,
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\mathrm{MCL},p}
is smooth in
\widetilde{\mathbf{p}}
since the function is linear in
\widetilde{\mathbf{p}}
.
Finally, since
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\mathrm{MCL},q}\in\mathcal{C}^{2}(\real^{r},\real^{n})
is an immersion,
Lemma

1
applies and we know that the Moore–Penrose pseudo inverse is continuously differentiable.
Then, we know that
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\mathrm{MCL},p}
is continuously differentiable in
\widetilde{\mathbf{q}}
by composition of continuously differentiable functions.

\Box

Theorem 2
.

The MCL embedding
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\mathrm{MCL}}
from (
37
) is a symplectic map (
29
).

Proof
:
For all
\widetilde{\mathbf{y}}=(\widetilde{\mathbf{q}}^{\top},\widetilde{\mathbf{p}}^{\top})^{\top}\in\real^{2r}
, the derivative of the nonlinear mapping
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\mathrm{MCL}}
can be written as

\displaystyle\left.\mathsf{D}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\mathrm{MCL}}\right|_{\widetilde{\mathbf{y}}}=\begin{pmatrix}\left.\mathsf{D}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\mathrm{MCL},q}\right|_{\widetilde{\mathbf{q}}}&\mathbf{0}_{n\times r}\\
\left.\mathsf{D}_{\widetilde{\mathbf{q}}}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\mathrm{MCL},p}\right|_{\widetilde{\mathbf{y}}}&\left(\left.\mathsf{D}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\mathrm{MCL},q}\right|_{\widetilde{\mathbf{q}}}^{\dagger}\right)^{\top}\end{pmatrix}\in\real^{2n\times 2r},

where
\mathbf{0}_{n\times r}\in\real^{n\times r}
is the matrix of all zeros
and
\mathsf{D}_{\widetilde{\mathbf{q}}}(\cdot)
denotes the partial derivative w.r.t.
\widetilde{\mathbf{q}}
.
Using the above expression for
\left.\mathsf{D}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\mathrm{MCL}}\right|_{\widetilde{\mathbf{y}}}
, we can write the left-hand side of (
29
) as

\displaystyle\left.\mathsf{D}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\mathrm{MCL}}\right|_{\widetilde{\mathbf{y}}}^{\top}\mathbf{J}_{2n}\left.\mathsf{D}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\mathrm{MCL}}\right|_{\widetilde{\mathbf{y}}}=\begin{pmatrix}2\skewPart\left(\left.\mathsf{D}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\mathrm{MCL},q}\right|_{\widetilde{\mathbf{q}}}^{\top}\left.\mathsf{D}_{\widetilde{\mathbf{q}}}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\mathrm{MCL},p}\right|_{\widetilde{\mathbf{y}}}\right)&\left.\mathsf{D}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\mathrm{MCL},q}\right|_{\widetilde{\mathbf{q}}}^{\top}\left(\left.\mathsf{D}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\mathrm{MCL},q}\right|_{\widetilde{\mathbf{q}}}^{\dagger}\right)^{\top}\\
-\left.\mathsf{D}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\mathrm{MCL},q}\right|_{\widetilde{\mathbf{q}}}^{\dagger}\left.\mathsf{D}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\mathrm{MCL},q}\right|_{\widetilde{\mathbf{q}}}&\mathbf{0}_{r\times r}\end{pmatrix},

(38)

where
\skewPart(\mathbf{A}):=\frac{1}{2}(\mathbf{A}-\mathbf{A}^{\top})
denotes the skew-symmetric part of
\mathbf{A}\in\real^{r\times r}
.
To prove the symplecticity, we need to show that (
38
) is equal to
\mathbf{J}_{2r}
. We observe that the off-diagonal terms already match due to

\mathbf{I}_{r}=\left.\mathsf{D}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\mathrm{MCL},q}\right|_{\widetilde{\mathbf{q}}}^{\dagger}\left.\mathsf{D}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\mathrm{MCL},q}\right|_{\widetilde{\mathbf{q}}}=\left(\left.\mathsf{D}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\mathrm{MCL},q}\right|_{\widetilde{\mathbf{q}}}^{\dagger}\left.\mathsf{D}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\mathrm{MCL},q}\right|_{\widetilde{\mathbf{q}}}\right)^{\top}=\left.\mathsf{D}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\mathrm{MCL},q}\right|_{\widetilde{\mathbf{q}}}^{\top}\left(\left.\mathsf{D}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\mathrm{MCL},q}\right|_{\widetilde{\mathbf{q}}}^{\dagger}\right)^{\top}.

Thus, it remains to show that the term with the skew-symmetric part in (
38
) equals zero for which we use that

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathbf{p}}=\mathbf{I}_{r}\widetilde{\mathbf{p}}=\left.\mathsf{D}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\mathrm{MCL},q}\right|_{\widetilde{\mathbf{q}}}^{\top}\left(\left.\mathsf{D}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\mathrm{MCL},q}\right|_{\widetilde{\mathbf{q}}}^{\dagger}\right)^{\top}\widetilde{\mathbf{p}}=\left.\mathsf{D}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\mathrm{MCL},q}\right|_{\widetilde{\mathbf{q}}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\mathrm{MCL},p}(\widetilde{\mathbf{q}},\widetilde{\mathbf{p}}).

(39)

For a better presentation, we reformulate this equation to index notation and use the notation

(\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\mathrm{MCL},q})_{i,j}=\left(\left.\mathsf{D}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\mathrm{MCL},q}\right|_{\widetilde{\mathbf{q}}}\right)_{ij}
for the Jacobian with respect to
\widetilde{\mathbf{q}}
.
Then, we can write (
39
) in index notation for
1\leq i\leq r
as

\displaystyle(\widetilde{\mathbf{p}})_{i}=\sum_{k=1}^{r}(\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\mathrm{MCL},q})_{k,i}\left(\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\mathrm{MCL},p}\right)_{k}.

Deriving this equation w.r.t.
\widetilde{\mathbf{q}}
yields by using the product rule for
1\leq i,j\leq r

\displaystyle 0=\sum_{k=1}^{r}(\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\mathrm{MCL},q})_{k,ij}\left(\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\mathrm{MCL},p}\right)_{k}+\underbrace{\sum_{k=1}^{r}(\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\mathrm{MCL},q})_{k,i}\left(\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\mathrm{MCL},p}\right)_{k,j}}_{=\left(\left(\left.\mathsf{D}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\mathrm{MCL},q}\right|_{\widetilde{\mathbf{q}}}\right)^{\top}\left.\mathsf{D}_{\widetilde{\mathbf{q}}}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\mathrm{MCL},p}\right|_{\widetilde{\mathbf{y}}}\right)_{ij}},

where we identify in the underbrace the term which we want to make an assertion about for (
38
).
We move this term to the left-hand side and obtain for
1\leq i,j\leq r

\displaystyle\left(\left.\mathsf{D}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\mathrm{MCL},q}\right|_{\widetilde{\mathbf{q}}}^{\top}\left.\mathsf{D}_{\widetilde{\mathbf{q}}}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\mathrm{MCL},p}\right|_{\widetilde{\mathbf{y}}}\right)_{ij}=-\sum_{k=1}^{r}(\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\mathrm{MCL},q})_{k,ij}\left(\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\mathrm{MCL},p}\right)_{k}.

(40)

Since
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\mathrm{MCL},q}\in\mathcal{C}^{2}(\real^{2r},\real^{2n})
, we know by the Lemma of Schwarz that the Hessian of each component
(\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\mathrm{MCL},q})_{i}
is symmetric, i.e. in the presented index notation
(\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\mathrm{MCL},q})_{k,ij}=(\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\mathrm{MCL},q})_{k,ji}
.
Thus we know that the terms in (
40
) are symmetric in
i
and
j
and the skew-symmetric part vanishes.
This concludes the proof that (
38
) equals
\mathbf{J}_{2r}
and thus
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\mathrm{MCL}}
is a symplectic map.

\Box

37
) via a specific choice for
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\mathrm{MCL},q}
and (b) that this specific
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\mathrm{MCL},q}
is indeed an immersion.

(

as special case of MCL)

3.4
Blockwise-quadratic (BQ) state approximation and the Galerkin-BQ-ROM

The SMG-QMCL approach in Section
3.3
employs a state-dependent projection (
28
) which leads to a nonlinear Hamiltonian ROM (
34
) with computational complexity
\mathcal{O}(r^{5})
in the online stage
for linear problems
. In this section, we propose an alternative approach to derive approximately Hamiltonian ROMs that are computationally more efficient in online computations. This approach also retains the physical interpretation of the state variables at the reduced level by choosing the following quadratic approximation for
\widetilde{\mathbf{y}}=(\widetilde{\mathbf{q}}^{\top},\widetilde{\mathbf{p}}^{\top})^{\top}\in\real^{2r}

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\mathrm{BQ}}(\widetilde{\mathbf{y}}):=

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\mathrm{BQ},q}(\widetilde{\mathbf{q}})\\
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\mathrm{BQ},p}(\widetilde{\mathbf{p}})\end{pmatrix}:=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{q}_{\text{ref}}+\mathbf{V}_{q}\widetilde{\mathbf{q}}+\overline{\mathbf{V}}_{q}(\widetilde{\mathbf{q}}\otimes\widetilde{\mathbf{q}})\\
\mathbf{p}_{\text{ref}}+\mathbf{V}_{p}\widetilde{\mathbf{p}}+\overline{\mathbf{V}}_{p}(\widetilde{\mathbf{p}}\otimes\widetilde{\mathbf{p}})\end{pmatrix},

(41)

which we refer to as
blockwise-quadratic
(BQ) approximation mapping.
We then choose
\mathbf{V}_{q}=\mathbf{V}_{p}=\mbox{\boldmath$\Phi$}
in accord with cotangent lift algorithm
1
. The matrices
\overline{\mathbf{V}}_{q}
and
\overline{\mathbf{V}}_{p}
are obtained from centered snapshots
\{\mathbf{y}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{n_{\mathrm{s}}}
through the numerical solution of the following pair of optimization problems:

\argmin_{\overline{\mathbf{V}}_{q}}\left(J_{q}(\mathbf{V}_{q},\overline{\mathbf{V}}_{q},\{\widetilde{\mathbf{q}}_{j}\}^{n_{s}}_{j=1})+\gamma_{q}\left\|\overline{\mathbf{V}}_{q}\right\|_{F}^{2}\right);\quad\argmin_{\overline{\mathbf{V}}_{p}}\left(J_{p}(\mathbf{V}_{p},\overline{\mathbf{V}}_{p},\{\widetilde{\mathbf{p}}_{j}\}^{n_{s}}_{j=1})+\gamma_{p}\left\|\overline{\mathbf{V}}_{p}\right\|_{F}^{2}\right),

(42)

where

\displaystyle J_{q}(\mathbf{V}_{q},\overline{\mathbf{V}}_{q},\{\widetilde{\mathbf{q}}_{j}\}^{n_{s}}_{j=1})

\displaystyle:=\sum_{j=1}^{n_{\mathrm{s}}}\left\|\mathbf{q}_{j}-\mbox{\boldmath$\Phi$}\widetilde{\mathbf{q}}_{j}-\overline{\mathbf{V}}_{q}(\widetilde{\mathbf{q}}_{j}\otimes\widetilde{\mathbf{q}}_{j})\right\|_{2}^{2},

(43)

\displaystyle J_{p}(\mathbf{V}_{p},\overline{\mathbf{V}}_{p},\{\widetilde{\mathbf{p}}_{j}\}^{n_{s}}_{j=1})

\displaystyle:=\sum_{j=1}^{n_{\mathrm{s}}}\left\|\mathbf{p}_{j}-\mbox{\boldmath$\Phi$}\widetilde{\mathbf{p}}_{j}-\overline{\mathbf{V}}_{p}(\widetilde{\mathbf{p}}_{j}\otimes\widetilde{\mathbf{p}}_{j})\right\|_{2}^{2},

with the reduced snapshots
\widetilde{\mathbf{q}}_{j}=\mbox{\boldmath$\Phi$}^{\top}\mathbf{q}_{j}
and
\widetilde{\mathbf{p}}_{j}=\mbox{\boldmath$\Phi$}^{\top}\mathbf{p}_{j}
from
\mathbf{y}_{j}=(\mathbf{q}_{j}^{\top},\mathbf{p}_{j}^{\top})^{\top}\in\real^{2n}
for
j=1,\dots,n_{\mathrm{s}}
and the scalar regularization parameters
\gamma_{q},\gamma_{p}\geq 0
.

Since the BQ state approximation is not a symplectic map, we can not use the SMG projection for deriving ROMs. Instead, we use a Galerkin projection
that requires the residual (
25
) to be orthogonal to the columns of
\mathbf{V}=\begin{pmatrix}\mbox{\boldmath$\Phi$}&\mathbf{0}\\
\mathbf{0}&\mbox{\boldmath$\Phi$}\end{pmatrix}
, that is
\mathbf{V}^{\top}\mathbf{r}(t;\boldsymbol{\mu})=\mathbf{0}
.
Fusing BQ approximation (
41
) with the Galerkin projection, we obtain the
Galerkin-BQ-ROM

\dot{\widetilde{\mathbf{y}}}(t)=\mathbf{V}^{\top}\mathbf{J}_{2n}\nabla_{\mathbf{y}}H(\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\mathrm{BQ}}(\widetilde{\mathbf{y}}(t)))=\mathbf{J}_{2r}\mathbf{V}^{\top}\nabla_{\mathbf{y}}H(\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\mathrm{BQ}}(\widetilde{\mathbf{y}}(t))).

(44)

For linear Hamiltonian systems of the form (
23
), the Galerkin-BQ-ROM simplifies to

\displaystyle\dot{\widetilde{\mathbf{q}}}(t)

\displaystyle=\phantom{-}\mbox{\boldmath$\Phi$}^{\top}\mathbf{H}_{p}\mathbf{p}_{\text{ref}}+\mbox{\boldmath$\Phi$}^{\top}\mathbf{H}_{p}\mbox{\boldmath$\Phi$}\widetilde{\mathbf{p}}(t)+\mbox{\boldmath$\Phi$}^{\top}\mathbf{H}_{p}\overline{\mathbf{V}}_{p}\left(\widetilde{\mathbf{p}}(t)\otimes\widetilde{\mathbf{p}}(t)\right),

(45)

\displaystyle\dot{\widetilde{\mathbf{p}}}(t)

\displaystyle=-\mbox{\boldmath$\Phi$}^{\top}\mathbf{H}_{q}\mathbf{q}_{\text{ref}}-\mbox{\boldmath$\Phi$}^{\top}\mathbf{H}_{q}\mbox{\boldmath$\Phi$}\widetilde{\mathbf{q}}(t)-\mbox{\boldmath$\Phi$}^{\top}\mathbf{H}_{q}\overline{\mathbf{V}}_{q}\left(\widetilde{\mathbf{q}}(t)\otimes\widetilde{\mathbf{q}}(t)\right),

where we used the orthogonality properties of the basis matrices
\mbox{\boldmath$\Phi$}^{\top}\overline{\mathbf{V}}_{q}=\mbox{\boldmath$\Phi$}^{\top}\overline{\mathbf{V}}_{p}=\mathbf{0}
. This system of equations can be rewritten as

\begin{pmatrix}\dot{\widetilde{\mathbf{q}}}(t)\\
\dot{\widetilde{\mathbf{p}}}(t)\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\phantom{-}\widetilde{\mathbf{H}}_{\mathrm{ref},p}\\
-\widetilde{\mathbf{H}}_{\mathrm{ref},q}\end{pmatrix}+\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{0}&\mathbf{I}_{r}\\
-\mathbf{I}_{r}&\mathbf{0}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\widetilde{\mathbf{H}}_{q}&\mathbf{0}\\
\mathbf{0}&\widetilde{\mathbf{H}}_{p}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\widetilde{\mathbf{q}}(t)\\
\widetilde{\mathbf{p}}(t)\end{pmatrix}\\
+\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{0}&\widetilde{\overline{\mathbf{H}}}_{p}\\
-\widetilde{\overline{\mathbf{H}}}_{q}&\mathbf{0}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\widetilde{\mathbf{q}}(t)\otimes\widetilde{\mathbf{q}}(t)\\
\widetilde{\mathbf{p}}(t)\otimes\widetilde{\mathbf{p}}(t)\end{pmatrix},

(46)

where the reduced operators are

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \widetilde{\mathbf{H}}_{\mathrm{ref},q}&:=\mbox{\boldmath$\Phi$}^{\top}\mathbf{H}_{q}\mathbf{q}_{\mathrm{ref}}\in\real^{r},&\widetilde{\mathbf{H}}_{q}&:=\mbox{\boldmath$\Phi$}^{\top}\mathbf{H}_{q}\mbox{\boldmath$\Phi$}\in\real^{r\times r},&\widetilde{\overline{\mathbf{H}}}_{q}&:=\mbox{\boldmath$\Phi$}^{\top}\mathbf{H}_{q}\overline{\mathbf{V}}_{q}\in\real^{r\times r(r+1)/2},\\
\widetilde{\mathbf{H}}_{\mathrm{ref},p}&:=\mbox{\boldmath$\Phi$}^{\top}\mathbf{H}_{p}\mathbf{p}_{\mathrm{ref}}\in\real^{r},&\widetilde{\mathbf{H}}_{p}&:=\mbox{\boldmath$\Phi$}^{\top}\mathbf{H}_{p}\mbox{\boldmath$\Phi$}\in\real^{r\times r},&\widetilde{\overline{\mathbf{H}}}_{p}&:=\mbox{\boldmath$\Phi$}^{\top}\mathbf{H}_{p}\overline{\mathbf{V}}_{p}\in\real^{r\times r(r+1)/2}.\end{aligned}

(47)

Approximation (
28
) of the SMG-QMCL-ROM transforms the high-dimensional problem (
24
) into a reduced nonlinear Hamiltonian system. In sharp contrast, the use of the Galerkin-BQ-ROM leads to a reduced linear Hamiltonian system with a quadratic perturbation term. Nonetheless, (
41
) exploits knowledge about the canonical structure of the FOM, leading to an approximately Hamiltonian ROM (
46
) that retains the intrinsic coupled structure of the FOM and has interpretable states. The numerical results in Section
4
demonstrate that this is indeed a very accurate strategy. The Galerkin-BQ-ROM is summarized in Algorithm
3
.

The Galerkin-BQ-ROM requires
\mathcal{O}(r^{3})
online multiplication operations to evaluate the right-hand side of the ROM and
\mathcal{O}(r^{4})
multiplication operations to evaluate the Jacobian of this right-hand side. The SMG-QMCL-ROM, on the other hand, requires
\mathcal{O}(r^{5})
multiplications for both of these tasks. In other words, by allowing an approximate Hamiltonian structure we achieve a computationally more efficient online phase compared to the SMG-QMCL-ROM from Section
3.3
. Importantly, we do not compromise the improved accuracy from data-driven quadratic manifolds.

4
Numerical results

In this section, the proposed model reduction methods are applied to
two parametrized wave equations, which are prototypical of transport problems
for which slowly-decaying Kolmogorov
N
-widths have been observed in similar settings in
[
24
]
. Section
4.1
provides details about the numerical implementations and the reported error measures.
In Section
4.2
, we demonstrate that the proposed approaches yield more accurate ROMs than the linear symplectic ROMs for a model that generalizes in the parameter and extrapolates in time. Finally, in Section
4.3
, we demonstrate the parameter extrapolation capabilities of the proposed approaches on a two-dimensional nonlinear wave equation.

4.1
Practical considerations & error measures

All numerical experiments in this paper are conducted using MATLAB version 2022a. For time integration we use the implicit midpoint rule for all FOM and ROM simulations. For time-continuous dynamical systems the corresponding time-marching equations are

\frac{\mathbf{y}_{k+1}-\mathbf{y}_{k}}{\Delta t}=\mathbf{f}\left(\frac{\mathbf{y}_{k}+\mathbf{y}_{k+1}}{2}\right),

where
\Delta t
is the fixed time step. The implicit midpoint rule is a second-order symplectic integrator that exhibits bounded energy error for nonlinear Hamiltonian systems
[
27
]
. The Galerkin-BQ-ROM (
46
) is solved at every time step using Newton’s method. The SMG-QMCL-ROM (
34
), on the other hand, uses a quasi-Newton scheme that neglects the second-order derivatives of
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\mathrm{QMCL}}
for an improvement of the online runtime, following the procedures from
[
36
]
.
The regularization parameters
\gamma_{q},\gamma_{p}
in (
42
) are chosen to ensure accurate and stable ROMs throughout the range of time integration. They are found by means of a two-dimensional grid search across a sufficiently wide range of parameter values.
For both the linear and the nonlinear wave equation example, we use the same
\gamma_{q}
value for the SMG-QMCL-ROMs and the Galerkin-BQ-ROMs which leads to the same projection error in
\mathbf{q}
for both approaches. Furthermore, all the numerical examples in this work use the same regularization parameter values for different basis sizes.

In the following, we introduce the error measures used in the remainder of the paper with a general approximation
\mathbf{\Gamma}
, which can either be
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\mathrm{LSL}}
(
5
),
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\mathrm{QMCL}}
(
28
), or
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\mathrm{BQ}}
(
41
). With
\mathbf{\Gamma}_{q}
, we denote the restriction of the mapping to the variable
\mathbf{q}
. The average
relative projection error
in
\mathbf{q}(t;\boldsymbol{\mu})
is computed as

{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\text{err}_{\text{proj,q}}=}}\frac{1}{M}\sum_{i=1}^{M}\frac{\lVert\mathbf{Q}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{i})-{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{\Gamma}_{q}}(\mathbf{V}^{\top}\mathbf{Q}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{i}))\rVert^{2}_{F}}{\lVert\mathbf{Q}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{i})\rVert^{2}_{F}},

(48)

where
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{\mu}_{M}\in\mathcal{P}
denotes a set of parameters. This error is valid for both the training and the test parameters.
An equivalent metric
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\text{err}_{\text{proj,p}}}}
is defined for
\mathbf{p}(t;\boldsymbol{\mu})
. The average
relative state error
is computed as

{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\text{err}_{\text{sim}}=}}\frac{1}{M}\sum_{j=1}^{M}\frac{\lVert\mathbf{Y}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{j})-{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{\Gamma}}(\widetilde{\mathbf{Y}}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{j}))\rVert^{2}_{F}}{\lVert\mathbf{Y}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{j})\rVert^{2}_{F}},

(49)

where
\widetilde{\mathbf{Y}}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{j})
is obtained from the ROM simulations and
\mathbf{\Gamma}(\widetilde{\mathbf{Y}}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{j}))
is the reconstruction of a trajectory at parameter value
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{j}
in the original state space.
We introduce two separate energy error measures for comparing the preservation of the Hamiltonian in the different ROMs. Since the FOM state trajectories for the linear wave equation preserve the energy exactly, the
error in the Hamiltonian
for the linear wave equation example in Section
4.2
is computed as

{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\Delta H_{\text{lin}}(t)=}}\left|H(\mathbf{\Gamma}(\widetilde{\mathbf{y}}(t;\boldsymbol{\mu}));\boldsymbol{\mu})-H(\mathbf{\Gamma}(\widetilde{\mathbf{y}}(0;\boldsymbol{\mu}));\boldsymbol{\mu})\right|,

(50)

where
H(\mathbf{\Gamma}(\widetilde{\mathbf{y}}(t;\boldsymbol{\mu}));\boldsymbol{\mu})
is the FOM energy approximation. For nonlinear Hamiltonian FOMs, the FOM state trajectories do not preserve the energy exactly because the the implicit midpoint scheme only preserves invariances up to the quadratic order. As a result, the
error in the Hamiltonian
for the nonlinear wave equation example in Section
4.3
is computed as

{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\Delta H_{\text{nonlin}}(t)=}}\left|H(\mathbf{\Gamma}(\widetilde{\mathbf{y}}(t;\boldsymbol{\mu}));\boldsymbol{\mu})-H(\mathbf{y}(t;\boldsymbol{\mu});\boldsymbol{\mu})\right|.

(51)

4.2
Parametrized linear wave equation

We revisit the linear wave example from Section
3.1
. This model problem is similar to the parametric linear wave example in
[
38
]
. Let
\Omega=\left(-0.5,0.5\right)\subset\real
be the spatial domain and consider the parametrized one-dimensional wave equation

\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial t^{2}}\varphi(x,t;\mu)=\mu^{2}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x^{2}}\varphi(x,t;\mu),

(52)

with the state
\varphi(x,t;\mu)
at the spatial coordinate
x\in\Omega
, time
t\in(0,T]
, and the scalar parameter
\mu\in\mathcal{P}=[5/12,5/7]
. Homogenous Dirichlet boundary conditions

\varphi(-0.5,t;\mu)=\varphi(0.5,t;\mu)=0,

(53)

are imposed for
t\in(0,T]
and
\mu\in\mathcal{P}
. We consider a parametric initial condition of the form
\varphi_{0}(x;\mu):=h(s(x;\mu))
based on the spline function

h(s(x;\mu)):=\begin{cases}1-\frac{3}{2}s(x;\mu)^{2}+\frac{3}{4}s(x;\mu)^{3}&0\leq s\leq 1,\\
\frac{1}{4}(2-s(x;\mu))^{3}&1\leq s\leq 2,\\
0&s>2\end{cases}

(54)

with
s(x;\mu):=\left(4\left|x+\frac{1}{2}-\frac{\mu}{2}\right|\right)/\mu
for which the exact solution is given by
\varphi(x,t;\mu)=\varphi_{0}(x-\mu t;\mu)
.

4.2.1
Hamiltonian PDE formulation and FOM implementation details

We rewrite (
52
) as an infinite-dimensional Hamiltonian system with
q(x,t;\mu):=\varphi(x,t;\mu)
and
p(x,t;\mu):=\partial\varphi(x,t;\mu)/\partial t
. The associated Hamiltonian functional is

\mathcal{H}(q(x,t;\mu),p(x,t;\mu);\mu)=\int_{\Omega}\left[\frac{1}{2}p(x,t;\mu)^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\mu^{2}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial x}q(x,t;\mu)\right)^{2}\right]\ \text{d}x,

(55)

and the original PDE can be recast as a Hamiltonian PDE

\frac{\partial}{\partial t}q(x,t;\mu)=\frac{\delta\mathcal{H}}{\delta p}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{(q,p;\mu)}}=p(x,t;\mu),\qquad\frac{\partial}{\partial t}p(x,t;\mu)=-\frac{\delta\mathcal{H}}{\delta q}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{(q,p;\mu)}}=\mu^{2}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x^{2}}q(x,t;\mu).

(56)

We discretize the spatial domain
\Omega
with
n=2048
equally spaced grid points leading to
a Hamiltonian FOM of dimension
2n=4096
.

Using a finite difference scheme, we obtain the following space-discretized Hamiltonian

H(\mathbf{q}(t;\mu),\mathbf{p}(t;\mu))=\Delta x\sum^{n}_{i=1}\left[\frac{p_{i}(t;\mu)^{2}}{2}+\frac{\mu^{2}(q_{i+1}(t;\mu)-q_{i}(t;\mu))^{2}}{4\Delta x^{2}}+\frac{\mu^{2}(q_{i}(t;\mu)-q_{i-1}(t;\mu))^{2}}{4\Delta x^{2}}\right],

(57)

with

\displaystyle q_{i}(t;\mu)

\displaystyle:=\varphi(x_{i},t;\mu),

\displaystyle\mathbf{q}(t;\mu)

\displaystyle=(q_{1}(t;\mu),\ldots,q_{n}(t;\mu))^{\top}\in\real^{n},

\displaystyle p_{i}(t;\mu)

\displaystyle:=\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\varphi(x_{i},t:\mu),

\displaystyle\mathbf{p}(t;\mu)

\displaystyle=(p_{1}(t;\mu),\ldots,p_{n}(t;\mu))^{\top}\in\real^{n}.

The corresponding parametrized Hamiltonian FOM equals

\dot{\mathbf{y}}(t;\mu)=\begin{pmatrix}\dot{\mathbf{q}}(t;\mu)\\
\dot{\mathbf{p}}(t;\mu)\end{pmatrix}=\mathbf{J}_{2n}\nabla_{\mathbf{y}}H(\mathbf{y}(t;\mu))=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{0}&\mathbf{I}_{n}\\
\mu^{2}\mathbf{D}_{\text{fd}}&\mathbf{0}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{q}(t;\mu)\\
\mathbf{p}(t;\mu)\end{pmatrix}.

(58)

4.3
Parametrized two-dimensional nonlinear wave equation

For a second numerical example, we consider the two-dimensional nonlinear wave equation with parametric nonlinearity. This parametric model problem is similar to the two-dimensional nonlinear wave equation example in
[
31
]
. Let
\Omega=(-10,10)\times(-10,10)\subset\real^{2}
be the spatial domain and consider the parametrized two-dimensional nonlinear wave equation

\frac{\partial^{2}\varphi}{\partial t^{2}}(x,y,t;\mu)=\frac{\partial^{2}\varphi}{\partial x^{2}}(x,y,t;\mu)+\frac{\partial^{2}\varphi}{\partial y^{2}}(x,y,t;\mu)-\mu\varphi(x,y,t;\mu)^{3},

(59)

with the state
\varphi(x,y,t;\mu)
at
(x,y)\in\Omega
, time
t\in(0,T]
, and the scalar parameter
\mu\in\mathcal{P}=[0.1,3]
. Periodic boundary conditions

\varphi(-10,y,t;\mu)=\varphi(10,y,t;\mu),\qquad\varphi(x,-10,t;\mu)=\varphi(x,10,t;\mu),

(60)

are imposed for
t\in(0,T]
and
\mu\in\mathcal{P}
.

4.3.1
Hamiltonian PDE formulation and FOM implementation details

We rewrite (
59
) as an infinite-dimensional canonical Hamiltonian system with
q(x,y,t;\mu):=\varphi(x,y,t;\mu)
and
p(x,y,t;\mu):=\partial\varphi(x,y,t;\mu)/\partial t
. The associated space-time continuous Hamiltonian functional is

\mathcal{H}(q(x,y,t;\mu),p(x,y,t;\mu);\mu)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}\left[p(x,y,t;\mu)^{2}+\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial x}q(x,y,t;\mu)\right)^{2}+\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial y}q(x,y,t;\mu)\right)^{2}\right]\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}y\\
+\int_{\Omega}\frac{\mu}{4}q(x,y,t;\mu)^{4}\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}y

(61)

and the original nonlinear PDE can be recast as a canonical Hamiltonian PDE

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}q(x,y,t;\mu)

\displaystyle=\frac{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\delta}\mathcal{H}}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\delta}p}(q,p;\mu)=p(x,y,t;\mu),

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}p(x,y,t;\mu)

\displaystyle=-\frac{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\delta}\mathcal{H}}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\delta}q}(q,p;\mu)=\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x^{2}}q(x,y,t;\mu)+\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial y^{2}}q(x,y,t;\mu)-\mu q(x,y,t;\mu)^{3}.

We discretize the two-dimensional spatial domain
\Omega
with
n_{x}=n_{y}=100
equally spaced grid points in both spatial directions leading to system states of dimension
2n
with
n=n_{x}n_{y}=10,000
. Using a finite difference scheme in both spatial directions, we obtain the following space-discretized Hamiltonian

H(\mathbf{q}(t;\mu),\mathbf{p}(t;\mu);\mu)=\Delta x\Delta y\sum_{i,j=1}^{n_{x},n_{y}}\left[p_{i,j}(t;\mu)^{2}+\frac{(q_{i+1,j}(t;\mu)-q_{i,j}(t;\mu))^{2}}{4\Delta x^{2}}+\frac{(q_{i,j}(t;\mu)-q_{i-1,j}(t;\mu))^{2}}{4\Delta x^{2}}\right]\\
+\Delta x\Delta y\sum_{i,j=1}^{n_{x},n_{y}}\left[\frac{(q_{i,j+1}(t;\mu)-q_{i,j}(t;\mu))^{2}}{4\Delta y^{2}}+\frac{(q_{i,j}(t;\mu)-q_{i,j-1}(t;\mu))^{2}}{4\Delta y^{2}}+\frac{\mu}{4}q_{i,j}(t;\mu)^{4}\right],

(62)

with

\displaystyle q_{i,j}(t;\mu)

\displaystyle:=\varphi(x_{i},y_{j},t;\mu),

\displaystyle\mathbf{q}(t;\mu)

\displaystyle=(q_{1,1}(t;\mu),\ldots,q_{n_{x},n_{y}}(t;\mu))^{\top}\in\real^{n},

\displaystyle p_{i,j}(t;\mu)

5
Conclusions & future work

We have presented two projection-based model reduction approaches that use data-driven quadratic manifolds to derive accurate structure-preserving reduced-order models of canonical Hamiltonian systems. The SMG-QMCL approach derives Hamiltonian ROMs using the quadratic manifold cotangent lift mapping which is based on a quadratic manifold approximation for the generalized position vector. More generally, we proposed the manifold cotangent lift approximation mapping which is a symplectic map and can thus be used with the SMG projection to build a symplectic SMG-MCL-ROM. Note, that this construction does not require the approximation to be a quadratic mapping, as in the QMCL, so the SMG-MCL-ROM could be used to formulate symplectic MOR with other nonlinear approximation mappings like e.g., higher-order polynomials or autoencoders in future work. The Galerkin-BQ approach, on the other hand, augments the linear symplectic subspace with quadratic Kronecker product terms to derive approximately Hamiltonian ROMs. Both approaches are offline–online separable
for linear Hamiltonian systems
which means that the ROMs of both approaches are independent of the FOM dimension. The Galerkin-BQ-ROM approach is particularly attractive from a computational efficiency standpoint due to the ROMs admitting a convenient linear-quadratic model structure with computational complexity on the order of
\mathcal{O}(r^{4})
compared to
\mathcal{O}(r^{5})
for the SMG-QMCL-ROM. On the other hand, the SMG-QMCL-ROM is favorable in terms of long-term energy conservation. Both of these novel approaches together constitute a first step towards the model reduction of dynamical systems on nonlinear manifolds using interpretable (e.g., polynomial) manifold constructions that ensure that the approximate solution satisfies key physical properties as dictated by the original high-dimensional problem.

The numerical experiments with the parametrized linear
and nonlinear
wave equations demonstrate the advantages of using data-driven quadratic manifold approximations for structure-preserving model reduction of transport-dominated problems. The numerical results also show that the proposed approaches produce stable ROMs with higher accuracy and better predictive capabilities than their linear counterparts. Notably, the proposed methods yield accurate predictions of the high-dimensional state even for parameter values that were not included in the training data.

This work has opened a number of avenues for future work.
Improving the computational efficiency of the proposed nonlinear ROMs based on data-driven quadratic manifolds is necessary. To reduce the online computational cost, the proposed approaches could be combined with the very recently developed structure-preserving DEIM
[
45
]
to improve the computational efficiency of the nonlinear SMG-QMCL and Galerkin-BQ ROMs.

To further reduce the computational cost
, alternative formulations for learning manifold approximations in high-dimensional state spaces could be exploited to formulate more suitable symplectic mappings.
In another direction, the proposed methodologies could be extended to noncanonical Hamiltonian systems with state-dependent and degenerate forms by building on the recent linear subspace approaches
[
28
]
.
Finally, we seek to broaden the application of the presented framework by learning Hamiltonian dynamics directly from time-domain simulation data in a non-intrusive fashion using operator inference methods
[
49
,
58
,
25
]
.
