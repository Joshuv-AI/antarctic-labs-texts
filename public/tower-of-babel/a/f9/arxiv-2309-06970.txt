A new path method for exponential ergodicity of Markov processes on ℤ d \mathbb{Z}^{d} , with applications to stochastic reaction networks
David F. Anderson, Daniele Cappelletti, Wai-Tong Louis Fan, Jinsu Kim

Abstract
This paper provides a new path method that can be used to determine when an ergodic continuous-time Markov chain on ℤ d \mathbb{Z}^{d} converges exponentially fast to its stationary distribution in L 2 L^{2} . Specifically, we provide general conditions that guarantee the positivity of the spectral gap. Importantly, our results do not require the assumption of time-reversibility of the Markov model. We then apply our new method to the well-studied class of stochastically modeled reaction networks. Notably, we show that each complex-balanced model that is also “open” has a positive spectral gap, and is therefore exponentially ergodic. We further illustrate how our results can be applied for models that are not necessarily complex-balanced. Moreover, we provide an example of a detailed-balanced (in the sense of reaction network theory), and hence complex-balanced, stochastic reaction network that is not exponentially ergodic. We believe this to be the first such example in the literature.



Keywords:
Path method, Exponential ergodicity, continuous-time Markov chains on
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, spectral gap, Poincaré inequality, stochastic reaction networks.

Introduction

Consider an ergodic continuous-time Markov chain
X=(X(t))_{t\geq 0}
with a discrete countable state space
\mathbb{S}\subset\mathbb{Z}^{d}
and a unique stationary distribution
\pi
. For such a process, a natural question is: what is the rate of convergence of the distribution of the process to
\pi
?

Let
\pi(f)=\sum_{x\in\mathbb{S}}f(x)\,\pi(x)
be the expectation of a function
f
on
\mathbb{S}
with respect to
\pi
, and let
\|f\|_{L^{2}(\pi)}=\big(\pi(f^{2})\big)^{1/2}
be the
L^{2}
-norm.
Denote by
P_{t}
the Markov semigroup for
X
.
That is, for
f\in L^{2}(\pi)
,
P_{t}f
is the function on
\mathbb{S}
whose
x
th element is
P_{t}f(x)=\mathbb{E}_{x}[f(X(t)]
.
Then we say that the distribution
converges exponentially fast in
L^{2}
if there is a constant
C>0
so that
for each compactly supported function
f
(or, equivalently, for each
f\in L^{2}(\pi)
) we have

\left\|P_{t}f-\pi(f)\right\|_{L^{2}(\pi)}\leq\|f-\pi(f)\|_{L^{2}(\pi)}e^{-Ct},\text{ for all }t\geq 0.

(1)

Of course, we can also have convergence in other norms. For example, we have exponential convergence in the total variation norm if there is an
\eta>0
such that for all
x\in\mathbb{S}
we have

\displaystyle\|P^{t}(x,\cdot)-\pi(\cdot)\|_{\text{TV}}\leq B(x)e^{-\eta t},\quad\text{for all }t\geq 0,

(2)

for some function
B(x):\mathbb{S}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}

[
25
]
, where
P^{t}(x,\cdot)
is the probability distribution of the process at time
t
with initial condition
x
and where the total variation distance between two probability measures on a measurable space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F})
is defined as
\|\mu-\nu\|_{\text{TV}}:=\sup_{A\in\mathcal{F}}|\mu(A)-\nu(A)|
. In our case of a discrete state space,
\|\mu-\nu\|_{\text{TV}}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{S}}|\mu(x)-\nu(x)|
, and so is an
L^{1}
norm
[
36
]
. Hence,
L^{2}
exponential convergence immediately implies exponential convergence in total variation via the Cauchy-Schwartz inequality.

A natural, and related, way to quantify the convergence of the distribution is via the mixing time of the process. Let
\varepsilon\in(0,\tfrac{1}{2})
. The
mixing time
with distance
\varepsilon
for the process with initial condition
x\in\mathbb{S}
is

\displaystyle\tau_{x}^{\varepsilon}:=\inf_{t\geq 0}\{\|P^{t}(x,\cdot)-\pi(\cdot)\|_{\text{TV}}\leq\varepsilon\}.

(3)

See
[
36
]
for a pedagogical introduction to mixing times. Hence, when (
2
) holds, an upper bound for the mixing time follows since
\tau_{x}^{\varepsilon}\leq\frac{1}{\eta}\left[\ln(B(x)+\ln(\varepsilon^{-1})\right]=O(\ln(B(x)))
.

For continuous-time Markov chains on a countable state space, common methods to demonstrate exponential ergodicity include (i) the use of Foster-Lyapunov functions
[
12
,
15
,
16
,
25
]
and (ii) analysis of the spectral gap of the model
[
16
,
36
,
37
,
45
]
. We take the second approach and in the first part of this paper we provide new sufficient conditions, in the form of a new path method, that imply that the spectral gap is positive (and, in particular, that the Poincaré inequality holds) which in turn implies that (
1
) holds. This, in turn, implies the mixing time is of order
-\log(\pi(x))
, when
X(0)=x
. These results are located in Theorem
2.5
and Corollaries
2.6
and
2.8
. Very loosely, our results apply to models whose stationary distributions have tails that decrease quickly (faster than exponential, such as the Poisson distribution, among others) and that allow for transitions of the form
\pm e_{i}
, where
\{e_{i}\}
is the canonical basis for
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
(there are more technical assumptions related to lower bounds for the rates of these transitions). Importantly, we
do not
assume that the models are time-reversible. That is, we need not have that the stationary distribution
\pi
satisfies
\pi(x)q(x,z)=\pi(z)q(z,x)
for all states
x,z
, with
q(\cdot,\cdot)
denoting the transition rates. In fact, we even allow our models to be irreversible in that a positive rate of transition from state
x
to state
z
need not imply there is a positive rate of transition from state
z
to state
x
.

To illustrate the new path method, we present three key examples. First, in Example
2.1
, a simple birth-death model is provided in order to motivate our new approach. In Example
2.3
, we present a Markov system where the well-known “canonical path method” falls short, while our method establishes exponential ergodicity. Finally, in Example
2.4
, we demonstrate the necessity of the assumption pertaining to transitions of the form
\pm e_{i}
for the class of models we are interested in (stochastic reaction networks).

In the second part of this paper, we apply our method to the class of models that motivated this work: stochastic reaction networks. Stochastic reaction networks are used ubiquitously in the biology literature to model biological interaction systems (which include intracellular processes, viral infections, signaling systems, etc.). Reaction networks are often depicted via a
reaction graph
, which is a graphical representation of the interactions between the constituent “species” of the model. See Figure
1
for a network describing the intracellular interactions of the EnvZ/OmpR signal transduction system in
E. coli

[

,
43
]
.

E. coli

The constituent “species” are written in black,
and the reactions are the transitions implied by the directed graph. For example, the reaction
X_{P}+Y\to X_{p}Y
uses one

molecule and one

Often, the goal of the mathematical theory for reaction networks is to relate topological conditions of the underlying reaction network (which are easy to check) to the qualitative behavior of the associated dynamical system (which is typically difficult to quantify). In previous research pertaining to the discrete stochastic models, classes of reaction networks characterized by graph topological conditions have been identified for which key long-term behaviors are guaranteed, independent of system parameters. These behaviors include non-explosion
[

Specifically, we utilize our methods via Theorem

and its corollaries to prove exponential ergodicity in
L^{2}
for a subclass
of stochastic reaction networks that are “open,” meaning that each species can enter and leave the system.
Notably, our results apply (but are not limited to)
complex-balanced
models. In particular, we are able to state the following result:

An open and complex-balanced stochastic reaction network is
L^{2}
-exponentially ergodic.

A precise statement is given in Corollary

, and a more general formulation is given in Corollary

.
The “complex-balanced” condition of reaction network theory is a balance condition similar to the “traffic conditions” found in probability theory
[

]
; the concept in the setting of reaction network theory was introduced in the seminal papers
[

,
32
,
33
]
and has been an object of study ever since (for example, see
[

,

,

).

Finally, in Example
2.4
we provide an example of a detailed-balanced, and hence complex-balanced, reaction network (which is time-reversible in the sense of Markov chains) that is not exponentially ergodic. We believe this to be the first such model analyzed in the literature.

Main analytical results

In Section
2.1
, we provide a detailed overview of how to apply the analytical methods relating the Poincaré inequality to
L^{2}
exponential convergence in the current context (continuous-time Markov chain on a discrete state space, with time-reversibility
not
assumed). Importantly, in our Assumption
2.1
we will carefully specify the regularity conditions needed on the Markov model that guarantee the objects under consideration are well-defined and allow the analytical methods to go through. While much of this material is, to a greater or lesser extent, known, see for example
[
17
,
22
,
37
,
41
,
45
]
, all the references we are aware of on the subject make assumptions that we do not, with the most notable being the common assumption that the Markov process is time-reversible. We therefore provide our own proofs for the preliminary results. We note that our proofs are somewhat similar to those found in the concise lecture notes by Ramon van Handel
[
45
]
.
However, in our opinion there are important technical differences, especially related to when certain limits and sums can be exchanged.
Still, in the interest of readability we have placed many of the proofs from this section in the appendices.

Next, in Section
2.2
, we provide the statement and proof of our first main result, which provides sufficient conditions for
L^{2}
exponential ergodicity of continuous-time Markov chains on
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
. In Section
2.3
, we provide corollaries that are easier to apply for continuous-time Markov chains on
\mathbb{Z}^{d}_{\geq 0}
. Finally, in Section
2.4
, we provide two important examples. In the first example, we demonstrates why new theory was needed by showing that the canonical path method stands silent on certain models of interest, while our new theory is strong enough to prove exponential ergodicity. In the second example, we demonstrate the necessity of one of our main structural/analytic assumptions found in the corollaries: that transitions of the form
\pm e_{i}
have rates that are lower bounded (together with other technical assumptions). Moreover, we believe that this is the first example in the literature of a complex-balanced (and detailed-balanced) stochastic reaction network that is shown to be not exponentially ergodic in
L^{2}
.

2.1
Models and methods

2.1.1
The basic model

We assume basic knowledge of the theory of continuous-time Markov chains, as can be found, for example, in
[
40
]
.
For some positive integer
d
, we let
X
be a continuous-time
Markov chain with a countable state space
\mathbb{S}\subset\mathbb{Z}^{d}
and stationary distribution
\pi
. For
x,z\in\mathbb{S}
with
x\neq z
, we denote by
q(x,z)
the transition rate from state
x
to
z
; that is,

\mathbb{P}(X(t+\Delta t)=z\ |\ X(t)=x)=q(x,z)\Delta t+o(\Delta t),\quad\text{as}\quad\Delta t\to 0.

(4)

We will denote
q_{x}=\sum_{z\in\mathbb{S}\setminus\{x\}}q(x,z)
and
q(x,x)=-q_{x}
.
We will not assume that
X
is time-reversible. That is, we allow the situation
\pi(x)q(x,z)\neq\pi(z)q(z,x)
for some
x,z\in\mathbb{S}
. In fact, we even allow for the state space to be irreversible, that is,
q(x,z)>0
and
q(z,x)=0
for some
x,z\in\mathbb{S}
.

We will assume throughout that our collection of rates,
\{q(x,z)\}
, satisfies the following regularity assumption.

Assumption 2.1
.

The collection of rates
\{q(x,z)\}
satisfies the following four conditions.

(i)

For
x,z\in\mathbb{S}
, with
x\neq z
, we have
0\leq q(x,z)<\infty
.

(ii)

For each
x\in\mathbb{S}
, we have
q_{x}=\sum_{z\in\mathbb{S}\setminus\{x\}}q(x,z)<\infty
.

(iii)

For each
z\in\mathbb{S}
, we have
\sup_{x\in\mathbb{S}\setminus\{z\}}q(x,z)<\infty
.

(iv)

X
is an irreducible and non-explosive continuous-time Markov chain on a countable state space
\mathbb{S}\subset\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, with unique stationary distribution
\pi
.

\triangle

The first condition is standard and states that all rates are non-negative and bounded. The second condition guarantees that the holding time in each state is positive with probability one (no instantaneous states). The third condition is more technical in nature, but is required, for example, in the proof of Lemma
2.1
. Finally, the last condition guarantees that the distribution of the process
X
is uniquely determined by the transition rates
\{q(x,z)\}
, and that the distribution of
X(t)
converges to
\pi
, as
t
goes to infinity. The most common way to ensure that condition (iv) holds is to assume that
\sum_{x}\pi(x)q_{x}<\infty

[
7
]
. Assumption
2.1
ensures that the Kolmogorov forward equations

\frac{dP_{x}(X(t)=y)}{dt}=\left(\sum_{z\in\mathbb{S}\setminus\{y\}}q(z,y)P_{x}(X(t)=z)\right)-q_{y}P_{x}(X(t)=y)

(5)

hold for all
x,y\in\mathbb{S}
and
t\in[0,\infty)
, where
P_{x}(\cdot):=P(\cdot|X(0)=x)
(see the appendix in
[
7
]
). As a consequence, we have that
\pi
satisfies

\pi(y)\sum_{z\in\mathbb{S}\setminus\{y\}}q(y,z)=\sum_{z\in\mathbb{S}\setminus\{y\}}\pi(z)q(z,y).

(6)

Denote by
\mathcal{A}
and
Dom(\mathcal{A})
the infinitesimal generator of
X
and its domain in
L^{2}(\pi)
, respectively (see
[
26
, (Chapter 1)]
for relevant definitions). Then for
f\in Dom(\mathcal{A})
,

\mathcal{A}f(x)=\sum_{z\in\mathbb{S}\setminus\{x\}}(f(z)-f(x))\,q(x,z).

(7)

Let
\mathcal{B}_{c}
be the collection of compactly supported functions on
\mathbb{S}
. The following technical lemma demonstrates the usefulness of Assumption
2.1
. For completeness, we placed the proof in Appendix
B
.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
X
be a continuous-time Markov chain on
\mathbb{S}\subset\mathbb{Z}^{d}
with transition rates
\{q(x,z)\}
that satisfy Assumption
2.1
.
Then,
\mathcal{B}_{c}\subset Dom(\mathcal{A})
and

\|\mathcal{A}f\|_{\infty}<\infty\qquad\text{for }f\in\mathcal{B}_{c}.

(8)

Remark 2.1
.

The proof of Lemma
2.1
still holds if we only assume points (i)-(iii) in Assumption
2.1
, provided that we consider
X
to be the
minimal
process, i.e. the process that is absorbed in a cemetery state
\infty
after a potential explosion. In this case,
[
7
, Theorem 4]
can still be applied and the result be derived.

2.1.2
Methods: Poincaré inequalities and exponential
L^{2}
convergence

We make precise some notation from the introduction and introduce some new ones. For the stationary distribution
\pi
let

\langle f,\,g\rangle_{L^{2}(\pi)}:=\sum_{x\in\mathbb{S}}f(x)g(x)\pi(x)

be the inner product of the
L^{2}
-space
L^{2}(\pi)
. We let
\pi(f)=\sum_{x\in\mathbb{S}}f(x)\,\pi(x)
be the expectation of
f
,

\|f\|_{L^{2}(\pi)}=\big(\pi(f^{2})\big)^{1/2}
the
L^{2}
-norm,
and

Var_{\pi}(f)=\pi(f^{2})-\left(\pi(f)\right)^{2}=\left\|f-\pi(f)\right\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\pi)}

the variance, all with respect to
\pi
.

Denote by
P_{t}
the Markov semigroup for the process
X
.
Recall that if
f\in Dom(\mathcal{A})
, then
P_{t}f\in Dom(\mathcal{A})
and, moreover,
\mathcal{A}P_{t}f=P_{t}\mathcal{A}f

[
26
, Proposition 1.5]
.

For
f\in L^{2}(\pi)
and
g\in Dom(\mathcal{A})
, we
define

\displaystyle{\mathcal{E}}(f,g):=

\displaystyle-\langle f,\,\mathcal{A}g\rangle_{L^{2}(\pi)}=-\sum_{x,z\in\mathbb{S}}f(x)\,\big(g(z)-g(x)\big)\,\pi(x)\,q(x,z).

(9)

The bilinear operator
{\mathcal{E}}
is referred to as the Dirichlet form of the process
X
(see
[
38
, Chapter 1]
). Of particular interest will be
{\mathcal{E}}(f,f)
for
f
from a rich enough class of functions.
A slightly different representation will be useful. So, for
f\in Dom(\mathcal{A})
, let

\displaystyle{\mathcal{E}}^{*}(f):=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\sum_{x,z\in\mathbb{S}}\big(f(x)-f(z)\big)^{2}\,\pi(x)\,q(x,z).

(10)

The proof of the following lemma is found in Appendix
C
.

Lemma 2.2
.

Suppose that
X
satisfies Assumption
2.1
.
For
f\in Dom(\mathcal{A})
with
\|f\|_{\infty}<\infty
, both the sum (
9
) for
{\mathcal{E}}(f,f)
and the sum (
10
) for
{\mathcal{E}}^{*}(f)
converge absolutely. Also,
{\mathcal{E}}(f,f)={\mathcal{E}}^{*}(f)
.

With Lemma
2.2
in hand, we define the
spectral gap
as

\displaystyle\mathrm{gap}({\mathcal{E}})

\displaystyle:=\inf\left\{\frac{{\mathcal{E}}(f,f)}{Var_{\pi}(f)}:\;f\in Dom(\mathcal{A}),\|f\|_{\infty}<\infty,Var_{\pi}(f)\neq 0\right\}.

(11)

Note that
\mathrm{gap}({\mathcal{E}})>0
if and only if there is a
C>0
for which

{\mathcal{E}}(f,f)\geq C\,Var_{\pi}(f)

(12)

for all bounded
f\in Dom(\mathcal{A})
. The inequality (
12
) is sometimes called the
Poincaré inequality
. In light of Lemma
2.2
, we have the following alternative representation for
\text{gap}({\mathcal{E}})
,

\displaystyle\text{gap}({\mathcal{E}})=\inf\left\{{\mathcal{E}}^{*}(f):f\in Dom(\mathcal{A}),\|f\|_{\infty}<\infty,\pi(f)=0,\pi(f^{2})=1\right\},

(13)

which will be useful to us throughout.

The following theorem is key to our analysis, and explains our interest in the spectral gap. In short, the result states that in our setting
\text{gap}({\mathcal{E}})>0
implies exponential
L^{2}
convergence of the distribution of
X(t)
to
\pi
, as
t
tends to infinity.
As mentioned in the preamble to this section, the result is considered to be well-known. However, we were not able to find a version exactly fitting our needs.
We also stress again that the unpublished lecture notes from Ramon van Handel has a proof that is very similar to ours (though there are important differences on the justification for passing certain limits through sums). In particular, see
[
45
, Theorem 2.18 and Remark 2.19]
.

Theorem 2.3
.

Suppose Assumption
2.1
is satisfied. If the process
X
is time-reversible, then the following statements are equivalent.

(i)

\mathrm{gap}({\mathcal{E}})\geq C
for some
C\in(0,\infty)
.

(ii)

For all
f\in\mathcal{B}_{c}
and
t\geq 0
,

\left\|P_{t}f-\pi(f)\right\|_{L^{2}(\pi)}\leq e^{-2Ct}\,\left\|f-\pi(f)\right\|_{L^{2}(\pi)}.

(14)

(iii)

For
f\in\mathcal{B}_{c}
there is a constant
C_{f}
so that for all
t\geq 0
,

\left\|P_{t}f-\pi(f)\right\|_{L^{2}(\pi)}\leq C_{f}e^{-2Ct}.

(15)

Moreover, if the process is not time-reversible, we have the implications
(i)\iff(ii)\implies(iii)
.

Proof.

We do not assume that the process is time-reversible and begin by showing
(i)\implies(ii)
.

Suppose
\mathrm{gap}({\mathcal{E}})\geq C
for some
C\in(0,\infty)
. Let
f\in\mathcal{B}_{c}
be arbitrary. We have
f\in Dom(\mathcal{A})
by Lemma
2.1
. Because
f\in Dom(\mathcal{A})
,
[
26
, Proposition 1.5]
implies that
P_{t}f\in Dom(\mathcal{A})
for all
t\geq 0
. Recalling that
P_{t}f(x)=\mathbb{E}_{x}[f(X(t))]
, we immediately have
\|P_{t}f\|_{\infty}\leq\|f\|_{\infty}<\infty
. Hence, we can consider
{\mathcal{E}}(P_{t}f,\,P_{t}f)
and we note that by our assumption that
\mathrm{gap}({\mathcal{E}})\geq C
, we have that

\displaystyle{\mathcal{E}}(P_{t}f,\,P_{t}f)\geq C\,Var_{\pi}(P_{t}f),

(16)

for all
t\geq 0
.

Observe that
\pi(P_{t}f)=\pi(f)
is constant in
t
. Hence,

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}Var_{\pi}(P_{t}f)

\displaystyle=\frac{d}{dt}\left\{\pi((P_{t}f)^{2})-[\pi(P_{t}f)]^{2}\right\}=\frac{d}{dt}\pi((P_{t}f)^{2})

\displaystyle=\frac{d}{dt}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{S}}\pi(x)(P_{t}f(x))^{2}.

(17)

Next, and again by
[
26
, Proposition 1.5]
,
\frac{d}{dt}P_{t}f=\mathcal{A}P_{t}f
. Hence, the series giving the term-wise derivatives of the final line above is

\displaystyle\sum_{x\in\mathbb{S}}\pi(x)\frac{d}{dt}(P_{t}f(x))^{2}=2\sum_{x\in\mathbb{S}}\pi(x)P_{t}f(x)\,\mathcal{A}P_{t}f(x)=2\langle P_{t}f,\,\mathcal{A}P_{t}f\rangle_{L^{2}(\pi)}.

Because
f\in\mathcal{B}_{c}
, we know
\|f\|_{\infty}<\infty
. Hence, by Lemma
A.3
the sum above converges uniformly for

. Thus, we may pass the derivative through the sum in (

) and conclude

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}Var_{\pi}(P_{t}f)

\displaystyle=2\langle P_{t}f,\,\mathcal{A}P_{t}f\rangle_{L^{2}(\pi)}

\displaystyle=-2\,{\mathcal{E}}(P_{t}f,P_{t}f)

(18)

\displaystyle\leq-2CVar_{\pi}(P_{t}f),

for
t\geq 0
, where the final inequality follows from (
16
).
Hence for all
t\geq 0
, we have

Var_{\pi}(P_{t}f)\leq e^{-2Ct}Var_{\pi}(f),

which is exactly (
14
).

The proof that
(ii)\implies(i)
is now straightforward by using (
18
) (see
[
45
, Proof of Proposition 2.18]
).
The fact that
(iii)\implies(i)
(and
(ii)
) in the time-reversible setting follows from a convexity argument. See the proof of Theorem 2.18 in
[
45
]
for these details.

The next lemma formally connects the previous result to the convergence of the distribution in the total variation norm, and hence makes the connection to mixing times.

Lemma 2.4
.

Suppose
X
is an irreducible and positive-recurrent continuous-time Markov chain with countable state space
\mathbb{S}\subset\mathbb{Z}^{d}
and stationary distribution
\pi
. Suppose that there is a
C>0
for which

\left\|P_{t}f-\pi(f)\right\|_{L^{2}(\pi)}\leq e^{-Ct}\,\left\|f-\pi(f)\right\|_{L^{2}(\pi)}

(19)

for all
f\in\mathcal{B}_{c}
and
t\geq 0
. Then

\displaystyle\|P^{t}(x,\cdot)-\pi(\cdot)\|_{\text{TV}}\leq\frac{2}{\pi(x)}e^{-Ct}.

Proof.

Let
K
be a fixed, compact subset of
\mathbb{S}
and let
f(x)=\mathbbm{1}_{K}(x)
. For any
x\in\mathbb{S}
, we have

\displaystyle\pi(x)|P^{t}

\displaystyle(x,K)-\pi(K)|\leq\sum_{z\in\mathbb{S}}\pi(z)|P^{t}(z,K)-\pi(K)|

(Cauchy-Schwarz)

\displaystyle=1\cdot\|P_{t}f-\pi(f)\|_{L^{2}(\pi)}

\displaystyle\leq 2e^{-Ct},

where the final inequality follows from (

for our choice of

.
Hence, taking the sup over the compact sets

\displaystyle\|P^{t}(x,\cdot)-\pi(\cdot)\|_{\text{TV}}\leq\frac{2}{\pi(x)}e^{-Ct},

and the result is shown.
∎

Note that the conclusion of Lemma

\displaystyle\tau_{\varepsilon}^{x}

\displaystyle=\inf\{t>0:\|P^{t}(x,\cdot)-\pi(\cdot)\|_{\text{TV}}\leq\varepsilon\}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\mathrm{gap}({\mathcal{E}})}\left(-\ln\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\right)-\ln(\pi(x))\right)=\frac{1}{\mathrm{gap}({\mathcal{E}})}\left(\left|\ln\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\right)\right|+|\ln(\pi(x))|\right).

(20)

2.2
Statement and proof of our main result

In this section we again consider a non-explosive, irreducible continuous-time Markov chain

As before, we will assume throughout that our process satisfies the mild regularity Assumption

Definition 2.1

Let

We denote by

of the path.
We say this path is

\triangle

Note that we allow for the possibility that

in the definition above, in which case

Our new assumption–Assumption

below– involves the existence of very particular sets of paths. It is likely that the conditions on the paths will be difficult to digest at first glance, so we first motivate with an example. The detailed analysis of this example will also make the proof of Theorem

Example 2.1
.

Consider the birth and death model on
\{0,1,2,\dots\}
with transition rates

q(x,x+1)=1\quad\text{and}\quad q(x,x-1)=x\quad\text{for }x\geq 0.

This model has infinitesimal generator

\mathcal{A}f(x)=f(x+1)-f(x)+x(f(x-1)-f(x))

and stationary distribution
\pi(x)=\frac{e^{-1}}{x!}
(This model corresponds to the reaction network
\emptyset\overset{1}{\underset{1}{\rightleftarrows}}X_{1}

\infty

While it is straightforward to prove exponential ergodicity for this model via the use of Foster-Lyapunov functions, we want to instead prove positivity of the spectral gap of the process so as to motivate our constructions. Hence, we want to show the existence of a
C>0
so that

Var_{\pi}(f)\leq C\,\mathcal{E}^{*}(f).

for any

with

.
To do so, we start with the following bound:

\displaystyle Var_{\pi}(f)

\displaystyle=\sum_{x=0}^{\infty}\left(f(x)-\sum_{x^{\prime}=0}^{\infty}f(x^{\prime})\pi(x^{\prime})\right)^{2}\pi(x)=\sum_{x=0}^{\infty}\left(\sum_{x^{\prime}=0}^{\infty}(f(x)-f(x^{\prime}))\pi(x^{\prime})\right)^{2}\pi(x)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{x=0}^{\infty}\sum_{x^{\prime}=0}^{\infty}(f(x)-f(x^{\prime}))^{2}\pi(x)\pi(x^{\prime})

\displaystyle=2\sum_{x=0}^{\infty}\sum_{x^{\prime}=x+1}^{\infty}(f(x)-f(x^{\prime}))^{2}\pi(x)\pi(x^{\prime}).

To bound the final double sum above by

x<x^{\prime}
via the active path

\gamma(x,x^{\prime})=(x,x+1,\dots,x^{\prime})

\displaystyle\sum_{x=0}^{\infty}\sum_{x^{\prime}=x+1}^{\infty}(f(x)-f(x^{\prime}))^{2}\pi(x)\pi(x^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\sum_{x=0}^{\infty}\sum_{x^{\prime}=x+1}^{\infty}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{x^{\prime}-x}(f(x_{i})-f(x_{i+1})\right)^{2}\pi(x)\pi(x^{\prime})

(22)

where we used

\sum_{i=1}^{x^{\prime}-x}(f(x_{i})-f(x_{i+1}))^{2}\pi(x_{i})q(x_{i},x_{i+1})\leq\sum_{x=0}^{\infty}\sum_{z=0}^{\infty}\big(f(x)-f(z)\big)^{2}\,\pi(x)\,q(x,z)=2\mathcal{E}^{*}(f).

\sum_{x=0}^{\infty}\sum_{x^{\prime}=x+1}^{\infty}|x^{\prime}-x|\frac{\pi(x)\pi(x^{\prime})}{\displaystyle\min_{i\in\{1,\dots,|x^{\prime}-x|\}}\pi(x_{i})q(x_{i},x_{i+1})}<\infty,

(23)

However, it turns out that this term is actually infinity for the present example and our bounds, especially that of (
22

2.2
).
To show that the term on the left of (
23

•

q(x_{i},x_{i+1})=1
for all

,

•

because

\displaystyle\min_{i\in\{1,\dots,|x^{\prime}-x|\}}\pi(x_{i})q(x_{i},x_{i+1})=\pi(x^{\prime}-1)=e^{-1}\frac{1}{(x^{\prime}-1)!}
.

Hence,

\displaystyle\sum_{x=0}^{\infty}\sum_{x^{\prime}=x+1}^{\infty}|x^{\prime}-x|\frac{\pi(x)\pi(x^{\prime})}{\displaystyle\min_{i\in\{1,\dots,|x^{\prime}-x|\}}\pi(x_{i})q(x_{i},x_{i+1})}=\sum_{x=0}^{\infty}\sum_{x^{\prime}=x+1}^{\infty}(x^{\prime}-x)\pi(x)\cdot\frac{1}{x^{\prime}}=\infty,

and our first attempt to prove that the gap is positive has failed.

To fix the method for this example we note that if we had changed things so that a modified version of the term

\frac{\pi(x^{\prime})}{\displaystyle\min_{i\in\{1,\dots,|x^{\prime}-x|\}}\pi(x_{i})q(x_{i},x_{i+1})}

\frac{1}{(x^{\prime})^{3}}
(or smaller), then the double sum in (

x^{\prime}-3

Therefore, we begin by defining a function,

\displaystyle t(x)=\begin{cases}x-3\quad&\text{for $x>3$}\\
x&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Then, for each pair of points

x^{\prime}

1.

A path from

\displaystyle\gamma(x,t(x))=\begin{cases}(x,x-1,x-2,x-3)&\text{ if $x>3$}\\

(24)

(37)

where we used

is defined in (

We may plug the inequality in (

\displaystyle Var_{\pi}(f)\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{(x,x^{\prime})\in\mathbb{S}\times\mathbb{S}}(f(x)-f(x^{\prime}))^{2}\pi(x)\pi(x^{\prime})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 4\overline{L}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(x,x^{\prime})\in\mathbb{S}\times\mathbb{S}\\

\displaystyle+2\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(x,x^{\prime})\in\mathbb{S}\times\mathbb{S}\\

For each pair

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{|\gamma(t(x),t(x^{\prime}))|-1}(f(z_{i+1})-f(z_{i}))^{2}\pi(x)\pi(x^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{|\gamma(t(x),t(x^{\prime}))|-1}(f(z_{i+1})-f(z_{i}))^{2}\,\frac{q({z_{i},z_{i+1})}\,\pi(z_{i})}{q({z_{i},z_{i+1})}\,\pi(z_{i})}\pi(x)\pi(x^{\prime})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{c_{\text{min}}}\,\left(\max_{z\in\gamma(t(x),t(x^{\prime}))}\frac{\pi(x)\pi(x^{\prime})}{\pi(z)}\right)\sum_{i=1}^{|\gamma(t(x),t(x^{\prime}))|-1}(f(z_{i+1})-f(z_{i}))^{2}\,q({z_{i},z_{i+1})}\,\pi(z_{i})

where

to the particular path

It follows from (

\displaystyle 2\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(x,x^{\prime})\in\mathbb{S}\times\mathbb{S}\\
x\neq x^{\prime}\end{subarray}}

\displaystyle\left(|\gamma(t(x),t(x^{\prime}))|\sum_{i=1}^{|\gamma(t(x),t(x^{\prime}))|-1}(f(z_{i+1})-f(z_{i}))^{2}\right)\pi(x)\pi(x^{\prime})

x\neq x^{\prime}\end{subarray}}|\gamma(t(x),t(x^{\prime}))|\left(\frac{\pi(x)\pi(x^{\prime})}{\pi_{\text{min}}(\gamma(t(x),t(x^{\prime})))}\right)

(42)

for some constant

By the symmetry of (

\displaystyle\sum_{x\in\mathbb{S}}\sum_{i=1}^{|\gamma_{x}|-1}(f(x_{i+1})-f(x_{i}))^{2}\pi(x)<c\cdot{\mathcal{E}}^{*}(f).

(43)

We have

\displaystyle\sum_{x\in\mathbb{S}}\sum_{i=1}^{|\gamma_{x}|-1}(f(x_{i+1})-f(x_{i}))^{2}\pi(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{x\in\mathbb{S}}\sum_{i=1}^{|\gamma_{x}|-1}(f(x_{i+1})-f(x_{i}))^{2}\frac{q{(x_{i},x_{i+1})}\,\pi(x_{i})}{q{(x_{i},x_{i+1})}\,\pi(x_{i})}\pi(x)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{c_{\text{min}}}\,\left(\sup_{x\in\mathbb{S}}\frac{\pi(x)}{\pi_{\text{min}}(\gamma(x))}\right)\,\sum_{x\in\mathbb{S}}\sum_{i=1}^{|\gamma_{x}|-1}(f(x_{i+1})-f(x_{i}))^{2}\,q{(x_{i},x_{i+1})}\,\pi(x_{i}).

\displaystyle\sum_{x\in\mathbb{S}}\sum_{i=1}^{|\gamma_{x}|-1}(f(x_{i+1})-f(x_{i}))^{2}\,q{(x_{i},x_{i+1})}\,\pi(x_{i})

\displaystyle\leq\overline{M}\sum_{x,z\in\mathbb{S}}(f(z)-f(x))^{2}q(x,z)\pi(x)

where

∎

2.3
Special cases that are easier to apply for models on
\mathbb{Z}^{d}_{\geq 0}

The conditions in Assumption
2.2
are quite technical. We therefore present a few corollaries that are significantly more “user-friendly.”

Our first corollary considers models restricted to
\mathbb{Z}^{d}_{\geq 0}
and states that the conditions in Assumption
2.2
hold so long as (i) the stationary distribution has a type of super-exponential decay, and (ii) transitions of the form
\pm e_{i}
, where
\{e_{i}\}
is the canonical basis for
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, have positive rate so long as the transition would not push the system into a region with a negative component.
We note that the super-exponential decay condition of (
45
) below holds for many probability distributions showing up in our area of interest of biochemical reaction systems (see section
3
). Examples include models whose stationary distribution is a product of Poissons
[
9
]
, some products of Poisson and Beta-binomials
[
19
]
, and some models with non-mass-action kinetics
[
8
,
31
]
. Moreover, in
[
47
]
, a detailed analysis of the decay of the tails of the stationary distribution for one-dimensional models is given; even though without a lower bound on
\pi(x)
we cannot deduce (
45
), it seems possible the link will be established with future work.

Note that in Corollary
2.6
below the fact that
\mathbb{S}=\mathbb{Z}^{d}_{\geq 0}
is irreducible follows from condition 2 in that corollary.

Corollary 2.6
.

Suppose
X
is a positive-recurrent continuous-time Markov chain with irreducible state space
\mathbb{S}=\mathbb{Z}^{d}_{\geq 0}
and stationary distribution
\pi
. Suppose that Assumptions
2.1
hold. Suppose further that the following two conditions hold.

1.

There exist an integer
K>0
and an
\alpha>0
so that for each
i\in\{1,\dots,d\}
,

\displaystyle\dfrac{\pi(x)}{\pi(x-e_{i})}\leq\dfrac{1}{x_{i}^{\alpha}}

(45)

if
x
is such that
x_{i}\geq K
, and

2.

There is a
c_{\text{min}}>0
so that for each
i\in\{1,\dots,d\}
, we have

\displaystyle\inf_{x\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}_{\geq 0}}q(x,x+e_{i})\geq c_{\text{min}}\quad\text{and}\quad\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}_{\geq 0}\\
x_{i}\geq 1\end{subarray}}q(x,x-e_{i})\geq c_{\text{min}}.

Then the conditions in Assumption
2.2
hold. Hence, the conclusions of Theorem
2.5
hold as well.

We begin with the following lemma, which states that a process satisfying the conditions of Corollary
2.6
has all moments.

Lemma 2.7
.

Let
X
and
\pi
be as in Corollary
2.6
. Let
m\geq 3
be an integer. Then there is a
c_{m}>0
, depending upon
m
, for which

\pi(x)\leq c_{m}\prod_{i=1}^{d}\frac{1}{(x_{i}+1)^{m}},

for all
x\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}_{\geq 0}
.

Proof.

Let
K
and
\alpha
be as in the statement of Corollary
2.6
. Fix a
K^{\prime}>K
so that

\frac{(K!)}{(x!)^{\alpha}}\leq\frac{1}{x^{m}},

(46)

for all
x\geq K^{\prime}
.
With this choice, if
x_{1}\geq K^{\prime}
, then (
45
) with
i=1
implies that

\displaystyle\pi(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{\pi(x)}{\pi(x-e_{1})}\frac{\pi(x-e_{1})}{\pi(x-2e_{1})}\cdots\frac{\pi(x-(x_{1}-K-1)e_{1})}{\pi(x-(x_{1}-K)e_{1})}\pi(x-(x_{1}-K)e_{1})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{x_{1}^{\alpha}}\cdot\frac{1}{(x_{1}-1)^{\alpha}}\cdots\frac{1}{(K+1)^{\alpha}}\pi(x-(x_{1}-K)e_{1})

(repeatedly applying (
45
))

\displaystyle=\frac{(K!)^{\alpha}}{(x_{1}!)^{\alpha}}\pi(x-(x_{1}-K)e_{1})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{x_{1}^{m}}\cdot\pi(x-(x_{1}-K)e_{1}),

(47)

where the final inequality follows from (
46
).
To generalize the above, let
I_{K^{\prime}}(x)=\{i:x_{i}\geq K^{\prime}\}
be those indices for which the
ith
coordinate of
x
is larger than
K^{\prime}
. For each
x
we define
u(x)\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}_{\geq 0}
via

\displaystyle u(x)_{i}=\begin{cases}K\quad&\text{if $i\in I_{K^{\prime}}(x)$}\\
x_{i}&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Applying the above inequality to those indices
I_{K^{\prime}}(x)
, there is a
c_{m}>0
such that for any
x\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}_{\geq 0}

\displaystyle\pi(x)

\displaystyle\leq\prod_{i\in I_{K^{\prime}}(x)}\frac{1}{x_{i}^{m}}\cdot\pi\left(x-\sum_{i\in I_{K}(x)}(x_{i}-K)e_{i}\right)=\prod_{i\in I_{K^{\prime}}(x)}\frac{1}{x_{i}^{m}}\pi(u(x))

\displaystyle\leq c_{m}\prod_{i=1}^{d}\frac{1}{(x_{i}+1)^{m}}\pi(u(x))\leq c_{m}\prod_{i=1}^{d}\frac{1}{(x_{i}+1)^{m}},

where we used that
\pi(u(x))\leq 1
in the final inequality. Hence, the result is shown.
∎

We turn to the proof of Corollary
2.6
.

Proof of Corollary
2.6
.

We begin by defining
t(x)
, the terminal state, for each
x\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}_{\geq 0}
. To do so, we begin by letting
k_{0}\geq K+\frac{3}{\alpha}+1
be a positive integer
and let
I_{k_{0}}(x)=\{i:x_{i}\geq k_{0}\}
be those indices for which
x_{i}\geq k_{0}
. We then define the terminal state for
x
component-wise via

t(x)_{i}=\begin{cases}x_{i}-\lceil\frac{3}{\alpha}\rceil&\text{ if }i\in I_{k_{0}}(x)\\
x_{i}&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

(48)

where
\lceil\frac{3}{\alpha}\rceil
is the smallest integer larger than
\frac{3}{\alpha}
(i.e., we round up).

We turn to defining
\gamma_{x}=\gamma(x,t(x))
for each
x
. If
\|x\|_{\infty}<k_{0}
, in which case
I_{k_{0}}(x)
is empty, then
t(x)=x
and we take
\gamma(x,t(x))=(x)
. Now suppose that
\|x\|_{\infty}\geq k_{0}
. In this case,
I_{k_{0}}(x)
is non-empty and for each
i\in I_{k_{0}}(x)
we will subtract off
e_{i}
exactly
\lceil\frac{3}{\alpha}\rceil
times. Note that there are
\displaystyle\frac{(|I_{k_{0}}(x)|\cdot\lceil\frac{3}{\alpha}\rceil)!}{(\lceil\frac{3}{\alpha}\rceil!)^{|I_{k_{0}}(x)|}}
possible such paths connecting
x
and
t(x)
using only subtractions of
e_{i}
for
i\in I_{k_{0}}(x)
. We choose one of those paths at random.

For example, consider the situation with
d=3
,
\alpha=1
,
and
x=(k_{0}+2,k_{0}-1,k_{0}+1)
. Here,
I_{k_{0}}(x)=\{1,3\}
and
\lceil\frac{3}{\alpha}\rceil=3
and so
t(x)=(k_{0}-1,k_{0}-1,k_{0}-2)
. There are
\frac{(2\cdot 3)!}{(3!)^{2}}=20
possible paths to choose from. If we choose the path that decreases the first component 3 times in a row followed by the third component 3 times in a row, then the specific path
\gamma_{x}=\gamma(x,t(x))
is

((k_{0}+2,k_{0}-1,k_{0}+1),\dots,(k_{0}-1,k_{0}-1,k_{0}+1),\dots,(k_{0}-1,k_{0}-1,k_{0}-2)).

Returning to the general case, we now observe the following.

•

The paths constructed have a maximum length of
\lceil\frac{3}{\alpha}\rceil d
, so (
28
) holds.

•

Each edge in
\gamma(x,t(x))
must be within
\lceil\frac{3}{\alpha}\rceil d
transitions taken from the set
\{-e_{i}:i\in\{1,\dots,d\}\}
. Hence, the number of such paths that a particular edge can be contained within is bounded and (
29
) holds.

•

Combining (
45
) with the construction above immediately yields

\sup_{x\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}_{\geq 0}}\frac{\pi(x)}{\pi_{\text{min}}(\gamma_{x})}\leq 1,

and so (
30
) holds.

Hence, condition 1 of Assumption
2.2
holds.

To verify condition 2 of Assumption
2.2
, we first
point out a bound we will use later. Doing an analysis similar to that found in (
47
), we may apply (
45
) one time for each edge in the path
\gamma(x,t(x))
to conclude that there exist constants
c_{1}>0
and
c_{2}>0

that are independent of the choice of
x\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}_{\geq 0}
, such that

\displaystyle\frac{\pi(x)}{\pi(t(x))}

\displaystyle\leq\prod_{i\in I_{k_{0}}(x)}\left(\frac{c_{1}}{(x_{i})^{\alpha}}\right)^{\lceil\frac{3}{\alpha}\rceil}\leq\prod_{i=1}^{d}\frac{c_{2}}{(x_{i}+1)^{3}}.

(49)

We turn to the paths connecting the terminal states.
Recall that
\mathcal{T}
is the set of terminal states
\{t(x):x\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}_{\geq 0}\}
.
We must still define
\Gamma_{\mathcal{T}}
so that (
31
) holds.
Towards that end, select a pair of terminal states:
t(x),t(x^{\prime})\in\mathcal{T}
, with
t(x)\neq t(x^{\prime})
.
We arbitrarily choose to orient the path as starting from
t(x)
and terminating at
t(x^{\prime})
. That is, we construct the path
\gamma(t(x),t(x^{\prime}))
.
Moreover, to construct the path
\gamma(t(x),t(x^{\prime}))
we only use transitions of the form
\{\pm e_{i},i\in\{1,\dots d\}\}
, where, as always,
\{e_{i}\}
is the canonical basis in
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
.

For two vectors
x,y\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
we denote by
x\wedge y
the vector whose
i
th coordinate is
x_{i}\wedge y_{i}=\min\{x_{i},y_{i}\}
.
The main idea for the construction of the desired
\gamma(t(x),t(x^{\prime}))
is to use two sub-paths: one connects
t(x)
to
t(x)\wedge t(x^{\prime})
by only using transitions of the form
\{-e_{i},i\in\{1,\dots,d\}\}
, and the other one connects
t(x)\wedge t(x^{\prime})
to
t(x^{\prime})
by only using transitions of the form
\{e_{i},i\in\{1,\dots,d\}\}
.

We now make this precise. Let
I^{-}=\{i:t(x)_{i}>t(x^{\prime})_{i}\}
and
I^{+}=\{i:t(x)_{i}<t(x^{\prime})_{i}\}
. Note that at least one of
I^{-}
or
I^{+}
is non-empty since
t(x)\neq t(x^{\prime})
. Note that there are

\frac{\left(\sum_{i\in I^{-}}(t(x)_{i}-t(x^{\prime})_{i})\right)!}{\prod_{i\in I^{-}}(t(x)_{i}-t(x^{\prime})_{i})!}

paths connecting
t(x)
to
t(x)\wedge t(x^{\prime})
using only transitions of the form
-e_{i}
for those
i\in I^{-}
. The particular choice of path does not matter and we select one at random. We now have our path,
\gamma^{-}=\gamma(t(x),t(x)\wedge t(x^{\prime}))
connecting
t(x)
to
t(x)\wedge t(x^{\prime})
.

We then similarly connect
t(x)\wedge t(x^{\prime})
to
t(x^{\prime})
via a path using only transitions of the form
e_{i}
for
i\in I^{+}
. We denote this path via
\gamma^{+}=\gamma(t(x)\wedge t(x^{\prime}),t(x^{\prime}))
. The path connecting
t(x)
to
t(x^{\prime})
, denoted
\gamma(t(x),t(x^{\prime}))
, is then defined by moving first along
\gamma^{-}
and then along
\gamma^{+}.

Note that, by construction, if
z
is an element of
\gamma(t(x),t(x^{\prime}))
, i.e. a state visited along the path
\gamma^{-}
or
\gamma^{+}
, then we either have
z\leq t(x)
(meaning
z_{i}\leq t(x)_{i}
for all
i\in\{1,\dots,d\}
) or
z\leq t(x^{\prime})
. Combining this fact with (
45
) implies that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\displaystyle\pi_{\text{min}}(\gamma(t(x),t(x^{\prime})))}\leq C_{K}\max\left\{\frac{1}{\pi(t(x))},\frac{1}{\pi(t(x^{\prime}))}\right\},

(50)

where the constant
C_{K}>0
is needed since some coordinates of
t(x)
or
t(x^{\prime})
may be smaller than
K
.

Finally, note that

|\gamma(t(x),t(x^{\prime})|=\sum_{i\in I^{+}\cup I^{-}}|t(x)_{i}-t(x^{\prime})_{i}|\leq|x|+|x^{\prime}|.

(51)

We may combine all of the above to show that (
31
) holds:

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(x,x^{\prime})\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}_{\geq 0}\times\mathbb{Z}^{d}_{\geq 0}\\
x\neq x^{\prime}\end{subarray}}|\gamma(t(x),t(x^{\prime}))|\left(\frac{\pi(x)\pi(x^{\prime})}{\pi_{\text{min}}(\gamma(t(x),t(x^{\prime})))}\right)

\displaystyle\leq C_{K}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(x,x^{\prime})\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}_{\geq 0}\times\mathbb{Z}^{d}_{\geq 0}\\
x\neq x^{\prime}\end{subarray}}(|x|+|x^{\prime}|)\pi(x)\pi(x^{\prime})\max\left\{\frac{1}{\pi(t(x))},\frac{1}{\pi(t(x^{\prime}))}\right\}

(using (
50
) and (
51
))

\displaystyle\leq C_{K}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(x,x^{\prime})\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}_{\geq 0}\times\mathbb{Z}^{d}_{\geq 0}\\
x\neq x^{\prime}\end{subarray}}(|x|+|x^{\prime}|)\pi(x)\pi(x^{\prime})\left(\frac{1}{\pi(t(x))}+\frac{1}{\pi(t(x^{\prime}))}\right)

\displaystyle\leq C_{K}\cdot c_{3}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(x,x^{\prime})\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}_{\geq 0}\times\mathbb{Z}^{d}_{\geq 0}\\
x\neq x^{\prime}\end{subarray}}(|x|+|x^{\prime}|)\left(\pi(x^{\prime})\prod_{i=1}^{d}\frac{1}{(x_{i}+1)^{3}}+\pi(x)\prod_{i=1}^{d}\frac{1}{(x^{\prime}_{i}+1)^{3}}\right),

(from (
49
))

which is finite by applying Lemma
2.7
with

.
Hence, we have demonstrated that condition 2 of Assumption
2.2
holds.

Finally, since all the paths we constructed above only consist of birth and death events (i.e. the transitions given by
e_{i}
and
-e_{i}
), the second condition of Corollary
2.6
(pertaining to the infimum of the rates) implies that condition 3 of Assumption
2.2
holds. Hence, the proof is complete.
∎

If the stationary distribution
\pi
is of a product form, while also satisfying condition 1 of Corollary
2.6
, then we can relax the second condition in Corollary
2.6
. That condition required that births and deaths can happen for each index at each state in
\mathbb{Z}^{d}_{\geq 0}
(so long as the transition did not push the process out of
\mathbb{Z}^{d}_{\geq 0}
), and moreover that the rates for those transitions had a uniform lower bound.
Loosely speaking, the
new condition is that there is a partition of the indices
\{1,\dots,d\}
, denoted by the disjoint, non-empty sets
\{J_{0},\dots,J_{m}\}
so that (i) the transitions
\pm e_{i}
have positive rate for all states if
i\in J_{0}
(so long as the transition does not push the process negative in the
i
th component), and (ii) the transitions
\pm e_{i}
, for
i\in J_{\ell}
, have positive rate for any state for which
x_{j}
is greater than some given threshold (denoted
N
below) for some
j\in\cup_{i=0}^{\ell-1}J_{i}
. Moreover, the rates of these transitions must still have a uniform lower bound. Thus, loosely, the components associated to the indices in
J_{0}\cup\cdots\cup J_{\ell-1}
act as catalysts, or at least switches, for the birth and death transitions for the components associated to the indices in
J_{\ell}
. Note that if
J_{0}=\{1,\dots,d\}
then this condition is the same as condition 2 of Corollary
2.6
.

Note that in the corollary below the fact that
\mathbb{S}=\mathbb{Z}^{d}_{\geq 0}
is irreducible follows from conditions 2 and 3.

Corollary 2.8
.

Suppose
X
is an irreducible and positive-recurrent continuous-time Markov chain with countable state space
\mathbb{S}=\mathbb{Z}^{d}_{\geq 0}
and stationary distribution
\pi
for which Assumption
2.1
holds.
Suppose that the following three conditions hold.

1.

\pi(x)=\prod_{i}^{d}\pi_{i}(x_{i})
where
\pi_{i}
is a probability measure on
\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
. Moreover, there is an integer
K\geq 1
and a constant
\alpha>0
so that for each

we have
\dfrac{\pi_{i}(n)}{\pi_{i}(n-1)}\leq\dfrac{1}{n^{\alpha}}
whenever

.

2.

There exists an
c_{\text{min}}>0
and a non-empty subset
J_{0}\subset\{1,2,\dots,d\}
so that for each

we have

\displaystyle=\pi(x+e_{1}+e_{2})q(x+e_{1}+e_{2},x),

\displaystyle\pi(x)q(x,x+e_{2})

\displaystyle=\pi(x+e_{2})q(x+e_{2},x)

Verification is straightforward, and is left to the reader. It is further not complicated (by using techniques of reaction network theory such as deficiency and the tree-like structure of the network
[
28
,
24
,
39
]
) to show that the model is detailed balanced in the sense of
[
33
]
, which implies time-reversibility but is not equivalent to it
[
34
,
20
]
. By Theorem
2.3
, it is enough to demonstrate that
\text{gap}({\mathcal{E}})=0
to prove the model is not
L^{2}
exponentially ergodic. (Moreover, because the model is time-reversible, even the weaker form of exponential convergence given by (iii) in Theorem
2.3
does not hold.) Hence, by (
13
), it is sufficient to show that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\inf\left\{\sum_{x,y\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}_{\geq 0}}q(x,y)[f(y)-f(x)]^{2}\pi(x):f\in\mathcal{B}_{c},\ \pi(f)=0,\ \pi(f^{2})=1\right\}=0,

(62)

where, as always,
\mathcal{B}_{c}
is the set of functions with bounded support.

Let

f_{n}(x)=c_{n}I_{n}(x)-d_{n},

where
I_{n}
is the indicator function on
\{(n,0),(n+1,1)\}
and
c_{n}
and
d_{n}
will be chosen so that
\pi(f_{n})=0
and
\pi(f_{n}^{2})=1
.
It is straightforward to check that these conditions imply

\displaystyle c_{n}^{2}=\frac{1}{\pi(n,0)+\pi(n+1,1)-(\pi(n,0)+\pi(n+1,1))^{2}}.

From (
61
), the dominant term in the denominator is
\pi(n,0)=e^{-2}\frac{1}{n!}
. Hence, we conclude that

c_{n}^{2}\approx e^{2}\cdot n!.

Now we compute the term in (
62
) with
f_{n}
. We just have to consider those transitions taking us in to and out of the set
\{(n,0),(n+1,1)\}
. We have (the first two are the only possible transitions out of the set and the next two are the two transitions that can take you in):

\displaystyle\begin{split}q&((n+1,1),(n+2,2))[f_{n}(n+2,2)-f_{n}(n+1,1)]^{2}\pi(n+1,1)\\
&+q((n+1,1),(n+1,2))[f_{n}(n+1,2)-f_{n}(n+1,1)]^{2}\pi(n+1,1)\\
&+q((n+2,2),(n+1,1))[f_{n}(n+1,1)-f_{n}(n+2,2)]^{2}\pi(n+2,2)\\
&+q((n+1,2),(n+1,1))[f_{n}(n+1,1)-f_{n}(n+1,2)]^{2}\pi(n+1,2)\\
=&\ c_{n}^{2}\left[2\pi(n+1,1)+2(n+2)\pi(n+2,2)+2\pi(n+1,2)\right].\end{split}

(63)

Thus, combining
c_{n}^{2}\approx e^{2}n!
with (
61
), we have that the term above satisfies

\displaystyle\approx 2\cdot n!\frac{1}{(n+1)!}+2(n+2)\cdot n!\frac{1}{2(n+2)!}+2\cdot n!\frac{1}{2(n+1)!},

which goes to zero, as
n\to\infty
. Hence, the infimum of (
62
) is zero. Thus, for this particular model,
\text{gap}({\mathcal{E}})=0
and it is not exponentially ergodic.

Now we can ask: what would have been different in the above calculation if we further allow transitions of the form
\pm e_{i}
(i.e., what if the model satisfied the assumptions of our Corollaries
2.6
and
2.8
)? Because of the presence of a rate for
+e_{1}
that is lower bounded (by, say,
\lambda>0
), we would get the following term added to (
63
)

\displaystyle q((n,0),(n+1,0))[f_{n}(n+1,0)-f_{n}(n,0)]^{2}\pi(n,0)\geq\lambda c_{n}^{2}\pi(n,0)\approx\lambda\cdot n!\cdot\frac{1}{n!}=1.

Hence, we could not have concluded that the gap was zero in this new scenario.
Of course, in this new scenario we
know
the gap is strictly positive by our Corollaries
2.6
and
2.8
.

\triangle

3
Applications to stochastic reaction networks

In this section we explore the applicability of our results to a wide class of continuous-time Markov chains used heavily in biochemistry, ecology, and epidemiology. The models are referred to as
stochastic reaction networks
. We will provide the necessary definition of the models under consideration in Section
3.1
, then examine a specific example, and finally prove exponential ergodicity for large families of stochastic reaction networks that are of particular interest to the community.

3.1
Reaction networks and their associated stochastic model

We give a minimal introduction to reaction networks and their associated dynamical systems. For a more thorough introduction, see
[
13
]
.
We begin with the definition of a reaction network.

Definition 3.1
.

A reaction network
is given by a triple of finite sets
(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{C},\mathcal{R})
where:

1.

The species set

\mathcal{S}=\{X_{1},X_{2},\cdots,X_{d}\}
contains the species of the reaction network.

2.

The reaction set

\mathcal{R}=\{R_{1},R_{2},\cdots,R_{r}\}
consists of ordered pairs
(y,y^{\prime})\in\mathcal{R}
, with
y\neq y^{\prime}
, where

\displaystyle y=\sum_{i=1}^{d}y_{i}X_{i}\hskip 11.38092pt\textrm{and}\hskip 11.38092pty^{\prime}=\sum_{i=1}^{d}y^{\prime}_{i}X_{i}

(64)

and where the values
y_{i},y^{\prime}_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
are the
stoichiometric coefficients
. We will write reactions
(y,y^{\prime})
as
y\rightarrow y^{\prime}
, and refer to
y
as the
source complex
and
y^{\prime}
as the
product complex
.

3.

The complex set

\mathcal{C}
consists of the linear combinations of the species in (
64
). Specifically,

\mathcal{C}=\{y\ :\ y\rightarrow y^{\prime}\in\mathcal{R}\}\cup\{y^{\prime}\ :\ y\rightarrow y^{\prime}\in\mathcal{R}\}
.

\triangle

Note that when we are working in a general setting we are denoting our species via
X_{1},\dots,X_{d}
. However, when a specific model is under consideration, such as that found in Figure
1
, more suggestive notation is often utilized (for example, in that model
X_{p}
and
Y_{p}
are the “p”hosporolated versions of certain proteins).

Depending on the context,
y
can denote either the linear combination in (
64
) or the vector whose
i
-th component is
y_{i}
, i.e.
y=(y_{1},y_{2},\cdots,y_{d})^{\top}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}_{\geq 0}
.
For example, when
\mathcal{S}=\{X_{1},X_{2},\dots,X_{d}\}
,
y=2X_{1}+X_{2}
is associated with
(2,1,0,0,\dots,0)^{\top}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}_{\geq 0}
.
When
y=(0,0,\dots,0)^{\top}
, we denote the complex by
\emptyset
. This complex is used for the death of species, such as
X_{1}\to\emptyset
, or the production of species from outside of the system, such as
\emptyset\to X_{1}
.

The triple of sets found in Definition
3.1
is most often illustrated with a
reaction graph
in which the nodes are the complexes, each complex is written exactly one time (even if it appears in more than one reaction), and the directed edges are given by the reactions.

Example 3.1
.

The reaction graph

S+E\rightleftarrows SE\rightarrow E+P,

has
\mathcal{S}=\{S,E,SE,P\}
,
\mathcal{C}=\{S+E,SE,E+P\}
and
\mathcal{R}=\{S+E\rightarrow SE,SE\rightarrow S+E,SE\rightarrow E+P\}
. This is a standard model for substrate-enzyme kinetics.
\triangle

3.1.1
Stochastic model

Given a reaction network
(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{C},\mathcal{R})
, a
(stochastic) kinetics
is an assignment of a rate or
intensity
function
\lambda_{y\to y^{\prime}}:\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
to each reaction
y\to y^{\prime}\in\mathcal{R}
. We then call
(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{C},\mathcal{R},\{\lambda_{y\to y^{\prime}}\})
a
stochastic reaction system
. The change in species counts is modeled by means of a continuous-time Markov chain with state space
\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{d}
, whose transition rates are given by

\displaystyle q(x,x^{\prime})=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}y\to y^{\prime}\in\mathcal{R}\\
y^{\prime}-y=x^{\prime}-x\end{subarray}}\lambda_{y\to y^{\prime}}(x),

where the sum is over those reactions whose occurrence causes a net change that is precisely
x^{\prime}-x
.
In case of an explosion occurring at a finite-time
T_{\infty}
, we consider
X(t)=\Delta
for any
t\geq T_{\infty}
, where
\Delta
is a cemetery state not contained in
\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{d}

[
40
]
. The infinitesimal generator
\mathcal{A}
of the associated Markov process acts on functions via the operation

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}f(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{x^{\prime}}q(x,x^{\prime})(f(x^{\prime})-f(x))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{y\rightarrow y^{\prime}\in\mathcal{R}}\lambda_{y\rightarrow y^{\prime}}(x)(f(x+y^{\prime}-y)-f(x)),

(65)

for any function
f
in its domain satisfying
f(\Delta)=0
.

A popular choice of stochastic kinetics is given by
(stochastic) mass-action kinetics
, where for any reaction
y\to y^{\prime}\in\mathcal{R}

\displaystyle\lambda_{y\to y^{\prime}}(x)=\kappa_{y\to y^{\prime}}\prod_{i=1}^{d}\frac{x_{i}!}{(x_{i}-y_{i})!}\mathbbm{1}_{\{x_{i}\geq y_{i}\}},

(66)

for reaction constant
\kappa_{y\to y^{\prime}}
. We denote
\mathcal{K}=\{\kappa_{y\to y^{\prime}}\}
. When the intensity functions are given by mass-action kinetics we write the stochastic system as
(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{C},\mathcal{R},\mathcal{K})
.

Note that the first three conditions of Assumption
2.1
automatically hold for mass-action systems. However, the fourth condition (non-explosivity) may not.

3.2
Exponential ergodicity for stochastic reaction systems

We start this section with an example on how to use our results in the context of stochastic reaction networks. We then proceed to state general results pertaining models related to complex-balancing (but not necessarily complex-balanced).

Example 3.2
.

Consider the following mass-action system, with the rate constants written next to the associated reaction:

X_{1}
0
X_{2}
X_{1}+X_{2}
2X_{1}
2X_{2}
\kappa_{1}
\delta
\kappa_{2}
\delta
\rho
\rho

This is model where particles of both types promote their own creations via the auto-catalytic reactions
X_{1}+X_{2}\to 2X_{1}
and
X_{1}+X_{2}\to 2X_{2}
. Auto-catalytic models have been extensively studied in biochemistry and related mathematical questions have been popularised in
[

]
. Here we assume that the degradation rates of both types of particles is the same, which is not unrealistic in many concrete examples where degradation is due to homogeneous dilution. We further assume that the two molecules are equally likely to be produced via the auto-catalytic mechanism. The above example has been studied in
[
19

, where it is proved that the model is positive recurrent and the stationary distribution is given by

\pi(x)=\frac{M}{x_{1}!x_{2}!}\frac{\Gamma(x_{1}+\gamma_{1})\Gamma(x_{2}+\gamma_{2})}{\Gamma(x_{1}+x_{2}+\gamma_{1}+\gamma_{2})}\left(\frac{\kappa_{1}+\kappa_{2}}{\delta}\right)^{x_{1}+x_{2}},

where
\Gamma
denotes the Gamma function,

\gamma_{1}=\frac{\delta\kappa_{1}}{\rho(\kappa_{1}+\kappa_{2})},\quad\gamma_{2}=\frac{\delta\kappa_{2}}{\rho(\kappa_{1}+\kappa_{2})},

and
M
is a normalizing constant that can be explicitly calculated as

M=\frac{\Gamma(\gamma_{1}+\gamma_{2})}{\Gamma(\gamma_{1})\Gamma(\gamma_{2})}e^{-\frac{\kappa_{1}+\kappa_{2}}{\delta}}.

Note that in this case, for each
x\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}_{\geq 0}
with

we have

\displaystyle\frac{\pi(x)}{\pi(x-e_{1})}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{x_{1}}\frac{x_{1}+\gamma_{1}-1}{x_{1}+x_{2}+\gamma_{1}+\gamma_{2}-1}\frac{\kappa_{1}+\kappa_{2}}{\delta}\leq\frac{1}{x_{1}}\frac{\kappa_{1}+\kappa_{2}}{\delta}.

In particular, if
\sqrt{x_{1}}\geq(\kappa_{1}+\kappa_{2})/\delta
then

\frac{\pi(x)}{\pi(x-e_{1})}\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{x_{1}}}.

Similarly, if
x_{2}\geq\min\{1,(\kappa_{1}+\kappa_{2})^{2}/\delta^{2}\}
we have

\frac{\pi(x)}{\pi(x-e_{2})}\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{x_{2}}}.

Hence, by Corollary
2.6
we can conclude that the model is exponentially ergodic, the second condition of Corollary
2.6
being guaranteed by the presence of the reactions
X_{1}\rightleftharpoons 0\rightleftharpoons X_{2}
.
\triangle

The main scope of this section, however, is not to investigate the full applicability of our results in the setting of stochastic reaction networks, but to merely suggest examples of applications. Hence, for the sake of simplicity, we do not state here the results obtained in

3.2.1
Reaction networks related to complex-balancing

A special case of particular relevance in reaction network theory is given by
complex-balanced mass-action systems
: these are mass-action systems
(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{C},\mathcal{R},\mathcal{K})
for which there exists a positive vector
c\in\mathbb{R}^{d}_{>0}
satisfying

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}y^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}:\\

y^{\prime}\to y\in\mathcal{R}\end{subarray}}\kappa_{y^{\prime}\to y}\prod_{i=1}^{d}c_{i}^{y^{\prime}_{i}},

for each fixed complex
y\in\mathcal{C}
. In this case, the vector
c
is termed a
complex-balanced equilibrium
. The study of complex-balanced networks dates back to
[
27
,
33
]
, where necessary and sufficient conditions for its existence are given, and has an important role in the development of reaction network theory. We give a minimal introduction in Section
D
, where we explain why the vector
c
is called an
equilibrium
.

We will apply our theory to a class of stochastic reaction systems that are related to complex-balancing, but are not necessarily complex-balanced themselves. To this aim, the only result we need from the literature is the following one, which is proven in
[
14
]
and extends previous work done in
[
9
,
6
,
21
]
.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{C},\mathcal{R},\mathcal{K})
be a mass-action system with a complex-balanced equilibrium

. Consider the stochastic process
\{X(t),t\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}\}
given by the stochastic reaction system
(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{C},\mathcal{R},\{\lambda_{y\to y^{\prime}}\})
where the rate functions are of the form

\lambda_{y\to y^{\prime}}(x)=\kappa_{y\to y^{\prime}}\prod_{i=1}^{d}\prod_{j=0}^{y_{i}-1}\theta_{i}(x_{i}-j)

(67)

for the same rate constants
\kappa_{y\to y^{\prime}}
as in
(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{C},\mathcal{R},\mathcal{K})
and some functions
\theta_{i}:\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
satisfying

\displaystyle\theta_{i}(n)

\displaystyle=0\quad\text{if and only if }n\leq 0

(68)

\displaystyle\lim_{j\to\infty}\theta_{i}(j)

\displaystyle=\infty.

(69)

Then,
\{X(t),t\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}\}
is non-explosive and admits the stationary distribution

\pi(x)=M\prod_{i=1}^{d}\frac{c_{i}^{x_{i}}}{\prod_{j=1}^{x_{i}}\theta_{i}(j)},\quad\text{for each $x\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{d}$},

(70)

where
M
is a positive normalizing constant and the empty product is to be considered equal to 1.

Remark 3.1
.

Note that stochastic mass-action kinetics satisfies (
67
), (
68
), and (
69
) with the choice

\theta_{i}(j)=\begin{cases}j&\text{if }j\geq 1\\
0&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}.

(71)

In this case, (
70
) is a product-form Poisson distribution.

We can state the following results.

Theorem 3.2
.

Let
(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{C},\mathcal{R},\mathcal{K})
be a mass-action system with a complex-balanced equilibrium
c\in\mathbb{R}^{d}_{>0}
, such that

\displaystyle\{X_{i}\to 0:X_{i}\in\mathcal{S}\}\bigcup\{0\to X_{i}:X_{i}\in\mathcal{S}\}\subseteq\mathcal{R}.

\{X(t),t\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}\}

(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{C},\mathcal{R},\{\lambda_{y\to y^{\prime}}\})

\lambda_{y\to y^{\prime}}(x)=\kappa_{y\to y^{\prime}}\prod_{i=1}^{d}\prod_{j=0}^{y_{i}-1}\theta_{i}(x_{i}-j)

\kappa_{y\to y^{\prime}}
as in
(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{C},\mathcal{R},\mathcal{K})
and some functions

\displaystyle\theta_{i}(n)=0

\displaystyle\text{if }n>0

Proof.

First of all, note that the presence of the reactions

and

The first condition of Corollary

and

The proof is then concluded by the application of Corollary

Appendix A
Technical results related to relevant operators

In this brief section, we suppose that
X
is an irreducible and positive-recurrent continuous-time Markov chain with countable state space
\mathbb{S}\subset\mathbb{Z}^{d}
and stationary distribution
\pi
that satisfies Assumption
2.1
.
We let
P_{t}
denote the semigroup operator on
L^{2}(\pi)
associated with
X
defined by
P_{t}f(x)=\mathbb{E}_{x}[f(X_{t})]
,
and denote the infinitesimal generator by
\mathcal{A}
and its domain in
L^{2}(\pi)
by
Dom(\mathcal{A})
.

The first two results are standard (for example, see
[
26
]
) but are required for our proofs and so are included for completeness. In particular, they allow us to utilize the results from Chapter 1 of
[
26
]
(especially Proposition 1.5, which we use throughout). The third result is required for the proof of Theorem
2.3
. It is straightforward, but we were not able to find it in the literature and so include it here.

Lemma A.1
.

P_{t}
is a contraction on
L^{2}(\pi)
. That is, for
f\in L^{2}(\pi)
, we have
\|P_{t}f\|_{L^{2}(\pi)}^{2}\leq\|f\|_{L^{2}(\pi)}^{2}
.

Lemma A.2
.

P_{t}
is strongly continuous on
L^{2}(\pi)
. That is, for
f\in L^{2}(\pi)
, we have
\lim_{t\to 0}P_{t}f=f
, in
L^{2}(\pi)
.

Lemma A.3
.

For
f\in Dom(\mathcal{A})
with
\|f\|_{\infty}<\infty
, the sum

\sum_{x\in\mathbb{S}}\pi(x)P_{t}f(x)\,\mathcal{A}P_{t}f(x)

converges uniformly over
t\geq 0
.

Proof.

Let
K_{n}\subset K_{n+1}
be an increasing sequence of compact sets with
\cup_{n}K_{n}=\mathbb{S}
. Denote
f(t)=\sum_{x\in\mathbb{S}}\pi(x)P_{t}f(x)\,\mathcal{A}P_{t}f(x)
and
f_{n}(t)=\sum_{x\in K_{n}}\pi(x)P_{t}f(x)\,\mathcal{A}P_{t}f(x)
. Also denote
a_{n}=\sqrt{\sum_{x\in K_{n}^{c}}\pi(x)}
, and note that
a_{n}\to 0
, as
n\to\infty
. Then, because
|P_{t}f(x)|\leq\|f\|_{\infty}
and
\mathcal{A}P_{t}f=P_{t}\mathcal{A}f
(see
[
26
, Proposition 1.5]
), we have

\displaystyle|

\displaystyle f(t)-f_{n}(t)|\leq\sum_{x\in K_{n}^{c}}\pi(x)|P_{t}f(x)|\,|\mathcal{A}P_{t}f(x)|

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\sum_{x\in K_{n}^{c}}\pi(x)|P_{t}f(x)|^{2}}\sqrt{\sum_{x\in K_{n}^{c}}\pi(x)|P_{t}\mathcal{A}f(x)|^{2}}

(Cauchy-Schwarz)

\displaystyle\leq\|f\|_{\infty}\sqrt{\sum_{x\in K_{n}^{c}}\pi(x)}\sqrt{\sum_{x\in\mathbb{S}}\pi(x)|P_{t}\mathcal{A}f(x)|^{2}}

(
|P_{t}f(x)|\leq\|f\|_{\infty}
and converted
K_{n}^{c}
to
\mathbb{S}
)

\displaystyle\leq\left(\|f\|_{\infty}\|\mathcal{A}f\|_{L^{2}(\pi)}\right)\cdot a_{n},

where in the final inequality we used that
P_{t}
is a contraction on
L^{2}(\pi)
. Hence, the convergence is uniform in
t\geq 0
.
∎

Appendix D
Deterministic model and complex-balanced systems

Given a reaction network
(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{C},\mathcal{R})
, a
(deterministic) kinetics
is an assignment of a function
\lambda_{y\to y^{\prime}}:\mathbb{R}^{d}_{\geq 0}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
to each reaction
y\to y^{\prime}\in\mathcal{R}
. We then call
(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{C},\mathcal{R},\{\lambda_{y\to y^{\prime}}\})
a
deterministic reaction system
, and the associated ordinary differential equation governing the dynamics of the model is

\displaystyle x(t)=x(0)+\sum_{y\to y^{\prime}\in\mathcal{R}}(y^{\prime}-y)\int_{0}^{t}\lambda_{y\to y^{\prime}}(x(s))ds,

(76)

where we recall that
y,y^{\prime}
are vectors in
\mathbb{Z}^{d}_{\geq 0}
. Note that
y^{\prime}-y
is the vector describing the net gain of each species due to one instance of the reaction
y\to y^{\prime}
.

A popular choice of kinetics is given by
(deterministic) mass-action kinetics
, where the form of the kinetics is given by

\lambda_{y\to y^{\prime}}^{D}(x)=\kappa_{y\to y^{\prime}}\prod_{i=1}^{d}x_{i}^{y_{i}}:=\kappa_{y\to y^{\prime}}x^{y},

for some positive constant
\kappa_{y\to y^{\prime}}
, called a
reaction constant
, where we take
0^{0}=1
and where we define the notation
x^{y}
above. We denote
\mathcal{K}=\{\kappa_{y\to y^{\prime}}\}
and in the case of a deterministic mass-action system we write the system as
(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{C},\mathcal{R},\mathcal{K})
as opposed to
(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{C},\mathcal{R},\{\lambda_{y\to y^{\prime}}\})
. In the reaction graphs, the rate constants are typically placed next to the reaction arrow as in
y\xrightarrow{\kappa_{y\to y^{\prime}}}y^{\prime}
when we need to incorporate them. See Figure
1
for an example of this methodology.

A fixed-point of (
76
) is said to be a
complex-balanced equilibrium
if, at equilibrium, each complex has the following property: the total flux into the complex (according to the reaction graph) is equal to the total flux out of the complex (according to the reaction graph).

Definition D.1
.

A deterministic mass-action system
(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{C},\mathcal{R},\mathcal{K})
is
complex-balanced
if the system (
76
)
admits a positive vector
c\in\mathbb{R}^{d}_{>0}
such that for each complex
y\in\mathcal{C}
,

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}y^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}\\
y\to y^{\prime}\in\mathcal{R}\end{subarray}}\kappa_{y\to y^{\prime}}c^{y}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}y^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}\\
y^{\prime}\to y\in\mathcal{R}\end{subarray}}\kappa_{y^{\prime}\to y}c^{y^{\prime}}.

(77)

It is shown in
[
33
]
that a vector
c
satisfying (
77
) is necessarily a steady state of (
76
) and is called a
complex-balanced equilibrium
. In
[
27
,
33
,
32
]
it is further shown that if a complex-balanced equilibrium exists, then necessarily all positive steady states of the mass-action system are complex-balanced. Sufficient and necessary condition for complex-balancing are also given, and the stability of complex-balanced equilibria is studied.
