The massless modular Hamiltonian
Roberto Longo, Gerardo Morsella

Abstract
We compute the vacuum local modular Hamiltonian associated with a space ball region in the free scalar massless Quantum Field Theory. We give an explicit expression on the one particle Hilbert space in terms of the higher dimensional Legendre differential operator. The quadratic form of the massless modular Hamiltonian is expressed in terms of an integral of the energy density with the parabolic distribution. We then get the formula for the local entropy of a wave packet. This gives the vacuum relative entropy of a coherent state on the double cone von Neumann algebras associated with the free scalar QFT. Among other points, we provide the passivity characterisation of the modular Hamiltonian within the standard subspace setup.



1
Introduction

In this paper, we provide the formula for the vacuum modular Hamiltonian associated with the free massless scalar quantum field in a bounded spacetime region (double cone).

Background for the modular Hamiltonian.
Let
{\mathcal{M}}
be a von Neumann algebra and
\varphi
a faithful normal state on
{\mathcal{M}}
. As is well known, the Tomita-Takesaki modular theory provides us with a canonical one-parameter automorphism group of
{\mathcal{M}}
associated with
\varphi
, the modular group
\sigma^{\varphi}
. Thus, the quantum system
{\mathcal{M}}
is equipped with an intrinsic evolution
\sigma^{\varphi}
, that is characterised by the KMS thermal equilibrium condition
[
33
]
.
In the GNS representation, the modular group is implemented by a unitary one-parameter group
\Delta_{\varphi}^{is}
, whose generator
\log\Delta_{\varphi}
is the modular Hamiltonian associated with
\varphi
(see
[
25
]
for a discussion on the matter).

In Quantum Field Theory, for each spacetime region
O
, we have the von Neumann algebra
{\mathcal{A}}(O)
of observables localised in
O
. By the Reeh-Schlieder theorem, the vacuum vector is cyclic and separating for
{\mathcal{A}}(O)
if both
O
and its causal complement have non empty interiors, see
[
17
]
. Thus the restriction of vacuum state
\varphi
to
{\mathcal{A}}(O)
is faithful; the modular Hamiltonian associated with
{\mathcal{A}}(O)
and
\varphi
gives us a local Hamiltonian
\log\Delta_{O}
.

The problem of computing
\log\Delta_{O}
is then natural. Among other motivations, the modular Hamiltonian is related to Araki’s relative entropy
[
2
]
, that is recently playing an important role in Quantum Field Theory, also in relation with quantum entropy/energy inequalities; an account of this wide research topic goes beyond the purpose of this introduction (see
[
3
,
10
,
23
,
29
,
28
,
36
]
and refs. therein).

If
W
is a wedge region, there is an important model independent result
[
6
]
:
\Delta_{W}^{-is}
is identified with the
2\pi
-rescaled boost unitary transformations leaving
W
globally invariant, a result rich of consequences, see
[
17
]
.
For a different spacetime region
O
, the understanding of the local modular structure is definitely more problematic because, in general,
\Delta_{O}^{is}
has no geometric description due to the lack of enough spacetime symmetries. A basic issue concerns the case where
O
is a double cone, the causal envelop of a time-zero ball.
In the free massless QFT, the geometric description of the double cone vacuum modular group was derived in
[
18
]
. The general conformal case was later analysed solely in terms of the local von Neumann algebras
[
16
,
7
]
.
Among other results, we mention here the forward light cone case in the free massless QFT
[
8
]
and the approximate local estimates in
[
15
]
.

The modular theory has a version for standard subspaces, see
[
20
,
32
,
21
]
. Let
{\mathcal{H}}
be a complex Hilbert space and
H\subset{\mathcal{H}}
a standard subspace, i.e.
H
is a real linear, closed subspace of
{\mathcal{H}}
such that
H\cap iH^{\prime}=\{0\}
and
{\overline{H+iH}}={\mathcal{H}}
, with
H^{\prime}
the symplectic complement of
H
. The modular operator
\Delta_{H}
associated with
H
is a canonical positive, non-singular selfadjoint operator on
{\mathcal{H}}
associated with
H
that satisfies

\Delta^{is}_{H}H=H\,,\quad s\in\mathbb{R}\,.

In a free scalar QFT, the vacuum modular (unitary) group associated with the von Neumann algebra of a region
O
is the second quantisation of a unitary one-parameter group in the one-particle Hilbert space, indeed of the modular group of the local standard subspace
H(O)
. We shall henceforth denote by
\Delta_{O}
the modular operator
\Delta_{H(O)}
on the one particle Hilbert space. Also, we set
H(B)=H(O)
,
\Delta_{B}=\Delta_{O}
if
O
is the double cone with basis the unit space ball centred at origin
B
. Hence, in this paper

\text{Modular Hamiltonian of $B$}=\log\Delta_{B}\,.

In the massless case,
\Delta_{B}^{is}
is associated with a one parameter group of conformal transformations that globally preserve
O

[
18
]
. Nonetheless, the description of
\log\Delta_{B}
has not been worked out so far. We shall assume that the spatial dimention
d
is greater than one. The case
d=1
is essentially the same on a natural subspace of test functions, see
[
26
, Thm. 5.9]
.

In terms of the wave Cauchy data, we shall see that the local massless modular Hamiltonian is given by

\log\Delta_{B}=-2\pi i\left[\begin{matrix}0&\frac{1}{2}(1-r^{2})\\
\frac{1}{2}(1-r^{2})\nabla^{2}-r\partial_{r}-D&0\end{matrix}\right]\,,

(1)

with
D=(d-1)/2
the scaling dimension of the free scalar field. Namely

\log\Delta_{B}=-2\pi i\left[\begin{matrix}0&M\\
L&0\end{matrix}\right]\,,

(2)

with

\displaystyle M=\ \text{Multiplication operator by}\ \,\frac{1}{2}(1-r^{2})\,,

(3)

\displaystyle L=\ \text{Legendre operator}\ \,\frac{1}{2}(1-r^{2})\nabla^{2}-r\partial_{r}-D\,.

(4)

As we shall see,
the right-hand side of (
1
) gives indeed an essentially selfadjoint operator on the one particle Hilbert space on the smooth, compactly supported function domain.

We shall derive the following formula for the massless modular Hamiltonian of
B
in terms of the classical stress-energy tensor
T
:

-(\Phi,\log\Delta_{B}\Phi)=2\pi\int_{x_{0}=0}\frac{1-r^{2}}{2}\langle T_{00}\rangle_{\Phi}(x)dx+\pi D\int_{x_{0}=0}\Phi^{2}dx\,,

(5)

with
\Phi
a real wave with smooth, compactly supported Cauchy data. Here
T_{00}
is energy density

T_{00}=\frac{1}{2}\big((\partial_{0}\Phi)^{2}+|\nabla_{{\bf x}}\Phi|^{2}\big)\ .

(6)

The right-hand side of (
5
) is similar to a formula for the modular Hamiltonian sketched by Casini, Huerta and Myers
[
9
, (2.23)]
in terms of the QFT stress-energy tensor, when the Cauchy data are supported in
B
.

The measure of information.

One main consequence of our analysis is a formula for the entropy density carried by a wave packet. Let’s explain the framework.

Suppose
\Phi
is a scalar wave packet, a solution of the wave equation
\square\Phi=0
. At a given time, we can measure the signal contained say in space ball
B
. The quantity
S_{\Phi}
that represents the mean information stored by
\Phi
in
B
at that time is called the entropy of
\Phi
with respect to
B
and has been introduced in
[
23
,
24
,
11
]
, although different space regions (wedges) were there considered.

With
{\mathcal{H}}
a complex Hilbert space and
H\subset{\mathcal{H}}
a factorial standard subspace, one defines the entropy of a vector
k\in{\mathcal{H}}
with respect to
H
as

S_{k}=\Im(k,P_{H}i\log\Delta_{H}\,k)\ .

(7)

Here,
P_{H}:H+H^{\prime}\to H
,
P_{H}:h+h^{\prime}\mapsto h
, is the cutting projection associated with
H
,
\Delta_{H}
is the modular operator associated with
H
and
H^{\prime}
is the symplectic complement of
H
.

Motivated by Quantum Field Theory, one equips the waves’ real linear space with a complex Hilbert space structure, where the imaginary part of the scalar product is given by the time-independent symplectic form

\Im(\Phi,\Psi)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{x_{0}=t}\big(\Psi\partial_{0}\Phi-\Phi\partial_{0}\Psi\big)d{\bf x}\,.

Then one considers the local net of standard subspaces associated with the resulting
{\mathcal{H}}
:
H(B)
is the closure of the real linear space of waves with Cauchy data supported in
B
. So one defines
S_{\Phi}
as the entropy of the vector
\Phi
with respect to
H(B)
. We have

S_{\Phi}=\pi\int_{B}\big(\Psi\Phi^{\prime}-\Phi\Psi^{\prime}\big)d{\bf x}\ ,

(8)

(time zero integral) where
\Psi=i\log\Delta_{B}\Phi
, the prime denotes the time derivative and
\Delta_{H(B)}=\Delta_{B}
.

One then has to compute (
8
).
In
[
24
,
11
]
, this computation has been worked out in the case of a wave for a half-space region (whose causal envelop is a wedge), a case motivated by the study of the Quantum Null Energy Condition inequality.

From the point of view of information theory, it is however natural to consider the case that
B
is a bounded region. Note that the computation in
[
24
,
11
]
relies on the explicit knowledge of the modular Hamiltonian
\log\Delta_{W}

[
6
]
.

Now,
P_{H(B)}
acts by cutting the Cauchy data
[
11
]
, so we have at our disposal all the ingredients to compute the local entropy
S_{\Phi}(R)
of the wave packet
\Phi
in the (causal envelop of) the radius
R
space ball
B_{R}(\bar{\bf x})
around the space point
\bar{\bf x}
, at time
t
. We shall see that
S_{\Phi}(R)

is the sum of two terms:

\displaystyle S_{\Phi}(R)

\displaystyle=\pi\int_{B_{R}(\bar{\bf x})}\frac{R^{2}-r^{2}}{R}\langle T_{00}(t,{\bf x})\rangle_{\Phi}d{\bf x}\qquad\qquad\text{stress-energy tensor term}

\displaystyle+\pi\frac{d-1}{2R}\int_{B_{R}(\bar{\bf x})}\Phi^{2}(t,{\bf x})d{\bf x}\quad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\text{Born type term}

with
r=|{\bf x}-\bar{\bf x}|
and
T_{00}
the energy density of
\Phi
(
6
).

We note here the appearance of the parabolic distribution
\frac{1}{2}(1-r^{2})
in the stress-energy term of
the formula for the modular Hamiltonian. We wonder about possible deep roots for this, somehow similarly to the appearance of the Wigner semicircular distribution
[
35
]
in the Free Probability framework
[
34
]
. The parabolic distribution in three-dimensional space
is a higher-dimensional generalisation of the Wigner semicircular distribution and is related to the marginal distribution of a spherical distribution.

Content of this paper.

Our paper is organised as follows. First, we collect functional analytic results. Among these, we have
a standard subspace version of the Pusz and Woronowicz
[
31
]
complete passivity characterisation, up to a proportionality constant, of the modular Hamiltonian. This reflects the second principle of thermodynamics. By our results, our local Hamiltonian is completely passive in our standard subspace sense.
Then we describe the massless modular group in the wave setup, so we get an explicit description of the massless Hamiltonian. We then provide our local entropy formula for a wave packet and discuss some of the implications in Quantum Field Theory.
Our general references for Operator Algebras and Quantum Field Theory are
[
13
,
17
,
33
]
.

Abstract preliminaries

2.1
Real linear invariant subspaces

We now characterise the closed real linear subspaces of a complex Hilbert space
{\mathcal{H}}
that are invariant for a one-parameter unitary group.
The following proposition is indeed more general than what we shall need.

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
{\mathcal{H}}
be a Hilbert space,
H\subset{\mathcal{H}}
a closed, real linear subspace and
A:D(A)\subset{\mathcal{H}}\to{\mathcal{H}}
a selfadjoint operator. With
V(s)=e^{isA}
,
s\in{\mathbb{R}}
,
K=iA
and
{\mathcal{C}}
(resp.
{\mathcal{B}}
) the real algebra of complex, continuous functions
g
on
\mathbb{R}
vanishing at
\infty
(resp. complex, bounded Borel functions on
{\mathbb{R}}
) such that
g(-t)=\bar{g}(t)
, the following are equivalent:

(i)

V(s)H=H\,,\quad s\in\mathbb{R}
,

(ii)

g(A)H\subset H
,
g\in{\mathcal{C}}
,

(iii)

g(A)H\subset H
,
g\in{\mathcal{B}}
,

(iv)

(K^{2}-1)^{-1}H\subset H
and
K(K^{2}-1)^{-1}H\subset H
,

(v)

(K\pm 1)^{-1}H\subset H
,

(vi)

K|_{H}
is skew-selfadjoint on
H
, namely

D(K)\cap H
is dense in
H
,
K(D(K)\cap H)\subset H
and
K:(D(K)\cap H)\subset H\to H

is skew-selfadjoint.

(i)\Rightarrow(v)
:
Let
h
be a real
L^{1}
-function on
{\mathbb{R}}
and
g=\hat{h}
its Fourier transform. Then

g(A)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}h(s)V(s)ds\,.

If
(i)
holds true, then
g(A)H\subset H
for such
g
. In particular
(v)
holds, similarly as in (
9
).

(v)\Rightarrow(iv)
follows by the identities

2K(K^{2}-1)^{-1}=(K+1)^{-1}+(K-1)^{-1}\,,\quad 2(K^{2}-1)^{-1}=(K+1)^{-1}-(K-1)^{-1}\,.

(iv)\Rightarrow(ii)
:
The complexification
{\mathcal{C}}_{\mathbb{C}}
of
{\mathcal{C}}
is the
C^{*}
-algebra
C
of continuous functions on
\mathbb{R}
vanishing at infinity, namely every
F\in C
is written uniquely as

F=f+ig\,,\quad f,g\in{\mathcal{C}}\ ,\qquad f(t)=\frac{1}{2}[F(t)+\bar{F}(-t)],\ g(t)=\frac{1}{2i}[F(t)-\bar{F}(-t)].

Note that

{\mathcal{C}}
contains the real algebra
{\mathcal{C}}_{0}
of functions of the form
f(t)=p((t^{2}+1)^{-1},it(t^{2}+1)^{-1})
with
p
a two-variables polynomial with real coefficients and zero constant coefficient.
Note that
{\mathcal{C}}
and
{\mathcal{C}}_{0}
are closed with respect to complex conjugation.

Since
{\mathcal{C}}_{0}
separates the points of
{\mathbb{R}}
, given any
f\in{\mathcal{C}}
, there exists a sequence
F_{n}=f_{n}+ig_{n}
with
f_{n},g_{n}\in{\mathcal{C}}_{0}
such that
F_{n}\to f
uniformly on
{\mathbb{R}}
, thus
f_{n}\to f
uniformly on
{\mathbb{R}}
.
We conclude that
{\mathcal{C}}_{0}
is norm dense in
{\mathcal{C}}
.
Therefore we have
(ii)
.

(ii)\Rightarrow(iii)
: Let
g\in{\mathcal{B}}
. Given
h\in H,\,k\in H^{\perp}
(real orthogonal), by Lusin’s theorem there exists a bounded sequence of continuous functions
g_{n}

such that
g_{n}\to g
almost everywhere w.r.t. the spectral measure of
A
associated with
h,k
. By replacing
g_{n}(t)
with
g_{n}(t)+\bar{g}_{n}(-t)
, we may assume that
g_{n}\in{\mathcal{C}}
.
By Lebesgue’s dominated convergence theorem, we have
(k,g_{n}(A)h)\to(k,g(A)h)
. As
\Re(k,g_{n}(A)h)=0
, we have
\Re(k,g(A)h)=0
, that implies
g(A)H\subset H
because
h,k
can be arbitrarily chosen.

(vi)\Rightarrow(v)
:
K|_{H}
generates a one-parameter group
V_{H}
of orthogonal operators on
H
, therefore

(1\pm K)^{-1}H=-\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-s}V_{H}(\mp s)ds\,H\subset H\,.

(9)

Clearly,
(i)\Rightarrow(vi)
,
(iii)\Rightarrow(ii)
and
(iii)\Rightarrow(i)
.

\square

, with

In this section, and in the following one, we assume

to be

, i.e.

Associated with

\displaystyle E_{H}:H+H^{\perp}\to H\ ,\quad h+h^{\perp}\mapsto h\ ,

Note that

[

\displaystyle E_{H}=(1+\Delta_{H})^{-1}+J_{H}\Delta_{H}^{1/2}(1+\Delta_{H})^{-1}\ ,

(10)

\displaystyle P_{H}=(1-\Delta_{H})^{-1}+J_{H}\Delta_{H}^{1/2}(1-\Delta_{H})^{-1}\ ,

(11)

\displaystyle Q_{H}=\frac{1}{2}(1+S_{H})\ ;

(12)

more precisely, (

is the closure of the operator
a(\Delta_{H})+b(\Delta_{H})J_{H}
on
D(a(\Delta_{H}))\cap D(b(\Delta_{H}))

a(\lambda)=(1-\lambda)^{-1}\ ,\quad b(\lambda)=\lambda^{1/2}(1-\lambda)^{-1}\ ,\ \lambda\in(0,+\infty)\smallsetminus\{1\}\,.

Formulas (
10
) and (
11
) were obtained respectively in
[
14
]
(see also
[
30
]
) and in
[
11
]
; formula (

E_{H}=2Q_{H}(1+\Delta_{H})^{-1}\ ,\quad P_{H}=2Q_{H}(1-\Delta_{H})^{-1}\ ,

hence

P_{H}=E_{H}(1+\Delta_{H})(1-\Delta_{H})^{-1}=E_{H}\coth\Big(\frac{1}{2}\log\Delta_{H}\Big)

(13)

(denoting by the same symbol an operator and its closure).

Proposition 2.2
.

P_{H}i
restricts to a skew-selfadjoint operator on

P_{H}i|_{H}=i\coth\Big(\frac{1}{2}\log\Delta_{H}\Big)\Big|_{H}\,.

2.3
Entropy and quadratic forms

Recall the
entropy of a vector

k
in a Hilbert space
{\mathcal{H}}
with respect to a factorial standard subspace
H
is defined by

S_{k}=\Im(k,P_{H}i\log\Delta_{H}\,k)=\Re(k,P^{*}_{H}\log\Delta_{H}\,k)\,,

(17)

where
P_{H}
is the cutting projection
P_{H}:H+H^{\prime}\to H
. Formula (

) is to be understood in the sense of quadratic forms; indeed, we now define the quadratic form
S_{k}=S(k,k)
.

Let
{\mathcal{D}}=D(\sqrt{|\log\Delta_{H}|}\,F)
, with
F
the spectral projection of
\Delta_{H}
relative to the interval
(0,1)
. Then
{\mathcal{D}}

With

S(h,k)=\Re(h,P^{*}_{H}\log\Delta_{H}\,k)=\Im(h,P_{H}i\log\Delta_{H}k)\,.

(18)

More precisely, following the discussion il
[
11
]
, let

be the spectral family of

,
the right-hand side of (

\Im(h,P_{H}i\log\Delta_{H}k)=\int_{0}^{+\infty}a(\lambda)\log\lambda\,d(h,E(\lambda)k)-\int_{0}^{+\infty}b(\lambda)\log\lambda\,d(h,J_{H}E(\lambda)k)\ .

Note that

.

is essentially selfadjoint on

, namely

,
so it suffices to show that the closure

of

With

, let

be the spectral projection of

relative to the set

. Then

; moreover

We conclude that

\square

2.4
Standard subspaces and passivity

We provide here a standard subspace version of Pusz-Woronowicz’s complete passivity characterisation of the modular Hamiltonian in the
C^{*}
-algebraic setting
[
31
]
.

Let
{\mathcal{H}}
be a complex Hilbert space and
H
standard subspace.
In this section,
A
is a selfadjoint linear operator on
{\mathcal{H}}
such that
e^{isA}H=H
,
s\in\mathbb{R}
, namely
H
is
iA
-invariant as in Prop.
2.1
.

We shall say that
A
is
active/passive
with respect to
H
if

\pm(\xi,A\xi)\geq 0\,,\quad\xi\in D(A)\cap H\,.

A
is
n
-active/passive
w.r.t.
H
if the generator of
e^{itA}\otimes e^{itA}\cdots\otimes e^{itA}
is active/passive with respect to the
n
-fold tensor product
H\otimes H\otimes\cdots\otimes H
, (closed real linear span of monomials
h_{1}\otimes h_{2}\otimes\cdots\otimes h_{n}
,
h_{i}\in H
,
cf.
[
27
]
).

A
is
completely active/passive
if
A
is
n
-active/passive for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

Note that
A
is active/passive iff

\pm(\xi,A\xi)\geq 0\,,\quad\xi\in{\cal D}\,,

with
{\cal D}\subset D(A)\cap H
a real linear space such that the closure of
{\cal D}
in the graph topology of
A
is equal to
D(A)\cap H
.

Since
e^{itA}
leaves
H
globally invariant,
e^{itA}
commutes with
\Delta_{H}^{is}
and
J_{H}
. We have

J_{H}\Delta_{H}J_{H}=\Delta_{H}^{-1}\,\quad J_{H}AJ_{H}=-A\,.

(20)

Proposition 2.5
.

\log\Delta_{H}
is completely passive w.r.t.
H
.

\log\Delta_{H}
is passive w.r.t.
H
, see
[
24
]
. Hence it is completely passive because the modular unitary group of

H\otimes H\cdots\otimes H
is
\Delta_{H}^{is}\otimes\Delta_{H}^{is}\cdots\otimes\Delta_{H}^{is}
, see
[
27
]
.

\square

Let
{\mathcal{D}}_{\rm an}\subset{\mathcal{H}}
be the subspace of vectors with bounded spectrum with respect to both
A
and
\log\Delta
.

Lemma 2.6
.

If
A
is passive w.r.t.
H
, then
A\log\Delta_{H}
is a positive selfadjoint operator on
{\mathcal{H}}
.

Denote by
S_{H}
the Tomita operator of
{\mathcal{H}}
.
As
S_{H}
commutes with
e^{itA}
,
\Delta_{H}^{is}
and
J_{H}
, we have
S_{H}{\mathcal{D}}_{\rm an}={\mathcal{D}}_{\rm an}
.
Let
\xi\in{\mathcal{D}}_{\rm an}
, so
(1+S_{H})\xi\in H
. Then, by passivity,

0\geq((1+S_{H})\xi,A(1+S_{H})\xi)\ ,

(21)

thus

0\geq((1+S_{H})i\xi,A(1+S_{H})i\xi)=((1-S_{H})\xi,A(1-S_{H})\xi)\ .

(22)

Summing up (
21
) and (
22
) we get

0\geq(\xi,A\xi)+(S_{H}\xi,AS_{H}\xi)=(\xi,A\xi)+(J_{H}\Delta_{H}^{1/2}\xi,AJ_{H}\Delta_{H}^{1/2}\xi)=(\xi,A\xi)-(J_{H}\Delta_{H}^{1/2}\xi,J_{H}A\Delta_{H}^{1/2}\xi)\\
=(\xi,A\xi)-(A\Delta_{H}^{1/2}\xi,\Delta_{H}^{1/2}\xi)=(\xi,A(1-\Delta_{H})\xi)\,,

thus
A(1-\Delta_{H})\leq 0
because
{\mathcal{D}}_{\rm an}
is a core for
A(1-\Delta_{H})
.
But
A(1-\Delta_{H})\leq 0
is equivalent to
A\log\Delta_{H}\geq 0
.

\square

We shall say that a standard subspace is
abelian
if
\Delta_{H}=1
, see
[
21
]
.

Theorem 2.7
.

A
is completely active with respect to
H
iff
\log\Delta_{H}=\lambda A
for some
\lambda\leq 0
.

Assume that
A
is completely active with respect to
H
.
Let
\Lambda\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
be the joint spectrum of
A
and
\log\Delta_{H}
.
By Lemma
2.6
,
\Lambda
is contained in the region
Q=\{(a,b)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:\,ab\geq 0\}
. By (
20
) we have
-\Lambda=\Lambda
and by
complete passivity we have
\Lambda+\Lambda+\cdots+\Lambda\subset Q
(finite sum).

Let
C_{1},C_{2}
be two different points in
\Lambda
. Then
n_{1}C_{1}+n_{2}C_{2}\in Q
for all integers
n_{1},n_{2}\in{\mathbb{Z}}
.
So
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
must belong to a same straight line
Z
through the origin
(0,0)
. Thus
\Lambda\subset Z
.
If
Z
is vertical, then
\log\Delta_{H}=0
, namely
H
is abelian, so
\log\Delta_{H}=\lambda A
with
\lambda=0
.
If
Z
is not vertical then
A=\lambda\log\Delta_{H}
with
\lambda>0
.

For the converse, it remains to show that
A
is completely active if
\Delta_{H}=1
, namely if
H
is abelian. In this case, the scalar product of
{\mathcal{H}}
is real on
H
, and
iH
is the real orthogonal complement of
H
.
As
A
maps
H
into
iH
, we have
\Re(h,Ah)=0
for
h\in D(A)\cap H
, thus
(h,Ah)=0
because
A
is selfadjoint. So
A
is active, thus completely active by repeating this argument for
H\otimes H\cdots\otimes H
.

\square

3
Preliminaries on the waves’ space

Let
{\mathcal{S}}
denote the real linear space of smooth, compactly supported real functions on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. We shall always assume
d\geq 2
unless otherwise specified.

As is known, if
f,g\in{\mathcal{S}}
, there is a unique smooth real function
\Phi
on
\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
which is a solution
\Phi
of the wave equation

\square\Phi\equiv\partial^{2}\Phi/\partial x_{0}^{2}-\partial^{2}\Phi/\partial x_{1}^{2}\cdots-\partial^{2}\Phi/\partial x_{d}^{2}=0

(a wave packet or, briefly, a wave) with Cauchy data
\Phi|_{x_{0}=0}=f
,
\partial_{0}\Phi|_{x_{0}=0}=g
.
We set
\Phi=w(f,g)
and denote
by
{\mathcal{T}}
the real linear space of these
\Phi
’s; we will often use the identification

{\mathcal{S}}^{2}\longleftrightarrow{\mathcal{T}}\,,\qquad\langle f,g\rangle\longleftrightarrow w(f,g)\,.

(23)

The one particle Hilbert space is
{\mathcal{H}}=L^{2}\big({\mathfrak{H}}_{0},\delta(p^{2})\big)

with
\mathfrak{H}_{0}
the positive massless hyperboloid, namely
\mathfrak{H}_{0}
is the boundary of the forward light cone. We denote by
(\cdot,\cdot)
the scalar product of
{\mathcal{H}}
. The Fourier transform of a
\Phi\in{\mathcal{T}}
is a distribution of the form
\hat{\Phi}(p)=\delta(p^{2})F(p)
with a compactly supported smooth function
F:{\mathfrak{H}}_{0}\cup(-{\mathfrak{H}}_{0})\to{\mathbb{C}}
, so that

{\mathcal{T}}
real linearly embeds into
{\mathcal{H}}
by

\Phi\mapsto(2\pi)^{d/2}F|_{\mathfrak{H}_{0}}
. We may thus consider
{\mathcal{T}}
as a dense subset of
{\mathcal{H}}
.

Consider the symplectic form on
{\mathcal{T}}

\beta(\Phi,\Psi)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{x_{0}=0}\big(\Psi\partial_{0}\Phi-\Phi\partial_{0}\Psi)dx\,.

(24)

This is the imaginary part of the restriction of the scalar product of
{\mathcal{H}}
to
{\mathcal{T}}
:

\Im(\langle f_{1},g_{1}\rangle,\langle f_{2},g_{2}\rangle)=\beta(\langle f_{1},g_{1}\rangle,\langle f_{2},g_{2}\rangle)\ .

We denote by
H_{0}^{s}
the real Hilbert space of real-valued tempered distributions
f\in S^{\prime}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
such that

||f||_{s}^{2}=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}{|{\bf p}|^{2s}}|\hat{f}({\bf p})|^{2}d{\bf p}<+\infty\,,\quad s\in{\mathbb{R}}\,.

(25)

It is clear that
{\mathcal{S}}
is dense in
H^{\pm 1/2}
and that
\mu:H_{0}^{1/2}\to H^{-1/2}
with

\widehat{\mu f}({\bm{p}})={{|\bm{p}|}}\hat{f}({\bm{p}})\,.

(26)

is a unitary operator.

Then

\imath_{0}=\left[\begin{matrix}0&\mu^{-1}\\
-\mu&0\end{matrix}\right]\,,

(27)

namely
\imath_{0}\langle f,g\rangle=\langle\mu^{-1}g,-\mu f\rangle
,
is a unitary operator
\imath_{0}
on
H=H^{1/2}\oplus H^{-1/2}
.

As
\imath_{0}^{2}=-1
, the unitary
\imath_{0}
defines a complex structure (multiplication by the imaginary unit) on

H_{0}
that becomes a complex Hilbert space with scalar product

(\Phi,\Psi)=\beta(\Phi,\imath_{0}\Psi)+i\beta(\Phi,\Psi)\ .

In terms of the Cauchy data, the scalar product is given by

(\langle f,g\rangle,\langle h,k\rangle)=\frac{1}{2}\big((f,\mu h)+(g,\mu^{-1}k)+i[(h,g)-(f,k)]\big),\qquad\langle f,g\rangle,\langle h,k\rangle\in H^{1/2}\oplus H^{-1/2},

(where
(\cdot,\cdot)
is the standard
L^{2}
scalar product).
Furthermore the map

\langle f,g\rangle\in H\mapsto w(f,g)\in{\mathcal{H}}

is isometric and complex linear, so it extends to a unitary operator. We will then identify these spaces; namely, the one particle Hilbert space is

{\mathcal{H}}=\text{real Hilbert space}\ H\ \text{with complex structure given by}\ \imath_{0}\,.

Note that

H^{1/2}
and
H^{-1/2}
are naturally a dual pair under the bilinear form

\langle f,g\rangle\in H^{1/2}\times H^{-1/2}\mapsto\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\bar{\hat{f}}\hat{g}\ .

(28)

Let
Z
be an open, non-empty subset of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and denote by
Z^{\prime}
the interior of its complement.
We shall denote by
H(Z)
the closed, real linear subspace of
{\mathcal{H}}

H(Z)=\{\Phi\in{\mathcal{T}}(Z)\}^{-}\,,

where

{\mathcal{T}}(Z)=\{\Phi=w(f,g)\in{\mathcal{T}}:{\rm supp}(f),\,{\rm supp}(g)\subset Z\}\,.

If
Z
and
Z^{\prime}
are non-empty, then
H(Z)
is a standard subspace of
{\mathcal{H}}
, the one associated with
Z
. We shall be mainly interested in the case
Z
or
Z^{\prime}
is the unit ball
B
of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Clearly, setting

H^{\pm 1/2}(Z)=\text{closure of $C_{0}^{\infty}(Z)$ in $H^{\pm 1/2}$}\,,

we have

H(Z)=H^{1/2}(Z)\oplus H^{-1/2}(Z)\,.

Here and in the following,
C^{\infty}_{0}(Z)
denotes the space of real
C^{\infty}
function on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with compact support in
Z
.
By duality
[
1
]
, see also
[
12
,
20
]
, we have

H(B)^{\prime}=H(B^{\prime})\,.

(29)

4
Massless Hamiltonian

We now compute the formula for the massless Hamiltonian.

4.1
The modular group

In the following,
O
will denote the double cone on the Minkowski spacetime
\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
with base the open unit ball
B
centered at the origin in the time zero hyperplane
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. As above,
H(O)=H(B)
is the standard subspace in the massless, scalar one-particle Hilbert space
{\mathcal{H}}
. We assume
d\geq 2
.

The modular group
\Delta_{B}^{is}
associated with
H(B)
has been computed in
[
18
]
in terms of the action on the field or, equivalently, on the spacetime test functions.
\Delta_{B}^{is}
is associated with a one-parameter group of conformal transformation that preserves
O
, namely

(u,v)\mapsto\big((Z(u,s),Z(v,s)\big)\,,

(30)

where
Z
is given by

Z(z,s)=\frac{g(z,s)}{f(z,s)}

with

f(z,s)=\frac{(1+z)+e^{-s}(1-z)}{2}\ ,\quad g(z,s)=\frac{(1+z)-e^{-s}(1-z)}{2}\ .

Here we need to compute
\Delta_{B}^{is}
in terms of waves.
Let
\Phi
be a wave and set

(V(s)\Phi)(u,v)=\gamma(u,v;s)\Phi\big(Z(u,s),Z(v,s)\big)\,,

(31)

with

u=x_{0}+r,\quad v=x_{0}-r,\quad r=|{\bf x}|\equiv\sqrt{x_{1}^{2}+\cdots+x_{d}^{2}}

and we omit the remaining spherical coordinates as the action is trivial on them.

The cocycle
\gamma
given by

\gamma(u,v;s)=F(u,s)F(-v,-s),\quad F(z,s)\equiv f^{-D}(z,s)\ ,

with

D=\frac{d-1}{2}\,;

(32)

D
is the scaling dimension.
We have:

Theorem 4.1
.

The modular group of
H(B)
is given by

\Delta_{B}^{-is}=V(2\pi s)\,.

That
\Delta_{B}^{-is/2\pi}
is associated with the flow
Z
(
30
) and the cocycle
\gamma
follows rather directly from the test function formula
[
18
]
, but for the determination of the value of the constant
D
. Now, as in
[
18
]
,
H(B)
is equivalent to
H(W)
, with
W
a wedge region, by a unitary operator that is obtained by composing unitaries associated with translations and ray inversion map. The cocycle is associated with the Jacobian of the ray inversion map. Thus, by computation similar to those in
[
18
]
, the cocycle is of the form
f^{-D}
for some constant
D>0
.

However, it will follow from Lemma
4.8
that
D=(d-1)/2
is the only value compatible with the complex linearity of the generator of
V
.
Of course,
D
can be determined by direct calculations too.

\square

4.2
The generator of the modular group

We now compute the
K=\frac{d}{ds}V(s)\big|_{s=0}
, the generator of
V
in Theorem
4.1
, that is proportional to the modular Hamiltonian.

With the above notations, denoting by a prime the derivative with respect to
s
-parameter, we have (Identity
6.2
)

Z(z,0)=z\,,\qquad Z^{\prime}(z,0)=(1-z^{2})/2

and

\gamma(u,v;0)=1\,,\qquad\gamma^{\prime}(u,v;0)=-\frac{D}{2}(u+v)=-D\,x_{0}\ .

Therefore

(V(s)\Phi)(u,v)^{\prime}=\gamma^{\prime}(u,v;s)\Phi\big(Z(u,s),Z(v,s)\big)\\
+\gamma(u,v;s)\Big(\partial_{u}\Phi\big(Z(u,s),Z(v,s)\big)Z^{\prime}(u,s)+\partial_{v}\Phi\big(Z(u,s),Z(v,s)\big)Z^{\prime}(v,s)\Big)\,.

We then have:

Proposition 4.2
.

We have

(K\Phi)(u,v)=-\frac{D}{2}(u+v)\Phi-\frac{1}{2}u^{2}\partial_{u}\Phi-\frac{1}{2}v^{2}\partial_{v}\Phi+\frac{1}{2}\partial_{u}\Phi+\frac{1}{2}\partial_{v}\Phi\ .

In terms of the
x_{0},r
coordinates

(K\Phi)(x_{0},r)=\frac{1}{2}\big(1-(x^{2}_{0}+r^{2})\big)\partial_{0}\Phi-x_{0}r\partial_{r}\Phi-D\,x_{0}\Phi\ .

(33)

We compute:

\displaystyle(K\Phi)(u,v)

\displaystyle=(V(s)\Phi)(u,v)^{\prime}\big|_{s=0}

\displaystyle=\gamma^{\prime}(u,v;0)\Phi(u,v)+\partial_{u}\Phi(u,v)Z^{\prime}(u,0)+\partial_{v}\Phi(u,v)Z^{\prime}(v,0)

\displaystyle=-\frac{D}{2}(u+v)\Phi(u,v)+\frac{1}{2}\partial_{u}\Phi(u,v)(1-u^{2})+\frac{1}{2}\partial_{v}\Phi(u,v)(1-v^{2})

\displaystyle=-\frac{D}{2}(u+v)\Phi-\frac{1}{2}u^{2}\partial_{u}\Phi-\frac{1}{2}v^{2}\partial_{v}\Phi+\frac{1}{2}\partial_{u}\Phi+\frac{1}{2}\partial_{v}\Phi\ .

Now

\partial_{u}=\frac{1}{2}(\partial_{0}+\partial_{r}),\quad\partial_{v}=\frac{1}{2}(\partial_{0}-\partial_{r})\,,

so

(K\Phi)(x_{0},r)=-\frac{1}{4}\big((x_{0}+r)^{2}(\partial_{0}+\partial_{r})\Phi+(x_{0}-r)^{2}(\partial_{0}-\partial_{r})\big)\Phi-{D}x_{0}\Phi+\frac{1}{2}\partial_{0}\Phi\\
=-\frac{1}{2}(x^{2}_{0}+r^{2})\partial_{0}\Phi-x_{0}r\partial_{r}\Phi-{D}x_{0}\Phi+\frac{1}{2}\partial_{0}\Phi\\
=\frac{1}{2}\big(1-(x^{2}_{0}+r^{2})\big)\partial_{0}\Phi-x_{0}r\partial_{r}\Phi-D\,x_{0}\Phi\ .

(34)

\square

Corollary 4.3
.

We have

\displaystyle(K\Phi)|_{x_{0}=0}=\frac{1}{2}(1-r^{2})\partial_{0}\Phi|_{x_{0}=0}\,,

(35)

\displaystyle(\partial_{0}K\Phi)|_{x_{0}=0}=\frac{1}{2}(1-r^{2})\nabla^{2}\Phi-r\partial_{r}\Phi-D\Phi|_{x_{0}=0}\,.

(36)

The first equality follows immediately from (
33
).
Again from (
33
) we have

\partial_{0}K\Phi|_{x_{0}=0}=\frac{1}{2}(1-r^{2})\partial^{2}_{0}\Phi-r\partial_{r}\Phi-D\Phi|_{x_{0}=0}

thus (
36
) holds.

\square

The above corollary translates into the following:

Proposition 4.4
.

We have

K:w(f,g)\mapsto w\Big(\frac{1}{2}(1-r^{2})g,\frac{1}{2}(1-r^{2})\nabla^{2}f-r\partial_{r}f-D\,f\Big)\,.

In other words,
K
is the operator on
{\mathcal{S}}^{2}
given by the
2\times 2
matrix

K=\left[\begin{matrix}0&\frac{1}{2}(1-r^{2})\\
\frac{1}{2}(1-r^{2})\nabla^{2}-r\partial_{r}-D&0\end{matrix}\right]\,.

(37)

Now the symplectic form
\beta
on
{\mathcal{T}}
(
24
) is given, in terms of Cauchy data, as

\beta(\Phi,\Psi)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\big(f_{2}g_{1}-f_{1}g_{2}\big)d{\bf x}\ .

(38)

with
\Phi=w(f_{1},g_{1})
,
\Psi=w(f_{2},g_{2})
.

Thus

2\beta(\Phi,K\Phi)=\int_{x_{0}=0}(K\Phi)\partial_{0}\Phi-\Phi\partial_{0}(K\Phi)dx\\
=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{1}{2}(1-r^{2})g^{2}d{\bf x}-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{1}{2}(1-r^{2})f\nabla^{2}fd{\bf x}+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}rf\partial_{r}fd{\bf x}+D\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f^{2}d{\bf x}\ .

(39)

So, taking into account Identity (
6.1
), we have

2\beta(\Phi,K\Phi)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(1-r^{2})\big(g^{2}+|\nabla f|^{2}\big)d{\bf x}+D\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f^{2}d{\bf x}\ ,

namely

\beta(\Phi,K\Phi)=\frac{1}{4}\int_{x_{0}=0}(1-r^{2})\big((\partial_{0}\Phi)^{2}+|\nabla\Phi|^{2}\big)dx+\frac{D}{2}\int_{x_{0}=0}\Phi^{2}dx\,.

(40)

So we have obtained the following proposition.

Proposition 4.5
.

With
A=-\imath_{0}K
, we have

(\Phi,A\Phi)=\beta(\Phi,K\Phi)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{x_{0}=0}(1-r^{2})\langle T_{00}\rangle_{\Phi}dx+\frac{D}{2}\int_{x_{0}=0}\Phi^{2}dx

(41)

with
\langle T_{00}\rangle_{\Phi}=\frac{1}{2}\big((\partial_{0}\Phi)^{2}+|\nabla\Phi|^{2}\big)
the energy density given by
classical stress-energy tensor
T
.

Since
A
is selfadjoint,
(\Phi,A\Phi)
is real, so

(\Phi,A\Phi)=\Im i(\Phi,A\Phi)=\Im(\Phi,\imath_{0}A\Phi)=\Im(\Phi,K\Phi)=\beta(\Phi,K\Phi)\,,

so the proposition follows from (
40
).

\square

Since

\beta(K\Phi,\Psi)+\beta(\Phi,K\Psi)=0\,,

by the polarisation identity, we then get

\beta(\Phi,K\Psi)=\frac{1}{2}\Big(\beta\big(\Phi+\Psi,K(\Phi+\Psi)\big)-\beta\big(\Phi-\Psi,K(\Phi-\Psi)\big)\Big)\\
=\frac{1}{2}\int_{x_{0}=0}(1-r^{2})\langle T_{00}\rangle_{\Phi,\Psi}dx+\frac{D}{2}\int_{x_{0}=0}\Phi\Psi dx\,,

(42)

with

\langle T_{00}\rangle_{\Phi,\Psi}=\frac{1}{2}\big(\partial_{0}\Phi\partial_{0}\Psi+\nabla_{{\bf x}}\Phi\nabla_{{\bf x}}\Psi\big)\ .

(43)

(\square\Phi_{\lambda})(x)=\lambda^{2}(\square\Phi)(\lambda x)=0\,,

thus
\Phi_{\lambda}\in{\mathcal{T}}
and we have a real linear map bijection
\delta_{\lambda}:{\mathcal{T}}\to{\mathcal{T}}

\delta_{\lambda}:\Phi\in{\mathcal{T}}\to\lambda^{D}\Phi_{\lambda}\in{\mathcal{T}}\ .

(44)

In terms of the Cauchy data, we have
\delta_{\lambda}:w(f,g)\to w(\lambda^{D}f_{\lambda},\lambda^{D+1}g_{\lambda})
.

If
\Phi,\Psi\in{\mathcal{T}}(B)

\beta(\delta_{\lambda}\Phi,\delta_{\lambda}\Psi)=\frac{\lambda^{d}}{2}\int_{x_{0}=0}\Big(\Psi(\lambda x)\big(\partial_{0}\Phi\big)(\lambda x)-\Phi(\lambda x)\big(\partial_{0}\Psi\big)(\lambda x)\Big)dx=\beta(\Phi,\Psi)\ ,

namely
\delta_{\lambda}
preserves the symplectic form
\beta
. One can see that
\delta_{\lambda}
commutes with the complex structure on
\imath_{0}
, so
\delta_{\lambda}
is a unitary

\delta_{\lambda}:{\mathcal{H}}\to{\mathcal{H}}\,.

Let
B_{R}
the ball in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with center at the origin and radius
R>0
. With
H(B_{R})
the standard subspace of
{\mathcal{H}}
associated with
B_{R}
, we have

\delta_{\lambda}:H(B_{R})\to H(B_{\lambda^{-1}R})\,.

With
\Delta_{R}
the modular operator of
H(B_{R})
, we then have
\Delta_{{\lambda^{-1}R}}=\delta_{\lambda}\Delta_{R}\delta_{\lambda^{-1}}
, so

\log\Delta_{R}=\delta_{R}\log\Delta_{1}\delta_{R^{-1}}\,.

(45)

The above discussion yields the following formula for the matrix elements of the modular Hamiltonian.

Theorem 4.12
.

With
\log\Delta_{R}
the massless modular Hamiltonian for the radius
R
ball
B_{R}
, we have

-\Re(\Phi,\log\Delta_{R}\Psi)=2\pi\int_{B_{R}}\frac{R^{2}-r^{2}}{2R}\langle T_{00}\rangle_{\Phi,\Psi}d{\bf x}+2\pi\frac{d-1}{R}\int_{B_{R}}\Phi\Psi d{\bf x}\,,

where
\Phi,\Psi\in{\mathcal{T}}(B_{R})
.

Immediate by the above discussion.

\square

5
Entropy density of a wave packet

With
{\mathcal{H}}
our wave Hilbert space, the following proposition gives an explicit formula for the action of the cutting projection
P_{B}
on
{\mathcal{H}}
relative to
H(B)
, cf.
[
14
]
.

Proposition 5.1
.

P_{B}
is given by the matrix

P_{B}=\left[\begin{matrix}P_{+}&0\\
0&P_{-}\end{matrix}\right]\,,

(46)

with
P_{\pm}:D(P_{\pm})\subset H^{\pm 1/2}\to H^{\pm 1/2}
the operator of multiplication by the characteristic function
\chi_{B}
of
B
in
H^{\pm 1/2}
.

By duality (
29
),
H(B)^{\prime}=H(B^{\prime})
, therefore
D(P_{B})=H(B)+H(B^{\prime})
.

By Prop.
2.2
,
P_{B}\imath_{0}|_{H(B)}
is a real linear, densely defined operator on
H_{0}(B)
.
Clearly
P_{B}
acts as in (
46
) on vectors in
H(B)+H(B^{\prime})
given by smooth functions, hence on all
D(P_{B})
because
P_{B}
is a closed operator
[
11
]
.

\square

Proposition 5.2
.

Let
\Phi\in{\mathcal{T}}
and set
\Psi=K\Phi
. Then
\Psi=w(f,g)\in H(B)+H(B^{\prime})
and

P_{B}\Psi=w(\chi_{B}f,\chi_{B}g)
, with
\chi_{B}
the characteristic function of
B
.

As
\Phi
belongs to
D(\log\Delta_{B})=D(K)
, it follows that
K\Phi\in D(P_{B})
, thus
P_{B}\Psi=w(\chi_{B}f,\chi_{B}g)
by Prop.
5.1
.

\square

We can now compute the local entropy of a wave packet
\Phi
.

Proposition 5.3
.

Let
\Phi\in{\mathcal{T}}
. The entropy
S_{\Phi}
of
\Phi
with respect to
H_{0}(B)
is given by

S_{\Phi}=\pi\int_{B}\big(\Psi\Phi^{\prime}-\Phi\Psi^{\prime}\big)d{\bf x}\ ,

(47)

with
\Psi=K\Phi
and the prime denotes the time derivative.

The proof now follows from Proposition
5.2
in analogy with the proof given in
[
11
]
for the wedge region case.

\square

Theorem 5.4
.

The entropy
S_{\Phi}
of the wave
\Phi\in{\mathcal{T}}
in the unit ball
B
, i.e. with respect to
H(B)
, is given by

S_{\Phi}=2\pi\int_{B}\frac{1-r^{2}}{2}\langle T_{00}\rangle_{\Phi}\,d{\bf x}+\pi D\int_{B}\Phi^{2}d{\bf x}\,.

(48)

In particular,
S_{\Phi}
is finite for all
\Phi\in{\mathcal{T}}
.

With
\Phi,\Psi\in{\mathcal{T}}
, define the real linear quadratic form

q_{B}(\Phi,\Psi)=2\pi\int_{B}\frac{1-r^{2}}{2}\langle T_{00}\rangle_{\Phi,\Psi}\,d{\bf x}+\pi D\int_{B}\Phi\Psi d{\bf x}\,.

Clearly,
q_{B}(\Phi,\Phi)<\infty
if
\Phi\in{\mathcal{T}}
. We want to show that

S(\Phi,\Phi)=q_{B}(\Phi,\Phi)\,,\quad\Phi\in{\mathcal{T}}\,,

(49)

where
S
is the entropy form with respect to
H(B)
.
As
S
is a closed by Prop.
2.4
, it suffices to show that
q_{B}
is closable on
{\mathcal{T}}
and that (
49
) holds on a form core for
S
.

Now,
q_{B}
is given by

q_{B}(\Phi,\Psi)=\Re(\Phi,P^{*}_{B}AP_{B}\Psi)\,,\quad\Phi,\Psi\in{\mathcal{T}}\,,

similarly as in
[
11
, Thm. 3.5]
. The real linear operator
P^{*}_{0}AP_{B}
is Hermitian, thus closable. As
q_{B}
is positive,
q_{B}
is closable, cf. the proof of
[
19
, Thm. 1.27]
.

On the other hand, (
49
) holds if
\Phi\in{\mathcal{T}}(B)
by Theorem
4.12
. Then it holds if
\Phi\in D(\log\Delta_{B})
by the same argument, using Prop.
5.3
. As
D(\log\Delta_{B})
is a form core for
S
(see Sect.
2.3
), we conclude that
S=q_{B}
on
{\mathcal{T}}
.

\square

Denote by
B_{R}(\bar{\bf x})
the radius
R
space ball around the point
\bar{\bf x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Corollary 5.5
.

The entropy
S_{\Phi}(R)=S_{\Phi}(R,t,\bar{\bf x})
of the wave packet
\Phi\in{\mathcal{T}}
in the space region

with

, (

integral).

In view of Theorem
4.12
and the formula for the cutting projection in Proposition
5.2
, the theorem follows, in the time zero case, by the entropy formula (

). By translation covariance, we get the formula at an arbitrary time.

\square

6
Appendix. Elementary relations

For the reader’s convenience, we collect a couple of elementary identities that are used in the text.
Note first that
\partial_{r}
is the partial derivative in the direction

\vec{r}=(\frac{x_{1}}{r},\dots\frac{x_{d}}{r})
, thus

r\partial_{r}f={\bm{x}}\cdot{\nabla}f\,,

(51)

for any
f\in{\cal S}
.

Identity 6.1
.

Let
f\in{\mathcal{S}}
. We have

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{1}{2}(1-r^{2})|\nabla f|^{2}d{\bf x}=-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{1}{2}(1-r^{2})f\nabla^{2}fd{\bf x}+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}rf\partial_{r}f\,d{\bf x}\,.

The identity follows immediately by the following two relations:

-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{1}{2}(1-r^{2})f\nabla^{2}fd{\bf x}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{1}{2}(1-r^{2})|\nabla f|^{2}d{\bf x}+\frac{d}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f^{2}d{\bf x}\ ,

(52)

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}rf\partial_{r}f\,d{\bf x}=-\frac{d}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f^{2}d{\bf x}\ .

(53)

Concerning the second relation, by (
51
) we have

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}rf\partial_{r}f\,d{\bf x}=\sum_{k}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}x_{k}f\partial_{k}f\,d{\bf x}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}x_{k}\partial_{k}(f^{2})d{\bf x}\\
=-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f^{2}d{\bf x}=-\frac{d}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f^{2}d{\bf x}\ .

Then the first relation follows by

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(1-r^{2})f\nabla^{2}fd{\bf x}

\displaystyle=-\sum_{k}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\partial_{k}\big((1-r^{2})f\big)\partial_{k}f\,d{\bf x}

\displaystyle=2\sum_{k}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}x_{k}f\partial_{k}f\,d{\bf x}-\sum_{k}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(1-r^{2})(\partial_{k}f)^{2}d{\bf x}

\displaystyle=-d\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f^{2}d{\bf x}-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(1-r^{2})|\nabla f|^{2}d{\bf x}\ .

\square

Identity 6.2
.

With
Z
and
\gamma
as in Section
3
, we have

Z^{\prime}(z,s)|_{s=0}=(1-z^{2})/2\,,

\gamma^{\prime}(u,v;0)=-\frac{D}{2}(u+v)=-D\,x_{0}\,.

Denoting by a prime the derivative with respect to the
s
-parameter, we have

f(z,0)=1,\quad f^{\prime}(z,0)=-\frac{e^{-s}(1-z)}{2}\big|_{s=0}=-\frac{1-z}{2}\,,

g(z,0)=z,\quad g^{\prime}(z,s)=\frac{e^{-s}(1-z)}{2}|_{s=0}=\frac{1-z}{2}\,.

Since
Z=g/f
, we get

Z^{\prime}(z,s)|_{s=0}=(1-z^{2})/2\,.

We have

\gamma^{\prime}(u,v;s)\big|_{s=0}=\big(F(u,s)F(-v,-s)\big)^{\prime}\big|_{s=0}=F^{\prime}(u,s)\big|_{s=0}-F^{\prime}(-v,-s)\big|_{s=0}\ .

Since

F^{\prime}(z,s)\big|_{s=0}=-d\big(f(z,s)\big)^{-D-1}f^{\prime}(z,s)\big|_{s=0}=\frac{D}{2}(1-z)\ ,

we have

\gamma^{\prime}(u,v;0)=-\frac{D}{2}(u+v)=-D\,x_{0}\,.

\square
