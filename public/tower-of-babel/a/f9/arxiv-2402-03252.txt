Fair Active Ranking from Pairwise Preferences
Sruthi Gorantla, Sara Ahmadian

Abstract
We investigate the problem of probably approximately correct and fair (PACF) ranking of items by adaptively evoking pairwise comparisons. Given a set of n n items that belong to disjoint groups, our goal is to find an ( ϵ , δ ) (\epsilon,\delta) -PACF-Ranking according to a fair objective function that we propose. We assume access to an oracle, wherein, for each query, the learner can choose a pair of items and receive stochastic winner feedback from the oracle. Our proposed objective function asks to minimize the ℓ q \ell_{q} norm of the error of the groups, where the error of a group is the ℓ p \ell_{p} norm of the error of all the items within that group, for p , q ≥ 1 p,q\geq 1 . This generalizes the objective function of ϵ \epsilon -Best-Ranking, proposed by Saha and Gopalan, 2019 . By adopting our objective function, we gain the flexibility to explore fundamental fairness concepts like equal or proportionate errors within a unified framework. Adjusting parameters p p and q q allows tailoring to specific fairness preferences. We present both group-blind and group-aware algorithms and analyze their sample complexity. We provide matching lower bounds up to certain logarithmic factors for group-blind algorithms. For a restricted class of group-aware algorithms, we show that we can get reasonable lower bounds. We conduct comprehensive experiments on both real-world and synthetic datasets to complement our theoretical findings.



1
Introduction

Ranking is a fundamental problem in data mining and machine learning that arises in a wide range of applications, such as search engines, recommender systems, and information retrieval. The simplest and most extensively studied version of ranking uses noisy pairwise comparisons, as initiated by
Feige et al., 1994
. Recently, it has also been studied with dueling bandits
(
Busa-Fekete and Hüllermeier, 2014
)
.

Ranking problems have been studied under fairness constraints to mitigate or eliminate the bias and discrimination in the solutions constructed by the existing algorithms. The primary focus of these works is on generating rankings with respect to socially salient attributes
(
Zehlike et al., 2022a
;
Zehlike et al., 2022b
;
Pitoura et al., 2022
)
that asks for equal or proportional representation within every prefix of the ranking. Although such fairness constraints are especially useful in human-centric applications, such as hiring, credit allocation, recidivism prediction, and college admissions, they may still produce adverse outcomes for particular groups of individuals. This is primarily because the error in ranking is measured as an aggregate of errors over all the items. The resulting ranking may systematically discriminate against minority groups in terms of error, even after applying representation fairness constraints. The error function does not take into account biases in the data or differences in the data distributions for different demographic groups.

We introduce a fair ranking inspired by social fairness concepts, which focuses on generating rankings that fairly distribute error across groups. Our metric is versatile in the definition of the error within each group and in the aggregation of the errors over different groups. This yields a metric that generalizes several well-known notions of fairness.

In this paper, we study active, PAC ranking of
n
items using pairwise comparisons. In this setting, the learner receives preference feedback, for requested pairwise comparisons, according to the well-known Plackett-Luce (PL) probability model. The learner’s goal is to find a near-optimal ranking, with respect to tolerance parameter
\epsilon
, with high probability
(1-\delta)
, using as few pairwise comparison rounds as possible. Overall, our contributions can be summarized as follows:

1.

We introduce a fair variant of the error metric for rankings based on social fairness concepts (Definition
1
). To this end, we also discuss the limitations of previous fair ranking definitions (Section
3
), and how algorithms optimized for our proposed objective function overcome these limitations.

2.

We study two classes of algorithms depending on whether they have access to the group labels of the items or not. We call the algorithms without access
group-blind
and those with access
group-aware
. We design efficient algorithms output probably approximately correct rankings for our fair objective function and analyse their sample complexity bounds. We provide matching lower bounds up to certain logarithmic factors for group-blind algorithms.
For a restricted class of group-aware algorithms, we show that we can get reasonable lower bounds.

3.

We empirically evaluate our algorithms on real-world and synthetic datasets and show that our
group-aware
algorithm has significantly lower sample complexity than the
group-blind
. We also show that our
group-aware
algorithm achieves lower overall error as well as lower error on all the groups than
group-blind
algorithm.

In
Section

2
, we discuss several related works. In
Section

3
, we introduce our metric and define PAC ranking for our metric. In Sections
4
and
5
, we show our main theoretical and experimental results respectively.

Related Works

Fairness in ranking.

Group fairness notions ask for groups to be treated equally, such as asking for equality of opportunity in supervised learning
(
Hardt et al., 2016
)
, equitable clustering costs across groups in clustering
(
Ghadiri et al., 2021
;
Abbasi et al., 2021
;
Chlamtac et al., 2022
;
Gorantla et al., 2023
)
, and equal representation of the groups in ranking
(
Celis et al., 2018
)
or subset selection
(
Kleinberg and Raghavan, 2018
)
.
On the contrary, individual fairness treats fairness at the individual level and not as some aggregate function of groups. It asks for similar individuals to be treated similarly for the task at hand
(
Dwork et al., 2012
)
.
Particularly in ranking, the group fairness notions include ensuring sufficient representation of all the groups in each prefix of the top
k
ranking
(
Celis et al., 2018
;
Zehlike et al., 2017
;
Zehlike et al., 2022a
)
or every
k
consecutive ranks
(
Gorantla et al., 2021
)
.
Other works ask for equality of exposure of the groups
(
Singh and Joachims, 2018
)
, fair ranking under uncertain merit scores
(
Singh et al., 2021
)
, fair ranking with noisy sensitive attributes
(
Mehrotra and Vishnoi, 2022
)
, fair ranking in the presence of implicit bias
(
Celis et al., 2020
)
, etc.
However, these are all algorithmic solutions that assume access to the merit (or relevance) scores of the items and maximize some objective function such as
NDCG, Precision@
k
.
In contrast, we assume that we only have oracle access to pairwise preferences of the items and study the problem of inferring a ranking with a minimal number of queries with high confidence
1-\delta
, and up to an error
\epsilon
, while ensuring fairness in errors incurred by the groups. Even though previous works such as
(
Saha and Gopalan, 2019
)
studied non-fair variant of the problem in the pairwise preference model, to the best of our knowledge, we are the first to study this with fairness consideration.

Cascaded norms for fairness.

Cascaded norm objectives have been used in generalizing the cost-based objective functions to account for different costs borne by different groups.
Metric space clustering
objective asks for minimizing the
\ell_{p}
-norm of the distances between points in a cluster and their center. Most interesting cases are when
p\in\{1,2,\infty\}
as they correspond to the well studied
k
-median,
k
-means, and
k
-center, respectively.

Chlamtac et al., 2022
later generalized this further to account for group fairness. They ask to minimize the
\ell_{q}
-norm of the cost of the groups, where cost of a group is the
\ell_{p}
-norm of the distances between the points in a cluster from that group and their cluster center.
This generalizes the fair clustering notions such as
Socially Fair

k
-means and
k
-medians clustering.
This objective function allows us to treat many problems under one umbrella.
Taking inspiration from this, we propose the notion of
\epsilon
-Best-
Fair
-Ranking
, using norms of errors in a cascaded fashion, first within the groups and then across the groups.

Active ranking from pairwise comparisons.

Pairwise comparisons are well-motivated for the sake of ranking as they provide an easier way of collecting peoples’ preferences; it is easier to compare two candidates for a job rather than assign an absolute score to them independently and compare the scores to get relative preferences. Even mathematically speaking, pairwise preferences enforce a weaker constraint on the data collection process than asking for exact scores because the former need not satisfy transitivity in the pairwise preferences of the items while the latter induces transitivity on the items.
The
passive
sample complexity for ranking with pairwise comparisons has been resolved under different model assumptions in recent works
(
Gleich and Lim, 2011
;
Rajkumar and Agarwal, 2016
)
.
The exact sample complexity depends on the objective function defined to measure the “goodness” of the ranking.
However, collecting pairwise preference labels might be expensive.
Active learning has become a prevailing technique for designing efficient supervised learning algorithms, where one only needs to query labels that are most “informative”.
In many settings, active learning gives an exponential improvement in the sample complexity compared to its
passive
counterparts under some weak distributional assumptions
(
Balcan and Long, 2013
)
.
Recent works have studied ranking in the
active
or adaptive setting
(
Ailon, 2012
;
Jamieson and Nowak, 2011
;
Saha and Gopalan, 2019
;
Ren et al., 2021
)
, among which
Ren et al., 2021
;
Saha and Gopalan, 2019
studied PAC sample complexity bounds in a multi-wise comparisons feedback model.
There have also been works on estimating the parameters of the pairwise preference model that generates the rankings
Khetan and Oh, 2016
.

The active ranking problem.

Given a set
{\mathcal{I}}
of items, a ranking
\boldsymbol{\sigma}
defines a total ordering of the items. Let us assume that each item
i\in{\mathcal{I}}
is associated with a true relevance score
\theta_{i}\in{\mathbb{R}}
, where the higher the value of
\theta_{i}
, the better to rank it at the top.
There is only one ranking that is consistent with the true scores – the one that ranks the items in the descending order of their true scores.
However, we consider the setup where the true scores are not directly available, but any pair of items can be compared via an oracle, which has access to their true scores.
Depending on the type of feedback from the oracle, the goal is to find a ranking of the items by
actively
querying for pairwise comparisons of the items one after the other.
It is useful to note that sorting
n
items based on pairwise comparisons fits in this problem setup, which needs
\Theta(n\log n)
actively chosen pairwise comparison queries.
Note that the pairwise comparison queries are fixed up front in the
passive
setup; hence, the output of one query does not affect what other queries are made by the algorithm, unlike the active setup.

Plackett-Luce (PL) feedback oracle.

PL model is extensively used in generating stochastic rankings
Rajkumar and Agarwal, 2016
;
Saha and Gopalan, 2019
;
Singh et al., 2021
.
The probability of sampling a ranking in the PL model is given by,

\Pr[\boldsymbol{\sigma}|\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{n}]=\prod_{i\in[n]}\frac{\theta_{\sigma^{-1}(i)}}{\sum_{j=i}^{n}\theta_{\sigma^{-1}(j)}}.

An appealing property of the PL model is that the pairwise winner probabilities for a pair of items
\{i,i^{\prime}\}\in{\mathcal{I}}
are very easy to calculate. That is,

\Pr[i\mid\{i,i^{\prime}\}]=\frac{\theta_{i}}{\theta_{i}+\theta_{i^{\prime}}}~~\text{and}~~\Pr[i^{\prime}\mid\{i,i^{\prime}\}]=\frac{\theta_{i^{\prime}}}{\theta_{i}+\theta_{i^{\prime}}}.

Saha and Gopalan, 2019
also studied the PAC sample complexity bounds under the PL model to leverage the property of independence of irrelevant attributes satisfied by the PL model, which helps in consistently aggregating pairwise preferences to find a total ordering. Hence, we also study our problem under the PL model.

Ranking performance.

In a ranking
\boldsymbol{\sigma}
, each item
i
suffers an error based on the items incorrectly ordered above
i
.
One way of quantifying this error is as follows,

d_{i}(\boldsymbol{\sigma};\theta):=\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}i^{\prime}\in[n]~\text{s.t.}\\
\theta_{i}>\theta_{i^{\prime}}\land\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{-1}(i)>\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{-1}(i^{\prime})\end{subarray}}\theta_{i}-\theta_{i^{\prime}}.

If there are no items with higher scores ranked after
i

to be zero.
Then, the performance of a candidate ranking
\boldsymbol{\sigma}\in\Sigma_{{\mathcal{I}}}
can be measured as an aggregate of the errors of the items.
One such metric proposed by

Using this,

-Best-Ranking
iff,

\text{err}(\boldsymbol{\sigma};\theta):=\max_{i\in{\mathcal{I}}}~~d_{i}(\boldsymbol{\sigma};\theta)<\epsilon.

Addressing the restriction on algorithms.

For the algorithms that are allowed to make pairwise comparisons of items from different groups, it becomes challenging to bound the KL divergence between true and alternative instances for some of the pairwise comparisons.

The gap in our lower and upper bound for the

6
Conclusion

We study the Probably Approximately Correct (PAC) version of the problem of adaptively fair ranking
n
items from pairwise comparisons in the Plackett-Luce (PL) preference model. We propose a fair metric for measuring the quality of rankings for different groups that generalizes ranking metrics that do not consider group fairness requirements.
We study the problem under two settings: (i) where the ranking algorithm has access to group membership of items (
group-aware
), and (ii) where the ranking algorithm does not have access to group membership of items (
group-blind
). For the first setting, we show how the algorithm of
Saha and Gopalan, 2019
can be adjusted to find a fair ranking with optimal sample complexity, and we prove a matching lower bound on the sample complexity up to some
\log
factors.
For the second setting, we design an algorithm and prove its sample complexity.
We also provide a reasonable lower bound for a restricted class of algorithms.

The main open question is to close the gap between the lower and upper bounds for both types of algorithms.
It would also be interesting to study the problem under alternative choice models, such as the multinomial probit, Mallows, nested logit, generalized extreme-value models, etc.

\displaystyle\text{err}^{\texttt{fair}}(\boldsymbol{\sigma};\theta)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\epsilon}{n^{1/q}}\left(\sum_{h\in[\gamma]}|G_{h}|\right)^{1/q}\leq\frac{\epsilon}{n^{1/q}}\cdot n^{1/q}=\epsilon.

(9)

When
q>p
, we have that
1/q<1/p
. We need to upper bound the term in the summation in
Equation

8
.
Since the number of groups can be between
1
and
n
, and the total number of items needs to be exactly
n
, we can write the following optimization problem where
x_{h}
represents the number of items from group
h
,

\displaystyle\max_{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}~~~

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{h\in[n]}x_{h}^{q/p}\right)^{1/q}

(10)

such that

\displaystyle\sum_{h\in[n]}x_{h}=n

(11)

and

\displaystyle x_{h}\in\{0,1,2,\ldots,n\},~~\forall h\in[n].

(12)

Consider the following relaxation of the program above,

\displaystyle\max_{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}~~~

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{h\in[n]}x_{h}^{q/p}\right)^{1/q}

(13)

such that

\displaystyle\sum_{h\in[n]}x_{h}=n

(14)

and

\displaystyle 0\leq x_{h}\leq n,~~\forall h\in[n].

(15)

It is easy to see that the all ones vector,
\mathbbm{1}
, is the minimizer of
\sum_{i\in[n]}x_{i}^{q/p}
inside the convex polytope formed by the constraints (
14
) and (
15
).
Therefore, the maximum value of this convex function is achieved at a vertex of the convex polytope.
Since the polytope lives in an
n
dimensional space, any vertex is formed by at least
n
equality constraints, one of which has to be (
14
).
Amongst the constraints in (
15
), at least
n-1
coordinates of
x
have to be
0
, and exactly one has to be
n
.
There is no other way to satisfy at least
n
constraints with equality.
Moreover,
(\cdot)^{1/q}
is a non-decreasing function.
Hence, the maximum of the objective function in
Equation

13
also occurs at a vertex of the convex polytope.
Note that this is also a feasible point for the program defined by
Equations

10
,
11
and
12
.
Therefore, the maximum value of the objective function is
n^{1/p}
, which gives us that,

\displaystyle\text{err}^{\texttt{fair}}(\boldsymbol{\sigma};\theta)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\epsilon}{n^{1/p}}\cdot n^{1/p}=\epsilon.

(16)

From
Equations

9
and
16
we can conclude that
Algorithm

2
returns an
\epsilon
-Best-
Fair
-Ranking
with probability at least
1-\delta
.

From Theorem 8 in
Saha and Gopalan, 2019
, we know that
Beat-the-Pivot
with error parameter
\epsilon
and confidence parameter
\delta
has sample complexity
{\mathcal{O}}\left(\frac{n}{\epsilon^{2}}\log\frac{n}{\delta}\right)
.
Therefore, the sample complexity of
Algorithm

2
is
{\mathcal{O}}\left(\frac{n}{{\tilde{\epsilon}}^{2}}\log\frac{n}{\delta}\right)={\mathcal{O}}\left(\frac{n^{1+\max\left\{\frac{2}{q},\frac{2}{p}\right\}}}{\epsilon^{2}}\log\frac{n}{\delta}\right)
.

∎

B.2.2
Lower bound

Lemma 5

(Lower bound on Sample Complexity)
.

Given an error parameter
\epsilon\in(0,1)
, a confidence parameter
\delta\in(0,1)
, and a symmetric
(\epsilon,\delta)
-PAC-Ranker
algorithm
{\mathcal{A}}
for WI feedback, there exists a PL instance
\nu
such that the sample complexity of
{\mathcal{A}}
on
\nu
is at least

\Omega\bigg(\frac{n^{1+\max\left\{\frac{2}{p},\frac{2}{q}\right\}}}{\epsilon^{2}}\bigg).

Similarly to
(
Saha and Gopalan, 2019
)
, we construct a hard class of instances and use the Lemma on multi-armed bandits given by
Kaufmann et al., 2014
that gives a change-of-measure argument to lower bound the sample complexity.
We restate the lemma below,

Lemma 6

(Lemma
1
,
Kaufmann et al., 2014
)
.

Let
\eta
and
\eta^{\prime}
be two bandit models for
N
arms (assignments of reward distributions to arms), such that
\eta_{i}~(\text{resp.}\,\eta^{\prime}_{i})
is the reward distribution of any arm
i\in[N]
under the bandit model
\eta~(\text{resp.}\,\eta^{\prime})
, and such that for all such arms
i
,
\eta_{i}
and
\eta^{\prime}_{i}
are mutually absolutely continuous. Then for any almost-surely finite stopping time
\tau
with respect to
({\mathcal{F}}_{t})_{t}
,

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{N}{\mathbf{E}}_{\eta}[N_{i}(\tau)]KL(\eta_{i},\eta_{i}^{\prime})\geq\sup_{{\mathcal{E}}\in{\mathcal{F}}_{\tau}}kl(Pr_{\eta}({\mathcal{E}}),Pr_{\eta^{\prime}}({\mathcal{E}})),

where
kl(x,y):=x\log(\frac{x}{y})+(1-x)\log(\frac{1-x}{1-y})
is the binary relative entropy,
N_{i}(\tau)
denotes the number of times arm
i
is played in
\tau
rounds, and
Pr_{\eta}({\mathcal{E}})
and
Pr_{\eta^{\prime}}({\mathcal{E}})
denote the probability of any event
{\mathcal{E}}\in{\mathcal{F}}_{\tau}
under bandit models
\eta
and
\eta^{\prime}
, respectively.

Proof of
Lemma

5
.

Let us assume that the items belong to exactly one group. This result will be useful in proving sample complexity for multiple-group case.
Note that when there is only one group, both
group-blind
and
group-aware
algorithms should incur the same minimum sample complexity to learn a ranking.
Also, the parameter
q
and the weight function do not matter.
For simplicity, let us assume that
n
is a multiple of
4
.

Class of instances.

Let
{\tilde{\epsilon}}=\epsilon\cdot\left(\frac{4}{n}\right)^{1/p}
.
Let
n^{\prime}:=3n/4
.
Let
T:=\{n^{\prime}+1,n^{\prime}+2,\ldots,n\}
.
Now consider the class of instances
{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{[m]}
for any
m\in[n^{\prime}]
, where for any
S\subseteq[n^{\prime}]
such that
|S|=m
,
\nu_{S}
represents the instance where

\forall i\in S,~~\theta_{i}=\theta\left(\frac{1}{2}+{\tilde{\epsilon}}\right)^{2},\quad\forall i\in T,~~\theta_{i}=\theta\left(\frac{1}{4}-{\tilde{\epsilon}}^{2}\right),\quad\text{and}\quad\forall i\not\in S\cup T,~~\theta_{i}=\theta\left(\frac{1}{2}-{\tilde{\epsilon}}\right)^{2}.

Remark
.

Note that
S
uniquely represents an instance
\nu_{S}\in{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{[m]}
for any fixed
m\in[n^{\prime}]
.

Lemma 7
.

For any
\theta>\frac{1}{1-2{\tilde{\epsilon}}}
and for any problem instance
\nu_{S}\in{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{[n/4]}
, any
\epsilon
-Best-
Fair
-Ranking
, say
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{S}
, has to satisfy the following:
the number of items from
S\cup T
in ranks
\frac{n}{4}+2
to
n
should be strictly less than
\frac{n}{4}
.

Proof.

Note that when
\theta>\frac{1}{1-2{\tilde{\epsilon}}}
, for any
i\in S,i^{\prime}\in T
and
i^{\prime\prime}\notin S\cup T
,

\theta_{i}-\theta_{i^{\prime}}>{\tilde{\epsilon}},~~\theta_{i^{\prime}}-\theta_{i^{\prime\prime}}>{\tilde{\epsilon}},~~\text{and}~~\theta_{i}-\theta_{i^{\prime\prime}}>{\tilde{\epsilon}}.

Let us assume that there exists an
\epsilon
-Best-
Fair
-Ranking
for
\nu_{S}
, say
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{S}
, such that the ranks
\frac{n}{4}+2
to
n
have
\geq\frac{n}{4}
items from
S\cup T
.
Then,
\leq\frac{n}{4}
items from
S\cup T
are in the ranks
1
to
\frac{n}{4}+1
, since
|S\cup T|=\frac{n}{2}
.
So there will be at least one item from
[n^{\prime}]{\setminus}S
in ranks
1
to
\frac{n}{4}+1
, which implies that at least
\frac{n}{4}
items in
S\cup T
incur an error
>{\tilde{\epsilon}}
.
Therefore, the overall error will be,

\displaystyle\text{err}^{\texttt{fair}}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{S};\theta_{S})

\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{i\in[n]}d_{i}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{S};\theta_{S})^{p}\right)^{1/p}>\left(\frac{n}{4}\cdot{\tilde{\epsilon}}^{p}\right)^{1/p}=\left(\frac{n}{4}\cdot\left(\epsilon\cdot\left(\frac{4}{n}\right)^{1/p}\right)^{p}\right)^{1/p}

\displaystyle=\left(\epsilon^{p}\right)^{1/p}=\epsilon,

which contradicts our assumption that
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{S}
is an
\epsilon
-Best-
Fair
-Ranking
.
∎

The alternative instances.

We now fix any set
S^{*}\subset[n^{\prime}]
such that
|S^{*}|=\frac{n}{4}
. Lower bound on the sample complexity is now obtained by applying
Lemma

6
on a pair of instances
(\nu_{S^{*}},\nu_{{\tilde{S}}^{*}})
, for all possible choices of
{\tilde{S}}^{*}={S^{*}}\cup S^{\prime}
, where
S^{\prime}\subset[n^{\prime}]{\setminus}{S^{*}}
and
|S^{\prime}|=\frac{n}{4}
.
Note that there will be
n/2\choose n/4
choices of
{\tilde{S}}^{*}
.

Describing the event.

For any ranking
\boldsymbol{\sigma}\in\Sigma_{n}
, we denote by
\boldsymbol{\sigma}(r:r^{\prime})
the set of items in the ranking,
\boldsymbol{\sigma}
, from rank
r
to rank
r^{\prime}
, for any
1\leq r\leq r^{\prime}\leq n
.
Consider the event
{\mathcal{E}}
for an instance
S
that the algorithm
{\mathcal{A}}
outputs a ranking such that the number of items from
S\cup T
in the ranks
n/4+2
to
n
is
<n/4
. That is,

{\mathcal{E}}(S):=\left\{\left|(S\cup T)\cap\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{{\mathcal{A}}}\left(\frac{n}{4}+2:n\right)\right|<\frac{n}{4}\right\}.

This is a high probability event for
S^{*}
because otherwise, the error will be more than
\epsilon
, from
Lemma

7
.
On the contrary, for the alternative instances with
{\tilde{S}}^{*}
, this is a low probability event because all the items from
T
have to appear in the ranks
n/4+2
to
n
or otherwise,
\geq n/4
items from
{\tilde{S}}^{*}\cup T
will be in ranks
n/4+2
to
n
and they all incur an error
>{\tilde{\epsilon}}
due to an item from
T
in the ranks
1
to
n/4+1
; therefore the total error will be more than
\epsilon
.

It is easy to note that as
{\mathcal{A}}
is an
(\epsilon,\delta)
-PAC-Ranker
, obviously

Pr_{S^{*}}({\mathcal{E}}({S^{*}}))>Pr_{S^{*}}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{{\mathcal{A}}}\text{ is an {\bf$\epsilon$-Best-Ranking}})>1-\delta,

(17)

and

Pr_{{\tilde{S}}^{*}}({\mathcal{E}}({S^{*}}))<\delta,

(18)

for any alternative instance
{\tilde{S}}^{*}
.

We can further tighten
Equation

18
using
symmetric property
of
{\mathcal{A}}
as follows,

Lemma 8
.

For any symmetric
(\epsilon,\delta)
-PAC-Ranker
,
{\mathcal{A}}
, and any problem instance
\nu_{S}\in{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{[n^{\prime}]}
such that
|S|=\frac{n}{2}
,

Pr_{S}\left({\mathcal{E}}(S)\right)<\frac{\delta}{{n/2\choose n/4}},

where
Pr_{S}(\cdot)
denotes the probability of an event under the underlying problem instance
\nu_{S}
and the internal randomness of the algorithm
{\mathcal{A}}
(if any).

Proof.

Let us first fix an
m=n/2
and
m^{\prime}=n/4
.
Consider a problem instance
\nu_{S}\in{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{[m]}
. Recall from Remark
Remark
that we use the notation
S\in{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{[m]}
to denote a problem instance in
{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{[m]}
.
Then the probability of making an error over all possible choices of
S\in{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{[m]}
:

\sum_{S\in{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{[m]}}Pr_{S}\Big({\mathcal{A}}~\text{makes an error $>\epsilon$ on }S\Big)\geq\sum_{S_{1}\in{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{[m^{\prime}]}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}S_{2}\in[n^{\prime}]{\setminus}S\\
\text{s.t.}|S_{2}|=\frac{n}{4}\end{subarray}}Pr_{S_{1}\cup S_{2}}\Big({\mathcal{E}}(S_{1}))\Big)

(19)

where the above analysis follows from a similar result proved by
Kalyanakrishnan et al., 2012
to derive sample complexity lower bound for classical multi-armed bandit setting towards recovering top-
q
items (see Theorem
8
,
Kalyanakrishnan et al., 2012
).

Clearly the possible number of instances in
{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{[m]}
, i.e.
|{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{[m]}|={{n^{\prime}}\choose{m}}
, as any set
S\subset[n^{\prime}]
of size
m
can be chosen from
[n^{\prime}]
in
{{n^{\prime}}\choose{m}}
ways.

Now from
symmetry
of algorithm
{\mathcal{A}}
and by construction of the class of our problem instances
{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{[m]}
, for any two instances
S_{1}
and
\hat{S}_{1}
in
{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{[m^{\prime}]}
, and for any choices of
S_{2}\in[n^{\prime}]\setminus S^{\prime}_{1}
and
\hat{S}_{2}\in[n^{\prime}]\setminus S^{\prime}_{2}
such that
|\hat{S}_{1}|=|\hat{S}_{2}|=n/4
we have that:

Pr_{S_{1}\cup S_{2}}\Big({\mathcal{E}}(S_{1})\Big)=Pr_{\hat{S}_{1}\cup\hat{S}_{2}}\Big({\mathcal{E}}(\hat{S}_{1})\Big).

Then the above equivalently implies that for all
S_{1}\in{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{[m^{\prime}]}
and any
S_{2}\in[n^{\prime}]\setminus S
such that
|S_{2}|=\frac{n}{4}
,
\exists p\in[0,1]

Pr_{S_{1}\cup S_{2}}({\mathcal{E}}(S_{1}))=p.

Then using above in
Equation

19
we can further derive,

\displaystyle\sum_{S\in{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{[m]}}Pr_{S}\Big({\mathcal{A}}~\text{makes an error $>\epsilon$ on }S\Big)

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{S_{1}\in{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{[m^{\prime}]}}\sum_{S_{2}\in[n^{\prime}]{\setminus}S_{1}:|S_{2}|=n/4}Pr_{S_{1}\cup S_{2}}\Big({\mathcal{E}}(S_{1}))\Big)

\displaystyle={n^{\prime}\choose m^{\prime}}{n^{\prime}-m^{\prime}\choose n/4}p

\displaystyle={3n/4\choose n/4}{n/2\choose n/4}p.

On the L.H.S. there are
{3n/4\choose n/2}={3n/4\choose n/4}
choices of
S
.
Therefore, if
p>\frac{\delta}{{n/2\choose n/4}}
, we get that,

\displaystyle\sum_{S\in{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{[m]}}Pr_{S}\Big({\mathcal{A}}~\text{makes an error $>\epsilon$ on }S\Big)

\displaystyle>{\frac{3n}{4}\choose\frac{n}{4}}\delta.

which in turn implies that there exists at least one instance
\nu_{S}\in{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{[m]}
such that

Pr_{S}\Big({\mathcal{A}}~\text{makes an error $>\epsilon$ on }S\Big)\geq\delta
, which violates the
(\epsilon,\delta)
-PAC-Ranker
property of algorithm
{\mathcal{A}}
. Thus it has to be the case that
p<\frac{\delta}{{n/2\choose n/4}}
,
which concludes the proof.
∎

Owing to
Lemma

8
we get,

Pr_{{\tilde{S}}^{*}}\Big({\mathcal{E}}({S^{*}})\Big)<\frac{\delta}{{n/2\choose n/4}}.

(20)

We will crucially use
Equation

17
and
Equation

20
in the following lemma.

Lemma 9

(Lemma 26,
Saha and Gopalan, 2019
)
.

For any
\delta\in(0,1)
, and
\alpha\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
,

kl\bigg(1-\delta,\dfrac{\delta}{\alpha}\bigg)>\ln\dfrac{\alpha}{4\delta}.

This lemma leads to the desired tighter upper bound for
kl(Pr_{\nu_{{S^{*}}}}({\mathcal{E}}),Pr_{\nu_{{\tilde{S}}^{*}}}({\mathcal{E}}))\geq kl(1-\delta,\frac{\delta}{{n/2\choose n/4}})\geq\ln\frac{{n/2\choose n/4}}{4\delta}
.

Note that for the problem instance
\nu_{S^{*}}\in{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{[m]}
, the probability distribution associated with a particular arm
B\in{\mathcal{B}}
– a pair of items – is given by:

\nu^{B}_{S^{*}}\sim Categorical(p_{1},p_{2}),\text{ where }p_{i}=Pr(i|B),~~\forall i\in[2],\,\forall B\in{\mathcal{B}},

where
Pr(i|B)
is the probability of item
i
winning in the Plackett-Luce model for the items in set
B
.
Now applying
Lemma

6
, for some event
{\mathcal{E}}\in{\mathcal{F}}_{\tau}
we get,

\displaystyle\sum_{\{B\in{\mathcal{B}}\}}{\mathbf{E}}_{\nu^{B}_{S^{*}}}[N_{B}(\tau_{{\mathcal{A}}})]KL(\nu^{B}_{S^{*}},\nu^{B}_{{\tilde{S}}^{*}})\geq kl(Pr_{\nu_{S^{*}}}({\mathcal{E}}),Pr_{\nu_{{\tilde{S}}^{*}}}({\mathcal{E}})),

(21)

where
N_{B}(\tau_{{\mathcal{A}}})
denotes the number of times arm
B
is played by
{\mathcal{A}}
in
\tau
rounds.
Note that whenever
B
is such that
B\subset([n^{\prime}]{\setminus}{\tilde{S}}^{*})\cup{S^{*}},KL(\nu^{B}_{S^{*}},\nu^{B}_{{\tilde{S}}^{*}})=0
.
Therefore, we will only focus on
B
such that
B\not\subset([n^{\prime}]{\setminus}{\tilde{S}}^{*})\cup{S^{*}}
.

We simplify the right-hand side of
Equation

21
using the following lemma.

Lemma 10
.

For any
m\in[n^{\prime}]
and problem instance
\nu_{S}\in{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{[m]}
, and any arm
B
,
KL(\nu^{B}_{S^{*}},\nu^{B}_{{\tilde{S}}^{*}})\leq 64{\tilde{\epsilon}}^{2}
.

Proof.

Let
B=\{a,b\}
, i.e., the pair of items
a,b
for which we make the oracle call.
Let

R=\frac{\frac{1}{2}+{\tilde{\epsilon}}}{\frac{1}{2}-{\tilde{\epsilon}}}.

Case 1: None of the items in
B
are from
T
.

In this case, we again have four cases.

1.

Both items are good:

\nu_{{S^{*}}}^{B}(a)=\nu_{{S^{*}}}^{B}(b)=\frac{\theta\left(\frac{1}{2}+{\tilde{\epsilon}}\right)^{2}}{\theta\left(\frac{1}{2}+{\tilde{\epsilon}}\right)^{2}+\theta\left(\frac{1}{2}+{\tilde{\epsilon}}\right)^{2}}=\frac{1}{2}
.

2.

a
is good and
b
is bad:

\nu_{{S^{*}}}^{B}(a)=\frac{\theta\left(\frac{1}{2}+{\tilde{\epsilon}}\right)^{2}}{\theta\left(\frac{1}{2}+{\tilde{\epsilon}}\right)^{2}+\theta\left(\frac{1}{2}-{\tilde{\epsilon}}\right)^{2}}=\frac{R^{2}}{R^{2}+1}
and
\nu_{{S^{*}}}^{B}(b)=\frac{\theta\left(\frac{1}{2}-{\tilde{\epsilon}}\right)^{2}}{\theta\left(\frac{1}{2}-{\tilde{\epsilon}}\right)^{2}+\theta\left(\frac{1}{2}+{\tilde{\epsilon}}\right)^{2}}=\frac{1}{R^{2}+1}
.

3.

a
is bad and
b
is good:

\nu_{{S^{*}}}^{B}(a)=\frac{\theta\left(\frac{1}{2}-{\tilde{\epsilon}}\right)^{2}}{\theta\left(\frac{1}{2}-{\tilde{\epsilon}}\right)^{2}+\theta\left(\frac{1}{2}+{\tilde{\epsilon}}\right)^{2}}=\frac{1}{R^{2}+1}
and
\nu_{{S^{*}}}^{B}(b)=\frac{\theta\left(\frac{1}{2}+{\tilde{\epsilon}}\right)^{2}}{\theta\left(\frac{1}{2}+{\tilde{\epsilon}}\right)^{2}+\theta\left(\frac{1}{2}-{\tilde{\epsilon}}\right)^{2}}=\frac{R^{2}}{R^{2}+1}

4.

Both items are bad:

\nu_{{S^{*}}}^{B}(a)=\nu_{{S^{*}}}^{B}(b)=\frac{\theta\left(\frac{1}{2}-{\tilde{\epsilon}}\right)^{2}}{\theta\left(\frac{1}{2}-{\tilde{\epsilon}}\right)^{2}+\theta\left(\frac{1}{2}-{\tilde{\epsilon}}\right)^{2}}=\frac{1}{2}
.

Now we use the upper bound from
Popescu et al., 2016
,
KL({\mathbf{p}}_{1},{\mathbf{p}}_{2})\leq\sum_{x\in{\mathcal{X}}}\frac{p_{1}^{2}(x)}{p_{2}(x)}-1
, for two probability mass functions
{\mathbf{p}}_{1}
and
{\mathbf{p}}_{2}
on the discrete random variable
{\mathcal{X}}
.
In this case, the KL divergence is non-zero when there is one item from
([n^{\prime}]{\setminus}{\tilde{S}}^{*})\cup{S^{*}}
, say
a
, and one item from
{\tilde{S}}^{*}{\setminus}{S^{*}}
, say
b
.
Let
{\tilde{S}}^{*}
be such that both
a
and
b
are good for that alternative instance. We will have subcases:

1.

When
a\in{S^{*}}
:

\displaystyle KL(\nu^{B}_{S^{*}},\nu^{B}_{{\tilde{S}}^{*}})

\displaystyle\leq\left(\frac{R^{2}}{R^{2}+1}\right)^{2}\cdot\frac{2}{1}+\left(\frac{1}{R^{2}+1}\right)^{2}\cdot\frac{2}{1}-1

\displaystyle=\frac{2R^{4}+2-(R^{2}+1)^{2}}{(R^{2}+1)^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{2R^{4}+2-R^{4}-2R^{2}-1}{(R^{2}+1)^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{R^{4}-2R^{2}+1}{(R^{2}+1)^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{(R^{2}-1)^{2}}{(R^{2}+1)^{2}}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{\left(\frac{1}{2}+{\tilde{\epsilon}}\right)^{2}-\left(\frac{1}{2}-{\tilde{\epsilon}}\right)^{2}}{\left(\frac{1}{2}+{\tilde{\epsilon}}\right)^{2}+\left(\frac{1}{2}-{\tilde{\epsilon}}\right)^{2}}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{2{\tilde{\epsilon}}}{\frac{1}{2}+2{\tilde{\epsilon}}^{2}}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\left(\frac{2{\tilde{\epsilon}}}{\frac{1}{2}}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=64{\tilde{\epsilon}}^{2}.

2.

When
a\in([n^{\prime}]{\setminus}{\tilde{S}}^{*})
:

\displaystyle KL(\nu^{B}_{S^{*}},\nu^{B}_{{\tilde{S}}^{*}})

\displaystyle\leq\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{2}\cdot\frac{R^{2}+1}{1}+\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{2}\cdot\frac{R^{2}+1}{R^{2}}-1

\displaystyle=\frac{R^{2}+1}{4}+\frac{R^{2}+1}{4R^{2}}-1

\displaystyle=\frac{R^{4}+R^{2}+R^{2}+1-4R^{2}}{4R^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{R^{4}-2R^{2}+1}{4R^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{(R^{2}-1)^{2}}{4R^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}\left(\frac{R^{2}-1}{R}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}\left(R-\frac{1}{R}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 64{\tilde{\epsilon}}^{2}.

Case 2: Exactly one item in
B
is from
T
.

Let
b\in T
.
Again, we have several cases depending on item
a
.

1.

a\in{S^{*}}
:

\nu_{{S^{*}}}^{B}(a)=\frac{\theta\left(\frac{1}{2}+{\tilde{\epsilon}}\right)^{2}}{\theta\left(\frac{1}{2}+{\tilde{\epsilon}}\right)^{2}+\theta\left(\frac{1}{4}-{\tilde{\epsilon}}^{2}\right)}=\frac{R}{R+1}
and

\nu_{{S^{*}}}^{B}(b)=\frac{\theta\left(\frac{1}{4}-{\tilde{\epsilon}}^{2}\right)}{\theta\left(\frac{1}{2}+{\tilde{\epsilon}}\right)^{2}+\theta\left(\frac{1}{4}-{\tilde{\epsilon}}^{2}\right)}=\frac{1}{R+1}
.

2.

a\in[n^{\prime}]{\setminus}{S^{*}}
:

\nu_{{S^{*}}}^{B}(a)=\frac{\theta\left(\frac{1}{2}-{\tilde{\epsilon}}\right)^{2}}{\theta\left(\frac{1}{2}-{\tilde{\epsilon}}\right)^{2}+\theta\left(\frac{1}{4}-{\tilde{\epsilon}}^{2}\right)}=\frac{1}{R+1}
and

\nu_{{S^{*}}}^{B}(b)=\frac{\theta\left(\frac{1}{4}-{\tilde{\epsilon}}^{2}\right)}{\theta\left(\frac{1}{2}-{\tilde{\epsilon}}\right)^{2}+\theta\left(\frac{1}{4}-{\tilde{\epsilon}}^{2}\right)}=\frac{R}{R+1}
.

Note that here we have only one case where
a\not\in T
is bad in
{S^{*}}
and good in
{\tilde{S}}^{*}
.
Then,

\displaystyle KL(\nu^{B}_{S^{*}},\nu^{B}_{{\tilde{S}}^{*}})

\displaystyle\leq\left(\frac{1}{R+1}\right)^{2}\cdot\frac{R+1}{R}+\left(\frac{R}{R+1}\right)^{2}\cdot\frac{R+1}{1}-1

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{R(R+1)}+\frac{R^{2}}{R+1}-1

\displaystyle=\frac{1+R^{3}-R^{2}-R}{R(R+1)}

\displaystyle=\frac{(1-R)^{2}(1+R)}{R(R+1)}

\displaystyle=\frac{(1-R)^{2}}{R}

Proof.

Correctness.

\min\{\epsilon_{h},\epsilon_{h^{\prime}}\}

We will now show that this ensures that the
\text{err}_{p,q}(\boldsymbol{\sigma};\theta)<\epsilon
.
For ease of exposition, let us define the following intra-group and inter-group errors for each item,

d^{(h)}_{i}(\boldsymbol{\sigma};\theta):=\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}i^{\prime}\in G_{h}~\text{s.t.}\\
\theta_{i}>\theta_{i^{\prime}}\land\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{-1}(i)>\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{-1}(i^{\prime})\end{subarray}}\theta_{i}-\theta_{i^{\prime}}\qquad\text{and}\qquad d^{(\neg h)}_{i}(\boldsymbol{\sigma};\theta):=\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}i^{\prime}\in[n]{\setminus}G_{h}~\text{s.t.}\\
\theta_{i}>\theta_{i^{\prime}}\land\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{-1}(i)>\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{-1}(i^{\prime})\end{subarray}}\theta_{i}-\theta_{i^{\prime}}.

Fix a group
h\in[\gamma]
and let
n_{h}=|G_{h}|
, the size of the group.
Let
{\tilde{\epsilon}}_{h}=\frac{\epsilon_{h}}{2}\cdot\left(\frac{2}{n_{h}}\right)^{1/p}
and
\delta^{\prime}_{h}=\frac{\delta}{2n\gamma}
.
First,
Beat-the-Pivot
is run for the items within the group
h
.
Therefore, from
Lemma

4
we know that
\boldsymbol{\sigma}
is an
{\tilde{\epsilon}}_{h}
-Best-Rank with probability at least
1-\delta^{\prime}_{h}
.
Therefore, for any item
i\in G_{h}
,

\Pr\left[d_{i}^{(h)}(\boldsymbol{\sigma};\theta)>{\tilde{\epsilon}}_{h}\right]<\delta^{\prime}_{h}=\frac{\delta}{2n\gamma}.

For any pair of items
i,i^{\prime}
from two different groups
h,h^{\prime}
respectively, their ordering will be decided using
Beat-the-Pivot
with error parameter
\min\{{\tilde{\epsilon}}_{h},{\tilde{\epsilon}}_{h^{\prime}}\}
and confidence parameter
\frac{\delta}{2n^{2}\gamma}
.
W.l.o.g. let
{\tilde{\epsilon}}_{h}\leq{\tilde{\epsilon}}_{h^{\prime}}
.
Then,

\Pr\left[d_{i}^{(\neg h)}(\boldsymbol{\sigma};\theta)>{\tilde{\epsilon}}_{h}\right]<\frac{\delta}{2n^{2}\gamma}~~\text{and}~~\Pr\left[d_{i^{\prime}}^{(\neg h^{\prime})}(\boldsymbol{\sigma};\theta)>{\tilde{\epsilon}}_{h^{\prime}}\right]<\Pr\left[d_{i^{\prime}}^{(\neg h^{\prime})}(\boldsymbol{\sigma};\theta)>{\tilde{\epsilon}}_{h}\right]<\frac{\delta}{2n^{2}\gamma}.

Using this, correctness of
Algorithm

1
can be shown as follows,

\displaystyle\Pr[\text{err}^{\texttt{fair}}(\boldsymbol{\sigma};\theta)>\epsilon]

\displaystyle=\Pr\left[\left(\sum_{h\in[\gamma]}\frac{\phi_{h}}{|G_{h}|}\text{err}_{h,p}{(\boldsymbol{\sigma};\theta)^{q}}\right)^{1/q}>\epsilon\right]

\displaystyle\leq\Pr\left[\bigvee\limits_{h\in[\gamma]}\bigvee\limits_{i\in G_{h}}d_{i}(\boldsymbol{\sigma};\theta)>{\tilde{\epsilon}}_{h}\right].

Because otherwise, if for all
h\in[\gamma]
, and
\forall i\in G_{h}

d_{i}(\boldsymbol{\sigma};\theta)\leq\epsilon_{h}
, we have that,

\displaystyle\text{err}_{h}(\boldsymbol{\sigma};\theta)

\displaystyle\leq\left(n_{h}\left(\frac{\epsilon_{h}}{2}\left(\frac{2}{n_{h}}\right)^{1/p}\right)^{p}\right)^{1/p}

\displaystyle=\left(n_{h}\cdot\frac{\epsilon_{h}^{p}}{2^{p}}\cdot\frac{2}{n_{h}}\right)^{1/p}

\displaystyle=\epsilon_{h}\cdot\frac{2^{1/p}}{2}\leq\epsilon_{h}

\displaystyle\because~~p\geq 1\implies 1/p\leq 1

\displaystyle\implies\text{err}^{\texttt{fair}}

\displaystyle\leq\left(\sum_{h\in[\gamma]}\frac{\phi_{h}}{n_{h}}\left(\epsilon\cdot\left(\frac{n_{h}}{\phi_{h}\gamma}\right)^{1/q}\right)^{q}\right)^{1/q}

\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{h\in[\gamma]}\frac{\phi_{h}}{n_{h}}\cdot\epsilon_{q}\cdot\frac{n_{h}}{\phi_{h}\gamma}\right)^{1/q}\leq\epsilon.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\Pr[\text{err}^{\texttt{fair}}

\displaystyle(\boldsymbol{\sigma};\theta)>\epsilon]

\displaystyle\leq\Pr\left[\bigvee\limits_{h\in[\gamma]}\bigvee\limits_{i\in G_{h}}\left(d_{i}^{(h)}(\boldsymbol{\sigma};\theta)>{\tilde{\epsilon}}_{h}\lor d_{i}^{(\neg h)}(\boldsymbol{\sigma};\theta)>{\tilde{\epsilon}}_{h}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\Pr\left[\bigvee\limits_{h\in[\gamma]}\bigvee\limits_{i\in G_{h}}d_{i}^{(h)}(\boldsymbol{\sigma};\theta)>{\tilde{\epsilon}}_{h}\right]+\Pr\left[\bigvee\limits_{h\in[\gamma]}\bigvee\limits_{i\in G_{h}}d_{i}^{(\neg h)}(\boldsymbol{\sigma};\theta)>{\tilde{\epsilon}}_{h}\right]

\displaystyle=\Pr\left[\bigvee\limits_{h\in[\gamma]}\bigvee\limits_{i\in G_{h}}d_{i}^{(h)}(\boldsymbol{\sigma};\theta)>{\tilde{\epsilon}}_{h}\right]+\Pr\left[\bigvee\limits_{h\in[\gamma]}\bigvee\limits_{i\in G_{h}}\max\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}i^{\prime}\in G_{h^{\prime}}\text{ s.t. }h>h^{\prime}\land,\\

Sample Complexity.

•

Group-wise rankings.

Let
n_{h}=|G_{h}|
.
From
Lemma

4
we know that the sample complexity to find a group-wise ranking
\sigma_{h}
for group
h\in[\gamma]
is
{\mathcal{O}}\left(\frac{n_{h}}{{\tilde{\epsilon}}_{h}^{2}}\log\frac{n_{h}}{\delta}\right)
.
Therefore, the sum of the sample complexities to find all the group-wise rankings is
{\mathcal{O}}\left(\sum_{h=1}^{\gamma}\frac{n_{h}}{{\tilde{\epsilon}}_{h}^{2}}\log\frac{n_{h}}{\delta}\right)
.

•

Merging group-wise rankings.

The algorithm merges two lists at a time. Hence, it takes
O(\log\gamma)
iterations of the
while
loop before the algorithm terminates.
In each iteration of the
while
loop, we need to find a merged list, of length
n_{h}+n_{h^{\prime}}
, the sum of the lengths of the two sorted lists that are being merged.
To fill each position in the merged list, we make
O\left(\frac{1}{\min\{{\tilde{\epsilon}}_{h},{\tilde{\epsilon}}_{h^{\prime}}\}^{2}}\log\frac{2n^{2}\gamma}{\delta}\right)=O\left(\frac{1}{\min\{{\tilde{\epsilon}}_{h},{\tilde{\epsilon}}_{h^{\prime}}\}^{2}}\log\frac{2n\gamma}{\delta}\right)
oracle calls, since
\gamma\leq n
.
Note that since the values of
{\tilde{\epsilon}}_{h}
are different for different groups, merging different lists takes a different number of Oracle calls, which makes it hard to analyze the sample complexity. Hence, from now on, we will analyze the sample complexity using the smallest group-wise error. Let
g:=\arg\min_{h\in[\gamma]}{\tilde{\epsilon}}_{h}
.
Now, the sample complexity of merging two lists will be
O\left((n_{h}+n_{h^{\prime}})\cdot\frac{1}{{\tilde{\epsilon}}_{g}^{2}}\log\frac{2n\gamma}{\delta}\right)=O\left((n_{h}+n_{h^{\prime}})\cdot\frac{1}{{\tilde{\epsilon}}_{g}^{2}}\log\frac{n}{\delta}\right)
.
Therefore, the total sample complexity for one iteration of the while loop can be upper bounded by
O\left(\left(\sum_{h\in[\gamma]}n_{h}\right)\cdot\frac{1}{{\tilde{\epsilon}}_{g}^{2}}\log\frac{n}{\delta}\right)
which is
O\left(\frac{n}{{\tilde{\epsilon}}_{g}^{2}}\log\frac{n}{\delta}\right)
.
Note that this is the sample complexity for every iteration of the
while
loop.
Therefore, the total sample complexity of the algorithm can be upper bounded by
O\left(\frac{n\log\gamma}{{\tilde{\epsilon}}_{g}^{2}}\log\frac{n}{\delta}\right)
.

Therefore, the total sample complexity is,

\displaystyle{\mathcal{O}}\left(\left(\sum_{h=1}^{\gamma}\frac{n_{h}}{{\tilde{\epsilon}}_{h}^{2}}\log\frac{n}{\delta}\right)+\left(\frac{n\log\gamma}{{\tilde{\epsilon}}_{g}^{2}}\log\frac{n}{\delta}\right)\right)

Note that neither of these terms strictly dominates the other for all problem instances.
Substituting
{\tilde{\epsilon}}_{h}=\frac{\epsilon_{h}}{2}\cdot\left(\frac{2}{n_{h}}\right)^{1/p}
we get that the sample complexity is,

\displaystyle{\mathcal{O}}\left(\left(\sum_{h=1}^{\gamma}\frac{n_{h}^{1+\frac{2}{p}}}{\epsilon_{h}^{2}}\log\frac{n}{\delta}\right)+\left(\frac{n\cdot n_{g}^{2/p}\cdot\log\gamma}{\epsilon_{g}^{2}}\log\frac{n}{\delta}\right)\right).

∎

8
to get

Pr_{{\tilde{S}}_{h}^{*}}({\mathcal{E}}_{h}(S^{*}_{h}))<\frac{\delta}{{n_{h}/2\choose n_{h}/4}},\forall h\in[\gamma]{\setminus}\Gamma.

(25)

From
Lemma

10
we then have that,
KL(\nu^{B}_{S^{*}_{h}}),\nu^{B}_{{\tilde{S}}_{h}^{*}})\leq 64{\tilde{\epsilon}}_{h}^{2}
for every
h\in[\gamma]{\setminus}\Gamma
and for every
B\in{\mathcal{B}}_{h}
.
Therefore, applying
Lemma

6
for every group
h\in[\gamma]{\setminus}\Gamma
we get,

\displaystyle\sum_{\{B\in{\mathcal{B}}_{h}\}}{\mathbf{E}}_{\nu^{B}_{S_{h}^{*}}}[N_{B}(\tau_{A})]KL(\nu^{B}_{S_{h}^{*}},\nu^{B}_{{\tilde{S}}_{h}^{*}})\leq\sum_{\{B\in{\mathcal{B}}_{h}\}}{\mathbf{E}}_{\nu^{B}_{S_{h}^{*}}}[N_{B}(\tau_{A})]{\frac{n_{h}}{2}-1\choose\frac{n_{h}}{4}-1}64{\tilde{\epsilon}}^{2}.

Finally noting that
\tau_{A}^{(h)}\geq\sum_{B\in{\mathcal{B}}_{h}}[N_{B}(\tau_{A})]
, we get

\displaystyle{\frac{n_{h}}{2}-1\choose\frac{n_{h}}{4}-1}64{\tilde{\epsilon}}^{2}{\mathbf{E}}_{\nu^{B}_{S_{h}^{*}}}[\tau_{A}^{(h)}]=\sum_{S\in{\mathcal{B}}_{h}}{\mathbf{E}}_{\nu^{B}_{S_{h}^{*}}}[N_{B}(\tau_{A}^{(h)})]({\frac{n_{h}}{2}-1\choose\frac{n_{h}}{4}-1}64{\tilde{\epsilon}}^{2})\geq{\frac{n_{h}}{2}\choose\frac{n_{h}}{4}}\ln\frac{{\frac{n_{h}}{2}\choose\frac{n_{h}}{4}}}{4\delta}.

Therefore,

\displaystyle{\mathbf{E}}[\tau_{A}^{(h)}]

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{{\tilde{\epsilon}}_{h}^{2}}\frac{n_{h}/2}{n_{h}/4}\ln\frac{{\frac{n_{h}}{2}\choose\frac{n_{h}}{4}}}{4\delta}=\frac{2}{{\tilde{\epsilon}}^{2}}\ln\frac{{\frac{n_{h}}{2}\choose\frac{n_{h}}{4}}}{\delta}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{2}{{\tilde{\epsilon}}_{h}^{2}}\ln\frac{2^{n_{h}/2}\sqrt{6}}{\sqrt{\pi\left(3n_{h}/2+2\right)}\delta}=\frac{n_{h}}{{\tilde{\epsilon}}_{h}^{2}}+\frac{1}{{\tilde{\epsilon}}_{h}^{2}}\ln\frac{6}{\pi\left(3n_{h}/2+2\right)\delta}\geq\frac{n_{h}}{{\tilde{\epsilon}}_{h}^{2}}.

Since
\tau_{A}\geq\sum_{h\in[\gamma]{\setminus}\Gamma}\tau_{A}^{(h)}
, we get,

\displaystyle{\mathbf{E}}[\tau_{A}]\geq\sum_{h\in[\gamma]{\setminus}\Gamma}{\mathbf{E}}[\tau_{A}^{(h)}]\geq\sum_{h\in[\gamma]{\setminus}\Gamma}\frac{n_{h}}{{\tilde{\epsilon}}_{h}^{2}}\geq\frac{1}{2}\sum_{h\in[\gamma]}\frac{n_{h}}{{\tilde{\epsilon}}_{h}^{2}}.

Therefore, the sample complexity to figure out the group-wise rankings is
\Omega\left(\sum_{h\in[\gamma]}\frac{n_{h}}{{\tilde{\epsilon}}_{h}^{2}}\right)
.

∎
