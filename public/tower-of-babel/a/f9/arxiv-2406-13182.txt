The saddlepoint approximation factors over sample paths of recursively compounded processes
Jesse Goodman

Abstract
This paper presents an identity between the multivariate and univariate saddlepoint approximations applied to sample path probabilities for a certain class of stochastic processes. This class, which we term the recursively compounded processes , includes branching processes and other models featuring sums of a random number of i.i.d. terms; and compound Poisson processes and other Lévy processes in which the additive parameter is itself chosen randomly. For such processes, f ^ X 1 , … , X N | X 0 = x 0 ​ ( x 1 , … , x N ) = ∏ n = 1 N f ^ X n | X 0 = x 0 , … , X n − 1 = x n − 1 ​ ( x n ) , \hat{f}_{X_{1},\dotsc,X_{N}\,|\,X_{0}=x_{0}}(x_{1},\dotsc,x_{N})=\prod_{n=1}^{N}\hat{f}_{X_{n}\,|\,X_{0}=x_{0},\dotsc,X_{n-1}=x_{n-1}}(x_{n}), where the left-hand side is a multivariate saddlepoint approximation applied to the random vector ( X 1 , … , X N ) (X_{1},\dotsc,X_{N}) and the right-hand side is a product of univariate saddlepoint approximations applied to the conditional one-step distributions given the past. Two proofs are given. The first proof is analytic, based on a change-of-variables identity linking the functions that arise in the respective saddlepoint approximations. The second proof is probabilistic, based on a representation of the saddlepoint approximation in terms of tilted distributions, changes of measure, and relative entropies.



1
Introduction

The saddlepoint approximation provides a systematic and explicit method for approximating the unknown probability density function
f(x)
of a random variable
X
whose moment generating function is known.
Namely, define

M_{X}(s)=\mathbb{E}(e^{sX}),\qquad K_{X}(s)=\log M_{X}(s),

(1.1)

the
moment generating function
(MGF) and
cumulant generating function
(CGF), respectively.
For given
x
, we seek a solution
\hat{s}
to the
saddlepoint equation

K^{\prime}_{X}(\hat{s})=x.

(1.2)

If such a solution exists, we call
\hat{s}
the
saddlepoint
and we define

\hat{f}_{X}(x)=\frac{\exp\left(K_{X}(\hat{s})-\hat{s}x\right)}{\sqrt{2\pi K_{X}^{\prime\prime}(\hat{s})}},

(1.3)

the
saddlepoint approximation
to the probability density function of
X
.

The saddlepoint approximation has a straightforward generalisation to the multivariate case.
The multivariate MGF and CGF for
X_{1},\dotsc,X_{N}
are

\displaystyle M_{X_{1},\dotsc,X_{N}}(s_{1},\dotsc,s_{N})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}(e^{s_{1}X_{1}+\dotsb+s_{N}X_{N}}),

(1.4)

\displaystyle K_{X_{1},\dotsc,X_{N}}(s_{1},\dotsc,s_{N})

\displaystyle=\log M_{X_{1},\dotsc,X_{N}}(s_{1},\dotsc,s_{N}),

and we write
K^{\prime}_{X_{1},\dotsc,X_{N}}(s_{1},\dotsc,s_{N})
,
K^{\prime\prime}_{X_{1},\dotsc,X_{N}}(s_{1},\dotsc,s_{N})
for the gradient (column) vector and Hessian matrix, respectively, both of which are defined everywhere in the interior of the domain.
The saddlepoint equation for
\hat{s}_{1},\dotsc,\hat{s}_{N}
reads

K^{\prime}_{X_{1},\dotsc,X_{N}}(\hat{s}_{1},\dotsc,\hat{s}_{N})=\begin{pmatrix}x_{1}\\
\vdots\\
x_{N}\end{pmatrix}

(1.5)

and, if a solution exists, the saddlepoint approximation becomes

\hat{f}_{X_{1},\dotsc,X_{N}}(x_{1},\dotsc,x_{N})=\frac{\exp\bigl(K_{X_{1},\dotsc,X_{N}}(\hat{s}_{1},\dotsc,\hat{s}_{N})-\sum_{n=1}^{N}\hat{s}_{n}x_{n}\bigr)}{\sqrt{\det\bigl(2\pi K^{\prime\prime}_{X_{1},\dotsc,X_{N}}(\hat{s}_{1},\dotsc,\hat{s}_{N})\bigr)}}.

(1.6)

The saddlepoint approximation can be applied equally to integer-valued random variables, with
\hat{f}_{X}(x)
yielding an approximation to the probability mass function
f_{X}(x)=\mathbb{P}(X=x)
rather than the probability density function.

More generally, the saddlepoint approximation can be applied in various ways to conditional distributions, marginal distributions, and so on, with the choice often determined in practice by which distributions have readily computable CGFs.
See
[
1
]
for a range of examples.
In this paper, we consider a class of processes for which we can compute both a multivariate CGF and CGFs for certain conditional distributions, yielding two different ways in which we can apply the saddlepoint approximation.
We illustrate with an example.

Example 1

(Galton-Watson branching process)
.

Let
(X_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}}
be a Galton-Watson branching process defined recursively by

X_{n}=\sum_{j=1}^{X_{n-1}}Y^{(j)}_{n},\quad n\in\mathbb{N},

(1.7)

where
X_{0}=x_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
is fixed and
Y^{(j)}_{n}
,
j,n\in\mathbb{N}
, are i.i.d. copies of a random variable
Y
defining the offspring distribution.
The offspring distribution is typically selected in a simple way with a known CGF
K_{Y}(s)
.

For fixed
x_{1},\dotsc,x_{N}
, the sample path probability
\mathbb{P}\left(\left.X_{1}=x_{1},\dotsc,X_{N}=x_{N}\,\right|X_{0}=x_{0}\right)
factors using the Markov property as

f_{X_{1},\dotsc,X_{N}\,|\,X_{0}=x_{0}}(x_{1},\dotsc,x_{N})=\prod_{n=1}^{N}f_{X_{n}\,|\,X_{n-1}=x_{n-1}}(x_{n}).

(1.8)

Given
X_{n-1}=x_{n-1}
, the conditional distribution of
X_{n}
is that of a sum of
x_{n-1}
i.i.d. copies of the offspring distribution
Y
.
The corresponding CGF
K_{X_{n}\,|\,X_{n-1}=x_{n-1}}(s)=x_{n-1}K_{Y}(s)
is known, so we may use
N
univariate saddlepoint approximations to obtain separate approximations
\hat{f}_{X_{n}\,|\,X_{n-1}=x_{n-1}}(x_{n})
to each transition probability
\mathbb{P}\left(\left.X_{n}=x_{n}\,\right|X_{n-1}=x_{n-1}\right)
in the product from (
1.8
).

Alternatively, we may deal directly with the correlated random vector
(X_{1},\dotsc,X_{N})
.
The multivariate CGF has an explicit, if slightly complicated, form:

K_{X_{1},\dotsc,X_{N}\,|\,X_{0}=x_{0}}(s_{1},\dotsc,s_{N})=x_{0}K_{Y}(s_{1}+K_{Y}(s_{2}+K_{Y}(\dotsb+K_{Y}(s_{N-1}+K_{Y}(s_{N}))\dotsb))).

(1.9)

We may therefore use a single multivariate saddlepoint approximation to obtain a direct approximation
\hat{f}_{X_{1},\dotsc,X_{N}\,|\,X_{0}=x_{0}}(x_{1},\dotsc,x_{N})
to the sample path probability in (
1.8
).

The main result of this paper will show that for this branching process, and for other processes with a similar recursive structure, these two seemingly different approximations are equal.

2
Main result

2.1
Recursively compounded processes

We consider random processes featuring
compound sums
formed from a random number of i.i.d. summands, similar to
Example
1
.
The general definition allows for additional independent terms, time inhomogeneity, dependence on the entire history, non-integer values, and random variables of differing dimensions.

Definition 2
.

We call a process
(X_{0},X_{1},\dotsc,X_{N})

recursively compounded
if, for each
n=1,\dotsc,N
, the conditional distribution of
X_{n}
given
X_{0},\dotsc,X_{n-1}
is the same as
Q_{n}(X_{0},\dotsc,X_{n-1})
, where

Q_{n}(x_{0},\dotsc,x_{n-1})=\xi_{n}+\sum_{m=0}^{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^{d_{m}}Z_{n,m,i}(x_{m,i});

(2.1)

where
\xi_{n}
is a random variable and
Z_{n,m,i}(t)
are processes, all with values in
\mathbb{R}^{d_{n}}
and all independent of each other and of
X_{0},\dotsc,X_{n-1}
; and where, for each
m<n
and each
i\leq d_{m}
, one or more of the following conditions holds:

(a)

X_{m,i}
takes values in
\mathbb{Z}_{+}
and
Z_{n,m,i}(t)
is defined for
t\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
by

Z_{n,m,i}(t)=\sum_{j=1}^{t}Y_{n,m,i,j},

(2.2)

where
Y_{n,m,i,j}
,
j\in\mathbb{N}
, are i.i.d. with values in
\mathbb{R}^{d_{n}}
, independent of everything else;

(b)

X_{m,i}
takes values in
\left[0,\infty\right)
and
(Z_{n,m,i}(t))_{t\in\left[0,\infty\right)}
is a Lévy process with values in
\mathbb{R}^{d_{n}}
, independent of everything else; or

(c)

X_{m,i}
has arbitrary values and
Z_{n,m,i}(t)=ct
for some constant
c\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{n}}
.

Note that in (
2.2
) and elsewhere, sums
\sum_{j=n}^{m}a_{j}
are taken to be identically 0 when
m=n-1
.
The multivariate MGF
M_{X_{1},\dotsc,X_{N}}(s_{1},\dotsc,s_{N})
and the resulting saddlepoint approximation have a simple extension to allow each
X_{n}
to be vector-valued, see the discussion in
Section
3.1
.

2.2
Main result: sample path probabilities

The main result of this paper is that, applied to a sample path probability for a recursively compounded process, the saddlepoint approximation can be factored across steps.

Theorem 3
.

Let
(X_{0},X_{1},\dotsc,X_{N})
be a recursively compounded process and let
x_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{n}}
for all
n
.
Then

\hat{f}_{X_{1},\dotsc,X_{N}\,|\,X_{0}=x_{0}}(x_{1},\dotsc,x_{N})=\prod_{n=1}^{N}\hat{f}_{X_{n}\,|\,X_{0}=x_{0},\dotsc,X_{n-1}=x_{n-1}}(x_{n}).

(2.3)

In (
2.3
), each factor on the right-hand side is a saddlepoint approximation applied to the conditional distribution of
X_{n}
given
X_{m}=x_{m}
for
m<n
.
In the notation of
Definition
2
,

\hat{f}_{X_{n}\,|\,X_{0}=x_{0},\dotsc,X_{n-1}=x_{n-1}}(x_{n})=\hat{f}_{Q_{n}(x_{0},\dotsc,x_{n-1})}(x_{n}),

(2.4)

the saddlepoint approximation evaluated at
x_{n}
for the random variable
Q_{n}(x_{0},\dotsc,x_{n-1})
.

Remark 4
.

Part of the assertion of
Theorem
3
is that the two sides of (
2.3
) have the same domain.
That is, if one side is undefined (either because the corresponding saddlepoint
\hat{s}
does not exist or because the determinant from (
1.6
) vanishes) then so is the other, and vice versa.

2.3
Examples

Example 5
.

The general form of a recursively compounded process with non-negative-integer-valued random variables, as in
Definition
2

(a)
, could be described as a time-inhomogeneous age-dependent discrete-time branching process with immigration.
If the process is vector-valued, the branching process is a multitype branching process.
The random variables
\xi_{n}
describe the immigration, and the random variables
Y^{(j)}_{n,n-a,i}
describe the offspring distribution at time
n
for individuals of type
i
and current age
a\in\mathbb{N}
.

Example 6
.

Pedeli, Davison and Fokianos
[
4
]
model a time series of non-negative-integer valued data as an integer-valued auto-regressive process of order
p
by recursively setting

X_{n}=\xi_{n}+\mathrm{Binomial}(X_{n-1},\alpha_{1})+\dotsb+\mathrm{Binomial}(X_{n-p},\alpha_{p}),

(2.5)

where
\xi_{n}
are i.i.d. innovations and where all terms are chosen conditionally independently at each step.
This is a recursively compounded process as in
Definition
2

(a)
:
Y_{n,m,1}
has the Bernoulli
(\alpha_{n-m})
distribution if
n-p\leq m<n
, with
Y_{n,m,1}=0
for
m<n-p
.
(Slight adjustments might be needed to handle the first
p
time series values, which in
[
4
]
are taken as fixed.
This can be accommodated in the setup of
Definition
2
by storing
p
initial values
X_{0},X_{-1},\dotsc,X_{1-p}
as an initial vector
\vec{X}_{0}
.)

The probabilities
\alpha_{1},\dotsc,\alpha_{p}
play the role of autoregressive coefficients, to be estimated based on data.
Due to the integer-valued nature of the model, standard estimators based on Gaussian regression may be unsatisfactory, and maximum-likelihood-based estimators are proposed instead.
Computing the likelihood means computing the sample path probability
\mathbb{P}\left(\left.X_{1}=x_{1},\dotsc,X_{N}=x_{N}\,\right|\smash{\vec{X}_{0}=\vec{x}_{0}}\right)
, where
x_{1},\dotsc,x_{N}
are observed values from a time series (after storing the first
p
time series values in
\vec{x}_{0}
).
Exact calculation becomes impractical once
p
is even moderately large, but the saddlepoint approximation remains computable, so the authors use (
2.3
) as an approximate likelihood function and derive an estimator from it.

Theorem
3
shows that this approximate likelihood, and the corresponding estimator, are equivalent whether computed by a multivariate or a step-wise approach, as in the left- and right-hand sides, respectively, of (
2.3
).

Example 7
.

Davison, Hautphenne and Kraus
[
2
]
perform inference on birth and death rates for a population modelled by a continuous-time branching process observed at discrete times.
The branching property implies that this process is a recursively compounded process, and indeed it reduces to a Galton-Watson branching process as in
Example
1
if the observation times are equally spaced.
Similar to
Example
6
, the authors use the saddlepoint approximation to obtain approximations to a sample path probability, which is interpreted an approximate likelihood, and then maximise this approximate likelihood to obtain saddlepoint-based estimates for the birth and death rates.

The saddlepoint approximation can be applied either in univariate form to approximate the separate transition probabilities, or in multivariate form to approximate the sample path probabilities.
The authors implement both approaches
[
2
, Web Appendix C]
, with the univariate saddlepoint equations solved algebraically and the multivariate saddlepoint equation solved numerically.
The two approaches yielded similar calculated values, in accordance with
Theorem
3
, though the non-trivial numerical errors suggest the potential delicacy of solving multivariate saddlepoint equations numerically.

3
Proofs

We give two proofs of
Theorem
3
, one analytic and one probabilistic.
Both proofs rely on the structure of recursively compounded processes as expressed via CGFs.
We begin by stating vector and derivative conventions for multivariate MGFs and other functions.

3.1
Conventions for vectors, gradients, Jacobians, and Hessians

We allow each
X_{n}
to have vector values,
X_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{n}}
, so that the MGF
M_{X_{n}}(s_{n})
and CGF
K_{X_{n}}(s_{n})
become functions of a vector-valued argument
s_{n}
of the same dimension.
To match with (
1.1
), we follow the vector convention of
[
3
]
:
X_{n}
is interpreted as a column vector or
d_{n}\times 1
matrix, and
s_{n}
is a row vector or
1\times d_{n}
matrix, so that
s_{n}X_{n}
is a
1\times 1
matrix, i.e., a scalar.
With this convention,
M_{X_{n}}(s_{n})=\mathbb{E}(e^{s_{n}X_{n}})=\mathbb{E}(e^{s_{n,1}X_{n,1}+\dotsb+s_{n,d_{n}}X_{n,d_{n}}})
is the multivariate MGF of
X_{n}
, where
X_{n,i}
and
s_{n,i}
,
i=1,\dotsc,d_{n}
, denote the entries of the vectors
X_{n}
and
s_{n}
, respectively.
The multivariate MGF
M_{X_{1},\dotsc,X_{N}}(s_{1},\dotsc,s_{N})
from (
1.4
) coincides with the multivariate MGF of the concatenated vector
\vec{X}=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}X_{1}\\
\vdots\\
X_{N}\end{smallmatrix}\right)
with vector argument
\vec{s}=\begin{pmatrix}s_{1}&\dotsb&s_{N}\end{pmatrix}
, interpreted as column and row vectors, respectively, both written in block form and having dimension
D=\sum_{n=1}^{N}d_{n}
.

Let
\varphi(s_{1},\dotsc,s_{N})
be a function with
k
-dimensional row vector values depending on row vectors
s_{1},\dotsc,s_{N}
of dimensions
d_{1},\dotsc,d_{N}
.
By
\varphi^{\prime}(s_{1},\dotsc,s_{N})
we mean the
D\times k
matrix of partial derivatives
\frac{\partial\varphi_{j}}{\partial s_{m,i}}
, with rows indexed by the entries
s_{m,i}
(ordered lexicographically as
s_{1,1},\dotsc,s_{1,d_{1}}
;
s_{2,1},\dotsc,s_{2,d_{2}};\dotsc;s_{N,1},\dotsc,s_{N,d_{N}}
) and columns indexed by the entries of
\varphi
.
Thus when
\varphi
has scalar values,
\varphi^{\prime}
means the gradient, interpreted as a column vector; when
\varphi
has vector values,
\varphi^{\prime}
means the Jacobian matrix (or possibly its transpose, depending on one’s preferred row and column conventions for Jacobians).
When
\varphi
has scalar values,
\varphi^{\prime\prime}(s_{1},\dotsc,s_{N})
means the
D\times D
Hessian matrix of mixed partial derivatives
\frac{\partial^{2}\varphi}{\partial s_{m,i}\partial s_{n,j}}
.

With these conventions, the saddlepoint approximation uses almost identical notation when applied to vector-valued random variables, except that the denominator of (
1.3
) is replaced by
\sqrt{\det(2\pi K^{\prime\prime}_{X}(\hat{s}))}
similar to (
1.6
).
In the remainder of the paper,
X_{n}
is vector-valued of dimension
d_{n}
, but the reader may restrict their attention to scalar-valued
X_{n}
with virtually no change to the text.

3.2
The multivariate CGF of
X_{0},\dotsc,X_{N}

The key feature uniting
Definition
2

(a)
–
(c)
is that the CGFs for
Z_{n,m,i}(t)
depend linearly on
t
.
Indeed, it is easy to see that
Z_{n,m,i}(t+t^{\prime})
has the same distribution as the sum of two independent random variables with distributions
Z_{n,m,i}(t)
and
Z_{n,m,i}(t^{\prime})
; here we assume that
t,t^{\prime}
belong to
\mathbb{Z}_{+}
,
\left[0,\infty\right)
, or
\mathbb{R}
, depending on which of
(a)
–
(c)
applies.
Thus

\displaystyle M_{Z_{n,m,i}(t+t^{\prime})}(s_{n})

\displaystyle=M_{Z_{n,m,i}(t)}(s_{n})M_{Z_{n,m,i}(t^{\prime})}(s_{n}),

(3.1)

\displaystyle K_{Z_{n,m,i}(t+t^{\prime})}(s_{n})

\displaystyle=K_{Z_{n,m,i}(t)}(s_{n})+K_{Z_{n,m,i}(t^{\prime})}(s_{n}),

leading to

K_{Z_{n,m,i}(t)}(s)=tK_{Z_{n,m,i}(1)}(s)

(3.2)

for all applicable
t
.

Similarly, the CGF of
Q_{n}(x_{0},\dotsc,x_{n-1})
is affine in
x_{0},\dotsc,x_{n-1}
.
To state this compactly in vector notation, define
g_{n,m}(s_{n})
for
m<n
to be the
d_{m}
-dimensional row vector whose
i^{\text{th}}
entry
g_{n,m,i}(s_{n})
is given by

g_{n,m,i}(s_{n})=K_{Z_{n,m,i}(1)}(s_{n}).

(3.3)

Then, by
Definition
2
and (
3.2
),

\displaystyle K_{Q_{n}(x_{0},\dotsc,x_{n-1})}(s_{n})

\displaystyle=K_{\xi_{n}}(s_{n})+\sum_{m=0}^{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^{d_{m}}K_{Z_{n,m,i}(1)}(s_{n})x_{m,i}

\displaystyle=K_{\xi_{n}}(s_{n})+\sum_{m=0}^{n-1}g_{n,m}(s_{n})x_{m}.

(3.4)

Lemma 8
.

For
m=N,N-1,\dotsc,1,0
, let the functions
\tau_{m}(s_{m},\dotsc,s_{N})
with
d_{m}
-dimensional row vector values be defined recursively by
\tau_{N}(s_{N})=s_{N}
and

\tau_{m}(s_{m},\dotsc,s_{N})=s_{m}+\sum_{n=m+1}^{N}g_{n,m}(\tau_{n}(s_{n},\dotsc,s_{N}))\quad\text{for }0\leq m<N.

(3.5)

Then

\displaystyle K_{X_{0},\dotsc,X_{N}}(s_{0},\dotsc,s_{N})=K_{X_{0}}(\tau_{0}(s_{0},\dotsc,s_{N}))+\sum_{n=1}^{N}K_{\xi_{n}}(\tau_{n}(s_{n},\dotsc,s_{N})).

(3.6)

The proof of
Lemma
8
is conceptually simple, recursively applying the conditional distributions from
Definition
2
to simplify the MGF and CGF.
The details and notation become slightly intricate and will reappear only in the proof of
Lemma
15
below.

Proof.

Condition on the past before step
n
and use (
3.4
) to obtain

\displaystyle M_{X_{0},\dotsc,X_{n}}(s_{0},\dotsc,s_{n})

\displaystyle\quad=\mathbb{E}\left(\exp\left(\sum_{m=0}^{n-1}s_{m}X_{m}\right)\mathbb{E}\left(\left.e^{s_{n}X_{n}}\,\right|X_{0},\dotsc,X_{n-1}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\quad=\mathbb{E}\left(\exp\left(\sum_{m=0}^{n-1}s_{m}X_{m}\right)\left.\exp\left(K_{\xi_{n}}(s_{n})+\sum_{m=0}^{n-1}g_{n,m}(s_{n})x_{m}\right)\right|_{x_{m}=X_{m}\,\forall m<n}\right)

\displaystyle\quad=\exp\left(K_{\xi_{n}}(s_{n})\right)\mathbb{E}\left(\exp\left(\sum_{m=0}^{n-1}[s_{m}+g_{n,m}(s_{n})]X_{m}\right)\right).

(3.7)

Taking logarithms of both sides, the final equation can be equivalently written as

K_{X_{0},\dotsc,X_{n}}(s_{0},\dotsc,s_{n})=K_{\xi_{n}}(s_{n})+K_{X_{0},\dotsc,X_{n-1}}(s_{0}+g_{n,0}(s_{n}),\dotsc,s_{n-1}+g_{n,n-1}(s_{n})).

(3.8)

To complete the proof, we show by induction that for
n=N,N-1,\dotsc,1,0
,

K_{X_{0},\dotsc,X_{N}}(s_{0},\dotsc,s_{N})=K_{X_{0},\dotsc,X_{n}}(\rho_{n,0},\dotsc,\rho_{n,n})+\sum_{m=n+1}^{N}K_{\xi_{m}}(\tau_{m}(s_{m},\dotsc,s_{N})),

(3.9)

where we have abbreviated

\rho_{n,m}=\rho_{n,m}(s_{m},\dotsc,s_{N})=s_{m}+\sum_{k=n+1}^{N}g_{k,m}(\tau_{k}(s_{k},\dotsc,s_{N})),\quad 0\leq m\leq n\leq N,

(3.10)

and where for
n=N
we interpret the empty sums as
0
.
Thus in the base case
n=N
we have
\rho_{N,m}(s_{m},\dotsc,s_{N})=s_{m}
and the sum in (
3.9
) vanishes.
In particular, (
3.9
) holds trivially for
n=N
.

To advance the induction, suppose (
3.9
) holds for a given value
n\in\left\{1,\dotsc,N\right\}
.
Apply (
3.8
) with
s_{m}
replaced by
\rho_{n,m}
to obtain

\displaystyle K_{X_{0},\dotsc,X_{N}}(s_{0},\dotsc,s_{N})

\displaystyle=K_{\xi_{n}}(\rho_{n,n})+\sum_{m=n+1}^{N}K_{\xi_{m}}(\tau_{m}(s_{m},\dotsc,s_{N}))

\displaystyle\quad+K_{X_{0},\dotsc,X_{n-1}}\left(\rho_{n,0}+g_{n,0}(\rho_{n,n}),\dotsc,\rho_{n,n-1}+g_{n,n-1}(\rho_{n,n})\right).

(3.11)

Comparing (
3.5
) and (
3.10
), we see that

\rho_{n,n}=s_{n}+\sum_{k=n+1}^{N}g_{k,n}(\tau_{k}(s_{k},\dotsc,s_{N}))=\tau_{n}(s_{n},\dotsc,s_{N}),

(3.12)

so the first term in the right-hand side of (
3.11
) can be merged into the summation as the
m=n
term.
Similarly

\rho_{n,m}+g_{n,m}(\tau_{n}(s_{n},\dotsc,s_{N}))=s_{m}+\sum_{k=n}^{N}g_{k,m}(\tau_{k}(s_{k+1},\dotsc,s_{N}))=\rho_{n-1,m},

(3.13)

so that (
3.11
) reduces to

K_{X_{0},\dotsc,X_{N}}(s_{0},\dotsc,s_{N})=\sum_{m=n}^{N}K_{\xi_{m}}(\tau_{m}(s_{m},\dotsc,s_{N}))+K_{X_{0},\dotsc,X_{n-1}}\left(\rho_{n-1,0},\dotsc,\rho_{n-1,n-1}\right),

(3.14)

which is (
3.9
) with
n
replaced by
n-1
.
By induction, (
3.9
) holds for all
0\leq n<N
.
Applying (
3.12
) with
n=0
gives
\rho_{0,0}=\tau_{0}(s_{0},\dotsc,s_{N})
, so (
3.9
) with
n=0

3.3
First proof of
Theorem
3

Henceforth, fix
x_{0}
and
x_{1},\dotsc,x_{N}
, and write
\vec{x}
for the column vector of dimension
D=\sum_{n=1}^{N}d_{n}
formed by concatenating the column vectors
x_{1},\dotsc,x_{N}
.
For notational convenience, we will abbreviate
Q_{1}(x_{0}),\dotsc,Q_{N}(x_{0},\dotsc,x_{N-1})
as
Q_{1},\dotsc,Q_{N}
.

In
Definition
2
, the conditional distribution of
(X_{1},\dotsc,X_{N})
is specified recursively in terms of
X_{0}
, which has an arbitrary starting distribution.
Conditioning on
X_{0}=x_{0}
is therefore equivalent to setting
X_{0}
to be the constant random variable with value
x_{0}
and CGF

K_{X_{0}}(s_{0})=s_{0}x_{0}.

(3.15)

We assume this choice of
X_{0}
henceforth.
Then

K_{X_{1},\dotsc,X_{N}\,|\,X_{0}=x_{0}}(s_{1},\dotsc,s_{N})=K_{X_{0},X_{1},\dotsc,X_{N}}(0,s_{1},\dotsc,s_{N})

(3.16)

and the left-hand side of (
2.3
) is the saddlepoint approximation arising from this CGF.
On the other hand, the product in the right-hand side of (

) can be interpreted as a multivariate saddlepoint approximation

\prod_{n=1}^{N}\hat{f}_{X_{n}\,|\,X_{0}=x_{0},\dotsc,X_{n-1}=x_{n-1}}(x_{n})=\hat{f}_{Q_{1},\dotsc,Q_{N}}(x_{1},\dotsc,x_{N})

(3.17)

where
Q_{1},\dotsc,Q_{N}
are independent and have multivariate CGF

K_{Q_{1},\dotsc,Q_{N}}(s_{1},\dotsc,s_{N})=\sum_{n=1}^{N}K_{Q_{n}}(s_{n}).

(3.18)

To see this, note that the Hessian matrix
K^{\prime\prime}_{Q_{1},\dotsc,Q_{N}}(s_{1},\dotsc,s_{N})
is block-diagonal with diagonal blocks
K_{Q_{n}}^{\prime\prime}(s_{n})
, so its determinant factors as a product of the determinants of its diagonal blocks.
Similarly, the gradient
K^{\prime}_{Q_{1},\dotsc,Q_{N}}(s_{1},\dotsc,s_{N})
is the concatenation of the gradients

, so the multivariate saddlepoint equation
K^{\prime}_{Q_{1},\dotsc,Q_{N}}(\hat{s}_{1},\dotsc,\hat{s}_{N})=\vec{x}
is equivalent to the system of
N
separate saddlepoint equations
K_{Q_{n}}^{\prime}(s_{n})=x_{n}
for
n=1,\dotsc,N
.

The proof of
Theorem
3
will proceed by relating the CGFs arising in these two multivariate saddlepoint approximations.
We begin with additional definitions to shorten and unify notation.

Since we have taken
X_{0}
to be deterministic, its effects can be merged with the random variables
\xi_{n}
by defining

\tilde{\xi}_{n}=Q_{n}(x_{0},0,\dotsc,0)=\xi_{n}+\sum_{i=1}^{d_{0}}Z_{n,0,i}(x_{0,i})\quad\text{for }n=1,\dotsc,N.

(3.19)

Note that our independence assumptions imply that
\tilde{\xi}_{1},\dotsc,\tilde{\xi}_{N}
are independent random variables.
By (

),

\displaystyle K_{\tilde{\xi}_{1},\dotsc,\tilde{\xi}_{N}}(s_{1},\dotsc,s_{N})=\sum_{n=1}^{N}K_{Q_{n}(x_{0},0,\dotsc,0)}(s_{n})=\sum_{n=1}^{N}\left(\big.K_{\xi_{n}}(s_{n})+g_{n,0}(s_{n})x_{0}\right).

(3.20)

Also define

T(s_{1},\dotsc,s_{N})=\begin{pmatrix}\tau_{1}(s_{1},\dotsc,s_{N})&\tau_{2}(s_{2},\dotsc,s_{N})&\dotsb&\tau_{N}(s_{N})\end{pmatrix},

(3.21)

i.e.,
T
is a mapping from
D
-dimensional row vectors to
D
-dimensional row vectors (in block form) whose

block is given by the function
\tau_{m}(s_{m},\dotsc,s_{N})
defined in

8
.
Likewise, define

to be the

-dimensional row vector whose

-dimensional row vector

(3.22)

With these definitions,

\displaystyle K_{X_{1},\dotsc,X_{N}\,|\,X_{0}=x_{0}}(s_{1},\dotsc,s_{N})

(3.23)

\displaystyle\quad=K_{X_{0}}(\tau_{0}(0,s_{1},\dotsc,s_{N}))+\sum_{n=1}^{N}K_{\xi_{n}}(\tau_{n}(s_{n},\dotsc,s_{N}))

by (

Lemma

\displaystyle\quad=\tau_{0}(0,s_{1},\dotsc,s_{N})x_{0}+\sum_{n=1}^{N}K_{\xi_{n}}(\tau_{n}(s_{n},\dotsc,s_{N}))

\displaystyle\quad=\sum_{n=1}^{N}\left(\big.K_{\xi_{n}}(\tau_{n}(s_{n},\dotsc,s_{N}))+g_{n,0}(\tau_{n}(s_{n},\dotsc,s_{N}))x_{0}\right)

by (

\displaystyle\quad=K_{\tilde{\xi}_{1},\dotsc,\tilde{\xi}_{N}}\circ T(s_{1},\dotsc,s_{N})

by (
3.20

3.21
)

whereas by independence

\displaystyle K_{Q_{1},\dotsc,Q_{N}}(s_{1},\dotsc,s_{N})=\sum_{n=1}^{N}K_{Q_{n}}(s_{n})

(3.24)

\displaystyle\quad=\sum_{n=1}^{N}\left(K_{\xi_{n}}(s_{n})+g_{n,0}(s_{n})x_{0}+\sum_{m=1}^{n-1}g_{n,m}(s_{n})x_{m}\right)

\displaystyle\quad=\sum_{n=1}^{N}\left(K_{\xi_{n}}(s_{n})+g_{n,0}(s_{n})x_{0}\right)+\sum_{m=1}^{N}\sum_{n=m+1}^{N}g_{n,m}(s_{n})x_{m}

\displaystyle\quad=K_{\tilde{\xi}_{1},\dotsc,\tilde{\xi}_{N}}(s_{1},\dotsc,s_{N})+G(s_{1},\dotsc,s_{N})\vec{x}

\displaystyle\text{by \eqref{KtildexiFormula} and \eqref{GmFormula}}.

Moreover (

\tau_{1},\dotsc,\tau_{N}
, can be expressed as the vector identity

T(s_{1},\dotsc,s_{N})=\begin{pmatrix}s_{1}&\dotsb&s_{N}\end{pmatrix}+G\circ T(s_{1},\dotsc,s_{N}).

(3.25)

Lemma 9
.

The functions

\displaystyle(s_{1},\dotsc,s_{N})

\displaystyle\mapsto K_{X_{1},\dotsc,X_{N}\,|\,X_{0}=x_{0}}(s_{1},\dotsc,s_{N})-\sum_{n=1}^{N}s_{n}x_{n}

(3.26)

\displaystyle\text{and}\qquad(\tau_{1},\dotsc,\tau_{N})

\displaystyle\mapsto K_{Q_{1},\dotsc,Q_{N}}(\tau_{1},\dotsc,\tau_{N})-\sum_{n=1}^{N}\tau_{n}x_{n}

are identical under the change of variables
\begin{pmatrix}\tau_{1}&\dotsb&\tau_{N}\end{pmatrix}=T(s_{1},\dotsc,s_{N})
.

Proof.

By (
3.23
) and (
3.25
),

\displaystyle K_{X_{1},\dotsc,X_{N}\,|\,X_{0}=x_{0}}(s_{1},\dotsc,s_{N})-\sum_{n=1}^{N}s_{n}x_{n}

\displaystyle\quad=K_{\tilde{\xi}_{1},\dotsc,\tilde{\xi}_{N}}\circ T(s_{1},\dotsc,s_{N})-\begin{pmatrix}s_{1}&\dotsb&s_{N}\end{pmatrix}\vec{x}

by (
3.23
)

\displaystyle\quad=K_{\tilde{\xi}_{1},\dotsc,\tilde{\xi}_{N}}\circ T(s_{1},\dotsc,s_{N})-\left(\big.T(s_{1},\dotsc,s_{N})-G\circ T(s_{1},\dotsc,s_{N})\right)\vec{x}

by (

\displaystyle\quad=\bigl(K_{\tilde{\xi}_{1},\dotsc,\tilde{\xi}_{N}}\circ T(s_{1},\dotsc,s_{N})+G\circ T(s_{1},\dotsc,s_{N})\vec{x}\bigr)-T(s_{1},\dotsc,s_{N})\vec{x}

\displaystyle\quad=K_{Q_{1},\dotsc,Q_{N}}\circ T(s_{1},\dotsc,s_{N})-T(s_{1},\dotsc,s_{N})\vec{x}

\displaystyle\text{by \eqref{KQ1QNFormula}.}\qed

(3.27)

First proof of
Theorem
3
.

The mapping
T
is smooth, and the identity (
3.25
) shows that
T
has a smooth inverse

T^{-1}(\tau_{1},\dotsc,\tau_{N})=\begin{pmatrix}\tau_{1}&\dotsb&\tau_{N}\end{pmatrix}-G(\tau_{1},\dotsc,\tau_{N}).

(3.28)

In particular,
T
and
T^{-1}
have non-singular Jacobians.

Therefore, using
Lemma
9
, the functions
(s_{1},\dotsc,s_{N})\mapsto K_{X_{1},\dotsc,X_{N}\,|\,X_{0}=x_{0}}(s_{1},\dotsc,s_{N})-\sum_{n=1}^{N}s_{n}x_{n}
and
(\tau_{1},\dotsc,\tau_{N})\mapsto K_{Q_{1},\dotsc,Q_{N}}(\tau_{1},\dotsc,\tau_{N})-\sum_{n=1}^{N}\tau_{n}x_{n}
from (
3.26
) have the same critical points under the change of variables
\begin{pmatrix}\tau_{1}&\dotsb&\tau_{N}\end{pmatrix}=T(s_{1},\dotsc,s_{N})
.
These critical points correspond precisely to the saddlepoints
\hat{s}_{1}^{(X)},\dotsc,\hat{s}_{N}^{(X)}
and
\hat{\tau}_{1}^{(Q)},\dotsc,\hat{\tau}_{N}^{(Q)}
in the respective saddlepoint approximations.
Hence, for a given vector
\vec{x}
, whether we can solve the saddlepoint equations, and whether the solution is unique, is the same for both saddlepoint approximations.

The functions from (
3.26
) have Hessian matrices that reduce to
K^{\prime\prime}_{X_{1},\dotsc,X_{N}\,|\,X_{0}=x_{0}}(s_{1},\dotsc,s_{N})
and
K^{\prime\prime}_{Q_{1},\dotsc,Q_{N}}(\tau_{1},\dotsc,\tau_{N})
, respectively.
By
Lemma
9
, at their respective critical points, these Hessian matrices are related in terms of the Jacobian matrix
T^{\prime}
, namely

K^{\prime\prime}_{X_{1},\dotsc,X_{N}\,|\,X_{0}=x_{0}}\bigl(\hat{s}_{1}^{(X)},\dotsc,\hat{s}_{N}^{(X)}\bigr)\\
=T^{\prime}\bigl(\hat{s}_{1}^{(X)},\dotsc,\hat{s}_{N}^{(X)}\bigr)K^{\prime\prime}_{Q_{1},\dotsc,Q_{N}}\bigl(\hat{\tau}_{1}^{(Q)},\dotsc,\hat{\tau}_{N}^{(Q)}\bigr)T^{\prime}\bigl(\hat{s}_{1}^{(X)},\dotsc,\hat{s}_{N}^{(X)}\bigr)^{T}

(3.29)

where
T
denotes transpose.

For any
\mathbb{R}^{D}
-valued random variable
R
, the Hessian
K^{\prime\prime}_{R}(s)
is either singular for all
s
in its domain (if
R
is supported on a hyperplane of dimension
D-1
or lower) or positive definite for all
s
in its domain.
Therefore if one of the Hessians in (
3.29
) is singular, then both the Hessians
K^{\prime\prime}_{X_{1},\dotsc,X_{N}\,|\,X_{0}=x_{0}}(s_{1},\dotsc,s_{N})
and
K^{\prime\prime}_{Q_{1},\dotsc,Q_{N}}(\tau_{1},\dotsc,\tau_{N})
are identically singular, and the corresponding saddlepoint approximations are both undefined (or both equal to
\infty
, depending on our definition in this degenerate case).

Otherwise, the functions in (
3.26
) are both strictly convex and hence yield unique saddlepoints
\hat{s}_{1}^{(X)},\dotsc,\hat{s}_{N}^{(X)}
and
\hat{\tau}_{1}^{(Q)},\dotsc,\hat{\tau}_{N}^{(Q)}
.
By
Lemma
9
, the numerators in the corresponding multivariate saddlepoint approximations are equal.
Moreover, by (
3.29
) and the squareness of the Jacobian matrix
T^{\prime}
, the denominators are related by

\sqrt{\det\bigl(2\pi K^{\prime\prime}_{X_{1},\dotsc,X_{N}\,|\,X_{0}=x_{0}}(\hat{s}_{1}^{(X)},\dotsc,\hat{s}_{N}^{(X)})\bigr)}\\
=\sqrt{\det\bigl(2\pi K^{\prime\prime}_{Q_{1},\dotsc,Q_{N}}(\hat{\tau}_{1}^{(Q)},\dotsc,\hat{\tau}_{N}^{(Q)})\bigr)\det\bigl(T^{\prime}(\hat{s}_{1}^{(X)},\dotsc,\hat{s}_{N}^{(X)})\bigr)^{2}}.

(3.30)

By (
3.21
) and (
3.5
), the block entries of
T
have the form
\tau_{n}(s_{n},\dotsc,s_{N})=s_{n}+h_{n}(s_{n+1},\dotsc,s_{N})
, where
h_{n}
depends only on
s_{m}
for
m>n
.
Consequently the Jacobian matrix
T^{\prime}
is lower-triangular with ones on the diagonal,

T^{\prime}=\begin{pmatrix}I_{d_{1}\times d_{1}}&0&\dotsb&0&0\vphantom{I_{d_{N}\times d_{N}}}\\
\nabla_{s_{2}}h_{1}&I_{d_{2}\times d_{2}}&\makebox[33.10362pt]{\smash{$\ddots$}}&0&0\vphantom{I_{d_{N}\times d_{N}}}\\
\smash{\vdots}&\smash{\vdots}&\makebox[33.10362pt]{\smash{$\ddots$}}&0&0\vphantom{I_{d_{N}\times d_{N}}}\\
\nabla_{s_{N-1}}h_{1}&\nabla_{s_{N-1}}h_{2}&\dotsb&\makebox[33.10362pt]{$I_{d_{N-1}\times d_{N-1}}$}&0\\
\nabla_{s_{N}}h_{1}&\nabla_{s_{N}}h_{2}&\dotsb&\nabla_{s_{N}}h_{N-1}&\makebox[33.10362pt]{$I_{d_{N}\times d_{N}}$}\end{pmatrix}

(3.31)

in block form, where the diagonal blocks
I_{d_{n}\times d_{n}}
denote identity matrices of the indicated sizes.
In particular,
\det(T^{\prime})=1
, and therefore the denominators of the multivariate saddlepoint approximations
\hat{f}_{X_{1},\dotsc,X_{N}\,|\,X_{0}=x_{0}}(x_{1},\dotsc,x_{N})
and
\hat{f}_{Q_{1},\dotsc,Q_{N}}(x_{1},\dotsc,x_{N})
are also equal.
Along with (
3.17
), this completes the proof.
∎

3.4
Second proof of
Theorem
3

The second proof interprets the saddlepoint approximation in terms of distributional operations.

Definition 10

(Exponential tilting)
.

Let
X
be a random variable with values in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and let
s
be a row vector of dimension
d
such that
M_{X}(s)
is defined.
A random variable
X^{(s)}
has the
tilted
distribution (corresponding to
X
and
s
) if

\mathbb{E}(h(X^{(s)}))=\frac{\mathbb{E}(h(X)e^{sX})}{M_{X}(s)}

(3.32)

for all functions
h\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
for which the latter expectation is defined.
Equivalently, if we write
\mu_{X}
for the measure defined by
\mu_{X}(A)=\mathbb{P}(X\in A)
, then the distribution of
X^{(s)}
is specified by the Radon-Nikodym derivative

\frac{d\mu_{X^{(s)}}}{d\mu_{X}}(x)=\frac{e^{sx}}{M_{X}(s)}\quad\text{for $\mu_{X}$-almost-every $x$}.

(3.33)

For instance, if
X
is a continuous random variable with density function
f_{X}(x)
with respect to Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, then
X^{(s)}
has density function
f_{X^{(s)}}(x)=f_{X}(x)e^{sx}/M_{X}(s)
.

Note that
Definition
10
deals with distributions, rather than the pointwise values of random variables.
Abusing notation slightly, we will ignore the distinction between random variables and their distributions under
\mathbb{P}
, and we will regard
X^{(s)}
as a generic random variable having the specified distribution.

From (
3.32
) we can obtain the MGF and CGF of the tilted distribution:

M_{X^{(s)}}(\sigma)=\frac{M_{X}(s+\sigma)}{M_{X}(s)},\qquad K_{X^{(s)}}(\sigma)=K_{X}(s+\sigma)-K_{X}(s),

(3.34)

In particular, when
s
is an interior point of the domain of
K_{X}
, we can differentiate w.r.t.
\sigma
and set
\sigma=0
to interpret the derivatives
K^{\prime}(s),K^{\prime\prime}(s)
as cumulants of the tilted distribution:

K_{X}^{\prime}(s)=\mathbb{E}\left(X^{(s)}\right),\qquad K_{X}^{\prime\prime}(s)=\Var\left(X^{(s)}\right),

(3.35)

where in the multivariate case
\Var
means the variance-covariance matrix.
Thus the denominator in the saddlepoint approximation (
1.3
)/(
1.6
) can be recognised as the normalising factor for a Gaussian random variable with the same variance-covariance matrix as the tilted distribution.
Moreover, the saddlepoint equation (
1.2
) can be expressed as the condition that the tilted distribution with parameter
\hat{s}=\hat{s}(x)
should have mean
x
.

By construction, the tilted distribution
X^{(s)}
is absolutely continuous with respect to the distribution of
X
.
Define the relative entropy, or Kullback-Leibler divergence, by

H\!\left(\left.\big.\smash{X^{(s)}}\,\right|X\right)=\int\frac{d\mu_{X^{(s)}}}{d\mu_{X}}\log\left(\frac{d\mu_{X^{(s)}}}{d\mu_{X}}\right)d\mu_{X}=\int\log\left(\frac{d\mu_{X^{(s)}}}{d\mu_{X}}\right)d\mu_{X^{(s)}}.

(3.36)

Lemma 11

(Tilted relative entropy)
.

H\!\left(\left.\big.\smash{X^{(s)}}\,\right|X\right)=sK_{X}^{\prime}(s)-K_{X}(s).

(3.37)

Proof.

By (
3.33
) we have
\log\left(\frac{d\mu_{X^{(s)}}}{d\mu_{X}}\right)=\log\left(\frac{e^{sx}}{M_{X}(s)}\right)=sx-K_{X}(s)
.
According to (
3.36
), the relative entropy is the expected value of this quantity when
x
is replaced by
X^{(s)}
:

\displaystyle H\!\left(\left.\big.\smash{X^{(s)}}\,\right|X\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left(sX^{(s)}-K_{X}(s)\right)=s\mathbb{E}\left(X^{(s)}\right)-K_{X}(s)

(3.38)

which reduces to (
3.37
) by (
3.35
).
∎

Proposition 12
.

The saddlepoint approximation (
1.2
) and (
1.3
)/(
1.6
) can be expressed in terms of tilted distributions by

\hat{f}_{X}(x)=\frac{\exp\left(-H\!\left(\left.\big.\smash{X^{(\hat{s})}}\,\right|X\right)\right)}{\sqrt{\det(2\pi\Var(X^{(\hat{s})}))}}\quad\text{where $\hat{s}=\hat{s}(x)$ solves}\quad\mathbb{E}(X^{(\hat{s})})=x.

(3.39)

Proof.

By construction, the saddlepoint
\hat{s}=\hat{s}(x)
solves
K_{X}^{\prime}(\hat{s})=x
, so
K_{X}(\hat{s})-\hat{s}x=K_{X}(\hat{s})-\hat{s}K_{X}^{\prime}(\hat{s})
reduces to a relative entropy as in
Lemma
11
.
∎

The next lemma shows that the saddlepoint equation
\mathbb{E}(X^{(\hat{s})})=x
uniquely determines the tilted distribution
X^{(\hat{s})}
, in those cases where a solution exists.

Lemma 13

(Tilted distributions are determined by their means)
.

Let
X
be a random variable with values in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and let
s,\tilde{s}
be
d
-dimensional row vectors for which the tilted distributions
X^{(s)},X^{(\tilde{s})}
exist and have finite means.
If
\mathbb{E}(X^{(s)})=\mathbb{E}(X^{(\tilde{s})})
, then
X^{(s)}\overset{d}{=}X^{(\tilde{s})}
.

Proof.

The hypotheses imply that
K_{X}(s),K_{X}(\tilde{s})
are defined.
From (
3.34
) we find that
\sigma\mapsto K_{X}(s+\sigma(\tilde{s}-s))-K_{X}(s)
is the CGF for the scalar random variable
Y=(\tilde{s}-s)X^{(s)}
, and by convexity this CGF is defined at least for
\sigma\in[0,1]
and analytic for
\sigma\in(0,1)
.
If
Y
is non-constant, then
Y^{(\sigma)}
is also non-constant and has strictly positive variance for all
\sigma\in(0,1)
.
Then
K_{Y}
is strictly convex in
(0,1)
and
K^{\prime}_{Y}(\sigma)=(\tilde{s}-s)\mathbb{E}(X^{(s+\sigma(\tilde{s}-s))})
is strictly increasing in
\sigma\in(0,1)
, and taking
\sigma\searrow 0,\sigma\nearrow 1
shows that
X^{(s)},X^{(\tilde{s})}
cannot have equal means.
Conversely, if
Y
is identically constant, then
X^{(s)}
and hence
X
are supported in a hyperplane
(\tilde{s}-s)x=c
, and it follows easily that
X^{(s)}\overset{d}{=}X^{(\tilde{s})}
.
∎

The idea of the second proof of
Theorem
3
is to relate the tilted distributions for
X_{1},\dotsc,X_{N}
and
Q_{1},\dotsc,Q_{N}
, then apply (
3.39
).
Key to this strategy is that, for every operation on random variables for which there is a generating function identity, there will be a corresponding distribution identity between their tilted distributions and a corresponding identity between their tilted relative entropies.
The most basic such identities show that tilting preserves independence and additive structure: if
Y_{1},Y_{2}
are independent and
X=Y_{1}+Y_{2}
, then

X^{(s)}\overset{d}{=}Y_{1}^{(s)}+Y_{2}^{(s)}\quad\text{and}\quad H\!\left(\left.\big.\smash{X^{(s)}}\,\right|X\right)=H\!\left(\left.\big.\smash{Y_{1}^{(s)}}\,\right|Y_{1}\right)+H\!\left(\left.\big.\smash{Y_{2}^{(s)}}\,\right|Y_{2}\right)

(3.40)

and the tilted joint distribution is given by

\begin{gathered}(Y_{1},Y_{2})^{(s_{1},\,s_{2})}\overset{d}{=}(Y_{1}^{(s_{1})},Y_{2}^{(s_{2})})\quad\text{and}\quad\\
H\!\left(\left.\big.\smash{(Y_{1},Y_{2})^{(s_{1},\,s_{2})}}\,\right|(Y_{1},Y_{2})\right)=H\!\left(\left.\big.\smash{Y_{1}^{(s_{1})}}\,\right|Y_{1}\right)+H\!\left(\left.\big.\smash{Y_{2}^{(s_{2})}}\,\right|Y_{2}\right).\end{gathered}

(3.41)

Here
Y_{1}^{(s_{1})},Y_{2}^{(s_{2})}
are taken to be independent, and generally in what follows, different tilted distributions within the same expression are taken to be independent.
In addition, (
3.40
)–(
3.41
) are taken to mean that if the tilted distribution(s) on one side of the equality exist, then so do those on the other side and the equality in distribution holds.
Both (
3.40
)–(
3.41
) can be proved by showing that the two sides have the same CGFs, as in the proof of the more general assertion of
Lemma
14
below.

For a process
(Z(t))
satisfying condition
(a)
,
(b)
, or
(c)
from
Definition
2
, (
3.40
) shows that
Z(t+\tau)^{(s)}
has the same distribution as a sum of independent copies of
Z(t)^{(s)}
and
Z(\tau)^{(s)}
, for all applicable values
t,\tau
from
\mathbb{Z}_{+}
,
\mathbb{R}_{+}
, or
\mathbb{R}
, respectively.
Thus the tilted distributions
Z(t)^{(s)}
can be realised as the marginal distributions of a process satisfying the same condition
(a)
,
(b)
, or
(c)
, and we denote a generic realisation of this process by
(Z^{(s)}(t))
for
t
in
\mathbb{Z}_{+}
,
\mathbb{R}_{+}
, or
\mathbb{R}
, respectively.

More generally, the following two lemmas show that tilting preserves the class of recursively compounded processes.

Lemma 14
.

Let
Q_{n}(x_{0},\dotsc,x_{n-1})
be as in
Definition
2

Then the process

(3.42)

satisfies

for all choices of

, where
\xi_{n}^{(s_{n})}
and the processes
