A Random Adaptation Perspective on Distributed Averaging
Rohit Parasnis, Ashwin Verma, Massimo Franceschetti, Behrouz Touri

Abstract
We propose a random adaptation variant of time-varying distributed averaging dynamics in discrete time. We show that this leads to novel interpretations of fundamental concepts in distributed averaging, opinion dynamics, and distributed learning. Namely, we show that the ergodicity of a stochastic chain is equivalent to the almost sure ( a.s .) finite-time agreement attainment in the proposed random adaptation dynamics. Using this result, we provide a new interpretation for the absolute probability sequence of an ergodic chain. We then modify the base-case dynamics into a time-reversed inhomogeneous Markov chain, and we show that in this case ergodicity is equivalent to the uniqueness of the limiting distributions of the Markov chain. Finally, we introduce and study a time-varying random adaptation version of the Friedkin-Johnsen model and a rank-one perturbation of the base-case dynamics.

1.

An Interpretation of Ergodicity:

We show that the classical notion of ergodicity is

2.

An Interpretation of Absolute Probability Sequence:
Using the above characterization of ergodicity, we interpret the

3.

Ergodicity vis-a-vis Uniqueness of the Limiting Distribution:
We propose a time-reversed, transposed variant of the aforementioned dynamics and use it to show that the limiting distribution of the state vector is unique if and only if the given stochastic chain is ergodic.
This leads to a new insight: absolute probability sequences are to ergodic chains what stationary distributions are to regular stochastic matrices.

2
RANDOM ADAPTATION DYNAMICS

In
distributed
averaging dynamics, we are given a sequence of stochastic matrices
\{{Q}(t)\}{{}_{t=0}^{\infty}}
, and we are interested in studying the dynamics

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{x}}(t+1)=Q(t){\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{x}}(t),

(1)

for some initial time
t_{0}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and initial
state

\mathbf{x}(t_{0})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. As mentioned in the introduction, each row of
Q(t)
is a stochastic vector that can be viewed as the probability mass function of a certain random variable taking
n
values. Our random adaptation viewpoint formulates a very natural sequence of random variables that exhibits this behavior. Consider
n
agents assuming a random state
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}x}_{i}(t)\in\mathbb{R}
(more precisely,
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}x}_{i}(t,\omega)\in\mathbb{R}
where
\omega\in\Omega
) evolving over discrete time
t\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
. Let the starting time
t_{0}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and the initial states
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{x}}(t_{0})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
be an arbitrary deterministic vector. For a given sequence
\{Q(t)\}
of stochastic matrices, the random adaptation scheme is
defined
as follows: At time
t\geq t_{0}
, agent
i
adopts agent
j
’s state with probability
q_{ij}(t)
independently of other agents as well as every agent’s past choices and states, i.e.,
{\text{Pr}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}x}_{i}(t+1)={\color[rgb]{0,0,0}x}_{j}(t)\mid{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{x}}(t),\ldots,{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{x}}(t_{0}))=q_{ij}(t)}
for all
i,j\in[n]
. Therefore, we can write

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{x}}(t+1)=A(t){\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{x}}(t),

(2)

where
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{x}}(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is the random state vector (whose
i
-th entry is
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}x}_{i}(t)
) and
A(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
is a binary random stochastic matrix, i.e.,
\{a_{ij}(t):i,j\in[n]\}
are Bernoulli random variables with parameters
\widehat{a}_{ij}(t)=q_{ij}(t)
for all
i,j\in[n]
.

We now observe a few important properties of (
2
): (i) The random process
\{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{x}}(t)\}_{t=0}^{\infty}
is a non-homogeneous Markov chain with state space size at most
n^{n}
as the state of each agent at every time
instant
is in
\{x_{i}(t_{0})\mid i\in[n]\}
. (ii) For each
t\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, the rows of
A(t)
are independent random vectors. (iii) The random matrices
\{A(t)\}_{t=0}^{\infty}
are independent and hence, for each
t\in\mathbb{N}
,
A(t)
is independent of
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{x}}(t)
.

3
MAIN RESULTS

We first define two properties that will be shown to be closely related. The first property relates to stochastic chains.

Definition 1
(Ergodicity

[
21
,
23
]
).

A deterministic (non-random) stochastic chain
\{Q(t)\}_{t=0}^{\infty}
is said to be ergodic if, for every
t_{0}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, there exists a vector
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\boldsymbol{\psi}}(t_{0})\in\mathcal{P}_{n}

such that
\lim_{t\to\infty}Q(t:t_{0})=\mathbf{1}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\boldsymbol{\psi}}^{T}(t_{0})
.

Put differently, a stochastic chain
\{Q(t)\}_{t=0}^{\infty}
is called ergodic if every backward matrix product
Q(t:t_{0})
converges to a rank-one matrix that has identical rows.

The second property relates to the random adaptation dynamics (
2
).

Definition 2
(
Finite Agreement
).

We say that the adaptation dynamics (
2
) has an a.s. finite agreement property if for all initial time
s

t_{0}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and initial
states

{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{x}}(t_{0})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
there exist a random scalar
y=y(t_{0},\mathbf{x}(t_{0}))
and a random (stopping) time
T\geq t_{0}
such that
\mathbf{x}(t)=y\mathbf{1}
for all
t\geq T
.

In other words, the random adaptation dynamics (
2
) has the finite agreement property if at
some
time after the initiation of the dynamics, all the agents
adopt
the same state.

Note that the ergodicity of
\{Q(t)\}
does not imply finite agreement for the deterministic dynamics (
1
). For example, suppose
n=2
, let
\mathbf{x}(0)={\mathbf{e}}^{(1)}
, and consider the static chain defined by

Q(t)=\begin{pmatrix}p&1-p\\
1-q&q\\
\end{pmatrix}

for all
t\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, where
p,q\in(0,1)
. Then it can be verified that
\{Q(t)\}_{t=0}^{\infty}
is ergodic. However, we also have
{x_{1}(t)-x_{2}(t)=(p+q-1)^{t}}
for all
t\in\mathbb{N}
, which implies that no agreement is reached in finite time unless
p+q=1
.

On the contrary, if
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{x}}(t_{0})\in\{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{e}}^{(1)},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{e}}^{(2)}\}
the random adaptation variation (
2
) of the same static chain ensures that the two agents reach an agreement at time
{T:=\inf\{t\geq t_{0}:x_{1}(t)\oplus x_{2}(t)=0\}}
, where
\oplus
denotes
the

exclusive OR
operation. Since
T
is a geometric random variable with the parameter
{p(1-p)+q(1-q)>0}
, it is finite
a.s.
In fact, even for
n\neq 2
, every ergodic chain guarantees finite agreement for all initial conditions, as we now show.

Theorem 1
.

The random adaptation dynamics (
2
) has the finite agreement property if and only if
{\{\widehat{A}(t)\}_{t=0}^{\infty}=\{Q(t)\}_{t=0}^{\infty}}
is an ergodic chain.

Proof.

We establish ergodicity first as a sufficient condition and then as a necessary condition for
a.s.
finite agreement with an arbitrary initial condition
(t_{0},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{x}}(t_{0}))
.

Proof of Sufficiency

It suffices to show that an agreement occurs in the network infinitely often
a.s
., because once an agreement is reached, the state vector stops evolving in time.

We first note that by Definition
1
, there exists a
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\boldsymbol{\psi}}(t_{0})\in\mathcal{P}_{n}}
such that
{\lim_{t\to\infty}Q(t:t_{0})=\mathbf{1}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\boldsymbol{\psi}}^{T}(t_{0})}

a.s
. As
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\boldsymbol{\psi}}^{T}(t_{0})\mathbf{1}=1}
, there exists an index
\ell(t_{0})\in[n]
such that
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\psi}_{\ell(t_{0})}(t_{0})\geq\frac{1}{n}}

{\lim_{t\to\infty}Q_{i\ell(t_{0})}(t:t_{0})\geq\frac{1}{n}}
for all
i\in[n]
. Consequently, there exists a time
t_{1}\geq t_{0}
such that
Q_{i\ell(t_{0})}(t_{1}:t_{0})\geq\frac{1}{2n}
for all
i\in[n]
. By Lemma
1
, this implies that
\text{Pr}\left(A_{[n]\{\ell(t_{0})\}}(t_{1}:t_{0})=\mathbf{1}\right)\geq\left(\frac{1}{2n}\right)^{n}
. Since
A(t_{1}:t_{0})\in\mathcal{P}_{n\times n}
is binary, it follows that
{\text{Pr}(A(t_{1}:t_{0})=\mathbf{1}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{e}}^{\ell(t_{0})})^{T})\geq\left(\frac{1}{2n}\right)^{n}}
.

As
t_{0}
is arbitrary, we can repeat the above analysis with different starting times and obtain an increasing sequence of times
\{t_{k}\}_{k=0}^{\infty}
such that
{\text{Pr}(A(t_{k+1}:t_{k})=\mathbf{1}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{e}}^{\ell(t_{k})})^{T})\geq\left(\frac{1}{2n}\right)^{n}}
. Since
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{x}}(t_{k+1})=A(t_{k+1}:t_{k}){\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{x}}(t_{k})
, this further implies that
\text{Pr}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{x}}(t_{k+1})={\color[rgb]{0,0,0}x}_{\ell}(t_{k})\mathbf{1})\geq\left(\frac{1}{2n}\right)^{n}
. As a result, letting
C_{k}
denote the event that an agreement exists in the network at time
t_{k}\geq t_{0}
, we have
\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\text{Pr}(C_{k})=\infty
. Now,
\{C_{k}\}_{k=0}^{\infty}
are independent events because
\{A(t)\}_{t=0}^{\infty}
are independent and

\{[t_{k},t_{k+1}-1]:k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\}
are disjoint intervals. Therefore, by the Second Borel-Cantelli Lemma
[
40
, Theorem 2.3.6]
, infinitely many events among
\{C_{k}\}_{k=0}^{\infty}
occur
a.s.
, which proves the assertion.

Proof of Necessity

Suppose there exist
{T=T(t_{0},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{x}}(t_{0}))<\infty}
and
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}y}={\color[rgb]{0,0,0}y}(t_{0},x(t_{0}))\in\mathbb{R}
such that
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{x}}(t)={\color[rgb]{0,0,0}y}\mathbf{1}

a.s.
for all

. Then
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{x}}(t)=A(t:t_{0}){\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{x}}(t_{0})
implies that
{\lim_{t\to\infty}A(t:t_{0}){\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{x}}(t_{0})={\color[rgb]{0,0,0}y}\mathbf{1}}

a.s.

Besides, we know that
\|{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{x}}(t)\|_{\infty}\leq\|{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{x}}(t_{0})\|_{\infty}
for all
{t\geq t_{0}}
. Therefore, by the Dominated Convergence Theorem
[
40
, Theorem 1.6.7]
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}y}\right]\mathbf{1}=\mathbb{E}\left[\lim_{t\to\infty}A(t:t_{0}){\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{x}}(t_{0})\right]

(3)

\displaystyle=\lim_{t\to\infty}\mathbb{E}[A(t:t_{0}){\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{x}}(t_{0})]=\lim_{t\to\infty}\widehat{A}(t:t_{0}){\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{x}}(t_{0}),

(4)

where the last equality follows from the independence of
\{A(t)\}_{t=0}^{\infty}
. As a result,
{\lim_{t\to\infty}Q(t:t_{0}){\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{x}}(t_{0})=\widehat{y}\mathbf{1}}
.
For the initial condition
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{x}}(t_{0})={\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{e}}^{(i)}
, this implies that the

and hence,

Remark 1
.

Theorem
1
enables us to comment further on ergodic chains. To elaborate, we can repeat some of the arguments used in the proof above to show that if

such that

for all

. Moreover,
\{A(t)\}_{t=0}^{\infty}\in\mathcal{P}_{n\times n}
being binary implies that
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\boldsymbol{\pi}}(t_{0})
is binary, i.e.,
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\boldsymbol{\pi}}(t_{0})\in\{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{e}}^{(i)}:i\in[n]\}}
. Finally, taking expectations on both sides yields
{\lim_{t\to\infty}\widehat{A}(t:t_{0})=\mathbf{1}\widehat{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\boldsymbol{\pi}}}^{T}(t_{0})}
, i.e.,
{\lim_{t\to\infty}Q(t:t_{0})=\mathbf{1}\widehat{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\boldsymbol{\pi}}}^{T}(t_{0})}

{\widehat{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\boldsymbol{\pi}}}^{T}(t_{0})\in\mathcal{P}_{n}}

Definition 3
(Absolute Probability Sequence).

For a deterministic stochastic chain
\{Q(t)\}_{t=0}^{\infty}
, a sequence of stochastic vectors
\{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\boldsymbol{\psi}}(t)\}_{t=0}^{\infty}
is said to be an absolute probability sequence if
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\boldsymbol{\psi}}^{T}(t+1)Q(t)={\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\boldsymbol{\psi}}^{T}(t)

We now connect this novel concept with the dynamics (

Theorem 2
.

Suppose that
\{Q(t)\}_{t=0}^{\infty}=\{\widehat{A}(t)\}_{t=0}^{\infty}

\{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\boldsymbol{\psi}}(t)\}_{t=0}^{\infty}=\{\widehat{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\boldsymbol{\pi}}}(t)\}_{t=0}^{\infty}

{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}y}={\color[rgb]{0,0,0}y}(t_{0},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{x}}(t_{0}))
be the agreed value of

\lim_{t\to\infty}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{x}}(t)={\color[rgb]{0,0,0}y}\mathbf{1}

such that
\{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}x}_{i}(t_{0})\}_{i=1}^{n}
are all distinct. Then the probability distribution of
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}y}
is given by

{p_{i}(t_{0}):=\text{Pr}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}y}={\color[rgb]{0,0,0}x}_{i}(t_{0}))=\psi_{i}(t_{0})}

.

Proof.

By Remark
1
, we almost surely have
{\lim_{t\to\infty}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{x}}(t)=\lim_{t\to\infty}A(t:t_{0}){\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{x}}(t_{0})=\mathbf{1}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\boldsymbol{\pi}}^{T}(t_{0}){\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{x}}(t_{0})}
. Thus,
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}y}={\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\boldsymbol{\pi}}^{T}(t_{0}){\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{x}}(t_{0})}
, which implies that
{\widehat{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}y}}={\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\pi}}}^{T}(t_{0}){\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{x}}(t_{0})}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\psi_{i}(t_{0})x_{i}(t_{0})

On the other hand, the definition of expectation implies that
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\widehat{y}}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}p_{i}(t_{0})x_{i}(t_{0})

Hence,
{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\psi_{i}(t_{0}){\color[rgb]{0,0,0}x}_{i}(t_{0})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}p_{i}(t_{0}){\color[rgb]{0,0,0}x}_{i}(t_{0})}

4.1
Time-Reversed Non-homogeneous Markov Chains

Let
\{A(t)\}_{t=0}^{\infty}
be a random sequence of independent binary matrices, and let
{\{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{e}}^{(i)}:i\in[n]\}}
be the state space of a time-reversed Markov chain whose probability transition matrix is
Q(t):=\widehat{A}(t)
at time
t
. To be precise, the Markov chain is a random process
\{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{z}}(t)\}
that starts at an arbitrary time instant
t_{\infty}\in\mathbb{N}
with an arbitrary probability distribution given by
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{p}}_{\infty}\in\mathcal{P}_{n}
(where
{({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{p}}_{\infty})_{i}:=\text{Pr}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{z}}(t_{\infty})={\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{e}}^{(i)})}
and evolves backwards in time with
{\text{Pr}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{z}}(t)={\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{e}}^{(j)}\mid{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{z}}(t+1)={\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{e}}^{(i)})=q_{ij}(t)}
for all
{t<t_{\infty}}
. Equivalently,

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{z}}^{T}(t)={\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{z}}^{T}(t+1)A(t)

(5)

for all
t\in\{t_{\infty}-1,t_{\infty}-2,\ldots,0\}
. Note that (
5
) is nothing but a time-reversed, transposed variant of (
2
).

To relate these dynamics to time-homogeneous chains, recall that the limiting probability distribution of a regular Markov chain is a stationary distribution independent of the initial distribution
[
42
]
.
Analogously, we ask, is the limiting distribution of a time-reversed inhomogeneous Markov chain an absolute probability sequence of the associated stochastic chain that is independent of
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{p}}_{\infty}
? As we show, the answer is yes if and only if the stochastic chain is ergodic.

Theorem 3
.

Consider the dynamics (
5
) with a variable starting time
t_{\infty}
. Let
\{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\boldsymbol{\psi}}(t)\}_{t=0}^{\infty}
be an absolute probability sequence for
\{Q(t)\}_{t=0}^{\infty}
. Then the limiting distribution
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{p}}(t):=\sum_{i=1}^{n}p_{i}(t){\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{e}}^{(i)}\in\mathcal{P}_{n}
with
p_{i}(t):=\lim_{t_{\infty}\to\infty}\text{Pr}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{z}}(t)={\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{e}}^{(i)})
exists and is invariant w.r.t. the initial distribution
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{p}}_{\infty}
for all
t\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
if and only if
\{Q(t)\}_{t=0}^{\infty}
is ergodic, in which case
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{p}}(t)={\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\boldsymbol{\psi}}(t)
for all
t\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
.

Proof.

We first note that for all
t\leq t_{\infty}
, we have
\text{Pr}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{z}}(t)={\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{e}}^{(i)})=\mathbb{E}[{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}z}_{i}(t)]={\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\widehat{z}}_{i}(t)
for all
{i\in[n]}
, which means that
p_{\infty}={\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\widehat{\mathbf{z}}}(t_{\infty})
and more generally that the probability distribution of
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{z}}(t)
is determined by
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\widehat{\mathbf{z}}}^{T}(t)={\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\widehat{\mathbf{z}}}^{T}(t_{\infty})\widehat{A}(t_{\infty}:t)={\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{p}}_{\infty}^{T}Q(t_{\infty}:t)
for all
t\leq t_{\infty}
. Thus,
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{p}}^{T}(t)={\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{p}}_{\infty}^{T}\lim_{t_{\infty}\to\infty}Q(t_{\infty}:t)
(if the limit exists). Using this, we first establish the sufficiency of ergodicity and then its necessity for the invariance assertion to hold.

If
\{Q(t)\}_{t=0}^{\infty}
is ergodic, then
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{p}}^{T}(t)
is given by
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{p}}_{\infty}^{T}\lim_{t_{\infty}\to\infty}Q(t_{\infty}:t)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(a)}}{{=}}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{p}}_{\infty}^{T}\mathbf{1}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\boldsymbol{\psi}}^{T}(t)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(b)}}{{=}}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\boldsymbol{\psi}}^{T}(t)
, where
(a)
follows from Remark
1
and
(b)
holds because
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{p}}_{\infty}\in\mathcal{P}_{n}
. Since
\{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\boldsymbol{\psi}}(t)\}_{t=0}^{\infty}
are unique (see
[
20
]
, Theorem 1), it follows that
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{p}}(t)={\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\boldsymbol{\psi}}(t)

a.s.
does not vary with
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{p}}_{\infty}
.

On the other hand, if
\{Q(t)\}_{t=0}^{\infty}
is not ergodic, then there exists a
t_{0}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
such that either
{\lim_{t_{\infty}\to\infty}Q(t_{\infty}:t_{0})}
does not exist (in which case there is nothing to prove), or there exists an index
\ell\in[n]
such that the column vector
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{v}}:=\lim_{t_{\infty}\to\infty}Q_{[n]\,\{\ell\}}(t_{\infty}:t_{0})}
satisfies
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{v}}\neq\alpha\mathbf{1}
for all
{\alpha\in\mathbb{R}}
. Therefore, we can write
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{v}}=\alpha\mathbf{1}+\beta{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{w}}
for some
\alpha,\beta>0
and
some

{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{w}}
with
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{w}}^{T}\mathbf{1}=0
. Note that
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{w}}^{T}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{v}}\not=0}
.
Note
also
that for small enough
\tilde{\beta}>0
,
\tilde{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{w}}}={\frac{1}{n}\mathbf{1}+\tilde{\beta}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{w}}\in\mathcal{P}_{n}}
. Now, for
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{p}}_{\infty}=\frac{1}{n}\mathbf{1}
and
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{p}}_{\infty}=\tilde{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{w}}}
,
the value of

{p}_{\ell}(t_{0})=\left({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{p}}_{\infty}^{T}\lim_{t_{\infty}\to\infty}A(t_{\infty}:t_{0})\right)_{\ell}
would be
\frac{1}{n}\mathbf{1}^{T}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{v}}
and
\frac{1}{n}\mathbf{1}^{T}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{v}}+\tilde{\beta}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{w}}^{T}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{v}}
, respectively, which along with
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{w}}^{T}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{v}}\neq 0
violate
s
the invariance condition.

Theorem
3
also shows that, just as stationary distributions are the limiting probability distributions of Markov chains defined by regular matrices, absolute probability sequences can be interpreted as the limiting distributions of time-reversed Markov chains defined by ergodic stochastic chains.

4.2
Random Adaptation Approach to Friedkin-Johnsen Model

The dynamics (
1
) can be viewed as a time-varying version of the French-Degroot opinion dynamics model where agent opinions move towards convex combinations of other agents’ opinions. The Friedkin-Johnsen model, in addition to being partly influenced by neighbors, introduces a
prejudice
that affects the agents’ opinions. Mathematically,

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{x}}(t+1)=\Lambda W(t){\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{x}}(t)+(I-\Lambda){\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{u}},

(6)

where
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{x}}(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
denotes the vector of opinions,
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{u}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is the vector of the agents’ prejudices,
W(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
denotes the
influence matrix
, which describes how the agents influence each other, and
\Lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
is a diagonal matrix whose
i
th diagonal entry,
\lambda_{i}\in[0,1]
, denotes the
susceptibility
of agent
i
to social influence and
1-\lambda_{i}
denotes the susceptibility of agent
i
to her prejudice
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}u}_{i}
.

Similar to (
6
), we can provide a random adaptation variation of the Friedkin-Johnsen model as follows. In the
t
-th time period, agent
i
decides between adapting to
her
neighbor’s opinion versus adapting to
her
prejudice. Her choice is independent of other agents’ choices and her own past choices. With a probability
\gamma(t)\in(0,1)
, she follows the adaptation scheme described earlier, and with probability
1-\gamma(t)
, she resets her opinion to her prejudice
u_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
. As before, we assume the initial state vector
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{x}}(0)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
to be arbitrary. This results in the update rule

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{x}}(t+1)=\Lambda(t)A(t){\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{x}}(t)+(I-\Lambda(t)){\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{u}},

(7)

where
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{x}}(t)
and
A(t)
have their usual meanings,
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{u}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a vector of external influences/prejudices, and
\{\Lambda(t)\}_{t=0}^{\infty}
is a sequence of diagonal matrices whose diagonal entries
\{\lambda_{i}(t):i\in[n]\}
are Bernoulli random variables with
\text{Pr}(\lambda_{i}(t)=1)=\gamma_{i}(t)
.

Remark 2
.

Observe that (
7
) is a special case of (
2
) by letting
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{y}}^{T}(t):=[{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{x}}^{T}(t)\,\,{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{u}}^{T}]
and
by
noting
that

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{y}}(t+1)=B(t){\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{y}}(t),

(8)

where
B(t):=\begin{pmatrix}\Lambda(t)A(t)\,&I-\Lambda(t)\\
O_{n\times n}\,&I_{n\times n}\end{pmatrix}
.
As a result, for any
t,t_{0}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
with
t\geq t_{0}
, we have
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{y}}(t)=B(t:t_{0}){\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{y}}(t_{0})
with
B_{[n]}(t:t_{0})=P(t:t_{0})
, where
P(t):=\Lambda(t)A(t)
. Note also that
P(t)\leq A(t)
because
\Lambda(t)\leq I
.

We now define two terms:
dominance in expectation
and
simultaneously malleable
agents, and we show that, if
\{Q(t)\}_{t=0}^{\infty}
is an ergodic chain with simultaneously malleable agents that dominate in expectation, then all the agents’ opinions will almost surely enter the
prejudice set
(the set of external influences)
\mathcal{U}:=\{u_{i}:i\in[n]\}
in finite time.

Definition 4
(Dominance in Expectation).

The agents of a set
S\subseteq[n]
are said to dominate in expectation if
\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\mathbf{1}^{T}Q_{\bar{S}S}(t)\mathbf{1}=\infty
and
\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\mathbf{1}^{T}Q_{S\bar{S}}(t)\mathbf{1}<\infty
.

In the the average-case scenario, if a set of agents
S\subseteq[n]
dominate in expectation, then the agents of
S
significantly influence the rest of the agents
\bar{S}
without themselves being significantly influenced by
\bar{S}
in the long run.

Definition 5
(Simultaneously Malleable Agents).

The agents of a set
S\subseteq[n]
are said to be simultaneously malleable if
\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\prod_{i\in S}(1-\gamma_{i}(t))=\infty
.

Essentially, simultaneously malleable agents are those whose probability of simultaneously adapting to their respective external influences does not vanish too fast with time.

Theorem 4
.

For the dynamics (
7
), suppose
{\{Q(t)\}_{t=0}^{\infty}=\{\widehat{A}(t)\}_{t=0}^{\infty}}
is ergodic, and suppose there exists a set
S\subseteq[n]
of simultaneously malleable agents that dominate in expectation. Then there a.s. exists a time
T<\infty
such that
\{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}x}_{i}(T)\}_{i=1}^{n}\subseteq\mathcal{U}
.

Proof.

First, note that the decisions taken in the network at time
t
are independent across agents. As a result,

\text{Pr}\left(\bigcap_{i\in S}\left\{\lambda_{i}(t)=0\right\}\right)=\prod_{i\in S}(1-\gamma_{i}(t)).

In light of Definition
5
and the Second Borel-Cantelli Lemma, this further implies that there exists an increasing sequence of random times
\{T_{k}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
such that
\lambda_{i}(T_{k}-1)=0

a.s.
for all
i\in S
and all
k\in\mathbb{N}
. This means that
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}x}_{i}(T_{k})=u_{i}

a.s.
for all
i\in S
and all
k\in\mathbb{N}
.

On the other hand, we can use the union bound to show that

{\text{Pr}(\bigcup_{i\in S}\bigcup_{i\in\bar{S}}\{a_{ij}(t)=1\})}
is at most

\sum_{i\in S}\sum_{j\in\bar{S}}q_{ij}(t)=\mathbf{1}^{T}Q_{S\bar{S}}(t)\mathbf{1}
. Since
\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\mathbf{1}^{T}Q_{S\bar{S}}(t)\mathbf{1}<\infty
, it follows from the First Borel-Cantelli Lemma
[
40
, Theorem 2.3.1]
that there exists a random time
T^{*}<\infty
such that
a_{ij}(t)=0

a.s.
for all
i\in S
,
j\in\bar{S}
, and
t\geq T^{*}
. This means that there
a.s.
exists a point of time
T^{*}
after which the agents in
S
are never influenced by those in
\bar{S}
.

Let
K:=\inf\{k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}:T_{k}>T^{*}\}
. Then,
T_{K}>T^{*}
and
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}x}_{i}(T_{K})=u_{i}

a.s.
for all
i\in S
. Hence,
{\{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}x}_{i}(t):i\in S\}\subseteq\mathcal{U}\bigcup\{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}x}_{i}(T_{K}):i\in S\}\subseteq\mathcal{U}}
for all
{t\geq T_{K}}
.

It remains to show the existence of a time
T\geq T_{K}
such that
\{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}x}_{i}(t):i\in\bar{S}\}\subseteq\mathcal{U}
for all
t\geq T
. By the definition of ergodicity, the truncated chain
\{Q(t)\}_{t=\tau}^{\infty}
is ergodic for all
\tau\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
. It follows from Remark
1
that there exists a random vector
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\boldsymbol{\pi}}(\tau)\in\{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{e}}^{(i)}:i\in[n]\}}
such that
\lim_{t\to\infty}A(t:\tau)=\mathbf{1}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\boldsymbol{\pi}}^{T}(\tau)

a.s.
Thus,
{\lim_{t\to\infty}A(t:T_{K})=\mathbf{1}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\boldsymbol{\pi}}^{T}(T_{K})}

a.s.
On the other hand, for
t\geq T_{K}\geq T^{*}
, we have
a_{ij}(t)=0

a.s.
for all
i\in S
and
{j\in\bar{S}}
. Hence,
A_{S\bar{S}}(t:T_{K})=O

a.s.
for all
t\geq T_{K}
. It follows that
\lim_{t\to\infty}A_{S\bar{S}}(t:T_{K})=O

a.s.
, which means that
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\boldsymbol{\pi}}^{T}(T_{K})\notin\{e^{(i)}:i\in\bar{S}\}

a.s
. Since
\mathbf{1}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\boldsymbol{\pi}}^{T}(T_{K})
has identical rows, this further implies that the columns of
{\lim_{t\to\infty}A(t:T_{K})}
indexed by
\bar{S}
are all zero
a.s.
It now follows from Remark
2
that

\displaystyle\limsup_{t\to\infty}B_{[n]\bar{S}}(t:T_{K})

\displaystyle=\limsup_{t\to\infty}P_{[n]\bar{S}}(t:T_{K})

\displaystyle\leq\limsup_{t\to\infty}A_{[n]\bar{S}}(t:T_{K})=O\quad a.s.

Equivalently, for all sufficiently large
t
, the entries of
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{y}}(t)=B(t:T_{K}){\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{y}}(T_{K})
are binary convex combinations of
{\{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}x}_{i}(T_{K}):i\in S\}\bigcup\mathcal{U}=\mathcal{U}}
. This completes the proof.

We now consider a special case of (
7
) in which the probability distributions of the agents’ opinions converge to limits that can be computed using closed-form expressions.

Theorem 5
.

Suppose the matrix pairs
\{(\Lambda(t),A(t))\}_{t=0}^{\infty}
are independent and identically distributed. Also, suppose
\Gamma<I
, where
\Gamma:=\widehat{\Lambda}(t)
and

for all

. Finally, suppose that

and that
\{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}x}_{i}(0)\}\bigcap\mathcal{U}=\emptyset
. Then the following assertions hold.

(i)

We have
{\lim_{t\to\infty}\text{Pr}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}x}_{i}(t)=u_{j})=v_{ij}}
for all
{i,j\in[n]}
, where

are the entries of

\displaystyle V:=\left(I-\Gamma Q\right)^{-1}\left(I-\Gamma\right).

(9)

(ii)

There a.s. exists a random time
T<\infty
such that
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}x}_{i}(t)\in\mathcal{U}}
for all

.

Proof.

We first recall from Remark
2
that
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{y}}(t)=B(t:0){\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{y}}(0)}
for all

. Since
\mathcal{U}
has

\{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}x}_{i}(0)\}\bigcap\mathcal{U}=\emptyset

\displaystyle\text{Pr}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}x}_{i}(t)=u_{j})

\displaystyle=\text{Pr}\left(B_{i\,n+j}(t:0)=1\right)=\mathbb{E}\left[B_{i\,n+j}(t:0)\right]

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(a)}}{{=}}\widehat{B}_{i\,n+j}(t:0)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(b)}}{{=}}\left(\left(\widehat{B}(0)\right)^{t}\right)_{i\,n+j},

(10)

where
(a)
and
(b)
hold because
\{(\Lambda(t),A(t))\}_{t=0}^{\infty}
are i.i.d.

Thus, it suffices to evaluate
\lim_{t\to\infty}(\widehat{B}(0))^{t}

{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\widehat{\mathbf{x}}}(t+1)=\Gamma Q\widehat{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{x}}}(t)+(I-\Gamma){\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{u}}

{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\widehat{\mathbf{y}}}(t)=(\widehat{B}(0))^{t}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{y}}(0)}

\lim_{t\to\infty}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\widehat{\mathbf{y}}}(t)=\begin{pmatrix}O_{n\times n}&V\\

That is,

\lim_{t\to\infty}(\widehat{B}(0))^{t}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{y}}(0)=\begin{pmatrix}O_{n\times n}&V\\

As
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{y}}(0)
(which stacks the initial states and the external influences) is arbitrary, it follows that
{\left(\lim_{t\to\infty}(\widehat{B}(0))^{t}\right)_{i\,n+j}=v_{ij}}
for all

.

4.3
Rank-One Perturbation of the Friedkin-Johnsen Variant

Another random adaptation-based variant of the Friedkin-Johnsen model can be obtained by letting the opinion of each agent ‘mutate’ to any external influence with a fixed probability distribution, i.e., in the
t
th time period, agent
i
either adapts to a neighbor’s opinion or adapts to one of the prejudices independently of her past choices. With probability
\gamma_{i}(t)\in(0,1)
, the agent follows the adaptation scheme described earlier (in (
2
)), and with probability
{1-\gamma_{i}(t)}
, however, instead of adapting to one fixed prejudice, she chooses an opinion from the set
{\mathcal{U}=\{u_{i}:i\in[n]\}}
, according to a stochastic vector
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{q}}
on
\mathcal{U}
. That is,

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{x}}(t+1)=\Lambda(t)A(t){\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{x}}(t)+(I-\Lambda(t))C(t){\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{u}},

(11)

where
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{x}}(t)
and
A(t)
are as before,
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{u}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is the vector of external influences,
\{\Lambda(t)\}_{t=0}^{\infty}
is a sequence of random binary diagonal matrices with
\text{Pr}(\lambda_{i}(t)=1)=\gamma_{i}(t)
, and
\{C(t)\}_{t=0}^{\infty}
is a sequence of i.i.d. binary stochastic random matrices. For any
i\in[n]
,
\text{Pr}(C_{ij}(t)=1)=q_{j}
for all
j\in[n]
, independent of the other rows. Here
\widehat{C}(t)=\mathbf{1}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{q}}^{T}
, for all
t\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, which is a rank-one matrix for all
t\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
.

Theorem 6
.

Consider the dynamics (
11
) where
|\mathcal{U}|=n
,
\{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}x}_{i}(0):i\in[n]\}\bigcap\mathcal{U}={\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\emptyset}
, and the matrix pairs
\{(\Lambda(t),A(t))\}_{t=0}^{\infty}
are i.i.d. with

and
\Gamma:=\widehat{\Lambda}(t)
for all
t\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
. Also, suppose that
\Gamma<I
. Then the following hold true.

(i)

We have
\lim_{t\to\infty}\text{Pr}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}x}_{i}(t)=u_{j})=v_{ij}
for all
{i,j\in[n]}
, where
\{v_{ij}:i,j\in[n]\}
are the entries of

\displaystyle V

\displaystyle:=(I-\Gamma Q)^{-1}(I-\Gamma)\mathbf{1}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{q}}^{T}.

(ii)

There a.s. exists a random time
T<\infty
such that
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}x}_{i}(t)\in\mathcal{U}}
for all
t\geq T
.

Proof.

We can rewrite the dynamics as
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{y}}(t+1)=D(t){\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{y}}(t)}
, where
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{y}^{T}}(t)=\begin{bmatrix}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{x}}^{T}(t)&{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{u}}^{T}\end{bmatrix}
and

\displaystyle D(t)

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\Lambda(t)A(t)&({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}I}-\Lambda(t))C(t)\\
O_{n\times n}&I_{n\times n}\end{bmatrix}.

(12)

So, we have
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{y}}(t)=D(t:0){\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{y}}(0)
, for all
t\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
. Since the elements of
\mathcal{U}
are distinct and
\{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}x}_{i}(0)\mid i\in[n]\}\bigcap\mathcal{U}={\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\emptyset}
, similar to (
4.2
), we have
\text{Pr}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}x}_{i}(t)=u_{j})=\left(\left(\widehat{D}(0)\right)^{t}\right)_{i\,n+j}
. To compute the limiting marginal probability note that

\displaystyle\left(\widehat{D}(0)\right)^{t}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\Gamma Q&({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}I}-\Gamma)\widehat{C}(0)\\
O_{n\times n}&I_{n\times n}\end{bmatrix}^{t}=\begin{bmatrix}\left(\Gamma Q\right)^{t}&R(t)\\
O_{n\times n}&I_{n\times n}\end{bmatrix},

(13)

where
{R(t)=\sum_{k=0}^{t-1}\left(\Gamma Q\right)^{k}(I-\Gamma)\mathbf{1}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{q}}^{T}.}

Since
{Q\in\mathcal{P}_{n\times n}}
, we know that the maximum absolute value of eigenvalues of
\Gamma Q
is less than
1
since
\Gamma Q\leq\gamma_{\max}Q
, where
\gamma_{\max}=\max_{i\in[n]}\gamma_{i}\in(0,1)
. Using a result on Neumann Series (Eq. (7.10.11) in
[
43
]
), we have
{\lim_{t\to\infty}R(t)=(I-\Gamma Q)^{-1}(I-\Gamma)\mathbf{1}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{q}}^{T}}=V
, and
\lim_{t\to\infty}\left(\Gamma Q\right)^{t}=O_{n\times n}.

For
(ii)
, note that the probability that all the agents adapt to an external influence at any time is
{\prod_{i=1}^{n}(1-\gamma_{i})>0}
. Since
\{\Lambda(t)\}_{t=0}^{\infty}
are independent, it follows from the Second Borel-Cantelli Lemma that there exists an increasing sequence of random times
\{T_{k}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
such that
\Lambda(T_{k})=1
. This implies that
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}x}_{i}(T_{1}+1)\in\mathcal{U}
for all
i\in[n]
. Therefore, for all
(t_{0},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{x}}(t_{0}))\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
, there
a.s.
exists a random time
T<\infty
such that
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}x}_{i}(t)\in\mathcal{U}
for all
t\geq T
.

Remark 3
.

Suppose, in addition to the assumptions in Theorem
6
, all the agents have identical susceptibility, i.e.,
\Gamma=\gamma I
for some
\gamma\in(0,1)
. Then, since
{Q\in\mathcal{P}_{n\times n}}
, we have
{R(t)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{k}(1-\gamma)\mathbf{1}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{q}}^{T}}
, and since
{\gamma\in(0,1)}
, we have
{V=\mathbf{1}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{p}}^{T}}
. Furthermore, for this case, the result extends to all stochastic chains
\{Q(t)\}_{t=0}^{\infty}
and not just to identically distributed chains, as
{R(t)=\sum_{k=0}^{t}\gamma^{k}(1-\gamma)Q(k+1:0)\mathbf{1}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{q}}^{T}}
with
{Q(t+1:0)\mathbf{1}=\mathbf{1}}
, for all
t\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, which implies that
{V=\lim_{t\to\infty}R(t)=\mathbf{1}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{q}}^{T}}
. Therefore, in this case, the limiting marginal probability distribution is independent of the degree of susceptibility.

Remark 4
.

Note that the dynamics studied in Sections
4.2
and
4.3
can be obtained as special cases of the generalized model studied in
[
44
]
. However, while the results of
[
44
]
imply convergence in distribution and provide the expected values of the steady states, they do not characterize the distributions of these steady states, nor do they show the a.s. convergence of the agents’ opinions to the prejudice set.

6
CONCLUSION

We proposed and studied a random adaptation variant of time-varying distributed averaging dynamics in discrete time. We have shown that our models give rise to novel interpretations for the concepts of ergodicity and absolute probability sequences, both of which are pivotal to the study of stochastic chains. We have also proposed a time-varying, stochastic analog of the well-known Friedkin-Johnsen opinion dynamics and analyzed the asymptotic behavior of the probability distributions of the agents’ opinions. Finally, we have considered a rank-one perturbation of our base-case stochastic dynamics and studied its asymptotic behavior.

Our random adaptation interpretation of averaging (and related) dynamics opens up many avenues avenues for further investigations including the (time-varying) controlled variation of Friedkin-Johnsen dynamics through the lens of random adaptation dynamics, and the connection of mutation-adaptation learning dynamics (in game theoretic setting) and our proposed random adaptation schemes.
