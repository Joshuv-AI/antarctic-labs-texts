Almost symmetric good semigroups
L. Casabella, M. D’Anna

Abstract
The class of good semigroups is a class of subsemigroups of ℕ h \displaystyle\mathbb{N}^{h} , that includes the value semigroups of rings associated to curve singularities and their blowups, and allows to study combinatorically the properties of these rings. In this paper we give a characterization of almost symmetric good subsemigroups of ℕ h \displaystyle\mathbb{N}^{h} , extending known results in numerical semigroup theory and in one-dimensional ring theory, and we apply these results to obtain new results on almost Gorenstein one-dimensional analytically unramified rings. Keywords : good semigroup, almost-symmetry, curve singularity, almost Gorenstein rings, canonical ideal. Mathematics Subject Classification : 13A15, 13H10, 14H99, 20M12, 20M25

Introduction

Let
\displaystyle\mathbb{N}
be the set of nonnegative integers;
good semigroups
are a class of subsemigroups of
\displaystyle\mathbb{N}^{h}
, with
\displaystyle h\in\mathbb{N}
,
\displaystyle h\geq 1
(see the formal definition in Section 1). Their definition was given in
[
4
]
in order to describe value semigroups of noetherian, analytically unramified,
one-dimensional, semilocal reduced rings, even though the properties of these value semigroups were already studied in
[
5
]
,
[
7
]
,
[
8
]
,
[
9
]
,
[
15
]
,
[
16
]
,
[
17
]
. The class of rings mentioned above includes the local rings arising from curve singularities (and from their blowups), possibly with more than one branch. However, as shown in
[
4
]
, there are good semigroups that are not value semigroups of any ring.

If
\displaystyle S
is a value subsemigroup of
\displaystyle\mathbb{N}^{h}
, the integer
\displaystyle h
represents the number of branches of the singularity. In the one branch case, the value semigroup is a numerical semigroup, and the algebraic properties of the ring can be translated and studied at semigroup level. Some of the results in this direction have been proved to hold for
\displaystyle h>1
as well. For instance, the numerical characterization of a one-dimensional analytically irreducible local Gorenstein domain via symmetric numerical semigroups (see
[
22
]
) can be generalized to analytically unramified local rings (see
[
16
]
and also
[
8
]
); analogously, the numerical characterization of the canonical module in the analytically irreducible case (see
[
20
]
) can be given also in the more general case (see
[
9
]
).

Dealing with birational extensions of an analytically unramified ring
\displaystyle R
, one has to work with both local and semilocal rings; this fact is well represented at semigroup level, since it is possible to give a satisfying definition of local good semigroup that, for value semigroups, corresponds to the local ring case; with this definition one can show that any good semigroup is a direct product of local good semigroups that, when
\displaystyle S=v(R)
, correspond to the value semigroups of the localizations of
\displaystyle R
at its maximal ideals (see
[
4
]
).

These facts motivated the interest in studying good semigroups, and, since not every good semigroup is a value semigroup of some ring, it is often necessary to work on good semigroups in a purely combinatorial setting.
Despite their name, good semigroups present some problems that make their study difficult; e.g. they are not finitely generated as monoids (but, on the other hand, they can be completely determined by a finite set of elements; see
[
17
]
and
[
10
]
) and they are not closed under finite intersections.
Moreover, if we consider good ideals of good semigroups (e.g. the ideals
arising as values of ideals of the corresponding ring), this class is not closed under sums and differences (see, for instance,
[
4
]
and
[
21
]
).
Therefore, unlike what happens for numerical semigroups (in analogy to analytically irreducible domains), it is not easy to define for good semigroups some concepts that translates analogous ring concepts, like the embedding dimension and the type (see
[
12
]
and
[
23
]
).
Finally, when working with good semigroups, technical problems often arise if
\displaystyle h\geq 3
and, for this reason,
several papers on the subject are limited to the very special case
\displaystyle h=2
(see e.g.
[
17
]
,
[
5
]
,
[
11
]

and
[
12
]
).

Almost Gorenstein analytically unramified rings and almost symmetric numerical semigroups were introduced in
[
6
]
in order to find two classes of rings and numerical semigroups, respectively, that are close to Gorenstein rings and to symmetric numerical semigroups, in certain aspects.
The notion of almost Gorenstein ring was generalized for any one-dimensional ring in
[
18
]
and, subsequently, for rings in any dimension admitting a canonical ideal (see
[
19
]
). Since then, almost Gorenstein rings have been intensively studied, being a useful intermediate class between Cohen-Macaulay and Gorenstein rings. Similarly, the notion of almost symmetric numerical semigroups was generalized to good semigroups in
[
4
]
.

A remarkable property shown in
[
6
]
for a one-dimensional analytically unramified local ring
\displaystyle(R,\mathfrak{m})
is that such a ring is almost Gorenstein of maximal embedding dimension if and only if
\displaystyle\mathfrak{m}:\mathfrak{m}
is Gorenstein, and an analogous result was proved in the same paper for numerical semigroups. These two results were the starting point for other similar investigations. In particular, in
[
18
]
the authors generalized the first one for any one-dimensional Cohen-Macaulay local ring admitting a canonical ideal and, in
[
14
]
, the following more general result is proved:

Proposition

[
14
, Proposition 3.2]
. Let
\displaystyle(R,\mathfrak{m})
be a one-dimensional Cohen-Macaulay local ring
admitting a canonical ideal. Then

\displaystyle R
is a one-dimensional almost Gorenstein
ring if and only if
\displaystyle\mathfrak{m}
is a canonical ideal of
\displaystyle\mathfrak{m}:\mathfrak{m}
.

Note that an analogous result was stated in a non-noetherian setting in
[
2
]
, but with an additional hypothesis.
As for numerical semigroups, the corresponding result of the above proposition was proved in
[
3
]
.

If we consider good semigroups, fewer results of this kind are known. More precisely, a partial result was proved in
[
12
]
for the case
\displaystyle h=2
, assuming that
\displaystyle S
is a local good semigroup of maximal embedding dimension. In this context, the main goal of this paper is to prove the following result:

Theorem 2.4.

Let
\displaystyle h\geq 2
, let

\displaystyle S\subseteq\mathbb{N}^{h}
be a local almost symmetric good semigroup and let
\displaystyle M=S\setminus\{\boldsymbol{0}\}

be its maximal ideal.
Then
\displaystyle S
is almost symmetric if and only if
\displaystyle M-M=\{\boldsymbol{\alpha}\in\mathbb{N}^{h}\mid\boldsymbol{\alpha}+M\subseteq M\}
is a good semigroup and
\displaystyle M
is a canonical ideal of
\displaystyle M-M
.

Hence, after recalling in Section 1 the basic notions necessary to develop our arguments,
in Section 2 we give a description of the canonical ideal of
\displaystyle M-M
, assuming it is a good semigroup (Proposition
2.3
), and we use it to prove the main theorem,
together with a characterization of local almost symmetric good semigroups of maximal embedding dimension via
\displaystyle M-M
(Corollary
2.6
). Afterwards, we generalize these results to
the non-local case and, in Section 3, we show how our results imply new characterizations of almost Gorenstein analytically unramified one-dimensional local or semilocal rings (see, e.g. Proposition
3.3
).

1
Preliminaries on good semigroups

Let
\displaystyle\mathbb{N}
be the set of nonnegative integers and let
\displaystyle h\geq 2
be an integer. As usual,
\displaystyle\leq
stands for the natural partial ordering on
\displaystyle\mathbb{N}^{h}
:
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\alpha}\leq\boldsymbol{\beta}
if
\displaystyle\alpha_{i}\leq\beta_{i}
for every
\displaystyle i=1,\dots,h
.
Given
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta}\in\mathbb{N}^{h}
, the infimum of the set
\displaystyle\{\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta}\}
(with respect to
\displaystyle\leq
) will be denoted by
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\alpha}\wedge\boldsymbol{\beta}
. Hence
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\alpha}\wedge\boldsymbol{\beta}=(\min(\alpha_{1},\beta_{1}),\dots,\min(\alpha_{h},\beta_{h}))
.

A submonoid
\displaystyle S\subseteq\mathbb{N}^{h}
is said to be a
good semigroup
if the following
three properties are satisfied:

(G1)

if
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta}\in S
, then
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\alpha}\wedge\boldsymbol{\beta}\in S
;

(G2)

if
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta}\in S
,
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\alpha}\neq\boldsymbol{\beta}
and
\displaystyle\alpha_{i}=\beta_{i}
for some
\displaystyle i\in\{1,\dots,h\}
, then there exists
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\delta}\in S
such that
\displaystyle\delta_{i}>\alpha_{i}=\beta_{i}
and
\displaystyle\delta_{j}\geq\min(\alpha_{j},\beta_{j})
for all
\displaystyle j\neq i
(and equality holds if
\displaystyle\alpha_{j}\neq\beta_{j}
);

(G3)

there exists
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{c}\in\mathbb{N}^{h}
such that
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{c}+\mathbb{N}^{h}\subseteq S
.

Notice that for
\displaystyle h=1
, properties
(G1)
and
(G2)
become meaningless
and property
(G3)
, for a submonoid
\displaystyle S
of
\displaystyle\mathbb{N}
, means that
\displaystyle S
is
a numerical semigroup. Hence we can consider numerical semigroups as the good semigroups in the case
\displaystyle h=1
.

A
relative ideal
of a good semigroup
\displaystyle S\subseteq\mathbb{N}^{h}
is a subset
\displaystyle E
of
\displaystyle\mathbb{Z}^{h}
such that
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\alpha}+\boldsymbol{\beta}\in E
for any
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\alpha}\in S
and
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\beta}\in E
, and there is a
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\delta}\in S
, such that
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\delta}+E\subseteq S
. If
\displaystyle E\subseteq S
then we will simply say that
\displaystyle E
is an
ideal
of
\displaystyle S
.
A relative ideal
\displaystyle E
satisfying
(G1)
and
(G2)
is called
good ideal
. Note that a relative ideal necessarily satisfies
(G3)
, since it is closed up to sums with elements of
\displaystyle S
.
Observe that, given two relative ideals
\displaystyle E,F
of
\displaystyle S
, the following sets are relative ideals as well:

•

\displaystyle E+F:=\{\boldsymbol{\alpha}+\boldsymbol{\beta}\mid\boldsymbol{\alpha}\in E,\boldsymbol{\beta}\in F\}
,

•

\displaystyle E-F:=\{\boldsymbol{\alpha}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}\mid\boldsymbol{\alpha}+F\subseteq E\}
.

However, if
\displaystyle E
and
\displaystyle F
are good, it is not true in general that
\displaystyle E+F
and
\displaystyle E-F
are good, as shown in
[
4
, Example 2.10]
.

If the only element of
\displaystyle S
with a zero component is
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{0}=(0,0,\dots 0)
, we will say that
\displaystyle S
is
local
. In this case, the set
\displaystyle M:=S\setminus\{\boldsymbol{0}\}
is a
good ideal and we will call it the
maximal ideal
of
\displaystyle S
. Moreover, by property
(G1)
, a local good semigroup has a minimum nonzero element. We will denote it by
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{e}=(e_{1},\dots,e_{h})
and call it the
multiplicity
vector of
\displaystyle S
.

In
[
4
]
it is proved that any good semigroup can be uniquely expressed as a direct product of local ones, i.e.
\displaystyle S=S_{1}\times\dots\times S_{r}
and, denoting by
\displaystyle M_{i}
the maximal ideal of
\displaystyle S_{i}
, we will denote by
\displaystyle J
the product
\displaystyle M_{1}\times\dots\times M_{r}
. In this case,
\displaystyle J
is a good ideal, that we will call the
Jacobson ideal
of
\displaystyle S
. Notice that any
\displaystyle S_{i}
in this expression is a
subsemigroup of
\displaystyle\mathbb{N}^{h_{i}}
, with
\displaystyle h_{1}+h_{2}+\dots+h_{r}=h
, and the subset of components
\displaystyle\{j_{1},\dots,j_{h_{i}}\}\subseteq\{1,\dots,h\}
, where
\displaystyle S_{i}
lives,
will be called the
support
of
\displaystyle S_{i}
.

The above notations will be fixed for the rest of the paper. In addition, we will always assume that
\displaystyle S\neq\mathbb{N}^{h}
.

Notice that, for any relative ideal
\displaystyle E
,
it holds true that
\displaystyle E-E
is a submonoid of
\displaystyle\mathbb{N}^{h}
, not necessarily good, containing
\displaystyle S
.
In particular, if
\displaystyle S
is a local good semigroup, then

\displaystyle S-M=M-M=\{\boldsymbol{\alpha}\in\mathbb{N}^{h}\mid\boldsymbol{\alpha}+M\subseteq M\}
is both a monoid
such that
\displaystyle S\subsetneq M-M\subseteq\mathbb{N}^{h}
and a relative ideal of
\displaystyle S
.
The same holds, in general, for
\displaystyle J-J
.

In the next section, we will need the following lemma in order to deal with the non-local case. Its proof is straightforward.

Lemma 1.1
.

Let
\displaystyle S\subset\mathbb{N}^{h}
be a good semigroup and let
\displaystyle S=S_{1}\times\dots\times S_{r}
be its representation
as direct product of local good semigroups. Then
\displaystyle J-J=(M_{1}-M_{1})\times\dots\times(M_{r}-M_{r})
.

A relative ideal that plays a central role in the theory of good semigroups is the standard canonical ideal. In order to give its definition, we need to introduce some more notation.

Definition 1.2
.

Let
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\alpha}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
,
\displaystyle i\in\{1,\dots,d\}
. We define:

\displaystyle\Delta_{i}(\alpha):=\{\beta\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}\mid\alpha_{i}=\beta_{i}\text{, }\alpha_{j}<\beta_{j}\kern 5.0pt\forall j\neq i\}
;

\displaystyle\Delta(\alpha):=\{\beta\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}\mid\exists\,i\text{ such that }\alpha_{i}=\beta_{i}\text{, }\alpha_{j}<\beta_{j}\kern 5.0pt\forall j\neq i\}=\bigcup_{i}\Delta_{i}(\alpha)
;

\displaystyle\Delta_{i}^{S}(\alpha):=\Delta_{i}(\alpha)\cap S
;

\displaystyle\Delta^{S}(\alpha):=\Delta(\alpha)\cap S
.

The minimum
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{c}\in\mathbb{N}^{h}
satisfying
(G3)
will be called the
conductor
of
\displaystyle S
.
We additionally set
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\gamma}:=\boldsymbol{c}-\boldsymbol{1}
(where
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{1}=(1,1,\dots,1)
) and call it the
Frobenius vector
of
\displaystyle S
.
Observe that, by properties
(G1)
and
(G2)
,
\displaystyle\Delta^{S}(\boldsymbol{\gamma})=\varnothing
.

Definition 1.3
.

The
standard canonical ideal
of
\displaystyle S
is

K(S):=\{\boldsymbol{\alpha}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}\mid\Delta^{S}(\boldsymbol{\gamma}-\boldsymbol{\alpha})=\varnothing\}.

This relative ideal was firstly defined in the local case in
[
9
]
. In
[
4
]

it was noticed that its definition can be stated in the general case
and that, if
\displaystyle S=S_{1}\times\dots\times S_{r}
, then
\displaystyle K(S)=K(S_{1})\times\dots\times K(S_{r})
. It is not difficult to see that
\displaystyle K(S)
is a good relative ideal of
\displaystyle S
such that

\displaystyle S\subseteq K(S)\subseteq\mathbb{N}^{h}
.

A good relative ideal
\displaystyle K^{\prime}
of
\displaystyle S
is called
canonical ideal
of
\displaystyle S
if there exists
\displaystyle x\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
such that
\displaystyle K^{\prime}=K(S)+x
.

The class of canonical ideals can be characterized via a duality property that we are going to state. This property was proved in
[
21
]
for the local case, but it can be stated for any good semigroup, using the representation of
\displaystyle K(S)
as a direct product of standard canonical ideals.

Lemma 1.4
.

[
21
, Theorem 5.2.7]

Given a good relative ideal
\displaystyle K^{\prime}
of
\displaystyle S
, the following conditions are equivalent:

(i)

\displaystyle K^{\prime}
is a canonical ideal of
\displaystyle S
;

(ii)

\displaystyle K^{\prime}-(K^{\prime}-I)=I
for every good relative ideal
\displaystyle I
of
\displaystyle S
.

In the same paper, the authors proved the following key result, that, again, holds true in the non-local case as well.

Lemma 1.5
.

[
21
, Proposition 5.2.7]

If
\displaystyle E
is a good relative ideal of
\displaystyle S
, then
\displaystyle K(S)-E
is a good relative ideal of
\displaystyle S
.

We list below three key properties of canonical ideals that will be useful in the next section. Let
\displaystyle K^{\prime}
be a canonical ideal of
\displaystyle S
and let
\displaystyle E,F
be two relative ideals. Then:

•

\displaystyle E\subseteq F
if and only if
\displaystyle K^{\prime}-F\subseteq K^{\prime}-E
;

•

\displaystyle E=F
if and only if
\displaystyle K^{\prime}-F=K^{\prime}-E
;

•

\displaystyle K^{\prime}-K^{\prime}=S
.

These properties can be easily deduced by Lemma
1.4
, keeping in mind, as for the third one, that
\displaystyle K^{\prime}-K^{\prime}
is independent of translations of
\displaystyle K^{\prime}
and that
\displaystyle K(S)=K(S)-S
.

An important class of good semigroups is the class of symmetric semigroups, which corresponds to the class of Gorenstein rings.

Definition 1.6
.

A good semigroup
\displaystyle S
is called
symmetric
if, for every
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\alpha}\in\mathbb{N}^{d}
,
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\alpha}\in S
if and only if
\displaystyle\Delta^{S}(\boldsymbol{\gamma}-\boldsymbol{\alpha})=\varnothing
.

Notice that, for any good semigroup
\displaystyle S
,
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\alpha}\in S
implies
\displaystyle\Delta^{S}(\boldsymbol{\gamma}-\boldsymbol{\alpha})=\varnothing
, but in general the converse is not true.

The definition of symmetry is clearly equivalent to the condition
\displaystyle S=K(S)
; hence a good semigroup
\displaystyle S
is symmetric if and only if every ideal of
\displaystyle S
is bidual (or reflexive), i.e.
\displaystyle S-(S-E)=E
. In the non-local case, the symmetry of a good semigroup is also equivalent to the property that every component
\displaystyle S_{i}
of
\displaystyle S
is symmetric. This fact was proved explicitly
in
[
12
]
for the case
\displaystyle h=2
, but the same proof holds in general.

Good semigroups are not finitely generated in general. Therefore, it is not possible
to define the embedding dimension of a local good semigroup just by generalizing the definition given for
local rings or for numerical semigroups. A definition of embedding dimension of a local good semigroup is given in
[
23
]
, but it is very technical and we will not need to use it
in this paper. In any case, keeping in mind that a one-dimensional noetherian local ring
\displaystyle(R,\mathfrak{m})
is of maximal embedding dimension (i.e. its embedding dimension is the same as its multiplicity) if and only if
\displaystyle\mathfrak{m}:\mathfrak{m}=x^{-1}\mathfrak{m}
(where
\displaystyle x
is a minimal reduction of
\displaystyle\mathfrak{m}
) and the same holds for numerical semigroups,
we can say that a local good semigroup is of
maximal embedding dimension

if
\displaystyle M-M=M-\boldsymbol{e}
. Notice that this condition implies that

\displaystyle M-M
is a good semigroup (not necessarily local).
This definition is consistent with the one
given in
[
23
]
.

2
Almost symmetric good semigroups

2.1
The local case

Definition 2.1
.

A local good semigroup
\displaystyle S
is called
almost symmetric
if
\displaystyle K(S)+M=M
.

Notice that this is equivalent to saying that
\displaystyle K(S)+M\subseteq M
, since the reverse inclusion always holds, because
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{0}\in K(S)
.

Moreover, for any local good semigroup,
it holds true that
\displaystyle M-M\subseteq K(S)\cup\Delta(\boldsymbol{\gamma})
: if

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\alpha}\in(M-M)\setminus\Delta(\gamma)
and
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\alpha}\not\in K(S)
, then there would exist a nonzero
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\beta}\in\Delta^{S}(\boldsymbol{\gamma}-\boldsymbol{\alpha})
; therefore
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\alpha}+\boldsymbol{\beta}\in\Delta^{S}(\boldsymbol{\gamma})
, contradiction.
Notice that, in the case of numerical semigroups, the above inclusion is reduced to
\displaystyle M-M\subseteq K(S)\cup F
, where
\displaystyle F
is the Frobenius number of
\displaystyle S
.

In
[
4
]
it is shown that almost symmetric local good semigroups can be
characterized
by the equality of these two sets:

Proposition 2.2
.

[
4
, Lemma 3.5]

A local good semigroup
\displaystyle S
is almost symmetric if and only if
\displaystyle K(S)\cup\Delta(\boldsymbol{\gamma})=M-M
.

Proposition
2.2
shows that, if
\displaystyle S
is an almost symmetric local good semigroup, then
\displaystyle M-M
is a good relative ideal of
\displaystyle S
, because it is the union of a good relative ideal and
\displaystyle\Delta(\gamma)
. Hence, in this case,
\displaystyle M-M
is a (not necessarily local) good semigroup.

In order to prove Theorem
2.4
, we firstly need the following.

Proposition 2.3
.

Let
\displaystyle S
be a local good semigroup, of multiplicity vector
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{e}
, such that
\displaystyle M-M
is a good semigroup. Then the standard canonical ideal of
\displaystyle M-M
is

K(M-M)=(K(S)-(M-M))-\boldsymbol{e}.

Proof.

For the sake of simplicity we set
\displaystyle T=M-M
and
\displaystyle K=K(S)
. Our aim is to show that
\displaystyle K(T)=(K-T)-\boldsymbol{e}
.

Firstly we prove that
\displaystyle K-T
is a canonical ideal of
\displaystyle T
.
In fact,
\displaystyle K-T
is not only a good relative ideal of
\displaystyle S
, but also of
\displaystyle T
, because
if
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\delta}\in T=M-M
and
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\beta}\in K-T
, we get
\displaystyle(\boldsymbol{\alpha}+\boldsymbol{\beta})+\boldsymbol{\delta}=(\boldsymbol{\alpha}+\boldsymbol{\delta})+\boldsymbol{\beta}\in T+(K-T)\subseteq K
, that is
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\alpha}+\boldsymbol{\beta}\in K-T
.
By Lemmas
1.4
and
1.5
, it is sufficient to show that, for every good relative ideal
\displaystyle E
of
\displaystyle T
, it holds
\displaystyle(K-T)-((K-T)-E)=E
.
Using the fact that for any three relative ideals
\displaystyle A,B,C
it holds that

\displaystyle(A-B)-C=A-(B+C)
, we obtain

(K-T)-((K-T)-E)=(K-T)-(K-(T+E)).

Since
\displaystyle E
and
\displaystyle K-E
are both relative ideals of
\displaystyle T
, then
\displaystyle T+E=E
and
\displaystyle T+(K-E)=K-E
, hence we get

\displaystyle\displaystyle(K-T)-(K-(T+E))=(K-T)-(K-E)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle K-(T+(K-E))=K-(K-E).

Being
\displaystyle E
a good relative ideal of
\displaystyle T\supset S
, it is also a good relative ideal of
\displaystyle S
, therefore
\displaystyle K-(K-E)=E
, again by Lemma
1.4
.

It remains to prove that

\displaystyle T\subseteq(K-T)-\boldsymbol{e}\subseteq\mathbb{N}^{d}
. As for the first inclusion, this is equivalent to saying that
\displaystyle T+\boldsymbol{e}\subseteq K-T
. But
\displaystyle T+\boldsymbol{e}=(M-M)+\boldsymbol{e}\subseteq M
and
\displaystyle M+T=M+(M-M)\subseteq M\subset K
; thus
\displaystyle T+\boldsymbol{e}\subseteq M\subseteq K-T
, as desired.

We prove now the second inclusion,
\displaystyle(K-T)-\boldsymbol{e}\subseteq\mathbb{N}^{d}
. First of all, we show that
\displaystyle S-\mathbb{N}^{h}\subseteq T+\boldsymbol{e}
. Let
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\alpha}\in S-\mathbb{N}^{h}
; we have to prove that
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\alpha}-\boldsymbol{e}\in T
. This is equivalent to proving that for any
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\delta}\in M
,
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\alpha}-\boldsymbol{e}+\boldsymbol{\delta}\in M
. But
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\delta}-\boldsymbol{e}\geq 0
, i.e.
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\delta}-\boldsymbol{e}\in\mathbb{N}^{h}
, hence
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\alpha}+(\boldsymbol{\delta}-\boldsymbol{e})\in S
. Since
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{0}\notin S-\mathbb{N}^{d}
, because
\displaystyle S\subsetneq\mathbb{N}^{h}
, and
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\alpha}+(\boldsymbol{\delta}-\boldsymbol{e})\geq\boldsymbol{\alpha}
, we must have
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\alpha}+\boldsymbol{\delta}-\boldsymbol{e}\in M
.

Finally notice that
\displaystyle K-\mathbb{N}^{h}=S-\mathbb{N}^{h}
: indeed,
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{0}\in K
and so
\displaystyle K+\mathbb{N}^{h}=\mathbb{N}^{h}
; therefore
\displaystyle S-\mathbb{N}^{h}=(K-K)-\mathbb{N}^{h}=K-(K+\mathbb{N}^{h})=K-\mathbb{N}^{h}
. Hence
\displaystyle K-\mathbb{N}^{h}\subseteq T+\boldsymbol{e}
and
dualizing with
\displaystyle K
we obtain
\displaystyle K-(T+\boldsymbol{e})\subseteq\mathbb{N}^{d}
, that is our claim.
∎

We observe that the fact that
\displaystyle K(S)-(M-M)
is a canonical ideal of
\displaystyle M-M

can be deduced by
[
21
, Corollary 5.2.12]
, where the proof uses an explicit
computation of
\displaystyle K-E
for any relative ideal
\displaystyle E
of
\displaystyle S
. We included our proof
of this part in the above proposition, for the sake of completeness.

We now use Proposition
2.3
to prove Theorem
2.4

Theorem 2.4
.

A local good semigroup
\displaystyle S
is almost symmetric if and only if
\displaystyle M-M
is a good relative ideal of
\displaystyle S
and
\displaystyle M-\boldsymbol{e}
is the standard canonical ideal of
\displaystyle M-M
.

Proof.

For the sake of simplicity we set
\displaystyle K=K(S)
,
\displaystyle T=M-M
and
\displaystyle K^{\prime}=K(T)
.

\displaystyle(\Leftarrow)

Since the inclusion
\displaystyle T\subseteq K\cup\Delta(\boldsymbol{\gamma})
holds for every good semigroup, it is sufficient to show that
\displaystyle K\subseteq T
. By assumption
\displaystyle K^{\prime}=M-\boldsymbol{e}
, and by Proposition
2.3

\displaystyle K^{\prime}=(K-T)-\boldsymbol{e}
. Hence
\displaystyle M=K-T
, that, dualizing with
\displaystyle K
, is equivalent to
\displaystyle K-M=K-(K-T)=T
. But since
\displaystyle K
is a relative ideal of
\displaystyle S
, we have
\displaystyle K\subseteq K-M=T
, as desired.

\displaystyle(\Rightarrow)

We have already noticed that
\displaystyle S
almost symmetric implies that
\displaystyle T
is good.
In order to prove the second assertion we firstly need the following:

Claim: every good relative ideal
\displaystyle I
of
\displaystyle T
is bidual as an ideal of
\displaystyle S
.

Let
\displaystyle I
be a good relative ideal of
\displaystyle T
. We have
\displaystyle K+I\subseteq T+I=I
,
where the first inclusion is true because
\displaystyle S
is almost symmetric and the second one because
\displaystyle I
is an ideal of
\displaystyle T
. Conversely,
\displaystyle I\subseteq K+I
because
\displaystyle 0\in K
. Hence

(2.1)

K+I=I.

Now suppose that
\displaystyle I
is not bidual as ideal of
\displaystyle S
, then

\displaystyle I\subsetneq S-(S-I)\subseteq K-(S-I)
. Dualizing with
\displaystyle K
, this is equivalent to

\displaystyle S-I\subsetneq K-I
.
Therefore

S-I\subsetneq K-I=K-(K+I)=(K-K)-I=S-I

that is a contradiction. Hence the claim holds true.

We are now ready to prove that
\displaystyle M
is a canonical ideal of
\displaystyle T
.
By Lemma
1.4
, it is sufficient to show that
\displaystyle M-(M-I)=I
for every good ideal
\displaystyle I
of
\displaystyle T
. Firstly, we show that
\displaystyle M-I=S-I
. If not, there exists an element
\displaystyle x\in S-I
such that
\displaystyle x\not\in M-I
. Then
\displaystyle x+I
is an ideal of
\displaystyle S
not contained in
\displaystyle M
, i.e.

\displaystyle x+I=S
; and this means that
\displaystyle S
is itself a relative ideal of
\displaystyle T
, a contradiction. Hence
\displaystyle M-I=S-I
.

Since, by the claim,
\displaystyle I
is bidual as an ideal of
\displaystyle S
,

M-(M-I)\subseteq S-(M-I)=S-(S-I)=I.

The other inclusion is always true, hence we get
\displaystyle M-(M-I)=I
, as desired.
Finally, it is straightforward to check that
\displaystyle T\subseteq M-\boldsymbol{e}\subseteq\mathbb{N}^{h}
,
hence
\displaystyle M-\boldsymbol{e}
is the standard canonical ideal of
\displaystyle T
.

∎

\displaystyle J=M_{1}\times\dots\times M_{r}

\displaystyle J-J=(M_{1}-M_{1})\times\dots\times(M_{r}-M_{r})

\displaystyle K(S)=K(S_{1})\times\dots\times K(S_{r})

[
4
]
) and the previous theorems.
More precisely, we will say that
\displaystyle S
is
almost symmetric
if its components
are almost symmetric for every
\displaystyle i=1,\dots,r
. Moreover, denoting by
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{e_{i}}

the multiplicity vector of
\displaystyle S_{i}
, we can state the following results. Their proofs
are straightforward since, if
\displaystyle E,F
are relative ideals of
\displaystyle S
of the form

\displaystyle E_{1}\times\dots\times E_{r}
and
\displaystyle F_{1}\times\dots\times F_{r}
, where the supports of

\displaystyle E_{i}
and
\displaystyle F_{i}
are the same as the supports of
\displaystyle S_{i}
, then

\displaystyle E-F=(E_{1}-F_{1})\times\dots\times(E_{r}-F_{r})
and hence we can reduce our proofs to the local case.

Proposition 2.8
.

Preserving the above notations,
let
\displaystyle S_{1}\times\dots\times S_{r}
be a good semigroup and let
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{e}=(\boldsymbol{e_{1}},\dots,\boldsymbol{e_{r}})
and assume that
\displaystyle J-J
is a good semigroup. Then the standard canonical ideal of
\displaystyle J-J
is

K(J-J)=(K(S)-(J-J))-\boldsymbol{e}.

.

A good semigroup
\displaystyle S

\displaystyle J-J

\displaystyle S

\displaystyle J-\boldsymbol{e}

\displaystyle J-J
.

.

A good semigroup
\displaystyle S

\displaystyle J-J

3
Almost Gorenstein rings

We conclude this paper by applying the previous results to noetherian,
analytically unramified, residually rational, one-dimensional, reduced, semilocal rings.

As it is described in
[
4
]
, under these hypotheses, the integral closure
\displaystyle\overline{R}
of
\displaystyle R
is a direct product
of discrete valuation rings
\displaystyle V_{1}\times\dots\times V_{h}
, and therefore it is possible to associate with such a ring
\displaystyle R

a value semigroup
\displaystyle v(R)
that is a good subsemigroup of
\displaystyle\mathbb{N}^{h}
.
Note that the residually rational hypothesis means that all the residue fields of
\displaystyle R

and
\displaystyle\overline{R}
are isomorphic; if moreover the cardinality of these residue fields
is bigger than
\displaystyle h
, then the connection between the ring and its value semigroup
is particularly meaningful and we will assume these two hypotheses till the end of the paper. One of the reasons for this connection is
the fact that, if
\displaystyle I\subseteq J
are two fractional ideals of
\displaystyle R
, then
the length
\displaystyle\ell(J/I)
of
\displaystyle J/I
, as an
\displaystyle R
-module, can be computed looking at the value ideals
\displaystyle v(I)
and
\displaystyle v(J)
of
\displaystyle v(R)
; in particular, we will use the
fact that
\displaystyle I=J
if and only if
\displaystyle v(I)=v(J)
(see
[
9
, Corollary 2.5]
).
We also recall that algebroid curves belong to this class of rings
and that, in this case, the integer
\displaystyle h
represents the number of branches of the singularity.

If
\displaystyle R
is semilocal with maximal ideals
\displaystyle\mathfrak{m}_{1},\dots,\mathfrak{m}_{j}

(with
\displaystyle j\leq h
), then
\displaystyle R\cong R_{\mathfrak{m}_{1}}\times\dots\times R_{\mathfrak{m}_{j}}

(see
[
4
, Corollary 3.2]
). From this, it follows immediately that
\displaystyle v(R)=v(R_{\mathfrak{m}_{1}})\times\dots\times v(R_{\mathfrak{m}_{j}})
is the unique representation of
\displaystyle v(R)

as a direct product of local good semigroups. Moreover, the Jacobson radical

\displaystyle J(R)
coincides with
\displaystyle\mathfrak{m}_{1}R_{\mathfrak{m}_{1}}\times\dots\times\mathfrak{m}_{j}R_{\mathfrak{m}_{j}}
and hence
\displaystyle v(J(R))=J
, where
\displaystyle J
is the Jacobson ideal of
\displaystyle v(R)
.

In this context, we give the following definition.

Definition 3.1
.

A noetherian,
analytically unramified, one-dimensional, reduced local ring is said
to be
almost Gorenstein
if
\displaystyle\mathfrak{m}\omega_{R}=\mathfrak{m}
,
where
\displaystyle\omega_{R}
is a canonical ideal of
\displaystyle R
with the property that

\displaystyle R\subseteq\omega_{R}\subseteq\overline{R}
.

If
\displaystyle R
is semilocal,
following again
[
4
]
, we will say that
\displaystyle R
is almost Gorenstein if

\displaystyle R_{\mathfrak{m}_{i}}
is almost Gorenstein for any maximal ideal
\displaystyle\mathfrak{m}_{i}

of
\displaystyle R
.

In the local case,
\displaystyle(R,\mathfrak{m})
is almost Gorenstein if and only if
\displaystyle v(R)

is almost symmetric and the Cohen-Macaulay type of
\displaystyle R
equals the type of
\displaystyle v(R)

(
[
4
, Proposition 3.7]
). In fact, setting
\displaystyle v(R)=S
and
\displaystyle M=v(\mathfrak{m})
, in that paper it is proved that if
\displaystyle R
is almost Gorenstein then
\displaystyle v(\mathfrak{m}:\mathfrak{m})=M-M=K(S)\cup\Delta(\boldsymbol{\gamma})
and, therefore, we could restate that result saying that
\displaystyle(R,\mathfrak{m})

is almost Gorenstein if and only if
\displaystyle v(R)
is almost symmetric
and
\displaystyle v(\mathfrak{m}:\mathfrak{m})=M-M

(notice that, in general, only the inclusion
\displaystyle v(\mathfrak{m}:\mathfrak{m})\subseteq M-M
holds).
In light of Theorem
2.4
we can further reformulate this fact in the following way:

Corollary 3.2
.

Preserving the above assumptions, set
\displaystyle S=v(R)
and

\displaystyle M=v(\mathfrak{m})
. Then
\displaystyle(R,\mathfrak{m})

is almost Gorenstein if and only if
\displaystyle M-\boldsymbol{e}
is the standard canonical
ideal of
\displaystyle M-M
and
\displaystyle v(\mathfrak{m}:\mathfrak{m})=M-M
.

This result is not particularly useful from a computational point of view, since it is not easy at all to determine
\displaystyle\mathfrak{m}:\mathfrak{m}
and its value semigroup. However, if we restrict ourselves to the
maximal embedding dimension case, we get a stronger result.

Proposition 3.3
.

Preserving the above assumptions, set
\displaystyle S=v(R)
,

\displaystyle M=v(\mathfrak{m})
and assume that
\displaystyle R
is of maximal embedding dimension.
The following conditions are equivalent:

(i)

\displaystyle(R,\mathfrak{m})
is almost Gorenstein;

(ii)

\displaystyle M-\boldsymbol{e}
is a good symmetric semigroup;

(iii)

\displaystyle S
is almost symmetric.

Proof.

\displaystyle(i)\Leftrightarrow(ii)

It is well known that
\displaystyle R
is of maximal embedding dimension if and only if

\displaystyle\mathfrak{m}:\mathfrak{m}=\mathfrak{m}x^{-1}
, where
\displaystyle x\in\mathfrak{m}
is a minimal reduction of
\displaystyle\mathfrak{m}
, i.e., in
our context, an element of minimal non-zero value (that is
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{e}
). Hence
\displaystyle v(\mathfrak{m}x^{-1})=M-\boldsymbol{e}
. Moreover, the equality
\displaystyle\mathfrak{m}:\mathfrak{m}=\mathfrak{m}x^{-1}
holds if and only if
\displaystyle\mathfrak{m}x^{-1}
is a ring.

Applying
[
6
, Proposition 25]
, we get that
\displaystyle R
is almost Gorenstein if and only if

\displaystyle\mathfrak{m}:\mathfrak{m}=\mathfrak{m}x^{-1}
is a Gorenstein ring, that implies immediately that

\displaystyle M-\boldsymbol{e}=v(\mathfrak{m}x^{-1})
is a good symmetric semigroup.

Conversely, if
\displaystyle M-\boldsymbol{e}
is a good symmetric semigroup, since by hypothesis

\displaystyle\mathfrak{m}x^{-1}=\mathfrak{m}:\mathfrak{m}
is a ring, it has to be Gorenstein, having a symmetric value semigroup. The thesis follows again applying
[
6
, Proposition 25]
.

\displaystyle(ii)\Leftrightarrow(iii)

We need only to observe that
\displaystyle v(\mathfrak{m}:\mathfrak{m})\subseteq M-M\subseteq M-\boldsymbol{e}=v(\mathfrak{m}x^{-1})
and so
the hypothesis that
\displaystyle R
is of maximal embedding dimension implies
that
\displaystyle\subseteq M-M\subseteq M-\boldsymbol{e}
, i.e.
\displaystyle S
is of maximal embedding dimension.
Now the thesis follows by Corollary
2.6

∎

Example 3.4
.

The semigroup considered in Example
2.7
is the value semigroup of
the ring
\displaystyle R=k[[x,y,z,w]]/P_{1}\cap P_{2}

\displaystyle k

\displaystyle 3
elements,
\displaystyle P_{1}=(x^{3}-y^{2},z-x^{2},w-xy)
and
\displaystyle P_{2}=(x^{3}-y^{2},z,w)
. This fact can
be seen by considering the homomorphism
\displaystyle k[[x,y,z,w]]\rightarrow k[[t]]\times k[[u]]

induced by
\displaystyle x\mapsto(t^{2},u^{2})
,
\displaystyle y\mapsto(t^{3},u^{3})

\displaystyle z\mapsto(t^{4},0)
and
\displaystyle w\mapsto(t^{5},0)
, whose kernel is
\displaystyle P_{1}\cap P_{2}

\displaystyle R

\displaystyle 4

\displaystyle M-\boldsymbol{e}

\displaystyle R
is almost Gorenstein.

In light of the above discussion on the semilocal case, we can easily generalize the previous results.

Corollary 3.5
.

Let
\displaystyle R
be a one-dimensional, noetherian, analytically unramified, residually rational, reduced
semilocal ring. Set
\displaystyle S=v(R)
and let
\displaystyle J=v(J(R))
be its Jacobson ideal. The following conditions are equivalent:

(i)

\displaystyle R
is almost Gorenstein;

(ii)

\displaystyle J(R)
is a canonical ideal of
\displaystyle J(R):J(R)
;

(iii)

\displaystyle S
is almost symmetric and
\displaystyle v(J(R):J(R))=J-J
;

(iv)

\displaystyle J
is a canonical ideal of
\displaystyle J-J
and
\displaystyle v(J(R):J(R))=J-J
.

Proof.

\displaystyle(i)\Leftrightarrow(ii)

By definition
\displaystyle R
is almost Gorenstein if and only if
\displaystyle R_{\mathfrak{m}_{i}}
is almost Gorenstein for every
\displaystyle i=1,\dots,j
. By
[
14
, Proposition 3.2]
this is equivalent to saying that

\displaystyle\mathfrak{m}_{i}R_{\mathfrak{m}_{i}}
is a canonical ideal of
\displaystyle({\mathfrak{m}_{i}R_{\mathfrak{m}_{i}}}:{\mathfrak{m}_{i}R_{\mathfrak{m}_{i}}})
. Since
\displaystyle J(R)=\mathfrak{m}_{1}R_{\mathfrak{m}_{1}}\times\dots\times\mathfrak{m}_{j}R_{\mathfrak{m}_{j}}
and
\displaystyle J(R):J(R)=({\mathfrak{m}_{1}R_{\mathfrak{m}_{1}}}:{\mathfrak{m}_{1}R_{\mathfrak{m}_{1}}})\times\dots\times({\mathfrak{m}_{j}R_{\mathfrak{m}_{j}}}:{\mathfrak{m}_{j}R_{\mathfrak{m}_{j}}})
,
the thesis follows immediately.

\displaystyle(iii)\Leftrightarrow(iv)

In both conditions it is assumed that
\displaystyle v(J(R):J(R))=J-J
, hence

\displaystyle J-J
is good and the equivalence follows applying Theorem
2.9
.

\displaystyle(i)\Leftrightarrow(iii)

By
[
4
]
we know that, for every
\displaystyle i=1,\dots,j
,
the ring
\displaystyle R_{\mathfrak{m}_{i}}
is almost Gorenstein if and only if
\displaystyle S_{i}

is almost symmetric and
\displaystyle v(\mathfrak{m}_{i}R_{\mathfrak{m}_{i}}:\mathfrak{m}_{i}R_{\mathfrak{m}_{i}})=M_{i}-M_{i}
.
The thesis is now straightforward.

∎

Proposition
3.3
.

(i)

\displaystyle R
