Energy conservation and fluctuation theorem are incompatible for quantum work
Karen V. Hovhannisyan, Alberto Imparato

Abstract
Characterizing fluctuations of work in coherent quantum systems is notoriously problematic. Here we reveal the ultimate source of the problem by proving that ( 𝔄 \mathfrak{A} ) energy conservation and ( 𝔅 \mathfrak{B} ) the Jarzynski fluctuation theorem cannot be observed at the same time. Condition 𝔄 \mathfrak{A} stipulates that, for any initial state of the system, the measured average work must be equal to the difference of initial and final average energies, and that untouched systems must exchange deterministically zero work. Condition 𝔅 \mathfrak{B} is only for thermal initial states and encapsulates the second law of thermodynamics. We prove that 𝔄 \mathfrak{A} and 𝔅 \mathfrak{B} are incompatible for work measurement schemes that are differentiable functions of the state and satisfy two mild structural constraints. This covers all existing schemes and leaves the theoretical possibility of jointly observing 𝔄 \mathfrak{A} and 𝔅 \mathfrak{B} open only for a narrow class of exotic schemes. For the special but important case of state-independent schemes, the situation is much more rigid: we prove that, essentially, only the two-point measurement scheme is compatible with 𝔅 \mathfrak{B} .

\mathfrak{A}

Since the JE necessarily holds in classical mechanics, requiring it also in the quantum regime is a way of imposing a weak form of quantum–classical correspondence principle on the distribution of work, without going into the specifics of the quantum–classical transition itself. Thus, elegantly incorporating both the second law of thermodynamics and the correspondence principle, the JE can serve as a high-level filter through which physically meaningful definitions of work fluctuations ought to be able to pass, especially given the foundational role fluctuation theorems play in statistical mechanics

Undeniably, energy conservation is another such filter, and that virtually no definition of work fluctuations can pass through both, means that quantum work cannot be thought of as a classical random variable. In our definition, a measurement scheme obeys energy conservation if the work statistics measured by the scheme reflect energy conservation for the
unmeasured
system. Specifically, the average work performed on an unmeasured thermally isolated system is equal to the difference between the final and initial average energies of the system, and a proper measurement of work must output a random variable the first moment of which coincides with

\mathfrak{A}_{1}\hskip-1.99168pt
. Another simple consequence of energy conservation is that, if a system is untouched, then zero work with probability
1

\mathfrak{A}_{2}\hskip-1.99168pt

\mathfrak{A}_{1}

\mathfrak{A}

\mathfrak{A}_{1}
and
\mathfrak{A}_{2}
imposed jointly, the “
detailed

Our analysis is divided into two parts. First, we focus on work measuring schemes that
do not

\mathfrak{A}_{1}

Then, we ask whether

\mathfrak{A}_{1}

\mathfrak{B}

2
Formal setup

Throughout this paper, we will focus on closed systems (also called “conservative” in classical mechanics). This setting is the most general: any open system is a part of a larger closed system consisting of the system itself and all the external systems with which it is correlated, interacts, and will interact during the process under study
[
31
,
32
]
. Arguably, this picture also incorporates the process of quantum measurement
[
33
,
34
,
35
]
. For closed systems, a process is described by a time-dependent Hamiltonian
H(t)
that at the beginning (
t=t_{\mathrm{in}}
) has some value
H
and at the end (
t=t_{\mathrm{fin}}
) some value
H^{\prime}
, generating the unitary evolution operator
U=\overrightarrow{\exp}\big[-i\int_{t_{\mathrm{in}}}^{t_{\mathrm{fin}}}dtH(t)\big]
.

For any quantum process, the observed statistics of any quantity (e.g., work) can be described by a positive operator-valued measure (POVM)
[
36
]
. Since the whole physical arrangement through which the process is executed and measured is encapsulated in the POVM, by a “work measuring scheme” we will understand a POVM and a set of outcomes prescribed to it:
\{M_{W},W\}_{W}
, where
M_{W}
are the POVM elements and
W
are the associated outcomes. Thus, any random variable representing work is nothing but a (generalized) quantum measurement with outcomes
W
occurring with probabilities

\displaystyle p_{W}=\tr(\rho M_{W}),

(1)

where
\rho
is the initial state of the system.

When the measuring device, through which the statistics is observed, does not depend on the system’s initial state, then, expectedly, the corresponding POVM is also state-independent
[
9
]
. Surely, the scheme shall depend on the process (i.e.,
H
,
H^{\prime}
, and
U
). In general, however, both the POVM elements and the outcomes will also depend on the system’s initial state. Such state-dependent schemes comprise the most general set of work measurements.

In these terms,
\mathfrak{A}_{1}
means that

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \sum_{W}W\tr(\rho M_{W})&=\tr(U\rho\,U^{\dagger}H^{\prime})-\tr(\rho H)\\
&=\tr(\rho\,\Omega)\end{aligned}

(2)

is required to hold for all
\rho
’s. Here,
\tr(\rho H)
and
\tr(U\rho U^{\dagger}H^{\prime})
are, respectively, the initial and final average energies, and

\displaystyle\Omega=U^{\dagger}H^{\prime}U-H

(3)

is the so-called Heisenberg operator of work (HOW)
[
5
]
.

Condition
\mathfrak{B}
imposes

\displaystyle\sum_{W}e^{-\beta W}\tr(\tau_{\beta}M_{W})=e^{-\beta(F_{\beta}[H^{\prime}]-F_{\beta}[H])},

(4)

for any
\beta>0
, where
\tau_{\beta}=\frac{1}{Z_{\beta}[H]}e^{-\beta H}
is the thermal state, with
Z_{\beta}[H]=\tr e^{-\beta H}
and
F_{\beta}[H]=-\beta^{-1}\ln Z_{\beta}[H]
being, respectively, the partition function and equilibrium free energy.

Lastly,
\mathfrak{A}_{2}
concerns systems that are untouched, i.e., when
H(t)=H=\mathrm{const}
. In such a case,
\mathfrak{A}_{2}
demands that
p_{W=0}=\tr(\rho M_{W=0})=1
for any density matrix
\rho
.

In the TPM scheme

P_{a}\rho P_{a}/\tr(\rho P_{a})

with conditional probability
\tr\left[U\frac{P_{a}\rho P_{a}}{\tr(\rho P_{a})}U^{\dagger}P^{\prime}_{k}\right]

and

\displaystyle W_{ak}=E^{\prime}_{k}-E_{a},\qquad p_{ak}^{\mathrm{TPM}}=\tr\big(\rho M_{ak}^{\mathrm{TPM}}\big),

\displaystyle M_{ak}^{\mathrm{TPM}}=P_{a}U^{\dagger}P^{\prime}_{k}UP_{a}.

(6)

,

is the zero operator. Whereas there exist such

, and

\sum W_{ak}M_{ak}^{\mathrm{TPM}}

\mathfrak{A}_{1}

[

,
8
,
9
,

]
.

. Moreover, in the classical limit, where all the commutators go to zero, the discrepancy with

41

, and

42

12
]
), is yet another indication that the classical approach to determining the statistics of work is unlikely to provide useful guidance for the quantum regime.

3
Results

3.1
State-independent schemes

Let us define the JE class to be the set of all state-independent schemes that satisfy
\mathfrak{B}
, i.e., the Jarzynski equality for all thermal states. The following lemma, proven in Appendix
A
, gives a
complete characterization of the JE class.

Lemma 1

(Characterization of the JE class)
.

A state-independent scheme
\{(M_{W},W)\}_{W}
with a discrete set of outcomes
\{W\}
satisfies
\mathfrak{B}
iff

\displaystyle\{W\}_{W}=\{E^{\prime}_{k}-E_{a}\}_{a=1}^{A}\phantom{|}{}_{k=1}^{K},

(7)

so that the outcomes and the corresponding POVM elements can be labeled as
W_{ak}=E^{\prime}_{k}-E_{a}
and
M_{ak}
, along with

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a,\,b,\,m;\\
E^{\prime}_{m}-E_{b}+E_{a}=E^{\prime}_{k}\end{subarray}}\tr(M_{bm}P_{a})=\tr P^{\prime}_{k}

(8)

and

\displaystyle\tr(M_{bm}P_{a})=0\;\;\,\mathrm{if}\;\;\,E^{\prime}_{m}-E_{b}+E_{a}\not\in\{E^{\prime}_{k}\}_{k=1}^{K}.

(9)

A key consequence of Lemma
1
, also proven in Appendix
A
, is the following no-go theorem.

Theorem 1

(No-go for state-independent schemes)
.

If a state-independent scheme satisfies
\mathfrak{B}
, then it is incompatible with
\mathfrak{A}_{1}
.

Note that the JE class strictly contains the TPM scheme, which is expressed in the fact that Eqs. (
8
) and (
9
) [and even the more specific Eqs. (
A7
) and (
A8
)] allow for more general POVMs. However, if in addition to
\mathfrak{B}
, we merely require the scheme to respect
\mathfrak{A}_{1}

on thermal states,
the following theorem, proven in Appendix
B
, will hold.

Theorem 2

(JE
\Leftrightarrow
TPM)
.

For the (generic) case of nondegenerate
H
and nondegenerate set of outcomes, the schemes in the JE class that satisfy Eq. (
2
) for thermal initial states are equivalent to the TPM scheme.

We emphasize that here we do not require Eq. (
2
) to hold for nonthermal states. Theorem
2
shows that the TPM scheme is essentially uniquely determined by that, for thermal states, it reproduces the correct average work and Jarzynski equality. This relation can be expressed as JE
\Rightarrow
TPM. The reverse, namely, that the TPM scheme satisfies the JE (i.e., JE
\Leftarrow
TPM), has been known from the very conception of the scheme
[
3
,
4
]
, and was in fact what motivated its experimental implementations
[
43
,
44
,
45
]
. Theorem
2
thus means that, basically, JE
\Leftrightarrow
TPM for state-independent schemes.

Note that this equivalence establishes a crucial connection between the main result of Ref.
[
9
]
and the fluctuation theorem, thereby significantly extending and generalizing the physical scope of the former. In Ref.
[
9
]
, it was proven that, if a scheme produces the same statistics as the TPM scheme on diagonal states, then it simply coincides with the TPM scheme. With Theorem
2
, we now know that the a priori less restrictive and more physically meaningful condition of satisfying energy conservation and JE on thermal states is already sufficient to ensure identicality with the TPM scheme.

3.2
State-dependent schemes

Having excluded the possibility of simultaneously satisfying the conditions
\mathfrak{A}_{1}
and
\mathfrak{B}
with work measuring schemes that are limited to being state-independent, let us explore the opportunities when no such limitation is posed. That is, when the work-measuring POVM,
\{M_{W}\}
, and the corresponding set of outcomes,
\{W\}
, are allowed to depend not only on the process (i.e.,
U
,
H
, and
H^{\prime}
), but also on the system’s initial state,
\rho
. These schemes comprise the largest set of measurement protocols allowed by quantum mechanics.

On the one hand, this dramatically increases the set of POVMs to choose from. On the other hand, this happens at the cost of abandoning “universality”—in order to implement such a scheme, one has to acquire additional knowledge about incoming states and readjust the measurement setup accordingly, which is inconvenient from the practical standpoint.
Nonetheless, state-dependent schemes can be interesting from practical point of view. First of all, it not uncommon in experiments to have some information about how the system is prepared before the work exchange protocol is implemented, which means that one has some knowledge of the initial state. This is particularly relevant when the protocol is designed to extract work—one needs to at least partially know the initial state to be able to extract a nontrivial amount of work
[
46
,
47
]
. As regards the need to adjust the measurement device, it is standard practice in, e.g., adaptive metrology (see Refs.
[
48
,
49
,
50
,
51
]
for an illustrative sampler), and should therefore be feasible also in our context. Lastly, a state-independent measurement performed on multiple copies of the system is equivalent to a state-dependent measurement performed on a single system; such multi-copy schemes for work measurement were explored in Refs.
[
9
,
52
,
53
]
.

Expectedly, with this additional freedom,
\mathfrak{A}_{1}
,
\mathfrak{B}
, and
\mathfrak{A}_{2}
become more compatible. In fact, by allowing for completely arbitrary state-dependent schemes, we can resolve the inconsistency problem altogether. For example, by implementing the following protocol: when
[\rho,H]=\mathbb{0}
, apply the TPM scheme; when
[\rho,H]\neq\mathbb{0}
, apply the HOW scheme.
Formally, this satisfies
\mathfrak{A}_{1}
,
\mathfrak{B}
, and
\mathfrak{A}_{2}
simultaneously. However, this scheme is highly discontinuous. Indeed, if one adds an infinitesimal amount of coherence to a state that is diagonal in the energy eigenbasis, the statistics will experience a dramatic jump from having
AK
outcomes
E^{\prime}_{k}-E_{a}
to having only
d
outcomes
\Omega_{i}
, where
d
is the system’s Hilbert space dimension and
\Omega_{i}
are the eigenvalues of
\Omega
[see Eq. (
3
)]. Importantly,
\{\Omega_{i}\}
is not a subset of
\{E^{\prime}_{k}-E_{a}\}
whenever
[U^{\dagger}H^{\prime}U,H]\neq\mathbb{0}
. In addition to this discontinuity of the outcomes, a radical change of the probability distribution itself will also take place.

We deem such discontinuous situations pathological, especially because any measurement has a finite resolution (e.g., of how diagonal a state is). Moreover, we note that all the characteristics of the system and the process of our interest here depend on the initial state continuously. At the same time, it is natural to expect of the measurement that is able to faithfully track those characteristics to depend on them continuously. Thus, as the composition of continuous functions is also continuous, the said measurement shall depend on the initial state continuously.

We formalize this intuition by requiring the measurement schemes to depend on the state continuously, at least within a certain convex subset of all states. More specifically, let us consider a closed
\epsilon
-ball in the state space around the infinite-temperature (
\beta=0
) state
\tau_{0}=\mathbbm{1}/d
:
\mathcal{B}_{\epsilon}:=\{\sigma\;:\,\;\|\sigma-\tau_{0}\|<\epsilon\}
, where
\epsilon>0
and
\|\cdot\|
is the standard operator norm (in
\ell_{2}
space)
[
54
]
. Let us moreover choose
\epsilon>0
such that all
\rho
’s in
\mathcal{B}_{\epsilon}
are positive-definite—such an
\epsilon
always exists as all the eigenvalues of
\tau_{0}
are equal to
1/d>0
, and the eigenvalues of a Hermitian operator depend continuously on the operator
[
54
]
. Our main continuity assumption is that there exists such an
\epsilon_{0}>0
for which all the elements
M_{W}
of the POVM, and their corresponding outcomes
W
, are continuous functions of
\rho
in
\mathcal{B}_{\epsilon_{0}}
. If the scheme is continuous on the set of all density matrices, then of course the choice of
\epsilon_{0}
will be arbitrary.

In Appendix
C
, we prove that requiring the POVM elements and their outcomes to be differentiable with respect to
\rho
, which is a bit stronger than mere continuity, is so restrictive that the following theorem, which is the main result of this paper, holds.

Theorem 3

(No-go for state-dependent schemes)
.

No measurement scheme that is described by a POVM the elements and outcomes of which are at least once differentiable in
\rho
in some open ball
\mathcal{B}_{\epsilon_{0}}
centered at
\tau_{0}
, are at least twice differentiable with respect to
U
at
U=\mathbbm{1}
, and satisfy two mild structural constraints
1
and
2
described in Appendix
C
, can simultaneously satisfy
\mathfrak{A}_{1}
,
\mathfrak{A}_{2}
, and
\mathfrak{B}
.

The structural constraints
1
and
2
essentially boil down to requiring that the only source of complexness of the matrices
M_{W}
in the eigenbasis of
H
can be the noncommutativity of
U
and
\rho
with
H
. All work measurement schemes known to the authors satisfy these constraints.

While requiring continuity and differentiability can hardly be considered a limitation in physics, Constraints
1
and
2
, however mild they might seem (see Appendix
C
), do leave a narrow perspective open for the existence of a state-continuous scheme that could simultaneously satisfy
\mathfrak{A}_{1}
,
\mathfrak{A}_{2}
, and
\mathfrak{B}
. Of course, there might simply be a more general proof of incompatibility that would not require the Constraints
1
and
2
. We defer the explicit description of these constraints to Appendix
C
because there is a certain amount of notation that needs to be established for stating them.

Importantly, while not being able to provide full compatibility of
\mathfrak{A}_{1}
,
\mathfrak{A}_{2}
, and
\mathfrak{B}
, continuously state-dependent schemes do make those conditions more compatible. Indeed, with state-independent schemes, we could simultaneously satisfy
\mathfrak{A}_{1}
and
\mathfrak{A}_{2}
(the HOW scheme) as well as
\mathfrak{A}_{2}
and
\mathfrak{B}
(the TPM scheme), but not
\mathfrak{A}_{1}
and
\mathfrak{B}
. Now, with the additional freedom, in Appendix
D
, by combining the ordinary (“forward”) TPM scheme with what we call “reverse” TPM scheme, we construct a state-dependent measurement that simultaneously satisfies
\mathfrak{A}_{1}
and
\mathfrak{B}
(but not
\mathfrak{A}_{2}
). Thus, all the requirements are pairwise compatible when continuous state-dependent POVMs are allowed. While this paper was in preparation, three more state-dependent schemes simultaneously satisfying
\mathfrak{A}_{1}
and
\mathfrak{B}
(but, again, not
\mathfrak{A}_{2}
) were reported in Refs.
[
21
,
23
,
26
]
.

Complementing Theorem
3
, the following lemma shows that, strikingly, the two manifestations of the energy conservation,
\mathfrak{A}_{1}
and
\mathfrak{A}_{2}
, are incompatible in a wide range of practically relevant situations.

Lemma 2
.

If, for some state
\rho
with nonzero coherences (
[\rho,H]\neq\mathbb{0}
), the outcomes of a work measuring scheme do not depend on the evolution operator
U
, then the scheme cannot simultaneously satisfy conditions
\mathfrak{A}_{1}
and
\mathfrak{A}_{2}
, provided the POVM elements of the scheme are differentiable at least once with respect to
U
at
U=\mathbbm{1}
.

Lemma
2
follows as a corollary from Lemma
C.1
in Appendix
C
. It covers a large class of schemes that includes the TPM scheme, the multi-copy schemes in Refs.
[
9
,
52
,
53
]
, the scheme we introduced in Appendix
D
, and those later proposed in Refs.
[
21
,
23
]
. This conflict between the two manifestations of energy conservation,
\mathfrak{A}_{1}
and
\mathfrak{A}_{2}
, opens up a completely new perspective on the limited applicability of these schemes for states with coherences in the energy eigenbasis.

Notably, Lemma
2
does not cover the HOW scheme, as all its outcomes depend on
U
; and indeed, it satisfies both
\mathfrak{A}_{1}
and
\mathfrak{A}_{2}
by design, but, as discussed in Sec.
2
, it does not satisfy
\mathfrak{B}
. In fact, the HOW scheme is not covered also by the stronger Lemma
C.1
(see Appendix
C
).

In a sense, the strategy of our proof of Theorem
3
in Appendix
C
is inspired by this example, in that we start with requiring
\mathfrak{A}_{1}
and
\mathfrak{A}_{2}
, and then show that the structure enforced by them cannot be made compatible with
\mathfrak{B}
.

To give a non-trivial example to apply Theorem
3
to, let us, inspired by Ref.
[
13
]
, introduce the state-dependent operator of work

\displaystyle\Upsilon_{\rho}=-\hat{\beta}(\rho)^{-1}\ln\!\big[e^{\hat{\beta}(\rho)H/2}e^{-\hat{\beta}(\rho)U^{\dagger}H^{\prime}U}e^{\hat{\beta}(\rho)H/2}\big],~

(10)

where
\hat{\beta}(\rho):=\arg\min\limits_{\beta}\|\rho-\tau_{\beta}\|
. This defines an “operator scheme”—the POVM elements are the eigenprojectors of the operator, and the outcomes are the corresponding eigenvalues. This scheme is obviously designed to satisfy
\mathfrak{B}
and, when
[U^{\dagger}H^{\prime}U,H]=\mathbb{0}
,
\Upsilon_{\rho}
coincides with the Heisenberg operator of work
\Omega
[defined in Eq. (
3
)], which also means that
\Upsilon_{\rho}
has a proper classical limit. One also immediately notes that this scheme satisfies
\mathfrak{A}_{2}
. In Appendix
E
, we show that this scheme is indeed covered by Theorem
3
, and hence, it cannot satisfy
\mathfrak{A}_{1}
because it already satisfies
\mathfrak{A}_{2}
and
\mathfrak{B}
. And indeed, in Appendix
E
, we explicitly show that
\Upsilon_{\rho}
violates
\mathfrak{A}_{1}
even for thermal states. In addition to that, to mark its debut, we prove some other curious facts about
\Upsilon_{\rho}
, unrelated to the main point of this paper.

4
Discussion

In this paper, we unearthed the fundamental reason why, despite numerous attempts in the literature, no satisfactory measurement scheme for quantum work has been found so far. By taking an abstract approach, we asked whether any quantum measurement, defined as broadly as possible, can produce statistics that are consistent with energy conservation for the unmeasured system and Jarzynski equality.

These conditions ensure that the measurement results reflect the reality of the unmeasured system. Say, nothing happens to the system—there are no systems with which it could exchange energy. Even without measuring the system, energy conservation law already tells us that, with probability
1
, exactly
0
work is performed on the system. Therefore, any meaningful measurement of work, when applied to the system, should output
0
, with probability
1
, even if the measurement process disrupts the system. This was our condition
\mathfrak{A}_{2}
.

By the same logic, when we take a system in a known state and drive it unitarily according to a protocol we fully control, then, even without measuring the system, energy conservation tells us that the average work performed on the system is equal to the difference between the final and initial average energies of the system. Thus, we imposed the condition
\mathfrak{A}_{1}
that a proper measurement of work should output a random variable the first moment of which coincides with that average, no matter how disruptive the measurement may be.

Note that
\mathfrak{A}_{1}
and
\mathfrak{A}_{2}
are not conditions about the measurement process itself. Namely, we do not ask whether energy is conserved during the joint evolution of the system, apparatus, and external driving agents.

The main result of this paper, Theorem
3
, is that no reasonable quantum measurement can output such a random variable that would respect the above two manifestations of energy conservation and, at the same time, satisfy the Jarzynski equality whenever the system is in a thermal state (condition
\mathfrak{B}
). This uncovers a deep connection between energy conservation law and fluctuation theorems that is present
only
in the quantum regime.

Our proof merely assumes (i) differentiability of the measurement operators and outcomes with respect to the state and (ii) that, in the eigenbasis of
H
, complexness in
M_{W}
can originate only from noncommutativity of
U
and
\rho
with
H
. While (i) can hardly constitute a loophole in any conceivable physical situation, breaking (ii) could in principle provide a leeway for devising a meaningful work measurement scheme. The possibility of finding a more general proof that would not require (ii) is not excluded and is an interesting problem for future research.

Exploring the extent to which state-dependent schemes can be helpful in the related problems of quantum back-action evasion
[
52
,
53
]
, measuring fluctuations of heat
[
55
,
56
]
, current
[
57
,
58
,
59
]
, entropy production
[
60
,
61
,
62
]
, and energetic cost of measurements
[
63
,
35
,
60
,
64
,
65
,
66
]
, is an interesting research avenue.

To prove Lemma

\displaystyle\sum_{W}\tr(M_{W}e^{-\beta H})e^{-\beta W}=\tr e^{-\beta H^{\prime}}\quad\forall\beta,

(A1)

where
\{M_{W}\}_{W}
is a POVM that does not depend on the state of the system, but may depend on the process (i.e.,

,

, and

Recalling the eigenresolutions

and

.

Keeping in mind that everything above is invariant under a global constant energy shift, we choose the ground state of

to have zero energy. Moreover, since the eigenresolution

already accounts for possible degeneracies, all values of

are distinct. Therefore, the order of the eigenvectors of

can be chosen such that

, which cannot be. Likewise, there cannot exist such

and

|E_{b}+W^{\prime}|\leq|E_{a}+W|
for all other pairs

. And since the equality between the left- and right-hand sides of Eq. (

\displaystyle\sum_{|E_{a}+W|=E^{\prime}_{K}}\tr(M_{W}P_{a})=g^{\prime}_{K}.

(A5)

Moreover, if there exist such

that

, then necessarily

. Indeed, taking

. In other words, for all

,

is equal to the sum of all the terms on the left-hand side with maximal

. Thus, for any

, we can eliminate those terms from both sides of Eq. (
A4
) and reiterate the arguments above. Doing so

This procedure also shows that, if

does not coincide with any of

[check Eq. (

Now, let us prove Theorem

. For a state-independent scheme to satisfy

On the other hand, Lemma

, and more specifically, Eq. (

among which the matrices

are allowed to have coherences. So much so that, for any nontrivial

, there exists a

such that

has coherences where

cannot.

For brevity of presentation, let us conduct the proof of this fact for the case of nondegenerate set of work outcomes, namely, when

\displaystyle E^{\prime}_{k}-E_{a}=E^{\prime}_{m}-E_{b}\;\;\Longrightarrow\;\;k=m\;\;\mathrm{and}\;\;a=b.~~~

(A6)

Note that this case is the generic one—those

Now, for this configuration, Eq. (

APPENDIX B
Proof of Theorem
2

To prove Theorem
2
, let us, taking into account Eqs. (
7
) and (
A7
), rewrite Eq. (
2
) with
\rho=\tau_{\beta}
as

\displaystyle\sum_{a}e^{-\beta E_{a}}\big[\sum_{k}E^{\prime}_{k}

\displaystyle\tr(P_{a}U^{\dagger}P^{\prime}_{k}U)-g_{a}E_{a}\big]

\displaystyle=\sum_{a,k}e^{-\beta E_{a}}(E^{\prime}_{k}-E_{a})\tr M_{ak},

which has to hold for any
\beta
. Keeping in mind that no two
E_{a}
’s are the same, and setting
\beta=0,1,\dots,A-1
, we will arrive at a linear system of equations with the coefficient matrix being a Vandermonde matrix
[
54
]
with a non-zero determinant. Hence, for any

,

\displaystyle\sum_{k}(E^{\prime}_{k}-E_{a})\tr(P_{a}U^{\dagger}P^{\prime}_{k}U)=\sum_{k}(E^{\prime}_{k}-E_{a})\tr M_{ak},

(B9)

where we have noted that
\sum_{k}\tr(P_{a}U^{\dagger}P^{\prime}_{k}U)=g_{a}
.

At this point, we assume that the operators
M_{ak}
do not depend on
E^{\prime}_{k}
’s within a small neighborhood. This assumption is natural for two reasons. First, the unitary evolution operator,
U=\overrightarrow{\exp}\big[-i\int_{t_{\mathrm{in}}}^{t_{\mathrm{fin}}}dtH(t)\big]
does not change when values of
H(s)
are changed on a measure-zero set, and since changing the eigenvalues of
H^{\prime}=H(t_{\mathrm{fin}})
is such an operation,
U
remains intact. Second, since
0=E^{\prime}_{1}<E^{\prime}_{2}<\cdots<E^{\prime}_{K}
and
K
is finite, there exists an
\epsilon>0
such that the
\epsilon
-neighborhoods of
E^{\prime}_{k}
’s are non-overlapping; we will assume
E^{\prime}_{k}
-independence in these neighborhoods. Now, before the last-moment change in the eigenvalues of
H^{\prime}
, the process runs without “knowing” about the change, so if
M_{ak}
’s were going to measure probabilities of work outcomes for
E^{\prime}_{k}
’s, then a small variation that preserves the order of
E^{\prime}_{k}
’s should not impact the measurement probabilities and hence the operators.

With this assumption, we can differentiate Eq. (
B9
) with respect to
E^{\prime}_{k}
’s, with all other parameters fixed, to find that

\displaystyle\tr M_{ak}=\tr(P_{a}U^{\dagger}P^{\prime}_{k}U)

(B10)

for all
a
’s and
k
’s.

When the initial Hamiltonian has no degeneracies, i.e.,
P_{a}=|a\rangle\langle a|
for all
a
’s, then Eq. (
A7
) implies that
M_{ak}\propto|a\rangle\langle a|
, and Eq. (
B10
) shows that

\displaystyle M_{ak}=|a\rangle\langle a|U^{\dagger}P^{\prime}_{k}U|a\rangle\langle a|=M_{ak}^{\mathrm{TPM}},

(B11)

where the second equality is due to Eq. (
6
). Hence, for nondegenerate
H
’s, the JE class consists of the TPM scheme
only
. For degenerate
H
’s, the statistics produced by the schemes in the JE class are guaranteed to coincide with those of the TPM scheme
only
on diagonal initial states of the form
\rho=\sum_{a}\rho_{a}P_{a}
[which can be easily checked by using Eqs. (
A7
), (
B10
), and (
5
)]. Whenever
\rho
is not proportional to the identity operator in one of the eigensubspaces of
H
, there will exist a POVM that is in the JE class, satisfies Eq. (
2
) for thermal states, but is nevertheless such that
\tr(\rho M_{ak})\neq p_{ak}^{\mathrm{TPM}}
.

We emphasize that the
H
’s and
H^{\prime}
’s for which the JE class is larger than the TPM scheme comprise a
0
-measure subset of the set of all possible
H
’s and
H^{\prime}
’s. Which in particular means that, for any
H
and
H^{\prime}
yielding a degenerate set of energy difference, there exist infinitesimal perturbations of
H
and
H^{\prime}
that remove the degeneracy. Therefore, if we ask which schemes satisfy the JE and average work condition for thermal initial states for all processes at least in an arbitrarily small neighborhood of a given process, then the TPM scheme will be the only such scheme. In this sense, the JE class is equivalent to the TPM scheme.

APPENDIX C
Proof of Theorem
3

To prove that certain requirements are incompatible, it is sufficient to prove that there exists at least one state and one process for which those requirements entail contradicting results.

We will look for such a state within the very same ball
\mathcal{B}_{\epsilon_{0}}
in which the scheme is continuous. The class of processes among which we will look for the desired process will be cyclic Hamiltonian processes (
H^{\prime}=H
), the evolution operator generated by which has the form
U=e^{-ixh}
, where
x
is a real number and
h
is a Hermitian operator. We will call this class of processes
\mathbf{\Pi}
:

\displaystyle\mathbf{\Pi}=\big\{(H,H^{\prime},U)\;:\,\;H^{\prime}=H,\;U=e^{-ixh}\big\},

(C12)

where
h
spans all finite-norm Hermitian operators living in the system’s Hilbert space. This class of processes will allow us to conveniently include
\mathfrak{A}_{2}
in our analysis, because, for any process in
\mathbf{\Pi}
, we can make the system untouched by simply taking the
x\to 0
limit. Conveniently, the system becomes untouched also in the limit
h\to H
.

Let us now explore the consequences of imposing
\mathfrak{A}_{2}
. To do so, for each
\rho
and
h
, we define

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}_{\rho,h}=\big\{W\;:\,\;\lim_{x\to 0}W=0\big\}

(C13)

to be the set of outcomes
W
that are either
0
or tend to
0
as
x\to 0
. The rest of the outcomes will tend to non-zero values in the same limit; we denote the set of those outcomes by
\mathcal{N}_{\rho,h}
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}_{\rho,h}=\big\{W\;:\,\;\lim_{x\to 0}W\neq 0\big\}.

(C14)

Then, the requirement
\mathfrak{A}_{2}
means that, in the
x\to 0
limit,

\displaystyle\sum_{W\in\mathcal{N}_{\rho,h}}p_{W}\to 0\quad\mathrm{and}\quad\sum_{W\in\mathcal{O}_{\rho,h}}p_{W}\to 1,

(C15)

where
p_{W}=\tr(\rho M_{W})
, as in Eq. (
1
). For positive-definite states (
\rho>\mathbb{0}
), since all their eigenvalues are strictly positive, Eq. (
C15
) entails

\displaystyle\sum_{W\in\mathcal{N}_{\rho,h}}M_{W}\to\mathbb{0}\quad\mathrm{and}\quad\sum_{W\in\mathcal{O}_{\rho,h}}M_{W}\to\mathbbm{1}.

(C16)

We emphasize that, in general, like
M_{W}
’s, the outcomes
W
depend on
H
,
h
,
x
, and
\rho
.

If
M_{W}
is differentiable at least once with respect to
x
at
x=0
, then
p_{W}=\tr(M_{W}\rho)
will also be differentiable in
x
at
x=0
. Hence, we can write the Taylor series with the remainder in the Peano form:

\displaystyle p_{W}(x)=p_{W}(0)+xp^{\prime}_{W}(0)+o(x),

(C17)

where the small-o is as per the standard asymptotic notation.

Now, if
p_{W}\to 0
as
x\to 0
, then
p_{W}(0)=0
. Moreover,
p^{\prime}_{W}(0)
will also have to be zero, because otherwise, for sufficiently small
x
’s,
p_{W}
would change its sign when
x\to-x
, whereas
p_{W}
must always remain non-negative. Therefore, while maintaining nonnegativity,

\displaystyle\lim_{x\to 0}p_{W}=0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad p_{W}=o(x)

(C18)

With exactly the same reasoning,

\displaystyle\lim_{x\to 0}M_{W}=\mathbb{0}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad M_{W}=o(x).

(C19)

As discussed above,
\lim_{x\to 0}p_{W}=0
necessitates
\lim_{x\to 0}M_{W}=\mathbb{0}
whenever
\rho
is positive-definite. Note that
o(x)
is allowed to be anything from
x^{2}\tilde{m}_{W}
, with some
\tilde{m}_{W}\geq\mathbb{0}
, like in the TPM scheme, to something exotic, say,
|x|^{3/2}\tilde{m}_{W}
.

Let us now see what
\mathfrak{A}_{1}
entails for processes in
\mathbf{\Pi}
. Recalling Eqs. (
2
) and (
3
), namely that
\langle W\rangle=\tr(\Omega\rho)
, we can apply the Baker–Hausdorff lemma
[
68
]
to expand
\Omega
as

\displaystyle\Omega=x\omega+i\frac{x^{2}}{2}[h,\omega]+O(x^{3}),

(C20)

for
x\ll 1
, where we have introduced the operator

\displaystyle\omega:=i[h,H],

(C21)

which leads us to

\displaystyle\langle W\rangle=x\tr(\rho\,\omega)+i\frac{x^{2}}{2}\tr(\rho[h,\omega])+O(x^{3}).

(C22)

At this point, we are ready to prove the following lemma that establishes incompatibility between
\mathfrak{A}_{1}
and
\mathfrak{A}_{2}
—the two aspects of energy conservation—in special, but practically relevant, circumstances.

Lemma C.1
.

If, for a state-dependent scheme
\{(M_{W},W)\}_{W}
, there exist a state
\rho
and a process in
\mathbf{\Pi}
, such that
\tr(\rho[h,H])\neq 0
, for which the outcomes
W
either do not go to zero as
x\to 0
(but also do not diverge), or go to zero as
o(x)
, then the scheme cannot simultaneously satisfy
\mathfrak{A}_{1}
and
\mathfrak{A}_{2}
.

Note that, since
\tr(\rho[h,H])=\tr(h[H,\rho])
, the state must have nonzero coherences in the energy eigenbasis; namely,
[\rho,H]\neq\mathbb{0}
. Obviously, for any such
\rho
, there always exist an infinite amount of
h
’s for which the condition
\tr(\rho[h,H])\neq 0
in Lemma
C.1
is satisfied. Also note that the case of
W
simply being equal to zero is included in the
o(x)
notation.

It is worth emphasizing that, for Lemma
C.1
to hold, the scheme does not have to be continuous in
\rho
.

Proof of Lemma
C.1
.

As we see from Eq. (
C22
), once we enforce
\mathfrak{A}_{1}
, the condition
\tr(\rho[h,H])\neq 0
of the lemma necessitates that

\displaystyle\langle W\rangle\propto x.

(C23)

On the other hand, from Eqs. (
C15
) and (
C18
) we see that, enforcing
\mathfrak{A}_{2}
necessitates that, if
\lim_{x\to 0}W\neq 0
, then
p_{W}=o(x)
, which means that
Wp_{W}=o(x)
. By the condition of the lemma, if
\lim_{x\to 0}W=0
, then
W=o(x)
, which means that
Wp_{W}=o(x)
also in this case. Hence,
\langle W\rangle=\sum_{W}Wp_{W}=o(x)
, which is in clear contradiction with Eq. (
C23
). This thus proves that, under the assumptions of the lemma,
\mathfrak{A}_{1}
and
\mathfrak{A}_{2}
are incompatible.
∎

Note that the HOW scheme provides an important example of a scheme that is
not
covered by Lemma
C.1
. Indeed, from Eq. (
C20
) we observe that all the outcomes of the HOW scheme go to zero
\propto x
, which is in straightforward violation of the conditions of Lemma
C.1
.

We immediately see that Lemma
2
in the main text follows from Lemma
C.1
as a simple corollary. Indeed, as noted above, for any
\rho
such that
[\rho,H]\neq\mathbb{0}
, there always exists a

such that
\tr(\rho[h,H])\neq 0
. And if the outcomes do not depend on

, it means that they are either zero, which falls under the category of

, or they are not zero. Hence, all the conditions of Lemma
C.1
are met.

Proof of Theorem

.
Our proof strategy here will be to impose
\mathfrak{A}_{1}
and
\mathfrak{A}_{2}
and show that they lead to results that are not compatible with

By the assumption of the theorem, all

’s are differentiable at least twice with respect to

. Therefore, the argumentation between Eqs. (

Let us now add to the set

\displaystyle W\in\mathbf{N}_{\rho,h}\Rightarrow\bigg\{\!\begin{array}[]{l}W=U_{W}+O(x),\\

may depend on

, and

For the complementary set

\displaystyle\mathbf{O}_{\rho,h}=\big\{W\;:\,\;\lim_{x\to 0}M_{W}>\mathbb{0}\big\}\;\subseteq\mathcal{O}_{\rho,h},

\displaystyle W\in\mathbf{O}_{\rho,h}\Rightarrow\bigg\{\!\begin{array}[]{l}W=xV_{W}+x^{2}v_{W}+o(x^{2}),\\

where, by definition,

,

,

,

) may depend on

,

, and

; see the discussion around Eqs. (

APPENDIX D
A scheme simultaneously satisfying
\mathfrak{A}_{1}
and
\mathfrak{B}

Let us construct a scheme that satisfies
\mathfrak{A}_{1}
and
\mathfrak{B}
and depends continuously on the system’s state. The example illustrates that
\mathfrak{A}_{2}
is an independent and essential requirement for any meaningful measurement of work.

As a first step, we devise a measurement scheme in the specific class of processes in which the system starts out in a possibly coherent (in the eigenbasis of
H
) state and unitarily evolves into a diagonal state:
(H,\rho)\longrightarrow(H^{\prime},\rho_{D})
, where
\rho_{D}=V\rho V^{\dagger}
and
[\rho_{D},H^{\prime}]=\mathbb{0}
. Here, we notice that the time-reversed process,
(H^{\prime},\rho_{D})\longrightarrow(H,\rho)
, with
\rho=V^{\dagger}\rho_{D}V
, is describable by the TPM scheme.

Now, if the work statistics of a unitary process is
\{(W_{\alpha},p_{\alpha})\}_{\alpha}
, then, to the time-reversed process, it is reasonable to prescribe the work statistics
\{(-W_{\alpha},p_{\alpha})\}_{\alpha}
.

With this prescription, thinking of the
\mathcal{P}=\{(H,\rho)\overset{V}{\longrightarrow}(H^{\prime},\rho_{D})\}
process as the reverse of
\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}=\{(H,\rho_{D})\overset{V^{\dagger}}{\longrightarrow}(H^{\prime},\rho)\}
, and describing the latter by the TPM scheme [see Eqs. (
5
) and (
6
)], for the work statistics of
\mathcal{P}
we obtain

\displaystyle W_{ak}

\displaystyle=-(E_{a}-E^{\prime}_{k}),

\displaystyle p_{ak}

\displaystyle=\tr\left(P^{\prime}_{k}\rho_{D}\right)\tr\left(P_{a}\frac{V^{\dagger}P^{\prime}_{k}\rho_{D}P^{\prime}_{k}V}{\tr\left(P^{\prime}_{k}\rho_{D}\right)}\right)

\displaystyle=\tr\left(P_{a}V^{\dagger}P^{\prime}_{k}\rho_{D}P^{\prime}_{k}V\right).

Put in other words:

\displaystyle W_{ak}=E^{\prime}_{k}-E_{a},\qquad p_{ak}=\tr(M_{ak}\rho),

(D59)

with

\displaystyle M_{ak}=V^{\dagger}P^{\prime}_{k}VP_{a}V^{\dagger}P^{\prime}_{k}V.

(D60)

Note that
M_{ak}
depends on the initial state through
V
. We call this measurement “reverse” TPM scheme as opposed to the standard TPM scheme that directly measures the “forward” process. Interestingly, the work distribution produced by the reverse TPM scheme coincides with that of the scheme proposed in Ref.
[
7
]
. Extraction of ergotropy
[
46
]
is an important example of a process where the system goes from a possibly coherent state to a diagonal one (the passive state
[
71
,
72
]
), and the reverse TPM scheme is thus a natural choice for measuring ergotropy fluctuations.

Having at our disposal this scheme, we can now describe any coherent-to-coherent process by decomposing it into coherent-to-incoherent and incoherent-to-coherent processes. More specifically, consider an arbitrary Hamiltonian process
(H,\rho)\longrightarrow(H^{\prime},\rho^{\prime})
, with
\rho^{\prime}=U\rho U^{\dagger}
, for which both
[\rho,H]\neq\mathbb{0}
and
[\rho^{\prime},H^{\prime}]\neq\mathbb{0}
, and decompose it into

\displaystyle(H,\rho)\overset{R}{\xrightarrow{\hskip 17.07164pt}}(H,\widetilde{\rho})\overset{UR^{\dagger}}{\xrightarrow{\hskip 17.07164pt}}(H^{\prime},\rho^{\prime}),~

(D61)

where
\widetilde{\rho}=R\rho R^{\dagger}
is diagonal in
H
. Moreover,
R
is chosen such that the diagonal of
\widetilde{\rho}
is ordered in the same way as the diagonal of
\rho
. More specifically, say,
\hat{\pi}
is the permutation matrix that reorders
\rho_{D}^{\downarrow}
into
\rho_{D}
:
\rho_{D}=\hat{\pi}\rho_{D}^{\downarrow}\hat{\pi}^{\mathrm{T}}
, where
\rho_{D}
is a diagonal matrix whose diagonal coincides with that of
\rho
in the eigenbasis of
H
. Then, if
r^{\downarrow}
is the vector composed of the eigenvalues of
\rho
, organized in the decreasing order, then
\widetilde{\rho}=\hat{\pi}r^{\downarrow}\hat{\pi}^{\mathrm{T}}
. Thus,
R
is the unitary operator that rotates
\rho
into
\widetilde{\rho}
. The operator
R
is chosen in this way to guarantee that, when
\rho
is diagonal, then
R=\mathbbm{1}
.

Now, using Eqs. (
D59
) and (
D60
), for the first part of the process (
D61
), we obtain

\displaystyle W^{\textup{I}}_{ab}=E_{b}-E_{a},\qquad p^{\textup{I}}_{ab}=\tr(M^{\textup{I}}_{ab}\rho),

(D62)

where the POVM is given by

\displaystyle M^{\textup{I}}_{ab}=R^{\dagger}P_{b}RP_{a}R^{\dagger}P_{b}R.

(D63)

Following the standard TPM scheme, for the second part of the process, we find

\displaystyle W^{\textup{II}}_{ck}=E^{\prime}_{k}-E_{c},\qquad p^{\textup{II}}_{ck}=\tr(M^{\textup{II}}_{ck}\rho),

(D64)

where

\displaystyle M^{\textup{II}}_{ck}=R^{\dagger}P_{c}RU^{\dagger}P_{k}^{\prime}UR^{\dagger}P_{c}R.

(D65)

Finally, for the overall process (
D61
), let us write

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} W_{abck}&=W^{\textup{I}}_{ab}+W^{\textup{II}}_{ck},\\
p_{abck}&=p^{\textup{I}}_{ab}p^{\textup{II}}_{ck}=\tr\left(M^{\textup{I}}_{ab}\otimes M^{\textup{II}}_{ck}\cdot\rho\otimes\rho\right),\end{aligned}

(D66)

where
M_{abck}=M^{\textup{I}}_{ab}\otimes M^{\textup{II}}_{ck}
is a POVM that depends on the initial state through
R
.

From the perspective of a single copy of the system, this scheme is equivalent to the following state-dependent POVM. With the same outcomes
W_{abck}
,
p_{abck}
can be written as

\displaystyle p_{abck}=\tr\big[\widetilde{M}(\rho)_{abck}\,\rho\big],

(D67)

where

\displaystyle\widetilde{M}(\rho)_{abck}=p^{\textup{II}}_{ck}M^{\textup{I}}_{ab}.

(D68)

Obviously, the operators
\widetilde{M}(\rho)_{abck}
constitute a POVM.

By construction, this definition satisfies
\mathfrak{A}_{1}
and, keeping in mind that
R=\mathbbm{1}
for initially diagonal states, it coincides with the TPM scheme, and therefore satisfies
\mathfrak{B}
. However, for
[\rho,H]\neq\mathbb{0}
, this definition does not satisfy
\mathfrak{A}_{2}
. Indeed, in order to measure work for some
[\rho,H]\neq\mathbb{0}
in the trivial case of
H^{\prime}=H
and
U=\mathbbm{1}
, the scheme will first apply a unitary to diagonalize
\rho
with
H
, thereby producing some nontrivial statistics according to Eqs. (
D62
) and (
D63
); then, it will rotate that state back to
\rho
, again producing nontrivial statistics as per Eqs. (
D64
) and (
D65
). In total, for the trivial process, the scheme will produce rather complicated work statistics described by Eq. (
D66
).

APPENDIX E
The other operator of work

The operator
\Upsilon_{\rho}
is similar to the operator for work proposed in Ref.
[
13
]
. There, the work operator was introduced according to
e^{-\beta\hat{W}}=e^{-\beta U^{\dagger}H^{\prime}U}e^{\beta H}
. However, the work operator defined in this way will not be Hermitian whenever
[U^{\dagger}H^{\prime}U,H]\neq\mathbb{0}
. Our operator
\Upsilon_{\rho}
is Hermitian by construction and is defined for all states.

First of all, let us show that this scheme is covered by Theorem
3
. It is self-evident that
\hat{\beta}(\rho)
, and therefore
\Upsilon_{\rho}
, are differentiable functions of
\rho
, which means that both the measurement operators and outcomes of this scheme are differentiable with respect to
\rho
. Moreover,
\Upsilon_{\rho}
satisfies Constraint
1
, because, for processes (
C12
),
U^{\dagger}HU=H+x\omega+O(x^{2})
, and therefore, except for
\omega
, all the operators in the expansion of the relevant quantities with respect to
x
will be operator functions of
H
. Consequently, they will all be real in the eigenbasis of
H
, for all values of
\rho
, which means that the dependence of complexness on
\rho
is trivial. As regards Constraint
2
, it is satisfied by construction:
\Upsilon_{\rho}
is an operator scheme, which means that all
M_{W}
, and thus all
m_{W}
are eigenprojectors of a Hermitian operator, and are thus mutually orthogonal, which in turn means that
Y^{+}Y^{-}=\mathbb{0}
[see also the discussion under Eq. (
C37
)].

Since
\Upsilon_{\rho}
satisfies
\mathfrak{A}_{2}
and
\mathfrak{B}
by construction, the fact that Theorem
3
applies to this scheme means that it necessarily has to violate
\mathfrak{A}_{1}
.

To explicitly show that this is indeed the case, let us consider the processes
\Pi
, defined in Eq. (
C12
), and thermal initial states
\tau\propto e^{-\beta H}
. First, we rewrite Eq. (
10
) as

\displaystyle\Upsilon_{\tau}=-\beta^{-1}\ln\big(\tau^{-1/2}U^{\dagger}\tau U\tau^{-1/2}\big).

(E69)

Through a simple calculation employing the Baker–Hausdorff lemma and the Taylor expansion of
\ln(1+x)
around
x=0
, Eq. (
E69
) will lead us to

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} -\beta\Upsilon_{\tau}=&\,ix\tau^{-1/2}[h,\tau]\tau^{-1/2}\\
&-\frac{x^{2}}{2}\tau^{-1/2}[h,[h,\tau]]\tau^{-1/2}\\
&+\frac{x^{2}}{2}\tau^{-1/2}[h,\tau]\tau^{-1}[h,\tau]\tau^{-1/2}+O(x^{3}).\end{aligned}

Therefore,

\displaystyle\beta\langle\Upsilon_{\tau}\rangle_{\tau}=-\frac{x^{2}}{2}\tr(\tau^{-1}[h,\tau]^{2})+O(x^{3}).

(E70)

Whereas, reading from Eq. (
C22
), we have

\displaystyle\beta\langle\Omega\rangle_{\tau}=\frac{x^{2}}{2}\tr([h,\tau]\,[h,\beta H])+O(x^{3}).

(E71)

Neglecting the
O(x^{3})
terms and working in the eigenbasis of
H
, we can rewrite Eqs. (
E70
) and (
E71
) as

\displaystyle\beta\langle\Upsilon_{\tau}\rangle_{\tau}

\displaystyle=x^{2}\sum_{k<j}|h_{kj}|^{2}(p_{k}-p_{j})^{2}\frac{p_{k}+p_{j}}{2p_{k}p_{j}}

(E72)

\displaystyle=x^{2}\sum_{k<j}|h_{kj}|^{2}p_{j}(e^{\Delta_{kj}}-1)\sinh\Delta_{kj}

(E73)

and

\displaystyle\beta\langle\Omega\rangle_{\tau}

\displaystyle=x^{2}\sum_{k<j}|h_{kj}|^{2}(p_{k}-p_{j})\Delta_{kj}

(E74)

\displaystyle=x^{2}\sum_{k<j}|h_{kj}|^{2}p_{j}(e^{\Delta_{kj}}-1)\Delta_{kj},

(E75)

where
p_{k}=e^{-\beta E_{k}}/Z
are the eigenvalues of
\tau
, and
\Delta_{kj}=\beta(E_{j}-E_{k})
; when transitioning from (
E72
) to (
E73
) and from (
E74
) to (
E75
), we used that
p_{k}=p_{j}e^{\Delta_{kj}}
.

Now, whenever
[h,H]\neq\mathbb{0}
, there exist such
k_{0}
and
j_{0}
for which
\Delta_{k_{0}j_{0}}>0
and
h_{k_{0}j_{0}}\neq 0
. Since
\Delta_{kj}\geq 0
for all
k<j
, Eqs. (
E73
) and (
E75
) thus imply that

\displaystyle\langle\Upsilon_{\tau}\rangle_{\tau}>\langle\Omega\rangle_{\tau},

(E76)

provided
x
is small enough for the
O(x^{3})
terms to be irrelevant. This inequality simply means that
\Upsilon_{\tau}
violates
\mathfrak{A}_{1}
for all thermal states
\tau
as long as
[h,H]\neq\mathbb{0}
.

E.1
Two curious properties of
\Upsilon_{\rho}

Below, we will prove two results about
\Upsilon_{\rho}
that are not related to the main goal of this appendix. However, we feel compelled to present them to more completely characterize the yet another “definition of work” that
\Upsilon_{\rho}
represents.

Proposition E.1
.

\displaystyle\langle\Omega\rangle_{\tau}-\langle\Upsilon_{\tau}\rangle_{\tau}\leq\beta^{-1}S(\tau\,\|\,U^{\dagger}\tau^{\prime}U);

(E77)

Proposition E.2
.

\displaystyle\mathrm{Spec}(\Upsilon_{\tau})^{\downarrow}\succ\mathrm{Spec}(\Omega)^{\downarrow},

(E78)

for any
\beta
.

Here,
\tau^{\prime}=e^{-\beta H^{\prime}}/\tr e^{-\beta H^{\prime}}
and
\mathrm{Spec}(O)
is the spectrum of the operator
O
. The superscript
\downarrow
indicates that the set is ordered from large to small, and
\succ
is the majorization relation
[
54
]
.

Proof of Proposition
E.1
.

Let us first observe that, as a trivial consequence of the operator-concavity of the logarithm,

\displaystyle\tr(\rho\ln O)\leq\ln\tr(\rho O),

(E79)

for any density operator
\rho
and Hermitian operator
O\geq\mathbb{0}
. Furthermore, noticing that
e^{\beta H/2}e^{-\beta U^{\dagger}H^{\prime}U}e^{\beta H/2}>\mathbb{0}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\langle\Upsilon_{\tau}\rangle_{\tau}=-\beta^{-1}\tr\Big(\tau\ln e^{-\beta\small{\Upsilon_{\tau}}}\Big)\geq\Delta F,

(E80)

where we took into account that, by construction,

\displaystyle\big\langle e^{-\beta\small{\Upsilon_{\tau}}}\big\rangle_{\tau}=e^{-\beta\Delta F}=\frac{Z_{\beta}[H^{\prime}]}{Z_{\beta}[H]}.

(E81)

Interestingly, inequality (
E80
) provides another aspect by which
\Upsilon_{\tau}
resembles work.

Finally, observing that

\displaystyle\Delta F=\langle\Omega\rangle_{\tau}-\beta^{-1}S(\tau\,\|\,U^{\dagger}\tau^{\prime}U),

(E82)

we justify Eq. (
E77
).
∎

Proof of Proposition
E.2
.

For this proof, we invoke the following two theorems, proven in Refs.
[
73
,
74
]
, that hold for arbitrary Hermitian operators
A
and
B
.

Theorem E.1

(The Theorem of Ref.
[
73
]
)
.

An arbitrary neighborhood of
e^{A+B}
contains an
X
for which there exist two unitary operators
\mathcal{U}
and
\mathcal{V}
such that

\displaystyle\mathrm{Spec}(X)=\mathrm{Spec}\big(Y^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathcal{U}Y^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathcal{V}\big),

(E83)

where

\displaystyle Y=e^{A/2}e^{B}e^{A/2}.

(E84)

Theorem E.2

(Theorem 1 of Ref.
[
74
]
)
.

If
X_{1}\geq X_{2}\geq\cdots\geq X_{d}\geq 0
and
Y_{1}\geq Y_{2}\geq\cdots\geq Y_{d}\geq 0
are the eigenvalues of positive-semidefinite Hermitian matrices
X
and
Y
, then unitary operators
\mathcal{U}
and
\mathcal{V}
exist such that
\mathrm{Spec}(X)=\mathrm{Spec}\big(Y^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathcal{U}Y^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathcal{V}\big)
if and only if

\displaystyle X_{1}X_{2}\cdots X_{k}\leq Y_{1}Y_{2}\cdots Y_{k},

(E85)

for
k=1,2,...,d-1
, and

\displaystyle\det X=\det Y.

(E86)

In our situation,
A=\beta H
and
B=-\beta U^{\dagger}H^{\prime}U
, and thus
e^{A+B}=e^{-\beta\Omega}
and
Y=e^{-\beta\Upsilon_{\tau}}
. Therefore, Theorems
E.1
and
E.2
imply that, given some matrix norm
\|\cdot\|
(e.g., the operator norm), for any
\epsilon>0
, there exists an
\Omega^{(\epsilon)}
such that
\|\Omega-\Omega^{(\epsilon)}\|<\epsilon
and, according to Eq. (
E85
),

\displaystyle\Omega^{(\epsilon)}_{d}+\cdots+\Omega^{(\epsilon)}_{d-k+1}\geq\Gamma_{d}+\cdots+\Gamma_{d-k+1},

(E87)

for
k=1,2,...,d-1
, where
\Omega^{(\epsilon)}_{1}\geq\cdots\geq\Omega^{(\epsilon)}_{d}
are the ordered eigenvalues of
\Omega^{(\epsilon)}
and
\Gamma_{1}\geq\cdots\geq\Gamma_{d}
are the ordered eigenvalues of
\Upsilon_{\tau}
. Eq. (
E86
), in turn, entails

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{d}\Omega^{(\epsilon)}_{k}=\tr\Omega^{(\epsilon)}=\tr\Upsilon_{\tau}=\sum_{k=1}^{d}\Gamma_{k}.

(E88)

Taking the limit
\epsilon\to 0
in Eqs. (
E87
) and (
E88
), it is straightforward to see that
\sum_{m=1}^{k}\Omega_{m}\leq\sum_{m=1}^{k}\Gamma_{m}
, for
k=1,...,d-1
and
\tr\Omega=\tr\Upsilon_{\tau}
, which concludes the proof of Proposition
E.2
.
∎

Supplementary Note

S
Classical limit of the operator of work

Here, we will explore the question of whether the operator of work starts obeying the Jarzynski equality in the classical limit of a quantum continuous-variable system. We will study the simplest such example—the driven harmonic oscillator.

From the Baker–Cambell–Hausdorff formula,

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \langle e^{-\beta\Omega}\rangle_{\tau_{\beta}}=&\frac{1}{Z_{\beta}[H]}\tr\big(e^{-\beta H}e^{-\beta\Omega}\big)\\
=&\frac{1}{Z_{\beta}[H]}\tr e^{-\beta(H+\Omega)+\frac{1}{2}\beta^{2}[H,\Omega]+\cdots}\\
=&\frac{1}{Z_{\beta}[H]}\tr e^{-\beta U^{\dagger}H^{\prime}U+\frac{1}{2}\beta^{2}[H,U^{\dagger}H^{\prime}U]+\cdots},\end{aligned}

(S1)

one could naively expect that, since the commutator terms go to zero as
\hbar\to 0
, one would have that
\langle e^{-\beta\Omega}\rangle_{\tau_{\beta}}
will tend to

\displaystyle\frac{1}{Z_{\beta}[H]}\tr e^{-\beta U^{\dagger}H^{\prime}U}=e^{-\beta(F_{\beta}[H^{\prime}]-F_{\beta}[H])}.

However, due to the presence of infinite-norm operators, the
\hbar\to 0
limit may not necessarily be continuous in such systems. In order to see this, let us take an oscillator with the Hamiltonian

\displaystyle H=\frac{1}{2}\big(q^{2}+p^{2}\big),

(S2)

that is, the mass and the frequency are equal to
1
, so that
\beta\hbar
is dimensionless.

Now, performing a process as a result of which the frequency of the oscillator changes,
\beta^{2}[H,U^{\dagger}H^{\prime}U]
will necessarily contain a term proportional to
\beta^{2}[p^{2},q^{2}]=-2i\beta^{2}\hbar\{p,q\}
, where
\{\cdot,\cdot\}
is the anticommutator. Introducing the creation and annihilation operators
a
and
a^{\dagger}
, we will have
\beta^{2}[p^{2},q^{2}]=2(\beta\hbar)^{2}\left[(a^{\dagger})^{2}-a^{2}\right]
. On the one hand, if
\{|n\rangle\}_{n=0}^{\infty}
are the eigenvectors of
a^{\dagger}a
, then

\displaystyle\langle n+2|\beta^{2}[p^{2},q^{2}]|n\rangle=2(\beta\hbar)^{2}\sqrt{(n+1)(n+2)},

meaning that the norm of
\beta^{2}[p^{2},q^{2}]
is infinity for any, no matter how small, non-zero value of
\hbar\beta
.

On the other hand, if
\beta\hbar=0
,
\|\beta^{2}[p^{2},q^{2}]\|=0
, which means that the
\beta\hbar\to 0
limit is not continuous for
\beta^{2}[H,U^{\dagger}H^{\prime}U]
, which, in turn, implies that the naïve
\beta\hbar\to 0
limit in Eq. (
S1
) has to be taken with extra care. We do this calculation for a specific illustrative example below and show that indeed
\langle e^{-\beta\Omega}\rangle
converges to
e^{-\beta(F_{\beta}[H^{\prime}]-F_{\beta}[H])}
as
\beta\hbar\to 0
.

Our goal will thus be to explicitly calculate

\displaystyle\Omega=U^{\dagger}H^{\prime}U-H

(S3)

and then calculate

\displaystyle\big\langle e^{-\beta\Omega}\big\rangle_{\tau_{\beta}}=\sum_{n}\langle\psi_{n}|\tau_{\beta}|\psi_{n}\rangle\,e^{-\beta W_{n}},

(S4)

where
W_{n}
are the eigenvalues of
\Omega
and
|\psi_{n}\rangle
are its eigenvectors. Then, we are going to take the
\beta\hbar\to 0
limit. We know that, for any finite
\beta\hbar
,
\langle e^{-\beta\Omega}\rangle_{\tau_{\beta}}
will be larger than
e^{-\beta(F_{\beta}[H^{\prime}]-F_{\beta}[H])}

[
5
]
.

Given the infinite-dimensional Hilbert space, one should of course distinguish between
\Omega
’s with spectra bounded and unbounded from below. In this section, we consider only
\Omega
’s whose spectra are bounded from below.

Since during the driving the Hamiltonian of the oscillator remains quadratic in
q
and
p
, the generated unitary evolution operator
U
is Gaussian. Therefore, the transformation of
q
and
p
in the Heisenberg picture will be symplectic
[
75
]
, which means that, introducing the column vectors
\mathbf{z}=(q,p)
and
\mathbf{z}^{\prime}=(U^{\dagger}qU,\,U^{\dagger}pU)
, we can write

\displaystyle\mathbf{z}^{\prime}=S\mathbf{z},

(S5)

where
S
is a
2\times 2
symplectic matrix that depends only on
U
.

Now, if

\displaystyle H^{\prime}=\mathbf{z}^{\mathrm{T}}\Lambda\mathbf{z},

(S6)

where
\Lambda>\mathbb{0}_{2}
is a
2\times 2
real, symmetric matrix (
\mathbb{0}_{2}
is the
2\times 2
zero matrix), then

\displaystyle U^{\dagger}H^{\prime}U=(\mathbf{z}^{\prime})^{\mathrm{T}}\Lambda\mathbf{z}^{\prime}=\mathbf{z}^{\mathrm{T}}S^{\mathrm{T}}\Lambda S\mathbf{z},

(S7)

and therefore, recalling Eq. (
S2
),

\displaystyle\Omega=\mathbf{z}^{\mathrm{T}}\Xi\mathbf{z}

(S8)

with

\displaystyle\Xi=S^{\mathrm{T}}\Lambda S-\frac{1}{2}\mathbbm{1}_{2},

(S9)

where
\mathbbm{1}_{2}
is the
2\times 2
identity matrix. As mentioned above, we will consider the only the processes for which

\displaystyle\Xi>\mathbb{0}_{2}.

(S10)

In which case, by the Williamson’s theorem
[
76
,
75
]
, there exists a symplectic transformation,
\Sigma
, that takes
\mathbf{z}
to new coordinates
\tilde{\mathbf{z}}=(\tilde{q},\tilde{p})
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathbf{z}}=\Sigma\mathbf{z},

(S11)

in which
\Xi
is proportional to the identity. Namely, if
w>0
is the symplectic eigenvalue of
\Xi
, then

\displaystyle\Omega=\tilde{\mathbf{z}}^{T}(\Sigma^{-1})^{T}\Xi\Sigma^{-1}\tilde{\mathbf{z}}=w(\tilde{q}^{2}+\tilde{p}^{2}).

(S12)

Introducing the symplectic form
I=\Big(\!\begin{array}[]{cc}0&1\\
-1&0\end{array}\!\Big)
, one can calculate
w
by noting that the eigenvalues of
\Xi I
are
\pm iw
. Using that, it is easy to show that

\displaystyle w=\det(\Xi^{1/2})=\sqrt{\det\Xi}.

(S13)

Moreover, the symplectic transformation in Eq. (
S12
) is given by

\displaystyle\Sigma=w^{-1/2}\Xi^{1/2}.

(S14)

In order to calculate the trace in Eq. (
S4
), we will make use of some ready formulas for Gaussian states, presented in Ref.
[
77
]
. The results Ref.
[
77
]
are given for states, therefore we will introduce density operator

\displaystyle\kappa_{\beta}=\frac{1}{Z_{W}}e^{-\beta\Omega},

(S15)

where

\displaystyle Z_{W}=\tr e^{-\beta\Omega}=\left(e^{\beta\hbar w}-e^{-\beta\hbar w}\right)^{-1},

(S16)

so that

\displaystyle\big\langle e^{-\beta\Omega}\big\rangle_{\tau_{\beta}}=Z_{W}\tr\big(\kappa_{\beta}\tau_{\beta}\big).

(S17)

Now, since both
\Omega
and
H
are purely quadratic, we can write

\displaystyle\langle q|\kappa_{\beta}|\bar{q}\rangle

\displaystyle=e^{-A_{W}q^{2}-A^{*}_{W}\bar{q}^{2}-2B_{W}q\bar{q}+C_{W}},

(S18)

\displaystyle\langle q|\tau_{\beta}|\bar{q}\rangle

\displaystyle=e^{-A_{H}q^{2}-A^{*}_{H}\bar{q}^{2}-2B_{H}q\bar{q}+C_{H}},

(S19)

and in Ref.
[
77
]
it is shown that

\displaystyle\langle q|\kappa_{\beta}\tau_{\beta}|\bar{q}\rangle=e^{-Aq^{2}-D\bar{q}^{2}-2Bq\bar{q}+C},

(S20)

with

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} A&=A_{W}-\frac{B_{W}^{2}}{A_{W}^{*}+A_{H}},\\
D&=A_{H}^{*}-\frac{B_{H}^{2}}{A_{W}^{*}+A_{H}},\\
B&=-\frac{B_{W}B_{H}}{A_{W}^{*}+A_{H}},\\
C&=C_{W}+C_{H}+\frac{1}{2}\ln\frac{\pi}{A_{W}^{*}+A_{H}}.~~\end{aligned}

(S21)

Along with Eq. (
S21
), Eq. (
S20
) immediately yields

\displaystyle\tr(\kappa_{\beta}\tau_{\beta})

\displaystyle=\int\limits_{-\infty}^{\infty}dq\langle q|\kappa_{\beta}\tau_{\beta}|q\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{\pi e^{C_{W}+C_{H}}}{\sqrt{\phantom{\big|}\!|A^{*}_{W}+A_{H}|^{2}-(B_{W}+B_{H})^{2}}}.

(S22)

All that is left to do now is to determine how
A_{W}
,
B_{W}
, and
C_{W}
are expressed in terms of
\Xi
. We again use formulas from Ref.
[
77
]
: If

\displaystyle x^{W}=\left(\!\begin{array}[]{cc}x^{W}_{qq}&x^{W}_{qp}\\
\!\phantom{\Big|}\!x^{W}_{qp}&x^{W}_{pp}\end{array}\!\right),

with the elements defined as

\displaystyle x^{W}_{qq}=2\langle q^{2}\rangle_{\kappa_{\beta}},\quad\;x^{W}_{pp}=2\langle p^{2}\rangle_{\kappa_{\beta}},\quad\;x^{W}_{qp}=\langle\{q,p\}\rangle_{\kappa_{\beta}},

(S25)

then

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} A_{W}&=\frac{1+\hbar^{-2}\det x^{W}}{4\hbar^{-1}x_{qq}^{W}}-i\frac{x_{qp}^{W}}{2x^{W}_{qq}},~~~\\
B_{W}&=\frac{1-\hbar^{-2}\det x^{W}}{4\hbar^{-1}x_{qq}^{W}},\\
C_{W}&=-\ln\sqrt{\pi\hbar^{-1}x_{qq}^{W}}.\end{aligned}

(S26)

Obviously, identical formulas apply for
H
.

Let us now calculate the correlators in Eq. (
S25
). In view of Eq. (
S11
),

\displaystyle\mathbf{z}=\Sigma^{-1}\tilde{\mathbf{z}}.

(S27)

So, keeping in mind Eq. (
S12
), by introducing creation-annihilation operators, and doing the simple algebra, we arrive at

\displaystyle x^{W}=\hbar\coth(\hbar\beta w)(\Sigma^{-1})^{\mathrm{T}}\Sigma^{-1},

(S28)

When taking Eqs. (
S13
) and (

) into account, this formula amounts to

\displaystyle x^{W}=\hbar\sqrt{\det\Xi}\coth\Big(\hbar\beta\sqrt{\det\Xi}\Big)\Xi^{-1}.

(S29)

Analogously, reading the

S29

\displaystyle x^{H}=\hbar\coth\Big(\frac{\beta\hbar}{2}\Big)\mathbbm{1}_{2}.

(S30)

It is easy to check that, in the

\displaystyle x^{W}=\frac{\beta^{-1}}{\det\Xi}\bigg(\!\begin{array}[]{cc}\Xi_{22}&-\Xi_{12}\\
-\Xi_{12}&\Xi_{11}\end{array}\!\bigg)+O(\beta\hbar),

where one should keep in mind that

is a symmetric matrix (because

\displaystyle x^{H}=2\beta^{-1}\mathbbm{1}_{2}+O(\beta\hbar).

(S33)

Using these in Eq. (

\displaystyle\tr(\kappa_{\beta}\tau_{\beta})=\beta\hbar\sqrt{\frac{\det\Xi}{\det(\Xi+\mathbbm{1}_{2}/2)}}+O[(\beta\hbar)^{2}].

(S34)

In order to finally calculate
\big\langle e^{-\beta\Omega}\big\rangle_{\tau_{\beta}}
, let us first notice that, due to Eq. (

\det(\Xi+\mathbbm{1}_{2}/2)=\det\Lambda

\displaystyle Z_{W}=\frac{(\beta\hbar)^{-1}}{2\sqrt{\det\Xi}}+O(1).

(S35)

Substituting all these into Eq. (

\displaystyle\big\langle e^{-\beta\Omega}\big\rangle_{\tau_{\beta}}=\frac{1}{2\sqrt{\det\Lambda}}+O(\beta\hbar).

(S36)

Lastly, calculating

and

in the same way as we calculated

\displaystyle e^{-\beta(F_{\beta}[H^{\prime}]-F_{\beta}[H])}=\frac{Z_{\beta}[H^{\prime}]}{Z_{\beta}[H]}=\frac{1}{2\sqrt{\det\Lambda}}+O(\beta\hbar),

which means that

\displaystyle\big\langle e^{-\beta\Omega}\big\rangle_{\tau_{\beta}}=e^{-\beta(F_{\beta}[H^{\prime}]-F_{\beta}[H])}+O(\beta\hbar).

(S37)

This expression shows that the HOW scheme for continuous-variable systems is consistent in the classical limit, at least in the somewhat special case of

. The case of

remains as an interesting open problem.
