ON THE EHRHART POLYNOMIAL OF SCHUBERT MATROIDS
NEIL J.Y. FAN AND YAO LI

Abstract




A
BSTRACT
.
In this paper, we give a formula for the number of lattice points in the dilations of Schubert matroid polytopes. As applications, we obtain the Ehrhart polynomials of uniform and minimal matroids as special cases, and give a recursive formula for the Ehrhart polynomials of
(a,b)
-Catalan matroids. Ferroni showed that uniform and minimal matroids are Ehrhart positive. We show that all sparse paving Schubert matroids are Ehrhart positive and their Ehrhart polynomials are coefficient-wisely bounded by those of minimal and uniform matroids. This confirms a conjecture of Ferroni for the case of sparse paving Schubert matroids. Furthermore, we introduce notched rectangle matroids, which include minimal matroids, sparse paving Schubert matroids and panhandle matroids. We show that three subfamilies of notched rectangle matroids are Ehrhart positive, and conjecture that all notched rectangle matroids are Ehrhart positive.

Introduction

Let
S
be a subset of
[n]:=\{1,2,\ldots,n\}
. The
Schubert matroid

{\rm SM}_{n}(S)
is the matroid with ground set
[n]
and bases

\{T\subseteq[n]\colon T\leq S\},

where
T\leq S
means that:
|T|=|S|
and
the
i
-th smallest element of
T
does not exceed that of
S
for
1\leq i\leq|T|
. Schubert matroids were first studied by Crapo
[
8
]
under the name of nested matroids and rediscovered in various contexts. They have been called shifted matroids
[
1
]
, freedom matroids
[
9
]
, generalized Catalan matroids
[
6
]
and PI-matroids
[
3
]
in the literature.
In particular, uniform matroids, minimal matroids
[
16
]
,
(a,b)
-Catalan matroids
[
5
]
and panhandle matroids
[
20
]
are subclasses of Schubert matroids.
It is also worth mentioning that Schubert matroids
are subfamilies of lattice path matroids

[
5
,
6
,
22
]
, or more generally transversal matroids
[
1
]

and positroids
[
28
]
.

It follows from
Derksen and Fink
[
12
, Theorem 5.4]
that essentially Schubert matroids form a basis of the indicator function space of all matroids. In a more elementary language, the Ehrhart polynomial of an arbitrary matroid polytope is an integer linear combination of Ehrhart polynomials of Schubert matroid polytopes. Moreover, Schubert matroid polytopes are the Minkowski summands of the Newton polytopes of key polynomials and Schubert polynomials, or more generally, Schubitopes, see Fink, Mészáros and St. Dizier
[
19
]
.

Suppose that
M=([n],\mathcal{B})
is a matroid with ground set
[n]
and base set
\mathcal{B}
.
The
matroid polytope

\mathcal{P}(M)
associated to
M
is the convex hull

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}(M)=\mathrm{conv}\{e_{B}\colon B\in\mathcal{B}\},

(1.1)

where
e_{B}=e_{b_{1}}+\cdots+e_{b_{k}}
for
B=\{b_{1},\ldots,b_{k}\}\subseteq[n]
and
\{e_{i}\colon 1\leq i\leq n\}
is the standard basis of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
Given a polytope
\mathcal{P}
and a positive integer
t
, the
t
-dilation
t\mathcal{P}
of
\mathcal{P}
is defined as
t\mathcal{P}=\{t\alpha|\alpha\in\mathcal{P}\}
. Let
i(\mathcal{P},t)=|t\mathcal{P}\cap\mathbb{Z}^{n}|
denote the number of lattice points in
t\mathcal{P}
. It is well known that for integral polytopes,
i(\mathcal{P},t)
is a polynomial in
t
, called the
Ehrhart polynomial
of
\mathcal{P}
. For simplicity, write
i(M,t)
for
i(\mathcal{P}(M),t)
.

It was conjectured by De Loera, Haws and Köppe
[
25
]
that all matroids are
Ehrhart positive
, i.e., the Ehrhart polynomial of any matroid polytope has positive coefficients. Moreover, since matroid polytopes are specific families of generalized permutohedra, Castillo and Liu
[
24
]
further conjectured that generalized permutohedra are also Ehrhart positive. For the study of Ehrhart positivity of various polytopes, see the survey of Liu
[
24
]
.
Recently, Ferroni
[
15
,
16
]
showed that hypersimplices and minimal matroids are Ehrhart positive. In
[
17
]
, Ferroni showed that all sparse paving matroids of rank 2 are Ehrhart positive, but provided counterexamples to both aforementioned conjectures of all ranks greater than or equal to 3. Ferroni, Jochemko and Schröter
[
18
]
further showed that all matroids of rank 2 are Ehrhart positive and are coefficient-wisely bounded by minimal and
uniform matroids.

In this paper, we consider the Ehrhart polynomials of Schubert matroid polytopes. We provide a formula for the number of lattice points in the
t
-dilation
t\mathcal{P}({\rm SM}_{n}(S))
of
\mathcal{P}({\rm SM}_{n}(S))
. To this end, we first show that
t\mathcal{P}({\rm SM}_{n}(S))
is in fact the Newton polytope of the key polynomial
\kappa_{t\alpha}(x)
, where
\alpha
is the indicator vector of
S
. It follows from Fink, Mészáros and St. Dizier
[
19
]
that each lattice point in the Newton polytope of
\kappa_{t\alpha}(x)
is an exponent vector of
\kappa_{t\alpha}(x)
. Then we use Kohnert algorithm to generate all the different monomials of
\kappa_{t\alpha}(x)
and thus obtain a formula for the number of lattice points in
t\mathcal{P}({\rm SM}_{n}(S))
.

As applications, we obtain the Ehrhart polynomials of hypersimplices
[
21
]
and minimal matroids
[
16
]
as simple special cases, and give a recursive formula for the Ehrhart polynomials of
(a,b)
-Catalan matroids. We also show that all sparse paving Schubert matroids are Ehrhart positive by proving that they are coefficient-wisely bounded by the minimal and uniform matroids. Ferroni
[
16
]
conjectured that all matroids are coefficient-wisely bounded by the minimal and uniform matroids, which was disproved by Ferroni
[
17
]
later on. We confirm this conjecture for the case of sparse paving Schubert matroids. Moreover, we introduce notched rectangle matroids, and show that three subfamilies of notched rectangle matroids are
Ehrhart positive. We conjecture that all notched rectangle matroids are Ehrhart positive.

To describe our results, we need some notations.
Assume that
S\subseteq[n]
is a finite set of positive integers. Since we only consider Schubert matroids
{\rm SM}_{n}(S)
, it suffices to let
n
be the maximal element of
S
. The
indicator vector

\mathbb{I}(S)
of
S
is the 0-1 vector
\mathbb{I}(S)=(i_{1},\ldots,i_{n})
, where
i_{j}=1
if
j\in S
, and 0 otherwise. Clearly,
i_{n}=1
. For simplicity, write
\mathbb{I}(S)=(0^{r_{1}},1^{r_{2}},\ldots,0^{r_{2m-1}},1^{r_{2m}})
,
where
0^{r_{1}}
represents
r_{1}
copies of 0’s,
1^{r_{2}}
represents
r_{2}
copies of 1’s, etc. Thus
S
can be written as an integer sequence
r(S)=(r_{1},r_{2},\ldots,r_{2m})
of length
2m
,
where
r_{1}\geq 0
and
r_{i}>0
for
i\geq 2
.
It is easy to see that given such an integer sequence
r
, there is a unique set
S
whose indicator vector
\mathbb{I}(S)
can be written in this way. We will use
S
,
r
or
r(S)
interchangeably with no further clarification. For example, let
S=\{2,6,7,10\}\subseteq[10]
, then
\mathbb{I}(S)=(0,1,0^{3},1^{2},0^{2},1)
and
r(S)=(1,1,3,2,2,1)
.

Given
r=(r_{1},r_{2},\ldots,r_{2m})
, define two integer sequences
u=(u_{1},\ldots,u_{m})
and
v=(v_{1},\ldots,v_{m})
as follows. For
1\leq i\leq m
, let

\displaystyle u_{i}

\displaystyle=\min\left\{r_{2i-1},\sum_{j=i+1}^{m}r_{2j}\right\}\ \ \text{and}\ \ v_{i}=\min\left\{r_{2i},\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}r_{2j-1}\right\},

(1.2)

where empty sums are interpreted as 0.
Assume that
a,b,t\geq 0
and
c\in\mathbb{Z}
are all integers, define

\displaystyle F(a,b,c,t)=\sum_{j=0}^{a+b}(-1)^{j}{a+b\choose j}{(t+1)(b-j)+a+c-1\choose a+b-1}.

(1.3)

By convention,
{0\choose 0}=1
and
{n\choose k}=0
if
k<0
or
n<k
. Notice that if
j>\frac{bt+c}{t+1}
in (

(t+1)(b-j)+a+c-1<a+b-1
, and thus
{(t+1)(b-j)+a+c-1\choose a+b-1}=0
.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
S\subseteq[n]
with
r(S)=(r_{1},\ldots,r_{2m})
. We have

\displaystyle i({\rm SM}_{n}(S),t)=\sum_{(c_{1},\ldots,c_{m})}\prod_{j=1}^{m}F(r_{2j-1},r_{2j},c_{j},t),

(1.4)

where
c_{1}+\cdots+c_{m}=0
, and for
1\leq j\leq m
,

\displaystyle-tv_{j}\leq c_{j}\leq tu_{j}\ \ \text{and}\ \ c_{1}+\cdots+c_{j}\geq 0.

Since the variable
t
appears as the upper limit of the sum,
(
1.4
) is not a legitimate polynomial. Nevertheless, there are still many applications.
For example, let
S=\{n-k+1,\ldots,n\}
, where
n>k\geq 1
. Then we obtain the uniform matroid
U_{k,n}
. In this case,
r=(n-k,k)
,
m=1,\ c_{1}=0
, by Theorem
1.1
,

i(U_{k,n},t)=F(n-k,k,0,t).

The Ehrhart polynomial
i(U_{k,n},t)
was first obtained by Katzman
[
21
]
and then shown to have positive coefficients by Ferroni
[
15
]
.

Corollary 1.2

(Katzman
[
21
]
)
.

We have

i(U_{k,n},t)=F(n-k,k,0,t)=\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}(-1)^{i}{n\choose i}{(k-i)t-i+n-1\choose n-1}.

Let
S=\{2,3,\ldots,k,n\}
, where
n>k\geq 2
, we are lead to the minimal matroid
T_{k,n}
.
Minimal matroids were first studied independently by Dinolt
[
13
]
and Murty
[
27
]
. Ferroni
[
16
]
showed that
T_{k,n}
is the graphic matroid of a
(k+1)
-cycle with one edge replaced by
n-k
parallel copies. In this case,
r(S)=(1,k-1,n-k-1,1)

and

for

1.1
,

i(T_{k,n},t)=\sum_{j=0}^{t}F(1,k-1,j,t)F(n-k-1,1,-j,t).

Since both

and

are binomials, we can re-obtain the following closed formula of

Corollary 1.3

(Ferroni
[
16
]
)
.

We have

\displaystyle i(T_{k,n},t)=\frac{1}{{n-1\choose k-1}}{t+n-k\choose n-k}\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}{n-k+j-1\choose j}{t+j\choose j}.

(1.5)

It is apparent that both
i(T_{k,n},t)
and
i(T_{k,n},t-1)
have positive coefficients.

\sigma\in\Gamma_{n}

1.3
Sparse paving Schubert matroids

Let

2
Preliminaries

A
matroid
is a pair
M=(E,\mathcal{I})
consisting of a finite set
E
, called the ground set, and a collection
\mathcal{I}
of subsets of
E
, called
independent sets
, such that:

(1)

\emptyset\in\mathcal{I}
;

(2)

If
J\in\mathcal{I}
and
I\subseteq J
, then
I\in\mathcal{I}
;

(3)

If
I,J\in\mathcal{I}
and
|I|<|J|
, then there exists
j\in J\setminus I

such that
I\cup\{j\}\in\mathcal{I}
.

By (2), a matroid
M
is determined by the collection
\mathcal{B}
of maximal independent sets, called the bases of
M
. By (3), all the bases have the same cardinality, called the rank of
M
, denoted as
{\rm rk}(M)
. So we can write
M=(E,\mathcal{B})
. The dual of
M
is the matroid
M^{*}=(E,\mathcal{B}^{*})
, where
\mathcal{B}^{*}=\{E\setminus B:B\in\mathcal{B}\}
. It is easy to check that the dual of a Schubert matroid
{\rm SM}_{n}(S)
is isomorphic to
{\rm SM}_{n}(S^{\prime})
, where
r(S^{\prime})
is the reverse of
r(S)
.

A subset
I
of
E
is called
dependent
if it is not an independent set. If
C\subseteq E
is dependent but every proper subset of
C
is independent, we say that
C
is a
circuit
. A subset
F
of
E
is called a
flat
if
{\rm rk}_{M}(F\cup\{a\})>{\rm rk}_{M}(F)
for every
a\notin F
. A
hyperplane

H
is a flat such that

is obtained from

by interchanging

and

\displaystyle i((a,b,c,d),t)+i((b,a,d,c),t)=i(U_{b+d,a+b+c+d},t)+i(U_{b,a+b},t)i(U_{d,c+d},t).

(4.7)

Proof.
Since
F(a+c,b+d,0,t)
is the number of solutions of
x_{1}+\cdots+x_{a+b+c+d}=(b+d)t
, where
0\leq x_{i}\leq t
, which is equal to the sum of number of solutions of

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}x_{1}+\cdots+x_{a+b}=bt+j,\\
x_{a+b+1}+\cdots+x_{a+b+c+d}=dt-j,\\
0\leq x_{i}\leq t,\ \text{for $1\leq i\leq a+b+c+d$},\end{array}\right.

for all possible integers
j
.
It is clear that if
j<-bt
, then the first equation of (
4
) has no solutions. If
j<-ct
, then the second equation has no solution. Thus
j\geq-t\cdot\min\{b,c\}
. Similarly, one can check that
j\leq t\cdot\min\{a,d\}
.
Therefore,

\displaystyle F(a+c,b+d,0,t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=-t\cdot\min\{b,c\}}^{t\cdot\min\{a,d\}}F(a,b,j,t)F(c,d,-j,t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{t\cdot\min\{a,d\}}F(a,b,j,t)F(c,d,-j,t)+\sum_{j=-t\cdot\min\{b,c\}}^{0}F(a,b,j,t)F(c,d,-j,t)

\displaystyle\hskip 20.00003pt-F(a,b,0,t)F(c,d,0,t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{t\cdot\min\{a,d\}}F(a,b,j,t)F(c,d,-j,t)+\sum_{j=0}^{t\cdot\min\{b,c\}}F(b,a,j,t)F(d,c,-j,t)

\displaystyle\hskip 20.00003pt-F(a,b,0,t)F(c,d,0,t)

\displaystyle=i((a,b,c,d),t)+i((b,a,d,c),t)-i(U_{b,a+b},t)i(U_{d,c+d},t),

where the last step holds by (
4.6
).

Remark
. As pointed out by the referee, the statement of Theorem
4.2
is actually a fact of matroid subdivisions. More precisely, we split the uniform matroid polytope

\mathcal{P}(U_{b+d,a+c+b+d})=\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{a+b+c+d}:\sum_{i=1}^{a+b+c+d}x_{i}=b+d\right\}

into two pieces:

\mathcal{P}_{1}=\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{a+b+c+d}:\sum_{i=1}^{c+d}x_{a+b+i}\leq d,\sum_{i=1}^{a+b+c+d}x_{i}=b+d\right\}

and

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{2}=\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{a+b+c+d}:\sum_{i=1}^{a+b}x_{i}\leq b,\sum_{i=1}^{a+b+c+d}x_{i}=b+d\right\}.

And their intersection is a common facet

H=\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{a+b+c+d}:\sum_{i=1}^{a+b}x_{i}=b,\sum_{i=1}^{c+d}x_{a+b+i}=d\right\}.

One can check that

\mathcal{P}_{1}=\mathcal{P}({\rm SM}_{n}(a,b,c,d))
and
\mathcal{P}_{2}
can be obtained by a rotation of the Schubert matroid polytope

\mathcal{P}({\rm SM}_{n}(c,d,a,b))=\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{a+b+c+d}:\sum_{i=1}^{a+b}x_{c+d+i}\leq b,\sum_{i=1}^{a+b+c+d}x_{i}=b+d\right\}.

Thus
i(\mathcal{P}_{2},t)=i((c,d,a,b),t)=i((b,a,d,c),t)
. Moreover, it is easy to see that

i(H,t)=i(U_{b,a+b},t)i(U_{d,c+d},t)
.
Therefore, we have

\displaystyle i(U_{b+d,a+b+c+d},t)=i((a,b,c,d),t)+i((b,a,d,c),t)-i(U_{b,a+b},t)i(U_{d,c+d},t).

Corollary 4.3
.

We have

\displaystyle i((1,1,a,b),t)+i((1,1,b-1,a+1),t)=(t+2)i(U_{b,a+b+1},t).

(4.11)

Proof.
Since
F(1,1,i,t)=t+1-i
for

, by (
4.6
), we find

\displaystyle i((1,1,a,b),t)+i((1,1,b-1,a+1),t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{t}((t-i+1)F(a,b,-i,t)+(t-i+1)F(b-1,a+1,-i,t))

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{t}((t-i+1)F(a,b,-i,t)+(t-i+1)F(a,b,i-t,t))

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{t}((t-i+1)F(a,b,-i,t)+(i+1)F(a,b,-i,t))

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{t}(t+2)F(a,b,-i,t)

\displaystyle=(t+2)i(U_{b,a+b+1},t),

where the second step holds by (
4.3
) and (
4.4
), and the last step follows from (
4.5
).

Proof of Theorem

.

By (

), we find

.

Recall that the minimal matroid

is the Schubert matroid

Proof of Corollary
1.3
. Since
r=(1,k-1,n-k-1,1)
, by (
4.6
), we have

\displaystyle i(T_{k,n},t)=\sum_{j=0}^{t}F(1,k-1,j,t)F(n-k-1,1,-j,t).

(4.12)

By (
4.3
) and (
4.4
), we have

\displaystyle F(1,k-1,j,t)=F(k-1,1,-j,t)=F(k,0,t-j,t)=\binom{k+t-j-1}{t-j}

and

\displaystyle F(n-k-1,1,-j,t)=F(n-k,0,t-j,t)={t-j+n-k-1\choose n-k-1}.

Then

\displaystyle i(T_{k,n},t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{t}{t-j+n-k-1\choose n-k-1}\binom{k+t-j-1}{t-j}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{t}{j+n-k-1\choose n-k-1}\binom{k+j-1}{j}.

(4.13)

Thus we need to show that

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{t}{j+n-k-1\choose n-k-1}\binom{k+j-1}{j}=\frac{1}{{n-1\choose k-1}}{t+n-k\choose n-k}\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}{n-k+j-1\choose j}{t+j\choose j}.

(4.14)

Let
s=n-k
in (

), then we aim to show that

\displaystyle\binom{s+k-1}{s}\sum_{j=0}^{t}\binom{j+s-1}{s-1}\binom{j+k-1}{k-1}=\binom{t+s}{s}\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\binom{j+s-1}{s-1}\binom{t+j}{j}.

(4.15)

It is easy to see that the left hand side of (

) is the coefficient of
y^{s-1}x^{k-1}
in

\displaystyle\binom{s+k-1}{s}\sum_{j=0}^{t}(1+y)^{s+j-1}(1+x)^{k+j-1}

\displaystyle=\binom{k-1+s}{s}(1+y)^{s-1}(1+x)^{k-1}\cdot\frac{1-((1+y)(1+x))^{t+1}}{-x-y-xy}.

(4.16)

Similarly, the right hand side of (

) is the coefficient of
y^{s-1}x^{t}
in

\displaystyle\binom{t+s}{s}\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}(1+y)^{s+j-1}(1+x)^{t+j}

\displaystyle=\binom{t+s}{s}(1+y)^{s-1}(1+x)^{t}\cdot\frac{1-((1+y)(1+x))^{k}}{-x-y-xy}.

(4.17)

One can check that the coefficient of

in (
4
) is equal to the coefficient of

in (
4
). Thus (

) follows. This completes the proof.

5
(a,b)
-Catalan matroids

In this section, we give a proof of Theorem
1.7
.
Recall that the
(a,b)
-Catalan matroid
\mathbf{C}_{n}^{a,b}
is the Schubert matroid
{\rm SM}_{(a+b)n}(S)
, where

r(S)=(\overbrace{a,b,a,b,\ldots,a,b}^{2n}).

Given a composition
\sigma
of
n
,

\ell(\sigma)
denotes the number of parts of
\sigma
,
d(\sigma)
denotes the cardinality of the equivalent class containing
\sigma
.
And
\Gamma_{n}
is the set of pairwise non-equivalent compositions of
n
with at least two parts and minimal parts larger than 1.

It can be seen that (
1.7
) is equivalent to

\displaystyle F(na,nb,0,t)

\displaystyle=n\cdot i(\mathbf{\overline{C}}_{n}^{a,b},t)+i(U_{b,a+b},t)^{n}+\sum_{\sigma\in\Gamma_{n}}(-1)^{\ell(\sigma)-1}\frac{nd(\sigma)}{\ell(\sigma)}\cdot i(\overline{\mathbf{C}}_{\sigma}^{a,b},t),

(5.1)

where
\displaystyle i(\overline{\mathbf{C}}_{\sigma}^{a,b},t)=\prod_{j=1}^{\ell(\sigma)}i(\overline{\mathbf{C}}_{\sigma_{j}}^{a,b},t)
and

i(\overline{\mathbf{C}}_{\sigma_{j}}^{a,b},t)=i(\mathbf{C}_{\sigma_{j}}^{a,b},t)-i(U_{b,a+b},t)\cdot i(\mathbf{C}_{\sigma_{j}-1}^{a,b},t).

We shall prove (
5.1
) by interpreting both sides in terms of weighted enumerations of certain lattice paths.

Let us begin with interpreting
i(\mathbf{C}_{n}^{a,b},t)
and
F(na,nb,0,t)
separately. By Theorem
1.1
, since
r=(a,b,\ldots,a,b)
, for
1\leq j\leq n
, we have

\displaystyle u_{j}=\min\{a,(n-j)b\},\ v_{j}=\min\{b,(j-1)a\}.

(5.2)

Thus

\displaystyle i(\mathbf{C}_{n}^{a,b},t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{(c_{1},\ldots,c_{n})}\prod_{j=1}^{n}F(a,b,c_{j},t),

where
c_{1}+\cdots+c_{n}=0
,
c_{1}+\cdots+c_{j}\geq 0
and
-tv_{j}\leq c_{j}\leq tu_{j}
, for
1\leq j\leq n
.

On the other hand, since

F(na,nb,0,t)
is the number of solutions of

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}x_{1}+x_{2}+\cdots+x_{(a+b)n}=bnt,\\
0\leq x_{i}\leq t,\ \text{for $1\leq i\leq(a+b)n$}.\end{array}\right.

which is equivalent to the system of equations

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{c}x_{1,1}+\cdots+x_{1,a+b}=bt+c^{\prime}_{1},\\
x_{2,1}+\cdots+x_{2,a+b}=bt+c^{\prime}_{2},\\
\vdots\\
x_{n,1}+\cdots+x_{n,a+b}=bt+c^{\prime}_{n},\end{array}\right.

for all possible integers
c^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,c^{\prime}_{n}
,
where
0\leq x_{i,j}\leq t
for
1\leq i\leq n
and
1\leq j\leq a+b
, and
c^{\prime}_{1}+\cdots+c^{\prime}_{n}=0
. It is easy to see that we can require
-bt\leq c^{\prime}_{j}\leq at
for
1\leq j\leq n
. Thus

\displaystyle F(na,nb,0,t)=\sum_{(c^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,c^{\prime}_{n})}\prod_{j=1}^{n}F(a,b,c^{\prime}_{j},t),

where
c^{\prime}_{1}+\cdots+c^{\prime}_{n}=0
, and
-bt\leq c^{\prime}_{j}\leq at
for
1\leq j\leq n
.

Let

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{n}^{a,b}=\left\{(c_{1},\ldots,c_{n})\,|\,\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}=0,\ \sum_{i=1}^{j}c_{i}\geq 0\ \text{and}\ -tv_{j}\leq c_{j}\leq tu_{j},\ \forall\ 1\leq j\leq n\right\}

(5.7)

and

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{n}^{a,b}=\left\{(c_{1},\ldots,c_{n})\,|\,\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}=0,\ -bt\leq c_{j}\leq at,\ \forall\ 1\leq j\leq n\right\}.

(5.8)

Clearly,
\mathcal{C}_{n}^{a,b}\subseteq\mathcal{F}_{n}^{a,b}
.
We can view each sequence
\mathbf{c}=(c_{1},\ldots,c_{n})\in\mathcal{F}_{n}^{a,b}
as a lattice path from
(0,0)
to
(n,0)
such that
c_{j}
represents: an up step
(0,0)\rightarrow(1,c_{j})
if
c_{j}>0
, a down step
(0,0)\rightarrow(1,-|c_{j}|)
if
c_{j}<0
, or a horizontal step
(0,0)\rightarrow(1,0)
if
c_{j}=0
. Assign a weight to
\mathbf{c}
as

{\rm wt}(\mathbf{c})=\prod_{j=1}^{n}F(a,b,c_{j},t).

In particular,
F(a,b,0,t)^{n}
is the weight of the path
(0,0,\ldots,0)
. Then

i(\mathbf{C}_{n}^{a,b},t)=\sum_{\mathbf{c}\in\mathcal{C}_{n}^{a,b}}{\rm wt}(\mathbf{c})\ \ \text{and}\ \ F(na,nb,0,t)=\sum_{\mathbf{c}\in\mathcal{F}_{n}^{a,b}}{\rm wt}(\mathbf{c})

can be viewed as weighted enumerations of lattice paths in
\mathcal{C}_{n}^{a,b}
and
\mathcal{F}_{n}^{a,b}
, respectively.

Let

\displaystyle\mathcal{\overline{C}}_{n}^{a,b}=\{(c_{1},\ldots,c_{n})\in\mathcal{C}_{n}^{a,b}\,|\,c_{n}\neq 0\}.

(5.9)

Then we find

\displaystyle i(\mathbf{\overline{C}}_{n}^{a,b},t)=\sum_{\mathbf{c}\in\mathcal{\overline{C}}_{n}^{a,b}}{\rm wt}(\mathbf{c}).

(5.10)

Moreover, given a composition
\sigma=(\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{s})
of
n
, denote

\displaystyle\mathcal{\overline{C}}_{\sigma}^{a,b}=\{(\mathbf{c}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{c}_{s})\,|\,\mathbf{c}_{j}\in\mathcal{\overline{C}}_{\sigma_{j}}^{a,b},\ \text{for}\ 1\leq j\leq s\},

(5.11)

then

i(\mathcal{\overline{C}}_{\sigma}^{a,b},t)=\prod_{j=1}^{\ell(\sigma)}i(\overline{\mathbf{C}}_{\sigma_{j}}^{a,b},t)=\sum_{\mathbf{c}\in\mathcal{\overline{C}}_{\sigma}^{a,b}}{\rm wt}(\mathbf{c}).

Therefore, (
5.1
) is equivalent to

\displaystyle\sum_{\mathbf{c}\in\mathcal{F}_{n}^{a,b}}{\rm wt}(\mathbf{c})=n\cdot\sum_{\mathbf{c}\in\mathcal{\overline{C}}_{n}^{a,b}}{\rm wt}(\mathbf{c})+{\rm wt}((0,\ldots,0))+\sum_{\sigma\in\Gamma_{n}}(-1)^{\ell(\sigma)-1}\frac{nd(\sigma)}{\ell(\sigma)}\sum_{\mathbf{c}\in\mathcal{\overline{C}}_{\sigma}^{a,b}}{\rm wt}(\mathbf{c}).

(5.12)

To prove (
5.12
), we shall enumerate the number of appearances of each
\mathbf{c}\in\mathcal{F}_{n}^{a,b}
in the right hand side of (
5.12
) by inclusion-exclusion. To this end, define two shifting operators
R
and
L
on each
\mathbf{c}=(c_{1},\ldots,c_{n})\in\mathcal{F}_{n}^{a,b}
as follows

\displaystyle R(\mathbf{c})

\displaystyle=(c_{2},\ldots,c_{n},c_{1}),

\displaystyle L(\mathbf{c})

\displaystyle=(c_{n},c_{1},\ldots,c_{n-1}),

and let
R^{m}(\mathbf{c})=(c_{m+1},\ldots,c_{n},c_{1},\ldots,c_{m})
denote the effect of applying
R
to
\mathbf{c}

m
times, where
m\geq 1
. Similar
L^{m}(\mathbf{c})
denote applying
L
to
\mathbf{c}

m
times. It is clear that
R^{n}(\mathbf{c})=L^{n}(c)=\mathbf{c}
.
Denote
\mathcal{L}^{m}_{\mathbf{c}}
by the list of paths obtained from
\mathbf{c}
by applying
L
to
\mathbf{c}

m-1
times, i.e.,

\mathcal{L}^{m}_{\mathbf{c}}=(\mathbf{c},L(\mathbf{c}),L^{2}(\mathbf{c}),\ldots,L^{m-1}(\mathbf{c})).

It is quite possible that
\mathcal{L}^{m}_{\mathbf{c}}

Similarly, let

\mathcal{R}^{m}_{\mathbf{c}}=(\mathbf{c},R(\mathbf{c}),R^{2}(\mathbf{c}),\ldots,R^{m-1}(\mathbf{c})).

Obviously, each path in
\mathcal{L}^{m}_{\mathbf{c}}

\mathcal{R}^{m}_{\mathbf{c}}

n\ast\mathcal{\overline{C}}_{n}^{a,b}

\mathbf{c}

\mathcal{R}^{n}_{\mathbf{c}}

\Gamma_{n}

\Gamma_{n}=\{\sigma^{1},\sigma^{2},\ldots,\sigma^{\gamma_{n}}\}

\displaystyle\left(n\ast\mathcal{\overline{C}}_{n}^{a,b},(-1)^{\ell(\sigma^{1})-1}\frac{nd(\sigma^{1})}{\ell(\sigma^{1})}\ast\mathcal{\overline{C}}_{\sigma^{1}}^{a,b},\ldots,(-1)^{\ell(\sigma^{\gamma_{n}})-1}\frac{nd(\sigma^{\gamma_{n}})}{\ell(\sigma^{\gamma_{n}})}\ast\mathcal{\overline{C}}_{\sigma^{\gamma_{n}}}^{a,b}\right).

(5.13)

To prove (
5.12
),
we aim to show that, after cancellations, each

Lemma 5.2
.

We have

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{n}^{a,b}=\bigcup_{\mathbf{c}\in\mathcal{C}_{n}^{a,b}}\mathcal{E}_{\mathbf{c}}.

Proof.
By Lemma

Sparse paving Schubert matroids

In this section, we study sparse paving Schubert matroids. Recall that a matroid
M
if sparse paving if and only if every subset of cardinality
{\rm rk}(M)
is either a basis or a circuit-hyperplane.

Let
r=(k-1,1,1,n-k-1)
, where
n>k\geq 2
, we obtain a Schubert matroid, denoted as
{\rm Sp}_{k,n}
. As will be shown,
{\rm Sp}_{k,n}
is sparse paving, and a Schubert matroid is sparse paving if it is either a uniform matroid or a uniform matroid with one basis removed. Equivalently, a sparse paving Schubert matroid is a notch matroid with the upper bounding path
U=N^{n-k}E^{k}
, see Bon and de Mier
[
6
, Definition 8.1]
.

Proposition 6.1
.

A Schubert matroid
{\rm SM}_{n}(S)
of rank
n-k
is sparse paving if and only if it is uniform or
r(S)=(k-1,1,1,n-k-1)
, i.e.,

S=\{k+1,\ldots,n\}\ \ \text{or}\ \ \{k,k+2,\ldots,n\}.

Proof.
If
S=\{k+1,\ldots,n\}
, then
{\rm SM}_{n}(S)
is the uniform matroid
U_{n-k,n}
, which is sparse paving by definition. Suppose that
S=\{k,k+2,\ldots,n\}
. Then we aim to show that every
(n-k)
-subset of
[n]
is either a basis or a circuit-hyperplane. In fact, it is easy to see that there is exactly one
(n-k)
-subset of
[n]
which is not a basis of
{\rm SM}_{n}(S)
, i.e.,
T=\{k+1,\ldots,n\}
. It is also straightforward to check that
T
is both a circuit and a flat. Moreover,
T
is also a hyperplane since
{\rm rk}_{S}(T)=n-k-1
. Thus
{\rm SM}_{n}(S)
is sparse paving.

On the contrary, suppose that a Schubert matroid
M={\rm SM}_{n}(S)
is sparse paving and it is not uniform. Then every subset of cardinality
n-k
is either a basis or a circuit-hyperplane. Since
M
is not uniform, it has a circuit-hyperplane. It is easy to see that
M
is connected, by
[
6
, Theorem 8.3]
,
M
is a notch matroid. Thus
r(S)=(k-1,1,1,n-k-1)
.

By the proof of Proposition
6.1
, if
S=\{k,k+2,\ldots,n\}
, then its corresponding sparse paving Schubert matroid
{\rm Sp}_{k,n}
has exactly one circuit-hyperplane, i.e.,
\{k+1,\ldots,n\}
. The following lemma follows from Ferroni
[
17
, Corollary 4.6]
.

Lemma 6.2
.

We have

i({\rm Sp}_{k,n},t)=i({\rm Sp}_{n-k,n},t)=i(U_{k,n},t)-i(T_{k,n},t-1).

By Lemma
6.2
, we find that
i(U_{k,n},t)-i({\rm Sp}_{k,n},t)=i(T_{k,n},t-1)
.
By Ferroni
[
16
, Theorem 1.6]
,
i(T_{k,n},t-1)
has positive coefficients. Therefore,

i({\rm Sp}_{k,n},t)\leq i(U_{k,n},t).

To finish the proof of Theorem
1.10
,
we still need to show that
i(T_{k,n},t)\leq i({\rm Sp}_{k,n},t)
, that is,

i(U_{k,n},t)-i(T_{k,n},t-1)-i(T_{k,n},t)

has positive coefficients.
We aim to prove two slightly stronger statements, i.e.,

\displaystyle i(U_{k,n},t)\geq i(U_{2,n},t)

(6.1)

and

\displaystyle i(U_{2,n},t)-i(T_{k,n},t-1)-i(T_{k,n},t)\geq 0.

(6.2)

In order to prove (
6.1
), we need the combinatorial interpretation of the coefficients of
i(U_{k,n},t)
. For any
m\geq 0
,
Ferroni
[
15
, Theorem 4.3]
showed that the coefficient of
t^{m}
in
i(U_{k,n},t)
is

\displaystyle[t^{m}]i(U_{k,n},t)=\frac{1}{(n-1)!}\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}W(j,n,m+1)A(m,k-j-1),

(6.3)

where
W(j,n,m+1)
are the weighted Lah numbers and
A(m,k-j-1)
are the Eulerian numbers. In particular,

W(0,n,k)={n\brack k}

is the unsigned Stirling number of the first kind. The following properties of
{n\brack k}
are well known,

t(t+1)\cdots(t+n-1)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}{n\brack k}t^{k},

and

\displaystyle{n\brack k}=(n-1){n-1\brack k}+{n-1\brack k-1},

(6.4)

see, for example, Stanley
[
30
]
.

Lemma 6.3
.

For
3\leq k\leq\frac{n}{2}
, we have the coefficient-wise relation

i(U_{2,n},t)\leq i(U_{k,n},t).

Proof.
By (
6.3
), we have

[t^{m}]i(U_{2,n},t)=\frac{1}{(n-1)!}(W(0,n,m+1)A(m,1)+W(1,n,m+1)A(m,0)).

There are four cases to consider.

Case 1.
k<m
. By (
6.3
),

[t^{m}]i(U_{k,n},t)\geq\frac{1}{(n-1)!}(W(0,n,m+1)A(m,k-1)+W(1,n,m+1)A(m,k-2)).

Since the Eulerian numbers are unimodal and
k-1\geq 2
, we have
A(m,k-1)\geq A(m,1)
and
A(m,k-2)\geq A(m,0)
. So

[t^{m}]i(U_{k,n},t)\geq[t^{m}]i(U_{2,n},t).

Case 2.
k=m=3
. When
n=4,5
, the lemma holds obviously.
When
n\geq 6
, it is easy to see that

W(0,n,4)\leq W(1,n,4)\ \text{and}\ W(1,n,4)\leq W(2,n,4).

By (
6.3
),

\displaystyle[t^{3}]i(U_{3,n},t)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(n-1)!}(W(0,n,4)+4W(1,n,4)+W(2,n,4))

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{(n-1)!}(4W(1,n,4)+W(2,n,4))

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{(n-1)!}(4W(0,n,4)+W(1,n,4)))

\displaystyle=[t^{3}]i(U_{2,n},t).

Case 3.
k=m\geq 4
. By (
6.3
),

\displaystyle[t^{m}]i(U_{k,n},t)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{(n-1)!}(W(1,n,m+1)A(m,m-2)+W(m-2,n,m+1)A(m,1)).

Since

A(m,m-2)\geq A(m,0)=1,
it is enough to show that

\displaystyle W(m-2,n,m+1)\geq W(0,n,m+1).

(6.5)

We shall give a combinatorial proof of (
6.5
).

Denote
\mathcal{W}(\ell,n,m)
by the set of partitions of weight
\ell
of
[n]
into
m
linearly ordered blocks, and let
W(\ell,n,m)
be the cardinality of
\mathcal{W}(\ell,n,m)
, see Ferroni
[
15
]
. For a partition
\pi
with linearly ordered blocks, the weight of
\pi
is

w(\pi)=\sum_{B\in\pi}w(B)
,
where
w(B)
is the number of elements in
B
that are smaller than the first element in
B
. In order to prove (
6.5
), we construct an injection from
\mathcal{W}(0,n,m+1)
to
\mathcal{W}(m-2,n,m+1)
.

By definition,

\mathcal{W}(0,n,m+1)
is the set of partitions of
[n]
into
m+1
blocks, the elements of each block are arranged increasingly. Suppose that
\tau\in\mathcal{W}(0,n,m+1)
. We aim to construct a partition
\tau^{\prime}\in\mathcal{W}(m-2,n,m+1)
from
\tau
. Let
B_{1},\ldots,B_{j}
be the blocks of
\tau
having more than one element and the smallest element of
B_{i}
is smaller than the smallest element of
B_{i+1}
for
1\leq i\leq j-1
. Apparently,

|B_{1}|+\cdots+|B_{j}|=n-(m+1-j).

For any linearly ordered block
B=(b_{1},\ldots,b_{s})
with
w(B)=0
, that is,
b_{1}
is the smallest element of
B
. Assume that
b_{m}
is the largest of element of
B
. Let
B^{\prime}
be obtained from
B
by cyclically shifting
b_{m}
to the first position, i.e.,
B^{\prime}=(b_{m},\ldots,b_{s},b_{1},\ldots,b_{m-1})
. Then
w(B^{\prime})=|B|-1
. Therefore,

\displaystyle w(B_{1}^{\prime})+\cdots+w(B_{j}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=|B_{1}|-1+\cdots+|B_{j}|-1=n-(m+1).

Since
k=m\leq\frac{n}{2}
, we have
n-(m+1)\geq m-1
. Thus we can construct a partition
\tau^{\prime}\in\mathcal{W}(m-2,n,m+1)
with weight
m-2
from
\tau
as follows. There exists some index
i\,(1\leq i<j)
such that

w(B_{1}^{\prime})+\cdots+w(B_{i}^{\prime})\leq m-2\ \text{and}\ w(B_{1}^{\prime})+\cdots+w(B_{i+1}^{\prime})>m-2.

We can cyclically shift a suitable element of
B_{i+1}
to the first position to obtain a new block
B_{i+1}^{\prime\prime}
, such that

w(B_{1}^{\prime})+\cdots+w(B_{i}^{\prime})+w(B_{i+1}^{\prime\prime})=m-2.

Keep the other blocks of
\tau^{\prime}
the same with those of
\tau
. It is easy to see that this construction is an injection from
\mathcal{W}(0,n,m+1)
to
\mathcal{W}(m-2,n,m+1)
. This completes the proof of (
6.5
).

Case 4.
k>m
. By (
6.3
),

[t^{m}]i(U_{k,n},t)\geq\frac{1}{(n-1)!}(W(k-1,n,m+1)A(m,0)+W(k-2,n,m+1)A(m,1)).

We aim to show that

\displaystyle\varphi(n,k,m):=W(k-2,n,m+1)-W(0,n,m+1)\geq 0

(6.6)

and

\displaystyle\psi(n,k,m):=W(k-1,n,m+1)-W(1,n,m+1)\geq 0.

(6.7)

By Ferroni
[
15
, Remark 3.9]
,
W(\ell,n,m)=W(n-m-\ell,n,m)
, then we have

\varphi(n,k,m)=W(n-m-k+1,n,m+1)-W(0,n,m+1).

Since
m<k\leq\frac{n}{2}
, we find
n-(m+1)\geq n-m-k+1>0
. By the same arguments in the proof of (
6.5
), we can conclude that
W(n-m-k+1,n,m+1)\geq W(0,n,m+1)
. Thus
\varphi(n,k,m)\geq 0
.

Similarly, since
n-(m+1)\geq k-1>1
, we can also utilize the same arguments in the proof of (
6.5
) to show that
W(k-1,n,m+1)\geq W(1,n,m+1)
. That is,
\psi(n,k,m)\geq 0
.
This completes the proof.

Lemma 6.4
.

For positive integers
m,n
, we have

{n+1\brack m+1}=\sum_{j=0}^{n}{j\brack m}\frac{n!}{j!}.

Proof.
Recall that
{n+1\brack m+1}
represents the number of permutations on
[n+1]
with
m+1
cycles. Alternatively, we can first choose
j
numbers from
1,2,\ldots,n
to form
m
cycles, there are
{n\choose j}{j\brack m}
such ways, then the left
n-j
numbers and the number
n+1
form another cycle, there are
(n-j)!
such ways.

Now we are ready to give a proof of Theorem
1.10
.

Proof of Theorem
1.10
. By Lemma
6.2
and Lemma
6.3
, it suffices to show that

i(U_{2,n},t)-i(T_{k,n},t-1)-i(T_{k,n},t)

has positive coefficients. By Lemma
6.2
again, it is enough to only consider
k\leq\frac{n}{2}
.

We first simplify
[t^{m}]i(U_{2,n},t)
,
[t^{m}]i(T_{k,n},t)
and
[t^{m}]i(T_{k,n},t-1)
for
0\leq m\leq n
, separately.

By Ferroni
[
15
, Corollary 3.13]
,

W(\ell,n,m)=\sum_{j=0}^{\ell}\sum_{i=0}^{n-m}(-1)^{i+j}{n\choose j}{m+\ell-j-1\choose m-1}{j\brack j-i}{n-j\brack m+i-j}.

Then

\displaystyle W(1,n,m+1)=(m+1){n\brack m+1}-n{n-1\brack m}.

(6.8)

Since
A(m,1)=2^{m}-m-1
and
A(m,0)=1
, by (
6.3
) and (
6.8
), we have

\displaystyle(n-1)![t^{m}]i(U_{2,n},t)

\displaystyle=W(0,n,m+1)A(m,1)+W(1,n,m+1)A(m,0)

\displaystyle=2^{m}{n\brack m+1}-n{n-1\brack m}

(6.9)

\displaystyle=2^{m}\left((n-1){n-1\brack m+1}+{n-1\brack m}\right)-n\left((n-2){n-2\brack m}+{n-2\brack m-1}\right)

\displaystyle=(n-2)\left(2^{m}{n-1\brack m+1}-(n-1){n-2\brack m}\right)+2\left(2^{m-1}{n-1\brack m}-(n-1){n-2\brack m-1}\right)

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt+2^{m}{n-1\brack m+1}-(n-2){n-2\brack m}+(n-2){n-2\brack m-1}

\displaystyle\geq(n-2)\left(2^{m}{n-1\brack m+1}-(n-1){n-2\brack m}\right)+2\left(2^{m-1}{n-1\brack m}-(n-1){n-2\brack m-1}\right)

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt+2^{m}{n-1\brack m+1}-(n-2){n-2\brack m}.

(6.10)

Replacing
t
with
t-1
in (
1.5
) and extracting the coefficient of
t^{m}
, we find

\displaystyle(n-1)!\cdot[t^{m}]i(T_{k,n},t-1)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\sum_{l=0}^{j}\frac{(k-1)!}{j!}\binom{n-k-1+j}{j}{j\brack l}{n-k\brack m-l}

(6.11)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\sum_{l=0}^{j}\frac{(k-1)!}{j!}\frac{n-k-1+j}{n-k-1}\binom{n-k-2+j}{j}{j\brack l}{n-k\brack m-l}.

Since
n-k-1+j\leq n-2
and
\frac{n-k-1+j}{n-k-1}\leq\frac{n-2}{n-k-1}\leq 2
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{n-k-1+j}{n-k-1}{n-k\brack m-l}

\displaystyle=\frac{n-k-1+j}{n-k-1}\left((n-k-1){n-k-1\brack m-l}+{n-k-1\brack m-l-1}\right)

\displaystyle\leq(n-2){n-k-1\brack m-l}+2{n-k-1\brack m-l-1}.

Then we conclude that

\displaystyle(n-1)!\cdot[t^{m}]i(T_{k,n},t-1)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\sum_{l=0}^{j}\frac{(k-1)!}{j!}\binom{n-k-2+j}{j}\left((n-2){n-k-1\brack m-l}+2{n-k-1\brack m-l-1}\right){j\brack l}.

(6.12)

Similarly, by (
1.5
),

\displaystyle(n-1)!\cdot[t^{m}]i(T_{k,n},t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\sum_{l=0}^{j}\frac{(k-1)!}{j!}\binom{n-k-1+j}{j}{j+1\brack l+1}{n-k+1\brack m-l+1}

(6.13)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\sum_{l=0}^{j}\frac{(k-1)!}{j!}\frac{n-k-1+j}{n-k-1}\binom{n-k-2+j}{j}{j+1\brack l+1}{n-k+1\brack m-l+1}.

Since
\frac{n-k-1+j}{n-k-1}(n-k)=(n-2)+(j-k+2+\frac{j}{n-k-1})
and
\frac{n-k-1+j}{n-k-1}\leq 2
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{n-k-1+j}{n-k-1}{n-k+1\brack m-l+1}

\displaystyle=\frac{n-k-1+j}{n-k-1}\left((n-k){n-k\brack m-l+1}+{n-k\brack m-l}\right)

\displaystyle\leq(n-2){n-k\brack m-l+1}+\left(j-k+2+\frac{j}{n-k-1}\right){n-k\brack m-l+1}+2{n-k\brack m-l}.

Therefore, we find

\displaystyle(n-1)!\cdot[t^{m}]i(T_{k,n},t)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\sum_{l=0}^{j}\frac{(k-1)!}{j!}\binom{n-k-2+j}{j}\left((n-2){n-k\brack m-l+1}+2{n-k\brack m-l}\right){j+1\brack l+1}+h(n,m),

(6.14)

where

h(n,m)=\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\sum_{l=0}^{j}\frac{(k-1)!}{j!}\binom{n-k-2+j}{j}\left(j-k+2+\frac{j}{n-k-1}\right){n-k\brack m-l+1}{j+1\brack l+1}.

Consequently, by (
6.9
), (
6.11
) and (
6.13
), we have

\displaystyle[t^{m}](i(U_{2,n},t)-i(T_{k,n},t-1)-i(T_{k,n},t))

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(n-1)!}\left(2^{m}{n\brack m+1}-n{n-1\brack m}\right)

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt-\frac{1}{(n-1)!}\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\sum_{l=0}^{j}\frac{(k-1)!}{j!}\binom{n-k-1+j}{j}\left({j\brack l}{n-k\brack m-l}+{j+1\brack l+1}{n-k+1\brack m-l+1}\right).

Denote by

\displaystyle f(n,m)

\displaystyle=2^{m}{n\brack m+1}-n{n-1\brack m}

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt-\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\sum_{l=0}^{j}\frac{(k-1)!}{j!}\binom{n-k-1+j}{j}\left({j\brack l}{n-k\brack m-l}+{j+1\brack l+1}{n-k+1\brack m-l+1}\right).

We aim to show that
f(n,m)\geq 0
for
n\geq 4
and
m\geq 0
by induction on
n
.

It is easy to check that
f(4,m)\geq 0
for any
m\geq 0
. Moreover, since

\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\binom{n-k-1+j}{j}=\frac{k}{n-k}{n-1\choose k},

we have

\displaystyle f(n,0)

\displaystyle=(n-1)!-(k-1)!(n-k)!-(n-k)!(k-1)!\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}\binom{n-k-1+j}{j}

\displaystyle=(n-1)!-(k-1)!(n-k)!-(n-k)!(k-1)!\left(\frac{k}{n-k}{n-1\choose k}-1\right)

\displaystyle=0.

Assume that
f(n-1,m)\geq 0
for
n\geq 5
and
m\geq 0
. For
m\geq 1
, by (
6.10
), (
6.12
) and (
6.14
), we derive that

\displaystyle f(n,m)

\displaystyle\geq(n-2)\left(2^{m}{n-1\brack m+1}-(n-1){n-2\brack m}\right)+2\left(2^{m-1}{n-1\brack m}-(n-1){n-2\brack m-1}\right)

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt-\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\sum_{l=0}^{j}\frac{(k-1)!}{j!}\binom{n-k-2+j}{j}\left((n-2){n-k-1\brack m-l}+2{n-k-1\brack m-l-1}\right){j\brack l}

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt-\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\sum_{l=0}^{j}\frac{(k-1)!}{j!}\binom{n-k-2+j}{j}\left((n-2){n-k\brack m-l+1}+2{n-k\brack m-l}\right){j+1\brack l+1}

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt+2^{m}{n-1\brack m+1}-(n-2){n-2\brack m}-h(n,m)

\displaystyle=(n-2)f(n-1,m)+2f(n-1,m-1)+2^{m}{n-1\brack m+1}-(n-2){n-2\brack m}-h(n,m)

\displaystyle\geq 2^{m}{n-1\brack m+1}-(n-2){n-2\brack m}-h(n,m).

To complete the proof, let

g(n,m)=2^{m}{n-1\brack m+1}-(n-2){n-2\brack m}-h(n,m).

We aim to prove that
g(n,m)\geq 0
for any
n\geq 4
and
m\geq 0
by induction on
n
.

It’s easy to check
g(4,m)=0
for any
m\geq 0
and
g(n,-1)=0
. Assume that
g(n-1,m)\geq 0
for
n\geq 5
and
m\geq 0
. Then we have

\displaystyle g(n,m)

\displaystyle=2^{m}{n-1\brack m+1}-(n-2){n-2\brack m}

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt-\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\sum_{l=0}^{j}\frac{(k-1)!}{j!}\left(j-k+2+\frac{j}{n-k-1}\right)\binom{n-k-2+j}{j}{j+1\brack l+1}{n-k\brack m-l+1}

\displaystyle=2^{m}\left((n-2){n-2\brack m+1}+{n-2\brack m}\right)-(n-2)\left((n-3){n-3\brack m}+{n-3\brack m-1}\right)

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt-\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\sum_{l=0}^{j}\frac{(k-1)!}{j!}\left(j-k+2+\frac{j}{n-k-1}\right)

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\times\frac{n-k-2+j}{n-k-2}\binom{n-k-3+j}{j}\left((n-k-1){n-k-1\brack m-l+1}+{n-k-1\brack m-l}\right){j+1\brack l+1}

\displaystyle\geq(n-2)\left(2^{m}{n-2\brack m+1}-(n-3){n-3\brack m}\right)+2\left(2^{m-1}{n-2\brack m}-(n-3){n-3\brack m-1}\right)

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt-\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\sum_{l=0}^{j}\frac{(k-1)!}{j!}\left(j-k+2+\frac{j}{n-k-2}\right)

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\times\binom{n-k-3+j}{j}\left((n-2){n-k-1\brack m-l+1}+2{n-k-1\brack m-l}\right){j+1\brack l+1}

\displaystyle\geq(n-2)g(n-1,m)+2g(n-1,m-1)

\displaystyle\geq 0,

where the third step follows from the relations

\left(j-k+2+\frac{j}{n-k-1}\right)\frac{n-k-2+j}{n-k-2}(n-k-1)\leq\left(j-k+2+\frac{j}{n-k-2}\right)(n-2)

and

\left(j-k+2+\frac{j}{n-k-1}\right)\frac{n-k-2+j}{n-k-2}\leq 2\left(j-k+2+\frac{j}{n-k-2}\right).

This completes the proof.
