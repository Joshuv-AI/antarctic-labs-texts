Skew circuits and circumference in a binary matroid
Sean McGuinness

Abstract
The circumference of a matroid is defined to be the size of the largest circuit. For disjoint sets X X and Y Y in a matroid M M having ground set E E we define κ M ​ ( X , Y ) = min X ⊆ A ⊆ E − Y ⁡ r ⁡ ( A ) + r ⁡ ( E − A ) − r ⁡ ( M ) . \kappa_{M}(X,Y)=\min_{X\subseteq A\subseteq E-Y}r(A)+r(E-A)-r(M). 1
Introduction

Many properties of cycles in graphs have their counterparts for circuits in matroids. Our starting point for investigation, is understanding in what way the property of intersection of long cycles in graphs can be extended to matroids. For graphs, connectivity plays a significant role in forcing long cycles to intersect and of particular interest here is an old conjecture of Smith (see
[
3
]
):

1.1 Conjecture
( Smith, 1984 )

If
C
and
D
are longest cycles in a
k
-connected graph, where
k\geq 2,
then
C
and
D
meet in at least
k
vertices.

The conjecture remains unsolved, and perhaps the best known result related to it can be found in
[
4
]
, where it is shown that two longest cycles in a
k
-connected
graph must meet in at least
ck^{\frac{3}{5}}
vertices (
c\approx 0.2615
). The key tool here is Menger’s theorem (see
[
3
]
) which guarantees the existence of many vertex-disjoint paths between two edge-disjoint cycles in a highly connected graph.
In
[
8
]
, the properties of longest cycles sharing
3,4,
or
5
vertices are studied and Smith’s conjecture is verified for
k\leq 6.

The above conjecture raises some natural questions for matroids. For a matroid
M
having at least one circuit, we define the
circumference
, denoted
c(M)
to be the size of a largest circuit in
M
. A
k
-connected matroid is defined in the following way (see
[
12
]
). Let
M
be a matroid having rank function
r.

For sets
X
and
Y
, let
\sqcap_{M}(X,Y)=r(X)+r(Y)-r(X\cup Y).

The
connectivity function
for
M
is the function
\lambda_{M}:2^{E(M)}\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}
where
\lambda_{M}(A)=r(A)+r(E(M)-A)-r(M);
that is,
\lambda_{M}(A)=\sqcap_{M}(A,E(M)-A).

For disjoint subsets
X
and
Y
we define
\kappa_{M}(X,Y)=\min_{X\subseteq A\subseteq E(M)-Y}\lambda_{M}(A)
which we refer to as the
linkage
between
X
and
Y
. We say that
X
and
Y
are
\mathbf{k}
-linked
if
\kappa_{M}(X,Y)\geq k.
For a subset
A\subset E(M),
we say that
A
(or the partition
(A,E(M)-A)
) is
\mathbf{k}
-separating
if

\lambda_{M}(A)<k
. The matroid
M
is said to be
\mathbf{k}
-connected
if it has no
(k-1)
-separating partition
(A,E(M)-A)
where
\min\{|A|,|E(M)-A|\}\geq k-1.
It follows from the definition of a
k
-connected matroid, that if
|E(M)|\geq 2k-2,
then all circuits must have size at least
k.
When
|E(M)|<2k-2,

it is possible that
M
can have small circuits and as such, the linkage between two disjoint circuits can be less than
k
.

Two sets
X
and
Y
in matroid are said to be
skew
if
r(X)+r(Y)=r(X\cup Y).

One interpretation of Smith’s conjecture for a graphic matroid
M
is that it asserts that if
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
are two
k
-linked disjoint circuits of size
c(M)
, then
\sqcap_{M}(C_{1},C_{2})\geq k-1.
Conversely, if
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
are disjoint
k
-linked circuits for which
\sqcap_{M}(C_{1},C_{2})<k-1,
then
|C_{1}|+|C_{2}|<2c(M).
Based on this, one might guess that for matroids in general if
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
are
k
-linked skew circuits in a matroid
M
, then
|C_{1}|+|C_{2}|
should be smaller than
2c(M)
by an amount depending on
k.
This has been verified in certain special cases.
In
[
9
]
, it was shown that if
M
is a
k
-connected regular matroid with circumference at least
k
and
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
are skew circuits, then
|C_{1}|+|C_{2}|\leq 2(c(M)-k+1).

In
[
10
]
, the authors showed that if
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
are skew circuits in a connected matroid, then
|C_{1}|+|C_{2}|\leq 2c(M)-2.
The case of large circuits in cographic matroids is studied in
[
13
]
where the intersection of largest bonds in
k
-connected graphs is studied. Generally, finding similar bounds for matroids of higher connectivity is hard. We conjecture that a bound of the form
2c(M)-k
exists when the linkage between two skew circuits is sufficiently large. Specifically, we conjecture:

1.2 Conjecture

Let
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
be skew circuits in a matroid
M
having circumference
c.
Then for all nonnegative integers
k
there exists an integer
\alpha(k)
, depending only on
k
, such that if
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
are
\alpha(k)
-linked, then

|C_{1}|+|C_{2}|\leq 2c-k.

The main result of this paper is that we verify this conjecture for binary matroids.

1.3 Theorem

Let
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
be skew circuits in a binary matroid
M.
Then for all nonnegative integers
k
there exists an integer
\alpha(k)
, depending only on
k
, such that if
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
are
\alpha(k)
-linked, then

|C_{1}|+|C_{2}|\leq 2c(M)-k.

We note that it suffices to prove the theorem for any infinite sequence of positive integers, in particular when
k
is an even integer. The existence of the constant
\alpha(k)
follows implicitly from the proof of the above theorem and we give no explicit value other than to say that it is large.

Notation

For the most part, we shall follow the definitions and notation for matroids given in
[
12
]
with the exception of some notation for sets.
For finite sets
X
and
Y
of a universal set
U
we write
X\parallel Y
if
X\cap Y=\mathchar 31
and we write
X\mathrel{\vbox{\halign{#\cr$\subset$\cr\kern-0.5pt\cr$\supset$ \cr}}}Y
(resp.
X\mathrel{\vbox{\halign{#\cr$\subseteq$\cr\kern-0.5pt\cr$\supseteq$ \cr}}}Y
) if either
X\subset Y
or
Y\subset X
(resp.
X\subseteq Y
or
Y\subseteq X
).
We write
X\bowtie_{U}Y
if
U-X\parallel U-Y,
where we drop the index
U
when it is implicit. We write
X\ \mathrel{\bowtie\!\!\!\!\parallel}\ \ Y
if either
X\parallel Y
or
X\bowtie Y.

For a set
X
and a nonnegative integer

, we let
{\binom{X}{k}}
denote the set of all

-subsets of

For finite nonempty subsets of integers

, we write

For a set

2
An overview of the proof

If for some
i\in\{1,2\}
,
|C_{i}|\leq c(M)-k,
then the conclusion of Theorem
1
is immediate. As such, one can assume that for
i=1,2,

|C_{i}|>c(M)-k.

The proof of Theorem
1
boils down to finding a certain pair of circuits of which there are two possible scenarios.

We define a
cycle
of
M
to be a disjoint union of circuits, including the empty set.
We let
{\cal C}_{M}
denote the set of all cycles in
M
and for a subset
X\subseteq E(M)
, we let
{\cal C}_{M}(X)
be the set of cycles contained in
X.
For cycles
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
, we define addition
C_{1}+C_{2}:=C_{1}\triangle C_{2}
(where
\triangle
denotes the symmetric difference operation). Since the matroids considered in this paper are binary,
{\cal C}_{M}
is seen to be closed under this addition.

Suppose
M
,
C_{1},

C_{2}
, and
k
are as in the statement of Theorem
1
. To prove this theorem, it will suffice to show that at least one of two things must occur:

S1
)

There is a cycle
C
where
C+C_{1}
and
C+C_{2}
are circuits and

|C-(C_{1}\cup C_{2})|\geq\frac{k}{2}
.

S2
)

There are disjoint cycles
C_{1}^{\prime}
and
C_{2}^{\prime}
where
C_{1}+C_{2}+C_{i}^{\prime},\ i=1,2
are circuits and
|(C_{1}\cup C_{2})+(C_{1}^{\prime}\cup C_{2}^{\prime})|\geq k.

If S1) occurs, then we have

2c(M)\geq|C+C_{1}|+|C+C_{2}|=|C_{1}|+|C_{2}|+2|C-(C_{1}\cup C_{2})||\geq|C_{1}|+|C_{2}|+k.

If S2) occurs, then we have

2c(M)\geq\sum_{i=1}^{2}|C_{1}+C_{2}+C_{i}^{\prime}|=|C_{1}|+|C_{2}|+|(C_{1}\cup C_{2})+(C_{1}^{\prime}\cup C_{2}^{\prime})|\geq|C_{1}|+|C_{2}|+k.

To find such cycles, we first use a version of Tutte’s Linking Lemma to reduce the problem to a minor
N
where
C_{i},\ i=1,2
are skew circuits in
N
and
C_{1}\cup C_{2}
spans
N.
Furthermore, the matroid
N
will also have the property that
\kappa_{N}(C_{1},C_{2})=|E(N)-(C_{1}\cup C_{2})|.
It will suffice to prove the theorem with
N
in place of
M.
Assuming
X=E(N)-(C_{1}\cup C_{2})=\{x_{1},\dots,x_{t}\}
, for each
i\in[t]
we pick a circuit
D_{i}
in
(C_{1}\cup C_{2})+x_{i}.
The cycles we are looking for to satisfy S1) or S2) will be of the form
D_{I}
where
I\subset[t]
and
D_{I}=\sum_{i\in I}D_{i}.

To begin with, suppose there exist constants
\alpha_{i}(k),\ i=1,2
depending only on
k
with the property that if
I\subset[t]
has size at least
\alpha_{i},
then there is a subset
J\in{\binom{I}{k}}
for which
D_{J}+C_{i}
is a circuit. Then one can use Ramsey’s theorem for hypergraphs to show that there is a constant
\alpha(k)
depending only on
k
such that if
I\subset[t]
has size at least
\alpha
, then for some
J\in{\binom{I}{k}}
both
D_{J}+C_{1}
and
D_{J}+C_{2}
are circuits. In this case, one can find a circuit
C
as described in S1).
Thus we assume that no such constants
\alpha_{i},\ i=1,2
exist and in particular we may assume that for an integer
p
chosen as large as we want, for all

I\in{\binom{[p]}{k}},

D_{I}+C_{1}
is not a circuit. For the remainder of the proof, we shall show that there is a pair of cycles
C_{i}^{\prime},\ i=1,2
as described in S2).
To do this, we use a theorem of Balogh and Bollobás
[
2
]
regarding unavoidable configurations in
0,1
-matrices. This theorem asserts that for any integer
\ell\geq 1,
if a simple
0,1
-matrix has a large enough number of rows, then one can find an induced submatrix which, after permuting its rows and columns, is either
I_{\ell}
,
I_{\ell}^{c}
, or
T_{\ell}
; that is, the
\ell\times\ell
identity matrix, its complement, or the
\ell\times\ell
lower-triangular matrix with
1
’s below or on the diagonal. We apply this theorem to the matrices
A_{i},\ i=1,2
where
A_{i}
is the
0,1
-matrix whose rows correspond to the binary indicator vectors for the subsets
D_{j}\cap C_{i},\ j\in[p].

This will enable us to show that provided
p
is large enough, one can construct pairwise disjoint sets
U_{i}\subset[p],\ i\in[4]
such that for
i=1,2,3,

D_{U_{i}}\cap C_{1}\subset D_{U_{i+1}}\cap C_{1}
and the sets
D_{U_{i}}\cap C_{2},\ i\in[4]
are pairwise disjoint. Furthermore, for all
1\leq i<j\leq 4,\ |U_{i}\cup U_{j}|\geq k.
It can now be shown that
D_{U_{1}\cup U_{2}}
and
D_{U_{3}\cup U_{4}}
are cycles satisfying S2).

3
Exploiting linkage

We shall make use of the following result of Tutte
[
14
]
known as
Tutte’s linking lemma
, which, in the case of graphic matroids, implies Menger’s theorem. We shall use a stronger version of this theorem found in
[
6
]
.

3.1 Lemma

Let
M
be a matroid and let
X,Y
be disjoint subsets of elements. Then there is a minor
N
of
M
such that,
E(N)=X\cup Y
,
\sqcap_{N}(X,Y)=\kappa_{N}(X,Y)=\kappa_{M}(X,Y)
, and
N\big|X=M\big|X,\ N\big|Y=M\big|Y
.

For our purposes, we shall need a stronger version of the above lemma which can be found in
[
7
]
:

3.2 Lemma

Let
M
be a matroid and let
m\geq 1
be an integer. Suppose that for a subset
X\subset E(M)
and subsets
Y_{i}\subset E(M),\ i\in[m],
we have
Y_{1}\subseteq Y_{2}\subseteq\cdots\subseteq Y_{m}\subseteq E(M)-X.
Then
M
has a minor
N
with ground set
X\cup Y_{m}
such that
for all
i\in[m]
,
\kappa_{N}(X,Y_{i})=\kappa_{M}(X,Y_{i})
, and
N\big|X=M\big|X
and
N\big|Y_{1}=M\big|Y_{1}.

The first step in the proof of Theorem
1
is to reduce the proof to a matroid
N
where
C_{1}\cup C_{2}
spans
N
and
\kappa_{N}(C_{1},C_{2})=\kappa_{M}(C_{1},C_{2}).

This can be done using the following lemma.

3.3 Lemma

Let
X
and
Y
be disjoint sets of a matroid
M.
Then there exists a minor
N
such that
X\cup Y\subseteq E(N),

X\cup Y
spans
N
,
\sqcap_{N}(X,Y)=\sqcap_{M}(X,Y)
,
\kappa_{N}(X,Y)=\kappa_{M}(X,Y)

and
N\big|X=M\big|X,

N\big|Y=M\big|Y.

Proof.

By induction on
|E(M)-X-Y|.
The lemma is clearly true if
X\cup Y
spans
M
(since taking
N=M
will suffice). Thus we may assume that
X\cup Y
does not span
M
and that the lemma is true for all matroids
M^{\prime}
where
|E(M^{\prime})-X-Y|<|E(M)-X-Y|.
Let
e\in E(M)-\mathrm{cl}_{M}(X\cup Y)
and let
Y_{1}=Y
and
Y_{2}=E(M)-X-e.
By Lemma
3
, there exists a minor
M_{1}
of
M
with ground set
X\cup Y_{2}=E(M)-e
such that

\kappa_{M_{1}}(X,Y)=\kappa_{M}(X,Y).
We note either
M_{1}=M/e
or
M_{1}=M\backslash e.

Since
e\not\in\mathrm{cl}_{M}(X\cup Y),
we have
\sqcap_{M_{1}}(X,Y)=\sqcap_{M}(X,Y)
,
M_{1}\big|X=M\big|X,

M_{1}\big|Y=M\big|Y
. By induction, there exists a minor
N
of
M_{1}
containing
X\cup Y
such that
X\cup Y
spans
N
and
\kappa_{N}(X,Y)=\kappa_{M_{1}}(X,Y)=\kappa_{M}(X,Y),

\sqcap_{N}(X,Y)=\sqcap_{M_{1}}(X,Y)=\sqcap_{M}(X,Y).
Moreover,
N\big|X=M_{1}\big|X=M\big|X
and
N\big|Y=M_{1}\big|Y=M\big|Y.

∎

3.1
Reducing the problem to a minor
N

The advantage of the previous lemmas is that they allow us to reduce the proof of Theorem
1
to a minor
N
of
M
for which

\kappa_{N}(C_{1},C_{2})=\kappa_{M}(C_{1},C_{2})
and
\sqcap_{N}(C_{1},C_{2})=\sqcap_{M}(C_{1},C_{2})=0.

For the remainder of this paper, we shall let
C_{i}=\{e_{i1},\dots e_{in_{i}}\},\ i=1,2
be two disjoint circuits in a binary matroid
M
where
\sqcap(C_{1},C_{2})=0.
By Lemma
3
, there is a minor
M^{\prime}
of
M
containing
C_{1}\cup C_{2}
where
C_{1}\cup C_{2}
spans
M^{\prime}
,
\kappa_{M^{\prime}}(C_{1},C_{2})=\kappa_{M}(C_{1},C_{2}),

\sqcap_{M^{\prime}}(C_{1},C_{2})=\sqcap_{M}(C_{1},C_{2})=0,
and
M^{\prime}\big|C_{1}=M\big|C_{1},\ M^{\prime}\big|C_{2}=M\big|C_{2}.

We may assume that for
i=1,2,

\mathrm{cl}_{M^{\prime}}(C_{i})=C_{i}.

To see this, it suffices to show for
e\in\mathrm{cl}_{M^{\prime}}(C_{1})-C_{1}
that
\kappa_{M^{\prime}\backslash e}(C_{1},C_{2})=\kappa_{M^{\prime}}(C_{1},C_{2}).

Clearly
\kappa_{M^{\prime}\backslash e}(C_{1},C_{2})\leq\kappa_{M^{\prime}}(C_{1},C_{2})
, noting that
r(M^{\prime}\backslash e)=r(M^{\prime}).
Let
A\cup B
be a partition of
E(M^{\prime}\backslash e)
where
C_{1}\subset A
and
C_{2}\subset B.

Let
A^{\prime}=A+e
and
B^{\prime}=B.
Then
A^{\prime}\cup B^{\prime}
is a partition of
E(M)
where
C_{1}\subset A^{\prime}
and
C_{2}\subset B^{\prime}
and
r(A^{\prime})+r(B^{\prime})=r(A)+r(B).
Thus
\kappa_{M^{\prime}}(C_{1},C_{2})\leq\kappa_{M^{\prime}\backslash e}(C_{1},C_{2})
and hence equality must hold.

By Lemma
3
, there exists a minor
N^{\prime}=M^{\prime}/X\backslash Y
of
M^{\prime}
such that
E(N^{\prime})=C_{1}\cup C_{2}
, and
\sqcap_{N^{\prime}}(C_{1},C_{2})=0
and
\kappa_{N^{\prime}}(C_{1},C_{2})=\kappa_{M^{\prime}}(C_{1},C_{2})
and
N^{\prime}\big|C_{i}=M^{\prime}\big|C_{i},\ i=1,2.
Here we may assume that
X
is independent since if contracting some elements of
X
results in a loop
e\in X
, then deleting or contracting
e
yields the same matroid, in which case one could assume that
e\in Y.

Let
N=M^{\prime}\backslash Y=M^{\prime}\big|C_{1}\cup C_{2}\cup X
and let
\kappa_{N}(C_{1},C_{2})=t.

3.4 Observation

For
i=1,2
,
C_{i}
is the only circuit of
N
in
C_{i}\cup X
and for all subsets
X^{\prime}\subseteq X,

r_{N}(C_{i}\cup X^{\prime})=r_{N}(C_{i})+|X^{\prime}|.

Proof.

Suppose to the contrary that for some
i
,
C_{i}\cup X
contains a circuit
C
where
C\neq C_{i}.

Then
C^{\prime}=C-X
is a cycle in
N^{\prime}=N/X
where
C^{\prime}\subset C_{i}.
However, this contradicts the assumption that
N^{\prime}\big|C_{i}=M\big|C_{i}.

It now follows that for all
i\in\{1,2\}
and for all subsets
X^{\prime}\subseteq X,

r_{N}(C_{i}\cup X^{\prime})=|C_{i}|-1+|X^{\prime}|=r_{N}(C_{i})+|X^{\prime}|.

∎

3.5 Observation

t=\kappa_{N}(C_{1},C_{2})=|X|.

Proof.

Let
A\subset E(N)
where
C_{1}\subseteq A\subseteq E(N)-C_{2}.
Let
X_{1}=A\cap X
and let
X_{2}=X-A.
Then

\displaystyle\lambda_{N}(A)

\displaystyle=r_{N}(A)+r_{N}(E(N)-A)-r(N)

\displaystyle=r_{N}(C_{1})+|X_{1}|+r_{N}(C_{2})+|X_{2}|-r(N)=|X|.

The second equality follows from Observation
3.1
and the fact that
C_{i},\ i=1,2
are skew circuits for which
C_{1}\cup C_{2}
spans
N.

Thus it follows that
t=\kappa_{N}(C_{1},C_{2})=|X|.

∎

For the remainder of this paper, we let
X=\{x_{1},\dots,x_{t}\}
and let
N
be as described above. To prove Theorem
1
, it will suffice to prove it for the matroid
N.

For all subsets
A\subseteq E(N)
and for all
j\in\{1,2\},
we shall let
A^{j}
denote the set
A\cap C_{j}.

3.2
The circuits
D_{i}

Given that
C_{1}\cup C_{2}
spans
N
, it follows that for all
i\in[t]
there is a circuit in
N
which contains
x_{i}
and which is contained in
(C_{1}\cup C_{2})+x_{i}.
We let
D_{i}
be one such circuit.
For all subsets
\mathchar 31\neq I\subseteq[t],
let
D_{I}=\sum_{i\in I}D_{j}
. It is seen that
D_{I}
is a cycle in
{\cal C}_{N}-{\cal C}_{N}(C_{1}\cup C_{2}).
Since for
j=1,2,

C_{j}
is the only circuit in
C_{j}\cup X
(by Observation
3.1
), it follows that for
j=1,2,

C_{j}\not\subseteq D_{I}
. For if for
C_{j}\subseteq D_{I}
, then
D_{I}+C_{j}
is a cycle where
X\subset D_{I}+C_{j}\subseteq C_{3-j}\cup X
. It would then follow by Observation
3.1
that
D_{I}+C_{j}=C_{3-j},
which is impossible.
Thus we have the following:

3.6 Observation
For
\mathchar 31\neq I\subseteq[t],
and for all
j\in\{1,2\},

\mathchar 31\neq D_{I}^{j}\subset C_{j}.
Furthermore, no non-empty cycle of
{\cal C}_{N}(C_{1}\cup C_{2})=\{\mathchar 31,C_{1},C_{2},C_{1}\cup C_{2}\}
is contained in
D_{I}+C_{j}.

Note that if
I
and
I^{\prime}
are distinct, nonempty subsets of
[t]
, then for
j=1,2,

D_{I}^{j}\neq D_{I^{\prime}}^{j};
for if
D_{I}^{j}=D_{I^{\prime}}^{j},
then we would have for
I^{\prime\prime}=I\triangle I^{\prime},

D_{I^{\prime\prime}}^{j}=\mathchar 31,
contradicting the previous observation.

3.3
When
D_{I}+C_{j}
is not a circuit

Suppose
I\subseteq[t]
and
j\in\{1,2\}.
If for some
j\in[2],

D_{I}+C_{j}
is not a circuit, then it breaks into two cycles and we will show that there is a specific structure in this case.

3.7 Lemma

Let
I\subseteq[t]
and let
j\in\{1,2\}.
If
D_{I}+C_{j}
is not a circuit, then there is a partition
I=I_{1}\dot{\cup}I_{2}
such that
D_{I_{1}}^{j}\mathrel{\vbox{\halign{#\cr$\subset$\cr\kern-0.5pt\cr$\supset$ \cr}}}D_{I_{2}}^{j}
and
D_{I_{1}}^{3-j}\ \mathrel{\bowtie\!\!\!\!\parallel}\ \ D_{I_{2}}^{3-j}
. Furthermore, if
D_{I}+C_{1}+C_{2}
is not a circuit, then there is a partition
I=I_{1}\dot{\cup}I_{2}
such that for
i=1,2,

D_{I_{1}}^{i}\mathrel{\vbox{\halign{#\cr$\subset$\cr\kern-0.5pt\cr$\supset$ \cr}}}D_{I_{2}}^{i}.

Proof.

It suffices to prove the first statement for
j=1.
Suppose that
D_{I}+C_{1}
is not a circuit.
By Observation
3.2
,
no non-empty cycle of
{\cal C}_{N}(C_{1}\cup C_{2})=\{\mathchar 31,C_{1},C_{2},C_{1}\cup C_{2}\}
is contained in
D_{I}+C_{1}.
Since
D_{I}+C_{1}
is not a circuit, there are disjoint cycles
G_{i}\in{\cal C}_{N}-{\cal C}_{N}(C_{1}\cup C_{2}),\ i=1,2
for which

D_{I}+C_{1}=G_{1}\dot{\cup}G_{2}.
Then there is a partition
I_{1}\dot{\cup}I_{2}=I
of
I
such that for
i=1,2,

I_{i}=\{j\in I\ \big|\ x_{j}\in G_{i}\cap X\}.

Since
D_{I_{1}}\cap X=G_{1}\cap X,
it follows that for some
H\in{\cal C}_{N}(C_{1}\cup C_{2}),

D_{I_{1}}=G_{1}+H
and

D_{I_{2}}=G_{2}+H+C_{1}.
It can be easily checked that for all
H\in{\cal C}(C_{1}\cup C_{2}),

D_{I_{1}}^{1}\mathrel{\vbox{\halign{#\cr$\subset$\cr\kern-0.5pt\cr$\supset$ \cr}}}D_{I_{2}}^{1}
and
D_{I_{1}}^{2}\ \mathrel{\bowtie\!\!\!\!\parallel}\ \ D_{I_{2}}^{2}
. For example, if
H=\mathchar 31,
then
D_{I_{1}}=G_{1},

D_{I_{2}}=G_{2}+C_{1}
and
D_{I_{1}}^{1}\subset D_{I_{2}}^{2}
and
D_{I_{1}}^{1}\parallel D_{I_{2}}^{2}.

To prove the second statement, suppose
D_{I}+C_{1}+C_{2}
is not a circuit. Let
i\in I
and replace
D_{i}
with
D_{i}^{\prime}=D_{i}+C_{1}.
Let
D_{I}^{\prime}=D_{i}^{\prime}+D_{I-i}.
Then
D_{I}^{\prime}+C_{2}
is not a circuit and hence by the first part, there is a partition
I=I_{1}\dot{\cup}I_{2}
where
(D^{\prime}_{I_{1}})^{1}\ \mathrel{\bowtie\!\!\!\!\parallel}\ \ (D^{\prime}_{I_{2}})^{1}
and
(D^{\prime}_{I_{1}})^{2}\mathrel{\vbox{\halign{#\cr$\subset$\cr\kern-0.5pt\cr$\supset$ \cr}}}(D^{\prime}_{I_{2}})^{2}.
Here we assume
i\in I_{1}
and
D^{\prime}_{I_{1}}=D_{i}^{\prime}+D_{I_{1}-i}
and
D^{\prime}_{I_{2}}=D_{I_{2}}.

Given that
(D^{\prime}_{I_{1}})^{1}=D_{I_{1}}^{1}+C_{1},

(D^{\prime}_{I_{2}})^{1}=D_{I_{2}}^{1},
and
(D^{\prime}_{I_{k}})^{2}=D_{I_{k}}^{2},\ k=1,2
, it follows that
D_{I_{1}}^{1}\mathrel{\vbox{\halign{#\cr$\subset$\cr\kern-0.5pt\cr$\supset$ \cr}}}D_{I_{2}}^{1}
and
D_{I_{1}}^{2}\mathrel{\vbox{\halign{#\cr$\subset$\cr\kern-0.5pt\cr$\supset$ \cr}}}D_{I_{2}}^{2}.
This proves the second part.
∎

4
Ramsey’s Theorem and unavoidable configurations in matrices

A key component in the proof of Theorem
1
involves an application of
Ramsey’s theorem
in its form for hypergraphs (see
[
11
]
).
The
complete

\mathbf{r}
-uniform hypergraph
on a set of vertices
S
is the hypergraph whose edges are all the
r
-subsets of
S.
For
|S|=n,
we let
K_{n}^{r}
denote the complete
r
-hypergraph on
n
vertices. Ramsey’s theorem for hypergraphs can be stated as follows:

4.1 Theorem

Suppose
s_{1},\dots,s_{k}
are positive integers, all at least
r.
There is an integer
R^{r}(s_{1},\dots,s_{k})
such that if
n\geq R^{r}(s_{1},\dots,s_{k}),
then for any
k
-colouring of the edges of
K_{n}^{r}
with colours
1,\dots,k
, there exists
i\in[k]
and a complete
r
-uniform subhypergraph
K_{s_{i}}^{r}
on
s_{i}
vertices, all of whose edges have colour
i.

As an application of the above theorem, we shall show that a circuit
C
as described in S1) exists unless for some
j\in\{1,2\}
there are large subsets
I\subseteq[t]
(where
t=|X|
) such that for all
J\in{\binom{I}{k}}
,
D_{J}+C_{j}
is not a circuit. This follows from the next lemma.

4.2 Lemma

Suppose that there are constants
\alpha_{i}(k),\ i=1,2
, depending only on
k
, such that for all subsets
I\subseteq[t],
if
|I|\geq\alpha_{i}(k),
then there is a
k
-subset
J\subseteq I
such that
D_{J}+C_{i}
is a circuit.
Then there is a constant
\alpha(k)
such that for all subsets
I\subseteq[t]
where
|I|\geq\alpha(k),
there is a subset
J\subseteq I
such that all
J^{\prime}\in{\binom{J}{k}}
and for all
i\in[2],

D_{J}+C_{i}
is a circuit.

Proof.

Let
I\subseteq[t]
and let
I^{\prime}\subseteq I.
Suppose we colour the
k
-subsets of
I^{\prime}
blue or red in such a way that a
k
-subset
J
is coloured blue if
D_{J}+C_{1}
is a circuit; otherwise, it is coloured red.
By Theorem
4
, there is a constant
R^{k}(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{1})
such that if
|I^{\prime}|\geq R^{k}(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{1})
then for any
2
-colouring of the uniform
k
-hypergraph on
I^{\prime}
, there is an
\alpha_{1}
-subset
I^{\prime\prime}\subseteq I^{\prime}
whose associated
k
-hypergraph is monochromatic. By assumption,
I^{\prime\prime}
must contain at least one blue hyperedge, and hence each hyperedge of
I^{\prime\prime}
is blue.
Let
\alpha=R^{k}(R^{k}(\alpha_{2},\alpha_{2}),\alpha_{1})
and suppose that
|I|\geq\alpha.
Then either there exists an
R^{k}(\alpha_{2},\alpha_{2})
-subset
I^{\prime}\subseteq I
all of whose
k
-subsets are blue, or there exists an
\alpha_{1}
-subset all of whose
k
-subsets are coloured red. By our assumptions on
\alpha_{1}
, the latter is impossible and thus the former holds. Let
I^{\prime}\subseteq I
be an
R^{k}(\alpha_{2},\alpha_{2})
-subset all of whose
k
-subsets are blue. Now if we colour the
k
-subsets
J
of
I^{\prime}
green or yellow, depending on whether
D_{J}+C_{2}
is a circuit or not, it follows by Theorem
4
that there is an
\alpha_{2}
-subset of
I^{\prime\prime}\subseteq I^{\prime}
whose
k
-uniform hypergraph is monochromatic. By our assumptions on
\alpha_{2}
, all the
k
-subsets of
I^{\prime\prime}
must be green. Thus every
k
-subset
J
of
I^{\prime\prime}
is both blue and green and thus for
i=1,2,

D_{J}+C_{i}
is a circuit.
∎

By the above lemma, we may assume for the remainder that for an integer

, chosen as large as needed, for all
I\in{\binom{[p]}{k}}
,
D_{I}+C_{1}
is not a circuit. Consequently, for all

there is a partition

such that

where

are disjoint cycles such that for

D_{I_{i}}\cap X=G_{i}\cap X.
Importantly, we note that since

is the disjoint union of the cycles

and

, one can show (following the proof of Lemma

) that
D_{I_{1}}^{1}\mathrel{\vbox{\halign{#\cr$\subset$\cr\kern-0.5pt\cr$\supset$ \cr}}}D_{I_{2}}^{1}
and
D_{I_{1}}^{2}\ \mathrel{\bowtie\!\!\!\!\parallel}\ \ D_{I_{2}}^{2}
.

4.1
A theorem on unavoidable configurations in matrices

A matrix is

if it has no repeated rows. For matrices

For a positive integer

Let

For all positive integers

, let
\beta(i)=(2i)^{2^{i}}.
The above theorem will allow us to choose a large subset of the circuits of

\{D_{1},\dots,D_{p}\}
in a favorable way.

4.1.1
The matrices

,

, and

we define a

if and only if

We observe that the matrix

is simple. Let

be the

with

; that is,
A_{1}
(resp.

formed by the first

Suppose that

Since

that for some

By permuting the rows of

Now let

(A)

U_{4,\frac{j}{2}}<U_{3,j}<U_{2,j}<U_{1,\frac{j}{2}+1}

For example,

, the sets

are such that

U_{1,1}<U_{2,1}<U_{3,1}<U_{4,1}<U_{3,2}<U_{2,2}<U_{1,2}<U_{2,3}<U_{3,3}<U_{4,2}.

and for

it follows from Observation

We have that

7
Completing the proof of Theorem
1

We may assume that
k
is even. Recall that by Observations
6.1
and
6.1
,
B_{1}^{\prime}=T_{p}
and
B_{2}^{\prime}=I_{p}
. By Lemma
6.2
, there are pairwise disjoint subsets
U_{i}\subset[q],\ i\in[4]
such that for all
1\leq i<j\leq 4,

(U_{i},U_{j},H)
is an
U_{i}\cup U_{j}
-triple and
|U_{i}\cup U_{j}|\geq k.
Thus we have:

A)

For all
1\leq i<j\leq 4,

D_{U_{i}}^{1}\mathrel{\vbox{\halign{#\cr$\subset$\cr\kern-0.5pt\cr$\supset$ \cr}}}D_{U_{j}}^{1}
and

B)

for all
1\leq i<j\leq 4,

D_{U_{i}}^{2}\ \mathrel{\bowtie\!\!\!\!\parallel}\ \ D_{U_{j}}^{2}
.

It follows by (A) that the sets
D_{U_{i}}^{1},\ i\in[4]
are totally ordered by inclusion. Thus we may assume that for all
1\leq i<j\leq 4,

D_{U_{i}}^{1}\subset D_{U_{j}}^{1}.

Let
V_{1}=U_{1}\cup U_{2}
and let
V_{2}=U_{3}\cup U_{4}
. Then
D_{V_{1}}=D_{U_{1}}+D_{U_{2}}
and
D_{V_{2}}=D_{U_{3}}+D_{U_{4}}.
We have that
D_{V_{1}}^{1}\parallel D_{V_{2}}^{1}.
Furthermore, by B), it follows that
D_{V_{1}}^{2}\parallel D_{V_{2}}^{2}
and hence
D_{V_{1}}
and
D_{V_{2}}
are disjoint cycles. Suppose
D_{V_{1}}+C_{1}+C_{2}
is not a circuit. Then Lemma
3.3
implies that there is a partition
V_{1}=S_{1}\dot{\cup}S_{2}
such that for
i=1,2,

D_{S_{1}}^{i}\mathrel{\vbox{\halign{#\cr$\subset$\cr\kern-0.5pt\cr$\supset$ \cr}}}D_{S_{2}}^{i}.
However, given that
B_{2}^{\prime}=I_{q}
, it follows that
D_{S_{1}}^{2}\not\mathrel{\vbox{\halign{#\cr$\subset$\cr\kern-0.5pt\cr$\supset$ \cr}}}D_{S_{2}}^{2}.
Thus
D_{V_{1}}+C_{1}+C_{2}
is a circuit and the same applies to
D_{V_{2}}+C_{1}+C_{2}.

Since for
i=1,2
,
|V_{i}|\geq k,
we now see that the circuits
C_{i}^{\prime}=D_{V_{i}},\ i=1,2
satisfy S2) (with

This completes the proof.
