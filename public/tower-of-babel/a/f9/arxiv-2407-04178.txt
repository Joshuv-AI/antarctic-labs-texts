Monomial web basis for the SL(N) skein algebra of the twice punctured sphere Thanks: The first author was partially supported by 101107744–DefHyp. The second author was partially supported by the Simons Foundation grant 327929.
Tommaso Cremaschi, Daniel C. Douglas

Abstract
. We give a new proof of a slightly modified version of a result of Queffelec–Rose, by constructing a linear basis for the SL ⁡ ( n ) \mathrm{SL}(n) skein algebra of the twice punctured sphere for any non-zero complex number q q , excluding finitely many roots of unity of small order. In particular, the skein algebra is a commutative polynomial algebra in n − 1 n-1 generators, where each generator is represented by an explicit SL ⁡ ( n ) \mathrm{SL}(n) web, without crossings, on the surface. This includes the case q = 1 q=1 , where the skein algebra is identified with the coordinate ring of the SL ⁡ ( n ) \mathrm{SL}(n) character variety of the twice punctured sphere. The proof of both the spanning and linear independence properties of the basis depends on the so-called SL ⁡ ( n ) \mathrm{SL}(n) quantum trace map, due originally to Bonahon–Wong in the case n = 2 n=2 . Two consequences of our method are that the quantum trace map and the so-called splitting map embed the polynomial algebra into the Fock–Goncharov quantum higher Teichmüller space and the Lê–Sikora stated skein algebra, respectively, of the annulus. We end by discussing the relationship with Fock–Goncharov duality.

Constructing explicit linear bases of skein algebras is another difficult problem. For small values of

In more detail, such a blueprint is motivated by the concept from mirror symmetry of Fock–Goncharov duality
[
21
,
19
,
31

or, rather, Fock–Goncharov–Shen duality
[

30

whose elements are naturally indexed by a discrete sub-monoid

\mathfrak{L}
of the punctured surface
\mathfrak{S}
, and where

is the dimension of the character variety. Here, naturality means that this indexing behaves well under changing the triangulation, in particular transforming according to a tropical cluster mutation formula. Such canonical bases have strong positivity properties; see for example Section
4.4.6
, which relates to the physical point of view of Gaiotto–Moore–Neitzke
[
27

[
11
,
20

and
n=3

is that such bases are realized by planar webs that can be constructed by a local to global process, first constructing the webs locally in triangles, then gluing together these local webs across the triangulation to obtain the global basis web. This is the procedure followed in the proof of Theorem
1
.

The key to a skein theoretic realization of Fock–Goncharov duality is the
\mathrm{SL}(n)
quantum trace map
[
5
,
8
,
17
,
25
,
40
,
49
,
57
]

from the skein algebra
\mathcal{S}_{n}^{q}(\mathfrak{S})
to a quantum torus

. Manifesting the tropical geometric nature of Fock–Goncharov duality, the highest term of the quantum trace polynomial of a basis web should have exponents given by the positive tropical integer points of that web. This is true when

is
2
or

As was somewhat unexpected to the authors, the quantum trace map also plays an important role in our proof in establishing the spanning property of the annulus basis. This is because the inadmissible values of

in Theorem

include the, a priori, unknown roots of a family of polynomials. These roots are forced to be roots of unity of small order by the quantum trace analysis; see Section

1.1.
Webs

Consider an edge oriented graph with finitely many vertices, possibly with multiple edges between vertices and possibly with more than one component, that is properly embedded in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
such that, in addition:

(1)

the graph is
n
-valent, namely, there are
n
edges incident to each vertex;

(2)

each vertex is either a sink or source, namely, the orientations of the edges at the vertex go all in or all out, respectively;

(3)

the graph can contain loop components, namely, components without any vertices and which are homeomorphic to a circle;

(4)

the ends of the incident edges at each vertex are distinct and lie in a two-dimensional plane in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
;

(5)

the graph is equipped with a
framing
, meaning a continuous assignment of a unit normal vector at each point of the graph, in the following sense: at a nonvertex point, lying on some unique edge or loop component, this unit vector is in the plane perpendicular to the tangent of the edge at the point; and, at a vertex, this unit vector is perpendicular to the two-dimensional plane spanned by the incident edges;

(6)

this framing determines a
cyclic order
of the
half-edges
incident at each vertex, according to the right hand rule for sources and the left hand rule for sinks; moreover, at each vertex we choose, as part of the data, a
linear order
of these half-edges compatible with the cyclic order, which is equivalent to choosing a distinguished half-edge.

Such data is called a
based
\mathrm{SL}(n)
web
(or just
web
)
in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, denoted by
W
. If one forgets the linear order at each vertex, just remembering the cyclic order, we call this an
unbased web in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, denoted by
W^{\circ}
. Webs are considered up to
isotopy
through the family of webs. The
empty web
and
empty unbased web
, denoted by
\varnothing
, are the unique representatives of their respective isotopy classes.

Alternatively, one may think of a web as an oriented
n
-valent ribbon graph, obtained by gluing rectangle edges to disk vertices, and equipped with a distinguished half-edge at each vertex. Note here that there are two orientations being considered, the edge orientations and the orientation as a surface. In this way of thinking, the framing vector, normal to the tangent plane of the ribbon at each point, is determined by the surface orientation.

In figures, we represent webs by
diagrams
in the plane
\mathbb{R}^{2}\cong\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\{0\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
, which includes over and under crossing data. A diagram determines a web, up to isotopy, by equipping the diagram with the
blackboard framing
, namely the constant unit framing in the positive vertical direction, that is, the positive third coordinate of
\mathbb{R}^{3}
thought of as pointing out of the page toward the eye of the reader. Note that, by possibly introducing kinks, any web can be isotoped to have the blackboard framing, and so can be represented by a diagram. We indicate in diagrams the choice of the preferred half-edge at each vertex, determining the linear order, by placing a
cilia
just before the preferred edge in the cyclic order, again, counterclockwise at sources and clockwise at sinks. See, for example, Figure
3

\mathfrak{A}=\{(x,y,0)\in\mathbb{R}^{3};0<x^{2}+y^{2}<\infty\}
. Let
\mathfrak{I}=(0,1)\subset\mathbb{R}
be the
open unit interval
(or just
interval
). The
thickened annulus
is
\mathfrak{A}\times\mathfrak{I}\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
. A
web in the thickened annulus
is a web in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
that is contained in
\mathfrak{A}\times\mathfrak{I}
, considered up to isotopy staying within the thickened annulus. An
unbased web in the thickened annulus
is defined similarly. From here on, ‘web’ will always mean ‘web in the thickened annulus’, unless stated otherwise.

Remark 1.1
.

Essentially the same definition allows one to define based and unbased webs more generally in three-dimensional manifolds
\mathfrak{M}
(in place of
\mathfrak{A}\times\mathfrak{I}
). See, for example,
[
48
]
.

By a
planar isotopy
of a web, we mean an isotopy that can be achieved at the level of diagrams on the surface by an ambient isotopy of the surface. In particular, planar isotopies preserve the number of crossings of the diagram.

1.2.
Skeins

Equip the thickened annulus
\mathfrak{A}\times\mathfrak{I}\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
with the orientation inherited as an open sub-set of
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, the latter having its usual orientation.

The

(or just

in

by the relations shown in Figure

actually depends on

and not just

We refer to the relations (A), (B), (C), (D) in Figure

,

The

is the vector space obtained by quotienting the vector space of formal linear combinations of isotopy classes of unbased webs
W^{\circ}
by the relations shown in Figure

For clarity, we sometimes use the notation

The skein modules

and

have an algebra structure, the multiplication being defined by stacking diagrams, where multiplying left to right means stacking from top to bottom. In other words, the product
WW^{\prime}
is obtained by rescaling

into
W\subset\mathfrak{A}\times(1/2,1)
and rescaling

into
W^{\prime}\subset\mathfrak{A}\times(0,1/2)
followed by inserting both of these rescaled webs into
\mathfrak{A}\times\mathfrak{I}
. Similarly for unbased webs. We then refer to the
skein algebra

\mathcal{S}_{n}^{q}
and the
unbased skein algebra

\left(\mathcal{S}_{n}^{q}\right)^{\circ}
. Because of the topology of the annulus, we see that
\mathcal{S}_{n}^{q}
and
\left(\mathcal{S}_{n}^{q}\right)^{\circ}
are, in fact, commutative algebras, so
WW^{\prime}=W^{\prime}W
. The unit in these algebras is the class
\left[\varnothing\right]
of the empty web or empty unbased web.

(

Assume

In this paper, we prefer to work with the based skein algebra

Essentially the same definition allows one to define based and unbased skein modules more generally for oriented three-dimensional manifolds

2.
Main result

For
i=1,2,\dots,n-1
, define the
i
-th irreducible basis web

B_{i}\in\mathcal{S}_{n}^{q}
as in Figure
6
. Here, we have used a pictorial shorthand, where
i
(resp.

i
(resp.

The cross indicates the puncture not at infinity.

The

The following statement is the main result of the paper.

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
Q\subset\mathbb{C}^{\ast}
be the set of
q^{1/n}
such that
q=(q^{1/n})^{n}
is a
(2m)
-root of unity for some
m=2,3,\dots,n-1
and
q\neq\pm 1
. Then for all
q^{1/n}\in\mathbb{C}^{\ast}\setminus Q
, the skein algebra
\mathcal{S}_{n}^{q}
is a commutative polynomial algebra in
n-1
variables, with generators the
n-1
irreducible basis webs
B_{1},B_{2},\dots,B_{n-1}
. If
q^{1/n}\in Q
, then
B_{i}=0\in\mathcal{S}_{n}^{q}
for some
i=1,2,\dots,n-1
.

The proof of the last sentence of the theorem is not hard, and is given in Section
3.2
. The theorem is proved in Section
4.5.3
.

Since the skein algebra
\mathcal{S}_{n}^{q}
is commutative, there is an algebra map
\mathbb{C}[x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{n-1}]\to\mathcal{S}_{n}^{q}
from the commutative polynomial algebra in
n-1
variables, defined by sending
x_{i}
to
B_{i}
. To prove the theorem, we need to show that this map is injective and surjective, for
q^{1/n}\in\mathbb{C}^{\ast}\setminus Q
.
In other words, the monomials of the form
B_{1}^{k_{1}}B_{2}^{k_{2}}\cdots B_{n-1}^{k_{n-1}}\in\mathcal{S}_{n}^{q}
for
k_{i}\in\mathbb{N}
are linearly independent, and these monomials span, that is,
\mathcal{S}_{n}^{q}=\mathrm{sub}\left(B_{1},B_{2},\dots,B_{n-1}\right)
.

By Corollary
1.4
, we immediately obtain:

Corollary 2.2
.

Theorem
2.1
is also true for the unbased skein algebra
\left(\mathcal{S}_{n}^{q}\right)^{\circ}
. ∎

Combining Theorem
2.1
and Corollary
2.2
, we gather:

Corollary 2.3
.

For
q^{1/n}\in\mathbb{C}^{\ast}\setminus Q
, the skein algebras
\mathcal{S}_{n}^{q}
and
\left(\mathcal{S}_{n}^{q}\right)^{\circ}
are finitely generated, with an explicit finite presentation. Indeed, they are isomorphic to the commutative polynomial algebra
\mathbb{C}[x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{n-1}]
. ∎

3.3.
Countable generating set

For
m\in\mathbb{Z}
, define the
m
-th power knot

\gamma_{m}
in
\mathfrak{A}\times\mathfrak{I}
as follows. For
m>0
, we put
\gamma_{m}
to be the permutation knot
\gamma_{m}=\widetilde{\left(\begin{smallmatrix}2&3&\ldots&m&1\end{smallmatrix}\right)}_{+}
. For
m<0
,
\gamma_{m}
is the reverse permutation knot
\overline{\gamma_{|m|}}
. And
\gamma_{0}=\varnothing
. For example, the rightmost picture in Figure
7
is
\gamma_{4}
. We will also think of
\gamma_{m}
as an element of
\mathcal{S}_{n}^{q}
. Note that in the fundamental group of the annulus, the projection
\widehat{\gamma_{m}}
represents the
m
-th power of the counterclockwise generator. Then, we have:

Proposition 3.2
.

For any
q^{1/n}\in\mathbb{C}^{\ast}
, we have
\mathcal{S}_{n}^{q}=\mathrm{sub}\left(\gamma_{m};m\in\mathbb{Z}\right)
.

The primary content of Proposition
3.2
, which is that the curves
\gamma_{m}
generate the
HOMFLYPT skein algebra
, namely framed oriented links considered up to the crossing change, kink removing, and unknot removing relations, is well known
[
51
]
. For completeness, and because we will need the essential statement (Lemma
3.3
) to prove some more refined statements later on (Lemmas
3.6
and
3.7
), we provide a detailed proof of Proposition
3.2
.

Lemma 3.3
.

For
m>0
, let
\beta=\widetilde{\sigma}\in\mathrm{Brd}_{m}
be a permutation knot. Then
\gamma_{m}
is isotopic in the annulus to a mutant of
\beta
. Similarly, the reverse
\overline{\gamma_{m}}=\gamma_{-m}
is isotopic in the annulus to the reverse of a mutant of
\beta
.

Proof.

The algorithm of
[
51
]
to determine which crossings need to be changed, namely which mutant of
\beta
to take, is to change the crossings in such a way as to obtain the descending knot, namely the knot which decreases in height until climbing up at the very end. Once the knot is descending, it is clear that it is isotopic in the annulus to
\gamma_{m}
.
∎

Remark 3.4
.

A slight upgrade of Lemma
3.3
, which we will not need, is that if moreover
\beta
has
m-1
positive crossings and no negative crossings, then (resp. the reverse of)
\beta
is isotopic in the annulus to (resp. the reverse of)
\gamma_{m}
.

For links
L
and
L^{\prime}
and a natural number
N
, we will use the notation
\left[L\right]\overset{N}{=}\left[L^{\prime}\right]
to indicate that
\left[L\right]=a^{\prime}\left[L^{\prime}\right]+\sum_{i}a_{i}\left[L_{i}\right]\in\mathcal{S}_{n}^{q}
for scalars
a^{\prime}\neq 0,a_{i}
such that the links
L_{i}
have no more than
N-1
crossings. We say
L
and
L^{\prime}
are
equivalent up to
N
crossings
. For example, if
L
and
L^{\prime}
are links appearing as on the left hand side of the crossing change relation in Figure
3
, then
\left[L\right]\overset{N}{=}\left[L^{\prime}\right]
for
N
the number of crossings of
L
. In particular, if
\beta\in\mathrm{Brd}_{m}
is a braid link, and
\beta^{\prime}\in\mathrm{Brd}_{m}
is a mutant of
\beta
, then
\left[\beta\right]\overset{N}{=}\left[\beta^{\prime}\right]
and
\left[\left(\overline{\beta}\right)\right]\overset{N}{=}\left[\left(\overline{\beta^{\prime}}\right)\right]
.

Lemma 3.5
.

If
K
is any non-contractible knot, not necessarily a braid knot, in the thickened annulus with
N
crossings, then there is a permutation knot
\beta=\widetilde{\sigma}\in\mathrm{Brd}_{m}
for some
m>0
such that
\left[K\right]\overset{N}{=}\left[\beta\right]
or
\left[K\right]\overset{N}{=}\left[\left(\overline{\beta}\right)\right]
.

Proof.

By a planar isotopy (Section
1.1
), take the knot into a box such that outside the box the knot consists of parallel strands going around the annulus. Note that the knot intersects only the top and bottom edges of the box. There might be U-turns on either edge of the box. An innermost U-turn is a U-turn that does not completely contain another U-turn. Given any U-turn, by iteratively applying the crossing change relation, there is a knot
K_{1}
such that
\left[K\right]\overset{N}{=}\left[K_{1}\right]
and in
K_{1}
the U-turn lies above all other strands. If this U-turn is innermost, then there is a planar isotopy of
K_{1}
removing the U-turn from the box and sliding it around the annulus into the other side of the box. Repeat this process, yielding a knot
K_{p}
with no U-turns and
\left[K\right]\overset{N}{=}\left[K_{p}\right]
. Note that
K_{p}
has
m
strands, for some
m>0
, with the same orientation, all either going from bottom to top or from top to bottom. If
K_{p}
has a bigon, which we can take to be innermost, by iteratively applying the crossing change relation there is a knot
K_{p+1}
such that
\left[K_{p}\right]\overset{N}{=}\left[K_{p+1}\right]
and in
K_{p+1}
an edge of the bigon lies above all other strands. By an isotopy, but not a planar isotopy, of
K_{p+1}
, the bigon can be removed, after which the number of crossings of
K_{p+1}
has strictly decreased. In a similar way, a monogon can be removed by applying crossing change relations followed by the kink removing relation. By repeating this process, we obtain a knot
K_{q}
with no U-turns, no bigons, no monogons, and
\left[K\right]\overset{N}{=}\left[K_{q}\right]
. If
K_{q}
is oriented upward, then
K_{q}=\beta=\widetilde{\sigma}\in\mathrm{Brd}_{m}
is a permutation knot, else
K_{q}=\overline{\beta}
for the permutation knot
\beta
obtained by reversing the orientation of
K_{q}
.
∎

Proof of Proposition
3.2
.

Denote by
\mathcal{G}\subset\mathcal{S}_{n}^{q}
the sub-algebra
\mathrm{sub}\left(\gamma_{m};m\in\mathbb{Z}\right)
. So we want to argue that
\mathcal{S}_{n}^{q}=\mathcal{G}
.

(1) It suffices to show that
\left[W\right]\in\mathcal{G}
for any web
W
. Indeed, every element of
\mathcal{S}_{n}^{q}
can be written as a linear combination of classes of webs.

(2) It suffices to show that
\left[L\right]\in\mathcal{G}
for any link
L
. Indeed, by iteratively applying the vertex removing relation, the class
\left[W\right]
of a web can be written as a linear combination of classes of links.

(3) Assume by induction that
\left[L\right]\in\mathcal{G}
if
L
has at most
N-1
crossings. The base case of a trivial link is immediate, using the unknot removing relation if need be. Note that if
\left[L\right]\overset{N}{=}\left[L^{\prime}\right]
, then
\left[L\right]\in\mathcal{G}
if and only if
\left[L^{\prime}\right]\in\mathcal{G}
.

It suffices to show that
\left[K\right]\in\mathcal{G}
for any knot
K
. Indeed, by iteratively applying the crossing change relation, we have
\left[L\right]\overset{N}{=}\left[K_{1}\cup K_{2}\cup\cdots\cup K_{p}\right]=\left[K_{1}\right]\left[K_{2}\right]\cdots\left[K_{p}\right]
for knots
K_{i}
lying at distinct heights.

(4) It suffices to show that
\left[\beta\right]\in\mathcal{G}
for any permutation knot
\beta=\widetilde{\sigma}\in\mathrm{Brd}_{m}
and its reverse
\overline{\beta}
. Indeed, if the knot
K
is contractible, then the relations imply
\left[K\right]\propto\left[\varnothing\right]\in\mathcal{G}
. Else, by Lemma
3.5
, there is a permutation knot
\beta=\widetilde{\sigma}\in\mathrm{Brd}_{m}
such that
\left[K\right]\overset{N}{=}\left[\beta\right]
or
\left[K\right]\overset{N}{=}\left[\left(\overline{\beta}\right)\right]
.

(5) We show that such a class
\left[\beta\right]
or
\left[\left(\overline{\beta}\right)\right]
as in Part (4) is contained in
\mathcal{G}
, completing the proof. By Lemma
3.3
, the generator
\gamma_{m}
is isotopic in the annulus to a mutant
\beta^{\prime}
of
\beta
. So
\left[\gamma_{m}\right]=\left[\beta^{\prime}\right]\in\mathcal{S}_{n}^{q}
. By applying the crossing change relation for each differing crossing, we have
\left[\beta^{\prime}\right]\overset{N}{=}\left[\beta\right]
, in other words
\left[\gamma_{m}\right]\overset{N}{=}\left[\beta\right]
. Since
\left[\gamma_{m}\right]\in\mathcal{G}
by definition, we have
\left[\beta\right]\in\mathcal{G}
as desired. By the second statement of Lemma
3.3
, the same argument gives
\left[\left(\overline{\beta}\right)\right]\in\mathcal{G}
.
∎

Lemma 3.6
.

Let

\mathfrak{A}\times\mathfrak{I}

4.4.
Ideally triangulated punctured surfaces and the quantum trace map

From now on,
\mathfrak{S}=\mathfrak{S}^{\prime}-\mathfrak{P}
will be a punctured surface.

4.4.1.
Ideal triangulations

An
ideal triangulation

\mathfrak{L}
of the punctured surface
\mathfrak{S}
is a cell decomposition of
\mathfrak{S}^{\prime}
such that the
0
-cells consist of the puncture set
\mathfrak{P}
, the
1
-cells are ideal arcs, and the
2
-cells are ideal triangles. It is well-known
[
5
]
that if
\mathfrak{S}
has

\mathfrak{L}
exists if and only if the Euler characteristic of
\mathfrak{S}
is strictly less than
d/2
. Note that the only punctured surfaces excluded in this way are the monoangle, the biangle, and the sphere with one or two punctures. For simplicity, assume ideal triangulations do not contain any self-folded triangles, in which case the once punctured monoangle should also be excluded.

The
1
-cells, namely the ideal arcs, often called ideal edges, of an ideal triangulation
\mathfrak{L}
are partitioned into the internal edges and the boundary edges, the latter which are exactly the boundary components
\mathfrak{K}
of
\mathfrak{S}
. The
2
-cells are called either triangles or faces.

4.4.2.
Quantum trace map: introduction

Let
\mathfrak{S}
be an ideally triangulated surface with ideal triangulation
\mathfrak{L}
. Fix once and for all an arbitrary
(2n)
-root
\omega^{1/2}
of
q^{1/n}
. So
(\omega^{1/2})^{2n}=q^{1/n}
. Arbitrarily identify each ideal triangle of
\mathfrak{L}
with the ideal triangle
\mathfrak{D}_{3}
, then choose an arbitrary order of the triangles
\mathfrak{D}_{3}
of
\mathfrak{L}
.

In Section
4.4.5

\mathcal{T}_{n}^{\omega}(\mathfrak{D}_{3})
, called the

-root Fock–Goncharov quantum torus
(or just

), to the ideal triangle
\mathfrak{D}_{3}
, rather, to
\mathfrak{D}_{3}
plus the choice of a preferred ideal vertex of this triangle, whose choice is only for bookkeeping purposes and which we will likely not mention again.

Recall that if
\mathcal{A}
and

are algebras, then their tensor product

over
\mathbb{C}
is also an algebra, where the product of

with

is
(aa^{\prime})\otimes(bb^{\prime})

\bigotimes_{\mathfrak{D}_{3}\in\mathfrak{L}}\mathcal{T}_{n}^{\omega}(\mathfrak{D}_{3})
be the tensor product algebra of the quantum tori
\mathcal{T}_{n}^{\omega}(\mathfrak{D}_{3})
associated to the triangles
\mathfrak{D}_{3}
, in the given order. Note that, since the algebras
\mathcal{A}\otimes\mathcal{B}
and

are naturally isomorphic in the obvious way, this order is essentially immaterial.

Theorem 4.6

For an ideally triangulated surface

4.4.3.
Good position of a web with respect to a split ideal triangulation

The
split ideal triangulation

\widehat{\mathfrak{L}}
associated to an ideal triangulation
\mathfrak{L}
is the cell complex obtained by blowing up each edge of
\mathfrak{L}
to a biangle
\mathfrak{D}_{2}
, including the boundary edges. In total, the number of

\mathfrak{D}_{3}
then faces three biangles, each internal biangle faces two triangles, and each external biangle, namely those coming from boundary edges, faces one triangle. Note that there is a natural bijection between the triangles of
\mathfrak{L}
and those of
\widehat{\mathfrak{L}}
.

We now describe what we mean by the
good position
of a stated web
W^{\circ}
in
\mathfrak{S}\times\mathfrak{I}
with respect to a split ideal triangulation
\widehat{\mathfrak{L}}
of
\mathfrak{S}
. Note this is a purely topological notion, and the states themselves will not play any role. The web
W^{\circ}
is in good position if it satisfies the following properties: (1) the restriction of
W^{\circ}
to
\mathfrak{D}_{2}\times\mathfrak{I}
or
\mathfrak{D}_{3}\times\mathfrak{I}
for each thickened face, either a biangle
\mathfrak{D}_{2}
or a triangle
\mathfrak{D}_{3}
, of
\widehat{\mathfrak{L}}
is a boundary web in the sense of Section
4.1
; (2) the restriction of
W^{\circ}
to
\mathfrak{D}_{3}\times\mathfrak{I}
for each triangle face is a disjoint union of oriented arcs
A^{\circ}_{1},A^{\circ}_{2},\dots,A^{\circ}_{k}
, each at a constant height with a constant vertical framing and connecting two different edges of the triangle. Note by (1) that, over each triangle, the arcs are at different heights, and their framings point in the positive vertical direction. We may assume the indexing is such that the arc
A^{\circ}_{i}
lies above
A^{\circ}_{i+1}
.

Remark 4.7

Let

\mathfrak{S}\times\mathfrak{I}
, and

4.4.6.
Digression: an open problem originating in the physical point of view

There is also a different construction of quantum traces from spectral networks
[
25
,
57
,
58
]
, motivated by the physical problem of counting ‘framed BPS states’. At least for simple oriented loops
L^{\circ}
, this different construction appears, experimentally, to agree with the construction of
\tau^{q}_{\mathfrak{L}}(L^{\circ})
given above. For
n=2
, such an agreement has been put on a more solid mathematical footing
[
39
]
.

The physical interpretation of quantum traces leads to a precise mathematical prediction, essentially in
[
25
,
57
,
58
]
and based on ideas in
[
27
]
, concerning their structure, and hints that even for simple closed curves
L^{\circ}
the quantum trace
\tau^{q}_{\mathfrak{L}}(L^{\circ})
contains significantly more information than the classical trace. The precise statement is that if we write

\tau^{q}_{\mathfrak{L}}(L^{\circ})=\sum_{\{i_{1}(\mathfrak{D}_{3}),\dots,i_{N}(\mathfrak{D}_{3})\in\mathbb{Z};\mathfrak{D}_{3}\in\mathfrak{L}\}}c(i_{1}(\mathfrak{D}_{3}),\dots,i_{N}(\mathfrak{D}_{3});\mathfrak{D}_{3}\in\mathfrak{L})\bigotimes_{\mathfrak{D}_{3}\in\mathfrak{L}}\left[X_{1}^{i_{1}(\mathfrak{D}_{3})/n}\cdots X_{N}^{i_{N}(\mathfrak{D}_{3})/n}\right]

in
\bigotimes_{\mathfrak{D}_{3}\in\mathfrak{L}}\mathcal{T}_{n}^{\omega}(\mathfrak{D}_{3})
, then the complex coefficients
c(i_{1}(\mathfrak{D}_{3}),i_{2}(\mathfrak{D}_{3}),\dots,i_{N}(\mathfrak{D}_{3});\mathfrak{D}_{3}\in\mathfrak{L})
with respect to the Weyl ordered monomials are sums of quantum positive integers

c(i_{1}(\mathfrak{D}_{3}),i_{2}(\mathfrak{D}_{3}),\dots,i_{N}(\mathfrak{D}_{3});\mathfrak{D}_{3}\in\mathfrak{L})=\sum_{k>0}m_{k}[k]

where
[k]=(q^{k}-q^{-k})/(q-q^{-1})
and
m_{k}\in\mathbb{N}
is the multiplicity. This is still an open problem, even for
n=2
. Physically, these multiplicities are interpreted as counting the framed BPS states in quantum dimension
[k]
. Some evidence is presented in
[
58
]
, including experiments performed together with the second author of the current paper.

Note that if the curve
L^{\circ}
satisfies the property that it passes through each triangle
\mathfrak{D}_{3}\in\mathfrak{L}
at most once, then it follows readily from the state sum formula that all the non-zero coefficients
c(i_{1}(\mathfrak{D}_{3}),i_{2}(\mathfrak{D}_{3}),\dots,i_{N}(\mathfrak{D}_{3});\mathfrak{D}_{3}\in\mathfrak{L})
are equal to
1*[1]=1
. It is the ‘self interaction’ that occurs when the curve passes through triangles more than once that allows for the creation of quantum coefficients
[k]
for
k\geq 2
. Note also that these coefficients are global in nature, as they are the remnant of rewriting, for every triangle, the product over arcs of Weyl ordered terms as a scalar times a single Weyl ordered term, followed by collecting these local scalars into a single global scalar for the triangulation and gathering like monomials as a sum.

As one final note, this prediction is true for simple loops in the annulus, with ideal triangulation as in Section
4.5.1
below, since a loop passes through each triangle exactly once. Indeed, the only two simple loops are the clockwise and counterclockwise loop. The recent paper
[
41
]
could be helpful in studying this problem for general surfaces. We emphasize that, at least a priori, this problem depends heavily on the choice of ideal triangulation.

Remark 4.9
.

The prediction discussed in this sub-sub-section is related to the so-called ‘framed no-exotics conjecture’ from the physical viewpoint
[
25
,
27
,
57
]
. Closely related mathematical conjectures appear in the context of quantum cluster varieties, see
[
12
]
and
[
11

, where the analogous predicted property of the relevant coefficients is called ‘Lefschetz type’. It is not entirely clear the degree to which these results and conjectures from quantum cluster geometry explain the above predicted property of quantum traces of loops. For instance, there is a somewhat subtle distinction between the Fock–Goncharov cluster
\mathcal{X}
variables
X_{i}
and their
n
-roots
X_{i}^{1/n}
; see
[
40

42
]
for a detailed discussion of this distinction. In particular, the above prediction applies to all simple loops, whose quantum traces are only guaranteed to be Laurent polynomials in the
X_{i}^{1/n}

; contrast with the ‘quantum theta bases’ of
[
1
,
53
]
.

Lemma 4.10

Let

and

be the standard quantum left and right matrices. In general, an entry

(1)

M_{ii}
is a monomial.

(2)

M_{ii}
commutes with
M_{i^{\prime}i^{\prime}}
.

(3)

\mathrm{deg}(M_{ii})>\mathrm{deg}(M_{(i-1)(i-1)})
, and
\mathrm{deg}{M}_{ii}
is strictly greater than any of the degrees of the monomials appearing as summands in
M_{ij}
for

(4)

For all

\mathrm{deg}(M_{ii})(a,b,c)=\begin{cases}n-a,&n+1-i\leq a,1\leq a\leq n-1;\\
-a,&n+1-i>a,1\leq a\leq n-1;\\
0,&a=0.\end{cases}

(5)

For
k=1,2,\dots,n-1

and

by

and
f_{k}(n-k)=g_{k}(n-k)
. Then for all
(a,b,c)\in\Theta_{n}^{\prime}

\displaystyle\mathrm{deg}\left(\prod_{i=n+1-k}^{n}M_{ii}\right)(a,b,c)

\displaystyle=\min\{f_{k}(n-a),g_{k}(n-a)\}\in\mathbb{N},

\displaystyle\mathrm{deg}\left(\prod_{i=k+1}^{n}M_{ii}\right)(a,b,c)

\displaystyle=\min\{f_{k}(a),g_{k}(a)\}\in\mathbb{N}.

Proof.

(1) By definition,
M
is the product of lower triangular, and diagonal, matrices
M_{v}
,

for some

, as well as lower triangular matrices involving no variables. Rather,

under addition, in particular it contains the zero function. We denote by
\mathcal{C}
the sub-monoid that is the intersection of the balanced Knutson–Tao fan with those functions
f
satisfying
f(a,b,c)=0
for all
a=0
.

Let the functions
t^{L}_{k}
and
t^{R}_{k}
in
\mathbb{N}^{\Theta_{n}^{\prime}}
for
k=1,2,\dots,n-1
be as in Section
4.5.3
. One checks
t^{R}_{k}\in\mathcal{C}
for each
k
. By the symmetry
t^{L}_{n-k}=t^{R}_{k}
this is also true for the
t^{L}_{k}
. Moreover, we have the following specific values for the rhombus numbers:
r(t^{R}_{k})^{\mathrm{bl}}_{kj}=1
for all
j=1,2,\dots,n-k
and all other rhombus numbers
r(t^{R}_{k})^{\mathrm{bl}}_{ij}
,
i\neq k
, and
r(t^{R}_{k})^{\mathrm{t}}_{ij}
and
r(t^{R}_{k})^{\mathrm{br}}_{ij}
are zero.

A non-zero element
f
of a monoid is
irreducible
if
f
cannot be written as the sum of two non-zero elements of the monoid. If it happens that this monoid is
positive
, namely a sub-monoid of
\mathbb{N}^{m}
, then it is not hard to see that the set of irreducible elements forms the unique minimum spanning set over
\mathbb{N}
of the monoid, which we call the
Hilbert basis
; see, for instance,
[
15
, Section 3]
.

Proposition 6.1
.

The sub-monoid
\mathcal{C}
of the balanced Knutson–Tao fan defined above is positive, namely
\mathcal{C}\subset\mathbb{N}^{\Theta_{n}^{\prime}}
, and its Hilbert basis consists of the
n-1
elements
t^{R}_{k}
for
k=1,2,\dots,n-1
. Viewing
\mathcal{C}\subset\mathbb{N}^{n-1}
by the inclusion
f\mapsto(f(n-1,b,c),\dots,f(2,b^{\prime},c^{\prime}),f(1,b^{\prime\prime},c^{\prime\prime}))
, which is independent of the choices of
b,c,\dots,b^{\prime},c^{\prime},b^{\prime\prime},c^{\prime\prime}
, these
n-1
Hilbert basis elements are linearly independent over
\mathbb{R}
, so span over
\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
a single real
(n-1)
-dimensional cone in
\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{n-1}
containing
\mathcal{C}
as a sub-monoid.

Proof.

If we can show that
\mathcal{C}
is contained in the span over
\mathbb{N}
of
\{t_{k}^{R};k=1,2,\dots,n-1\}\subset\mathbb{N}^{\Theta_{n}^{\prime}}
, this will establish that
\mathcal{C}
is positive. That these
n-1
elements form the Hilbert basis of
\mathcal{C}
, and the second statement of the proposition, in particular that the proposed inclusion
\mathcal{C}\to\mathbb{N}^{n-1}
is indeed injective, then follows by the linear independence property of Lemma
4.12
.

So assume
f\in\mathcal{C}\subset\mathbb{R}^{\Theta_{n}^{\prime}}
. We want to produce
c_{k}\in\mathbb{N}
such that
f=\sum_{k}c_{k}t^{R}_{k}
. By definition of
\mathcal{C}
, the rhombus numbers
r(f)^{\mathrm{bl}}
,
r(f)^{\mathrm{t}}
,
r(f)^{\mathrm{br}}
are in
\mathbb{N}
, and
f(a,b,c)=0
for
a=0
. We claim that
r(f)^{\mathrm{bl}}_{ij}
is independent of
j
and that we can take
c_{k}=r(f)^{\mathrm{bl}}_{kj}
.

We begin by considering the rhombus numbers
r(f)^{\mathrm{t}}_{i(n-i)}
and
r(f)^{\mathrm{br}}_{i1}
, which hug the edge of the triangle between the top and bottom right vertices. Each such rhombus has an acute and obtuse vertex lying on this edge with the same value under
f
, namely zero in this case, and each adjacent pair
(1,i-1,n-i),(1,i,n-i-1)
of vertices is a common side of a unique top and bottom right rhombus, namely
r(f)^{\mathrm{t}}_{i(n-i)}
and
r(f)^{\mathrm{br}}_{(n-i)1}
. These rhombus numbers are
\pm(f(1,i-1,n-i)-f(1,i,n-i-1))/n
. Since they are both in
\mathbb{N}
by assumption, they are zero, so we have
f(1,i-1,n-i)=f(1,i,n-i-1)
for all
i
. That is,
f(1,b,c)
is constant, independent of
b,c
. By induction on
a
, the same argument implies
f(a,b,c)
is independent of
b,c
. Abusing notation, write
f(a)=f(a,b,c)
. It follows that the rhombus number
r(f)^{\mathrm{bl}}_{kj}=(-f({k-1})+2f(k)-f({k+1}))/n
is independent of
j
, so call it
c_{k}
. This completes the first part of the claim.

We gather we have a linear system
\left(\begin{smallmatrix}c_{n-1}\\
\vdots\\
c_{1}\end{smallmatrix}\right)=T^{-1}\left(\begin{smallmatrix}f(1)\\
\vdots\\
f({n-1})\end{smallmatrix}\right)
where the matrix
T^{-1}
appeared in the proof of Lemma
4.12
. Abusing notation and putting
t^{R}_{k}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
and
t^{L}_{k}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
as in the proof of the same lemma, solving this system for the values of
f
,

\displaystyle\left(\begin{smallmatrix}f(1)\\
\vdots\\
f({n-1})\end{smallmatrix}\right)

\displaystyle=T\left(\begin{smallmatrix}c_{n-1}\\
\vdots\\
c_{1}\end{smallmatrix}\right)=\sum_{k}c_{n-k}\left(\begin{smallmatrix}t^{R}_{k}(n-1)\\
\vdots\\
t^{R}_{k}(1)\end{smallmatrix}\right)=\sum_{k}c_{n-k}\left(\begin{smallmatrix}t^{L}_{k}(1)\\
\vdots\\
t^{L}_{k}(n-1)\end{smallmatrix}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k}c_{n-k}\left(\begin{smallmatrix}t^{R}_{n-k}(1)\\
\vdots\\
t^{R}_{n-k}(n-1)\end{smallmatrix}\right)=\sum_{k}c_{k}\left(\begin{smallmatrix}t^{R}_{k}(1)\\
\vdots\\
t^{R}_{k}(n-1)\end{smallmatrix}\right)

where the third and fourth equalities use the symmetries discussed at the beginning of Section
4.5.3
. In other words,
f=\sum_{k}c_{k}t^{R}_{k}
, completing the second part of the claim.
∎

We have so far been discussing the positive sub-monoid
\mathcal{C}\subset\mathbb{N}^{\Theta_{n}^{\prime}}
associated to the discrete triangle. Consider now the triangulated annulus
\mathfrak{A}
as in Section
4.5.1
. Associate a copy
\mathcal{C}^{L}
(resp.
\mathcal{C}^{R}
) of
\mathcal{C}
to the left (resp. right) triangle
\mathfrak{D}_{3}^{L}
(resp.
\mathfrak{D}_{3}^{R}
) of the ideal triangulation
\mathfrak{L}
of
\mathfrak{A}
. The
balanced Knutson–Tao fan for the annulus
, denoted
\mathcal{C}(\mathfrak{A},\mathfrak{L})
, is the sub-set of elements
(f^{L},f^{R})
of the Cartesian product
\mathcal{C}\times\mathcal{C}
satisfying
f^{L}(a,0,n-a)=f^{R}(n-a,0,a)
and
f^{L}(a,n-a,0)=f^{R}(n-a,a,0)
for all
a
.

For example, the pair
(t_{k}^{L},t_{k}^{R})
is in
\mathcal{C}(\mathfrak{A},\mathfrak{L})
for all
k=1,2,\dots,n-1
. The following is clear:

Corollary 6.2
.

The balanced Knutson–Tao fan
\mathcal{C}(\mathfrak{A},\mathfrak{L})\subset\mathcal{C}\times\mathcal{C}
for the annulus consists of the points
(\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}m_{k}t_{k}^{L},\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}m_{k}t_{k}^{R})
for
m_{k}\in\mathbb{N}
. In particular, the natural projection of
\mathcal{C}(\mathfrak{A},\mathfrak{L})
to either component
\mathcal{C}
is a bijection. So, by Proposition
6.1
there is an inclusion of
\mathcal{C}(\mathfrak{A},\mathfrak{L})
as a sub-monoid of a single real
(n-1)
-dimensional cone in
\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{n-1}
spanned over
\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
by the
n-1
Hilbert basis elements
(t_{k}^{L},t_{k}^{R})
of
\mathcal{C}(\mathfrak{A},\mathfrak{L})
. ∎

4.14
, proof of Proposition

, the points of

are precisely the pairs of left and right highest degrees of the quantum traces
\tau^{q}_{\mathfrak{L}}\circ\iota((B^{\circ}_{1})^{m_{1}}(B^{\circ}_{2})^{m_{2}}\cdots(B^{\circ}_{n-1})^{m_{n-1}})\in\mathcal{T}_{n}^{\omega}(\mathfrak{D}_{3}^{L})\otimes\mathcal{T}_{n}^{\omega}(\mathfrak{D}_{3}^{R})

Remark 6.3
.

We view Theorem
2.1
and Corollary
6.2
together as a quantum topological manifestation, in the setting of the annulus, of quantum Fock–Goncharov duality, rather, quantum Fock–Goncharov–Shen duality
[
11
,
19
,
20
,
29
,
30
,
31
]
; see also
[
14
,
15
,
40
,
67
]
for a study of the
n=3
case for any punctured surface.

For a punctured surface
\mathfrak{S}
, without boundary for simplicity, Fock–Goncharov associate two dual moduli spaces
\mathcal{X}_{\mathrm{PGL}(n)}
and
\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{SL}(n)}
. These are positive spaces, so their points with respect to a semi-field are defined. For example, for
n=2
then the points
\mathcal{X}_{\mathrm{PGL}(2)}(\mathbb{R}_{>0})
and
\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{SL}(2)}(\mathbb{R}_{>0})
over
\mathbb{R}_{>0}
recover the classical Teichmüller space, rather, enhanced Teichmüller space, and Penner’s decorated Teichmüller space of the surface, respectively, and recover so-called higher Teichmüller spaces when
n=n
. For the semi-field of tropical integers, denoted
\mathbb{Z}^{t}
, where multiplication (resp. addition) in
\mathbb{Z}^{t}
is the usual addition (resp. max) operation in
\mathbb{Z}
, the points
\mathcal{X}_{\mathrm{PGL}(n)}(\mathbb{Z}^{t})
and
\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{SL}(n)}(\mathbb{Z}^{t})
are also defined. To work in coordinates, one needs to choose additional topological data such as an ideal triangulation
\mathfrak{L}
of
\mathfrak{S}
.

Classical Fock–Goncharov duality, so when
q^{1/n}=1
, asserts that the tropical integer points
\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{SL}(n)}(\mathbb{Z}^{t})
index the elements of a ‘canonical’ basis of the coordinate ring
\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{X}_{\mathrm{PGL}(n)})
, and vice-versa. This basis should satisfy, for example, strong positivity properties. Fock–Goncharov–Shen duality asserts that the positive tropical integer points
\mathcal{A}^{+}_{\mathrm{PGL}(n)}(\mathbb{Z}^{t})
, where the positivity is taken with respect to the Goncharov–Shen potential, index the elements of a canonical basis of the coordinate ring
\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{SL}(n)})
of the character variety.

There are also quantum versions of this duality. Since the skein algebra
\mathcal{S}_{n}^{q}(\mathfrak{S})
is a quantization of the coordinate ring
\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{SL}(n)})
of the character variety, Fock–Goncharov–Shen duality suggests that the positive tropical integer points
\mathcal{A}^{+}_{\mathrm{PGL}(n)}(\mathbb{Z}^{t})
should index the elements of a canonical basis of the skein algebra, in particular a canonical topological basis of
\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{SL}(n)})
when
q^{1/n}=1
. Indeed, following Goncharov–Shen’s lead, upon choosing an ideal triangulation
\mathfrak{L}
of
\mathfrak{S}
we can think of the corresponding balanced Knutson–Tao fan as a model for
\mathcal{A}^{+}_{\mathrm{PGL}(n)}(\mathbb{Z}^{t})
. When the surface
\mathfrak{S}=\mathfrak{A}
is the annulus with triangulation
\mathfrak{L}
as above, then this model for
\mathcal{A}^{+}_{\mathrm{PGL}(n)}(\mathbb{Z}^{t})
, rather, the subset constrained to be zero on the boundary, is precisely the balanced Knutson–Tao fan
\mathcal{C}(\mathfrak{A},\mathfrak{L})
appearing in Corollary
6.2
, whose points index the monomial basis elements of the skein algebra
\mathcal{S}_{n}^{q}(\mathfrak{A})
appearing in Theorem
2.1
via their highest degrees with respect to the quantum trace map
\tau^{q}_{\mathfrak{L}}
. That these points appear as the highest polynomial degrees of the basis elements, as detected by the quantum trace map, is another indication of the tropical geometric nature of this duality.
