On the continuous dual Hahn process
Włodek Bryc

Abstract
. We extend the continuous dual Hahn process ( 𝕋 t ) (\mathbb{T}_{t}) of Corwin and Knizel from a finite time interval to the entire real line by taking a limit of a closely related Markov process ( 𝐓 t ) (\mathbf{T}_{t}) . We also characterize processes ( 𝐓 t ) (\mathbf{T}_{t}) by conditional means and variances under bidirectional conditioning, and we prove that continuous dual Hahn polynomials are orthogonal martingale polynomials for both processes.



2020 Mathematics Subject Classification
60J25;33C45

This is an expanded version of the paper with additional material.

1.
Introduction

In this paper we are interested in a family of Markov transition probabilities on the real line which are constructed from the orthogonality measures of the continuous dual Hahn polynomials.
Together with the appropriate marginal laws that arise from a point mass as the initial law, these transition probabilities define a class of continuous time Markov processes
(\mathbf{T}_{t})
which appeared in the construction of quadratic harnesses in
[
2
, Section 3]
. Together with an appropriate family of
\sigma
-finite positive measures as the entrance laws, see (
4.4
), these transition probabilities appeared in the description of the multipoint Laplace transform for stationary measures of the open KPZ equation in
[
8
, Theorem 1.4(5)]
.
Following
[
8
]
, we shall use the name the continuous dual Hahn process, and we will use their suggestive notation
(\mathbb{T}_{t})
.
Our goal is to extend the time domain of the process
(\mathbb{T}_{t})
from a finite interval described in
[
8
, (1.10)]
, to the real line. The need for an extension of the time domain arose in
[
3
, Theorem 1.3]
, although for the purposes of that paper the extension to
t\in[0,\infty)
would suffice.
We accomplish our goal by analyzing the Markov process
(\mathbf{T}_{t})
as one of its parameters diverges to
\infty
.

The actual process
(\mathbb{T}_{t})
constructed here differs slightly from the continuous dual Hahn process in
[
8
]
: the process that appears in Refs
[
3
,
8
]
corresponds to
(4\mathbb{T}_{s/2})
. On the other hand,
(\mathbf{T}_{t})
as constructed in this note, is a direct extension of the family of Markov processes from
[
2
]
to a half-line as the time domain. We will obtain the entrance laws for the process
(\mathbb{T}_{t})
by taking a limit of the appropriately scaled marginal laws for the process
(\mathbf{T}_{t})
.

Our approach to the construction, which relies on verification of the Chapman-Kolmogorov equations, is somewhat different than in Refs.
[
2
,
8
]
, which used explicit formulas for the orthogonality measures of the continuous dual Hahn polynomials. In the presence of atoms, such explicit formulas lead to proliferation of cases, which we avoid by relying on properties of the orthogonal martingale polynomials for
(\mathbf{T}_{t})
. In particular, as in
[
5
, Section 3.2]
, we deduce the Chapman-Kolmogorov equations from the algebraic relations between two families of orthogonal polynomials.

The paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
we recall the definition of the continuous dual Hahn polynomials and discuss the probability measures which make them orthogonal. In Section
3
we use these measures to construct the family of transition probabilities and marginal laws for Markov process
(\mathbf{T}_{t})
. Our main result, Theorem
3.5
, establishes the Chapman-Kolmogorov equations. In Section
4
we
introduce the
\sigma
-finite entrance laws that define process
(\mathbb{T}_{t})
for all
t\in(-\infty,\infty)
.
In Section
5
we characterize the Markov process
(\mathbf{T}_{t})
by the formulas for the conditional mean and the conditional variance.

2.
Continuous dual Hahn polynomials

2.1.
Favard’s Theorem

We first recall a version of Favard’s theorem in the form that encompasses in one statement orthogonality with respect to both finitely supported and infinitely supported measures.
This form of Favard’s theorem is ”well known” to the experts and it is implicit in many proofs, in particular in the argument presented in
[
10
, Section 2.5]
. The explicit reference (with a proof) is

[
5
, Theorem A.1]
.

Theorem 2.1

(Favard’s Theorem)
.

Let
\alpha_{n},\beta_{n}
be real,
n\geq 0
. Consider monic
polynomials

\{p_{n}\}
defined by the recurrence

(2.1)

x{p}_{n}(x)=p_{n+1}(x)+\alpha_{n}p_{n}(x)+\beta_{n}p_{n-1}(x),\;n\geq 0,

with the initial conditions
p_{0}(x)=1
,
p_{-1}(x)=0
. Then the following two conditions are equivalent:

(i)

For all
n\geq 1
,

(2.2)

\prod_{j=1}^{n}\beta_{j}\geq 0.

(ii)

There exists a (not necessarily unique) probability measure
\nu
with all moments such that for all
m,n\geq 0
,

(2.3)

\int p_{n}(x)p_{m}(x)\nu(dx)=\delta_{m,n}\prod_{j=1}^{n}\beta_{j}.

Furthermore, suppose that (
2.2
) holds. Then either
\beta_{n}>0
for all
n\geq 1
, and then measure
\nu
has infinite support, or there is a positive integer
n\geq 1
such that
\beta_{n}=0
. In the latter case, denote by
N
the first positive integer such that
\beta_{N}=0
.
Then condition (
2.2
) contains no further restrictions on the values
\beta_{n}
for
n>N
and the orthogonality measure

\nu(dx)
is a (unique) discrete probability measure supported on the finite set of

N\geq 1
real and distinct zeros of the polynomial

p_{N}(x)
.

2.2.
The three step recurrence for the continuous dual Hahn polynomials

The continuous dual Hahn polynomials are monic polynomials which depend
on three parameters. These parameters are
traditionally denoted by
a,b,c
, but to avoid confusion with the parameters
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{a}\color[rgb]{0,0,0},\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{b}\color[rgb]{0,0,0},\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{c}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}
for the Markov process
(\mathbf{T}_{t})
, we will denote them by
\alpha,\beta,\gamma
.
We always assume that parameter
\alpha
is real, and that parameters
\beta,\gamma
are either both real or form a complex conjugate pair.
Then sequences

(2.4)

A_{n}=(n+\alpha+\beta)(n+\alpha+\gamma),\quad C_{n}=n(n-1+\beta+\gamma),\quad n=0,1,\dots

are real.
The continuous dual Hahn polynomials, see
[
11
, (1.3.5)]
, are monic polynomials
\{p_{n}(x|\alpha,\beta,\gamma)\}
in real variable
x
,
defined by the
three step recurrence relation

(2.5)

xp_{n}(x|\alpha,\beta,\gamma)=p_{n+1}(x|\alpha,\beta,\gamma)+(A_{n}+C_{n}-\alpha^{2})p_{n}(x|\alpha,\beta,\gamma)+A_{n-1}C_{n}p_{n-1}(x|\alpha,\beta,\gamma),

n=0,1,\dots
, with the usual initialization
p_{-1}(x|\alpha,\beta,\gamma)=0
,
p_{0}(x|\alpha,\beta,\gamma)=1
; then (
2.5
) gives

(2.6)

p_{1}(x|\alpha,\beta,\gamma)=x-\alpha\beta-\alpha\gamma-\beta\gamma.

By comparing recursion (
2.5
) with
[
11
, (1.3.4)]
, we get

(2.7)

p_{n}(x|\alpha,\beta,\gamma)=(-1)^{n}(\alpha+\beta,\alpha+\gamma)_{n}\;{{}_{3}}F_{2}(-n,\alpha-\sqrt{-x},\alpha+\sqrt{-x};\alpha+\beta,\alpha+\gamma;1),

where

(2.8)

{{}_{3}}F_{2}(a_{1},a_{2},a_{3};b_{1},b_{2};z)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(a_{1},a_{2},a_{3})_{k}}{(b_{1},b_{2})_{k}}\frac{z^{k}}{k!}

denotes the generalized hypergeometric function.
Here and throughout the paper we use the following notation for the products of Gamma functions and the Pochhammer symbols:

\Gamma(a,b,\dots,c)=\Gamma(a)\Gamma(b)\dots\Gamma(c),\quad(a)_{n}=a(a+1)\dots(a+n-1),\quad(a,b,\dots,c)_{n}=(a)_{n}(b)_{n}\dots(c)_{n}.

With the above restrictions on the parameters, polynomials
\{p_{n}(x|\alpha,\beta,\gamma)\}
are well defined and real valued, but they do not have to be orthogonal. Favard’s theorem allows us to recognize for which choices of the parameters polynomials

\{p_{n}(x|\alpha,\beta,\gamma)\}
are orthogonal.
If parameters
\alpha,\beta,\gamma
are such that (
2.2
) holds with
\beta_{n}=A_{n-1}C_{n}
,
then polynomials

p_{n}(x|\alpha,\beta,\gamma)
are orthogonal in the following sense: there exists a probability measure
\nu({\textnormal{d}}x|\alpha,\beta,\gamma)
such that

(2.9)

\int_{\mathbb{R}}p_{n}(x|\alpha,\beta,\gamma)p_{m}(x|\alpha,\beta,\gamma)\nu({\textnormal{d}}x|\alpha,\beta,\gamma)=0

for
m\neq n
. From (
2.3
) it is clear that in the case of a measure with
N
atoms, the integral (
2.9
) is zero also for
m=n\geq N
.

Measures
\nu({\textnormal{d}}x|\alpha,\beta,\gamma)
play a prominent role in our construction, as we will define the transition probabilities and the marginal distributions for the Markov process
(\mathbf{T}_{t})
by specifying the parameters

\alpha,\beta,\gamma
. We will refer to
\nu({\textnormal{d}}x|\alpha,\beta,\gamma)
as the orthogonality measure for the polynomials
\{p_{n}(x|\alpha,\beta,\gamma)\}
.

For the constructions, we need to know that the orthogonality measure
\nu({\textnormal{d}}x|\alpha,\beta,\gamma)
is unique, i.e., that it is determined by moments. This fact should be known, but we did not find a published reference.
So for completeness we adapt an argument from an unpublished preprint

[
6
, Proposition 3.1]
, who considered a larger family of polynomials in a different parametrization.

Lemma 2.2
.

Orthogonality measures for the polynomials defined by (
2.5
) are determined uniquely by moments.

Proof.

Since finitely supported measures are determined uniquely by moments, we only need to consider the case when
A_{k}C_{k+1}>0
for all
k\geq 0
. In particular, we assume that
A_{0}C_{1}=(\alpha+\beta)(\alpha+\gamma)(\beta+\gamma)>0
.
We will use a criterion that involves the numerator polynomials
q_{n}(x)
, which solve recursion (
2.5
) with the initial conditions
q_{0}(x)=0
,
q_{1}(x)=1
and
n\geq 1
(see e.g.
[
10
, Section 2.3]
or
[
1
, Section 2.1]
).
Let

(2.10)

\widetilde{p}_{n}(x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{A_{0}A_{1}\dots A_{n-1}C_{1}C_{2}\dots C_{n}}}\;p_{n}(x|\alpha,\beta,\gamma)

and

\widetilde{q}_{n}(x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{A_{0}A_{1}\dots A_{n-1}C_{1}C_{2}\dots C_{n}}}\;\;q_{n}(x)

be the corresponding normalized polynomials.
By a theorem of Hamburger,
[
1
, page 84]
, the moment problem is determined
uniquely, if and only if at some point
x_{0}\in{\mathbb{R}}
we have

(2.11)

\sum_{n}|\widetilde{p}_{n}(x_{0})|^{2}+\sum_{n}|\widetilde{q}_{n}(x_{0})|^{2}=\infty.

We shall verify that this condition holds with
x_{0}=-\alpha^{2}
.

By (
2.7
),

p_{n}(-\alpha^{2}|\alpha,\beta,\gamma)=(-1)^{n}(\alpha+\beta,\alpha+\gamma)_{n}.

Therefore, noting that

(2.12)

A_{0}A_{1}\dots A_{n-1}C_{1}C_{2}\dots C_{n}=n!(\alpha+\beta,\alpha+\gamma,\beta+\gamma)_{n},

we have

|\widetilde{p}_{n}(-\alpha^{2})|^{2}=\frac{(\alpha+\beta,\alpha+\gamma)_{n}}{n!(\beta+\gamma)_{n}}\sim\frac{\Gamma(\alpha+\beta+n,\alpha+\gamma+n)}{n!\Gamma(\beta+\gamma+n)}\sim\frac{1}{n^{1-2\alpha}},

where we write
a_{n}\sim b_{n}
if
\lim_{n\to\infty}a_{n}/b_{n}\in(0,\infty)
. Thus (
2.11
) holds if
\alpha\geq 0
.

To verify that (
2.11
) holds for
\alpha\leq 0
, we analyze the numerator polynomials. With
x=-\alpha^{2}
, recursion (
2.5
) simplifies to

q_{n+1}(-\alpha^{2})+A_{n}q_{n}(-\alpha^{2})=-C_{n}\left(q_{n}(-\alpha^{2})+A_{n-1}q_{n-1}(-\alpha^{2})\right),\quad n\geq 1.

So with
q_{0}=0,q_{1}=1
we have

q_{n+1}(-\alpha^{2})+A_{n}q_{n}(-\alpha^{2})=(-1)^{n}\prod_{k=1}^{n}C_{k},\quad n\geq 0.

It is easy to check that the solution of this recursion (with
q_{0}=0,q_{1}=1
)
is

q_{n}(-\alpha^{2})=(-1)^{n-1}\left(\prod_{k=1}^{n-1}A_{k}\right)\sum_{m=0}^{n-1}\prod_{k=1}^{m}\tfrac{C_{k}}{A_{k}},\quad n\geq 0.

After normalization, we get

(2.13)

|\widetilde{q}_{n}(-\alpha^{2})|^{2}=\frac{1}{A_{0}^{2}}\left(\prod_{k=0}^{n-1}\frac{A_{k}}{C_{k+1}}\right)\left(\sum_{m=0}^{n-1}\prod_{k=1}^{m}\tfrac{C_{k}}{A_{k}}\right)^{2}.

To verify (
2.11
) we now use the fact that

(2.14)

\frac{C_{n}}{A_{n}}=1-\frac{1+2\alpha}{n}+O(1/n^{2}),\;\frac{A_{n}}{C_{n+1}}=1-\frac{1-2\alpha}{n}+O(1/n^{2}).

Thus

(2.15)

|\widetilde{q}_{n}(-\alpha^{2})|^{2}\sim\prod_{k=0}^{n-1}\left(1-\frac{1-2\alpha}{k}\right)\;\left(\sum_{m=0}^{n-1}\prod_{k=1}^{m}\left(1-\frac{1+2\alpha}{k}\right)\right)^{2}\\
\sim\exp\left(-\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\tfrac{1-2\alpha}{k}\right)\;\left(\sum_{m=0}^{n-1}\exp(-\sum_{k=1}^{m}\tfrac{1+2\alpha}{k})\right)^{2}\\
\sim\frac{1}{n^{1-2\alpha}}\left(\sum_{m=0}^{n-1}\frac{1}{m^{1+2\alpha}}\right)^{2}\sim\frac{1}{n^{1-2\alpha}}n^{-4\alpha}=\frac{1}{n^{1+2\alpha}}.

Thus (
2.11
) holds also if
\alpha\leq 0
, completing the proof.

3.
Transition probabilities and the marginal laws

Our first goal is to define a family of Markov kernels

\{\mathfrak{p}_{s,t}(x,{\textnormal{d}}y):s<t\}
which will serve as the transition probabilities. These kernels will be chosen from the orthogonality measures of the continuous dual Hahn polynomials and depend on a real parameter
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{c}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}
.
A subtle point in the construction is that due to restriction (
2.2
) these orthogonality measures cannot be defined for all real
x
, and the excluded set of
x
’s depends on the value of
s
. This leads to a rarely considered case of Markov processes with a ”time-dependent” state space, as in
[
9
, Sections 9, 10]
.

For
s\in{\mathbb{R}}
, we introduce a family of sets

(3.1)

E_{s}=\begin{cases}[-(\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{c}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}-s)^{2},\infty)&s\leq\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{c}\color[rgb]{0,0,0},\\
\{-(\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{c}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}-s+N)^{2}:N=0,1,\dots,\;N<s-\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{c}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\}\cup[0,\infty)&s>\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{c}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}.\end{cases}

The orthogonality measures of the continuous dual Hahn polynomials allow us to define measures
\mathfrak{p}_{s,t}(x,{\textnormal{d}}y)
at spatial locations
x\in E_{s}
.
We will extend artificially our definition to
x\not\in E_{s}
, but as in
[
9
]
, the resulting Markov process will really be defined on the product of the sets
E_{s}
.
Thus sets
E_{s}
play an important role in the construction of the Markov family, and will appear in several statements below.

For
-\infty<s<t<\infty
, we define the family of Markov kernels by inserting times
s,t
and location
x
into the parameters
of the orthogonality measure introduced in formula (
2.9
).
Let

(3.2)

\mathfrak{p}_{s,t}(x,{\textnormal{d}}y):=\begin{cases}\nu({\textnormal{d}}y|\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{c}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}-t,t-s-{\textnormal{i}}\sqrt{x},t-s+{\textnormal{i}}\sqrt{x})&x\in E_{s},\;x\geq 0,\\
\nu({\textnormal{d}}y|\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{c}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}-t,t-s-\sqrt{-x},t-s+\sqrt{-x})&x\in E_{s},\;x<0,\\
\delta_{-(c-t)^{2}}({\textnormal{d}}y)&x\not\in E_{s}.\end{cases}

We need to verify that these probability measures are well defined and that their supports are contained in the corresponding sets
E_{t}
.

Proposition 3.1
.

Probability measures (
3.2
) are well defined for all
-\infty<s<t<\infty
.
Furthermore,

(3.3)

\mathfrak{p}_{s,t}(x,E_{t})=1.

Proof.

It is clear that the conclusion holds if
x\not\in E_{s}
, so we only need to consider
x\in E_{s}
.
To verify that the probability measure is well defined, we analyze the factors in product (
2.2
) with
\beta_{n}=A_{n-1}C_{n}
. With parameters as specified in (
3.2
), from (
2.4
) we get

(3.4)

A_{n}C_{n+1}=(n+1)(n+2(t-s))\left((\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{c}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}+n-s)^{2}+x\right).

First, consider the boundary case
x=-(\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{c}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}-s)^{2}
. Then
A_{0}C_{1}=0
, so by Theorem
2.1
measure
\mathfrak{p}_{s,t}(x,{\textnormal{d}}y)
is concentrated at the root
\alpha\beta+\alpha\gamma+\beta\gamma=-(\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{c}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}-t)^{2}
of polynomial (
2.6
).
We get
\mathfrak{p}_{s,t}(x,{\textnormal{d}}y)=\delta_{-(\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{c}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}-t)^{2}}({\textnormal{d}}y)
. In particular, we have
\mathfrak{p}_{s,t}(x,E_{t})=1
.

Next, consider the non-boundary cases
x\in E_{s}
with
x\geq 0
. Then products (
3.4
) are strictly positive, so measure
\mathfrak{p}_{s,t}(x,{\textnormal{d}}y)
is well defined and has infinite support (except for the already considered boundary case of
x=0
,
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{c}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}=s
). In (
3.2
), we have measure
\nu({\textnormal{d}}y|\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{c}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}-t,t-s+{\textnormal{i}}\sqrt{x},t-s-{\textnormal{i}}\sqrt{x})
with the complex-conjugate pair of parameters with positive real part
t-s
. From
[
11
, Section 1.3]
we see that

\mathfrak{p}_{s,t}(x,{\textnormal{d}}y)
has absolutely continuous component with a density supported on
(0,\infty)
, and if

t>\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{c}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}
then in addition to the absolutely continuous component, measure
\mathfrak{p}_{s,t}(x,{\textnormal{d}}y)
has also a discrete component with atoms at points
y_{k}=-(\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{c}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}-t+k)^{2}
for
k=0,1,\dots
such that
t>\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{c}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}+k
. Thus
\mathfrak{p}_{s,t}(x,E_{t})=1
.

Finally, we consider the non-boundary cases with
x\in E_{s}
such that
-(\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{c}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}-s)^{2}<x<0
.

(A)

If
s<\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{c}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}
, then products (
3.4
) are positive, as
A_{n}C_{n+1}\geq A_{0}C_{1}>0
. So measure
\mathfrak{p}_{s,t}(x,{\textnormal{d}}y)
is well defined and has infinite support. It remains to verify that
\mathfrak{p}_{s,t}(x,E_{t})=1
.

Since
x<0
, we have
x=-v^{2}
for some
0<v<\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{c}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}-s
and in (
3.2
), we have measure
\nu({\textnormal{d}}y|\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{c}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}-t,t-s+v,t-s-v)
with three real parameters.
Since
t-s+v>0
and

t-s-v>t-\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{c}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}
, at least two of the parameters of measure
\nu({\textnormal{d}}y|\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{c}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}-t,t-s+v,t-s-v)
are positive: the second positive parameter is either
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{c}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}-t>0
or
t-s-v>0
. From
[
11
, Section 1.3]
we see that

\mathfrak{p}_{s,t}(x,{\textnormal{d}}v)
has absolutely continuous component with a density supported on
(0,\infty)
and with atoms
at points
y_{k}=-(\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{c}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}-t+k)^{2}
, if
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{c}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}-t<0
, or at points
\tilde{y}_{k}=-(t-s-v+k)^{2}
if
t-s-v<0
, and then
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{c}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}-t>0
. It is clear that points
y_{k}
are in
E_{t}
. On the other hand, if
t-s-v<0
, then
\tilde{y}_{k}\geq\tilde{y}_{0}=-(v+s-t)^{2}>-(\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{c}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}-t)^{2}
so
\tilde{y}_{k}\in E_{t}
, as we have
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{c}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}>t
in this case.

(B)

If
s>\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{c}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}
and
x\in E_{s}
, then
x=-(\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{c}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}-s+N)^{2}
for some
N
such that
s>\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{c}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}+N
.
We see that (
3.4
) factors as

A_{n}C_{n+1}=(n+1)(n+2(t-s))\left(2\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{c}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}+n+N-2s\right)\left(n-N\right).

Since
2\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{c}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}+n+N-2s\leq 2(\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{c}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}+N-s)<0
for
n\leq N
, the last two factors are both negative for
n=0,1,\dots,N-1
, and
A_{N}C_{N+1}=0
. So the products (
2.2
) are positive and then 0.
By Theorem
2.1
, measure
\mathfrak{p}_{s,t}(x,{\textnormal{d}}y)
is atomic with
N+1
atoms at the roots of its
(N+1)
-th orthogonal polynomial
p_{N+1}(y|\alpha,\beta,\gamma)
as written in (
3.12
), which in view of (
3.15
) factors as

Q_{N+1}(y;x,t,s)=(\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{c}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}-t-\sqrt{-y},\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{c}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}-t+\sqrt{-y})_{N+1}=\prod_{k=0}^{N}((\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{c}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}-t+k)^{2}+y).

The roots of this polynomial are
-(\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{c}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}-t)^{2},-(\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{c}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}-t+1)^{2},\dots,-(\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{c}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}-t+N)^{2}
and they lie in
E_{t}
.

\sqcap\kern-8.0pt\hbox{$\sqcup$}

Remark 3.2
.

From the proof of Proposition
3.1
we note that if
x=-(\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{c}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}-s)^{2}
, then measure
\mathfrak{p}_{s,t}(x,{\textnormal{d}}y)=\delta_{-(\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{c}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}-t)^{2}}({\textnormal{d}}y)
is degenerate. As a consequence, a Markov process with transition probabilities
\mathfrak{p}_{s,t}(x,{\textnormal{d}}y)
which is at location
x\not\in E_{s}
at time
s
, will follow the parabola

b_{n,n}(x,s)=1
, and for all

we have

(3.13)

Q_{n}(y;x,t,s)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}b_{n,k}(x,s)p_{k}(y;t),\quad n=1,2,\dots

We remark that by linear independence, any set of monic polynomials can be expressed as a unique linear combination of the polynomials
p_{k}(y;t)
,
k=0,1,\dots
. The main point of Lemma

is that the coefficients of the linear combination (

Proof.

From (

p_{n}(y;t)=(-1)^{n}(\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{a}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}+\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{c}\color[rgb]{0,0,0},\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{b}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}+\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{c}\color[rgb]{0,0,0})_{n}\,{}_{3}F_{2}\left(-n,\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{c}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}-t-\sqrt{-y},\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{c}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}-t+\sqrt{-y};\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{a}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}+\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{c}\color[rgb]{0,0,0},\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{b}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}+\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{c}\color[rgb]{0,0,0};1\right)

is a linear combination of the linearly independent monic polynomials

(3.14)

(\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{c}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}-t-\sqrt{-y},\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{c}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}-t+\sqrt{-y})_{k}=\prod_{j=0}^{k-1}((\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{c}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}-t+j)^{2}+y),\quad k=0,1,\dots,n

in variable

. From (

(-1)^{n}\frac{(\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{a}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}+\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{c}\color[rgb]{0,0,0},\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{b}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}+\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{c}\color[rgb]{0,0,0})_{n}(-n)_{k}}{k!(\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{a}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}+\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{c}\color[rgb]{0,0,0},\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{b}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}+\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{c}\color[rgb]{0,0,0})_{k}},\quad k=0,1,\dots,n,

do not depend on

. By Assumption

This means that polynomials (

with the coefficients that do not depend on

.
Since

Q_{n}(y;x,t,s)=\;_{3}F_{2}\left(-n,\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{c}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}-t-\sqrt{-y},\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{c}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}-t+\sqrt{-y};\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{c}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}-s-\sqrt{-x},\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{c}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}-s+\sqrt{-x};1\right)\\
\cdot(-1)^{n}(\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{c}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}-s-\sqrt{-x},\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{c}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}-s+\sqrt{-x})_{n},

from (

), with
the coefficients

(-1)^{n}\frac{(-n)_{k}(\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{c}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}-s-\sqrt{-x},\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{c}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}-s+\sqrt{-x})_{n}}{k!(\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{c}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}-s-\sqrt{-x},\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathsf{c}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}-s+\sqrt{-x})_{k}},\quad k=0,1,\dots,n

The fact that

is just a consequence of the fact that both sets of polynomials are monic.

\sqcap\kern-8.0pt\hbox{$\sqcup$}

We note that
Q_{n}(x;x,s,s)=0
for
n\geq 1
. To see this, as in
[
5

, we use
the three step recurrence relation (
2.5
) to verify that
Q_{1}(x;x,s,s)=0
,
Q_{2}(x;x,s,s)=0
, and then automatically (
2.5

Q_{n}(x;x,s,s)=0
for all

.

From (

), applied to each term in
Q_{n}(y;x,t,s)=Q_{n}(y;x,t,s)-Q_{n}(x;x,s,s)
, after canceling the first term with
p_{0}(y;t)=1
, we get

(3.16)

Q_{n}(y;x,t,s)=\sum_{k=1}^{n}b_{n,k}(x,s)(p_{k}(y;t)-p_{k}(x;s)),\quad n=1,2,\dots

Formula (
3.16
) immediately implies that
\{p_{n}(x;s)\}
are in fact orthogonal martingale polynomials for the Markov process
(\mathbf{T}_{s})
. This implication is known, see
[
5
, Proposition 3.6]
but we include proof for completeness.

Proposition 3.8
.

If
x\in E_{s}
, then

(3.17)

\int_{\mathbb{R}}p_{n}(y;t)\mathfrak{p}_{s,t}(x,{\textnormal{d}}y)=p_{n}(x;s).

Proof.

The proof is by induction on
n
.
Trivially, (
3.17
) holds for
n=0
, as
p_{0}(x;s)=1
. For the induction step, suppose that the martingale property (
3.17
) holds for polynomials

p_{k}(x;t)
with
k\leq n-1
, where
n\geq 1
. Since for
x\in E_{s}

polynomials
Q_{n}(y;x,t,s)
and
Q_{0}(y;x,t,s)\equiv 1
are orthogonal,
from (
3.16
)
we get

0=\int_{\mathbb{R}}Q_{n}(y;x,t,s)\mathfrak{p}_{s,t}(x,{\textnormal{d}}y)\\
=\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}b_{n,k}(x,s)\int_{\mathbb{R}}(p_{k}(y;t)-p_{k}(x;s))\mathfrak{p}_{s,t}(x,{\textnormal{d}}y)+b_{n,n}(x,s)\int_{\mathbb{R}}(p_{n}(y;t)-p_{n}(x;s))\mathfrak{p}_{s,t}(x,{\textnormal{d}}y)\\
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{proj-mart}}}{{=}}0+\int_{\mathbb{R}}(p_{n}(y;t)-p_{n}(x;s))\mathfrak{p}_{s,t}(x,{\textnormal{d}}y).

where we used the induction assumption for all the terms with
k\leq n-1
and we used
b_{n,n}(x,s)=1
in the last term. Thus

\int_{\mathbb{R}}(p_{n}(y;t)-p_{n}(x;s))\mathfrak{p}_{s,t}(x,{\textnormal{d}}y)=0
, which ends the proof by induction.

\sqcap\kern-8.0pt\hbox{$\sqcup$}

We remark that for
x\not\in{\mathbb{E}}_{s}
, polynomials

do not have to be orthogonal, so (
3.17
) may fail if
x\not\in E_{s}
.

Proof of Theorem
3.5
.

We note that (
3.9

To prove that (

as
\mathfrak{p}_{s,t}(x,E_{t})=1
.
Then for

, we have

(3.18)

\int_{\mathbb{R}}Q_{n}(z;x,u,s)\mu({\textnormal{d}}z)=0.

Indeed,

\int_{\mathbb{R}}Q_{n}(z;x,u,s)\mu({\textnormal{d}}z)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}Q_{n}(z;x,u,s)\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathfrak{p}_{s,t}(x,{\textnormal{d}}y)\mathfrak{p}_{t,u}(y,{\textnormal{d}}z)\\

\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{Q2p+}}}{{=}}\sum_{k=1}^{n}b_{n,k}(x,s)\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathfrak{p}_{s,t}(x,{\textnormal{d}}y)\int_{\mathbb{R}}(p_{k}(z;u)-p_{k}(x;s))\mathfrak{p}_{t,u}(y,{\textnormal{d}}z)\\
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{P(Et)=1}}}{{=}}\sum_{k=1}^{n}b_{n,k}(x,s)\int_{E_{t}}\mathfrak{p}_{s,t}(x,{\textnormal{d}}y)\int_{\mathbb{R}}(p_{k}(z;u)-p_{k}(x;s))\mathfrak{p}_{t,u}(y,{\textnormal{d}}z)\\
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{proj-mart}}}{{=}}\sum_{k=1}^{n}b_{n,k}(x,s)\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathfrak{p}_{s,t}(x,{\textnormal{d}}y)(p_{k}(y;t)-p_{k}(x;s))\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{proj-mart}}}{{=}}0,

where we used martingale property first at

, and then again at

It is well known, see e.g.

For

Proof of Theorem

Lemma 5.4
.

Fix
k=1,2,\dots
. Suppose that
\varphi(x,y)
is a polynomial such that for all
n=0,1,\dots
we have

(5.19)

{\mathbb{E}}[Y_{t}^{k}q_{n}(Y_{u};u)|Y_{s}]={\mathbb{E}}[\varphi(Y_{s},Y_{u})q_{n}(Y_{u};u)|Y_{s}].

Then

{\mathbb{E}}[Y_{t}^{k}|Y_{s},Y_{u}]=\varphi(Y_{s},Y_{u}).

Proof.

By Dynkin’s
\pi-\lambda
lemma, it is enough to show that for any pair of bounded measurable functions
f,g:{\mathbb{R}}\to[0,\infty)
we have

(5.20)

{\mathbb{E}}[f(Y_{s})Y_{t}^{k}g(Y_{u})]={\mathbb{E}}[f(Y_{s})g(Y_{u})\varphi(Y_{s},Y_{u})].

By our assumption, (
5.20
) holds if
g(Y_{u})
is replaced by a polynomial
p(Y_{u})
, a linear combination of the polynomials
\{q_{n}(Y_{u};u):n=0,1,\dots\}
.

By Lemma
2.2
, the law of
Y_{u}
is determined by moments. Recall that for a probability measure determined by moments,
polynomials are dense in
L_{2}
,
[
1
, Corollary 2.3.3]
.
So if
g
is an arbitrary bounded measurable function, then
for any
\varepsilon>0
, there exists a polynomial
p
such that

{\mathbb{E}}[|g(Y_{u})-p(Y_{u})|^{2}]<\varepsilon^{2}
. Since (
5.20
) holds for the polynomial
p
, the difference between the left hand side and the right hand side of (
5.20
) is arbitrarily small. Indeed,

\left|{\mathbb{E}}[f(Y_{s})Y_{t}^{k}g(Y_{u})]-{\mathbb{E}}[f(Y_{s})g(Y_{u})\varphi(Y_{s},Y_{u})]\right|\\
\leq|{\mathbb{E}}[f(Y_{s})Y_{t}^{k}(g(Y_{u})-p(Y_{u}))]|+|{\mathbb{E}}[f(Y_{s})\left(g(Y_{u})-p(Y_{u})\right)\varphi(Y_{s},Y_{u})]|.

By the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, the first term can be bounded by

|({\mathbb{E}}[f^{2}(Y_{s})Y_{t}^{2k}])^{1/2}({\mathbb{E}}[|g(Y_{u})-p(Y_{u})|^{2}])^{1/2}\leq({\mathbb{E}}[f^{2}(Y_{s})Y_{t}^{2k}])^{1/2}\varepsilon,

and similarly the second term is at most

({\mathbb{E}}[f^{2}(Y_{s})\varphi^{2}(Y_{s},Y_{u})])^{1/2}(E[|g(Y_{u})-p(Y_{u})|^{2}])^{1/2}\leq({\mathbb{E}}[f^{2}(Y_{s})\varphi^{2}(Y_{s},Y_{u})])^{1/2}\varepsilon.

Since
\varepsilon>0
is arbitrary, (
5.20
) follows.

Proof of (

We use Lemma

To verify (
5.23
), we use the vector form of the martingale property, component-wise.
That is, we write martingale identity

{\mathbb{E}}[Y_{t}q_{n}(Y_{u};u)|Y_{s}]={\mathbb{E}}\left[Y_{t}{\mathbb{E}}[q_{n}(Y_{u};u)|Y_{t}]\middle|Y_{s}\right]={\mathbb{E}}[Y_{t}q_{n}(Y_{t};t)|Y_{s}]

{\mathbb{E}}[Y_{t}\vec{q}(Y_{u};u)|Y_{s}]={\mathbb{E}}[Y_{t}\vec{q}(Y_{t};t)|Y_{s}]={\mathbb{E}}[\vec{q}(Y_{t};t){\boldsymbol{J}}(t)|Y_{s}]={\mathbb{E}}[\vec{q}(Y_{t};t)|Y_{s}]{\boldsymbol{J}}(t)=\vec{q}(Y_{s};s){\boldsymbol{J}}(t).

Similarly, we have

{\mathbb{E}}[Y_{u}\vec{q}(Y_{u};u)|Y_{s}]=\vec{q}(Y_{s};s){\boldsymbol{J}}(u).

Since
Y_{s}\vec{q}(Y_{s};s)=\vec{q}(Y_{s};s){\boldsymbol{J}}(s)

we see that formula (
5.23
), is a consequence of a simple algebraic identity

{\boldsymbol{J}}(t)=\frac{u-t}{u-s}{\boldsymbol{J}}(s)+\frac{t-s}{u-s}{\boldsymbol{J}}(u),

left-multiplied by
\vec{q}(Y_{s};s)
. Identity (

) holds as

.
