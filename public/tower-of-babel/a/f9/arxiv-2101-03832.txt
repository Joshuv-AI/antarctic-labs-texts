Abstract
. We study the distribution of eigenvalues of almost-Hermitian random matrices associated with the classical Gaussian and Laguerre unitary ensembles. In the almost-Hermitian setting, which was pioneered by Fyodorov, Khoruzhenko and Sommers in the case of GUE, the eigenvalues are not confined to the real axis, but instead have imaginary parts which vary within a narrow “band” about the real line, of height proportional to 1 N \tfrac{1}{N} , where N N denotes the size of the matrices. We study vertical cross-sections of the 1-point density as well as microscopic scaling limits, and we compare with other results which have appeared in the literature in recent years. Our approach uses Ward’s equation and a property which we call “cross-section convergence”, which relates the large- N N limit of the cross-sections of the density of eigenvalues with the equilibrium density for the corresponding Hermitian ensemble: the semi-circle law for GUE and the Marchenko-Pastur law for LUE. As an application of our approach, we prove the bulk universality of the almost-circular ensembles.

1.
Introduction

This note is the result of investigations of eigenvalue ensembles in the almost-Hermitian regime. In particular we study almost-Hermitian counterparts of the Gaussian Unitary Ensemble and of the Laguerre Unitary Ensemble (in the singular case), which we call

Almost-Hermitian GUE (AGUE)
and
Almost-Hermitian LUE (ALUE)
, respectively.

Almost-Hermitian (or “weakly non-Hermitian”) random matrices were introduced by Fyodorov, Khoruzhenko, and Sommers in the papers
[
31
,
32
,
33
]
. Their work concerns eigenvalues of elliptic Ginibre matrices where the droplets collapse to the interval
[-2,2]
at a suitable rate. We take this as our model
for almost-Hermitian GUE. The emergent
structure is physically interesting, and has been subject of several investigations in recent years, see in particular
[
6
]
and the references there.

As is to be expected, the AGUE tends to follow Wigner’s semi-circle law, and the ALUE tends to follow the Marchenko-Pastur law.
However, in the almost-Hermitian regime,
the particles are not confined to the real axis,
but instead vary randomly within a thin band about
\mathbb{R}
of height proportional to
\tfrac{1}{N}
, where
N
denotes the size of the matrices. See Figure
1
.

In both cases, the droplets are similar, highly eccentric elliptic discs.
Yet, the particle distributions are quite different, with heavy clustering going on
near the left edge for ALUE, in contrast with the much more evenly spread configurations for AGUE (compare Figure
5
). This reflects the singular behaviour of the critical Marchenko-Pastur density.

On the other hand, we shall prove that there is a universal microscopic behaviour in the bulk, given by a kernel found in
[
31
,
33
]
, which interpolates between
the sine-kernel and the Ginibre kernel.

We shall introduce a class of bandlimited ensembles in the interface between dimensions 1 and 2, which we use to formulate and prove certain theorems of a general character. However, ultimately, we shall resort to asymptotic properties of the respective orthogonal polynomials (Hermite and Laguerre) in order to deduce full universality
results.

Our microscopic analysis uses the theory for Ward’s equation from the paper
[
14
]
. In particular, we shall find a close connection between the scaling limits in
[
6
,
31
,
33
]
and the translation invariant solutions to Ward’s equation, which are characterized in
[
14
]
. (We also refer to a recent work
[
5
]
for an implementation of Ward’s equation in the study of translation invariant scaling limits of planar symplectic ensembles.)

Notational conventions.

A typical point in the complex plane
\mathbb{C}
is denoted
\zeta=\xi+i\eta
.

We write
{\partial}=\tfrac{1}{2}(\tfrac{{\partial}}{{\partial}\xi}-i\tfrac{{\partial}}{{\partial}\eta})
and
\bar{\partial}=\tfrac{1}{2}(\tfrac{{\partial}}{{\partial}\xi}+i\tfrac{{\partial}}{{\partial}\eta})
for the complex derivatives and

\Delta={\partial}\bar{\partial}=\tfrac{1}{4}(\tfrac{{\partial}^{2}}{{\partial}\xi^{2}}+\tfrac{{\partial}^{2}}{{\partial}\eta^{2}})
for
\tfrac{1}{4}
times the usual Laplacian.

We write
dA(\zeta)=\tfrac{1}{\pi}\,d^{\,2}\,\zeta=\tfrac{1}{\pi}\,d\xi\,d\eta
for
\tfrac{1}{\pi}
times Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{C}
and

dA_{N}=(dA)^{\otimes N}
for the corresponding volume measure in
\mathbb{C}^{N}
.
When
\mu
is a measure and
f
is a function, we write
\mu(f)
for
\int f\,d\mu
.

1.1.
Purpose and aims

The theory of almost-Hermitian ensembles consists at this point largely of a number of important model cases, which are typically studied using properties of specific orthogonal polynomials.
One could say that the present work grew out of a desire to classify and provide a “unifying structure” to this theory.

The first question is how to suitably define
a general class of “almost-Hermitian ensembles” which has desired potential theoretic properties. This question does not depend on a determinantal structure, and thus is more natural to study
in a context of
\beta
-ensembles. We shall isolate a suitable class of potentials and establish several new results concerning convergence to the equilibrium. This convergence forms the starting
point for our further analysis of almost-Hermitian ensembles. (This approach is analogous with established practices in dimensions 1 and 2, and could in that context be viewed as a first step towards a Gaussian field theory in the almost-Hermitian case).

We also study (and survey) various types of microscopic limits in the almost-Hermitian regime, which typically interpolate between well-known limits in dimensions 1 and 2. In our approach using the
loop equation and compactness, questions about particular scaling limits are reduced to easier questions, such as establishing their apriori translation invariance.

That being said, with the exception of the so-called “almost-circular ensembles” below, establishing translation invariance is in general a subtle matter. While one might expect that translation invariance should hold quite generally in the bulk, we will not resolve that issue here. (The problem that arises concerns uniqueness of solution to Ward’s equation given certain apriori conditions, and is closely related with the one discussed in
[
14
]
.)

Instead of striving for largest possible generality, we take a detailed look at the model cases, where the analysis is already non-trivial and offers new angles. Apart from leading to new proofs of results which were rigorously established only fairly recently, our
analysis has some new ingredients that have led to spin-offs in for example
[
5
]
.

For comparison, the universality problem in the normal matrix setting was settled only recently in a fairly general setting in
[
36
]
; the techniques used there break down in the almost-Hermitian setting. (For example, the conformal mappings of the exterior of a thin droplet to the exterior disc have large derivatives at the end-points: the derivatives tend to infinity as the droplets collapse to an interval.)

1.2.
General setup

Given an
N
-point configuration
\{\zeta_{j}\}_{1}^{N}\subset\mathbb{C}
and a suitable function (external potential)
Q_{N}:\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
, we associate the total energy

(1.1)

H_{N}=\sum_{j\neq k}\log\frac{1}{|\zeta_{j}-\zeta_{k}|}+N\sum_{j=1}^{N}Q_{N}(\zeta_{j}).

We next fix a positive parameter
\beta
and form
the canonical Gibbs measure

(1.2)

\displaystyle d{\mathbf{P}}_{N}^{\beta}=\tfrac{1}{Z_{N}^{\beta}}e^{-\beta H_{N}}\,dA_{N},\qquad(Z_{N}^{\beta}=\int_{\mathbb{C}^{N}}e^{-\beta\cdot H_{N}}\,dA_{N}).

We assume that
the potentials
Q_{N}
increase monotonically, as
N\to\infty
, to an external potential
V
which obeys

V(\zeta)=+\infty
when
\zeta\not\in\mathbb{R}
.

Here and throughout, “external potential” means a lower semicontinuous function
W:\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
which is finite on some set of positive capacity and satisfies

(1.3)

\liminf_{\zeta\to\infty}\tfrac{W(\zeta)}{2\log|\zeta|}\geq k

where
k>1
is a fixed constant. We shall assume that this holds, with the same
k
, for all
W=Q_{N}
and for
W=V
.
Then, to a compactly supported Borel probability measure
\mu
on
\mathbb{C}
, we associate the logarithmic
W
-energy

(1.4)

I_{W}[\mu]=\iint_{\mathbb{C}^{2}}(\log\tfrac{1}{|\zeta-\eta|})\,d\mu(\zeta)\,d\mu(\eta)+\mu(W).

In addition to (
1.3
), we shall sometimes assume the extra growth condition

(1.5)

W(\zeta)\geq(1+\delta)\log(1+|\zeta|^{2})-C\qquad\zeta\in\mathbb{C}

for some small
\delta>0
and
C\in\mathbb{R}
.

By
[
51
, Theorem I.1.3]
, there exists a unique equilibrium measure
\sigma_{W}
, i.e., a compactly supported Borel probability measure which minimizes the
energy (
1.4
). The support
S_{W}=\operatorname{supp}\sigma_{W}
is called the
droplet
in external potential
W
.

We shall always assume that each
Q_{N}
is smooth in a neighbourhood of the droplet
S_{Q_{N}}
, except possibly for some singular points at which
\Delta Q_{N}=+\infty
. The singularities are
assumed to be benign in the sense that the basic structure theorem for equilibrium measures in
[
51
, Theorem II.1.3]
applies. Namely, the equilibrium measure
\sigma_{Q_{N}}
is absolutely continuous with respect to
dA
and

d\sigma_{Q_{N}}=\Delta Q_{N}\cdot\mathbf{1}_{S_{Q_{N}}}\,dA.

Finally, we assume that the equilibrium measure
\sigma_{V}
, which is supported in
\mathbb{R}
(and equals to the weak limit of the measures
\sigma_{Q_{N}}
, see Subsection
2.1
), is
absolutely continuous with respect to Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{R}
. By slight of abuse of notation, we denote its linear density by
\sigma_{V}(\xi)
(so
d\sigma_{V}(\xi)=\sigma_{V}(\xi)\,d\xi
).
The probability density
\sigma_{V}(\xi)
is assumed to be continuous on
\mathbb{R}
, again with the possible exception of finitely many singular points.

1.3.
Limiting droplet and cross-sections

Denote by
\{\zeta_{j}\}_{1}^{N}
a random sample with respect to the Boltzmann-Gibbs measure (
1.2
), and write the 1-point function as

\mathbf{R}_{N}^{\,\beta}(\zeta)=\lim_{{\varepsilon}\to 0}\frac{\mathbf{E}_{N}^{\,\beta}(\#(\{\zeta_{j}\}_{1}^{N}\cap D(\zeta,{\varepsilon})))}{{\varepsilon}^{2}}.

(
D(\zeta,{\varepsilon})
is the open disc with center
\zeta
and radius
{\varepsilon}
.)

Now fix an arbitrary zooming-point
p\in\mathbb{R}
and define the blow-up map

(1.6)

\Gamma_{N,p}:\zeta\mapsto z,\qquad z=\Gamma_{N,p}(\zeta)={\scriptstyle\sqrt{N\Delta Q_{N}(p)}}\cdot(\zeta-p).

Let
\{z_{j}\}_{1}^{N}
be the rescaled sample,
z_{j}=\Gamma_{N,p}(\zeta_{j})
.

The potentials
Q_{N}
used in this note have the property that the Laplacian
\Delta Q_{N}(p)
is proportional to
N
.
More precisely, we shall assume throughout that the limit

(1.7)

\rho(p)=\lim_{N\to\infty}\sqrt{\tfrac{N}{\Delta Q_{N}(p)}}

is well-defined and finite for each
p\in S_{V}
.
We next form the function

(1.8)

a(p)=\tfrac{\pi}{2}\cdot\rho(p)\cdot\sigma_{V}(p),\qquad p\in\mathbb{R}

with the understanding that
a(p)=0
if
p\not\in S_{V}
.
The function (
1.8
) has the following
geometric interpretation: let

(1.9)

\gamma_{N}(p)=\Gamma_{N,p}(S_{Q_{N}})\cap(i\mathbb{R})

be the cross-section of the rescaled droplet with the imaginary axis. If we impose the symmetry
Q_{N}(\bar{\zeta})=Q_{N}(\zeta)
, and some other natural conditions (Subsection
2.1
), the number
a(p)
represents the
limiting height of the rescaled cross-section
, i.e.,

(1.10)

a(p)=\tfrac{1}{2}\lim_{N\to\infty}|\gamma_{N}(p)|,\qquad p\in\mathbb{R}.

We shall also use the
statistical
cross-sections
c_{N}^{\beta}
of the ensemble,

(1.11)

c_{N}^{\beta}(p)=\tfrac{1}{N}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathbf{R}_{N}^{\beta}(p+i\eta)\,d\eta,\qquad p\in\mathbb{R}.

A fairly general convergence result, asserting that (under some additional assumptions)
c_{N}^{\beta}\to\pi\sigma_{V}
in the sense of measures on
\mathbb{R}
, is given in Theorem
2.2
below.

The
1
-point function of the rescaled system
\{z_{j}\}_{1}^{N}
is denoted by

(1.12)

R_{N}^{\,\beta}(z)=\tfrac{1}{N\Delta Q_{N}(p)}\mathbf{R}_{N}^{\,\beta}(\zeta),\qquad z=\Gamma_{N}(\zeta).

We are interested in describing all possible limits
R^{\,\beta}=\lim_{N\to\infty}R_{N}^{\,\beta}
, and we shall here restrict to the
determinantal case when
\beta=1
. We shall therefore in the sequel drop the superscript, writing
R_{N}
in place of
R_{N}^{\,1}
, etc.

We recall the following fact from the theory of determinantal Coulomb gas processes (see e.g.
[
15
, Lemma 1]
and the references there).

Lemma 1.1
.

If
R_{N}\to R
in
L^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}
(along some subsequence) then
\{z_{j}\}_{1}^{N}
converges (along the same subsequence) in the sense of point fields to a unique infinite determinantal
point-field
\{z_{j}\}_{1}^{\infty}
with
1
-point function
R
.

With these preliminaries out of the way, we turn to our main objects of study.

1.4.
Almost-Hermitian GUE

Fix a positive parameter
c
and consider the sequence of potentials

(1.13)

Q_{N}(\zeta)=\tfrac{1}{2}\xi^{\,2}+\tfrac{1}{2}\tfrac{N}{c^{2}}\eta^{\,2},\qquad(\zeta=\xi+i\eta).

The droplet in potential (
1.13
) can be found by means of the following useful lemma from
[
35
,
53
]
. (The lemma has an alternative proof by solving
an obstacle problem, which may be left for the interested reader.)

Lemma 1.2
.

The droplet
S_{Q}
in potential
Q=a\xi^{2}+b\eta^{2}
is the elliptic disc with equation

\tfrac{a^{2}+ab}{2b}\,\xi^{\,2}+\tfrac{ab+b^{2}}{2a}\,\eta^{2}\leq 1.

Using the lemma, we find that the droplet in potential (
1.13
) is given by

(1.14)

S_{Q_{N}}=\{\,\xi+i\eta\,;\,(1+\tfrac{c^{2}}{N})\xi^{2}+\tfrac{N}{c^{2}}(1+\tfrac{N}{c^{2}})\eta^{2}\leq 4\,\}.

Note that
Q_{N}\nearrow V
where
V
is the Gaussian potential

(1.15)

V(\xi)=\tfrac{1}{2}\xi^{\,2},\qquad(\xi\in\mathbb{R}),

with the understanding that
V=+\infty
on
\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb{R}
. The equilibrium measure in potential
V
is Wigner’s semi-circle law (
[
27
, Section 1.4]
),

(1.16)

\sigma_{\mathrm{sc}}(\xi)=\tfrac{1}{2\pi}\sqrt{4-\xi^{\,2}}\cdot\mathbf{1}_{[-2,2]}(\xi).

Let
c_{N}(\xi)=\tfrac{1}{N}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathbf{R}_{N}(\xi+i\eta)\,d\eta
be the
N
:th cross-section (with
\beta=1
). The
following global result can be found in
[
6
, Theorem 3 (a)]
(cf.
[
32
,
44
]
) and will here be reproved by different methods.

Theorem 1.3
.

(“
Pointwise cross-section convergence for AGUE
”)
c_{N}(\xi)\to\pi\cdot\sigma_{\mathrm{sc}}(\xi)
as
N\to\infty
for each
\xi
with
-2<\xi<2
.

Remark
.

We shall use Theorem
1.3
as a basic tool for our microscopic investigations. In fact the pointwise convergence
\tfrac{1}{\pi}c_{N}(\xi)\to\sigma_{\mathrm{sc}}(\xi)
for
-2<\xi<2
is precisely what we need to
uniquely fix a translation invariant bulk scaling limit using our method below. This contrasts with the strategy in
[
6
]
, where convergence of cross-sections is obtained as a consequence
of microscopic investigations.

Following the terminology in
[
14
]
, we put

\gamma(z)=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-\frac{1}{2}z^{2}}
.
Given a Borel subset (or “window”)
E\subset\mathbb{R}
we then consider the entire function

(1.17)

\gamma*\mathbf{1}_{E}(z)=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{E}e^{-\frac{1}{2}(z-t)^{2}}\,dt.

Now fix a point
p_{*}\in S_{V}
in the bulk, i.e.,
-2<p_{*}<2
, and let
R_{N}
be the
1
-point function of the rescaled process about
p_{*}
.

Note that for the potential (
1.13
), the numbers
\rho(p_{*})
and
a(p_{*})
in (
1.7
), (
1.10
) reduce to

(1.18)

\rho(p_{*})=\sqrt{\tfrac{N}{\Delta Q_{N}}}=2c,\qquad a(p_{*})=\pi\cdot c\cdot\sigma_{\mathrm{sc}}(p_{*}).

The following theorem is equivalent with the bulk scaling limits found in
[
6
,
31
,

]
.

Theorem 1.4
.

(“
Bulk scaling limit for AGUE
”) Under the above assumptions,
R_{N}
converges locally uniformly to the limit
R(z)=F(2\operatorname{Im}z)
where

F(z)=\gamma*\mathbf{1}_{(-2a,2a)}(z)
and
a=a(p_{*})
is given by (
1.18
).

c=1

(b)

c=1.5

(c)

c=2

We now discuss how the scaling limits in Theorem
1.4
interpolate between the sine-kernel and the Ginibre kernel.

We recall (e.g.
[
14
]
) that the Ginibre kernel is the function

(1.19)

G(z,w)=e^{z\bar{w}-\frac{1}{2}|z|^{2}-\frac{1}{2}|w|^{2}},

and that its associated 1-point function
R(z)=G(z,z)=1
is characteristic for the infinite Ginibre ensemble.

Now let
F=\gamma*\mathbf{1}_{(-2a,2a)}
be the entire function appearing in Theorem
1.4

and put

(1.20)

K(z,w)=G(z,w)\cdot F(\tfrac{z-\bar{w}}{i})=G(z,w)\cdot\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{-2a}^{\,2a}e^{\frac{1}{2}(z-\bar{w}-it)^{2}}\,dt.

The 1-point function
R
appearing in Theorem
1.4
is then
R(z)=F(2\operatorname{Im}z)=K(z,z),

and
K
is a correlation kernel for the limiting point field
\{z_{j}\}_{1}^{\infty}
.

If in (
1.20
) we pass to the limit
c\to\infty
, we find that the kernel
K
converges to the Ginibre kernel.
Similarly, the limit
c\to 0
gives the well-known sine-kernel process on
\mathbb{R}
, but to see this we need a further rescaling.
Given the limiting point-field
\{z_{j}\}_{1}^{\infty}
we form a new one
\{\tilde{z}_{j}\}_{1}^{\infty}
by

\tilde{z}_{j}=\alpha\cdot z_{j}
, where
\alpha=\tfrac{2}{\pi}a(p_{*}).
The correlation kernel of

\{\tilde{z}_{j}\}_{1}^{\infty}
is

\displaystyle\tilde{K}(\tilde{z},\tilde{w})

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{\alpha^{2}}K(z,w),\qquad\qquad[\tilde{z}=\alpha\cdot z,\,\tilde{w}=\alpha\cdot w]

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\alpha}e^{-\frac{(\operatorname{Im}\tilde{z})^{2}+(\operatorname{Im}\tilde{w})^{2}}{\alpha^{2}}}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}e^{-\frac{\alpha^{2}u^{2}}{2}+iu(\tilde{z}-\bar{\tilde{w}})}\,du.

Sending
c\to 0
(i.e.
\alpha\to 0
) we see readily that
\tilde{K}
converges to
\pi
times the sine-kernel

K^{\mathrm{sin}}(x,y)=\tfrac{1}{\pi}\tfrac{\sin(\pi x-\pi y)}{x-y},\qquad(x,y\in\mathbb{R}).

This well-known convergence follows from the Gaussian approximation of the Dirac delta:

\frac{1}{|a|\sqrt{\pi}}e^{-(x/a)^{2}}\to\delta(x),\qquad\text{as }a\to 0,

see e.g.
[
6
, Remark 4.(c)]
and
[
9
, Section 2.3]
.

It could be said that the form of the kernel
\tilde{K}
is more natural from a one-dimensional perspective,
whereas the form of
K
in (
1.20
) is more natural from a two-dimensional one. The kernel
\tilde{K}
is of the form appearing in
[
6
]
, whereas
K
appears in the
context of planar ensembles in the papers
[
14
,
15
]
.

We now turn to edge scaling limits for the almost-Hermitian GUE. A limiting point field was found by Bender

We can now restate the main result from
[

in the following way.

Theorem 1.5
.

(“
Boundary scaling limit for modified AGUE
”)
R_{N}
converges locally uniformly to the limit

(1.22)

\displaystyle\begin{split}R(z)&=R_{(c)}(z)=\sqrt{2\pi}\,4c^{2}\,e^{\frac{4}{3}c^{6}-2(\operatorname{Im}z)^{2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{4c^{3}(u+\operatorname{Re}z)}|\operatorname{Ai}(2c(z+u)+c^{4})|^{2}\,du.\end{split}

We now discuss how (
1.22
) interpolates between the linear Airy process and the planar
\operatorname{erfc}
-process.
We start with the planar case, which corresponds to letting
c\to\infty
in (
1.22
). For this, we fix a point
z
in
\mathbb{C}\setminus(-\infty,0]

and apply the well-known asymptotic formula

(1.23)

\operatorname{Ai}z=\tfrac{1}{2\sqrt{\pi}z^{1/4}}e^{-\frac{2}{3}z^{3/2}}\cdot(1+O(\tfrac{1}{z})),\qquad(z\to\infty).

Inserting (
1.23
) in (
1.22
) and letting
c\to\infty
, one finds

\displaystyle R_{(c)}(z)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\tfrac{2}{\pi}}c^{2}e^{\frac{4}{3}c^{6}-2(\operatorname{Im}z)^{2}}

\displaystyle\times\int_{0}^{\infty}|2c(z+u)+c^{4}|^{-\frac{1}{2}}e^{4c^{3}(u+\operatorname{Re}z)}e^{-\frac{4}{3}\operatorname{Re}((2c(z+u)+c^{4})^{\frac{3}{2}})}\,du\cdot(1+O(c^{-1}))

\displaystyle\to\sqrt{\tfrac{2}{\pi}}\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-2(u+\operatorname{Re}z)^{2}}\,du\cdot(1+O(c^{-1}))=\int_{-\infty}^{0}\gamma(z+\bar{z}-t)\,dt.

Here, we have used

\tfrac{4}{3}c^{6}-2(\operatorname{Im}z)^{2}+4c^{3}(u+\operatorname{Re}z)-\tfrac{4}{3}\operatorname{Re}((2c(z+u)+c^{4})^{\frac{3}{2}})=-2(u+\operatorname{Re}z)^{2}+O(c^{-1}).

Hence as
c\to\infty
,
R_{(c)}
converges to the limit

R_{(c=\infty)}(z)=\gamma*\mathbf{1}_{(-\infty,0)}(z+\bar{z})=\tfrac{1}{2}\operatorname{erfc}(\tfrac{z+\bar{z}}{\sqrt{2}}).

This is the 1-point function which appears in random normal matrix theory after the process of rescaling about a regular boundary point of the droplet (see
[
14
]
and references).

We next consider the limit of (
1.22
) as
c\to 0
.
A standard polarization argument shows that the 1-point function
R_{(c)}
in (
1.22
) corresponds to the correlation kernel

\displaystyle\begin{split}K(z,w)&=\sqrt{2\pi}\,4c^{2}\,e^{\frac{4}{3}c^{6}-(\operatorname{Im}z)^{2}-(\operatorname{Im}w)^{2}}\\
&\times\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{2c^{3}(2u+z+\bar{w})}\operatorname{Ai}(2c\,(z+u)+c^{4})\text{Ai}(2c\,(\bar{w}+u)+c^{4})\,du.\end{split}

Now put

\tilde{z}=\alpha\cdot z
,

1.6.

We now discuss the almost-circular ensembles, a family of random normal matrices whose eigenvalues tend to be distributed within a narrow band around the unit circle of width proportional to

The terminology “almost-circular” comes from the fact that in the one-dimensional limit, this ensemble tends to the circular unitary ensemble distributed on the unit circle, cf.
[

[

for a recent work on circular

The almost-circular ensemble we consider is associated with a radially symmetric potential

Without loss of generality, we shall assume that

[

.
A typical example of such a model is the
induced Ginibre ensemble

2.
Generalities about the bandlimited Coulomb gas

In this section, we introduce and study a fairly general setup for dealing with almost-Hermitian structures. We begin by studying limiting droplets in the bandlimited regime.
After that we study statistical cross-sections by using some basic large deviation estimates.
This prompts us to impose yet more restrictive conditions on the potentials
Q_{N}
.

2.1.
The limiting droplet

Let
Q_{N}
and
V
be potentials satisfying the conditions in Subsection
1.2
. We now impose some standing assumptions.

First of all, we will assume that each
Q_{N}
is continuous in some fixed neighbourhood of
S_{V}
. We assume also that
Q_{N}
is real-analytic in this neighbourhood
with the possible exception of one (or several) singular points
at which
\Delta Q_{N}=+\infty
for each
N
(so that we include the ALUE in our setting, for example). With another very minor restriction, we assume that each droplet
S_{Q_{N}}
equals to the corresponding coincidence set
S_{Q_{N}}^{*}
for the obstacle problem (cf.
[
51
]
or Subsection
3.1
below).

In addition, we will assume that

Q_{N}(\zeta)=Q_{N}(\bar{\zeta})
and
Q_{N}(\zeta)\geq Q_{N}(\operatorname{Re}\zeta)
for all
\zeta\in\mathbb{C}
,
and that
\Delta Q_{N}\geq mN
on
S_{Q_{N}}
for some constant
m>0
.

Under these assumptions, Sakai’s regularity theorem (see
[
15
]
and references) implies that the boundaries
{\partial}S_{Q_{N}}
consist of finitely many Jordan curves, which are real analytic with the possible
exception of finitely many singular points. We shall assume that there are no singular points and that there are continuous functions
h_{N}:\mathbb{R}\to[0,\infty)
such that

S_{Q_{N}}=\{\xi+i\eta\,;\,-h_{N}(\xi)\leq\eta\leq h_{N}(\xi)\}.

Since the
Q_{N}
increase monotonically to
V
, we can apply a general convergence result in
[
51
, Theorem I.6.2]
to conclude the weak convergence in the sense of measures

\sigma_{Q_{N}}\to\sigma_{V}
as
N\to\infty
.
Moreover, since
\sigma_{Q_{N}}=\Delta Q_{N}\cdot\mathbf{1}_{S_{Q_{N}}}\,dA
we have,
for each bounded continuous function
f
, that

\tfrac{1}{\pi}\lim_{N\to\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\,d\xi\int_{-h_{N}(\xi)}^{h_{N}(\xi)}f(\xi+i\eta)\Delta Q_{N}(\xi+i\eta)\,d\eta=\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(\xi)\sigma_{V}(\xi)\,d\xi.

We next impose the condition that the limit

\rho(\xi)=\lim_{N\to\infty}\sqrt{\tfrac{N}{\Delta Q_{N}(\xi)}}

exists as a finite
and strictly positive number whenever
\sigma_{V}(\xi)>0
.
Using this assumption and the mean-value theorem, we obtain for all
\xi
with
\sigma_{V}(\xi)>0
that

(2.1)

\displaystyle\pi\cdot\sigma_{V}(\xi)

\displaystyle=\lim_{N\to\infty}\int_{-h_{N}(\xi)}^{h_{N}(\xi)}\Delta Q_{N}(\xi+i\eta)\,d\eta=\tfrac{2}{\rho(\xi)}\lim_{N\to\infty}h_{N}(\xi){\scriptstyle\sqrt{N\Delta Q_{N}(\xi)}}.

Let us particularly mention that
h_{N}(\xi)\to 0
as
N\to\infty
.

Let
\Gamma_{N,p}(\zeta)={\scriptstyle\sqrt{N\Delta Q_{N}(p)}}\cdot(\zeta-p)
be the magnification map about a point
p\in\mathbb{R}
, and consider the vertical segment
\gamma_{N}(p)=\Gamma_{N,p}(S_{Q_{N}})\cap(i\mathbb{R})
. Writing

\gamma_{N}(p)=[-a_{N}(p)i,a_{N}(p)i]
we then have

a_{N}(p)={\scriptstyle\sqrt{N\Delta Q_{N}(p)}}\cdot h_{N}(p).

In view of (
2.1
), we have shown the following theorem.

Theorem 2.1
.

(“
Asymptotic shape of the droplet
”) If
Q_{N}
satisfies the above conditions, and if
\gamma_{N}(p)=[-a_{N}(p)i,a_{N}(p)i]
then

a_{N}(p)\to a(p)
as
N\to\infty
where

a(p)=\tfrac{\pi}{2}\cdot\rho(p)\cdot\sigma_{V}(p).

In particular, the droplets
S_{Q_{N}}
are
bandlimited
in the sense that there is a constant
A
such that
|\operatorname{Im}\zeta|\leq\tfrac{A}{N}
for each
\zeta\in S_{Q_{N}}
.

2.2.
Statistical cross-sections

Recall from potential theory that the
Robin’s constant

\gamma(V)
in external potential
V
is defined by

\gamma(V)=I_{V}[\sigma_{V}]
where
I_{V}
is the energy-functional (
1.4
).
We say that a sequence
Q_{N}
of potentials obeying the conditions in Subsection
2.1
is
an
admissible sequence
if the following lower bound for the partition function holds:

(2.2)

\liminf_{N\to\infty}\tfrac{1}{N^{2}}\log Z_{N}^{\beta}\geq-\beta\cdot\gamma(V).

Theorem 2.2
.

(“
Weak cross-section convergence
”) Suppose that
(Q_{N})
is an admissible sequence and that the restriction
V|_{\mathbb{R}}
is continuous in a

\mathbb{R}
-neighbourhood of the
V
-droplet
S_{V}
.
Then
\tfrac{1}{\pi}c_{N}^{\beta}\to\sigma_{V}
in the weak sense of measures on
\mathbb{R}
.

Note that the continuity assumption on
V|_{\mathbb{R}}
is satisfied for AGUE but not for ALUE.

Our proof of Theorem
2.2
in the following section uses a modification
of arguments from
[
37
]
(as well as the planar version in
[
12
, Appendix A]
).

2.3.
Proof of Theorem
2.2

Given an external potential
W
we write
L_{W}
for the kernel

L_{W}(\zeta,\eta)=\log\tfrac{1}{|\zeta-\eta|}+\tfrac{1}{2}(W(\zeta)+W(\eta)).

To a random configuration
\{\zeta_{j}\}_{1}^{N}
we associate the corresponding empirical measure

\mu_{N}=\tfrac{1}{N}\sum_{1}^{N}\delta_{\zeta_{j}}.

The
discrete
W
-energy

of this measure is defined to be

(2.3)

I_{W}^{\sharp}[\mu_{N}]=\tfrac{1}{N(N-1)}\sum_{j\neq k}L_{W}(\zeta_{j},\zeta_{k})=\tfrac{1}{N(N-1)}\sum_{j\neq k}\log\tfrac{1}{|\zeta_{j}-\zeta_{k}|}+\mu_{N}(W).

This energy is closely related to the Hamiltonian
H_{N}
in external potential
W
, since

H_{N}=\sum_{j\neq k}\log\tfrac{1}{|\zeta_{j}-\zeta_{k}|}+N\sum_{j=1}^{N}W(\zeta_{j})=N(N-1)I_{W}^{\sharp}[\mu_{N}]+\sum_{j=1}^{N}W(\zeta_{j}).

We now come to a simple but useful observation. Write
Q_{N}^{*}(\zeta)=Q_{N}(\operatorname{Re}\zeta)
. Recalling that
Q_{N}^{*}\leq Q_{N}
on
\mathbb{C}
we obtain

(2.4)

I_{Q_{N}^{*}}^{\sharp}[\mu_{N}]\leq I_{Q_{N}}^{\sharp}[\mu_{N}].

Following
[
12
,
37
]
we next fix a small
\epsilon>0
and potentials
Q_{N}
and
V
obeying the conditions in Subsection
2.1
.
We then consider the event

A(N,\epsilon)=\{I_{Q_{N}}^{\sharp}(\mu_{N})\leq\gamma(V)+\epsilon\}.

Lemma 2.3
.

Fix
a\geq 0
and suppose that the lower bound condition (
2.2
) is satisfied.
Then there is a positive integer
N_{0}
depending on
\epsilon
but not on
a
such that if
N\geq N_{0}
,

{\mathbf{P}}_{N}^{\beta}(\{\mu_{N}\not\in A(N,\epsilon+a)\})\leq e^{-\frac{1}{2}\beta aN^{2}}.

Proof.

If
\mu_{N}\not\in A(N,\epsilon+a)
, then

(2.5)

I_{Q_{N}}^{\sharp}[\mu_{N}]\geq\gamma(V)+\epsilon+a.

By (
1.5
) and the elementary inequality
|\zeta-\eta|^{2}\leq(1+|\zeta|^{2})(1+|\eta|^{2})

we find that

L_{Q_{N}}(\zeta,\eta)\geq\tfrac{c_{1}}{2}(Q_{N}(\zeta)+Q_{N}(\eta))-c_{2},

where
c_{1}=1-1/k>0
and
c_{2}=C/k.
This gives

(2.6)

I_{Q_{N}}^{\sharp}[\mu_{N}]\geq\tfrac{c_{1}}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}Q_{N}(\zeta_{j})-c_{2}.

Now fix a small number
\theta>0
and take a convex combination of the inequalities (
2.5
) and (
2.6
). We obtain
that if
\mu_{N}\not\in A(N,\epsilon+a)
,

(2.7)

I_{Q_{N}}^{\sharp}[\mu_{N}]\geq(1-\theta)(\gamma(V)+\epsilon+a)+\theta(\tfrac{c_{1}}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}Q_{N}(\zeta_{j})-c_{2}),

which implies that the Hamiltonian (
1.1
) satisfies

\displaystyle\begin{split}H_{N}&\geq N(N-1)(1-\theta)(\gamma(V)+\epsilon+a)\\
&+k((N-1)\theta c_{1}+1)\sum_{j=1}^{N}\log(1+|\zeta_{j}|^{2})-N(\theta c_{3}N+c_{4}).\end{split}

Consequently,

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{C}^{N}\setminus A(N,\epsilon+a)}e^{-\beta H_{N}}\,dA_{N}

\displaystyle\leq e^{-\beta N(N-1)(1-\theta)(\gamma(V)+\epsilon+a)+\beta N(\theta c_{3}N+c_{4})}

\displaystyle\qquad\times[\int_{\mathbb{C}}(1+|\zeta|^{2})^{-k\beta((N-1)\theta c_{1}+1)}\,dA(\zeta)]^{N}.

Since

\int_{\mathbb{C}}(1+|\zeta|^{2})^{-\alpha}\,dA(\zeta)=\tfrac{1}{\alpha-1}
for
\alpha>1
, the integral in brackets is no larger than
1
, so
we obtain that for all large enough
N
,

\displaystyle{\mathbf{P}}_{N}^{\beta}(\{\mu_{N}\not\in A(N,\epsilon+a)\})

\displaystyle\leq\tfrac{1}{Z_{N}}e^{-\beta N(N-1)(1-\theta)(\gamma(V)+\epsilon+a)+\beta N(\theta c_{3}N+c_{4})}.

Next we use the lower bound

Z_{N}\geq\exp(-N^{2}(\beta\gamma(V)+o(1)))
from assumption (
2.2
).
This implies that

{\mathbf{P}}_{N}^{\beta}(\{\mu_{N}\not\in A(N,\epsilon+a)\})\leq e^{N(N-1)[\beta(\theta(\gamma(V)+c_{3}+\epsilon)-(1-\theta)a)+o(1)]}.

Finally we fix
\theta
with
0<\theta<\tfrac{1}{8}
and
N_{0}
such that for all
N\geq N_{0}
,

\theta(\gamma(V)+c_{3}+\epsilon)+o(1)<\tfrac{a}{8}.

Then for large enough
N
,

{\mathbf{P}}_{N}^{\beta}(\{\mu_{N}\not\in A(N,\epsilon+a)\})\leq e^{N(N-1)[\beta(\frac{a}{8}-(1-\theta)a)]}\leq e^{-\frac{1}{2}\beta aN^{2}},

which completes the proof.
∎

Now fix a constant
C>0
and write
S_{Q_{N}}^{\,+}=\{\zeta\in\mathbb{C}\,;\,\operatorname{dist}(\zeta,S_{Q_{N}})\leq C\tfrac{\log N}{N}\}.

Lemma 2.4
.

Suppose that for each
N
we have
\mu_{N}\in A(N,\epsilon)
and
\operatorname{supp}\mu_{N}\subset S_{Q_{N}}^{\,+}
.
Suppose also
that
V|_{\mathbb{R}}
is continuous in an
\mathbb{R}
-neighbourhood of
S_{V}
.
Then each subsequence of the measures
\mu_{N}
has a further subsequence converging weak* to a probability measure
\mu
supported on
\mathbb{R}

such that
I_{V}[\mu]\leq\gamma(V)+\epsilon
.

Proof.

By hypothesis, the measures
\mu_{N}
are all supported in some large compact set.
Pick a subsequence
\mu_{N_{k}}
converging weak* to a probability measure
\mu
which is necessarily supported on
\mathbb{R}
.
Also pick
{\varepsilon}>0
and write
Q_{N}^{*}(\zeta)=Q_{N}(\operatorname{Re}\zeta)
. We also write
V^{*}(\zeta)=V(\operatorname{Re}\zeta)
. By (
2.4
) we know that

I_{Q_{N}^{*}}^{\sharp}[\mu_{N}]\leq\gamma(V)+\epsilon
.

Now recall that the
Q_{N}^{*}
increase monotonically to
V^{*}
and that
V^{*}
is continuous on some compact set
K
which contains the sets
S_{Q_{N}}^{\,+}
for all large
N
.
Hence the convergence is uniform on
K
,
by Dini’s theorem. In particular
we can find an integer
n

such that
\sup\{|Q_{N}^{*}(\zeta)-V^{*}(\zeta)|\,;\,\zeta\in K\}<{\varepsilon}
when
N\geq n
.
Then for
N_{k}\geq n
,

|\mu_{N_{k}}(Q_{N_{k}}^{*}-V^{*})|<{\varepsilon}
.
Choosing
n
larger if necessary, we can arrange that
|\mu_{N_{k}}(V^{*})-\mu(V)|<{\varepsilon}
, since
V^{*}
is continuous on
K
and
\mu(V)=\mu(V^{*})
.
Hence
|\mu_{N_{k}}(Q_{N_{k}}^{*})-\mu(V)|<2{\varepsilon}
when
N_{k}\geq n
.
We have shown that

\mu_{N_{k}}(Q_{N_{k}}^{*})\to\mu(V)
as
k\to\infty
.

From here on, we follow a routine argument which can be found in
[
51
, p. 146]
for example. For a large real number
M
, we put

L_{M}(\zeta,\eta)=\min\{M,\log\tfrac{1}{|\zeta-\eta|}\}.

Since
L_{M}
is continuous while
\mu_{N_{k}}\to\mu
weak*,

\displaystyle\iint\log\frac{1}{|\zeta-\eta|}\,d\mu(\zeta)\,d\mu(\eta)

\displaystyle=\lim_{M\to\infty}\lim_{k\to\infty}\iint L_{M}(\zeta,\eta)\,d\mu_{N_{k}}(\zeta)\,d\mu_{N_{k}}(\eta)

\displaystyle\leq\lim_{M\to\infty}\limsup_{k\to\infty}\{\tfrac{1}{N_{k}^{2}}\sum_{j\neq l}\log\tfrac{1}{|\zeta_{j}-\zeta_{l}|}+\tfrac{M}{N_{k}}\}

\displaystyle=\limsup_{k\to\infty}\tfrac{1}{N_{k}(N_{k}-1)}\sum_{j\neq l}\log\tfrac{1}{|\zeta_{j}-\zeta_{l}|}.

Since
I_{Q_{N_{k}}^{*}}^{\sharp}[\mu_{N_{k}}]\leq\gamma(V)+\epsilon
it follows that

\displaystyle I_{V}[\mu]

\displaystyle=\lim_{M\to\infty}\lim_{k\to\infty}(\iint L_{M}(\zeta,\eta)\,d\mu_{N_{k}}(\zeta)\,d\mu_{N_{k}}(\eta)+\mu_{N_{k}}(Q_{N_{k}}^{*}))

\displaystyle\leq\limsup_{k\to\infty}I_{Q_{N_{k}}^{*}}^{\sharp}[\mu_{N_{k}}]\leq\gamma(V)+\epsilon.

The proof is complete.
∎

Lemma 2.5
.

Let
d_{N}(\zeta)=\operatorname{dist}(\zeta,S_{Q_{N}})
and put

D_{N}=\max_{1\leq j\leq N}\{d_{N}(\zeta_{j})\}.

Then for each
q>0
there exists
C>0
and
N_{0}
so that for
N\geq N_{0}
we have

{\mathbf{P}}_{N}^{\beta}(\{D_{N}>C\tfrac{\log N}{N}\})\leq\tfrac{1}{N^{q}}.

Proof.

This follows from
[
12
, Theorem 2]
. More precisely, in the estimate
[
12
, (1.11)]
(with
Q=Q_{N}
),
we take
c=c_{1}N
where
c_{1}>0
is independent of
N
. Then taking
t=A\log N
with a large enough
A
and defining
C
accordingly,
we finish the proof.
∎

We are now ready to finish our proof of Theorem
2.2
.

For a fixed small
\epsilon>0
, we pick
N_{0}(\epsilon)
so that if
N\geq N_{0}
then, with large probability,

\operatorname{supp}\mu_{N}\subset S_{Q_{N}}^{+}
and
\mu_{N}\in A(N,2\epsilon)
. Indeed, by lemmas
2.3
and
2.5
, we can arrange that the probability of the complementary event is at most
N^{-q}+e^{-\frac{1}{2}\epsilon\beta N^{2}}
where
q>0
may be chosen as large as we please (by adjusting the value of
C
).

Now let
\epsilon=\epsilon_{n}\to 0
through a suitable sequence, and
for each
n
, use Lemma
2.4
to pick a weak* subsequential limit
\mu^{\epsilon_{n}}
of the measures
\mu_{N}
, with
I_{V}[\mu^{\epsilon_{n}}]\leq\gamma(V)+2\epsilon_{n}
and
\mu^{\epsilon_{n}}
supported
in some fixed compact subset of
\mathbb{R}
.

Letting
n\to\infty
, the corresponding measures
\mu^{\epsilon_{n}}
will converge
weak* along a subsequence to a measure
\mu
with
I_{V}[\mu]\leq\gamma(V)
. This implies that
\mu=\sigma_{V}
by unicity of the equilibrium measure (cf.
[
51
, Theorem I.1.3]
). We have shown that with probability
1-o(1)
, every subsequence of the measures
\mu_{N}
has a further subsequence converging weakly to
\sigma_{V}
, which implies that the full sequence
\mu_{N}\to\sigma_{V}
weakly as measures.

Now let
f
be a bounded continuous function on
\mathbb{R}
and define
f^{*}
on
\mathbb{C}
by
f^{*}(\zeta)=f(\operatorname{Re}\zeta)
.
Then the uniformly bounded random variables
\mu_{N}(f^{*})
converge to
\sigma_{V}(f^{*})=\sigma_{V}(f)
in probability as
N\to\infty
.
Taking expectations we obtain
\mathbf{E}_{N}^{\beta}[\mu_{N}(f^{*})]\to\sigma_{V}(f)
as
N\to\infty
. However, by Fubini’s theorem,

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}_{N}^{\beta}[\mu_{N}(f^{*})]

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{N}\mathbf{E}_{N}^{\beta}[f(\operatorname{Re}\zeta_{1})+\ldots+f(\operatorname{Re}\zeta_{N})]

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{\pi}\tfrac{1}{N}\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(\xi)\,d\xi\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathbf{R}_{N}^{\beta}(\xi+i\eta)\,d\eta

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(\xi)\,c_{N}^{\beta}(\xi)\,d\xi,

so we obtain the desired convergence
c_{N}^{\beta}\to\pi\sigma_{V}
in the sense of measures on
\mathbb{R}
.
∎

2.4.
An entropy estimate

The lower bound on the partition function in (
2.2
) can be checked directly for our model cases of AGUE and ALUE.
However, the stronger conditions in the following lemma are usually easier to check (e.g. in the case of AGUE).

Lemma 2.6
.

Suppose that the equilibrium measures
\sigma_{Q_{N}}
obey the following energy and entropy limits:

(2.8)

\lim_{N\to\infty}\tfrac{1}{N}\sigma_{Q_{N}}(Q_{N})=0,\qquad\lim_{N\to\infty}\tfrac{1}{N}\sigma_{Q_{N}}(\,\log\Delta Q_{N})=0.

Then the lower bound (
2.2
) holds, i.e.,

\liminf_{N\to\infty}\tfrac{1}{N^{2}}\log Z_{N}^{\beta}\geq-\beta\gamma(V)
.

Proof.

We start by fixing a continuous compactly supported function
{\varphi}
with

\int_{\mathbb{C}}\varphi\,dA=1
and noting that

\displaystyle Z_{N}=\int_{\mathbb{C}^{N}}\exp\{-\beta\sum_{j\neq k}L_{Q_{N}}(\zeta_{j},\zeta_{k})-\sum_{j=1}^{N}(\beta Q_{N}(\zeta_{j})+\log{\varphi}(\zeta_{j}))\}\prod_{j=1}^{N}\,\varphi(\zeta_{j})\,dA(\zeta_{j}).

By Jensen’s inequality,

\displaystyle\log Z_{N}

\displaystyle\geq\int_{\mathbb{C}^{N}}\{-\beta\sum_{j\neq k}L_{Q_{N}}(\zeta_{j},\zeta_{k})-\sum_{j=1}^{N}(\beta Q_{N}+\log{\varphi})(\zeta_{j})\}\prod_{j=1}^{N}\,\varphi(\zeta_{j})\,dA(\zeta_{j})

\displaystyle=-\beta N(N-1)I_{Q_{N}}[{\varphi}]-N\int_{\mathbb{C}}(\beta\,Q_{N}+\log\varphi)\,\varphi\,dA,

with the understanding that
0\log 0=0
, and where we write
I_{Q_{N}}[{\varphi}]
in place of
I_{Q_{N}}[{\varphi}\,dA]
.
This leads to

(2.9)

\displaystyle\tfrac{1}{N^{2}}\log Z_{N}

\displaystyle\geq-\beta(1-\tfrac{1}{N})I_{Q_{N}}[{\varphi}]-\tfrac{\beta}{N}\int Q_{N}\varphi\,dA-\tfrac{1}{N}\int\varphi\log\varphi\,dA.

For small
\delta>0
we let
\chi(\zeta)=\delta^{-2}\mathbf{1}_{D(0,\delta)}(\zeta)
and define

{\varphi}_{\delta,N}(\zeta)=\chi*\sigma_{Q_{N}}(\zeta)=\tfrac{\sigma_{Q_{N}}(D(\zeta,\delta))}{\delta^{2}}.

As
\delta\to 0
we have that
I_{Q_{N}}[{\varphi}_{\delta,N}]\to I_{Q_{N}}[\sigma_{Q_{N}}]=\gamma(Q_{N})
(see remark in
[
12
, Appendix A]
).

Thus letting
\delta\to 0
the right hand side in (
2.9
) converges to

(2.10)

\displaystyle-\beta(1-\tfrac{1}{N})\gamma(Q_{N})-\tfrac{1}{N}\int_{S_{Q_{N}}}Q_{N}\Delta Q_{N}\,dA-\tfrac{1}{N}\int_{S_{Q_{N}}}\Delta Q_{N}\,\log\Delta Q_{N}\,dA.

Since
\gamma(Q_{N})\uparrow\gamma(V)
as
N\to\infty
(cf.
[
51
, Theorem I.6.2]
), we see that (
2.10
) can be estimated from below by
-\beta\gamma(V)+o(1)
, due to the assumptions (
2.8
).
∎

3.
Generalities about the determinantal case

Let
(Q_{N})
be any sequence of potentials satisfying the conditions in Subsection
2.1
, and set
\beta=1
.

We shall adapt to the present setting some basic techniques from the paper
[
14
]
pertaining to weighted polynomials, Ward’s equation, and so forth. The adaptations are straightforward, and we shall be correspondingly brief.

Recall that the
\beta=1
process is determinantal, i.e., the intensity
k
-point functions of
\{\zeta_{j}\}_{1}^{N}
can be represented as determinants

\mathbf{R}_{N,k}(\eta_{1},\ldots,\eta_{k})=\det({\mathbf{K}}_{N}(\eta_{i},\eta_{j}))_{k\times k},

where
{\mathbf{K}}_{N}
is a correlation kernel.

Indeed, as is well-known, we can take
{\mathbf{K}}_{N}
to be the reproducing kernel of the subspace
{\mathcal{W}}_{N}
of
L^{2}=L^{2}(\mathbb{C},dA)

consisting of all weighted polynomials
f=q\cdot e^{-NQ_{N}/2}
, where
q
is a holomorphic polynomial of degree at most
N-1
. (See e.g.
[
51
, Section IV.7]
.)
This
canonical correlation kernel

is used without exception below; note in particular that

\mathbf{R}_{N}(\zeta)={\mathbf{K}}_{N}(\zeta,\zeta).

3.1.
Auxiliary estimates

We now record a few basic estimates for weighted polynomials, which are proved using standard techniques, e.g. in
[
14
, Section 3]
.
We begin with the following pointwise-
L^{2}
estimate.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
f=q\cdot e^{-NQ_{N}/2}\in{\mathcal{W}}_{N}
and fix a point
\zeta_{0}
at which
\Delta Q_{N}(\zeta_{0})>0
. Suppose that there exists a constant
C_{0}=C_{0}(\zeta_{0})
such that

(3.1)

\Delta Q_{N}(\zeta)\leq C_{0}\Delta Q_{N}(\zeta_{0})\qquad\text{when}\qquad|\zeta-\zeta_{0}|\leq\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{N\Delta Q_{N}(\zeta_{0})}}.

Then there exists a constant
C=C(C_{0})
such that

(3.2)

|f(\zeta_{0})|^{2}\leq CN\Delta Q_{N}(\zeta_{0})\int_{D(\zeta_{0},\frac{1}{\sqrt{N\Delta Q_{N}(\zeta_{0})}})}|f|^{2}\,dA.

Moreover (with the same
C
)

\mathbf{R}_{N}(\zeta_{0})\leq CN\Delta Q_{N}(\zeta_{0}).

Proof.

Consider the function

F(\zeta)=|f(\zeta)|^{2}e^{NC_{0}\Delta Q_{N}(\zeta_{0})|\zeta-\zeta_{0}|^{2}}.

By hypothesis,
F
is logarithmically subharmonic in the disc
D(\zeta_{0},\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{N\Delta Q_{N}(\zeta_{0})}})
since

\Delta\log F(\zeta)=\Delta|f(\zeta)|^{2}+NC_{0}\Delta Q_{N}(\zeta_{0})=-N(\Delta Q_{N}(\zeta)-C_{0}\Delta Q_{N}(\zeta_{0}))\geq 0.

An application of the sub-mean inequality gives

\displaystyle|f(\zeta_{0})|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq N\Delta Q_{N}(\zeta_{0})\int_{D(\zeta_{0},\frac{1}{\sqrt{N\Delta Q_{N}(\zeta_{0})}})}F\,dA

\displaystyle\leq e^{C_{0}}N\Delta Q_{N}(\zeta_{0})\int_{D(\zeta_{0},\frac{1}{\sqrt{N\Delta Q_{N}(\zeta_{0})}})}|f|^{2}\,dA,

which proves (
3.2
) with
C=e^{C_{0}}
.

To verify the last statement, we consider the weighted polynomial

f(\zeta)=\tfrac{{\mathbf{K}}_{N}(\zeta,\zeta_{0})}{\sqrt{{\mathbf{K}}_{N}(\zeta_{0},\zeta_{0})}}
, which satisfies
|f(\zeta_{0})|^{2}=\mathbf{R}_{N}(\zeta_{0})
. Applying (
3.2
) to this
f
, observing that

\int_{\mathbb{C}}|f|^{2}=1
, we immediately
obtain the inequality
\mathbf{R}_{N}(\zeta_{0})\leq CN\Delta Q_{N}(\zeta_{0})
.
∎

It is convenient to prove a stronger form of Lemma
3.1
, which incorporates the decay of
\mathbf{R}_{N}
outside of the droplet
S_{Q_{N}}
. For this, we recall some standard potential theoretic
notions.

Given an external potential
W
, we define the
obstacle function

\check{W}(\zeta)
as the supremum of
s(\zeta)
where
s
runs through the set of subharmonic functions
on
\mathbb{C}
which satisfy
s\leq W
everywhere and
s(\zeta)\leq\log|\zeta|^{2}+O(1)
as
\zeta\to\infty
. The coincidence set
S_{W}^{*}=\{W=\check{W}\}
contains the droplet
S_{W}
, but may in general be slightly larger (see
[
51
, p.144]
). The obstacle function
\check{W}
is harmonic in the complement of the droplet
S_{W}
and is
C^{1,1}
-smooth on
\mathbb{C}
, see
[
51
]
.

Lemma 3.2
.

If
f\in{\mathcal{W}}_{N}
and
|f|\leq C
on
S_{Q_{N}}
, then
|f|\leq Ce^{-N(Q_{N}-\check{Q}_{N})/2}
on
\mathbb{C}
.

Proof.

This is a standard fact (e.g.
[
51
]
) but it is easy enough to recall a proof. We can assume that
C=1
. Writing
f=q\cdot e^{-NQ_{N}/2}
where
q
has degree at most
N-1
,
we consider the subharmonic function
s=\tfrac{1}{N}\log|q(\zeta)|^{2}
which satisfies
s(\zeta)\leq\log|\zeta|^{2}+O(1)
as
\zeta\to\infty
. Since
|f|\leq 1
on
\mathbb{C}
we see that

s(\zeta)=\tfrac{1}{N}\log|f(\zeta)|^{2}+Q_{N}(\zeta)\leq Q_{N}(\zeta)
on
\mathbb{C}
. A suitable version of the maximum principle shows that
s\leq\check{Q}_{N}
on
\mathbb{C}
, finishing the proof of the lemma.
∎

Lemma 3.3
.

Suppose in addition to (
3.1
) that there is a constant
C_{1}
such that

\Delta Q_{N}\leq C_{1}N
on
S_{Q_{N}}
. Then there is a constant
C
such that, for all
\zeta
in a neighbourhood of the droplet,

\mathbf{R}_{N}(\zeta)\leq CN^{2}e^{-N(Q_{N}-\check{Q}_{N})(\zeta)}.

Proof.

Fix a point
\zeta_{1}
in a neighbourhood of the droplet, and take
f(\zeta)=\tfrac{{\mathbf{K}}_{N}(\zeta,\zeta_{1})}{\sqrt{{\mathbf{K}}_{N}(\zeta_{1},\zeta_{1})}}
. Next fix
\zeta_{0}\in S_{Q_{N}}
and use (
3.2
) to conclude
that

|f(\zeta_{0})|^{2}\leq CC_{1}N^{2}\int_{\mathbb{C}}|f|^{2}\,dA=CC_{1}N^{2}.

Since
\zeta_{0}\in S_{Q_{N}}
was arbitrary, Lemma
3.2
shows that

|f(\zeta)|^{2}\leq CC_{1}N^{2}e^{-N(Q_{N}-\check{Q}_{N})(\zeta)}
.

Taking
\zeta=\zeta_{1}
we find that
\mathbf{R}_{N}(\zeta)={\mathbf{K}}_{N}(\zeta,\zeta)\leq CC_{1}N^{2}e^{-N(Q_{N}-\check{Q}_{N})(\zeta)}
, as desired.
∎

Remark
.

We stress that the conditions of Lemma
3.3
holds for AGUE (since
\Delta Q_{N}
is then essentially a constant multiple of
N
), but not for ALUE.

Lemma 3.4
.

Suppose that the conditions of Lemma
3.3
are satisfied. Then the cross-sections

c_{N}(p)=\tfrac{1}{N}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathbf{R}_{N}(p+i\eta)\,d\eta
are uniformly bounded on
\mathbb{R}
.

Proof.

Write
d_{N}(\zeta)=\operatorname{dist}(\zeta,S_{Q_{N}})
.
By Taylor’s formula,

(3.3)

Q_{N}(\zeta)-\check{Q}_{N}(\zeta)\geq M_{N}\cdot d_{N}(\zeta)^{2},\qquad M_{N}=\min\{\Delta Q_{N}(\zeta)\,;\,\zeta\in S_{Q_{N}}\}

for all
\zeta
close enough to the droplet, see e.g.
[
12
, Section 2]
. By assumption we have
M_{N}\geq mN
for some
m>0
, so
there is a constant
C
such that

(3.4)

c_{N}(p)\leq CN\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}e^{-mN^{2}d_{N}(p+i\eta)^{2}}\,d\eta.

By Theorem
2.1
, the droplet
S_{Q_{N}}
is contained in a strip
|\eta|\leq\tfrac{A}{N}
, so the right hand side in (
3.4
) can be estimated by

C(2A+2\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-my^{2}}\,dy)<\infty.

∎

3.2.
Rescaling in the bulk

Now fix a point
p_{*}\in\mathbb{R}
with
\sigma_{V}(p_{*})>0
, where as always
V=\lim Q_{N}
.

We admit potentials such that
\Delta Q_{N}
has singularities (such as for ALUE),
but we assume that each
\Delta Q_{N}
is finite at the point
p_{*}

and that there are positive constants
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
(with
c_{2}
possibly depending on
p_{*}
)
such that
c_{1}N\leq\Delta Q_{N}(\zeta)\leq c_{2}N
for all
\zeta\in D(p_{*},\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{N\Delta Q_{N}(p_{*})}})
and all large
N
.

We then rescale by the blow-up map
\Gamma_{N}
in (
1.6
), i.e.,
we rescale the system
\{\zeta_{j}\}_{1}^{N}
by magnifying distances by the factor
{\scriptstyle\sqrt{N\Delta Q_{N}(p_{*})}}
, about the origin
p_{*}
. The resulting process
is denoted
\{z_{j}\}_{1}^{N}
and we write
R_{N}
for its
1
-point intensity.

It is easy to see that the rescaled process
\{z_{j}\}_{1}^{N}
is determinantal as well, with (canonical) correlation kernel

K_{N}(z,w)=\tfrac{1}{N\Delta Q_{N}(p_{*})}{\mathbf{K}}_{N}(\zeta,\eta),\qquad z=\Gamma_{N}(\zeta),\quad w=\Gamma_{N}(\eta).

The following lemma is a slight modification of
[
14
, Theorem 1.1]
. A proof can be accomplished by arguments from
[
14
, Section 3]
(with “
Q_{N}
” in lieu of “
Q
”). We omit repeating those details here, but merely point out that the local boundedness in Lemma
3.1
is sufficient for the normal families argument in
[
14
, Section 3]
to carry through.

Lemma 3.5
.

(“
Structure of limiting kernels
”) Under the above conditions, there exists a sequence
c_{N}
of cocycles such that each subsequence
of the kernels
c_{N}K_{N}
has a further subsequence which converges locally uniformly to a Hermitian kernel
K
of the form

K(z,w)=G(z,w)\cdot L(z,w),

where
L
is a Hermitian-entire function and
G
is the Ginibre kernel (
1.19
).

Here we have used the following notation (cf.
[
14
]
).
A continuous function
f(z,w)
is called
Hermitian
if
f(z,w)=\overline{f(w,z)}
. If
f
is also analytic
(or entire) in
z
and
\bar{w}
, then
f
is called
Hermitian-analytic
(or
Hermitian-entire
). A Hermitian function of the form

f(z,w)=g(z)\overline{g(w)}
, where
g
is continuous and unimodular, is said to be a cocycle.

By the convergence in Lemma
3.5
it follows that each limiting kernel
K
appearing in Lemma
3.5
is the correlation kernel of a unique determinantal point field
\{z_{j}\}_{1}^{\infty}
.
In order to determine
K
it clearly suffices to determine the
limiting 1-point function

R(z)=L(z,z)
, for then
L
is determined by
R
via polarization, and so

K=LG
is also determined.

In what follows the subsequential limits
R=\lim R_{N_{k}}
play a fundamental role. We refer to such limits as limiting 1-point functions about
p_{*}
.

Now recall our assumption that the limit
\rho(p_{*})
in (
1.7
) exists, i.e., we assume that

\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{N\Delta Q_{N}(p_{*})}}=\sqrt{\tfrac{N}{\Delta Q_{N}(p_{*})}}\cdot\tfrac{1}{N}=\tfrac{\rho(p_{*})}{N}\cdot(1+o(1))
as
N\to\infty
.

We immediately obtain the following rescaled version of the pointwise cross-section convergence.

Lemma 3.6
.

If the cross-section
c_{N}(p_{*})
converges to
\pi\cdot\sigma_{V}(p_{*})
, then
for each limiting
1
-point function
R
at
p_{*}
and each
x\in\mathbb{R}
,

\int_{\mathbb{R}}R(x+iy)\,dy=\pi\cdot\rho(p_{*})\cdot\sigma_{V}(p_{*}).

Given a limiting kernel
K
, we define the
Berezin kernel

B
via

B(z,w)=\tfrac{|K(z,w)|^{2}}{R(z)}
and we put

C(z)=\int_{\mathbb{C}}\tfrac{B(z,w)}{z-w}\,dA(w).

Theorem 3.7
.

Suppose that
(Q_{N})
is an admissible sequence satisfying the cross-section convergence. Fix
p_{*}\in\mathbb{R}
where
\sigma_{V}(p_{*})>0
and
\rho(p_{*})>0
and rescale as above. Then each limiting 1-point function
R=\lim R_{N_{k}}
is nontrivial, i.e.,
R>0
everywhere on
\mathbb{C}
. Furthermore,
R
gives rise to a solution to Ward’s equation

\bar{\partial}C(z)=R(z)-1-\Delta\log R(z).

Proof.

The proof again follows by appealing to general results from
[
14
]
.
Indeed, it follows from Lemma
3.6
that a limiting 1-point function
R
cannot vanish identically. Then in fact
R>0
everywhere and Ward’s equation
holds in view of the zero-one law in
[
14
, Theorem 1.3]
.
∎

3.3.
Characterization of translation invariant scaling limit

We now come to the important realization that the cross-section convergence uniquely
determines a limiting 1-point function
R
, provided that the latter can be shown to be translation invariant. Indeed, this now follows in a straightforward
way by appealing to the theory for Ward’s equation in
[
14
]
.

Similar as in
[
14
]
, we shall say a limiting 1-point function
R
in Lemma
3.5
is
(horizontal)
translation invariant
if
R(z+t)=R(z)
for all
t\in\mathbb{R}
.

Theorem 3.8
.

Keep the assumptions of Theorem
3.7
. Then each translation invariant limiting 1-point function
R
at
p_{*}

is of the form

(3.5)

\displaystyle R(z)=\gamma*\mathbf{1}_{(-2a,2a)}(2\operatorname{Im}z)=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{-2a}^{\,2a}e^{-\frac{1}{2}(2\operatorname{Im}z-t)^{2}}\,dt,\quad a=\tfrac{\pi}{2}\cdot\rho(p_{*})\cdot\sigma_{V}(p_{*}).

Proof.

The formula (
3.5
) (for some
a>0
) follows immediately from the characterization of translation invariant solutions to Ward’s equation in
[
14
, Theorem 1.6]
.
Moreover, Lemma
3.6
fixes the value of
a
in (
3.5
).
∎

3.4.
Universality of almost-circular ensembles

The theory mainly described in the almost-Hermitian setup so far applies to the almost-circular setup as well.

Proof of Theorem
1.9
.

Since
Q_{N}
is radially symmetric, the limiting empirical distribution is uniform on the unit circle.
Furthermore, by (
1.38
) and (
1.39
), we have

(3.6)

\tfrac{1}{\pi}c_{N}=\tfrac{1}{\pi}(1-r_{N})\Delta Q_{N}(1)\cdot(1+o(1))=\tfrac{1}{2\pi}\cdot(1+o(1)),\qquad(N\to\infty).

This in particular gives rises to the rescaled version of the pointwise cross-section convergence

(3.7)

\int_{\mathbb{R}}R(x+iy)\,dy=\tfrac{\rho}{2}.

Moreover, since
Q_{N}
is radially symmetric, it is easy to show the translation invariance, see e.g.,
[
14
, Subsection 6.4]
.
Now Theorem
3.8
completes the proof.
∎

4.
Pointwise convergence of cross-sections

In this section we prove Theorem
1.3
and Theorem
1.6
about pointwise convergence of cross-sections to
\pi
times the equilibrium measure
\sigma_{V}(p)
.

4.1.
Cross-sections for AGUE

Let

Q_{N}(\zeta)=\tfrac{1}{2}\xi^{2}+\tfrac{1}{2}\cdot\tfrac{N}{c^{2}}\eta^{2}

and fix numbers
\delta,\alpha
with
0<\delta<\alpha<2
. Put
I_{\alpha,\delta}=\{\xi\in\mathbb{R}\,;\,\delta\leq|\xi|\leq\alpha\}
.

We shall prove in detail that
\tfrac{1}{\pi}c_{N}(p)\to\sigma_{\mathrm{sc}}(p)
uniformly for
p\in I_{\alpha,\delta}
. The pointwise convergence for
p=0
may be
handled in a similar way, see a remark below.

We first recall that the 1-point function can be written

\mathbf{R}_{N}(\zeta)=\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}|w_{j}(\zeta)|^{2},

where
w_{j}=q_{j}\cdot e^{-NQ_{N}/2}\in{\mathcal{W}}_{N}
is an orthonormal basis of the weighted polynomials subspace
{\mathcal{W}}_{N}
of
L^{2}
(see Section
3
).

Consider now an arbitrary potential
Q
of the form
Q(\xi+i\eta)=a\xi^{2}+b\eta^{2}
where
a
and
b
are positive constants.

The following orthonormal polynomials in weight
e^{-NQ_{N}/2}
are found in
[
23
]
,

q_{j}(\zeta)=(ab)^{\frac{1}{4}}\sqrt{\tfrac{N}{j!}}(\tfrac{\tau}{2})^{\frac{j}{2}}H_{j}(\sqrt{\tfrac{Nab}{b-a}}\,\zeta),\quad\tau=\tfrac{b-a}{b+a},

where
H_{j}
is the
j
:th Hermite polynomial,

H_{j}(z)=(-1)^{j}e^{z^{2}}\tfrac{d^{j}}{dz^{j}}e^{-z^{2}}.

In other words, the
1
-point function in potential
Q=a\xi^{2}+b\eta^{2}
is given by

(4.1)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\mathbf{R}_{N}(\zeta)=e^{-NQ(\zeta)}N\sqrt{ab}\cdot F_{N}(\sqrt{\tfrac{Nab}{b-a}}\,\zeta),\cr F_{N}(z)=\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}\tfrac{(\tau/2)^{j}}{j!}\left|H_{j}(z)\right|^{\,2}.\cr\end{cases}

We have the following lemma. (See
[
45
, Proposition 2.3]
for a related statement.)

Lemma 4.1
.

With
x=\operatorname{Re}z
and
F_{N}
as in (
4.1
), we have

\displaystyle\tfrac{{\partial}F_{N}}{{\partial}x}(z)=\tfrac{4\tau\,x}{1+\tau}F_{N}(z)-\tfrac{4(\tau/2)^{N}}{1+\tau}\tfrac{\mathrm{Re}[H_{N-1}(z)H_{N}(\bar{z})]}{(N-1)!}.

Proof.

We apply two standard facts for Hermite polynomials, namely the three-term recursion
H_{j+1}(z)=2zH_{j}(z)-H^{\prime}_{j}(z)
and the
identity
H^{\prime}_{j}(z)=2jH_{j-1}(z)
.
These lead to

(4.2)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\tfrac{{\partial}F_{N}}{{\partial}z}&=\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}\tfrac{(\tau/2)^{j}}{j!}\cdot 2jH_{j-1}(z)\cdot\left(2\bar{z}H_{j-1}(\bar{z})-H^{\prime}_{j-1}(\bar{z})\right)\\
&=2\tau\bar{z}\sum_{j=0}^{N-2}\tfrac{(\tau/2)^{j}}{j!}|H_{j}(z)|^{2}-\tau\sum_{j=0}^{N-2}\tfrac{(\tau/2)^{j}}{j!}H_{j}(z)H^{\prime}_{j}(\bar{z}).\end{split}

Using once more the three-term recursion, the last sum is recognized as

\displaystyle\tfrac{{\partial}F_{N}}{{\partial}\bar{z}}(z)-2\bar{z}\tfrac{(\tau/2)^{N-1}}{(N-1)!}|H_{N-1}(z)|^{2}+\tfrac{(\tau/2)^{N-1}}{(N-1)!}H_{N-1}(z)H_{N}(\bar{z}).

Inserting this in (
4.2
), we obtain after some straightforward manipulations,

(4.3)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\tfrac{{\partial}F_{N}}{{\partial}z}(z)=2\tau\bar{z}F_{N}(z)-\tau\tfrac{{\partial}F_{N}}{{\partial}\bar{z}}(z)-2\tfrac{(\tau/2)^{N}}{(N-1)!}H_{N-1}(z)H_{N}(\bar{z}).\end{split}

The lemma follows from (
4.3
) by taking real parts and rearranging.
∎

Now consider the cross-section
c_{N}
, which we write in the form

(4.4)

c_{N}(\xi)=\tfrac{1}{N^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathbf{R}_{N}(\xi+i\tfrac{y}{N})\,dy.

Fix a point
\xi\in I_{\alpha,\delta}
.

By (
4.1
) with
a=\tfrac{1}{2}
and
b=\tfrac{1}{2}\tfrac{N}{c^{2}}
we have

\tfrac{1}{N}\mathbf{R}_{N}(\xi+i\tfrac{y}{N})=\tfrac{\sqrt{N}}{2c}\,e^{-\frac{N}{2}\xi^{2}-\frac{1}{2c^{2}}y^{2}}\,F_{N}(\tfrac{N}{\sqrt{2(N-c^{2})}}(\xi+i\tfrac{y}{N})).

Applying Lemma
4.1
, we obtain after some straightforward computations

(4.5)

\begin{split}\tfrac{1}{N}\tfrac{{\partial}\mathbf{R}_{N}}{{\partial}\xi}(\xi+i\tfrac{y}{N})=&-\tfrac{1}{c\sqrt{2}}\tfrac{N+c^{2}}{(N-1)!}\sqrt{\tfrac{N}{N-c^{2}}}e^{-\frac{N}{2}\xi^{2}-\frac{1}{2c^{2}}y^{2}}(\tfrac{N-c^{2}}{2(N+c^{2})})^{N}\cr&\times\mathrm{Re}\{H_{N-1}(\tfrac{N}{\sqrt{2(N-c^{2})}}(\xi+i\tfrac{y}{N}))H_{N}(\tfrac{N}{\sqrt{2(N-c^{2})}}(\xi-i\tfrac{y}{N}))\}.\cr\end{split}

Thus we have asymptotically, as
N\to\infty
,

\displaystyle\tfrac{1}{N^{2}}\tfrac{{\partial}\mathbf{R}_{N}}{{\partial}\xi}(\xi+i\tfrac{y}{N})=

\displaystyle-(1+o(1))\cdot\tfrac{1}{c\sqrt{2}}e^{-\frac{N}{2}\xi^{2}-\frac{1}{2c^{2}}y^{2}-2c^{2}}\tfrac{1}{2^{N}(N-1)!}

(4.6)

\displaystyle\times\mathrm{Re}\{H_{N-1}(\sqrt{\tfrac{N}{2}}\xi+i\tfrac{y}{\sqrt{2N}})H_{N}(\sqrt{\tfrac{N}{2}}\xi-i\tfrac{y}{\sqrt{2N}})\}.

We now apply the inequality (
A.6
) in (
4.1
), which gives that there are constants
C=C(\alpha,\delta)
and
k=k(\alpha,\delta)
such that whenever
\xi\in I_{\alpha,\delta}
,

\tfrac{1}{N^{2}}\tfrac{{\partial}\mathbf{R}_{N}}{{\partial}\xi}(\xi+i\tfrac{y}{N})\leq Ce^{-\frac{1}{2c^{2}}y^{2}}\max\{1,\tfrac{y^{2N}}{N^{N}}\}\cosh^{2}(ky).

Using this and differentiating with respect to
\xi
in (
4.4
) we find that the derivative
c_{N}^{\prime}
satisfies

|c_{N}^{\prime}(\xi)|\leq C\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{-\frac{1}{2c^{2}}y^{2}}\max\{1,y^{2N}N^{-N}\}\cosh^{2}(ky)\,dy\leq C_{1},

with a new constant
C_{1}=C_{1}(\alpha,\delta,c)
. This proves equicontinuity of the functions
c_{N}
on
I_{\alpha,\delta}
.

Since the functions
c_{N}
are also uniformly bounded (Lemma
3.4
) we can apply the Arzela-Ascoli theorem and conclude that each subsequence of the functions
c_{N}
has a further subsequence which converges uniformly on
I_{\alpha,\delta}

to a limit
c(\xi)
. In view of Theorem
2.2
, we must then have
c=\pi\cdot\sigma_{V}
on the interval
I_{\alpha,\delta}

where
\sigma_{V}
is the equilibrium measure in potential
V(\xi)=\tfrac{1}{2}\xi^{2}
, i.e.,
\sigma_{V}=\sigma_{\mathrm{sc}}
is Wigner’s semi-circle law. We have thus shown
locally uniform convergence
c_{N}(\xi)\to\pi\sigma_{V}(\xi)
for
\xi\in(-2,2)\setminus\{0\}
. It is not hard to obtain convergence also at
\xi=0
, by using the Mehler-Heine formulas in (
A.2
). This detail may be left to the reader.
Our proof of Theorem
1.3
is thereby complete.
∎

4.2.
Cross-sections for ALUE

We now prove Theorem
1.6
on the cross-sections of the ALUE.
To this end we fix a real number
c>0
and a non-negative integer
\nu
and take

(4.7)

Q_{N}(\zeta)=-\tfrac{1}{N}\log\left\{K_{\nu}(aN|\zeta|)\cdot|\zeta|^{\nu}\right\}-b\operatorname{Re}\zeta,

where we abbreviate

(4.8)

a=a_{N}=\tfrac{1}{c^{2}}N,\quad b=b_{N}=\tfrac{1}{c^{2}}N-1.

Let us fix a small
\alpha>0
and put
I_{\alpha}=[\alpha,4-\alpha]
.
We must prove that the cross-sections
c_{N}(\xi)
converge uniformly on
I_{\alpha}
to
\pi\cdot\sigma_{\mathrm{MP}}(\xi)=\tfrac{1}{2\xi}\sqrt{\xi(4-\xi)}
.

For this purpose, we first recall from
[
3
,
49
,
2
]
that
the
j
:th orthonormal polynomial
q_{j}
in weight
e^{-NQ_{N}/2}

can be expressed in terms of the Laguerre polynomial
L_{j}^{\nu}(z)=\tfrac{z^{-\nu}e^{z}}{j!}\tfrac{d^{j}}{dz^{j}}(e^{-z}z^{j+\nu})
via

(4.9)

q_{j}(\zeta)=C_{N}\cdot\tau^{j}\sqrt{\tfrac{j!}{(j+\nu)!}}L_{j}^{\nu}(\tfrac{a^{2}-b^{2}}{2b}N\zeta),\qquad(\tau=\tau_{N}=\tfrac{b}{a}=1-\tfrac{c^{2}}{N}),

where

C_{N}=\sqrt{aN}(\tfrac{a^{2}-b^{2}}{2a}N)^{\frac{\nu+1}{2}}
.

Hence if we introduce the function

(4.10)

G_{N}(z)=\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}\tfrac{\tau^{2j}j!}{(j+\nu)!}|L_{j}^{\nu}(z)|^{2},

then the 1-point function in external potential
Q_{N}
becomes

(4.11)

\mathbf{R}_{N}(\zeta)=C_{N}^{\,2}\cdot G_{N}(\tfrac{a^{2}-b^{2}}{2b}N\zeta)\cdot e^{-NQ_{N}(\zeta)}.

Now pick
\xi
in the interval
I_{\alpha}=[\alpha,4-\alpha]
, and put

\zeta=\xi+i\tfrac{y}{N},\qquad(y\in\mathbb{R}).

Using (
1.26
) and (
1.24
), it follows from (
4.11
) that

\displaystyle\tfrac{1}{N^{2}}\mathbf{R}_{N}(\zeta)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\tfrac{\pi}{2}}\tfrac{1}{c}N^{\nu}\cdot\xi^{\nu-\frac{1}{2}}e^{-N\xi-\frac{y^{2}}{2c^{2}\xi}}\cdot G_{N}(N\zeta)\cdot(1+o(1)),

where we have used

N(\tfrac{N}{c^{2}}\cdot|\zeta|-(\tfrac{N}{c^{2}}-1)\cdot\operatorname{Re}\zeta)=\tfrac{N^{2}}{c^{2}}\cdot\sqrt{\xi^{2}+y^{2}/N^{2}}-(\tfrac{N^{2}}{c^{2}}-N)\xi=N\xi+\tfrac{y^{2}}{2c^{2}\xi}+O(\tfrac{1}{N}).

We next use a summation identity found in
[
24
, (10.12.42)]
to write

(4.12)

\tfrac{j!}{(j+\nu)!}|L_{j}^{\nu}(z)|^{2}=\sum_{k=0}^{j}\tfrac{|z|^{2k}}{k!(k+\nu)!}L_{j-k}^{\nu+2k}(z+\bar{z}).

As a consequence, we can write, in turn:

\displaystyle G_{N}(z)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}\tau^{2j}\sum_{k=0}^{j}\tfrac{|z|^{2k}}{k!(k+\nu)!}L_{j-k}^{\nu+2k}(z+\bar{z}),

\displaystyle\tfrac{1}{N^{2}}\mathbf{R}_{N}(\zeta)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\tfrac{\pi}{2}}\tfrac{1}{c}N^{\nu}\cdot\xi^{\nu-\frac{1}{2}}e^{-N\xi-\frac{y^{2}}{2c^{2}\xi}}\cdot\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}\tau^{2j}\sum_{k=0}^{j}\tfrac{|N\zeta|^{2k}}{k!(k+\nu)!}L_{j-k}^{\nu+2k}(2N\xi)(1+o(1)).

Observing that

|N\zeta|^{2k}=(N\xi)^{2k}(1+(\tfrac{y}{N\xi})^{2})^{k}
and integrating in
y
, we find for
\xi\in I_{\alpha}

(4.13)

\begin{split}c_{N}(\xi)&=\sqrt{\tfrac{\pi}{2}}\tfrac{1}{c}N^{\nu}\cdot\xi^{\nu-\frac{1}{2}}e^{-N\xi}\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}\tau^{2j}\sum_{k=0}^{j}\tfrac{(N\xi)^{2k}}{k!(k+\nu)!}L_{j-k}^{\nu+2k}(2N\xi)\cr&\times J(N,k)\cdot(1+o(1)),\quad\text{where}\quad J(N,k)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{-\frac{y^{2}}{2c^{2}\xi}}(1+(\tfrac{y}{N\xi})^{2})^{k}\,dy.\cr\end{split}

We pause to evaluate the integral
J(N,k)
.

Lemma 4.2
.

J(N,k)=c\cdot\sqrt{2\pi\,\xi}\cdot k!\,(\tfrac{-2c^{2}}{N^{2}\xi})^{k}\,L_{k}^{-k-1/2}(\tfrac{N^{2}\xi}{2c^{2}}).

Proof.

We shall use the following integral representation of the confluent Hypergeometric function
U(a,b,z)
found in
[
50
, (13.4.4)]

U(a,b,z)=\tfrac{1}{\Gamma(a)}\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-zt}t^{a-1}(1+t)^{b-a-1}\,dt.

By a change of variable and using
\Gamma(\tfrac{1}{2})=\sqrt{\pi}
, this leads to

\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{-ay^{2}}(1+(by)^{2})^{k}\,dy=\tfrac{\sqrt{\pi}}{b}U(\tfrac{1}{2},k+\tfrac{3}{2},\tfrac{a}{b^{2}})=\tfrac{\sqrt{\pi}}{b}(\tfrac{a}{b^{2}})^{-k-\tfrac{1}{2}}U(-k,-k+\tfrac{1}{2},\tfrac{a}{b^{2}}).

For the last identity, we also use the well-known (Kummer’s) transformation

U(a,b,z)=z^{1-b}\,U(a-b+1,2-b,z),

see
[
50
, (13.2.29)]
.
We finish the proof of the lemma by use of the functional relation
U(-n,\nu+1,z)=(-1)^{n}\,n!\,L_{n}^{\nu}(z)
found in
[
50
, (13.6.9)]
.
∎

We next use the closed form of Laguerre polynomial in
[
55
, Chapter V]
,

L_{n}^{\nu}(x)=\sum_{j=0}^{n}\tfrac{\Gamma(n+\nu+1)}{(n-j)!\Gamma(j+\nu+1)}\tfrac{(-x)^{j}}{j!}

to infer that

J(N,k)=c\cdot\sqrt{2\pi\,\xi}\cdot(1+O(\tfrac{1}{N^{2}})).

Inserting this in (
4.13
), we arrive at

(4.14)

\begin{split}c_{N}(\xi)&=\pi(N\xi)^{\nu}\cdot e^{-N\xi}\cdot\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}\tau^{2j}\sum_{k=0}^{j}\tfrac{(N\xi)^{2k}}{k!(k+\nu)!}L_{j-k}^{\nu+2k}(2N\xi)\cdot(1+o(1))\cr&=\pi(N\xi)^{\nu}\cdot e^{-N\xi}\cdot\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}\tau^{2j}\tfrac{j!}{(j+\nu)!}[L_{j}^{\nu}(N\xi)]^{2}\cdot(1+o(1)),\cr\end{split}

where we used (
4.12
) with
z=N\xi
to obtain the last equality.

On the other hand, we shall verify that

(4.15)

\lim_{N\to\infty}(N\xi)^{\nu}e^{-N\xi}\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}\tfrac{j!}{(j+\nu)!}[L_{j}^{\nu}(N\xi)]^{2}=\tfrac{1}{2\pi\xi}\sqrt{(4-\xi)\xi}.

To prove (
4.15
), we use the classical Christoffel-Darboux formula

\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\tfrac{j!}{(j+\nu)!}L_{j}^{\nu}(x)L_{j}^{\nu}(y)=\tfrac{n!}{(n-1+\nu)!}\tfrac{1}{x-y}(L_{n-1}^{\nu}(x)L_{n}^{\nu}(y)-L_{n-1}^{\nu}(y)L_{n}^{\nu}(x))

together with differentiation rule
\tfrac{d}{dx}L_{j}^{\nu}(x)=-L_{j-1}^{\nu+1}(x)
to obtain

\displaystyle(N\xi)^{\nu}e^{-N\xi}\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}\tfrac{j!}{(j+\nu)!}[L_{j}^{\nu}(N\xi)]^{2}

\displaystyle=(N\xi)^{\nu}e^{-N\xi}\tfrac{N!}{(N-1+\nu)!}

\displaystyle\times(L_{N-1}^{\nu}(N\xi)L_{N-1}^{\nu+1}(N\xi)-L_{N}^{\nu}(N\xi)L_{N-2}^{\nu+1}(N\xi)).

Next we apply the following Plancherel-Rotach type formula from
[
28
, Section III]
:
for
x=4nX
with
\frac{\varepsilon}{n}\leq X<1
and fixed
m
,

(4.16)

L_{n+m}^{\nu}(x)=x^{-\nu/2}e^{x/2}(-1)^{n+m}(2\pi\sqrt{X(1-X)})^{-1/2}n^{\nu/2-1/2}(g_{n,m}^{\nu}(X)+O(\tfrac{m}{n})),

where

g_{n,m}^{\nu}(X)=\sin(2n(\sqrt{X(1-X)}-\arccos\sqrt{X})-(2m+\nu+1)\arccos\sqrt{X}+3\pi/4).

Using (
4.16
), we obtain that

\displaystyle\quad L_{N-1}^{\nu}(N\xi)L_{N-1}^{\nu+1}(N\xi)-L_{N}^{\nu}(N\xi)L_{N-2}^{\nu+1}(N\xi)

\displaystyle=(N\xi)^{-\nu}e^{N\xi}N^{\nu-1}\cdot\tfrac{2}{\pi}\tfrac{1}{\xi\sqrt{4-\xi}}(g_{N,-1}^{\nu}(X)g_{N,-1}^{\nu+1}(X)-g_{N,0}^{\nu}(X)g_{N,-2}^{\nu+1}(X))\cdot(1+o(1)),

where
X=\frac{\xi}{4}
. Hence, using an elementary trigonometric reduction formula, we infer that

\displaystyle g_{N,-1}^{\nu}(X)g_{N,-1}^{\nu+1}(X)-g_{N,0}^{\nu}(X)g_{N,-2}^{\nu+1}(X)

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{2}(\sqrt{X}-\cos(3\arccos\sqrt{X}))=\tfrac{1}{4}\sqrt{\xi}(4-\xi).

This gives that the convergence (
4.15
) holds locally uniformly for
\xi\in(0,4)
.
(We refer to
[
34
, Propoistion 1]
or
[
27
, Subsection 7.2.3]
for further expansion of (
4.15
) up to order
O(1/N^{2}).
)

Combining (
4.14
) and (
4.15
), using
that
\tau=a/b\to 1
as
N\to\infty
, it follows that

c_{N}(\xi)\to\pi\cdot\sigma_{\mathrm{MP}}(\xi)
for all
\xi\in I_{\alpha}.
This finishes our proof for Theorem
1.6
.
∎

Bulk scaling limits

In this section we obtain bulk scaling limits for the almost-Hermitian GUE and LUE, i.e., we prove Theorem
1.4
and Theorem
1.7
, respectively.

5.1.
Bulk scaling limits for AGUE

We now prove Theorem
1.4
.
To this end, recall that
Q_{N}(\zeta)=\tfrac{1}{2}\xi^{2}+\tfrac{1}{2}\tfrac{N}{c^{2}}\eta^{2}
. We also fix a small parameter
\alpha
,
0<\alpha<2
and
write
I_{\alpha}=[-2+\alpha,2-\alpha]
. Finally we fix a point
p_{*}\in I_{\alpha}
.

Let
R=\lim R_{N_{k}}
be a limiting rescaled 1-point function about
p_{*}
(guaranteed to exist by Lemma
3.5
). By Theorem
3.8
it suffices to prove that

R
is horizontal translation invariant, for then
R
will be given by the explicit form in (
3.5
).

Next recall that
R_{N}(z)=\tfrac{1}{N\Delta Q_{N}}\mathbf{R}_{N}(\zeta)
, where
z={\scriptstyle\sqrt{N\Delta Q_{N}}}\cdot(\zeta-p_{*})
, i.e.,

\zeta=p_{*}+\tfrac{2c}{N}\,z\cdot(1+O(N^{-\frac{1}{2}})).

Thus using (
4.1
) and Lemma
4.1

(with
a=\tfrac{1}{2}
,
b=\tfrac{1}{2}\tfrac{N}{c^{2}}
,
\tau=\tfrac{b-a}{b+a}
)
we obtain after some elementary manipulations,

(5.1)

\displaystyle\tfrac{{\partial}R_{N}}{{\partial}x}(z)

\displaystyle=-e^{-NQ_{N}(\zeta)}\tfrac{4a}{\sqrt{(b-a)\Delta Q_{N}}}\tfrac{(\tau/2)^{N}}{(N-1)!}\cdot\operatorname{Re}\{H_{N-1}(\sqrt{\tfrac{Nab}{b-a}}\zeta)H_{N}(\sqrt{\tfrac{Nab}{b-a}}\bar{\zeta})\}

(5.2)

\displaystyle=-e^{-NQ_{N}(\zeta)}(1+o(1))\tfrac{4\sqrt{2}c^{2}e^{-2c^{2}}}{2^{N}N!}\cdot\operatorname{Re}\{H_{N-1}(\sqrt{\tfrac{N}{2}}\zeta_{1})H_{N}(\sqrt{\tfrac{N}{2}}\bar{\zeta_{1}})\},

where we write

\zeta_{1}=p_{*}+\tfrac{cz}{\sqrt{N/2}}\cdot(1+O(N^{-\frac{1}{2}}))

Here, the second line follows from
\tau^{N}=e^{-2c^{2}}+O(N^{-1})
.

Now fix a large number
M
.
Applying the Plancherel-Rotach asymptotic in Lemma
A.1
, we obtain
for all
w
with
|w|\leq M
and all
p_{*}\in I_{\alpha}
that

(5.3)

\displaystyle H_{N}(\sqrt{\tfrac{N}{2}}\cdot p_{*}+\tfrac{w}{\sqrt{N}})=e^{\frac{N}{4}p_{*}^{2}}2^{\frac{N}{2}}\sqrt{N!}\cdot N^{-\frac{1}{4}}\cdot O(1),\qquad(N\to\infty),

where the
O(1)
constant depends on
M
and
\alpha
. Inserting this in (

in a given compact subset of

, that

To be more precise, it follows from

Letting

∎

We now prove Theorem

Again we fix a small

)

Recall that

(5.4)

Moreover, when

Next recall the relation (

(5.7)

\mathbf{R}_{N}(\zeta)=C_{N}^{2}\cdot G_{N}(\tfrac{a^{2}-b^{2}}{2b}N\zeta)\cdot e^{-NQ_{N}(\zeta)},\quad\scriptstyle(a=\tfrac{N}{c^{2}}\,\hskip 8.19447ptb=\tfrac{N}{c^{2}}-1,\hskip 8.19447ptC_{N}=\sqrt{aN}(\tfrac{a^{2}-b^{2}}{2a}N)^{\frac{\nu+1}{2}}).

We will invoke an identity found in

(5.8)

\displaystyle G_{N}(z)

\displaystyle=\tfrac{\tau^{2N}e^{\bar{z}}}{4\pi^{2}z^{\nu}}U_{N}(z),\qquad\scriptstyle(\tau=1-\tfrac{c^{2}}{N}),

(5.9)

\displaystyle U_{N}(z)

\displaystyle=\oint_{\gamma_{1}}du\oint_{\gamma_{2}}dv\left(\frac{v(u-1)}{(v-1)u}\right)^{\nu}\left(\frac{v}{u}\right)^{N}\frac{e^{z\frac{u}{u-1}-\bar{z}\frac{v}{v-1}}}{(\tau^{2}v-u)(v-1)(u-1)},

Lemma 5.1
.

With

and

\tfrac{{\partial}U_{N}}{{\partial}x}(z)=(2\pi i)^{2}(-1)^{\nu}e^{-\bar{z}}L_{N+\nu-1}^{1-\nu}(z)L_{N-1}^{\nu+1}(\bar{z})\cdot(1+o(1)),\qquad(N\to\infty).

Proof.

By a straightforward computation,

\displaystyle{\scriptstyle\frac{{\partial}}{{\partial}x}\Big[\frac{e^{z\frac{u}{u-1}-\bar{z}\tfrac{v}{v-1}}}{\tau^{2}v-u}\Big]}={\scriptstyle\frac{e^{z\frac{u}{u-1}-\bar{z}\tfrac{v}{v-1}}}{(u-1)(v-1)}}\cdot(1+o(1)).

Differentiating under the integral sign, we obtain that

\tfrac{{\partial}U_{N}}{{\partial}x}(z)=\oint_{\gamma_{1}}\tfrac{(u-1)^{\nu-2}}{u^{N+\nu}}e^{z\frac{u}{u-1}}\,du\oint_{\gamma_{2}}\tfrac{v^{N+\nu}}{(v-1)^{\nu+2}}e^{-\bar{z}\frac{v}{v-1}}\,dv\cdot(1+o(1)).

We now recall an integral representation for Laguerre polynomials, found for example in
[
3
, Section III]
,

(5.10)

L_{j}^{\nu}(z)=\tfrac{1}{2\pi i}\oint_{\gamma}\tfrac{e^{-z\frac{s}{1-s}}}{(1-s)^{\nu+1}s^{j+1}}\,ds,

where the contour
\gamma
encircles the origin

but not the essential singularity at
s=1
. The identity (
5.10
) immediately implies

\tfrac{1}{2\pi i}\oint_{\gamma_{1}}\tfrac{(u-1)^{\nu-2}}{u^{N+\nu}}e^{z\frac{u}{u-1}}\,du=(-1)^{\nu}L_{N+\nu-1}^{1-\nu}(z).

On the other hand the change of variable

gives

\displaystyle\tfrac{1}{2\pi i}\oint_{\gamma_{2}}\tfrac{v^{N+\nu}}{(v-1)^{\nu+2}}e^{-\bar{z}\frac{v}{v-1}}\,dv

\displaystyle=e^{-\bar{z}}\tfrac{1}{2\pi i}\oint_{\gamma}\tfrac{e^{-\bar{z}\frac{w}{1-w}}}{(1-w)^{\nu+2}w^{N}}\,dw=e^{-\bar{z}}L_{N-1}^{\nu+1}(\bar{z}).

Combining all of the above, we obtain the lemma.
∎

Combining the identities (
5.5

5.8
) and using (

(5.11)

\displaystyle\begin{split}R_{N}(z)&=\tfrac{a}{\Delta Q_{N}(p_{*})}e^{-NQ_{N}(\zeta)}(\tfrac{a^{2}-b^{2}}{2a}N)^{\nu+1}G_{N}(\tfrac{a^{2}-b^{2}}{2b}N\,\zeta(z))\\
&=\tfrac{a}{\Delta Q_{N}(p_{*})}e^{-NQ_{N}(\zeta)}(\tfrac{a^{2}-b^{2}}{2a}N)^{\nu+1}\tfrac{\tau^{2N}}{4\pi^{2}}(\tfrac{a^{2}-b^{2}}{2b}N\,\zeta(z))^{-\nu}e^{\tfrac{a^{2}-b^{2}}{2b}N\,\bar{\zeta}(z)}U_{N}(\tfrac{a^{2}-b^{2}}{2b}N\,\zeta(z))\\
&=\tfrac{aN}{\Delta Q_{N}(p_{*})}\tfrac{a^{2}-b^{2}}{2a}\tfrac{\tau^{2N+\nu}}{4\pi^{2}}\zeta(z)^{-\nu}e^{-NQ_{N}(\zeta)}e^{\tfrac{a^{2}-b^{2}}{2b}N\,\bar{\zeta}(z)}U_{N}(\tfrac{a^{2}-b^{2}}{2b}N\,\zeta(z))\cdot(1+o(1))\\
&=\tfrac{c\sqrt{p_{*}}}{\sqrt{2}\pi^{3/2}}e^{-2y^{2}-2c\sqrt{p_{*}}yi-2c^{2}}U_{N}(\tfrac{a^{2}-b^{2}}{2b}N\,\zeta(z))\cdot(1+o(1)).\end{split}

For the last equality, we also used
\tau^{2N}=e^{-2c^{2}}(1+o(1))
and (
5.6
).

Therefore it remains to show that

\tfrac{{\partial}}{{\partial}x}U_{N}(\tfrac{a^{2}-b^{2}}{2b}N\,\zeta(x+iy))\to 0
as

, where

is the map (

However, by Lemma

(and since

\begin{split}\tfrac{{\partial}}{{\partial}x}U_{N}&(\tfrac{a^{2}-b^{2}}{2b}N\,\zeta(z))=Ce^{-\frac{a^{2}-b^{2}}{2b}N\bar{\zeta}}\cr&\times L_{N+\nu-1}^{1-\nu}(\tfrac{a^{2}-b^{2}}{2b}N\,\zeta)\cdot L_{N-1}^{\nu+1}(\tfrac{a^{2}-b^{2}}{2b}N\,\bar{\zeta})\cdot(1+o(1)),\cr\end{split}

where the constant

is independent of

. By (

\tfrac{a^{2}-b^{2}}{2b}N\zeta=Np_{*}+\tfrac{c^{2}}{2}p_{*}+2c\sqrt{p_{*}}\,z+O(N^{-1}).

When

\displaystyle=C\tfrac{1}{N}\,e^{\frac{a^{2}-b^{2}}{2b}N\frac{\zeta(z)-\bar{\zeta}(z)}{2}}\cdot O(1)

\displaystyle=C\tfrac{1}{N}e^{2c\sqrt{p_{*}}i\operatorname{Im}z}\cdot O(1)=O(\tfrac{1}{N}).

We have shown that each subsequential limit

∎

(a)

\zeta=1/\sqrt{2}

(b)

\zeta=\sqrt{2}

(c)

\zeta=2

We rescale the process

R(z)=\tfrac{d}{2}\,|z|^{2d-2}\,K_{\nu}(|z|^{d})e^{\,\operatorname{Re}z^{d}}\int_{0}^{2c}s\,e^{\,-s^{2}/2}|J_{\nu}(s\,z^{d/2})|^{2}\,ds.

and

namely between (1-dimensional) anti-symmetric sine-processes and (2-dimensional) Mittag-Leffler fields.

To this end, we first note that passing to the limit as
c\to\infty

(7.5)

R_{(c=\infty)}(z)=\tfrac{d}{2}\,|z|^{2d-2}\,K_{\nu}(|z|^{d})I_{\nu}(|z|^{d}).

To obtain a 1-dimensional perspective, we rescale once more. Given the limiting point-field
\{z_{j}\}_{1}^{\infty}

\{\tilde{z}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{\infty}

\tilde{z}_{j}=c^{2}z_{j}

In the limit as

\tilde{R}_{(c)}

by

(7.6)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\tilde{R}_{(c=0)}(e^{\frac{2\pi ik}{d}}\cdot x)&=\tfrac{\pi d}{4}\,x^{d-1}(J_{\nu}(x^{\frac{d}{2}})^{2}-J_{\nu+1}(x^{\frac{d}{2}})J_{\nu-1}(x^{\frac{d}{2}})),\quad(x>0).\end{split}

R_{(c=\infty)}(z)=\begin{cases}e^{-|z|^{2}}\sinh(|z|^{2})&\text{if }\nu=+\tfrac{1}{2},\\

The

[

]
.

On the other hand, for

8.
Further results and concluding remarks

In this section, we give further results and provide some concluding remarks. We shall consider variants of our main model ensembles
(generalized ALUE, induced AGUE, hard edge ensembles) and compare with other related works.

8.1.
Almost-Hermitian LUE with rectangular parameter

For a non-negative integer
\nu
(and
\beta=1
), a random sample
\{\zeta_{j}\}_{1}^{N}
picked with respect to the ALUE-potential
Q_{N}
in (
1.24
) can be realized as the eigenvalues of the product matrix
X=X_{1}X_{2}^{*}

where

(8.1)

X_{1}=\sqrt{1+\tau}\,P+\sqrt{1-\tau}\,Q,\quad X_{2}=\sqrt{1+\tau}\,P-\sqrt{1-\tau}\,Q,\quad\tau=1-\tfrac{c^{2}}{N}.

Here
P
and
Q
are rectangular matrices of size
N\times(N+\nu)
, with independent, centered, complex Gaussian entries of variance
\tfrac{1}{4N}
. (See e.g.,
[
4
,
40
]
)

We now fix
\alpha\geq 0
and consider the case when
\nu=\nu_{N}
(not necessarily an integer) varies with
N
as

\nu_{N}=\alpha N\cdot(1+o(1))

where
o(1)\to 0
as
N\to\infty
.
Thus we consider the potential

(8.2)

Q_{N}(\zeta)=\tfrac{1}{N}\log\left[K_{\nu_{N}}(\tfrac{N^{2}|\zeta|}{c^{2}})\cdot|\zeta|^{\nu_{N}}\right]-(\tfrac{N}{c^{2}}-1)\cdot\operatorname{Re}\zeta.

We shall use the code-notation ALUE
(\alpha)
to denote a corresponding random sample
\{\zeta_{j}\}_{1}^{N}
(and various limits as
N\to\infty
).

By
[
4
, Theorem 1]
, the droplet
S_{Q_{N}}
is approximately given by the equation

(\xi-\alpha-2)^{2}+(N\eta/c^{2})^{2}\leq 4(\alpha+1).

Note in particular that for
\alpha>0
, the origin is outside of the droplet for large
N
since
(\alpha+2)^{2}>4(\alpha+1).
This makes the analysis somewhat easier, with more tools being available.

Also note that
Q_{N}
converges pointwise to the limit

(8.3)

V(\xi)=\xi-\alpha\log\xi,\qquad\xi>0,

and
V=+\infty
off of
[0,\infty)
. Cf.
[
4
, Subsection 3.1]
.

It is well known (again
[
4
]
and references) that the equilibrium density in potential (
8.3
) is the Marchenko-Pastur law
\sigma_{\mathrm{MP}(\alpha)}
given by

(8.4)

\sigma_{\mathrm{MP}(\alpha)}(\xi)=\tfrac{1}{2\pi\,\xi}\sqrt{(\lambda_{+}-\xi)(\xi-\lambda_{-})}\cdot\mathbf{1}_{[\lambda_{-},\lambda_{+}]}(\xi),\qquad\lambda_{\pm}=(\sqrt{\alpha+1}\pm 1)^{2}.

Now write
c_{N}(\xi)=\tfrac{1}{N}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathbf{R}_{N}(\xi+i\eta)\,d\eta
for the usual cross-section. We have the following result, which generalizes Theorem
1.6
.

Theorem 8.1
.

(“
Cross-section convergence for ALUE
(\alpha)
”) We have the convergence
\tfrac{1}{\pi}c_{N}\to\sigma_{\mathrm{MP}(\alpha)}
in the weak sense of measures, as
N\to\infty
.

Remark on the proof.

Our proof in the case
\alpha=0
in Subsection
4.2
generalizes without difficulty to the case when
\alpha>0
, and in fact we could have treated the general case
\alpha\geq 0
at once. Indeed, the
j:
th orthonormal polynomial
q_{j}
in weight
e^{-NQ_{N}/2}
can be taken as

(8.5)

q_{j}(\zeta)=\tfrac{N}{c}(N-\tfrac{c^{2}}{2})^{\frac{\nu_{N}+1}{2}}(1-\tfrac{c^{2}}{N})^{j}\sqrt{\tfrac{j!}{(j+\nu_{N})!}}L_{j}^{\nu_{N}}(\tfrac{N(2N-c^{2})}{2(N-c^{2})}\,\zeta).

With minor modifications, these polynomials can be analyzed using similar techniques as in the case
\alpha=0
. Details are omitted.
∎

Now fix a bulk point
p_{*}
(i.e.,
\lambda_{-}<p_{*}<\lambda_{+}
). We rescale the process
\{\zeta_{j}\}_{1}^{N}
about
p_{*}
as usual (see (
1.12
)) where
Q_{N}
is given by (
8.2
), and
denote by
R_{N}
the 1-point function of the rescaled system
\{z_{j}\}_{1}^{N}
.
It is easy to see that
the limit (
1.7
) is given by
\rho(p_{*})=2c\sqrt{p_{*}}
for all
\alpha

(cf.
[
4
, Subsection 3.1]
for details).

We have the following generalization of Theorem
1.7
.

Theorem 8.2
.

(“
Bulk scaling limit for ALUE
(\alpha)
”) For any
\alpha\geq 0
, if
\lambda_{-}<p_{*}<\lambda_{+}
and if
\nu>-1
is an integer then
R_{N}\to R
locally uniformly where
R(z)=F(2\operatorname{Im}z)
and
F(z)=\gamma*\mathbf{1}_{(-2a,2a)}(z)
,
a=a(p_{*})=\frac{\pi}{2}\cdot\rho(p_{*})\cdot\sigma_{\mathrm{MP}(\alpha)}(p_{*})
.

Remark on the proof.

Our proof in the case
\alpha=0
in Subsection
5.2
generalizes in a straightforward way to
\alpha>0
, using again the form of orthogonal polynomials (
8.5
). We omit details.
∎

8.2.
Induced Almost-Hermitian GUE

We now briefly consider the
induced AGUE
. This is a natural generalization of AGUE introduced in the paper
[
7
]
, obtained by inserting a point charge (of strength
2\nu
) at the origin.

For the definition, we fix
c>0
and put

q_{N}(\zeta)=\tfrac{1}{2}\xi^{2}+\tfrac{1}{2}\tfrac{N}{c^{2}}\eta^{2}.

Ne next fix
a real number
\nu>-1/2
and consider the potential

(8.6)

Q_{N}(\zeta)=q_{N}(\zeta)+\tfrac{2\nu}{N}\log\tfrac{1}{|\zeta|}.

Let
\{\zeta_{j}\}_{1}^{N}
be a random sample with respect to the corresponding Boltzmann-Gibbs measure (
1.2
) (with
\beta=1
) and rescale about the origin via
z_{j}={\scriptstyle\sqrt{N\Delta q_{N}}}\cdot\zeta_{j}
.
Writing
R_{N}^{(\nu,c)}(z)
for the 1-point function of
\{z_{j}\}_{1}^{N}
, it is natural to try to characterize limiting 1-point functions
R^{(\nu,c)}=\lim_{k\to\infty}R_{N_{k}}^{(\nu,c)}
. It was observed
in
[
7
]
that when
\nu
is an integer,
R^{(\nu,c)}
as well as a correlation kernel
K^{(\nu,c)}
,
can be obtained from the known case
\nu=0
by an inductive procedure. For example, since
K^{(\nu=0)}
is nothing but the kernel
K
from (
1.20
), we obtain after a brief computation

(8.7)

\begin{split}R^{(1,c)}(z)&=R^{(0,c)}(z)-B^{(0,c)}(0,z)\\
&=R^{(0,c)}(z)-\tfrac{1}{4\textup{erf}(\sqrt{2}c)}e^{-|z|^{2}}|\textup{erf}(\tfrac{2c+iz}{\sqrt{2}})+\textup{erf}(\tfrac{2c-iz}{\sqrt{2}})|^{2},\end{split}

where we used the interpretation of the Berezin kernel
B^{(0,c)}(0,z)=\tfrac{|K^{(0,c)}(0,z)|^{2}}{R^{(0,c)}(0)}
as the
difference
R^{(0,c)}(z)-R^{(1,c)}(z)
, see e.g.
[
13
, Lemma 7.6.2]
. A similar reasoning is used in
[
7
]
to go from
\nu=1
to
\nu=2
, and this procedure
can in principle be repeated to obtain correlation kernels for all positive integers
\nu
. (We are not aware a result for general real
\nu
at this time.)

We now explain how the above kernels
K^{(\nu,c)}
(for integers
\nu
) interpolate between known insertion ensembles in dimension one in
[
43
,
22
]
, and in dimension
two from the papers
[
26
,
17
]
. It will suffice to treat the case
\nu=1
in detail.

More precisely, let
V(\xi)
be the Gaussian potential,
V(\xi)=\tfrac{1}{2}\xi^{2}
for
\xi\in\mathbb{R}
and
+\infty
otherwise, and consider the corresponding insertion potential

V_{N}(\zeta)=V(\zeta)+\tfrac{2\nu}{N}\log\tfrac{1}{|\zeta|}
.

It is shown in
[
43
, Theorem 1.1]
that in the appropriate scaling limit, a microscopic correlation kernel at the origin takes the following form, for
x,y\in\mathbb{R}
with

(8.8)

K_{\mathbb{R}}^{(\nu)}(x,y)=\tfrac{\pi}{2}\tfrac{\sqrt{xy}}{(x-y)}(J_{\nu+\frac{1}{2}}(\pi x)J_{\nu-\frac{1}{2}}(\pi y)-J_{\nu+\frac{1}{2}}(\pi y)J_{\nu-\frac{1}{2}}(\pi x)).

On the other hand, taking
Q_{N}(\zeta)=|\zeta|^{2}+\tfrac{2\nu}{N}\log\tfrac{1}{|\zeta|}
and rescaling in a natural way about the origin, one obtains
a point field with correlation kernel

(8.9)

K^{(\nu)}_{\mathbb{C}}(z,w)=G(z,w)P(\nu,z\bar{w}),\qquad P(a,z)=\tfrac{\gamma(a,z)}{\Gamma(a)},

where
G
is the Ginibre kernel (
1.19
) and
\gamma(a,z)
is the lower incomplete Gamma function. This kernel appears in
[
26
]
and in
[
17
]
, for example.

,

(b)

Sending
c\to\infty
in (

) we can recover (

), i.e.,

\lim_{c\to\infty}R^{(1,c)}(z)=1-e^{-|z|^{2}}=R^{(1)}_{\mathbb{C}}(z).

Here, we have used

as
c\to\infty
and
\textup{erf}(x)\to 1
as
x\to+\infty
.

To pass to the corresponding one-dimensional limit, after an appropriate rescaling, we are led to consider the 1-point function

\tilde{R}^{(\nu,c)}(z)=\tfrac{1}{\alpha^{2}}R^{(\nu,c)}(\tfrac{z}{\alpha}),\qquad\alpha=\tfrac{2}{\pi}c.

Write
\tilde{K}^{(0,c)}
for a corresponding rescaled correlation kernel,
\tilde{R}^{(\nu,c)}(z)=\tilde{K}^{(\nu,c)}(z,z)
.

By the discussion after the formulation of Theorem

, we can arrange that
\lim_{c\to 0}\tilde{K}^{(0,c)}=\pi K^{\mathrm{sin}}
. Hence
\tilde{R}^{(1,c)}(z)
converges to
\tilde{R}_{\mathbb{R}}^{(1)}(x)=\pi K_{\mathbb{R}}^{(1)}(x,x)
, as
c\to 0
if
z=x
is real (and
\tilde{R}^{(1,c)}(z)\to 0
otherwise)
where

K_{\mathbb{R}}^{(1)}(x,y)=\tfrac{1}{\pi}\tfrac{\sin(\pi x-\pi y)}{x-y}-\tfrac{1}{\pi^{2}}\tfrac{\sin(\pi x)\sin(\pi y)}{xy}

which is the same as (
8.8
) when
\nu=1
.

We have shown that the 1-point function
R^{(\nu,c)}
interpolates between
R^{(\nu)}_{\mathbb{R}}
and
R^{(\nu)}_{\mathbb{C}}
when
\nu=1
, see Figure

a similar proof works in the case when
\nu
is an arbitrary integer.

8.3.
Almost-Hermitian hard-edge ensembles

Let
(Q_{N})_{1}^{\infty}
be a suitable sequence of potentials, for instance the AGUE-potentials in (
1.24
), or a more general admissible sequence in the sense of Section
2
. In particular, the potential
Q_{N}
is real-analytic in a neighbourhood of the boundary
{\partial}S_{Q_{N}}
.

In the hard edge setting we completely confine the gas to the droplet by redefining the potential
Q_{N}
by setting

Q_{N}(\zeta)=+\infty
when
\zeta\not\in S_{Q_{N}}.

Similarly, we redefine the limiting potential
V=\lim Q_{N}
by setting
V=+\infty
outside of
S_{V}
. This kind of hard edge ensembles are studied in the papers
[
14
,
15
,
16
,
21
,
52
]
.

Fix a point
p_{*}
in the bulk, i.e., a point

p_{*}\in\mathbb{R}
satisfying
\sigma_{V}(p_{*})>0
. We also assume that the limit
\rho(p_{*})
in (
1.7
) exists and is strictly positive. We recall that the asymptotic height of the rescaled droplet is then given by
the expression
a(p_{*})
in (
1.8
), i.e.,

a(p_{*})=\tfrac{\pi}{2}\cdot\rho(p_{*})\cdot\sigma_{V}(p_{*}).

Under these hypothesis we look at the cross-sections

c_{N}(p_{*})=\tfrac{1}{N}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathbf{R}_{N}(p_{*}+i\eta)\,d\eta,

where
\mathbf{R}_{N}
is the
1
-point function for the hard edge ensemble
\{\zeta_{j}\}_{1}^{N}
associated with the redefined potential. (So in particular
\mathbf{R}_{N}=0
on
\mathbb{C}\setminus S_{Q_{N}}
.)

As in the case of “free-boundary ensembles”, we expect that the cross-sections should enjoy the convergence
\tfrac{1}{\pi}c_{N}(p_{*})\to\sigma_{V}(p_{*})
, but our above proofs in the cases
of AGUE/ALUE, which depend on computations with particular orthogonal polynomials, do not immediately carry over to a hard edge setting. In order not to complicate matters, let us

assume
for the sake of argument that this limit holds, i.e., that

(8.10)

\lim_{N\to\infty}c_{N}(p_{*})=\pi\cdot\sigma_{V}(p_{*}).

Given these assumptions, we now rescale about
p_{*}
precisely as in the free-boundary case (see (
1.6
)) and we write
R_{N}
for the
1
-point function of the rescaled system.
The structure theorem for limiting kernels (analogue of Lemma
3.5
) then takes the following form.

Lemma 8.3
.

(“
Structure of limiting hard edge kernels
”) Under the above hypotheses, there exists a sequence
c_{N}(z,w)
of cocycles such that each subsequence of the kernels

c_{N}K_{N}
has a further subsequence which converges locally uniformly on the set

\Sigma(p_{*})=\{(z,w)\in\mathbb{C}^{2}\,;\,|\operatorname{Im}z|<a(p_{*}),\,|\operatorname{Im}w|<a(p_{*})\}

to a Hermitian kernel of the form

K(z,w)=G(z,w)\cdot L(z,w)
where
G
is the Ginibre kernel and
L
is a Hermitian-analytic function on
\Sigma(p_{*})
.

Remark on the proof.

This follows by a simple adaptation of the normal-families argument used in the proof of Lemma
3.5
. See
[
15
, Subection 1.9]
for related comments.
∎

By the lemma, we can form limiting
1
-point functions
R(z)=\lim R_{N_{k}}(z)
which are smooth in the strip

|\operatorname{Im}z|<a(p_{*})
and satisfy
R(z)=0
when
|\operatorname{Im}z|>a(p_{*})
. Since

R
is not identically zero by the assumption (
8.10
), we can assert that
R>0
throughout the strip, and that Ward’s equation

(8.11)

\bar{\partial}C(z)=R(z)-1-\Delta\log R(z),\qquad|\operatorname{Im}z|<a(p_{*})

holds if we understand
C(z)
as the function

B(z,w)=\tfrac{|K(z,w)|^{2}}{R(z)}\mathbf{1}_{\Sigma(p_{*})}(z,w),\qquad C(z)=\int\tfrac{B(z,w)}{z-w}\,dA(w).

Note that (
8.11
) gives a hard edge analogue of Corollary
3.7
. The proof in the hard edge case works basically the same way as in the free boundary case,
again see
[
15
, Subection 1.9]
.

Now recall the function
F=\gamma*\mathbf{1}_{(-2a,2a)}
appearing in the free boundary case (Theorem
1.4
), i.e.,

F(z)=\gamma*\mathbf{1}_{(-2a,2a)}(z)=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{-2a}^{\,2a}e^{-\frac{1}{2}(z-t)^{2}}\,dt.

We can now state the following theorem.

Theorem 8.4
.

Under the above assumptions (in particular we assume that the cross-section convergence (
8.10
) holds), each translation invariant
limiting 1-point function
R
at
p_{*}
is of the form

(8.12)

\displaystyle R(z)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{1}_{\{|\operatorname{Im}z|<a\}}\cdot\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{-2a}^{2a}\tfrac{e^{-\frac{1}{2}(2\operatorname{Im}z-t)^{2}}}{F(t)}\,dt,

where
a=a(p_{*})
is given in (
1.8
).

Proof.

We appeal to the characterization of translation invariant solutions to the hard edge Ward equation (
8.11
) found in
[
15
, Theorem 6]
, which gives that each translation
invariant
R
must be given by the formula (
8.12
) for some
a>0
. Then the rescaled version of the cross-section convergence (
8.10
) fixes the value of
a
as
a(p_{*})
.
∎

The assumptions (cross-section convergence and translation invariance) may in general be subtle to check, even in the simplest model cases such as for hard edge AGUE. We shall not dwell on this
matter here (it will be the topic of a forthcoming study), but we remark that Theorem
8.4
yields further support for the conjecture in
[
15
]
that the parameter-value
a=a(p_{*})
should
yield the correct “physical” solution to Ward’s equation in a strip.

.

8.4.
Further related works

Beyond the topics we study in this work, there have been several investigations on almost-Hermitian regime in various contexts. In
[
6
]
, a fixed-trace version of
AGUE is studied, which brings about some new challenges since this ensemble is not determinantal.
The paper
[
38
]
studies
the interpolation problem between the Tracy-Widom and
Gumbel distributions.
An almost-Hermitian analogue of the Jacobi unitary ensemble
was recently discovered in
[
48
]
.
The bulk statistics for almost-Hermitian Gaussian ensembles in orthogonal and symplectic symmetry classes are found in
[
29
]
and
[
39
]
respectively, and edge statistics is studied in
[
8
]
. Analogous problems for Laguerre ensembles are investigated in
[
2
,
10
]
. These works, which construct
2\times 2
matrix-valued kernels of Pfaffian point processes, use explicit computations with skew-orthogonal polynomials.

We also want to mention the paper
[
30
]
, where a different kind of almost-Hermitian model is introduced, motivated by applications to resonance statistics in quantum chaotic scattering. This model is non-determinantal, but in
[
30
]
it is argued that one can still define determinantal universality limits given by certain explicit kernels. Some rigorous results in this direction were obtained by Kozhan in
[
42
]
. (We thank Yan Fyodorov for this remark.)

We finally want to mention that in dimensions 1 and 2, various microscopic limits in the determinantal case
\beta=2
have been used to extract information about other
\beta
-ensembles, and specifically, they were used to study “freezing transitions”, which occur for large
\beta
, in
[
18
,
46
]
and the references there. As far as we are aware, a rigorous analysis for almost Hermitian
\beta
-ensembles, relying on properties of various explicit kernels found above, has not yet been carried out, and could potentially be quite interesting.

Appendix A
Plancherel-Rotach asymptotics for Hermite polynomials

In this appendix we collect certain asymptotic formulas for Hermite polynomials which are used in our proofs of cross-section convergence in Subsection
4.1
and of translation invariance in Subsection
5.1
. In both cases, we fix a point
p\in(-2,2)
, and we look at asymptotics for the scaled Hermite polynomial
H_{n}(\sqrt{\tfrac{N}{2}}\zeta)

where
n=N-1
or
n=N
and
\zeta
is in a microscopic neighbourhood of
p
(of radius proportional to
N^{-1/2}
) or on the vertical line

p+i\mathbb{R}
. Recall that here
H_{n}(z)=(-1)^{n}e^{z^{2}}\tfrac{d^{n}}{dz^{n}}e^{-z^{2}}
is the
n
:th “physicists’ Hermite polynomial”, which has leading coefficient
2^{n}
.

It turns out that our desired estimates can be deduced using strong asymptotic formulas of Plancherel-Rotach type, as given in the paper
[
57
]
. These formulas look a little different depending on whether
-2<p<0
,
p=0
, or
0<p<2
.

The following partially overlapping asymptotic formulas are known.
By
[
57
, Corollary 4.1]
, we have
for
z
in a neighborhood of
p
, where
0<p<2
,

(A.1)

\displaystyle\begin{split}H_{n}(\sqrt{\tfrac{n}{2}}z)&=(\tfrac{2n}{e})^{\frac{n}{2}}(4-z^{2})^{-\frac{1}{4}}e^{\frac{nz^{2}}{4}}\\
&\times 2\cos[(n+\tfrac{1}{2})\arccos\tfrac{z}{2}-\tfrac{nz}{4}\sqrt{4-z^{2}}-\tfrac{\pi}{4}]\cdot(1+o(1)).\end{split}

For comparison, note that
[
57
]
uses monic orthogonal polynomials
\pi_{n}
rather than
H_{n}
, so
H_{n}=2^{n}\pi_{n}
. (There is a similar formula for
-2<p<0
in
[
57
]
, which we skip stating here.)

In a microscopic neighbourhood of the origin, we have instead the following Mehler-Heine formula (see
[
50
, Section 18.11]
)

(A.2)

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\tfrac{(-1)^{n}\sqrt{n}}{2^{2n}n!}H_{2n}(\tfrac{z}{2\sqrt{n}})=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}}\cos z,\quad\lim_{n\to\infty}\tfrac{(-1)^{n}}{2^{2n}n!}H_{2n+1}(\tfrac{z}{2\sqrt{n}})=\tfrac{2}{\sqrt{\pi}}\sin z,

which holds uniformly for
z
in any compact subset of
\mathbb{C}
.

Finally, for
z

in the complement of a neighbourhood of the interval
[-2,2]
, we have by
[
45
, Lemma 2.5]
or
[
57
, Corollary 4.1]

(A.3)

\displaystyle\begin{split}H_{n}(\sqrt{\tfrac{n}{2}}z)&=(\tfrac{n}{2e})^{\frac{n}{2}}(z+\sqrt{z^{2}-4})^{n}(\tfrac{z+\sqrt{z^{2}-4}}{2\sqrt{z^{2}-4}})^{\frac{1}{2}}e^{\frac{n}{4}z(z-\sqrt{z^{2}-4})}\cdot(1+o(1)).\end{split}

Using (
A.1
), (
A.2
) and Stirling’s formula, one easily obtains the following lemma.

Lemma A.1
.

Suppose that
|p|\leq\alpha
where
\alpha<2
and
fix
M>0
. Then for all
z
with
|z|\leq M
, we have

(A.4)

H_{N}(\sqrt{\tfrac{N}{2}}\cdot p+\tfrac{z}{\sqrt{N}})=e^{\frac{N}{4}p^{2}}2^{\frac{N}{2}}\sqrt{N!}\cdot N^{-\frac{1}{4}}\cdot O(1),\qquad(N\to\infty),

where the
O(1)
-constant may depend on
M
and
\alpha
.

A.1.
Computation for cross-section

Again fix
p
with
0<p<2
.

Recall from (
4.5
) that the
\tfrac{{\partial}}{{\partial}\xi}
-derivative of the 1-point function with respect to potential (
1.13
) obeys

\displaystyle\tfrac{1}{N^{2}}\tfrac{{\partial}\mathbf{R}_{N}}{{\partial}\xi}(p+i\tfrac{y}{N})=

\displaystyle-(1+o(1))\tfrac{1}{c\sqrt{2}}e^{-\frac{N}{2}p^{2}-\frac{1}{2c^{2}}y^{2}-2c^{2}}\tfrac{1}{2^{N}(N-1)!}

(A.5)

\displaystyle\times\mathrm{Re}\{H_{N-1}(\sqrt{\tfrac{N}{2}}(p+i\tfrac{y}{N}))H_{N}(\sqrt{\tfrac{N}{2}}(p-i\tfrac{y}{N}))\}.

We use (
A.1
) to conclude that there is
\delta=\delta(p)>0
and
k=k(\delta)>0
such that when
|y|\leq\delta N
, then

\displaystyle H_{N}(\sqrt{\tfrac{N}{2}}(p-i\tfrac{y}{N}))

\displaystyle=(\tfrac{2N}{e})^{\frac{N}{2}}e^{\frac{N}{4}p^{2}}\cdot\cosh(ky)\cdot O(1)

and

\displaystyle H_{N-1}(\sqrt{\tfrac{N}{2}}(p+i\tfrac{y}{N}))

\displaystyle=(\tfrac{2N}{e})^{\frac{N-1}{2}}e^{\frac{N}{4}p^{2}}\cdot\cosh(ky)\cdot O(1).

Therefore there is a constant
C
such that for all
|y|\leq N\delta
,

\tfrac{1}{N^{2}}\tfrac{\partial\mathbf{R}_{N}}{\partial\xi}(\xi+i\tfrac{y}{N})\leq C_{1}e^{-\frac{1}{2c^{2}}y^{2}}\cosh^{2}(ky),

where
C_{1}=C_{1}(C,k)
is a new constant.

On the other hand, for
y
such that
|y|/N>\delta
, letting
z=p-i\tfrac{y}{N}

\displaystyle\quad H_{N}(\sqrt{\tfrac{N}{2}}(p-i\tfrac{y}{N}))H_{N-1}(\sqrt{\tfrac{N}{2}}(p+i\tfrac{y}{N}))

\displaystyle\leq C_{2}(\tfrac{2N}{e})^{N-\frac{1}{2}}|\tfrac{z+\sqrt{z^{2}-4}}{2}|^{2N-1}e^{\frac{N}{2}\mathrm{Re}\,[z(z-\sqrt{z^{2}-4})]}

\displaystyle\leq C_{3}(\tfrac{2N}{e})^{N-\frac{1}{2}}(\tfrac{C_{4}y}{N})^{2N}e^{-\frac{y^{2}}{2N}}.

Combining all of the above, we obtain that

(A.6)

\tfrac{1}{N^{2}}\tfrac{{\partial}\mathbf{R}_{N}}{\partial\xi}(p+i\tfrac{y}{N})\leq Ce^{-\frac{1}{2c^{2}}y^{2}}\max\{1,y^{2N}N^{-N}\}\cosh^{2}(ky).

This latter estimate (
A.6
) is used in Subsection
4.1
to deduce convergence of cross-sections of the AGUE.

Appendix B
Plancherel-Rotach asymptotics for Laguerre polynomials

In this appendix, we explain how to deduce certain asymptotic formulas for Laguerre polynomials, which were used in our proof of translation invariance of bulk scaling limits for the ALUE, in Subsection
5.2
. More precisely, we shall adapt to our present situation some Plancherel-Rotach type asymptotic formulas from Vanlessen’s paper
[
56
]
.

Fix a small
\delta>0
and define a “bulk region” by

B_{\delta}=\{z\in\mathbb{C}\,;\,\delta<\mathrm{Re}\,z<1-\delta,\,-\delta<\mathrm{Im}\,z<\delta\}.

The following lemma is a special case of
[
56
, Theorem 2.4, (b)]
.

Lemma B.1
.

For
z\in B_{\delta}
we have the following form of Plancherel-Rotach asymptotic for Laguerre polynomials, as
n\to\infty
.

(B.1)

\displaystyle\begin{split}L_{n}^{\alpha}(4nz)&=(4nz)^{-\frac{\alpha}{2}}e^{2nz}(2\pi\sqrt{z(1-z)})^{-\frac{1}{2}}n^{-\frac{1}{2}}(\tfrac{(n+\alpha)!}{n!})^{\frac{1}{2}}\\
&\times[\cos(2n\sqrt{z(1-z)}-(2n+\alpha+1)\arccos\sqrt{z}+\tfrac{\pi}{4})(1+O(1/n))\\
&\quad+\cos(2n\sqrt{z(1-z)}-(2n+\alpha-1)\arccos\sqrt{z}+\tfrac{\pi}{4})O(1/n)].\end{split}

Proof.

We explain how to see this formula from
[
56
, Theorem 2.4, (b)]
.
Consider the Laguerre-type weight

w(x)=x^{\alpha}e^{-x},\qquad x\in[0,\infty).

We denote that the associated orthonormal polynomials (on
\mathbb{R}
) are given in terms of the Laguerre polynomials
L_{n}^{\alpha}
by

(B.2)

p_{n}(x)=(\tfrac{n!}{(n+\alpha)!})^{\frac{1}{2}}\,L_{n}^{\alpha}(x).

Then by
[
56
, Theorem 2.4, (b)]
(in the special case
m=1
,
Q(x)=x
,
A_{1}=\tfrac{1}{2}
,
\beta^{(0)}=4
,
\beta_{n}=4n
) we have that for
z
as above,

\displaystyle p_{n}(\beta_{n}z)

\displaystyle=(\beta_{n}z)^{-\alpha/2}e^{Q(\beta_{n}z)/2}\sqrt{\tfrac{2}{\pi\beta_{n}}}\tfrac{1}{z^{1/4}(1-z)^{1/4}}

\displaystyle\times[\cos(\tfrac{1}{2}(\alpha+1)\arccos(2z-1)-\pi n\int_{1}^{z}\psi_{n}(s)\,ds-\tfrac{\pi}{4})(1+O(1/n))

\displaystyle\quad+\cos(\tfrac{1}{2}(\alpha-1)\arccos(2z-1)-\pi n\int_{1}^{z}\psi_{n}(s)\,ds-\tfrac{\pi}{4})O(1/n)].

Here the function
\int_{1}^{z}\psi_{n}(s)\,ds
is explicitly computable (see
[
56
, Remark 2.5, 3.14, 3.15]
) and the result is

\displaystyle\int_{1}^{z}\psi_{n}(s)\,ds=\tfrac{1}{2\pi}H_{n}(z)\sqrt{z(1-z)}-\tfrac{2}{\pi}\arccos\sqrt{z},

where
H_{n}
is certain polynomial with real coefficients of degree
m-1
, i.e., it is a constant. (Indeed,
H_{n}
is the polynomial appearing in the density

\tfrac{m}{2\pi}\sqrt{\tfrac{1-x}{x}}\,H_{n}(x)

of the associated equilibrium measure.)

In the special case when
Q(x)=x
, we have
H_{n}(x)=4
, (again see
[
56
, Remark 2.3]
) thus

\int_{1}^{z}\psi_{n}(s)\,ds=\tfrac{2}{\pi}(\sqrt{z(1-z)}-\arccos\sqrt{z}).

Combining all of the above we obtain that when
Q(x)=x
,

\displaystyle p_{n}(4nz)

\displaystyle=(4nz)^{-\frac{\alpha}{2}}e^{2nz}(2\pi\sqrt{z(1-z)})^{-1/2}n^{-1/2}

\displaystyle\times[\cos(\tfrac{1}{2}(\alpha+1)\arccos(2z-1)-2n(\sqrt{z(1-z)}-\arccos\sqrt{z})-\tfrac{\pi}{4})\cdot(1+O(1/n))

\displaystyle\quad+\cos(\tfrac{1}{2}(\alpha-1)\arccos(2z-1)-2n(\sqrt{z(1-z)}-\arccos\sqrt{z})-\tfrac{\pi}{4})O(1/n)].

Note that since
\arccos(2z-1)=2\arccos\sqrt{z},
we can simplify above expression as

\displaystyle p_{n}(4nz)

\displaystyle=(4nz)^{-\alpha/2}e^{2nz}(2\pi\sqrt{z(1-z)})^{-1/2}n^{-1/2}

\displaystyle\times[\cos(2n\sqrt{z(1-z)}-(2n+\alpha+1)\arccos\sqrt{z}+\tfrac{\pi}{4})\cdot(1+O(1/n))

\displaystyle\quad+\cos(2n\sqrt{z(1-z)}-(2n+\alpha-1)\arccos\sqrt{z}+\tfrac{\pi}{4})O(1/n)].

Now (
B.1
) follows from this and the relation (
B.2
).
∎

B.1.
Computation for translation invariance

We now use Lemma
B.1
to verify the following estimate, which is used
in our proof of translation invariance in Subsection
5.2
. We will use the notation in Subsection
5.2
,
i.e., we fix a point
p_{*}\in I_{\alpha}=[\alpha,4-\alpha]
where
\alpha>0
is small and we write

\zeta(z)=p_{*}+\tfrac{2c\sqrt{p_{*}}}{N}z+o(\tfrac{1}{N}).

Moreover, we put
a=\tfrac{N}{c^{2}}
and
b=\tfrac{N}{c^{2}}-1
.

Lemma B.2
.

In the above setting, we have as
N\to\infty
,

\displaystyle L_{N+\nu-1}^{1-\nu}(\tfrac{a^{2}-b^{2}}{2b}N\,\zeta(z))L_{N-1}^{\nu+1}(\tfrac{a^{2}-b^{2}}{2b}N\,\bar{\zeta}(z))=\tfrac{1}{N}\,e^{\frac{a^{2}-b^{2}}{2b}N\frac{\zeta(z)+\bar{\zeta}(z)}{2}}\cdot O(1).

Proof.

Recall that we have for
p_{*}\in I_{\alpha}
we have

\tfrac{a^{2}-b^{2}}{2b}N\zeta=Np_{*}+\tfrac{c^{2}}{2}p_{*}+2c\sqrt{p_{*}}\,z+O(N^{-1}).

Hence by (
B.1
) together with the asymptotic

(\tfrac{(n+\alpha)!}{n!})^{\frac{1}{2}}=n^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}\cdot(1+o(1)),

we obtain

(B.3)

\displaystyle\begin{split}L_{n}^{\alpha}(4nz)&=(4nz)^{-\alpha/2}e^{2nz}(2\pi\sqrt{z(1-z)})^{-1/2}n^{\alpha/2-1/2}\cdot O(1)\\
&=n^{-1/2}e^{2nz}\cdot O(1).\end{split}

Setting
n=N+\nu-1
and then
n=N-1
we obtain

\displaystyle L_{N+\nu-1}^{1-\nu}(\tfrac{a^{2}-b^{2}}{2b}N\,\zeta(z))

\displaystyle=N^{-1/2}e^{\frac{a^{2}-b^{2}}{2b}N\frac{\zeta(z)}{2}}\cdot O(1)

\displaystyle L_{N-1}^{\nu+1}(\tfrac{a^{2}-b^{2}}{2b}N\,\bar{\zeta}(z))

\displaystyle=N^{-1/2}e^{\frac{a^{2}-b^{2}}{2b}N\frac{\bar{\zeta}(z)}{2}}\cdot O(1),

which completes the computation.
∎
