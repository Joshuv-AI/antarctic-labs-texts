A total Lagrangian, objective and intrinsically locking-free Petrov–Galerkin S ​ E ​ ( 3 ) {S\!E}(3) Cosserat rod finite element formulation
Jonas Harsch, Simon Sailer, Simon R. Eugster, [0.25cm] Institute for Nonlinear MechanicsUniversity of StuttgartPfaffenwaldring 9, 70569 Stuttgart, Germany

Abstract
Based on more than three decades of rod finite element theory, this publication combines the successful contributions found in literature and eradicates the arising drawbacks like loss of objectivity, locking, path-dependence and redundant coordinates. Specifically, the idea of interpolating the nodal orientations using relative rotation vectors, proposed by Crisfield and Jelenić in 1999, is extended to the interpolation of nodal Euclidean transformation matrices with the aid of relative twists; a strategy that arises from the S ​ E ​ ( 3 ) {S\!E}(3) -structure of the Cosserat rod kinematics. Applying a Petrov–Galerkin projection method, we propose a rod finite element formulation where the virtual displacements and rotations as well as the translational and angular velocities are interpolated instead of using the consistent variations and time-derivatives of the introduced interpolation formula. Properties such as the intrinsic absence of locking, preservation of objectivity after discretization and parametrization in terms of a minimal number of nodal unknowns are demonstrated by representative numerical examples in both statics and dynamics.



Keywords:
rod finite elements,
{S\!E}(3)
, objectivity, locking, Petrov–Galerkin, large deformations

Introduction

The theory of shear-deformable (spatial) rod formulations dates back to the pioneer works of Cosserat
[
1
]
, Timoshenko
[
2
]
, Reissner
[
3
]
and Simo
[
4
]
. Thus, depending on the chosen literature, shear-deformable rods are called (special) Cosserat rods
[
5
]
, Simo–Reissner beams, spatial Timoshenko beams, geometrically exact beams
[
6
]
, etc. Due to this ambiguity of naming conventions
[
7
]
, it is best to describe the used rod finite element formulation by the underlying kinematics only
[
8
,
9
]
. Nonetheless, there is a wide agreement in English literature preferring the name special Cosserat rod. Thus, we adopt this notation but for simplicity suppress the prefix “special” in the subsequent treatment. In addition, there is a vast amount of rod finite element formulations found in literature. Introducing all of them concerning their historical development and their tiny differences or improvements is out of the scope of this publication and the interested reader is referred to the exhaustive literature survey of both shear-deformable and shear-rigid rods given by Meier et al.
[
10
]
. However, we want to introduce the most important developments found in literature that can be seen as individual milestones leading to the present formulation.

Cardona and Geradin
[
11
]
introduced the first total Lagrangian shear-deformable spatial rod finite element formulation where the nodal rotations are parametrized in terms of total rotation vectors. To prevent the well-known singularity of this parametrization, they introduced a strategy of also using the complement rotation vector. By application of a Bubnov–Galerkin projection, the virtual work contributions are discretized in space. Focusing on details of the rotation vector parametrization, the identical rod formulation is found in Ibrahimbegović et al.
[
12
]
. In 1999, Crisfield and Jelenić
[
13
]
made a groundbreaking discovery in the theory of shear-deformable rod finite element formulations. All previously existing rod finite element formulations violate the objectivity requirement in the discrete approximation. Moreover, they presented a solution to the described problem by interpolating the nodal orientations using relative rotation vectors. The follow-up publication
[
14
]
introduces an objective rod finite element formulation using incremental rotation vectors for the description of the nodal rotations and thus working in an updated Lagrangian setting. To simplify the linearization of the internal virtual work functional, the authors applied a Petrov–Galerkin
[
15
]
projection by introducing the virtual rotation expressed in the inertial frame. This results in a non-symmetric and configuration-dependent mass matrix. A remedy for this is to use instead the virtual rotation vector expressed in the cross-section-fixed basis together with the corresponding angular velocity (again expressed in the cross-section-fixed basis). As will be discussed in the course of the subsequent treatment, this combination yields a symmetric and possibly constant mass matrix, typically met in rigid body dynamics
[
16
]
, however, at the cost of a non-symmetric stiffness matrix.

The previously mentioned rod finite element formulations rely on the uncoupled composition approximations of the centerline points as elements of
\mathbb{R}^{3}
and the cross-sectional orientations as elements of the special orthogonal group
S\!O(3)
denoted by
\mathbb{R}^{3}\times S\!O(3)
. In contrast, helicoidal approximations
[
17
]
and strain-based approaches
[
18
,
19
]
use a coupled interpolation of these two fields. Based on these developments, Sonneville et al.
[
20
]
extended the idea of interpolating the nodal orientations using relative rotation vectors
[
13
]
to the interpolation of nodal Euclidean transformation matrices using relative twists. That is, an interpolation strategy where positions and orientations are intrinsically coupled. Exclusively working on the Lie group
{S\!E}(3)
, the authors
[
20
]
applied a Bubnov–Galerkin projection, i.e., the virtual displacements are given by the variation of the nodal values.

Inspired by this interpolation, the present paper introduces a novel rod finite element formulation using only the coupled interpolation strategy of
{S\!E}(3)
while retaining all other favorable properties of the classical formulations. In this way, the present rod finite element combines all the important properties of the formulations in the above literature and tries to eliminate all their drawbacks. In particular, the main contributions of this paper are the following:

•

We present a novel total Lagrangian (thus path-independent), intrinsically locking-free and objective rod finite element formulation parametrized by the nodal total rotation vectors and centerline points. Using the complement rotation vector in combination with a relative interpolation strategy circumvents possible occurring singularities for element deformations in which the relative rotation angle remains below
\pi
. Therefore, a minimal number of nodal unknowns is obtained in contrast to formulations relying on redundant coordinates
[
6
,
21
,
9
]
.

•

Application of a Petrov–Galerkin projection results in a very simple inertial virtual work functional that ultimately leads to a symmetric mass matrix that is constant for most applications. In contrast to Sonneville et al.
[
20
]
, this approach neither requires the involved expressions of the
{S\!E}(3)
-tangent operator and its inverse nor their derivatives.

•

Depending on the specific application, a system of ordinary differential equations or the nonlinear generalized force equilibrium is obtained. These can respectively be solved using a standard ODE solver (e.g. Runge–Kutta family, generalized-
\alpha
) or root finding algorithms (e.g. Newton–Raphson, Riks) instead of requiring highly specialized Lie group versions of them
[
20
]
. Hence, the formulation can easily be integrated into existing flexible multibody dynamic software packages.

•

Besides the presentation of the linearized internal forces, static and dynamic benchmark examples demonstrate the power of the new formulation. Using a two-node element – that can be integrated by a two-point Gauss–Legendre quadrature rule – second-order spatial convergence is achieved for both centerline and orientation.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
, the Cosserat rod theory is briefly recapitulated. The section closes with the formulation of the internal, external and inertial virtual work functionals of the shear-deformable spatial rod. Section
3
introduces a novel Petrov–Galerkin rod finite element formulation based on a very efficient two-node
{S\!E}(3)
-interpolation strategy that preserves objectivity of the discretized strain measures. Introducing some bookkeeping enables the precise formulation of the discrete virtual work functionals that result in the tuples of internal, external and gyroscopic forces as well as the symmetric and possibly constant mass matrix. Further, the linearization of internal forces is given. To demonstrate the performance of the presented finite element formulation carefully selected benchmark examples are studied in Section
4
, meaning that each of this minimal set of examples demonstrates at least one affirmed property of the proposed formulation. To close the paper, Section
5
presents concluding remarks.

While most parts of the paper can be read with a rudimentary knowledge of matrix Lie groups, the interested reader is referred to Appendix
A
for a deeper understanding of some manipulations. Therein, a concise overview of matrix Lie groups is given with all equations and references relevant to this work. In particular, the special orthogonal group
S\!O(3)

[

For this purpose, another Petrov-Galerkin formulation is presented, where the virtual nodal rotations and the nodal angular velocities are expressed in the inertial frame.

2.1
Centerline and cross-section orientation

We introduce the Euclidean 3-space
\mathbb{E}^{3}
as an abstract 3-dimensional real inner-product space
[
5
]
. A basis for
\mathbb{E}^{3}
is a linearly independent set of three vectors
\mathbf{e}_{x},\mathbf{e}_{y},\mathbf{e}_{z}\in\mathbb{E}^{3}
. The basis is said to be right-handed if
\mathbf{e}_{x}\cdot(\mathbf{e}_{y}\times\mathbf{e}_{z})>0
and orthonormal if their base vectors are mutually orthogonal and have unit length. In this paper, only right-handed orthonormal bases are considered. For a given basis
K=\{\mathbf{e}_{x}^{K},\ \mathbf{e}_{y}^{K},\ \mathbf{e}_{z}^{K}\}
, the respective components
a_{i}^{K}
,
i\in\{x,y,z\}
of a vector
\mathbf{a}=a_{x}^{K}\mathbf{e}_{x}^{K}+a_{y}^{K}\mathbf{e}_{y}^{K}+a_{z}^{K}\mathbf{e}_{z}^{K}\in\mathbb{E}^{3}
can be collected in the triple
{}_{K}\mathbf{a}=(a_{x}^{K},\ a_{y}^{K},\ a_{z}^{K})\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
. Thus, we carefully distinguish
\mathbb{R}^{3}
from the Euclidean 3-space
\mathbb{E}^{3}
. The rotation between two bases
K_{0}
and
K_{1}
is captured by the proper orthogonal transformation matrix
\mathbf{A}_{K_{0}K_{1}}\in S\!O(3)
, which relates the coordinate representations
{}_{K_{0}}\mathbf{a}
and
{}_{K_{1}}\mathbf{a}
in accordance with
{}_{K_{0}}\mathbf{a}=\mathbf{A}_{K_{0}K_{1}}\,{}_{K_{1}}\mathbf{a}
. Let
\xi\in\mathcal{J}=[0,1]\subset\mathbb{R}
denote the centerline parameter and
(\eta,\zeta)\in\mathcal{A}(\xi)\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
the cross-section parameters of the cross-section area at
\xi
. Considering the rod as a three-dimensional continuum, a point
Q
of the rod can be addressed by

{}_{I}\mathbf{r}_{OQ}(\bm{\xi},t)={}_{I}\mathbf{r}_{OP}(\xi,t)+\mathbf{A}_{IK}(\xi,t)\,{}_{K}\mathbf{r}_{PQ}(\eta,\zeta)\,,\quad\bm{\xi}=(\xi,\ \eta,\ \zeta)\in\mathcal{B}=\mathcal{J}\times\mathcal{A}(\mathcal{J})\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}\,.

(1)

Herein,
{}_{I}\mathbf{r}_{OP}
are the Cartesian components with respect to the inertial basis
I
of the time-dependent centerline curve
\mathbf{r}_{OP}
, where the subscripts
O
and
P
refer to the origin
O
and the centerline points, see Figure
1
. To not overload the notation in (
1
), we abstained from explicitly denoting the dependence of the points
P
and
Q
on
\xi
and
\bm{\xi}
, respectively. Moreover,
{}_{K}\mathbf{r}_{PQ}
denote the cross-section coordinates, which are transformed to the
I
-basis using the transformation matrices
\mathbf{A}_{IK}
. Note that the base vectors of the cross-section-fixed
K
-bases are functions of the centerline parameter
\xi
and time
t
, i.e.,
\mathbf{e}_{i}^{K}=\mathbf{e}_{i}^{K}(\xi,t),\,i\in\{x,y,z\}
.

2.2
Variations, velocities and curvature

Let
\dot{(\bullet)}
and
(\bullet)_{,\xi}
respectively denote the derivative with respect to time
t
and centerline parameter
\xi
. The variation is denoted by
\delta(\bullet)
. The virtual displacement
{}_{I}\delta\mathbf{r}_{P}
and the centerline velocity
{}_{I}\mathbf{v}_{P}
are given by the variation as well as the time derivative of the centerline

{}_{I}\delta\mathbf{r}_{P}=\delta\left({}_{I}\mathbf{r}_{OP}\right)\,,\quad{}_{I}\mathbf{v}_{P}={}_{I}\dot{\mathbf{r}}_{OP}\,.

(2)

The angular velocity of the
K
-basis relative to the inertial
I
-basis, in components w.r.t. the
K
-basis, is defined by

{}_{K}\bm{\omega}_{IK}\coloneqq j^{-1}_{S\!O(3)}\left({}_{K}\widetilde{\bm{\omega}}_{IK}\right)\,,\quad\text{with}\quad{}_{K}\widetilde{\bm{\omega}}_{IK}\coloneqq\mathbf{A}_{IK}^{\!\mathrm{T}}\dot{\mathbf{A}}_{IK}\,,

(3)

where
j_{S\!O(3)}\colon\mathbb{R}^{3}\to\mathfrak{so}(3)=\{\mathbf{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}|\mathbf{B}^{\!\mathrm{T}}=-\mathbf{B}\}
is the linear and bijective map such that
\widetilde{\bm{\omega}}\mathbf{r}=j_{S\!O(3)}(\bm{\omega})\mathbf{r}=\bm{\omega}\times\mathbf{r}
for all
\bm{\omega},\mathbf{r}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
, see (
73
) from Appendix
A
. Analogously, we define the scaled curvature as

{}_{K}\bar{\bm{\kappa}}_{IK}\coloneqq j^{-1}_{S\!O(3)}\left({}_{K}\widetilde{\bar{\bm{\kappa}}}_{IK}\right)\,,\quad\text{with}\quad{}_{K}\widetilde{\bar{\bm{\kappa}}}_{IK}\coloneqq\mathbf{A}_{IK}^{\!\mathrm{T}}\mathbf{A}_{IK,\xi}

(4)

and the virtual rotation as

{}_{K}\delta\bm{\phi}_{IK}\coloneqq j^{-1}_{S\!O(3)}\left({}_{K}\delta\widetilde{\bm{\phi}}_{IK}\right)\,,\quad\text{with}\quad{}_{K}\delta\widetilde{\bm{\phi}}_{IK}\coloneqq\mathbf{A}_{IK}^{\!\mathrm{T}}\delta\mathbf{A}_{IK}\,.

(5)

2.3
Reference arc length

For the reference centerline curve
{}_{I}\mathbf{r}_{OP}^{0}
, the length of the rod’s tangent vector is
J=\|{}_{I}\mathbf{r}_{OP,\xi}^{0}\|
. Thus, for a given centerline parameter
\xi
, the reference arc length
s
is defined by

s(\xi)\coloneqq\int_{0}^{\xi}J(\bar{\xi})\,\mathrm{d}\bar{\xi}\,.

(6)

Following Harsch and Eugster
[
26
]
, the derivative with respect to the reference arc length
s
of a function
\mathbf{f}\colon\mathcal{J}\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
can be computed by

\mathbf{f}_{,s}(\xi,t)\coloneqq\mathbf{f}_{,\xi}(\xi,t)\frac{1}{J(\xi)}\,.

(7)

2.4
Objective strain measures

The objective strain measures of a Cosserat rod, see Antman
[
5
, Section 8.2]
, are given by

{}_{K}\bm{\kappa}_{IK}={}_{K}\bar{\bm{\kappa}}_{IK}/J\quad\text{and}\quad{}_{K}\bm{\gamma}={}_{K}\bar{\bm{\gamma}}/J\,,\quad\text{with}\quad{}_{K}\bar{\bm{\gamma}}\coloneqq\mathbf{A}_{IK}^{\!\mathrm{T}}{}_{I}\mathbf{r}_{OP,\xi}\,,

(8)

which can be gathered in the six-dimensional tuple
\bm{\varepsilon}=({}_{K}\bm{\gamma},\ {}_{K}\bm{\kappa}_{IK})\in\mathbb{R}^{6}
. Therein, the dilatation and shear is captured by
{}_{K}\bm{\gamma}
, while
{}_{K}\bm{\kappa}_{IK}
measures torsion and bending, see Antman
[
5
, Section 8.6]
.

2.5
Internal virtual work

Without loss of generality, we restrict ourselves to hyperelastic material models where the strain energy density with respect to the reference arc length
W=W({}_{K}\bm{\gamma},{}_{K}\bm{\kappa}_{IK};\xi)
depends on the strain measures (
8
) and possibly explicitly on the centerline parameter
\xi
. By that, the internal virtual work functional is defined as

\displaystyle\delta W^{\mathrm{int}}\coloneqq

\displaystyle-\int_{\mathcal{J}}\delta WJ\mathrm{d}\xi=-\int_{\mathcal{J}}\left\{\delta({}_{K}\bar{\bm{\gamma}})^{\!\mathrm{T}}{}_{K}\mathbf{n}+\delta({}_{K}\bar{\bm{\kappa}}_{IK})^{\!\mathrm{T}}{}_{K}\mathbf{m}\right\}\mathrm{d}\xi\,,

(9)

where we have introduced the constitutive equations

{}_{K}\mathbf{n}\coloneqq\left(\frac{\partial W}{\partial{}_{K}\bm{\gamma}}\right)^{\!\mathrm{T}}\,,\quad{}_{K}\mathbf{m}\coloneqq\left(\frac{\partial W}{\partial{}_{K}\bm{\kappa}_{IK}}\right)^{\!\mathrm{T}}\,.

(10)

Note that even in the inelastic case, where no strain energy density
W
is available, the internal virtual work (
9
) can be used, with internal forces and moments
{}_{K}\mathbf{n}
and
{}_{K}\mathbf{m}
given by different constitutive laws
[
8
]
. Using
\delta({}_{K}\bm{\gamma})
and
\delta({}_{K}\bm{\kappa}_{IK})
derived in Appendix
B
, the internal virtual work (
9
) takes the form

\delta W^{\mathrm{int}}=-\int_{\mathcal{J}}\Big\{({}_{I}\delta\mathbf{r}_{P})_{,\xi}^{\!\mathrm{T}}\,\mathbf{A}_{IK}\,{}_{K}\mathbf{n}+({}_{K}\delta\bm{\phi}_{IK})_{,\xi}^{\!\mathrm{T}}\,{}_{K}\mathbf{m}-{}_{K}\delta\bm{\phi}_{IK}^{\!\mathrm{T}}\left[{}_{K}\bar{\bm{\gamma}}\times{}_{K}\mathbf{n}+{}_{K}\bar{\bm{\kappa}}_{IK}\times{}_{K}\mathbf{m}\right]\Big\}\mathrm{d}\xi\,.

(11)

As in Equation (2.10) of Simo and Vu-Quoc
[
4
]
, we introduce the diagonal elasticity matrices
\mathbf{C}_{\bm{\gamma}}=\operatorname{diag}(k_{\mathrm{e}},k_{\mathrm{s}},k_{\mathrm{s}})
and
\mathbf{C}_{\bm{\kappa}}=\operatorname{diag}(k_{\mathrm{t}},k_{\mathrm{b}_{y}},k_{\mathrm{b}_{z}})
with constant coefficients. In the following, the simple quadratic strain energy density

W({}_{K}\bm{\gamma},{}_{K}\bm{\kappa}_{IK};\xi)=\frac{1}{2}\left({}_{K}\bm{\gamma}-{}_{K}\bm{\gamma}^{0}\right)^{\!\mathrm{T}}\mathbf{C}_{\bm{\gamma}}\left({}_{K}\bm{\gamma}-{}_{K}\bm{\gamma}^{0}\right)+\frac{1}{2}\left({}_{K}\bm{\kappa}_{IK}-{}_{K}\bm{\kappa}_{IK}^{0}\right)^{\!\mathrm{T}}\mathbf{C}_{\bm{\kappa}}\left({}_{K}\bm{\kappa}_{IK}-{}_{K}\bm{\kappa}_{IK}^{0}\right)

(12)

is used, where the superscript
0
refers to the evaluation in the rod’s reference configuration.

2.6
External virtual work

Assume the line distributed external forces
{}_{I}\mathbf{b}\colon\mathcal{J}\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
and moments
{}_{K}\mathbf{c}\colon\mathcal{J}\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
to be given as densities with respect to the reference arc length. Moreover, for
i\in\{0,1\}
, point forces
{}_{I}\mathbf{b}_{i}\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
and point moments
{}_{K}\mathbf{c}_{i}\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
can be applied to the rod’s boundaries at
\xi_{0}=0
and
\xi_{1}=1
. By that, the corresponding external virtual work functional is given by

\delta W^{\mathrm{ext}}=\int_{\mathcal{J}}\left\{{}_{I}\delta\mathbf{r}_{P}^{\!\mathrm{T}}{}_{I}\mathbf{b}+{}_{K}\delta\bm{\phi}_{IK}^{\!\mathrm{T}}{}_{K}\mathbf{c}\right\}J\mathrm{d}\xi+\sum_{i=0}^{1}\left[{}_{I}\delta\mathbf{r}_{P}^{\!\mathrm{T}}{}_{I}\mathbf{b}_{i}+{}_{K}\delta\bm{\phi}_{IK}^{\!\mathrm{T}}{}_{K}\mathbf{c}_{i}\right]_{\xi_{i}}\,.

(13)

2.7
Inertial virtual work

Let
\rho_{0}\colon\mathcal{J}\to\mathbb{R}
denote the rod’s mass density per unit reference volume and
\mathrm{d}A
the cross-section surface element. It is convenient to define the following abbreviations

A_{\rho_{0}}(\xi)\coloneqq\int_{\mathcal{A}(\xi)}\rho_{0}\,\mathrm{d}A\,,\quad{}_{K}\mathbf{S}_{\rho_{0}}(\xi)\coloneqq\int_{\mathcal{A}(\xi)}\rho_{0}{}_{K}\widetilde{\mathbf{r}}_{PQ}\,\mathrm{d}A\,,\quad{}_{K}\mathbf{I}_{\rho_{0}}(\xi)\coloneqq\int_{\mathcal{A}(\xi)}\rho_{0}{}_{K}\widetilde{\mathbf{r}}_{PQ}\,{}_{K}\widetilde{\mathbf{r}}_{PQ}^{\,\mathrm{T}}\mathrm{d}A\,.

(14)

Further, using the mass differential
\mathrm{d}m=\rho_{0}J\mathrm{d}A\mathrm{d}\xi
and expressing the variation and second time derivative of
\mathbf{r}_{OQ}
in terms of the rod’s kinematics (
1
), the inertial virtual work functional of the Cosserat rod can be written as (cf. Equation (9.54) of Eugster and Harsch
[
8
]
for a coordinate-free version)

\displaystyle\delta W^{\mathrm{dyn}}

\displaystyle=-\int_{\mathcal{B}}{}_{I}\delta\mathbf{r}_{OQ}^{\!\mathrm{T}}{}_{I}\ddot{\mathbf{r}}_{OQ}\,\mathrm{d}m=-\int_{\mathcal{J}}\begin{pmatrix}{}_{I}\delta\mathbf{r}_{P}\\
{}_{K}\delta\bm{\phi}_{IK}\end{pmatrix}^{\!\mathrm{T}}\left\{\overline{\mathbf{M}}\begin{pmatrix}{}_{I}\mathbf{v}_{P}\\
{}_{K}\bm{\omega}_{IK}\end{pmatrix}^{{\cdot}}+\mathbf{g}\right\}J\mathrm{d}\xi\,,

(15)

where we have introduced the two quantities

\overline{\mathbf{M}}=\begin{pmatrix}A_{\rho_{0}}\mathbf{1}_{3\times 3}&\mathbf{A}_{IK}{}_{K}\mathbf{S}_{\rho_{0}}^{\!\mathrm{T}}\\
{}_{K}\mathbf{S}_{\rho_{0}}\mathbf{A}_{IK}^{\!\mathrm{T}}&{}_{K}\mathbf{I}_{\rho_{0}}\end{pmatrix}\,,\quad\mathbf{g}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{A}_{IK}{}_{K}\widetilde{\bm{\omega}}_{IK}{}_{K}\mathbf{S}_{\rho_{0}}^{\!\mathrm{T}}{}_{K}\bm{\omega}_{IK}\\
{}_{K}\widetilde{\bm{\omega}}_{IK}{}_{K}\mathbf{I}_{\rho_{0}}{}_{K}\bm{\omega}_{IK}\end{pmatrix}\,.

(16)

Note that the quantity
\mathbf{S}_{\rho_{0}}
vanishes if the centerline points
{}_{I}\mathbf{r}_{OP}(\xi,t)
coincide with the cross-sections’ center of mass. For this case
\overline{\mathbf{M}}
and
\mathbf{g}
get independent of the rod’s configuration.

3
Finite element formulation

3.1
Rod kinematics in homogenous coordinates

The frame
\mathcal{I}=\{O,\mathbf{e}_{x}^{I},\mathbf{e}_{y}^{I},\mathbf{e}_{z}^{I}\}
is the set collecting the origin
O
together with the inertial base vectors
\mathbf{e}_{i}^{I}
,
i\in\{x,y,z\}
. A generic and possibly non-inertial frame
\mathcal{K}=\{P,\mathbf{e}_{x}^{K},\mathbf{e}_{y}^{K},\mathbf{e}_{z}^{K}\}
is given by a point
P
together with a right-handed orthonormal basis
K
. The point
Q
relative to the
\mathcal{K}
-frame is addressed by the triple
{}_{K}\mathbf{r}_{PQ}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
, which are the components of the vector
\mathbf{r}_{PQ}\in\mathbb{E}^{3}
between
P
and
Q
with respect to the
K
-basis. Rigid body motions between two frames
\mathcal{K}_{0}
and
\mathcal{K}_{1}
are captured by the Euclidean transformation matrix

\mathbf{H}_{\mathcal{K}_{0}\mathcal{K}_{1}}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{A}_{K_{0}K_{1}}&{}_{K_{0}}\mathbf{r}_{P_{0}P_{1}}\\
\mathbf{0}_{1\times 3}&1\end{pmatrix}\,,

(17)

which relates the triples
{}_{K_{1}}\mathbf{r}_{P_{1}Q}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
and
{}_{K_{0}}\mathbf{r}_{P_{0}Q}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
in accordance with

\begin{pmatrix}{}_{K_{0}}\mathbf{r}_{P_{0}Q}\\
1\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}{}_{K_{0}}\mathbf{r}_{P_{0}P_{1}}+\mathbf{A}_{K_{0}K_{1}}\,{}_{K_{1}}\mathbf{r}_{P_{1}Q}\\
1\end{pmatrix}=\mathbf{H}_{\mathcal{K}_{0}\mathcal{K}_{1}}\begin{pmatrix}{}_{K_{1}}\mathbf{r}_{P_{1}Q}\\
1\end{pmatrix}\,.

(18)

In fact, the Euclidean transformation matrix
\mathbf{H}_{\mathcal{K}_{0}\mathcal{K}_{1}}
is an element of the special Euclidean group
{S\!E}(3)
considered here as a Lie subgroup of the general linear group
G\!L(4)
. A brief introduction to the Lie group setting and particularly an overview of the herein required mappings is given in Appendix
A
. Direct computation readily verifies that the inverse of
\mathbf{H}_{\mathcal{K}_{0}\mathcal{K}_{1}}
is

\mathbf{H}_{\mathcal{K}_{0}\mathcal{K}_{1}}^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{A}_{K_{0}K_{1}}^{\!\mathrm{T}}&-\mathbf{A}_{K_{0}K_{1}}^{\!\mathrm{T}}{}_{K_{0}}\mathbf{r}_{P_{0}P_{1}}\\
\mathbf{0}_{1\times 3}&1\end{pmatrix}\,.

(19)

Consequently, using (
18
), the motion of the rod (
1
) can be written in homogenous coordinates as

\begin{pmatrix}{}_{I}\mathbf{r}_{OQ}\\
1\end{pmatrix}=\mathbf{H}_{\mathcal{I}\mathcal{K}}\begin{pmatrix}{}_{K}\mathbf{r}_{PQ}\\
1\end{pmatrix}\,,\quad\text{with}\quad\mathbf{H}_{\mathcal{I}\mathcal{K}}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{A}_{IK}&{}_{I}\mathbf{r}_{OP}\\
\mathbf{0}_{1\times 3}&1\end{pmatrix}\,.

(20)

The group structure of
{S\!E}(3)
then allows to decompose the Euclidean transformation matrix
\mathbf{H}_{\mathcal{I}\mathcal{K}}
into

\mathbf{H}_{\mathcal{I}\mathcal{K}}=\mathbf{H}_{\mathcal{I}\mathcal{K}_{0}}\mathbf{H}_{\mathcal{K}_{0}\mathcal{K}}\,.

(21)

3.2
{S\!E}(3)
-interpolation

In order to introduce the idea of the
{S\!E}(3)
-interpolation strategy, we discretize the rod by a single two-node finite element. In contrast to Sonneville et al.
[
20
]
, we choose a parametrization of
\mathbf{H}_{\mathcal{I}\mathcal{K}_{0}}
and
\mathbf{H}_{\mathcal{I}\mathcal{K}_{1}}
in terms of time-dependent generalized coordinates
\mathbf{q}(t)=\big({}_{I}\mathbf{r}_{OP_{0}}(t),\bm{\psi}_{0}(t),{}_{I}\mathbf{r}_{OP_{1}}(t),\bm{\psi}_{1}(t)\big)\in\mathbb{R}^{12}
given by the nodal positions
{}_{I}\mathbf{r}_{OP_{0}}(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
,
{}_{I}\mathbf{r}_{OP_{1}}(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
and the nodal rotation vectors
\bm{\psi}_{0}(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
,
\bm{\psi}_{1}(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
. Using the exponential map of
S\!O(3)
, defined in (
77
), the parametrization is

\mathbf{H}_{\mathcal{I}\mathcal{K}_{0}}(\mathbf{q})=\begin{pmatrix}\operatorname{Exp}_{S\!O(3)}(\bm{\psi}_{0})&{}_{I}\mathbf{r}_{OP_{0}}\\
\mathbf{0}_{3\times 1}&1\end{pmatrix}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathbf{H}_{\mathcal{I}\mathcal{K}_{1}}(\mathbf{q})=\begin{pmatrix}\operatorname{Exp}_{S\!O(3)}(\bm{\psi}_{1})&{}_{I}\mathbf{r}_{OP_{1}}\\
\mathbf{0}_{3\times 1}&1\end{pmatrix}\,.

(22)

With the
{S\!E}(3)
-logarithm map from (

corresponding to the relative Euclidean transformation
\mathbf{H}_{\mathcal{K}_{0}\mathcal{K}_{1}}=\mathbf{H}_{\mathcal{I}\mathcal{K}_{0}}^{-1}\,\mathbf{H}_{\mathcal{I}\mathcal{K}_{1}}
as

\bm{\theta}_{\mathcal{K}_{0}\mathcal{K}_{1}}=\operatorname{Log}_{{S\!E}(3)}\big(\mathbf{H}_{\mathcal{K}_{0}\mathcal{K}_{1}}\big)=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{T}_{S\!O(3)}^{-\mathrm{T}}(\bm{\psi}_{01})\,{}_{K_{0}}\mathbf{r}_{P_{0}P_{1}}\\
\bm{\psi}_{01}\end{pmatrix}\,.

(23)

Herein
\bm{\psi}_{01}
corresponds to the relative rotation vector parameterizing the transformation between the
K_{0}
- and
K_{1}
-basis according to
\mathbf{A}_{K_{0}K_{1}}=\operatorname{Exp}_{S\!O(3)}(\bm{\psi}_{01})
. Due to the singularity of
\operatorname{Log}_{S\!O(3)}

\|\bm{\psi}_{01}\|=\pi

\omega=\|\bm{\psi}_{01}\|<\pi
. A discretization with a higher number of elements always cures this problem. By linearly scaling the relative twist
\bm{\theta}_{\mathcal{K}_{0}\mathcal{K}_{1}}
and using the
{S\!E}(3)
-exponential map (

\mathcal{K}_{0}

\mathcal{K}

\mathbf{H}_{\mathcal{K}_{0}\mathcal{K}}(\xi,\mathbf{q})=\operatorname{Exp}_{{S\!E}(3)}\big(\xi\,\bm{\theta}_{\mathcal{K}_{0}\mathcal{K}_{1}}(\mathbf{q})\big)\,.

(24)

The Euclidean transformation
\mathbf{H}_{\mathcal{K}_{0}\mathcal{K}}
satisfies
\mathbf{H}_{\mathcal{K}_{0}\mathcal{K}}(0,\mathbf{q})=\mathbf{1}_{4\times 4}
and
\mathbf{H}_{\mathcal{K}_{0}\mathcal{K}}(1,\mathbf{q})=\mathbf{H}_{\mathcal{K}_{0}\mathcal{K}_{1}}
. To obtain the Euclidean transformation for a point within the finite element, the expressions (

{S\!E}(3)
-interpolation

\mathbf{H}_{\mathcal{I}\mathcal{K}}(\xi,\mathbf{q})\coloneqq\mathbf{H}_{\mathcal{I}\mathcal{K}_{0}}(\mathbf{q})\mathbf{H}_{\mathcal{K}_{0}\mathcal{K}}(\xi,\mathbf{q})=\mathbf{H}_{\mathcal{I}\mathcal{K}_{0}}(\mathbf{q})\operatorname{Exp}_{{S\!E}(3)}\left(\xi\,\bm{\theta}_{\mathcal{K}_{0}\mathcal{K}_{1}}(\mathbf{q})\right)\,,

(25)

originally proposed in Equation (55) of Sonneville et al.
[

]
. In order to see the interpolation for the position and orientation, we explicitly compute the exponential map in (

\displaystyle\mathbf{H}_{\mathcal{I}\mathcal{K}}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{A}_{IK_{0}}&{}_{I}\mathbf{r}_{OP_{0}}\\
\mathbf{0}_{1\times 3}&1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\operatorname{Exp}_{S\!O(3)}(\xi\bm{\psi}_{01})&\quad\mathbf{T}_{S\!O(3)}^{\!\mathrm{T}}(\xi\bm{\psi}_{01})\,\xi\,\mathbf{T}_{S\!O(3)}^{-\mathrm{T}}(\bm{\psi}_{01}){}_{K_{0}}\mathbf{r}_{P_{0}P_{1}}\\
\mathbf{0}_{1\times 3}&1\end{pmatrix}

(26)

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{A}_{IK}&{}_{I}\mathbf{r}_{OP}\\
\mathbf{0}_{1\times 3}&1\end{pmatrix}

\mathbf{0}_{1\times 3}&1\end{pmatrix}\,.

Consequently, the rod’s orientation is discretized by

\mathbf{A}_{IK}(\xi,\mathbf{q})=\mathbf{A}_{IK_{0}}(\mathbf{q})\operatorname{Exp}_{S\!O(3)}\big(\xi\bm{\psi}_{01}(\mathbf{q})\big)\,,

which corresponds to Equation (4.7) of Crisfield and Jelenić

3.3
Objectivity, discretized strain measures and absence of locking of
{S\!E}(3)
-interpolation

Using the just introduced Euclidean transformation matrices, we can recognize the strain measures of the Cosserat rod theory (
8
) in the following equation

\mathbf{H}_{\mathcal{I}\mathcal{K}}^{-1}\mathbf{H}_{\mathcal{I}\mathcal{K},s}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{A}_{IK}^{\!\mathrm{T}}&-\mathbf{A}_{IK}^{\!\mathrm{T}}{}_{I}\mathbf{r}_{OP}\\
\mathbf{0}_{1\times 3}&1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{A}_{IK,s}&{}_{I}\mathbf{r}_{OP,s}\\
\mathbf{0}_{1\times 3}&0\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{A}_{IK}^{\!\mathrm{T}}\mathbf{A}_{IK,s}&\mathbf{A}_{IK}^{\!\mathrm{T}}{}_{I}\mathbf{r}_{OP,s}\\
\mathbf{0}_{1\times 3}&0\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}{}_{K}\widetilde{\bm{\kappa}}_{IK}&{}_{K}\bm{\gamma}\\
\mathbf{0}_{1\times 3}&0\end{pmatrix}\,.

(29)

With the proposed interpolation (
25

\mathbf{H}_{\mathcal{I}\mathcal{K}}^{-1}\mathbf{H}_{\mathcal{I}\mathcal{K},s}=\big(\mathbf{H}_{\mathcal{I}\mathcal{K}_{0}}\mathbf{H}_{\mathcal{K}_{0}\mathcal{K}}\big)^{-1}\big(\mathbf{H}_{\mathcal{I}\mathcal{K}_{0}}\mathbf{H}_{\mathcal{K}_{0}\mathcal{K}}\big)_{,s}=\mathbf{H}_{\mathcal{K}_{0}\mathcal{K}}^{-1}\mathbf{H}_{\mathcal{K}_{0}\mathcal{K},s}

(30)

depend only on the relative Euclidean transformation
\mathbf{H}_{\mathcal{K}_{0}\mathcal{K}}
and its reference arc length derivative. Objectivity is the requirement that a quantity remains unaltered under a change of observer, i.e., a change of the
\mathcal{I}
-frame. Whether we use an
\mathcal{I}
-frame or an
\mathcal{I}^{+}
-frame, which are related by the time-dependent Euclidean transformation
\mathbf{H}_{\mathcal{I}^{+}\mathcal{I}}
, the discretized strain measures in
\mathbf{H}_{\mathcal{I}^{+}\mathcal{K}}^{-1}\mathbf{H}_{\mathcal{I}^{+}\mathcal{K},s}
correspond to the strain measures obtained by (
30

25
).

Using a little bit of Lie algebra introduced in the Appendix

3.4
Kinematics of element nodes

The concepts of the previous paragraphs can be synthesized in a rod finite element with a piecewise two-node
{S\!E}(3)
-interpolation that results in constant strains within an element. Let the rod be discretized by
n_{\mathrm{el}}
two-node elements. The nodal Euclidean transformation matrices
\mathbf{H}_{\mathcal{I}\mathcal{K}_{i}}
,
i=0,\dots,n_{\mathrm{el}}
are parameterized in terms of the time-dependent nodal generalized coordinates
\mathbf{q}_{i}(t)=\big({}_{I}\mathbf{r}_{OP_{i}}(t),\bm{\psi}_{i}(t)\big)\in\mathbb{R}^{6}
given by the nodal centerline points
{}_{I}\mathbf{r}_{OP_{i}}(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
and the nodal total rotation vectors
\bm{\psi}_{i}(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
. Using the exponential map of
S\!O(3)
, see comment before (
22
), the nodal parametrization is

\mathbf{H}_{\mathcal{I}\mathcal{K}_{i}}(\mathbf{q}_{i})=\begin{pmatrix}\operatorname{Exp}_{S\!O(3)}(\bm{\psi}_{i})&{}_{I}\mathbf{r}_{OP_{i}}\\
\mathbf{0}_{3\times 1}&1\end{pmatrix}\,.

(34)

The rod’s parameter space
\mathcal{J}
is divided into
n_{\mathrm{el}}
linearly spaced element intervals
\mathcal{J}^{e}=[\xi^{e},\xi^{e+1})
via
\mathcal{J}=\bigcup_{e=0}^{n_{\mathrm{el}}-1}\mathcal{J}^{e}
. This means a totality of
n_{\mathrm{el}}+1
nodes and
n_{\mathbf{q}}=6(n_{\mathrm{el}}+1)
unknowns. The nodal quantities can be assembled in the global tuple of generalized coordinates
\mathbf{q}(t)=\big(\mathbf{q}_{0}(t),\mathbf{q}_{1}(t),\dots,\mathbf{q}_{N-1}(t)\big)\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{\mathbf{q}}}
. Introducing an appropriate Boolean connectivity matrix
\mathbf{C}_{e}\in\mathbb{R}^{12\times n_{\mathbf{q}}}
, the element generalized coordinates
\mathbf{q}^{e}(t)=\big({}_{I}\mathbf{r}_{OP_{e}}(t),\bm{\psi}_{e}(t),{}_{I}\mathbf{r}_{OP_{e+1}}(t),\bm{\psi}_{e+1}(t)\big)\in\mathbb{R}^{12}
can be extracted from the global generalized coordinates
\mathbf{q}
via
\mathbf{q}^{e}=\mathbf{C}_{e}\mathbf{q}
. By introducing an additional set of Boolean connectivity matrices
\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{r},0},\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{r},1},\mathbf{C}_{\bm{\psi},0},\mathbf{C}_{\bm{\psi},1}\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times 12}
, the centerline points
{}_{I}\mathbf{r}_{OP_{e}}
,
{}_{I}\mathbf{r}_{OP_{e+1}}
and total rotation vectors
\bm{\psi}_{e}
,
\bm{\psi}_{e+1}
can be extracted from the element generalized coordinates
\mathbf{q}^{e}
via
{}_{I}\mathbf{r}_{OP_{e}}=\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{r},0}\mathbf{q}^{e}
,
{}_{I}\mathbf{r}_{OP_{e+1}}=\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{r},1}\mathbf{q}^{e}
,
\bm{\psi}_{e}=\mathbf{C}_{\bm{\psi},0}\mathbf{q}^{e}
and
\bm{\psi}_{e+1}=\mathbf{C}_{\bm{\psi},1}\mathbf{q}^{e}
. Note, the Boolean connectivity matrices are only used for the mathematical description of these extraction procedures. During a numerical implementation a different and much more efficient approach is used.

3.5
Element-wise
{S\!E}(3)
-interpolation

For a given element interval
\mathcal{J}^{e}=[\xi^{e},\xi^{e+1})
the corresponding linear Lagrange basis functions
N_{0}^{e}
,
N_{1}^{e}
and their first derivatives
N^{e}_{0,\xi}
,
N^{e}_{1,\xi}
are defined by

N^{e}_{0}(\xi)=\frac{\xi^{e+1}-\xi}{\xi^{e+1}-\xi^{e}}\,,\quad N^{e}_{1}(\xi)=\frac{\xi-\xi^{e}}{\xi^{e+1}-\xi^{e}}\,,\quad N^{e}_{0,\xi}(\xi)=\frac{-1}{\xi^{e+1}-\xi^{e}}\,,\quad N^{e}_{1,\xi}(\xi)=\frac{1}{\xi^{e+1}-\xi^{e}}\,.

(35)

Further, we introduce the characteristic function
\chi_{\mathcal{J}^{e}}\colon\mathcal{J}\to\{0,1\}
, being one for
\xi\in\mathcal{J}^{e}=[\xi^{e},\xi^{e+1})
and zero elsewhere. By that, we can extend the global two-node
{S\!E}(3)
-interpolation (
25
) to a piecewise two-node
{S\!E}(3)
-interpolation given by

\displaystyle\mathbf{H}_{\mathcal{I}\mathcal{K}}(\xi,\mathbf{q})

\displaystyle=\sum_{e=0}^{n_{\mathrm{el}}-1}\chi_{\mathcal{J}^{e}}(\xi)\mathbf{H}_{\mathcal{I}\mathcal{K}_{e}}(\mathbf{q})\operatorname{Exp}_{{S\!E}(3)}\left(N^{e}_{1}(\xi)\,\bm{\theta}_{\mathcal{K}_{e}\mathcal{K}_{e+1}}(\mathbf{q})\right)\,,

(36)

\displaystyle\bm{\theta}_{\mathcal{K}_{e}\mathcal{K}_{e+1}}(\mathbf{q})

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Log}_{{S\!E}(3)}\left[\mathbf{H}^{-1}_{\mathcal{I}\mathcal{K}_{e}}(\mathbf{q})\,\mathbf{H}_{\mathcal{I}\mathcal{K}_{e+1}}(\mathbf{q})\right]\,.

For symmetry reasons, cf. Crisfield and Jelenić
[
13
]
, it might be advantageous to use a modified
{S\!E}(3)
-interpolation given by

\displaystyle\mathbf{H}_{\mathcal{I}\mathcal{K}}(\xi,\mathbf{q})

\displaystyle=\sum_{e=0}^{n_{\mathrm{el}}-1}\chi_{\mathcal{J}^{e}}(\xi)\mathbf{H}_{\mathcal{I}\mathcal{R}_{e}}(\mathbf{q})\operatorname{Exp}_{{S\!E}(3)}\left(N^{e}_{0}(\xi)\,\bm{\theta}_{\mathcal{R}_{e}\mathcal{K}_{e}}(\mathbf{q})+N^{e}_{1}(\xi)\,\bm{\theta}_{\mathcal{R}_{e}\mathcal{K}_{e+1}}(\mathbf{q})\right)\,,

(37)

\displaystyle\mathbf{H}_{\mathcal{I}\mathcal{R}_{e}}(\mathbf{q})

\displaystyle=\mathbf{H}_{\mathcal{I}\mathcal{K}_{e}}(\mathbf{q})\operatorname{Exp}_{{S\!E}(3)}\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\operatorname{Log}_{{S\!E}(3)}\left[\mathbf{H}^{-1}_{\mathcal{I}\mathcal{K}_{e}}(\mathbf{q})\,\mathbf{H}_{\mathcal{I}\mathcal{K}_{e+1}}(\mathbf{q})\right]\right)\,,

\displaystyle\bm{\theta}_{\mathcal{R}_{e}\mathcal{K}_{e}}(\mathbf{q})

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Log}_{{S\!E}(3)}\left[\mathbf{H}^{-1}_{\mathcal{I}\mathcal{R}_{e}}(\mathbf{q})\,\mathbf{H}_{\mathcal{I}\mathcal{K}_{e}}(\mathbf{q})\right]\,,\quad\bm{\theta}_{\mathcal{R}_{e}\mathcal{K}_{e+1}}(\mathbf{q})=\operatorname{Log}_{{S\!E}(3)}\left[\mathbf{H}^{-1}_{\mathcal{I}\mathcal{R}_{e}}(\mathbf{q})\,\mathbf{H}_{\mathcal{I}\mathcal{K}_{e+1}}(\mathbf{q})\right]\,.

Since this strategy requires twice as many
{S\!E}(3)
-exponential evaluations and three times as many
{S\!E}(3)
-logarithm evaluations, the interpolation (
36
) is preferred due to superior efficiency and simplicity.

Using similar arguments that resulted in (
33
), the interpolation (
36
) leads to piecewise constant strains

\bm{\varepsilon}(\xi,\mathbf{q})=\sum_{e=0}^{n_{\mathrm{el}}-1}\chi_{\mathcal{J}^{e}}(\xi)\frac{\bm{\theta}_{\mathcal{K}_{e}\mathcal{K}_{e+1}}(\mathbf{q})}{\xi^{e+1}-\xi^{e}}\frac{1}{J}\,.

(38)

Using the same reasoning following (
33
) and since the piecewise two-node
{S\!E}(3)
-interpolation can exactly represent constant strains within each element, neither membrane nor shear locking will appear with this discretization. As we need no further numerical strategies to avoid locking as for instance re-interpolation of strain measures
[
28
,
29
]
or mixed formulations
[
30
,
31
,
32
]
, we call the finite element formulation intrinsically locking-free.

3.6
Petrov–Galerkin projection

Following a Petrov–Galerkin projection
[
15
]
, we introduce the nodal virtual displacements
\delta\mathbf{s}_{i}(t)=\big({}_{I}\delta\mathbf{r}_{P_{i}}(t),{}_{K_{i}}\delta\bm{\phi}_{IK_{i}}(t)\big)\in\mathbb{R}^{6}
given by the nodal centerline variation
{}_{I}\delta\mathbf{r}_{P_{i}}(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
and the nodal virtual rotation
{}_{K_{i}}\delta\bm{\phi}_{IK_{i}}(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
. They are related to the variations of the nodal generalized coordinates by
{}_{I}\delta\mathbf{r}_{P_{i}}=\delta({}_{I}\mathbf{r}_{OP_{i}})
and
{}_{K_{i}}\delta\bm{\phi}_{IK_{i}}=\mathbf{T}_{S\!O(3)}(\bm{\psi}_{i})\delta\bm{\psi}_{i}
, see Equation (49) of Cardona and Geradin
[
11
]
. Led by the observation that
\overline{\mathbf{M}}
in (
16
) is symmetric, it is advisable to introduce the nodal minimal velocities
\mathbf{u}_{i}(t)=\big({}_{I}\mathbf{v}_{P_{i}}(t),{}_{K_{i}}\bm{\omega}_{IK_{i}}(t)\big)\in\mathbb{R}^{6}
given by the nodal centerline velocity
{}_{I}\mathbf{v}_{P_{i}}(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
and the nodal angular velocity
{}_{K_{i}}\bm{\omega}_{IK_{i}}(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
. Similar to the virtual displacements, the nodal minimal velocities are related to the time derivative of the nodal generalized coordinates by the nodal kinematic differential equation

\dot{\mathbf{q}}_{i}=\begin{pmatrix}{}_{I}\dot{\mathbf{r}}_{OP_{i}}\\
\dot{\bm{\psi}}_{i}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{1}_{3\times 3}&\mathbf{0}_{3\times 3}\\
\mathbf{0}_{3\times 3}&\mathbf{T}^{-1}_{S\!O(3)}(\bm{\psi}_{i})\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}{}_{I}\mathbf{v}_{P_{i}}\\
{}_{K_{i}}\bm{\omega}_{IK_{i}}\end{pmatrix}=\mathbf{B}_{i}(\mathbf{q}_{i})\mathbf{u}_{i}\,.

(39)

Consequently, during a subsequent Galerkin projection, the symmetry of
\overline{\mathbf{M}}
is preserved and results in a symmetric mass matrix, see (
44
).
Since the inverse tangent map
\mathbf{T}^{-1}_{S\!O(3)}
used in (
39
) exhibits singularities for
\|\bm{\psi}\|=k2\pi
with
k=0,1,2,\dots
, we
apply the following strategy to avoid them. For
k=0
, we use the first order approximation
\mathbf{T}^{-1}_{S\!O(3)}(\bm{\omega})=\mathbf{1}_{3\times 3}+\frac{1}{2}\widetilde{\bm{\omega}}
. For
k>0
, the concept of complement rotation vectors
[
11
,
12
]
is applied. Due to the Petrov–Galerkin projection, it is sufficient to introduce a nodal update that is performed after each successful time step (in statics after each successful Newton increment). This update, which corresponds to a change of coordinates for the orientation parametrization, is given by

\bm{\psi}=\begin{cases}\bm{\psi}\,,&\|\bm{\psi}\|\leq\pi\,,\\
\bm{\psi}^{C}=\big(1-2\pi/\|\bm{\psi}\|\big)\bm{\psi}\,,&\|\bm{\psi}\|>\pi\,,\end{cases}

(40)

with
\operatorname{Exp}_{S\!O(3)}(\bm{\psi})=\operatorname{Exp}_{S\!O(3)}(\bm{\psi}^{C})
, i.e., there is no difference whether the nodal transformation matrix
\mathbf{A}_{IK}
is described by the rotation vector
\bm{\psi}
or by its complement
\bm{\psi}^{C}=\big(1-2\pi/\|\bm{\psi}\|\big)\bm{\psi}
. Thus, for reasonable time steps (Newton increments) a both minimal and singularity free parametrization of
S\!O(3)
is obtained without changing the rod’s virtual work formulation since all relevant nodal quantities are interpolated relatively

\mathbf{u}^{e}(t)=\big({}_{I}\mathbf{v}_{P_{e}}(t),{}_{K_{e}}\!\bm{\omega}_{IK_{e}}(t),{}_{I}\mathbf{v}_{P_{e+1}}(t),{}_{K_{e+1}}\!\bm{\omega}_{IK_{e+1}}(t)\big)\in\mathbb{R}^{12}
from the global quantities in agreement with
\delta\mathbf{s}^{e}=\mathbf{C}_{e}\delta\mathbf{s}
and
\mathbf{u}^{e}=\mathbf{C}_{e}\mathbf{u}
. Further, the centerline variations
{}_{I}\delta\mathbf{r}_{P_{e}}
,
{}_{I}\delta\mathbf{r}_{P_{e+1}}
and centerline velocities
{}_{I}\mathbf{v}_{P_{e}}
,
{}_{I}\mathbf{v}_{P_{e+1}}
can be extracted from the tuple of virtual displacements
\delta\mathbf{s}^{e}
and minimal velocities
\mathbf{u}^{e}
using
{}_{I}\delta\mathbf{r}_{P_{e}}=\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{r},0}\delta\mathbf{s}^{e}
,
{}_{I}\delta\mathbf{r}_{P_{e+1}}=\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{r},1}\delta\mathbf{s}^{e}
and
{}_{I}\mathbf{v}_{P_{e}}=\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{r},0}\mathbf{u}^{e}
,
{}_{I}\mathbf{v}_{P_{e+1}}=\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{r},1}\mathbf{u}^{e}
, respectively. Identical extraction operations can be defined for the nodal virtual rotations and angular velocities via
\mathbf{C}_{\bm{\psi},0},\mathbf{C}_{\bm{\psi},1}
.

In the sense of a Petrov–Galerkin projection
[
15
]
, a different interpolation for the virtual displacements is chosen. We chose a simple interpolation strategy based on the previously introduced linear Lagrange basis function. As outlined in the previous paragraph, in order to obtain a symmetric and possibly constant mass matrix, the angular velocities are interpolated similarly. Formalizing that, we introduce the interpolations

\displaystyle{}_{I}\delta\mathbf{r}_{P}(\xi,\delta\mathbf{s})

\displaystyle=\sum_{e=0}^{n_{\mathrm{el}}-1}\chi_{\mathcal{J}^{e}}(\xi)\left(N^{e}_{0}(\xi){}_{I}\delta\mathbf{r}_{P_{e}}+N^{e}_{1}(\xi){}_{I}\delta\mathbf{r}_{P_{e+1}}\right)\,,

(41)

\displaystyle{}_{I}\mathbf{v}_{P}(\xi,\mathbf{u})

\displaystyle=\sum_{e=0}^{n_{\mathrm{el}}-1}\chi_{\mathcal{J}^{e}}(\xi)\left(N^{e}_{0}(\xi){}_{I}\mathbf{v}_{P_{e}}+N^{e}_{1}(\xi){}_{I}\mathbf{v}_{P_{e+1}}\right)\,,

\displaystyle{}_{K}\delta\bm{\phi}_{IK}(\xi,\delta\mathbf{s})

\displaystyle=\sum_{e=0}^{n_{\mathrm{el}}-1}\chi_{\mathcal{J}^{e}}(\xi)\left(N^{e}_{0}(\xi)\,{}_{K_{e}}\!\delta\bm{\phi}_{IK_{e}}+N^{e}_{1}(\xi)\,{}_{K_{e+1}}\!\delta\bm{\phi}_{IK_{e+1}}\right)\,,

\displaystyle{}_{K}\bm{\omega}_{IK}(\xi,\mathbf{u})

\displaystyle=\sum_{e=0}^{n_{\mathrm{el}}-1}\chi_{\mathcal{J}^{e}}(\xi)\left(N^{e}_{0}(\xi)\,{}_{K_{e}}\!\bm{\omega}_{IK_{e}}+N^{e}_{1}(\xi)\,{}_{K_{e+1}}\!\bm{\omega}_{IK_{e+1}}\right)\,.

Choices like
{}_{K}\delta\mathbf{r}_{P}
, i.e., centerline variations in the cross-section
K
-basis, introduce cumbersome changes of bases when for instance the generalized contact force directions should be computed, cf. Bosten et al.
[
34
]
. This is quite inconvenient especially when dealing with complex flexible multibody system connected by perfect bilateral constraints as treated in Géradin and Cardona
[
35
]
.

3.7
Discrete virtual work formulations

Now we have all ingredients for discretizing the previously introduced continuous virtual work formulations in space. Inserting (
41
) into the internal virtual work (

), their discretization is obtained

\displaystyle\delta W^{\mathrm{int}}(\delta\mathbf{s};\mathbf{q})

\displaystyle=\delta\mathbf{s}^{\!\mathrm{T}}\mathbf{f}^{\mathrm{int}}(\mathbf{q})\,,\quad\mathbf{f}^{\mathrm{int}}(\mathbf{q})=\sum_{e=0}^{n_{\mathrm{el}}-1}\mathbf{C}_{e}^{\!\mathrm{T}}\mathbf{f}^{\mathrm{int}}_{e}(\mathbf{C}_{e}\mathbf{q})\,,

(42)

\displaystyle\mathbf{f}^{\mathrm{int}}_{e}(\mathbf{q}^{e})

\displaystyle=-\int_{\mathcal{J}^{e}}\sum_{i=0}^{1}\Big\{N^{e}_{i,\xi}\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{r},i}^{\!\mathrm{T}}\mathbf{A}_{IK}\,{}_{K}\mathbf{n}+N^{e}_{i,\xi}\mathbf{C}_{\bm{\psi},i}^{\!\mathrm{T}}{}_{K}\mathbf{m}-N^{e}_{i}\mathbf{C}_{\bm{\psi},i}^{\!\mathrm{T}}\left({}_{K}\bar{\bm{\gamma}}\times{}_{K}\mathbf{n}+{}_{K}\bar{\bm{\kappa}}_{IK}\times{}_{K}\mathbf{m}\right)\Big\}\mathrm{d}\xi\,,

where we have introduced the internal forces
\mathbf{f}^{\mathrm{int}}

\mathbf{f}^{\mathrm{int}}_{e}

\displaystyle=\delta\mathbf{s}^{\!\mathrm{T}}\mathbf{f}^{\mathrm{ext}}(\mathbf{q})\,,

(43)

\displaystyle\mathbf{f}^{\mathrm{ext}}(\mathbf{q})

\displaystyle=\sum_{e=0}^{n_{\mathrm{el}}-1}\mathbf{C}_{e}^{\!\mathrm{T}}\mathbf{f}^{\mathrm{ext}}_{e}(\mathbf{C}_{e}\mathbf{q})+\mathbf{C}_{n_{\mathrm{el}}-1}^{\!\mathrm{T}}\left[\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{r},1}^{\!\mathrm{T}}{}_{I}\mathbf{b}_{1}+\mathbf{C}_{\bm{\phi},1}^{\!\mathrm{T}}{}_{K}\mathbf{c}_{1}\right]_{\xi=1}+\mathbf{C}_{0}^{\!\mathrm{T}}\left[\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{r},0}^{\!\mathrm{T}}{}_{I}\mathbf{b}_{0}+\mathbf{C}_{\bm{\phi},0}^{\!\mathrm{T}}{}_{K}\mathbf{c}_{0}\right]_{\xi=0}\,,

\displaystyle\mathbf{f}^{\mathrm{ext}}_{e}(\mathbf{q}^{e})

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{J}^{e}}\sum_{i=0}^{1}\Big\{N^{e}_{i}\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{r},i}^{\!\mathrm{T}}{}_{I}\mathbf{b}+N^{e}_{i}\mathbf{C}_{\bm{\psi},i}^{\!\mathrm{T}}{}_{K}\mathbf{c}\Big\}J\mathrm{d}\xi\,,

where we have introduced the external forces
\mathbf{f}^{\mathrm{ext}}
and their element contributions
\mathbf{f}^{\mathrm{ext}}_{e}
. Finally, the discretization of the inertial virtual work contributions (

) is given by

\displaystyle\delta W^{\mathrm{dyn}}(\delta\mathbf{s};\mathbf{q},\mathbf{u})

where

(45)

\displaystyle\mathbf{f}^{\mathrm{gyr}}_{e}(\mathbf{q}^{e},\mathbf{u}^{e})

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{J}^{e}}\sum_{i=0}^{1}N^{e}_{i}\Big\{\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{r},i}^{\!\mathrm{T}}\mathbf{A}_{IK}{}_{K}\widetilde{\bm{\omega}}_{IK}{}_{K}\mathbf{S}_{\rho_{0}}^{\!\mathrm{T}}{}_{K}\bm{\omega}_{IK}+\mathbf{C}_{\bm{\psi},i}^{\!\mathrm{T}}{}_{K}\widetilde{\bm{\omega}}_{IK}{}_{K}\mathbf{I}_{\rho_{0}}{}_{K}\bm{\omega}_{IK}\Big\}J\mathrm{d}\xi\,,

where we have introduced the symmetric mass matrix

and

and nodal angular velocities

(47)

and

), i.e., the position vector
{}_{I}\mathbf{r}_{OP}
and the transformation matrix
\mathbf{A}_{IK}
are given in Appendix
C
. As already noted, the quantity
\mathbf{S}_{\rho_{0}}
vanishes if the centerline points
{}_{I}\mathbf{r}_{OP}
coincide with the cross-sections’ center of mass. For this case the gyroscopic forces
\mathbf{f}^{\mathrm{gyr}}
and the term
\mathbf{M}\dot{\mathbf{u}}
get independent of the generalized coordinates
\mathbf{q}
which is why we do not consider their linearizations here. The linearization of the external forces is omitted as well since a possible
\mathbf{q}

Arising element integrals of the form
\int_{\mathcal{J}^{e}}f(\xi)\mathrm{d}\xi

3.8
Equations of motion and static equilibrium

The principle of virtual work states that the totality of virtual work contributions has to vanish for arbitrary virtual displacements at all time instants
t
, see Chapter 8 of dell’Isola and Steigmann
[
36
]
, i.e.,

\delta W^{\mathrm{tot}}=\delta W^{\mathrm{int}}+\delta W^{\mathrm{ext}}+\delta W^{\mathrm{dyn}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle!}}{{=}}0\quad\forall\delta\mathbf{s}(t),\forall t\,.

(48)

Thus, the equations of motion

\dot{\mathbf{u}}=\mathbf{M}^{-1}(\mathbf{q})\left(\mathbf{f}^{\mathrm{int}}(\mathbf{q})+\mathbf{f}^{\mathrm{ext}}(\mathbf{q})-\mathbf{f}^{\mathrm{gyr}}(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{u})\right)

(49)

have to be fulfilled for each instant of time
t
. Further, the nodal minimal velocities
\mathbf{u}_{i}
are related to the time derivatives of the nodal generalized coordinates
\dot{\mathbf{q}}_{i}
via the nodal kinematic differential equation (
39
), which can be assembled to a global kinematic differential equation of the form
\dot{\mathbf{q}}=\mathbf{B}(\mathbf{q})\mathbf{u}
. Depending on the specific application, either the system of ordinary differential equations

\displaystyle\dot{\mathbf{q}}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{B}(\mathbf{q})\mathbf{u}\,,

(50)

\displaystyle\dot{\mathbf{u}}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{q})^{-1}\left(\mathbf{f}^{\mathrm{int}}(\mathbf{q})+\mathbf{f}^{\mathrm{ext}}(\mathbf{q})-\mathbf{f}^{\mathrm{gyr}}(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{u})\right)\,,

or the nonlinear generalized force equilibrium

\mathbf{f}^{\mathrm{int}}(\mathbf{q})+\mathbf{f}^{\mathrm{ext}}(\mathbf{q})=\mathbf{0}

(51)

is obtained. The system of ordinary differential equations can be solved using standard higher-order ODE solvers (e.g. family of explicit
[
37
]
and implicit
[
38
,
39
]
Runge–Kutta methods or structure-preserving algorithms
[
40
,
41
]
). This is a remarkable result since existing
{S\!E}(3)
rod formulations require highly customized Lie group solvers
[
42
,
20
]
. In order to apply well-established methods from structural dynamics like the Newmark-
\beta
[
43
]
or the generalized-
\alpha
method
[
44
]
, a slightly modified update of the generalized coordinates has to be applied, see Equation (37a) and (37b) of Arnold et al.
[
25
]
. Alternatively, a generalized-
\alpha
formulation for first-order differential equations
[
45
]
can be used without any modifications. The nonlinear generalized force equilibrium (
51
) is solved by any root finding algorithm (e.g. Newton–Raphson, Riks). Note that a system of linear equations with a non-symmetric matrix must be solved in each iteration.

4
Numerical experiments

This section demonstrates the power of the developed
{S\!E}(3)
finite element formulation by a variety of selected benchmark examples. The
cantilever experiment
slightly extends the investigations of Meier et al.
[
28
]
and gives numerical evidence for the absence of locking and further demonstrates a second-order spatial convergence of the proposed formulation. The
helix experiment
, which was used in Harsch et al.
[
9
]
to study locking for a director beam formulation, is used as a second example to show that the
{S\!E}(3)
-formulation is intrinsically locking-free. Objectivity after discretization is demonstrated in the example
superimposed rotation of deformed cantilever
. Large inhomogeneous deformations are studied in the example
rod bent to a helical form
[
46
]
. Finally, dynamics is examined by the
flexible heavy top
example.

We used a Newton–Raphson method to solve all static experiments with an absolute tolerance
atol
in terms of the max error of the total generalized forces. Prescribed boundary conditions were incorporated into the principle of virtual work using perfect bilateral constraints
[
35
]
. The dynamic problem was solved using a standard fourth order Runge–Kutta method as well as the generalized-
\alpha
method for first order differential equations
[
45
]
. For simplicity, boundary conditions were enforced on acceleration level only. Unless stated otherwise, arising element integrals were evaluated using a two-point Gauss–Legendre quadrature rule.

5
Conclusion

We have presented a rod finite element formulation based on a two-node
{S\!E}(3)
-interpolation, that is, the interpolation of relative Euclidean transformation matrices using relative twists. In contrast to the typical uncoupled interpolation of centerline points and relative rotations using relative rotation vectors, the proposed
{S\!E}(3)
-interpolation leads to element-wise constant strain measures. Thus, by construction, the resulting finite element formulation will show an absence of both membrane and shear locking and can be applied in scenarios where very high slenderness ratios are present. Objectivity of the discretized strain measures followed from the interpolation strategy with relative Euclidean transformation matrices.

With a Petrov–Galerkin projection method it is possible to discretize the arising virtual work functionals in space resulting in a set of ordinary differential equations (in dynamics) or a set of nonlinear equations (in statics) both of which can be solved using standard numerical schemes. Thus, the drawback of existing
{S\!E}(3)
rod formulations, which require highly specialized Lie group solvers is circumvented in an elegant way. Further, for typical applications, the arising mass matrix is symmetric and constant. Since the virtual displacements and rotations are interpolated instead of using the consistent variations of the proposed
{S\!E}(3)
-interpolation formula, a non-symmetric stiffness matrix is obtained, which can be disadvantageous in terms of performance and storage.
The nodal kinematics of the proposed formulation, i.e., the nodal Euclidean transformation matrices, are parameterized using total centerline points and total rotation vectors together with the
S\!O(3)
-exponential map. Arising singularities are circumvented by introducing the concept of complement rotation vectors together with the proposed relative interpolation strategy. Thus, a parametrization with a minimal number of six nodal unknowns has been achieved.
The paper is closed by demonstrating all the individual properties of the proposed formulation by numerical benchmark examples in statics and dynamics.

Matrix Lie groups

A.1
The general linear group
G\!L(n)

In the course of this treatment, we restrict ourselves to matrix Lie groups over the real numbers, that is, Lie groups
G
that are closed subgroups of the general linear group
G\!L(n)=\{\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}|\det(\mathbf{A})\neq 0\}
, which is the group of all
n\times n
invertible matrices with real entries
[
52
]
. For
\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B}\in G\!L(n)
, the group operation is given by the usual matrix product
(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B})\mapsto\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}
. The inverse and transpose of
\mathbf{A}
are respectively denoted by
\mathbf{A}^{-1}
and
\mathbf{A}^{\!\mathrm{T}}
. The identity is given by the
n\times n
identity matrix
\mathbf{1}_{n\times n}
. Analogously, we denote the
n\times n
matrix containing only zeros by
\mathbf{0}_{n\times n}
.

Let
\mathfrak{gl}(n)=T_{\mathbf{1}_{n\times n}}G\!L(n)
be the tangent space of
G\!L(n)
at the identity
\mathbf{1}_{n\times n}
. In Section 3.3 of Baker
[
53
]
, it is shown that
\mathfrak{gl}(n)
corresponds to the set of all real
n\times n
matrices, which constitute an
n^{2}
-dimensional vector space. The Lie algebra of
G\!L(n)
consists of the vector space
\mathfrak{gl}(n)
together with the bilinear map

[\bullet,\bullet]\colon\mathfrak{gl}(n)\times\mathfrak{gl}(n)\to\mathfrak{gl}(n)\,,\quad(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y})\mapsto[\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y}]=\mathbf{X}\mathbf{Y}-\mathbf{Y}\mathbf{X}\,,

(59)

which is a Lie bracket as it is skew-symmetric and satisfies the Jacobi identity
[\mathbf{X},[\mathbf{Y},\mathbf{Z}]]+[\mathbf{Y},[\mathbf{Z},\mathbf{X}]]+[\mathbf{Z},[\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y}]]=\mathbf{0}_{n\times n}

\forall\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y},\mathbf{Z}\in\mathfrak{gl}(n)
. For a fixed argument
\mathbf{X}\in\mathfrak{gl}(n)
the Lie bracket defines the linear operator

\operatorname{ad}_{\mathbf{X}}\colon\mathfrak{gl}(n)\to\mathfrak{gl}(n)\,,\quad\mathbf{Y}\mapsto\operatorname{ad}_{\mathbf{X}}(\mathbf{Y})=[\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y}]\,,

(60)

called the adjoint map. Thus, instead of
[\mathbf{X},[\mathbf{X},[\mathbf{X},[\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y}]]]]
, we can write
\operatorname{ad}_{\mathbf{X}}^{4}(\mathbf{Y})
, see Hall
[
52
]
Section 3.1. Note that the adjoint map to the power of zero is defined as
\operatorname{ad}_{\mathbf{X}}^{0}(\mathbf{Y})\coloneqq\mathbf{Y}
. Further, the identity
\operatorname{ad}_{-\mathbf{X}}^{i}(\mathbf{Y})=(-1)^{i}\operatorname{ad}_{\mathbf{X}}^{i}(\mathbf{Y})
readily follows from iteratively applying the bilinearity of the Lie bracket. According to Baker
[
53
]
Section 2.1, the matrix exponential is defined by the power series

\exp\colon\mathfrak{gl}(n)\to G\!L(n)\,,\quad\mathbf{X}\mapsto\mathbf{A}=\exp(\mathbf{X})=\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\frac{\mathbf{X}^{i}}{i!}\,.

(61)

As in Section 2.6 of Iserles et al.
[
54
]
, for a smooth curve
\mathbf{Z}(t)\in\mathfrak{gl}(n)
, the derivative of the exponential map can be written as

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\exp\left(\mathbf{Z}(t)\right)=\operatorname{dexp}_{\mathbf{Z}(t)}\left(\dot{\mathbf{Z}}(t)\right)\exp\left(\mathbf{Z}(t)\right)=\exp\left(\mathbf{Z}(t)\right)\operatorname{dexp}_{-\mathbf{Z}(t)}\left(\dot{\mathbf{Z}}(t)\right)\,,

(62)

where, for
\mathbf{X}\in\mathfrak{gl}(n)
, the linear map
\operatorname{dexp}_{\mathbf{X}}
is defined in terms of a power series as

\operatorname{dexp}_{\mathbf{X}}\colon\mathfrak{gl}(n)\to\mathfrak{gl}(n)\,,\quad\mathbf{Y}\mapsto\operatorname{dexp}_{\mathbf{X}}(\mathbf{Y})\coloneqq\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(i+1)!}\operatorname{ad}_{\mathbf{X}}^{i}(\mathbf{Y})\,.

(63)

Rearranging the equations in (
62
) to

\operatorname{dexp}_{-\mathbf{Z}(t)}\left(\dot{\mathbf{Z}}(t)\right)=\exp\left(\mathbf{Z}(t)\right)^{-1}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\exp\left(\mathbf{Z}(t)\right)\quad\text{and}\quad\operatorname{dexp}_{\mathbf{Z}(t)}\left(\dot{\mathbf{Z}}(t)\right)=\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\exp\left(\mathbf{Z}(t)\right)\exp\left(\mathbf{Z}(t)\right)^{-1}\,,

(64)

it is reasonable that
\operatorname{dexp}_{-\mathbf{X}(t)}
and
\operatorname{dexp}_{\mathbf{X}(t)}
are called left- and right-trivialized differentials, respectively. According to Equation (2.46) of Iserles et al.
[
54
]
, the inverse of
\operatorname{dexp}_{\mathbf{X}}
can also be written by the power series

\displaystyle\operatorname{dexp}^{-1}_{\mathbf{X}}\colon\mathfrak{gl}(n)\to\mathfrak{gl}(n)\,,\quad\mathbf{Y}\mapsto\operatorname{dexp}^{-1}_{\mathbf{X}}(\mathbf{Y})\coloneqq\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\frac{B_{i}}{i!}\operatorname{ad}_{\mathbf{X}}^{i}(\mathbf{Y})\,,

(65)

where
B_{i}
denotes the
i
-th Bernoulli number.

Let the tuple
(\mathfrak{g},[\cdot,\cdot])
be a subalgebra of
(\mathfrak{gl}(n),[\cdot,\cdot])
, i.e.,
\mathfrak{g}
is a subspace of
\mathfrak{gl}(n)
with dimension
k\leq n^{2}
and
[\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y}]\in\mathfrak{g}
for
\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y}\in\mathfrak{g}
. Then, we can always find a linear and bijective map

j\colon\mathbb{R}^{k}\to\mathfrak{g}\,,\quad\mathbf{x}\mapsto\mathbf{X}=j(\mathbf{x})\,.

(66)

Since
j
and
\operatorname{dexp}_{-j(\mathbf{x})}
are linear maps, it is convenient to construct the tangent operator
\mathbf{T}(\mathbf{x})\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times k}
, which is the linear map defined as

\mathbf{T}(\mathbf{x})\coloneqq j^{-1}\circ\operatorname{dexp}_{-j(\mathbf{x})}\circ j=j^{-1}\circ\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{i}}{(i+1)!}\operatorname{ad}_{j(\mathbf{x})}^{i}\circ j=\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{i}}{(i+1)!}\lceil\mathbf{x}\rfloor^{i}\,,

(67)

where in the last equality we have introduced the mapping

\lceil\bullet\rfloor\colon\mathbb{R}^{k}\to\mathbb{R}^{k\times k}\,,\quad\mathbf{x}\mapsto\lceil\mathbf{x}\rfloor=j^{-1}\circ\operatorname{ad}_{j(\mathbf{x})}\circ j\,.

(68)

Often this mapping is applied in the form
j\big(\lceil\mathbf{x}\rfloor\mathbf{y}\big)=\operatorname{ad}_{j(\mathbf{x})}\big(j(\mathbf{y})\big)
, for
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
. If
j(\mathbf{x})
and
j(\mathbf{y})
commute, then powers of the adjoint map
\operatorname{ad}_{j(\mathbf{x})}^{i}(j(\mathbf{y}))
vanish for
i>0
resulting in
\mathbf{T}(\mathbf{x})\mathbf{y}=\left(j^{-1}\circ\operatorname{dexp}_{-j(\mathbf{x})}\circ j\right)\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{y}
. The inverse of the tangent operator is given by

\mathbf{T}(\mathbf{x})^{-1}=\left(j^{-1}\circ\operatorname{dexp}_{-j(\mathbf{x})}\circ j\right)^{-1}=j^{-1}\circ\operatorname{dexp}^{-1}_{-j(\mathbf{x})}\circ j=j^{-1}\circ\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{i}B_{i}}{i!}\operatorname{ad}_{j(\mathbf{x})}^{i}\circ j=\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{i}B_{i}}{i!}\lceil\mathbf{x}\rfloor^{i}\,.

(69)

Since the matrix rank of
\lceil\mathbf{x}\rfloor
is at most
k
, the Cayley–Hamilton theorem guarantees the existence of functions
a_{0}(\lceil\mathbf{x}\rfloor)
,
\dots
,
a_{k-1}(\lceil\mathbf{x}\rfloor)
such that

\lceil\mathbf{x}\rfloor^{k}=\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}a_{i}(\lceil\mathbf{x}\rfloor)\lceil\mathbf{x}\rfloor^{i}\,.

(70)

Hence, we can express the tangent operator (
67
) and its inverse (
69
) by a finite number of powers of
\lceil\mathbf{x}\rfloor
. Using similar arguments, also the exponential map (
61
) can be expressed by a finite number of matrix powers.

A.2
The special orthogonal group
S\!O(3)

As a subgroup of
G\!L(3)
, the special orthogonal group

S\!O(3)=\{\mathbf{A}\in G\!L(3)|\mathbf{A}^{\!\mathrm{T}}\mathbf{A}=\mathbf{1}_{3\times 3},\,\operatorname{det}(\mathbf{A})=+1\}

(71)

is the group of all proper orthogonal transformations. In Baker
[
53
]
Section 3.3, it is shown that the tangent space at the identity

\mathfrak{so}(3)=T_{\mathbf{1}_{3\times 3}}S\!O(3)=\{\bm{\Omega}\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}|\bm{\Omega}+\bm{\Omega}^{\!\mathrm{T}}=\mathbf{0}_{3\times 3}\}

(72)

corresponds to the three-dimensional space of skew-symmetric
3\times 3
matrices, which in dynamics often corresponds to the space of angular velocities. Since
\mathfrak{so}(3)
is a subspace of
\mathfrak{gl}(3)
and the Lie bracket
[\bm{\Omega}_{1},\bm{\Omega}_{2}]\in\mathfrak{so}(3)
for
\bm{\Omega}_{1},\bm{\Omega}_{2}\in\mathfrak{so}(3)
, the tuple
(\mathfrak{so}(3),[\cdot,\cdot])
is a Lie subalgebra of
(\mathfrak{gl}(3),[\cdot,\cdot])
.

For
\mathfrak{so}(3)
, the map (
66
) can be identified explicitly as

j_{S\!O(3)}(\bullet)=\widetilde{(\bullet)}\colon\mathbb{R}^{3}\to\mathfrak{so}(3)\,,\quad\bm{\omega}=\begin{pmatrix}\omega_{1}\\
\omega_{2}\\
\omega_{3}\end{pmatrix}\mapsto\bm{\Omega}=j_{S\!O(3)}(\bm{\omega})=\widetilde{\bm{\omega}}=\begin{pmatrix}0&-\omega_{3}&\omega_{2}\\
\omega_{3}&0&-\omega_{1}\\
-\omega_{2}&\omega_{1}&0\end{pmatrix}\,,

(73)

where we have introduced the tilde symbol for compact notation. This map is related to the cross product by
\widetilde{\bm{\omega}}\mathbf{r}=\bm{\omega}\times\mathbf{r}
for
\bm{\omega},\mathbf{r}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
.

In order to minimize the number of transcendental function evaluations
[
23
,
20
]
, we introduce the mappings

\alpha(\widetilde{\bm{\omega}})=\frac{\sin(\|\bm{\omega}\|)}{\|\bm{\omega}\|}\,,\quad\beta(\widetilde{\bm{\omega}})=2\frac{1-\cos(\|\bm{\omega}\|)}{\|\bm{\omega}\|^{2}}\,,\quad\gamma(\widetilde{\bm{\omega}})=\frac{\alpha(\widetilde{\bm{\omega}})}{\beta(\widetilde{\bm{\omega}})}=\frac{\|\bm{\omega}\|}{2}\cot(\|\bm{\omega}\|/2)\,,

(74)

with
\lim_{\|\bm{\omega}\|\to 0}\alpha(\widetilde{\bm{\omega}})=\lim_{\|\bm{\omega}\|\to 0}\beta(\widetilde{\bm{\omega}})=\lim_{\|\bm{\omega}\|\to 0}\gamma(\widetilde{\bm{\omega}})=1
. Noting that
\widetilde{\bm{\omega}}^{3}=-\|\bm{\omega}\|^{2}\widetilde{\bm{\omega}}
and carefully separating even and odd parts of the power series of the exponential map (
61
), see Murray et al.
[
55
]
Section 2.2, the exponential map from
\mathfrak{so}(3)
to
S\!O(3)
has an analytical form known as Rodrigues’ formula

\exp_{S\!O(3)}\colon\mathfrak{so}(3)\to S\!O(3)\,,\quad\bm{\Omega}\mapsto\mathbf{A}=\exp_{S\!O(3)}(\bm{\Omega})=\mathbf{1}_{3\times 3}+\alpha(\bm{\Omega})\bm{\Omega}+\frac{\beta(\bm{\Omega})}{2}\bm{\Omega}^{2}\,.

(75)

For the case
\|\bm{\Omega}\|\to 0
, following the typical approach in computational methods, the first order approximation
\exp_{S\!O(3)}(\bm{\Omega})=\mathbf{1}_{3\times 3}+\bm{\Omega}
is applied.

From explicitly computing (
75
) and denoting
\omega=\|\bm{\omega}\|
, the diagonal terms reveal the identity
\cos\omega=\tfrac{1}{2}(\operatorname{tr}(\mathbf{A})-1)
, which can be solved for
\omega=\omega(\mathbf{A})
. The off diagonals directly lead to
\widetilde{\bm{\omega}}=\tfrac{\omega}{2\sin(\omega)}(\mathbf{A}-\mathbf{A}^{\!\mathrm{T}})
. Using
\sin^{2}(\omega(\mathbf{A}))=1-(\operatorname{tr}(\mathbf{A})-1)^{2}/4
, an analytic form of the
S\!O(3)
-logarithm map is given by

\log_{S\!O(3)}\colon S\!O(3)\to\mathfrak{so}(3)\,,\quad\mathbf{A}\mapsto\bm{\Omega}=\log_{S\!O(3)}(\mathbf{A})=\frac{\omega(\mathbf{A})}{2\sin\big(\omega(\mathbf{A})\big)}(\mathbf{A}-\mathbf{A}^{\!\mathrm{T}})\,.

(76)

Again, for
\omega\to 0
, we use the first order approximation
\bm{\Omega}=(\mathbf{A}-\mathbf{A}^{\!\mathrm{T}})/2
. For notational convenience, we further introduce the capitalized exponential and logarithm maps

\displaystyle\operatorname{Exp}_{S\!O(3)}\colon\mathbb{R}^{3}\to S\!O(3)\,,\quad

\displaystyle\bm{\omega}\mapsto\mathbf{A}

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Exp}_{S\!O(3)}(\bm{\omega})=\exp_{S\!O(3)}\circ j_{S\!O(3)}(\bm{\omega})\,,

(77)

\displaystyle\operatorname{Log}_{S\!O(3)}\colon S\!O(3)\to\mathbb{R}^{3}\,,\quad

\displaystyle\mathbf{A}\mapsto\bm{\omega}

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Log}_{S\!O(3)}(\mathbf{A})=j_{S\!O(3)}^{-1}\circ\log_{S\!O(3)}(\mathbf{A})

\displaystyle=\frac{\omega(\mathbf{A})}{2\sin\big(\omega(\mathbf{A})\big)}\big(A_{32}-A_{23},\,A_{13}-A_{31},\,A_{21}-A_{12}\big)\,,

which directly relate
\mathbb{R}^{3}
with
S\!O(3)
and vice versa.

For a lighter notation, we shortly suppress the subscripts of
j
and
\lceil\bullet\rfloor
indicating
S\!O(3)
. Let

N_{0,\xi}^{e}(\xi)+N_{1,\xi}^{e}(\xi)=0
, one readily sees that
\delta W^{\mathrm{int}}(\delta\mathbf{s};\mathbf{q})=0
.
Using (
109
) in the discrete inertial virtual work (
44
) together with
N_{0}^{e}(\xi)+N_{1}^{e}(\xi)=1
leads to

\delta W^{\mathrm{dyn}}(\delta\mathbf{s};\mathbf{q},\mathbf{u})=-{}_{I}\mathbf{c}^{\!\mathrm{T}}\left(\sum_{e=0}^{n_{\mathrm{el}}-1}\int_{\mathcal{J}^{e}}A_{\rho_{0}}{}_{I}\mathbf{v}_{P}J\,\mathrm{d}\xi\right)^{{\cdot}}=-{}_{I}\mathbf{c}^{\!\mathrm{T}}{}_{I}\dot{\mathbf{L}}(\mathbf{u})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle!}}{{=}}0\,,

(110)

which, in agreement with the principle of virtual work, must vanish if no external forces are applied to the rod. Consequently, the rate of change of the linear momentum
{}_{I}\dot{\mathbf{L}}=\mathbf{0}_{3\times 1}
vanishes and the linear momentum
{}_{I}{\mathbf{L}}
is preserved.

D.3
Discrete conservation of angular momentum

The present rod finite element formulation uses nodal virtual rotations expressed in the individual cross-section-fixed bases
{}_{K_{i}}\delta\bm{\phi}_{IK_{i}}(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
. For an arbitrary
{}_{I}\bm{\eta}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
, the nodal virtual displacements
\delta\mathbf{s}^{i}=({}_{I}\bm{\eta}\times{}_{I}\mathbf{r}_{OP_{i}},\ \mathbf{A}_{IK_{i}}^{\!\mathrm{T}}{}_{I}\bm{\eta})

lead to a virtual rotation around the origin of all nodal points. With respect to the inertial basis, by construction, the nodal cross-sections are all virtually rotated the same. However, due to the chosen interpolation (
41
)
3
, the cross-sections within the elements are not all rotated with the same constant virtual rotation
{}_{I}\bm{\eta}
. Hence, for this formulation a virtual rotation of the entire rod is not contained in the set of admissible virtual displacements. Consequently, there is no algorithmic access to the discrete angular momentum and it is not possible to construct a numerical time integration scheme that preserves the discrete total angular momentum.

If such a conservation property is crucial for a specific application, the present rod formulation can easily be modified. Instead of using the nodal virtual rotations
{}_{K_{i}}\delta\bm{\phi}_{IK_{i}}(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
expressed in the cross-section-fixed basis
K_{i}
, the nodal virtual rotations
{}_{I}\delta\bm{\phi}_{IK_{i}}(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
expressed in the inertial basis
I
are used. Analogously, the nodal angular velocities
{}_{K_{i}}\bm{\omega}_{IK_{i}}(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
are replaced by
{}_{I}\bm{\omega}_{IK_{i}}(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
. Proceeding similar to the original formulation, new discretized internal and inertial virtual work contributions are obtained. They are

\displaystyle\delta W^{\mathrm{int}}(\delta\mathbf{s};\mathbf{q})

\displaystyle=\delta\mathbf{s}^{\!\mathrm{T}}\mathbf{f}^{\mathrm{int}}(\mathbf{q})\,,\quad\mathbf{f}^{\mathrm{int}}(\mathbf{q})=\sum_{e=0}^{n_{\mathrm{el}}-1}\mathbf{C}_{e}^{\!\mathrm{T}}\mathbf{f}^{\mathrm{int}}_{e}(\mathbf{C}_{e}\mathbf{q})\,,

(111)

\displaystyle\mathbf{f}^{\mathrm{int}}_{e}(\mathbf{q}^{e})

\displaystyle=-\int_{\mathcal{J}^{e}}\sum_{i=0}^{1}\Big\{N^{e}_{i,\xi}\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{r},i}^{\!\mathrm{T}}\mathbf{A}_{IK}{}_{K}\mathbf{n}+N^{e}_{i,\xi}\mathbf{C}_{\bm{\psi},i}^{\!\mathrm{T}}\mathbf{A}_{IK}{}_{K}\mathbf{m}-N^{e}_{i}\mathbf{C}_{\bm{\psi},i}^{\!\mathrm{T}}\left({}_{I}\mathbf{r}_{OP,\xi}\times\mathbf{A}_{IK}{}_{K}\mathbf{n}\right)\Big\}\mathrm{d}\xi\,,

and

\displaystyle\delta W^{\mathrm{dyn}}(\delta\mathbf{s};\mathbf{q},\mathbf{u})

\displaystyle=-\delta\mathbf{s}^{\!\mathrm{T}}\left\{\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{q})\dot{\mathbf{u}}+\mathbf{f}^{\mathrm{gyr}}(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{u})\right\}\,,

(112)

\displaystyle\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{q})

\displaystyle=\sum_{e=0}^{n_{\mathrm{el}}-1}\mathbf{C}_{e}^{\!\mathrm{T}}\mathbf{M}_{e}(\mathbf{C}_{e}\mathbf{q})\mathbf{C}_{e}\,,\quad\mathbf{f}^{\mathrm{gyr}}(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{u})=\sum_{e=0}^{n_{\mathrm{el}}-1}\mathbf{C}_{e}^{\!\mathrm{T}}\mathbf{f}^{\mathrm{gyr}}(\mathbf{C}_{e}\mathbf{q},\mathbf{C}_{e}\mathbf{u})\,,

\displaystyle\mathbf{M}_{e}(\mathbf{q}^{e})

\displaystyle=\begin{multlined}\int_{\mathcal{J}^{e}}\sum_{i=0}^{1}\sum_{k=0}^{1}N^{e}_{i}N^{e}_{k}\Big\{\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{r},i}^{\!\mathrm{T}}A_{\rho_{0}}\mathbf{1}_{3\times 3}\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{r},k}+\mathbf{C}_{\bm{\psi},i}^{\!\mathrm{T}}{}_{I}\mathbf{I}_{\rho_{0}}\mathbf{C}_{\bm{\psi},k}\Big\}J\mathrm{d}\xi\,,\end{multlined}

\displaystyle\mathbf{f}^{\mathrm{gyr}}_{e}(\mathbf{q}^{e},\mathbf{u}^{e})

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{J}^{e}}\sum_{i=0}^{1}N^{e}_{i}\mathbf{C}_{\bm{\psi},i}^{\!\mathrm{T}}{}_{I}\widetilde{\bm{\omega}}_{IK}{}_{I}\mathbf{I}_{\rho_{0}}{}_{I}\bm{\omega}_{IK}J\mathrm{d}\xi\,,

with

{}_{I}\mathbf{I}_{\rho_{0}}(\mathbf{q})=\mathbf{A}_{IK}(\mathbf{q}){}_{K}\mathbf{I}_{\rho_{0}}\mathbf{A}_{IK}(\mathbf{q})^{\!\mathrm{T}}\,.

(113)

Note, with this reformulation the mass matrix
\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{q})
and the gyroscopic forces
\mathbf{f}^{\mathrm{gyr}}(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{u})
depend on the generalized coordinates
\mathbf{q}
, which is clearly a drawback with respect to the original formulation.

Conservation of energy and linear momentum follows in the same way as just shown and is not repeated here. As the virtual rotations are now formulated with respect to the inertial basis, the nodal virtual displacements

\delta\mathbf{s}^{i}=\begin{pmatrix}{}_{I}\bm{\eta}\times{}_{I}\mathbf{r}_{OP_{i}}\\
{}_{I}\bm{\eta}\end{pmatrix}\,,

(114)

induce a virtual rotation for the entire rod and conservation of total angular momentum can be shown. Indeed, for this discretization, the discrete angular momentum takes the form

{}_{I}\mathbf{J}(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{u})=\int_{\mathcal{B}}{}_{I}\mathbf{r}_{OQ}\times{}_{I}\dot{\mathbf{r}}_{OQ}\mathrm{d}m=\sum_{e=0}^{n_{\mathrm{el}}-1}\int_{\mathcal{J}^{e}}\left\{{}_{I}\mathbf{r}_{OP}\times A_{\rho_{0}}{}_{I}\mathbf{v}_{P}+{}_{I}\mathbf{I}_{\rho_{0}}{}_{I}\bm{\omega}_{IK}\right\}J\,\mathrm{d}\xi\,.

(115)

Inserting (
114
) into the discrete internal virtual work (
111
) and recognizing the identities already used for the linear momentum leads to a vanishing internal virtual work functional
\delta W^{\mathrm{int}}(\delta\mathbf{s};\mathbf{q})=0
.
Using (
114
) in the discrete inertial virtual work (
112
) results in

\delta W^{\mathrm{dyn}}(\delta\mathbf{s};\mathbf{q},\mathbf{u})=-{}_{I}\bm{\eta}^{\!\mathrm{T}}\sum_{e=0}^{n_{\mathrm{el}}-1}\int_{\mathcal{J}^{e}}\left\{{}_{I}\mathbf{r}_{OP}\times A_{\rho_{0}}{}_{I}\dot{\mathbf{v}}_{P}+{}_{I}\mathbf{I}_{\rho_{0}}{}_{I}\dot{\bm{\omega}}_{IK}+{}_{I}\widetilde{\bm{\omega}}_{IK}{}_{I}\mathbf{I}_{\rho_{0}}{}_{I}\bm{\omega}_{IK}\right\}J\,\mathrm{d}\xi=-{}_{I}\bm{\eta}^{\!\mathrm{T}}{}_{I}\dot{\mathbf{J}}(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{u})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle!}}{{=}}0\,,

(116)

which, according to the principle of virtual work, must be zero for vanishing external virtual work functionals. It readily follows that
{}_{I}\dot{\mathbf{J}}=\mathbf{0}_{3\times 1}
and that the total angular momentum
{}_{I}\mathbf{J}
is conserved in these cases.
