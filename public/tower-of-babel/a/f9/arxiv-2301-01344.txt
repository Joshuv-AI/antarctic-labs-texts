Vestigial singlet pairing in a fluctuating magnetic triplet superconductor: Applications to graphene moiré systems
Prathyush P. Poduval, Mathias S. Scheurer

Abstract
Motivated by the phenomenology of graphene moiré superlattices, we study a 2D model with strong tendencies towards both magnetism and triplet superconductivity. Individually, their respective order parameters, 𝑵 \boldsymbol{N} and 𝒅 \boldsymbol{d} , cannot order at finite temperature. Nonetheless, the model exhibits a variety of vestigial phases, including charge- 4 ​ e 4e superconductivity and broken time-reversal symmetry. Our main focus is on a phase characterized by finite 𝒅 ⋅ 𝑵 \boldsymbol{d}\cdot\boldsymbol{N} , which has the same symmetries as the BCS state, a Meissner effect, and metastable supercurrents, yet rather different spectral properties: most notably, the suppression of the electronic density of states at the Fermi can resemble that of either a fully gapped or nodal superconductor, depending on parameters. This could provide a possible explanation for recent tunneling experiments in graphene moiré systems.



Strongly correlated systems often exhibit complex phase diagrams with multiple phases, characterized by long-range or quasi-long-range order (QLRO) of different order parameters. \frac{\rho_{F}(\phi_{0})}{\rho_{F}(\phi_{0}=0)}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+\alpha^{2}}},\quad\alpha=\frac{3\phi_{0}\lambda^{2}r_{N}}{2Tv_{N}^{2}(r_{d}r_{N}-\phi_{0}^{2})}.

(4)

Note that
\phi_{0}^{2}
is bounded above by
r_{d}r_{N}
, at which point the bosonic fields would condense and continuous symmetries would be broken, which cannot happen at finite
T
. As
\phi_{0}
increases,
\alpha
increases the suppression of the DOS, and near the instability point of
\phi_{0}^{2}=r_{d}r_{N}
, there are no states near the Fermi surface.

To complement this analysis, we have also studied the Hamiltonian associated with setting
q=0
in Eq. (
2b
) within self-consistent Hartree-Fock, only allowing for spin-rotation invariant operators to condense
Note1
. For small
\alpha
, one also finds only a partial suppression of the low-energy spectral weight, akin to Eq. (
4
); including higher-order corrections leads to a hard gap for
\alpha\geq 1
.

Electromagnetic response.—
We will finally demonstrate that the superconducting phase characterized by
\phi_{0}\neq 0
has the same electromagnetic phenomenology as BCS superconductors, despite the unusual electronic spectral properties. To this end, we study off-diagonal long-range order (ODLRO)
Penrose and Onsager 1956
;
Penrose 1951
;
Yang 1962
which implies the Meissner effect
Sewell 1990
, flux quantization
Nieh et al. 1995
, Josephson effect and persistent currents
Sewell 1997
. First focusing on the electrons, we show that
\braket{c^{\dagger}_{s_{1},+}(\boldsymbol{x}_{1})c^{\dagger}_{s_{2},-}(\boldsymbol{x}_{2})c^{\phantom{\dagger}}_{s_{2}^{\prime},-}(\boldsymbol{x}_{2}^{\prime})c^{\phantom{\dagger}}_{s_{1}^{\prime},+}(\boldsymbol{x}_{1}^{\prime})}\rightarrow n_{0}(\Psi^{*}_{\text{F}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{12}))_{s_{1},s_{2}}(\Psi_{\text{F}}(\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}_{12}))_{s_{1}^{\prime},s_{2}^{\prime}}
, with
\Psi_{\text{F}}\neq 0
, as
|\boldsymbol{x}_{j}-\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}_{j}|\rightarrow\infty
at finite
\boldsymbol{x}_{12}=\boldsymbol{x}_{1}-\boldsymbol{x}_{2}
and
\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}_{12}=\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}_{1}-\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}_{2}
, to leading (first) order in
\phi_{0}
; as non-zero
\Psi_{\text{F}}
to linear order in
\phi_{0}
implies that it cannot vanish identically for generic
\phi_{0}
, this is sufficient to show the presence of ODLRO. We find the “macroscopic wave function” to be a singlet,
\Psi_{\text{F}}(\boldsymbol{x})=is_{y}\psi_{\text{F}}(\boldsymbol{x})
, as expected since spin-rotation symmetry is preserved at finite
T
, with
\psi_{\text{F}}(\boldsymbol{x})
shown in Fig.
4
(a).
Alternatively, one can demonstrate ODLRO to arbitrary order in
\phi_{0}
, by focusing on the bosons: to zeroth order in
\lambda
, we find
\braket{(\boldsymbol{d}^{\dagger}(\boldsymbol{x}_{1})\boldsymbol{N}(\boldsymbol{x}_{2}))(\boldsymbol{d}(\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}_{1})\boldsymbol{N}(\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}_{2}))}\rightarrow\psi^{*}_{\text{B}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{12})\psi_{\text{B}}(\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}_{12})
as
|\boldsymbol{x}_{j}-\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}_{j}|\rightarrow\infty
, with
\psi_{\text{B}}(\boldsymbol{x})
plotted in Fig.
4
(b) along with an analytic asymptotic form for large
\boldsymbol{x}
; in
Note1
, we show that this leads to the same constraints as the conventional form of bosonic ODLRO
Penrose and Onsager 1956
;
Penrose 1951
.
Finally, the connection to the textbook theory of superconductivity can be made more explicit by deriving the analogue of the time-dependent Ginzburg-Landay theory: we reinstate fluctuations via
\phi_{0}\rightarrow\phi(\boldsymbol{x},\tau)
and integrate out all other degrees of freedom yielding

\mathcal{S}_{\text{GL}}=\int_{\boldsymbol{x},\tau}\hskip-3.00003pt\left[\rho|D_{\tau}\phi|^{2}+(r_{\phi}+|c_{1}|^{-1})|\phi|^{2}+v^{2}\,|\boldsymbol{D}\phi|^{2}\right]

(5)

to leading order in
\phi
and gauge-covariant derivatives
(D_{\tau},\boldsymbol{D})_{\mu}=\partial_{\mu}-i2eA_{\mu}
. For demonstration purposes, we evaluated the coefficients in
\mathcal{S}_{\text{GL}}
to leading (zeroth) order in
\mathcal{S}_{c}
and find
\rho,v_{\phi}>0
and
r_{\phi}<0
for low
T
[see Fig.
4
(c,d)]; the state with QLRO in
\phi_{0}
thus corresponds, as usual, to the Higgs phase, with Meissner effect and massive Higgs mode, but without Goldstone modes.

Conclusion.—
We have studied the finite-
T
vestigial phases, see Fig.
1
(a), associated with two primary order parameters,
\boldsymbol{d}
and
\boldsymbol{N}
, describing a fully gapped triplet superconductor and spin magnetism, respectively.
A crucial result is the DOS of phase
(B_{1,2})
in Fig.
3
(c): varying
\phi_{0}
changes the low-energy DOS from partial suppression, akin to that of a nodal superconducting state, to a hard gap. As
\phi_{0}
is expected to change with electron filling, this could explain the tunneling data in
Kim et al. 2022
;
Oh et al. 2021
.
We finally point out that the suppression of
\boldsymbol{N}
would immediately also suppress
\phi_{0}
in our model and could, therefore, explain why superconductivity is connected to the reset behavior in trilayer graphene
Park et al. 2021
;
Hao et al. 2021
;
Kim et al. 2022
;
Oh et al. 2021
.

Mean-field form of the bosonic interactions

In the main text, we view the field theory defined by the action
\mathcal{S}=\mathcal{S}_{e}+\mathcal{S}_{\chi}+\mathcal{S}_{c}+\mathcal{S}_{V}
as an effective low-energy theory that arises when high-energy electronic degrees of freedom have already been integrated out. Due to the symmetry and locality constraints, it only depends on a few parameters,
r_{\mu}
,
v_{\mu}
,
b_{1,2,3}
,
c_{1,2}
. As can be seen in Fig.

(a), in particular, (the sign of) the parameters

and
b_{2}
entering

crucially determine the phase of the system. We here provide an estimate for these parameters using mean-field theory. To this end, we replace the bosonic fields by classical homogeneous and time-independent vectors,
\boldsymbol{N}_{q}\rightarrow\delta_{q,0}\boldsymbol{N}
,
\boldsymbol{d}_{q}\rightarrow\delta_{q,0}\boldsymbol{d}
, in
\mathcal{S}_{e}+\mathcal{S}_{\chi}+\mathcal{S}_{c}

(6)

which we now view as our full action, also containing the high-energy degrees of freedom. Integrating out the electronic degrees of freedom and expanding the resulting action in terms of
\boldsymbol{N}
and
\boldsymbol{d}
to quartic order, one obtains exactly the same terms as in

defined in the main text, as expected by symmetry. Moreover, one finds

c_{1}=b_{2}=-b_{1}/2<0,\quad\text{with}\quad b_{1}=32\,\lambda^{4}T\sum_{\omega_{n}}\int\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}\boldsymbol{k}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\frac{1}{(\omega_{n}^{2}+\epsilon_{\boldsymbol{k}}^{2})^{2}}>0.

(7)

;
Kozii et al. 2019
;
Scheurer and Samajdar 2020

Suppression of DOS at

In this section, we derive a compact approximate analytical expression for the suppression of the density of states (DOS) as a result of the anomalous term
\Sigma_{3}=\Sigma_{\gamma_{y}}\gamma_{y}
. To this end, we focus on the limit of large bosonic velocities
v_{\mu}
in
\chi_{\mu}
and replace the
\boldsymbol{q}
integral in Eq. (

\displaystyle\Sigma_{\gamma_{y}}(\omega+i0^{+},\boldsymbol{k})

\displaystyle=3\phi_{0}\lambda^{2}\frac{r_{N}}{v_{N}^{2}}\left(f(\omega+i0^{+},\epsilon_{\boldsymbol{k}})-f(\omega+i0^{+},-\epsilon_{\boldsymbol{k}})\right).

(28)

Note that we would first need to re-parametrize the integral in terms of

\tilde{\boldsymbol{q}}=0

Appendix E
Meissner Effect From ODLRO

The consequences of ODLRO defined in terms of four-fermion or two-boson correlators are well-known
Yang 1962
;
Sewell 1990
;
Nieh et al. 1995
;
Sewell 1997
. As a result of spin-rotation symmetry, we cannot capture ODLRO using a correlator of only two bosons. Instead, we have to study the four-boson density matrix

\displaystyle\rho(\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\boldsymbol{x}_{2},\boldsymbol{x}_{1}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{x}_{2}^{\prime})=\langle\boldsymbol{N}(\boldsymbol{x}_{1})\cdot\boldsymbol{d}^{*}(\boldsymbol{x}_{2})\boldsymbol{N}(\boldsymbol{x}_{1}^{\prime})\cdot\boldsymbol{d}(\boldsymbol{x}_{2}^{\prime})\rangle.

(31)

Although the derivation is in close analogy to the two-boson or four-fermion case, we here show explicitly how the Meissner effect follows from

\rho(\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\boldsymbol{x}_{2},\boldsymbol{x}_{1}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{x}_{2}^{\prime})\to\phi_{0}^{*}(\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\boldsymbol{x}_{2})\phi_{0}(\boldsymbol{x}_{1}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{x}_{2}^{\prime})\neq 0,\quad|\boldsymbol{x}_{j}-\boldsymbol{x}_{j}^{\prime}|\rightarrow\infty.

(32)

Let us consider the system to be in the presence of a spatially uniform orbital magnetic field of strength
\boldsymbol{B}=B_{0}\hat{z}
in the out of plane direction. Note that an in-plane orbital magnetic field does not couple to the bosons as the spatial motion is constrained to the two-dimensional plane of the system. The corresponding vector potential is given by
\boldsymbol{A}(\boldsymbol{x})=\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{B}\times\boldsymbol{x}
, with
\boldsymbol{x}=(x,y,0)
. Under an in-plane translation by
\boldsymbol{a}
, the vector potential transforms as

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{A}(\boldsymbol{x})\to\boldsymbol{A}(\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{a})

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{A}(\boldsymbol{x})-\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{B}\times\boldsymbol{a}

(33)

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{A}(\boldsymbol{x})-\frac{1}{2}\nabla\left[\boldsymbol{a}\cdot(\boldsymbol{x}\times\boldsymbol{B})\right]

(34)

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{A}(\boldsymbol{x})+\nabla\chi_{\boldsymbol{a}}(\boldsymbol{x}),

(35)

where
\chi_{\boldsymbol{a}}(\boldsymbol{x})=-\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{a}\cdot(\boldsymbol{x}\times\boldsymbol{B})
. Note that the triplet pairing field
\boldsymbol{d}
is a charge-
2e
bosonic field, while the magnetization field
\boldsymbol{N}
is neutral. Therefore, under simultaneous gauge transformation and displacement by
\boldsymbol{a}
in the presence of a magnetic field, the fields transform as

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{d}(\boldsymbol{x})

\displaystyle\rightarrow e^{i\frac{2e}{\hbar c}\chi_{a}(\boldsymbol{x})}\boldsymbol{d}(\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{a}),

(36)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{N}(\boldsymbol{x})

\displaystyle\rightarrow\boldsymbol{N}(\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{a}),

(37)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{A}(\boldsymbol{x})

\displaystyle\rightarrow\boldsymbol{A}(\boldsymbol{x}).

(38)

As a result of gauge covariance and translational symmetry, the four-body density matrix obeys

\displaystyle\rho(\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\boldsymbol{x}_{2},\boldsymbol{x}_{1}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{x}_{2}^{\prime})=e^{i\frac{2e}{\hbar c}\left(\chi_{\boldsymbol{a}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{2}^{\prime})-\chi_{\boldsymbol{a}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{2})\right)}\rho(\boldsymbol{x}_{1}-\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{x}_{2}-\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{x}_{1}^{\prime}-\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{x}_{2}^{\prime}-\boldsymbol{a}).

(39)

Now suppose the system has ODLRO, i.e., Eq. (
32
) holds. In combination with Eq. (
39
), this implies

\displaystyle\phi_{0}^{*}(\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\boldsymbol{x}_{2})\phi_{0}(\boldsymbol{x}_{1}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{x}_{2}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=e^{i\frac{2e}{\hbar c}\left(\chi_{\boldsymbol{a}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{2}^{\prime})-\chi_{\boldsymbol{a}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{2})\right)}\phi_{0}^{*}(\boldsymbol{x}_{1}-\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{x}_{2}-\boldsymbol{a})\phi_{0}(\boldsymbol{x}_{1}^{\prime}-\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{x}_{2}^{\prime}-\boldsymbol{a})

(40)

\displaystyle\Longrightarrow\phi_{0}(\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\boldsymbol{x}_{2})

\displaystyle=f_{\boldsymbol{a}}e^{i\frac{2e}{\hbar c}\chi_{\boldsymbol{a}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{2})}\phi_{0}(\boldsymbol{x}_{1}-\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{x}_{2}-\boldsymbol{a}),

(41)

where
f_{\boldsymbol{a}}
is a position-independent phase factor. Now suppose we perform two different translations by
\boldsymbol{a}
and
\boldsymbol{b}
. We can perform
\boldsymbol{a}
first and then
\boldsymbol{b}
. Alternatively, we can do
\boldsymbol{b}
first and then
\boldsymbol{a}
. They respectively give us

\displaystyle\phi_{0}(\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\boldsymbol{x}_{2})

\displaystyle=f_{\boldsymbol{b}}f_{\boldsymbol{a}}e^{i\frac{2e}{\hbar c}\chi_{\boldsymbol{a}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{2})}e^{i\frac{2e}{\hbar c}\chi_{\boldsymbol{b}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{2}-\boldsymbol{a})}\phi_{0}(\boldsymbol{x}_{1}-\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{x}_{2}-\boldsymbol{a}),

(42)

\displaystyle\phi_{0}(\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\boldsymbol{x}_{2})

\displaystyle=f_{\boldsymbol{b}}f_{\boldsymbol{a}}e^{i\frac{2e}{\hbar c}\chi_{\boldsymbol{b}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{2})}e^{i\frac{2e}{\hbar c}\chi_{\boldsymbol{a}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{2}-\boldsymbol{b})}\phi_{0}(\boldsymbol{x}_{1}-\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{x}_{2}-\boldsymbol{a}).

(43)

This is only consistent if

\displaystyle e^{i\frac{2e}{\hbar c}\left(\chi_{\boldsymbol{b}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{2})+\chi_{\boldsymbol{a}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{2}-\boldsymbol{b})-\chi_{\boldsymbol{a}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{2})-\chi_{\boldsymbol{b}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{2}-\boldsymbol{a})\right)}=1.

(44)

We can evaluate
\chi_{\boldsymbol{b}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{2})+\chi_{\boldsymbol{a}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{2}-\boldsymbol{b})-\chi_{\boldsymbol{a}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{2})-\chi_{\boldsymbol{b}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{2}-\boldsymbol{a})=\boldsymbol{B}\cdot\left(\boldsymbol{a}\times\boldsymbol{b}\right)
. Thus, the condition for equality of phases becomes

\displaystyle\frac{2e}{\hbar c}\boldsymbol{B}\cdot\left(\boldsymbol{a}\times\boldsymbol{b}\right)=2\pi n,

(45)

for some integer
n
. The only solution for arbitrary
\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{b}
is thus
\boldsymbol{B}=0
.

Appendix F
Demonstration of Off Diagonal Long Range Order

In this section, we calculate the ODLRO wavefunctions for both the bosons and fermions. The idea is to calculate the
4-
body correlator
\langle\boldsymbol{N}(\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}_{1})\cdot\boldsymbol{d}(\boldsymbol{x}_{2}^{\prime})^{*}\boldsymbol{N}(\boldsymbol{x}_{1})\cdot\boldsymbol{d}(\boldsymbol{x}_{2})\rangle
for the bosons and
\langle c^{\dagger}_{\tau_{1}^{\prime}s_{1}^{\prime}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{1}^{\prime})c^{\dagger}_{\tau_{2}^{\prime}s_{2}^{\prime}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{2}^{\prime})c_{\tau_{1},s_{1}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{1})c_{\tau_{2},s_{2}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{2})\rangle
for the fermions. Due to the
U(1)
symmetry breaking mediated by
\boldsymbol{N}\cdot\boldsymbol{d}
attaining a finite expectation value (and correspondingly
c^{\dagger}_{\tau}c^{\dagger}_{-\tau}
for the fermions), the ODLRO factorizes into a product of functions of
\boldsymbol{x}_{1}-\boldsymbol{x}_{2}
and
\boldsymbol{x}_{1}^{\prime}-\boldsymbol{x}_{2}^{\prime}
in the limit
\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}\to\infty
, where
\boldsymbol{x}=\frac{\boldsymbol{x}_{1}+\boldsymbol{x}_{2}}{2}
and
\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}=\frac{\boldsymbol{x}_{1}^{\prime}+\boldsymbol{x}_{2}^{\prime}}{2}
, giving rise to ODLRO.
These wavefunctions decay as a function of their respective relative coordinates
\boldsymbol{x}_{1}-\boldsymbol{x}_{2}
and
\boldsymbol{x}_{1}^{\prime}-\boldsymbol{x}_{2}^{\prime}
. We now calculate these “macroscopic wavefunctions” explicitly for the bosonic and fermionic cases.

F.1
Bosonic ODLRO

The bosonic ODLRO is given by
\langle\boldsymbol{N}(\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}_{1})\cdot\boldsymbol{d}(\boldsymbol{x}_{2}^{\prime})^{*}\boldsymbol{N}(\boldsymbol{x}_{1})\cdot\boldsymbol{d}(\boldsymbol{x}_{2})\rangle\simeq\langle\boldsymbol{N}(\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}_{1})\cdot\boldsymbol{d}(\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}_{2})^{*}\rangle\langle\boldsymbol{N}(\boldsymbol{x}_{1})\cdot\boldsymbol{d}(\boldsymbol{x}_{2})\rangle
as
\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}\to\infty
. All the correlators are evaluated at time
t=0
. As discussed in the main text, to demonstrate ODLRO, it is sufficient to evaluate these correlators to first non-trivial order in the coupling constants. For bosonic ODLRO it is in fact sufficient to focus on zeroth order, i.e., neglecting the coupling to the fermions. Using the translation invariance of the system (and summing over the Matsubara frequencies
i\Omega
since we are evaluating the correlator at time
t=0
), we then have

\displaystyle\psi_{B}(\boldsymbol{x})

\displaystyle=\langle\boldsymbol{N}(\boldsymbol{x})\cdot\boldsymbol{d}(\boldsymbol{x}=0)\rangle=\int_{\boldsymbol{q}}T\sum_{i\Omega}e^{i\boldsymbol{q}\cdot\boldsymbol{x}}\langle\boldsymbol{N}_{-q}\cdot\boldsymbol{d}_{q}\rangle

(46)

\displaystyle=\int_{\boldsymbol{q}}T\sum_{i\Omega}e^{i\boldsymbol{q}\cdot r}\frac{\phi_{0}}{[(i\Omega)^{2}-E_{+}^{2}(\boldsymbol{q})][(i\Omega)^{2}-E_{-}^{2}(\boldsymbol{q})]}

(47)

\displaystyle=\int_{\boldsymbol{q}}e^{i\boldsymbol{q}\cdot\boldsymbol{x}}\frac{\phi_{0}}{2E_{+}(\boldsymbol{q})E_{-}(\boldsymbol{q})(E_{+}(\boldsymbol{q})+E_{-}(\boldsymbol{q}))}

(48)

\displaystyle\simeq\int_{\boldsymbol{q}}e^{i\boldsymbol{q}\cdot\boldsymbol{x}}\frac{\phi_{0}}{a+b\boldsymbol{q}^{2}}

(49)

\displaystyle=\frac{\phi_{0}}{b}\int_{\boldsymbol{q}}e^{i\boldsymbol{q}\cdot\sqrt{\frac{a}{b}}\boldsymbol{x}}\frac{1}{1+\boldsymbol{q}^{2}}=2\pi\phi_{0}K_{0}\left(\sqrt{\frac{a}{b}}|\boldsymbol{x}|\right)/b

(50)

\displaystyle=2\pi\phi_{0}K_{0}\left(|\boldsymbol{x}|/\xi\right)/b,

(51)

where
K_{0}
is the zeroth modified Bessel function of second kind. In the third line, we evaluated the Matsubara sum at
T=0
, and in the fourth line we series expanded
2E_{+}(\boldsymbol{q})E_{-}(\boldsymbol{q})\left(E_{+}(\boldsymbol{q})+E_{-}(\boldsymbol{q})\right)
about
\boldsymbol{q}=0
up to quadratic order. The length scale
\xi=\sqrt{\frac{b}{a}}
is determined by
r_{\mu},v_{\mu}
. In the limit of
|v_{N}-v_{d}|\ll v_{N}+v_{d}
, we get

\displaystyle\xi=\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\frac{({v_{N}^{2}}+{v_{d}^{2}})\left(\sqrt{{r_{N}}{r_{d}}-\phi_{0}^{2}}+{r_{N}}+{r_{d}}\right)}{{r_{N}}{r_{d}}-\phi_{0}^{2}}}.

(52)

In Fig.
4
(b), we plot the numerical ODLRO wavefunction
\psi_{B}(\boldsymbol{x})
with the full functional dependence on
\boldsymbol{q}
in Eq. (
48
) included, and compare it with the asymptotic analytical form in Eq. (
51
). We find good agreement between the numerical and analytical results.

F.2
Fermionic ODLRO

Similarly, we can find the fermionic ODLRO, which in real space is generically written as
\langle c^{\dagger}_{\tau_{1}^{\prime}s_{1}^{\prime}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{1}^{\prime})c^{\dagger}_{\tau_{2}^{\prime}s_{2}^{\prime}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{2}^{\prime})c_{\tau_{1},s_{1}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{1})c_{\tau_{2},s_{2}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{2})\rangle\sim\langle c^{\dagger}_{\tau_{1}^{\prime}s_{1}^{\prime}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{1}^{\prime})c^{\dagger}_{\tau_{2}^{\prime}s_{2}^{\prime}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{2}^{\prime})\rangle\langle c_{\tau_{1},s_{1}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{1})c_{\tau_{2},s_{2}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{2})\rangle
in the limit
\boldsymbol{x}_{j}-\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}_{j}\to\infty
. Here,
\tau,s
are the valley and spin indices respectively. To demonstrate ODLRO, we thus have to evaluate the
2-
fermion correlators, which in momentum space becomes

\left(\Psi^{*}_{\text{F}}(\boldsymbol{x})\right)_{s_{1},s_{2}}=\langle c^{\dagger}_{\tau_{1},s_{1}}(\boldsymbol{x},t=0)c^{\dagger}_{\tau_{2},s_{2}}(\boldsymbol{x}=0,t=0)\rangle=\int_{k}e^{i\boldsymbol{k}\cdot\boldsymbol{x}}\langle c^{\dagger}_{k,\tau_{1},s_{1}}c^{\dagger}_{-k,\tau_{2},s_{2}}\rangle.

(53)

Since the superconducting pairing takes place only between electrons between opposite valleys, we will have only
\tau_{2}=-\tau_{1}
giving non-zero correlators. Without loss of generality we chose
\tau_{1}=+,\tau_{2}=-
.
Up to first order in
\phi_{0}
, we have

\displaystyle\langle c^{\dagger}_{k,+,s_{1}}c^{\dagger}_{-k,-,s_{2}}\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle c^{\dagger}_{k,+,s_{1}}c^{\dagger}_{-k,-,s_{2}}\left(-\int_{q}\frac{1}{2}\frac{\phi_{0}\lambda^{2}}{M_{q}}\mathbf{S}_{q}\cdot\mathbf{D}_{q}^{\dagger}\right)\rangle_{0},

(54)

where
\langle...\rangle
is the average with respect to the interacting and
\langle...\rangle_{0}
with respect to the non-interacting ground state. We define
G(k)=\delta_{ss^{\prime}}\delta_{\tau\tau^{\prime}}G_{V,k}=\frac{\delta_{ss^{\prime}}\delta_{\tau\tau^{\prime}}}{i\omega_{n}-\epsilon_{\boldsymbol{k}}}=-\langle c_{s,\tau}c^{\dagger}_{s^{\prime},\tau^{\prime}}\rangle
to be the Green’s function in the fermionic basis (assuming
\epsilon_{\boldsymbol{k}}=\epsilon_{-\boldsymbol{k}}
). Equation (
54
) can then be evaluated as,

\displaystyle-\frac{\phi_{0}\lambda^{2}}{2}

\displaystyle\int_{q}\frac{1}{M_{q}}\langle c^{\dagger}_{k,+,s_{1}}c^{\dagger}_{-k,-,s_{2}}\left(\mathbf{S}_{q}\cdot\mathbf{D}_{q}^{\dagger}\right)\rangle_{0}

(55)

\displaystyle=-\frac{\phi_{0}\lambda^{2}}{2}\int_{q}\frac{1}{M_{q}}\langle c_{k+,s_{1}}^{\dagger}c_{-k-,s_{2}}^{\dagger}\left(\sum_{k_{1},k_{2},p1=\pm,p_{2}=\pm}p_{1}p_{2}\left(c^{\dagger}_{k_{1}+q,p_{1}}\mathbf{s}c_{k_{1},p_{1}}\right)\cdot\left(c_{k_{2}+q,p_{2}}is_{y}\mathbf{s}c_{-k_{2},-p_{2}}\right)\right)\rangle_{0}

(56)

\displaystyle=-2\frac{\phi_{0}\lambda^{2}}{2}\int_{q}\frac{1}{M_{q}}\langle c_{k+,s_{1}}^{\dagger}c_{-k-,s_{2}}^{\dagger}\left(\sum_{k_{1},p}-\left(c_{-k_{1},-p}is_{y}\mathbf{s}(-G_{V,k_{1}+q,p})\mathbf{s}c_{k_{1},p}\right)\right)\rangle_{0}

(57)

\displaystyle=-6\frac{\phi_{0}\lambda^{2}}{2}\int_{q}\frac{1}{M_{q}}\langle c_{k+,s_{1}}^{\dagger}c_{-k-,s_{2}}^{\dagger}\left(\sum_{k_{1},p}\left(c_{-k_{1},-p}is_{y}G_{V,k_{1}+q,p}c_{k_{1},p}\right)\right)\rangle_{0}

(58)

\displaystyle=-6\frac{\phi_{0}\lambda^{2}}{2}\int_{q}\frac{1}{M_{q}}\langle c_{k+,s_{1}}^{\dagger}c_{-k-,s_{2}}^{\dagger}\left(\sum_{k_{1}}\left(c_{-k_{1},-}is_{y}G_{V,k_{1}+q,+}c_{k_{1},+}+c_{-k_{1},+}is_{y}G_{V,k_{1}+q,-}c_{k_{1},-}\right)\right)\rangle_{0}

(59)

\displaystyle=-6\frac{\phi_{0}\lambda^{2}}{2}\int_{q}\frac{1}{M_{q}}\left(-(-G_{V,-k,-})(is_{y})_{s_{2}s_{1}}G_{V,k+q,+}G_{V,k,+}+(-G_{V,k,+})(is_{y})_{s_{1}s_{2}}G_{V,-k+q,-}G_{V,-k,-}\right)

(60)

\displaystyle=-6\frac{\phi_{0}\lambda^{2}}{2}\int_{q}\frac{1}{M_{q}}G_{V,k}G_{V,-k}\left(G_{V,-k+q}+G_{V,k+q}\right)(is_{y})_{s_{2}s_{1}}.

(61)

We continue by calculating the Matsubara sum over
i\Omega_{n}
and over
i\omega_{n}
[see Eq. (
53
)],

\displaystyle T^{2}\sum_{i\omega_{n},i\Omega_{n}}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(i\Omega_{n}^{2}-E_{+}(\boldsymbol{q})^{2})(i\Omega_{n}^{2}-E_{-}(\boldsymbol{q})^{2})}G_{V,k}G_{V,-k}\left(G_{V,-k+q}+G_{V,k+q}\right)

(62)

\displaystyle=-T^{2}\sum_{i\omega_{n},i\Omega}\frac{1}{(i\omega_{n})^{2}-\epsilon_{\boldsymbol{k}}^{2}}\frac{1}{((i\Omega_{n})^{2}-E_{+}(\boldsymbol{q})^{2})((i\Omega_{n})^{2}-E_{-}(\boldsymbol{q})^{2})}\left(\frac{1}{i\omega_{n}+i\Omega_{n}-\epsilon_{-\boldsymbol{k}+\boldsymbol{q}}}+\frac{1}{-i\omega_{n}+i\Omega_{n}-\epsilon_{\boldsymbol{k}+\boldsymbol{q}}}\right)

(63)

\displaystyle=:X(\epsilon_{\boldsymbol{k}},\boldsymbol{q}).

(64)

For simplicity, we here focus on the limit where the remaining sum over
\boldsymbol{q}
in Eq. (
54
) is determined by its
\boldsymbol{q}=0
component. With
E_{\pm}\equiv E_{\pm}(\boldsymbol{q}=0)
and
v_{N},r_{N}=1
, we have

\displaystyle\hat{X}(\epsilon)\equiv X(\epsilon,\boldsymbol{q}\to 0)

(65)

\displaystyle=\frac{n_{f}(\epsilon)^{2}}{2\epsilon}\left(-2\frac{e^{\beta\epsilon}}{E_{+}^{2}E_{-}^{2}}+\frac{2}{\left(E_{+}^{2}-4\epsilon^{2}\right)\left(E_{-}^{2}-4\epsilon^{2}\right)}+\left(\frac{2\epsilon n_{B}(E_{+})-E_{+}n_{f}(E_{+})}{E_{+}(E_{+}^{2}-E_{-}^{2})(E_{+}^{2}-4\epsilon^{2})n_{f}(E_{+})n_{B}(2\epsilon)}+E_{+}\leftrightarrow E_{-}\right)\right),

(66)

we can then finally write

\displaystyle\Psi^{*}_{\text{F}}(\boldsymbol{x})

\displaystyle=3|\phi_{0}|\lambda^{2}s_{y}\left(\frac{1}{V}\sum_{\boldsymbol{k}}e^{i{\boldsymbol{k}}\cdot\boldsymbol{x}}\hat{X}(\epsilon_{\boldsymbol{k}})\right),

(67)

\displaystyle=\frac{3|\phi_{0}|\lambda^{2}s_{y}}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}dkkJ_{0}({\boldsymbol{k}}\cdot\boldsymbol{x})\hat{X}(\hbar^{2}({\boldsymbol{k}}^{2}-\boldsymbol{k}_{F}^{2})/(2m)).

(68)

In the second line, we assumed
\epsilon_{\boldsymbol{k}}=\hbar^{2}({\boldsymbol{k}}^{2}-{\boldsymbol{k}}_{F}^{2})/2m
.
Using this expression, we calculate the spatial profile of the fermionic ODLRO wavefunction numerically for various values of
\epsilon_{F}\equiv\epsilon_{\boldsymbol{k}_{F}}
in Fig.
4
(a). Unlike the case of the bosonic ODLRO (which was exponentially decaying), the fermionic ODLRO has an oscillating component superimposed on an exponentially decaying envelope.

Appendix G
Ginzburg-Landau theory

We here calculate the Landau-Ginzburg theory for the bosonic superfluid condensate parameter to leading (zeroth) order in the fermion-boson coupling
\lambda
. To tis end, we assume that
\phi_{0}
is now spatially and temporally varying. This results in non-zero Fourier modes
\phi_{q}
for
\boldsymbol{q},i\Omega\neq 0
.

In momentum space, the bosonic action is generalized according to

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{B}

\displaystyle=\int_{q}[\chi_{N}^{-1}(q)\boldsymbol{N}_{q}\cdot\boldsymbol{N}_{-q}+\chi_{SC}^{-1}(q)\boldsymbol{d}_{q}^{*}\cdot\boldsymbol{d}_{q}+(\phi_{0}\boldsymbol{d}_{q}\cdot\boldsymbol{N}_{-q}+\text{H.c.})]

(69)

\displaystyle=\int_{q}\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{N}_{-q}^{T}&{\boldsymbol{d}_{q}^{\dagger}}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\chi_{N}^{-1}(q)&\phi_{0}\\
\phi_{0}&\chi_{d}^{-1}(q)\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{N}_{q}\\
\boldsymbol{d}_{q}\end{pmatrix}

(70)

\displaystyle\to\int_{q,k}\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{N}_{-q-q_{2}}^{T}&{\boldsymbol{d}_{q+q_{2}}^{\dagger}}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\chi_{N}^{-1}(q)\delta_{q_{2}=0}&\phi_{q_{2}}\\
\phi_{-q_{2}}^{*}&\chi_{d}^{-1}(q)\delta_{q_{2}=0}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{N}_{q}\\
\boldsymbol{d}_{q}\end{pmatrix}

(71)

So after integrating out
\boldsymbol{d}
and
\boldsymbol{N}
, the effective action for
\phi
reads as

\mathcal{S}_{\text{eff}}=\frac{1}{2}Tr\ln G^{-1}[\phi],

(72)

where

\displaystyle G^{-1}[\phi](q+q_{1},q)

\displaystyle=G_{0}^{-1}(q)\delta_{q_{1},0}+\Gamma_{q+q_{1},q}

(73)

\displaystyle G_{0}^{-1}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}\chi_{N}^{-1}(q)&0\\
0&\chi_{d}^{-1}(q)\end{pmatrix}

(74)

\displaystyle\Gamma_{q+q_{1},q}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}0&\phi_{q_{1}}\\
\phi_{-q_{1}}^{*}&0\end{pmatrix}.

(75)

To derive the Ginzburg-Landau theory for
\phi
, we expand
Tr\ln G^{-1}
upto second order in
\Gamma
, which is equivalent to second order in
\phi
. This gives us

\displaystyle S_{\text{GL}}=Tr\ln(G_{0}^{-1}+\Gamma)\simeq TrG_{0}^{-1}+TrG_{0}\Gamma-\frac{1}{2}TrG_{0}\Gamma G_{0}\Gamma

(76)

Because of the diagonal structure of
G_{0}
, and the off diagonal structure of
\Gamma
, the linear term
TrG_{0}\Gamma
is
0
. The quadratic term becomes

\displaystyle\sum_{q^{\prime},q}TrG_{0}(q^{\prime}+q)\Gamma(q^{\prime}+q,q^{\prime})G_{0}(q^{\prime})\Gamma(q^{\prime},q^{\prime}+q)

\displaystyle=\sum_{q^{\prime},q}Tr\begin{pmatrix}0&\chi_{N}(q^{\prime}+q)\phi_{q}\\
\chi_{d}(q^{\prime}+q)\phi_{-q}^{*}&0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}0&\chi_{N}(q^{\prime})\phi_{-q}\\
\chi_{d}(q^{\prime})\phi_{q}^{*}&0\end{pmatrix}

(77)

\displaystyle=\sum_{q^{\prime},q}\chi_{N}(q^{\prime}+q)\chi_{d}(q^{\prime})\phi_{q}\phi_{q}^{*}+\chi_{N}(q^{\prime})\chi_{d}(q^{\prime}+q)\phi_{-q}\phi_{-q}^{*}

(78)

\displaystyle=\sum_{q^{\prime},q}\left(\chi_{N}(q^{\prime}+q)\chi_{d}(q^{\prime})+\chi_{N}(q^{\prime})\chi_{d}(q^{\prime}-q)\right)\phi_{q}\phi_{q}^{*}

(79)

\displaystyle=\sum_{q^{\prime},q}\left(\chi_{N}(q^{\prime}+q)\chi_{d}(q^{\prime})+\chi_{N}(q^{\prime}+q)\chi_{d}(q^{\prime})\right)\phi_{q}\phi_{q}^{*}

(80)

\displaystyle=2\sum_{q^{\prime},q}\chi_{N}(q^{\prime}+q)\chi_{d}(q^{\prime})\phi_{q}\phi_{q}^{*}

(81)

We need to evaluate

\displaystyle\sum_{q^{\prime}}\chi_{N}(q^{\prime}+q)\chi_{d}(q^{\prime})=\int_{\boldsymbol{q}^{\prime}}T\sum_{i\Omega^{\prime}\in\text{Bosonic}}\left(\frac{1}{((i\Omega^{\prime}+i\Omega)^{2}-r_{N}-v_{N}^{2}(\boldsymbol{q}^{\prime}+\boldsymbol{q})^{2})((i\Omega^{\prime})^{2}-r_{d}-v_{d}^{2}\boldsymbol{q}^{\prime 2})}\right)

(82)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\int_{\boldsymbol{q}^{\prime}}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{r_{N}+v_{N}^{2}({\boldsymbol{q}^{\prime}}+{\boldsymbol{q}}/2)^{2}}}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{r_{d}+v_{d}^{2}({\boldsymbol{q}^{\prime}}-{\boldsymbol{q}}/2)^{2}}}\right)\left(\frac{1}{i\Omega^{2}-\left(\sqrt{r_{N}+v_{N}^{2}({\boldsymbol{q}^{\prime}}+{\boldsymbol{q}}/2)^{2}}+\sqrt{r_{d}+v_{d}^{2}({\boldsymbol{q}^{\prime}}-{\boldsymbol{q}}/2)^{2}}\right)^{2}}\right).

(83)

By expanding the above expression up to second order in
i\Omega
,
\boldsymbol{q}
, we find the effective action for the
\phi
field to be

\displaystyle T\sum_{i\Omega,\boldsymbol{q}}\left(r_{\phi}-\rho(i\Omega)^{2}+v^{2}\boldsymbol{q}^{2}\right)|\phi_{(\boldsymbol{q},i\Omega)}|^{2}

(84)

where the coefficients are given by

\displaystyle r_{\phi}

\displaystyle=-\int_{\boldsymbol{q}^{\prime}}\frac{\pi}{\sqrt{g_{d}}\sqrt{g_{N}}\left(\sqrt{g_{d}}+\sqrt{g_{N}}\right)}

(85)

\displaystyle\rho

\displaystyle=\int_{\boldsymbol{q}^{\prime}}\frac{\pi}{\sqrt{g_{d}}\sqrt{g_{N}}\left(\sqrt{g_{d}}+\sqrt{g_{N}}\right)^{3}}

(86)

\displaystyle v^{2}

\displaystyle=\int_{\boldsymbol{q}^{\prime}}\frac{\pi\left(4\boldsymbol{q}^{\prime 2}(\sqrt{g_{d}}+\sqrt{g_{N}})\left(\frac{v_{d}^{2}}{g_{d}^{3/2}}-\frac{v_{N}^{2}}{g_{N}^{3/2}}\right)\left(\frac{v_{N}^{2}}{\sqrt{g_{N}}}-\frac{v_{d}^{2}}{\sqrt{g_{d}}}\right)-\left(\sqrt{g_{d}}+\sqrt{g_{N}}\right)^{2}\left(\frac{v_{d}^{2}\left(3v_{d}^{2}\boldsymbol{q}^{\prime 2}-2g_{d}\right)}{g_{d}^{5/2}}+\frac{v_{N}^{2}\left(3v_{N}^{2}\boldsymbol{q}^{\prime 2}-2g_{N}\right)}{g_{N}^{5/2}}\right)\right)}{16\left(\sqrt{g_{d}}+\sqrt{g_{N}}\right)^{4}}

(87)

\displaystyle-\frac{2\pi\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{g_{d}}}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{g_{N}}}\right)\left(\frac{3\boldsymbol{q}^{\prime 2}\left(\sqrt{g_{N}}v_{d}^{2}-\sqrt{g_{d}}v_{N}^{2}\right)^{2}}{g_{d}g_{N}}-\left(\sqrt{g_{d}}+\sqrt{g_{N}}\right)\left(\frac{v_{d}^{2}\left(2g_{d}-\boldsymbol{q}^{\prime 2}v_{d}^{2}\right)}{g_{d}^{3/2}}+\frac{v_{N}^{2}\left(2g_{N}-\boldsymbol{q}^{\prime 2}v_{N}^{2}\right)}{g_{N}^{3/2}}\right)\right)}{16\left(\sqrt{g_{d}}+\sqrt{g_{N}}\right)^{4}}

(88)

with
g_{\mu}=r_{\mu}+v_{\mu}^{2}\boldsymbol{q}^{\prime 2}
. We numerically calculate the quantities
r_{\phi},\rho,v^{2}
and plot it in Fig.
4
(c,d) of the main text.

Appendix H
Self-consistent equations in special limits

In this appendix, we complement the previous analysis by studying two simple limits of the model for phase (B)—mean-field theory and the limit of zero energy-momentum transfer of the bosons. This allows us to study possible non-perturbative solutions systematically. In both cases, we find that the soft gap behavior obtained within perturbation theory is also found in these descriptions as long as
T
is large enough/the coupling constants,
\lambda
or
\phi_{0}
, are small enough.

H.1
Mean-field Theory

In this section, we consider the effective interaction contributed by the
\mathcal{S}_{2}
part of the action between the electrons at time
t=0
, in the limit where we replace the
q
integral with the corresponding value of the integrand at
q=0
, and then perform a mean-field decomposition of the interaction. Defining the Bogoliubov-de Gennes basis as before,
\xi_{k}=\begin{pmatrix}c_{k,+}&is_{y}c_{-k,-}^{\dagger}\end{pmatrix}^{T}
, with Pauli matrix
\gamma_{i}
acting on it, and
\tilde{\phi}_{0}=\phi_{0}\lambda^{2}r_{N}/v_{N}^{2}
the corresponding interaction potential is given by

\displaystyle V

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\frac{1}{\chi_{d}^{-1}\chi_{N}^{-1}-|\phi_{0}|^{2}}\left(\tilde{\phi}_{0}\mathbf{S}_{q=0}\cdot\mathbf{D}_{q=0}^{\dagger}+\tilde{\phi}_{0}^{*}\mathbf{D}_{q=0}\cdot\mathbf{S}_{-q=0}\right)|_{q=0}

(89)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\frac{1}{r_{N}r_{d}-|\phi_{0}|^{2}}\int_{\boldsymbol{k}_{1},\boldsymbol{k}_{2}}\left[-\tilde{\phi}_{0}\left(c^{\dagger}_{\boldsymbol{k}_{1}}\mathbf{s}\tau_{z}c_{\boldsymbol{k}_{1}}\right)\cdot\left(c_{\boldsymbol{k}_{2}}\mathbf{s}is_{y}\tau_{y}c_{-\boldsymbol{k}_{2}}\right)+h.c\right]

(90)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{r_{N}r_{d}-|\phi_{0}|^{2}}\int_{\boldsymbol{k}_{1},\boldsymbol{k}_{2}}\left[\tilde{\phi}_{0}\left(\xi^{\dagger}_{\boldsymbol{k}_{1}}\mathbf{s}\gamma_{z}\xi_{\boldsymbol{k}_{1}}\right)\cdot\left(\xi_{\boldsymbol{k}_{2}}^{\dagger}\mathbf{s}i\gamma_{-}\xi_{\boldsymbol{k}_{2}}\right)+h.c\right],

(91)

while the free Hamiltonian is given by

\displaystyle H_{0}=\int_{\boldsymbol{k}}\xi_{\boldsymbol{k}}^{\dagger}\epsilon_{\boldsymbol{k}}\gamma_{z}\xi_{\boldsymbol{k}}.

(92)

We consider only the effective Hamiltonian at time
t=0
, which is why there are no Matsuabra indices.

We perform a Hartree-Fock decomposition of
V
, which gives us

\displaystyle V

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{r_{N}r_{d}-|\phi_{0}|^{2}}\int_{\boldsymbol{k}_{1},\boldsymbol{k}_{2}}\left[\tilde{\phi}_{0}\left(\xi^{\dagger}_{\boldsymbol{k}_{1}}\mathbf{s}\gamma_{z}\xi_{\boldsymbol{k}_{1}}\right)\cdot\left(\xi_{\boldsymbol{k}_{2}}^{\dagger}\mathbf{s}i\gamma_{-}\xi_{\boldsymbol{k}_{2}}\right)+h.c\right]

(93)

\displaystyle\to\frac{c}{2}\int_{\boldsymbol{k}}\xi^{\dagger}_{\boldsymbol{k}}\left(\gamma_{y}C_{\boldsymbol{k}}\gamma_{z}+\gamma_{z}C_{\boldsymbol{k}}\gamma_{y}\right)\xi_{\boldsymbol{k}},

(94)

where
C_{\boldsymbol{k}}=-\langle\xi_{\boldsymbol{k}}\xi_{\boldsymbol{k}}^{\dagger}\rangle
,
c=6\frac{\tilde{\phi}_{0}}{r_{N}r_{d}-\phi_{0}^{2}}
, choosing a gauge with real
\phi_{0}
; further take
\phi_{0}
to be positive such that
c>0
. Note that this correlator is related to the Green’s function
G
by
C_{\boldsymbol{k}}=T\sum_{i\omega_{n}}G(k)
. Note that all the Hartree terms vanish since we do not allow for spontaneous breaking of spin-rotation invariance (recall we study finite
T
in 2D). The effective
2-
particle Hamiltonian is given by

\displaystyle H

\displaystyle=\int_{\boldsymbol{k}}\xi_{\boldsymbol{k}}^{\dagger}\left(\epsilon_{\boldsymbol{k}}\gamma_{z}+\frac{c}{2}\gamma_{y}C_{\boldsymbol{k}}\gamma_{z}+\frac{c}{2}\gamma_{z}C_{\boldsymbol{k}}\gamma_{y}\right)\xi_{\boldsymbol{k}}

(95)

\displaystyle=\int_{\boldsymbol{k}}\xi_{\boldsymbol{k}}^{\dagger}\left[\tilde{\epsilon}_{\boldsymbol{k}}\gamma_{z}+\tilde{\Delta}_{\boldsymbol{k}}\gamma_{y}\right]\xi_{\boldsymbol{k}}

(96)

where
\tilde{\epsilon}_{\boldsymbol{k}},\tilde{\Delta}_{\boldsymbol{k}}
are the self consistent band structure and gap. Making connection with the diagrammatic self consistency relationship to be discussed below, we can foresee that the resulting self consistent equation we get will be the same as (
106
) but with
\tilde{\epsilon},\tilde{\Delta}
replaced with the corresponding
i\omega_{n}
averaged value, and the whole equation itself will be
i\omega_{n}
averaged.

The correlators in terms of
\tilde{\epsilon},\tilde{\Delta}
are given by

\displaystyle C_{\boldsymbol{k}}=T\sum_{i\omega_{n}}\frac{1}{i\omega_{n}-\left[\tilde{\epsilon}_{\boldsymbol{k}}\gamma_{z}+\tilde{\Delta}_{\boldsymbol{k}}\gamma_{y}\right]}=\frac{n_{f}(E_{\boldsymbol{k}})-n_{f}(-E_{\boldsymbol{k}})}{2E_{\boldsymbol{k}}}\left[\tilde{\epsilon}_{\boldsymbol{k}}\gamma_{z}+\tilde{\Delta}_{\boldsymbol{k}}\gamma_{y}\right],

(97)

where
E_{\boldsymbol{k}}=\sqrt{\tilde{\epsilon}_{\boldsymbol{k}}^{2}+\tilde{\Delta}_{\boldsymbol{k}}^{2}}>0
. Thus, using (
95
), the self consistency equations become

\displaystyle\tilde{\epsilon}_{\boldsymbol{k}}

\displaystyle=\epsilon_{\boldsymbol{k}}+c\tilde{\Delta}_{\boldsymbol{k}}\frac{n_{f}(E_{\boldsymbol{k}})-n_{f}(-E_{\boldsymbol{k}})}{2E_{\boldsymbol{k}}}

(98)

\displaystyle\tilde{\Delta}_{\boldsymbol{k}}

\displaystyle=c\tilde{\epsilon}_{\boldsymbol{k}}\frac{n_{f}(E_{\boldsymbol{k}})-n_{f}(-E_{\boldsymbol{k}})}{2E_{\boldsymbol{k}}}.

(99)

Let us define
\beta_{\boldsymbol{k}}=c\frac{n_{f}(-E_{\boldsymbol{k}})-n_{f}(E_{\boldsymbol{k}})}{2E_{\boldsymbol{k}}}=c\frac{\tanh\left(\frac{E_{\boldsymbol{k}}}{2T}\right)}{2E_{\boldsymbol{k}}}
and first assume
\beta_{\boldsymbol{k}}<1
, which always holds as long as
T>c/4
. The self consistency equations can then be rearranged as

\displaystyle\tilde{\epsilon}_{\boldsymbol{k}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{1-\beta_{\boldsymbol{k}}^{2}}\epsilon_{\boldsymbol{k}}

(100a)

\displaystyle\tilde{\Delta}_{\boldsymbol{k}}

\displaystyle=\frac{-\beta_{\boldsymbol{k}}}{1-\beta_{\boldsymbol{k}}^{2}}\epsilon_{\boldsymbol{k}}.

(100b)

Using this, we find
E_{\boldsymbol{k}}=\frac{\sqrt{1+\beta_{\boldsymbol{k}}^{2}}}{1-\beta_{\boldsymbol{k}}^{2}}\epsilon_{\boldsymbol{k}}
. Note, however, that
\beta_{\boldsymbol{k}}
also depends on
E_{\boldsymbol{k}}
and, thus, this relation should be thought of as a self consistency equation, to be solved for
\beta_{{\boldsymbol{k}}}
or
E_{\boldsymbol{k}}
.

Equations (
100
) allow to derive asymptotic relations. In the limit
\epsilon_{\boldsymbol{k}}\to 0
, we then have
E_{\boldsymbol{k}}\to 0
and
\beta_{\boldsymbol{k}}\to\frac{c}{4T}
, ensuring the self-consistent solutions are well controlled in the
\epsilon_{\boldsymbol{k}}\to 0
regime that we are interested in. Near
\epsilon_{\boldsymbol{k}}=0
and for large
T\gg c
(
\beta_{\boldsymbol{k}}\ll 1
), the renormalized spectrum is given by
E_{\boldsymbol{k}}=\frac{\sqrt{1+\beta_{\boldsymbol{k}}^{2}}}{1-\beta_{\boldsymbol{k}}^{2}}\epsilon_{\boldsymbol{k}}\simeq\sqrt{1+3\beta_{\boldsymbol{k}}^{2}}\epsilon_{\boldsymbol{k}}\simeq\sqrt{1+\frac{3c^{2}}{16T^{2}}}\epsilon_{\boldsymbol{k}}
. The suppression of DOS is now given by

\frac{\rho_{F}(\phi_{0})}{\rho_{F}(\phi_{0}=0)}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+\alpha^{\prime 2}}},\quad\alpha^{\prime}=\frac{3\sqrt{3}\phi_{0}\lambda^{2}r_{N}}{2v_{N}^{2}T(r_{d}r_{N}-\phi_{0}^{2})},

which is of the same form as Eq. (
4
), found through the perturbative calculation presented in the main text and derived in Sec.
C
.

When
T/c=1/4
, we have
\beta_{\boldsymbol{k}}^{2}=1
for
\epsilon_{\boldsymbol{k}}\rightarrow 0
, and Eq. (
100
) are not valid. At this point, the self consistent solutions open up a gap in
E_{\boldsymbol{k}}
when
\epsilon_{\boldsymbol{k}}=0
. This gap follows by solving the equation
\beta_{\boldsymbol{k}}^{2}=1
. When
\epsilon_{\boldsymbol{k}}=0
and
\beta_{\boldsymbol{k}}=1
, we also have
\tilde{\epsilon}_{\boldsymbol{k}}=-\tilde{\Delta}_{\boldsymbol{k}}
[see Eq. (
99
)] which gives
E_{\boldsymbol{k}}=\sqrt{2}\tilde{\epsilon}_{\boldsymbol{k}}
. For
T/c
approaching
1/4
from below, we find that
\beta_{\boldsymbol{k}}\simeq\frac{c}{4T}\left(1-\frac{1}{12}\frac{E_{\boldsymbol{k}}^{2}}{T^{2}}\right)
. Thus the condition that
\beta_{\boldsymbol{k}}^{2}=1
gives us
E_{\boldsymbol{k}}=\sqrt{12}T\sqrt{1-\frac{4T}{c}}
.

To summarize, for
T>c/4
, self consistent energy and gap (
\tilde{\epsilon},\tilde{\Delta}
) are proportional to
\epsilon
. As
T
approaches
c/4
from above, the slope of proportionality approaches
\infty
at
\epsilon=0
, and becomes non-analytic at
T=c/4
. Going below
T=c/4
, this non-analyticity at
\epsilon=0
turns into a discontinuity at
\epsilon=0
, with the self consistent solutions developing a finite gap. The value of this gap at
T=0
is given as
|\tilde{\Delta}|=|\tilde{\epsilon}|=\frac{|c|}{2\sqrt{2}}
. Figure
8
illustrates the behavior obtained by numerical solution of the self-consistency equations.

Figure 8
:
The self consistent solution for
\tilde{\epsilon}_{\boldsymbol{k}},\tilde{\Delta}_{\boldsymbol{k}}
and
E_{\boldsymbol{k}}
as a function of
\epsilon_{\boldsymbol{k}}
for various temperatures. At
T/c=1/4
, the self-consistent solutions become non-analytic having an infinite slope at
\epsilon_{\boldsymbol{k}}=0
, and a gap opens up as the temperature decreases. There is a discontinuity in
\tilde{\epsilon}_{\boldsymbol{k}},\tilde{\Delta}_{\boldsymbol{k}}
at
\epsilon_{\boldsymbol{k}}=0
, where the gap value has different signs for
\epsilon_{\boldsymbol{k}}\rightarrow 0^{-},0^{+}
.

H.2
Zero energy-momentum transfer

In this section, we consider the limit where the bosonic fields
\boldsymbol{N},\boldsymbol{d}
do not transfer any momentum or Matsubara frequency in the interaction (
q=0
in
\mathcal{S}_{c}
). Additionally, we consider only the effect of
\mathcal{S}_{2}
on the self energy to study the effect of the anomalous contribution. In this limit, we would like to analyze the self consistent solution of the Green’s function up to all orders in
\lambda
within the large-
N
theory of the main text. The ansatz of the full Green’s function is given by
G^{-1}=i\omega_{n}-\tilde{\epsilon}_{k}\gamma_{z}-\tilde{\Delta}_{k}\gamma_{y}
, since
\Sigma_{3}
renormalizes only the anomalous term
\tilde{\Delta}_{k}
and the spectrum
\tilde{\epsilon}_{k}
. We have

\displaystyle G=\frac{i\omega_{n}+\tilde{\epsilon}_{k}\gamma_{z}+\tilde{\Delta}_{k}\gamma_{y}}{(i\omega_{n})^{2}-\tilde{\epsilon}_{k}^{2}-\tilde{\Delta}_{k}^{2}}.

(101)

Thus the self-consistent analogue of
\Sigma_{3}
in Eq. (
3
) becomes (where we have replaced the integration over
q
by the
q=0
value of the integrand, and
\tilde{\phi}_{0}=\phi_{0}\lambda^{2}r_{N}/v_{N}^{2}
)

\displaystyle\Sigma_{3}=6T\frac{\tilde{\phi}_{0}}{r_{N}r_{d}-\phi_{0}^{2}}\frac{\tilde{\epsilon}_{k}\gamma_{y}+\tilde{\Delta}_{k}\gamma_{z}}{(i\omega_{n})^{2}-\tilde{\epsilon}_{k}^{2}-\tilde{\Delta}_{k}^{2}}.

(102)

From the self-energy equation we get

\displaystyle G^{-1}

\displaystyle=G_{0}^{-1}-\Sigma_{3}

(103)

\displaystyle i\omega_{n}-\tilde{\epsilon}_{k}\gamma_{z}-\tilde{\Delta}_{k}\gamma_{y}

\displaystyle=i\omega_{n}-\epsilon_{\boldsymbol{k}}\gamma_{z}-6T\frac{\tilde{\phi}_{0}}{r_{N}r_{d}-\phi_{0}^{2}}\frac{\tilde{\epsilon}_{k}\gamma_{y}+\tilde{\Delta}_{k}\gamma_{z}}{(i\omega_{n})^{2}-\tilde{\epsilon}_{k}^{2}-\tilde{\Delta}_{k}^{2}}

(104)

\displaystyle\tilde{\epsilon}_{k}

\displaystyle=\epsilon_{\boldsymbol{k}}+Tc\frac{\tilde{\Delta}_{k}}{(i\omega_{n})^{2}-\tilde{\epsilon}_{k}^{2}-\tilde{\Delta}_{k}^{2}}

(105)

\displaystyle\tilde{\Delta}_{k}

\displaystyle=Tc\frac{\tilde{\epsilon}_{k}}{(i\omega_{n})^{2}-\tilde{\epsilon}_{k}^{2}-\tilde{\Delta}_{k}^{2}},

(106)

where
c=\frac{6\tilde{\phi}_{0}}{r_{N}r_{d}-\phi_{0}^{2}}
. Right at the Fermi surface,
\epsilon_{\boldsymbol{k}}=0
, the self consistency equations reduce to

\displaystyle\tilde{\epsilon}_{k}

\displaystyle=Tc\frac{\tilde{\Delta}_{k}}{(i\omega_{n})^{2}-\tilde{\epsilon}_{k}^{2}-\tilde{\Delta}_{k}^{2}}

(107)

\displaystyle\tilde{\Delta}_{k}

\displaystyle=Tc\frac{\tilde{\epsilon}_{k}}{(i\omega_{n})^{2}-\tilde{\epsilon}_{k}^{2}-\tilde{\Delta}_{k}^{2}}

(108)

\displaystyle\Longrightarrow\tilde{\epsilon}_{k}

\displaystyle=T^{2}c^{2}\frac{\tilde{\epsilon}_{k}}{(\omega_{n}^{2}+\tilde{\epsilon}_{k}^{2}+\tilde{\Delta}_{k}^{2})^{2}}

(109)

There are two possible solutions to Eqs. (
107
) and (
108
). The first is
\tilde{\epsilon}_{k}=\tilde{\Delta}_{k}=0
; this is exactly what we find within perturbation theory. For a solution with
\tilde{\epsilon}_{k}\neq 0
to exist, it must hold (assuming
\tilde{\epsilon}_{k}
,
\tilde{\Delta}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}
as expected in the gauge that we use)

\displaystyle 1=T^{2}\frac{c^{2}}{(\omega_{n}^{2}+\tilde{\epsilon}_{k}^{2}+\tilde{\Delta}_{k}^{2})^{2}}<T^{2}\frac{c^{2}}{\pi^{4}T^{4}}

(110)

Thus, a non-zero solution only exists if
T<c/\pi^{2}\sim c/9
. As compared to Hartree-Fock, the critical temperature for a non-perturbative solution is lower.
