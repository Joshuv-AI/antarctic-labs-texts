Associated Permutations of Complete Non-Ambiguous Trees
Daniel Chen, Sebastian Ohlig

Abstract
We explore new connections between complete non-ambiguous trees (CNATs) and permutations. We give a bijection between tree-like tableaux and a specific subset of CNATs. This map is used to establish and solve a recurrence relation for the number of tree-like tableaux of a fixed size without occupied corners, proving a conjecture by Laborde-Zubieta. We end by establishing a row/column swapping operation on CNATs and identify new areas for future research.

Introduction

In this paper, we study complete non-ambiguous trees (CNATs), a combinatorial object defined by
Aval et al. 2014
. Recent research has found a range of intriguing mathematical cross-connections, such as the bijection in
Dugan et al. 2019
to fully-tiered trees of weight
0
.

We will link CNATs to another combinatorial object, tree-like tableaux. Defined by
Aval et al. 2011
, they have been found to have applications in the PASEP model of statistical mechanics (
Corteel and Williams 2006
). In this context
Laborde-Zubieta 2015
first analysed occupied corners, that is vertices of the tableaux that have no cells below them or to their right.

We bridge these two areas of research by giving an explicit bijection (
4.15
), with the help of which we prove a formula for the number of tree like tableaux without occupied corners,
a(n)
, that was conjectured in Remark 3.3 of
Laborde-Zubieta 2015
. We verify that
a(n)
is indeed given by:

a(n)=\sum_{k=\lceil\frac{n-1}{2}\rceil}^{n}{\binom{k+1}{n-k}(-1)^{n-k}k!}

A more complete outline of our work follows below.

In
Section

2.1
, we introduce the notion of an associated permutation, matrix and leaf matrix of a given CNAT. Subsequently, we study properties of CNAT leaf matrices and derive a necessary and sufficient condition for a collection of vertices to be the set of leaves of at least one CNAT (
3.3
). We then consider when a collection of vertices is the set of leaves of
exactly
one CNAT, and we prove the striking result that there are exactly
2^{n-2}
such sets (
3.12
) for an
n\times n
CNAT.

In
Section

4
, we focus on upper-diagonal CNATs, which are CNATs whose leaf matrix is anti-diagonal. We prove a bijection between these CNATs and permutations (
4.13
) and also a constructive bijection to tree-like tableaux (
4.15
). This allows a recurrence relation to be established (
4.18
,
4.21
) which we go on to solve, proving the conjecture in Laborde-Zubieta’s paper.

We also prove that any CNAT can be reduced to upper-diagonal form through a series of row and column swaps which keep it a valid CNAT at each step (
4.29
). Finally we provide suggestions for further research (
Section

5
).

CNATs and CNMs

2.1
Definitions

Consider the bidimensional grid
\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{N}
. For convenience, given any
v=(x,y)\in\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{N}
we define
X(v)=x,\;Y(v)=y
. Then the definition of a CNAT is recalled from
Aval et al. 2014
as follows:

Definition 2.1
.

A
non-ambiguous tree
is a subset
T\subset\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{N}
satisfying the following constraints:

1.

(Existence of a root)
(1,1)\in T
.

2.

(Non-ambiguity)
\forall p\neq(1,1)\in T
, exactly one of the following is true:

\exists
r\in T
s.t.
X(r)=X(p)
and
Y(r)<Y(p)

\exists
r^{\prime}\in T
s.t.
Y(r^{\prime})=Y(p)
and
X(r^{\prime})<X(p)

In other words, each vertex other than the root has exactly one possible precursor. This motivates the adjective ”non-ambiguous”.

3.

(Minimality) If there exists a
p\in T
s.t.
X(p)=x
(resp.
Y(p)=y
), then for all
x^{\prime}<x
(resp.
y^{\prime}<y
) there is a
p^{\prime}\in T
s.t.
X(p^{\prime})=x^{\prime}
(resp.
Y(p^{\prime})=y^{\prime}
). Simply put,
T
contains no empty rows or columns.

If
T
also satisfies the condition that every vertex has zero or two children, then it is referred to as a
complete non-ambiguous tree
(CNAT). For convenience, we will display CNATs with the root vertex in the upper left corner.

:
A complete non-ambiguous tree

By connecting each vertex to its precursor in the same row or column, every vertex except for the root has a unique parent, resulting in a binary tree structure rooted at
(0,0)
. This implies that a CNAT with
2n-1
vertices has
n
leaves and
n-1
internal vertices. Furthermore, every row and column has exactly one leaf, thus a CNAT with
2n-1
vertices is contained in an
n\times n
grid. We refer to the CNAT as having size
n
, or dimensions
n\times n
.

Definition 2.2

(CNMs)
.

For a complete non-ambiguous tree
T
, a
complete non-ambiguous matrix
(CNM) is the unique representation of
T
as a binary matrix

defined by the relation:

a_{i,j}=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }(i,j)\in T\\
0&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

We shall refer to a given
a_{i,j}=1
as a
vertex
of the CNM and call it a
leaf
(resp.
internal vertex
) if the corresponding vertex
(i,j)
is a leaf (resp. internal vertex) in
T
.

CNATs and CNMs are in clear bijection with each other, but operating with CNMs allows to us to consider various matrix statistics and operations, leading to interesting results.

Definition 2.3

(Leaf Matrices)
.

For a given CNM
M
, the

Since there is exactly one leaf in every row and column,

\{1,2,\dots n\}

\pi(M)\in S_{n}

2.2
Determinants

Given these preliminary definitions, we may formulate our first result.

Proposition 2.5
.

The determinant of every complete non-ambiguous matrix
M
is
\text{sgn}(\pi(M))
, where
\text{sgn}:S_{n}\to\{1,-1\}
is the sign of the permutation.

Proof.

We consider the set of leaves in
M
given by the matrix
p(M)
.
By the non-ambiguity condition, for every leaf
m_{i,j}
of
M
, either the column of
M_{\cdot,j}
or the row
M_{i,\cdot}
is zero except for the vertex
m_{i,j}
. Without loss of generality, assume it is the row.
We add
-M_{i,\cdot}
to all rows
M_{k,\cdot}
in which
m_{k,j}=1
, resulting in a matrix
M^{\prime}
. Since row operations do not affect the determinant,
\det(M^{\prime})=\det(M)
. Note that after these operations are finished, the matrix
M^{\prime}
has the same determinant as

, but
m_{i,j}
is the only non-zero vertex in
M_{\cdot,j}
and
M_{i,\cdot}
.

After applying multiple such clearing operations, only the leaf matrix
p(M)
remains in the structure. Its determinant, being the permutation matrix representing
\pi
by definition, is exactly the value of
\text{sgn}(\pi(M))
.

3
Leaf Matrices

In this section we will prove results about CNATs arising from specific leaf matrices, recalling
2.3
.

Definition 3.1
.

A permutation matrix with associated permutation
\sigma
on
\{1,\dots,n\}
is
irreducible
if there is no
j
with
1<j<n
such that
\{1,\dots,j\}
is mapped to itself when applying
\sigma
.

Remark 3.2
.

Sequence A003319 in
Sloane and Inc. 2022
counts the number of irreducible permutations of
\{1\dots n\}
.

Theorem 3.3

(Irreducibility)
.

A permutation matrix is the leaf matrix of some CNM if and only if it is irreducible.

Proof.

\left(\Rightarrow\right)
We assume that there exists a CNM
M
with
p(M)
not irreducible and will arrive at a contradiction.

By definition, there exists a
j<n
such that the set
\{1\dots j\}
maps to itself; thus in
p(M)
, the submatrix of the first
j
rows and columns is a permutation matrix
M^{\prime}
. But then there can be no vertices below or to the right of
M^{\prime}
in
M
, because the vertices in
M^{\prime}
represent leaves. This means that the tree represented by
M
is disconnected, contradiction.

Figure 3.1
:
A permutation submatrix has no vertices below or to its right.

\left(\Leftarrow\right)
We will prove by induction that every irreducible permutation matrix of size
k
is the leaf matrix of at least one CNM of size
k
. Note that the case
k=2
is clear.

Fix
k>2
and suppose that for
2\leq j<k
the statement holds. We will prove it holds true for
k
. Fix a permutation matrix
M
of size
k
which is irreducible; we will add points to
M
to construct a CNM whose leaf matrix is the original permutation matrix and be done. Initially, label all
k^{2}
entries of
M
as unmarked.

Consider the vertices
l_{x},l_{y}
in
M
with maximal
x,y
coordinate respectively. Since
M
is irreducible,
l_{x}
and
l_{y}
are distinct.

Add the unmarked point
p=(X(l_{y}),Y(l_{x}))
to
M
, and label all entries in the column containing
l_{x}
and row containing
l_{y}
as marked. Note that at this point, the unmarked entries form a permutation submatrix. Then, for all leaves
v_{i}\in M
with
Y(v_{i})>Y(l_{x})
and
X(v_{i})>X(l_{y})
, we mark all entries in the column and row containing
v_{i}
, and freely choose to add either one of the (marked) points
(X(v_{i}),Y(l_{x}))
or
(X(l_{y}),Y(v_{i}))
. In other words, we choose to connect each
v_{i}
either to the left or upwards. The unmarked entries still form a permutation submatrix, because we repeatedly marked rows and columns whose intersection is a vertex of
M
.

Call this permutation submatrix of unmarked entries
M^{\prime}
, which has size strictly smaller than
M
. Furthermore,
M^{\prime}
contains
p
, and in
M^{\prime}
there are no unmarked vertices below and to the right of
p
, because all
v_{i}
were marked. We also claim that
M^{\prime}
is irreducible, the proof of which follows below.

Assume for sake of contradiction that
M^{\prime}
is not irreducible. Then by definition, it has some submatrix
S
that is its own permutation matrix, and is not the entirety of
M^{\prime}
. Note that the point
p
defined above is not vertically below or horizontally to the right of
S
(see
Fig.

3.1
), so it must either be inside
S
, or below
and
to the right of (i.e. diagonally below)
S
. If
p
is in
S
, then every position diagonally below
S
is also diagonally below
p
, so there are no unmarked vertices outside of
S
, implying that
S
is the entirety of
M^{\prime}
, contradiction. If
p
is diagonally below
S
, then
S
was unchanged by all of the markings, so
M
has the same submatrix
S
, contradicting the fact that
M
is irreducible.

Thus, by inductive hypothesis,
M^{\prime}
is the leaf matrix of some valid CNM. After adding the required points to
M
that turn
M^{\prime}
into a CNM, we see that
M
also becomes a valid CNM, because
p
becomes the parent of the leaves
l_{x}
and
l_{y}
in
M
, and each
v_{i}
becomes a leaf. Thus the leaf matrix of
M
is equal to the initial permutation matrix, as desired.

∎

Remark 3.4
.

In the above proof, we used induction where the connection of the rightmost and bottom-most vertices reduced the problem to a smaller case. This is equivalent to an iterative argument, where at each iteration we perform the aforementioned connection of the rightmost and bottom-most vertices, and the connection of the
v_{i}
. Thus we will refer to this as a ”step” of the construction in the proof of sufficiency of
3.3
.

Example 3.5
.

Fig.

3.2
below illustrates one step of the construction process.

Any new vertices added are coloured red. We connect the rightmost and bottom-most vertices, then any vertices enclosed by this connection (denoted

in the above proof, coloured blue) can connect to the left or upwards. In the given example, the connection occurs upwards. After removing rows and columns we obtain a smaller permutation matrix (highlighted in gray) which is irreducible, illustrating how the inductive argument yields the desired result.

Remark 3.6
.

In the above proof of sufficiency, each
v_{i}
has two possible connection choices (left or upwards). We may choose among these freely.

Definition 3.7

(L-subset)
.

For a matrix
M
and
k>1
, we define the

k^{th}
L-subset
to be:

\{m_{ij}\in M\mid\left(\left(i=k\right)\land\left(j\leq k\right)\right)\,\lor\,\left(\left(j=k\right)\land\,\left(i\leq k\right)\right)\}

Figure 3.3
:
The fifth L-subset in a
6\times 6
CNM

Lemma 3.8
.

If a permutation matrix
M
is the leaf matrix of exactly one CNAT, then it has no vertices on the diagonal.

Proof.

Suppose
M
represents a unique CNAT, and
M
has a vertex
v^{\prime}
on the diagonal. We will show a contradiction.

We use the constructive argument in the proof of sufficiency in
3.3
.
At each step, because
M
is irreducible,
v^{\prime}
can never be the rightmost or bottom-most vertex, thus it must eventually be enclosed in the connection of those vertices. Then by
3.6
, we can construct two possible CNATs by choosing to connect
v^{\prime}
to the left or upwards, contradicting uniqueness.
∎

Remark 3.9
.

Note that
3.8
implies that the
n
-th L-subset of a leaf matrix of size
n
always has two vertices, since both the last row and the last column must contain a vertex, which cannot be on the diagonal, meaning that the two vertices need to be distinct.

Lemma 3.10
.

If a permutation matrix
M
of size
n
is the leaf matrix of exactly one CNAT, then every L-subset other than the
n
-th one has exactly one vertex.

Proof.

We will induct on
n
. Note that by manual verification,
n=2
holds.

Fix
n>2
, and suppose the result holds for
n-1
. We will show it holds for
n
.

Suppose the matrix
M
represents a unique CNAT. We use the construction in the proof of sufficiency in
3.3
. By uniqueness, there must not be any vertices enclosed in the box defined connecting the rightmost and bottom-most vertices. But this means that after this connection step, the size of
M
decreases by exactly one, that is
M^{\prime}
has size
n-1
. Let
p
be the connection point of the rightmost and bottom-most vertices in
M
, and let the
n-1
-th L-subset be
J
. Note that by
3.9
,
J
has two vertices in
M^{\prime}
.

We distinguish two cases:

Case 1
:
p\not\in J

We deduce that
J
must have two vertices,
u,v
in
M
. Without loss of generality, let
X(u)<X(v)
. Since
u,v
were not enclosed by the connection of the rightmost and bottom-most vertices, we must have
X(u)<X(v_{y})
and
Y(v)<Y(v_{x})
. But then, in the next step of the construction, we have
X(u)<X(p)
and
Y(v)<Y(p)
so
p
will be enclosed and then we have a choice, contradiction. So Case 1 cannot occur.

Case 2
:
p\in J

By the inductive hypothesis, every L-subset of
M^{\prime}
other than
J
has one vertex. But
J
has two vertices in
M^{\prime}
by
3.9
, so it has one vertex in
M
. Thus, every L-subset in
M
other than the
n
-th indeed contains exactly one vertex.

∎

Theorem 3.11
.

A permutation matrix
M
of size
n
is the leaf matrix of exactly one CNAT
\iff
every L-subset other than the
n
-th one has exactly one vertex, and there are no vertices on the diagonal.

Proof.

\left(\Rightarrow\right)
Consequence of
3.8
and
3.10
.

\left(\Leftarrow\right)
Suppose every L-subset of
M
(other than the
n
-th) contains exactly one vertex and there are none on the diagonal.

Then, for every
k
-th L-subset (
1<k\leq n
), we claim that the submatrix of the first
k-1
rows and columns must have exactly one empty column and one empty row.

We prove this claim by induction. Note that the base case,
k=2
, is true since by assumption there are no vertices on the main diagonal. For the inductive step, fixing
k>2
, the addition of an L-subset to the first
k-1
rows and columns will result in a submatrix of size
k
. We then place the vertex in the
k
-th L-subset - there are two choices since we must place it below the empty column in the submatrix of size
k-1
, or to the right of the empty row (anywhere else will be below or to the right of an existing leaf). This results in one empty row and one empty column in the submatrix of size
k
.

By the above, every leaf has exactly one possible parent, so
M
represents at most one CNAT. But by
3.3

M
represents
at least
one CNAT, due to the fact that in every submatrix of size
k
, the
k
-th row or column is empty, since every L-subset only contains one leaf and there are none on the diagonal. Hence,
M
represents a unique CNAT.

∎

Corollary 3.12
.

There are
2^{n-2}
permutation matrices of size
n
which are the leaf matrix of exactly one CNAT.

Proof.

For each
2\leq k<n
, by the above we have two choices of where to place the vertex in the
k
-th L-subset. The root cannot be a leaf, and we have no choice of where to place the vertex in the
n
-th L-subset since we must fill in the final empty row and column. Multiplying all options yields the desired result.

∎

4
Upper-Diagonal CNMs

In this section, we will consider CNMs in which the leaf matrix is anti-diagonal. Bijections will be given from these to other combinatorial objects. To do this, we will need the definitions of a tree-like tableau and its occupied corners according to
Aval et al. 2011
as well as those of fully-tiered trees and their weights, both as used in
Dugan et al. 2019
.

4.1
Definitions

Definition 4.1

(Upper-Diagonal CNMs)
.

An
upper-diagonal CNM
is a CNM
M
in which the leaf matrix
p(M)
is anti-diagonal.

Figure 4.1
:
An upper-diagonal complete non-ambiguous matrix

Definition 4.2

(Second Diagonal)
.

The
second diagonal
of an upper-diagonal CNM
M=(m_{ij})
with size
n
is the set of matrix entries
\{m_{1,n-1},m_{2,n-2},\dots,m_{n-1,1}\}
.

Definition 4.3

(Tree-Like Tableaux and Occupied Corners)
.

A
tree-like tableau
of size
n
is a collection of left-aligned rows of cells, where each cell is either empty or contains a point (referred to as an empty cell and pointed cell respectively) and there are
n
pointed cells. The following conditions must also be satisfied:

1.

(Existence of a root) The top-left cell contains a point, referred to as the root.

2.

(Non-ambiguity) Each pointed cell other than the root has another pointed cell in the same row and to the left, or another in the same column and above, but not both.

3.

(Minimality) There are no rows or columns which contain no pointed cells.

A cell of the tree-like-tableau is a
corner
if there are no cells below or to the right of it. If a corner cell contains a point, then it is an
occupied corner
.

\bullet

\ \bullet

\bullet

:

Note the similarities between the definition of tree-like tableaux and CNATs.

The following two definitions are taken from
Dugan et al. 2019
.

Definition 4.4

(Tiered Trees)
.

For
n\in\mathbb{N}
, let
[n]=\{1,\dots,n\}
.
A
tiered tree
of size
n
is a labelled tree on
n
vertices with vertex set
V=[n]
, together with a
tiering function

t:V\mapsto[n]
such that if
u,v\in V
with
u
adjacent to
v
and
u<v
, then
t(u)<t(v)
. If
t(u)=a
, then
u
is said to be on tier
a
.

If
t
is bijective, then we say the tree is
fully-tiered
and call it a
fully-tiered tree
.

The tiers of a fully-tiered tree can be graphically represented by ordering vertices into vertical levels, with one vertex per level, where
u
is on the j-th level if
t(u)=j
.

Figure 4.3
:
An example of a fully-tiered tree

Definition 4.5

(Weights)
.

We define the weight of a fully-tiered tree
T
with tiering function
t
as follows: Let
v
be the vertex of
T
with minimal label and let
T_{1},\dots,T_{k}
be the connected components of
T\setminus\{v\}
. The
weight
of
T
,
\omega(T)
, is defined recursively as follows:

\omega(T)=\begin{cases}0&\text{if }\lvert T\rvert=1\\
\sum_{i=1}^{k}\left(\omega_{i}+\omega(T_{i})\right)&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

The
\omega_{i}
are defined like as follows: for each
T_{i}
let
u_{i}
be the unique vertex in
T_{i}
to which
v
is connected. Then:

\omega_{i}=|\{u_{j}\in T_{i}\mid t(u_{j})>t(v)\,\land\,u_{j}<u_{i}\}|

In other words,
\omega_{i}
is the zero-indexed position of
u_{i}
in the set of all vertices in
T_{i}
that could have been connected to
v
.

(Upper-Diagonal FTTs)

Bijections to Other Objects

We give bijections between upper-diagonal CNMs, permutations and tree-like tableaux.

Lemma 4.10
.

If
n
is the vertex of the highest level in an upper-diagonal FTT of size
n
, then
n
is a leaf.

Proof.

Suppose there is some
n
such that there exists an upper-diagonal FTT, say
T
, on
n
vertices in which the vertex with maximal label
n
is not a leaf. Consider the smallest such
n
; we will show a contradiction by finding a smaller upper-diagonal FTT on fewer vertices in which the highest label is not a leaf.

Note that since the vertex
n
has degree at least 2 by assumption,
n\geq 3
. Manually checking all cases for
n=3
, we see that the vertex 3 is indeed always a leaf.

For the rest of the proof, let
T^{\prime}
be the graph obtained from
T
by deleting the vertex
1
and all edges connected to it.

Case 1
:
1
is connected to
n
.

Since
n
is not a leaf, there exists a neighbour
u
of
n
in T, which is distinct from 1.
Let
T_{1}
be the connected component of
T^{\prime}
containing
u
and
n
. Examining weights and the definition of the
\omega_{i}
, we have
\omega_{1}>0
since
1
could have been connected to a smaller vertex
T_{1}
,
u
. By definition of weight,
\omega(T)=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\left(\omega_{i}+\omega(T_{i})\right)\geq\omega_{1}\geq 1
. But
T
is an upper-diagonal FTT and thus has
\omega(T)=0
by definition, contradiction.

Case 2
:
1
is not connected to
n
.

Without loss of generality, let
T_{1}
be the connected component of
T^{\prime}
containing
n
. Note that
n
has degree
\geq 2
in
T_{1}
and is the largest vertex of
T_{1}
. Using
4.5
and the fact that
T
has weight zero:

\omega(T_{0})=\sum_{i}(\omega_{i}+\omega(T_{i}))=0

All these sum terms are non-negative, so
\omega(T_{1})=0
.
After relabelling the vertices of
T_{1}
such that every vertex
v
is in tier
v
, since
\omega(T_{1})=0
we have that
T_{1}
(which is non-trivial since it contains at least two vertices) is an upper-diagonal FTT on fewer vertices than

with the maximal vertex not being a leaf, contradicting the minimality of

.
∎

Lemma 4.11
.

If
T
is an upper-diagonal FTT of size
n
, then adding the vertex
n+1
in tier
n+1
to
T
gives another upper-diagonal FTT,
T^{\prime}
.

Proof.

Note that
T\cup\{n+1\}
(i.e.
T^{\prime}
) is a fully-tiered tree with
t(v)=v
for all its vertices
v
. We will show that
T^{\prime}
has weight
0
by strong induction. This is true for
n=1
,
n=2
by inspection, so fix
n>2
and suppose the result holds for up to
n-1
; we will prove it for

.

T^{\prime}
is a fully-tiered tree with

for all vertices
v
(because

is). Consider the effect of adding the vertex

to
T
on the computation of the weight.

If
n+1
is connected to 1,
n+1
becomes its own connected component after the removal of
1
during the computation process, and so

has weight 0 since the other connected components remain the same.

If
n+1
is not connected to 1, let

be the connected component of

which contains the vertex connected to

in

. All other components are unaffected by the addition of

, and so contribute 0 to the weight sum. But flattening the labels of

and applying the induction hypothesis yields that

has weight 0, therefore

has weight 0. So

is an upper-diagonal FTT since it satisfies all criteria in

∎

Theorem 4.13
.

There is a bijection between upper-diagonal CNMs of size
n
and permutations in
S_{n-1}
.

Proof.

Dugan et al. 2019
gives a bijection between CNATs and weight
0
fully-tiered trees. In this, leaves of the CNAT at position
(x,y)
are mapped to vertices with label
x
and tier
n+1-y
. Since upper-diagonal CNMs have
X(v)=n+1-Y(v)
for all leaves
v
, under this map the upper-diagonal CNMs are sent precisely to those fully-tiered trees with weight
0
that have
t(v)=v
for all vertices
v\in\{1,2,\dots,n\}
. By
4.9
, these are exactly the upper-diagonal FTTs.

If we can find a map bijectively sending upper-diagonal FTTs of size
n
to permutations in
S_{n-1}
, then composing it with the above bijection results in a bijection between upper-diagonal CNMs of size
n
and permutations in
S_{n-1}
.

Let
T_{n}
be an upper-diagonal FTT of size
n
. In the following, we will construct a sequence of upper-diagonal FTTs
T_{n},T_{n-1},\dots,T_{1}
, represented by a sequence of
n-1
distinct integers

, with
1\leq i\leq n-1
. This representation will be bijective, and interpreting the sequence as an element of
S_{n-1}
gives rise to a permutation.

For the sake of iteration, let
S=\emptyset
and for each
n>i\geq 1
, let
T_{i}
be the tree obtained by removing the vertex with maximal label and its edges in
T_{i+1}
; this vertex is exactly
i+1
. Let
a_{i}
be the label of the unique vertex to which

connects in
T_{i}
. We add
a_{i+1}
to our sequence
S
.

Note that by
4.10
,
i+1
was a leaf, so we removed exactly one vertex and one edge. Since

comes from removing the vertex
i+1
and its associated edge from

, by

it is also an upper-diagonal FTT.

Thus we may repeat this process until we reach the tree on one vertex. We obtain a sequence of
n-1
numbers

, where due to the maximality of the vertex

in
T_{i}
, we have
1\leq a_{i}\leq n-i
.

Note that any such sequence satisfying

gives rise to a valid upper-diagonal FTT, given that

is constructed from

where the maximal vertex is a leaf, and thus

has weight 0 by

. We deduce that the set of all possible

is in bijection with the set of sequences

which satisfy

.

Finally, to reversibly extract the permutation out of the

as follows. Let

For each

to be the

th element of

as the sequence

-th element was removed. Iterating this procedure for increasing values of

, the sequence of

is eventually exhausted. Since

, this procedure always works. Note that at the end of this process, we have a sequence

, where the

are distinct values in

. Interpreting this as an element of

yields a mapping from the

We have shown that upper-diagonal CNMs of size

are in bijection with upper-diagonal fully-tiered trees of size

, which are in bijection with the

as defined above, which are in bijection with permutations in

.

∎

Theorem 4.15
.

4.3
Proving a Conjecture by Laborde-Zubieta

It was conjectured in
Laborde-Zubieta 2015
in Remark 3.3 that the number of tree-like tableaux of size
n
having no occupied corners is equal to the sequence A184185 in
Sloane and Inc. 2022
, which counts the number of permutations in
S_{n}
having no cycles of the form
(i,i+1,\dots,i+j)
for
j\geq 0
.

Recall that in the bijective construction for
4.13
, occupied corners of a tree-like tableau were sent exactly to the vertices in the second diagonal of the corresponding upper-diagonal CNM and vice versa. Hence, the number of TLTs of size
n
having no occupied corners is equal to the number of upper-diagonal CNMs of size
n+1
with a completely zero second diagonal. Now, we establish and explicitly solve a recurrence relation for the latter value.

Definition 4.18
.

For
0\leq k<n-1
, we define
f(n,k)
to be the number of
n\times n
upper-diagonal CNMs
M
with the first
k
elements of the second diagonal starting with exactly
k
zeros, followed by a one. For
k=n-1
, we extend this definition by defining
f(n,n-1)
to be the number of upper-diagonal CNMs with the second diagonal being completely zero.

Definition 4.19
.

We define the sum function
s(n,k)
by:

s(n,k)=\sum_{j=0}^{k}{f(n,j)}

Remark 4.20
.

Since
f(n,k)
counts the number of
n\times n
upper-diagonal CNMs with
k
leading zeroes in the second diagonal of the matrix, summing over all possibilities we deduce by
4.14
that
s(n,n-1)=(n-1)!

Theorem 4.21

(Recursion Formula for
f(n,k)
)
.

f(n,k)=\begin{cases}0&\text{if }k=-1\text{ (by convention)}\\[10.0pt]
\sum_{j=k-1}^{n-2}f(n-1,j)&\text{if }0\leq k<n-1\\[10.0pt]
\sum_{j=0}^{n-4}(n-3-j)f(n-1,j)&\text{if }k=n-1\end{cases}

Proof.

Consider an
n\times n
upper-diagonal CNM with second diagonal containing
k
leading zeros.

Case 1
:
k<n-1

By assumption, there exists a vertex
v
of the matrix at position

(n-1-k,k+1)
.
Thus, applying the non-ambiguity condition to the leaves
(n-k,k+1)
and
(n-k-1,k+2)
, this forces the column
k+2
and the row
n-k
to be empty except for these anti-diagonal nodes. Removing this column and row, we are left with a size
n-1
upper-diagonal CNM, in which
v
has become a leaf in the anti-diagonal of the new matrix, and in which the second diagonal starts with
at least

k-1
zeroes, since all columns to the right of
v
are shifted down by one, and the deletion of row
k+2
removes a leading zero whenever
k>0
. The cases for the number of leading zeroes are mutually exclusive, so summing over all values we obtain:

f(n,k)=\sum_{j=k-1}^{n-2}f(n-1,j)

Note that the convention
f(n,-1)=0
allows us to incorporate
k=0
in this recursive definition also.

Case 2
:
k=n-1

We evaluate
f(n,n-1)
by subtracting all other cases from the total of
(n-1)!
(by
4.20
).

\displaystyle f(n,n-1)

\displaystyle=(n-1)!-\sum_{i=0}^{n-2}f(n,i)

\displaystyle=\left[(n-1)\sum_{i=0}^{n-2}f(n-1,i)\right]-\left[\sum_{i=0}^{n-2}\sum_{j=i-1}^{n-2}f(n-1,j)\right]\;\text{ (by Case 1)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{n-4}(n-3-j)f(n-1,j)\;\text{ (by swapping double summation)}

∎

Lemma 4.22
.

f(n,0)=(n-2)!

Proof.

By Case 1:

\displaystyle f(n,0)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=-1}^{n-2}{f(n-1,j)}

\displaystyle=\underbrace{f(n-1,-1)}_{\text{= 0}}+\sum_{j=0}^{n-2}{f(n-1,j)}

By
4.20
, this evaluates to
(n-2)!
, as required.
∎

Lemma 4.23
.

For
0<k<n-1
,

f(n,k)=f(n,k-1)-f(n-1,k-2)

Proof.

\displaystyle f(n-1,k-2)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=k-2}^{n-2}{f(n-1,j)}-\sum_{j=k-1}^{n-2}{f(n-1,j)}

\displaystyle=f(n,k-1)-f(n,k)

(by Case 1)

Rearranging yields the desired result.
∎

Lemma 4.24
.

f(n,n-1)=f(n+1,n-1)-f(n,n-2)

Proof.

Recalling that
s(n-1,n-2)=(n-2)!
yields:

\begin{split}s(n-1,n-3)&=(n-2)!-f(n-1,n-2)\end{split}

(1)

But if we consider
\Delta=s(n,n-2)-s(n-1,n-3)
:

\displaystyle\Delta

\displaystyle=f(n,0)+f(n,n-2)-f(n-1,n-3)

\displaystyle\;\;\;\;\;\;+\sum_{j=2}^{n-2}{\underbrace{\left(f(n,j-1)-f(n-1,j-2)\right)}_{\text{= \begin{math}f(n,j)\end{math}}}}

\displaystyle=s(n,n-2)-f(n,1)+f(n,n-2)-f(n-1,n-3)

Cancelling
s(n,n-2)
from both sides and rearranging yields:

s(n-1,n-3)=f(n,1)+f(n-1,n-3)-f(n,n-2)

Substituting this into
Eq.

1
:

f(n,1)+f(n-1,n-3)-f(n,n-2)=(n-2)!-f(n-1,n-2)

But note that, by
4.23
, we have
f(n,1)=f(n,0)-f(n-1,-1)=(n-2)!
. Thus we can cancel the
(n-2)!
term and rearrange:

\begin{split}f(n-1,n-2)=f(n,n-2)-f(n-1,n-3)\end{split}

Shifting indices proves the lemma.
∎

Theorem 4.26
.

For
0\leq k<n-1
:

f(n,k)=\sum_{j=0}^{k}\binom{k-j}{j}(-1)^{j}(n-2-j)!

Proof.

For convenience, let
g(n,k)
be the RHS of the above equation. We will induct on the lexicographic ordering of pairs
(n,k)
.

Base case
:
(n,k)=(2,0)
is true. Additionally, the statement is clearly true whenever
k=-1
.

Inductive step:
Fix
n,k
with
k\leq n-2
and suppose for all pairs
(2,0)\leq(a,b)<(n,k)
we have
f(a,b)=g(a,b)
. We will show

.

If
k=0
, then
g(n,k)=(n-2)!=f(n,k)
.

Otherwise:

\displaystyle f(n,k)

\displaystyle=f(n,k-1)-f(n-1,k-2)

By
4.23

\displaystyle=g(n,k-1)-g(n-1,k-2)

By inductive hypothesis

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\binom{k-1-j}{j}(-1)^{j}(n-2-j)!

\displaystyle\;\;\;\;\;\;-\sum_{j=0}^{k-2}\binom{k-2-j}{j}(-1)^{j}(n-3-j)!

By definition

Extracting the first term of the former sum and re-indexing yields

\displaystyle f(n,k)

\displaystyle=(n-2)!+\sum_{j=0}^{k-2}{\binom{k-2-j}{j+1}(-1)^{j+1}(n-3-j)!}

\displaystyle\;\;\;\;\;\;-\sum_{j=0}^{k-2}{\binom{k-2-j}{j}(-1)^{j}(n-3-j)!}

\displaystyle=(n-2)!-\sum_{j=0}^{k-2}\left[\binom{k-2-j}{j+1}+\binom{k-2-j}{j}\right](-1)^{j}(n-3-j)!

By Pascal’s Identity, this implies

\displaystyle f(n,k)

\displaystyle=(n-2)!-\sum_{j=0}^{k-2}\binom{k-1-j}{j+1}(-1)^{j}(n-3-j)!

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\binom{k-j}{j}(-1)^{j}(n-2-j)!

\displaystyle=g(n,k)

We have:

4.4
Row and Column swaps

To end this section, we will prove that any CNAT can be transformed into an upper-diagonal one through a series of row and column swaps. Note that this will give an alternative proof of
2.5
, since the determinant of any upper-diagonal matrix is
-1
and any row or column swap multiplies the determinant by
-1
.

Theorem 4.29
.

For any
n\times n
CNAT
M=M_{0}
, we can obtain a sequence of valid CNATs
\{M_{i}\}
where each
M_{i+1}
was the result of a row or column swap in
M_{i}
, and for which there is an
N
with
M_{N}
upper-diagonal.

Proof.

We will prove the existence of such row and columns swaps by induction on
n
. Note that a sequence of swaps always exists for
n=1,n=2
.

Firstly, let the leaf in the leftmost column be in row
k
. Note that the only vertex in row
k
is the leaf, and thus we can perform the valid sequence of row swaps

k\leftrightarrow k+1,k+1\leftrightarrow k+2,\dots,n-1\leftrightarrow n

in order to move the row containing the first column’s leaf down to the bottom.

Next, consider the effect of deleting the leftmost column of the CNAT, as well as all edges to vertices in the column. We obtain a forest of smaller CNATs
F_{i}
(although each is embedded in the larger
n\times n
grid, i.e. Minimality is not satisfied) with any two occupying disjoint rows and columns. We can flatten the labels of each
F_{i}
(so that Minimality is satisfied), and thus by induction each of the
F_{i}
can be transformed to upper diagonal form through row and column swaps, so that for any two leaves
m_{a,b},m_{c,d}
in an
F_{i}
, if
a>c
then
b<d
. After this, the final upper-diagonal CNM is constructed by ”interweaving” column swaps as follows.

Consider the column
c\geq 2
containing the leaf in row
n-1
, and the
F_{i}
that this leaf belongs to. Since this leaf is the bottommost leaf of
F_{i}
and
F_{i}
is upper-diagonal, we must have the root of
F_{i}
being in the same column
c
. Thus any columns to the left of
c
will not be part of the same
F_{i}
, therefore we can perform the column swaps
c\leftrightarrow c-1,c-1\leftrightarrow c-2,\cdots,3\leftrightarrow 2
so that the leaf in row
n-1
is correctly in column
2
. All of these column swaps are valid because the
F_{i}
have disjoint rows and columns, and we did not change any vertices in the first column.

Similarly, consider the column
d
containing the leaf in row
n-2
, and its
F_{i}
. Note that
d\geq 3
since we already fixed the leaf in column
2
. If this
F_{i}
has not yet been considered, then the same logic as above applies and we can do the sequence of column swaps
d\leftrightarrow d-1,\cdots,4\leftrightarrow 3
to send the leaf in row
n-2
to column
3
. On the other hand if this
F_{i}
is the same as before, then the same sequence of swaps is still valid because there are no vertices of
F_{i}
between column
2
(the last column considered) and
d
. This follows from the fact that
F_{i}
is upper diagonal and the leaves at columns
2
and
d
are on adjacent rows.

This logic can be continued for rows
n-3,n-4,\cdots,2,1
at which point the upper-diagonal CNAT will have been constructed.
∎

Example 4.30
.

In
