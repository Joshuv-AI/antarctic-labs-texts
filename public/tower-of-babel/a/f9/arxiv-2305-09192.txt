Decomposition of (infinite) digraphs along directed 1-separations
Nathan Bowler, Florian Gut, Meike Hatzel, Ken-ichi Kawarabayashi, Irene Muzi, Florian Reich, Nathan Bowler – Universität Hamburg, Germany nathan.bowler@uni-hamburg.de Florian Gut – Universität Hamburg, Germany florian.gut@uni-hamburg.de Meike Hatzel – National Institute of Informatics, Tokyo, Japan research@meikehatzel.com Ken-ichi Kawarabayashi – National Institute of Informatics, Tokyo, Japan k_keniti@nii.ac.jp Irene Muzi – Birkbeck, University of London, United Kingdom irene.muzi@gmail.com Florian Reich – Universität Hamburg, Germany florian.reich@uni-hamburg.de

Abstract
We introduce torsoids , a canonical structure in matching covered graphs, corresponding to the bricks and braces of the graph. This allows a more fine-grained understanding of the structure of finite and infinite directed graphs with respect to their 1-separations. Keywords: directed separations, digraphs, infinite digraphs, decomposition, perfect matching, tight cuts, torsoids

Introduction

Separations in (di-)graphs are a structural property that has long been of interest for mathematicians.
In undirected graphs for small
k
, there are simple and canonical combinatorial structures displaying all
k
-separations of a given
k
-connected undirected graph and the relationships between them.
For
k=0
this is trivial; it is simply the decomposition of the graph into its connected components.
For
k=1
this is the block-cut decomposition
[
diestel17
, Chapter 3.1]
and for
k=2
it is the Tutte decomposition
[
Tutte66
]
.
Recent work of Carmesin and Kurkofka has developed an analogous such structure with
k=3

[
Carmesin23
]
.

For digraphs the situation is very different, with almost nothing being known.
In the case
k=0
we again have the decomposition of the digraph into its strongly connected components, together with the partial order on those components given by the reachability relation.
However, already for
k=1
previous progress on this problem was limited to a partial result by Lovász
[
Lovasz1987
]
1
1

1

In fact Lovász, like us, works in the more general framework of matching covered graphs.
.

In order to explain what Lovász’ result means for digraphs, we need to consider an operation on strongly connected digraphs.
Let
D
be a digraph, and let
(A,B)
be a directed 1-separation of
D
with separator
v.

Let
D_{A}
be the digraph obtained from
D
by contracting all of
B
onto
v
and let
D_{B}
be the digraph obtained by contracting all of
A
onto
v.

We say that
D_{A}
and
D_{B}
are obtained from
D
by
pulling it apart
along
(A,B).

Now imagine taking a strongly connected digraph, pulling it apart along some directed 1-separation, then continuing to pull those parts apart for as long as possible until you are left with a list of strongly 2-connected digraphs.
In this context, Lovász’ result says that it does not matter in what order you carry out this process or which 1-separations you pick, you will always end up with the same list up to list rearrangement and digraph isomorphism.

To understand the limitations of this result, let’s compare it with the Tutte decomposition.
An analogous decomposition procedure would be the following.
Given a 2-connected
undirected
graph
G
and a 2-separation
(A,B)
of
G
with separator
\{x,y\}
, we could define
G_{A}
and
G_{B}
to be the graphs obtained from
G[A]
and
G[B]
respectively by adding the edge
xy
to each of them.
We could once again consider a procedure of repeatedly pulling a graph apart along such separations until we are left with a list of 3-connected graphs, and it follows from the Tutte decomposition that you will always end up with the same list of 3-connected graphs up to list rearrangement and graph isomorphism.

However, this adaption to 3-connected graphs yields a weaker result than the original Tutte decomposition in a number of ways.
First, the elements of the list are only given up to isomorphism, but in the Tutte decomposition they are given as the torsos of a canonical tree-decomposition of
G.

Second, the adapted decomposition procedure cuts up the 2-connected graph too much.
In the Tutte decomposition, some of the torsos are cycles, and these are sensibly not cut up any further because there is no way to do so canonically.
But the adapted decomposition procedure will happily cut up an
n
-cycle into
n-2
triangles, and in so doing lose the possibility of finding canonical representatives in the original graph.
Third, the Tutte decomposition provides a global tree structure along which the parts are arranged, and this information is not contained in the list of 3-connected graphs obtained by the adapted decomposition procedure.

Lovász’ result has the same 3 limitations.
The elements of the list are only given up to isomorphism, not as canonical structures within the original digraph.
It cuts any directed
n
-cycle up into
n-1
directed 2-cycles, although there is no hope of finding canonical representatives for these in the original graph.
Finally, it does not provide any global structure along which the parts are arranged.

In this paper, we resolve the first two limitations of Lovász’ result, but not the third.
More precisely, we find canonical structures, which we call torsoids, within the digraph corresponding to the parts into which it would be cut by Lovász’ procedure.
However, we do not cut up directed cycles any further, since there would be no way to find canonical structures representing the parts.
As an illustration of the canonicity of our results and the deeper structural understanding they provide, we are able to extend Lovász’ results to infinite digraphs.

Having discussed the limitations of Lovász’ result, it is worth noting one major advantage.
He was able to prove his result in the more general context of
matching covered graphs
(that is, connected graphs such that every edge is contained in at least one perfect matching).
There is a well-understood correspondence between strongly connected digraphs and bipartite matching-covered graphs
[
mccuaig2000evendicycles
,
rst1999pfaffianorientations
]
, under which directed 1-separations correspond to tight cuts.
Lovász’ result deals with the process of cutting up (not necessarily bipartite) matching covered graphs along their tight cuts.

Since all our arguments also work just as easily in this more general context, and since there has been a renewed interest in the structure of matching covered graphs in recent years
[
Norine2006
,
Norine2007
,
hatzel2019
,
Giannopoulou21
]
, we also phrase all of our results in this paper in these more general terms.

Our paper is structured as follows.
After introducing some preliminaries in
section

2
and exploring the basic structure of infinite matching covered graphs and their tight cuts in
section

3
and
section

4
, we introduce our new canonical torsoids in
section

5
.
After exploring their relation to tight cuts in
, we generalise Lovász’ result to infinite matching covered graphs in
.

2.1
Connection to matching covered graphs

Every strongly connected (possibly infinite) directed graph corresponds to a bipartite graph with a perfect matching.
We can obtain this bipartite graph with colour classes
V_{1}
(black) and
V_{0}
(white) from a digraph

by splitting every vertex
v
into two vertices

, one of each colour class, that are connected by an edge, such that all in-edges of
v
become in-edges of the ‘white’ vertex
v_{0}
and all out-edges of
v
become out-edges of the ‘black’ vertex

Then we remove all directions from the edges.
Clearly, this yields a bipartite graph and the new edges form a perfect matching in it.
We refer to this graph as
G_{D}
and to the canonical perfect matching in it as
M_{D}.

Figure 1:
A strongly connected directed graph
D
and the corresponding bipartite graph
G_{D}
with the canonical perfect matching
M_{D}
.

Conversely, we can also obtain a directed graph from any given (possibly infinite) bipartite graph with a perfect matching, by directing all non-matching edges from
V_{1}
to
V_{0}
and then contracting all matching-edges into single vertices.
For a given graph
G
with perfect matching
M
, we call this the
M
-direction of

and denote it
\mathchoice{D\!\left(G,M\right)}{D\!\left(G,M\right)}{D\left(G,M\right)}{D\left(G,M\right)}.

For an undirected graph

we refer to the set of all perfect matchings in
G
by
\mathchoice{\mathcal{M}\!\left(G\right)}{\mathcal{M}\!\left(G\right)}{\mathcal{M}\left(G\right)}{\mathcal{M}\left(G\right)}.

An undirected graph

is called

, if every edge lies in a perfect matching that has finite symmetric difference to the matching
M.

We write
\mathchoice{\mathcal{M}\!\left(G,M\right)}{\mathcal{M}\!\left(G,M\right)}{\mathcal{M}\left(G,M\right)}{\mathcal{M}\left(G,M\right)}
for the set of all perfect matchings that have finite symmetric difference to

We simply say

is

, when it is matching covered with respect to some matching.
Note that on finite graphs this is equivalent to saying that every edge lies in a perfect matching as
\mathchoice{\mathcal{M}\!\left(G,M\right)}{\mathcal{M}\!\left(G,M\right)}{\mathcal{M}\left(G,M\right)}{\mathcal{M}\left(G,M\right)}=\mathchoice{\mathcal{M}\!\left(G\right)}{\mathcal{M}\!\left(G\right)}{\mathcal{M}\left(G\right)}{\mathcal{M}\left(G\right)}
for all perfect matchings

in

Observation 2.1
.

Given an undirected graph
G
that is matching covered with respect to a perfect matching
M
, then the symmetric difference between any two perfect matchings in
\mathchoice{\mathcal{M}\!\left(G,M\right)}{\mathcal{M}\!\left(G,M\right)}{\mathcal{M}\left(G,M\right)}{\mathcal{M}\left(G,M\right)}
is a collection of disjoint cycles.

Definition 2.2

(tight cuts and tight sets, BoBs)
.

Let
G
be a graph that is matching covered with respect to a perfect matching
M.

A subset

\mathchoice{\partial\!\left(X\right)}{\partial\!\left(X\right)}{\partial\left(X\right)}{\partial\left(X\right)}
and we refer to
\mathchoice{\partial\!\left(X\right)}{\partial\!\left(X\right)}{\partial\left(X\right)}{\partial\left(X\right)}
as a

.

A tight set

and its corresponding tight cut
\mathchoice{\partial\!\left(X\right)}{\partial\!\left(X\right)}{\partial\left(X\right)}{\partial\left(X\right)}
are called

if
\left|X\right|=1
or
\left|\mathchoice{V\!\left(G\right)}{V\!\left(G\right)}{V\left(G\right)}{V\left(G\right)}\setminus X\right|=1.

Tight cuts

are

if there are
X_{1},X_{2}\subset\mathchoice{V\!\left(G\right)}{V\!\left(G\right)}{V\left(G\right)}{V\left(G\right)}

for

Tight sets

and
X_{2}
are

if
\mathchoice{\partial\!\left(X_{1}\right)}{\partial\!\left(X_{1}\right)}{\partial\left(X_{1}\right)}{\partial\left(X_{1}\right)}
and
\mathchoice{\partial\!\left(X_{2}\right)}{\partial\!\left(X_{2}\right)}{\partial\left(X_{2}\right)}{\partial\left(X_{2}\right)}

A bipartite graph with no non-trivial tight sets is called a

, while a non-bipartite graph with no non-trivial tight sets is called a

.
Since thus every graph with no non-trivial tight sets is either a brick or a brace, we refer to such a graph as a

For a graph

and a maximal family

\vec{\mathcal{C}}\coloneqq\{X\subseteq\mathchoice{V\!\left(G\right)}{V\!\left(G\right)}{V\left(G\right)}{V\left(G\right)}:\mathchoice{\partial\!\left(X\right)}{\partial\!\left(X\right)}{\partial\left(X\right)}{\partial\left(X\right)}\in\mathcal{C}\}
, i.e. the set of tight sets corresponding to tight cuts in

Note that a tight set

is contained in

if
\mathchoice{\partial\!\left(X\right)}{\partial\!\left(X\right)}{\partial\left(X\right)}{\partial\left(X\right)}
is nested with

Furthermore, any two elements of

are nested.

In this paper we do not demand any additional properties of the perfect matching

or the set
\mathchoice{\mathcal{M}\!\left(G,M\right)}{\mathcal{M}\!\left(G,M\right)}{\mathcal{M}\left(G,M\right)}{\mathcal{M}\left(G,M\right)}
of a matching covered graph

Therefore, unless stated otherwise, we consider tight cuts and tight sets with respect to an arbitrary but fixed perfect matching of

, without explicitly mentioning that perfect matching.

Proposition 2.3
.

Let

be a connected, matching covered graph.
For any tight set
X\subset\mathchoice{V\!\left(G\right)}{V\!\left(G\right)}{V\left(G\right)}{V\left(G\right)}

For any two distinct edges

\mathchoice{\partial\!\left(X\right)}{\partial\!\left(X\right)}{\partial\left(X\right)}{\partial\left(X\right)}

The cycle containing

Now for any two vertices

as above.

Definition 2.4
.

Let

The integer

Proposition 2.5
.

The tight sets in a matching covered bipartite graph

with respect to a perfect matching

correspond one-to-one to the 1-separations in
\mathchoice{D\!\left(G,M\right)}{D\!\left(G,M\right)}{D\left(G,M\right)}{D\left(G,M\right)}.

Proof.

Let
X
be a tight set in

Then
M
has exactly one edge

with one endpoint in
X
and the other in
\mathchoice{V\!\left(G\right)}{V\!\left(G\right)}{V\left(G\right)}{V\left(G\right)}\setminus X.

Let

We show that

Without loss of generality assume the unique vertex in

Then there is no edge between a vertex in

and
\mathchoice{V\!\left(G\right)}{V\!\left(G\right)}{V\left(G\right)}{V\left(G\right)}\setminus X

Thus all edges between

\mathchoice{V\!\left(G\right)}{V\!\left(G\right)}{V\left(G\right)}{V\left(G\right)}\setminus X

Therefore there are no edges from
\left(\mathchoice{V\!\left(D\right)}{V\!\left(D\right)}{V\left(D\right)}{V\left(D\right)}\setminus X^{\prime}\right)\setminus\left(X^{\prime}\cup\{v_{e}\}\right)
to
\left(X^{\prime}\cup\{v_{e}\}\right)\setminus\left(\mathchoice{V\!\left(D\right)}{V\!\left(D\right)}{V\left(D\right)}{V\left(D\right)}\setminus X^{\prime}\right).

Let

Let

be the two vertices such that the edge

gets contracted to

Consider the set

We claim that

Suppose towards a contradiction that it is not.
First, we consider the case that there is a matching

\mathchoice{V\!\left(G\right)}{V\!\left(G\right)}{V\left(G\right)}{V\left(G\right)}\setminus X.

As

has no neighbour in

Second, we consider the case that there is a matching

∎

3
Even and odd sets in infinite graphs

Let
G
be a matching covered graph with respect to a perfect matching
M.

A set

if
\left|\mathchoice{\partial_{G}\!\left(X\right)}{\partial_{G}\!\left(X\right)}{\partial_{G}\left(X\right)}{\partial_{G}\left(X\right)}\cap M\right|
is finite.
For a set

.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
G
be a matching covered graph with respect to a perfect matching

If

Let

and

We split the set of edges

in two disjoint parts:

As every cycle has an even number of edges in

\mathchoice{\partial\!\left(X\right)}{\partial\!\left(X\right)}{\partial\left(X\right)}{\partial\left(X\right)}\cap M

For the notion of torsoids (see

Thereafter, we explore how tight set partitions interact with tight sets in

4.1
Basic facts about crossing tight sets

For this

we fix a matching covered graph

The first few results in this

were already known for finite matching covered graphs
[

,
matchingtheory_book
,
Lovasz1987
]
, and their proofs for infinite matching covered graphs do not contain any new ideas.
However, to keep this paper self-contained we give these proofs here.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
X
and
X^{\prime}
be tight sets with
X\cap X^{\prime}
odd.
Then both
X\cap X^{\prime}
and
X\cup X^{\prime}
are tight sets and there is no edge with endpoints in both
X\setminus X^{\prime}
and
X^{\prime}\setminus X.

If
X\setminus X^{\prime}\neq\emptyset
then there is an edge with endpoints in
X\cap X^{\prime}
and
X\setminus X^{\prime}.

Proof.

First we show that
X\cap X^{\prime}
is tight.
For any matching
M
,

|M\cap\partial(X\cap X^{\prime})|\leq|M\cap\partial(X)|+|M\cap\partial(X^{\prime})|=2\,,

but since
X\cap X^{\prime}
is odd,
|M\cap\partial(X\cap X^{\prime})|
must also be odd and so must be equal to one.
An identical argument shows that
X\cup X^{\prime}
is tight.

Next, suppose for a contradiction that there is an edge
e
with endpoints in
X\setminus X^{\prime}
and
X^{\prime}\setminus X.

Let
M
be any matching containing
e
, and let
f
be the unique edge in
M\cap\partial(X\cap X^{\prime}).

As
f\in\partial(X)
and
\partial(X)
contains both
e
and
f
, contradicting tightness of
X.

Thus there can be no such an edge
e.

Finally, suppose that
X\setminus X^{\prime}
is nonempty.
By connectivity of
G
there must be some edge
e\in\partial(X\setminus X^{\prime}).

Let
M
be a matching containing
e.

Then
|M\cap\partial(X\setminus X^{\prime})|
is a positive even number, so it is at least 2.
Every edge in
M\cap\partial(X\setminus X^{\prime})
must be in one of
M\cap\partial(X)
and
M\cap\partial(X^{\prime})
, and each of those sets contains only one edge, so one of the edges of
M\cap\partial(X\setminus X^{\prime})
must be in
M\cap\partial(X^{\prime}).

Then that edge has one endpoint in
X\setminus X^{\prime}
and the other in
X^{\prime}.

We have just seen that the endpoint in
X^{\prime}
cannot be in
X^{\prime}\setminus X
, and so it must be in
X\cap X^{\prime}.

∎

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
X
,
X^{\prime}
and

be tight sets such that

and

Then there is no edge between these two sets.

Proof.

Applying

4.1
to

, we see that

is tight.
But then applying the same

to

and the complement of

we get the desired result.
∎

Lemma 4.3
.

Let

,

and

be tight sets such that

Proof.

Suppose not for a contradiction.
By

applied to

and the complement of

there is an edge

with one end in

and the other in

But this contradicts

and the complement of

∎

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
(X_{i})_{i\in I}
be a family of tight sets such that the union of any two of them is also tight.
Then

is either also tight or is the whole vertex set.

Proof.

Suppose for a contradiction that there is a matching

containing two edges

and
f
in the boundary of

Choose

such that the endpoints of

and

and

Then both

and

are also in the boundary of

, contradicting the tightness of this set.

Thus any matching

contains at most one edge in
\mathchoice{\partial\!\left(X\right)}{\partial\!\left(X\right)}{\partial\left(X\right)}{\partial\left(X\right)}.
If

Thus

is odd, and any matching contains exactly one edge in the boundary of

Applying this to the complements of a family of tight sets also gives the following statement.

.

Let
(X_{i})_{i\in I}
be a family of tight sets such that the intersection of any two of them is odd.
Then
X\coloneqq\bigcap_{i\in I}X_{i}
is either also tight or else is empty.
\square

Lemma 4.6
.

Let

be a tight set and

So by applying

Suppose for a contradiction that it is empty.
Let

be a perfect matching, let

\mathchoice{\mathsf{coll}\!\left(\mathcal{P}\right)}{\mathsf{coll}\!\left(\mathcal{P}\right)}{\mathsf{coll}\left(\mathcal{P}\right)}{\mathsf{coll}\left(\mathcal{P}\right)}.

Lemma 4.22
.

Let
\rho\colon\mathcal{P}\to\mathcal{Q}
be a correspondence of tight set partitions of
G.

Then
\rho
is also a graph isomorphism from
\mathchoice{\mathsf{coll}\!\left(\mathcal{P}\right)}{\mathsf{coll}\!\left(\mathcal{P}\right)}{\mathsf{coll}\left(\mathcal{P}\right)}{\mathsf{coll}\left(\mathcal{P}\right)}
to
\mathchoice{\mathsf{coll}\!\left(\mathcal{Q}\right)}{\mathsf{coll}\!\left(\mathcal{Q}\right)}{\mathsf{coll}\left(\mathcal{Q}\right)}{\mathsf{coll}\left(\mathcal{Q}\right)}.

Proof.

First we show that
\rho^{-1}
is a graph homomorphism.
Let
e
be an edge joining
\rho(P)
to
\rho(P^{\prime})
in
\mathchoice{\mathsf{coll}\!\left(\mathcal{Q}\right)}{\mathsf{coll}\!\left(\mathcal{Q}\right)}{\mathsf{coll}\left(\mathcal{Q}\right)}{\mathsf{coll}\left(\mathcal{Q}\right)}.

Let
v
be the endpoint of
e
in
\rho(P)
and
w
the endpoint in
\rho(P^{\prime}).

Suppose for a contradiction that
v
is in an element
P^{\prime\prime}
of
\mathcal{P}
other than
P
and
P^{\prime}.

Applying
lemma

4.2
to
P^{\prime\prime}
,
\rho(P)
and
\rho(P^{\prime})
, we see that
w
cannot also be in
P^{\prime\prime}.
But then we get the desired contradiction by applying
lemma

4.1
to
P^{\prime\prime}
and the complement of
\rho(P).

Thus
v
must lie in
P\cup P^{\prime}
, and a symmetric argument shows that
w
is also in
P\cup P^{\prime}.

If
w\in P
then
P\cap\rho(P^{\prime})
is nonempty and so by
lemma

4.21

P
and
P^{\prime}
are neighbours in
\mathchoice{\mathsf{coll}\!\left(\mathcal{P}\right)}{\mathsf{coll}\!\left(\mathcal{P}\right)}{\mathsf{coll}\left(\mathcal{P}\right)}{\mathsf{coll}\left(\mathcal{P}\right)}.

The same result follows if
v
is in
P^{\prime}
, since then
P^{\prime}\cap\rho(P)
is nonempty.
If neither of these happens then
v
is in
P
and
w
is in
P^{\prime}
, so
e
itself witnesses that
P
and
P^{\prime}
are neighbours in
\mathchoice{\mathsf{coll}\!\left(\mathcal{P}\right)}{\mathsf{coll}\!\left(\mathcal{P}\right)}{\mathsf{coll}\left(\mathcal{P}\right)}{\mathsf{coll}\left(\mathcal{P}\right)}.

So in any case they are neighbours, completing the proof that
\rho^{-1}
is a graph homomorphism.

Since
\rho^{-1}
is also a correspondence from
\mathcal{Q}
to
\mathcal{P}
, the same argument applied to
\rho^{-1}
shows that
\rho
is also a graph homomorphism, and thus a graph isomorphism.
∎
