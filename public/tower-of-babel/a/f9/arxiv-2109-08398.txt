Duality and tangles of set separations
Reinhard Diestel, Christian Elbracht, Joshua Erde, Maximilian Teegen

Abstract
Applications of tangles of connectivity systems suggest a duality between these, in which for two sets X X and Y Y\! the elements x x of X X map to subsets Y x Y_{x} of Y Y\! , and the elements y y of Y Y\! map to subsets X y X_{y} of X X , so that x ∈ X y x\in X_{y} if and only if y ∈ Y x y\in Y_{x} for all x ∈ X x\in X and y ∈ Y y\in Y\! . We explore this duality, and relate the tangles arising from the dual systems to each other.

Introduction

Tangles are a relatively novel, indirect, notion of cluster in discrete structures. While originally introduced by Robertson and Seymour
[
12
]
for graphs and matroids, they have since been generalised to a much wider range of settings
[
2
,
6
]
.

Rather than specifying a cluster by naming its elements, tangles describe clusters indirectly by a set of pointers. This makes them particularly well suited to capturing ‘fuzzy’ clusters: clusters that appear obvious when viewed from a distance, but where allocating individual points to any of them can present a problem.

More precisely, if
C
is a cluster in a data set
V\!
, a tangle will capture its location by orienting some suitable bipartitions of
V\!
towards their side that contains most of
C
. If
S
is a set of bipartitions of
V\!
each oriented towards
C
in this way, we say that the partitions in
S
are oriented
consistently
: not arbitrarily, but all towards that fixed cluster
C
.

Clearly, this can be done reliably only if each
s\in S
splits
C
unevenly, if one of its sides contains noticeably more of
C
than the other. If
s
has this property with respect to every cluster in
V\!
, let us call it a
bottleneck partition
of
V\!
.

The idea behind tangles, now, is to offer axiomatic definitions of both ‘bottleneck partitions’ and ‘consistent’ that do not refer to any pre-conceived clusters but still bear out this idea: definitions such that most concrete examples of a set
C
of points in a dataset
V\!
which most people would intuitively see as a cluster are divided unevenly by those bipartitions of
V\!
which the axioms designate as ‘bottleneck partitions’, and hence orient them, and such that the orientations of the bottleneck separations designated as ‘consistent’ are those induced in this way by some intuitively perceived cluster. With such axiomatic definitions of bottleneck partitions, and of consistency for their collective orientations, in place, a
tangle
is then defined as a consistent orientation of all the bottleneck partitions.

In graphs, there are some natural notions of bottleneck partitions. For example, one could take the vertex bipartitions that are crossed by few edges.
1
1

1

Tangles orienting these are known as
edge-tangles
of the graph. Its
vertex-tangles
, those considered by Robertson and Seymour, orient bipartitions of the edge set, the bottleneck partitions being those for which few vertices are incident with edges on both sides.

For bipartitions of an arbitrary data set it is not so clear how to identify a set of bipartitions that will split any perceived cluster unevenly, and can hence serve as ‘bottleneck partitions’, without referring to such clusters. Indeed one of the challenges in defining tangles in such a general setting lies in coming up with a notion of bottleneck partition that satisfies the requirements of tangle theory, such as submodularity.

In a typical clustering application this is facilitated by the existence of a natural
similarity function

\sigma\colon V^{2}\to\mathbb{N}
, which assigns to a pair
(u,v)
of points a large value if these points are deemed to be similar and should therefore be clustered together. For points in the Euclidean plane, for example, this could be the multiplicative inverse of their Euclidean distance. Note that although any such function appeals to some extrinsic notion of similarity of pairs of points, assuming the existence of such a function is much weaker than knowing how to cluster them, at least in the sense of partitioning
V\!
into ‘groups of similar points’. Indeed the rationale of tangles is that they offer a way to cluster
V\!
without the need to come up with such a partition.

However, tangles give rise to a ‘fractional’ such partition, which assigns every point

to every tangle

We begin in Section
2
by recalling the fundamental terminology and instances of separation systems. More details about separation systems, and in particular about their tangles, can be looked up in

Section

Moreover this correspondence is in some sense idempotent in that, if we go from a tangle of

is the set of items in the shop and

In Section
5
, finally, we note that the duality of set partitions can be cast in an algebraic framework, by defining a natural boundary operator on the oriented bipartitions of a given set. It then becomes an instance of algebraic duality, as between the homology and cohomology defined by this boundary operator. This gives rise to a host of questions that can be addressed purely algebraically. This is done in
[
7
]
, while questions that are more directly related to clustering and tangles are addressed here. These include the introduction of an inner product of set partitions, whose associated notion of orthogonality agrees with geometric orthogonality in certain simple cases. As this inner product is defined for arbitrary finite set partitions, such as in our online shop example, it might therefore deserve further study.

Basic terminology

Throughout this paper, whenever we speak of ‘partitions’ of a set we shall mean bipartitions, that is, partitions into two disjoint subsets. These subsets are allowed to be empty, although sometimes we may require that they are not.

3.4
The algebraic view

In Section
5
we shall point out that the duality defined in Section
3.2
is an instance of the algebraic duality familiar from algebra or topology. Very roughly, we may consider as a boundary operator the map that sends an oriented separation of a set
V\!
to the formal sum of the of elements of
V\!
to which it points minus the others. Then the associated coboundary operator performs the dual operation of mapping the elements of
V\!
to (co-)chains of oriented separations in a way that the two maps commute, just as in our online shop example.

As we shall note in Section
5.2
, our boundary operator gives rise to an inner product on the separations of
V\!
modulo its kernel. The notion of orthogonality associated with this inner product coincides with geometric orthogonality if our set
V\!
is the unit disc and our separations are the partitions of
V\!
given by straight lines through the origin. But it is defined for all finite set separations, and thus adds an interesting geometric perspective to their study beyond this particular example.

4
Tangles from dual separation systems

In our running example of an online shop, we have found that each element

gives rise naturally to a partition of
V\!
, and vice versa. However, these are not the only set of partitions, or separations, of

and

In order to apply the main tools and theorems of tangle theory to our given sets of partitions of

and

[
5
]

or
P
that have order less than some constant

with respect to some submodular order function.

It will turn out that the dual nature of our given

and

will allow us to define quite a natural order function on all the separations of

and
P
, respectively, which we will prove is submodular. This order function is qualitatively similar to, but not identical to (see
[
2
]
), the order function described in the introduction.

We can then study the tangle structure of the set of separations of

or

of order less than

. We will show that the correspondence mentioned in the introduction between arbitrary partitions of

and

and

4.1
Tangles on the sides of a bipartite graph

Let us make the discussion from the start of this section more precise. Suppose that we have a dual pair of separation systems

and

, whose duality is witnessed by a bipartite graph

with partition classes

and

as in Section

Let us denote by

with

Then the structure of the bipartite graph

\vec{S}(X)
to the separations in
\vec{S}(Y)

\vec{S}(X)
induces a separation in

We call

Similarly,

9

9

Similarly, the map

\prescript{\triangleright}{}{(}\cdot)\colon 2^{\vec{S}(X)}\!\to 2^{\vec{S}(Y)}

\prescript{\triangleright}{}{\tau}:=\{(C,D)\in\vec{S}(Y)\colon(C,D)^{\triangleleft}\in\tau\}\subseteq\vec{S}(Y).

\vec{S}(Y)

\vec{S}(Y)

Explicitly, let us define the order function
\left|\cdot\right|_{X}\colon\vec{S}(X)\to\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{N}
where

\left|A,B\right|_{X}:=\sum_{y\in Y}\left(\min\{\left|N(y)\cap A\right|,\left|N(y)\cap B\right|\}-\left|N(y)\cap A\cap B\right|/2\right).

Here the first term will be larger when
N(y)
is more evenly split by
(A,B)
.

The extra term of
-\left|N(y)\cap A\cap B\right|/2
is to adjust for double-counting: We can think of the term
\min\{\left|N(y)\cap A\right|,\left|N(y)\cap B\right|\}
as counting the number of edges from
y
to the smaller of
N(y)\cap A
and
N(y)\cap B
. However then, if
x
is contained in both
A
and
B
, we would count all the edges incident with
x
in
\sum_{y\in Y}\min\{\left|N(y)\cap A\right|,\left|N(y)\cap B\right|\}
, and so moving
x
out of
A
or
B
would not increase the order even if it made the separation more balanced. With the extra term
-\left|N(y)\cap A\cap B\right|/2
however, a neighbour
x
in
A\cap B
is treated as lying half in
A
and half in
B
, in the sense that it is counted with a factor of
\frac{1}{2}
in
N(y)
.

Similarly, we define
\left|\cdot\right|_{Y}\colon\vec{S}(Y)\to\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{N}
where

\left|C,D\right|_{Y}:=\sum_{x\in X}\left(\min\{\left|N(x)\cap C\right|,\left|N(x)\cap D\right|\}-\left|N(x)\cap C\cap D\right|/2\right).

Note that these functions are symmetric and non-negative, as required of an order function for separation systems.
Moreover, the function
\left|\cdot\right|_{X}
attains its maximum value on the separation
(X,X)
, and since orientations of all of
\vec{S}(X)
are not enlightening, we will in the following assume implicitly that any
\vec{S}_{k}(X)
we consider does not contain the separation
(X,X)
.

Less obviously, these order functions are submodular, and so
\vec{S}(X)
and
\vec{S}(Y)
equipped with these functions are submodular universes.
Submodularity is a fundamental property for order functions at the heart of tangle theory, and so we include the proof even though it is straightforward. However, the reader is invited to skip the proofs of the next two lemmas at first reading, to remain with the flow of the narrative.

Lemma 4.1
.

The order function
\left|\cdot\right|_{X}
is submodular.

Proof.

We show that
\left|\cdot\right|_{X}
is a sum of submodular functions. For this consider, for
y\in Y\!
, the order function on
\vec{S}(X)
given by

\left|A,B\right|_{y}:=\min\{\left|N(y)\cap A\right|,\left|N(y)\cap B\right|\}-\left|N(y)\cap A\cap B\right|/2

and note that
\left|A,B\right|_{X}=\sum_{y\in Y}\left|A,B\right|_{y}
, thus it is enough to show that
\left|\cdot\right|_{y}
is submodular for every
y\in Y\!
. Fix some
y
in
Y\!
. For
Z\subseteq X
we denote
N_{Z}:=\left|N(y)\cap Z\right|
.

Let
(A_{1},B_{1})
and
(A_{2},B_{2})
be separations in
\vec{S}(X)
, and suppose without loss of generality that
N_{A_{i}}\leq N_{B_{i}}
. Let
A_{i}^{\prime}:=A_{i}\smallsetminus B_{i}
,
B_{i}^{\prime}:=B_{i}\smallsetminus A_{i}
and
Z_{i}:=A_{i}\cap B_{i}
. Note that
\left|A_{i},B_{i}\right|_{y}=N_{A_{i}^{\prime}}+N_{Z_{i}}/2
.

We observe that

\left|A_{1}\cap A_{2},B_{1}\cup B_{2}\right|_{y}=N_{A_{1}^{\prime}\cap A_{2}^{\prime}}+\frac{1}{2}(N_{Z_{1}\cap Z_{2}}+N_{Z_{1}\cap A_{2}^{\prime}}+N_{A_{1}^{\prime}\cap Z_{2}}),

and

\left|A_{1}\cup A_{2},B_{1}\cap B_{2}\right|_{y}=\min\{N_{A_{1}^{\prime}\cup A_{2}^{\prime}},N_{B_{1}^{\prime}\cap B_{2}^{\prime}}\}+\frac{1}{2}(N_{Z_{1}\cap Z_{2}}+N_{Z_{1}\cap B_{2}^{\prime}}+N_{B_{1}^{\prime}\cap Z_{2}}).

Summing these two, we get

\displaystyle\left|A_{1}\cap A_{2},B_{1}\cup B_{2}\right|_{y}+\left|A_{1}\cup A_{2},B_{1}\cap B_{2}\right|_{y}

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle N_{A_{1}^{\prime}\cap A_{2}^{\prime}}+\frac{1}{2}(N_{Z_{1}\cap Z_{2}}+N_{Z_{1}\cap A_{2}^{\prime}}+N_{A_{1}^{\prime}\cap Z_{2}})

\displaystyle+{}

\displaystyle\min\{N_{A_{1}^{\prime}\cup A_{2}^{\prime}},N_{B_{1}^{\prime}\cap B_{2}^{\prime}}\}+\frac{1}{2}(N_{Z_{1}\cap Z_{2}}+N_{Z_{1}\cap B_{2}^{\prime}}+N_{B_{1}^{\prime}\cap Z_{2}})

\displaystyle\leq{}

\displaystyle N_{A_{1}^{\prime}\cap A_{2}^{\prime}}+N_{A_{1}^{\prime}\cup A_{2}^{\prime}}

\displaystyle+{}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}(N_{Z_{1}\cap Z_{2}}+N_{Z_{1}\cap A_{2}^{\prime}}+N_{Z_{1}\cap B_{2}^{\prime}}+N_{Z_{1}\cap Z_{2}}+N_{A_{1}^{\prime}\cap Z_{2}}+N_{B_{1}^{\prime}\cap Z_{2}})

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle N_{A_{1}^{\prime}}+N_{A_{2}^{\prime}}+\frac{1}{2}(N_{Z_{1}}+N_{Z_{2}})

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\left|A_{1},B_{1}\right|_{y}+\left|A_{2},B_{2}\right|_{y}.

Similarly,

\left|A_{1}\cap B_{2},B_{1}\cup A_{2}\right|_{y}=\min\{N_{A_{1}^{\prime}\cap B_{2}^{\prime}},N_{B_{1}^{\prime}\cup A_{2}^{\prime}}\}+\frac{1}{2}(N_{Z_{1}\cap Z_{2}}+N_{Z_{1}\cap B_{2}^{\prime}}+N_{A_{1}^{\prime}\cap Z_{2}}).

and

\left|A_{1}\cup B_{2},B_{1}\cap A_{2}\right|_{y}=\min\{N_{A_{1}^{\prime}\cup B_{2}^{\prime}},N_{B_{1}^{\prime}\cap A_{2}^{\prime}}\}+\frac{1}{2}(N_{Z_{1}\cap Z_{2}}+N_{Z_{1}\cap A_{2}^{\prime}}+N_{B_{1}^{\prime}\cap Z_{2}}).

Summing these two, we get

\displaystyle\left|A_{1}\cap B_{2},B_{1}\cup A_{2}\right|_{y}+\left|A_{1}\cup B_{2},B_{1}\cap A_{2}\right|_{y}

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\min\{N_{A_{1}^{\prime}\cap B_{2}^{\prime}},N_{B_{1}^{\prime}\cup A_{2}^{\prime}}\}+\frac{1}{2}(N_{Z_{1}\cap Z_{2}}+N_{Z_{1}\cap B_{2}^{\prime}}+N_{A_{1}^{\prime}\cap Z_{2}})

\displaystyle+{}

\displaystyle\min\{N_{A_{1}^{\prime}\cup B_{2}^{\prime}},N_{B_{1}^{\prime}\cap A_{2}^{\prime}}\}+\frac{1}{2}(N_{Z_{1}\cap Z_{2}}+N_{Z_{1}\cap A_{2}^{\prime}}+N_{B_{1}^{\prime}\cap Z_{2}})

\displaystyle\leq{}

\displaystyle N_{A_{1}^{\prime}\cap B_{2}^{\prime}}+N_{A_{2}^{\prime}\cap B_{1}^{\prime}}

\displaystyle+{}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}(N_{Z_{1}\cap Z_{2}}+N_{Z_{1}\cap A_{2}^{\prime}}+N_{Z_{1}\cap B_{2}^{\prime}}+N_{Z_{1}\cap Z_{2}}+N_{A_{1}^{\prime}\cap Z_{2}}+N_{B_{1}^{\prime}\cap Z_{2}})

\displaystyle\leq{}

\displaystyle N_{A_{1}^{\prime}}+N_{A_{2}^{\prime}}+\frac{1}{2}(N_{Z_{1}}+N_{Z_{2}})

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\left|A_{1},B_{1}\right|_{y}+\left|A_{2},B_{2}\right|_{y}.

Thus
\left|\cdot\right|_{y}
is submodular and so is
\left|\cdot\right|_{X}=\sum_{y\in Y}\left|\cdot\right|_{y}

∎

Next, we show that the shifting operation does not increase the order of a separation.
For this we first show the following lemma, giving an alternative representation of the order function:

Lemma 4.2
.

For all
(A,B)\in\vec{S}(X)
we have

\left|A,B\right|_{X}=\left|E(A_{B}^{\triangleright},B)\right|+\left|E(B_{A}^{\triangleright},A)\right|-\left|E(A_{B}^{\triangleright}\cap B_{A}^{\triangleright},X)\right|/2-\left|E(Y,A\cap B)\right|/2.

Proof.

This can be calculated by rearranging sums:

\displaystyle\left|A,B\right|_{X}

\displaystyle={}\sum_{y\in Y}\left(\min\{\left|N(y)\cap A\right|,\left|N(y)\cap B\right|\}-\frac{\left|N(y)\cap A\cap B\right|}{2}\right)

\displaystyle={}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}y\in Y\\
\left|N(y)\cap A\right|\geq\left|N(y)\cap B\right|\end{subarray}}\left|N(y)\cap B\right|+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}y\in Y\\
\left|N(y)\cap B\right|\geq\left|N(y)\cap A\right|\end{subarray}}\left|N(y)\cap A\right|

\displaystyle\qquad-\Big(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}y\in Y\\
\left|N(y)\cap A\right|=\left|N(y)\cap B\right|\end{subarray}}\frac{\left|N(y)\right|}{2}+\sum_{y\in Y}\frac{\left|N(y)\cap A\cap B\right|}{2}\Big)

\displaystyle={}\left|E(A_{B}^{\triangleright},B)\right|+\left|E(B_{A}^{\triangleright},A)\right|-\left|E(A_{B}^{\triangleright}\cap B_{A}^{\triangleright},X)\right|/2-\left|E(Y,A\cap B)\right|/2.\qed

With this we can now prove that shifting a separation indeed cannot increase
the order of a separation:

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
(A,B)
be a separation of
X
, then
\left|A,B\right|_{X}\geq\left|A^{\triangleright}_{B},B_{A}^{\triangleright}\right|_{Y}
. Similarly if
(C,D)
is a
separation of
Y\!
, then
\left|C,D\right|_{Y}\geq\left|C^{\triangleleft}_{D},D^{\triangleleft}_{C}\right|_{X}
.

Proof.

This is true by the following calculation:

\displaystyle\left|A_{B}^{\triangleright},B_{A}^{\triangleright}\right|_{Y}

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\sum_{x\in X}\left(\min\{\left|N(x)\cap A_{B}^{\triangleright},N(x)\cap B_{A}^{\triangleright}\right|\}-\left|N(x)\cap A_{B}^{\triangleright}\cap B_{A}^{\triangleright}\right|/2\right)

\displaystyle\leq{}

\displaystyle\sum_{a\in A}\left|N(a)\cap B_{A}^{\triangleright}\right|+\sum_{b\in B}\left|N(b)\cap A_{B}^{\triangleright}\right|-\sum_{x\in A\cap B}\left|N(x)\right|/2-\sum_{y\in A_{B}^{\triangleright}\cap B_{A}^{\triangleright}}\left|N(y)\right|/2

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\sum_{b\in B_{A}^{\triangleright}}\left|N(b)\cap A\right|+\sum_{a\in A_{B}^{\triangleright}}\left|N(a)\cap B\right|-\sum_{y\in A_{B}^{\triangleright}\cap B_{A}^{\triangleright}}\left|N(y)\right|/2-\sum_{x\in A\cap B}\left|N(x)\right|/2

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle E(A_{B}^{\triangleright},B)+E(B_{A}^{\triangleright},A)-E(A_{B}^{\triangleright}\cap B_{A}^{\triangleright},X)/2-E(Y,A\cap B)/2

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\left|A,B\right|_{X}.\qed

Finally, in order to define the tangles of
\vec{S}(X)
and
\vec{S}(Y)
we need to define the notion of
consistency
that we require our orientations to satisfy. There are a few natural choices that one could make here, however in most contexts it turns out that these definitions are in some sense weakly equivalent, in that tangles under any one definition tend to induce tangles of slightly lower order under the other definitions.

With that in mind, let us define a
tangle of
\vec{S}_{k}(X)
(in
G
)
as an orientation
\tau
of
\vec{S}_{k}(X)
which satisfies the following property:

\textit{There are no }(A_{1},B_{1}),(A_{2},B_{2}),(A_{3},B_{3})\in\tau\textit{ with }A_{1}\cup A_{2}\cup A_{3}=X.

We define tangles of
\vec{S}_{k}(Y)
in
G
in a similar manner. This is perhaps the simplest definition to take, and is a direct analogue of the corresponding notion of ‘consistency’ used to define tangles in matroids. We will discuss later in more detail the extent to which our results hold for tangles defined in terms of other notions of ‘consistency.’

We are now ready to state the main results of this section. We will show that, with the aid of this order function, we can relate the tangles of
\vec{S}(X)
to those of
\vec{S}(Y)
.

Theorem 4.4
.

Let
\tau
be a tangle of
\vec{S}_{4k}(X)
, then
\tau^{\prime}:=\prescript{\triangleright}{}{\tau}\cap\vec{S}_{k}(Y)
is a tangle of
\vec{S}_{k}(Y)
.

By symmetry, we then obtain a similar conclusion as in
4.4
when we shift a
4k
-tangle of
Y\!
.

A natural question then to ask at this point, is, even if the shifting operations themselves are not idempotent, whether the operation they induce on tangles is in some way ‘idempotent’: That is, if we shift a tangle twice, do we end up with the original tangle? It turns out that, again up to a constant factor, this is indeed the case.

Theorem 4.5
.

Let
\tau
be a tangle of
\vec{S}_{16k}(X)
, let
\tau^{\prime}=\prescript{\triangleright}{}{\tau}\cap\vec{S}_{4k}(Y)
, and let
\tau^{\prime\prime}=\prescript{\triangleleft}{}{\tau}^{\prime}\cap\vec{S}_{k}(X)
. Then
\tau^{\prime\prime}\subseteq\tau
.

It is possible to prove Theorems
4.4
and
4.5
directly. However, there is perhaps a more interesting way to prove them indirectly, which potentially gives slightly more illumination to the connection between these two types of tangles, albeit at the cost of a slight increase in the factors of
k
. The idea is to view the tangles of the two partition classes as two different facets of tangles on the edge set of the bipartite graph.

We give these proofs in the next section. Direct proofs of Theorems
4.4
and
4.5
are given in the extended version of this paper
[
3
]
.

4.2
Tangles of the edges

We will show that the tangles on the sides of a bipartite graph are closely related to a third type of tangle - one defined on the separations of the edges. Let us briefly introduce some new notation for this purpose.

We denote the set of all separations of
E
, the edge set of our bipartite graph, as
\vec{S}(E)
, and the set of the corresponding unoriented separations as
S(E)
.
The following order function on the separations in
\vec{S}(E)
is a natural variation on our previous order function for separations in
\vec{S}(X)
:

\left|C,D\right|_{E}:=\sum_{v\in V}\left(\min(\left|E(v)\cap C\right|,\left|E(v)\cap D\right|)-\left|E(v)\cap C\cap D\right|/2\right),

where
E(v)
denotes the set of incident edges of
v
.
We will again assume that any
\vec{S}_{k}(E)
we consider does not contain
(E,E)
.

We say that an orientation
\tau
of a subset
\vec{S}_{k}(E)
of
\vec{S}(E)
is a
tangle
of
\vec{S}_{k}(E)
, if
\tau
is an orientation of
\vec{S}_{k}(E)
with the following property:

\textit{There are no }(C_{1},D_{1}),(C_{2},D_{2}),(C_{3},D_{3})\in\tau\textit{ with }C_{1}\cup C_{2}\cup C_{3}=E.

Given a separation in
\vec{S}(X)
, there is a reasonably natural candidate for a separation in
\vec{S}(E)
which it ‘induces’: A separation
(A,B)
of
X
naturally defines a separation
(A,B)^{E}:=(E(A),E(B))
of
E
, where
E(A)
denotes the set of all edges of
G
which have an end vertex in
A
. Note that
((A,B)^{E})^{\ast}=(B,A)^{E}
.

The other way around is less obvious, but it will be necessary to associate to each separation in
\vec{S}(E)
a separation in
\vec{S}(X)
and
\vec{S}(Y)
. We will do so similarly to how we associated to each separation in
\vec{S}(Y)
a separation in
\vec{S}(X)
. There we obtained, given a separation
(A,B)\in\vec{S}(Y)
, a separation in
\vec{S}(X)
by asking for every vertex in
X
whether that vertex has more neighbours in
A
or in
B
. In a similar manner we will now ask, given a separation
(C,D)
in
\vec{S}(E)
, for each vertex in
X
whether more of the adjacent edges lie in
C
or in
D
. Formally, given a separation
(C,D)
of
E
, we obtain a separation
(C,D)^{\blacktriangleleft}:=(C^{\blacktriangleleft}_{D},D^{\blacktriangleleft}_{C})
of
X
by defining

C^{\blacktriangleleft}_{D}=\{x\in X\>:\>\left|E(x)\cap C\right|\geq\left|E(x)\cap D\right|\}

and

D^{\blacktriangleleft}_{C}=\{x\in X\>:\>\left|E(x)\cap C\right|\leq\left|E(x)\cap D\right|\}.

In an analogous manner we can define a separation
(C,D)^{\blacktriangleright}
of
Y\!
, however by the symmetry of the situation we will only ever need to talk about the map
(\cdot)^{\blacktriangleleft}
.

Now this shifting operation on the separations preserves the partial order of separations in the following sense:

Lemma 4.6
.

If
(C,D)\leq(C^{\prime},D^{\prime})
, then
(C,D)^{\blacktriangleleft}\leq(C^{\prime},D^{\prime})^{\blacktriangleleft}

Proof.

If
(C,D)\leq(C^{\prime},D^{\prime})
, then
C\subseteq C^{\prime}
and
D\supseteq D^{\prime}
. Thus, for
x\in X
, we have that
\left|E(x)\cap C\right|\leq\left|E(x)\cap C^{\prime}\right|
and
\left|E(x)\cap D\right|\geq\left|E(x)\cap D^{\prime}\right|
.

Now if
x\in C^{\blacktriangleleft}_{D}
, then
\left|E(x)\cap C\right|\geq\left|E(x)\cap D\right|
and thus

\left|E(x)\cap C^{\prime}\right|\geq\left|E(x)\cap C\right|\geq\left|E(x)\cap D\right|\geq\left|E(x)\cap D^{\prime}\right|,

hence
x\in C^{\prime\blacktriangleleft}_{D^{\prime}}
. Similarly, if
x\in D^{\prime\blacktriangleleft}_{C^{\prime}}
, then
\left|E(x)\cap D^{\prime}\right|\geq\left|E(x)\cap C^{\prime}\right|
and thus

\left|E(x)\cap D\right|\geq\left|E(x)\cap D^{\prime}\right|\geq\left|E(x)\cap C^{\prime}\right|\geq\left|E(x)\cap C\right|,

hence
x\in C^{\blacktriangleleft}_{D}
. Thus
C^{\blacktriangleleft}_{D}\subseteq C^{\prime\blacktriangleleft}_{D^{\prime}}
and
D^{\prime\blacktriangleleft}_{C^{\prime}}\subseteq D^{\blacktriangleleft}_{C}
, i.e.
(C,D)^{\blacktriangleleft}\leq(C^{\prime},D^{\prime})^{\blacktriangleleft}
.
∎

Unlike the shifting operations considered in the previous section, there is less of a symmetry here: The separation
(A,B)^{E}
fully determines the separation
(A,B)
, whereas the separation
(C,D)^{\blacktriangleleft}
in some way ‘compresses’ the information in the separation
(C,D)
into a rough estimate. Generally there are multiple different separations
(C,D)
in
\vec{S}(E)
for which the
(C,D)^{\blacktriangleleft}
coincide, and so the operation
(\cdot)^{\blacktriangleleft}
is not injective.

As with
(\cdot)^{\triangleright}
, this function induces a pullback map:
given a subset
\tau
of
\vec{S}(X)
, we define

\tau_{E}:=\{(C,D)\>:\>(C,D)^{\blacktriangleleft}\in\tau\}.

Note that, as
(E(A),E(B))^{\blacktriangleleft}=(A,B)
, the set of all the separations
(A,B)^{E}
is a subset of
\tau_{E}
.

For shifting in the other direction we take a slightly different notion. Given a subset
\tau
of
\vec{S}(E)
, let us define

\tau_{X}:=\{(C,D)^{\blacktriangleleft}\>:\>(C,D)\in\tau\},

and let
\tau_{Y}
be defined analogously.
Note that this is a genuinely different way to move between tangles of
\vec{S}(E)
and
\vec{S}(X)
; rather than ‘pulling back’ the tangle from
\vec{S}(X)
to
\vec{S}(E)
via the shift
(\cdot)^{E}
, giving rise to a set of separations

\prescript{X}{}{\tau}:=\{(A,B)\in\vec{S}(X)\>:\>(A,B)^{E}\in\tau\},

we’re ‘pushing forward’ via the shift
(\cdot)^{\blacktriangleleft}
.

We note that in this particular case, since, assuming the graph is connected, it is clear that
\left((A,B)^{E}\right)^{\blacktriangleleft}=(A,B)
, we have that
\tau_{X}\supseteq\prescript{X}{}{\tau}
and so, since
\prescript{X}{}{\tau}
is automatically a partial orientation of
\vec{S}(X)
, if the restriction of
\tau_{X}
to some lower order is a tangle, then the restriction of
\prescript{X}{}{\tau}
to the same order will also satisfy (
4.1
). In particular, working with this definition results in slightly stronger results than working with
\prescript{X}{}{\tau}
, however the main purpose of this change is that it will result in slightly simpler proofs, see for example
4.15
.

We will show that given a tangle
\tau
of
\vec{S}_{4k}(X)
, the set
\tau_{E}\cap\vec{S}_{k}(E)
is in fact a tangle of
\vec{S}_{k}(E)
and dually, given a tangle
\tau
is of
\vec{S}_{2k}(E)
, the set
\tau_{X}\cap\vec{S}_{k}(X)
is a tangle of
\vec{S}_{k}(X)
. We will then be able to use this to obtain proofs of
4.4
and
4.5
from the symmetry between
X
and
Y\!
.

We note first that, as is the case for
\left|\cdot\right|_{X}
and
\left|\cdot\right|_{Y}
, the order function
\left|\cdot\right|_{E}
is submodular.

However, since we will not use this fact, and its proof is almost identical to that of Lemma
4.1
, the proof is included only in the extended version of the paper.

Lemma 4.7
.

The order function
\left|\cdot\right|_{E}
is submodular.

However, unlike for the correspondence between
\left|\cdot\right|_{X}
and
\left|\cdot\right|_{Y}
, we will no longer be able to show that the order of the shift of a separation is non-increasing, instead we will only be able to show that, when shifting from a separation of the vertices to the corresponding separation of the edges, we can bound how much the order increases. More precisely, simple calculations show that:

Proposition 4.8
.

Given a separation
(A,B)
of
X
, we have

\left|A,B\right|_{X}\leq\left|(A,B)^{E}\right|_{E}
and
\left|(A,B)^{E}\right|_{E}\leq 2\left|A,B\right|_{X}
.

Proof.

For the first statement we note that:

\displaystyle\left|A,B\right|_{X}={}

\displaystyle\sum_{y\in Y}\left(\min\{\left|N(y)\cap A\right|,\left|N(y)\cap B\right|\}-\left|N(y)\cap A\cap B\right|/2\right)

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\sum_{y\in Y}\left(\min\{\left|E(y)\cap E(A)\right|,\left|E(y)\cap E(B)\right|\}-\left|E(y)\cap E(A)\cap E(B)\right|/2\right)

\displaystyle\leq{}

\displaystyle\sum_{v\in V}\left(\min\{\left|E(v)\cap E(A)\right|,\left|E(v)\cap E(B)\right|\}-\left|E(v)\cap E(A)\cap E(B)\right|/2\right)

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\left|E(A),E(B)\right|_{E}

For the second statement we observe that, for
x\in X
we have, since
x\in A
or
x\in B
, that

\min\{\left|E(x)\cap E(A)\right|,\left|E(x)\cap E(B)\right|\}=\left|E(x)\cap E(A)\cap E(B)\right|

and thus

\sum_{x\in X}\left(\min\{\left|E(x)\cap E(A)\right|,\left|E(x)\cap E(B)\right|\}-\left|E(x)\cap E(A)\cap E(B)\right|/2\right)=\left|E(A)\cap E(B)\right|/2.

As clearly
\left|A,B\right|_{X}\geq\left|E(A)\cap E(B)\right|/2
it follows that

\displaystyle\left|E(A),E(B)\right|_{E}

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\sum_{v\in V}\left(\min\{\left|E(v)\cap E(A)\right|,\left|E(v)\cap E(B)\right|\}-\left|E(v)\cap E(A)\cap E(B)\right|/2\right)

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\sum_{x\in X}\left(\min\{\left|E(x)\cap E(A)\right|,\left|E(x)\cap E(B)\right|\}-\left|E(x)\cap E(A)\cap E(B)\right|/2\right)

\displaystyle+\sum_{y\in Y}\left(\min\{\left|E(y)\cap E(A)\right|,\left|E(y)\cap E(B)\right|\}-\left|E(y)\cap E(A)\cap E(B)\right|/2\right)

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\left|E(A)\cap E(B)\right|/2+\sum_{y\in Y}\left(\min\{\left|N(y)\cap A\right|,\left|N(y)\cap B\right|\}-\left|N(y)\cap A\cap B\right|/2\right)

\displaystyle\leq{}

\displaystyle 2\left|A,B\right|_{X}\qed

For
(\cdot)^{\blacktriangleleft}
on the other hand, we will be able to show that this is a non-increasing operation:

Lemma 4.9
.

Let
(C,D)
be a separation of
E
, then
\left|C,D\right|_{E}\geq\left|C^{\blacktriangleleft}_{D},D_{C}^{\blacktriangleleft}\right|_{X}
.

For the proof of
4.9
we will need to carefully analyse how we can ‘locally’ change a separation in
\vec{S}(E)
without changing the shift.
Recall that, given a separation
(A,B)
in
\vec{S}(X)
, there are other separations apart from
(A,B)^{E}
in
\vec{S}(E)
which still shift to
(A,B)
. So, in order to prove
4.9
we will analyse what these different separations of
E
inducing the same separation
(A,B)
of
X
look like.
For this, we will show which ’local’, i.e. single-edge, changes we can make to a given separation
(C,D)
to bring it closer to one of the type
(A,B)^{E}
, without increasing its order.

So, let us start analysing these ‘local’ changes.
Firstly, in the next lemma we show that we can move a single edge from
C
to
D
without increasing the order of
(C,D)
or changing its shift
(C,D)^{\blacktriangleleft}
, if at the end vertex in
X
of that edge there are fewer incident edges in
C
than in
D
.

Lemma 4.10
.

Let
(C,D)
be a separation of
E
and let
e\in E
be incident with
C^{\blacktriangleleft}_{D}\smallsetminus D^{\blacktriangleleft}_{C}
. Then
\left|C\cup\{e\},D\smallsetminus\{e\}\right|_{E}\leq\left|C,D\right|_{E}
and
{(C\cup\{e\},D\smallsetminus\{e\})^{\blacktriangleleft}=(C,D)^{\blacktriangleleft}}
.

Proof.

We may suppose that
e\in D
, as otherwise there is nothing to show. Let
e=vw
. We observe that, since
v\in C_{D}^{\blacktriangleleft}\smallsetminus D_{C}^{\blacktriangleleft}
, we have

\displaystyle\min(\left|E(v)\cap C\right|,\left|E(v)\cap D\right|)-\left|E(v)\cap C\cap D\right|/2

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left|E(v)\cap D\right|-\left|E(v)\cap C\cap D\right|/2

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\left|E(v)\cap(D\smallsetminus\{e\})\right|-\left|E(v)\cap(C\cup\{e\})\cap(D\smallsetminus\{e\})\right|/2+1,

and

\displaystyle\min(\left|E(w)\cap C\right|,\left|E(w)\cap D\right|)-\left|E(w)\cap C\cap D\right|/2

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\min(\left|E(w)\cap(D\smallsetminus\{e\})\right|,\left|E(w)\cap(C\cup\{e\})\right|)-\left|E(w)\cap(C\cup\{e\})\cap(D\smallsetminus\{e\})\right|/2-1.

Thus
\left|C\cup\{e\},D\smallsetminus\{e\}\right|_{E}\leq\left|C,D\right|_{E}
. Moreover,
v\in C^{\blacktriangleleft}_{D}\smallsetminus D_{C}^{\blacktriangleleft}
and therefore also
v\in(C\cup\{e\})_{D\smallsetminus\{e\}}^{\blacktriangleleft}\smallsetminus(D\smallsetminus\{e\})_{C\cup\{e\}}^{\blacktriangleleft}
and thus
(C,D)^{\blacktriangleleft}=(C\cup\{e\},D\smallsetminus\{e\})^{\blacktriangleleft}
.
∎

Using this, we can show that from within the set of those separations of
E
with the same shift
(A,B)
, we can always find some
(C,D)
of minimal order which is ‘close’ to
(A,B)^{E}
, in the sense that every edge incident with
A\smallsetminus B
is contained in
C\smallsetminus D
and every edge incident with
B\smallsetminus A
is contained in
D\smallsetminus C
:

Lemma 4.11
.

Let
(C,D)\in\vec{S}(E)
. Then there exists a separation
(C^{\prime},D^{\prime})
of
E
with
\left|C^{\prime},D^{\prime}\right|_{E}\leq\left|C,D\right|_{E}
and
(C^{\prime},D^{\prime})^{\blacktriangleleft}=(C,D)^{\blacktriangleleft}
such that every edge
e
incident with
C_{D}^{\blacktriangleleft}\smallsetminus D_{C}^{\blacktriangleleft}
lies in
C^{\prime}\smallsetminus D^{\prime}
and every edge incident with
D_{C}^{\blacktriangleleft}\smallsetminus C_{D}^{\blacktriangleleft}
lies in
D^{\prime}\smallsetminus C^{\prime}
.

Proof.

Suppose
(C^{\prime},D^{\prime})
is chosen such that
{(C^{\prime},D^{\prime})^{\blacktriangleleft}=(C,D)^{\blacktriangleleft}}
, such that the order of
(C^{\prime},D^{\prime})
is at most the order of
(C,D)
, i.e.
\left|C^{\prime},D^{\prime}\right|_{E}\leq\left|C,D\right|_{E}
, and such that there are as few edges as possible incident with
C_{D}^{\blacktriangleleft}\smallsetminus D_{C}^{\blacktriangleleft}
which do not lie in
C^{\prime}\smallsetminus D^{\prime}
and as few edges as possible incident with
D_{C}^{\blacktriangleleft}\smallsetminus C_{D}^{\blacktriangleleft}
which do not lie in
D^{\prime}\smallsetminus C^{\prime}
.

Suppose that there exists some such edge
e
incident with
C_{D}^{\blacktriangleleft}\smallsetminus D_{C}^{\blacktriangleleft}
which does not lie in
C^{\prime}\smallsetminus D^{\prime}
or some such edge
e
incident with
D_{C}^{\blacktriangleleft}\smallsetminus C_{D}^{\blacktriangleleft}
which does not lie in
D^{\prime}\smallsetminus C^{\prime}
. Let us assume we are in the former case, as the argument in the latter case is identical.

Since
(C^{\prime},D^{\prime})^{\blacktriangleleft}=(C,D)^{\blacktriangleleft}
, by
4.10
we could then consider the separation
(C^{\prime}\cup\{e\},D^{\prime}\smallsetminus\{e\})
which satisfies
{(C^{\prime}\cup\{e\},D^{\prime}\smallsetminus\{e\})^{\blacktriangleleft}=(C^{\prime},D^{\prime})^{\blacktriangleleft}}
and
{\left|C^{\prime}\cup\{e\},D^{\prime}\smallsetminus\{e\}\right|\leq\left|C^{\prime},D^{\prime}\right|\leq\left|C,D\right|}
, contradicting the choice of
(C^{\prime},D^{\prime})
.
∎

This observation enables us to perform the necessary calculations to prove
4.9
.

Proof of
4.9
.

By
4.11
we may suppose that every edge incident with
C_{D}^{\blacktriangleleft}\smallsetminus D_{C}^{\blacktriangleleft}
lies in
C\smallsetminus D
and every edge incident with
D_{C}^{\blacktriangleleft}\smallsetminus C_{D}^{\blacktriangleleft}
lies in
D\smallsetminus C
.

In this case, we can calculate
\left|C,D\right|_{E}
as follows.

\displaystyle\left|C,D\right|_{E}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{v\in V}\left(\min(\left|E(v)\cap C\right|,\left|E(v)\cap D\right|)-\left|E(v)\cap C\cap D\right|/2\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{v\in Y}\left(\min(\left|E(v)\cap C\right|,\left|E(v)\cap D\right|)-\left|E(v)\cap C\cap D\right|/2\right)+\sum_{v\in C_{D}^{\blacktriangleleft}\cap D_{C}^{\blacktriangleleft}}\frac{1}{2}\left|E(v)\right|

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\sum_{v\in Y}\left(\min(\left|N(v)\cap C_{D}^{\blacktriangleleft}\right|,\left|N(v)\cap D_{C}^{\blacktriangleleft}\right|)-\frac{1}{2}\left|N(v)\cap C_{D}^{\blacktriangleleft}\cap D_{C}^{\blacktriangleleft}\right|\right)

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\left|E(C_{D}^{\blacktriangleleft})\cap E(D_{C}^{\blacktriangleleft})\right|+\sum_{v\in C_{D}^{\blacktriangleleft}\cap D_{C}^{\blacktriangleleft}}\frac{1}{2}\left|N(v)\right|

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\sum_{v\in Y}\left(\min(\left|N(v)\cap C_{D}^{\blacktriangleleft}\right|,\left|N(v)\cap D_{C}^{\blacktriangleleft}\right|)-\left|N(v)\cap C_{D}^{\blacktriangleleft}\cap D_{C}^{\blacktriangleleft}\right|/2\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left|C^{\blacktriangleleft}_{D},D_{C}^{\blacktriangleleft}\right|_{X}.\qed

Analysing local changes will also play a crucial role in showing that, given a tangle
\tau
in
\vec{S}(E)
, the restriction of
\tau_{X}
to a lower order is actually an orientation. For this we will need to make sure that separations obtained from one another by local changes cannot be oriented differently in
\tau_{E}
. However, whereas
4.10
allows us to move certain edges from
C\smallsetminus D
to
D\smallsetminus C
without changing the shift or increasing the order, for showing that the restriction of
\tau_{X}
to a lower order actually is an orientation we will need to analyse a different type of local change.

More precisely, the next lemma will allow us to move certain edges from
D\smallsetminus C
(or, symmetrically,
C\smallsetminus D
), to
C\cap D
without increasing the order. Such an operation might change the shift of a separation, but, by
4.6
, it does so only in a controlled way: moving an edge from
D\smallsetminus C
to
D\cap C
will only result in a shift that is larger, in the sense of the partial order on the separation system, than the shift of the original
(C,D)
. Moreover, such a local change does not change the way a separation is oriented by a tangle.

Lemma 4.12
.

Let
(C,D)
be a separation of
E
and let
e\in E
be incident with
C_{D}^{\blacktriangleleft}
. Then
\left|C\cup\{e\},D\right|_{E}\leq\left|C,D\right|_{E}
and
{(C\cup\{e\},D)^{\blacktriangleleft}\geq(C,D)^{\blacktriangleleft}}
.

Proof.

If
e\in C
, then it is nothing to show, so suppose that
e\in D\smallsetminus C
. Let
e=vw
. We observe that, since
v\in C_{D}^{\blacktriangleleft}
, we have

\displaystyle\min(\left|E(v)\cap C\right|,\left|E(v)\cap D\right|)-\left|E(v)\cap C\cap D\right|/2

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left|E(v)\cap D\right|-\left|E(v)\cap C\cap D\right|/2

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left|E(v)\cap D\right|-\left|E(v)\cap(C\cup\{e\})\cap D\right|/2+\frac{1}{2}

and

\displaystyle\min(\left|E(w)\cap C\right|,\left|E(w)\cap D\right|)-\left|E(w)\cap C\cap D\right|/2

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\min(\left|E(w)\cap(C\cup\{e\})\right|,\left|E(w)\cap D\right|)-\left|E(w)\cap(C\cup\{e\})\cap D\right|/2-\frac{1}{2}.

Thus
\left|C\cup\{e\},D\right|_{E}\leq\left|C,D\right|_{E}
. We have
(C,D)^{\blacktriangleleft}\leq(C\cup\{e\},D)^{\blacktriangleleft}
by
4.6
.
∎

We now have all the ingredients at hand needed to show that the shift of a tangle, restricted to an appropriate order, is still a tangle. Let us start by considering the shift
\tau_{X}
of a tangle
\tau
of
\vec{S}(E)
.

Theorem 4.13
.

If
\tau
is a tangle of
\vec{S}_{2k}(E)
, then
\tau_{X}\cap\vec{S}_{k}(X)
is a tangle of
\vec{S}_{k}(X)
.

Proof.

We first note that the set
\tau_{X}\cap\vec{S}_{k}(X)
contains at least one of
(A,B)
and
(B,A)
for every separation
(A,B)\in\vec{S}_{k}(X)
. Indeed, by Proposition
4.8

\left|(A,B)^{E}\right|_{E}\leq 2\left|A,B\right|_{X}
, and so since
\tau
is a tangle of
\vec{S}_{2k}(E)
either
(A,B)^{E}\in\tau
or
(B,A)^{E}\in\tau
.

Let us now show that for no separation
\{A,B\}
we have both
(A,B)
and
(B,A)
in
\tau_{X}\cap\vec{S}_{k}(X)
.
Suppose otherwise, then
\tau
contains separations
(C_{1},D_{1})
and
(C_{2},D_{2})
such that
(C_{1},D_{1})^{\blacktriangleleft}=(A,B)
and
(C_{2},D_{2})^{\blacktriangleleft}=(B,A)
.

Note that, by
4.8
, we have that
{\left|E(A),E(B)\right|_{E}\leq 2\left|A,B\right|_{X}<2k}
, hence
(A,B)^{E}\in\tau
or
(B,A)^{E}\in\tau
.
As
(E(A),E(B))^{\blacktriangleleft}=(A,B)
, we may suppose without loss of generality that either
(C_{1},D_{1})=(A,B)^{E}
or
{(C_{2},D_{2})=(B,A)^{E}}
. We suppose the former one, the latter case is similar.

Now pick a separation
(C,D)\in\tau
such that
(C,D)^{\blacktriangleleft}\geq(C_{2},D_{2})^{\blacktriangleleft}=(B,A)
and the set
(D_{1}\cap D)\smallsetminus(C_{1}\cup C)
is as small as possible. Then, since
\tau
satisfies (
4.2
), we have
C_{1}\cup C\neq E
. Hence there exists some edge
e\in(D_{1}\cap D)\smallsetminus(C_{1}\cup C)
.

Let
x
be the end vertex of
e
in
X
. Note that
e\in E(B)\smallsetminus E(A)
since
E(A)=C_{1}
and
e\notin C_{1}
. Thus
x\in B\smallsetminus A
. Moreover, as
B={C_{2}}_{D_{2}}^{\blacktriangleleft}\subseteq C_{D}^{\blacktriangleleft}
, we have that
x\in C_{D}^{\blacktriangleleft}
.
Thus
e
is incident with
C_{D}^{\blacktriangleleft}
.

Consequently, we can apply
4.12
to get that
\left|C\cup\{e\},D\right|_{E}\leq\left|C,D\right|_{E}
. Thus
\tau
orients
(C\cup\{e\},D)
and therefore
(C\cup\{e\},D)\in\tau
, as
(D,C\cup\{e\})\in\tau
would contradict (
4.2
) because of
(C,D)\in\tau
and
D\cup C=E
.

But this implies that
(C\cup\{e\},D)\in\tau
is a better choice for
(C,D)
, since
(C\cup\{e\},D)^{\blacktriangleleft}\geq(C,D)^{\blacktriangleleft}
by
4.6
and

(D_{1}\cap D)\smallsetminus(C_{1}\cup C)\supsetneq(D_{1}\cap D)\smallsetminus(C_{1}\cup(C\cup\{e\}))

as

.

Thus
\tau_{X}\cap\vec{S}_{k}(X)
is indeed an orientation.
That

satisfies the tangle property (
4.1
) now follows like this: If

would be a triple in

contradicting the tangle property (
4.1
), then
\tau
would need to orient
(A_{1},B_{1})^{E}
,
(A_{2},B_{2})^{E}
and
(A_{3},B_{3})^{E}
by
4.8
. By the above observation

orients them as
(A_{1},B_{1})^{E}
,
(A_{2},B_{2})^{E}
and
(A_{3},B_{3})^{E}
, since
\tau_{X}\cap\vec{S}_{k}(X)
does not contain any

. However, the three separations
(A_{1},B_{1})^{E}
,
(A_{2},B_{2})^{E}
and

in

then contradict the tangle property (
4.2
), as every edge in

is incident with at least one of the sets

.
∎

A similar conclusion holds for the shift
\tau_{E}
of a tangle
\tau
of
\vec{S}_{4k}(X)
.

Theorem 4.14
.

Given a tangle
\tau
of
\vec{S}_{4k}(X)
, then
\tau_{E}\cap\vec{S}_{k}(E)
is a tangle of
\vec{S}_{k}(E)
.

Proof.

By
4.9
, given some separation
(C,D)\in\vec{S}_{k}(E)
we have that
\left|(C,D)^{\blacktriangleleft}\right|_{X}\leq\left|(C,D)\right|_{E}
, thus
\tau
contains exactly one of the two separations
(C,D)^{\blacktriangleleft}
and
{((C,D)^{\blacktriangleleft})^{\ast}=(D,C)^{\blacktriangleleft}}
, and consequently
\tau_{E}\cap\vec{S}_{k}(E)
contains exactly one of
(C,D)
and
(D,C)
, i.e.
\tau_{E}\cap\vec{S}_{k}(E)
is an orientation of
\vec{S}_{k}(E)
.

So, it remains to show that
\tau_{E}\cap\vec{S}_{k}(E)
satisfies the tangle property (
4.2
). Let us suppose for a contradiction that there is some set

\{(C_{1},D_{1}),(C_{2},D_{2}),(C_{3},D_{3})\}\subseteq\tau_{E}\cap\vec{S}_{k}(E)

such that

C_{1}\cup C_{2}\cup C_{3}=E
.

Let
(A_{i},B_{i})=(C_{i},D_{i})^{\blacktriangleleft}
for each
i=1,2,3
. Then, since
(A_{i},B_{i})\in\tau
for each
i
, and
\tau
is a tangle, it follows that the set

is non-empty.

Since
Z\subseteq B_{i}={D_{i}}^{\blacktriangleleft}_{C_{i}}
for each

, we have that
|E(z)\cap D_{i}|\geq|E(z)\cap C_{i}|
for all

and

. However, since
C_{1}\cup C_{2}\cup C_{3}=E
,

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{3}\left|C_{i},D_{i}\right|_{E}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{3}\sum_{v\in V}\left(\min\{\left|E(v)\cap C_{i}\right|,\left|E(v)\cap D_{i}\right|\}-\frac{1}{2}\left|E(v)\cap C_{i}\cap D_{i}\right|\right)

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{i=1}^{3}\sum_{z\in Z}\left(\min\{\left|E(z)\cap C_{i}\right|,\left|E(z)\cap D_{i}\right|\}-\frac{1}{2}\left|E(z)\cap C_{i}\cap D_{i}\right|\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{z\in Z}\sum_{i=1}^{3}\left(\left|E(z)\cap C_{i}\right|-\frac{1}{2}\left|E(z)\cap C_{i}\cap D_{i}\right|\right)

As

for every

\displaystyle\left|Z,X\right|_{X}=\left|E(Z,Y)\right|/2<3k.

Hence,
\tau
needs to orient

. As

), it follows that

.

Finally, since
\left|A_{3},B_{3}\right|_{X}\leq\left|C_{3},D_{3}\right|_{E}<k

\tau

) that

).

.

Corollary 4.17
.

Let

\vec{S}_{64k}(X)

.

, we have that

Moreover, again by

But now, by

and thus,

\displaystyle\tau^{\prime\prime}

\displaystyle=\left(\left(\left(\tau_{E}\cap\vec{S}_{16k}(E)\right)_{Y}\cap\vec{S}_{8k}(Y)\right)_{E}\cap\vec{S}_{2k}(E)\right)_{X}\cap\vec{S}_{k}(X)

\displaystyle\subseteq\left(\tau_{E}\cap\vec{S}_{16k}(E)\right)_{X}\cap\vec{S}_{k}(X).

we have that

\left(\tau_{E}\cap\vec{S}_{16k}(E)\right)_{X}\cap\vec{S}_{k}(X)\subseteq\tau,

which shows the claim.
∎

4.3
Variations, Generalisations and open problems

A natural question to consider at this point is how much these results depend on the very specific set up we have here.

For example, whilst we considered a very specific type of tangle, there are other types of ‘tangle-like’ clusters which one might wish to consider. Perhaps the most general condition one could consider here would be that of a
regular profile
. A
profile
of a set separation system is an orientation which neither contains any two separations pointing away from each other – i.e., if
{(A_{1},B_{1})\leq(A_{2},B_{2})}
for distinct separations
\{A_{1},B_{1}\},\{A_{2},B_{2}\}
then a profile cannot contain both,
(B_{1},A_{1})
and
(A_{2},B_{2})\in P
–
nor does it contain any triple of separations of the form

\{(A_{1},B_{1}),(A_{2},B_{2}),(B_{1}\cap B_{2},A_{1}\cup A_{2})\}.

A
regular
orientation is one which does not contain any cosmall separations, that is a separation
(V,B)
where
V\!
is the underlying set and
B\subseteq V\!
. Tangles are regular profiles, but regular profiles model a broader class of clusters.

Similar statements as in Theorems
4.4
and
4.5
can be shown to hold via similar arguments for regular profiles. In particular the following Theorems hold:

Theorem 4.18
.

Let
P
be a regular profile of
\vec{S}_{3k}(X)
, then
P^{\prime}:=\prescript{\triangleright}{}{P}\cap\vec{S}_{k}(Y)
is a regular profile of
\vec{S}_{k}(Y)
.

Theorem 4.19
.

Let
P
be a regular profile of
\vec{S}_{9k}(X)
,
P^{\prime}=\prescript{\triangleright}{}{P}\cap\vec{S}_{k}(Y)
and
P^{\prime\prime}=\prescript{\triangleleft}{}{P}^{\prime}\cap\vec{S}_{k}(X)
, then
P^{\prime\prime}\subseteq P
.

We can also deduce statements analogue to
4.13
and
4.14
for profiles, i.e. we can consider regular profiles on subset of the set
\vec{S}(E)
of separations of the edges of our bipartite graph and show the following:

Proposition 4.20
.

Let
P
be a regular profile of
\vec{S}_{2k}(E)
, and let us denote
{P_{X}:=\{(C,D)^{\blacktriangleleft}\>:\>(C,D)\in P\}}
. Then, the set
P_{X}\cap\vec{S}_{k}(X)
is a regular profile of
\vec{S}_{k}(X)
.

Proposition 4.21
.

Let
P
be a regular profile of
\vec{S}_{3k}(X)
and let us define
{P_{E}:=\{(C,D)\in\vec{S}_{3k}(E)\>:\>(C,D)^{\blacktriangleleft}\in P\}}

Then

is a regular profile of
\vec{S}_{k}(E)
.

However, since the arguments are similar to those in
Section

4.2
we refer interested readers to the extended version of this paper.

Another possible variation of the problem is to consider other ways to relate tangles of the different systems to each other. Given our shifting operation between the two separation systems
\vec{S}(X)
and
\vec{S}(Y)
we defined a ‘pull-back’ type operation that maps subsets of
\vec{S}(X)
to subsets of
\vec{S}(Y)
and investigated its action on tangles. However, as in the definition of
\tau_{X}
there is another way to extend our shifting operations from acting on single separations to acting on subsets via a ‘push-forward’ type action. It is perhaps equally natural to ask how the tangles of
\vec{S}_{k}(X)
and
\vec{S}_{k}(Y)
behave under these operations.

Given a tangle
\tau
of
\vec{S}_{k}(X)
one may define the set

\tau^{\triangleright}:=\{(A,B)^{\triangleright}\>:\>(A,B)\in\tau\}\subseteq\vec{S}_{k}(Y),

and similarly, if
\tau
is a tangle of
\vec{S}_{k}(Y)
, we may define

\tau^{\triangleleft}:=\{(A,B)^{\triangleleft}\>:\>(A,B)\in\tau\}\subseteq\vec{S}_{k}(X).

Note that
\tau^{\triangleright}
and
\tau^{\triangleleft}
, generally, are no more than subsets of
\vec{S}_{k}(Y)
or
\vec{S}_{k}(X)
, respectively, they need not be an orientation, not even a partial orientation.

However, we can show that this push-forward
\tau^{\triangleright}
is, when restricted appropriately, contained in a corresponding pull-back
\prescript{\triangleright}{}{\tau}
and thus needs to be a partial orientation satisfying (
4.1
).

Proposition 4.22
.

Let
\tau
be a tangle of
\vec{S}_{16k}(X)
, then

(\tau\cap\vec{S}_{k}(X))^{\triangleright}\subseteq\prescript{\triangleright}{}{\tau}.

Proof.

The only way in which this may fail is that for some
(C,D)\in\prescript{\triangleright}{}{\tau}
we have
(D,C)\in(\tau\cap\vec{S}_{k}(X))^{\triangleright}
.
Let us say this happens because of some
{(A,B)\in\tau\cap\vec{S}_{k}(X)}
with
(A,B)^{\triangleright}=(D,C)
.

Then also
(A,B)\in\prescript{\triangleleft}{}{(\prescript{\triangleright}{}{\tau}\cap\vec{S}_{4k}(Y))}\cap\vec{S}_{k}(X)
by
4.5
, and hence
(A,B)^{\triangleleft}=(D,C)\in\prescript{\triangleright}{}{\tau}\cap\vec{S}_{4k}(Y)

\prescript{\triangleright}{}{\tau}\cap\vec{S}_{4k}(Y)
is a tangle.
∎

A third variation of this idea is motivated by applications, see for example
[
10
]
. There we often wish to work with systems of set partitions, rather than more general set separations. Again here much of the work in previous sections remains true in this setting, with slight tweaks to the definitions and results.

More explicitly, given as before a bipartite graph
G
on partition classes
X
and
Y\!
let
\vec{\mathcal{B}}(X)
and
\vec{\mathcal{B}}(Y)
be the universe of all the partitions of
X
and
Y\!
, respectively.

Given a partition
(A,B)
of
X
, we can define, as before, the shift of
(A,B)
to be the partition
(C,D)
of
Y\!
where
C
is the set of all elements of
Y\!
with more neighbours in
A
than in
B
and
D
is the set of all elements of
Y\!
with more neighbours in
B
than in
A
. However, a small issue arises here as to what to do with those vertices which have an equal number of neighbours in
A
and
B
. Since we need the shift of a partition to be a partition we need to break the symmetry in some way here and we define our shifting operation not for unoriented, but for oriented partitions, namely we define a partition
(A,B)^{\triangleright}:=(C,D)
of
Y\!
by letting

C:=\{y\in Y\>:\>|N(y)\cap A|\geq|N(y)\cap B|\}

and

D:=\{y\in Y\>:\>|N(y)\cap A|<|N(y)\cap B|\}.

In particular, in general this operation may not commute with the involutions on
\vec{\mathcal{B}}(X)
and
\vec{\mathcal{B}}(Y)
, i.e., it may be the case that
(A,B)^{\triangleright}\neq((B,A)^{\triangleright})^{\ast}
.

There is again a natural order function for these partitions given by

\left|A,B\right|_{X}\coloneqq\sum_{y\in Y}\min\{\left|N(y)\cap A\right|,\left|N(y)\cap B\right|\},

which can again be seen to be submodular, and for a suitable definition of a tangle we can show that analogues of Theorems
4.4
and
4.5
hold for tangles of
\vec{\mathcal{B}}(X)
and
\vec{\mathcal{B}}(Y)
. In particular the following is true:

Theorem 4.23
.

Let
\tau
be a tangle of
\vec{\mathcal{B}}_{4k}(X)
, then
\tau^{\prime}:=\prescript{\triangleright}{}{\tau}\cap\vec{\mathcal{B}}_{k}(Y)
is a tangle of
\vec{\mathcal{B}}_{k}(Y)
.

Theorem 4.24
.

Let
\tau
be a tangle of
\vec{\mathcal{B}}_{16k}(X)
, let
\tau^{\prime}=\prescript{\triangleright}{}{\tau}\cap\vec{\mathcal{B}}_{4k}(Y)
, and let
\tau^{\prime\prime}=\prescript{\triangleleft}{}{\tau}^{\prime}\cap\vec{\mathcal{B}}_{k}(X)
, then
\tau^{\prime\prime}\subseteq\tau
.

Again the arguments closely follow the proofs of Theorems
4.4
and
4.5
, so we refer an interested reader to the extended version of this paper.

It would be nice if one could find a unified result implying these different variations. Unfortunately, it seems that the nature of the result means that strengthening or weakening the notion of tangle we consider does not make the statement stronger or weaker, but rather incomparable. Indeed, since we wish to show that tangles on
X
shift to tangles on
Y\!
, if we consider a stronger notion of tangle, then fewer orientations are tangles, and so it is required to show that a stronger property holds for the shifts, but under a stronger assumption on the original orientations. Similarly, if we consider a weaker notion of tangles, then more orientations will be tangles, and so it is required to show that a weaker property holds for the shifts, but we only have weaker assumptions on the original orientations.

A similar problem arises if one wants to relate the statements for set partitions to the statements about set separations: In principle, every tangle of separations of a set induces a tangle of its partitions. And conversely, every tangle of partitions of a set induces a tangle of separations of that set of lower order, except that the ‘regularity’ conditions of these two types of tangles are not compatible: For set separations we just require that we do not contain any cosmall separations, whereas for partitions we want more, namely that the big side of our partition of the vertices in
X
not only contains more than one vertex, but also its neighbourhood contains at least two vertices from
Y\!
.
Thus the statements for these two types of tangles are, formally, independent from each other, although most of the proof strategy is very similar.

A homological view of duality

Our aim in this section is to point out that the duality of set partitions outlined in Section

Although motivated by the naive duality of set partitions as in Section

, we shall set up our algebraic framework for the slightly larger class of set separations, in line with the rest of this paper. The homology of set separations is tantamount to that of hypergraphs

]

In this section,

are inverses of each other as oriented separations. The reason for this restriction is not that we cannot deal with pairs of inverse separations, but that we do not want the inverse of a separation
