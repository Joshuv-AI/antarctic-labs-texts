Abstract
. We study the inverse problem for the Hankel operators in the general case. Following the work of Gérard–Grellier, the spectral data is obtained from the pair of Hankel operators Γ \Gamma and Γ ​ S \Gamma S , where S S is the shift operator. The theory of complex symmetric operators provides a convenient language for the description of the spectral data. We introduce the abstract spectral data for the general case, and use the dynamical system approach, originated in the [ 15 ] , to reduce the problem to asymptotic stability of some contraction, constructed from the spectral data. The asymptotic stability is usually the hard part of the problem, but in the investigated earlier by Gérard–Grellier case of compact operators we get it almost for free. For the case of compact operators we get a concrete representation of the abstract spectral data as two intertwining sequences of singular values, and two sequences of finitely supported probability measures. This representation is different from one treated by Gérard–Grellier, and we provide the translation from one language to the other; theory of Clark measures is instrumental there.

1.
Introduction and some preparation work

1.1.
Introduction to Hankel operators

A Hankel operator is a bounded linear operator in
\ell^{2}=\ell^{2}({\mathbb{Z}}_{+})
with matrix whose entries
depend on the sum of indices,

\displaystyle\Gamma=\left(\gamma_{j+k}\right)_{j,k\geq 0}\,.

Denoting by
S
the
shift operator
in
\ell^{2}
,

\displaystyle S(x_{0},x_{1},x_{2},\ldots)

\displaystyle=(0,x_{0},x_{1},x_{2},\ldots)\,,

and by
S^{*}
its adjoint (the
backward shift
),

\displaystyle S^{*}(x_{0},x_{1},x_{2},\ldots)

\displaystyle=(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},\ldots),

we can see that an operator
\Gamma
on
\ell^{2}
is a Hankel operator if and only if

\displaystyle\Gamma S=S^{*}\Gamma;

sometimes this formula is used as the definition of a Hankel operator.

The Fourier Transform
\{a_{k}\}{\rule[-3.01385pt]{0.0pt}{4.30554pt}}_{\!k\in{\mathbb{Z}}}\mapsto\sum_{k\in{\mathbb{Z}}}a_{k}z^{k}
on
\ell^{2}({\mathbb{Z}})
(sometimes
called
z
-transform) identifies
\ell^{2}({\mathbb{Z}})
with
L^{2}(\mathbb{T})
, and
\ell^{2}({\mathbb{Z}}_{+})
with the Hardy
space
H^{2}\subset L^{2}(\mathbb{T})
, so Hankel operators are often treated as the operators in the Hardy
space.

Hankel operators play important role in analysis, connecting function theory and operator theory.
The inverse spectral problem for Hankel operators was initially motivated by the theory of stationary random
processes. Investigating geometry of the “past” and “future” of such processes, V. V. Peller
and S. V. Khruschev posed a problem of describing all non-negative self-adjoint operators
unitarily equivalent to the modulus of a Hankel operator. After some preliminary results, see

[
14
]
, the problem was fully solved by S. Treil
[
21
]
. It turned out
that any
non-negative
self-adjoint operator
A
that is not invertible and whose kernel is either infinite-dimensional or
trivial is unitarily equivalent to the modulus of a Hankel operator (note that trivially the
modulus of a Hankel operator is always not invertible and cannot have finite-dimensional kernel).

Later, motivated by dynamical systems, A. Megretskii, V. V. Peller, and S. Treil completely
described self-adjoint operators unitarily equivalent to some Hankel operator, see
[
15
]
. The
description was more complicated than for a modulus, and involved some “almost symmetry” of the
spectral measure.

We should mention here, that in both problems the solution (a Hankel operator) is trivially not
unique.

Recently the interest in the inverse spectral problem for Hankel operators was renewed in
connection with the so-called cubic Szegö equation, which is a model completely integrable
Hamiltonian system. In pioneering series of papers
[
5
]
,
[
6
]
P. Gerard and S. Grellier
investigated the inverse spectral problem for Hankel operators and its connection with the Szegö
equation. For the case of compact operators they have the inverse problem completely solved. One of
their discoveries was that the spectral data not for
\Gamma
, but for
both

\Gamma
and

\Gamma S
completely determines the operator
\Gamma
, so the inverse problem have a unique
solution.

2.
Preliminaries

2.1.
Direct integral of Hilbert spaces and model for self-adjoint operators

Let us recall some basic facts about direct (a.k.a. von Neuman) integral of Hilbert spaces, and
the model for self-adjoint operators, see
[
1
]
. In this paper we present a simplified
version of the direct integral, which is sufficient for our purposes.

Let
\mathcal{E}
be a separable Hilbert space, and let
\{e_{k}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
be an orthonormal basis in

\mathcal{E}
. Let
\mu
be a finite compactly supported Borel measure on
{\mathbb{R}}
, and let
N:\operatorname{supp}\mu\to{\mathbb{N}}\cup\{\infty\}
be the dimension function, i.e. a Borel measurable function (we only need

N
to be defined
\mu
-a.e.).

Denote
\mathcal{E}(t):=\overline{\operatorname{span}}\{e_{k}:1\leq k\leq N(t)\}
, and define the direct integral

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}:=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\!\!\oplus\,\,\mathcal{E}(t)\mathrm{d}\mu(t)

(2.1)

as the subspace of the
\mathcal{E}
-valued
L^{2}
-space
L^{2}(\mu;\mathcal{E})
, consisting of all functions
f\in L^{2}(\mu;\mathcal{E})
such that

\displaystyle f(t)\in\mathcal{E}(t)\qquad\mu\text{-a.e.}

The direct integral form of the spectral theorem states that a self-adjoint operator in a separable
Hilbert space (recall that in this paper all Hilbert spaces are separable) is unitarily equivalent
to the multiplication operator
M
by the independent
variable

\displaystyle Mf(t)=tf(t)

in a direct integral of form (
2.1
).

The spectral type
[\mu]
of the measure
\mu
, i.e. the equivalence class of all measures mutually
absolutely continuous with
\mu
, and the dimension function
N
(defined
\mu
-a.e.) give us a
complete set of unitary invariants for a self-adjoint operator. This means that two self-adjoint
operators are unitarily equivalent if and only if the measures in the direct integral are mutually
absolutely continuous and the dimension functions coincide
\mu
-a.e.

2.2.
Complex Symmetric Operators

Matrix of a Hankel operator
\Gamma
is symmetric, and a convenient way to explore that fact is to
use the theory of so called
complex symmetric operators
.

Let us recall some main definitions and basic facts,
cf
[
3
]
.

Definition 2.1
.

An operator
\mathfrak{C}
in a complex Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
is called a
conjugation
, if
it is

(i)

conjugate-linear

\mathfrak{C}(\alpha x+\beta y)=\bar{\alpha}\mathfrak{C}x+\bar{\beta}\mathfrak{C}y
for all

;

(ii)

and
involutive
:
\mathfrak{C}^{2}=I
;

(iii)

and
isometric
:
\|\mathfrak{C}x\|=\|x\|
for all
x\in\mathcal{H}
.

Definition 2.2
.

Let
\mathfrak{C}
be a conjugation on Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
. A bounded linear operator
T
on
\mathcal{H}
is called

\mathfrak{C}
-symmetric
if
T^{*}=\mathfrak{C}T\mathfrak{C}
.

It is well-known and not hard to see that a conjugate-linear operator
\mathfrak{C}
is a conjugation if and
only if there exists an orthonormal basis
\{e_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
of
\mathcal{H}
such that
\mathfrak{C}e_{n}=e_{n}
for all
n
; such basis
is often called a
\mathfrak{C}
-real orthonormal basis
.
Note that given a cojugation
\mathfrak{C}
the choice of
\mathfrak{C}
-real basis is not unique.

It is easy to see that for a
\mathfrak{C}
-symmetric operator
T
its matrix in any
\mathfrak{C}
-real orthonormal
basis is symmetric.

On the other hand, if a matrix of an operator
T
in some orthonormal basis
\{e_{n}\}_{n}
is
symmetric, then
T
is
\mathfrak{C}
-symmetric for the conjugation
\mathfrak{C}
given by

\mathfrak{C}(\sum\limits_{n}\alpha_{n}e_{n})=\sum\limits_{n}\overline{\alpha}_{n}e_{n}
. Note that a
conjugation
\mathfrak{C}
that
T
is
\mathfrak{C}
-symmetric is generally not unique, and the above is just one
possible choice.

The matrix of a Hankel operator in the standard basis in
\ell^{2}
is symmetric, thus Hankel
operator is
\mathfrak{C}
-symmetric with respect to the canonical conjugation on
\ell^{2}
,

\displaystyle\mathfrak{C}(z_{1},z_{2},z_{3},\ldots)=(\bar{z}_{1},\bar{z}_{2},\bar{z}_{3},\ldots).

(2.2)

Finally, we will need a simple but important formula for an arbitrary conjugation
\mathfrak{C}
:

\displaystyle\langle\mathfrak{C}x,y\rangle=\langle\mathfrak{C}y,x\rangle\qquad\forall x,y\in\mathcal{H}.

(2.3)

It can be easily proved using properties (i)–(iii) of a conjugation, or just by decomposing

x
and
y
in a
\mathfrak{C}
-real orthonormal basis.

2.3.
Polar decomposition of
\mathfrak{C}
-symmetric operators

Let us recall that an operator
U
is called a
partial isometry
if its restriction to
(\operatorname{Ker}U)^{\perp}
is an isometry; note that
(\operatorname{Ker}U)^{\perp}
does not need to be
U
-invariant.

A bounded operator in a Hilbert space admits a
polar decomposition

T=U|T|
, where
|T|:=(T^{*}T)^{1/2}
, and
U
is a partial isometry with
\operatorname{Ker}U=\operatorname{Ker}T
; note
that under these assumptions the partial isometry
U
is unique. Sometimes the partial isometry

U
can be replaced by a unitary operator (even in
\operatorname{Ker}T\neq\{0\}
), although unlike the
finite-dimensional case this is not always possible.

For a complex symmetric operator we can say a bit more about its polar decomposition. Following

[
3
]
, we say that
a conjugate-linear operator
\mathfrak{J}
in Hilbert space is a
partial conjugation
if
(\operatorname{Ker}\mathfrak{J})^{\perp}
is invariant for
\mathfrak{J}
and
\mathfrak{J}|{\rule[-3.01385pt]{0.0pt}{4.30554pt}}_{\!(\operatorname{Ker}\mathfrak{J})^{\perp}}
is a conjugation.
Again, following
[
3
]
we say a conjugation
\mathfrak{J}
is supported on (a subspace)
\mathcal{K}

if
\mathcal{K}=(\operatorname{Ker}\mathfrak{J})^{\perp}
.

We will need the following theorem,
[
3
, Theorem 2]
:

Theorem 2.3
.

A bounded
\mathfrak{C}-
symmetric operator
T
admits the polar decomposition
T=U|T|
with
U=\mathfrak{C}\mathfrak{J}

where
\mathfrak{J}
is a partial conjugation,
\operatorname{Ker}\mathfrak{J}=\operatorname{Ker}T
, commuting with

|T|
.

Remark 2.4
.

Note that in the above theorem the partial conjugation
\mathfrak{J}
is unique and the partial isometry
U

is
\mathfrak{C}-
symmetric.

Remark 2.5
.

We can always find a conjugation
\widetilde{\mathfrak{J}}
, such that
\widetilde{\mathfrak{J}}=\mathfrak{J}
on
(\operatorname{Ker}\mathfrak{J})^{\perp}
. To do that we can take an arbitrary conjugation
\mathfrak{J}_{1}
on
\operatorname{Ker}\mathfrak{J}
, and then define
\widetilde{\mathfrak{J}}\big|_{(\operatorname{Ker}\mathfrak{J})^{\perp}}:=\mathfrak{J}_{1}
.
Then
T=\mathfrak{C}\widetilde{\mathfrak{J}}|T|=\widetilde{U}|T|
, where
\widetilde{U}=\mathfrak{C}\widetilde{\mathfrak{J}}
is clearly a
\mathfrak{C}
-symmetric unitary
operator.

2.4.
Abstract spectral data for Hankel operators

For a Hankel operator
\Gamma
we will always denote
\Gamma_{1}:=\Gamma S=S^{*}\Gamma
(the
identity
\Gamma S=S^{*}\Gamma
is an alternative definition of a Hankel operator, see the
Introduction above).

W can write

\displaystyle|\Gamma|^{2}-|\Gamma_{1}|^{2}=\Gamma^{*}\Gamma-\Gamma^{*}SS^{*}\Gamma=\Gamma^{*}(I-SS^{*})\Gamma=\Gamma^{*}(e_{0}e_{0}^{*})\Gamma,

so denoting

\displaystyle|\Gamma|^{2}-|\Gamma_{1}|^{2}=uu^{*}.

(2.4)

We will need the following simple statement.

Lemma 2.6
.

Let
R=R^{*}
be a self-adjoint operator in a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
, and let
p\in\mathcal{H}
.

There exists a conjugation
\mathfrak{J}_{p}
commuting with
R
and preserving
p
, i.e. such that

\mathfrak{J}_{p}p=p
.

Moreover,
\mathfrak{J}_{p}
is uniquely determined on

\mathcal{H}_{0}:=\overline{\operatorname{span}}\{R^{n}p:n\geq 0\}
, and
\mathcal{H}_{0}
is a reducing subspace for
\mathfrak{J}_{p}
, meaning that both

\mathcal{H}_{0}
and
\mathcal{H}_{0}^{\perp}
are
\mathfrak{J}_{p}
-invariant.

Finally,
\mathfrak{J}_{p}
is unique if and only if
p
is cyclic for
R
.

Proof.

Defining
\mathfrak{J}_{p}
on
\mathcal{H}_{0}
by

\displaystyle\mathfrak{J}_{p}\Bigl(\sum_{k\geq 0}\alpha_{k}R^{k}p\Bigr)=\sum_{k\geq 0}\overline{\alpha}_{k}R^{k}p,

(2.5)

we get a conjugation on
\mathcal{H}_{0}
, commuting with
R\big|_{\mathcal{H}_{0}}
.

To extend
\mathfrak{J}_{p}
to all
\mathcal{H}
we just need to construct a conjugation on
\mathcal{H}_{0}^{\perp}
commuting
with
R\big|_{\mathcal{H}_{0}^{\perp}}
, or equivalently, construct a conjugation commuting with the
multiplication operator
M
by the independent variable in the direct integral representation
(
2.1
) of

R\big|_{\mathcal{H}_{0}^{\perp}}
. But that is trivial, the conjugation

\displaystyle\sum_{k}f_{k}e_{k}\mapsto\sum_{k}\overline{f}_{k}e_{k}

(
f_{k}
are scalar-valued functions) give one possibility.

As for the uniqueness, if
\mathfrak{J}_{p}
commutes with
R
then
\mathfrak{J}_{p}R^{k}p=R^{k}p
for all
k\geq 0
, so
by conjugate linearity
\mathfrak{J}_{p}
on
\mathcal{H}_{0}
must be given by (
2.5
). We can see from
this formula that
\mathfrak{J}_{p}\mathcal{H}_{0}=\mathcal{H}_{0}
. Since
\mathfrak{J}_{p}
is an isometry, it preserves orthogonality, so

\mathfrak{J}_{p}(\mathcal{H}_{0}^{\perp})\subset\mathcal{H}_{0}^{\perp}
.

Finally, a conjugation commuting with the multiplication
M
by the independent variable is clearly
not unique, so
\mathfrak{J}_{p}
is unique if and only if
\mathcal{H}_{0}=\mathcal{H}
.
∎

The lemma below gives a complete description of all conjugations
\mathfrak{J}_{p}
from Lemma

.

Lemma 2.7
.

Let
\mathfrak{J}_{p}
be a conjugation from Lemma
2.6
. Then any other such conjugation
\mathfrak{J}_{p}^{\prime}
is
given by
\mathfrak{J}_{p}^{\prime}=\Psi\mathfrak{J}_{p}
, where
\Psi
is unitary
\mathfrak{J}_{p}
-symmetric operators commuting with

R
and preserving
p
,
\Psi p=p
.

Proof.

If
\mathfrak{J}_{p}^{\prime}
is another conjugation, commuting with
P
and preserving
p
, then
\mathfrak{J}_{p}^{\prime}==\Psi\mathfrak{J}_{p}
, where
\Psi:=\mathfrak{J}_{p}^{\prime}\mathfrak{J}_{p}
. It is easy to see that
\Psi
is a unitary operator, commuting
with
R
and preserving
p
. It is also
easy to see that
\psi
is
\mathfrak{J}_{p}
-symmetric.

On the other hand, if
\Psi
is a
\mathfrak{J}_{p}
-symmetric unitary operator, commuting with
R
and such
that
\Psi p=p
, then the (conjugate-linear) operator
\mathfrak{J}^{\prime}_{p}:=\psi\mathfrak{J}_{p}
is a conjugation

and preserving

.

Indeed, the operator
\mathfrak{J}_{p}^{\prime}
is trivially conjugate-linear, isometric, preserves

and commutes
with
R
. To show that
\mathfrak{J}_{p}^{\prime}
is a conjugation, it remains to show that
\mathfrak{J}_{p}^{\prime}
is an

-symmetry of

, we get that
\Psi\mathfrak{J}_{p}=\mathfrak{J}_{p}\Psi^{*}

∎

4.
Abstract inverse spectral problem for Hankel operators

In this section, we consider the inverse spectral problem for Hankel operators
\Gamma
with
abstract spectral data.

4.1.
Setup

Assume that we are given a tuple
(R,R_{1},p,\mathfrak{J}_{p},\boldsymbol{\varphi},\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{1})
, where

(i)

R,R_{1}\geq 0
, are self-adjoint operators in
\mathcal{H}
,
\operatorname{Ker}R=\{0\}
, and
p\in\mathcal{H}
, such
that

R^{2}-R_{1}^{2}=pp^{*}.

Note that the above identity implies that
p\in\operatorname{Ran}R
and
\|R^{-1}p\|\leq 1
.

(ii)

\mathfrak{J}_{p}
is a conjugation commuting with
R
and
R_{1}
and preserving
p
,
\mathfrak{J}_{p}p=p
.

(iii)

\boldsymbol{\varphi}
is a
\mathfrak{J}_{p}
-symmetric unitary operator commuting with
R
.

(iv)

\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{1}
is a
\mathfrak{J}_{p}
-symmetric partial isometry,
\operatorname{Ker}\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{1}=\operatorname{Ker}R_{1}
, commuting
with
R_{1}
;
note that if
\operatorname{Ker}R_{1}=\{0\}
then
\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{1}
is unitary.

Note, that the operator
R_{1}
can have a non-trivial (one-dimensional) kernel.

Proposition 4.1
.

\operatorname{Ker}R_{1}\neq\{0\}
is and only if

\displaystyle p\in\operatorname{Ran}R^{2},\qquad\text{and}\qquad\|R^{-1}p\|=1.

(4.1)

Proof.

Let
R_{1}x=0
for
x\neq 0
. Then
R_{1}^{2}x=0
, so

\displaystyle R^{2}x=\langle x,p\rangle p,

(4.2)

which means that
p=\alpha R^{-2}x
,
\alpha\neq 1
, i.e. that
p\in\operatorname{Ran}R^{2}
.
By homogeneity we can assume without loss of generality that
\alpha=1
, and substituting

x=R^{-2}p
into (
4.2
) we get that

\displaystyle p=\langle R^{-2}p,p\rangle p,

so
\|R^{-1}p\|^{2}=\langle R^{-2}p,p\rangle=1
.

Reversing the above reasoning, we see that conditions (
4.1
) imply (
4.2
) for
x=R^{-1}p
, so
x\in\operatorname{Ker}R_{1}^{2}=\operatorname{Ker}R_{1}
.
∎

Note that
\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{1}|{\rule[-3.01385pt]{0.0pt}{4.30554pt}}_{\!(\operatorname{Ker}R_{1})^{\perp}}

Lemma 4.3

(Douglas Lemma)
.

Let
A
and
B
be bounded operators in a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
such that

\|Bx\|\leq\|Ax\|\qquad\forall x\in\mathcal{H}.

(
or, equivalently,
B^{*}B\leq A^{*}A
)
, and let
\operatorname{Ker}A=\operatorname{Ker}A^{*}=\{0\}
.

Then the operator
T=BA^{-1}

(
defined initially on a dense set
)
extends to a
contraction,

\|T\|\leq 1
and its adjoint is given by
T^{*}=(A^{*})^{-1}B^{*}
; notice that the condition
\|T\|\leq 1
implies that
\operatorname{Ran}B^{*}\in\operatorname{Dom}(A^{*})^{-1}
, so the operator
T^{*}
is well defined on all

x\in\mathcal{H}
.

Remark 4.4
.

If
A
is a bounded operator with
\operatorname{Ker}A=\operatorname{Ker}A^{*}=\{0\}
, and
B
is an invertible operator,
commuting with
A
, then trivially
B\operatorname{Ran}A=B^{-1}\operatorname{Ran}A=\operatorname{Ran}A
.

If
A^{-1}
denotes the
natural
inverse of
A
, i.e. the densely defined operator with

\operatorname{Dom}A^{-1}=\operatorname{Ran}A
, then

\displaystyle BA^{-1}=A^{-1}B

with the natural domains of both
BA^{-1}
and

A^{-1}B
being
\operatorname{Ran}A
.

In this paper we will often use this commutation relation.

Recall the following simple definition.

Definition 4.5
.

An operator

if

in the strong
operator
topology as

\displaystyle\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}\|T^{n}x\|\rightarrow 0,\qquad\forall x\in\mathcal{H}.

Since the backward shift
S^{*}
is trivially asymptotically stable, so is its restriction to any

S^{*}
-invariant subspace. In particular,
S^{*}\big|_{(\operatorname{Ker}\Gamma)^{\perp}}
is asymptotically
stable, so if we have unitary equivalence (

), (
4.4
), the operator

\Sigma^{*}=\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{1}R_{1}R^{-1}\boldsymbol{\varphi}^{*}
must be asymptotically stable.

Turns out that this condition is also sufficient.

4.3.
Some discussions

As it was already discussed above, the
abstract spectral data

is the tuple
R,R_{1},\mathfrak{J}_{p},\boldsymbol{\varphi},\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{1},p
, described in the beginning of Section

4.1
, modulo unitary equivalence of the tuples. It is also natural to
define the abstract spectral data modulo equivalence relations

\displaystyle\mathfrak{J}_{p}^{\prime}=\boldsymbol{\psi}\mathfrak{J}_{p},\qquad\boldsymbol{\varphi}^{\prime}=\boldsymbol{\varphi}\boldsymbol{\psi}^{*},\qquad\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{1}^{\prime}=\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{1}\boldsymbol{\psi}^{*},

,
\boldsymbol{\varphi}
,
\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{1}
and
\mathfrak{J}_{p}^{\prime}
,
\boldsymbol{\varphi}^{\prime}
,
\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{1}^{\prime}
; here

is a
unitary,
\mathfrak{J}_{p}
-symmetric operator, commuting with
R
and preserving

,
\boldsymbol{\psi}p=p
.

The results presented in the above subsection, imply that the map from a Hankel operator to the
abstract spectral data (interpreted modulo unitary equivalence and equivalence relations
(

)) is injective.

It is trivially not surjective, because it is quite easy to construct an abstract spectral data
such that the operator
\Sigma^{*}
is not asymptotically stable. What is more non-trivial, it was
shown in
[
11
]
that even in the case when there no trivial obstacles to the asymptotic
stability (in
[
11
]
the case of
p
being cyclic for
R
was considered), there exists
abstract spectral data, which does not appear from a Hankel operator.

The conjugation
\mathfrak{J}_{p}
in the abstract spectral data looks a bit mysterious, especially in light
of Proposition
4.9
, where
\mathfrak{J}_{p}
is absent from the formulas for
\gamma_{k}
(while

\mathfrak{J}_{p}
appear in the relations between

and
\widehat{q}
, and between
\Sigma^{*}
and
\widehat{\Sigma}^{*}
,
the formulas for all these object do not involve
\mathfrak{J}_{p}
).

4.4.
Proofs

The whole proof consists of three different parts: existence, uniqueness and the trivial kernel
condition.

4.4.1.
Existence of the Hankel operator
\Gamma

Recalling that
\Sigma^{*}=\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{1}R_{1}R^{-1}\boldsymbol{\varphi}^{*}
we can write

\displaystyle I-\Sigma\Sigma^{*}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\varphi}(I-R^{-1}R_{1}^{2}R^{-1})\boldsymbol{\varphi}^{*}=\boldsymbol{\varphi}R^{-1}(R^{2}-R_{1}^{2})R^{-1}\boldsymbol{\varphi}^{*}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\varphi}R^{-1}pp^{*}R^{-1}\boldsymbol{\varphi}^{*}=qq^{*},

where
q:=\boldsymbol{\varphi}R^{-1}p
(recall that
\|R^{-1}p\|\leq 1
).

Applying both sides of the identity

\displaystyle I-\Sigma\Sigma^{*}=qq^{*},

(4.16)

to a vector
x\in\mathcal{H}
and taking the inner product with
x
, we get

\displaystyle\|x\|^{2}-\|\Sigma^{*}x\|^{2}=|\langle x,q\rangle{\rule[-3.01385pt]{0.0pt}{4.30554pt}}_{\!\mathcal{H}}|^{2}.

Replacing
x
in the above identity by
(\Sigma^{*})^{k}x
,
k=1,2,\ldots,n-1
, summing the results
and telescoping, we get that

\displaystyle\|x\|^{2}=\|(\Sigma^{*})^{n}x\|^{2}=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\|(\Sigma^{*})^{k}x\|^{2}.

Letting
n\to\infty
and using the asymptotic stability of
\Sigma^{*}
, we conclude that

\displaystyle\|x\|^{2}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\|(\Sigma^{*})^{k}x\|^{2},

(4.17)

so the operator
\mathcal{V}:\mathcal{H}\to\ell^{2}
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}x:=\left(\langle x,q\rangle,\langle\Sigma^{*}x,q\rangle,\langle(\Sigma^{*})^{2}x,q\rangle,\ldots\right)=\left(\langle(\Sigma^{*})^{k}x,q\rangle\right)_{k=0}^{\infty},

(4.18)

is an isometry. The definition (
4.18

immediately implies that
\mathcal{V}
intertwines

S^{*}
and
\Sigma^{*}

\displaystyle S^{*}\mathcal{V}=\mathcal{V}\Sigma^{*}.

Now we define the operators
\Gamma
and

\displaystyle\Gamma:=\mathfrak{C}\mathcal{V}R\boldsymbol{\varphi}\mathfrak{J}_{p}\mathcal{V}^{*}\qquad\Gamma_{1}:=\mathfrak{C}\mathcal{V}R_{1}\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{1}\mathfrak{J}_{p}\mathcal{V}^{*}.

4.4.3.
The trivial kernel condition

It is easy to see that
\operatorname{Ker}\Gamma=\{0\}
if and only if
\mathcal{V}:\mathcal{H}\to\ell^{2}
defined by
(
4.18
) is a unitary operator, i.e. if and only if
\operatorname{Ran}\mathcal{V}=\ell^{2}
.

So, if
\operatorname{Ker}\Gamma=\{0\}
, then
\Sigma^{*}
is unitarily equivalent to the backward shift

S^{*}
, and comparing identities

\displaystyle I-SS^{*}=e_{0}e_{0}^{*},\qquad I-\Sigma\Sigma^{*}=qq^{*},

where, recall
q=\boldsymbol{\varphi}R^{-1}p
, we conclude that
\|R^{-1}p\|=\|q\|=1
. We can also see that
the identity
I-\Sigma\Sigma^{*}=qq^{*}
implies that
\Sigma^{*}q=0
.

Also in this case, since
\Gamma_{1}=\Gamma S
, we can conclude that
\operatorname{Ker}\Gamma_{1}=\{0\}
; thus

\operatorname{Ker}R_{1}=\{0\}
, and therefore
\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{1}
is a unitary operator.

Now, assuming
q\in\operatorname{Ran}R
, let us get a contradiction.

Let
q=Rx
,
x\in\mathcal{H}
. Define
f:=\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{1}\boldsymbol{\varphi}^{*}x
, thus
x=\boldsymbol{\varphi}\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{1}^{*}f
and
q=R\boldsymbol{\varphi}\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{1}^{*}f
, Hence

\displaystyle\Sigma^{*}R\boldsymbol{\varphi}\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{1}^{*}f=\Sigma^{*}q=0.

But on the other hand we have

\displaystyle\Sigma^{*}R\boldsymbol{\varphi}\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{1}^{*}=\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{1}R_{1}R^{-1}R\boldsymbol{\varphi}^{*}\boldsymbol{\varphi}\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{1}^{*}=R_{1}=\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{1}\boldsymbol{\varphi}^{*}R\Sigma,

where the last equality follows from
R_{1}
being a self-adjoint operator. Hence

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{1}\boldsymbol{\varphi}^{*}R\Sigma f=0,

so
\Sigma f=0
(because the operators
R
,
\boldsymbol{\varphi}^{*}
,
\boldsymbol{\varphi}
have trivial kernels), which
contradicts to the fact that
\Sigma
is an isometry. Hence
q\notin\operatorname{Ran}R
.

Now we prove the sufficiency part. Suppose
\|q\|=1
and
q\notin\operatorname{Ran}R
.

Let us first show that

\operatorname{Ker}R_{1}=\{0\}
.
Let
R_{1}x=0
for a
x\neq 0
. Applying to
x
the identity

\displaystyle R_{1}^{2}=R\big(I-\boldsymbol{\varphi}^{*}q(\boldsymbol{\varphi}^{*}q)^{*}\big)R,

we get that

\displaystyle Rx=\langle Rx,\boldsymbol{\varphi}^{*}q\rangle\boldsymbol{\varphi}^{*}q;

note that
Rx\neq 0
because
R
has trivial kernel.
This implies
Rx=\alpha\boldsymbol{\varphi}^{*}q
,
|\alpha|=1
, so

\displaystyle q=\alpha^{-1}\boldsymbol{\varphi}Rx=\alpha^{-1}R\boldsymbol{\varphi}x,

which contradicts the assumption
q\notin\operatorname{Ran}R
. So, indeed,
\operatorname{Ker}R_{1}=\{0\}
. Note that in
this case
\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{1}
is a unitary operator.

From the definition of
\Sigma^{*}
we get that
\Sigma=\boldsymbol{\varphi}R^{-1}R_{1}\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{1}^{*}
, is well defined
for all
x\in\mathcal{H}
, see Lemma
4.3
. Since all operators have trivial kernels, we conclude
that
\operatorname{Ker}\Sigma=\{0\}
.

Applying both sides of the identity (
4.16
) to
q
, we get that
\Sigma\Sigma^{*}q=0
, so
\Sigma^{*}q=0
.

Now left and right multiplying (
4.16
) by
\Sigma
and
\Sigma^{*}
respectively, we
get

\displaystyle\Sigma^{*}\Sigma-\Sigma^{*}\Sigma\Sigma^{*}\Sigma=\Sigma^{*}\big(qq^{*}\big)\Sigma=\Sigma^{*}q(\Sigma^{*}q)^{*}=0,

hence
\Sigma^{*}\Sigma
is a projection. Furthermore, since
\operatorname{Ker}\Sigma=\{0\}
, we have

\Sigma^{*}\Sigma=I
, and so
\Sigma
is an isometry.

Since
\Sigma
is an isometry,
\|q\|=1
, and
\Sigma^{*}q=0
, it is an easy exercise to show that

\{\Sigma^{k}q\}_{k=0}^{\infty}
is an orthonormal system. Rewriting decomposition (
4.18
)
for
\mathcal{V}
as

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}x=\left(\langle x,\Sigma^{k}q\rangle\right)_{k=0}^{\infty}\in\ell^{2},

we immediately see that
\mathcal{V}
is surjective. It was already proved that
\mathcal{V}
is an isometry, so

\mathcal{V}
is unitary.
∎

4.5.
Some remarks

5.
Concrete representations of the abstract spectral data for simple cases

The abstract spectral data treated in Section
4
looks like some completely
non-tractable abstract nonsense. However in many interesting situations the abstract spectral data
can be represented using very concrete and understandable objects.

5.1.
The case of
p
being cyclic for
R

If
p
is a cyclic vector for

R
, the pair
R
,
p
is completely determined by the spectral measure
\rho=\rho{\rule[-3.01385pt]{0.0pt}{4.30554pt}}_{\!R,p}
of the
operator
R
with respect to the vector
p
.

In this case the pair
R
,
p
can be represented as (unitarily equivalent to) the multiplication
operator
M
,
Mf(s)=sf(s)
in
\mathcal{H}=L^{2}(\rho)
and the vector
p=\mathbf{1}\in L^{2}(\rho)
. Under these
assumptions there is a unique conjugation
\mathfrak{J}_{p}
, commuting with
R
and preserving
p
, namely

\mathfrak{J}_{p}f=\bar{f}
.

The unitary operator
\boldsymbol{\varphi}
is given by the multiplication by an appropriate unimodular function,
which we will be denoting by
\varphi
(without boldface). Thus we could write
\boldsymbol{\varphi}=\varphi(R)
.

Note, that the condition
\|R^{-1}p\|\leq 1
is translated to

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{\mathrm{d}\rho(s)}{s^{2}}\leq 1

(5.1)

It is easy to see that if
p
is cyclic for
R
(and so for
R^{2}
), it is also cyclic for
R_{1}^{2}=R^{2}-pp^{*}
, and so for
R_{1}
. The spectral measure
\rho^{[1]}=\rho{\rule[-3.01385pt]{0.0pt}{4.30554pt}}_{\!R_{1},p}
is uniquely
determined from
\rho
, and the partial isometry
\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{1}
is defined by a function
\varphi_{1}
,

\varphi_{1}(s)|=1

\rho^{[1]}
-a.e. on
(0,\infty)
, and
\varphi_{1}(0)=0
, and we can write
\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{1}=\varphi_{1}(R_{1})
.

Thus, the abstract spectral data in this case is given by

(i)

A compactly supported measure
\rho
on
(0,\infty)
satisfying (
5.1
) (and the measure
\rho^{[1]}
derived from it);

(ii)

Two unimodular functions
\varphi
and
\varphi_{1}
on
(0,\infty)
, defined
\rho
-a.e. and

\rho^{[1]}
-a.e. respectively.

The conjugation
\mathfrak{J}_{p}
is implicit in this case.

This case was studied in details in
[
11
]
, where the asymtotic stability of the operator

\Sigma^{*}
was investigated. There is no obvious obstacles for the asymptotic stability of

\Sigma^{*}
in this situation, and it was conjectured for some time, that in this case the operator

\Sigma^{*}
is always asymptotically stable. However, it was shown in
[
11
]
that this is not
true; some counterexamples as well as some sufficient conditions for the asymptotic stability were
presented there, but describing these result is out of the scope of the current paper.

We will only mention that the conjugate linear Hankel operator
H_{u}
, studied in
[
11
]

coincides with our operator
\mathfrak{C}\Gamma=\Gamma^{*}\mathfrak{C}
, treated as the operator in the Hardy space

H^{2}
. The symbol
u
in
[
11
]
was in our terms just the function
u(z)=\sum_{k\geq 0}\bar{\gamma}_{k}z^{k}
, which is just our vector
u=\Gamma^{*}e_{0}
transferred to
H^{2}
via

z
-transform. Our functions
\varphi
,
\varphi_{1}
correspond to the functions
\Psi^{*}
and
\widetilde{\Psi}
from

[
11
]
.

5.2.
The case of a compact operator
R
with cyclic
p

In this case the setup can simplified even more. The spectral measures
\rho
and
\rho^{[1]}
in
this case can be written down as

\displaystyle\rho=\sum_{k\geq 1}w_{k}\delta_{\lambda_{k}},\qquad\rho^{[1]}=\sum_{k\geq 1}w_{k}^{[1]}\delta_{\mu_{k}}

(5.2)

where
\lambda_{1}>\lambda_{2}>\ldots>\lambda_{k}>\ldots>0
are eigenvalues of
R
,

\mu_{1}>\mu_{2}>\ldots>\mu_{k}>\ldots\geq 0
are eigenvalues of
R_{1}
and
w_{k}=\rho(\{\lambda_{k}\})
,
w_{k}^{[1]}=\rho^{[1]}(\{\mu_{k}\})
. Recall, see Proposition
4.1
,
that the operator
R_{1}
can have nontrivial kernel. In the infinite-dimensional case we denote this
zero eigenvalue by
\mu_{\infty}
, so
\mu_{k}>0
for
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
; if
\dim\mathcal{H}=n
, then
\mu_{n}\geq 0

and
\lambda_{n}>0
.

Note that the eigenvalues
\lambda_{k}
,
\mu_{k}
satisfy the intertwining
relations

\displaystyle\lambda_{1}>\mu_{1}>\lambda_{2}>\mu_{2}\ldots>\lambda_{k}>\mu_{k}>\ldots\geq 0,

(5.3)

and
\lambda_{k}
is either a finite sequence
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n}
(in this case

\mu_{n}
can be
0
), or
\lambda_{k}\searrow 0
.

The intertwining relation (
5.3
) can be easily obtained from the minimax
characterization of the eigenvalues, or via standard formulas for rank one perturbations relating
Weil’s
m
-functions, see Remark
9.1
. We leave details for the reader.

The only new twist in the compact case is that the weights
w_{k}
(and therefore
w^{[1]}_{k}
) can be
determined from the eigenvalues
\lambda_{k}
of
R
and non-zero eigenvalues
\mu_{k}
of
R_{1}
. The
key to that is the following
abstract Borg’s theorem
.

Theorem 5.1

(Abstract Borg’s Theorem)
.

Given two sequences
\{\lambda_{k}\}_{k\geq 1}
and
\{\mu_{k}\}_{k\geq 1}
satisfying intertwining
relations (
5.3
) and such that
\lambda_{k}^{2}\to 0
as
k\to\infty
, there
exists a unique
(
up to unitary equivalence
)
triple
(W,W_{1},p)
, such that

(i)

W=W^{*}\geq 0
,
\operatorname{Ker}W=\{0\}
is a
compact operator with simple eigenvalues
\{\lambda_{k}^{2}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
;

(ii)

p\in\mathcal{H}
and
W_{1}=W-pp^{*}
is a compact operator with non-zero eigenvalues
\{\mu_{k}^{2}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}

(
W_{1}
can
also have a simple eigenvalue at
0
, and it is not hard to show that all the eigenvalues are
simple
)
.

Moreover,
\|W^{-1/2}p\|=1
if and only if

\displaystyle\prod_{k\geq 1}\frac{\mu_{k}^{2}}{\lambda_{k}^{2}}=0,\quad\text{ or, equivalently, }\quad\sum_{k\geq 1}\Bigg(\frac{\lambda_{k}^{2}}{\mu_{k}^{2}}-1\Bigg)=\infty;

(5.4)

in addition, if (
5.4
) holds, then
\|W^{-1}p\|=\infty
if and only if

\displaystyle\sum_{k\geq 1}^{\infty}\left(\frac{\mu_{k}^{2}}{\lambda_{k+1}^{2}}-1\right)=\infty.

(5.5)

Applying this theorem, from given sequences
\{\lambda_{k}\}_{k\geq 1}
,
\{\mu_{k}\}_{k\geq 1}
we
get the (unique up to unitary equivalence) triple
W
,
W_{1}
,
p
, and defining
R=W^{1/2}
,

R_{1}=W_{1}^{1/2}
(non-negative square roots) we get the unique triple
R
,
R_{1}
,
p
.

Thus, the spectral data in this case is given by sequences
\{\lambda_{k}\}_{k\geq 1}
,

\{\mu_{k}\}_{k\geq 1}
of eigenvalues of
R
and
R_{1}
and the sequences
\{\xi_{k}\}_{k\geq 1}
,

\{\eta_{k}\}_{k\geq 1}
,
|\xi_{k}|=|\eta_{k}|=1
representing the functions
\varphi
and
\varphi_{1}
,

\xi_{k}=\varphi(\lambda_{k})
,
\eta_{k}=\varphi_{1}(\mu_{k})
.

It will be shown below, see Section
7
, that the operator
\Sigma^{*}
in the compact case is
always asymptotically stable, so for any abstract spectral data there exist a unique Hankel
operator.

Condition
\|W^{-1/2}p\|=1
is equivalent to the condition
\|R^{-1}p\|=1
, and the condition

\|W^{-1}p\|=\infty
means
R^{-1}p\notin\operatorname{Ran}R
. Therefore, for the resulting Hankel operator

\Gamma
,
\operatorname{Ker}\Gamma=\{0\}
if and only if conditions (
5.4
) and (
5.5
) hold.

Remark 5.2
.

As it will be seen from the proof, the abstract Borg’s theorem also
holds (and is trivial)
for finite rank operators, i.e. for finite sequences
\{\lambda_{k}\}_{k=1}^{n}
,
\{\mu_{k}\}_{k=1}^{n}
.
In this case

\displaystyle\lambda_{1}>\mu_{1}>\lambda_{2}>\mu_{2}>\ldots\lambda_{1}>\mu_{n}\geq 0,

and
\{\lambda_{k}\}_{k=1}^{n}
,
\{\mu_{k}\}_{k=1}^{n}
are exactly the eigenvalues of
W
and
W_{1}
.

Condition (
5.4
) holds in this case if and only if
\mu_{n}=0
, and the condition
(
5.5
) is never satisfied. This agrees with the simple fact that a finite
rank Hankel operator always have a non-trivial kernel.

6.
The abstract spectral data for general compact operators

Consider now the case when

is a general

\mathcal{H}_{0}:=\overline{\operatorname{span}}\{R^{n}p:n\geq 0\}
, and let

The spectral measures

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}

\displaystyle:=\overline{\operatorname{span}}\{\operatorname{Ker}(R-\lambda_{k}I):k\geq 1\}\ominus\mathcal{H}_{0},

Trivially

Similarly, we can conclude that

Trivially

Let

,

If

The proof is elementary, an we leave it as an exercise for the reader; see also
[

]
.

In this section we will construct an operator

satisfying

(7.2)

We will show that for the abstract spectral data from Section

the operator

All the steps are pretty elementary, so essentially we will get the asymptotic stability for free.

7.1.
Construction of
A

One can immediately see that an operator
A
formally
given by

\displaystyle A=R^{-1/2}\Sigma^{*}R^{1/2}=R^{-1/2}R_{1}\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{1}\boldsymbol{\varphi}^{*}R^{1/2}

formally satisfies (
7.2
). Let us make this construction rigorous.

We know that
R_{1}^{2}\leq R^{2}
, so by Heinz inequality with exponent
1/2
we have that
R_{1}\leq R
,
and so by Douglas Lemma (Lemma
4.3
), the operator
Q:=R_{1}^{1/2}R^{-1/2}
(defined
initially on a dense set
\operatorname{Ran}R^{1/2}
) extends to a contraction (
\|R_{1}^{1/2}R^{-1/2}\|\leq 1
),
and its adjoint is given by
Q^{*}=R^{-1/2}R^{1/2}
(and is defined on the whole space
\mathcal{H}
).

Defining

\displaystyle A:=Q^{*}\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{1}Q\boldsymbol{\varphi}^{*}

(7.3)

we immediately see that
\|A\|\leq 1
. Using the commutation
relations
\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{1}R_{1}^{1/2}=R_{1}^{1/2}\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{1}
and
\boldsymbol{\varphi}^{*}R^{-1/2}=R^{-1/2}\boldsymbol{\varphi}^{*}
, for the
latter see Remark
4.4
, we can write
A=R^{-1/2}R_{1}\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{1}\boldsymbol{\varphi}^{*}R^{1/2}
and see
that (
7.2
) is satisfied.

7.2.
The structure of
Q

Recall that we defined
\mathcal{H}_{0}
as
\mathcal{H}_{0}:=\overline{\operatorname{span}}\{R^{n}p:n\geq 0\}
.

Lemma 7.3
.

The operator
Q
with respect to the decomposition
\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{H}_{0}\oplus\mathcal{H}_{0}^{\perp}
has the
block diagonal structure

\displaystyle Q=\begin{pmatrix}Q_{0}&0\\
0&I\end{pmatrix},

(7.4)

where
Q_{0}
is a strict contraction
(
i.e.
\|Q_{0}x\|<\|x\|
for all
x\neq 0
)
.

The proof can be found in
[
22
]
. For the reader’s convenience we
present it here.

Proof of Lemma
7.3
.

First, notice that
\mathcal{H}_{0}
and
\mathcal{H}_{0}^{\perp}
are invariant for both
R^{2}
and
R_{1}^{2}
and that

R^{2}
and
R_{1}^{2}
coincide on
\mathcal{H}_{0}^{\perp}
. Therefore
\mathcal{H}_{0}
and
\mathcal{H}_{0}^{\perp}
are both invariant
for
R^{1/2}
and
R_{1}^{1/2}
and
R^{1/2}
coincides with
R_{1}^{1/2}
on
\mathcal{H}_{0}^{\perp}
, so the
operator
Q
has the block diagonal structure (
7.4
). Thus, to prove the lemma, we only
need to show that
Q_{0}
is a strict contraction.

Direct computations show that

\displaystyle Q^{*}QRQ^{*}Q

\displaystyle=R^{-1/2}R_{1}^{2}R^{-1/2}=R^{-1/2}(R^{2}-pp^{*})R^{-1/2}

\displaystyle=R-R^{-1/2}p(R^{-1/2}p)^{*}

(7.5)

(note that since
\|R^{-1}p\|\leq 1
we can conclude that
\|R^{-1/2}p\|^{2}=\langle R^{-1}p,p\rangle\leq\|p\|
, so
R^{-1/2}p\in\mathcal{H}
).

Take now
x\in\mathcal{H}
such that
\|Qx\|=\|x\|
. This is equivalent to the identity

\displaystyle\langle(I-Q^{*}Q)x,x\rangle=0,

and since
I-Q^{*}Q\geq 0
, is equivalent to
(I-Q^{*}Q)x=0
, or equivalently, that
Q^{*}Qx=x
.

Using identity
Q^{*}Qx=x
and (
7.5
) we get

\displaystyle\langle Rx,x\rangle=\langle Q^{*}QRQ^{*}Qx,x\rangle=\langle Rx,x\rangle-|\langle x,R^{-1/2}p\rangle|^{2},

so
x\perp R^{-1/2}p
. Then

\displaystyle Q^{*}QRx=Q^{*}QRQ^{*}Qx=Rx-R^{-1/2}p(R^{-1/2}p)^{*}x=Rx;

the first equality here follows from
Q^{*}Qx=x
, the second one from (
7.5
), and the last
one from the above orthogonality
x\perp R^{-1/2}p
.

Therefore
Q^{*}QRx=Rx
, so
\|Q^{*}QRx\|=\|Rx\|
. Thus

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{1}:=\{x\in\mathcal{H}:\|Qx\|=\|x\|\}=\operatorname{Ker}(I-Q^{*}Q)

is an
R
-invariant subspace, orthogonal to
R^{-1/2}p
.

Since
\mathcal{H}_{1}
is
R
-invariant, it is also
R^{1/2}
-invariant, so
\mathcal{H}_{1}
is orthogonal to

\displaystyle\overline{\operatorname{span}}\{R^{-1/2+k/2}:k\geq 0\}\subset\overline{\operatorname{span}}\{R^{k/2}:k\geq 0\}\supset\mathcal{H}_{0}

(in fact, we have equalities above, not just inclusions, but this is not necessary for the proof),
so
\|Qx\|<\|x\|
for any
x\in\mathcal{H}_{0}\setminus\{0\}
. But this exactly means that

Q_{0}=Q\big|_{\mathcal{H}_{0}}
is a
strict contraction.
∎

7.3.
Weak asymptotic stability of a completely non-unitary contraction

Recall that a contraction
T
is called
completely non-unitary
, if there is no reducing
subspace for
T
on which
T
acts unitarily.

Lemma 7.4
.

A completely non-unitary contraction
T
on a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
is always weakly
asymptotically stable.

We will show later in Section
7.4
that the contraction
A
constructed above in
Section
7.1
is weakly asymptotically stable, which will complete the proof of
asymptotic stability of
\Sigma^{*}
.

Proof of Lemma
7.4
.

Every completely non-unitary contraction
T
admits the
functional model
, i.e. it
is
unitaryly equivalent to the model operator
\mathcal{M}_{\theta}
on the model space
\mathcal{K}_{\theta}
, where

\theta
is the so-called
characteristic function
of
T
, see for example
[
20
, Sect. VI.2]
,
[
17
, Ch. 1, Sect. 1.3]
. Without going into details, which
are not important for our purposes, we just mention that the model space
\mathcal{K}_{\theta}
is a subspace
of a vector-valued space
L^{2}(E)=L^{2}(\mathbb{T},m;E)
of square integrable (with respect to the normalized
Lebesgue measure
m
on
\mathbb{T}
) functions with values in an auxiliary Hilbert space
E
. The model
operator
\mathcal{M}_{\theta}
, to which
A
is unitarily equivalent, is just the
compression
of the
multiplication operator
M_{z}
by the independent variable
z

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{\theta}f=P{\rule[-3.01385pt]{0.0pt}{4.30554pt}}_{\!\mathcal{K}_{\theta}}M_{z}f,\qquad f\in\mathcal{K}_{\theta};

recall that the multiplication operator
M_{z}
is defined by
M_{z}f(z)=zf(z)
,
z\in\mathbb{T}
.

What is also essential for our purposes, is that the multiplication operator
M_{z}
is the

dilation
of the model operator
\mathcal{M}_{\theta}
, i.e. that for all
n\geq 1

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{\theta}^{n}f=P{\rule[-3.01385pt]{0.0pt}{4.30554pt}}_{\!\mathcal{K}_{\theta}}M_{z}^{n}f,\qquad f\in\mathcal{K}_{\theta}.

Since trivially
M_{z}^{n}\to 0
in the weak operator topology of
B(L^{2}(\mathbb{T},m;E))
as
n\to+\infty
,
we conclude that
\mathcal{M}_{\theta}^{n}\to 0
as
n\to+\infty
in the weak operator topology of

B(\mathcal{K}_{\theta})
, and so
T^{n}\to 0
in the weak operator topology as well.
∎

7.4.
A
is completely non-unitary

Proposition 7.5
.

The contraction

defined above in Section

is completely non-unitary if and only
if

To prove this proposition we need the following simple observation.

.

Let

be a contraction and

(i)

(ii)

.

The proof of this lemma is trivial, and we omit it.

Lemma 7.7

If

is also a strict contraction.

Proof.

If

is a strict contraction, then

is also a strict contraction. Using the polar

, we see that

∎

Proof of Proposition

.

Assume that

is a reducing subspace for

such that
\boldsymbol{\varphi}^{*}E\subset\mathcal{H}_{0}^{\perp}
.

Since

in the second equality we used the fact that

Since

\displaystyle\|Ax\|=\|\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{1}\boldsymbol{\varphi}^{*}x\|=\|x\|,

acts isometrically on

.

Also, this implies that

.
In addition,

is unitary, and (see Remark

) we know

\displaystyle AE=\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{1}\boldsymbol{\varphi}^{*}E=E.

\displaystyle f

8.
Relation to previous work

8.1.
Quick recap of Clark theory

Let us quickly describe basic facts from Clark theory,
[
2
]
, that we will use.

Recall that a function
\theta\in H^{2}
is called
inner
, if
|\theta|=1
a.e. on
\mathbb{T}
.

For an inner function
\theta
, the
model space

\mathcal{K}_{\theta}
is defined as
\mathcal{K}_{\theta}:=H^{2}\ominus\theta H^{2}
, and the
model operator

\mathcal{M}_{\theta}:\mathcal{K}_{\theta}\to\mathcal{K}_{\theta}
is the
compression of the forward shift

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{\theta}f:=P{\rule[-3.01385pt]{0.0pt}{4.30554pt}}_{\!\mathcal{K}_{\theta}}Sf,\qquad f\in\mathcal{K}_{\theta}.

We consider only the simplest version of the theory when
\theta
is an inner function satisfying

\theta(0)=0
, although in
[
2
]
the case of general inner functions was treated.

If
\theta(0)=0
, then
z^{0},\theta\in\mathcal{K}_{\theta}
, and one can consider a rank one unitary
perturbation
\mathcal{U}=\mathcal{U}_{\theta}
of
\mathcal{M}_{\theta}
(i.e. a unitary operator
\mathcal{U}
such that

\operatorname{rank}(\mathcal{M}_{\theta}-\mathcal{U})=1
),

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}f=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\mathcal{M}_{\theta}f=Sf&f\in\mathcal{K}_{\theta}\ominus\theta;\\
z^{0},&f=\theta\end{array}\right.

The Clark measure
\rho=\rho_{\theta}
in this case is the spectral measure of the operator

\mathcal{U}_{\theta}
with
respect to the
vector
z^{0}
. Alternatively, it can be defined as the Borel measure such that

\displaystyle\operatorname{Re}\frac{1+\theta}{1-\theta}=\text{Poisson extension of }\rho,

in the unit disc
\mathbb{D}
, or, equivalently,

\displaystyle\frac{1+\theta(z)}{1-\theta(z)}=\int_{\mathbb{T}}\frac{1+z\overline{\xi}}{1-z\overline{\xi}}\mathrm{d}\rho(\xi),\qquad z\in\mathbb{D}.

For an inner function the Clark measure is always singular, and if
\theta(0)=0
, then
\rho
is
a probability measure. Also, if
\theta
is a finite Blaschke product, then
\rho
is a measure
with finite support

\displaystyle\rho=\sum_{k=1}^{n}a_{k}\delta_{\xi_{k}},\qquad n=\deg\theta.

The Clark operator
\mathcal{C}=\mathcal{C}_{\theta}:\mathcal{K}_{\theta}\to L^{2}(\rho)
,
\rho=\rho_{\theta}
is a unitary
operator, intertwining
\mathcal{U}_{\theta}
and the multiplication operator
M_{\rho}:L^{2}(\rho)\to L^{2}(\rho)
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}\mathcal{U}_{\theta}=M_{\rho}\mathcal{C},

is given by the formula

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}f(\xi)=\lim_{r\to 1^{-}}f(r\xi),\qquad f\in\mathcal{K}_{\theta}.

(8.1)

It was shown in
[
2
]
that convergence in (
8.1
) can be understood in the sense
of convergence in
L^{2}(\rho)
, and it was later shown in
[
18
]
that convergence in
(
8.1
) is
\rho
-a.e., and that this even holds for non-tangential boundary values.
However, the reader does not need to worry about it, because in the case that we are interested in,
when
\theta
is a finite Blaschke product, the subspace
\mathcal{K}_{\theta}
consists of rational
functions, so there is no question about convergence.

Note also that
\mathcal{C}z^{0}=\mathbf{1}\in L^{2}(\rho)
.

9.
Appendix: Proof of the Abstract Borg’s Theorem

In this appendix, we will present the proof of the Abstract Borg’s Theorem (Theorem
5.1
). The proof follows
[
22
]
and presented for the convenience of the reader.

First of all notice that the intertwining condition (
5.3
) implies that the
vector
p
must is cyclic for
W
. Since everything is defined up to unitary equivalence, we can
assume without loss of generality that
W
is the multiplication
M_{s}
by the independent variable

s
in the weighted space
L^{2}(\rho)
, where
\rho
is the spectral measure, corresponding to the
vector
p
.

Here the spectral measure can be defined as the unique (finite, Borel, compactly
supported) measure on
{\mathbb{R}}
such that

\displaystyle((W-zI)^{-1}p,p)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{d\rho(s)}{s-z}\qquad\forall z\in{\mathbb{C}}\setminus{\mathbb{R}}.

Since
W
is a compact operator with eigenvalues
(\lambda_{k}^{2})_{k\geq 1}
, the measure
\rho
is purely atomic, equivalently saying

\displaystyle\rho=\sum_{k\geq 1}a_{k}\delta_{\lambda_{k}^{2}}

(9.1)

for some
a_{k}>0
. Note that in this representation the vector
p
is represented by the function
1
in
L^{2}(\rho)
.

Moreover, the choice of the weights
a_{k}
completely defines the triple
(W,W_{1},p)
(up to
unitary equivalence). Indeed if the weights
a_{k}
are known
(recall that in our representation
p\equiv 1
),
the operator
W_{1}
is uniquely defined in the above spectral representation of
W
as
W_{1}=W-pp^{*}
.

9.1.
Preliminaries

Let us recall some standard facts in perturbation theory. Let
W=W^{*}
be a bounded self-adjoint operator, and let
p
be its cyclic vector. The spectral measure
\rho

corresponding to the vector
p
is defined as the unique Borel measure on
R
such that

\displaystyle F(z):=((W-zI)^{-1}p,p)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{d\rho(s)}{s-z}\qquad\forall z\in{\mathbb{C}}\setminus{\mathbb{R}}.

(9.2)

As it is customary in perturbation theory, define a family of rank one perturbations

\displaystyle W^{\{\alpha\}}:=W+\alpha pp^{*},\qquad\alpha\in{\mathbb{R}}.

Note that in this notation
W_{1}=W^{\{-1\}}
.

The spectral measures
\rho^{\{\alpha\}}
are defined as the unique Borel measures such that

\displaystyle F^{\{\alpha\}}(z):=((W^{\{\alpha\}}-zI)^{-1}p,p)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{d\rho^{\{\alpha\}}(s)}{s-z}\qquad\forall z\in{\mathbb{C}}\setminus{\mathbb{R}};

(9.3)

i.e. the functions
F^{\{\alpha\}}
are the Cauchy transforms of the measures
\rho^{\{\alpha\}}
.

The relation between the Cauchy transforms
F
and
F^{\{\alpha\}}
is given by the famous Aronszajn–Krein formula,

\displaystyle F^{\{\alpha\}}=\frac{F}{1+\alpha F},

(9.4)

which is an easy corollary of the standard resolvent identities.

Recall that our operator
W_{1}
is exactly the operator
W^{\{-1\}}
, so let us for the consistency
denote
F_{1}:=F^{\{-1\}}
. Identity (
9.4
) can then be rewritten as

\displaystyle F_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{F}{1-F}

(9.5)

so

\displaystyle\frac{F}{F_{1}}

\displaystyle=1-F.

(9.6)

Hence to prove the first part of Theorem
5.1
(existence and uniqueness of the triple
W
,

W_{1}
,
p
), it is sufficient to show that there exists a unique measure
\rho
of form (
9.1
)
such that
if
F
is the Cauchy transform of
\rho

\displaystyle F(z)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{d\rho(s)}{s-z}\qquad\forall z\in{\mathbb{C}}\setminus{\mathbb{R}}

(9.7)

then the function
F_{1}
defined by (
9.5
) has the poles exactly at points
\mu_{k}^{2}
.
Here the points
\lambda_{k}^{2}
and
\mu_{k}^{2}
are given, and the weights
a_{k}>0
are to be found.

Remark 9.1
.

The intertwining relations (
5.3
) follow immediately from (
9.5
). Indeed,
one can see from (
9.5
) that poles
\mu_{k}^{2}
of
F_{1}
are exactly the points
x\in{\mathbb{R}}

where
F(x)=1
. The integral representation (
9.7
) can be written as

\displaystyle F(x)=\sum_{k\geq 1}\frac{a_{k}}{\lambda_{k}^{2}-x}.

Each term is positive for
x<\lambda_{k}^{2}
and negative for
x>\lambda_{k}^{2}
, so it is not hard to
conclude that

\displaystyle\lambda_{1}^{2}>\mu_{1}^{2}>\lambda_{2}^{2}>\mu_{2}^{2}\ldots>\lambda_{k}^{2}>\mu_{k}^{2}>\ldots\geq 0,

which is equivalent to the intertwining relations (
5.3
).

9.2.
Guessing
the function
F

We want to reconstruct the spectral
measure
\rho
from the sequences
\{\lambda_{k}\}_{k\geq 1}
and
\{\mu_{k}\}_{k\geq 1}
.

Denote

\displaystyle\sigma:=\sigma(W)=\{\lambda_{k}^{2}\}_{k\geq 1}\cup\{0\},\qquad\sigma_{1}:=\sigma(W_{1})=\{\mu_{k}^{2}\}_{k\geq 1}\cup\{0\}.

It trivial that
F
and
F_{1}
are analytic in
{\mathbb{C}}\setminus\sigma
and
{\mathbb{C}}\setminus\sigma_{1}

respectively, and having simple poles at points
\lambda_{k}
and
\mu_{k}
,
k\geq 1
respectively
(note that while the measure
\rho_{1}=\rho^{\{-1\}}
can have a mass at
0
, the point
0
is not
an isolated singularity).

Identity (
9.6
) (which holds on
{\mathbb{C}}\setminus(\sigma\cup\sigma_{1})
) implies that
F/F_{1}

has simple poles at the points
\lambda_{k}^{2}
,
k\geq 1
, and that it can be analytically extended to

{\mathbb{C}}\setminus\sigma
. The (isolated) zeroes of
1-F
must be at points where
F(z)=0
, so they must
be only at the poles of
F_{1}
, i.e. at the points
\mu_{k}^{2}
,
k\geq 1
.

So the function
F/F_{1}=1-F
must be a function analytic on
{\mathbb{C}}\setminus\sigma
with simple poles
at the points
\lambda_{k}^{2}
,
k\geq 1
and simple zeroes at the points
\mu_{k}^{2}
.

One can try to guess such a function. The simplest guess would be

\displaystyle 1-F(z)=\prod_{k\geq 1}\left(\frac{z-\mu_{k}^{2}}{z-\lambda_{k}^{2}}\right)\,.

We will show that this is indeed the case, namely that the product converges and
1-F
is indeed
given by the above formula.
From there we will trivially get the formula for
F
,
and then compute the weights
a_{k}
.

9.2.1.
Convergence of the product

Denote

\displaystyle\Phi(z):=\prod_{k\geq 0}\left(\frac{z-\mu_{k}^{2}}{z-\lambda_{k}^{2}}\right)\,.

(9.8)

We have guessed that
1-F(z)=\Phi(z)
. But first we need to show that the product (
9.8
)
is well defined.

Lemma 9.2
.

The product (
9.8
) converges uniformly on compact subsets of
{\mathbb{C}}\setminus\sigma
, and
moreover

\displaystyle\Phi(\infty):=\lim_{z\to\infty}\Phi(z)=1

(9.9)

Proof.

Fix a compact
K\subset{\mathbb{C}}\setminus\sigma

Trivially

\displaystyle\left|1-\frac{z-\mu_{k}^{2}}{z-\lambda_{k}^{2}}\right|=\left|\frac{\lambda_{k}^{2}-\mu_{k}^{2}}{z-\lambda_{k}^{2}}\right|\leq C(K)(\lambda_{k}^{2}-\mu_{k}^{2})\qquad\forall z\in K.

(9.10)

Since

\displaystyle\sum_{k\geq 1}(\lambda_{k}^{2}-\mu_{k}^{2})\leq\sum_{k\geq 1}(\lambda_{k}^{2}-\lambda_{k+1}^{2})\leq\lambda_{1}^{2}<\infty

(9.11)

we have

\displaystyle\sum_{k\geq 1}\left|1-\frac{z-\mu_{k}^{2}}{z-\lambda_{k}^{2}}\right|\leq C(K)\lambda_{1}^{2}<\infty\qquad\forall z\in{\mathbb{C}}\setminus\sigma.

But this implies that the product (
9.8
) converges uniformly on compact subsets of

{\mathbb{C}}\setminus\sigma
.

To prove the second statement, take any
R>2\lambda_{1}^{2}
. Then, see (
9.10
)

\displaystyle\left|1-\frac{z-\mu_{k}^{2}}{z-\lambda_{k}^{2}}\right|=\left|\frac{\lambda_{k}^{2}-\mu_{k}^{2}}{z-\lambda_{k}^{2}}\right|\leq\frac{2}{R}(\lambda_{k}^{2}-\mu_{k}^{2})\qquad\forall z:\ |z|>R.

Using (
9.11
) we get that for all
|z|>R

\displaystyle\sum_{k\geq 1}\left|1-\frac{z-\mu_{k}^{2}}{z-\lambda_{k}^{2}}\right|\leq\frac{2\lambda_{1}^{2}}{R},

which immediately implies (
9.9
).
∎

9.3.
Herglotz functions and the uniqueness

To prove the uniqueness part of Theorem
5.1
, it is sufficient to show that our
function
F
must be
exactly
1-\Phi(z)
. Let us recall the following definition.

Definition 9.3
.

An analytic function
f
defined on the upper half-place is Herglotz (or
Nevanlinna) if
\operatorname{Im}f(z)\geq 0
for all
z\in{\mathbb{C}}_{+}
.

Note, that the Cauchy Transform of a measure supported on a real line is a Herglotz function.

Lemma 9.4
.

The function
\Phi
defined by (
9.8
) satisfies the following properties

(i)

\Phi(\overline{z})=\overline{\Phi(z)}
; in particular,
\Phi(x)
is real for all

x\in{\mathbb{R}}\setminus\sigma
.

(ii)

The function
\Phi
has simple poles at points
\lambda_{k}^{2}
, and its only zeroes are the
simple zeroes at points
\mu_{k}^{2}
.

(iii)

\displaystyle\lim_{z\to\infty}\Phi(z)=1
.

(iv)

The function
-\Phi
is a Herglotz
(
Nevanlina
)

function in
{\mathbb{C}}_{+}
, i.e.
\operatorname{Im}\Phi(z)<0
for
all
z\in{\mathbb{C}}_{+}
.

Proof of Lemma
9.4
.

Statements (i), (ii) are trivial, statement (iii) was stated and proved in Lemma
9.2
above.

Let us prove statement (iv). Let
\Arg
denote the
principal value
of the argument, taking
values in the interval
(-\pi,\pi]
. It is easy to see that for
z\in{\mathbb{C}}_{+}

\displaystyle-\Arg\left(\frac{z-\mu_{k}^{2}}{z-\lambda_{k}^{2}}\right)=-\Arg\left(\frac{\mu_{k}^{2}-z}{\lambda_{k}^{2}-z}\right)=\alpha_{k}(z)

where
\alpha_{k}(z)>0
is the aperture of the angle at which an observer at the point
z\in{\mathbb{C}}_{+}

sees the interval
[\mu_{k}^{2},\lambda_{k}^{2}]
. Since for the whole real line
{\mathbb{R}}
the aperture is

\pi
and the intervals
[\mu_{k}^{2},\lambda_{k}^{2}]
do not intersecr
{\mathbb{R}}_{-}=(-\infty,0)
, we can
conclude that

\displaystyle 0<-\sum_{k\geq 1}\Arg\left(\frac{\mu_{k}^{2}-z}{\lambda_{k}^{2}-z}\right)<\pi,

so
-\operatorname{Im}\Phi(z)>0
for all
z\in{\mathbb{C}}_{+}
.
∎

it is easy to see that the function
1-F
satisfies properties (i)–(iv) from Lemma

9.4
. So the fact that
F=1-\Phi
follows from the Lemma below.

Lemma 9.5
.

The function
\Phi
defined by (
9.8
) is the only analytic in

{\mathbb{C}}\setminus\sigma
function satisfying properties
(i)
–
(iv)
of Lemma
9.4
.

Proof.

Let
\Phi_{1}
be another such function.
Both functions have simple poles at
\lambda_{k}^{2}
and simple zeros at
\mu_{k}^{2}
(and these are the
only isolated singularities and zeroes for both functions), hence
\Psi(z):=\Phi(z)/\Phi_{1}(z)
is
analytic and zero-free in
{\mathbb{C}}\setminus\{0\}
.

Moreover, for
x\in{\mathbb{R}}\setminus\{0\}
we have
\Psi(x)>0
. Indeed, on
{\mathbb{R}}\setminus\sigma\setminus\sigma_{1}
the functions
\Phi_{1,2}
are real and have the same sign, so
\Psi(x)>0

on
{\mathbb{R}}\setminus\sigma\setminus\sigma_{1}
. Since
\Psi
is continuous and zero-free on
{\mathbb{R}}\setminus\{0\}
, this tells us that
\Psi
is positive on
{\mathbb{R}}\setminus\{0\}
.

Next, let us notice that
\Psi(z)
does not take real negative values. If
\operatorname{Im}z>0
, then

\operatorname{Im}\Phi(z)>0
,
\operatorname{Im}\Phi_{1}(z)>0
, so
\Psi(z)=\Phi(z)/\Phi_{1}(z)
cannot be negative real. If
\operatorname{Im}z<0
, the symmetry
\Psi(\overline{z})=\overline{\Psi(z)}
implies the same conclusion. And, as we
just discussed above, on the real line
\Psi
takes positive real values.

So,
\Psi
omits infinitely many points, therefore by the Picard’s Theorem the point
0
is not an
essential singularity for
\Psi
. Trivial analysis shows that
0
cannot be a pole, otherwise

1/\Psi
is analytic at 0, which also contradicts to the fact that
\Psi
can’t take negative real
values. Hence the point
0
is a removable singularity for function
\Psi
, so
\Psi
is an entire
function.
By Liouville’s Theorem, condition
\Psi(\infty)=1
implies that
\Psi\equiv 1
for all
z\in{\mathbb{C}}
, so
\Psi\equiv\Psi_{1}

We can avoid using Picard’s theorem by considering the square root
\Psi^{1/2}
, where we take the
principal branch of the square root (cut along the negative half-axis). Since
\Psi
does not take
negative real values, the function
\Psi^{1/2}
is well defined (and analytic) on
{\mathbb{C}}\setminus\{0\}
. Trivially
\operatorname{Re}\Psi(z)^{1/2}\geq 0
, so by the Casorati–Weierstrass Theorem

cannot be
the essential singularity for
\Psi^{1/2}
. Again, trivial reasoning shows that
0
cannot be a
pole, so again,
\Psi^{1/2}
is an entire function. The condition
\Psi^{1/2}(\infty)=1
then
implies that
\Psi^{1/2}(z)\equiv 1
.
∎

9.4.
Existence and computing the spectral measure
\rho

To prove the existence part of Theorem
5.1
, we just need to show that the function

F=1-\Phi
is the Cauchy Transform of a measure
\rho

\displaystyle\rho=\sum_{n\geq 1}a_{n}\delta_{\lambda_{n}^{2}},\qquad a_{n}>0.

(9.12)

One can almost get this result for free.
Namely, the function
F=1-\Phi
is a Herglotz function, analytic in
{\mathbb{C}}\setminus\sigma
, and
statement
(iii) of Lemma
9.4
implies that
F(\infty)=0
. Thus,
it follows from general theory of Herglotz functions that the function that
F
is a Cauchy
transform of a measure
\rho
supported on
\sigma
. Moreover, since
F
has simple poles at points

\lambda_{n}^{2}
, one can see that
\rho(\{\lambda_{n}^{2}\})\neq 0
. Therefore, to prove existence, it
remains to show that the measure
\rho
does not have an atom at
0
.

But a proof of this fact
would require some computation, which is not much easier than
an elementary proof using the partial fraction decomposition of the function

\Phi
presented below. This proof does not use any theory of Herglotz functions, and also gives
formulae for weights
a_{n}
.

Lemma 9.6
.

The function
\Phi
defined by (
9.8
) can be decomposed as

\displaystyle\Phi(z)=1-\sum_{n\geq 1}\frac{a_{n}}{\lambda_{n}^{2}-z},

(9.13)

where

\displaystyle a_{n}=(\lambda_{n}^{2}-\mu_{n}^{2})\prod_{k\neq n}\left(\frac{\lambda_{n}^{2}-\mu_{k}^{2}}{\lambda_{n}^{2}-\lambda_{k}^{2}}\right)

(9.14)

This representation immediately implies that
F
is the Cauchy Transform of the measure
\rho

given by (
9.12
) with weights
a_{n}
defined by (
9.14
).

Proof of Lemma
9.6
.

Consider functions
\Phi{\rule[-3.01385pt]{0.0pt}{4.30554pt}}_{\!N}

\displaystyle\Phi{\rule[-3.01385pt]{0.0pt}{4.30554pt}}_{\!N}(z)=\prod_{k=1}^{N}\left(\frac{z-\mu_{k}^{2}}{z-\lambda_{k}^{2}}\right)

Trivially

\displaystyle\Phi{\rule[-3.01385pt]{0.0pt}{4.30554pt}}_{\!N}(z)=1-\sum_{n\geq 1}\frac{a_{n}^{N}}{\lambda_{n}^{2}-z}

(9.15)

where

\displaystyle a_{n}^{N}=(\lambda_{n}^{2}-\mu_{n}^{2})\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}k=1\\
k\neq n\end{subarray}}^{N}\left(\frac{\lambda_{n}^{2}-\mu_{k}^{2}}{\lambda_{n}^{2}-\lambda_{k}^{2}}\right)\qquad\text{if }n\leq N,

and
a_{n}^{N}=0
if
n>N
.

We know, see Lemma
9.2
that
\Phi_{N}(z)
converges to
\Phi(z)
uniformly on
compact subsets of
{\mathbb{C}}\setminus\sigma
.

Hence, to prove the lemma it remains to show that

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{n=1}^{N}\frac{a_{n}^{N}}{\lambda_{n}^{2}-z}\to\sum\limits_{n\geq 1}\frac{a_{n}}{\lambda_{n}^{2}-z}\qquad\text{as }N\to\infty

uniformly on compact subsets of
{\mathbb{C}}\setminus\sigma
.

Take
z=0
in (
9.15
). Then

\displaystyle 1-\sum\limits_{n\geq 1}\frac{a_{n}^{N}}{\lambda_{n}^{2}}=\prod\limits_{k=1}^{N}\left(\frac{\mu_{k}^{2}}{\lambda_{k}^{2}}\right)>0,

so
\sum\limits_{n\geq 1}\frac{a_{n}^{N}}{\lambda_{n}^{2}}\leq 1
.

Notice that for any fixed
n
the sequence
a_{n}^{N}\nearrow a_{n}
as
N\to\infty
, so

\sum\limits_{n\geq 1}\frac{a_{n}}{\lambda_{n}^{2}}\leq 1
.

Take an arbitrary compact
K\subset{\mathbb{C}}\setminus\sigma
. Clearly for any
z\in K

\left|\frac{a_{n}^{N}}{\lambda_{n}^{2}-z}\right|\leq\frac{a_{n}^{N}}{\operatorname{dist}(K,\sigma)}\leq\frac{a_{n}}{\operatorname{dist}(K,\sigma)}\leq\frac{\lambda_{1}^{2}}{\operatorname{dist}(K,\sigma)}\cdot\frac{a_{n}}{\lambda_{n}^{2}},

so the condition
\sum_{n\geq 1}a_{n}/\lambda_{n}^{2}\leq 1
implies that
the series
\sum\limits_{n\geq 1}\frac{a_{n}^{N}}{\lambda_{n}^{2}-z}
converges uniformly on the compact

K
.
∎

9.5.
The trivial kernel condition

To complete the proof of Theorem
5.1
, it remains to show that the condition
\|W^{-1/2}p\|=1
is equivalent to (
5.4
), and that if (
5.4
) holds, then
the condition
\|W^{-1}p\|=\infty
is equivalent to (
5.5
).

Let us first investigate the condition
\|W^{-1/2}p\|=1
. The operator
W
is the multiplication
by the independent variable
s
in the weighted space
L^{2}(\rho)
, where
\rho=\sum_{k\geq 1}a_{n}\delta_{\lambda_{n}^{2}}
with
a_{n}
given by
(

).
Recall, see Lemma
9.6
, that

\displaystyle\prod\limits_{k=1}^{\infty}\Bigg(\frac{z-\mu_{k}^{2}}{z-\lambda_{k}^{2}}\Bigg)=1-\sum\limits_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{a_{k}}{\lambda_{k}^{2}-z}

(9.16)

Plugging the real
x<0
into (

) and
taking the limit of both sides as

we get that

\displaystyle\prod\limits_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{\mu_{k}^{2}}{\lambda_{k}^{2}}=1-\sum\limits_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{a_{k}}{\lambda_{k}^{2}};

the interchange of limit and sum (product) can be justified, for example, by the monotone
convergence theorem.

Therefore
\sum_{k\geq 1}a_{k}/\lambda_{k}^{2}=1
if and only if
\prod_{k\geq 0}(\mu_{k}^{2}/\lambda_{k}^{2})=0
. The latter condition is equivalent to

\displaystyle\sum_{k\geq 1}\left(1-\frac{\mu_{k}^{2}}{\lambda_{k}^{2}}\right)=\infty,

which is exactly the condition (
5.4
).

Now, let us investigate the condition
\|W^{-1}p\|=\infty
. It can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\sum_{k\geq 1}\frac{a_{k}}{\lambda_{k}^{4}}=\infty.

(9.17)

Rewriting identity (
9.16
) as

\displaystyle\prod_{k=1}^{\infty}\left(\frac{z-\mu_{k}^{2}}{z-\lambda_{k}^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle=1-\sum\limits_{k=1}^{\infty}a_{k}\frac{(\lambda_{k}^{2}-z)+z}{\lambda_{k}^{2}(\lambda_{k}^{2}-z)}

\displaystyle=1-\sum\limits_{k\geq 1}\frac{a_{k}}{\lambda_{k}^{2}}-\sum\limits_{k\geq 1}\frac{a_{k}z}{\lambda_{k}^{2}(\lambda_{k}^{2}-z)},

we see that if the condition (
5.4
) holds, then

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{z}\prod\limits_{k=1}^{\infty}\Bigg(\frac{z-\mu_{k}^{2}}{z-\lambda_{k}^{2}}\Bigg)=\sum\limits_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{a_{k}}{\lambda_{k}^{2}(\lambda_{k}^{2}-z)}.

Substituting
z=-\lambda_{N}^{2}
we get that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\lambda_{N}^{2}}\prod\limits_{k=1}^{\infty}\Bigg(\frac{\lambda_{N}^{2}+\mu_{k}^{2}}{\lambda_{N}^{2}+\lambda_{k}^{2}}\Bigg)=\sum\limits_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{a_{k}}{\lambda_{k}^{2}(\lambda_{N}^{2}+\lambda_{k}^{2})}.

By the monotone convergence theorem

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}\sum\limits_{k\geq 1}\frac{a_{k}}{\lambda_{k}^{2}(\lambda_{N}^{2}+\lambda_{k}^{2})}=\sum\limits_{k\geq 1}\frac{a_{k}}{\lambda_{k}^{4}},

so the condition (
9.17
) can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{\lambda_{N}^{2}}\prod\limits_{k=1}^{\infty}\Bigg(\frac{\lambda_{N}^{2}+\mu_{k}^{2}}{\lambda_{N}^{2}+\lambda_{k}^{2}}\Bigg)=\infty

(9.18)

The following simple lemma completes the proof.

Lemma 9.7
.

The condition (
9.18
) holds if and only if

\displaystyle\sum_{k\geq 1}\left(\frac{\mu_{k}^{2}}{\lambda_{k+1}^{2}}-1\right)=\infty.

Proof.

First of all notice that

\displaystyle 0<C\leq\prod\limits_{k=N}^{\infty}\Bigg(\frac{\lambda_{N}^{2}+\mu_{k}^{2}}{\lambda_{N}^{2}+\lambda_{k}^{2}}\Bigg)\leq 1

(9.19)

with
C>0
independent of
N
.

Indeed, for all
k\geq N
we trivially have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\leq\frac{\lambda_{N}^{2}+\mu_{k}^{2}}{\lambda_{N}^{2}+\lambda_{k}^{2}}\leq 1.

(9.20)

The upper bound in (
9.20
) trivially implies the upper bound in (
9.19
).

To get the lower bound in (
9.19
) we use the estimate (
9.20
)
and the inequality

\displaystyle\ln x\geq(\ln 2)(x-1),\qquad\forall\ x\in[1/2,1].

Thus

\displaystyle\sum_{k=N}^{\infty}\ln 2\left(\frac{\lambda_{N}^{2}+\mu_{k}^{2}}{\lambda_{N}^{2}+\lambda_{k}^{2}}-1\right)=\sum_{k=N}^{\infty}-\ln 2\frac{\lambda_{k}^{2}-\mu_{k}^{2}}{\lambda_{N}^{2}+\lambda_{k}^{2}}\geq-\ln 2\sum_{k=N}^{\infty}\frac{\lambda_{k}^{2}-\mu_{k}^{2}}{\lambda_{N}^{2}}\geq-\ln 2

we see that the lower bound in (
9.19
) holds with
C_{1}=\frac{1}{2}
.

The estimate (
9.19
) implies that the condition (
9.18
) is
equivalent to

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{\lambda_{N}^{2}}\prod\limits_{k=1}^{N-1}\Bigg(\frac{\lambda_{N}^{2}+\mu_{k}^{2}}{\lambda_{N}^{2}+\lambda_{k}^{2}}\Bigg)=\infty.

Since
\lambda_{N}^{2}\leq\lambda_{N}^{2}+\lambda_{k}^{2}\leq 2\lambda_{N}^{2}
for
k\geq N
, the above
condition is equivalent to

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}\prod\limits_{k=1}^{N-1}\Bigg(\frac{\lambda_{N}^{2}+\mu_{k}^{2}}{\lambda_{N}^{2}+\lambda_{k+1}^{2}}\Bigg)=\infty,

(9.21)

Denote

\displaystyle G_{N}(z):=\prod\limits_{k=1}^{N-1}\Bigg(\frac{\mu_{k}^{2}-z}{\lambda_{k+1}^{2}-z}\Bigg)

The function
G_{N}
is analytic in the half-plane
\operatorname{Re}z<\lambda_{N}^{2}
and satisfies the
inequality
|G_{N}(z)|\geq 1
there. Therefore the function
\ln|G_{N}|
is harmonic and non-negative in
the disc
D_{N}
of radius
2\lambda_{N}^{2}
centered at
-\lambda_{N}^{2}
. So by the Harnack inequality

\displaystyle\frac{1}{3}\ln|G_{N}(-\lambda_{N}^{2})|\leq\ln|G_{N}(0)|\leq 3\ln|G_{N}(-\lambda_{N}^{2})|.

Note that
G_{N}(0)=\prod_{k=1}^{N-1}\mu_{k}^{2}/\lambda_{k+1}^{2}
, so the condition (
9.21
) (which translates to the condition
\lim_{N\to\infty}G{\rule[-3.01385pt]{0.0pt}{4.30554pt}}_{\!N}(-\lambda_{N}^{2})=\infty
) is
equivalent to

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}G{\rule[-3.01385pt]{0.0pt}{4.30554pt}}_{\!N}(0)=\lim_{N\to\infty}\prod_{k=1}^{N-1}\frac{\mu_{k}^{2}}{\lambda_{k+1}^{2}}=\infty.

The latter condition is equivalent to the condition

\displaystyle\prod_{k\geq 1}\frac{\mu_{k}^{2}}{\lambda_{k+1}^{2}}=\infty,

which in turn is equivalent to (
5.5
).
∎
