Cutoff profile of ASEP on a segment
Alexey Bufetov, Peter Nejjar

Abstract
. This paper studies the mixing behavior of the Asymmetric Simple Exclusion Process (ASEP) on a segment of length N N . Our main result is that for particle densities in ( 0 , 1 ) , (0,1), the total-variation cutoff window of ASEP is N 1 / 3 N^{1/3} and the cutoff profile is 1 − F GUE , 1-F_{\mathrm{GUE}}, where F GUE F_{\mathrm{GUE}} is the Tracy-Widom distribution function. This also gives a new proof of the cutoff itself, shown earlier by Labbé and Lacoin. Our proof combines coupling arguments, the result of Tracy-Widom about fluctuations of ASEP started from the step initial condition, and exact algebraic identities coming from interpreting the multi-species ASEP as a random walk on a Hecke algebra.



1.
Introduction

We consider ASEP on the segment
[1;N]:=\{1,\ldots,N\}
with
k\leq N
particles. This is a continuous time Markov chain with state space

\Omega^{N,k}:=\left\{\xi\in\{0,1\}^{N}:\sum_{i=1}^{N}\xi(i)=k\right\}.

We think of the
1^{\prime}s
as particles, and of the
0^{\prime}s
as holes. The dynamics of ASEP can be described as follows: Each particle waits an exponential time with parameter
1
, after which with probability
p>1/2
it attempts to make a unit step to the right, and with probability
q=1-p<1/2
it attempts to make a unit step to the left. The attempt is succesfull if the target site lies in
[1;N]
and is occupied by a hole, the hole and the particle exchanging their positions when the particle moves a unit step. If the attempt is not successful, nothing happens.

For
\xi\in\Omega^{N,k},
we denote by
\xi_{t}
the state at time
t
of the ASEP started from
\xi,
and we denote by
P_{t}^{\xi}
the law of
\xi_{t}
.
The ASEP dynamics on
[1;N]
with
k
particles has a unique stationary measure which we denote by
\pi_{N,k}
.

Recall that the total-variation distance of two probability measures
\mu,\mu^{\prime}
on
\Omega^{N,k}
is given by

||\mu-\mu^{\prime}||_{\mathrm{TV}}:=\max_{A\subset\Omega^{N,k}}|\mu(A)-\mu^{\prime}(A)|.

We define the maximal total-variation distance between the distribution at given time and the stationary distribution as

d^{N,k}(t):=\max_{\xi\in\Omega^{N,k}}||P_{t}^{\xi}-\pi_{N,k}||_{\mathrm{TV}},

and for
c\in\mathbb{R},
we define the time point

(1)

g(k,c):=\frac{(\sqrt{k}+\sqrt{N-k})^{2}+cN^{1/3}}{p-q}.

The main result of this paper is the following:

Theorem 1
.

Assume that
k=k_{N}
satisfies
\lim_{N\to\infty}k_{N}/N=\alpha
, and
\alpha\in(0,1)
. For any
c\in\mathbb{R}
we have

\lim_{N\to\infty}d^{N,k_{N}}\left(g(k_{N},c)\right)=1-F_{\mathrm{GUE}}(cf(\alpha)),

where
f(\alpha)=\frac{(\alpha(1-\alpha))^{1/6}}{(\sqrt{\alpha}+\sqrt{1-\alpha})^{4/3}}
, and
F_{\mathrm{GUE}}
is the
\mathrm{GUE}
Tracy-Widom distribution defined in (
10
).

Proof.

This is an immediate consequence of Theorem
3
(which gives an upper bound for the limit on the lefthand side), proven in Section
3
, and Theorem
6
(which gives a lower bound), proven in Section
4
.
∎

Theorem
1
gives the cutoff window and the cutoff profile (or shape) of ASEP, we refer to Chapter 18 of the textbook
[
23
]
by Levin-Peres (with contributions by Wilmer) for definitions and examples in the general context of Markov chains. A fortiori, Theorem
1
also gives an independent proof of the cutoff itself, which was previously shown by Labbé-Lacoin in
[
22
, Theorem 2]
. On
[
22
, page 1556]
the authors mention that the cutoff window for the process is expected to be
N^{1/3}
and that the cutoff profile is expected to be a function of the
\mathrm{Airy}_{2}
process. Our Theorem
1
confirms (and gives a precise meaning to) this conjecture.

1.1.
Historic overview

Detailed information about the relaxation of ergodic Markov chains to equilibrium has been the goal of a vast literature, see e.g. classical works by Aldous
[
1
]
, Diaconis-Shahshahani
[
15
]
, the review article by Diaconis
[
13
]
, the aforementioned textbook
[
23
]
, and references therein. Of particular interest is the so-called
cutoff
phenomenon; a sequence of Markov chains exhibits this phenomenon if the distance between its distribution and the stationary measure abruptly falls from 1 to 0 on a certain time scale. There are different metrics for this distance, leading to different notions of cutoff, see the paper
[
20
]
by Hermon-Lacoin-Peres for their differences and similarities. The most commonly used are separation and total-variation cutoff, we study the latter in this paper. Once the cutoff phenomenon is established, it is natural to ask for a more refined information: What happens at the critical time point on a finer scale? The answer to this question is given by the
cutoff window
, which is a finer time scale at which the total-variation distance goes from 1 to 0
not
abruptly, and a
cutoff profile
, which gives the exact limiting function for the total-variation distance in such a critical scaling. We refer to the article
[
28
]
by Teyssier, and the work of Nestoridi-Thomas
[
26
]
for recent interesting results about cutoff profiles.

Cutoff-type questions were previously investigated for ASEP as well. The first result in this direction was obtained by Diaconis-Ram in
[
14
]
, where the so called
pre-cutoff
for a discrete time variation of ASEP was proved with the use of representations of Hecke algebra. The pre-cutoff is a claim that there is a unique time scale in which the total-variation changes from 1 to 0, but not necessarily abruptly (again, we refer to
[
23
]
for formal definitions). In the work

[
3
]
by Benjamini-Berger-Hoffman-Mossel, the pre-cutoff was shown for the ASEP on a segment, the ansatz of
[
3
]
to bound
d^{N,k}
by studying hitting times is also used in the present work. Whether cutoff holds was an open question for over a decade, until it was proven in
[
22
]
with the use of hydrodynamics of ASEP on
\mathbb{Z}
and a careful probabilistic analysis of the system.

It is important to mention that the
p=1,q=0
case of Theorem
1
can be obtained in a quite simple way from the result of Johansson
[
21
, Theorem 1.6]
, see
[
2
, Theorem 1.6]
by Angel-Holroyd-Romik. However, it was not clear how one can generalise such type of results to
q\neq 0
. Our proof of Theorem
1
can be viewed as such a generalisation.

Let us also mention that Theorem
1
seems to be the first example when the fluctuation term
N^{1/3},
which governs the Kardar-Parisi-Zhang universality class (see Corwin’s review
[
11
]
), appears as a cutoff window in the study of mixing times, and the Tracy-Widom distribution appears as a cutoff profile.

1.2.
Our tools and further questions

Our proof combines several ingredients. The first one is the result
[
30
, Theorem 3]
by Tracy-Widom about fluctuations of ASEP started from the step initial condition, see Theorem
2
below. Our argument is based on a comparison of the ASEP dynamics on a segment with the ASEP dynamics on all integers, and
[
30
, Theorem 3]
is (not surprisingly) the original source of the function
F_{\mathrm{GUE}}
in Theorem
1
.

The second ingredient is the use of the multi-species ASEP and its close connection to random walks on Hecke algebra. Using it, we are able to use certain symmetries of Hecke algebra in order to relate the ASEP started from the step initial condition and ASEP started from initial conditions that we are interested in. Somewhat similar ideas were used for TASEP (
q=0
case) by Borodin-Bufetov in
[
5
]
and Bufetov-Ferrari in
[
8
]
for the study of shocks. An important novelty of this paper is the extension of these ideas to ASEP case, which requires the use of
Mallows elements
in Hecke algebra, see Section
5
below.

The third ingredient is a variety of probabilistic coupling techniques that are needed throughout the paper for all steps of the argument.

As already mentioned earlier, our approach provides an independent (and rather short) proof of the cutoff for ASEP. Let us mention some further questions where our approach can be of use.

ASEP is arguably the most well-known representative of a fairly large class of integrable stochastic systems in the KPZ universality class. One can study other systems from this class instead. For example, one can study mixing times of the so called q-TASEP on the interval
[1;N]
. It was shown by Bufetov in
[
7
]
that a variety of integrable systems can be interpreted as random walks on Hecke algebras. It is possible that our technique can be used to study the mixing times for them as well.

In a different direction, it is important to note that the aforementioned papers
[
14
]
,
[
3
]
,
[
22
]
, studied the mixing times of the multi-species ASEP (this process can be also referred to as a random Metropolis scan or a biased card shuffling) on a segment as well. As shown in
[
22
, Section 4.4]
, on the level of cutoff this question can be reduced to a question about the single-species ASEP. The situation is significantly more delicate for the cutoff profile of the multi-species ASEP. Even for TASEP this question was resolved only very recently by Bufetov-Gorin-Romik in
[
9
]
; the obtained cutoff profile is the GOE Tracy-Widom distribution function (this is
not
the
F_{\mathrm{GUE}}
function from Theorem
1
). Based on this, we conjecture that the cutoff profile for the multi-species ASEP is the GOE Tracy-Widom distribution function as well (and the cutoff window is
N^{1/3}
). Note that the result of
[
9
]
was based on highly nontrivial recent developments (see Borodin-Gorin-Wheeler
[
6
]
, Bisi-Cunden-Gibbons-Romik
[
4
]
, Galashin
[
17
]
, Dauvergne
[
12
]
and Bufetov-Korotkikh
[
10
]
). Parts of the approach from the current paper and from
[
9
]
definitely can be of use for this question; nevertheless, this remains an interesting open problem.

We also mention in Remark
5.5
below further possible directions.

2.
Preliminaries

2.1.
Basic Coupling and graphical construction

While our main result deals with an ASEP on
[1;N]
which has only particles and holes, it will be important for us to consider
ASEP on
\mathbb{Z}
with countably many colors of particles; instead of colors we may speak interchangeably of types, classes, or species of particles.
The state space of this multi-species ASEP is the set of all bijections
\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{Z}
which we denote by
\mathfrak{S},
for
w\in\mathfrak{S},
having
w(i)=j
is interpreted as position
i
being occupied by the particle with color
j
.
Our convention is that particles with lower color have priority over particles with higher color.

Here we briefly give the graphical construction of this multi-species ASEP which goes back to Harris
[
19
]
. Let
(\mathcal{P}(z),z\in\mathbb{Z})
be a collection of independent, rate
p
Poisson processes constructed on some probability space
(\hat{\Omega},\mathcal{A},\mathbb{P}).
By
\mathcal{P}_{t}(z)
we denote the value of the Poisson process at time
t
.
For fixed
t
, the independence of the Poisson processes implies that for almost every
\omega\in\hat{\Omega}
there is a sequence
(i_{n},n\in\mathbb{Z})
of integers such that

\cdots<i_{n-2}<i_{n-1}<i_{n}<i_{n+1}<i_{n+2}<\cdots,

and
\mathcal{P}_{t}(i_{n})=0,n\in\mathbb{Z}.

Given
w\in\mathfrak{S},
and
z\in\mathbb{Z}
, we denote the swapped configuration

\sigma_{z,z+1}(w)(i)=\begin{cases}w(i+1)&\mathrm{for}\,i=z\\
w(i-1)&\mathrm{for}\,i=z+1\\
w(i)&\mathrm{else}.\end{cases}

The dynamics of ASEP is now as follows: We fix a (possibly random) initial configuration
w_{0}\in\mathfrak{S}
and define the parameter

Q:=\frac{q}{p}\in[0,1).

When at time
\tau
the Poisson processes
\mathcal{P}(z)
has a jump, we update the process as follows: If
w_{\tau^{-}}(z)<w_{\tau^{-}}(z+1)
, then we update
w_{\tau}=\sigma_{z,z+1}(w_{\tau^{-}})
, whereas if

w_{\tau^{-}}(z)>w_{\tau^{-}}(z+1),
we toss an independent coin such that with probability
1-Q
,
w_{\tau}=w_{\tau^{-}},
and with probability
Q
we have
w_{\tau}=\sigma_{z,z+1}(w_{\tau^{-}})
. Note that to construct the process up to time
t,
it suffices to apply these update rules inside each of the finite boxes
[i_{n}+1;i_{n+1}],
and inside each box there are a.s. only finitely many jumps of the Poisson processes (in particular, there is a.s. a well-defined first jump) during the interval
[0,t]
, and no two jumps happen at the same time, hence the graphical construction is well-defined. This construction also allows to obtain the multi-species ASEP on a finite segment
[a;b]
using finitely many Poisson processes
\mathcal{P}(a),\ldots,\mathcal{P}(b-1).
This is a process on the set of permutations of
[a;b]
; we denote this set of permutations by
S_{a;b}
.

To recover the ASEP which has only particles and holes, it suffices to fix an integer
k
and identify all particles whose color lies in
(-\infty,k]
as particles, and all particles with color in
(k,+\infty)
as holes, i.e. we map
w(\cdot)\mapsto 1_{(-\infty,k]}(w(\cdot)).
This defines a map
X^{k}:\mathfrak{S}\to\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{Z}}
(resp. a map
X_{[a;b]}^{k}:S_{a;b}\to\{0,1\}^{[a;b]}
for the finite ASEP), the image of which is the ASEP on
\mathbb{Z}
(resp.
[a;b]
) with particles and holes. In particular for
k\in[1;N]
we recover the ASEP in
\Omega^{N,k}
described in the introduction.

Later, we will also consider ASEPs with second class particles; they can be obtained by fixing
k_{1},k_{2}\in\mathbb{Z},k_{1}<k_{2},
and identifying all particles whose color lies in
(-\infty,k_{1}]
as first class particles, all particles whose color lies in
(k_{1},k_{2}]
as second class particles, and all particles with color in
(k_{2},\infty)
as holes.

The graphical construction allows us to couple different ASEPs together: We use the same collection of Poisson processes to construct ASEPs which start from different initial configurations and/or at different time points. We call this coupling the
basic coupling
, which also allows us to couple ASEPs on a segment
[a;b]
with ASEPs on
\mathbb{Z}
.

2.2.
Invariant measure for ASEP

Define the
Mallows measure
on
S_{a;b}
as

\mathcal{Q}_{a;b}(w):=Q^{(b-a+1)(b-a)/2-l\left(w\right)}Z_{a;b},

where
l(w)
is the number of inversions of
w
and

(2)

Z_{a;b}:=\left(\sum_{w\in S_{a;b}}Q^{(b-a+1)(b-a)/2-l\left(w\right)}\right)^{-1}=\left(\sum_{w\in S_{a;b}}Q^{l\left(w\right)}\right)^{-1}=\prod_{i=1}^{b-a+1}\frac{1-Q}{1-Q^{i}}.

It is immediate that the Mallows measure is invariant for the multi-species ASEP on
[a;b]
as described in Section
2.1
. Furthermore, under the map
X_{[a;b]}^{k}:S_{a;b}\to\{0,1\}^{[a;b]}
(with
k\in[a;b]
) from Section
2.1
, the Mallows measure becomes the stationary measure for the ASEP on
[a;b]
with
k-a+1
particles and
b-k
holes which we denote by
\pi_{[a;b],k}
. For the case
[a;b]=[1;N]
we will use a shortened notation
\pi_{[1;N],k}=:\pi_{N,k}.

2.3.
Single-species ASEPs

Given
\xi\in\Omega^{N,k}
, we attach a label (an integer) to each particle from right to left:
Let

x_{k}^{\xi}(0)<\cdots<x_{1}^{\xi}(0)

be the initial positions of the
k
particles of
\xi
. We denote by
x_{i}^{\xi}(t)
the position at time
t
of the particle that started in
x_{i}^{\xi}(0)
.

We will later often compare finite ASEPs with positive recurrent ASEPs on
\mathbb{Z}.
The latter are supported on
\bigcup_{Z\in\mathbb{Z}}\Omega_{Z},
where for
Z\in\mathbb{Z}

(3)

\Omega_{Z}=\left\{\zeta\in\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{Z}}:\sum_{j<Z}\zeta(j)=\sum_{j\geq Z}1-\zeta(j)<\infty\right\}.

We note that
there is a partial order on
\Omega_{Z}
: For
\zeta^{\prime},\zeta^{\prime\prime}\in\Omega_{Z}
, we define

(4)

\zeta^{\prime}\preceq\zeta^{\prime\prime}\iff\sum_{j=r}^{\infty}1-\zeta^{\prime\prime}(j)\leq\sum_{j=r}^{\infty}1-\zeta^{\prime}(j)\quad\mathrm{for\,all\,}r\in\mathbb{Z}.

It is easy to see that under the basic coupling, this order is preserved, i.e. if
\zeta^{\prime}\preceq\zeta^{\prime\prime},
then also
\zeta^{\prime}_{t}\preceq\zeta^{\prime\prime}_{t},t\geq 0.

Likewise, we use the same symbol to denote the analogous partial order on
\{0,1\}^{[a;b]}
:
For
\xi^{\prime},\xi^{\prime\prime}\in\{0,1\}^{[a;b]}
, we define

(5)

\xi^{\prime}\preceq\xi^{\prime\prime}\iff\sum_{j=r}^{b}1-\xi^{\prime\prime}(j)\leq\sum_{j=r}^{b}1-\xi^{\prime}(j)\quad\mathrm{for\,all\,}r\in[a;b].

We denote the minimal and maximal element in
\Omega^{N,k_{N}}
w.r.t. this order
as
\xi^{0},\xi^{1}
:

(6)

\xi^{0}=\mathbf{1}_{[1;k_{N}]}\in\Omega^{N,k_{N}},\quad\xi^{1}=\mathbf{1}_{[N-k_{N}+1;N]}\in\Omega^{N,k_{N}}.

We define the corresponding elements in
\Omega_{N+1-k_{N}}
as

(7)

\zeta^{0}=\mathbf{1}_{[1;k_{N}]}+\mathbf{1}_{\mathbb{Z}_{>N}}\in\Omega_{N+1-k_{N}},\quad\zeta^{1}=\mathbf{1}_{\mathbb{Z}_{>(N-k_{N})}}\in\Omega_{N+1-k_{N}}.

Analogous to the finite case, we label the particles of
\zeta^{0}
from right to left. We set

\{i\in\mathbb{Z}:\zeta^{0}(i)=1\}=\{x_{i}^{\zeta^{0}}(0),i\leq k_{N}\}
with
x_{k_{N}}^{\zeta^{0}}(0)<x_{k_{N}-1}^{\zeta^{0}}(0)<\cdots
and denote by
x_{i}^{\zeta^{0}}(t)
the position of the particle of
\zeta^{0}
that started in
x_{i}^{\zeta^{0}}(0)
.
We also label the holes of
\zeta^{0},
but from right to left, i.e. we write

\{i\in\mathbb{Z}:\zeta^{0}(i)=0\}=\{H_{i}^{\zeta^{0}},i\leq k_{N}\}
with
H_{k_{N}}(0)>H_{k_{N}-1}(0)>\cdots
, and
H_{i}^{\zeta^{0}}(t)
is the position at time
t
of the hole that started in
H_{i}^{\zeta^{0}}(0).
We label the particles and holes of
\zeta^{1}
in the same way.

Given a particle configuration
\zeta\in\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{Z}}

we define the (possibly infinite) position of the leftmost particle and rightmost hole of

\zeta
as

(8)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(\zeta)=\inf\{i\in\mathbb{Z}:\zeta(i)=1\}\quad\mathcal{R}(\zeta)=\sup\{i\in\mathbb{Z}:\zeta(i)=0\}.

We define analogously
\mathcal{L}(\xi),\mathcal{R}(\xi)
for
\xi\in\{0,1\}^{[a;b]}
.
With this notation, we have in particular

(9)

\displaystyle x_{k_{N}}^{\zeta^{0}}(t)=\mathcal{L}(\zeta_{t}^{0})\quad H_{k_{N}}^{\zeta^{0}}(t)=\mathcal{R}(\zeta_{t}^{0}).

Finally, throughout the paper, we will often omit writing integer brackets.

2.4.
ASEP with step initial data

Let us introduce the Tracy-Widom
\mathrm{GUE}
distribution. This probability distribution originates in the theory of random matrices
[
29
]
, namely it is the limit law of the rescaled largest eigenvalue of
a matrix drawn from the Gaussian Unitary Ensemble (GUE). Its
cumulative distribution function is given by

(10)

F_{\mathrm{GUE}}(s)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{n}}{n!}\int_{s}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}x_{1}\ldots\int_{s}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}x_{n}\det(K_{2}(x_{i},x_{j})_{1\leq i,j\leq n}),

where
K_{2}(x,y)
is the Airy kernel
K_{2}(x,y)=\frac{Ai(x)Ai^{\prime}(y)-Ai(y)Ai^{\prime}(x)}{x-y},x\neq y,
defined for
x=y
by continuity and
Ai
is the Airy function.

The ASEP with step initial data is the ASEP on
\mathbb{Z}
which starts from the initial configuration
x_{m}^{\mathrm{step}}(0)=-m,m>0
. The following fluctuation result plays an important role in our proof.

Theorem 2

(
[
30
]
, Theorem 3 )
.

Consider ASEP with step initial data and
p>q
. Let
\gamma=p-q,m>0,\sigma=m/t,c_{1}=1-2\sqrt{\sigma},c_{2}=\sigma^{-1/6}(1-\sqrt{\sigma})^{2/3}
.
Then, uniformly for
\sigma
in a compact subset of
(0,1),
we have

(11)

\lim_{t\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(\frac{x_{m}^{\mathrm{step}}(t/\gamma)-c_{1}t}{-c_{2}t^{1/3}}\leq s\right)=F_{\mathrm{GUE}}(s).

We will need Theorem
2
in the form of the following corollary. Note that
the
c^{\prime}N^{\kappa},c^{\prime\prime}N^{\kappa^{\prime}}
terms in the following are irrelevant in the
N\to\infty
limit as they get absorbed by the
N^{1/3}
fluctuations.

Corollary 1
.

We have for
k_{N}
with
k_{N}/N\to\alpha\in(0,1)
and arbitrary
\kappa,\kappa^{\prime}\in[0,1/3)
and
c^{\prime},c^{\prime\prime}\in\mathbb{R}
that

(12)

\lim_{N\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(x_{k_{N}+c^{\prime}N^{\kappa}}^{\mathrm{step}}(g(k_{N},c))\leq N-2k_{N}+c^{\prime\prime}N^{\kappa^{\prime}}\right)=1-F_{\mathrm{GUE}}(cf(\alpha)),

where
f(\alpha)=\frac{(\alpha(1-\alpha))^{1/6}}{(\sqrt{\alpha}+\sqrt{1-\alpha})^{4/3}}.

Proof.

We set
a=a(N)=k_{N}/N\to\alpha
and
D=(\sqrt{a}+\sqrt{1-a})^{2}.
Next we define
\tilde{N}=DN+cN^{1/3}=g(k_{N},c)(p-q)
. Writing everything in terms of
\tilde{N},
we get (with
\sigma
as in Theorem
2
) that

\displaystyle k_{N}+c^{\prime}N^{\kappa}=\frac{a}{D}\tilde{N}-\frac{ac}{D^{4/3}}\tilde{N}^{1/3}+o(\tilde{N}^{1/3})

\displaystyle\sigma=(k_{N}+c^{\prime}N^{\kappa})/\tilde{N}=\frac{a}{D}-\frac{ac}{D^{4/3}}\tilde{N}^{-2/3}+o(\tilde{N}^{-2/3})

\displaystyle N-2k_{N}+c^{\prime\prime}N^{\kappa^{\prime}}=(1-2\sqrt{\sigma})\tilde{N}-c\tilde{N}^{1/3}\left(\frac{(1-2a)}{D^{4/3}}+\frac{\sqrt{a}}{D^{5/6}}\right)+o(\tilde{N}^{1/3}).

We may thus rewrite

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(x_{k_{N}+c^{\prime}N^{\kappa}}^{\mathrm{step}}(g(k_{N},c))\leq N-2k_{N}+c^{\prime\prime}N^{\kappa^{\prime}}\right)

\displaystyle=1-\mathbb{P}\left(x_{k_{N}+c^{\prime}N^{\kappa}}^{\mathrm{step}}(g(k_{N},c))>N-2k_{N}+c^{\prime\prime}N^{\kappa^{\prime}}\right)

\displaystyle=1-\mathbb{P}\left(\frac{x_{\frac{a}{D}\tilde{N}-\frac{ac}{D^{4/3}}\tilde{N}^{1/3}+o(\tilde{N}^{1/3})}(\tilde{N}/(p-q))-(1-2\sqrt{\sigma})\tilde{N}}{-\tilde{N}^{1/3}}<c\left(\frac{(1-2a)}{D^{4/3}}+\frac{\sqrt{a}}{D^{5/6}}\right)+o(1)\right).

In order to apply Theorem
2
with
\tilde{N}=t,
we still have to divide by
\sigma^{-1/6}(1-\sqrt{\sigma})^{2/3}
.
An elementary computation reveals

\frac{\left(\frac{(1-2a)}{D^{4/3}}+\frac{\sqrt{a}}{D^{5/6}}\right)}{\sigma^{-1/6}(1-\sqrt{\sigma})^{2/3}}=\frac{(a(1-a))^{1/6}}{D^{2/3}}+o(1)\to_{N\to\infty}\frac{(\alpha(1-\alpha))^{1/6}}{(\sqrt{\alpha}+\sqrt{1-\alpha})^{4/3}}=f(\alpha).

Using Theorem
2
thus yields

\displaystyle\lim_{\tilde{N}\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(\frac{x_{\frac{a}{D}\tilde{N}-\frac{ac}{D^{4/3}}\tilde{N}^{1/3}+o(\tilde{N}^{1/3})}(\tilde{N}/(p-q))-(1-2\sqrt{\sigma})\tilde{N}}{-\sigma^{-1/6}(1-\sqrt{\sigma})^{2/3}\tilde{N}^{1/3}}<c\frac{\left(\frac{(1-2a)}{D^{4/3}}+\frac{\sqrt{a}}{D^{5/6}}\right)}{\sigma^{-1/6}(1-\sqrt{\sigma})^{2/3}}+o(1)\right)

\displaystyle=F_{\mathrm{GUE}}(cf(\alpha)),

finishing the proof.
∎

3.
Upper bound

The aim of this section is to show that
1-F_{\mathrm{GUE}}(cf(\alpha))
is an upper bound for the cutoff profile. More precisely, we will show the following Theorem, see Figure
1
for an illustration of the proof idea.

Theorem 3
.

Let

5.1.
Random walk on Hecke algebra

Let
S_{n}
be the symmetric group of permutations of
n

recall that we denote by

the number of inversions in it. Let
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
be the set of all nearest neighbor transpositions from

We will fix the parameter (introduced earlier in Section

A
Hecke algebra

\mathcal{H}(S_{n})
is the algebra with a linear basis

and the multiplication which satisfies the following rules for any

(45)

\begin{cases}T_{s}T_{w}=T_{sw},\qquad&\mbox{if $l(sw)=l(w)+1$}\\

It is clear that such rules can be used for a computation of the product
T_{w_{1}}T_{w_{2}}
for any
w_{1},w_{2}\in S_{n}

Let
\mathfrak{i}:\mathcal{H}(S_{n})\to\mathcal{H}(S_{n})

\mathfrak{i}\left(T_{w}\right)=T_{w^{-1}}

Proposition 5.1

The map
\mathfrak{i}

and also, trivially,
\mathfrak{i}^{2}\left(T_{1}\right)=T_{1}

For

,
a<b

\mathcal{H}_{a;b}

by this element of Hecke algebra by assigning to a permutation

.

5.2.
Multi-species ASEP as a random walk on Hecke algebra

Let us make the link here between the random walk

and the multi-species ASEP on

as constructed in Section

immediately induces a stochastic process

: The first rule of (

Let

5.4.
Idea of the proof

We have recalled all the necessary notions, and will give a proof of Theorem
4
in the following. Before this, let us briefly describe the idea of the proof.

Theorem
4
asks us to analyze the continuous time ASEP which starts with a nontrivial initial configuration in a very precise manner. There are currently no general tools which seem to be applicable to such sorts of questions (see, however,
[
27
]
); ASEP with step initial data being an exception where a detailed result in the form of Theorem
2
is available. Fortunately, the algebraic framework of the Hecke algebra allows to get an
exact distribution identity
which relates ASEP started with our initial configuration and the step-initial condition. It appears in the following way.

In principle, any initial configuration
w
is generated by the element
T_{w}
of the Hecke algebra. So if we are interested in the continuous time ASEP, we might want to study
W_{a;b}(t)T_{w}
. How to do this? The first crucial idea is that we can study

\mathfrak{i}\left(T_{w^{-1}}\mathfrak{i}\left(W_{a;b}(t)\right)\right)=\mathfrak{i}\left(T_{w^{-1}}W_{a;b}(t)\right)

instead due to Proposition
5.1
. Probabilistically, it is very important (and highly non-intuitive) that we first run continuous time ASEP from the identity permutation, which can be projected to the step initial condition, and only after this we apply the multiplication by
T_{w^{-1}}
. Since we have a precise information about the step initial condition ASEP, this gives hope to compute something about the configuration that we are interested in. Yet it is arguably impossible for general
w
. And here comes the second crucial idea — we utilize Mallows elements which can be used to create an initial configuration which is sufficiently close to
\zeta^{0}
(defined in (
7
)), the particle configuration we are interested in. The use of Mallows elements requires some estimates to relate it to the deterministic initial configuration
\zeta^{0}
, but all of them in our situation hold with a large margin.

5.5.
Distribution identity

Let us fix three positive integers
S,M,R
and a positive real
t
. We will consider random walks on the Hecke algebra
\mathcal{H}_{-S-R;S+M}
. Recall that the stochastic process
W_{-S-R;S+M}(t)
and the Mallows elements were defined in Section
5.1
.

Proposition 5.2
.

With
\,{\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt=}\limits^{d}}}
denoting equality in distribution, we have

W_{-S-R;S+M}(t)\mathcal{M}_{-S-R;0}\mathcal{M}_{-S;S+M}\,{\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt=}\limits^{d}}}\,\mathfrak{i}\left(\mathcal{M}_{-S;S+M}\mathcal{M}_{-S-R;0}W_{-S-R;S+M}(t)\right).

Proof.

Note that
\mathcal{M}_{-S-R;0}
,
\mathcal{M}_{-S;S+M}
, are invariant under the action of
\mathfrak{i}
.
As for
W_{a;b}(t),
note that for arbitrary
k\geq 1
and
s_{1},\ldots,s_{k}\in\mathfrak{S}_{a;b}
we have
\mathfrak{i}(W_{a;b}(t))=T_{s_{k}}\cdots T_{s_{1}}
iff
W_{a;b}(t)=T_{s_{1}}\cdots T_{s_{k}},
so that we show
\mathfrak{i}(W_{a;b}(t))\,{\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt=}\limits^{d}}}W_{a;b}(t)
by computing

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\mathfrak{i}(W_{a;b}(t))=T_{s_{k}}T_{s_{k-1}}\cdots T_{s_{1}})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(W_{a;b}(t)=T_{s_{1}}T_{s_{2}}\cdots T_{s_{k}})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(W_{a;b}(t)=T_{s_{k}}T_{s_{k-1}}\cdots T_{s_{1}}).

Setting
a=-S-R,b=S+M
and applying Proposition
5.1
yields the result.
∎

We will need one particular corollary of this distribution identity. Denote by
\pi_{S,R,M;t}
the random permutation generated by

W_{-S-R;S+M}(t)\mathcal{M}_{-S-R;0}\mathcal{M}_{-S;S+M},

and denote by
\hat{\pi}_{S,R,M;t}
the random permutation generated by

\mathcal{M}_{-S;S+M}\mathcal{M}_{-S-R;0}W_{-S-R;S+M}(t).

Corollary 2
.

For any
x\leq y\in\mathbb{R}
, we have

(48)

\mathbb{P}\left(\min_{-S-R\leq i\leq 0}\pi_{S,R,M;t}^{-1}(i)>x,\max_{0<j\leq S+M}\pi_{S,R,M;t}^{-1}(j)\leq y\right)\\
=\mathbb{P}\left(\min_{-S-R\leq i\leq 0}\hat{\pi}_{S,R,M;t}(i)>x,\max_{0<j\leq S+M}\hat{\pi}_{S,R,M;t}(j)\leq y\right).

Proof.

Immediate from Proposition
5.2
.
∎

The event on the left-hand side of (
48
) distinguishes whether a particle is of color
\leq 0
and
>0
. Therefore, the probability of this event can be expressed as a probability of the corresponding event in a single-species ASEP. Similarly, the right-hand side of (
48
) distinguishes whether a particle is of color
\leq x
, inside
(x;y]
, or
>y
. Therefore, the probability of this event can be expressed as a probability of the corresponding event in a two-species ASEP, i.e. ASEP which contains first-class particles, second-class particles, and holes. Let us present these simpler processes and events more formally.

5.7.

In the remainder of the section we will consider sequences of parameters

Proposition 5.4

We have

Throughout the proof,

.

\mathcal{L}(\mathfrak{C}_{t})\leq\mathcal{L}(\widehat{\zeta}^{0,S}_{t})\mathrm{\,\,and\,\,}\mathcal{R}(\mathfrak{C}_{t})\geq\mathcal{R}(\widehat{\zeta}^{0,S}_{t})

Remark 5.5
.

It is plausible that an extension of Theorem
1
to the case of slowly varying parameters
p-q\to 0
,
N^{1/3}(p-q)\to\infty
can be obtained by an upgrade of our method. This will require certain additional estimates, in particular, regarding the asymptotics of Mallows measure. On the other hand, if
(p-q)
becomes to decay to 0 faster, new effects should appear, first on the level of the cutoff window, and then on the level of the cutoff itself (cf.
[
16
]
).

Another interesting direction would be to understand whether our method can be adjusted to the study of mixing times in ASEP with open boundaries, cf.
[
18
]
.
