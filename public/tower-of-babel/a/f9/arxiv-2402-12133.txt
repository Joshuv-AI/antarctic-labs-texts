The Prime Geodesic Theorem and Bounds for Character Sums Thanks: The author acknowledges the support of the Masason Foundation.
Ikuya Kaneko

Abstract
. We establish the prime geodesic theorem for the modular surface with exponent 2 3 + ε \frac{2}{3}+\varepsilon , improving upon the long-standing exponent 25 36 + ε \frac{25}{36}+\varepsilon of Soundararajan–Young (2013). This was previously known conditionally on the generalised Lindelöf hypothesis for quadratic Dirichlet L L -functions. Our argument goes through a well-trodden trail via the automorphic machinery, and refines the techniques of Iwaniec (1984) and Cai (2002) to a maximum extent. A key ingredient is an asymptotic for bilinear forms with a counting function in Kloosterman sums via hybrid Weyl-strength subconvex bounds for quadratic Dirichlet L L -functions due to Young (2017), zero density estimates due to Heath-Brown (1995) near the edge of the critical strip, and an asymptotic for averages of Zagier L L -series due to Balkanova–Frolenkov–Risager (2022). Furthermore, we strengthen our exponent to 5 8 + ε \frac{5}{8}+\varepsilon conditionally on the generalised Lindelöf hypothesis for quadratic Dirichlet L L -functions, which breaks the existing barrier.

Introduction

1.1.
Brief Retrospection

The prime geodesic theorem concerns the asymptotic behaviour of the counting function of oriented primitive closed geodesics on hyperbolic manifolds. If the underlying surface
\Gamma\backslash\mathbb{H}
with
\mathbb{H}\coloneqq\{z=x+iy\in\mathbb{C}:y>0\}
the upper half-plane comes from a cofinite Fuchsian group
\Gamma\subset\mathrm{PSL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
, then this problem has sparked a lot of attention of number theorists; see
[

,

,
Kuz78b
,
Ran77

]
for classical triumphs.

A closed geodesic

on
\Gamma\backslash\mathbb{H}
corresponds bijectively to a hyperbolic conjugacy class in
\Gamma
; cf.
[
Hub59
]
. If
\mathrm{N}(P)
denotes the norm of this conjugacy class, then the hyperbolic length of

equals
\log\mathrm{N}(P)

\Lambda_{\Gamma}(P)\coloneqq\log\mathrm{N}(P_{0})
with

the primitive closed geodesic underlying

Note the resemblance with the prime number theorem. The role of the Riemann zeta function in the proof of the prime number theorem is now played by the Selberg zeta function of
\Gamma

2.
Bounds for Character Sums

This section addresses certain character sums appearing in the proof of Theorem
1.1

Lemma 2.1
.

For a primitive Dirichlet character
\chi
modulo a cubefree conductor
q
, suppose that the hybrid subconvex bound

(2.1)

L\left(\frac{1}{2}+it,\chi\right)\ll_{\varepsilon}(q(1+|t|))^{\theta+\varepsilon}

holds for some
0\leq\theta<\frac{1}{4}
. Then we have for any
x\geq q^{\frac{4r^{2}\theta-r-1}{2r(r-2)}}
,
r\geq 3
, and
\varepsilon>0
that

\sum_{n\leq x}\chi(n)\ll_{\varepsilon,r}x^{\alpha}q^{\beta+\varepsilon},

where

\alpha=\left(\frac{1}{2}+\theta\right)\frac{r-1}{r(1+\theta)-1},\qquad\beta=\frac{r(4r-3)\theta+\theta}{4r(r(1+\theta)-1)}.

Remark 2.2
.

Lemma
2.1
with
\theta=\frac{1}{6}
and
r=2
or
3
is in harmony with
[
PY23
, Theorem 1.6]
; see also the remark in the first paragraph on
[
PY23
, Page 1890]
. Furthermore, our restriction on
x
is reminiscent of the Pólya–Vinogradov threshold
x\geq q^{\frac{1}{2}}
, and becomes removable under the generalised Lindelöf hypothesis
\theta=0
when
r\in\mathbb{N}
is sufficiently large; see Lemma
2.3
. It appears that Petrow–Young
[
PY23
, Theorem 1.6]
omitted such a constraint being a bit terse.

Proof.

The strategy obeys verbatim the idea of Petrow–Young; see
[
PY23
, Theorem 1.6]
and the sketch of proof that follows. For completeness, we spell out the argument with exactitude.

Let
0<h\leq x
be a parameter to be chosen later. Let
w
be a smooth weight function so that
w(t)=1
for
0\leq t\leq x
,
w(t)=0
for
t\geq x+h
, and satisfying
w^{(j)}(t)\ll_{j}h^{-j}
for all
t>0
and
j\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
. Following the notation of Petrow–Young
[
PY23
, Page 1889]
, we define

S(\chi,w)\coloneqq\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\chi(n)w(n)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{(\sigma)}\widetilde{w}(s)L(s,\chi)ds.

Integration by parts ensures an arbitrary saving unless
\mathrm{Im}(s)\ll(\frac{x}{h})^{1+\varepsilon}
. Choosing
\sigma=\frac{1}{2}
and using the assumption (
2.1
) implies

S(\chi,w)\ll_{\varepsilon}x^{\frac{1}{2}}q^{\theta+\varepsilon}\left(\frac{x}{h}\right)^{\theta+\varepsilon}.

We observe that

\sum_{n\leq x}\chi(n)=S(\chi,w)-\sum_{x<n\leq x+h}\chi(n)w(n).

It now follows from
[
IK04
, Theorem 12.6]
(cf.
[
Bur62
,
Bur63
]
) and partial summation that

\sum_{n\leq x}\chi(n)\ll_{\varepsilon,r}x^{\frac{1}{2}}q^{\theta+\varepsilon}\left(\frac{x}{h}\right)^{\theta+\varepsilon}+h^{1-\frac{1}{r}}q^{\frac{r+1}{4r^{2}}+\varepsilon}

for any
r\in\mathbb{N}
. The optimal choice of
h
boils down to

h=x^{(\frac{1}{2}+\theta)\frac{1}{1+\theta-\frac{1}{r}}}q^{(\theta-\frac{r+1}{4r^{2}})\frac{1}{1+\theta-\frac{1}{r}}}\leq x.

In order for the last inequality to hold, we must assume that
x\geq q^{\frac{4r^{2}\theta-r-1}{2r(r-2)}}
, because otherwise the optimal bound comes from the choice
h=x
. The proof of Lemma
2.1
is complete.
∎

3.
Averages of Zagier
L
-Series

Implicit in the work of Soundararajan–Young
[
SY13
]
and Balkanova–Frolenkov
[
BF19a
]
is a connection of the pseudoprime counting function
\Psi_{\Gamma}(x)
with real quadratic fields, which is encoded in Zagier
L
-series described below. Given
q\in\mathbb{N}
and a discriminant
\delta
, we define

(3.1)

\rho_{q}(\delta)\coloneqq\#\{x{\@displayfalse\pmod{2q}}:x^{2}\equiv\delta{\@displayfalse\pmod{4q}}\},

and

\lambda_{q}(\delta)\coloneqq\sum_{q_{1}^{2}q_{2}q_{3}=q}\mu(q_{2})\rho_{q_{3}}(\delta),\qquad\rho_{q}(\delta)=\sum_{q_{1}q_{2}=q}\mu(q_{2})^{2}\lambda_{q_{1}}(\delta).

It is straightforward to verify that both functions are multiplicative in
q
for
\delta
fixed. We now define the Zagier
L
-series (sometimes termed generalised Dirichlet
L
-functions) by

(3.2)

L(s,\delta)\coloneqq\frac{\zeta(2s)}{\zeta(s)}\sum_{q=1}^{\infty}\frac{\rho_{q}(\delta)}{q^{s}}=\sum_{q=1}^{\infty}\frac{\lambda_{q}(\delta)}{q^{s}},\qquad\mathrm{Re}(s)>1.

It extends meromorphically to the whole complex plane and vanishes unless
\delta\equiv 0,1{\@displayfalse\pmod{4}}
. The Zagier
L
-series may be viewed as a simultaneous extension of the Riemann zeta function and quadratic Dirichlet
L
-functions in the sense that if
\delta=0
, then
L(s,\delta)=\zeta(2s-1)
, while if
D
is a fundamental discriminant, then
L(s,D)=L(s,\chi_{D})
. In general, if
\delta=D\ell^{2}
denotes a nonzero discriminant, then
[
Zag77
, Proposition 3]
states that

(3.3)

L(s,\delta)=\ell^{\frac{1}{2}-s}T_{\ell}^{(D)}(s)L(s,\chi_{D}),

where

T_{\ell}^{(D)}(s)\coloneqq\sum_{\ell_{1}\ell_{2}=\ell}\frac{\mu(\ell_{1})\chi_{D}(\ell_{1})\tau_{s}(\ell_{2})}{\sqrt{\ell_{1}}},\qquad\tau_{s}(n)\coloneqq n^{s-\frac{1}{2}}\sum_{d\mid n}d^{1-2s}.

Furthermore, the corresponding completed Zagier
L
-series

\Lambda(s,\delta)\coloneqq\left(\frac{\pi}{|\delta|}\right)^{-\frac{s}{2}}\Gamma\left(\frac{s}{2}+\frac{1}{4}-\frac{\mathrm{sgn}(\delta)}{4}\right)L(s,\delta)

satisfies the functional equation (see
[
Zag77
, Proposition 3]
and
[
SY13
, Lemma 2.1]
)

\Lambda(s,\delta)=\Lambda(1-s,\delta).

By
[
Dia99
, Section 0.8]
, it is well known that the generalised Riemann Hypothesis for
L(s,\delta)
is equivalent to the one for
L(s,\chi_{D})
. Similarly, the generalised Lindelöf hypothesis for
L(s,\delta)
in the hybrid aspect is equivalent to
\theta=0
; cf.
[
BF18
, Lemma 4.2]
. The Zagier
L
-series (
3.2
) and its incarnation appeared for example in the literature
[
Dia99
,
GH85
,
Sie56
,
Zag77
]
.

In this work, we study averages over certain discriminants of Zagier
L
-series on the critical line
\mathrm{Re}(s)=\frac{1}{2}
. Given an integer
n\geq 3
, let
\delta=n^{2}-4
denote a nonzero discriminant. The work of Balkanova–Frolenkov
[
BF19b
, Theorem 1.3]
then implies that
L(\frac{1}{2}+it,n^{2}-4)
exhibits a density function

(3.4)

m_{t}(x)\coloneqq\begin{cases}\frac{1}{2\zeta(\frac{3}{2})}\left(\log(x^{2}-4)-\frac{\pi}{2}+3\gamma-2\frac{\zeta^{\prime}(\frac{3}{2})}{\zeta(\frac{3}{2})}-\log 8\pi\right)&\text{if $t=0$},\\
\frac{\zeta(1+2it)}{\zeta(\frac{3}{2}+it)}+\frac{2^{\frac{1}{2}+it}\sin(\frac{\pi}{4}+\frac{i\pi t}{2})}{\pi^{it}}\frac{\zeta(it)}{\zeta(\frac{3}{2}-it)}\Gamma(it)(x^{2}-4)^{-it}&\text{if $t\neq 0$}.\end{cases}

The problem of approximating moments of Zagier
L
-series, thought of as a multiple Dirichlet series, is pioneered by Kuznetsov
[
Kuz78a
,
Kuz78b
]
. In particular, he employs the Kuznetsov formula
[
Kuz78b
]
to write averages of Zagier
L
-series in terms of sums of Kloosterman sums.

We now record a result of Balkanova–Frolenkov–Risager
[
BFR22
, Theorem 1.2]
in a more well-suited form.

Lemma 3.1
.

Keep the notation as above. Then for any
\frac{5}{8}\leq\delta<\frac{3}{4}
and
\varepsilon>0
, the bound

(3.5)

\mathcal{E}_{\Gamma}(x)\ll_{\varepsilon}x^{\delta+\varepsilon}

is equivalent to an asymptotic

(3.6)

\sum_{3\leq n\leq X}L\left(\frac{1}{2}+it,n^{2}-4\right)=\int_{3}^{X}m_{t}(x)dx+O_{\varepsilon}(X^{2(2\delta-1)+\varepsilon})

uniformly for
|t|\ll X^{\varepsilon}
.

In practice,
[
BFR22
, Theorem 1.2]
shows that (
3.6
) implies (
3.5
), but the converse claim is also valid via comparable considerations. Moreover, Balkanova–Frolenkov–Risager
[
BFR22
, Theorem 1.3]
established that the exponent
\frac{1}{2}+\varepsilon
in (
3.6
) is optimal, which is commensurate with the conjectural exponent
\delta=\frac{5}{8}
in (
3.5
), the limit of the present technology. Henceforth, we leave the quantity
\delta
unspecified until the end of the proof of Theorem
1.1
.

Direct corollaries of Lemma
3.1
include a nontrivial bound for character sums of interest.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
1\leq A\leq|B|
and
R\geq 1
. Then we have for some fixed
\frac{5}{8}\leq\delta<\frac{3}{4}
in (
3.5
) and for any
\varepsilon>0
that

(3.7)

\sum_{R<r\leq 2R}\sum_{B<a\leq A+B}\left(\frac{a^{2}-4}{r}\right)\ll_{\varepsilon}(A^{1+\varepsilon}+B^{2(2\delta-1)+\varepsilon})R^{\frac{1}{2}}.

Remark 3.3
.

We may obtain an asymptotic for averages of
L(\sigma+it,n^{2}-4)
for
\frac{1}{2}\leq\sigma\leq 1
in the spirit of Lemma
2.5
via a suitable form of the Phragmén–Lindelöf principle. This would lead to a bound on par with the quality of (
3.7
) in some nontrivial ranges of
\delta
. Moreover, at the current state of zero density estimates discussed in Section
4
, Lemma
3.2
strengthens Lemma
2.5
(that we choose to abandon) when
\sigma
is near the right edge of the critical strip.

Proof.

By (
3.3
), we henceforth shift our attention from Zagier
L
-series to quadratic Dirichlet
L
-functions since
T_{\ell}^{(D)}(s)
obeys an analogue of the Riemann hypothesis. In (
2.4
) and (
2.5
), we choose
\sigma=\frac{1}{2}
, and execute the inner sum over
a
in (
3.7
) via Lemma
3.1
. This yields

\sum_{R<r\leq 2R}\sum_{B<a\leq A+B}\left(\frac{a^{2}-4}{r}\right)\ll_{\varepsilon}\left|\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{2}+it\right)R^{\frac{1}{2}+it}\int_{B}^{A+B}m_{t}(x)dxdt\right|+B^{2(2\delta-1)+\varepsilon}R^{\frac{1}{2}}.

The definition of the density function (
3.4
) ensures that the integration over
x\in(B,A+B]
is bounded by
A^{1+\varepsilon}
, where the epsilon loss stems from an additional logarithm when
t=0
. Estimating trivially the rest via Stirling’s approximation completes the proof of Lemma
3.2
.
∎

We direct the reader to a survey paper of Iwaniec
[
Iwa84b
, Page 189]
that explains, among other formulations, the best possible cancellations (up to

) in bilinear forms as in Lemma
3.2
, and predicts that

(3.8)

\sum_{R<r\leq 2R}\sum_{B<a\leq A+B}\left(\frac{a^{2}-4}{r}\right)\ll_{\varepsilon}(A+R)(ABR)^{\varepsilon}.

He claims that (
3.8
) would yield the conjectural exponent
\frac{1}{2}+\varepsilon
in the prime geodesic theorem. This is heuristically as strong as the twisted Linnik–Selberg conjecture formulated in
[
Iwa84a
,
Iwa84b
,
BFR22
]
; cf.
[
BKS19
, Conjecture 1.1]
. It is desirable to establish an analogue of (
3.8
) conditionally on the quasi generalised Riemann hypothesis involving
\frac{1}{2}\leq\sigma\leq 1
, which has a possibility of improving further upon Theorem
1.1
thanks to an independence of
B
(which is quite large in our scenario) as well as an absence of the cumbersome constraint
\frac{5}{8}\leq\delta<\frac{3}{4}
.

4.
Zero Density Estimates

The proof of Theorem
1.1
also requires strong zero density estimates for quadratic families of Dirichlet
L
-functions, which often play a key role in the statistical theory of
L
-functions.

Lemma 4.1

(Heath-Brown
[
HB95
, Theorem 3]
)
.

For
0\leq\sigma\leq 1
and
T\geq 1
, we define

N(\sigma,T,\chi)\coloneqq\#\{\rho=\beta+i\gamma\in\mathbb{C}:L(\rho,\chi)=0,\,\sigma\leq\beta\leq 1,\,|\gamma|\leq T\},

where the zeros are counted with multiplicity. Then we have for any
\frac{1}{2}\leq\sigma\leq 1
and
\varepsilon>0
that

(4.1)

\ \sideset{}{{}^{\flat}}{\sum}_{|D|\leq Q}N(\sigma,T,\chi_{D})\ll_{\varepsilon}Q^{\frac{3(1-\sigma)}{2-\sigma}+\varepsilon}T^{\frac{3-2\sigma}{2-\sigma}+\varepsilon},

where
\flat
denotes that the sum runs through fundamental discriminants
D
of norm up to
Q
.

Lemma
4.1
is a consequence of quadratic large sieve
[
HB95
, Theorem 3]
and is strong in the conductor aspect, while weaker in the archimedean aspect since Jutila
[
Jut75
, Theorem 2]
derives an upper bound of
(QT)^{\frac{7-6\sigma}{2(3-2\sigma)}+\varepsilon}
. The sharpest results hitherto known feature
[
HB95
, Theorem 3]
and
[
Cor24
, Theorem 1.3]
in the conductor aspect and in the archimedean aspect, respectively. The folklore conjecture of Jutila
[
Jut72
,
Jut75
,
Jut76
,
Jut77b
]
, which remains out of reach at the current state of knowledge, asserts that

(4.2)

\ \sideset{}{{}^{\flat}}{\sum}_{|D|\leq Q}N(\sigma,T,\chi_{D})\ll_{\varepsilon}(QT)^{\frac{3}{2}(1-\sigma)+\varepsilon}.

Note that there is a subtle difference in its formulation in the sense that he conjectured the exponent
\frac{3}{2}-\sigma+\varepsilon
. The reason for its poorer quality when
\sigma
is close to
1
consists in the fact that he considers the left-hand side of (
4.2
) without excluding the contribution of imprimitive quadratic characters, and counts the zeros possibly several times. If one confines oneself to fundamental discriminants, then the corresponding exponent boils down to
\frac{3}{2}(1-\sigma)+\varepsilon
that shrinks as
\sigma
approaches
1
. In this regard, Lemma
4.1
also enjoys such a requisite shrinkage with respect to
\sigma
, and shows quantitatively that the zeros near the edge must be sparse.

Many fundamental consequences in analytic number theory employ Lemma
4.1
as a proxy for the generalised Lindelöf hypothesis for quadratic Dirichlet
L
-functions or Zagier
L
-series, without recourse to unproven progress concerning the quasi generalised Riemann hypothesis. As an immediate manifestation of this philosophy, we notice that Corollary
1.2
was previously known conditionally on the generalised Lindelöf hypothesis; hence our unconditional result is attributed essentially to the sparsity of the zeros near the edge, exhibited in Lemma
4.1
.

As shall be seen in the subsequent sections, the quality of (
4.1
) in the archimedean aspect is irrelevant for our purposes. For prior breakthroughs towards density hypotheses (not limited to quadratic families), we refer to
[
HB79a
,
HB79b
,
HB79c
,
Hux76
,
Jut75
,
Jut77a
,
Mon71
]
.

5.
Mean Value Estimates

In the process of applying the Kuznetsov formula and then treating sums of Kloosterman sums nontrivially, Iwaniec
[
Iwa84a
]
and Cai
[
Cai02
]
expressed the Kloosterman sum
S(n,n;c)
in terms of a sum of additive characters weighted by certain counting functions, and eschewed the mere usage of the Weil bound, namely

(5.1)

S(n,n;c)\coloneqq\ \sideset{}{{}^{\ast}}{\sum}_{a{\@displayfalse\pmod{c}}}e\left(\frac{an+\overline{a}n}{c}\right)=\sum_{a{\@displayfalse\pmod{c}}}\rho(c,a)e\left(\frac{an}{c}\right),

where the asterisk denotes summation restricted to reduced residue classes, and
e(z)\coloneqq e^{2\pi iz}
. Furthermore,
\rho(c,a)
stands for the number of solutions
d{\@displayfalse\pmod{c}}
of the congruence

d^{2}-ad+1\equiv 0{\@displayfalse\pmod{c}}.

If
c\in\mathbb{N}
is squarefree, then
\rho(c,a)
is in conjunction with the counting function
\rho_{c}(a^{2}-4)
given by (
3.1
) up to a scalar. Note that the expression on the right-hand side of (
5.1
) should be thought of as a reverse version of
[
SY13
, Lemma 2.3]
. It is important that the Kloosterman sum in question deals with the case of the same argument
m=n
. This possesses some nice structural properties via an observation in the work of Kuznetsov
[
Kuz78b
]
, lending itself to purely arithmetic approaches in terms of real quadratic fields, as explicated in Section
3
.

Given
1\leq A\leq|B|
and
C\geq 1
, we define the mean values (see the last display on
[
IS85
, Page 326]
for its first appearance)

\mathcal{F}(A,B,C)\coloneqq\sum_{B<a\leq A+B}\sum_{c\leq C}\rho(c,a),

where
B
is not necessarily positive. Because

\sum_{a{\@displayfalse\pmod{c}}}\rho(c,a)=\varphi(c),

where
\varphi(c)
denotes Euler’s totient function, we deduce

(5.2)

\mathcal{F}(A,B,C)=\sum_{c\leq C}\left(\frac{A}{c}+O(1)\right)\varphi(c)=\frac{6}{\pi^{2}}AC+O(A+C^{2}).

This is identical to
[
Iwa84a
, Equation (42)]
and insufficient for our purposes as the exponent of
C
is too large. The following asymptotic gives a substantial improvement to both
[
Iwa84a
, Theorem 3]
and
[
Cai02
, Lemma 7]
, and constitutes the core of this paper.

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
1\leq A\leq|B|
and
C\geq 1
. Suppose that (
2.2
) holds for some
\frac{1}{2}\leq\alpha\leq 1
and
0\leq\beta\leq\frac{1}{2}
. Then we have for some fixed
\frac{5}{8}\leq\delta<\frac{3}{4}
and for any
\frac{1}{2}\leq\sigma\leq 1
,
\theta\leq\frac{3+2\beta-(3+\beta)\sigma}{(3-2\alpha)(2-\sigma)}
, and
\varepsilon>0
that

(5.3)

\mathcal{F}(A,B,C)=\frac{6}{\pi^{2}}AC+O_{\varepsilon}((|B|^{\frac{2(3+2\beta-(3+\beta)\sigma)}{(3-2\alpha)(2-\sigma)}}C+|B|^{2(2\delta-1)-\frac{2(3+2\beta-(3+\beta)\sigma)}{(3-2\alpha)(2-\sigma)}}C+AC^{\frac{1}{2}})(|B|C)^{\varepsilon}).

Proof.

Write
c=k\ell
where
k
is a squarefree odd integer and
4\ell
is a squarefull integer coprime to
k
. Then the multiplicativity of
\rho(c,a)
with respect to
c
implies

\rho(c,a)=\rho(k,a)\rho(\ell,a).

Note that the congruence
d^{2}-ad+1\equiv 0{\@displayfalse\pmod{k}}
is equivalent to
x^{2}\equiv a^{2}-4{\@displayfalse\pmod{k}}
in
x{\@displayfalse\pmod{k}}
, and the number of incongruent solutions of the latter equals

\rho(k,a)=\prod_{p\mid k}\left(1+\left(\frac{a^{2}-4}{p}\right)\right)=\sum_{r\mid k}\left(\frac{a^{2}-4}{r}\right).

Following the notation of Iwaniec
[
Iwa84a
, Page 154]
, let
\mathscr{L}
be the set of
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
such that
p\mid\ell
implies
p^{2}\mid\ell
for all primes
p>2
. Then

\mathcal{F}(A,B,C)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\ell rs\leq C\\
\ell\in\mathscr{L},\,(rs,4\ell)=1\end{subarray}}\mu(rs)^{2}\sum_{B<a\leq A+B}\rho(\ell,a)\left(\frac{a^{2}-4}{r}\right)\eqqcolon\mathcal{F}_{0}(A,B,C)+\mathcal{F}_{\infty}(A,B,C),

where, for a parameter
R
to be chosen later,
\mathcal{F}_{0}(A,B,C)
(resp.
\mathcal{F}_{\infty}(A,B,C)
) denotes the summation restricted to
\ell r\leq R
(resp.
\ell r>R
). Hence, depending on the size of
A
and
R
, we either execute the sum over
a
via the Weil–Deligne bound for character sums with the quadratic polynomial
a^{2}-4
(see
[
Sch76
, Theorem 2G]
and
[
Wei48
, Page 207]
), or execute the sum over
r
via strong subconvex bounds as in Lemma
2.3
including
0\leq\theta<\frac{1}{4}
, which replaces the usage of the Burgess bound in the conductor aspect as in
[
Cai02
,
Iwa84a
]
. Another key realisation is that by quadratic reciprocity, one can regard the Jacobi–Kronecker symbol
(\frac{p}{q})
either as a character in
p{\@displayfalse\pmod{q}}
or as a character in
q{\@displayfalse\pmod{p}}
.

For the first contribution
\mathcal{F}_{0}(A,B,C)
, Iwaniec
[
Iwa84a
, Equation (44)]
proved that

(5.4)

\mathcal{F}_{0}(A,B,C)=\lambda AC+O_{\varepsilon}((CR^{\frac{1}{2}}+ACR^{-\frac{1}{2}}+AC^{\frac{1}{2}})(|B|C)^{\varepsilon})

for an absolute and effectively computable constant
\lambda>0
. We stress that any improvement of
[
Iwa84a
, Lemma 2*]
would not lead to a stronger error term in (
5.4
) due to the occurrence of the sum over
\ell
. The problem now boils down to the estimation of
\mathcal{F}_{\infty}(A,B,C)
to balance with the first term in the error term on the right-hand side of (
5.4
). The second term is in turn absorbed into the other terms, and the third term corresponds to the last term in (
5.3
).

This is where the assumption (
2.2
) comes into play. We apply a dyadic subdivision of the summation over
r
in
\mathcal{F}_{\infty}(A,B,C)
into intervals of the type
(R_{1},R_{2}]
with
\ell^{-1}R\leq R_{1}<R_{2}\leq 2R_{1}
. It is also advantageous to split the sum over
a
according as
a\in\mathcal{M}
or
a\in\overline{\mathcal{M}}
, where

\mathcal{M}\coloneqq\{B<a\leq A+B:\text{$L(s,\chi_{a^{2}-4})$ has a zero in $[\sigma,1]\times[-\log R,\log R]$}\},

and
\overline{\mathcal{M}}
stands for the complement of
\mathcal{M}
. By Lemma
4.1
, the cardinality of
\mathcal{M}
is at most (cf.
[
BBCL22
, Section 2.3]
and
[
Sar85
, Lemma 2.5]
)

(5.5)

\mathrm{Card}(\mathcal{M})\ll_{\varepsilon}|B|^{\frac{6(1-\sigma)}{2-\sigma}+\varepsilon}.

If the restriction to
\mathcal{M}
is indicated as a superscript by
\mathcal{F}^{\mathcal{M}}_{\infty}(A,B,C)
, then we attach in (
2.2
) the condition that
r
be squarefree and coprime to a given
q
by the device on
[
IS85
, Page 329]
(cf.
[
Iwa84a
, Lemma 3*]
and
[
Cai02
, Lemma 4]
), obtaining

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}^{\mathcal{M}}_{\infty}(A,B,C)

\displaystyle\ll_{\varepsilon}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\ell\leq R\\
\ell\in\mathscr{L}\end{subarray}}\sum_{a\in\mathcal{M}}\rho(\ell,a)\sum_{R_{1}\geq\ell^{-1}R}\frac{C}{\ell R_{1}}R_{1}^{\alpha}|B|^{2\beta}(|B|C)^{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle\ll_{\varepsilon}|B|^{2\beta}CR^{\alpha-1}(|B|C)^{\varepsilon}\sum_{a\in\mathcal{M}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\ell\leq R\\
\ell\in\mathscr{L}\end{subarray}}\rho(\ell,a)\ell^{-\alpha}

provided
R\geq|B|^{4\theta}
, where we use the following asymptotic with
S=\frac{C}{\ell r}
and
q=4\ell r
:

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}s\leq S\\
(s,q)=1\end{subarray}}\mu(s)^{2}=\frac{6}{\pi^{2}}\prod_{p\mid q}\left(1+\frac{1}{p}\right)^{-1}S+O(\tau(q)S^{\frac{1}{2}}).

In the second display on
[
Iwa84a
, Page 156]
, Iwaniec estimates the sum over
\ell
as
(|B|C)^{\varepsilon}
via elementary manipulations. From the assumption

along with partial summation, our sum over

, and hence (

) yields

(5.6)

\mathcal{F}^{\mathcal{M}}_{\infty}(A,B,C)\ll_{\varepsilon}|B|^{2\beta+\frac{6(1-\sigma)}{2-\sigma}}CR^{\alpha-1}(|B|C)^{\varepsilon}

provided

. On the other hand, an unconditional bound in Lemma

yields

(5.7)

\mathcal{F}^{\overline{\mathcal{M}}}_{\infty}(A,B,C)\ll_{\varepsilon}B^{2(2\delta-1)}CR^{-\frac{1}{2}}(|B|C)^{\varepsilon}.

Altogether, we balance (

) and (

) with

, deducing for

(5.8)

\mathcal{F}(A,B,C)=\lambda AC+O_{\varepsilon}((|B|^{\frac{2(3+2\beta-(3+\beta)\sigma)}{(3-2\alpha)(2-\sigma)}}C+|B|^{2(2\delta-1)-\frac{2(3+2\beta-(3+\beta)\sigma)}{(3-2\alpha)(2-\sigma)}}C+AC^{\frac{1}{2}})(|B|C)^{\varepsilon})

for an absolute and effectively computable constant

. It is important that our choice of

pivots upon the term corresponding to exceptional

). Comparing (

∎

\sum_{t_{j}>0}X^{it_{j}}e^{-\frac{t_{j}}{T}}\ll_{\varepsilon}TX^{\frac{3+2\beta-(3+\beta)\sigma}{(3-2\alpha)(2-\sigma)}+\varepsilon}+TX^{2\delta-1-\frac{3+2\beta-(3+\beta)\sigma}{(3-2\alpha)(2-\sigma)}+\varepsilon}+T^{\frac{3}{2}}(\log T)^{2},

where we optimise
C=X^{\frac{1}{2}}
and
N=TX^{\varepsilon}
. Then the standard Fourier-theoretic technique of Luo–Sarnak
[
LS95
, Pages 235–236]
verifies the claim. The proof of Lemma
6.1
is complete.
∎

Cai
[
Cai02
, Section 7]
then proceeds to an interpolation with the Luo–Sarnak bound
[
LS95
, Equation (58)]
. Nonetheless, this manoeuvre is unnecessary in our case since all the powers of
T
on the right-hand side of (
6.1
) are at least
1
, whose purpose is to enable the subsequent application of integration by parts (or partial summation). Note that Cai
[
Cai02
, Section 7]
omitted the process of eliminating a restriction in the last display on
[
Cai02
, Page 67]
; hence his deduction as stated remains inexact in a narrow sense. Unlike his crude line of argument, Lemma
6.1
imposes no restriction on
T
. Moreover, there is a salient difference between the test functions in
[
Iwa84a
, Equation (29)]
and Cai
[
Cai02
, Section 5]
. The former addresses the spectral exponential sum in the explicit formula
[
Iwa84a
, Lemma 1]
as it is, while the latter addresses an unweighted version to adapt the machinery of Luo–Sarnak
[
LS95
, Appendix]
.

We now make use of one of the key ideas of Soundararajan–Young
[
SY13
]
. Let
Y\in[\sqrt{x},x]
be a parameter to be chosen later. Let
k:(0,\infty)\to[0,\infty)
be a smooth function with total integral
1
. If
k(u)
is supported on
[Y,2Y]
and satisfies
[
SY13
, Equation (9)]
, then it follows from
[
SY13
, Equation (17)]
and the last display in the corrections thereof that

(6.4)

\Psi_{\Gamma}(x)=x+E(x;k)+O_{\varepsilon}(Y^{\frac{1}{2}}x^{\frac{1}{4}+\frac{\theta}{2}+\varepsilon}+x^{\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\theta}{2}+\varepsilon}+Yx^{-1}),

where
[
SY13
, Equation (20)]
yields

E(x;k)\coloneqq\int_{0}^{\infty}(\Psi_{\Gamma}(x+u)-x-u)k(u)du=\sum_{|t_{j}|\leq\frac{x^{1+\varepsilon}}{Y}}\frac{1}{s_{j}}\int_{Y}^{2Y}(x+u)^{s_{j}}k(u)du+O_{\varepsilon}(x^{\frac{1}{2}+\varepsilon}).

Estimating the integral on the right-hand side via the supremum of the integrand, we obtain

(6.5)

\sum_{|t_{j}|\leq\frac{x^{1+\varepsilon}}{Y}}\frac{1}{s_{j}}\int_{Y}^{2Y}(x+u)^{s_{j}}k(u)du\ll x^{\frac{1}{2}}\sup_{Y\leq u\leq 2Y}\left|\sum_{|t_{j}|\leq\frac{x^{1+\varepsilon}}{Y}}\frac{(x+u)^{it_{j}}}{\frac{1}{2}+it_{j}}\right|.

It remains to handle the spectral exponential sum inside the absolute value. To this end, we denote by
\mathcal{S}(X,T)
the left-hand side of (
6.1
). If we abbreviate
T^{\ast}=\frac{x^{1+\varepsilon}}{Y}
and
X=x+u
for a given
u\in[Y,2Y]
, then by integration by parts (or partial summation)

\sum_{|t_{j}|\leq\frac{x^{1+\varepsilon}}{Y}}\frac{(x+u)^{it_{j}}}{\frac{1}{2}+it_{j}}=\int_{1}^{T^{\ast}}\frac{d\mathcal{S}(T,X)}{\frac{1}{2}+iT}=\frac{\mathcal{S}(T^{\ast},X)}{\frac{1}{2}+iT^{\ast}}+i\int_{1}^{T^{\ast}}\frac{\mathcal{S}(T,X)}{(\frac{1}{2}+iT)^{2}}dT.

Applying Lemma
6.1
and substituting the definitions of
T^{\ast}
and
X
, we deduce

(6.6)

\ll_{\varepsilon}x^{\frac{3+2\beta-(3+\beta)\sigma}{(3-2\alpha)(2-\sigma)}+\varepsilon}+x^{2\delta-1-\frac{3+2\beta-(3+\beta)\sigma}{(3-2\alpha)(2-\sigma)}+\varepsilon}+x^{\frac{1}{2}+\varepsilon}Y^{-\frac{1}{2}}.

Combining (
6.5
) and (
6.6
) thus yields

(6.7)

E(x;k)\ll_{\varepsilon}x^{\frac{1}{2}+\frac{3+2\beta-(3+\beta)\sigma}{(3-2\alpha)(2-\sigma)}+\varepsilon}+x^{2\delta-\frac{1}{2}-\frac{3+2\beta-(3+\beta)\sigma}{(3-2\alpha)(2-\sigma)}+\varepsilon}+x^{1+\varepsilon}Y^{-\frac{1}{2}}.

Note that the first term dominates the last term whenever
Y\gg x^{1-\frac{2(3+2\beta-(3+\beta)\sigma)}{(3-2\alpha)(2-\sigma)}+\varepsilon}
. It is now convenient to minimise
Y
so that

Y=x^{1-\frac{2(3+2\beta-(3+\beta)\sigma)}{(3-2\alpha)(2-\sigma)}+\varepsilon}.

Hence, we conclude from (
6.4
) and (
6.7
) that

(6.8)

\mathcal{E}_{\Gamma}(x)\ll_{\varepsilon}x^{\frac{1}{2}+\frac{3+2\beta-(3+\beta)\sigma}{(3-2\alpha)(2-\sigma)}+\varepsilon}+x^{2\delta-\frac{1}{2}-\frac{3+2\beta-(3+\beta)\sigma}{(3-2\alpha)(2-\sigma)}+\varepsilon}+x^{\frac{1}{2}(1-\frac{2(3+2\beta-(3+\beta)\sigma)}{(3-2\alpha)(2-\sigma)})+\frac{1}{4}+\frac{\theta}{2}+\varepsilon}+x^{\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\theta}{2}+\varepsilon}.

By (
2.3
), the optimal choice of
\sigma
(based on the first and third terms) boils down to

0.90909\cdots=\frac{10}{11}\leq\sigma=\frac{20+30\theta-4\theta^{2}}{22+27\theta-2\theta^{2}}\leq\frac{16}{17}=0.94117\cdots,\qquad 0\leq\theta\leq\frac{1}{6},

which obeys the assumption in Lemma
6.1
, justifying its validity. Here the upper bound for
\theta
stems from the restriction
\theta\leq\frac{3+2\beta-(3+\beta)\sigma}{(3-2\alpha)(2-\sigma)}
. Furthermore, by the definition (
3.5
) of
\frac{5}{8}\leq\delta<\frac{3}{4}
, one must equate

(6.9)

\delta=\max\left(\frac{1}{2}+\frac{3+2\beta-(3+\beta)\sigma}{(3-2\alpha)(2-\sigma)},2\delta-\frac{1}{2}-\frac{3+2\beta-(3+\beta)\sigma}{(3-2\alpha)(2-\sigma)}\right)\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\delta=\frac{5}{8}+\frac{\theta}{4}.

Note that the exponent
\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\theta}{2}
in the last term on the right-hand side of (
6.8
) is always smaller than
\delta=\frac{5}{8}+\frac{\theta}{4}
because
0\leq\theta\leq\frac{1}{6}
. Putting everything together leads to the desired claim

\mathcal{E}_{\Gamma}(x)\ll_{\varepsilon}x^{\frac{5}{8}+\frac{\theta}{4}+\varepsilon}.

The proof of Theorem
1.1
is now complete.
