Topological edge spectrum along curved interfaces
Alexis Drouot, Xiaowen Zhu

Abstract
. We prove that that if the boundary of a topological insulator divides the plane in two regions containing arbitrarily large balls, then it acts as a conductor. Conversely, we show that topological insulators that fit within strips do not need to admit conducting boundary modes.



1.
Introduction and main results

1.1.
Introduction

Topological insulators are novel materials with striking properties. They are phases of matter insulating in their bulk (the Hamiltonian has a spectral gap), but that turn into conductors when truncated to half-spaces (the spectral gap fills). The resulting edge conductance is equal to the difference of the bulk topological invariants across the cut, a principle known as the bulk-edge correspondence
[
19
,
11
,
12
,
2
,
14
,
15
,
22
,
5
,
9
,
20
]
. Here, we consider truncations of topological insulators in regions more sophisticated than half-spaces (for example, sectors or filled parabolas). We investigate how the shape of the resulting material affects the spectrum.

Figure 1.
The first three truncations systematically give rise to edge spectrum. In the last case, we construct Haldane-type topological insulators
H_{-},H_{+}
such that
H_{e}
is also an insulator.

The main operator in our analysis is approximately equal to

(1.1)

H_{e}:=\begin{cases}H_{+}\quad\text{ on }&\Omega,\\
H_{-}\quad\text{ on }&\Omega^{c}={\mathbb{Z}}^{2}\setminus\Omega,\end{cases}

for two Hamiltonians
H_{\pm}
on
\ell^{2}({\mathbb{Z}}^{2},{\mathbb{C}}^{d})
with distinct bulk invariants within a joint spectral gap
{\mathcal{G}}
(potentially one of them representing the vacuum); and
\Omega
a subset of
{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}
. We refer to §
1.2
for precise definitions and assumptions. We ask for geometric conditions on
\Omega
that guarantee that
H_{e}
has spectrum filling
{\mathcal{G}}
.

Our main result, Theorem
1
, asserts that if both
\Omega
and
\Omega^{c}
contain arbitrarily large balls then
H_{e}
has spectrum filling
{\mathcal{G}}
(referred to as edge spectrum). Hence
H_{e}
behaves like a conductor near
\partial\Omega
. Examples of such domains
\Omega
include half-planes, sectors, regions enclosed by hyperbolas, and so on; they exclude strips or half-strips – see Figure
1
. In this last case, we actually show that there exist examples of distinct topological insulators
H_{\pm}
such that
H_{e}
remains an insulator – Theorem
3
. Therefore, topological materials fitting in strips can violate the bulk-edge correspondence: boundaries or interfaces are not systematically conductors. This violation was suggested in a question of G.M. Graf in an online talk by G.C. Thiang
[
16
]
.

Our experience of the world, is by nature, an approximation of the reality. Experiment samples – here,
\Omega
– are always finite and spectral measurements are valid only up to an uncertainty
\delta>0
. Hence, in practice
\Omega
never contains arbitrarily large balls – but neither can experiments assess with full certainty that spectral gaps completely fill: they can only measure emergence of a
\delta
-dense set of spectrum. Theorem
2
is a quantitative formulation of Theorem
1
that relates to these observations. It predicts that there exist constants
\alpha,R_{0}
such that for all
R\geq R_{0}
, the following holds. Assume that both
\Omega,\Omega^{c}
contain a ball of radius
R
. Then the spectrum of
H_{e}
within
{\mathcal{G}}
is
C\ln(R)/R
-dense:

(1.2)

\forall\lambda_{*}\in{\mathcal{G}},\ \ \exists\lambda\in\Sigma(H_{e}),\ \ |\lambda-\lambda_{*}|\leq\dfrac{\alpha\ln(R)}{R}.

This justifies why topological insulators truncated to sufficiently large balls appear in experiments conducting along their boundaries.

1.2.
Topological insulators and interface operators

We briefly review standard facts from condensed matter physics. Electronic propagation through a given material is described via a selfadjoint operator
H
on a Hilbert space – here
\ell^{2}({\mathbb{Z}}^{2},{\mathbb{C}}^{d})
. The spectrum
\Sigma(H)
of
H
characterizes the electronic nature of the material:
H
is a conductor at energy
\lambda
if and only if
\lambda\in\Sigma(H)
and an insulator otherwise.

In the rest of this paper,
\nu\in(0,1]
is a fixed parameter.

We work here with short-range Hamiltonians: operators on
\ell^{2}({\mathbb{Z}}^{2},{\mathbb{C}}^{d})
whose kernels satisfy

(1.3)

\forall{\bm{x}},{\bm{y}}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{2},\qquad\big|H({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})\big|\leq\nu^{-1}e^{-2\nu|{\bm{x}}-{\bm{y}}|},\qquad|{\bm{x}}-{\bm{y}}|:=|x_{1}-y_{1}|+|x_{2}-y_{2}|.

Under (
1.3
), one can define the bulk conductance of
H
at an insulating energy. For
\lambda\notin\Sigma(H)
, let
P_{\lambda}(H)={\mathbb{1}}_{(-\infty,\lambda)}(H)
be the spectral projector below energy
\lambda
, and
\Lambda_{1}
(respectively
\Lambda_{2}
) the indicator function of
{\mathbb{N}}\times{\mathbb{Z}}
(respectively
{\mathbb{Z}}\times{\mathbb{N}}
). Then the operator
P_{\lambda}(H)\big[[P_{\lambda}(H),\Lambda_{1}],[P_{\lambda}(H),\Lambda_{2}]\big]
is trace-class (see
[
12
]
and Remark
1
below) and

(1.4)

\sigma(H,\lambda):=-2\pi i\Tr\left(P_{\lambda}(H)\big[[P_{\lambda}(H),\Lambda_{1}],[P_{\lambda}(H),\Lambda_{2}]\big]\right)

is well-defined. We comment that if
{\mathcal{G}}
is a subinterval of
\Sigma(H)^{c}
(referred to below as a spectral gap), then

(1.5)

\lambda,\lambda^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{G}}\ \Rightarrow\ \sigma(H,\lambda)=\sigma(H,\lambda^{\prime}).

Therefore, there is no ambiguity in using the notation
\sigma(H,{\mathcal{G}})
for
\sigma(H,\lambda)
,
\lambda\in{\mathcal{G}}
. It represents the bulk conductance for energies in
{\mathcal{G}}

[
3
]
. Under the gap condition
\lambda\notin\Sigma(H)
,
\sigma(H,\lambda)
is an integer that measures topological aspects of the Hamiltonian
H
.

In this work, we ask under which conditions interfaces between two topologically distinct insulators – the bulk materials – carry currents. We make the following assumption on the bulk components:

Assumption 1
.

H_{\pm}
are two selfadjoint, short-range Hamiltonians on
\ell^{2}({\mathbb{Z}}^{2},{\mathbb{C}}^{d})
, with a common spectral gap
{\mathcal{G}}
(an interval contained in
\Sigma(H_{+})^{c}\cap\Sigma(H_{-})^{c}
) and distinct bulk conductances within
{\mathcal{G}}
:

(1.6)

\sigma(H_{+},{\mathcal{G}})\neq\sigma(H_{-},{\mathcal{G}}).

Given a domain
\Omega\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}
, we define its boundary by

(1.7)

\partial\Omega:=\{{\bm{x}}\in\Omega,\ B_{1}({\bm{x}})\not\subset\Omega\}\cup\{{\bm{x}}\in\Omega^{c},\ B_{1}({\bm{x}})\not\subset\Omega^{c}\},

where
B_{r}({\bm{x}})
denotes the
\ell^{1}
-ball of radius
r
centered at
{\bm{x}}
in
{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}
. We will denote the distance from
{\bm{x}}
to
\partial\Omega
by
d({\bm{x}},\partial\Omega)
. We make the following assumptions on the interface operator:

Assumption 2
.

H_{e}
is a selfadjoint, short-range Hamiltonian on
\ell^{2}({\mathbb{Z}}^{2},{\mathbb{C}}^{d})
satisfying the kernel condition:

(1.8)

\forall{\bm{x}},{\bm{y}}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{2},\qquad\big|E({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})\big|\leq\nu^{-1}e^{-2\nu d({\bm{x}},\partial\Omega)},\qquad E:=H_{e}-{\mathbb{1}}_{\Omega}H_{+}{\mathbb{1}}_{\Omega}-{\mathbb{1}}_{\Omega^{c}}H_{-}{\mathbb{1}}_{\Omega^{c}}.

The condition (
1.8
) means that
H_{e}
is equal to
H_{+}
on
\Omega
and
H_{-}
on
\Omega^{c}
, up to corrections decaying exponentially away from
\partial\Omega
.

1.3.
Main results

To formulate our main results, we will need the notion of filling radius for a subset
\Omega
of
{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}
:

(1.9)

{\operatorname{Fr}}(\Omega)=\sup\{r:\ \exists\ {\bm{x}}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{2},\ B_{r}({\bm{x}})\cap{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}\subset\Omega\}.

It measures the size of the largest ball contained in
\Omega
:
{\operatorname{Fr}}(\Omega)\geq r
if and only if
\Omega
contains a ball of radius
r
.

Theorem 1
.

Let
H_{\pm},H_{e}
satisfying Assumptions
1
and
2
. If
{\operatorname{Fr}}(\Omega)={\operatorname{Fr}}(\Omega^{c})=\infty
, then
{\mathcal{G}}\subset\Sigma(H_{e})
.

This means that if the boundary of a topological insulators divides the plane in two regions of infinite filling radius, then it is a conductor. Theorem
1
will actually follow from a more quantitative statement. Given
\delta>0
, we say that a set
S\subset{\mathcal{G}}
is
\delta
-dense within
{\mathcal{G}}
if either
|{\mathcal{G}}|<\delta

or if

(1.10)

\forall\lambda_{*}\in{\mathcal{G}},\ \exists\lambda\in S\text{ s.t. }|\lambda_{*}-\lambda|\leq\delta.

Theorem 2
.

There exist constants
\alpha_{\nu}>0
and
R_{\nu}>0
, depending on
\nu
only, such that the following holds. For
H_{\pm},H_{e}
satisfying Assumptions
1
and
2
and any
R\geq R_{\nu}
:

{\operatorname{Fr}}(\Omega),\ {\operatorname{Fr}}(\Omega^{c})\geq R\quad\Rightarrow\quad\Sigma(H_{e})\cap{\mathcal{G}}\ \
is
\alpha_{\nu}\frac{\ln R}{R}
–dense within
{\mathcal{G}}
.

Theorem
2
has the following physical interpretation. Assume that
H_{e}
represents the truncation of a topological insulator
H_{+}
in the ball
B_{R}(0)
, and that we have a measurement procedure that can infer if an energy is within
\delta
of
\Sigma(H_{e})
. Theorem
2
asserts that if
\ln(R)/R\ll\delta
, then experiments measure that the spectral gap of
H_{+}
closes when truncating it to
B_{R}(0)
.
This imperfect conclusion (
H_{e}
actually has discrete spectrum when truncated to
B_{R}(0)
) is due to the limitation of the measuring procedure.

Theorem
2
implies Theorem
1
: if
{\operatorname{Fr}}(\Omega)={\operatorname{Fr}}(\Omega^{c})=\infty
, then
{\operatorname{Fr}}(\Omega)
and
{\operatorname{Fr}}(\Omega^{c})
are larger than
R
for any
R
, so
\Sigma(H_{e})\cap{\mathcal{G}}
is
\delta
-dense within
{\mathcal{G}}
for any
\delta>0
. This means that
\Sigma(H_{e})\cap{\mathcal{G}}
is actually dense within
{\mathcal{G}}
; since it is a closed subset of
{\mathcal{G}}
we conclude that
\Sigma(H_{e})\cap{\mathcal{G}}={\mathcal{G}}
, equivalently
{\mathcal{G}}\subset\Sigma(H_{e})
.

A natural question is whether the conclusion of Theorem
1
fails for unbounded sets
\Omega
with finite filling radius.

Theorem 3
.

Fix
L>0
and
\Omega\subset[-L,L]\times{\mathbb{Z}}
. There exists operators
H_{\pm}
satisfying Assumption
1
for a gap
{\mathcal{G}}
containing
0
; and
H_{e}
satisfying Assumption
2
such that
0\notin\Sigma(H_{e})
.

In other words, a topological insulator fitting in a strip does not have to be a conductor. This constitutes a violation of the bulk-edge correspondence: an interface between two distinct topological phases does not have to support edge states, for instance if the interface is the boundary of a half-strip.

Figure 2.
A region
\Omega
that does not satisfy the assumption of Theorem
1
: the largest ball that fits in
\Omega^{c}
has radius
1
, so
{\operatorname{Fr}}(\Omega^{c})=1<\infty
. There actually exist operators
H_{\pm},H_{e}
satisfying Assumptions
1
,
2
such that
{\mathcal{G}}\not\subset\Sigma(H_{e})
.

1.4.
Sketch of proof.

We explain here the main ideas leading to Theorem
1
. Strictly speaking, the paper will focus on quantitative forms of these ideas to obtain Theorem
2
, which (as explained above) implies Theorem
1
.

Let
H
be a selfadjoint, short-range Hamiltonian on
\ell^{2}({\mathbb{Z}}^{2},{\mathbb{C}}^{d})
and
\lambda\notin\Sigma(H)
. Our main argument is the observation that
\sigma(H,\lambda)
can be computed from the sole knowledge of
H
within a ball centered at any point
n
of
{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}
, as long as its radius
R
is sufficiently large (depending on
\nu
, but independent of
n
). While this may seem paradoxical, the global condition
\lambda\notin\Sigma(H)
actually ensures that the result of the computation is independent of
n
.

We proceed now by contradiction. Let
H_{\pm},H_{e}
satisfy the assumptions of Theorem
1
, and assume that
\lambda\in{\mathcal{G}}\setminus\Sigma(H_{e})
. We can then define
\sigma(H_{e},\lambda)
, and, thanks to the above observations, compute it from sole knowledge of
H_{e}
on balls of the form
B_{R}({\textbf{n}})
.

Pick now
n
so that
B_{R}({\textbf{n}})
lies deep in
\Omega
, i.e. in
\Omega
and far from
\partial\Omega
; this is possible because under
{\operatorname{Fr}}(\Omega)=\infty
,
\Omega
contains arbitrarily large balls. From Assumption
2
,
H_{e}
is roughly
H_{+}
there and we deduce
\sigma(H_{e},\lambda)=\sigma(H_{+},\lambda)
. Likewise, pick
n
so that
B_{R}({\textbf{n}})
lies deep in
\Omega^{c}
and deduce
\sigma(H_{e},\lambda)=\sigma(H_{-},\lambda)
. This contradicts the assumption
\sigma(H_{+},\lambda)\neq\sigma(H_{-},\lambda)
. Therefore,
\lambda\in\Sigma(H_{e})
.

We comment that this proof also applies to materials made off three or more topological insulators, with at least two of them with different bulk invariant filling regions with infinite filling radius.

1.5.
Relation to existing results

The question of how the shape of the truncation affects the edge spectrum has considered before. The bulk-edge correspondence predicts the emergence of edge spectrum for half-space truncations: it gives the resulting interface conductance as a difference of Chern numbers
[
19
,
11
,
12
,
2
,
14
,
5
,
9
,
20
]
.

In
[
13
]
, the authors focus on truncated quantum Hall Hamiltonians and derive a global analytic condition on
\Omega
for the emergence of edge spectrum. They verify that this condition holds for regions with asymptotically flat boundary. This includes local perturbation of sectors.

More recently the techniques have drifted to coarse geometry and K-theory. In
[
24
,
21
]
the authors prove that magnetic Hamiltonians truncated to corners or sectors, and their local perturbations, have edge spectrum. The furthest-reaching work is due to Ludewig–Thiang
[
20
]
. It predicts that truncations of general topological insulators to domains coarsely equivalent to half-planes admit edge spectrum.

Our work derives a simple visual criterion for emergence of edge spectrum: both
\Omega
and
\Omega^{c}
contain arbitrary large balls (equivalently,
{\operatorname{Fr}}(\Omega)={\operatorname{Fr}}(\Omega^{c})=\infty
). It is not evident how our condition relates to those of
[
13
,
20
]
. Our proof returns to an analytic approach, which has the benefit to come up with a quantitative form of the result. This version explains in what sense experimentalists observe edge spectrum in bounded samples.

The shape of edge states matters in technological applications: they are the vectors of conduction along the edge. When the boundary is weakly curved – which correspond to the adiabatic or semiclassical regime – several works constructed edge states as wavepackets
[
4
,
6
,
10
,
18
]
. The assumptions in the present work are significantly weaker – we only assume existence of a spectral gap – but the result is also significantly weaker: we only prove existence of edge spectrum.

1.6.
Open problems

An open problem is whether the bulk-edge correspondence generalizes to truncations to domains
\Omega
satisfying
{\operatorname{Fr}}(\Omega)={\operatorname{Fr}}(\Omega^{c})=\infty
. We believe that this condition will need to be strengthened to something more quantitative for the bulk-edge correspondence to hold. There are already results that use the K-theoretic and coarse geometry framework
[
20
]
; it would be nice to provide analytic proofs.

It has been shown that the bulk-edge correspondence holds when the gap condition (
{\mathcal{G}}\cap\Sigma(H_{\pm})
is empty) is replaced by a mobility gap condition (
H_{\pm}
exhibits dynamical localization within
{\mathcal{G}}
); see
[
12
]
. At this point we do not know if relaxing Assumption
1
to a mobility gap gives rise to edge spectrum.

The main proposition

We proved Theorem
1
using Theorem

.
Theorem

will essentially follow from Proposition

Assumption 3
.

H
is a selfadjoint, short-range operator on
\ell^{2}({\mathbb{Z}}^{2};{\mathbb{C}}^{d})

\lambda\in{\mathbb{R}}
and

(\lambda-\delta,\lambda+\delta)\cap\Sigma(H)=\emptyset.

Proposition 1
.

There exists a constant
C_{\nu}\geq 1

,

,

|(H_{1}-H_{2})({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})|\leq\epsilon,\quad{\bm{x}},{\bm{y}}\in B_{4r}({\textbf{n}}).

Then we have:

|\sigma(H_{1},\lambda)-\sigma(H_{2},\lambda)|\leq\frac{C_{\nu}}{\delta^{12}}\left(e^{-\frac{\delta r}{2C_{\nu}}}+\epsilon^{1/2}\right).

2
assuming Proposition
1
.

1. We recall that

is a fixed parameter. In this first step, we set the values the constants

and

; we set

, that is,
\alpha_{\nu}\frac{\ln R}{R}

.

\displaystyle(\lambda_{*}-\delta,\lambda_{*}+\delta)\cap\Sigma(H_{e})=\emptyset,

(2.7)

\displaystyle(\lambda_{*}-\delta,\lambda_{*}+\delta)\cap\Sigma(H_{+})=\emptyset,

(2.8)

\displaystyle(\lambda_{*}-\delta,\lambda_{*}+\delta)\cap\Sigma(H_{-})=\emptyset,

Since

(H_{e}-H_{+})({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})=(H_{e}-{\mathbb{1}}_{\Omega}H_{+}{\mathbb{1}}_{\Omega}-{\mathbb{1}}_{\Omega^{c}}H_{-}{\mathbb{1}}_{\Omega^{c}})({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})=E({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}}),

, which is a spectral gap of

3.
Proof of Proposition
1

3.1.
On short-range Hamiltonians

Throughout the proofs below, we will use the following estimates, proved in Appendix
A
: For
a\in(0,1]
,
R>0
, we have

(3.1)

\displaystyle\sum_{s\in{\mathbb{Z}}}e^{-2a|s|}

\displaystyle\leq\dfrac{2}{a},\quad\sum\limits_{{\bm{x}}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}}e^{-2a|{\bm{x}}|}\leq\dfrac{4}{a^{2}},\quad\text{and}\quad\sum_{|{\bm{x}}|\geq R,{\bm{x}}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}}e^{-2a|{\bm{x}}|}\leq\dfrac{8}{a^{2}}e^{-aR}.

We make here a few observations on the selfadjoint, short-range Hamiltonians
H
on
\ell^{2}({\mathbb{Z}}^{2},{\mathbb{C}}^{d})
. First, they are bounded in terms of the (fixed) parameter
\nu\in(0,1]
quantifying the short-range condition (
1.3
). Specifically, an application of Schur’s test gives:

(3.2)

\|H\|\leq\dfrac{4}{\nu^{3}}.

We refer to Appendix
A
for the proof.

As in
[
12
,
1
]
, we introduce

(3.3)

S_{\alpha}:=\sup_{{\bm{x}}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}}\sum_{{\bm{y}}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}}|H({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})|\left(e^{\alpha|{\bm{x}}-{\bm{y}}|}-1\right).

We note that if
H
is short range under the definition (
1.3
), then for any
\alpha\in(0,2\nu)
,
S_{\alpha}<+\infty
. Also, for later use, if

\alpha\in(0,\nu]
:

(3.4)

\dfrac{S_{\alpha}}{\alpha}\leq\dfrac{S_{\nu}}{\nu}\leq\dfrac{16}{\nu^{4}},

Again, see Appendix
A
for the proof.

We recall the Combes–Thomas inequality
[
8
]
:

Proposition 2
.

[
1
]
*Theorem 10.5
Let
H
be a selfadjoint, short-range operator on
\ell^{2}({\mathbb{Z}}^{2};{\mathbb{C}}^{d})
. If
\alpha\in(0,2\nu)
and
z\in{\mathbb{C}}
are such that
\Delta:=d(z,\Sigma(H))>S_{\alpha}
, then we have

(3.5)

|(H-z)^{-1}({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})|\leq\frac{1}{\Delta-S_{\alpha}}\exp(-\alpha|{\bm{x}}-{\bm{y}}|).

3.2.
Spectral projections

An application of the Combes–Thomas inequality is control of the kernels of spectral projections:

Lemma 3.1
.

There exists a constant
C_{\nu}
, such that for any
H,\lambda
satisfying Assumption
3
:

(3.6)

\big|P_{\lambda}(H)({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})\big|\leq\frac{C_{\nu}}{\delta}\exp\left(-\dfrac{\delta}{C_{\nu}}|{\bm{x}}-{\bm{y}}|\right).

Proof.

Set
\alpha=2^{-5}\nu^{4}\delta
, so that
\alpha\leq\nu

and
S_{\alpha}\leq\frac{16\alpha}{\nu^{4}}\leq\frac{\delta}{2}
, see (
3.4
). Let
\gamma
be a contour enclosing
\Sigma(H)\cap(-\infty,\lambda)
, at least
\delta
-distant from
\Sigma(H)
. For
z\in\gamma
, we have:

(3.7)

(H-z)^{-1}({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})\leq\dfrac{e^{-\alpha|{\bm{x}}-{\bm{y}}|}}{\Delta-S_{\alpha}}\leq\frac{e^{-\alpha|{\bm{x}}-{\bm{y}}|}}{\delta-\frac{\delta}{2}}\leq\frac{2}{\delta}\exp\left(-\alpha|{\bm{x}}-{\bm{y}}|\right).

Integrating this over
\gamma
, we have:

(3.8)

\big|P_{\lambda}(H)({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})\big|=\left|\dfrac{1}{2\pi i}\oint_{\gamma}(H-z)^{-1}({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})dz\right|\leq\frac{|\gamma|}{\pi\delta}\exp\left(-\alpha|{\bm{x}}-{\bm{y}}|\right).

Without loss of generalities, we may assume that
|\gamma|\leq 2\pi+4\|H\|
– see Figure
3

From the bound (
3.2
) on
\|H\|
, we deduce that

(3.9)

\dfrac{|\gamma|}{\pi}\leq 2+\dfrac{16}{\pi\nu^{3}}\leq\dfrac{8}{\nu^{3}}.

We conclude that

(3.10)

\big|P_{\lambda}(H)({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})\big|\leq\frac{8}{\nu^{3}\delta}\exp\left(-\dfrac{\nu^{4}\delta}{32}|{\bm{x}}-{\bm{y}}|\right).

This yields (
3.6
) (with for instance
C_{\nu}=32\nu^{-4}
).
∎

Figure 3.
The contour
\gamma
. We note that the spectrum of
H
is contained in
[-\|H\|,\|H\|]
, so it has diameter less than
2\|H\|
. Since
\delta\leq 1
, the two half-circles have perimeter less than
2\pi
and the length of
\gamma
is less than
4\|H\|+2\pi
. Note that
\gamma
does not need to enclose
\lambda
.

Remark 1
.

As a result, if
H
,
\lambda
satisfy Assumption
3
, then the open bounded interval
(\lambda-\delta,\lambda+\delta)
satisfies condition
[
12
]
*(1.2); and it follows that
\sigma(H,\lambda)
is well-defined and is an integer
[
12
]
*Proposition 3.

Lemma 3.2
.

There exists a constant
C_{\nu}
such that the following holds. Let
r,\epsilon>0
, and two triplets
H_{1},\lambda,\delta
and
H_{2},\lambda,\delta
satisfying Assumption
3
, such that for
{\bm{x}},{\bm{y}}\in B_{4r}(0)
,

(3.11)

|H_{1}({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})-H_{2}({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})|\leq\epsilon.

Then for
({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})
in
B_{2r}(0)
:

(3.12)

|P_{\lambda}(H_{1})({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})-P_{\lambda}(H_{2})({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})|\leq\dfrac{C_{\nu}}{\delta^{6}}\left(\exp\left(-\dfrac{\delta r}{C_{\nu}}\right)+\epsilon\right).

Proof.

Let
\alpha,\gamma
be as in the proof of Lemma
3.1
. We have:

(3.13)

\big(P_{\lambda}(H_{1})-P_{\lambda}(H_{2})\big)({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})=\sum_{{\bm{x}}^{\prime},{\bm{y}}^{\prime}}\dfrac{1}{2\pi i}\oint_{\gamma}(H_{2}-z)^{-1}({\bm{x}},{\bm{x}}^{\prime})\big(H_{2}({\bm{x}}^{\prime},{\bm{y}}^{\prime})-H_{1}({\bm{x}}^{\prime},{\bm{y}}^{\prime})\big)(H_{1}-z)^{-1}({\bm{y}}^{\prime},{\bm{y}})dz.

From (
3.7
) then (
3.9
), and using the bound (
3.9
) for
|\gamma|
:

(3.14)

\displaystyle\big|\big(P_{\lambda}(H_{1})-P_{\lambda}(H_{2})\big)({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})\big|

\displaystyle\leq\dfrac{2|\gamma|}{\pi\delta^{2}}\sum_{{\bm{x}}^{\prime},{\bm{y}}^{\prime}}\exp\left(-\alpha|{\bm{x}}-{\bm{x}}^{\prime}|-\alpha|{\bm{y}}-{\bm{y}}^{\prime}|\right)\big|H_{2}({\bm{x}}^{\prime},{\bm{y}}^{\prime})-H_{1}({\bm{x}}^{\prime},{\bm{y}}^{\prime})\big|

(3.15)

\displaystyle\leq\dfrac{2^{4}}{\nu^{3}\delta^{2}}\sum_{{\bm{x}}^{\prime},{\bm{y}}^{\prime}}\exp\left(-\alpha|{\bm{x}}-{\bm{x}}^{\prime}|-\alpha|{\bm{y}}-{\bm{y}}^{\prime}|\right)\big|H_{2}({\bm{x}}^{\prime},{\bm{y}}^{\prime})-H_{1}({\bm{x}}^{\prime},{\bm{y}}^{\prime})\big|.

We split the RHS sum in two parts, depending whether
{\bm{x}}^{\prime},{\bm{y}}^{\prime}\in B_{4r}(0)
or not. When they do, we can use the bound (
3.11
) on the kernel of
H_{1}-H_{2}
. It yields

(3.16)

\displaystyle\sum_{x^{\prime},y^{\prime}\in B_{4r}(0)}\exp\left(-\alpha|{\bm{x}}-{\bm{x}}^{\prime}|-\alpha|{\bm{y}}-{\bm{y}}^{\prime}|\right)\big|H_{2}(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})-H_{1}(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\big|

(3.17)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\epsilon\sum_{x^{\prime},y^{\prime}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}}\exp\left(-\alpha|{\bm{x}}-{\bm{x}}^{\prime}|-\alpha|{\bm{y}}-{\bm{y}}^{\prime}|\right)

(3.18)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\epsilon\sum_{x^{\prime},y^{\prime}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}}\exp\left(-\alpha|{\bm{x}}^{\prime}|-\alpha|{\bm{y}}^{\prime}|\right)\leq\epsilon\left(\dfrac{16}{\alpha^{2}}\right)^{2},

where in the last line we used (
3.1
).

When now restricting to
{\bm{x}}^{\prime}
or
{\bm{y}}^{\prime}\in B_{4r}(0)^{c}
, we note that for
{\bm{x}},{\bm{y}}\in B_{2r}(0)
either
|{\bm{x}}-{\bm{x}}^{\prime}|\geq 2r
or
|{\bm{y}}-{\bm{y}}^{\prime}|\geq 2r
. Thus, we have

(3.19)

\displaystyle\sum_{{\bm{x}}^{\prime}\text{ or }{\bm{y}}^{\prime}\in B_{2r}(0)^{c}}\exp\left(-\alpha|{\bm{x}}-{\bm{x}}^{\prime}|-\alpha|{\bm{y}}-{\bm{y}}^{\prime}|\right)\big|H_{2}({\bm{x}}^{\prime},{\bm{y}}^{\prime})-H_{1}({\bm{x}}^{\prime},{\bm{y}}^{\prime})\big|

(3.20)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\dfrac{2}{\nu}\sum_{{\bm{x}}^{\prime}\text{ or }{\bm{y}}^{\prime}\in B_{4r}(0)^{c}}\exp\left(-\alpha|{\bm{x}}-{\bm{x}}^{\prime}|-\alpha|{\bm{y}}-{\bm{y}}^{\prime}|\right)\leq\dfrac{4}{\nu}\sum_{{\bm{x}}^{\prime}\in B_{2r}(0)^{c},{\bm{y}}^{\prime}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}}\exp\left(-\alpha|{\bm{x}}^{\prime}|-\alpha|{\bm{y}}^{\prime}|\right)

(3.21)

\displaystyle\leq\dfrac{4}{\nu}\left(\frac{32}{\alpha^{2}}e^{-\alpha r}\right)\left(\frac{16}{\alpha^{2}}\right)=\frac{8}{\nu}\left(\frac{2^{8}}{\alpha^{4}}\right)e^{-\alpha r}.

In the last line, we applied (
3.1
). So, heading back to (
3.15
) and using the value
\alpha=2^{-5}\nu^{4}\delta
from the proof of Lemma
3.1
, we obtain

(3.22)

\big|\big(P_{\lambda}(H_{1})-P_{\lambda}(H_{2})\big)(x,y)\big|\leq\dfrac{2^{7}}{\nu^{4}\delta^{2}}\left(\dfrac{2^{8}}{\alpha^{4}}\right)(e^{-\alpha r}+\epsilon)=\dfrac{2^{35}}{\nu^{20}\delta^{6}}\left(\exp\left(-\dfrac{\nu^{4}\delta}{32}r\right)+\epsilon\right).

This yields (
3.12
) (with
C_{\nu}=2^{35}\nu^{-20}
– we made no attempts to minimize this constant).
∎

3.3.
Technical result.

The key technical step in the proof of Proposition
1
is:

Proposition 3
.

Fix
\varepsilon>0,\ C>0,\ r>0,\ \beta\in(0,1]
. Let
A_{0},A_{1},A_{2}
be three operators on
\ell^{2}({\mathbb{Z}}^{2},{\mathbb{C}}^{d})
with the following properties:

(i)

For
j\in\{0,1,2\}
,
|A_{j}({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})|\leq Ce^{-2\beta|x-y|}
;

(ii)

There exists
k\in\{0,1,2\}
such that if
{\bm{x}},{\bm{y}}\in B_{2r}(0)
,then
\big|A_{k}({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})\big|\leq C\varepsilon
.

Then
B:=A_{0}[A_{1},\Lambda_{1}][A_{2},\Lambda_{2}]
is trace-class and

(3.23)

\big|\Tr(B)\big|\leq C^{3}\frac{2^{16}}{\beta^{6}}\left(e^{-\beta r}+\varepsilon^{1/2}\right).

Let us start with a simple result:

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
A
be a bounded operator on
\ell^{2}({\mathbb{Z}}^{2},{\mathbb{C}}^{d})
with
|A({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})|\leq e^{-2\beta|{\bm{x}}-{\bm{y}}|}
. Then

(3.24)

\big|[A,\Lambda_{1}]({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})\big|\leq e^{-2\beta|x_{1}|-2\beta|y_{1}|-2\beta|x_{2}-y_{2}|}.

If moreover
|A({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})|\leq\varepsilon
for
({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})\in B_{2r}(0)
, then

(3.25)

\big|[A,\Lambda_{1}]({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})\big|\leq\varepsilon^{1/2}e^{-\beta|x_{1}|-\beta|y_{1}|-\beta|x_{2}-y_{2}|},\qquad{\bm{x}},{\bm{y}}\in B_{2r}(0).

Proof.

The kernel of
[A,\Lambda_{1}]
is

(3.26)

[A,\Lambda_{1}]({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})=A({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})(\Lambda_{1}({\bm{y}})-\Lambda_{1}({\bm{x}})).

We note that
|\Lambda_{1}({\bm{y}})-\Lambda_{1}({\bm{x}})|=0
if
x_{1},y_{1}
are both positive or both negative; and it is at most
1
otherwise, that is if
x_{1}y_{1}\leq 0
. Therefore, we have the bound

(3.27)

\big|[A,\Lambda_{1}]({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})\big|\leq Ce^{-2\beta|{\bm{x}}-{\bm{y}}|}\mathbb{1}_{x_{1}y_{1}\leq 0}.

Whenever
x_{1}y_{1}\leq 0
, we have

(3.28)

|{\bm{x}}-{\bm{y}}|=|x_{1}-y_{1}|+|x_{2}-y_{2}|=|x_{1}|+|y_{1}|+|x_{2}-y_{2}|.

It follows that

(3.29)

\big|[A,\Lambda_{1}]({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})\big|\leq e^{-2\beta|x_{2}-y_{2}|-2\beta|x_{1}|-2\beta|y_{1}|}.

This completes the proof of (
3.24
). To prove (
3.25
), we recall that
|\Lambda_{1}({\bm{y}})-\Lambda_{1}({\bm{x}})|\leq 1
; hence
\big|[A,\Lambda_{1}]({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})\big|\leq\varepsilon
. It suffices then to interpolate this bound with (
3.24
).
∎

For the proof of Proposition
3
, we will use the following inequality:
for
\beta\in(0,1]
,
{\bm{x}},{\bm{w}}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}
,

(3.30)

\sum_{{\bm{y}},{\bm{z}}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}}e^{-2\beta|{\bm{x}}-{\bm{y}}|-2\beta|y_{2}-z_{2}|-2\beta|y_{1}|-2\beta|z_{1}|-2\beta|z_{1}-w_{1}|-2\beta|z_{2}|-2\beta|w_{2}|}\leq\left(\dfrac{4}{\beta}\right)^{4}e^{-\beta|{\bm{x}}|-\beta|{\bm{w}}|}.

We refer to Appendix
A
for a proof.

Proof of Proposition
3
.

1. By a scaling argument, we can assume that
C=1
. We first control the kernel of
B
:

(3.31)

|B({\bm{x}},{\bm{w}})|=\left|\sum_{y,z\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}}A_{0}({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})B_{1}({\bm{y}},{\bm{z}})B_{2}({\bm{z}},{\bm{x}})\right|,

where
B_{j}=[A_{j},\Lambda_{j}]
. We control the kernels of
A_{0}
,
B_{1},B_{2}
using assumption
(i)
and (
3.24
). It yields:

(3.32)

\displaystyle|B({\bm{x}},{\bm{w}})|

\displaystyle=\left|\sum_{{\bm{y}},{\bm{z}}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}}A_{0}({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})B_{1}({\bm{y}},{\bm{z}})B_{2}({\bm{z}},{\bm{w}})\right|

(3.33)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{{\bm{y}},{\bm{z}}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}}e^{-2\beta|{\bm{x}}-{\bm{y}}|-2\beta|y_{2}-z_{2}|-2\beta|y_{1}|-2\beta|z_{1}|-2\beta|z_{1}-w_{1}|-2\beta|z_{2}|-2\beta|w_{2}|}

(3.34)

\displaystyle\leq\left(\dfrac{4}{\beta}\right)^{4}e^{-\beta|{\bm{x}}|-\beta|{\bm{w}}|}.

Thus
|B({\bm{x}},{\bm{w}})|
decays exponentially, hence
B
is trace-class; moreover

(3.35)

\displaystyle\Tr(B)\leq\sum_{{\bm{x}}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}}|B({\bm{x}},{\bm{x}})|\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{{\bm{x}},{\bm{y}},{\bm{z}}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}}e^{-2\beta|{\bm{x}}-{\bm{y}}|-2\beta|y_{2}-z_{2}|-2\beta|y_{1}|-2\beta|z_{1}|-2\beta|z_{1}-x_{1}|-2\beta|z_{2}|-2\beta|x_{2}|}

(3.36)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\sum_{{\bm{x}},{\bm{y}},{\bm{z}}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}}f(2\beta,{\bm{x}},{\bm{y}},{\bm{z}}).

2. We now split the sum in (
3.36
) in two pieces:
|{\bm{x}}|\geq r
and
|{\bm{x}}|\leq r
. Thanks to (
3.30
) and (
3.1
), we have

(3.37)

\sum_{|{\bm{x}}|\geq r}f(2\beta,{\bm{x}},{\bm{y}},{\bm{z}})\leq\left(\dfrac{4}{\beta}\right)^{4}\sum_{|{\bm{x}}|\geq r}e^{-2\beta|{\bm{x}}|}\leq\left(\dfrac{4}{\beta}\right)^{4}\dfrac{8}{\beta^{2}}e^{-\beta r}=\frac{2^{11}}{\beta^{6}}e^{-\beta r}.

We focus below on
|{\bm{x}}|\leq r
.

3. If
k=0
in (ii), then we split the sum in (
3.36
) according to
|{\bm{y}}|\geq 2r
and
|{\bm{y}}|\leq 2r
. In the former case,
|{\bm{x}}-{\bm{y}}|\geq r
. Therefore, when
|{\bm{x}}|\leq r,\ |{\bm{y}}|\geq 2r
, we deduce that

(3.38)

\displaystyle\big|A_{0}({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})\big|

\displaystyle\leq e^{-2\beta|{\bm{x}}-{\bm{y}}|}\leq e^{-\beta r-\beta|{\bm{x}}-{\bm{y}}|},

(3.39)

\displaystyle\big|A_{0}({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})B_{1}({\bm{y}},{\bm{z}})B_{2}({\bm{z}},{\bm{x}})\big|

\displaystyle\leq e^{-\beta r}f(\beta,{\bm{x}},{\bm{y}},{\bm{z}}).

If now
|{\bm{y}}|\leq 2r
(and
|{\bm{x}}|\leq r\leq 2r
), then we can use (ii). Interpolating with (i) gives, for
|{\bm{x}}|\leq r,\ |{\bm{y}}|\leq 2r
:

(3.40)

\displaystyle\big|A_{0}({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})\big|

\displaystyle\leq\varepsilon^{1/2}e^{-\beta|{\bm{x}}-{\bm{y}}|},

(3.41)

\displaystyle\big|A_{0}({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})B_{1}({\bm{y}},{\bm{z}})B_{2}({\bm{z}},{\bm{x}})\big|

\displaystyle\leq\varepsilon^{1/2}f(\beta,{\bm{x}},{\bm{y}},{\bm{z}}).

Summing the bounds (
3.39
) and (
3.41
) produces:

(3.42)

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}|{\bm{x}}|\leq r,\\
{\bm{y}},{\bm{z}}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}\end{subarray}}\big|A_{0}({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})B_{1}({\bm{y}},{\bm{z}})B_{2}({\bm{z}},{\bm{x}})\big|

\displaystyle\leq e^{-\beta r}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}|{\bm{x}}|\leq r,\\
|{\bm{y}}|\geq 2r,{\bm{z}}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}\end{subarray}}f(\beta,{\bm{x}},{\bm{y}},{\bm{z}})+\varepsilon^{1/2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}|{\bm{x}}|\leq r,\\
|{\bm{y}}|\leq 2r,{\bm{z}}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}\end{subarray}}f(\beta,{\bm{x}},{\bm{y}},{\bm{z}})

(3.43)

\displaystyle\leq\left(e^{-\beta r}+\varepsilon^{1/2}\right)\sum_{{\bm{x}},{\bm{y}},{\bm{z}}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}}f(\beta,{\bm{x}},{\bm{y}},{\bm{z}})

(3.44)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2^{16}}{\beta^{6}}\left(e^{-\beta r}+\varepsilon^{1/2}\right).

where we used (
3.30
) and (
3.1
) to get

(3.45)

\sum_{{\bm{x}},{\bm{y}},{\bm{z}}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}}f(\beta,{\bm{x}},{\bm{y}},{\bm{z}})\leq\left(\frac{8}{\beta}\right)^{4}\sum_{{\bm{x}}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}}e^{-\beta|{\bm{x}}|}\leq\left(\frac{8}{\beta}\right)^{4}\frac{16}{\beta^{2}}=\frac{2^{16}}{\beta^{6}}.

4. We now work on
k=1
. We split the sum in
{\bm{y}},{\bm{z}}\in B_{2r}(0)
and
{\bm{y}}
or
{\bm{z}}
outside
B_{2r}(0)
. In the latter case, either
|{\bm{x}}-{\bm{y}}|\geq r
or
|{\bm{z}}-{\bm{x}}|\geq r
. So either

(3.46)

\big|A_{0}({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})\big|\leq e^{-\beta r}e^{-\beta|{\bm{x}}-{\bm{y}}|}\text{ or }\big|B_{2}({\bm{z}},{\bm{x}})\big|\leq e^{-\beta r}e^{-\beta|{\bm{z}}-{\bm{x}}|}.

In either case, we recover the bound (
3.39
). In the former case, we can use (
3.25
), and recover (
3.41
). Since (
3.39
) and (
3.41
) lead to (
3.44
), we obtain this bound here as well.

5. The case
k=2
follows the same path as
k=0
. This completes the proof.
∎

3.4.
Comparison of bulk conductances

We will use the following result
[
12
]
*Lemma 7(ii), which essentially states that the bulk conductance is independent of
\Lambda_{1},\Lambda_{2}
:

Proposition 4
.

Let
H
be a short-range operator on
\ell^{2}({\mathbb{Z}}^{2},{\mathbb{C}}^{d})
and
\lambda\notin\Sigma(H)
. For any
n
,

(3.47)

\sigma(H,\lambda)=-2\pi i\Tr\left(P_{\lambda}(H)\big[[P_{\lambda}(H),\Lambda_{1}(\cdot-n_{1})],[P_{\lambda}(H),\Lambda_{2}(\cdot-n_{2})]\big]\right).

We are now ready to prove Proposition
1
.

Proof of Proposition
1
.

1. For simplicity, use the notation
P_{j}=P_{\lambda}(H_{j})
. Let
T
be the translation by
n
:
Tu(\cdot)=u(\cdot-{\textbf{n}})
. We have
\Lambda_{j}(\cdot-n_{j})=T\Lambda_{j}(\cdot)T^{*}
and
T^{*}P_{\lambda}(H)T=P_{\lambda}(T^{*}HT)
. Using these and Proposition
4
, as well as the cyclicity of the trace, we obtain

(3.48)

\displaystyle\sigma(H_{j},\lambda)

\displaystyle=-2\pi i\Tr\big(P_{j}\big[[P_{j},T\Lambda_{1}T^{*}],[P_{j},T\Lambda_{2}T^{*}]\big]\big)

(3.49)

\displaystyle=-2\pi i\Tr\big(P_{j}\big[T[T^{*}P_{j}T,\Lambda_{1}]T^{*},T[T^{*}P_{j}T,\Lambda_{2}]T^{*}\big]\big)

(3.50)

\displaystyle=-2\pi i\Tr\big(P_{j}T\big[[T^{*}P_{j}T,\Lambda_{1}],[T^{*}P_{j}T,\Lambda_{2}]\big]T^{*}\big)

(3.51)

\displaystyle=-2\pi i\Tr\big(T^{*}P_{j}T\big[[T^{*}P_{j}T,\Lambda_{1}],[T^{*}P_{j}T,\Lambda_{2}]\big]\big)=\sigma(T^{*}H_{j}T,\lambda).

Therefore, by replacing
H_{j}
by
T^{*}H_{j}T
, we can simply assume that
{\textbf{n}}=0
.

2. Now we write

(3.52)

\sigma(H_{1},\lambda)-\sigma(H_{2},\lambda)=T(P_{1}-P_{2},P_{1},P_{1})+T(P_{2},P_{1}-P_{2},P_{1})+T(P_{2},P_{2},P_{1}-P_{2}),

where
T(A_{0},A_{1},A_{2})
is the trilinear form

(3.53)

T(A_{0},A_{1},A_{2})=-2\pi i\Tr\left(A_{0}[A_{1},\Lambda_{1}][A_{2},\Lambda_{2}]\right)+2\pi i\Tr\left(A_{0}[A_{2},\Lambda_{2}][A_{1},\Lambda_{1}]\right).

From Lemmas
3.1
and
3.2
, we have (for a constant
C_{\nu}
depending on
\nu
only):

(3.54)

\displaystyle\big|P_{j}({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})\big|

\displaystyle\leq\dfrac{C_{\nu}}{\delta}e^{-\delta|{\bm{x}}-{\bm{y}}|/C_{\nu}},\qquad j=1,2;

(3.55)

\displaystyle\big|P_{1}({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})-P_{2}({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})\big|

\displaystyle\leq\dfrac{C_{\nu}}{\delta^{6}}\left(e^{-\delta|{\bm{x}}-{\bm{y}}|/C_{\nu}}+\epsilon\right),\qquad{\bm{x}},{\bm{y}}\in B_{2r}({\textbf{n}}).

Therefore, the triplets
(A_{0},A_{1},A_{2})
involved in (
3.52
) satisfy the assumptions of Proposition
3
, with constants

(3.56)

C=\dfrac{C_{\nu}}{\delta},\quad\beta=\dfrac{\delta}{2C_{\nu}},\quad\varepsilon=\dfrac{1}{\delta^{5}}\left(e^{-\delta r/C_{\nu}}+\epsilon\right).

So, we deduce that

(3.57)

\big|\sigma(H_{1},\lambda))-\sigma(H_{2},\lambda)\big|\leq\dfrac{C_{\nu}}{\delta^{3+6}}\left(e^{\frac{\delta r}{2C_{\nu}}}+\dfrac{1}{\delta^{5/2}}(e^{-\frac{\delta r}{C_{\nu}}}+\epsilon)^{1/2}\right)\leq\dfrac{C_{\nu}}{\delta^{12}}\left(e^{-\frac{\delta r}{2C_{\nu}}}+\epsilon^{1/2}\right).

This completes the proof of Proposition
1
.
∎

4.
Violation of the bulk-edge correspondence in strips

In this section, we show that topological insulators lying within strips do not necessarily support edge states along their boundary; this means that geometrically,
\Omega
needs to be unbounded in all directions for the bulk spectral gaps to systematically fill.

Specifically, for any
L>0
, we construct an edge operator
H_{e}
satisfying Assumption
2
with:

•

The bulk operators
H_{\pm}
are insulating at energy
0
, with bulk conductance
\mp 1
;

•

\Omega\subset{\mathbb{Z}}\times[-L,L]
, in particular
{\operatorname{Fr}}(\Omega)\leq L
;

•

0\notin\Sigma(H_{e})
: the bulk gap did not fully close.

Hence, although the bulk operators
H_{\pm}
represent topologically distinct topological phases, the interface
\partial\Omega
does not support conducting states for
H_{e}
. In particular, a material made of topologically distinct insulators across
\partial\Omega
,
\Omega={\mathbb{N}}\times[-L,L]
, violates the bulk-edge correspondence. This was suspected by G.M. Graf, but the problem was left open in an online talk by G.C. Thiang
[
16
]
.

4.1.
Haldane model

Our bulk operators are based on Haldane’s model
[
17
]
, which we review briefly. For each site of the honeycomb lattice, the Haldane Hamiltonian models tunneling to the three nearest neighbors and
complex
coupling (for instance induced by a periodic magnetic field) to the six second-nearest neighbors, see Figure
4
. We will use a version based on the
{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}
-lattice (which only differ from the standard honeycomb version by a linear change of variable):

H_{\pm}=H_{0}\pm S

where
H_{0},S
are selfadjoint, short-range Hamiltonians on
\ell^{2}({\mathbb{Z}}^{2},{\mathbb{C}}^{d})
given by:

\begin{split}&(H_{0}\psi)_{n}=\begin{bmatrix}\psi_{n}^{B}+\psi_{n-e_{1}}^{B}+\psi_{n-e_{2}}^{B}\\
\psi_{n}^{A}+\psi_{n+e_{1}}^{A}+\psi_{n+e_{2}}^{A}\end{bmatrix},\\
&(S\psi)_{n}=is\begin{bmatrix}\psi_{n+e_{1}}^{A}-\psi_{n-e_{1}}^{A}+\psi_{n-e_{2}}^{A}-\psi_{n+e_{2}}^{A}+\psi_{n+e_{2}-e_{1}}^{A}-\psi_{n+e_{1}-e_{2}}^{A}\\
-\psi_{n+e_{1}}^{B}+\psi_{n-e_{1}}^{B}-\psi_{n-e_{2}}^{B}+\psi_{n+e_{2}}^{B}-\psi_{n+e_{2}-e_{1}}^{B}+\psi_{n+e_{1}-e_{2}}^{B}\end{bmatrix}.\end{split}

The parameter
s
above quantifies the ratio between first and second-nearest neighbor coupling. We restrict it to
(0,1]
here.

Figure 4.
The black arrows represent tunneling to the three nearest neighbors; the dashed blue arrows represent complex coupling to six second-nearest neighbors in the Haldane model
[
17
]
.

As a result, the discrete Fourier transform w.r.t.
{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}
is

\begin{split}\hat{H}_{\pm}(\xi)&=\begin{bmatrix}\pm 2s\eta(\xi)&\overline{\omega(\xi)}\\
\omega(\xi)&\mp 2s\eta(\xi)\end{bmatrix},\qquad\xi\in[-\pi,\pi]^{2},\\
\omega(\xi)&:=1+e^{i\xi_{1}}+e^{i\xi_{2}},\\
\eta(\xi)&:=\sin(\xi_{1})-\sin(\xi_{2})+\sin(\xi_{2}-\xi_{1}).\end{split}

The eigenvalues of
\hat{H}_{\pm}(\xi)
are
\pm\lambda(\xi)
with

(4.1)

\lambda(\xi)=\sqrt{(2s\eta(\xi))^{2}+|\omega(\xi)|^{2}}.

The functions
\eta
and
\omega
do not vanish simultaneously; therefore
\lambda
never vanishes and the Hamiltonians
H_{\pm}
are insulating at energy
0
:
0\notin\Sigma(H_{\pm})
, equivalently
H_{\pm}
are invertible. Because of translational invariance, it is known their bulk conductance equals the Chern number of their low energy eigenbundle (see e.g.
[
23
]
*Equations (13) and Corollary 8.4.4) and we have
\sigma(H_{\pm};0)=\mp 1
, see
[
7
]
*§8. In particular, the insulators described by
H_{\pm}
are topologically distinct and
H_{\pm}
satisfy Assumption
1
.

4.2.
Edge operator

Fix
L>0
and
\Omega\subset[-L,L]\times{\mathbb{N}}
, we define the edge operator by

H_{e}:=H_{+}-2{\mathbb{1}}_{\Omega^{c}}S{\mathbb{1}}_{\Omega^{c}}.

Then formally,
H_{e}
is
H_{+}
in
\Omega
and
H_{-}
in
\Omega^{c}
. We prove Theorem
3
, formulated here as:

Proposition 5
.

H_{e}
satisfies Assumption
2
at energy
0
; however there exists a numerical constant
\rho_{0}>0
such that if
0<s\leq\rho_{0}L^{-1}
then
0\notin\Sigma(H_{e})
.

This implies that an interface lying in a strip, between two topologically distinct insulating phases does not necessarily fill the bulk spectral gap.

Remark 2
.

A general argument implies that the edge conductance of
H_{e}
across the
x_{2}
-axis is
0
. Indeed, this conductance is stable under perturbations within strips orthogonal to the
x_{2}
-axis, such as
2{\mathbb{1}}_{\Omega^{c}}S{\mathbb{1}}_{\Omega^{c}}
; so it is equal to that of
H_{e}+2{\mathbb{1}}_{\Omega^{c}}S{\mathbb{1}}_{\Omega^{c}}=H_{+}
, which is
0
.

To the best of our knowledge, there is no general argument that implies that
H_{e}
has edge conductance across the
x_{1}
-axis equal to
0
. For
0<s\leq\rho_{0}L^{-1}
, it is a consequence of Proposition
5
:
H_{e}
has no state with energy near
0
. For
\Omega={\mathbb{N}}\times[-L,L]
, this implies that no quantum particle may travel from one end of
\partial\Omega
to the other with high probability.

Proposition
5
is a consequence of the uncertainty principle: a function localized in frequency may not be localized in position. For
s
small, the Fourier transforms of
H_{\pm}
have eigenvalues of order
1
, unless
\xi
is near the zeroes
\xi_{\pm}^{*}=\pm 2\pi/3(1,-1)
of
\omega(\xi)
– in which case they are of order
s
. Therefore, a
O(s)
-perturbation (such as
S
) may not close the gap unless it generates states for
H_{e}
that are concentrated in frequency near
\xi_{\pm}^{*}
. By (a taylored version of) the uncertainty principle, such states may not be localized within a strip (such as
\Omega
).

4.3.
Proof

We prove Proposition
5
here. We will need the following lemmas:

Lemma 4.1
.

(1)

There exists
\lambda_{0}>0
, such that

\lambda(\xi)\geq\lambda_{0}\cdot s,\quad\text{~for~all~}\quad\xi\in[-\pi,\pi]^{2},\quad s\in(0,1].

(2)

There exists
\mu_{0}>0
such that

|\omega(\xi)|\geq\mu_{0}\cdot d(\xi,\{\xi_{\pm}^{*}\}),\quad\text{~for~all~}\quad\xi\in[-\pi,\pi]^{2}.

Remark 3
.

At the physical level these are well-known bounds; we taylor them here to our needs. Part (1) means that
H_{\pm}
have a spectral gap at energy
0
; part (2) means that the Wallace Hamiltonian
H_{0}
have a Dirac cone.

Proof of Lemma
4.1
.

(1) From (
4.1
) and
s\in(0,1]
, we have

(4.2)

\lambda(\xi)=\sqrt{(2s\eta(\xi))^{2}+|\omega(\xi)|^{2}}\geq s\sqrt{(2\eta(\xi))^{2}+|\omega(\xi)|^{2}}.

Moreover,

\begin{split}\omega(\xi)=0\quad&\Leftrightarrow\quad\xi=\xi^{*}_{\pm}=\pm\dfrac{2\pi}{3}(1,-1)\quad\Rightarrow\quad\eta(\xi^{*}_{\pm})=\pm\frac{3\sqrt{3}}{2}.\end{split}

Thus
\omega(\xi)
and
\eta(\xi)
cannot vanish simultaneously and
\sqrt{(2\eta(\xi))^{2}+|\omega(\xi)|^{2}}
never vanishes. By continuity,
\sqrt{(2\eta(\xi))^{2}+|\omega(\xi)|^{2}}\geq\lambda_{0}
for some
\lambda_{0}>0
. This proves (1) by going back to (
4.2
).

(2) We first write down
\omega
as a function valued in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
instead of
{\mathbb{C}}
:

(4.3)

\omega(\xi)=(1+\cos(\xi_{1})+\cos(\xi_{2}),\sin(\xi_{1})+\sin(\xi_{2})).

With this notation,

\nabla(\omega(\xi))=\begin{bmatrix}-\sin(\xi_{1})&-\sin(\xi_{2})\\
\cos(\xi_{1})&\cos(\xi_{2})\end{bmatrix},\qquad\nabla(\omega(\xi^{*}_{\pm}))=\dfrac{1}{2}\begin{bmatrix}\mp\sqrt{3}&\pm\sqrt{3}\\
-1&-1\end{bmatrix}.

As a result, for any
u=(u_{1},u_{2})^{T}
,

\begin{split}|\nabla\omega(\xi^{*}_{\pm})u|^{2}&=\frac{1}{4}[3(u_{1}-u_{2})^{2}+(u_{1}+u_{2})^{2}]=\frac{1}{4}(4u_{1}^{2}+4u_{2}^{2}-4u_{1}u_{2})\geq\frac{1}{2}|u|^{2}.\end{split}

Assume for any
n
, there is
\xi_{n}\neq\xi_{\pm}^{*}\in[-\pi,\pi]^{2}
such that

(4.4)

|\omega(\xi_{n})|\leq\frac{d(\xi_{n},\{\xi_{\pm}^{*}\})}{n}.

By compactness of
[-\pi,\pi]^{2}
, there exists a subsequence of
\xi_{n}
that converges to some
\xi_{\infty}
. From (
4.4
) and

, we deduce
|\omega(\xi_{\infty})|=0
hence
\xi_{\infty}
is either
\xi^{*}_{+}
or
\xi_{-}^{*}
. As a result, as
n\rightarrow\infty
,

\begin{split}\frac{1}{n}\geq\frac{|\omega(\xi_{n})-\omega(\xi_{\infty})|}{|\xi_{n}-\xi_{\infty}|}&=\frac{|\nabla\omega(\xi_{\infty})(\xi_{n}-\xi_{\infty})|+O(|\xi_{n}-\xi_{\infty}|^{2})}{|\xi_{n}-\xi_{\infty}|}\\
&\geq\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}+O(|\xi_{n}-\xi_{\infty}|)\to\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}.\end{split}

We get a contradiction. Thus there is some
\mu_{0}>0
such that
|\omega(\xi)|\geq\mu_{0}|\xi-\xi_{\pm}^{*}|
.∎

Lemma 4.2
.

There exists
C_{0}>0
such that for all
s\in(0,1]
,
L>0
and
u\in\ell^{2}({\mathbb{Z}}^{2},{\mathbb{C}}^{2})
:

\supp u\subset{\mathbb{Z}}\times[-L,L]\qquad\Rightarrow\qquad\|H_{+}^{-1}u\|_{\ell^{2}}\leq C_{0}L^{1/3}s^{-2/3}\|u\|_{\ell^{2}}.

Proof of Lemma
4.2
.

Recall that
\omega^{-1}(0)=\{\xi^{*}_{+},\xi^{*}_{-}\}
. Since eigenvalues of
\hat{H}_{+}^{-1}(\xi)
are
\pm\frac{1}{\lambda(\xi)}
, for any
\delta>0

(4.5)

\begin{split}\|H_{+}^{-1}u\|^{2}_{\ell^{2}({\mathbb{Z}}^{2})}&=\|\hat{H}_{+}^{-1}(\xi)\hat{u}(\xi)\|_{L^{2}([-\pi,\pi]^{2})}^{2}\\
&\leq\int_{[-\pi,\pi]^{2}}\frac{1}{(\lambda(\xi))^{2}}|\hat{u}(\xi)|^{2}d\xi\\
&\leq\int_{B_{\delta}(\xi^{*}_{\pm})}\frac{1}{\lambda_{0}^{2}s^{2}}|\hat{u}(\xi)|^{2}d\xi+\int_{\left(B_{\delta}(\xi^{*}_{\pm})\right)^{c}}\frac{1}{\mu_{0}^{2}\delta^{2}}|\hat{u}(\xi)|^{2}d\xi\\

thanks to Plancherel’s formula
\|\hat{u}\|_{L^{2}([-\pi,\pi]^{2})}=2\pi\|u\|_{\ell^{2}({\mathbb{Z}}^{2})}
. If

\hat{u}(\xi_{1},n_{2})=\sum\limits_{n_{1}}e^{-n_{1}\xi_{1}}u(n_{1},n_{2})=0,\text{~if~}n_{2}\notin[-L,L].

Thus we have

\begin{split}\int_{B_{\delta}(\xi^{*}_{\pm})}\frac{1}{\lambda_{0}^{2}s^{2}}|\hat{u}(\xi)|^{2}d\xi&\leq\frac{1}{\lambda_{0}^{2}}\int_{B_{\delta}(\xi^{*}_{\pm})}\left|\sum\limits_{n_{2}\in[-L,L]}e^{-in_{2}\xi_{2}}\hat{u}(\xi_{1},n_{2})\right|^{2}d\xi\\
&\leq\frac{1}{\lambda_{0}^{2}s^{2}}\int_{B_{\delta}(\xi^{*}_{\pm})}2L\sum\limits_{n_{2}\in[-L,L]}|\hat{u}(\xi_{1},n_{2})|^{2}d\xi\\

where we use the Plancherel’s formula on

In particular, taking

(1) We have
