Abstract
For a nonempty compact subset σ \sigma in the plane, the space A ​ C ​ ( σ ) AC(\sigma) is the closure of the space of complex polynomials in two real variables under a particular variation norm. In the classical setting, A ​ C ​ [ 0 , 1 ] AC[0,1] contains several other useful dense subsets, such as continuous piecewise linear functions, C 1 C^{1} functions and Lipschitz functions. In this paper we examine analogues of these results in this more general setting.



1
Introduction

A standard method in analysis is to first prove some fact for particularly well-behaved functions, and then extend the result to a more general class via a limiting argument. In order to do this one must of course know that the functions that you are interested in can be approximated by the simpler well-behaved ones.

The class of
AC(\sigma)
spaces, comprising ‘absolutely continuous’ functions on a nonempty compact subset
\sigma
of the plane, was introduced in
Ash
and
AD1
to provide a functional calculus model which unified the theories for the well-bounded operators of Smart and Ringrose
Sm
;
Rin1
;
Rin2
and the trigonometrically well-bounded operators of Berkson and Gillespie
BG2
, while overcoming some of the problems inherent in the definition of
AC
-operators
BG1
;
BDG
. Much is now known about the algebraic structure of these spaces
AS1
;
AS2
;
AS3
;
DA
;
DL
, but rather less has been recorded about the relationship between these spaces and other standard spaces of functions.

Throughout the paper we will freely swap between considering the plane as
\mathbb{C}
or
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. Subsets of
\mathbb{R}
may be considered as embedded in
\mathbb{C}
or as subsets of the first coordinate axis in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
as appropriate. Given a nonempty subset
\sigma\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2}
, the space
AC(\sigma)
is defined to be the closure of the complex polynomials
p(x,y)
in two real variables inside the space
BV(\sigma)
of functions of bounded variation (in the sense of
AD1
).

In the classical situation of
AC[0,1]
, it is useful to know that this space contains several standard spaces as dense subsets;
C^{1}[0,1]
, the Lipschitz functions, and the continuous piecewise linear functions. The aim of this paper is to examine to what extent the analogous facts are true in this more general setting.

In Section
2
we shall briefly give the main definitions of the spaces involved. A more detailed account can be found in
DLS
. Note that the definitions given here involve a significant simplification of the original ones used in
AD1
. A discussion of the equivalence of the different definitions can be found in the appendix to
AS3
.

In Section
3
we shall introduce the space
\mathrm{CTPP}(\sigma)
of continuous piecewise planar functions on
\sigma
, which forms a natural analogue of the continuous piecewise linear functions on the line.
An important fact which we shall use in this section is that being absolutely continuous is a ‘local property’ in the sense that a function is absolutely continuous on
\sigma
if and only if it is absolutely continuous on some neighbourhood of each point in
\sigma
.

In the final section we shall show that (suitably interpreted), we always have the inclusions
C^{1}(\sigma)\subseteq AC(\sigma)\subseteq C(\sigma)
. (These inclusions are of course in general proper, since that is the case when
\sigma=[0,1]
.)

2
Definitions

Suppose that
\sigma
is a nonempty subset of the plane and that
f:\sigma\to\mathbb{C}
. The main concept of interest to us will be the variation of
f
over
\sigma
. The definition looks reminiscent of the usual definition of variation for a function defined on an interval, but must now take account of the fact that there is no longer a natural ordering to the points of the set.

Suppose that
S=\bigl[{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0},{\boldsymbol{x}}_{1},\dots,{\boldsymbol{x}}_{n}\bigr]=[{\boldsymbol{x}}_{j}]_{j=0}^{n}
is a finite ordered list of points in the plane.
Note that the elements of such a list do not need to be distinct. For the moment assume that
S
contains at least two distinct points.
Let

\gamma_{S}
denote the piecewise linear curve joining the points of
S
in order.

Definition 1

Suppose that
\ell
is a line in the plane. We say that
\overline{\vphantom{\vbox to5.16663pt{}}{\boldsymbol{x}}_{j}\,{\boldsymbol{x}}_{j+1}}
, the line segment joining
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{j}
to
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{j+1}
, is a
crossing segment
of
S
on
\ell
if any one of the following hold:

1.

{\boldsymbol{x}}_{j}
and
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{j+1}
lie on (strictly) opposite sides of
\ell
;

2.

j=0
and
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{j}\in\ell
;

3.

j>0
,
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{j}\notin\ell
and
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{j+1}\in\ell
.

Let
\vf(S,\ell)
denote the number of crossing segments of
S
on
\ell
and define the
variation factor
of
S
to be

\vf(S)=\max_{\ell}\vf(S,\ell).

To cover the case where
S
contains a single point we set
\vf([{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}],\ell)=1
if
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}\in\ell
and zero otherwise, and set
\vf([{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}])=1
.
Note that
\vf(S)
is well-defined since
1\leq\vf(S)\leq n
. Informally,
\vf(S)
may be thought of as the maximum number of times any line crosses
\gamma_{S}
.

It is clear that the variation factor is invariant under rotations or translations of the set
S
, and indeed under any affine transformation of
S
.

We define the
curve variation of
f
on the list
S
to be

\cvar(f,S)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left\lvert f({\boldsymbol{x}}_{i})-f({\boldsymbol{x}}_{i-1})\right\rvert.

(1)

We include the case
S=\bigl[{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}\bigr]
by setting
\cvar(f,\bigl[{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}\bigr])=0
. The
two-dimensional variation
of a function
f:\sigma\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
is defined to be

\var(f,\sigma)=\sup_{S}\frac{\cvar(f,S)}{\vf(S)},

(2)

where the supremum is taken over all finite ordered lists of elements of
\sigma
.
The
variation norm
is

\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{{BV}(\sigma)}=\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{\infty}+\var(f,\sigma)

and this is used to define the set of functions of bounded variation on
\sigma
,

{BV}(\sigma)=\{f:\sigma\to\mathbb{C}\thinspace:\thinspace\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{{BV}(\sigma)}<\infty\}.

We record for later the most important properties of these spaces. Proofs can be found in
DLS
.

1.

BV(\sigma)
is a Banach algebra.

2.

If
\sigma=[a,b]
is an interval, then
\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{{BV}(\sigma)}
is just the usual variation norm.

3.

f\in BV(\sigma)
if and only if
\mathop{\mathrm{Re}}(f)
,
\mathop{\mathrm{Im}}(f)\in BV(\sigma)
.

4.

(Affine invariance) If
\phi
is an invertible affine transformation of the plane and
\sigma^{\prime}=\phi(\sigma)
, then
\Phi(f)=f\circ\phi^{-1}
is an isometric isomorphism from
BV(\sigma)
to
BV(\sigma^{\prime})
.

5.

Let
\sigma_{\mathbb{R}}=\{\mathop{\mathrm{Re}}z\thinspace:\thinspace z\in\sigma\}
and suppose that
g\in BV(\sigma_{\mathbb{R}})
. Then the function
f(z)=g(\mathop{\mathrm{Re}}z)
is in
BV(\sigma)
and
\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{{BV}(\sigma)}\leq\left\lVert g\right\rVert_{BV(\sigma_{\mathbb{R}})}
. By affine invariance, this result can be applied to any function which is constant on a family of parallel lines.

6.

BV(\sigma)
always contains the polynomials in two real variables (considering
\sigma
as a subset of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
).

It follows from (
6
) that the closure of the polynomials in two real variables is always a closed subalgebra of
BV(\sigma)
. This subalgebra is called the algebra of absolutely continuous functions on
\sigma
and is denoted
AC(\sigma)
. Since convergence in the
BV
norm implies uniform convergence, it is clear that every absolutely continuous function is in fact continuous. Again, in the case that
\sigma=[0,1]
, the space defined here is the same as the usual space
AC[0,1]
. (This is indeed true for any compact subset of
\mathbb{R}
. The spaces
AC(\sigma)
for
\sigma\subseteq\mathbb{R}
were introduced by Saks
S
, if not earlier.) Properties (
3
), (
4
) and (
5
) also hold for
AC(\sigma)
spaces.

It is easy to construct examples where a function is of bounded variation on each of two compact sets, but not of bounded variation on their union. The following result from
AD3
gives a sufficient condition on the subsets for the ‘joined function’ to be of bounded variation.

Theorem 2.1
(
(
AD3
, Theorem 3.1)
)

Suppose that
\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2}\subseteq\mathbb{C}
are nonempty compact sets which are disjoint except at their boundaries. Suppose that
\sigma=\sigma_{1}\cup\sigma_{2}
is convex and that
f:\sigma\to\mathbb{C}
. If
f|\sigma_{1}\in BV(\sigma_{1})
and
f|\sigma_{2}\in BV(\sigma_{2})
then
f\in BV(\sigma)
with

\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{{BV}(\sigma)}\leq\left\lVert f|\sigma_{1}\right\rVert_{BV(\sigma_{1})}+\left\lVert f|\sigma_{2}\right\rVert_{BV(\sigma_{2})}.

An important fact is the following result from
DLS
that says that being absolutely continuous is a local property. We shall say that a set
U
is a
compact neighbourhood
of a point
{\boldsymbol{x}}\in\sigma
(with respect to
\sigma
) if there exists an open neighbourhood
V\subseteq\mathbb{C}
of
x
such that
U=\sigma\cap\overline{V}
.

Theorem 2.2
(Patching Lemma)

Suppose that
f:\sigma\to\mathbb{C}
. Then
f\in{AC}(\sigma)
if and only if for every point
{\boldsymbol{x}}\in\sigma
there exists a compact neighbourhood
U_{\boldsymbol{x}}
of
{\boldsymbol{x}}
in
\sigma
such that
f|U_{\boldsymbol{x}}\in{AC}(U_{\boldsymbol{x}})
.

We shall denote the Lipschitz functions on
\sigma
by
{\mathrm{Lip}}(\sigma)
. This is a Banach algebra under the norm
\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{{\mathrm{Lip}}(\sigma)}=\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{\infty}+L_{\sigma}(f)
, where

L_{\sigma}(f)=\sup\left\{\frac{|f({\boldsymbol{x}})-f({\boldsymbol{x}}^{\prime})|}{|{\boldsymbol{x}}-{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\prime}|}\thinspace:\thinspace{\boldsymbol{x}}\neq{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\prime}\in\sigma\right\}.

(For the degenerate case of a singleton set, we set
L_{\sigma}(f)
to be zero for all
f
.)
In the following proposition
(
DLS
, Theorem 3.11)
,
C_{\sigma}
stands for the variation constant of
\sigma
, which is the variation of the identity function
\zeta({\boldsymbol{z}})={\boldsymbol{z}}
over
\sigma
(considered as a subset of
\mathbb{C}
). If
\sigma
lies inside a rectangle, then
C_{\sigma}
is at most the sum of the width plus the height of the rectangle.

Proposition 1

If
f\in{\mathrm{Lip}}(\sigma)
then
\var(f,\sigma)\leq C_{\sigma}L_{\sigma}(f)
and so

\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{{BV}(\sigma)}\leq\max\{1,C_{\sigma}\}\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{{\mathrm{Lip}}(\sigma)}
. Thus
{\mathrm{Lip}}(\sigma)\subseteq BV(\sigma)
.

Unfortunately, depending on
\sigma
, there may be Lipschitz functions which are not absolutely continuous. Examples are given in
AD1
and
DLS
.

Continuous piecewise linear functions on an interval are of course Lipschitz and hence absolutely continuous. The analogue for subsets of the plane are continuous piecewise planar functions. We shall denote the space of continuous piecewise planar functions on
\sigma
by
\mathrm{CTPP}(\sigma)
. The main part of this paper is to show that this space is always a dense subset of
AC(\sigma)
.

For
k=1,2,3,\dots
, let
C^{k}(\sigma)
denote the algebra of functions
f
for which there is an open neighbourhood
U
of
\sigma
and a
k
-times continuously differentiable function
F
on
U
such that
f=F|\sigma
.

In the classical case,
C^{1}[0,1]\subseteq AC(\sigma)
. We shall use the fact that all
C^{1}(\sigma)
functions can be approximated by functions in
\mathrm{CTPP}(\sigma)
to show that this result extends to arbitrary
\sigma
.

3
CTPP Functions

As before, we shall assume that
\sigma
is a nonempty compact subset of the plane.
We will say that a function
f:\sigma\to\mathbb{C}
is
planar
if there exist
a,b,c\in\mathbb{C}
with
f(x,y)=ax+by+c
.
If
a,b,c\in\mathbb{R}
, then (using property (
5
) of
BV(\sigma)
given in Section
2
)

\var(f,\sigma)=\max_{\sigma}f-\min_{\sigma}f=\max_{{\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{w}}\in\sigma}|f({\boldsymbol{x}})-f({\boldsymbol{w}})|.

(3)

This is no longer valid if
f
is complex-valued. For complex-valued planar functions, one can split
f
into its real and imaginary components, and find that

\displaystyle\max_{\sigma}|f|-\min_{\sigma}|f|\leq\var(f,\sigma)\leq\var\big(\text{Re}(f),\sigma\big)+\var\big(\text{Im}(f),\sigma\big).

Applying (
3
) to the real and imaginary parts shows that
f
is of bounded variation. Note that
f
is planar if and only if
\text{Re}(f)
and
\text{Im}(f)
are both planar.

We shall say that
P\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2}
is a polygon if it is a compact simply connected set whose boundary consists of a finite number of line segments. By the Two Ears Theorem, all polygons can be triangulated.

Suppose that
P
is a polygon in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
and let
\mathcal{A}=\{A_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
be a triangulation of
P
. That is, the triangles
A_{i}
are proper, closed, and have pairwise disjoint interiors, and
\cup_{i}A_{i}=P
. We say that
F:P\to\mathbb{C}
is
triangularly piecewise planar over

\mathcal{A}
if
F|A_{i}
is planar for each
A_{i}
. The set of all such functions will be denoted by
\mathrm{CTPP}(P,\mathcal{A})
, and the set of
continuous and triangularly piecewise planar functions
on
P
is defined to be

\displaystyle\mathrm{CTPP}(P)=\bigcup_{\mathcal{A}}\mathrm{CTPP}(P,\mathcal{A}).

For a given triangulation
\mathcal{A}
of a polygon
P
, note that
f\in\mathrm{CTPP}(P,\mathcal{A})
is necessarily continuous since if
A_{i}
and
A_{j}
are two triangles in
\mathcal{A}
, then
f|A_{i}
and
f|A_{j}
must agree on
A_{i}\cap A_{j}
.

If
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}
are two triangulations of
P
, we shall say that
\mathcal{A}
is a refinement of
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}
if every triangle in
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}
is the union of triangles in
\mathcal{A}
. Clearly in this case, if
f\in\mathrm{CTPP}(P,\mathcal{A}^{\prime})
then
f\in\mathrm{CTPP}(P,\mathcal{A})
. Any two triangulations will admit a common refinement.

Definition 2

A function
f:\sigma\to\mathbb{C}
is continuous and triangularly piecewise planar if there exists a polygon
P
containing
\sigma
, and
F\in\mathrm{CTPP}(P)
such that
F|\sigma=f
. The set of all such functions will be denoted by
\mathrm{CTPP}(\sigma)
.

If a suitable polygon
P
exists, one can if fact choose any polygon which contains
\sigma
as the basis for the triangulation.

Lemma 1

Suppose that
f\in\mathrm{CTPP}(\sigma)
. If
P_{0}
is a polygon containing
\sigma
, then there exists
F_{0}\in\mathrm{CTPP}(P_{0})
such that
f=F_{0}|\sigma
.

Proof

Suppose that
P_{0}
is a polygon that contains
\sigma
. By definition, there exists a polygon
P
, a triangulation
\mathcal{A}=\{A_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
of
P
, and
F\in\mathrm{CTPP}(P,\mathcal{A})
such that
F|\sigma=f
. If
P
contains
P_{0}
, then we can take
F_{0}=F|P_{0}
. Otherwise, let
R
be a rectangle whose interior contains both
P
and
P_{0}
. Then
R\backslash\text{int}(P)
can be triangulated, resulting in a triangulation of
R
given by
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}=\{A_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m}
. Let
P_{k}=\bigcup_{i=1}^{k}A_{i}
. The ‘ear-clipping’ triangulation algorithm allows us to do this in a way that for
n+1\leq k\leq m
, the triangle
A_{k}
has at least one side adjoining
P_{k-1}
, and at least one side disjoint from
P_{k-1}
(except at the vertices).

One may now extend
F
inductively, triangle by triangle. If
F
is already defined on
P_{k-1}
, then there exists a planar function on
A_{k}
which agrees with
F
on the intersection
A_{k}\cap P_{k-1}
. The triangulation
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}
generates a triangulation
\mathcal{A}_{0}
of
P_{0}
as follows. For each
i
, the set
A_{i}\cap P_{0}
is a union of polygons, and thus can be written as a union of triangles. So
F_{0}=F|P_{0}\in\mathrm{CTPP}(P_{0},\mathcal{A}_{0})
and
F_{0}|\sigma=f
.

Lemma 2

\textup{CTPP}(\sigma)
is a vector space.

Proof

Closure under scalar multiplication is clear. Suppose that
f,g\in\mathrm{CTPP}(\sigma)
, and that
P
is a polygon containing
\sigma
. By Lemma
1
, there exist
F,G\in\mathrm{CTPP}(P)
such that
F|\sigma=f
and
G|\sigma=g
. Clearly
F+G
is continuous and, by using a common triangulation for
F
and
G
, is planar on polygonal regions of
R
. Hence
F+G\in\mathrm{CTPP}(R)
as required.

3.1
Real
\mathrm{CTPP}
functions

Some estimates are easier for real-valued functions, so we shall start by dealing with this case first, and then later give the corresponding results for complex-valued functions. We shall denote the real-valued functions in
\mathrm{CTPP}(\sigma)
and
AC(\sigma)
by
\mathrm{CTPP}_{\mathbb{R}}(\sigma)
and
AC_{\mathbb{R}}(\sigma)
respectively.

Suppose that
\sigma
is a subset of a polygon
P
, that
f\in\mathrm{CTPP}_{\mathbb{R}}(\sigma)
, that
F\in\mathrm{CTPP}_{\mathbb{R}}(P)
is an extension of
f
to
P
. Since
f
is continuous, it attains a maximum value
M
and a minimum value
m
on
\sigma
. The function
{\tilde{F}}:P\to\mathbb{R}

{\tilde{F}}({\boldsymbol{x}})=\begin{cases}M,&\text{if $F({\boldsymbol{x}})>M$,}\\
m,&\text{if $F({\boldsymbol{x}})<m$,}\\
F({\boldsymbol{x}}),&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

is also an extension of
f
in
\mathrm{CTPP}_{\mathbb{R}}(P)
(with respect to a possibly refined triangulation). Thus a suitable extension of
f
to
P
can always be chosen to have the same range and the same supremum norm. For a continuous function
f:\sigma\to\mathbb{C}
, let
\Delta_{\sigma}(f)=\sup\{|f({\boldsymbol{x}})-f({\boldsymbol{x}}^{\prime})|\thinspace:\thinspace{\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\prime}\in\sigma\}
. Obviously
\Delta_{\sigma}(f)\leq 2\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{\infty}
.

We begin by showing that all
\mathrm{CTPP}_{\mathbb{R}}
functions are Lipschitz, and hence of bounded variation. For a triangle
A
, let
r_{A}
denote the inradius of
A
. Given a triangulation
\mathcal{A}=\{A_{j}\}
of a rectangle
R
, let
r(\mathcal{A})=\min_{j}r_{A_{j}}
.

Lemma 3

Suppose that
A
is a triangle and that
F:A\to\mathbb{R}
is planar. Then
L_{A}(F)
, the Lipschitz constant of
F
on
A
, satisfies

L_{A}(F)\leq\frac{\Delta_{A}(F)}{2r_{A}}.

Proof

Since
F
is planar,
\nabla F
is constant and, for distinct
{\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\prime}\in A
,

\frac{|F({\boldsymbol{x}})-F({\boldsymbol{x}}^{\prime})|}{\|{\boldsymbol{x}}-{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\prime}\|}=\left|\nabla F\cdot\left(\frac{{\boldsymbol{x}}-{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\prime}}{\|{\boldsymbol{x}}-{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\prime}\|}\right)\right|.

It follows that there is a unit vector
{\boldsymbol{u}}
so that
L_{A}(F)=|\nabla F\cdot{\boldsymbol{u}}|
. Choose
{\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\prime}
on the incircle of
A
so that
{\boldsymbol{x}}-{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\prime}=2r_{A}{\boldsymbol{u}}
. Then

L_{A}(F)=\frac{1}{2r_{A}}\left|\nabla F\cdot({\boldsymbol{x}}-{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\prime})\right|=\frac{1}{2r_{A}}|F({\boldsymbol{x}})-F({\boldsymbol{x}}^{\prime})|\leq\frac{\Delta_{A}(F)}{2r_{A}}.

Note that Lemma
3
can be improved slightly, but clearly the bound must depend on the smallest width of the triangle.

Theorem 3.1

\mathrm{CTPP}_{\mathbb{R}}(\sigma)\subseteq{\mathrm{Lip}}(\sigma)\subseteq BV(\sigma)
.

Proof

Let
R
be a rectangle that contains
\sigma
and suppose that
f\in\mathrm{CTPP}_{\mathbb{R}}(\sigma)
. By Lemma
1
, there exists a triangulation
\mathcal{A}=\{A_{j}\}_{j=1}^{n}
of
R
and
F\in\mathrm{CTPP}_{\mathbb{R}}(R,\mathcal{A})
such that
F|\sigma=f
. Clearly
L_{R}(F)\geq\max_{j}L_{A_{j}}(F)
.

Suppose
{\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\prime}\in R
are distinct. The line segment
\overline{\vphantom{\vbox to5.16663pt{}}{\boldsymbol{x}}\,{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\prime}}
joining
{\boldsymbol{x}}
and
{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\prime}
can be written as a union of finitely many subsegments, denoted
\overline{\vphantom{\vbox to5.16663pt{}}{\boldsymbol{x}}_{j-1}\,{\boldsymbol{x}}_{j}}
(with
j=1,\dots,m
), with each subsegment entirely contained in a single triangle
A_{j}
in
\mathcal{A}
. Then, using Lemma
3
,

\displaystyle|F({\boldsymbol{x}})-F({\boldsymbol{x}}^{\prime})|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j=1}^{m}|F({\boldsymbol{x}}_{j})-F({\boldsymbol{x}}_{j-1})|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j=1}^{m}L_{A_{j}}(F)\left\lVert{\boldsymbol{x}}_{j}-{\boldsymbol{x}}_{j-1}\right\rVert

\displaystyle\leq\max_{j}L_{A_{j}}(F)\sum_{j=1}^{m}\left\lVert{\boldsymbol{x}}_{j}-{\boldsymbol{x}}_{j-1}\right\rVert

\displaystyle=\max_{j}L_{A_{j}}(F)\left\lVert{\boldsymbol{x}}-{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\prime}\right\rVert

and so
L_{\sigma}(f)\leq L_{R}(F)=\max_{j}L_{A_{j}}(F)
. Thus,
\mathrm{CTPP}_{\mathbb{R}}(\sigma)\subseteq{\mathrm{Lip}}(\sigma)
. The second inclusion is just Proposition
1
.

Note that the Lipschitz constant of
f
may be strictly smaller than the constant for an extension
F
. It is not always possible to choose an extension
F
with
L_{\sigma}(f)=L_{R}(F)
.

Example 1

Fix
\alpha>0
, let
\sigma=\{(x,y)\thinspace:\thinspace 0\leq x\leq\alpha,\ y=x^{2}\}
, and let
f\in\mathrm{CTPP}_{\mathbb{R}}(\sigma)
be
f(x,y)=y
. Clearly any
\mathrm{CTPP}_{\mathbb{R}}
extension
F
to a polygon
P
must have
L_{P}(F)\geq 1
. On the other hand an elementary calculation shows that
L_{\sigma}(f)=2\alpha/\sqrt{1+4\alpha^{2}}
, which, for small
\alpha
, will be much smaller than
L_{P}(F)
.

Corollary 1

Suppose that
f\in\mathrm{CTPP}_{\mathbb{R}}(\sigma)
has an extension
F\in\mathrm{CTPP}_{\mathbb{R}}(R,\mathcal{A})
to a rectangle
R
containing
\sigma
, and assume that
\Delta_{R}(F)=\Delta_{\sigma}(f)
. Then

\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{{BV}(\sigma)}\leq\left(1+\frac{\ell+h}{r(\mathcal{A})}\right)\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{\infty}

where
\ell
and
h
are the side lengths of
R
.

Proof

Using Proposition
1
and Lemma
3

\displaystyle\var(f,\sigma)\leq\var(F,R)

\displaystyle\leq C_{R}L_{R}(F)

\displaystyle\leq(\ell+h)\max_{j}\frac{\Delta_{A_{j}}(F)}{2r_{A_{j}}}

\displaystyle\leq(\ell+h)\left\lVert F\right\rVert_{\infty}\max_{j}\frac{1}{r_{A_{j}}}

\displaystyle=\frac{(\ell+h)}{r(\mathcal{A})}\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{\infty}.

We also note that the bound that appears in Corollary
1
may be far from sharp.

Example 2

Let
\alpha
,
f
and
\sigma
be as in Example
1
.
Then
\var(f,\sigma)=\alpha^{2}
and so
\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{BV(\sigma)}=2\alpha^{2}
. Let
R
be the rectangle with corners at

4
\mathrm{CTPP}_{\mathbb{R}}(\sigma)
is contained in

Our next step is to show that every function in
\mathrm{CTPP}_{\mathbb{R}}(\sigma)
is not just of bounded variation, but is absolutely continuous.
Suppose that
f\in\mathrm{CTPP}_{\mathbb{R}}(\sigma)
and that

is a piecewise planar extension of

to some rectangle

containing
\sigma
. If

is absolutely continuous, then so is its restriction

functions defined on a rectangle.

Suppose then that

is a rectangle in the plane and that
f\in\mathrm{CTPP}_{\mathbb{R}}(R)
with respect to a fixed triangulation
\mathcal{A}
of

. Our aim is to show that each point

admits a compact neighbourhood

on which

is absolutely continuous and then to use the Patching Lemma (Theorem
2.2
).

Note that since we can choose
R
as large as we like, it is sufficient to do this for each interior point of
R
as this will ensure that
f
is absolutely continuous on any compact subset of the interior of

. Doing this avoids some special cases in the argument below.

Definition 3

We will say that an interior point

is

•

for

if it lies in exactly one triangle of
\mathcal{A}

•

an

for

if it lies in exactly two triangles of
\mathcal{A}
;

•

a

for

if it lies in three or more triangles of
\mathcal{A}
.

Note that these three cases are exhaustive and mutually exclusive, and that the classification of

depends on the triangulation. Dealing with the first class of points is straightforward.

Suppose first that
{\boldsymbol{x}}
is a planar point lying in the triangle

. In this case
{\boldsymbol{x}}
is an interior point of

. As

is planar on

.

Lemma 4

Suppose that
{\boldsymbol{x}}
is an edge point for

Proof

By affine invariance, we may assume that
{\boldsymbol{x}}=(0,0)
. Moreover, we may assume that the two triangles that

. Thus, there exists some small square

centred at the origin such that if

a_{2}x+b_{2}y+c_{2}&\text{if $x\leq 0$}.\end{cases}

Since

is continuous,

and

. Let

It follows from Property (
