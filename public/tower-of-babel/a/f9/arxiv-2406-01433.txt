Travelling waves for Maxwell’s equations in nonlinear and symmetric media
Jarosław Mederski, Jacopo Schino

Abstract
. We look for travelling wave fields E ⁡ ( x , y , z , t ) = U ⁡ ( x , y ) ​ cos ⁡ ( k ​ z + ω ​ t ) + U ~ ​ ( x , y ) ​ sin ⁡ ( k ​ z + ω ​ t ) , ( x , y , z ) ∈ ℝ 3 , t ∈ ℝ , E(x,y,z,t)=U(x,y)\cos(kz+\omega t)+\widetilde{U}(x,y)\sin(kz+\omega t),\quad(x,y,z)\in\mathbb{R}^{3},\,t\in\mathbb{R}, satisfying Maxwell’s equations in a nonlinear and cylindrically symmetric medium. We obtain a sequence of solutions with diverging energy consisting of transverse magnetic field modes. In addition, we consider a general nonlinearity, controlled by an N -function.

Introduction

We are interested in self-trapped beams of light propagating in a nonlinear dielectric and cylindrically symmetric medium by means of solutions to the system of Maxwell’s equations
coupled with a nonlinear constitutive relation between the electric field and the electric
displacement field. More precisely, we are looking for travelling electric fields of the form

(1.1)

E(x,y,z,t)=U(x,y)\cos(kz+\omega t)+\widetilde{U}(x,y)\sin(kz+\omega t),

where
U,\widetilde{U}\colon\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
are the profiles of the travelling waves,
\omega>0
is the temporal frequency and
k\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
the spatial wave number in the direction of propagation.

In modelling optical nonlinear materials
[
18
]
, it is natural to seek solutions having cylindrical symmetry, and in physics literature one considers usually transverse electric field modes (
TE modes
), or tranverse magnetic field modes (
TM modes
)
[
9
]
. If the electric field is perpendicular to the direction of propagation, i.e.

(1.2)

U(x,y)=\frac{\beta(r)}{r}\begin{pmatrix}-y\\
x\\
0\end{pmatrix},\quad(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{2},\;r=|(x,y)|,

then
E=U(x,y)\cos(kz+\omega t)
is the cylindrically symmetric TE mode (
\widetilde{U}=0
), and the system of Maxwell’s equations can be reduced to the study of a nonlinear second order differential equation with boundary conditions at infinity, see Stuart
[
20
,
21
]
and the references therein. If
the magnetic field
B
is transverse to the direction of propagation, and takes the form

(1.3)

B=\frac{\beta(r)}{r}\begin{pmatrix}-y\\
x\\
0\end{pmatrix}\cos(kz+\omega t),

then the equations governing these transverse magnetic field modes are more
complicated than those for TE modes because of the form of the constitutive relation in
a nonlinear optical medium. However, as observed in
[
24
,
9
]
, when studying TM-modes, the electric field takes the form (
1.1
) with two profiles
U
and
\widetilde{U}
, with possibly
U\neq 0
and
\widetilde{U}\neq 0
.

In order to find the exact propagation of the electromagnetic field in a nonlinear medium,
we consider a nonlinear polarization of the form

\chi(\langle|E|^{2}\rangle)E,

where
\chi
is the scalar nonlinear susceptibility, which depends only on the time averaged intensity of
E
over one period
T=2\pi/\omega
, that is,
\langle|E(x,y,z)|^{2}\rangle=\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}|E(x,y,z,t)|^{2}\,dt
, see
[
19
]
.
According to Maxwell’s equations, one has to
solve the
nonlinear electromagnetic wave equation

(1.4)

\nabla\times\Big(\frac{1}{\mu}\nabla\times E\Big)+\partial_{tt}\left(\varepsilon E+\chi(\langle|E|^{2}\rangle)E\right)=0\quad\hbox{for }(x,y,z,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{3}\times\mathbb{R},

where
\varepsilon
is the permittivity of the medium,
\mu
is the magnetic permeability. For simplicity, we assume that
\mu=1
; then, in absence of charges and currents, Faraday’s law
\nabla\times E+\partial_{t}B=0
determines the magnetic induction
B
, and Ampére’s law
\nabla\times B=\partial_{t}D
together with
\mathop{\mathrm{div}\,}D=0
and
\mathop{\mathrm{div}\,}B=0
shows that Maxwell’s equations are satisfied, where we consider the linear magnetic material law
B=\mu H=H
and the nonlinear electric material law
D=\varepsilon(x,y)E+\chi(\langle|E|^{2}\rangle)E
, see
[
20
,
21
,
1
,
14
]
. Altogether, we find the
exact propagation
of the electromagnetic field in a nonlinear medium according to Maxwell’s equations. Recall that in
cylindrically symmetric
media,
\varepsilon
,
U
,
\widetilde{U}
depend only on
r=|(x,y)|
.

Problem (
1.4
) has been studied in cylindrically symmetric media in a series of previous works
[
22
,
20
,
21
,
24
,
23
,
25
,
26
]
, where TE and TM modes have been obtained for asymptotically constant susceptibility
\chi
, i.e. in the so-called
saturation effect
. In this work, however, we want to treat the probably most common type of nonlinearity in the physics and engineering literature, i.e. the
Kerr nonlinearity

\chi(\langle|E^{2}|\rangle)E=\chi^{(3)}\langle|E|^{2}\rangle E,\quad\chi^{(3)}>0.

Up to our knowledge, the first analytical work in this context is due to McLeod, Stuart and Troy
[
11
]
, where TE modes were obtained. Namely, they showed that
\beta(r)
must satisfy

(1.5)

\beta^{\prime\prime}+\frac{1}{r}\beta-\frac{1}{r^{2}}\beta+\beta^{3}-\beta=0,\quad\beta(0)=\beta(+\infty)=0

(see also Pohl
[
16
]
), where
\beta
is as in (
1.3
), and for a given integer
n\geq 1
, (
1.5
) has at least one solutions with precisely
n
zeros in
(0,+\infty)
whose derivative has precisely
n+1
zeros. Then, up to some constant
\varepsilon
and
\chi^{(3)}
, this leads to infinitely many different TE modes
E=U(r)\cos(kz+\omega t)
with (
1.2
). Observe that a TE mode of this form is divergence free and
\widetilde{U}=0
.

The aim of this work is to find infinitely many fields
E
of the form (
1.1
) which need not be divergence free, hence different from TE modes. Moreover, we admit a permittivity depending on
r
,
U
need not be of the form (
1.2
), and the ODE methods do not seem to be applicable. Our approach is variational, and
recall that the first author and Reichel in
[
14
]
have recently observed that the search for travelling waves of the form (
1.1
) satisfying (
1.4
) leads to the following nonlinear elliptic problem

(1.6)

L\begin{pmatrix}U\\
\widetilde{U}\end{pmatrix}-\omega^{2}\epsilon(x,y)\begin{pmatrix}U\\
\widetilde{U}\end{pmatrix}=\omega^{2}\chi\left(\frac{1}{2}(|U|^{2}+|\widetilde{U}|^{2})\right)\begin{pmatrix}U\\
\widetilde{U}\end{pmatrix},

where

L=\begin{pmatrix}-\partial_{yy}+k^{2}&\partial_{xy}&0&0&0&k\partial_{x}\\
\partial_{xy}&-\partial_{xx}+k^{2}&0&0&0&k\partial_{y}\\
0&0&-\partial_{xx}-\partial_{yy}&k\partial_{x}&k\partial_{y}&0\\
0&0&-k\partial_{x}&-\partial_{yy}+k^{2}&\partial_{xy}&0\\
0&0&-k\partial_{y}&\partial_{xy}&-\partial_{xx}+k^{2}&0\\
-k\partial_{x}&-k\partial_{y}&0&0&0&-\partial_{xx}-\partial_{yy}\end{pmatrix}

is the second-order differential operator
L\colon\dom(L)\subset L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})^{6}\to L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})^{6}
, which is elliptic and self-adjoint,
and

\dom(L):=\left\{\begin{pmatrix}U\\
\widetilde{U}\end{pmatrix}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})^{6}:L\begin{pmatrix}U\\
\widetilde{U}\end{pmatrix}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})^{6}\right\},

where
L((U,\widetilde{U})^{T})
is defined in the sense of distributions. In view of
[
14
, Theorem 2.3]
, the spectrum
\sigma(L)
equals
\{0\}\cup[k^{2},\infty)
, where
0
is an eigenvalue of infinite multiplicity and
[k^{2},\infty)
consists of absolutely continuous spectrum. In
[
14
]
, periodic media have been considered, but we borrow some ideas of the functional setting from this work to investigate the cylindrically symmetric situation.

We look for solutions
u\colon\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}^{6}
to the following slight generalization of (
1.6
) given by

(1.7)

Lu-V(x)u=f(u)\quad\hbox{for }x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}

involving the operator
L
, where we assume
f(u)=F^{\prime}(u)
. From now on, the spacial variable in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
will be denoted by

instead of
(x,y)
.
Observe that, if

(1.8)

V(x)=\omega^{2}\varepsilon(x),\quad F(u)=\omega^{2}\chi\Big(\frac{1}{2}|u|^{2}\Big),\quad u\in\mathbb{R}^{6},

then (
1.7
) leads to (
1.6
) and we obtain the
exact
propagation of the travelling electromagnetic waves
(E,B)
, where
E
is given by (
1.1
) and
B
is provided by Faraday’s law and a subsequent time integration.

By a
(weak) solution
to (
1.7

(1.9)

J(u):=\frac{1}{2}b_{L}(u,u)-\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}V(x)|u|^{2}\,dx-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}F(u)\,dx

defined on a Banach space
X\subset L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})^{6}
given later, where
p>2
. Here,

is the bilinear form associated with

b_{L}(u,\varphi)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\langle Lu,\varphi\rangle\,dx
for all
u\in\dom(L)
and all
\varphi\in{\mathcal{C}}_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2})^{6}
.

Now, for
\alpha,\widetilde{\alpha}\in{\mathcal{C}}_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
and for
\beta,\widetilde{\beta}\in{\mathcal{C}}_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2})^{3}

let us denote

\begin{array}[]{ll}\overset{\circ}{\nabla}\begin{pmatrix}\alpha\\
\widetilde{\alpha}\end{pmatrix}:=\begin{pmatrix}\partial_{x}\alpha\\
\partial_{y}\alpha\\
k\widetilde{\alpha}\\
\partial_{x}\widetilde{\alpha}\\
\partial_{y}\widetilde{\alpha}\\
-k\alpha\end{pmatrix}\colon\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}^{6},\qquad\overset{\circ}{\nabla}\times\begin{pmatrix}\beta\\
\widetilde{\beta}\end{pmatrix}:=\begin{pmatrix}\partial_{y}\beta_{3}-k\widetilde{\beta}_{2}\\

\partial_{x}\beta_{2}-\partial_{y}\beta_{1}\\
\partial_{y}\widetilde{\beta}_{3}+k\beta_{2}\\
-k\beta_{1}-\partial_{x}\widetilde{\beta}_{3}\\
\partial_{x}\widetilde{\beta}_{2}-\partial_{y}\widetilde{\beta}_{1}\end{pmatrix}\colon\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}^{6},\end{array}

and observe that

,

, and thus

\widetilde{\alpha}\end{pmatrix}\in\ker(L)
. Moreover,

2.
Variational setting

We introduce the following notation. If
u=\begin{pmatrix}U\\
{\tilde{U}}\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{6}
, then
|u|:=\big(|U|^{2}+|{\tilde{U}}|^{2}\big)^{1/2}
. In the sequel,
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{2}
denotes the inner product in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})^{6}
and
|\cdot|_{q}
denotes the usual
L^{q}
-norm for
q\in[1,+\infty]
. Furthermore, we denote by
C
a generic positive constant which may vary from one inequality to the next. We always assume that
k\neq 0
. By
\lesssim
we denote the inequality
\leq
up to a positive multiplicative constant.

Now, let us recall from
[
17
]
some basic facts about
N
-functions and Orlicz spaces. A function
\Phi\colon\mathbb{R}\to[0,\infty)
is called an
N
-function if and only if it is even, convex, and satisfies

\lim_{t\to 0}\frac{\Phi(t)}{t}=0,\quad\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{\Phi(t)}{t}=\infty,\quad\text{and}\quad\Phi(t)>0\text{ for all }t>0.

We can associate with it a second function
\Psi\colon\mathbb{R}\to[0,\infty)
defined as

\Psi(t):=\sup\left\{s|t|-\Phi(s):s\geq 0\right\},

which is again an
N
-function and is called the
complementary function
of
\Phi
. It is easy to check that
\Phi
is the complementary function of
\Psi
.

We say that
\Phi
satisfies the
\Delta_{2}
condition globally if and only if there exists
K>0
such that

\Phi(2t)\leq K\Phi(t)\quad\text{for all }t\in\mathbb{R}.

We say it satisfies the
\nabla_{2}
condition globally if and only if there exists
K^{\prime}>1
such that

\Phi(K^{\prime}t)\geq 2K^{\prime}\Phi(t)\quad\text{for all }t\in\mathbb{R}.

Recalling that convex functions are differentiable almost everywhere, we give the following characterization for the
\Delta_{2}
and
\nabla_{2}
conditions (see
[
17
, Theorem II.III.3]
).

Lemma 2.1
.

The following properties are equivalent.

•

\Phi
satisfies the
\Delta_{2}
condition globally.

•

There exists
\kappa>1
such that
t\Phi^{\prime}(t)\leq\kappa\Phi(t)
for a.e.
t\in\mathbb{R}
.

•

There exists
\kappa^{\prime}>1
such that
t\Psi^{\prime}(t)\geq\kappa^{\prime}\Psi(t)
for a.e.
t\in\mathbb{R}
.

•

\Psi
satisfies the
\nabla_{2}
condition globally.

The set

L^{\Phi}:=\left\{u\colon\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}^{6}:\Phi\circ|u|\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})\right\}

is a vector space if
\Phi
satisfies the
\Delta_{2}
condition globally. In this case, it is called an Orlicz space. It becomes a Banach space if endowed with the norm

|u|_{\Phi}:=\inf\left\{\alpha>0:\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\Phi\left(\frac{|u|}{\alpha}\right)\,dx\leq 1\right\},

and it is reflexive if
\Phi
satisfies the
\Delta_{2}
and
\nabla_{2}
conditions globally, with
L^{\Psi}
begin its dual space (see
[
17
, Theorem IV.I.10 and Corollary IV.II.9]
). We can define likewise the space of scalar functions

L^{\Phi}(\mathbb{R}^{2}):=\left\{u\colon\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}:\Phi\circ u\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})\right\}

and the corresponding norm, still denoted
|\cdot|_{\Phi}
. Then,
L^{\Phi}=L^{\Phi}(\mathbb{R}^{2})^{6}
and their norms are equivalent (cf. e.g.
[
15
, Lemma 2.1]
).

An important property is the following one.

Lemma 2.2
.

If
\Phi
satisfies the
\Delta_{2}
condition globally, then there exists
C>0
such that

\Psi\bigl(\Phi^{\prime}(t)\bigr)\leq C\Phi(t)

for every
t\in\mathbb{R}
. In particular, if
u\in L^{\Phi}
, then
\Phi^{\prime}\circ|u|\in L^{\Psi}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
.

Proof.

From Lemma
2.1
and
[
17
, Theorem I.III.3]
, there holds

\Psi\bigl(\Phi^{\prime}(t)\bigr)=t\Phi^{\prime}(t)-\Phi(t)\leq(\kappa-1)\Phi(t).\qed

We conclude with the following properties about convergence and boundedness.

Lemma 2.3
.

Consider
(u_{n})\subset L^{\Phi}
.

•

Let
u\in L^{\Phi}
. If
\lim_{n}|u_{n}-u|_{\Phi}=0
, then
\lim_{n}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\Phi(u_{n}-u)\,dx=0
. The opposite implication holds if
\Phi
satisfies the
\Delta_{2}
condition globally.

•

Assume that
\Phi
satisfies the
\Delta_{2}
condition globally. Then,
(u_{n})
is bounded if and only if
\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\Phi(|u_{n}|)\,dx\right)
is bounded.

Let us introduce the space

Next, again from the

2.1
,
\Phi
has at most a power-type behaviour at the origin. Taking (N2) into account, there exists
q>2
such that

\lim_{t\to 0}\frac{|\Phi^{\prime}(t)|}{|t|^{q-1}}=\infty.

Then, using also (N3) and since we can assume
q=p
, we have
|t|^{p}\lesssim\Phi(t)
for every
t\in[-1,1]
and
|t|^{2}\lesssim\Phi(t)
for every
t\in(-\infty,-1)\cup(1,\infty)
, thus

L^{\Phi}\subset L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})^{6}+L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{2})^{6}.

Exploiting Lemma
2.1
once again, we infer

L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})^{6}\cap L^{p^{\prime}}(\mathbb{R}^{2})^{6}\subset L^{\Psi},

where
\frac{1}{p}+\frac{1}{p^{\prime}}=1
. This will be used in the proof of Lemma
2.4
below.

Recall that the second-order differential operator
L\colon\dom(L)\subset L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})^{6}\to L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})^{6}
is elliptic and self-adjoint on the domain
\dom(L)=\{u\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})^{6}:Lu\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})^{6}\}
. Its associated bilinear form
b_{L}:\dom(b_{L})\times\dom(b_{L})\to\mathbb{R}
is given by

b_{L}(u,v)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\overset{\circ}{\nabla}\times u\cdot\overset{\circ}{\nabla}\times v\,dx

with
\dom(b_{L})=\{u\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})^{6}:\overset{\circ}{\nabla}\times u\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})^{6}\}
.
Recall also that the operator
L\colon\dom(L)\subset L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})^{6}\to L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})^{6}
has spectrum
\sigma(L)=\{0\}\cup[k^{2},\infty)
, where
0
is an eigenvalue of infinite multiplicity and
[k^{2},\infty)
consists of absolutely continuous spectrum. Finally,

b_{L}(v,v)=\|v\|^{2},\quad\hbox{for }v\in{\mathcal{V}}.

Theorem 2.4
.

The spaces
{\mathcal{V}}
and
{\mathcal{W}}
are closed subspaces of
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})^{6}
and orthogonal with respect to
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{2}
, and
X={\mathcal{V}}\oplus{\mathcal{W}}
is the completion of
{\mathcal{C}}_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2})^{6}
with respect to the norm
\|\cdot\|
. Moreover,
\mathrm{cl\,}_{L^{\Phi}}{\mathcal{V}}\cap\mathrm{cl\,}_{L^{\Phi}}{\mathcal{W}}=\{0\}
.

Proof.

In order to get the Helmholtz decomposition
we argue in a similar way as in
[
14
, Theorem 2.1]
, and for the reader’s convenience we sketch this part, which will be useful to prove also the latter statement that
\mathrm{cl\,}_{L^{\Phi}}{\mathcal{V}}\cap\mathrm{cl\,}_{L^{\Phi}}{\mathcal{W}}=\{0\}
.

Note that
{\mathcal{V}}
and
{\mathcal{W}}
are closed subspaces of
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})^{6}
and orthogonal with respect to
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{2}
. Let
\varphi=\begin{pmatrix}\phi\\
\widetilde{\phi}\end{pmatrix}\in{\mathcal{C}}_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2})^{6}
and let
\alpha,\widetilde{\alpha}\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})\cap{\mathcal{C}}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
be the unique solutions to

-\Delta\alpha+k^{2}\alpha=-\big(\partial_{x_{1}}\phi_{1}+\partial_{x_{2}}\phi_{2}+k\widetilde{\phi}_{3}\big)

and

-\Delta\widetilde{\alpha}+k^{2}\widetilde{\alpha}=-\big(\partial_{x_{1}}\widetilde{\phi}_{1}+\partial_{x_{2}}\widetilde{\phi}_{2}-k\phi_{3}\big)

respectively. Since
\alpha
is the Bessel potential of the
{\mathcal{C}}_{0}^{\infty}
-function
-(\partial_{x_{1}}\phi_{1}+\partial_{x_{2}}\phi_{2}+k\widetilde{\phi}_{3})
we find that
\alpha\in W^{l,s}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
for every
l\in\mathbb{N}
and
1<s<\infty
, cf.
[
28
]
, and similarly for
\widetilde{\alpha}
. Moreover, we find
\alpha_{n},\widetilde{\alpha}_{n}\in{\mathcal{C}}_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
such that
\alpha_{n}\to\alpha
and
\widetilde{\alpha}_{n}\to\widetilde{\alpha}
in
W^{l,s}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
for any
l\in\mathbb{N}
and
1<s<\infty
. In particular, this implies

\varphi_{\mathcal{W}}:=\overset{\circ}{\nabla}\begin{pmatrix}\alpha\\
\widetilde{\alpha}\end{pmatrix}=\lim_{n\to\infty}\overset{\circ}{\nabla}\begin{pmatrix}\alpha_{n}\\
\widetilde{\alpha}_{n}\end{pmatrix}\in{\mathcal{W}},

and
\varphi_{\mathcal{W}}\in W^{l,s}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
for all
l\in\mathbb{N}
and
1<s<\infty
. In addition,
{\mathcal{W}}
is clearly contained in the completion of
{\mathcal{C}}_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2})^{6}
with respect to the norm
\|\cdot\|
. Since, in particular,
\varphi_{\mathcal{W}}\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})^{6}
, we obtain by integration by parts

\displaystyle\Big\langle\varphi,\overset{\circ}{\nabla}\begin{pmatrix}\beta\\
\widetilde{\beta}\end{pmatrix}\Big\rangle_{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}(\partial_{x_{1}}\phi_{1}+\partial_{x_{2}}\phi_{2}+k\widetilde{\phi}_{3})\beta+(\partial_{x_{1}}\widetilde{\phi}_{1}+\partial_{x_{2}}\widetilde{\phi}_{2}-k\phi_{3})\widetilde{\beta}\,dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}(-\Delta\alpha+k^{2}\alpha)\beta+(-\Delta\widetilde{\alpha}+k^{2}\widetilde{\alpha})\widetilde{\beta}\,dx=\Big\langle\varphi_{\mathcal{W}},\overset{\circ}{\nabla}\begin{pmatrix}\beta\\
\widetilde{\beta}\end{pmatrix}\Big\rangle_{2}

for every
\beta,\widetilde{\beta}\in{\mathcal{C}}_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
. Therefore,
\varphi_{\mathcal{V}}:=\varphi-\varphi_{\mathcal{W}}\in{\mathcal{V}}
, and
we obtain the following Helmholtz-type decomposition

\varphi=\varphi_{\mathcal{V}}+\varphi_{\mathcal{W}}\quad\hbox{with }\varphi_{\mathcal{V}}\in{\mathcal{V}}\hbox{ and }\varphi_{\mathcal{W}}\in{\mathcal{W}}.

Moreover, we have shown that
\varphi\in X
. It remains to show that also
{\mathcal{V}}
is contained in the completion of
{\mathcal{C}}_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2})^{6}
with respect to
\|\cdot\|
. To see this, let
v\in{\mathcal{V}}
and take
(\varphi_{n})\subset{\mathcal{C}}_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2})^{6}

such that
\varphi_{n}\to v
in
H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})^{6}
. Let us decompose
\varphi_{n}=\varphi_{\mathcal{V}}^{n}+\varphi_{\mathcal{W}}^{n}\in{\mathcal{V}}\oplus{\mathcal{W}}
and let
\mathrm{cl\,}_{L^{2}\cap L^{\Phi}}{\mathcal{V}}
denote the closure of
{\mathcal{V}}
in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})^{6}\cap L^{\Phi}
. Observe that
\big(\mathrm{cl\,}_{L^{2}\cap L^{\Phi}}{\mathcal{V}}\big)\cap{\mathcal{W}}=\{0\}
, so there is a continuous
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})^{6}\cap L^{\Phi}
-projection of
\big(\mathrm{cl\,}_{L^{2}\cap L^{\Phi}}{\mathcal{V}}\big)\oplus{\mathcal{W}}
onto
{\mathcal{W}}
. Since
\varphi_{n}\to v
in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})^{6}\cap L^{\Phi}
, we infer that
\varphi_{\mathcal{W}}^{n}\to 0
in
{\mathcal{W}}
. Similarly, arguing with the closure of
{\mathcal{W}}
in
H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})^{6}
we get
\varphi_{\mathcal{V}}^{n}\to v
in
{\mathcal{V}}
. Therefore

\|v-\varphi_{n}\|^{2}=\|v-\varphi_{\mathcal{V}}^{n}\|^{2}+\|\varphi^{n}_{\mathcal{W}}\|^{2}\to 0

as
n\to\infty
, and we conclude that
{\mathcal{V}}\oplus{\mathcal{W}}
is the completion of
{\mathcal{C}}_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2})^{6}
with respect to the norm
\|\cdot\|
.

Now, let
u\in\mathrm{cl\,}_{L^{\Phi}}{\mathcal{V}}\cap\mathrm{cl\,}_{L^{\Phi}}{\mathcal{W}}
and
\varphi=\varphi_{\mathcal{V}}+\varphi_{\mathcal{W}}
as above.
Consider
(\varphi_{n})\subset{\mathcal{V}}
and
(\psi_{n})\subset{\mathcal{C}}_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
such that
\lim_{n}|u-\varphi_{n}|_{\Phi}=\lim_{n}|u-\overset{\circ}{\nabla}\psi_{n}|_{\Phi}=0
as
n\to\infty
.
Observe that
\varphi_{\mathcal{V}},\varphi_{\mathcal{W}}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})^{6}\cap L^{p^{\prime}}(\mathbb{R}^{2})^{6}\subset L^{\Psi}
, whence

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}u\varphi_{\mathcal{W}}\,dx

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\varphi_{n}\overset{\circ}{\nabla}\varphi\,dx=0,

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}u\varphi_{\mathcal{V}}\,dx

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\overset{\circ}{\nabla}\psi_{n}\varphi_{\mathcal{V}}\,dx=0.

Therefore,
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}u\varphi\,dx=0
for every
\varphi\in{\mathcal{C}}_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2})^{6}
, and
u=0
. ∎

3.
Analysis of Cerami sequences

Note that (F1) and (F2) imply that for any
\varepsilon>0
there is
C_{\varepsilon}>0
such that

(3.1)

F(u)\leq\varepsilon|u|^{2}+C_{\varepsilon}\Phi(u)\quad\text{for every }u\in\mathbb{R}^{6}.

For
v\in{\mathcal{V}}
, let
w(v)
denote the unique minimizer of

w\mapsto\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}V(x)|v+w|^{2}\,dx+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}F(v+w)\,dx

in
{\mathcal{W}}\subset L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})^{6}\cap L^{\Phi}
. Let
\widetilde{J}\colon{\mathcal{V}}\to\mathbb{R}
be given by

\widetilde{J}(v):=J(v+w(v))=\frac{1}{2}\big(|\nabla v|^{2}_{2}+k^{2}|v|^{2}_{2}\big)-\Big(\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}V(x)|v+w(v)|^{2}\,dx+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}F(v+w(v))\,dx\Big).

In a way similar to
[
6
, Proof of Theorem 4.4]
we show that
\widetilde{J}\in{\mathcal{C}}^{1}({\mathcal{V}})
and

\widetilde{J}^{\prime}(v)(\varphi)=J^{\prime}(v+w(v))(\varphi)

for every
v,\varphi\in{\mathcal{V}}
. In view of (V) and (
3.1
), we find
r>0
such that

(3.2)

\inf_{\|v\|=r,\,v\in{\mathcal{V}}}\widetilde{J}(v)\geq\inf_{\|v\|=r,\,v\in{\mathcal{V}}}J(v)>0.

Arguing as in the proof of
[
14
, Lemma 6.3]
and using Lemma
2.2
we obtain the following property.

Lemma 3.1
.

If (F3) is satisfied, then for every
\varepsilon>0
there exists
c_{\varepsilon}>0
such that for every
u=v+w\in{\mathcal{V}}\oplus{\mathcal{W}}
and every
t\in[0,\sqrt{1-2/\gamma}]

J(u)\geq J(tv)+J^{\prime}(u)\biggl(\frac{1-t^{2}}{2}u+t^{2}w\biggr)-\varepsilon t^{2}|u|_{2}|w|_{2}-t^{2}c_{\varepsilon}\big|\Phi^{\prime}(|u|)\big|_{\Psi}|w|_{\Phi}.

Lemma 3.2
.

If
v\in{\mathcal{V}}
is such that
\overset{\circ}{\nabla}\times v=0
in the sense of distributions, then
v=0
.

Proof.

In this proof, we shall denote points in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
by
(x,y,z)
. Let
v=\begin{pmatrix}U\\
\widetilde{U}\end{pmatrix}
and define
E
via (
1.1
) with
t=0
. Observe that
\overset{\circ}{\nabla}\times v=0
implies
\nabla\times E=0
, and
v\in{\mathcal{V}}
implies
\mathop{\mathrm{div}\,}E=0
, both in the sense of distributions. From
\nabla\times E=0
,
[
10
, Lemma 1.1 (i)]
, and the fact that
E\in L^{2}_{\textup{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
, there exists
\xi\in H^{1}_{\textup{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
such that
E=\nabla\xi
. Then,
0=\mathop{\mathrm{div}\,}E=\Delta\xi
, so
E
is harmonic in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
as well. Writing
\Delta E(x,y,z)=0
explicitly, we obtain

\begin{split}\partial_{x}^{2}v(x,y)\cos(kz)&+\partial_{x}^{2}\widetilde{v}(x,y)\sin(kz)+\partial_{y}^{2}v(x,y)\cos(kz)+\partial_{y}^{2}\widetilde{v}(x,y)\sin(kz)\\
&-k^{2}v(x,y)\cos(kz)-k^{2}\widetilde{v}(x,y)\sin(kz)=0,\end{split}

whence, taking
z=0
and
z=\pi/(2k)
,

-\Delta v+k^{2}v=-\Delta\widetilde{v}+k^{2}\widetilde{v}=0\quad\text{in }\mathbb{R}^{2}.

Since
v\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
, we conclude.
∎

The next Lemma refines the abstract condition

15
, (I7)]
.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
(v_{n})\subset{\mathcal{V}}
,
v\in{\mathcal{V}}\setminus\{0\}
,
(w_{n})\subset{\mathcal{W}}
, and
(t_{n})\subset(0,\infty)
such that
t_{n}\to\infty
and
v_{n}\rightharpoonup v
as
n\to\infty
. Then

\lim_{n}\frac{1}{t_{n}^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}F\bigl(t_{n}(v_{n}+w_{n})\bigr)\,dx=\infty.

Proof.

Observe that (F3) implies

\frac{1}{t_{n}^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}F\bigl(t_{n}(v_{n}+w_{n})\bigr)\,dx\geq\frac{c_{2}}{t_{n}^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\Phi\bigl(t_{n}|v_{n}+w_{n}|\bigr)\,dx.

From (N3), there exist
C,D>0
such that for every
t\in\mathbb{R}
,
C\Phi(t)\geq t^{2}-D
. For all
R>0
,

\int_{B(0,R)}|v_{n}+w_{n}|^{2}\,dx\leq\frac{C}{t_{n}^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\Phi\bigl(t_{n}|v_{n}+w_{n}|\bigr)\,dx+\pi R^{2}D,

thus the statement is true if
|v_{n}+w_{n}|
is unbounded in
L^{2}(B(0,R))
for some
R>0
.

Now, assume that
|v_{n}+w_{n}|
is bounded in
L^{2}(B(0,R))
for all
R>0
. Up to a subsequence,
v_{n}(x)\to v(x)
for a.e.
x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
and
w_{n}\to w
in
L^{2}_{\textup{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
for some
w
as
n\to\infty
. For every
\varepsilon>0
and every
n
, we define

\Omega_{n}^{\varepsilon}:=\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:|v_{n}(x)+w_{n}(x)|\geq\varepsilon\right\}.

Let us assume by contradiction that
\lim_{n}|\Omega_{n}^{\varepsilon}|=0
for all
\varepsilon>0
, i.e.
\lim_{n}|v_{n}+w_{n}|=0
in measure. Then, up to a subsequence,
v_{n}(x)+w_{n}(x)\to 0
for a.e.
x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
as
n\to\infty
. Consequently,
w_{n}\to-v
a.e. in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
and, therefore,
w_{n}\rightharpoonup-v
in
L^{2}_{\textup{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
as
n\to\infty
. Since
\overset{\circ}{\nabla}\times w_{n}=0
for every
n
in the sense of distributions, this implies that the same holds for
v
. From Lemma
3.2
,
v=0
, a contradiction. Finally, taking
\varepsilon>0
such that
\lim_{n}|\Omega_{n}^{\varepsilon}|>0
along a subsequence, we get

\lim_{n}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\frac{\Phi\bigl(t_{n}|v_{n}+w_{n}|\bigr)}{t_{n}^{2}}\,dx\geq\lim_{n}\int_{\Omega_{n}^{\varepsilon}}\frac{\Phi\bigl(t_{n}|v_{n}+w_{n}|\bigr)}{t_{n}^{2}|v_{n}+w_{n}|}|v_{n}+w_{n}|\,dx=\infty.\qed

Lemma 3.4
.

If
X\subset{\mathcal{V}}
has a finite dimension and
(v_{n})\subset X
is such that
\lim_{n}\|v_{n}\|=\infty
, then
\lim_{n}\widetilde{J}(v_{n})=-\infty
.

Proof.

Let us denote
\widetilde{v}_{n}:=v_{n}/\|v_{n}\|
. Up to a subsequence, there exists
\widetilde{v}\in X
such that
\lim_{n}\widetilde{v}=v
. In particular,
\widetilde{v}\neq 0
. Using Lemma
3.3
, we obtain

\lim_{n}\frac{\widetilde{J}(v_{n})}{\|v_{n}\|^{2}}\leq\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{\|v_{n}\|^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}F\bigl(\|v_{n}\|\widetilde{v}_{n}+w(v_{n})\bigr)\,dx=-\infty.\qed

Normally, the Ambrosetti–Rabinowitz-type condition (F3) (cf.
[
2
]
) provides the boundedness of Palais–Smale sequences by simply estimating
\widetilde{J}(v_{n})-\frac{1}{\gamma}\widetilde{J}^{\prime}(v_{n})(v_{n})
. As we shall see below, in our problem obtaining the boundedness is much more complicated even if
F(u)=\frac{1}{p}|u|^{p}
, and achieved only for Cerami sequences (cf.
[
7
]
).

Lemma 3.5
.

If
(v_{n})\subset{\mathcal{V}}
is a Cerami sequence for
\widetilde{J}
such that
\liminf_{n}\widetilde{J}(v_{n})\geq 0
, then it is bounded.

We recall that a Cerami sequence for
\widetilde{J}
is a sequence
(v_{n})\subset{\mathcal{V}}
such that
\bigl(\widetilde{J}(v_{n})\bigr)
is bounded and
\lim_{n}(1+\|v_{n}\|)\widetilde{J}^{\prime}(v_{n})=0
, and that a Cerami sequence at the level
c\in\mathbb{R}
is a Cerami sequence such that
\lim_{n}\widetilde{J}(v_{n})=c
.

Proof of Lemma
3.5
.

Let
\beta\geq\limsup_{n}\widetilde{J}(v_{n})
.
For every
n
, let us denote
w_{n}:=w(v_{n})
and
u_{n}:=v_{n}+w_{n}
. From the second inequality in (F3),

\frac{1}{2}\|v_{n}\|^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\essinf V|v_{n}|_{2}^{2}\geq J(u_{n})+\frac{1}{2}\essinf V|w_{n}|_{2}^{2}+c_{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\Phi(|u_{n}|)\,dx.

Adding
\|v_{n}\|^{2}/2-\essinf V|v_{n}|_{2}^{2}/2
on both sides, we obtain

(3.3)

\|v_{n}\|^{2}-\essinf V|v_{n}|_{2}^{2}\\
\geq J(u_{n})+\frac{1}{2}\|v_{n}\|^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\essinf V|v_{n}|_{2}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\essinf V|w_{n}|_{2}^{2}+c_{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\Phi(|u_{n}|)\,dx.

Set

s_{n}^{2}:=\frac{1}{2}\|v_{n}\|^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\essinf V|v_{n}|_{2}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\essinf V|w_{n}|_{2}^{2}+c_{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\Phi(|u_{n}|)\,dx

and
\widetilde{v}_{n}:=v_{n}/s_{n}
. Assume by contradiction that
(u_{n})
is unbounded. From Theorem
2.4
, this implies that
\lim_{n}s_{n}=\infty
along a subsequence. We want to prove that

(3.4)

\liminf_{n}|\widetilde{v}_{n}|_{\Phi}>0,

thus let us assume by contradiction that
|\widetilde{v}_{n}|_{\Phi}\to 0
along a subsequence as
n\to\infty
. This, together with (F1) and (F2), implies that for every
s>0

(3.5)

\lim_{n}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}F(s\widetilde{v}_{n})\,dx=0.

From (F3), there holds

J(u_{n})-\frac{1}{2}J^{\prime}(u_{n})(u_{n})\geq\frac{\gamma-2}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}F(u_{n})\,dx,

thus
(u_{n})
is bounded in
L^{\Phi}
. From Theorem
2.4
,
(w_{n})
is bounded in
L^{\Phi}
. Moreover, there exists
\delta>0
such that
s_{n}\geq\delta|u_{n}|_{2}^{2}\geq\delta|w_{n}|_{2}^{2}
. From Lemma
3.1
with
u=u_{n}
and
t=s/s_{n}
(observe that
t\leq\sqrt{1-2/\gamma}
for
n\gg 1
) and Lemma
2.2
,
for every
\varepsilon>0
there exists
c_{\varepsilon}>0
such that

\begin{split}\beta&\geq\liminf_{n}J(u_{n})\\
&\geq\liminf_{n}J(s\widetilde{v}_{n})+\liminf_{n}J^{\prime}(u_{n})\biggl(\frac{1-(s/s_{n})^{2}}{2}u_{n}+(s/s_{n})^{2}w_{n}\biggr)\\
&\quad-\varepsilon\limsup_{n}(s/s_{n})^{2}|u_{n}|_{2}|w_{n}|_{2}-c_{\varepsilon}\limsup_{n}(s/s_{n})^{2}\big|\Phi^{\prime}(|u_{n}|)\big|_{\Psi}|w_{n}|_{\Phi}\\
&\geq\liminf_{n}J(s\widetilde{v}_{n})+\liminf_{n}J^{\prime}(u_{n})\biggl(\frac{1-(s/s_{n})^{2}}{2}u_{n}+(s/s_{n})^{2}w_{n}\biggr)-s^{2}\frac{\varepsilon}{\delta}.\end{split}

Observe that the minimality of
w_{n}
implies
J^{\prime}(u_{n})(w_{n})=0
, hence, since
\lim_{n}J^{\prime}(u_{n})(u_{n})=0
, we obtain

\beta\geq\liminf_{n}J(u_{n})-s^{2}\frac{\varepsilon}{\delta},

which, together with (
3.5
), yields

\beta\geq s^{2}\biggl(\liminf_{n}\bigl(\|\widetilde{v}_{n}\|^{2}-\esssup V|\widetilde{v}_{n}|_{2}^{2}\bigr)-\frac{\varepsilon}{\delta^{2}}\biggr).

Taking
\sigma\in(0,1)
such that

\esssup V\leq\frac{\sigma}{2}\essinf V+(1-\sigma)k^{2},

we obtain, for every
n
,

\begin{split}\|\widetilde{v}_{n}\|^{2}-\esssup V|\widetilde{v}_{n}|_{2}^{2}&\geq\|\widetilde{v}_{n}\|^{2}-(1-\sigma)k^{2}|\widetilde{v}_{n}|_{2}^{2}-\frac{\sigma}{2}\essinf V|\widetilde{v}_{n}|_{2}^{2}\\
&\geq\sigma\biggl(\|\widetilde{v}_{n}\|^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\essinf V|\widetilde{v}_{n}|_{2}^{2}\biggr)\geq\sigma+o_{n}(1),\end{split}

where the last inequality comes from (
3.3
) and
J(u_{n})\geq o_{n}(1)
. In conclusion, taking

s=2\sqrt{\beta/\sigma}
and
\varepsilon=\sigma\delta^{2}/8
, we get the contradiction

\beta\geq s^{2}\biggl(\frac{\sigma}{2}-\frac{\varepsilon}{\delta^{2}}\biggr)=\frac{3}{2}\beta.

Using (
3.4
) and the fact that
(\widetilde{v}_{n})
is bounded, we find
\widetilde{v}\in{\mathcal{V}}\setminus\{0\}
such that
\widetilde{v}_{n}\rightharpoonup\widetilde{v}
as
n\to\infty
along a subsequence. Then, from Lemma
3.3
,

0=\lim_{n}\frac{J(u_{n})}{s_{n}^{2}}\leq\lim_{n}\frac{1}{2}\|\widetilde{v}_{n}\|^{2}-\frac{1}{s_{n}^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}F(s_{n}\widetilde{v}_{n}+w_{n})\,dx=-\infty,

a contradiction.
∎

Now we introduce the cylindrical symmetry of the problem. Let

g=\begin{pmatrix}\cos\alpha&-\sin\alpha\\
\sin\alpha&\cos\alpha\end{pmatrix}\in{\mathcal{S}}{\mathcal{O}}(2)

with
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
, and let
U\colon\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
. We define

(g\star U)(x):=\begin{pmatrix}g&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}U(g^{-1}x)=\begin{pmatrix}U_{1}(g^{-1}x)\cos\alpha-U_{2}(g^{-1}x)\sin\alpha\\
U_{1}(g^{-1}x)\sin\alpha+U_{2}(g^{-1}x)\cos\alpha\\
U_{3}(g^{-1}x)\end{pmatrix}

for
x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
, where
g^{-1}x=(x_{1}\cos\alpha+x_{2}\sin\alpha,-x_{1}\sin\alpha+x_{2}\cos\alpha)
.

Lemma 3.6
.

There holds

\Big|\overset{\circ}{\nabla}\times\begin{pmatrix}(g\star U)(x)\\
(g\star\widetilde{U})(x)\end{pmatrix}\Big|=\Big|\overset{\circ}{\nabla}\times\begin{pmatrix}U(g^{-1}x)\\
\widetilde{U}(g^{-1}x)\end{pmatrix}\Big|

for
U,\widetilde{U}\colon\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
and
g\in{\mathcal{S}}{\mathcal{O}}(2)

Proof.

Lemma 3.7
.

The functional
\widetilde{J}|_{{\mathcal{V}}_{{\mathcal{S}}{\mathcal{O}}(2)}}
satisfies the Cerami condition at every nonngative level (that is, every Cerami sequence at a nonnegative level has a converging subsequence).

Proof.

First, we claim that

is compactly embedded into
L^{r}(\mathbb{R}^{2})^{6}
for any

.
Indeed, let

be such that
v_{n}\rightharpoonup 0
as
n\to\infty
. Passing to a subsequence

a.e. on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
as
n\to\infty
. Then,
(|v_{n}|)
is bounded in
H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
and
{\mathcal{O}}(2)
-invariant. Hence,
|v_{n}|\to v_{0}
in
L^{r}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
along a subsequence as
n\to\infty
, which yields
v_{0}=0
.

Next, let
(v_{n})\subset{\mathcal{V}}_{{\mathcal{S}}{\mathcal{O}}(2)}
be a Cerami sequence for
\widetilde{J}|_{{\mathcal{V}}_{{\mathcal{S}}{\mathcal{O}}(2)}}
as in the statement. From Lemma
3.5
, there exists
v\in{\mathcal{V}}_{{\mathcal{S}}{\mathcal{O}}(2)}
such that
v_{n}\rightharpoonup v
along a subsequence as
n\to\infty

v_{n}\to v
in

and a.e. in
\mathbb{R}^{2}

n\to\infty

\begin{split}o_{n}(1)&=\widetilde{J}^{\prime}(v_{n})(v_{n})-\widetilde{J}^{\prime}(v_{n})(v)\\
&=b_{L}(v_{n},v_{n}-v)-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}V(x)\langle v_{n}+w_{n},v_{n}-v\rangle+\langle f(v_{n}+w_{n}),v_{n}-v\rangle\,dx\\

which implies that
