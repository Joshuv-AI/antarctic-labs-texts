Towards Energy- and Cost-Efficient 6G Networks
Tommy Azzino, Aria HasanzadeZonuzy, Jianghong Luo, Navid Abedini, Tao Luo

Abstract
As the world enters the journey toward the 6th generation (6G) of wireless technology, the promises of ultra-high data rates, unprecedented low latency, and a massive surge in connected devices require crucial exploration of network energy saving (NES) solutions to minimize the carbon footprint and overall energy usage of future cellular networks. On the other hand, network-controlled repeaters (NCRs) have been introduced by 3rd generation partnership project (3GPP) as a cost-effective solution to improve network coverage. However, their impact on network power consumption and energy efficiency has not been thoroughly investigated. This paper studies NES schemes for next-generation 6G networks aided by NCRs and proposes optimal NES strategies aiming at maximizing the overall energy efficiency of the network. Repeaters are shown to allow for power savings at next-generation nodeB (gNB) , and offer higher overall energy efficiency (EE) and spectral efficiency (SE) , thus providing an energy-efficient and cost-efficient alternative to increase the performance of future 6G networks.



I
Introduction

With the
6th generation (6G)
of cellular networks on the horizon, we are on the precipice of yet another groundbreaking era. The relentless growth in mobile data traffic, driven by emerging applications and the proliferation of connected devices, sets the stage for the necessity of
6G
. According to forecasts from
international telecommunication union (ITU)
, global mobile data traffic is projected to reach impressive levels by 2030, necessitating novel approaches to wireless communication
[
1
]
.
Besides the elevated ambitions of
6G

[
2
]
, a fundamental question remains: Can we accomplish this technological marvel while being environmentally responsible?

Future
6G
communication systems are foreseen to massively exploit the
millimeter-wave (mmWave)
frequency band to enable capacity-eager applications, e.g. autonomous driving and extended reality. However, propagation at
mmWave
frequencies suffers from severe path loss and blockage, limiting the coverage of the network in these bands, especially in dense urban environments. To overcome these challenges, densification of
small cell (SC)
systems with massive antenna arrays becomes necessary, resulting in higher
capital expenditure (CAPEX)
for cellular operators and service providers
[
3
]
.

Among other wireless backhauling/fronthauling technologies, such as
reflective intelligent surface (RIS)
and
integrated access and backhaul (IAB)
,
network-controlled repeater (NCR)
represents a low power and cost-effective solution to increase coverage, especially in
mmWave
, and serve an ever-expanding network of users, therefore reducing
CAPEX

[
4
]
. As defined in
3rd generation partnership project (3GPP)
’s Release 18,
NCR
is the evolution of the wireless repeater based on the
amplify-forward (AF)
concept, with superior capabilities, such as adaptive
beamforming (BF)
and
time-division duplexing (TDD)
-aware operation
[
5
]
.

According to the NextG Alliance, sustainability is a fundamental objective for
6G
and will be considered in the design of networks to reduce CO2 emissions and improve
energy efficiency (EE)

[
6
]
.
In pursuit of environmentally sustainable networks,
network energy saving (NES)
has become a vital component, with the aim of reducing the power consumption of the network and increasing
EE
, that is, reducing
operational expenditure (OPEX)
for network operators. Release 18, commercially recognized as
5th generation (5G)
-Advanced, introduces antenna and transmit power adaptation techniques for the first time to increase
EE
. However, in the release,
NES
and energy-efficient optimization considering repeaters have not been addressed.

This paper advances the current literature by investigating techniques to enable
NES
for repeater-supported wireless networks. In particular, we focus on the optimization of a set of
next-generation nodeB (gNB)
’s and
NCR
’s parameters, to maximize the network
EE
. Moreover, we investigate trade-offs involving energy-efficient operation and
quality of service (QoS)
degradation for the
user equipment (UE)
to quantify the
EE
gains and the corresponding
spectral efficiency (SE)
losses.
To this end, we conduct an analytical study of simple network topologies with and without a repeater (or
NCR
), and a detailed system-level simulation study to evaluate the impact of energy-efficient operations based on a realistic network deployment. Finally, we provide strategies to enable energy-efficient operations and
NES
based on the availability of different
power amplifier (PA)
technologies.

The paper is organized as follows. Section
II
presents the related work in this domain.
Section
III
outlines the system model, while Section
IV
describes the energy-efficient optimization of the
gNB
and
NCR
configurations. The simulation results are presented and discussed in Section
V
. Finally, Section
VI
concludes the paper.

II
Related Work

In the realm of
EE
and
NES
for future green
6G
networks,
[
4
]
provides a survey on new architectural changes associated with
6G
networks with a focus on sustainable networks characterized by ubiquitous coverage, pervasive
artificial intelligence (AI)
and enhanced protocol stack. In the context of sustainable networks, the authors of
[
7
]
investigated the performance of
RISs
for
internet of things (IoT)
. In particular, they introduced a power consumption model for
RIS
to optimize the energy efficiency of the system. In
[
8
]
, the authors considered a cellular network assisted by multiple solar-powered relays. Repeaters are scattered in the cell area so that
UEs
can enjoy higher-quality links and the base station can save energy by reducing its transmit power. They proposed a power control method to reduce interference in the sub-6 GHz frequency range and hence improve network performance. However, they did not consider energy-efficient optimization. Finally, in
[
9
]
, the authors explored different potential techniques for saving network power through the adaptation of resources in the time, frequency, spatial, and power domains. In addition, they provide energy efficiency and throughput trade-off analysis to identify which domain has the highest potential for power savings. However, no
EE
optimization was also considered.

To our knowledge, this is the first work to address the energy-efficient optimization of
6G
networks comprising
SCs
and
NCRs
.

Link-level Direct Topology

First, we present the

EE
of the

), are shown for the fixed and varying

There is an opportunity for

only in high

reduction, excluding any possibility of

.

Regarding the
EE
-optimal strategy, it can be shown that the highest available bandwidth is always
EE
. However, when there is room to back off from the maximum configuration (that is,
SE
is compromised), the optimal strategy to achieve
EE
operation would be different according to the
PA
efficiency model. With fixed
PA
efficiency, the optimal strategy is to first reduce the
PA
output power
PAout_{gNB}
, and then (if there is still room for energy savings) adapt the number of active transmitting antenna elements
N_{Tx,gNB}
. The rationale behind this is that reducing the number of active transmitting antennas is more costly than reducing
PAout_{gNB}
from a
SE
perspective (
SNR\propto PAout_{gNB}\cdot N_{Tx,gNB}^{2}
). In contrast, reducing
N_{Tx,gNB}
or
PAout_{gNB}
allows for the same level of
NES
(
P_{gNB}(DL)\propto PAout_{gNB}\cdot N_{Tx,gNB}
). With varying
PA
efficiency, the optimal
EE
strategy is to first reduce the number of active transmitting antennas until it reaches a minimum value and then reduce
PAout_{gNB}
. This is because, in terms of
NES
, reducing
PAout_{gNB}
may not provide significant savings (since
P_{gNB}(DL)\propto\frac{PAout_{gNB}}{\eta(PAout_{gNB})}
and, for varying
PA
efficiency, this may be approximately constant).

In conclusion, we see that for fixed PA efficiency, the network can achieve up to 30% EE improvement with a maximum 10% rate degradation. For varying PA efficiency,
EE
gains can reach 60%, but with a potential
\approx
20% SE decrease.

Link-level Indirect Topology

For the

, we want to assess whether a higher network

can be achieved by backing off from the most

and

and

will mainly influence

and

?

It can be seen that

\displaystyle effSNR(DL)

\displaystyle=\frac{SNR_{AC}}{1+\frac{1+SNR_{AC}}{SNR_{BH}}}.

(13)

A closer examination of the above formula reveals that if

This is in fact typically the case in practice, due to the more favorable

.

V-C

Direct vs. Indirect Topology

This subsection compares two topologies in terms of
EE
and
SE
. We aim to determine whether it is more energy-efficient to achieve target coverage using a
gNB
(e.g., a small cell) with an assisting
NCR
, or a single yet stronger
gNB
(e.g., a macro cell). We adopt the following methodology for this analysis.
A target
DL

SNR
(for example,
0
dB) is set for
UEs
. In a first topology (
SC
+
NCR
), we start by determining the direct coverage range of the
SC
(i.e. a distance from the
SC
, where a
UE
would achieve the target
SNR
directly from the
SC
), and place the
NCR
at the edge of the
SC
coverage region (
d_{BH}=94
m). Next, the coverage range of the
NCR
is calculated (that is, the distance from the
NCR
where
UEs
would achieve the
effSNR(DL)
target
SNR
from the
NCR
–
d_{AC}=49
m). In a second topology, we choose the smallest
MC
(in terms of the number of transmitting antennas) that can provide the same coverage range (i.e.,
d_{BH}+d_{AC}
) as that of the first topology. The two topologies are then compared in terms of
EE
and
SE
.

Fig.
5(a)
shows that
SC
+
NCR
provides a much better rate in the
NCR
’s coverage area (highlighted in green), that is, on average more than 2.6X the
MC
’s
SE
in the same region. Outside of this region,
SC
provides a less spectral-efficient service, 26% reduced on average compared to
MC
.

Fig.
5(b)
shows the
EE
comparison between the two topologies. As confirmed by the region highlighted in green,
SC
+
NCR
offers 2.1X better
EE
on average in the
NCR
’s coverage area. Outside of
NCR
coverage,
SC
provides slightly lower
EE
compared to
MC
, (-15% on average).

We can conclude that
NCRs
are a promising solution (both in terms of spectral and energy efficiencies) to provide a target coverage. Especially if there is a hotspot region, a repeater will be able to improve the experience of
UEs
in that region at a lower network energy consumption.

System-level

In the system-level analysis, we consider a realistic deployment of 20
SCs
and 62
NCRs
in the downtown area of Philadelphia, as shown in Fig.
6
. This deployment is the result of an optimization process that aims to find the optimal number and location of
SCs
and
NCRs
to achieve a target coverage of
80\%
in
frequency range (FR)
-2 while minimizing the deployment cost given a specific geographical region. Each
SC
comprises three sectors and each sector might have one or more
NCRs
to extend network coverage. Details for this deployment optimization are provided in
[
13
]
.
From the optimal deployment, we consider two topologies: one comprising both
SCs
and
NCRs
(
With Repeaters
in the figures) and the other comprising only

(addressed as
Without Repeaters
). For comparison between the two topologies, we focus on the reduced set of
UE
locations that are covered in the topology

. This is because certain

, which are served via an
NCR
from the optimization solution, can also be served directly with a
SC
at lower
SNR
. With this system-level study, we would like to confirm the findings of the link-level analysis.
Furthermore, we want to measure the impact

have in terms of
UE
throughput and

/

performance at the system level.

and serve them with higher

. However, this comes at the cost of increased network power consumption, as can be seen in Fig.

.

From link-level studies, we saw that
EE
-optimal strategies can be adopted to improve the energy efficiency of the network. At the system level, for the topology
With Repeaters
, we observe that there is a great opportunity for
NESs
through the
BH
links. Fig.
7(c)
shows the CDF of the
EE
per sector with
EE
optimization and without optimization (i.e., the baseline with highest
SE
) for the case of varying
PA
efficiency. The
EE
-optimal topology
With Repeaters
outperforms the
EE
-optimal topology
Without Repeaters
in terms of the overall
EE
, with an average increase of 8.5%, and the baseline topology
With Repeaters
, with an average increase of 18% in
EE
. The impact of
EE
optimization on the performance of
UEs
can be seen in Fig.
7(d)
. Specifically, the figure shows that a topology
With Repeaters
(after
EE
optimization) can provide better coverage for the cell edge
UEs
, with an average 5%
DL
rate improvement compared to the topology
Without Repeaters
. At the same time, the
UE
rate suffers a slight 9% degradation on average compared to the baseline topology
With Repeaters
. Thus,
EE
optimization brings considerably higher
EE
and
NES
at the expense of slightly degraded
UE
performance, as we found in our analytical study.

VI
Conclusions

In this paper, we introduced the challenge of optimizing the network
EE
in the context of future
6G
networks enabled by
NCRs
. We presented
NES
strategies to improve
EE
, thus decreasing network power consumption, by adapting the transmitting and/or receiving configurations of
gNB
and
NCR
. Our link and system-level results show that it is more energy-efficient to sacrifice
SE
in high
SNR
regimes. To this end, the best strategy depends on the
PA
implementation based on the availability of bias adjustment. Bias adjustment represents a more advanced
PA
technology that improves
EE
compared to previous/legacy
PA
technologies. We showed that repeaters guarantee a higher overall
SE
compared to a deployment without repeaters and allow power savings at
gNB
through their typically strong
BH
link, leading to a higher overall
EE
. Therefore, repeaters represent an energy- and cost-efficient solution to increase the coverage and capacity of future
6G
networks.
Finally, despite our work focuses on
FR
-2, the optimization results can be exploited for
FR
-3, which is a candidate spectrum targeted for future
6G
deployments.
