Numerical study of non-adiabatic quantum thermodynamics of the driven resonant level model: Non-equilibrium entropy production and higher order corrections
Kaiyi Tong, Wenjie Dou

Abstract
We present our numerical study on quantum thermodynamics of the resonant level model subjected to non-equilibrium condition as well as external driving. Following our previous work on non-equilibrium quantum thermodynamics (Phys. Rev. B 101, 184304 [2020]), we expand the density operator into a series of power in the driving speed, where we can determine the non-adiabatic thermodynamic quantities. Particularly, we calculate the non-equilibrium entropy production rate as well as higher order non-adiabatic corrections to the energy and/or population. In the limit of weak system-bath coupling, our results reduce to the one from the quantum master equation.

\displaystyle\delta\dot{S}=-k_{B}\sum_{\alpha\nu}\int_{0}^{\infty}\dot{R}_{\alpha}\dot{R}_{\nu}tr(e^{-i\hat{H}t^{\prime}/\hbar}\partial_{\nu}\hat{\rho}_{ss}e^{i\hat{H}t^{\prime}/\hbar}\partial_{\alpha}\ln\hat{\rho}_{ss})dt^{\prime}.

II
Theory

II.1
The driven resonant-level model

The driven resonant-level model is probably the simplest yet a heuristic model for the study of quantum thermodynamics in the strongly coupled regime. The model consists of a system, two baths, as well as the couplings between them:

\hat{H}=\hat{H}_{\text{sys}}+\hat{H}_{\text{bath}}+\hat{H}_{\text{int}},

(2)

The system consists of one level with a time-dependent on-site energy:

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{\text{sys}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\epsilon_{d}(t)\hat{d}^{\dagger}\hat{d},

(3)

The bath consists of a set of non-interacting Fermions:

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{\text{bath}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k\alpha}\epsilon_{k\alpha}\hat{c}_{k\alpha}^{\dagger}\hat{c}_{k\alpha},

(4)

Here
\alpha=L
(or
R
) indicates the left (or right) bath. The couplings between the system and the baths are bilinear:

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{\text{int}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k\alpha}V_{k\alpha}(\hat{c}_{k\alpha}^{\dagger}\hat{d}+\hat{d}^{\dagger}\hat{c}_{k\alpha}).

(5)

To describe the strength of the coupling, we define the hybridization function
\Gamma_{\alpha}(\epsilon)

\Gamma(\epsilon)=\sum_{\alpha}\Gamma_{\alpha}(\epsilon)=\sum_{k\alpha}2\pi|V_{k\alpha}|^{2}\delta(\epsilon-\epsilon_{k\alpha}).

(6)

Below, we consider the wide-band limit, such that
\Gamma
is a constant (independent of energy
\epsilon
).

An illustrative picture of the driving resonant-level model.

II.2
Analytical analysis

We now try to define the quantum thermodynamic quantities for the driven resonant-level model. The main quantities of interests are dot population, work, and entropy production:

\displaystyle N=tr(\hat{\rho}\hat{d}^{\dagger}\hat{d})

(7)

\displaystyle W=tr(\hat{\rho}\partial_{t}\hat{H})

(8)

\displaystyle S=-k_{B}tr(\hat{\rho}\ln\hat{\rho})

(9)

Here
\hat{\rho}
is the density operator. Note that, we have avoided defined heat and/or energy as they are extensive quantities and requires spectral treatment.
Dou et al. 2018
;
Dou et al. 2020
Since the Hamiltonian is quadratic, we can rewrite the above quantities using the one-body density operator
\hat{\sigma}
:

\displaystyle N

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle d|\hat{\sigma}|d\rangle

(10)

\displaystyle W

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\dot{\epsilon}_{d}\langle d|\hat{\sigma}|d\rangle=\dot{\epsilon}_{d}N

(11)

\displaystyle S

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\text{Tr}(\hat{\sigma}\ln\hat{\sigma})-\text{Tr}((1-\hat{\sigma})\ln(1-\hat{\sigma}))

(12)

Here
Tr
denotes the one-body trace (trace in orbital representation). Note that for the resonant level model, work
W
is proportional to dot population by a factor of
\dot{\epsilon}_{d}
, where
\dot{\epsilon}_{d}
is the speed of driving. Such that we will mainly focus on population and entropy below.

The task is then to calculate the density operator, which can be very difficult for the time-dependent Hamiltonian. Nevertheless, we have the equation of motion for the density matrix

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\hat{\sigma}=\dot{\epsilon}_{d}\partial_{\epsilon_{d}}\hat{\sigma}-\frac{i}{\hbar}[\hat{h},\hat{\sigma}]

(13)

Here
\hat{h}
is the one body Hamiltonian. In the limit of slow driving, we can expand the density operator into a series of power in the driving speed,

\hat{\sigma}=\hat{\sigma}^{(0)}+\hat{\sigma}^{(1)}+\hat{\sigma}^{(2)}+\cdots,

(14)

By matching the order in driving speed from both sides of the above equation, we arrive at a set of equations:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\hat{\sigma}^{(0)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{i}{\hbar}[\hat{h},\hat{\sigma}^{(0)}],

(15)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\hat{\sigma}^{(n)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\dot{\epsilon}_{d}\partial_{\epsilon_{d}}\sigma^{(n-1)}-\frac{i}{\hbar}[\hat{h},\sigma^{(n)}],n\geq 1

(16)

The steady state solution of Eq.
15
gives us the
0
th order density matrix,
\hat{\sigma}^{(0)}=\hat{\sigma}_{ss}
, such that we can proceed to calculate the higher order density matrix

\displaystyle\hat{\sigma}^{(n)}

\displaystyle=-\dot{\epsilon}_{d}\int_{0}^{t}e^{-i\hat{h}(t-t^{\prime})/{\hbar}}\partial_{\epsilon_{d}}\hat{\sigma}^{(n-1)}e^{{i\hat{h}(t-t^{\prime})}/{\hbar}}dt^{\prime}

(17)

\displaystyle\approx-\dot{\epsilon}_{d}\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-{i\hat{h}t}/{\hbar}}\partial_{\epsilon_{d}}\hat{\sigma}^{(n-1)}e^{{i\hat{h}t}/{\hbar}}dt.

In the second line of the above equation, we have used the Markovian approximation, assuming the timescale of driving is much smaller as compared with the timescale for the relaxation. The Markovian approximation is consistent with the slow driving approximation.

II.2.1
Zeroth Order Quantities

Since the zeroth order entropy is an extensive quantity, we mainly calculate the zeroth order dot population here. To do so, we need to diagonalize the one-body Hamiltonian:

\hat{h}=\sum_{k\alpha}\epsilon_{k\alpha}|{\psi}_{k\alpha}\rangle\langle{\psi}_{k\alpha}|,

(18)

The diagonalization can be done analytically through the following transformation:

|{\psi}_{k\alpha}\rangle=|{c}_{k\alpha}\rangle+\frac{V_{k\alpha}}{\epsilon_{k\alpha}-\epsilon_{d}+i\Gamma/2}\left(|d\rangle+\sum_{k^{\prime}\alpha^{\prime}}\frac{V_{k^{\prime}\alpha^{\prime}}}{\epsilon_{k\alpha}-\epsilon_{k^{\prime}\alpha^{\prime}}+i\eta)}|{c}_{k^{\prime}\alpha^{\prime}}\rangle\right).

(19)

With the diagonalized Hamiltonian, we can determine the steady-state single particle density matrix

\displaystyle\hat{\sigma}^{(0)}=\sum_{k\alpha}f(\epsilon_{k\alpha}-\mu_{\alpha})|{\psi}_{k\alpha}\rangle\langle{\psi}_{k\alpha}|

(20)

Here,
f
is the Fermi function
f(\epsilon_{k\alpha}-\mu_{\alpha})=\left[1+e^{\beta(\epsilon_{k\alpha}-\mu_{\alpha})}\right]^{-1}
, and
\mu_{\alpha}
is the chemical potential for the
\alpha
lead.

With the analytical results shown above, we can obtain the zeroth order population:

\displaystyle N^{(0)}=\left<d\right|\hat{\sigma}^{(0)}\left|d\right>=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int d\epsilon A(\epsilon)\bar{f}(\epsilon),

(21)

Here
A(\epsilon)
is the spectral function

\displaystyle A(\epsilon)=\frac{\Gamma}{(\epsilon-\epsilon_{d})^{2}+(\Gamma/2)^{2}},

(22)

and
\bar{f}(\epsilon)
is the weighted Fermi function
\bar{f}(\epsilon)=\sum_{\alpha}\frac{\Gamma_{\alpha}}{\Gamma}\left[1+e^{\beta(\epsilon-\mu_{\alpha})}\right]^{-1}
. Details of the deviation are shown in Appendix
A
.

II.2.2
First Order Quantities

Now we turn to the first order correction. With
0
th order
\hat{\sigma}^{(1)}
, we can proceed to calculate the first order density:

\hat{\sigma}^{(1)}=-\dot{\epsilon}_{d}\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-{i\hat{h}t}/{\hbar}}\partial_{\epsilon_{d}}\hat{\sigma}^{(0)}e^{{i\hat{h}t}/{\hbar}}dt.

(23)

The first order correction to the population is then given by

N^{(1)}=\left<d\right|\hat{\sigma}^{(1)}\left|d\right>=-\frac{\hbar\dot{\epsilon}_{d}}{4\pi}\int d\epsilon A^{2}{\partial}_{\epsilon}\bar{f}(\epsilon).

(24)

Detailed derivation can be found in the appendix of Refs.
(
35
;
40
)
. Note that the population is linearly proportional to driving speed
\dot{\epsilon}_{d}
. Such that the first order work depends on
\dot{\epsilon}_{d}
quadratically,
W^{(1)}=\gamma^{(1)}\dot{\epsilon}^{2}_{d}
. Here
\gamma^{(1)}
is the frictional coefficient:

\gamma^{(1)}=\frac{N^{(1)}}{\dot{\epsilon}_{d}}=-\frac{\hbar}{4\pi}\int d\epsilon A^{2}{\partial}_{\epsilon}\bar{f}(\epsilon),

(25)

which accounts for the dissipative effects.

We could also define a correlation function of the random force to qualify the fluctuation

\displaystyle D_{\mu\nu}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\langle e^{{i\hat{H}t}/{\hbar}}\delta\hat{F}_{\mu}e^{-{i\hat{H}t}/{\hbar}}\delta\hat{F}_{\nu}(0)\rangle_{S}

(26)

\displaystyle\delta\hat{F}_{\alpha}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\partial}_{\alpha}\hat{H}-tr({\partial}_{\alpha}\hat{H}\hat{\rho}_{ss})

(27)

\langle\cdots\rangle_{S}
denote the symmetric average of the correlation functions. Here
\alpha
and
\mu
are degrees of freedom for the motion. In the driven resonant level model (with
\alpha=\mu=\epsilon_{d}
), we can calculate the fluctuation analytically

D=\frac{\hbar}{4\pi}\int d\epsilon A^{2}\bar{f}(\epsilon)(1-\bar{f}(\epsilon)).

(28)

We then turn to the first order correction to entropy, which can be calculated through Taylor expansion of Eq.
12
:

S^{(1)}=-k_{B}\text{Tr}\left[\hat{\sigma}^{(1)}\ln\hat{\sigma}^{(0)}-\hat{\sigma}^{(1)}\ln{(1-\hat{\sigma}^{(0)})}\right],

(29)

The non-adiabatic entropy rate is the derivative of the above equation.

\displaystyle\dot{S}^{(2)}=\partial_{t}S^{(1)}=\frac{\dot{Q}^{(2)}}{T}+\delta\dot{S},

(30)

The first term in Eq.
30
is the first order correction to the heat
Dou et al. 2020

\dot{Q}^{(2)}=-k_{B}T\dot{\epsilon}_{d}\text{Tr}(\left[{\partial}_{\epsilon_{d}}{\sigma}^{(1)}\ln{{\sigma}^{(0)}}-{\partial}_{\epsilon_{d}}{\sigma}^{(1)}\ln{(1-{\sigma}^{(0)})}\right].

(31)

The second term
\delta\dot{S}
in Eq.
30
is the additional entropy production rate due to the external driving

\displaystyle\delta\dot{S}=

\displaystyle-k_{B}\dot{\epsilon}_{d}\left[\hat{\sigma}^{(1)}{\partial}_{\epsilon_{d}}\ln\hat{\sigma}^{(0)}-\hat{\sigma}^{(1)}{\partial}_{\epsilon_{d}}\ln{(1-\hat{\sigma}^{(0)})}\right]

(32)

At equilibrium, we can calculate the entropy production rate explicitly and show that

\gamma^{(1)}=\beta D=\frac{\hbar T\delta\dot{S}}{\dot{\epsilon}_{d}}.

(33)

The first equality in the above equation is the fluctuation-dissipation theorem. Under non-equilibrium condition, the fluctuation-dissipation theorem is no longer obeyed and we have not been able to determine the entropy production rate. In Sec.
II.3
, we will show how to evaluate entropy production rate numerically.

II.2.3
Second Order Quantities

Evaluation of the second order corrections to thermodynamic quantities is very tricky, which will require

\hat{\sigma}^{(2)}=-\dot{\epsilon}_{d}\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-{i\hat{h}t}/{\hbar}}{\partial}_{\epsilon_{d}}{\sigma}^{(1)}e^{{i\hat{h}t}/{\hbar}}dt.

(34)

Nevertheless, as shown in Appendix
B
, we manage to evaluate the second order correction to the population analytically for the equilibrium case,

N^{(2)}=\left<d\right|\hat{\sigma}^{(2)}\left|d\right>=\frac{\hbar^{2}\dot{\epsilon}_{d}^{2}}{12\pi}\int d\epsilon A^{3}{\partial}_{\epsilon}^{2}{f}(\epsilon).

(35)

Similarly to the first order case, we can define the friction term
\gamma^{(2)}
as

\gamma^{(2)}=\frac{N^{(2)}}{\dot{\epsilon}_{d}^{2}}=\frac{\hbar^{2}}{12\pi}\int d\epsilon A^{3}{\partial}_{\epsilon}^{2}{f}(\epsilon).

(36)

Out of equilibrium, we do not have analytical results for the second order corrections. We could simply replace
f
by
\bar{f}
in the above equation as our trial results. In general, however, the higher order correction can be evaluated numerically using hierarchical quantum master equation (HQME).

II.3
Numerical Methods

The derivations in evaluating the non-adiabatic corrections can be very lengthy and a compact form of solutions may not available for certain quantities. In this subsection, we consider a finite number of degrees of freedom for the baths in a similar fashion to the Ref.
(
41
)
, such that we can evaluate non-adiabatic corrections numerically.

To do so, we discrete the baths degrees of freedom, and we build the one-body Hamiltonian
\hat{h}
with finite number of levels from the baths.
\hat{h}
can be written in the matrix form:

\displaystyle\hat{h}=\begin{pmatrix}\epsilon_{d}&\mathbb{V_{L}}&\mathbb{V_{R}}\\
\mathbb{V_{L}}^{\dagger}&\hat{\epsilon}_{L}&0\\
\mathbb{V_{R}}^{\dagger}&0&\hat{\epsilon}_{R}\end{pmatrix}

(37)

Here,
\mathbb{V_{\alpha}}=\{V_{k\alpha}\}
is a vector representation of the system-bath coupling, and
\hat{\epsilon}_{\alpha}=\{\epsilon_{k\alpha}\}
is a matrix representation of the energies in the baths.

We then proceed to build the steady state density matrix
\hat{\sigma}_{ss}
. We first define non-interacting density operator

\displaystyle\hat{\sigma}_{0}=\begin{pmatrix}f(\epsilon_{d}-\frac{1}{2}\mu_{L}-\frac{1}{2}\mu_{R})&0&0\\
0&f(\hat{\epsilon}_{L}-\mu_{L})&0\\
0&0&f(\hat{\epsilon}_{R}-\mu_{R})\end{pmatrix}

(38)

The interacting steady-state density operator is obtained by transforming
\hat{\sigma}_{0}
in the basis where
\hat{h}
is diagonal and zero out all non-diagonal terms of
U^{\dagger}\hat{\sigma}_{0}U
. Here
U^{\dagger}
diagonalizes the
\hat{h}
. Such that
\hat{\sigma}_{ss}
and
\hat{h}
are both diagonal in such a basis.

\displaystyle\hat{h}=\sum_{m}\epsilon_{m}|m\rangle\langle m|

(39)

\displaystyle\hat{\sigma}_{ss}=\sum_{m}\sigma_{m}|m\rangle\langle m|

(40)

Such a scheme is similar to the scattering formulation of the density operator.
Ness 2017

With the eigenvalues and eigenbasis of
\hat{h}
and
\hat{\sigma}_{ss}
, the zeroth order population is easily obtained:

N^{(0)}=\sum_{m}|\langle d|m\rangle|^{2}\sigma_{m}

(41)

The first order correction requires
\hat{\sigma}^{(1)}
(Eq.
23
), which can be evaluated in the eigenbasis of
\hat{h}
:

\displaystyle\hat{\sigma}^{(1)}=\pi\hbar\dot{\epsilon}_{d}\sum_{mn}|m\rangle\langle m|\partial_{\epsilon_{d}}\sigma_{ss}|n\rangle\delta(\epsilon_{m}-\epsilon_{n})\langle n|

(42)

Using Hellman-Feynman theorem, we can rewrite the above as (see the appendix in Ref.
(
35
)
)

\displaystyle\hat{\sigma}^{(1)}=\pi\hbar\dot{\epsilon}_{d}\sum_{mn}|m\rangle\left<m|d\right>\frac{\sigma_{m}-\sigma_{n}}{\epsilon_{m}-\epsilon_{n}}\delta(\epsilon_{m}-\epsilon_{n})\left<d|n\right>\langle n|

(43)

Such that the first order population is

N^{(1)}=\pi\hbar\dot{\epsilon}_{d}\sum_{mn}|\left<m|d\right>|^{2}\frac{\sigma_{m}-\sigma_{n}}{\epsilon_{m}-\epsilon_{n}}\delta(\epsilon_{m}-\epsilon_{n})|\left<d|n\right>|^{2}

(44)

In numerical simulations, we replace the delta function by a Gaussian with a broadening parameter
\eta
:

\displaystyle\pi\delta(\epsilon_{m}-\epsilon_{n})

\displaystyle\rightarrow

\displaystyle\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2t^{2}}}\exp{\left[\frac{(\epsilon_{m}-\epsilon_{n})^{2}}{2\eta^{2}}\right]}

(45)

We also take care of the denominator
\epsilon_{m}-\epsilon_{n}
in Eq.
44
as

\displaystyle\frac{\sigma_{m}-\sigma_{n}}{\epsilon_{m}-\epsilon_{n}}

\displaystyle\rightarrow

\displaystyle\Re\frac{\sigma_{m}-\sigma_{n}}{\epsilon_{m}-\epsilon_{n}+i\eta}

(46)

In practice, the broadening parameter
\eta
is set to be in the order of the energy spacing to better converge the results.

The correlation function in Eq.
26
can be recast in the eigenbasis (see the supplementary material in Ref.
(
40
)
):

D=\pi\hbar\dot{\epsilon}_{d}\sum_{mn}|\left<m|d\right>|^{2}\sigma_{m}(1-\sigma_{n})\delta(\epsilon_{m}-\epsilon_{n})|\left<d|n\right>|^{2}

(47)

which is readily to be calculated. Finally,
\delta\dot{S}
can be written in the eigenbasis as

\delta\dot{S}=\pi\hbar\dot{\epsilon}_{d}\sum_{mn}\langle m|\hat{\sigma}^{(1)}|n\rangle\langle n|\partial_{\epsilon_{d}}(\ln\hat{\sigma}_{ss}-\ln(1-\hat{\sigma}_{ss}))|m\rangle

(48)

Here,
\langle m|\partial_{\epsilon_{d}}\ln\hat{\sigma}_{ss}|n\rangle
is done numerically with finite difference. Note that
|m\rangle
is the adiabatic basis. To calculate the matrix element properly, we rotate the matrix
\ln\hat{\sigma}_{ss}
back to the diabatic basis. After done derivative, we rotate the matrix back to the adiabatic basis.

III
Results and discussion

III.1
Weak coupling limit: Master Equations

In this subsection, we consider the the weak coupling limit. In such a limit, we can trace out the bath degrees of freedom and derive an equation of motion for the dot density
\sigma_{d}
only
Dou et al. 2015
;
Dou and Subotnik 2016
:

{\partial}_{t}\sigma_{d}=-\dot{\epsilon_{d}}{\partial}_{\epsilon_{d}}\sigma_{d}-\frac{\Gamma}{\hbar}\left[\sigma_{d}-\bar{f}(\epsilon_{d}\right].

(49)

Again, we can expand the dot density into a series of powers in terms of the driving speeds,
\sigma_{d}=\sigma_{d}^{(0)}+\sigma_{d}^{(1)}+\sigma_{d}^{(2)}+\cdots
. Similar to the procedures in Sec.
II.2
, we obtain the
n
th order population as

\displaystyle N^{(0)}=\bar{f}(\epsilon_{d}),

(50)

\displaystyle N^{(1)}=-\frac{\hbar\dot{\epsilon}_{d}}{\Gamma}{\partial}_{\epsilon_{d}}\bar{f}(\epsilon_{d}),

(51)

\displaystyle N^{(2)}=\frac{\hbar^{2}\dot{\epsilon}_{d}^{2}}{\Gamma^{2}}{\partial}_{\epsilon_{d}}^{2}\bar{f}(\epsilon_{d}).

(52)

Similarly, we can also obtain the friction and fluctuation,

\displaystyle\gamma^{(1)}=-\frac{\hbar}{\Gamma}{\partial}_{\epsilon_{d}}\bar{f}(\epsilon_{d}),

(53)

\displaystyle D=\frac{\hbar k_{B}T}{\Gamma}\bar{f}(\epsilon_{d})[1-\bar{f}(\epsilon_{d})]

(54)

To get the entropy production rate, we define the entropy for the dot as
S=-\sigma_{d}\ln\sigma_{d}-(1-\sigma_{d})\ln(1-\sigma_{d})
. Similar to the procedures in
II.2
, we arrive at the entropy production rate as

\displaystyle\frac{\hbar T\delta\dot{S}}{\dot{\epsilon}_{d}}=[\gamma^{(1)}]^{2}/(\beta D).

(55)

Note that the above equation is only true for the driven resonant level model in the weak coupling limit. We expect that our numerical results can reduce to the master equation results in the limit of
\Gamma<kT
. What is missing in the master equation is mainly the level broadening effects. Hence, we refer to the master equation results as unbroadened results.

III.2.2
Second Order Results

We now look at the second order correction to the population (or second order friction). Here, HQME is used to simulate the exact dynamics for the driven resonant level model, where we can calculate the exact population as a function of time. IV
Conclusion

Using the expansion of the density operator in the power of driving speed, we have identified the non-adiabatic correction to the quantum thermodynamic quantities in the strongly coupled regimes. With numerical tools, we have calculated non-adiabatic corrections for the resonant level model. We have verified our numerical results against analytical results for the zeroth and first order correction to the population in and out of equilibrium. We then have calculated the non-adiabatic entropy production out of equilibrium. We show that, at equilibrium, the friction, random force, and entropy production agree with each other. Out of equilibrium, friction, random force and entropy production are all correlation functions between different (generalized) random forces. Our results agree with the one from the master equation in the limit of weak couplings. Future work must study the non-adiabatic entropy production and higher order corrections go beyond simple resonant level model.
