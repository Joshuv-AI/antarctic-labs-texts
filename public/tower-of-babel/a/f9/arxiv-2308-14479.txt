A convergent interacting particle method for computing KPP front speeds in random flows Thanks: Submitted to the editors DATE.
Tan Zhang, Zhongjian Wang, Jack Xin, Zhiwen Zhang

Abstract
We aim to efficiently compute spreading speeds of reaction-diffusion-advection (RDA) fronts in divergence free random flows under the Kolmogorov-Petrovsky-Piskunov (KPP) nonlinearity. We study a stochastic interacting particle method (IPM) for the reduced principal eigenvalue (Lyapunov exponent) problem of an associated linear advection-diffusion operator with spatially random coefficients. The Fourier representation of the random advection field and the Feynman-Kac (FK) formula of the principal eigenvalue (Lyapunov exponent) form the foundation of our method implemented as a genetic evolution algorithm. The particles undergo advection-diffusion, and mutation/selection through a fitness function originated in the FK semigroup. We analyze convergence of the algorithm based on operator splitting, present numerical results on representative flows such as 2D cellular flow and 3D Arnold-Beltrami-Childress (ABC) flow under random perturbations. The 2D examples serve as a consistency check with semi-Lagrangian computation. The 3D results demonstrate that IPM, being mesh free and self-adaptive, is simple to implement and efficient for computing front spreading speeds in the advection-dominated regime for high-dimensional random flows on unbounded domains where no truncation is needed.

Front propagation in complex fluid flows is a nonlinear phenomenon commonly found in many scientific and technological areas such as chemical reaction fronts in liquid, transport in porous media, and turbulence combustion
[

2
Efficient IPM for KPP front speeds

We develop the interacting particle (Lagrangian) methods (IPM) for computing
c^{*}
in random flows.

2.1
Computing
\mu
by FK semigroup

Let us consider
\bm{v}=\bm{v}(\bm{x},\omega)
,
a mean zero and divergence free flow field, to be constructed by a sum of deterministic space periodic term and a stochastic perturbation term.
The stochastic perturbation term is mean zero, stationary, ergodic, and has a specific energy spectral density. Here
\omega
is an element of the probability space describing all possible environments and we assume that the realizations of
\bm{v}(\bm{x},\omega)
are almost surely divergence free. To compute the KPP front speed
c^{*}(\bm{z})
along direction
\bm{z}
, we denote
q
as the solution to the linearized corrector equation with
(\bm{z},\bm{e})>0
:

q_{t}=\mathcal{A}\,q:=\kappa\Delta_{\bm{x}}q+(-2\kappa\lambda\bm{e}+\bm{v})\cdot\nabla_{\bm{x}}q+(\kappa\lambda^{2}-\lambda\bm{v}\cdot\bm{e}+f^{\prime}(0))q,

(3)

with compactly supported non-negative initial condition
q(\bm{x},0)\not\equiv 0
. Recall that the minimal front speed in
\bm{z}
is:
c^{*}(\bm{z})=\inf_{(\bm{z},\lambda\bm{e})>0}\mu(\lambda\bm{e})/(\bm{z},\lambda\bm{e})
, where

\mu(\lambda\bm{e})=\lim_{t\to+\infty}\frac{1}{t}\ln\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}q(\bm{x},t)d\bm{x},

(4)

also known as the principal Lyapunov exponent of (
3
). To design the interacting particle (Lagrangian) methods, we split the operator
\mathcal{A}
into
\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{L}+\mathcal{C}
, where
\mathcal{L}
and
\mathcal{C}
are defined by:

\mathcal{L}:=\kappa\Delta_{\bm{x}}+(-2\kappa\lambda\bm{e}+\bm{v})\cdot\nabla_{\bm{x}},

(5)

\mathcal{C}:=c(\bm{x},\omega)=(\kappa\lambda^{2}-\lambda\bm{v}\cdot\bm{e}+f^{\prime}(0)).

(6)

Then for each random realization, to approximate the operator
\mathcal{L}
, we define the following SDE system:

dX_{s}^{t_{2},t_{1},\bm{x}}=\bm{b}(X_{s}^{t_{2},t_{1},\bm{x}})\,ds+\sqrt{2\kappa}\,d\,\textbf{w}(s),\quad X_{t_{1}}^{t_{2},t_{1},\bm{x}}=\bm{x},\quad t_{2}\geq s\geq t_{1},

(7)

where
\bm{b}=-2\kappa\lambda\bm{e}+\bm{v}
is the drift term,

\textbf{w}(t)
is a
d
-dimensional Brownian motion independent of
\omega
.
The FK formula
is:

\mu(\lambda\bm{e})=\lim_{t\to+\infty}\frac{1}{t}\ln\mathbb{E}\Big(\exp\big(\int_{0}^{t}c(X_{s}^{t,0,\bm{x}})ds\big)\Big).

(8)

Here the expectation is over the probability space induced by the
d
-dimensional Brownian motion
\textbf{w}(t)
. For a given
\bm{e}
and its corresponding operator
\mathcal{A}
in the following analysis, we denote
\mu(\lambda)=\mu(\lambda\bm{e})
for simplicity. Only during the computation of front speed
c^{*}
, we search
\bm{e}
to minimize
\mu(\lambda\bm{e})/(\bm{z},\lambda\bm{e})
, subject to
(\bm{z},\bm{e})>0
.

Comparing the computation in
formulas (
4
) and (
8
), we see that the FK formula provides an alternative strategy to design (Lagrangian) particle methods to compute
\mu(\lambda)
and overcome the drawbacks in the traditional Eulerian methods, especially in the cases when the magnitude of
\bm{v}
is large or the dimensional
d
is high (e.g.
d=3
). Directly applying the FK formula (
8
) to is unstable as the sample paths visiting the maximal points of the potential
c
make the main contribution to the expectation
\mathbb{E}
in (
8
). This may lead to inaccurate or even divergent results. A more accurate strategy for the computation of
\mu(\lambda)
is to study the convergence of the FK group associated with the above SDEs (
7
) and the potential
c
. Specifically, let
\mathcal{D}
denote the space where the system lies in,
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{D})
denote the set of probability measures over
\mathcal{D}
and
S=\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(\mathcal{D})
. Then let
Q_{\cdot,\cdot}
denote the evolution operator which is associated with the process
X_{s}^{t_{2},t_{1},\bm{x}}
in the SDE system i.e. for any measure
m\in P(\mathcal{D})
, function
\phi\in S
, we have

m(Q_{t_{2},t_{1}}\phi)=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{x}\sim m}\big(\phi(X_{t_{2}}^{t_{2},t_{1},\bm{x}})\big).

(9)

Similarly, we define the associated weighted counterpart as

m(Q_{t_{2},t_{1}}^{c}\phi)=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{x}\sim m}\big(\phi(X_{t_{2}}^{t_{2},t_{1},\bm{x}})\exp(\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}c(X_{s}^{t_{2},t_{1},\bm{x}})ds)\big).

(10)

The
\mathcal{L}(t_{1})
and
\mathcal{A}(t_{1})
are the infinitesimal generators of two evolution operator
Q_{t_{2},t_{1}}
and
Q_{t_{2},t_{1}}^{c}
. By the definition of
Q_{t_{2},t_{1}}^{c}
and as in
[
4
,
2
]
, we define the FK operator
\Phi_{t_{2},t_{1}}^{c}
by:

\Phi_{t_{2},t_{1}}^{c}(m)(\phi)=\frac{m(Q_{t_{2},t_{1}}^{c}\phi)}{m(Q_{t_{2},t_{1}}^{c}1)}=\frac{\mathbb{E}_{\bm{x}\sim m}\big(\phi(X_{t_{2}}^{t_{2},t_{1},\bm{x}})\exp(\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}c(X_{s}^{t_{2},t_{1},\bm{x}})ds)\big)}{\mathbb{E}_{\bm{x}\sim m}\big(\exp(\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}c(X_{s}^{t_{2},t_{1},\bm{x}})ds)\big)}.

(11)

Due to steady velocity fields here, we denote
Q_{t,0}^{c}=Q_{t}^{c}
and
\Phi_{t,0}^{c}=\Phi_{t}^{c}
. One can easily verify the following property of the FK semigroup
\Phi_{t}^{c}

[
15
]
.

Proposition 2.1
.

For any
m\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{D})
and
\phi\in S
, there exists
C_{a}>0
such that

\Big|\Phi^{c}_{t}(m)(\phi)-\int_{\Omega}\phi dm_{c}\Big|\leq C_{a}||\phi||\exp(-\delta_{c}t),

(12)

where
\delta_{c}=\inf\{\mu(\lambda)-\Re(z):z\in\sigma(\mathcal{A})\setminus\{\mu(\lambda)\}\}>0
is the spectral gap of the operator
\mathcal{A}
,
m_{c}
is the invariant measure of
\Phi^{c}_{t}
.

The exponential decay property of the FK operator
\Phi_{t}^{c}
in the above proposition allows us to establish the existence of invariant measure
m_{c}
of
\Phi_{t}^{c}
from a simple initial measure
m
(uniform or Gaussian) after long enough evolution.

2.2
Random fields realization

We use random Fourier methods
[
16
]
for producing a random velocity field
v_{F}(x)
to approximate random velocity field
v_{r}(x,\omega)
in some stochastic sense. And we also require
v_{F}(x)
can be described by combinations between a finite number of random variables to allow it to be easily realized during the calculation. This approximation method can be verified by computing the average of mean-square displacement among
N_{r}
independent realizations
\{v_{F}^{j}\}_{j=1}^{N_{r}}
of the approximate velocity fields which are generated by successive calls to the random number generator (different random seeds).
For a random velocity field with spectral density
E(k)
, its stochastic Fourier integral representation is

v_{r}(x)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-2\pi ikx}E^{1/2}(|k|)d\tilde{W}(k),

(13)

where
d\tilde{W}(k)
is a complex Gaussian white noise. By Riemann sum approximation over a finite partition of
2N_{F}+1
equal width intervals of width
\Delta k
, we define
k_{max}=(N_{F}+\frac{1}{2})\Delta k
. This partition extends over a finite interval
[-k_{max},k_{max}]
. Using the midpoint of each sub-interval to evaluate the integrand, we get the following Riemann-Stieltjes sum for the approximating velocity field:

v_{F}(x)=\sum_{j=-N_{F}}^{N_{F}}e^{-2\pi ij\Delta kx}E^{1/2}(|j|\Delta k)\Delta\tilde{W}_{j}.

(14)

Here the complex random variables
\Delta\tilde{W}_{j}(k)
are defined in terms of the complex white noise process:

\Delta\tilde{W}_{j}(k)=\int_{j-\Delta k/2}^{j+\Delta k/2}d\tilde{W}(k),

(15)

where
\{\Delta\tilde{W}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{N_{F}}
are statistically independent complex Gaussian variables and
\Delta\tilde{W}_{0}
is a real Gaussian variable with mean zero and variance
\Delta k
independent from all other variables. Therefore we can transform the equation (
14
) into:

v_{F}(x)=E^{1/2}(0)\Delta\tilde{W}_{0}+2\textbf{Re}\sum_{j=1}^{N_{F}}e^{-2\pi ij\Delta kx}E^{1/2}(|j|\Delta k)\Delta\tilde{W}_{j},

(16)

which shares the same distribution. Here
Re
denotes the real part of the following expression. By expanding the complex random variable
\Delta\tilde{W}_{j}
into real and imaginary parts, we obtain a concise expression of the approximate velocity field as a discrete sum of real Fourier modes. The approximate velocity field is:

v_{F}(x)=\sum_{j=0}^{N_{F}}\sqrt{2E(k_{j})\Delta k_{j}}[\zeta_{j}\cos(2\pi k_{j}x)+\eta_{j}\sin(2\pi k_{j}x)].

(17)

Here
k_{j}=j\Delta k
denote the locations of equally spaced grid points
and
\Delta k_{j}=\Delta k
for
j=1,2,...,N_{F}
and
\Delta k_{0}=\frac{1}{2}\Delta k
. The
\{\zeta_{j}\}_{j=0}^{N_{F}}
and
\{\eta_{j}\}_{j=0}^{N_{F}}
are independent Gaussian variables with mean zero and variance one.

The exact correlation function of random velocity field
v(x)
with given
E(k)
is:

R(x)=\langle v(x_{0})v(x_{0}+x)\rangle=2\int_{0}^{\infty}\cos(2\pi kx)E(k)dk.

(18)

The correlation function of the Fourier generated random field
v_{F}(x)
is

\displaystyle R_{F}(x)=

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{N_{F}}(\sqrt{2E(k_{j})\Delta k_{j}})^{2}[\cos(2\pi k_{j}x_{0})\cos(2\pi k_{j}(x+x_{0}))\ +

\displaystyle\sin(2\pi k_{j}x_{0})\sin(2\pi k_{j}(x+x_{0}))]=\sum_{j=0}^{N_{F}}2E(k_{j})\Delta k_{j}\cos(2\pi k_{j}x).

(19)

By comparing these two correlation functions (
18
) and (
19
), we find that
R_{F}(x)
is just the finite discrete expression of
R(x)
by discretization the Riemann integral. As the number of Fourier modes
N_{F}
gets larger and the step
\Delta k
gets smaller, this approximation will become more accurate. In our numerical experiments, we select these two parameters according to the correlation length of energy spectral density.

Numerical discretization and resampling techniques

3
Convergence analysis

We show the convergence of the interactive particle (Lagrangian) method in computing the KPP front speed of the random perturbation velocity field. In
[
15
]
, the authors have given the convergence analysis of the cases with deterministic velocity field and bounded domain. Here we want to focus on the cases with a randomly perturbed velocity field and a bounded domain
\mathcal{D}
. Due to each random realization, the realized velocity field will perform as a deterministic case, so we divide the analysis into four parts. The first part studies the approximation of the random field with specific energy spectral density by the random Fourier method. The second part studies the error estimate of an operator splitting method to approximate the evolution of parabolic operators. The third part studies the error estimate of the interacting particle (Lagrangian) method in
\mu
computation
The fourth part studies the error estimate in computing
\mu
via the random Fourier method.

3.1
Approximating random field
with a prescribed energy spectra by random Fourier method

Here we first represent the linearized corrector KPP equation (
3
) into a non-autonomous parabolic equation:

q_{t}=\kappa\Delta_{\bm{x}}q+\bm{b}(\bm{x},\omega)\cdot\nabla_{\bm{x}}q+c(\bm{x},\omega)q,\quad\bm{x}=(x_{1},...,x_{d})^{T}\in\mathcal{D},\quad t\in[0,T],

(33)

with the initial condition
q(\bm{x},0)=q_{0}
,
\bm{b}(\bm{x},\omega)=-2\kappa\lambda\bm{e}+\bm{v}
,
c(\bm{x},\omega)=\kappa\lambda^{2}-\lambda\bm{v}\cdot\bm{e}+f^{\prime}(0)
and

is the computational time.
For each realization of the random field, our strategy is to generate an approximation
\bm{v}^{\prime}(\bm{x})
to the random velocity field,
\bm{v}(\bm{x},\omega)
, with long-range correlation using methods that can be used with confidence to predict some important scaling laws and can simulate the spreading of particles accurately and efficiently. And
\bm{v}^{\prime}(x)
is composed of two parts, the same deterministic part in
\bm{v}(\bm{x},\omega)
and the approximated velocity field
\bm{v}_{F}
. Here we use the simplest tracer statistic of the sort to measure the spreading of the particles.
In the SDE system (
7
)

d\bm{X}_{s}=\bm{b}(\bm{X}_{s},\omega)ds+\sqrt{2\kappa}d\textbf{w}(s),\quad\bm{X}_{0}=\bm{x}^{\prime},

(34)

here
\textbf{w}(s)
is
d
-dimensional standard Brownian motion and its variance at time
s
in each dimensional is
2\kappa s
. The randomly perturbed term
\bm{b}(\bm{X}_{s},\omega)
is independent of
\textbf{w}(s)
, so their variances are additional. Also we know that
\bm{b}(\bm{X}_{s},\omega)=\bm{b}_{0}(\bm{X}_{s})+\bm{v}_{r}(\bm{X}_{s},\omega)
and here
\bm{b}_{0}(\bm{X}_{s})
is the deterministic part. Due to
\bm{v}_{r}
is mean zero, so
\langle\bm{b}(\bm{X}_{s},\omega)\rangle=\bm{b}_{0}(\bm{X}_{s})

\langle\cdot\rangle
denotes the average over probability space induced by
\omega
. And the covariance function between two points
\bm{X}_{s},\bm{X}_{s}+\bm{x}
can be computed by:

\displaystyle\langle(\bm{b}(\bm{X}_{s},\omega)-\bm{b}_{0}(\bm{X}_{s}))\cdot(\bm{b}(\bm{X}_{s}+\bm{x},\omega)-\bm{b}_{0}(\bm{X}_{s}+\bm{x}))\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\bm{v}_{r}(\bm{X}_{s},\omega)\cdot\bm{v}_{r}(\bm{X}_{s}+\bm{x},\omega)\rangle

\displaystyle=R(\bm{x}).

(35)

In our following analysis and experiments, the random perturbation is only a one-dimensional perturbation. For example, in 2D cases,
\bm{v}_{r}(\bm{x},\omega)=(0,v_{r}(x,\omega))
. For a zero mean, Gaussian random velocity field
v_{r}(x,\omega)
, it is completely characterized by its two-point correlation function,
R(x)=\left\langle v_{r}(x_{0},\omega)v_{r}(x_{0}+x,\omega)\right\rangle

R(x)=\left\langle v_{r}(x_{0},\omega)v_{r}(x_{0}+x,\omega)\right\rangle=\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\cos(kx)E(k)dk.

(36)

where
E(k)
is the energy spectral density of the random velocity field

. Then we know that the mean square displacement is also completely determined by the correlation function
R(x)
. So the important thing is that the generated velocity field’s correlation function
R_{F}(x)
should be an approximation to the true random velocity field’s correlation function
R(x)
. Now we move our focus on the random Fourier method to generate an approximation to the random velocity field.

Following the generation method mentioned in Section
2.2
, to make the random Fourier method accurate at low frequency, we would require the energy spectral density

to satisfy the following properties.

P1.

E(k)
is integrable. Since
E(k)
is non-negative, this also implies
E(k)\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R})
.

P2.

E(k)
is finite at zero point, i.e.
E(0)<+\infty
.

P3.

E(k),E^{\prime}(k),E^{\prime\prime}(k)
are all bounded a.e.

P4.

Let

. For
\forall C_{g}>0
,
\exists y_{0}>0
,
\forall y>y_{0},g(y)<C_{g}/y^{2}
, i.e.
g(y)
is
o(y^{-2})
as

goes to infinity.

From properties
P1
to

, we can also imply that the correlation function

is always finite.
Then by comparing these two correlation functions (
18

and the number of Fourier mode

For a specific

\displaystyle e_{tru}

\displaystyle=\big|2\int_{0}^{\infty}\cos(2\pi kx)E(k)dk-2\int_{0}^{k_{max}}\cos(2\pi kx)E(k)dk\big|

\displaystyle=2\big|\int_{k_{max}}^{\infty}\cos(2\pi kx)E(k)dk\big|<g(k_{max}).

(37)

And the discretization error

can be represented by:

e_{dis}=\big|2\int_{0}^{k_{max}}\cos(2\pi kx)E(k)dk-\sum_{j=0}^{N_{F}}2E(k_{j})\Delta k_{j}\cos(2\pi k_{j}x)\big|.

(38)

So that we can see that the error

can be bounded by

e_{R}=|R(x)-R_{F}(x)|\leq e_{tru}+e_{dis}<e_{dis}+g(k_{max}).

(39)

Lemma 1
.

For random velocity field

(41)

From the random Fourier method mentioned in Section

the multi-dimensional modulo operation.

\mathbb{R}^{d}

5
Conclusions

We developed an efficient interacting particle method (IPM) to compute KPP front speeds
c^{*}
in 2D and 3D stochastic flows, with convergence analysis based on the random Fourier method.
We first computed
c^{*}

in periodic flows, including 2D cellular flow and 3D Arnold-Beltrami-Childress (ABC) flow, to reach agreement with semi-Lagrangian and spectral methods.
Then we computed and analyzed the effect of random perturbations on
c^{*}
.
As the perturbation strengths increase,
we observed that the vortical structures (ballistic orbits)
[
3
,
37
,
18
,
9
]
in 3D ABC flow are collapsing,
resulting in smaller
c^{*}
.
Compared with mesh based methods, IPM
has several striking advantages: (1) it is scalable in spatial dimension and can handle stochastic 3D problems at ease;
(2) it is scalable in domain size and can handle infinite domain as well as Lyapunov exponent approximation directly.
In experiments on
\mathbb{R}^{3}
,
the empirical particle distributions from IPM show convergence under dynamic normalization while the average particle fitness approximates the Lyapunov exponent.

Appendix A
Convergence of perturbed
\mu

We review
perturbation theory of linear operators
with the following notation:

T(a)=T+a\cdot T^{\prime}.

(91)

Here
T
is the original operator,
T^{\prime}
is the perturbation operator and
a
is the coefficient of the perturbation term. Then we have the following property

Proposition A.1
.

Let
T(a)
be continuous at
a=0
, then the eigenvalues of
T(a)
are continuous at
a=0
. If
\lambda
is an eigenvalue of
T(0)=T
, then the
\lambda
-group is well-defined for sufficiently small
|a|
and the total projection
P(a)
for the
\lambda
-group is continuous at
a=0
.

Next, we would like to introduce some notations we use to describe the convergence between operators in the generalized sense.

Definition A.2
.

Consider closed linear manifolds
M
and
N
of a Banach space
Z
. We use
S_{M}
to denote the unit sphere of
M
(the set of all
v\in M
which satisfy
||v||=1
). For any two closed linear manifolds
M,N
of
Z
, we define

\delta(M,N)=\sup_{u\in S_{M}}dist(u,M),

(92)

\tilde{\delta}(M,N)=\max[\delta(M,N),\delta(N,M)]^{1}.

(93)

Definition A.3
.

We denote that
T_{n}
converge to
T
in the generalized sense if
\tilde{\delta}(T_{n},T)\to 0
.

There is a proposition that is a sufficient condition for the generalized convergence and its proof can be found in
[
10
]
.

Proposition A.4
.

Let
T\in\mathcal{C}(X,Y)
. Let
A_{n},n=0,1,2,\cdots
, be
T
-bounded so that
||A_{n}v||\leq a_{n}||v||+b_{n}||Tv||
for
v\in D(T)\subset D(A_{n})
. If
a_{n}\to 0
and
b_{n}\to 0
, then
T_{n}=T+A_{n}\in\mathcal{C}(X,Y)
for sufficiently large
n
and
T_{n}\to T
in the generalized sense.

Next, we would like to review some results which can make the above propositions applicable to an infinite system. Here we use
\Sigma(T)
to denote the spectrum of the operator
T\in\mathcal{C}(X)
and we suppose that the spectrum
\Sigma(T)
has an isolated point
\lambda
. Then obviously we can divide
\Sigma(T)
into two parts
\Sigma^{\prime}(T)
and
\Sigma^{\prime\prime}(T)
. Here
\Sigma^{\prime}(T)
consists of the single point
\lambda
and any simple closed curve
\Gamma
enclosing
\lambda
but no other point in
\Sigma(T)
may be chosen. Following this idea, we can divide the spectrum of an infinity system and focus on the finite system
\Sigma^{\prime}(T)
which contains the principal eigenvalue and we have the following proposition to analyze the continuity of a finite system of eigenvalues.

Proposition A.5
.

The change of a finite system
\Sigma^{\prime}(T)
of eigenvalues of a closed operator
T
is small (in the sense of
A.1
) when
T
is subjected to a small perturbation in the sense of generalized convergence.

Appendix B
Computing upper bounds in the assumptions

From the random Fourier method mentioned in Section
2.2
, the approximated velocity field
\bm{v}_{F}
generated by (

R_{F}

In the above convergence analysis in

\bm{b}_{0}(\bm{x})

||\bm{b}(\bm{x},\omega)||_{L^{2}}\leq||\bm{b}_{0}(\bm{x})||_{L^{2}}+||\bm{v}_{r}(\bm{x},\omega)||_{L^{2}}\leq M_{1}+||\bm{v}_{r}(\bm{x},\omega)||_{L^{2}}.

Here we give the analysis for 2D cases as an example, a similar analysis can be done for higher dimensions. Here
\bm{x}=(x,y)
and due to
\bm{v}_{r}
is a 1D random perturbation, then we have
\bm{v}_{r}(\bm{x},\omega)=(0,v_{r}(x,\omega))

v_{r}(x,\omega)\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R})\cap L^{2}(\mathbb{R})

L

\mathcal{D}=[0,L]^{2}
.
Then for the term
||\bm{v}_{r}(\bm{x},\omega)||_{L^{2}}

\mathbb{E}_{\omega}[||\bm{v}_{r}(\bm{x},\omega)||_{L^{2}}^{2}]\leq\mathbb{E}_{\omega}[\int_{0}^{L}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}|v_{r}(x,\omega)|^{2}dxdy]=L\cdot\mathbb{E}_{\omega}[\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}|v_{r}(x,\omega)|^{2}dx].

(95)

We denote
\widehat{v_{r}}(k,\omega):=\mathcal{F}[v_{r}](k)
, the Fourier transform of
v_{r}(x,\omega)
. From the definition of energy spectral density, we know that
E(k)=\mathbb{E}_{\omega}[|\widehat{v_{r}}(k,\omega)|^{2}]
. Then by Plancherel theorem, we can get the following relationship.

L\cdot\mathbb{E}_{\omega}[\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}|v_{r}(x,\omega)|^{2}dx]=L\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\mathbb{E}_{\omega}[|\widehat{v_{r}}(k,\omega)|^{2}]dk=L\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}E(k)dk=R(0)L.

(96)

Then we can get
\mathbb{E}_{\omega}[||\bm{b}(\bm{x},\omega)||_{L^{2}}]\leq M_{1}+\sqrt{R(0)L}
. And we know the variance
Var(||\bm{b}(\bm{x},\omega)||_{L^{2}})

\leq\mathbb{E}_{\omega}[||\bm{v}_{r}||_{L^{2}}^{2}]\leq R(0)L
. Then for a probability
p
close to 1, we can find the corresponding upper bound
M_{1,r}
, so that
P(||\bm{b}(\bm{x},\omega)||_{L^{2}}<M_{1,r})>p
.
For
\bm{v}_{F}
, it will have a period
1/\Delta k
in dimension

, then we can give the analysis for
