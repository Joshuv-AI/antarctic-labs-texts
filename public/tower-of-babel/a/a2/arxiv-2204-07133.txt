FUNDAMENTAL SOLUTION OF THE VLADMIROV-TAIBLESON OPERATOR ON NONCOMMUTATIVE VILENKIN GROUPS Thanks: The authors are supported by the FWO Odysseus 1 grant G.0H94.18N: Analysis and Partial Differential Equations.
Julio Delgado and J.P. Velasquez-Rodriguez

Abstract
. The fundamental solution and the heat semigroup of the Vladimirov-Taibleson operator on constant-order noncommutative Vilenkin groups are obtained, together with some estimates on the associated heat kernel. We also show the existence of a fundamental solution for the ”Vladimirov Laplacian” on the p p -adic Heisenberg group and the p p -adic Engel group, and discuss possible extensions of our results to more general homogeneous operators on graded p p -adic Lie groups.

1.
Preliminaries and statement of the main results

In this section we provide some basic definitions and the main properties on locally compact Vilenkin groups that we will require. Our main goal here is to explain in detail our results and the way they extend to the noncommutative case the well known results about the existence of a fundamental solution for the Vladimirov-Taibleson operator and the Vladimirov Laplacian on
\mathbb{Q}_{p}^{d}
. The starting point is to recall the formal definition of the class of groups we are about to study:

Definition 1.1
.

We say that a topological group
G
is a
locally compact Vilenkin group
if
G
is a locally compact, Hausdorff, totally disconnected, unimodular, type I topological group, endowed with a strictly decreasing sequence of compact open subgroups
\mathscr{G}=\{G_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
such that

(i)

It holds:

|G_{n}/G_{n+1}|<\infty,

for every
n\in\mathbb{Z}.

(ii)

G=\bigcup_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}G_{n},\text{ }\text{ }\text{and}\text{ }\text{ }\bigcap_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}G_{n}=\{e\}.

(iii)

The sequence
\{G_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
form a basis of neighbourhoods at
e\in G
. If we also have

\sup_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}|G_{k}/G_{k+1}|<\infty,

we say that
G
is a bounded-order Vilenkin group. If
|G_{k}/G_{k+1}|
is constant we say that
G
is a constant-order Vilenkin group.

Vilenkin groups are locally profinite groups endowed with a sequence of compact open subgroups that forms a basis of neighbourhoods at the identity. The reason to consider such sequence is because by the Birkhoff-Kakutani theorem its existence guaranties the metrisability of these groups, and the obtained metric is compatible with the group structure.

Definition 1.2
.

Let
G
be a locally compact Vilenkin group with a sequence of compact open subgroups
\mathscr{G}=\{G_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
. For
x,y\in G
the distance between
x
and
y
is defined as:

\varrho_{\mathscr{G}}(x,y)=|xy^{-1}|_{\mathscr{G}}:=\begin{cases}0&\text{ }\text{ }\text{if}\text{ }x=y,\\
|G_{n}|&\text{ }\text{ }\text{if}\text{ }xy^{-1}\in G_{n}\setminus G_{n+1}.\end{cases}

In this work we will consider exclusively constant-order Vilenkin groups, let us say
|G_{k}/G_{k+1}|=\varkappa
for all
k\in\mathbb{Z}
, so that the metric on the group takes the form

\varrho_{\mathscr{G}}(x,y)=|xy^{-1}|_{\mathscr{G}}:=\begin{cases}0&\text{ }\text{ }\text{if}\text{ }x=y,\\
\varkappa^{-n}&\text{ }\text{ }\text{if}\text{ }xy^{-1}\in G_{n}\setminus G_{n+1}.\end{cases}

Some examples of constant-order locally compact Vilenkin groups are:

Example 1.3
.

The field of
p
-adic numbers
G=\mathbb{Q}_{p}
, where
p
is a prime number, together with the sequence of compact open subgroups

G_{n}:=\{x\in\mathbb{Q}_{p}\text{ }:|x|_{p}\leq p^{-n}\}=p^{n}\mathbb{Z}_{p}.

More generally, the additive group of any nonarchimedean local field
\mathbb{K}
, together with the compact open balls associated to the ultrametric topology of the field, are examples of locally compact abelian Vilenkin groups. The Vladimirov operator, which gives the analogue of fractional derivative in this setting, was originally studied in this framework and, for
\alpha\in\mathbb{C}
with
\mathfrak{Re}(\alpha)>0
, it is defined as

D^{\alpha}f(x):=\frac{1-p^{\alpha}}{1-p^{-\alpha-1}}\int_{\mathbb{Q}_{p}}\frac{f(x-y)-f(x)}{|y|_{p}^{\alpha+1}}dy,

acting initially on locally constant functions with compact support. Notice that for any
x,y\in G=\mathbb{Q}_{p}
, the distance function
|\cdot|_{\mathscr{G}}
associated to
\mathscr{G}=\{G_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
has the property
|x-y|_{\mathscr{G}}=|x-y|_{p}
, so that we can write

D^{\alpha}f(x):=\frac{1-p^{\alpha}}{1-p^{-\alpha-1}}\int_{\mathbb{Q}_{p}}\frac{f(x-y)-f(x)}{|y|_{\mathscr{G}}^{\alpha+1}}dy.

Example 1.4
.

Let
p\neq 2
be a prime number. We will denote by
\mathbb{H}_{d}(\mathbb{Q}_{p})
, or simply
\mathbb{H}_{d}
for short, the
2d+1
-dimensional Heisenberg group over
\mathbb{Q}_{p}
, in turn defined as

\mathbb{H}_{d}(\mathbb{Q}_{p})=\left\{\begin{bmatrix}1&x^{t}&z\\
0&I_{d}&y\\
0&0&1\end{bmatrix}\in GL_{d+2}(\mathbb{Q}_{p})\text{ }:\text{ }x,y\in\mathbb{Q}_{p}^{d},\text{ }z\in\mathbb{Q}_{p}\right\}.

\mathbb{H}_{d}
is an analytic
d
-dimensional manifold locally homeomorphic to
\mathbb{Q}_{p}^{2d+1}
and the operations on
\mathbb{H}_{d}
are analytic functions so
\mathbb{H}_{d}
is a
p
-adic Lie group. Let us denote by
\mathfrak{h}_{d}
its associated Lie algebra. We can write explicitly

\mathfrak{h}_{d}(\mathbb{Q}_{p})=\left\{\begin{bmatrix}0&\textbf{a}^{t}&c\\
0&0_{d}&\textbf{b}\\
0&0&0\end{bmatrix}\in\mathcal{M}_{d+2}(\mathbb{Q}_{p})\text{ }:\text{ }\textbf{a},\textbf{b}\in\mathbb{Q}_{p}^{d},c\in\mathbb{Q}_{p}\right\},

which is a nilpotent Lie algebra over
\mathbb{Q}_{p}
. This implies that the global convergence of the exponential map, and for any
u\in\mathfrak{h}_{d}
, let us say

u:=\begin{bmatrix}0&\textbf{a}^{t}&c\\
0&0_{d}&\textbf{b}\\
0&0&0\end{bmatrix},

the exponential map evaluates to

\mathbb{exp}(u)=\begin{bmatrix}1&\textbf{a}^{t}&c+\frac{1}{2}\textbf{a}\cdot\textbf{b}\\
0&I_{d}&\textbf{b}\\
0&0&1\end{bmatrix}.

The exponential map transform sub-ideals of the Lie algebra
\mathfrak{h}_{d}
to subgroups of
\mathbb{H}_{d}
. Actually, we can turn the exponential map into a group homomorphism by using the Baker–Campbell–Hausdorff formula. Let us define the operation “
\diamond
” on
\mathfrak{h}_{d}
by

X\diamond Y:=X+Y+\frac{1}{2}[X,Y].

Then, clearly
(\mathbb{H}_{d}(\mathbb{Q}_{p}),\times)\cong(\mathfrak{h}_{d}(\mathbb{Q}_{p}),\diamond)
, where ”
\times
” is the matrix product, is a locally profinite topological group, and it can be endowed with the sequence of subgroups
G_{n}:=(\mathbb{exp}(p^{n}\mathfrak{h}_{d}(\mathbb{Z}_{p})),\times)\cong(p^{n}\mathfrak{h}_{d}(\mathbb{Z}_{p}),\diamond)
, where

p^{n}\mathfrak{h}_{d}(\mathbb{Z}_{p})=p^{n}\mathbb{Z}_{p}X_{1}+...+p^{n}\mathbb{Z}_{p}X_{d}+p^{n}\mathbb{Z}_{p}Y_{1}+...+p^{n}\mathbb{Z}_{p}Y_{d}+p^{2n}\mathbb{Z}_{p}Z,

so
(\mathfrak{h}_{d}(\mathbb{Q}_{p}),*)\cong\mathbb{H}_{d}(\mathbb{Q}_{p})
is a locally compact Vilenkin group. Here
X_{1},...,Y_{d},Z
are the usual
\mathbb{Q}_{p}
-basis for
\mathfrak{h}_{d}
. An alternative definition for
\mathbb{H}_{d}(\mathbb{Q}_{p})
can be given in terms of symplectic vector spaces. Let
V
be a
2d
-dimensional vector space over the field
F=\mathbb{Q}_{p}
so that
V\cong\mathbb{Q}_{p}^{2d}
. Let
\langle,\rangle_{V}
be a non-degenerated symplectic form. The Heisenberg group associated to
V
is the group with underlying set
V\times F
and the following group operation:

(x,y,z)\cdot(x^{\prime},y^{\prime},z^{\prime}):=(x+x^{\prime},y+y^{\prime},z+z^{\prime}+\langle(x,y),(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\rangle,\text{ }\text{ }\text{ }(x,y),(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\in V\cong\mathbb{Q}_{p}^{d}\times\mathbb{Q}_{p}^{d}.

One can see that the previous definition and the initial definition are equivalent in the sense they define isomorphic groups.

Example 1.5
.

More generally, let
p
be a prime number,
p\neq 2
, and let
\mathbb{K}
be nonarchimedean local field with ring of integers
\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{K}}
, prime ideal
{\mathfrak{p}}=\mathscr{p}\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{K}}
and residue field
\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{K}}/{\mathfrak{p}}\cong\mathbb{F}_{q}
, where
q=p^{[\mathbb{K}:\mathbb{Q}_{p}]}
. Let
\mathfrak{g}
be a nilpotent
\mathbb{K}
-Lie algebra. We say that
\mathfrak{g}
is
graded
if there exists a decomposition of the form

\mathfrak{g}=\bigoplus_{k=1}^{r^{\prime}}V_{k},\text{ }\text{ }\text{ }[V_{i},V_{j}]\subseteq V_{i+j}.

Let
V_{\nu_{1}},...,V_{\nu_{r}}
the non-trivial subspaces appearing in the above decomposition, so that

\mathfrak{g}=\bigoplus_{k=1}^{r}V_{\nu_{k}},\text{ }\text{ }b_{k}:=dim(V_{\nu_{k}}).

We will call the number
Q:=\nu_{1}b_{1}+\nu_{2}b_{2}+...+\nu_{r}b_{r}
the homogeneous dimension of
\mathfrak{g}
. In particular, if the elements of the first subspace appearing in the decomposition of
\mathfrak{g}
generate the whole algebra, we say that
\mathfrak{g}
is
stratified
. If the Lie algebra of a nilpotent Lie group
G
is graded, we say that
G
is a
graded Lie group
. When the Lie algebra is stratified we say that
G
is a
stratified group
.
A consequence of the graded structure on a
\mathbb{K}
-Lie algebra is the existence of dilations on the group. That is, there exists a family of linear mappings
D_{\gamma}:\mathfrak{g}\to\mathfrak{g}
,
\gamma\in\mathbb{K}^{*}
, satisfying :

-

the mappings
D_{\gamma}
are diagonalisable and each
V_{j}
is the eigenspace associated to one of the eigenvalues of
D_{\gamma}
.

-

each
D_{\gamma}
is a morphism of the Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
, that is, a linear mapping from
\mathfrak{g}
to itself which respects the Lie bracket:

[D_{\gamma}X,D_{\gamma}Y]=D_{\gamma}[X,Y],\text{ }\text{ }\text{for all}\text{ }X,Y\in\mathfrak{g},\text{ }\text{ }\text{and all}\text{ }\gamma\in\mathbb{K}^{*}.

The dilations are transported to the Lie group by the exponential map in the following way: the maps

\mathbb{exp}\text{ }\circ D_{\gamma}\circ\text{ }\mathbb{exp}^{-1},\text{ }\text{ }\gamma\in\mathbb{K}^{*},

are automorphisms of the group
G
. We will also denote by
D_{\gamma}
the dilations on the group. We may write

\gamma x:=D_{\gamma}(x).

A graded group
G
can be endowed with the structure of a locally compact Vilenkin group with a sequence compatible with the dilations. For doing so let
X_{1},...,X_{d}
be a
\mathbb{K}
-basis of eigenvectors in
\mathfrak{g}
associated to the dilations
D_{\gamma}
so
D_{\gamma}
is diagonal in this basis. Define

\mathfrak{g}_{0}:=\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{K}}X_{1}+...+\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{K}}X_{d},\text{ }\text{ }\text{and}\text{ }\text{ }G_{0}:=\mathbb{exp}(\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{K}}X_{1}+...+\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{K}}X_{d}).

Hence, if
|\gamma|_{\mathbb{K}}=q^{-n}
,

G_{n}:=\mathbb{exp}(\gamma\mathfrak{g}_{0})=D_{\gamma}(G_{0}),\text{ }\text{ }\gamma\mathfrak{g}_{0}=\mathscr{p}^{n\nu_{1}}\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{K}}X_{1}+...+\mathscr{p}^{n\nu_{r}}\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{K}}X_{d}=D_{\gamma}(\mathfrak{g}_{0}),

defines a sequence
\{G_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
of compact open sub-groups of
G
such that

G=\bigcup_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}G_{n},\text{ }\text{ }\text{ }\text{and}\text{ }\text{ }\text{ }\bigcap_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}G_{n}=\{e\}.

With the above filtration
G
is a constant Vilenkin group since

\displaystyle|G_{n}/G_{n+1}|

\displaystyle=|\mathfrak{g}_{n}/\mathfrak{g}_{n+1}|

\displaystyle=q^{\nu_{1}b_{1}+...+\nu_{r}b_{r}}

\displaystyle=q^{Q},

for all
n\in\mathbb{Z}
. For instance in the case
\mathbb{K}=\mathbb{Q}_{p}
and
G=\mathbb{H}_{d}(\mathbb{Q}_{p})
we can define the dilations

D_{\gamma}(x,y,z):=\mathbb{exp}(\gamma x_{1}X_{1}+...\gamma x_{d}X_{d}+\gamma y_{1}Y_{1}+...+\gamma y_{d}Y_{d}+\gamma^{2}zZ)=(\gamma x,\gamma y,\gamma^{2}z),

and the sequence of subgroups associated to this dilations, the same defined in Example
1.4
, make
\mathbb{H}_{d}(\mathbb{Q}_{p})
a constant-order Vilenkin group since

|G_{n}/G_{n+1}|=p^{2d+2},\text{ }\text{ }\text{for all}\text{ }\text{ }n\in\mathbb{Z}.

Example 1.6
.

Consider the
4
-dimensional
p
-adic Lie group
\mathbb{E}_{4}(\mathbb{Q}_{p})
, or
\mathbb{E}_{4}
for simplicity, which is defined here as the exponential image of the filiform
\mathbb{Q}_{p}
-Lie algebra
\mathfrak{e}_{4}
, called by some authors the Engel algebra, defined in terms of the
\mathbb{Q}_{p}
-basis
\{X,Y_{1},Y_{2},Y_{3}\}
and the commutation relations

[X,Y_{i}]=Y_{i+1},\text{ }\text{ }i=1,2,\text{ }\text{ }\text{and}\text{ }\text{ }\text{ }\text{ }[X,Y_{3}]=0.

We can think on
\mathfrak{e}_{4}
as the matrix algebra containing all the matrices of the form

\begin{bmatrix}0&x&0&y_{3}\\
0&0&x&y_{2}\\
0&0&0&y_{1}\\
0&0&0&0\end{bmatrix},\text{ }\text{ }\text{ }x,y_{1},y_{2},y_{3}\in\mathbb{Q}_{p}.

In this way
\mathbb{E}_{4}
, which is the exponential image of the
\mathfrak{e}_{4}
, is a nilpotent subgroup of
GL_{4}(\mathbb{Q}_{p})
that we call here
the Engel group
. With the coordinates

(x,y_{1},y_{2},y_{3}):=\mathbb{exp}(xX+y_{1}Y_{1}+y_{2}Y_{2}+y_{3}Y_{3}),

we can identify
\mathbb{E}_{4}
with
\mathbb{Q}_{p}^{4}
endowed with the group law:

\displaystyle(x,y_{1},y_{2},y_{3})\times(x^{\prime},y_{1}^{\prime},y_{2}^{\prime},y_{3}^{\prime}):=(x+x^{\prime},y_{1}+y_{1}^{\prime},y_{2}+y_{2}^{\prime}-xy_{1}^{\prime},y_{3}+y_{3}^{\prime}+\frac{1}{2}x^{2}y_{1}^{\prime}-xy_{2}^{\prime}).

Denote by ”
\diamond
” the BCH product on
\mathfrak{e}_{4}
. Then
\mathbb{E}_{4}\cong(\mathfrak{e}_{4},\diamond)
is a constant-order locally compact Vilenkin group since it can be endowed with the sequence of compact open subgroups
(G_{n},\times)\cong(p^{n}\mathfrak{e}_{4},\diamond)
where

G_{n}:=\mathbb{exp}(p^{n}\mathfrak{e}_{4}):=\mathbb{exp}(p^{n}\mathbb{Z}_{p}X+p^{n}\mathbb{Z}_{p}Y_{1}+p^{2n}\mathbb{Z}_{p}Y_{2}+p^{3n}\mathbb{Z}_{p}Y_{3}),

and it clearly holds
|G_{n}/G_{n+1}|=p^{7}
for all
n\in\mathbb{Z}
. Here
Q=7
is the homogeneous dimension of
\mathbb{E}_{4}
.

There are two ways to generalise the Vladimirov operator defined in Example
1.3
to the multidimensional case. The first one is the Taibleson operator, or the Vladimirov-Taibleson operator, a very special pseudo-differential operator with many works about its properties in the mathematical literature. It is defined via the formula

\mathscr{D}^{\alpha}f(x):=\frac{1-p^{\alpha}}{1-p^{-\alpha-d}}\int_{\mathbb{Q}_{p}^{d}}\frac{f(x-y)-f(x)}{\|y\|_{p}^{\alpha+d}}dy,\text{ }\text{ }\text{ }\mathfrak{Re}(\alpha)>0,

or equivalently via Fourier transform as

\mathscr{D}^{\alpha}f(x):=\int_{\mathbb{Q}_{p}^{d}}\|\xi\|_{p}^{\alpha}\widehat{f}(\xi)e^{2\pi i\{x\cdot\xi\}_{p}}d\xi,

acting initially, as before, on locally constant functions with compact support. This operator is an invariant operator on
\mathbb{Q}_{p}^{d}
whose convolution kernel, called the
mulidimensional Riesz kernel
, is given by the expression

\langle\mathfrak{r}_{\alpha},f\rangle=\frac{1-p^{-n}}{1-p^{\alpha-n}}f(0)+\frac{1-p^{-\alpha}}{1-p^{\alpha-n}}\int_{\|x\|_{p}>1}\|x\|_{p}^{\alpha-n}f(x)dx+\frac{1-p^{-\alpha}}{1-p^{\alpha-n}}\int_{\|x\|_{p}\leq 1}\|x\|_{p}^{\alpha-n}(f(x)-f(0))dx,

so that for
\mathfrak{Re}(\alpha)>0
and any locally constant function
f
with compact support it holds
\mathscr{D}^{\alpha}f=f*\mathfrak{r}_{-\alpha}
. See
[
23
]
for more details. Here we are using the notation

\|x\|_{p}=\max_{1\leq k\leq d}|x_{i}|_{p}.

The heat equation with the Vladimirov-Taibleson operator, or diffusion equation with
\mathscr{D}^{\alpha}
, is the parabolic equation

(1)

\frac{d}{dt}u(t,x)+\mathscr{D}^{\alpha}u(t,x)=0,\text{ }\text{ }t>0,\text{ }\text{ }x\in\mathbb{Q}_{p}^{d},

and its applications to mathemathical physics and other areas has been widely discussed in the literature. For example the reader can consult
[
2
]
where the connections between this and more general convolution operators with ultrametric diffusion models are explored. From the mathematical point of view, Equation (
1
) is interesting on its own and its fundamental solution, usually called
the heat kernel

h_{\alpha}(t,x)
associated to
\mathscr{D^{\alpha}}
, has some remarkable properties, for instance it is a positive function that defines a transition density of a right continuous strict Markov process without second kind discontinuities
[
23
]
. Another remarkable property of this kernel is the estimate

h_{\alpha}(t,x)\asymp\frac{t}{(t^{1/\alpha}+\|x\|_{p})^{\alpha+d}},\text{ }\text{ }\text{ }\text{for}\text{ }\alpha>0,

which in principle guaranties that for
0<\alpha<d
we can obtain the fundamental solution of
\mathscr{D}^{\alpha}
by integrating
h_{\alpha}(t,x)
in the variable
t
, as it is proven in
[
4
]
. Anyway, there are more general methods to obtain explicitly the fundamental solution for
\mathscr{D}^{\alpha}
, which is actually given by

E_{\alpha}(x)=\begin{cases}\frac{1-p^{-\alpha}}{1-p^{\alpha-d}}\|x\|_{p}^{\alpha-d},&\text{ }\text{ }\text{if}\text{ }\mathfrak{Re}(\alpha)\neq d,\\
\frac{1-p^{n}}{p^{n}\ln{p}}\ln{\|x\|_{p}},&\text{ }\text{ }\text{if}\text{ }\mathfrak{Re}(\alpha)=d,\end{cases}

but it is important to remark that for the case
\mathfrak{Re}(\alpha)\geq d
one obtains in principle just a
formal fundamental solution
, meaning that the linear functional
E_{\alpha}
is not well defined for every locally constant function with compact support. The reason is that via Fourier transform
\mathcal{F}_{\mathbb{Q}_{p}^{d}}E_{\alpha}(\xi)=\|\xi\|_{p}^{-\alpha}
but the function
\|\xi\|_{p}^{-\alpha}
is not locally integrable for
\mathfrak{Re}(\alpha)\geq d
. See
[
23
,
24
]
for more details about the Taibleson operator and its fundamental solution.

There is a natural way to generalise the above ideas to constant-order Vilenkin groups. Consider
G=\mathbb{Q}_{p}^{d}
together with the sequence of compact open subgroups

G_{n}:=\{x\in\mathbb{Q}_{p}\text{ }:\|x\|_{p}\leq p^{-n}\}=p^{n}\mathbb{Z}_{p}^{d}.

Then
\mathbb{Q}_{p}^{d}
is a constant-order Vilenkin group and for the metric associated to
\mathscr{G}=\{G_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
it holds
|x-y|_{\mathscr{G}}=\|x-y\|_{p}^{d}
. Accordingly, we can write the Taibleson operator in terms of the ultrametric
|\cdot|_{\mathscr{G}}
as:

\mathscr{D}^{\alpha}f(x):=\frac{1-p^{d(\alpha/d)}}{1-p^{-d(\alpha/d+1)}}\int_{\mathbb{Q}_{p}^{d}}\frac{f(x-y)-f(x)}{|y|_{\mathscr{G}}^{\alpha/d+1}}dx.

So actually, the Taibleson operator is a particular example of a class of operators on ultrametric spaces defined in terms a certain ultrametric function
[
4
]
that, in particular for Vilenkin groups, we choose as the ultrametric associated to a sequence of compact open subgroups. Hence we can ”extend” the study of the Vladimirov-Taibleson operator, in principle, to constant order Vilenkin groups simply by replacing in the original definition the
p
-adic norm with the natural ultrametric of the group.

Definition 1.7
.

Let
G
be a constant-order Vilenkin group, endowed with the sequence of compact open subgroups
\mathscr{G}=\{G_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
, let us write
|G_{n}/G_{n+1}|=\varkappa
for all
n\in\mathbb{Z}
. We define for
\mathfrak{Re}(\alpha)>0
the Vladimirov-Taibleson operator
D^{\alpha}
, acting initially on locally constant functions with compact support, via the formula:

D^{\alpha}f(x):=\frac{1-\varkappa^{\alpha}}{1-\varkappa^{-\alpha-1}}\int_{G}\frac{f(xy^{-1})-f(x)}{|y|_{\mathscr{G}}^{-\alpha-1}}dx.

One of our contributions in this paper is to compute the fundamental solution for the above defined operator on constant-order noncommutative Vilenkin groups, extending in this way to the noncommutative case the well known results for the Vladimirov-Taibleson operator on local fields and abelian Vilenkin groups.

Theorem 1.8
.

A fundamental solution for the Vladimirov-Taibleson operator
D^{\alpha}
,
\mathfrak{Re}(\alpha)>0,\mathfrak{Im}(\alpha)\notin(2\pi i/\ln\varkappa)\mathbb{Z}
,
\mathfrak{Re}(\alpha)\neq 1
, is given by

E_{\alpha}(x)=\frac{1-\varkappa^{-\alpha}}{1-\varkappa^{\alpha-1}}|x|_{\mathscr{G}}^{\alpha-1}.

Observe that the given definition of the Vladimirov-Taibleson operator and its fundamental solution depend on the choice of ultrametric on the group, so that the definition depends on the choice a sequence of compact open subgroups
\mathscr{G}
, implying that there can be several definitions of this operator for the same group. However in some cases there is a natural choice of ultrametric, like for
\mathbb{Q}_{p}^{d}
where the definition is given in terms of the
p
-adic norm, and more generally the same occurs for graded
\mathbb{K}
-Lie groups where there is a natural and convenient definition of Vladimirov-Taibleson operator in terms of an homogeneous ultrametric function. A modification we need to do with respect to the definition previously given is that for graded
\mathbb{K}
-Lie groups we want to work with an specific choice of distance function on the group. The reason for this choice is that we want our distance function to be an homogeneous quasi-norm in the sense of the following definition:

Definition 1.9
.

Let
G
be a graded
\mathbb{K}
-Lie group together with the dilations
D_{\gamma},\gamma\in\mathbb{K}^{*}
. An
homogeneous quasi-norm
is a continous non-negative function
|\cdot|:G\to[0,\infty)
satisfying

•

|x^{-1}|=|x|
for all
x\in G
,

•

|\gamma x|=|\gamma|_{\mathbb{K}}|x|
for all
x\in G
and
\gamma\in\mathbb{K}^{*}
,

•

|x|=0
if and only if
x=e
.

Definition 1.10
.

Let
G
be a graded
\mathbb{K}
-Lie group with homogeneous dimension
Q
, considered as a Vilenkin group with the sequence of subgroups
\mathscr{G}=\{G_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
determined by the dilations on the algebra. We define the distance function

on

=|xy^{-1}|_{G}:=\begin{cases}0&\text{ }\text{ }\text{if}\text{ }x=y,\\
|xy^{-1}|_{\mathscr{G}}^{1/Q}&\text{ }\text{ }\text{if}\text{ }xy^{-1}\in G_{n}\setminus G_{n+1}.\end{cases}

Remark 1.11
.

The above defined metric is an homogeneous quasi-norm in the sense of Definition

and
\gamma\in\mathbb{K}^{*}
with

|\gamma(xy^{-1})|_{\mathscr{G}}=q^{-Q(n+k)}=|\gamma|_{\mathbb{K}}^{Q}|xy^{-1}|_{\mathscr{G}}
.

Considering the above, the definition of Vladimirov-Taibleson operator that we will use on graded
\mathbb{K}
-Lie groups is the following:

Definition 1.12
.

Let
G
be a graded
\mathbb{K}
-Lie group with homogeneous dimension
Q
. Take
\alpha\in\mathbb{C}
with
\mathfrak{Re}(\alpha)>0
. We define the Vladimirov-Taibleson operator on

as the linear operator acting initially on locally constant functions with compact support according to the following formula:

\mathscr{D}^{\alpha}f(x):=\frac{1-q^{\alpha}}{1-q^{-(\alpha+Q)}}\int_{G}\frac{f(xy^{-1})-f(x)}{|y|_{G}^{\alpha+Q}}dy,\text{ }\text{ }x\in G.

And for this operator its fundamental solution is:

Theorem 1.13
.

A fundamental solution for
\mathscr{D}^{\alpha}
,
\mathfrak{Re}(\alpha)>0,\mathfrak{Im}(\alpha)\notin(2\pi i/\ln q)\mathbb{Z}

Remark 1.14
.

The Vladimirov-Taibleson
\mathscr{D}^{\alpha}
is an
\alpha
-homogeneous operator since

\displaystyle\mathscr{D}^{\alpha}(f\circ D_{\gamma})(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1-q^{\alpha}}{1-q^{-(\alpha+Q)}}\int_{G}\frac{f(\gamma(xy^{-1}))-f(\gamma x)}{|y|_{G}^{\alpha+Q}}dy

\displaystyle=\frac{1-q^{\alpha}}{1-q^{-(\alpha+Q)}}|\gamma|_{\mathbb{K}}^{\alpha+Q}\int_{G}\frac{f(\gamma(xy^{-1}))-f(\gamma x)}{|\gamma y|_{G}^{\alpha+Q}}dy

\displaystyle=\frac{1-q^{\alpha}}{1-q^{-(\alpha+Q)}}|\gamma|_{\mathbb{K}}^{\alpha}\int_{G}\frac{f((\gamma x)y^{-1})-f(\gamma x)}{|y|_{G}^{\alpha+Q}}dy

\displaystyle=|\gamma|_{\mathbb{K}}^{\alpha}(\mathscr{D}^{\alpha}f)\circ D_{\gamma}(x).

We will come back to this fact and some of its implications on Section 2 where we study homogeneous operators like the ”Vladimirov Laplacian” and the ”Vladimirov Sub-Laplacian” on the
p
-adic Heisenberg group.

Remark 1.15
.

When
0<\alpha<Q
, there is an alternative way to obtain the heat kernel, its estimates, and the fundamental solution of the Vladimirov-Taibleson operator on graded
\mathbb{K}
-Lie groups, and more generally on constant-order Vilenkin groups, by using isotropic Markov semigroups and non-local Dirichlet forms, see
[
3
,
4
]
for all the details. In those works the authors studied Markov semigroups on general ultrametric measure spaces and obtained some interesting estimates for the heat kernel. We want to remark that the same estimates hold true in particular for constant-order Vilenkin groups and graded
\mathbb{K}
-Lie groups, even thought the authors in
[
3
,
4
]
do not point out this fact. We will give more details about it in Section 1.5. However, our approach is different in nature as it involves the representation theory of Vilenkin groups, and it also produces a more general result since we can get the fundamental solution for
\alpha\in\mathbb{C}
with
Q\neq\mathfrak{Re}(\alpha)>0
, and even for
\mathfrak{Re}(\alpha)=Q
when the group is compact.

The second operator that generalise the Vladimirov operator to the multidimensional case is the
Vladimirov Laplacian

\mathfrak{L}^{\alpha}
,
\alpha>0
, introduced by Vladimirov in
[
31
]
for the particular case
\alpha=2
. We define it here as the invariant
\alpha
-homogeneous operator acting on locally constant functions with compact support via the formula:

\mathfrak{L}^{\alpha}f(x):=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\partial^{\alpha}_{x_{j}}f(x),

where

\partial^{\alpha}_{x_{j}}f(x):=\frac{1-p^{\alpha}}{1-p^{-(\alpha+1)}}\int_{\mathbb{Q}_{p}}\frac{f(x-x_{j})-f(x)}{|x_{j}|_{p}^{\alpha+1}}dx_{j}.

The operator
\mathfrak{L}^{\alpha}
was studied in
[
4
]
and the existence of its fundamental solution was proven for
0<\alpha<d
. In addition, the following properties of
\mathfrak{L}^{\alpha}
were established:

•

(\mathfrak{L}^{\alpha},\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{Q}_{p}^{d}))
is a non-negative symmetric operator, where
\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{Q}_{p}^{d})
denote the collection of locally constant functions with compact support on
\mathbb{Q}_{p}^{d}
.

•

(\mathfrak{L}^{\alpha},\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{Q}_{p}^{d}))
admits a system of compactly supported eigenfunctions. Besides the operator is essentially self adjoint.

•

The semigroup
e^{-t\mathfrak{L}^{\alpha}}
is symmeric and Markovian. It admits a heat kernel
h_{\mathfrak{L}^{\alpha}}
that satisfies the following estimate

h_{\mathfrak{L}^{\alpha}_{2}}(t,x)\asymp t^{-d/\alpha}\prod_{j=1}^{d}\min\{1,\frac{t^{1+1/\alpha}}{|x_{j}|_{p}^{1+\alpha}}\}.

•

The semigroup is transient if and only if
0<\alpha<d
.

For more general graded
\mathbb{K}
-Lie groups the question we want to investigate is whether something similar occurs for an operator like

\mathfrak{L}^{\alpha}:=\sum_{k=1}^{r}\sum_{j=1}^{b_{k}}\partial_{X_{k,j}}^{\alpha/\nu_{k}},

where
\{X_{k,j}\}
is a Malcev basis associated to the gradation

\mathfrak{g}=\bigoplus_{k=1}^{r}V_{\nu_{k}},\text{ }\text{ }b_{k}:=dim(V_{\nu_{k}}).

Here we are using the notation introduced in the following definition:

Definition 1.16
.

Let
G
be a graded
\mathbb{K}
-Lie group with Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
. We define the
directional VT operator
in the direction of

\partial_{X}^{\alpha}f(x):=\frac{1-q^{\alpha}}{1-q^{-(\alpha+1)}}\int_{\mathbb{K}}\frac{f(x\cdot\mathbb{exp}(tX)^{-1})-f(x)}{|t|_{\mathbb{K}}^{\alpha+1}}dt.

We call
\mathfrak{L}^{\alpha}
the

on

, and in particular for the Heisenberg group
\mathbb{H}_{d}
it takes the form:

\mathfrak{L}^{\alpha}=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\partial_{X_{j}}^{\alpha}+\partial_{Y_{j}}^{\alpha}+\partial_{Z}^{\alpha/2},

and for the Engel group
\mathbb{E}_{4}
:

\mathfrak{L}^{\alpha}=\partial_{X}^{\alpha}+\partial_{Y_{1}}^{\alpha}+\partial_{Y_{2}}^{\alpha/2}+\partial_{Y_{3}}^{\alpha/3}.

We will show the existence of a fundamental solution for

on
\mathbb{H}_{d}
and

Let

(iv)

The heat semigroup

2.
The Vladimirov-Taibleson operator

The proofs of Theorems
1.8
and
1.13
consist of two parts. The first one is finding the fundamental solution of the Vladimirov-Taibleson on
compact Vilenkin groups
, which we define formally in Definition
2.1
. For the sake of our discussion, all the compact Vilenkin groups that we are going to consider are subgroups of a bigger locally compact Vilenkin group. Actually, if
G
is a locally compact Vilenkin group endowed with the sequence of compact open subgroups
\mathscr{G}=\{G_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
, we are interested exclusively on the subgroups
G_{k}
in this sequence, which are compact Vilenkin groups on their own, together with the sequence of compact open subgroups
\mathscr{G}_{k}=\{G_{k+n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
. We will compute the fundamental solution of the Vladimirov-Taibleson operator on these groups and after that, for the second part of the proof, we will use the fundamental solution obtained in the compact case to obtain the fundamental solution in the non-compact case.

2.1.
Compact Vilenkin groups

Definition 2.1
.

We will say that a topological group
G
is a compact noncommutative Vilenkin group if
G
is a profinite group that possesses a decreasing sequence of compact open normal subgroups
G:=G_{0}\supset G_{1}\supset G_{2}\supset...
such that

(i)

2\leq\kappa_{n}:=|G_{n}/G_{n+1}|<\infty
, for all
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}.

(ii)

\{G_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
is a basis of neighbourhoods at
e\in G
and
\bigcap_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}G_{n}=\{e\}.

Let
G
be a compact Vilenkin group together with the sequence of compact open subgroups
\{G_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
. We will denote by
Rep(G)
the collection of all continuous finite-dimensional unitary representations on
G
, and
\widehat{G}
will denote the collection of irreducible representation in
Rep(G)
. By the Peter-Weyl Theorem the matrix entries of the representations
[\xi]\in\widehat{G}
form an orthonormal basis of the space
L^{2}(G)
and therefore any function
f\in L^{2}(G)
can be written as

f(x)=\mathcal{F}_{G}^{-1}\circ\mathcal{F}_{G}(f)(x)=\mathcal{F}_{G}^{-1}(\widehat{f})(x)=\sum_{[\xi]\in\widehat{G}}d_{\xi}Tr(\xi(x)\widehat{f}(\xi)),

where
\widehat{f}(\xi)
denotes the Fourier coefficient of
f
with respect to
[\xi]\in\widehat{G}
defined as

\widehat{f}(\xi)=\mathcal{F}_{G}[f](\xi):=\int_{G}f(x)\xi^{*}(x)dx,

dx
denotes the unique normalised Haar measure on
G
, and the inverse Fourier transform is defined as

\mathcal{F}_{G}^{-1}\varphi(x):=\sum_{[\xi]\in\widehat{G}}d_{\xi}Tr[\xi(x)\varphi(\xi)].

Recall that every matrix representation
\pi:G\to GL_{d_{\pi}}(\mathbb{C})
,
[\pi]\in Rep(G)
, is a continuous function and
GL_{d_{\pi}}(\mathbb{C})
does not contain small subgroups. Thus we can find a neighbourhood
V
of
I_{d_{\pi}}
such that the only subgroup of
GL_{d_{\pi}}(\mathbb{C})
contained in
V
is
\{I_{d_{\pi}}\}
. Let
U:=\pi^{-1}(V)
. Clearly
U
is a neighbourhood of
e\in G
and hence some
G_{n}
is contained in
U
and
\pi(U)\subset\{I_{d_{\pi}}\}
. This shows that the kernel of every
\pi
with
[\pi]\in Rep(G)
must contain one of the sub-groups
G_{n}
. We will denote

n_{\pi}:=\min\{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}:\pi|_{G_{n}}=I_{d_{\pi}}\},

for an arbitrary
[\pi]\in Rep(G)
. We also use the notation

G_{n}^{\bot}:=\{[\pi]\in Rep(G)\text{ }:\text{ }\pi|_{G_{n}}=I_{d_{\pi}}\},\text{ }\text{ }Rep_{n}(G):=G^{\bot}_{n}\setminus G_{n-1}^{\bot},\text{ }\text{ }\widehat{G}_{n}:=Rep_{n}(G)\cap\widehat{G}.

We have just proven the decomposition of
Rep(G)
and
\widehat{G}
as the disjoint unions

Rep(G)=\bigcup_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}Rep_{n}(G),\text{ }\text{ }\text{and}\text{ }\text{ }\widehat{G}=\bigcup_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}\widehat{G}_{n},

and in this way any
f\in L^{2}(G)
can be written as

f(x)=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}\sum_{[\xi]\in\widehat{G}_{n}}d_{\xi}Tr(\xi(x)\widehat{f}(\xi)),

and the action of a densely defined linear operator
T
on any function
f\in L^{2}(G)
as

Tf(x)=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}\sum_{[\xi]\in\widehat{G}_{n}}d_{\xi}Tr\big(\xi(x)\sigma_{T}(x,\xi)\widehat{f}(\xi)\big).

Here the symbol of the operator is defined as

\sigma_{T}(x,\xi):=\xi^{*}(x)T\xi(x),

and in particular it does not depend on the variable
x\in G
when
T
is left invariant.

Definition 2.2
.

We define the function
\langle\cdot\rangle_{\mathscr{G}}:Rep(G)\to\mathbb{R}
in the following way:

\langle\pi\rangle_{\mathscr{G}}:=\begin{cases}1\text{ }&\text{ }\text{if}\text{ }\text{ }\pi\text{ }\text{is the identity representation;}\\
|G/G_{n}|\text{ }&\text{ }\text{if}\text{ }\text{ }\pi\in Rep_{n}(G),\text{ }\text{ }n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}.\end{cases}

In this way it holds for any pair of nontrivial representations

\langle\pi\otimes\xi\rangle_{\mathscr{G}}=\max\{\langle\xi\rangle_{\mathscr{G}},\langle\pi\rangle_{\mathscr{G}}\},\text{ }\text{ }[\pi],[\xi]\in Rep(G).

In the context of compact Vilenkin groups we only consider densely defined operators whose domain contains one of the two relevant classes of ”smooth functions” on
G
. The first class of relevant smooth functions on a compact Vilenkin group is the collection of locally constant functions on
G
with a fixed index of locally constancy, here denoted by
\mathcal{D}(G)
. That is,
f\in\mathcal{D}(G)
if there is a compact open subgroup
K\leq G
such that
f(xy)=f(x)
for all
y\in K
, and one can check that this is equivalent to ask the same condition for some of the subgroups
G_{l}
,
l\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
. We call a natural number
l
the index of local constancy of
f
, and we write
ind(f)=l
, if
l
is the minimum natural number such that
f(xy)=f(x)
for all
y\in G_{l}
.

The second class is the collection of Schwartz functions on
G
that we define here, initially, via Fourier transform. We say that
f\in\mathcal{S}(G)
if for all
m>0
it holds

\|\widehat{f}(\xi)\|_{HS}\lesssim\langle\xi\rangle^{-m}_{\mathscr{G}},\text{ }\text{ }\text{ }\text{for all}\text{ }[\xi]\in\widehat{G}.

2.2.
Fundamental solution: compact case

Let
G
be a compact constant-order Vilenkin group together with the sequence of compact open subgroups
\mathscr{G}
, let us say
|G_{k}/G_{k+1}|=\varkappa
for all
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
. The Vladimirov-Taibleson operator on
G
takes the form

D^{\alpha}u(x)=\frac{1-\varkappa^{\alpha}}{1-\varkappa^{-\alpha-1}}\int_{G}|y|_{\mathscr{G}}^{-\alpha-1}(u(xy^{-1})-u(x))dx.

By now we will not focus on this operator and instead we want to consider the following closely related operator on
G
:

\mathbb{D}^{\alpha}u(x):=\frac{1-\varkappa^{-1}}{1-\varkappa^{-\alpha-1}}u(x)+\frac{1-\varkappa^{\alpha}}{1-\varkappa^{-\alpha-1}}\int_{G}|y|_{\mathscr{G}}^{-\alpha-1}(u(xy^{-1})-u(x))dx.

We can express it as a right-convolution operator and we will call its convolution kernel, the
Riesz kernel
associated to
\mathscr{G}
. This kernel is determined by the complex-valued function

\mathfrak{r}_{s}^{\mathscr{G}}(x):=\frac{|x|_{\mathscr{G}}^{s-1}}{\Gamma_{\mathscr{G}}(s)},\text{ }\text{ }\text{ }\text{ }s\notin 1+\frac{2\pi i}{\ln{\varkappa}}\mathbb{Z},

where

\Gamma_{\mathscr{G}}(x)=\frac{1-\varkappa^{s-1}}{1-\varkappa^{-s}},\text{ }s\neq 0.

As a distribution, the Riesz kernel has a meromorphic continuation to
\mathbb{C}
given by

\displaystyle\langle\mathfrak{r}_{s}^{\mathscr{G}},f\rangle=\frac{1-\varkappa^{-1}}{1-\varkappa^{s-1}}f(e)+\frac{1-\varkappa^{-s}}{1-\varkappa^{s-1}}\int_{G}|x|_{\mathscr{G}}^{s-1}(f(x)-f(e))dx,

with poles at
1+\frac{2\pi i}{\ln{\kappa}}\mathbb{Z}
. In particular, for
\mathfrak{Re}(s)>0

\langle\mathfrak{r}_{s}^{\mathscr{G}},f\rangle=\frac{1-\varkappa^{-s}}{1-\varkappa^{s-1}}\int_{G}|x|_{\mathscr{G}}^{s-1}f(x)dx.

Remark 2.3
.

Given a distribution
h\in\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(G)
, its Fourier transform
\mathcal{F}_{G}h\equiv\widehat{h}\in\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\widehat{G})
is defined via the formula

\langle\mathcal{F}_{G}h,\widehat{\varphi}\rangle:=\langle h,\iota\circ\mathcal{F}_{G}^{-1}\widehat{\varphi}\rangle,\text{ }\text{ }\text{ }\widehat{\varphi}\in\mathcal{D}(\widehat{G}),

where
(\iota\circ f)(x):=f(x^{-1})
. We can write explicitly

\iota\circ\mathcal{F}_{G}^{-1}\widehat{\varphi}(x)=\sum_{[\xi]\in\widehat{G}}d_{\xi}Tr\big(\xi^{*}(x)\widehat{\varphi}(\xi)\big),\text{ }\text{ }\text{ }\langle h,\iota\circ\mathcal{F}_{G}^{-1}\widehat{\varphi}\rangle=\sum_{[\xi]\in\widehat{G}}d_{\xi}Tr\big(\langle h,\xi^{*}(x)\rangle\widehat{\varphi}(\xi)\big).

In this way we see that the action of the Fourier transform of a distribution is determined by its values in the matrix coefficients
\xi_{ij}
. We can identify
\mathcal{F}_{G}h
with the map
\widehat{h}\in\mathcal{M}(\widehat{G})
defined in each
[\xi]\in\widehat{G}
as
\widehat{h}(\xi):=\langle h,\xi^{*}\rangle.

With a straightforward calculation we can check that

\widehat{\mathfrak{r}}_{s}^{\mathscr{G}}(\xi)=\langle\xi\rangle^{s}_{\mathscr{G}}I_{d_{\xi}},\text{ }[\xi]\in\widehat{G},

which shows that
\mathfrak{r}_{s}^{\mathscr{G}}*\mathfrak{r}_{t}^{\mathscr{G}}=\mathfrak{r}_{s+t}^{\mathscr{G}}
and in particular

\langle\mathfrak{r}_{s}^{\mathscr{G}}*\mathfrak{r}_{-s}^{\mathscr{G}},f\rangle=\langle\mathfrak{r}_{0}^{\mathscr{G}},f\rangle=\lim_{s\to 0}\langle\mathfrak{r}_{s}^{\mathscr{G}},f\rangle=\langle\delta_{e},f\rangle.

Using this information we can compute a fundamental solution for
\mathbb{D}^{\alpha}.

Definition 2.4
.

Let
T:\mathcal{D}(G)\subset D(T)\to\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(G)
be a densely defined linear operator. We say that
F\in\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(G)
is a
fundamental solution
of
T
if
T*F=\delta_{e}
.

Theorem 2.5
.

A fundamental solution for the VT-type operator
\mathbb{D}^{\alpha}
,
\mathfrak{Re}(\alpha)>0,\mathfrak{Im}(\alpha)\notin(2\pi i/\ln\varkappa)\mathbb{Z}
, is given by

E_{\alpha}(x)=\begin{cases}\frac{1-\varkappa^{-\alpha}}{1-\varkappa^{\alpha-1}}|x|_{\mathscr{G}}^{\alpha-1}&\text{ }\text{ }\text{ if }\text{ }\text{ }\mathfrak{Re}(\alpha)\neq 1,\\
\frac{1-\varkappa}{\varkappa\ln\varkappa}\ln{(|x|_{\mathscr{G}})}&\text{ }\text{ }\text{if}\text{ }\text{ }\mathfrak{Re}(\alpha)=1.\end{cases}

Proof.

According to Definition
2.4
we are looking for a distribution
E_{\alpha}\in\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(G)
such that

\langle\xi\rangle^{\alpha}\mathcal{F}E_{\alpha}=1_{\widehat{G}}\text{ },\text{ }\text{ }\langle 1_{\widehat{G}},\widehat{f}\rangle:=\sum_{[\xi]\in\widehat{G}}d_{\xi}Tr(\widehat{f}(\xi)),

where
1_{\widehat{G}}:=\mathcal{F}(\delta_{e})
. To find it we will use the Laurent expansion at
-\alpha
of the distribution
\langle\xi\rangle^{s}
. Let us write

\langle\xi\rangle^{s}=\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}c_{m}(s+\alpha)^{m}.

The real parts of the poles of the meromorphic continuation of
\langle\xi\rangle^{s}
are negative rational numbers so
\langle\xi\rangle^{s+\alpha}=\mathcal{F}_{G}(\mathfrak{r}_{s+\alpha}^{\mathscr{G}})
is holomorphic at
s=-\alpha
. This implies
c_{0}=0
for
m<0
so

\langle\xi\rangle^{s+\alpha}=c_{0}\langle\xi\rangle^{\alpha}+\sum_{m\in\mathbb{N}}c_{m}(s+\alpha)^{m}\langle\xi\rangle^{\alpha}(s+\alpha)^{m}.

By the Lebesgue dominated convergence theorem

\lim_{s\to-\alpha}\langle\langle\xi\rangle^{s+\alpha},\widehat{f}\rangle=\lim_{s\to-\alpha}\langle\mathfrak{r}_{s}^{\mathscr{G}},\iota\circ f\rangle=\langle\delta_{e},f\rangle,

therefore we can choose
E_{\alpha}
as the distribution
c_{0}
in the Laurent expansion of
\langle\xi\rangle^{s}
. Moreover, if
-\alpha
is not a pole of
|x|_{\mathscr{G}}^{s}
we have

\mathcal{F}E_{\alpha}=\lim_{s\to-\alpha}\langle\xi\rangle^{s}.

Now, in order to compute
E_{\alpha}
, we need to consider two cases.

•

If
\mathfrak{Re}(\alpha)\neq 1
, we know that

\langle\langle\xi\rangle^{s},\widehat{f}\rangle=\frac{1-\varkappa^{s}}{1-\varkappa^{-(s+1)}}\int_{G}|x|_{\mathscr{G}}^{-(s+1)}f(x^{-1})dx=\int_{G}|x|_{\mathscr{G}}^{-(s+1)}f(x)dx,

So, by the Lebesgue dominated convergence theorem, the following interchange of the integral with the limit is allowed

\displaystyle\lim_{s\to-\alpha}\langle\langle\xi\rangle^{s},\widehat{f}\rangle

\displaystyle=\lim_{s\to-\alpha}\frac{1-\varkappa^{s}}{1-\varkappa^{-(s+1)}}\int_{G}|x|_{\mathscr{G}}^{-(s+1)}f(x)dx

\displaystyle=\int_{G}\lim_{s\to-\alpha}\frac{1-\varkappa^{s}}{1-\varkappa^{-(s+1)}}|x|_{\mathscr{G}}^{-(s+1)}f(x)dx

\displaystyle=\frac{1-\varkappa^{-\alpha}}{1-\varkappa^{\alpha-1}}\int_{G}|x|_{\mathscr{G}}^{\alpha-1}f(x)dx.

•

If
\mathfrak{Re}(\alpha)=1
, we rewrite

\displaystyle\langle\langle\xi\rangle^{s},\widehat{f}\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1-\varkappa^{s}}{1-\varkappa^{-(s+1)}}\int_{G}|x|_{\mathscr{G}}^{-(s+1)}f(x)dx

\displaystyle=\int_{G}\frac{1-\varkappa^{s}}{1-\varkappa^{-(s+1)}}\varkappa^{v_{\mathscr{G}}(x)(s+1)}f(x)dx,

where
v_{\mathscr{G}}(x)=n
if
x\in G_{n}\setminus G_{n+1}.
Using again the Laurent series we get

\displaystyle\frac{1-\varkappa^{s}}{1-\varkappa^{-(s+1)}}\varkappa^{v_{\mathscr{G}}(x)(s+1)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1-\varkappa^{-1}}{\ln\varkappa}(s+1)^{-1}

\displaystyle+\frac{(1-\varkappa^{-1})\ln\varkappa^{v_{\mathscr{G}}(x)}-\ln\varkappa/\varkappa+(\varkappa-1/2\varkappa)\ln\varkappa}{\ln\varkappa}

\displaystyle+\mathcal{O}(s+1),

and therefore

\lim_{s\to-\alpha}\langle\langle\xi\rangle^{s},f\rangle=\int_{G}\frac{(1-\varkappa^{-1})\ln|x|_{\mathscr{G}}-\ln\varkappa/\varkappa+(\varkappa-1/2\varkappa)\ln\varkappa}{\ln\varkappa}f(x)dx.

Finally, since the sum of a fundamental solution and a constant is also a fundamental solution, we get

\langle E_{\alpha},f\rangle=\int_{G}\frac{(1-\varkappa^{-1})\ln|x|_{\mathscr{G}}}{\ln\varkappa}f(x)dx.

The proof is concluded.
∎

2.4.
Fundamental solution: non-compact case

Now that we know the fundamental solution in the compact case we can move to the the locally compact case. Let us take
f\in\mathcal{D}(G_{n})
so
f(x)=0
for
x\in G\setminus G_{n}.
Recall that
\mathbb{1}_{A}
denotes the characteristic function in
A
.
We want to to prove that
E_{\alpha}(x)
in Theorem
1.8
is a fundamental solution for
D^{\alpha}
, that is
D^{\alpha}*E_{\alpha}=\delta_{e}
on
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(G)
. To do that we will show that almost everywhere on
G
and for any
\varepsilon>0
we have
|f*D^{\alpha}*E_{\alpha}(x)-f(x)|<\varepsilon
so that
f*D^{\alpha}*E_{\alpha}(x)=f(x)
. The argument here is that we can write the action of the Vladimirov-Taibleson operator on
f
in terms of the action of the operators
\mathbb{D}^{\alpha}
in the following way

\displaystyle\int_{G}\frac{f(xy^{-1})-f(x)}{|y|_{\mathscr{G}}^{\alpha+1}}dy

\displaystyle=\mathbb{1}_{G_{n}}(x)\int_{G_{n}}\frac{f(xy^{-1})-f(x)}{|y|_{\mathscr{G}}^{\alpha+1}}dy+\mathbb{1}_{G_{n}}(x)\int_{G\setminus G_{n}}\frac{f(xy^{-1})-f(x)}{|y|_{\mathscr{G}}^{\alpha+1}}dy

\displaystyle\text{ }+\mathbb{1}_{G\setminus G_{n}}(x)\int_{G_{n}}\frac{f(xy^{-1})-f(x)}{|y|_{\mathscr{G}}^{\alpha+1}}dy+\mathbb{1}_{G\setminus G_{n}}(x)\int_{G\setminus G_{n}}\frac{f(xy^{-1})-f(x)}{|y|_{\mathscr{G}}^{\alpha+1}}dy.

Now we proceed by parts:

•

The first integral can be rewritten as:

\mathbb{1}_{G_{n}}(x)\int_{G_{n}}\frac{f(xy^{-1})-f(x)}{|y|_{\mathscr{G}}^{\alpha+Q}}dy=\varkappa^{n\alpha}\mathbb{1}_{G_{n}}(x)\int_{G_{n}}\frac{f(xy^{-1})-f(x)}{|y|_{\mathscr{G}_{n}}^{\alpha+Q}}d_{n}y.

•

If
x\in G_{n}
and
y\in G\setminus G_{n}
, then
xy^{-1}\in G\setminus G_{n}
so

\mathbb{1}_{G_{n}}(x)\int_{G\setminus G_{n}}\frac{f(xy^{-1})-f(x)}{|y|_{\mathscr{G}}^{\alpha+1}}dy=-f(x)\int_{G\setminus G_{n}}|y|_{\mathscr{G}}^{-(\alpha+1)}dy=\frac{1-\varkappa^{-1}}{1-\varkappa^{\alpha}}\varkappa^{n\alpha}f(x).

•

If
x\in G\setminus G_{n}
and
y\in G_{n}
then
f(xy^{-1})=f(x)=0
, so

\mathbb{1}_{G\setminus G_{n}}(x)\int_{G_{n}}\frac{f(xy^{-1})-f(x)}{|y|_{\mathscr{G}}^{\alpha+1}}dy=0.

•

If
x\in G\setminus G_{n}
and
y\in G\setminus G_{n}
, the integral is non-zero if and only if
xy^{-1}\in G_{n}
, and when this happens
|x|_{G}=|y|_{G}
. Then we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{1}_{G\setminus G_{n}}(x)\int_{G\setminus G_{n}}\frac{f(xy^{-1})-f(x)}{|y|_{\mathscr{G}}^{\alpha+1}}dy

\displaystyle=\mathbb{1}_{G\setminus G_{n}}(x)\int_{G\setminus G_{n}}\frac{f(xy^{-1})}{|y|_{\mathscr{G}}^{\alpha+1}}dy

\displaystyle=\mathbb{1}_{G\setminus G_{n}}(x)|x|_{\mathscr{G}}^{-(\alpha+1)}\int_{xG_{n}}f(xy^{-1})dy

\displaystyle=\mathbb{1}_{G\setminus G_{n}}(x)|x|_{\mathscr{G}}^{-(\alpha+1)}\int_{G_{n}}f(y)dy.

Summing up:

\displaystyle D^{\alpha}f(x)=\varkappa^{n\alpha}\mathbb{D}_{n}^{\alpha}f(x)+\frac{1-\varkappa^{\alpha}}{1-\varkappa^{-(\alpha+1)}}\mathbb{1}_{G\setminus G_{n}}(x)|x|_{\mathscr{G}}^{-(\alpha+1)}\int_{G_{n}}f(y)dy,

and in particular for
f\in\tilde{\mathcal{D}}(G_{n})
we have
D^{\alpha}f=\varkappa^{\alpha}\mathbb{D}^{\alpha}_{n}f
. Moreover, the previous equalities hold true if we replace
n
for any
l
such that
l\leq n
. Using this calculation we are now in position to prove Theorem
1.8
.

Proof of Theorem
1.8
:

Let us start by remarking that for
f\in\tilde{\mathcal{D}}(G_{n})
it holds
D^{\alpha}f=\varkappa^{l\alpha}\mathbb{D}_{l}^{\alpha}f
for any
l\leq n
. If we fix
x\in G
and choose
l\leq n
so that
x\in G_{l}
we obtain

\displaystyle D^{\alpha}f*E_{\alpha}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1-\varkappa^{-\alpha}}{1-\varkappa^{\alpha-1}}\varkappa^{l\alpha}\int_{G}\mathbb{D}_{l}^{\alpha}f(y)|y^{-1}x|_{\mathscr{G}}^{\alpha-1}dx

\displaystyle=\frac{1-\varkappa^{-\alpha}}{1-\varkappa^{\alpha-1}}\varkappa^{l\alpha}\int_{G_{l}}\mathbb{D}_{l}^{\alpha}f(y)|y^{-1}x|_{\mathscr{G}}^{\alpha-1}dx

\displaystyle=\frac{1-\varkappa^{-\alpha}}{1-\varkappa^{\alpha-1}}\varkappa^{l\alpha}\int_{G_{l}}\mathbb{D}_{l}^{\alpha}f(y)(\varkappa^{-l(\alpha-1)})|y^{-1}x|_{\mathscr{G}_{l}}^{\alpha-1}dx

\displaystyle=\frac{1-\varkappa^{-\alpha}}{1-\varkappa^{\alpha-1}}\int_{G_{l}}\mathbb{D}_{l}^{\alpha}f(y)|y^{-1}x|_{\mathscr{G}_{l}}^{\alpha-1}d_{l}x=f(x).

For more general
f\in\mathcal{D}(G_{n})
and
l\leq n
something similar holds and we only need to observe that

\frac{1-\varkappa^{-\alpha}}{1-\varkappa^{\alpha-1}}\frac{1-\varkappa^{\alpha}}{1-\varkappa^{-(\alpha+1)}}\int_{G}\mathbb{1}_{G\setminus G_{l}}(z)|z|_{\mathscr{G}}^{-(\alpha+1)}|z^{-1}x|_{\mathscr{G}}^{\alpha-1}dz\int_{G_{n}}f(y)dy\to 0,

as
l\to-\infty
. This concludes the proof.
∎

2.5.
The isotropic Laplace operator

To conclude this section we give a quick review of some of the results exposed in
[
3
,
4
]
and the way they can be applied to graded
\mathbb{K}
-Lie groups. Specially, we are interested in the heat kernel estimates obtained in
[
4
]
for the isotropic Laplace operator. Since the arguments in
[
4
]
work on general ultrametric measure spaces we can apply them in particular to graded
\mathbb{K}
-Lie groups and obtain in this way an estimate for the heat kernel of the Vladimirov-Taibleson operator and some other interesting homogeneous operators. We will focus exclusively on graded
\mathbb{K}
-Lie groups but our analysis in this section extends naturally to constant-order Vilenkin groups.

Let
G
be a graded
\mathbb{K}
-Lie group and consider the ultrametric measure space
(G,d_{\alpha},dx)
, where
dx
is the normalised Haar measure on
G
and

d_{\alpha}(x,y):=\frac{|y^{-1}x|_{G}^{\alpha}}{q^{\alpha}},\text{ }\text{ }\alpha>0,

so that the balls in this metric have the property

|B_{\alpha}(x,r)|\asymp r^{\frac{Q}{\alpha}}.

Our goal is to get some estimate on the heat kernel associated to
\mathscr{D}^{\alpha}
,
\alpha>0
, but, instead of starting with
\mathscr{D}^{\alpha}
and its heat semigroup, the authors in
[
4
]
begin by constructing a Markov semigroup associated to a triple
(\rho,d_{\alpha},dx)
, where
\rho
is a function
\rho:[0,\infty]\to[0,1]
strictly monotone increasing and left-continuous such that
\rho(0+)=0
and
\rho(\infty)=1
. The heat kernel associated to this triple has in fact several very nice explicit forms that can be used to obtain the desired estimates, as it is shown in
[
4
, Theorem 2.14]
. In particular, if we pick the function
\rho(r):=e^{-r^{-1}}
, the infinitesimal generator of the Markov semigroup associated to the triple
(\rho,d_{\alpha},dx)
coincides with
\mathscr{D}^{\alpha}
, so that their heat kernels coincide and we obtain the estimates for the heat kernel of
\mathscr{D}^{\alpha}
. For the sake of completeness, let us give more details about this construction.

Define the family of orthoprojectors
\{P_{r}\}_{r\in[0,\infty)}
by

P_{r}f(x)=\frac{1}{|B_{\alpha}(x,r)|}\int_{B_{\alpha}(x,r)}f(y)dy,\text{ }\text{ }r>0,

and
P_{0}=0
, where
B_{\alpha}(x,r)
denotes the ball of radius
r>0
with center in
x\in G
. Then for any function
\rho:[0,\infty]\to[0,1]
strictly monotone increasing and left-continuous such that
\rho(0+)=0
and
\rho(\infty)=1
, the formula

Pf(x):=\int_{0}^{\infty}P_{r}f(x)d\rho(r),

defines a Markov operator on Borel bounded functions, and a bounded operator on
L^{2}(G)
. The non-negative powers of this operator are given by

P^{t}f(x):=\int_{0}^{\infty}P_{r}f(x)d\rho^{t}(r),

and they form a strongly continuous symmetric Markov semigroup on
L^{2}(G)
. If we pick the function
\rho(r):=e^{-r^{-1}}
then for any
t>0
these operators admit an integral kernel called the

heat kernel of the semigroup
. For this kernel the following expression is known:

h_{\alpha}(t,x,y)=\int_{d_{\alpha}(x,y)}^{\infty}\frac{d\rho^{t}(r)}{|B_{\alpha}(x,r)|},

and the following estimate holds true
[
4
, Theorem 2.14]
:

h_{\alpha}(t,x,y)\asymp\frac{t}{(t^{1/\alpha}+|y^{-1}x|_{G})^{\alpha+Q}}.

We can construct a Dirichlet form
(\mathcal{E},Dom_{\mathcal{E}})
from the heat kernel
h_{\alpha}
via the formula

\mathcal{E}(f,f):=\lim_{t\to 0}\frac{1}{2t}\int_{G}\int_{G}h_{\alpha}(t,x,y)(f(x)-f(y))^{2}dxdy,

whose generator coincides with the infinitesimal generator of the semigroup
P_{\rho}^{t}
, which is a self-adjoint unbounded operator
\mathcal{L}_{\alpha}
on
L^{2}(G)
given by

\mathcal{L}_{\alpha}f=\lim_{t\to 0}\frac{P^{t}f-f}{t}.

The authors in
[
4
]
refer to this operator as the
isotropic Laplace operator
associated with
(\rho,d_{\alpha},dx)
. Using the polarization identity we can write this non-local Dirichlet forms in terms of a kernel
J_{\alpha}(x,y)
as

\mathcal{E}(f,g)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{G}\int_{G}(f(x)-f(y))(g(x)-g(y))J_{\alpha}(x,y)dxdy,

with the kernel
J_{\alpha}(x,y)
given by

\displaystyle J_{\alpha}(x,y)

\displaystyle=\int_{d_{\alpha}(x,y)}^{\infty}\frac{s^{-2}ds}{|B_{\alpha}(x,s)|}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{q^{Q(n+k)}}\int_{q^{\alpha(k+n)}/q^{\alpha}}^{q^{\alpha(k+n+1)}/q^{\alpha}}d(-1/r)

\displaystyle=\frac{q^{\alpha}}{q^{(Q+\alpha)k}}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{q^{n(Q+\alpha)}}\big(1-\frac{1}{q^{\alpha}}\big)=-\frac{1-q^{\alpha}}{1-q^{-(\alpha+Q)}}|y^{-1}x|_{G}^{-(\alpha+Q)},

where in the above calculations we took
d_{\alpha}(x,y)=q^{\alpha k}/q^{\alpha}
fixed. According to
[
4
, Theorem 3.2]
the isotropic Laplace operator
\mathcal{L}_{\alpha}
acts on functions
f\in\mathcal{D}(G)
as

\mathcal{L}_{\alpha}f(x)=\int_{G}f(x)-f(y)J_{\alpha}(x,y)dy,

so we actually have the equality
\mathcal{L}_{\alpha}=\mathscr{D}^{\alpha}
, the heat kernel
h_{\alpha}(t,x,y)
is the heat kernel of
\mathscr{D}^{\alpha}
and it actually has the form
h_{\alpha}(t,x,y)=h_{\alpha}(t,y^{-1}x)
. We can try to obtain the fundamental solution for
\mathscr{D}^{\alpha}
by integrating
h_{\alpha}
in the variable
t\in(0,\infty)
, but in fact this works if and only if the heat semigroup is transient which is equivalent to
0<\alpha<Q
. In such case it holds

E_{\alpha}(x)=\int_{0}^{\infty}h_{\alpha}(t,x)dt=\frac{1-q^{-\alpha}}{1-q^{\alpha-Q}}|x|_{G}^{\alpha-Q},

which gives an alternative proof of Theorem
1.13
for the special case
0<\alpha<Q
.

3.
The Heisenberg group

3.1.
Homogeneous operators on
\mathbb{Q}_{p}^{d}

As we remarked in the introduction, the Vladimirov-Taibleson operator defines an homogeneous operator on graded
\mathbb{K}
-Lie groups. Of course there are many other interesting examples of homogeneous operators, and it is our purpose to introduce some of them along this section. A nice way to explain our ideas is by revisiting the commutative case, so we begin by recalling some properties of invariant
\nu
-homogeneous operators on
\mathbb{Q}_{p}^{d}
,
\nu>0
. Via Fourier analysis invariant
\nu
-homogeneous operators, that is operators satisfying
T_{\sigma}(f(\gamma x))=|\gamma|_{p}^{\nu}T_{\sigma}f(\gamma x)
for
\gamma\in\mathbb{Q}_{p}^{*}
, can be written in the form

T_{\sigma}f(x)=\int_{\mathbb{Q}_{p}^{d}}\sigma(\xi)\widehat{f}(\xi)e^{2\pi i\{x\cdot\xi\}_{p}}d\xi,

where
\sigma:\mathbb{Q}_{p}^{d}\to\mathbb{C}
is a
\nu
-homogeneous function. If
T_{\sigma}
is positive, elliptic and homogeneous of positive degree, which is equivalent to
\sigma(\xi)\geq 0
and
\sigma(\xi)=0
iff
\xi=0
, we can extend
T_{\sigma}
to a self adjoint operator and we can use its associated functional calculus to define the heat semigroup

e^{-tT_{\sigma}}f(x)=\int_{\mathbb{Q}_{p}^{d}}e^{-t\sigma(\xi)}\widehat{f}(\xi)e^{2\pi i\{x\cdot\xi\}_{p}}d\xi,

and obtain from its kernel the fundamental solution and the Riesz potential associated to
T_{\sigma}
. The simplest example of this construction occurs with the Vladimirov-Taibleson operator
\mathscr{D}^{\alpha}
, an
\alpha
-homogeneous operator on
\mathbb{Q}_{p}^{d}
which via Fourier transform can be expressed as

\mathscr{D}^{\alpha}f(x)=\int_{\mathbb{Q}_{p}^{d}}\|\xi\|_{p}^{\alpha}\widehat{f}(\xi)e^{2\pi i\{x\cdot\xi\}_{p}}d\xi.

The heat kernel for this operator is given by

h_{\alpha}(t,x)=\int_{\mathbb{Q}_{p}^{d}}e^{-t\|\xi\|_{p}^{\alpha}}\widehat{f}(\xi)e^{2\pi i\{x\cdot\xi\}_{p}}d\xi,

and it is studied in
[
23
]
, where the reader can find a detailed analysis of the Cauchy problem associated to the Taibleson operator. The same analysis and similar estimates as in
[
23
]
hold true for more general positive elliptic homogeneous operators, and we can use their heat kernels to obtain, at least formally, their fundamental solutions in an standard way. That is, if
h_{\sigma}(t,x)
is the heat kernel of
T_{\sigma}
, a fundamental solution for
T_{\sigma}
is given by

\textbf{h}_{\sigma}(x)=\int_{0}^{\infty}h_{\sigma}(t,x)dt.

Moreover, under the hypothesis that the heat kernel satisfies some reasonable estimate, our guess is
h_{\sigma}(t,x)\asymp t^{d/\nu}
as for the Vladimirov-Taibleson operator, we can also define fractional powers of
T_{\sigma}
and obtain their fundamental solution by means of the Riesz potential

\mathcal{I}_{a}(x):=\frac{1}{\Gamma(a/\nu)}\int_{0}^{\infty}t^{a/\nu-1}h_{\sigma}(t,x)dt,\text{ }\text{ }0<\mathfrak{Re}(a)<d.

Remark 3.1
.

A remarkable property of these kernels is that they are homogeneous, as we can check by using the homogeneity of
T_{\sigma}
and the properties of the heat kernel. First, for
\textbf{h}_{\sigma}
we obtain with a couple of changes of variable

\displaystyle\textbf{h}_{\sigma}(\gamma x)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{\mathbb{Q}_{p}^{d}}e^{-t\sigma(\xi)}e^{2\pi i\{\gamma x\cdot\xi\}_{p}}d\xi dt

\displaystyle=|\gamma|_{p}^{-d}\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{\mathbb{Q}_{p}^{d}}e^{-t|\gamma|_{p}^{-\nu}\sigma(\xi)}e^{2\pi i\{x\cdot\xi\}_{p}}d\xi dt

\displaystyle=|\gamma|_{p}^{\nu-d}\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{\mathbb{Q}_{p}^{d}}e^{-t\sigma(\xi)}e^{2\pi i\{x\cdot\xi\}_{p}}d\xi dt=|\gamma|_{p}^{\nu-d}\textbf{h}_{\sigma}(x),

Similarly we have for
\mathcal{I}_{a}
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{a}(\gamma x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\Gamma(a/\nu)}\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{\mathbb{Q}_{p}^{d}}t^{a/\nu-1}e^{-t\sigma(\xi)}e^{2\pi i\{\gamma x\cdot\xi\}_{p}}d\xi dt

\displaystyle=|\gamma|_{p}^{-d}\frac{1}{\Gamma(a/\nu)}\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{\mathbb{Q}_{p}^{d}}t^{a/\nu-1}e^{-t|\gamma|_{p}^{-\nu}\sigma(\xi)}e^{2\pi i\{x\cdot\xi\}_{p}}d\xi dt

\displaystyle=|\gamma|_{p}^{a-d}\frac{1}{\Gamma(a/\nu)}\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{\mathbb{Q}_{p}^{d}}t^{a/\nu-1}e^{-t\sigma(\xi)}e^{2\pi i\{x\cdot\xi\}_{p}}d\xi dt=|\gamma|_{p}^{a-d}\mathcal{I}_{a}(x).

The same ideas exposed above for the case
G=\mathbb{Q}_{p}^{d}
are applicable when we move to the noncommutative case, with some important modifications. The main one is that for noncommutative groups the representation theory, and therefore the Fourier analysis, is more complicated and for that reason it is necessary to appeal to more advanced arguments. Still we can study pseudo-differential operators on noncommutative groups and we will focus now our attention in a very particular class of homogeneous operators that we call here
directional VT operators.
These are operators defined in terms of the elements of the Lie algebra of a graded
\mathbb{K}
-Lie group reassembling the definition of directional derivatives on Lie groups.

Definition 3.2
.

Let
G
be a graded
\mathbb{K}
-adic Lie group with Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
. We define the
directional VT operator
in the direction of
X\in\mathfrak{g}
by the formula

\partial_{X}^{\alpha}f(x):=\frac{1-q^{\alpha}}{1-q^{-(\alpha+1)}}\int_{\mathbb{K}}\frac{f(x\cdot\mathbb{exp}(tX)^{-1})-f(x)}{|t|_{\mathbb{K}}^{\alpha+1}}dt.

Remark 3.3
.

A property we want to remark here is the homogeneity of the directional VT operators: for
\gamma\in\mathbb{K}
and
X\in V_{\nu_{k}}\subset\mathfrak{g}
we have

\displaystyle\partial_{X}^{\alpha}(f\circ D_{\gamma})(u)

\displaystyle=\frac{1-q^{\alpha}}{1-q^{-(\alpha+1)}}\int_{\mathbb{K}}\frac{f(\gamma(u\cdot\mathbb{exp}(tX)^{-1}))-f(\gamma u)}{|t|_{\mathbb{K}}^{\alpha+1}}dt

\displaystyle=|\gamma|_{\mathbb{K}}^{\nu_{k}(\alpha+1)}\frac{1-q^{\alpha}}{1-q^{-(\alpha+1)}}\int_{\mathbb{K}}\frac{f(\gamma u\cdot\mathbb{exp}(\gamma^{\nu_{k}}tX)^{-1}))-f(\gamma u)}{|\gamma^{\nu_{k}}t|_{\mathbb{K}}^{\alpha+1}}dt

\displaystyle=|\gamma|_{\mathbb{K}}^{\alpha\nu_{k}}\frac{1-q^{\alpha}}{1-q^{-(\alpha+1)}}\int_{\mathbb{K}}\frac{f(\gamma u\cdot\mathbb{exp}(tX)^{-1}))-f(\gamma u)}{|t|_{\mathbb{K}}^{\alpha+1}}dt

\displaystyle=|\gamma|_{\mathbb{K}}^{\alpha\nu_{k}}(\partial^{\alpha}_{X}f)\circ D_{\gamma}(u).

It is important to remark that the assignation of a VT operator to the elements of the Lie algebra is not linear, and therefore it doesn’t preserve the Lie algebra structure. This is one of the biggest differences with the theory of real Lie groups: in the totally disconnected case we do not have an infinitesimal representation, and neither a correspondence between the Lie algebra and some notion of derivatives for functions
f:G\to\mathbb{C}
. Despite this fact the directional VT operators and the polynomials in the directional VT operators are interesting on their own, and there is a particular example that has been studied before in the literature called
the Vladimirov Laplacian.
This operator is defined initially on
\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{Q}_{p}^{d})
as

\mathfrak{L}^{\alpha}:=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\partial^{\alpha}_{e_{j}}=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\partial^{\alpha}_{x_{j}},

where
e_{1},...,e_{d}
is the usual basis of
\mathbb{Q}_{p}^{d}
and, as the authors in
[
4
, Section 5.3.1]
pointed out, it has the following properties:

•

(\mathfrak{L}^{\alpha},\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{Q}_{p}^{d}))
is a non-negative symmetric, essentially self-adjoint operator.

•

The semigroup
e^{-t\mathfrak{L}^{\alpha}}
is symmetric and Markovian, and it admits a heat kernel
h_{\mathfrak{L}^{\alpha}}(t,x)
with the property

h_{\mathfrak{L}^{\alpha}}(t,x)\asymp t^{-d/\alpha}.

•

The semigroup is transient if and only if
0<\alpha<d
.

•

For all
f\in\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{Q}_{p}^{d})

\mathfrak{L}^{\alpha}f(x)=\int_{\mathbb{Q}_{p}^{d}}(f(x)-f(y))J_{\alpha}(x,dy),

where

J_{\alpha}(x,dy)=\sum_{j=1}^{d}J_{\alpha}(x_{j}-y_{j})dj_{j}=-\frac{1-p^{\alpha}}{1-p^{-\alpha-1}}\sum_{j=1}^{d}|x_{j}-y_{j}|_{p}^{-\alpha-1}dy_{j}.

As we mentioned in the introduction, for more general graded
\mathbb{K}
-Lie groups the question we want to investigate in this section is whether something similar occurs for an operator like

\mathfrak{L}^{\alpha}:=\sum_{k=1}^{r}\sum_{j=1}^{b_{k}}\partial_{X_{k,j}}^{\alpha/\nu_{k}},

where
\{X_{k,j}\}
is a Malcev basis associated to the gradation

\mathfrak{g}=\bigoplus_{k=1}^{r}V_{\nu_{k}},\text{ }\text{ }b_{k}:=dim(V_{\nu_{k}}).

We will show that the answer to this question is affirmative when
G=\mathbb{H}_{d}
or
G=\mathbb{E}_{4}
but first we need a quick review of some basic facts about harmonic analysis on
\mathbb{H}_{d}
and
\mathbb{E}_{4}
.

3.2.
Harmonic analysis on the Heisenberg group

One of the simplest noncommutative groups one can think about is the Heisenberg group introduced in Example
1.4
. This
p
-adic Lie group has been extensively studied and we know explicitly a lot of its properties, for instance we can use the Kirilov orbit method to compute explicitly the unitary dual
\widehat{\mathbb{H}}_{d}(\mathbb{Q}_{p})
of
\mathbb{H}_{d}(\mathbb{Q}_{p})
, or simply
\widehat{\mathbb{H}}_{d}
and
\mathbb{H}_{d}
for short. Unitary irreducible representations of the
p
-adic Heisenberg group follow the same pattern as in the real case. Given
[\pi]\in\widehat{\mathbb{H}}_{d}
we have two possibilities:
\pi
restricted to the center
\mathcal{Z}(\mathbb{H}_{d})
of
G=\mathbb{H}_{d}(\mathbb{Q}_{p})
is trivial, or it is not. In the first case, since
\mathbb{H}_{d}/\mathcal{Z}(\mathbb{H}_{d})\cong\mathbb{Q}_{p}^{2d}
is abelian, so the representation must have the form

\pi_{\xi,\eta}(\textbf{x},\textbf{y},z)=e^{2\pi i\{\xi\cdot\textbf{x}+\eta\cdot\textbf{y}\}_{p}},\text{ }\text{ }(\xi,\eta)\in\mathbb{Q}_{p}^{2d}.

In the second case it must hold
\pi(\textbf{x},\textbf{y},z)|_{\mathcal{Z}(\mathbb{H}_{d})}=e^{2\pi i\{\lambda z\}_{p}}
for some
\lambda\in\mathbb{Q}_{p}^{*}
, and by the Kirillov orbith method we know that the
p
-adic Schrodinger representations

\pi_{\lambda}(\textbf{x},\textbf{y},z)=e^{2\pi i\{\lambda(z+\textbf{x}\cdot\textbf{y}+\textbf{y}\cdot u)\}_{p}}\varphi(u+x),\text{ }\text{ }\varphi\in L^{2}(\mathbb{Q}_{p}^{d}),

are unitary irreducible representations of
\mathbb{H}_{d}
on the Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}_{\lambda}:=L^{2}(\mathbb{Q}_{p}^{d})
. Moreover, we can check that any unitary irreducible representation of
\mathbb{H}_{d}
non trivial on the center is equivalent to some of the representations
\pi_{\lambda}
. In this way we can identify the unitary dual
\widehat{\mathbb{H}}_{d}
with the set
\mathbb{Q}_{p}^{*}\cup\mathbb{Q}_{p}^{2d}
. There is a Borel measure on
\mathbb{Q}_{p}^{*}\cup\mathbb{Q}_{p}^{2d}
that can be transferred to
\widehat{\mathbb{H}}_{d}
and that assigns measure zero to the elements of
\mathbb{Q}_{p}^{2d}
. For that reason the relevant representations for us are the ones parameterized by
\mathbb{Q}_{p}^{*}
and we will mostly think on the unitary dual of
\mathbb{H}_{d}
as the collection of Schrodinger representations
\pi_{\lambda}.

Remark 3.4
.

Schrodinger representations have several possible explicit realisations in the real case, that is, for the Heisenberg group over the real numbers. For instance in
[
12
]
the authors used a different approach, in such a way that all the Schrodinger representations
\pi_{\lambda}
are obtained by composing the representations
\pi_{1}
and
\pi_{-1}
with the dilations
D_{\lambda}
. We can do a similar thing in the
p
-adic case with the difference that this time we cannot count with the existence of square roots. More precisely, after identifying
\widehat{\mathbb{H}}_{d}
with
\mathbb{Q}_{p}^{*}
, we can find a finite subset
\widehat{G}_{0}
of
\widehat{G}
such that every
\pi_{\lambda}
with
\lambda\in\mathbb{Q}_{p}^{*}
is equivalent to
\pi_{\epsilon}\circ D_{\mu}
for some
\epsilon\in\widehat{G}_{0}
and
\mu\in\mathbb{Q}_{p}^{*}
. To see this let us point to the fact that any nonzero
p
-adic number
\lambda
can be written in one of the following four ways:

•

\lambda=\mu^{2}
,
\mu\in\mathbb{Q}_{p}^{*}
,

•

\lambda=s_{0}\mu^{2}
,
\mu\in\mathbb{Q}_{p}^{*}
and
s_{0}\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}\setminus p\mathbb{Z}_{p}
is not a square,

•

\lambda=s_{0}\mu^{2}
,
\mu\in\mathbb{Q}_{p}^{*}
and
s_{0}\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}\setminus p\mathbb{Z}_{p}
is not a square,

•

\lambda=ps_{0}\mu^{2}
,
\mu\in\mathbb{Q}_{p}^{*}
and
s_{0}\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}\setminus p\mathbb{Z}_{p}
is not a square,

•

\lambda=p\mu^{2}
,
\mu\in\mathbb{Q}_{p}^{*}
.

Fix
L^{2}(\mathbb{Q}_{p}^{d})
as our representation space and consider the linear maps
d_{\mu}:L^{2}(\mathbb{Q}_{p}^{d})\to L^{2}(\mathbb{Q}_{p}^{d})
, given by
d_{\mu}\varphi(u):=\varphi(\mu u)
,
\mu\in\mathbb{Q}_{p}^{*}
. Let
\epsilon\in\{1,p,s_{0},ps_{0}\},
where
s_{0}\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}\setminus p\mathbb{Z}_{p}
is not a square. Then we can see that the composition of the representation

\pi_{\epsilon}(\textbf{x},\textbf{y},z)\varphi(u)=e^{2\pi i\{\epsilon(z+\textbf{x}\cdot\textbf{y}+\textbf{y}\cdot u)\}_{p}}\varphi(u+x),

with the dilation
D_{\mu}
give us

\displaystyle(\pi_{\epsilon}\circ D_{\mu})(\textbf{x},\textbf{y},z)\varphi(u)

\displaystyle=e^{2\pi i\{\epsilon(\mu^{2}z+\frac{\mu^{2}}{2}\textbf{x}\cdot\textbf{y}+\mu\textbf{y}\cdot u)\}_{p}}\varphi(u+\mu x)

\displaystyle=e^{2\pi i\{\epsilon\mu^{2}(z+\textbf{x}\cdot\textbf{y}+\textbf{y}\cdot\frac{u}{\mu})\}_{p}}d_{\mu}\varphi(\frac{u}{\mu}+x)

\displaystyle=(d_{\mu}^{-1}\circ\pi_{\epsilon\mu^{2}}(\textbf{x},\textbf{y},z)\circ d_{\mu})\varphi(u).

This shows that
\pi_{\epsilon}\circ D_{\mu}
and
\pi_{\epsilon\mu^{2}}
are equivalent representations of
\mathbb{H}_{d}(\mathbb{Q}_{p})
on
L^{2}(\mathbb{Q}_{p}^{d})
. Also, we can obtain all the Schrodinger representations by composing the representations
\pi_{\epsilon}
,
\epsilon\in\mathbb{Q}_{p}^{*}/(\mathbb{Q}_{p}^{*})^{2}
, with the dilations of the group.

Remark 3.5
.

For
\mu,\epsilon
and
\lambda
as in the above remark we have the relation
|\mu|_{p}=|\epsilon|_{p}^{-1/2}|\lambda|_{p}^{1/2}
.

With the above realisation of the unitary dual of
\mathbb{H}_{d}
the group Fourier transform takes the following particular form. Given a function
f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{H}_{d})
, its Fourier transform
\widehat{f}(\lambda):=\widehat{f}(\pi_{\lambda})
is the measurable field of bounded operators acting on each representation space
\mathcal{H}_{\lambda}=L^{2}(\mathbb{Q}_{p}^{d})
via the formula

\displaystyle\widehat{f}(\lambda)\varphi(u)

\displaystyle:=\int_{\mathbb{H}_{d}}f(\textbf{x},\textbf{y},z)\pi_{\lambda}^{*}(\textbf{x},\textbf{y},z)\varphi(u)d\textbf{x}d\textbf{y}dz

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{Q}_{p}^{2d+1}}f(\textbf{x},\textbf{y},z)e^{2\pi i\{\lambda(-z+\frac{1}{2}\textbf{x}\cdot\textbf{y}-\textbf{y}\cdot u)\}_{p}}\varphi(u-\textbf{x})d\textbf{x}d\textbf{y}dz

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{Q}_{p}^{d}\times\mathbb{Q}_{p}^{d}}\mathcal{F}_{\mathbb{Q}_{p}^{2d+1}}[f](\xi,\lambda(\frac{u+w}{2}),\lambda)e^{2\pi i\{\xi(u-w)\}_{p}}\varphi(w)dwd\xi.

For any
\lambda\in\mathbb{Q}_{p}^{*}
the operator
\widehat{f}(\lambda)
is a Hilbert-Schmidt operator with integral kernel

K_{f,\lambda}(u,w)=\int_{\mathbb{Q}_{p}^{d}}\mathcal{F}_{\mathbb{Q}_{p}^{2d+1}}[f](\xi,\lambda(\frac{u+w}{2}),\lambda)e^{2\pi i\{\xi(u-w)\}_{p}}d\xi.

Its Hilbert-Schmidt norm is

\|\widehat{f}(\lambda)\|_{HS}=|\lambda|_{p}^{-d/2}\|\mathcal{F}_{\mathbb{Q}_{p}^{2d+1}}[f](\cdot,\cdot,\lambda)\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{Q}_{p}^{2d})},

so the Plancherel formula takes the form

\|f\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{H}_{d})}^{2}=\int_{\mathbb{Q}_{p}^{*}}\|\widehat{f}(\lambda)\|_{HS}^{2}|\lambda|_{p}^{d}d\lambda.

Moreover, we can check that the following Fourier inversion formula:

f(x,y,z)=\int_{\mathbb{Q}_{p}^{*}}Tr[\pi_{\lambda}(x,y,z)\widehat{f}(\lambda)]|\lambda|_{p}^{d}d\lambda.

We are particularly interested in functions
f\in\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{H}_{d})
because of the nice properties of their Fourier transform. We collect some of them in the following proposition.

Proposition 3.6
.

For
f\in\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{H}_{d})
the following properties hold:

(i)

For every
\lambda\in\mathbb{Q}_{p}^{*}
the operator
\widehat{f}(\lambda)
has finite rank and therefore it is trace class.

(ii)

There is an
n_{f}\in\mathbb{Z}
such that
\widehat{f}(\lambda)=0
for
|\lambda|_{p}>p^{n_{f}}.

(iii)

If additionally
f\in\tilde{\mathcal{D}}(\mathbb{H}_{d})
then there is an
l_{f}\in\mathbb{Z}
such that
\widehat{f}(\lambda)=0
for
|\lambda|_{p}<p^{l_{f}}
.

We want to use the group Fourier transform to deal with a special class of left invariant operators that we call here
pseudo-differential operators
. These are densely defined linear operators defined in terms of a
symbol
, which we understand here as a measurable field of linear operators

\sigma_{T}:=\{\sigma_{T}(\lambda):\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\mathbb{Q}_{p}^{d})\to\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\mathbb{Q}_{p}^{d})\text{ };\text{ }\text{ }\lambda\in\mathbb{Q}_{p}^{*}\},

such that for all
\lambda\in\mathbb{Q}_{p}^{*}
it holds:

\mathcal{H}_{\lambda}^{\infty}=\mathcal{D}(L^{2}(\mathbb{Q}_{p}^{d}))\subset Dom(\sigma_{T}(\lambda)):=\{\varphi\in\mathcal{H}_{\lambda}\text{ }:\text{ }\sigma_{T}(\lambda)\varphi\in\mathcal{H}_{\lambda}\}.

Given a symbol
{\sigma_{T}(\lambda)}_{\lambda\in\mathbb{Q}_{p}^{*}}
we can define a densely defined left invariant operator
T
that can be written in terms of its symbol
\sigma_{T}
as

Tf(x,y,z)=\int_{\mathbb{Q}_{p}^{*}}Tr[\pi_{\lambda}(x,y,z)\sigma_{T}(\lambda)\widehat{f}(\lambda)]|\lambda|_{p}^{d}d\lambda,

where the symbol
\sigma_{T}
can be ”recovered” from the operator
T
by means of the expression

\sigma_{T}(\lambda):=\pi_{\lambda}^{*}(x,y,z)T\pi_{\lambda}(x,y,z)=T\pi_{\lambda}(0,0,0)=\mathcal{F}_{\mathbb{H}_{d}}[\textbf{k}](\lambda)=\widehat{\textbf{k}}_{T}(\lambda),

where
\textbf{k}_{T}
is the right convolution kernel of
T
. An advantage of this symbolic representation is that in case every operator
\sigma_{T}(\lambda)
is invertible, and the inverses form a measurable field of bounded operators, a (formal) fundamental solution for
T
is given by

\langle E_{T},f\rangle:=\int_{\mathbb{Q}_{p}^{*}}Tr[\widehat{f\circ\iota}(\lambda)\sigma_{T}(\lambda)^{-1}]|\lambda|_{p}^{d}d\lambda,

where
f\circ\iota(x,y,z):=f((x,y,z)^{-1})
and the above expression is well defined because by Proposition
3.6
the above integral is finite for any
f\in\tilde{\mathcal{D}}(\mathbb{H}_{d})
. In particular, in the case where
T
is a
\nu
-homogeneous operator of positive degree the invertibility of the symbol
\sigma_{T}
reduces to its invertibility on a finite number of the representation spaces. The reason is that a pseudo-differential operator
T
is homogeneous if and only if its associated symbol
\sigma_{T}
has the following property:

d_{\gamma}^{-1}\circ\sigma_{T}(\gamma^{2}\lambda)\circ d_{\gamma}=|\gamma|_{p}^{\nu}\sigma_{T}(\lambda),

where
d_{\gamma}:L^{2}(\mathbb{Q}_{p}^{d})\to L^{2}(\mathbb{Q}_{p}^{d})
is defined as
d_{\gamma}\varphi(u):=\varphi(\gamma u)
. Consequently, in order to check the invertibility of
\sigma_{T}
on each representation space we only need to check the invertibility of
\sigma_{T}(\varepsilon)
for
\varepsilon\in\mathbb{Q}_{p}^{*}/(\mathbb{Q}_{p}^{*})^{2}
. We will ilustrate all this in the next subsection by using as an example the Vladimirov Laplacian on
G
.

3.3.
The Vladimirov Laplacian on the Heisenberg group

Finally we have collected all the necessary ingredients to fulfil the main goal of this section: to show the existence of a fundamental solution for the Vladimirov Laplacian
\mathfrak{L}^{\alpha}
,
\alpha>0
, on
\mathbb{H}_{d}
, and to give some estimate on its heat kernel. Recall that the Vladimirov Laplacian on
\mathbb{H}_{d}
is the left invariant linear operator defined in terms of the VT directional operators as:

\mathfrak{L}^{\alpha}=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\partial_{X_{j}}^{\alpha}+\partial_{Y_{j}}^{\alpha}+\partial_{Z}^{\alpha/2}.

The operators
\partial^{\alpha}_{X_{j}},\partial_{Y_{j}}^{\alpha},\partial_{Z}^{\alpha}
are left invariant pseudo-differential operators and their associated symbols are:

•

\displaystyle\sigma_{\partial_{X_{j}}^{\alpha}}(\lambda)\varphi(u):=\partial_{X_{j}}^{\alpha}\pi_{\lambda}(0,0,0)\varphi(u)

\displaystyle=\frac{1-p^{\alpha}}{1-p^{-(\alpha+1)}}\int_{\mathbb{Q}_{p}}\frac{\varphi(u-u_{j})-\varphi(u)}{|u_{j}|_{p}^{\alpha+1}}du_{j}\ =\partial_{u_{j}}^{\alpha}\varphi(u).

•

\displaystyle\sigma_{\partial_{Y_{j}}^{\alpha}}(\lambda)\varphi(u):=\partial_{Y_{j}}^{\alpha}\pi_{\lambda}(0,0,0)\varphi(u)

\displaystyle=\frac{1-p^{\alpha}}{1-p^{-(\alpha+1)}}\int_{\mathbb{Q}_{p}}\frac{e^{2\pi i\{\lambda u_{j}y_{j}\}_{p}}-1}{|y_{j}|_{p}^{\alpha+1}}dy_{j}\varphi(u)=|\lambda|_{p}^{\alpha}|u_{j}|^{\alpha}\varphi(u).

•

\displaystyle\sigma_{\partial_{Z}^{\alpha}}(\lambda)\varphi(u):=\partial_{Z}^{\alpha}\pi_{\lambda}(0,0,0)\varphi(u)

\displaystyle=\frac{1-p^{\alpha}}{1-p^{-(\alpha+1)}}\int_{\mathbb{Q}_{p}}\frac{e^{2\pi i\{\lambda z\}_{p}}-1}{|z|_{p}^{\alpha+1}}dz\varphi(u)=|\lambda|_{p}^{\alpha}\varphi(u).

In this way we can see that the symbol of
\mathfrak{L}^{\alpha}
is given by

\sigma_{\mathfrak{L}^{\alpha}}(\lambda)\varphi(u)=\big(\sum_{j=1}^{d}\partial_{u_{j}}^{\alpha}+|\lambda|_{p}^{\alpha}|u_{j}|^{\alpha}\big)\varphi(u)+|\lambda|_{p}^{\alpha/2}\varphi(u),

which is a Schrodinger type operator like the ones studied by the authors in
[
30
, Chapter X]
. We can apply their results to each operator

\partial_{u_{j}}^{\alpha}+|\lambda|_{p}^{\alpha}|u_{j}|^{\alpha},

to prove that each
\sigma_{\mathfrak{L}^{\alpha}}(\lambda)
is self adjoint in
\mathcal{H}_{\pi_{\lambda}}=L^{2}(\mathbb{Q}_{p}^{d})
and invertible with compact inverse. We collect the results in
[
30
, Chapter X]
in the following lemma.

Lemma 3.7
.

Let
\sigma(v)
and
V(x)
be positive functions such that they tend to
+\infty
at infinity in
\mathbb{Q}_{p}.
Then the operator
T_{\sigma}+V
is positive, bounded from below and self adjoint on
L^{2}(\mathbb{Q}_{p})
, and its spectrum is discrete and it consist of real eigenvalues
\lambda_{1}\leq\lambda_{2}\leq...,

\lambda_{k}\to\infty
as
k\to\infty
, where every eigenvalue has finite multiplicity. The corresponding eigenfunctions
\psi_{k}\in D(T_{\sigma}+V)
form an orthonormal basis of
L^{2}(\mathbb{Q}_{p})
and the following variational principle is valid:

\lambda_{k}=\min_{\varphi_{1},...,\varphi_{k}}\max\{((T_{\sigma}+V)\psi,\psi)_{L^{2}(\mathbb{Q}_{p})}\text{ }:\text{ }\psi\in Span\{\varphi_{1},...,\varphi_{k}\},\text{ }\|\psi\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{Q}_{p})}=1\}.

As a corollary of the above lemma the operator
\sigma_{\mathfrak{L}^{\alpha}}(\lambda)
is bounded from below and self adjoint on
L^{2}(\mathbb{Q}_{p}^{d})
with compact inverse. Moreover,
\mathfrak{L}^{\alpha}
is essentially self adjoint and for its self adjoint extension to
L^{2}(\mathbb{H}_{d})
, here denoted by
\mathfrak{L}^{\alpha}_{2}
, the following version of the functional calculus applies.

Lemma 3.8
.

Let
E,E_{\lambda}
be the spectral measures of
\mathfrak{L}^{\alpha}_{2}
and
\sigma_{\mathfrak{L}^{\alpha}}(\lambda)
respectively, so that

\mathfrak{L}^{\alpha}_{2}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}sdE(s)\text{ }\text{ }\text{ }\text{and}\text{ }\text{ }\text{ }\sigma_{\mathfrak{L}^{\alpha}}(\lambda)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}sdE_{\lambda}(s).

Then for any borel subset
B\subset\mathbb{R}
, the orthogonal projection
E(B)
is a bounded left invariant operator. The group Fourier transform of its convolution kernel
E(B)\delta_{0}
is a measurable field of bounded operators given by

\mathcal{F}_{\mathbb{H}_{d}}(E(B)\delta_{e})(\pi_{\lambda})=E_{\lambda}(B).

Moreover, if
\phi
is a measurable function on
\mathbb{R}
, the spectral multiplier operator
\phi(\mathfrak{L}_{2}^{\alpha})
is defined by

\phi(\mathfrak{L}_{2}^{\alpha}):=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\phi(s)dE(s),

and its domain
Dom(\phi(\mathfrak{L}_{2}^{\alpha}))
is the space of functions
f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{H}_{d})
such that

\int_{\mathbb{R}}|\phi(s)|^{2}(dE(s)f,f)<\infty.

In particular, if
\phi\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
then
\phi(\mathfrak{L}_{2}^{\alpha})
is left invariant and bounded on
L^{2}(\mathbb{H}_{d})
, and its convolution kernel, denoted by
\phi(\mathfrak{L}_{2}^{\alpha})\delta_{e}
, is the unique distribution
\phi(\mathfrak{L}_{2}^{\alpha})\delta_{e}\in\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{H}_{d})^{\prime}
such that

\phi(\mathfrak{L}_{2}^{\alpha})f=f*\phi(\mathfrak{L}_{2}^{\alpha})\delta_{e},

for all
f\in\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{H}_{d})
, and its group Fourier transform is

\mathcal{F}\{\phi(\mathfrak{L}_{2}^{\alpha})\delta_{e}\}(\pi_{\lambda})=\phi(\sigma_{\mathfrak{L}^{\alpha}}(\lambda)).

By applying the above functional calculus to
\mathfrak{L}^{\alpha}
with
\phi_{t}(s)=e^{-ts}
we can check that the convolution kernel of the heat semigroup
e^{-t\mathfrak{L}_{2}^{\alpha}}
, i.e. the heat kernel of
\mathfrak{L}^{\alpha}
, has the following explicit representation:

(2)

h_{\mathfrak{L}^{\alpha}_{2}}(t,x,y,z)=\int_{\mathbb{Q}_{p}^{*}}Tr[\pi_{\lambda}(x,y,z)e^{-t\sigma_{\mathfrak{L}^{\alpha}}(\lambda)}]|\lambda|_{p}^{d}d\lambda.

We collect some properties of the above kernel in the following theorem.

Theorem 3.9
.

The heat kernel
h_{\mathfrak{L}^{\alpha}_{2}}
associated to
\mathfrak{L}^{\alpha}
has the following properties:

(i)

h_{\mathfrak{L}^{\alpha}_{2}}(t,\cdot)*h_{\mathfrak{L}^{\alpha}_{2}}(s,\cdot)=h_{\mathfrak{L}^{\alpha}_{2}}(t+s,\cdot)
, for any
s,t>0
.

(ii)

h_{\mathfrak{L}^{\alpha}_{2}}(|\gamma|_{p}^{\alpha}t,\gamma x,\gamma y,\gamma^{2}z)=|\gamma|^{-(2d+2)}h_{\mathfrak{L}^{\alpha}_{2}}(t,x,y,z)
, for all
(x,y,z)\in\mathbb{H}_{d}
and any
t>0
,
\gamma\in\mathbb{Q}_{p}^{*}
.

(iii)

h_{\mathfrak{L}^{\alpha}_{2}}(t,x,y,z)=\overline{h_{\mathfrak{L}^{\alpha}_{2}}(t,(x,y,z)^{-1})},
for all
x\in\mathbb{H}_{d}
.

(iv)

The heat semigroup
e^{-t\mathfrak{L}_{2}^{\alpha}}
is symmetric and Markovian. Moreover, the following estimate holds for its kernel:

h_{\mathfrak{L}^{\alpha}_{2}}(t,x,y,z)\asymp t^{-(2d+2)/\alpha}\prod_{j=1}^{d}\min\{1,\frac{t^{1+1/\alpha}}{|x_{j}|_{p}^{1+\alpha}}\}\times\prod_{j=1}^{d}\min\{1,\frac{t^{1+1/\alpha}}{|y_{j}|_{p}^{1+\alpha}}\}\times\min\{1,\frac{t^{1+2/\alpha}}{|z|_{p}^{1+\alpha/2}}\}.

In particular for
(x,y,z)\in G_{0}\setminus G_{1}
:

h_{\mathfrak{L}^{\alpha}_{2}}(t,x,y,z)\asymp t^{-(2d+2)/\alpha}.

Proof.

(i)

Just notice that

\mathcal{F}[h_{\mathfrak{L}^{\alpha}_{2}}(t,\cdot)*h_{\mathfrak{L}^{\alpha}_{2}}(s,\cdot)](\lambda)=e^{-s\sigma_{\mathfrak{L}^{\alpha}}(\lambda)}e^{-t\sigma_{\mathfrak{L}^{\alpha}}(\lambda)}=e^{-(t+s)\sigma_{\mathfrak{L}^{\alpha}}(\lambda)}=\mathcal{F}[h_{\mathfrak{L}^{\alpha}_{2}}(t+s,\cdot)](\lambda).

(ii)

Using a simple change of variable and the
\alpha
-homogeneity of
\mathfrak{L}^{\alpha}
we get:

\displaystyle h_{\mathfrak{L}^{\alpha}_{2}}(|\gamma|_{p}^{\alpha}t,\gamma x,\gamma y,\gamma^{2}z)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{Q}_{p}^{*}}Tr[\pi_{\lambda}(\gamma x,\gamma y,\gamma^{2}z)e^{-t|\gamma|_{p}^{\alpha}\sigma_{\mathfrak{L}^{\alpha}}(\lambda)}]|\lambda|_{p}^{d}d\lambda

\displaystyle=|\gamma^{2}|_{p}^{-(d+1)}\int_{\mathbb{Q}_{p}^{*}}Tr[d_{\gamma}^{-1}\pi_{\lambda\gamma^{2}}(x,y,z)d_{\gamma}e^{-td_{\gamma}^{-1}\sigma_{\mathfrak{L}^{\alpha}}(\lambda\gamma^{2})d_{\gamma}}]|\gamma^{2}\lambda|_{p}^{d}|\gamma^{2}|_{p}d\lambda

\displaystyle=|\gamma|_{p}^{-(2d+2)}\int_{\mathbb{Q}_{p}^{*}}Tr[\pi_{\lambda}(x,y,z)e^{-t\sigma_{\mathfrak{L}^{\alpha}}(\lambda)}]|\lambda|_{p}^{d}d\lambda

\displaystyle=|\gamma|_{p}^{-(2d+2)}h_{\mathfrak{L}^{\alpha}_{2}}(t,x,y,z).

(iii)

\displaystyle\overline{h_{\mathfrak{L}^{\alpha}_{2}}(t,(x,y,z)^{-1})}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{Q}_{p}^{*}}\overline{Tr[\pi_{\lambda}(x,y,z)^{-1}e^{-t\sigma_{\mathfrak{L}^{\alpha}}(\lambda)}]}|\lambda|_{p}^{d}d\lambda

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{Q}_{p}^{*}}Tr[\pi_{\lambda}(x,y,z)e^{-t\sigma_{\mathfrak{L}^{\alpha}}(\lambda)}]|\lambda|_{p}^{d}d\lambda=h_{\mathfrak{L}^{\alpha}_{2}}(t,x,y,z).

(iv)

This follows from the analysis for the Vladimirov Laplacian on
\mathbb{Q}_{p}^{d}
done in
[
4
, Section 5.3]
, see
[
4
, Proposition 5.13]
.

∎

As a consequence of the above lemma we can obtain the fundamental solution of
\mathfrak{L}^{\alpha}
by integrating the heat kernel when
0<\alpha<2d+2.

Corollary 3.10
.

Let
0<\alpha<2d+2.
Then a fundamental solution for the Vladimirov Laplacian exists and it defines an
\alpha-(2d+2)
-homogeneous function given by

\textbf{h}_{\mathfrak{L}^{\alpha}_{2}}(x,y,z):=\int_{0}^{\infty}h_{\mathfrak{L}^{\alpha}_{2}}(t,x,y,z)dt.

In consequence:

\textbf{h}_{\mathfrak{L}^{\alpha}_{2}}(x,y,z)\asymp|(x,y,z)|_{G}^{\alpha-(2d+2)}.

Proof.

First,
\textbf{h}_{\mathfrak{L}^{\alpha}_{2}}
is well defined because of the estimate on the heat kernel provided in Lemma
3.13
. Clearly
\textbf{h}_{\mathfrak{L}^{\alpha}_{2}}
defines a fundamental solution for
\mathfrak{L}^{\alpha}
so we only need to check the homogeneity property. Using Lemma
3.13
again and a change of variables we get:

\displaystyle\textbf{h}_{\mathfrak{L}^{\alpha}_{2}}(\gamma x,\gamma y,\gamma^{2}z)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}h_{\mathfrak{L}^{\alpha}_{2}}(t,\gamma x,\gamma y,\gamma^{2}z)dt

\displaystyle=|\gamma|_{p}^{\alpha}\int_{0}^{\infty}h_{\mathfrak{L}^{\alpha}_{2}}(|\gamma|_{p}^{\alpha}(|\gamma|_{p}^{-\alpha}t),\gamma x,\gamma y,\gamma^{2}z)|\gamma|_{p}^{-\alpha}dt

\displaystyle=|\gamma|_{p}^{\alpha}\int_{0}^{\infty}h_{\mathfrak{L}^{\alpha}_{2}}(|\gamma|_{p}^{\alpha}t,\gamma x,\gamma y,\gamma^{2}z)dt

\displaystyle=|\gamma|_{p}^{\alpha-(2d+2)}\int_{0}^{\infty}h_{\mathfrak{L}^{\alpha}_{2}}(t,x,y,z)dt

\displaystyle=|\gamma|_{p}^{\alpha-(2d+2)}\textbf{h}_{\mathfrak{L}^{\alpha}_{2}}(x,y,z).

∎

Another consequence of the functional calculus for
\mathfrak{L}_{2}^{\alpha}
and Theorem
3.13
is that fractional powers of
\mathfrak{L}_{2}^{\alpha}
and their fundamental solutions exist and they are well defined homogeneous distributions.

Corollary 3.11
.

Let
\beta\in\mathbb{C}
such that
0<\mathfrak{Re}(\beta)<2d+2
. Then the linear operator
L^{\beta}:=(\mathfrak{L}_{2}^{\alpha})^{\beta/\alpha}
defined via functional calculus possesses a fundamental solution, which is an
\beta-(2d+2)
homogeneous distribution, determined by the Riesz potential

\mathcal{I}_{\beta}(x,y,z):=\frac{1}{\Gamma(\beta/\alpha)}\int_{0}^{\infty}t^{\beta/\alpha-1}h_{\mathfrak{L}^{\alpha}_{2}}(t,x,y,z)dt.

To conclude this section let us remark that for the case
\alpha\geq 2d+2
, it is also possible to obtain a fundamental solution in the formal sense because for that we only need the invertibility of the symbol. The same is true for the operator

\mathfrak{L}^{\alpha}_{sub}=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\partial_{X_{j}}^{\alpha}+\partial_{Y_{j}}^{\alpha},\text{ }\text{ }\alpha>0,

that we call here the ”Vladimirov sub-Laplacian”. The symbol of the above
\alpha
-homogeneous operator is

\sigma_{\mathfrak{L}^{\alpha}_{sub}}(\lambda)\varphi(u)=\big(\sum_{j=1}^{d}\partial_{u_{j}}^{\alpha}+|\lambda|_{p}^{\alpha}|u_{j}|^{\alpha}\big)\varphi(u),

and by Lemma
3.7
a functional calculus like in Lemma
3.8
exists for this operator. However, without the estimates on the heat kernel of the operator we can only construct a formal fundamental solution

\langle E_{\mathfrak{L}^{\alpha}_{sub}},f\rangle:=\int_{\mathbb{Q}_{p}^{*}}Tr[\widehat{f\circ\iota}(\lambda)\sigma_{\mathfrak{L}^{\alpha}_{sub}}(\lambda)^{-1}]|\lambda|_{p}^{d}d\lambda,

where
f\circ\iota(x,y,z):=f((x,y,z)^{-1})
, so that a very interesting problem would be to provide some heat kernel estimates for
\mathfrak{L}^{\alpha}_{sub}
. More generally, since our analysis here to obtain the fundamental solution of
\mathfrak{L}^{\alpha}
work in principle for graded
\mathbb{K}
-Lie groups, an interesting problem in this setting is to prove the existence of a fundamental solution for more general homogeneous operators. This has been done already for real Lie groups with ideas very similar to the ones we use here but there are some technical difficulties that one must overcome. Anyway, by now we restrict our attention to the Vladimirov Laplacian on some groups like
\mathbb{H}_{d}
and
\mathbb{E}_{4}
where we have all the necessary ingredients for our proofs.

3.4.
The group
\mathbb{E}_{4}

To conclude our work we want to give a quick review of the way our arguments work for the Engel group
\mathbb{E}_{4}
. The analysis is the same as for the Heisenberg group so we only need to precise the details that are different from our exposition on
\mathbb{H}_{d}
. The Engel group
\mathbb{E}_{4}
introduced in
1.6
is a graded group for which the Kirillov theory applies and we can obtain explicitly the unitary dual
\widehat{\mathbb{E}}_{4}
of
\mathbb{E}_{4}
in terms of the orbits
\mathcal{O}
of the co-adjoint action of
\mathbb{E}_{4}
. There is a Borel measure on
\widehat{\mathbb{E}}_{4}
which assigns measure zero to all the co-adjoint orbits except for those corresponding with the representations

\pi_{\lambda,\mu}(x,y_{1},y_{2},y_{3})\varphi(u)=e^{2\pi i\{-\frac{\mu}{2\lambda}y_{1}+\lambda y_{3}-\lambda y_{2}u+\frac{\lambda}{2}y_{1}u^{2}\}_{p}}\varphi(u+x),

so that we can identify
\widehat{\mathbb{E}}_{4}
with
\mathbb{Q}_{p}^{4}
. With this identification, the group Fourier transform for functions in
L^{2}(\mathbb{E}_{4})
takes the form

\displaystyle\widehat{f}(\lambda,\mu)\varphi(u):=\widehat{f}(\pi_{\lambda,\mu})\varphi(u)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{E}_{4}}f(x,y_{1},y_{2},y_{3})\pi_{\lambda,\mu}(x,y_{1},y_{2},y_{3})\varphi(u)dx

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{Q}_{p}}\Big(\int_{\mathbb{Q}_{p}}\mathcal{F}_{\mathbb{Q}_{p}^{4}}[f](\xi,\frac{\lambda}{2}v^{2}-\frac{\mu}{2\lambda},-\lambda v,\lambda)e^{2\pi i\{(u-v)\xi\}_{p}}\Big)\varphi(v)dv,

and in particular for functions
f\in\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{E}_{4})
we have the following properties:

Proposition 3.12
.

For
f\in\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{E}_{4})
the following properties hold:

(i)

For every
\lambda,\mu\in\mathbb{Q}_{p}
, the operator
\widehat{f}(\pi_{\lambda,\mu})
has finite rank and therefore it is trace class.

(ii)

There is an
n_{f}\in\mathbb{Z}
such that
\widehat{f}(\pi_{\lambda,\mu})=0
for
\|(\lambda,\mu)\|_{p}>p^{n_{f}}.

(iii)

If additionally
f\in\tilde{\mathcal{D}}(\mathbb{E}_{4})
, then there is an
l_{f}\in\mathbb{Z}
such that
\widehat{f}(\lambda)=0
for
\|(\lambda,\mu)\|_{p}<p^{l_{f}}
.

For the group
\mathbb{E}_{4}
the VT directional operators
\partial^{\alpha}_{X},\partial_{Y_{j}}^{\alpha}
are left invariant pseudo-differential operators and their associated symbols are:

•

\displaystyle\sigma_{\partial_{X}^{\alpha}}(\lambda)\varphi(u):=\partial_{X}^{\alpha}\pi_{\lambda}(0,0,0)\varphi(u)

\displaystyle=\frac{1-p^{\alpha}}{1-p^{-(\alpha+1)}}\int_{\mathbb{Q}_{p}}\frac{\varphi(u-u_{j})-\varphi(u)}{|u_{j}|_{p}^{\alpha+1}}du_{j}\ =D_{u}^{\alpha}\varphi(u).

•

\displaystyle\sigma_{\partial_{Y_{1}}^{\alpha}}(\lambda)\varphi(u):=\partial_{Y_{1}}^{\alpha}\pi_{\lambda}(0,0,0)\varphi(u)

\displaystyle=\frac{1-p^{\alpha}}{1-p^{-(\alpha+1)}}\int_{\mathbb{Q}_{p}}\frac{e^{2\pi i\{y_{1}(\frac{\lambda}{2}u^{2}-\frac{\mu}{2\lambda}))\}_{p}}-1}{|y_{j}|_{p}^{\alpha+1}}dy_{1}\varphi(u)

\displaystyle=|\frac{\lambda}{2}u^{2}-\frac{\mu}{2\lambda}|_{p}^{\alpha}\varphi(u).

•

\displaystyle\sigma_{\partial_{Y_{2}}^{\alpha}}(\lambda)\varphi(u):=\partial_{Y_{2}}^{\alpha}\pi_{\lambda}(0,0,0)\varphi(u)

\displaystyle=|\lambda u|_{p}^{\alpha}\varphi(u).

•

\displaystyle\sigma_{\partial_{Y_{3}}^{\alpha}}(\lambda)\varphi(u):=\partial_{Y_{3}}^{\alpha}\pi_{\lambda}(0,0,0)\varphi(u)

\displaystyle=\frac{1-p^{\alpha}}{1-p^{-(\alpha+1)}}\int_{\mathbb{Q}_{p}}\frac{e^{2\pi i\{\lambda y_{3}\}_{p}}-1}{|y_{3}|_{p}^{\alpha+1}}dy_{3}\varphi(u)=|\lambda|_{p}^{\alpha}\varphi(u).

Hence, the Vladimirov Laplacian on
\mathbb{E}_{4}

\mathfrak{L}^{\alpha}=\partial_{X}^{\alpha}+\partial_{Y_{1}}^{\alpha}+\partial_{Y_{2}}^{\alpha/2}+\partial_{Y_{3}}^{\alpha/3},\text{ }\text{ }\text{ }\alpha>0,

is a left invariant
\alpha
-homogeneous pseudo-differential operator with symbol

\sigma_{\mathfrak{L}^{\alpha}}(\lambda,\mu)\varphi(u)=(D_{u}^{\alpha}+|\frac{\lambda}{2}u^{2}-\frac{\mu}{2\lambda}|_{p}^{\alpha}+|\lambda u|_{p}^{\alpha/2}+|\lambda|_{p}^{\alpha/3})\varphi(u),

and by using again Lemma
3.7
we can check that
\sigma_{\mathfrak{L}^{\alpha}}(\lambda,\mu)
defines an invertible self-adjoint operator with compact inverse in each representation space. Thus, we can associate to
\mathfrak{L}_{2}^{\alpha}
, the self-adjoint extension of
\mathfrak{L}^{\alpha}
, a functional calculus and we can obtain the following theorem:

Theorem 3.13
.

The heat kernel
h_{\mathfrak{L}^{\alpha}_{2}}
associated to
\mathfrak{L}^{\alpha}
has the following properties:

(i)

h_{\mathfrak{L}^{\alpha}_{2}}(t,\cdot)*h_{\mathfrak{L}^{\alpha}_{2}}(s,\cdot)=h_{\mathfrak{L}^{\alpha}_{2}}(t+s,\cdot)
, for any
s,t>0
.

(ii)

h_{\mathfrak{L}^{\alpha}_{2}}(|\gamma|_{p}^{\alpha}t,\gamma x,\gamma y_{1},\gamma^{2}y_{2},\gamma^{3}y_{3})=|\gamma|^{-7}h_{\mathfrak{L}^{\alpha}_{2}}(t,x,y_{1},y_{2},y_{3})
, for all
(x,y_{1},y_{2},y_{3})\in\mathbb{E}_{4}
and any
t>0
,
\gamma\in\mathbb{Q}_{p}^{*}
.

(iii)

h_{\mathfrak{L}^{\alpha}_{2}}(t,x,y_{1},y_{2},y_{3})=\overline{h_{\mathfrak{L}^{\alpha}_{2}}(t,(x,y_{1},y_{2},y_{3})^{-1})},
for all
(x,y_{1},y_{2},y_{3})\in\mathbb{E}_{4}
.

(iv)

The heat semigroup
e^{-t\mathfrak{L}_{2}^{\alpha}}
is symmetric and Markovian. Moreover, the following estimate holds for its kernel:

h_{\mathfrak{L}^{\alpha}_{2}}(t,x,y_{1},y_{2},y_{3})\asymp t^{-7/\alpha}.

Corollary 3.14
.

Let
0<\alpha<7.
Then a fundamental solution for the Vladimirov Laplacian exists and it defines an
\alpha-7
-homogeneous function given by

\textbf{h}_{\mathfrak{L}^{\alpha}_{2}}(x,y_{1},y_{2},y_{3}):=\int_{0}^{\infty}h_{\mathfrak{L}^{\alpha}_{2}}(t,x,y_{1},y_{2},y_{3})dt.

Consequently:

\textbf{h}_{\mathfrak{L}^{\alpha}_{2}}(x,y_{1},y_{2},y_{3})\asymp|(x,y_{1},y_{2},y_{3})|_{\mathbb{E}_{4}}^{\alpha-7}.

Corollary 3.15
.

Let
\beta\in\mathbb{C}
such that
0<\mathfrak{Re}(\beta)<7
. Then the linear operator
L^{\beta}:=(\mathfrak{L}_{2}^{\alpha})^{\beta/\alpha}
defined via functional calculus possesses a fundamental solution, which is an
\beta-7
homogeneous distribution, determined by the Riesz potential

\mathcal{I}_{\beta}(x,y_{1},y_{2},y_{3}):=\frac{1}{\Gamma(\beta/\alpha)}\int_{0}^{\infty}t^{\beta/\alpha-1}h_{\mathfrak{L}^{\alpha}_{2}}(t,x,y_{1},y_{2},y_{3})dt.
