Uniform Operational Consistent Query Answering
Marco Calautti, Ester Livshits, Andreas Pieris, Markus Schneider

Abstract
. Operational consistent query answering (CQA) is a recent framework for CQA, based on revised definitions of repairs and consistent answers, which opens up the possibility of efficient approximations with explicit error guarantees. The main idea is to iteratively apply operations (e.g., fact deletions), starting from an inconsistent database, until we reach a database that is consistent w.r.t. the given set of constraints. This gives us the flexibility of choosing the probability with which we apply an operation, which in turn allows us to calculate the probability of an operational repair, and thus, the probability with which a consistent answer is entailed. A natural way of assigning probabilities to operations is by targeting the uniform probability distribution over a reasonable space such as the set of operational repairs, the set of sequences of operations that lead to an operational repair, and the set of available operations at a certain step of the repairing process. This leads to what we generally call uniform operational CQA. The goal of this work is to perform a data complexity analysis of both exact and approximate uniform operational CQA, focusing on functional dependencies (and subclasses thereof), and conjunctive queries. The main outcome of our analysis (among other positive and negative results), is that uniform operational CQA pushes the efficiency boundaries further by ensuring the existence of efficient approximation schemes in scenarios that go beyond the simple case of primary keys, which seems to be the limit of the classical approach to CQA.



1.
Introduction

Consistent query answering (CQA) is an elegant framework, introduced in the late 1990s by Arenas, Bertossi, and Chomicki
(
Arenas
et al. 1999
)
, that allows us to compute conceptually meaningful answers to queries posed over inconsistent databases, that is, databases that do not conform to their specifications.
The key elements underlying CQA are (i) the notion of
(database) repair
of an inconsistent database
D
, that is, a consistent database whose difference with
D
is somehow minimal, and (ii) the notion of query answering based on
consistent answers
, that is, answers that are entailed by every repair.
Since deciding whether a candidate answer is a consistent answer is most commonly intractable in data complexity (in fact, even for primary keys and conjunctive queries, the problem is coNP-hard
(
Chomicki and
Marcinkowski 2005
)
), there was a great effort on drawing the tractability boundary for CQA; see, e.g.,
(
Fuxman
et al. 2005
;
Fuxman and Miller 2007
;
Geerts
et al. 2015
;
Koutris and Suciu 2014
;
Koutris and
Wijsen 2015
;
Koutris and
Wijsen 2021
)
.
Much of this effort led to interesting dichotomy results that precisely characterize when CQA is tractable/intractable in data complexity.
However, the tractable fragments do not cover many relevant scenarios that go beyond primary keys.

As extensively argued in
(
Calautti
et al. 2018
)
, the goal of a practically applicable CQA approach should be efficient approximate query answering with explicit error guarantees rather than exact query answering. In the realm of the CQA approach described above, one could try to devise efficient probabilistic algorithms with bounded one- or two-sided error. However, it is unlikely that such algorithms exist since, even for very simple scenarios (e.g., primary keys and conjunctive queries), placing the problem in tractable randomized complexity classes such as RP or BPP would imply that the polynomial hierarchy collapses
(
Karp and Lipton 1980
)
.
Another promising idea is to replace the rather strict notion of consistent answers with the more refined notion of relative frequency, that is, the percentage of repairs that entail an answer, and then try to approximate it via a fully polynomial-time randomized approximation scheme (FPRAS); computing it exactly is, unsurprisingly,
\sharp
P-hard
(
Maslowski and
Wijsen 2013
)
. Indeed, for primary keys and conjunctive queries, one can approximate the relative frequency via an FPRAS; this is implicit in
(
Dalvi and Suciu 2007
)
, and it has been made explicit in
(
Calautti
et al. 2019
)
. Moreover, a recent experimental evaluation revealed that approximate CQA in the presence of primary keys and conjunctive queries is not unrealistic in practice
(
Calautti
et al. 2021a
)
.
However, it seems that the simple case of primary keys is the limit of this approach. We have strong indications that in the case of FDs the problem of computing the relative frequency does not admit an FPRAS, while in the case of keys it is a highly non-trivial problem
(
Calautti et al. 2021b
)
.

The above limitations of the classical CQA approach led the authors of
(
Calautti
et al. 2018
)
to propose a new framework for CQA, based on revised definitions of repairs and consistent answers, which opens up the possibility of efficient approximations with error guarantees. The main idea underlying this new framework is to replace the declarative approach to repairs with an
operational
one that explains the process of constructing a repair.
In other words, we can iteratively apply operations (e.g., fact deletions), starting from an inconsistent database, until we reach a database that is consistent w.r.t. the given set of constraints. This gives us the flexibility of choosing the probability with which we apply an operation, which in turn allows us to calculate the probability of an operational repair, and thus, the probability with which an answer is entailed.

Probabilities can be naturally assigned to operations in many scenarios leading to inconsistencies. This is illustrated by the following example from
(
Calautti
et al. 2018
)
.
Consider a data integration scenario that results in a database containing the facts
{\rm Emp}(1,{\rm Alice})
and
{\rm Emp}(1,{\rm Tom})
that violate the constraint that the first attribute of the relation name
{\rm Emp}
(the id) is a key.
Suppose we have a level of
trust in each of the sources; say we believe that each is 50% reliable. With probability
0.5\cdot 0.5=0.25
we do not trust either tuple and apply the operation that removes both facts. With probability
(1-0.25)/2=0.375
we remove either
{\rm Emp}(1,{\rm Alice})
or
{\rm Emp}(1,{\rm Tom})
.

The preliminary data complexity analysis of operational CQA performed in
(
Calautti
et al. 2018
)
revealed that computing the probability of a candidate answer is
\sharp
P-hard and inapproximable, even for primary keys and conjunctive queries.
However, these negative results should not be seen as the end of the story, but rather as the beginning since operational CQA gives us the flexibility to choose the probabilities assigned to operations.
Indeed, the main question left open by
(
Calautti
et al. 2018
)
is the following: how can we choose the probabilities assigned to operations so that the existence of an FPRAS is guaranteed?

A natural way of choosing those probabilities is to follow the uniform probability distribution over a reasonable space. The obvious candidates for such a space are (i) the set of operational repairs, (ii) the set of sequences of operations that lead to a repair (note that multiple such sequences can lead to the same repair), and (iii) the set of available operations at a certain step of the repairing process. This leads to the so-called
uniform operational CQA
.
The obvious question is how the complexity of exact and approximate operational CQA is affected if we assign probabilities to operations according to the above refined ways. In particular, we would like to understand whether uniform operational CQA allows us to go beyond the relatively simple case of primary keys.

Our goal is to perform a complexity analysis of uniform operational CQA, and provide answers to the above central questions.
Our main findings can be summarized as follows:

(1)

Exact uniform operational CQA remains
\sharp
P-hard, even in the case of primary keys and conjunctive queries.

(2)

Uniform operational CQA admits an FPRAS if we focus on primary keys and conjunctive queries.

(3)

In the case of arbitrary keys and FDs, although assigning probabilities to operations based on uniform repairs and sequences (approaches (i) and (ii) discussed above) does not lead (or it remains open whether it leads) to the approximability of operational CQA, the approach of uniform operations renders the problem approximable. The latter is a significant result since it goes beyond the simple case of primary keys.

2.
Preliminaries

We recall the basics on relational databases, functional dependencies, and conjunctive queries.
In the rest of the paper, we assume the disjoint countably infinite sets
\mathbf{C}
and
\mathbf{V}
of
constants
and
variables
, respectively. For
n>0
, let
[n]
be the set
\{1,\ldots,n\}
.

Relational Databases.

A
(relational) schema

\mathbf{S}
is a finite set of relation names with associated arity; we write
R/n
to denote that
R
has arity
n>0
. Each relation name
R/n
is associated with a tuple of distinct attribute names
(A_{1},\ldots,A_{n})
; we write
\mathsf{att}(R)
for the set
\{A_{1},\ldots,A_{n}\}
of attributes .
A
fact
over
\mathbf{S}
is an expression of the form
R(c_{1},\ldots,c_{n})
, where
R/n\in\mathbf{S}
, and
c_{i}\in\mathbf{C}
for each
i\in[n]
.
A
database

D
over
\mathbf{S}
is a finite set of facts over
\mathbf{S}
.
The
active domain
of
D
, denoted
\mathsf{dom}(D)
, is the set of constants occurring in
D
.
For a fact
f=R(c_{1},\ldots,c_{n})
, with
(A_{1},\ldots,A_{n})
being the tuple of attribute names of
R
, we write
f[A_{i}]
for the constant
c_{i}
.

Functional Dependencies.

A
functional dependency
(FD)
\phi
over a schema
\mathbf{S}
is an expression of the form
R:X\rightarrow Y
, where
R/n\in\mathbf{S}
and
X,Y\subseteq\mathsf{att}(R)
. When
X
or
Y
are singletons, we avoid the curly brackets, and simply write the attribute name.
We call
\phi
a
key
if
X\cup Y=\mathsf{att}(R)
.
Given a set
\Sigma
of keys over
\mathbf{S}
, we say that
\Sigma
is a set of
primary keys
if,
for each
R\in\mathbf{S}
, there exists at most one key in
\Sigma
of the form
R:X\rightarrow Y
.
A database
D
satisfies an FD
\phi=R:X\rightarrow Y
, denoted
D\models\phi
, if, for every two facts
R(\bar{c}_{1}),R(\bar{c}_{2})\in D
the following holds:
R(\bar{c}_{1})[A]=R(\bar{c}_{2})[A]
for every
A\in X
implies
R(\bar{c}_{1})[B]=R(\bar{c}_{2})[B]
for every
B\in Y
.
We say that
D
is
consistent
w.r.t. a set
\Sigma
of FDs, written
D\models\Sigma
, if
D\models\phi
for every
\phi\in\Sigma
; otherwise, we say that
D
is
inconsistent
w.r.t.
\Sigma
.

Conjunctive Queries.

A
(relational) atom

\alpha
over a schema
\mathbf{S}
is an expression of the form
R(t_{1},\ldots,t_{n})
, where
R/n\in\mathbf{S}
, and
t_{i}\in\mathbf{C}\cup\mathbf{V}
for each
i\in[n]
.
A
conjunctive query
(CQ)
Q
over
\mathbf{S}
is an expression of the form
\textrm{Ans}(\bar{x})\ \text{:-}\ R_{1}(\bar{y}_{1}),\ldots,R_{n}(\bar{y}_{n})
, where
R_{i}(\bar{y}_{i})
, for
i\in[n]
, is an atom over
\mathbf{S}
,
\bar{x}
are the
answer variables
of
Q
, and each variable in
\bar{x}
is mentioned in
\bar{y}_{i}
for some
i\in[n]
. We may write
Q(\bar{x})
to indicate that
\bar{x}
are the answer variables of
Q
. When
\bar{x}
is empty,
Q
is called
Boolean
.
The semantics of CQs is given via homomorphisms. Let
\mathsf{var}(Q)
and
\mathsf{const}(Q)
be the set of variables and constants in
Q
, respectively. A
homomorphism
from a CQ
Q
of the form
\textrm{Ans}(\bar{x})\ \text{:-}\ R_{1}(\bar{y}_{1}),\ldots,R_{n}(\bar{y}_{n})
to a database
D
is a function
h:\mathsf{var}(Q)\cup\mathsf{const}(Q)\rightarrow\mathsf{dom}(D)
, which is the identity over
\mathbf{C}
, such that
R_{i}(h(\bar{y}_{i}))\in D
for each
i\in[n]
.
A tuple
\bar{c}\in\mathsf{dom}(D)^{|\bar{x}|}
is an
answer to
Q
over
D
if there is a homomorphism
h
from
Q
to
D
with
h(\bar{x})=\bar{c}
. Let
Q(D)
be the answers to
Q
over
D
. For Boolean CQs, we write
D\models Q
, and say that
D

entails

Q
, if
()\in Q(D)
.

3.
Operational CQA

We proceed to recall the recent operational approach to consistent query answering, introduced in
(
Calautti
et al. 2018
)
. Although this new framework can deal with arbitrary integrity constraints (i.e., tuple-generating dependencies, equality-generating dependencies, and denial constraints), for our purposes we need its simplified version that only deals with functional dependencies.

Operations and Violations.

The notion of operation is the building block of the operational approach. In the original framework, the operations are standard updates
+F
that add a set
F
of facts to the database, or
-F
that remove
F
from the database. However, since in this work we deal with FDs, we only need to remove facts because the addition of a fact would never resolve a conflict.
The formal definition of the notion of operation follows. As usual, we write
\mathcal{P}(S)
for the powerset of a set
S
.

Definition 3.1.

(
Operation
)
For a database
D
over a schema
\mathbf{S}
, a
D
-operation
is a function
\mathit{op}:\mathcal{P}(D)\rightarrow\mathcal{P}(D)
such that, for some non-empty set
F\subseteq D
of facts, for every
D^{\prime}\in\mathcal{P}(D)
,
\mathit{op}(D^{\prime})=D^{\prime}\setminus F
. We write
-F
to refer to this operation.

The operations
-F
depend on the database
D
as they are defined over
D
. Since
D
will be clear from the context, we may refer to them simply as operations, omitting
D
. Also, when
F
contains a single fact
f
, we write
-f
instead of the more formal
-\{f\}
.
The main idea of the operational approach to CQA is to iteratively apply operations, starting from an inconsistent database
D
, until we reach a database
D^{\prime}\subseteq D
that is consistent w.r.t. the given set
\Sigma
of FDs. However, as discussed in
(
Calautti
et al. 2018
)
, we need to ensure that at each step of this repairing process, at least one violation is resolved. To this end, we need to keep track of all the reasons that cause the inconsistency of
D
w.r.t.
\Sigma
. This brings us to the notion of FD violation.

Definition 3.2.

(
FD Violation
)
For a database
D
over a schema
\mathbf{S}
, a
D
-violation
of an FD
\phi=R:X\rightarrow Y
over
\mathbf{S}
is a set
\{f,g\}\subseteq D
of facts such that
\{f,g\}\not\models\phi
.
We denote the set of
D
-violations of
\phi
by
\mathsf{V}(D,\phi)
. Furthermore, for a set
\Sigma
of FDs, we denote by
\mathsf{V}(D,\Sigma)
the set
\{(\phi,v)\mid\phi\in\Sigma\textrm{~~and~~}v\in\mathsf{V}(D,\phi)\}
.

Thus, a pair
(\phi,\{f,g\})\in\mathsf{V}(D,\Sigma)
means that one of the reasons why the database
D
is inconsistent w.r.t.
\Sigma
is because it violates
\phi
due to the facts
f
and
g
.
As discussed in
(
Calautti
et al. 2018
)
, apart from forcing an operation to be fixing, i.e., to fix at least one violation, we also need to force an operation to remove a set of facts only if it contributes as a whole to a violation. Such operations are called justified.

Definition 3.3.

(
Justified Operation
)
Let
D
be a database over a schema
\mathbf{S}
, and
\Sigma
a set of FDs over
\mathbf{S}
. For a database
D^{\prime}\subseteq D
, a

D
-operation
-F
is called
(D^{\prime},\Sigma)
-justified

if there exists
(\phi,\{f,g\})\in\mathsf{V}(D^{\prime},\Sigma)
such that
F\subseteq\{f,g\}
.

Note that justified operations do not try to minimize the number of atoms that need to be removed. As argued in
(
Calautti
et al. 2018
)
, a set of facts that collectively contributes to a violation should be considered as a justified operation during the iterative repairing process since we do not know a priori which atoms should be deleted, and therefore, we need to explore all the possible scenarios.

Repairing Sequences.

As said above, the main idea of the operational approach is to iteratively apply justified operations.
This is formalized via the notion of repairing sequence.
Consider a database
D
and a set
\Sigma
of FDs. Given a sequence
s=(\mathit{op}_{i})_{1\leq i\leq n}
of
D
-operations, we define
D_{0}^{s}=D
and
D_{i}^{s}=\mathit{op}_{i}(D_{i-1}^{s})

for
i\in[n]
. In other words,
D_{i}^{s}
is obtained by applying to
D
the first
i
operations of
s
. The notion of repairing sequence follows:

Definition 3.4.

(
Repairing Sequence
)
Consider a database
D
and a set
\Sigma
of FDs. A sequence of
D
-operations
s=(\mathit{op}_{i})_{1\leq i\leq n}
is called
(D,\Sigma)
-repairing
if, for every
i\in[n]
,
\mathit{op}_{i}
is
(D_{i-1}^{s},\Sigma)
-justified.
Let
\mathsf{RS}(D,\Sigma)
be the set of all
(D,\Sigma)
-repairing sequences.

It is easy to verify that
the length of a
(D,\Sigma)
-repairing sequence is linear in the size of
D
. It is also clear that the set
\mathsf{RS}(D,\Sigma)
is finite.
For a
(D,\Sigma)
-repairing sequence
s=(\mathit{op}_{i})_{1\leq i\leq n}
, we define its
result
as the database
s(D)=D_{n}^{s}
, and call it
complete
if
s(D)\models\Sigma
, i.e., it leads to a consistent database. Let
\mathsf{CRS}(D,\Sigma)
be the set of all complete
(D,\Sigma)
-repairing sequences.

Operational Repairs.

A
candidate (operational) repair
of a database
D
w.r.t. a set
\Sigma
of FDs is a database
D^{\prime}
such that
D^{\prime}=s(D)
for some
s\in\mathsf{CRS}(D,\Sigma)
. Let
\mathsf{CORep}(D,\Sigma)
be the set of all candidate repairs of
D
w.r.t.
\Sigma
.
Although every database of
\mathsf{CORep}(D,\Sigma)
corresponds to a conceptually meaningful way of repairing the database
D
, we would like to have a mechanism that allows us to choose which candidate repairs should be considered for query answering purposes, and assign likelihoods to those repairs.

The fact that we can operationally repair an inconsistent database via repairing sequences gives us the flexibility of choosing which operations (that is, fact deletions) are more likely than others, which in turn allows us to talk about the probability of a repair, and thus, the probability with which an answer is entailed.
The idea of assigning likelihoods to operations extending sequences can be described as follows: for all possible extensions
s\cdot\mathit{op}_{1},\ldots,s\cdot\mathit{op}_{k}
of a repairing sequence
s
, we assign
probabilities
p_{1},\ldots,p_{k}
to them so they add up to
1
. This is done by exploiting a
tree-shaped Markov chain
that arranges its states (i.e., repairing sequences) in a rooted tree, where (i) the empty sequence of operations, which is by definition repairing, is the root, (ii) the children of each state are its possible extensions, and (iii) the set of states corresponding to complete sequences coincide with the set of leaves.
We write
\varepsilon
for the empty sequence of operations.
We further write
\mathsf{Ops}_{s}(D,\Sigma)
for the set of
(D,\Sigma)
-repairing sequences
\{s^{\prime}\in\mathsf{RS}(D,\Sigma)\mid s^{\prime}=s\cdot\mathit{op}\text{ for some }D\text{-operation }\mathit{op}\}
.

Definition 3.5.

(
Repairing Markov Chain
)
For a database
D
and a set
\Sigma
of FDs, a
(D,\Sigma)
-repairing Markov chain
is an edge-labeled rooted tree
T=(V,E,\mathbf{P})
, where
V=\mathsf{RS}(D,\Sigma)
,
E\subseteq V\times V
, and
\mathbf{P}:E\rightarrow[0,1]
, such that:

(1)

the root is the empty sequence
\varepsilon
,

(2)

for a non-leaf node
s\in V
,
\{s^{\prime}\mid(s,s^{\prime})\in E\}=\mathsf{Ops}_{s}(D,\Sigma)
,

(3)

for a non-leaf node
s\in V
,
\sum_{t\in\{s^{\prime}\mid(s,s^{\prime})\in E\}}\mathbf{P}(s,t)=1
, and

(4)

\{s\in V\mid s\text{ is a leaf}\}=\mathsf{CRS}(D,\Sigma)
.

A
repairing Markov chain generator
w.r.t.
\Sigma
is a function
M_{\Sigma}
assigning to every database
D
a
(D,\Sigma)
-repairing Markov chain.

Example 3.6.

Consider the database
D=\{f_{1},f_{2},f_{3}\}
over the schema
\mathbf{S}=\{R/3\}
, where
f_{1}=R(a_{1},b_{1},c_{1})
,
f_{2}=R(a_{1},b_{2},c_{2})
and
f_{3}=R(a_{2},b_{1},c_{2})
. Consider also the set
\Sigma=\{\phi_{1},\phi_{2}\}
of FDs over
\mathbf{S}
, where
\phi_{1}=R:A\rightarrow B
and
\phi_{2}=R:C\rightarrow B
, assuming that
(A,B,C)
is the tuple of attributes of
R
.
It is easy to see that
D\not\models\Sigma
. In particular, we have that
\mathsf{V}(D,\Sigma)=\{(\phi_{1},\{f_{1},f_{2}\}),(\phi_{2},\{f_{2},f_{3}\})\}
.
It is easy to verify that for the edge-labeled rooted tree
T=(V,E,\mathbf{P})
in Figure
1
,
V=\mathsf{RS}(D,\Sigma)
, for a non-leaf node
s
the set of its children is
\mathsf{Ops}_{s}(D,\Sigma)
, and the set of leaves coincides with
\mathsf{CRS}(D,\Sigma)
. Hence, providing that
p_{1}+p_{2}+p_{3}+p_{4}+p_{5}=1
,
p_{6}+p_{7}+p_{8}=1
and
p_{9}+p_{10}+p_{11}=1
,

is a

The purpose of a repairing Markov chain generator is to provide a mechanism for defining a family of repairing Markov chains independently of the database. One can design a repairing Markov chain generator
M_{\Sigma}
once, and whenever the database
D
changes, the desired
(D,\Sigma)
-repairing Markov chain is simply
M_{\Sigma}(D)
.

We now recall the notion of operational repair: they are candidate operational repairs obtained via repairing sequences that are
reachable
leaves of a repairing Markov chain, i.e., leaves with non-zero probability. The probability of a leaf is coming from the so-called leaf distribution of a repairing Markov chain. Formally, given a database
D
and a set
\Sigma
of FDs, the
leaf distribution
of a
(D,\Sigma)
-repairing Markov chain
T=(V,E,\mathbf{P})
is a function
\pi
that assigns to each leaf
s
of
T
a number from
[0,1]
as follows: assuming that
(s_{0},s_{1})
,
(s_{1},s_{2})
,
\ldots
,
(s_{n-1},s_{n})
, where
n\geq 0
,
\varepsilon=s_{0}
and
s=s_{n}
, is the unique path in
T
from
\varepsilon
to
s
,
\pi(s)=\mathbf{P}(s_{0},s_{1})\cdot\mathbf{P}(s_{1},s_{2})\cdot\cdots\cdot\mathbf{P}(s_{n-1},s_{n})
.
The set of
reachable leaves
of
T
, denoted
\mathsf{RL}(T)
, is the set of leaves of
T
that have non-zero probability according to the leaf distribution of
T
.

Definition 3.7.

(
Operational Repair
)
Given a database
D
, a set
\Sigma
of FDs, and a repairing Markov chain generator
M_{\Sigma}
w.r.t.
\Sigma
, an
(operational) repair
of
D
w.r.t.
M_{\Sigma}
is a database
D^{\prime}\in\mathsf{CORep}(D,\Sigma)
such that
D^{\prime}=s(D)
for some
s\in\mathsf{RL}(M_{\Sigma}(D))
. Let
\mathsf{ORep}(D,M_{\Sigma})
be the set of all operational repairs of
D
w.r.t.
M_{\Sigma}
.

An operational repair may be obtainable via multiple repairing
sequences that are reachable leaves of the underlying
repairing Markov chain. The probability of a repair
D^{\prime}
is calculated by summing up the probabilities of all reachable leaves
s
so that
D^{\prime}=s(D)
.

Definition 3.8.

(
Operational Semantics
)
Given a database
D
, a set
\Sigma
of FDs, and a repairing Markov chain generator
M_{\Sigma}
w.r.t.
\Sigma
, the probability of an operational repair
D^{\prime}
of
D
w.r.t.
M_{\Sigma}
is

\mathsf{P}_{D,M_{\Sigma}}(D^{\prime})\ =\ \sum\limits_{s\in\mathsf{RL}(M_{\Sigma}(D))\text{ and }D^{\prime}=s(D)}\pi(s),

where
\pi
is the leaf distribution of
M_{\Sigma}(D)
.
The
operational semantics
of
D
w.r.t.
M_{\Sigma}
is defined as the set of repair-probability pairs
{[\![D]\!]}_{M_{\Sigma}}=\left\{\left(D^{\prime},\mathsf{P}_{D,M_{\Sigma}}(D^{\prime})\right)\mid D^{\prime}\in\mathsf{ORep}(D,M_{\Sigma})\right\}
.

Operational CQA.

We now have in place all the necessary notions to recall the operational approach to consistent query answering, and define the main problem of interest.
For a database
D
, a set
\Sigma
of FDs, a Markov chain generator

M_{\Sigma}
w.r.t.
\Sigma
, a query
Q(\bar{x})
, and a tuple

Until now, a repairing Markov chain generator is a general function.
We proceed to discuss the novel idea of uniform operational CQA, which provides concrete ways of defining such a function.

4.
Uniform Operational CQA

A natural way of defining a repairing Markov chain is to assign probabilities to operations according to the uniform probability distribution over a reasonable space. The obvious options for such a space are (i) the set of candidate operational repairs, (ii) the set of complete repairing sequences, and (iii) the set of available operations at a certain step of the repairing process.
More precisely, given a set
\Sigma
of FDs, it is natural to consider the repairing Markov chain generators
M_{\Sigma}^{\mathsf{ur}}
(uniform repairs),
M_{\Sigma}^{\mathsf{us}}
(uniform sequences), and
M_{\Sigma}^{\mathsf{uo}}
(uniform operations) w.r.t.
\Sigma
such that, for a database
D
:

(i)

\mathsf{ORep}(D,M_{\Sigma}^{\mathsf{ur}})=\mathsf{CORep}(D,\Sigma)
, and for
D^{\prime}\in\mathsf{ORep}(D,M_{\Sigma}^{\mathsf{ur}})
,
\mathsf{P}_{D,M_{\Sigma}^{\mathsf{ur}}}(D^{\prime})=\frac{1}{|\mathsf{ORep}(D,M_{\Sigma}^{\mathsf{ur}})|}
.

(ii)

For every
s\in\mathsf{CRS}(D,\Sigma)
, assuming that
\pi
is the leaf distribution of
M_{\Sigma}^{\mathsf{us}}(D)
,
\pi(s)=\frac{1}{|\mathsf{CRS}(D,\Sigma)|}
.

(iii)

For every
s,s^{\prime}\in\mathsf{RS}(D,\Sigma)
, assuming that
M_{\Sigma}^{\mathsf{uo}}(D)=(V,E,\mathbf{P})
,
s^{\prime}\in\mathsf{Ops}_{s}(D,\Sigma)
implies
\mathbf{P}(s,s^{\prime})=\frac{1}{|\mathsf{Ops}_{s}(D,\Sigma)|}
.

We now explain, by means of an example, how these Markov chain generators are defined; the formal definitions are in Appendix

Our Main Objective.

The data complexity of
\mathsf{OCQA}
for

(

, showing that it is, in general, intractable. In particular:

(

There exist a set
\Sigma

\Sigma

\sharp

With the above intractability result in place, the authors of
(

)
asked whether

is approximable, i.e., whether the target probability can be approximated via a
fully polynomial-time randomized approximation scheme
(FPRAS, for short).
Formally, an FPRAS for
\mathsf{OCQA}(\Sigma,M_{\Sigma},Q(\bar{x}))
is a randomized algorithm
\mathsf{A}
that takes as input a database
D
, a tuple
\bar{c}\in\mathsf{dom}(D)^{|\bar{x}|}
,
\epsilon>0
, and
0<\delta<1

||D||
,
||\bar{c}||
,
1

o

1/\epsilon

\log(1/\delta)
, and produces a random variable
\mathsf{A}(D,\bar{c},\epsilon,\delta)
such that

\text{\rm Pr}\left(|\mathsf{A}(D,\bar{c},\epsilon,\delta)-\mathsf{P}_{M_{\Sigma},Q}(D,\bar{c})|\ \leq\ \epsilon\cdot\mathsf{P}_{M_{\Sigma},Q}(D,\bar{c})\right)\ \geq\ 1-\delta.

It was shown that
the problem in question
does not admit an FPRAS, under the widely accepted complexity assumption that
{\rm RP}\neq{\rm NP}
. Recall that RP is the complexity class of problems that are efficiently solvable via a randomized algorithm with a bounded one-sided error (i.e., the answer may mistakenly be “no”)
(

.

Theorem 4.2 (
(
Calautti
et al. 2018
)
).

Unless
{\rm RP}={\rm NP}
, there exist a set
\Sigma
of primary keys, a Markov chain generator
M_{\Sigma}
w.r.t.
\Sigma
, and a CQ

such that there is no FPRAS for
\mathsf{OCQA}(\Sigma,M_{\Sigma},Q)
.

Having the natural Markov chain generators discussed above in place, the question is how the complexity of exact and approximate operational CQA is affected, i.e., how Theorems
4.1
and
4.2
are affected if we consider these more refined Markov chain generators instead of an arbitrary one. The goal of this work is to perform such a complexity analysis.
Our main findings are as follows:

(1)

The complexity of exact operational CQA remains
\sharp
P-hard, even in the case of primary keys.

(2)

Operational CQA is approximable, i.e., it admits an FPRAS, if we focus on primary keys.

(3)

In the case of arbitrary keys and FDs, although the Markov chain generators based on uniform repairs and sequences do not lead (or it remains open whether they lead) to the approximability of operational CQA, the Markov chain generator based on uniform operations renders the problem approximable.

The latter should be attributed to the “local” nature of the Markov chain generator based on uniform operations.

The rest of the paper is devoted to discussing the high-level ideas underlying the above results; the formal proofs are in the appendix.

5.
Uniform Repairs

We start our complexity analysis by considering the Markov chain generator based on uniform repairs, and show the following result:

Theorem 5.1.

(1)

There exist a set
\Sigma
of primary keys, and a CQ
Q
such that
\mathsf{OCQA}(\Sigma,M_{\Sigma}^{\mathsf{ur}},Q)
is
\sharp
P
-hard.

(2)

For a set
\Sigma
of primary keys, and a CQ
Q
,
\mathsf{OCQA}(\Sigma,M_{\Sigma}^{\mathsf{ur}},Q)
admits an FPRAS.

(3)

Unless
{\rm RP}={\rm NP}
, there exist a set
\Sigma
of FDs, and a CQ
Q
such that there is no FPRAS for
\mathsf{OCQA}(\Sigma,M_{\Sigma}^{\mathsf{ur}},Q)
.

Notice that the above result does not cover the case of arbitrary keys, which remains an open problem. We can extract, however, from the proof of item (3) that for keys, unless
{\rm RP}={\rm NP}
, the problem of
counting
the number of operational repairs does not admit an FPRAS. We see this as an indication that item (3) holds even in the case of keys.
We now discuss how Theorem
5.1
is shown.

We start with the simple observation that, for a database
D
, a set
\Sigma
of FDs, a CQ
Q(\bar{x})
, and a tuple
\bar{c}\in\mathsf{dom}(D)^{|\bar{x}|}
,

\displaystyle\mathsf{P}_{M_{\Sigma}^{\mathsf{ur}},Q}(D,\bar{c})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{|\{D^{\prime}\in\mathsf{CORep}(D,\Sigma)\mid\bar{c}\in Q(D^{\prime})\}|}{|\mathsf{CORep}(D,\Sigma)|}.

This ratio is the percentage of candidate operational repairs of
D
w.r.t.
\Sigma
that entail
Q(\bar{c})
, which we call the
repair relative frequency
of
Q(\bar{c})
w.r.t.
D
and
\Sigma
, and denote
\mathsf{rrfreq}_{\Sigma,Q}(D,\bar{c})
.
Therefore, we can conveniently restate the problem
\mathsf{OCQA}(\Sigma,M_{\Sigma}^{\mathsf{ur}},Q)
as the problem of computing the repair relative frequency of
Q(\bar{c})

D

\Sigma
, which does not depend on the Markov chain generator

PROBLEM
:

\mathsf{RRFreq}(\Sigma,Q(\bar{x}))

INPUT
:

A database
D
, and a tuple
\bar{c}\in\mathsf{dom}(D)^{|\bar{x}|}
.

OUTPUT
:

\mathsf{rrfreq}_{\Sigma,Q}(D,\bar{c})
.

We proceed to discuss how we establish Theorem
5.1
by directly considering the problem
\mathsf{RRFreq}(\Sigma,Q)
instead of
\mathsf{OCQA}(\Sigma,M_{\Sigma}^{\mathsf{ur}},Q)
;

Item (1).

We show that
\mathsf{RRFreq}(\Sigma,Q)
is
\sharp

\Sigma
consisting of a single key of the form
R:A\rightarrow B
, where
R
is a binary relation name with
(A,B)
being its tuple of attributes, and a Boolean CQ
Q
. This is done via a polynomial-time Turing reduction from a graph-theoretic problem called
\sharp H\text{-}\mathsf{Coloring}
, where
H
is an undirected graph, to
\mathsf{RRFreq}(\Sigma,Q)
. The problem
\sharp H\text{-}\mathsf{Coloring}
takes as input an undirected graph
G
, and asks for the number of homomorphisms from
G
to
H
. The key of the proof is to carefully choose
H
so that (i)
\sharp H\text{-}\mathsf{Coloring}
is
\sharp
P-hard, and (ii) it allows us to devise the desired polynomial-time Turing reduction, i.e., for an undirected graph
G
, we can construct in polynomial time in
||G||

D_{G}
such that the number of homomorphisms from
G
to
H
can be computed in polynomial time in
||G||
, assuming that we have access to an oracle for the problem
\mathsf{RRFreq}(\Sigma,Q)

\mathsf{rrfreq}_{\Sigma,Q}(D_{G},())

For choosing

, we exploit an interesting dichotomy from
(

\sharp H\text{-}\mathsf{Coloring}
is solvable in polynomial time or is
\sharp

.

Item (2).

For showing that, for a set
\Sigma
of primary keys and a CQ
Q
,
\mathsf{RRFreq}(\Sigma,Q)
admits an FPRAS, we rely on Monte Carlo sampling.
We first show the existence of an efficient sampler:

Lemma 5.2.

Given a database
D
, and a set
\Sigma
of primary keys, we can sample elements of
\mathsf{CORep}(D,\Sigma)
uniformly at random in polynomial time in
||D||
.

The above lemma tells us that there exists a randomized algorithm
\mathsf{SampleRep}
that takes as input
D
and
\Sigma
, runs in polynomial time in
||D||
, and produces a random variable
\mathsf{SampleRep}(D,\Sigma)
such that
\mathsf{Pr}(\mathsf{SampleRep}(D,\Sigma)=D^{\prime})=\frac{1}{|\mathsf{CORep}(D,\Sigma)|}
for every database
D^{\prime}\in\mathsf{CORep}(D,\Sigma)
.
Notice, however, that the efficient sampler provided by Lemma
5.2
does not immediately imply the existence of an FPRAS for
\mathsf{RRFreq}(\Sigma,Q)
since the number of samples should be proportional to
\frac{1}{\mathsf{rrfreq}_{\Sigma,Q}(D,\bar{c})}

(
Dagum
et al. 2000
)
. Hence, to obtain an FPRAS using Monte Carlo sampling, we need show that the repair relative frequency is never “too small”.

Lemma 5.3.

Consider a set
\Sigma
of primary keys, and a CQ
Q(\bar{x})
. For every database
D

\bar{c}\in\mathsf{dom}(D)^{|\bar{x}|}

\mathsf{rrfreq}_{\Sigma,Q}(D,\bar{c})\ \geq\ \frac{1}{(2\cdot||D||)^{||Q||}}

\mathsf{rrfreq}_{\Sigma,Q}(D,\bar{c})>0

Given a set
\Sigma
of primary keys and a CQ
Q
, by exploiting Lemmas
5.2

5.3

\mathsf{RRFreq}(\Sigma,Q)
.

Item (3).

For showing that there exist a set
\Sigma
of FDs and a CQ

\mathsf{RRFreq}(\Sigma,Q)

An undirected graph
G

\mathsf{IS}(G)

Recall that the

A database

Consider a non-trivially
\Sigma

Unless
{\rm RP}={\rm NP}

\Sigma

The above result exploits the fact that, unless

. We show the following auxiliary result:

Lemma 5.6.

Assume that
\mathsf{RRFreq}(\Sigma,Q)
admits an FPRAS, for every set
\Sigma
of FDs and CQ
Q
. Given a non-trivially
\Sigma_{K}
-connected database
D
, the problem of computing
|\mathsf{CORep}(D,\Sigma_{K})|
admits an FPRAS.

To establish the above result, we show that there exists a set
\Sigma_{F}
of FDs such that, for every non-trivially
\Sigma_{K}
-connected database
D
, we can construct in polynomial time in
||D||
a database
D_{F}
such that

\mathsf{CG}(D_{F},\Sigma_{F})
consists of a graph
G
that is isomorphic to
\mathsf{CG}(D,\Sigma_{K})
, and an additional node that is connected via an edge with every node of
G
. Therefore, by Lemma
5.4
, we get that

|\mathsf{CORep}(D_{F},\Sigma_{F})|\ =\ |\mathsf{CORep}(D,\Sigma_{K})|+1.

Let us clarify that this is the place where we need the power of FDs; it is unclear how we can devise a set of keys that has the same properties as
\Sigma_{F}
.
We then construct an atomic Boolean CQ
Q_{F}
with

\mathsf{rrfreq}_{\Sigma_{F},Q_{F}}(D_{F},())\ =\ \frac{1}{|\mathsf{CORep}(D_{F},\Sigma_{F})|}\ =\ \frac{1}{|\mathsf{CORep}(D,\Sigma_{K})|+1};

we use
()
to denote the empty tuple. Now, by exploiting the above equality, the fact that
D_{F}
can be constructed in polynomial time, and the FPRAS for
\mathsf{RRFreq}(\Sigma_{F},Q_{F})
(which exists by hypothesis), we can devise an FPRAS for the problem of computing
|\mathsf{CORep}(D,\Sigma_{K})|
given a non-trivially
\Sigma_{K}
-connected database
D
, as claimed.

It is now straightforward to see that from Proposition
5.5
and Lemma
5.6
, we get that, unless
{\rm RP}={\rm NP}
, there exist a set
\Sigma
of FDs and a CQ
Q
such that there is no FPRAS for
\mathsf{RRFreq}(\Sigma,Q)
.

6.
Uniform Sequences

We now concentrate on the Markov chain generator based on uniform sequences, and establish the following complexity result.

Theorem 6.1.

(1)

There exist a set
\Sigma
of primary keys, and a CQ
Q
such that
\mathsf{OCQA}(\Sigma,M_{\Sigma}^{\mathsf{us}},Q)
is
\sharp
P
-hard.

(2)

For a set
\Sigma
of primary keys, and a CQ
Q
,
\mathsf{OCQA}(\Sigma,M_{\Sigma}^{\mathsf{us}},Q)
admits an FPRAS.

Notice that the above result does not cover the cases of arbitrary keys and FDs. Unfortunately, despite our efforts, we have not managed to prove or disprove the existence of an FPRAS for the problem in question. We conjecture that there is no FPRAS even for keys, i.e., unless
{\rm RP}={\rm NP}
, there exist a set
\Sigma
of keys, and a CQ
Q
such that there is no FPRAS for
\mathsf{OCQA}(\Sigma,M_{\Sigma}^{\mathsf{us}},Q)
.
We proceed to discuss how Theorem
6.1
is shown.

As for Theorem
5.1
, we can conveniently restate the problem in question as a problem of computing a “relative frequency” ratio that does not depend on the Markov chain generator.
In particular, for a database
D
, a set
\Sigma
of FDs, a CQ
Q(\bar{x})
, and a tuple
\bar{c}\in\mathsf{dom}(D)^{|\bar{x}|}
,

\displaystyle\mathsf{P}_{M_{\Sigma}^{\mathsf{us}},Q}(D,\bar{c})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{|\{s\in\mathsf{CRS}(D,\Sigma)\mid\bar{c}\in Q(s(D))\}|}{|\mathsf{CRS}(D,\Sigma)|}.

This ratio is the percentage of complete
(D,\Sigma)
-repairing sequences that lead to an operational repair that entails
Q(\bar{c})
, which we call the
sequence relative frequency
of
Q(\bar{c})
w.r.t.
D
and
\Sigma
, and denote
\mathsf{srfreq}_{\Sigma,Q}(D,\bar{c})
.
Thus, we can restate
\mathsf{OCQA}(\Sigma,M_{\Sigma}^{\mathsf{us}},Q)
as the problem of computing the sequence relative frequency of

w.r.t.

and
\Sigma
, which is independent from the Markov chain generator

:

PROBLEM
:

\mathsf{SRFreq}(\Sigma,Q(\bar{x}))

INPUT
:

A database
D
, and a tuple
\bar{c}\in\mathsf{dom}(D)^{|\bar{x}|}
.

OUTPUT
:

\mathsf{srfreq}_{\Sigma,Q}(D,\bar{c})
.

We now discuss how we establish Theorem
6.1
by directly considering the problem
\mathsf{SRFreq}(\Sigma,Q)
instead of
\mathsf{OCQA}(\Sigma,M_{\Sigma}^{\mathsf{us}},Q)
; further details can be found in Appendix
C
.

Item (1).

Let
\Sigma
and
Q
be the singleton set of primary keys and the Boolean CQ, respectively, for which
\mathsf{RRFreq}(\Sigma,Q)
is
\sharp
P-hard;
\Sigma
and

are obtained from the proof of Theorem
5.1
(1).
We show that also
\mathsf{SRFreq}(\Sigma,Q)
is
\sharp
P-hard via a polynomial-time Turing reduction from
\sharp H\text{-}\mathsf{Coloring}
. Actually, we can exploit the same construction as in the proof of item (1) of Theorem

.

Item (2).

For showing that, for a set
\Sigma
of primary keys and a CQ

,
\mathsf{SRFreq}(\Sigma,Q)
admits an FPRAS, we rely again on Monte Carlo sampling.
We first show that an efficient sampler exists. This relies on a non-trivial technical lemma, which states that, for a database
D
,
|\mathsf{CRS}(D,\Sigma)|
can be computed in polynomial time in
||D||
.

Lemma 6.2.

For a database
D
, and a set
\Sigma
of primary keys, we can sample elements of
\mathsf{CRS}(D,\Sigma)
uniformly at random in polynomial time in
||D||
.

To establish that the problem in question admits an FPRAS based on Monte Carlo sampling, it remains to show the following:

Lemma 6.3.

Consider a set
\Sigma
of primary keys, and a CQ
Q(\bar{x})
. For every database
D
, and tuple
\bar{c}\in\mathsf{dom}(D)^{|\bar{x}|}

\mathsf{srfreq}_{\Sigma,Q}(D,\bar{c})\ \geq\ \frac{1}{(2\cdot||D||)^{||Q||}}

whenever
\mathsf{srfreq}_{\Sigma,Q}(D,\bar{c})>0
.

Given a set
\Sigma
of primary keys and a CQ
Q
, by exploiting Lemmas

and

, we can easily devise an FPRAS for

We finally consider the Markov chain generator based on uniform operations, and establish the following complexity result.

Theorem 7.1.

(1)

There exist a set
\Sigma
of primary keys, and a CQ
Q
such that
\mathsf{OCQA}(\Sigma,M_{\Sigma}^{\mathsf{uo}},Q)
is
\sharp
P
-hard.

(2)

For a set
\Sigma
of keys, and a CQ
Q
,
\mathsf{OCQA}(\Sigma,M_{\Sigma}^{\mathsf{uo}},Q)
admits an FPRAS.

Notice that the above result does not cover the case of FDs, which remains an open problem. However, as we explain below, for FDs we can establish an approximability result under the assumption that only operations that remove a single fact (not a pair of facts) are considered. But let us first discuss the proof of Theorem
7.1
.

Unlike Theorems
5.1
and
6.1
presented above, there is no obvious way to conveniently restate the problem of interest as a problem of computing a “relative frequency” ratio.
Thus, the proof of Theorem
7.1
, which we discuss next, has to deal with
\mathsf{OCQA}(\Sigma,M_{\Sigma}^{\mathsf{uo}},Q)
for a set
\Sigma
of FDs and a CQ
Q
; details are in Appendix
D
.

Item (1).

As we did for item (1) of Theorem
6.1
, we reuse the construction underlying the proof of item (1) of Theorem
5.1
.

Item (2).

We show that
\mathsf{OCQA}(\Sigma,M_{\Sigma}^{\mathsf{uo}},Q)
, where
\Sigma
is a set of keys and
Q
a CQ, admits an FPRAS by relying once again on Monte Carlo sampling. The existence of an efficient sampler follows easily from the definition of the Markov chain generator
M_{\Sigma}^{\mathsf{uo}}
. In particular:

Lemma 7.2.

Given a database
D
, and a set
\Sigma
of keys, we can sample elements of
\mathsf{RL}(M_{\Sigma}^{\mathsf{uo}}(D))
according to the leaf distribution of
M_{\Sigma}^{\mathsf{uo}}(D)
in polynomial time in
||D||
.

The interesting task towards an FPRAS for the problem in question is to show that the target probability is never “too small”.

Proposition 7.3.

Consider a set
\Sigma
of keys, and a CQ
Q(\bar{x})
. There is a polynomial
\mathsf{pol}
such that, for every database
D
, and
\bar{c}\in\mathsf{dom}(D)^{|\bar{x}|}
,

\mathsf{P}_{M_{\Sigma}^{\mathsf{uo}},Q}(D,\bar{c})\ \geq\ \frac{1}{\mathsf{pol}(||D||)}

whenever
\mathsf{P}_{M_{\Sigma}^{\mathsf{uo}},Q}(D,\bar{c})>0
.

We proceed to discuss the main ideas underlying the proof of the above result. For the sake of clarity, we focus on atomic queries, i.e., CQs with only one atom. The generalization to arbitrary CQs can be found in the appendix.
In the sequel, let
\Sigma
be a set of keys,
Q(\bar{x})
an atomic query,
D
a database, and
\bar{c}
a tuple of
\mathsf{dom}(D)^{|\bar{x}|}
.

Clearly, if there is no homomorphism
h
from
Q
to
D
with
h(\bar{x})=\bar{c}
, then
\mathsf{P}_{M_{\Sigma}^{\mathsf{uo}},Q}(D,\bar{c})=0
. Assume now that such a homomorphism
h
exists, and let
f
be the fact of
D
obtained after applying
h
to the single atom of
Q
. It is not difficult to see that

\mathsf{P}_{M_{\Sigma}^{\mathsf{uo}},Q}(D,\bar{c})\ \geq\ \underbrace{\sum\limits_{D^{\prime}\in\mathsf{ORep}(D,M_{\Sigma}^{\mathsf{uo}})\text{ and }f\in D^{\prime}}\mathsf{P}_{D,M_{\Sigma}^{\mathsf{uo}}}(D^{\prime})}_{\Lambda}.

Thus, it suffices to show that there exists a polynomial
\mathsf{pol}
such that
\Lambda\geq\frac{1}{\mathsf{pol}(||D||)}
.
Let
S_{f}
and
S_{\neg f}
be the sets of sequences of
\mathsf{RL}(M_{\Sigma}^{\mathsf{uo}}(D))
that keep
f
and remove
f
, respectively, i.e.,

\displaystyle S_{f}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\{s\in\mathsf{RL}(M_{\Sigma}^{\mathsf{uo}}(D))\mid f\in s(D)\}

\displaystyle S_{\neg f}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\{s\in\mathsf{RL}(M_{\Sigma}^{\mathsf{uo}}(D))\mid f\not\in s(D)\}.

With
\pi
being the leaf distribution of
M_{\Sigma}^{\mathsf{uo}}(D)
,
\Lambda=\frac{\Lambda_{f}}{\Lambda_{f}+\Lambda_{\neg f}}
, where

\Lambda_{f}\ =\ \sum_{s\in S_{f}}\pi(s)\qquad\text{and}\qquad\Lambda_{\neg f}\ =\ \sum_{s\in S_{\neg f}}\pi(s).

Therefore, to establish the desired lower bound
\frac{1}{\mathsf{pol}(||D||)}
for
\Lambda
, it suffices to show that there exists a polynomial
\mathsf{pol}^{\prime}
such that
\Lambda_{\neg f}\leq\mathsf{pol}^{\prime}(||D||)\cdot\Lambda_{f}
. Indeed, in this case we can conclude that

\Lambda\ =\ \frac{\Lambda_{f}}{\Lambda_{f}+\Lambda_{\neg f}}\ \geq\ \frac{\Lambda_{f}}{\Lambda_{f}+\mathsf{pol}^{\prime}(||D||)\cdot\Lambda_{f}}\ =\ \frac{1}{1+\mathsf{pol}^{\prime}(||D||)},

and the claim follows with
\mathsf{pol}(||D||)=1+\mathsf{pol}^{\prime}(||D||)
. The rest of the proof is devoted to showing that a polynomial
\mathsf{pol}^{\prime}
such that
\Lambda_{\neg f}\leq\mathsf{pol}^{\prime}(||D||)\cdot\Lambda_{f}
exists.

To get this inequality, we establish a rather involved technical lemma that relates the sequences of
S_{\neg f}
with the sequences of
S_{f}
; as usual, we write
\pi
for the leaf distribution of
M_{\Sigma}^{\mathsf{uo}}(D)
:

Lemma 7.4.

There exists a function
\mathsf{F}:S_{\neg f}\rightarrow S_{f}
such that:

(1)

There exists a polynomial
\mathsf{pol}^{\prime\prime}
such that, for every
s\in S_{\neg f}
,
\pi(s)\leq\mathsf{pol}^{\prime\prime}(||D||)\cdot\pi(\mathsf{F}(s))
.

(2)

For every
s^{\prime}\in S_{f}
,
|\{s\in S_{\neg f}\mid\mathsf{F}(s)=s^{\prime}\}|\leq 2\cdot||D||-1
.

For showing item (1) of Lemma
7.4
, we transform each sequence
s\in S_{\neg f}
into a sequence
s^{\prime}\in S_{f}
, and let
\mathsf{F}(s)=s^{\prime}
. This is done by first deleting or replacing the operation
\mathit{op}
in
s
that removes
f
. In particular, if
\mathit{op}=-f
, then we simply delete it; otherwise, if
\mathit{op}=-\{f,g\}
, then we replace it with the operation
-g
. Notice, however, that there is no guarantee that the sequence
\hat{s}
, obtained after removing
\mathit{op}
from
s
, is a complete sequence of
\mathsf{CRS}(D,\Sigma)
. This is because
s(D)
might contain facts that are in a conflict with
f
, and thus, by keeping
f
, there is no guarantee that
\hat{s}(D)\models\Sigma
. We then convert
\hat{s}
into a complete sequence
s^{\prime}
by simply adding at the end of
\hat{s}
additional operations (in some arbitrary order) that resolve all the conflicts.
Now, to show that
\pi(s)\leq\mathsf{pol}^{\prime\prime}(||D||)\cdot\pi(s^{\prime})
, for some polynomial
\mathsf{pol}^{\prime\prime}
, we rely on the following two crucial facts:
(1) Although the probabilities of the operations in
s^{\prime}
coming after the operation in
s
that removes
f
might decrease, we can show that they do not decrease “too much”.
(2) The number of operations that we need to add at the end of
\hat{s}
in order to get
s^{\prime}
depends only on
\Sigma
(not on
||D||
). More precisely, by exploiting the fact that
\Sigma
consists of keys, we can show that
f
can be in a conflict with
at most

k\geq 0
facts of
s(D)
, where
k
is the number of keys in
\Sigma
over the relation name of
f
. This implies that we do not need to add more than
k
operations at the end of
\hat{s}
.
Note that the above facts do
not
hold for FDs.
To establish that
\pi(s)\leq\mathsf{pol}^{\prime\prime}(||D||)\cdot\pi(s^{\prime})
using the above facts, we rely on the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality for
n
-dimensional Euclidean spaces.
Finally, once we have
\mathsf{F}
in place, it is then not difficult to show item (2) via a combinatorial argument.

It is now easy to establish the existence of the polynomial
\mathsf{pol}^{\prime}
such that
\Lambda_{\neg f}\leq\mathsf{pol}^{\prime}(||D||)\cdot\Lambda_{f}
. Indeed, with
\mathsf{F}
and
\mathsf{pol}^{\prime\prime}
being the function and the polynomial, respectively, provided by Lemma
7.4
,

\displaystyle\Lambda_{\neg f}\ =\ \sum_{s\in S_{\neg f}}\pi(s)\

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{s\in S_{\neg f}}\mathsf{pol}^{\prime\prime}(||D||)\cdot\pi(\mathsf{F}(s))

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\mathsf{pol}^{\prime\prime}(||D||)\cdot(2\cdot||D||-1)\cdot\sum_{s\in S_{f}}\pi(s)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathsf{pol}^{\prime\prime}(||D||)\cdot(2\cdot||D||-1)\cdot\Lambda_{f},

and the claim follows with
\mathsf{pol}^{\prime}(||D||)=\mathsf{pol}^{\prime\prime}(||D||)\cdot(2\cdot||D||-1)
.

An FPRAS for FDs.

Recall that Theorem
7.1
does not cover the case of FDs, which remains an open problem. At this point, one may wonder whether Monte Carlo sampling can be used for devising an FPRAS in the case of FDs. Indeed, the efficient sampler provided by Lemma
7.2
holds even for FDs since the proof of that lemma does not exploit keys in any way, but only the “local” nature of the Markov chain generator. However, we do not have a result analogous to Proposition
7.3
, which states that the target probability is never “too small”.
In fact, there exist a set
\Sigma
of FDs, a Boolean atomic query
Q
, and a family of databases
\{D_{n}\}_{n>0}
with
|D_{n}|=n
, such that
0<\mathsf{P}_{M_{\Sigma}^{\mathsf{uo}},Q}(D_{n},())\leq\frac{1}{2^{n-1}}
; the proof is in the appendix.
Hence, for devising an FPRAS in the case of FDs (if it exists), we need a more sophisticated machinery than the one based on Monte Carlo sampling.
On the other hand, we can establish a result analogous to Proposition
7.3
for FDs, assuming that only operations that remove a single fact (not a pair of facts) are considered. Given a set
\Sigma
of FDs, let
M_{\Sigma}^{\mathsf{uo},1}
be the Markov chain generator defined as
M_{\Sigma}^{\mathsf{uo}}
, with the difference that only sequences consisting of operations that remove a single fact are considered. We then get the following:

Theorem 7.5.

For a set
\Sigma
of FDs, and a CQ
Q
,
\mathsf{OCQA}(\Sigma,M_{\Sigma}^{\mathsf{uo},1},Q)
admits an FPRAS.

Note that singleton operations do not alter the data complexity of exact operational CQA; we can show that item (1) of Theorem
7.1
continues to hold.
Let us also clarify that focusing on singleton operations does not affect Theorem
5.1
and Theorem
6.1
; all the details about these results can be found in Appendix
E
.

8.
Future Work

Although we understand pretty well uniform operational CQA, there are still interesting open problems on approximability: (i) the case of keys and uniform repairs (we only have a negative result for the problem of counting repairs), (ii) the case of keys/FDs and uniform sequences, and (iii) the case of FDs and uniform operations (we only have a positive result assuming singleton operations).

Appendix A
Uniform Operational CQA

We provide the formal definitions of the “uniform” Markov chain generators discussed in Section
4
, and show that they indeed capture our intention. In what follows, for a database
D
, a set
\Sigma
of FDs, and a sequence
s=\mathit{op}_{1},\ldots,\mathit{op}_{n}\in\mathsf{RS}(D,\Sigma)
, we write
s_{0}
for the empty sequence
\varepsilon
, and
s_{i}
for the sequence
\mathit{op}_{1},\ldots,\mathit{op}_{i}
, for
i\in[n]
.

A.1.
Uniform Repairs

We start with the Markov chain generator based on the uniform probability distribution over the set of candidate operational repairs. As discussed in the main body, since multiple complete repairing sequences can lead to the same consistent database, we focus on canonical complete sequences. Recall that, for a database
D
, and a set
\Sigma
of FDs, we say that a
(D,\Sigma)
-repairing sequence
s\in\mathsf{CRS}(D,\Sigma)
is canonical if there is no
s^{\prime}\in\mathsf{CRS}(D,\Sigma)
such that
s(D)=s^{\prime}(D)
and
s^{\prime}\prec s
for some arbitrary ordering
\prec
over the set
\mathsf{RS}(D,\Sigma)
, and we write
\mathsf{CanCRS}(D,\Sigma)
for the set of all sequences of
\mathsf{CRS}(D,\Sigma)
that are canonical. Furthermore, for a sequence
s\in\mathsf{RS}(D,\Sigma)
, we write
\mathsf{CanCRS}_{s}(D,\Sigma)
for the set of all sequences
s^{\prime}
of
\mathsf{CanCRS}(D,\Sigma)
that have
s
as a prefix, i.e.,
s^{\prime}=s\cdot s^{\prime\prime}
for some (possibly empty) sequence
s^{\prime\prime}
.
We are now ready to define the desired Markov chain generator.

Definition A.1.

(
Uniform Repairs
)
Consider a set
\Sigma
of FDs. Let
M_{\Sigma}^{\mathsf{ur}}
be the function assigning to a database
D
the
(D,\Sigma)
-repairing Markov chain
(V,E,\mathbf{P})
, where, for each
(s,s^{\prime})\in E
,

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}(s,s^{\prime})\ =\ \left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\frac{|\mathsf{CanCRS}_{s^{\prime}}(D,\Sigma)|}{|\mathsf{CanCRS}_{s}(D,\Sigma)|}&\text{if }\mathsf{CanCRS}_{s}(D,\Sigma)\neq\emptyset\\
&\\
\frac{1}{|\mathsf{Ops}_{s}(D,\Sigma)|}&\text{otherwise.}\hskip 79.66771pt\hfill\vrule height=4.0pt,width=4.0pt,depth=0.0pt\end{array}\right.

Note that the above Markov chain generator is well-defined since, for each
s\in\mathsf{RS}(D,\Sigma)
that is not complete,

|\mathsf{CanCRS}_{s}(D,\Sigma)|\ =\ \sum_{s^{\prime}\in\mathsf{Ops}_{s}(D,\Sigma)}|\mathsf{CanCRS}_{s^{\prime}}(D,\Sigma)|,

and thus, for a non-leaf node
s\in V
,
\sum_{t\in\{s^{\prime}\mid(s,s^{\prime})\in E\}}\mathbf{P}(s,t)=1
.
We now show that the above definition captures our intention.

Proposition A.2.

Consider a set
\Sigma
of FDs. For every database
D
:

(1)

\mathsf{ORep}(D,M_{\Sigma}^{\mathsf{ur}})=\mathsf{CORep}(D,\Sigma)
.

(2)

For every
D^{\prime}\in\mathsf{ORep}(D,M_{\Sigma}^{\mathsf{ur}})
,
\mathsf{P}_{D,M_{\Sigma}^{\mathsf{ur}}}(D^{\prime})=\frac{1}{|\mathsf{ORep}(D,M_{\Sigma}^{\mathsf{ur}})|}
.

Proof.

Item (1).
It suffices to prove that
\mathsf{RL}(M_{\Sigma}^{\mathsf{ur}}(D))=\mathsf{CanCRS}(D,\Sigma)
. Let
M_{\Sigma}^{\mathsf{ur}}(D)=(V,E,\mathbf{P})
, and assume that
\pi
is its leaf distribution. Recall that for a sequence
s=\mathit{op}_{1},\ldots,\mathit{op}_{n}\in\mathsf{CRS}(D,\Sigma)
,
\pi(s)=\mathbf{P}(s_{0},s_{1})\cdots\mathbf{P}(s_{n-1},s_{n})
.

(
\supseteq
) Assume first that
s=\mathit{op}_{1},\ldots,\mathit{op}_{n}\in\mathsf{CanCRS}(D,\Sigma)
. This implies that
\mathsf{CanCRS}_{s_{i}}(D,\Sigma)\neq\emptyset
, for each
i\in\{0,1,\ldots,n\}
. Therefore,
\mathbf{P}(s_{i},s_{i+1})>0
, for
i\in\{0,1,\ldots,n\}
, and thus
\pi(s)>0
. The latter implies that
s\in\mathsf{RL}(M_{\Sigma}^{\mathsf{ur}}(D))
, which in turn shows that
\mathsf{RL}(M_{\Sigma}^{\mathsf{ur}}(D))\supseteq\mathsf{CanCRS}(D,\Sigma)
, as needed.

(
\subseteq
) Assume now that
s=\mathit{op}_{1},\ldots,\mathit{op}_{n}\in\mathsf{RL}(M_{\Sigma}^{\mathsf{ur}}(D))
. By contradiction, assume that
s\not\in\mathsf{CanCRS}(D,\Sigma)
. Since
s\in\mathsf{RL}(M_{\Sigma}^{\mathsf{ur}}(D))
,
s
must be complete. The fact that
s
is complete but not canonical implies that there exists
i\in\{0,\ldots,n\}
such that
\mathsf{CanCRS}_{s_{i}}(D,\Sigma)=\emptyset
. In particular, let
\ell
be the smallest integer in
\{0,1,\ldots,n\}
such that
\mathsf{CanCRS}_{s_{\ell}}(D,\Sigma)=\emptyset
. Clearly,
\ell>0
, since
\mathsf{CanCRS}_{\epsilon}(D,\Sigma)
is always non-empty. Thus, by the first rule of the expression defining
\mathbf{P}
in Definition
A.1
, we have that
\mathbf{P}(s_{\ell-1},s_{\ell})=0
. Hence,
\pi(s)=0
, and thus,
s\not\in\mathsf{RL}(M_{\Sigma}^{\mathsf{ur}}(D))
, which contradicts our hypothesis.

Item (2).
By the proof of item (1),
\mathsf{RL}(M_{\Sigma}^{\mathsf{ur}}(D))=\mathsf{CanCRS}(D,\Sigma)
. Hence, we conclude that

|\mathsf{ORep}(D,M_{\Sigma}^{\mathsf{ur}})|\ =\ |\mathsf{CanCRS}(D,\Sigma)|\ =\ |\mathsf{RL}(M_{\Sigma}^{\mathsf{ur}}(D))|.

Therefore, it suffices to show that, for
s\in\mathsf{CanCRS}(D,\Sigma)
,
\pi(s)=\frac{1}{|\mathsf{CanCRS}(D,\Sigma)|}
.
Let
s=\mathit{op}_{1},\ldots,\mathit{op}_{n}\in\mathsf{CanCRS}(D,\Sigma)
. Since
s\in\mathsf{RL}(M_{\Sigma}^{\mathsf{ur}}(D))
,
\pi(s)
is equal to

\frac{|\mathsf{CanCRS}_{s_{1}}(D,\Sigma)|}{|\mathsf{CanCRS}_{s_{0}}(D,\Sigma)|}\cdots\frac{|\mathsf{CanCRS}_{s_{n}}(D,\Sigma)|}{|\mathsf{CanCRS}_{s_{n-1}}(D,\Sigma)|}=\frac{|\mathsf{CanCRS}_{s_{n}}(D,\Sigma)|}{|\mathsf{CanCRS}_{s_{0}}(D,\Sigma)|}.

Since
\mathsf{CanCRS}_{s_{0}}(D,\Sigma)=\mathsf{CanCRS}_{\epsilon}(D,\Sigma)=\mathsf{CanCRS}(D,\Sigma)
, and
\mathsf{CanCRS}_{s_{n}}(D,\Sigma)=\{s_{n}\}
, then

\pi(s)=\frac{1}{|\mathsf{CanCRS}(D,\Sigma)|}
, as needed.

A.2.
Uniform Sequences

We now proceed to define the Markov chain generator based on the uniform probability distribution over the set of complete repairing sequences.
It is defined similarly to the Markov chain generator above
with the difference that we consider arbitrary, not necessarily canonical, complete sequences.

Definition A.3.

(
Uniform Sequences
)
Consider a set
\Sigma
of FDs. Let
M_{\Sigma}^{\mathsf{us}}
be the function assigning to a database
D
the
(D,\Sigma)

B.2.
Proof of Item (2) of Theorem
5.1

We prove that, for a set
\Sigma
of primary keys, and a CQ
Q
, the problem
\mathsf{RRFreq}(\Sigma,Q)
admits an FPRAS. Our proof consists of two main steps, which we briefly explain before going into the detailed proofs.

The first step is to show that, given a database
D
, we can sample elements of
\mathsf{CORep}(D,\Sigma)
uniformly at random in polynomial time in
||D||
. The existence of such an efficient sampler implies that we can employ Monte Carlo Sampling to obtain a
polynomial-time randomized approximation with additive (or absolute) error
for
\mathsf{RRFreq}(\Sigma,Q(\bar{x}))
, that is, a randomized algorithm
\mathsf{A}
that takes a input a database
D
, a tuple
\bar{c}\in\mathsf{dom}(D)^{|\bar{x}|}
,
\epsilon>0
, and
0<\delta<1
runs in polynomial time in
||D||
,
||\bar{c}||
,
1/\epsilon
and
\log(1/\delta)
, and produces a random variable
\mathsf{A}(D,\bar{c},\epsilon,\delta)
such that

\mathsf{Pr}\left(|\mathsf{A}(D,\bar{c},\epsilon,\delta)-\mathsf{rrfreq}_{\Sigma,Q}(D,\bar{c})|\ \leq\ \epsilon\right)\ \geq\ 1-\delta.

More precisely,
\mathsf{A}(D,\bar{c},\epsilon,\delta)
samples
N=O\left(\frac{\log(\frac{1}{\delta})}{\epsilon^{2}}\right)
elements of the set
\mathsf{CORep}(D,\Sigma)
, and returns the number

\frac{S}{N}\cdot|\mathsf{CORep}(D,\Sigma)|,

where
S
is the number of sampled repairs

such that
\bar{c}\in Q(D^{\prime})
.

However, in general, the existence of an efficient sampler does not guarantee the existence of an FPRAS, which bounds the
multiplicative (or relative)
error.
In order to obtain an FPRAS via Monte Carlo Sampling, the number of samples should be proportional to
\frac{1}{\mathsf{rrfreq}_{\Sigma,Q}(D,\bar{c})}

(
Dagum
et al. 2000
)
.
This brings us to the second step of our proof, where we show that
the ratio
\mathsf{rrfreq}_{\Sigma,Q}(D,\bar{c})
is never “too small”. Formally, we show that, for every database
D
, it either holds that
\mathsf{rrfreq}_{\Sigma,Q}(D,\bar{c})=0
or
\mathsf{rrfreq}_{\Sigma,Q}(D,\bar{c})\geq\frac{1}{\mathsf{pol}(||D||)}
for some polynomial
\mathsf{pol}
. In this case, we can use Monte Carlo Sampling (with a different, yet polynomial, number of samples) to obtain an FPRAS.

We now proceed to formally show the existence of an efficient sample, and the fact that target ratio is never “too small”

Step 1: Efficient Sampler

For every relation name

blocks of
\{B_{1},\dots,B_{m}\}
. Assume, without loss of generality, that these are the blocks
B_{1},\dots,B_{\ell}
. We can transform the repair
E
into a repair
E^{\prime}\in R_{D,\Sigma,h(Q)}^{\mathsf{ne}}
by bringing back an arbitrary fact of each block
B_{j}
for
j\in[\ell]
. Therefore, the repair
E
is mapped to
|B_{1}|\times\dots\times|B_{\ell}|
distinct repairs of
R_{D,\Sigma,h(Q)}^{\mathsf{ne}}
.

Observe that at most
2^{m}-1
repairs
E\in R_{D,\Sigma,h(Q)}^{\mathsf{e}}
are mapped to the same repair
E^{\prime}\in R_{D,\Sigma,h(Q)}^{\mathsf{ne}}
. This holds since the repair
E^{\prime}
determines, for every block
B_{j}
that is not one of
B_{1},\dots,B_{m}
, whether we keep a fact of
B_{j}
in the repair and which fact of
B_{j}
we keep. For the blocks
B_{1},\dots,B_{m}
, a repair
E
that is mapped to
E^{\prime}
can either contain the same fact as
E^{\prime}
contains from this block, or none of the facts of the block. Hence, there are two possibilities for each block of
\{B_{1},\dots,B_{m}\}
and the total number of possibilities is
2^{m}
. However, we have to disregard one of these possibilities, as it represents
E^{\prime}
itself (where for every block of
\{B_{1},\dots,B_{m}\}
we keep the same fact as
E^{\prime}
). We conclude that

\left|R_{D,\Sigma,h(Q)}^{\mathsf{e}}\right|\leq(2^{m}-1)\times\left|R_{D,\Sigma,h(Q)}^{\mathsf{ne}}\right|

and

\displaystyle|\mathsf{CORep}(D,\Sigma)|

\displaystyle=\left|R_{D,\Sigma,h(Q)}^{\mathsf{e}}\right|+\left|R_{D,\Sigma,h(Q)}^{\mathsf{ne}}\right|

\displaystyle\leq(2^{m}-1)\times\left|R_{D,\Sigma,h(Q)}^{\mathsf{ne}}\right|+\left|R_{D,\Sigma,h(Q)}^{\mathsf{ne}}\right|

\displaystyle=2^{m}\times\left|R_{D,\Sigma,h(Q)}^{\mathsf{ne}}\right|.

Note that
(2^{m}-1)\times\left|R_{D,\Sigma,h(Q)}^{\mathsf{ne}}\right|
is just an upper bound on
\left|R_{D,\Sigma,h(Q)}^{\mathsf{e}}\right|
because, as said above, each repair
E
of
R_{D,\Sigma,h(Q)}^{\mathsf{e}}
is mapped to several distinct repairs of
R_{D,\Sigma,h(Q)}^{\mathsf{ne}}
.

Finally, each repair of
R_{D,\Sigma,h(Q)}^{\mathsf{ne}}
keeps a fact of every block in
\{B_{1},\dots,B_{m}\}
. Here, we are interested in the repairs that keep all the facts of
h(Q)
, as these repairs
E
satisfy
\bar{c}\in Q(E)
. Clearly,

|\{E\in\mathsf{CORep}(D,\Sigma)\mid h(Q)\subseteq E\}|=\frac{1}{|B_{1}|\times\dots\times|B_{m}|}\times\left|R_{D,\Sigma,h(Q)}^{\mathsf{ne}}\right|

as all the facts of a block are symmetric.
Hence, we conclude that:

\displaystyle\frac{|\{E\in\mathsf{CORep}(D,\Sigma)\mid h(Q)\subseteq E\}|}{|\mathsf{CORep}(D,\Sigma)|}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\frac{1}{|B_{1}|\times\dots\times|B_{m}|}\times\left|R_{D,\Sigma,h(Q)}^{\mathsf{ne}}\right|}{2^{m}\times\left|R_{D,\Sigma,h(Q)}^{\mathsf{ne}}\right|}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|B_{1}|\times\dots\times|B_{m}|\times 2^{m}}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{|D|^{m}\times 2^{m}}\geq\frac{1}{|D|^{|Q|}\times 2^{|Q|}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(2|D|)^{|Q|}}\geq\frac{1}{(2||D||)^{||Q||}}

and this is clearly a lower bound on
\mathsf{rrfreq}_{\Sigma,Q}(D,\bar{c})
, as needed.

Here is a simple example that illustrates the argument given in the proof of Lemma
5.3
.

Example B.3.

Consider again the database
D
depicted in Figure
2
, and the set
\Sigma=\{R:A_{1}\rightarrow A_{2}\}
consisting of a single key.
Let
Q
be the CQ
\textrm{Ans}(x)\ \text{:-}\ R(a_{1},x)
. A homomorphism
h
from
Q
to
D
with
h(Q)\models\Sigma
and
h(x)=b_{1}
is such that
h(Q)=\{R(a_{1},b_{1})\}
. The fact
R(a_{1},b_{1})
belongs to the block
\{f_{1,1},f_{1,2},f_{1,3}\}
. Hence, the set
R_{D,\Sigma,h(Q)}^{\mathsf{ne}}
consists of the repairs:

\displaystyle\{f_{1,1},f_{2,1}\}\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\{f_{1,2},f_{2,1}\}\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\{f_{1,3},f_{2,1}\}

\displaystyle\{f_{1,1},f_{2,1},f_{3,1}\}\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\{f_{1,2},f_{2,1},f_{3,1}\}\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\{f_{1,3},f_{2,1},f_{3,1}\}

\displaystyle\{f_{1,1},f_{2,1},f_{3,2}\}\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\{f_{1,2},f_{2,1},f_{3,2}\}\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\{f_{1,3},f_{2,1},f_{3,2}\}

and the set
R_{D,\Sigma,h(Q)}^{\mathsf{e}}
consists of the repairs:

\displaystyle\{f_{2,1}\}\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\{f_{2,1},f_{3,1}\}\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\{f_{2,1},f_{3,2}\}

According to the mapping defined in the proof of Lemma
5.3
, the repair
\{f_{2,1}\}
is mapped to the repairs in

\{\{f_{1,1},f_{2,1}\},\{f_{1,2},f_{2,1}\},\{f_{1,3},f_{2,1}\}\}

that have one additional fact from the block of
R(a_{1},b_{1})
. Similarly, the repair
\{f_{2,1},f_{3,1}\}
is mapped to the repairs in

\{\{f_{1,1},f_{2,1},f_{3,1}\},\{f_{1,2},f_{2,1},f_{3,1}\},\{f_{1,3},f_{2,1},f_{3,1}\}\}

and the repair
\{f_{2,1},f_{3,2}\}
is mapped to the repairs in

\{\{f_{1,1},f_{2,1},f_{3,2}\},\{f_{1,2},f_{2,1},f_{3,2}\},\{f_{1,3},f_{2,1},f_{3,2}\}\}.

Hence, each repair of
R_{D,\Sigma,h(Q)}^{\mathsf{e}}
is mapped to precisely three repairs of
R_{D,\Sigma,h(Q)}^{\mathsf{ne}}
, since three is the size of the block of
R(a_{1},b_{1})
. Moreover, in this case,
2^{m}-1=1
, and a single repair of
R_{D,\Sigma,h(Q)}^{\mathsf{e}}
is mapped to every repair of
R_{D,\Sigma,h(Q)}^{\mathsf{ne}}
.

Since all the facts of a block are symmetric with each other, precisely
\frac{1}{3}
of the repairs in
R_{D,\Sigma,h(Q)}^{\mathsf{ne}}
contain the fact
R(a_{1},b_{1})
—three repairs. Thus, it holds that

|\{E\in\mathsf{CORep}(D,\Sigma)\mid h(Q)\subseteq E\}|\ =\ \frac{1}{3}\times 9=3

and

|\mathsf{CORep}(D,\Sigma)|\ =\ 12.

We conclude that

\frac{|\{E\in\mathsf{CORep}(D,\Sigma)\mid h(Q)\subseteq E\}|}{|\mathsf{CORep}(D,\Sigma)|}\ =\ \frac{3}{12}\ =\ \frac{1}{4}.

Note that

\frac{1}{(2|D|)^{|Q|}}\ =\ \frac{1}{12}

is indeed a lower bound on that value, and it is also a lower bound on the ratio
\mathsf{rrfreq}_{\Sigma,Q}(D,(b_{1}))
that, in this case, equals
\frac{1}{4}
.

B.3.
Proof of Item (3) of Theorem
5.1

As discussed in the main body of the paper, the proof of item (3) of Theorem
5.1
proceeds in two main steps, which correspond to Proposition
5.5
and Lemma
5.6
.
Before giving the formal proofs, we first need to introduce some auxiliary notions and results. In the sequel, we concentrate on undirected graphs without self-loops.

Auxiliary Notions and Results

Consider an undirected graph
G=(V,E)
and an integer
\Delta\geq 0
. We say that
G
has
degree

\Delta
if each node of
V
participates in at most
\Delta
edges. Moreover,
G
is connected if there is a path between every two nodes of
G
. We call
G

trivially connected
if
|V|\leq 1
; otherwise, it is
non-trivially connected
. Finally,
\mathsf{IS}(G)
denotes the set of
all
independent sets of
G
.

For a database
D
and a set
\Sigma
of FDs, the
conflict graph of
D
w.r.t.
\Sigma
is the undirected graph
\mathsf{CG}(D,\Sigma)=(V,E)
, where
V=D
, and
\{f,g\}\in E
if
\{f,g\}\not\models\Sigma
. We call
D
non-trivially (resp., trivially)
\Sigma
-connected if
\mathsf{CG}(D,\Sigma)
is non-trivially (resp., trivially) connected.

In order to prove the desired claims, we establish an auxiliary result that relates the number of candidate repairs of an inconsistent database that is non-trivially connected with the number of independent sets of the underlying conflict graph; this is Lemma
5.4
in the main body, which we recall and prove here:

Lemma
5.4
.

Consider a non-trivially
\Sigma
-connected database
D
, where
\Sigma
is a set of FDs. It holds that
|\mathsf{CORep}(D,\Sigma)|=|\mathsf{IS}(\mathsf{CG}(D,\Sigma))|
.

Proof.

(\subseteq)
Consider a candidate repair
D^{\prime}\in\mathsf{CORep}(D,\Sigma)
. By definition,
D^{\prime}
is consistent, i.e., there are no two facts
f,g\in D^{\prime}
such that
\{f,g\}\not\models\Sigma
. By definition of the conflict graph of
D
w.r.t.
\Sigma
, we conclude that no two facts
f,g\in D^{\prime}
are connected via an edge in
\mathsf{CG}(D,\Sigma)
. Hence,
D^{\prime}
is an independent set of
\mathsf{CG}(D,\Sigma)
.

(\supseteq)
Consider now an independent set
D^{\prime}\in\mathsf{IS}(\mathsf{CG}(D,\Sigma))
. Since there are no two facts
f,g\in D^{\prime}
that are connected via an edge of
\mathsf{CG}(D,\Sigma)
,
D^{\prime}
is consistent w.r.t.
\Sigma
. It remains to show that there exists a sequence
s\in\mathsf{CRS}(D,\Sigma)
such that
s(D)=D^{\prime}
; we distinguish the two cases where either
D^{\prime}\neq\emptyset
or
D^{\prime}=\emptyset
.

Case 1.

Let us first concentrate on the case where
D^{\prime}\neq\emptyset
. In order to define the repairing sequence
s\in\mathsf{CRS}(D,\Sigma)
such that
s(D)=D^{\prime}
, we first define a convenient stratification of the facts of
D
. We inductively define the strata
L_{0},L_{1},\ldots
as follows:

•

L_{0}=D^{\prime}
.

•

For each
i\geq 1
,

\displaystyle L_{i}=\{f\in D\ |\

\displaystyle f\not\in L_{0}\cup\ldots\cup L_{i-1}\textrm{ and }

\displaystyle\textrm{there is }f^{\prime}\in L_{i-1}\textrm{ with }\{f,f^{\prime}\}\not\models\Sigma\}.

Observe that, since
\mathsf{CG}(D,\Sigma)
is connected, each fact
f\in D
occurs in some
L_{i}
, i.e., if
n
is the smallest integer such that
L_{\ell}=\emptyset
, for
\ell>n
, we have that
D=\bigcup^{n}_{i=0}L_{i}
.

Let
L_{i}=\left\{f^{i}_{1},\ldots,f^{i}_{|L_{i}|}\right\}
, for each
i\in[n]
. We now construct the desired sequence
s
as follows. We let the first
|L_{n}|
operations be
-f^{n}_{1},\ldots,-f^{n}_{|L_{n}|}
. To see that
-f^{n}_{j}
is a
(D^{s}_{j-1},\Sigma)
-justified operation for every
1\leq j\leq|L_{n}|
, observe that, by definition of
L_{n}
, each
f^{n}_{j}
is in a violation with some fact in
L_{n-1}
, which has not been removed yet. The operations
\mathit{op}_{|L_{n}|+1},\ldots,\mathit{op}_{|L_{n}|+|L_{n-1}|}
will be
-f^{n-1}_{1},\ldots,-f^{n-1}_{|L_{n-1}|}
, which are all justified because there exists a violation for each fact with some fact from
L_{n-2}
that has not been removed yet. The next operations of
s
are defined in the same way for the remaining strata, until the last
|L_{1}|
operations, which will be
-f^{1}_{1},\ldots,-f^{1}_{|L_{1}|}
. Again, these operations are justified since, by definition, each of the facts
f^{1}_{1},\ldots,f^{1}_{|L_{1}|}
is in conflict with some fact from
L_{0}=D^{\prime}
.
Summing up, the sequence
s
is

-f^{n}_{1},\ldots,-f^{n}_{|L_{n}|},-f^{n-1}_{1},\ldots,-f^{n-1}_{|L_{n-1}|},\ldots,-f^{1}_{1},\ldots,-f^{1}_{|L_{1}|}.

We have that
s\in\mathsf{RS}(D,\Sigma)
and that
s(D)=D^{\prime}\models\Sigma
. Hence,
s\in\mathsf{CRS}(D,\Sigma)
, which implies

D^{\prime}\in\mathsf{CORep}(D,\Sigma)
, as needed.

Case 2.

The case where
D^{\prime}=\emptyset
is treated similarly. We only need to slightly adjust the last operation of the sequence
s
. Fix some fact
f^{*}\in D
. We stratify the facts of
D
as in the first case, but we let
L_{0}=\{f^{*}\}
. We then define
s
in the same way as above. Let
L_{n}
be again the last non-empty stratum. Since
\mathsf{CG}(D,\Sigma)
is non-trivially connected,
D\not\models\Sigma
, and thus, we have that
n>0
, i.e., at least stratum
L_{1}
is non-empty.
Then, we have that the first
|L_{n}|
operations are
-f^{n}_{1},\ldots,-f^{n}_{|L_{n}|}
. We continue with the remaining strata
L_{n-1}
to
L_{2}
as before. The last
|L_{1}|
operations are defined as
-f^{1}_{1},\ldots,-f^{1}_{|L_{1}|-1},-\left\{f^{1}_{|L_{1}|},f^{*}\right\}
. Note that, by definition of
L_{1}
, every fact
f^{1}_{1},\ldots,f^{1}_{|L_{1}|-1}
is in a violation with
f^{*}
, and thus, their removal is a justified operation. Now, there are only two facts left,
f^{1}_{|L_{1}|}
and
f^{*}
, which together violate
\Sigma
(recall that
L_{1}
is non-empty). Therefore,
-\left\{f^{1}_{|L_{1}|},f^{*}\right\}
is a justified operation, and we have that
s\in\mathsf{RS}(D,\Sigma)
and
s(D)=D^{\prime}=\emptyset\models\Sigma
. Hence,
s\in\mathsf{CRS}(D,\Sigma)
, which in turn implies that
D^{\prime}\in\mathsf{CORep}(D,\Sigma)
, as needed.

We are now ready to proceed with the two main steps of the proof of item (3) of Theorem
5.1
, which correspond to Proposition
5.5
and Lemma
5.6
, respectively. Note that both results are essentially dealing with the following counting problem for a set
\Sigma
of FDs:

PROBLEM
:

\sharp\mathsf{CORep}^{\mathsf{con}}(\Sigma)

INPUT
:

A non-trivially
\Sigma
-connected database
D
.

OUTPUT
:

The number
|\mathsf{CORep}(D,\Sigma)|
.

Step 1: An Inapproximability Result About Keys.

The formal statement, already given in the main body, and its proof follow. Note that the statement of Proposition
5.5
given below is more compact than the one given in the main body of the paper since we explicitly use the name of the problem
\sharp\mathsf{CORep}^{\mathsf{con}}(\Sigma)
.

Proposition
5.5
.

Unless
{\rm RP}={\rm NP}
, there exists a set
\Sigma
of keys over
\{R\}
such that
\sharp\mathsf{CORep}^{\mathsf{con}}(\Sigma)
does not admit an FPRAS.

Before giving the proof of Proposition
5.5
, we need an auxiliary result about the problem of counting the number of independent sets of undirected graphs.
For an integer
\Delta\geq 0
, we define

PROBLEM
:

\sharp\mathsf{IS}_{\Delta}

INPUT
:

An undirected graph
G
of degree
\Delta
.

OUTPUT
:

The number
|\mathsf{IS}(G)|
.

We know from
(
Sly 2010
)
that the following holds:

Proposition B.4.

For every
\Delta\geq 6
, unless
{\rm RP}={\rm NP}
,
\#\mathsf{IS}_{\Delta}
does not admit an FPRAS.

Note that the above result states the inapproximability of
\sharp\mathsf{IS}_{\Delta}
for arbitrary, not necessarily non-trivially connected graphs. However, for showing Proposition
5.5
, we need the stronger version of Proposition
B.4
that establishes the inapproximability of
\sharp\mathsf{IS}_{\Delta}
even for non-trivially connected graphs.
Let
\sharp\mathsf{IS}^{\mathsf{con}}_{\Delta}
be the problem defined as
\sharp\mathsf{IS}_{\Delta}
with the difference that the input is a non-trivially connected undirected graph. We proceed to show the following:

Lemma B.5.

For every
\Delta\geq 6
, unless
{\rm RP}={\rm NP}
,
\#\mathsf{IS}^{\mathsf{con}}_{\Delta}
does not admit an FPRAS.

Proof.

By contradiction, assume that
\#\mathsf{IS}^{\mathsf{con}}_{\Delta}
admits an FPRAS, for some
\Delta\geq 6
, i.e., there is a randomized algorithm
\mathsf{A}
that takes as input a non-trivially connected graph
G=(V,E)
of degree
\Delta
,
\epsilon>0
, and
0<\delta<1
, runs in polynomial time in
||G||
,
1/\epsilon
, and
\log(1/\delta)
, and produces a random variable
\mathsf{A}(G,\epsilon,\delta)
such that

\mathsf{Pr}\left((1-\epsilon)\cdot|\mathsf{IS}(G)|\leq\mathsf{A}(G,\epsilon,\delta)\leq(1+\epsilon)\cdot|\mathsf{IS}(G)|\right)\ \geq\ 1-\delta.

From this, we can construct an FPRAS
\mathsf{A}^{\prime}
for
\#\mathsf{IS}_{\Delta}
as follows. Given a graph
G=(V,E)
of degree
\Delta
, let the connected components, i.e., the maximal connected subgraphs, of
G
be
(\mathsf{CC}_{i})_{1\leq i\leq n}
with
\mathsf{CC}_{i}=(V_{i},E_{i})
.
Furthermore, assume, w.l.o.g., that
\mathsf{CC}_{1},\ldots,\mathsf{CC}_{\ell}
, are all the trivially connected components of
G
, for some
\ell\leq n
.
Given
G
,
\epsilon>0
, and
0<\delta<1
,
\mathsf{A}^{\prime}
is defined as

\mathsf{A}^{\prime}(G,\epsilon,\delta)\ =\ 2^{\ell}\cdot\prod\limits^{n}_{i=\ell+1}\mathsf{A}\left(\mathsf{CC}_{i},\frac{\epsilon}{2n},\frac{\delta}{2n}\right).

Note that a run of
\mathsf{A}
does not depend on any other run, and thus, the random variables
\mathsf{A}(\mathsf{CC}_{i},\frac{\epsilon}{2n},\frac{\delta}{2n})
are independent from each other. It is also easy to see that

|\mathsf{IS}(G)|\ =\ 2^{\ell}\cdot\prod_{i=\ell+1}^{n}|\mathsf{IS}(\mathsf{CC}_{i})|.

Therefore, since
\mathsf{A}
is an FPRAS for
\#\mathsf{IS}^{\mathsf{con}}_{\Delta}
, we have that

\mathsf{Pr}\left(\left(1-\frac{\epsilon}{2n}\right)^{n}\cdot|\mathsf{IS}(G)|\leq\mathsf{A^{\prime}}(G,\epsilon,\delta)\leq\right.\\
\left.\left(1+\frac{\epsilon}{2n}\right)^{n}\cdot|\mathsf{IS}(G)|\right)\geq\left(1-\frac{\delta}{2n}\right)^{n}.

Finally, we know (see, e.g.,
(
Jerrum
et al. 1986
)
) that
the following inequalities hold: for
0\leq x\leq 1
and
m\geq 1
,

1-x\leq\left(1-\frac{x}{2m}\right)^{m}\quad\text{and}\quad\left(1+\frac{x}{2m}\right)^{m}\leq 1+x.

Consequently,

\mathsf{Pr}\left((1-\epsilon)\cdot|\mathsf{IS}(G)|\leq\mathsf{A^{\prime}}(G,\epsilon,\delta)\leq(1+\epsilon)\cdot|\mathsf{IS}(G)|\right)\geq 1-\delta.

Hence,
\mathsf{A}^{\prime}
fulfils the probabilistic guarantees required for an FPRAS. To confirm the desired running time of
\mathsf{A^{\prime}}
, note that there are at most
n=|V|
connected components of
G
, which can be computed in polynomial time via any textbook algorithm. Thus, since
\mathsf{A}
is an FPRAS for
\#\mathsf{IS}^{\mathsf{con}}_{\Delta}
, for each each
i\in[n]
, the random variable
\mathsf{A}(\mathsf{CC}_{i},\frac{\epsilon}{2n},\frac{\delta}{2n})
can be computed in polynomial time w.r.t.
\mathsf{CC}_{i}
,
\frac{|V|}{\epsilon}
, and
\frac{|V|}{\delta}
. Since
\mathsf{A}^{\prime}
multiplies at most
|V|
such random variables,
\mathsf{A^{\prime}}
is an FPRAS for
\sharp\mathsf{IS}_{\Delta}
, which contradicts Proposition
B.4
.

With Lemma
B.5
in place, we can now prove Proposition
5.5
.

Proof of Proposition
5.5
.

Consider a non-trivially connected undirected graph
G=(V,E)
with degree
\Delta=6
. Let
\mathbf{S}
be the schema consisting of the single relation name
\{R/\Delta+1\}
with
(A_{1},\ldots,A_{\Delta+1})
being the tuple of attributes of
R
, and
\Sigma_{K}=\{\phi_{1},\ldots,\phi_{\Delta+1}\}
a set of keys over
\mathbf{S}
, where
\phi_{i}=R:A_{i}\rightarrow\mathsf{att}(R)
for each
i\in[\Delta+1]
.
We show that, unless
{\rm RP}={\rm NP}
,
we can construct a non-trivially
\Sigma_{K}
-connected database
D_{G}
in polynomial time in
||G
|| such that
|\mathsf{IS}(G)|=|\mathsf{CORep}(D_{G},\Sigma_{K})|
. Hence, the existence of an FPRAS for
\sharp\mathsf{CORep}^{\mathsf{con}}(\Sigma_{K})
would imply the existence of an FPRAS for
\sharp\mathsf{IS}^{\mathsf{con}}_{\Delta}
, which in turn contradicts Lemma
B.5
.

The key property that
D_{G}
should enjoy is the following: there exists a bijection
\mu:V\rightarrow D_{G}
from the set of nodes of
G
to the facts of
D_{G}
such that
(u,v)\in E
iff
\{\mu(u),\mu(v)\}\not\models\Sigma_{K}
. The latter immediately implies that
|\mathsf{IS}(G)|=|\mathsf{IS}(\mathsf{CG}(D_{G},\Sigma_{K}))|
, which, together with the fact that
G
is non-trivially connected, and hence,
D_{G}
is non-trivially
\Sigma_{K}
-connected, implies that
|\mathsf{IS}(G)|=|\mathsf{CORep}(D_{G},\Sigma_{K})|
by Lemma
5.4
. The formal construction of
D_{G}
follows.

The Database
D_{G}
.

It is known that the edges of
G
are
(\Delta+1)
-colourable, and such a coloring can be constructed in polynomial time in
||G||

(
Misra and Gries 1992
)
. Therefore, we are able to efficiently assign the colours
C=\{c_{1},\ldots,c_{\Delta+1}\}
to the edges of
G
in such a way that none of the nodes belongs to two distinct edges of the same colour. Let
M:E\rightarrow C
be such a coloring.
The database
D_{G}
is such that
\mathsf{dom}(D_{G})=E\cup F
,
where
F
is a finite a set of constants with
E\cap F=\emptyset
, and has the following facts:

(1)

for each node
v\in V
, we add to
D_{G}
a fact of the form
R(a^{v}_{1},\ldots,a^{v}_{\Delta+1})
, and

(2)

the constants of such facts are defined as follows:

(a)

for every edge
e=\{u,v\}\in E
, assuming that
M(e)=c_{i}
, we let
a^{u}_{i}=a^{v}_{i}=e
, and

(b)

for every
a^{v}_{i}
not defined in the above step, we let
a^{v}_{i}=f
for some constant
f\in F
only to be used once.

We use
R(\bar{a}^{v})
to denote
R(a^{v}_{1},\ldots,a^{v}_{\Delta+1})
, for short. Note that every
a^{v}_{i}
is well-defined since
v
has at most one edge of colour
c_{i}
, and thus,
a^{v}_{i}
is uniquely defined by either the edge with colour
c_{i}
, or in case there is no such edge, by a fresh constant
f\in F
. Let us also stress that we can build
D_{G}
in polynomial time in
||G||
.
We can now prove the following crucial property of
D_{G}
:

Lemma B.6.

Consider two nodes
u,v
of
G
. Then,
\{u,v\}
is an edge in
G
iff
\{R(\bar{a}^{u}),R(\bar{a}^{v})\}\not\models\Sigma_{K}
.

Proof.

Consider an edge

By assumption,

Thus, it suffices to show that the left-hand side of the above inequality is bounded from above by

\left.\frac{1+\epsilon^{\prime}}{1+|\mathsf{CORep}(D,\Sigma_{K})|}\right).

By expanding the products in the above expression, we obtain

\frac{\epsilon^{\prime}}{1-\epsilon^{\prime}}\ \leq\ \frac{\epsilon^{\prime}}{1-\epsilon^{\prime}}\cdot|\mathsf{CORep}(D,\Sigma_{K})|.

Recalling that

Having Lemma

The formal statement, already given in the main body of the paper, and its proof follow:

Lemma
6.2

For a database
D

\Sigma

\mathsf{CRS}(D,\Sigma)

Proof.

The algorithm
\mathsf{SampleSeq}
, depicted in Algorithm

, is a recursive algorithm that returns a sequence

with probability
\frac{1}{|\mathsf{CRS}(D,\Sigma)|}
. The algorithm starts with the empty sequence
\varepsilon
, and, at each step, extends the sequence by selecting one of the justified operations at that point. That is, if the current sequence is
s
, then we select one of the
(s(D),\Sigma)
-justified operations. The probability of selecting an operation
\mathit{op}
is:

\frac{|\mathsf{CRS}(op(s(D)),\Sigma)|}{|\mathsf{CRS}(s(D),\Sigma)|}.

Hence, the probability of returning a sequence
s=\mathit{op}_{1},\dots,\mathit{op}_{n}
of
\mathsf{CRS}(D,\Sigma)
is:

\displaystyle\frac{|\mathsf{CRS}(\mathit{op}_{1}(D),\Sigma)|}{|\mathsf{CRS}(\varepsilon(D),\Sigma)|}\times\frac{|\mathsf{CRS}(\mathit{op}_{2}(\mathit{op}_{1}(D)),\Sigma)|}{|\mathsf{CRS}(\mathit{op}_{1}(D),\Sigma)|}\times\dots

\displaystyle\times\frac{|\mathsf{CRS}(\mathit{op}_{n}(\dots D\dots),\Sigma)|}{|\mathsf{CRS}(\mathit{op}_{n-1}(\dots D\dots),\Sigma)|}

\displaystyle=\frac{|\mathsf{CRS}(\mathit{op}_{n}(\dots D\dots),\Sigma)|}{|\mathsf{CRS}(\varepsilon(D),\Sigma)|}=\frac{1}{|\mathsf{CRS}(D,\Sigma)|}

Most of the terms in the product cancel each other, and

|\mathsf{CRS}(\mathit{op}_{n}(\dots D\dots),\Sigma)|=|\mathsf{CRS}(s(D),\Sigma)|=1

since
s(D)\models\Sigma
; hence, there is a single complete repairing sequence for
s(D)
w.r.t.
\Sigma
—the empty sequence.

Since the length of a sequence is bounded by
|D|-1
, the number of justified operations at each step is polynomial in
||D||
(as this is the number of facts involved in violations of the constraints plus the number of conflicting pairs of facts), and, by Lemma

, for a set
\Sigma
of primary keys, we can compute

in polynomial time in
||D||
for any database
D
, we get that the total running time of the algorithm is also polynomial in
||D||
, as needed.

Step 2: Polynomial Lower Bound

Now that we have an efficient sampler for the complete repairing sequences, we show that there is a polynomial lower bound on
\mathsf{srfreq}_{\Sigma,Q}(D,\bar{c})

The formal statement, already given in the main body of the paper, and its proof follow:

Lemma
6.3
.

Consider a set
\Sigma
of primary keys, and a CQ
Q(\bar{x})
. For every database
D
, and tuple
\bar{c}\in\mathsf{dom}(D)^{|\bar{x}|}
,

\mathsf{srfreq}_{\Sigma,Q}(D,\bar{c})\ \geq\ \frac{1}{(2\cdot||D||)^{||Q||}}

whenever
\mathsf{srfreq}_{\Sigma,Q}(D,\bar{c})>0
.

Proof.

The proof is very similar to the proof of Lemma
5.3
, except that here we reason about sequences rather than repairs. Recall that we treat a query
Q
as the set
\{R_{i}(\bar{y}_{i})\mid i\in[n]\}
of atoms on the right-hand side of
:-
, and, for a database
D
and a homomorphism
h
from
Q
to
D
, we denote by
h(Q)
the set
\{R_{i}(h(\bar{y}_{i}))\mid i\in[n]\}
.
Here, we denote by
S_{D,\Sigma,h(Q)}^{\mathsf{e}}
the set of sequences
s\in\mathsf{CRS}(D,\Sigma)
such that
s(D)\cap B_{j}=\emptyset
for at least one block of
\{B_{1},\dots,B_{m}\}
(recall that these are the blocks that contains the facts of
h(Q)
), and by
S_{D,\Sigma,h(Q)}^{\mathsf{ne}}
the set of sequences
s\in\mathsf{CRS}(D,\Sigma)
such that
s(D)\cap B_{j}\neq\emptyset
for every block of
\{B_{1},\dots,B_{m}\}
.

Now, for every sequence
s\in S_{D,\Sigma,h(Q)}^{\mathsf{e}}
, and for every block
B_{j}
such that
s(D)\cap B_{j}=\emptyset
, the last operation of
s
over the facts of
B_{j}
must remove a pair
\{f,g\}
of facts. We map each sequence
s\in S_{D,\Sigma,h(Q)}^{\mathsf{e}}
to a sequence
s^{\prime}\in S_{D,\Sigma,h(Q)}^{\mathsf{ne}}
by replacing the last operation of
s
over each such
B_{j}\in\{B_{1},\dots,B_{m}\}
with an operation that removes only one of the facts of the pair—either
f
or
g
. Hence, if
s(D)\cap B_{j}=\emptyset
for precisely
\ell
of the blocks of
\{B_{1},\dots,B_{m}\}
, the sequence
s
is mapped to
2^{\ell}
distinct sequences of
S_{D,\Sigma,h(Q)}^{\mathsf{ne}}
.

Similarly to the proof of Lemma
5.3
, for every sequence
s^{\prime}\in S_{D,\Sigma,h(Q)}^{\mathsf{ne}}
, there are
2^{m}-1
sequences
s\in S_{D,\Sigma,h(Q)}^{\mathsf{e}}
that are mapped to it. This is because the sequence
s^{\prime}
determines all the operations over the blocks outside
\{B_{1},\dots,B_{m}\}
, and for each block
B_{j}\in\{B_{1},\dots,B_{m}\}
, it determines all the operations over
B_{j}
except for the last one. If
s^{\prime}(D)\cap B_{j}=\{f\}
and the last operation of
s^{\prime}
over
B_{j}
removes the fact
g
, then the last operation of
s
over
B_{j}
either also removes
g
or removes the pair
\{f,g\}
. If the last operation of
s^{\prime}
over
B_{j}
removes a pair
\{g,h\}
of facts, then the last operation of
s
over
B_{j}
must also remove the same pair of facts. Hence, there are at most two possible cases for each block of
\{B_{1},\dots,B_{m}\}
and
2^{m}
possibilities in total. And, again, we have to disregard the possibility that is equivalent to
s^{\prime}
itself.

Therefore, we have that:

\left|S_{D,\Sigma,h(Q)}^{\mathsf{e}}\right|\ \leq\ (2^{m}-1)\times\left|S_{D,\Sigma,h(Q)}^{\mathsf{ne}}\right|

and

\displaystyle|\mathsf{CRS}(D,\Sigma)|

\displaystyle=\left|S_{D,\Sigma,h(Q)}^{\mathsf{e}}\right|+\left|S_{D,\Sigma,h(Q)}^{\mathsf{ne}}\right|

\displaystyle\leq(2^{m}-1)\times\left|S_{D,\Sigma,h(Q)}^{\mathsf{ne}}\right|+\left|S_{D,\Sigma,h(Q)}^{\mathsf{ne}}\right|

\displaystyle=2^{m}\times\left|S_{D,\Sigma,h(Q)}^{\mathsf{ne}}\right|.

As said above, each sequence
s
of
S_{D,\Sigma,h(Q)}^{\mathsf{e}}
can be mapped to
2^{\ell}
distinct sequences of
S_{D,\Sigma,h(Q)}^{\mathsf{ne}}
, where
\ell
is the number of blocks in
\{B_{1},\dots,B_{m}\}
for which
E\cap B_{j}=\emptyset
. Moreover, there are sequences
s^{\prime}\in S_{D,\Sigma,h(Q)}^{\mathsf{ne}}
such that no sequence
s\in S_{D,\Sigma,h(Q)}^{\mathsf{e}}
is mapped to
s^{\prime}
. These are the sequences
s^{\prime}
where the last operation of
s^{\prime}
over every block of
\{B_{1},\dots,B_{m}\}
is a pair removal (but
s^{\prime}
keeps some fact of each
B_{j}
). Hence,
(2^{m}-1)\times|S_{D,\Sigma,h(Q)}^{\mathsf{ne}}|
is only an upper bound on
|S_{D,\Sigma,h(Q)}^{\mathsf{e}}|
.
Since all the facts of a single block are symmetric,

\left|\{s\in\mathsf{CRS}(D,\Sigma)\mid h(Q)\subseteq s(D)\}\right|=\frac{1}{|B_{1}|\times\dots\times|B_{m}|}\times\left|S_{D,\Sigma,h(Q)}^{\mathsf{ne}}\right|

and, we conclude that

\displaystyle\frac{|\{s\in\mathsf{CRS}(D,\Sigma)\mid h(Q)\subseteq s(D)\}|}{|\mathsf{CRS}(D,\Sigma)|}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\frac{1}{|B_{1}|\times\dots\times|B_{m}|}\times\left|S_{D,\Sigma,h(Q)}^{\mathsf{ne}}\right|}{2^{m}\times\left|S_{D,\Sigma,h(Q)}^{\mathsf{ne}}\right|}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|B_{1}|\times\dots\times|B_{m}|\times 2^{m}}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{|D|^{m}\times 2^{m}}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{(2|D|)^{|Q|}}\geq\frac{1}{(2||D||)^{||Q||}}

Since all the sequences of
\{s\in\mathsf{CRS}(D,\Sigma)\mid h(Q)\subseteq s(D)\}
are such that

and so

, this concludes our proof.

Step 1: Efficient Sampler

Given a database
D
, the definition of
M_{\Sigma}^{\mathsf{uo}}
immediately implies the existence of an efficient sampler that returns a sequence
s\in\mathsf{RL}(\Sigma,M_{\Sigma}^{\mathsf{uo}}(D))
with probability
\pi(s)
, where
\pi
is the leaf distribution of
M_{\Sigma}^{\mathsf{uo}}(D)
. The algorithm is very similar to Algorithm
1
, except that if the current sequence is
s
, the probability to select a
(s(D),\Sigma)
-justified operation is

\frac{1}{|\mathsf{Ops}_{s}(D,\Sigma)|}.

Hence, we immediately obtain the following result, already given in the main body of the paper:

Lemma
7.2
.

Given a database
D
, and a set
\Sigma
of keys, we can sample elements of
\mathsf{RL}(M_{\Sigma}^{\mathsf{uo}}(D))
according to the leaf distribution of
M_{\Sigma}^{\mathsf{uo}}(D)
in polynomial time in
||D||
.

Step 2: Polynomial Lower Bound

The rest of the section is devoted to showing that there is a polynomial lower bound on
\mathsf{P}_{M_{\Sigma}^{\mathsf{uo}},Q}(D,\bar{c})
.

Proposition
7.3
.

Consider a set
\Sigma
of keys, and a CQ
Q(\bar{x})
. There is a polynomial
\mathsf{pol}
such that, for every database
D
, and
\bar{c}\in\mathsf{dom}(D)^{|\bar{x}|}
,

\mathsf{P}_{M_{\Sigma}^{\mathsf{uo}},Q}(D,\bar{c})\ \geq\ \frac{1}{\mathsf{pol}(||D||)}

whenever
\mathsf{P}_{M_{\Sigma}^{\mathsf{uo}},Q}(D,\bar{c})>0
.

As usual, we treat the CQ
Q
as the set
\{R_{i}(\bar{y}_{i})\mid i\in[n]\}
of atoms occurring on the right-hand side of
:-
. Moreover, for a database
D
and a homomorphism
h
from
Q
to
D
, we write
h(Q)
for the set
\{R_{i}(h(\bar{y}_{i}))\mid i\in[n]\}
.
Clearly, if there is no homomorphism
h
from
Q
to
D
with
h(Q)\models\Sigma
and
h(\bar{x})=\bar{c}
, then
\mathsf{P}_{M_{\Sigma}^{\mathsf{uo}},Q}(D,\bar{c})=0
.
Assume now that such a homomorphism
h
exists. We first prove the claim for the case where
|h(Q)|=1
, and then generalize it to the case where
|h(Q)|=m
for some
m\in[|Q|]
.

The Case
|h(Q)|=1

Let
f
be the single fact of
h(Q)
, and

\mathsf{P}_{D,M_{\Sigma}^{\mathsf{uo}},Q}(h)\ =\ \sum\limits_{D^{\prime}\in\mathsf{ORep}(D,M_{\Sigma}^{\mathsf{uo}})\textrm{~and~}h(Q)\subseteq D^{\prime}}\mathsf{P}_{D,M_{\Sigma}^{\mathsf{uo}}}(D^{\prime}).

Note that since
h(\bar{x})=\bar{c}
, it holds that

\mathsf{P}_{M_{\Sigma}^{\mathsf{uo}},Q}(D,\bar{c})\ \geq\ \mathsf{P}_{D,M_{\Sigma}^{\mathsf{uo}},Q}(h).

Hence, it suffices to show that there is a polynomial
\mathsf{pol}
such that
\mathsf{P}_{D,M_{\Sigma}^{\mathsf{uo}},Q}(h)\geq\frac{1}{\mathsf{pol}(||D||)}
.
Let
S_{f}
and
S_{\neg f}
be the sets of sequences of
\mathsf{RL}(M_{\Sigma}^{\mathsf{uo}}(D))
that keep
f
and remove
f
, respectively, i.e.,

\displaystyle S_{f}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\{s\in\mathsf{RL}(M_{\Sigma}^{\mathsf{uo}}(D))\mid f\in s(D)\}

\displaystyle S_{\neg f}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\{s\in\mathsf{RL}(M_{\Sigma}^{\mathsf{uo}}(D))\mid f\not\in s(D)\}.

With
\pi
being the leaf distribution of
M_{\Sigma}^{\mathsf{uo}}(D)
,

\mathsf{P}_{D,M_{\Sigma}^{\mathsf{uo}},Q}(h)\ =\ \frac{\Lambda_{f}}{\Lambda_{f}+\Lambda_{\neg f}},

where

\Lambda_{f}\ =\ \sum_{s\in S_{f}}\pi(s)\qquad\text{and}\qquad\Lambda_{\neg f}\ =\ \sum_{s\in S_{\neg f}}\pi(s).

Therefore, to get the desired lower bound
\frac{1}{\mathsf{pol}(||D||)}
for
\mathsf{P}_{D,M_{\Sigma}^{\mathsf{uo}},Q}(h)
, it suffices to show that there exists a polynomial
\mathsf{pol}^{\prime}
such that
\Lambda_{\neg f}\leq\mathsf{pol}^{\prime}(||D||)\cdot\Lambda_{f}
. Indeed, in this case we can conclude that

\displaystyle\mathsf{P}_{D,M_{\Sigma}^{\mathsf{uo}},Q}(h)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\Lambda_{f}}{\Lambda_{f}+\Lambda_{\neg f}}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\frac{\Lambda_{f}}{\Lambda_{f}+\mathsf{pol}^{\prime}(||D||)\cdot\Lambda_{f}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{1+\mathsf{pol}^{\prime}(||D||)},

and the claim follows with
\mathsf{pol}(||D||)=1+\mathsf{pol}^{\prime}(||D||)
.

We proceed to show that a polynomial
\mathsf{pol}^{\prime}
such that
\Lambda_{\neg f}\leq\mathsf{pol}^{\prime}(||D||)\cdot\Lambda_{f}
exists. To this end, we establish an involved technical lemma that relates the sequences of
S_{\neg f}
with the sequences of
S_{f}
; as usual, we write
\pi
for the leaf distribution of
M_{\Sigma}^{\mathsf{uo}}(D)
:

Lemma D.1.

There exists a function
\mathsf{F}:S_{\neg f}\rightarrow S_{f}
such that:

(1)

There exists a polynomial
\mathsf{pol}^{\prime\prime}
such that, for every
s\in S_{\neg f}
,
\pi(s)\leq\mathsf{pol}^{\prime\prime}(||D||)\cdot\pi(\mathsf{F}(s))
.

(2)

For every
s^{\prime}\in S_{f}
,
|\{s\in S_{\neg f}\mid\mathsf{F}(s)=s^{\prime}\}|\leq 2\cdot||D||-1
.

Proof.

The bulk of the proof is devoted to showing item (1), whereas item (2) is shown via a simple combinatorial argument.

Item (1).

Let
s\in\mathsf{RL}(M_{\Sigma}^{\mathsf{uo}}(D))
be a repairing sequence that removes
f
, i.e.,
s\in S_{\neg f}
. We transform
s
into a repairing sequence
s^{\prime}\in\mathsf{RL}(M_{\Sigma}^{\mathsf{uo}}(D))
that does not remove
f
, i.e.,
s^{\prime}\in S_{f}
, by deleting or replacing the operation that removes
f
, and adding additional operations at the end of the sequence as follows. Assume that

s=\mathit{op}_{1},\,\,\,\,\mathit{op}_{2},\,\,\,\,\dots\,\,\,\,,\mathit{op}_{i-1},\,\,\,\,\mathit{op}_{i},\,\,\,\,\mathit{op}_{i+1},\,\,\,\,\dots\,\,\,\,,\mathit{op}_{n}

where
\mathit{op}_{i}=-f
. Then, we define the sequence

s^{\prime}=\mathit{op}_{1},\,\,\,\,\mathit{op}_{2},\,\,\,\,\dots\,\,\,\,,\mathit{op}_{i-1},\,\,\,\,\mathit{op}_{i+1},\,\,\,\,\dots\,\,\,\,,\mathit{op}_{n},\,\,\,\,\mathit{op}_{1}^{\prime},\,\,\,\,\dots\,\,\,\,,\mathit{op}_{\ell}^{\prime}

where
\mathit{op}_{1}^{\prime},\dots,\mathit{op}_{\ell}^{\prime}
are new operations that we will describe later.
If
\mathit{op}_{i}
is of the form
-\{f,g\}
, then

\displaystyle s^{\prime}=\mathit{op}_{1},\,\,\,\,\mathit{op}_{2},\,\,\,\,\dots\,\,\,\,,\mathit{op}_{i-1},\,\,\,\,\mathit{op}_{i}^{*},\,\,\,\,

\displaystyle\mathit{op}_{i+1},\,\,\,\,\dots\,\,\,\,,\mathit{op}_{n},

\displaystyle\,\,\,\,\mathit{op}_{1}^{\prime},\,\,\,\,\dots\,\,\,\,,\mathit{op}_{\ell}^{\prime}

where
\mathit{op}_{i}^{*}
is the operation
-g
, i.e., it removes only the fact
g
.

An important observation here is that since the sequence
s
removes
f
, the repair
s(D)
might contain facts that conflict with
f
, but at most
k
such facts, where
k
is the number of keys in
\Sigma
over the relation name of
f
. This is a property of keys. Indeed, if
s(D)
contains
k+1
facts that conflict with
f
, then it contains two facts
g_{1},g_{2}
that violate the same key with
f
, in which case
g_{1},g_{2}
also jointly violate this key and cannot appear in the same repair. Therefore, at the end of the sequence
s^{\prime}
we add
\ell
new operations (for some
\ell\leq k
) that remove the facts of
s(D)
that conflict with
f
, in some arbitrary order. Note that the sequence
s^{\prime}
is a valid repairing sequence, as an additional fact (the fact
f
) cannot invalidate a justified repairing operation, and we can remove the
\ell
conflicting facts at the end in any order, as they are all in conflict with
f
.
Here is a simple example illustrating the construction of
s^{\prime}
:

Example D.2.

Consider again the database
D
depicted in Figure
2
, and the set
\Sigma=\{R:A_{1}\rightarrow A_{2},R:A_{2}\rightarrow A_{1}\}
of keys. Consider also the query
Q
and homomorphism
h
from Example
B.3
. Recall that
h(Q)=\{R(a_{1},b_{1})\}
. The following sequence is a sequence that removes the fact
R(a_{1},b_{1})
:

s_{1}=-f_{1,2},-f_{1,1},-f_{3,1}

Note that
s(D)
contains the facts
f_{1,3}
and
f_{2,1}
that conflict with
f_{1,1}
.
This sequence is mapped to the following sequence
s^{\prime}
:

s_{1}^{\prime}=-f_{1,2},-f_{3,1},-f_{1,3},-f_{2,1}

where we delete the operation
-f_{1,1}
that removes the fact of
h(Q)
, and add, at the end of the sequence, the operations
-f_{1,3}
and
-f_{2,1}
that remove the facts of
s(D)
that conflict with
f_{1,1}
.

As another example, the sequence:

s_{2}=-f_{3,1},-\{f_{1,1},f_{1,2}\}

is mapped to the sequence:

s_{2}^{\prime}=-f_{3,1},-f_{1,2},-f_{2,1},-f_{1,3}.

Here, the pair removal
-\{f_{1,1},f_{1,2}\}
is replaced by the singleton removal
-f_{1,2}
, and, at the end of the sequence, we again add two additional operations that remove (in some arbitrary order) the facts
f_{1,3}
and
f_{2,1}
that conflict with
f_{1,1}
.

Now, according to the definition of
M_{\Sigma}^{\mathsf{uo}}
, we have that

\pi(s)\ =\ \frac{1}{N_{1}}\times\frac{1}{N_{2}}\times\dots\times\frac{1}{N_{i-1}}\times\frac{1}{N_{i}}\times\frac{1}{N_{i+1}}\times\dots\times\frac{1}{N_{n}}

where
N_{j}
is the total number of
(D^{s}_{j-1},\Sigma)
-justified repairing operations before applying the operation
\mathit{op}_{j}
of the sequence (recall that
D^{s}_{j-1}
is the database obtained from
D
by applying the first
j-1
operations of
s
). Hence,

\mathbf{P}((\mathit{op}_{1},\dots,\mathit{op}_{j-1}),(\mathit{op}_{1},\dots,\mathit{op}_{j}))\ =\ \frac{1}{N_{j}}.

Then,

\displaystyle\pi(s^{\prime})=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N_{1}}\times\frac{1}{N_{2}}\times\dots\times\frac{1}{N_{i-1}}\times\left[\frac{1}{N_{i}}\right]\times\frac{1}{N^{\prime}_{i+1}}\times\dots\times\frac{1}{N^{\prime}_{n}}\times

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\ell+1}\times\frac{1}{2(\ell-1)+1}\times\frac{1}{3}.

The probability
\mathbf{P}((\mathit{op}_{1},\dots,\mathit{op}_{j-1}),(\mathit{op}_{1},\dots,\mathit{op}_{j}))
, for
2\leq j\leq i-1
, is not affected by the decision to remove or keep
f
at the
i
th step. The probability
\mathbf{P}((\mathit{op}_{1},\dots,\mathit{op}_{j-1}),(\mathit{op}_{1},\dots,\mathit{op}_{j}))
for
i+2\leq j\leq n
, on the other hand, might decrease in the sequence
s^{\prime}
compared to the sequence
s
, because the additional fact
f
(that is removed by
s
but not by
s^{\prime}
) might be involved in violations with the remaining facts of the database and introduce additional justified operations, in which case
N_{j}\leq N_{j}^{\prime}
. Similarly, the probability
\mathbf{P}((\mathit{op}_{1},\dots,\mathit{op}_{i-1}),(\mathit{op}_{1},\dots,\mathit{op}_{i-1},\mathit{op}_{i+1}))
(in the case where
\mathit{op}_{i}=-f
) or
\mathbf{P}((\mathit{op}_{1},\dots,\mathit{op}_{i}^{\star}),(\mathit{op}_{1},\dots,\mathit{op}_{i}^{\star},\mathit{op}_{i+1}))
(in the case where
\mathit{op}_{i}=-\{f,g\}
) can only decrease compared to the probability
\mathbf{P}((\mathit{op}_{1},\dots,\mathit{op}_{i}),(\mathit{op}_{1},\dots,\mathit{op}_{i},\mathit{op}_{i+1}))
in
s
; hence,
N_{i+1}\leq N_{i+1}^{\prime}
.

The term
\frac{1}{N_{i}}
denotes the probability of
\mathit{op}^{\star}_{i}
, and it only appears in the expression if the sequence
s
removes the fact
f
jointly with some other fact
g
(and the operation
\mathit{op}^{\star}_{i}
removes
g
by itself). Since all the
(D_{i-1}^{s^{\prime}},\Sigma)
-justified operations have the same probability to be selected, the probabilities
\mathbf{P}((\mathit{op}_{1},\dots,\mathit{op}_{i-1}),(\mathit{op}_{1},\dots,\mathit{op}_{i}))
and
\mathbf{P}((\mathit{op}_{1},\dots,\mathit{op}_{i-1}),(\mathit{op}_{1},\dots,\mathit{op}_{i}^{\star})
are the same.
Finally, at the end of the sequence, the only remaining conflicts are those involving
f
. As said above, there are
\ell
facts that conflict with
f
for some
\ell\leq k
at that point, and each one of them violates a different key with
f
. Hence, there are
2\ell+1
justified operations before applying
\mathit{op}_{1}^{\prime}
(removing one of the
\ell
conflicting facts, removing one of these facts jointly with
f
, or removing
f
), there are
2(\ell-1)+1
possible operations before applying
\mathit{op}_{2}^{\prime}
and so on.

Example D.3.

We continue with Example
D.2
. For the sequence
s_{1}
, we have that

\displaystyle\pi(s_{1})

\displaystyle=\mathbf{P}(\varepsilon,(-f_{1,2}))\times\mathbf{P}((-f_{1,2}),(-f_{1,2},-f_{1,1}))

\displaystyle\times\mathbf{P}((-f_{1,2},-f_{1,1}),(-f_{1,2},-f_{1,1},-f_{3,1}))=\frac{1}{14}\times\frac{1}{10}\times\frac{1}{5}.

This holds since, at first, all six facts are involved in violations of the keys, and there are eight conflicting pairs; hence, the total number of justified operations is
14
. After removing the fact
f_{1,2}
, the number of justified operations reduces to
10
, and after removing the fact
f_{1,1}
, this number is
5
.
Now, for the sequence
s_{1}^{\prime}
,

\displaystyle\pi(s_{1}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\mathbf{P}(\varepsilon,(-f_{1,2}))\times\mathbf{P}((-f_{1,2}),(-f_{1,2},-f_{3,1}))

\displaystyle\times\mathbf{P}((-f_{1,2},-f_{3,1}),(-f_{1,2},-f_{3,1},-f_{1,3}))

\displaystyle\times\mathbf{P}((-f_{1,2},-f_{3,1},-f_{1,3}),(-f_{1,2},-f_{3,1},-f_{1,3},-f_{2,1}))

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{14}\times\frac{1}{10}\times\frac{1}{5}\times\frac{1}{3}.

Indeed, the probability of applying
-f_{1,2}
(i.e.,
\mathbf{P}(\varepsilon,(-f_{1,2}))
) is the same for both sequences (
\frac{1}{14}
), while the probability of applying the operation
-f_{3,1}
in
s_{1}^{\prime}
(i.e.,
\mathbf{P}((-f_{1,2}),(-f_{1,2},-f_{3,1}))
) is smaller than the probability (
\mathbf{P}((-f_{1,2},-f_{1,1}),(-f_{1,2},-f_{1,1},-f_{3,1}))
) of applying this operation in
s_{1}
:
\frac{1}{10}
compared to
\frac{1}{5}
. Finally, there are
\ell=2
facts in
s(D)
that conflict with
f_{1,1}
and we have that

\mathbf{P}((-f_{1,2},-f_{3,1}),(-f_{1,2},-f_{3,1},-f_{1,3}))=\frac{1}{2\times 2+1}=\frac{1}{5}

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}((-f_{1,2},-f_{3,1},-f_{1,3}),(-f_{1,2},-f_{3,1},-f_{1,3},-f_{2,1}))

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\times(2-1)+1}=\frac{1}{3}.

As for the sequence
s_{2}
, it holds that

\displaystyle\pi(s_{2})

\displaystyle=\mathbf{P}(\varepsilon,(-f_{3,1}))\times\mathbf{P}((-f_{3,1}),(-f_{3,1},-\{f_{1,1},f_{1,2}\}))=\frac{1}{14}\times\frac{1}{10}

while for the sequence
s_{2}^{\prime}
, it holds that

\displaystyle\pi(s_{2}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\mathbf{P}(\varepsilon,(-f_{3,1}))\times\mathbf{P}((-f_{3,1}),(-f_{3,1},-f_{1,2}))

\displaystyle\times\mathbf{P}((-f_{3,1},-f_{1,2}),(-f_{3,1},-f_{1,2},-f_{2,1}))

\displaystyle\times\mathbf{P}((-f_{3,1},-f_{1,2},-f_{2,1}),(-f_{3,1},-f_{1,2},-f_{2,1},-f_{1,3}))

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{14}\times\frac{1}{10}\times\frac{1}{5}\times\frac{1}{3}.

Again, the probability of applying the operation
-f_{3,1}
is the same in
s_{2}
and
s_{2}^{\prime}
. The probability of applying
-\{f_{1,1},f_{1,2}\}
in
s_{2}
is the same as the probability of applying the operation
-f_{1,2}
in
s_{2}^{\prime}
, and the probability of the two additional operations is again
\frac{1}{5}\times\frac{1}{3}
.

For every
j\in\{i+1,\dots,n\}
, we denote by
r_{j}
the difference between
N_{j}
and
N_{j}^{\prime}
(that is,
N_{j}^{\prime}=N_{j}+r_{j}
). Hence, it holds that

\displaystyle\pi(s)

\displaystyle=\pi(s^{\prime})\times\left[\frac{1}{N_{i}}\right]\times\frac{1}{N_{i+1}}\times\dots\times\frac{1}{N_{n}}\times(N_{i+1}+r_{i+1})\times\dots\times

\displaystyle\times(N_{n}+r_{n})\times(2\ell+1)\times\dots\times 3

\displaystyle\leq\pi(s^{\prime})\times\frac{1}{N_{i+1}}\times\dots\times\frac{1}{N_{n}}\times(N_{i+1}+r_{i+1})\times\dots\times

\displaystyle\times(N_{n}+r_{n})\times(2\ell+1)\times\dots\times 3

Note that here, the term
\frac{1}{N_{i}}
only appears if the original sequence
s
removes
f
alone, in which case the term
\frac{1}{N_{i}}
does not appear in the expression for
\pi(s^{\prime})
.
We will show that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N_{i+1}}\times\dots\times\frac{1}{N_{n}}

\displaystyle\times(N_{i+1}+r_{i+1})\times\dots\times(N_{n}+r_{n})

\displaystyle\times(2\ell+1)\times\dots\times 3\leq\mathsf{pol}^{\prime\prime}(||D||)

for some polynomial
\mathsf{pol}^{\prime\prime}
, or, equivalently,

\displaystyle(N_{i+1}+r_{i+1})\times\dots\times(N_{n}+r_{n})

\displaystyle\times(2\ell+1)\times\dots\times 3

\displaystyle\leq\mathsf{pol}^{\prime\prime}(||D||)\times N_{i+1}\times\dots\times N_{n}.

Note that since
\ell\leq k
, and
k
is a constant (since we are interested in data complexity),
(2\ell+1)\times\dots\times 3
is bounded by a constant. From this point, we denote this value by
c
. Thus, we prove that

(N_{i+1}+r_{i+1})\times\dots\times(N_{n}+r_{n})\times c\leq\mathsf{pol}^{\prime\prime}(||D||)\times N_{i+1}\times\dots\times N_{n}.

To show the above, we need to reason about
the values
r_{j}
. For
j\in\{i+1,\dots,n\}
, let
N_{j}^{f}
be the number of facts in the database that conflict with
f
after applying all the operations of
s^{\prime}
that occur before
\mathit{op}_{j}
, and before applying the operation
\mathit{op}_{j}
. Moreover, for every
p\in\{1,\dots,k\}
, let
n_{j}^{p}
be the number of facts in the database that violate the
p
th key jointly with
f
at that point. Note that
n_{j}^{1}+\dots+n_{j}^{p}\geq N_{j}^{f}
, as the same fact might violate several distinct keys jointly with
f
. If
n_{j}^{p}\geq 2
, then every fact that violates the
p
th key jointly with
f
participates in a violation of the constraints even if
f
is not present in the database (as all the facts that violate the same key with
f
also violate this key among themselves). Hence, for each one of these
n_{j}^{p}
facts, the operation that removes this fact is a justified repairing operation regardless of the presence or absence of
f
in the database, and it is counted as one of the
N_{j}
operations that can be applied at that point in the sequence
s
. The addition of
f
then adds
n_{j}^{p}
new justified operations (the removal of a pair of facts that includes
f
and one of the
n_{j}^{p}
conflicting facts).

On the other hand, if
n_{j}^{p}=1
, then the single fact that violates the
p
th key jointly with
f
at that point might not participate in any violation once we remove
f
. In this case, the presence of
f
implies two additional justified operations in
s^{\prime}
compared to
s
—the removal of this fact by itself and a pair removal that includes
f
and this fact. If
n_{j}^{p}=0
, then clearly the
p
th key has no impact on the number of justified repairing operations w.r.t.
f
at that point. Now, assume, without loss of generality, that for some
1\leq p_{1}<p_{2}\leq k
, it holds that
n_{j}^{p}\geq 2
for all
p\leq p_{1}
,
n_{j}^{p}=1
for all
p_{1}<p\leq p_{2}
, and
n_{j}^{p}=0
for all
p>p_{2}
. It then holds that

r_{j}\leq N_{j}^{f}+(p_{2}-p_{1})+1

(
N_{j}^{f}
operations remove
f
jointly with one of its conflicting facts, at most
p_{2}-p_{1}
operations remove a fact that violates the
p
th key with
f
if
n_{j}^{p}=1
, and one operation removes
f
itself.) Moreover,

\displaystyle N_{j}

\displaystyle\geq n_{j}^{1}+\dots+n_{j}^{p_{1}}+\frac{n_{j}^{1}(n_{j}^{1}-1)}{2}+\dots+\frac{n_{j}^{p_{1}}(n_{j}^{p_{1}}-1)}{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{(n_{j}^{1})^{2}+\dots+(n_{j}^{p_{1}})^{2}+n_{j}^{1}+\dots+n_{j}^{p_{1}}}{2}.

Because, as already said, for every
p
with
n_{j}^{p}\geq 2
, the
n_{j}^{p}
operations that remove the facts that violate the
p
th key with
f
are also justified operations at the
j
th step in
s
, and there are
\frac{n_{j}^{p}(n_{j}^{p}-1)}{2}
additional justified operations that remove a pair from these
n_{j}^{p}
facts, as each such pair of facts jointly violates the
p
th key.

Example D.4.

We continue with Example
D.3
. Let

s_{3}=-f_{3,1},-f_{1,1},-f_{1,2}

Before applying the operation
-f_{1,2}
of
s_{3}
, there are five justified operations:

-f_{1,2}\,\,\,\,-f_{1,3}\,\,\,\,-f_{3,2}\,\,\,\,-\{f_{1,2},f_{1,3}\}\,\,\,\,-\{f_{1,2},f_{3,2}\}

At this point, the database contains three facts that conflict with
f_{1,1}
. The facts
f_{1,2}
and
f_{1,3}
jointly violate with it the key
R:A_{1}\rightarrow A_{2}
, while the fact
f_{2,1}
jointly violates with it the key
R:A_{2}\rightarrow A_{1}
.

Observe that the operations
-f_{1,2},-f_{1,3},-\{f_{1,2},f_{1,3}\}
are justified operations at this point, even though the fact
f_{1,1}
no longer appears in the database, because
f_{1,2}
conflict with
f_{1,3}
. If we bring
f_{1,1}
back, we will have two additional justified operations that involve these fact (one for each fact):
-\{f_{1,1},f_{1,2}\}
and
-\{f_{1,1},f_{1,3}\}
.

Contrarily, the fact
f_{2,1}
is not involved in any violation of the constraints at this point (before applying the operation
-f_{1,2}
of
s_{3}
); hence, removing this fact is not a justified operation. However, if we bring
f_{1,1}

that

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}((-f_{3,1}),(-f_{3,1},-f_{1,2}))=\frac{1}{5+5}=\frac{1}{10}

According to the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality for
n
-dimensional euclidean spaces, it holds that

\left(\sum_{i=1}^{v}x_{i}y_{i}\right)^{2}\leq\left(\sum_{i=1}^{v}x_{i}^{2}\right)\times\left(\sum_{i=1}^{v}y_{i}^{2}\right),

where
v\geq 1
is an integer, and
x_{i},y_{i}
for

are real numbers.
By defining
y_{i}=1
for every

, we then obtain that

\displaystyle\left(x_{1}+\dots+x_{v}\right)^{2}\leq v\times\left(x_{1}^{2}+\dots+x_{v}^{2}\right).

Hence, we have that

\displaystyle N_{j}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{(n_{j}^{1})^{2}+\dots+(n_{j}^{p_{1}})^{2}+n_{j}^{1}+\dots+n_{j}^{p_{1}}}{2}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\frac{(n_{j}^{1}+\dots+n_{j}^{p_{1}})^{2}}{p_{1}}+n_{j}^{1}+\dots+n_{j}^{p_{1}}}{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{(n_{j}^{1}+\dots+n_{j}^{p_{1}})^{2}+p_{1}\times(n_{j}^{1}+\dots+n_{j}^{p_{1}})}{2p_{1}}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{(N_{j}^{f}-(p_{2}-p_{1}))^{2}+p_{1}\times[N_{j}^{f}-(p_{2}-p_{1})]}{2p_{1}}.

Note that
N_{j}^{f}-(p_{2}-p_{1})
is a lower bound on

because for every

, and for

p

and

n_{j}^{1}+\dots+n_{j}^{k}\geq N_{j}^{f}
. Therefore,

\displaystyle n_{j}^{1}+\dots+n_{j}^{p_{1}}

\displaystyle\geq N_{j}^{f}-(n_{j}^{p_{1}+1}+\dots+n_{j}^{p_{2}})-(n_{j}^{p_{2}+1}+\dots+n_{j}^{k})

\displaystyle\geq N_{j}^{f}-(p_{2}-p_{1}).

We conclude that

r_{j}\leq N_{j}^{f}+(p_{2}-p_{1})+1

N_{j}\geq\frac{(N_{j}^{f}-(p_{2}-p_{1}))^{2}+p_{1}\times[N_{j}^{f}-(p_{2}-p_{1})]}{2p_{1}}.

Hence, it holds that

N_{j}\geq\frac{(r_{j}-2(p_{2}-p_{1})-1)^{2}+p_{1}\times[r_{j}-2(p_{2}-p_{1})-1]}{2p_{1}}.

If

p_{1}\times[r_{j}-2(p_{2}-p_{1})-1]\geq 0

N_{j}\geq\frac{(r_{j}-2(p_{2}-p_{1})-1)^{2}}{2p_{1}}

\displaystyle r_{j}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{2p_{1}N_{j}}+2(p_{2}-p_{1})+1\leq\sqrt{2kN_{j}}+2k+k

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{4k^{2}N_{j}}+3k\sqrt{N_{j}}=5k\sqrt{N_{j}}.

If
r_{j}<2(p_{2}-p_{1})+1

r_{j}\leq 5k\sqrt{N_{j}}

Recall that our goal is to show that

(N_{i+1}+r_{i+1})\times\dots\times(N_{n}+r_{n})\times c\leq\mathsf{pol}^{\prime\prime}(|D|)\times N_{i+1}\times\dots\times N_{n}.

We have that

(N_{i+1}+r_{i+1})\times\dots\times(N_{n}+r_{n})\leq(N_{i+1}+5k\sqrt{N_{i+1}})\times\dots\times(N_{n}+5k\sqrt{N_{n}}).

Thus, it suffices to show that

(\sqrt{N_{i+1}}+5k)\times\dots\times(\sqrt{N_{n}}+5k)\times c\leq\mathsf{pol}^{\prime\prime}(||D||)\times\sqrt{N_{i+1}}\times\dots\times\sqrt{N_{n}}.

For brevity, let
x_{j}=\sqrt{N_{j}}
. Moreover, we can clearly define
\mathsf{pol}^{\prime\prime}(||D||)
as
c\times\mathsf{pol}^{\prime\prime\prime}(||D||)
for some polynomial
\mathsf{pol}^{\prime\prime\prime}
, and get rid of the constant

(x_{i+1}+5k)\times\dots\times(x_{n}+5k)\leq\mathsf{pol}^{\prime\prime\prime}(||D||)\times x_{i+1}\times\dots\times x_{n}

for some polynomial
\mathsf{pol}^{\prime\prime\prime}
,
or, equivalently,

\frac{x_{i+1}+5k}{x_{i+1}}\times\dots\times\frac{x_{n}+5k}{x_{n}}\leq\mathsf{pol}^{\prime\prime\prime}(||D||).

Note that in the sequence
s
, there are
n-j+1
operations after the operation
\mathit{op}_{j}
(including the operation
\mathit{op}_{j}
). Since the number of justified operations can only decrease after applying a certain operation, this means that

. Hence, we have that

\frac{x+5k}{x}

\frac{x+5k}{x}=1+\frac{5k}{x}

\displaystyle\frac{x_{i+1}+5k}{x_{i+1}}

\displaystyle\times\dots\times\frac{x_{n}+5k}{x_{n}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\sqrt{n-i}+5k}{\sqrt{n-i}}\times\frac{\sqrt{n-i-1}+5k}{\sqrt{n-i-1}}\times\dots\times\frac{1+5k}{1}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\left\lfloor\sqrt{n-i}\right\rfloor+5k}{\left\lfloor\sqrt{n-i}\right\rfloor}\times\frac{\left\lfloor\sqrt{n-i-1}\right\rfloor+5k}{\left\lfloor\sqrt{n-i-1}\right\rfloor}\times\dots\times\frac{1+5k}{1}

Next, for every
m\geq 1

\sqrt{m-1}\geq\sqrt{m}-1

and thus,

\left\lfloor\sqrt{m-1}\right\rfloor\geq\left\lfloor\sqrt{m}\right\rfloor-1

We then obtain the following:

\displaystyle\frac{\left\lfloor\sqrt{n-i}\right\rfloor+5k}{\left\lfloor\sqrt{n-i}\right\rfloor}\times\frac{\left\lfloor\sqrt{n-i-1}\right\rfloor+5k}{\left\lfloor\sqrt{n-i-1}\right\rfloor}\times\dots\times\frac{1+5k}{1}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\left\lfloor\sqrt{n-i}\right\rfloor+5k}{\left\lfloor\sqrt{n-i}\right\rfloor}\times\frac{\left\lfloor\sqrt{n-i}\right\rfloor-1+5k}{\left\lfloor\sqrt{n-i}\right\rfloor-1}\times\dots\times\frac{1+5k}{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{(\left\lfloor\sqrt{n-i}\right\rfloor+5k)!}{(\left\lfloor\sqrt{n-i}\right\rfloor)!\times(5k)!}={\left\lfloor\sqrt{{n-i}}\right\rfloor+5k\choose 5k}

\displaystyle\leq\left(\frac{e(\left\lfloor\sqrt{{n-i}}\right\rfloor+5k)}{5k}\right)^{5k}\leq\left(\frac{e(\left\lfloor\sqrt{{n}}\right\rfloor+5k)}{5k}\right)^{5k}

\displaystyle\leq\left(\frac{e}{5k}\right)^{5k}\times(\sqrt{|D|}+5k)^{5k}

(Observe that the maximal length
n
of a sequence is
|D|-1
.)
The claim follows with

\mathsf{pol}^{\prime\prime\prime}(||D||)\ =\ \left(\frac{e}{5k}\right)^{5k}\times(\sqrt{||D||}+5k)^{5k}.

Recall that
c=(2\ell+1)\times\dots\times 3
, where
\ell
is the number of facts that conflict with

. Therefore, for every sequence
s
that removes

that does not remove

\pi(s)\ \leq\ (2k+1)!\times\mathsf{pol}^{\prime\prime\prime}(||D||)\times\pi(s^{\prime}),

and item (1) of Lemma

follows with

\mathsf{pol}^{\prime\prime}(||D||)\ =\ (2k+1)!\times\mathsf{pol}^{\prime\prime\prime}(||D||).

Item (2).

We now show that the function
\mathsf{F}
from sequences that remove

to sequences that do not remove
f
, maps at most
2|D|-1
sequences of the first type to the same sequence of the second type.
Given a sequence

, we can obtain this sequence either from a sequence
s\in\mathsf{RL}(M_{\Sigma}^{\mathsf{uo}}(D))
that has one additional operation that removes

, or from a sequence
s
that removes

jointly with some other fact
g
, while

removes the fact
g
by itself. (Some of the operations at the end of
s^{\prime}
might not appear in

, as they remove facts that conflict only with

.) Since the length of the sequence
s^{\prime}
is at most
|D|-1
, there are at most
|D|
possible ways to insert an additional operation that removes
f
, and
|D|-1
ways to add
f
to an existing operation. Hence, there are at most
|D|+|D|-1
sequences that remove
f
that are mapped to the sequence
s^{\prime}
.
Here is an example that illustrates the above combinatorial argument.

Example D.5.

We continue with Example
D.4
. Consider again the sequence

. Recall that

s_{3}^{\prime}=-f_{3,1},-f_{1,2},-f_{2,1},-f_{1,3}

This sequence can be obtained from any of the following sequences that have an additional operation that removes

-f_{1,1},-f_{3,1},-f_{1,2}

-f_{3,1},-f_{1,1},-f_{1,2}

Note that the operations

do not appear in these sequences, as after removing

The sequence
s_{3}^{\prime}
can also be obtained from the following sequences that replace an operation of

that removes a single fact with an operation that removes a pair of conflicting facts:

\displaystyle-\{f_{1,1},f_{3,1}\},-f_{1,2}

\displaystyle-f_{3,1},-\{f_{1,1},f_{1,2}\}

.

Having Lemma

\mathsf{pol}^{\prime}(||D||)=\mathsf{pol}^{\prime\prime}(||D||)\cdot(2\cdot||D||-1)
.

The Case
|h(Q)|\geq 1

We now generalize the proof given above for the case
|h(Q)|=1
to the case
|h(Q)|=m
for some
1\leq m\leq|Q|
.
As in the case where
|h(Q)|=1
, we map sequences that remove at least one of the facts of
h(Q)
to sequences that keep all these facts, by deleting or replacing every operation that removes a fact of
h(Q)
and adding a constant number of operations at the end of the sequence that remove all the facts that conflict with some fact of
h(Q)
.

More formally, let
s\in\mathsf{RL}(M_{\Sigma}^{\mathsf{uo}}(D))
be a repairing sequence that removes
r
of the facts of
h(Q)
(for some
1\leq r\leq m

s=\mathit{op}_{1},\,\,\,\,\dots\,\,\,\,,\mathit{op}_{i_{1}},\,\,\,\,\dots\,\,\,\,,\mathit{op}_{i_{2}},\,\,\,\,\dots\,\,\,\,,\mathit{op}_{i_{r}},\,\,\,\,\dots,\mathit{op}_{n}

where the operations
\mathit{op}_{i_{1}},\dots,\mathit{op}_{i_{r}}
remove these
r
facts. Note that there are no conflicts among the facts of
h(Q)
; hence, it cannot be the case that a single operation removes two of these facts. We transform
s
into a sequence
s^{\prime}\in\mathsf{RL}(M_{\Sigma}^{\mathsf{uo}}(D))
where each operation
\mathit{op}_{i_{j}}
that removes a single fact is deleted, and every operation
\mathit{op}_{i_{j}}
that removes a pair
\{f,g\}
of facts where
f\in h(Q)
and
g\not\in h(Q)
, is replaced by the operation
o^{\star}_{i_{j}}
that removes only the fact
g
. At the end of the sequence
s^{\prime}
, we add operations
\mathit{op}_{1}^{\prime},\dots,\mathit{op}_{\ell}^{\prime}
that remove the facts that are in conflict with one of the facts of
h(Q)
that appears in
s(D)
. As we have explained before, for each such fact, the sequence
s
keeps at most
k
conflicting facts, where
k
is the maximal number of keys in
\Sigma
over the same relation
R
; hence, the total number of conflicting facts that
s
does not remove is bounded by
m\times k
, and this is a bound on the number
\ell
of additional operations (that remove these conflicting facts one by one in some arbitrary order). As in the case where
h(Q)=1
, the probability of applying the additional
\ell
operations at the end of the sequence is some constant that we denote by
\frac{1}{c}
. We provide below more details about this constant.

The probability
\mathbf{P}((\mathit{op}_{1},\dots,\mathit{op}_{j-1}),(\mathit{op}_{1},\dots,\mathit{op}_{j}))
, for
2\leq j\leq i_{1}-1
, is not affected by the decision to remove or keep a certain fact at the
i_{1}
th step. However, for
j\geq i_{1}
, the probability of applying the operation
\mathit{op}_{j}
might decrease in the sequence
s^{\prime}
compared to the sequence
s
, because the additional facts of
h(Q)
(that are removed by
s
but not by
s^{\prime}
) might be involved in violations with the remaining facts of the database and introduce additional justified repairing operations at each step. As we have already shown, if the number of
(D^{s}_{j-1},\Sigma)
-justified operations before applying the operation
\mathit{op}_{j}
of
s
is
N_{j}
, then the addition of a fact can increase this number by at most
5k\sqrt{N_{j}}
. Hence, the addition of at most
m
facts (the facts of
h(Q)
) can increase this number by at most
5km\sqrt{N_{j}}
. We again denote by
r_{j}
the factor by which the number of operations increases, and we have that
r_{j}\leq 5km\sqrt{N_{j}}
.

Now, all the arguments for the case where
|h(Q)|=1
apply also in this case, with the only difference being the value of
r_{j}
. Therefore, we conclude that

\pi(s)\ \leq\ \mathsf{pol}^{\prime\prime}(||D||)\times\pi(s^{\prime})

with

\mathsf{pol}^{\prime\prime}(||D||)\ =\ c\times\left(\frac{e}{5km}\right)^{5km}\times(\sqrt{||D||}+5km)^{5km}.

Recall that
\frac{1}{c}
is the probability of applying the additional operations at the end of the sequence, and
r
is the number of facts of
h(Q)
that are removed by the sequence
s
. We would like to provide a lower bound on this probability (hence, an upper bound on
c
).
Clearly, the lowest probability is obtained when the number of additional operations is the highest (as for each additional operation we need to multiply the probability by a number lower than one) and when the probability of each individual operation is the lowest.
As mentioned above, for each one of the
r
facts of
h(Q)
that are removed by
s
, there are at most
k
facts that conflict with it and are not removed by
s
. Hence,
r\times k
is an upper bound on the number of additional operations. Moreover, the lowest probability of each operation is obtained when the number of justified operations at the point of applying it is the highest. When there are
\ell
facts in a database
D^{\prime}
that are involved in violations of the constraints, an upper bound on the number of
(D^{\prime},\Sigma)
-justified operations (that is obtained when every fact is in conflict with every other fact) is

\ell+\frac{\ell(\ell-1)}{2}=\frac{\ell^{2}+\ell}{2}=\frac{\ell(\ell+1)}{2}\leq\frac{(\ell+1)^{2}}{2}\leq(\ell+1)^{2}.

Therefore, we have that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{c}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{(rk+r+1)^{2}}\times\frac{1}{(rk+r)^{2}}\times\frac{1}{(rk+r-1)^{2}}\times\dots\times\frac{1}{3}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{((rk+r+1)^{2})!}\geq\frac{1}{((mk+m+1)^{2})!}

and

c\ \leq\ ((mk+m+1)^{2})!

(Observe that
rk+r
is the number of facts involved in violations if each of the
r
facts of
h(Q)
that
s
removes conflicts with
k
facts of
s(D)
.)
Now, it holds that

\displaystyle((mk+m+1)^{2})!\times\left(\frac{e}{5km}\right)^{5km}\times(\sqrt{|D|}+5km)^{5km}\leq

\displaystyle((|Q||\Sigma|+|Q|+1)^{2})!\times e^{5|Q||\Sigma|}\times(\sqrt{|D|}+5|Q||\Sigma|)^{5|Q||\Sigma|}

Hence, with

\mathsf{pol}^{\prime\prime}(||D||)=((|Q||\Sigma|+|Q|+1)^{2})!\times e^{5|Q||\Sigma|}\times(\sqrt{||D||}+5|Q||\Sigma|)^{5|Q||\Sigma|}

we have thet

\pi(s)\ \leq\ \mathsf{pol}^{\prime\prime}(||D||)\times\pi(s^{\prime}),

as needed.

Finally, we show that our mapping from sequences that remove at least one of the facts of
h(Q)
to sequences that do not remove any of these facts maps at most polynomially many sequences of the first type to the same sequence of the second type. Given a sequence
s^{\prime}
that does not remove any of the facts of
h(Q)
, we can obtain this sequence from any sequence
s
that has additional operations that remove some of the facts of
h(Q)
individually or operations that remove these facts jointly with another fact (while
s^{\prime}
removes only one of these facts). The sequence
s
can remove any number
1\leq r\leq m
of facts of
h(Q)
. And, in the case where it removes
r
of the facts of
h(Q)
, for every
\ell\leq r
there are
{r\choose{\ell}}
possible ways to choose a subset of size
\ell
of
h(Q)
of facts that will be removed by themselves
(while the remaining
r-\ell
facts will be removed jointly with another fact). Since the length of the sequence
s
is at most
|D|-1
, there are at most
{{|D|+\ell-1}\choose\ell}
possible choices for the positions of the additional singleton deletions, and
{{|D|-1}\choose{r-\ell}}
possible choices for the individual fact removals that will become pair removals. Hence, the number of sequences that remove a fact of
h(Q)
that are mapped to the sequence
s^{\prime}
is at most

\displaystyle\sum_{r=1}^{m}\sum_{\ell=0}^{r}{r\choose{\ell}}\times{{|D|+\ell-1}\choose\ell}\times{{|D|-1}\choose{r-\ell}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{r=1}^{m}\sum_{\ell=0}^{r}{\left(\frac{er}{\ell}\right)^{\ell}}\times\left(\frac{e(|D|+\ell-1)}{\ell}\right)^{\ell}\times\left(\frac{e(|D|-1)}{r-\ell}\right)^{r-\ell}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{r=1}^{|Q|}\sum_{\ell=0}^{|Q|}{\left(e|Q|\right)^{\ell}}\times\left(e(|D|+\ell-1)\right)^{\ell}\times\left(e(|D|-1)\right)^{|Q|-\ell}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle|Q|\times(|Q|+1)\times\left(e|Q|\right)^{|Q|}\times\left(e(|D|+|Q|-1)\right)^{|Q|}\times

\displaystyle\left(e(|D|-1)\right)^{|Q|}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle(e|Q|)^{2}\times\left(e|Q|\right)^{|Q|}\times\left(e(|D|+|Q|-1)\right)^{|Q|}\times\left(e(|D|-1)\right)^{|Q|}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(e|Q|\right)^{|Q|+2}\times\left(e(|D|+|Q|-1)\right)^{|Q|}\times\left(e(|D|-1)\right)^{|Q|}.

This number is clearly polynomial in
||D||
. We denote this number by
\mathsf{pol}^{\prime}(||D||)
.
Finally, similarly to the case where
|h(Q)|=1
,

\mathsf{P}_{D,M_{\Sigma}^{\mathsf{uo}},Q}(h)\ \geq\ \frac{1}{1+\mathsf{pol}^{\prime\prime}(||D||)\times\mathsf{pol}^{\prime}(||D||)}.

With
\mathsf{pol}(||D||)=1+\mathsf{pol}^{\prime\prime}(||D||)\times\mathsf{pol}^{\prime}(||D||)
, we obtain that

\mathsf{P}_{M_{\Sigma}^{\mathsf{uo}},Q}(D,\bar{c})\geq\mathsf{P}_{D,M_{\Sigma}^{\mathsf{uo}},Q}(h)\ \geq\ \frac{1}{\mathsf{pol}(||D||)},

which concludes our proof.

D.3.
The case of Functional Dependencies

Unlike the case of keys, in the case of FDs, there is no polynomial lower bound on the target probability, as we show next. This means that we cannot rely on Monte Carlo Sampling for devising an FPRAS. On the other hand, this does not preclude the existence of an FPRAS in the case of FDs, which remains an open problem.

Proposition D.6.

Consider the FD set
\{R:A_{1}\rightarrow A_{2}\}
over the schema
\{R/3\}
, and the Boolean CQ
\textrm{Ans}()\ \text{:-}\ R(0,0,0)
. There exists a family
\{D_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
of databases such that

0<\mathsf{P}_{M_{\Sigma}^{\mathsf{uo}},Q}(D_{n},())\leq\frac{1}{2^{|D_{n}|-1}}.

Proof.

Let
D_{n}
be the database that contains the fact
R(0,0,0)
and
n-1
additional facts
R(0,1,i)
for
i\in\{1,\dots,n-1\}
. Observe that each fact
R(0,1,i)
is in conflict with
R(0,0,0)
, but there are no conflicts among two facts
R(0,1,i)
and
R(0,1,j)
for
i\neq j
. Clearly, it holds that
0<\mathsf{P}_{M_{\Sigma}^{\mathsf{uo}},Q}(D,())
as the operational repair that keeps the fact
R(0,0,0)
entails

.
We prove by induction on
n
, the number of facts in the database, that for a database
D
that contains the fact
R(0,0,0)
and
n-1
facts of the form
R(0,1,i)

\mathsf{P}_{M_{\Sigma}^{\mathsf{uo}},Q}(D,())\leq\frac{1}{2^{n-1}}

Base Case.

For

,

and there are no violations of the FD. In this case, it is rather straightforward to see that

\mathsf{P}_{M_{\Sigma}^{\mathsf{uo}},Q}(D,())\ =\ \frac{1}{2^{1-1}}\ =\ 1.

Inductive Step.

We assume that the claim holds for
n=1,\dots,p
and prove that it holds for

. Let
D
be such a database with
p+1
facts; that is,
D
contains the fact
R(0,0,0)
and
p
facts of the form
R(0,1,i)
. Whenever we have
p

R(0,1,i)
in the database, there are
1+2p
justified operations:
(1)
the removal of
R(0,0,0)
,
(2)
the removal of a fact of the form
R(0,1,i)
, or
(3)
the removal of a pair
\{R(0,0,0),R(0,1,i)\}

(2)
keep the fact
R(0,0,0)
in the database. We denote these operations by
\mathit{op}_{1},\dots,\mathit{op}_{p}
. For every
i\in\{1,\dots,p\}
, we have that

\mathbf{P}(\varepsilon,(op_{i}))=\frac{1}{1+2p}.

After removing a fact of the form
R(0,1,i)
from the database, we have
p-1
such facts left, regardless of which specific fact we remove. For every
i\in\{1,\dots,p\}
, we denote by
D_{i}
the database
\mathit{op}_{i}(D)
. By the inductive hypothesis, we have that

\mathsf{P}_{M_{\Sigma}^{\mathsf{uo}},Q}(D_{i},())\ \leq\ \frac{1}{2^{p-1}}.

Every sequence
s\in\mathsf{RL}(M_{\Sigma}^{\mathsf{uo}}(D))
with
R(0,0,0)\in s(D)
is of the form
\mathit{op}_{i}\cdot s_{i}
for some
i\in[p]
and
s_{i}\in\mathsf{RL}(M_{\Sigma}^{\mathsf{uo}}(D_{i}))
with
R(0,0,0)\in s_{i}(D)
. The probability

\mathsf{P}_{M_{\Sigma}^{\mathsf{uo}},Q}(D,())
can then be written as

\displaystyle\mathsf{P}_{M_{\Sigma}^{\mathsf{uo}},Q}(D,())

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}s\in\mathsf{RL}(M_{\Sigma}^{\mathsf{uo}}(D))\\
()\in Q(s(D))\end{subarray}}\pi(s)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{p}\left(\mathbf{P}(\varepsilon,(\mathit{op}_{i}))\times\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}s_{i}\in\mathsf{RL}(M_{\Sigma}^{\mathsf{uo}}(D_{i}))\\
()\in Q(s_{i}(D_{i}))\end{subarray}}\pi(s_{i})\right).

As said above, for every
i\in\{1,\dots,p\}
,

\mathsf{P}_{M_{\Sigma}^{\mathsf{uo}},Q}(D_{i},())\ =\ \sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}s_{i}\in\mathsf{RL}(M_{\Sigma}^{\mathsf{uo}}(D_{i}))\\
()\in Q(s_{i}(D_{i}))\end{subarray}}\pi(s_{i})\ \leq\ \frac{1}{2^{p-1}}.

Therefore, we conclude that

\displaystyle\mathsf{P}_{M_{\Sigma}^{\mathsf{uo}},Q}(D,())

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{p}\left(\frac{1}{1+2p}\times\frac{1}{2^{p-1}}\right)=\frac{p}{(1+2p)\times 2^{p-1}}

\displaystyle=\frac{p}{2^{p-1}+p\times 2^{p}}\leq\frac{p}{p\times 2^{p}}=\frac{1}{2^{p}}

and the claim follows.

D.4.
Proof of Theorem
7.5

We now show that if only singleton removals are allowed, then we can devise an FPRAS even for arbitrary FDs.
For a database
D
and a set
\Sigma
of FDs, we denote by
\mathsf{RS}^{1}(D,\Sigma)
the set of sequences in
\mathsf{RS}(D,\Sigma)
mentioning only operations of the form
-f
, i.e., removing a single fact.
Similarly, we denote
\mathsf{Ops}^{1}_{s}(D,\Sigma)=\{s^{\prime}\in\mathsf{RS}^{1}(D,\Sigma)\mid s^{\prime}=s\cdot\mathit{op}\text{ for some }D\text{-operation }\mathit{op}\}
.
Then, we define the Markov chain generator
M_{\Sigma}^{\mathsf{uo},1}
such that for every
s,s^{\prime}\in\mathsf{RS}^{1}(D,\Sigma)
, assuming that
M_{\Sigma}^{\mathsf{uo},1}(D)=(V,E,\mathbf{P})
, if
s^{\prime}\in\mathsf{Ops}^{1}_{s}(D,\Sigma)
then

\mathbf{P}(s,s^{\prime})\ =\ \frac{1}{|\mathsf{Ops}^{1}_{s}(D,\Sigma)|}.

Observe, however, that the Markov chain generator
M_{\Sigma}^{\mathsf{uo},1}
is defined over all the sequences of
\mathsf{RS}(D,\Sigma)
. If
s\in\mathsf{RS}^{1}(D,\Sigma)
but
s^{\prime}\in\mathsf{RS}(D,\Sigma)\setminus\mathsf{RS}^{1}(D,\Sigma)
(and
s^{\prime}\in\mathsf{Ops}_{s}(D,\Sigma)
), then we define
\mathbf{P}(s,s^{\prime})=0
. If
s\in\mathsf{RS}(D,\Sigma)\setminus\mathsf{RS}^{1}(D,\Sigma)
, none of the leaves of the subtree
T_{s}
is reachable with non-zero probability, and thus,
\mathbf{P}(s,s^{\prime})
, for any
s^{\prime}\in\mathsf{Ops}_{s}(D,\Sigma)
, can get an arbitrary probability (as long as the sum of probabilities equals one), e.g.,
\frac{1}{|\mathsf{Ops}_{s}(D,\Sigma)|}
.

We can now show that, assuming singleton removals, for FDs the problem of interest admits an FPRAS. The formal statement, already given in the main body of the paper, and its proof follow:

Theorem
7.5
.

For a set
\Sigma
of FDs, and a CQ
Q
,
\mathsf{OCQA}(\Sigma,M_{\Sigma}^{\mathsf{uo},1},Q)
admits an FPRAS.

The proof consists of the usual two steps: (1) existence of an efficient sampler, and (2) provide a polynomial lower bound on he target probability.

Step 1: Efficient Sampler

We can sample elements of
\mathsf{RL}(M_{\Sigma}^{\mathsf{uo},1}(D))
according to the leaf distribution of
M_{\Sigma}^{\mathsf{uo},1}(D)
in polynomial time in
||D||
. This is done by employing the same iterative algorithm as the one used to sample elements of
\mathsf{RL}(M_{\Sigma}^{\mathsf{uo}}(D))
, but with the difference that only justified operations that consist of singleton removals are considered. In particular, at each step, the algorithm extends the current sequence
s
by selecting one of the
(s(D),\Sigma)
-justified operations of the form
-f
with probability

\frac{1}{|\mathsf{Ops}^{1}_{s}(D,\Sigma)|}.

Hence, we immediately obtain the following result:

Lemma D.7.

Given a database
D
, and a set
\Sigma
of keys, we can sample elements of
\mathsf{RL}(M_{\Sigma}^{\mathsf{uo},1}(D))
according to the leaf distribution of
M_{\Sigma}^{\mathsf{uo},1}(D)
in polynomial time in
||D||
.

Step 2: Polynomial Lower Bound

It remains to show that there exists a polynomial lower bound on the target probability.

Lemma D.8.

Consider a set
\Sigma
of keys, and a CQ
Q(\bar{x})
. For every database
D
, and
\bar{c}\in\mathsf{dom}(D)^{|\bar{x}|}
,

\mathsf{P}_{M_{\Sigma}^{\mathsf{uo},1},Q}(D,\bar{c})\ \geq\ \frac{1}{\left(e\cdot||D||\right)^{||Q||}}

whenever
\mathsf{P}_{M_{\Sigma}^{\mathsf{uo},1},Q}(D,\bar{c})>0
.

Proof.

Consider a database
D
. If there is no homomorphism
h
from
Q
to
D
such that
h(Q)\models\Sigma
and
h(\bar{x})=\bar{c}
, then clearly
\mathsf{P}_{M_{\Sigma}^{\mathsf{uo},1},Q}(D,\bar{c})=0
.
We now focus on the case where such a homomorphism
h
exists. Assume that
|h(Q)|=m
for some
m\leq|Q|
.
We prove by induction on
n
, that is, the number of facts in
D\setminus h(Q)
that are involved in violations of the FDs (i.e., the facts
f\in(D\setminus h(Q))
such that
\{f,g\}\not\models\Sigma
for some
g\in D
), the following:

\mathsf{P}_{D,M_{\Sigma}^{\mathsf{uo},1},Q}(h)\ \geq\ \frac{1}{{{n+m}\choose m}}.

Base Case.

For
n=0
, since
h(Q)\models\Sigma
, there are no violations of the FDs in
D
, and
D
has a single operational repair, which is
D
itself. In this case, the probability of obtaining an operational repair that contains all the facts of
h(Q)
is
1=\frac{1}{{{0+m}\choose m}}
, as needed.

Inductive Step.

We now assume that the claim holds for databases where
n=0,\dots,k-1
, and we prove that it holds for databases
D
where
n=k
. Every repairing sequence
s\in\mathsf{RL}(M_{\Sigma}^{\mathsf{uo},1}(D))
for which
h(Q)\subseteq s(D)
is such that the first operation of
s
removes a fact of
D\setminus h(Q)
that is involved in violations of the FDs. Let
f_{1},\dots,f_{k}

be these facts of
D\setminus h(Q)
, and for each
i\in\{1,\dots,k\}
, let
\mathit{op}_{i}
be the operation that removes the fact
f_{i}
. We then have that

\mathbf{P}(\varepsilon,(op_{i}))\ \geq\ \frac{1}{k+m}.

This is because the probability of removing a certain fact is
\frac{1}{k+p}
, where
p
is the number of facts involved in violations among the facts of
h(Q)
. Since
p\leq m
, we get that
\frac{1}{k+m}\leq\frac{1}{k+p}
.

After removing a conflicting fact of
D\setminus h(Q)
from the database, we have at most
k-1
such facts left, regardless of which specific fact we remove. For every
i\in\{1,\dots,p\}
, we denote by
D_{i}
the database
\mathit{op}_{i}(D)
and by
n_{i}
the number of facts of
D_{i}\setminus h(Q)
that are involved in violations of the FDs; hence, we have that
n_{i}\leq k-1
. By the inductive hypothesis, we have that

\mathsf{P}_{D_{i},M_{\Sigma}^{\mathsf{uo},1},Q}(h)\ \geq\ \frac{1}{{{n_{i}+m}\choose m}}\ \geq\ \frac{1}{{{k-1+m}\choose m}}.

Clearly, every sequence
s\in\mathsf{RL}(M_{\Sigma}^{\mathsf{uo}}(D))
with
h(Q)\subseteq s(D)
is of the form
\mathit{op}_{i}\cdot s_{i}
for some
i\in\{1,\dots,k\}
and
s_{i}\in\mathsf{RL}(M_{\Sigma}^{\mathsf{uo}}(D_{i}))
with
h(Q)\subseteq s_{i}(D)
. Now, the following holds

\mathsf{P}_{D,M_{\Sigma}^{\mathsf{uo},1},Q}(h)\ =\ \sum_{i=1}^{k}\left(\mathbf{P}(\varepsilon,(\mathit{op}_{i}))\times\mathsf{P}_{D_{i},M_{\Sigma}^{\mathsf{uo},1},Q}(h)\right).

Therefore, we can conclude that

\displaystyle\mathsf{P}_{D,M_{\Sigma}^{\mathsf{uo},1},Q}(h)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{k}\left(\frac{1}{k+m}\times\frac{1}{{{k-1+m}\choose m}}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{k}{k+m}\times\frac{1}{{{k-1+m}\choose m}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{k}{k+m}\times\frac{1}{\frac{(k-1+m)!}{m!\times(k-1)!}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{m!\times k!}{(k+m)!}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{{{k+m}\choose m}}.

Finally, it is well known that
{n\choose k}\leq\left(\frac{en}{k}\right)^{k}
. We conclude that, for a database
D
such that
D\setminus h(Q)
contains
n
facts that are involved in violations of the FDs, we have that

\displaystyle\mathsf{P}_{D,M_{\Sigma}^{\mathsf{uo},1},Q}(h)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{{{n+m}\choose n}}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\left(\frac{e(n+m)}{m}\right)^{m}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{m^{m}}{e^{m}}\times\frac{1}{(n+m)^{m}}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\left(\frac{m}{e}\right)^{m}\times\frac{1}{|D|^{m}}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\left(\frac{1}{e}\right)^{|Q|}\times\frac{1}{|D|^{|Q|}}.

Since
h(Q)\subseteq D^{\prime}
implies
\bar{c}\in Q(D^{\prime})
, it holds that

\mathsf{P}_{M_{\Sigma}^{\mathsf{uo},1},Q}(D,\bar{c})\ \geq\ \mathsf{P}_{D,M_{\Sigma}^{\mathsf{uo},1},Q}(h)\ \geq\ \frac{1}{\left(e|D|\right)^{|Q|}}\ \geq\ \frac{1}{\left(e||D||\right)^{||Q||}},

which concludes our proof.

E.1.
Uniform Repairs

In this section, we prove the version of Theorem

Theorem E.1.

(1)

There exist a set
\Sigma

Q
such that
\mathsf{OCQA}(\Sigma,M_{\Sigma}^{\mathsf{ur},1},Q)
is
\sharp
P
-hard.

(2)

For a set
\Sigma
of primary keys, and a CQ
Q
,
\mathsf{OCQA}(\Sigma,M_{\Sigma}^{\mathsf{ur},1},Q)
admits an FPRAS.

(3)

Unless
{\rm RP}={\rm NP}
, there exist a set
\Sigma
of FDs, and a CQ
Q
such that there is no FPRAS for
\mathsf{OCQA}(\Sigma,M_{\Sigma}^{\mathsf{ur},1},Q)
.

As for Theorem
5.1
, we can conveniently restate the problem of interest as the problem of computing a “relative frequency” ratio.
Indeed,
for a database
D
, a set
\Sigma
of FDs, a CQ
Q(\bar{x})
, and a tuple
\bar{c}\in\mathsf{dom}(D)^{|\bar{x}|}
,

\mathsf{P}_{M_{\Sigma}^{\mathsf{ur},1},Q}(D,\bar{c})=\mathsf{rrfreq}^{1}_{\Sigma,Q}(D,\bar{c})
, where

\mathsf{rrfreq}^{1}_{\Sigma,Q}(D,\bar{c})\ =\ \frac{|\{D^{\prime}\in\mathsf{CORep}^{1}(D,\Sigma)\mid\bar{c}\in Q(D^{\prime})\}|}{|\mathsf{CORep}^{1}(D,\Sigma)|}.

Hence,
\mathsf{OCQA}(\Sigma,M_{\Sigma}^{\mathsf{ur},1},Q(\bar{x}))
coincides with the following problem, which is independent from the Markov chain generator
M_{\Sigma}^{\mathsf{ur},1}
:

PROBLEM
:

\mathsf{RRFreq}^{1}(\Sigma,Q(\bar{x}))

INPUT
:

A database
D
, and a tuple
\bar{c}\in\mathsf{dom}(D)^{|\bar{x}|}
.

OUTPUT
:

\mathsf{rrfreq}^{1}_{\Sigma,Q}(D,\bar{c})
.

We proceed to establish Theorem
E.1
by directly considering the problem
\mathsf{RRFreq}^{1}(\Sigma,Q)
instead of
\mathsf{OCQA}(\Sigma,M_{\Sigma}^{\mathsf{ur},1},Q)
.

Proof of Item (1) of Theorem
E.1

We provide a polynomial-time Turing reduction from the
\sharp
P-hard problem
\mathsf{\sharp Pos2DNF}

(
Scott Provan and
O. Ball 1983
)
.
A positive 2DNF formula is a Boolean formula of the form
\varphi=C_{1}\vee\cdots\vee C_{n}
, where each
C_{i}
is a conjunction of two variables occurring positively in
C_{i}
. Let
\mathsf{var}(\varphi)
be the set of Boolean variables occurring in
\varphi
. An assignment for
\varphi
is a function
\mu:\mathsf{var}(\varphi)\rightarrow\{0,1\}
. Such an assignment is satisfying for
\varphi
if
\mu(\varphi)=1
, i.e., the formula obtained after replacing each variable
x
of
\varphi
with
\mu(x)
, evaluates to
1
. We write
\mathsf{sat}(\varphi)
for the set of satisfying assignments for
\varphi
, i.e., the assignments for
\varphi
that evaluate
\varphi
to
1
. The problem in question is

PROBLEM
:

\mathsf{\sharp Pos2DNF}

INPUT
:

A positive 2DNF formula
\varphi
.

OUTPUT
:

The number
|\mathsf{sat}(\varphi)|
.

Consider the schema
\mathbf{S}=\{V/2,C/2,T/1\}
, and let
(A,B)
be the tuple of attributes of
V
.
We define the set
\Sigma
over
\mathbf{S}
consisting of

V:A\rightarrow B

and the (constant-free) Boolean CQ
Q
over
\mathbf{S}

\textrm{Ans}()\ \text{:-}\ C(x,y),V(x,z),V(y,z),T(z).

Our goal is to show that
\mathsf{RRFreq}^{1}(\Sigma,Q)
is
\sharp
P-hard via a polynomial-time Turing reduction from
\mathsf{\sharp Pos2DNF}
.
Given a positive 2DNF formula
\varphi=C_{1}\vee\cdots\vee C_{n}
, we define the database

D_{\varphi}\ =\ \{V(c_{x},0),V(c_{x},1)\mid x\in\mathsf{var}(\varphi)\}\ \cup\ \\
\underbrace{\{C(c_{x},c_{y})\mid C_{i}=(x\wedge y)\text{ for some }i\in[n]\}\ \cup\ \{T(1)\}}_{D_{c}},

where, for each
x\in\mathsf{var}(\varphi)
,
c_{x}
is a constant, which essentially encodes
\varphi
.
We now define the algorithm
\mathsf{SAT}
, which accepts as input a positive 2DNF formula
\varphi
, and performs the following steps:

(1)

Construct the database
D_{\varphi}
.

(2)

Compute the number
r=\mathsf{rrfreq}^{1}_{\Sigma,Q}(D_{\varphi},())
.

(3)

Output the number
2^{|\mathsf{var}(\varphi)|}\cdot r
.

It is clear that the above algorithm runs in polynomial time in
||\varphi||
. Hence, to prove that it is indeed a Turing reduction from
\mathsf{\sharp Pos2DNF}
to
\mathsf{RRFreq}^{1}(\Sigma,Q)
, it suffices to prove that

\mathsf{rrfreq}^{1}_{\Sigma,Q}(D_{\varphi},())\ =\ \frac{|\mathsf{sat}(\varphi)|}{2^{|\mathsf{var}(\varphi)|}}.

Since we consider only single fact removals, a database
D
is an operational repair of
\mathsf{CORep}^{1}(D_{\varphi},\Sigma)
iff it is of the form

\{V(c_{x},\star)\mid x\in\mathsf{var}(\varphi)\text{ and }\star\in\{0,1\}\}\cup D_{c},

which keeps precisely one fact
V(c_{x},\star)
, for each variable
x
in
\varphi
. Hence,
|\mathsf{CORep}^{1}(D_{\varphi},\Sigma)|=2^{|\mathsf{var}(\varphi)|}
.
Thus, with
\mathsf{CORep}^{1}(D_{\varphi},\Sigma,Q)
being the set of repairs
D
in
\mathsf{CORep}^{1}(D_{\varphi},\Sigma)
such that
D\models Q
, it is easy to see that
|\mathsf{CORep}^{1}(D_{\varphi},\Sigma,Q)|=|\mathsf{sat}(\varphi)|
.
Consequently,

\mathsf{rrfreq}^{1}_{\Sigma,Q}(D_{\varphi},())\ =\ \frac{|\mathsf{CORep}^{1}(D_{\varphi},\Sigma,Q)|}{|\mathsf{CORep}^{1}(D_{\varphi},\Sigma)|}=\frac{|\mathsf{sat}(\varphi)|}{2^{|\mathsf{var}(\varphi)|}},

and the claim follows.

Proof of Item (2) of Theorem
E.1

We can employ a proof similar to the one underlying item (2) of Theorem
5.1
, which consists of two steps: (1) existence of an efficient sampler, and (2) provide a polynomial lower bound for the target ratio.
The key difference is that now we focus on the set of repairs
\mathsf{CORep}^{1}(D,\Sigma)
, rather than
\mathsf{CORep}(D,\Sigma)
. Thus, each repair in
\mathsf{CORep}^{1}(D,\Sigma)
is obtained by keeping from
D
precisely one fact from each block of
D
.

We first show the existence of an efficient sampler.

Lemma E.2.

Given a database
D
, and a set
\Sigma
of primary keys, we can sample elements of
\mathsf{CORep}^{1}(D,\Sigma)
uniformly at random in polynomial time in
||D||
.

Proof.

Let
B_{1},\dots,B_{n}
be the blocks of
D
w.r.t.
\Sigma
. That is, for every relation name
R
of the schema with
R:X\rightarrow Y\in\Sigma
, we split the set of facts of
D
over
R
into blocks of facts that agree on the values of all the attributes in
X
. If there is no such key in
\Sigma
, then every fact is a separate block.
As aforementioned, every repair of
\mathsf{CORep}^{1}(D,\Sigma)
contains one fact of each block. Hence,

|\mathsf{CORep}^{1}(D,\Sigma)|\ =\ |B_{1}|\times\cdots\times|B_{n}|.

In order to sample an element of
\mathsf{CORep}^{1}(D,\Sigma)
with probability

\frac{1}{|B_{1}|\times\cdots\times|B_{n}|}

we simply need to select, for each block
B_{i}
, one of its
|B_{i}|
possible outcomes (one of its facts that will appear in the repair), uniformly at random, namely with probability
\frac{1}{|B_{i}|}
).

It remains to show that there exists a polynomial lower bound on the target ratio.

Lemma E.3.

Consider a set
\Sigma
of primary keys, and a CQ
Q(\bar{x})
. For every database
D
, and tuple
\bar{c}\in\mathsf{dom}(D)^{|\bar{x}|}
,

\mathsf{rrfreq}^{1}_{\Sigma,Q}(D,\bar{c})\ \geq\ \frac{1}{(||D||)^{||Q||}}

whenever
\mathsf{rrfreq}^{1}_{\Sigma,Q}(D,\bar{c})>0
.

Proof.

Let
D
be a database. If there is no homomorphism
h
from
Q
to
D
such that
h(Q)\models\Sigma
and
h(\bar{x})=\bar{c}
, then it clearly holds that

\mathsf{rrfreq}^{1}_{\Sigma,Q}(D,\bar{c})\ =\ 0.

We now consider the case where such a homomorphism
h
exists. Assume that
|h(Q)|=m
for some
1\leq m\leq|Q|
.
As in the proof of Lemma
E.2
, let
B_{1},\dots,B_{n}
be the blocks of
D
w.r.t.
\Sigma
. Assume, without loss of generality, that the facts of
h(Q)
belong to the blocks
B_{1},\dots,B_{m}
. Note that no two facts of
h(Q)
belong to the same block, as two facts that belong to the same block always jointly violate the corresponding key, and it holds that
h(Q)\models\Sigma
.

Since all the facts of a block are symmetric to each other, each of these facts has an equal chance to appear in a repair. In particular, every operational repair contains one fact from each block in
\{B_{1},\dots,B_{m}\}
, and precisely

\frac{1}{|B_{1}|\times\dots\times|B_{m}|}

repairs of
\mathsf{CORep}^{1}(D,\Sigma)
contain all the facts of
h(Q)
. Hence,

\displaystyle\mathsf{rrfreq}^{1}_{\Sigma,Q}(D,\bar{c})

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\frac{|\{E\in\mathsf{CORep}^{1}(D,\Sigma)\mid h(Q)\subseteq E\}|}{|\mathsf{CORep}^{1}(D,\Sigma)|}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\frac{\frac{1}{|B_{1}|\times\dots\times|B_{m}|}\times|\mathsf{CORep}^{1}(D,\Sigma)|}{|\mathsf{CORep}^{1}(D,\Sigma)|}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|B_{1}|\times\dots\times|B_{m}|}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|D|^{m}}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|D|^{|Q|}}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(||D||)^{||Q||}},

and the claim follows.

Proof of Item (3) of Theorem
E.1

The proof of this item proceeds similarly to the one used to prove item (3) of Theorem
5.1
. Here we highlight the key differences.

We first need to prove a result analogous to Lemma
5.4
, but for the setting of singleton operations.
For an undirected graph
G
,
\mathsf{IS}_{\neq\emptyset}(G)
denotes the set of all
non-empty
independent sets of

.

Lemma E.4.

Consider a non-trivially
\Sigma
-connected database

, where
\Sigma
is a set of FDs. Then,
|\mathsf{CORep}^{1}(D,\Sigma)|=|\mathsf{IS}_{\neq\emptyset}(\mathsf{CG}(D,\Sigma))|

Proof.

(\subseteq)
Consider a candidate repair
D^{\prime}\in\mathsf{CORep}^{1}(D,\Sigma)
. By definition,
D^{\prime}
is consistent w.r.t.
\Sigma
, i.e., there are no two facts
f,g
of
D^{\prime}
such that
\{f,g\}\not\models\Sigma
. Thus, by definition of the conflict graph of
D
w.r.t.
\Sigma
, we get that no two facts of
D^{\prime}
are connected via an edge in
\mathsf{CG}(D,\Sigma)
. Hence,
D^{\prime}
is an independent set of
\mathsf{CG}(D,\Sigma)
. It remains to show that
D^{\prime}\neq\emptyset
.
Since
D^{\prime}\in\mathsf{CORep}^{1}(D,\Sigma)
, there is a sequence
s=\mathit{op}_{1},\ldots,\mathit{op}_{n}\in\mathsf{CRS}^{1}(D,\Sigma)
such that
s(D)=D^{\prime}
. Since
\mathit{op}_{n}
must be
(s_{n-1}(D),\Sigma)
-justified, there must be a violation
(\phi,\{f,g\})\in\mathsf{V}(s_{n-1}(D),\Sigma)
, for some FD
\phi\in\Sigma
. Moreover, since
s(D)\models\Sigma
, this is the only violation. Hence,
\mathit{op}_{n}=-f
, and then
g\in s(D)
, or
\mathit{op}_{n}=-g
, and then
f\in s(D)
. Thus,
s(D)=D^{\prime}\neq\emptyset
.

(\supseteq)
Consider now an independent set
D^{\prime}\in\mathsf{IS}_{\neq\emptyset}(\mathsf{CG}(D,\Sigma))
, which is by definition non-empty. We have already shown in the proof of Lemma
5.4
that one can construct a sequence
s\in\mathsf{CRS}(D,\Sigma)
such that
s(D)=D^{\prime}
. In particular, by inspecting that proof, we can see that indeed
s
uses only operations of the form
-f
, and thus,
s\in\mathsf{CRS}^{1}(D,\Sigma)
. Hence,
D^{\prime}\in\mathsf{CORep}^{1}(D,\Sigma)
.

The rest of the proof proceeds in two steps. We first prove the following result, which is analogous to Proposition
5.5
. We write
\sharp\mathsf{CORep}^{\mathsf{con},1}(\Sigma)
for the problem of computing
|\mathsf{CORep}^{1}(D,\Sigma)|
, given a non-trivially
\Sigma
-connected database
D
.

Proposition E.5.

Unless
{\rm RP}={\rm NP}
, there exists a set
\Sigma
of keys over
\{R\}
such that
\sharp\mathsf{CORep}^{\mathsf{con},1}(\Sigma)
does not admit an FPRAS.

Proof.

We provide a reduction from the problem of counting
non-empty
independent sets of non-trivially connected graphs of bounded degree.
With
\sharp\mathsf{IS}^{\mathsf{con}}_{\Delta,\neq\emptyset}
, for some integer
\Delta\geq 0
, being the problem of computing
|\mathsf{IS}_{\neq\emptyset}(G)|
, given a non-trivially connected graph
G
with degree
\Delta
, we first need to prove that:

Lemma E.6.

Unless
{\rm RP}={\rm NP}
,
\sharp\mathsf{IS}^{\mathsf{con}}_{\Delta,\neq\emptyset}
has no FPRAS, for all
\Delta\geq 6
.

Proof.

By contradiction, assume that
\sharp\mathsf{IS}^{\mathsf{con}}_{\Delta,\neq\emptyset}
admits an FPRAS, for some
\Delta\geq 6
. We then show that
\sharp\mathsf{IS}^{\mathsf{con}}_{\Delta}
admits an FPRAS, contradicting Lemma
B.5
.
Assume that
\mathsf{A}
is an FPRAS for
\sharp\mathsf{IS}^{\mathsf{con}}_{\Delta,\neq\emptyset}
. Let
\mathsf{A}^{\prime}
be the randomized algorithm that, given a non-trivially connected undirected graph
G
,
\epsilon>0
and
0<\delta<1
, is such that
\mathsf{A}^{\prime}(G,\epsilon,\delta)=\mathsf{A}(G,\epsilon,\delta)+1
.
We proceed to show that
\mathsf{A}^{\prime}
is an FPRAS for
\#\mathsf{IS}^{\mathsf{con}}_{\Delta}
.
Since
\mathsf{A}
is an FPRAS for
\sharp\mathsf{IS}^{\mathsf{con}}_{\Delta,\neq\emptyset}
,

\mathsf{Pr}\left((1-\epsilon)\cdot|\mathsf{IS}_{\neq\emptyset}(G)|\leq\mathsf{A}(G,\epsilon,\delta)\leq(1+\epsilon)\cdot|\mathsf{IS}_{\neq\emptyset}(G)|\right)\geq 1-\delta.

By adding 1 in all sides of the inequality, we obtain that

\mathsf{Pr}\left((1-\epsilon)\cdot|\mathsf{IS}_{\neq\emptyset}(G)|+1\leq\mathsf{A}^{\prime}(G,\epsilon,\delta)\leq\right.\\
\left.(1+\epsilon)\cdot|\mathsf{IS}_{\neq\emptyset}(G)|+1\right)\geq 1-\delta.

Since

\displaystyle(1-\epsilon)\cdot|\mathsf{IS}_{\neq\emptyset}(G)|+1

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle(1-\epsilon)\cdot|\mathsf{IS}_{\neq\emptyset}(G)|+1-\epsilon

\displaystyle(1+\epsilon)\cdot|\mathsf{IS}_{\neq\emptyset}(G)|+1

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle(1+\epsilon)\cdot|\mathsf{IS}_{\neq\emptyset}(G)|+1+\epsilon,

by factorizing the terms in the two inequalities, we obtain that

\displaystyle(1-\epsilon)\cdot|\mathsf{IS}_{\neq\emptyset}(G)|+1

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle(1-\epsilon)\cdot(|\mathsf{IS}_{\neq\emptyset}(G)|+1)

\displaystyle(1+\epsilon)\cdot|\mathsf{IS}_{\neq\emptyset}(G)|+1

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle(1+\epsilon)\cdot(|\mathsf{IS}_{\neq\emptyset}(G)|+1).

Since
|\mathsf{IS}_{\neq\emptyset}(G)|+1=|\mathsf{IS}(G)|
, we conclude that

\mathsf{Pr}\left((1-\epsilon)\cdot|\mathsf{IS}(G)|\leq\mathsf{A}^{\prime}(G,\epsilon,\delta)\leq(1+\epsilon)\cdot|\mathsf{IS}(G)|\right)\geq 1-\delta,

and the claim follows.

With the above lemma in place, we establish our main claim by showing that there exists a set
\Sigma_{K}
of keys such that, given a non-trivially connected undirected graph
G
, we can construct a non-trivially
\Sigma_{K}
-connected database
D_{G}
in polynomial time in
||G||
such that
|\mathsf{IS}_{\neq\emptyset}(G)|=|\mathsf{CORep}^{1}(D_{G},\Sigma_{K})|
.
Hence, unless
{\rm RP}={\rm NP}
, the existence of an FPRAS for
\sharp\mathsf{CORep}^{\mathsf{con},1}(\Sigma_{K})
would imply an FPRAS for
\sharp\mathsf{IS}^{\mathsf{con}}_{\Delta,\neq\emptyset}
, contradicting Lemma
E.6
.

The set
\Sigma_{K}
and the database
D_{G}
are defined in exactly the same way as in the proof of Proposition
5.5
. We recall that
D_{G}
and
\Sigma_{K}
are such that
|\mathsf{IS}(G)|=|\mathsf{IS}(\mathsf{CG}(D_{G},\Sigma_{K}))|
. Hence,
|\mathsf{IS}_{\neq\emptyset}(G)|=|\mathsf{IS}_{\neq\emptyset}(\mathsf{CG}(D_{G},\Sigma_{K}))|
. Since
D_{G}
is non-trivially
\Sigma_{K}
-connected, by Lemma
E.4
,
|\mathsf{IS}_{\neq\emptyset}(\mathsf{CG}(D_{G},\Sigma_{K}))|=|\mathsf{CORep}^{1}(D_{G},\Sigma_{K})|
. Hence,
|\mathsf{IS}_{\neq\emptyset}(G)|=|\mathsf{CORep}^{1}(D_{G},\Sigma_{K})|
, as needed.

It remains to prove a result analogous to Lemma
5.6
. Let
\Sigma_{K}
be the set of keys provided by Proposition
E.5
.

Lemma E.7.

Assume that
\mathsf{RRFreq}^{1}(\Sigma,Q)
admits an FPRAS, for every set
\Sigma
of FDs and CQ
Q
.
\sharp\mathsf{CORep}^{\mathsf{con},1}(\Sigma_{K})
admits an FPRAS.

Proof.

The proof of this claim proceeds in the same way as the one of Lemma
5.6
. The key difference is that now, given a non-trivially
\Sigma_{K}
-connected database
D
, we must show that for the set
\Sigma_{F}
of FDs and the Boolean CQ
Q_{F}
as defined in that proof, the database
D_{F}
obtained from
D
is such that

\mathsf{rrfreq}^{1}_{\Sigma_{F},Q_{F}}(D_{F},())=\frac{1}{\left|\mathsf{CORep}^{1}(D,\Sigma_{K})\right|+1}.

This is done using the same argument as in the proof of Lemma
5.6
, with the difference that we exploit Lemma
E.4
, instead of Lemma
5.4
, to prove that
|\mathsf{CORep}^{1}(D_{F},\Sigma_{F})|=|\mathsf{CORep}^{1}(D,\Sigma_{K})|+1
.

It is now straightforward to see that from Proposition
E.5
and Lemma
E.7
, we can conclude item (3) of Theorem
E.1
.

E.2.
Uniform Sequences

In this section, we prove the version of Theorem
6.1
that considers singleton operations:

Theorem E.8.

(1)

There exist a set
\Sigma
of primary keys, and a CQ
Q
such that
\mathsf{OCQA}(\Sigma,M_{\Sigma}^{\mathsf{us},1},Q)
is
\sharp
P
-hard.

(2)

For a set
\Sigma
of primary keys, and a CQ
Q
,
\mathsf{OCQA}(\Sigma,M_{\Sigma}^{\mathsf{us},1},Q)
admits an FPRAS.

As for Theorem
6.1
, we can conveniently restate the problem of interest as the problem of computing a “relative frequency” ratio.
Indeed, for a database
D
, a set
\Sigma
of FDs, a CQ
Q(\bar{x})
, and a tuple
\bar{c}\in\mathsf{dom}(D)^{|\bar{x}|}
,

\mathsf{P}_{M_{\Sigma}^{\mathsf{us},1},Q}(D,\bar{c})=\mathsf{srfreq}^{1}_{\Sigma,Q}(D,\bar{c})
, where

\mathsf{srfreq}^{1}_{\Sigma,Q}(D,\bar{c})=\frac{|\{s\in\mathsf{CRS}^{1}(D,\Sigma)\mid\bar{c}\in Q(s(D))\}|}{|\mathsf{CRS}^{1}(D,\Sigma)|}.

Hence,
\mathsf{OCQA}(\Sigma,M_{\Sigma}^{\mathsf{us},1},Q(\bar{x}))
coincides with the following problem, which is independent from the Markov chain generator
M_{\Sigma}^{\mathsf{us},1}
:

PROBLEM
:

\mathsf{SRFreq}^{1}(\Sigma,Q(\bar{x}))

INPUT
:

A database
D
, and a tuple
\bar{c}\in\mathsf{dom}(D)^{|\bar{x}|}
.

OUTPUT
:

\mathsf{srfreq}^{1}_{\Sigma,Q}(D,\bar{c})
.

We proceed to establish Theorem
E.8
by directly considering the problem
\mathsf{SRFreq}^{1}(\Sigma,Q(\bar{x}))
instead of
\mathsf{OCQA}(\Sigma,M_{\Sigma}^{\mathsf{us},1},Q)
.

Proof of Item (1) of Theorem
E.8

We provide a polynomial-time Turing reduction from
\mathsf{\sharp Pos2DNF}
. In fact, the reduction is identical to the one used to prove item (1) of Theorem
E.1
. We only need to argue that, given a positive 2DNF formula
\varphi
,

\mathsf{srfreq}^{1}_{\Sigma,Q}(D_{\varphi},())\ =\ \frac{|\mathsf{sat}(\varphi)|}{2^{|\mathsf{var}(\varphi)|}},

where
D_{\varphi}
,
\Sigma
and
Q
are as in the proof of item (1) of Theorem
E.1
.

A database
D
is a repair in
\mathsf{CORep}^{1}(D_{\varphi},\Sigma)
iff it keeps from
D_{\varphi}
precisely one fact
V(c_{x},\star)
, for each variable
x
of
\varphi
. Hence,
|\mathsf{CORep}^{1}(D_{\varphi},\Sigma)|=2^{|\mathsf{var}(\varphi)|}
.
Moreover, since no two violations in
\mathsf{V}(D_{\varphi},\Sigma)
share a fact, each such a repair is the result of precisely
|\mathsf{var}(\varphi)|!
sequences of
\mathsf{CRS}^{1}(D_{\varphi},\Sigma)
(i.e., operations can be applied in any order).
Hence,
|\mathsf{CRS}^{1}(D_{\varphi},\Sigma)|=2^{|\mathsf{var}(\varphi)|}\cdot|\mathsf{var}(\varphi)|!
.
Thus, with
\mathsf{CRS}^{1}(D_{\varphi},\Sigma,Q)
being the set of sequences
s
of
\mathsf{CRS}^{1}(D_{\varphi},\Sigma)
such that
s(D_{\varphi})\models Q
, it is straightforward to see that
|\mathsf{CRS}^{1}(D_{\varphi},\Sigma,Q)|=|\mathsf{sat}(\varphi)|\cdot|\mathsf{var}(\varphi)|!
.
Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathsf{srfreq}^{1}_{\Sigma,Q}(D_{\varphi},())

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{|\mathsf{CRS}^{1}(D_{\varphi},\Sigma,Q)|}{|\mathsf{CRS}^{1}(D_{\varphi},\Sigma)|}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{|\mathsf{sat}(\varphi)|\cdot|\mathsf{var}(\varphi)|!}{2^{|\mathsf{var}(\varphi)|}\cdot|\mathsf{var}(\varphi)|!}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{|\mathsf{sat}(\varphi)|}{2^{|\mathsf{var}(\varphi)|}},

and the claim follows.

Proof of Item (2) of Theorem
E.8

As for item (2) of Theorem
6.1
, the proof consists of two steps: (1) existence of an efficient sampler, and (2) provide a polynomial lower bound on the target ratio.

We first show that an efficient sampler exists.

Lemma E.9.

Given a database
D
, and a set
\Sigma
of primary keys, we can sample elements of
\mathsf{CRS}^{1}(D,\Sigma)
uniformly at random in polynomial time in
||D||
.

Proof.

The algorithm
\mathsf{SampleSeq}
(Algorithm
1
) that is used to sample elements of
\mathsf{CRS}(D,\Sigma)
can be used to sample elements of
\mathsf{CRS}^{1}(D,\Sigma)
as well. The only difference lies on the set of
(s(D),\Sigma)
-justified operations that, in the case of
\mathsf{CRS}(D,\Sigma)
consists of both single-fact removals and pair removals, while in the case of
\mathsf{CRS}^{1}(D,\Sigma)
it consists only of single-fact removals.

We now show the polynomial lower bound on the target ratio.

Lemma E.10.

Consider a set
\Sigma
of primary keys, and a CQ
Q(\bar{x})
. For every database
D
, and tuple
\bar{c}\in\mathsf{dom}(D)^{|\bar{x}|}
,

\mathsf{srfreq}^{1}_{\Sigma,Q}(D,\bar{c})\ \geq\ \frac{1}{(||D||)^{||Q||}}

whenever
\mathsf{srfreq}^{1}_{\Sigma,Q}(D,\bar{c})>0
.

Proof.

Let
D
be a database. If there is no homomorphism
h
from
Q
to
D
such that
h(Q)\models\Sigma
and
h(\bar{x})=\bar{c}
, then clearly it holds that

\mathsf{srfreq}^{1}_{\Sigma,Q}(D,\bar{c})\ =\ 0.

We now consider the case where such a homomorphism
h
exists. Assume that
|h(Q)|=m
for some
1\leq m\leq|Q|
. As in the proof of Lemma
E.3
, let
B_{1},\dots,B_{n}
be the blocks of
D
w.r.t.
\Sigma
. Assume, w.l.o.g., that the facts of
h(Q)
belong to the blocks
B_{1},\dots,B_{m}
.

Since all the facts of a block are symmetric to each other, if for some
f\in B_{i}
, there are
m
sequences
s
in
\mathsf{CRS}^{1}(B_{i},\Sigma)
such that
f\in s(B_{i})
, then the same holds for every fact
g\in B_{i}
. Moreover, since every operational repair of
\mathsf{RL}(M_{\Sigma}^{\mathsf{us},1})
keeps precisely one fact from each block, and the blocks are independent (in the sense that an operation over some block has no impact on the justified operations of another block), we can conclude that precisely

\frac{1}{|B_{1}|\times\dots\times|B_{m}|}

of the sequences
s
in
\mathsf{CRS}^{1}(D,\Sigma)
are such that
h(Q)\subseteq s(D)
(i.e., the sequence
s
keeps the fact
B_{i}\cap h(Q)
for every
B_{i}\in\{B_{1},\dots,B_{m}\}
).

We then have that

\displaystyle\mathsf{srfreq}^{1}_{\Sigma,Q}(D,\bar{c})

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\frac{|\{s\in\mathsf{CRS}^{1}(D,\Sigma)\mid h(Q)\subseteq s(D)\}|}{|\mathsf{CRS}^{1}(D,\Sigma)|}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\frac{\frac{1}{|B_{1}|\times\dots\times|B_{m}|}\times|\mathsf{CRS}^{1}(D,\Sigma)|}{|\mathsf{CRS}^{1}(D,\Sigma)|}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|B_{1}|\times\dots\times|B_{m}|}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|D|^{m}}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|D|^{|Q|}}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(||D||)^{||Q||}},

and the claim follows.

E.3.
Uniform Operations

In this last section, we prove that item (1) of Theorem
7.1
holds also in the case of singleton operations.

Theorem E.11.

There exist a set
\Sigma
of primary keys, and a CQ
Q
such that
\mathsf{OCQA}(\Sigma,M_{\Sigma}^{\mathsf{uo},1},Q)
is
\sharp
P
-hard.

Proof.

We use the reduction form the proof of Theorem
E.1
(1). We only need to argue that, given a positive 2DNF formula
\varphi
,

\mathsf{P}_{M_{\Sigma}^{\mathsf{uo},1},Q}(D_{\varphi},())\ =\ \frac{|\mathsf{sat}(\varphi)|}{2^{|\mathsf{var}(\varphi)|}},

where
D_{\varphi}
,
\Sigma
and
Q
are as in the proof of item (1) of Theorem
E.1
.

Let
M_{\Sigma}^{\mathsf{uo},1}(D_{\varphi})=(V,E,\mathbf{P})
.
By the definition of the Markov chain generator,
\mathsf{RL}(M_{\Sigma}^{\mathsf{uo},1}(D_{\varphi}))=\mathsf{CRS}^{1}(D_{\varphi},\Sigma)
.
Moreover, we note that each variable
x
of
\varphi
induces a violation
\{V(c_{x},0),V(c_{x},1)\}
in
D_{\varphi}
, which can be resolved with one of two operations removing a single fact. Hence, every complete sequence in
\mathsf{CRS}^{1}(D_{\varphi},\Sigma)
is of length precisely
|\mathsf{var}(\varphi)|
, and for every non-leaf node
s\in V
that is also in
\mathsf{Ops}^{1}_{D_{\varphi}}(\Sigma,
),
|\mathsf{Ops}^{1}_{s}(D_{\varphi},\Sigma)|=2\cdot(|\mathsf{var}(\varphi)|-|s|)
.
Hence, by Definition of
M_{\Sigma}^{\mathsf{uo},1}
, with
\pi
being the leaf distribution of
M_{\Sigma}^{\mathsf{uo}}(D_{\varphi})
, for each
s=\mathit{op}_{1},\ldots,\mathit{op}_{n}\in\mathsf{CRS}^{1}(D_{G},\Sigma)=\mathsf{RL}(M_{\Sigma}^{\mathsf{uo},1}(D_{\varphi}))
,

\pi(s)\ =\ \mathbf{P}(s_{0},s_{1})\cdots\mathbf{P}(s_{n-1},s_{n})\ =\ \frac{1}{2^{|\mathsf{var}(\varphi)|}\cdot|\mathsf{var}(\varphi)|!}.

This means that each sequence
s\in\mathsf{CRS}^{1}(D_{\varphi},\Sigma)=\mathsf{RL}(M_{\Sigma}^{\mathsf{uo},1}(D_{\varphi}))
is assigned the same non-zero probability, i.e.,
\pi
is the uniform distribution over
\mathsf{CRS}^{1}(D_{\varphi},\Sigma)
. The latter implies that
\mathsf{P}_{M_{\Sigma}^{\mathsf{uo},1},Q}(D_{\varphi},())=\mathsf{srfreq}^{1}_{\Sigma,Q}(D_{\varphi},())
. As we have already seen that

\mathsf{rrfreq}^{1}_{\Sigma,Q}(D_{\varphi},())\ =\ \mathsf{srfreq}^{1}_{\Sigma,Q}(D_{\varphi},())\ =\ \frac{|\mathsf{sat}(\varphi)|}{2^{|\mathsf{var}(\varphi)|}}

the claim follows.
