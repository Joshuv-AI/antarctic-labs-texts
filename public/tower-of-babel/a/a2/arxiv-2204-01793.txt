Sampling repulsive Gibbs point processes using random graphs
Tobias Friedrich ∗, Andreas Göbel ∗, Maximilian Katzmann †, Martin S. Krejca ‡, Marcus Pappik ∗

Abstract
We study computational aspects of repulsive Gibbs point processes, which are probabilistic models of interacting particles in a finite-volume region of space. We introduce an approach for reducing a Gibbs point process to the hard-core model, a well-studied discrete spin system. Given an instance of such a point process, our reduction generates a random graph drawn from a natural geometric model. We show that the partition function of a hard-core model on graphs generated by the geometric model concentrates around the partition function of the Gibbs point process. Our reduction allows us to use a broad range of algorithms developed for the hard-core model to sample from the Gibbs point process and approximate its partition function. This is, to the extend of our knowledge, the first approach that deals with pair potentials of unbounded range. We compare the resulting algorithms with recently established results and study further properties of the random geometric graphs with respect to the hard-core model.

Introduction

Gibbs point processes are a tool for modelling a variety of phenomena that can be described as distributions of random spatial events
[
6
,
36
]
. Such phenomena include the location of stars in the universe, a sample of cells under the microscope, or the location of pores and cracks in the ground (see
[
37
,
45
]
for more on applications of Gibbs point processes). In statistical physics, such point processes are frequently used as stochastic models for gases or liquids of interacting particles
[
39
]
.

A Gibbs point process on a finite-volume region
\mathds{V}
is parameterized by a fugacity
\lambda
and a pair potential
\phi
expressing the interactions between pairs of points. Every point configuration in the region is assigned a weight according to the pair interactions
\phi
between all pairs of points in the configuration. One can then think of a Gibbs point process as a Poisson point process of intensity
\lambda
, where the density of each configuration is scaled proportionally to its weight. The density is normalized by the partition function, which is the integral of the weights over the configuration space (see
Section

1.1
for a formal definition of the model). The most famous example of such a process is the
hard-sphere model
, a model of a random packing of equal-sized spheres with radius
r
. The pair potential in the hard-sphere model defines hard-core interactions, i.e., configurations where two points closer than some distance
2r
have weight zero, while all other configurations have weight one. In this article, we consider Gibbs point processes with
repulsive
potentials, that is, pair potentials in which adding a point to a configuration does not increase its weight. The hard-sphere model, for example, does have a repulsive pair potential, however, we do not restrict ourselves to hard-core potentials and allow for soft-core interactions.

The two most fundamental algorithmic tasks considered on Gibbs point processes are to sample from the model and to compute its partition function, which are closely related. Understanding for which potentials and fugacities these two tasks are tractable is an ambitious endeavour. Towards this goal, there has been a plethora of algorithmic results on Gibbs point processes spanning several decades. Notably, the Markov chain Monte Carlo method was developed for sampling an instance of the hard-sphere model with 224 particles
[
32
]
. Since then, a variety of exact and approximate sampling algorithms for such point processes have been proposed in the literature, and their efficiency has been studied extensively both without
[
19
,
22
]
and with rigorous running time guarantees
[
35
,
24
,
21
,
34
,
1
]
. The key objective of rigorous works is to identify a parameter regime for their respective model for which a randomized algorithm for sampling and approximating the partition function exists with running time polynomial in the volume of the region. In addition, deterministic algorithms for approximating the partition function have also appeared in the literature
[
17
,
26
]
with running time quasi-polynomial in the volume of the region.

This recent flurry of algorithmic results on Gibbs point processes can be attributed to progress in understanding the computational properties of discrete spin systems, such as the hard-core model. Within these works, two main approaches can be identified for transferring insights from discrete spin systems to Gibbs point processes. The first one, which includes results such as
[
23
,
34
,
1
]
, considers properties proven to hold in discrete spin systems and translates them to the continuous setting of Gibbs point processes. More precisely, these works consider the notion of strong spatial mixing, which has been strongly connected to algorithmic properties of discrete spin systems
[
47
,
40
,
16
]
, and translate it to an analogous notion for Gibbs point processes to obtain algorithmic results. A common pattern in these works is that once the parameter regime for which strong spatial mixing holds is established, one needs to prove from scratch that this implies efficient algorithms in Gibbs point processes. In addition, the definition of strong spatial mixing for Gibbs points processes assumes that the pair interactions of two particles is always of bounded range, i.e., if two particles are placed at distance greater than some constant
r\in\mathds{R}_{\geq 0}
, they do not interact with each other.

The second approach, used in
[
18
,
17
]
, is to discretize the model, i.e., reduce it to an instance of the hard-core model and then solve the respective algorithmic problem for the hard-core model. In this case, the algorithmic arsenal developed over the years for the hard-core model is now readily available for the instances resulting from this reduction. The main downside of these approaches is that they only apply to the hard-sphere model, a special case of bounded-range repulsive interactions.

Our contributions.

We introduce a natural approach for reducing repulsive point processes to the hard-core model. Given an instance
(\mathds{V},\lambda,\phi)
of such a point process, we generate a random graph by sampling
n\in\upTheta\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({\mathds{V}^{2}}}}\right)
point-vertices independently and uniformly at random in
\mathds{V}
and by connecting each pair of points with an edge drawn with an appropriate probability, which depends on
\phi
. We show that computational properties of the hard-core model on graphs generated by this model and with an appropriately scaled fugacity transfer to the originating Gibbs point process.

We first show that the partition function of the hard-core model on these graphs concentrates around the partition function of the Gibbs point process. Using existing algorithms for the hard-core model as a black box, our result immediately yields randomized approximation algorithms for the partition function of the point process in running time polynomial in the volume of
\mathds{V}
.
Furthermore, we show that sampling an independent set from the generated hard-core model and returning the positions of its vertices in
\mathds{V}
results in an approximate sampler from the distribution of the Gibbs point process. Our approach, in contrast to all previous algorithmic work in the literature, does not require the pair potential
\phi
of the point process to be of bounded range. This includes various models of interest in statistical physics, such as the (hard-core) Yukawa model
[
15
,
38
]
, the Gaussian overlap model
[
7
]
, the generalized exponential model
[
5
]
, and the Yoshida–Kamakura model
[
48
]
.

Finally, we identify the parameter regime (in terms of
\lambda
and
\phi
) for which the generated hard-core instance exhibits strong spatial mixing. This parameter regime is identical to the best known regime for which repulsive point processes with bounded-range potentials have strong spatial mixing
[
34
]
.

1.1
Gibbs point processes

We now formally define the notion of a Gibbs point process.
As usual in the theory of point processes, we assume the underlying space to be a complete, separable metric space
(\mathds{X},d)
equipped with the Borel algebra
\mathcal{B}=\mathcal{B}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathds{X}}}\right)
and a reference measure
\nu
on
(\mathds{X},\mathcal{B})
that assigns finite volume to bounded measurable sets.
In this work, we study Gibbs point processes
P_{\mathds{V}}^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\lambda,\phi}}\right)}
on bounded measurable regions
\mathds{V}\subseteq\mathds{X}
that are parameterized by a
fugacity
parameter
\lambda\in\mathds{R}_{\geq 0}
and a repulsive (i.e., non-negative), symmetric, measurable
potential function

\phi\colon\mathds{X}^{2}\to\mathds{R}_{\geq 0}\cup\{\infty\}
.
Such a process
P_{\mathds{V}}^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\lambda,\phi}}\right)}
is defined by a density with respect to a Poisson point process
Q_{\lambda}
with intensity
\lambda
on
\mathds{X}
.
For every finite point configuration
\bm{x}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})\in\mathds{V}^{k}
, this density is proportional to
\mathrm{e}^{-H\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x_{1},\dots,x_{k}}}\right)}
, where
H
is the
Hamiltonian

H\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x_{1}\dots,x_{k}}}\right)=\sum\nolimits_{\{i,j\}\in\binom{[k]}{2}}\phi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x_{i},x_{j}}}\right).

More precisely, the density can be expressed explicitly as

\frac{\text{d}P_{\mathds{V}}^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\lambda,\phi}}\right)}}{\text{d}Q_{\lambda}}(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})=\frac{\mathds{1}_{\forall i\in[k]\colon x_{i}\in\mathds{V}}\cdot\mathrm{e}^{-H\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x_{1},\dots,x_{k}}}\right)}\mathrm{e}^{\lambda\nu\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathds{V}}}\right)}}{\Xi_{\mathds{V}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\lambda,\phi}}\right)},

where the normalizing constant
\Xi_{\mathds{V}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\lambda,\phi}}\right)
is the
partition function

\displaystyle\Xi_{\mathds{V}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\lambda,\phi}}\right)

\displaystyle=1+\sum_{k\in\mathds{N}_{\geq 1}}\frac{\lambda^{k}}{k!}\int_{\mathds{V}^{k}}\mathrm{e}^{-H\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x_{1},\dots,x_{k}}}\right)}\nu^{k}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\text{d}\bm{x}}}\right).

1.2
Randomized reduction to the hard-core model

Our approach is to reduce the problem of sampling from a repulsive Gibbs point process and approximating its partition function to the analogous problems for a discrete hard-core model, which we briefly introduce.
For an undirected graph
G=(V,E)
, let
\mathcal{I}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(G}}\right)\subseteq 2^{V}
denote the set of independent sets of
G
.
For a vertex activity
\gamma\in\mathds{R}_{\geq 0}
, the
hard-core model
on
G
is a probability distribution
\mu_{G}^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\gamma}}\right)}
on
\mathcal{I}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(G}}\right)
that assigns each independent set
I\in\mathcal{I}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(G}}\right)
a probability proportional to
\gamma^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|I}}\right|}
.
The normalizing constant of this distribution,
Z_{G}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\gamma}}\right)=\sum_{I\in\mathcal{I}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(G}}\right)}\gamma^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|I}}\right|}
, is called the
hard-core partition function
on
G
.

The goal is to reduce the problem of approximate sampling from
P_{\mathds{V}}^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\lambda,\phi}}\right)}
to approximate sampling from
\mu_{G}^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\gamma}}\right)}
and, similarly, to reduce the problem of approximating
\Xi_{\mathds{V}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\lambda,\phi}}\right)
to approximating
Z_{G}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\gamma}}\right)
for a suitably chosen graph
G
and vertex activity
\gamma
.
The advantage of this approach is that sampling from a hard-core model as well as approximating hard-core partition functions are well studied problems.
Specifically, a sequence of recent papers
[
3
,
4
,
10
,
11
,
12
]
established approximate sampling from
\mu_{G}^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\gamma}}\right)}
in
\widetilde{\mathrm{O}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|V}}\right|}}}\right)
running time and randomized approximation of
Z_{G}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\gamma}}\right)
in
\widetilde{\mathrm{O}}\big({\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|V}}\right|^{2}}\big)
running time for graphs
G
with maximum degree
d
for all
\gamma
strictly below the
tree threshold

\gamma_{\text{c}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(d}}\right)\coloneqq\frac{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(d-1}}\right)^{d-1}}{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(d-2}}\right)^{d}}
.

Our reduction is inspired by the discretization schemes in
[
17
,
18
]
.
These approaches are limited to the hard-sphere model (and similar models with hard-core interactions) in specific regions of Euclidean space.
In this setting, the utilized graph
G
is essentially a unit-disk graph in
\mathds{V}
.
This procedure comes with two major disadvantages.
Firstly, the analysis heavily depends geometric arguments and is therefore restricted to regions in Euclidean space that satisfy various requirements.
Secondly, it is not obvious how this technique extends to general repulsive potentials
\phi
and especially how to account for soft-core interactions.

We circumvent the above problems by investigating hard-core models on a suitably chosen family of random graphs.
For a bounded measurable region
\mathds{V}\subseteq\mathds{X}
, let
u_{\mathds{V}}
denote the uniform distribution on
\mathds{V}
.
That is, for
x\sim u_{\mathds{V}}
, we have
\mathrm{Pr}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{x\in A}}}\right]=\frac{\nu\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(A}}\right)}{\nu\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathds{V}}}\right)}
for every measurable
A\subseteq\mathds{V}
, and
\mathrm{Pr}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{x\notin\mathds{V}}}}\right]=0
.
For a repulsive potential
\phi
and a positive integer
n\in\mathds{N}_{\geq 1}
, we consider a random-graph model
\zeta^{(n)}_{\mathds{V},\phi}
on the set of undirected graphs with vertex set
[n]
, where
\zeta^{(n)}_{\mathds{V},\phi}
is defined by the following natural procedure to generate a graph:

1.

For each
i\in[n]
, draw a uniform random point
x_{i}\sim u_{\mathds{V}}
independently.

2.

For all
i,j\in[n]
with
i\neq j
, connect
i
and
j
with an edge with probability
1-\mathrm{e}^{-\phi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x_{i},x_{j}}}\right)}
independently.

Readers familiar with graphons might notice that this random-graph model can be expressed as a graphon-based random graph (
W
-random graph) for a suitably chosen graphon
W
.
We discuss this perspective later in the introduction.
Moreover, we would like to mentioned that a similar graph construction based on points from a Poisson point process was used in
[
8
]
to prove uniqueness of the infinite-volume Gibbs measure for
\lambda<\frac{1}{C_{\phi}}
via percolation.

The key property of the graphs from
\zeta^{(n)}_{\mathds{V},\phi}
is that, for a suitably chosen vertex activity
\gamma
, their hard-core partition functions concentrate around
\Xi_{\mathds{V}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\lambda,\phi}}\right)
.
This property is at the core of our reduction.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
(\mathds{X},d)
be a complete, separable metric space, let
\mathcal{B}=\mathcal{B}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathds{X}}}\right)
be the Borel algebra, and let
\nu
be a locally finite reference measure on
(\mathds{X},\mathcal{B})
.
Let
\mathds{V}\subseteq\mathds{X}
be bounded and measurable, let
\lambda\in\mathds{R}_{\geq 0}
, and let
\phi\colon\mathds{X}^{2}\to\mathds{R}_{\geq 0}\cup\{\infty\}
be a symmetric repulsive potential.
For all
\varepsilon\in(0,1]
,
\delta\in(0,1]
and
n\geq 4\varepsilon^{-2}\delta^{-1}\max\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\mathrm{e}^{6}\lambda^{2}\nu\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathds{V}}}\right)^{2},\ln\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(4\varepsilon^{-1}}}\right)^{2}}}\right\}
, it holds that, for
G\sim\zeta^{(n)}_{\mathds{V},\phi}
,

\mathrm{Pr}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|Z_{G}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\lambda\nu\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathds{V}}}\right)}{n}}}\right)-\Xi_{\mathds{V}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\lambda,\phi}}\right)}}\right|\geq\varepsilon\Xi_{\mathds{V}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\lambda,\phi}}\right)}}}\right]\leq\delta.

Informally,
Theorem

1.1
says that, for
n\in\upTheta\big({\nu\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathds{V}}}\right)^{2}}\big)
and
G\sim\zeta^{(n)}_{\mathds{V},\phi}
, the hard-core partition function
Z_{G}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\gamma\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(n}}\right)}}\right)
with
\gamma\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(n}}\right)=\frac{\lambda\nu\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathds{V}}}\right)}{n}
is strongly concentrated around the partition function of the repulsive Gibbs point process
\Xi_{\mathds{V}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\lambda,\phi}}\right)
.
In
[
17
, Proposition
5.8
]
, it was argued that the partition function of an unrestricted Poisson point process in a bounded measurable region
\mathds{V}
of Euclidean space cannot be approximated by the hard-core partition function
Z_{G}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\gamma\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(n}}\right)}}\right)
for any graph
G
on
n
vertices if
n\in\mathrm{o}\big({\nu\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathds{V}}}\right)^{2}}\big)
.
As the unrestricted Poisson point process is a special case of a repulsive Gibbs point process with constant zero potential, this implies that our concentration result in
Theorem

1.1
is tight in terms of its asymptotic dependency on the volume
\nu\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathds{V}}}\right)
.

A remarkable aspect of
Theorem

1.1
is the generality in which it holds with respect to the underlying space
\mathds{X}
.
In particular, we do not need any additional assumptions regarding its geometry.
We achieve this by deriving
Theorem

1.1
from a corollary of the Efron–Stein inequality
[
14
]
.
This corollary gives a convenient-to-use way for proving concentration of functions of independent random inputs, given that changing one input of the function only leads to small relative changes of its output.
Using this corollary, we prove
Theorem

1.1
by bounding changes in the hard-core partition function of a graph that are caused by small alternations of the graph structure.
We proceed by discussing this approach in detail.

Proving concentration

We prove
Theorem

1.1
in two steps.
First, we show that, for
G\sim\zeta^{(n)}_{\mathds{V},\phi}
and
\gamma\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(n}}\right)=\frac{\lambda\nu\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathds{V}}}\right)}{n}
, the expected hard-core partition function
\mathrm{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{Z_{G}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\gamma\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(n}}\right)}}\right)}}}\right]
converges rapidly to the partition function of the point process
\Xi_{\mathds{V}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\lambda,\phi}}\right)
as
n
grows.
We prove this by fixing
n\in\upTheta\big({\nu\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathds{V}}}\right)^{2}}\big)
sufficiently large and rewriting
\mathrm{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{Z_{G}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\gamma\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(n}}\right)}}\right)}}}\right]
as a sum of expectations.
We then relate each of the first
m<n
terms in that sum with the corresponding term in
\Xi_{\mathds{V}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\lambda,\phi}}\right)
, which requires comparing the expected number of independent sets of size
k\leq m
in a graph
G\sim\zeta^{(n)}_{\mathds{V},\phi}
with
\frac{n^{k}}{k!\nu\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathds{V}}}\right)^{k}}\int_{\mathds{V}^{k}}\mathrm{e}^{-H\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\bm{x}}}\right)}\text{d}\bm{x}
.
Finally, we argue that all terms of order higher than
m
can be discarded.

Once convergence of the expectation is established, it remains to prove that the distribution of the partition functions
Z_{G}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\gamma\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(n}}\right)}}\right)
for
G\sim\zeta^{(n)}_{\mathds{V},\phi}
concentrates around this expectation.
To prove the latter, we derive
Corollary

3.3
from the Efron–Stein inequality
[
14
]
. This corollary roughly states that the output of a function
f
on a product of probability spaces concentrates around its expectation if
f
exhibits sufficiently small
relative
changes when any component of its input is changed. Similar methods for proving concentration usually require the output of the function
f
to exhibit small
absolute
changes (i.e.,
f
to be Lipschitz, see
[
30
,
31
]
), which does not hold in our setting.

To apply this concentration bound, we need to express the partition function of a graph drawn from
\zeta^{(n)}_{\mathds{V},\phi}
as a function of independent random inputs.
To this end, we model a random graph
G\sim\zeta^{(n)}_{\mathds{V},\phi}
based on
n
points
\bm{x}=(x_{i})_{i\in[n]}
, each independently drawn from
u_{\mathds{V}}
, and
\frac{n(n-1)}{2}
independent random variables
\bm{y}=(y_{i,j})_{1\leq i<j\leq n}
, each uniformly distributed on the real interval
[0,1]
.
Given the random vectors
\bm{x}
and
\bm{y}
, we construct a graph by connecting vertices
i<j
by an edge if and only if
y_{i,j}\leq 1-\mathrm{e}^{-\phi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x_{i},x_{j}}}\right)}
.
Note that the resulting graph is distributed according to
\zeta^{(n)}_{\mathds{V},\phi}
.
Thus, we express the hard-core partition function on the random-graph model
\zeta^{(n)}_{\mathds{V},\phi}
as a function
f(\bm{x},\bm{y})
for
\bm{x}
and
\bm{y}
as described above.
The effect of changing a component of
\bm{y}
is bounded by the relative change of the hard-core partition function when adding or removing an edge.
On the other hand, the effect of changing a component of
\bm{x}
, say
x_{i}
, is bounded by considering the change of the hard-core partition function when altering the neighborhood of a single vertex
i
.
Bounding both effects and applying
Corollary

3.3
yields the desired concentration result (
Theorem

1.1
).

In fact, a similar argument as above applies to a broad class of antiferromagnetic spin systems on graphon-based random graphs that contains our application as a special case.
We discuss his more general setting after demonstrating the sampling and approximation results for Gibbs point processes that can be obtained from
Theorem

1.1
.

1.3
Algorithmic implications

We proceed by showcasing some algorithmic results for repulsive Gibbs point processes that follow from
Theorem

1.1
.
More specifically, we focus on
\varepsilon
-approximate sampling from the point process and obtaining a randomized
\varepsilon
-approximation of the partition function.
Formally, the problem of
\varepsilon
-approximate sampling
from
P_{\mathds{V}}^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\lambda,\phi}}\right)}
is defined as producing a random point configuration with a distribution that has a total variation distance of at most
\varepsilon
to
P_{\mathds{V}}^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\lambda,\phi}}\right)}
.
Analogously, the problem of
\varepsilon
-approximating

\Xi_{\mathds{V}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\lambda,\phi}}\right)
is defined as computing some value
x\in\mathds{R}
such that
(1-\varepsilon)\Xi_{\mathds{V}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\lambda,\phi}}\right)\leq x\leq(1+\varepsilon)\Xi_{\mathds{V}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\lambda,\phi}}\right)
.
Moreover, an algorithm is called a
randomized

\varepsilon
-approximation
if it outputs an
\varepsilon
-approximation of
\Xi_{\mathds{V}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\lambda,\phi}}\right)
with probability at least
\frac{2}{3}
.
The choice of the constant
\frac{2}{3}
is rather arbitrary here, as the error probability can be made smaller than every
\delta\in\mathds{R}_{>0}
by taking the median of
\mathrm{O}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({\log\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\delta^{-1}}}\right)}}}\right)
independent runs, as long as the error probability of each run is some constant smaller than
\frac{1}{2}
.
Furthermore, we consider an
\varepsilon
-approximate sampler and a (randomized)
\varepsilon
-approximation algorithm as efficient if their running time is polynomial in the volume
\nu\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathds{V}}}\right)
and in
\varepsilon^{-1}
.

Recent rigorous results establish bounds on the fugacity regime of different models for which these algorithmic problems can be solved efficiently.
Often, these bounds are stated in terms of the
temperedness constant

C_{\phi}
, which is defined as

C_{\phi}=\sup_{x_{1}\in\mathds{X}}\int_{\mathds{X}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|1-\mathrm{e}^{-\phi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x_{1},x_{2}}}\right)}}}\right|\nu\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\text{d}x_{2}}}\right).

This value can be seen as measure for the strength of interactions between points.

Given our concentration result (
Theorem

1.1
), a straightforward idea for approximating
\Xi_{\mathds{V}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\lambda,\phi}}\right)
is to sample a graph
G\sim\zeta^{(n)}_{\mathds{V},\phi}
and try to approximate its hard-core partition function.
A refined version of this procedure leads to the following theorem.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
(\mathds{X},d)
be a complete, separable metric space, let
\mathcal{B}=\mathcal{B}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathds{X}}}\right)
be the Borel algebra, and let
\nu
be a locally finite reference measure on
(\mathds{X},\mathcal{B})
.
Let
\mathds{V}\subseteq\mathds{X}
be bounded and measurable, let
\lambda\in\mathds{R}_{\geq 0}
, and let
\phi\colon\mathds{X}^{2}\to\mathds{R}_{\geq 0}\cup\{\infty\}
be a symmetric repulsive potential.
Assume there is a sampler for
\zeta^{(n)}_{\mathds{V},\phi}
with running time
t_{\mathds{V},\phi}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(n}}\right)
.

If
\lambda<\frac{\mathrm{e}}{C_{\phi}}
,
then, for all
\varepsilon\in(0,1]
, there is a randomized
\varepsilon
-approximation algorithm for
\Xi_{\mathds{V}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\lambda,\phi}}\right)
with running time in
\widetilde{\mathrm{O}}\big({\nu\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathds{V}}}\right)^{4}\varepsilon^{-6}}\big)+t_{\mathds{V},\phi}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\widetilde{\mathrm{O}}\big({\nu\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathds{V}}}\right)^{2}\varepsilon^{-2}}\big)}}\right)
.

With respect to sampling from
P_{\mathds{V}}^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\lambda,\phi}}\right)}
, it is less obvious how
Theorem

1.1
can be utilized.
However, under mild assumptions, we obtain an approximate sampler, based on
Theorem

1.1
, by the following procedure: Sample an independent set
I\in\mathcal{I}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(G}}\right)
(approximately) from
\mu_{G}^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\gamma\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(n}}\right)}}\right)}
and output the point configuration
\{x_{i}\}_{i\in I}
.
Given that
P_{\mathds{V}}^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\lambda,\phi}}\right)}
is simple, which means that drawing a point configuration that contains the same point multiple times has probability zero, a refined version of the approach sketched above leads to the following result.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
(\mathds{X},d)
be a complete, separable metric space, let
\mathcal{B}=\mathcal{B}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathds{X}}}\right)
be the Borel algebra, and let
\nu
be a locally finite reference measure on
(\mathds{X},\mathcal{B})
.
Let
\mathds{V}\subseteq\mathds{X}
be bounded and measurable, let
\lambda\in\mathds{R}_{\geq 0}
, and let
\phi\colon\mathds{X}^{2}\to\mathds{R}_{\geq 0}\cup\{\infty\}
be a symmetric repulsive potential.
Assume we can sample from the uniform distribution
u_{\mathds{V}}
in time
t_{\mathds{V}}
and, for every
x,y\in\mathds{V}
, we can evaluate
\phi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x,y}}\right)
in time
t_{\phi}
.

If the Gibbs point process
P_{\mathds{V}}^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\lambda,\phi}}\right)}
is simple and
\lambda<\frac{\mathrm{e}}{C_{\phi}}
,
then, for every
\varepsilon\in\mathds{R}_{>0}
, there exists an
\varepsilon
-approximate sampling algorithm for
P_{\mathds{V}}^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\lambda,\phi}}\right)}
with running time in
\widetilde{\mathrm{O}}\big({\nu\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathds{V}}}\right)^{2}\varepsilon^{-4}+\nu\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathds{V}}}\right)^{2}\varepsilon^{-3}t_{\mathds{V}}+\nu\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathds{V}}}\right)^{4}\varepsilon^{-6}t_{\phi}}\big)
.

There are two main differences in the assumptions of the approximation result (
Theorem

1.2
) and the sampling result (
Theorem

1.3
).
First, the sampling result requires the point process to be simple.
The reason is that, in order to bound the total variation distance between the output of our sampler and
P_{\mathds{V}}^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\lambda,\phi}}\right)}
, we derive a density of that output with respect to a Poisson point process.
This task is greatly simplified by assuming that
P_{\mathds{V}}^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\lambda,\phi}}\right)}
is simple, as it allows for an easier characterization of the output distribution of our sampling, based on a theorem by Rényi–Mönch (see
[
13
, Theorem 9.2.XII]
).
However, assuming the point process to be simple is only a minor restriction, as it is satisfied for most applications of point processes.
For example, it is trivially satisfied if the reference volume measure
\nu
is not-atomic (i.e., assigns volume
0
to single points).
This includes the most frequently studied case of Gibbs point processes in Euclidean space but also a variety of other spaces, such as Gibbs point processes in hyperbolic spaces or in Riemannian manifolds.

Second, our sampling result requires efficient sampling from the uniform distribution
u_{\mathds{V}}
and an efficient way to compute the potential
\phi
.
In contrast to that,
Theorem

1.2
only assumes an efficient way to sample a graph from
\zeta^{(n)}_{\mathds{V},\phi}
.
We state the theorems in this way to emphasize that, for approximating
\Xi_{\mathds{V}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\lambda,\phi}}\right)
, we only need to sample from the random-graph model
\zeta^{(n)}_{\mathds{V},\phi}
.
Our sampling procedure additionally requires the position
x_{i}\in\mathds{V}
for each vertex
i\in[n]
along with the graph to output the point configuration, associated to a random independent set drawn from the hard-core model.

Last, we briefly discuss the origin of the fugacity bound
\frac{\mathrm{e}}{C_{\phi}}
in our algorithmic results.
Write
\gamma\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(n}}\right)=\frac{\lambda\nu\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathds{V}}}\right)}{n}
for every
n\in\mathds{N}_{\geq 1}
.
Note that our algorithms rely on either an efficient approximation of the hard-core partition function
Z_{G}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\gamma\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(n}}\right)}}\right)
or an efficient approximate sampler for an independent set from
\mu_{G}^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\gamma\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(n}}\right)}}\right)}
for a random graph
G\sim\zeta^{(n)}_{\mathds{V},\phi}
.
As discussed earlier, such computational results are known for general graphs of maximum degree
d
as long as the parameter
\gamma
is below the corresponding tree threshold
\gamma_{\text{c}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(d}}\right)
.
Observe that
\gamma_{\text{c}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(d}}\right)\approx\frac{\mathrm{e}}{d}
for large
d
.
Thus, roughly speaking, we can perform the necessary computational tasks as long as
\gamma\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(n}}\right)=\frac{\lambda\nu\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathds{V}}}\right)}{n}<\frac{\mathrm{e}}{d_{G}}
, where
d_{G}
is the maximum degree of the graph
G
that was drawn from
\zeta^{(n)}_{\mathds{V},\phi}
.
Equivalently, this is
\lambda<\frac{\mathrm{e}n}{d_{G}\nu\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathds{V}}}\right)}
.
The main observation is now that, for
G\sim\zeta^{(n)}_{\mathds{V},\phi}
, the expected degree of an arbitrary vertex of
G
is upper-bounded by
\frac{nC_{\phi}}{\nu\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathds{V}}}\right)}
.
By proving that, with sufficiently high probability, the maximum degree
d_{G}
is not much larger than this value, we obtain the desired bound of
\frac{\mathrm{e}}{C_{\phi}}
.

1.4
Strong spatial mixing, connective constants, and improved bounds

A property closely tied to the existence of efficient algorithms for the hard-core model is strong spatial mixing. Strong spatial mixing describes a particular way how dependencies between distant vertices in a graph
G
decay. The definition is easiest stated in terms of the occupation probability of a vertex
v\in V_{G}
(i.e., the probability that
v
is in the independent set, drawn from a hard-core model on
G
) conditioned on certain vertices being occupied or unoccupied. Given two such conditions that differ at some vertex set
S\subset V_{G}
, strong spatial mixing requires that the resulting difference in the occupation probability of every vertex
v
is exponentially small in the graph distance between
v
and
S
1
1

1

Often it is more convenient to work with the occupation ratio, which is the occupation probability divided by the probability of the vertex to be unoccupied. However, the resulting strong spatial mixing definitions are equivalent.
(see
Definition

6.1
and remark
Remark

6.2
for more details).

In a seminal paper, Weitz
[
47
]
proved that, for a graph
G
of maximum degree
d_{G}
and vertex
v\in V_{G}
, we can construct a tree with root
v
such that the occupation probability of
v
in the tree is the same as in
G
.
This continues to hold when conditioning on the state of other vertices by translating the condition to the tree appropriately. It follows that if this tree exhibits strong spatial mixing with respect to the root, then this property also holds for
G
. Moreover, in this case, a recursive computation on this tree can be used to approximate the occupation probability of
v
, which results in a sampling and approximation algorithm with running time
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|V_{G}}}\right|^{\mathrm{O}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({\log(d_{G})}}}\right)}
, for the hard-core model at vertex activity up to
\gamma_{\text{c}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(d_{G}}}\right)
on
G
.

Subsequently, this result was improved by a more refined analysis
[
42
,
40
]
. We elaborate further. The tree used by Weitz, which we refer to as the
Weitz tree
, is a truncated version of a self-avoiding walk tree
2
2

2

In
[
47
]
the tree is actually not truncated but certain vertices in the tree are fixed to be always occupied or unoccupied. However, this is equivalent to truncating the tree.
(see
Section

6.3
for a formal definition). This truncation accounts for the effect of cycles on the hard-core distribution in the original graph. In
[
42
,
40
]
, it was shown that strong spatial mixing results can be derived by bounding the connective constant
\Delta
, which describes the growth of the Weitz tree (see
Definition

6.4
). In particular, it was shown that strong spatial mixing applies up to a vertex activity of
\gamma<\gamma_{\text{c}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\Delta}}\right)
, which improves bounds derived from the maximum degree. Consequentially, this implies sampling and approximation algorithm for the hard-core model for this parameter regime with running time
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|V_{G}}}\right|^{\mathrm{O}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({\log(\Delta)}}}\right)}
.

Inspired by these results on the hard-core model, Michelen and Perkins
[
33
]
recently introduced the potential-weighted connective constant
\Delta_{\phi}
for repulsive Gibbs point processes (see
Section

6.1
for a formal definition).
It can be seen as an alternative to
C_{\phi}
that is more sensitive to the structure of the underlying space
\mathds{X}
.
In particular, for any non-trivial potential,
\Delta_{\phi}
is strictly smaller than the temperedness constant.
Moreover, it was shown in
[
34
]
that repulsive Gibbs point processes with bounded-range potentials exhibit a notion of strong spatial mixing up to a fugacity of
\lambda<\mathrm{e}/\Delta_{\phi}
(see
[
34
, Definition 1]
).
This result was used to derive a polynomial-time approximate sampling algorithm and a randomized approximation algorithm for the partition function of bounded-range repulsive Gibbs point processes in the same fugacity regime.

Given a repulsive Gibbs point process with fugacity
\lambda<\mathrm{e}/\Delta_{\phi}
, we show in
Section

6
that the hard-core model that is obtained from our reduction exhibits strong spatial mixing with high probability. In particular our result holds without bounded-range assumptions. We prove this by using the results in
[
42
,
40
]
. Towards this, we establish a rigorous connection between the potential-weighted connective constant of a repulsive Gibbs point process and the connective constant of a graph from
\zeta^{(n)}_{\mathds{V},\phi}
.
More precisely, we show that, for any
\epsilon>0
and
n\geq\upTheta\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({\nu\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathds{V}}}\right)}}}\right)
, the connective constant of a graph from
\zeta^{(n)}_{\mathds{V},\phi}
is bounded by
\mathrm{e}^{\epsilon}\frac{n}{\nu\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathds{V}}}\right)}\Delta_{\phi}
with probability at least
1-\frac{1}{n}
(see
Theorem

6.7
for the formal statement).
To obtain this result, we make use of the fact that the construction of the Weitz tree leaves some degree of freedom.
That is, for the same graph
G
and root vertex
v\in V_{G}
, different Weitz trees can be constructed, which differ in how the self-avoiding walk tree is truncated.
In our setting, we carefully need to choose the truncation based on the underlying location of vertices in
\mathds{V}
.
In particular, it is important for us to define the connective constant in terms of the Weitz tree and not the full self-avoiding walk tree.
The latter would only yield a bound of
\frac{n}{\nu\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathds{V}}}\right)}C_{\phi}
, which would be no improvement over the maximum degree of
G\sim\zeta^{(n)}_{\mathds{V},\phi}
.

Given the above graphical interpretation of
\Delta_{\phi}
, we immediately obtain that, for all
\lambda<\mathrm{e}/\Delta_{\phi}
, a hard-core model with vertex activity
\gamma(n)=\frac{\nu\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathds{V}}}\right)}{n}\lambda
exhibits strong spatial mixing on
G\sim\zeta^{(n)}_{\mathds{V},\phi}
with probability at least
1-\frac{1}{n}
(see
Corollary

6.10
for the formal statement).
This result holds for any repulsive potential, without bounded-range assumption.
However, it should be noted that the strong spatial mixing is with respect to the graph distance and not the distance metric of the underlying space
\mathds{X}
.

This strong spatial mixing result for
G\sim\zeta^{(n)}_{\mathds{V},\phi}
gives our reduction further algorithmic consequences.
Using the deterministic algorithm for approximating that partition function of a hard-core model proposed by Weitz
[
47
]
(see also
[
42
,
40
]
) and
Theorem

1.1
, our strong spatial mixing result yields a randomized approximation for the partition function of repulsive Gibbs point processes with arbitrary range potentials for
\lambda<\mathrm{e}/\Delta_{\phi}
with quasi-polynomial running time
\nu\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathds{V}}}\right)^{\mathrm{O}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({\ln(\nu\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathds{V}}}\right))}}}\right)}
.
A similar result can be derived in the setting of approximate sampling.

1.5
A more general concentration result: antiferromagnetic partition functions on graphon-based random graphs

So far, we discussed how concentration of hard-core partition functions
Z_{G}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\lambda\nu\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathds{V}}}\right)}{n}}}\right)
for random graphs
G\sim\zeta^{(n)}_{\mathds{V},\phi}
, stated in
Theorem

1.1
is obtained from
Corollary

3.3
.
However, as hinted earlier, a more general concentration result can be obtained for a large class of antiferromagnetic two-state spin systems on graphon-based random graph models.
As such spin systems have been studied extensively
[
28
,
41
,
43
]
, we believe this result to be of independent interest.
In what follows, we outline this more general concentration result and show how
Theorem

1.1
follows as a special case of it.

We start by introducing the class of spin systems to which it applies.
For an undirected graph
G=(V,E)
with vertices
V
and edges
E\subseteq\binom{V}{2}
, we denote by
\Sigma_{G}
the set of all functions
\sigma\colon V\to\{0,1\}
.
To simplify notation, we assume
V=[n]
for some
n\in\mathds{N}
.
A
two-state spin system
with parameters
\gamma,\beta_{0},\beta_{1}\in\mathds{R}_{\geq 0}
on
G
is a probability distribution
\mu_{G}^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\gamma,\beta_{0},\beta_{1}}}\right)}
on
\Sigma_{G}
with

\mu_{G}^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\gamma,\beta_{0},\beta_{1}}}\right)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sigma}}\right)=\frac{\gamma^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert\sigma}}\right\rvert_{1}}\beta_{0}^{m_{G}^{(0)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sigma}}\right)}\beta_{1}^{m_{G}^{(1)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sigma}}\right)}}{Z_{G}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\gamma,\beta_{0},\beta_{1}}}\right)},

where
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert\sigma}}\right\rvert_{1}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\sigma^{-1}(1)}}\right|
counts the number of vertices that are assigned to
1
,
m_{G}^{(a)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sigma}}\right)=\sum_{\{i,j\}\in E}\mathds{1}_{\sigma\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(i}}\right)=\sigma\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(j}}\right)=a}
counts the number of edges with both endpoints assigned to
a\in\{0,1\}
, and the normalizing constant
Z_{G}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\gamma,\beta_{0},\beta_{1}}}\right)
is the partition function

Z_{G}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\gamma,\beta_{0},\beta_{1}}}\right)=\sum\nolimits_{\sigma\in\Sigma_{G}}\gamma^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert\sigma}}\right\rvert_{1}}\beta_{0}^{m_{G}^{(0)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sigma}}\right)}\beta_{1}^{m_{G}^{(1)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sigma}}\right)}.

A two-state spin system is
antiferromagnetic
if
\beta_{0}\beta_{1}\leq 1
.
Our concentration result applies to antiferromagnetic two-state spin systems with
\beta_{0}=1
.
In this case, we omit
\beta_{0}
completely, write
\beta=\beta_{1}\in[0,1]
, and denote the partition function by
Z_{G}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\gamma,\beta}}\right)
.

Our concentration result for partition functions
Z_{G}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\gamma,\beta}}\right)
applies to all graphon-based random-graph models.
Here, we refer to graphons in the most general sense, as defined in
[
29
, Chapter
13
]
.
That is, for a probability space
\mathcal{X}=(X,\mathcal{A},\xi)
, a
graphon
is a symmetric function
W\colon X^{2}\to[0,1]
that is measurable with respect to the product algebra
\mathcal{A}^{2}=\mathcal{A}\otimes\mathcal{A}
.
Note that, even though we call the function
W
the graphon, we mean implicitly that a graphon is a tuple of an underlying probability space and a suitable function
W
.
One useful aspect of a graphon
W
is that it naturally defines a family of random-graph models, sometimes called
W
-random graphs (see
[
29
, Chapter
11
]
).
For every
n\in\mathds{N}_{\geq 1}
, we denote by
\mathds{G}_{W,n}
a distribution on undirected graphs with vertex set
[n]
that is induced by the following procedure for generating a random graph:

1.

Draw a tuple
(x_{1},\dots x_{n})\in X^{n}
according to the product distribution
\xi^{n}
.

2.

For all
i,j\in[n],i\neq j
, add the edge
\{i,j\}
independently with probability
W\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x_{i},x_{j}}}\right)
.

Observe that
\mathds{G}_{W,n}
encompasses classical random-graph models, such as Erdős–Rényi random graphs and geometric random graphs.

Applying
Corollary

3.3
and using essentially the same arguments as in our proof sketch for
Theorem

1.1
yields the following result.

Theorem 1.4
.

Let
W
be a graphon on the probability space
\mathcal{X}=(X,\mathcal{A},\xi)
.
Let
\gamma\colon\mathds{N}_{\geq 1}\to\mathds{R}_{\geq 0}
such that
\gamma\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(n}}\right)\leq\gamma_{0}n^{-\frac{1+\alpha}{2}}
for some
\gamma_{0}\in\mathds{R}_{\geq 0}
and
\alpha\in\mathds{R}_{>0}
.
For all
\beta\in[0,1]
,
\varepsilon\in(0,1]
,
\delta\in(0,1]
,
n\geq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(2\gamma_{0}^{2}\varepsilon^{-2}\delta^{-1}}}\right)^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}
, and
G\sim\mathds{G}_{W,n}
, it holds that

\mathrm{Pr}\big[{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|Z_{G}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\gamma\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(n}}\right),\beta}}\right)-\mathrm{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{Z_{G}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\gamma\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(n}}\right),\beta}}\right)}}}\right]}}\right|\geq\varepsilon\mathrm{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{Z_{G}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\gamma\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(n}}\right),\beta}}\right)}}}\right]}\big]\leq\delta.

Remark 1.5
.

In fact,
Theorem

1.4
can easily be extended to an even more general setting, where we consider a sequence of probability spaces
(\mathcal{X}_{n})_{n\in\mathds{N}}
and an associated sequence of graphons
(W_{n})_{n\in\mathds{N}}
(i.e., each
W_{n}
is a graphon on
\mathcal{X}_{n}
).
This makes the result for example applicable to popular models studied in network theory, such as hyperbolic random graphs
[
27
]
and geometric inhomogeneous random graphs
[
9
]
.

A surprising aspect of
Theorem

1.4
is that, even though
\mathrm{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{Z_{G}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\gamma\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(n}}\right),\beta}}\right)}}}\right]\geq 1+n\gamma\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(n}}\right)
diverges for
\gamma\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(n}}\right)\in\omega(n^{-1})
as
n
increases,
Theorem

1.4
still ensures that the distribution of the partition functions gets more and more concentrated as long as
\gamma\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(n}}\right)\in\mathrm{o}\big({n^{-\frac{1}{2}}}\big)
.

We derive
Theorem

1.1
as a special case of
Theorem

1.4
.
To see how this works, first observe that, for
\beta=0
, it holds that
Z_{G}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\gamma\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(n}}\right),\beta}}\right)
is the hard-core partition function of a graph
G
with parameter
\gamma\in\mathds{R}_{\geq 0}
.
Moreover, by setting
\mathcal{X}=(\mathds{V},\mathcal{B},u_{\mathds{V}})
, considering a graphon
W_{\phi}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x_{1},x_{2}}}\right)=1-\mathrm{e}^{-\phi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x_{1},x_{2}}}\right)}
,
x_{1},x_{2}\in\mathds{V}
, on
\mathcal{X}
, we obtain
\zeta^{(n)}_{\mathds{V},\phi}=\mathds{G}_{W_{\phi},n}
.
This way of expressing
\zeta^{(n)}_{\mathds{V},\phi}
establishes a connection between repulsive Gibbs point processes and hard-core models on graphon-based random graphs.
Lastly, setting
\gamma_{0}=\lambda\nu\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathds{V}}}\right)
and
\gamma\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(n}}\right)=\gamma_{0}n^{-1}
and applying
Theorem

1.4
yields the desired concentration result for hard-core partition functions on
\zeta^{(n)}_{\mathds{V},\phi}
.

1.6
Organization of the technical details

The technical details in the appendix are organized as follows.
In
Section

2
, we formally introduce the notion of antiferromagnetic spin systems and Gibbs point processes.
Note that for the latter one, we base our definition on the notion of random counting measures.
This is common in the theory of point processes but slightly differs from the definition given in the introduction.
In
Section

3
, we prove our concentration result for partition functions of antiferromagnetic spin systems on graphon-based random graphs.
We continue by showing in

how to efficiently approximate the partition functions of a Gibbs point process with arbitrary repulsive potentials via hard-core partition functions of random graphs based on a suitably constructed graphon. In

2
Preliminaries

We formally introduce the discrete antiferromagnetic spin systems we investigate, as well as Gibbs point processes.

2.1
Antiferromagnetic spin systems

For an undirected graph
G=(V,E)
with vertices
V
and edges
E\subseteq\binom{V}{2}
, we denote by
\Sigma_{G}
the set of all functions
\sigma:V\to\{0,1\}
.
Without loss of generality, we are going to assume the canonical vertex set
V=[n]
for some
n\in\mathds{N}
.
A
two-state spin system
with parameters
\gamma,\beta_{0},\beta_{1}\in\mathds{R}_{\geq 0}
on
G
is a probability distribution
\mu_{G}^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\gamma,\beta_{0},\beta_{1}}}\right)}
on
\Sigma_{G}
with

\mu_{G}^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\gamma,\beta_{0},\beta_{1}}}\right)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sigma}}\right)=\frac{\gamma^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert\sigma}}\right\rvert_{1}}\beta_{0}^{m_{G}^{(0)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sigma}}\right)}\beta_{1}^{m_{G}^{(1)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sigma}}\right)}}{Z_{G}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\gamma,\beta_{0},\beta_{1}}}\right)},

where
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert\sigma}}\right\rvert_{1}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\sigma^{-1}(1)}}\right|
counts the number of vertices assigned that are to
1
,
m_{G}^{(a)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sigma}}\right)=\sum_{\{i,j\}\in E}\mathds{1}_{\sigma\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(i}}\right)=\sigma\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(j}}\right)=a}
counts the number of edges with both endpoints assigned to
a\in\{0,1\}
and
Z_{G}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\gamma,\beta_{0},\beta_{1}}}\right)
is the normalizing constant

Z_{G}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\gamma,\beta_{0},\beta_{1}}}\right)=\sum_{\sigma\in\Sigma_{G}}\gamma^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert\sigma}}\right\rvert_{1}}\beta_{0}^{m_{G}^{(0)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sigma}}\right)}\beta_{1}^{m_{G}^{(1)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sigma}}\right)}.

Note that we implicitly assume
\beta_{0}\neq 0
or
\beta_{1}\neq 0
, as
\mu_{G}^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\gamma,\beta_{0},\beta_{1}}}\right)}
may not be defined otherwise.

Usually,
\mu_{G}^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\gamma,\beta_{0},\beta_{1}}}\right)}
is referred to as the
Gibbs distribution
of the model and
Z_{G}
is called the
partition function
.
Further, a two-state spin system is
antiferromagnetic
if
\beta_{0}\beta_{1}\leq 1
.
For our concentration result, we focus on the setting where
\beta_{0}=1
.
In this case, we omit
\beta_{0}
completely and write
\beta=\beta_{1}\in[0,1]
and denote the partition function by
Z_{G}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\gamma,\beta}}\right)
.
Of special interest within this class of antiferromagnetic two-state spin systems in the
hard-core model
, which results from setting
\beta=0
.
In this case, we might just omit the edge interactions
\beta
completely and write
\mu_{G}^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\gamma}}\right)}
and
Z_{G}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\gamma}}\right)
.
Note that this implies that only configurations
\sigma\in\Sigma_{G}
for which
\sigma^{-1}(1)
is an independent set in
G
can have non-zero probability.
For us, this model is especially relevant, as we show that concentration of hard-core partition functions on random graphs can be used to derive randomized approximations for the partition function of repulsive Gibbs point processes, which are introduced in the next section.

2.2
Gibbs point processes

We introduce the notion of Gibbs point processes that is used throughout this paper.
For a formal treatment, it is common to model point processes as random counting measures.
Note that this is different from the simplified definition that we gave in the introduction.
For a more detailed overview on the theory of point processes and specifically Gibbs point processes, see
[
25
]
.

Let
(\mathds{X},d)
be a complete, separable metric space and let
\mathcal{B}=\mathcal{B}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathds{X}}}\right)
be the Borel algebra of that space.
Let
\nu
be a locally finite reference measure on
(\mathds{X},\mathcal{B})
such that all bounded measurable sets have finite measure.
Denote by
\mathcal{N}
the set of all locally finite counting measures on
(\mathds{X},\mathcal{B})
.
Formally, this is the set of all measures
\eta
on
(\mathds{X},\mathcal{B})
with values in
\mathds{N}\cup\{\infty\}
such that
\nu\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(A}}\right)<\infty
implies
\eta\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(A}}\right)<\infty
for all
A\in\mathcal{B}
.
For each
A\in\mathcal{B}
, define a map
N_{A}:\mathcal{N}\to\mathds{N}\cup\{\infty\}
with
\eta\mapsto\eta\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(A}}\right)
and let
\mathcal{R}
be the sigma algebra on
\mathcal{N}
that is generated by the set of those maps
\{N_{A}\mid A\in\mathcal{B}\}
.
A
point process
on
\mathds{X}
is now a measurable map from some probability space to the measurable space
(\mathcal{N},\mathcal{R})
.
With some abuse of terminology, we call any probability distribution on
(\mathcal{N},\mathcal{R})
a point process, as we can only use the identity as measurable mapping from
\eta
to itself.
Moreover, a point process is call
simple
if
N_{x}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\eta}}\right)\leq 1
with probability
1
, where we write
N_{x}
for
N_{\{x\}}
.

Note that every counting measure
\eta\in\mathcal{N}
is associated with a multiset of points in
\mathds{X}
.
To see this, define
X_{\eta}=\{x\in\mathds{X}\mid N_{x}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\eta}}\right)>0\}
.
Then
\eta
can be expressed as a weighted sum of Dirac measures

\eta=\sum_{x\in X_{\eta}}N_{x}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\eta}}\right)\delta_{x}.

In this sense,
\eta
is associated with a multiset of points
x\in X_{\eta}
, each occurring with finite multiplicity
N_{x}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\eta}}\right)
.
We may use such a
point configuration
interchangeably with its corresponding counting measure.

An important example for point processes are Poisson point processes.
A
Poisson point process
with intensity
\kappa\in\mathds{R}_{\geq 0}
on
(\mathds{X},d)
is uniquely defined by the following properties

•

for all bounded measurable
A\subseteq\mathds{X}
it holds that
N_{A}
is Poisson distributed with intensity
\kappa\nu\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(A}}\right)
and

•

for all
m\in\mathds{N}_{\geq 2}
and disjoint measurable
A_{1},\dots,A_{m}\subseteq\mathds{X}
it holds that
N_{A_{1}},\dots,N_{A_{m}}
are independent.

Generally speaking, a
Gibbs point process
is a point process that is absolutely continuous with respect to a Poisson point process.
For a bounded measurable
\mathds{V}\subseteq\mathds{X}
let
\mathcal{N}_{\mathds{V}}
denote the set of locally finite counting measures
\eta\in\mathcal{N}
that satisfy
N_{A}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\eta}}\right)=0
for all measurable
A\subseteq\mathds{X}\setminus\mathds{V}
.
In this work we are interested in Gibbs point processes
P_{\mathds{V}}^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\lambda,\phi}}\right)}
on bounded measurable regions
\mathds{V}\subseteq\mathds{X}
that are parameterized by a
fugacity
parameter
\lambda\in\mathds{R}_{\geq 0}
and non-negative, symmetric, measurable
potential function

\phi:\mathds{X}^{2}\to\mathds{R}_{\geq 0}\cup\{\infty\}
.
Formally, such a process
P_{\mathds{V}}^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\lambda,\phi}}\right)}
is defined by having a density with respect to a Poisson point process with intensity
\lambda
of the form

\frac{\text{d}P_{\mathds{V}}^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\lambda,\phi}}\right)}}{\text{d}Q_{\lambda}}(\eta)=\frac{\mathds{1}_{\eta\in\mathcal{N}_{\mathds{V}}}\mathrm{e}^{-H\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\eta}}\right)}\mathrm{e}^{\lambda\nu\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathds{V}}}\right)}}{\Xi_{\mathds{V}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\lambda,\phi}}\right)}

where
H:\mathcal{N}\to\mathds{R}_{\geq 0}\cup\{\infty\}
is the
Hamiltonian
defined by

H\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\eta}}\right)=\sum_{\{x,y\}\in\binom{X_{\eta}}{2}}N_{x}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\eta}}\right)N_{y}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\eta}}\right)\phi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x,y}}\right)+\sum_{x\in X_{\eta}}\frac{N_{x}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\eta}}\right)(N_{x}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\eta}}\right)-1)}{2}\phi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x,x}}\right).

The normalizing constant
\Xi_{\mathds{V}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\lambda,\phi}}\right)
is usually called the
(grand-canonical) partition function
and can be written explicitly as

\displaystyle\Xi_{\mathds{V}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\lambda,\phi}}\right)

\displaystyle=1+\sum_{k\in\mathds{N}_{\geq 1}}\frac{\lambda^{k}}{k!}\int_{\mathds{V}^{k}}\mathrm{e}^{-H\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\delta_{x_{1}}+\dots+\delta_{x_{k}}}}\right)}\nu^{k}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\text{d}\bm{x}}}\right)

\displaystyle=1+\sum_{k\in\mathds{N}_{\geq 1}}\frac{\lambda^{k}}{k!}\int_{\mathds{V}^{k}}\prod_{\{i,j\}\in\binom{[k]}{2}}\mathrm{e}^{-\phi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x_{i},x_{j}}}\right)}\nu^{k}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\text{d}\bm{x}}}\right).

3
Concentration of partition functions of antiferromagnetic spin systems on graphon-based random graphs

The main tool we will use to derive our concentration bounds is the Efron–Stein inequality.
For
N\in\mathds{N}_{\geq 1}
let
\{(\Omega_{i},\mathcal{F}_{i},\mu_{i})\}_{i\in[N]}
be a collection of probability spaces and let
f:\Omega\to\mathds{R}
be a measurable function on the product space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mu)=\bigotimes_{i\in[N]}(\Omega_{i},\mathcal{F}_{i},\mu_{i})
.
For each
i\in[N]
define a function
\Delta_{i}^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(f}}\right)}:\Omega\times\Omega_{i}\to\mathds{R}_{\geq 0}
, where, for every
\bm{x}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{N})\in\Omega
and
y_{i}\in\Omega_{i}
, the value
\Delta_{i}^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(f}}\right)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\bm{x},y_{i}}}\right)
is defined as the squared difference in
f
that is caused by replacing
x_{i}
in
\bm{x}
with
y_{i}
.
Formally, this is
\Delta_{i}^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(f}}\right)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\bm{x},y_{i}}}\right)=(f(\bm{x})-f(\bm{y}))^{2}
where
\bm{y}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{i-1},y_{i},x_{i+1},\dots,x_{N})
.
The Efron–Stein inequality bounds the variance of
f
under
\mu
based on the local squared deviations
\Delta_{i}^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(f}}\right)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\bm{x},y_{i}}}\right)
.

Theorem 3.1
(Efron–Stein inequality
[
14
]
).

Let
\{(\Omega_{i},\mathcal{F}_{i},\mu_{i})\}_{i\in[N]}
be probability spaces with product space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mu)=\bigotimes_{i\in[N]}(\Omega_{i},\mathcal{F}_{i},\mu_{i})
.
For every
\mathcal{F}
-measurable function
f:\Omega\to\mathds{R}
it holds that

\mathrm{Var}_{\mu}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{f}}}\right]\leq\frac{1}{2}\sum\nolimits_{i\in[N]}\mathrm{E}_{\mu\times\mu_{i}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{\Delta_{i}^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(f}}\right)}}}}\right].

Remark 3.2
.

The Efron–Stein inequality is usually stated for functions of independent real-valued random variables. However, it extends to functions on products of arbitrary probability spaces.

Theorem

3.1
immediately gives a concentration result for
f
whenever
\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i\in[N]}\mathrm{E}_{\mu\times\mu_{i}}\big[{\Delta_{i}^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(f}}\right)}}\big]
is of order of magnitude
\mathrm{E}_{\mu}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{f}}}\right]^{2}
by using Chebyshev’s inequality.
However, obtaining such a bound might turn out difficult, especially if
\mathrm{E}_{\mu}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{f}}}\right]
is hard to compute explicitly.
For our setting, we derive the following corollary of
Theorem

3.1
.

Corollary 3.3
.

[Corollary of the Efron–Stein inequality]

Let
\{(\Omega_{i},\mathcal{F}_{i},\mu_{i})\}_{i\in[N]}
be
probability spaces with product space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mu)=\bigotimes_{i\in[N]}(\Omega_{i},\mathcal{F}_{i},\mu_{i})
, and let
f\colon\Omega\to\mathds{R}
be an
\mathcal{F}
-measurable function.
Assume that there are
c_{i}\in\mathds{R}_{\geq 0}
for
i\in[N]
such that
C\coloneqq\sum_{i\in[N]}c_{i}^{2}<2
and, for all
\bm{x}=(x_{j})_{j\in[N]}\in\Omega
and
\bm{y}=(y_{j})_{j\in[N]}\in\Omega
that disagree only at position
i
, it holds that

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|f(\bm{x})-f(\bm{y})}}\right|\leq c_{i}\cdot\min\{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|f(\bm{x})}}\right|,\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|f(\bm{y})}}\right|\}.

Then, for all
\varepsilon\in\mathds{R}_{>0}
, it holds that

\mathrm{Pr}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|f-\mathrm{E}_{\mu}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{f}}}\right]}}\right|\geq\varepsilon\mathrm{E}_{\mu}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{f}}}\right]}}}\right]\leq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{2}{2-C}-1}}\right)\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}.

Proof.

First, we observe that
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|f(\bm{x})-f(\bm{y})}}\right|\leq c_{i}\min\{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|f(\bm{x})}}\right|,\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|f(\bm{y})}}\right|\}\leq c_{i}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|f(\bm{x})}}\right|
implies
\mathrm{E}_{\mu\times\mu_{i}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{\Delta_{i}^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(f}}\right)}}}}\right]\leq c_{i}^{2}\mathrm{E}_{\mu}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{f^{2}}}}\right]
for all
i\in[N]
.
Thus, by
Theorem

3.1
, we have
\mathrm{Var}_{\mu}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{f}}}\right]\leq\frac{C}{2}\mathrm{E}_{\mu}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{f^{2}}}}\right]
.
Now, recall that by definition
\mathrm{Var}_{\mu}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{f}}}\right]=\mathrm{E}_{\mu}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{f^{2}}}}\right]-\mathrm{E}_{\mu}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{f}}}\right]^{2}
, which implies
\mathrm{E}_{\mu}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{f^{2}}}}\right]-\mathrm{E}_{\mu}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{f}}}\right]^{2}\leq\frac{C}{2}\mathrm{E}_{\mu}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{f^{2}}}}\right]
.
Rearranging for
\mathrm{E}_{\mu}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{f^{2}}}}\right]
and using the fact that
\frac{C}{2}<1
yields
\mathrm{E}_{\mu}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{f^{2}}}}\right]\leq\frac{2}{2-C}\mathrm{E}_{\mu}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{f}}}\right]^{2}
.
Substituting this back into the definition of the variance, we obtain

\mathrm{Var}_{\mu}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{f}}}\right]\leq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{2}{2-C}-1}}\right)\mathrm{E}_{\mu}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{f}}}\right]^{2}.

The claim follows immediately by applying Chebyshev’s inequality.
∎

Remark 3.4
.

Usually, we want to characterize concentration asymptotically in
N
.
In this setting,
Corollary

3.3
tells us that, if
c_{i}\in\mathrm{O}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({N^{-\frac{1+\alpha}{2}}}}}\right)
for all
i\in[N]
and some
\alpha>0
, then, for all
\varepsilon\in\mathds{R}_{>0}
and
\delta\in(0,1]
such that
\varepsilon^{2}\delta<1
, it is sufficient to choose
N\in\upTheta\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({\delta^{-\frac{1}{\alpha}}\varepsilon^{-\frac{2}{\alpha}}}}}\right)
to ensure

\mathrm{Pr}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|f-\mathrm{E}_{\mu}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{f}}}\right]}}\right|\geq\varepsilon\mathrm{E}_{\mu}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{f}}}\right]}}}\right]\leq\delta.

We are now ready to use
Corollary

3.3
and derive a concentration result for the partition functions of antiferromagnetic two-state spin systems for graphon-based random graph model.

Let us recall the definition of graphons and graphon-based random graphs that we are using (see
[
29
, Chapter
10
&
13
]
).
Let
\mathcal{X}=(X,\mathcal{A},\xi)
be a probability space.
A graphon on
\mathcal{X}
is a symmetric function
W:X^{2}\to[0,1]
that is measurable with respect to the product algebra
\mathcal{A}^{2}=\mathcal{A}\otimes\mathcal{A}
.
For
n\in\mathds{N}_{\geq 1}
we denote by
\mathcal{G}_{n}
the set of all graphs on the canonical vertex set
[n]=\{1,\dots,n\}
.
Note that each graph in
\mathcal{G}_{n}
is fully characterized by its edge set
E
.
For every
n\in\mathds{N}_{\geq 1}
the random graph model induced by a graphon
W
on a probability space
(X,\mathcal{A},\xi)
is described by generating a random graph
G=([n],E)
by

•

drawing a tuple
(x_{1},\dots x_{n})\in X^{n}
according to the product distribution
\xi^{n}
and

•

adding the edge
\{i,j\}
for all
i,j\in[n],i\neq j
independently with probability
W\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x_{i},x_{j}}}\right)
.

Formally, this gives a probability distribution
\mathds{G}_{W,n}
on
\mathcal{G}_{n}
with

\mathds{G}_{W,n}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(G}}\right)=\mathop{\text{\Large$\int_{\text{\normalsize$\scriptstyle X^{n}$}}$}}\nolimits\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\prod_{\{i,j\}\in E}W\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x_{i},x_{j}}}\right)}}\right)\cdot\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\prod_{\{i,j\}\in\binom{[n]}{2}\setminus E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-W\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x_{i},x_{j}}}\right)}}\right)}}\right)\xi^{n}(\text{d}\bm{x})

for all
G\in\mathcal{G}_{n}
, where
\bm{x}=(x_{i})_{i\in[n]}
inside the integral.

To apply
Corollary

3.3
to partition functions on random graphs from
\mathds{G}_{W,n}
, we will need to bound how much the partition function changes when applying small modifications to the structure of a graph.
More specifically, we want to get a bound on the relative change of the partition function, given that we

•

add or remove a single edge, or

•

add or remove a set of edges that are all incident to the same vertex.

The following two lemmas provide such bounds.

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
G=(V,E)
be an undirected graph and, for any
e\in E
let
G^{\prime}=(V,E\setminus\{e\})
.
For all
\gamma\in\mathds{R}_{\geq 0}
and
\beta\in[0,1]
it holds that

0\leq Z_{G^{\prime}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\gamma,\beta}}\right)-Z_{G}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\gamma,\beta}}\right)\leq\gamma^{2}Z_{G}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\gamma,\beta}}\right)

and especially

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|Z_{G^{\prime}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\gamma,\beta}}\right)-Z_{G}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\gamma,\beta}}\right)}}\right|\leq\gamma^{2}\min\{Z_{G}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\gamma,\beta}}\right),Z_{G^{\prime}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\gamma,\beta}}\right)\}.

Proof.

Without loss of generality, assume
V=[n]
for some
n\in\mathds{N}_{\geq 2}
and let
e=\{i,j\}
for
i,j\in[n]
.
Note that
\Sigma_{G}=\Sigma_{G^{\prime}}
, as their vertex sets are identical.
Further, observe that, for all
\sigma\in\Sigma_{G}
, it holds that
m_{G}^{(1)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sigma}}\right)\geq m_{G^{\prime}}^{(1)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sigma}}\right)
.
Thus, we have
\beta^{m_{G}^{(1)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sigma}}\right)}\leq\beta^{m_{G^{\prime}}^{(1)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sigma}}\right)}
and
Z_{G}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\gamma,\beta}}\right)\leq Z_{G^{\prime}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\gamma,\beta}}\right)
, which proves

0\leq Z_{G^{\prime}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\gamma,\beta}}\right)-Z_{G}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\gamma,\beta}}\right).

We proceed by rewriting the partition function of
G^{\prime}
as

Z_{G^{\prime}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\gamma,\beta}}\right)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\sigma\in\Sigma_{G^{\prime}}:\\
\sigma\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(i}}\right)=0\text{ or }\sigma\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(j}}\right)=0\end{subarray}}\gamma^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert\sigma}}\right\rvert_{1}}\beta^{m_{G^{\prime}}^{(1)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sigma}}\right)}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\sigma\in\Sigma_{G^{\prime}}:\\
\sigma\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(i}}\right)=\sigma\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(j}}\right)=1\end{subarray}}\gamma^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert\sigma}}\right\rvert_{1}}\beta^{m_{G^{\prime}}^{(1)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sigma}}\right)}.

Observe that

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\sigma\in\Sigma_{G^{\prime}}:\\
\sigma\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(i}}\right)=0\text{ or }\sigma\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(j}}\right)=0\end{subarray}}\gamma^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert\sigma}}\right\rvert_{1}}\beta^{m_{G^{\prime}}^{(1)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sigma}}\right)}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\sigma\in\Sigma_{G}:\\
\sigma\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(i}}\right)=0\text{ or }\sigma\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(j}}\right)=0\end{subarray}}\gamma^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert\sigma}}\right\rvert_{1}}\beta^{m_{G}^{(1)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sigma}}\right)}\leq Z_{G}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\gamma,\beta}}\right).

For every
k\in[n]
, let
N_{G^{\prime}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(k}}\right)
denote the neighbors of vertex
k
in
G^{\prime}
.
We have

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\sigma\in\Sigma_{G^{\prime}}:\\
\sigma\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(i}}\right)=\sigma\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(j}}\right)=1\end{subarray}}\gamma^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert\sigma}}\right\rvert_{1}}\beta^{m_{G^{\prime}}^{(1)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sigma}}\right)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\sigma\in\Sigma_{G^{\prime}}:\\
\sigma\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(i}}\right)=\sigma\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(j}}\right)=0\end{subarray}}\gamma^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert\sigma}}\right\rvert_{1}+2}\beta^{m_{G^{\prime}}^{(1)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sigma}}\right)}\beta^{\sum_{k\in N_{G^{\prime}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(i}}\right)}\sigma\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(k}}\right)}\beta^{\sum_{k\in N_{G^{\prime}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(j}}\right)}\sigma\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(k}}\right)}

\displaystyle\leq\gamma^{2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\sigma\in\Sigma_{G^{\prime}}:\\
\sigma\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(i}}\right)=\sigma\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(j}}\right)=0\end{subarray}}\gamma^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert\sigma}}\right\rvert_{1}}\beta^{m_{G^{\prime}}^{(1)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sigma}}\right)}

\displaystyle=\gamma^{2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\sigma\in\Sigma_{G}:\\
\sigma\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(i}}\right)=\sigma\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(j}}\right)=0\end{subarray}}\gamma^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert\sigma}}\right\rvert_{1}}\beta^{m_{G}^{(1)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sigma}}\right)}

\displaystyle\leq\gamma^{2}Z_{G}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\gamma,\beta}}\right).

We conclude that
Z_{G^{\prime}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\gamma,\beta}}\right)\leq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1+\gamma^{2}}}\right)Z_{G}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\gamma,\beta}}\right)
and thus

Z_{G^{\prime}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\gamma,\beta}}\right)-Z_{G}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\gamma,\beta}}\right)\leq\gamma^{2}Z_{G}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\gamma,\beta}}\right).

The upper bound on
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|Z_{G^{\prime}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\gamma,\beta}}\right)-Z_{G}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\gamma,\beta}}\right)}}\right|
follows immediately.
∎

Lemma 3.6
.

Let
G=(V,E)
be an undirected graph and without loss of generality assume
V=[n]
for
n\in\mathds{N}
.
Let
E_{H},E_{H^{\prime}}\subseteq\{\{n+1,i\}\mid i\in[n]\}
, and set
H=([n+1],E\cup E_{H})
and
H^{\prime}=([n+1],E\cup E_{H^{\prime}})
.
For all
\gamma\in\mathds{R}_{\geq 0}
and
\beta\in[0,1]
it holds that

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|Z_{H}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\gamma,\beta}}\right)-Z_{H^{\prime}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\gamma,\beta}}\right)}}\right|\leq\gamma Z_{G}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\gamma,\beta}}\right)\leq\gamma\min\{Z_{H}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\gamma,\beta}}\right),Z_{H^{\prime}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\gamma,\beta}}\right)\}.

Proof.

By
Lemma

3.5
, we know that removing an edge from a graph doesn’t decrease the partition function.
Thus,
Z_{H}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\gamma,\beta}}\right)
is maximized by choosing
E_{H}=\emptyset
and minimized by choosing
E_{H}=\{\{n+1,i\}\mid i\in[n]\}
.
Consequently, we have

Z_{H}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\gamma,\beta}}\right)\leq(1+\gamma)Z_{G}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\gamma,\beta}}\right)

and

Z_{H}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\gamma,\beta}}\right)\geq Z_{G}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\gamma,\beta}}\right)+\gamma\geq Z_{G}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\gamma,\beta}}\right).

As the same holds for
H^{\prime}
, we obtain

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|Z_{H}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\gamma,\beta}}\right)-Z_{H^{\prime}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\gamma,\beta}}\right)}}\right|\leq\gamma Z_{G}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\gamma,\beta}}\right)

and the claim follows by noting that
Z_{G}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\gamma,\beta}}\right)\leq\min\{Z_{H}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\gamma,\beta}}\right),Z_{H^{\prime}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\gamma,\beta}}\right)\}
.
∎

Based on
Lemmas

3.6
and
3.5
, we use
Corollary

3.3
to prove the following statement.

Theorem 3.7
.

Let
W
be a graphon on the probability space
\mathcal{X}=(X,\mathcal{A},\xi)
.
Let
\gamma:\mathds{N}_{\geq 1}\to\mathds{R}_{\geq 0}
such that
\gamma\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(n}}\right)\leq\gamma_{0}n^{-\frac{1+\alpha}{2}}
for some
\gamma_{0}\in\mathds{R}_{\geq 0}
and
\alpha\in\mathds{R}_{>0}
.
For all
\beta\in[0,1]
,
\varepsilon\in\mathds{R}_{>0}
,
n>\gamma_{0}^{\frac{2}{\alpha}}
and
G\sim\mathds{G}_{W,n}
it holds that

\mathrm{Pr}\big[{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|Z_{G}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\gamma\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(n}}\right),\beta}}\right)-\mathrm{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{Z_{G}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\gamma\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(n}}\right),\beta}}\right)}}}\right]}}\right|\geq\varepsilon\mathrm{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{Z_{G}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\gamma\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(n}}\right),\beta}}\right)}}}\right]}\big]\leq\frac{\gamma_{0}^{2}}{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(n^{\alpha}-\gamma_{0}^{2}}}\right)\varepsilon^{2}}.

Proof.

We aim for applying
Corollary

3.3
to prove our claim.
To this end, for each
n\in\mathds{N}_{\geq 1}
we need to write the partition function
Z_{G}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\gamma\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(n}}\right),\beta}}\right)
for
G\sim\mathds{G}_{W,n}
as a function on a product of
\sigma
-finite probability spaces.
At first, an obvious choice seems to be
\mathcal{X}^{n}
together with
\binom{n}{2}
additional binary random variables, one for each potential edge
\{i,j\}\in\binom{[n]}{2}
.
However, note that the edges might not necessarily be independent, meaning that the resulting product distribution would not resemble
\mathds{G}_{W,n}
.
Instead, let
\mathcal{Y}=([0,1],\mathcal{B}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left([0,1]}}\right),u)
, where
\mathcal{B}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left([0,1]}}\right)
is the Borel algebra restricted to
[0,1]
and
u
is the uniform distribution on that interval.
We consider the probability space
\mathcal{X}^{n}\otimes\mathcal{Y}^{\binom{n}{2}}
.

For
\bm{x}\in X^{n}
and
\bm{y}\in[0,1]^{\binom{n}{2}}
let
\bm{x}\circ\bm{y}\in X^{n}\times[0,1]^{\binom{n}{2}}
denote the concatenation of
\bm{x}
and
\bm{y}
.
We construct a measurable function
g:X^{n}\times[0,1]^{\binom{n}{2}}\to\mathcal{G}_{n}
by mapping every
\bm{z}=\bm{x}\circ\bm{y}\in X^{n}\times[0,1]^{\binom{n}{2}}
with
\bm{x}=(x_{i})_{i\in[n]}\in X^{n}
and
\bm{y}=(y_{i,j})_{1\leq i<j\leq n}\in[0,1]^{\binom{n}{2}}
to
g(\bm{z})=([n],E)
such that, for all
i<j
, it holds that
\{i,j\}\in E
if and only if
W\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x_{i},x_{j}}}\right)\geq y_{i,j}
.

4
Application to repulsive Gibbs point processes

We use our concentration results for antiferromagnetic spin systems to relate repulsive Gibbs point processes to a hard-core model on carefully constructed classes of random graphs.
To this end, let
(\mathds{X},d)
be a complete, separable metric space, let
\mathcal{B}=\mathcal{B}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathds{X}}}\right)
be the Borel algebra and let
\nu
be a locally finite reference measure on
(\mathds{X},\mathcal{B})
.
For every bounded and measurable
\mathds{V}\subseteq\mathds{X}
we define a probability space
\mathcal{X}_{\mathds{V}}=(\mathds{V},\mathcal{B}_{\mathds{V}},u_{\mathds{V}})
, where
\mathcal{B}_{\mathds{V}}
denotes the restriction of
\mathcal{B}
to
\mathds{V}
and
u_{\mathds{V}}
is the probability measure on
(\mathds{V},\mathcal{B}_{\mathds{V}})
that is defined via the constant density
\frac{1}{\nu\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathds{V}}}\right)}
with respect to
\nu
restricted to
\mathds{V}
.
For every symmetric, repulsive and measurable pair potential function
\phi:\mathds{X}^{2}\to\mathds{R}_{\geq 0}\cup\{\infty\}
and all
n\in\mathds{N}_{\geq 1}
.
Define
W_{\phi}:\mathds{V}^{2}\to[0,1]
with
W_{\phi}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x,y}}\right)=1-\mathrm{e}^{-\phi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x,y}}\right)}
and observe that
W_{\phi}
is a graphon on
\mathcal{X}_{\mathds{V}}
. We proceed by considering the random graph model
\zeta^{(n)}_{\mathds{V},\phi}=\mathds{G}_{W_{\phi},n}
.

The following lemma relates the expected hard-core partition function on
\zeta^{(n)}_{\mathds{V},\phi}
with the partition function of the continuous Gibbs point process
\Xi_{\mathds{V}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\lambda,\phi}}\right)
.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
(\mathds{X},d)
be a complete separable metric space, let
\mathcal{B}=\mathcal{B}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathds{X}}}\right)
be the Borel algebra and let
\nu
be a locally finite reference measure on
(\mathds{X},\mathcal{B})
.
Let
\mathds{V}\subseteq\mathds{X}
be bounded and measurable, let
\lambda\in\mathds{R}_{\geq 0}
and let
\phi:\mathds{X}^{2}\to\mathds{R}_{\geq 0}\cup\{\infty\}
be a symmetric repulsive potential.
For all
\varepsilon\in\mathds{R}_{>0}
and
n\geq 2\varepsilon^{-1}\max\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\mathrm{e}^{6}\lambda^{2}\nu\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathds{V}}}\right)^{2},\ln\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(2\varepsilon^{-1}}}\right)^{2}}}\right\}
it holds that

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-\varepsilon}}\right)\Xi_{\mathds{V}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\lambda,\phi}}\right)\leq\mathrm{E}_{G\sim\zeta^{(n)}_{\mathds{V},\phi}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{Z_{G}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\lambda\nu\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathds{V}}}\right)}{n}}}\right)}}}\right]\leq\Xi_{\mathds{V}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\lambda,\phi}}\right).

Proof.

We start by rewriting the hard-core partition function as

Z_{G}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\lambda\nu\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathds{V}}}\right)}{n}}}\right)=1+\sum_{k=1}^{n}\lambda^{k}\frac{\nu\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathds{V}}}\right)^{k}}{n^{k}}\sum_{S\in\binom{[n]}{k}}\prod_{\{i,j\}\in\binom{S}{2}}\mathds{1}_{\{i,j\}\notin E}.

Thus, by linearity of expectation we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{E}_{G\sim\zeta^{(n)}_{\mathds{V},\phi}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{Z_{G}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\lambda\nu\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathds{V}}}\right)}{n}}}\right)}}}\right]

\displaystyle=1+\sum_{k=1}^{n}\lambda^{k}\frac{\nu\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathds{V}}}\right)^{k}}{n^{k}}\sum_{S\in\binom{[n]}{k}}\mathrm{E}_{G\sim\zeta^{(n)}_{\mathds{V},\phi}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{\prod_{\{i,j\}\in\binom{S}{2}}\mathds{1}_{\{i,j\}\notin E}}}}\right]

\displaystyle=1+\sum_{k=1}^{n}\lambda^{k}\frac{\nu\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathds{V}}}\right)^{k}}{n^{k}}\sum_{S\in\binom{[n]}{k}}\mathrm{Pr}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{\bigwedge_{\{i,j\}\in\binom{S}{2}}\{i,j\}\notin E}}}\right].

Next, observe that for all
S\in\binom{[n]}{k}
with
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|S}}\right|=k

\displaystyle\mathrm{Pr}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{\bigwedge_{\{i,j\}\in\binom{S}{2}}\{i,j\}\notin E}}}\right]

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathds{V}^{n}}\prod_{\{i,j\}\in\binom{S}{2}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-W_{\phi}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x_{i},x_{j}}}\right)}}\right)u^{n}_{\mathds{V}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\text{d}\bm{x}}}\right)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathds{V}^{n}}\prod_{\{i,j\}\in\binom{S}{2}}\mathrm{e}^{-\phi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x_{i},x_{j}}}\right)}u^{n}_{\mathds{V}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\text{d}\bm{x}}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\nu\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathds{V}}}\right)^{n}}\int_{\mathds{V}^{n}}\prod_{\{i,j\}\in\binom{S}{2}}\mathrm{e}^{-\phi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x_{i},x_{j}}}\right)}\nu^{n}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\text{d}\bm{x}}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\nu\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathds{V}}}\right)^{n-k}}{\nu\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathds{V}}}\right)^{n}}\int_{\mathds{V}^{k}}\prod_{\{i,j\}\in\binom{[k]}{2}}\mathrm{e}^{-\phi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x_{i},x_{j}}}\right)}\nu^{k}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\text{d}\bm{x}}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\nu\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathds{V}}}\right)^{k}}\int_{\mathds{V}^{k}}\prod_{\{i,j\}\in\binom{[k]}{2}}\mathrm{e}^{-\phi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x_{i},x_{j}}}\right)}\nu^{k}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\text{d}\bm{x}}}\right).

This yields

\displaystyle\mathrm{E}_{G\sim\zeta^{(n)}_{\mathds{V},\phi}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{Z_{G}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\lambda\nu\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathds{V}}}\right)}{n}}}\right)}}}\right]

\displaystyle=1+\sum_{k=1}^{n}\lambda^{k}\frac{1}{n^{k}}\sum_{S\in\binom{[n]}{k}}\int_{\mathds{V}^{k}}\prod_{\{i,j\}\in\binom{[k]}{2}}\mathrm{e}^{-\phi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x_{i},x_{j}}}\right)}\nu^{k}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\text{d}\bm{x}}}\right)

\displaystyle=1+\sum_{k=1}^{n}\lambda^{k}\frac{\binom{n}{k}}{n^{k}}\int_{\mathds{V}^{k}}\prod_{\{i,j\}\in\binom{[k]}{2}}\mathrm{e}^{-\phi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x_{i},x_{j}}}\right)}\nu^{k}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\text{d}\bm{x}}}\right)

\displaystyle=1+\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{\lambda^{k}}{k!}\prod_{i=0}^{k-1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-\frac{i}{n}}}\right)\int_{\mathds{V}^{k}}\prod_{\{i,j\}\in\binom{[k]}{2}}\mathrm{e}^{-\phi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x_{i},x_{j}}}\right)}\nu^{k}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\text{d}\bm{x}}}\right),

from which the upper bound

\mathrm{E}_{G\sim\zeta^{(n)}_{\mathds{V},\phi}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{Z_{G}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\lambda\nu\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathds{V}}}\right)}{n}}}\right)}}}\right]\leq\Xi_{\mathds{V}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\lambda,\phi}}\right)

follows immediately.

For the lower bound set

S_{m}=1+\sum_{k=1}^{m}\frac{\lambda^{k}}{k!}\int_{\mathds{V}^{k}}\prod_{\{i,j\}\in\binom{[k]}{2}}\mathrm{e}^{-\phi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x_{i},x_{j}}}\right)}\nu^{k}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\text{d}\bm{x}}}\right)

for any
1\leq m\leq n
.
Observe that

\mathrm{E}_{G\sim\zeta^{(n)}_{\mathds{V},\phi}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{Z_{G}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\lambda\nu\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathds{V}}}\right)}{n}}}\right)}}}\right]\geq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-\frac{m}{n}}}\right)^{m}S_{m}.

Thus, for
n\geq 2\varepsilon^{-1}m^{2}
Bernoulli’s inequality yields

\mathrm{E}_{G\sim\zeta^{(n)}_{\mathds{V},\phi}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{Z_{G}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\lambda\nu\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathds{V}}}\right)}{n}}}\right)}}}\right]\geq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-\frac{m^{2}}{n}}}\right)S_{m}\geq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}}}\right)S_{m}.

Furthermore, note that

\displaystyle\Xi_{\mathds{V}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\lambda,\phi}}\right)-S_{m}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=m+1}^{\infty}\frac{\lambda^{k}}{k!}\int_{\mathds{V}^{k}}\prod_{\{i,j\}\in\binom{[k]}{2}}\mathrm{e}^{-\phi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x_{i},x_{j}}}\right)}\nu^{k}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\text{d}\bm{x}}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k=m+1}^{\infty}\frac{\lambda^{k}\nu\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathds{V}}}\right)^{k}}{k!},

where the last inequality comes from the fact that
\phi
is non-negative.
Next, observe that this is equal to the error of the Taylor expansion of
\mathrm{e}^{\lambda\nu\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathds{V}}}\right)}
around
0
, truncated after
m
terms.
Thus, by Lagrange’s remainder formula, we obtain

\Xi_{\mathds{V}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\lambda,\phi}}\right)-S_{m}\leq\frac{\mathrm{e}^{\lambda\nu\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathds{V}}}\right)}}{(m+1)!}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\lambda\nu\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathds{V}}}\right)}}\right)^{m+1}.

Choosing
m\geq\max\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\mathrm{e}^{3}\lambda\nu\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathds{V}}}\right),\ln\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(2\varepsilon^{-1}}}\right)}}\right\}
and using the fact that
(m+1)!>\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{m+1}{\mathrm{e}}}}\right)^{m+1}
yields

\Xi_{\mathds{V}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\lambda,\phi}}\right)-S_{m}\leq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\mathrm{e}^{2}\lambda\nu\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathds{V}}}\right)}{m+1}}}\right)^{m+1}\leq\mathrm{e}^{-(m+1)}\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{2}.

As
\Xi_{\mathds{V}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\lambda,\phi}}\right)\geq 1
, we get

S_{m}\geq\Xi_{\mathds{V}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\lambda,\phi}}\right)-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\geq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}}}\right)\Xi_{\mathds{V}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\lambda,\phi}}\right).

For
n\geq 2\varepsilon^{-1}m^{2}=2\varepsilon^{-1}\max\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\mathrm{e}^{6}\lambda^{2}\nu\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathds{V}}}\right)^{2},\ln\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(2\varepsilon^{-1}}}\right)^{2}}}\right\}
we obtain

\mathrm{E}_{G\sim\zeta^{(n)}_{\mathds{V},\phi}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{Z_{G}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\lambda\nu\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathds{V}}}\right)}{n}}}\right)}}}\right]\geq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-\frac{m}{n}}}\right)^{m}S_{m}\geq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}}}\right)^{2}\Xi_{\mathds{V}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\lambda,\phi}}\right)\geq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-\varepsilon}}\right)\Xi_{\mathds{V}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\lambda,\phi}}\right),

which proves the claim.
∎

See

Proof.

By setting
\alpha=1
and

and using the fact that

4.1
Approximating the partition function

One of the main applications of
Theorem

1.1
is that it yields a rather simple randomized procedure for approximating
\Xi_{\mathds{V}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\lambda,\phi}}\right)
.
The rough idea is as follows:

1.

For
n\in\mathds{N}
sufficiently large, sample a graph
G
from
\zeta^{(n)}_{\mathds{V},\phi}
.

2.

Approximate
Z_{G}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\lambda\nu\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathds{V}}}\right)}{n}}}\right)
and use the result as an approximation for
\Xi_{\mathds{V}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\lambda,\phi}}\right)
.

We are especially interested in obtaining an algorithm that is asymptotically efficient in the volume
\nu\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathds{V}}}\right)
, as this gives a natural way to parameterize the algorithmic problem.
More specifically, we want to characterize the regime of the fugacity
\lambda
in terms of the potential
\phi
for which we can get a randomized
\varepsilon
-approximation of
\Xi_{\mathds{V}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\lambda,\phi}}\right)
in time polynomial in
\nu\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathds{V}}}\right)
and
\frac{1}{\varepsilon}
.
We characterize this fugacity regime in terms of the temperedness constant

C_{\phi}=\esssup_{x_{1}\in\mathds{X}}\int_{\mathds{X}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|1-\mathrm{e}^{-\phi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x_{1},x_{2}}}\right)}}}\right|\nu\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\text{d}x_{2}}}\right),

where
\esssup
denotes the essential supremum (i.e., an upper bound that holds almost everywhere).

In order to ensure that the approximation algorithm runs efficiently in
\nu\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathds{V}}}\right)
, two ingredients are important.
First, we need to bound how large
n
needs to be chosen to ensure that
Z_{G}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\lambda\nu\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathds{V}}}\right)}{n}}}\right)
is close to
\Xi_{\mathds{V}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\lambda,\phi}}\right)
with high probability.
Second, we need to ensure that
Z_{G}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\lambda\nu\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathds{V}}}\right)}{n}}}\right)
can be approximated in time polynomial in
\nu\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathds{V}}}\right)
.
Obviously, both requirements are satisfied if
n\in\mathrm{poly}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({\nu\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathds{V}}}\right)}}}\right)
is sufficient and if
Z_{G}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\lambda\nu\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathds{V}}}\right)}{n}}}\right)
can be approximated in time
\mathrm{poly}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({n}}}\right)
.
To tackle the first part,
Theorem

1.1
gives a useful tool.
For the second part, we will use some well known results on approximating the hard-core partition function.

Theorem 4.2
(
[
44
, Corollary
8.4
]
and
[
3

]
).

Let
G=(V,E)
be an undirected graph with maximum vertex degree bounded by

and let

with

\gamma<\gamma_{\text{c}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(d_{G}}}\right)=\frac{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(d_{G}-1}}\right)^{d_{G}-1}}{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(d_{G}-2}}\right)^{d_{G}}}.

Then, for all

with running time

.

.

]
the result above is only stated for

We use the following simple lemma.

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
(\mathds{X},d)
be a complete, separable metric space, let
\mathcal{B}=\mathcal{B}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathds{X}}}\right)
be the Borel algebra and let
\nu
be a locally finite reference measure on
(\mathds{X},\mathcal{B})
.
Let
\mathds{V}\subseteq\mathds{X}
be bounded and measurable, let
\lambda\in\mathds{R}_{\geq 0}
and let
\phi:\mathds{X}^{2}\to\mathds{R}_{\geq 0}\cup\{\infty\}

C_{\phi}>0

For
\alpha\in\mathds{R}_{>0}

q\in(0,1]

Proof.

By union bound, it is sufficient to argue that, for each
i\in[n]

\mathrm{Pr}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{d_{G}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(i}}\right)\geq(1+\alpha)\frac{n-1}{\nu\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathds{V}}}\right)}C_{\phi}}}}\right]\leq q,

i\in[n]

The first lemma is a domination result for the size of independent sets, drawn from a hard-core model.

Lemma 5.6
.

Let
G\in\mathcal{G}_{n}
for some
n\in\mathds{N}
and let
\gamma\in\mathds{R}_{\geq 0}
.
For
\sigma\sim\mu_{G}^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\gamma}}\right)}
it holds that
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|S_{\sigma}}}\right|
is stochastically dominated by a binomial random variable with
n
trials and success probability
\frac{\gamma}{1+\gamma}
.

Proof.

We use a coupling argument to prove this statement.
Consider the following procedure for sampling a set
S_{n}\subseteq[n]
:

1.

Start with
S_{0}=\emptyset
.

2.

For each
i\in[n]
, set
S_{i}=S_{i-1}\cup\{i\}
with probability
\mathrm{Pr}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{\sigma\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(i}}\right)=1\ \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\ \vphantom{\sigma\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(i}}\right)=1}\bigwedge_{j\in[i-1]}\sigma\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(j}}\right)=\mathds{1}_{j\in S_{i-1}}}}\right.}}}\right]
for
\sigma\sim\mu_{G}^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\gamma}}\right)}
.

Note that the resulting set
S_{n}
follows the same distribution as
S_{\sigma}
for
\sigma\sim\mu_{G}^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\gamma}}\right)}
.
Due to the definition of this process, it suffices to consider sequences
(S_{i})_{i\in[n]\cup\{0\}}
such that the event
\{\bigwedge_{j\in[i-1]}\sigma\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(j}}\right)=\mathds{1}_{j\in S_{i-1}}\}
has non-zero probability.
Further, note that

\mathrm{Pr}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{\sigma\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(i}}\right)=1\ \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\ \vphantom{\sigma\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(i}}\right)=1}\bigwedge_{j\in[i-1]}\sigma\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(j}}\right)=\mathds{1}_{j\in S_{i-1}}}}\right.}}}\right]\leq\frac{\gamma}{1+\gamma}

for all
i\in[n]
.
Now, we consider a modified process
(S^{\prime}_{i})_{i\in[n]\cup\{0\}}
with
S^{\prime}_{0}=\emptyset
and
S^{\prime}_{i}=S^{\prime}_{i-1}\cup\{i\}
with probability
\frac{\gamma}{1+\gamma}
.
Observe that
(S_{i})_{i\in[n]\cup\{0\}}
and
(S^{\prime}_{i})_{i\in[n]\cup\{0\}}
can be coupled in such a way that
S_{i}\subseteq S^{\prime}_{i}
whenever
S_{i-1}\subseteq S^{\prime}_{i-1}
for all
i\in[n]
.
As initially
S_{0}=S^{\prime}_{0}
, the same coupling yields
S_{n}\subseteq S^{\prime}_{n}
.
Finally, observing that
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|S^{\prime}_{n}}}\right|
follows a binomial distribution with
n
trials and success probability
\frac{\gamma}{1+\gamma}
concludes the proof.
∎

The second lemma is the analog of

However, proving it is slightly more technically involved.
We start by introducing some additional notation and terminology.
For two counting measures

\eta_{1}\leq\eta_{2}
if
\eta_{1}(B)\leq\eta_{2}(B)

A measurable function

is called

if
h(\eta_{1})\leq h(\eta_{2})
for all
\eta_{1}\leq\eta_{2}
.
Moreover, for some
\kappa\in\mathds{R}_{\geq 0}
, let
Q_{\kappa}
denote the Poisson point process with intensity
\kappa
and let
P
be a point process that has a density

with respect to
Q_{\kappa}
.
A function

is called a

for

(w.r.t.
Q_{\kappa}
) if, for all
\eta\in\mathcal{N}
and

, it holds that

f_{P}(\eta+\delta_{x})=\zeta\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\eta,x}}\right)f_{P}(\eta).

The domination lemma we are aiming for is implied by the following result.

Theorem 5.7
(
[
20
, Theorem
1.1
]
).

Let
Q_{\kappa}
be a Poisson point process of intensity

.

and

\zeta_{1}
and
\zeta_{2}

and

With that, we show the following simple domination result.

Let
Q_{\lambda}
denote a Poisson point process with intensity

.
Note that
a density of

.
Moreover, let

is a Papangelou intensity for

For all

, let

and observe that

is increasing.
Further, note that, for all

and

\zeta_{1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\eta_{1},x}}\right)=\mathds{1}_{x\in\mathds{V}}\prod_{y\in X_{\eta_{1}}}\mathrm{e}^{-N_{y}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\eta_{1}}}\right)\phi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x,y}}\right)}\leq\mathds{1}_{x\in\mathds{V}}=\zeta_{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\eta_{2},x}}\right).

By

Consequently, for
\eta\sim P_{\mathds{V}}^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\lambda,\phi}}\right)}
and
\xi\sim P

\mathrm{Pr}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{\eta\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathds{V}}}\right)\geq k}}}\right]\leq\mathrm{Pr}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{\xi(\mathds{V})\geq k}}}\right]

and observing that

follows a Poisson distribution with parameter
\lambda\nu\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathds{V}}}\right)
concludes the proof.
∎

6
Potential-weighted connective constant and strong spatial mixing

Throughout this section, we consider the setting introduced in
Section

4
.
In this section, we relate the potential-weighted connective constant
\Delta_{\phi}
of a repulsive potential with a high-probability bound on a modified version of the connective constant of a graph
G\sim\zeta^{(n)}_{\mathds{V},\phi}
.
This modified connective constant represents the growth rate of a truncated version of the self-avoiding walk tree as used by Weitz
[
47
]
.
Besides giving a graphical interpretation for the potential-weighted connective constant, an immediate consequence of the result is that the hard-core models studied in
Section

4
with high probability exhibit strong spatial mixing for
\gamma<\frac{\mathrm{e}}{\Delta_{\phi}}
.

6.1
Potential-weighted connective constant

The potential-weighted connective constant was introduced in
[
33
]
to measure the strength of interaction induced by a potential
\phi
in a way that is, compared to the temperedness constant
C_{\phi}
, more sensitive to the particular geometry of the underlying space
\mathds{X}
.
To this end, we set
V_{\phi}(0)=1
and, for
k\in\mathds{N}_{\geq 1}
,

V_{\phi}(k)=\sup_{x_{0}\in\mathds{X}}\int_{\mathds{X}^{k}}\prod_{j=1}^{k}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(-\sum_{i=0}^{j-2}\mathds{1}_{d\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x_{i},x_{j}}}\right)<d\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x_{i},x_{i+1}}}\right)}\phi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x_{i},x_{j}}}\right)}}\right)\cdot\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-\mathrm{e}^{-\phi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x_{j-1},x_{j}}}\right)}}}\right)}}\right)\nu^{k}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\text{d}\bm{x}}}\right),

where
\bm{x}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})
.
The potential-weighted connective constant is now defined as

\Delta_{\phi}=\lim_{k\to\infty}V_{\phi}(k)^{1/k}=\inf_{k\to\infty}V_{\phi}(k)^{1/k},

where existence if the limit and the second equality are implied by the fact that we assume
\phi
to be repulsive, which implies that
V_{\phi}(k)
is sub-multiplicative.
Note that for all
k\in\mathds{N}
it holds that
V_{\phi}(k)\leq C_{\phi}^{k}
and therefore
\Delta_{\phi}\leq C_{\phi}
.

6.2
Strong spatial mixing

Strong spatial mixing is a frequently used notion of correlation decay in discrete spin systems.
In this section, we focus on strong spatial mixing for the hard-core model.
Recall that for a graph
G=(V_{G},E_{G})
and a parameter
\gamma\in\mathds{R}_{\geq 0}
we write
\mu_{G}^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\gamma}}\right)}
for the hard-core distribution on
G
at weight
\gamma
, which is a distribution on
\Sigma_{G}
the set of all functions
\Sigma_{G}=\{\sigma:V\to\{0,1\}\}
.
We extend this notation to conditional distributions.
To this end, let
S\subseteq V_{G}
and let
\tau:S\to\{0,1\}
.
Write
\sigma_{S}=\tau
for the event that
\sigma\sim\mu_{G}^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\gamma}}\right)}
coincides with
\tau
on
S
.
We call
\tau

feasible
if
\mu_{G}^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\gamma}}\right)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sigma_{S}=\tau}}\right)>0
.
In that case, we write

\mu_{G}^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\gamma}}\right)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\,\cdot\,\mid\,\sigma_{S}=\tau}}\right)=\frac{\mu_{G}^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\gamma}}\right)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\cdot}}\right)}{\mu_{G}^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\gamma}}\right)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sigma_{S}=\tau}}\right)}

for the distribution of
\sigma\sim\mu_{G}^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\gamma}}\right)}
conditioned on
\sigma_{S}=\tau
.
Often, strong spatial mixing is phrased in terms of the so-called
occupation ratios
.
For
G
and
\gamma
as above and
v\in V_{G}
we write

R_{G}^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\gamma}}\right)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(v}}\right)=\frac{\mu_{G}^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\gamma}}\right)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sigma(v)=1}}\right)}{\mu_{G}^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\gamma}}\right)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sigma(v)=0}}\right)}.

Further, for
S\subset V_{G}
with
v\notin S
and feasible
\tau:S\to\{0,1\}
we define
R_{G}^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\gamma}}\right)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(v\mid\tau}}\right)
analogously using the distribution
\mu_{G}^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\gamma}}\right)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\,\cdot\,\mid\,\sigma_{S}=\tau}}\right)
instead.
Based on that, we now state the definition of strong spatial mixing as given in
[
40
]
.

Definition 6.1
(
[
40
]
).

The hard-core model with vertex activity
\gamma\in\mathds{R}_{\geq 0}
is set to satisfy strong spatial mixing on a family of graphs
\mathcal{F}
if there exists a constant
\delta\in[0,1)
such that for all
G\in\mathcal{F}
, vertices
v\in V_{G}
,
S\subseteq V_{G}\setminus\{v\}
, and feasible
\tau,\tau^{\prime}:S\to\{0,1\}
it holds that

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|R_{G}^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\gamma}}\right)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(v\mid\tau}}\right)-R_{G}^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\gamma}}\right)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(v\mid\tau^{\prime}}}\right)}}\right|\leq\mathrm{O}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({\delta^{s}}}}\right),

where
s
is the graph distance between
v
and the vertices on which
\tau
and
\tau^{\prime}
differ (i.e.,
\{u\in S\mid\tau(u)\neq\tau^{\prime}(u)\}
).
We call
\delta
the decay rate.

Remark 6.2
.

Note that this definition is actually weaker than the definition of (exponential) strong spatial mixing that is usually used in the literature (cf.
[
47
]
Definition
2.2
and the remark following it).
Usually, this definition requires the existence of constants
\alpha\geq 0,\beta>0
(independent of
G,v,\tau_{1}
and
\tau_{2}
) such that

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|R_{G}^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\gamma}}\right)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(v\mid\tau}}\right)-R_{G}^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\gamma}}\right)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(v\mid\tau^{\prime}}}\right)}}\right|\leq\alpha\mathrm{e}^{-\beta s}.

In contrast,
Definition

6.1
only requires such a decay if the distance
s\geq s_{0}
is sufficiently large, where the required lower bound
s_{0}
might depend on
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|V_{G}}}\right|
.
In fact, this is the case for the strong spatial mixing result for families of finite graphs in
[
40
]
, which requires
s\geq s_{0}\in\upTheta\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({\ln(\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|V_{G}}}\right|)}}}\right)
.
Such a bound on
s_{0}
still allows for applying Weitz’s algorithm to obtain an efficient deterministic approximation algorithm for the partition function of the hard-core model.
However, it is not sufficient for other applications that require the exponential decay to also hold at constant distances, such as recent exact sampling algorithms
[
2
,
16
]
or the rapid mixing result for Glauber dynamics in
[
47
]
.

6.3
Self-avoiding walk tree and Weitz tree

Due to
[
47
]
, it is well known that strong spatial mixing on a given graph can be studied in terms of a tree construction that is closely related to the
self-avoiding walk tree
.
We refer to this construction as the
Weitz tree
.
Moreover, it was shown by
[
40
]
that bounding the growth-rate of Weitz trees for a family of graphs can be used to find a vertex activity regime in which the graph family exhibits strong spatial mixing.
In the following paragraphs, we briefly introduce both trees.

Self-avoiding walk tree

Given an undirected graph
G=(V_{G},E_{G})
and a vertex
r\in V_{G}
, we denote by
T_{r,G}=(V_{T},E_{T})
the self-avoiding walk tree with root
r
.
It is constructed as follow:

1.

Let
\mathcal{P}_{r,G}
denote the set of all simple paths
p=r,v_{1},\dots,v_{k}
(identified by their vertex sequence) in
G
starting at
r
, where we call
k
the length of
p
.

V_{T}
contains exactly one vertex
w_{p}
for every such path
p\in\mathcal{P}_{r,G}
.
In particular, there is a unique vertex that corresponds to the path
p=r
of length
0
, which is denoted by
w_{r}
and considered the root of
T_{r,G}
.

2.

For two paths
p,p^{\prime}\in\mathcal{P}_{r,G}
we say that
w_{p}\in V_{T}
is a child of
w_{p^{\prime}}\in V_{T}
(and connect them by an edge in
E_{T}
) if and only if
p
is obtained from
p^{\prime}
by adding one vertex.
That is, if
p^{\prime}=r,v_{1},\dots v_{k}
, then
p=r,v_{1},\dots v_{k},v_{k+1}
for some vertex
v_{k+1}\notin V_{G}\setminus\{r,v_{1},\dots v_{k}\}
adjacent to
v_{k}
.

Weitz tree

For our purpose, it is crucial to differentiate between the self-avoiding walk tree and a truncated version that we call the Weitz tree.
Its construction is analogous to the self-avoiding walk tree above but with an additional restriction on the set of paths involved.
This restriction on the paths allows for some degrees of freedom, as it depends on assigning an ordering to the neighbors
N_{G}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(v}}\right)
of each vertex
v\in V_{G}
.
Formally, we call a family of functions
F=(f_{v})_{v\in V_{G}}
a
neighborhood ordering
for
G
if, for every
v\in V_{G}
, it holds that
f_{v}
is a bijection
N_{G}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(v}}\right)\to[\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|N_{G}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(v}}\right)}}\right|]
.
For a vertex
r\in V_{G}
we now write
\mathcal{P}_{r,G}^{F}
for the set of simple paths
p=v_{0},v_{1},\dots,v_{k}
in
G
with the following properties

1.

It holds that
v_{0}=r
.

2.

For every
2\leq i\leq k
and all
0\leq j\leq i-2
it holds that, if
v_{i}\in N_{G}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(v_{j}}}\right)
, then
f_{v_{j}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(v_{i}}}\right)>f_{v_{j}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(v_{j+1}}}\right)
.

Obviously it holds that
\mathcal{P}_{r,G}^{F}\subseteq\mathcal{P}_{r,G}
.
The Weitz tree
T_{r,G}^{F}
with root
r
is now defined analogously to the self-avoiding walk tree
T_{r,G}
but restricted to paths in
\mathcal{P}_{r,G}^{F}
.
It is not hard to see that
T_{r,G}^{F}
is in fact a subtree of
T_{r,G}
.
Since we are going to study a notion of growth rate of
T_{r,G}^{F}
, it is useful to denote by
L_{r,G}^{F}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(k}}\right)
the number of vertices at layer
k\in\mathds{N}
of
T_{r,G}^{F}
.
This is equal to the number of paths
p=r,v_{1},\dots,v_{k}\in\mathcal{P}_{r,G}^{F}
of length
k
.

Remark 6.3
.

Note that this construction of the Weitz tree is not exactly as described in
[
47
]
.
The goal of constructing those trees is to study hard-core models on them.
In the original construction, paths where allowed to close cycles.
Whenever this happens, the spin of the vertex that closes the cycle was fixed to either
0
or
1
, depending on the chosen neighborhood ordering.
For the hard-core model, this corresponds to either removing the vertex (for spin
0
), or the vertex and all its neighbors (for spin
1
).
This procedure leads to our notion of the Weitz tree.

6.4
Connective constant and strong spatial mixing

With the definition of strong spatial mixing and the construction of the Weitz tree given, we now get to the definition of the connective constant and its implications for strong spatial mixing.
Our definition of connective constant is inspired by
[
40
]
.
However, there are two things that should be noted.
Firstly, we emphasize that our definition of connective constant refers to the Weitz tree instead of the full self-avoiding walk.
Secondly, for our application to random graphs from
\zeta^{(n)}_{\mathds{V},\phi}
, it is important which neighborhood ordering is used for constructing the tree.
To reflect this, we use the following definition.

Definition 6.4
.

We say a family of graphs
\mathcal{F}
has a connective constant bounded by
\Delta
if there are constants
a,c\geq 0
such that for all
G\in\mathcal{F}
the following holds: there is a neighborhood ordering
F
for
G
such that for all
m\geq a\ln(\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|V_{G}}}\right|)
and all
r\in V_{G}
we have

\sum_{k=0}^{m}L_{r,G}^{F}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(k}}\right)\leq c\Delta^{m}.

In
[
40
]
, it was proven that a bound on the connective constant of a graph family immediately translates to a regime of strong spatial mixing.
For our purposes, we will actually need some more detailed information about the rate of decay.
The following statement can be extracted from the proof of the main theorem in
[
40
]
.

Theorem 6.5
(
[
40
]
).

Suppose
\mathcal{F}
is a family of graphs with connective constant bounded by
\Delta
as in
Definition

6.4
for constants
a
and
c
.
For all
\varepsilon>0
,
\gamma\leq\mathrm{e}^{-\varepsilon}\gamma_{\text{c}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\Delta}}\right)
, and graphs
G\in\mathcal{F}
the following holds:
For all
v\in V_{G}
, all
S\subseteq V_{G}\setminus\{v\}
, and all feasible
\tau,\tau^{\prime}:S\to\{0,1\}
that only differ at distance
s\geq a\ln(\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|V_{G}}}\right|)
from
v
, it holds that

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|R_{G}^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\gamma}}\right)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(v\mid\tau}}\right)-R_{G}^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\gamma}}\right)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(v\mid\tau^{\prime}}}\right)}}\right|\leq c^{1/q}\frac{M}{L}\mathrm{e}^{-\varepsilon s/q},

where
1<q\leq 2
,
M=\sinh^{-1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sqrt{\gamma}}}\right)
and
L=\frac{1}{2\sqrt{\gamma\cdot(\gamma+1)}}
.

Remark 6.6
.

Theorem

6.5
follows immediately from tracking the constants in the proof of the main theorem of
[
41
]
.
Note further that in the proof in
[
41
]
the bound from
Theorem

6.5
is stated with
\frac{L}{M}
instead of
\frac{M}{L}
.
However, closely inspecting the proof and the lemmas used therein reveals that this is attributed to a typo.

6.5
Connective constant and spatial mixing for random discretizations of Gibbs point processes

Our first main result of this section is the following bound on the connective constant for random graphs from
\zeta^{(n)}_{\mathds{V},\phi}
.

Theorem 6.7
.

Let
\mathds{V}\subseteq\mathds{X}
be a bounded and measurable region with volume
\nu\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathds{V}}}\right)>0
.
Let
\phi:\mathds{X}^{2}\to\mathds{R}_{\geq 0}\cup\{\infty\}
be a symmetric repulsive potential with
C_{\phi}<\infty
and
\Delta_{\phi}>0
.
For every
\varepsilon>0
there exists some
n_{0}\in\upTheta\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({\nu\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathds{V}}}\right)}}}\right)
such that for all
n\geq n_{0}
the following holds:
There exists a family of graphs
\mathcal{F}\subseteq\mathcal{G}_{n}
with connective constant bounded by
\mathrm{e}^{\varepsilon}\frac{n}{\nu\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathds{V}}}\right)}\Delta_{\phi}
such that the constants
a,c
from
Definition

6.4
are independent of
\mathds{V}
and
n
, and for
G\sim\zeta^{(n)}_{\mathds{V},\phi}
it holds that

\mathrm{Pr}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{G\in\mathcal{F}}}}\right]\geq 1-\frac{1}{n}.

Proof.

Fix some
\varepsilon>0
.
By the definition of
\Delta_{\phi}
and the fact that
\Delta_{\phi}>0
, we know that there is some
k_{0}
such that
V_{\phi}(k)\leq\mathrm{e}^{k\varepsilon/2}\Delta_{\phi}^{k}
for all
k\geq k_{0}
.
We set
a=4\varepsilon^{-1}
,
c=(1-\mathrm{e}^{-\varepsilon/2})\cdot\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left((C_{\phi}/\Delta_{\phi})^{k_{0}}+2}}\right)
, and
n_{0}=\max\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\frac{2}{\Delta_{\phi}}\nu\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathds{V}}}\right),\mathrm{e}^{a^{-1}k_{0}}}}\right\}
.

Next, fix any
n\geq n_{0}
.
Our goal is to show that, for
G\sim\zeta^{(n)}_{\mathds{V},\phi}
, we can find some neighborhood ordering for
G
such that, with probability at least
1-1/n
, the requirements of
Definition

6.4
are satisfied for
a,c
as above and
\Delta=\mathrm{e}^{\varepsilon}\frac{n}{\nu\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathds{V}}}\right)}\Delta_{\phi}
.

We start by constructing the class of neighborhood orderings we consider.
Let
G\in\mathcal{G}_{n}
be a graph on
[n]
.
For every
\bm{x}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\in\mathds{V}^{n}
we construct a neighborhood ordering
F_{x,G}=(f_{i})_{i\in[n]}
on
G
such that, for every vertex
i\in[n]
and neighbors
j_{1},j_{2}\in N_{G}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(i}}\right)
, it holds that
d\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x_{i},x_{j_{1}}}}\right)<d\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x_{i},x_{j_{2}}}}\right)
implies
f_{i}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(j_{1}}}\right)<f_{i}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(j_{2}}}\right)
.
That is, we order the neighbors of each vertex increasingly by distance, breaking ties arbitrarily (e.g., by vertex IDs).
It now suffices to show that, for
G\sim\zeta^{(n)}_{\mathds{V},\phi}
, the following event has probability at least
1-1/n
: there is a sequence of points
\bm{x}\in\mathds{V}^{n}
such that for all
m\geq a\ln(n)
and all
r\in[n]

\sum_{k=0}^{m}L_{r,G}^{F_{\bm{x},G}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(k}}\right)\leq c\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathrm{e}^{\varepsilon}\frac{n}{\nu\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathds{V}}}\right)}\Delta_{\phi}}}\right)^{m}.

We denote this event by
A
, so that the desired statement is simply expressed as
\zeta^{(n)}_{\mathds{V},\phi}(A)\geq 1-1/n
.

To prove this, we study a distribution
\kappa
on the space
\mathds{V}^{n}\times\mathcal{G}_{n}
, equipped with the product sigma field
\mathcal{B}_{\mathds{V}}^{n}\otimes 2^{\mathcal{G}_{n}}
.
Consider the following procedure:

1.

For each
i\in[n]
, draw a uniform random point
X_{i}\sim u_{\mathds{V}}
independently.
We call the resulting random vector
X=(X_{i})_{i\in[n]}

2.

Construct a graph
G
on vertex set
[n]
as follows.
For all
i,j\in[n]
with
i\neq j
, connect vertices
i
and
j
with an edge with probability
1-\mathrm{e}^{-\phi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(X_{i},X_{j}}}\right)}
independently.

We take
\kappa
to be the distribution of
(X,G)
generated as above.
We further write
\kappa_{X}
and
\kappa_{G}
for the respective marginals and note that
\kappa_{X}=u^{n}_{\mathds{V}}
and
\kappa_{G}=\zeta^{(n)}_{\mathds{V},\phi}
.
Further, let
B
denote the following event: for all
m\geq a\ln(n)
and all
r\in[n]
it holds that

\sum_{k=0}^{m}L_{r,G}^{F_{X,G}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(k}}\right)\leq c\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathrm{e}^{\varepsilon}\frac{n}{\nu\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathds{V}}}\right)}\Delta_{\phi}}}\right)^{m}.

Note that the main difference between the events
A
and
B
is that
A
asks for the existence of a point sequence
\bm{x}
for a given random graph
G
, whereas
B
is a statement about a random pair
(X,G)
.
In particular, it holds that
B\subseteq\mathds{V}^{n}\times A
and consequently

\zeta^{(n)}_{\mathds{V},\phi}(A)=\kappa_{G}(A)=\kappa(\mathds{V}^{n}\times A)\geq\kappa(B).

Thus, the theorem is proven by showing that
\kappa(B)\geq 1-1/n
.

We prove this by first bounding the expectation of
L_{r,G}^{F_{X,G}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(k}}\right)
with respect to
\kappa
for every fixed
r\in[n]
.
To this end, define
\psi:\mathds{X}^{k+1}\to[0,1]
by

\psi(y_{0},y_{1},\dots,y_{k})=\prod_{j=1}^{k}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(-\sum_{i=0}^{j-2}\mathds{1}_{d\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(y_{i},y_{j}}}\right)<d\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(y_{i},y_{i+1}}}\right)}\phi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(y_{i},y_{j}}}\right)}}\right)\cdot\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-\mathrm{e}^{-\phi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(y_{j-1},y_{j}}}\right)}}}\right)}}\right).

For every sequence of distinct vertices
i_{1},\dots,i_{k}\in[n]\setminus\{r\}
it now holds that

\mathrm{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{\mathds{1}_{r,i_{1},\dots,i_{k}\in\mathcal{P}_{r,G}^{F_{X,G}}}\ \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\ \vphantom{\mathds{1}_{r,i_{1},\dots,i_{k}\in\mathcal{P}_{r,G}^{F_{X,G}}}}X}}\right.}}}\right]\leq\psi(X_{r},X_{i_{1}},\dots,X_{i_{k}}).

Applying linearity of expectation to sum over all such sequences of distinct vertices
i_{1},\dots,i_{k}\in[n]\setminus\{r\}
and applying law of total expectation then yields that

\displaystyle\mathrm{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{L_{r,G}^{F_{X,G}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(k}}\right)}}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\nu\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathds{V}}}\right)^{-n}\int_{\mathds{V}^{n}}\sum_{i_{1},\dots,i_{k}}\psi(x_{r},x_{i_{1}},\dots,x_{i_{k}})\nu^{n}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\text{d}\bm{x}}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\nu\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathds{V}}}\right)^{-k}\frac{(n-1)!}{(n-1-k)!}\sup_{z_{0}\in\mathds{V}}\int_{\mathds{V}^{k}}\psi(z_{0},z_{1},\dots,z_{k})\nu^{k}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\text{d}\bm{z}}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{n}{\nu\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathds{V}}}\right)}}}\right)^{k}V_{\phi}(k),

where
\bm{x}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
and
\bm{z}=(z_{1},\dots,z_{k})
.

Next, we aim to obtain a tail bound for
\sum_{k=0}^{m}L_{r,G}^{F_{X,G}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(k}}\right)
for every
m\geq a\ln(n)
.
To this end, we first bound the expectation, starting with splitting up the sum as

\mathrm{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{\sum_{k=0}^{m}L_{r,G}^{F_{X,G}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(k}}\right)}}}\right]\leq\sum_{k=0}^{m}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{n}{\nu\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathds{V}}}\right)}}}\right)^{k}V_{\phi}(k)=\sum_{k=0}^{k_{0}-1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{n}{\nu\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathds{V}}}\right)}}}\right)^{k}V_{\phi}(k)+\sum_{k=k_{0}}^{m}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{n}{\nu\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathds{V}}}\right)}}}\right)^{k}V_{\phi}(k).

We proceed by bounding each of the sums separately.
For the first sum, note that it trivially holds that
V_{\phi}(k)\leq C_{\phi}^{k}
and, in particular,
\Delta_{\phi}\leq C_{\phi}
.
Moreover, for our choice of
n_{0}
, we
\frac{n}{\nu\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathds{V}}}\right)}C_{\phi}\geq 2
.
Combining both observations yields

\sum_{k=0}^{k_{0}-1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{n}{\nu\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathds{V}}}\right)}}}\right)^{k}V_{\phi}(k)\leq\sum_{k=0}^{k_{0}-1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{n}{\nu\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathds{V}}}\right)}C_{\phi}}}\right)^{k}=\frac{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{n}{\nu\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathds{V}}}\right)}C_{\phi}}}\right)^{k_{0}}-1}{\frac{n}{\nu\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathds{V}}}\right)}C_{\phi}-1}\leq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{n}{\nu\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathds{V}}}\right)}C_{\phi}}}\right)^{k_{0}}.

For the second sum, recall that
V_{\phi}(k)\leq\mathrm{e}^{k\varepsilon/2}\Delta_{\phi}^{k}
for all
k\geq k_{0}
.
Therefore, we have

\sum_{k=k_{0}}^{m}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{n}{\nu\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathds{V}}}\right)}}}\right)^{k}V_{\phi}(k)\leq\sum_{k=k_{0}}^{m}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathrm{e}^{\varepsilon/2}\frac{n}{\nu\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathds{V}}}\right)}\Delta_{\phi}}}\right)^{k}\leq\frac{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathrm{e}^{\varepsilon/2}\frac{n}{\nu\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathds{V}}}\right)}\Delta_{\phi}}}\right)^{m+1}}{\mathrm{e}^{\varepsilon/2}\frac{n}{\nu\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathds{V}}}\right)}\Delta_{\phi}-1}.

Combining both bounds yields

\displaystyle\mathrm{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{\sum_{k=0}^{m}L_{r,G}^{F_{X,G}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(k}}\right)}}}\right]\leq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{n}{\nu\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathds{V}}}\right)}C_{\phi}}}\right)^{k_{0}}}{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathrm{e}^{\varepsilon/2}\frac{n}{\nu\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathds{V}}}\right)}\Delta_{\phi}}}\right)^{m}}+\frac{\mathrm{e}^{\varepsilon/2}\frac{n}{\nu\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathds{V}}}\right)}\Delta_{\phi}}{\mathrm{e}^{\varepsilon/2}\frac{n}{\nu\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathds{V}}}\right)}\Delta_{\phi}-1}}}\right)\cdot\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathrm{e}^{\varepsilon/2}\frac{n}{\nu\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathds{V}}}\right)}\Delta_{\phi}}}\right)^{m}.

Since
\mathrm{e}^{\varepsilon/2}\frac{n}{\nu\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathds{V}}}\right)}\Delta_{\phi}\geq 2
for
n\geq n_{0}
, we further bound

\displaystyle\mathrm{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{\sum_{k=0}^{m}L_{r,G}^{F_{X,G}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(k}}\right)}}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\frac{n}{\nu\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathds{V}}}\right)}C_{\phi}}{\mathrm{e}^{\varepsilon/2}\frac{n}{\nu\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathds{V}}}\right)}\Delta_{\phi}}}}\right)^{k_{0}}+2}}\right)\cdot\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathrm{e}^{\varepsilon/2}\frac{n}{\nu\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathds{V}}}\right)}\Delta_{\phi}}}\right)^{m}

\displaystyle\leq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{C_{\phi}}{\Delta_{\phi}}}}\right)^{k_{0}}+2}}\right)\cdot\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathrm{e}^{\varepsilon/2}\frac{n}{\nu\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathds{V}}}\right)}\Delta_{\phi}}}\right)^{m}.

Using this upper bound for the expectation and Markov’s inequality, we have for
(X,G)\sim\kappa
that

\mathrm{Pr}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{\sum_{k=0}^{m}L_{r,G}^{F_{X,G}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(k}}\right)\geq c\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathrm{e}^{\varepsilon}\frac{n}{\nu\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathds{V}}}\right)}\Delta_{\phi}}}\right)^{m}}}}\right]\leq(1-\mathrm{e}^{-\varepsilon/2})\cdot\mathrm{e}^{-m\varepsilon/2}.

Applying the union bound over
m\geq m_{0}=a\ln(n)
we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{Pr}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{\exists m\geq m_{0}:~\sum_{k=0}^{m}L_{r,G}^{F_{X,G}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(k}}\right)\geq c\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathrm{e}^{\varepsilon}\frac{n}{\nu\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathds{V}}}\right)}\Delta_{\phi}}}\right)^{m}}}}\right]\leq(1-\mathrm{e}^{-\varepsilon/2})\cdot\sum_{m=m_{0}}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-m\varepsilon/2}\leq n^{-2},

where the last inequality is obtained by factoring out
\mathrm{e}^{-m_{0}\varepsilon/2}
and using the fact that
m_{0}=a\ln(n)\geq 4\varepsilon^{-1}\ln(n)
.
Finally, applying the union bound over the choice of root vertices
r
shows that
\kappa(B^{c})\leq 1/n
and consequently
\kappa(B)\geq 1-1/n
, proving the theorem.
∎

We proceed by studying which notion of strong spatial mixing for graphs from
\zeta^{(n)}_{\mathds{V},\phi}
we can obtain from
Theorem

6.7
.
To this end, we apply
Theorem

6.5
, which yields the following result.

Theorem 6.8
.

Suppose
\mathds{V}\subseteq\mathds{X}
is a bounded and measurable region with volume
\nu\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathds{V}}}\right)>0
.
Let
\phi:\mathds{X}^{2}\to\mathds{R}_{\geq 0}\cup\{\infty\}
be a symmetric repulsive potential with
C_{\phi}<\infty
and
\Delta_{\phi}>0
, and let
\lambda<\frac{\mathrm{e}}{\Delta_{\phi}}
.
There exists some
n_{0}\in\upTheta\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({\nu\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathds{V}}}\right)}}}\right)
such that for all
n\geq n_{0}
there is a family of graphs
\mathcal{F}\subseteq\mathcal{G}_{n}
with the following properties:

1.

For
G\sim\zeta^{(n)}_{\mathds{V},\phi}
it holds that

\mathrm{Pr}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{G\in\mathcal{F}}}}\right]\geq 1-\frac{1}{n}.

2.

Set
\gamma=\lambda\cdot\frac{\nu\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathds{V}}}\right)}{n}
. There are constants
\alpha\geq 0,\beta>0,a\geq 0
independent
n
and
\mathds{V}
such that for every
G\in\mathcal{F}
, all
v\in V_{G}
,
S\subseteq V_{G}\setminus\{v\}
and feasible
\tau,\tau^{\prime}:S\to\{0,1\}
that only differ at distance
s\geq a\ln(n)
from
v
it holds that

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|R_{G}^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\gamma}}\right)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(v\mid\tau}}\right)-R_{G}^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\gamma}}\right)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(v\mid\tau^{\prime}}}\right)}}\right|\leq\alpha\mathrm{e}^{-\beta s}.

Proof.

First, note that for
\lambda<\frac{\mathrm{e}}{\Delta_{\phi}}
we can choose
\varepsilon>0
such that
\lambda\leq\frac{\mathrm{e}^{1-\varepsilon}}{\Delta_{\phi}}
.
By
Theorem

6.7
we know that for
n_{0}\in\upTheta\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({\nu\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathds{V}}}\right)}}}\right)
sufficiently large it holds that for all
n\geq n_{0}
that there is a graph family
\mathcal{F}\subseteq\mathcal{G}_{n}
with connective constant bounded by
\Delta=\mathrm{e}^{\varepsilon/2}\frac{n}{\nu\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathds{V}}}\right)}\Delta_{\phi}
such that
G\sim\zeta^{(n)}_{\mathds{V},\phi}
is in
\mathcal{F}
with probability at least
1-\frac{1}{n}
.
Thus,
\mathcal{F}
satisfies the first requirement of our theorem.

We proceed by establishing the second part of the theorem.
To this end, let
\mathcal{F}
and
\Delta
be as above.
Further, let
a,c
be the constants given by
Theorem

6.7
and recall that they are independent of
n
and
\nu\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathds{V}}}\right)
.
Observe that

\gamma=\lambda\cdot\frac{\nu\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathds{V}}}\right)}{n}\leq\frac{\mathrm{e}^{1-\varepsilon}}{\Delta_{\phi}}\cdot\frac{\nu\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathds{V}}}\right)}{n}\leq\frac{\mathrm{e}^{1-\varepsilon/2}}{\Delta}.

Thus, applying
Theorem

6.5
proves our claim as soon as we show that
\frac{M}{L}
for
M=\sinh^{-1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sqrt{\gamma}}}\right)
and
L=\frac{1}{2\sqrt{\gamma\cdot(\gamma+1)}}
is uniformly bounded in
n
and
\nu\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathds{V}}}\right)
.
Recalling that
\gamma=\lambda\cdot\frac{\nu\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathds{V}}}\right)}{n}
, we see that
\frac{M}{L}
is decreasing in
n
and bounded by a constant independent of
\nu\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathds{V}}}\right)
as soon as
n_{0}\geq\lambda\cdot\nu\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathds{V}}}\right)
.
∎

Remark 6.9
.

Note that from the proof of
Theorem

6.8
it actually even follows that the parameter
\alpha
goes to
0
as
n
increases.
This is to be expected, since the occupation ratios go to zero as
\gamma\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(n}}\right)
decreases.

We obtain the following corollary.

Corollary 6.10
.

Suppose
\mathds{V}\subseteq\mathds{X}
is a bounded and measurable region with volume
\nu\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathds{V}}}\right)>0
.
Let
\phi:\mathds{X}^{2}\to\mathds{R}_{\geq 0}\cup\{\infty\}
be a symmetric repulsive potential with
C_{\phi}<\infty
and
\Delta_{\phi}>0
, and let
\lambda<\frac{\mathrm{e}}{\Delta_{\phi}}
.
There exists some
n_{0}\in\upTheta\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({\nu\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathds{V}}}\right)}}}\right)
such that for all
n\geq n_{0}
there is a family of graphs
\mathcal{F}\subseteq\mathcal{G}_{n}
with the following properties:

1.

For
G\sim\zeta^{(n)}_{\mathds{V},\phi}
it holds that

\mathrm{Pr}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{G\in\mathcal{F}}}}\right]\geq 1-\frac{1}{n}.

2.

The hard-core model with activity
\gamma=\lambda\cdot\frac{\nu\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathds{V}}}\right)}{n}
exhibits strong spatial mixing in the sense of
Definition

6.1
on
\mathcal{F}
with decay rate independent of
n
and
\mathds{V}
.

We finish this section with discussing some algorithmic consequences of the results above.
However, we keep the discussion informal since the results can be obtained from standard techniques.
We start with the observation that
Theorem

6.5
does in fact not only imply strong spatial mixing on each graph in the family
\mathcal{F}
, but it also implies a notion of strong spatial mixing in the Weitz trees that were used for bounding the connective constant of
\mathcal{F}
.
More precisely, each of the trees exhibits strong spatial mixing in term of the occupation ratio of the root, given boundary conditions sufficiently far down the tree (see
[
40
]
).
This can be turned into a deterministic approximation algorithm for the partition function of the hard-core model as argued in
[
47
,
40
]
with running time
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|V_{G}}}\right|^{\mathrm{O}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({\log(\Delta)}}}\right)}
for every graph in
G\in\mathcal{F}
.
Using
Theorem

1.1
, we may use this to obtain a randomized algorithm with running time
\nu\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathds{V}}}\right)^{\mathrm{O}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({\log(\nu\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathds{V}}}\right))}}}\right)}
(i.e., quasi-polynomial in
\nu\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathds{V}}}\right)
) for approximating
\Xi_{\mathds{V}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\lambda,\phi}}\right)
for
\lambda<\frac{\mathrm{e}}{\Delta_{\phi}}
via the following procedure:
We first draw a random graph
G\sim\zeta^{(n)}_{\mathds{V},\phi}
together with its (random) vertex locations
X=X_{1},\dots,X_{n}\in\mathds{V}^{n}
for
n
sufficiently large to satisfy
Theorem

1.1
and
Theorem

6.8
.
We then use the algorithm given in
[
47
]
together with the Weitz trees based on the neighborhood ordering
F_{X,G}
(see proof of
Theorem

6.7
) to approximate the hard-core partition function on
G
at vertex activity
\lambda\frac{\nu\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathds{V}}}\right)}{n}
.
An analogous procedure can be used to approximately sample from
P_{\mathds{V}}^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\lambda,\phi}}\right)}
with similar running time.
