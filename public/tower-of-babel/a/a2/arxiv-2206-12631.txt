Type systems and maximal subgroups of Thompson’s group V V
James Belk, Collin Bleak, Martyn Quick, Rachel Skipper

Abstract
We introduce the concept of a type system 𝒫 \mathcal{P} , that is, a partition on the set of finite words over the alphabet { 0 , 1 } \{0,1\} compatible with the partial action of Thompson’s group V V , and associate a subgroup Stab V ⁡ ( 𝒫 ) \operatorname{Stab}_{V}(\mathcal{P}) of V V . We classify the finite simple type systems and show that the stabilizers of various simple type systems, including all finite simple type systems, are maximal subgroups of V V . We also find an uncountable family of pairwise non-isomorphic maximal subgroups of V V . These maximal subgroups occur as stabilizers of infinite simple type systems and have not been described in previous literature: specifically, they do not arise as stabilizers in V V of finite sets of points in Cantor space. Finally, we show that two natural conditions on subgroups of V V (both related to primitivity) are each satisfied only by V V itself, giving new ways to recognise when a subgroup of V V is not actually proper.



Introduction

In this article, we investigate the maximal subgroups of Richard J. Thompson’s group
V
. The group
V
was first introduced in 1965
[
Tho65
]
as part of a triple of groups
F<T<V
where it was shown that these groups are all infinite, finitely presented groups, and that
T
and
V
are simple. These groups were then the first examples of infinite, finitely presented groups that were known to be simple. Indeed, until the arrival of the finitely presented infinite simple groups of Burger and Mozes in 1997
[
BM97
]
, all known examples of infinite, finitely presented simple groups were in some way modelled on, or built by extending, the constructions of
T
or
V
.

The Thompson groups in general have had a wide impact, appearing in the theory of many fields of mathematics, and playing host as examples of or counterexamples to numerous questions and conjectures about the existence of groups with various unusual properties. Some examples of where these groups arise include the word problem for groups
[
MT73
,
Tho80
]
, in homotopy and shape theory
[
Dyd77a
,
Dyd77b
,
FH93
]
, in the algebra of string rewriting
[
Coh93
]
, in the theory of diagram groups over semigroup presentations
[
GS97
]
, in group cohomology
[
Bro87
,
Bro92
,
BG85
]
, and even in dynamical systems and analysis
[
GS87
]
. There are now hundreds of research articles strongly connected to these groups, and all three groups have difficult questions associated with them (e.g., the question of whether or not
F
is amenable is a particularly well-known open question).

A particular area of abiding mystery for R. Thompson’s group
V
is its subgroup structure: both in terms of determining its lattice of subgroups as well as trying to understand the isomorphism types of its subgroups. Since all finite symmetric groups and hence all finite groups embed into
V
, as well as
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
and all free products of finite groups, one might guess that most naturally occurring finitely generated groups embed into
V
. However, the paper
[
BSD13
]
shows that even the group
\mathbb{Z}^{2}*\mathbb{Z}
fails to embed as a subgroup of
V
. Furthermore, it is also known that the Baumslag–Solitar groups
B(m,n)
, for
m\neq\pm n
, do not embed into
V
, as cyclic groups are undistorted in
V
(see
[
BBGL+13
,
BMN16
,
BCR18
]
). On the positive side, many known
\mathrm{co}\kern 0.70007pt\mathcal{CF}
groups embed in
V
and a form of Lehnert’s Conjecture is that a group is a
\mathrm{co}\kern 0.70007pt\mathcal{CF}
group if and only if it embeds as a finitely generated subgroup of
V
(see, e.g.,
[
Leh08
,
BMN16
,
Far18
]
). In terms of the lattice structure of the subgroups of
V
, a first observation is that, as
V
is finitely generated, every proper subgroup of
V
is contained in some maximal proper subgroup.

A standard way of representing the group
V
is as a subgroup of the automorphism group of the standard binary Cantor space
\mathfrak{C}
. It is a folklore result that the stabilizer in
V
of a point in
\mathfrak{C}
is a maximal subgroup of
V
(see Corollary
2.3
), with an explicit proof of this result for
F
in
[
Sav10
]
. In 2017 Golan and Sapir produced the first maximal subgroups of
F
that do not arise as point stabilizers
[
GS17a
]
or as index
p
subgroups of
F
for
p
a prime, and this has begun an active area of research for
F

[
GS17b
,
GS17c
,
AN21b
,
AN21a
]
. The groups arising in the above research can be interpreted as groups preserving certain sub-families of dyadic rationals. In contrast to this, our approach involves partitioning the set of finite prefixes of points in Cantor space and looking at the subgroups of
V
which preserve the given partition. This is a wholly new way of constructing maximal subgroups for Thompson-type groups.

It is well known that the partial action of
V
on the Cantor algebra of clopen subsets of
\mathfrak{C}
shares some characteristics with the natural action of a finite symmetric group, see, for example,
[
BQ17
]
. Our study of maximal subgroups of
V
further exploits this connection. Recall that a subgroup of the symmetric group of degree
n
is either intransitive (in which case it is contained in a direct product of smaller symmetric groups), transitive but imprimitive (in which case it is contained in a wreath product), or primitive. As a consequence, one has a description of the maximal subgroups of the symmetric group
S_{n}
and the famous O’Nan–Scott Theorem
[
AS85
]
is often used to provide further information on the possibilities for primitive maximal subgroups. The subgroups that we introduce can be viewed as analogous to the intransitive and imprimitive maximal subgroups of
S_{n}
. Let
\Omega
be the set of finite (perhaps empty) strings on the alphabet
\{0,1\}
. We think of these as being addresses of basic clopen sets in
\mathfrak{C}
. We say that a partition
\mathcal{P}
of
\Omega
is a
type system
if the corresponding equivalence relation
\sim
satisfies

\alpha\sim\beta\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\text{$\alpha 0\sim\beta 0$ and $\alpha 1\sim\beta 1$}

for all
\alpha,\beta\in\Omega
. The elements of
\mathcal{P}
are called
types
. Such a type system is
simple
if the partition
\mathcal{P}
is not a refinement of any other non-trivial type system. Every type system has an associated subgroup
\operatorname{Stab}_{V}(\mathcal{P})
that we define in Definition
3.3
(iii)
.
This can be viewed as the set of transformations in
V
that preserve a partition on the set of cones in
\mathfrak{C}
and is therefore the analogue of the wreath product from the permutation groups setting. Indeed, we prove that under natural conditions,
\operatorname{Stab}_{V}(\mathcal{P})
is a maximal subgroup of Thompson’s group
V
.

{restatable*}

thmFiniteSimpleThm

Let
\mathcal{P}
be a finite simple type system on
\Omega
. Then
\operatorname{Stab}_{V}(\mathcal{P})
is a maximal subgroup of
V
.

A major step in proving the theorem is to first fully classify which finite type systems can be simple. To do so, we consider an associated graph, called the
type graph
, which has one vertex for each type with directed edges from the type of
\alpha
to the types of
\alpha 0
and
\alpha 1
for all
\alpha\in\Omega
. We refer to a strongly connected component of this graph as a
nucleus
, and we classify simple type systems based on the number and characteristics of the nuclei.

{restatable*}

thmFiniteTypes

A finite simple type system on
\Omega
is either nuclear, atomic binuclear, or atomic quasinuclear.

Theorem
6.14
shows that the maximal subgroups of
V
associated with finite simple nuclear type systems are isomorphic to full groups of irreducible, branching subshifts of finite type (or occasionally, they are finite index overgroups of such a group). These groups were introduced by Matsumoto in
[
Mat15a
]
and studied extensively by Matui in
[
Mat15b
]
.

The taxonomy of simple type systems is somewhat complex, and also includes systems which need not be finite but which nonetheless lead to maximal subgroups.

{restatable*}

thmBranchType

Let
\mathcal{P}
be a simple type system on
\Omega
that is either multinuclear or atomic branching quasinuclear. Then
\operatorname{Stab}_{V}(\mathcal{P})
is a maximal subgroup of
V
.

It is easy to specify the point stabilizer of any given “irrational” point in Cantor space as the stabilizer of an infinite simple type system. Thus, constructing uncountably many distinct maximal subgroups of
V
is not a difficult task. However, it was not known if
V
admitted uncountably many pairwise non-isomorphic maximal subgroups. Also, all but one (up to automorphic image) of the previously known maximal subgroups arose as stabilizers of finite sets of points with the same infinite tail class (with the exception being
T
and its automorphic images). In contrast to these examples, our technique (see Section
7
) provides for the construction of an uncountable family of pairwise non-isomorphic maximal subgroups of
V
. Furthermore, these maximal subgroups are all “new”: they have never been described in the literature, none of these subgroups stabilize any finite set of points in
\mathfrak{C}
, and finally, they are not related in any obvious way to automorphic images of
T
(specifically, they do not preserve any circular order on a dense set of points in
\mathfrak{C}
).

{restatable*}

thmUncountablyManyIso

There is an uncountable family
\mathcal{M}
of pairwise non-isomorphic maximal subgroups of
V
. Moreover, every
G
in
\mathcal{M}
arises as a stabilizer of a simple nuclear type system, does not preserve any circular order on any dense set of points in
\mathfrak{C}
, and does not stabilize any finite set of points in
\mathfrak{C}
.

As alluded to above, there is an obvious family of (maximal) subgroups of
V
which do not preserve any type system, namely Thompson’s group
T
and its set of images under automorphisms of
V
. If we were to view stabilizers of type systems as analogues of imprimitive groups, then these last groups would play the role analogous to the primitive subgroups of
V
. We do not know if these are the only “primitive” maximal subgroups of
V
, and we mention various questions around this in Section
9
.

A second goal of this article is to provide theorems which can be used to detect when a subgroup of
V
is actually proper. In this vein, we consider notions in the setting of the partial action of
V
on the cones of
\mathfrak{C}
that relate to transitivity and to an alternative version of primitivity.

{restatable*}

thmTwoFoldTrans

Let
G
be a subgroup of Thompson’s group
V
that acts
2
-fold transitively on the set of proper cones of
\mathfrak{C}
. Then
G=V
.

We call our alternative form of primitivity (in the setting of partial actions on cones)
swap-primitivity
and prove the following:

{restatable*}

thmswapPrimitiveIsV

Let
G
be a subgroup of
V
and suppose that
G
is swap-primitive. Then
G=V
.

Section
9
discusses several questions that we have not been able to address. The first of which is below.

Question 1
.

Suppose
G
is a maximal subgroup of
V
which does not preserve a type system. Is
G
an automorphic image of
T
?

1
Background

In this section, we define Thompson’s group
V
and set up the notation for the rest of the paper.

First, let
\mathfrak{C}
denote the Cantor set consisting of the infinite words over the alphabet
\{0,1\}
. Let
\Omega
be the set of all finite words in the same alphabet
\{0,1\}
. We refer to the set
\Omega
as the set of
addresses
. Any address
\alpha
determines a
cone

\alpha\mathfrak{C}
, which is one of the basic clopen subsets of Cantor space
\mathfrak{C}
:

\alpha\mathfrak{C}=\{\,\alpha w\mid w\in\mathfrak{C}\,\}.

We write
\mathopen{|}\alpha\mathclose{|}
for the
length
of the address
\alpha
as a word in
\{0,1\}
. If
\alpha
is any finite word, then
\overline{\alpha}
denotes the element
\alpha\alpha\dots
of
\mathfrak{C}
obtained by repeating
\alpha
infinitely many times.

We say a set of cones
\alpha_{1}\mathfrak{C}
,
\alpha_{2}\mathfrak{C}
, …,
\alpha_{n}\mathfrak{C}
(respectively, a set of addresses
\alpha_{1}
,
\alpha_{2}
, …,
\alpha_{n}
) has
small support
if
\alpha_{1}\mathfrak{C}\cup\alpha_{2}\mathfrak{C}\cup\dots\cup\alpha_{n}\mathfrak{C}\neq\mathfrak{C}
. We say two cones
\alpha\mathfrak{C}
and
\beta\mathfrak{C}
(respectively, two addressses
\alpha
and
\beta
) are
incomparable
if
\alpha\mathfrak{C}\cap\beta\mathfrak{C}=\varnothing
. We write
\alpha\perp\beta
to denote that the addresses
\alpha
and
\beta
are incomparable. Note that two cones are either incomparable or there is an inclusion of one into the other. If
\alpha\mathfrak{C}\supseteq\beta\mathfrak{C}
, we will write
\alpha\preccurlyeq\beta
for the addresses. This is equivalent to the word
\alpha
being a prefix of the word
\beta
. For this reason, for
\alpha\in\Omega
and
w\in\mathfrak{C}
, we shall also write
\alpha\preccurlyeq w
if
\alpha
is a prefix of
w
.

Let
\alpha_{1}\mathfrak{C}
,
\alpha_{2}\mathfrak{C}
, …,
\alpha_{n}\mathfrak{C}
and
\beta_{1}\mathfrak{C}
,
\beta_{2}\mathfrak{C}
, …,
\beta_{n}\mathfrak{C}
be two partitions of
\mathfrak{C}
into disjoint cones. We can then define a map
\mathfrak{C}\to\mathfrak{C}
given by
\alpha_{i}w\mapsto\beta_{i}w
for
i=1
,
2
, …,
n
and all
w\in\mathfrak{C}
. Such a map will be called a
prefix substitution map
. If
\alpha
and
\beta
are incomparable addresses, we denote by
(\alpha\;\;\beta)
the prefix substitution map given by
\alpha w\mapsto\beta w
and
\beta w\mapsto\alpha w
for
w\in\mathfrak{C}
, and with all other points of
\mathfrak{C}
fixed. We shall refer to such prefix substitution maps as
transpositions
. We extend this in the natural way to define disjoint cycle notation for prefix substitution maps that arise as finite permutations of disjoint cones.

We are now ready to define Thompson’s group
V
.

Definition 1.1
.

Thompson’s group

V
is the set of all prefix substitution maps on
\mathfrak{C}
with multiplication given by function composition.

Note that Thompson’s group
V
has an alternative definition using paired tree diagram. We direct the reader to
[
CFP96
]
for a detailed description of this viewpoint.

The action of
V
on the Cantor set induces a partial right action of
V
on the set
\Omega
of addresses.

Definition 1.2
.

Let
g\in V
and
\alpha
be an address. If
(\alpha\mathfrak{C})^{g}=\beta\mathfrak{C}
for some address
\beta
and
g
is given by the prefix substitution
\alpha\mapsto\beta
on
\alpha\mathfrak{C}
, then we define
\alpha^{g}=\beta
. If there is no address
\beta
for which this holds, then
\alpha^{g}
is undefined.

It follows immediately from the definition that if
g\in V
then
\alpha^{g}
is defined for all but finitely many addresses
\alpha\in\Omega
. The following observations are straightforward and will be used without comment in what follows.

Lemma 1.3
.

Let
g
and
h
be elements of
V
and
\alpha
and
\beta
be addresses in
\Omega
.

(i)

If
\alpha^{g}
is defined and
\alpha\preccurlyeq\beta
, then
\beta^{g}
is defined. Moreover, if
\beta=\alpha\eta
for some word
\eta
, then
\beta^{g}=(\alpha^{g})\eta
.

(ii)

Suppose that
\alpha^{g}
is defined. Then
(\alpha^{g})^{h}
is defined if and only if
\alpha^{gh}
is defined and, when it is,
(\alpha^{g})^{h}=\alpha^{gh}
.

2
Stabilizers of finite sets of fixed tail class

As a starting point for our study of maximal subgroups of
V
, we will prove that the stabilizer of any finite set of points in
\mathfrak{C}
which all have the same tail class is a maximal subgroup. To do this, we will first apply a criterion for primitive group actions given in
[
DM96
]
. Given a group
G
acting transitively on a set
S
and points
a,b\in S
, denote by
\Delta=\{\,(a,b)^{g}\mid g\in G\,\}
the orbit of the pair
(a,b)
and by
\Gamma(a,b)
the associated
orbital graph
; that is,
\Gamma(a,b)
has vertex set
S
and edge set
\Delta
. The required criterion is:

Proposition 2.1
(
[
DM96
, Theorem 3.2A]
).

Let
G
be a group acting transitively on a set
S
. Then the action of
G
on
S
is primitive if and only if the orbital graph
\Gamma(a,b)
is connected for every distinct
a,b\in S
.

Theorem 2.2
.

Let
G
be a group that acts
2k
-transitively on a set
X
with
\mathopen{|}X\mathclose{|}\geqslant 2k+1
for some positive integer
k
. Then
G
acts primitively on the set of subsets of
X
with cardinality
k
.

Proof.

Let
S
denote the set of subsets of
X
with cardinality
k
. Since
G
is, in particular,
k
-transitive on
X
, it is the case that
G
acts transitively on
S
. Let
A
and
B
be a pair of distinct subsets of
X
with
\mathopen{|}A\mathclose{|}=\mathopen{|}B\mathclose{|}=k
, let
\Delta=\{\,(A,B)^{g}\mid g\in G\,\}
be the orbit of the pair
(A,B)
and
\Gamma
be the orbital graph as above. We shall show that
\Gamma
is connected as an undirected graph.

Let
C
be any subset of
X
with
\mathopen{|}C\mathclose{|}=k
. Take
x\in X\setminus C
and
y\in C
. Define
C^{\prime}
to be the union of
C\setminus\{y\}
with
\{x\}
. Since
\mathopen{|}X\mathclose{|}\geqslant 2k+1
, there is a subset
D
of
X
such that
\mathopen{|}C\cap D\mathclose{|}=\mathopen{|}A\cap B\mathclose{|}
,
\mathopen{|}D\mathclose{|}=k
, and
x,y\notin D
. As
G
acts
2k
-transitively, there exists
g\in G
such that
(A,B)^{g}=(C,D)
; that is,
(C,D)\in\Delta
. Now as
D
does not contain
x
or
y
, it is also the case that
\mathopen{|}C^{\prime}\cap D\mathclose{|}=\mathopen{|}C\cap D\mathclose{|}
. Hence, using
2k
-transitivity again, there exists
h\in G
such that
(C,D)^{h}=(C^{\prime},D)
. Thus
(C^{\prime},D)\in\Delta
. This shows that there are (undirected) edges joining
C
and
D
and joining
C^{\prime}
and
D
in
\Gamma
. Hence we may travel from the subset
C
to a subset obtained by removing one element and replacing it by another. By induction there is a path in
\Gamma
from
C
to any other subset of cardinality
k
. The result now follows by Proposition
2.1
.

Recall that two points
u
and
v
in
\mathfrak{C}
have the same
tail class
if
u=\alpha w
and
v=\beta w
for some finite words
\alpha,\beta\in\Omega
and some tail
w\in\mathfrak{C}
. Fix a tail class
X
. Then since a prefix substitution preserves the tail class, Thompson’s group
V
acts on
X
. Moreover, it is straightforward to show that this action is highly transitive; that is,
V
is
n
-transitive for every positive integer
n
. In particular, we may apply Theorem
2.2
for any value of
k
. Hence
V
acts primitively on the set of subsets of
X
of cardinality
k
. The corresponding stabilizer is therefore a maximal subgroup of
V
and we have established the following:

Corollary 2.3
.

Let
D
be a non-empty finite subset of
\mathfrak{C}
consisting of elements of the same tail class. Then
\operatorname{Stab}_{V}(D)
is a maximal subgroup of
V
.

3
Labelling subgroups of
V

In this section, we use the partial action of
V
on
\Omega
to define a collection of subgroups of
V
that will provide many of our examples of maximal subgroups.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
\sim
be an equivalence relation on the set
\Omega
of addresses.

(i)

We say that
\sim
is
coherent
if, for all addresses
\alpha
and
\beta
,
\alpha\sim\beta
implies that both
\alpha 0\sim\beta 0
and
\alpha 1\sim\beta 1
.

(ii)

We say that
\sim
is
reduced
if it has the property that if
\alpha
and
\beta
are addresses such that both
\alpha 0\sim\beta 0
and
\alpha 1\sim\beta 1
, then
\alpha\sim\beta
.

(iii)

If
\sim
is an equivalence relation on
\Omega
that is both coherent and reduced, we refer to the resulting collection
\mathcal{P}
of equivalence classes on
\Omega
as a
type system
.

(iv)

If
\mathcal{P}
is a type system, we refer to the equivalence classes in
\mathcal{P}
as
types
. For any address
\alpha
, if
\alpha
belongs to the equivalence class
P\in\mathcal{P}
then we say that
\alpha
has
\mathcal{P}
-type

P
. When it is clear which type system is under consideration, we shall simply refer to the
type
of
\alpha
.

The definition of coherent and reduced for an equivalence relation
\sim
on
\Omega
ensures that type systems interact well with the partial action of Thompson’s group
V
.
Notationally, if
P
is a type in some type system
\mathcal{P}
, then we shall denote by
P_{0}
the type of
\alpha 0
where
\alpha
is any address whose type is
P
. Analogously we denote
P_{1}
to be the type of
\alpha 1
. The assumption that
\sim
is coherent ensures that
P_{0}
and
P_{1}
are well-defined types and hence the free monoid with basis
\{0,1\}
has an action on the type system
\mathcal{P}
.

Remark 3.2
.

Under the assumption that
\sim
is coherent and reduced, the equivalence relation actually determines a congruence on a free Cantor algebra. Then
\mathcal{P}
embeds in a natural way in the quotient Cantor algebra determined by this congruence. We shall, however, not make explicit use of such Cantor algebras here and instead will explore this in a future article.

We now introduce certain subsets of
V
determined by an equivalence relation on the addresses (or, equivalently, on the set of cones in
\mathfrak{C}
).

Definition 3.3
.

Let
\sim
be an equivalence relation on the set
\Omega
of addresses.

(i)

We say that an element
g
of
V

fixes the parts
of
\sim
if for all but finitely many
\alpha\in\Omega
,
\alpha^{g}
is defined and
\alpha^{g}\sim\alpha
.

(ii)

We say that
g\in V

stabilizes

\sim
if there is a subset
\Omega_{0}\subseteq\Omega
with
\mathopen{|}\Omega\setminus\Omega_{0}\mathclose{|}<\infty
such that
\alpha^{g}
is defined for all
\alpha\in\Omega_{0}
and, for
\alpha,\beta\in\Omega_{0}
,
\alpha\sim\beta
if and only if
\alpha^{g}\sim\beta^{g}
. (That is, for all but finitely many addresses,
g
preserves equivalence of addresses under
\sim
.)

(iii)

Let
\mathcal{P}
denote the partition of
\Omega
determined by the equivalence relation
\sim
. We define

\operatorname{Fix}_{V}(\mathcal{P})=\{\,g\in V\mid\text{$g$~fixes the parts of~$\sim$}\,\},

i.e., the set of elements of
V
that
fix the parts in
\mathcal{P}
, and

\operatorname{Stab}_{V}(\mathcal{P})=\{\,g\in V\mid\text{$g$~stabilizes~$\sim$}\,\},

i.e., the set of elements of
V
that
stabilize the partition
\mathcal{P}
.

By definition,
\operatorname{Fix}_{V}(\mathcal{P})\subseteq\operatorname{Stab}_{V}(\mathcal{P})
and we shall now observe that they are indeed subgroups of
V
. The lemma also indicates one of the gains of assuming our relation is coherent: one has greater control over when an element belongs to
\operatorname{Fix}_{V}(\mathcal{P})
. In fact, there is little lost by assuming that
\mathcal{P}
is a type system as we shall observe in Lemma
3.7
. To aid our arguments, we shall use the notation
\Omega_{0}\subseteq_{cf}\Omega
to indicate that
\Omega_{0}
is a cofinite subset of
\Omega
; that is, when
\mathopen{|}\Omega\setminus\Omega_{0}\mathclose{|}<\infty
.

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
\sim
be an equivalence relation on the set
\Omega
of addresses and let
\mathcal{P}
be its associated partition. Then:

(i)

\operatorname{Fix}_{V}(\mathcal{P})
is a subgroup of
V
.

(ii)

\operatorname{Stab}_{V}(\mathcal{P})
is a subgroup of
V
.

(iii)

\operatorname{Fix}_{V}(\mathcal{P})
is a normal subgroup of
\operatorname{Stab}_{V}(\mathcal{P})
.

(iv)

Suppose that
\sim
is coherent and let
g\in V
. Then
g\in\operatorname{Fix}_{V}(\mathcal{P})
if and only if there exists a partition
\alpha_{1}\mathfrak{C},\ldots,\alpha_{n}\mathfrak{C}
of
\mathfrak{C}
into cones so that each
\alpha_{i}^{g}
is defined and
\alpha_{i}^{g}\sim\alpha_{i}
.

Proof.

(i)
Certainly the identity element belongs to
\operatorname{Fix}_{V}(\mathcal{P})
.
Suppose
g,h\in\operatorname{Fix}_{V}(\mathcal{P})
. Then there exist
\Omega_{1},\Omega_{2}\subseteq_{cf}\Omega
such that
\alpha^{g}
is defined and satisfies
\alpha^{g}\sim\alpha
for all
\alpha\in\Omega_{1}
and such that
\alpha^{h}
is defined and satisfies
\alpha^{h}\sim\alpha
for all
\alpha\in\Omega_{2}
.
It is straightforward to observe
\beta^{g^{-1}}\sim\beta
for all
\beta\in\Omega_{1}^{g}
and
\Omega_{1}^{g}\subseteq_{cf}\Omega
, so
g^{-1}\in\operatorname{Fix}_{V}(\mathcal{P})
.
Let
\Omega_{0}=\Omega_{1}\cap(\Omega_{2}\cap\Omega_{1}^{g})^{g^{-1}}
. Then
\Omega_{0}\subseteq_{cf}\Omega
and if
\alpha\in\Omega_{0}
, then
\alpha^{g}
is defined, belongs to
\Omega_{2}
and satisfies
\alpha^{g}\sim\alpha
. Hence
\alpha^{gh}=(\alpha^{g})^{h}\sim\alpha^{g}\sim\alpha
. This shows that
gh\in\operatorname{Fix}_{V}(\mathcal{P})
. We conclude that
\operatorname{Fix}_{V}(\mathcal{P})
is indeed a subgroup of
V
.

(ii)
Suppose
g,h\in\operatorname{Stab}_{V}(\mathcal{P})
. Then there exist subsets
\Omega_{1},\Omega_{2}\subseteq_{cf}\Omega
such that
\alpha^{g}
is defined for all
\alpha\in\Omega_{1}
,
\alpha^{g}\sim\beta^{g}
if and only if
\alpha\sim\beta
whenever
\alpha,\beta\in\Omega_{1}
,
\alpha^{h}
is defined for all
\alpha\in\Omega_{2}
and
\alpha^{h}\sim\beta^{h}
if and only if
\alpha\sim\beta
whenever
\alpha,\beta\in\Omega_{2}
.
As in
(i)
, it is straightforward to observe
\gamma^{g^{-1}}\sim\delta^{g^{-1}}
if and only if
\gamma\sim\delta
for
\gamma,\delta\in\Omega_{1}^{g}
and hence
g^{-1}\in\operatorname{Stab}_{V}(\mathcal{P})
.
Similarly, for
\Omega_{0}=\Omega_{1}\cap(\Omega_{2}\cap\Omega_{1}^{g})^{g^{-1}}\subseteq_{cf}\Omega
, it is the case that
\alpha^{gh}
is defined for all
\alpha\in\Omega_{1}
. Let
\alpha,\beta\in\Omega_{0}
and suppose
\alpha\sim\beta
. Then
\alpha^{g}\sim\beta^{g}
since
\alpha,\beta\in\Omega_{1}
and then
\alpha^{gh}\sim\beta^{gh}
since
\alpha^{g},\beta^{g}\in\Omega_{2}
. This argument reverses and hence
gh\in\operatorname{Stab}_{V}(\mathcal{P})
.
We deduce that
\operatorname{Stab}_{V}(\mathcal{P})
is a subgroup of
V
.

(iii)
Let
h\in\operatorname{Fix}_{V}(\mathcal{P})
and
g\in\operatorname{Stab}_{V}(\mathcal{P})
. Then there exist
\Omega_{1},\Omega_{2}\subseteq_{cf}\Omega
such that
\alpha^{h}
is defined and
\alpha^{h}\sim\alpha
for all
\alpha\in\Omega_{1}
and such that
\alpha^{g}
is defined for all
\alpha\in\Omega_{2}
and, for
\alpha,\beta\in\Omega_{2}
,
\alpha\sim\beta
if and only if
\alpha^{g}\sim\beta^{g}
.
Then
\bigl((\Omega_{1}\cap\Omega_{2})^{h}\cap\Omega_{2}\bigr)^{h^{-1}}
is a well-defined cofinite subset of
\Omega
contained in
\Omega_{1}
. Hence we can set
\Omega_{0}=\bigl((\Omega_{1}\cap\Omega_{2})^{h}\cap\Omega_{2}\bigr)^{h^{-1}g}\subseteq_{cf}\Omega
.
If
\alpha\in\Omega_{0}
, then
\alpha^{g^{-1}h}\in\Omega_{2}
and so
\alpha^{g^{-1}hg}
is defined. Furthermore,
\alpha^{g^{-1}}\in\bigl((\Omega_{1}\cap\Omega_{2})^{h}\cap\Omega_{2}\bigr)^{h^{-1}}\subseteq\Omega_{1}
and so
\alpha^{g^{-1}h}\sim\alpha^{g^{-1}}
. Since both
\alpha^{g^{-1}},\alpha^{g^{-1}h}\in\Omega_{2}
, we deduce
\alpha^{g^{-1}hg}\sim\alpha^{g^{-1}g}=\alpha
. This shows
g^{-1}hg\in\operatorname{Fix}_{V}(\mathcal{P})
.

(iv)
Suppose that
g
is given by a prefix replacement map with domain cones
\alpha_{1}\mathfrak{C}
,
\alpha_{2}\mathfrak{C}
, …,
\alpha_{k}\mathfrak{C}
and that
\alpha_{i}^{g}\sim\alpha_{i}
for
i=1
,
2
, …,
k
.
Let
\Omega_{0}
be the set of addresses having some
\alpha_{i}
as a prefix, so that
\Omega_{0}\subseteq_{cf}\Omega
. If
\alpha\in\Omega_{0}
, say
\alpha=\alpha_{i}\eta
for some word
\eta
, then by repeated use of the fact that
\sim
is coherent, we conclude that
\alpha^{g}=(\alpha_{i}^{g})\eta\sim\alpha_{i}\eta=\alpha
. Hence
g\in\operatorname{Fix}_{V}(\mathcal{P})
.

Conversely, suppose
g\in\operatorname{Fix}_{V}(\mathcal{P})
. Let
\Omega_{0}\subseteq_{cf}\Omega
such that
\alpha^{g}
is defined for all
\alpha\in\Omega_{0}
and satisfies
\alpha^{g}\sim\alpha
. By repeated subdivision, we can express
g
as a prefix replacement map with all domain cones
\alpha_{i}\mathfrak{C}
indexed by addresses
\alpha_{i}
in
\Omega_{0}
. Then
\alpha_{i}^{g}\sim\alpha_{i}
for all
i
, as required.

We shall view an equivalence relation
\sim
as providing a “labelling” of the addresses according to which equivalence classes they belong to; that is, in the case of a type system, according to the type of the addresses. For a coherent equivalence relation
\sim
with corresponding partition
\mathcal{P}
, we shall call
\operatorname{Fix}_{V}(\mathcal{P})
and
\operatorname{Stab}_{V}(\mathcal{P})
the
labelling subgroups
for
\mathcal{P}
.

If
\mathcal{P}
has only finitely many finite classes,
\operatorname{Fix}_{V}(\mathcal{P})
is in fact the kernel of the action of
\operatorname{Stab}_{V}(\mathcal{P})
on the collection of infinite equivalence classes as we shall demonstrate (see Lemma
3.6
(ii)
below). Let
\sim
be an equivalence relation on
\Omega
and
\mathcal{P}
be its associated partition. If
g\in\operatorname{Stab}_{V}(\mathcal{P})
then there exists some subset
\Omega_{0}\subseteq_{cf}\Omega
such that
\alpha^{g}
is defined for all
\alpha\in\Omega_{0}
and, for
\alpha,\beta\in\Omega_{0}
,
\alpha\sim\beta
if and only if
\alpha^{g}\sim\beta^{g}
. As a consequence, if
P\in\mathcal{P}
is an
infinite
equivalence class, then
\alpha^{g}\sim\beta^{g}
for all
\alpha,\beta\in P\cap\Omega_{0}
and hence
(P\cap\Omega_{0})^{g}\subseteq Q
for some, necessarily infinite, class
Q\in\mathcal{P}
. We can accordingly define an action of
\operatorname{Stab}_{V}(\mathcal{P})
on the infinite classes in
\mathcal{P}
.

Definition 3.5
.

If
P
is an infinite class in
\mathcal{P}
and
g\in\operatorname{Stab}_{V}(\mathcal{P})
, define
P^{g}
to be the infinite class
Q\in\mathcal{P}
such that
\alpha^{g}\in Q
for all but finitely many
\alpha\in P
.

Lemma 3.6
.

Let
\sim
be an equivalence relation on
\Omega
and let
\mathcal{P}
be the associated partition. Suppose that the collection of infinite classes in
\mathcal{P}
, denoted
\mathcal{P}^{\ast}
, is non-empty. Then:

(i)

\operatorname{Stab}_{V}(\mathcal{P})
acts on
\mathcal{P}^{\ast}
via the above definition and
\operatorname{Fix}_{V}(\mathcal{P})
is contained in the kernel of this action.

(ii)

If
\mathcal{P}
contains only finitely many finite classes, then
\operatorname{Fix}_{V}(\mathcal{P})
is the kernel of the action of
\operatorname{Stab}_{V}(\mathcal{P})
on
\mathcal{P}^{\ast}
.

Proof.

For part
(i)
the proof is straightforward.
For part
(ii)
, suppose that
\mathcal{P}
contains only finitely many finite classes. Let
g
belong to the kernel of the action of
\operatorname{Stab}_{V}(\mathcal{P})
on
\mathcal{P}^{\ast}
. There exists some
\Omega_{0}\subseteq_{cf}\Omega
such that
\alpha^{g}
is defined for all
\alpha\in\Omega_{0}
and, for
\alpha,\beta\in\Omega_{0}
,
\alpha\sim\beta
if and only if
\alpha^{g}\sim\beta^{g}
. If
P\in\mathcal{P}^{\ast}
, then
P^{g}=P
by definition, so
\alpha^{g}\in P
for all but finitely many
\alpha\in P
. In particular, there exists some
\alpha\in P\cap\Omega_{0}
such that
\alpha^{g}\in P
and then
\beta^{g}\sim\alpha^{g}
for all
\beta\in P\cap\Omega_{0}
. This shows
(P\cap\Omega_{0})^{g}\subseteq P
; that is,
\alpha^{g}\sim\alpha
for all
\alpha\in P\cap\Omega_{0}
.

Take
\Omega_{1}=\Omega_{0}\cap\bigcup\mathcal{P}^{\ast}
; that is, the set of addresses in
\Omega_{0}
that belong to an infinite class. Since
\mathcal{P}
has only finitely many finite classes,
\Omega_{1}
is a cofinite subset of
\Omega
. Then
\alpha^{g}
is defined for all
\alpha\in\Omega_{1}
and, by the previous paragraph,
\alpha^{g}\sim\alpha
for all
\alpha\in\Omega_{1}
. This shows
g\in\operatorname{Fix}_{V}(\mathcal{P})
and completes the proof of part
(ii)
.

Lemma 3.7
.

Let
\sim
be an equivalence relation on
\Omega
and
\mathcal{P}
be its associated partition. Define, for
\alpha,\beta\in\Omega
,
\alpha\approx\beta
if and only if
\alpha\eta\sim\beta\eta
for all but finitely many words
\eta
. Then
\approx
is a coherent and reduced equivalence relation on
\Omega
such that

(i)

if
\sim
is coherent and
\alpha\sim\beta
for some
\alpha,\beta\in\Omega
, then
\alpha\approx\beta
;

(ii)

if
\mathcal{Q}
is the type system determined by
\approx
, then
\operatorname{Fix}_{V}(\mathcal{Q})=\operatorname{Fix}_{V}(\mathcal{P})
and
\operatorname{Stab}_{V}(\mathcal{P})\leqslant\operatorname{Stab}_{V}(\mathcal{Q})
.

Proof.

It is straightforward to verify that
\approx
is an equivalence relation on
\Omega
and that it is both coherent and reduced. If we assume that
\sim
is coherent, then part
(i)
also follows directly.

For part
(ii)
, suppose that
g\in\operatorname{Fix}_{V}(\mathcal{P})
. Then there exists
\Omega_{0}\subseteq_{cf}\Omega
such that
\alpha^{g}
is defined and
\alpha^{g}\sim\alpha
for all
\alpha\in\Omega_{0}
. If
\alpha\in\Omega_{0}
, then
\alpha\eta\in\Omega_{0}
for all but finitely many words
\eta
and
\alpha^{g}\eta=(\alpha\eta)^{g}\sim\alpha\eta
for all such
\eta
. Hence
\alpha^{g}\approx\alpha
for
\alpha\in\Omega_{0}
and we deduce
g\in\operatorname{Fix}_{V}(\mathcal{Q})
. Conversely, suppose
g\in\operatorname{Fix}_{V}(\mathcal{Q})
and let
\Omega_{0}\subseteq_{cf}\Omega
such that
\alpha^{g}
is defined and
\alpha^{g}\approx\alpha
for all
\alpha\in\Omega_{0}
. There exist incomparable addresses
\beta_{1}
,
\beta_{2}
, …,
\beta_{k}
in
\Omega_{0}
such that
\mathfrak{C}
is the disjoint union of the cones
\beta_{i}\mathfrak{C}
. Now,
\beta_{i}^{g}\approx\beta
for each
i
, so there exists
\Delta_{i}\subseteq_{cf}\Omega
such that
(\beta_{i}^{g})\eta\sim\beta_{i}\eta
for all
\eta\in\Delta_{i}
. Set
\Omega_{1}=\{\,\beta_{i}\eta\mid\eta\in\Delta_{i},\;1\leqslant i\leqslant k\,\}
. Then
\Omega_{1}\subseteq_{cf}\Omega
and
\alpha^{g}\sim\alpha
for all
\alpha\in\Omega_{1}
; that is,
g\in\operatorname{Fix}_{V}(\mathcal{P})
. This demonstrates that
\operatorname{Fix}_{V}(\mathcal{Q})=\operatorname{Fix}_{V}(\mathcal{P})
.

Finally, let
g\in\operatorname{Stab}_{V}(\mathcal{P})
. There exists
\Omega_{2}\subseteq_{cf}\Omega
such that
\alpha^{g}
is defined for all
\alpha\in\Omega_{2}
and, for
\alpha,\beta\in\Omega_{2}
,
\alpha\sim\beta
if and only if
\alpha^{g}\sim\beta^{g}
. By removing at most finitely many addresses, we may assume that
\alpha 0,\alpha 1\in\Omega_{2}
for all
\alpha\in\Omega_{2}
. Let
\alpha,\beta\in\Omega_{2}
. If
\alpha\approx\beta
then
\alpha\eta\sim\beta\eta
for all but finitely many words
\eta
. All such
\alpha\eta
and
\beta\eta
are in
\Omega_{2}
by our assumption and hence
(\alpha\eta)^{g}\sim(\beta\eta)^{g}
for all but finitely many
\eta
; that is,
\alpha^{g}\eta\sim\beta^{g}\eta
. Thus
\alpha^{g}\approx\beta^{g}
.

Conversely suppose
\alpha^{g}\approx\beta^{g}
. Then
(\alpha\eta)^{g}=\alpha^{g}\eta\sim\beta^{g}\eta=(\beta\eta)^{g}
for all but finitely many words
\eta
. Since
\alpha\eta,\beta\eta\in\Omega_{2}
, we deduce
\alpha\eta\sim\beta\eta
for such
\eta
. Hence
\alpha\approx\beta
. In conclusion, we have shown that
g\in\operatorname{Stab}_{V}(\mathcal{Q})
and established the inclusion
\operatorname{Stab}_{V}(\mathcal{P})\leqslant\operatorname{Stab}_{V}(\mathcal{Q})
.

Lemma
3.7
shows that we lose little by assuming that
\mathcal{P}
is a type system rather than working with some arbitrary equivalence relation. Accordingly, we shall work with type systems from this point forward. The following gives a characterization of when an element belongs to
\operatorname{Stab}_{V}(\mathcal{P})
under this hypothesis.

Lemma 3.8
.

Let
\mathcal{P}
be a type system on
\Omega
and
\sim
be its associated equivalence relation. Let
g\in V
and let
\Gamma
be any set of addresses with finite complement such that
\alpha^{g}
is defined for all
\alpha\in\Gamma
.
Then
g\in\operatorname{Stab}_{V}(\mathcal{P})
if and only if, for all
\alpha,\beta\in\Gamma
,
\alpha\sim\beta
if and only if
\alpha^{g}\sim\beta^{g}
.

Proof.

Certainly if the condition holds then
g\in\operatorname{Stab}_{V}(\mathcal{P})
when we take
\Omega_{0}=\Gamma
where
\Omega_{0}
is as in Definition
3.3
.
Conversely, suppose
g\in\operatorname{Stab}_{V}(\mathcal{P})
. Then there exists
\Omega_{0}\subseteq_{cf}\Omega
such that
\alpha^{g}
is defined for all
\alpha\in\Omega_{0}
and, for
\alpha,\beta\in\Omega_{0}
,
\alpha\sim\beta
if and only if
\alpha^{g}\sim\beta^{g}
. As
\mathopen{|}\Omega\setminus\Omega_{0}\mathclose{|}
is finite, there is a positive integer
n
such that
\delta\in\Omega_{0}
whenever
\mathopen{|}\delta\mathclose{|}\geqslant n
.

Let
\alpha,\beta\in\Gamma
and suppose
\alpha\sim\beta
. If
\eta
is any word of length
n
, then
\alpha\eta,\beta\eta\in\Omega_{0}
and
\alpha\eta\sim\beta\eta
since
\sim
is coherent. Therefore
(\alpha^{g})\eta=(\alpha\eta)^{g}\sim(\beta\eta)^{g}=(\beta^{g})\eta
for all such words
\eta
. Since
\sim
is reduced, we now conclude
\alpha^{g}\sim\beta^{g}
.
A similar argument shows that if
\alpha^{g}\sim\beta^{g}
, then
\alpha\sim\beta
.
This establishes the required condition.

Definition 3.12
.

(i)

A type system
\mathcal{Q}
(determined by the equivalence relation
\approx
) is said to be a
quotient
of the type system
\mathcal{P}
(determined by the equivalence relation
\sim
) if, for all
\alpha,\beta\in\Omega
,
\alpha\sim\beta
implies
\alpha\approx\beta
.

(ii)

The
trivial relation
on
\Omega
is that given by equality: two addresses
\alpha
and
\beta
are related under this relation only when
\alpha=\beta
. The
universal relation
is given by
\alpha\sim\beta
for all
\alpha,\beta\in\Omega
. The partitions determined by these two equivalence relations are both examples of type systems.

(iii)

A type system
\mathcal{P}
is said to be
simple
if its only proper quotient is the one determined by the universal relation.

(iv)

A type system
\mathcal{P}
is said to be
finite
if it consists of only finitely many equivalence classes on
\Omega
.

The language of “quotient” here is appropriate since if
\mathcal{Q}
is a quotient of the type system
\mathcal{P}
, then there is an associated surjective map
\mathcal{P}\to\mathcal{Q}
. Also observe that every type system is a quotient of the one determined by the trivial relation, while the type system for the universal relation is a quotient of every type system. We now observe that for most type systems,
\operatorname{Stab}_{V}(\mathcal{P})
and
\operatorname{Fix}_{V}(\mathcal{P})
are in fact proper subgroups of
V
.

Lemma 3.13
.

Let
\mathcal{P}
be a type system on
\Omega
with associated equivalence relation
\sim
that is neither the trivial nor the universal relation. Then
\operatorname{Stab}_{V}(\mathcal{P})\neq V
.

Proof.

Since
\sim
is not the trivial relation, there are distinct addresses
\alpha
and
\beta
such that
\alpha\sim\beta
. If
\alpha
were a prefix of
\beta
, then there is a choice of letter
x\in\{0,1\}
such that
\alpha x\perp\beta x
. Hence, by coherence, there is a pair of incomparable addresses related under
\sim
. If it were the case that all triples of incomparable addresses were related under
\sim
, then any pair of addresses of length at least
2
would be related under
\sim
and, as it is reduced, we would conclude
\sim
is the universal relation. Hence there exist pairwise incomparable addresses
\alpha
,
\beta
and
\gamma
such that
\alpha\sim\beta\nsim\gamma
. Then
(\alpha\;\;\gamma)\notin\operatorname{Stab}_{V}(\mathcal{P})
by Lemma
3.8
and so
\operatorname{Stab}_{V}(\mathcal{P})
is a proper subgroup of
V
.

We shall associate two additional structures to a type system. This first is a graph which encodes how the type changes as one descends the binary rooted tree whose vertices are labelled by the addresses in
\Omega
. The second is a semigroup that will enable us to extend the notion of “type” of a cone to any clopen subset of
\mathfrak{C}
.

Definition 3.14
.

Let
\mathcal{P}
be a type system with
\sim
its associated equivalence relation on
\Omega
. We associate a directed graph
\Gamma(\mathcal{P})
, called the
type graph
, as follows.
The vertices of
\Gamma(\mathcal{P})
are the types in
\mathcal{P}
. If
P
is a type, say with some address
\alpha
of type
P
, then there is an edge labelled
0
from
P
to
Q
if the address
\alpha 0
has type
Q
. Similarly there is an edge labelled
1
from
P
to
R
if the address
\alpha 1
has type
R
.

Note that the fact that
\sim
is coherent ensures that the definition of the edges in
\Gamma(\mathcal{P})
is independent of the choice of representative
\alpha\in P
. Hence this graph is well-defined.

Notation 3.15
.

Let
\mathcal{P}
be a type system and
P\in\mathcal{P}
, say with some representative
\alpha
. We write
P_{\gamma}
for the type of the address
\alpha\gamma
for each
\gamma\in\Omega
.

The coherence of
\sim
ensures this is well-defined independent of the choice of
\alpha
. Furthermore, if one starts at vertex
P
in the graph
\Gamma(\mathcal{P})
and follows the path labelled by the word
\gamma
, then this path terminates at the vertex
P_{\gamma}
. In particular,
P_{0}
and
P_{1}
are the two vertices adjacent to
P
and correspond to the types of the two descendants of
\alpha
in the binary rooted tree labelled by the addresses in
\Omega
.

Observe that if
P
is an infinite class in
\mathcal{P}
then both
P_{0}
and
P_{1}
are also infinite classes. Hence there is a subgraph
\Gamma^{\ast}
of
\Gamma(\mathcal{P})
whose vertices are those members of
\mathcal{P}
that are infinite classes. We have observed in Lemma
3.6
above that
\operatorname{Stab}_{V}(\mathcal{P})
acts upon the set
\mathcal{P}^{\ast}
of these infinite classes and consequently it also acts, via automorphisms, on this subgraph
\Gamma^{\ast}
.

The semigroup associated to the equivalence relation

The semigroup
S(\mathcal{P})
that we shall associate to a type system
\mathcal{P}
will be a homomorphic image of the semigroup
S(\mathfrak{C})
of continuous functions from
\mathfrak{C}
to the set
\mathbb{N}
of natural numbers (endowed with the discrete topology) that are not identically zero. If
U
is a subset of
\mathfrak{C}
, we write
\chi_{U}
for the characteristic function of
U
; that is, the function that takes the value
1
on
U
and the value
0
elsewhere. To simplify notation, we use
\chi_{\alpha}
, for
\alpha\in\Omega
, as an abbreviation for the characteristic function of the cone
\alpha\mathfrak{C}
. These characteristic functions generate
S(\mathfrak{C})
and we shall exploit the following presentation for this semigroup.

Lemma 3.16
.

The semigroup
S(\mathfrak{C})
of non-zero continuous functions
\mathfrak{C}\to\mathbb{N}
is isomorphic to the additively-written commutative semigroup with presentation consisting of generators
x_{\alpha}
for each
\alpha\in\Omega
and relations
x_{\alpha}=x_{\alpha 0}+x_{\alpha 1}
for each
\alpha\in\Omega
.

Proof.

Define
T
be the commutative semigroup with presentation

\langle\,\text{$x_{\alpha}$ (for $\alpha\in\Omega$)}\mid\text{$x_{\alpha}=x_{\alpha 0}+x_{\alpha 1}$ for each $\alpha\in\Omega$}\,\rangle.

Since the characteristic functions
\chi_{\alpha}
satisfy the same relations, we observe that there is a surjective homomorphism
\phi\colon T\to S(\mathfrak{C})
given by
x_{\alpha}\mapsto\chi_{\alpha}
. Suppose that
f
and
g
are elements of
T
that satisfy
f\phi=g\phi
. Express
f
and
g
as sums of generators
x_{\alpha}
and let
N
be the length of the longest address
\alpha
involved in these sums. By repeated use of the relations
x_{\alpha}=x_{\alpha 0}+x_{\alpha 1}
, we can write
f=\sum_{\gamma\in C}c_{\gamma}x_{\gamma}
and
g=\sum_{\gamma\in C}d_{\gamma}x_{\gamma}
where
C
is the set of addresses of length
N
and
c_{\gamma}
and
d_{\gamma}
are non-negative integers. The images of
f
and
g
under
\phi
take the values
c_{\gamma}
and
d_{\gamma}
respectively on the (disjoint) cones
\gamma\mathfrak{C}
and the hypothesis then implies that
c_{\gamma}=d_{\gamma}
for each
\gamma\in C
. It follows that
\phi
is injective and hence
S(\mathfrak{C})\cong T
, as claimed.

Fix a type system
\mathcal{P}
determined by some coherent and reduced equivalence relation
\sim
on
\Omega
. We shall associate a semigroup
S(\mathcal{P})
with generators that correspond to the types in
\mathcal{P}
. We reserve the lowercase letters
p
,
q
and
r
for the generators that correspond to the types in
\mathcal{P}
denoted by
P
,
Q
and
R
. This will be extended to include any decoration that is applied to one these symbols. Thus, if
P\in\mathcal{P}
, then
p_{\gamma}
will denote the generator of
S(\mathcal{P})
that corresponds to the type
P_{\gamma}
, this being the type that is reached by following the path in the graph
\Gamma(\mathcal{P})
labelled by the word
\gamma
. In particular,
p_{0}
and
p_{1}
will be the generators that correspond to the types
P_{0}
and
P_{1}
, these being the two vertices of
\Gamma(\mathcal{P})
that are adjacent to
P
(attached by edges with labels
0
and
1
, respectively). Similarly, when we write
p^{(1)}
,
p^{(2)}
, …,
p^{(m)}
, these will be the generators of
S(\mathcal{P})
that correspond to some family
P^{(1)}
,
P^{(2)}
, …,
P^{(m)}
of types in
\mathcal{P}
.

Definition 3.17
.

Suppose that
\mathcal{P}
is a type system on
\Omega
. Define
S(\mathcal{P})
to be the commutative semigroup (whose operation we write additively) with generators
p
in one-one correspondence with the types
P
in
\mathcal{P}
subject to all relations
p=p_{0}+p_{1}
for each
P\in\mathcal{P}
.

Remark 3.18
.

In
[
GLU21
]
, the authors introduce the notion of an arboreal semigroup presentation, connecting it to a labelled planar forest diagram. The main difference between our semigroup and theirs is that we are taking our semigroup to be commutative and working with a single binary rooted tree instead of a more general forest.

Recall that the assumption of coherence ensures that the types
P_{0}
and
P_{1}
are well-defined independent of the choice of representative for
P
. Hence the above definition for
S(\mathcal{P})
makes sense in this context. We denote the generators of
S(\mathcal{P})
with a lowercase letter since it is common for the relations assumed to imply that
p=q
for particular distinct types
P
and
Q
from
\mathcal{P}
. We are therefore able to distinguish between equality within this semigroup and genuine equality between types from
\mathcal{P}
. As a first observation, and linking back to the graph
\Gamma(\mathcal{P})
defined above, if
P,Q\in\mathcal{P}
and there is a path from
P
to
Q
in
\Gamma(\mathcal{P})
then, upon applying the defining relation at each step of the path, we observe

p=q+x

for some
x\in S(\mathcal{P})
(and where, according to our convention,
p
and
q
are the generators of
S(\mathcal{P})
associated to
P
and
Q
).

Definition 3.19
.

By Lemma
3.16
, there is a surjective homomorphism
\psi\colon S(\mathfrak{C})\to S(\mathcal{P})
given by
\chi_{\alpha}\mapsto p
whenever the address
\alpha
belongs has type
P
in
\mathcal{P}
. If
U
is a non-empty clopen subset of
\mathfrak{C}
, we define the
s-type of
U
(under
\mathcal{P}
)
, denoted
\operatorname{s-type}_{\mathcal{P}}(U)
(or
\operatorname{s-type}(U)
if
\mathcal{P}
is understood), to be the image of the characteristic function
\chi_{U}
under this homomorphism.

The idea of this definition is to extend the concept of “type” from addresses (which correspond to cones) to arbitrary clopen subsets of
\mathfrak{C}
. However, one should note that it is possible for two addresses
\alpha
and
\beta
to have different types in
\mathcal{P}
but that the defining relations result in the corresponding cones
\alpha\mathfrak{C}
and
\beta\mathfrak{C}
having the same s-type. (This reflects our earlier comment that we might have types satisfying
P\neq Q
but the corresponding generators
p
and
q
being equal in
S(\mathcal{P})
.) Despite this possible collapse in s-types, this extension of “type” to clopen sets is useful and the following is the key property that we shall use:

Lemma 3.20
.

Let
\mathcal{P}
be a type system with associated equivalence relation
\sim
and
S(\mathcal{P})
be the commutative semigroup it determines. Suppose that
U
and
U^{\prime}
are non-empty clopen subsets of
\mathfrak{C}
that have the same s-type. Then there exist decompositions
U=\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}\gamma_{i}\mathfrak{C}
and
U^{\prime}=\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{i}\mathfrak{C}
into the same number of disjoint cones such that
\gamma_{i}\sim\delta_{i}
for
i=1
,
2
, …,
n
.

Proof.

The s-type of a non-empty clopen subset
U
is its image of the characteristic function
\chi_{U}
under the homomorphism
\psi\colon S(\mathfrak{C})\to S(\mathcal{P})
and so we seek to identify the kernel of
\psi
as a congruence on
S(\mathfrak{C})
. Define a relation
\mathrel{\rho}
on
S(\mathfrak{C})
by
f\mathrel{\rho}g
if there exist decompositions
f=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\chi_{\alpha_{i}}
and
g=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\chi_{\beta_{i}}
where
\alpha_{i}\sim\beta_{i}
for each
i
. This relation is certainly reflexive and symmetric. Suppose that
f\mathrel{\rho}g
and
g\mathrel{\rho}h
. Then there are decompositions

f=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\chi_{\alpha_{i}},\qquad g=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\chi_{\beta_{i}}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\chi_{\gamma_{j}},\qquad h=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\chi_{\delta_{j}}

(1)

such that
\alpha_{i}\sim\beta_{i}
for each
i
and
\gamma_{j}\sim\delta_{j}
for each
j
. Let
N
be the maximum length of the addresses
\beta_{i}
and
\gamma_{i}
. Repeatedly exploit the relation
\chi_{\alpha}=\chi_{\alpha 0}+\chi_{\alpha 1}
to express each
\chi_{\beta_{i}}
as a sum of characteristic functions
\chi_{\beta_{i}\zeta}
where
\mathopen{|}\zeta\mathclose{|}=N-\mathopen{|}\beta_{i}\mathclose{|}
. There is an analogous decomposition of
\chi_{\alpha_{i}}
as a sum of terms
\chi_{\alpha_{i}\zeta}
and here
\alpha_{i}\zeta\sim\beta_{i}\zeta
since
\sim
is coherent. We can do the same thing for the terms corresponding to the relationship
g\mathrel{\rho}h
. Hence we can assume that in Equation (
1
) that
\mathopen{|}\beta_{i}\mathclose{|}=\mathopen{|}\gamma_{j}\mathclose{|}=N
for all
i
and
j
. However, this means that any pair of addresses from the
\beta_{i}
and the
\gamma_{j}
are either equal or incomparable and so the two decompositions for the function
g
must be essentially the same. Consequently
m=n
in this case and we may relabel the addresses so that
\beta_{i}=\gamma_{i}
for each
i
. It then follows that
\alpha_{i}\sim\delta_{i}
and hence
f\mathrel{\rho}h
. We conclude that
\mathrel{\rho}
is an equivalence relation on
S(\mathfrak{C})
. It is also a congruence on this semigroup since if
f\mathrel{\rho}g
then it immediately follows that
(f+\chi_{\gamma})\mathrel{\rho}(g+\chi_{\gamma})
for any address
\gamma
.

If
f\mathrel{\rho}g
, then by definition
f\psi=g\psi
in
S(\mathcal{P})
. On the other hand, it follows from Lemma
3.16
and the definition of
S(\mathcal{P})
that the kernel of
\psi
is the congruence on
S(\mathfrak{C})
generated by the condition
(\chi_{\alpha},\chi_{\beta})\in\ker\psi
whenever
\alpha\sim\beta
. Since
\chi_{\alpha}\mathrel{\rho}\chi_{\beta}
when
\alpha\sim\beta
, it follows that
\ker\rho
is contained in the congruence
\mathrel{\rho}
. Therefore, the above congruence
\mathrel{\rho}
is the kernel of
\psi
.

Now consider the clopen subsets
U
and
U^{\prime}
as in the statement of the lemma. As they have the same s-type,
\chi_{U}\mathrel{\rho}\chi_{U^{\prime}}
; that is, there are decompositions
\chi_{U}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\chi_{\gamma_{i}}
and
\chi_{U^{\prime}}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\chi_{\delta_{i}}
such that
\gamma_{i}\sim\delta_{i}
for each
i
. The definition of the characteristic function then implies that
U
is the disjoint union of the cones
\gamma_{i}\mathfrak{C}
while
U^{\prime}
is the disjoint union of the cones
\delta_{i}\mathfrak{C}
. This completes the proof.

This semigroup
S(\mathcal{P})
will be used to produce elements of
V
that act (partially) on the set
\Omega
of addresses in some specific manner. For example, Lemmas
5.1
and
5.3
below consider the structure of this semigroup under certain hypotheses on the type system
\mathcal{P}
(with equivalence relation
\sim
). The last part of each of these two lemmas observes that, for appropriate addresses
\alpha
,
\beta
,
\alpha^{\prime}
and
\beta^{\prime}
with
\alpha\sim\alpha^{\prime}
and
\beta\sim\beta^{\prime}
, we can show that
U=\mathfrak{C}\setminus(\alpha\mathfrak{C}\cup\beta\mathfrak{C})
and
U^{\prime}=\mathfrak{C}\setminus(\alpha^{\prime}\mathfrak{C}\cup\beta^{\prime}\mathfrak{C})
have the same s-type. We then may apply Lemma
3.20
to
U
and
U^{\prime}
and hence construct two decompositions of
\mathfrak{C}
into disjoint cones that can be paired such that corresponding cones have the same type. With use of Lemma
3.4
(iv)
, our conclusion is that there is an element
g\in\operatorname{Fix}_{V}(\mathcal{P})
satisfying
\alpha^{g}=\alpha^{\prime}
and
\beta^{g}=\beta^{\prime}
.

4
Simple type systems

In this section, we classify finite simple type systems. Recall from Definition
3.12
that a simple type system is one whose only proper quotient consists of a single type and that a type system is finite when it has finitely many equivalence classes.

Our first task is to develop useful language to describe type systems and subsets of type systems.

Definition 4.1
.

Let
\mathcal{P}
be a type system on
\Omega
and let
\mathcal{S}
be a subset of
\mathcal{P}
.

(i)

We say that
\mathcal{S}
is
child-closed
if whenever
P\in\mathcal{S}
then also
P_{0}
and
P_{1}
belong to
\mathcal{S}
.

(ii)

If
P\in\mathcal{S}
and
\gamma
is a word, we say
\gamma
determines a path through
\mathcal{S}
from
P
if
P_{\zeta}\in\mathcal{S}
for all prefixes
\zeta
of
\gamma
.

(iii)

We say that
\mathcal{S}
is
strongly connected
if for all
P,Q\in\mathcal{S}
there exists a non-empty word
\gamma
so that
\gamma
determines a path through
\mathcal{S}
with
P_{\gamma}=Q
.

(iv)

We say that
S
is a
nucleus
if
S
is child-closed and strongly connected.

We begin by introducing a number of special categories of type systems that will arise within the final classification of finite simple type systems.

Definition 4.2
.

A type system
\mathcal{P}
on
\Omega
is called
nuclear
if there exists a positive integer
t
and a subset
\mathcal{Q}
of
\mathcal{P}
such that:

(a)

if
\alpha
is an address with
\mathopen{|}\alpha\mathclose{|}\geqslant t
, then the type of
\alpha
is a member of
\mathcal{Q}
;

(b)

the subset
\mathcal{Q}
is a nucleus (that is, child-closed and strongly connected).

The following are variations on the theme of a nuclear type system. Indeed, a nuclear type system is a special case of a multinuclear type system, but with precisely one nucleus.

Definition 4.3
.

Let
\mathcal{P}
be a type system on
\Omega
.

(i)

We shall say that
\mathcal{P}
is
multinuclear
if there exist a positive integer
t
and disjoint subsets
\mathcal{Q}^{(1)}
,
\mathcal{Q}^{(2)}
, …,
\mathcal{Q}^{(k)}
of
\mathcal{P}
such that

(a)

if
\alpha\in\Omega
with
\mathopen{|}\alpha\mathclose{|}\geqslant t
then the type of
\alpha
is a member of one of the
\mathcal{Q}^{(i)}
; and

(b)

for each index
i
, the subset
\mathcal{Q}^{(i)}
is a nucleus.

The subsets
\mathcal{Q}^{(i)}
will be called the
nucleii
of the type system.

(ii)

We say that
\mathcal{P}
is
binuclear
if it is multinuclear with precisely two nucleii
\mathcal{Q}^{(1)}
and
\mathcal{Q}^{(2)}
.

(iii)

We say that
\mathcal{P}
is
quasinuclear
if there exist a positive integer
t
, and non-empty disjoint subsets
\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{R}\subseteq\mathcal{P}
such that

((a))

if
\alpha\in\Omega
with
\mathopen{|}\alpha\mathclose{|}\geqslant t
then the type of
\alpha
is in
\mathcal{Q}\cup\mathcal{R}
;

((b))

the set
\mathcal{R}
is a nucleus;

((c))

the set
\mathcal{Q}
is strongly connected;

((d))

the set
\mathcal{R}\cup\mathcal{Q}
is child-closed; and

((e))

for every
Q\in\mathcal{Q}
and
R\in\mathcal{R}
, there is a word
\gamma
so that
Q_{\gamma}=R
.

Given such a setup, we define
\mathcal{Q}^{\dagger}
to be the set of equivalence classes
Q\in\mathcal{Q}
such that
Q_{0}
and
Q_{1}
are both in
\mathcal{Q}
. If
\mathcal{Q}^{\dagger}
is non-empty, then we say that
\mathcal{P}
is
branching quasinuclear
. If
\mathcal{P}
is quasinuclear, we refer to the set
\mathcal{Q}\cup\mathcal{R}
as the
terminal set of
\mathcal{P}
and to
\mathcal{R}
as the
nucleus of
\mathcal{P}
.

The terms appearing in Definitions
4.2
and
4.3
appear in our classification of finite simple type systems. In order to aid our proof of this classification, we introduce some additional terminology and establish the lemmas that we need.

Definition 4.4
.

Let
\mathcal{P}
be a type system on
\Omega
and let
\mathcal{S}
be a subset of
\mathcal{P}
.

(i)

We say that
\mathcal{S}
contains a
cycle
if there exists
P\in\mathcal{S}
and a non-empty word
\gamma
that determines a path through
\mathcal{S}
at
P
with
P_{\gamma}=P
. Such a path will be called a
cycle (in
\mathcal{S}
) based at
P
.

(ii)

We say that
\mathcal{S}
is
stable
if for every
Q\in\mathcal{S}
, there exists some
P\in\mathcal{S}
such that there is a cycle in
\mathcal{S}
based at
P
and also a word
\gamma
that determines a path through
\mathcal{S}
from
P
with
P_{\gamma}=Q
.

Strongly connected subsets are necessarily stable, but stable subsets are more general and help us to encode the nature of binuclear and quasinuclear type systems. For example, with the notation of Definition
4.3
(iii)
, if
\mathcal{P}
is quasinuclear then the set
\mathcal{Q}\cup\mathcal{R}
is stable but not strongly connected.

The following observation is immediate from the definitions.

Lemma 4.5
.

Let
\mathcal{P}
be a type system on
\Omega
and suppose there is a cycle in
\mathcal{P}
based at some
P
in
\mathcal{P}
. Then
\mathcal{Q}=\{\,P_{\zeta}\mid\zeta\in\Omega\,\}
is child-closed and stable.

This set
\mathcal{Q}
above is the set of vertices in the graph
\Gamma(\mathcal{P})
that one can reach by following a path starting at
P
.

Lemma 4.6
.

Let
\mathcal{P}
be a simple type system on
\Omega
. If
\mathcal{S}
is a non-empty subset of
\mathcal{P}
that is child-closed and such that the complement
\mathcal{P}\setminus\mathcal{S}
contains a cycle, then
\mathopen{|}\mathcal{S}\mathclose{|}=1
.

Proof.

Write
\sim
for the equivalence relation on
\Omega
that determines
\mathcal{P}
. Let
P\in\mathcal{P}\setminus\mathcal{S}
such that there is a cycle in
\mathcal{P}\setminus\mathcal{S}
based at
P
. We define a new relation
\approx
on
\Omega
by the rule
\alpha\approx\beta
when

(\ast)

for all but finitely many words
\eta
, either
\alpha\eta\sim\beta\eta
or the
\mathcal{P}
-types of
\alpha\eta
and of
\beta\eta
both belong to
\mathcal{S}
.

We shall show that
\approx
defines a type system on
\Omega
that is a quotient of
\mathcal{P}
. It is immediate that this relation is reflexive and symmetric. Suppose
\alpha\approx\beta
and
\beta\approx\gamma
. For all but finitely many words
\eta
, the condition appearing in
(\ast)
applies to the pair
\alpha\eta
and
\beta\eta
and to the pair
\beta\eta
and
\gamma\eta
. If
\alpha\eta\sim\beta\eta\sim\gamma\eta
, then
\alpha\eta\sim\gamma\eta
since
\sim
is an equivalence relation. Otherwise, it must be the case that the
\mathcal{P}
-types of all three of
\alpha\eta
,
\beta\eta
and
\gamma\eta
belong to
\mathcal{S}
(with two of these types coinciding if
\alpha\eta\sim\beta\eta
or
\beta\eta\sim\gamma\eta
). We conclude that
\alpha\approx\gamma
. This shows that
\approx
is an equivalence relation on
\Omega
. It is also straightforward to verify that
(\ast)
defines a relation that is coherent and reduced. Let
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
be the type system on
\Omega
determined by
\approx
. This is a quotient of
\mathcal{P}
in view of the first part of the condition appearing in
(\ast)
.

Suppose that
\approx
is the universal relation on
\Omega
. Recall that there is a cycle in
\mathcal{P}\setminus\mathcal{S}
based at
P
. Let
\alpha
be an address of
\mathcal{P}
-type
P
and let
\gamma
be a non-empty word that determines a cycle in
\mathcal{P}\setminus\mathcal{S}
based at
P
. Let
\beta
be an address with
\mathcal{P}
-type in
\mathcal{S}
. By assumption,
\alpha\approx\beta
. Hence, for sufficiently large
k
, the condition in
(\ast)
holds when
\eta=\gamma^{k}
and so either
\alpha\eta\sim\beta\eta
or the
\mathcal{P}
-types of
\alpha\eta
and
\beta\eta
both belong to
\mathcal{S}
. However
\alpha\eta
has
\mathcal{P}
-type
P
, which is not in
\mathcal{S}
, since
P_{\gamma}=P
, while
\beta\eta
has
\mathcal{P}
-type in
\mathcal{S}
as
\mathcal{S}
is child-closed. This is a contradiction.

Consequently, by simplicity of the type system
\mathcal{P}
, it must be the case that
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}=\mathcal{P}
. Now if
\alpha
and
\beta
have
\mathcal{P}
-types in
\mathcal{S}
, then the
\mathcal{P}
-types of
\alpha\eta
and
\beta\eta
are in
\mathcal{S}
for all words
\eta
and hence
\alpha\approx\beta
. It follows therefore that
\alpha\sim\beta
and we conclude that
\mathopen{|}\mathcal{S}\mathclose{|}=1
.

The majority of the remaining work to classify the finite simple type systems is contained in the following result:

Lemma 4.7
.

Let
\mathcal{P}
be a simple type system on
\Omega
which admits a cycle. Then
\mathcal{P}
contains precisely one, two or three stable child-closed subsets. Moreover,

(i)

if
\mathcal{P}
contains exactly two stable child-closed subsets then one has cardinality
1
and is a subset of the other;

(ii)

if
\mathcal{P}
contains three stable child-closed subsets then two of them have cardinality
1
and the third is the union of the first two.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{P}
be a simple type system that contains some cycle. By Lemma
4.5
, there is at least one stable child-closed subset of
\mathcal{P}
.

Suppose first that
\mathcal{Q}
and
\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}
are two distinct stable child-closed subsets of
\mathcal{P}
. We may assume that there exists
Q\in\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}\setminus\mathcal{Q}
. Then by stability there is some
P\in\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}
with a cycle
C
in
\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}
based at
P
and a (non-empty) word
\gamma
such that
\gamma
determines a path through
\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}
from
P
to
Q
. Since
\mathcal{Q}
is child-closed, necessarily
P\notin\mathcal{Q}
and the cycle
C
based at
P
is disjoint from
\mathcal{Q}
. Hence we deduce that
\mathopen{|}\mathcal{Q}\mathclose{|}=1
by Lemma
4.6
. If it is not the case that
\mathcal{Q}\subseteq\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}
, then there is some member of
\mathcal{Q}\setminus\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}
and the same argument shows
\mathopen{|}\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}\mathclose{|}=1
. As a consequence
\mathcal{Q}
and
\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}
are then disjoint and both singleton sets. Note in this case that the union
\mathcal{Q}\cup\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}
is also child-closed and stable.

We have shown that if
\mathcal{Q}
and
\mathcal{Q^{\prime}}
are distinct stable child-closed sets then either

(1)

\mathcal{Q}\subseteq\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}
and
\mathopen{|}\mathcal{Q}\mathclose{|}=1
(or
vice versa
), or

(2)

\mathopen{|}\mathcal{Q}\mathclose{|}=\mathopen{|}\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}\mathclose{|}=1
and
\mathcal{Q}\cup\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}
is also child-closed and stable.

In particular, if there are exactly two stable child-closed subsets then their configuration is as described in part (i) of the statement.

We now consider each of the above possibilities to consider how the stable child-closed subsets could relate to a third such subset
\mathcal{Q}^{\prime\prime}
. Suppose first that
\mathcal{Q}\subseteq\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}
so that
\mathopen{|}\mathcal{Q}\mathclose{|}=1
as in (1) above. Then since
\mathopen{|}\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}\mathclose{|}>1
it must be the case that
\mathcal{Q}^{\prime\prime}\subseteq\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}
also and
\mathopen{|}\mathcal{Q}^{\prime\prime}\mathclose{|}=1
. Then
\mathcal{Q}\cup\mathcal{Q}^{\prime\prime}
is stable and child-closed, so since both
\mathcal{Q}\cup\mathcal{Q}^{\prime\prime}
and
\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}
contain more than one type it must be the case that
\mathcal{Q^{\prime}}=\mathcal{Q}\cup\mathcal{Q}^{\prime\prime}
. In particular,
\mathcal{Q}^{\prime\prime}=\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}\setminus\mathcal{Q}
. Alternatively if
\mathcal{Q}
and
\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}
are as in (2), then applying the above observation to the sets
\mathcal{Q}\cup\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}
and
\mathcal{Q}^{\prime\prime}
shows that the only possibility is that
\mathcal{Q}^{\prime\prime}=\mathcal{Q}\cup\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}
. In conclusion, since in either case there is only one possibility for
\mathcal{Q}^{\prime\prime}
, we deduce that
\mathcal{P}
cannot contain more than three stable child-closed subsets and, moreover, if there are three then they are arranged as specified in (ii) in the statement.

We give specialised names for the categories of type systems that arise as finite simple type systems. Firstly, if a binuclear (or multinuclear) type system has the property that the cardinality of each of its nucleii is
1
, then we say the type system is
atomic binuclear
(or
atomic multinuclear
, respectively). Similarly, a quasinuclear type system such that its nucleus contains a single type will be called
atomic quasinuclear
.

We can now state our main result of this section.

\FiniteTypes

Proof.

\mathcal{P}

Suppose first that
\mathcal{P}

. Then, by Lemma

Second consider the case when
\mathcal{P}

5
Maximality of stabilizer subgroups

We now turn our attention to showing that, for simple type systems which are nuclear, multinuclear, or atomic quasinuclear, the corresponding stabilizer is a maximal subgroup of Thompson’s group
V
(see Theorems
Introduction
and
5.12
). In particular, this includes all finite simple type systems as described in Theorem
Introduction
(see Theorem
Introduction
).

Simple type systems
\mathcal{P}
that are atomic quasinuclear but not branching are dealt with in Theorem
5.12
where we observe that
\operatorname{Stab}_{V}(\mathcal{P})
equals the stabilizer of a finite set of points in
\mathfrak{C}
of the same tail class. The majority of this section is therefore concerned with the multinuclear case and the atomic branching quasinuclear case. We analyze these two cases in parallel establishing information that will enable us to prove Theorem
Introduction
. The essential strategy is as follows: If there is a subgroup
H
of
V
that strictly contains
\operatorname{Stab}_{V}(\mathcal{P})
then we will use Proposition
5.10
to produce a type system that is a quotient of
\mathcal{P}
while Lemma
5.11
will be used to show that this is a
proper
quotient. Since
\mathcal{P}
is simple, we will use the definition of this type system to deduce that
H
contains all transpositions in
V
and hence

completing the proof.

We begin by establishing various facts about the type systems under consideration, starting with information about the associated semigroup
S(\mathcal{P})
as defined in Definition
3.17
. (See also Definition
3.19
for the meaning of s-type.) In particular, Lemma
5.1
provides results about multinuclear type systems, while Lemma
5.3
gives analogous results about quasinuclear type systems that are both atomic and branching. (Recall that a quasinuclear type system is atomic when its nucleus
\mathcal{R}
contains a single type.) Note that nuclear and binuclear type systems are special cases of multinuclear type systems, so facts about multinuclear type systems apply to them.

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
\mathcal{P}
be a multinuclear type system on
\Omega
with
\mathcal{Q}^{(1)}
,
\mathcal{Q}^{(2)}
, …,
\mathcal{Q}^{(k)}
its nucleii and

\alpha

\mathcal{Q}^{(i)}

. Then

(i)

of

(ii)

if
\mathcal{P}

(iii)

if

,
\alpha^{\prime}

Proof.

(i)

\mathcal{Q}^{(i)}

\delta_{1}1
,
\delta_{2}0
and
\delta_{2}1
is not a prefix of
\alpha
or of
\beta
. Let
\delta_{i}x
, for
x\in\{0,1\}
, satisfy
\delta_{i}x\not\preccurlyeq\alpha
and
\delta_{i}x\not\preccurlyeq\beta
. Since
\delta_{i}x
is shorter than these two addresses, we deduce
\delta_{i}x\mathfrak{C}\subseteq U=\mathfrak{C}\setminus(\alpha\mathfrak{C}\cup\beta\mathfrak{C})
. Write
P
for the type of
\delta_{i}x
. Then the corresponding element
p
is a summand of the s-type of
U
and, since
I
is an ideal of
S(\mathcal{P})
, we deduce
\operatorname{s-type}(U)\in I
, as claimed.

(v)
Suppose
\mathopen{|}\alpha\mathclose{|},\mathopen{|}\alpha^{\prime}\mathclose{|}\geqslant N
. Again, for some
x\in\{0,1\}
, the address
\delta_{i}x
is not a prefix of
\alpha
or
\alpha^{\prime}
, so that
\delta_{i}
is incomparable with both these addresses. By assumption, the type of
\delta_{i}x
is in
\mathcal{Q}
. Follow a sufficiently long path in
\Gamma(\mathcal{P})
from the corresponding vertex to some member of
\mathcal{Q}^{\dagger}
to produce an address
\beta
with
\delta_{i}\preccurlyeq\beta
,
\mathopen{|}\beta\mathclose{|}\geqslant N
and such that
\beta
has type in
\mathcal{Q}^{\dagger}
. Then
\beta
is incomparable with both
\alpha
and
\alpha^{\prime}
, as required.

(vi)
Let
\alpha
,
\alpha^{\prime}
,
\beta
and
\beta^{\prime}
be as in the statement. Let
P
and
Q
be the types of
\alpha
and
\beta
, respectively, and let
x
and
y
be the respective s-types of the complements
\mathfrak{C}\setminus(\alpha\mathfrak{C}\cup\beta\mathfrak{C})
and
\mathfrak{C}\setminus(\alpha^{\prime}\mathfrak{C}\cup\beta^{\prime}\mathfrak{C})
. Then
p+q+x=p+q+y
since both sides are the s-type of
\mathfrak{C}
. If
P=Q=R
, then we deduce immediately that
x=y
since
r
is the identity element in
S(\mathcal{P})
. Suppose then that either
P
or
Q
is a member of
\mathcal{Q}
. Then
p+q\in I
, since
I
is an ideal of
S(\mathcal{P})
. We also know

by part

. Since

is a group, we deduce

completing the proof of the lemma.

We shall give a name to the integer parameter that appears in the above two lemmas.

Definition 5.4
.

We define the
stable depth

\mathrm{sd}(\mathcal{P})
of the type systems
\mathcal{P}
under consideration as follows:

(i)

If
\mathcal{P}
is a multinuclear type system, its
stable depth
is
t+2
, where
t
is the smallest integer such that an address
\alpha
has type in one of the nucleii whenever
\mathopen{|}\alpha\mathclose{|}\geqslant t
.

(ii)

If
\mathcal{P}
is a quasinuclear type system that is both branching and atomic, its
stable depth
is the smallest integer
N
such that parts
(iv)
–
(vi)
of Lemma
5.3
hold.

The use of this same terminology for both classes of type systems will allow us to use Lemmas
5.1
and
5.3
in the same way. Our definition ensures that
\mathrm{sd}(\mathcal{P})\geqslant 2
in either case. Hence if
\alpha
and
\beta
are addresses of length at least the stable depth, then they necessarily have small support.

Lemma 5.5
.

Let
\mathcal{P}
be a type system on
\Omega
, determined by the equivalence relation
\sim
, that is either multinuclear or atomic branching quasinuclear. Let
N\geqslant\mathrm{sd}(\mathcal{P})
and
\alpha
,
\alpha^{\prime}
,
\beta_{1}
,
\beta_{2}
, …,
\beta_{n}
be addresses of length at least
N
. Then there exist pairwise incomparable addresses
\gamma_{1}
,
\gamma_{2}
, …,
\gamma_{n}
such that, for each
i
,
\beta_{i}\sim\gamma_{i}
,
\mathopen{|}\gamma_{i}\mathclose{|}\geqslant N
and
\gamma_{i}
is incomparable with both
\alpha
and
\alpha^{\prime}
.

Proof.

Consider first the case when
\mathcal{P}
is multinuclear and adopt the notation of Definition
4.3
(i)
. By definition,
N\geqslant t+2
. Any address
\theta
of length at least
N
has type in one of the nucleii
\mathcal{Q}^{(i)}
and any descendent of
\theta
also has type belonging to
\mathcal{Q}^{(i)}
. As a consequence, it is sufficient to deal with the case when
\alpha
,
\alpha^{\prime}
,
\beta_{1}
,
\beta_{2}
, …,
\beta_{n}
all have types in the same nucleus
\mathcal{Q}^{(i)}
, since incomparability follows immediately for addresses with types in different nucleii. We proceed by induction on
n
. Let
\delta
be a prefix of
\alpha
of length
t
, so that
\delta
also has type in
\mathcal{Q}^{(i)}
. Since
\mathopen{|}\alpha\mathclose{|},\mathopen{|}\alpha^{\prime}\mathclose{|}\geqslant t+2
, at least one of
\delta 00
,
\delta 01
,
\delta 10
and
\delta 11
is incomparable with both
\alpha
and
\alpha^{\prime}
. Write
\zeta
for one of these four addresses satisfying this requirement. Now follow a sufficiently long path in
\Gamma(\mathcal{P})
from the type of
\zeta
to that of
\beta_{1}
to produce an address
\gamma_{1}
that is incomparable with
\alpha
and satisfies
\gamma_{1}\sim\beta_{1}
and
\mathopen{|}\gamma_{1}\mathclose{|}\geqslant N
. This covers the base case
n=1
.

Now assume that
n>1
and there are incomparable addresses
\gamma_{1}
,
\gamma_{2}
, …,
\gamma_{n-1}
such that
\beta_{i}\sim\gamma_{i}
,
\mathopen{|}\gamma_{i}\mathclose{|}\geqslant N
and
\gamma_{i}\perp\alpha,\alpha^{\prime}
for
i=1
,
2
, …,
n-1
. Now
\gamma_{n-1}0
and
\gamma_{n-1}1
are incomparable. Follow paths in
\Gamma(\mathcal{P})
from the types of
\gamma_{n-1}0
and
\gamma_{n-1}1
to those of
\beta_{n-1}
and
\beta_{n}
. Replace
\gamma_{n-1}
by the first of the resulting addresses and define
\gamma_{n}
to be the second. Then the new
\gamma_{n-1}
and
\gamma_{n}
are incomparable with the previous
\gamma_{1}
,
\gamma_{2}
, …,
\gamma_{n-2}
and with both
\alpha
and
\alpha^{\prime}
and satisfy
\gamma_{n-1}\sim\beta_{n-1}
and
\gamma_{n}\sim\beta_{n}
. This completes the induction step in the multinuclear case.

Now suppose that
\mathcal{P}
is quasinuclear and both branching and atomic. We use the notation of Definition
4.3
(iii)
and write
R
for the unique type in
\mathcal{R}
. By Lemma
5.3
(v)
, there exists
\zeta
with type in
\mathcal{Q}^{\dagger}
such that
\mathopen{|}\zeta\mathclose{|}\geqslant N
and
\zeta
is incomparable with both
\alpha
and
\alpha^{\prime}
. We claim that there exist pairwise incomparable addresses
\zeta_{1}
,
\zeta_{2}
, …,
\zeta_{n}
that have
\zeta
as prefix and that each has type in
\mathcal{Q}^{\dagger}
. When
n=1
,
\zeta_{1}=\zeta
achieves the claim. Suppose as inductive hypothesis that we have found such
\zeta_{1}
,
\zeta_{2}
, …,
\zeta_{n-1}
. Then
\zeta_{n-1}0
and
\zeta_{n-1}1
both have type in
\mathcal{Q}
and from these we can follow paths in
\Gamma(\mathcal{P})
to types in
\mathcal{Q}^{\dagger}
. Replace
\zeta_{n-1}
by the resulting address determined by the first and take
\zeta_{n}
to be the second resulting address. We have then completed the induction and established our claim. Now for each
i
, there is a path from the type
\zeta_{i}
to that of
\beta_{i}
(whether this is
R
or belongs to
\mathcal{Q}
). Hence we find an address
\gamma_{i}
with
\zeta_{i}
as prefix and
\gamma_{i}\sim\beta_{i}
. The resulting
\gamma_{1}
,
\gamma_{2}
, …,
\gamma_{n}
then satisfy the claim of the lemma.

Remark 5.6
.

For many of the applications of the above lemma, we shall not specify the addresses
\alpha
and
\alpha^{\prime}
. In these cases, all we seek are incomparable addresses
\gamma_{i}
that satisfy
\gamma_{i}\sim\beta_{i}
.

The following lemma will be useful for showing that, for suitable type systems
\mathcal{P}
, if an overgroup
H
of
\operatorname{Fix}_{V}(\mathcal{P})
contains a suitable transposition
(\alpha\;\;\beta)
with
\alpha\nsim\beta
then it contains many transpositions of this form.

Lemma 5.7
.

Let
\mathcal{P}
be a type system that is determined by the equivalence relation
\sim
on
\Omega
and assume that
\mathcal{P}
is either multinuclear or atomic branching quasinuclear. Let
N\geqslant\mathrm{sd}(\mathcal{P})
.

(i)

If
\alpha
,
\alpha^{\prime}
,
\beta
and
\beta^{\prime}
are addresses of length at least
N
such that
\alpha\sim\alpha^{\prime}
,
\beta\sim\beta^{\prime}
,
\alpha\perp\beta
and
\alpha^{\prime}\perp\beta^{\prime}
, then there exists
g\in\operatorname{Fix}_{V}(\mathcal{P})
such that
\alpha^{g}=\alpha^{\prime}
and
\beta^{g}=\beta^{\prime}
.

(ii)

Suppose that
H
is a subgroup of
V
with
\operatorname{Fix}_{V}(\mathcal{P})\leqslant H
such that
(\alpha\;\;\beta)\in H
for some incomparable addresses
\alpha
and
\beta
with
\mathopen{|}\alpha\mathclose{|},\mathopen{|}\beta\mathclose{|}\geqslant N
. Then
(\alpha^{\prime}\;\;\beta^{\prime})\in H
for all incomparable addresses
\alpha^{\prime}
and
\beta^{\prime}
with
\mathopen{|}\alpha^{\prime}\mathclose{|},\mathopen{|}\beta^{\prime}\mathclose{|}\geqslant N
,
\alpha^{\prime}\sim\alpha
and
\beta^{\prime}\sim\beta
.

Proof.

(i)
Let
U=\mathfrak{C}\setminus(\alpha\mathfrak{C}\cup\beta\mathfrak{C})
and
U^{\prime}=\mathfrak{C}\setminus(\alpha^{\prime}\mathfrak{C}\cup\beta^{\prime}\mathfrak{C})
. By Lemma
5.1
(iii)
or by Lemma
5.3
(vi)
, as appropriate, the s-types of
U
and
U^{\prime}
are equal. Now apply Lemma
3.20
to these complements and hence express

\mathfrak{C}=\alpha\mathfrak{C}\cup\beta\mathfrak{C}\cup\biggl(\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}\gamma_{i}\mathfrak{C}\biggr)=\alpha^{\prime}\mathfrak{C}\cup\beta^{\prime}\mathfrak{C}\cup\biggl(\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{i}\mathfrak{C}\biggr)

as disjoint unions of cones such that
\gamma_{i}\sim\delta_{i}
for each
i
. We may therefore define an element
g
of
V
by the prefix substitutions
\alpha\mapsto\alpha^{\prime}
,
\beta\mapsto\beta^{\prime}
and
\gamma_{i}\mapsto\delta_{i}
for
i=1
,
2
, …,
n
. This element achieves what is claimed.

(ii)
By part
(i)
, there exists
g\in\operatorname{Fix}_{V}(\mathcal{P})
with
\alpha^{g}=\alpha^{\prime}
and
\beta^{g}=\beta^{\prime}
. Then
(\alpha^{\prime}\;\;\beta^{\prime})=(\alpha\;\;\beta)^{g}\in H
.

Definition 5.8
.

If
\mathcal{P}
is a multinuclear type system on
\Omega
with
\mathcal{Q}^{(1)}
,
\mathcal{Q}^{(2)}
, …,
\mathcal{Q}^{(k)}
its nucleii, we denote by
\operatorname{Addr}(\mathcal{Q}^{(i)})
the set of addresses
\alpha
with type in
\mathcal{Q}^{(i)}
and write

\operatorname{Supt}(\mathcal{Q}^{(i)})=\bigcup\{\,\alpha\mathfrak{C}\mid\text{$\alpha$~has type in~$\mathcal{Q}^{(i)}$}\,\}.

Since each nucleus
\mathcal{Q}^{(i)}
of a multinuclear type system is child-closed, if
\alpha
and
\beta
have types in
\mathcal{Q}^{(i)}
and
\mathcal{Q}^{(j)}
, respectively, with
i\neq j
, then
\alpha\perp\beta
. Consequently, the above multinuclear type system
\mathcal{P}
produces a disjoint union decomposition

\mathfrak{C}=\bigcup_{i=1}^{k}\operatorname{Supt}(\mathcal{Q}^{(i)})

associated to its nucleii.

Lemma 5.9
.

Suppose
\mathcal{P}
is a multinuclear type system on
\Omega
with
\mathcal{Q}^{(1)}
,
\mathcal{Q}^{(2)}
, …,
\mathcal{Q}^{(k)}
its nucleii. Let
U
and
V
be non-empty clopen subsets of
\mathfrak{C}
such that

(i)

\operatorname{s-type}(U)=\operatorname{s-type}(V)
,

(ii)

U\cap\operatorname{Supt}(\mathcal{Q}^{(i)})\neq\operatorname{Supt}(\mathcal{Q}^{(i)})
for all
i
, and

(iii)

V\cap\operatorname{Supt}(\mathcal{Q}^{(i)})\neq\operatorname{Supt}(\mathcal{Q}^{(i)})
for all
i
.

Then there exists
g\in\operatorname{Fix}_{V}(\mathcal{P})
such that
Ug=V
.

Proof.

Let
\sim
be the equivalence relation that determines the type system
\mathcal{P}
. For
i=1
,
2
, …,
k
, define
S_{i}=\operatorname{Supt}(\mathcal{Q}^{(i)})
. As
\operatorname{s-type}(U)=\operatorname{s-type}(V)
, Lemma
3.20
produces decompositions

U=\bigcup_{j=1}^{n}\gamma_{j}\mathfrak{C}\and V=\bigcup_{j=1}^{n}\delta_{j}\mathfrak{C}

into disjoint cones such that
\gamma_{j}\sim\delta_{j}
for
j=1
,
2
, …,
n
. Refining these addresses if necessary, we may assume that each
\gamma_{j}
and each
\delta_{j}
has type in some nucleus
\mathcal{Q}^{(i)}
. Now fix
i\in\{1,2,\dots,k\}
and, after relabelling for convenience of notation, suppose that
\gamma_{1}
,
\gamma_{2}
, …,
\gamma_{r}
are the corresponding addresses for
U
that have type in
\mathcal{Q}^{(i)}
. Then
\delta_{1}
,
\delta_{2}
, …,
\delta_{r}
are the addresses for
V
with type in
\mathcal{Q}^{(i)}
. Hence

U\cap S_{i}=\bigcup_{j=1}^{r}\gamma_{j}\mathfrak{C}\and V\cap S_{i}=\bigcup_{j=1}^{r}\delta_{j}\mathfrak{C}.

Consequently,
U\cap S_{i}
is empty if and only if
V\cap S_{i}
is empty (namely, when
r=0
). If this were the case, then immediately the complements
S_{i}\setminus U
and
S_{i}\setminus V
has the same s-type as they both equal
S_{i}
. Let us suppose then that
r>0
. The above formulae for the intersections then ensure that
\operatorname{s-type}(U\cap S_{i})=\operatorname{s-type}(V\cap S_{i})
. Now the latter is an element of the group
G_{i}
appearing in Lemma
5.1
(i)
and, since
S_{i}\setminus U
and
S_{i}\setminus V
are non-empty by hypothesis,

\operatorname{s-type}(S_{i})=\operatorname{s-type}(U\cap S_{i})+\operatorname{s-type}(S_{i}\setminus U)=\operatorname{s-type}(V\cap S_{i})+\operatorname{s-type}(S_{i}\setminus V)

is an equation in this group. Therefore
\operatorname{s-type}(S_{i}\setminus U)=\operatorname{s-type}(S_{i}\setminus V)
in this case also. We may therefore apply Lemma
3.20
to the clopen sets
S_{i}\setminus U
and
S_{i}\setminus V
and hence express
S_{i}
as disjoint unions

S_{i}=\biggl(\bigcup_{j=1}^{r}\gamma_{j}\mathfrak{C}\biggr)\cup\biggl(\bigcup_{\ell=1}^{s}\gamma^{\prime}_{\ell}\mathfrak{C}\biggr)=\biggl(\bigcup_{j=1}^{r}\delta_{j}\mathfrak{C}\biggr)\cup\biggl(\bigcup_{\ell=1}^{s}\delta^{\prime}_{\ell}\mathfrak{C}\biggr)

where
\gamma^{\prime}_{\ell}\sim\delta^{\prime}_{\ell}
for each
\ell
. Repeating this argument for each
i\in\{1,2,\dots,k\}
, enables us to write

\mathfrak{C}=\biggl(\bigcup_{j=1}^{n}\gamma_{j}\mathfrak{C}\biggr)\cup\biggl(\bigcup_{\ell=1}^{m}\gamma^{\prime}_{\ell}\biggr)=\biggl(\bigcup_{j=1}^{n}\delta_{j}\mathfrak{C}\biggr)\cup\biggl(\bigcup_{\ell=1}^{m}\delta^{\prime}_{\ell}\biggr)

where
\gamma^{\prime}_{\ell}\sim\delta^{\prime}_{\ell}
for each
\ell
.
(As an aside, the value of
n
here is unchanged from that when we refined the addresses
\gamma_{j}
and
\delta_{j}
to ensure they had type in the nucleii
\mathcal{Q}^{(i)}
. What we have done is find suitable partitions of the complements
\mathfrak{C}\setminus U
and
\mathfrak{C}\setminus V
.)
We may now define an element
g
of
V
by the prefix substitutions
\gamma_{j}\mapsto\delta_{j}
for
1\leqslant j\leqslant n
and
\gamma^{\prime}_{\ell}\mapsto\delta^{\prime}_{\ell}
for
1\leqslant\ell\leqslant m
. The result is an element
g
in
\operatorname{Fix}_{V}(\mathcal{P})
that maps
U
to
V
.

Proposition 5.10
.

Let
\mathcal{P}
be a type system that is determined by the equivalence relation
\sim
on
\Omega
and assume that
\mathcal{P}
is either multinuclear or atomic branching quasinuclear. Let
H
be a subgroup of
V
such that
\operatorname{Fix}_{V}(\mathcal{P})\leqslant H
and let
N\geqslant\mathrm{sd}(\mathcal{P})
. Define a relation
\approx
on
\Omega
by the rule
\alpha\approx\beta
when

for all words
\eta
with
\mathopen{|}\eta\mathclose{|}=N
, there exist incomparable addresses
\gamma
and
\delta
with
\mathopen{|}\gamma\mathclose{|},\mathopen{|}\delta\mathclose{|}\geqslant N
,
\gamma\sim\alpha\eta
,
\delta\sim\beta\eta
and
(\gamma\;\;\delta)\in H
.

Then the partition determined by
\approx
is a type system on
\Omega
that is a quotient of
\mathcal{P}
.

Proof.

Suppose first that
\alpha
and
\beta
are addresses with
\alpha\sim\beta
. Let
\eta
be any word with
\mathopen{|}\eta\mathclose{|}=N
. Since
\sim
is coherent, necessarily
\alpha\eta\sim\beta\eta
. By Lemma
5.5
, there exist incomparable addresses
\gamma
and
\delta
with
\mathopen{|}\gamma\mathclose{|},\mathopen{|}\delta\mathclose{|}\geqslant N
and
\gamma\sim\alpha\eta\sim\beta\eta\sim\delta
. Hence
(\gamma\;\;\delta)\in\operatorname{Fix}_{V}(\mathcal{P})\leqslant H
. As this holds for all choices of
\eta
, we conclude that
\alpha\approx\beta
. It follows first that
\approx
is reflexive (upon taking
\alpha=\beta
) and secondly that, once we know
\approx
is a coherent and reduced equivalence relation on
\Omega
, it will define a type system which is a quotient of
\sim
. The definition of
\approx
is certainly symmetric in
\alpha
and
\beta
. The next step then is to show that
\approx
is transitive.

Suppose that
\alpha
,
\beta
and
\gamma
are addresses satisfying
\alpha\approx\beta\approx\gamma
. Let
\eta
be any word of length
N
. Then, by definition of
\approx
and Lemma
5.7
(ii)
,
(\alpha^{\prime}\;\;\beta^{\prime})\in H
for all incomparable addresses
\alpha^{\prime}
and
\beta^{\prime}
of length
\geqslant N
with
\alpha^{\prime}\sim\alpha\eta
and
\beta^{\prime}\sim\beta\eta
and
(\beta^{\prime}\;\;\gamma^{\prime})\in H
for all incomparable addresses
\beta^{\prime}
and
\gamma^{\prime}
of length
\geqslant N
with
\beta^{\prime}\sim\beta\eta
and
\gamma^{\prime}\sim\gamma\eta
. By Lemma
5.5
, there exist a choice of incomparable addresses
\alpha^{\prime}
,
\beta^{\prime}
and
\gamma^{\prime}
such that
\alpha^{\prime}\sim\alpha\eta
,
\beta^{\prime}\sim\beta\eta
and
\gamma^{\prime}\sim\gamma\eta
. Then
(\alpha^{\prime}\;\;\beta^{\prime}),(\beta^{\prime}\;\;\gamma^{\prime})\in H
for these addresses and so

(\alpha^{\prime}\;\;\gamma^{\prime})=(\alpha^{\prime}\;\;\beta^{\prime})^{(\beta^{\prime}\;\;\gamma^{\prime})}\in H.

This holds for all
\eta
of length
N
and so we conclude
\alpha\approx\gamma
. This shows that
\approx
is transitive.

We now show that the equivalence relation
\approx
is coherent. Suppose that
\alpha\approx\beta
for some addresses
\alpha
and
\beta
. Let
\eta
be any word of length
N
and write
\eta=\zeta y
where
\mathopen{|}\zeta\mathclose{|}=n-1
and
y\in\{0,1\}
. If
x\in\{0,1\}
, then
x\zeta
has length
n
, so by the hypothesis combined with Lemma
5.7
(ii)
,
(\gamma\;\;\delta)\in H
for all incomparable addresses
\gamma
and
\delta
of length
\geqslant N
with
\gamma\sim\alpha x\zeta
and
\delta\sim\beta x\zeta
. By Lemma
5.5
, there exist incomparable addresses
\gamma
,
\delta
and
\delta^{\prime}
of length
\geqslant N
such that
\gamma\sim\alpha x\zeta
and
\delta\sim\delta^{\prime}\sim\beta x\zeta
. Since
\sim
is coherent,
\delta y\sim\delta^{\prime}y
and therefore
(\delta y\;\;\delta^{\prime}y)\in\operatorname{Fix}_{V}(\mathcal{P})\leqslant H
. On the other hand,
(\gamma\;\;\delta)\in H
by assumption, so

(\gamma y\;\;\delta^{\prime}y)=(\delta y\;\;\delta^{\prime}y)^{(\gamma\;\;\delta)}\in H.

Note
\alpha x\eta=\alpha x\zeta y\sim\gamma y
and
\beta x\eta=\beta x\zeta y\sim\delta^{\prime}y
. Since
\eta
is an arbitrary word of length
N
, we conclude
\alpha x\approx\beta x
for any
x\in\{0,1\}
. This shows that
\approx
is coherent.

Finally, suppose
\alpha
and
\beta
are addresses such that
\alpha 0\approx\beta 0
and
\alpha 1\approx\beta 1
. Let
x,y\in\{0,1\}
and
\zeta
be any word of length
N-1
. Then
\eta=x\zeta
is an arbitrary word of length
N
. Let
\gamma
and
\delta
be incomparable addresses of length
\geqslant N
such that
\gamma\sim\alpha\eta
and
\delta\sim\beta\eta
. Now
\gamma y\sim\alpha\eta y=\alpha x\zeta y
and
\delta y\sim\beta\eta y=\beta x\zeta y
. Since
\alpha x\approx\beta x
by assumption, use of Lemma
5.7
(ii)
tells us that
(\gamma y\;\;\delta y)\in H
. This is true for both
y\in\{0,1\}
and therefore

(\gamma\;\;\delta)=(\gamma 0\;\;\delta 0)\,(\gamma 1\;\;\delta 1)\in H.

As
\eta
is an arbitrary word of length
N
, we conclude that
\alpha\approx\beta
. This shows that
\approx
is reduced and completes the proof of the proposition.

Lemma 5.11
.

Let
\mathcal{P}
be a type system that is determined by the equivalence relation
\sim
on
\Omega
and assume that
\mathcal{P}
is either multinuclear or atomic branching quasinuclear. Let
N\geqslant\mathrm{sd}(\mathcal{P})
and let
H
be a subgroup of

\operatorname{Stab}_{V}(\mathcal{P})<H
. Then there exist incomparable addresses
\alpha
and
\beta
of length at least
N
and

such that
\alpha\sim\beta
but
\alpha^{h}\nsim\beta^{h}
.

Proof.

There exists some
h\in H
that does not stabilize the equivalence relation
\sim
. Replacing

by its inverse if necessary, we can assume there exist addresses
\alpha
and
\beta
such that
\alpha^{h}
and
\beta^{h}
are both defined,
\alpha\sim\beta

Theorem 5.12
.

Let
\mathcal{P}

The proof of this theorem will occupy the remainder of this section. Let
\mathcal{P}
be a simple type system on
\Omega
, defined by the equivalence relation
\sim
, that is atomic quasinuclear. Suppose that the positive integer
t
and the subsets
\mathcal{Q}
and
\mathcal{R}
of
\mathcal{P}
are as in Definition

(iii)

is the unique type in
\mathcal{R}

\mathcal{Q}^{\dagger}=\varnothing
. This means that if

Since every address of length

\mathcal{Q}
or has type
R
and since if
\alpha\in R
then
\alpha 0,\alpha 1\in R
also, there exist finitely many distinct addresses
\beta_{1}
,
\beta_{2}
, …,
\beta_{m}
that are minimal subject to having type that is in
\mathcal{Q}
. These addresses are consequently incomparable with each other. Let

be the type of
\beta_{1}
. If we start at the vertex

in the graph
\Gamma(\mathcal{P})
, there is one edge leading to a member of
\mathcal{Q}
. Repeatedly following the unique edge to the member of
\mathcal{Q}
must eventually lead back to

in view of Condition
(iii)(e)
of the definition. Equally, in following this path we must have passed through all members of
\mathcal{Q}
, so there exist letters
x_{1}
,
x_{2}
, …,
x_{n}
in

\mathcal{Q}
are
Q^{(1)}
,
Q^{(2)}
, …,

where

is the type of the address

for

,

, …,

. Furthermore, when we define

has the same type

as
\beta_{1}
.

Lemma 5.13
.

Under the above hypothesis that
\mathcal{P}
is a simple type system that is atomic quasinuclear with equivalence relation
\sim
and for which
\mathcal{Q}^{\dagger}=\varnothing
, the word
\zeta
labelling the cycle starting at the vertex
Q^{(1)}
cannot be expressed as a power of some word of shorter length.

Proof.

Suppose that
\zeta
is a power of a word of length
\ell<n
. This means
x_{i+\ell}=x_{i}
for every value of
i\geqslant 1
with
i+\ell\leqslant n
. Define a relation
\approx
on
\Omega
by the rule
\alpha\approx\beta
when

for all words
\eta
with
\mathopen{|}\eta\mathclose{|}=t
, either
\alpha\eta\sim\beta\eta
, or
\alpha\eta\in Q^{(i)}
and
\beta\eta\in Q^{(j)}
where
i\equiv j\pmod{\ell}
.

The goal of this definition is to glue together every
\ell
th equivalence class
Q^{(i)}
while ensuring that the result still defines a type system. The fact that
\approx
is reflexive and symmetric follows immediately, while it is straightforward to check that
\approx
is transitive. Suppose
\alpha\approx\beta
. Let
\theta
be any word of length
t-1
and let

\alpha x\theta\sim\beta x\theta
, then it follows from coherence that
\alpha x\theta y\sim\beta x\theta y

\theta y
is an arbitrary word of length

\alpha x\approx\beta x
. Otherwise,
\alpha x\theta\in Q^{(i)}
and
\beta x\theta\in Q^{(j)}
for some
i
and
j
with
i\equiv j\pmod{\ell}
. Our assumption on the word
\zeta
ensures that the letter
y
either labels the edges in
\Gamma(\mathcal{P})
from both
Q^{(i)}
and
Q^{(j)}
to vertices in
\mathcal{Q}
or it labels the edges to the vertex
R
. Hence either
\alpha x\theta y\in Q^{(i+1)}
and
\beta x\theta y\in Q^{(j+1)}
(computed modulo
n
) or they both have type
R
, in which case
\alpha x\theta y\sim\beta x\theta y
. Again, since
\theta y
is an arbitrary word of length
t

\alpha x\approx\beta x
in this case. This shows that
\approx
is a coherent equivalence relation on
\Omega
.

Suppose
\alpha 0\approx\beta 0
and
\alpha 1\approx\beta 1
. Let
\eta
be an arbitrary word of length
t
and write
\eta=x\theta

and
\mathopen{|}\theta\mathclose{|}=t-1

\mathopen{|}\alpha\eta\mathclose{|},\mathopen{|}\beta\eta\mathclose{|}\geqslant t

\alpha\eta
has type
R
, so that
\alpha\eta 0,\alpha\eta 1\in R

\alpha x\approx\beta x

\alpha x\zeta y=\alpha\eta y

\beta x\zeta y=\beta\eta y

\sim
for both choices of

\beta\eta 0,\beta\eta 1\in R
. As

is reduced, the only possibility is that
\beta\eta

\alpha\eta\sim\beta\eta

\alpha\eta\in Q^{(i)}

\alpha\eta y_{1}\in Q^{(i+1)}
(computed modulo

\alpha\eta y_{2}\in R
for the appropriate choice of

\beta\eta y_{1}\in Q^{(j)}

and
\beta\eta y_{2}\in R

is the label on the edge from

to

. If

\beta_{2}

\FiniteSimpleThm

6
Description of the stabilizers of simple type systems

As proven in Section
4
, every finite simple type system is either nuclear, atomic binuclear, or atomic quasinuclear. Furthermore, as observed in the previous section, the stabilizer subgroup associated to any finite simple type system is a maximal subgroup of
V
. In this section we describe the maximal subgroups associated with each of the three types.

Atomic multinuclear type systems

As we shall see, any atomic multinuclear type system with two or more nuclei has maximal stabilizer, even though such a type system is simple if and only if it has exactly two nuclei.

Proposition 6.1
.

Let
\mathcal{P}
be an atomic multinuclear type system with
n
nuclei where
n\geqslant 2
. Then there exists a partition
\{E_{1},E_{2},\dots,E_{n}\}
of
\mathfrak{C}
into clopen sets such that
\operatorname{Stab}_{V}(\mathcal{P})=\operatorname{Stab}_{V}(\{E_{1},E_{2},\dots,E_{n}\})
.

Proof.

Let
\{Q_{1}\}
,
\{Q_{2}\}
, …,
\{Q_{n}\}
be the nuclei of
\mathcal{P}
and, for each
i
, let
E_{i}
be the union of all cones
\alpha\mathfrak{C}
for which
\alpha
has type
Q_{i}
. Since each
\{Q_{i}\}
is child-closed and all but finitely many addresses have one of the types
Q_{i}
, the collection
\{E_{1},E_{2},\dots,E_{n}\}
forms a partition of
\mathfrak{C}
. Moreover, since each of these sets is open (being a union of cones) they must all be clopen. Note then that, since the associated equivalence relation on
\Omega
is reduced, an address
\alpha\in\Omega
has type
Q_{i}
if and only if
\alpha\mathfrak{C}\subseteq E_{i}
.

Now if an element
g\in G
stabilizes this partition, then it permutes the sets
E_{1}
,
E_{2}
, …,
E_{n}
according to some permutation
\sigma\in S_{n}
. Then, for all
\alpha\in\Omega
, if
\alpha
has one of the types
Q_{i}
then
\alpha^{g}
is defined and has type
Q_{i^{\sigma}}
, and it follows that
g\in\operatorname{Stab}_{V}(\mathcal{P})
. Conversely, if
g\in\operatorname{Stab}_{V}(\mathcal{P})
, there is a permutation
\sigma\in S_{n}
such that, for each
i
, the element
g
maps all but finitely many addresses of type
Q_{i}
to addresses of type
Q_{i^{\sigma}}
Then
g
maps all but finitely many cones in
E_{i}
into
E_{i^{\sigma}}
, and it follows easily that
(E_{i})^{g}=E_{i^{\sigma}}
for each
i
and hence
g
stabilizes the partition.

Remark 6.2
.

If
\{E_{1},E_{2},\dots,E_{n}\}
is any partition of
\mathfrak{C}
into clopen sets, then the stabilizer of
\{E_{1},E_{2},\dots,E_{n}\}
is in fact the stabilizer of a uniquely determined atomic multinuclear type system
\mathcal{P}
. Specifically, define an equivalence relation
\sim
on
\Omega
by
\alpha\sim\beta
if and only if

\{\,\gamma\in\Omega\mid\alpha\gamma\mathfrak{C}\subseteq E_{i}\,\}=\{\,\gamma\in\Omega\mid\beta\gamma\mathfrak{C}\subseteq E_{i}\,\}

for each
i
. Then
\sim
determines a type system
\mathcal{P}
which is atomic multinuclear, and
\operatorname{Stab}_{V}(\mathcal{P})
is precisely the stabilizer of
\{E_{1},E_{2},\dots,E_{n}\}
.

Since the stabilizer of a finite simple type system is maximal, it follows from Proposition
6.1
that the stabilizer of a partition
\{E_{1},E_{2},\dots,E_{n}\}
of the Cantor set into clopen subsets is maximal for
n=2
. The following proposition extends this result to arbitrary values of
n
.

Proposition 6.3
.

Let
\{E_{1},E_{2},\dots,E_{n}\}
be a partition of
\mathfrak{C}
into clopen sets, where
n\geqslant 2
. Then the stabilizer
\operatorname{Stab}_{V}(\{E_{1},E_{2},\dots,E_{n}\})
is a maximal subgroup of
V
isomorphic to the permutational wreath product
V\operatorname{wr}S_{n}\cong V^{n}\rtimes S_{n}
, where
S_{n}
denotes the symmetric group of degree
n
.

Proof.

Let
G
be the stabilizer of the partition. To prove that
G
is maximal, let
H
be a subgroup of
V
that properly contains
G
, and let
h\in H\setminus G
. Then there exist
E_{i}
,
E_{j}
and
E_{k}
with
j\neq k
such that
(E_{i})^{h}
intersects both
E_{j}
and
E_{k}
. Thus we can find disjoint cones
\alpha\mathfrak{C},\beta\mathfrak{C}\subseteq E_{i}
such that
\alpha^{h}
and
\beta^{h}
are both defined,
\alpha^{h}\mathfrak{C}
is properly contained in
E_{j}
, and
\beta^{h}\mathfrak{C}
is properly contained in
E_{k}
. Since
(\alpha\;\;\beta)
stabilizes
\{E_{1},E_{2},\dots,E_{n}\}
, the transposition
(\alpha^{h}\;\;\beta^{h})=(\alpha\;\;\beta)^{h}
lies in
H
. Conjugating further by elements of
G
, we find that
H
contains all transpositions
(\gamma\;\;\delta)
for which
\gamma\mathfrak{C}
is properly contained in some
E_{r}
and
\delta\mathfrak{C}
is properly contained in some
E_{s}
with
r\neq s
. Note that
H
also contains all transpositions whose cones are contained in the same
E_{r}
. It follows that
H
contains all transpositions
(\gamma\;\;\delta)
for which
\gamma
and
\delta
are sufficiently long, and therefore
H=V
. This proves that
G
is maximal.

Finally, observe that for each
E_{i}
the subgroup of elements of
V
that are supported on
E_{i}
is isomorphic to
V
. It follows that the subgroup of all elements of
V
that map each
E_{i}
to itself is isomorphic to
V^{n}
, so
G
is isomorphic to the given semidirect product.

.

.

Nuclear type systems

We now turn to the maximal subgroups determined by a nuclear type system. We shall observe that these subgroups are isomorphic to full groups of subshifts of finite type. This class of Thompson-like groups was introduced by Matsumoto in
[
Mat15a
]
and studied extensively by Matui in
[
Mat15b
]
.

We begin by defining these groups. Given a finite directed multigraph
\Gamma
with edge set
\mathcal{E}(\Gamma)
, the corresponding
(one-sided) subshift of finite type
is the pair
(X_{\Gamma},\sigma)
, where

X_{\Gamma}=\{\,e_{1}e_{2}e_{3}\dots\in\mathcal{E}(\Gamma)^{\mathbb{N}}\mid\text{$(e_{1},e_{2},e_{3},\dots)$ is an infinite directed path in $\Gamma$}\,\}

and
\sigma\colon X_{\Gamma}\to X_{\Gamma}
is the shift map. Note that
X_{\Gamma}
inherits a topology as a closed subset of the infinite product
\mathcal{E}(\Gamma)^{\mathbb{N}}
, where
\mathcal{E}(\Gamma)
has the discrete topology. With respect to this topology,
X_{\Gamma}
is compact, Hausdorff, and totally disconnected.

Let
A_{\Gamma}
denote the adjacency matrix for
\Gamma
, i.e., the matrix whose
(i,j)
-th entry is the number of directed edges from vertex
i
to vertex
j
. The subshift
(X_{\Gamma},\sigma)
is said to be
irreducible
if
A_{\Gamma}
is an irreducible matrix, or equivalently if
\Gamma
is strongly connected. An irreducible subshift
(X_{\Gamma},\sigma)
is
branching
if
\Gamma
is not a directed cycle, or equivalently if the adjacency matrix
A_{\Gamma}
is not a permutation matrix. If
(X_{\Gamma},\sigma)
is irreducible and branching, then
X_{\Gamma}
has no isolated points and is therefore a Cantor space.

Given a finite directed path
\alpha=(e_{1},e_{2},\dots,e_{n})
in
\Gamma
, the corresponding
cone

C(\alpha,X_{\Gamma})
is the set of all points in
X_{\Gamma}
that have
e_{1}e_{2}\dots e_{n}
as a prefix. Note that
C(\alpha,X_{\Gamma})
is a clopen subset of
X_{\Gamma}
and each point in
C(\alpha,X_{\Gamma})
has the form
\alpha\psi
for some infinite directed path
\psi
in
\Gamma
whose initial vertex is the same as the terminal vertex of
\alpha
. If
\alpha
and
\beta
are two finite directed paths in
\Gamma
with the same terminal vertex, there is a
prefix-replacement homeomorphism

C(\alpha,X_{\Gamma})\to C(\beta,X_{\Gamma})
that maps each point
\alpha\psi
in
C(\alpha,X_{\Gamma})
to the corresponding point
\beta\psi
in
C(\beta,X_{\Gamma})
.

Definition 6.10
.

Let
\Gamma
be a finite directed multigraph and let
(X_{\Gamma},\sigma)
be the resulting subshift of finite type.

(i)

A homeomorphism
f\colon U\to V
between open subsets of
X_{\Gamma}
is
Thompson-like
if for every point
p\in U
there exists a cone
C(\alpha,X_{\Gamma})\subseteq U
containing
p
and a cone
C(\beta,X_{\Gamma})
containing
p^{f}
such that
f
maps
C(\alpha,X_{\Gamma})
to
C(\beta,X_{\Gamma})
by a prefix-replacement homeomorphism.

(ii)

The
full group

V(\Gamma)
over
\Gamma
is the group of all Thompson-like homeomorphisms
X_{\Gamma}\to X_{\Gamma}
.

(iii)

More generally, if
E\subseteq X_{\Gamma}
is a non-empty clopen set, the
restricted full group

V(\Gamma,E)
is the group of all Thompson-like homeomorphisms
E\to E
.

Since
X_{\Gamma}
is compact, a homeomorphism
f\colon X_{\Gamma}\to X_{\Gamma}
lies in
V(\Gamma)
if and only if there exist partitions
C(\alpha_{1},X_{\Gamma})
,
C(\alpha_{2},X_{\Gamma})
, …,
C(\alpha_{n},X_{\Gamma})
and
C(\beta_{1},X_{\Gamma})
,
C(\beta_{2},X_{\Gamma})
, …,
C(\beta_{n},X_{\Gamma})
of
X_{\Gamma}
into cones such that
f
maps each
C(\alpha_{i},X_{\Gamma})
to
C(\beta_{i},X_{\Gamma})
by a prefix-replacement homeomorphism. A similar statement holds for elements of
V(\Gamma,E)
for any clopen subset
E
of
X_{\Gamma}
. Note also that
V(\Gamma,E)
and
V(\Gamma,E^{\prime})
are isomorphic whenever there exists a Thompson-like homeomorphism
E\to E^{\prime}
.

The theory of the groups
V(\Gamma)
and
V(\Gamma,E)
was developed by Matui in
[
Mat15b
]
, where
V(\Gamma)
is viewed as the topological full group of the étale groupoid associated to
(X_{\Gamma},\sigma)
and
V(\Gamma,E)
is the topological full group of the restriction of this étale groupoid to
E
. Matui proves the following results about such groups.

Theorem 6.11
(Matui
[
Mat15b
]
).

Let
(X_{\Gamma},\sigma)
be an irreducible, branching subshift of finite type and let
G=V(\Gamma,E)
for some non-empty clopen set
E\subseteq X_{\Gamma}
. Then:

(i)

The derived subgroup
[G,G]
is simple and finitely generated.

(ii)

The group
G
is finitely presented. Indeed, it has type
\mathrm{F}_{\infty}
.

(iii)

The abelianization
G/[G,G]
is isomorphic to
(H_{0}\otimes\mathbb{Z}_{2})\oplus H_{1}
where

H_{0}=\operatorname{coker}(I-A_{\Gamma})\and H_{1}=\ker(I-A_{\Gamma}).

Here
I
is the
n\times n
identity matrix and we regard
I-A_{\Gamma}
as the homomorphism
\mathbb{Z}^{n}\to\mathbb{Z}^{n}
determined by the right action of the matrix on row vectors.

For part
(iii)
above, note that it follows from the Rank–Nullity Theorem that
H_{1}
is isomorphic to the torsion-free part of
H_{0}
. Note also that
H_{0}\otimes\mathbb{Z}_{2}\cong H_{0}/2H_{0}
is trivial if and only if
H_{0}
is finite of odd order. We conclude that
G
is simple if and only if
H_{0}
is finite of odd order and
G
is virtually simple if and only if
H_{0}
is finite.

Remark 6.12
.

Our definitions and notation are very different from those used by Matui in
[
Mat15b
]
, though the groups and conclusions are the same. Matui uses a subshift of finite type to define an étale groupoid, and then considers the topological full group associated to this étale groupoid. In particular, our group
V(\Gamma)
is denoted
\llbracket G\rrbracket
in
[
Mat15b
]
, our group
V(\Gamma,E)
is denoted
\llbracket G|Y\rrbracket
, and the corresponding derived subgroups are denoted
D(\llbracket G\rrbracket)
and
D(\llbracket G|Y\rrbracket)
, respectively.

We now relate the restricted full groups
V(\Gamma,E)
to nuclear type systems.

Definition 6.13
.

If
\mathcal{P}
is a nuclear type system, we define the
nucleus graph
for
\mathcal{P}
to be the induced subgraph of the type graph for
\mathcal{P}
whose vertices correspond to the types in the nucleus.

By the definition of nuclear type systems, this nucleus graph is always strongly connected.

Theorem 6.14
.

Let
\mathcal{P}
be a finite nuclear type system, let
\Delta
be its nucleus graph and let
(X_{\Delta},\sigma)
be the corresponding subshift of finite type. Then there exists a non-empty clopen set
E\subseteq X_{\Delta}
such that
\operatorname{Fix}_{V}(\mathcal{P})\cong V(\Delta,E)
and the corresponding
H_{0}
is isomorphic to
S(\mathcal{P})
.

Proof.

As usual, let
\Gamma(\mathcal{P})
be the type graph of
\mathcal{P}
, so that
\Delta\subseteq\Gamma(\mathcal{P})
, and let
A
be the type of the empty string. Let
E_{A}
be the clopen set in
X_{\Gamma(\mathcal{P})}
consisting of all infinite directed paths with initial vertex
A
, and note each such path is determined by the
0
–
1
labeling on its edges. The
0
–
1
labeling on the edges of
\Gamma(\mathcal{P})
defines a map
\pi\colon X_{\Gamma(\mathcal{P})}\to\mathfrak{C}
which associates to each directed path its sequence of binary labels. The map
\pi
restricts to a homeomorphism
E_{A}\to\mathfrak{C}
. Note that
\pi
maps each cone
C(\textbf{e},X_{\Gamma(\mathcal{P})})
to the cone
\alpha\mathfrak{C}
, where
\alpha
is the label on the finite path
e
, and therefore
\pi
conjugates
V(\Gamma(\mathcal{P}),E_{A})
to
\operatorname{Fix}_{V}(\mathcal{P})
. Thus
\operatorname{Fix}_{V}(\mathcal{P})
is isomorphic to
V(\Gamma(\mathcal{P}),E_{A})
.

Now, we can view
X_{\Delta}
as the subspace of
X_{\Gamma(\mathcal{P})}
consisting of all infinite directed paths whose initial vertex lies in the nucleus. Note that
C(\alpha,X_{\Delta})=C(\alpha,X_{\Gamma(\mathcal{P})})
for any finite path
\alpha
starting at a vertex in the nucleus. If
E\subseteq X_{\Delta}
is a clopen set, it follows that Thompson-like homeomorphisms of
E
with respect to
X_{\Delta}
are the same as Thompson-like homeomorphisms of
E
with respect to
X_{\Gamma(\mathcal{P})}
, and therefore
V(\Delta,E)=V(\Gamma(\mathcal{P}),E)
.

We now show that there exists a clopen set
E\subseteq X_{\Delta}
which maps to
E_{A}
by a Thompson-like homeomorphism. Since
\mathcal{P}
is nuclear, we can partition
\mathfrak{C}
into finitely many cones
\alpha_{1}\mathfrak{C}
,
\alpha_{2}\mathfrak{C}
, …,
\alpha_{k}\mathfrak{C}
such that the type of each
\alpha_{i}
lies in the nucleus. Then each
\alpha_{i}
corresponds to a path
\gamma_{i}
in
\Gamma(\mathcal{P})
from
A
to some vertex
v_{i}
in
\Delta
. The list
v_{1}
,
v_{2}
, …,
v_{k}
of these terminal vertices may include repetition, but since
\Delta
is strongly connected and is not a cycle, there exists an
m>0
so that each vertex of
\Delta
is the endpoint of at least
k
distinct directed paths in
\Delta
of length
m
. It follows that we can find
k
distinct directed paths
\delta_{1}
,
\delta_{2}
, …,
\delta_{k}
in
\Delta
of length
m
such that each
\delta_{i}
ends at the corresponding vertex
v_{i}
. Let

E=C(\delta_{1},\Delta)\cup C(\delta_{2},\Delta)\cup\dots\cup C(\delta_{k},\Delta)

and note that these cones are pairwise disjoint since
\delta_{1}
,
\delta_{2}
, …,
\delta_{k}
are distinct and all have the same length. Then the homeomorphism
f\colon E\to E_{A}
that maps each
C(\delta_{i},\Delta)
to
C(\gamma_{i},\Gamma(\mathcal{P}))
by prefix replacement is Thompson-like and therefore
V(\Delta,E)\cong V(\Gamma(\mathcal{P}),E_{A})\cong\operatorname{Fix}_{V}(\mathcal{P})
.

Finally, we know from Lemma
5.1
(iii)
that
S(\mathcal{P})
is an abelian group. Moreover, if
\mathcal{N}
denotes the set of types in the nucleus of
\mathcal{P}
, it is easy to see that

S(\mathcal{P})\cong\langle\,t\in\mathcal{N}\mid\text{$t=t_{0}+t_{1}$ for each $t\in\mathcal{N}$}\,\rangle.

Let
A_{\Gamma}
be the adjacency matrix for
\Gamma
, and for each type
t
let
e[t]
be the corresponding standard basis row vector. Note that for each type
t
, the corresponding row of
A_{\Gamma}
is
e[t_{0}]+e[t_{1}]
, so the corresponding row of
I-A_{\Gamma}
is
e[t]-e[t_{0}]-e[t_{1}]
. Then
H_{0}=\operatorname{coker}(I-A_{\Gamma})
has precisely the same presentation as
S(\mathcal{P})
, which proves that they are isomorphic.

Combining this with Theorem
6.11
and the fact that
\operatorname{Fix}_{V}(\mathcal{P})
has finite index in

when
\mathcal{P}
is finite (by Lemma

), we obtain the following:

Corollary 6.15
.

Let
\mathcal{P}
be a finite nuclear type system. Then:

(i)

The derived subgroup of
\operatorname{Fix}_{V}(\mathcal{P})
is simple and finitely generated.

(ii)

The groups
\operatorname{Fix}_{V}(\mathcal{P})
and
\operatorname{Stab}_{V}(\mathcal{P})
are finitely presented. Indeed, they have type
\mathrm{F}_{\infty}
.

(iii)

\operatorname{Fix}_{V}(\mathcal{P})
is simple if and only if
S(\mathcal{P})
is finite of odd order, and
\operatorname{Fix}_{V}(\mathcal{P})
and
\operatorname{Stab}_{V}(\mathcal{P})
are virtually simple if and only if
S(\mathcal{P})
is finite.

7
Uncountably many distinct maximal subgroups.

In this section, we shall show that uncountable many maximal subgroups arise as the stabilizers of simple nuclear (albeit infinite) type systems, and with in fact uncountably many isomorphism types of these groups being represented. Furthermore, these constructed groups are not found as point stabilizers (e.g., as in
[
Sav10
]
); they do not even stabilize any finite set of points.

Uncountably many isomorphism types

An essential ingredient for recognising when two simple multinuclear type systems
\mathcal{P}_{1}
and
\mathcal{P}_{2}
on
\Omega
induce isomorphic groups
\operatorname{Fix}_{V}(\mathcal{P}_{1})
and
\operatorname{Fix}_{V}(\mathcal{P}_{2})
is Rubin’s Theorem
[
Rub96
]
. We provide some background to this famous theorem and explain how it applies in our context. We follow the presentation of Rubin’s Theorem as it appears in
[
BEM23
]
.

Let
X
be a topological space and
G
be a group acting on
X
. For
g\in G
define the
support of
g
to be the set
\operatorname{Supt}(g)=\{\,x\in X\mid xg\neq x\,\}
.
If the action of
G
on
X
is faithful we say the action is a
Rubin action
if

(R1)

X
is locally compact, Hausdorff, and has no isolated points, and

(R2)

for each open set
U\subseteq X
and each
p\in U
, the closure of the orbit of
p
under the group
G_{U}=\{\,g\in G\mid\operatorname{Supt}(g)\subseteq U\,\}
contains a neighbourhood of
p
.

If the action satisfies Condition
(R2)
we say the action is
locally dense
following the language of Brin from
[
Bri04
]
. This condition is equivalent to Rubin’s original condition that no point of
U
has a nowhere dense orbit under the action of
G_{U}
.

In any case, we can now state the formulation of Rubin’s Theorem from
[
BEM23
]
.

Theorem 7.1
(Rubin’s Theorem).

If a group
G
has a Rubin action on two spaces
X
and
Y
, then there is a
G
-equivariant homeomorphism
\phi:X\to Y
.

We begin with a foundational observation.

Lemma 7.2
.

If
\mathcal{P}
is a multinuclear type system on
\Omega
then
\operatorname{Fix}_{V}(\mathcal{P})
acts on
\mathfrak{C}
with a Rubin action.

Proof.

Firstly, note that Cantor space satisfies the conditions of Condition
(R1)
in the definition of a Rubin action.

Secondly, we now show that the action of
G=\operatorname{Fix}_{V}(\mathcal{P})
satisfies Condition
(R2)
of the definition of a Rubin action. Let
U
be an open neighbourhood of some point
u\in\mathfrak{C}
. Let
\alpha
be a sufficiently long prefix of
u
such that
\mathopen{|}\alpha\mathclose{|}\geqslant\mathrm{sd}(\mathcal{P})
and
\alpha\mathfrak{C}\subseteq U
. Then
\alpha
has type in some nucleus
\mathcal{Q}
of
\mathcal{P}
. Let
w\in\alpha\mathfrak{C}
with
w\neq u
and let
N
be an arbitrary positive integer. Take
\zeta
to be a sufficiently long prefix of
w
such that
\alpha\preccurlyeq\zeta
,
\zeta
is not a prefix of
u
and
\mathopen{|}\zeta\mathclose{|}\geqslant N
. Then let
\beta
be such that
\alpha\preccurlyeq\beta\preccurlyeq u
with
\beta\perp\zeta
. As both addresses
\beta
and
\zeta
have
\alpha
as prefix, they must both have type in the same nucleus
\mathcal{Q}
. Hence there exists a word
\gamma
such that
\zeta\gamma
has the same type as
\beta
. Then
g=(\beta\;\;\zeta\gamma)\in G
and, as both addresses have
\alpha
as a prefix,
g\in G_{\alpha\mathfrak{C}}\subseteq G_{U}
. The image of
u
under
g
has
\zeta
as a prefix and thus the orbit of
u
under
G_{U}
contains an element that shares a prefix of length
N
with
w
. Since
N
is arbitrary, we conclude that
w
lies in the closure of the orbit of
u
under
G_{U}
. Hence the orbit of
u
is dense in
\alpha\mathfrak{C}
. This demonstrates that
G=\operatorname{Fix}_{V}(\mathcal{P})
has a Rubin action on
\mathfrak{C}
.

Corollary 7.3
.

If
\mathcal{P}
is a multinuclear type system on
\Omega
then the action of
\operatorname{Stab}_{V}(\mathcal{P})
on
\mathfrak{C}
is a Rubin action that stabilizes no non-empty finite subset of
\mathfrak{C}
.

Proof.

Since
\operatorname{Fix}_{V}(\mathcal{P})\leqslant\operatorname{Stab}_{V}(\mathcal{P})
, it follows by Lemma
7.2
that
\operatorname{Stab}_{V}(\mathcal{P})
also has a Rubin action on
\mathfrak{C}
. We then deduce immediately from Condition
(R2)
that no non-empty finite subset of
\mathfrak{C}
is stabilized by
\operatorname{Stab}_{V}(\mathcal{P})
.

We shall employ Rubin’s theorem to separate isomorphism types of stabilizers of simple nuclear type systems, and show by construction that there are uncountably many such isomorphism types for such stabilizers. To do this, we note that the semigroups associated to such partitions are invariants of conjugacy under homeomorphisms of
\mathfrak{C}
.

Lemma 7.4
.

Let
\mathcal{P}
and
\mathcal{Q}
be multinuclear type systems on
\Omega
. If
\operatorname{Fix}_{V}(\mathcal{P})\cong\operatorname{Fix}_{V}(\mathcal{Q})
, then
S(\mathcal{P})\cong S(\mathcal{Q})
.

Proof.

First observe that Lemma
7.2
tells us that the groups
\operatorname{Fix}_{V}(\mathcal{P})
and
\operatorname{Fix}_{V}(\mathcal{Q})
have Rubin actions on
\mathfrak{C}
. Hence, if
\theta\colon\operatorname{Fix}_{V}(\mathcal{P})\to\operatorname{Fix}_{V}(\mathcal{Q})
is an isomorphism, then there is a homeomorphism
\phi\colon\mathfrak{C}\to\mathfrak{C}
such that
g\theta=\phi^{-1}g\phi
for all
g\in\operatorname{Fix}_{V}(\mathcal{P})
. We shall use
\phi
to construct an isomorphism between the semigroups
S(\mathcal{P})
and
S(\mathcal{Q})
.

Let
U_{1}
and
U_{2}
be non-empty proper clopen subsets of
\mathfrak{C}
with the same s-type in
S(\mathcal{P})
. Hence, by Lemma
5.9
, there exists
g\in\operatorname{Fix}_{V}(\mathcal{P})
such that
U_{1}g=U_{2}
. Thus
(U_{1}\phi)(g\theta)=U_{2}\phi
. Express
U_{1}\phi=\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}\gamma_{i}\mathfrak{C}
as a disjoint union of cones in
\mathfrak{C}
. Since
g\theta\in\operatorname{Fix}_{V}(\mathcal{Q})
, we may refine the addresses
\gamma_{i}
and so assume that each
\gamma_{i}
is sufficiently long that
\gamma_{i}^{g\theta}\sim\gamma_{i}
. Hence

\operatorname{s-type}_{\mathcal{Q}}(U_{2}\phi)=\operatorname{s-type}_{\mathcal{Q}}\biggl(\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}\gamma_{i}^{g\theta}\mathfrak{C}\biggr)=\operatorname{s-type}_{\mathcal{Q}}(U_{1}\phi)

since the addresses
\gamma_{i}
and
\gamma_{i}^{g\psi}
have the same type in
\mathcal{Q}
.

As observed in Definition
3.19
, there is a homomorphisms
\psi\colon S(\mathfrak{C})\to S(\mathcal{P})
that maps the characteristic function
\chi_{U}
of each non-empty clopen subset
U
to its s-type in the semigroup
S(\mathcal{P})
. Furthermore, if
\alpha
is any address, then there is a disjoint union decomposition
(\alpha\mathfrak{C})\phi=(\alpha 0\mathfrak{C})\phi\cup(\alpha 1\mathfrak{C})\phi
and hence
\chi_{(\alpha\mathfrak{C})\phi}=\chi_{(\alpha 0\mathfrak{C})\phi}+\chi_{(\alpha 1\mathfrak{C})\phi}
. Therefore
\operatorname{s-type}_{\mathcal{Q}}((\alpha\mathfrak{C})\phi)=\operatorname{s-type}_{\mathcal{Q}}((\alpha 0\mathfrak{C})\phi)+\operatorname{s-type}_{\mathcal{Q}}((\alpha 1\mathfrak{C})\phi)
. This shows that the map
\chi_{\alpha\mathfrak{C}}\mapsto\operatorname{s-type}_{\mathcal{Q}}((\alpha\mathfrak{C})\phi)
preserves the defining relations listed in Lemma
3.16
and hence induces a homomorphism
S(\mathfrak{C})\to S(\mathcal{Q})
. Putting these two homomorphism together with the observation made in the previous paragraph, we deduce that there is an induced homomorphism
\theta_{\ast}\colon S(\mathcal{P})\to S(\mathcal{Q})
given by
\operatorname{s-type}_{\mathcal{P}}(U)\mapsto\operatorname{s-type}_{\mathcal{Q}}(U)
for each non-empty clopen subset
U
of
\mathfrak{C}
. Finally, the inverse
\theta^{-1}\colon\operatorname{Fix}_{V}(\mathcal{Q})\to\operatorname{Fix}_{V}(\mathcal{P})
corresponds to the homeomorphism
\phi^{-1}
and hence the corresponding induced semigroup homomorphism
(\theta^{-1})_{\ast}\colon S(\mathcal{Q})\to S(\mathcal{P})
is given by
\operatorname{s-type}_{\mathcal{Q}}(U)\mapsto\operatorname{s-type}_{\mathcal{P}}(U\phi^{-1})
when
U
is a non-empty proper clopen subset of
\mathfrak{C}
. Consequently,
(\theta^{-1})_{\ast}
is the inverse of
\theta_{\ast}
and we conclude that
\theta_{\ast}
is an isomorphism between the semigroups
S(\mathcal{P})
and
S(\mathcal{Q})
.

\UncountablyManyIso

Proof.

Let
\{p_{n}\}
be a strictly increasing sequence of prime numbers. We shall construct a simple

\mathcal{P}
such that
\operatorname{Stab}_{V}(\mathcal{P})=\operatorname{Fix}_{V}(\mathcal{P})
and

. By Theorem
Introduction
the corresponding

. Denote by
\mathcal{M}
the set of maximal
subgroups corresponding to different increasing sequences of primes. By construction

is uncountable, and by Lemma

the elements of

are pairwise non-isomorphic. Finally, observe that for a given such system
\mathcal{P}
, by

7.3
, the group
\operatorname{Stab}_{V}(\mathcal{P})
does not stabilize any finite set of points.

The type system
\mathcal{P}
has types

,

, and

for

and

, which obey the following rules:

•

has type

;

•

and
X_{1}=Y
;

•

Y_{0}=X
and
Y_{1}=Y
;

•

\bigl(Z^{(n-1,k)}\bigr)_{0}=Z^{(n,0)}
and
\bigl(Z^{(n-1,k)}\bigr)_{1}=Z^{(n-1,k+1)}
for

;

•

\bigl(Z^{(n-1,p_{n})}\bigr)_{0}=X
and
\bigl(Z^{(n-1,p_{n})}\bigr)_{1}=Z^{(n-1,0)}
.

Note that these rules uniquely define a type system
\mathcal{P}
and that
\mathcal{P}
is nuclear since every type has type
X
as a descendant.
From this it also follows that for any two incomparable addresses
\alpha,\beta\in\Omega
there are words
\gamma,\delta\in\Omega
so that
(\alpha\gamma\;\;\beta\delta)\in\operatorname{Fix}_{V}(\mathcal{P})
and thus we see that
\operatorname{Stab}_{V}(\mathcal{P})
does not preserve any circular order on a dense set of points in
\mathfrak{C}
.

The semigroup
S(\mathcal{P})
has presentation

\langle\,x,y,z^{(0)},z^{(1)},\ldots\mid x=z^{(0)}+y,\;y=x+y,\;\text{$z^{(n-1)}=p_{n}z^{(n)}+x+z^{(n-1)}$ for $n\geqslant 1$}\,\rangle

where the generator

corresponds to the type

for each

.
Since
\mathcal{P}
is nuclear, we know from Lemma

that

is an abelian group. The relation

implies that

is the identity element, so the presentation simplifies to

\langle\,y,z^{(0)},z^{(1)},\ldots\mid z^{(0)}=-y,\;\text{$p_{n}z^{(n)}=0$ for $n\geqslant 1$}\,\rangle,

which is a presentation for
\mathbb{Z}\oplus\bigoplus_{n\geqslant 1}\mathbb{Z}/p_{n}\mathbb{Z}
.

By Lemma
3.6
,
\operatorname{Stab}_{V}(\mathcal{P})
acts on the set

of infinite types (which in this case is the set of types as all types in
\mathcal{P}
are infinite) with the kernel of this action being
\operatorname{Fix}_{V}(\mathcal{P})

to map to itself (as the only type with a loop on it) and then an induction argument shows all types are fixed. Hence
\operatorname{Stab}_{V}(\mathcal{P})=\operatorname{Fix}_{V}(\mathcal{P})
.

All that remains is to prove that
\mathcal{P}
is simple. Suppose
\mathcal{Q}
is a proper quotient of
\mathcal{P}
and let
\approx
be the associated equivalence relation on
\mathcal{P}
. We must prove that
\approx
is the universal relation. Since
\mathcal{Q}

identifies at least two distinct types in
\mathcal{P}
.

Now, since

8
Conditions which prevent maximality

In the interest of better understanding maximal subgroups of Thompson’s group
V
, it is useful to be able to determine when a subgroup is in fact a proper subgroup. In view of the results of previous sections, we are particularly interested in the case of subgroups that do not stabilize a non-trivial type system
\mathcal{P}
; that is, in some sense are “primitive” in their partial action on the addresses in
\Omega
. In this section, we study two conditions that are related to this primitivity and we discover that any subgroup satisfying either of these conditions is actually
V
.

We start by proving the surprising fact that
V
has no proper subgroups whose partial action on
\Omega
is two-fold transitive. Recall first that for an address
\alpha
in
\Omega
and an element
g
in
V
, we say that
\alpha^{g}=\beta\in\Omega
if
\alpha\mathfrak{C}
is taken to
\beta\mathfrak{C}
under
g
via a prefix replacement. Otherwise we say
\alpha^{g}
is undefined. This definition extends naturally, for any positive integer
k
, to the set

\Sigma_{k}=\biggl\{(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\dots,\alpha_{k})\in\Omega^{k}\biggm|\text{$\alpha_{i}\mathfrak{C}\cap\alpha_{j}\mathfrak{C}=\varnothing$ for all $i\neq j$ and $\bigcup_{i=1}^{k}\alpha_{i}\mathfrak{C}\neq\mathfrak{C}$}\biggr\}.

Indeed, we define

(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\dots,\alpha_{k})^{g}=(\beta_{1},\beta_{2},\dots,\beta_{k})

if
\alpha_{i}^{g}=\beta_{i}
for all
i
. Otherwise, we say
(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\dots,\alpha_{k})^{g}
is undefined.

Remark

When
k>1
, it will often be the case that for a given
g
there will be an infinite set of sequences
(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\dots,\alpha_{k})
for which
(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\dots,\alpha_{k})^{g}
is undefined. Nevertheless, we still refer to this as a partial action.

Definition 8.1
.

A subgroup
G
of
V
will be said to act
k
-fold transitively on proper cones
when the induced partial action of
G
on
\Sigma_{k}
is
transitive; that is, if for all
(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\dots,\alpha_{k})
,
(\beta_{1},\beta_{2},\dots,\beta_{k})\in\Sigma_{k}
, there exists
g\in G
such that
\alpha_{i}^{g}=\beta_{i}
for
i=1
,
2
, …,
k
.

Lemma 8.2
.

Let
G
be a subgroup of
V
that acts
2
-fold transitively on proper cones. Let
g
be an element of
G

. Then there exists a

that agrees with

0\mathfrak{C}

Let

and such that
\alpha^{g}
is defined. Then
\alpha^{g}=\beta
for some address

and consequently

. Since

is
2
-fold transitive, there exist elements

such that

0^{k}=0,\quad(10)^{k}=\alpha,\quad 0^{k^{\prime}}=0,\quad\beta^{k^{\prime}}=10.

Then

agrees with

on
0\mathfrak{C}
and
(10)^{h}=10
.

Lemma 8.3
.

Let
G

2

.

We shall first show that

Theorem

says that a maximal subgroup of

can only act either zero- or one-fold transitively on proper cones. We now consider a different condition related to primitivity.

We shall use the term

for a transposition

where the set of addresses

has small support.

Definition 8.4
.

Let

\gamma_{0}

\gamma_{1}

(i)

and

, and

(ii)

.

We say that the

(\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1},\dots,\gamma_{n})
is

(as there are

. If

is a

-chain from
\gamma
to
\delta
, then any subchain
(\gamma_{i},\gamma_{i+1},\dots,\gamma_{j})
of

-chain (namely from

to
\gamma_{j}
).

Definition 8.5
.

Let
G
be a subgroup of
V
. We say that
G
is

if for any pair of non-empty addresses
\gamma,\delta\in\Omega
, there is a
G
-chain connecting
\gamma
to
\delta
; that is, there is a sequence
(\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1},\dots,\gamma_{n})
with
\gamma_{0}=\gamma
,
\gamma_{n}=\delta
and such that
(\gamma_{i}\;\;\gamma_{i+1})
is a small transposition in
G
for each

.

Remark 8.6
.

This definition of swap-primitive is related to a criterion for primitivity of actions. Specifically, an action of a group
G
on a set
X
is primitive if and only if for every pair
\{x_{1},x_{2}\}
of distinct elements of
X
, the
G
-orbit of the (undirected) edge
\{x_{1},x_{2}\}
is a connected graph with vertex set
X
(see Proposition
2.1
). A subgroup
G
of
V
is swap-primitive if the graph whose edges correspond to transpositions in
G
of small support is connected. Thus swap-primitivity follows from the hypothesis that the partial action of
G
on
\Omega
is primitive in the graph-theoretic sense together with the hypothesis that
G
has at least one transposition with small support.

We will prove in Theorem
Introduction
that the only subgroup of
V
that acts swap-primitively is
V
itself. We shall need several technical lemmas and start with some basic observations about properties of chains of minimal length.

Lemma 8.7
.

Let
G
be a subgroup of
V
. If
\gamma,\delta\in\Omega
and there is a
G
-chain
(\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1},\dots,\gamma_{n})
connecting
\gamma
to
\delta
such that
\gamma_{i}
is incomparable to
\gamma_{i+2}
for some index
i
, then the sequence
(\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1},\dots,\gamma_{i},\gamma_{i+2},\dots\gamma_{n})
is also a
G
-chain connecting
\gamma
to
\delta
.

Proof.

Note that for every index
j
, the addresses
\gamma_{j}
and
\gamma_{j+1}
are incomparable by assumption that
(\gamma_{j}\;\;\gamma_{j+1})
is a transposition. Under the hypothesis that
\gamma_{i}\perp\gamma_{i+2}
for some index
i
, observe that
G
also contains
(\gamma_{i}\;\;\gamma_{i+2})=(\gamma_{i}\;\;\gamma_{i+1})^{(\gamma_{i+1}\;\;\gamma_{i+2})}
and this is a small transposition. Consequently the link
\gamma_{i+1}
may be omitted and we still have a
G
-chain connecting
\gamma
to
\delta
.

Lemma 8.8
.

Let
G
be a subgroup of
V
and
\gamma
and
\delta
be addresses such that
C=(\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1},\dots,\gamma_{n})
is a
G
-chain of minimal length connecting
\gamma
to
\delta
. Set

\displaystyle E_{C}

\displaystyle=\{\,\gamma_{2i}\mid i\in\mathbb{Z},\;0\leqslant 2i\leqslant n\,\}

\displaystyle O_{C}

\displaystyle=\{\,\gamma_{2i+1}\mid i\in\mathbb{Z},\;0\leqslant 2i+1\leqslant n\,\}.

Let
\lambda_{e}
be the shortest element of
E_{C}
and
\lambda_{o}
be the shortest element of
O_{C}
. Then

(i)

every element of
E_{C}
has
\lambda_{e}
as a prefix,

(ii)

every element of
O_{C}
has
\lambda_{o}
as a prefix, and

(iii)

\lambda_{e}
is incomparable to
\lambda_{o}
.

Proof.

First note that, for every index
i\leqslant n-2
, the address
\gamma_{i}
is comparable to
\gamma_{i+2}
by Lemma
8.7
. Write
E_{C}=\{\gamma_{0},\gamma_{2},\dots,\gamma_{2m}\}
and let
\lambda_{e}
be its shortest member. We shall show, by induction on
m
, that
\lambda_{e}
is a prefix of every member of
E_{C}
. If
m=0
, then the result is trivial. We first assume that
\lambda_{e}
is an element of
\{\gamma_{0},\gamma_{2},\dots,\gamma_{2m-2}\}
. By induction,
\lambda_{e}
is a prefix of each of these
m-1
elements. Now
\gamma_{2m-2}
and
\gamma_{2m}
are comparable. If
\gamma_{2m-2}\preccurlyeq\gamma_{2m}
then it follows
\lambda_{e}\preccurlyeq\gamma_{2m}
. If
\gamma_{2m}\preccurlyeq\gamma_{2m-2}
, then they share
\lambda_{e}
as a prefix (since both are at least as long as
\lambda_{e}
). This establishes the claim in this case. The case when
\lambda_{e}=\gamma_{2m}
follows similarly by applying induction to the set
\{\gamma_{2},\gamma_{4},\dots,\gamma_{2m}\}
instead. Hence by induction
\lambda_{e}
is indeed a prefix of each member of
E_{C}
. Part
(ii)
follows similarly.

Suppose that
\lambda_{e}
and
\lambda_{o}
are comparable. We consider the case that
\lambda_{e}\preccurlyeq\lambda_{o}
. Suppose
\gamma_{2j}=\lambda_{e}
. If
j>0
, consider the address
\gamma_{2j-1}\in O_{C}
. This has
\lambda_{o}
as a prefix and hence
\gamma_{2j}\preccurlyeq\gamma_{2j-1}
by our hypothesis. This is impossible since
(\gamma_{2j-1}\;\;\gamma_{2j})
is assumed to be a valid transposition. If

, the same argument applies using the transposition
(\gamma_{0}\;\;\gamma_{1})
instead, while the case when
\lambda_{o}\preccurlyeq\lambda_{e}
is also similar. We conclude that
\lambda_{e}
and
\lambda_{o}
are incomparable, as claimed.

Corollary 8.9
.

Let
G
be a subgroup of
V
that is swap-primitive. Suppose further that
\gamma
and
\delta
are incomparable addresses of small support such that
C=(\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1},\dots,\gamma_{n})
is a
G
-chain of minimal length connecting
\gamma
to
\delta
where
n\geqslant 2
. Let
E_{C}
and
O_{C}
denote the members of the chain of even and odd index, respectively. Then

and all of the elements of
E_{C}
begin with one of the letters in
\{0,1\}
and all those of
O_{C}
begin with the other letter.

Proof.

First note that if
n=2
, then a
G
-chain
(\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2})
connecting
\gamma
to
\delta
cannot be minimal since
\gamma_{0}
and
\gamma_{2}
are incomparable and we may use Lemma
8.7
to reduce to a
G
-chain of length
1
. Hence it must be the case that

. By Lemma
8.8
, it remains to show that
\gamma_{0}
and
\gamma_{1}
begin with different letters.

Suppose that
0
is the first letter of both
\gamma_{0}
and
\gamma_{1}
. Let
D=(\delta_{0},\delta_{1},\dots,\delta_{m})
be a
G
-chain of minimal length connecting
\gamma_{0}
to
1
. Applying Lemma
8.8
to
C
establishes that all
\gamma_{i}
have
0
as their first letter, while upon applying it to

, we observe that all terms
\delta_{i}
with even index have
0
as first letter and that
1
is the shortest term with odd index. Consequently
\delta_{1}
has
1
as its first letter. Therefore
\delta_{1}
is incomparable to all the addresses
\gamma_{i}
.

Since

is a
G
-chain with
\delta_{0}=\gamma_{0}
, it is the case that
(\gamma_{0}\;\;\delta_{1})\in G
. Suppose that
(\gamma_{i}\;\;\delta_{1})\in G

. Then
(\gamma_{i+1}\;\;\delta_{1})=(\gamma_{i}\;\;\gamma_{i+1})^{(\gamma_{i}\;\;\delta_{1})}\in G

and therefore

. This means that
\gamma
and
\delta
can actually be connected by a

contrary to assumption.

A symmetric argument shows that it cannot be the case that both
\gamma_{0}
and
\gamma_{1}
have

as their first letter. Hence one of them starts with the letter
0
and the other starts with
1
.

Lemma 8.12

Let

that is swap-primitive suppose and that

Lemma 8.13

Let

be a subgroup of

that is swap-primitive. Then there are words

(iv)

(00\;\;01\nu)\in G

Suppose that

that contains one of the transpositions from

\mathopen{|}\alpha\mathclose{|}\geqslant 2

and reverse the roles of

and
\beta
. To fix notation, assume that

are non-empty, so

and

. It follows by Lemma
8.11
that
(010\;\;00\nu 0)\in G
also. By Lemma
8.15
, there is a
G
-chain of length
2
connecting
01
to
0
and this has the form
(01,1\zeta,0)
for some
\zeta\in\Omega
and hence
(01\;\;1\zeta),(0\;\;1\zeta)\in G
. We now compute

(010\;\;00\nu 0)^{(01\;\;1\zeta)(010\;\;00\nu 0)(0\;\;1\zeta)(01\;\;1\zeta)}=(00\;\;0110)

to deduce
(00\;\;0110)\in G
. Taken together with
(01\;\;00\nu)\in G
, we conclude that
G=V
by Lemma
8.14
. The case when
(1\;\;01\delta)\in G
is similar.

Questions

At this time, we are aware of the following types of maximal subgroups of Thompson’s group
V
: stabilizers of certain type systems (both finite and infinite) and the images of the group
T
under automorphisms of
V
. The stabilizers of a finite set of elements in
\mathfrak{C}
of the same tail class considered in Corollary
2.3
can be expressed as stabilizers of type systems. In the case that the tail class is irrational, the corresponding type system will be infinite, while for a rational tail class the type system is atomic quasinuclear type system as discussed in Theorem
5.12
. It was pointed out to us by James Hyde that the centralizer of the transposition
(0\;\;1)
is also a maximal subgroup of
V
. For this we can also construct an infinite type system that is stabilized by the subgroup.

In the present article, we have primarily concentrated our attention on finite type systems and a subsequent one will focus on the maximal subgroups arising as stabilizers of infinite type systems. The questions that follow are intended to explore the maximal subgroups at the boundaries of what can be achieved with our techniques.
