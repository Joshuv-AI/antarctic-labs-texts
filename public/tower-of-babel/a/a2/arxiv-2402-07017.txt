Space-time shape optimization of rotating electric machines
A. Cesarano 1 , C. Dapogny 2 , P. Gangl 1

Abstract
. This article is devoted to the shape optimization of the internal structure of an electric motor, and more precisely of the arrangement of air and ferromagnetic material inside the rotor part with the aim to increase the torque of the machine. The governing physical problem is the time-dependent, non linear magneto-quasi-static version of Maxwell’s equations. This multiphase problem can be reformulated on a 2d section of the real cylindrical 3d configuration; however, due to the rotation of the machine, the geometry of the various material phases at play (the ferromagnetic material, the permanent magnets, air, etc.) undergoes a prescribed motion over the considered time period. This original setting raises a number of issues. From the theoretical viewpoint, we prove the well-posedness of this unusual non linear evolution problem featuring a moving geometry. We then calculate the shape derivative of a performance criterion depending on the shape of the ferromagnetic phase via the corresponding magneto-quasi-static potential. Our numerical framework to address this problem is based on a shape gradient algorithm. The non linear time periodic evolution problems for the magneto-quasi-static potential is solved in the time domain, with a Newton-Raphson method. The discretization features a space-time finite element method, applied on a precise, meshed representation of the space-time region of interest, which encloses a body-fitted representation of the various material phases of the motor at all the considered stages of the time period. After appraising the efficiency of our numerical framework on an academic problem, we present a quite realistic example of optimal design of the ferromagnetic phase of the rotor of an electric machine.

1.
Introduction

Electric motors are devices meant to convert electric energy into mechanical energy.
For multiple reasons that are related to burning scientific and societal challenges,
they have recently aroused a tremendous enthusiasm in the academic and industrial communities.
Notably, as they can achieve much superior yield to thermal engines, their systematic deployment would allow for decisive energy savings,
not depending on expensive and increasingly scarce fossil fuels.
They are also regarded as a promising energy production means in the perspective of the current environmental crisis, as their carbon footprint can be inferior to that of thermal motors by up to 80
\%
.
We refer to classical reference books such as
[
24
,
43
]
for a more exhaustive presentation of electric machines.

Anticipating a little on the more complete description of
Section

2
, an electric motor is composed of an external, static part called stator, and an internal, rotating part – the rotor. An alternating electric current is injected through the coils placed
in the stator, thus generating a magnetic field which sets the rotor in motion. The resulting mechanical work is transmitted to a shaft attached to its core and can be used directly or stored, see
Fig.

1
below for an illustration.
The efficiency of this workflow strongly depends on the arrangement of the costly components of the rotor, in particular the ferromagnetic material and the
permanent magnets (usually made of rare-earth elements), and the optimization of their layout presages tremendous performance improvements, see
[
55
]
about this issue.

Over the past decades, optimal design has been a thriving field of research at the crossroads of mathematics, physics and engineering;
multiple frameworks are available to address such issues, with competing assets and drawbacks.
Among them, shape optimization aims to improve an initial guess through smooth variations of the boundaries or material interfaces at play; this relies on the information contained in the “shape gradients” of the objective and constraint functionals of the problem – see e.g.
[
2
,
3
,
23
,
36
,
54
,
67
]
and
[
20
,
31
,
11
,
58
]
for numerical implementations.
On the other hand, topology optimization methods affect the connectivity of a design,
for instance by leveraging topological derivatives, indicating where holes can be fruitfully nucleated inside a shape
[
5
,
6
,
56
,
57
]
,
or via a suitable reformulation of the physical situation and the optimal design problem under scrutiny in terms of density functions
[
10
,
66
]
.
Surprisingly enough, such techniques have only been applied recently in the physical context of electric motors.
Among the contributions in this direction and without aiming exhaustivity, let us mention the articles
[
34
,
35
,
52
]
using density-based topology optimization strategies, and those
[
15
,
49
,
61
]
relying on the level set method;
see also
[
44
]
about a coupling between the level set method and a body-fitted meshed representation of the machine enabling more accurate finite element simulations.
We refer to
[
26
,
51
]
for recent surveys of optimal design techniques in the field of electromagnetism, and to
[
12
,
73
]
for a particular focus on their application to electric machines.

The specific setting of electric motors raises several challenges. At first, although the boundary value problem governing the behavior of the motor
is a reduction of the full 3d Maxwell’s equations to its 2d cross-section, it features a non linear material law which is essential to capture the decisive saturation effect of the ferromagnetic constituent of the rotor
[
19
]
.
The physical problem at hand is also time-dependent; moreover, as the rotor is in rotation, the optimal design criterion depends on all the stages of this periodic motion; this feature is fairly awkward in view of optimal design because of the tremendous computational cost incurred, see e.g.
[
40
,
65
]
.
To the best of our knowledge and with the exception of the recent contribution
[
29
]
, all the existing studies about the shape or topology optimization of electric motors resort to an additional simplification referred to as the magneto-static approximation of Maxwell’s equations: the physical problem is thereby reduced to a series of decoupled static (elliptic and non linear) problems posed on the various rotated configurations of the motor, which can be solved independently from one another.
Unfortunately, this convenient reformulation does not allow to account for subtle and realistic temporal effects such as eddy currents.

The present article is a natural continuation to the series
[
30
,
53
]
.
We wish to optimize the repartition of the constituent materials of an electric motor; yet, contrary to the prevailing practice in the literature,
we rely on the genuine magneto-quasi-static description of the physical situation.
The boundary value problem at stake can no longer be reduced to a collection of independent stationary problems: it is a non linear, time-dependent problem of mixed elliptic-parabolic type
[
8
]
,
equipped with time periodicity conditions, in which the optimization criterion brings into play all the rotated geometric configurations of the internal structure of the motor.

From the theoretical viewpoint, our first contribution is to prove the well-posedness of this physical evolution problem, which leverages techniques from the theory of non linear partial differential equations.
We next analyze shape optimization problems where the performance functionals at play depend on the solution to this problem.
From the numerical viewpoint, the specific nature of the considered time-dependent problem raises the need for an adapted strategy. Our framework relies on
a space-time finite element discretization, see e.g.
[
46
,
68
]
in the context of linear problems,
[
71
]
for a quasilinear problem, or more recently
[
28
]
for an application in the context of electric machines.
In a nutshell, the time variable is treated as if it were an additional space variable, thus converting a time-dependent problem posed in a
d
-dimensional domain into a static problem on a
(d+1)
-dimensional space time cylinder;
the latter is solved by applying the finite element method on a mesh of the latter.
While this approach suffers from an increase in the dimension of the problem,
it enjoys a number of unique assets over classical time-stepping methods (see e.g.
[
69
]
):

•

The adaptive refinement of the mesh and the parallelization of the solution of the evolution problem can be realized in space and time, jointly;

•

The adjoint problem involved in the shape derivative of the optimization criterion can be solved concurrently with the state equation, thus allowing for a further level of parallelization;

•

Shapes evolving over the considered time period are static in the space-time domain; hence, evolution problems involving such moving domains can be treated by “standard”, time independent numerical methods once a space-time mesh adapted to the moving geometry is available;

•

Time periodicity conditions are straightforward to enforce by direct identification of the degrees of freedom on the bottom and top sections of the space-time cylinder.

The next

\Omega

\Omega

detail the calculation of a shape derivative in this context.
After discussing a few details of our numerical implementation in

5

Section

6

a few conclusions and perspectives of our work are then given in
Section

7
.
The article ends with two
Appendices

and
B

Modeling of the physical behavior of an electric motor

This section introduces the physical and mathematical aspects of the optimal design of electric motors.
After a few generalities about these devices and their modeling in terms of the Maxwell’s equations in

Section

2.2
.
The internal structure of the considered motors is presented in
Section

2.3
, and
we eventually set the mathematical framework and the main notations used throughout the article in the next
Section

.

2.1.
The eddy current problem for the 3d Maxwell’s equations

This section describes, in an informal manner, the general operation of an electric motor and its mathematical formulation via the eddy current problem stemming from the 3d Maxwell’s equations;
our main references about this topic are Chap. 2 in
[
26
]
,
[
62
]
and
[
72
]
.

The motor under scrutiny has a cylindrical structure and its transverse section is sketched in
Fig.

1
.
It is made of an inner rotating part called rotor,
and a static outer part, the stator; these regions are separated by a thin air gap and the core of the rotor is connected to a transmission shaft.
The stator is an arrangement of ferromagnetic material and coils made of thin copper wires;
the rotor is composed of ferromagnetic material, air inclusions and several permanent magnets.

In the classical language of electromagnetism, for which we refer to
[
33
,
39
]
and whose main concepts and notations are used in this presentation,
the device is activated when a time-dependent electric current
\mathbf{J}_{i}
is powered into the coils of the stator.
According to Ampère’s law, this generates a time-dependent magnetic field
\mathbf{H}
in the whole region of interest,
which in turn induces an electric field
\mathbf{E}
by Faraday’s law. The resulting Lorentz force sets in motion the charge carriers contained in the rotor and thereby the attached shaft; this
mechanical work is eventually used or stored.

In order to put this rough sketch into equations, let us first note that, as opposed to light waves, the present application falls in the regime of low-frequency electromagnetism, so that the displacement currents, i.e. the time derivative of the magnetic induction
\mathbf{D}
, can be neglected,
see
[
62
]
for an intuitive explanation of this fact and
[
17
]
for a mathematical justification.
The magnetic field
{\mathbf{H}}
generated around the motor is then related to the total density of current
{\mathbf{J}}
via the following magneto-quasi-static version of Ampère’s law:

(2.1)

\textbf{curl}(\mathbf{H})=\mathbf{J}.

On the other hand, this field is related to the magnetic induction (or magnetic flux density)
{\mathbf{B}}
via the following constitutive law:

(2.2)

\displaystyle\mathbf{H}=\nu(|\mathbf{B}|)\mathbf{B}-\mathbf{M}.

Here, the coefficient
\nu
represents the magnetic reluctivity, i.e. the inverse of the magnetic permeability;
it expresses how the material develops a magnetic field in the presence of a magnetization force.
The vector field
{\mathbf{M}}
denotes the permanent magnetization, i.e. the density of permanent magnetic dipoles within the motor; this field vanishes outside the permanent magnets of the rotor.
The reluctivity
\nu
takes different values depending on the material phase – a dependence with respect to the spatial location which is omitted in
Eq.

2.2
for simplicity.
It assumes constant values
\nu_{a}
,
\nu_{m}
,
\nu_{c}
in air, permanent magnets and copper, respectively.
By contrast, in ferromagnetic materials, it is a function of the intensity
\lvert\mathbf{B}\lvert
of the magnetic induction: for small values of
\mathbf{H}
, the material amplifies the magnetic flux density
\mathbf{B}
, whereas for high values of
\mathbf{H}
, the relation between both quantities is similar to that occurring in void. We refer to
[
59
]
about the calibration of such a reluctivity function from physical measurements.

According to Gauss’s law, the magnetic induction
{\mathbf{B}}
is solenoidal, i.e.

(2.3)

\displaystyle\mbox{div}(\mathbf{B})=0.

In turn, the time variations of
\mathbf{B}
induce an electric field
{\mathbf{E}}
according to Faraday’s law:

(2.4)

\textbf{curl}(\mathbf{E})=-\frac{\partial\mathbf{B}}{\partial t}.

This model is completed by Ohm’s law, which expresses the density
\mathbf{J}
of current inside the region of interest as
the superposition of the density
\mathbf{J}_{i}
of current impressed in the coils and that produced by the Lorentz force acting on the charge carriers:

(2.5)

\mathbf{J}=\mathbf{J}_{i}+\sigma({\mathbf{v}}\times\mathbf{B}+\mathbf{E}),

where
{\mathbf{v}}
is the velocity field of the rotating part and
\sigma
is the electric conductivity, which takes a different, constant value inside each material phase.

The combination of these relations allows to express all the above quantities in terms of a single vector field – the vector potential
\mathbf{A}
– and to characterize the latter by a partial differential equation.
Indeed, at first,

Eq.

2.3
implies the existence of a vector field
\mathbf{A}
such that
\mathbf{B}=\textbf{curl}(\mathbf{A})
, which is determined up to the addition of the gradient of a scalar field.
Judging from
Eq.

2.4
, the vector potential
\mathbf{A}
can be selected as the unique such vector field satisfying
\mathbf{E}=-\frac{\partial\mathbf{A}}{\partial t}
.
Now injecting the constitutive relation
Eq.

2.2
and the expression
Eq.

2.5
of the current density into Ampère’s law
Eq.

2.1
,
we obtain the three-dimensional magneto-quasi-static version of Maxwell’s equation, also referred to as the eddy current problem:

(2.6)

\displaystyle\sigma\left(\frac{\partial\mathbf{A}}{\partial t}-\mathbf{v}\times\textbf{curl}(\mathbf{A})\right)+\textbf{curl}\Big(\nu(\lvert\textbf{curl}(\mathbf{A})\lvert)\textbf{curl}(\mathbf{A})\Big)=\mathbf{J}_{i}+\textbf{curl}(\mathbf{M}).

The partial differential equation
Eq.

2.6
is complemented by suitable time and spatial boundary conditions.
As far as the former are considered, our study concerns the permanent regime of the motor, where the material distribution retrieves its initial configuration and all physical quantities return to their initial values after one rotation.
The equation
Eq.

2.6
is then equipped with a time periodic condition over the considered time period
[0,T]
.
As regards boundary conditions, at each time
t\in[0,T]
, the usual transmission conditions are assumed at the interfaces between the various material phases,
and no magnetic flux leaves the computational domain:

(2.7)

\mathbf{B}\cdot\mathbf{n}=0\text{ on the outer boundary of the stator},

where
\mathbf{n}
is the unit normal vector field to this boundary, pointing outward the device.

2.2.
The 2d magneto-quasi-static equation

As we have mentioned, the full 3d motor under scrutiny has a cylindrical structure.
Taking advantage of its relatively large size along the shaft axis, that we suppose without loss of generality to be the line passing through the origin, oriented along the third coordinate vector
{\mathbf{e}}_{3}
, and
of the invariance of its geometry in this direction,
it is customary to approximate the three-dimensional problem
Eq.

2.6
with a simpler two-dimensional version, posed in the cross-section of the device
[
42
,
70
]
.

This reduction is justified since the impressed current density
\mathbf{J}_{\text{i}}
and the magnetization
\mathbf{M}
are of the form

\mathbf{J}_{i}(t,x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})=(0,0,f(t,x_{1},x_{2})),\>\>\mathbf{M}(t,x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})=(M_{1}(t,x_{1},x_{2}),M_{2}(t,x_{1},x_{2}),0)

and the velocity
\mathbf{v}
representing the rotation of the rotor reads

\mathbf{v}(t,x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})=(v_{1}(t,x_{1},x_{2}),v_{2}(t,x_{1},x_{2}),0).

In turn, the vector potential
\mathbf{A}
is oriented along the axis
\mathbf{e}_{3}
and its values depend only on the spatial position in the cross-section
D
, i.e.

\mathbf{A}(t,x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})=(0,0,u(t,x_{1},x_{2})),

for a suitable function
u(t,x_{1},x_{2})
.

Under these assumptions, the eddy current equation
Eq.

2.6
reduces to a 2d partial differential equation for the scalar field
u
which is posed in the cross-section
D
of the machine;
an elementary calculation indeed yields:

(2.8)

\displaystyle\sigma\left(\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}+v\cdot\nabla u\right)-\mbox{div}(\nu(|\nabla u|)\nabla u)=f-\text{\rm div}M^{\perp},\qquad x\in D,\;t\in(0,T).

Here, we have dropped the bold font for vector fields when they lie inside the cross-section plane of the motor; we have set

M(t,x_{1},x_{2}):=(M_{1}(t,x_{1},x_{2}),M_{2}(t,x_{1},x_{2})),\>\>v(t,x_{1},x_{2}):=(v_{1}(t,x_{1},x_{2}),v_{2}(t,x_{1},x_{2})),

and we use the notation
y^{\perp}=(-y_{2},y_{1})
for the 90
∘
clockwise rotate of a two-dimensional vector
y=(y_{1},y_{2})
.

Figure 1
.
Two-dimensional cross-section
D
of an electric machine of the form considered in
Section

2
: the ferromagnetic material is depicted in red, air is in dark blue, the coils are in yellow, and the permanent magnets are in light blue.

Eventually, the 2d equation
Eq.

2.8
is complemented with the homogeneous Dirichlet boundary condition

u=0\text{ on } \partial D,

inherited from the no-flux assumption
Eq.

2.7
made in the complete 3d model of
Section

2.1
.
As far as temporal boundary conditions are concerned, the time periodic setting implies that:

\forall x=(x_{1},x_{2})\in D,\quad u(0,x)=u(T,x).

Remark 2.1
.

The mathematical analyses conducted in this article can be adapted straightforwardly to handle other time and boundary conditions about
u
, and notably the more classical initial condition
u(0,\cdot)=u_{0}
.
Since the time periodic setting is the most relevant one for our purpose, we do not discuss those alternative instances for brevity.

In engineering applications, the performance of the motor is often measured in terms of the average torque induced by the rotation of the motor shaft over the time period
(0,T)
.
This quantity
\text{\rm Tor}(u)
is evaluated in terms of the potential
u
by the so-called Arkkio’s method
[
63
]
:

(2.9)

\text{\rm Tor}(u):=\frac{1}{T}\frac{L\,\nu_{a}}{(r_{s}-r_{r})}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Sigma}\;Q(x_{1},x_{2})\nabla u\cdot\nabla u\>\text{\rm d}x\text{\rm d}t

where
L
is the length of the true, 3d electric machine in the
{\mathbf{e}}_{3}
direction,
\Sigma
is an annulus with inner and outer radii
r_{r}<r_{s}
lying in the air gap between the rotor and the stator, and the
2\times 2
matrix
Q(x)
is defined by:

\displaystyle\forall x=(x_{1},x_{2})\in D,\quad Q(x_{1},x_{2})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2}}}\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}x_{1}x_{2}&\frac{x_{2}^{2}-x_{1}^{2}}{2}\\[5.0pt]
\frac{x_{2}^{2}-x_{1}^{2}}{2}&-x_{1}x_{2}\end{array}\right).

2.3.
Description of the geometric structure of the motor

In this section, we get into more specifics about the constituent material phases of the motor and we
present the corresponding model governing the expressions of the conductivity and reluctivity coefficients
\sigma
and
\nu
in the formulation of the evolution problem
Eq.

2.8
.
In the first
Section

2.3.1
, we describe the internal structure of the considered motor when it is at rest;
we then discuss its time evolution due to the rotation of the rotor in
Section

2.3.2
.

2.3.1.
Structure of the motor at rest

The structure of the 2d cross-section
D
reflects the three-dimensional arrangement of the motor introduced in the previous
Section

2.1
; it features three disjoint annulus-shaped regions:

\overline{D}=\overline{D_{\text{\rm rot}}}\cup\overline{D_{\text{\rm gap}}}\cup\overline{D_{\text{\rm stat}}},

where the open sets
D_{\text{rot}}
,
D_{\text{\rm gap}}
and
D_{\text{stat}}
stand for the rotor, the separating air gap and the stator, respectively.
The stator
D_{\text{\rm stat}}
consists of

•

A region
D_{\text{stat,f}}
filled with ferromagnetic material;

•

A region
D_{\text{stat,a}}
made of air;

•

The coils
D_{\text{stat,c}}
featuring copper wires.

The part
D_{\text{rot}}
is composed of:

•

A ferromagnetic core, occupying the region
\Omega
;

•

Air, occupying the region
\Omega_{\text{a}}
;

•

Permanent magnets represented by the domain
D_{\text{\rm mag}}
.

In this study, the interface
\Gamma:=\partial\Omega\cap\partial\Omega_{a}
between the phases of the rotor made of ferromagnetic material and air is the center of attention when it comes to optimal design,
while the region
D_{\text{\rm mag}}
occupied by the permanent magnets and the stator
D_{\text{\rm stat}}
are not subject to optimization,
see
Fig.

1
for an illustration of this structure.

The conductivity and reluctivity coefficients
\sigma
and
\nu
have different expressions in the various phases featured in these decompositions.
In order to emphasize their dependence on the actual shape
\Omega\subset D_{\text{rot}}

of the optimized ferromagnetic core, they are labeled with an
Ω
subscript.

(2.12)

\displaystyle\sigma_{\Omega}(x)=\begin{cases}\sigma_{f}&x\in\Omega\cup D_{\text{stat,f}},\\
\sigma_{a}&x\in\Omega_{\text{a}}\cup D_{\text{\rm gap}}\cup D_{\text{stat,a}},\\
\sigma_{m}&x\in D_{\text{\rm mag}},\\
\sigma_{c}&x\in D_{\text{stat,c}},\end{cases}\>\>\>\text{ and }\nu_{\Omega}(x,s)=\begin{cases}\hat{\nu}(s)&x\in\Omega\cup D_{\text{stat,f}},\\
\nu_{a}&x\in\Omega_{\text{a}}\cup D_{\text{\rm gap}}\cup D_{\text{stat,a}},,\\
\nu_{m}&x\in D_{\text{\rm mag}},\\
\nu_{c}&x\in D_{\text{stat,c}}.\end{cases}

In practice, the value of the electric conductivity
\sigma_{a}
in the air region
\Omega_{\text{a}}\cup D_{\text{\rm gap}}\cup D_{\text{stat,a}}
is negligible;
moreover, due to the laminated structure of the part filled with ferromagneric material, which is made of iron sheets,
and since the copper wires typically used in electric machines are insulated, the values of
\sigma
are also negligible in
\Omega\cup D_{\text{stat,f}}
and
D_{\text{stat,c}}
:

\sigma_{f}=\sigma_{a}=\sigma_{c}=0.

The reluctivity
\nu
takes constant, positive values
\nu_{a}
,
\nu_{m}
and
\nu_{c}
in air, in the magnets, and in the wires, respectively.
However, it is a function
\hat{\nu}:{\mathbb{R}}_{+}\to{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
of the amplitude of the gradient of the potential
u
in the regions
\Omega
and
D_{\text{stat,f}}
occupied by ferromagnetic material.
As evidenced in
[
59
]
, the physical properties of the latter imply that
the mapping
s\mapsto\hat{\nu}(s)s
in
Eq.

2.12
is strongly monotone and Lipschitz continuous, i.e.

(2.13)

\text{There exists }0<\underline{\nu}\leq\overline{\nu}<\infty\>\>\text{ s.t. }\>\>\forall s_{1},\>s_{2}\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+},\quad\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}(\hat{\nu}(s_{1})s_{1}-\hat{\nu}(s_{2})s_{2})(s_{1}-s_{2})\geq\underline{\nu}(s_{1}-s_{2})^{2},\\[5.0pt]
|\hat{\nu}(s_{1})s_{1}-\hat{\nu}(s_{2})s_{2}|\leq\;\overline{\nu}|s_{1}-s_{2}|.\end{array}\right.

In particular,
Eq.

2.13
implies that
\hat{\nu}
is uniformly bounded away from
0
and
\infty
:

\forall s\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+},\quad\underline{\nu}\leq\hat{\nu}(s)\leq\overline{\nu}.

2.3.2.
Description of the motion of the rotor

The operation of the motor is analyzed through a representative time period
(0,T)
; during the latter,

D_{\text{\rm rot}}
moves according to the smooth velocity field
v:(0,T)\times\overline{D_{\text{\rm rot}}}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
.
The corresponding flow
\varphi:(0,T)\times D_{\text{\rm rot}}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
is the solution to the following ordinary differential equation:

(2.14)

\forall x\in D_{\text{\rm rot}},\quad\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}\frac{\partial\varphi}{\partial t}(t,x)=v(t,\varphi(t,x))&\text{on }(0,T),\\
\varphi(0,x)=x;\end{array}\right.

we also let the shorthand
\varphi_{t}\equiv\varphi(t,\cdot)
.
It follows from the standard theory of ordinary differential equations that:

(2.15)

•

\varphi
is smooth over
[0,T]\times\overline{D_{\text{\rm rot}}}
;

•

For each
t\in[0,T]
, the mapping
x\mapsto\varphi(t,x)
is a diffeomorphism from
\overline{D_{\text{\rm rot}}}
onto itself;

We also assume that the considered motion of the rotor is
T
-periodic, and so it holds:

(2.16)

\forall x\in D_{\text{\rm rot}},\quad\varphi(0,x)=\varphi(T,x)=x.

Eventually, for convenience in the mathematical analysis, we extend
\varphi_{t}
to a smooth diffeomorphism of
D
such that:

(2.17)

\varphi_{t}(D_{\text{\rm gap}})=D_{\text{\rm gap}},\text{ and }\varphi_{t}\equiv\text{\rm Id}\text{ on }D_{\text{\rm stat}}.

This motion of the rotor induces an evolution in time of the constituent material phases of
D_{\text{\rm rot}}
.
Let
\Omega(t):=\varphi_{t}(\Omega)
and
\Gamma(t):=\varphi_{t}(\Gamma)
(resp.
D_{\text{\rm mag}}(t)=\varphi_{t}(D_{\text{\rm mag}})
, etc.) denote the deformed versions of
\Omega
and
\Gamma
(resp.
D_{\text{\rm mag}}
, etc.) induced by
\varphi_{t}
.
From the mathematical viewpoint, the material coefficients
\sigma
and
\nu
in
Eq.

2.22
depend on time, as:

(2.18)

\forall x\in D,\>\>s\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+},\quad\sigma_{\Omega(t)}(\varphi_{t}(x))=\sigma_{\Omega}(x)\text{ and }\nu_{\Omega(t)}(\varphi_{t}(x),s)=\nu_{\Omega}(x,s).

In practice, the cross-section
D
of the motor is a two-dimensional disk with center
0
and
\varphi_{t}
corresponds to the rotation with origin
0
and angle
\alpha(t):=\frac{2\pi t}{T}
:

(2.19)

\varphi_{t}(x)=R_{\alpha(t)}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}x_{1}\\
x_{2}\end{array}\right),\text{ where } R_{\alpha(t)}:=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\cos\alpha(t)&-\sin\alpha(t)\\
\sin\alpha(t)&\cos\alpha(t)\end{array}\right).

With this definition, a simple calculation reveals that the velocity field
v
is divergence-free and that the Jacobian determinant of
\varphi_{t}
identically equals
1
:

(2.20)

\forall t\in[0,T],\>\>x\in D,\>\>\text{\rm div}\>v(t,x)=0,\text{ and } \det\nabla\varphi_{t}(x)=1.

Remark 2.2
.

Even though this very specific setting where the space dimension
d
equals
2
and the motion
\varphi(t,x)
of the rotor accounts for a rotation is the relevant one in practice,
the general case where
d
is arbitrary and
\varphi_{t}
is an arbitrary smooth diffeomorphism of
D
satisfying
Eqs.

2.15
,
2.16
and
2.17
, with associated velocity field
v(t,x)=\frac{\partial\varphi}{\partial t}(t,\varphi_{t}^{-1}(x))
, does not pose any additional difficulty in the analysis.
For this reason, in the subsequent developments, we retain generic notations for
d
and
\varphi
insofar as possible.

2.4.
Notations and mathematical formulation of the magneto-quasi-static problem

The following notations are adopted throughout the remainder of this article.

•

We denote by
\text{\rm Id}:{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
the identity mapping of
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
and by
\text{\rm I}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d\times d}
the identity matrix with size
d
.

•

For any time
t\in[0,T]
,
n_{\Omega(t)}
(resp.
n
) stands for the unit normal vector to the deformed interface
\Gamma(t)
(resp. to the interface at rest
\Gamma
), pointing outward
\Omega(t)
(resp. outward
\Omega
).

•

We denote by

Q=(0,T)\times D,\text{ and } Q_{\Omega}:=\Big\{(t,x)\in Q,\>\>x\in\Omega(t)\Big\}

the (open) space-time cylinders induced by
D
and
\Omega
over the time period
(0,T)
.

•

Let
\alpha:D\to{\mathbb{R}}
be a smooth quantity on
\overline{\Omega}
and
D\setminus\Omega
, which is possibly discontinuous across the interface
\Gamma
; we denote by

\alpha^{\pm}(x)=\lim\limits_{t>0\atop t\to 0}\alpha(x\pm tn(x))

the one-sided limits of
\alpha
at
x\in\Gamma
, from outside and inside
\Omega
, respectively.

•

In particular, let
u:D\to{\mathbb{R}}
be a smooth function on
\overline{\Omega}
and
D\setminus\Omega
, which is possibly discontinuous at the interface
\Gamma
.
For any
x\in\Gamma
, we denote by

\frac{\partial u^{\pm}}{\partial n}(x)=\lim\limits_{t\to 0\atop t>0}\nabla u(x\pm tn(x))\cdot n(x)\text{ and }\nabla_{\Gamma}u^{\pm}(x)=\nabla u(x\pm tn(x))-\frac{\partial u^{\pm}}{\partial n}(x)n(x),

the one-sided normal derivatives and tangential gradients of
u
at
x
.

•

Let
w:D\to{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
be a smooth enough vector field. We denote by

w_{\Gamma}:\Gamma\to{\mathbb{R}}^{d},\quad w_{\Gamma}(x)=w(x)-\Big(w(x)\cdot n(x)\Big)n(x)

the tangential part of
w
on
\Gamma
.

•

Let
u:Q\to{\mathbb{R}}
be a smooth enough scalar function of time and space; then,

–

\nabla u(t,x)
denotes the gradient of
u
with respect to the space variable
x
only;

–

\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}(t,x)
is the partial derivative of
u
with respect to the time variable, i.e. the derivative of the partial mapping
t\mapsto u(t,x)
for fixed
x\in D
;

–

\frac{\text{\rm d}u}{\text{\rm d}t}(t,x)
is the total (or material) derivative of
u
according to the velocity field
v(t,x)
, that is:

(2.21)

\frac{\text{\rm d}u}{\text{\rm d}t}(t,x)=\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}(t,x)+v(t,x)\cdot\nabla u(t,x);

in other terms,
\frac{\text{\rm d}u}{\text{\rm d}t}(t,x)
is the derivative of the mapping
t\mapsto u(t,\varphi_{t}(y))
evaluated at time
t
and point
y=\varphi_{t}^{-1}(x)
.

•

Let
w=(w_{1},\ldots,w_{d}):Q\to{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
be a (smooth enough) vector-valued function of space and time.
We denote by
\nabla w
, or often
\left[\nabla w\right]
, the derivative of
w
with respect to the spatial variable,
i.e. the
d\times d
matrix field with entries:

\left[\nabla w\right]_{ij}=\frac{\partial w_{i}}{\partial x_{j}},\quad i,j=1,\ldots,d.

Summarizing the previous discussions and taking these notations into account, the transverse component of the vector potential inside the motor, which we denote by
u_{\Omega}:Q\to{\mathbb{R}}
to emphasize its dependence on the phase
\Omega
to be optimized, satisfies the following evolution problem:

(2.22)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}\sigma_{\Omega(t)}\frac{\text{\rm d}u_{\Omega}}{\text{\rm d}t}-\text{\rm div}\left(\nu_{\Omega(t)}(x,\lvert\nabla u_{\Omega}\lvert)\nabla u_{\Omega}\right)=f&\text{in }(0,T)\times D,\\
u_{\Omega}(t,x)=0&\text{for }t\in(0,T),\>x\in\partial D,\\
u_{\Omega}(0,x)=u_{\Omega}(T,x)&\text{for }x\in D_{\text{mag}},\end{array}\right.

where the coefficients
\sigma_{\Omega(t)}
and
\nu_{\Omega(t)}
are given by
Eq.

2.12
.
The precise mathematical setting of this problem and its well-posedness are discussed in
Section

4.1
below.

Remark 2.3
.

•

In passing from the formulation
Eq.

2.8
to
Eq.

2.22
, we have absorbed the term
\text{\rm div}(M^{\perp})
at the right-hand side of the problem
Eq.

2.8
into the source
f
.
This convenient notational simplification is slightly abusive though, as both terms
f
and
\text{\rm div}(M^{\perp})
are not exactly of the same mathematical nature: while
f(t,\cdot)
will typically belong to
L^{2}(D)
in our mathematical analysis,
the quantity
M^{\perp}(t,\cdot)
will be smooth on
D_{\text{\rm mag}}
and vanish outside this set, so that
\text{\rm div}(M^{\perp})(t,\cdot)\in H^{-1}(D)
. Nevertheless, the subsequent developments are easily adapted to handle this term, see
Remark

4.4
about this point.

•

The subsequent mathematical discussions rely on the fact that the domain
D_{\text{\rm mag}}
is non empty. If it were to be the case,
Eq.

2.22
would simply boil down to decoupled elliptic problems.

•

As we have mentioned, the non linearity of the boundary value problem
Eq.

2.22
(notably, the dependence of the reluctivity on
\lvert\nabla u\lvert
) is key in the correct modeling of the physical problem. However, the linear setting, where

\hat{\nu}(s)\equiv\nu_{f}\text{ for some } \nu_{f}>0,

will sometimes be considered in the mathematical setting, as it allows for a less technical exposition of the main ideas.

3.
Generalities about shape optimization and treatment of an academic problem

In this section, we enter into the optimal design framework of this article.
After introducing the model shape optimization problem under scrutiny in
Section

3.1
,
we detail in
Section

3.2
the calculation of a “simple” shape derivative as a handful preliminary to our subsequent analyses.

3.1.
Shape optimization in a nutshell

In this article, we aim to optimize the shape of the phase
\Omega
made of ferromagnetic material
with respect to a physical criterion, that is

(3.1)

\min\limits_{\Omega\subset D_{\text{\rm rot}}}J(\Omega).

The performance criterion under scrutiny
J(\Omega)
depends on
\Omega
via the transverse component of vector potential
u_{\Omega}(t,x)
of the magnetic flux density, solution to the evolution problem
Eq.

2.22
.
We consider a model objective function of the form

(3.2)

J(\Omega)=\int_{Q}j(u_{\Omega}(t,x))\>\text{\rm d}x\text{\rm d}t,

where
j:{\mathbb{R}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
is a smooth function, satisfying the growth conditions:

\exists\>C>0,\>\>\forall u\in{\mathbb{R}},\quad\lvert j(u)\lvert\leq C(1+\lvert u\lvert)^{2},\>\>\lvert j^{\prime}(u)\lvert\leq C(1+\lvert u\lvert),\text{ and }\lvert j^{\prime\prime}(u)\lvert\leq C.

Note that different quantities of interest could be considered without much changes to our analysis.
Notably,
J(\Omega)
could also depend on the gradient of
u_{\Omega}
, as in the torque functional
\text{\rm Tor}(u_{\Omega})
defined in
Eq.

2.9
, see
Section

6
.
Besides, constraints (e.g. on the volume of
\Omega
) could be added to the problem
Eq.

3.1
without much change to the subsequent theoretical developments.

The numerical solution of the problem
Eq.

3.1
usually calls for the derivative of the objective function
J(\Omega)
with respect to the
domain – a notion which can be defined in various ways.
Here, we rely on Hadamard’s boundary variation method, see e.g.
[
36
,
54
,
67
]
or
[
2
]
for a recent overview.
In a nutshell, variations of a shape
\Omega\subset D_{\text{\rm rot}}
are considered under the form

\Omega_{\theta}:=(\text{\rm Id}+\theta)(\Omega),\quad\theta\in W^{1,\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{R}^{d}),\>\>||\theta||_{W^{1,\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{R}^{d})}<1,

that is,
\Omega_{\theta}
is a version of
\Omega
whose points have been displaced according to the “small’ vector field
\theta
, see
Fig.

2
.

Figure 2
.
The variation
\Omega_{\theta}
of
\Omega
featured in the method of Hadamard is obtained by displacing its points according to the “small” vector field
\theta
.

A function
F(\Omega)
of the domain is then called shape differentiable at a particular domain
\Omega
provided the underlying mapping
\theta\mapsto F(\Omega_{\theta})
,
from
W^{1,\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{R}^{d})
into
{\mathbb{R}}
, is Fréchet differentiable at
\theta=0
. The corresponding shape derivative
F^{\prime}(\Omega)(\theta)
gives rise to the following expansion:

(3.3)

F(\Omega_{\theta})=F(\Omega)+F^{\prime}(\Omega)(\theta)+\text{\rm o}(\theta),\text{ where }\frac{\lvert\text{\rm o}(\theta)\lvert}{\lvert\lvert\theta\lvert\lvert_{W^{1,\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{R}^{d})}}\xrightarrow{\theta\to 0}0.

In particular, a deformation
\theta\in W^{1,\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{R}^{d})
such that
F^{\prime}(\Omega)(\theta)<0
is a descent direction for
F
from
\Omega
, since for a small enough descent step
\tau>0
,

F(\Omega_{\tau\theta})=F(\Omega)+\tau F^{\prime}(\Omega)(\theta)+\text{\rm o}(\tau),

i.e. a “small” deformation of
\Omega
according to
\theta
produces a design
\Omega_{\tau\theta}
which is “better” with respect to the criterion
F(\Omega)
.

In the present application, we only optimize the interface
\Gamma
between the phases
\Omega
,
\Omega_{\text{a}}
of the rotor, respectively made of ferromagnetic material and air.
Accordingly, the deformations
\theta
involved in Hadamard’s method ought to be restricted to the subspace

\Theta_{\text{\rm ad}}:=\Big\{\theta\in W^{1,\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{R}^{d}),\quad\theta=0\text{ on }\overline{D_{\text{\rm stat}}},\>\>\theta\cdot n=0\text{ on } \partial D_{\text{\rm rot}}\cup\partial D_{\text{\rm mag}}\Big\}.

Let us end this section with a few informal words about the general structure of the shape derivative of a “smooth enough” function of the domain
F(\Omega)
,
such as those considered in the sequel; we refer to e.g. §5.9 in
[
36
]
for more details about this subject.
Usually, several equivalent formulas are available for
F^{\prime}(\Omega)(\theta)
.
A rigorous calculation usually yields the so-called volume (or distributed) expression of this derivative, which is of the form:

(3.4)

F^{\prime}(\Omega)(\theta)=\int_{D}\Big(M_{\Omega}:\nabla\theta+s_{\Omega}\cdot\theta\Big)\>\text{\rm d}x,

where
M_{\Omega}:D\to{\mathbb{R}}^{d\times d}
and
s_{\Omega}:D\to{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
are matrix and vector fields depending on
\Omega
,
u_{\Omega}
and the function
F(\Omega)
,
notably via a so-called adjoint state
p_{\Omega}
, solution to a problem similar to
Eq.

2.22
, with a different right-hand side.
Often,
Eq.

3.4
can be turned into an equivalent, surface expression:

(3.5)

F^{\prime}(\Omega)(\theta)=\int_{\Gamma}v_{\Omega}(\theta\cdot n)\>\text{\rm d}s,

featuring a scalar field
v_{\Omega}:\partial\Omega\to{\mathbb{R}}
.
This alternative structure encodes the intuitive fact that
F^{\prime}(\Omega)(\theta)
solely depends on the normal component of the deformation
\theta
– roughly speaking, vector fields taking only tangential directions on the boundary
\partial\Omega
only account for a “reparametrization” of
\Omega
. The surface form
Eq.

3.5
is also helpful for numerical purpose, as it immediately reveals a descent direction for
F(\Omega)
.
Indeed, letting
\theta=-v_{\Omega}n
on
\partial\Omega
immediately yields
F^{\prime}(\Omega)(\theta)<0
.

Mathematically, the surface formula
Eq.

3.5
for
F^{\prime}(\Omega)(\theta)
is often obtained from its volume counterpart
Eq.

3.4
after integration by parts,
assuming some regularity from the boundary
\partial\Omega
and the functions
u_{\Omega}
,
p_{\Omega}
, see e.g. the proofs of
Lemmas

3.1
and
4.1
below.

3.2.
Calculation of the shape derivative of a “simple” functional of the domain

We now present the calculation of the derivative of a “simple” functional
J(\Omega)
, depending on the shape
\Omega

via the integration of a fixed, smooth function
f(t,x)
on the transformed versions
\Omega(t)=\varphi_{t}(\Omega)
of
\Omega
for
t\in(0,T)
.
We seize this opportunity to introduce a few technical preliminaries useful throughout this article in
Section

3.2.1
, before proceeding to the calculation, properly speaking, in
Section

3.2.2
.

3.2.1.
Technical preliminaries

For any vector field
\theta\in\Theta_{\text{\rm ad}}
, let us introduce the corresponding space-time deformation mapping
\Theta:Q\to(0,T)\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}

(3.6)

\Theta(t,x):=(\Theta_{t}(t,x),\Theta_{x}(t,x))=(t,\varphi_{t}\circ(\text{\rm Id}+\theta)\circ\varphi^{-1}_{t}(x)),

whose notation omits its dependence on
\theta
for simplicity.
The space-time cylinder
Q_{\Omega_{\theta}}
induced by
\Omega_{\theta}
then reads:

Q_{\Omega_{\theta}}:=\Theta(Q_{\Omega})=\Big\{(t,x)\in Q,\>\>x\in\varphi_{t}(\Omega_{\theta})\Big\}.

Remark 3.1
.

Loosely speaking, the space-time deformation mapping
\Theta
transmits the effect of
\theta
on the configuration at rest
\Omega
(which is deformed into
\Omega_{\theta}
) to the rotated configurations
\Omega(t)=\varphi_{t}(\Omega)
(which become
\varphi_{t}(\Omega_{\theta})
).
Contrary to the works
[
13
,
14
]
, we do not aim to find a time-dependent shape, but rather optimize the design of its configuration at rest with respect to a measure of performance depending on its known deformations
\varphi_{t}(\Omega)
.
The action of
\Theta
on the space-time cylinder
Q_{\Omega}
is exemplified in
Fig.

3
.

Figure 3
.
One deformation
\theta
of the shape
\Omega
, with the associated deformation
\Theta
of the space-time cylinder
Q_{\Omega}
.

The derivative of
\Theta(t,x)
with respect to the variables
(t,x)
is the following
(d+1)\times(d+1)
matrix field
F(\theta):Q\to{\mathbb{R}}^{(d+1)\times(d+1)}
:

F(\theta)=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}F_{tt}(\theta)&F_{tx}(\theta)\\
F_{xt}(\theta)&F_{xx}(\theta)\end{array}\right),

whose
1\times 1
and
1\times d
blocks
F_{tt}(\theta)
and
F_{tx}(\theta)
read:

F_{tt}(\theta)(t,x)=\frac{\partial\Theta_{t}}{\partial t}(t,x)=1,\quad F_{tx}(\theta)(t,x)=\nabla\Theta_{t}(t,x)=0,

and whose
d\times 1
and
d\times d
blocks
F_{xt}(\theta)
and
F_{xx}(\theta)
are given by:

\begin{array}[]{l}\begin{array}[]{>{\displaystyle}cc>{\displaystyle}l}F_{xt}(\theta)(t,x)&=&\frac{\partial\Theta_{x}}{\partial t}(t,x)\\[6.99997pt]
&=&\frac{\partial\varphi_{t}}{\partial t}((\text{\rm Id}+\theta)(\varphi^{-1}_{t}(x)))+\Big[\nabla\varphi_{t}((\text{\rm Id}+\theta)(\varphi^{-1}_{t}(x)))\Big]\Big[(\text{\rm I}+\nabla\theta)(\varphi_{t}^{-1}(x))\Big]\frac{\partial}{\partial t}(\varphi_{t}^{-1}(x)),\end{array}\\[30.00005pt]
\begin{array}[]{>{\displaystyle}cc>{\displaystyle}l}F_{xx}(\theta)(t,x)&=&\nabla\Theta_{x}(t,x)\\[5.0pt]
&=&\Big[\nabla\varphi_{t}((\text{\rm Id}+\theta)(\varphi_{t}^{-1}(x)))\Big]\>\Big[(\text{\rm I}+\nabla\theta)(\varphi^{-1}_{t}(x))\Big]\>\Big[\nabla\varphi_{t}^{-1}(x)\Big].\end{array}\end{array}

A simple calculation immediately yields the expression of the matrix field
F(\theta)^{-T}
:

(3.7)

F(\theta)^{-T}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}1&b(\theta)^{T}\\
0&F_{xx}(\theta)^{-T}\end{array}\right),\text{ where }b(\theta):Q\to{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\text{ is defined by }b(\theta):=-\Big[F_{xx}(\theta)\Big]^{-1}F_{xt}(\theta).

Let now
u:Q\to{\mathbb{R}}
be a smooth enough function; the derivative of the composite function
v:=u\circ\Theta:Q\to{\mathbb{R}}
satisfies:

\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}\\[2.5pt]
\nabla u\end{array}\right)\circ\Theta=F(\theta)^{-T}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\frac{\partial v}{\partial t}\\[2.5pt]
\nabla v\end{array}\right),

and so

(3.8)

\left(\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}\right)\circ\Theta=\frac{\partial v}{\partial t}+b(\theta)\cdot\nabla v,\>\text{ and }\>(\nabla u)\circ\Theta=\Big[F_{xx}(\theta)\Big]^{-T}\nabla v.

We now list a series of useful technical identities in the calculation of shape derivatives.
These can be rigorously established along the lines of Chap. 5 in
[
36
]
; for brevity, we limit ourselves to formal statements and computations which in particular omit the functional setting.

•

The vector field
b(0):Q\to{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
equals:

\begin{array}[]{>{\displaystyle}cc>{\displaystyle}l}b(0)(t,x)&=&-F_{xt}(0)(t,x)\\[7.5pt]
&=&-\frac{\partial\varphi_{t}}{\partial t}(\varphi_{t}^{-1}(x))-\Big[\nabla\varphi_{t}(\varphi_{t}^{-1}(x))\Big]\frac{\partial}{\partial t}(\varphi_{t}^{-1}(x))\\[7.5pt]
&=&-\frac{\partial}{\partial t}(\varphi_{t}(\varphi_{t}^{-1}(x)))\\[7.5pt]
&=&0,\end{array}

where we have used the fact that

F_{xx}(0)(t,x)=\Big[\nabla\varphi_{t}(\varphi_{t}^{-1}(x))\Big]\Big[\nabla\varphi_{t}^{-1}(x)\Big]=\nabla(\varphi_{t}\circ\varphi_{t}^{-1}(x))=\text{\rm I}.

•

It follows easily from the above two relations that the mapping
\theta\mapsto b(\theta)
is Fréchet differentiable at
\theta=0
, with derivative:

(3.9)

b^{\prime}(0)(\theta)=-F_{xt}^{\prime}(0)(\theta).

•

The derivatives of the mappings
\theta\mapsto F_{xt}(\theta)
and
\theta\mapsto F_{xx}(\theta)
at
\theta=0
read:

(3.10)

F_{xt}^{\prime}(0)(\theta)(t,x)=\left[\nabla\left(\frac{\partial\varphi_{t}}{\partial t}\right)(\varphi_{t}^{-1}(x))\right]\theta(\varphi_{t}^{-1}(x))+\Big[\nabla^{2}\varphi_{t}(\varphi_{t}^{-1}(x))\theta(\varphi_{t}^{-1}(x))\Big]\frac{\partial}{\partial t}(\varphi_{t}^{-1}(x))\\
+\Big[\nabla\varphi_{t}(\varphi_{t}^{-1}(x))\Big]\Big[\nabla\theta(\varphi_{t}^{-1}(x))\Big]\frac{\partial}{\partial t}(\varphi_{t}^{-1}(x)),

and

(3.11)

F_{xx}^{\prime}(0)(\theta)(t,x)=\Big[\nabla^{2}\varphi_{t}(\varphi_{t}^{-1}(x))\theta(\varphi_{t}^{-1}(x))\Big]\Big[\nabla\varphi_{t}^{-1}(x)\Big]+\Big[\nabla\varphi_{t}(\varphi_{t}^{-1}(x))\Big]\Big[\nabla\theta(\varphi_{t}^{-1}(x))\Big]\Big[\nabla\varphi_{t}^{-1}(x)\Big].

•

The determinant
m(\theta):Q\to{\mathbb{R}}
of the space-time change of variables induced by
\Theta
in
Eq.

3.6
,

(3.12)

m(\theta):=\left\lvert\det F(\theta)\right\lvert,

is a Fréchet differentiable function of
\theta
at
0
and:

(3.13)

m^{\prime}(0)(\theta)(t,x):=\text{\rm tr}\left(\Big[\nabla^{2}\varphi_{t}(\varphi_{t}^{-1}(x))\theta(\varphi_{t}^{-1}(x))\Big]\Big[\nabla\varphi_{t}^{-1}(x)\Big]\right)+(\text{\rm div}\theta)(\varphi_{t}^{-1}(x)).

•

Let
f:Q\to{\mathbb{R}}
be a smooth scalar function.
The mapping
\theta\mapsto f\circ\Theta
is Fréchet differentiable at
\theta=0
, and its derivative
f_{1}(\theta)
reads:

(3.14)

f_{1}(\theta)(t,x):=\left(\Big[\nabla\varphi_{t}(\varphi_{t}^{-1}(x))\Big]^{T}\nabla f(t,x)\right)\cdot\theta(\varphi_{t}^{-1}(x)).

•

Likewise, let
w:Q\to{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
be a smooth vector field; then the mapping
\theta\mapsto w\circ\Theta
is Fréchet differentiable at
\theta=0
, and its derivative
w_{1}(\theta)
reads

(3.15)

w_{1}(\theta)(t,x):=\Big[\nabla w(t,x)\Big]\Big[\nabla\varphi_{t}(\varphi_{t}^{-1}(x))\Big]\theta(\varphi_{t}^{-1}(x)).

•

The matrix-valued mapping
A(\theta):Q\to{\mathbb{R}}^{d\times d}
defined by

(3.16)

A(\theta):=m(\theta)\>F_{xx}^{-1}(\theta)F_{xx}^{-T}(\theta);

is Fréchet differentiable at
\theta=0
, with derivative:

(3.17)

A^{\prime}(0)(\theta)=m^{\prime}(0)(\theta)\text{\rm I}-F_{xx}^{\prime}(0)(\theta)-F_{xx}^{\prime}(0)(\theta)^{T}.

Remark 3.2
.

When the motion
\varphi_{t}
is induced by a rotation as in
Eq.

2.19
, some of the above expressions can be conveniently simplified.
Indeed, it holds in this case:

\frac{\text{\rm d}R_{\alpha(t)}}{\text{\rm d}t}=\alpha^{\prime}(t)\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}-\sin(\alpha(t))&-\cos(\alpha(t))\\
\cos(\alpha(t))&-\sin(\alpha(t))\end{array}\right)\qquad\mbox{ and }\qquad\Big[\nabla v(t,x)\Big]=\alpha^{\prime}(t)\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&-1\\
1&0\end{array}\right),

and so:

\begin{array}[]{ccl}F_{xx}^{\prime}(0)(\theta)&=&R_{\alpha(t)}\Big[\nabla\theta(\varphi_{t}^{-1}(x))\Big]R_{-\alpha(t)},\\[5.0pt]
F_{xt}^{\prime}(0)(\theta)&=&\left(\frac{\text{\rm d}R_{\alpha(t)}}{\text{\rm d}t}\right)\theta(\varphi_{t}^{-1}(x))+R_{\alpha(t)}\Big[\nabla\theta(\varphi_{t}^{-1}(x))\Big]\left(\frac{\text{\rm d}R_{-\alpha(t)}}{\text{\rm d}t}\right),\end{array}

and for any smooth enough vector field
w:Q\to{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
,

w_{1}(\theta)=\Big[\nabla w(t,x)\Big]R_{\alpha(t)}\theta(\varphi_{t}^{-1}(x)).

3.2.2.
An academic shape optimization problem

In this section, we calculate the derivative of the following shape functional:

(3.18)

J(\Omega)=\int_{Q_{\Omega}}f(t,x)\>\text{\rm d}x\text{\rm d}t,

depending on the domain
\Omega
via the integral of a smooth function
f\in{\mathcal{C}}^{\infty}([0,T]\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d})
over the space-time cylinder
Q_{\Omega}
, which involves all the deformed version
\Omega(t)
of
\Omega
,
t\in(0,T)
.
Again, this quite academic question is a useful preliminary to the treatment of the more “physical” shape functionals described in
Section

4
.

Lemma 3.1
.

The functional
J(\Omega)
in
Eq.

3.18
is shape differentiable at any bounded Lipschitz shape
\Omega\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, and its shape derivative reads, in volume form:

(3.19)

J^{\prime}(\Omega)(\theta)=\int_{Q_{\Omega}}\text{\rm tr}\left(\Big[\nabla^{2}\varphi_{t}(\varphi_{t}^{-1}(x))\theta(\varphi_{t}^{-1}(x))\Big]\Big[\nabla\varphi_{t}^{-1}(x)\Big]\right)f(t,x)\>\text{\rm d}x\text{\rm d}t\\
+\int_{Q_{\Omega}}(\text{\rm div}\theta)(\varphi_{t}^{-1}(x))f(t,x)\>\text{\rm d}x\text{\rm d}t+\int_{Q_{\Omega}}\Big[\nabla\varphi_{t}(\varphi_{t}^{-1}(x))\Big]^{T}\nabla f(t,x)\cdot\theta(\varphi_{t}^{-1}(x))\>\text{\rm d}x\text{\rm d}t,

or equivalently, in surface form:

(3.20)

J^{\prime}(\Omega)(\theta)=\int_{\partial\Omega}v_{\Omega}\>\theta\cdot n\>\text{\rm d}s,\text{ where }v_{\Omega}(x):=\int_{0}^{T}|\det\nabla\varphi_{t}(x)|f(t,\varphi_{t}(x))\>\text{\rm d}t.

Proof.

For any small enough deformation
\theta\in\Theta_{\text{\rm ad}}
, the definition of
J(\Omega_{\theta})
reads:

J(\Omega_{\theta})=\int_{Q_{\Omega_{\theta}}}f(t,x)\>\text{\rm d}x\text{\rm d}t.

Using a change of variables based on the mapping
\Theta
in
Eq.

3.6
and the fact that, by definition
\Theta^{-1}(Q_{\Omega_{\theta}})=Q_{\Omega}
, we obtain:

J(\Omega_{\theta})=\int_{Q_{\Omega}}m(\theta)(f\circ\Theta)(t,x)\>\text{\rm d}x\text{\rm d}t,

where we recall the notation
m(\theta)=\left\lvert\det F(\theta)\right\lvert
.
Combining the expansions
Eqs.

3.13
and
3.14
of the two terms in the above integrand,
we see that the mapping
\theta\mapsto J(\Omega_{\theta})
is differentiable at
\theta=0
, with derivative

J^{\prime}(\Omega)(\theta)=\int_{Q_{\Omega}}\text{\rm tr}\left(\Big[\nabla^{2}\varphi_{t}(\varphi_{t}^{-1}(x))\theta(\varphi_{t}^{-1}(x))\Big]\Big[\nabla\varphi_{t}^{-1}(x)\Big]\right)f(t,x)\>\text{\rm d}x\text{\rm d}t\\
+\int_{Q_{\Omega}}(\text{\rm div}\theta)(\varphi_{t}^{-1}(x))f(t,x)\>\text{\rm d}x\text{\rm d}t+\int_{Q_{\Omega}}\Big[\nabla\varphi_{t}(\varphi_{t}^{-1}(x))\Big]^{T}\nabla f(t,x)\cdot\theta(\varphi_{t}^{-1}(x))\>\text{\rm d}x\text{\rm d}t,

which is the announced formula
Eq.

3.19
.

To infer the surface expression
Eq.

3.20
, we first express
Eq.

3.19
as nested integrals over
(0,T)
and
\varphi_{t}(\Omega)
and we use a change of variables in terms of the mapping
\varphi_{t}
in the spatial integral to obtain

(3.21)

J^{\prime}(\Omega)(\theta)=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\lvert\det\nabla\varphi_{t}(x)\lvert\text{\rm tr}\left(\Big[\nabla^{2}\varphi_{t}(x)\theta(x)\Big]\Big[\nabla\varphi_{t}^{-1}(\varphi_{t}(x))\Big]\right)f(t,\varphi_{t}(x))\>\text{\rm d}x\text{\rm d}t\\
+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}|\det\nabla\varphi_{t}(x)|(\text{\rm div}\theta)(x)f(t,\varphi_{t}(x))\>\text{\rm d}x\text{\rm d}t+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}|\det\nabla\varphi_{t}(x)|\Big[\nabla\varphi_{t}(x)\Big]^{T}\nabla f(t,\varphi_{t}(x))\cdot\theta(x)\>\text{\rm d}x\text{\rm d}t.

We now proceed according to the strategy summarized in e.g.
[
2
]
: integrating by parts in all the terms of the above expression containing derivatives of
\theta
yields an expression of the form:

J^{\prime}(\Omega)(\theta)=\int_{\Omega}M_{\Omega}\cdot\theta\>\text{\rm d}x+\int_{\Gamma}v_{\Omega}(\theta\cdot n)\>\text{\rm d}s+\int_{\Gamma}t_{\Omega}\cdot\theta_{\Gamma}\>\text{\rm d}s,

where
M_{\Omega}:D\to{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
,
v_{\Omega}:\Gamma\to{\mathbb{R}}
and
t_{\Omega}:\Gamma\to{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
depend on the function
f
and on
\Omega
.
If we expect that
J^{\prime}(\Omega)(\theta)
admits an expression with the desirable structure
Eq.

3.5
, the vector fields
M_{\Omega}
and
t_{\Omega}
must vanish.
That this indeed holds is eventually verified by an elementary, albeit tedious calculation.

In the present case, an integration by parts in the second integral on the right-hand side of
Eq.

3.21
(which is the only one involving derivatives of
\theta
)
produces the following expression:

J^{\prime}(\Omega)(\theta)=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Gamma}\lvert\det\nabla\varphi_{t}(x)\lvert f(t,\varphi_{t}(x))\>\theta(x)\cdot n(x)\>\text{\rm d}s(x)\text{\rm d}t+r(\theta),

where
r(\theta)
is a collection of integrals posed on the domain
\Omega
involving only
\theta
(and not its derivatives).
A simple, albeit tedious calculation which is omitted for brevity reveals that
r(\theta)
actually vanishes, which allows to conclude.
∎

4.
Calculation of the shape derivative in the magneto-quasi-static context

In this section, we turn to the calculation of the shape derivative of a “physical” objective function
J(\Omega)

which depends on the shape
\Omega
via the potential
u_{\Omega}
,
solution to the magneto-quasi-static problem
Eq.

2.22
where
\Omega
represents the phase occupied by ferromagnetic material.
After sketching some mathematical preliminaries about this evolution problem in
Section

4.1
and a few technical features about its solution in
Section

4.2
,
we proceed to the calculation of the shape derivative
J^{\prime}(\Omega)(\theta)
in
Section

4.3
, under the simplifying assumption that the reluctivity
\nu
of the ferromagnetic material is constant, in which case
Eq.

2.22
becomes linear. We eventually provide the formal extension of this result in the general situation of a non linear material in
Section

4.4
.

4.1.
Mathematical preliminaries about the magneto quasi-static evolution problem

The analysis of the evolution problem
Eq.

2.22
brings into play suitable functional spaces for functions depending on the time and space variables. For the sake of convenience,
a few basic facts about these are recalled in
Section

B.1
; we refer to e.g. §1 in Chap. XVIII of
[
21
]
, or Chap. 23 in
[
74
]
for more exhaustive presentations.

Let us consider the functional space
L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}_{0}(D))
, whose dual
L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}_{0}(D))^{*}
can be identified with
L^{2}(0,T;H^{-1}(D))
, see
Eq.

B.2
.
We also introduce

W:=\left\{u\in L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}_{0}(D))\text{ s.t. }\sigma_{\Omega(t)}\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}\in L^{2}(0,T;H^{-1}(D))\right\},

which is a Hilbert space when equipped with the norm

||u||_{W}^{2}:=\int_{0}^{T}\lvert\lvert u(t,\cdot)\lvert\lvert^{2}_{H^{1}_{0}(D)}\>\text{\rm d}t+\int_{0}^{T}\left\lvert\left\lvert\sigma_{\Omega(t)}\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}(t,\cdot)\right\lvert\right\lvert^{2}_{H^{-1}(D)}\>\text{\rm d}t.

Intuitively,
W
is a variant of the more classical space
W(0,T;H^{1}_{0}(D),L^{2}(D))
defined in
Eq.

B.3
,
which is adapted to handle the fact that the coefficient
\sigma_{\Omega(t)}
vanishes outside the region
D_{\text{\rm mag}}(t)\subset D
, see
Eq.

2.12
.
The properties of
W(0,T;H^{1}_{0}(D),L^{2}(D))
summarized in
Lemma

B.1
are also verified by
W
, as the proof of this result can be straightforwardly adapted to the present case.
In particular, any element
u\in W
induces a continuous mapping
[0,T]\ni t\mapsto u(t,\cdot)\in L^{2}(D_{\text{\rm mag}})
, which allows to introduce the closed subspace
W_{\text{\rm per}}
of
W
defined by:

(4.1)

W_{\text{\rm per}}:=\Big\{u\in W\text{ s.t. }u(t=0,\cdot)=u(t=T,\cdot)\text{ on }D_{\text{\rm mag}}\Big\}.

Remark 4.1
.

•

As the conductivity
\sigma_{\Omega(t)}
equals
\sigma_{m}>0
on
D_{\text{\rm mag}}(t)
and vanishes outside
D_{\text{\rm mag}}(t)
,
the time derivative
\frac{\partial}{\partial t}(u(t,\varphi_{t}(\cdot)))
of a function
u\in W
belongs to
L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}(D_{\text{\rm mag}})^{*})
.

•

The time periodicity condition satisfied by functions
u\in W_{\text{\rm per}}
only holds in
D_{\text{\rm mag}}
, as an application of
Lemma

B.1
(ii).

Using this language, the magneto-quasi-static problem
Eq.

2.22
can be expressed in variational form:

(4.2)

\text{Search for }u_{\Omega}\in W_{\text{\rm per}},\text{ s.t. }\forall w\in L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}_{0}(D)),\\
\int_{Q}\sigma_{\Omega(t)}\left(\frac{\partial u_{\Omega}}{\partial t}+v\cdot\nabla u_{\Omega}\right)w\>\text{\rm d}x\text{\rm d}t+\int_{Q}\nu_{\Omega(t)}(x,\lvert\nabla u_{\Omega}\lvert)\nabla u_{\Omega}\cdot\nabla w\>\text{\rm d}x\text{\rm d}t=\int_{Q}fw\>\text{\rm d}x\text{\rm d}t,

or equivalently:

(4.3)

\text{Search for }u_{\Omega}\in W_{\text{\rm per}},\text{ s.t. for a.e. }t\in(0,T),\\
\forall w\in H^{1}_{0}(D),\quad\int_{D}\sigma_{\Omega(t)}\left(\frac{\partial u_{\Omega}}{\partial t}+v\cdot\nabla u_{\Omega}\right)w\>\text{\rm d}x+\int_{D}\nu_{\Omega(t)}(x,\lvert\nabla u_{\Omega}\lvert)\nabla u_{\Omega}\cdot\nabla w\>\text{\rm d}x=\int_{D}fw\>\text{\rm d}x.

The following theorem deals with the well-posedness of the variational problem
Eq.

4.2
.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
\varphi_{t}
be a time-dependent diffeomorphism of
\overline{D}
satisfying
Eqs.

2.15
,
2.16
and
2.17
.
For any source
f\in L^{2}(0,T;H^{-1}(D))
, the evolution problem
Eq.

4.2
has a unique solution
u_{\Omega}\in W_{\text{\rm per}}
, which has a Lipschitz dependence on
f
:
there exists a constant
C>0
such that the solutions
u_{1}
,
u_{2}\in W_{\text{\rm per}}
associated to the respective right-hand sides
f_{1}
,
f_{2}\in L^{2}(0,T;H^{-1}(D))
satisfy:

\lvert\lvert u_{1}-u_{2}\lvert\lvert_{W}\leq C\lvert\lvert f_{1}-f_{2}\lvert\lvert_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{-1}(D))}.

The proof of this result is sketched in
Appendix

B
.
Let us point out that the analysis of a similar statement about a linear version of
Eq.

4.2
was presented recently in
[
28
]
.

4.2.
A few properties of the solution to the magneto-quasi-static problem

This section is devoted to a few useful technical facts about functions in the space
W
, and more precisely about the solution
u_{\Omega}
to
Eq.

4.2
.
Throughout,
\varphi_{t}
is a smooth time-dependent diffeomorphism of
\overline{D}
satisfying
Eqs.

2.15
,
2.16
and
2.17

and the coefficients
\sigma_{\Omega(t)}
and
\nu_{\Omega(t)}
are given by
Eq.

2.12
.

The first remark is an avatar of the so-called Reynolds (or transport) theorem:

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
u,p\in W
; the following identity holds in the sense of distributions on
(0,T)
:

\frac{\text{\rm d}}{\text{\rm d}t}\left(\int_{D}\sigma_{\Omega(t)}(x)u(t,x)p(t,x)\>\text{\rm d}x\right)=\int_{D}\sigma_{\Omega(t)}(x)\frac{\text{\rm d}u}{\text{\rm d}t}(t,x)p(t,x)\>\text{\rm d}x\\
+\int_{D}\sigma_{\Omega(t)}(x)u(t,x)\frac{\text{\rm d}p}{\text{\rm d}t}(t,x)\>\text{\rm d}x+\int_{Q}\sigma_{\Omega(t)}\text{\rm div}v(t,x)u(t,x)p(t,x)\>\text{\rm d}x\text{\rm d}t,

where we recall the notation
\frac{\text{\rm d}u}{\text{\rm d}t}
,
\frac{\text{\rm d}p}{\text{\rm d}t}
in
Eq.

2.21
for the total time derivatives of
u
and
p
.

Proof.

By
Lemma

B.1
(i), it is enough to prove that the desired identity holds at every
t\in(0,T)
when
u
and
p
belong to the space
{\mathcal{C}}^{\infty}([0,T],H^{1}_{0}(D))
.
To achieve this, we first use a change of variables based on the mapping
\varphi_{t}
:

\begin{array}[]{>{\displaystyle}cc>{\displaystyle}l}\int_{D}\sigma_{\Omega(t)}(x)u(t,x)p(t,x)\>\text{\rm d}x&=&\int_{D}\lvert\det\nabla\varphi_{t}(x)\lvert\sigma_{\Omega(t)}(\varphi_{t}(x))u(t,\varphi_{t}(x))p(t,\varphi_{t}(x))\>\text{\rm d}x\\[10.00002pt]
&=&\int_{D}\lvert\det\nabla\varphi_{t}(x)\lvert\sigma_{\Omega}(x)\>u(t,\varphi_{t}(x))p(t,\varphi_{t}(x))\>\text{\rm d}x,\end{array}

where the second line follows from
Eq.

2.18
.
Taking derivatives with respect to time, we obtain:

\frac{\text{\rm d}}{\text{\rm d}t}\left(\int_{D}\sigma_{\Omega(t)}(x)u(t,x)p(t,x)\>\text{\rm d}x\right)=\int_{D}|\det\nabla\varphi_{t}(x)|\sigma_{\Omega}(x)\frac{\text{\rm d}u}{\text{\rm d}t}(t,\varphi_{t}(x))p(t,\varphi_{t}(x))\>\text{\rm d}x\\
+\int_{D}|\det\nabla\varphi_{t}(x)|\sigma_{\Omega}(x)u(t,\varphi_{t}(x))\frac{\text{\rm d}p}{\text{\rm d}t}(t,\varphi_{t}(x))\>\text{\rm d}x\\
+\int_{D}|\det\nabla\varphi_{t}(x)|\text{\rm tr}\left(\nabla\varphi_{t}(x)^{-1}\nabla\left(\frac{\partial\varphi_{t}}{\partial t}\right)(x)\right)\sigma_{\Omega}(x)u(t,\varphi_{t}(x))p(t,\varphi_{t}(x))\>\text{\rm d}x.

Here, we have used the usual formula for the derivative of the determinant mapping.
Another change of variables now yields:

\frac{\text{\rm d}}{\text{\rm d}t}\left(\int_{D}\sigma_{\Omega(t)}(x)u(t,x)p(t,x)\>\text{\rm d}x\right)=\int_{D}\sigma_{\Omega(t)}(x)\frac{\text{\rm d}u}{\text{\rm d}t}(t,x)p(t,x)\>\text{\rm d}x+\int_{D}\sigma_{\Omega(t)}(x)u(t,x)\frac{\text{\rm d}p}{\text{\rm d}t}(t,x)\>\text{\rm d}x\\
+\int_{D}\text{\rm tr}\left(\nabla\varphi_{t}(\varphi_{t}^{-1}(x))^{-1}\nabla\left(\frac{\partial\varphi_{t}}{\partial t}\right)(\varphi_{t}^{-1}(x))\right)\sigma_{\Omega(t)}(x)u(t,x)p(t,x)\>\text{\rm d}x.

The desired identity eventually results from the following elementary calculation and the definition
Eq.

2.14
of the velocity field
v(t,x)
:

\begin{array}[]{>{\displaystyle}cc>{\displaystyle}l}\nabla\left(\frac{\partial\varphi_{t}}{\partial t}\right)(\varphi_{t}^{-1}(x))\nabla\varphi_{t}(\varphi_{t}^{-1}(x))^{-1}&=&\nabla\left(\frac{\partial\varphi_{t}}{\partial t}\right)(\varphi_{t}^{-1}(x))\nabla(\varphi_{t}^{-1}(x))\\[7.5pt]
&=&\nabla\left(\frac{\partial\varphi_{t}}{\partial t}(\varphi_{t}^{-1}(x))\right)\\[7.5pt]
&=&\nabla v(t,x).\end{array}

∎

Corollary 4.1
.

The following relation holds, for all
u,p\in W
:

\int_{Q}\sigma_{\Omega(t)}\frac{\text{\rm d}u}{\text{\rm d}t}p\>\text{\rm d}x\text{\rm d}t=\int_{D}\left(\sigma_{\Omega(T)}u(T,x)p(T,x)-\sigma_{\Omega(0)}u(0,x)p(0,x)\right)\>\text{\rm d}x\\
-\int_{Q}\sigma_{\Omega(t)}\frac{\text{\rm d}p}{\text{\rm d}t}u\>\text{\rm d}x\text{\rm d}t-\int_{Q}\sigma_{\Omega(t)}(\text{\rm div}v)up\>\text{\rm d}x\text{\rm d}t.

Proof.

For a.e.
t\in(0,T)
,
Lemma

4.1
states that:

\int_{D}\sigma_{\Omega(t)}(x)\frac{\text{\rm d}u}{\text{\rm d}t}(t,x)p(t,x)\>\text{\rm d}x=\frac{\text{\rm d}}{\text{\rm d}t}\left(\int_{D}\sigma_{\Omega(t)}(x)u(t,x)p(t,x)\>\text{\rm d}x\right)\\
-\int_{D}\sigma_{\Omega(t)}(x)u(t,x)\frac{\text{\rm d}p}{\text{\rm d}t}(t,x)\>\text{\rm d}x-\int_{D}\sigma_{\Omega(t)}\text{\rm div}v(t,x)u(t,x)p(t,x)\>\text{\rm d}x.

In particular, the mapping
t\mapsto\int_{D}\sigma_{\Omega(t)}u(t,x)p(t,x)\>\text{\rm d}x
is absolutely continuous on
(0,T)
.
The result follows immediately from integration of this identity over the time period
(0,T)
.
∎

The variational problem
Eq.

4.2
implicitly encompasses jump conditions for
u_{\Omega}(t,\cdot)
at the interface
\Gamma(t)

where the conductivity and the reluctivity are discontinuous; these are the subject of the next lemma:

Lemma 4.2
.

Let the right-hand side
f
belong to the space
L^{2}(0,T,L^{2}(D))
; the corresponding solution
u_{\Omega}(t,\cdot)
to
Eq.

4.2
is continuous across
\Gamma(t)
:

(4.4)

\forall t\in(0,T),\>\>x\in\Gamma,\quad u_{\Omega}^{-}(t,\varphi_{t}(x))=u_{\Omega}^{+}(t,\varphi_{t}(x)),

and so are its normal flux and tangential gradient:

(4.5)

\forall t\in(0,T),\>\>x\in\Gamma,\quad\Big(\nu_{\Omega}\nabla u_{\Omega}(t,\varphi_{t}(x))\Big)^{-}\cdot n_{\Omega(t)}(\varphi_{t}(x))=\Big(\nu_{\Omega}\nabla u_{\Omega}(t,\varphi_{t}(x))\Big)^{+}\cdot n_{\Omega(t)}(\varphi_{t}(x)),

(4.6)

\forall t\in(0,T),\>\>x\in\Gamma,\quad\nabla_{\Gamma(t)}u_{\Omega}^{-}(t,\varphi_{t}(x))=\nabla_{\Gamma(t)}u_{\Omega}^{+}(t,\varphi_{t}(x)).

Finally, it holds:

(4.7)

\forall t\in(0,T),\>\>x\in\Gamma,\quad\frac{\text{\rm d}u_{\Omega}^{-}}{\text{\rm d}t}(t,\varphi_{t}(x))=\frac{\text{\rm d}u_{\Omega}^{+}}{\text{\rm d}t}(t,\varphi_{t}(x)).

Proof.

Since
u_{\Omega}\in L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}_{0}(D))
, the function
u_{\Omega}(t,\cdot)
belongs to
H^{1}_{0}(D)
for a.e.
t\in(0,T)
, and so
Eq.

4.4
holds true.

Let now
t\in(0,T)
be given; by considering smooth test functions
w\in{\mathcal{C}}^{\infty}_{c}(D)
with compact support inside
\Omega(t_{0})
or
D\setminus\overline{\Omega(t_{0})}
in
Eq.

4.3
, then integrating by parts, we see that:

(4.8)

\sigma_{\Omega(t)}\frac{\text{\rm d}u_{\Omega}}{\text{\rm d}t}(t,x)-\text{\rm div}\left(\nu_{\Omega(t)}(x,\lvert\nabla u_{\Omega}(t,x)\lvert)\nabla u_{\Omega}(t,x)\right)=f(t,x)

in the sense of distributions in
\Omega(t)
or
D\setminus\overline{\Omega(t)}
.
Repeating the calculation with arbitrary smooth test functions
w\in{\mathcal{C}}^{\infty}_{c}(\overline{D})
and using
Eq.

4.8
, we now obtain:

\int_{\Gamma(t)}\left(\nu_{\Omega(t)}\frac{\partial u_{\Omega}}{\partial n}\right)^{-}w\>\text{\rm d}s-\int_{\Gamma(t)}\left(\nu_{\Omega(t)}\frac{\partial u_{\Omega}}{\partial n}\right)^{+}w\>\text{\rm d}s=0,

and
Eq.

4.5
follows immediately.

Eventually,
Eq.

4.6
is obtained by taking the tangential gradient in
Eq.

4.4
, and taking time derivatives in
Eq.

4.4
yields the last jump relation
Eq.

4.7
.
∎

Remark 4.2
.

In the following, we denote by
\nabla_{\Gamma(t)}u_{\Omega}(t,\varphi_{t}(x))
and
\nu_{\Omega(t)}\nabla u_{\Omega}(t,\varphi_{t}(x))\cdot n_{\Omega(t)}(\varphi_{t}(x))
the common values of the one-sided limits of the tangential gradient and normal flux of

through
\Gamma(t)
.

4.3.
Calculation of the shape derivative of a physical objective function
J(\Omega)
in the linear magneto-quasi-static setting

In this section, we detail the calculation of the shape derivative of the physical objective function
J(\Omega)
in
Eq.

3.2
involving the magneto-quasi-static potential
u_{\Omega}
,
with a particular emphasis on the arguments which are quite specific to the present physical context.
To keep technicality at a minimum, our analysis is conducted in the linear case where the reluctivity
\hat{\nu}
of the ferromagnetic material is a constant function
\hat{\nu}\equiv\nu_{f}
, see
Remark

2.3
.
The needed adaptations to the general case of a non constant function
\hat{\nu}
are elementary, albeit tedious; they are discussed in the next
Section

4.4
.
Throughout the remainder of this section, we rely on the following shorthand in notation: we denote by
\Omega_{1}:=\Omega
the optimized phase of the rotor filled by ferromagnetic material,
and by
\Omega_{2}:=D\setminus\overline{\Omega}
its complement in
D
.
Accordingly, we denote by
\nu_{1}
,
\sigma_{1}
the reluctivity and conductivity inside
\Omega_{1}
, and by
\nu_{2}
,
\sigma_{2}
the piecewise constant functions matching the repartition
Eq.

2.12
.

The evolution problem
Eq.

2.22
then rewrites:

(4.9)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}\sigma_{\Omega(t)}\frac{\text{\rm d}u_{\Omega}}{\text{\rm d}t}-\text{\rm div}\left(\nu_{\Omega(t)}\nabla u_{\Omega}\right)=f&\text{in }Q,\\
u_{\Omega}(t,x)=0&\text{for }t\in(0,T),\>x\in\partial D,\\
u_{\Omega}(0,x)=u_{\Omega}(T,x)&\text{for }x\in D_{\text{mag}},\end{array}\right.

where

\sigma_{\Omega(t)}(x)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}\sigma_{1}&\text{if } x\in\Omega(t),\\
\sigma_{2}&\text{otherwise},\end{array}\right.\text{ and }\nu_{\Omega(t)}(x)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}\nu_{1}&\text{if } x\in\Omega(t),\\
\nu_{2}&\text{otherwise}.\end{array}\right.

Proposition 4.1
.

Let the source term
f:Q\to{\mathbb{R}}
be smooth; the functional
J(\Omega)
in
Eq.

3.2
is shape differentiable at any bounded and Lipschitz shape
\Omega\subset D_{\text{\rm rot}}
, and its shape derivative reads, under volume form:

(4.10)

\forall\theta\in\Theta_{\text{\rm ad}},\quad J^{\prime}(\Omega)(\theta)=\int_{Q}m^{\prime}(0)(\theta)j(u_{\Omega})\>\text{\rm d}x\text{\rm d}t\\
+\int_{Q}\sigma_{\Omega(t)}\left(m^{\prime}(0)(\theta)\frac{\text{\rm d}u_{\Omega}}{\text{\rm d}t}-\Big[F_{xx}^{\prime}(0)(\theta)\Big]v\cdot\nabla u_{\Omega}+v_{1}(\theta)\cdot\nabla u_{\Omega}+b^{\prime}(0)(\theta)\cdot\nabla u_{\Omega}\right)\>p_{\Omega}\>\text{\rm d}x\text{\rm d}t\\
+\int_{Q}\nu_{\Omega(t)}\Big[A^{\prime}(0)(\theta)\Big]\nabla u_{\Omega}\cdot\nabla p_{\Omega}\>\text{\rm d}x\text{\rm d}t-\int_{Q}\Big(m^{\prime}(0)(\theta)f+f_{1}(\theta)\Big)p_{\Omega}\>\text{\rm d}x\text{\rm d}t,

where the quantities
A^{\prime}(0)(\theta)
,
m^{\prime}(0)(\theta)
,
F_{xx}^{\prime}(0)(\theta)
and
b^{\prime}(0)(\theta)
are respectively defined by
Eqs.

3.17
,
3.13
,
3.11
and
3.9
,
and
f_{1}(\theta)
and
v_{1}(\theta)
are the derivatives of the mappings
\theta\mapsto f\circ\Theta
and
\theta\mapsto v\circ\Theta
, see
Eqs.

3.14
and
3.15
.

The adjoint state
p_{\Omega}
is the unique solution in
W_{\text{\rm per}}
to the evolution problem:

(4.11)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}-\sigma_{\Omega(t)}\frac{\text{\rm d}p_{\Omega}}{\text{\rm d}t}-\text{\rm div}\left(\nu_{\Omega(t)}\nabla p_{\Omega}\right)-\sigma_{\Omega(t)}(\text{\rm div}v)p_{\Omega}=-j^{\prime}(u_{\Omega})&\text{in }Q,\\
p_{\Omega}(t,x)=0&\text{for }t\in(0,T),\>x\in\partial D,\\
p_{\Omega}(0,x)=p_{\Omega}(T,x)&\text{for }x\in D.\end{array}\right.

It is equivalently characterized as the solution to the following variational problem:

(4.12)

\text{Search for }p_{\Omega}\in W_{\text{\rm per}}\text{ s.t. }\forall w\in L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}_{0}(D)),\\
\int_{Q}\Big(-\sigma_{\Omega(t)}\frac{\text{\rm d}p_{\Omega}}{\text{\rm d}t}w+\nu_{\Omega(t)}\nabla p_{\Omega}\cdot\nabla w-\sigma_{\Omega(t)}(\text{\rm div}v)p_{\Omega}w\Big)\>\text{\rm d}x\text{\rm d}t=-\int_{Q}j^{\prime}(u_{\Omega})w\>\text{\rm d}x\text{\rm d}t.

Assuming
\Omega
to be smooth, the shape derivative
J^{\prime}(\Omega)(\theta)
admits the surface form:

(4.13)

\forall\theta\in\Theta_{\text{\rm ad}},\quad J^{\prime}(\Omega)(\theta)=\int_{\Gamma}v_{\Omega}\>(\theta\cdot n)\>\text{\rm d}s,\text{ where }\\
v_{\Omega}(x):=-(\sigma_{2}-\sigma_{1})\int_{0}^{T}\lvert\det\nabla\varphi_{t}(x)\lvert\frac{\text{\rm d}u_{\Omega}}{\text{\rm d}t}(t,\varphi_{t}(x))p_{\Omega}(t,\varphi_{t}(x))\>\text{\rm d}t\\
-(\nu_{2}-\nu_{1})\int_{0}^{T}\lvert\det\nabla\varphi_{t}(x)\lvert\nabla_{\Gamma(t)}u_{\Omega}(t,\varphi_{t}(x))\cdot\nabla_{\Gamma(t)}p_{\Omega}(t,\varphi_{t}(x))\>\text{\rm d}t\\
+\left(\frac{1}{\nu_{2}}-\frac{1}{\nu_{1}}\right)\int_{0}^{T}\lvert\det\nabla\varphi_{t}(x)\lvert\Big(\nu_{\Omega(t)}\nabla u_{\Omega}(t,\varphi_{t}(x))\cdot n_{\Omega(t)}(\varphi_{t}(x))\Big)\Big(\nu_{\Omega(t)}\nabla p_{\Omega}(t,\varphi_{t}(x))\cdot n_{\Omega(t)}(\varphi_{t}(x))\Big)\>\text{\rm d}t.

Remark 4.3
.

•

The well-posedness of the (linear) adjoint problem
Eq.

4.11
follows from similar, although simpler arguments to those involved in the proof of
Theorem

4.1
about the (non linear) state problem
Eq.

2.22
.

•

Like
u_{\Omega}(t,\cdot)
, the adjoint state
p_{\Omega}(t,\cdot)
satisfies jump relations at the interface
\Gamma(t)
; a similar analysis to that in the proof of
Lemma

4.2
indeed yields:

\forall t\in(0,T),\>\>x\in\Gamma,\quad p_{\Omega}^{-}(t,\varphi_{t}(x))=p_{\Omega}^{+}(t,\varphi_{t}(x)),

and

\forall t\in(0,T),\>\>x\in\Gamma,\quad(\nu_{\Omega}\nabla p_{\Omega}(t,\varphi_{t}(x)))^{-}\cdot n_{\Omega(t)}(\varphi_{t}(x))=(\nu_{\Omega}\nabla p_{\Omega}(t,\varphi_{t}(x)))^{+}\cdot n_{\Omega(t)}(\varphi_{t}(x)).

Remark 4.4
.

•

The smoothness assumption about the source
f
in the statement of
Proposition

4.1
can be weakened in various ways. For instance, the statement holds mutatis mutandis if
f
only belongs to
L^{2}(0,T;H^{-1}(D))
, but for each time
t\in(0,T)
,

f(t,\cdot)
has support in the region
D_{\text{\rm stat}}
where deformations
\theta\in\Theta_{\text{\rm ad}}
vanish.

•

As we have evoked in
Remark

2.3
, in realistic applications,
the right-hand side
f
contains a contribution of the form
\text{\rm div}(L\mathds{1}_{D_{\text{\rm mag}}(t)})
where the smooth vector field
L(t,x)
stands for the
90^{\circ}
clockwise rotate
M^{\perp}
of the magnetization
M
.
In such case, the last integral in the right-hand side of
Eq.

4.10
should be supplemented with:

(4.14)

-\int_{Q_{D_{\text{\rm mag}}}}\left(m^{\prime}(0)(\theta)L+L_{1}(\theta)-\Big[F_{xx}^{\prime}(0)(\theta)\Big]L\right)\cdot\nabla p_{\Omega}\>\text{\rm d}x\text{\rm d}t,

where
L_{1}(\theta)
is the first-order term in the asymptotic expansion of the vector field
L\circ\Theta
at
\theta=0
, see
Eq.

3.15
.

Proof of
Proposition

4.1
.

However technically tedious, the proof follows a quite classical trail, as
exposed for instance in
[
2
,
54
,
36
]
. We proceed in five steps.

Step 1: We prove that the mapping
\Omega\mapsto u_{\Omega}
has a “Lagrangian derivative”.

More precisely, we prove that the transported potential
\theta\mapsto\overline{u_{\Omega}}(\theta):=u_{\Omega_{\theta}}\circ\Theta
is Fréchet differentiable from a neighborhood of
0
in
\Theta_{\text{\rm ad}}
into
W_{\text{\rm per}}
.
This starts from the variational problem satisfied by the magneto-quasi-static field
u_{\Omega_{\theta}}\in W_{\text{\rm per}}
:

(4.15)

\forall w\in L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}_{0}(D)),\quad\int_{Q}\sigma_{\varphi_{t}(\Omega_{\theta})}(x)\frac{\text{\rm d}u_{\Omega_{\theta}}}{\text{\rm d}t}w\>\text{\rm d}x\text{\rm d}t+\int_{Q}\nu_{\varphi_{t}(\Omega_{\theta})}(x)\nabla u_{\Omega_{\theta}}\cdot\nabla w\>\text{\rm d}x\text{\rm d}t=\int_{Q}fw\>\text{\rm d}x\text{\rm d}t.

A change of variables via
\Theta
in the above integrals yields:

(4.16)

\int_{Q}(\sigma_{\varphi_{t}(\Omega_{\theta})}\circ\Theta_{x})m(\theta)\left(\left(\frac{\partial u_{\Omega_{\theta}}}{\partial t}\right)\circ\Theta+(v\circ\Theta)\cdot(\nabla u_{\Omega_{\theta}}\circ\Theta)\right)\>(w\circ\Theta)\>\text{\rm d}x\text{\rm d}t\\
+\int_{Q}(\nu_{\varphi_{t}(\Omega_{\theta})}\circ\Theta_{x})m(\theta)\Big(\nabla u_{\Omega_{\theta}}\circ\Theta\Big)\cdot\Big(\nabla w\circ\Theta\Big)\>\text{\rm d}x\text{\rm d}t=\int_{Q}m(\theta)(f\circ\Theta)(w\circ\Theta)\>\text{\rm d}x\text{\rm d}t,

where we recall the expression
Eq.

3.12
of
m(\theta)
.
Now, from the definitions
Eq.

2.18
of
\sigma_{\Omega}
and
\nu_{\Omega}
, it holds:

\sigma_{\varphi_{t}(\Omega_{\theta})}\circ\Theta_{x}=\sigma_{\Omega(t)},\text{ and }\nu_{\varphi_{t}(\Omega_{\theta})}\circ\Theta_{x}=\nu_{\Omega(t)},

so that, by using the relations
Eq.

3.8
and taking test functions of the form
w\circ\Theta^{-1}
,
w\in L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}_{0}(D))
,
we see that the transported function
\overline{u_{\Omega}}(\theta)
satisfies the variational problem:

(4.17)

\forall w\in L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}_{0}(D)),\quad\int_{Q}\sigma_{\Omega(t)}m(\theta)\left(\frac{\partial\overline{u_{\Omega}}(\theta)}{\partial t}+b(\theta)\cdot\nabla\overline{u_{\Omega}}(\theta)+\Big[F_{xx}^{-1}(\theta)\Big](v\circ\Theta)\cdot\nabla\overline{u_{\Omega}}(\theta)\right)\>w\>\text{\rm d}x\text{\rm d}t\\
+\int_{Q}\nu_{\Omega(t)}\Big[A(\theta)\Big]\nabla\overline{u_{\Omega}}(\theta)\cdot\nabla w\>\text{\rm d}x\text{\rm d}t=\int_{Q}m(\theta)(f\circ\Theta)w\>\text{\rm d}x\text{\rm d}t,

where
A(\theta)
and
b(\theta)
are respectively defined by
Eq.

3.16
and
Eq.

3.7
.

This problem can be reformulated as a more abstract equation for
\overline{u_{\Omega}}(\theta)
:

\text{Search for }u\in W_{\text{\rm per}}\text{ s.t. }{\mathcal{F}}(\theta,u)=0,

where we have defined the mapping
{\mathcal{F}}:\Theta_{\text{\rm ad}}\times W_{\text{\rm per}}\mapsto L^{2}(0,T;H^{-1}(D))
by:

(4.18)

{\mathcal{F}}(\theta,u)=\left[\begin{array}[]{>{\displaystyle}r}w\mapsto\int_{Q}\sigma_{\Omega(t)}m(\theta)\left(\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}+b(\theta)\cdot\nabla u+\Big[F_{xx}^{-1}(\theta)\Big](v\circ\Theta)\cdot\nabla u\right)\>w\>\text{\rm d}x\text{\rm d}t\\
+\int_{Q}\nu_{\Omega(t)}\Big[A(\theta)\Big]\nabla u\cdot\nabla w\>\text{\rm d}x\text{\rm d}t-\int_{Q}m(\theta)(f\circ\Theta)w\>\text{\rm d}x\text{\rm d}t\end{array}\right].

An elementary verification shows that this equation fulfills the assumptions of the implicit function theorem at the point
(0,u_{\Omega})
, see e.g.
[
45
]
Chap. I, Th. 5.9.
Hence, the mapping
\theta\mapsto\overline{u_{\Omega}}(\theta)
is Fréchet differentiable at
\theta=0
. Its derivative, denoted by
\mathring{u_{\Omega}}(\theta)
, is the so-called Lagrangian derivative of
u_{\Omega}
.

Step 2: We characterize the Lagrangian derivative
\mathring{u_{\Omega}}(\theta)\in W_{\text{\rm per}}
.

Relying on the information that the mapping
\theta\mapsto\overline{u_{\Omega}}(\theta)
is differentiable at
\theta=0
, taking derivatives in
Eq.

4.17
shows that
the function
\mathring{u_{\Omega}}(\theta)\in W_{\text{\rm per}}
satisfies, for all
w\in L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}_{0}(D))
:

(4.19)

\int_{Q}\sigma_{\Omega(t)}\frac{\text{\rm d}\mathring{u_{\Omega}}(\theta)}{\text{\rm d}t}\>w\>\text{\rm d}x\text{\rm d}t+\int_{Q}\nu_{\Omega(t)}\nabla\mathring{u_{\Omega}}(\theta)\cdot\nabla w\>\text{\rm d}x\text{\rm d}t=-\int_{Q}\sigma_{\Omega(t)}m^{\prime}(0)(\theta)\frac{\text{\rm d}u_{\Omega}}{\text{\rm d}t}\>w\>\text{\rm d}x\text{\rm d}t\\
+\int_{Q}\sigma_{\Omega(t)}\Big[F_{xx}^{\prime}(0)(\theta)\Big]v\cdot\nabla u_{\Omega}w\>\text{\rm d}x\text{\rm d}t-\int_{Q}\sigma_{\Omega(t)}v_{1}(\theta)\cdot\nabla u_{\Omega}w\>\text{\rm d}x\text{\rm d}t-\int_{Q}\sigma_{\Omega(t)}b^{\prime}(0)(\theta)\cdot\nabla u_{\Omega}w\>\text{\rm d}x\text{\rm d}t\\
-\int_{Q}\nu_{\Omega(t)}\Big[A^{\prime}(0)(\theta)\Big]\nabla u_{\Omega}\cdot\nabla w\>\text{\rm d}x\text{\rm d}t+\int_{Q}\Big(m^{\prime}(0)(\theta)f+f_{1}(\theta)\Big)w\>\text{\rm d}x\text{\rm d}t.

This is a well-posed evolution problem for
\mathring{u_{\Omega}}(\theta)\in W_{\text{\rm per}}
.

Step 3: We calculate the derivative of the functional
J(\Omega)
in
Eq.

3.2
.

A change of variables based on the mapping
\Theta
in the definition of
J(\Omega_{\theta})
yields the following expression:

J(\Omega_{\theta})=\int_{Q}m(\theta)j(\overline{u_{\Omega}}(\theta))\>\text{\rm d}x\text{\rm d}t,

which naturally brings into play the transported function
\overline{u_{\Omega}}(\theta)
.
As we have proved the differentiability of the latter with respect to the deformation
\theta
, we now obtain, by taking derivatives in the above expression:

(4.20)

J^{\prime}(\Omega)(\theta)=\int_{Q}\Big(m^{\prime}(0)(\theta)j(u_{\Omega})+j^{\prime}(u_{\Omega})\mathring{u_{\Omega}}(\theta)\Big)\>\text{\rm d}x\text{\rm d}t.

Step 4: We apply the adjoint method to derive the volume form of
J^{\prime}(\Omega)(\theta)
.

The expression
Eq.

4.20
is not suitable for practical calculations, as it depends in a complicated way on
\theta
, via the Lagrangian derivative
\mathring{u_{\Omega}}(\theta)
,
which is only known implicitly, as the solution to
Eq.

4.19
. In particular,
Eq.

4.20
does not allow for the simple identification of a descent direction for
J(\Omega)
.

To overcome this classical issue, we rely on the adjoint method from optimal control theory, see e.g.
[
50
]
or
[
60
]
for an intuitive presentation.
We introduce the adjoint state
p_{\Omega}\in W_{\text{\rm per}}
as the solution to the variational problem
Eq.

4.12
, and take
w=\mathring{u_{\Omega}}(\theta)
as test function in there to obtain:

-\int_{Q}j^{\prime}(u_{\Omega})\mathring{u_{\Omega}}(\theta)\>\text{\rm d}x\text{\rm d}t=\int_{Q}\left(-\sigma_{\Omega(t)}\frac{\text{\rm d}p_{\Omega}}{\text{\rm d}t}\mathring{u_{\Omega}}(\theta)+\nu_{\Omega(t)}\nabla p_{\Omega}\cdot\nabla\mathring{u_{\Omega}}(\theta)-\sigma_{\Omega(t)}(\text{\rm div}v)p_{\Omega}\mathring{u_{\Omega}}(\theta)\right)\>\text{\rm d}x\text{\rm d}t.

Using
Corollary

4.1
and the fact that
\mathring{u_{\Omega}}(\theta)\in W_{\text{\rm per}}
together with
\sigma_{\Omega(T)}=\sigma_{\Omega(0)}
due to
Eqs.

2.16
and
2.17
, it follows:

\int_{Q}\Big(\sigma_{\Omega(t)}\frac{\text{\rm d}\mathring{u_{\Omega}}(\theta)}{\text{\rm d}t}p_{\Omega}+\nu_{\Omega(t)}\nabla p_{\Omega}\cdot\nabla\mathring{u_{\Omega}}(\theta)\Big)\>\text{\rm d}x\text{\rm d}t=-\int_{Q}j^{\prime}(u_{\Omega})\mathring{u_{\Omega}}(\theta)\>\text{\rm d}x\text{\rm d}t.

Now introducing the variational characterization
Eq.

4.19
of
\mathring{u_{\Omega}}(\theta)
, we obtain:

(4.21)

\begin{array}[]{>{\displaystyle}cc>{\displaystyle}l}\int_{Q}j^{\prime}(u_{\Omega})\mathring{u_{\Omega}}(\theta)\>\text{\rm d}x\text{\rm d}t&=&\int_{Q}\sigma_{\Omega(t)}m^{\prime}(0)(\theta)\frac{\text{\rm d}u_{\Omega}}{\text{\rm d}t}\>p_{\Omega}\>\text{\rm d}x\text{\rm d}t-\int_{Q}\sigma_{\Omega(t)}\Big[F_{xx}^{\prime}(0)(\theta)\Big]v\cdot\nabla u_{\Omega}\>p_{\Omega}\>\text{\rm d}x\text{\rm d}t\\[10.00002pt]
&&+\int_{Q}\sigma_{\Omega(t)}v_{1}(\theta)\cdot\nabla u_{\Omega}\>p_{\Omega}\>\text{\rm d}x\text{\rm d}t+\int_{Q}\sigma_{\Omega(t)}b^{\prime}(0)(\theta)\cdot\nabla u_{\Omega}\>p_{\Omega}\>\text{\rm d}x\text{\rm d}t\\[10.00002pt]
&&+\int_{Q}\nu_{\Omega(t)}\Big[A^{\prime}(0)(\theta)\Big]\nabla u_{\Omega}\cdot\nabla p_{\Omega}\>\text{\rm d}x\text{\rm d}t-\int_{Q}\Big(m^{\prime}(0)(\theta)f+f_{1}(\theta)\Big)p_{\Omega}\>\text{\rm d}x\text{\rm d}t.\end{array}

Injecting this expression into
Eq.

4.20
, we obtain the volume form
Eq.

4.10
of the shape derivative of
J(\Omega)
.

Step 5: We infer the surface form
Eq.

4.13
of
J^{\prime}(\Omega)(\theta)
from the volume form
Eq.

4.10
.

Let us write
Eq.

4.10
under the form

J^{\prime}(\Omega)(\theta)=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{4}I_{i}(\theta),

where
I_{i}(\theta)
,
i=1,\ldots,4
are the four integral quantities at the right-hand side of
Eq.

4.10
.
Our derivation of the surface form of
J^{\prime}(\Omega)(\theta)
follows the methodology exposed in
[
2
]
, which we have already exemplified in the proof of
Lemma

3.1
:
we integrate by parts all the terms featured in the
I_{i}(\theta)

which involve derivatives of
\theta
, and among the resulting terms, we only retain those featuring boundary integrals on
\Gamma
of the normal component
\theta\cdot n
,
since the other contributions are expected to cancel according to the prediction of the structure theorem for shape derivatives.

Let us treat the first integral
I_{1}(\theta)
; injecting the expression
Eq.

3.13
of
m^{\prime}(0)(\theta)
, we see that:

I_{1}(\theta)=\int_{Q}(\text{\rm div}\theta)(\varphi_{t}^{-1}(x))j(u_{\Omega}(t,x))\>\text{\rm d}x\text{\rm d}t+r(\theta).

Here and throughout the proof,
r(\theta)
is a remainder that may change from one line to the next, of the form

r(\theta)=\int_{D}s_{\Omega}\cdot\theta\>\text{\rm d}x+\int_{\Gamma}t_{\Omega}\cdot\theta_{\Gamma}\>\text{\rm d}s,

for some vector fields
s_{\Omega}:D\to{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
,
t_{\Omega}:\Gamma\to{\mathbb{R}}^{d}

that depend on
u_{\Omega}
,
p_{\Omega}
and the motion
\varphi_{t}
.
Decomposing the integral in the above right-hand side as a nested integral over
(0,T)
and
D
,
and using a change of variables by
\varphi_{t}
in the innermost integral, we obtain:

\begin{array}[]{>{\displaystyle}cc>{\displaystyle}l}I_{1}(\theta)&=&\int_{0}^{T}\int_{D}|\det\nabla\varphi_{t}(x)|\text{\rm div}\theta(x)j(u_{\Omega}(t,\varphi_{t}(x)))\>\text{\rm d}x\text{\rm d}t+r(\theta)\\
&=&\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\partial D}|\det\nabla\varphi_{t}(x)|j(u_{\Omega}(t,\varphi_{t}(x)))\>(\theta\cdot n)(x)\>\text{\rm d}x\text{\rm d}t+r(\theta),\end{array}

where we have used an integration by parts from the first line to the second one, together with the continuity of
u_{\Omega}(t,\cdot)
across
\Gamma(t)
, see
Lemma

4.2
. Since
\theta\in\Theta_{\text{\rm ad}}
vanishes on
\partial D
, it follows that:

I_{1}(\theta)=r(\theta).

Let us now turn to the second integral
I_{2}(\theta)
, which rewrites:

\begin{array}[]{>{\displaystyle}cc>{\displaystyle}r}I_{2}(\theta)&=&\int_{Q}\sigma_{\Omega(t)}\left(m^{\prime}(0)(\theta)\frac{\text{\rm d}u_{\Omega}}{\text{\rm d}t}-\Big[F_{xx}^{\prime}(0)(\theta)\Big]v\cdot\nabla u_{\Omega}+v_{1}(\theta)\cdot\nabla u_{\Omega}-F_{xt}^{\prime}(0)(\theta)\cdot\nabla u_{\Omega}\right)\>p_{\Omega}\>\text{\rm d}x\text{\rm d}t\\[10.00002pt]
&=&\int_{0}^{T}\int_{D}\sigma_{\Omega(t)}(x)\Big((\text{\rm div}\theta)(\varphi_{t}^{-1}(x))\frac{\text{\rm d}u_{\Omega}}{\text{\rm d}t}(t,x)-\Big[\nabla\varphi_{t}(\varphi_{t}^{-1}(x))\Big]\Big[\nabla\theta(\varphi_{t}^{-1}(x))\Big]\Big[\nabla\varphi_{t}^{-1}(x)\Big]v(t,x)\cdot\nabla u_{\Omega}(t,x)\\[10.00002pt]
&&-\Big[\nabla\varphi_{t}(\varphi_{t}^{-1}(x))\Big]\Big[\nabla\theta(\varphi_{t}^{-1}(x))\Big]\frac{\partial}{\partial t}(\varphi_{t}^{-1}(x))\cdot\nabla u_{\Omega}\Big)p_{\Omega}(t,x)\>\text{\rm d}x\text{\rm d}t+r(\theta),\end{array}

where we have used the derivatives
Eqs.

3.13
,
3.10
and
3.11
.
Again, the change of variables
x\mapsto\varphi_{t}(x)
in the innermost integral yields:

I_{2}(\theta)=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{D}\sigma_{\Omega}(x)\lvert\det\nabla\varphi_{t}(x)\lvert\Big((\text{\rm div}\theta)(x)\frac{\text{\rm d}u_{\Omega}}{\text{\rm d}t}(t,\varphi_{t}(x))-\Big[\nabla\varphi_{t}(x)\Big]\Big[\nabla\theta(x)\Big]\Big[\nabla\varphi_{t}^{-1}(\varphi_{t}(x))\Big]v(t,\varphi_{t}(x))\cdot\nabla u_{\Omega}(t,\varphi_{t}(x))\\
-\Big[\nabla\varphi_{t}(x)\Big]\Big[\nabla\theta(x)\Big]\frac{\partial\varphi_{t}^{-1}}{\partial t}(\varphi_{t}(x))\cdot\nabla u_{\Omega}(t,\varphi_{t}(x))\Big)p_{\Omega}(t,\varphi_{t}(x))\>\text{\rm d}x\text{\rm d}t+r(\theta),

and so

I_{2}(\theta)=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{D}\sigma_{\Omega}(x)|\det\nabla\varphi_{t}(x)|\Big((\text{\rm div}\theta)(x)\frac{\text{\rm d}u_{\Omega}}{\text{\rm d}t}(t,\varphi_{t}(x))-\Big[\nabla\theta(x)\Big]\Big[\nabla\varphi_{t}^{-1}(\varphi_{t}(x))\Big]v(t,\varphi_{t}(x))\cdot\Big[\nabla\varphi_{t}(x)\Big]^{T}\nabla u_{\Omega}(t,\varphi_{t}(x))\\
-\Big[\nabla\theta(x)\Big]\frac{\partial\varphi_{t}^{-1}}{\partial t}(\varphi_{t}(x))\cdot\Big[\nabla\varphi_{t}(x)\Big]^{T}\nabla u_{\Omega}(t,\varphi_{t}(x))\Big)p_{\Omega}(t,\varphi_{t}(x))\>\text{\rm d}x\text{\rm d}t+r(\theta).

Decomposing the innermost integral as the sum of its contributions on
\Omega
and
D\setminus\overline{\Omega}
,
then integrating by parts with
Proposition

A.1
, we obtain:

I_{2}(\theta)=-(\sigma_{2}-\sigma_{1})\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Gamma}|\det\nabla\varphi_{t}(x)|\frac{\text{\rm d}u_{\Omega}}{\text{\rm d}t}(t,\varphi_{t}(x))p_{\Omega}(t,\varphi_{t}(x))(\theta\cdot n)(x)\>\text{\rm d}s\text{\rm d}t\\
+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Gamma}|\det\nabla\varphi_{t}(x)|\Big(\Big[\nabla\varphi_{t}^{-1}(\varphi_{t}(x))\Big]v(t,\varphi_{t}(x))\cdot n(x)+\frac{\partial\varphi_{t}^{-1}}{\partial t}(\varphi_{t}(x))\cdot n(x)\Big)\\
\Big(\sigma_{2}\nabla u_{\Omega}^{+}(t,\varphi_{t}(x))-\sigma_{1}\nabla u_{\Omega}^{-}(t,\varphi_{t}(x))\Big)\cdot\Big(\Big[\nabla\varphi_{t}(x)\Big]\theta(x)\Big)\>p_{\Omega}(t,\varphi_{t}(x))\>\text{\rm d}s\text{\rm d}t+r(\theta).

Eventually, remarking that the definition
Eq.

2.14
of the velocity
v(t,x)
implies that

\Big[\nabla\varphi_{t}^{-1}(\varphi_{t}(x))\Big]v(t,\varphi_{t}(x))+\frac{\partial\varphi_{t}^{-1}}{\partial t}(\varphi_{t}(x))=\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\left(\varphi_{t}^{-1}(\varphi_{t}(x))\right)=0,

we obtain:

I_{2}(\theta)=-(\sigma_{2}-\sigma_{1})\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Gamma}|\det\nabla\varphi_{t}(x)|\frac{\text{\rm d}u_{\Omega}}{\text{\rm d}t}(t,\varphi_{t}(x))p_{\Omega}(t,\varphi_{t}(x))(\theta\cdot n)(x)\>\text{\rm d}s\text{\rm d}t+r(\theta).

We next turn to the third integral
I_{3}(\theta)
on the right-hand side of
Eq.

4.10
. At first, injecting the derivatives
Eqs.

3.11
and
3.17
into the defining formula, we have:

\begin{array}[]{>{\displaystyle}cc>{\displaystyle}l}I_{3}(\theta)&=&\int_{Q}\nu_{\Omega(t)}\Big[A^{\prime}(0)(\theta)\Big]\nabla u_{\Omega}\cdot\nabla p_{\Omega}\>\text{\rm d}x\text{\rm d}t\\[10.00002pt]
&=&\int_{0}^{T}\int_{D}\nu_{\Omega(t)}\Big(m^{\prime}(0)(\theta)\text{\rm I}-\Big[F_{xx}^{\prime}(0)(\theta)\Big]-\Big[F_{xx}^{\prime}(0)(\theta)\Big]^{T}\Big)\nabla u_{\Omega}\cdot\nabla p_{\Omega}\>\text{\rm d}x\text{\rm d}t\\[10.00002pt]
&=&\int_{0}^{T}\int_{D}\nu_{\Omega(t)}(x)\Bigg((\text{\rm div}\theta)(\varphi_{t}^{-1}(x))\text{\rm I}-\Big[\nabla\varphi_{t}(\varphi_{t}^{-1}(x))\Big]\Big[\nabla\theta(\varphi_{t}^{-1}(x))\Big]\Big[\nabla\varphi_{t}^{-1}(x)\Big]\\
&&-\Big[\nabla\varphi_{t}^{-1}(x)\Big]^{T}\Big[\nabla\theta(\varphi_{t}^{-1}(x))\Big]^{T}\Big[\nabla\varphi_{t}(\varphi_{t}^{-1}(x))\Big]^{T}\Bigg)\nabla u_{\Omega}(t,x)\cdot\nabla p_{\Omega}(t,x)\>\text{\rm d}x\text{\rm d}t+r(\theta).\end{array}

Hence, a change of variables based on the diffeomorphism
\varphi_{t}
in the innermost integral produces:

I_{3}(\theta)=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{D}\nu_{\Omega}(x)\lvert\det\nabla\varphi_{t}(x)\lvert\Bigg((\text{\rm div}\theta)(x)\text{\rm I}-\Big[\nabla\varphi_{t}(x)\Big]\Big[\nabla\theta(x)\Big]\Big[\nabla\varphi_{t}^{-1}(\varphi_{t}(x))\Big]\\
-\Big[\nabla\varphi_{t}^{-1}(\varphi_{t}(x))\Big]^{T}\Big[\nabla\theta(x)\Big]^{T}\Big[\nabla\varphi_{t}(x)\Big]^{T}\Bigg)\nabla u_{\Omega}(t,\varphi_{t}(x))\cdot\nabla p_{\Omega}(t,\varphi_{t}(x))\>\text{\rm d}x\text{\rm d}t+r(\theta),

and so

I_{3}(\theta)=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{D}\nu_{\Omega}(x)\lvert\det\nabla\varphi_{t}(x)\lvert(\text{\rm div}\theta)(x)\nabla u_{\Omega}(t,\varphi_{t}(x))\cdot\nabla p_{\Omega}(t,\varphi_{t}(x))\>\text{\rm d}x\text{\rm d}t\\
-\int_{0}^{T}\int_{D}\nu_{\Omega}(x)\lvert\det\nabla\varphi_{t}(x)\lvert\Big[\nabla\theta(x)\Big]\Big[\nabla\varphi_{t}^{-1}(\varphi_{t}(x))\Big]\nabla u_{\Omega}(t,\varphi_{t}(x))\cdot\Big[\nabla\varphi_{t}(x)\Big]^{T}\nabla p_{\Omega}(t,\varphi_{t}(x))\>\text{\rm d}x\text{\rm d}t\\
-\int_{0}^{T}\int_{D}\nu_{\Omega}(x)\lvert\det\nabla\varphi_{t}(x)\lvert\Big[\nabla\theta(x)\Big]\Big[\nabla\varphi_{t}^{-1}(\varphi_{t}(x))\Big]\nabla p_{\Omega}(t,\varphi_{t}(x))\cdot\Big[\nabla\varphi_{t}(x)\Big]^{T}\nabla u_{\Omega}(t,\varphi_{t}(x))\>\text{\rm d}x\text{\rm d}t+r(\theta).

Decomposing each integral over
D
as the sum of two integrals over
\Omega
and
D\setminus\overline{\Omega}
,
then using the integration by parts formulas in
Proposition

I_{3}(\theta)=-\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Gamma}\lvert\det\nabla\varphi_{t}(x)\lvert\Big(\nu_{2}\nabla u_{\Omega}^{+}(t,\varphi_{t}(x))\cdot\nabla p_{\Omega}^{+}(t,\varphi_{t}(x))-\nu_{1}\nabla u_{\Omega}^{-}(t,\varphi_{t}(x))\cdot\nabla p_{\Omega}^{-}(t,\varphi_{t}(x))\Big)(\theta\cdot n)(x)\>\text{\rm d}s(x)\text{\rm d}t\\
+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Gamma}\lvert\det\nabla\varphi_{t}(x)\lvert\Bigg(\nu_{2}\Big(\Big[\nabla\varphi_{t}^{-1}(\varphi_{t}(x))\Big]\nabla u_{\Omega}^{+}(t,\varphi_{t}(x))\cdot n(x)\Big)\Big(\Big[\nabla\varphi_{t}(x)\Big]^{T}\nabla p_{\Omega}^{+}(t,\varphi_{t}(x))\cdot\theta(x)\Big)\\
-\nu_{1}\Big(\Big[\nabla\varphi_{t}^{-1}(\varphi_{t}(x))\Big]\nabla u_{\Omega}^{-}(t,\varphi_{t}(x))\cdot n(x)\Big)\Big(\Big[\nabla\varphi_{t}(x)\Big]^{T}\nabla p_{\Omega}^{-}(t,\varphi_{t}(x))\cdot\theta(x)\Big)\Bigg)\>\text{\rm d}s(x)\text{\rm d}t\\
+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Gamma}\lvert\det\nabla\varphi_{t}(x)\lvert\Bigg(\nu_{2}\Big(\Big[\nabla\varphi_{t}^{-1}(\varphi_{t}(x))\Big]\nabla p_{\Omega}^{+}(t,\varphi_{t}(x))\cdot n(x)\Big)\Big(\Big[\nabla\varphi_{t}(x)\Big]^{T}\nabla u_{\Omega}^{+}(t,\varphi_{t}(x))\cdot\theta(x)\Big)\\

and we now proceed to simplify each of the three integrals in the above right-hand side, which we denote by
J_{1}(\theta)
,
J_{2}(\theta)
and
J_{3}(\theta)
, respectively.

At first, we introduce the tangential and normal components of the gradients of
u_{\Omega}(t,\cdot)
and
p_{\Omega}(t,\cdot)
; using the jump relations from

4.2
and
4.3
,
J_{1}(\theta)
rewrites:

J_{1}(\theta)=-(\nu_{2}-\nu_{1})\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Gamma}\lvert\det\nabla\varphi_{t}(x)\lvert\nabla_{\Gamma(t)}u_{\Omega}(t,\varphi_{t}(x))\cdot\nabla_{\Gamma(t)}p_{\Omega}(t,\varphi_{t}(x))(\theta\cdot n)(x)\>\text{\rm d}s(x)\text{\rm d}t\\
-\left(\frac{1}{\nu_{2}}-\frac{1}{\nu_{1}}\right)\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Gamma}\lvert\det\nabla\varphi_{t}(x)\lvert\Big(\nu_{\Omega(t)}\nabla u_{\Omega}(t,\varphi_{t}(x))\cdot n_{\Omega(t)}(\varphi_{t}(x))\Big)\Big(\nu_{\Omega(t)}\nabla p_{\Omega}(t,\varphi_{t}(x))\cdot n_{\Omega(t)}(\varphi_{t}(x))\Big)(\theta\cdot n)(x)\>\text{\rm d}s(x)\text{\rm d}t.

Let us next deal with

; to this end, we observe that

\lvert\det\nabla\varphi_{t}(x)\lvert\nu_{2}\Big(\Big[\nabla\varphi_{t}^{-1}(\varphi_{t}(x))\Big]\nabla u_{\Omega}^{+}(t,\varphi_{t}(x))\cdot n(x)\Big)=\nu_{2}\Big(\nabla u_{\Omega}^{+}(t,\varphi_{t}(x))\cdot\left(\Big[\text{\rm com}(\nabla\varphi_{t}(x))\Big]n(x)\right)\Big),

Hence, using

A.1

\begin{array}[]{>{\displaystyle}cc>{\displaystyle}l}\lvert\det\nabla\varphi_{t}(x)\lvert\nu_{2}\Big(\Big[\nabla\varphi_{t}^{-1}(\varphi_{t}(x))\Big]\nabla u_{\Omega}^{+}(t,\varphi_{t}(x))\cdot n(x)\Big)&=&\lvert\det\nabla\varphi_{t}(x)\lvert\nu_{1}\Big(\Big[\nabla\varphi_{t}^{-1}(\varphi_{t}(x))\Big]\nabla u_{\Omega}^{-}(t,\varphi_{t}(x))\cdot n(x)\Big)\\
&=&\lvert\text{\rm com}(\nabla\varphi_{t}((x)))n(x)\lvert\Big(\nu_{\Omega(t)}\nabla u_{\Omega}\cdot n_{\Omega(t)}(t,\varphi_{t}(x))\Big),\end{array}

and so:

(4.23)

J_{2}(\theta)=\\
\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Gamma}\lvert\text{\rm com}(\nabla\varphi_{t}(x))n(x)\lvert\Big(\nu_{\Omega}\nabla u_{\Omega}\cdot n_{\Omega(t)}(t,\varphi_{t}(x))\Big)\Big(\Big[\nabla\varphi_{t}(x)\Big]^{T}\nabla p_{\Omega}^{+}(t,\varphi_{t}(x))\cdot\theta(x)-\Big[\nabla\varphi_{t}(x)\Big]^{T}\nabla p_{\Omega}^{-}(t,\varphi_{t}(x))\cdot\theta(x)\Big)\>\text{\rm d}s(x)\text{\rm d}t.

We now bring into play the jump relations satisfied by

4.3
to simplify the second parenthesis in the above integrand:

\Big[\nabla\varphi_{t}(x)\Big]^{T}\nabla p_{\Omega}^{+}(t,\varphi_{t}(x))\cdot\theta(x)-\Big[\nabla\varphi_{t}(x)\Big]^{T}\nabla p_{\Omega}^{-}(t,\varphi_{t}(x))\cdot\theta(x)\\
\begin{array}[]{>{\displaystyle}cc>{\displaystyle}l}&=&\Big(\nabla p_{\Omega}^{+}(t,\varphi_{t}(x))-\nabla p_{\Omega}^{-}(t,\varphi_{t}(x))\Big)\cdot\Big(\Big[\nabla\varphi_{t}(x)\Big]\theta(x)\Big)\\[5.0pt]

Hence,

equals:

J_{2}(\theta)=\left(\frac{1}{\nu_{2}}-\frac{1}{\nu_{1}}\right)\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Gamma}\lvert\det\nabla\varphi_{t}(x)\lvert\Big(\nu_{\Omega}\nabla u_{\Omega}\cdot n_{\Omega(t)}(t,\varphi_{t}(x))\Big)\Big(\nu_{\Omega}\nabla p_{\Omega}\cdot n_{\Omega(t)}(t,\varphi_{t}(x))\Big)(\theta\cdot n)(x)\>\text{\rm d}s(x)\text{\rm d}t.

Applying a similar treatment to

, it follows:

J_{3}(\theta)=-(\nu_{2}-\nu_{1})\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Gamma}\lvert\det\nabla\varphi_{t}(x)\lvert\nabla_{\Gamma(t)}u_{\Omega}(t,\varphi_{t}(x))\cdot\nabla_{\Gamma(t)}p_{\Omega}(t,\varphi_{t}(x))(\theta\cdot n)(x)\>\text{\rm d}s(x)\text{\rm d}t\\
+\left(\frac{1}{\nu_{2}}-\frac{1}{\nu_{1}}\right)\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Gamma}\lvert\det\nabla\varphi_{t}(x)\lvert\Big(\nu_{\Omega}\nabla u_{\Omega}(t,\varphi_{t}(x))\cdot n_{\Omega(t)}(\varphi_{t}(x))\Big)\Big(\nu_{\Omega}\nabla p_{\Omega}(t,\varphi_{t}(x))\cdot n_{\Omega(t)}(\varphi_{t}(x))\Big)(\theta\cdot n)(x)\>\text{\rm d}s(x)\text{\rm d}t\\
+r(\theta).

Eventually, the fourth and last integral
I_{4}(\theta)
in the right-hand side of
Eq.

4.21
equals:

I_{4}(\theta)=\int_{Q}\Big(m^{\prime}(0)(\theta)f+f_{1}(\theta)\Big)p_{\Omega}\>\text{\rm d}x\text{\rm d}t=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{D}(\text{\rm div}\theta)(\varphi_{t}^{-1}(x))f(t,x)p_{\Omega}(t,x)\>\text{\rm d}x\text{\rm d}t+r(\theta).

Since

is continuous across
\Gamma(t)
(see again
Remark

4.3
), it follows like in the treatment of
I_{1}(\theta)
that

I_{4}(\theta)=r(\theta).

Eventually, the desired surface expression

4.13
of the shape derivative
J^{\prime}(\Omega)(\theta)
is obtained by gathering the above expressions of the various integrals

,
i=1,\ldots,4
and
J_{j}(\theta)
,
j=1,\ldots,3
,
and by checking that the integrals collected in the various remainders

do cancel, which is elementary, but tedious.
This concludes the proof of the theorem.
∎

Remark 4.5
.

As usual in the practice of the adjoint method, the defining problem for

is (the transpose of) the linearized version of the state problem
Eq.

2.22
for
u_{\Omega}
.
In particular, the well-posedness of
Eq.

4.25
is not an immediate consequence of that of
Eq.

2.22
. In the present context, this property results from
similar arguments to those in the proof of
Theorem

4.1
, once we have observed that the matrix field

A(x):=\left(\hat{\nu}(|\nabla u_{\Omega}|)\text{\rm I}+\frac{\hat{\nu}^{\prime}(|\nabla u_{\Omega}|)}{|\nabla u_{\Omega}|}\nabla u_{\Omega}\otimes\nabla u_{\Omega}\right)

induces a linear and uniformly elliptic operator in
D_{\text{\rm mag}}
. To see this, we first note that,
by taking
s_{2}=s
,
s_{1}=s+h
in
Eq.

2.13
for
s>0
and sufficiently small

, then dividing both sides by

and letting

(4.26)

s\hat{\nu}^{\prime}(s)+\hat{\nu}(s)\geq\underline{\nu}.

Now, for any unit vector
\xi\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, the operator attached to
A(x)
satisfies:

A(x)\xi\cdot\xi=\hat{\nu}(|\nabla u_{\Omega}|)\lvert\xi\lvert^{2}+\frac{\hat{\nu}^{\prime}(|\nabla u_{\Omega}|)}{|\nabla u_{\Omega}|}\lvert\nabla u_{\Omega}\cdot\xi\lvert^{2}.

Then, if

If on the contrary

the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality together with

\begin{array}[]{>{\displaystyle}cc>{\displaystyle}l}A(x)\xi\cdot\xi&\geq&\left(\hat{\nu}(|\nabla u_{\Omega}|)+\frac{\hat{\nu}^{\prime}(|\nabla u_{\Omega}|)}{|\nabla u_{\Omega}|}\lvert\nabla u_{\Omega}\lvert^{2}\right)\lvert\xi\lvert^{2},\\
&\geq&\underline{\nu}\lvert\xi\lvert^{2},\end{array}

which is the expected conclusion.

In this section, we describe the main numerical methods employed in our shape optimization framework.
After sketching the overall strategy in

5.1

we outline in

We eventually discuss in

5.1.
Sketch of the shape optimization workflow

In a nutshell, we solve

which improves the shape

by successive deformations along descent directions for the objective function

The numerical discretization is based on a space-time finite element framework: the 3d space-time cylinder

2

https://dev.opencascade.org/doc/refman/html/index.html

The global strategy is outlined in
Algorithm

5.2.
Resolution of the state and adjoint equations

At any iteration
n
of the execution of
Algorithm

1
,
the space-time cylinder
Q=(0,T)\times D
is equipped with a tetrahedral mesh
{\mathcal{K}}^{n}
in which the deformed structures of the motor (in particular, the ferromagnetic phase
\Omega^{n}(t)
, the air phase
\Omega^{n}_{\text{a}}(t)
, etc.) for
t\in[0,T]
are explicitly discretized.
Hence, the motion of the structure of the motor induced by
\varphi_{t}
is directly encoded in the space-time mesh.

This mesh serves as the support for the numerical solution of the state and adjoint equations
Eqs.

2.22
and
4.25
for
u_{\Omega^{n}}
and
p_{\Omega^{n}}
via a space-time finite element method.
These are discretized on the mesh
{\mathcal{K}}^{n}
by means of continuous, piecewise linear finite elements as suggested in
[
68
]
,
see also
[
27
]
for an application to the parallel simulation of an electric machine. We mention that also higher order polynomial degrees could be used, see e.g.
[
47
]
for a related investigation in an optimal control setting.
The non linearity in the magneto-quasi-static problem
Eq.

2.22
for the state
u_{\Omega^{n}}
is handled thanks to a Newton-Raphson method with damping in order to ensure a decrease of the residual.
The time-periodic conditions can be treated in a straightforward way by identifying the degrees of freedom lying on the bottom and top sections of the mesh
{\mathcal{K}}^{n}
of the space-time cylinder
Q
provided that the bottom and top surfaces of the space-time cylinder feature an identical mesh.

Our numerical experiments rely on the finite element environment
NGSolve

[
64
]
.
Note that the precise setting at hand does not allow to take advantage of the capabilities of this package to compute automatically shape derivatives in the context of more “standard” shape optimization problems
[
31
]
.

Remark 5.1
.

•

As we have mentioned in the introduction, in principle, space-time finite element approaches allow for parallelization of the numerical resolution of an evolution problem in space-time by means of classical domain decomposition approaches, see e.g.
[
27
]
. In the same spirit, adaptive mesh refinement in space-time is possible
[
47
]
.
Last but not least, the state and adjoint equations could actually be gathered into a single system and be solved in parallel, allowing for another level of parallelization when compared to more conventional time-stepping schemes.
While we do solve the associated systems of linear equations in parallel, as discussed in
Section

6.2.2
, adaptivity and the combination of state and adjoint are not exploited in the present study, but they are interesting leads for future work.

•

The present use of a space-time finite element method is possible since the physical problem under scrutiny arises in two space dimensions,
thus resulting in a 3d space-time mesh. This strategy could be extended to the case of three space dimensions, provided numerical method for meshing
(3+1)
-dimensional space-time domains and solving related variational problems be available
[
47
]
.

5.3.
Evaluation of the shape derivative
J^{\prime}(\Omega)(\theta)
and calculation of a descent direction

At any iteration
n=0,\ldots
of
Algorithm

1
, the reference to which is omitted in this section for notational simplicity,
we rely on the volume form of the shape derivative
J^{\prime}(\Omega)(\theta)
to identify a descent direction for
J(\Omega)
.
As discussed in e.g.
[
2
,
32
]
, the use of the volume form is a priori less convenient to achieve this purpose than that of the surface form.
Nevertheless, it presents several advantages, such as the less stringent regularity demanded from
\Omega
,
u_{\Omega}
,
p_{\Omega}
, a better consistency between the theoretical and discrete shape optimization settings
[
37
]
, and the possibility to conduct part of the evaluation in an automated way
[
31
]
.

To exploit the volume form of
J^{\prime}(\Omega)(\theta)
, we rely on the so-called “Hilbertian method”, for which we refer to e.g.
[
7
,
18
,
22
]
.
Let
V
be a Hilbert space of vector fields on
D
, equipped with an inner product
b(\cdot,\cdot)
, which is continuously embedded in
\Theta_{\text{\rm ad}}
.
We solve the following auxiliary variational problem for the shape gradient
\theta
of the derivative
J^{\prime}(\Omega)
associated to the Hilbert space
(V,b(\cdot,\cdot))
:

(5.2)

\text{Search for }\theta\in V\text{ s.t. for all }\eta\in V,\quad b(\theta,\eta)=J^{\prime}(\Omega)(\eta),

If
\theta
is the solution to
Eq.

5.3
, then
-\theta
is a descent direction for
J(\Omega)
, since

J^{\prime}(\Omega)(-\theta)=-b(\theta,\theta)<0.

Moreover,
\theta
benefits from the features encoded in the space
V
, such as for instance a higher regularity than mere vector fields in
\Theta_{\text{\rm ad}}
, etc.

Several choices are possible about
V
and
b
, starting from Sobolev spaces
H^{m}(D)^{d}
, for
m
large enough.
One efficient and popular choice, albeit formal, is the following:

(5.3)

\displaystyle V=H^{1}_{0}(D)^{d},\text{ with the inner product }b(\theta,\eta):=\int_{D}\Big(\alpha(x)(\nabla\theta+\nabla\theta^{\top}):\nabla\eta+\beta(x)\theta\cdot\eta\Big)\>\text{\rm d}x,

where
\alpha,\beta:D\to{\mathbb{R}}
are smooth, positive and non negative weights, respectively.
Intuitively, this choice, inspired by the linear elasticity system, produces shape gradient which “resemble” elastic displacements, and thereby cause as little compression as possible in the mesh.
In the same spirit, one may also enrich
b(\cdot,\cdot)
with a term attached to the Cauchy-Riemann equations, as a means to urge the solution
\theta
to
Eq.

5.2
to be (approximately) conformal, thus preserving (approximately) the angles of the mesh, see
[
38
]
.

The numerical solution of the identification problem
Eq.

5.2
raises the need to calculate the volume form of
J^{\prime}(\Omega)(\theta)

– by this, we mean, for a given shape
\Omega
, to calculate the quantity
J^{\prime}(\Omega)(\theta)
for a wide range of deformation fields
\theta
.
To achieve this, we note that the shape derivatives
Eqs.

3.19
,
4.10
and
4.24
of the functionals considered in this article can be written under the form

J^{\prime}(\Omega)(\theta)=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\varphi_{t}(D)}\Big(\mathcal{S}_{0}(t,x)\cdot\theta(\varphi_{t}^{-1}(x))+\mathcal{S}_{1}(t,x):\nabla\theta(\varphi_{t}^{-1}(x))\Big)\>\text{\rm d}x\>\text{\rm d}t,

for some vector and matrix fields
{\mathcal{S}}_{0}:Q\to{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
and
{\mathcal{S}}_{1}:Q\to{\mathbb{R}}^{d\times d}
,
see
[
48
]
for a general result in this spirit.
Hence, a change of variables yields:

\displaystyle J^{\prime}(\Omega)(\theta)=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\int_{D}\lvert\mbox{det}\nabla\varphi_{t}(x)\lvert\Big(\mathcal{S}_{0}(t,\varphi_{t}(x))\cdot\theta(x)+\mathcal{S}_{1}(t,\varphi_{t}(x)):\nabla\theta(x)\Big)\;\mbox{d}x\>\mbox{d}t

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{D}g_{0}(x)\cdot\theta(x)+g_{1}(x):\nabla\theta(x)\;\mbox{d}x,

with

(5.4)

g_{0}(x)=\int_{0}^{T}\mathcal{S}_{0}(t,\varphi_{t}(x))|\mbox{det}\nabla\varphi_{t}(x)|\;\text{\rm d}t,\text{ and }g_{1}(x)=\int_{0}^{T}\mathcal{S}_{1}(t,\varphi_{t}(x))|\mbox{det}\nabla\varphi_{t}(x)|\;\text{\rm d}t.

Hence, in practice, when it comes to evaluate
J^{\prime}(\Omega)(\theta)
, we first calculate the fields
g_{0}(x)
and
g_{1}(x)
on a triangular mesh
{\mathcal{T}}
of the spatial domain
D
.
For instance,
{\mathcal{T}}
can be a mesh of the bottom surface of the space-time cylinder
Q
enclosed in the total 3d mesh
{\mathcal{K}}
.
We use a piecewise constant approximation of the functions
g_{i}
(
i=0,1
) on
{\mathcal{T}}
: the constant value of
g_{i}
on each triangle
T\in{\mathcal{T}}
is obtained by evaluating the “vertical line integral” featured in
Eq.

5.4
at the centroid of
T
;
this is realized, in turn, by traveling the space-time mesh
{\mathcal{K}}
of
Q

and using a composite trapezoidal rule.

Eventually, once a descent direction
\theta
is obtained for
J(\Omega)
and a suitable descent step
\tau>0
is selected, the corresponding space-time deformation mapping

\Theta(t,x)=(t,\varphi_{t}\circ(\text{\rm Id}+\tau\theta)\circ\varphi_{t}^{-1}(x))

is used to update the space-time mesh
{\mathcal{K}}
, see again
Fig.

3
.

6.2.
Optimization of the structure of a rotating electric machine

In this section, we consider the optimization of the structure of an electric motor.

6.2.1.
Description of the geometric setting

The considered machine operates at steady state with a constant rotational speed of 600 rotations per minute.
The geometry of its 2d cross-section
D
is symmetric with respect to both coordinates axes, see again
Fig.

1
; hence,
the time horizon
T
is chosen as the duration of a 90 degree rotation, i.e.
T=0.025
s.

The conductivities of the various materials at play take the values:

\sigma_{f}=\sigma_{a}=\sigma_{c}=0,\quad\sigma_{m}=10^{6}\>\>\text{A}^{2}\cdot\text{s}^{3}\cdot\text{kg}^{-1}\cdot\text{m}^{-3},

and the reluctivity of air, copper and magnet equal:

\nu_{a}=\nu_{c}=\frac{10^{7}}{4\pi}\>\>\text{Am}\cdot\text{V}^{-1}\cdot\text{s}^{-1},\text{ and }\nu_{m}=\frac{\nu_{a}}{1.05}\>\>\text{Am}\cdot\text{V}^{-1}\cdot\text{s}^{-1}.

The material behaviour of the ferromagnetic material is characterized by the nonlinear reluctivity function
\hat{\nu}
defined by:

\hat{\nu}(s)=\nu_{a}-(\nu_{a}-c_{1})\text{exp}(-c_{2}s^{c_{3}}),

with the parameters
c_{1}=200
,
c_{2}=0.001
,
c_{3}=6
, see
Fig.

6
(a).
We also assume that the magnet region
D_{\text{\rm mag}}
is the support of a permanent magnetization field
M
.
The direction of
M
is depicted in
Fig.

6
(b) and its magnitude equals
\lvert M\lvert=\nu_{m}B_{r}\>\text{A}\cdot\text{m}^{-1}
, where
B_{r}=1.216\>\text{V}\cdot\text{s}\cdot\text{m}^{-2}
is the magnetic remanence.

The coil region
D_{\text{stat,c}}
of the stator is occupied by a large number of thin copper wires wound around the iron core of the machine.
This phase is subdivided into six regions
\Omega_{U_{+}},\Omega_{U_{-}},\Omega_{V_{+}},\Omega_{V_{-}},\Omega_{W_{+}},\Omega_{W_{-}}
and the source current density
f
is spatially homogeneous with smooth time variations in each of these:

(6.2)

\displaystyle f(t,x)=

\displaystyle\Big(\mathds{1}_{\Omega_{U^{+}}}(x)-\mathds{1}_{\Omega_{U^{-}}}(x)\Big)I_{U}(t)+\Big(\mathds{1}_{\Omega_{V^{+}}}(x)-\mathds{1}_{\Omega_{V^{-}}}(x)\Big)I_{V}(t)+\Big(\mathds{1}_{\Omega_{W^{+}}}(x)-\mathds{1}_{\Omega_{W^{-}}}(x)\Big)I_{W}(t).

In this expression, the current densities read:

\displaystyle I_{U}(t)=a\,\sin(4\alpha(t)+\psi),

\displaystyle I_{V}(t)=a\,\sin\left(4\alpha(t)+\psi+\frac{4}{3}\pi\right),

\displaystyle I_{W}(t)=a\,\sin\left(4\alpha(t)+\psi+\frac{2}{3}\pi\right),

where
\alpha(t)=\frac{\pi}{2}\frac{t}{T}
and
\psi=\frac{\pi}{18}
is called the current angle.
Here, the amplitude
a
is given by
a=I\,c/{\mathcal{A}}
where
I=1555.64
A is the product of the current intensity and the number of copper wires per coil,
c=2.75
is the stacking factor and
{\mathcal{A}}\approx 1.8\cdot 10^{-4}
m
2
is the area of one coil.

In this application, the transformation
\varphi_{t}
stands for a rotation of the inner part
D_{\text{\rm rot}}
at constant angular velocity
\alpha^{\prime}(t)=\frac{\pi}{2T}
s
-1
, i.e.,

\varphi_{t}(x)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}R_{\alpha(t)}x&\text{if }x\in D_{\text{\rm rot}}\\
x&\text{if }x\in D_{\text{\rm stat}},\end{array}\right.\qquad\text{where }R_{\alpha(t)}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\cos\alpha(t)&-\sin\alpha(t)\\
\sin\alpha(t)&\cos\alpha(t)\end{array}\right).

In this setting, we aim to optimize the average torque of the device by acting on the shape of the phase
\Omega\subset D_{\text{\rm rot}}
containing ferromagnetic material
– and thereby on the shape of the air pockets near the magnets in the rotor domain – while keeping fixed the region
D_{\text{\rm mag}}
containing the permanent magnets:

(6.3)

\min\limits_{\Omega\subset D}J(\Omega),\text{ where }J(\Omega):=-\text{\rm Tor}(u_{\Omega}),

where the magneto-quasi-static potential
u_{\Omega}
is the solution to the non linear evolution problem
Eq.

2.22
and the torque
\text{\rm Tor}(u)
is defined in
Eq.

2.9
.
Note that, strictly speaking,
J(\Omega)
is not of the form considered in
Section

4
,
as it involves the gradient of the potential
u_{\Omega}
. Nevertheless, as noted in
Section

3.1
, our calculations performed can be adapted straightforwardly to handle this case.

Practical implementation details

At each iteration

In the first optimization iteration

4

[
9

Since, at the current state,

We run the simulations on RICAM’s RADON cluster
4
4

4

https://www.oeaw.ac.at/ricam/hpc

The solution of the state problem

7.
Conclusion and perspectives

In this article, we have addressed a shape optimization problem related to the internal structure of an electric motor.
This work departs from the related literature by the fact that the physical problem at stake is described by a version of the non linear magneto-quasi-static equation featuring a time evolving geometry – a rich setting, which captures fine, realistic effects.
After proving the well-posedness of the physical problem and calculating the shape derivative of a generic optimization criterion in this context, we have proposed a shape gradient workflow
based on the space-time finite element method. We have validated our numerical strategy on an academic problem and tackled a more realistic example in the physical context of interest.

The work in this article paves the way to multiple further investigations. On the one hand, the physical model, although already complicated enough, could be enriched to include multiphysics effects,
for instance couplings between electromagnetic and thermal effects.
This could require time horizons larger than one single rotation period, and could be addressed by combining the present space-time finite element method with time-stepping techniques.
On a different note, constraints about the mechanical integrity and stability of the optimized design could be modeled with the help of structural mechanics, as suggested in
[
73
]
. From the numerical viewpoint,
more robust methods could be implemented to deal with the update of the shape, such as the level set method
[
3
]
or one of its avatars
[
1
,
20
]
retaining an exact, meshed description of the optimized phases. Eventually, optimal design strategies based on topological derivatives could also be considered in this context, see e.g.
[
29
]
for a related investigation.

Appendix A
A few useful formulas in shape calculus

The following integration by parts formula is a version of the usual Green’s formula that is used repeatedly in the main part of the article.

Proposition A.1
.

Let
\Omega\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
be a Lipschitz bounded domain.

(i)

Let
\theta\in W^{1,\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
a:{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}
be a smooth scalar field. Then:

\int_{\Omega}\frac{\partial\theta_{i}}{\partial x_{j}}a\>\text{\rm d}x=\int_{\partial\Omega}\theta_{i}an_{j}\>\text{\rm d}s-\int_{\Omega}\frac{\partial a}{\partial x_{j}}\theta_{i}\>\text{\rm d}x.

(ii)

Let
\theta\in W^{1,\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
a,b:{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
be smooth vector fields.
The following integration by parts formula holds:

\int_{\Omega}\nabla\theta a\cdot b\>\text{\rm d}x=\int_{\partial\Omega}(\theta\cdot b)(a\cdot n)\>\text{\rm d}s-\int_{\Omega}(\text{\rm div}a)(\theta\cdot b)\>\text{\rm d}x-\int_{\Omega}\nabla ba\cdot\theta\>\text{\rm d}x

Let us also recall the following relation between the normal and tangent vectors to the boundary of a domain and those attached to a deformed configuration.

Lemma A.1
.

Let
\Omega\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
be a bounded Lipschitz domain, and let
\varphi:{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
be a Lipschitz continuous homeomorphism with Lipschitz inverse.
Then, for any tangential vector field
\tau:\partial\Omega\to{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, the vector field
\nabla\varphi(\varphi^{-1}(y))\tau(\varphi^{-1}(y))

is tangential to
\partial(\varphi(\Omega))
.
Besides, the unit normal vector fields
n_{\Omega}
and
n_{\varphi(\Omega)}
are related by:

\forall x\in\Omega,\quad n_{\varphi(\Omega)}(\varphi(x))=\frac{1}{|\text{\rm com}(\nabla\varphi(x))n_{\Omega}(x)|}\text{\rm com}(\nabla\varphi(x))n_{\Omega}(x),

where
\text{\rm com}(M)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d\times d}
is the cofactor matrix of a
d\times d
matrix
M
.

B.1.
A few words about time-space functional spaces

This section gathers a few definitions and basic facts about the time-space functional spaces which are naturally involved in the mathematical formulation of evolution problems.

Let
V\subset H\subset V^{*}
be an evolution triple, that is:

•

(V,\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{V})
and
(H,\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{H})
are real and separable Hilbert spaces, and
V^{*}
is the topological dual of
V
;
we denote by
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{V^{*},V}
the usual duality pairing between
V^{*}
and
V
.

•

The inclusion
V\subset H
is continuous and dense.

Given a fixed time interval
[0,T]
, we define the space
L^{2}(0,T;V)
by:

(B.1)

L^{2}(0,T;V)=\left\{u:[0,T]\to V,\>\>\int_{0}^{T}\lvert\lvert u(t)\lvert\lvert_{V}^{2}\>\text{\rm d}t<\infty\right\}.

The latter is a Hilbert space when equipped with the natural inner product

\langle u(t),v(t)\rangle_{L^{2}(0,T;V)}:=\int_{0}^{T}\langle u(t),v(t)\rangle_{V}\>\text{\rm d}t.

Its topological dual
L^{2}(0,T;V)^{*}
is naturally identified to
L^{2}(0,T;V^{*})
via the pairing:

(B.2)

\langle f,u\rangle_{L^{2}(0,T;V^{*}),L^{2}(0,T;V)}=\int_{0}^{T}\langle f(t),u(t)\rangle_{V^{*},V}\>\text{\rm d}t.

Definition B.1
.

Let
V\subset H\subset V^{*}
be an evolution triple, and let
Z
be another Hilbert space.
A function
u\in L^{2}(0,T;V)
has a time derivative in the space
L^{2}(0,T;Z)
if there exists
z\in L^{2}(0,T;Z)
such that, for all real-valued test functions
\varphi\in{\mathcal{C}}^{\infty}_{c}(0,T)
, it holds:

\int_{0}^{T}u(t)\varphi^{\prime}(t)\>\text{\rm d}t=-\int_{0}^{T}z(t)\varphi(t)\>\text{\rm d}t.

This derivative is then denoted by
\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}=z
.

Let
V\subset H\subset V^{*}
be an evolution triple. The time-dependent problems considered in this article bring into play Hilbert spaces of the form

(B.3)

W(0,T;V,H)=\Big\{u\in L^{2}(0,T;V)\text{ s.t. }\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}\in L^{2}(0,T;V^{*})\Big\},

equipped with the natural norm

||u||_{W(0,T;V,H)}^{2}:=\int_{0}^{T}\lvert\lvert u(t)\lvert\lvert^{2}_{V}\>\text{\rm d}t+\int_{0}^{T}\left\lvert\left\lvert\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}(t)\right\lvert\right\lvert^{2}_{V^{*}}\>\text{\rm d}t.

We recall the following facts about such functional spaces, see Lemmas 1 and 2 of Chap XVIII, §1.2 of
[
21
]
.

Lemma B.1
.

(i)

The subspace

\left\{u(t)=\sum\limits_{k=0}^{K}t^{k}u_{k},\>\>K\geq 0,\>\>u_{k}\in V\right\}\subset W(0,T;V,H)

of polynomial functions in the time variable with coefficients in
V
is dense in
W(0,T;V,H)
.
In particular, the space
{\mathcal{C}}^{\infty}([0,T],V)
of smooth
V
-valued functions on
[0,T]
is dense in
W(0,T;V,H)
.

(ii)

The space
W(0,T;V,H)
is continuously embedded in the space
{\mathcal{C}}([0,T];H)
of continuous
H
-valued functions on
[0,T]
.

The last point of this lemma is of crucial importance in practice, as it gives a rigorous meaning to the time (e.g. initial, periodic) conditions usually imposed to the solution of an evolution problem when it is sought in
W(0,T;V,H)
.

B.2.
Preliminary results about the well-posedness of some non linear problems

Let us first recall the following consequence of the Banach fixed point theorem, about the solution to a non linear stationary equation in a Hilbert space, whose operator satisfies suitable monotonicity and continuity conditions,
see Th. 25 B. from
[
75
]
.

Theorem B.1
.

Let
(V,\lvert\lvert\cdot\lvert\lvert_{V})
be a real Hilbert space and let
{\mathcal{A}}:V\to V^{*}
be a (possibly non linear) mapping satisfying the following properties:

(i)

{\mathcal{A}}
is strongly monotone, i.e. there exists
\alpha>0
such that:

\forall u,v\in V,\quad\langle{\mathcal{A}}u-{\mathcal{A}}v,u-v\rangle_{V^{*},V}\>\geq\>\alpha\lvert\lvert u-v\lvert\lvert_{V}^{2}.

(ii)

{\mathcal{A}}
is Lipschitz continuous, i.e. there exists
L>0
such that:

\forall u,v\in V,\quad\lvert\lvert{\mathcal{A}}u-{\mathcal{A}}v\lvert\lvert_{V^{*}}\>\leq\>L\lvert\lvert u-v\lvert\lvert_{V}.

Then, for each
b\in V^{*}
, the equation

(B.4)

{\mathcal{A}}u=b

has a unique solution
u\in V
. This solution has a Lipschitz continuous dependence with respect to the data: there exists a constant
C>0
depending only on
\alpha
and
L
such that, if
u_{1}
,
u_{2}
are the solutions to
Eq.

B.4
with respective right-hand sides
b_{1}
,
b_{2}\in V^{*}
, it holds:

\lvert\lvert u_{1}-u_{2}\lvert\lvert_{V}\>\leq\>C\lvert\lvert b_{1}-b_{2}\lvert\lvert_{V^{*}}.

In particular, the above result is the pivotal ingredient in the proof of the following lemma about the well-posedness of a stationary version of the considered evolution problem
Eq.

2.22
, see
[
59
]
for a similar argument:

Lemma B.2
.

Let
{\mathcal{O}}
be a bounded Lipschitz domain in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, let
\hat{\nu}:{\mathbb{R}}_{+}\to{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
be a function satisfying
Eq.

2.13
, and let
B\in{\mathcal{C}}^{\infty}(\overline{{\mathcal{O}}};{\mathbb{R}}^{d\times d})
be a matrix-valued function such that
B(x)
is invertible for all
x\in\overline{{\mathcal{O}}}
. Then for all data
f\in L^{2}({\mathcal{O}})
and
g\in H^{1/2}(\partial{\mathcal{O}})
, the boundary value problem

(B.5)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}-\text{\rm div}(\hat{\nu}(\lvert B^{T}(x)\nabla u\lvert)B(x)B^{T}(x)\nabla u)=f&\text{in } {\mathcal{O}},\\
u=g&\text{on }\partial{\mathcal{O}},\end{array}\right.

has a unique solution
u\in H^{1}({\mathcal{O}})
which is Lipschitz continuous with respect to the data: the solutions
u_{1}
,
u_{2}\in H^{1}({\mathcal{O}})
associated to different data
(f_{1},g_{1})
,
(f_{2},g_{2})\in L^{2}({\mathcal{O}})\times H^{1/2}(\partial{\mathcal{O}})
satisfy the following inequality:

\lvert\lvert u_{1}-u_{2}\lvert\lvert_{H^{1}({\mathcal{O}})}\leq C\Big(\lvert\lvert f_{1}-f_{2}\lvert\lvert_{L^{2}({\mathcal{O}})}+\lvert\lvert g_{1}-g_{2}\lvert\lvert_{H^{1/2}(\partial{\mathcal{O}})}\Big),

for a constant
C
which depends only on the domain
{\mathcal{O}}
, the matrix
B
and the coefficients
\underline{\nu}
,
\overline{\nu}
in
Eq.

2.13
.

Proof.

Let
\widetilde{g}\in H^{1}({\mathcal{O}})
be such that
\widetilde{g}\lvert_{\partial{\mathcal{O}}}=g
.
Letting the change of unknown functions
w=u-\widetilde{g}\in H^{1}_{0}({\mathcal{O}})
, the considered boundary value problem
Eq.

B.5
rewrites:

(B.6)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}-\text{\rm div}(\hat{\nu}(\lvert B^{T}(x)(\nabla w+\nabla\widetilde{g})\lvert)B(x)B^{T}(x)(\nabla w+\nabla\widetilde{g}))=f&\text{in } {\mathcal{O}},\\
w=0&\text{on }\partial{\mathcal{O}}.\end{array}\right.

Let us equip
H^{1}_{0}({\mathcal{O}})
with the inner product

\langle u,v\rangle_{H^{1}_{0}({\mathcal{O}})}:=\int_{\mathcal{O}}\nabla u\cdot\nabla v\>\text{\rm d}x,

and let us introduce the operator
{\mathcal{A}}:H^{1}_{0}({\mathcal{O}})\to H^{-1}({\mathcal{O}})
defined by:

{\mathcal{A}}w =-\text{\rm div}(\hat{\nu}(\lvert B^{T}(x)(\nabla w+\nabla\widetilde{g})\lvert)B(x)B^{T}(x)(\nabla w+\nabla\widetilde{g})).

We aim to show that
{\mathcal{A}}
is strongly monotone; to this end, let
u_{1},u_{2}\in H^{1}_{0}({\mathcal{O}})
be arbitrary functions; introducing the shorthand
U_{i}=B^{T}(x)(\nabla u_{i}+\nabla\widetilde{g})\in L^{2}({\mathcal{O}})^{d}
,
i=1,2
, an elementary calculation yields:

\left\langle{\mathcal{A}}u_{1}-{\mathcal{A}}u_{2},u_{1}-u_{2}\right\rangle_{H^{-1}({\mathcal{O}}),H^{1}_{0}({\mathcal{O}})}\\
\begin{array}[]{>{\displaystyle}cc>{\displaystyle}l}&\hskip-128.0374pt=&\hskip-56.9055pt\int_{\mathcal{O}}\Big(\hat{\nu}(\lvert U_{1}\lvert)U_{1}-\hat{\nu}(\lvert U_{2}\lvert)U_{2}\Big)\cdot(U_{1}-U_{2})\>\text{\rm d}x\\[10.00002pt]
&\hskip-128.0374pt=&\hskip-56.9055pt\underline{\nu}\int_{\mathcal{O}}\lvert U_{1}-U_{2}\lvert^{2}\>\text{\rm d}x+\int_{\mathcal{O}}\Big((\hat{\nu}(\lvert U_{1}\lvert)-\underline{\nu})U_{1}-(\hat{\nu}(\lvert U_{2}\lvert)-\underline{\nu})U_{2}\Big)\cdot(U_{1}-U_{2})\>\text{\rm d}x\\[10.00002pt]
&\hskip-128.0374pt=&\hskip-56.9055pt\underline{\nu}\int_{\mathcal{O}}\lvert U_{1}-U_{2}\lvert^{2}\>\text{\rm d}x\\[10.00002pt]
&&\hskip-56.9055pt+\int_{\mathcal{O}}\Big((\hat{\nu}(\lvert U_{1}\lvert)-\underline{\nu})\lvert U_{1}\lvert^{2}+(\hat{\nu}(\lvert U_{2}\lvert)-\underline{\nu})\lvert U_{2}\lvert^{2}-(\hat{\nu}(\lvert U_{1}\lvert)+\hat{\nu}(\lvert U_{2}\lvert)-2\underline{\nu})U_{1}\cdot U_{2}\Big)\>\text{\rm d}x\\[10.00002pt]
&\hskip-128.0374pt\geq&\hskip-56.9055pt\underline{\nu}\int_{\mathcal{O}}\lvert U_{1}-U_{2}\lvert^{2}\>\text{\rm d}x\\[10.00002pt]
&&\hskip-56.9055pt+\int_{\mathcal{O}}\Big((\hat{\nu}(\lvert U_{1}\lvert)-\underline{\nu})\lvert U_{1}\lvert^{2}+(\hat{\nu}(\lvert U_{2}\lvert)-\underline{\nu})\lvert U_{2}\lvert^{2}-(\hat{\nu}(\lvert U_{1}\lvert)+\hat{\nu}(\lvert U_{2}\lvert)-2\underline{\nu})\lvert U_{1}\lvert\lvert U_{2}\lvert\Big)\>\text{\rm d}x\\[10.00002pt]
&\hskip-128.0374pt=&\hskip-56.9055pt\underline{\nu}\int_{\mathcal{O}}\lvert U_{1}-U_{2}\lvert^{2}\>\text{\rm d}x+\int_{\mathcal{O}}\Big(\hat{\nu}(\lvert U_{1}\lvert)\lvert U_{1}\lvert-\hat{\nu}(\lvert U_{2}\lvert)\lvert U_{2}\lvert\Big)(\lvert U_{1}\lvert-\lvert U_{2}\lvert)\>\text{\rm d}x\\[10.00002pt]
&&\hskip-56.9055pt-\underline{\nu}\int_{{\mathcal{O}}}\lvert\lvert U_{1}\lvert-\lvert U_{2}\lvert\lvert^{2}\>\text{\rm d}x\\[10.00002pt]
&\hskip-128.0374pt\geq&\hskip-56.9055pt\underline{\nu}\>\lvert\lvert U_{1}-U_{2}\lvert\lvert^{2}_{L^{2}({\mathcal{O}})^{d}},\end{array}

where we have used the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality to pass from the third to the fourth line and
Eq.

2.13
to obtain the last line.
Now, by definition of
U_{1}
,
U_{2}
it holds:

\lvert\lvert U_{1}-U_{2}\lvert\lvert^{2}_{L^{2}({\mathcal{O}})^{d}}=\int_{{\mathcal{O}}}B(x)B^{T}(x)(\nabla u_{1}-\nabla u_{2})\cdot(\nabla u_{1}-\nabla u_{2})\>\text{\rm d}x.

Besides, since
B
is a smooth matrix-valued function on
\overline{{\mathcal{O}}}
such that
B(x)
is invertible for all
x\in\overline{{\mathcal{O}}}
, there exists a constant
\underline{\gamma}>0
such that

\forall x\in\overline{{\mathcal{O}}},\>\forall\xi\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d},\quad B(x)B^{T}(x)\xi\cdot\xi>\underline{\gamma}|\xi|^{2}.

We eventually infer from these facts that:

\left\langle{\mathcal{A}}u_{1}-{\mathcal{A}}u_{2},u_{1}-u_{2}\right\rangle_{H^{-1}({\mathcal{O}}),H^{1}_{0}({\mathcal{O}})}\geq\underline{\nu}\underline{\gamma}\lvert\lvert\nabla u_{1}-\nabla u_{2}\lvert\lvert_{L^{2}({\mathcal{O}})^{d}}^{2},

which precisely expresses that strong monotonicity of
{\mathcal{A}}
.

A similar calculation shows that
{\mathcal{A}}:H^{1}_{0}({\mathcal{O}})\to H^{-1}({\mathcal{O}})
is a Lipschitz continuous operator, with a Lipschitz constant depending only on
\overline{\nu}
and the matrix
B(x)
.

It follows from
Theorem

B.1
that the problem
Eq.

B.6
has a unique solution
w
, with Lipschitz continuous dependence on the data function
f
,
which readily implies the desired statement about
Eq.

B.5
.
∎

Our study of
Eq.

2.22
rests on results about the well-posedness of non linear evolution problems which stem from the general theory of maximal monotone operators.
For simplicity, we limit ourselves with a relatively simple statement which is enough for our purpose, see e.g. Chap. 32 of
[
75
]
(and notably Th. 32.D).

Theorem B.2
.

Let
V\subset H\subset V^{*}
be an evolution triple.
For every
t\in(0,T)
, let
{\mathcal{A}}(t):V\to V^{*}
be a strongly monotone and Lipschitz continuous operator, whose monotonicity and Lipschitz constants do not depend on
t
. Then for each
f\in L^{2}(0,T;V^{*})
, the evolution equation

\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}+{\mathcal{A}}u=f&\text{in }(0,T),\\
u(0)=u(T),\end{array}\right.

has a unique solution
u
in
W(0,T;V,H)
.

Remark B.1
.

Strictly speaking, the statement of Th. 32.D in
[
75
]
features a non linear operator
{\mathcal{A}}
which is independent of time.
However, inspection of the proof of this result reveals that it carries over mutatis mutandis to the time-dependent case, provided the strong monotonicity and Lipschitz constants of
{\mathcal{A}}
are independent of time.

B.3.
Proof of
Theorem

4.1

We proceed in four steps. In the first three steps, we assume that there exists a solution
u\in W_{\text{\rm per}}
to the variational problem
Eq.

4.2
-
Eq.

4.3
,
and we construct an equivalent problem for
u
, see
Eq.

B.9
; the well-posedness of the latter results from the application of
Theorem

B.2
.
This procedure implies, in particular, that a solution
u
to
Eq.

4.2
is unique, if it exists.
In the final step, we construct a solution to
Eq.

4.2
from the solution to
Eq.

B.9
, thus concluding about the existence part of the statement.

Step 1: We use a change of variables to obtain an equivalent variational problem to
Eq.

4.3
featuring a fixed arrangement of phases within
D
.

Let us assume that
Eq.

4.2
-
Eq.

4.3
has a solution
u
in the space
W_{\text{\rm per}}
defined by
Eq.

4.1
.
Then, for a.e.
t\in(0,T)
, the following identity is satisfied for all test functions
w\in H^{1}_{0}(D)
:

(B.7)

\int_{D}\sigma_{\Omega(t)}(x)\left( \frac{\partial u}{\partial t}(t,x)+v(t,x)\cdot\nabla u(t,x)\right)w(x)\>\text{\rm d}x\\
+\int_{D}\nu_{\Omega(t)}(x,\lvert\nabla u(t,x)\lvert)\nabla u(t,x)\cdot\nabla w(x)\>\text{\rm d}x=\int_{D}f(t,x)w(x)\>\text{\rm d}x.

We introduce the transported function

\overline{u}\in L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}_{0}(D)),\quad\overline{u}(t,x):=u(t,\varphi_{t}(x))\text{ for }t\in[0,T]\text{ and }x\in D.

By considering test functions of the form
\overline{w}=w\circ\varphi_{t}^{-1}
,
w\in H^{1}_{0}(D)
, a change of variables based on
\varphi_{t}
in
Eq.

B.7
yields, for a.e.
t\in(0,T)
:

(B.8)

\forall w\in H^{1}_{0}(D),\>\>\int_{D}\sigma_{\Omega}(x) \frac{\partial\overline{u}}{\partial t}(t,x)w(x)\>\text{\rm d}x\\
+\int_{D}\nu_{\Omega}(x,\lvert B_{t}^{T}(x)\nabla\overline{u}(t,x)\lvert)B_{t}(x)B^{T}_{t}(x)\nabla\overline{u}(t,x)\cdot\nabla w(x)\>\text{\rm d}x=\int_{D}f(t,\varphi_{t}(x))w(x)\>\text{\rm d}x,

where, we have defined the smooth matrix-valued function:

B_{t}(x)=\nabla\varphi_{t}^{-1}(x),

and we have used the calculus identities
Eq.

2.18
.
The evolution problem
Eq.

B.8
is of mixed parabolic-elliptic problem, since
\sigma_{\Omega}
vanishes outside the subset
D_{\text{\rm mag}}
of
D
, where it takes the value
\sigma_{m}>0
.

Step 2: We reformulate
Eq.

B.8
as a parabolic evolution problem posed on
D_{\text{\rm mag}}
.

By using test functions
w
with compact support inside
D\setminus\overline{D_{\text{\rm mag}}}
in
Eq.

B.8
, we see that
\overline{u}
satisfies:

-\text{\rm div}\Big(\nu_{\Omega}(x,\lvert B_{t}^{T}(x)\nabla\overline{u}(t,x)\lvert)B_{t}(x)B^{T}_{t}(x)\nabla\overline{u}(t,x)\Big)=f(t,\varphi_{t}(x))\text{ in } D\setminus\overline{D_{\text{\rm mag}}}.

On the other hand, an integration by parts in
Eq.

B.8
reveals that the spatial restriction of
\overline{u}(t,\cdot)
to
D_{\text{\rm mag}}
, which we still denote by
\overline{u}
, satisfies the following problem:

(B.9)

\forall w\in H^{1}(D_{\text{\rm mag}}),\quad\sigma_{m}\int_{D_{\text{\rm mag}}} \frac{\partial\overline{u}}{\partial t}(t,x)w(x)\>\text{\rm d}x+\left\langle{\mathcal{A}}\overline{u},w\right\rangle_{H^{1}(D_{\text{\rm mag}})^{*},H^{1}(D_{\text{\rm mag}})}\\
+\left\langle{\mathcal{B}}\overline{u},w\right\rangle_{H^{1}(D_{\text{\rm mag}})^{*},H^{1}(D_{\text{\rm mag}})}=\int_{D_{\text{\rm mag}}}f(t,\varphi_{t}(x))w(x)\>\text{\rm d}x.

Here, we have introduced the linear operator
{\mathcal{A}}:H^{1}(D_{\text{\rm mag}})\to H^{1}(D_{\text{\rm mag}})^{*}
and the non linear operator
{\mathcal{B}}:H^{1}(D_{\text{\rm mag}})\to H^{1}(D_{\text{\rm mag}})^{*}
defined by, respectively:

\forall u,w\in H^{1}(D_{\text{\rm mag}}),\>\>\left\langle{\mathcal{A}}u,w\right\rangle_{H^{1}(D_{\text{\rm mag}})^{*},H^{1}(D_{\text{\rm mag}})}=\nu_{m}\int_{D_{\text{\rm mag}}}B_{t}(x)B_{t}^{T}(x)\nabla u(t,x)\cdot\nabla w(x)\>\text{\rm d}x,

and

\forall u,v\in H^{1}(D_{\text{\rm mag}}),\quad\left\langle{\mathcal{B}}u,w\right\rangle_{H^{1}(D_{\text{\rm mag}})^{*},H^{1}(D_{\text{\rm mag}})}=\\
-\int_{\partial D_{\text{\rm mag}}}\Big(\nu_{\Omega}(x,\lvert B_{t}^{T}(x)\nabla{\mathcal{L}}_{t}u(t,x)\lvert)B_{t}(x)B_{t}^{T}(x)\nabla{\mathcal{L}}_{t}u(t,x)\Big)\cdot n(x)\>w(x)\>\text{\rm d}s(x),

where
n
is the unit normal vector to
\partial D_{\text{\rm mag}}
pointing outward
D_{\text{\rm mag}}
and for
z\in H^{1}(D_{\text{\rm mag}})
,
{\mathcal{L}}_{t}z\in H^{1}(D\setminus\overline{D_{\text{\rm mag}}})
is the unique solution to the boundary value problem:

(B.10)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}-\text{\rm div}(\nu(x,|B^{T}_{t}(x)\nabla{\mathcal{L}}_{t}z(x)|)B_{t}(x)B_{t}^{T}(x)\nabla{\mathcal{L}}_{t}z(x))=f(t,x)&\text{in } D\setminus\overline{D_{\text{\rm mag}}},\\
{\mathcal{L}}_{t}z=z&\text{on }\partial D_{\text{\rm mag}},\\
{\mathcal{L}}_{t}z=0&\text{on }\partial D.\end{array}\right.

Let us then analyze
{\mathcal{A}}
and
{\mathcal{B}}
.

•

The linear operator
{\mathcal{A}}
is continuous and monotone; more precisely, it holds:

\forall u\in H^{1}(D_{\text{\rm mag}}),\quad\left\langle{\mathcal{A}}u,u\right\rangle_{H^{1}(D_{\text{\rm mag}})^{*},H^{1}(D_{\text{\rm mag}})}\geq C\left\lvert\left\lvert\nabla u\right\lvert\right\lvert^{2}_{L^{2}(D_{\text{\rm mag}})^{d}}.

Here and throughout the rest of the proof,
C
stands for a positive constant which may change from one line to the other but is independent of time.
The existence of such a constant in the above inequality is guaranteed by the smoothness and invertibility of the mapping
\varphi
.

•

The operator
{\mathcal{B}}
is Lipschitz continuous, with a Lipschitz constant independent of
t
,
as readily follows from the application of
Lemma

B.2
to the non linear operator
{\mathcal{L}}_{t}:H^{1}(D_{\text{\rm mag}})\to H^{1}(D\setminus\overline{D_{\text{\rm mag}}})
.

Moreover, for all elements
u_{1}
,
u_{2}\in H^{1}(D_{\text{\rm mag}})
, several integrations by parts followed by a calculation identical to that conducted in the proof of
Lemma

B.2
yield:

\langle{\mathcal{B}}u_{1}-{\mathcal{B}}u_{2},u_{1}-u_{2}\rangle_{H^{1}(D_{\text{\rm mag}})^{*},H^{1}(D_{\text{\rm mag}})}\geq C\lvert\lvert\nabla{\mathcal{L}}_{t}u_{1}-\nabla{\mathcal{L}}_{t}u_{2}\lvert\lvert^{2}_{L^{2}(D\setminus\overline{D_{\text{\rm mag}}})^{d}}.

Since the mappings
u\mapsto\lvert\lvert\nabla u\lvert\lvert_{L^{2}(D)^{d}}
and
u\mapsto\lvert\lvert u\lvert\lvert_{H^{1}(D\setminus\overline{D_{\text{\rm mag}}})}
are equivalent norms on the subspace of
H^{1}(D\setminus\overline{D_{\text{\rm mag}}})
functions with null trace on
\partial D
, we obtain:

\begin{array}[]{ccl}\langle{\mathcal{B}}u_{1}-{\mathcal{B}}u_{2},u_{1}-u_{2}\rangle_{H^{1}(D_{\text{\rm mag}})^{*},H^{1}(D_{\text{\rm mag}})}&\geq&C\lvert\lvert u_{1}-u_{2}\lvert\lvert^{2}_{H^{1}(D\setminus\overline{D_{\text{\rm mag}}})}\\
&\geq&C\lvert\lvert u_{1}-u_{2}\lvert\lvert^{2}_{H^{1/2}(\partial D_{\text{\rm mag}})},\end{array}

where the second line follows from the trace inequality.

The combination of both points reveals that the sum
{\mathcal{A}}+{\mathcal{B}}
is Lipschitz continuous; it is also strongly monotone since, for all functions
u_{1}
,
u_{2}\in H^{1}(D_{\text{\rm mag}})
, it holds:

\begin{array}[]{>{\displaystyle}cc>{\displaystyle}l}\left\langle({\mathcal{A}}+{\mathcal{B}})u_{1}-({\mathcal{A}}+{\mathcal{B}})u_{2},u_{1}-u_{2}\right\rangle_{H^{1}(D_{\text{\rm mag}})^{*},H^{1}(D_{\text{\rm mag}})}&\geq&C\left(\left\lvert\left\lvert\nabla(u_{1}-u_{2})\right\lvert\right\lvert^{2}_{L^{2}(D_{\text{\rm mag}})^{d}}+\lvert\lvert u_{1}-u_{2}\lvert\lvert^{2}_{H^{1/2}(\partial D_{\text{\rm mag}})}\right)\\
&\geq&C\left\lvert\left\lvert u_{1}-u_{2}\right\lvert\right\lvert_{H^{1}(D_{\text{\rm mag}})}^{2},\end{array}

as follows from (an avatar of) Poincaré’s inequality. Moreover, the Lipschitz and strong monotonicity constants of
({\mathcal{A}}+{\mathcal{B}})
are independent of time.

Step 3: We use the abstract
Theorem

B.2
.

The application of
Theorem

B.2
reveals that the variational problem
Eq.

B.9
has a unique solution
\overline{u}\in W(0,T;H^{1}(D_{\text{\rm mag}}),L^{2}(D_{\text{\rm mag}}))
such that

\overline{u}(t=0,\cdot)=\overline{u}(t=T,\cdot)
.
This almost readily implies that the solution
u\in W_{\text{\rm per}}
to the original evolution problem
Eq.

4.2
is unique, when it exists.
Indeed, if
u_{i}
,
i=1,2
are two such solutions, let us define the functions
\overline{u_{i}}\in L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}_{0}(D))
and
z_{i}\in L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}(D_{\text{\rm mag}}))
by:

\overline{u_{i}}(t,x)=u_{i}(t,\varphi_{t}(x)),\text{ and }z_{i}(t,\cdot)=\overline{u_{i}}(t,\cdot)\lvert_{D_{\text{\rm mag}}}.

As a result of the previous steps, both functions
z_{i}
belong to
W(0,T;H^{1}(D_{\text{\rm mag}}),L^{2}(D_{\text{\rm mag}}))
and satisfy the evolution equation
Eq.

B.9
with the time periodic condition
z_{i}(t=0,\cdot)=z_{i}(t=T,\cdot)
,
i=1,2
. As a result,
z_{1}=z_{2}
, i.e. for a.e.
t\in[0,T]
,
\overline{u_{1}}(t,\cdot)
and
\overline{u_{2}}(t,\cdot)
coincide on
D_{\text{\rm mag}}
. Moreover, since
\overline{u_{i}}(t,\cdot)\lvert_{D\setminus\overline{D_{\text{\rm mag}}}}={\mathcal{L}}_{t}z_{i}
on
D\setminus\overline{D_{\text{\rm mag}}}
, it immediately follows that
\overline{u_{1}} =\overline{u_{2}}
in
L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}_{0}(D))
, so that
u_{1}=u_{2}
.

Summarizing, we have proved that there exists at most one solution
u\in W_{\text{\rm per}}
to
Eq.

B.9
.

Step 4: We construct a solution
u\in W_{\text{\rm per}}
to
Eq.

B.9
.

This task essentially relies on the previous three steps.
Let
z\in W(0,T;H^{1}(D_{\text{\rm mag}}),L^{2}(D_{\text{\rm mag}}))
be the unique solution to the well-posed variational problem
Eq.

B.9
equipped with time periodic boundary conditions:

z(t=0,\cdot)=z(t=T,\cdot)\text{ in }L^{2}(D_{\text{\rm mag}}).

In particular,
z(t,\cdot)
is an
H^{1}(D_{\text{\rm mag}})
function for a.e.
t\in[0,T]
, and so we may introduce the unique solution

z_{\text{\rm ext}}(t,\cdot)={\mathcal{L}}_{t}z(t,\cdot)\in H^{1}(D\setminus\overline{D_{\text{\rm mag}}})
to the version of
Eq.

B.10
featuring Dirichlet data
z(t,\cdot)
on
\partial D_{\text{\rm mag}}
.
Let now
\overline{u}(t,\cdot)\in H^{1}_{0}(D)
be defined by:

\overline{u}(t,x)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}z(t,x)&\text{if } x\in D_{\text{\rm mag}},\\
z_{\text{\rm ext}}(t,x)&\text{if } x\in D\setminus\overline{D_{\text{\rm mag}}}.\\
\end{array}\right.

By construction,
\overline{u}
belongs to
L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}_{0}(D))
, and it is one solution to
Eq.

B.8
.

It is also easily seen that
\sigma_{\Omega}\frac{\partial\overline{u}}{\partial t}\in L^{2}(0,T;H^{-1}(D))
, since
\frac{\partial z}{\partial t}\in L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}(D_{\text{\rm mag}})^{*})
and for a.e.
t\in[0,T]
and for any test function
\psi\in H^{1}_{0}(D)
,

\left\langle\sigma_{\Omega}\frac{\partial\overline{u}}{\partial t}(t,\cdot),\psi\right\rangle_{H^{-1}(D),H^{1}_{0}(D)}=\sigma_{m}\left\langle\frac{\partial z}{\partial t}(t,\cdot),\psi\right\rangle_{H^{1}(D_{\text{\rm mag}})^{*},H^{1}(D_{\text{\rm mag}})}.

Eventually, let
u\in W_{\text{\rm per}}
be defined by

u(t,x)=\overline{u}(t,\varphi_{t}^{-1}(x)),\quad t\in[0,T],\>\>x\in D.

By reusing the calculations conducted in the first step, we see that
u
is one solution to
Eq.

B.9
. The Lipschitz dependence of this function on the data function
f
results immediately from the Lipschitz dependence of the solutions to the problems
Eqs.

B.10
and
B.9
with respect to their right-hand sides, and we omit the details for brevity. This terminates the proof.

∎
