Quantum Algorithm for Simulating Hamiltonian Dynamics with an Off-diagonal Series Expansion
Amir Kalev, Itay Hen

Abstract
We propose an efficient quantum algorithm for simulating the dynamics of general Hamiltonian systems. Our technique is based on a power series expansion of the time-evolution operator in its off-diagonal terms. The expansion decouples the dynamics due to the diagonal component of the Hamiltonian from the dynamics generated by its off-diagonal part, which we encode using the linear combination of unitaries technique. Our method has an optimal dependence on the desired precision and, as we illustrate, generally requires considerably fewer resources than the current state-of-the-art. We provide an analysis of resource costs for several sample models.



1
Introduction

Simulating the dynamics of quantum many-body systems is a central challenge in Physics, Chemistry and the Material Sciences as well as in other areas of science and technology.
While for classical algorithms this task is in general intractable, quantum circuits offer a way around the classical bottlenecks by way of ‘circuitizing’ the time evolution of the system in question. However, present-day quantum computing devices allow for the programming of only small and noisy quantum circuits, a state of matters that places severe constraints on the types of applications these devices may be used for in practice. The qubit and gate costs of circuitization procedures have therefore rightfully become key factors in determining the feasibility of any potential application and increasingly more efficient algorithms are continuously being devised.

We propose a novel approach to resource-efficient Hamiltonian dynamics simulations on quantum circuits that we argue offers certain advantages, which directly translate to a shorter algorithm runtime, over state-of-the-art quantum simulation algorithms
[
Berry1
,
2018arXiv180500675H
]
(see Sec.
for a detailed comparison). We accomplish this by utilizing a series expansion of the quantum time-evolution operator in its off-diagonal elements wherein the operator is expanded around its diagonal component
[
ODE
,
ODE2
,
pmr
]
. This expansion allows one to effectively integrate out the diagonal component of the evolution, thereby reducing the overall gate and qubit complexities of the algorithm as compared to existing methods.

In our approach, the time evolution is broken up into identical short-time segments, each of which is accurately approximated
using a number of terms in the off-diagonal series that is logarithmic in the inverse of the required precision. Each segment is then executed with the help of the linear combination of unitaries (LCU) lemma
[
Berry1
]
.
Our algorithm enables the simulation of a wide range of realistic models, including systems of spins, bosons or fermions.

The paper is organized as follows. In Sec.
2
, we introduce the off-diagonal expansion insofar as it applies to the time-evolution operator. In Sec.
3
, we present the Hamiltonian dynamics algorithm that we construct based on the expansion and in Sec.
we provide a comparison between the present scheme and two of the leading approaches to quantum simulations, the Taylor-series based approach of Berry
et al.

[
Berry1
]
and the interaction-picture representation approach devised by Low and Wiebe
[
2018arXiv180500675H
]
. We examine several examples in some detail. A summary and some conclusions are given in Sec.
.

2
Off-diagonal series expansion of the time-evolution operator

We next derive an expansion of the time evolution operator based on the off-diagonal series expansion recently introduced in Refs.
[
ODE
,
ODE2
,
pmr
]
in the context of quantum Monte Carlo simulations.
While we focus in what follows on time-independent Hamiltonians for simplicity, we note that an extension of the following derivation to include time-dependent Hamiltonians also exists
[
timeDepHamSim
]
.

2.1
Permutation matrix representation of the Hamiltonian

We begin by casting the Hamiltonian in the form

H=\sum_{i=0}^{M}D_{i}P_{i}=D_{0}+\sum_{i=1}^{M}D_{i}P_{i}\,,

(1)

where the
D_{i}
operators are diagonal in some known basis, which we will refer to as the computational basis and denote by
\{|z\rangle\}
,
P_{0}:=\mathbb{1}
, and the
P_{i}
operators (for
i>0
) are permutation operators, i.e.,
P_{i}|z\rangle=|z^{\prime}(i,z)\rangle
where
z^{\prime}\neq z
, i.e., they do not have any fixed points (equivalently, their diagonal elements are all zero). While the above formulation may appear restrictive it is important to note that any Hamiltonian can be written in this form. In particular, for models of spin-
1/2
particles (qubits), the
D_{i}
’s are diagonal in the Pauli-
Z
basis, and the
P_{i}
’s are a tensor products of Pauli-
X
operators,
P_{i}\in\{\mathbb{1},X\}^{\otimes N}
where
N
is the number of spins.

We will refer to the principal diagonal matrix
D_{0}
as the diagonal component of the Hamiltonian, while the set
\{D_{i}P_{i}\}_{i=1}^{M}
of off-diagonal operators (in the computational basis) give the system its ‘off-diagonal dimension’. We will call ‘diagonal energies’ the (real) numbers obtained by acting with
D_{0}
on computational basis states:
D_{0}|z\rangle=E_{z}|z\rangle
. Similarly, by applying the generalized permutation operator
D_{i}P_{i}
on a basis state, we obtain
D_{i}P_{i}|z\rangle=d_{i}(z^{\prime})|z^{\prime}\rangle
, where
d_{i}(z^{\prime})
will be in general a complex number (
z^{\prime}
depends on
z
and
i
). With these notations in hand, we move on to discuss the off-diagonal series expansion of the time-evolution operator.

2.2
Expansion of the time-evolution operator

We next consider the evolution of a state under a time-independent Hamiltonian
H
for time
t
. We expand the time evolution operator
{\textrm{e}}^{-iHt}
using the off-diagonal series expansion.

We first consider the action of
{\textrm{e}}^{-iHt}
on a basis state
|z\rangle
:

\displaystyle{\textrm{e}}^{-iHt}|z\rangle=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-it)^{n}}{n!}H^{n}|z\rangle=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-it)^{n}}{n!}\Big(\sum_{i=0}^{M}D_{i}P_{i}\Big)^{n}|z\rangle=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-it)^{n}}{n!}\sum_{{S}_{j}^{(n)}\in\mathcal{S}_{n}}{S}_{j}^{(n)}|z\rangle\,,

(2)

where in the last step we have also expanded the multinomial
(\sum_{i}D_{i}P_{i})^{n}
, and
\mathcal{S}_{n}
denotes the set of all
(M+1)^{n}
operators that appear in the expansion of the multinomial
(\sum_{i}D_{i}P_{i})^{n}
. We proceed by ‘stripping’ all the diagonal operators off the sequences
{S}_{j}^{(n)}
. We do so by evaluating their action on the relevant basis states, leaving only the off-diagonal operators unevaluated inside the sequence (for example, for the
n=2
sequence
D_{1}P_{1}D_{0}
we write
D_{1}P_{1}D_{0}|z\rangle=E_{z}D_{1}P_{1}|z\rangle=E_{z}D_{1}|z_{1}\rangle=E_{z}d_{1}(z_{1})|z_{1}\rangle=E_{z}d_{1}(z_{1})P_{1}|z\rangle
, where
|z_{1}\rangle=P_{1}|z\rangle
). Collecting all terms together, we arrive at:

\displaystyle{\textrm{e}}^{-iHt}|z\rangle=\sum_{q=0}^{\infty}\sum_{{\bf{i}}_{q}}d_{{\bf i}_{q}}P_{{\bf{i}}_{q}}|z\rangle\Bigl(\sum_{n=q}^{\infty}\frac{(-it)^{n}}{n!}{\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k_{0},\ldots,k_{q}\\
\text{s.t.}\sum_{i}k_{i}=n-q\end{subarray}}{\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!}{E^{k_{0}}_{z}\cdots E^{k_{q}}_{z_{q}}}\Bigr)\,,

(3)

where the boldfaced index
{\bf i}_{q}=(i_{1},\ldots,i_{q})
is a tuple of indices
i_{j}
, with
j=1,\ldots,q
, each ranging from
1
to
M
and
P_{{\bf i}_{q}}:=P_{i_{q}}\cdots P_{i_{2}}P_{i_{1}}
. In addition, similar to the diagonal energy
E_{z}=\langle z|D_{0}|z\rangle
, we denote
E_{z_{j}}=\langle z_{j}|D_{0}|z_{j}\rangle
are the energies of the states
|z\rangle,|z_{1}\rangle,\ldots,|z_{q}\rangle
obtained from the action of the ordered
P_{i_{j}}
operators appearing in the sequence
P_{{\bf i}_{q}}
on
|z\rangle
, then on
|z_{1}\rangle
, and so forth. Explicitly,
P_{i_{1}}|z\rangle=|z_{1}\rangle,P_{i_{2}}|z_{1}\rangle=|z_{2}\rangle
, etc. (Note that the sequence of states, and similarly the energies, should actually be denoted
|z_{1}(z,i_{1})\rangle,|z_{2}(z,i_{1},i_{2})\rangle,\ldots
. For conciseness we will be using the abbreviations
|z_{1}\rangle,|z_{2}\rangle,\ldots
) Last, we have denoted
d_{{\bf i}_{q}}=\prod_{j=1}^{q}d_{i_{j}}(z_{j})
where

d_{i_{j}}(z_{j})=\langle z_{j}|D_{i_{j}}|z_{j}\rangle

(4)

can be considered the ‘hopping strength’ of
P_{i_{j}}
with respect to
|z_{j}\rangle
(see Ref.
[
ODE
]
for a complete and detailed derivation).

The infinite sum in parentheses in Eq. (
3
) evaluates to the efficiently calculable
divided-differences
representation
[
dd:67
,
deboor:05
]

\sum_{n=q}^{\infty}\frac{(-it)^{n}}{n!}{\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k_{0},\ldots,k_{q}\\
\text{s.t.}\sum_{i}k_{i}=n-q\end{subarray}}{\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!}{E^{k_{0}}_{z}\cdots E^{k_{q}}_{z_{q}}}={\textrm{e}}^{-it[E_{z},\ldots,E_{z_{q}}]}\,,

(5)

where the complex coefficient
{\textrm{e}}^{-it[E_{z},\ldots,E_{z_{q}}]}
is the
divided difference of the exponential function
over the multi-set of the energies
\{E_{z},\ldots,E_{z_{q}}\}

[
dd:67
,
deboor:05
]
(more details can be found in Appendix
).

We may therefore write

{\textrm{e}}^{-iHt}|z\rangle=V_{z}(t)|z\rangle\,,

(6)

where

V_{z}(t)=\sum_{q=0}^{\infty}\sum_{{\bf i}_{q}}\alpha_{{\bf i}_{q}}^{(z)}(t)P_{{\bf i}_{q}}

(7)

and where we have denoted

\alpha_{{\bf i}_{q}}^{(z)}(t)={\textrm{e}}^{-it[E_{z},\ldots,E_{z_{q}}]}d_{{\bf i}_{q}}.

(8)

(In the special case of
q=0
,
\alpha_{0}^{(z)}(t)={\textrm{e}}^{-itE_{z}}
.) In Appendix
, we show that one can pull out a global phase from
{\textrm{e}}^{-it[E_{z},\ldots,E_{z_{q}}]}
to obtain
{\textrm{e}}^{-itE_{z}}{\textrm{e}}^{-it[\Delta E_{z},\ldots,\Delta E_{z_{q}}]}
where
\Delta E_{z_{j}}=E_{z_{j}}-E_{z}
(and specifically
\Delta E_{z}=0
). Therefore, we can write
\alpha_{{\bf i}_{q}}^{(z)}(t)
as:

\alpha_{{\bf i}_{q}}^{(z)}(t)={\textrm{e}}^{-itE_{z}}{\textrm{e}}^{-it[\Delta E_{z},\ldots,\Delta E_{z_{q}}]}d_{{\bf i}_{q}}\,,

(9)

where the divided-difference inputs are now energy differences rather than total diagonal energies.

3
The Hamiltonian dynamics algorithm

3.1
Preliminaries

We first set some definitions and notations that will be used in the description of the algorithm.
We denote the max norm of a matrix
A
by
\|A\|_{\rm max}=\max_{i,j}|A_{ij}|
, where
A_{ij}
are the matrix elements of
A
in the computational basis. For every diagonal matrix
D_{i}
(with
i>0
) we define the bounds
\Gamma_{i}\geq\|D_{i}\|_{\rm max}
, and denote
\Gamma_{{\bf i}_{q}}=\prod_{j=1}^{q}\Gamma_{i_{j}}
. We define the dimensionless time
T=t\Gamma
with
\Gamma=\sum_{i=1}^{M}\Gamma_{i}
, the repetition number
r=\lceil T/\ln(2)\rceil
, and the short time interval
\Delta t=t/r\approx\ln(2)/\sum_{i=1}^{M}\Gamma_{i}
.

3.2
Decomposition to short-time evolutions

To simulate the time evolution of
{\textrm{e}}^{-iHt}
, we execute
r
times in succession a short-time circuit for the operator

U={\textrm{e}}^{-iH\Delta t}\,.

(10)

Hereafter we omit the explicit dependence on
\Delta t
for brevity. We write

\displaystyle U

\displaystyle=U\sum_{z}|z\rangle\langle z|=\sum_{z}U|z\rangle\langle z|=\sum_{z}V_{z}|z\rangle\langle z|,

(11)

where
V_{z}
is given by Eq. (
7
) upon replacing
t
with
\Delta t
.
We can rewrite
U
as follows:

\displaystyle U=\sum_{z}{\textrm{e}}^{-i\Delta tE_{z}}\sum_{q=0}^{\infty}\sum_{{\bf i}_{q}}{\textrm{e}}^{-i\Delta t[\Delta E_{z},\ldots,\Delta E_{z_{q}}]}d_{{\bf i}_{q}}P_{{\bf i}_{q}}|z\rangle\langle z|

\displaystyle=\Big(\sum_{z}\sum_{q=0}^{\infty}\sum_{{\bf i}_{q}}{\textrm{e}}^{-i\Delta t[\Delta E_{z},\ldots,\Delta E_{z_{q}}]}d_{{\bf i}_{q}}P_{{\bf i}_{q}}|z\rangle\langle z|\Big){\textrm{e}}^{-i\Delta tD_{0}}:=U_{{\rm od}}{\textrm{e}}^{-i\Delta tD_{0}}\,.

(12)

We thus find that the off-diagonal expansion enables the effective decoupling of the evolution due to the diagonal part of the Hamiltonian from the evolution due its off-diagonal part, allowing us
U
as a product of
U_{{\rm od}}
and
{\textrm{e}}^{-i\Delta tD_{0}}
. In the special case where the off-diagonal part of the Hamiltonian is zero (thus,
d_{{\bf i}_{q}}=0
for all
{{\bf i}_{q}}
), our method reduces directly to simulating diagonal Hamiltonians on a quantum computer. The circuit implementation of the diagonal unitary
{\textrm{e}}^{-i\Delta tD_{0}}
can be done with a gate cost
{\cal O}(C_{D_{0}})
where
C_{D_{0}}
is the gate cost of calculating a matrix element of
D_{0}

[
NielsenChuang
]
(see Appendix
for more details). This cost depends only of the locality of
D_{0}
, and is independent of its norm.

To simulate
U_{{\rm od}}
we will use the LCU technique
[
Berry1
]
, starting with writing
U_{{\rm od}}
as a sum of unitary operators. To do that, we first note that
|{\textrm{e}}^{-i\Delta t[\Delta E_{z_{q}},\ldots,\Delta E_{z}]}|\leq\Delta t^{q}/q!
(this follows from the mean-value theorem for divided differences
[
deboor:05
]
). In addition,
d_{{\bf i}_{q}}/\Gamma_{{\bf i}_{q}}
are complex numbers lying inside the unit circle. Therefore, the norm of the complex number

\beta_{{\bf i}_{q}}^{(z)}=\frac{q!}{\Gamma_{{\bf i}_{q}}\Delta t^{q}}{\textrm{e}}^{-i\Delta t[\Delta E_{z},\ldots,\Delta E_{z_{q}}]}d_{{\bf i}_{q}}

(13)

is not larger than 1. We can thus write
\beta_{{\bf i}_{q}}^{(z)}
as the average of two phases

\displaystyle\beta_{{\bf i}_{q}}^{(z)}=\cos\phi_{{\bf i}_{q}}^{(z)}{\textrm{e}}^{i\chi_{{\bf i}_{q}}^{(z)}}=\frac{1}{2}\Big({\textrm{e}}^{i(\chi_{{\bf i}_{q}}^{(z)}+\phi_{{\bf i}_{q}}^{(z)})}+{\textrm{e}}^{i(\chi_{{\bf i}_{q}}^{(z)}-\phi_{{\bf i}_{q}}^{(z)})}\Big).

(14)

Using this notation, we can write
U_{\rm od}
as

U_{\rm od}=\sum_{k=0,1}\sum_{q=0}^{\infty}\sum_{{\bf i}_{q}}\frac{\Gamma_{{\bf i}_{q}}\Delta t^{q}}{2q!}U_{{\bf i}_{q}}^{(k)}\,,

(15)

where

\displaystyle U_{{\bf i}_{q}}^{(k)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{z}{\textrm{e}}^{i(\chi_{{\bf i}_{q}}^{(z)}+(-1)^{k}\phi_{{\bf i}_{q}}^{(z)})}P_{{\bf i}_{q}}|z\rangle\langle z|=P_{{\bf i}_{q}}\Phi_{{\bf i}_{q}}^{(k)}\,,

(16)

and
\Phi_{{\bf i}_{q}}^{(k)}=\sum_{z}{\textrm{e}}^{i(\chi_{{\bf i}_{q}}^{(z)}+(-1)^{k}\phi_{{\bf i}_{q}}^{(z)})}|z\rangle\langle z|
is a (diagonal) unitary transformation. Since
P_{{\bf i}_{q}}
is a bona-fide permutation matrix, it follows that
U_{{\bf i}_{q}}^{(k)}
is a unitary transformation. Thus, Eq. (
15
) is the short-time off-diagonal evolution operator
U_{\rm od}
represented as a linear combination of unitary transformations.

3.3
The LCU setup

To simulate the evolution under
U_{\rm od}
on a finite-size circuit, we truncate the series,
Eq. (
15
), at some maximal order
Q
, which leads to the approximate

\widetilde{U}_{\rm od}=\sum_{k=0,1}\sum_{q=0}^{Q}\sum_{{\bf i}_{q}}\frac{\Gamma_{{\bf i}_{q}}\Delta t^{q}}{2q!}U_{{\bf i}_{q}}^{(k)}\,.

(17)

Since the coefficients of the off-diagonal operator expansion fall factorially with
q
(similar to the truncation of the Taylor series in Ref.
[
Berry1
]
), setting

Q={\cal O}\Bigl(\frac{\log(T/\epsilon)}{\log\log(T/\epsilon)}\Bigr)\,,

(18)

ensures
1
1

1

Formally, Eq. (
18
) should read
Q={\cal O}\Bigl(\frac{\log(T/\epsilon)}{W(\log(T/\epsilon))}\Bigr)
where
W(x)
is the
W
-Lambert function
[
Corless1996
]
. The
W
-Lambert function can be approximated as
W(x)=\log x-\log\log x+o(1)
.
that the error per evolution segment is smaller than
\epsilon/r
:

\sum_{q=Q+1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{q!}\Bigr(\frac{T}{r}\Bigr)^{q}=\sum_{q=Q+1}^{\infty}\frac{\ln(2)^{q}}{q!}\leq\frac{\epsilon}{r}\,,

(19)

where the last step follows from the inequality
q!\geq(q/e)^{q}
.
This choice ensures that the overall error is bounded by
\epsilon
(as measured by the spectral-norm of the difference between the approximation and the true dynamics).

We next provide the details of the circuit we implement to execute the LCU routine and the resource costs associated with it.

3.3.1
State preparation

The first ingredient of the LCU is the preparation of the state

\displaystyle|\psi_{0}\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{s}}\sum_{q=0}^{Q}\sum_{{\bf i}_{q}}\sqrt{\Gamma_{{\bf i}_{q}}\frac{\Delta t^{q}}{q!}}|{\bf i}_{q}\rangle\Bigl(\frac{|0\rangle+|1\rangle}{\sqrt{2}}\Bigr)\,

(20)

where
|{\bf i}_{q}\rangle=|i_{1}\rangle\cdots|i_{q}\rangle|0\rangle^{\otimes(Q-q)}
is shorthand for
Q
quantum registers, each of which has dimension
M
(equivalently, a quantum register with
\lceil Q\log(M+1)\rceil
qubits). In addition, since
\sum_{{\bf i}_{q}}\Gamma_{{\bf i}_{q}}=(\sum_{i}\Gamma_{i})^{q}

s=\sum_{q=0}^{Q}\frac{\Delta t^{q}}{q!}\sum_{{\bf i}_{q}}\Gamma_{{\bf i}_{q}}=\sum_{q=0}^{Q}\frac{(\sum_{i}\Gamma_{i}\Delta t)^{q}}{q!}\approx 2\,,

(21)

by construction [recall that
\sum_{i=1}^{M}\Gamma_{i}\Delta t\approx\ln(2)
].

We construct
|\psi_{0}\rangle
in two steps: Starting with the state
|0\rangle^{\otimes Q}
we transform the first register to the normalized version of

|0\rangle+\sqrt{\sum_{q=1}^{Q}\frac{(\Gamma\Delta t)^{q}}{q!}}|1\rangle.

(22)

where
\Gamma=\sum_{i=1}^{M}\Gamma_{i}
. Then the
|0\rangle
state of the
q
-th register (
q=2,\ldots,Q
) is transformed to the normalized version of

\sqrt{\frac{(\Gamma\Delta t)^{q-1}}{(q-1)!}}|0\rangle+\sqrt{\sum_{q^{\prime}=q}^{Q}\frac{(\Gamma\Delta t)^{q^{\prime}}}{q^{\prime}!}}|1\rangle.

(23)

conditioned on the
(q-1)
-th register being in the
|1\rangle
state. The resulting state, up to normalization, is

|0\rangle^{\otimes Q}\to\sum_{q=0}^{Q}\sqrt{\frac{(\Gamma\Delta t)^{q}}{q!}}|1\rangle^{\otimes q}|0\rangle^{\otimes(Q-q)}.

(24)

The gate cost of this step is
{\cal O}(Q)
. Next, we act on each of the registers with a unitary transformation that takes a
|1\rangle
state to the normalized version of
\sum_{i=1}^{M}\sqrt{\Gamma_{i}}|i\rangle
. Finally we apply a Hadamard transformation on the last (qubit) register, resulting in the state
|\psi_{0}\rangle
. The gate cost of this step is
{\cal O}(M)

[
1629135
]
.
Denoting the unitary transformation that takes
|0\rangle^{\otimes Q+1}
to
|\psi_{0}\rangle
by
B
, we find that the gate cost of
B
is
{\cal O}(MQ)

[
Berry1
]
.

3.3.2
Controlled-unitary transformation

The second ingredient of the LCU routine is the construction of the controlled operation

U_{C}|{\bf i}_{q}\rangle|k\rangle|z\rangle=|{\bf i}_{q}\rangle|k\rangle U_{{\bf i}_{q}}^{(k)}|z\rangle=|{\bf i}_{q}\rangle|k\rangle P_{{\bf i}_{q}}\Phi_{{\bf i}_{q}}^{(k)}|z\rangle\,,

(25)

where
|k\rangle
is a single qubit ancillary state in the computational basis. The number of ancilla qubits here is
\lceil Q\log(M+1)\rceil+1
. Equation (
25
) indicates that
U_{C}
can be carried out in two steps: a controlled-phase operation (
U_{C\Phi}
) followed by a controlled-permutation operation (
U_{CP}
).

The controlled-phase operation
U_{C\Phi}
requires a somewhat intricate calculation of non-trivial phases. We therefore carry out the required algebra with the help of additional ancillary registers and then ‘push’ the results into phases. The latter step is done by employing the unitary

\displaystyle U_{\text{ph}}|\varphi\rangle={\textrm{e}}^{-i\varphi}|\varphi\rangle\,,

(26)

whose implementation cost depends only on the precision with which we specify
\varphi
and is independent of Hamiltonian parameters
[
NielsenChuang
]
(for completeness we provide an explicit construction of
U_{\text{ph}}
in Appendix
).
With the help of the (controlled) unitary transformation

U_{\chi\phi}|{\bf i}_{q}\rangle|k\rangle|z\rangle|0\rangle=|{\bf i}_{q}\rangle|k\rangle|z\rangle|\chi_{{\bf i}_{q}}^{(z)}+(-1)^{k}\phi_{{\bf i}_{q}}^{(z)}\rangle\,,

(27)

we can write

U_{C{\Phi}}=U_{\chi\phi}^{\dagger}(\mathbb{1}\otimes U_{\text{ph}})U_{\chi\phi}\,,

(28)

so that

U_{C{\Phi}}|{\bf i}_{q}\rangle|k\rangle|z\rangle=|{\bf i}_{q}\rangle|k\rangle\Phi_{{\bf i}_{q}}^{(k)}|z\rangle\,.

(29)

This is illustrated in Fig.
