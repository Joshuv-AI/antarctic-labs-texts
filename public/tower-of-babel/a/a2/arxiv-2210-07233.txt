Shape Preserving Facial Landmarks with Graph Attention Networks
Andrés Prados-Torreblanca, José M. Buenaposada, Luis Baumela

Abstract
Top-performing landmark estimation algorithms are based on exploiting the excellent ability of large convolutional neural networks (CNNs) to represent local appearance. However, it is well known that they can only learn weak spatial relationships. To address this problem, we propose a model based on the combination of a CNN with a cascade of Graph Attention Network regressors. To this end, we introduce an encoding that jointly represents the appearance and location of facial landmarks and an attention mechanism to weigh the information according to its reliability. This is combined with a multi-task approach to initialize the location of graph nodes and a coarse-to-fine landmark description scheme. Our experiments confirm that the proposed model learns a global representation of the structure of the face, achieving top performance in popular benchmarks on head pose and landmark estimation. The improvement provided by our model is most significant in situations involving large changes in the local appearance of landmarks. The code is publicly available at \color bmv@sectioncolorhttps://github.com/andresprados/SPIGA

1
Introduction

Landmarks (or keypoints) are a widely used representation to address high-level vision tasks such as image retrieval
[
Moskvyak et al.(2021)Moskvyak, Maire, Dayoub, and
Baktashmotlagh
]
, facial expression recognition
[
Sun et al.(2019)Sun, Li, Huan, Liu, and Han
]
, face reenactment
[
Zhang et al.(2020)Zhang, Zeng, Wang, Pan, Liu, Liu, Ding, and
Fan
]
, etc.
The performance of computer vision algorithms on the final task depends, to a great extent, on the accuracy and robustness of this intermediate representation. Thus, although many algorithms with excellent performance have recently emerged, research is still very intense in this area.

Top facial landmark estimation methods may be broadly grouped into coordinate and heatmap regression approaches.
Coordinate regression approaches
directly estimate the landmark position by projecting the representation estimated by a CNN encoder onto a set of 2D coordinates
[
Feng et al.(2018)Feng, Kittler, Awais, Huber, and Wu
,
Kowalski et al.(2017)Kowalski, Naruniec, and Trzcinski
,
Feng et al.(2020)Feng, Kittler, Awais, and Wu
,
Trigeorgis et al.(2016)Trigeorgis, Snape, Nicolaou, Antonakos, and
Zafeiriou
,
Lin et al.(2021)Lin, Zhu, Wang, Liao, Qian, Lu, and Zhou
]
. They are the most efficient since they only require an encoder architecture to compute the facial representation.
The
heatmap regression approach
is based on appending multiple encoder-decoder modules to estimate a 2D data structure modeling the landmark position likelihood, the heatmap
[
Honari et al.(2016)Honari, Yosinski, Vincent, and Pal
,
Wu et al.(2018)Wu, Qian, Yang, Wang, Cai, and Zhou
,
Wang et al.(2019)Wang, Bo, and Fuxin
,
Huang et al.(2020)Huang, Deng, Shen, Zhang, and Ye
,
Kumar et al.(2020)Kumar, Marks, Mou, Wang, Jones, Cherian,
Koike-Akino, Liu, and Feng
,
Huang et al.(2021)Huang, Yang, Li, Kim, and Wei
]
. The landmark coordinates are typically estimated at the maximum of each heatmap. This architecture provides an increase in accuracy at the expense of a considerable boost in computational and memory requirements. A fundamental limitation of both approaches is their degradation when there is ambiguity or noise contaminating the local landmark appearance. This typically happens at the presence of occlusions, heavy make-up, blur and extreme illuminations or poses. This is because of the known fact that CNNs cannot learn simple spatial relationships
[
Santoro et al.(2017)Santoro, Raposo, Barrett, Malinowski, Pascanu,
Battaglia, and Lillicrap
]
and, in the case of facial landmarks, are unable to learn a global representation of the face structure. However, a human face is a highly structured object with a prominent landmark configuration. Therefore, an effective way of representing the local appearance of each landmark and its geometric relationship to the other landmarks is needed.

This problem has been partially addressed in the literature with a local attention module combining landmarks with facial boundaries
[
Wu et al.(2018)Wu, Qian, Yang, Wang, Cai, and Zhou
,
Huang et al.(2020)Huang, Deng, Shen, Zhang, and Ye
,
Huang et al.(2021)Huang, Yang, Li, Kim, and Wei
]
. This is a solution that learns short-distance geometrical relationships. An alternative solution combines the advantages of a CNN description with traditional Ensemble of Regression Trees (ERT)
[
Valle et al.(2018)Valle, Buenaposada, Valdés, and
Baumela
,
Valle et al.(2019)Valle, Buenaposada, Valdés, and
Baumela
]
. Although this solution is able to learn long-distance geometrical dependencies, it is not fully satisfactory because of the limited learning capabilities of ERTs and the impossibility of end-to-end training.
Other approaches use a Graph Convolutional Network (GCN) to learn the facial geometrical structure
[
Li et al.(2020)Li, Lu, Zheng, Liao, Lin, Luo, Cheng, Xiao, Lu, Kuo,
and Miao
,
Lin et al.(2021)Lin, Zhu, Wang, Liao, Qian, Lu, and Zhou
]
. This is achieved by combining the landmark local description, extracted from the CNN representation, with geometrical information represented by the relative landmark locations. However, poor initialization and the lack of an advanced attention mechanism reduce the performance of these models.
More recent approaches use transformers
[
Li et al.(2022)Li, Guo, Rhee, Han, and Han
,
Xia et al.(2022)Xia, Qu, Huang, Zhang, Wang, and Xu
]
in a cascade shape regressor, obtaining very good results due to the built-in attention mechanisms.

In this paper, we present the SPIGA (
Shape Preserving wIth GAts
) model for the estimation of human face landmarks. We follow the traditional regressor cascade approach
[
Cao et al.(2014)Cao, Wei, Wen, and Sun
]
and present an algorithm that combines a multi-stage heatmap backbone with a cascade of Graph Attention Network (GAT) regressors
[
Velickovic et al.(2018)Velickovic, Cucurull, Casanova, Romero, Lio,
and Bengio
]
. The backbone provides a top-performing facial appearance representation. The cascaded GAT regressor is endowed with a positional encoding and attention mechanism that learn the geometrical relationship among landmarks. Another element of our proposal that improves the convergence of the GAT cascade is a coarse-to-fine feature extraction procedure and a good initialization. To do this, we train our backbone with a multi-task approach that also estimates the head pose, using its projection to establish the initial landmark locations. We evaluate the performance of our proposal in 300W, COFW-68, MERL-RAV and WFLW datasets. It achieves top performance on both head pose and face landmarks estimation. The improvement is most significant in situations involving large appearance changes, such as occlusions, heavy make-up, blur and extreme illuminations.
We make the following contributions:
1) A GAT cascade with an attention mechanism to weigh the information provided by each landmark according to its reliability;
2) A positional encoding to jointly represent relative landmark locations and local appearance;
3) A multi-task approach to initialize the location of graph nodes;
4) A coarse-to-fine landmark description scheme.

2
Shape Regressor Model

We propose a coarse-to-fine cascade of landmark regressors
[
Dollar et al.(2010)Dollar, Welinder, and Perona
,
Cao et al.(2014)Cao, Wei, Wen, and Sun
]
that iteratively refines the landmarks coordinates while preserving the face shape. Our approach involves three critical components: 1) the initialization, 2) the features used for regression, and 3) the regressors that estimate the face shape deformation at each step of the cascade.

In our proposal, we use a multi-task CNN backbone to provide both, the initialization and the local appearance representation. We set the initial shape of the face,
{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\bf x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\bf x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\bf x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\bf x$}}}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{L\times 2}
, by projecting
L
landmarks from a generic 3D rigid face mesh oriented using the head pose backbone prediction. At each cascade step
t
, a GAT-based
[
Velickovic et al.(2018)Velickovic, Cucurull, Casanova, Romero, Lio,
and Bengio
]
regressor computes a displacement vector,
\Delta{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\bf x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\bf x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\bf x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\bf x$}}}_{t}
, to update the landmarks location,
{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\bf x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\bf x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\bf x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\bf x$}}}_{t}={\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\bf x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\bf x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\bf x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\bf x$}}}_{t-1}+\Delta{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\bf x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\bf x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\bf x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\bf x$}}}_{t}
. After
K

{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\bf x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\bf x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\bf x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\bf x$}}}_{K}={\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\bf x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\bf x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\bf x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\bf x$}}}_{0}+\sum_{t=1}^{K}\Delta{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\bf x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\bf x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\bf x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\bf x$}}}_{t}
. We denote the 2D location of
l
-th landmark at step
t
as
{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\bf x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\bf x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\bf x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\bf x$}}}_{t}^{l}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
. In Fig.
1
we show the regressor with a two-step cascade configuration.

2.1
Initialization by Head Pose Estimation

Our multi-task backbone, termed

(

M

Each HG module in MTN is composed of a shared encoder with two task branches:

Geometric and Visual Feature Extraction

For each step in the cascaded regressor, the input features are a combination of local appearance at each landmark (i.e. visual features) and global representation of the facial structure (i.e. geometric features).
How visual and positional information is extracted and combined has a direct impact on the performance of the regressor (see Table
5
).

t
-th step regressor.

Let
\textstyle\tt F
be the output feature map of the last stacked HG module in the MTN. We extract local appearance information from a square window,
\mathcal{W}_{t}
, of size

, centered at each landmark location,
{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\bf x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\bf x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\bf x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\bf x$}}}_{t-1}^{l}

Relative distances between landmarks provide enhanced geometrical features compared to their absolute locations since they explicitly represent the facial shape. and Miao
]
. Let
{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\bf q$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\bf q$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\bf q$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\bf q$}}}_{t}^{l}=\{{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\bf x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\bf x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\bf x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\bf x$}}}_{t-1}^{l}-{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\bf x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\bf x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\bf x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\bf x$}}}_{t-1}^{i}\}_{i\neq l}\in\mathbb{R}^{2\times(L-1)}

{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\bf r$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\bf r$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\bf r$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\bf r$}}}_{t}^{l}=\Phi_{t}({\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\bf q$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\bf q$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\bf q$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\bf q$}}}_{t}^{l})

Let
{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\bf f$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\bf f$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\bf f$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\bf f$}}}_{t}^{l}

.
At each step

), and for each landmark

{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\bf v$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\bf v$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\bf v$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\bf v$}}}_{t}^{l}

{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\bf r$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\bf r$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\bf r$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\bf r$}}}_{t}^{l}
, computed from the current shape,
{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\bf x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\bf x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\bf x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\bf x$}}}_{t-1}

2.3
Cascade Shape Regressor Using GATs

The step regressor architecture (Fig.
2
) is composed of stacked GAT layers inspired by the ones in the Attentional Graph Neural Net
[
Sarlin et al.(2020)Sarlin, DeTone, Malisiewicz, and
Rabinovich
]
. We consider the facial shape as a single densely connected graph where nodes are the landmark locations,
{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\bf x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\bf x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\bf x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\bf x$}}}_{t}
. To weigh the shared information across nodes, we compute a dynamic adjacency matrix per GAT layer
s
,
\mathcal{A}_{t}^{s}
. We learn these matrices as an attention from a given landmark to every other in the graph.

The input to the first GAT layer at step
t
are the encoded features,
\{{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\bf f$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\bf f$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\bf f$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\bf f$}}}_{t}^{i}\}_{i=1}^{L}
. Let
{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\bf f$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\bf f$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\bf f$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\bf f$}}}_{t}^{i,s-1}
be the features of the
i
-th landmark produced by the (
s
-1)-th GAT layer, that are also the input to
s
-th layer (
{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\bf f$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\bf f$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\bf f$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\bf f$}}}_{t}^{i,0}\equiv{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\bf f$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\bf f$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\bf f$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\bf f$}}}_{t}^{i}
). From now on, we drop the step-index
t
to simplify the notation.
The updated feature vector after the
s
-th layer is defined as
{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\bf f$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\bf f$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\bf f$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\bf f$}}}^{i,s}={\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\bf f$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\bf f$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\bf f$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\bf f$}}}^{i,s-1}+MLP([{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\bf f$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\bf f$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\bf f$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\bf f$}}}^{i,s-1}||{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\bf m$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\bf m$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\bf m$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\bf m$}}}^{i,s}])

where
[\cdot||\cdot]
is the concatenation operator,
{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\bf m$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\bf m$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\bf m$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\bf m$}}}^{i,s}
is the information aggregated, or message, of the nodes neighboring
i
. Focusing on the message generation procedure, a query vector
{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\bf h$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\bf h$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\bf h$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\bf h$}}}_{q}^{i,s}
, is assigned to landmark

and key
{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\bf h$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\bf h$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\bf h$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\bf h$}}}_{k}^{j,s}
, and value vectors
{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\bf h$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\bf h$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\bf h$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\bf h$}}}_{v}^{j,s}
, to every other landmark

\alpha_{ij}=

{}_{j}({\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\bf h$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\bf h$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\bf h$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\bf h$}}}_{q}^{i,s}\cdot{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\bf h$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\bf h$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\bf h$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\bf h$}}}_{k}^{j,s})

{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\bf m$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\bf m$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\bf m$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\bf m$}}}^{i,s}=\sum_{i\neq j}\alpha_{ij}{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\bf h$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\bf h$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\bf h$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\bf h$}}}_{v}^{j,s}
,
where

{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\bf h$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\bf h$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\bf h$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\bf h$}}}_{q}^{i,s}={\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\tt W$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\tt W$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\tt W$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\tt W$}}}_{1}^{s}{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\bf f$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\bf f$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\bf f$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\bf f$}}}^{i,s}+{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\bf b$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\bf b$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\bf b$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\bf b$}}}_{1}^{s}

{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\bf h$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\bf h$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\bf h$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\bf h$}}}_{k}^{j,s}={\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\tt W$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\tt W$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\tt W$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\tt W$}}}_{2}^{s}{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\bf f$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\bf f$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\bf f$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\bf f$}}}^{j,s}+{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\bf b$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\bf b$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\bf b$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\bf b$}}}_{2}^{s}

{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\bf h$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\bf h$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\bf h$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\bf h$}}}_{v}^{j,s}={\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\tt W$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\tt W$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\tt W$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\tt W$}}}_{3}^{s}{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\bf f$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\bf f$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\bf f$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\bf f$}}}^{j,s}+{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\bf b$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\bf b$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\bf b$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\bf b$}}}_{3}^{s}
.
Matrices
{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\tt W$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\tt W$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\tt W$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\tt W$}}}_{i}
and bias vectors
{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\bf b$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\bf b$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\bf b$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\bf b$}}}_{i}

activation and scaling the result, to be in the interval
[-w_{t}/2,w_{t}/2]
. In practice, this constraint makes the single-step regressor search problem simpler, boosting training convergence. Given a trained MTN backbone, we train the cascade with the
\mathcal{L}_{CR}=\sum_{t=1}^{K}L1_{smooth}[\tilde{{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\bf x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\bf x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\bf x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\bf x$}}}}-({\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\bf x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\bf x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\bf x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\bf x$}}}_{t-1}+\Delta{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\bf x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\bf x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\bf x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\bf x$}}}_{t})]
loss, where
\tilde{{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\bf x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\bf x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\bf x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\bf x$}}}}
are the ground truth landmark coordinates.

3
Experiments

To train and evaluate our method, we conduct different experiments in four complementary datasets which have been acquired in-the-wild and bear different levels of difficulty:

300W

[
Sagonas et al.(2016)Sagonas, Tzimiropoulos, Zafeiriou, and
Pantic
]
provides 68 manually annotated landmarks.
We employ the 300W private extension, which uses 3837 images as training set and adds 600 test images divided into indoor and outdoor subgroups.

COFW-68
is a re-annotated version of COFW
[
Burgos-Artizzu et al.(2013)Burgos-Artizzu, Perona, and
Dollar
]
with 68 landmarks. It is conceived for testing landmark detectors with occlusions in a cross-dataset approach. The testing set in COFW-68 is made of 507 images. The annotations include the landmark positions and the visibility labels for the same 68 points as in 300W.

WFLW

[
Wu et al.(2018)Wu, Qian, Yang, Wang, Cai, and Zhou
]
is composed of challenging in-the-wild images and provides 98 manually annotated landmarks. The dataset has 7500 training and 2500 testing faces. It is divided into 6 subgroups: pose, expression, illumination, make-up, occlusion and blur.

MERL-RAV

[
Kumar et al.(2020)Kumar, Marks, Mou, Wang, Jones, Cherian,
Koike-Akino, Liu, and Feng
]
is a re-annotated version of 19,000 AFLW images with 68 landmarks, like 300W. It provides 15,449 training and 3,865 test faces divided into 3 orientation subsets: frontal, half-profile and profile. This recent dataset includes externally occluded visibility and self-occluded labels.

3.1
Evaluation Metrics

In order to quantify the head pose estimation error, we use the Mean Absolute Error (MAE) metric,

MAE=\frac{1}{N}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{N}|\tilde{p}_{i}-p_{i}|
,
where
N
is the number of testing images,
\tilde{p}_{i}
is the ground truth and
p_{i}
represents a single predicted pose parameter.

Focusing on the landmark estimation task, Normalized Mean Error (NME) is the standard metric,

NME=\frac{100}{N}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{N}\sum\limits_{l=1}^{L}\frac{||\tilde{{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\bf x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\bf x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\bf x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\bf x$}}}}_{l}^{i}-{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\bf x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\bf x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\bf x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\bf x$}}}_{l}^{i}||_{2}}{d_{i}}
.
Where
\tilde{{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\bf x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\bf x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\bf x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\bf x$}}}}_{l}^{i}
and
{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle\bf x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle\bf x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle\bf x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle\bf x$}}}_{l}^{i}
denote, respectively, the ground-truth and predicted coordinates of the
i
-th landmark and
d_{i}
is a normalization value which varies depending on the dataset: inter-ocular (int-ocul), distance between outer eye corners; inter-pupils, distance between pupil/eye centers; and box, computed as the geometric mean of the landmarks ground truth bounding box (
d=\sqrt{w_{bbox}*h_{bbox}}
).

We also use Failure Rate (FR) and Area Under the Curve (AUC). FR evaluates the robustness of algorithms in terms of NME, indicating the percentage of images with an NME above a given threshold. AUC is calculated by computing the area under the Cumulative Error Distribution (CED) curve from 0 to the FR threshold. We introduce the Normalized mean Percentile Error 90 (
NPE_{90}
) which represents the NME for the image at the 90% of the dataset, sorted by NME. This metric is particularly convenient for small data subsets where the FR is not representative.

Landmark Detection Results

WFLW is the most popular benchmark to evaluate the performance of facial landmark detection. Recent methods that adopt this dataset use the bounding boxes provided by HRnet
[
Wang et al.(2021)Wang, Sun, Cheng, Jiang, Deng, Zhao, Liu, Mu, Tan,
Wang, Liu, and Xiao
]

By doing so, they achieve better performance (see Table
2
, AWing results improve from 4.36 to 4.21 NME). In Table
2
, we clearly distinguish the bounding boxes used in the evaluation. Another important aspect to perform a fair comparison is the use of additional training data.
In our discussion we do not consider methods that train with images or annotations other than those provided by WFLW.

In Table
2
, we show that our model outperforms current state-of-the-art (SOTA) in most of the WFLW subsets, as well as in the full set metrics. When it is compared with other GraphNets-based methods, our approach is 4% and 32% better in terms of NME and FR than SLD
[
Li et al.(2020)Li, Lu, Zheng, Liao, Lin, Luo, Cheng, Xiao, Lu, Kuo,
and Miao
]
, and 7% and 23% better than SDFL
[
Lin et al.(2021)Lin, Zhu, Wang, Liao, Qian, Lu, and Zhou
]
. These results show that our relative positional encoding and the per layer graph attention mechanism have a strong impact on the performance of GraphNets. Further, our proposal is also more accurate than recent approaches based on transformers, when these models are trained only with WFLW data, DTLD-s
[
Li et al.(2022)Li, Guo, Rhee, Han, and Han
]
and SPLT
[
Xia et al.(2022)Xia, Qu, Huang, Zhang, Wang, and Xu
]

If we analyze the performance on some of the subsets, our method is 35%, 25%, 23% and 39% better than the previous SOTA, ADNet
[
Huang et al.(2021)Huang, Yang, Li, Kim, and Wei
]
, in the illumination, make-up, occlusion and blur subsets. This proves the importance of learning a global representation of the facial structure, that CNNs alone do not provide.
Additionally, the low FR across the different subsets and better AUC values reaffirm that our model achieves a balanced trade-off between robustness and precision, taking advantage of the complementary benefits from the CNN and GAT architectures.

4
Conclusions

We presented SPIGA, a face landmark regressor that combines a CNN with a cascade of Graph Attention Networks (GATs). The CNN provides the local appearance representation. The GAT regressor is endowed with a positional encoding and attention mechanism that learn the geometrical relationship among landmarks and encourage the model to produce plausible face shapes. It establishes a new SOTA in the WLFW, COFW-68 and MERL-RAV datasets. In our experimentation we verify that the positional encoding is the component that contributes most to the final result and the first steps of the cascade focus on improving the robustness.
In addition, at each step, the regressor ”looks” at distant and reliable landmarks in the first GAT layer and progressively focuses its attention on closer landmarks in the following ones. These insights from our ablation analysis confirm that SPIGA is learning a global representation and explains why its improvement is most significative in challenging situations involving occlusions, heavy make-up, blur and extreme illumination.
