Elastic bounds of the coupling tensor of anisotropic composite laminates: a polar approach - PREPRINT- Final, extended version published in Proceedings of the Royal Society A https://dx.doi.org/10.1098/rspa.2024.0050
Paolo VANNUCCI LMV - Laboratoire de Mathématiques de Versailles UMR8100 UVSQ - Université de Versailles et Saint Quentin 45, Avenue des Etats-Unis, 78035 Versailles - France paolo.vannucci@uvsq.fr

Abstract
In the equivalent single layer theories of anisotropic laminates, the coupling tensor 𝔹 \mathbb{B} describes the relation between the in- and out-of plane behavior of the plate. This tensor has some peculiar characteristics and in particular it is not defined, so it is normally considered as an unbounded tensor. We show in this paper a way to determine some relations between the polar invariants of 𝔹 \mathbb{B} and those of the tensors describing the in- and -out-of plane behaviors of the laminate, 𝔸 \mathbb{A} and 𝔻 \mathbb{D} respectively. These relations constitute a set of bounds for the invariants of all the tensors of the laminate, 𝔸 , 𝔹 \mathbb{A},\mathbb{B} and 𝔻 \mathbb{D} . It is hence shown that also the components of 𝔹 \mathbb{B} must satisfy some conditions. Some peculiar cases, interesting for applications, are also considered. Key words: anisotropy; elastic moduli bounds; polar formalism; tensor invariants; laminates PACS 46.25; 46.35; 62.20.de MSC 74B05; 74E10; 74E30; 74K20



1
Introduction

In the classical theory of elastic laminates, the behavior of the plate is described by a law of the type

\left\{\begin{array}[]{c}\mathbf{N}\\
\mathbf{M}\end{array}\right\}=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}h\mathbb{A}&\dfrac{h^{2}}{2}\mathbb{B}\\
\dfrac{h^{2}}{2}\mathbb{B}&\dfrac{h^{3}}{12}\mathbb{D}\end{array}\right]\left\{\begin{array}[]{c}\bm{\varepsilon}\\
\bm{\kappa}\end{array}\right\},

(1)

where,
[
1
,
2
]
,
h
is the plate’s thickness,
\mathbf{N},\mathbf{M}
are respectively the tensor of membrane forces and bending moments,
\bm{\varepsilon},\bm{\kappa}
the extension and curvature tensors,
\mathbb{A},\mathbb{D}
the stiffness tensors of the extension and bending behaviors and
\mathbb{B}
the coupling tensor.
\mathbb{A},\mathbb{B},\mathbb{D}
are tensors of the elastic type, i.e. fourth-rank tensors with the major and minor symmetries. In other equivalent single layer models for laminated plates, namely in the theory of Reissner-Mindlin,
[
3
,
4
]
or in the third-order shear deformation theory of Reddy,
[
5
,
6
,
7
]
, the definition of the curvatures changes and other tensors, describing the transverse shear behavior, are to be included, but the definition, and the properties, of
\mathbb{A},\mathbb{B}
and
\mathbb{D}
do not change.

A deep investigation of the mechanical and mathematical properties of
\mathbb{B}
has been presented in
[
8
]
. Moreover, in
[
9
,
10
,
11
]
, also the thermal coupling effects have been investigated.

Unlike
\mathbb{A}
and
\mathbb{D}
, that are positive definite,
\mathbb{B}
is not defined. At least, this is what is commonly admitted, and not only for
\mathbb{B}
, but also for other coupling tensors describing some sort of coupling between two physical phenomena, e.g. in the theory of quasi-crystals,
[
12
]
. Actually, the positive definiteness of
\mathbb{A}
and
\mathbb{D}
, like that of any other elastic tensor, is a consequence of the work, necessarily positive, done by the external forces. In the case of
\mathbb{A}
and
\mathbb{D}
, this is computed considering the laminate as uncoupled. The case of coupled laminates, i.e. having
\mathbb{B}\neq\mathbb{O}
, is almost unconsidered in the literature and
\mathbb{B}
is commonly seen as an
undefined
tensor, i.e. in general not positively nor negatively defined.

The positive definiteness of an elastic tensor gives as a result some bounds on the elastic moduli of the tensor in a given mathematical representation, e.g. the well known bounds on the Lamé’s constants or on the Young’s modulus and the Poisson’s ratio for isotropic materials. More delicate is the case of anisotropic materials,
[
1
,
13
]
, for which a clear and definite set of bounds for the elastic moduli has not yet been defined in three-dimensional elasticity for any possible elastic syngony, namely for the most general one of triclinic materials. However, the same problem has been solved definitely in two-dimensional elasticity, using the polar formalism,
[
14
,
15
,
2
]
. By this mathematical technique, introduced as early as 1979 by G. Verchery,
[
16
]
, any elastic tensor is represented by invariant moduli and angles. In this way, the bounds on the elastic tensor are given on its invariants, so intrinsically representing the elastic limits of the material.
The same procedure has been used to determine the so-called
geometrical bounds
,
[
17
]
, i.e. the bounds determining the elastic domain for a laminate when the stacking is considered.

The question of the bounds on
\mathbb{B}
remains open. This paper addresses exactly this subject and in particular it proposes an approach to give a first answer the following questions:

i.

is it possible to establish some bounds for the moduli of
\mathbb{B}
?

ii.

how the known bounds on
\mathbb{A}
and
\mathbb{D}
are modified when
\mathbb{B}\neq\mathbb{O}
?

iii.

is it possible to give an explicit form to the bounds on the moduli of
\mathbb{B}
?

iv.

can such bounds be given, in all the cases, in an invariant form?

v.

how the existence of some peculiar circumstances, e.g. a material symmetry, does affect the bounds on
\mathbb{B}
?

This research has two motivations: the first one, is a purely scientific question, interesting
per se
, to fill a gap still existing in the scientific literature: is it possible to give some bounds to tensor
\mathbb{B}
? Another motivation can be found in optimization problems: any design problem for a coupled laminate is correctly formulated only if the design space is well determined, so as to properly define the feasibility domain.

The paper is organized as follows: in the next Section, the polar formalism is briefly recalled, especially for representing tensors
\mathbb{A},\mathbb{B},\mathbb{D}
. Then, the procedure used to determine the bounds on
\mathbb{B}
is detailed and subsequently the attempt to establish a general solution is explained. Some special cases are then considered and finally some conclusion is drawn in the end.

2
Recall of the polar formalism for a laminate

The reader is referred to
[
2
,
14
]
for a complete presentation of the polar formalism. Here, only major results of this theory, relevant to the topic of this paper, are presented, without proofs.
For a given plane elastic tensor
\mathbb{T}
, the polar formalism allows to express the cartesian components at a direction
\theta
as

{\begin{split}&{T_{1111}{(}\theta{)}{=}{T}_{0}{+}{2}{T}_{1}{+}{R}_{0}\cos{4}\left({{{\varPhi}}_{0}{-}\theta}\right){+}{4}{R}_{1}\cos{2}\left({{{\varPhi}}_{1}{-}\theta}\right)},\\
&{T_{1112}{(}\theta{)}{=}{R}_{0}\sin{4}\left({{{\varPhi}}_{0}{-}\theta}\right){+}{2}{R}_{1}\sin{2}\left({{{\varPhi}}_{1}{-}\theta}\right)},\\
&{T_{1122}{(}\theta{)}{=}{-}{T}_{0}{+}{2}{T}_{1}{-}{R}_{0}\cos{4}\left({{{\varPhi}}_{0}{-}\theta}\right)},\\
&{T_{1212}{(}\theta{)}{=}{T}_{0}{-}{R}_{0}\cos{4}\left({{{\varPhi}}_{0}{-}\theta}\right)},\\
&{T_{1222}{(}\theta{)}{=}{-}{R}_{0}\sin{4}\left({{{\varPhi}}_{0}{-}\theta}\right){+}{2}{R}_{1}\sin{2}\left({{{\varPhi}}_{1}{-}\theta}\right)},\\
&{T_{2222}{(}\theta{)}{=}{T}_{0}{+}{2}{T}_{1}{+}{R}_{0}\cos{4}\left({{{\varPhi}}_{0}{-}\theta}\right){-}{4}{R}_{1}\cos{2}\left({{{\varPhi}}_{1}{-}\theta}\right)}.\end{split}}

(2)

What is important to remark is the fact that the moduli
T_{0},T_{1},R_{0},R_{1}
as well as the difference of the angles
\varPhi_{0}-\varPhi_{1}
are tensor invariants; the value of one of the two polar angles, usually
\varPhi_{1}
, fixes the frame. To be remarked that the polar method allows for a decomposition of anisotropic 2D elasticity into different
elastic phases
: an
isotropic phase
, characterized by the two invariants
T_{0}
and
T_{1}
, and two
anisotropic phases
, whose amplitudes are determined by the invariants
R_{0}
and
R_{1}
; these two anisotropy phases are shifted of the angle
\varPhi_{0}-\varPhi_{1}
, the fifth tensor invariant. It is worth noting that any rotation of the angle
\theta
is simply done in the polar method: it is sufficient to subtract
\theta
from each one of the two polar angles. A general sketch of the decomposition of elasticity into elastic phases is given in Fig.
1
, where the angles
\varPhi_{0},\varPhi_{1}
and
\varPhi_{0}-\varPhi_{1}
are also indicated.

In the polar formalism, the elastic symmetries are determined by the following values of the invariants:

i.

ordinary orthotropy:
\varPhi_{0}-\varPhi_{1}=k\dfrac{\pi}{4},\ k\in\{0,1\}
;

ii.

R_{0}
-orthotropy:
R_{0}=0
,
[
19
]
;

iii.

square symmetry:
{R_{1}=0}
;

iv.

isotropy:
R_{0}=R_{1}=0
.

These elastic symmetries are the only possible ones in 2D elasticity; in particular, we see that two cases of ordinary orthotropy can exist, sharing the same values of the invariants
T_{0},T_{1},R_{0},R_{1}
. Moreover, square symmetry is the 2D corresponding of the 3D cubic syngony: it is the case of a layer having two couples of mutually orthogonal symmetry axes, rotated of
\pi/4
, and with the same values of the elastic moduli along the two orthogonal axes. It is, namely, the case of composite plies reinforced by balanced fabrics, i.e. by fabrics having the same amount of fibers in warp and weft,
[
20
]
. Finally, the case of
R_{0}
-orthotropy has been discovered for the first time in 2D elasticity thanks to the polar method,
[
19
]
, and later also in 3D elasticity,
[
21
]
. In Fig.
2
, some examples of the above possible cases are given.

; d) a

-orthotropic material, obtained superposing two T300/5208 carbon epoxy plies rotated of
\pi/4
.

The above polar transformations apply also to tensors
\mathbb{A},\mathbb{B}
and
\mathbb{D}
; in particular, when a laminate is composed by identical layers, the case considered in this research,
[
25
,
26
]
, then (we indicate by a superscript
A,B
or
D
a polar quantity of
\mathbb{A},\mathbb{B}
or
\mathbb{D}
respectively, while a polar quantity of the basic layer has no superscript)

T_{0}^{A}=T_{0}^{D}=T_{0},\ T_{1}^{A}=T_{1}^{D}=T_{1},\ T_{0}^{B}=T_{1}^{B}=0.

(3)

The polar formalism can be applied also to other situations, e.g. to the piezoelectric tensor,
[
27
]
, or to thermo-elastic problems,
[
28
,
9
]
. Some special cases of elastic non-classical materials have also been studied through the polar formalism,
[

,

,

]
, however in this research we will consider only classical elastic tensors.

Regarding a second-rank symmetric tensor
L
, in the polar formalism it is

\begin{split}&L_{11}(\theta)=T+R\cos 2(\varPhi-\theta),\\
&L_{12}(\theta)=R\sin 2(\varPhi-\theta),\\
&L_{22}(\theta)=T-R\cos 2(\varPhi-\theta),\end{split}

(4)

with
T,R
two invariants and
\varPhi
an angle determined by the choice of the frame. About these relations, we can rephrase the comments given for eq. (
2
), adding that actually they correspond to the analytical expression of the well known graphical construction of the Mohr’s circle,
[
32
]
. In the following, we will indicate by
t_{\varepsilon},r_{\varepsilon},\varphi_{\varepsilon}
the polar components of
\bm{\varepsilon}
and by
t_{\kappa},r_{\kappa},\varphi_{\kappa}
those of
\bm{\kappa}
.

3
Statement of the problem

For a coupled laminate, i.e. with
\mathbb{B}\neq\mathbb{O}
, the density of the elastic energy per unit of area of the plate is

U=\frac{1}{2}\left\{\begin{array}[]{c}\textbf{N}\\
\textbf{M}\end{array}\right\}\cdot\left\{\begin{array}[]{c}\bm{\varepsilon}\\
\bm{\kappa}\end{array}\right\}=\frac{1}{2}\left\{\begin{array}[]{c}\bm{\varepsilon}\\
\bm{\kappa}\end{array}\right\}\cdot\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}h\mathbb{A}&\dfrac{h^{2}}{2}\mathbb{B}\\
\dfrac{h^{2}}{2}\mathbb{B}&\dfrac{h^{3}}{12}\mathbb{D}\end{array}\right]\left\{\begin{array}[]{c}\bm{\varepsilon}\\
\bm{\kappa}\end{array}\right\},

(5)

i.e., for being
\mathbb{B}=\mathbb{B}^{\top}
by virtue of the major symmetries,
[
33
]
,

U=\frac{h}{24}(12\ \bm{\varepsilon}\cdot\mathbb{A}\bm{\varepsilon}+12h\ \bm{\varepsilon}\cdot\mathbb{B}\bm{\kappa}+h^{2}\bm{\kappa}\cdot\mathbb{D}\bm{\kappa}).

(6)

The energy density
U
must be positive for each possible strain state, i.e.
\forall\bm{\varepsilon},\bm{\kappa}
; this is the condition leading to express the bounds for the elastic moduli. Following an approach first introduced by Verchery and detailed in
[
2
,
34
]
, we express all the tensors in the previous equation by their polar components, once fixed
\theta=0
. Some standard passages lead to

\begin{split}U&=2h\{2T_{1}t_{\varepsilon}^{2}+[T_{0}+R_{0}^{A}\cos 4(\varPhi_{0}^{A}-\varphi_{\varepsilon})]r_{\varepsilon}^{2}+4R_{1}^{A}t_{\varepsilon}r_{\varepsilon}\cos 2(\varPhi_{1}^{A}-\varphi_{\varepsilon})\}\\
&+2h^{2}\{2R_{1}^{B}t_{\varepsilon}r_{\kappa}\cos 2(\varPhi_{1}^{B}-\varphi_{\kappa})+2R_{1}^{B}t_{\kappa}r_{\varepsilon}\cos 2(\varPhi_{1}^{B}-\varphi_{\varepsilon})\\
&+R_{0}^{B}r_{\varepsilon}r_{\kappa}\cos 2(2\varPhi_{0}^{B}-\varphi_{\varepsilon}-\varphi_{\kappa})\}\\
&+\frac{h^{3}}{6}\{2T_{1}t_{\kappa}^{2}+[T_{0}+R_{0}^{D}\cos 4(\varPhi_{0}^{D}-\varphi_{\kappa})]r_{\kappa}^{2}+4R_{1}^{D}t_{\kappa}r_{\kappa}\cos 2(\varPhi_{1}^{D}-\varphi_{\kappa})\}.\end{split}

(7)

In this expression the first term in curly braces is due to extension, the second one to coupling and the third one to bending. Let us order the polar moduli of
\bm{\varepsilon}
and
\bm{\kappa}
as a column vector
\{v\}
:

\{v\}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{c}t_{\varepsilon}\\
r_{\varepsilon}\\
t_{\kappa}\\
r_{\kappa}\end{array}\right\};

(8)

then, we can rewrite
U
as the quadratic form

U=\frac{h}{24}\{v\}^{\top}[M]\{v\},

(9)

where
[M]
is the
4\times 4
symmetric matrix

\hskip-71.13188pt[M]=\footnotesize\left[\begin{array}[]{cccc}96T_{1}&96R_{1}^{A}\cos 2(\varPhi_{1}^{A}-\varphi_{\varepsilon})&0&48hR_{1}^{B}\cos 2(\varPhi_{1}^{B}-\varphi_{\kappa})\\
96R_{1}^{A}\cos 2(\varPhi_{1}^{A}-\varphi_{\varepsilon})&48[T_{0}+R_{0}^{A}\cos 4(\varPhi_{0}^{A}-\varphi_{\varepsilon})]&48hR_{1}^{B}\cos 2(\varPhi_{1}^{B}-\varphi_{\varepsilon})&24hR_{0}^{B}\cos 2(2\varPhi_{0}^{B}-\varphi_{\varepsilon}-\varphi_{\kappa})\\
0&48hR_{1}^{B}\cos 2(\varPhi_{1}^{B}-\varphi_{\varepsilon})&8h^{2}T_{1}&8h^{2}R_{1}^{D}\cos 2(\varPhi_{1}^{D}-\varphi_{\kappa})\\
48hR_{1}^{B}\cos 2(\varPhi_{1}^{B}-\varphi_{\kappa})&24hR_{0}^{B}\cos 2(2\varPhi_{0}^{B}-\varphi_{\varepsilon}-\varphi_{\kappa})&8h^{2}R_{1}^{D}\cos 2(\varPhi_{1}^{D}-\varphi_{\kappa})&4h^{2}[T_{0}+R_{0}^{D}\cos 4(\varPhi_{0}^{D}-\varphi_{\kappa})]\end{array}\right].

(10)

So,
U>0\ \forall\bm{\varepsilon},\bm{\kappa}\iff[M]
is positive definite
\forall\varphi_{\varepsilon},\varphi_{\kappa}
. This happens if and only if, see
[
35
]
p. 340,

M1=96T_{1}>0,

(11)

\hskip-8.53581ptM2=\det\small\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}96T_{1}&96R_{1}^{A}\cos 2(\varPhi_{1}^{A}-\varphi_{\varepsilon})\\
96R_{1}^{A}\cos 2(\varPhi_{1}^{A}-\varphi_{\varepsilon})&48[T_{0}+R_{0}^{A}\cos 4(\varPhi_{0}^{A}-\varphi_{\varepsilon})]\end{array}\right]>0\ \forall\varphi_{\varepsilon},

(12)

\hskip-42.67912ptM3=\det\small\left[\begin{array}[]{ccc}96T_{1}&96R_{1}^{A}\cos 2(\varPhi_{1}^{A}-\varphi_{\varepsilon})&0\\
96R_{1}^{A}\cos 2(\varPhi_{1}^{A}-\varphi_{\varepsilon})&48[T_{0}+R_{0}^{A}\cos 4(\varPhi_{0}^{A}-\varphi_{\varepsilon})]&48hR_{1}^{B}\cos 2(\varPhi_{1}^{B}-\varphi_{\varepsilon})\\
0&48hR_{1}^{B}\cos 2(\varPhi_{1}^{B}-\varphi_{\varepsilon})&8h^{2}T_{1}\end{array}\right]>0\ \forall\varphi_{\varepsilon},

(13)

M4=\det[M]>0\ \forall\varphi_{\varepsilon},\varphi_{\kappa}.

(14)

The first condition, eq. (
11
), is redundant:
T_{1}>0
is a general condition for this polar modulus, see
[
2
]
p. 154. Because
T_{1}
is a modulus of the basic layer, i.e. of a real material, this condition is automatically satisfied. The other conditions on
M2,M3
and
M4
are discussed in the next Section. Before doing that, we notice that only the condition on
M4
concerns also the curvature field, while those on
M2
and
M3
depend just on the extension field.

4
General solution

We consider first the different conditions on
M2,M3
and
M4
, then we examine them together in the next Section.

4.1
Condition on M2

Equation (
12
) gives the following condition for the positive definiteness of
[M]
:

T_{1}[T_{0}+R_{0}^{A}\cos 4(\varPhi_{0}^{A}-\varphi_{\varepsilon})]-2{R_{1}^{A}}^{2}\cos^{2}2(\varPhi_{1}^{A}-\varphi_{\varepsilon})>0\ \forall\varphi_{\varepsilon}.

(15)

This condition is exactly the same already found in
[
34
]
for proving the bounds on the polar moduli of an anisotropic layer; here, the condition refers to tensor
\mathbb{A}
. Following the same steps outlined in
[
2
]
or in
[
34
]
, it can be proved that eq. (
15
) is equivalent to the three conditions

\begin{split}&T_{0}-R_{0}^{A}>0,\\
&T_{0}T_{1}-{R_{1}^{A}}^{2}>0,\\
&T_{1}(T_{0}^{2}-{R_{0}^{A}}^{2})-2{R_{1}^{A}}^{2}(T_{0}-R_{0}^{A}\cos 4\varPhi_{A})>0,\end{split}

(16)

where
\varPhi_{A}=\varPhi_{0}^{A}-\varPhi_{1}^{A}
, an invariant of
\mathbb{A}
. Actually, as shown in
[
2
,
34
]
, the second condition above is less restrictive than the third one, so that it can be discarded. Finally, the condition on
M2
becomes

\begin{split}&T_{0}-R_{0}^{A}>0,\\
&T_{1}(T_{0}^{2}-{R_{0}^{A}}^{2})-2{R_{1}^{A}}^{2}(T_{0}-R_{0}^{A}\cos 4\varPhi_{A})>0.\end{split}

(17)

We remark that conditions (
17
) are written in terms of tensor invariants of
\mathbb{A}
, so they are frame independent.

Of course, the order in which the components of
\bm{\varepsilon}
and of
\bm{\kappa}
appear in vector
\{v\}
, eq. (
8
) is completely arbitrary, any other order being allowed. In particular, if one choses the order putting first the components of
\bm{\kappa}
, then those of
\bm{\varepsilon}
, i.e. if we had put

\{v\}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{c}t_{\kappa}\\
r_{\kappa}\\
t_{\varepsilon}\\
r_{\varepsilon}\end{array}\right\},

(18)

then we had found for
M2
a similar expression, but with the index
D
replacing the index
A
everywhere, i.e. the conditions would concern in this case the moduli of
\mathbb{D}
and not those of
\mathbb{A}
. Because of the arbitrariness of the order of the components of
\{v\}
, we can conclude that actually the above conditions (
15
) and (
17
) must hold necessarily also for the components of
\mathbb{D}
:

T_{1}[T_{0}+R_{0}^{D}\cos 4(\varPhi_{0}^{D}-\varphi_{\kappa})]-2{R_{1}^{D}}^{2}\cos^{2}2(\varPhi_{1}^{D}-\varphi_{\kappa})>0\ \forall\varphi_{\kappa}

(19)

and

\begin{split}&T_{0}-R_{0}^{D}>0,\\
&T_{1}(T_{0}^{2}-{R_{0}^{D}}^{2})-2{R_{1}^{D}}^{2}(T_{0}-R_{0}^{D}\cos 4\varPhi_{D})>0,\end{split}

(20)

with of course
\varPhi_{D}=\varPhi_{0}^{D}-\varPhi_{1}^{D}
.

4.2
Condition on M3

Developing the determinant in eq. (
13
), we get the condition

\begin{split}h^{2}T_{1}[T_{0}T_{1}+T_{1}R_{0}^{A}\cos 4(\varPhi_{0}^{A}-\varphi_{\varepsilon})-2{R_{1}^{A}}^{2}\cos^{2}2(\varPhi_{1}^{A}-\varphi_{\varepsilon})&\\
-6{R_{1}^{B}}^{2}\cos^{2}2(\varPhi_{1}^{B}-\varphi_{\varepsilon})]&>0\ \forall\varphi_{\varepsilon}.\end{split}

(21)

To solve this condition, we consider just the term in square brackets, as
T_{1}>0
, as already said; moreover, we introduce the angles

\alpha=\varPhi_{1}^{A}-\varphi_{\varepsilon}\ \Rightarrow\ \varPhi_{0}^{A}-\varphi_{\varepsilon}=\varPhi_{A}+\alpha,

(22)

and

\delta_{A}=\varPhi_{1}^{B}-\varPhi_{1}^{A}\ \Rightarrow\ \varPhi_{1}^{B}-\varphi_{\varepsilon}=\delta_{A}+\alpha.

(23)

The angle
\delta_{A}
is the
shift angle
of tensor
\mathbb{A}
with respect to tensor
\mathbb{B}
, whose geometrical meaning is shown by Fig.
3
. With this, eq. (
21
) is equivalent to

T_{1}[T_{0}+R_{0}^{A}\cos 4(\varPhi_{A}+\alpha)]>2{R_{1}^{A}}^{2}\cos^{2}2\alpha+6{R_{1}^{B}}^{2}\cos^{2}2(\delta_{A}+\alpha)>0\ \forall\alpha.

(24)

Using standard trigonometry, this can be transformed first to

\begin{split}&T_{0}T_{1}-{R_{1}^{A}}^{2}-3{R_{1}^{B}}^{2}>(T_{1}R_{0}^{A}\sin 4\varPhi_{A}+3{R_{1}^{B}}^{2}\sin 4\delta_{A})\sin 4\alpha-\\
&-(T_{1}R_{0}^{A}\cos 4\varPhi_{A}-3{R_{1}^{B}}^{2}\cos 4\delta_{A}-{R_{1}^{A}}^{2})\cos 4\alpha\ \ \ \forall\alpha,\end{split}

then to

\begin{split}&T_{0}T_{1}-{R_{1}^{A}}^{2}-3{R_{1}^{B}}^{2}>[(T_{1}R_{0}^{A}\sin 4\varPhi_{A}+3{R_{1}^{B}}^{2}\sin 4\delta_{A})^{2}+\\

with

(27)

For being satisfied for each possible value of
\alpha
, eq. (
26
) gives the condition

\begin{split}&T_{0}T_{1}-{R_{1}^{A}}^{2}-3{R_{1}^{B}}^{2}>[(T_{1}R_{0}^{A}\sin 4\varPhi_{A}+3{R_{1}^{B}}^{2}\sin 4\delta_{A})^{2}+\\

6
Conclusion

Coming back to the questions posed in Sect.
1
, the result found above show that:

i.

it is possible to establish some bounds involving
also
the moduli of
\mathbb{B}
but not
exclusively
these ones;

ii.

the moduli of
\mathbb{A}
and
\mathbb{D}
known for the case of uncoupled laminates are still valid but in addition some other bounds, relating the moduli of
\mathbb{A},\mathbb{B}
and
\mathbb{D}
are added in the case of coupled laminates;

iii.

not all the bounds relating the moduli of
\mathbb{A},\mathbb{B}
and
\mathbb{D}
can be given in an explicit form;

iv.

all the bounds depend exclusively on tensor invariants and shift angles, so all of them are frame independent;

v.

the existence of some kind of symmetries affects the bounds and normally allows to express them in a simpler form.

The previous results show also that
\mathbb{B}
remains an undefined tensor.
The bounds found in this paper reveal hence the existence of some supplementary conditions to be satisfied by the moduli of
\mathbb{A}
and
\mathbb{D}
, these conditions imposing some sort of interaction with the moduli of
\mathbb{B}
. This result, unknown until now, is interesting
per se
, because it shows that coupling is not unbounded, but also in practical applications, namely in optimization problems, to correctly define the feasibility domain in the search of optimal coupled laminates.
