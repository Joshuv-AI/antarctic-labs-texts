The Pricing of Vanilla Options with Cash Dividends as a Classic Vanilla Basket Option Problem
Jherek Healy

Abstract
In the standard Black-Scholes-Merton framework, dividends are represented as a continuous dividend yield and the pricing of Vanilla options on a stock is achieved through the well-known Black-Scholes formula. In reality however, stocks pay a discrete fixed cash dividend at each dividend ex-date. This leads to the so-called piecewise lognormal model, where the asset jumps from a fixed known amount at each dividend date. There is however no exact closed-form formula for the pricing of Vanilla options under this model. Approximations must be used. While there exists many approximations taylored to this specific problem in the litterature, this paper explores the use of existing well-known basket option formulas for the pricing of European options on a single asset with cash dividends in the piecewise lognormal model.

2
The piecewise-lognormal model

We assume that the stock price
S
follows a lognormal process between dividend dates, and jumps at each dividend ex-date
t_{i}
from the cash dividend amount
\alpha_{i}
. The corresponding stochastic differential equation reads

\begin{cases}\mathop{}\!\kern 0.0pt\mathrm{d}S=r_{R}(t)S(t)\mathop{}\!\kern 0.0pt\mathrm{d}t+\sigma(t)S(t)\mathop{}\!\kern 0.0pt\mathrm{d}W\textmd{, }t_{i}\leq t<t_{i+1}\,,\\
S(t_{i})=S(t_{i}^{-})-\alpha_{i}\,,\end{cases}

(1)

where
(t_{i})_{i}
denotes the full ordered set of dividend dates,
r_{R}
is the stock lending rate (also known as repo rate), and
W
is a Brownian motion.

The price of a European call option of strike
K
, maturity
T
on
S
reads

\displaystyle V_{C}(T)

\displaystyle=B(0,T_{p})\mathbb{E}_{Q}\left[\max(S(T)-K,0)\right]\,,

(2)

where
B(0,T_{p})
is the discount factor to the payment date (typically two business days after the maturity date), calculated using the risk free rate, or the collateral rate. Let
\tilde{V}_{C}=\frac{V_{C}}{B(0,T_{p})}
be the undiscounted option price.
Integrating Equation
1
leads then to

\displaystyle\tilde{V}_{C}(T)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}\left[\max\left(S(0)e^{\int_{0}^{T}r_{R}(s)-\frac{\sigma^{2}(s)}{2}\mathop{}\!\kern 0.0pt\mathrm{d}s+\int_{0}^{T}\sigma(s)\mathop{}\!\kern 0.0pt\mathrm{d}W(s)}-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}e^{\int_{t_{i}}^{T}r_{R}(s)-\frac{\sigma^{2}(s)}{2}\mathop{}\!\kern 0.0pt\mathrm{d}s+\int_{t_{i}}^{T}\sigma(s)\mathop{}\!\kern 0.0pt\mathrm{d}W(s)}-K,0\right)\right]\,,

(3)

where
n
is the number of dividends whose ex-date falls between the valuation date and the maturity date.

3
Recasting the problem in terms of a basket option

Let

\displaystyle\tilde{W}(s)

\displaystyle=W(t)-\int_{0}^{t}\sigma(s)ds\,,

(4)

\displaystyle M(t)

\displaystyle=e^{\int_{0}^{t}\sigma(s)\mathop{}\!\kern 0.0pt\mathrm{d}W(s)-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\sigma^{2}(s)\mathop{}\!\kern 0.0pt\mathrm{d}s}\,.

(5)

We have

\tilde{V}_{C}(T)=C(0,T)\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}\left[M(T)\max\left(S(0)-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\alpha_{i}e^{\int_{0}^{t_{i}}\frac{\sigma^{2}(s)}{2}\mathop{}\!\kern 0.0pt\mathrm{d}s-\int_{0}^{t_{i}}\sigma(s)\mathop{}\!\kern 0.0pt\mathrm{d}W(s)}}{C(0,t_{i})}-\frac{K}{M(T)C(0,T)},0\right)\right]\,,

with
C(0,T)=e^{\int_{0}^{T}r_{R}(s)\mathop{}\!\kern 0.0pt\mathrm{d}s}
.
The Girsanov theorem
(
Shreve 2004
, Theorem 5.2.3)
tells us that
\tilde{W}
is a Brownian motion under the probability measure
\tilde{Q}
defined by
M
. The Radon-Nikodym theorem
(
Shreve 2004
, Lemma 5.2.2)
leads to

\tilde{V}_{C}(T)=C(0,T)\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{Q}}\left[\max\left(S(0)-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\alpha_{i}e^{-\int_{0}^{t_{i}}\frac{\sigma^{2}(s)}{2}\mathop{}\!\kern 0.0pt\mathrm{d}s+\int_{0}^{t_{i}}\sigma(s)\mathop{}\!\kern 0.0pt\mathrm{d}\tilde{W}(s)}}{C(0,t_{i})}-\frac{Ke^{-\int_{0}^{T}\frac{\sigma^{2}(s)}{2}\mathop{}\!\kern 0.0pt\mathrm{d}s+\int_{0}^{T}\mathop{}\!\kern 0.0pt\mathrm{d}\tilde{W}(s)}}{C(0,T)},0\right)\right]\,.

(6)

The expectation in Equation
6
corresponds to the undiscounted price under the standard Black-Scholes model of a European put option of strike
S(0)
and maturity
T
on a basket composed of the lognormal assets
(S_{i})
with drift
\mu_{i}
and volatility
\sigma_{i}
defined by

\displaystyle S_{i}(0)

\displaystyle=\alpha_{i}\,,

(7)

\displaystyle\mu_{i}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{t_{i}}r_{R}(s)\mathop{}\!\kern 0.0pt\mathrm{d}s\,,

(8)

\displaystyle\sigma_{i}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{t_{i}}\sigma^{2}(s)\mathop{}\!\kern 0.0pt\mathrm{d}s\,,

(9)

for
1\leq i\leq n
.

If
t_{n}=T
, we let
S_{n}=\alpha_{n}+K
instead of
S_{n}=\alpha_{n}
and the basket in composed of
n
assets. Otherwise, we define
S_{n+1}=K,\mu_{n+1}=-\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}r_{R}(s)\mathop{}\!\kern 0.0pt\mathrm{d}s,\sigma_{n+1}^{2}=-\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}\sigma^{2}(s)\mathop{}\!\kern 0.0pt\mathrm{d}s
and the basket is composed of
n+1
assets.

Thus, we have shown that any classic basket option approximation may be used to price European options on a stock paying discrete cash dividends, under the piecewise lognormal model.

4
Remarks

4.1
Dividend policy

In absence of a dividend policy, the piecewise lognormal model allows for negative stock prices, if
\alpha_{i}>S(t_{j}^{-})
.

Haug et al. 2003
explore two natural choices : the liquidator policy where
S(t_{i})=\max(0,S(t_{i}^{-})-\alpha_{i})
and the survivor policy where
S(t_{i})=\max(S(t_{i}^{-}),S(t_{i}^{-})-\alpha_{i})
.

The liquidator dividend policy does not impact the call option price, because we have

\displaystyle\max\left(\max\left(S(0)-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\alpha_{i}e^{\int_{0}^{t_{i}}\frac{\sigma^{2}(s)}{2}\mathop{}\!\kern 0.0pt\mathrm{d}s-\int_{0}^{t_{i}}\sigma(s)\mathop{}\!\kern 0.0pt\mathrm{d}W(s)}}{C(0,t_{i})},0\right)-\frac{K}{M(T)C(0,T)},0\right)=

\displaystyle\ \max\left(S(0)-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\alpha_{i}e^{\int_{0}^{t_{i}}\frac{\sigma^{2}(s)}{2}\mathop{}\!\kern 0.0pt\mathrm{d}s-\int_{0}^{t_{i}}\sigma(s)\mathop{}\!\kern 0.0pt\mathrm{d}W(s)}}{C(0,t_{i})}-\frac{K}{M(T)C(0,T)},0\right)

for
K\geq 0
.

It will however impact the put option price
V_{P}
, and put options must then be priced using the put-call parity formula
V_{C}-V_{P}=B(0,T_{p})(F(0,T)-K)
with forward equal to the undiscounted call option price of strike zero
F(0,T)=\tilde{V}_{C}(T,K=0)
.

4.2
Affine dividends

In addition to cash dividends, we may include proportional dividends where
S
jumps as
S(t_{j}^{+})=S(t_{j}^{-})-\beta_{j}S(t_{j}^{-})
. The formulae will not change, except for the definition of
C(0,T)
which then must include the proportional dividends as follows

C(0,T)=e^{\int_{0}^{T}r_{R}(s)\mathop{}\!\kern 0.0pt\mathrm{d}s}\prod_{j:0<t_{j}\leq T}(1-\beta_{j})\,.

Numerical tests

We assess the accuracy of the basket approach against the modern and accurate cash dividend approximation of
Sahel and Gocsei 2011
, the second-order approximation of
Zhang 2011
and the third-order formula of
Le Floc’h 2015
respectively named ”GS”, ”Zhang-2” and ”LL-3”. As basket option approximation, we evaluate the one of
Ju 2002
named ”BB-Ju” the more standard Curran based approximation of
Deelstra et al. 2010
named ”BB”, and the related lower bound approximation named ”BB-LB” in the figures and tables.

Deelstra et al. 2010
present several variations. While those do not significantly change the results, using their notation, we choose
\delta_{i}=\delta_{3}=e^{r(T-j)}
and
f_{s}(\Lambda)=f_{3}(\Lambda)=F\mathbb{G}_{F}
as it led to the best results in
(
Deelstra et al. 2010

.

5.4
Performance

The basket approximation from
Deelstra et al. 2010
requires a one-dimensional numerical integration and as such is slower than the alternatives. We use an adaptive Simpson quadrature for the integration, with an error tolerance set to
10^{-7}
, such that the result of the quadrature may be considered exact for all practical purposes.
For a large number of dividends, this basket approximation will however be faster than third-order approximations as the computational complexity is in
O(n^{2})
where
n
is the number of dividends vs.
O(n^{3})
for third-order methods.

6
Conclusion

We have shown that any basket option approximation for the standard Black-Scholes model may be directly used to price a European option on single aasset under the piecewise-lognormal model.

For a single cash dividend, the Curran based basket approximation leads to prices as accurate as advanced second-order or third-order cash dividend specific approximations. In the context of many dividends, the basket approach is found to be the most accurate one while the computational cost evolves as the square of the number of dividends, in similar fashion as the second-order cash dividend approximations. The need for a numerical integration makes it slower than other existing cash-dividend approximations in general. This is however unlikely to be a real issue in a financial system. The lower bound basket approximation, while less accurate, does not require a numerical integration and was found to be of similar accuracy as the approximation of Sahel and Gocsei, while being faster the the latter.

The technique presented in this paper may be easily extended to to price vanilla basket options under the piecewise lognormal model through the classic Black-Scholes basket option approximations, something that specific discrete dividend approximations typically do not allow.
