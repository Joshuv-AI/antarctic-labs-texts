Estimation of entropy-regularized optimal transport maps between non-compactly supported measures
Matthew Werenski, James M. Murphy, Shuchin Aeron

Abstract
This paper addresses the problem of estimating entropy-regularized optimal transport (EOT) maps with squared-Euclidean cost between source and target measures that are subGaussian. In the case that the target measure is compactly supported or strongly log-concave, we show that for a recently proposed in-sample estimator, the expected squared L 2 L^{2} -error decays at least as fast as O ( n − 1 / 3 ) O(n^{-1/3}) where n n is the sample size. For the general subGaussian case we show that the expected L 1 L^{1} -error decays at least as fast as O ( n − 1 / 6 ) O(n^{-1/6}) , and in both cases we have polynomial dependence on the regularization parameter. While these results are suboptimal compared to known results in the case of compactness of both the source and target measures (squared L 2 L^{2} -error converging at a rate O ⁡ ( n − 1 ) O(n^{-1}) ) and for when the source is subGaussian while the target is compactly supported (squared L 2 L^{2} -error converging at a rate O ( n − 1 / 2 ) O(n^{-1/2}) ), their importance lie in eliminating the compact support requirements. The proof technique makes use of a bias-variance decomposition where the variance is controlled using standard concentration of measure results and the bias is handled by T1-transport inequalities along with sample complexity results in estimation of EOT cost under subGaussian assumptions. Our experimental results point to a looseness in controlling the variance terms and we conclude by posing several open problems.

Let
\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
be the set of probability measures with finite second moment and consider
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. For notational convenience, for any functional
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
and a given measure
\mu

\mu(f)=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{X}\sim\mu}[f(\bm{X})]
with the same notation applied to joint measures. By
\mu\otimes\nu
we mean the product measure on
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

We will use the notation
\exp_{\varepsilon}(c)=\exp\left(\frac{c}{\varepsilon}\right)
. We will write
a\lesssim b
to mean that there is a constant
C>0
which may only depend on the dimension
d
such that
a\leq Cb
. For a function
F:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
we will write
\left\lVert F\right\rVert_{L^{p}(\mu)}=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{X}\sim\mu}\left[\left\lVert F(\bm{X})\right\rVert_{2}^{p}\right]^{1/p}
.

Throughout this work we will consider random variables of the following type.

Definition 1
.

A random vector
\bm{X}
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
is said to be
\sigma^{2}
-norm-subGaussian
if
\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\|\bm{X}\|^{2}/2d\sigma^{2}\right)\right]\leq 2.

and we define its norm by

\left\lVert\bm{X}\right\rVert_{\psi_{2}}=\inf\left\{\sigma>0\ \bigg|\ \mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\frac{\|\bm{X}\|^{2}}{2d\sigma^{2}}\right)\right]\leq 2\right\}.

Norm-subGaussian random vectors were introduced in
[
23

2.1
Preliminaries on Entropy-Regularized Optimal Transport

For
\varepsilon>0
The EOT problem with squared-Euclidean cost between two probability measures
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is the following minimization:

\displaystyle S_{\varepsilon}(\mu,\nu)=\min_{\pi}\pi\left(\frac{1}{2}\|\bm{x}-\bm{y}\|^{2}\right)+\varepsilon\mathsf{KL}(\pi||\mu\otimes\nu),

(1)

with
\mathsf{KL}
being the Kullback-Liebler divergence

\mathsf{KL}(\pi||\mu\otimes\nu)=\int\log\left(\frac{d\pi(\bm{x},\bm{y})}{d\mu(\bm{x})\cdot d\nu(\bm{y})}\right)d\pi(\bm{x},\bm{y})

where the optimization is performed over
\Pi(\mu,\nu)
– the set of couplings of
\mu
and
\nu
. These are probability measures over
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that

\pi[A\times\mathbb{R}^{d}]=\mu[A]
and
\pi[\mathbb{R}^{d}\times A]=\nu[A]
for every Borel measurable set
A
.

The optimization (
1
) also has a dual formulation which is given by

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} S_{\varepsilon}(\mu,\nu)=\max_{f,g}\mu(f)+\nu(g)-\varepsilon(\mu\otimes\nu)\left(\exp_{\varepsilon}\left(f+g-\frac{1}{2}\|\bm{x}-\bm{y}\|^{2}\right)\right)+\varepsilon,\end{aligned}

where the optimization is over all
f\in L_{1}(\mu),g\in L_{1}(\nu)
.
We will frequently refer to the pair
(f_{\varepsilon},g_{\varepsilon})
realizing the maximum as the (optimal) dual potentials. The optimal coupling
\pi_{\varepsilon}
is unique and the dual potentials
(f_{\varepsilon},g_{\varepsilon})
are unique up to almost everywhere equivalence and shifting by a constant (ı.e., the pair
(f_{\varepsilon}+c,g_{\varepsilon}-c)
is also optimal for any constant
c
) as long as
(\mu\otimes\nu)\left(\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert\bm{x}-\bm{y}\right\rVert^{2}\right)<\infty
(
[
28
]
Theorem 4.2). In addition,
(f_{\varepsilon},g_{\varepsilon})
is related to
\pi_{\varepsilon}
by the following formula:

\displaystyle\frac{d\pi_{\varepsilon}(\bm{x},\bm{y})}{d\mu(\bm{x})\otimes d\nu(\bm{y})}=\exp_{\varepsilon}\left(f_{\varepsilon}(\bm{x})+g_{\varepsilon}(\bm{y})-\frac{1}{2}\|\bm{x}-\bm{y}\|^{2}\right).

(2)

We denote the right hand side by
p_{\varepsilon}(\bm{x},\bm{y})
and refer to it as the
relative density
of
\pi_{\varepsilon}
with respect to
d\mu(\bm{x})\otimes d\nu(\bm{y})
.
As a consequence, the optimal dual potentials can be expressed as

\displaystyle f_{\varepsilon}(\bm{x})

\displaystyle=-\varepsilon\log\nu\left(\exp_{\varepsilon}\left(g_{\varepsilon}(\bm{y})-\frac{1}{2}\|\bm{x}-\bm{y}\|^{2}\right)\right)

\displaystyle g_{\varepsilon}(\bm{y})

\displaystyle=-\varepsilon\log\mu\left(\exp_{\varepsilon}\left(f_{\varepsilon}(\bm{x})-\frac{1}{2}\|\bm{y}-\bm{x}\|^{2}\right)\right)

and the optimal dual potentials can be taken so that equality holds for every
\bm{x}
and
\bm{y}

[
27
]
.

Given these preliminaries, the main object of study in this paper is the entropy-regularized map, introduced in
[
31
]
for approximating the unregularized OT map, defined as follows:

Definition 2
.

Let
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. The
entropy-regularized map
from
\mu
to
\nu
is defined by

T_{\varepsilon}(\bm{x})=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{Y}\sim\pi_{\varepsilon}(\cdot|\bm{x})}[\bm{Y}]=\nu\left(\bm{y}p_{\varepsilon}(\bm{x},\bm{y})\right).

As pointed out in the introduction, this map has been studied and utilized in several recent works
[
31
,
32
,
26
,
34
]
.

2.2
Estimators of EOT Maps

In practice all of the objects in the previous section must be estimated from a finite collection of samples. Given a pair of measures
\mu,\nu
one can draw a finite set of samples
\bm{X}_{1},...,\bm{X}_{n}\overset{i.i.d}{\sim}\mu,\bm{Y}_{1},...,\bm{Y}_{n}\overset{i.i.d}{\sim}\nu
. We use the shorthands
\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y}
for the random sets
\{\bm{X}_{1},...,\bm{X}_{n}\}
and
\{\bm{Y}_{1},...,\bm{Y}_{n}\}
(allowing multiplicities) and define the empirical measures

\mu_{\mathcal{X}}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{\bm{X}_{i}},\hskip 28.45274pt\nu_{\mathcal{Y}}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\delta_{\bm{Y}_{j}},\hskip 28.45274pt

where
\delta_{\bm{z}}
is the Dirac measure at the point
\bm{z}
.

When working with discrete measures we write
S_{\varepsilon}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y})=S_{\varepsilon}(\mu_{\mathcal{X}},\nu_{\mathcal{Y}})
and use
\pi_{n},(f_{n},g_{n}),
and
p_{n}
to denote the entropy-regularized optimal coupling, dual potentials, and relative densities respectively. One important difference when working with empirical measures is that we have
d\mu_{\mathcal{X}}(\bm{X}_{i})\otimes d\nu_{\mathcal{Y}}(\bm{Y}_{j})=\frac{1}{n^{2}}
which gives the relation
\pi_{n}(\bm{X}_{i},\bm{Y}_{j})=\frac{1}{n^{2}}p_{n}(\bm{X}_{i},\bm{Y}_{j}).

We can also define the sample EOT map

T_{n}(\bm{X}_{i})=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{Y}\sim\pi_{n}(\cdot|\bm{X}_{i})}[\bm{Y}]=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{1}{n}p_{n}(\bm{X}_{i},\bm{Y}_{j})\bm{Y}_{j}.

Note that the functions
f_{n},g_{n},p_{n}
and
T_{n}
are only defined on
\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y},\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y},
and
\mathcal{X}
respectively.
The potentials
f_{n}
and
g_{n}
can be
extended
to all of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
as two functions
f_{n}^{(e)},g_{n}^{(e)}
that satisfy the following two relations:

\displaystyle\exp_{\varepsilon}(f_{n}^{(e)}(\bm{x}))

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\exp_{\varepsilon}\left(g_{n}^{(e)}(\bm{Y}_{j})-\frac{1}{2}\|\bm{x}-\bm{Y}_{j}\|^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\exp_{\varepsilon}(g_{n}^{(e)}(\bm{y}))

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\exp_{\varepsilon}\left(f_{n}^{(e)}(\bm{X}_{i})-\frac{1}{2}\|\bm{X}_{i}-\bm{y}\|^{2}\right),

where
g_{n},g_{n}^{(e)}
and
f_{n},f_{n}^{(e)}
agree with each other on the samples
\mathcal{Y}
and
\mathcal{X}
, respectively
[
27
]
. This allows one to define the extended relative density

p_{n}^{(e)}(\bm{x},\bm{y})=\exp_{\varepsilon}\left(f_{n}^{(e)}(\bm{x})+g_{n}^{(e)}(\bm{y})-\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert\bm{x}-\bm{y}\right\rVert^{2}\right).

Since
g_{n},g_{n}^{(e)}
and
f_{n},f_{n}^{(e)}
agree with each other on the samples it follows that the extended relative density satisfies
p_{n}^{(e)}(\bm{X}_{i},\bm{Y}_{j})=p_{n}(\bm{X}_{i},\bm{Y}_{j})
. Based on this notion an extended sample entropy-regularized map is proposed in
[
31
]
via,

\displaystyle T_{n}^{(e)}(\bm{x})=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{1}{n}p_{n}^{(e)}(\bm{x},\bm{Y}_{j})\bm{Y}_{j}.

As pointed to in the introduction, a question which has garnered particular interest is to quantify how well
T_{n}^{(e)}
approximates
T_{\varepsilon}
, especially when the measures are not compactly supported.

Existing results have only been able to show that
T_{n}^{(e)}
converges to
T_{\varepsilon}
at a rate of
O(e^{-1/\varepsilon}n^{-1})
with respect to
\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\mu)}^{2}

[
32
]
when the measures are compactly supported, and
O(e^{-1/\varepsilon^{2}}n^{-1/2})
when the source is subGaussian and the target is compactly supported
[
26
]
. In order to alleviate the poor scaling in
\varepsilon
, an alternative estimator of
T_{\varepsilon}
that does not require an extension is given in
[
34
]
. Given a point
\bm{x}
at which an estimate of the map is sought and samples
\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y}
, define an augmented set of samples
{\mathcal{X}}^{(1)}=\{\bm{x},\bm{X}_{2},\cdots,\bm{X}_{n}\}
, (i.e., the first sample in
\mathcal{X}
is replaced by the deterministic point
\bm{x}
). Let
p_{n}^{(1)}
be the corresponding relative density. Then the estimate is defined via:

\displaystyle T_{n}^{(1)}(\bm{x})=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{1}{n}p_{n}^{(1)}(\bm{x},\bm{Y}_{j})\bm{Y}_{j}.

(3)

The main idea here is that
no extension is required
since the point at which the map needs to be estimated is in the sample set.

They also propose in
[
34
]
to divide the samples
\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y}
into
k
disjoint batches each containing
m=\frac{n}{k}
samples (assume
k,m,n
are all integers). For each batch
\ell
let the map
T^{(1)}_{\ell,m}(\bm{x})
be the estimate in (
3
) computed on
m
samples. The final estimate is computed as an average over these batches:

\hat{T}_{n}(\bm{x})=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{\ell=1}^{k}T_{\ell,m}^{(1)}(\bm{x}).

(4)

In
[
34
]

\hat{T}_{n}
is analyzed in the case where
\mu
and
\nu
have compact support and they obtain essentially the same result as Theorem
4
below.

3
Analysis Overview

Our analysis is done in essentially three steps. The first can be seen as a decomposition into a “bias" term and a “variance" term. It is an application of Jensen’s inequality which actually holds for general random vectors.

Lemma 1
.

For any measures
\mu,\nu
we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y}}[\|\hat{T}_{n}-T_{\varepsilon}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mu)}]\leq 2\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y}}[\|\hat{T}_{n}-\mathbb{E}[\hat{T}_{n}]\|_{L^{2}(\mu)}^{2}]+2\|\mathbb{E}[\hat{T}_{n}]-T_{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{2}(\mu)}^{2}.

The proof of this result and most of the technical results are deferred to the supplement. Here the “bias" term is
\|\mathbb{E}[\hat{T}_{n}]-T_{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{2}(\mu)}^{2}
which expresses the difference in the expectation and the function
T_{\varepsilon}
we are trying to estimate. The “variance" term is
\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y}}[\|\hat{T}_{n}-\mathbb{E}[\hat{T}_{n}]\|_{L^{2}(\mu)}^{2}]
which expresses how much that estimate
\hat{T}_{n}
deviates from its expectation.

The two quantities are handled in quite different ways. It turns out that the variance term is easier to analyze and is done so by the following result.

Lemma 2
.

Let
\bm{Y}\sim\nu
. Then

\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y}}[\|\hat{T}_{n}-\mathbb{E}[\hat{T}_{n}]\|_{L^{2}(\mu)}^{2}]\leq\mathbb{E}[\left\lVert\bm{Y}-\mathbb{E}\bm{Y}\right\rVert^{2}]/k.

In particular if
\left\lVert\bm{Y}\right\rVert_{\psi_{2}}<\infty
, then

\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y}}[\|\hat{T}_{n}-\mathbb{E}[\hat{T}_{n}]\|_{L^{2}(\mu)}^{2}]\leq 2\log(2)d\left\lVert\bm{Y}\right\rVert_{\psi_{2}}^{2}/k.

The bias term is more complicated. The general strategy is to define a measure
\tilde{\pi}
which satisfies the following property. For every measurable set
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
it holds

\tilde{\pi}[A]=\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y}}\left[\pi_{m}[A]\right]=(\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y}}\left[\pi_{m}\right])[A].

The evaluation of
\pi_{m}[A]
can be done for fixed samples
\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y}
via the following formula:

\pi_{m}[A]=\frac{1}{m^{2}}\sum_{i,j=1}^{m}p_{m}(\bm{X}_{i},\bm{Y}_{j})\cdot\boldsymbol{1}[(\bm{X}_{i},\bm{Y}_{j})\in A].

The measure
\tilde{\pi}
can be considered the average sample entropy-regularized coupling. The measure
\tilde{\pi}
depends on the choice of
m
, but since all the results involving
\tilde{\pi}
hold for any
m\in\mathbb{N}
we exclude it from the notation. Also note that we use
m
instead of
n
to define
\tilde{\pi}
because we are interested in an estimate from a single batch of size
m
instead of a sum over
k
batches (which would use
n
samples). This measure has several properties of note.

Proposition 1
.

The measure
\tilde{\pi}
has density
d\tilde{\pi}(\bm{x},\bm{y})
given by

\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y}}\left[p_{m}(\bm{X}_{1},\bm{Y}_{1})\big|\bm{X}_{1}=\bm{x},\bm{Y}_{1}=\bm{y}]d\mu(\bm{x})d\nu(\bm{y}).

(5)

In addition
\tilde{\pi}
has marginals
\mu
and
\nu
. As a consequence,
\tilde{\pi}
has conditional density
d\tilde{\pi}(\bm{y}|\bm{x})
given by

\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y}}\left[p_{m}(\bm{X}_{1},\bm{Y}_{1})\ |\ \bm{X}_{1}=\bm{x},\bm{Y}_{1}=\bm{y}\right]d\nu(\bm{y}).

From (
5
) we can see that the interpretation of
d\tilde{\pi}(\bm{x},\bm{y})
is that it is the average amount of mass the empirical coupling gives to the pair
(\bm{x},\bm{y})
, conditioned on both points being the first sample in
\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{Y}
respectively.

An important consequence of Proposition
1
is the following identity for
\hat{T}_{n}(\bm{x})
.

Corollary 1
.

For fixed
\bm{x}
it holds

\mathbb{E}[\hat{T}_{n}(\bm{x})]=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{Y}\sim\tilde{\pi}(\cdot|\bm{x})}[\bm{Y}].

This provides the identity

\mathbb{E}[\hat{T}_{n}](\bm{x})-T_{\varepsilon}(\bm{x})=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{Y}\sim\tilde{\pi}(\cdot|\bm{x})}[\bm{Y}]-\mathbb{E}_{\bm{Y}\sim\pi_{\varepsilon}(\cdot|\bm{x})}[\bm{Y}].

In several settings discussed below, using transport inequalities
[
18
]
we are able to control the deviation on the right using a KL-Divergence bound:

\displaystyle\|\mathbb{E}_{\bm{Y}\sim\tilde{\pi}(\cdot|\bm{x})}[\bm{Y}]-\mathbb{E}_{\bm{Y}\sim\pi_{\varepsilon}(\cdot|\bm{x})}[\bm{Y}]\|^{2}\lesssim\mathsf{KL}(\tilde{\pi}(\cdot|\bm{x})\ ||\ \pi_{\varepsilon}(\cdot|\bm{x})).

(6)

The final step is to bound the KL-divergence, which is handled by the following two results:

Proposition 2
.

With the notation above, we have for any measures
\mu,\nu
:

\displaystyle\mathsf{KL}(\tilde{\pi}\ ||\ \pi_{\varepsilon})

\displaystyle=\mu(\mathsf{KL}(\tilde{\pi}(\cdot|\bm{X})\ ||\ \pi_{\varepsilon}(\cdot|\bm{X})))

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y}}[|S_{\varepsilon}(\mu,\nu)-S_{\varepsilon}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y})|].

This result is Lemma 8 in
[
34
]
, and we include a proof using our notation in the supplement. The final result that we will require is the following.

Theorem 1
.

(
[
27
]
)
Let
\mu,\nu
be
\sigma^{2}
-norm-subGaussian. Then

\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y}}[|S_{\varepsilon}(\mu,\nu)-S_{\varepsilon}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y})|]\lesssim\varepsilon\left(1+\frac{\sigma^{\lceil 5d/2\rceil+6}}{\varepsilon^{\lceil 5d/4\rceil+3}}\right)\cdot\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}

4
Compactly-Supported and Strongly Log-Concave Target Measures

As noted above, the procedure above is applicable as long as we are able to justify a KL-Divergence upper bound. In this section we cover two cases where this can be done using known results: compactly supported and strongly log-concave measures. These require the following definition.

Definition 3
.

Let
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. The
Laplace functional
of
\mu
is given by

E_{\mu}(\lambda)=\sup_{f}\mathbb{E}_{\bm{X}\sim\mu}[e^{\lambda f(\bm{X})}]

where the supremum is over all functions
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
which are 1-Lipschitz with respect to the Euclidean distance and satisfy
\mu(f)=0
.

The following T1-transport inequality is classical.

Theorem 2
.

(
[
5
,
24
]
)
Let
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Then

W_{1}(\mu,\nu)\leq\sqrt{2C\mathsf{KL}(\nu||\mu)}

for some
C>0
and all
\nu
if and only if

E_{\mu}(\lambda)\leq e^{C\lambda^{2}/2},\hskip 28.45274pt\forall\ \lambda\geq 0.

This theorem is useful in our setting because of the inequality

\displaystyle\|\mathbb{E}_{\bm{Y}\sim\tilde{\pi}(\cdot|\bm{x})}[\bm{Y}]-\mathbb{E}_{\bm{Y}\sim\pi_{\varepsilon}(\cdot|\bm{x})}[\bm{Y}]\|^{2}\leq W_{1}^{2}(\tilde{\pi}(\cdot|\bm{x}),\pi_{\varepsilon}(\cdot|\bm{x}))

(7)

which follows from taking a
W_{1}
-optimal coupling of
\tilde{\pi}(\cdot|\bm{x})
and
\pi_{\varepsilon}(\cdot|\bm{x})
followed by Jensen’s inequality. Therefore, whenever we can apply Theorem
2
we have

\displaystyle\|\mathbb{E}_{\bm{Y}\sim\tilde{\pi}(\cdot|\bm{x})}[\bm{Y}]-\mathbb{E}_{\bm{Y}\sim\pi_{\varepsilon}(\cdot|\bm{x})}[\bm{Y}]\|^{2}\leq 2C\mathsf{KL}(\tilde{\pi}(\cdot|\bm{x})\ ||\ \pi_{\varepsilon}(\cdot|\bm{x}))

which is the key step that we required in the previous section. We have now turned our task to showing that the Laplace functional of
\pi_{\varepsilon}(\cdot|\bm{x})
satisfies the bound in Theorem
2
.

\bm{x}
we have

W_{1}^{2}(\pi_{\varepsilon}(\cdot|\bm{x}),\tilde{\pi}(\cdot|\bm{x}))\leq 8R\cdot\mathsf{KL}(\tilde{\pi}(\cdot|\bm{x})\ ||\ \pi_{\varepsilon}(\cdot|\bm{x})).

Combining the arguments of this section and the previous we have the following result.

Theorem 4
.

Let
\mu
be
\sigma^{2}
-norm-subGaussian and let
\nu
be a probability measure with support contained in
B(0,R)
for
R<\infty
. Let
\sigma_{0}^{2}=\max(\sigma^{2},R^{2}/(2d\log 2))
so that
\mu,\nu
are both
\sigma_{0}^{2}
-norm-subGaussian. Then

\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y}}\left[\left\lVert\hat{T}_{n}-T_{\varepsilon}\right\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\mu)}\right]\lesssim\frac{R^{2}}{k}+R\left(1+\frac{\sigma_{0}^{\lceil 5d/2\rceil+6}}{\varepsilon^{\lceil 5d/4\rceil+3}}\right)\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}

In particular if
k=n^{1/3},m=n^{2/3}

\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y}}\left[\left\lVert\hat{T}_{n}-T_{\varepsilon}\right\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\mu)}\right]\lesssim R\left(1+R+\frac{\sigma_{0}^{\lceil 5d/2\rceil+6}}{\varepsilon^{\lceil 5d/4\rceil+3}}\right)\frac{1}{n^{1/3}}.

4.2
Strongly Log-Concave Measures

Another setting where Theorem
2
can be applied is when the measure
\nu
is strongly log-concave.

Definition 4
.

A probability measure
\nu
is said to be
c
-
strongly log-concave
if it has density with respect to the Lebesgue measure of the form
q(\bm{y})\propto e^{-W(\bm{y})}
for a
c
-strongly convex function
W
. That is,

\nabla^{2}W(\bm{y})\succeq c\text{I}

for every
\bm{y}
in the domain of
W
.

Strongly log-concave measures satisfy the following bound, which is essentially contained in
[
24
]
.

Theorem 5
.

Let
\nu
be
c
-strongly log-concave. Then the Laplace functional satisfies

E_{\nu}(\lambda)\leq e^{\frac{1}{c}\lambda^{2}/2}.

For completeness, a proof is given in the supplement and is based on the relationship between strong log-concavity, log-Sobolev inequalities, and the Laplace functional. The next result allows us to apply Theorem
5
to the family of measures
\pi_{\varepsilon}(\cdot|\bm{x})
.

Lemma 3
.

Let
\nu
be
c
-strongly log-concave. Then for every
\bm{x}
the measure
\pi_{\varepsilon}(\cdot|\bm{x})
is also
c
-strongly log-concave.

This result follows from (
2
) and the fact that the function
\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert^{2}-g_{\varepsilon}
is convex. Importantly the constant strong log-concavity parameter
c
is independent of the point
\bm{x}
.

Combining Lemma
3
, Theorem
5
, and Theorem
2
we obtain the following bound.

Theorem 6
.

Let
\nu
be
c
-strongly log-concave. Then for every
\bm{x}
we have

W_{1}^{2}(\pi_{\varepsilon}(\cdot|\bm{x}),\tilde{\pi}(\cdot|\bm{x}))\leq\frac{2}{c}\mathsf{KL}(\pi_{\varepsilon}(\cdot|\bm{x})\ ||\ \tilde{\pi}(\cdot|\bm{x})).

The following result is now immediate from the approach outlined in Section
3
.

Theorem 7
.

Let
\mu
be
\sigma^{2}
-norm-subGaussian and let
\nu
be
c
-strongly log-concave and mean zero. Let
\sigma_{0}=\max(\sigma^{2},K/\sqrt{c})
, where
K>0
is a universal constant, so that
\mu
and
\nu
are
\sigma_{0}^{2}
-norm-subGaussian. Then

\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y}}\left[\left\lVert\hat{T}_{n}-T_{\varepsilon}\right\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\mu)}\right]\lesssim\frac{d}{ck}+\frac{1}{c}\left(1+\frac{\sigma_{0}^{\lceil 5d/2\rceil+6}}{\varepsilon^{\lceil 5d/4\rceil+3}}\right)\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}.

In particular if
k=n^{1/3},m=n^{2/3}

\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y}}\left[\left\lVert\hat{T}_{n}-T_{\varepsilon}\right\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\mu)}\right]\lesssim\frac{1}{c}\left(d+\frac{\sigma_{0}^{\lceil 5d/2\rceil+6}}{\varepsilon^{\lceil 5d/4\rceil+3}}\right)\cdot\frac{1}{n^{1/3}}

5
General SubGaussian Measures

We start with a result on the Laplace functional of norm-subGaussian random variables.

Lemma 4
.

Let
\bm{X}\sim\mu
be such that
\left\lVert\bm{X}\right\rVert_{\psi_{2}}^{2}<\infty
. Then

E_{\mu}(\lambda)\leq e^{8d\left\lVert\bm{X}\right\rVert_{\psi_{2}}^{2}\lambda^{2}}.

Next we will prove a utility result on subGaussian random variables; we state it in generality and specialize to our case.

Lemma 5
.

Let
\mu,\nu
be probability measures on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and let
\pi
be a probability measure on
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
with marginals
\mu
and
\nu
. Let
\bm{X}\sim\mu,\bm{Y}\sim\nu,
and let
\bm{Y}^{\bm{x}}
be distributed according to
\pi(\cdot|\bm{x})
. Then it holds

\mathbb{E}_{\bm{X}}\left[\left\lVert\bm{Y}^{\bm{X}}\right\rVert_{\psi_{2}}^{2}\right]\leq 2\left\lVert\bm{Y}\right\rVert_{\psi_{2}}^{2}

This result essentially says that most of the conditional measures of a joint distribution are at least as well concentrated as the marginal. This is a key technical tool as will be seen in the proof of the following result.

Theorem 8
.

Let
\mu,\nu
be
\sigma^{2}
-norm-subGaussian. Then

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left\lVert\hat{T}_{n}-T_{\varepsilon}\right\rVert_{L^{1}(\mu)}\right]\lesssim\frac{\sqrt{d\sigma^{2}}}{\sqrt{k}}+\sqrt{d\sigma^{2}\left(1+\frac{\sigma^{\lceil 5d/2\rceil+6}}{\varepsilon^{\lceil 5d/4\rceil+3}}\right)}\frac{1}{m^{1/4}}.

In particular if
k=n^{1/3},m=n^{2/3}
then

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left\lVert\hat{T}_{n}-T_{\varepsilon}\right\rVert_{L^{1}(\mu)}\right]\lesssim\sqrt{d\sigma^{2}\left(1+\frac{\sigma^{\lceil 5d/2\rceil+6}}{\varepsilon^{\lceil 5d/4\rceil+3}}\right)}\frac{1}{n^{1/6}}.

Proof.

We start in a similar way to the previous proofs:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left\lVert\hat{T}_{n}-T_{\varepsilon}\right\rVert_{L^{1}(\mu)}\leq\mathbb{E}\left\lVert\hat{T}_{n}-\mathbb{E}[\hat{T}_{n}]\right\rVert_{L^{1}(\mu)}+\left\lVert\mathbb{E}[\hat{T}_{n}]-T_{\varepsilon}\right\rVert_{L^{1}(\mu)}.

The first term is handled by Jensen’s inequality:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left\lVert\hat{T}_{n}-\mathbb{E}[\hat{T}_{n}]\right\rVert_{L^{1}(\mu)}\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\left\lVert\hat{T}_{n}-\mathbb{E}[\hat{T}_{n}]\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\mu)}^{2}\right]^{1/2}

and the resulting term can be controlled by Lemma
2
.

For the other term, we have:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\bm{X}}\left[\left\lVert\mathbb{E}[\hat{T}_{n}](\bm{X})-T_{\varepsilon}(\bm{X})\right\rVert\right]\leq\mathbb{E}_{\bm{X}}\left[W_{1}(\tilde{\pi}(\cdot|\bm{X}),\pi_{\varepsilon}(\cdot|\bm{X}))\right].

The strategy now is to apply Theorem
2
conditionally with a constant that depends on the value of
\bm{X}
. In particular if we let
\bm{Y}^{\bm{x}}\sim\tilde{\pi}(\cdot|\bm{x})
then we have by Lemma
4
and Theorem
2
that:

\displaystyle W_{1}(\tilde{\pi}(\cdot|\bm{x}),\pi_{\varepsilon}(\cdot|\bm{x}))\leq\sqrt{16d\left\lVert\bm{Y}^{\bm{x}}\right\rVert^{2}_{\psi_{2}}\mathsf{KL}(\tilde{\pi}(\cdot|\bm{x})\ ||\ \pi_{\varepsilon}(\cdot|\bm{x}))}

Using this inequality above and applying Cauchy-Schwarz we have:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\bm{X}}\left[W_{1}(\tilde{\pi}(\cdot|\bm{X}),\pi_{\varepsilon}(\cdot|\bm{X}))\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{\bm{X}}\left[\sqrt{16d\left\lVert\bm{Y}^{\bm{X}}\right\rVert^{2}_{\psi_{2}}\mathsf{KL}(\tilde{\pi}(\cdot|\bm{X})\ ||\ \pi_{\varepsilon}(\cdot|\bm{X}))}\right]

\displaystyle\leq 4\sqrt{d}\mathbb{E}_{\bm{X}}\left[\left\lVert\bm{Y}^{\bm{X}}\right\rVert^{2}_{\psi_{2}}\right]^{1/2}\cdot\mathbb{E}_{\bm{X}}\left[\mathsf{KL}(\tilde{\pi}(\cdot|\bm{X})\ ||\ \pi_{\varepsilon}(\cdot|\bm{X}))\right]^{1/2}.

The result now follows from Lemma
5
, Proposition
2
, and Theorem
1
.
∎

The main reason that we obtain a different bound in Theorem
8
compared to Theorems
4
and
7
is that we do not have a uniform control on the constant used when conditionally applying Theorem
2
. This is handled by Cauchy-Schwarz inequality which adds an additional square-root, leading to a bound of
n^{-1/6}
instead of
n^{-1/3}
. We suspect that this may be avoidable (see Conjecture
2
).

7
Open Problems and Conclusion

The Variance Conjecture.

The most impactful open question is stated in Conjecture
1
, and is empirically observed in Figures
2
and
3
. A positive answer would provide a substantial strengthening of Lemma
2
from convergence of the order
k^{-1}
to
n^{-1}
. As a consequence the bounds in Theorems
4
and
7
can be improved from order
k^{-1}+m^{-1/2}
to
n^{-1}+m^{-1/2}
. Choosing
m=n
gives the bound
n^{-1}+n^{-1/2}
which in turn improves the convergence rate from
n^{-1/3}
to
n^{-1/2}
. In addition, the result in Theorem
8
can be improved from
n^{-1/6}
to
n^{-1/4}
in a similar way.

Squaring in the subGaussian Case.

As discussed after the proof of Theorem
8
, conditional application of Theorem
2
appears to be a substantial technical hurdle to obtaining a version of Theorem
8
of the same type as Theorems
4
and
7
. However, we expect that the following result should be possible.

Conjecture 2
.

In the same setting as Theorem
8
, it holds that

\mathbb{E}\left[\left\lVert\hat{T}_{n}-T_{\varepsilon}\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\mu)}^{2}\right]\lesssim n^{-1/3}

where the implicit constant may depend on any parameter besides
n
.

It may be the case that obtaining such a result requires a different analysis altogether. Additionally, if Conjecture
1
holds then this should be improvable to
n^{-1/2}
.

Convergence at a rate of
n^{-1}
.

It is shown in
[
32
]
that for compactly supported measures the entropy-regularized map converges at a rate of
n^{-1}
, even faster than the rate of
n^{-1/2}
recovered in our proofs. At least for multivariate Gaussian source and target we empirically observe the same rate of convergence in Figure
1
. Obtaining this rate explicitly is likely an interesting avenue of study.

Conjecture 3
.

Suppose that
\mu,\nu
are mean-zero multivariate Gaussians. Then

\mathbb{E}\left[\left\lVert\hat{T}_{n}-T_{\varepsilon}\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\mu)}^{2}\right]\lesssim\frac{1}{n}.

It is still open to identify in which settings one can expect this even faster rate to apply.

Conclusion

In this work we have established a framework for obtaining convergence rates for an estimate of the EOT map, established convergence rates in three settings, two of which do not require compactness of the support, and posed three important open questions based on numerical investigations. Furthermore, it will be interesting to directly compare the extended and the in-sample estimators discussed in Section
2
. While it is intuitive that they should be close to each other since they both converge to
T_{\varepsilon}
with
n
, quantifying the similarity will shed more light on possible tradeoffs between the two estimators including the polynomial vs exponential dependence on
\varepsilon
vs the rate in
n
. Finally, establishing high-probability versions of these results is also an important direction of study.

Proof of Lemma
2

Proof.

Throughout the proof the expectations are to be taken with respect to the random sets
\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{Y}
unless otherwise stated.

First recall the formula for the estimator (
4
),

\hat{T}_{n}=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{\ell=1}^{k}T^{(1)}_{\ell,m}

and therefore

\mathbb{E}[\hat{T}_{n}]=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{\ell=1}^{k}\mathbb{E}[T_{\ell,m}^{(1)}].

Since
\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\mu)}
is a Hilbert space we have

\displaystyle\left\lVert\hat{T}_{n}-\mathbb{E}[\hat{T}_{n}]\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\mu)}^{2}

\displaystyle=\left\langle\frac{1}{k}\sum_{\ell=1}^{k}\left(T_{\ell,m}^{(1)}-\mathbb{E}\left[T_{\ell,m}^{(1)}\right]\right),\frac{1}{k}\sum_{\ell^{\prime}=1}^{k}\left(T_{\ell^{\prime},m}^{(1)}-\mathbb{E}\left[T_{\ell^{\prime},m}^{(1)}\right]\right)\right\rangle_{L^{2}(\mu)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{k^{2}}\sum_{\ell=1}^{k}\left\lVert T_{\ell,m}^{(1)}-\mathbb{E}\left[T_{\ell,m}^{(1)}\right]\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\mu)}^{2}+\frac{1}{k^{2}}\sum_{\ell\neq\ell^{\prime}}^{k}\left\langle T_{\ell,m}^{(1)}-\mathbb{E}\left[T_{\ell,m}^{(1)}\right],T_{\ell^{\prime},m}^{(1)}-\mathbb{E}\left[T_{\ell^{\prime},m}^{(1)}\right]\right\rangle_{L^{2}(\mu)}.

Taking expectations on both sides and using the fact that the
T_{\ell,m}^{(1)}
are independent and identically distributed we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left\lVert\hat{T}_{n}-\mathbb{E}[\hat{T}_{n}]\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\mu)}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{k^{2}}\sum_{\ell=1}^{k}\mathbb{E}\left\lVert T_{\ell,m}^{(1)}-\mathbb{E}\left[T_{\ell,m}^{(1)}\right]\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\mu)}^{2}+\frac{1}{k^{2}}\sum_{\ell\neq\ell^{\prime}}\mathbb{E}\left\langle T_{\ell,m}^{(1)}-\mathbb{E}\left[T_{\ell,m}^{(1)}\right],T_{\ell^{\prime},m}^{(1)}-\mathbb{E}\left[T_{\ell^{\prime},m}^{(1)}\right]\right\rangle_{L^{2}(\mu)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{k}\mathbb{E}\left\lVert T_{1,m}^{(1)}-\mathbb{E}\left[T_{1,m}^{(1)}\right]\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\mu)}^{2}+\frac{k^{2}-k}{k^{2}}\left\langle\mathbb{E}\left[T_{1,m}^{(1)}-\mathbb{E}\left[T_{1,m}^{(1)}\right]\right],\mathbb{E}\left[T_{2,m}^{(1)}-\mathbb{E}\left[T_{2,m}^{(1)}\right]\right]\right\rangle_{L^{2}(\mu)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{k}\mathbb{E}\left\lVert T_{1,m}^{(1)}-\mathbb{E}\left[T_{1,m}^{(1)}\right]\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\mu)}^{2}

where we have used in the third line the independence and identical distribution to move to remove the summations and also bring the expectations inside the inner product.

Now exchanging the order of integration we have

\mathbb{E}\left\lVert T_{1,m}^{(1)}-\mathbb{E}\left[T_{1,m}^{(1)}\right]\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\mu)}^{2}=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{X}}\left[\mathbb{E}\left[\left\lVert T_{1,m}^{(1)}(\bm{X})-\mathbb{E}\left[T_{1,m}^{(1)}(\bm{X})\right]\right\rVert^{2}\right]\right].

Now observe that for every
i=1,...,m
we have by exchangeability

\mathbb{E}_{\bm{X}}\left[\mathbb{E}\left[\left\lVert T_{1,m}^{(1)}(\bm{X})-\mathbb{E}\left[T_{1,m}^{(1)}(\bm{X})\right]\right\rVert^{2}\right]\right]=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{X}}\left[\mathbb{E}\left[\left\lVert T_{1,m}^{(i)}(\bm{X})-\mathbb{E}\left[T_{1,m}^{(i)}(\bm{X})\right]\right\rVert^{2}\right]\right].

Importantly by the variational formula for variance we have the inequality for every fixed
\bm{x}
and
i

\mathbb{E}\left[\left\lVert T_{1,m}^{(i)}(\bm{x})-\mathbb{E}\left[T_{1,m}^{(i)}(\bm{x})\right]\right\rVert^{2}\right]\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\left\lVert T_{1,m}^{(i)}(\bm{x})-\mathbb{E}[\bm{Y}]\right\rVert^{2}\right]

From this it follows that

\mathbb{E}_{\bm{X}}\left[\mathbb{E}\left[\left\lVert T_{1,m}^{(1)}(\bm{X})-\mathbb{E}\left[T_{1,m}^{(1)}(\bm{X})\right]\right\rVert^{2}\right]\right]\leq\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mathbb{E}_{\bm{X}}\left[\mathbb{E}\left[\left\lVert T_{1,m}^{(i)}(\bm{X})-\mathbb{E}[\bm{Y}]\right\rVert^{2}\right]\right]

The key observation at this step is that
T_{1,m}^{(i)}(\bm{X})
is a deterministic function of
(\bm{X}_{1},...,\bm{X}_{i-1},\bm{X},\bm{X}_{i+1},...,\bm{X}_{m},\mathcal{Y})
and
T_{1,m}(\bm{X}_{i})
is the same deterministic function of
(\bm{X}_{1},...,\bm{X}_{i-1},\bm{X}_{i},\bm{X}_{i+1},...,\bm{X}_{m},\mathcal{Y})
and the random variables
(\bm{X}_{1},...,\bm{X}_{i-1},\bm{X},\bm{X}_{i+1},...,\bm{X}_{m},\mathcal{Y})
and
(\bm{X}_{1},...,\bm{X}_{i-1},\bm{X}_{i},\bm{X}_{i+1},...,\bm{X}_{m},\mathcal{Y})
have the same distribution (both are distributed according to
\mu^{\otimes m}\otimes\nu^{\otimes m}
). From this we can conclude that

\mathbb{E}_{\bm{X}}\left[\mathbb{E}\left[\left\lVert T_{1,m}^{(i)}(\bm{X})-\mathbb{E}[\bm{Y}]\right\rVert^{2}\right]\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\left\lVert T_{1,m}(\bm{X}_{i})-\mathbb{E}[\bm{Y}]\right\rVert^{2}\right].

Now using the above we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mathbb{E}_{\bm{X}}\left[\mathbb{E}\left[\left\lVert T_{1,m}^{(i)}(\bm{X})-\mathbb{E}[\bm{Y}]\right\rVert^{2}\right]\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mathbb{E}\left[\left\lVert T_{1,m}(\bm{X}_{i})-\mathbb{E}[\bm{Y}]\right\rVert^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left\lVert T_{1,m}(\bm{X}_{i})-\mathbb{E}[\bm{Y}]\right\rVert^{2}\right].

From here we will use equation (
2.2
) for
T_{1,m}(\bm{X}_{i})
and (
9
) to write

T_{1,m}(\bm{X}_{i})-\mathbb{E}[\bm{Y}]=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\frac{1}{m}p_{1,m}(\bm{X}_{i},\bm{Y}_{j})(\bm{Y_{j}}-\mathbb{E}[\bm{Y}])

where the notation
p_{1,m}
is intended to mean the relative density
p_{m}
on batch 1.

From this formula we see that if we let
\mathbf{Y}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times d}
be the matrix with
j
’th row
\bm{Y}_{j}-\mathbb{E}[\bm{Y}]
and let
\mathbf{P}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}
be the matrix with
\mathbf{P}_{i,j}=\frac{1}{m}p_{1,m}(\bm{X}_{i},\bm{Y}_{j})
then

T_{1,m}(\bm{X}_{i})-\mathbb{E}[\bm{Y}]=[\mathbf{P}\mathbf{Y}]_{i}

the
i
’th column of the matrix multiplication. Therefore

\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left\lVert T_{1,m}(\bm{X}_{i})-\mathbb{E}[\bm{Y}]\right\rVert^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left\lVert[\mathbf{P}\mathbf{Y}]_{i}\right\rVert^{2}=\left\lVert\mathbf{P}\mathbf{Y}\right\rVert_{F}^{2}

where
\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{F}
is the Frobenius norm of a matrix. In addition,
\mathbf{P}
is a doubly stochastic matrix and as such
\mathbf{P}\in B_{m}
the Birkhoff polytope
[
3
]
.
Since the function

\mathbf{A}\mapsto\left\lVert\mathbf{A}\mathbf{Y}\right\rVert_{F}^{2}

is convex we have

\left\lVert\mathbf{P}\mathbf{Y}\right\rVert_{F}^{2}\leq\max_{\mathbf{B}\in B_{n}}\left\lVert\mathbf{B}\mathbf{Y}\right\rVert_{F}^{2}=\left\lVert\mathbf{Y}\right\rVert_{F}^{2}

which follows from Bauer’s Maximum Principle
[
2
]
, which states that the maximum of a convex function on a convex set occurs at the extreme points, and the fact that the extreme points of
B_{n}
are permutation matrices
[
3

\displaystyle=\varepsilon\log\mu\left(\exp_{\varepsilon}\left((1-\lambda)\left(f_{\varepsilon}(\bm{x})-\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert\bm{x}\right\rVert^{2}+\langle\bm{y}_{0},\bm{x}\rangle\right)+\lambda\left(f_{\varepsilon}(\bm{x})-\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert\bm{x}\right\rVert^{2}+\langle\bm{y}_{1},\bm{x}\rangle\right)\right)\right)

Now a version of Hölder’s inequality states that for any random variables
\bm{U},\bm{V}
and
\lambda\in(0,1)
it holds

\log\mathbb{E}\left[e^{(1-\lambda)\bm{U}+\lambda\bm{V})}\right]\leq(1-\lambda)\log\mathbb{E}[e^{\bm{U}}]+\lambda\mathbb{E}[e^{\bm{V}}].

(12)

Applying (
12
) to the random variables

\displaystyle\bm{U}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\left(f_{\varepsilon}(\bm{X})-\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert\bm{X}\right\rVert^{2}+\langle\bm{y}_{0},\bm{X}\rangle\right)

\displaystyle\bm{V}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\left(f_{\varepsilon}(\bm{X})-\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert\bm{X}\right\rVert^{2}+\langle\bm{y}_{1},\bm{X}\rangle\right)

above gives

\displaystyle\varepsilon\log\mu\left(\exp_{\varepsilon}\left((1-\lambda)\left(f_{\varepsilon}(\bm{x})-\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert\bm{x}\right\rVert^{2}+\langle\bm{y}_{0},\bm{x}\rangle\right)+\lambda\left(f_{\varepsilon}(\bm{x})-\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert\bm{x}\right\rVert^{2}+\langle\bm{y}_{1},\bm{x}\rangle\right)\right)\right)

\displaystyle\leq(1-\lambda)\varepsilon\log\mu\left(\exp_{\varepsilon}\left(f_{\varepsilon}(\bm{x})-\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert\bm{x}\right\rVert^{2}+\langle\bm{y}_{0},\bm{X}\rangle\right)\right)+\lambda\varepsilon\log\mu\left(\exp_{\varepsilon}\left(f_{\varepsilon}(\bm{x})-\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert\bm{x}\right\rVert^{2}+\langle\bm{y}_{1},\bm{X}\rangle\right)\right)

\displaystyle=(1-\lambda)\left(\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert\bm{y}_{0}\right\rVert^{2}-g_{\varepsilon}(\bm{y}_{0})\right)+\lambda\left(\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert\bm{y}_{1}\right\rVert^{2}-g_{\varepsilon}(\bm{y}_{1})\right)

Proof of Theorem

Proof.

Before proceeding to the proof we collect a few facts.

First we have

\mathbb{E}\left\lVert\bm{Y}-\mathbb{E}\bm{Y}\right\rVert^{2}=\text{Tr}\text{Cov}(\bm{Y})

and by the Brascamp-Lieb inequality
[

]
and the fact that inverses reverse the PSD ordering we have

\text{Cov}(\bm{Y})\precsim\mathbb{E}[(\nabla^{2}W(\bm{Y}))^{-1}]\precsim(c\text{Id})^{-1}=\frac{1}{c}\text{Id}.

Now using the fact that

we have

\mathbb{E}\left\lVert\bm{Y}-\mathbb{E}\bm{Y}\right\rVert^{2}=\text{Tr}\text{Cov}(\bm{Y})\leq\text{Tr}\frac{1}{c}\text{Id}=\frac{d}{c}.

This will be useful for bounding the variance term.

By Theorem 5.2.15 in
[

]
it holds that there exists a universal constant
K
such that

\mathbb{E}\left[e^{c\bm{Y}_{i}^{2}/K}\right]\leq 2

for
i=1,...,d
and therefore

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\frac{\left\lVert\sqrt{c}\bm{Y}\right\rVert^{2}}{2dK}\right)\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{d}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\exp\left(\frac{c\bm{Y}_{i}^{2}}{2K}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{d}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\frac{c\bm{Y}_{i}^{2}}{K^{\prime}}\right)\right]\leq 2

and therefore
\bm{Y}
is
(K^{\prime}/\sqrt{c})
-norm-subgaussian for a universal constant

.

Now we can proceed to the main proof.

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y}}\left[\left\lVert\hat{T}_{n}-T_{\varepsilon}\right\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\mu)}\right]

\displaystyle\lesssim\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y}}[\|\hat{T}_{n}-\mathbb{E}[\hat{T}_{n}]\|_{L^{2}(\mu)}^{2}]+\|\mathbb{E}[\hat{T}_{n}]-T_{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{2}(\mu)}^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{d}{ck}+\frac{1}{c}\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\mathbb{E}|S_{\varepsilon}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y})-S_{\varepsilon}(\mu,\nu)|

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{d}{ck}+\frac{1}{c}\left(1+\frac{\sigma_{0}^{\lceil 5d/2\rceil+6}}{\varepsilon^{\lceil 5d/4\rceil+3}}\right)\cdot\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}.

C.1
Proof of Lemma
4

The proof requires several preliminary results on subGaussian random variables.

Lemma 6
.

Let
\bm{X}
be a scalar random variable
\sigma^{2}\in\mathbb{R}
and
K>2
be such that

\mathbb{E}\left[e^{\bm{X}^{2}/\sigma^{2}}\right]\leq K.

Then

\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\frac{\bm{X}^{2}}{\sigma^{2}\log(K)/\log(2)}\right)\right]\leq 2.

Proof.

Using basic logarithm properties we have

\frac{\log(2)}{\log(K)}=\log_{K}(2),\hskip 14.22636ptK^{\log_{K}(2)}=2,\hskip 14.22636pt0=\log_{K}(1)<\log_{K}(2)<\log_{K}(K)=1.

Importantly the last inequality implies that the function
t\mapsto t^{\log_{K}(2)}
is concave.

From this we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\frac{\bm{X}^{2}}{\sigma^{2}\log(K)/\log(2)}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\frac{\bm{X}^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}\right)^{\log_{K}(2)}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\frac{\bm{X}^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}\right)\right]^{\log_{K}(2)}

\displaystyle(\text{Jensen})

\displaystyle\leq K^{\log_{K}(2)}=2.

\displaystyle(\text{By Assumption})

∎

Lemma 7
.

Let
\bm{X}
be a scalar random variable such that
\mathbb{E}[\bm{X}]=0
and
\mathbb{E}[e^{\bm{X}^{2}/\sigma^{2}}]\leq 2
. Then for every
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
it holds

\mathbb{E}[\exp(\lambda\bm{X})]\leq\exp\left(\sigma^{2}\lambda^{2}\right).

Proof.

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\exp(\lambda\bm{X})]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(\lambda\bm{X})^{k}}{k!}\right]

\displaystyle=1+\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{k=2}^{\infty}\frac{(\lambda\bm{X})^{k}}{k!}\right]

\displaystyle(\mathbb{E}\bm{X}=0)

\displaystyle\leq 1+\frac{\lambda^{2}}{2}\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{X}^{2}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{|\lambda\bm{X}|^{k}}{k!}\right]

\displaystyle(\frac{1}{k!}\leq\frac{1}{2}\frac{1}{(k-2)!}\text{ for }k\geq 2)

\displaystyle=1+\frac{\lambda^{2}}{2}\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{X}^{2}\exp(|\lambda\bm{X}|)\right]

\displaystyle\leq 1+\frac{\lambda^{2}}{2}\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{X}^{2}\exp\left(\frac{\bm{X}^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}+\frac{\sigma^{2}\lambda^{2}}{2}\right)\right]

\displaystyle(\text{Young's Inequality})

\displaystyle=1+\frac{\sigma^{2}\lambda^{2}}{2}e^{\sigma^{2}\lambda^{2}/2}\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{\bm{X}^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}\exp\left(\frac{\bm{X}^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\right)\right]

\displaystyle\leq 1+\frac{\sigma^{2}\lambda^{2}}{2}e^{\sigma^{2}\lambda^{2}/2}\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\frac{\bm{X}^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}\right)\right]

\displaystyle(t\leq e^{t/2})

\displaystyle\leq 1+\sigma^{2}\lambda^{2}e^{\sigma^{2}\lambda^{2}/2}

\displaystyle(\text{By Assumption})

\displaystyle\leq e^{\lambda^{2}\sigma^{2}}

\displaystyle(1+xe^{x/2}\leq e^{x}\ \forall\ x\geq 0)

∎

Lemma 8
.

Let
\left\lVert\bm{X}\right\rVert_{\psi_{2}}^{2}<\infty
. For every mean-zero 1-Lipschitz function
f
it holds

\mathbb{E}[e^{f(\bm{X})^{2}/8d\left\lVert\bm{X}\right\rVert_{\psi_{2}}^{2}}]\leq 2.

Proof.

Let
\sigma_{0}
be a constant to be determined later. Let
\bm{X}^{\prime}
be an iid copy of
\bm{X}
. We compute:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\frac{f(\bm{X})^{2}}{\sigma_{0}^{2}}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\frac{(f(\bm{X})-\mathbb{E}[f(\bm{X}^{\prime})])^{2}}{\sigma_{0}^{2}}\right)\right]

\displaystyle(\mathbb{E}[f(\bm{X}^{\prime})]=0)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\frac{(f(\bm{X})-f(\bm{X}^{\prime}))^{2}}{\sigma_{0}^{2}}\right)\right]

\displaystyle(\text{Jensen})

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\frac{\left\lVert\bm{X}-\bm{X}^{\prime}\right\rVert^{2}}{\sigma_{0}^{2}}\right)\right]

\displaystyle(f\text{ 1-Lip.})

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\frac{2\left\lVert\bm{X}\right\rVert^{2}+2\left\lVert\bm{X}^{\prime}\right\rVert^{2}}{\sigma_{0}^{2}}\right)\right]

\displaystyle(\text{Jensen})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\frac{2\left\lVert\bm{X}\right\rVert^{2}}{\sigma_{0}^{2}}\right)\right]^{2}

\displaystyle(\text{Independence})

Note that if
\sigma_{0}^{2}=4d\left\lVert\bm{X}\right\rVert_{\psi_{2}}^{2}
then

\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\frac{2\left\lVert\bm{X}\right\rVert^{2}}{\sigma_{0}^{2}}\right)\right]^{2}=\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\frac{\left\lVert\bm{X}\right\rVert^{2}}{2d\left\lVert\bm{X}\right\rVert_{\psi_{2}}^{2}}\right)\right]^{2}\leq 2^{2}=4

where the bound follows from the definition of
\left\lVert\bm{X}\right\rVert_{\psi_{2}}
. By applying Lemma
6
with
K=4
this shows that

\mathbb{E}[e^{f(\bm{X})^{2}/8d\left\lVert\bm{X}\right\rVert_{\psi_{2}}^{2}}]\leq 2.

∎

Combining the previous two results we can prove the required bound on the Laplace functional as follows.

Proof of Lemma
4
.

Let

be any mean-zero 1-Lipschitz function. By Lemma

we have

\mathbb{E}[e^{f(\bm{X})^{2}/8d\left\lVert\bm{X}\right\rVert_{\psi_{2}}^{2}}]\leq 2

and therefore by Lemma

C.2
Proof of Lemma
5

Proof.

Note that by the law of total expectation we have

2\geq\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\frac{\left\lVert\bm{Y}\right\rVert^{2}}{2d\left\lVert\bm{Y}\right\rVert_{\psi_{2}}^{2}}\right)\right]=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{X}}\left[\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\frac{\left\lVert\bm{Y}^{\bm{X}}\right\rVert^{2}}{2d\left\lVert\bm{Y}\right\rVert_{\psi_{2}}^{2}}\right)\right]\right].

Since the expectation is finite we have with probability 1 (with respect to
\bm{X}
) that

\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\frac{\left\lVert\bm{Y}^{\bm{X}}\right\rVert^{2}}{2d\left\lVert\bm{Y}\right\rVert_{\psi_{2}}^{2}}\right)\right]<\infty.

In particular we can use Lemma
6
to show that for
\mu
almost every
\bm{x}

\left\lVert\bm{Y}^{\bm{x}}\right\rVert_{\psi_{2}}\leq\max\left(\left\lVert\bm{Y}\right\rVert_{\psi_{2}},\left\lVert\bm{Y}\right\rVert_{\psi_{2}}\sqrt{\frac{\log\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left((\left\lVert\bm{Y}^{\bm{x}}\right\rVert^{2}/2d\left\lVert\bm{Y}\right\rVert^{2}_{\psi_{2}}\right)\right]}{\log(2)}}\right).

For convenience define the set

A=\left\{\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\ \bigg|\ \mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\left\lVert\bm{Y}^{\bm{x}}\right\rVert^{2}/2d\left\lVert\bm{Y}\right\rVert^{2}_{\psi_{2}}\right)\right]>2\right\}.

Now squaring and taking expectations on both sides we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\bm{X}}\left[\left\lVert\bm{Y}^{\bm{X}}\right\rVert_{\psi_{2}}^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert\bm{Y}\right\rVert_{\psi_{2}}^{2}\mathbb{E}_{\bm{X}}\left[\max\left(1,\sqrt{\frac{\log\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\left\lVert\bm{Y}^{\bm{X}}\right\rVert^{2}/2d\left\lVert\bm{Y}\right\rVert^{2}_{\psi_{2}}\right)\right]}{\log(2)}}\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\left\lVert\bm{Y}\right\rVert_{\psi_{2}}^{2}\mathbb{P}(\bm{X}\notin A)+\left\lVert\bm{Y}\right\rVert_{\psi_{2}}^{2}\mathbb{E}\left[\boldsymbol{1}[\bm{X}\in A]\frac{\log\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\left\lVert\bm{Y}^{\bm{X}}\right\rVert^{2}/2d\left\lVert\bm{Y}\right\rVert^{2}_{\psi_{2}}\right)\right]}{\log(2)}\right]

where we have used that when
\bm{X}\in A
the maximum occurs at the second term and when
\bm{X}\not\in A
the maximum is achieved by the first.

To control the latter term we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\bm{X}}\left[\boldsymbol{1}[\bm{X}\in A]\frac{\log\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\left\lVert\bm{Y}^{\bm{X}}\right\rVert^{2}/2d\left\lVert\bm{Y}\right\rVert^{2}_{\psi_{2}}\right)\right]}{\log(2)}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{\bm{X}}\left[\frac{\log\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\left\lVert\bm{Y}^{\bm{X}}\right\rVert^{2}/2d\left\lVert\bm{Y}\right\rVert^{2}_{\psi_{2}}\right)\right]}{\log(2)}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\log 2}\log\mathbb{E}_{\bm{X}}\left[\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\left\lVert\bm{Y}^{\bm{X}}\right\rVert^{2}/2d\left\lVert\bm{Y}\right\rVert^{2}_{\psi_{2}}\right)\right]\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\log 2}\log\mathbb{E}\left[\exp(\left\lVert\bm{Y}\right\rVert^{2}/2d\left\lVert\bm{Y}\right\rVert^{2}_{\psi_{2}})\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\log 2}{\log 2}=1

Overall this gives the bound

\mathbb{E}_{\bm{X}}\left[\left\lVert\bm{Y}^{\bm{X}}\right\rVert_{\psi_{2}}^{2}\right]\leq(1+\mathbb{P}(\bm{X}\notin A))\left\lVert\bm{Y}\right\rVert_{\psi_{2}}^{2}\leq 2\left\lVert\bm{Y}\right\rVert_{\psi_{2}}^{2}

∎
