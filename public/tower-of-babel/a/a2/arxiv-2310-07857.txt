On ( 1 + ε ) (1+{\varepsilon}) -Approximate Flow Sparsifiers
Yu Chen, Zihan Tan

Abstract
Given a large graph G G with a subset | T | = k |T|=k of its vertices called terminals, a quality- q q flow sparsifier is a small graph G ′ G^{\prime} that contains T T and preserves all multicommodity flows that can be routed between terminals in T T , to within factor q q . The problem of constructing flow sparsifiers with good (small) quality and (small) size has been a central problem in graph compression for decades. A natural approach of constructing O ⁡ ( 1 ) O(1) -quality flow sparsifiers, which was adopted in most previous constructions, is contraction. Andoni, Krauthgamer, and Gupta constructed a sketch of size f ⁡ ( k , ε ) f(k,{\varepsilon}) that stores all feasible multicommodity flows up to a factor of ( 1 + ε ) (1+{\varepsilon}) , raised the question of constructing quality- ( 1 + ε ) (1+{\varepsilon}) flow sparsifiers whose size only depends on k , ε k,{\varepsilon} (but not the number of vertices in the input graph G G ), and proposed a contraction-based framework towards it using their sketch result. In this paper, we settle their question for contraction-based flow sparsifiers, by showing that quality- ( 1 + ε ) (1+{\varepsilon}) contraction-based flow sparsifiers with size f ⁡ ( ε ) f({\varepsilon}) exist for all 5 5 -terminal graphs, but not for all 6 6 -terminal graphs. Our hardness result on 6 6 -terminal graphs improves upon a recent hardness result by Krauthgamer and Mosenzon on exact (quality- 1 1 ) flow sparsifiers, for contraction-based constructions. Our construction and proof utilize the notion of tight spans in metric geometry, which we believe is a powerful tool for future work.

Introduction

Graph compression is a paradigm of converting large graphs into smaller ones that faithfully preserve crucial features, such as flow/cut values and distances. It involves reducing the size of graphs prior to subsequent computation, and thereby significantly saving computational resources.
This paradigm has proved powerful in designing faster and better approximation algorithms on graphs.

1.2
Technical Overview

The size lower bound (for exact flow sparsifiers) in
[
KM23
]
was proved by analyzing the slope of the “feasible demand polytope” of certain graphs. However, it seems hard for their approach to give strong lower bounds for quality-
(1+{\varepsilon})
flow sparsifiers. We employ a different approach, more similar to the ones in
[
Moi09
,
LM10
]
and
[
AGK14
]
.

In
[
Moi09
]
and
[
LM10
]
, the connection between the
0
-Extension
problem and the construction of flow/cut sparsifier without Steiner nodes was established. In a
0
-Extension instance, we are given an undirected edge-capacitated graph
G=(V,E,c)

T\subseteq V
of its vertices called
terminals

D
on terminals, and the goal is to find a mapping
f:V\to T
that maps each vertex to a terminal in
T
, such that each terminal is mapped to itself (i.e.,
f(t)=t
for all
t\in T
), and the sum
\sum_{(u,v)\in E}c{(u,v)}\cdot D(f(u),f(v))
is minimized. Typically, the integrality gap of its
semi-metric relaxation LP
was shown to be an upper bound of the best quality achievable by flow sparsifiers (without Steiner nodes). In order to investigate flow sparsifiers with Steiner vertices, we study the following variant of
0
-Extension, called
0
-Extension with Steiner Nodes

3

In an instance of the

•

•

a set

k

A solution consists of

•

a partition

•

a semi-metric

We define the

Typically, the following ratio is a central measure to be minimized, called

\rho=\frac{\sum_{(u,v)\in E}c(u,v)\cdot\delta(F(u),F(v))}{\sum_{(u,v)\in E}c(u,v)\cdot\ell_{(u,v)}}.

We are also allowed to manipulate the distances between these non-terminals, conditioned on not destroying the shortest-path distance metric (in
G

AGK14
]

We then turn to study the
\mathsf{0EwSN}

Observe that in the current graph, all terminal shortest paths are supported by vertex

(that is,
a
-

, and
b
-

shortest path is
(b,p,c)
). So we should not modify the lengths of edges
(a,p),(b,p),(c,p)
, as shortening one by
\delta
would force us to lengthening the other two by
\delta

q
-incident edges can be manipulated without distorting the terminal distances. For example, we can shorten
(b,q)
from

and

that is at distance
3
from

and
2
from

.

This simple example actually illustrates the first step (out of two) of our algorithm for the
\mathsf{0EwSN}
problem, which we call the

step. The main idea is to repeatedly reduce the distances from non-terminals to terminals in some simple way until we cannot do so anymore without destroying the terminal-induced metric. Clearly, at the end of this process, for every non-terminal
q
, and every terminal
t
, there should be another terminal
t^{\prime}
such that
\textnormal{{dist}}(q,t)+\textnormal{{dist}}(q,t^{\prime})=\textnormal{{dist}}(t,t^{\prime})
holds, and it is this tight constraint that prevents us from further shortening the
q
-
t

tight span
(first proposed and studied in
[
Dre84
]
), characterizing the resulting distances that we may get from the projection step.

Tight Span.

Let
D
be a metric on a set
T
of points. The
tight span
of
D
, denoted as
\mathsf{TS}(D)
, is defined as

\mathsf{TS}(D)=\bigg\{x=(x_{t})_{t\in T}\in(\mathbb{R}^{+})^{T}\text{ }\bigg|\text{ }x_{t}=\max_{t^{\prime}\in T}\left\{D(t,t^{\prime})-x_{t^{\prime}}\right\}\bigg\}.

So elements in
\mathsf{TS}(D)
are
|T|
-dimensional vectors with coordinates indexed by points in
T
.
Intuitively, we can think of an element
x\in\mathsf{TS}(D)
as an “imaginary point” in the metric space
(T,D)
, that is at distance
x_{t}
to each
t\in T
.
The distances
\left\{x_{t}\right\}_{t\in T}
need to satisfy triangle inequalities with the distances in
\left\{D(t,t^{\prime})\right\}_{t,t^{\prime}}
. That is,
for all pairs
t,t^{\prime}
of points in
T
,
x_{t}+x_{t^{\prime}}\geq D(t,t^{\prime})
must hold. Equivalently, for all
t\in T
,
x_{t}\geq\max_{t^{\prime}\in T}\left\{D(t,t^{\prime})-x_{t^{\prime}}\right\}
.
Moreover, for
x
to be in the tight span, it is additionally required that,
for each point
t\in T
, at least one inequality in
\left\{x_{t}+x_{t^{\prime}}\geq D(t,t^{\prime})\right\}_{t^{\prime}}
is tight
(and therefore the name), giving that

x_{t}=\max_{t^{\prime}\in T}\left\{D(t,t^{\prime})-x_{t^{\prime}}\right\}
.

We then show that, we can efficiently project all non-terminals into the tight span of the terminal metric, such that the length of every edge does not increase (where the tight span is equipped with the geodesic distance, or equivalently the
\ell_{\infty}
norm). This means that we only need to solve
\mathsf{0EwSN}
instance that are “embedded into the tight span”. (See
Section

4
.)

Now constructing a solution to
\mathsf{0EwSN}
(with a bounded size) is essentially partitioning the tight span into a finite number of components (and then each component is contracted as a Steiner node) that does not cut too many edges. A desirable property of a metric space for such a good partitioning to exist is the following property called
separability
. Specifically, we say that a metric space
(X,D)
(where
|X|=+\infty
) is
separable
, iff there exists a finite subset
\bar{X}\subseteq X
and a (randomized) mapping
f:X\to\bar{X}
, such that
for every pair
x,x^{\prime}\in X
,

\mathbb{E}[D(f(x),f(x^{\prime}))]=D(x,x^{\prime}).

For example, a line metric (where
X=[a,b]
and
D(x,x^{\prime})=|x-x^{\prime}|
) is separable, as we can simply define
\bar{X}=\left\{a,b\right\}
and choose a random threshold value
c\in[a,b]
and set
f(x)=a
iff
x\leq c
and
f(x)=b
iff
x>c
.
Similarly, a rectangle
\overline{abcd}
with an axis-aligned
\ell_{1}
metric is also separable (with
X=\left\{a,b,c,d\right\}
and
f
determined by two random threshold values).

The crucial distinction between our results for
5
-terminal and
6
-terminal networks is due to the following fact that we prove in
Section

5
and
Section

6
:

The tight span of all
5
-point metrics are separable, but there are
6
-point metrics whose tight spans are not separable.

To see why the tight spans of all
5
-point metrics are separable, a vague explanation is that
5
-point tight spans are “at most
2
-dimensional”, and
2
-dimensional rectangles with
\ell_{\infty}
norm, after properly changing the coordinate systems, become
2
-dimensional
\ell_{1}
spaces, and are therefore separable from the above discussion. (See
Section

5
.)
The intuition for
5
-point tight spans to be
2
-dimensional is as follows. Recall that the elements in
5
-point tight spans are
5
-dimensional vectors, and for such a vector
x
to be in the tight span, it is additionally required that, for each coordinate
t\in T
, at least one triangle inequality in
\left\{x_{t}+x_{t^{\prime}}\geq D(t,t^{\prime})\right\}_{t^{\prime}}
is tight. Therefore, in a proper region of the tight span, at least three triangle inequalities need to be tight, reducing the “degree of freedom” of the
5
-dimensional vector to be
2
(and therefore making the region essentially
2
-dimensional). Vaguely, if there are only two tight inequalities, say
T=\left\{a,b,c,d,e\right\}

x_{a}+x_{b}=D(a,b)
,
x_{c}+x_{d}=D(c,d)
, then nothing prevents us from further reducing
x_{e}
, the distance between
x
and
e
.

On the other hand, the tight span for
6
-point metrics can be
3
-dimensional, as in a proper region, all
6
-dimensional vectors may satisfy the tight constraints
x_{a}+x_{b}=D(a,b)
,
x_{c}+x_{d}=D(c,d)
, and
x_{e}+x_{f}=D(e,f)
, and so no coordinate can be further reduced. It appears that the geodesic metric in
3
-dimensonal tight span regions differs fundamentally from
2
-dimensonal regions (which are
\ell_{1}
metrics), and are therefore not separable. We manage to leverage this non-separability to construct hard instances for
\mathsf{0EwSN}
and eventually generalize them to provide a similar lower bound for the version in
[
AGK14
]
, which in turn leads to lower bounds for contraction-based flow sparsifiers. The construction and the analysis of the hard instances are the most technical components of the paper.
(See
Section

6
.)

Related Work

Cut sparsifiers.

Cut sparsifiers are closely related to flow sparsifiers.
Given a graph
G
and a set
T\subseteq V(G)
of terminals, a cut sparsifier of
G
with respect to
T
is a graph
G^{\prime}
with
T\subseteq V(G^{\prime})
, such that for every partition
(T_{1},T_{2})
of
T
, the size of the minimum cut separating
T_{1}
from
T_{2}
in
G
and the size of the minimum cut separating
T_{1}
from
T_{2}
in
G^{\prime}
, are within some small multiplicative factor
q
, which is also called the
quality
of the sparsifier.
Quality-
q
flow sparsifiers are also quality-
q
cut sparsifiers, but the converse is not true.

In a special case where
V(G^{\prime})=T
, Moitra
[
Moi09
]
showed that every graph with
k
terminals admits a cut sparsifier with quality
O(\log k/\log\log k)
, and the strongest lower bound is
\Omega(\sqrt{\log k}/\log\log k)

[
MM10
,
CLLM10
]
.
In another special case where
q=1
, it was shown that every
k
-terminal graph admits an exact cut sparsifier of size at most
2^{2^{k}}

[
HKNR98
,
KR14
]
, and the strongest lower bound is
2^{\Omega(k)}

[
KR14
,
KR13
,
KPZP17
]
. Closing this gap remains a very interesting open problem.
If we further assume that each terminal has degree
1
, then Chuzhoy
[
Chu12
]
has shown the construction of
O(1)
-quality cut sparsifier of size
O(k^{3})
, and Kratsch and Wahlstrom
[
KW12
]
have constructed quality-
1
cut sparsifiers of size
O(k^{3})
via a matroid-based approach.

There are also some other recent work on (i) constructing better cut sparsifiers for special types of graphs, for example trees
[
GR17
]
, planar graphs
[
KR17
,
KPZP17
,
GHP17
]
; (ii) preserving terminal min-cut values up to some threshold
[
CDK+21

(iii) dynamic cut/flow sparsifiers and their use in dynamic graph algorithms

DGGP19

Distance sparsifiers.

There are also rich lines of work for constructing vertex/edge sparsifiers for preserving distances (e.g. spanners, emulators, distance-preserving minor, distance oracles, etc). We refer the readers to the comprehensive survey

By default, all logarithms are to the base of

Let

)

.
For a vertex

For a pair

Demands, congestion, and quality of a sparsifier.

Let
G
be a graph and let
T
be a subset of its vertices called
terminals
. A demand
{\mathcal{D}}
on
T
is a function that assigns to each (unordered) pair
t,t^{\prime}\in T
a real number
{\mathcal{D}}(t,t^{\prime})\geq 0
. Let
F
be a multi-commodity flow on
G
. We say that
F

routes

{\mathcal{D}}
, iff for every pair
t,t^{\prime}\in T
,
F
sends
{\mathcal{D}}(t,t^{\prime})
units of flow from
t
to
t^{\prime}
(or from
t^{\prime}
to
t
) in
G
. The
congestion
of flow
F
in
G
, denoted by
\mathsf{cong}_{G}(F)
, is defined to be the maximum amount of flow sent via a single edge in
G
.
The
volume
of flow
F
, denoted by
\mathsf{vol}(F)
, is defined to be the sum, over all edges in
G
, the total amount of flow sent via it, so clearly
\mathsf{cong}_{G}(F)\geq\mathsf{vol}(F)/|E(G)|
.
The
congestion
of
{\mathcal{D}}
in
G
, denoted by
\mathsf{cong}_{G}({\mathcal{D}})
, is defined to be the minimum congestion of any flow that routes
{\mathcal{D}}
in
G
.

Let
H
be a graph with
T\subseteq V(H)
. We say that
H
is a
flow sparsifier
of
G
with respect to
T
with
quality

q\geq 1
, iff for any demand

A graph

4
Tight Span and its Projection

A key ingredient to our algorithm and lower bound is the theory of
tight span
in metric geometry. The notion of tight span was first proposed and studied in
[
Dre84
]
. As a central notion in T-theory
[
DMT96
]
, it has proved powerful in the study of phylogenetic analysis and optimal reconstruction of metrics

[
DHM01
,
DHLM06
,
HKMS08
,
KLM09
,
DHK+12
,
HMS21
]
.

Let
D
be a metric on a set
T
of points. The
tight span
of
D
, denoted as
\mathsf{TS}(D)
, is defined as

\mathsf{TS}(D)=\bigg\{x=(x_{t})_{t\in T}\in(\mathbb{R}^{+})^{T}\text{ }\bigg|\text{ }x_{t}=\max_{t^{\prime}\in T}\left\{D(t,t^{\prime})-x_{t^{\prime}}\right\}\bigg\}.

So elements in
\mathsf{TS}(D)
are
|T|
-dimensional vectors with coordinates indexed by points in
T
.
Intuitively, we can think of an element
x\in\mathsf{TS}(D)
as an “imaginary point” in the metric space
(T,D)
, that is at distance
x_{t}
to each
t\in T
.
The distances
\left\{x_{t}\right\}_{t\in T}
need to satisfy triangle inequalities with the distances in
\left\{D(t,t^{\prime})\right\}_{t,t^{\prime}}
. That is,
for all pairs
t,t^{\prime}
of points in
T
,
x_{t}+x_{t^{\prime}}\geq D(t,t^{\prime})
must hold. Equivalently, for all
t\in T
,
x_{t}\geq\max_{t^{\prime}\in T}\left\{D(t,t^{\prime})-x_{t^{\prime}}\right\}
.
Moreover, for
x
to be in the tight span, it is additionally required that,
for each point
t\in T
, at least one inequality in
\left\{x_{t}+x_{t^{\prime}}\geq D(t,t^{\prime})\right\}_{t^{\prime}}
is tight
(and therefore the name), giving that

x_{t}=\max_{t^{\prime}\in T}\left\{D(t,t^{\prime})-x_{t^{\prime}}\right\}
.
We say that
t
and
t^{\prime}
, as coordinates of the vector
x
, are
involved
in the triangle inequality
x_{t}+x_{t^{\prime}}\geq D(t,t^{\prime})
. So in other words,
x\in\mathsf{TS}(D)
means every coordinate of
x
is involved in some tight inequality.

4.2
Projection onto the tight span

We say that a vector
x\in\mathbb{R}^{T}
is
valid
iff it satisfies all triangle inequalities in
\left\{x_{t}+x_{t^{\prime}}\geq D(t,t^{\prime})\right\}_{t,t^{\prime}}
.
We now describe a mapping that maps all valid vectors to vectors in
\mathsf{TS}(D)
, that is non-expanding under
\ell_{\infty}
norm.
The existence of such a mapping was proved in Section 5.2 of
[
DHK+12
]
(there the mapping is called a “contraction”). Here we describe an algorithm that computes the mapping.

For a valid vector that does not lie in
\mathsf{TS}(D)
, we describe a process that
projects
x\in\mathbb{R}^{T}
onto
\mathsf{TS}(D)
, at a vector which we denote by
\mathsf{proj}(x)
. Intuitively,
\mathsf{proj}(x)
is obtained from
x
by decreasing the value of its coordinates at the same rate until some triangle inequalities become tight, freezing the coordinates that are involved in such inequalities and continuing on the remaining coordinates.

We now describe the process in detail. Throughout, we maintain a vector
\hat{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{T}
, that is initialized to be
x
. Recall that coordinates of
x
and
\hat{x}
are indexed by points in
T
. Throughout the algorithm, all coordinates are either
active
or
inactive
. Initially, they are all active. The algorithm performs iterations until all coordinates become inactive.

In an iteration, we compute

•

for each pair
t,t^{\prime}
of active coordinates,
\Delta_{t,t^{\prime}}=\frac{1}{2}\cdot(\hat{x}_{t}+\hat{x}_{t^{\prime}}-D(t,t^{\prime}))
; and

•

for each active
t
and inactive
t^{\prime}
,
\Delta_{t,t^{\prime}}=\hat{x}_{t}+\hat{x}_{t^{\prime}}-D(t,t^{\prime})
;

and we compute
\Delta_{t}=\min_{t^{\prime}\in T}\left\{\Delta_{t,t^{\prime}}\right\}
and
\Delta=\min_{t\in T}\left\{\Delta_{t}\right\}
. Then, for each active coordinate
t
,

•

update
\hat{x}_{t}\leftarrow\hat{x}_{t}-\Delta
; and

•

if
\Delta_{t}=\Delta
holds, mark
t
inactive.

When all coordiates are inactive, we stop and return
\hat{x}
as
\mathsf{proj}(x)
.
This completes the description of the algorithm.

We now prove some of its properties. We start by showing in the following claim that the projection
\mathsf{proj}(x)
computed by the algorithm indeed lies in
\mathsf{TS}(D)
. The proof is straightforward and is deferred to
Section

B.1
. Intuitively, the moment that a coordinate
t
becomes inactive, some inequality in
\left\{\hat{x}_{t}+\hat{x}_{t^{\prime}}-D(t,t^{\prime})\right\}_{t^{\prime}}
becomes tight and stays tight until the end.

Claim 6
.

\mathsf{proj}(x)
lies in
\mathsf{TS}(D)
.

We next prove the following crucial property of the projection function
\mathsf{proj}
, that states that
\mathsf{proj}
is non-expanding with respect to
\ell_{\infty}
norm.

Lemma 7
.

For any valid vectors
x,x^{\prime}
,
\left\|\mathsf{proj}(x)-\mathsf{proj}(x^{\prime})\right\|_{\infty}\leq\left\|x-x^{\prime}\right\|_{\infty}
.

Proof.

We start by proving the following claim.

Claim 8
.

For any coordinate
t\in T
, there is another coordinate
t^{\prime}\in T
, such that

\big(\mathsf{proj}(x)\big)_{t}+\big(\mathsf{proj}(x)\big)_{t^{\prime}}=D(t,t^{\prime}),\text{ and }x_{t}-\big(\mathsf{proj}(x)\big)_{t}\geq x_{t^{\prime}}-\big(\mathsf{proj}(x)\big)_{t^{\prime}}.

Proof.

Consider the iteration that coordinate
t
becomes inactive. From the algorithm, there exists another
t^{\prime}
such that in that iteration
\Delta=\Delta_{t,t^{\prime}}(=\Delta_{t}=\Delta_{t^{\prime}})
. From similar arguments in the proof of
6
, we know that after this iteration,
\hat{x}_{t}+\hat{x}_{t^{\prime}}=D(t,t^{\prime})
holds and coordinate
t^{\prime}
is inactive, and they will stay so until the end of the algorithm.
This means that coordinate
t^{\prime}
becomes inactive no later than
t^{\prime}
. From the algorithm, in each iteration, every active coordinate is decreased by the same amount (
\Delta
in that iteration) and every inactive coordinate stays the same. Therefore, the total decrease in
\hat{x}_{t}
is at least the total decrease in
\hat{x}_{t^{\prime}}
, implying that
x_{t}-\big(\mathsf{proj}(x)\big)_{t}\geq x_{t^{\prime}}-\big(\mathsf{proj}(x)\big)_{t^{\prime}}
.
∎

For convenience, we denote
\alpha=\mathsf{proj}(x)
and
\beta=\mathsf{proj}(x^{\prime})
, and for each coordinate
t\in T
, we denote

\hat{\alpha}_{t}=x_{t}-\alpha_{t}
and

\hat{\beta}_{t}=x^{\prime}_{t}-\beta_{t}
.
We choose
t
such that (it is easy to see that such
t
exists)

•

maximizes
|\alpha_{t}-\beta_{t}|
; and

•

among all
t^{\prime}
that maximize
|\alpha_{t}-\beta_{t}|
, there is no other
t^{\prime}
with
\hat{\alpha}_{t}\geq\hat{\alpha}_{t^{\prime}}
and
\hat{\beta}_{t}>\hat{\beta}_{t^{\prime}}
, or
\hat{\alpha}_{t}>\hat{\alpha}_{t^{\prime}}
and
\hat{\beta}_{t}\geq\hat{\beta}_{t^{\prime}}
.

Assume without loss of generality that
\alpha_{t}\geq\beta_{t}
. It suffices to show that, for every
t\in T
,
|\alpha_{t}-\beta_{t}|\leq\left\|x-x^{\prime}\right\|_{\infty}
.
First, by definition,

\alpha_{t}-\beta_{t}=(x_{t}-\hat{\alpha}_{t})-(x^{\prime}_{t}-\hat{\beta}_{t})\leq|x_{t}-x^{\prime}_{t}|-\hat{\alpha}_{t}+\hat{\beta}_{t}\leq\left\|x-x^{\prime}\right\|_{\infty}-\hat{\alpha}_{t}+\hat{\beta}_{t}.

By
8
, there exists another coordinate
t^{\prime}\in T
such that
\alpha_{t}+\alpha_{t^{\prime}}=D(t,t^{\prime})
and
\hat{\alpha}_{t}\geq\hat{\alpha}_{t^{\prime}}
.
From triangle inequality
\beta_{t}+\beta_{t^{\prime}}\geq D(t,t^{\prime})
,

\alpha_{t}-\beta_{t}=(D(t,t^{\prime})-\alpha_{t^{\prime}})-\beta_{t}\leq(D(t,t^{\prime})-\alpha_{t^{\prime}})-(D(t,t^{\prime})-\beta_{t^{\prime}})=\beta_{t^{\prime}}-\alpha_{t^{\prime}}.

and using similar arguments and the fact that
\hat{\alpha}_{t}\geq\hat{\alpha}_{t^{\prime}}
, we can show that

\beta_{t^{\prime}}-\alpha_{t^{\prime}}\leq\left\|x-x^{\prime}\right\|_{\infty}-\hat{\beta}_{t^{\prime}}+\hat{\alpha}_{t^{\prime}}\leq\left\|x-x^{\prime}\right\|_{\infty}-\hat{\beta}_{t^{\prime}}+\hat{\alpha}_{t}.

Altogether, we get that
2\cdot(\alpha_{t}-\beta_{t})\leq 2\left\|x-x^{\prime}\right\|_{\infty}-\hat{\beta}_{t^{\prime}}+\hat{\beta}_{t}
.
Now

•

if
\hat{\beta}_{t}\leq\hat{\beta}_{t^{\prime}}
, then

•

Remark 9

Metric

At the end of this section, we define a metric

For example, from

As another example (illustrated in

Associated vectors.

In order to talk about points in
\mathsf{TS}(D)
in a more convenient way, for each point
v\in\mathsf{TS}(D)
, instead of using the
6
-dimensional vector
(v_{a},v_{b},v_{c},v_{d},v_{e},v_{f})
, we will represent it in some other way tailored to the structure of
\mathsf{TS}(D)
.
Specifically, we form a (non-orthogonal) coordinate system as follows. Let
\vec{ch}
be the
x
-direction, let
\vec{cg}
be the
y
-direction, let
\vec{ca}
be the
z
-direction, and let
c
be the reference point, so every point is uniquely represented as a
3
-dimensional vector
(v[x],v[y],v[z])
in this system, which we call its
associated vector
.
For example, the associated vector for terminal
a
is
(0,0,1)
, for
h
is
(1,0,0)
and for
g
is
(0,2,0)
.
More generally,

•

if
v
is in the rectangle, then
v[x]=v_{b}-1
,
v[x]+v[y]=v_{c}
and
v[z]=0
;

•

if
v
is in the prism, then
v[x]+1-v[z]=v_{a}
,
v[x]+v[z]=v_{b}
and
v[x]+v[y]+v[z]=v_{c}
.

Solving and combining them, we obtain the following observation.

Observation 11
.

For every
v\in\mathsf{TS}(D)
,

v_{a}=\left|v[x]\right|+1-v[z],\quad\quad v_{b}=v[x]+1+v[z],\quad\quad v_{c}=v[x]+v[y]+v[z],

v_{d}=2-v[x]+v[z],\quad\quad v_{e}=\left|v[x]-1\right|+1-v[z],\quad\quad v_{f}=3-v[x]-v[y]-v[z].

Observation 12
.

If the associated vector of
v
is
(x,y,z)
and the associated vector of
v^{\prime}
is
(x^{\prime},y^{\prime},z^{\prime})
, then

•

(v,v^{\prime})_{\mathsf{TS}}\geq|x-x^{\prime}|+|z-z^{\prime}|
;

•

if
z=z^{\prime}=0
, then
(v,v^{\prime})_{\mathsf{TS}}=\frac{1}{2}\cdot\big(\left|(2x+y)-(2x^{\prime}+y^{\prime})\right|+\left|y-y^{\prime}\right|\big)
.

Proof.

Remember that the shortest path between
v
and
v^{\prime}
has to travel only in the critical directions. If we travel in direction
1
or
2
, then the
x
coordinate will not change, and the change of
z
coordinate is exactly the distance it travelled. If we travel in direction
3
or
4
, the
z
coordinate of a point will not change, and the change of
x
coordinate is exactly the distance it travelled. Thus for any shortest path between
v
and
v^{\prime}
, we need to travel at least
|x-x^{\prime}|
on direction
1
and
2
, and at least
|z-z^{\prime}|
on direction
3
or
4
. Therefore,
(v,v^{\prime})_{\mathsf{TS}}\geq|x-x^{\prime}|+|z-z^{\prime}|
.

Note that
z=z^{\prime}=0
, so
v
and
v^{\prime}
both lie in the rectangle. On the one hand, from
11
,
\left|v_{a}-v^{\prime}_{a}\right|
and
\left|v_{e}-v^{\prime}_{e}\right|
are both at most
\left|x-x^{\prime}\right|
, which equals
\left|v_{b}-v^{\prime}_{b}\right|
and
\left|v_{d}-v^{\prime}_{d}\right|
. On the other hand,
\left|v_{c}-v^{\prime}_{c}\right|=\left|v_{f}-v^{\prime}_{f}\right|=\left|(x+y)-(x^{\prime}+y^{\prime})\right|
. Therefore,

(v,v^{\prime})_{\mathsf{TS}}=\max\left\{\left|x-x^{\prime}\right|,\left|(x+y)-(x^{\prime}+y^{\prime})\right|\right\}=\frac{1}{2}\cdot\bigg(\left|(2x+y)-(2x^{\prime}+y^{\prime})\right|+\left|y-y^{\prime}\right|\bigg).

∎

6.2
Analysis step 1. fine-grained analysis of
\mathsf{cost}({\mathcal{F}},\delta)-\mathsf{OPT}

We first show that
\mathsf{OPT}:=\sum_{(u,v)\in E(G)}c(u,v)\cdot\textnormal{{dist}}_{\ell}(u,v)=O(L^{2})
.
Denote by
{\mathcal{P}}
the collection of all paths in
G
defined above.
For each path
P\in{\mathcal{P}}
connecting a pair
t_{P},t^{\prime}_{P}
of terminals in
T
, by triangle inequality, its contributed cost is
\sum_{(u,v)\in E(P)}c(u,v)\cdot\textnormal{{dist}}_{\ell}(u,v)=c(u,v)\cdot\textnormal{{dist}}_{\ell}(t_{P},t^{\prime}_{P})=c(u,v)\cdot D(t_{P},t^{\prime}_{P})
.
Therefore,

\mathsf{OPT}=\sum_{(u,v)\in E(G)}c(u,v)\cdot\textnormal{{dist}}_{\ell}(u,v)=\sum_{P\in{\mathcal{P}}}c(u,v)\cdot D(t_{P},t^{\prime}_{P}),

which implies that
\mathsf{OPT}\leq 90L^{2}
, as (i) there are
L^{2}
pairs
(i,j)
; and (ii) for each pair
(i,j)
there are at most
15
paths with capacity at most
2
and length at most
3
each.
Therefore, in order to prove
Theorem

5
for the
\mathsf{0EwSN}
instance, it suffices to show that, for any solution
({\mathcal{F}},\delta)
with
|{\mathcal{F}}|\leq N
,
\mathsf{cost}({\mathcal{F}},\delta)-\mathsf{OPT}\geq 10^{-15}\cdot L^{2}
.

First, from
Lemma

7
, we can assume that the solution
({\mathcal{F}},\delta)
satisfies that

•

each set
F\in{\mathcal{F}}
corresponds to a point
f_{F}
in
\mathsf{TS}(D)
; and

•

for each pair
F,F^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{F}}
,
\delta(F,F^{\prime})=(f_{F},f_{F^{\prime}})_{\mathsf{TS}}
.

For each vertex
v\in V(G)
, we denote by
f(v)
the point in
\mathsf{TS}(D)
that corresponds to the set in
{\mathcal{F}}
that contains
v
. For each path
P
, we
\mathsf{cost}(P)=\sum_{(u,v)\in E(P)}(f(u),f(v))_{\mathsf{TS}}
.

Note that
\mathsf{cost}({\mathcal{F}},\delta)-\mathsf{OPT}=\sum_{P\in{\mathcal{P}}}\mathsf{cost}(P)-D(t_{P},t^{\prime}_{P})
. We define the
loss of path
P
as
\ell(P)=\mathsf{cost}(P)-D(t_{P},t^{\prime}_{P})
, and will aim to give a lower bound the sum of loss of all paths.
As
f(t)=t
for all terminals, from triangle inequality, the loss of every path is non-negative. Moreover, for a path
P=(t,v_{1},\ldots,v_{k},t^{\prime})
, we can further decompose
\ell(P)
as (denoting
t=v_{0}
and
t^{\prime}=v_{k+1}
)

\begin{split}\ell(P)=\mathsf{cost}(P)-D(t,t^{\prime})&=\bigg(\sum_{0\leq j\leq k}(f(v_{j}),f(v_{j+1}))_{\mathsf{TS}}\bigg)-D(t,t^{\prime})\\
&=\sum_{0\leq j\leq k}\bigg((f(v_{j}),f(v_{j+1}))_{\mathsf{TS}}+(t,f(v_{j}))_{\mathsf{TS}}-(t,f(v_{j+1}))_{\mathsf{TS}}\bigg),\end{split}

where for each
j
, by triangle inequality,
(f(v_{j}),f(v_{j+1}))_{\mathsf{TS}}+(t,f(v_{j}))_{\mathsf{TS}}-(t,f(v_{j+1}))_{\mathsf{TS}}\geq 0
.

From
13
, for each vertex
v\in V(G)
and each of the
4
critical directions, there is some path in
{\mathcal{P}}
going through
v
in this direction, creating a term in the above inequality. In order to best utilize the structure of
\mathsf{TS}(D)
, our plan is to estimate the
4
terms related to each vertex together.
We first define vectors corresponding an edge in each of the four critical directions:

w_{1}=(0,0,1/L),w_{2}=(0,2/L,-1/L),w_{3}=(1/L,0,0),w_{4}=(-1/L,2/L,0).

For every
v\in V(G)
, every terminal
t
, and each
1\leq i\leq 4
, we define

\ell_{i}(v,t)=(f(v),f(v+w_{i}))_{\mathsf{TS}}+(f(v),t)_{\mathsf{TS}}-(f(v+w_{i}),t)_{\mathsf{TS}}.

and

\ell^{\prime}_{i}(v,t)=(f(v),f(v+w_{i}))_{\mathsf{TS}}-(f(v),t)_{\mathsf{TS}}+(f(v+w_{i}),t)_{\mathsf{TS}}.

We use the following claim, that decompose the loss of paths into the sum of
\ell_{i}(v,t)
terms.

Lemma 14
.

The following inequalities hold.

•

\sum_{i,j}\bigg(\ell(\mathsf{da1}[i,j])+\ell(\mathsf{be1}[i,j])\bigg)\geq\sum_{v}\bigg(\ell_{1}(v,d)+\ell_{1}(v,b)\bigg)
;

•

\sum_{i,j}\bigg(\ell(\mathsf{ad2}[i,j])+\ell(\mathsf{eb2}[i,j])\bigg)\geq\sum_{v}\bigg(\ell^{\prime}_{2}(v,d)+\ell^{\prime}_{2}(v,b)\bigg)
;

•

\sum_{i,j}\bigg(\ell(\mathsf{ad3}[i,j])+\ell(\mathsf{be3}[i,j])+2\ell(\mathsf{cf3}[i,j])\bigg)\geq\sum_{v}\bigg(\ell^{\prime}_{3}(v,d)+\ell_{3}(v,b)+2\ell_{3}(v,c)\bigg)
; and

•

\sum_{i,j}\bigg(\ell(\mathsf{da4}[i,j])+\ell(\mathsf{eb4}[i,j])+2\ell(\mathsf{cf4}[i,j])\bigg)\geq\sum_{v}\bigg(\ell_{4}(v,d)+\ell^{\prime}_{4}(v,b)+2\ell_{4}(v,c)\bigg)
.

Proof.

We only prove the first inequality, and the other three inequalities can be proved similarly. Consider the path
\mathsf{ad1}[i,j]=(d,v_{0},\dots,v_{L-j},a)
. Note that

\ell(\mathsf{ad1}[i,j])\geq\sum_{s=0}^{L-j-1}\bigg((f(v_{s}),f(v_{s+1}))_{\mathsf{TS}}+(d,f(v_{s}))_{\mathsf{TS}}-(d,f(v_{s+1}))_{\mathsf{TS}}\bigg)\geq\sum_{s=0}^{L-j-1}\ell_{1}(v_{s},d).

As every vertex
v\in V(G)
appears in some path
\mathsf{da1}[i,j]
,
\sum_{i,j}\ell(\mathsf{da1}[i,j])\geq\sum_{v}\ell_{1}(v,d)
(by
13
). Similarly,
\sum_{i,j}\ell(\mathsf{be1}[i,j])\geq\sum_{v}\ell_{1}(v,b)
, thus the first inequality is true.
∎

Claim 15
.

For every vertex
v\in V(G)
,

•

\ell_{1}(v,d)+\ell_{1}(v,b)\geq 2\left|f(v)[x]-f(v+w_{1})[x]\right|
;

•

\ell^{\prime}_{2}(v,d)+\ell^{\prime}_{2}(v,b)\geq 2\left|f(v)[x]-f(v+w_{2})[x]\right|
;

•

(a,f(v))_{\mathsf{TS}}+(b,f(v))_{\mathsf{TS}}\geq 2+2\big(f(v)[x]\big)^{+}
;

•

(d,f(v))_{\mathsf{TS}}+(e,f(v))_{\mathsf{TS}}\geq 2+2\big(1-f(v)[x]\big)^{+}
.

Proof.

Let
(x,y,z)
be the associated vector of
f(v)
and let
(x_{1},y_{1},z_{1})
be that of
f(v+w_{1})
.
From
12
,
(f(v),f(v+w_{1}))_{\mathsf{TS}}\geq\left|x-x_{1}\right|+\left|z-z_{1}\right|
. From
11
,
(f(v),b)_{\mathsf{TS}}+(f(v),d)_{\mathsf{TS}}=3+2z
and
(f(v+w_{1}),b)_{\mathsf{TS}}+(f(v+w_{1}),d)_{\mathsf{TS}}=3+2z_{1}
. Therefore,

\begin{split}\ell_{1}(v,d)+\ell_{1}(v,b)&=\bigg((f(v),f(v+w_{1}))_{\mathsf{TS}}+(d,f(v))_{\mathsf{TS}}-(d,f(v+w_{1}))_{\mathsf{TS}}\bigg)\\
&\quad\quad+\bigg((f(v),f(v+w_{1}))_{\mathsf{TS}}+(b,f(v))_{\mathsf{TS}}-(b,f(v+w_{1}))_{\mathsf{TS}}\bigg)\\
&\geq 2\left|x-x_{1}\right|+2\left|z-z_{1}\right|+3+2z-3-2z_{1}\geq 2\left|x-x_{1}\right|.\end{split}

The second inequality can be proved similarly.
For the third inequality, by
11
, we get that
(a,f(v))_{\mathsf{TS}}+(b,f(v))_{\mathsf{TS}}=2+x+\left|x\right|=2+2\max\{x,0\}
, and the last one can be proved similarly.
∎

6.3
Analysis step 2. reduction to a geometric problem in
\mathbb{R}^{2}

For each
v\in\mathsf{TS}(D)
, let
p(v)
be the projection of
f(v)
into the tight span of
D^{\prime}
, which is the metric on

induced by
D
, which is exactly the
2
-dimensional set (rectangle)
\overline{bcdf}
.

Observation 16
.

Let
(x,y,z)
be the associated vector of
v
. Then
p(v)=f(v)+(z/2)\cdot(w_{2}-w_{1})
, and so the associated vectors of
f(v)
and
p(v)
have the same
x
coordinates.

Proof.

Since
v_{c}+v_{f}=3
holds for all
v\in\mathsf{TS}(D)
, when we project
v
onto the rectangle, we only decrease
v_{b}
and
v_{d}
. By
11
,
v_{b}+v_{d}=3+2z
,
v_{b}+v_{c}=2x+y+1+z\geq 1+z
and
v_{b}+v_{f}=4-y\geq 2+z
. Therefore,
\Delta_{b}=z
. Similarly,
\Delta_{d}
is also
z
. So after projection, both
v_{b}
and
v_{d}
are decreased by
z
, both
v_{a}
and
v_{e}
are increased by
z
(note that
v_{a}+v_{d}=v_{b}+v_{e}=3
), and both
v_{c}
and
v_{f}
do not change. Thus,
x=v_{a}+v_{b}-2
does not change,
y=v_{c}-v_{b}+1
is increased by
z
and
z
becomes
0
. Thus,
p(v)=f(v)+z\cdot(0,1,-1)=f(v)+(z/2)\cdot(w_{2}-w_{1})
.
∎

Notice that
\mathsf{TS}(D^{\prime})\subsetneq\mathsf{TS}(D)
, and for any
u,v\in\mathsf{TS}(D^{\prime})
,
(u,v)_{\mathsf{TS}(D^{\prime})}=(u,v)_{\mathsf{TS}(D)}
holds, so we still denote
\mathsf{TS}=\mathsf{TS}(D)
.
From
Lemma

7
, for every pair
u,v\in TS(D)
,
(p(u),p(v))_{\mathsf{TS}}\leq(f(u),f(v))_{\mathsf{TS}}
.

Claim 17
.

For each point
v\in\mathsf{TS}(D)
,

•

(c,f(v))_{\mathsf{TS}}=(c,p(v))_{\mathsf{TS}}
;

•

(b,f(v))_{\mathsf{TS}}-(d,f(v))_{\mathsf{TS}}=(b,p(v))_{\mathsf{TS}}-(d,p(v))_{\mathsf{TS}}
.

Proof.

If
f(v)\in\mathsf{TS}(D^{\prime})
, then
f(v)=p(v)
and the inequalities clearly hold. Otherwise,
f(v)
lies in the
3
-dimensional set (the triangular prism). We denote by
(x,y,z)
the associated vector of
f(v)
. By
11
,
(c,f(v))_{\mathsf{TS}}=x+y+z
. From
16
,
p(v)=f(v)+(z/2)\cdot(w_{2}-w_{1})=(x,y+z,0)
, so
(c,p(v))_{\mathsf{TS}}=x+y+z=(c,f(v))_{\mathsf{TS}}
.
From
11
,
(d,f(v))_{\mathsf{TS}}=2-x+z
and
(b,f(v))_{\mathsf{TS}}=1+x+z
, so
(b,f(v))_{\mathsf{TS}}-(d,f(v))_{\mathsf{TS}}=2x-1
, which equals
(b,p(v))_{\mathsf{TS}}-(d,p(v))_{\mathsf{TS}}
as the associated vectors of
f(v)
and

have the same

coordinates (from

).
∎

For each
v\in V(G)
, we define

•

\ell_{x}(v)=\big|p(v)[y]-p(v+w_{3})[y]\big|+2\cdot\bigg((2p(v)[x]+p(v)[y])-(2p(v+w_{3})[x]+p(v+w_{3})[y])\bigg)^{+}
;

•

\ell_{y}(v)=2\big|p(v)[x]-p(v+w_{3}+w_{4})[x]\big|

Appendix A
Comparison between Variants of
0
-Extension Problems

In the classic 0-Extension problem
[
Kar98
]
, the input consists of

•

an edge-capacitated graph
G=(V,E,c)
;

•

a subset
T\subseteq V
of
k
vertices, that we call
terminals
; and

•

a metric
D
on terminals in
T
.

A solution to the 0-Extension problem is a partition
{\mathcal{F}}
of the vertex set
V
into
|T|
subsets, such that distinct terminals of
T
belong to different sets in
{\mathcal{F}}
; we call sets in
{\mathcal{F}}

clusters
, and for each vertex
u\in V
, we denote by
t(u)
terminal that lies in the same cluster as
u
.
The goal is to minimize the cost
\mathsf{cost}({\mathcal{F}})=\sum_{(u,v)\in E(G)}c(u,v)\cdot D(t(u),t(v))
.

A natural approach towards constructing low-cost solutions is by considering the following semi-metric relaxation LP.

(
semi-metric LP
)

\displaystyle\text{minimize}\quad\sum_{(u,v)\in E}c(u,v)\cdot\delta(u,v)

\displaystyle s.t.\quad(V,\delta)\text{ is a semi-metric space}

\displaystyle\delta(t,t^{\prime})=D(t,t^{\prime}),\quad\forall t,t^{\prime}\in T

Assume that we have solved the LP relaxation, and now want to round the obtained solution into a feasible solution to the 0-Extension problem. The
rounding problem
that we face can be formulated as follows.
The input consists of

•

an edge-capacitated graph
G=(V,E,c)
;

•

a subset
T\subseteq V
of
k
vertices, that we call
terminals
; and

•

lengths
\{\ell_{e}\}_{e\in E}
of edges of
G
.

Recall that
\textnormal{{dist}}_{\ell}(\cdot,\cdot)
is the shortest-path distance metric on
V
induced by the lengths
\{\ell_{e}\}_{e\in E}
.
A solution is a partition
{\mathcal{F}}
of the vertex set
V
into
|T|
subsets, such that distinct terminals of
T
belong to different sets in
{\mathcal{F}}
.
The goal is to minimize the cost
\mathsf{cost}({\mathcal{F}})=\sum_{(u,v)\in E(G)}c(u,v)\cdot\textnormal{{dist}}_{\ell}(t(u),t(v))
. In particular, the ratio between
\mathsf{cost}({\mathcal{F}})
and the value
\sum_{(u,v)\in E(G)}c(u,v)\cdot\ell(u,v)
is a central measure to be minimized, which is called the
average stretch
.

Intuitively, initially each edge in
G
has length
\ell(e)
, which induces a shortest-path distance metric on the terminals. The rounding problem aims to find a way of “moving” all non-terminals to terminals, so each edge
(u,v)\in E(G)
, assuming
u
is moved to
t(u)
and
v
is moved to
t(v)
, is stretched to an edge connecting
t(u)
and
t(v)
, and will therefore have resulting length
\textnormal{{dist}}_{\ell}(t(u),t(v))
. The cost of such a moving schedule is simply the total resulting length of all edges, while
\sum_{(u,v)\in E(G)}\ell(u,v)
can be viewed as the cost of the original graph.

The most standard “Steiner node version” of the classic
0
-Extension problem should be as follows. Its input consists of

•

an edge-capacitated graph
G=(V,E,c)
;

•

a subset
T\subseteq V
of
k
vertices, that we call
terminals
; and

•

a metric
D
on terminals in
T
.

A solution consists of

•

a partition
{\mathcal{F}}
of
V
, such that distinct terminals of
T
belong to different sets in
{\mathcal{F}}
; for each vertex
u\in V
, we denote by
F(u)
the cluster in
{\mathcal{F}}
that contains it;

•

a semi-metric
\delta
on the clusters in
{\mathcal{F}}
, such that
for all pairs
t,t^{\prime}\in T
,
\delta(F(t),F(t^{\prime}))=D(t,t^{\prime})
.

The cost of a solution
({\mathcal{F}},\delta)
is defined as
\mathsf{cost}({\mathcal{F}},\delta)=\sum_{(u,v)\in E}c(u,v)\cdot\delta(F(u),F(v))
, and its
size
is defined as
|{\mathcal{F}}|
.
The goal is to compute a solution
({\mathcal{F}},\delta)
with small size and cost.

Similarly, we can write down a semi-metric LP relaxation for this generalized version of
0
-Extension,
the
\mathsf{0EwSN}
problem defined in
Section

1.2
and
Section

3.1
is the
rounding problem
of this version
.

Comparing their rounding problems, the
\mathsf{0EwSN}
problem is essentially generalized from the classic 0-Extension problem in two aspects. First, we do not enforce all non-terminals to be moved to terminals, but allow them to be moved to some other “Steiner nodes”. Specifically, in
\mathsf{0EwSN}
we allow that some sets in
{\mathcal{F}}
do not contain any terminal (and therefore
|{\mathcal{F}}|
can be strictly greater than
|T|
), and so when such a set is contracted, they form a Steiner node in the sparsifier. Second, observe that we can equivalently view a solution to the 0-Extension problem as a pair
({\mathcal{F}},\delta)
instead of
{\mathcal{F}}
, whose cost is defined as
\mathsf{cost}({\mathcal{F}},\delta)=\sum_{(u,v)\in E(G)}c(u,v)\cdot\delta(F(u),F(v))
, same as
\mathsf{0EwSN}
. Only, in
0
-Extension
\delta
has to be exactly the shortest-path distance metric
\textnormal{{dist}}_{\ell}(\cdot,\cdot)
on
T
, while in
\mathsf{0EwSN}
, we only require that the restriction of
\delta
onto
T
dominates
\textnormal{{dist}}_{\ell}(\cdot,\cdot)
entry-wise, and in
\mathsf{0EwSN}_{\textsf{ave}}
, it is required that
some average distance between terminal clusters (clusters that contain a terminal) in
\delta
is at least the corresponding average distance between terminal in
\textnormal{{dist}}_{\ell}(\cdot,\cdot)
, an arguably weaker condition.

\hat{x}^{\prime}_{t}+\hat{x}^{\prime}_{t^{\prime}}=\hat{x}_{t}+\hat{x}_{t^{\prime}}-\Delta\geq\hat{x}_{t}+\hat{x}_{t^{\prime}}-\Delta_{t,t^{\prime}}=x_{t}+\hat{x}_{t^{\prime}}-\bigg(\hat{x}_{t}+\hat{x}_{t^{\prime}}-D(t,t^{\prime})\bigg)\geq D(t,t^{\prime}).

Next we show that, for each
t\in T
, some inequality
\left\{\hat{x}_{t}+\hat{x}_{t^{\prime}}\geq D(t,t^{\prime})\right\}_{t^{\prime}}
is tight for
\hat{x}
at the end of the algorithm. Consider the iteration when
t
becomes inactive. From the algorithm, there exists
t^{\prime}
such that
\Delta=\Delta_{t}=\Delta_{t,t^{\prime}}
. Then:

•

If
t^{\prime}
was active before this iteration, then in this iteration
\Delta=\Delta_{t,t^{\prime}}=\frac{1}{2}\cdot(\hat{x}_{t}+\hat{x}_{t^{\prime}}-D(t,t^{\prime}))
, and so after this iteration, the new
\hat{x}_{t}
and
\hat{x}_{t^{\prime}}
, which we denote by
\hat{x}^{\prime}_{t}
and
\hat{x}^{\prime}_{t}
, satisfy that

\hat{x}^{\prime}_{t}+\hat{x}^{\prime}_{t^{\prime}}=\hat{x}_{t}+\hat{x}_{t^{\prime}}-2\Delta=\hat{x}_{t}+\hat{x}_{t^{\prime}}-2\Delta_{t,t^{\prime}}=x_{t}+\hat{x}_{t^{\prime}}-2\cdot\frac{1}{2}\cdot\bigg(\hat{x}_{t}+\hat{x}_{t^{\prime}}-D(t,t^{\prime})\bigg)=D(t,t^{\prime}).

•

If
t^{\prime}
was inactive before this iteration, then
\Delta=\Delta_{t,t^{\prime}}=(\hat{x}_{t}+\hat{x}_{t^{\prime}}-D(t,t^{\prime}))
, and after this iteration the new
\hat{x}^{\prime}_{t}
and
\hat{x}^{\prime}_{t}
, satisfy that
\hat{x}^{\prime}_{t}=\hat{x}_{t}-\Delta
and
\hat{x}^{\prime}_{t^{\prime}}=\hat{x}_{t^{\prime}}
. Therefore,

\hat{x}^{\prime}_{t}+\hat{x}^{\prime}_{t^{\prime}}=\hat{x}_{t}+\hat{x}_{t^{\prime}}-\Delta=\hat{x}_{t}+\hat{x}_{t^{\prime}}-\Delta_{t,t^{\prime}}=x_{t}+\hat{x}_{t^{\prime}}-\bigg(\hat{x}_{t}+\hat{x}_{t^{\prime}}-D(t,t^{\prime})\bigg)=D(t,t^{\prime}).

In both cases, after this iteration,
t
and
t^{\prime}
become inactive, so the coordinates
\hat{x}_{t},\hat{x}_{t^{\prime}}
are no longer updated, implying that the equality
\hat{x}_{t}+\hat{x}_{t^{\prime}}=D(t,t^{\prime})
continues to hold for the following iterations and for the ending
\hat{x}
.

B.3
Determining the structure of
\mathsf{TS}(D)
in
Section

6.1

In this section, we explain why the tight span
\mathsf{TS}(D)
of the metric
D
is as described
Section

6.1
.
Recall that the metric
D
is defined on the terminal set
\left\{a,b,c,d,e,f\right\}
, and each point
v\in\mathsf{TS}(D)
is represented by a
6
-dimensional real vector
v=(v_{a},v_{b},v_{c},v_{d},v_{e},v_{f})
where
v_{t}
indicates the distance from
v
to terminal
t
.
We start with the following observation.

Observation 32
.

For any three terminals
t_{1},t_{2},t_{3}\in\left\{a,b,c,d,e,f\right\}
such that
D(t_{1},t_{2})+D(t_{2},t_{3})=D(t_{1},t_{3})
, then for any point
v\in\mathsf{TS}(D)
, if
v_{t_{1}}+v_{t_{2}}=D(t_{1},t_{2})
, then
v_{t_{1}}+v_{t_{3}}=D(t_{1},t_{3})
.

Proof.

We have
D(t_{1},t_{3})-v_{t_{1}}\leq v_{t_{3}}\leq v_{t_{2}}+D(t_{2},t_{3})=v_{t_{2}}+D(t_{1},t_{3})-D(t_{1},t_{2})=D(t_{1},t_{3})-v_{t_{1}}
, so
v_{t_{1}}+v_{t_{3}}=D(t_{1},t_{3})
.
∎

Consider now any point
v\in\mathsf{TS}(D)
, by definition there is a terminal
t\in\{a,b,c,d,e,f\}
such that
v_{c}+v_{t}=D(c,t)
. Note that for any
t\in\{a,b,c,d,e,f\}
, we have
D(c,t)+D(t,f)=D(c,f)
, thus by
32
, we have
v_{c}+v_{f}=D(c,f)
. By the same reason, we have either
v_{b}+v_{e}=D(b,e)
or
v_{b}+v_{d}=D(b,d)
, and similarly we have either
v_{a}+v_{d}=D(a,d)
or
v_{b}+v_{d}=D(b,d)
. Thus for any
v\in\mathsf{TS}(D)
, either

•

equalities
v_{c}+v_{f}=D(c,f)
,
v_{a}+v_{d}=D(a,d)
and
v_{b}+v_{e}=D(b,e)
hold, and
v
is in the triangular prism; or

•

equalities
v_{c}+v_{f}=D(c,f)
and
v_{b}+v_{d}=D(b,d)
hold, and
v
is in the rectangle. In this case,
v_{a}=\max\{2-v_{b},3-v_{d}\}
and
v_{e}=\max\{2-v_{d},3-v_{b}\}
.
