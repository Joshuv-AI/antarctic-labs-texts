A Wells-like exact sequence for abelian extensions of relative Rota–Baxter groups
Pragya Belwal, Nishant Rathee

Abstract
. Relative Rota–Baxter groups, a generalization of Rota–Baxter groups, are closely connected to skew left braces, which play a fundamental role in understanding non-degenerate set-theoretical solutions to the Yang-Baxter equation. In this paper, we explore the inducibility problem for automorphisms of abelian extensions of relative Rota–Baxter groups. This problem is intricately linked to the recently introduced second cohomology of relative Rota–Baxter groups. Specifically, we prove a Wells-like exact sequence for abelian extensions of relative Rota–Baxter groups. The sequence establishes a connection among the group of derivations, certain automorphism group, and the second cohomology of relative Rota–Baxter groups, thereby giving precise structural relationships between these groups.

1.
Introduction

Relative Rota–Baxter operators, also referred to as
\mathcal{O}
-operators, find their roots in the operator formulation of the classical Yang–Baxter equation, as introduced by Bai in
[
2
]
. Jiang, Sheng, and Zhu recently extended the concept to Lie groups in
[
17
]
, presenting a generalization of Rota–Baxter operators with weight 1, initially defined in
[
15
]
. The central theme of this development lies in the ability to further differentiate these operators, leading to the emergence of relative Rota–Baxter operators on corresponding Lie algebras. The relationship between relative Rota–Baxter groups and other algebraic structures, as well as their role in solving the set-theoretical Yang-Baxter equation, is explored in
[
1
]
.

Bardakov and Gubarev explored Rota–Baxter operators on (abstract) groups in
[
3
,
4
]
, demonstrating that a Rota–Baxter operator on a group gives rise to a skew left brace structure on that group. The investigation of Rota–Baxter groups within the context of skew left braces is further carried out in
[
10
]
, where a non-trivial example of a skew left brace that cannot be derived from Rota–Baxter operators on groups has been given. The ideas introduced in
[
3
]
are further employed in
[
22
]
to observe that the category of relative Rota–Baxter groups serves as a generalization of the category of skew left braces, since the category of bijective relative Rota–Baxter groups is isomorphic to the category of skew left braces.

The Wells exact sequence, originally introduced by Wells in
[
25
]
, serves as a crucial tool for addressing the inducibility problem for automorphisms associated with group extensions. The sequence has undergone adaptation and generalisations for various algebraic structures, enabling the exploration of automorphisms of extensions specific to those categories. In
[
7
,
16
]
, the inducibility problem has been explored for Lie algebra and Lie superalgebra extensions, leading to the derivation of a Wells-like exact sequence customized for these extensions. Additionally, a parallel examination of the inducibility problem for extensions of Lie-Yamaguti algebras is presented in
[
13
]
. Analogous sequences for set-theoretical solutions of Yang-Baxter equation are also developed. For example, a Wells-like exact sequence for skew braces is detailed in
[
23
,
21
]
, and a Wells-like exact sequence for the cohomology theory of linear cycle sets, developed in
[
18
]
, is defined in
[
6
]
. A similar exact sequence for quandle extensions has been developed in
[
5
]
. The inducibility and lifting problems concerning the extension of Rota–Baxter and relative Rota–Baxter operators across various algebraic structures have been investigated by numerous authors, see
[
19
,
11
,
12
]
.

In this paper, we delve into the inducibility problem concerning automorphisms of abelian extensions of relative Rota–Baxter groups. The organization of the paper is as follows: in Section
2
, we present the preliminaries of relative Rota–Baxter groups, which are essential for this work. We revisit some foundational facts about extensions of relative Rota–Baxter groups in Section
3
, focusing on the relationship between the second cohomology group and extensions of relative Rota–Baxter groups. In Section
4
, we introduce some automorphism groups for extensions of relative Rota–Baxter groups, and define an action of these groups on extensions. We prove that the automorphism group of an extension of relative Rota–Baxter groups contains those automorphisms that induce identity on the substructure, and the quotient is isomorphic to the set of derivations (Theorem
4.6
). This result leads to a Wells-like exact sequence for extensions of relative Rota–Baxter groups (Theorem
4.7
). In the end, we provide a module-theoretic criterion for an automorphism of a relative Rota-Baxter group to be inducible (Theorem
4.9
).

2.
Preliminaries

In this section, we revisit some basic concepts on relative Rota–Baxter groups essential for our study. For an in-depth understanding, we direct the reader to
[
17
,
22
]
. Our terminology aligns with that of
[
8
,
22
]
, which is distinct from other sources.

Definition 2.1
.

A relative Rota–Baxter group is a quadruple
(H,G,\phi,R)
, where
H
and
G
are groups,
\phi:G\rightarrow\operatorname{Aut}(H)
a group homomorphism (where
\phi(g)
is denoted by
\phi_{g}
) and
R:H\rightarrow G
is a map satisfying the condition

R(h_{1})R(h_{2})=R(h_{1}\phi_{R(h_{1})}(h_{2}))

for all
h_{1},h_{2}\in H
.

The map
R
is referred to as the relative Rota–Baxter operator on
H
.

We say that the relative Rota–Baxter group
(H,G,\phi,R)
is
trivial
if
\phi:G\to\operatorname{Aut}(H)
is the trivial homomorphism. Further, it is said to be
bijective
if the Rota–Baxter operator
R
is a bijection. We refer the reader to
[
8
,
17
]
for examples.

Remark 2.1
.

Note that if
(H,G,\phi,R)
is a trivial relative Rota–Baxter group, then
R:H\rightarrow G
is a group homomorphism.

Let
(H,G,\phi,R)
be a relative Rota–Baxter group, and let
K\leq H
and
L\leq G
be subgroups.

(1)

If
\phi_{\ell}(K)\subseteq K
for all
\ell\in L
, then we denote the restriction of
\phi
by
\phi|:L\to\operatorname{Aut}(K)
.

(2)

If
R(K)\subseteq L
, then we denote the restriction of
R
by
R|:K\to L
.

Definition 2.2
.

Let
(H,G,\phi,R)
be a relative Rota–Baxter group, and
K\leq H
and
L\leq G
be subgroups. Suppose that
\phi_{\ell}(K)\subseteq K
for all
\ell\in L
and
R(K)\subseteq L
. Then
(K,L,\phi|,R|)
is a relative Rota–Baxter group, which we refer as a relative Rota–Baxter subgroup of
(H,G,\phi,R)
and write
(K,L,\phi|,R|)\leq(H,G,\phi,R)
.

Definition 2.3
.

Let
(H,G,\phi,R)
be a relative Rota–Baxter group and
(K,L,\phi|,R|)\leq(H,G,\phi,R)
its relative Rota–Baxter subgroup. We say that
(K,L,\phi|,R|)
is an ideal of
(H,G,\phi,R)
if

(2.2)

\displaystyle K\trianglelefteq H\quad\mbox{and}\quad L\trianglelefteq G,

(2.3)

\displaystyle\phi_{g}(K)\subseteq K\mbox{ for all }g\in G,

(2.4)

\displaystyle\phi_{\ell}(h)h^{-1}\in K\mbox{ for all }h\in H\mbox{ and }\ell\in L.

We write
(K,L,\phi|,R|)\trianglelefteq(H,G,\phi,R)
to denote an ideal of a relative Rota–Baxter group.

The preceding definitions lead to the following result
[
22
, Theorem 5.3]
.

Theorem 2.4
.

Let
(H,G,\phi,R)
be a relative Rota–Baxter group and
(K,L,\phi|,R|)
an ideal of
(H,G,\phi,R)
. Then there are maps
\overline{\phi}:G/L\to\operatorname{Aut}(H/K)
and
\overline{R}:H/K\to G/L
defined by

\overline{\phi}_{\overline{g}}(\overline{h})=\overline{\phi_{g}(h)}\quad\textrm{and}\quad\overline{R}(\overline{h})=\overline{R(h)}

for
\overline{g}\in G/L
and
\overline{h}\in H/K
, such that
(H/K,G/L,\overline{\phi},\overline{R})
is a relative Rota–Baxter group.

Notation 2.1
.

We write
(H,G,\phi,R)/(K,L,\phi|,R|)
to denote the quotient relative Rota–Baxter group
(H/K,G/L,\overline{\phi},\overline{R})
.

Definition 2.5
.

Let
(H,G,\phi,R)
and
(K,L,\varphi,S)
be two relative Rota–Baxter groups.

(1)

A homomorphism
(\psi,\eta):(H,G,\phi,R)\to(K,L,\varphi,S)
of relative Rota–Baxter groups is a pair
(\psi,\eta)
, where
\psi:H\rightarrow K
and
\eta:G\rightarrow L
are group homomorphisms such that

(2.5)

\eta\;R=S\;\psi\quad\textrm{and}\quad\psi\;\phi_{g}=\varphi_{\eta(g)}\psi

for all
g\in G
.

(2)

The kernel of a homomorphism
(\psi,\eta):(H,G,\phi,R)\to(K,L,\varphi,S)
of relative Rota–Baxter groups is the quadruple

(\operatorname{ker}(\psi),\operatorname{ker}(\eta),\phi|,R|),

where
\operatorname{ker}(\psi)
and
\operatorname{ker}(\eta)
denote the kernels of the group homomorphisms
\psi
and
\eta
, respectively. The conditions in (
2.5
) imply that the kernel is itself a relative Rota–Baxter group. In fact, the kernel turns out to be an ideal of
(H,G,\phi,R)
.

(3)

The image of a homomorphism
(\psi,\eta):(H,G,\phi,R)\to(K,L,\varphi,S)
of relative Rota–Baxter groups is the quadruple

(\operatorname{im}(\psi),\operatorname{im}(\eta),\varphi|,S|),

where
\operatorname{im}(\psi)
and
\operatorname{im}(\eta)
denote the images of the group homomorphisms
\psi
and
\eta
, respectively. The image is itself a relative Rota–Baxter group.

(4)

A homomorphism
(\psi,\eta)
of relative Rota–Baxter groups is called an isomorphism if both
\psi
and
\eta
are group isomorphisms. Similarly, we say that
(\psi,\eta)
is an embedding of a relative Rota–Baxter group if both
\psi
and
\eta
are embeddings of groups.

Definition 2.6
.

The center of a relative Rota–Baxter group
(H,G,\phi,R)
is defined as

\operatorname{Z}(H,G,\phi,R)=\big(\operatorname{Z}^{\phi}_{R}(H),\operatorname{ker}(\phi),\phi|,R|\big),

where
\operatorname{Z}^{\phi}_{R}(H)=\operatorname{Z}(H)\cap\operatorname{ker}(\phi\,R)\cap\operatorname{Fix}(\phi)
,
\operatorname{Fix}(\phi)=\{x\in H\mid\phi_{g}(x)=x\mbox{ for all }g\in G\}
and
R:H^{(\circ_{R})}\rightarrow G
is viewed as a group homomorphism.

3.
Extensions and second cohomology of relative Rota–Baxter groups

Definition 3.1
.

Let

and

(1)

\mathcal{E}:\quad{\bf 1}\longrightarrow(K,L,\alpha,S)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(i_{1},i_{2})}}{{\longrightarrow}}(H,G,\phi,R)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\pi_{1},\pi_{2})}}{{\longrightarrow}}(A,B,\beta,T)\longrightarrow{\bf 1},

(i_{1},i_{2})
and
(\pi_{1},\pi_{2})
are morphisms of relative Rota–Baxter groups such that
(i_{1},i_{2})
is an embedding,
(\pi_{1},\pi_{2})
is an epimorphism of relative Rota–Baxter groups and
(\operatorname{im}(i_{1}),\operatorname{im}(i_{2}),\phi|,R|)=(\operatorname{ker}(\pi_{1}),\operatorname{ker}(\pi_{2}),\phi|,R|)
.

To avoid complexity of notation, we assume that
i_{1}
and
i_{2}
are inclusion maps. This allows us to write that
\phi
restricted to
L
is
\alpha
and
R
restricted to
K

S
.

(2)

We say that
\mathcal{E}
is an abelian extension if
K
and
L
are abelian groups and the relative Rota–Baxter group
(K,L,\alpha,S)
is trivial.

Here,
{\bf 1}
denotes the trivial relative Rota–Baxter group for which both the underlying groups are trivial.

Example 3.2
.

Let
\mathcal{K}=(K,L,\alpha,S)
and
\mathcal{A}=(A,B,\beta,T)
be two relative Rota–Baxter groups. Then the direct product of

and

is defined as

where

and

\displaystyle(\beta\times\alpha)_{(b,l)}(a,k)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\beta_{b}(a),\,\alpha_{l}(k)),

\displaystyle(T\times S)(a,k)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(T(a),\,S(k)),

for
a\in A
,
b\in B
,

and

.

Proposition 3.3
.

[
8
, Proposition 3.6]

An extension

\mathcal{E}:\quad{\bf 1}\longrightarrow(K,L,\alpha,S)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(i_{1},i_{2})}}{{\longrightarrow}}(H,G,\phi,R)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\pi_{1},\pi_{2})}}{{\longrightarrow}}(A,B,\beta,T)\longrightarrow{\bf 1}

(K,L,\alpha,S)
is an ideal of
(H,G,\phi,R)
and the quotient relative Rota–Baxter group

Remark 3.1
.

Let
(H,G,\phi,R)
be a relative Rota–Baxter group, and
(K,L,\phi|,R|)
be its ideal. The relative Rota–Baxter group

Using (

), we conclude that (
4.23
) and (
4.24
) are equal. Hence,
\kappa_{2}^{\eta}\;R=R\;\kappa_{1}^{\eta}.

For
b\in B
and
l\in L
, we have

(4.25)

\displaystyle\kappa_{1}^{\eta}\big(\phi_{s_{G}(b)l}(s_{H}(a)k)\big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\kappa_{1}^{\eta}\big(s_{H}(\beta_{b}(a))\rho(a,b)\nu_{b}(f(l,a)k)\big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle s_{H}(\beta_{b}(a))\kappa_{1}(\beta_{b}(a))\rho(a,b)\nu_{b}(f(l,a)k)

and

(4.26)

\displaystyle\phi_{\kappa_{2}^{\eta}(s_{G}(b)l)}\big(\kappa_{1}^{\eta}(s_{H}(a)k)\big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\phi_{(s_{G}(b)\kappa_{2}(b)l)}(s_{H}(a)\kappa_{1}(a)k)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle s_{H}(\beta_{b}(a))\rho(a,b)\nu_{b}(f(\kappa_{2}(b)l,a)\kappa_{1}(a)k).

By employing equation (
3.11
) and Lemma (
3.5
), it follows that the right-hand sides of both (
4.25
) and (
4.26
) coincide. Consequently, we have
\kappa_{1}^{\eta}\;\phi_{g}=\phi_{\kappa_{2}^{\eta}(g)}\;\kappa_{1}^{\eta}
. This establishes the well-definedness of
\eta
. Furthermore, it is easy to see that
\eta
is a group homomorphism.

We will now define a map
\zeta:\operatorname{Aut}^{\mathcal{A},\mathcal{K}}(\mathcal{H})\rightarrow\operatorname{Z}^{1}_{RRB}(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{K})
. Let
\gamma=(\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2})\in\operatorname{Aut}^{\mathcal{A},\mathcal{K}}(\mathcal{H})
. Then, for each
a\in A
and
b\in B
, there exists a unique element (say)
y^{\gamma_{1}}_{a}\in K
and
y^{\gamma_{2}}_{b}\in L
such that
\gamma_{1}(s_{H}(a))=s_{H}(a)y^{\gamma_{1}}_{a}
and
\gamma_{2}(s_{G}(b))=s_{G}(b)y^{\gamma_{2}}_{b}
. Thus, for
a\in A
and
b\in B
, we can define
\zeta
by

\zeta(\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}):=(\gamma_{1}^{\zeta},\gamma^{\zeta}_{2}),

where
\gamma_{1}^{\zeta}(a)=y^{\gamma_{1}}_{a}\mbox{ and }\gamma^{\zeta}_{2}(b)=y^{\gamma_{2}}_{b}.
To establish the well-definedness of
\zeta
, we must demonstrate that the pair
(\gamma_{1}^{\zeta},\gamma_{2}^{\zeta})
satisfies the conditions (
3.9
), (
3.10
), (
3.11
), and (
3.12
). Conditions (
3.9
) and (
3.10
) are the standard requirements for group derivations, well-documented in existing group theory literature (see
[
25
]
). We verify that
(\gamma_{1}^{\zeta},\gamma_{2}^{\zeta})
fulfills (
3.11
) and (
3.12
). For
a\in A
and
b\in B
, we have

\displaystyle\gamma_{1}\big(\phi_{s_{G}(b)}(s_{H}(a))\big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\gamma_{1}(s_{H}(\beta_{b}(a))\rho(a,b))

\displaystyle\phi_{\gamma_{2}(s_{G}(b))}\big(\gamma_{1}(s_{H}(a))\big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\gamma_{1}(s_{H}(\beta_{b}(a)))\rho(a,b)

\displaystyle\phi_{s_{G}(b)y^{\gamma_{2}}_{b}}(s_{H}(a)y^{\gamma_{1}}_{a})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle s_{H}(\beta_{b}(a))y^{\gamma_{1}}_{\beta_{b}(a)}\rho(a,b)

\displaystyle s_{H}(\beta_{b}(a))\rho(a,b)\nu_{b}(f(y^{\gamma_{2}}_{b},a)y^{\gamma_{1}}_{a})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle s_{H}(\beta_{b}(a))y^{\gamma_{1}}_{\beta_{b}(a)}\rho(a,b)~\mbox{, by \eqref{nuact}}

\displaystyle\nu_{b}(f(y^{\gamma_{2}}_{b},a)y^{\gamma_{1}}_{a})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle y^{\gamma_{1}}_{\beta_{b}(a)}.

This shows that
(\gamma_{1}^{\zeta},\gamma^{\zeta}_{2})
satisfies (
3.11
). Next, we have

\displaystyle R\big(\gamma_{1}(s_{H}(a))\big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\gamma_{2}\big(R(s_{H}(a))\big)

\displaystyle R(s_{H}(a)y^{\gamma_{1}}_{a})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\gamma_{2}\big(s_{G}(T(a))\chi(a)\big)

\displaystyle s_{G}(T(a))\chi(a)S(\nu^{-1}_{T(a)}(y^{\gamma_{1}}_{a}))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle s_{G}(T(a))\chi(a)y^{\gamma_{2}}_{T(a)}

\displaystyle S(\nu^{-1}_{T(a)}(y^{\gamma_{1}}_{a}))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle y^{\gamma_{2}}_{T(a)},

which shows that
(\gamma_{1}^{\zeta},\gamma_{2}^{\zeta})
satisfies (
3.12
). It is easy to see that
\zeta:\operatorname{Aut}^{\mathcal{A},\mathcal{K}}(\mathcal{H})\rightarrow\operatorname{Z}^{1}_{RRB}(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{K})
is a group homomorphism, and that
\eta
and
\zeta
are inverses of each other. This proves the theorem.
∎

We finally get the following Wells-like exact sequence for relative Rota–Baxter groups.

Theorem 4.7
.

Let
\mathcal{E}:\textbf{1}\rightarrow\mathcal{K}\rightarrow\mathcal{H}\overset{(\pi_{1},\pi_{2})}{\rightarrow}\mathcal{A}\rightarrow\textbf{1}
be an abelian extension of a relative Rota–Baxter group
\mathcal{A}
by a trivial relative Rota–Baxter group
\mathcal{K}
such that
[\mathcal{E}]\in\operatorname{Ext}_{(\nu,\mu,\sigma,f)}(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{K})
. Then there is an exact sequence of groups

1\rightarrow\operatorname{Z}^{1}_{RRB}(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{K})\rightarrow\operatorname{Aut}_{\mathcal{K}}(\mathcal{H})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rho(\mathcal{E})}}{{\longrightarrow}}\operatorname{C}_{(\nu,\mu,\sigma,f)}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\omega(\mathcal{E})}}{{\longrightarrow}}\operatorname{H}^{2}_{RRB}(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{K}),

where
\omega(\mathcal{E})
is, in general, only a derivation.

A pair of automorphisms
(\psi,\theta)\in\operatorname{Aut}(\mathcal{A})\times\operatorname{Aut}(\mathcal{K})
is said to be
inducible
if
(\psi,\theta)\in\operatorname{im}(\rho(\mathcal{E}))
. As a straightforward application of Theorem
4.7
, we get

Corollary 4.8
.

Let
\mathcal{E}:\textbf{1}\rightarrow\mathcal{K}\rightarrow\mathcal{H}\overset{(\pi_{1},\pi_{2})}{\rightarrow}\mathcal{A}\rightarrow\textbf{1}
be an abelian extension of a relative Rota–Baxter goup
\mathcal{A}
by a trivial relative Rota–Baxter group
\mathcal{K}
such that
[\mathcal{E}]\in\operatorname{Ext}_{(\nu,\mu,\sigma,f)}(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{K})
. If
\operatorname{H}^{2}_{RRB}(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{K})
is the trivial group, then every element of
\operatorname{C}_{(\nu,\mu,\sigma,f)}
is inducible.

Let
\mathcal{E}:\textbf{1}\rightarrow\mathcal{K}\rightarrow\mathcal{H}\overset{(\pi_{1},\pi_{2})}{\rightarrow}\mathcal{A}\rightarrow\textbf{1}
be an abelian extension of a relative Rota–Baxter group
\mathcal{A}
by a trivial relative Rota–Baxter group
\mathcal{K}
. Then
\mathcal{K}
can be viewed as an
\mathcal{A}
-module through the corresponding quadruplet of actions
(\nu,\mu,\sigma,f)
as defined before. An automorphism
\psi=(\psi_{1},\psi_{2})
of the relative Rota–Baxter group
\mathcal{A}
defines a new
\mathcal{A}
-module structure on
\mathcal{K}
given by
(\nu\psi_{2},\mu\psi_{1},\sigma\psi_{2},f\psi_{1})
, which we denote by
\mathcal{K}_{\psi}
, where

\displaystyle\nu\psi_{2}(b)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\nu_{\psi_{2}(b)},

\displaystyle\mu\psi_{1}(a)(k)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle s_{H}(\psi_{1}(a))^{-1}ks_{H}(\psi_{1}(a)),

\displaystyle\sigma\psi_{2}(b)(l)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle s_{G}(\psi_{2}(b))^{-1}ls_{G}(\psi_{2}(b)),

\displaystyle f\psi_{1}(l,a)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle f(l,\psi_{1}(a)),

for
a\in A
,
b\in B
,
k\in K
and
l\in L
. It is not difficult to show that the automorphism
\psi
induces an isomorphism
\psi^{*}
of cohomology groups

\psi^{*}:\operatorname{H}^{2}_{RRB}(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{K})\rightarrow\operatorname{H}^{2}_{RRB}(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{K}_{\psi})

defined by

\phi^{*}([(\tau_{1},\tau_{2},\rho,\chi)])=[\big(\tau_{1}^{(\psi,\operatorname{id})},\tau_{2}^{(\psi,\operatorname{id})},\rho^{(\psi,\operatorname{id})},\chi^{(\psi,\operatorname{id})}\big)].

Further, any
\mathcal{A}
-module isomorphism
\theta=(\theta_{1},\theta_{2}):\mathcal{K}\rightarrow\mathcal{K}_{\psi}
induces an isomorphism
\theta^{*}
of cohomology groups

\theta^{*}:\operatorname{H}^{2}_{RRB}(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{K})\rightarrow\operatorname{H}^{2}_{RRB}(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{K}_{\psi})

given by

\theta^{*}([(\tau_{1},\tau_{2},\rho,\chi)])=[\big(\tau_{1}^{(\operatorname{id},\theta^{-1})},\tau_{2}^{(\operatorname{id},\theta^{-1})},\rho^{(\operatorname{id},\theta^{-1})},\chi^{(\operatorname{id},\theta^{-1})}\big)],

where
\theta^{-1}=(\theta_{1}^{-1},\theta_{2}^{-1})
. With this framework, we have the following result.

Theorem 4.9
.

A pair of automorphisms
(\psi,\theta)\in\operatorname{Aut}(\mathcal{A})\times\operatorname{Aut}(\mathcal{K})
is inducible if and only if the following conditions hold:

(1)

\theta=(\theta_{1},\theta_{2}):\mathcal{K}\rightarrow\mathcal{K}_{\psi}
is an isomorphism of
\mathcal{A}
-modules.

(2)

\theta^{*}([(\tau_{1},\tau_{2},\rho,\chi)])=\psi^{*}([(\tau_{1},\tau_{2},\rho,\chi)])
.

Proof.

Let
(\psi,\theta)
be an inducible pair. Then there exists an automorphism
\gamma\in\operatorname{Aut}_{\mathcal{K}}(\mathcal{H})
such that
(\psi,\theta)=(\gamma_{\mathcal{A}},\gamma_{\mathcal{K}})
. For
a\in A
,
b\in B
,
k\in K
and
l\in L
, we have

(4.27)

\displaystyle\gamma_{1}|_{K}(\nu_{b}(k))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\gamma_{1}|_{K}(\phi_{s_{G}(b)}(k))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\phi_{\gamma_{2}(s_{G}(b))}(\gamma_{1}(k))\text{, since }(\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2})\in\operatorname{Aut}(\mathcal{H})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\phi_{s_{G}(\pi_{2}(\gamma_{2}(s_{G}(b))))}(\gamma_{1}(k))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\nu_{\gamma_{2_{B}}(b)}(\gamma_{1}|_{K}(k)),\text{ using that }\phi_{s_{G}(\pi_{2}(g))}=\phi_{g}\text{ for all }g\in G.

Similarly, we have,

(4.28)

\displaystyle\gamma_{2}|_{L}(\sigma_{b}(l))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sigma_{\gamma_{2_{B}}(b)}(\gamma_{2}|_{L}(l)),

(4.29)

\displaystyle\gamma_{1}|_{K}(\mu_{a}(k))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mu_{\gamma_{1_{A}}(a)}(\gamma_{1}|_{K}(k)),

(4.30)

\displaystyle\gamma_{1}|_{K}(f(l,a))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle f(\gamma_{2}|_{L}(l),\gamma_{1_{A}}(a)).

This shows that
((\operatorname{id},\operatorname{id}),\gamma_{\mathcal{K}})
is compatible with the pairs of actions
(\nu,\mu,\sigma,f)
and
(\nu\gamma_{2_{B}},\mu\gamma_{1_{A}},\sigma\gamma_{2_{B}},f\gamma_{1_{A}})
, and hence condition (1) holds.

For each
a\in A
, there exists a unique element (say)
\kappa_{1}(a)
in
K
such that
\gamma_{1}(s_{H}(a))=s_{H}(\gamma_{1_{A}}(a))\kappa_{1}(a)
. Similarly, for each
b\in B
, there exists a unique element (say)
\kappa_{2}(b)
in
L
such that
\gamma_{2}(s_{G}(b))=s_{G}(\gamma_{2_{B}}(b))\kappa_{2}(b)
. It is clear that, for the maps
\kappa_{1}:A\rightarrow K
given by
a\mapsto\kappa_{1}(a)
and
\kappa_{2}:B\rightarrow L
given by
b\mapsto\kappa_{2}(b)
, the pair
(\kappa_{1},\kappa_{2})
lies in
C_{RRB}^{1}
. Given elements
h_{1},h_{2}\in H
, they have unique expressions of the form
h_{1}=s_{H}(a_{1})k_{1}
and
h_{2}=s_{H}(a_{2})k_{2}
for some
a_{1},a_{2}\in A
and
k_{1},k_{2}\in K
. Then, we have

\displaystyle\gamma_{1}(h_{1}h_{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\gamma_{1}\big(s_{H}(a_{1}a_{2})\mu_{a_{2}}(k_{1})k_{2}\tau_{1}(a_{1},a_{2})\big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\gamma_{1}(s_{H}(a_{1}a_{2}))\gamma_{1}|_{K}\big(\mu_{a_{2}}(k_{1})k_{2}\tau_{1}(a_{1},a_{2})\big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle s_{H}(\gamma_{1_{A}}(a_{1})\gamma_{1_{A}}(a_{2}))\kappa_{1}(a_{1}a_{2})\gamma_{1}|_{K}(\mu_{a_{2}}(k_{1})k_{2})\gamma_{1}|_{K}(\tau_{1}(a_{1},a_{2}))

and

\displaystyle\gamma_{1}(h_{1})\gamma_{1}(h_{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle s_{H}(\gamma_{1_{A}}(a_{1}))\kappa_{1}(a_{1})\gamma_{1}|_{K}(k_{1})s_{H}(\gamma_{1_{A}}(a_{2}))\kappa_{1}(a_{2})\gamma_{1}|_{K}(k_{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle s_{H}(\gamma_{1_{A}}(a_{1}))s_{H}(\gamma_{1_{A}}(a_{2}))\mu_{\gamma_{1_{A}}(a_{2})}(\kappa_{1}(a_{1}))\mu_{\gamma_{1_{A}}(a_{2})}(\gamma_{1}|_{K}(k_{1}))\kappa_{1}(a_{2})\gamma_{1}|_{K}(k_{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle s_{H}(\gamma_{1_{A}}(a_{1})\gamma_{1_{A}}(a_{2}))\tau_{1}(\gamma_{1_{A}}(a_{1}),\gamma_{1_{A}}(a_{2}))\mu_{\gamma_{1_{A}}(a_{2})}(\kappa_{1}(a_{1}))

\displaystyle\;\mu_{\gamma_{1_{A}}(a_{2})}(\gamma_{1}|_{K}(k_{1}))\kappa(a_{2})\gamma_{1}|_{K}(k_{2}).

Since
\gamma_{1}(h_{1}h_{2})=\gamma_{1}(h_{1})\gamma_{1}(h_{2})
, the preceding two equations and (
4.29
), give

(4.31)

\gamma_{1}|_{K}(\tau_{1}(a_{1},a_{2}))(\tau_{1}(\gamma_{1_{A}}(a_{1}),\gamma_{1_{A}}(a_{2})))^{-1}=\mu_{\gamma_{1_{A}}(a_{2})}(\kappa_{1}(a_{1}))(\kappa_{1}(a_{1}a_{2}))^{-1}\kappa_{1}(a_{2}).

Similarly, given elements
g_{1},g_{2}\in G
, they have unique expressions of the form
g_{1}=s_{G}(b_{1})l_{1}
and
g_{2}=s_{G}(b_{2})l_{2}
for some
b_{1},b_{2}\in B
and
l_{1},l_{2}\in L
. Now, by similar computations, we get

(4.32)

\gamma_{2}|_{L}(\tau_{2}(b_{1},b_{2}))(\tau_{2}(\gamma_{2_{B}}(b_{1}),\gamma_{2_{B}}(b_{2})))^{-1}=\sigma_{\gamma_{2_{B}}(b_{2})}(\kappa_{2}(b_{1}))(\kappa_{2}(b_{1}b_{2}))^{-1}\kappa_{2}(b_{2}).

Given an element
h\in H
, it can be uniquely written as
h=s_{H}(a)k
for some
k\in K
. Then, we have

\displaystyle\gamma_{2}(R(h))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\gamma_{2}(R(s_{H}(a)k))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\gamma_{2}\big(s_{G}(T(a))\chi(a)S(\nu^{-1}_{T(a)}(k))\big)\mbox{, using Lemma \eqref{f rho chi eqn}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\gamma_{2}(s_{G}(T(a)))\gamma_{2}|_{L}(\chi(a))\gamma_{2}|_{L}(S(\nu^{-1}_{T(a)}(k)))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle s_{G}(\gamma_{2_{B}}(T(a)))\kappa_{2}(T(a))\gamma_{2}|_{L}(\chi(a))\gamma_{2}|_{L}(S(\nu^{-1}_{T(a)}(k))

and

\displaystyle R(\gamma_{1}(h))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle R(\gamma_{1}(s_{H}(a)k))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle R(\gamma_{1}(s_{H}(a))\gamma_{1}(k))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle R(s_{H}(\gamma_{1_{A}}(a))\kappa_{1}(a)\gamma_{1}|_{K}(k))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle s_{G}(T(\gamma_{1_{A}}(a)))\chi(\gamma_{1_{A}}(a))S(\nu^{-1}_{T(\gamma_{1_{A}}(a))}(\kappa_{1}(a)\gamma_{1}|_{K}(k))).

Since
\gamma_{2}(R(h))=R(\gamma_{1}(h))
, we have

(4.33)

\gamma_{2}|_{L}(\chi(a))(\chi(\gamma_{1_{A}}(a)))^{-1}=S\big(\nu^{-1}_{T(\gamma_{1_{A}}(a))}(\kappa_{1}(a))\big)\big(\kappa_{2}(T(a))\big)^{-1},

as

\displaystyle S(\nu^{-1}_{T(\gamma_{1_{A}}(a))}(\gamma_{1}|_{K}(k)))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle S(\nu^{-1}_{\gamma_{2_{B}}(T(a))}(\gamma_{1}|_{K}(k)))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle S(\gamma_{1}|_{K}(\nu^{-1}_{T(a)}(k)))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\gamma_{2}|_{L}(S(\nu^{-1}_{T(a)}(k))).

Given
h\in H
and
g\in G
, they can be uniquely written as
h=s_{H}(a)k
and
g=s_{G}(b)l
for some
a\in A,b\in B,k\in K
and
l\in L
. Then, we have

\displaystyle\gamma_{1}\phi_{g}(h)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\gamma_{1}\big(s_{H}(\beta_{b}(a))\rho(a,b)\nu_{b}(f(l,a)k)\big)\mbox{, using Lemma \eqref{f rho chi eqn}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\gamma_{1}\big(s_{H}(\beta_{b}(a))\big)\gamma_{1}|_{K}(\rho(a,b))\gamma_{1}|_{K}\big(\nu_{b}(f(l,a)k)\big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle s_{H}\big(\gamma_{1_{A}}(\beta_{b}(a))\big)\kappa_{1}(\beta_{b}(a))\gamma_{1}|_{K}(\rho(a,b))\gamma_{1}|_{K}\big(\nu_{b}(f(l,a)k)\big)

and

\displaystyle\phi_{\gamma_{2}(g)}\gamma_{1}(h)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\phi_{\gamma_{2}(s_{G}(b)l)}(\gamma_{1}(s_{H}(a)k))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\phi_{\gamma_{2}(s_{G}(b))\gamma_{2}|_{L}(l)}(\gamma_{1}(s_{H}(a))\gamma_{1}|_{K}(k))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\phi_{s_{G}(\gamma_{2_{B}}(b))\kappa_{2}(b)\gamma_{2}|_{L}(l)}(s_{H}(\gamma_{1_{A}}(a))\kappa_{1}(a)\gamma_{1}|_{K}(k))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle s_{H}\big(\beta_{\gamma_{2_{B}}(b)}(\gamma_{1_{A}}(a))\big)\rho(\gamma_{1_{A}}(a),\gamma_{2_{B}}(b))\nu_{\gamma_{2_{B}}(b)}\big(f(\kappa_{2}(b)\gamma_{2}|_{L}(l),\gamma_{1_{A}}(a))\kappa_{1}(a)\gamma_{1}|_{K}(k)\big),

using Lemma (
3.4
).

Notice that,

(4.34)

s_{H}\big(\gamma_{1_{A}}(\beta_{b}(a))\big)=s_{H}\big(\beta_{\gamma_{2_{B}}(b)}(\gamma_{1_{A}}(a))\big),

since
(\gamma_{1_{A}},\gamma_{2_{B}})
is an automorphism of
\mathcal{A}
. Using (
4.30
), (
4.34
) and Lemma (
3.5
), we get

(4.35)

\gamma_{1}(\rho(a,b))\big(\rho(\gamma_{1_{A}}(a),\gamma_{2_{B}}(b))\big)^{-1}=\nu_{\gamma_{2_{B}}(b)}\big(f(\kappa_{2}(b),\gamma_{1_{A}}(a))\kappa_{1}(a)\big)\big(\kappa_{1}(\beta_{b}(a))\big)^{-1}.

Thus, (
4.31
), (
4.32
), (
4.33
) and (
4.35
) prove condition (2), that is,
\theta^{*}([(\tau_{1},\tau_{2},\rho,\chi)])=\psi^{*}([(\tau_{1},\tau_{2},\rho,\chi)])
.

Conversely, let
(\psi,\theta)\in\operatorname{Aut}(\mathcal{A})\times\operatorname{Aut}(\mathcal{K})
satisfy conditions (1) and (2). Condition (2) guarantees the existence of maps
\kappa_{1}:A\rightarrow K
and
\kappa_{2}:B\rightarrow L
such that
(\kappa_{1},\kappa_{2})\in C^{1}_{RRB}
and

(\tau_{1}^{(\psi,\operatorname{id})},\tau_{2}^{(\psi,\operatorname{id})},\rho^{(\psi,\operatorname{id})},\chi^{(\psi,\operatorname{id})})\,(\tau^{(\operatorname{id},\theta^{-1})}_{1},\tau^{(\operatorname{id},\theta^{-1})}_{2},\rho^{(\operatorname{id},\theta^{-1})},\chi^{(\operatorname{id},\theta^{-1})})^{-1}=\partial^{1}_{RRB}(\kappa_{1},\kappa_{2}).

Define the maps
\gamma_{1}:H\rightarrow H
by

\gamma_{1}(s_{H}(a)k)=s_{H}(\psi_{1}(a))\kappa_{1}(a)\theta_{1}(k)

\text{and }\gamma_{2}:G\rightarrow G
by

\gamma_{2}(s_{G}(b)l)=s_{G}(\psi_{2}(b))\kappa_{2}(b)\theta_{2}(l)

for all
a\in A
,
b\in B
,
k\in K
and
l\in L
. It is easy to see that
\gamma=(\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2})\in\operatorname{Aut}_{\mathcal{K}}(\mathcal{H})
and
\gamma_{\mathcal{A}}=\psi,

\gamma_{\mathcal{K}}=\theta
. This completes the proof of the theorem.
∎
