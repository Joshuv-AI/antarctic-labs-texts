Abstract
. We prove that most permutations of degree n n have some power which is a cycle of prime length approximately log ⁡ n \log n . Explicitly, we show that for n n sufficiently large, the proportion of such elements is at least 1 − 5 / log ⁡ log ​ n 1-5/\log\log n with the prime between log ⁡ n \log n and ( log ⁡ n ) log ⁡ log ⁡ n (\log n)^{\log\log n} . The proportion of even permutations with this property is at least 1 − 7 / log ⁡ log ​ n 1-7/\log\log n .



1.
Introduction

The symmetric and the alternating groups
\mathrm{S}_{n}
and
\mathrm{A}_{n}
of degree
n
,
have been viewed as probability spaces with the uniform distribution since the
seminal work of Gruder
[
G
]
, Gončarov
[
Gon
]
and
Erdős-Turán
[
ET2
]
. There is an analogy between the disjoint
cycle decomposition of a permutation and the prime factorization of an integer
whereby the cycles correspond to prime numbers (see
[
aG
]
or
[
Ford
]

for a description). The probability that
a permutation
g\in\mathrm{S}_{n}
is an
n
-cycle is
1/n
, while the probability that
a number
p\leqslant x
is prime is
1/\log x
, and according to this analogy,
n

corresponds to
\log x
. Counting integers without small prime factors is like counting
permutations without small cycles: both lead to limits reminiscent of
Mertens’ third theorem:

\lim_{n\to\infty}\left(\log n\prod_{p\leqslant n}\left(1-\frac{1}{p}\right)\right)=e^{-\gamma},\quad\mbox{where $\gamma=0.5772\cdots$}

is the Euler–Mascheroni constant. Statistical methods have been invaluable for proving
theoretical results. For example, the number of cycles of
a uniformly distributed random element
g\in\mathrm{S}_{n}
(or
g\in\mathrm{A}_{n}
)
behaves as
n\to\infty
like a normal distribution
N(\mu,\sigma^{2})

(
c.f.

[
GS
]
*Theorem 1) with mean and variance

\mu=\sigma^{2}=\log n
, see
[
Gon
,
PR
]
.
This paper focuses on proving the following theorem.
We abbreviate
\log(n)
and
\log(\log(n))
as
\log n
and

\log\log n
, respectively. All logarithms are to the natural
base
e=2.718\cdots
.

Theorem 1
.

Suppose that
G\in\{\mathrm{A}_{n},\mathrm{S}_{n}\}
.
Let
\rho_{G}
be the proportion of permutations in
G
for which
some power is a cycle of prime length
p
, where
p

lies in the open interval
(\log n,(\log n)^{\log\log n})
.
Then, for
n
sufficiently large,

\rho_{G}\geqslant 1-c/\log\log n
where
c=5
when
G=\mathrm{S}_{n}
, and
c=7

when
G=\mathrm{A}_{n}
.

A permutation
g\in\mathrm{S}_{n}
having a single cycle of length greater than 1
is called a
k
-cycle, where the cycle has length
k
. Their theoretical
importance has long been recognised: the
presence of a
k
-cycle in a primitive subgroup

of
\mathrm{S}_{n}
was shown to imply
that
G
is
\mathrm{A}_{n}
or
\mathrm{S}_{n}
by Jordan in 1873 in the case where

(
[

]
,
or see
[
Wielandt
]
*Theorem 13.9). The same conclusion also holds for

by a result in Marggraff’s
dissertation
[

]
, see also
[
J
]
*Corollary 1.3,

[
Wielandt
]
*Theorem 13.5 and P. M. Neumann’s Mathematical
Review MR0424912.

To use Jordan’s result for deciding whether a given primitive subgroup
G

of
\mathrm{S}_{n}
is indeed
\mathrm{A}_{n}
or
\mathrm{S}_{n}
, one needs to locate a
p
-cycle
with

a prime, say by choosing random elements from
G
. It is
inefficient to do this directly since the proportion of such elements in

\mathrm{A}_{n}
or
\mathrm{S}_{n}
is

(see Section

). Instead one
searches for a ‘pre-

-cycle’, a permutation which powers to a
p
-cycle.
It is shown in
[

]
*Lemma 10.2.3 that the proportion of elements

\mathrm{A}_{n}
or
\mathrm{S}_{n}
that power to a

-cycle

, while an application of the main result

[

]
shows that considering only pre-

-cycles

, produces a proportion

.
Thus to approach Seress’s asymptotic proportion the primes

allowed to grow unboundedly with

.

Quite decisively, and perhaps surprisingly, the third author recently
showed
[
U1
]
*Theorem 2 that
almost all
permutations
in
G\in\{\mathrm{A}_{n},\mathrm{S}_{n}\}
are pre-
p
-cycles for some prime
p
.
The purpose of this paper
is to prove an even stronger statement: namely that, asymptotically, almost
all permutations in
G
power to a
p
-cycle where the
prime
p
is roughly
\log n
(Theorem
1
) and we
may derive, from Proposition
6
and the proof of Theorem

,
explicit values for

such that the bounds of Theorem

.
On the one hand we quantify the asymptotic results

]
,
giving a precise analysis with explicit bounds rather than asymptotics.
In addition we show (which came as a surprise to the authors) that the prime

chosen in a very small interval of length logarithmic in

.
Theorem

relies on Proposition

which strengthens a key technical

[

]
*Theorem VI of Erdős and Turán. Theorem

shows that the proportion of pre-

-cycles in

is at

for

. We prove in

1.1.
Recognising finite symmetric and alternating groups

We call a permutation
g\in\mathrm{S}_{n}
a

pre-
k
-cycle
if it powers to a
k
-cycle where
k>1
. For example,

g=(1,2,3,4)(5,6)(7,8,9)
is a pre-3-cycle as
g^{4}=(7,8,9)
, but
g

is not a pre-2-cycle. A simple argument (Lemma
3
) shows
that the disjoint cycle decomposition of a pre-
k
-cycle contains exactly one

k
-cycle and all other cycles (if any) have lengths coprime to
k
.
The number of
k
-cycles in
\mathrm{S}_{n}
equals
n!/c(k)
where

c(k)=k(n-k)!
. Hence the proportion
\rho_{n}
of cycles in
\mathrm{S}_{n}
equals

\sum_{k=2}^{n}1/c(k)=\sum_{j=0}^{n-2}\frac{1}{(n-j)j!}
. For
n
even,

n\rho_{n}-\kern-1.0pt\sum_{j=0}^{n-2}\frac{1}{j!}=\sum_{j=0}^{n-2}\frac{j}{(n-j)j!}\kern-1.0pt=\kern-1.0pt\sum_{j=0}^{n/2}\frac{j}{(n-j)j!}+\kern-3.0pt\sum_{j=n/2}^{n-2}\frac{j}{(n-j)j!}\kern-1.0pt<\kern-1.0pt\frac{2}{n}\sum_{j=0}^{n/2}\frac{j}{j!}+\kern-3.0pt\sum_{j=n/2}^{\infty}\frac{j}{j!}.

Thus
|n\rho_{n}-e|\to 0
as
n\to\infty
, and so
\rho_{n}=O(n^{-1})
.
By contrast, the proportion of permutations in
\mathrm{S}_{n}
which are
pre-
k
-cycles (for
2\leqslant k\leqslant n
) approaches 1 as
n\to\infty
.

Let
X\subseteq\mathrm{S}_{n}

and suppose that the subgroup
\langle X\rangle
of
\mathrm{S}_{n}
generated by
X
is primitive.
Then a probabilistic algorithm for testing whether

\langle X\rangle
contains
\mathrm{A}_{n}
involves a
random search for a pre-
p
-cycle, where
p
is a prime less than
n-2
,
and it turns out that such elements have density 1 as
n\to\infty

(Theorem
1
). If the subgroup

\langle X\rangle
does not contain
\mathrm{A}_{n}
, we want to limit the (fruitless)
search for pre-
p
-cycles, and for this we need explicit lower bounds on their
density in both
\mathrm{A}_{n}
and
\mathrm{S}_{n}
, see Remark
2
.

There are two important tools in our proof. The first is to quantify
the sum
\sum_{a<p\leqslant b}1/p^{2}
as a function of the real numbers
a
and
b
.
(Here, and henceforth,
p
denotes a prime.) The second is to estimate the
number of permutations whose cycle lengths do not have certain
‘forbidden lengths’. Erdős-Turán
[
ET2
]
*Theorem VI proved that
the probability
\rho
that the cycle lengths of
g\in\mathrm{S}_{n}
do not lie in
a subset
\mathcal{A}\subseteq\{1,\dots,n\}
is at most
1/\mu

where
\mu=\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}1/a
. Applying a result of
Ford
[
Ford
]
*Theorem 2.9 gives the bound
\rho\leqslant e^{1-\mu}
(take
r=1
,

T_{1}=\mathcal{A}
and
k_{1}=0
in Theorem 2.9).
We prove in Proposition
6
that
\rho<e^{\gamma-\mu}
,
where
\gamma=0.5772\cdots
. Our bound is less than
2/3
the size of
bounds of Erdős-Turán and Ford. This small improvement is needed
to obtain practically useful bounds in Theorem
10
.
Manstavičius
[
M
]
*p. 39 claimed that a slightly weaker bound,

viz.

\rho<e^{\gamma-\mu}(1+1/n)
,
followed from a pre-print of his.
Unfortunately, we could not locate his pre-print.

After describing the cycle structure of pre-
k
-cycles
in Section
2
,
we prove our bound on forbidden cycle lengths in Section
3
.
The sum
\sum_{a<p\leqslant b}1/p^{2}
is estimated in Section
4
,
and the main theorems are proved in Section
5
.

Remark 2
.

Suppose that the proportion of pre-
p
-cycles in
\mathrm{A}_{n}
or
\mathrm{S}_{n}

is at least
c_{0}=c_{0}(n)
.
Given a primitive permutation group
G\leqslant\mathrm{S}_{n}
, then the probability
that
\mathrm{A}_{n}\leqslant G
and
m
independent random selections
from
G
do
not
find a
pre-
p
-cycle for some
p
with
2\leqslant p\leqslant n-3
, is at most
(1-c_{0})^{m}
.
This upper bound is at most a prescribed “failure probability”
\varepsilon

if and only if
m\geqslant\log(\varepsilon)/\log(1-c_{0})
. Using Theorem
1
, we can
take
c_{0}(n)=1-c/\log\log n
, and a larger value is given in Theorem
10
.
Therefore we may take
c_{0}
to be an absolute constant thus requiring
only
m=m(\varepsilon)
random selections. We prove in Remark
11
that

c_{0}=0.05
works for
\mathrm{S}_{n}
and this gives
m(\varepsilon)\geqslant 20\log(\varepsilon^{-1})
.
By contrast, the standard analysis of this
algorithm
[
Seress
]
*pp. 226–227 requires

m=O(\log(n)\log(\varepsilon^{-1}))
random selections (because the proportion
of pre-
p
-cycles in the range
n/2<p<n-2
is approximately
\log 2/\log n
).
Thus, our analysis gives an algorithm that is faster by a factor
of
\log(n)
.

2.
Precycles

We begin with some notation. Fix
g\in\mathrm{S}_{n}
and let

\lambda=\lambda(g)
be the partition of
n

induced by the (disjoint) cycle lengths of
g
. We write
\lambda\vdash n
and

\lambda=\langle 1^{m_{1}}2^{m_{2}}\cdots n^{m_{n}}\rangle
where
m_{k}
is
the multiplicity of the part
k
, so
n=\sum_{k=1}^{n}km_{k}
. The vector

(m_{1},\dots,m_{n})
is called the
cycle type
of
g
. A pre-
k
-cycle

g\in\mathrm{S}_{n}
can be recognized by it
cycle partition

\lambda(g)
.

Lemma 3
.

Let
g\in\mathrm{S}_{n}
be a pre-
k
-cycle with

\lambda(g)=\langle 1^{m_{1}}2^{m_{2}}\cdots n^{m_{n}}\rangle
.
Then
m_{k}=1
and for
i\neq k
,
m_{i}>0
implies
\gcd(k,i)=1
.
That is,
g
has a unique
k
-cycle, and its
other cycles (if any) have lengths coprime to
k
.

Proof.

Suppose
g
has disjoint cycle decomposition
g_{1}\cdots g_{r}

where
g_{i}
is a cycle of length
\lambda_{i}
and

\lambda_{1}+\cdots+\lambda_{r}=n
. Note that

g^{\ell}=g_{1}^{\ell}\cdots g_{r}^{\ell}
and

g_{i}^{\ell}
is a product of
\gcd(\lambda_{i},\ell)
cycles each of
length
\lambda_{i}/\gcd(\lambda_{i},\ell)
. Suppose
g^{\ell}
is a

k
-cycle. Then there exists an
i
for which

\lambda_{i}/\gcd(\lambda_{i},\ell)=k
and
\gcd(\lambda_{i},\ell)=1
.
Also,
\lambda_{j}/\gcd(\lambda_{j},\ell)=1
for
j\neq i
.
Thus
\lambda_{i}=k
,
\gcd(\lambda_{i},\ell)=1
. For
j\neq i
,

\lambda_{j}\mid\ell
. Hence
\gcd(k,\lambda_{j})=1
.
∎

For a prime

\left\{g\in X\bigm|m_{p}(g)=1\textup{ and }\sum_{i\geqslant 2}m_{ip}(g)=0\right\}.

The density of

\sigma_{n}=\prod_{i=1}^{\lfloor n/p\rfloor}(1-1/(ip))

by

3.
Forbidden cycle lengths

Given a set
\mathcal{A}\subseteq\{1,\dots,n\}
of ‘forbidden’ cycle lengths,
let
\rho
be the proportion of elements of
\mathrm{S}_{n}
having no cycle length
in
\mathcal{A}
. Erdős and Turán prove
\rho\leqslant\mu^{-1}
in
[
ET2
]
*Theorem VI
where
\mu=\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}1/a
. To see that his bound is not optimal,
consider the case when
\mathcal{A}=\{1\}
and
\mu=1
. In this case,

\rho
is the proportion of derangements and the Erdős-Turán bound
is unhelpful. However, an inclusion-exclusion argument shows that

\rho=\sum_{k=0}^{n}(-1)^{k}/k!
. As
e^{-1}<\rho
when
n
is even and
\rho<e^{-1}

otherwise, we expect for large
n
a bound of the form
c/e
where
c=1+o(1)
.
We prove in Proposition
6

that there is a bound of the form
\rho\leqslant c\kern 1.0pte^{-\mu}
where
c=1.781\cdots
.
The constant
c
equals
e^{\gamma}
where
\gamma=0.5772\cdots
is the
Euler-Mascheroni constant. Our proof uses exponential generating functions,
and has similarities to calculations used by Gruder
[
G
]
, 15 years before Erdős and Turán
[
ET2
]
.

If
\mu<1
, then the Erdős-Turán bound gives no information.
On the other hand, if
\mu\geqslant 1
, then we will show that the upper
bound we obtain in Proposition
6
, namely
e^{\gamma-\mu}
, is strictly less
than the bound
\mu^{-1}
of Erdős and Turán. We use the fact
that
e^{\mu}\geqslant e\mu
, since the tangent to the curve
y=e^{x}
at
x=1

is
y=ex
. Hence

e^{\gamma-\mu}=\frac{c}{e^{\mu}}<\frac{1.782}{e^{\mu}}\leqslant\frac{1.782}{e\mu}<\frac{2}{3\mu}.

Similarly, our bound improves the bound
e^{1-\mu}
of
Ford
[
Ford
]
*Theorem 2.9 by a factor of
e^{1-\gamma}<2/3
. Note that
Ford’s bound can be sharpened using estimations in the proof
of Proposition
6
.

The centralizer
of
g
in
\mathrm{S}_{n}
has order
C(\lambda(g)):=\prod_{k=1}^{n}k^{m_{k}}m_{k}!
, and
hence the conjugacy class
g^{\mathrm{S}_{n}}
has size
n!/C(\lambda(g))
. Since the
conjugacy classes in
\mathrm{S}_{n}
are parameterized by the partitions of
n
,
it follows that
\sum_{\lambda\vdash n}n!/C(\lambda)=n!
, or equivalently that

\sum_{\lambda\vdash n}1/C(\lambda)=1
.

The reader when trying to compare our analysis with that in
[
ET2
]

may wish to know that the quantity
L(P)
defined
on
[
ET2
]
*p. 159 for a permutation
P
should be the number of
cycles of
P
with length equal
to some
a_{\nu}\in\mathcal{A}
, and not the number of
a_{\nu}\in\mathcal{A}
with the property
that
P
has a cycle of length
a_{\nu}
.

Remark 5
.

Given a set
X
and a property
P
, let
\rho(X)
denote the proportion of
elements
x\in X
that have property
P
. If
Y\subseteq X
,
then
|Y|\rho(Y)\leqslant|X|\rho(X)
. To see this let
P(X)
be the subset
of
x\in X
that have property
P
. Since
|P(X)|=|X|\rho(X)
and

P(Y)\subseteq P(X)
, we have
|Y|\rho(Y)\leqslant|X|\rho(X)
.

Proposition 6
.

The proportion

\rho(\mathrm{S}_{n})
of elements of
\mathrm{S}_{n}
having no cycle length in
\mathcal{A}\subseteq\{1,\dots,n\}

satisfies
\rho(\mathrm{S}_{n})<e^{\gamma-\mu}
where
\mu=\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}1/a

and
e^{\gamma}\approx 1.781
. The proportion
\rho(\mathrm{A}_{n})
of elements
of
\mathrm{A}_{n}
with no cycle length in
\mathcal{A}
satisfies
\rho(\mathrm{A}_{n})<2e^{\gamma-\mu}
.

Proof.

For
k\in\{1,\dots,n\}
, let
p_{k}
equal 0 if
k\in\mathcal{A}
, and 1 otherwise.
Set

P(z)=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{p_{k}z^{k}}{k}\qquad\textup{and}\qquad Q(z)=e^{P(z)}.

As
P(z)
is a polynomial,
Q(z)
is a differentiable function of the
complex variable
z
, so its Taylor series
\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}q_{m}z^{m}

at
z=0
converges to
Q(z)
for all
z
. We consider the coefficient
q_{n}
.
Since

Q(z)=\prod_{k\not\in\mathcal{A},\,k\leqslant n}e^{z^{k}/k}=\prod_{k\not\in\mathcal{A},\,k\leqslant n}\,\sum_{m_{k}=0}^{\infty}\frac{(z^{k}/k)^{m_{k}}}{m_{k}!},

the term
q_{n}z^{n}
is a sum whose summands have the form

\prod_{k\not\in\mathcal{A},\,k\leqslant n}\frac{z^{km_{k}}}{k^{m_{k}}(m_{k})!}=\frac{z^{n}}{C(\lambda)}\qquad\textup{for each partition $\lambda=\langle k^{m_{k}}\rangle_{k\not\in\mathcal{A}}$ with $n=\sum_{k\not\in\mathcal{A}}km_{k}$.}

It follows that
q_{n}=\sum_{\lambda\vdash n}1/C(\lambda)
where the parts of

\lambda
do not lie in
\mathcal{A}
(equivalently
m_{k}=0
for all
k\in\mathcal{A}
).
This is precisely the proportion
\rho(\mathrm{S}_{n})
of elements of
\mathrm{S}_{n}
none of
whose cycle lengths lie in
\mathcal{A}
. Alternatively,
[
G
]
*Eq. (18)
shows that
q_{n}=\rho(\mathrm{S}_{n})
.

We now compute an upper bound for
q_{n}
. Define the

n
th harmonic number to be

H_{n}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{1}{k},\qquad\textup{and let}\qquad\gamma=\lim_{n\to\infty}(H_{n}-\log n)=0.5772\cdots

be the Euler-Mascheroni constant. The error term
E(n)=H_{n}-\log n-\gamma

satisfies
0<E(n)<1/(2n)
by
[
H
]
*p. 75. Since
e^{x}<1+x+x^{2}+\cdots=1/(1-x)

for
0<x<1
, it follows that
e^{1/(2n)}<1+1/(2n-1)\leqslant 1+1/n
.
Hence

1<e^{E(n)}<1+\frac{1}{n}\quad\textup{and}\quad P(1)=\sum_{k\not\in\mathcal{A},\,k\leqslant n}\frac{1}{k}=H_{n}-\mu=\log n+\gamma-\mu+E(n).

The previous display implies

(1)

Q(1)=e^{P(1)}=e^{\log n}e^{\gamma-\mu}e^{E(n)}<ne^{\gamma-\mu}\left(1+\frac{1}{n}\right)=(n+1)e^{\gamma-\mu}.

Differentiating the equation
Q(z)=e^{P(z)}
gives

Q^{\prime}(z)=P^{\prime}(z)Q(z),\quad\textup{that is,}\quad\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}kq_{k}z^{k-1}=\left(\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}p_{n-k}z^{n-1-k}\right)\left(\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}q_{k}z^{k}\right).

Equating the coefficients of
z^{n-1}
on both sides gives

nq_{n}=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}p_{n-k}q_{k}\leqslant\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}q_{k}\leqslant\left(\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}q_{k}\right)-q_{n}=Q(1)-q_{n}.

It follows from (
1
) that

(n+1)q_{n}\leqslant Q(1)<(n+1)e^{\gamma-\mu}.

Cancelling
n+1
gives
q_{n}<e^{\gamma-\mu}
, that is,

\rho(\mathrm{S}_{n})<e^{\gamma-\mu}<1.782e^{-\mu}
. Finally, Lemma
5
implies
that

\rho(\mathrm{A}_{n})\leqslant 2\rho(\mathrm{S}_{n})<2e^{\gamma-\mu}<3.563e^{-\mu}
.
∎

4.
Estimating sums

In this section we bound the quantity
\mu
in Proposition
6
. One approach is to use
the Stieltjes integral
[
RS
]
*p. 67. Instead, we take an elementary
approach using finite sums, which we now briefly review. In the sequel,

k
denotes an integer and
p
denotes a prime. The

backward difference
of a function
f(k)
is defined by

(\nabla f)(k)=f(k)-f(k-1)
. An easy calculation shows

(2)

\displaystyle\sum_{k=a+1}^{b}(\nabla f)(k)

\displaystyle=f(b)-f(a),\quad\textup{and}

(3)

\displaystyle\nabla(f(k)g(k))

\displaystyle=(\nabla f)(k)g(k-1)+f(k)(\nabla g)(k).

Rearranging (
