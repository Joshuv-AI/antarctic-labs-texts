Ridgeless Interpolation with Shallow ReLU Networks in 1 ​ D 1D is Nearest Neighbor Curvature Extrapolation and Provably Generalizes on Lipschitz Functions


Abstract
. We prove a precise geometric description of all one layer ReLU networks z ⁡ ( x , θ ) z(x;\theta) with a single linear unit and input/output dimensions equal to one that interpolate a given dataset 𝒟 = { ( x i , f ⁡ ( x i ) ) } \mathcal{D}=\left\{(x_{i},f(x_{i}))\right\} and, among all such interpolants, minimize the ℓ 2 \ell_{2} -norm of the neuron weights. Such networks can intuitively be thought of as those that minimize the mean-squared error over 𝒟 \mathcal{D} plus an infinitesimal weight decay penalty. We therefore refer to them as ridgeless ReLU interpolants. Our description proves that, to extrapolate values z ⁡ ( x , θ ) z(x;\theta) for inputs x ∈ ( x i , x i + 1 ) x\in(x_{i},x_{i+1}) lying between two consecutive datapoints, a ridgeless ReLU interpolant simply compares the signs of the discrete estimates for the curvature of f f at x i x_{i} and x i + 1 x_{i+1} derived from the dataset 𝒟 \mathcal{D} . If the curvature estimates at x i x_{i} and x i + 1 x_{i+1} have different signs, then z ⁡ ( x , θ ) z(x;\theta) must be linear on ( x i , x i + 1 ) (x_{i},x_{i+1}) . If in contrast the curvature estimates at x i x_{i} and x i + 1 x_{i+1} are both positive (resp. negative), then z ⁡ ( x , θ ) z(x;\theta) is convex (resp. concave) on ( x i , x i + 1 ) (x_{i},x_{i+1}) . Our results show that ridgeless ReLU interpolants achieve the best possible generalization for learning 1 ​ d 1d Lipschitz functions, up to universal constants.

Introduction

The ability of overparameterized neural networks to simultaneously fit data (i.e. interpolate) and generalize to unseen data (i.e. extrapolate) is a robust empirical finding that spans the use of deep learning in tasks from computer vision
[
KSH12
,
HZRS16
]
, natural language processing
[
BMR+20
]
, and reinforcement learning
[
SHM+16
,
VBC+19
,
JEP+21
]
. This observation is surprising when viewed from the lens of traditional learning theory
[
VC71
,
BM02
]
, which advocates for capacity control of model classes and strong regularization to avoid overfitting.

Part of the difficulty in explaining conceptually why neural networks are able to generalize is that it is unclear how to understand, concretely in terms of the network function, various forms of implicit and explicit regularization used in practice. For example, a well-chosen initialization for gradient-based optimizers is key to ensuring good generalization properties of the resulting learned network
[
MM15
,
HZRS15
,
XBSD+18
]
. However, the specific geometric or analytic properties of the learned network ensured by a successful initialization scheme are hard to pin down.

In a similar vein, it is standard practice to experiment with explicit regularizers such as weight decay, obtained by adding an
\ell_{2}
penalty on model parameters to the underlying empirical risk. While weight decay is easy to describe via its effect on parameters, it is typically challenging to translate this into properties of a learned non-linear model. In the simple setting of one layer ReLU networks there has been some relatively recent progress in this direction. Specifically, starting with an observation in
[
NTS14
]
the articles
[
SESS19
,
OWSS19
,
PN20a
,
PN20b
,
PN21
]
explore and develop the fact that
\ell_{2}
regularization on parameters in this setting is provably equivalent to penalizing the total variation of the derivative of the network function (cf eg Theorem
1.3
from prior work below). While the results in these articles hold for any input dimension, in this article we consider the simplest case of input dimension
1
. In this setting, our main contributions are:

(1)

Given a dataset
\mathcal{D}=\left\{(x_{i},y_{i})\right\}
with scalar inputs and outputs, we obtain a complete characterization of all one layer ReLU networks with a single linear unit which fit the data and, among all such interpolating networks, do so with the minimal
\ell_{2}
norm of the neuron weights. There are infinitely many such networks and, unlike in prior work, our characterization is phrased directly in terms of the behavior of the network function on intervals
(x_{i},x_{i+1})
between consecutive datapoints. Our description is purely geometric and can be summarized informally as follows (see Theorem
1.2
for the precise statement):

•

If we order
x_{1}<\cdots<x_{m}
, then the data itself gives a discrete curvature estimate

\epsilon_{i}:=\mathrm{sgn}\left(s_{i}-s_{i-1}\right),\qquad s_{i}:=\frac{y_{i+1}-y_{i}}{x_{i+1}-x_{i}}

at
x_{i}
of whatever function generated the data. See Figure

•

If the curvature estimates
\epsilon_{i}
and
\epsilon_{i+1}
at
x_{i}
and
x_{i+1}
disagree, then the network must be linear on
(x_{i},x_{i+1})

•

If the curvature estimates agree and are positive (resp. negative), then the network function is convex (resp. concave) on

and lies below (resp. above) the straight line interpolant of the data. See Figures

(2)

The geometric description of the space of interpolants of

1.1.
Setup and Informal Statement of Results

Consider a one layer ReLU network

(1.1)

z(x)=z(x;\theta):=ax+b+\sum_{j=1}^{n}W_{j}^{(2)}\left[W_{j}^{(1)}x+b_{i}^{(1)}\right]_{+},\quad[t]_{+}:=\mathrm{ReLU}(t)=\max\left\{0,t\right\}

with a single linear unit
2
2

2

The presence of the linear term
ax+b
is not really standard in practice but is adopted in keeping with prior work
[
SESS19
,
OWSS19
,
PN20a
]
since it leads a cleaner mathematical formulation of results.
and input/output dimensions equal to one. For a given dataset

\mathcal{D}=\left\{\left(x_{i},y_{i}\right),\,i=1,\ldots,m\right\},\qquad-\infty<x_{1}<\cdots<x_{m}<\infty,\quad y_{i}\in\mathbb{R},

if the number of datapoints
m
is smaller than the network width
n
, there are infinitely many choices of the parameter vector
\theta
for which
z(x;\theta)
interpolates (i.e. fits) the data:

(1.2)

z(x_{i};\theta)=y_{i},\qquad\forall\,i=1,\ldots,m.

Without further information about how
\theta
was selected, little can be said about the function
x\mapsto z(x;\theta)
on intervals
(x_{i},x_{i+1})
between two consecutive datapoints when
n
is much larger than
m
. This precludes useful generalization guarantees that hold uniformly over all
\theta
subject only to the interpolation condition (
1.2
).

In practice interpolants are not chosen arbitrary. Instead, they are typically learned by some variant of gradient descent starting from a random initialization. For a given network architecture, initialization scheme, optimizer, data augmentation scheme, regularizer, and so on, understanding how the learned network uses the known labels
\left\{y_{i},\,i=1,\ldots,m\right\}
to extrapolate values of
z(x;\theta)
for
x
in intervals
(x_{i},x_{i+1})
away from the datapoints in
\mathcal{D}
is an important open problem. To obtain non-trivial generalization estimates and make progress on this problem, a fruitful line of inquiry in prior work has been to search for additional complexity measures based on margins
[
WLLM18
]
, PAC-Bayes estimates
[
DR17
,
DR18
,
NK19
]
, weight matrix norms
[
NTS15
,
BFT17
]
, information theoretic compression estimates
[
AGNZ18
]
, Rachemacher complexity
[
GRS18
]
, etc that, while perhaps not explicitly regularized, are hopefully small in trained networks. The idea is then that these complexity measures being small gives additional constrains on the capacity of the space of learned networks. We refer the interested reader to
[
JNM+19
]
for a review and empirically comparison of many such approaches.

In this article, we take a different approach to studying generalization. We do not seek general results that are valid for any network architecture. Instead, our goal is to describe completely, in concrete geometrical terms, the properties of one layer ReLU networks
z(x;\theta)
that interpolate a dataset
\mathcal{D}
in the sense of (
1.2
) with the minimal possible
\ell_{2}
penalty

C(\theta)=C(\theta,n)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left(\left|W_{j}^{(1)}\right|^{2}+\left|W_{j}^{(2)}\right|^{2}\right)

on the neuron weights. More precisely, we study the space of ridgeless ReLU interpolants

(1.3)

\mathrm{RidgelessReLU}(\mathcal{D}):=\left\{z(x;\theta)\quad|\quad z(x_{i};\theta)=y_{i}\,\,\,\,\forall(x_{i},y_{i})\in\mathcal{D},\quad C(\theta)=C_{*}\right\},

of a dataset
\mathcal{D}
, where

C_{*}:=\inf_{\theta,n}\left\{C(\theta,n)~|~z(x_{i};n,\theta)=y_{i}\,\,\,\,\forall(x_{i},y_{i})\in\mathcal{D}\right\}.

The elements of
\mathrm{RidgelessReLU}(\mathcal{D})
can intuitively be thought of as all ReLU networks that minimize a weakly penalized loss

(1.4)

\mathcal{L}(\theta;\mathcal{D})+\lambda C(\theta),\qquad\lambda\ll 1,

where
\mathcal{L}
is an empirical loss, such as the mean squared error over
\mathcal{D}
, and the strength
\lambda
of the weight decay penalty
C(\theta)
is infinitesimal. It it plausible but by no means obvious that, with high probability, gradient descent from a random initialization and a weight decay penalty whose strength decreases to zero over training converges to an element in
\mathrm{RidgelessReLU}(\mathcal{D})
. This article does not study optimization, and we therefore leave this as an interesting open problem. Our main result is simple description of
\mathrm{RidgelessReLU}(\mathcal{D})
and can informally be stated as follows:

Theorem 1.1

(Informal Statement of Theorem
1.2
)
.

Fix a dataset
\mathcal{D}=\left\{(x_{i},y_{i}),\,i=1,\ldots,m\right\}
. Each datapoint
(x_{i},y_{i})
gives an estimate

\epsilon_{i}:=\mathrm{sgn}\left(s_{i}-s_{i-1}\right),\qquad s_{i}:=\frac{y_{i+1}-y_{i}}{x_{i+1}-x_{i}}

for the local curvature of the data (Figure
1
). Among all continuous and piecewise linear functions
f
that fit
\mathcal{D}
exactly, the ones in
\mathrm{RidgelessReLU}(\mathcal{D})
are precisely those that:

•

Are convex (resp. concave) on intervals
(x_{i},x_{i+1})
at which neighboring datapoints agree on the local curvature in the sense that
\epsilon_{i}=\epsilon_{i+1}=1
(resp.
\epsilon_{i}=\epsilon_{i+1}=-1)
. On such intervals

lies below (resp. above) the straight line interpolant of the data. See Figures

.

•

Before giving a precise statement our results, we mention that, as described in detail below, the space
\mathrm{RidgelessReLU}(\mathcal{D})
has been considered in a number of prior articles
[
SESS19
,
OWSS19
,
PN20a
]
. Our starting point will be the useful but abstract characterization of

they obtained in terms of the total variation of the derivative of

(see (

)).

Let us also note that the conclusions of Theorem
1.1
(and Theorem
1.2
) also hold under seemingly very different hypotheses from ours. Namely, instead of
\ell_{2}
-regularization on the parameters,
[

]

\mathcal{D}

Further, our characterization of
\mathrm{RidgelessReLU}(\mathcal{D})

Finally, note that we allow networks

1.2

C(\theta)

. Further, instead of choosing

the same kind of result holds with high probability if
x_{i}
are drawn independently at random from
[0,1]
, with the
2L/m
on the right hand side replaced by
C\log(m)L/m
for some universal constant
C>0
. The appearance of the logarithm is due to the fact that among
m
iid points in
[0,1]
the the largest spacing between consecutive points scales like
C\log(m)/m
with high probability. Similar generalization results can easily be established, depending on the level of smoothness assumed for
f_{*}
and the uniformity of the datapoints
x_{i}
.

In writing this article, it at first appeared to the author that the generalization bounds (
1.7
) cannot be directly obtained from the relation (
1.5
) of prior work. The issue is that a priori the relation (
1.5
) gives bounds only on the global value of
\left|\left|Df\right|\right|_{TV}
, suggesting perhaps that it does not provide strong constraints on local information about the behavior of ridgeless interpolants on small intervals
(x_{i},x_{i+1})
. However, the relation (
1.5
) can actually be effectively
localized
to yield the estimates (
1.6
) and (
1.7
) but with worse constants. The idea is the following. Fix
f\in\mathrm{RidgelessReLU}(\mathcal{D})
. For any
i_{*}=3,\ldots,m-2
define the left, right and central portions of
\mathcal{D}
as follows:

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{L}

\displaystyle:=\left\{(x_{i},y_{i}),~i<i_{*}\right\},\quad\mathcal{D}_{C}:=\left\{(x_{i},y_{i}),~i_{*}-1\leq i\leq i_{*}+1\right\},\quad\mathcal{D}_{R}:=\left\{(x_{i},y_{i}),~i_{*}<i\right\}.

Consider further the left, right, and central versions of
f
, defined by

\displaystyle f_{L}(x)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}f(x),&~x<x_{i_{*}}\\
\ell_{i_{*}}(x),&~x>x_{i_{*}}\end{cases},\qquad f_{R}(x)=\begin{cases}f(x),&~x>x_{i_{*}}\\
\ell_{i_{*}}(x),&~x<x_{i_{*}}\end{cases}

and

f_{C}(x)=\begin{cases}f(x),&~x_{i*-1}<x<x_{i_{*}+1}\\
\ell_{i_{*}-1}(x),&~x<x_{i_{*}-1}\\
\ell_{i_{*}}(x),&~x>x_{i_{*}+1}\end{cases},

Using (
1.5
), we have
\left|\left|Df_{\mathcal{D}}\right|\right|_{TV}=\left|\left|Df\right|\right|_{TV}
. Further,

\displaystyle\left|\left|Df\right|\right|_{TV}\geq\left|\left|Df_{L}\right|\right|_{TV}+\left|\left|Df_{C}\right|\right|_{TV}+\left|\left|Df_{R}\right|\right|_{TV},

which, by again applying (
1.5
) but this time to
\mathcal{D}_{L},\mathcal{D}_{R}
and
f_{L},f_{R}
, yields the bound

\left|\left|Df\right|\right|_{TV}\geq\left|\left|f_{\mathcal{D}_{L}}\right|\right|_{TV}+\left|\left|Df_{C}\right|\right|_{TV}+\left|\left|Df_{\mathcal{D}_{R}}\right|\right|_{TV}.

Using that

\left|\left|Df_{\mathcal{D}}\right|\right|_{TV}=\sum_{i=2}^{m}\left|s_{i}-s_{i-1}\right|,\quad\left|\left|f_{\mathcal{D}_{L}}\right|\right|_{TV}=\sum_{i=2}^{i_{*}-2}\left|s_{i}-s_{i-1}\right|,\quad\left|\left|Df_{\mathcal{D}_{R}}\right|\right|_{TV}=\sum_{i=i_{*}+2}^{m-1}\left|s_{i}-s_{i-1}\right|

we derive the localized estimate

\left|s_{i_{*}+1}-s_{i_{*}}\right|+\left|s_{i_{*}}-s_{i_{*}-1}\right|+\left|s_{i_{*}-1}-s_{i_{*}-2}\right|\geq\left|\left|Df_{C}\right|\right|_{TV}

Note further that

\left|\left|Df_{C}\right|\right|_{TV}\geq\max_{x\in(x_{i},x_{i+1})}Df(x)-\min_{x\in(x_{i},x_{i+1})}Df(x),

where the max and min are taken over those
x
at which
Df(x)
exists. The interpolation condition
f(x_{i})=y_{i}
and
f(x_{i+1})=y_{i+1}
yields that

\max_{x\in(x_{i},x_{i+1})}Df(x)\geq s_{i}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\min_{x\in(x_{i},x_{i+1})}Df(x)\leq s_{i}.

Putting together the previous three lines of inequalities (and checking the edge cases
i=2,m-1
), we conclude that for any
i=2,\ldots,m-1
we have

\left|\left|Df(x)-s_{i}\right|\right|_{L^{\infty}(x_{i},x_{i+1})}\leq\left|s_{i+1}-s_{i}\right|+\left|s_{i}-s_{i-1}\right|+\left|s_{i-1}-s_{i-2}\right|,

where we set
s_{0}=s_{1}
. Thus, proceeding as in the last few lines of the proof of Corollary
1.4
, we conclude that

\left|\left|f\right|\right|_{\mathrm{Lip}}\leq 7\left|\left|f_{*}\right|\right|_{\mathrm{Lip}}

and that therefore for any
x\in[0,1]
we find

\left|f(x)-f_{*}(x)\right|\leq\frac{14L}{m}

when the datapoints
x_{i}
are uniformly spaced. These last two estimates are precisely like those in Corollary
1.4
but with slightly worse constants.

satisfies properties (1) and (2). This constitutes the majority of the argument and requires several preparatory results, starting with Proposition
3.1
and its Corollary
3.3
. With these in hand, we derive in Propositions
3.7
,
3.9

3.11
constraints on the local behavior of

on small intervals of the form
(x_{i},x_{i+1})
or
(x_{i-1},x_{i+1})

. Finally, establish in Proposition

that any

\mathrm{RidgelessReLU}(\mathcal{D})
. To start, we introduce some notation. For each
f\in\mathrm{PL(\mathcal{D})}
and every

, let us write

s_{\mathrm{in}}(x)=s_{\mathrm{in}}(f,x):=\lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0^{+}}Df(x-\epsilon),\qquad s_{\mathrm{out}}(x)=s_{\mathrm{out}}(f,x):=\lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0^{+}}Df(x+\epsilon)

for the incoming and outgoing slopes of
f
at
x
. For any
f\in\mathrm{PL}
the second derivative
D^{2}f
is an atomic measure and we have

D^{2}f=\sum_{j=1}^{k}c_{j}\delta_{\xi_{j}},\qquad c_{j}=s_{\mathrm{out}}(f,\xi_{j})-s_{\mathrm{in}}(f,\xi_{j})

where
\xi_{j}
are the points of discontinuity for the derivative
Df
. We will usually supress
f
from the notation. Thus,
Df
, and in particular
Dz
for any one layer ReLU network
z
, has a well-defined total variation

\left|\left|Df\right|\right|_{TV}:=\sum_{j=1}^{k}\left|c_{j}\right|.

Much of the remainder of our proof results on the following fundamental observation.

Proposition 3.1
.

Fix
f\in\mathrm{RidgelessReLU}(\mathcal{D})
. For every
i=1,\ldots,m-1
and
Df
is monotone on
(x_{i},x_{i+1})
in the sense that the functions
s_{\mathrm{in}}(f,x)
and
s_{\mathrm{out}}(f,x)
are both either non-increasing or non-decreasing for
x\in(x_{i},x_{i+1})
.

Proof.

We proceed by contradiction. That is, let us suppose that
f\in\mathrm{RidgelessReLU}(\mathcal{D})
and that for some
i
there exist

x_{i}\leq\xi_{1}<\xi_{2}<\xi_{3}<\xi_{4}\leq x_{i+1}

such that
f
is given by distinct affine functions with slopes
\sigma_{j}
when restricted to any of
(\xi_{j},\xi_{j+1})
for
j=1,2,3
but that the sequence
\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2},\sigma_{3}
is not monotone. Without loss of generality we assume

(3.1)

\sigma_{1},\sigma_{3}<\sigma_{2}.

Figure 4
.
The three possible relative configurations for
\sigma_{j},\sigma_{*}
are shown. On the left,
\sigma_{3}<\sigma_{*}\leq\sigma_{1}<\sigma_{2}
. In the center
\sigma_{1},\sigma_{3}<\sigma_{*}<\sigma_{2}
. On the right,
\sigma_{1}<\sigma_{*}<\sigma_{3}<\sigma_{2}
.

In particular, for all
\delta
sufficiently small, we have

(3.2)

\text{Total Variation of }Df\text{ on }(\xi_{1}-\delta,\xi_{4}+\delta)=2\sigma_{2}-\sigma_{1}-\sigma_{3}+\left|\sigma_{1}-\sigma_{\mathrm{in}}\right|+\left|\sigma_{3}-\sigma_{\mathrm{out}}\right|,

where

\sigma_{\mathrm{in}}:=s_{\mathrm{in}}(f,\xi_{1})=\lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0^{+}}Df(\xi_{1}-\epsilon)

and

\sigma_{\mathrm{out}}:=s_{\mathrm{out}}(f,\xi_{4})=\lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0^{+}}Df(\xi_{4}+\epsilon).

Define

\sigma_{*}:=\frac{f(\xi_{4})-f(\xi_{1})}{\xi_{1}-\xi_{4}}=\frac{\sigma_{1}(\xi_{2}-\xi_{1})+\sigma_{2}(\xi_{3}-\xi_{2})+\sigma_{3}(\xi_{4}-\xi_{3})}{\xi_{4}-\xi_{1}}.

Note that the constraint (
3.1
) and the fact that
\sigma_{*}
is a convex combination of
\sigma_{j}
guarantees that

(3.3)

\min\left\{\sigma_{1},\sigma_{3}\right\}<\sigma_{*}<\sigma_{2}.

See Figure
4
for the three possible cases. Consider
g\in\mathrm{PL(\mathcal{D})}
defined as follows:

g(x)=\begin{cases}f(x),&\quad x\in(\xi_{1},\xi_{4})^{c}\\
\sigma_{*}(x-\xi_{1})+f(\xi_{1}),&\quad x\in(\xi_{1},\xi_{4})\end{cases}.

The function
g
represents a ”straightening of f” between
\xi_{1}
and
\xi_{4}
, and we will now show that the total variation of
Dg
on
(\xi_{1}-\delta,\xi_{4}+\delta)
is strictly smaller than that of
Df
on the same interval. Since the total variations of
Df
and
Dg
agree on

this will contradict the minimality of
\left|\left|Df\right|\right|_{TV}
over
\mathrm{PL(\mathcal{D})}
. Indeed, considering all possible cases for the relative sizes of
\sigma_{\mathrm{in}},\sigma_{\mathrm{out}}
and
\sigma_{*}
we find for all
\delta

\displaystyle\text{Total Variation of }Dg\text{ on }(\xi_{1}-\delta,\xi_{4}+\delta)=\max\left\{\left|2\sigma_{*}-\sigma_{\mathrm{in}}-\sigma_{\mathrm{out}}\right|,\left|\sigma_{\mathrm{in}}-\sigma_{\mathrm{out}}\right|\right\}.

Combining this with the expression (
3.2
) for the total variation of
Df
and the following elementary Lemma completes the proof.

Lemma 3.2
.

For any
\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2},\sigma_{3},\sigma_{*}
satisfying (
3.3
) we have

2\sigma_{2}-\sigma_{1}-\sigma_{3}+\left|\sigma_{1}-\sigma_{\mathrm{in}}\right|+\left|\sigma_{3}-\sigma_{\mathrm{out}}\right|>\max\left\{\left|2\sigma_{*}-\sigma_{\mathrm{in}}-\sigma_{\mathrm{out}}\right|,\left|\sigma_{\mathrm{in}}-\sigma_{\mathrm{out}}\right|\right\}.

Proof.

We consider all four cases for the maximum on the right hand side. We have

\displaystyle 2\sigma_{2}-\sigma_{1}-\sigma_{3}+\left|\sigma_{1}-\sigma_{\mathrm{in}}\right|+\left|\sigma_{3}-\sigma_{\mathrm{out}}\right|-(2\sigma_{*}-\sigma_{\mathrm{in}}-\sigma_{\mathrm{out}})

\displaystyle\quad=2(\sigma_{2}-\sigma_{*})+\left|\sigma_{1}-\sigma_{\mathrm{in}}\right|-(\sigma_{1}-\sigma_{\mathrm{in}})+\left|\sigma_{3}-\sigma_{\mathrm{out}}\right|-(\sigma_{3}-\sigma_{\mathrm{out}})

\displaystyle\quad>0,

as desired. Similarly,

\displaystyle 2\sigma_{2}-\sigma_{1}-\sigma_{3}+\left|\sigma_{1}-\sigma_{\mathrm{in}}\right|+\left|\sigma_{3}-\sigma_{\mathrm{out}}\right|-(\sigma_{\mathrm{in}}+\sigma_{\mathrm{out}}-2\sigma_{*})

\displaystyle\quad=2(\sigma_{2}+\sigma_{*}-\sigma_{1}-\sigma_{3})+\left|\sigma_{1}-\sigma_{\mathrm{in}}\right|-(\sigma_{\mathrm{in}}-\sigma_{1})+\left|\sigma_{3}-\sigma_{\mathrm{out}}\right|-(\sigma_{\mathrm{out}}-\sigma_{3})

\displaystyle\quad>0,

as desired. Further,

\displaystyle 2\sigma_{2}-\sigma_{1}-\sigma_{3}+\left|\sigma_{1}-\sigma_{\mathrm{in}}\right|+\left|\sigma_{3}-\sigma_{\mathrm{out}}\right|-(\sigma_{\mathrm{in}}-\sigma_{\mathrm{out}})

\displaystyle\quad=2(\sigma_{2}-\sigma_{1})+\left|\sigma_{1}-\sigma_{\mathrm{in}}\right|-(\sigma_{\mathrm{in}}-\sigma_{1})+\left|\sigma_{3}-\sigma_{\mathrm{out}}\right|-(\sigma_{3}-\sigma_{\mathrm{out}})

\displaystyle\quad>0,

as desired. Finally,

\displaystyle 2\sigma_{2}-\sigma_{1}-\sigma_{3}+\left|\sigma_{1}-\sigma_{\mathrm{in}}\right|+\left|\sigma_{3}-\sigma_{\mathrm{out}}\right|-(\sigma_{\mathrm{out}}-\sigma_{\mathrm{in}})

\displaystyle\quad=2(\sigma_{2}-\sigma_{3})+\left|\sigma_{1}-\sigma_{\mathrm{in}}\right|-(\sigma_{1}-\sigma_{\mathrm{in}})+\left|\sigma_{3}-\sigma_{\mathrm{out}}\right|-(\sigma_{\mathrm{out}}-\sigma_{3})

\displaystyle\quad>0,

completing the proof.

Proposition 3.4

(

on colinear neighbors)

Fix

Suppose

satisfy

\min\left\{a,b\right\}<c<\max\left\{a,b\right\}.

Then for any
d\in\mathbb{R}
we have

\left|d-c\right|<\left|a-b\right|+\left|b-d\right|

Proof.

Suppose first
a<c<b
. Then

\displaystyle\left|a-b\right|+\left|b-d\right|-\left|d-c\right|

\displaystyle=b-a+\left|b-d\right|-\left|d-c\right|

\displaystyle=c-a+\left|b-d\right|-(d-b)-\left|d-c\right|+(d-c)

\displaystyle>0,

as desired. Similarly, suppose
b<c<a
then

(3.6)

\displaystyle\left|a-b\right|+\left|b-d\right|-\left|d-c\right|

\displaystyle=a-b+\left|b-d\right|-\left|d-c\right|.

If
d\geq c
then
d>b
and the right hand side of (
3.6
) becomes

a-b+\left|b-d\right|-(d-c)=a-b+d-b-d+c=c-b+a-b>0.

Finally, if
d\leq c
then the right hand side of (
3.6
) becomes

a-b+\left|d-b\right|-(c-d)=a-c+\left|d-b\right|-(d-b)>0.

This completes the proof.

i=1,\ldots,\left|\mathcal{I}\right|-1
we have

\mathrm{sgn}\left(s_{i_{q+1}}-s_{i_{q}}\right)=\epsilon_{i_{q+1}}.

Hence, for each
q=1,\ldots,\left|\mathcal{I}\right|-1
we conclude

\left|Df(\xi_{i_{q}})-Df(\xi_{i_{q+1}})\right|\geq\left|s_{i_{q}}-s_{i_{q+1}}\right|.

Thus,

\left|\left|Df\right|\right|_{TV}\geq\sum_{q=1}^{\left|\mathcal{I}\right|-1}\left|s_{i_{q}}-s_{i_{q+1}}\right|=\left|\left|Df_{\mathcal{D}}\right|\right|_{TV},

as desired.
