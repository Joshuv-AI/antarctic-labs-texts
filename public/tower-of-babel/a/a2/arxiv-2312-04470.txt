GaitGuard: Towards Private Gait in Mixed Reality
Diana Romero, Ruchi Jagdish Patel, Athina Markopoulou, Salma Elmalaki

Abstract
Augmented/Mixed Reality (AR/MR) technologies usher in a new era of immersive, collective experiences, differentiating them from traditional mobile systems. As these technologies evolve, prioritizing privacy and security is critical. This paper focuses on gait privacy, where gait — the way a person walks — can reveal sensitive information such as age, ethnicity, or disorders. We present GaitGuard, a real-time system that protects gait privacy against video-based gait extraction attacks in MR environments. GaitGuard leverages a multi-threaded framework to efficiently process video frames, incorporating dedicated modules for stream capture, body detection and tracking, and privacy leak mitigation. We compare and combine multiple mitigation techniques, offering guidance to navigate the privacy-utility tradeoff. Through extensive experiments covering 248 248 settings across mitigation regions, types, and tunable parameters, we assess the impact of these techniques on privacy, video quality, and system performance. GaitGuard reduces the confidence of video-based gait extraction attacks by introducing a substantial distribution shift (Jensen-Shannon Divergence of 0.63 0.63 , indicating highly altered gait features) and a decrease in identification risks by up to 68 % 68\% , while maintaining 29 29 FPS and preserving video clarity. GaitGuard provides a practical real-time solution for privacy-preserving MR applications without affecting the MR user experience based on 20 subjective user surveys.

Background and Related Work

II-A

Gait Analysis Methods

Gait analysis
is the systematic study of human locomotion, encompassing both walking and running. It characterizes walking as a series of gait cycles, where each cycle (also known as a stride) is defined as the period between successive ground contacts of the same foot. Within this cycle, several critical
gait events
occur, including
toe-off
and
heel-strike
, as illustrated in Figure
2
.

These gait events form the foundation for extracting
gait features
—quantitative metrics describing temporal and spatial properties of walking
[
26
]
. Commonly used features include:

•

Left and Right Step time
: Seconds between consecutive bilateral heel-strikes.

•

Left and Right Stance time
: Seconds from heel-strike to toe-off on the same leg.

•

Left and Right Swing time
: Seconds from toe-off to heel-strike on the same leg.

•

Right to Left and Left to Right Double support time
: Seconds from heel-strike of one leg to toe-off of the opposite leg.

•

Left and Right Step length
: Meter distance between ankles at heel-strike.

Instrumented gait analysis (IGA) is the precise and accurate analysis of gait patterns and characteristics. IGA systems often employ motion capture systems, force plates, walkways, and treadmills
[
28
]
. While this is the golden standard for gait assessment in research practice, these IGA systems are costly and invasive. To address these limitations, researchers have explored alternative, lower-cost sensing modalities for gait analysis, including inertial measurement units (IMUs)
[
24
,
29
]
, gyroscopes
[
25
]
, and depth sensors like Microsoft Kinect
[
30
,
31
]
. More recently, video-based gait analysis using 2D pose estimation has emerged as a compelling solution due to its accessibility and accuracy
[
26
,
32
]
.

In video-based approaches, camera feeds are analyzed using pose estimation algorithms that detect and track the positions of body keypoints—such as joints of the head, arms, and legs—over time. Among these tools, OpenPose has become a widely used framework for real-time multi-person pose estimation from standard video
[
33
]
. Studies have shown that gait features derived from OpenPose can approximate those obtained from IGA systems with reasonable accuracy
[
26
]
.

In this work, we focus on video-based gait features extraction that uses a non-intrusive means of extracting gait features from MR-captured camera streams.

Gait Privacy

Gait is a well-established biometric identifier that has been widely studied for user authentication on mobile and wearable systems

Recognizing these risks, regulations such as the California Privacy Rights Act (CPRA)

In contrast, MR presents a fundamentally different privacy challenge. Gait data in MR is often captured passively through onboard cameras—not just from the primary user, but also from nearby bystanders who are unaware of being recorded. Despite the sensitive nature of this data and the ubiquity of head-mounted cameras in MR, no prior work has systematically addressed gait privacy in this context.

II-C

Privacy in MR

The increasing prevalence of MR headsets introduces significant privacy and security concerns. These devices, equipped with advanced sensors, capture extensive data about users and their surroundings, including video, audio, and motion information. This data collection poses risks not only to users but also to non-consenting bystanders and passersby. Privacy vulnerabilities in MR environments span various domains, from visual data risks to perceptual manipulation attacks. Past works have highlighted the vulnerability of visual data in MR settings

Furthermore, sensor-based side-channel attacks have emerged across immersive platforms. These include inferring keystrokes using infrared tracking

III
Threat Model

Collaborative MR applications introduce novel privacy challenges due to their networked and distributed architecture. Unlike traditional single-user or locally processed applications, these systems rely on real-time data streaming between users as well as remote servers, creating multiple points of potential data interception and analysis.
The privacy risks are exacerbated by (i) the reliance on camera access for core MR functionalities, such as avatar rendering and environmental interaction, and (ii) the interconnectedness, where data traverses multiple entities and devices. Our analysis of 318 HoloLens applications revealed a concerning prevalence of camera access requests (26%), underscoring the widespread exposure to gait feature extraction within the current landscape of deployed MR systems. The Collaborative MR Environment.
Consider a typical collaborative MR session, depicted in Figure
3
. A user wearing an MR HMD, such as the HoloLens, captures a stream of camera and sensor data to facilitate immersive interaction. These applications typically involve local processing on the MR device, followed by raw or processed data transmitted over a network to the network manager. This network manager acts as a central hub, synchronizing and redistributing telemetry data, including potentially rendered video feeds or abstracted information, to other participants in the distributed session.

The networked architecture opens avenues for eavesdroppers and/or ‘‘honest-but-curious’’ entities,

Attacker 1: Operator of the Central Synchronization/Relay Service:

Many collaborative MR applications utilize a central server or cloud-based service, such as Photon Network Manager
[

An operator of this service, whether the application developer or a third-party provider, has privileged access to data as it passes through their infrastructure. This central point of data aggregation in a common MR architecture presents an opportunity for an honest-but-curious operator to observe and potentially log and extract gait-related information from multiple users’ data streams.

Attacker 2: Remote Collaborating User:

Participants in a collaborative MR session typically exchange data directly or indirectly. A remote user receives updates about other users, which often include avatar representations driven by their movements and often shared camera streams of the co-located HMD users. A curious remote user can passively record and analyze these received data streams to infer the gait patterns of other participants. The inherent data sharing mechanisms in collaborative MR applications, necessary to enable shared experiences, also expose gait information to remote users.

Attacker Capabilities and Goal.
Whatever the role of the attacker in the MR architecture, the only thing we assume that the attacker has
access
to is camera frames captured by the MR device during collaborative sessions; those camera frames contain individuals walking. As discussed above, this access to the camera frames is possible at various vantage points/entities, including: (i) the backend infrastructure,
e.g.,
central server or cloud-based service, or (ii) the rendered stream to a remote user (i.e., a user using a laptop, not an MR headset). Although some Mixed Reality (MR) systems may incorporate other sensor modalities such as IMU data, eye tracking, audio, or depth maps, we do not assume access to these. Instead, the attacker’s
goal
is to infer the gait of individual(s) captured in the RGB camera stream—either users in the MR application or bystanders.

Attack Characteristics.

First, the above threat is realistic and easy to launch, specifically enabled by the networked nature of collaborative MR.
Unlike attacks requiring physical proximity or device compromise, our threat model assumes no malicious software installation and leverages only legitimate access to MR session camera frame data facilitated by the network. Second, the attack is stealthy, as users have limited or no ability to monitor how their movement data is processed and transmitted across the networked system. Third, although large-scale, easy-to-launch gait privacy attacks in MR might not currently be a dominant threat, it has significant potential and is a growing concern. Proposed Defense.
To address the unique challenges of gait privacy in networked collaborative MR environments, we propose
GaitGuard
, a companion application on a trusted and paired mobile device. It intercepts video frames and applies lightweight privacy-preserving transformations to camera frames, in real-time. Furthermore, it operates on video frames where they are generated, thus mitigating risks from several types of attackers (central server and remote user). By balancing privacy protection with real-time performance requirements,
GaitGuard
aims to safeguard gait information while maintaining core MR functionality.
To the best of our knowledge, there is no prior work that provides real-time defense for camera streams, specifically for gait, and considering so many dimensions in the mitigation space (

IV-A

Design Considerations and Constraints

Building
GaitGuard
for real-world MR systems presents several unique challenges. First, video transformations must preserve essential visual content required for MR functionalities such as spatial mapping, QR code detection, and avatar rendering. Excessive distortion can degrade user experience and impair collaboration, making it crucial to maintain application utility. Second, commercial MR headsets, such as the HoloLens 2, have limited computational resources and operate within tightly restricted platforms. These systems restrict low-level access to camera streams, making on-device interception and transformation infeasible. These hardware and software limitations necessitate innovative approaches to implement effective privacy-preserving measures in MR environments.

IV-B

Architecture and Deployment Strategy

Although direct, on-headset transformation of gait-related features would provide the most seamless protection, current commercial platforms do not support such access. For instance, the HoloLens 2 runs Windows Holographic OS—a closed platform that prevents developers from intercepting or modifying raw camera streams
[
52
]
. Similar limitations have been encountered in prior work on MR privacy, such as BystandAR
[
9
]
, which also relied on third-party application processing to enforce privacy protections.

Moreover, the computational cost of video-based gait analysis renders it impractical for real-time execution on MR headsets. For example, OpenPose—a widely used pose estimation framework—achieves only 11 frames per second (fps) on a high-end Nvidia V100 GPU, and less than 1 fps on a standard CPU
[
53
]
. These rates fall far short of the 30 fps required to support smooth, real-time MR capture
[
54
]
.

To address these challenges,
GaitGuard
is architected to offload privacy processing to a companion mobile device. Camera frames from the MR headset are streamed in real time to the mobile device, where gait detection and mitigation are applied. The transformed frames are then returned to the MR application pipeline. This design enables low-latency operation while avoiding the limitations of headset compute power and OS restrictions. It also offers broad compatibility with existing MR systems, allowing deployment without modification to the headset firmware or applications.

IV-C

Assessing Gait Leakage from Egocentric MR Video

Before developing mitigation techniques, we first evaluated the extent to which gait information is exposed through egocentric video captured by MR headsets. To this end, we built
GaitExtract
, a lightweight gait analysis tool adapted from the clinically validated approach by Stenum et al.
[
26
]
, leveraging 2D keypoints extracted via OpenPose.

As illustrated in Figure
4
,
GaitExtract
is specifically tailored to the challenges posed by head-mounted MR footage. Unlike fixed-camera settings, egocentric video introduces dynamic perspectives, variable body poses, and partial occlusions—all of which complicate gait analysis. To address these issues,
GaitExtract
incorporates several MR-specific adaptations:

•

Estimating walking direction from moving, egocentric viewpoints.

•

Correcting leg-side misclassifications due to noisy or occluded keypoints.

•

Detecting gait events (e.g., heel-strike, toe-off) in unconstrained, real-world sequences.

•

Filtering incomplete or corrupted gait cycles from brief or interrupted video segments.

•

Aggregating gait metrics across valid walking intervals.

GaitExtract
supports two analysis modes: fully automated and partially automated. The fully automated mode extracts time-based gait features, excluding spatial metrics like step length. In contrast, the partially automated mode includes step length estimation but requires minimal manual calibration input. These two modes offer flexibility in gait analysis, allowing for comprehensive feature extraction with varying levels of user intervention.

For each walking sequence, where refers to a video instance of a participant walking in one direction, the tool outputs a ten-dimensional feature vector per leg, including step time, swing time, stance time, and double support time. These extracted features from
GaitExtract
are used as ground truth for evaluating the effectiveness of our gait mitigation strategies as they rely on clinically validated approach
[
26
]
.

Mitigation Design Aspects

We developed a set of mitigation strategies to obscure gait-identifying features in egocentric MR video. These strategies span four primary design aspects: ❶
location
, ❷

, ❸

❶
Location — Where to Apply Mitigation.

We explore spatial targeting of mitigation based on two approaches: joint-based masking and region-based masking.

Keypoint-Based Masking (KPM).

Inspired by prior work identifying specific anatomical landmarks as critical for gait recognition
[
26
]
, we evaluate several configurations of keypoint masking:

•

KPM (baseline):
Masks regions surrounding three core gait-related keypoints—midhip, left ankle, and right ankle—to directly disrupt detection of heel-strike and toe-off events.

•

Head and shoulders KPM:
Expands the masking to include head and shoulder joints, exploring whether concealing upper-body movement enhances privacy while preserving leg visibility.

•

Lower body KPM:
Focuses exclusively on lower body joints, including hips, knees, heels, and toes, which are most indicative of gait patterns.

•

All-except-head KPM:
Masks all body keypoints except the head, offering maximal gait-related obfuscation while retaining facial context for MR interaction tasks.

Lower Body-Based Masking (LBM).

In clinical gait assessments, lower-body motion is often the primary focus
[
55
]
. Building on this principle, we implement a region-based strategy that masks the entire lower body using a bounding box. This method is conceptually similar to lower body KPM, but avoids per-keypoint computation by directly covering the anatomical region—reducing processing overhead while maintaining coverage of gait-critical areas.

❷
Size — Area of Keypoint Masking.

To balance privacy protection with visual utility, we varied the masked regions’ size around each keypoint. As shown in Figure
5
, increasing mask size provides greater concealment of gait-relevant body parts, but also introduces more visual distortion in the frame.

For the baseline KPM approach, we evaluated box sizes of
50
,
100
,
150
, and
200
pixels to ensure adequate coverage of spatially dispersed keypoints. For the other KPM variants (e.g., head and shoulders, lower body, all-except-head), we tested box sizes of
50
,
100
, and
150
pixels. The larger masks in the baseline were necessary to minimize gaps between keypoints and ensure complete obfuscation of gait events.

In contrast, the Lower Body-Based Masking (LBM) approach uses a fixed-size bounding box that encompasses the entire lower body region. Since it does not rely on individual keypoints, it does not support variable mask sizing—prioritizing simplicity and computational efficiency over fine-grained control.

This exploration of mask size highlights the trade-off between visual fidelity and privacy strength—informing configurations based on application context and sensitivity of the data being captured.

Type — How to Obscure Gait Features.

Numerous methods have been proposed to reduce visual privacy risks in camera-captured data, including adversarial perturbations
[
56
]
, GAN-based anonymization
[
57
]
, imperceptible privacy filters
[
58
]

[

[

. Therefore, in this work, we deliberately evaluate lightweight mitigation techniques that are feasible under real-time constraints and evaluate their capability in protecting gait identifying information. Figure

illustrates the mitigation types explored and are explained as follows:

Black Box.

Inspired by the BystandAR system
[
9
]
, this approach places a solid black rectangle over gait-relevant regions. It serves as a baseline technique due to its simplicity and guaranteed concealment, though it introduces high visual disruption.

Differentially Private Pixelization (DP Pix).

Adapted from Fan et al.
[
63
]
, this method ensures differential privacy by pixelizing regions and adding noise to the resulting blocks. Originally designed for grayscale images, it was extended here to RGB by applying noise to each color channel separately. The process involves:

(1)

Determining
m
, the number of pixels in the target region (based on keypoints or the lower body).

(2)

Applying pixelization using a kernel of size
b\times b
.

(3)

Adding Laplace noise to each pixelized cell, with scale parameter
\lambda=\frac{3\cdot 255\cdot m}{b^{2}\cdot\epsilon}
.

For KPM,
m=\texttt{keypoint\_count}\cdot\texttt{box\_length}^{2}
, using up to 25 OpenPose keypoints. For LBM,
m
corresponds to the full lower body area. We tested kernel sizes
b\in\{10,20,30,40,50\}
with
\epsilon=0.1
.

Differentially Private Laplacian Noise (DP Laplace).

Also derived from Fan et al., this method applies Laplace noise directly to individual pixels without pixelization as follows:

(1)

Selecting
m
target pixels within
k
critical regions.

(2)

Calculating global sensitivity
\Delta f
based on
k
and
m
.

(3)

Adding Laplace noise to each of the
m
pixels, scaled by
\lambda=\frac{\Delta f}{\epsilon}
.

We tested values of
\epsilon\in\{0.1,10,100,1000\}
. For LBM,
k=1
and
m
represents all pixels within the lower body region.

Gaussian Blur.

Blurring is widely used for obfuscating sensitive information in videos, such as YouTube’s face-blurring feature
[
64
]
. We applied Gaussian blur with a kernel size of
c\times c
and standard deviation
\sigma=0
, evaluating
c\in\{5,25,45,65\}
to measure its impact on gait feature suppression and visual utility.

Random Noise.

We applied random noise within the masked regions defined by KPM or LBM using four distributions: uniform, normal, Laplace, and exponential. Each variant used a Hanning window to produce smooth edges. The noise intensity was controlled by a distribution parameter
\lambda\in\{50,100,150,200\}
as follows: (1) Uniform: Range
[-\lambda,\lambda]
, (2) Normal: Standard deviation
\lambda
, (3) Laplace: Scale parameter
\lambda
, and (4) Exponential: Rate parameter
\lambda
.

Average Color.

This method replaces each masked region with its average RGB color, preserving general scene context while reducing identifiable detail. It offers a visually minimal and computationally cheap way to degrade gait features.

❹
Adaptability — When to Apply Mitigation.

In addition to deciding where and how to apply mitigation, we explore
when
to apply it—based on the temporal structure of the gait cycle. We compare two strategies: static mitigation, which treats all frames equally, and dynamic mitigation, which focuses on frames most relevant to gait analysis.

Static Mitigation.

This approach applies mitigation uniformly across all video frames. By obfuscating every frame, it ensures comprehensive coverage and consistent privacy protection. Static mitigation is simple to implement and guarantees that no frame—regardless of gait relevance—is left exposed.

Dynamic Mitigation.

In contrast, dynamic mitigation selectively targets frames that are most informative for gait identification, such as those surrounding key gait events (e.g., heel-strike and toe-off). By concentrating resources on these high-risk frames, dynamic mitigation seeks to enhance privacy while minimizing computational load and visual degradation.

To automatically identify gait-relevant frames, we adapt a Spatial ConvNet model inspired by Simonyan et al.’s architecture for human activity recognition
[
65
]
. Our implementation uses five convolutional layers, two fully connected layers, and a softmax output to classify frames as either
gait events
or
non-gait events
, based on RGB crops of the lower body.

Gait event labels include frames immediately before, during, and after heel-strike or toe-off. The model is trained using stratified sampling across subjects to ensure balanced representation, and employs
Focal Loss
to mitigate class imbalance and focus learning on harder-to-classify samples. We evaluate performance across model variants by progressively removing 1 to 5 convolutional layers, assessing the trade-off between detection recall and runtime efficiency.

By comparing static and dynamic strategies, we aim to quantify their respective strengths in protecting gait privacy without compromising real-time system performance.

Mitigation Combinatorics.

Across all mitigation aspects—including spatial location (❶), mask size (❷), type of transformation (❸), and adaptability (❹)—we evaluate a total of
248 unique combinations
of mitigation techniques. A full list of these combinations is available in Appendix Table

. This comprehensive design space allows us to analyze the privacy-utility trade-offs and identify optimal strategies for deployment in MR environments.

V
GaitGuard
System Implementation

This section details the implementation of
GaitGuard
within a collaborative MR application, as illustrated in Figure
3
. The
GaitGuard
system is deployed as a companion application written in Python, running alongside an MR application on the HoloLens 2. The system consists of a multi-threaded, pipelined architecture, with each processing stage handled by a dedicated thread to ensure high throughput and real-time performance.

Collaborative Application.

We developed a prototype collaborative MR application for the HoloLens 2 to evaluate
GaitGuard
’s integration, privacy capabilities, and system overhead. The application was built using Unity 2021.3.25f1 and MRTK3 (Mixed Reality Toolkit for Unity). Multi-user support was enabled via Photon Unity Networking (PUN) 2, version 2.43. To stream camera frames from the HoloLens 2 to the
GaitGuard
server, we used the HL2SS (HoloLens 2 Sensor Streaming) framework
[
66
]
.

Pipelined Thread Architecture.

The
GaitGuard
system is designed around a pipelined processing architecture with four major modules, running in separate threads:
Thread A
which handles stream captures,
Thread B
which handles body detection and mitigation, and
Thread C
which handles encoding and transmission. These threads communicate via shared queues, allowing each stage to operate independently and asynchronously for real-time performance.

Stream Capture Module (Thread A).

This module receives raw video frames from the MR headset. It uses HL2SS APIs to establish a connection and configure the stream parameters, including resolution, frame rate, and codec. Once connected, it continuously captures frames and adds them to the
original_frames_queue
. Operating in its own thread, this module is isolated from processing delays in downstream modules, enabling uninterrupted frame acquisition from the headset.

Body Detection & Tracking and Mitigation Module (Thread B).

This thread combines two modules which processes incoming frames to detect human figures and apply privacy-preserving transformations. Human detection is performed using OpenCV’s Histogram of Oriented Gradients (HOG) descriptor with a pre-trained SVM classifier
[
67
]
. To reduce processing time, frames are resized to 320x240 and converted to grayscale.

For mitigation, the system applies the strategies described in Section
IV-D
. In the KPM approach, OpenPose is used to identify keypoints such as hips, ankles, and knees, and targeted mitigation is applied to those regions. In LBM, the lower half of the detected bounding boxes is treated as the region of interest and masked accordingly. Mitigation is applied statically or dynamically as explained in Section
IV-D
.

Processed frames, with selected mitigation applied, are then reassembled and placed in the
mitigated_frames_queue
for encoding.

Encoding & Transmission Module (Thread C).

This module retrieves mitigated frames from the queue, compresses them, and transmits them back to the MR system. To reduce bandwidth, frames are resized to 30% of their original resolution and encoded using JPEG with a quality setting of 10. This compression balances visual fidelity and transfer efficiency.

Transmission is handled via a UDP socket configured for high-throughput streaming. Running in a dedicated thread, this module ensures that the encoding and network operations do not interfere with upstream frame capture or mitigation, sustaining the system’s real-time capabilities.

By structuring
GaitGuard
as a pipelined, multi-threaded system, we maintain a steady frame rate and minimize latency across the capture, analysis, mitigation, and transmission stages. This architecture enables deployment in real-time MR applications. Critically, the mitigation process occurs on background-processed frames within the network manager, without affecting the user’s camera view (egocentric view) or virtual objects.

Evaluation Methodology

We implemented
GaitGuard

Data Collection.

We conducted an IRB-approved user study with
20

Participants walked between two markers (2.5m–2.75m apart) for approximately 5 minutes while being recorded using the HoloLens 2 camera (Figure

). This setup reflects natural MR collaboration settings that involve limited user movement. Our participant count aligns with prior work in AR bystander privacy research (e.g., 16 participants in BystandAR
[
9
]

Evaluation Metrics for Mitigation Effectiveness.

To measure the impact of each mitigation strategy on gait privacy, we applied all 248 configurations described in Section
IV-D
. We used the fully automated version of
GaitExtract
to extract gait features from both unmodified and mitigated video frames. This simulates a realistic adversarial scenario, where the attacker has limited resources and no manual calibration access.

Privacy Metrics.
Let
G
represent the gait feature vectors from original frames and
G^{\prime}
those from mitigated frames. We evaluated privacy protection using the following metrics:

•

Jensen-Shannon Divergence (JSD):
Measures the statistical difference between the distributions of
G
and
G^{\prime}
, by quantifying it as a value between 0 and 1. A higher JSD indicates greater distortion of gait features.

•

Mann-Whitney U Test:
A non-parametric test to determine whether
G
and
G^{\prime}
come from the same distribution. A p-value
<0.05
indicates significant differences, suggesting effective mitigation.

•

User Identification Accuracy:
Assesses how well individuals can be re-identified from gait features in
G^{\prime}
compared to
G
. Lower accuracy after mitigation indicates improved privacy.

Utility Metrics.

To assess visual quality and frame integrity post-mitigation, we used:

•

Peak Signal-to-Noise Ratio (PSNR):
Evaluates the level of distortion introduced across RGB channels.

•

Structural Similarity Index (SSIM)
[
68
]
:
Quantifies the perceived quality difference by comparing luminance, contrast, and structure between original and mitigated frames.

Cross-Validation with Multiple Gait-Analysis Methods.

To assess the robustness of the most effective mitigation technique, we evaluated it against two gait-analysis pipelines: (1) our adapted
GaitExtract
that relies on OpenPose, and (2) GaitLab
[
69
]
, a deep learning-based gait analysis framework. While
GaitExtract
uses numerical estimation of gait events, GaitLab extracts features using a neural network. Evaluating both provided complementary insights into how resilient our mitigation is against traditional and ML-based gait recognition.

GaitGuard

We evaluated
GaitGuard
’s runtime performance in a live collaborative setting. On the HoloLens 2, optimal application experience requires an app frame rate of 60 fps
[
70
]
, while Mixed Reality Capture (MRC) typically achieves 30 fps for camera streaming
[
54
]
. We measured how mitigation processing affects both application and streaming frame rates, under varying temporal configurations (e.g., static vs. dynamic modes).

VI-C

Summary of Privacy-Utility Trade-offs

We evaluated the privacy-utility trade-offs (PUT) of all 248 mitigation configurations by comparing user identification accuracy (privacy) against two standard utility metrics: PSNR and SSIM. The results, visualized in Table
IV
, reveal several key findings across mitigation types, locations, and parameter choices.

The best overall performance was achieved by average color mitigation at the lower body KPM location, yielding highest PSNR and SSIM while maintaining maximum observed privacy (68% reduction in identification accuracy). A close second was the same mitigation type at LBM location, with equivalent privacy but slightly lower utility (1.8 dB lower PSNR, 0.003 decrease in SSIM).
Average color consistently offered the best privacy-utility trade-off across all mitigation types. In contrast, the black box method, while equally effective in reducing identification accuracy, produced significantly worse utility results.

Mitigation applied only to the three gait-related keypoints in the baseline KPM proved insufficient. OpenPose compensates for missing keypoints by inferring positions from surrounding body parts, reducing privacy impact. The improved performance of the head & shoulders KPM configuration, which mitigates additional non-gait keypoints, further supports this, as it disrupts OpenPose’s estimation process more effectively.
Applying DP pixelization and average color to either LBM or lower body KPM location with a 150-pixel box size resulted in nearly identical privacy and utility outcomes. This suggests that with sufficiently large masked regions, keypoint-based mitigation can approximate the coverage and effectiveness of full lower-body masking.

Finally, dynamic mitigation strategies were found to provide privacy metrics comparable to their static counterparts while significantly improving utility. By focusing mitigation on gait-critical frames (e.g., during heel-strike and toe-off), dynamic methods reduce unnecessary visual distortion, making them ideal for deployment in real-time MR systems.

GaitGuard

These findings suggest that user perceptions of gait privacy are shaped both by technological interventions and awareness of the risks. While
GaitGuard

VII
Discussion and Practical Recommendations

Drawing from our extensive evaluation of
GaitGuard
—including 248 mitigation configurations, real-time performance assessments, and user perception studies—we distill several actionable insights for developers, privacy researchers, and MR system designers:

•

Prioritize Lower Body Mitigation.

Gait privacy is best protected by masking lower body regions rather than isolated keypoints. Techniques like LBM and lower body KPM significantly outperform minimal keypoint-based approaches, which can be bypassed by pose estimation systems using part affinity reconstruction.

•

Use Average Color for Strong Privacy-Utility Balance.

Among all evaluated methods, average color masking provided the most favorable trade-off—delivering high privacy gains without substantial image degradation. Unlike techniques that rely on black boxes or heavy noise, it offers natural obfuscation while preserving video usability.

•

Choose 150-Pixel Masks for Optimal Trade-offs.

A mask size of 150 pixels offers the strongest balance between privacy enhancement and visual clarity. Smaller sizes are ineffective for gait obfuscation, while larger sizes diminish utility with limited additional privacy benefit.

•

Adopt Dynamic Mitigation to Maximize Utility.

Temporal awareness enables better outcomes. Dynamic mitigation targeting gait-critical frames improves image quality (PSNR/SSIM) without sacrificing privacy. For real-time MR applications, this adaptive strategy is especially valuable.

•

Avoid Overengineered Detection Models.

Our experiments show that one convolutional layer is sufficient for gait event detection. Deeper models offer no meaningful gains in privacy and significantly reduce streaming performance. Lightweight, task-specific models suffice.

•

Communicate Risks to Build User Trust.

Participants expressed greater comfort and trust after being informed about gait privacy risks and mitigation techniques. This highlights the importance of transparency, education, and user agency in privacy-preserving MR system design.

•

Validate Across Analytical Pipelines.

Mitigation strategies should be evaluated against both traditional and ML-based gait analysis methods. We find that even simple mitigation can meaningfully disrupt both numerical and deep learning pipelines, though performance varies by method.

•

Additional Utility Metrics.
Although we considered general metrics of utility for image quality, including SSIM and PSNR, other application-based utility measures could be considered and added to
GaitGuard
accordingly.

•

Practical Applicability.
Ideally, implementing gait privacy mitigation directly on the MR device - as an OS service - would minimize latency and enhance control. However, current commercial MR headsets, such as the HoloLens 2, are often constrained by limited computational resources and operate within tightly restricted platforms. These systems typically restrict low-level access to camera streams, making on-device interception and transformation challenging or infeasible. It is conceivable that future iterations of MR hardware and software will provide greater flexibility and processing power, potentially enabling more robust on-device privacy solutions.

Key Takeaway:
Simple approaches work.

GaitGuard
demonstrates that practical, real-time gait privacy is achievable using lightweight, interpretable, and non-adversarial methods. By avoiding resource-intensive solutions such as GANs or adversarial perturbations,
GaitGuard
strikes an effective balance between usability, deployability, and privacy protection—making it an ideal candidate for MR platforms deployed in everyday environments.

VIII
Conclusion

We presented
GaitGuard
, a real-time system for mitigating gait privacy risks in MR environments. Designed for compatibility with commercial MR platforms,
GaitGuard
applies targeted mitigation strategies—most effectively, average color masking to lower-body regions—to disrupt video-based gait extraction while maintaining high visual utility.

Across 248 tested configurations,
GaitGuard
consistently achieved strong privacy outcomes, reducing identification accuracy by up to 68% with minimal performance overhead. Dynamic mitigation further improved utility by applying protection selectively at gait-critical moments. Importantly, our findings show that simple, interpretable methods—rather than complex adversarial or generative techniques—are sufficient for achieving robust gait privacy in practice.

GaitGuard
runs in real-time, integrates easily with collaborative MR workflows, and is deployable on commodity hardware. While our evaluation was conducted in controlled indoor settings, future work may explore robustness across more diverse environments and extended to other biometrics.

They could take breaks or ask questions during the experiment. All biometric data were anonymized, securely stored, and used solely for research.
