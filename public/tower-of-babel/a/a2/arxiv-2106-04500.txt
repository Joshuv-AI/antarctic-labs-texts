Spectral Measures for Derivative Powers via Matrix-Valued Clark Theory
Michael Bush, Constanze Liaw, Robert T.W. Martin

Abstract
The theory of finite-rank perturbations allows for the determination of spectral information for broad classes of operators using the tools of analytic function theory. In this work, finite-rank perturbations are applied to powers of the derivative operator, providing a full account from self-adjoint boundary conditions to computing aspects of the operators’ matrix-valued spectral measures. In particular, the support and weights of the Clark (spectral) measures are computed via the connection between matrix-valued contractive analytic functions and matrix-valued nonnegative measures through the Herglotz Representation Theorem. For operators associated with several powers of the derivative, explicit formulae for these measures are included. While eigenfunctions and eigenvalues for these operators with fixed boundary conditions can often be computed using direct methods from ordinary differential equations, this approach provides a more complete picture of the spectral information.

Introduction

Interest in rank-one perturbations was initiated by Weyl
[
18
]
, who implemented them to determine spectral properties of Sturm–Liouville operators subject to changing a boundary condition. Consequently, Aronszajn–Donoghue (see e.g.
[
15
]
) and Alexandrov–Clark (see e.g.
[
7
]
) theory were established; their investigations of the boundary values of the Cauchy transform ultimately resulted in a good understanding of these operators’ spectral properties.

Higher rank perturbations arise from changing several boundary conditions simultaneously. This naturally leads to a matrix-valued version of Aleksandrov–Clark theory, which was developed by Gesztesy–Tsekanovskii in
[
9
]
, Kapustin–Poltoratski
[
10
]
and Liaw–Martin–Treil
[
12
]
, among many others. The absolutely continuous parts of matrix-valued Aleksandrov–Clark measures were successfully studied as early as Kuroda’s work in the context of scattering theory
[
11
]
.
In a series of works (see e.g.
[
2
]
), Albeverio–Kurasov carried out applications of finite-rank perturbations to certain self-adjoint extensions of symmetric operators with finite deficiency indices. Their main focus was the scattering theory of singular form bounded—and even more singular—finite-rank perturbations. They were not interested in explicit connections with boundary conditions.

In Aleman–Martin–Ross
[
3
]
, changing several boundary conditions in a

Sturm–Liouville operator was brought in connection with a de Branges–Rovnyak model space from Aleksandrov–Clark theory. This was accomplished through presenting a tractable formula for the Livšic characteristic function (which generates the model space) of a simple symmetric operator. While the spectral information was encoded within, they were mainly interested in developing a general representation theory.

Here, we supplement the approach of Aleman–Martin–Ross
[
3
]
using results from Liaw–Martin–Treil
[
12
]
and the generalizations of Glazman–Krein–Naimark’s classical self-adjoint extension theory by Sun et al.
[
16
]
and Wang et al.
[
17
]
. We make concrete the connection between the Lagrange bracket (a sesquilinear form intrinsic to the nature of the operator) and the perturbation parameter; note that proofs from rank-one and rank-two cases do not immediately generalize to the higher rank setting. This allows us to paint a complete picture from boundary conditions to properties at the level of matrix-valued spectral information of symmetric differential expressions of any order with equal deficiency indices that satisfy what we call the patchwork property. As examples, we explicitly carry out this approach for some powers of derivative operators, obtaining full matrix-valued spectral information. Included are operators defined by considering expressions of the form
(-1)^{n}\frac{d^{2n}}{dx^{2n}}
with
n\in\mathbb{N}
on
L^{2}(0,\infty)
acting on particular domains, for which the endpoint at infinity is non-regular. While the operators (i.e. expressions paired with domains) considered in this work are rather rudimentary, the main contribution consists of an explicit proof of concept: this framework can be applied to bring boundary conditions in direct relation with matrix-valued spectral properties.

A related way to investigate spectral questions under changing boundary conditions is via boundary triplets
[
4
]
, a rather general framework, which is in principle very powerful. For example, it is connected with Dirichlet-to-Neumann maps from partial differential equations. Apart from Bush–Frymark–Liaw
[
6
]
, only scalar (i.e. not matrix-valued) spectral information was obtained and the connection to boundary conditions has not been worked out very explicitly. Nonetheless, these works suggest that we should expect the class of operators for which our current approach is feasible to be much larger.

2.4
Self-Adjoint Extensions for Rank-One Perturbations

We now present the classical framework used to describe self-adjoint extensions for operators with deficiency indices
(1,1)
(i.e. rank-one perturbations). First, we describe the extensions in terms of rank-one perturbations, then in terms of boundary conditions, and end by connecting the two descriptions. These results will be generalized to rank-two perturbations and finite-rank perturbations in Sections
2.5
and
4
, respectively.

First, we examine
K_{1}=-\frac{d^{2}}{dx^{2}}
. As previously mentioned,
K_{1}
is defined on a suitable dense domain and is a symmetric, closed, with defect indices
(1,1)
. So all self-adjoint extensions of
K_{1,\text{min}}
are indexed by a complex parameter
\alpha\in\mathscr{U}_{1}
.
The defect spaces are one-dimensional:

\mathcal{D}_{+}=\bigvee\phi^{+},\quad\phi^{+}(x)=\sqrt[4]{2}e^{\sqrt{-i}x},

and

\mathcal{D}_{-}=\bigvee\phi^{-},\quad\phi^{-}(x)=\sqrt[4]{2}e^{-\sqrt{i}x}.

(Here we have normalized the defect vectors.) It follows that all unitary extensions of
V=\gamma(K_{1})
are given by:

U_{\alpha}:=V+\alpha\left\langle{\phi^{+}},{\cdot}\right\rangle\phi^{-},\quad\quad\alpha\in\mathscr{U}_{1},

and all self-adjoint
extensions of
K_{1}
are given by
K_{1,\alpha}=\gamma^{-1}(U_{\alpha})
, where

\mathrm{Dom}({K_{1,\alpha}})=\mathrm{Dom}({K_{1,\text{min}}})+\bigvee(\alpha\phi^{-}-\phi^{+}).

(2.8)

On the other hand, all self-adjoint extensions of
K_{1,\text{min}}
can be obtained by imposing boundary conditions on the domain of
K_{1,\text{max}}
for
b,c\in\mathscr{U}_{1}
:

\mathrm{Dom}({K_{1}(b,c)}):=\{f\in\mathrm{Dom}({K^{*}_{1}})|\ bf(0)+cf^{\prime}(0)=0,\ \mbox{and}\ b\overline{c}\in\mathbb{R}\}.

Proposition 1
.

Each operator
K_{1}(b,c)
, for
b,c\in\mathscr{U}_{1}
is self-adjoint.

Proof.

Recall that every element
f\in\mathrm{Dom}({K_{1,\text{min}}})
is absolutely continuous and obeys
f(0)=0
. Hence
K_{1}(b,c)
is a symmetric extension of
K_{1,\text{min}}
. On the other hand it follows that
K_{1}(b,c)
is a symmetric restriction of
K_{1,\text{max}}
. Also, since
K_{1}(b,c)
is a symmetric operator by the previous claim, we have the following containment of domains,

\mathrm{Dom}({K_{1,\text{min}}})\subset\mathrm{Dom}({K_{1}(b,c)})\subset\mathrm{Dom}({K_{1}(b,c)^{*}})\subset\mathrm{Dom}({K_{1,\text{max}}}).

Now suppose that
g\in\mathrm{Dom}({K_{1}(b,c)^{*}})
. Then for any
f\in\mathrm{Dom}({K_{1}(b,c)})
, calculate that

\displaystyle\left\langle{K_{1}(b,c)f},{g}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}-f^{\prime\prime}(t)\overline{g}(t)dt

\displaystyle=-f^{\prime}(t)\overline{g}(t)|_{t=0}^{\infty}+\int_{0}^{\infty}f^{\prime}(t)\overline{g}^{\prime}(t)dt

\displaystyle=\left.\left(-f^{\prime}(t)\overline{g}(t)+f(t)\overline{g}^{\prime}(t)\right)\right|_{t=0}^{\infty}+\int_{0}^{\infty}f(t)\left(-\overline{g}^{\prime\prime}(t)\right)dt

\displaystyle=f^{\prime}(0)\overline{g}(0)-f(0)\overline{g}^{\prime}(0)+\left\langle{f},{K_{1}(b,c)^{*}g}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=-f(0)\left(\overline{c}b\overline{g}(0)+\overline{g}^{\prime}(0)\right)+\left\langle{f},{K_{1}(b,c)^{*}g}\right\rangle.

From the definition of the adjoint,
this proves that

0=\overline{c}b\overline{g}(0)+\overline{g}^{\prime}(0),

or by taking complex conjugates and using the fact that
b\overline{c}\in\mathbb{R}
and
|c|=1
,

bg(0)+cg^{\prime}(0)=0.

Hence,
g\in\mathrm{Dom}({K_{1}(b,c)})
. Therefore,
\mathrm{Dom}({K_{1}(b,c)})=\mathrm{Dom}({K_{1}(b,c)})^{*}
, as desired.
∎

Remark 1

\tau
is a
Sturm–Liouville differential operator
if it has the form

\tau f:=\frac{1}{r}\left(-\left(p(f^{\prime}+sf)\right)^{\prime}+sp(f^{\prime}+sf)+qf\right),

(2.9)

where
p,q,s,r
are real-valued Lebesgue measurable functions on an interval
(a,b)
with the properties

1.

p\neq 0
a.e. on
(a,b)
,

2.

r>0
a.e. on
(a,b)
,

3.

p^{-1},q,r,s\in L_{\text{loc}}^{1}((a,b))
,

and has domain

\mathrm{Dom}({\tau})=\left\{f\in AC_{\text{loc}}((a,b)):p(f^{\prime}+sf)\in AC_{\text{loc}}((a,b))\right\}.

Definition 9
.

Given a function
f\in AC_{\text{loc}}((a,b))
and the coefficent functions
p
and
s
of a Sturm–Liouville differential operator as described above, we define the
first quasi derivative

f^{[1]}
of

f^{[1]}:=p(f^{\prime}+sf)
.

When analyzing Sturm–Liouville operators, utilizing the first quasi derivative in places where one might use the classical first derivative in non-Sturm–Liouville theory is quite standard and has lead to myriad results. In the following argument, this is done to modify the Wronskian determinant. For
f,g\in\mathrm{Dom}({\tau})
, define the
modified Wronskian determinant

W(f,g)(x)
as

W(f,g)(x)=f(x)g^{[1]}(x)-f^{[1]}(x)g(x).

Throughout the rest of this paper, we will refer to the modified Wronskian determinant simply as the Wronskian.

Next, we will discuss the maximal and minimal operators associated with the Sturm–Liouville differential operator on
L^{2}((a,b);r(x)dx)
. The associated maximal operator
T_{\text{max}}
is given by
T_{\text{max}}f=\tau f
for
f
in the maximal domain

\mathrm{Dom}({T_{\text{max}}})=\left\{f\in L^{2}((a,b);r(x)dx):f\in\mathrm{Dom}({\tau}),\tau f\in L^{2}((a,b);r(x)dx)\right\}.

The associated minimal operator
T_{\text{min}}
is defined as
T_{\text{min}}f=\tau f
for
f
in the minimal domain

\mathrm{Dom}({T_{\text{min}}})=\left\{f\in\mathrm{Dom}({T_{\text{max}}}):\forall g\in\mathrm{Dom}({T_{\text{max}}}),W(f,g)(a)=W(f,g)(b)=0\right\}.

Before we discuss self-adjoint extensions/restrictions of these operators, we first examine the behavior that these operators can exhibit near the boundary points, as that behavior determines the deficiency indices.

Definition 10
.

A Sturm–Liouville operator is
regular
at an endpoint (either
a
or
b
) if the coefficient functions
p^{-1},q,r,s
are integrable near that endpoint.

Definition 11
.

A Sturm–Liouville operator
\tau
is said to be in the
limit-circle
(l.c.) case at an endpoint if for all
z\in\mathbb{C}
, all solutions to the eigenvalue equation
(\tau-z)u=0
are in
L^{2}((a,b);r(x)dx)
near that endpoint. Similarly, we say that
\tau
is in the
limit-point
(l.p.) case at an endpoint if for all
z\in\mathbb{C}
, there exists a solution to the eigenvalue equation
(\tau-z)u=0
that is not in
L^{2}((a,b);r(x)dx)
near that endpoint.

Note that
\tau
being regular near at endpoint implies that
\tau
is l.c. at that endpoint (see, e.g.
[
8
, Lemma 4.2]
). Further note that regularity at an endpoint allows for the evaluation of functions in the maximal domain at that endpoint via taking limits (from within the domain).

Theorem 1

(see, e.g.
[
8
, Theorem 4.6]
)
.

The deficiency indices of
T_{\text{min}}
are as follows

•

(0,0)
if
\tau
is l.c. at neither
a
nor
b
,

•

(1,1)
if
\tau

•

(2,2)
if
\tau
is l.c. at both

and

.

Moving forward, we will be concerned with the case where
\tau
is l.c. at both
a
and
b

and self-adjoint extensions of

have a one-to-one correspondence, so we will use the terms interchangeably. To describe the self-adjoint restrictions of
T_{\text{max}}
, we first let

be two functions with the following Wronskians

•

W(w_{1},\overline{w_{2}})(a)=W(w_{1},\overline{w_{2}})(b)=1
,

•

W(w_{1},\overline{w_{1}})(a)=W(w_{1},\overline{w_{1}})(b)=0
,

•

W(w_{2},\overline{w_{2}})(a)=W(w_{2},\overline{w_{2}})(b)=0

Towards the goal of determining self-adjoint boundary conditions, we use
w_{1}
and
w_{2}
to define the following linear functionals

BC_{\star}^{1}(f):=W(f,\overline{w_{2}})(\star),BC_{\star}^{2}(f):=W(\overline{w_{1}},f)(\star),

for

and
\star=a\text{ or }b

at the

Lemma 1

(see, e.g.
[

)
.

Let

such that

BC_{\star}^{1}(f)=f(\star)\text{ and }BC_{\star}^{2}(f)=f^{[1]}(\star)\text{ for all }f\in\mathrm{Dom}({T_{\text{max}}}),

where we again have

.

Theorem 2

(see, e.g.
[

)
.

Let

if and only if there exists matrices

with the properties

\text{rank}(B_{a}|B_{b})=2\text{ and }B_{a}JB_{a}^{*}=B_{b}JB_{b}^{*}\text{ with }J=\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix},

such that

\mathrm{Dom}({S})=\left\{f\in\mathrm{Dom}({T_{\text{max}}}):B_{a}\begin{pmatrix}[1.2]BC_{a}^{1}(f)\\

3
Matrix-Valued Clark Theory

In general, spectral measures can be decomposed into three parts: the absolutely continuous (with respect to Lebesgue measure), pure-point, and singular continuous spectra. Via known results for Clark measures on the disk
[
12
]
, we present formulae that describe the absolutely continuous and pure-point parts,
as they will be used later in our examples to compute spectral measures for derivatives operators. In
[
12
]
, it was also discussed that the description of the singular continuous parts is often not explicitly accessible.

At this time, we note that the uniqueness of the spectral measure for a given self-adjoint cyclic operator
T
is only guaranteed by choosing a basis of a cyclic vector
\varphi
. By choosing a different cyclic vector, one obtains a different (though unitarily equivalent) spectral measure for
T
. For the matrix-valued setting, we show analog statements of equivalence in Theorems
5
and
8
. This will allow us to fix a particular cyclic subspace of
\mathcal{H}
for each operator we examine (via choosing bases for its defect spaces). As such, there will be a unique (Clark) spectral measure in the contexts we are working with. Because of this uniqueness, we will refer to them as “the spectral measure" of the operator.

3.1
Livšic characteristic function

In order to use Clark Theory to investigate the spectral properties of differential operators, we must first construct an analytic function that encodes the spectral information of those operators. To that end, we will employ a Theorem of Livšic
[
13
,
14
]
using a construction outlined by Aleman–Martin–Ross
[
3
]
. We first let
T
be a simple, symmetric, closed operator with finite deficiency indices
(n,n)
that is densely defined on a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
. Note that since all of our operators of interest are differential operators, the underlying Hilbert spaces will be suitable function spaces. Given a basis
\left\{\psi_{k}\right\}_{k=1}^{n}
and an orthonormal basis
\left\{\tilde{\psi}_{k}\right\}_{k=1}^{n}
for the positive defect space of
T
, we construct the matrix-valued function

A_{T}(w,z)=\begin{pmatrix}\left\langle{\psi_{1}(w;x)},{\tilde{\psi}_{1}(z;x)}\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}&\dots&\left\langle{\psi_{1}(w;x)},{\tilde{\psi}_{n}(z;x)}\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\left\langle{\psi_{n}(w;x)},{\tilde{\psi}_{1}(z;x)}\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}&\dots&\left\langle{\psi_{n}(w;x)},{\tilde{\psi}_{n}(z;x)}\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}\\
\end{pmatrix}

(3.1)

for
w\in\mathbb{C}_{+}
and
z=\pm i
, where
x
is the independent variable of the functions in
\mathcal{H}
and
\left\langle{\cdot},{\cdot}\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}
denotes the inner product on
\mathcal{H}
. From this, we then define the matrix-valued function on
\mathbb{C}_{+}
,

B_{T}(w)=\frac{w-i}{w+i}(A_{T}(w,i))^{-1}A_{T}(w,-i).

(3.2)

The function
A_{T}
is known as
analytic Gram matrix
of T and
B_{T}
is the
Livšic characteristic function
of T. If
\left\{\psi_{k}^{1}\right\}_{k=1}^{n}
and
\left\{\psi_{k}^{2}\right\}_{k=1}^{n}
are different bases for the positive defect space of
T
and we construct Livšic characteristic functions
B_{T}^{1}
and
B_{T}^{2}
from them, then there exist constant matrices
R,Q\in\mathscr{U}_{n}
such that for all
w\in\mathbb{C}_{+}
,

B_{T}^{1}(w)=RB_{T}^{2}(w)Q.

(3.3)

More broadly speaking, we say that two Livšic characteristic functions
B,C
are equivalent if the condition (
3.3
) holds; i.e. if there exists constant matrices
R,Q\in\mathscr{U}_{n}
such that
B(w)=RC(w)Q
for all
w\in\mathbb{C}_{+}
. With this notion, we now discuss the aforementioned theorem of Livšic.

Theorem 3

(see, e.g.
[
13
,
14
]
)
.

Let
\mathcal{H}_{1},\mathcal{H}_{2}
be Hilbert spaces. Further let
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
be simple, symmetric, closed, expressions with finite deficiency indices
(n,n)
that are densely defined on
\mathcal{H}_{1}
and
\mathcal{H}_{2}
, respectively. Then,
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
are unitarily equivalent if and only if
B_{T_{1}}
and
B_{T_{2}}
are equivalent in the sense of Equation (
3.3
).

As is detailed in
[
3
]
, there is a close connection between the Livšic characteristic function and reproducing kernel Hilbert spaces (RKHS in short) of analytic functions. Namely, a simple, symmetric, closed, densely defined expression
T
with deficiency indices
(n,n)
can be associated with a vector-valued RKHS of analytic functions on
\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb{R}
via a model space. The kernel of that RKHS can then be used to produce a meromorphic function that is equivalent to the Livšic characteristic function in the sense of Equation (
3.3
). In
[
12
]
, it is further shown that the Clark measures of that Livšic characteristic function will then be spectral measures of the self-adjoint extensions of the model operator associated with the expression
T
.

Moving forward, the specific expression
T
that an analytic Gram matrix or a Livšic characteristic function is associated with will either be clear from the context or irrelevant to what is being discussed in that context. As such, we drop the subscript
T
.

3.2
Absolutely continuous spectrum

The first piece of the Lebesgue decomposition of Clark measures that we will look at is the absolutely continuous part of the measure, which entails computing its Lebesgue weight (also known as its Radon–Nikodym derivative). We begin by noting the following result for the absolutely continuous part of Clark measures corresponding to Livšic characteristic functions on the disk
[
12
, Theorem 2.1]
.

Lemma 2
.

For
b\in\mathscr{S}_{n}(\mathbb{D})
the Lebesgue weight of the absolutely continuous part of the Clark measure
\mu^{b\alpha^{*}}
, denoted by
\frac{d\mu^{b\alpha^{*}}}{d\mathfrak{m}}
is given by

\frac{d\mu^{b\alpha^{*}}}{d\mathfrak{m}}(\lambda)=\lim_{\zeta\mbox{\rotatebox[origin]{180.0}{$\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\leftarrow}}{{\scriptstyle\sphericalangle}}$}}\lambda}\left(I-\alpha b^{*}(\zeta)\right)^{-1}\Delta^{2}_{b\alpha^{*}}(\zeta)\left(I-b(\zeta)\alpha^{*}\right)^{-1};

(3.4)

for
\zeta\in\mathbb{D}
and
\mathfrak{m}\text{--a.e.~}\lambda\in\mathbb{T},
where
\mathfrak{m}
is the Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{T}
and
\Delta_{b\alpha^{*}}(\zeta)=(I-\alpha b^{*}(\zeta)b(\zeta)\alpha^{*})^{1/2}
.

Note that in general, the limits used to describe the absolutely continuous spectrum are defined Lebesgue almost everywhere. To simplify notation, we will not repeat this fact in any future instances where such a limit is defined or applied.

Remark 2
.

Equation (
3.4
) is the matrix-valued analog for a classical scalar-valued Clark theory result, that the spectral measure has Lebesgue density

\frac{d\mu}{d\mathfrak{m}}(\lambda)=\lim_{\zeta\mbox{\rotatebox[origin]{180.0}{$\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\leftarrow}}{{\scriptstyle\sphericalangle}}$}}\lambda}\frac{1-\left|b(\zeta)\right|^{2}}{\left|\alpha-b(\zeta)\right|^{2}};\quad\lambda\in\mathbb{T},\zeta\in\mathbb{D},

where
b
is scalar-valued
[
7
, Proposition 9.1.14]
.

We translate this to the upper half-plane. Specifically,
B(w)=b(\gamma(w))
, where
\gamma(w)=(w-i)(w+i)^{-1}
as was defined in Section
2.1
.

Lemma 3
.

For
B\in\mathscr{S}_{n}(\mathbb{C}^{+})
, the Lebesgue weight of the absolutely continuous part of the Clark measure
\mu^{B\alpha^{*}}
is given by

\frac{d\mu^{B\alpha^{*}}}{dm}(s)=\frac{1}{\pi(1+s^{2})}\frac{d\mu^{b\alpha^{*}}}{d\mathfrak{m}}(\gamma(s));\quad s\in\mathbb{R},

where
m
is the Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{R}
.

Using Lemmas
2
and
3
, we now compute the density of the absolutely continuous part of the Clark measure
\mu^{B\alpha^{*}}.

Theorem 4
.

For
B\in\mathscr{S}_{n}(\mathbb{C}^{+})
, the Lebesgue weight of the absolutely continuous part of the Clark measure
\mu^{B\alpha^{*}}
is given by

\frac{d\mu^{B\alpha^{*}}}{dm}(s)=\frac{1}{\pi(1+s^{2})}\lim_{w\downarrow s}(\alpha^{*}-B^{*}(w))^{-1}(I-B^{*}(w)B(w))(\alpha-B(w))^{-1},

for
s\in\mathbb{R}
and
w\in\mathbb{C}_{+}
.

Proof.

We begin with a substitution of Lemma
2
into Lemma
3
,

\frac{d\mu^{B\alpha^{*}}}{dm}(s)=\frac{1}{\pi(1+s^{2})}\lim_{\gamma(w)\mbox{\rotatebox[origin]{180.0}{$\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\leftarrow}}{{\scriptstyle\sphericalangle}}$}}\gamma(s)}\left(I-\alpha b^{*}(\gamma(w))\right)^{-1}\Delta^{2}_{b\alpha^{*}}(\gamma(w))\left(I-b(\gamma(w))\alpha^{*}\right)^{-1},

where we have identified
z\in\mathbb{D}
as the Cayley transform of
w\in\mathbb{C}_{+}
and
\lambda\in\mathbb{T}
as the Cayley transform of
s\in\mathbb{R}
. Next, we compute

\displaystyle\frac{d\mu^{B\alpha^{*}}}{dm}(s)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\pi(1+s^{2})}\lim_{w\downarrow s}\left(I-\alpha B^{*}(w)\right)^{-1}\Delta^{2}_{B\alpha^{*}}(w)\left(I-B(w)\alpha^{*}\right)^{-1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\pi(1+s^{2})}\lim_{w\downarrow s}\left(\alpha^{*}-B^{*}(w)\right)^{-1}\alpha^{*}\Delta^{2}_{B\alpha^{*}}(w)\alpha\left(\alpha-B(w)\right)^{-1}.

Substituting

\Delta^{2}_{B\alpha^{*}}(w)=(I-\alpha B^{*}(w)B(w)\alpha^{*})=\alpha(I-B^{*}(w)B(w))\alpha^{*}

into the above yields the desired result.
∎

Next, we address the well-posedness of this construction with respect to the choice of basis. Recall that Livšic’s Theorem tells us that choosing different bases for our defect spaces yields equivalent characteristic functions. Therefore, we examine how the absolutely continuous parts of Clark measures of equivalent characteristic functions are related. The following result verifies expectations and confirms the well-posedness for the absolutely continuous part. Later, we obtain an analogous result for the pure point parts.

Theorem 5
.

Let functions
B_{1},B_{2}\in\mathscr{S}_{n}(\mathbb{C}^{+})
be equivalent such that
B_{1}(w)=RB_{2}(w)Q
for all
w\in\mathbb{C}_{+}
, where
R,Q\in\mathscr{U}_{n}
are fixed constant matrices. Then, the Lebesgue weight of the absolutely continuous parts of their associated Clark measures are unitarily equivalent with a perturbation parameter conjugated by those matrices of equivalency. Namely,

\frac{d\mu^{B_{1}\alpha^{*}}}{dm}(s)=R\frac{d\mu^{B_{2}(Q\alpha^{*}R)}}{dm}(s)R^{*},

for all
s\in\mathbb{R}
.

Proof.

Suppose
B_{1},B_{2}\in\mathscr{S}_{n}(\mathbb{C}^{+})
are equivalent as described in the hypothesis. Then,

\displaystyle\pi(1+s^{2})\frac{d\mu^{B_{1}\alpha^{*}}}{dm}(s)

\displaystyle=\lim_{w\downarrow s}(\alpha^{*}-(RB_{2}(w)Q)^{*})^{-1}(I-(RB_{2}(w)Q)^{*}RB_{2}(w)Q)(\alpha-RB_{2}(w)Q)^{-1}

\displaystyle=\lim_{w\downarrow s}R(Q\alpha^{*}R-B_{2}^{*}(w))^{-1}Q(I-Q^{*}B_{2}^{*}(w)B_{2}(w)Q)Q^{*}(R^{*}\alpha Q^{*}-B_{2}(w))^{-1}R^{*}

\displaystyle=R\left(\lim_{w\downarrow s}(Q\alpha^{*}R-B_{2}^{*}(w))^{-1}(I-B_{2}^{*}(w)B_{2}(w))(R^{*}\alpha Q^{*}-B_{2}(w))^{-1}\right)R^{*}

\displaystyle=\pi(1+s^{2})R\frac{d\mu^{B_{2}(Q\alpha^{*}R)}}{dm}(s)R^{*}.

∎

3.3
Nevanlinna’s Formula

In
[
12
, Theorem 3.1]
, some of the authors obtained a generalization of Nevanlinna’s result on characterizing the pure point spectrum of a finite-rank unitary perturbation in terms of the Livšic characteristic function:

Theorem 6
.

Fix
b\in\mathscr{S}_{n}(\mathbb{D})
and
\alpha\in\mathscr{U}_{n}
. Then for any
\lambda\in\mathscr{U}_{1}
,

\mu^{b\alpha^{*}}\left(\{\lambda\}\right)=\lim_{\zeta\mbox{\rotatebox[origin]{180.0}{$\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\leftarrow}}{{\scriptstyle\sphericalangle}}$}}\lambda}(1-\zeta\bar{}\lambda)(I-b(z)\alpha^{*})^{-1}.

For our purposes, we translate this result to the upper half-plane.

Theorem 7
.

Fix
B\in\mathscr{S}_{n}(\mathbb{C}_{+})
and
\alpha\in\mathscr{U}_{n}
. Then for any
s\in\mathbb{R}
,

\mu^{B\alpha^{*}}\left(\{s\}\right)=\frac{2i}{\pi(1+s^{2})^{2}}\lim_{w\downarrow s}(s-w)(I-B(w)\alpha^{*})^{-1}.

(3.5)

Proof.

For
B\in\mathscr{S}_{n}(\mathbb{C}^{+})
and
\alpha\in\mathcal{U}_{n}
, we define the
Nevanlinna function
:

H_{\alpha}(w):=(I-B(w)\alpha^{*})^{-1}(I+B(w)\alpha^{*})

with
b
as above, and
\zeta=\gamma(w)
. Then

h_{\alpha}(\zeta)=(I-b(\zeta)\alpha^{*})^{-1}(I+b(\zeta)\alpha^{*})=H_{\alpha}(w);\quad\zeta=\gamma(w),

is a Herglotz function on the disk.
By the Nevanlinna represtentation formula for positive harmonic functions on
\mathbb{C}^{+}
, there is a unique (Clark) measure
\mu^{B\alpha^{*}}
so that

\operatorname{Re}{H_{\alpha}(w)}=c_{\alpha}\operatorname{Re}{w}+\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\operatorname{Re}{\frac{1}{i\pi}\frac{1}{t-w}}\mu^{B\alpha^{*}}(dt).

Furthermore, one can check that:

c_{\alpha}=\mu^{b\alpha^{*}}\left(\{1\}\right),

and that for any Borel set
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}
,

\mu^{B\alpha^{*}}(\Omega)=\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{\pi(1+t^{2})}(\mu^{b\alpha^{*}}\gamma)(dt).

In particular, given
\Omega=\{s\}
for a fixed
s\in\mathbb{R}
,

\displaystyle\mu^{B\alpha^{*}}\left(\{s\}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\pi(1+s^{2})}\mu^{b\alpha^{*}}\left(\{\gamma(s)\}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\pi(1+s^{2})}\lim_{\zeta\ \mbox{\rotatebox[origin]{180.0}{$\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\leftarrow}}{{\scriptstyle\sphericalangle}}$}}\gamma(s)}(1-\zeta\gamma(s)^{*})(I-b(\zeta)\alpha^{*})^{-1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\pi(1+s^{2})}\lim_{w\downarrow s}\left(1-\frac{w-i}{w+i}\frac{s+i}{s-i}\right)(I-B(w)\alpha^{*})^{-1}

\displaystyle=\frac{2i}{\pi(1+s^{2})}\lim_{w\downarrow s}\frac{s-w}{(w+i)(s-i)}(I-B(w)\alpha^{*})^{-1}.

\displaystyle=\frac{2i}{\pi(1+s^{2})^{2}}\lim_{w\downarrow s}(s-w)(I-B(w)\alpha^{*})^{-1}.

∎

The following result confirms the well-posedness for the pure point part of the Clark measure.

Theorem 8
.

Let functions
B_{1},B_{2}\in\mathscr{S}_{n}(\mathbb{C}_{+})
be equivalent such that
B_{1}(w)=RB_{2}(w)Q
for all
w\in\mathbb{C}_{+}
, where
R,Q\in\mathscr{U}_{n}
are fixed constant matrices. Then, the pure-point parts of their associated Clark measures are unitarily equivalent with a perturbation parameter conjugated by those matrices of equivalency. Specifically,

\mu^{B_{1}\alpha^{*}}(\{s\})=R\mu^{B_{2}(Q\alpha^{*}R)}(\{s\})R^{*},

for all
s\in\mathbb{R}
.

Proof.

Suppose
B_{1},B_{2}\in\mathscr{S}_{n}(\mathbb{C}_{+})
are equivalent as described in the hypothesis. Then,

\displaystyle\mu^{B_{1}\alpha^{*}}\left(\{s\}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{2i}{\pi(1+s^{2})^{2}}\lim_{w\downarrow s}(s-w)(I-RB_{2}(w)Q\alpha^{*})^{-1}

\displaystyle=\frac{2i}{\pi(1+s^{2})^{2}}\lim_{w\downarrow s}(s-w)(R^{*}-B_{2}(w)Q\alpha^{*})^{-1}R^{*}

\displaystyle=R\left(\frac{2i}{\pi(1+s^{2})^{2}}\lim_{w\downarrow s}(s-w)(I-B_{2}(w)Q\alpha^{*}R)^{-1}\right)R^{*}

\displaystyle=R\mu^{B_{2}(Q\alpha^{*}R)}\left(\{s\}\right)R^{*},

as desired.
∎

Here we note that what Theorem
8
states for the pure-point parts of Clark Measures is analogous to what Theorem
5
provided for the absolutely continuous parts. In order to discuss the implications of Clark measures being unitarily equivalent (with a transformation of the perturbation parameter) established, we will need results from Section
4
. We will return to both of these theorems in Subsection
4.4
.

4
Self-Adjoint Boundary Conditions for Finite-Rank Perturbations

In this section, we will apply the main theorem established by Sun et al. in

to express the set of all self-adjoint extensions of operators from a class that includes

for

in Theorems

.

First, we will discuss self-adjoint extensions in terms of finite-rank perturbations. Let
\phi_{k}^{\pm}
for

be the basis functions for the defect spaces
\mathcal{D}_{\pm}
of an operator
T
as defined in Equation (

Definition 12
.

The domains of the self-adjoint extensions of an operator
T
with deficiency indices
(n,n)
are given by

\mathrm{Dom}({T_{\alpha}}):=\mathrm{Dom}({T_{\text{min}}})+\bigvee_{i}\left(-\phi_{i}^{+}+\sum_{j=1}^{n}\alpha_{i,j}\phi_{j}^{-}\right),

where
\alpha\in\mathscr{U}_{n}

Here, it is important to note that

4.1
Self-Adjoint Extensions of
C
-Symmetric Operators

We begin by defining a space of
n\times n
matrix-valued functions whose elements will be used to determine coefficient functions for quasi-differential equations. For an interval
J:=(a,b)
with
-\infty<a<b\leq\infty
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
, let

\displaystyle Z_{n}(J)=

\displaystyle\bigg\{Q=\left(q_{r,s}\right)_{r,s=1}^{n}:Q\in\left(L_{\text{loc}}(J)\right)^{n\times n};

\displaystyle q_{r,r+1}\neq 0\text{ a.e. on }J,q_{r,r+1}^{-1}\in L_{\text{loc}}(J),1\leq r\leq n-1;

\displaystyle q_{r,s}=0\text{ a.e. on }J,2\leq r+1<s\leq n;

\displaystyle q_{r,s}\in L_{\text{loc}}(J),s\neq r+1,1\leq r\leq n-1\bigg\}.

Now, for

We define the zeroth-order quasi derivative

for

. We subscript

Lemma 4

(Extension of Naimark’s Patching Lemma)
.

Let

such that

and

is regular on

For the original statement of Naimark’s Patching Lemma, see
[
17
, Lemma 4]
. Using this patching result, we have an explicit expression for the minimal domain associated with

.

Lemma 5
.

The minimal domain associated with
M_{Q}
is given by

\mathrm{Dom}({S_{\text{min}}}):=\left\{f\in\mathrm{Dom}({S_{\text{max}}}):[f,g](b)=[f,g](a)=0,\forall g\in\mathrm{Dom}({S_{\text{max}}})\right\}.

Proof.

Let
f\in\mathrm{Dom}({S_{\text{min}}})
. For all
g\in\mathrm{Dom}({S_{\text{max}}})
, integrating by parts in
L^{2}(J,w)
yields

\left\langle{M_{Q}f},{g}\right\rangle_{L^{2}(J,w)}-\left\langle{f},{M_{Q}g}\right\rangle_{L^{2}(J,w)}=[f,g]_{a}^{b}.

By the patching Lemma, for all
g\in\mathrm{Dom}({S_{\text{max}}})
, there exists
g_{a}\in\mathrm{Dom}({S_{\text{max}}})
such that
g_{a}=g
in a neighborhood of
a
and
g_{a}=0
in a neighborhood of
b
. Thus, we have

[f,g](a)=[f,g_{a}](a)=[f,g_{a}](b)=0.

The same can be done for
g
in a neighborhood of
b
.

To show the backward direction, let
f\in\mathrm{Dom}({S_{\text{max}}})
such that

[f,g](b)=[f,g](a)=0

for all
g\in\mathrm{Dom}({S_{\text{max}}})
. Then,

\left\langle{M_{Q}f},{g}\right\rangle_{L^{2}(J,w)}-\left\langle{f},{M_{Q}g}\right\rangle_{L^{2}(J,w)}=[f,g]_{a}^{b}=0.

Hence,
f\in\mathrm{Dom}({S_{\text{max}}})^{*}=\mathrm{Dom}({S_{\text{min}}})
as desired.
∎

(GKN Theorem)

is a self-adjoint extension of

\displaystyle(i)

\displaystyle v_{1},\dots,v_{d}\text{ are linearly independent modulo }\mathrm{Dom}({S_{\text{min}}}),

(4.1)

\displaystyle(ii)

\displaystyle[v_{k},v_{\ell}]_{a}^{b}=0\text{ for all }k,\ell\in\{1,2,\dots,d\}

\displaystyle(iii)

\displaystyle\mathrm{Dom}({S}):=\{f\in\mathrm{Dom}({S_{\text{max}}}):[f,v_{k}]_{a}^{b}=0\text{ for all }k=1,2,\dots,d\}.

4.4
Connection to Characteristic Function Equivalence

Recall from Section
3
, that two
B_{1},B_{2}\in\mathscr{S}_{n}(\mathbb{C}_{+})
are said to be equivalent if there exist constant matrices
R,Q\in\mathscr{U}_{n}
such that

B_{1}(w)=RB_{2}(w)Q

for all
w\in\mathbb{C}_{+}
. Theorem
5
says that
B_{1}
and
B_{2}
have Clark measures whose absolutely continuous parts are unitarily equivalent with a transformation of the perturbation parameter,

\frac{d\mu^{B_{1}\alpha^{*}}}{dm}(s)=R\frac{d\mu^{B_{2}(Q\alpha^{*}R)}}{dm}(s)R^{*},

for all
s\in\mathbb{R}
. Similarly, from Theorem
8
, we have that
B_{1}
and
B_{2}
have Clark measures whose pure-point parts are unitarily equivalent with that same transformation of the perturbation parameter,

\mu^{B_{1}\alpha^{*}}(\{s\})=R\mu^{B_{2}(Q\alpha^{*}R)}(\{s\})R^{*},

for all
s\in\mathbb{R}
. Livšic’s theorem (Theorem
3
) gives us that choosing a different bases for the defect spaces leads to equivalent Livšic characteristic functions. Thus, choosing different bases for the defect spaces leads to a unitarily equivalent spectral measure with a perturbation parameter conjugated by those matrices of equivalence. In particular, the spectral type of the corresponding scalar measures
\operatorname{tr}\mu
is preserved, and Theorems
3
and
8
show that a similar property holds for the matrix-valued measures as well.

Via our setup for finite rank perturbations, Definition
12
, we have an explicit relationship for how changing the bases of the defect spaces affects the parameterization of the perturbation problem. Now suppose
B_{1}
and
B_{2}
are equivalent Livšic characteristic functions for the expression
T
given by choosing bases
\left\{\psi_{k}^{1}\right\}_{k=1}^{n}
and
\left\{\psi_{k}^{2}\right\}_{k=1}^{n}
, respectively, for the positive defect space of
T
. Then, the self-adjoint extension of
T
corresponding to a fixed
\alpha_{0}\in\mathscr{U}_{n}
for the choice of basis
\left\{\psi_{k}^{1}\right\}_{k=1}^{n}
is unitarily equivalent to the self-adjoint extension of
T
corresponding to
R^{*}\alpha_{0}Q^{*}
for the choice of basis
\left\{\psi_{k}^{2}\right\}_{k=1}^{n}
. This change can be propagated to parameterizing self-adjoint extensions via boundary conditions to a differential expression
T
through Theorems
12
and
14
.

. To this end, consider the eigenvalue equation

and

if
\Im(\sqrt{w})<0

lands in

for the principal branch of the square root, for the bases of the defect spaces we have

\phi_{1,0}^{+}(w;x)=\phi_{1,0}^{-}(w;x)=e^{i\sqrt{w}x}.

Thus, the defect indices of

are (1,1). As was pointed out in Subsection

, for this operator, the perturbation parameter
\alpha\in\mathscr{U}_{1}
(i.e. a complex number on the unit circle). Recall that this parameter
\alpha
determines the set of all self-adjoint extensions of

as given by Definition
12
and that this parameterization is equivalent to a choice of boundary conditions as shown in Proposition
2
.

Before we proceed with computing the analytic Gram matrix and the spectral measures for the self-adjoint extensions of
K_{1}
, we normalize the basis function for
\pm i

\tilde{\phi}_{1,0}(\pm i;x)=\sqrt[4]{2}e^{i\sqrt{\pm i}x}.

Next, we compute the analytic Gram matrix (which is just a scalar in this case) using Equation (
3.1
),

\displaystyle A(w,\pm i)

\displaystyle=\langle e^{i\sqrt{w}x},\sqrt[4]{2}e^{i\sqrt{\pm i}x}\rangle_{(0,\infty)}

\displaystyle=\sqrt[4]{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{i\sqrt{w}x}\overline{e^{i\sqrt{\pm i}x}}dx

\displaystyle=\left.\sqrt[4]{2}\frac{e^{\left(i\sqrt{w}+\overline{i\sqrt{\pm i}}\right)x}}{i\sqrt{w}+\overline{i\sqrt{\pm i}}}\right|_{x=0}^{\infty}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\sqrt[4]{2}}{i\sqrt{w}+\overline{i\sqrt{\pm i}}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt[4]{2}i}{\sqrt{w}-\overline{\sqrt{\pm i}}},

where
\left\langle{\cdot},{\cdot}\right\rangle_{(0,\infty)}
denotes the inner product on

. Then, utilizing Equation (
3.2
), the Livšic characteristic function is given by

\displaystyle B(w)

\displaystyle=\frac{w-i}{w+i}\frac{\sqrt{w}-\overline{\sqrt{i}}}{\sqrt{w}-\overline{\sqrt{-i}}}

\displaystyle=\frac{(w-i)(\sqrt{w}-(1-i)/\sqrt{2})}{(w+i)(\sqrt{w}+(1+i)/\sqrt{2})}.

Now, we can compute the absolutely continuous part of the spectral measure.

Theorem 15
.

The Lebesgue weight of the absolutely continuous part of the Clark measure of
K_{1,\alpha}
is given by

\frac{d\mu^{B\alpha^{*}}}{dm}(s)=\frac{2\Re(\sqrt{2s})}{\pi(|s|+1+2\Re(\sqrt{s}e^{-i\pi/4}))\mathscr{D}(s)},

where

\displaystyle\mathscr{D}(s)

\displaystyle:=2(1-\Re(\alpha))s^{2}+2|s|+|i\alpha-1|^{2}-2^{3/2}(\Re(\alpha)-1)s^{3/2}

(5.1)

\displaystyle+2(1-\Re(\alpha)+\Im(\alpha))s-2^{3/2}(\Im(\alpha)-1)s^{1/2}.

Proof.

Now, we can compute the Lebesgue weight of the spectral measure using Theorem
4
,

\frac{d\mu^{B\alpha^{*}}}{dm}(s)=\frac{1}{\pi(1+s^{2})}\lim_{w\downarrow s}(\alpha^{*}-B^{*}(w))^{-1}(I-B^{*}(w)B(w))(\alpha-B(w))^{-1}.

First, we examine
I-B^{*}B,

\displaystyle I-B^{*}(w)B(w)

\displaystyle=1-\frac{(\overline{w}+i)(\sqrt{\overline{w}}+e^{-i3\pi/4})}{(\overline{w}-i)(\sqrt{\overline{w}}+e^{-i\pi/4})}\frac{(w-i)(\sqrt{w}+e^{i3\pi/4})}{(w+i)(\sqrt{w}+e^{i\pi/4})}

\displaystyle=1-\frac{|w-i|^{2}}{|w+i|^{2}}\frac{|w|+1+2\Re(\sqrt{w}e^{-i3\pi/4})}{|w|+1+2\Re(\sqrt{w}e^{-i\pi/4})}

Letting
w
tend to
s\in\mathbb{R}
,

\displaystyle\lim_{w\downarrow s}(I-B^{*}(w)B(w))

(5.2)

\displaystyle=1-\frac{s^{2}+1}{s^{2}+1}\frac{|s|+1+2\Re(\sqrt{s}e^{-i3\pi/4})}{|s|+1+2\Re(\sqrt{s}e^{-i\pi/4})}

\displaystyle=\frac{\left(|s|+1+2\Re(\sqrt{s}e^{-i\pi/4})\right)-\left(|s|+1+2\Re(\sqrt{s}e^{-i3\pi/4})\right)}{|s|+1+2\Re(\sqrt{s}e^{-i\pi/4})}

\displaystyle=\frac{2\Re(\sqrt{2s})}{|s|+1+2\Re(\sqrt{s}e^{-i\pi/4})}.

Next, we determine
(\alpha^{*}-B^{*}(w))^{-1}
and
(\alpha-B(w))^{-1}
. Taking advantage of the fact that we have commutation in the rank-one case, we can compute
\left|\alpha-B(w)\right|^{-2}
. Note that since
\lim_{w\downarrow s}(I-B^{*}(w)B(w))
is zero for
s\leq 0
and
\left|\alpha-B(w)\right|^{-2}
is a power of a modulus, it follows that the Lebesgue weight itself is zero for
s\leq 0
. As such, we will assume that
s>0
for the rest of this proof.
We obtain

\displaystyle\left|\alpha-B(w)\right|^{-2}

\displaystyle=\left|\alpha-\frac{w+\sqrt{2w}+1}{w+i}\right|^{-2}

\displaystyle=\frac{\left|w+i\right|^{2}}{\left|(\alpha-1)w-\sqrt{2w}+i\alpha-1\right|^{2}}.

The denominator of the above expands to

\displaystyle 2(1-\Re(\alpha))|w|^{2}+2|w|+|i\alpha-1|^{2}-2^{3/2}\Re((\overline{\alpha}-1)\overline{w}\sqrt{w})

\displaystyle+2\Re((-i\overline{\alpha}-1)(\alpha-1)w)-2^{3/2}\Re((-i\alpha-1)\sqrt{w}).

Substituting this in, then letting
w
tend to

yields

\lim_{w\downarrow s}\left|\alpha-B(w)\right|^{-2}=\frac{s^{2}+1}{\mathscr{D}(s)},

(5.3)

where
\mathscr{D}(s)
is as defined in Equation (
5.1
). Multiplying Equations (
5.2
) and (

∎

6
Spectrum of Derivative Powers on an Interval

We will now examine the spectra of self-adjoint extensions of the following family of operators
L_{n}
acting on
L^{2}(-a,a)
as given in Definition
7
,

L_{n}=i^{n}\frac{d^{n}}{dx^{n}}.

As we did on the half-line, we will use the method detailed in
[
3
]
to obtain an analytic Gram matrix, Livšic characteristic function, and ultimately spectra information about the self-adjoint extensions of these operators.

Remark 6
.

Powers of the derivative on a finite interval will only have pure-point spectra. To see this, first note that one can use tools from classical ordinary differential equations theory to express the corresponding resolvent operators as integral operators with square-integrable kernels, thus making the resolvents Hilbert–Schmidt operators. Further computation will show that these resolvent operators have continuous inverses on the entire Hilbert space
L^{2}(-a,a)
. Using the invertibility of the resolvent operators, one can then show that the original operators of interest (power of the derivative on a finite interval) have only pure-point spectrum. This computation is carried out explicitly for the rank-one case in
[
5
]
.

6.1
First Derivative on Interval

Analogously to what was done in Subsections
5.1
for
K_{1}
and
5.2
for
K_{2}
, the first step in determining spectral information about the self-adjoint extensions of
L_{1}
is to determine its deficiency indices. The eigenvalue equation

L_{1}f:=if^{\prime}=wf

is solved on
L^{2}(-a,a)
by
f(w;x)=e^{-iwx}.
Thus, we have

\phi_{1,1}(w;x)=e^{-iwx},\quad\tilde{\phi}_{1,1}(\pm i;x)=\frac{e^{\pm x}}{\sqrt{2a}}.

Therefore,
L_{1}
has deficiency indices
(1,1)
and
\alpha\in\mathscr{U}_{1}=\mathbb{T}
. In other words, the self-adjoint extensions of
L_{1}
are parameterized by a choice of a complex number on the unit circle via Definition
12
. Furthermore, also recall that this parameterization is equivalent to boundary conditions as in Proposition
4
. Now, we compute the analytic Gram matrix
A
as outlined before (using Equation (
3.1
)), noting that the matrix is a scalar since we are in a rank-one setting,

\displaystyle A(w,\pm i)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2a}}\langle e^{-iwx},e^{\pm x}\rangle_{(-a,a)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2a}}\int_{-a}^{a}e^{-iwx}e^{\pm x}dx

\displaystyle=\left.\frac{1}{\sqrt{2a}}\frac{e^{(\pm 1-iw)x}}{\pm 1-iw}\right|_{x=-a}^{a}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2a}}\frac{\sinh((\pm 1-iw)a)}{\pm 1-iw}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2a}}\frac{\sin((w\pm i)a)}{w\pm i},

where
\left\langle{\cdot},{\cdot}\right\rangle_{(-a,a)}
denotes the inner product on
L^{2}(-a,a)
. Next, we compute the Livšic characteristic function using Equation (
3.2
),

\displaystyle B(w)

\displaystyle=\frac{w-i}{w+i}\frac{\sqrt{2a}(w+i)}{\sin((w+i)a)}\frac{\sin((w-i)a)}{\sqrt{2a}(w-i)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sin((w-i)a)}{\sin((w+i)a)}.

Note that the cancellation of the Blaschke factor in
B
is an artifact of this specific operator and does not happen in general.

Now that we have an expression for the Livšic characteristic function, we can compute some spectral results.

Theorem 16
.

The location of the point masses of the Clark measure of
L_{1,\alpha}
are the set

P_{\alpha}=\left\{s=\frac{1}{a}\tan^{-1}\left(\frac{\alpha^{*}+1}{\alpha^{*}-1}\tan(ia)\right)+\frac{n\pi}{a}:n\in\mathbb{Z}\right\},

where we extend the definition of the inverse tangent (i.e.
\tan^{-1}(\pm\infty)\equiv\pm\pi/2
).

Proof.

We begin by applying Nevanlinna’s formula for
s\in\mathbb{R}
,

\mu^{B\alpha^{*}}\left(\{s\}\right)=\frac{2i}{\pi(1+s^{2})^{2}}\lim_{w\downarrow s}(s-w)\left(1-\frac{\sin((w-i)a)}{\sin((w+i)a)}\alpha^{*}\right)^{-1}.

From this, we have that there can only be point spectrum when

\lim_{w\downarrow s}\frac{\sin((w-i)a)}{\sin((w+i)a)}=\frac{1}{\alpha^{*}}.

In the above limit, since the numerator and denominator cannot simultaneously be zero, we can evaluate the limit and simplify using trigonometric identities,

(\alpha^{*}+1)\tan(ia)=(\alpha^{*}-1)\tan(sa).

(6.1)

Thus, the set of point spectrum for the measure
\mu^{B\alpha^{*}}
is given by,

P_{\alpha}=\left\{s=\frac{1}{a}\tan^{-1}\left(\frac{\alpha^{*}+1}{\alpha^{*}-1}\tan(ia)\right)+\frac{n\pi}{a}:n\in\mathbb{Z}\right\}.

Note that for
\alpha=1,
the above definition is well defined by considering the extended real line and taking limits to define the inverse tangent of
\pm\infty
.
∎

Following this, we immediately verify an explicit intertwining property for the point spectra of Clark measures
\mu^{B\alpha^{*}}
related to the Livšic characteristic function
B
.

Corollary 1
.

The location of the point masses of the Clark measure of
L_{1,\alpha}
are monotonically increasing with respect to the argument of
\alpha
.

Proof.

Let
\alpha=e^{i\theta}.
Then, we have

s(\theta)=\frac{1}{a}\tan^{-1}\left(\frac{e^{-i\theta}+1}{e^{-i\theta}-1}\tan(ia)\right)+\frac{n\pi}{a}.

Computing the derivative yields,

\displaystyle\frac{ds}{d\theta}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{a}\frac{1}{1+\left(\frac{e^{-i\theta}+1}{e^{-i\theta}-1}\tan(ia)\right)^{2}}\left[2i\tan(ia)\frac{e^{-i\theta}}{(e^{-i\theta}-1)^{2}}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{a}\frac{1}{1+\left(\frac{e^{-i\theta}+1}{e^{-i\theta}-1}\tan(ia)\right)^{2}}\left[\frac{e^{-a}-e^{a}}{e^{-a}+e^{a}}\frac{1}{(e^{i\theta/2}-e^{-i\theta/2})^{2}}\right]

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2a}\frac{1}{1+\left(\frac{e^{-i\theta}+1}{e^{-i\theta}-1}\tan(ia)\right)^{2}}\frac{e^{-a}-e^{a}}{e^{-a}+e^{a}}\csc^{2}\left(\frac{\theta}{2}\right).

All that is left is to verify the sign of the derivative.

\displaystyle\text{sgn}\left(\frac{ds}{d\theta}\right)

\displaystyle=\text{sgn}\left(-\frac{1}{2a}\frac{1}{1+\left(\frac{e^{-i\theta}+1}{e^{-i\theta}-1}\tan(ia)\right)^{2}}\frac{e^{-a}-e^{a}}{e^{-a}+e^{a}}\csc^{2}\left(\frac{\theta}{2}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=-\text{sgn}\left(\frac{1}{1+\left(\frac{e^{-i\theta}+1}{e^{-i\theta}-1}\tan(ia)\right)^{2}}\right)\text{sgn}\left(\frac{e^{-a}-e^{a}}{e^{-a}+e^{a}}\right)

\displaystyle=\text{sgn}\left(1+\left(\frac{e^{-i\theta}+1}{e^{-i\theta}-1}\tan(ia)\right)^{2}\right)

yielding the result.
∎

Next, we will compute the masses of the atoms of the Clark measure.

Theorem 17
.

The weight of the Clark measure of
L_{1,\alpha}
on the set
P_{\alpha}
for
\alpha\neq\pm 1
is given by,

\mu^{B\alpha^{*}}\left(\{s\}\right)=-\frac{1}{2\pi a\Im(\alpha)}\frac{\sin(2sa)}{(1+s^{2})^{2}}.

For
\alpha=1
and
\alpha=-1
, the point spectra have weights

\mu^{B}\left(\{s\}\right)=\frac{2\coth(a)}{a\pi(1+s^{2})^{2}}
and
\mu^{-B}\left(\{s\}\right)=\frac{2\tanh(a)}{a\pi(1+s^{2})^{2}},
respectively.

Remark 7
.

It is not difficult to see that the measures
\mu^{B,\alpha}
are Poisson summable. For example,

\int_{{}_{\scriptstyle\mathbb{R}}}d\mu^{-B}(s)=\frac{2\tanh(a)}{a\pi}\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\frac{a^{4}}{(a^{2}+\pi^{2}n^{2})^{2}}<\infty.

Proof.

For
s\in P_{\alpha}
, we apply L’Hospital’s rule to compute the weight of the measure:

\displaystyle\mu^{B\alpha^{*}}\left(\{s\}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{2i}{\pi(1+s^{2})^{2}}\lim_{w\downarrow s}(-1)\left(-a\frac{\sin(2ia)}{\sin^{2}((w+i)a)}\alpha^{*}\right)^{-1}

(6.2)

\displaystyle=\frac{2i}{\alpha^{*}a\pi(1+s^{2})^{2}}\frac{\sin^{2}(sa+ia)}{\sin(2ia)}

\displaystyle=\frac{2i}{\alpha^{*}a\pi(1+s^{2})^{2}}\left[\frac{\sin^{2}(sa)\cos(ia)}{\sin(ia)}+2\sin(sa)\cos(sa)\right.

\displaystyle\left.\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad+\frac{\cos^{2}(sa)\sin(ia)}{\cos(ia)}\right].

From here, the computation varies dependent on
\alpha
. First examine the case of
\alpha\neq\pm 1
, applying the condition in Equation (
6.1
) to further simplify Equation (
6.2
),

\displaystyle\mu^{B\alpha^{*}}\left(\{s\}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{2i}{\alpha^{*}a\pi(1+s^{2})^{2}}\left[\sin^{2}(sa)\cot(sa)\frac{\alpha^{*}+1}{\alpha^{*}-1}+2\sin(sa)\cos(sa)\right.

\displaystyle\left.\hskip 9.24994pt\hskip 9.24994pt\hskip 9.24994pt\hskip 9.24994pt\hskip 9.24994pt\hskip 9.24994pt\hskip 9.24994pt\hskip 9.24994pt\hskip 9.24994pt+\cos^{2}(sa)\tan(sa)\frac{\alpha^{*}-1}{\alpha^{*}+1}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{i}{\alpha^{*}a\pi}\frac{\sin(2sa)}{(1+s^{2})^{2}}\left[\frac{\alpha^{*}+1}{\alpha^{*}-1}+2+\frac{\alpha^{*}-1}{\alpha^{*}+1}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{i}{\alpha^{*}a\pi}\frac{\sin(2sa)}{(1+s^{2})^{2}}\frac{(\alpha^{*})^{2}}{(\alpha^{*})^{2}-1}

\displaystyle=\frac{i}{a\pi}\frac{\sin(2sa)}{(1+s^{2})^{2}}\frac{1}{-2i\Im(\alpha)}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2\pi a\Im(\alpha)}\frac{\sin(2sa)}{(1+s^{2})^{2}}.

For
\alpha=1
, we have
s=(n+1/2)\pi/a
for
n\in\mathbb{Z}
. Thus, we can simplify the trigonometric functions in Equation (
6.2
),

\mu^{B}\left(\{s\}\right)=\frac{2i\cot(ia)}{a\pi(1+s^{2})^{2}}=\frac{2\coth(a)}{a\pi(1+s^{2})^{2}}.

If
\alpha=-1
, we analogously have,

\mu^{-B}\left(\{s\}\right)=-\frac{2i\tan(ia)}{a\pi(1+s^{2})^{2}}=\frac{2\tanh(a)}{a\pi(1+s^{2})^{2}}.

∎

As a spectral measure, the Clark measure must be nonnegative. However, that is not immediately obvious from the formula in Theorem
17
. We now verify this nonnegativity.

Proposition 5
.

The Clark measure
\mu^{B\alpha^{*}}
is nonnegative.

Proof.

Note that the nonnegativity of the measure for
\alpha=\pm 1
comes immediately from Theorem
17
.

Using the product definition of the sine function,

\sin(x)=x\prod_{k=1}^{\infty}\left[1-\left(\frac{x}{k\pi}\right)^{2}\right],

our measure becomes

\mu^{B\alpha^{*}}\left(\{s\}\right)=-\frac{s}{\pi\Im(\alpha)(1+s^{2})^{2}}\prod_{k=1}^{\infty}\left[1-\left(\frac{2sa}{k\pi}\right)^{2}\right].

Observe that the nonnegativity of this function depends on the signs of
\Im(\alpha)
and
s
and the number of negative terms in the product. We will now show that the number of negative terms in this product is finite and depends on the signs of
\Im(\alpha)
and
s
, leaving us with only four cases to consider rather than eight.

For the moment fix
\alpha\in\mathbb{T}
with
\Im(\alpha)>0
and further suppose that
s>0
. Since
s
is monotone with respect to the argument of
\alpha
, there exists an odd number
N\in\mathbb{N}
such that

N\frac{\pi}{2a}<s<(N+1)\frac{\pi}{2a}.

Noting that the terms of the product are increasing in the index variable
k
and that the
k
-th term of the product is negative when
|s|>k\pi/(2a)
, we have that an odd number (specifically the first N) of terms of the product are negative. Therefore, the product is negative, which implies that
\mu^{B\alpha^{*}}
is nonnegative on the set
P_{\alpha}\cap[0,\infty)
and therefore nonnegative on
[0,\infty)
by Theorem
16
.

Analogous logic can be used to determine the number of negative terms in the product for the other three cases for the sign of signs of
\Im(\alpha)
and
s
. Specifically, the product will have an even number of negative terms if one of, but not both,
\Im(\alpha)
and
s
are negative and the product will have an odd number of negative terms if both
\Im(\alpha)
and
s
are negative.
∎

6.2
Second Derivative on Interval

As was done in previous examples, we start the process of obtaining spectral information about the self-adjoint extensions of
L_{2}
by determining its deficiency indices. To do so, we first look at the eigenvalue equation

L_{2}f:=-f^{\prime\prime}=wf

on
L^{2}(-a,a)
. This has two solutions,
f(w;x)=e^{i\sqrt{w}x}
and
f(w;x)=e^{-i\sqrt{w}x}
. Therefore, we have basis functions for the defect spaces given by

\phi_{2,k}(w;x)=e^{(-1)^{k}i\sqrt{w}x},

for
k=1,2
. Specifically, substituting
w\in\mathbb{C}_{+}
into
\{\phi_{2,k}\}_{k=1}^{2}
will give a basis for
\mathcal{D}_{+}
and doing the same with
w\in\mathbb{C}_{-}
will give a basis for
\mathcal{D}_{-}
. Hence,
L_{2}
has deficiency indices
(2,2)
, which further implies
\alpha\in\mathscr{U}_{2}
. Note that this parameterization is equivalent to applying boundary conditions via Theorem
14
.
Next, we generate orthonormalized bases for
w=\pm i
yields

\tilde{\phi}_{2,k}(\pm i;x)=\mathcal{C}_{k,1}e^{i\sqrt{\pm i}x}+\mathcal{C}_{k,2}e^{-i\sqrt{\pm i}x},

where

\begin{array}[]{rclrcl}\mathcal{C}_{1,1}&=&\sqrt{\frac{\sqrt{2}}{\sinh\left(\sqrt{2}a\right)}},&\mathcal{C}_{1,2}&=&0,\\
\mathcal{C}_{2,1}&=&\left(\frac{1}{\mathcal{C}_{1,1}^{2}}\left(1+\frac{1}{\sinh(a)}\right)-2\sqrt{\sinh(a)}\right)^{-1/2},&\mathcal{C}_{2,2}&=&-\frac{\mathcal{C}_{2,1}}{\sqrt{\sinh(a)}}.\end{array}

Again, we will use Equation (
3.1
) to compute the elements of the analytic Gram matrix. Taking advantage of symmetries in the fact that all four matrix entries have a common form, we have

\displaystyle A(w,\pm i)_{j,\ell}

\displaystyle=\langle e^{(-1)^{j}i\sqrt{w}x},\mathcal{C}_{\ell,1}e^{i\sqrt{\pm i}x}+\mathcal{C}_{\ell,2}e^{-i\sqrt{\pm i}x}\rangle_{(-a,a)}

\displaystyle=\overline{\mathcal{C}}_{\ell,1}\langle e^{(-1)^{j}i\sqrt{w}x},e^{i\sqrt{\pm i}x}\rangle_{(-a,a)}

\displaystyle\quad+\overline{\mathcal{C}}_{\ell,2}\langle e^{(-1)^{j}i\sqrt{w}x},e^{-i\sqrt{\pm i}x}\rangle_{(-a,a)}

\displaystyle=\overline{\mathcal{C}}_{\ell,1}\int_{-a}^{a}e^{\left((-1)^{j}i\sqrt{w}+\overline{i\sqrt{\pm i}}\right)x}dx

\displaystyle\quad+\overline{\mathcal{C}}_{\ell,2}\int_{-a}^{a}e^{\left((-1)^{j}i\sqrt{w}+\overline{-i\sqrt{\pm i}}\right)x}dx

\displaystyle=\overline{\mathcal{C}}_{\ell,1}\frac{\sinh\left(\left((-1)^{j}i\sqrt{w}+\overline{i\sqrt{\pm i}}\right)a\right)}{(-1)^{j}i\sqrt{w}+\overline{i\sqrt{\pm i}}}

\displaystyle\quad+\overline{\mathcal{C}}_{\ell,2}\frac{\sinh\left(\left((-1)^{j}i\sqrt{w}+\overline{-i\sqrt{\pm i}}\right)a\right)}{(-1)^{j}i\sqrt{w}+\overline{-i\sqrt{\pm i}}}.

Since this is a rank-two case, the Livšic characteristic function is given by Equation (
5.4
)-(
5.5
). That is,

B(w)=\frac{w-i}{g(w)}\begin{pmatrix}A_{2,2}^{+}A_{1,1}^{-}-A_{1,2}^{+}A_{2,1}^{-}&A_{2,2}^{+}A_{1,2}^{-}-A_{1,2}^{+}A_{2,2}^{-}\\
-A_{2,1}^{+}A_{1,1}^{-}+A_{1,1}^{+}A_{2,1}^{-}&-A_{2,1}^{+}A_{1,2}^{-}+A_{1,1}^{+}A_{2,2}^{-}\end{pmatrix},

where

g(w)=(w+i)(A_{1,1}^{+}A_{2,2}^{+}-A_{1,2}^{+}A_{2,1}^{+}),

with
A(w,\pm i)_{j,\ell}
abbreviated as
A_{j,\ell}^{\pm}
.
This leads us to the following spectral result.

Theorem 18
.

The spectral measure of the operator
L_{2,\alpha}
has point spectra at
s\in\mathbb{R}
if the matrix
I-B(w)\alpha^{*}
has nontrivial kernel in the limit as
w\in\mathbb{C}_{+}
approaches
s
non-tangentially.

Proof.

This follows almost immediately from Equation (
3.5
) provided that
B(w)
is defined in the limit.
B
is well-defined in this limit because the denominators of
A(w,\pm i)_{j,\ell}
are never zero since
(-1)^{j+1}i\sqrt{s}
is either purely imaginary (if
s>0
), purely real (if
s<0
), or zero (if s=0) and
\overline{\pm i\sqrt{\pm i}}
always has both both real and imaginary part nonzero.

Also notice that (
5.4
) implies that the case
\lim_{w\downarrow s}g(w)=0
will not produce any eigenvalues.
∎
