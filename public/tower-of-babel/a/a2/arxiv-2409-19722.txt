The Vanilla Sequent Calculus is Call-by-Value
Beniamino Accattoli

Abstract
Existing Curry-Howard interpretations of call-by-value evaluation for the λ \lambda -calculus are either based on ad-hoc modifications of intuitionistic proof systems or involve additional logical concepts such as classical logic or linear logic, despite the fact that call-by-value was introduced in an intuitionistic setting without linear features. This paper shows that the most basic sequent calculus for minimal intuitionistic logic—dubbed here vanilla —can naturally be seen as a logical interpretation of call-by-value evaluation. This is obtained by establishing mutual simulations with a well-known formalism for call-by-value evaluation.

1
Introduction

The connection between functional languages and proof theory stems from Howard’s insight that the system of simple types for the
\lambda
-calculus is exactly Gentzen’s natural deduction for minimal intuitionistic logic
[
40
]
. Additionally,
\beta
-reduction
exactly matches the logical process of
detour elimination
, also called
normalization
. This correspondence concerns the unrestricted, or
call-by-name
, notion of
\beta
-reduction.

In practice, the evaluation mechanism at work in functional languages never follows that of call-by-name of the ordinary
\lambda
-calculus. Plotkin’s call-by-value
\lambda
-calculus
[
51
]
restricts
\beta
-reduction to fire when the argument is a
value
, that is, a variable or an abstraction, and it is often seen as a better fit for applications.

Call-by-Value and Natural Deduction.

Natural deduction, unfortunately, does not provide a solid logical foundation for call-by-value (shortened to CbV). On the positive side, values are proofs ending in a logical introduction rule. The restriction to values in the proof normalization process, however, has to be
enforced
; it does not arise naturally from the structure of natural deduction. Moreover, when one considers open terms and/or strong evaluation (that is, under abstraction), the normal forms of Plotkin’s CbV calculus have a complex inductive structure not corresponding to any natural concept in natural deduction.
The study of strong evaluation is essential for proof assistants based on dependent types. Notably, Leroy and Grégoire use strong CbV for Coq
[
36
]
. Strong evaluation is also advocated by Scherer and Rémy
[
57
]
as relevant for a solid theory of functional languages.
Lastly, in proof theory one speaks of
cut/detour elimination
, and they can be eliminated only if evaluation goes under abstractions.

The issue with normal forms is delicate. It is well-known that the rewriting rules of Plotkin’s calculus are not suited for open terms and strong evaluation, causing
premature normal forms
, as pointed out by Paolini and Ronchi della Rocca
[
50
,
49
,
55
]
. The literature contains many alternative CbV
\lambda
-calculi fixing this defect, as surveyed by Accattoli and Guerrieri
[
5
]
, often extending the syntax of the
\lambda
-calculus with
\mathsf{let}
-expressions or explicit substitutions, as for instance is the case in Moggi’s calculus
[
45
,
46
]
.

Logically, both
\mathsf{let}
-expressions and explicit substitutions are decorations for the (intuitionistic) cut rule. Even when one considers these alternative CbV
\lambda
-calculi not suffering of premature normal forms, natural deduction with cut is not really a solid logical foundation, because CbV
normal
terms correspond to proofs
with cuts
. That is,
not all cuts are eliminable
.

Call-by-Value and Proof Theory.

Beyond natural deduction for minimal intuitionistic logic (shortened to MIL), there are two main ways of looking at CbV via proof theory. One of them is as a certain intuitionistic fragment of linear logic, as first done by Girard
[
34
]
, which can also be seen as a certain embedding into the modal logic S4, as pointed out recently by Espírito Santo et al.
[
29
]
. Another way is via the duality between CbV and call-by-name (CbN) in classical logic, usually traced back to Filinski
[
30
]
. This duality was then analyzed through linear logic lenses by Danos et al.
[
20
]
, and crystalized as the computational interpretation of a sequent calculus by Curien and Herbelin
[
17
]
.

What is not ideal, however, is that these are
additional logical concepts
. In Plotkin’s CbV (and in many of the alternative CbV
\lambda
-calculi), indeed, there is no trace of linearity nor of classical principles. It is natural to wonder whether there is a neat logical way of modeling CbV using
only
MIL. There are works connecting CbV and MIL, such as Dyckhoff and Lengrand’s study of Herbelin’s LJQ
[
39
,
24
,
25
]
or Ohori
[
48
]
, but they are based on modifications of the deductive system
imposing
the CbV reading. This paper presents a fresh perspective on this question.

The Vanilla Sequent Calculus.

Our result is that the most basic presentation of Gentzen’s sequent calculus
[
33
]
for MIL, here dubbed
vanilla sequent calculus
and shown in Fig.
1
(the proper additive presentation of MIL requires some further details about contractions, see Sect.
3
), is a natural Curry-Howard reading of CbV evaluation. The system comes with a natural notion of
values
, which are proofs ending with right deduction rules. Crucially, we exploit a
splitting property
that is specific to sequent proofs of MIL: every proof of
\Gamma\vdash A
can be uniquely split into a value sub-proof of
\Delta\vdash A
followed by a sequence of
left
rules turning
\Delta
into
\Gamma
without touching
A
. Thanks to this feature, there is no need to modify the sequent calculus with a so-called
stoup
(i.e. a distinguished formula in a formula context) as in Curien and Herbelin
[
17
]
, adding restrictions on the shape of proofs as in Herbelin’s presentation of LJQ
[
39
]
or Ohori
[
48
]
, or having a separate judgement for values as in Lengrand and Dyckhoff’s presentation of LJQ
[
24
,
25
]
. Essentially, values are recovered on-the-fly, with no need to mark them out explicitly.

Beyond the splitting property, our fresh perspective stems from the observation that the ineliminable cuts in the CbV reading of natural deduction correspond exactly to occurrences of the left rule
(\Rightarrow_{l})
for
\Rightarrow
in the vanilla sequent calculus. In other words, the cut rule in CbV natural deduction is
overloaded
. When one looks at it through the vanilla sequent calculus, it represents
both
the vanilla
(\mathtt{cut})
and
(\Rightarrow_{l})
rules. Disentangling the two concepts, the vanilla sequent calculus provides a neater logical foundation where
CbV normal terms have no cuts
, as one would expect.

Concretely, we adopt proof terms for the vanilla sequent calculus defined without the application
ts
construct (which is specific to natural deduction) and with
distinct
explicit substitutions constructs for rules
(\mathtt{cut})
and
(\Rightarrow_{l})
, what we dub here
vanilla
\lambda
-terms
.

Cut Elimination at a Distance: the Vanilla
\lambda
-Calculus.

Once it is clarified that cut-free vanilla proofs are a good formalism for CbV normal forms, one needs to pair them with a natural notion of cut elimination. Quoting Zucker
[
61
]
: "we know at the outset what a cut-free derivation is, and the problem is to define the conversions".

For that, we give a very compact definition of cut elimination following the
at a distance
style of rewriting rules promoted by Accattoli and Kesner for natural deduction calculi
[
9
,
3
,
41
]
, and recently shown to be a good fit for the intuitionistic linear logic sequent calculus (without stoup) by Accattoli
[
1
]
. To avoid misunderstandings, note that, while rewriting at a distance is also an additional concept, it is fundamentally different from linear or classical logic. Linear and classical logic are
logical
concepts; they change the logic. Rewriting at a distance is a
rewriting
concept, not a logical one; the logic stays the same, what changes is only how proofs/terms are rewritten.

The key point of rewriting at a distance is that it avoids the many rewriting rules propagating explicit substitutions through the term structure, which are found in many extended
\lambda
-calculi, via generalizations of the rewriting rules exploiting contexts (that is, terms with a hole). This is particularly useful in the study of sequent calculi, where commuting cut elimination cases are a notorious burden. Essentially, rewriting at a distance allows one to have only
principal
cut elimination cases, avoiding completely the commutative ones. This is similar to what happens with proof nets, except that it is simpler, because the graphical language is avoided altogether by means of—again—contexts.

Rules at a distance rest on
on-the-fly
decompositions of a term into a context and a sub-term. In our setting, this decomposition becomes the splitting property mentioned above. Distance thus allows for a smooth treatment of values at the rewriting level.

Our proof terms plus cut elimination at a distance form what we dub as the
vanilla
\lambda
-calculus
.

Results.

The central result of the paper is that two standard translations, from natural deduction to sequent calculus, and back, induce termination-preserving simulations between the vanilla
\lambda
-calculus and one of the formalisms extending Plotkin’s CbV. To make things as smooth as possible, our choice of formalism for CbV is Accattoli and Paolini’s
value substitution calculus

[
12
]
, a natural deduction
\lambda
-calculus with explicit substitutions at a distance, used in various recent studies about CbV
[
5
,
4
,
7
,
8
,
11
]
.

For the sake of keeping this
fresh perspective
light and readable, we focus on explaining the inception of the calculus, rewriting rules at a distance, and the simulation results. As a check of good design for the vanilla
\lambda
-calculus, we also prove strong normalization for typed terms. This result is more challenging than the simulations, but it is expected and proved via an established technique, namely, the bi-orthogonal reducibility method. Therefore, we only give the statements and refer to the Appendix for the details of the proof, which is adapted from Accattoli
[
1
]
.

How Our Approach Fits in the Literature.

Certainly, ours is not the first computational interpretation of a sequent calculus for MIL, whether
vanilla
or not. Essentially, there are three kinds of interpretations in the literature. For the first two, we adopt Mint’s local / global terminology
[
43
]
:

1.

The local approach
. It decorates sequent proofs using a language of terms
without application
and with
\mathsf{let}
-expressions (or explicit substitutions) for both the cut rule and the left rule for
\Rightarrow
, as we do. In particular, this approach does
not
use ordinary
\lambda
-terms to decorate proofs. These alternative terms are then endowed with (usually many) rewriting rules mimicking the
propagation
of cuts (that is, they are not at a distance). This approach is followed e.g. by Gallier
[
32
]
, Ohori
[
48
]
, Dyckhoff and Lengrand
[
24
,
25
]
, and Cerrito and Kesner
[
15
]
. Gallier and Cerrito and Kesner do not notice the connection with CbV, while Ohori and Dyckhoff and Lengrand do embrace it, but use non-standard sequent calculi and complex sets of rewriting rules. Gallier (1993) revisits standard proof theoretical concepts under the influence of linear logic. Cerrito and Kesner (2004) develop a Curry-Howard for pattern matching. Ohori’s (1999) aim is to show a connection between proof theory and Sabry and Felleisen’s
A-normal forms

[
56
,
31
]
. This paper can be seen as a re-elaboration of Ohori’s work, where the focus is a minimalistic logical foundation rather than A-normal forms. Our aim is also similar to Dyckhoff and Lengrand
[
25
]
(2007) but the outcome is considerably simpler: they have three notions of cut and 14 rewriting rules, while we have only one cut and one rewriting rule.

2.

The global approach
. It amounts to decorate sequent proofs with ordinary
\lambda
-terms and endowing them with call-by-name rewriting. It is rooted in Prawitz’s
many-to-one
translation of sequent calculus to natural deduction
[
53
]
, it possibly first appears with proof terms in Pottinger
[
52
]
, and it is nicely presented by Barendregt and Ghilezan using the vanilla sequent calculus
[
14
]
. The idea is to decorate rules
(\mathtt{cut})
and
(\Rightarrow_{l})
using meta-level substitution, as follows:

\Gamma\vdash s\,{:}\,A

\Gamma,x\,{:}\,A\vdash t\,{:}\,B

\mathtt{cut}

\Gamma\vdash\{s{\shortrightarrow}x\}t\,{:}\,B

\Gamma\vdash s\,{:}\,A

\Gamma,x\,{:}\,B\vdash t\,{:}\,C

\Rightarrow_{l}

\Gamma,y\,{:}\,A\Rightarrow B\vdash\{ys{\shortrightarrow}x\}t\,{:}\,C

The serious drawback of the global approach is the potential
size mismatch
between a sequent calculus proof
\pi
and the
\lambda
-term
u_{\pi}
decorating
\pi
, stemming from the fact that in the sequent calculus the formula
B
in
(\Rightarrow_{l})
can be treated non-linearly, that is, it can be weakened or contracted, leading to duplications/erasures of
ys
. The non-linear use of
B
is referred to as
the root of all evil
by Danos et al.
[
20
]
, in their study of representations of System F in linear sequent calculi.

3.

The stoup approach.
A third approach departs from the vanilla sequent calculus, adopting a form of enriched sequent or adding further judgements to the deductive system. Typically, at the logical level it uses
two
judgements
\Gamma;\vdash A
and
\Gamma;B\vdash A
, where the space between the semicolon and
\vdash
is called
stoup
—terminology due to Girard
[
35
]
—and it is either empty or contains a single, distinguished formula. The non-decorated version of rule
(\Rightarrow_{l})
then becomes:

\Gamma;\vdash A

\Gamma;B\vdash C

\Rightarrow_{l}

\Gamma;A\Rightarrow B\vdash C

The key point is that the rules of the system (which can be defined in various ways) treat the stoup
linearly
, that is, the stoup formula is never weakened nor contracted. This fact is what circumvents the size mismatch of the global approach, and thus allows for a good match between sequent proofs with stoup and ordinary
\lambda
-terms. It is usually associated with CbN evaluation.

The stoup approach is first studied by Danos et al.
[
20
]
, building on Girard
[
35
]
. It then became the basis for Herbelin
[
38
]
, who studies the intuitionistic CbN case, and then Curien and Herbelin
[
17
]
, who study the classical case in both CbN and CbV, with three judgements and stoup on both sides of the sequent. From
[
17
]
, it is easy to extract a CbV intuitionistic fragment (as done for instance by Accattoli and Guerrieri
[
5
]
), but one obtains a sequent calculus with stoup, not the
vanilla
one. A different presentation of essentially the same system is the mentioned approach to LJQ by Dyckhoff and Lengrand
[
24
,
25
]
, who use two distinct judgements—with one dedicated to values and connected to focalization—rather than the stoup.

Nowadays, a number of works have built over Curien and Herbelin’s work, for instance
[
27
,
60
,
28
,
21
,
13
,
47
,
16
,
23
,
22
,
44
]
, to the point that it became the standard computational interpretation of sequent calculi.

Our work is orthogonal, and somewhat more basic, as it looks at the intuitionistic case without any form of stoup, focalization, or separate judgement.

Non-Canonicity
vs
Sharing.

In the proof theoretical literature, the vanilla sequent calculus is often criticized as
non-canonical
because (CbN) normal
\lambda
-terms have more than one cut-free proof, when one embraces the global approach—this is another face of Danos et al.’s
root of all evil
mentioned above.

A key point of our fresh perspective is that this is a
feature
rather than a drawback: it is simply the fact that CbV normal terms such as
{\sf let}\ x=yz\ {\sf in}\ (wxxx)
can be seen as
shared
representations of CbN normal forms such as
w(yz)(yz)(yz)
, and that a term can be shared in various ways—another one is
{\sf let}\ x=yz\ {\sf in}\ (wxx(yz))
. Therefore, the vanilla sequent calculus gives a first-class status to sub-term sharing for normal forms.

More precisely,
\mathsf{let}
-expressions annotate cuts and give a first-class status to sub-term sharing independently of the proof system. The concept of
value
induces a dynamic / static refinement of sub-term sharing. Dynamically, only values are
unshared
(that is, duplicated or erased). Non-values are never unshared, thus end up being
statically shared
in normal forms, as in
{\sf let}\ x=yz\ {\sf in}\ (wxxx)
, making sub-term sharing visible
denotationally
. These two roles are entangled in natural deduction for MIL while they are handled separately in sequent calculus via its two
left rules
, namely cut captures dynamic sharing and subtraction captures static sharing. Therefore, the left rules of the sequent calculus for MIL encapsulate the sharing features of values.

For an extensive informal discussion about the deep relationship between sharing and CbV see Accattoli’s dissemination paper
[
2
]
.

Routine Definitions: Terms and Contexts.

We assume given a countable set of variables
\mathcal{V}_{\mathtt{N}}
, where
\mathtt{N}
stresses that they are for the natural
\lambda
-calculus. The meta-level capture-avoiding substitution of
s
for
x
in
t
is denoted with
\{s{\shortrightarrow}x\}t
.

Contexts are terms with exactly one occurrence of the
hole

\langle\cdot\rangle
, an additional constant, standing for a removed sub-term. We shall use various notions of contexts. For the
\lambda
-calculus, the most general ones are
(general) contexts

C
, which simply allow the hole to be anywhere. The main operation about contexts is
plugging

C\langle t\rangle
where the hole
\langle\cdot\rangle
in context
C
is replaced by
t
. Plugging, as usual with contexts, can
capture variables—for instance
(\lambda x.\langle\cdot\rangle t)\langle x\rangle=\lambda x.xt
.

Routine Definitions: Types and Derivations.

Types are built out of an unspecified atomic type
X
and the implication
\cdot\Rightarrow\cdot
connective. Type contexts
\Gamma
are implicitly considered modulo exchange, that is,
\Gamma
is a partial function from variables to formulas
such that
\mathtt{dom}(\Gamma):=\{x\mid\Gamma(x)\mbox{ is defined}\}
is finite, usually written as
x_{1}\,{:}\,A_{1},\dots,x_{n}\,{:}\,A_{n}
(with
n\in\mathbb{N}
) if
\mathtt{dom}(\Gamma)\subseteq\{x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\}
and
\Gamma(x_{i})=A_{i}
for
1\leq i\leq n
. As it is standard, writing
\Gamma,x\,{:}\,A
implicitly assumes that
x\notin\mathtt{dom}(\Gamma)
.

We write
\pi\,\triangleright\Gamma\vdash_{\mathtt{N}}t\,{:}\,A
if
\pi
is a (

)
derivation
, that is, a tree constructed using the rules for
\vdash_{\mathtt{N}}
, having axioms as leaves, and of final judgment
\Gamma\vdash_{\mathtt{N}}t\,{:}\,A
.

Rewriting Rules.

We endow natural
\lambda
-terms with ESs with
small-step
rewriting rules, that is, rules based on meta-level substitution, obtaining Accattoli and Kesner’s
substitution calculus
(SC)
[
10
]
, of rewriting relation
\rightarrow_{\mathtt{SC}}

\rightarrow_{\mathtt{s}}
, and the perhaps less standard
\beta

rule

\rightarrow_{\mathtt{dB}}

This kind of rule circumvents the need of having commuting rewriting rules such as

Note that ESs can always be reduced, thus there are no ESs in

Clearly, the substitution calculus simulates

It is well-known that Plotkin’s approach works smoothly only in the important and yet limited case of weak evaluation of closed terms. Open terms are an issue, and even more so is strong evaluation, because they cause

Logically, the issue is that not all detours can be eliminated using Plotkin’s

Advantages of ESs by Value.

The VSC handles open terms correctly thanks to distance and having moved the value restrictions from
\beta
-redexes to substitution redexes. As evidence of correct behavior, let’s have a look at the variant
\Omega_{\mathtt{stuck}}
of
\Omega
that is a premature normal form in Plotkin’s calculus. It now (correctly) diverges in the VSC:

\begin{array}[]{lllllllllll}\Omega_{\mathtt{stuck}}&:=&(\lambda x.\delta)(yz)\delta&\rightarrow_{\mathtt{dB}}&({\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{[}}yz{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\shortrightarrow}}}x{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{]}}\delta)\delta\\

The VSC has a number of operational and denotational good properties, as shown by Accattoli and co-authors in various recent works
[

,
7
,
11

\lambda
-calculus, considerably improving over Plotkin’s presentation. At the same time, the VSC and Plotkin’s calculus induce the same contextual equivalence on closed terms (see
[
7
]
), thus the VSC is a
conservative
refinement of Plotkin’s calculus.

Limits of ESs by Value.

The VSC, however, is not free from glitches. In contrast to the CbN case, in the VSC
not all ESs are eliminable
, because of the value constraint. Namely, an ES such as
{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{[}}yz{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\shortrightarrow}}}x{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{]}}t
cannot be eliminated, since
yz
is normal and not a value. It might seem that this is the same issue that we pointed out for Plotkin’s approach, for which some detours are not eliminable.
The situation however is different: in Plotkin’s case, ineliminable detours break operational and denotational properties, while ineliminable ESs do not cause similar problems in the VSC.

It is important to stress that—in itself—the presence of ineliminable ESs is not a drawback. They actually are a
feature
of CbV and of the VSC. The fact that some ESs are ineliminable, indeed, means that sub-term sharing ends up in normal forms, and thus becomes
denotationally visible
, which is a good thing, since it opens the way to a mathematical understanding of sharing and efficiency. In Ehrhard’s CbV relational denotational model
[
26
]
, for instance,
{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{[}}yz{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\shortrightarrow}}}x{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{]}}(wxx)
and
w(yz)(yz)
have
different
interpretations; they instead have the
same
interpretation in the CbN relational model. An extensive high-level discussion of the relationship between sharing and CbV can be found in Accattoli’s dissemination paper
[
2
]
.

The glitch of the VSC is the fact that its ESs are still typed with cuts, as in the SC. Therefore, if some ESs are ineliminable then some cuts are ineliminable, in the CbV interpretation of natural deduction with cuts. This clearly goes against the expected property of the cut rule, which is
admissibility
, that is, that all cuts are eliminable. What happens in the VSC is that only cuts containing values are eliminable.

Ideally, one would like to have two
separate
constructors, one for eliminable cuts and one for ineliminable cuts. This does not seem to be naturally achievable in natural deduction, where the two are entangled. The aim of this paper is to show that, instead, it is exactly what naturally happens in the sequent calculus.

Translation of Cut-Free Vanilla Terms.

We have not yet introduced rewriting rules for vanilla
\lambda
-terms (that is, cut elimination), but there are no doubts about the expected notion of normal vanilla terms: they must be the cut-free ones. The next proposition states the starting observation of this work, that is, the fact that cut-free vanilla terms are mapped to natural terms that are CbV normal, but not necessarily CbN normal.

Proposition 4.1

Let
\mathtt{t}\in\Lambda_{\mathtt{S}}
be cut-free. Then
\underline{\mathtt{t}}_{\mathtt{N}}
is
\rightarrow_{\mathtt{VSC}}
-normal but not necessarily
\rightarrow_{\mathtt{SC}}
-normal.

Proof

By induction on
\mathtt{t}
. For
\rightarrow_{\mathtt{VSC}}
, simply note that, for cut-free vanilla terms, ESs and applications (which form
\rightarrow_{\mathtt{VSC}}
redexes) are only introduced by the translation of subtractions, and they receive as left sub-terms the application of a free variable to a term: thus the introduced application is not a
\rightarrow_{\mathtt{dB}}
-redex, nor it is a value (nor can reduce to one) thus the introduced ES is not a
\rightarrow_{\mathtt{vs}}
-redex. For
\rightarrow_{\mathtt{SC}}
, the translation of subtraction instead introduces
\rightarrow_{\mathtt{s}}
redexes.∎

In the other direction,
\rightarrow_{\mathtt{VSC}}
-normal natural terms are not mapped by
\overline{\cdot}^{\mathtt{S}}
to cut-free vanilla terms, because the translation of applications adds cuts. It is possible to optimize the translation as to avoid this mismatch, but the optimized translations would considerably complicate the translation of contexts (introduced and used in the next sections), which is why we refrain from it. At the same time, we shall show that the translation of normal natural terms are
almost
cut-free, in a suitable harmless way.

5
Defining Cut Elimination for the Vanilla
\lambda
-Calculus

In this section, we define cut elimination on vanilla
\lambda
-terms, obtaining the
vanilla
\lambda
-calculus
. Our cut elimination shall be based on meta-level substitution. The notion of value shall emerge naturally, and rules at a distance shall play a role.

Left Variable Occurrences.

At first sight, defining small-step cut elimination for vanilla terms is conceptually easy, since one would simply define it as follows:

{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\llbracket}}\mathtt{s}{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\shortrightarrow}}}\mathtt{x}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\rrbracket}}\mathtt{t}\ \ \ \rightarrow_{\mathtt{cut}}\ \ \ \lBrace\mathtt{s}{\shortrightarrow}\mathtt{x}\rBrace\mathtt{t}

for a notion of meta-level substitution
\lBrace\mathtt{s}{\shortrightarrow}\mathtt{x}\rBrace\mathtt{t}
for vanilla
\lambda
-terms.
The problem however is that the definition of
\lBrace\mathtt{s}{\shortrightarrow}\mathtt{x}\rBrace\mathtt{t}
cannot be the same as for natural terms.

In natural deduction, the free occurrences of a variable are introduced by axioms, and axioms can always be replaced by proofs having the same ending sequent. Since we consider axiom as a right rule, let us say that variables have only
right occurrences
, and that on right occurrences one can always define meta-level substitution
\{u{\shortrightarrow}x\}t
simply as the
replacement
of
x
by
u
in
t
.

On vanilla terms, subtractions
{\color[rgb]{0,1,0.5}{\lbrbrak}}\mathtt{y}\,{{\color[rgb]{0,1,0.5}{\rhd}}}\,\mathtt{s}{\color[rgb]{0,1,0.5}{|}}\mathtt{x}{\color[rgb]{0,1,0.5}{\rbrbrak}}\mathtt{t}
introduce
left occurrences
of
y
. In contrast to right occurrences, the left ones cannot be simply replaced by a vanilla term
\mathtt{u}
, because
{\color[rgb]{0,1,0.5}{\lbrbrak}}\mathtt{u}\,{{\color[rgb]{0,1,0.5}{\rhd}}}\,\mathtt{s}{\color[rgb]{0,1,0.5}{|}}\mathtt{x}{\color[rgb]{0,1,0.5}{\rbrbrak}}\mathtt{t}
does
not
belong to the grammar of vanilla terms. Therefore, the definition of meta-level substitution
\lBrace\mathtt{u}{\shortrightarrow}\mathtt{x}\rBrace\mathtt{t}
on vanilla terms is a bit tricky on subtractions.

\mathtt{u}

The Vanilla
\lambda
-Calculus.

For defining small-step cut elimination for all terms, we rely on the crucial observation that
every
vanilla term
\mathtt{t}

splits uniquely
as
\mathtt{t}=\mathtt{L}\langle\mathtt{v}\rangle
, where
\mathtt{L}
is a left context—defined in Fig.
8
—and
\mathtt{v}
is a value. Note that a similar splitting is also used in the value substitution rule
\rightarrow_{\mathtt{vs}}
of the VSC, but there is a key difference: in the VSC applications cannot be split as
L\langle v\rangle
(because application is not a left rule), which is why some ESs cannot be eliminated, while
all
vanilla terms can be split (because application is replaced by subtraction, which is a left rule).

We know how to substitute
\mathtt{v}
, and we are left to define what to do with
\mathtt{L}
. There are two options, namely mimicking the substitution rules by name (namely
\rightarrow_{\mathtt{s}}
) and by value (
\rightarrow_{\mathtt{vs}}
) for natural terms: either we carry
\mathtt{L}
along with
\mathtt{v}
in the propagation of the substitution, possibly duplicating or erasing it, as in the CbN rule
\rightarrow_{\mathtt{s}}
of the SC, or we commute it out, as in the CbV rule
\rightarrow_{\mathtt{vs}}
of the VSC. While in principle both are valid choices, the latter seems more natural because—even if we adopt the CbN approach—cut-free vanilla terms map to CbV normal natural terms, as shown by Proposition
4.1
, and
not
to the CbN ones. Essentially, to catch a CbN semantics we should also modify something about cut-free proofs, but that goes against the starting point of our work, namely that vanilla cut-free proofs are a good representation of CbV normal forms.

The cut elimination rule
\leadsto_{\mathtt{cut}}
is then defined in Fig.
8
by commuting out the left context
\mathtt{L}
, completing the definition of the vanilla
\lambda
-calculus.

Strong Normalization

The typical theorem for Curry-Howard correspondences is that the logical system ensures strong normalization of the typed terms. We thus provide such a result for our new correspondence between the vanilla
\lambda
-calculus and the vanilla sequent calculus.

In this section, we give only the statement, since the proof is too technical for a fresh perspective paper. The proof is developed in Appendix
0.D
and Appendix
0.E
, and is based on the bi-orthogonal reducibility method, adapting and slightly simplifying its presentation in Accattoli
[
1
]
.

Definition 2
(Strong normalization)

A vanilla term
\mathtt{t}
is strongly normalizing, also noted
\mathtt{t}\in\mathrm{SN}_{\mathtt{cut}}
, if
\mathtt{t}\leadsto_{\mathtt{cut}}\mathtt{s}
implies
\mathtt{s}\in\mathrm{SN}_{\mathtt{cut}}
.

(Typable terms are SN)

Let
\mathtt{t}\in\Lambda_{\mathtt{S}}
and
\Gamma\vdash_{\mathtt{S}}\mathtt{t}\,{:}\,A
. Then
\mathtt{t}\in\mathrm{SN}_{\mathtt{cut}}
.

9
Conclusions

We introduced the
vanilla

\lambda
-calculus, a computational interpretation of the simplest sequent calculus, and showed that it simulates and it is simulated by call-by-value evaluation. Technically, the simulations are clean and compact, thanks to the use of rewriting rules at a distance for both the new cut elimination rule for the vanilla
\lambda
-calculus and the presentation of call-by-value evaluation that we adopt from the literature.

Our study nicely complements the two famous cornerstones by Curry and Howard about minimal intuitionistic logic resting only on basic logical concepts:

1.

Curry 1958

[
18
]
: Hilbert’s system matches combinatory logic;

2.

Howard 1969
(but published only in 1980)
[
40
]
: Gentzen’s natural deduction matches the (call-by-name)
\lambda
-calculus;

3.

Here
: Gentzen’s sequent calculus matches call-by-value evaluation.

We believe that our work provides a fresh perspective over the sequent calculus. Its modern computational interpretation is usually defined starting from sequent calculi with stoups for classical logic, following Curien and Herbelin
[
17
]
. The basic,
vanilla
presentation of the intuitionistic case seems to have fallen into a blind spot of the literature. This work shows that it is far from being unworthy of attention.

Future Work.

Maraist et al.
[
42
]
propose a Curry-Howard correspondence for
call-by-need
using an affine logic with a duplication modality, tweaking the linear logic one for call-by-value. In ongoing work, we are using the results of this paper as the starting point for a Curry-Howard for call-by-need that is
not
based on linear/affine concepts.

Appendix 0.D
Rewriting Preliminaries for the Proof of Strong Normalization

A first straightforward property of SN is the following one.

Lemma 0.D.1
(Stability of SN under renaming)

If
\mathtt{t}\in\mathrm{SN}_{\mathtt{cut}}
then
\lBrace\mathtt{x}{\shortrightarrow}\mathtt{y}\rBrace\mathtt{t}\in\mathrm{SN}_{\mathtt{cut}}
for all variables
\mathtt{x},\mathtt{y}\in\mathcal{V}_{\mathtt{S}}
.

Proof

By induction on
\mathtt{t}\in\mathrm{SN}_{\mathtt{cut}}
. Since renaming cannot create, erase, or duplicate redexes,
if
\lBrace\mathtt{x}{\shortrightarrow}\mathtt{y}\rBrace\mathtt{t}\leadsto_{\mathtt{cut}}\mathtt{s}
then there exists
\mathtt{u}
such that
\mathtt{t}\leadsto_{\mathtt{cut}}\mathtt{u}
and

\lBrace\mathtt{x}{\shortrightarrow}\mathtt{y}\rBrace\mathtt{u}=\mathtt{s}
. Then, by
i.h.
on
\mathtt{u}
we obtain
\lBrace\mathtt{x}{\shortrightarrow}\mathtt{y}\rBrace\mathtt{u}=\mathtt{s}\in\mathrm{SN}_{\mathtt{cut}}
.
∎

Structure of This Section.

We are now going to prove three rewriting properties which shall be the main rewriting tools in the proof of SN by the reducibility method developed in the next section. Namely,

1.

Extension
: there shall be two extension properties, that extend SN from root sub-terms to the whole term, in special cases;

2.

Root cut expansion
: this property states that if the reduct of a root cut is SN then SN lifts to the term before reducing the cut.

3.

Structural stability
: we shall introduce a notion of structural equivalence for vanilla terms and prove that it preserves SN.

The first two properties can be found (under various names) in
all
proofs of SN, often without specific emphasis. The third one is characteristic of calculi with some form of explicit substitutions and rewriting rules at a distance.

Extension.

The
extension
property is the easy fact that, for abstractions and subtractions, SN follows from SN of the root sub-terms.

Lemma 0.D.2
(Neutral extension)

Let
\mathtt{t},\mathtt{s}\in\mathrm{SN}_{\mathtt{cut}}
.
Then
\lambda\mathtt{x}.\mathtt{t}
and
{\color[rgb]{0,1,0.5}{\lbrbrak}}\mathtt{x}\,{{\color[rgb]{0,1,0.5}{\rhd}}}\,\mathtt{s}{\color[rgb]{0,1,0.5}{|}}\mathtt{y}{\color[rgb]{0,1,0.5}{\rbrbrak}}\mathtt{t}
are in
\mathrm{SN}_{\mathtt{cut}}
.

Proof

By induction on
\mathtt{t}\in\mathrm{SN}_{\mathtt{cut}}
for
\lambda\mathtt{x}.\mathtt{t}
and by induction on
(\mathtt{t}\in\mathrm{SN}_{\mathtt{cut}},\mathtt{s}\in\mathrm{SN}_{\mathtt{cut}})
for
{\color[rgb]{0,1,0.5}{\lbrbrak}}\mathtt{x}\,{{\color[rgb]{0,1,0.5}{\rhd}}}\,\mathtt{s}{\color[rgb]{0,1,0.5}{|}}\mathtt{y}{\color[rgb]{0,1,0.5}{\rbrbrak}}\mathtt{t}
. In both cases, one shows that all reducts are in
\mathrm{SN}_{\mathtt{cut}}
, which follows immediately from the
i.h.
, because there cannot be interaction between the immediate sub-terms.∎

Similarly, when we extend a term
\mathtt{t}
with a left contexts
\mathtt{L}
that captures no variables of
\mathtt{t}
, SN follows from SN of
\mathtt{t}
and
\mathtt{L}
.

Lemma 0.D.3
(Disjoint left context extension)

Let
\mathtt{t}\in\mathrm{SN}_{\mathtt{cut}}
and
\mathtt{L}
be a left context such that:

1.

\mathtt{L}
does not captures variables in
\mathtt{fv}(\mathtt{t})
, and;

2.

\mathtt{L}\langle\mathtt{x}\rangle\in\mathrm{SN}_{\mathtt{cut}}
for a variable
\mathtt{x}
not captured by
\mathtt{L}
.

Then,
\mathtt{L}\langle\mathtt{t}\rangle\in\mathrm{SN}_{\mathtt{cut}}
.

Proof

By induction on
(\mathtt{t}\in\mathrm{SN}_{\mathtt{cut}},\mathtt{L}\langle\mathtt{x}\rangle\in\mathrm{SN}_{\mathtt{cut}})
. We look at the reducts of
\mathtt{L}\langle\mathtt{t}\rangle
:

•

\mathtt{L}\langle\mathtt{t}\rangle\leadsto_{\mathtt{cut}}\mathtt{L}\langle\mathtt{t}^{\prime}\rangle
because
\mathtt{t}\leadsto_{\mathtt{cut}}\mathtt{t}^{\prime}
. By
i.h.
(1st component),
\mathtt{L}\langle\mathtt{t}^{\prime}\rangle\in\mathrm{SN}_{\mathtt{cut}}
.

•

\mathtt{L}\langle\mathtt{t}\rangle\leadsto_{\mathtt{cut}}\mathtt{L}^{\prime}\langle\mathtt{t}\rangle
because
\mathtt{L}\langle\mathtt{x}\rangle\leadsto_{\mathtt{cut}}\mathtt{L}^{\prime}\langle\mathtt{x}\rangle
. By
i.h.
(2nd component),
\mathtt{L}^{\prime}\langle\mathtt{t}\rangle\in\mathrm{SN}_{\mathtt{cut}}
.

•

There are no other possible reducts, because by hypothesis
\mathtt{L}
does not captures variables in
\mathtt{fv}(\mathtt{t})
, and so
\mathtt{L}
and
\mathtt{t}
cannot interact.∎

Substitutivity.

For proving the second main rewriting property, namely
root cut expansion

Lemma 0.D.4

1.

If
\mathtt{t}\leadsto_{\mathtt{cut}}\mathtt{t}^{\prime}

\lBrace\mathtt{v}{\shortrightarrow}\mathtt{x}\rBrace\mathtt{t}\leadsto_{\mathtt{cut}}\lBrace\mathtt{v}{\shortrightarrow}\mathtt{x}\rBrace\mathtt{t}^{\prime}

2.

If
\mathtt{t}\leadsto_{\mathtt{cut}}\mathtt{t}^{\prime}
then
\lBrace\mathtt{s}{\shortrightarrow}\mathtt{x}\rBrace\mathtt{t}\leadsto_{\mathtt{cut}}\lBrace\mathtt{s}{\shortrightarrow}\mathtt{x}\rBrace\mathtt{t}^{\prime}

3.

If
\mathtt{v}\leadsto_{\mathtt{cut}}\mathtt{v^{\prime}}
then
\lBrace\mathtt{v}{\shortrightarrow}\mathtt{x}\rBrace\mathtt{t}\leadsto_{\mathtt{cut}}^{*}\lBrace\mathtt{v^{\prime}}{\shortrightarrow}\mathtt{x}\rBrace\mathtt{t}
.

4.

If
\mathtt{s}\leadsto_{\mathtt{cut}}\mathtt{s}^{\prime}
then
\lBrace\mathtt{s}{\shortrightarrow}\mathtt{x}\rBrace\mathtt{t}\leadsto_{\mathtt{cut}}^{*}\lBrace\mathtt{s}^{\prime}{\shortrightarrow}\mathtt{x}\rBrace\mathtt{t}
.

Point 1 is an easy induction on
\mathtt{t}\leadsto_{\mathtt{cut}}\mathtt{t}^{\prime}

\mathtt{t}
. For Point 4, let
\mathtt{s}=\mathtt{L}\langle\mathtt{v}\rangle
. There are various cases, depending on the step in
\mathtt{s}

•

Step in
\mathtt{L}

\mathtt{s}=\mathtt{L}\langle\mathtt{v}\rangle\leadsto_{\mathtt{cut}}\mathtt{L}^{\prime}\langle\mathtt{v}\rangle=\mathtt{s}^{\prime}

\begin{array}[]{r\hcolspace c\hcolspace l\hcolspace c\hcolspace l\hcolspace c\hcolspace l}\lBrace\mathtt{L}\langle\mathtt{v}\rangle{\shortrightarrow}\mathtt{x}\rBrace\mathtt{t}&=&\mathtt{L}\langle\lBrace\mathtt{v}{\shortrightarrow}\mathtt{x}\rBrace\mathtt{t}\rangle\\

•

Step in
\mathtt{v}

\mathtt{s}=\mathtt{L}\langle\mathtt{v}\rangle\leadsto_{\mathtt{cut}}\mathtt{L}\langle\mathtt{v^{\prime}}\rangle=\mathtt{s}^{\prime}
. Then:

\begin{array}[]{r\hcolspace c\hcolspace l\hcolspace c\hcolspace l\hcolspace c\hcolspace l}\lBrace\mathtt{L}\langle\mathtt{v}\rangle{\shortrightarrow}\mathtt{x}\rBrace\mathtt{t}&=&\mathtt{L}\langle\lBrace\mathtt{v}{\shortrightarrow}\mathtt{x}\rBrace\mathtt{t}\rangle\\

•

Step involving both
\mathtt{L}
and
\mathtt{v}
, that is,
\mathtt{L}=\mathtt{L}^{\prime}\langle{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\llbracket}}\mathtt{L}^{\prime\prime}\langle\mathtt{v^{\prime}}\rangle{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\shortrightarrow}}}\mathtt{x}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\rrbracket}}\mathtt{L}^{\prime\prime\prime}\rangle

\begin{array}[]{r\hcolspace c\hcolspace l\hcolspace c\hcolspace l\hcolspace c\hcolspace l}\mathtt{s}&=&\mathtt{L}^{\prime}\langle{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\llbracket}}\mathtt{L}^{\prime\prime}\langle\mathtt{v^{\prime}}\rangle{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\shortrightarrow}}}\mathtt{y}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\rrbracket}}\mathtt{L}^{\prime\prime\prime}\langle\mathtt{v}\rangle\rangle&\leadsto_{\mathtt{cut}}&\mathtt{L}^{\prime}\langle\mathtt{L}^{\prime\prime}\langle\lBrace\mathtt{v^{\prime}}{\shortrightarrow}\mathtt{y}\rBrace\mathtt{L}^{\prime\prime\prime}\langle\mathtt{v}\rangle\rangle\rangle&=&\mathtt{s}^{\prime}.\end{array}

Let
\mathtt{L}^{\bullet}:=\lBrace\mathtt{v^{\prime}}{\shortrightarrow}\mathtt{y}\rBrace\mathtt{L}^{\prime\prime\prime}

\mathtt{s}^{\prime}=\mathtt{L}^{\prime}\langle\mathtt{L}^{\prime\prime}\langle\mathtt{L}^{\bullet}\langle\lBrace\mathtt{v^{\prime}}{\shortrightarrow}\mathtt{y}\rBrace\mathtt{v}\rangle\rangle\rangle

Then:

\begin{array}[]{r\hcolspace c\hcolspace l\hcolspace c\hcolspace l}\lBrace\mathtt{L}\langle\mathtt{v}\rangle{\shortrightarrow}\mathtt{x}\rBrace\mathtt{t}&=&\lBrace\mathtt{L}^{\prime}\langle{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\llbracket}}\mathtt{L}^{\prime\prime}\langle\mathtt{v^{\prime}}\rangle{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\shortrightarrow}}}\mathtt{y}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\rrbracket}}\mathtt{L}^{\prime\prime\prime}\langle\mathtt{v}\rangle\rangle{\shortrightarrow}\mathtt{x}\rBrace\mathtt{t}\\

The

\mathrm{SN}_{\mathtt{cut}}

Proposition 0.D.1
(Root cut expansion)

If
\mathtt{s}\in\mathrm{SN}_{\mathtt{cut}}
and

0.D.1
Structural Equivalence

The proof of strong normalization shall exploit
structural equivalence
, which is standard and pervasive concept in
\lambda
-calculi with explicit substitutions and rewriting rules at a distance. In the vanilla
\lambda
-calculus, structural equivalence allows one to displace left rules everywhere but inside values. To define it, we need the notion of
weak contexts
, which are contexts whose hole can appear everywhere but inside abstractions.

Definition 3
(Weak contexts)

Weak contexts are defined by the following grammar:

\begin{array}[]{r\colspace rllllll}\textsc{Weak contexts}&\mathtt{W}&::=&\langle\cdot\rangle\mid{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\llbracket}}\mathtt{t}{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\shortrightarrow}}}\mathtt{x}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\rrbracket}}\mathtt{W}\mid{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\llbracket}}\mathtt{W}{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\shortrightarrow}}}\mathtt{x}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\rrbracket}}\mathtt{t}\mid{\color[rgb]{0,1,0.5}{\lbrbrak}}y\,{{\color[rgb]{0,1,0.5}{\rhd}}}\,\mathtt{t}{\color[rgb]{0,1,0.5}{|}}\mathtt{x}{\color[rgb]{0,1,0.5}{\rbrbrak}}\mathtt{W}\mid{\color[rgb]{0,1,0.5}{\lbrbrak}}\mathtt{y}\,{{\color[rgb]{0,1,0.5}{\rhd}}}\,\mathtt{W}{\color[rgb]{0,1,0.5}{|}}\mathtt{x}{\color[rgb]{0,1,0.5}{\rbrbrak}}\mathtt{t}\end{array}

Definition 4
(Structural equivalence)

Let
\mathtt{dom}(\mathtt{W})
be the set of variables captured by
\mathtt{W}
. Root structural equivalence

is defined as follows:

\begin{array}[]{r\hcolspace c\hcolspace l\colspace l}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\llbracket}}\mathtt{s}{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\shortrightarrow}}}\mathtt{x}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\rrbracket}}\mathtt{W}\langle\mathtt{t}\rangle&\sim&\mathtt{W}\langle{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\llbracket}}\mathtt{s}{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\shortrightarrow}}}\mathtt{x}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\rrbracket}}\mathtt{t}\rangle&\mathtt{x}\notin\mathtt{fv}(\mathtt{W}),\,\mathtt{fv}(\mathtt{s})\cap\mathtt{dom}(\mathtt{W})=\emptyset\\
{\color[rgb]{0,1,0.5}{\lbrbrak}}\mathtt{y}\,{{\color[rgb]{0,1,0.5}{\rhd}}}\,\mathtt{s}{\color[rgb]{0,1,0.5}{|}}\mathtt{x}{\color[rgb]{0,1,0.5}{\rbrbrak}}\mathtt{W}\langle\mathtt{t}\rangle&\sim&\mathtt{W}\langle{\color[rgb]{0,1,0.5}{\lbrbrak}}\mathtt{y}\,{{\color[rgb]{0,1,0.5}{\rhd}}}\,\mathtt{s}{\color[rgb]{0,1,0.5}{|}}\mathtt{x}{\color[rgb]{0,1,0.5}{\rbrbrak}}\mathtt{t}\rangle&\mathtt{x}\notin\mathtt{fv}(\mathtt{W}),\,(\mathtt{fv}(\mathtt{s})\cup\{\mathtt{y}\})\cap\mathtt{dom}(\mathtt{W})=\emptyset\end{array}

Definition 7
(Formula sets and seeds)

Let

•

Lemma 0.E.5
(Implicative seeds are non-empty)

If

\llbracket B\rrbracket

Let
\mathtt{x}\neq\mathtt{y}

{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\llbracket}}\mathtt{s}{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\shortrightarrow}}}\mathtt{x}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\rrbracket}}\mathtt{y}\in\llbracket B\rrbracket
for every
\mathtt{s}\in\llbracket A\rrbracket
, that is,
\mathtt{E}\mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\kern-2.72221pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\llbracket}}\mathtt{s}{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\shortrightarrow}}}\mathtt{x}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\rrbracket}}\mathtt{y}\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\kern-2.72221pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}\in\mathrm{SN}_{\mathtt{cut}}

Since candidates contain variables (Lemma
0.E.4
),
\mathcal{V}_{\mathtt{S}}\subseteq\llbracket B\rrbracket
. By duality,
\mathtt{E}\mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\kern-2.72221pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}\mathtt{y}\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\kern-2.72221pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}\in\mathrm{SN}_{\mathtt{cut}}

\mathtt{s}\in\mathrm{SN}_{\mathtt{cut}}
. Since we can assume that
\mathtt{x}\notin\mathtt{fv}(\mathtt{E}\mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\kern-2.72221pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}\mathtt{y}\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\kern-2.72221pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}})

{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\llbracket}}\mathtt{s}{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\shortrightarrow}}}\mathtt{x}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\rrbracket}}\mathtt{E}\mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\kern-2.72221pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}\mathtt{y}\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\kern-2.72221pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}\in\mathrm{SN}_{\mathtt{cut}}

{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\llbracket}}\mathtt{s}{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\shortrightarrow}}}\mathtt{x}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\rrbracket}}\mathtt{E}\mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\kern-2.72221pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}\mathtt{y}\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\kern-2.72221pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}\equiv\mathtt{E}\mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\kern-2.72221pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\llbracket}}\mathtt{s}{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\shortrightarrow}}}\mathtt{x}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\rrbracket}}\mathtt{y}\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\kern-2.72221pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}\in\mathrm{SN}_{\mathtt{cut}}
.∎

Now, we are ready to prove that formula sets
\llbracket A\rrbracket
are candidates, given by the third point of the next proposition. The proof is simple and yet tricky: by induction on
A
, it uses
Proposition
0.E.1
to prove 2 from 1, and 3 from 2, but it also needs 3 (on sub-formulas) to prove 1.

Proposition 0.E.2
(Formula sets are candidates)

Let
A
be a MIL formula.

1.

Seeds are generators
:
\mathcal{S}_{\llbracket A\rrbracket}
is a generator.

2.

Dual candidates
:
\mathcal{S}_{\llbracket A\rrbracket}^{\bot}
is a candidate.

3.

Candidates
:
\llbracket A\rrbracket
is a candidate.

Proof

We prove the first point, the second follows from the first and Proposition
0.E.1
, the third one follows from the second and Proposition
0.E.1
. By induction on
A
. Cases:

•

Base
, i.e.
A=X
. Note that
\mathcal{S}_{\llbracket X\rrbracket}
contains the variables by definition and that all its elements are normal, thus in
\mathrm{SN}_{\mathtt{cut}}
.

•

Implication
, i.e.
A=B\multimap C
.
By
i.h.
(point 3), both
\llbracket B\rrbracket
and
\llbracket C\rrbracket
are candidates. Then by Lemma
0.E.5
,
\mathcal{S}_{\llbracket B\multimap C\rrbracket}\neq\emptyset
.

Since variables are in
\llbracket B\rrbracket
, if
\lambda\mathtt{x}.\mathtt{u}\in\mathcal{S}_{\llbracket B\multimap C\rrbracket}
then
{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\llbracket}}\mathtt{x}{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\shortrightarrow}}}\mathtt{x}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\rrbracket}}\mathtt{u}\in\llbracket C\rrbracket
. By

(Point 3),
{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\llbracket}}\mathtt{x}{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\shortrightarrow}}}\mathtt{x}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\rrbracket}}\mathtt{u}\in\mathrm{SN}_{\mathtt{cut}}
and so does

.∎

Reducibility and Adequacy.

It is now time to introduce the last key notion, namely
reducible derivations
, and—as it is standard—prove
adequacy
, from which SN shall follow. When applying the bi-orthogonal reducibility method to a calculus with explicit substitutions (here cuts), it is essential to have
two
equivalent formulations of reducibility (one based on generators and one based on candidates), as to use the most convenient one in each case of the proof of adequacy. We define both, and prove their equivalence below.

Another technical detail (induced by the presence of explicit substitution) is that the rigid structure of terms
forces an order between the assignments in the typing context
\Gamma
, that is, it treats
\Gamma
as a

list
rather than as a multi-set. The proof of adequacy then considers that the sequent calculus comes with an
exchange rule, treated by one of the cases.

Definition 8
(Reducible derivations)

Let
\pi\;\triangleright\Gamma\vdash\mathtt{t}\,{:}\,A
be a typing derivation, and
\Gamma=\mathtt{x}_{1}\,{:}\,B_{1},\hbox to6.00006pt{.\hss.},\mathtt{x}_{k}\,{:}\,B_{k}
. Then:

•

\pi
is
value reducible
if

{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\llbracket}}\mathtt{v}_{1}{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\shortrightarrow}}}\mathtt{x}_{1}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\rrbracket}}\ldots{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\llbracket}}\mathtt{v}_{k}{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\shortrightarrow}}}\mathtt{x}_{k}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\rrbracket}}\mathtt{t}\in\llbracket A\rrbracket

for every value

\mathtt{v}_{i}\in\mathcal{S}_{\llbracket B_{i}\rrbracket}
such that the introduced cuts are independent, that is,

\mathtt{fv}(\mathtt{v}_{i})\cap\mathtt{dom}(\Gamma)=\emptyset
.

•

\pi
is
term reducible
if

{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\llbracket}}\mathtt{s}_{1}{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\shortrightarrow}}}\mathtt{x}_{1}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\rrbracket}}\ldots{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\llbracket}}\mathtt{s}_{k}{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\shortrightarrow}}}\mathtt{x}_{k}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\rrbracket}}\mathtt{t}\in\llbracket A\rrbracket

for every term

\mathtt{s}_{i}\in\llbracket B_{i}\rrbracket
such that the introduced cuts are independent, that is,

\mathtt{fv}(\mathtt{s}_{i})\cap\mathtt{dom}(\Gamma)=\emptyset
.

To ease notations, we shorten
{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\llbracket}}\mathtt{v}_{1}{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\shortrightarrow}}}\mathtt{x}_{1}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\rrbracket}}\ldots{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\llbracket}}\mathtt{v}_{k}{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\shortrightarrow}}}\mathtt{x}_{k}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\rrbracket}}\mathtt{t}
to

{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\llbracket}}\mathtt{v}_{i}{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\shortrightarrow}}}\mathtt{x}_{i}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\rrbracket}}_{\Gamma}\mathtt{t}
, and
{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\llbracket}}\mathtt{s}_{1}{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\shortrightarrow}}}\mathtt{x}_{1}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\rrbracket}}\ldots{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\llbracket}}\mathtt{s}_{k}{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\shortrightarrow}}}\mathtt{x}_{k}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\rrbracket}}\mathtt{t}
to
{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\llbracket}}\mathtt{s}_{i}{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\shortrightarrow}}}\mathtt{x}_{i}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\rrbracket}}_{\Gamma}\mathtt{t}
.

Lemma 0.E.6
(Value and term reducibility coincide)

Let
\pi\;\triangleright\Gamma\vdash\mathtt{t}\,{:}\,A
be a type derivation. Then
\pi
is value reducible if and
only if
\pi
is term reducible.

Proof

That term reducibility implies value reducibility is obvious because
\mathcal{S}_{\llbracket B_{i}\rrbracket}\subseteq\llbracket B_{i}\rrbracket
. We prove that value reductibility implies term reducibility. Let
\Gamma=\Pi,\mathtt{x}\,{:}\,B,\Delta
. The hypothesis is:

\begin{array}[]{l\hcolspace l\hcolspace llllll}\mathtt{E}\mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\kern-2.72221pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\llbracket}}\mathtt{v}_{i}{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\shortrightarrow}}}\mathtt{y}_{i}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\rrbracket}}_{\Pi}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\llbracket}}\mathtt{v}{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\shortrightarrow}}}\mathtt{x}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\rrbracket}}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\llbracket}}\mathtt{v}_{j}{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\shortrightarrow}}}\mathtt{z}_{j}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\rrbracket}}_{\Delta}\mathtt{t}\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\kern-2.72221pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}&\in&\mathrm{SN}_{\mathtt{cut}},\end{array}

(2)

for every
\mathtt{v}\in\mathcal{S}_{\llbracket B\rrbracket}
and appropriate
\mathtt{v}_{i}
and
\mathtt{v}_{j}
. We show that we can replace
\mathtt{v}
with
\mathtt{s}\in\llbracket B\rrbracket
, that is, that the following holds:

\begin{array}[]{l\hcolspace l\hcolspace llllll}\mathtt{E}\mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\kern-2.72221pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\llbracket}}\mathtt{v}_{i}{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\shortrightarrow}}}\mathtt{y}_{i}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\rrbracket}}_{\Pi}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\llbracket}}\mathtt{s}{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\shortrightarrow}}}\mathtt{x}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\rrbracket}}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\llbracket}}\mathtt{v}_{j}{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\shortrightarrow}}}\mathtt{z}_{j}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\rrbracket}}_{\Delta}\mathtt{t}\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\kern-2.72221pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}&\in&\mathrm{SN}_{\mathtt{cut}}.\end{array}

(3)

By iterating the reasoning on all other values
\mathtt{v}_{i}
and
\mathtt{v}_{j}
one obtains the statement. Since (
2
) holds for all
\mathtt{v}\in\mathcal{S}_{\llbracket B\rrbracket}
we have that

\mathtt{E}^{\prime}:=\mathtt{E}\mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\kern-2.72221pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\llbracket}}\mathtt{v}_{i}{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\shortrightarrow}}}\mathtt{y}_{i}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\rrbracket}}_{\Pi}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\llbracket}}\langle\cdot\rangle{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\shortrightarrow}}}\mathtt{x}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\rrbracket}}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\llbracket}}\mathtt{v}_{j}{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\shortrightarrow}}}\mathtt{z}_{j}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\rrbracket}}_{\Delta}\mathtt{t}\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\kern-2.72221pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}\in\mathcal{S}_{\llbracket B\rrbracket}^{\bot}=\llbracket B\rrbracket^{\bot}
.
Note that the cuts
{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\llbracket}}\mathtt{v}_{i}{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\shortrightarrow}}}\mathtt{y}_{i}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\rrbracket}}_{\Pi}
and
{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\llbracket}}\mathtt{v}_{j}{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\shortrightarrow}}}\mathtt{z}_{j}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\rrbracket}}_{\Delta}
can be added to the elimination context exactly because they are independent. Now, by duality we obtain
\mathtt{E}^{\prime}\mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\kern-2.72221pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}\mathtt{s}\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\kern-2.72221pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}\in\mathrm{SN}_{\mathtt{cut}}
for every
\mathtt{s}\in\llbracket B\rrbracket
, which is exactly (
3
).∎

From now on, we do not distinguish between value and term reducibility, and simply refer to
reducible derivations
. For the proof of adequacy, we need two auxiliary lemmas. The first one guarantees that the notion of reducible derivations does what it is supposed to do, despite its somewhat convoluted formulation (which is necessary for the proof to go through).

Lemma 0.E.7
(Intended meaning of reducibility)

Let
\pi\;\triangleright\Gamma\vdash\mathtt{t}\,{:}\,A
be a reducible derivation. Then
\mathtt{t}\in\llbracket A\rrbracket\subseteq\mathrm{SN}_{\mathtt{cut}}
.

Proof

Since
\llbracket B\rrbracket
is a candidate (Proposition
0.E.2
), it contains the variables (Lemma
0.E.4
). By reducibility,
{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\llbracket}}\mathtt{x}_{i}{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\shortrightarrow}}}\mathtt{x}_{i}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\rrbracket}}_{\Gamma}\mathtt{u}\in\llbracket B\rrbracket
. Note that
{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\llbracket}}\mathtt{x}_{i}{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\shortrightarrow}}}\mathtt{x}_{i}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\rrbracket}}_{\Gamma}\mathtt{u}\leadsto_{\mathtt{cut}}^{*}\mathtt{u}
, and that
\mathtt{u}\in\llbracket B\rrbracket
because candidates are closed under cut elimination (Lemma
0.E.3
.
3
). Finally, since
\llbracket B\rrbracket
is a candidate,
\llbracket B\rrbracket\subseteq\mathrm{SN}_{\mathtt{cut}}
.∎

The second auxiliary lemma slightly generalizes the root cut expansion property of the previous section to when the cut to expand appears in the hole of an elimination context
\mathtt{E}
, which is how we shall apply it when proving adequacy for reducible derivations.

Lemma 0.E.8
(Generalized root cut expansion)

If
\mathtt{E}\mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\kern-2.72221pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}\lBrace\mathtt{s}{\shortrightarrow}\mathtt{x}\rBrace\mathtt{t}\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\kern-2.72221pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}\in\mathrm{SN}_{\mathtt{cut}}
and
\mathtt{s}\in\mathrm{SN}_{\mathtt{cut}}
then
\mathtt{E}\mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\kern-2.72221pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\llbracket}}\mathtt{s}{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\shortrightarrow}}}\mathtt{x}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\rrbracket}}\mathtt{t}\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\kern-2.72221pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}\in\mathrm{SN}_{\mathtt{cut}}
.

Proof

Let
\mathtt{s}=\mathtt{L}\langle\mathtt{v}\rangle
. Note that:

\begin{array}[]{l\hcolspace l\hcolspace l\hcolspace l\hcolspace lll}\mathtt{E}\mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\kern-2.72221pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}\lBrace\mathtt{s}{\shortrightarrow}\mathtt{x}\rBrace\mathtt{t}\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\kern-2.72221pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}&=&\mathtt{E}\mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\kern-2.72221pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}\mathtt{L}\langle\lBrace\mathtt{v}{\shortrightarrow}\mathtt{x}\rBrace\mathtt{t}\rangle\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\kern-2.72221pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}\\
&\equiv&\mathtt{L}\langle\mathtt{E}\mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\kern-2.72221pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}\lBrace\mathtt{v}{\shortrightarrow}\mathtt{x}\rBrace\mathtt{t}\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\kern-2.72221pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}\rangle\\
&=&\mathtt{L}\langle\lBrace\mathtt{v}{\shortrightarrow}\mathtt{x}\rBrace\mathtt{E}\mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\kern-2.72221pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}\mathtt{t}\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\kern-2.72221pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}\rangle&=&\lBrace\mathtt{s}{\shortrightarrow}\mathtt{x}\rBrace\mathtt{E}\mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\kern-2.72221pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}\mathtt{t}\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\kern-2.72221pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}\end{array}

and that
\lBrace\mathtt{s}{\shortrightarrow}\mathtt{x}\rBrace\mathtt{E}\mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\kern-2.72221pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}\mathtt{t}\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\kern-2.72221pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}\in\mathrm{SN}_{\mathtt{cut}}
by structural stability of SN. By root cut expansion,

\begin{array}[]{l\hcolspace l\hcolspace l\hcolspace l\hcolspace l}\mathtt{t}^{\prime\prime\prime}&:=&\mathtt{E}\mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\kern-2.72221pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\llbracket}}\mathtt{v}_{i}{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\shortrightarrow}}}\mathtt{x}_{i}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\rrbracket}}_{\Delta}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\llbracket}}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\llbracket}}\lBrace\lambda\mathtt{z}.\mathtt{r}{\shortrightarrow}\mathtt{y}\rBrace\mathtt{u}{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\shortrightarrow}}}\mathtt{z}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\rrbracket}}\mathtt{r}{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\shortrightarrow}}}\mathtt{x}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\rrbracket}}\lBrace\lambda\mathtt{z}.\mathtt{r}{\shortrightarrow}\mathtt{y}\rBrace\mathtt{s}\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\kern-2.72221pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}&\in&\mathrm{SN}_{\mathtt{cut}}.\end{array}

And that
\mathtt{t}^{\prime\prime\prime}
can be written as follows:

\begin{array}[]{l\hcolspace l\hcolspace l}\mathtt{t}^{\prime\prime\prime}&=&\mathtt{E}\mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\kern-2.72221pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\llbracket}}\mathtt{v}_{i}{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\shortrightarrow}}}\mathtt{x}_{i}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\rrbracket}}_{\Gamma}\lBrace\lambda\mathtt{z}.\mathtt{r}{\shortrightarrow}\mathtt{y}\rBrace{\color[rgb]{0,1,0.5}{\lbrbrak}}\mathtt{y}\,{{\color[rgb]{0,1,0.5}{\rhd}}}\,\mathtt{u}{\color[rgb]{0,1,0.5}{|}}\mathtt{x}{\color[rgb]{0,1,0.5}{\rbrbrak}}\mathtt{s}\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\kern-2.72221pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}\end{array}

By applying generalized root cut expansion (Lemma
0.E.8
) to
\mathtt{t}^{\prime\prime\prime}
, we obtain exactly
\mathtt{t}^{\prime}\in\mathrm{SN}_{\mathtt{cut}}
.
∎

Corollary 2
(Typable terms are SN)

Let
\mathtt{t}
be a typable term. Then
\mathtt{t}\in\mathrm{SN}_{\mathtt{cut}}
.

Proof

Since
\mathtt{t}
is typable, we have
\pi\;\triangleright\mathtt{x}_{1}\,{:}\,B_{1},\ldots,\mathtt{x}_{k}\,{:}\,B_{k}\vdash\mathtt{t}\,{:}\,A
, for some derivation
\pi
. By adequacy (Theorem
0.E.1
),
\pi
is reducible. By
Lemma
0.E.7
,
\mathtt{t}\in\mathrm{SN}_{\mathtt{cut}}
.∎

Appendix 0.F
Proof of Strong Bisimulation for Structural Equivalence

For the sake of completeness, in this section we give most details of the easy but tedious proof that structural equivalence is a strong bisimulation. The property is first stated in Appendix
0.D
(and recalled below) but omitting the proof.

\gettoappendix
prop:strong-bisim

Proof

We first treat
\sim
(and its symmetric case) and then its context closure
\approx
, defined as
\mathtt{C}\langle\mathtt{t}\rangle\approx\mathtt{C}\langle\mathtt{t}^{\prime}\rangle
if
\mathtt{t}\sim\mathtt{t}^{\prime}
. In order to obtain the statement for
\equiv
, one needs to further add closure under reflexivity, which is obvious, and under transitivity, which is straightforward.

Now, we deal with root structural equivalence
\sim
for cut, that is, we deal with the case:

\begin{array}[]{c\hcolspace c\hcolspace cccc}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\llbracket}}\mathtt{s}{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\shortrightarrow}}}\mathtt{x}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\rrbracket}}\mathtt{W}\langle\mathtt{t}\rangle&\sim&\mathtt{W}\langle{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\llbracket}}\mathtt{s}{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\shortrightarrow}}}\mathtt{x}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\rrbracket}}\mathtt{t}\rangle\end{array}

With
\mathtt{x}\notin\mathtt{fv}(\mathtt{W})
and
\mathtt{W}
not capturing variables in
\mathtt{fv}(\mathtt{s})
. Root structural equivalence for subtractions is analogous but simpler. We first consider the cases of reduction in
{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\llbracket}}\mathtt{s}{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\shortrightarrow}}}\mathtt{x}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\rrbracket}}\mathtt{W}\langle\mathtt{t}\rangle
:

\mathtt{W}
. Then:
