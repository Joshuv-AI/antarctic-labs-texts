Abstract
. We consider n n eigenvalues of complex and symplectic induced spherical ensembles, which can be realised as two-dimensional determinantal and Pfaffian Coulomb gases on the Riemann sphere under the insertion of point charges. For both cases, we show that the probability that there are no eigenvalues in a spherical cap around the poles has an asymptotic behaviour as n → ∞ n\to\infty of the form exp ⁡ ( c 1 ​ n 2 + c 2 ​ n ​ log ⁡ n + c 3 ​ n + c 4 ​ n + c 5 ​ log ⁡ n + c 6 + 𝒪 ⁡ ( n − 1 12 ) ) \exp\Big(c_{1}n^{2}+c_{2}n\log n+c_{3}n+c_{4}\sqrt{n}+c_{5}\log n+c_{6}+\mathcal{O}(n^{-\frac{1}{12}})\Big) and determine the coefficients explicitly. Our results provide the second example of precise (up to and including the constant term) large gap asymptotic behaviours for two-dimensional point processes, following a recent breakthrough by Charlier.

1.
Introduction and main results

1.1.
Spherical ensembles

We consider two-dimensional Coulomb gases
[
63
]
(also known as one-component plasmas)
\boldsymbol{x}=\{x_{j}\}_{j=1}^{n}
on the Riemann sphere
\mathbb{S}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{3}:\|x\|=1\}
, under the insertion of point charges at the south and north poles,
{\rm{S}}=(0,0,-1)
and
{\rm{N}}=(0,0,1)
respectively. Here,
\|\cdot\|
is the Euclidean metric in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
.
In addition to the point charge insertions, we consider both determinantal and Pfaffian Coulomb gases.
Consequently, for given fixed non-negative integers
\alpha
and
c
determining point charges at the poles, the joint probability distributions of the models are given by

(1.1)

\displaystyle d\boldsymbol{\mathcal{P}}_{n}^{\mathbb{C}}(\boldsymbol{x})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\mathcal{Z}_{n}^{\mathbb{C}}}\prod_{j>k=1}^{n}\|x_{j}-x_{k}\|^{2}\prod_{j=1}^{n}\|x_{j}-{\rm{N}}\|^{2\alpha}\,\|x_{j}-{\rm{S}}\|^{2c}\,dA_{\mathbb{S}}(x_{j}),

(1.2)

\displaystyle d\boldsymbol{\mathcal{P}}_{n}^{\mathbb{H}}(\boldsymbol{x})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\mathcal{Z}_{n}^{\mathbb{H}}}\prod_{j>k=1}^{n}\|x_{j}-x_{k}\|^{2}\|x_{j}-\overline{x}_{k}\|^{2}\prod_{j=1}^{n}\|x_{j}-\overline{x}_{j}\|^{2}\|x_{j}-{\rm{N}}\|^{4\alpha}\,\|x_{j}-{\rm{S}}\|^{4c}\,dA_{\mathbb{S}}(x_{j}),

where
dA_{\mathbb{S}}
is the area measure on
\mathbb{S}
, and we use the convention
\overline{x}=(x_{1},-x_{2},x_{3})
for
x=(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})
.
Here,
\mathcal{Z}_{n}^{\mathbb{C}}
and
\mathcal{Z}_{n}^{\mathbb{H}}
are partition functions that make
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{P}}_{n}^{\mathbb{C}}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{P}}_{n}^{\mathbb{H}}
probability measures.
The superscripts
\mathbb{C}
and
\mathbb{H}
denoting determinantal and Pfaffian Coulomb gases, respectively, will become clear below.

For readers who are inclined towards the statistical physics perspective, it suffices to regard (
1.1
) and (
1.2
) as the primary subjects of our investigations.
On the other hand, for readers from the random matrix theory community, let us stress that these models have realisations of eigenvalues of random matrix models called complex
[
61
,
64
,
77
]
and symplectic
[
28
,
65
,
87
,
88
]
spherical induced ensembles.
We refer to
[
26
, Section 2.5]
and
[
27
, Section 6.3]
for comprehensive reviews, see also a recent work
[
91
]
and references therein.
A simple way to define these random matrix models is as
n\times n
complex or quaternion matrices, denoted by
G
, whose matrix probability distribution function is proportional to

(1.3)

\frac{\det(\textbf{G}\textbf{G}^{*})^{c\beta/2}}{\det(\mathbbm{1}_{n}+\textbf{G}\textbf{G}^{*})^{(2n+c+\alpha)\beta/2}},\qquad\beta=\begin{cases}2&\textup{for the complex case},\\
4&\textup{for the quaternionic case}.\end{cases}

For the special case when
c=\alpha=0
(i.e. without point charges), these models are called the complex and symplectic spherical ensembles.
In this case, an element-wise realisation of random matrices distributed as (
1.3
) is due to Krishnapur
[
77
]
.
To be more concrete, the matrix distribution (
1.3
) follows from
\textbf{G}_{1}\textbf{G}_{2}^{-1}
, where
\textbf{G}_{1}
and
\textbf{G}_{2}
are independent complex or symplectic Ginibre matrices whose elements are given by i.i.d. complex or quaternionic Gaussian random variables.
It is evident from the construction that the spherical ensembles are closely related to generalised eigenvalue problems
[
65
,
54
]
, which find applications in random plane geometry.
Beyond the spherical cases, the presence of point charges can also be realised at the level of matrix models via a so-called inducing procedure, see e.g.
[
61
,
88
,
60
]
. This construction requires the use of rectangular Ginibre matrices as well as Haar-distributed matrices within the symmetry classes under consideration.
In this inducing procedure, one needs to assume that
\alpha
and
c
are non-negative integer values since they are related to the rectangular parameters.

It follows from (
1.3
) that the joint distributions of eigenvalues
\boldsymbol{z}=\{z_{j}\}_{j=1}^{n}
are given by

(1.4)

\displaystyle d\mathbf{P}_{n}^{\mathbb{C}}(\boldsymbol{z})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{Z_{n}^{\mathbb{C}}}\prod_{j>k=1}^{n}|z_{j}-z_{k}|^{2}\prod_{j=1}^{n}e^{-nQ_{n}(z_{j})}\,dA(z_{j}),

(1.5)

\displaystyle d\mathbf{P}_{n}^{\mathbb{H}}(\boldsymbol{z})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{Z_{n}^{\mathbb{H}}}\prod_{j>k=1}^{n}|z_{j}-z_{k}|^{2}|z_{j}-\overline{z}_{k}|^{2}\prod_{j=1}^{n}|z_{j}-\overline{z}_{j}|^{2}e^{-2nQ_{n}(z_{j})}\,dA(z_{j}),

where
dA(z)=d^{2}z/\pi
and the external potential
Q_{n}
is given by

(1.6)

Q_{n}(z):=\frac{n+\alpha+c+1}{n}\log(1+|z|^{2})-\frac{2c}{n}\log|z|.

The models (
1.4
) and (
1.5
) are again two-dimensional Coulomb gas ensembles in the complex plane, with an
n
-dependent potential.
Due to their special integrable structures, they are determinantal and Pfaffian Coulomb gas ensembles, respectively.
We mention that the partition functions
Z_{n}^{\mathbb{C}}
and
Z_{n}^{\mathbb{H}}
can be expressed in terms of the gamma functions as

(1.7)

Z_{n}^{\mathbb{C}}=n!\prod_{k=0}^{n-1}\frac{\Gamma(k+c+1)\Gamma(n+\alpha-k)}{\Gamma(n+\alpha+c+1)},\qquad Z_{n}^{\mathbb{H}}=n!\,2^{n}\,\prod_{k=0}^{n-1}\frac{\Gamma(2k+2c+2)\Gamma(2n+2\alpha-2k)}{\Gamma(2n+2\alpha+2c+2)}.

To observe the equivalence between (
1.1
) and (
1.4
), as well as (
1.2
) and (
1.5
), recall that the stereographic projection
\phi:\mathbb{S}^{2}\to\mathbb{C}\cup\{\infty\}
is given by

(1.8)

\phi(x)=\begin{cases}\dfrac{x_{1}+ix_{2}}{1-x_{3}},&\textup{if }x\in\mathbb{S}^{2}\setminus{\rm{N}},\\
\infty,&\textup{if }x={\rm{N}},\end{cases}\qquad\phi^{-1}(z)=\Big(\frac{2\operatorname{Re}z}{1+|z|^{2}},\frac{2\operatorname{Im}z}{1+|z|^{2}},\frac{|z|^{2}-1}{1+|z|^{2}}\Big),

where
x=(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}).

Using these, one can easily check that the measures (
1.1
) and (
1.2
) are a pull-back of (
1.4
) and (
1.5
), whence the name
spherical
for the random matrix
G
was first coined
[
77
,
64
]
.
Furthermore, the partition functions
\mathcal{Z}_{n}^{\mathbb{C}}
and
\mathcal{Z}_{n}^{\mathbb{H}}
in (
1.1
) and (
1.2
) are also given by (
1.7
), up to explicit constants. From this viewpoint, the potential (
1.6
) in the complex plane can also be realised as point charge insertions at the origin and infinity, cf.
[
93
]
.

The potential
Q_{n}
in (
1.6
) is indeed a prominent example of
weakly confining potential
, see e.g.
[
25
]
and references therein.
Contrary to Coulomb gas ensembles with a confining potential that makes the particles lie in a compact set in the large-
n
limit, the ensembles (
1.4
) and (
1.5
) tend to be distributed in the whole complex plane.
To be more precise, due to the Laplacian growth property of the two-dimensional Coulomb gas ensemble,
for given fixed
\alpha
and
c
, the ensembles (
1.4
) and (
1.5
) tend to occupy the whole complex plane with the limiting density

(1.9)

\rho^{\rm sp}(z)=\frac{1}{(1+|z|^{2})^{2}}.

As a consequence, using the inverse stereographic projection, we infer that as
n\to\infty
, the ensembles (
1.1
) and (
1.2
) tend to be uniformly distributed on the whole Riemann sphere
\mathbb{S}
, see Figure
1
.
We also mention that the equilibrium measure problems associated with spherical Coulomb gases with point charges have been recently studied in
[
83
,
43
]
.

1.2.
Main results: large gap probabilities

In this work, we study the asymptotic behaviour of the large gap probabilities as
n\to\infty
of the ensembles (
1.4
) and (
1.5
):

(1.10)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathcal{P}_{n}(R;\alpha,c)&:=\mathbb{P}\Big[\textup{there is no eigenvalues in }\mathbb{D}(0,R)\Big].\end{split}

This problem was already considered a decade ago in the work
[
3
]
, where the leading term for the complex case with
\alpha=c=0
was obtained (i.e. the constant
A_{1}
in Theorem
1.1
).

More generally, obtaining large gap asymptotics is a classical and challenging problem in random matrix theory with a long history, which has attracted considerable attention over the years. The literature pertaining to this topic will be reviewed in Subsection
1.3
below.
Note that
\mathcal{P}_{n}
can also be written as

(1.11)

\mathcal{P}_{n}(R;\alpha,c)=\mathbb{P}\Big[\min\{|z_{1}|,\ldots,|z_{n}|\}\geq R\,\Big].

Hence
\mathcal{P}_{n}
can be viewed as a heavy-tail distribution for the distribution of the smallest moduli.
Analogous extreme distributions have been widely studied for various one-dimensional log-correlated point processes (such as the Tracy-Widom distribution for the Airy point process); this will be discussed in Subsection
1.3.4
.
In our case, the moduli
\{|z_{j}|\}_{j=1}^{n}
forms a permanantal point process (see e.g.
[
7
]
), and the quantity
\mathcal{P}_{n}
can also be interpreted as the large deviation probability of this process.
Furthermore,
\mathcal{P}_{n}
is intimately connected to an energy minimisation (electrostatics) problem, which is a fundamental aspect of potential theory, see Subsection
1.3.2
.
Additionally, it is equivalent to the free energy of a one-component plasma confined by hard walls, see Subsection
1.3.3
for further details.

For the models (
1.1
) and (
1.2
) on the sphere, the probability (
1.10
) coincides with the probability that there are no particles in a spherical cap (whose center is the south pole) of area

(1.12)

4\pi\frac{R^{2}}{1+R^{2}},

see Figure
3
for some illustrations.
Note that instead of (
1.10
), one may also consider

(1.13)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}_{n}(R;\alpha,c)&:=\mathbb{P}\Big[\textup{there is no eigenvalues in }\mathbb{D}(0,R)^{c}\Big]=\mathbb{P}\Big[\max\{|z_{1}|,\ldots,|z_{n}|\}\leq R\,\Big].\end{split}

Due to the sphere geometry, there is a duality relation between
\mathcal{P}_{n}
and
\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}_{n}
:

(1.14)

\mathcal{P}_{n}(R;\alpha,c)=\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}_{n}(1/R;c,\alpha).

Alternatively, (
1.14
) can also be directly obtained using Lemma
2.1
below.

In the sequel, we add the superscripts and write
\mathcal{P}_{n}^{\mathbb{C}}
and
\mathcal{P}_{n}^{\mathbb{H}}
to distinguish (
1.4
) and (
1.5
).
To state our main results, we need some elementary special functions.
Recall that the complementary error function is defined by
[
92
, Chapter 7]

(1.15)

\operatorname{erfc}(z):=\frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi}}\int_{z}^{\infty}e^{-t^{2}}\,dt

and that the Barnes
G
-function is defined recursively by
[
92
, Section 5.17]

(1.16)

G(z+1)=\Gamma(z)G(z),\qquad G(1)=1,

where
\Gamma
is the standard gamma function.
We write

(1.17)

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{1}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{2}\int_{-\infty}^{0}\log\Big(\tfrac{1}{2}\operatorname{erfc}(t)\Big)\,dt+\sqrt{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}\bigg[\log\Big(\tfrac{1}{2}\operatorname{erfc}(t)\Big)+t^{2}+\log t+\log(2\sqrt{\pi})\bigg]\,dt\approx-2.301,

(1.18)

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{2}

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\bigg[\frac{(1+t^{2})e^{-t^{2}}}{\sqrt{\pi}\operatorname{erfc}(t)}-\Big(t^{3}+\frac{3}{2}t\Big)\mathbbm{1}_{(0,\infty)}(t)\bigg]\,dt\approx 0.928.

We are now ready to state the asymptotics as
n\to\infty
of the large gap probabilities of the complex induced spherical ensemble (
1.4
).

Theorem 1.1

(
Large gap probabilities of the complex ensemble
)
.

Let
\alpha,c\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
and
R>0
be fixed.
Then as
n\to\infty
, we have

(1.19)

\displaystyle\log\mathcal{P}_{n}^{\mathbb{C}}(R;\alpha,c)=A_{1}n^{2}+A_{2}n\log n+A_{3}n+A_{4}\sqrt{n}+A_{5}\log n+A_{6}+\mathcal{O}(n^{-\frac{1}{12}}),

where

(1.20)

\displaystyle A_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\Big(\frac{R^{2}}{1+R^{2}}-\log(1+R^{2})\Big),

(1.21)

\displaystyle A_{2}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\frac{R^{2}}{1+R^{2}},

(1.22)

\displaystyle A_{3}

\displaystyle=\frac{R^{2}}{1+R^{2}}\Big(1+\alpha+c-\frac{1}{2}\log(2\pi)-\log R\Big)-\alpha\log(1+R^{2}),

(1.23)

\displaystyle A_{4}

\displaystyle=\frac{R}{1+R^{2}}\,\mathcal{I}_{1},

(1.24)

\displaystyle A_{5}

\displaystyle=\frac{1+\alpha+c}{2(1+R^{2})}-\frac{1}{6}-\frac{\alpha+c^{2}}{2},

(1.25)

\displaystyle\begin{split}A_{6}&=\Big(\frac{1+\alpha+c}{1+R^{2}}-\frac{1}{3}-\alpha-c^{2}\Big)\log R-\frac{\alpha^{2}-c^{2}}{2}\log(1+R^{2})\\
&\quad+\Big(\frac{1}{2}-(1+\alpha+c)\frac{R^{2}}{1+R^{2}}\Big)\frac{\log(2\pi)}{2}+\frac{(\alpha+c)(1+\alpha+c)}{2}\frac{R^{2}}{1+R^{2}}\\
&\quad-\frac{2}{3}\frac{1-R^{2}}{1+R^{2}}\,\mathcal{I}_{2}+\frac{1}{12}\Big(1+\frac{11}{1+R^{2}}\Big)-\zeta^{\prime}(-1)+\log G(c+1).\end{split}

Here,
\zeta
is the Riemann zeta function,
G
is the Barnes
G
-function, and the constants
\mathcal{I}_{1}
and
\mathcal{I}_{2}
are given by (
1.17
) and (
1.18
).

Remark 1.2

(Conjecture for the terms proportional to
n\log n
and
\sqrt{n}
)
.

In view of Theorem
1.1
and
[
34
, Theorem 1.7]
, we formulate the following conjecture for
A_{2}
and
A_{4}
, valid for a general radially symmetric potential
V
such that
\{z\in\mathbb{C}:|z|<R\}
is contained in the droplet:

(1.26)

A_{2}=-\frac{1}{2}\int_{|z|<R}\,d\sigma_{V}(z)\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad A_{4}=R\sqrt{\rho(R)}\,\mathcal{I}_{1},

where
d\sigma_{V}=\Delta V\,dA=\rho\,dA
is the equilibrium measure (
1.39
) associated with the potential
V
, see also Subsection
1.3.2
.
This conjecture is consistent with Theorem
1.1
, and also with the result
[
34
, Theorem 1.7]
on the Mittag-Leffler ensemble.
In our case,
\rho
is given by (
1.9
), whereas for the Mittag-Leffler ensemble,
\rho
is given by (
1.36
).

As previously mentioned, when
\alpha=c=0
,
A_{1}
was already obtained in
[
3

. However, a more precise expansion has not been discovered; indeed even the second term
A_{2}
is new to our knowledge.

The symplectic counterpart of Theorem
1.1
is as follows.

Theorem 1.3

(
Large gap probabilities of the symplectic ensemble
)
.

Let
\alpha,c\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
and
R>0
be fixed.
Then as
n\to\infty
, we have

(1.27)

\displaystyle\log\mathcal{P}_{n}^{\mathbb{H}}(R;\alpha,c)=\widehat{A}_{1}n^{2}+\widehat{A}_{2}n\log n+\widehat{A}_{3}n+\widehat{A}_{4}\sqrt{n}+\widehat{A}_{5}\log n+\widehat{A}_{6}+\mathcal{O}(n^{-\frac{1}{12}}),

where

(1.28)

\displaystyle\widehat{A}_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{R^{2}}{1+R^{2}}-\log(1+R^{2}),

(1.29)

\displaystyle\widehat{A}_{2}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\frac{R^{2}}{1+R^{2}},

(1.30)

\displaystyle\widehat{A}_{3}

\displaystyle=\frac{R^{2}}{1+R^{2}}\Big(2+2\alpha+2c-\frac{1}{2}\log(4\pi)-\log R\Big)-\Big(2\alpha+\frac{1}{2}\Big)\log(1+R^{2}),

(1.31)

\displaystyle\widehat{A}_{4}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\frac{R}{1+R^{2}}\,\mathcal{I}_{1},

(1.32)

\displaystyle\widehat{A}_{5}

\displaystyle=\frac{1+\alpha+c}{2(1+R^{2})}-\frac{5}{24}-\frac{\alpha+c}{2}-c^{2},

(1.33)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\widehat{A}_{6}&=\Big(\frac{1+\alpha+c}{1+R^{2}}-\frac{5}{12}-\alpha-c-2c^{2}\Big)\log R-(\alpha-c)\Big(\frac{1}{2}+\alpha+c\Big)\log(1+R^{2})\\
&\quad-\Big(c+(1+\alpha+c)\frac{R^{2}}{1+R^{2}}\Big)\frac{\log(4\pi)}{2}+\Big(c+\frac{1}{4}\Big)\log 2+\Big(\frac{1}{2}+\alpha+c\Big)(1+\alpha+c)\frac{R^{2}}{1+R^{2}}\\
&\quad-\frac{1}{3}\frac{1-R^{2}}{1+R^{2}}\mathcal{I}_{2}+\frac{1}{24}\Big(1+\frac{11}{1+R^{2}}\Big)-2\zeta^{\prime}(-1)+\log\Big(G(c+1)G(c+\tfrac{3}{2})\Big).\end{split}

Here, the constants
\mathcal{I}_{1}
and
\mathcal{I}_{2}
are given by (
1.17
) and (
1.18
).

1.3.
Related works and further results

In this subsection, we shall discuss the progress made regarding large gap probabilities and related topics, providing further motivations for our study and elucidating our contributions to this direction. In addition, we present further consequences arising from our main results.

1.3.1.
Large gap probabilities for the Ginibre ensemble

As previously mentioned, Ginibre matrices are non-Hermitian random matrices with i.i.d. Gaussian entries, see
[
26
,
27
]
for recent reviews.
The joint probability distributions of their eigenvalues are of the form (
1.4
) and (
1.5
), where the external potential is instead given by
|z|^{2}
. Its two-parameter extension, the Mittag-Leffler ensemble, has a potential of the form

(1.35)

Q^{\rm ML}(z)=|z|^{2b}-\frac{2c}{n}\log|z|,\qquad(b>0,\,c>-1).

The associated limiting spectrum is a centred disc of radius
b^{-\frac{1}{2b}}
and the density is given by

(1.36)

\rho^{\rm ML}(z)=b^{2}|z|^{2b-2}.

In particular, if
b=1
, this gives rise to the well-known circular law.

An early study on the expansion of large gap probabilities of the complex Ginibre ensemble was due to Forrester
[
62
]
, where he derived the first
four terms
in the expansion, i.e.
C_{j}
(
j=1,2,3,4
) in (
1.37
) below. (See also
[
70
,
71
]
for the first two terms).
Subsequently, using the theory of skew-orthogonal polynomials, the symplectic counterpart was addressed in
[
8
]
, where the authors obtained the
first two terms
, and partially conjectured the third one. (See also
[
9
]
for the extension to the product of Ginibre matrices.)

For a considerable period, obtaining a more precise expansion has been a longstanding open problem.
It is only now that the specialised methods of asymptotic analysis required for this task have been mastered in the work
[
34
]
of Charlier.
Consequently, it has been established that the large gap probabilities of the Mittag-Leffler ensemble take the form:

(1.37)

\exp\Big(C_{1}n^{2}+C_{2}n\log n+C_{3}n+C_{4}\sqrt{n}+C_{5}\log n+C_{6}+\mathcal{O}(n^{-\frac{1}{12}})\Big).

We mention that even though the large gap probabilities were stated in
[
34
]
only for the complex ensembles, the symplectic counterparts also follow by combining the results in
[
34
]
with
[
27
, Proposition 5.10]
.

The key ingredient for the analysis of Charlier is a uniform asymptotic expansion of the incomplete gamma function due to Temme, see
[
98
, Section 11.2.4]
and
[
92
, Section 8.12]
.
The basic concept of this method involves dividing the required summation based on different asymptotic regimes of the incomplete gamma function. Each division is meticulously chosen to minimise error bounds during asymptotic analysis, ensuring the smallest possible cumulative errors.
Even though some of these ideas trace back to Forrester’s work
[
62
]
more than 30 years ago, their implementation demands technical mastery and a very systematic approach, particularly to achieve precision up to the constants
C_{5}
and
C_{6}

[
34
]
. This method was also recently used in e.g.
[
33
,
11
]
to obtain precise results on counting statistics.

Contrary to
[
34
,
33
,
11
]
, the gap probability
\mathcal{P}_{n}
is not expressed in terms of the incomplete gamma function but in terms of the
incomplete beta function
(cf. Lemma
2.1
).
This difference with
[
34
,
33
,
11
]
has far-reaching consequences in the proofs of Theorems
1.1
and
1.3
.
Indeed, our analysis requires very detailed asymptotics of the incomplete beta functions for various regimes of the parameters, and in particular we rely on some results in
[
98
, Section 11.3.3]
.
We illustrate our proofs’ strategy in Figures
4
and
5
below.
Interestingly, after a very detailed and intricate analysis, the resulting error bounds in Theorems
1.1
and
1.3
are
\mathcal{O}(n^{-\frac{1}{12}})

1.3.2.
Leading order asymptotics and balayage measure

The leading order asymptotic behaviour of the large gap probabilities can alternatively be derived using a potential theoretic method
[
94
]
by investigating the associated balayage measure.
For this purpose, let us recall that for a given probability measure
\mu
on
\mathbb{C}
, the weighted logarithmic energy
I_{V}[\mu]
associated with the external potential
V
is given by

(1.38)

I_{V}[\mu]:=\int_{\mathbb{C}^{2}}\log\frac{1}{|z-w|}\,d\mu(z)\,d\mu(w)+\int_{\mathbb{C}}V\,d\mu.

Assuming that
V
is lower semi-continuous and finite on some set of positive capacity,
I_{V}[\mu]
has a unique minimiser
\sigma_{V}
with a support
S
called the droplet.
Furthermore, if
V
is
C^{2}
-smooth in a neighbourhood of
S
, then
\sigma_{V}
is absolutely continuous with respect to
dA
, and takes the form

(1.39)

d\sigma_{V}(z)=\Delta V(z)\,\mathbbm{1}_{S_{V}}(z)\,dA(z),\qquad\Delta=\partial\bar{\partial}.

This property is often called the Laplacian growth, especially in the context of the Hele-Shaw flow.
We now consider a gap
\Omega\subset\mathbb{C}
and redefine the potential by

(1.40)

V_{\Omega}(z)=\begin{cases}V(z)&\textup{if }z\not\in\Omega,\\
+\infty&\textup{if }z\in\Omega.\end{cases}

If
S\cap\Omega\neq\emptyset
, then the equilibrium measure associated with
V_{\Omega}
is no longer absolutely continuous with respect to
dA
, but rather takes the form

(1.41)

d\sigma_{V_{\Omega}}(z)=\Delta V(z)\,\mathbbm{1}_{S_{V}\setminus\Omega}(z)\,dA(z)+\nu(z)\,\mathbbm{1}_{\partial\Omega}(z)|dz|.

As the term
balayage
means
sweeping
in French, from an electrostatic perspective, the constraint that there are no particles in
\Omega
pushes them to the boundary of
\Omega
with a non-trivial distribution
\nu(z)
, cf. Figure
3
.
The balayage measure can be used to derive the leading order of the large gap probabilities. In particular, the leading order of the (logarithm of the) large gap probabilities is proportional to

(1.42)

I_{V}[\sigma_{V}]-I_{V_{\Omega}}[\sigma_{V_{\Omega}}],

see e.g.
[
1
,
44
]
.
From a probabilistic point of view, the leading term (
1.42
) is a large deviation rate function, see e.g.
[
95
]
. An advantage of this approach is that it can be applied not only to the
\beta=2
Coulomb gas ensembles but also to general
\beta>0
. However, for a general shape of domain, it is far from obvious to explicitly compute the balayage measure, and some non-trivial examples have been recently obtained in
[
2
,
35
]
.
On one hand, this approach usually has its limitations in deriving only the leading order asymptotic expansion.

In our present case, one can consider the balayage measure associated with the potential

(1.43)

Q^{R}_{n}(z):=\begin{cases}+\infty&\textup{if }|z|\leq R,\\
Q_{n}(z)&\textup{if }|z|>R,\end{cases}

where
Q_{n}
is given by (
1.6
). In this case, due to the rotational symmetry, the balayage measure becomes uniform distribution on
|z|=R
, see Figure
3
.
Then the leading order of our results also follows from computing the associated energy, see
[
35
, Eq.(2.9)]
.

1.3.3.
Partition functions for two-dimensional point processes

For a given external potential
V
, the partition functions of determinantal and Pfaffian Coulomb gases are given by

(1.44)

\displaystyle Z_{n,V}^{\mathbb{C}}

\displaystyle:=\int_{\mathbb{C}^{n}}\prod_{j>k=1}^{n}|z_{j}-z_{k}|^{2}\prod_{j=1}^{n}e^{-n\,V(z_{j})}\,dA(z_{j}),

(1.45)

\displaystyle Z_{n,V}^{\mathbb{H}}

\displaystyle:=\int_{\mathbb{C}^{n}}\prod_{j>k=1}^{n}|z_{j}-z_{k}|^{2}|z_{j}-\overline{z}_{k}|^{2}\prod_{j=1}^{n}|z_{j}-\overline{z}_{j}|^{2}e^{-2nV(z_{j})}\,dA(z_{j}),

respectively.
The asymptotic expansion of the partition function stands as one of the cornerstones in Coulomb gas theory.
For a regular potential
V
, substantial progress has been made in this direction
[
17
,
31
,
107
,
79
]
. Furthermore, for comprehensive literature and the latest advancements, we refer the reader to
[

For the partition functions
Z_{n}^{\mathbb{C}}
and

of induced spherical ensembles, their asymptotic expansions can be derived from well-known formulas involving the Barnes
G
-function.

Proposition 1.4

(
Free energy expansions of the induced spherical ensembles
)
.

As
n\to\infty
, the following asymptotic expansion holds.

•

(Complex ensemble)
We have

(1.46)

\displaystyle\log Z_{n}^{\mathbb{C}}=\mathcal{A}_{1}n^{2}+\mathcal{A}_{2}n\log n+\mathcal{A}_{3}n+\mathcal{A}_{4}\sqrt{n}+\mathcal{A}_{5}\log n+\mathcal{A}_{6}+\mathcal{O}(n^{-1}),

where

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{1}=-\frac{1}{2},\qquad\mathcal{A}_{2}=\frac{1}{2},\qquad\mathcal{A}_{3}=\frac{\log(2\pi)}{2}-1-\alpha-c,\qquad\mathcal{A}_{4}=0,\qquad\mathcal{A}_{5}=\frac{\alpha^{2}+c^{2}}{2}+\frac{1}{3},

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{6}=\frac{\log(2\pi)}{2}-\frac{1}{12}+2\zeta^{\prime}(-1)-\frac{1}{2}(\alpha+c)(\alpha+c+1-\log(2\pi))-\log\Big(G(\alpha+1)G(c+1)\Big).

•

(Symplectic ensemble)
We have

(1.47)

\displaystyle\log Z_{n}^{\mathbb{H}}=\widehat{\mathcal{A}}_{1}n^{2}+\widehat{\mathcal{A}}_{2}n\log n+\widehat{\mathcal{A}}_{3}n+\widehat{\mathcal{A}}_{4}\sqrt{n}+\widehat{\mathcal{A}}_{5}\log n+\widehat{\mathcal{A}}_{6}+\mathcal{O}(n^{-1}),

where

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathcal{A}}_{1}=-1,\qquad\widehat{\mathcal{A}}_{2}=\frac{1}{2},\qquad\widehat{\mathcal{A}}_{3}=\frac{\log(4\pi)}{2}-2-2\alpha-2c,\qquad\widehat{\mathcal{A}}_{4}=0,\qquad\widehat{\mathcal{A}}_{5}=\alpha^{2}+\frac{\alpha}{2}+c^{2}+\frac{c}{2}+\frac{5}{12},

\displaystyle\begin{split}\widehat{\mathcal{A}}_{6}&=\log(2\pi)-\frac{13}{24}+4\zeta^{\prime}(-1)-(\alpha+c)(\alpha+c+\tfrac{3}{2}-\log(2\pi))-\log\Big(G(\alpha+1)G(\alpha+\tfrac{3}{2})G(c+1)G(c+\tfrac{3}{2})\Big).\end{split}

For the complex case, we also refer to
[

]
. See also
[

]
and references therein for the asymptotic behaviours of the logarithmic energy of the configurations.

This is the type we are exploring in our present work, namely the potential takes the form (

1.3.4.
Large gap probabilities for one-dimensional point processes

Comparing with two-dimensional point processes, there have been far more investigations into the gap probabilities for one-dimensional point processes. For the reader’s convenience, let us list some of the literature.

Some early works where the leading order term of large gap probabilities for the eigenvalues of classical Hermitian random matrices include
[
19
,
46
,
47
,
73
,
84
,
85
,
101
,
66
]
. Some of these works also find interesting connections with spin glass model as well as free fermionic systems.
As in the two-dimensional cases, the leading order can be obtained using potential-theoretic computations, sometimes referred to as the Coulomb gas method.

For determinantal point processes, large gap problems can be interpreted as asymptotic expansions for structured determinants.
For one-dimensional point processes, the Riemann-Hilbert approach has been particularly successful in obtaining precise asymptotic behaviours. (On the other hand, in dimension two, developing a Riemann-Hilbert approach to large gap problems breaking the radial symmetry is still an outstanding open problem.)
However, multiplicative constants of large gap problems are in general very difficult to obtain and are still open in some cases (see below).

•

(Hankel determinants)
The form of the asymptotic expansion depends on the external potential of the Hermitian random unitary matrices, particularly on their edge behaviours of the associated equilibrium measure. For the generalised Gaussian unitary ensembles, a general result can be found in
[
51
,
36
]
, and for the Laguerre and Jacobi types, these can be found in
[
37
]
. Furthermore, the large gap asymptotics of the Muttalib-Borodin model with constant potential involve a generalisation of Hankel determinants and were established in
[
32
]
.

•

(Toeplitz determinants)
For the circular unitary ensemble, the probability of an arc being empty of eigenvalues, up to a multiplicative constant, was obtained in
[
103
]
. This was later extended in
[
57
, Proposition 1.1.3]
to situations where the gap region consists of multiple arcs of the same length symmetrically located on the unit circle, and higher order correction terms were obtained in
[
86
]
.

•

(Fredholm determinants)
In this case, the asymptotic expansions have been investigated for different limiting point processes.

–

(Sine point process)
After the asymptotics of gap probabilities on a single interval were conjectured in
[
53
]
, they were proved simultaneously and independently in
[
55
,
75
]
. The case of several interval gaps was investigated in
[
104
]
, where later the results were made more explicit in
[
48
]
. In particular, the oscillations were expressed in terms of Jacobi theta functions in
[
48
]
for the first time. Nonetheless, the multiplicative constant was still missing in
[
48
]
. For the two-interval case, this constant was recently obtained in
[
58
]
.

–

(Airy point process)
The conjecture on the large gap asymptotics made in
[
99
]
was shown in
[
49
,
14
]
up to and including the multiplicative constant. For the case of two intervals, this was obtained in
[
22
]
without a multiplicative constant, which was later obtained in
[
76
]
. The gap probabilities in the bulk were also obtained in
[
23
]
.

–

(Bessel point process)
The large gap asymptotics on a single interval were obtained in
[
100
]
up to, but not including, the multiplicative constant. The multiplicative constant conjectured in
[
100
]
was later established in
[
56
,
50
]
using different approaches. Recently, the case of several intervals has also been addressed in
[
24
]
with the multiplicative constants left undetermined.

Beyond the standard limiting point processes, there have been further developments on other types of point processes. These include the Wright’s generalised Bessel and Meijer-

[

Proof of Proposition

.

By using the definition of the Barnes

\displaystyle Z_{n}^{\mathbb{C}}

\displaystyle=\frac{n!}{\Gamma(n+\alpha+c+1)^{n}}\frac{G(n+c+1)}{G(c+1)}\frac{G(n+\alpha+1)}{G(\alpha+1)},

(2.2)

\displaystyle Z_{n}^{\mathbb{H}}

\displaystyle=n!\Big(\frac{2^{2n+2\alpha+2c+1}}{\pi\Gamma(2n+2\alpha+2c+2)}\Big)^{n}\frac{G(n+c+1)}{G(c+1)}\frac{G(n+c+3/2)}{G(c+3/2)}\frac{G(n+\alpha+1)}{G(\alpha+1)}\frac{G(n+\alpha+3/2)}{G(\alpha+3/2)}.

Then the desired asymptotic expansion follows from the well-known asymptotic behaviours of the gamma function
[

(2.3)

\log N!=N\log N-N+\frac{1}{2}\log N+\frac{1}{2}\log(2\pi)+\frac{1}{12N}+\mathcal{O}\Big(\frac{1}{N^{3}}\Big),\qquad(N\to\infty)

and of the Barnes

[

(2.4)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\log G(z+1)=\frac{z^{2}\log z}{2}-\frac{3}{4}z^{2}+\frac{\log(2\pi)z}{2}-\frac{\log z}{12}+\zeta^{\prime}(-1)-\frac{1}{240z^{2}}+\mathcal{O}\Big(\frac{1}{z^{4}}\Big),\qquad(z\to\infty).\end{split}

∎

Recall that the regularised incomplete beta function
I_{x}
is given by

(2.5)

I_{x}(a,b):=\frac{B_{x}(a,b)}{B(a,b)},\qquad B_{x}(a,b):=\int_{0}^{x}t^{a-1}(1-t)^{b-1}\,dt

where

(2.6)

B(a,b)=B_{1}(a,b):=\frac{\Gamma(a)\Gamma(b)}{\Gamma(a+b)}

is the beta function, see
[
92

.
Note that by definition, we have

(2.7)

I_{x}(a,b)=1-I_{1-x}(b,a).

We also note that for integer values

(2.8)

I_{x}(m,n-m+1)=\sum_{j=m}^{n}\binom{n}{j}x^{j}(1-x)^{n-j}.

This in turn implies that the regularised incomplete beta function is the cumulative distribution function of the binomial distribution, i.e. for
X\sim B(n,p)
,

(2.9)

\mathbb{P}(X\leq k)=I_{1-p}(n-k,k+1)=1-I_{p}(k+1,n-k).

It follows from the general theory of determinantal and Pfaffian point processes that the associated partition functions (

Combining this with (

\displaystyle h_{k,m}(R)

\displaystyle=2\int_{R}^{\infty}\frac{r^{2k+1+2c\frac{m}{n}}}{(1+r^{2})^{(n+\alpha+c+1)\frac{m}{n}}}\,dr=\int_{R}^{\infty}\Big(\frac{r^{2}}{1+r^{2}}\Big)^{k+c\,\frac{m}{n}}\Big(\frac{1}{1+r^{2}}\Big)^{(n+\alpha+1)\,\frac{m}{n}-k-2}\frac{2r}{(1+r^{2})^{2}}\,dr

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{x}t^{(n+\alpha+1)\,\frac{m}{n}-k-2}(1-t)^{k+c\,\frac{m}{n}}\,dt=B_{x}((n+\alpha+1)\,\tfrac{m}{n}-k-1,k+c\,\tfrac{m}{n}+1),

which gives

\displaystyle\frac{h_{k,n}(R)}{h_{k,n}}=I_{x}(n+\alpha-k,k+c+1),\qquad\frac{h_{2k+1,2n}(R)}{h_{2k+1,2n}}=I_{x}(2n+2\alpha-2k,2k+2c+2).

This completes the proof.
∎

To analyse the expressions in Lemma
2.1
, it is convenient to introduce the notation

(2.15)

N=\begin{cases}n+\alpha+c&\textup{ for the complex case,}\\
n+\alpha+c+\frac{1}{2}&\textup{ for the symplectic case.}\end{cases}

We further define

(2.16)

k_{-}=\lfloor\delta N\rfloor-\alpha-1,\qquad k_{+}=\lceil(1-\delta)N\rceil-\alpha-1,

and

(2.17)

k_{M}=\begin{cases}N-\lceil M\rceil-\alpha-1&\textup{ for the complex case,}\\
N-\lceil M\rceil-\alpha-\frac{3}{2}&\textup{ for the symplectic case.}\end{cases}

Here, we set

(2.18)

M=N^{\frac{1}{3}}

and choose
\delta\in(0,\min\{x,1-x\})
small enough so that
0<k_{-}<k_{+}<k_{M}<N
holds for large
N
.

By using (
2.16
) and (
2.17
), we divide the sum in (
2.11
) by the following four parts:

(2.19)

\log\mathcal{P}_{n}^{\mathbb{C}}(R;\alpha,c)=S_{0}+S_{1}+S_{2}+S_{3},

where

(2.20)

\displaystyle S_{0}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{k_{-}}\log I_{x}(k+\alpha+1,n+c-k),\qquad

\displaystyle S_{1}=\sum_{k=k_{-}+1}^{k_{+}-1}\log I_{x}(k+\alpha+1,n+c-k),

\displaystyle S_{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=k_{+}}^{k_{M}}\log I_{x}(k+\alpha+1,n+c-k),\qquad

\displaystyle S_{3}=\sum_{k=k_{M}+1}^{n-1}\log I_{x}(k+\alpha+1,n+c-k).

Similarly, we divide the sum in (
2.12
) by

(2.21)

\log\mathcal{P}_{n}^{\mathbb{H}}(R;\alpha,c)=\widehat{S}_{0}+\widehat{S}_{1}+\widehat{S}_{2}+\widehat{S}_{3},

where

(2.22)

\displaystyle\widehat{S}_{0}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{k_{-}}\log I_{x}(2k+2\alpha+2,2n+2c-2k),\quad

\displaystyle\widehat{S}_{1}=\sum_{k=k_{-}+1}^{k_{+}-1}\log I_{x}(2k+2\alpha+2,2n+2c-2k),

\displaystyle\widehat{S}_{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=k_{+}}^{k_{M}}\log I_{x}(2k+2\alpha+2,2n+2c-2k),\quad

\displaystyle\widehat{S}_{3}=\sum_{k=k_{M}+1}^{n-1}\log I_{x}(2k+2\alpha+2,2n+2c-2k).

See Figures
4
and
5
.
As previously mentioned, the divisions in (

) and (

) are chosen in a way to minimise cumulative errors. For the asymptotic behaviours of

,
S_{2}
, and
S_{3}

Lemma 2.2

(
Uniform asymptotics of the incomplete beta function
)
.

Let
x_{0}=a/N
. Then as
N\to\infty
, we have

(2.23)

I_{x}(a,N-a)=\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{erfc}\Big(-\eta\sqrt{\frac{N}{2}}\Big)+R_{a}(\eta),

where

(2.24)

R_{a}(\eta)=e^{-\frac{1}{2}\eta^{2}N}\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-\frac{1}{2}Nu^{2}}g(u)\,du.

Here,
\eta
is given by

(2.25)

-\frac{1}{2}\eta^{2}=x_{0}\log\Big(\frac{x}{x_{0}}\Big)+(1-x_{0})\log\Big(\frac{1-x}{1-x_{0}}\Big),\qquad\frac{\eta}{x-x_{0}}>0,

and
g
is given by

(2.26)

g(u)=\frac{dt}{du}\frac{1}{t-x}-\frac{1}{u+i\eta},

where the variables
t
and
u
are correlated by the bijection

(2.27)

\frac{1}{2}u^{2}=x_{0}\log\Big(\frac{t}{x_{0}}\Big)+(1-x_{0})\log\Big(\frac{1-t}{1-x_{0}}\Big),\qquad\frac{\operatorname{Im}t}{u}>0,\qquad t\in\mathcal{L},

on

(2.28)

\mathcal{L}=\Big\{t=\frac{\sin(\rho\sigma)}{\sin[(1+\rho)\sigma]}e^{i\sigma}:|\sigma|<\frac{\pi}{1+\rho}\Big\}\mapsto u\in\mathbb{R},\qquad\Big(\rho=\frac{x_{0}}{1-x_{0}}\Big).

Furthermore, we have

(2.29)

R_{a}(\eta)\sim\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi N}}e^{-\frac{1}{2}\eta^{2}N}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{k}c_{k}(\eta)}{N^{k}}

uniformly for
x\in(0,1)
and
x_{0}\in[\delta,1-\delta]
, where
0<\delta<\frac{1}{2}
is an arbitrarily fixed small constant.
Here, the coefficients
c_{n}(\eta)
is given by

(2.30)

c_{n}(\eta)=-i(2n-1)!!g_{2n},\qquad g_{n}=\frac{1}{n!}\frac{d^{n}}{du^{n}}g(u)\Big|_{u=0}.

In particular, the first two coefficients
c_{0}
and
c_{1}
are given by

(2.31)

\displaystyle c_{0}(\eta)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\eta}-\frac{\sqrt{x_{0}(1-x_{0})}}{x-x_{0}},

(2.32)

\displaystyle c_{1}(\eta)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\eta^{3}}+\frac{1-13x_{0}+13x_{0}^{2}}{12\sqrt{x_{0}(1-x_{0})}}\frac{1}{x-x_{0}}+\frac{(1-2x_{0})\sqrt{x_{0}(1-x_{0})}}{(x-x_{0})^{2}}+\frac{(x_{0}(1-x_{0}))^{3/2}}{(x-x_{0})^{3}}.

All of the
c_{k}(\eta)
are analytic at
\eta=0
.

Remark 2.3
.

In
[
98
, Section 11.3.3.2]
, the leading term of the right-hand side of (
2.23
) is incorrectly written as
\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{erfc}(-\eta\sqrt{\frac{N-a}{2}})
.
The corrected version follows from the integral

e^{-\frac{1}{2}\eta^{2}N}\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-\frac{1}{2}Nu^{2}}\frac{1}{u+i\eta}\,du=\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{erfc}\Big(-\eta\sqrt{\frac{N}{2}}\Big).

This typo still appears in
[
92
, Eq.(8.18.8)]
, where the first term of the right-hand side there should be corrected as

(2.33)

I_{x}(a,b)\sim\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{erfc}\Big(-\eta\sqrt{\frac{a+b}{2}}\Big).

The subleading terms in
[
98
,
92
]
are correct.
We note that the corrected version (
2.23
) can also be easily verified numerically.

3.1.
Analysis of
S_{0}
,
S_{2}
and
S_{3}

We provide the asymptotic formulas of the
S_{j}\,(j=0,2,3)
in increasing order of difficulty:
S_{0}
(Lemma
3.1
),
S_{3}
(Lemma
3.2
), and then
S_{2}
(Lemma
3.4
).

To simplify the notations, let us write

(3.1)

m_{k}:=k+\alpha+1

so that the summand of (
2.20
) can be rewritten as

I_{x}(k+\alpha+1,n+c-k)=I_{x}(m_{k},N+1-m_{k}).

Recall that
S_{0}
is given by (
2.20
).

Lemma 3.1

(
Asymptotic behaviour of
S_{0}
)
.

There exists
C>0
such that

(3.2)

S_{0}=\mathcal{O}(e^{-CN}),\qquad\text{as }N\to\infty.

Proof.

Recall that
k_{-}
is given by (
2.16
). Thus for
0\leq k\leq k_{-}
, we have
\alpha+1\leq m_{k}\leq\lfloor\delta N\rfloor
. Let
X\sim B(N,x)
, where
B
is the binomial distribution. Here
N
and
x
are given by (
2.15
) and (
2.10
).
Note that by (
2.9
), we have

(3.3)

\log I_{x}(m_{k},N+1-m_{k})=\log\Big(1-\mathbb{P}(X\leq m_{k}-1)\Big)=\log\Big(1-\mathbb{P}(X<m_{k})\Big).

For every
\alpha+1\leq m\leq\lfloor\delta N\rfloor
, by using Lemma
A.1
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(X<m)\leq\mathbb{P}(X<\delta N)\leq\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{erfc}\Big(\frac{x-\delta}{\sqrt{2x}}\sqrt{N}\Big)\leq\frac{1}{2}e^{-\frac{(x-\delta)^{2}}{2x}N}=\mathcal{O}(e^{-CN}),

where
C
is independent of
m
. Hence,
the summands of
S_{0}
in (
2.20
) are of order
\mathcal{O}(e^{-CN})
uniformly, which completes the proof.
∎

Next, we analyse the sum
S_{3}
in (
2.20
).
For this purpose, let us write

(3.4)

\theta_{M}:=\lceil M\rceil-M,\qquad\theta_{\delta}:=\delta N-\lfloor\delta N\rfloor,

where
M
is given by (
2.18
).

Lemma 3.2

(
Asymptotic behaviour of
S_{3}
)
.

As
N\to\infty
, we have

(3.5)

S_{3}=D_{3}N+D_{5}\log N+D_{6}-\frac{1}{6}\frac{M^{3}}{N}+\mathcal{O}\Big(\frac{1}{M}+\frac{M^{2}}{N}+\frac{M^{4}}{N^{2}}\Big),

where

\displaystyle D_{3}

\displaystyle=(M+\theta_{M}-c)\log x,\qquad D_{5}=\frac{1}{2}(M^{2}+(2\theta_{M}-1)M+\theta_{M}^{2}-\theta_{M}-c^{2}+c),

\displaystyle D_{6}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\bigg(M^{2}+(2\theta_{M}-1)M+\theta_{M}^{2}-\theta_{M}-c^{2}+c\bigg)\log\Big(\frac{1-x}{x}\Big)-\Big(\frac{1}{2}M^{2}+M\theta_{M}+\frac{1}{2}\theta_{M}^{2}-\frac{1}{12}\Big)\log M

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{3}{4}M^{2}+\Big(\theta_{M}-\frac{1}{2}\log 2\pi\Big)M-\Big(\frac{1}{2}\log 2\pi\Big)\theta_{M}-\zeta^{\prime}(-1)+\log G(c+1).

Here,
\theta_{M}
is given by (
3.4
),
\zeta
is the Riemann zeta function and
G
is the Barnes
G
-function.

Proof.

By the definitions (
3.1
), (
2.17
), and (
2.15
), as well as (
2.9
),
one can write

(3.6)

S_{3}=\sum_{\ell=c}^{\lceil M\rceil-1}\log\mathbb{P}(Y\leq\ell),\qquad Y\sim B(N,1-x).

Observe that

(3.7)

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\quad\log\mathbb{P}(Y\leq\ell)=(N-\ell)\log x+\log\bigg[\sum_{j=0}^{\ell}\binom{N}{j}(1-x)^{j}x^{\ell-j}\bigg]\\
&=(N-\ell)\log x+\log\bigg[\binom{N}{\ell}(1-x)^{\ell}\bigg]+\log\bigg(1+\sum_{j=0}^{\ell-1}\Big(\frac{x}{1-x}\Big)^{\ell-j}\frac{(j+1)\cdots\ell}{(N-\ell+1)\cdots(N-j)}\bigg).\end{split}

Notice here that the last term in the second line is of order
\mathcal{O}(\frac{\ell}{N})
.
Note also that

(3.8)

\log\binom{N}{\ell}=\ell\log N+\sum_{j=0}^{\ell-1}\log\Big(1-\frac{j}{N}\Big)-\log\ell!=\ell\log N-\frac{1}{N}\frac{\ell(\ell-1)}{2}-\log\ell!+\mathcal{O}\Big(\frac{\ell^{3}}{N^{2}}\Big).

Then, by combining (
3.7
) and (
3.8
), we obtain

(3.9)

\log\mathbb{P}(Y\leq\ell)=N\log x+\ell\log N+\ell\log\Big(\frac{1-x}{x}\Big)-\log\ell!-\frac{1}{N}\frac{\ell^{2}}{2}+\mathcal{O}\Big(\frac{\ell}{N}+\frac{\ell^{3}}{N^{2}}\Big).

By taking the sum of (
3.9
) over
c\leq\ell\leq\lceil M\rceil-1
, it follows that

(3.10)

\displaystyle\begin{split}S_{3}&=N(\lceil M\rceil-c)\log x+\Big(\frac{\lceil M\rceil^{2}-\lceil M\rceil}{2}-\frac{c^{2}-c}{2}\Big)\log\Big(\frac{1-x}{x}N\Big)\\
&\quad-\log G(\lceil M\rceil+1)+\log G(c+1)-\frac{1}{N}\frac{\lceil M\rceil^{3}}{6}+\mathcal{O}\Big(\frac{M^{2}}{N}+\frac{M^{4}}{N^{2}}\Big).\end{split}

Then the desired asymptotic formula (
3.5
) follows from (
2.4
) and
\lceil M\rceil=M+\theta_{M}
.
∎

Next, we analyse
S_{2}
. For this, we need the following version of the Euler-Maclaurin formula taken from
[
34
, Lemma 3.4]
.

Lemma 3.3

(cf. Lemma 3.4 in
[
34
]
)
.

Let
A
,
a_{0}
,
B
and
b_{0}
be bounded functions of
n\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
such that

a_{n}:=An+a_{0},\qquad b_{n}:=Bn+b_{0}

are integer-valued.
Furthermore, assume that
B-A
is positive and bounded away from 0. Let
f
be a function independent of
n
, and which is
C^{3}([\min\{\frac{a_{n}}{n},A\},\max\{\frac{b_{n}}{n},B\}])
for all
n\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
. Then as
n\to\infty
, we have

(3.11)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\sum_{j=a_{n}}^{b_{n}}f\Big(\frac{j}{n}\Big)&=n\int_{A}^{B}f(x)\,dx+\frac{(1-2a_{0})f(A)+(1+2b_{0})f(B)}{2}\\
&\quad+\frac{(-1+6a_{0}-6a_{0}^{2})f^{\prime}(A)+(1+6b_{0}+6b_{0}^{2})f^{\prime}(B)}{12n}\\
&\quad+\mathcal{O}\bigg(\frac{\mathfrak{m}_{A,n}(f^{\prime\prime})+\mathfrak{m}_{B,n}(f^{\prime\prime})}{n^{2}}+\sum_{j=a_{n}}^{b_{n}-1}\frac{\mathfrak{m}_{j,n}(f^{\prime\prime\prime})}{n^{3}}\bigg).\end{split}

Here, for any continuous function
g
on
[\min\{\frac{a_{n}}{n},A\},\max\{\frac{b_{n}}{n},B\}]
,

\mathfrak{m}_{A,n}(g):=\max_{x\in[\min\{\frac{a_{n}}{n},A\},\max\{\frac{a_{n}}{n},A\}]}|g(x)|,\qquad\mathfrak{m}_{B,n}(g):=\max_{x\in[\min\{\frac{b_{n}}{n},B\},\max\{\frac{b_{n}}{n},B\}]}|g(x)|.

We now derive the asymptotic behaviour of
S_{2}
. Compared to
S_{0}
and
S_{3}
, the asymptotics of
S_{2}
contribute to the leading order of the total summation (
2.19
).

Lemma 3.4

(
Asymptotic behaviour of
S_{2}
)
.

As
N\to\infty
, we have

(3.12)

S_{2}=C_{1}N^{2}+C_{2}N\log N+C_{3}N+C_{5}\log N+C_{6}+\mathcal{O}\Big(\frac{1}{M}+\frac{M}{N}\Big),

where

\displaystyle C_{1}

\displaystyle=-\int_{M/N}^{\delta}f_{1}(t)\,dt,\qquad C_{2}=-\frac{\delta}{2},\qquad C_{3}=\Big(\theta_{M}-\frac{1}{2}\Big)f_{1}\Big(\frac{M}{N}\Big)+\Big(\theta_{\delta}-\frac{1}{2}\Big)f_{1}(\delta)-\int_{M/N}^{\delta}f_{2}(t)\,dt,

\displaystyle C_{5}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}(M+\theta_{\delta}+\theta_{M}-1)-\frac{1}{12},

\displaystyle C_{6}

\displaystyle=\frac{1-6\theta_{M}+6\theta_{M}^{2}}{12}f_{1}^{\prime}\Big(\frac{M}{N}\Big)-\frac{1-6\theta_{\delta}+6\theta_{\delta}^{2}}{12}f_{1}^{\prime}(\delta)+\Big(\theta_{M}-\frac{1}{2}\Big)f_{2}\Big(\frac{M}{N}\Big)+\Big(\theta_{\delta}-\frac{1}{2}\Big)f_{2}(\delta)

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{12}\log M+\Big[\frac{1}{12}\Big(\delta-\log\Big(\frac{\delta}{1-\delta}\Big)\Big)-\frac{\delta x}{1-x-\delta}-x\log\Big(\frac{1-x-\delta}{1-x}\Big)\Big],

where
\theta_{M}
and
\theta_{\delta}
are given by (
3.4
), and the functions
f_{1}
,
f_{2}
are defined as

(3.13)

f_{1}(t):=t\log\Big(\frac{t}{1-x}\Big)+(1-t)\log\Big(\frac{1-t}{x}\Big),\qquad f_{2}(t):=\log\Big(\frac{1-x-t}{(1-x)(1-t)}\Big)+\frac{1}{2}\log\Big(2\pi t(1-t)\Big),

for
0<t<1-x
.

Proof.

Let
X\sim B(N,x)
. Then by (
2.16
) and (
2.17
), we have

(3.14)

S_{2}=\sum_{k=k_{+}}^{k_{M}}\log\mathbb{P}(X\geq k+\alpha+1)=\sum_{j=\lceil M\rceil}^{\lfloor\delta N\rfloor}\log\mathbb{P}(X\geq N-j).

Let
t=j/N
. By applying Lemma
A.2
, we have

\log\mathbb{P}(X\geq tN)=-Nf_{1}(t)-\frac{1}{2}\log N-f_{2}(t)+\frac{1}{N}f_{3}(t)+\mathcal{O}\Big(\frac{1}{t^{3}N^{3}}+\frac{1}{N^{2}}\Big),

where

(3.15)

f_{3}(t)=\frac{1}{12}-\frac{tx}{(1-x-t)^{2}}-\frac{1}{12t(1-t)}.

Note that

\sum_{j=\lceil M\rceil}^{\lfloor\delta N\rfloor}\mathcal{O}\Big(\frac{1}{j^{3}}+\frac{1}{N^{2}}\Big)=\mathcal{O}\Big(\frac{1}{M^{2}}+\frac{1}{N}\Big).

By using Lemma
3.3
with
A(N)=M/N
,
a_{0}(N)=\theta_{M}
,
B(N)=\delta
and
b_{0}(N)=-\theta_{\delta}
, we obtain

\displaystyle-N\sum_{j=\lceil M\rceil}^{\lfloor\delta N\rfloor}f_{1}\Big(\frac{j}{N}\Big)

\displaystyle=-N^{2}\int_{M/N}^{\delta}f_{1}(t)\,dt+\Big[\Big(\theta_{M}-\frac{1}{2}\Big)f_{1}\Big(\frac{M}{N}\Big)+\Big(\theta_{\delta}-\frac{1}{2}\Big)f_{1}(\delta)\Big]N

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1-6\theta_{M}+6\theta_{M}^{2}}{12}f_{1}^{\prime}\Big(\frac{M}{N}\Big)-\frac{1-6\theta_{\delta}+6\theta_{\delta}^{2}}{12}f_{1}^{\prime}(\delta)+\mathcal{O}\Big(\frac{1}{M}\Big),

\displaystyle-\sum_{j=\lceil M\rceil}^{\lfloor\delta N\rfloor}f_{2}\Big(\frac{j}{N}\Big)

\displaystyle=-N\int_{M/N}^{\delta}f_{2}(t)\,dt+\Big(\theta_{M}-\frac{1}{2}\Big)f_{2}\Big(\frac{M}{N}\Big)+\Big(\theta_{\delta}-\frac{1}{2}\Big)f_{2}(\delta)+\mathcal{O}\Big(\frac{1}{M}+\frac{1}{N}\Big),

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=\lceil M\rceil}^{\lfloor\delta N\rfloor}f_{3}\Big(\frac{j}{N}\Big)

\displaystyle=\int_{M/N}^{\delta}f_{3}(t)\,dt+\mathcal{O}\Big(\frac{1}{M}\Big)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{12}\Big(\delta-\log\Big(\frac{\delta}{1-\delta}\Big)\Big)-\frac{\delta x}{1-x-\delta}-x\log\Big(\frac{1-x-\delta}{1-x}\Big)+\frac{1}{12}\log\Big(\frac{M}{N}\Big)+\mathcal{O}\Big(\frac{1}{M}+\frac{M}{N}\Big).

Combining all of the above, the proof is complete.
∎

We conclude this subsection by presenting the asymptotics of
S_{0}+S_{2}+S_{3}
, derived from the previous lemmas.

Lemma 3.5

(
Asymptotic behaviour of
S_{0}+S_{2}+S_{3}
)
.

As
N\to\infty
, we have

(3.16)

S_{0}+S_{2}+S_{3}=E_{1}N^{2}+E_{2}N\log N+E_{3}N+E_{5}\log N+E_{6}+\mathcal{O}\Big(\frac{1}{M}+\frac{M^{2}}{N}+\frac{M^{4}}{N^{2}}\Big),

where

\displaystyle E_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\log x-\frac{1}{2}\delta^{2}\log\Big(\frac{\delta}{1-x}\Big)+\frac{1}{2}(1-\delta)^{2}\log\Big(\frac{1-\delta}{x}\Big)+\frac{\delta}{2},\qquad E_{2}=-\frac{\delta}{2},

\displaystyle E_{3}

\displaystyle=\Big(\frac{1}{2}-c\Big)\log x+(1-x-\delta)\log\Big(\frac{1-x-\delta}{1-x}\Big)+\delta-\frac{1}{2}\delta\log(2\pi\delta)-\frac{1}{2}(1-\delta)\log(1-\delta)-\Big(\frac{1}{2}-\theta_{\delta}\Big)f_{1}(\delta),

\displaystyle E_{5}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\Big(\theta_{\delta}+c-c^{2}\Big)-\frac{5}{12},

\displaystyle E_{6}

\displaystyle=\Big(\frac{1}{12}-\frac{c-c^{2}}{2}\Big)\log\Big(\frac{x}{1-x}\Big)-\frac{1}{4}\log(2\pi)-\zeta^{\prime}(-1)+\log G(c+1)

\displaystyle\quad-\frac{1-6\theta_{\delta}+6\theta_{\delta}^{2}}{12}f_{1}^{\prime}(\delta)-\Big(\frac{1}{2}-\theta_{\delta}\Big)f_{2}(\delta)+\frac{1}{12}\delta-\frac{\delta x}{1-x-\delta}-x\log\Big(\frac{1-x-\delta}{1-x}\Big)-\frac{1}{12}\log\Big(\frac{\delta}{1-\delta}\Big).

Here,
f_{1}
and
f_{2}
are defined as (
3.13
).

Proof.

First, note that since
S_{0}
is exponentially small due to Lemma
3.1
, it suffices to compute
S_{2}+S_{3}.

Note that by definition (
3.13
), we have

(3.17)

\int f_{1}(t)\,dt=\frac{1}{2}t^{2}\log\Big(\frac{t}{1-x}\Big)-\frac{1}{2}(1-t)^{2}\log\Big(\frac{1-t}{x}\Big)-\frac{t}{2},\qquad f_{1}^{\prime}(t)=\log\Big(\frac{tx}{(1-t)(1-x)}\Big),

and

(3.18)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\int f_{2}(t)\,dt=-(1-x-t)\log(1-x-t)+t\Big(\log\Big(\frac{\sqrt{2\pi}}{1-x}\Big)-1\Big)+\frac{1}{2}\Big(t\log t+(1-t)\log(1-t)\Big),\end{split}

up to integration constants.
Combining these formulas with Lemma
3.4
, we have

\displaystyle C_{1}N^{2}

\displaystyle=\bigg[-\frac{1}{2}\delta^{2}\log\Big(\frac{\delta}{1-x}\Big)+\frac{1}{2}(1-\delta)^{2}\log\Big(\frac{1-\delta}{x}\Big)+\frac{\delta}{2}+\frac{1}{2}\log x\bigg]N^{2}-(M\log x)N-\frac{1}{2}M^{2}\log N

\displaystyle\quad+\bigg[\frac{1}{2}M^{2}\log M+\frac{1}{2}M^{2}\log\Big(\frac{x}{1-x}\Big)-\frac{3}{4}M^{2}\bigg]+\frac{M^{3}}{6N}+\mathcal{O}\Big(\frac{M^{4}}{N^{2}}\Big),

\displaystyle C_{3}N

In this appendix, we compile some tail probabilities of the binomial distribution.

The following lemma provides an upper bound for the lower tail probability, which can be found in

Lemma A.1
.

Let
X\sim B(n,p)
. Then for any integer
k\leq\frac{n^{2}p}{n+1}-1
, we have

(A.1)

\mathbb{P}(X\geq k)\geq e^{-np}\sum_{j=k}^{\infty}\frac{(np)^{j}}{j!}\geq 1-\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{erfc}\Big(\frac{np-k}{\sqrt{2np}}\Big).

Let us also introduce a slightly improved version of the asymptotic behaviour in
[
59
]
adapted to our purpose.

Lemma A.2
.

Let
X\sim B(N,x)
where
0<x<1
. Let
\delta\in(0,\min\{\frac{1}{4},1-x\})
be a constant.
Suppose that
\phi
is an increasing function such that
0<\phi(N)<\delta N
and
\phi(N)\to\infty
as
N\to\infty
. Then as
N\to\infty
,

(A.2)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\log\mathbb{P}[X\geq(1-t)N]&=-\frac{1}{2}\Big(t\log\Big(\frac{t}{1-x}\Big)+(1-t)\log\Big(\frac{1-t}{x}\Big)\Big)N-\frac{1}{2}\log N-\log\Big(\frac{1-x-t}{(1-x)(1-t)}\Big)\\
&\quad-\frac{1}{2}\log(2\pi t(1-t))+\frac{1}{N}\bigg(\frac{1}{12}-\frac{1}{12}\frac{1}{t(1-t)}-\frac{xt}{(1-x-t)^{2}}\bigg)+\mathcal{O}\Big(\frac{1}{t^{3}N^{3}}+\frac{1}{N^{2}}\Big)\end{split}

uniformly for
t
such that
tN
is an integer with
\phi(N)<tN<\delta N
.

Proof.

Write

(A.3)

s=1-t,\qquad r=\frac{tx}{(1-t)(1-x)}.

Then by definition, we have

(A.4)

\mathbb{P}[X\geq(1-t)N]=\sum_{k=0}^{tN}\binom{N}{tN-k}x^{(1-t)N+k}(1-x)^{tN-k}=\binom{N}{tN}(x^{1-t}(1-x)^{t})^{N}\sum_{k=0}^{tN}r^{k}\prod_{j=1}^{k}\frac{1-\frac{j-1}{tN}}{1+\frac{j}{sN}}.

By using (
2.3
), we have

(A.5)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\log\binom{N}{tN}&=-\Big(t\log t+(1-t)\log(1-t)\Big)N-\frac{1}{2}\log N\\
&\quad-\frac{1}{2}\log(2\pi t(1-t))+1+\frac{1}{12N}\Big(1-\frac{1}{t(1-t)}\Big)+\mathcal{O}\Big(\frac{1}{t^{3}N^{3}}\Big).\end{split}

Next, we estimate the summation in (
A.4
). We will compute explicit bounds for this sum. For this purpose, let

(A.6)

\displaystyle\begin{split}L(k;N,t)&=1-\frac{k^{2}+(2t-1)k}{2stN},\\
U(k;N,t)&=1-\frac{k^{2}+(2t-1)k}{2stN}+\frac{3k^{4}+(20t-10)k^{3}+(9-24st)k^{2}+(4t-2)k}{24s^{2}t^{2}N^{2}}.\end{split}

We claim that for any
0\leq k\leq tN
,

(A.7)

L(k;N,t)\leq\prod_{j=1}^{k}\frac{1-\frac{j-1}{tN}}{1+\frac{j}{sN}}\leq U(k;N,t).

Fix
N,t
and let us write
L(k):=L(k;N,t)
,
U(k):=U(k;N,t)
.
First we show the left inequality of (
A.7
). Since the inequality is trivial if
L(k)\leq 0
, we may assume
L(k)>0
.
Note that it suffices to show

\frac{L(j)}{L(j-1)}\leq\frac{1-\frac{j-1}{tN}}{1+\frac{j}{sN}}

for every
j=1,\ldots,k
.
Through straightforward computations, we have

\displaystyle L(j)\Big(1+\frac{j}{sN}\Big)

\displaystyle=1-\frac{j^{2}-j}{2stN}-\frac{j^{3}+(2t-1)j^{2}}{2s^{2}tN^{2}},

\displaystyle L(j-1)\Big(1-\frac{j-1}{tN}\Big)

\displaystyle=1-\frac{j^{2}-j}{2stN}+\frac{(j-1)^{2}(j+2t-2)}{2st^{2}N^{2}}.

It is clear that the numerators of the last terms are non-negative for any positive integer
j
, thereby proving the lower bound part of (
A.7
).

Next, let us verify the right inequality of (
A.7
).
Let
0\leq\alpha\leq 1
. Note that if
tN
is large enough, we have

\displaystyle 8s^{2}U(\alpha tN)

\displaystyle=t^{2}N^{2}\alpha^{4}+\frac{20t-10}{3}tN\alpha^{3}+(3-8st-4stN)\alpha^{2}+\frac{(2t-1)(2-12stN)}{3tN}\alpha+8s^{2}

\displaystyle=\Big(tN\alpha^{2}+\frac{10t-5}{3}\alpha-2s\Big)^{2}+\Big(\frac{2}{9}+\frac{28}{9}st\Big)\alpha^{2}+\Big(\frac{4t-2}{3tN}+\frac{16}{3}st-\frac{8}{3}s\Big)\alpha+4s^{2}

\displaystyle\geq-\frac{2}{3tN}-\frac{8}{3}s+4s^{2}\geq\frac{1}{4}-\frac{2}{3tN}\geq 0.

Therefore, it follows that

(A.8)

U(k)\geq 0

for all integer
k=0,1,\dots,tN
. Thus it is enough to show again

\frac{1-\frac{j-1}{tN}}{1+\frac{j}{sN}}\leq\frac{U(j)}{U(j-1)}

for all
j=1,\dots,tN
. For this, note that

\displaystyle U(j-1)\Big(1-\frac{j-1}{tN}\Big)

\displaystyle=1-\frac{j^{2}-j}{2stN}+\frac{3j^{4}+(20t-10)j^{3}+(9-24st)j^{2}+(4t-2)j}{24s^{2}t^{2}N^{2}}-\frac{E_{1}(j)}{24s^{2}t^{3}N^{3}},

\displaystyle U(j)\Big(1+\frac{j}{sN}\Big)

\displaystyle=1-\frac{j^{2}-j}{2stN}+\frac{3j^{4}+(20t-10)j^{3}+(9-24st)j^{2}+(4t-2)j}{24s^{2}t^{2}N^{2}}+\frac{E_{2}(j)}{24s^{3}t^{2}N^{3}},

where

\displaystyle E_{1}(j)

\displaystyle=(j-1)^{2}\big[(j-2)(j-3)(3j-4)+4t(5(j-1)^{2}-6s(j-1)+1)\big],

\displaystyle E_{2}(j)

\displaystyle=j^{2}[(j-1)(j-2)(3j-1)+4t(5j^{2}-6sj+1)].

Since the quadratic function
f(u)=5u^{2}-6su+1
attains its minimum at
u=\frac{3s}{5}\in(0,1)
and both
f(0)
and
f(1)
are positive, we can deduce that
f(j)>0
for all integers
j
. Consequently, we can conclude that
E_{1}(j)
and
E_{2}(j)
are positive for all
j
, which leads to the upper bound part of (
A.7
).

Next, we assert that

(A.9)

\sum_{k=0}^{tN}r^{k}\prod_{j=1}^{k}\frac{1-\frac{j-1}{tN}}{1+\frac{j}{sN}}=\frac{1}{1-r}\Big(1-\frac{1}{N}\frac{tx}{(1-x-t)^{2}}+\mathcal{O}\Big(\frac{1}{N^{2}}\Big)\Big).

For this, note that for large enough
tN
and
k>tN
,

(A.10)

\displaystyle\begin{split}L(k;N,t)&=1-\frac{k^{2}+(2t-1)k}{2stN}\leq 1-\frac{(tN)^{2}+(2t-1)tN}{2stN}<0,\\
U(k;N,t)&=1-\frac{k^{2}+(2t-1)k}{2stN}+\frac{3k^{4}+(20t-10)k^{3}+(9-24st)k^{2}+(4t-2)k}{24s^{2}t^{2}N^{2}}>1\end{split}

since
3k^{4}/(24s^{2}t^{2}N^{2})
term dominates the size of
U(k;N,t)
. Let
N
be large enough so that
tN>\phi(N)
satisfies (
A.8
) and (
A.10
). Then one can observe that

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}r^{k}L(k;N,t)\leq\sum_{k=0}^{tN}r^{k}L(k;N,t)\leq\sum_{k=0}^{tN}r^{k}\prod_{i=1}^{k}\frac{1-\frac{i-1}{tN}}{1+\frac{i}{sN}}\leq\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}r^{k}U(k;N,t)\leq\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}r^{k}U(k;N,t).

Here, we have assumed
t<\delta<1-x
so that
r<1
. Then we obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}r^{k}L(t;N,t)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{1-r}\Big(1-\frac{1}{N}\frac{tx}{(1-x-t)^{2}}\Big),

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}r^{k}U(k;N,t)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{1-r}\Big(1-\frac{1}{N}\frac{tx}{(1-x-t)^{2}}+\frac{1}{N^{2}}\frac{tx(1-x^{2}-t(1-2x))}{(1-x-t)^{4}}\Big),

which gives rise to (
A.9
).
Then we conclude the proof by putting the asymptotics (
A.5
) and (
A.9
) to (
A.4
).
∎
