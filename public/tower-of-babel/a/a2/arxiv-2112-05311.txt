Adaptive projected SOR algorithms for nonnegative quadratic programming
Yuto Miyatake, Tomohiro Sogabe

Abstract
The choice of relaxation parameter in the projected successive overrelaxation (PSOR) method for nonnegative quadratic programming problems is problem-dependent. We present novel adaptive PSOR algorithms that adaptively control the relaxation parameter using the Wolfe conditions. The method and its variants can be applied to various problems without requiring additional assumptions, barring the positive semidefiniteness concerning the matrix that defines the objective function, and the cost for updating the parameter is negligible in the whole iteration. Numerical experiments show that the proposed methods often perform comparably to (or sometimes superior to) the PSOR method with a nearly optimal relaxation parameter.

Introduction

Given a symmetric positive semidefinite matrix
A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
and vector
\bm{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
we consider the following convex quadratic programming problem:

\begin{split}\text{minimize}&\quad V(\bm{x})=\frac{1}{2}\bm{x}^{\text{\tiny\sf T}}A\bm{x}-\bm{x}^{\text{\tiny\sf T}}\bm{b}\\
\text{subject to}&\quad\bm{x}\geq\bm{0}\quad(x_{i}\geq 0\text{ for all }i).\end{split}

(P1)

Throughout this paper, we assume that
A
has positive diagonal entries.
The problem (
P1
) is referred to as, for example, the nonnegative quadratic programming (NQP) or symmetric monotone linear complementarity problem.
The problem (
P1
) is related to the nonnegative constrained least squares problem

\begin{split}\text{minimize}&\quad V(\bm{x})=\|C\bm{x}-\bm{d}\|_{2}^{2}\\
\text{subject to}&\quad\bm{x}\geq\bm{0},\end{split}

(P2)

where
C\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
,
\bm{d}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
,
such that (
P1
) is converted to (
P2
)
with
A=C^{\text{\tiny\sf T}}C
and
\bm{b}=C^{\text{\tiny\sf T}}\bm{d}

, and vice versa.

The problem (
P1
) or its equivalent formulation arises from numerous contexts, some examples of which are listed here.
Early examples include, for example, a linear complementarity problem for solving free boundary problems for journal bearings by finite differences
ch41
.
In the context of machine learning, the problem is useful for computing the maximum margin hyperplanes in support vector machines
bu98
.
Some image reconstruction or deblurring techniques also require solving this problem (
P2
)
ca04
;
ns00
;
st08
.
The problem also appears in the study of financial derivatives
wh95
and
dynamic multi-rigid-body contact simulations

In this study, we are concerned with solving (
P1
) or (
P2
) numerically.
Iterative algorithms for solving (
P1
) or (
P2
) have been studied for more than half a century.
A straightforward approach is to apply a gradient method
to
V(\bm{x})
with an appropriate projection.
Furthermore, sophisticated variants exist based on matrix splitting.
Some can be interpreted as a stationary iterative method such as the successive overrelaxation (SOR) method for
A\bm{x}=\bm{b}
with an appropriate projection in each iteration

.
Other stationary iterative methods such as the accelerated overrelaxation method with an appropriate projection have recently been studied
ht15
;
ht16
.
Multiplicative iteration methods

;
sh07
, which are highly parallelizable, and interior-point methods

are also popular in the context of machine learning and linear complementarity problems, respectively.
A class of inner-outer iterative methods has also been studied

;
po69
;
zh16
.

This paper focuses on the SOR method with an appropriate projection, which is called the projected SOR (PSOR) method.
The SOR method can be understood as a cyclic coordinate descent method as shown in section
3.1
, and the PSOR method projects the iterate for each componentwise update.
This approach is expected to be effective for large scale problems witout requiring the estimation of some properties of the problem such as the largest absolute eigenvalue of

.
As is the case with the SOR method applied to a linear system
A\bm{x}=\bm{b}
, the convergence performance depends on the relaxation parameter

.
Notably, for (
P1
), as we will see through numerical experiments, the convergence behaviour often drastically changes during the iteration.
Therefore, changing the relaxation parameter during the iteration may be of great use.
We consider controlling the relaxation parameter adaptively and call such a method an adaptive projected SOR (APSOR) method.
We aim to develop APSOR methods with the following properties:
they perform effectively for a wide variety of (
P1
) without requiring a specific property for
A
, and
the cost for updating the parameter is negligible in the overall iterations.
We note that existing adaptive SOR techniques developed for solving linear systems can be incorporated with the PSOR method for (
P1
); however, most of them require a specific structure for matrix
A
or take a substantial cost for updating the relaxation parameter (a brief review will be given in section

).
Recently, the present authors proposed an adaptive SOR (ASOR) method for
A\bm{x}=\bm{b}
with the above two properties

.
In this paper, we show that the idea in
ms20
is also useful for (
P1
).
The main contributions of the present study are as follows.

•

We point out that the PSOR method has a form of projected gradient methods.
This is a straightforward but essential consequence of our previous report
ms18
, where we showed that the SOR method can be viewed as a discrete gradient scheme applied to a certain gradient system.
The key of the discussion is that the relaxation parameter can be viewed as the step size after an appropriate change of variables.

•

Using the above interpretation, we present an APSOR method based on the Wolfe conditions, which can be viewed as an application of
ms20
to (
P1
).
Preliminary numerical experiments suggest that the convergence behaviour may fluctuate because of the adaptive control of the relaxation parameter; the convergence behaviour strongly depends on the initial guess, which is particularly typical for an ill-conditioned
A
or symmetric positive semidefinite
A
.
To address these issues, we upgrade the APSOR method through two ways.
First, we develop an algorithm that fixes the relaxation parameter after performing some iterations.
Second, we propose an algorithm that finds a reasonable initial guess by shifting the matrix
A
in (
P1
) and applying the APSOR method to the shifted problem.

The paper is organized as follows.
In section
2
, the PSOR method is briefly reviewed.
New APSOR algorithms are presented in section

and numerically tested in section

.
Finally, we provide concluding remarks in section

Adaptive projected SOR methods

In this section, we propose adaptive projected SOR (APSOR) methods for solving (

We start our discussion by reviewing the geometric properties of the SOR method for solving
A\bm{x}=\bm{b}
:
the SOR method has a gradient structure, and the relaxation parameter can be regarded as a step size after a change of variables

.
This interpretation makes it possible to control the relaxation parameter adaptively based on a technique known in the context of continuous optimizations.
Building on our previous report

However, preliminary numerical experiments suggest that the convergence behaviour may fluctuate because of the adaptive control of the relaxation parameter; the convergence behaviour strongly depends on the initial guess, which is particularly typical for an ill-conditioned
A
or symmetric positive semidefinite
A

To address these issues, we further upgrade the APSOR method in two ways.

3.1
Geometric interpretation of the SOR method

For a continuously differentiable function
V:\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}
,
the continuous map

\nabla_{\mathrm{d}}:\mathbb{R}^{m}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}

is called a discrete gradient if it satisfies

\displaystyle V(\bm{x})-V({\bm{y}})

\displaystyle=\nabla_{\mathrm{d}}V(\bm{x},{\bm{y}})^{\text{\tiny\sf T}}(\bm{x}-{\bm{y}}),

(6)

\displaystyle\nabla_{\mathrm{d}}V(\bm{x},\bm{x})

\displaystyle=\nabla V(\bm{x})

(7)

for all
\bm{x},{\bm{y}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}

go96
;
mq99
;
qt96
.
The first condition is called the discrete chain rule.
The second merely requires the consistency of the discrete gradient to the gradient.
A map
\bm{x}^{(k)}\mapsto\bm{x}^{(k+1)}
defined by

\bm{x}^{(k+1)}=\bm{x}^{(k)}-hP\nabla_{\mathrm{d}}V(\bm{x}^{(k+1)},\bm{x}^{(k)})

(8)

with
h>0
and a symmetric positive definite matrix
P\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}
has the dissipation property

V(\bm{x}^{(k+1)})\leq V(\bm{x}^{(k)}),

(9)

because

\displaystyle V(\bm{x}^{(k+1)})-V(\bm{x}^{(k)})

\displaystyle=\nabla_{\mathrm{d}}V(\bm{x}^{(k+1)},\bm{x}^{(k)})^{\text{\tiny\sf T}}(\bm{x}^{(k+1)}-\bm{x}^{(k)})

(10)

\displaystyle=-h\nabla_{\mathrm{d}}V(\bm{x}^{(k+1)},\bm{x}^{(k)})^{\text{\tiny\sf T}}P\nabla_{\mathrm{d}}V(\bm{x}^{(k+1)},\bm{x}^{(k)})\leq 0.

(11)

A map of the form (
8

Note that the symbol

is called the step size because (
8

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\bm{x}(t)=-P\nabla V(\bm{x}(t)).

(12)

The construction of discrete gradients is not unique.
For example, the discrete gradient proposed by Itoh and Abe

is defined as follows:

\nabla_{\mathrm{d}}V(\bm{x},{\bm{y}})=\begin{bmatrix}\displaystyle\frac{f(x_{1},y_{2},\dots,y_{n})-f(y_{1},\dots,y_{n})}{x_{1}-y_{1}}\\
\displaystyle\frac{f(x_{1},x_{2},y_{3},\dots,y_{n})-f(x_{1},y_{2},\dots,y_{n})}{x_{2}-y_{2}}\\
\displaystyle\vdots\\
\displaystyle\frac{f(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})-f(x_{1},\dots,x_{n-1},y_{n})}{x_{n}-y_{n}}\end{bmatrix},

(13)

where the case

For other definitions, see, for example,

3.2
Adaptive projected SOR (APSOR) methods

The performance of the PSOR method (Algorithm
1
) strongly depends on the choice of the relaxation parameter
\omega
,
as is the case with the SOR method applied to linear systems without any constraints.
In this subsection, we develop adaptive projected SOR (APSOR) algorithms to solve (
P1
) based on the geometric interpretation discussed in section
3.1
.

Before presenting our algorithms, we briefly review the conventional approaches to control the relaxation parameter adaptively for linear systems.
Most existing adaptive SOR methods are categorized into two classes.
The first one is to approximate the spectral radius of the iteration matrix in each iteration using the information obtained during iterations
hy81
.
The parameter computed by this approach can be close to the optimal paramter with least iterations in practical,
but the applicability strongly depends on matrix properties.
The second one is to approximately minimize the residual during iterations
ba03
(see also
me14
;
rr16
).
This approach applies to any symmetric positive definite matrix, but the estimated parameter does not always satisfy the convergence condition
\omega\in(0,2)
.
Further, the computation of several additional matrix-vector multiplications is required to update the parameter,
which indicates that the cost for updating the parameter may be higher than that for updating the solution vector.

Compared to these approaches,
the adaptive SOR method that controls the step size
ms20
has the advantage that
no additional matrix-vector multiplications are required to update the step size (or equivalently, the relaxation parameter).
Furthermore, this approach can be applied to any symmetric positive definite linear systems.
We use this idea for the PSOR method applied to (
P1
).

We start our discussion by presenting a general form of an APSOR algorithm that can build in almost all adaptive SOR techniques.

Remark 4

Our preliminary numerical experiments suggest that the iteration seems to converge even for symmetric positive semidefinite cases.

We now present an APSOR algorithm building on
ms20
;
rl17
and show it in Algorithm
3
.
The idea is as follows.
We check the Armijo condition

V(\bm{x}^{(k+1)})\leq V(\bm{x}^{(k)})+c_{1}\nabla V(\bm{x}^{(k)})^{\text{\tiny\sf T}}(\bm{x}^{(k+1)}-\bm{x}^{(k)})

(28)

and the curvature condition

c_{2}\nabla V(\bm{x}^{(k)})^{\text{\tiny\sf T}}(\bm{x}^{(k+1)}-\bm{x}^{(k)})\leq\nabla V(\bm{x}^{(k+1)})^{\text{\tiny\sf T}}(\bm{x}^{(k+1)}-\bm{x}^{(k)})

(29)

after calculating
\bm{x}^{(k+1)}
.
If the Armijo condition is not satisfied, then the step size is decreased for the next iteration by a factor of
\rho\in(0,1)
:
h^{(k+1)}=\rho h^{(k)}
.
If the two conditions (
28
) and (
29
) are satisfied, then the step size for the next iteration is increased by
h^{(k+1)}=\lambda_{1}h^{(k)}
.
However, if the curvature condition (
29
) is not satisfied, the current step size is deemed too small and is increased for the next iteration by a larger factor of
\lambda_{2}>\lambda_{1}>1
:
h^{(k+1)}=\lambda_{2}h^{(k)}
.
There are a few parameters that must be predetermined in Algorithm
3
.
Two parameters
\varepsilon_{\omega}
and
M_{\omega}
are used to avoid a situation in which
\omega^{(k)}
takes a value close to
0
or
2
(because if this happens, the algorithm may stagnate).
The other parameters need to be predetermined to check the Wolfe-like conditions (
28
) and (
29
).
Fortunately,
ms20
showed that the combination
(c_{1},c_{2},\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\rho)=(0.89,0.95,1.15,1.4,0.85)
is useful for many linear systems.
This combination will also be employed in this study.
Note that our preliminary numerical experiments suggest that the convergence behaviour may not be very sensitive to the choice of these parameters, and for example, a small
c_{1}
such as
c_{1}=10^{-4}
as often employed in the optimization area exhibits a similar performance.

We note that in contrast to the standard applications of the Wolfe conditions that are used to determine the
current
step size, the above conditions are used to determine the
next
step size.
This procedure is possible because the dissipation property
V(\bm{x}^{(k+1)})\leq V(\bm{x}^{(k)})
always follows independently of the step size (as long as it is positive, and even if it is large).
Further, checking these conditions does not require calculation of additional matrix-vector products, which indicates that the cost for updating the step size or parameter is negligible.

3.3
Extensions of APSOR method

Preliminary experiments show the following behaviour for Algorithm
3
.

(a)

The convergence for Algorithm
1
looks linear, but with a slight fluctuation, whereas the rate of convergence may change during iterations.
These are also observed for Algorithm
3
, but the fluctuation tends to be much more significant.

(b)

The convergence performance depends on the initial guess, which is particularly typical for an ill-conditioned
A
or symmetric positive semidefinite
A
.

Below, we present two variants of our APSOR method (Algorithm
3
) to address these issues.

First, we consider (a).
We define the decrement for the PSOR algorithms as follows:

d^{(k)}:=\log_{10}\|\bm{x}^{(k)}-\bm{x}^{(k-1)}\|.

The linearity of the algorithms means that
s^{(k)}:=d^{(k)}-d^{(k-1)}
is almost constant.
However, a significant deviation often occurs for Algorithm
3
.
One method of avoiding such a situation is to fix the relaxation parameter (or equivalently, the step size) after performingx some iterations.
Below, we provide one strategy.

Let us consider the mean of
s^{(k)}
for
m+1
iterations, namely,

\overline{s}_{m}^{(k)}=\frac{1}{m}\big(s^{(k)}+s^{(k-1)}+\cdots+s^{(k-m+1)}\big)=\frac{1}{m}\big(d^{(k)}-d^{(k-m)}\big),

and the corresponding mean of the step sizes

\overline{h}_{m}^{(k)}=\frac{1}{m+1}\big(h^{(k-1)}+h^{(k-2)}+\cdots+h^{(k-m-1)}\big)

or

\overline{\omega}_{m}^{(k)}=\frac{1}{m+1}\big(\omega^{(k-1)}+\omega^{(k-2)}+\cdots+\omega^{(k-m-1)}\big).

Our idea is that
if

\overline{s}_{m}^{(k^{\prime})}>\overline{s}_{m}^{(k^{\prime}-1)}

(30)

for some
k^{\prime}
,
we set the step size to

h^{(k)}=\overline{h}_{m}^{(k^{\prime})}\quad\text{or equivalently}\quad\omega^{(k)}=\overline{\omega}_{m}^{(k^{\prime})}

for
k\geq k^{\prime}
.
We note that as is the case with the SOR method applied to linear systems, we need to wait for a few iterations until the convergence performance becomes almost linear.
Therefore, it seems appropriate to start checking the condition (
30
) after the first
l
iterations are performed.
We recommend setting
m\approx 10
and
determining
l
during the iterations.
An example is
l=l^{\prime}+m
, where
l^{\prime}
is the smallest integer such that
\overline{d}^{(l^{\prime})}<-2
.
The algorithm is summarized in Algorithm

.

The above examples are artificial and are not related to any practical problems.

5
Concluding remarks

In this study, we proposed an adaptive projected SOR method and its variants for nonnegative quadratic programming.
The relaxation parameter is controlled based on the Wolfe conditions.
The algorithms apply any nonnegative quadratic programming problems, and the cost for updating the parameter is negligible for the whole iteration.
Surprisingly, although the parameters of the Wolfe conditions need to be predetermined, the choice which was tuned for solving linear systems
ms20
works well: numerical experiments suggest that the proposed algorithms with the choice of parameters often perform comparably to (or sometimes superior to) the projected SOR method with a nearly optimal relaxation parameter, even if the optimal relaxation parameter seems to be less than
1
.

In future work, we will perform a more detailed convergence analysis for the proposed algorithms.
In particular, as stated in Remark
4
, convergence for symmetric positive semidefinite cases needs to be clarified.

Appendix A
Proof of Theorem
2.1

The proof of Theorem
3.2
goes along the same lines as that of Theorem
2.1
.
Although an equivalent statement to Theorem
2.1
is proved in
cr71a
, we provide the proof here for the readers’ convenience, incorporating some modifications and adopting the notation used in this paper.

The following lemma characterizes the unique solution to the KKT condition.

Lemma 3
(e.g.
ma77
)

Let
A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
.
Let
E\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
be a positive diagonal matrix.
Then, the following two statements are equivalent.

(i)

A\bm{x}-\bm{b}\geq\bm{0}
,
\bm{x}\geq\bm{0}
,
\bm{x}^{\text{\tiny\sf T}}(A\bm{x}-\bm{b})=\bm{0}
.

(ii)

\big(\bm{x}-\alpha E(A\bm{x}-\bm{b})\big)_{+}-\bm{x}=\bm{0}
holds for some or all
\alpha>0
.

Theorem
2.1
can be proved as follows.

Because
V
is bounded from below, say by
C
, and due to the dissipation property for
V
, we see that

C\leq V\big(\bm{x}^{(k+1)}\big)\leq V\big(\bm{x}^{(k)}\big)\leq\cdots\leq V\big(\bm{x}^{(0)}\big),

(31)

and thus, the following limit exists

\lim_{k\to\infty}V\big(\bm{x}^{(k)}\big)=V^{\ast}.

(32)

Because the set

V_{t}=\{\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}\mid V(\bm{x})\leq t\}

(33)

is empty or compact,

\bm{x}^{(k)}
belongs to the compact set
V_{V(\bm{x}^{(0)})}
.
Therefore, for the sequence
\{\bm{x}^{(k)}\}_{k=0}^{\infty}
there exists at least one accumulation point.

Let
\overline{\bm{x}}
be an accumulation point of
\{\bm{x}^{(k)}\}_{k=0}^{\infty}
.
The optimal solution
\bm{x}^{\ast}
to (
P1
) satisfies the KKT condition

A\bm{x}^{\ast}\geq\bm{0},\quad\bm{x}^{\ast}\geq\bm{0},\quad(\bm{x}^{\ast})^{\text{\tiny\sf T}}(A\bm{x}^{\ast}-\bm{b})=\bm{0}.

(34)

Note that a vector satisfying the KKT condition is unique because the objective function and feasible region of NQP are both convex.
Below, we show that

\big(\overline{\bm{x}}-\alpha E(A\overline{\bm{x}}-\bm{b})\big)_{+}-\overline{\bm{x}}=\bm{0}

(35)

for some
\alpha>0
and positive diagonal matrix
E
, which indicates due to Lemma
3
that

\overline{\bm{x}}
must coincide with
\bm{x}^{\ast}
.

Let
\{\bm{x}^{(k_{l})}\}_{l=0}^{\infty}
be a subsequence that converges to
\overline{\bm{x}}
.
Because of the continuity of
V
, we see that

\lim_{l\to\infty}V\big(\bm{x}^{(k_{l})}\big)=V(\overline{\bm{x}}).

(36)

From the convergence of
\{V(\bm{x}^{(k)})\}_{k=0}^{\infty}
and (
22
), it follows that

0=\lim_{l\to\infty}\Big(V\big(\bm{x}^{(k_{l}+1)}\big)-V\big(\bm{x}^{(k_{l})}\big)\Big)\leq-\lim_{l\to\infty}\gamma\big\|\bm{x}^{(k_{l}+1)}-\bm{x}^{(k_{l})}\big\|^{2}\leq 0.

(37)

This relation and the compactness of
V_{V(\bm{x}^{(0)})}
lead to

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\lim_{l\to\infty}\big\|\bm{x}^{(k_{l}+1)}-\bm{x}^{(k_{l})}\big\|^{2}=\lim_{l\to\infty}\big\|\big(\bm{x}^{(k_{l}+1)}-\overline{\bm{x}}\big)-\big(\bm{x}^{(k_{l})}-\overline{\bm{x}}\big)\big\|^{2}

(38)

\displaystyle=\lim_{l\to\infty}\big\|\bm{x}^{(k_{l}+1)}-\overline{\bm{x}}\big\|^{2}.

(39)

Therefore,

\lim_{l\to\infty}\bm{x}^{(k_{l}+1)}=\lim_{l\to\infty}\bm{x}^{(k_{l})}=\overline{\bm{x}}.

(40)

It follows that

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\lim_{l\to\infty}\big\|\bm{x}^{(k_{l}+1)}-\bm{x}^{(k_{l})}\big\|^{2}=\lim_{l\to\infty}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\big(x_{i}^{(k_{l}+1)}-x_{i}^{(k_{l})}\big)^{2}

(41)

\displaystyle=\lim_{l\to\infty}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\bigg\{\bigg[\frac{-1}{a_{ii}\big(1+\frac{h}{2}\big)}\bigg(h\sum_{j<i}a_{ij}x_{j}^{(k_{l}+1)}-\Big(1-\frac{h}{2}\Big)a_{ii}x_{i}^{(k_{l})}+h\sum_{j>i}a_{ij}x_{j}^{(k_{l})}-hb_{i}\bigg)\bigg]_{+}-x_{i}^{(k_{l})}\bigg\}^{2}

(42)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\bigg\{\bigg[\frac{-1}{a_{ii}\big(1+\frac{h}{2}\big)}\bigg(h\sum_{j<i}a_{ij}\overline{x}_{j}-\Big(1-\frac{h}{2}\Big)a_{ii}\overline{x}_{i}+h\sum_{j>i}a_{ij}\overline{x}_{j}-hb_{i}\bigg)\bigg]_{+}-\overline{x}_{i}\bigg\}^{2}

(43)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\bigg\{\Big[\overline{x}_{i}-\frac{h}{1+\frac{h}{2}}\frac{1}{a_{ii}}(A\overline{\bm{x}}-\bm{b})_{i}\Big]_{+}-\overline{x}_{i}\bigg\}^{2}.

(44)

Therefore, by setting

\alpha=\frac{h}{1+\frac{h}{2}},\quad E=D^{-1},

(45)

where
D
is the diagonal matrix of
A
,
we get from Lemma
3
that
\overline{\bm{x}}=\bm{x}^{\ast}
.
This shows that any convergent subsequence converges to the unique solution
\bm{x}^{\ast}
; hence, the whole sequence also converges to
\bm{x}^{\ast}
.
This completes the proof.
