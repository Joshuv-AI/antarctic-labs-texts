MIMO Precoding for Rydberg Atomic Receivers
Mingyao Cui, , Qunsong Zeng, , and Kaibin Huang

Abstract
Leveraging the strong atom-light interaction, a Rydberg atomic receiver can measure radio waves with extreme sensitivity. Existing research primarily focuses on improving the architecture and signal detection capability of atomic receivers, while traditional signal processing schemes at the transmitter side have remained unchanged. As a result, these schemes fail to maximize the throughput of atomic receivers, given that the coupling between atomic dipole moment and radio-wave magnitude results in a nonlinear transmission model in contrast to the traditional linear one. To address this issue, our work proposes to design customized precoding techniques for atomic multiple-input-multiple-output (MIMO) systems to achieve the channel capacity. A strong-reference approximation is initially proposed to linearize the nonlinear transition model of atomic receivers. This facilitates the derivation of atomic-MIMO channel capacity as min ⁡ ( N r / 2 , N t ) ​ log ⁡ ( SNR ) \min(N_{r}/2,N_{t})\log({\rm SNR}) at high signal-to-noise ratios (SNRs) for N r N_{r} receive atomic antennas and N t N_{t} classic transmit antennas. Then, a new digital precoding technique, termed In-phase-and-Quadrature (IQ) aware precoding is presented, which features independent processing of I/Q data streams using four real-valued matrices. The design is shown to be capacity-achieving for the atomic MIMO system. In addition, for the case of large-scale MIMO system, we extend the preceding fully-digital precoding design to the popular hybrid precoding architecture, which cascades a classical analog precoder with a low-dimensional version of the proposed IQ-aware digital precoder. By alternatively optimizing the digital and analog parts, the hybrid design is able to approach the performance of the optimal IQ-aware fully digital precoding. Simulation results validate the superiority of proposed IQ-aware precoding methods over existing techniques in the context of atomic MIMO communication.



I
Introduction

Ever since Hertz validated the existence of electromagnetic (EM) waves, the accurate measurement of EM waves has become the cornerstone of modern wireless communication, remote sensing, and radar
[
1
,
2
]
.
To date, a variety of antenna technologies have been developed, e.g., dipole antennas and aperture antennas. These technologies are based on the same measurement principle: an incident EM wave drives an antenna to generate induced currents, which results in informative electrical signals. This mechanism imposes a notable limitation on the sensitivity (i.e., minimum detectable field strength) of traditional antennas. According to the fluctuation-dissipation theorem
[
3
]
, the random movement of free electrons within metal generates indistinguishable thermal noise, known as Johnson-Nyquist noise, that contaminates electrical signals. As a result, traditional antennas typically achieve a sensitivity on the order of
1{\rm uV}/{\rm m}/\sqrt{\rm Hz}

[
4
]
, making ultra-precise EM-field measurement challenging to achieve
[
5
,
6
]
.

Recently, at the intersection of quantum sensing and wireless communication, an emerging antenna technology known as
Rydberg atomic receiver
has shown the potential in surpassing the sensitivity limitations of traditional antennas
[
7
,
8
]
.
Rydberg atoms, referring to excited atoms operating at high quantum states
[
9
]
, can strongly interact with incident EM waves, triggering electron transitions between resonant energy levels
[
10
]
. Receivers based on Rydberg atoms can monitor the strength of these transitions by leveraging quantum phenomena, such as
ac-Stark shift
and
electromagnetically induced transparency
(EIT), to detect information carried by the fields
[
8
]
.
Rydberg atomic receivers can overcome the limitations imposed by thermal noise, as the interaction between EM fields and atoms theoretically introduces no thermal noise. Furthermore, the quantum shot noise induced during the observation of Rydberg atoms’ quantum states is typically orders of magnitude smaller than the thermal noise of metal antennas
[
5
]
.
With these advantages, atomic receivers can theoretically achieve measurement sensitivity determined by standard quantum limit, e.g.,
1{\rm nV}/{\rm m}/\sqrt{\rm Hz}

[
11
]
.
Thus, such receivers show a promise for use in long-range communication scenarios, such as satellite communications and space-air-ground integrated networks
[
12
]
, where detecting weak signals is crucial.
Aligned with this vision, our work advocates the adoption of atomic receivers as a revolutionary approach to enhance wireless communication systems.

Existing research on the application of Rydberg atomic receivers in detecting modulated wireless signals primarily focuses on the lab demonstration of
single-input-single-output
(SISO) air interfaces.
Specifically, the EM field is captured by an atomic parameter called
Rabi frequency
, which characterizes the electron transition intensity.
By inferring symbols from Rabi frequencies, researchers
have successfully demonstrated the detection of amplitude-modulated (AM) and frequency-modulated (FM) signals
[
13
,
14
,
15
,
16
]
. To enable phase detection,
techniques such as holographic phase-sensing or heterodyne measurement have been employed
[
17
]
. These techniques utilize a
local oscillator
(LO) to send a known reference wave that interferes with the signal wave, thereby allowing phase-modulated symbols to be extracted from the phase difference between the two waves. This method has facilitated the implementation of
phase-shift keying
(PSK) and
quadrature-amplitude modulation
(QAM) communication systems
[
18
,
19
,
20
]
.
Another representative application involves the concurrent detection of multi-frequency signals spanning GHz frequency bands
[
21
,
22
,
23
]
. This is achieved either by mixing different species of alkali atoms within a single receiver or by exciting the atoms to distinct Rydberg quantum states.

In addition to the SISO air interface, recent studies have also explored the spatial diversity and multiplexing gains of atomic antenna arrays. In
[
24
]
, a
single-input-multiple-output
(SIMO) system was designed with four atomic antennas to recover AM signals.
A notable observation from this study was that the experimental receive
signal-to-noise ratio
(SNR) scales linearly with the number of atomic antennas, as in traditional systems.
This atomic SIMO configuration was later implemented for angle-of-arrival estimation using the heterodyne measurement scheme
[
25
,
26
]
. To further reap the multiplexing gain, our prior work developed a multi-user signal detection model for atomic
multiple-input-multiple-output
(MIMO) receivers
[
27
]
. We proved that atomic MIMO receivers exhibit a
non-linear
input-output relationship modeled by
biased phase retrieval

[
28
]
, in contrast to the
linear transition model
in classic MIMO systems. Subsequently, a maximum likelihood-based algorithm was proposed for the simultaneous detection of multi-user signals.

Previous efforts have primarily concentrated on improving the receiver architectures and detection algorithms, while the
design of a classic transmitter optimized for an atomic receiver remains largely
uncharted. Attributed to the altered input-output relationship, existing transmitter-side signal processing methods may not be optimal for atomic receivers.
For instance, classic MIMO communications rely on
singular value decomposition
(SVD)-based precoding at the transmitter side to achieve the channel capacity
\min(N_{r},N_{t})\log{\rm SNR}+\mathcal{O}(1)
, where
N_{r}
and
N_{t}
represent
the numbers of antennas at the receiver and transmitter, respectively
[
29
,
30
]
.
However, this
capacity is based on the
linear transition model
, while the atomic counterpart is non-linear. This difference raises three critical questions that remain unaddressed in the existing literature:

•

What is the information-theoretical limit of an atomic MIMO system?

•

How can we approach this limit through transmitter-side signal processing?

•

Is there any loss in degree-of-freedoms (DoFs) in atomic MIMO systems due to the non-linear transition?

Addressing these questions is pivotal for unleashing the full potential of atomic receivers for next-generation communication systems.
To this end, this work represents the first attempt on designing the transmit precoding to approach the channel capacity of Rydberg atomic MIMO systems.
We consider the point-to-point MIMO communications supported by the mentioned heterodyne measurement scheme
[
17
]
.
The key contributions are summarized as follows.

•

Channel capacity with atomic MIMO receivers
:
The non-linear input-output relationship of atomic MIMO receivers poses a challenge to express its mutual information analytically. To overcome this challenge, we propose a strong-reference approximation to linearize the transition model, which is motivated by the fact that the reference wave is much stronger than the signal wave
due to the proximity of the LOs to atomic receivers

[
18
,
19
,
20
]
.
Based on this approximation, we can convert the
non-linear magnitude detection
model of atomic receivers into a
linear real-part detection
model by discarding high-order infinitesimal quantities, which allows us to obtain the expression of channel capacity.

•

IQ-aware digital precoding
:
An IQ-aware digital precoding is proposed to achieve the channel capacity.
Traditional MIMO precoding widely adopts a complex-valued precoder to process in-phase and quadrature (IQ) baseband symbols.
This method can only produce
circularly-symmetric Gaussian
distributed signals, which are not optimal for the magnitude-detection based atomic MIMO receivers.
In contrast, the proposed IQ-aware digital precoding employs four real-valued matrices to independently process the I/Q baseband symbols, which has the ability to produce
general Gaussian
distributed signals.
The design is shown to achieve the channel capacity with atomic receivers, which is proven to be
\min(N_{r}/2,N_{t})\log{\rm SNR}
at high SNRs. This capacity result reveals that as long as the number of receive atomic antennas,
N_{r}
, is at least twice the number of transmit traditional antennas,
N_{t}
, atomic MIMO receivers experience no loss in spatial DoFs with respect to the traditional counterparts.

•

IQ-aware hybrid precoding
:
For the case of large-scale MIMO systems, we extend the preceding fully digital precoding design to the popular hybrid precoding architecture
[
31
]
. Our design cascades the high-dimensional complex analog precoder implemented using
phase shifters
(PSs) with a low-dimensional version of the proposed IQ-aware digital precoder, where both the fully-connected (FC) and the sub-connected (SC) PS networks are taken into account.
We exploit the alternating minimization approach to alternatively update the digital and analog precoders, with the objective of minimizing the Frobenius distance between the previous IQ-aware fully digital precoder and the current IQ-aware hybrid precoder.
Numerical results validate their close performance.
Moreover, we show that the proposed IQ-aware hybrid precoding schemes significantly outperform classical hybrid precoding methods in the context of atomic MIMO systems.

II
Review of Rydberg Atomic MIMO System

Figure 1:
Rydberg atomic MIMO system.

To begin with, we briefly review the system model of an atomic MIMO communication system. Readers can refer to our prior work
[
27
]
for a more comprehensive understanding of Rydberg atomic receivers.
As illustrated in Fig.
1
, the transmitter employs a conventional
N_{t}
-element antenna array for data transmission. The time-domain radiated signal from the
n
-th antenna,
n\in\{1,2,\cdots,N_{t}\}
, is represented as
x_{n}(t)=A_{n}\cos(\omega t + \gamma_n)
.
To realize phase demodulation at the receiver side, the heterodyne
measurement scheme, also known as the holographic-phase sensing measurement, is adopted
[
17
]
. This scheme introduces a LO as an auxiliary source to radiate predetermined reference signals to the atomic receiver. For ease of discussion, we assume that the reference signal,
x_{R}(t)
, shares the same frequency with the transmitted signal, and thereby it is expressed as
x_{R}(t)=A_{R}\cos(\omega t + \gamma_R)
.

The atomic receiver deploys
N_{r}
Rydberg atomic antennas for detecting the incident signals. Each antenna consists of a vapor cell filled with Alkali atoms. These atoms are excited to Rydberg states via two-photon transition process using a probe laser and a coupling laser
[
5
]
. The incident EM wave can strongly interact with the electric dipole moments of these Rydberg atoms, while the interaction results are read out by photodetectors (PDs) to perform signal detection
[
32
]
. Mathematically speaking, by adopting the multi-path wireless channel model, the EM wave
{\mathbf{E}}_{m}(t)
impinging on the
m
-th atomic antenna,
m\in\{1,2,\cdots,N_{r}\}
, is written as

\displaystyle{\mathbf{E}}_{m}(t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=1}^{N_{t}}\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\bm{\epsilon}_{mnl}\rho_{mnl}A_{n}\cos(\omega t + \phi_{mnl} + \gamma_{n})

\displaystyle\quad\quad+\bm{\epsilon}_{R,m}\rho_{R,m}A_{R}\cos(\omega t + \phi_{R,m} + \gamma_{R}).

(1)

Here,
L
denotes the number of transmitter-to-receiver channel paths. Variables
\bm{\epsilon}_{mnl}

\rho_{mnl}
, and
\varphi_{mnl}

n
-th transmit antenna to the
m
-th atomic antenna via the
\ell
-th path. Variables
\bm{\epsilon}_{R,m}
,
\rho_{R,m}
, and
\varphi_{R,m}

Suppose the signal frequency of the incident EM wave,
\omega
, is tuned to resonant with the transition frequency,
\omega_{ge}
, between two specific energy levels of Rydberg atoms, denoted as
\ket{g}
and
\ket{e}
. Then,
governed by the Lindblad master equation (Schrödinger equation in open quantum systems)
[
33
]
, the electrons of Rydberg atoms will oscillate between these two energy levels, a phenomenon referred to as
electron transition

[
27
]
.
The transition intensity is determined by a physical parameter called

[
33
]
:

\displaystyle\Omega_{m}=\frac{|\bm{\mu}^{T}_{\rm RF}{\mathbf{E}}_{{\rm RF},m}|}{\hbar}.

(2)

where
\bm{\mu}_{\rm RF}
represents the electric dipole moment of Rydberg atoms, and

\displaystyle{\mathbf{E}}_{{\rm RF},m}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=1}^{N_{t}}\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\bm{\epsilon}_{mnl}\rho_{mnl}A_{n}e^{j(\varphi_{mnl}+\gamma_{n})}

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad+\bm{\epsilon}_{R,m}\rho_{R,m}A_{R}e^{j(\varphi_{R,m}+\gamma_{R})},

(3)

where the signal frequency,
\omega

\omega_{ge}=\omega
.
Given that the signals,
\{{\mathbf{E}}_{{\rm RF},m}\}

\{\Omega_{m}\}

This is popularly accomplished by a quantum coherence process called

[

Briefly speaking, the electron transition process changes the rate at which Rydberg atoms absorb photons from an incoming laser
[

[\Omega_{1},\cdots,\Omega_{N_{r}}]^{T}
as a vector,
{\mathbf{y}}=[y_{1},\cdots,y_{N_{r}}]^{T}
, followed by

\displaystyle{\mathbf{y}}=|{\mathbf{H}}{\mathbf{x}}+{\mathbf{r}}|.

(4)

Here,
{\mathbf{H}}\in\mathbb{C}^{N_{r}\times N_{t}}
is the effective channel matrix, whose

-th entry is

{\mathbf{r}}=[r_{1},r_{2},\cdots,r_{N_{r}}]^{T}

Finally, by invoking the law of large numbers, the measurement noise can be modelled as the complex Gaussian distribution,
{\mathbf{w}}\sim\mathcal{CN}(0,\sigma^{2}{\mathbf{I}}_{N_{r}})
. As a result, we arrive at the following input-output relationship for atomic MIMO systems:

\displaystyle{\mathbf{y}}=|{\mathbf{H}}{\mathbf{x}}+{\mathbf{r}}+{\mathbf{w}}|,

(5)

which is a non-linear magnitude-detection model.
To analyze its achievable capacity,

{\mathbf{y}}

{\mathbf{x}}
:

\displaystyle C=\max_{p({\mathbf{x}})}\mathcal{I}({\mathbf{y}};{\mathbf{x}})=\mathcal{H}({\mathbf{y}})-\mathcal{H}({\mathbf{y}}|{\mathbf{x}}),

\mathcal{H}(\cdot)
denotes the entropy of its arguments and
\mathcal{I}(\cdot;\cdot)

III
Channel Capacity With Atomic MIMO Receivers

In this section, we introduce a
strong-reference approximation
to linearize the non-linear transition model in (
5
). This allows the mutual information,
\mathcal{I}({\mathbf{y}};{\mathbf{x}})
, as well as the channel capacity,
C
, to be explicitly formulated.

For conventional MIMO systems, both the amplitude and phase information of received signal are accessible, resulting in a linear transition model:
{\mathbf{y}}={\mathbf{H}}{\mathbf{x}}+{\mathbf{w}}
. It has been proven that the channel capacity,
C^{\prime}
, of this linear MIMO model is achievable if and only if
{\mathbf{x}}
follows a
circularly-symmetric
complex Gaussian (CSCG) distribution
1
1

1

A Gaussian random vector
{\mathbf{x}}
is CSCG if and only if
e^{j\theta}{\mathbf{x}}
has the same distribution with
{\mathbf{x}}
for any real scalar
\theta
.
, i.e.,

\displaystyle C^{\prime}=\max_{{\mathbf{x}}\sim\mathcal{CN}(0,{\mathbf{Q}})}\log\det\left({\mathbf{I}}_{N_{r}}+\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}{\mathbf{H}}{\mathbf{Q}}{\mathbf{H}}^{H}.\right),

(7)

where
{\mathbf{Q}}=\mathsf{E}({\mathbf{x}}{\mathbf{x}}^{H})
. For an atomic MIMO receiver, however, it is evident from (
5
) that it exhibits a non-linear input-output relationship, given that Rabi frequencies,
\{\Omega_{m}\}
, only capture the magnitude of incident EM waves. This fact makes it intractable to express the mutual information,
\mathcal{I}({\mathbf{y}};{\mathbf{x}})
, analytically, let alone calculating the channel capacity,
C
.

To overcome the above challenge, we notice that the LO (see Fig.
1
) is typically placed close to the atomic receiver
[
18
,
19
,
20
,
34
]
. As a result, the LO-to-receiver distance (e.g., tens of centimeters) is much shorter than the transmitter-to-receiver distance (e.g., tens of meters), resulting in a much stronger reference signal than the wireless signal and noise.
This admits a strong-reference approximation to linearize the transmission model in (
5
) as follows.

Lemma 1
.

(Strong-reference approximation):

If each entry of the reference signal is greatly stronger than the wireless signal and noise, i.e.,

|r_{m}|\gg|{\mathbf{H}}_{m,:}{\mathbf{x}}+w_{m}|
, we can approximate
{\mathbf{y}}
in (
5
) as

\displaystyle\boxed{{\mathbf{y}}-|{\mathbf{r}}|\approx\bar{{\mathbf{y}}}=\mathrm{Re}(\widetilde{{\mathbf{H}}}{\mathbf{x}})+\bar{{\mathbf{w}}},}

(8)

where
\widetilde{{\mathbf{H}}}_{m,:}\overset{\Delta}{=}e^{-j\angle r_{m}}{\mathbf{H}}_{m,:}
and
\bar{{\mathbf{w}}}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}{\mathbf{I}}_{N_{r}})
.

Proof:

(See Appendix
-A
).

\Box

Lemma
1
indicates that, in the strong-reference-signal regime, the
non-linear
magnitude detector in (
5
) can be transformed into a
linear
real-part detector in (
8
) by subtracting the offset
|{\mathbf{r}}|
.
More importantly, the complicated mutual information,
\mathcal{I}({\mathbf{y}};{\mathbf{x}})=\mathcal{I}({{\mathbf{y}}}-|{\mathbf{r}}|;{\mathbf{x}})
, is converted to a tractable expression:
\mathcal{I}({{\mathbf{y}}}-|{\mathbf{r}}|;{\mathbf{x}})\rightarrow\mathcal{I}(\bar{{\mathbf{y}}};{\mathbf{x}})
.
To write
\mathcal{I}(\bar{{\mathbf{y}}};{\mathbf{x}})
analytically, we introduce the subscripts
I
and
Q
to denote the real (in-phase) and imaginary (quadrature) components of a complex number, e.g.,
\widetilde{{\mathbf{H}}}=\widetilde{{\mathbf{H}}}_{I}+j\widetilde{{\mathbf{H}}}_{Q}
and
{\mathbf{x}}={\mathbf{x}}_{I}+j{\mathbf{x}}_{Q}
. Then, the received signal
\bar{{\mathbf{y}}}
in (
8
) is rewritten as

\displaystyle\bar{{\mathbf{y}}}=(\widetilde{{\mathbf{H}}}_{I},-\widetilde{{\mathbf{H}}}_{Q})\left(\begin{array}[]{c}{\mathbf{x}}_{I}\\
{\mathbf{x}}_{Q}\end{array}\right)+{\mathbf{w}}_{I}={\bar{{\mathbf{H}}}}{\bar{{\mathbf{x}}}}+\bar{{\mathbf{w}}},

where
\bar{{\mathbf{H}}}\overset{\Delta}{=}(\widetilde{{\mathbf{H}}}_{I},-\widetilde{{\mathbf{H}}}_{Q})\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{r}\times 2N_{t}}
and
\bar{{\mathbf{x}}}\overset{\Delta}{=}({{\mathbf{x}}}_{I}^{T},{{\mathbf{x}}}_{Q}^{T})^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{2N_{t}\times 1}
represent the
real-valued
channel and radiated signal, respectively. Attributed to the linear behavior of real-part detectors, the maximal throughput is achieved when the radiated signal
\bar{{\mathbf{x}}}
follows the Gaussian distribution
\bar{{\mathbf{x}}}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\bar{{\mathbf{Q}}})
, with the covariance matrix
\bar{{\mathbf{Q}}}=\mathsf{E}(\bar{{\mathbf{x}}}\bar{{\mathbf{x}}}^{T})\in\mathbb{R}^{2N_{t}\times 2N_{t}}
. Thereafter, the mutual information is expressed as

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}(\bar{{\mathbf{y}}};{\mathbf{x}})=\frac{1}{2}\log\det(
\mb{I}_{N_r} + \frac{2}{\sigma^2} \bar{\mb{H}} \bar{\mb{Q}} \bar{\mb{H}}^T
).

(11)

Here, the discount factor
\frac{1}{2}
arises because the variables
\bar{{\mathbf{H}}}
,
\bar{{\mathbf{x}}}
, and
\bar{{\mathbf{w}}}
are all real valued.
Moreover, considering the total power constraint imposed on the radiated signal, we have

\displaystyle\mathsf{Tr}\{\mathsf{E}({\mathbf{x}}{\mathbf{x}}^{H})\}=\mathsf{Tr}\{\mathsf{E}(\bar{{\mathbf{x}}}\bar{{\mathbf{x}}}^{T})\}=\mathsf{Tr}\{\bar{{\mathbf{Q}}}\}=P,

(12)

where
P
is the transmission power.
In summary, the channel capacity,
C
, of atomic MIMO systems can be formulated as

\displaystyle\boxed{C=\max_{\mathsf{Tr}\{\bar{{\mathbf{Q}}}\}=P,\>\bar{{\mathbf{Q}}}\succeq 0}\frac{1}{2}\log\det\left({\mathbf{I}}_{N_{r}}+\frac{2}{\sigma^{2}}\bar{{\mathbf{H}}}\bar{{\mathbf{Q}}}\bar{{\mathbf{H}}}^{T}\right).}

(13)

Remark 1
.

By comparing the channel capacities of conventional MIMO in (
7
) and atomic MIMO in (
13
), one can observe that the channel matrix is converted from a complex form,
{\mathbf{H}}
, to a real form,
\bar{{\mathbf{H}}}
.
Moreover, the latter capacity is directly related to the radiated signals
{\mathbf{x}}_{I}
and
{\mathbf{x}}_{Q}
via their joint distribution,
\bar{{\mathbf{x}}}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\bar{{\mathbf{Q}}})
, rather than their complex form,
{\mathbf{x}}\sim\mathcal{CN}(0,{{\mathbf{Q}}})
.
These observations suggest that achieving the channel capacity of atomic MIMO requires that
{\mathbf{x}}_{I}
and
{\mathbf{x}}_{Q}
(or equivalently
{\mathbf{x}}={\mathbf{x}}_{I}+j{\mathbf{x}}_{Q}
) follow a general Gaussian distribution, instead of a CSCG distribution in (
7
). This difference leads to a distinguishing feature of IQ-aware precoding for atomic MIMO.

IV
IQ-Aware Digital Precoding

In this section, we propose an IQ-aware digital precoding method to achieve the channel capacity,
C
, in (
13
) of atomic MIMO systems.
The asymptotic channel capacity at high SNRs is subsequently analyzed to unveil the system DoF.

IV-A

Precoding Design

We define
{\mathbf{s}}_{I}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{s}}
and
{\mathbf{s}}_{Q}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{s}}
as the data symbols in the in-phase and quadrature channels, respectively, where
N_{s}
is the number of complex data streams.
The overall baseband data is denoted as
\bar{{\mathbf{s}}}=[{\mathbf{s}}_{I}^{T},{\mathbf{s}}_{Q}^{T}]^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{2N_{s}}
in the real form and
{\mathbf{s}}={\mathbf{s}}_{I}+j{\mathbf{s}}_{Q}\in\mathbb{C}^{N_{s}}
in the complex form. The symbols
{\mathbf{s}}_{I}
and
{\mathbf{s}}_{Q}
follow i.i.d Gaussian distributions
\mathcal{N}(0,\frac{1}{2}{\mathbf{I}}_{N_{s}})
, resulting in the CSCG distribution of
{\mathbf{s}}
. The objective of precoding is to design a linear mapping
f:\bar{{\mathbf{s}}}\mapsto\bar{{\mathbf{x}}}
such that the covariance matrix
\bar{{\mathbf{Q}}}
can maximize the mutual information in (
13
).

IV-A
1
Sub-optimality of traditional digital precoding

To achieve the channel capacity of a MIMO system, the most intuitive method is to implement
traditional complex-valued digital precoding
as it has been demonstrated to be optimal in traditional MIMO systems.
This, however, is sub-optimal in the case of an atomic MIMO system.
Specifically, the classical precoding uses a complex matrix,
{\mathbf{F}}={\mathbf{F}}_{I}+j{\mathbf{F}}_{Q}\in\mathbb{C}^{N_{t}\times N_{s}}
, to jointly process the baseband symbols
{\mathbf{s}}_{I}
and
{\mathbf{s}}_{Q}
before the IQ modulator, as shown in Fig.
2
(a).
After merging the IQ components, the transmit signal in the complex form is thereby the product of
{\mathbf{F}}
and
{\mathbf{s}}
:

\displaystyle{\mathbf{x}}={\mathbf{F}}{\mathbf{s}}={\mathbf{F}}_{I}{\mathbf{s}}_{I}-{\mathbf{F}}_{Q}{\mathbf{s}}_{Q}+j({\mathbf{F}}_{Q}{\mathbf{s}}_{I}+{\mathbf{F}}_{I}{\mathbf{s}}_{Q}),

(14)

whose covariance is
{\mathbf{Q}}=\mathsf{E}({\mathbf{x}}{\mathbf{x}}^{H})={\mathbf{F}}{\mathbf{F}}^{H}
.
For subsequent exposition, it is useful to write
{\mathbf{x}}
in (
14
) as the equivalent real form:

\displaystyle\bar{{\mathbf{x}}}=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}{\mathbf{x}}_{I}\\
{\mathbf{x}}_{Q}\end{array}\right)=\underbrace{\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}{\mathbf{F}}_{I}&-{\mathbf{F}}_{Q}\\
{\mathbf{F}}_{Q}&{\mathbf{F}}_{I}\end{array}\right)}_{{\mathbf{F}}_{r}}{\left(\begin{array}[]{c}{\mathbf{s}}_{I}\\
{\mathbf{s}}_{Q}\end{array}\right)},

whose covariance matrix is
\bar{{\mathbf{Q}}}=\mathsf{E}(\bar{{\mathbf{x}}}\bar{{\mathbf{x}}}^{T})=\frac{1}{2}{\mathbf{F}}_{r}{\mathbf{F}}_{r}^{T}
.
The optimality of the precoder structure of
{\mathbf{F}}
for traditional MIMO and its sub-optimality for atomic MIMO are explained as follows.

(a) Classical complex-valued digital precoding for classical receivers and (b) proposed IQ-aware digital precoding for atomic receivers.

In a traditional MIMO system, the CSCG distribution is capacity achieving. Thus given CSCG distributed data symbols,
{\mathbf{s}}
, as input, a linear complex-valued precoder,
{\mathbf{F}}
, that retains the CSCG distribution can be optimal. Specifically, the optimal
{\mathbf{F}}
is aligned with the principal singular-vectors of the channel
{\mathbf{H}}
. On the contrary, the CSCG precoded symbols cannot achieve the channel capacity of an atomic MIMO system, making the precoder
{\mathbf{F}}
sub-optimal. To see the sub-optimality, one can observe from (
IV-A1
) that the real-equivalence of
{\mathbf{F}}
,
{\mathbf{F}}_{r}
, has a
block-coupled constraint
, i.e., its diagonal block components are identical while its off-diagonal block components are opposite. Obviously, introducing this structural constraint reduces the channel capacity of atomic MIMO in (
13
). To elaborate,
{\mathbf{F}}_{r}
under the constraint cannot always align with the principal singular-vectors of the real channel
\bar{{\mathbf{H}}}
to achieve its capacity. For example, we consider a simple scenario:
N_{r}=N_{t}=2
and
N_{s}=1
. The
2\times 2
complex channel matrix is
{\mathbf{H}}=[2,j;j,1]
and the reference signal is
{\mathbf{r}}=[1;1]
. In this context, the
2\times 4
real channel matrix is
\bar{{\mathbf{H}}}=[{\mathbf{H}}_{I},-{\mathbf{H}}_{Q}]=[2,0,0,-1;0,1,-1,0]
. The principal singular vectors of
\bar{{\mathbf{H}}}
are calculated as

\bar{{\mathbf{v}}}_{1}=[-0.8944,0,0,0.4472]^{T}
and
\bar{{\mathbf{v}}}_{2}=[0,-0.7071,0.7071,0]^{T}
. Evidently, the singular vectors
\bar{{\mathbf{v}}}_{1}
and
\bar{{\mathbf{v}}}_{2}
cannot form a precoding matrix satisfying the block-coupled constraint of
{\mathbf{F}}_{r}
. In this context, precoder
{\mathbf{F}}_{r}
and its complex-equivalence
{\mathbf{F}}
is not optimal.

IV-A
2
Proposed IQ-aware digital precoding

The sub-optimality of conventional precoding motivates the proposed
IQ-aware digital precoding
in Fig.
2
(b), which relaxes the block-coupled constraint and thereby is capable of generating general-Gaussian symbols despite CSCG input.
Specifically, compared with the joint manipulation of baseband IQ symbols using the complex precoder
{\mathbf{F}}
, the IQ-aware precoding employs four real-valued matrices,
{\mathbf{F}}_{k\ell}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{t}\times N_{s}},k\in\{1,2\},\ell\in\{1,2\}
, to independently process
{\mathbf{s}}_{I}
and
{\mathbf{s}}_{Q}
. By this means, the signals entering the IQ modulator become
{\mathbf{F}}_{11}{\mathbf{s}}_{I}+{\mathbf{F}}_{12}{\mathbf{s}}_{Q}
and
{\mathbf{F}}_{21}{\mathbf{s}}_{I}+{\mathbf{F}}_{22}{\mathbf{s}}_{Q}
. The output signal in complex form is thereby

\displaystyle{\mathbf{x}}={\mathbf{F}}_{11}{\mathbf{s}}_{I}+{\mathbf{F}}_{12}{\mathbf{s}}_{Q}+j({\mathbf{F}}_{21}{\mathbf{s}}_{I}+{\mathbf{F}}_{22}{\mathbf{s}}_{Q}).

(21)

One can verify that the equivalent real-form signal is

\displaystyle\bar{{\mathbf{x}}}=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}{\mathbf{x}}_{I}\\
{\mathbf{x}}_{Q}\end{array}\right)=\underbrace{\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}{\mathbf{F}}_{11}&{\mathbf{F}}_{12}\\
{\mathbf{F}}_{21}&{\mathbf{F}}_{22}\end{array}\right)}_{\bar{{\mathbf{F}}}}{\left(\begin{array}[]{c}{\mathbf{s}}_{I}\\
{\mathbf{s}}_{Q}\end{array}\right)},

whose covariance matrix is
\bar{{\mathbf{Q}}}=\frac{1}{2}\bar{{\mathbf{F}}}\bar{{\mathbf{F}}}^{T}
.
The matrix
\bar{{\mathbf{F}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{2N_{t}\times 2N_{s}}
represents the “IQ-aware digital precoder”. By comparing (
21
) and (
IV-A2
) with their counterparts in (
14
) and (
IV-A1
), two important features of IQ-aware digital precoding can be observed. First, the complex signal
{\mathbf{x}}
in (
21
) is no longer the product of a complex precoder and a complex symbol as in (
14
), making it possible for
{\mathbf{x}}
to be general Gaussian distributed. Second, the equivalent real-valued precoder
\bar{{\mathbf{F}}}
in (
IV-A2
), now is
block-decoupled
with independent block components,
\{{\mathbf{F}}_{11},{\mathbf{F}}_{12},{\mathbf{F}}_{21},{\mathbf{F}}_{22}\}
, which relaxes the block-coupled constraint of
{\mathbf{F}}_{r}
in (
IV-A1
).
These two features give the proposed IQ-aware digital precoding more flexibility in manipulating the transmitted signal
{\mathbf{x}}
, allowing for the utilization of SVD-based precoding technique to achieve the channel capacity.

To be specific, we let
\bar{{\mathbf{V}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{2N_{t}\times 2N_{t}}
be
the right-singular matrix of
\bar{{\mathbf{H}}}
,
and
\{\bar{\lambda}_{1},\cdots,\bar{\lambda}_{\min(N_{r},2N_{t})}\}
the corresponding singular values in a descending order.
The optimal IQ-aware digital precoder
\bar{{\mathbf{F}}}
is represented as

\displaystyle\bar{{\mathbf{F}}}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}{\mathbf{F}}_{11}&{\mathbf{F}}_{12}\\
{\mathbf{F}}_{21}&{\mathbf{F}}_{22}\end{array}\right)=\bar{{\mathbf{V}}}_{:,1:2N_{s}}\bar{{\mathbf{P}}}^{\frac{1}{2}}.

The power allocation matrix
\bar{{\mathbf{P}}}\overset{\Delta}{=}\mathrm{diag}\{\bar{p}_{1},\cdots,\bar{p}_{2N_{s}}\}
is determined by the water-filling principle:

\bar{p}_{k}=\left(\mu-\frac{\sigma^{2}}{\bar{\lambda}_{k}^{2}}\right)^{+}
,
where the water level
\mu
is adjusted to satisfy the power constraint
\mathsf{Tr}\{\bar{{\mathbf{Q}}}\}=\frac{1}{2}\mathsf{Tr}\{\bar{{\mathbf{P}}}\}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{2N_{s}}\bar{p}_{k}=P
. Consequently, by processing baseband data
{\mathbf{s}}_{I}
and
{\mathbf{s}}_{Q}
using
\{{\mathbf{F}}_{11},{\mathbf{F}}_{12},{\mathbf{F}}_{21},{\mathbf{F}}_{22}\}
, the channel capacity in (
13
) is achieved as:

\displaystyle C=\sum_{k=1}^{2N_{s}}\frac{1}{2}\log\left(1+\frac{\bar{\lambda}_{k}^{2}\bar{p}_{k}}{\sigma^{2}}\right),

(30)

where the number of real-data streams,
2N_{s}
, should not exceed the rank of the real-channel matrix, denoted as
\min(N_{r},2N_{t})
.

IV-B

Channel Capacity Analysis

To gain more insights into atomic MIMO receivers, we analyze the asymptotic behavior of the channel capacity,
C
, at high SNRs
with the consideration of the maximal number of multiplexed data streams, i.e.,
2N_{s}=\min(N_{r},2N_{t})
.

Lemma 2
.

Define
\text{SNR}=\frac{P}{\sigma^{2}}
. When
\text{SNR}\rightarrow+\infty
and
2N_{s}=\min(N_{r},2N_{t})
, the channel capacity is asymptotically

\displaystyle\boxed{C=\min\left(\frac{N_{r}}{2},N_{t}\right)\log\frac{\mathrm{SNR}}{\min\left(\frac{N_{r}}{2},N_{t}\right)}+\mathcal{O}(1).}

(31)

Proof:

Equation (
31
) holds because
p_{k}\rightarrow\frac{P}{N_{s}}
when
\text{SNR}\rightarrow+\infty
and
\log(1 + x)\rightarrow\log(x)
when
x\rightarrow+\infty
.

\Box

Lemma
2
suggests that the capacity of an atomic MIMO system is improved by
\min(N_{r}/2,N_{t})
bit/s/Hz for every 3 dB increment in SNR, which corresponds to the DoFs of
\lim_{\text{SNR}\rightarrow\infty}\frac{C_{e}}{\log\text{SNR}}=\min\left(\frac{N_{r}}{2},N_{t}\right)

[
35
]
.
This result reflects the fact that half DoFs are lost at the receiver side due to the absence of imaginary-part information in (
8
), while the DoFs remain intact at the transmitter side. Thereafter, a discount factor
\frac{1}{2}
is imposed on
N_{r}
, giving the DoFs of
\min(\frac{N_{r}}{2},N_{t})
.

\frac{1}{2}\min(N_{r},N_{t})

.

For a better understanding, we compare three DoFs,
\min(N_{r},N_{t})
,
\min(\frac{N_{r}}{2},N_{t})
, and
\frac{1}{2}\min(N_{r},N_{t})
, as a function of
N_{r}
in Fig.
3
.
They correspond to the following three cases, respectively: 1)
the traditional MIMO system, 2) the atomic MIMO system, 3) the In-phase transmission, where only
real-valued symbols
, such as
{\mathbf{s}}_{I}
, are transmitted through a traditional
N_{r}\times N_{t}
MIMO channel.
As
N_{r}
increases, the DoFs
\min(\frac{N_{r}}{2},N_{t})
gradually climb from
\frac{1}{2}\min(N_{r},N_{t})
to
\min(N_{r},N_{t})
. Specifically, when
N_{r}<N_{t}
, we have
\min\left(\frac{N_{r}}{2},N_{t}\right)=\frac{1}{2}\min\left({N_{r}},N_{t}\right)=\frac{1}{2}N_{r}
. The DoFs of atomic MIMO systems are exactly half of that of conventional systems. In this context, it is sufficient to achieve the capacity of atomic receivers by transmitting
\frac{N_{r}}{2}
complex-valued symbols, or equivalently
N_{r}
real-valued symbols, such as
{\mathbf{s}}_{I}
.
Moreover, as
N_{r}
grows from
N_{t}
to
2N_{t}
, the DoFs,
\min(N_{r},N_{t})
, saturate while the gap between
\min(N_{r},N_{t})
and
\min(N_{r}/2,N_{t})
narrows.
Particularly, when
N_{r}\geq 2N_{t}
, we have
\min(\frac{N_{r}}{2},N_{t})=\min(N_{r},N_{t})=N_{t}
. This relationship indicates that an atomic receiver does not suffer any DoF loss compared to its traditional counterpart if the number of atomic antennas is at least twice the number of transmit antennas. This result is intuitive because
N_{r}
real-valued observations are sufficient to recover
N_{t}\leq\frac{N_{r}}{2}
complex-valued symbols. Consequently, under a same high SNR, the capacity gap between atomic and traditional MIMO systems tends to a constant independent of SNR. In summary, to fully exploit the multiplexing gain of an atomic MIMO system, it is necessary to have
N_{r}\geq 2N_{t}
.

V
IQ-Aware Hybrid Precoding

For massive MIMO systems employing large-scale antenna arrays, hybrid precoding that incorporates digital and analog precoders is a more practical solution than fully digital precoding
[
36
,
37
,
38
,
39
]
.
Its essential idea is to cascade a low-dimensional digital processor (i.e., digital precoder) and a high-dimensional analog PS network (i.e., analog precoder), so as to reduce the number of costly radio-frequency (RF) chains. In this section, we extend the fully digital IQ-aware precoding in the preceding section to realize IQ-aware hybrid precoding.
Compared with traditional designs, a new challenge arises from the fact that the need of independent manipulation of IQ symbols cannot be implemented using analog circuits (i.e., a PS network).

To tackle this challenge, the proposed IQ-aware hybrid precoding architecture deploys a low-dimensional IQ-aware digital precoder to assist the analog PS network. As illustrated in Fig.
4
, both the FC and SC phase-shifter networks are considered. Compared with the IQ-aware fully digital precoder in (
IV-A2
) where the entire precoding matrix is block-decoupled, the IQ-aware hybrid precoder is constructed by the product of a block-decoupled digital precoder and a block-coupled analog precoder.
We will show that, despite the block-coupled constraint imposed on the analog part, the IQ-aware hybrid precoding is able to closely approximate the fully digital counterpart.

IQ-Aware Hybrid Precoding with a FC-PS Network

Problem statement

We first consider the IQ-aware hybrid precoding with a FC-PS network, where each RF chain is connected to all BS antennas via PSs. Denote the number of RF chains as
N_{\rm RF}
, satisfying
N_{s}\leq N_{\rm RF}\ll N_{t}
. In the baseband processor, an IQ-aware digital precoder
\bar{{\mathbf{D}}}
of dimension

is employed, with block components
{\mathbf{D}}_{k\ell}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{\rm RF}\times N_{s}},k\in\{1,2\},\ell\in\{1,2\}

{\mathbf{s}}_{I}
and
{\mathbf{s}}_{Q}

. At the RF front-end, the analog precoder is expressed by a complex matrix
{\mathbf{A}}={\mathbf{A}}_{I}+j{\mathbf{A}}_{Q}\in\mathbb{C}^{N_{t}\times N_{\rm RF}}
. Given that PSs can only adjust the phases of signals, each element of
{\mathbf{A}}
must satisfy the unit modulus constraint:
|{\mathbf{A}}_{m,n}|^{2}={\mathbf{A}}_{I,m,n}^{2}+{\mathbf{A}}_{Q,m,n}^{2}=1,\forall m,n
. Thereafter, the transmitted signal
{\mathbf{x}}
is given as
{\mathbf{x}}={\mathbf{A}}{\mathbf{s}}_{\rm rf}
, whose real form is

\displaystyle\bar{{\mathbf{x}}}=\underbrace{\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}{\mathbf{A}}_{I}&-{\mathbf{A}}_{Q}\\
{\mathbf{A}}_{Q}&{\mathbf{A}}_{I}\end{array}\right)}_{\bar{{\mathbf{A}}}}\underbrace{\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}{\mathbf{D}}_{11}&{\mathbf{D}}_{12}\\
{\mathbf{D}}_{21}&{\mathbf{D}}_{22}\end{array}\right)}_{\bar{{\mathbf{D}}}}\underbrace{\left(\begin{array}[]{c}{\mathbf{s}}_{I}\\
{\mathbf{s}}_{Q}\end{array}\right)}_{\bar{{\mathbf{s}}}}.

The covariance matrix of
\bar{{\mathbf{x}}}
is
\bar{{\mathbf{Q}}}=\frac{1}{2}\bar{{\mathbf{A}}}\bar{{\mathbf{D}}}\bar{{\mathbf{D}}}^{T}\bar{{\mathbf{A}}}^{T}

IV-A2
) and (
V-A1
), one can observe that the analog part
\bar{{\mathbf{A}}}
is subject to the block-coupled constraint, since analog PSs cannot distinguish the combined IQ signals. Fortunately, the hybrid precoder,
\bar{{\mathbf{A}}}\bar{{\mathbf{D}}}
, still has the ability to produce general Gaussian distributed signals
\bar{{\mathbf{x}}}
because the digital part,
\bar{{\mathbf{D}}}
, is IQ-aware and has independent block components. This ability allows the accurate approximation of the IQ-aware fully digital precoder,
\bar{{\mathbf{F}}}
, by the hybrid counterpart,
\bar{{\mathbf{A}}}\bar{{\mathbf{D}}}
.
To realize this objective, the joint optimization of the matrices
\bar{{\mathbf{A}}}
and
\bar{{\mathbf{D}}}
are formulated as

\displaystyle\min_{\bar{{\mathbf{A}}},\bar{{\mathbf{D}}}}\>\>

\displaystyle\|\bar{{\mathbf{F}}}-\bar{{\mathbf{A}}}\bar{{\mathbf{D}}}\|_{F}^{2},

(38)

\displaystyle\mathrm{s.t.}\>\>

\displaystyle{\mathbf{A}}_{I,m,n}^{2}+{\mathbf{A}}_{Q,m,n}^{2}=1,\>\forall m,n,

(
38
a)

\displaystyle\>\frac{1}{2}\mathsf{Tr}\{\bar{{\mathbf{A}}}\bar{{\mathbf{D}}}\bar{{\mathbf{D}}}^{T}\bar{{\mathbf{A}}}^{T}\}=P,

(
38
b)

wherein (

) is the total power constraint. In the subsequent discussions, we will devise an alternating minimization algorithm to solve
\bar{{\mathbf{A}}}\bar{{\mathbf{D}}}
from problem (
38
) and use numerical simulations to show that the achieved rate of
\bar{{\mathbf{A}}}\bar{{\mathbf{D}}}
can tightly approach the channel capacity in (

).

Problem transformation

Owing to the non-convex modulus constraint in (

), the block-coupled constraint of
\bar{{\mathbf{A}}}
, as well as the product of
\bar{{\mathbf{A}}}
and
\bar{{\mathbf{D}}}
, directly solving problem (
38

38

\bar{{\mathbf{A}}}
from its multiplication with
\bar{{\mathbf{D}}}
, which can significantly simplify the precoding design.

Note that the columns of the optimal precoder
\bar{{\mathbf{F}}}
are mutually orthogonal to mitigate the interference between data streams.
The designed hybrid precoder
\bar{{\mathbf{A}}}\bar{{\mathbf{D}}}
is thus expected to maintain column orthogonality as well.
In massive MIMO systems,
N_{t}\gg N_{RF}
, the relationship
\bar{{\mathbf{A}}}^{T}\bar{{\mathbf{A}}}\approx N_{t}{\mathbf{I}}_{2N_{\rm RF}}
holds with high probability
[
37
,
38
]
.
This is because the diagonal elements of
\bar{{\mathbf{A}}}^{T}\bar{{\mathbf{A}}}
are exactly
N_{t}
while the off-diagonal elements can be approximated as the summation of
2N_{t}
independent terms, which is much less than
N_{t}
with high probability for
N_{t}\gg 1
. These properties imply that the columns of the optimal
\bar{{\mathbf{D}}}
are approximately orthogonal as well. Motivated by this observation, we introduce a column-orthogonal constraint on
\bar{{\mathbf{D}}}
and thereby replace it by
\gamma\bar{{\mathbf{D}}}_{u}
, where
\bar{{\mathbf{D}}}^{T}_{u}\bar{{\mathbf{D}}}_{u}={\mathbf{I}}_{2N_{s}}
. The scalar
\gamma
is introduced to meet the total power constraint (

\gamma\approx\sqrt{\frac{2P}{N_{t}}}
by considering
\bar{{\mathbf{A}}}^{T}\bar{{\mathbf{A}}}\approx N_{t}{\mathbf{I}}_{2N_{\rm RF}}
.
Consequently, the hybrid precoding design is recast as

\displaystyle\min_{\bar{{\mathbf{A}}},\bar{{\mathbf{D}}}_{u}}\>\>

\displaystyle\|\bar{{\mathbf{F}}}-\gamma\bar{{\mathbf{A}}}\bar{{\mathbf{D}}}_{u}\|_{F}^{2},

(39)

\displaystyle\mathrm{s.t.}\>\>

\displaystyle{\mathbf{A}}_{I,m,n}^{2}+{\mathbf{A}}_{Q,m,n}^{2}=1,\>\forall m,n,

(
39
a)

\displaystyle\>\bar{{\mathbf{D}}}^{T}_{u}\bar{{\mathbf{D}}}_{u}={\mathbf{I}}_{2N_{s}}.

(

\bar{{\mathbf{D}}}_{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{2N_{\rm RF}\times 2N_{s}}

\bar{{\mathbf{A}}}
and
\bar{{\mathbf{D}}}_{u}

\bar{{\mathbf{D}}}_{c}\in\mathbb{R}^{2N_{\rm RF}\times(2N_{\rm RF}-2N_{s})}

\bar{{\mathbf{F}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{2N_{t}\times 2N_{s}}

\bar{{\mathbf{A}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{2N_{t}\times 2N_{\rm RF}}

\bar{{\mathbf{D}}}_{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{2N_{\rm RF}\times 2N_{s}}

\displaystyle\min_{\bar{{\mathbf{A}}},\bar{{\mathbf{D}}}_{u}}\|\bar{{\mathbf{F}}}-\gamma\bar{{\mathbf{A}}}\bar{{\mathbf{D}}}_{u}\|_{F}^{2}=\min_{\bar{{\mathbf{A}}},\widetilde{{\mathbf{D}}},\bar{{\mathbf{F}}}_{c}}\|\widetilde{{\mathbf{F}}}-\gamma\bar{{\mathbf{A}}}\widetilde{{\mathbf{D}}}\|_{F}^{2},

(See Appendix C).

\displaystyle(\frac{1}{\gamma}{\mathbf{Z}}_{I,m,n}-{\mathbf{A}}_{I,m,n})^{2}+(\frac{1}{\gamma}{\mathbf{Z}}_{Q,m,n}-{\mathbf{A}}_{Q,m,n})^{2}

\displaystyle{\mathbf{A}}_{I,m,n}^{2}+{\mathbf{A}}_{Q,m,n}^{2}=1.

V-B

IQ-Aware Hybrid Precoding with a SC-PS Network

V-B
1
Problem statement

To show that our proposed IQ-aware hybrid precoding is feasible to general analog circuits, we now turn to the investigation of SC-PS networks. In this architecture, each RF is connected to a subset of the BS array with

K=N_{t}/N_{\rm RF}
antennas. The number of required PSs is thus
N_{t}
. With much reduced PSs, the SC architecture exhibits lower spectral efficiency than the FC one, but is more energy-efficient.
The sub-connected feature states that the analog precoder
{\mathbf{A}}={\mathbf{A}}_{I}+j{\mathbf{A}}_{Q}
is now a block diagonal matrix:

\displaystyle{\mathbf{A}}={\rm blkdiag}\{{\mathbf{p}}_{1},{\mathbf{p}}_{2},\cdots,{\mathbf{p}}_{N_{\rm RF}}\},

(57)

where
{\mathbf{p}}_{n}=[{\rm exp}(j\theta_{n,1}),\cdots,{\rm exp}(j\theta_{n,K})]^{T}
represent the PSs connected to the
n
-th RF chain.
Thanks to this block diagonal property, one can verify that
{\mathbf{A}}^{H}{\mathbf{A}}=K{\mathbf{I}}_{N_{\rm RF}}
and
\bar{{\mathbf{A}}}^{T}\bar{{\mathbf{A}}}=K{\mathbf{I}}_{2N_{\rm RF}}
with ease. These facts automatically releases
\bar{{\mathbf{A}}}
from the total power constraint because
\frac{1}{2}\mathsf{Tr}\{\bar{{\mathbf{A}}}\bar{{\mathbf{D}}}\bar{{\mathbf{D}}}^{T}\bar{{\mathbf{A}}}^{T}\}=\frac{K}{2}\mathsf{Tr}\{\bar{{\mathbf{D}}}\bar{{\mathbf{D}}}^{T}\}=P
. Henceforth, the SC hybrid precoding design problem is formulated as

\displaystyle\max_{\bar{{\mathbf{A}}},\bar{{\mathbf{D}}}}\>\>

\displaystyle\|\bar{{\mathbf{F}}}-\bar{{\mathbf{A}}}\bar{{\mathbf{D}}}\|_{F}^{2}

(58)

\displaystyle\mathrm{s.t.}\>\>

\displaystyle{\mathbf{A}}={\rm blkdiag}\{{\mathbf{p}}_{1},{\mathbf{p}}_{2},\cdots,{\mathbf{p}}_{N_{\rm RF}}\},

(
58
a)

\displaystyle\mathsf{Tr}\{\bar{{\mathbf{D}}}\bar{{\mathbf{D}}}^{T}\}={2P}/{K}.

(
58
b)

Clearly, problem (
58
) differs from the FC hybrid precoding problem (
38
) by two aspects: 1)
{\mathbf{A}}
is block diagonal and 2)
{\mathbf{A}}
is independent of the total power constraint.
These differences enable us to alternatively optimize precoders
\bar{{\mathbf{A}}}
and
\bar{{\mathbf{D}}}
to solve (
58
), without the need of performing problem transformation as in Section
V-A2
.

Convergence and Complexity Analysis

V-C
1
Convergence analysis

For both Algorithms 1 and 2, the updated solutions in each iteration can monotonically decrease the objective functions
\|\widetilde{{\mathbf{F}}}\widetilde{{\mathbf{D}}}^{T}-\bar{{\mathbf{A}}}\|_{F}^{2}
and
\|\bar{{\mathbf{F}}}-\bar{{\mathbf{A}}}\bar{{\mathbf{D}}}\|_{F}^{2}
. Moreover, these two functions have a lower bound of zero. By invoking the monotone convergence theorem, the convergence of Algorithms 1 and 2 are guaranteed.

V-C
2
Computational complexity analysis

To evaluate the computational complexities of Algorithms
1
and
2
, we access the number of scalar multiplications required by each algorithm.
Consider one iteration of Algorithm
1
. Updating
{\mathbf{A}}_{I}
and
{\mathbf{A}}_{Q}

necessitates the calculation of matrices
{\mathbf{Z}}_{I}
and
{\mathbf{Z}}_{Q}
, which has a complexity of

.
Next, the optimization of
\widetilde{{\mathbf{D}}}_{u}
and
\widetilde{{\mathbf{F}}}_{c}
involves the calculation of matrix

and its SVD, resulting in a complexity on the order of

.
In summary, the overall complexity of Algorithm
1
is
\mathcal{O}(I_{0}(N_{t}N_{\rm RF}^{2}+N_{\rm RF}^{3}))
, where

is the number of iterations required for convergence.
In addition, consider one iteration of Algorithm
2
.
The complexity for updating
{\mathbf{A}}_{I}
and
{\mathbf{A}}_{Q}

Subsequently, the complexity of updating
\bar{{\mathbf{D}}}
by (
54
) is on the order of
\mathcal{O}(N_{t}N_{\rm RF}N_{s}+N_{\rm RF}N_{s}^{2})

To summarize, the computational complexity of Algorithm

is
\mathcal{O}(I_{0}(N_{t}N_{\rm RF}N_{s}+N_{\rm RF}N_{s}^{2}))

VII
Conclusions

In this paper, we explored precoding design for achieving the capacity of Rydberg atomic receivers in MIMO systems.
We proposed a strong-reference approximation to linearize the magnitude detection model into a real-part detection model. An IQ-aware digital precoding is accordingly designed to show that the channel capacity of atomic MIMO receiver scales as
\min(N_{r}/2,N_{t})\log(\rm SNR)+\mathcal{O}(1)
, revealing a DoF-lossless transmission when
N_{r}\geq 2N_{t}
.
To facilitate the implementation of analog RF circuits, we further delved into the IQ-aware hybrid precoding algorithms for both FC and SC architectures. Simulation results confirmed that the proposed IQ-aware hybrid precoding can tightly approach the optimal IQ-aware digital precoding.

This work presents a significant stride towards the transmitter-side
signal processing for enhancing atomic MIMO receivers. We conclude by outlining potential avenues for future research. Firstly, this study was limited to point-to-point MIMO systems. In the context of multi-user communications where each user utilizes an atomic receiver, it becomes crucial include inter-user interference into the IQ-aware precoding design. Secondly, our analyses and designs were built on the strong-reference approximation. In case the LO is absent or the strong-reference approximation is invalid, maximizing the mutual information of nonlinear transition model becomes necessary, which remains an open problem. Last but not the least, precoding design for the general heterodyne measurement, where the frequency of LO slightly differs from that of signal
[
19
]
, is an unexplored area as well.
