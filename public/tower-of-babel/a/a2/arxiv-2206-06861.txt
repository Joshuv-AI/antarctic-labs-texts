The Stieltjes–Fekete problem and degenerate orthogonal polynomials


Abstract
A result of Stieltjes famously relates the zeroes of the classical orthogonal polynomials with the configurations of points on the line that minimize a suitable energy with logarithmic interactions under an external field. The optimal configuration satisfies an algebraic set of equations: we call this set of algebraic equations the Stieltjes–Fekete problem. In this work we consider the Stieltjes-Fekete problem when the derivative of the external field is an arbitrary rational complex function. We show that, under assumption of genericity, its solutions are in one-to-one correspondence with the zeroes of certain non-hermitian orthogonal polynomials that satisfy an excess of orthogonality conditions and are thus termed “degenerate”. When the differential of the external field on the Riemann sphere is of degree 3 3 our result reproduces Stieltjes’ original result and provides its direct generalization for higher degree after more than a century since the original result.

Introduction and results

The weighted Fekete points are the solution of the problem defined as follows: given a smooth (real–valued)
external potential

\mathcal{Q}(z,\overline{z})
,
z\in{\mathbb{C}}
, satisfying suitable additional assumptions that depend on the context, find the configuration of
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
points
(z_{1},\dots,z_{n})\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
that provides
the maximum of the weighted Fekete functional

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}(z_{1},\dots,z_{n})=\prod_{j=1}^{n}\prod_{k=1\atop k\neq j}^{n}{\Big|z_{j}-z_{k}\Big|}{\rm e}^{-\frac{\mathcal{Q(}z_{j})+\mathcal{Q}(z_{k})}{2(n-1)}}.

(1.1)

Equivalently, the weighted Fekete points provide the minimum of the energy functional

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(z_{1},\dots,z_{n})=-2\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq n}^{n}\log|z_{j}-z_{k}|+\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mathcal{Q}(z_{j})\,.

(1.2)

The above energy has a clear electrostatic interpretation: the points
(z_{1},\dots,z_{n})
can be considered as charged particles in the external field
\mathcal{Q}

that interact with each other via a logarithmic potential, (namely the force is inversely proportional to the relative distance).
The problem of finding the critical configurations of (
1.2
) is referred to as Stieltjes-Fekete problem.
An introduction to the weighted Fekete problem and its connections with logarithmic potential in an external field can be found in
[
45
]
.

Depending on the setup, one may require that the points belong to some assigned domain
\mathcal{D}
.
A case of special interest is when the potential
\mathcal{Q}
is a harmonic function of the form
\mathcal{Q}(z)=2\operatorname{Re}(\widehat{\theta}(z))

with
\widehat{\theta}
analytic,
except for a finite number of singularities and branch cuts and with single valued derivative
\widehat{\theta}^{\prime}(z)
.

If a configuration
\mathscr{Z}_{n}=\{z_{1},\dots,z_{n}\}
forms a
critical configuration
, it is then such that the gradient of the energy
\mathcal{E}
vanishes, which promptly leads to the set of equations

\displaystyle 2\sum_{k\neq j}\frac{1}{z_{j}-z_{k}}=\partial\mathcal{Q}(z_{j})=\widehat{\theta}^{\prime}(z_{j}),\ \ \ j=1,\dots,n,

(1.3)

where
\partial=\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial x}-i\frac{\partial}{\partial y}\right)
,
z=x+iy
.
The following notable choices of
\widehat{\theta}(z)
are associated to classical orthogonal polynomials:

•

\widehat{\theta}(z)=z^{2}
and
\mathcal{D}={\mathbb{R}}
;

•

\widehat{\theta}(z)=z-(\alpha+1)\log z
,
\alpha>-1
, and

\mathcal{D}=(0,\infty)
;

•

\widehat{\theta}(z)=-(\alpha+1)\log(1-z)-(\beta+1)\log(1+z)
,
\alpha,\beta>-1
and the domain
\mathcal{D}=(-1,1)
.

Indeed, for the above three choices of
\widehat{\theta}(z)
and
\mathcal{D}
, the Fekete points are the zeros of the Hermite, Laguerre and Jacobi polynomials, respectively, and they provide a global minimum to the weighted Fekete functional.
The electrostatic interpretation of the zeros of the classical orthogonal polynomials is due to Stieltjes (although studied also by Bochner, Heine, Van Vleck, and Polya) and it is
one of the most elegant results in the theory of special functions (for a review see
[
31
]
).
In the present paper we choose
\widehat{\theta}^{\prime}(z)
to be a rational function, so that the condition of criticality (
1.3
) takes the form

\displaystyle\begin{split}&{\widehat{\theta}^{\prime}(z)=\frac{A(z)}{2B(z)}}\\
&\sum_{k\neq j}\frac{1}{z_{j}-z_{k}}=\frac{A(z_{j})}{2B(z_{j})},\ \ \ j=1,\dots,n,\end{split}

(1.4)

where
A,B
are two relatively prime
4
4

4

The equations clearly depend only on the ratio
A/B
; we will comment on the assumption of being relatively prime in App.
B
.
polynomials (with
B
monic).
Equations of similar nature as (
1.4
) are sometimes referred to as
Stieltjes–Bethe
equations because of their appearance in the Bethe-Ansatz for spin-chains
[
15

17
]
and can be considered on Riemann surfaces in higher genus
[

]

The question we pursue here is whether the criticality condition (
1.4

In the excellent review
[
31
]
on this circle of ideas the following questions were raised, to quote verbatim from loc. cit.:

\cdot
” […] Are there generalizations of electrostatic models to other families of polynomials?

\cdot
Why necessarily the global minimum of the energy
\mathscr{E}
should be considered? Which other types of equilibria described
above could be linked to the zeros of the polynomials?”

\cdot
What is the appropriate model for the complex zeros (when they exist)? […]”

Regarding the first point, in
[
39
]
it was shown that critical configurations are related to Heine-Stieltjes polynomials, namely, to polynomial solutions of second order ODEs with polynomial coefficients.

Our present paper extends the previous literature and addresses and answers precisely the remaining of the above three questions; the result can be described concisely by the following statement:

There is a generically one-to-one correspondence between the solutions of the Stieltjes–Bethe equations (
1.4
) and
the
maximally degenerate
orthogonal polynomials of degree
n
for a
semiclassical moment functional
of type
(A,B)

The meaning of the qualifier “generic” will be spelt out in Theorem

In the above statement
a degenerate orthogonal polynomial is an orthogonal polynomial satisfying an excess of orthogonality conditions (see Def.

A moment functional

We say that such a semiclassical functional

(A,B)

In our application the polynomials

are relatively prime given the nature of the equations (
1.4

]

\displaystyle z^{j}

We now need to introduce the concept of

Definition 1.2
.

A polynomial

We recall

For a given type of moment functional

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
A
and
B
be relatively prime polynomials with
B
having at least one multiple root if
\deg B<\deg A
. Further let
d=\max\{\deg A,\deg B-1\}

\theta^{\prime}(z)=-\frac{A(z)+B^{\prime}(z)}{B(z)}\,.

(1.11)

Suppose that for given integer
n
there exist constant parameters
s_{1},\dots,s_{d}
such that the semiclassical moment functional
\mathcal{M}
of type
(A,B)
admits a maximally degenerate polynomial
P_{n}
of degree
n
according to definition
1.2
.
Then

(1)

the function

solves the differential equation

\displaystyle F^{\prime\prime}(z)-V(z)F(z)=0\,,

(1.12)

where the potential
V(z)
is a rational function with poles only at the zeros of
B(z)
at most of twice the order and takes the form:

\displaystyle V(z)=\frac{1}{2}\left(\theta^{\prime}+\frac{B^{\prime}}{B}\right)^{\prime}+\frac{1}{4}\left(\theta^{\prime}+\frac{B^{\prime}}{B}\right)^{2}+\frac{Q}{B},\ \ \ \deg Q\leq d-1,

(1.13)

with
Q
a polynomials of
\deg Q\leq d-1
.
Equivalently the polynomial
P_{n}
satisfies the generalized Heine-Stieltjes equation

\displaystyle B(z)P_{n}^{\prime\prime}-A(z)P_{n}^{\prime}-Q(z)P_{n}(z)=0.

(1.14)

(2)

Let
(z_{1},\dots,z_{m})
denote the roots of
P_{n}
that are distinct from those of
B
; they satisfy the system of equations

\displaystyle\sum_{\ell\neq j\atop 1\leq\ell\leq m}\frac{1}{z_{j}-z_{\ell}}=\frac{A(z_{j})}{2B(z_{j})}-\sum_{{c:\atop B(c)=0=P_{n}(c)}}\frac{\sharp_{c}}{z_{j}-c},\ \ \ j=1,\dots,m\,,

(1.15)

and the positive integer

denotes the multiplicity of the root

of
B(z)
.

Under the genericity assumption that all roots

, the polynomial

does not share any root with

satisfy the system of equations

\displaystyle\sum_{\ell\neq j\atop 1\leq\ell\leq n}\frac{1}{z_{j}-z_{\ell}}=\frac{A(z_{j})}{2B(z_{j})},\ \ \ j=1,\dots,n.

(1.16)

Viceversa let
\mathscr{Z}=\{z_{1},\dots,z_{n}\}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
be a critical configuration satisfying the equilibrium equations (
1.16
),
then the polynomial
P_{n}(z)=\prod_{j=1}^{n}(z-z_{j})
is a (non-hermitian) maximally degenerate orthogonal polynomial for a semiclassical moment functional
\mathcal{M}{=\mathcal{M}_{n}}
of type
(A,B)
and item

2n>\operatorname{Re}\Lambda-1-\deg B,\ \ \ \ \Lambda:=-\mathop{\mathrm{res}}\limits_{z=\infty}\theta^{\prime}(z)\mathrm{d}z.

Remark 1.6

We remark that for real continuous functions

The Fekete energy (

In the recent paper

subject to constraint

This problem is very close to ours; by the method of Lagrange multiplier, their equation

\displaystyle\sum_{k\neq i}\frac{1}{z_{i}-z_{k}}=-\sum_{\ell=0}^{p}\frac{\nu_{\ell}}{z_{i}-b_{\ell}}+\rho^{*}r^{\prime}(z_{i}),

(i)

(ii)
we can have poles of arbitrary order (i.e. the multiplicities of the zeros of

are arbitrary);

(iii)
we connect the solution of the Stieltjes-Bethe equation with (degenerate) orthogonal polynomials, that is the main point of our work and that generalizes Stieltjes seminal result.

Another complementarily related result is the work
[

]
where, for an

The electrostatic partner

Finally another result that connects orthogonal polynomials to potential theory but goes in the opposite direction relative to our result is due to M. Ismail

Semiclassical moment functionals

We now provide a quick summary of the definition and some properties of semiclassical moment functionals. The notion originates in the work of Shohat
[
48
]
and then developed by Maroni
[
35
]
and Marcellán-Rocha
[
33
]
, and has also been extended to the bi-orthogonal case in
[
3
]
.

Recall now that for two polynomials
A(z),B(z)
of degree
a,b
, respectively,
the
semiclassical moment functional
of type
(A,B)
are those satisfying Definition
1.1
.

The main result of
[
20
,
33
,
35
]
(see also the introduction of
[
3
]
) is that any such moment functional can be represented in a similar form as (
1.17
):

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}[p]=\sum_{\ell=1}^{d}s_{\ell}\int_{\gamma_{\ell}}p(z){\rm e}^{\theta(z)}\mathrm{d}z\ ,\qquad\theta^{\prime}(z)=-\frac{A(z)+B^{\prime}(z)}{B(z)}

(2.1)

and
\gamma_{\ell}
are suitable contours that extend from a zero of
B
to another (or to infinity) and described in the next section.
If follows from a judicious use of Cauchy integral theorem and counting that there are
d=\max\{b-1,a\}
such linearly independent contours; this integer
d
is also the total degree of the poles of the meromorphic differential
\theta^{\prime}(z)\mathrm{d}z
on the Riemann sphere, minus
2
.

For brevity we will denote by
\Gamma=\sum_{\ell=1}^{d}s_{\ell}\gamma_{\ell}
the element of a suitable homology space and simply denote by
\int_{\Gamma}
the operation
\sum s_{\ell}\int_{\gamma_{\ell}}
.

Remark 2.1
.

We warn the reader that the symbol
\theta
as defined in (
2.1
) may be regular at some (or all) of the zeros of
B
if they simplify in the ratio that appears in (
2.1
); in particular this means that there may be different moment functional with the same symbols but a different number
d
of contours of integration. This happens when there are “hard-edge” contours of integration. An example is the case of the Jacobi polynomials with
\alpha=\beta=0
; in this case the symbol is
\theta=0
and the moment functional
\mathcal{M}[p]=\int_{-1}^{1}p(x)\mathrm{d}x
satisfies

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}\Big[(x^{2}-1)p^{\prime}(x)\Big]=\mathcal{M}\Big[-2xp(x)\Big],\ \ \ \forall p\in{\mathbb{C}}[x],

(2.2)

so that
B=x^{2}-1
and
A=-2x
. This follows from a simple integration by parts, where the contribution of the boundary evaluation vanishes thanks to the fact that
B(\pm 1)=0
.

The dual contours are exactly as before, extending to

.

Remark 2.5
.

We will not treat the case where all the zeros of
B
are simple (the classical Heine-Stieltjes electrostatic problem); in this case
the contours should be chosen, generically (i.e. for non-integer residues of

) as Pochhammer contours (group-commutators of the generators in the fundamental group of the plane minus the zeros of
B
) but there are case distinctions according to whether the residues of
\theta^{\prime}
at these poles are positive integers, negative integers or neither which complicate the description.

Remark 2.6
.

The case when
\deg A<\deg B
allows in general the possibility that
A
is a multiple of
B^{\prime}
. If
A
is an
integer
multiple of
B^{\prime}
,
A=kB^{\prime}
with
k=1,2,3\dots
then the weight of the semiclassical moment functional is

\displaystyle A=kB^{\prime}\ \ \Rightarrow\ \ {\rm e}^{\theta(z)}=\frac{1}{B(z)^{k+1}}.

(2.18)

In this case the moment functionals
\mathcal{M}
are of finite rank (they are linear combinations of derivatives of Dirac delta functions supported at the zeroes of
B
, with the order of derivative being equal to
k
times the multiplicity of said zero).

It then follows that the orthogonal polynomials of degree
n\geq(k+1)\deg B
are all the polynomials divisible by
B^{k+1}
, a type of solution that we could call “improper”. For
n=k\deg B+1
we find interesting, albeit simple,

of proper solution of the Stieltjes–Bethe equations, see Appendix
A
.

Connection with Painlevé equations.

These types of moment functionals were analyzed in
[
5
]
where it was shown that the Hankel determinant of the moments, when considered as function of the coefficients of
A,B
, are “isomonodromic tau functions” in the sense of
[
22
]
. In particular this means that specializing the symbol
\theta
one can connect the theory of orthogonal polynomials to certain solutions of the Painlevé equations II,
\dots
, VI, as well as many integrable generalizations thereof.

In this perspective the maximal degeneration of a polynomial implies that the tau-function must vanish and hence, in the cases that overlap with the theory of Painlevé transcendents, we are considering poles of the corresponding transcendent.

This connection was exploited in our companion paper
[
4
]
in connection with a conjecture of Shapiro and Tater
[
46
]
.

Degenerate orthogonal polynomials and Fekete problem

A polynomial
P_{n}(z)
of degree
\leq n

orthogonal

for a moment functional

if
[
8
]

\displaystyle\langle P_{n},z^{k}\rangle:=\mathcal{M}\Big[P_{n}(z)z^{k}\Big]=0,\ \ \ k=0,\dots,n-1.

Consider now semiclassical moment functional
\mathcal{M}
of type
(A,B)
and let
d=\max\{a,b-1\}
: then the orthogonality reads

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}[P_{n}(z)z^{k}]=\sum_{j=1}^{d}s_{j}\int_{\gamma_{j}}P_{n}(z)z^{k}{\rm e}^{\theta}\mathrm{d}z=\int_{\Gamma}P_{n}(z)z^{k}{\rm e}^{\theta}\mathrm{d}z=0,\ \ \ k=0,\dots,n-1,

(3.1)

where
\Gamma=\sum_{j=1}^{d}s_{j}\gamma_{j}
and
\theta

A,B
by (
2.1
) (up to an inessential additive constant). This is sometimes called “non-Hermitian” orthogonality because the inner product is complex and bilinear rather than sesquilinear.
Let
\mu_{k}({\bf s})=\int_{\Gamma}z^{k}{\rm e}^{\theta}\mathrm{d}z
be the moments and define

\displaystyle D_{n}({\bf s}):=\det\left[\mu_{j+k}({\bf s})\right]_{j,k=0}^{n-1}\,.

(3.2)

The polynomials of degree
\leq n
that satisfy the orthogonality (

) are expressible as the determinant

\displaystyle P_{n}(z)=\det\left[\begin{array}[]{cccc}\mu_{0}&\mu_{1}&\dots&\mu_{n}\\
\mu_{1}&\mu_{2}&\dots&\mu_{n+1}\\
\vdots\\
\mu_{n-1}&\dots&&\mu_{2n-1}\\
1&z&\dots&z^{n}\end{array}\right]\,.

We now recall the Definition
1.2
.
The polynomial

is called
\ell
–degenerate orthogonal
if, in addition to the orthogonality conditions (

), it satisfies

\displaystyle\left<P_{n},z^{k}\right>=0,\ \ \ k=0,1,\dots,n+\ell-1.

(3.8)

As we mentioned in the introduction, the notion of

–degeneracy translates into the vanishing of suitable determinants, as shown in the next Lemma, whose proof is immediate.

Lemma 3.1
.

The orthogonal polynomial

is

) if and only if the following determinants vanish:

\displaystyle D_{n+1,k}({\bf s}):=\det H_{n+1,k}=0,\ \ \ \ k=0,1,\dots,\ell-1,

(3.9)

where

are the matrices

\displaystyle H_{n+1,k}:=\left[\begin{array}[]{cccc}\mu_{0}&\mu_{1}&\dots&\mu_{n}\\
\mu_{1}&\mu_{2}&\dots&\mu_{n+1}\\
\vdots\\
\mu_{n-1}&\dots&&\mu_{2n-1}\\
\hline\cr\mu_{n+k}&\mu_{n+k+1}&\dots&\mu_{2n+k}\end{array}\right].

According to Definition
1.2
, a
0
–degenerate polynomial is just an orthogonal polynomial (no conditions are imposed) and generically it exists.
We will say that

is

The justification of the terminology is that the condition of

–degeneracy imposes

and hence, generically, we can impose at most

It is important to point out the following. Suppose that
D_{n,0}({\bf s})=0=D_{n+1,0}({\bf s})
: one then concludes that on this locus the expression (
3
) for the orthogonal polynomial

yields the identically zero polynomial. This is true for an arbitrary moment functional (namely in the indeterminates

Proposition 3.2
.

Suppose that
D_{n,0}=D_{n+1,0}=0
. Then
P_{n}(z)\equiv 0
; more specifically, considering
P_{n}(z)\in{\mathbb{C}}[z]\otimes{\mathbb{C}}[\mu_{0},\dots,\mu_{2n}]
then
P_{n}(z)^{2}
lies in the ideal generated by
D_{n,0}
and
D_{n+1,0}
. In particular we also have
D_{n+1,k}=0,\ \forall k\in{\mathbb{N}}

Proof.

Consider the expression for
P_{n}(z)
:

\displaystyle P_{n}(z)=\det\left[\begin{array}[]{cccc}\mu_{0}&\mu_{1}&\dots\mu_{n-1}&\mu_{n}\\
\mu_{1}&\mu_{2}&&\vdots\\
\vdots&&\\
\mu_{n-1}&\mu_{n}&\dots\mu_{2n-2}&\mu_{2n-1}\\
1&z&\dots&z^{n}\end{array}\right]=C_{n}z^{n}-C_{n-1}z^{n-1}+\dots+(-1)^{n}C_{0}

We are going to show that
C_{j}C_{r}k\in\left\langle D_{n,0},D_{n+1,0}\right\rangle
(the ideal generated by the two polynomials) for all

; this immediately implies the two subsequent statements.

Recall the (general) Desnanot-Jacobi identity; if
M
is an
n\times n
matrix and
J,K
are two subsets of

of the same cardinality

(and listed in increasing order) then we denote by

the

and the columns indexed by

Then the identity reads

\displaystyle\det M\det M^{\{a,b\};\{c,d\}}=\det M^{\{a\};\{c\}}\det M^{\{b\};\{d\}}-\det M^{\{b\};\{c\}}\det M^{\{a\};\{d\}},

(3.20)

for

with

and

Let

be the Hankel matrix of the moments of size

\displaystyle\det H\det H^{\{j,n+1\};\{k,n+1\}}=\det H^{\{j\};\{k\}}\det H^{\{n+1\};\{n+1\}}-\det H^{\{j\};\{n+1\}}\det H^{\{n+1\};\{k\}}.

(3.21)

The last term in the right side of (

) is precisely

up to an overall sign,
so that we have

\displaystyle C_{j}C_{k}=\left(\det H^{\{j\};\{k\}}\right)D_{n,0}-\left(\det H^{\{j,n+1\};\{k,n+1\}}\right)D_{n+1,0}

(3.22)

This proves that all the quadratic expressions belong to the claimed ideal.
\blacksquare

3.39
) with
F(z)=\sqrt{B(z)}P_{n}(z){\rm e}^{\frac{1}{2}\theta(z)}
, yields an analytic expression at all zeros of
P_{n}
.
Now, with
V
given by (
3.39
) we are seeking the linearly independent solution of the differential equation:

\displaystyle y(z)^{\prime\prime}-V(z)y(z)=0,\ \ \ \ \ \ V(z)=\frac{F(z)^{\prime\prime}}{F(z)}.

(3.46)

Using Abel’s theorem (stating that the Wronskian of two solutions of (
3.46
) is a constant) we can write a second linearly independent
solution by a quadrature

\displaystyle G_{q}(z)=F(z)\int_{q}^{z}\frac{\mathrm{d}w}{F(w)^{2}}.

(3.47)

The basepoint
q
of integration in (
3.47
) can be chosen arbitrarily and different choices of basepoint amount to adding to
G_{q}
a multiple of
F
. In the expressions the branch-cuts
\mathscr{B}
of the function
F
are as specified in Definition
2.3
and Fig.
3
.

The differential
\frac{\mathrm{d}w}{F(w)^{2}}
has double poles at the zeros of
P_{n}
but no residues; this is precisely guaranteed by the Fekete equilibrium equations (
3.36
). Therefore the antiderivative has simple poles without logarithmic singularity.
Upon multiplication by
F(z)
(which has simple zeros) the poles will cancel and this provides the proof that
G(z)
is locally analytic at all zeros of
P_{n}
.

Case
\deg A\geq\deg B
.
Consider the connected components of
{\mathbb{C}}\setminus\Gamma=\sqcup_{\mu}\mathscr{D}_{\mu}
: in the region
\mathscr{D}_{0}
that contains
\infty^{(2d_{\infty}-1)}
we use the latter as basepoint of integration. According to our choice of contours
\gamma_{j}
(Sec.
2.1
) in every other connected component
\mathscr{D}_{\mu}
,
such that the boundary is
\gamma_{j}=\partial\mathscr{D}_{\mu}
, there is exactly one endpoint, denoted by
c^{(\mu)}
of a dual contour
\widehat{\gamma}_{j}

(this implies possibly also a particular direction of approach)
; in those regions we therefore define
G_{c^{(\mu)}}(z)
by using the
basepoint
c^{(\mu)}
for
integration in the formula (
3.47
).
Then patching together these functions
G_{c^{(\mu)}}
in each component
\mathscr{D}_{\mu}
we
define
a piecewise analytic function
G(z)
by

\displaystyle G(z)=F(z)\int_{c^{(\mu)}}^{z}\frac{\mathrm{d}w}{F^{2}(w)}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}:=G_{c^{(\mu)}}(z)},\ \ \ z\in\mathscr{D}_{\mu}.

(3.48)

Observe that the regions inside a lasso (e.g. the one containing
p_{2}
in Fig.
3
) are not simply connected: if
c
denotes the end-pole (thus with
\operatorname{Re}(r_{c})>-\frac{1}{2}
) inside a lasso, then
G_{c}(z)
has a branch-cut (oriented towards infinity, see Fig.
3
) across which
G_{c}(z_{+})={\rm e}^{-i\pi r_{c}}G_{c}(z_{-})
and a local behaviour
G(z)=(z-c)^{-\frac{r_{c}}{2}+1}\mathcal{O}(1)
.
If
\gamma_{j}
is the boundary of
\mathscr{D}_{\mu}
, elementary calculus shows that for
z\in\gamma_{j}
we have

G(z_{+})-G(z_{-})=F(z)\int_{c^{(\mu)}}^{\infty^{(2d_{\infty}-1)}}\hskip-20.0pt\frac{\mathrm{d}w}{F^{2}(w)}=F(z)\int_{\widehat{\gamma}_{j}}\frac{\mathrm{d}w}{F^{2}(w)}=s_{j}F(z).

(3.49)

Now define

\displaystyle R_{n}(z):=\frac{G(z){\rm e}^{\frac{1}{2}\theta(z)}}{\sqrt{B(z)}}=P_{n}(z){\rm e}^{\theta(z)}\int_{c^{(\mu)}}^{z}\frac{{\rm e}^{-\theta(w)}\mathrm{d}w}{B(w)P_{n}^{2}(w)},\ \ \ z\in\mathscr{D}_{\mu},

(3.50)

and observe that in each region
\mathscr{D}_{\mu}
the function
R_{n}(z)
has no branch-cuts along
\mathscr{B}
because the branch-cuts of
{\rm e}^{\theta}
eliminate the branch-cuts of the integral within
\mathscr{D}_{\mu}
. As a consequence of (
3.49
)
we have

\displaystyle R_{n}(z_{+})=R_{n}(z_{-})+s_{j}P_{n}(z){\rm e}^{\theta(z)},\ \ \ z\in\gamma_{j}.

(3.51)

We can express
R_{n}
alternatively as a Cauchy transform; indeed, the equation (
3.51
) implies that

\displaystyle R_{n}(z)=H(z)+\sum_{j=1}^{d}s_{j}\int_{\gamma_{j}}\frac{P_{n}(w){\rm e}^{\theta(w)}\mathrm{d}w}{2i\pi(w-z)},

(3.52)

for some
entire
function
H(z)
, which we now show to be identically zero. To see this consider the component
\mathscr{D}_{k}
that contains the steepest ascent direction
\infty^{(2k+1)}
then the integral representation (
3.50
) shows that
R_{n}(z)
is bounded by
\mathcal{O}(z^{-n-b+1})
within the sector extending from the direction
\infty^{(2k)}
to
\infty^{(2k+2)}
and also slightly further (by adjusting the directions of approach to
\infty
of the boundary of
\mathscr{D}_{k}
). Since this holds for all the other regions that contain the steepest ascent directions, we see that
R_{n}(z)
decays like
z^{-n-b+1}
in overlapping open sectors whose union contains every direction. This implies that
H
must be identically zero by Liouville theorem, since the Cauchy integral is already bounded by
\mathcal{O}(z^{-1})
a priori.

Now consider the Wronskian
W(G,F)=1
; using the decay of
R_{n}(z)=\mathcal{O}(z^{-1-\ell}),\ \ \ell\geq 0
we deduce from (
3.25
) that

\displaystyle W(G,F)=-(A+B^{\prime})P_{n}R_{n}+B(P_{n}^{\prime}R_{n}-P_{n}R_{n}^{\prime})=\mathcal{O}(z^{d+n-1-\ell})\quad\mbox{as $|z|\to\infty$}.

(3.53)

Since
W\equiv 1
we conclude that actually
\ell=d+n-1
and
R_{n}(z)=\mathcal{O}(z^{-n-d})
. This implies that
P_{n}
is maximally degenerate from inspection of the Cauchy integral representation (
3.52
) (with
H\equiv 0
).

The case
\deg A<\deg B
.

The only point in the proof where we have used the condition
\deg A\geq\deg B
is in the choice of the base-point of integration
\infty^{(2d_{\infty}-1)}
, as described in Section
2.1
for this case.
We now suppose that
\deg A<\deg B
and that
B
has at least one multiple zero, see Section
2.1.2
.
The modifications to handle this case are of minor nature; since now none of the contours
\gamma_{j}
extends to infinity, we can choose dual contours
\widehat{\gamma}_{k}
that have intersection
\delta_{jk}
with the contours
\gamma_{j}
and extend to infinity (in any direction) as explained in Section
2.1.2
. Note that the only use we make of the dual contours is in the reasoning from formula (
3.49
) to the end of the above proof.

Now, the integrand in the expression (
3.50
) (or equivalent (
3.45
)) is integrable at infinity under the following conditions:

\displaystyle-2n+\operatorname{Re}\Lambda-\deg B<-1\ \ \Rightarrow\ \ \ 2n>\operatorname{Re}\Lambda-1-\deg B,\ \ \ \ \Lambda:=-\mathop{\mathrm{res}}\limits_{z=\infty}\theta^{\prime}(z)\mathrm{d}z.

(3.54)

The condition is not automatically guaranteed for small
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
because
\operatorname{Re}\Lambda
can be arbitrarily large.
If this inequality holds, however, then the integrand is
\mathcal{O}(z^{\Lambda-2n-\deg B})
and hence the integral with basepoint at infinity is
\mathcal{O}(z^{\Lambda-2n-\deg B+1})
: it then follows that the whole expression (
3.50
) is
\mathcal{O}(z^{-n-\deg B+1})=\mathcal{O}(z^{-n-\deg B+1})=\mathcal{O}(z^{-n-d})
. This implies the maximal degeneracy of
P_{n}
from the expression (
3.52
), where
H\equiv 0
is established by the use of Liouville’s theorem.
We then see that the rest of the reasoning as well as the proof of the expressions (
3.45
) proceed unimpeded.

\blacksquare

For the case
\deg A<\deg B
in Theorem
3.7
we have assumed that
B
has a double zero, as well as the condition (
3.44
) on the number of points
n
.
An interesting example with simple zeros of
B
is reported in Appendix
A
; in general it would not be very complicated to lift the condition on the multiple zero of
B
, the only price being additional description of contours (Pochhammer contours in the generic case). However it is less clear how to lift the bound (
3.44
) on the degree
n
(i.e. the minimal number of points for our proofs to proceed unimpeded): we thus do not know whether the theorem fails in these circumstances or only the proof needs modification.

Remark 3.8
.

By the way of illustration let us consider the Bessel case in Table
1
; the only contour of integration is in Fig.
4
and as for dual we can take the positive real axis: the piecewise analytic remainder function
G(z)
defined by (
3.23
), outside of the cardioid coincides with the definite integral
G_{\infty}(z):=F(z)\int_{\infty}^{z}\frac{\mathrm{d}x}{F^{2}(x)}
(formula (
3.47
)): here the direction of integration at
\infty
is immaterial since
F(z)^{2}\simeq z^{2n+\nu+2}
(as long as
2n+\nu>-1
). Inside the cardioid
G(z)
must be recessive near
z=0
along the positive direction and hence it must coincide with the definite integral
G_{0^{(1)}}(z):=F(z)\int_{0^{(1)}}^{z}\frac{\mathrm{d}x}{F^{2}(x)}
, where we recall that the notation
0^{(1)}
denotes the steepest ascent direction of the symbol
\theta
near the point
z=0
. Thus we easily conclude that, for
z\in\gamma
(with
\gamma
the cardioid, oriented as indicated in Fig.
4
),
G(z_{+})=G(z_{-})+sF(z)
with
s=\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\mathrm{d}x}{F(x)^{2}}
, as claimed.

Counting the number of solutions.

A naïve counting would suggest, based on Bézout’s theorem, that there are
(n+1)^{d-1}
solutions of the set of equations that characterize the maximal degeneracy:

\displaystyle D_{n+1,0}({\bf s})=0,\ \dots,\ \ D_{n+1,d-2}({\bf s})=0.

(3.55)

However, the conditions of Bézout’s theorem are not satisfied because Proposition
3.2
implies that the equations (
3.55
) have a common component consisting of the intersection
D_{n+1,0}=0=D_{n,0}
and hence there are infinitely many solutions of (
3.55
) in
[s_{1}:\dots:s_{d}]\in\mathbb{P}^{d-1}
as long as
d\geq 4
.

5
Conclusions and further problems

We conclude with a short discussion on the actual Fekete problem and some interesting connected problems that deserve further investigation.

-

The core idea of this paper stems from
[
4
]
where we studied the numerical similarity between the exactly-integrable spectrum of the quartic anharmonic oscillator and the roots of the rational solutions of the second Painlevé transcendent.

The setup in loc. cit. starts with a special case of the present result and requires the analysis of a particular type of maximally degenerate polynomials for a moment functional of type
(z^{2}-t,1)
; in this case the maximally degenerate polynomial is a
d=1
degenerate one, namely, there is only one condition
D_{n,0}({\bf s})=0
as in (
3.9
).

However the crossing condition of the exactly-integrable spectrum of the corresponding anharmonic oscillator requires
d=2
additional equations, namely

\displaystyle\int_{\gamma_{j}}P_{n}^{2}(z){\rm e}^{\theta(z)}\mathrm{d}z=0,\ \ \ j=1,2.

(5.1)

Note that since
\mathcal{M}[P_{n}^{2}]=0
, there is, in fact, only one extra condition in (
5.1
).
In the context of
[
4
]
this extra condition was shown to be equivalent to the existence of a multiple eigenvalue for a suitable Sturm–Liouville boundary value problem and the extra condition was a constraint now on the values of
t
.

Therefore, we can follow the same line of thought in this more general setting and formulate the following problem:
for a maximally degenerate polynomial
P_{n}
of type
(A,B)
, characterize the possible types
(A,B)
for which the following additional
d-1
conditions hold:

\displaystyle\int_{\gamma_{j}}P_{n}^{2}(z){\rm e}^{\theta(z)}\mathrm{d}z=0,\ \ \ j=1,2,\dots,d-1.

(5.2)

These “exceptional types” would be the direct analogues of the crossing conditions in the exactly integrable spectrum of the anharmonic oscillators that generalize the quartic example.

-

The case
\deg A<\deg B
and
B
having only simple zeros corresponds to the classical Heine-Stiltjes polynomials and the analysis remains open. The contour
\Gamma
is a Pochhammer contours (group-commutators of the generators in the fundamental group of the plane minus the zeros of
B
) but there are case distinctions according to whether the residues of
\theta^{\prime}
at these poles are positive integers, negative integers or neither which complicate the description.

-

The equilibrium equations (
3.36
) imply a hierarchy of similar equilibrium equation following ideas in
[
1
,
7
]
used in the context of the Stieltjes-Fekete problem associated to classical orthogonal polynomials. Briefly, the connection is as follows (in the simplest example of Hermite polynomials): the roots of the Hermite polynomials satisfy (
3.36
) with
A(z)=z
and
B\equiv 1
. The solutions can be thought of as the equilibrium (i.e.
stationary
) equations for the Hamiltonian

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{n}(p_{j}^{2}+x_{j}^{2})-\sum_{j\neq\ell}\ln(x_{j}-x_{\ell}).

(5.3)

It was shown in loc. cit. that the same are
also
equilibrium solutions of the “Calogero” Hamiltonian;

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{n}(p_{j}^{2}+x_{j}^{2})+\sum_{j\neq\ell}\frac{1}{(x_{j}-x_{\ell})^{2}}.

(5.4)

In fact in
[
1
]
they can be proved to be equilibrium equations of a hierarchy of Hamiltonians.

-

The natural question arises regarding the asymptotic behaviour of the solutions to the Stieltjes-Bethe equations for large
n
; the fact that these are the zeros of (degenerate) orthogonal polynomials opens the way to the use of the steepest descent method based on the Riemann–Hilbert analysis. However, the complication here is that the parameters
[s_{1}:\dots:s_{d}]
depend in a rather implicit way on
n
. An analysis in this direction was performed in the classical case of Heine–Stieltjes polynomials in
[
39
]
, based on different approaches relying on potential theory.

-

Finally, the equations (
3.36
) appear in the Bethe Ansatz, as already mentioned.
For example
[
17
]
they appear in the quantum separation of variables and hence our result implies that the solution of the Bethe Ansatz are particular cases of (semiclassical) orthogonal polynomials. This seems to be a significantly new result in a rather old subject and it is object of our future research.

Appendix A
One-parameter family of solutions of Stieltjes-Bethe equations

The following is an interesting example (see also the introduction in
[
47
]
) because it provides an example where all zeros of
B
are simple and also an example where the corresponding Stieltjes-Bethe equations admit a continuum of nontrivial solutions. At the same time this also shows that in the case
\deg A\leq\deg B-1
and with simple zeros of
B
, new phenomena occur (even under genericity assumptions) that are not present in the main theorems.

Let
A=kB^{\prime}
with
k=1,2,\dots
, so that
{\rm e}^{\theta}=\frac{1}{B^{k+1}}
. Note that all zeros of
B
must be simple for otherwise
A
would not be relatively prime to
B
.

The corresponding moment functionals are obtained by taking arbitrary linear combinations of small circles around the zeros of
B
; here we find the first difference with the generic case, in that all of these
d+1
moment functionals are linearly independent, which is a departure from the generic situation. For example if
B=(z-\beta_{1})(z-\beta_{2})

k=1

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{\ell}[z^{j}]=\mathop{\mathrm{res}}\limits_{z=\beta_{\ell}}\frac{z^{j}\mathrm{d}z}{B^{2}(z)}=\frac{j\beta_{\ell}^{j-1}}{(\beta_{1}-\beta_{2})^{2}}-2\frac{\beta_{\ell}^{j}}{(\beta_{\ell}-\beta_{3-\ell})^{3}},\ \ \ \ell=1,2.

(A.1)

One can then verify that the first three moments
j=0,1,2
form linearly independent vectors.

Now, the proofs of Theorems
3.5

3.7

n\geq\lfloor\frac{\deg B}{2}\rfloor+1
.
For
n=k\deg B+1
the Stieltjes–Bethe equations (
3.36
) have the immediate solution

\displaystyle P_{n}^{\prime}(z)=B^{k}(z)\ \ \ P_{n}(z)=\int_{0}^{z}B^{k}(x)\mathrm{d}x+C

(A.2)

Here we see that the roots of
P_{n}
provide a one–parameter family of solutions parametrized by
C\in{\mathbb{C}}
which, generically, consist of pairwise distinct points. The polynomial
P
is also the solution of

\displaystyle BP_{n}^{\prime\prime}-kB^{\prime}P_{n}^{\prime}=0,

(A.3)

namely a generalized Lamé equation with trivial Van Vleck polynomial, according to the terminology in
[
47
]
.
Now the question is whether the polynomial
P_{n}
is a maximally degenerate one; since
n=k\deg B+1
we can retrace the proof of Theorem
3.7
starting from

\displaystyle\int_{\infty}^{z}\frac{B^{k}(w)\mathrm{d}w}{P_{n}^{2}(w)}=-\frac{1}{P_{n}(z)}.

(A.4)

Let
\mathbb{D}_{j}
be a small disk around
\beta_{j}

j=1,\dots,\deg B

The function (see (

\displaystyle R_{n}(z):=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lr}\displaystyle\frac{P_{n}(z)}{B(z)^{k+1}}\int_{\infty}^{z}\frac{B^{k}(w)\mathrm{d}w}{P_{n}^{2}(w)}=-\frac{1}{B^{k+1}(z)}&z\not\in\cup_{j}\mathbb{D}_{j}\\[15.0pt]
\displaystyle\frac{P_{n}(z)}{B(z)^{k+1}}\int_{\beta_{j}}^{z}\frac{B^{k}(w)\mathrm{d}w}{P_{n}^{2}(w)}=\frac{P_{n}(z)}{P_{n}(\beta_{j})B(z)^{k+1}}-\frac{1}{B(z)^{k+1}}&z\in\mathbb{D}_{j}.\end{array}\right.

The function satisfies clearly

\displaystyle R_{n}(z_{+})=R_{n}(z_{-})+s_{j}\frac{P_{n}(z)}{B(z)^{k+1}},\ \ z\in\partial\mathbb{D}_{j}\ \ \ s_{j}:=\frac{1}{P_{n}(\beta_{j})}.

(A.7)

Thus

\displaystyle R_{n}(z)=\sum_{j=1}^{\deg B}\frac{1}{P_{n}(\beta_{j})}\oint_{\partial\mathbb{D}_{j}}\frac{P_{n}(w)\mathrm{d}w}{B^{k+1}(w)(w-z)2i\pi}

(A.8)

and it follows that
P_{n}(z)
is indeed a
family
of maximally degenerate polynomials of degree
n=k\deg B+1
, whose zeros form a one-parameter family of solutions of the Stieltjes-Bethe equations (
3.36
), with
A=kB^{\prime}
.
In this case the moment functional is

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}[q(x)]=\sum_{j=1}^{\deg B}\frac{1}{P_{n}(\beta_{j})}\oint_{\partial\mathbb{D}_{j}}\frac{q(w)\mathrm{d}w}{B^{k+1}(w)2i\pi}.

(A.9)

The case

In this case
\theta^{\prime}=(k-1)\frac{B^{\prime}}{B}

Appendix B
Lifting the primality condition on
A,B

The equilibrium equations (
3.36
) in Theorem
3.5
are insensitive to the multiplication
A(z)\mapsto\widetilde{A}(z)=A(z)E(z)
and
B(z)\mapsto\widetilde{B}(z)=B(z)E(z)
for
E
a polynomial. However this map does modify the definition of the moment functional for which
P_{n}(z)=\prod_{z=1}^{N}(z-z_{j})
is maximally degenerate orthogonal. On the face of it, this seems to be a conflict with our description, since the new moment functional of type
(\widetilde{A},\widetilde{B})
has
\widetilde{d}=\max\{\deg\widetilde{A},\deg\widetilde{B}-1\}=d+\deg E
and it is not clear that it admits the same maximally degenerate polynomial.

We are now going to show that this is generically the case, namely, the same
P_{n}
is maximally degenerate also for the modified moment functional provided that none of the roots of
E
coincides with one of
P_{n}
.

It suffices to consider the case
E(z)=(z-c)^{K}
; hence suppose that
\widetilde{A}(z)=A(z)(z-c)^{K}
and
\widetilde{B}(z)=B(z)(z-c)^{K}
(with
A,B
, relatively prime).

The new moment functional
\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}
is then related to the original
\mathcal{M}
by

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}[p(z)]=\sum_{j=1}^{d}s_{j}\int_{\gamma_{j}}p(z)\frac{{\rm e}^{\theta(z)}\mathrm{d}z}{(z-c)^{K}}+\sum_{\ell=1}^{K}(\ell-1)!\widetilde{s}_{\ell}\oint_{|z-c|=\epsilon}\frac{p(z)\mathrm{d}z}{(z-c)^{\ell}2i\pi}

(B.1)

where
\gamma_{j}
are the same contours defined earlier. Note that the new functional contains a linear combination of derivatives of the distributional delta function supported at
z=c
(as a consequence of Cauchy’s residue theorem).
To see that (
B.1
) holds, observe that the generating function of moments
\widetilde{\Phi}(\lambda):=\widetilde{M}[{\rm e}^{\lambda z}]
satisfies the ODE (see (
2.16
))

\displaystyle\bigg[\lambda B(\partial_{\lambda})-A(\partial_{\lambda})\bigg](\partial_{\lambda}-c)^{K}\widetilde{\Phi}(\lambda)=0

(B.2)

and at this point it is promptly verified that the general solution is

\displaystyle\widetilde{\Phi}(\lambda)=\sum_{j=1}^{d}s_{j}\int_{\gamma_{j}}\frac{{\rm e}^{\lambda z+\theta(z)}\mathrm{d}z}{(z-c)^{K}}+\sum_{\ell=1}^{K}(\ell-1)!\,\widetilde{s}_{\ell}\oint_{|z-c|=\epsilon}\frac{{\rm e}^{\lambda z}\mathrm{d}z}{(z-c)^{\ell}2i\pi}

(B.3)

from which the statement follows.

Suppose now that
P_{n}(z)
is a maximally degenerate polynomial for
\mathcal{M}
, namely

\displaystyle 0=\mathcal{M}[P_{n}z^{\ell}]=\sum_{j=1}^{d}s_{j}\int_{\gamma_{j}}P_{n}(z)z^{m}{{\rm e}^{\theta(z)}\mathrm{d}z},\ \ m=0,1,\dots,n-1,{\bf n,\dots,n+d-2},

(B.4)

for appropriate constants
s_{1},\dots,s_{d}
. We claim that
P_{n}
is also maximally degenerate with respect to
\widetilde{M}
for some additional constants
\widetilde{s}_{1},\dots,\widetilde{s}_{K}
.
Indeed, the new condition of maximal degeneracy now requires

\displaystyle 0=\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}[P_{n}z^{\ell}]=\sum_{j=1}^{d}s_{j}\int_{\gamma_{j}}P_{n}(z)z^{m}\frac{{\rm e}^{\theta(z)}\mathrm{d}z}{(z-c)^{K}}+\sum_{\ell=1}^{K}(\ell-1)!\,\widetilde{s}_{\ell}\oint_{|z-c|=\epsilon}\frac{P_{n}(z)z^{m}\mathrm{d}z}{(z-c)^{\ell}2i\pi}

\displaystyle m=0,\dots,n-1,{\bf n,\dots,n+d-2+K}.

(B.5)

Since the sequence of polynomials
z^{m}
and
(z-c)^{m}
span the same flag of spaces, we can equivalently state the
\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}
-orthogonality of
P_{n}
with respect to the sequence
(z-c)^{m},\ \ m=0,\dots,n-2+d+K
. Of these
n-1+d+K
conditions, the ones for
m=K,K+1,\dots,n+d-2+K
are already satisfied automatically (since they reduce to the orthogonality of
P_{n}
for the
\mathcal{M}
moment functional). The
K
conditions for
m=0,1,\dots,K-1
fix instead the constants
\widetilde{s}_{1},\dots,\widetilde{s}_{K}
: this defines a linear system for
\widetilde{\bf s}=(\widetilde{s}_{1},\dots,\widetilde{s}_{K})
with a matrix of coefficients which is upper triangular and Toeplitz, of the form:

\displaystyle P_{n}(c){\bf 1}_{{}_{K}}+\sum_{\ell=1}^{K-1}P^{(\ell)}_{n}(c)\Lambda_{{}_{K}}^{\ell},

(B.6)

with
{\bf 1}_{{}_{K}}
being the
K\times K
identity matrix and
\Lambda_{{}_{K}}
the upper shift matrix. If we choose
c
generically (i.e. not equal to any of the zeros of
P_{n}
) then the system for the extra parameters
\widetilde{\bf s}
is determined uniquely.
This shows that the new functional
\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}
is also maximally degenerate on polynomials of order
n
and that the maximally degenerate polynomial of degree
n
is the
same
as that for
\mathcal{M}
.

If
c
coincides with a zero of
P_{n}(c)
in general one finds an inconsistent system because the orthogonality to
(z-c)^{K-1}
yields the equation:

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{d}s_{j}\int_{\gamma_{j}}P_{n}(z)\frac{{\rm e}^{\theta(z)}\mathrm{d}z}{(z-c)}=0

(B.7)

which is never satisfied because the expression is proportional by a non-vanishing function to the second linearly independent solution
G(z)
(see (
3.50
)) of the second-order ODE (
3.33
), and two independent solutions cannot vanish at the same point.
