A sufficient and necessary condition for 𝒜 \displaystyle\mathcal{A} -quasiaffinity
Stefan Schiffer

Abstract
. We consider a homogeneous, constant rank differential operator 𝒜 \displaystyle\mathcal{A} and prove a characterisation theorem for 𝒜 \displaystyle\mathcal{A} -quasiaffine functions in the spirit of Ball, Currie and Olver [ BCO81 ] ; i.e. functions such that f ⁡ ( v ) = ∫ T N f ⁡ ( v + ψ ⁡ ( y ) ) ​ 𝑑 y f(v)=\int_{T_{N}}f(v+\psi(y))~\mathrm{d}y for all v \displaystyle v and all 𝒜 \displaystyle\mathcal{A} -free test functions ψ \displaystyle\psi with zero mean. This result is used to get a sufficient, but not necessary condition for the differential operator 𝒜 \displaystyle\mathcal{A} , such that linearity along the characteristic cone of 𝒜 \displaystyle\mathcal{A} implies 𝒜 \displaystyle\mathcal{A} -quasiaffinity. We show that this implication is true if 𝒜 \displaystyle\mathcal{A} admits a first order potential.

1.
Introduction

1.1.
\displaystyle\mathcal{A}
-quasiaffine functions

Let us consider a homogeneous differential operator
\displaystyle\mathcal{A}\colon C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{N},\mathbb{R}^{d})\to C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{N},\mathbb{R}^{l})
of order
\displaystyle k_{\mathcal{A}}
, given by

(1.1)

\mathcal{A}u=\sum_{|\alpha|=k_{\alpha}}A_{\alpha}\partial_{\alpha}u,

where
\displaystyle A_{\alpha}\in\Lin(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{R}^{l})
are constant coefficients. We call
\displaystyle f\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}
-quasiaffine
if for all
\displaystyle\mathcal{A}
-free test functions on the
\displaystyle N
-torus, i.e.
\displaystyle\psi\in C^{\infty}(T_{N},\mathbb{R}^{d})
with
\displaystyle\mathcal{A}\psi=0
and
\displaystyle\int_{T_{N}}\psi=0
, and all
\displaystyle v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

(1.2)

f(v)=\int_{T_{N}}f(v+\psi(y))\,\textup{d}y.

In this work, we prove a sufficient and necessary condition for a function
\displaystyle f
to be
\displaystyle\mathcal{A}
-quasiaffine depending on derivatives of
\displaystyle f
.

Let us shortly recall, that for differential operators as in (
1.1
) and
\displaystyle\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{N}\backslash\{0\}
, the Fourier symbol
\displaystyle\mathcal{A}[\xi]
of
\displaystyle\mathcal{A}
is defined as

\mathcal{A}[\xi]=\sum_{|\alpha|=k_{\alpha}}A_{\alpha}\xi^{\alpha}\in\Lin(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{R}^{l})

We assume that
\displaystyle\mathcal{A}
satisfies the following two conditions:

(a)

Constant rank property:
The rank of the linear operator
\displaystyle\mathcal{A}[\xi]
is constant, i.e. there is
\displaystyle r\in\mathbb{N}
, such that for all
\displaystyle\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{N}\backslash\{0\}

\dim\ker\mathcal{A}[\xi]=r;

(b)

Spanning property:

\mathrm{span}\left\{\bigcup_{\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{N}\backslash\{0\}}\ker\mathcal{A}[\xi]\right\}=\mathbb{R}^{d}.

Recently,
Raiţă
proved another characterisation of constant rank operators
[
Rai19
]
. Namely
\displaystyle\mathcal{A}
has constant rank if and only if it admits a
potential

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}\colon C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{N},\mathbb{R}^{m})\to C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{N},\mathbb{R}^{d})
, i.e. a differential operator
\displaystyle\mathcal{B}
, such that its Fourier symbol
\displaystyle\mathcal{B}[\xi]
satisfies

\Image\mathcal{B}[\xi]=\ker\mathcal{A}[\xi]

for all
\displaystyle\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{N}\backslash\{0\}
. Using this result, we are able to give several equivalent conditions of what it means to be an
\displaystyle\mathcal{A}
-quasiaffine function.

Theorem A
.

Let
\displaystyle f\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
and let
\displaystyle\mathcal{A}
satisfy the constant rank property and the spanning property. Moreover, let
\displaystyle\mathcal{B}
be a potential of
\displaystyle\mathcal{A}
. Then the following statements are equivalent.

(a)

\displaystyle f
is
\displaystyle\mathcal{A}
-quasiaffine;

(b)

\displaystyle f
is a polynomial and
\displaystyle\forall x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
\displaystyle\forall r\geq 2
,
\displaystyle\forall\xi_{1},...,\xi_{r}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
which are linear dependent and
\displaystyle\forall v_{1},...,v_{r}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
\displaystyle v_{i}\in\ker\mathcal{A}[\xi_{i}]
we have

(1.3)

D^{r}f(x)[v_{1},...,v_{r}]=0;

Moreover, we have the following equivalent conditions (cf.
[
GR19
]
)

(c)

\displaystyle f
is
\displaystyle C^{1}
and the Euler-Lagrange equation

(1.4)

\mathcal{B}^{T}(\nabla f(\mathcal{B}u))=0

is satisfied in the sense of distributions
\displaystyle\forall u\in C^{k_{\mathcal{B}}}(\bar{\Omega})
, i.e. for all
\displaystyle\varphi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{m})
we have

\int_{\Omega}\nabla f(\mathcal{B}u)\cdot\mathcal{B}\varphi=0;

(d)

The map
\displaystyle u\to f(u)
is sequentially weak
\displaystyle*
continuous from
\displaystyle L^{\infty}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{d})\cap\ker\mathcal{A}
to
\displaystyle L^{\infty}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{d})
, i.e. if
\displaystyle u_{n}\in L^{\infty}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{d})
with
\displaystyle\mathcal{A}u_{n}=0
and
\displaystyle u_{n}\overset{\ast}{\rightharpoonup}u
in
\displaystyle L^{\infty}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{d})
, then also
\displaystyle f(u_{n})\overset{\ast}{\rightharpoonup}f(u)
in
\displaystyle L^{\infty}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{d})
;

(e)

\displaystyle f
is a polynomial of degree
\displaystyle s\leq d
and the map
\displaystyle u\to f(u)
is sequentially weakly continuous from
\displaystyle L^{s}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{d})
to
\displaystyle\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\Omega)
(the space of distributions on
\displaystyle\Omega
), i.e. if
\displaystyle u_{n}\in L^{s}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{d})
with
\displaystyle\mathcal{A}u_{n}=0
and
\displaystyle u_{n}\rightharpoonup u
in
\displaystyle L^{s}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{d})
, then

\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{\Omega}f(u_{n})\varphi=\int_{\Omega}f(u)\varphi\quad\forall\varphi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\Omega).

We prove that condition
(b)
can in fact be weakened, indeed we may only consider
\displaystyle 2\leq r\leq\min\{N,k_{\mathcal{B}}\}+1
instead of
\displaystyle r\geq 2
(cf. Theorem
3.6
). This can be used to show the following result.

Theorem B
.

Let
\displaystyle\mathcal{A}
be a constant rank operator satisfying the spanning property and let
\displaystyle\mathcal{B}
be a potential of
\displaystyle\mathcal{A}
. Suppose that
\displaystyle\mathcal{B}
is of order one. Then
\displaystyle f\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
is
\displaystyle\mathcal{A}
-quasiaffine if and only if
\displaystyle f
is
\displaystyle\Lambda_{\mathcal{A}}
-affine, i.e. for all
\displaystyle v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\displaystyle w\in\Lambda_{\mathcal{A}}=\cup_{\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{N}\backslash\{0\}}\ker\mathcal{A}[\xi]
we have that

t\longmapsto f(v+tw)\quad\text{is affine.}

Let us give a few historical remarks, before finishing the introduction with a few applications of
\displaystyle\mathcal{A}
-quasiaffinity.
The study of
\displaystyle\mathcal{A}
-quasiaffine functions started with the operator
\displaystyle\mathcal{A}=\curl
, i.e. with the potential operator
\displaystyle\mathcal{B}=\nabla\colon C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{N},\mathbb{R}^{m})\to C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{N},\mathbb{R}^{N\times m})

\displaystyle\curl
-quasiaffine functions are also often called

. In this setting, it is a well-known result, that all
\displaystyle\curl
-quasiaffine functions are linear combinations of minors of
\displaystyle N\times m
-matrices (e.g.
[
Mor66
,
Res67
,
CDKS06
,
Dac08
]
).
Ball
,
Currie
and
Olver
considered the potential operator of higher gradients
\displaystyle\mathcal{B}=\nabla^{k}
and showed an analogue of Theorem
A
in this special setting
[
BCO81
]
. In particular, their work provides us with an example showing that the statement of Theorem
B
fails in the case, where the potential
\displaystyle\mathcal{B}
has degree larger than one. We shall mention again that the equivalences
(c)
-
(e)
in Theorem
A
are treated in the general setting of constant rank operators
\displaystyle\mathcal{A}
by

and

(d)
and

are examined in great detail.

1.3.
\displaystyle\mathcal{A}
-quasiaffine functions and minimisation problems

Let us outline a set of problems, where
\displaystyle\mathcal{A}
-quasiaffine functions play an important role. Consider the functional
\displaystyle I\colon L^{p}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{d})\to[0,\infty)
,

(1.5)

I(u)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\int_{\Omega}f(u(y))\,\textup{d}y&\text{if }\mathcal{A}u=0,\\
\infty&\text{else,}\end{array}\right.

A powerful tool to show that
\displaystyle I
posseses a minimiser is the
Direct Method
. To get this method to work we need to show the following:

(1)

\displaystyle I
is bounded from below and there exists
\displaystyle u
, such that
\displaystyle I(u)<\infty
;

(2)

\displaystyle I
is weakly lower semi-continuous, i.e. if
\displaystyle u_{n}\rightharpoonup u
, then
\displaystyle I(u)\leq\liminf_{n\to\infty}I(u_{n})
;

(3)

\displaystyle I
is
coercive
, i.e. for every
\displaystyle C>0
there is
\displaystyle R>0
, such that
\displaystyle\|u\|_{L^{p}}\geq C.

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}
-quasiaffinity comes into play for
(2)
and
(3)
. On the one hand, we note that if
\displaystyle f
is
\displaystyle\mathcal{A}
-quasiaffine, the functional
\displaystyle I
in (
1.5
) is already weakly continuous. Weak lower-semicontinuity of
\displaystyle I
is equivalent to the notion of
\displaystyle A
-quasiconvexity

[
FM99
]
, where we relax (
1.2
) to

f(v)\leq\int_{T_{N}}f(v+\psi(y))\,\textup{d}y

for all suitable test functions. It is, however, not so easy to show that a given function
\displaystyle f
is
\displaystyle\mathcal{A}
-quasiconvex. Therefore, one often studies functions of the form
\displaystyle g(f(v))
, where
\displaystyle f\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{e}
is a component-wise
\displaystyle\mathcal{A}
-quasiaffine function and
\displaystyle g\colon\mathbb{R}^{e}\to\mathbb{R}
is convex, which is, in the setting
\displaystyle\mathcal{A}=\curl
, referred to as
polyconvexity
(e.g.
[
AD92
,
Bal77
]
). A short calculation shows that any function of the form
\displaystyle g(f(\cdot))
for
\displaystyle\mathcal{A}
-quasiaffine
\displaystyle f
and convex
\displaystyle g
is already
\displaystyle\mathcal{A}
-quasiconvex.

On the other hand,
\displaystyle\mathcal{A}
-quasiaffine functions also can be used to consider non-standard coercivity conditions. Usually, we may just assume that
\displaystyle f(v)\geq C_{1}|v|^{p}-C_{2}
. Given some further restrictions on the problem, we may use a coercivity condition using
\displaystyle\mathcal{A}
-quasiaffine functions. As an example, consider
\displaystyle\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
open and bounded,
\displaystyle J\colon W^{1,N}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{N})
,
\displaystyle u_{0}\in W^{1,N}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{N})
,
\displaystyle f\colon\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}\to[0,\infty)
and

(1.6)

J(u)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\int_{\Omega}f(\nabla u(y))\,\textup{d}y&\text{if }u-u_{0}\in W^{1,N}_{0}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{N})\\
\infty&\text{else,}\end{array}\right.

Then the (non-standard) coercivity condition including the quasiaffine function
\displaystyle\det

f(A)\geq C|A|^{N}-C_{2}-\textup{det}(A)\quad\text{for all }A\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}

guarantees, that minimising sequences of
\displaystyle I
are bounded in
\displaystyle W^{1,N}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Moreover, if
\displaystyle I
is weakly lower-semicontinuous, this growth condition then implies existence of minimisers.

2.1.
Notation

Denote by
\displaystyle e_{1},...,e_{N}
the standard basis of
\displaystyle\mathbb{R}^{N}
.
For
\displaystyle N\in\mathbb{N}
let us define the
\displaystyle N
-torus as
\displaystyle[0,1]^{N}
with the usual identifications of faces. We may identify functions
\displaystyle u\in W^{k,p}(T_{N},\mathbb{R}^{d})
with
\displaystyle\mathbb{Z}^{N}
-periodic functions in
\displaystyle W^{k,p}_{\loc}(\mathbb{R}^{N},\mathbb{R}^{d})
.
For
\displaystyle u\in W^{1,p}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{d})
, we write

\partial_{j}u=\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{j}}u

to denote partial derivatives. With
\displaystyle\nabla u\in L^{p}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{N\times d})
we denote the gradient of
\displaystyle u
, a matrix consisting of the entries
\displaystyle\partial_{j}u_{i}
and, likewise,
\displaystyle\nabla^{r}
is the
\displaystyle r
-th gradient. In contrast to this, for
\displaystyle f\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
, we denote by
\displaystyle D^{r}f
the
\displaystyle r
-th derivative seen as a multilinear map from
\displaystyle(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{r}
to
\displaystyle\mathbb{R}
.

For a multiindex
\displaystyle\alpha=(\alpha_{1},...,\alpha_{N})
and for
\displaystyle\lambda=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\lambda_{i}e_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
write

|\alpha|:=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\alpha_{i},\quad\lambda^{\alpha}=\prod_{i=1}^{N}\lambda_{i}^{\alpha_{i}}.

For
\displaystyle u\in C^{|\alpha|}(\mathbb{R}^{N},\mathbb{R}^{d})
we write

\partial_{\alpha}u=\partial_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}...\partial_{n}^{\alpha_{n}}u.

For a function space
\displaystyle X\subset L^{1}(T_{N},\mathbb{R}^{d})
, we define

X_{\#}=\{u\in X\colon\int_{T_{N}}u=0\}.

2.2.
Constant Rank Operators

Consider a differential operator
\displaystyle\mathcal{A}\colon C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{N},\mathbb{R}^{d})\to C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{N},\mathbb{R}^{l})
with constant coefficients given by

(2.1)

\mathcal{A}u=\sum_{|\alpha|=k_{\mathcal{A}}}A_{\alpha}\partial_{\alpha}u,

where
\displaystyle A_{\alpha}\in\Lin(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{R}^{l})
. We denote by
\displaystyle\mathcal{A}^{\ast}
the adjoint operator of
\displaystyle\mathcal{A}
,

i.e.
\displaystyle\mathcal{A}^{\ast}\colon C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{N},\mathbb{R}^{l})\to C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{N},\mathbb{R}^{d})
and

\mathcal{A}^{\ast}v=(-1)^{k_{\mathcal{A}}}\sum_{|\alpha|=k_{\mathcal{A}}}A_{\alpha}^{T}\partial_{\alpha}u.

We say that
\displaystyle u\in L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{N},\mathbb{R}^{d})\cap\ker\mathcal{A}
, if
\displaystyle u\in L^{p}(\mathbb{R}_{N},\mathbb{R}^{d})
and for all
\displaystyle v\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{N},\mathbb{R}^{d})

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}u\cdot\mathcal{A}^{\ast}v=0.

Likewise, we can also define what it means for
\displaystyle u\in L^{p}_{\loc}(\mathbb{R}^{N},\mathbb{R}^{d})
to be in
\displaystyle\ker\mathcal{A}
and hence also for
\displaystyle u\in L^{p}(T_{N},\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Let us recall some basic notions for the operator
\displaystyle\mathcal{A}
(c.f.
[
Mur78
,
Mur81
,
Tar79
]
).

Definition 2.1
.

Let
\displaystyle\mathcal{A}
be a differential operator as in (
2.1
).

(a)

The Fourier symbol of the differential operator
\displaystyle\mathcal{A}
is the map
\displaystyle\mathbb{R}^{N}\backslash\{0\}\to\Lin(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{R}^{l})
defined by

\mathcal{A}[\xi]=\sum_{|\alpha|=k_{\mathcal{A}}}\xi^{\alpha}A_{\alpha};

(b)

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}
satisfies the
constant rank property
if there exists an
\displaystyle r\in\{0,...,N\}
such that

\dim\ker\mathcal{A}[\xi]=r;

(c)

The
characteristic cone

\displaystyle\Lambda_{\mathcal{A}}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
of
\displaystyle\mathcal{A}
is defined by

\Lambda_{\mathcal{A}}:=\bigcup_{\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{N}\backslash\{0\}}\ker\mathcal{A}[\xi].

(d)

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}
satisfies the
spanning property
, if the characteristic cone
\displaystyle\Lambda_{A}
spans up
\displaystyle\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

In adittion, we also consider a differential operator
\displaystyle\mathcal{B}\colon C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{N},\mathbb{R}^{m})\to C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{N},\mathbb{R}^{d})
of order
\displaystyle k_{\mathcal{B}}
, given by

\mathcal{B}u=\sum_{|\alpha|=k_{\mathcal{B}}}B_{\alpha}\partial_{\alpha}u.

Likewise, we define
\displaystyle\mathcal{B}^{\ast}
and the cone
\displaystyle\Lambda_{\mathcal{B}}
for
\displaystyle\mathcal{B}
.

Definition 2.2
.

We call a differential operator
\displaystyle\mathcal{B}
the potential of
\displaystyle\mathcal{A}
if
\displaystyle\forall\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{N}\backslash\{0\}
we have
\displaystyle\Image\mathcal{B}[\xi]=\ker\mathcal{A}[\xi]
.

Remark 2.3
.

As it was pointed out in
[
GR19
]
, the potential
\displaystyle\mathcal{B}
is not unique, even if we fix the order
\displaystyle k_{\mathcal{B}}
of
\displaystyle\mathcal{B}
and identify operators via homeomorphisms of the underlying space
\displaystyle\mathbb{R}^{m}
. Moreover, note that if
\displaystyle\mathcal{B}\colon C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{N},\mathbb{R}^{m})\to C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{N},\mathbb{R}^{d})
is a potential of
\displaystyle\mathcal{A}
, then also

\mathcal{B}\circ\divergence\colon C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{N},\mathbb{R}^{m}\otimes\mathbb{R}^{N})\to C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{N},\mathbb{R}^{d})

is a potential of
\displaystyle\mathcal{A}
.

Proposition 2.4
.

Let
\displaystyle\mathcal{A}
be a constant rank operator. The following statements are equivalent:

(a)

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}
is the potential of
\displaystyle\mathcal{A}
;

(b)

The following two properties hold

(b1)

\displaystyle\forall u\in L^{2}_{\#}(T_{N},\mathbb{R}^{d})\cap\ker\mathcal{A}
there exists
\displaystyle v\in W^{k_{\mathcal{B}},2}(T_{N},\mathbb{R}^{m})
such that
\displaystyle\mathcal{B}v=u
;

(b2)

\displaystyle\forall v\in C^{\infty}(T_{N},\mathbb{R}^{m})
we have
\displaystyle\mathcal{A}(\mathcal{B}v)=0
;

(c)

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}^{\ast}
is a potential of
\displaystyle\mathcal{B}^{\ast}
.

Proof.

(a)

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow

(c)
is just a purely algebraic calculation, using that
\displaystyle(\ker\mathcal{A}[\xi])^{\perp}=\Image\mathcal{A}^{\ast}[\xi]
. We just need to verify
(a)

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow

(b)
. To show that
(a)
implies
(b)
, note that we may write
\displaystyle u\in L^{2}\cap C^{\infty}(T_{N},\mathbb{R}^{d})
as

u(x)=\sum_{\lambda\in\mathbb{Z}^{N}}\hat{u}(\lambda)e^{-2\pi ix\cdot\lambda}.

Note that
\displaystyle u\in\ker\mathcal{A}
if and only if
\displaystyle\hat{u}(\lambda)\in\ker\mathcal{A}[\lambda]
, i.e.
\displaystyle\hat{u}(\lambda)=\mathcal{B}[\lambda](\hat{v}(\lambda))
for some
\displaystyle\hat{v}(\lambda)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. We may choose
\displaystyle v
in the orthogonal complement of
\displaystyle\ker\mathcal{B}[\lambda]
. Hence, we may bound

|\hat{v}(\lambda)|\leq C|\lambda|^{-k}\hat{u}(\lambda)

and thus
\displaystyle v
, defined by

v(x)=\sum_{\lambda\in\mathbb{Z}^{N}}\hat{v}(\lambda)e^{-2\pi ix\cdot\lambda},

is bounded in
\displaystyle W^{k_{\mathcal{B}},2}(T_{N},\mathbb{R}^{d})
and satisfies
\displaystyle\mathcal{B}v=u
.
\displaystyle\mathcal{A}\circ\mathcal{B}=0
follows by a calculation using the Fourier transform. The converse direction that
(b)
implies
(a)
follows a very similar argument.

∎

Definition 2.7
.

Let
\displaystyle\mathcal{A}
be a differential operator and
\displaystyle f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}

\displaystyle f
is called
\displaystyle\mathcal{A}

\displaystyle\forall x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

\displaystyle\varphi\in C_{\#}^{\infty}(T_{N},\mathbb{R}^{d})

\displaystyle\forall x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

\displaystyle\forall\psi\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{d})

(2.3)

f(x)\leq\frac{1}{|\Omega|}\int_{\Omega}f(x+\mathcal{B}\psi(y))\,\textup{d}y.

Definition 2.8
.

Let
\displaystyle\Lambda\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a cone, i.e.
\displaystyle t\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
,
\displaystyle v\in\Lambda

\displaystyle\Rightarrow~tv\in\Lambda
. We call
\displaystyle f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}

\displaystyle\xi_{1},...\xi_{r}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}\backslash\{0\}
linear dependent and
\displaystyle w_{1},...,w_{r}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
we have

(3.7)

D^{r}M(x)[\mathcal{B}[\xi_{1}](w_{1}),...,\mathcal{B}[\xi_{r}](w_{r})]=0.

Remark 3.7
.

If
\displaystyle k_{\mathcal{B}}=1
, then (
3.7
) only needs to hold for
\displaystyle r=2
. In this case, (
3.7
) is equivalent to
\displaystyle\Lambda_{\mathcal{A}}
-affinity. Hence, as a special case of Theorem
3.6
, we get the statement of Theorem
B
, that for first-order potentials,
\displaystyle\mathcal{A}
-quasiaffinity and hence
\displaystyle\mathcal{B}
-potential quasiaffinity are equivalent to
\displaystyle\Lambda_{\mathcal{A}}
-affinity.

Proof.

We just need to prove that if equation (
3.7
) is true for
\displaystyle 2\leq r\leq\min\{k_{\mathcal{B}},N\}+1
, then it also holds for
\displaystyle r\in\mathbb{N}
. Let us first deal with the case
\displaystyle\min\{k_{\mathcal{B}},N\}=N
. Note that then for
\displaystyle j>2
and
\displaystyle r=N+j
, there are
\displaystyle N+1
vectors
\displaystyle\xi_{i}
, which are already linear dependent; say
\displaystyle\xi_{1},...,\xi_{N+1}
are linear dependent. Then,

D^{N+1}(x)[\mathcal{B}[\xi_{1}](w_{1}),...,\mathcal{B}[\xi_{N+1}](w_{N+1})]=0.

Therefore, also

D^{N+j}(x)[\mathcal{B}[\xi_{1}](w_{1}),...,\mathcal{B}[\xi_{N+j}](w_{N+j})]=0.

Suppose now that
\displaystyle k_{\mathcal{B}}\leq N
. If
\displaystyle k_{\mathcal{B}}=1
, then for all
\displaystyle\xi_{1},\xi_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}\backslash\{0\}
and
\displaystyle w\in\mathbb{R}^{m}

\mathcal{B}[\xi_{1}+\xi_{2}](w)=\mathcal{B}[\xi_{1}](w)+\mathcal{B}[\xi_{2}](w)\in\spann\{\mathcal{B}[\xi_{1}](w),\mathcal{B}[\xi_{2}](w)\}.

We prove an analogue of this statement for
\displaystyle k_{\mathcal{B}}>1
. Again, make the reductions from the proof of Theorem
A
. We just need to show that, for
\displaystyle r>k_{\mathcal{B}}+1
,
\displaystyle\xi_{1},...,\xi_{r-1}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}\backslash\{0\}
linear independent and
\displaystyle w_{1},...,w_{r}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
, we have

(3.8)

D^{r}M(x)\left[\mathcal{B}[\xi_{1}](w_{1}),...,\mathcal{B}[\xi_{r-1}](w_{r-1}),\mathcal{B}[\xi_{1}+...+\xi_{r-1}](w_{r})\right]=0.

Claim:
For
\displaystyle w\in\mathbb{R}^{m}

(3.9)

\mathcal{B}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{r-1}\xi_{i}\right](w)\in\spann_{\lambda\in I}\left\{\mathcal{B}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{r-1}\lambda_{i}\xi_{i}\right](w)\right\},

where
\displaystyle r>k_{\mathcal{B}}+1
and the set
\displaystyle I
of coefficients is given by

I=\big\{\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{r-1}\colon\lambda_{i}=0\text{ for some }i\in\{1,...,r-1\}\big\}

Suppose that (
3.9
) is proven. Then, for a finite index set
\displaystyle J\subset I
, we can write,

\mathcal{B}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{r-1}\xi_{i}\right](w)=\sum_{\lambda\in J}\mathcal{B}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{r-1}\lambda_{i}\xi_{i}\right](w)

and use that, for each
\displaystyle\lambda\in J
, there is
\displaystyle i\in\{1,...,r-1\}
such that
\displaystyle\lambda_{i}=0
. W.l.o.g.
\displaystyle i=1
for some fixed
\displaystyle\lambda\in J
. Then

\displaystyle\displaystyle D^{r}M(x)\left[\mathcal{B}[\xi_{1}](w_{1}),...,\mathcal{B}[\xi_{r-1}](w_{r-1}),\mathcal{B}[\sum_{i=2}^{r-1}\lambda_{i}\xi_{r-1}](w_{r})\right]

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\frac{\partial}{\partial_{\mathcal{B}[\xi_{1}](w_{1})}}D^{r-1}M(x)\left[\mathcal{B}[\xi_{2}](w_{2}),...,\mathcal{B}[\xi_{r-1}](w_{r-1}),\mathcal{B}[\sum_{i=2}^{r-1}\lambda_{i}\xi_{r-1}](w_{r})\right].

Note that we assume in
3.7
that the right-hand side is
\displaystyle 0
, whenever
\displaystyle r-1\leq k_{\mathcal{B}}+1
, i.e.
\displaystyle r\leq k_{\mathcal{B}}+2
. Assuming that (
3.9
) holds, one can prove (
3.8
) for all
\displaystyle r\in\mathbb{N}
by an inductive argument.

It remains to prove the validity (
3.9
). Consider for
\displaystyle t_{1},...,t_{r-1}
the polynomial

P(t_{1},...,t_{r-1})=\mathcal{B}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{r-1}t_{i}\xi_{i}\right](w_{r}).

This polynomial has degree
\displaystyle k_{\mathcal{B}}<r-1
. Hence, in every monomial of
\displaystyle P
of the form
\displaystyle\prod_{i=1}^{r-1}t_{i}^{\alpha_{i}}
there is at least one
\displaystyle j\in\{1,...,r-1\}
, such that
\displaystyle\alpha_{j}=0
. But we can recover the coeffients of these monomials by considering

\mathcal{B}\left[\sum_{i=1,i\neq j}^{r-1}t_{i}\xi_{i}\right](w_{r}).

In particular, we can recover these coefficients by taking linear combinations of
\displaystyle P(\lambda)
for suitable
\displaystyle\lambda\in\{\mu\in\mathbb{R}^{r-1}\colon\mu_{j}=0\}\subset I
. Therefore, (
3.9
) holds. This concludes the proof of Theorem
3.6
.
∎

Theorem
B
is a special case of Theorem
3.6
. In this setting,
\displaystyle k_{\mathcal{B}}=1
, i.e.
\displaystyle\mathcal{A}
-quasiaffinity of
\displaystyle M
is equivalent to the fact that

D^{2}M(x)[\mathcal{B}[\xi](w_{1}),\mathcal{B}[\xi](w_{2})]=0.

As it was already established in Theorem
3.3

(d)
, this is indeed equivalent to
\displaystyle\Lambda_{\mathcal{A}}
-affinity of
\displaystyle M
.

Let us recall the
Ball-Currie-Olver
example showing that
\displaystyle\mathcal{A}
-quasiaffinity does
not
follow if (
3.7
) does not hold for all
\displaystyle 2\leq r\leq\min\{k_{\mathcal{B}},N\}+1
(cf.
[
BCO81
]
). Let us consider the setting
\displaystyle k_{\mathcal{B}}=2
.

Lemma 3.8

(Ball, Currie, Olver)
.

There is a first-order differential operator
\displaystyle\mathcal{A}
and a map
\displaystyle L:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
which is
\displaystyle\Lambda_{\mathcal{A}}
-affine, but not
\displaystyle\mathcal{A}
-quasiaffine.

Proof.

Consider the differential operator
\displaystyle\mathcal{B}=\nabla^{2}
, i.e.

(\nabla^{2}u)_{ijk}=\partial_{i}\partial_{j}u_{k}(i,j=1,...,N;k=1,...,m)

and
\displaystyle\mathcal{A}
the corresponding first order operator, such that
\displaystyle\mathcal{B}
is a potential of
\displaystyle\mathcal{A}
(cf.
[
Mey65
]
).
The characteristic cone of
\displaystyle\mathcal{A}
is the space of tensors of the form

\lambda\otimes\lambda\otimes b\colon\lambda\in\mathbb{S}^{N-1},~b\in\mathbb{R}^{m}.

Now choose
\displaystyle N=2
and
\displaystyle m=3
and consider the map
\displaystyle L
defined via

(3.10)

L(\nabla^{2}u)=\sum\limits_{\sigma\in S_{3}}\sgn(\sigma)\partial_{x}^{2}u_{\sigma(1)}\partial_{x}\partial_{y}u_{\sigma(2)}\partial_{y}^{2}u_{\sigma(3)}.

One can check that this is affine in
\displaystyle\Lambda_{\mathcal{A}}
. On the other hand, one can check that ,for

u(x_{1},x_{2})=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\cos(2\pi x_{1})\\
\cos(2\pi x_{2})\\
\cos(2\pi(x_{1}+x_{2})),\end{array}\right)

we have

\int_{T_{N}}L(u(x_{1},x_{2}))\,\textup{d}x=-\frac{1}{4}.

∎

We have seen in Theorem
3.6
, that the answer to the question, whether

f~\Lambda_{A}\text{-convex}\Longrightarrow f~\mathcal{A}\text{-quasiaffine}

really depends on the order of the operator
\displaystyle k_{\mathcal{B}}
. We note that in view of the following lemmata, the minimal order of
\displaystyle k_{\mathcal{B}}
of the potential
\displaystyle\mathcal{B}
cannot be bounded in terms of the order of
\displaystyle\mathcal{A}
. In view of Theorem
3.6
, the differential condition on
\displaystyle M
for being
\displaystyle\mathcal{A}
-quasiaffine therefore depends only on the order of
\displaystyle\mathcal{B}
and
not
on the order of
\displaystyle\mathcal{A}
.

Lemma 3.9
.

Let
\displaystyle\mathcal{B}\colon C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2},\mathbb{R}^{m})\to C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2},(\mathbb{R}^{2})^{k})
be a differential operator such that

\Image\mathcal{B}[\xi]=\Image\nabla^{k}[\xi]\quad\forall\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{N}\backslash\{0\},

where
\displaystyle\nabla^{k}\colon C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2},\mathbb{R})\to C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2},(\mathbb{R}^{2})^{k})
. Then the operator
\displaystyle\mathcal{B}
is of order
\displaystyle k_{\mathcal{B}}\geq k
.

Proof.

We note that

\dim(\Image\nabla^{k}[\xi])=1.

Consider
\displaystyle\xi_{0}=e_{1}+e_{2}
and the coordinates of

v_{1^{k}}=\partial_{1}^{k}u,\quad v_{2^{k}}=\partial_{2}^{k}u.

There exist
\displaystyle w\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
, such that

\mathcal{B}[\xi_{0}](w)\neq 0,(\mathcal{B}[\xi_{0}](w))_{1^{k}}=1=(\mathcal{B}[\xi_{0}](w))_{2^{k}}=1.

Due to continuity of
\displaystyle\mathcal{B}[\cdot](w)
, there exists an open ball
\displaystyle B_{r}(\xi_{0})
, such that, for all
\displaystyle\xi\in B_{r}(\xi_{0})
,

\mathcal{B}[\xi](w)\neq 0.

In particular, as the dimension of the image of
\displaystyle\nabla^{k}[\xi]
(and therefore also of the image of
\displaystyle\mathcal{B}[\xi]
) is one, we then have, for all
\displaystyle\xi\in B_{r}(\xi_{0})
,

\xi_{2}^{k}(\mathcal{B}[\xi](w))_{1^{k}}=\xi_{1}^{k}(\mathcal{B}[\xi](w))_{2^{k}}

Hence,
\displaystyle(\mathcal{B}[\xi](v))_{1^{k}}
and
\displaystyle(\mathcal{B}[\xi](v))_{2^{k}}
are polynomials of degree larger than
\displaystyle k
in
\displaystyle\xi
. Therefore,
\displaystyle\mathcal{B}
has at least order
\displaystyle k
.
∎

Corollary 3.10
.

Let
\displaystyle N>2
.

(a)

For any
\displaystyle k\in\mathbb{N}
, there exists a first-order operator
\displaystyle\mathcal{A}
, such that any potential
\displaystyle\mathcal{B}
of
\displaystyle\mathcal{A}
has order
\displaystyle k_{\mathcal{B}}\geq k
.

(b)

For any
\displaystyle k\in\mathbb{N}
, there exists a first-order operator
\displaystyle\mathcal{B}
, such that any annihilator
\displaystyle\mathcal{A}
of
\displaystyle\mathcal{B}
(i.e. an operator
\displaystyle\mathcal{A}
, such that
\displaystyle\mathcal{B}
is a potential of
\displaystyle\mathcal{A}
) has order
\displaystyle k_{\mathcal{A}}\geq k
.

Note that
(a)
follows directly from Lemma
3.9
and the result by
Meyers
, that
\displaystyle\nabla^{k}
admits a first-order annihilator
\displaystyle\mathcal{A}^{k}

[
Mey65
]
.
(b)
then follows from Proposition
2.4

(c)
. In particular,
\displaystyle\mathcal{B}=(\mathcal{A}^{k})^{\ast}
is of first order and only admits annihilators of order

.

4.
Examples for
\displaystyle\mathcal{A}
-quasiaffine functions

In this section we discuss some examples and results for
\displaystyle\mathcal{A}
-quasiaffine (or
\displaystyle\mathcal{B}
-potential-quasiaffine functions) for explicit
\displaystyle\mathcal{A}
.

4.1.
\displaystyle\mathcal{B}=\nabla
and
\displaystyle\mathcal{B}=\nabla^{k}

Consider the operator

\mathcal{B}=\nabla\colon C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{N},\mathbb{R}^{m})\longrightarrow C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{N},\mathbb{R}^{N\times n}),

which is given by the coordinates

(\mathcal{B}u)_{ij}=\partial_{i}u_{j}.

We have the following result in this special case (e.g.
[
Mor66
,
Res67
,
CDKS06
,
Dac08
]
).

Proposition 4.1
.

\displaystyle M\colon\mathbb{R}^{N\times m}\to\mathbb{R}
is a
\displaystyle\nabla
-potential-quasiaffine function if and only if it is a linear combination of
\displaystyle r\times r
minors (
\displaystyle 1\leq r\leq\min\{m,N\}
).

As
\displaystyle\curl
is the annihilator of
\displaystyle\nabla
, we therefore have a basis of
\displaystyle\curl
-quasiaffine functions.
For higher-order gradients (
\displaystyle\mathcal{B}=\nabla^{k}
), a characterisation is given in
[
BCO81
]
. Namely, a basis of
\displaystyle\nabla^{k}
-potential-quasiaffine functions are already
\displaystyle\nabla
-potential quasiaffine functions for the gradient acting on
\displaystyle C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{N},\mathbb{R}^{N}\otimes_{sym}...\otimes_{sym}\mathbb{R}^{N})
.

4.2.
The divergence operators on matrices

Consider the divergence operator acting on matrices, i.e.

\divergence\colon C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{N},\mathbb{R}^{N\times d})\longmapsto C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{N},\mathbb{R}^{d})

defined by

(\divergence u)_{i}=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\partial_{j}u_{ij}.

As, after a suitable rotation, the differential operator
\displaystyle\divergence
equals
\displaystyle\curl
for dimension
\displaystyle N=2
, we just consider dimension
\displaystyle N\geq 3
. Note that the characteristic cone
\displaystyle\Lambda_{\divergence}
is the space of matrices with rank
\displaystyle\geq N-1
.

Proposition 4.2
.

Let
\displaystyle N>2
and
\displaystyle\Lambda_{2}\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
be the set of
\displaystyle\mathbb{R}^{N\times d}
matrices with rank less or equal to
\displaystyle 2
. Then
\displaystyle f
is
\displaystyle\Lambda_{2}
-affine if and only if
\displaystyle f
is affine. Moreover, all
\displaystyle\divergence
-quasiaffine functions are already affine.

Proof.

Let
\displaystyle e_{ij}
be the standard basis of
\displaystyle\mathbb{R}^{N\times d}
matrices.
We only prove that
\displaystyle\Lambda_{2}
-affine functions
\displaystyle M
are already affine, the second follows directly from the observation, that these are contained in the characteristic cone of the divergence.
To this end, note that
\displaystyle\Lambda_{2}
-affinity of
\displaystyle M
implies that
\displaystyle M
is a polynomial and is the sum of some monomials
\displaystyle P
. In particular, if a matrix
\displaystyle A
is decomposed as
\displaystyle A=\sum_{i,j}A_{ij}e_{ij}
for
\displaystyle A_{ij}\in\mathbb{R}
,
\displaystyle M(A)
is a polynomial in
\displaystyle A_{ij}
.
Consider some matrix
\displaystyle B\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times d}
and the directions

\lambda e_{kl}+\mu e_{ij},\quad 1\leq i,k\leq N,\quad 1\leq l,j\leq d

for
\displaystyle\lambda,\mu\in\mathbb{R}
. The map

(\lambda,\mu)\mapsto M(B+(\lambda e_{kl}+\mu e_{ij}))

is affine. Hence, all coefficients containing of monomials
\displaystyle P
, such that
\displaystyle A_{ij}^{2}
or
\displaystyle(A_{ij}A_{kl})
are factors of
\displaystyle P
, vanish. Consequently, all coefficients of monomials with degree larger than one vanish. Therefore,
\displaystyle M
is already affine.
∎

4.3.
The div-curl Lemma and similar operators

Consider a constant rank operator
\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{1}\colon C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{N},\mathbb{R}^{d})\to C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{N},\mathbb{R}^{l})
and a potential
\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{1}\colon C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{N},\mathbb{R}^{m})\to C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{N},\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Then we may consider the operator
\displaystyle\mathcal{A}:=(\mathcal{A}_{1},\mathcal{B}_{1}^{\ast})\colon C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{N},\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d})\to C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{N},\mathbb{R}^{l}\times\mathbb{R}^{m})
defined by

(\mathcal{A}_{1},\mathcal{B}_{1}^{\ast})(u,v)=(\mathcal{A}_{1}u,\mathcal{B}_{1}^{\ast}v).

Note that we have

(\ker\mathcal{A}_{1}[\xi])^{\perp}=\ker\mathcal{B}_{1}^{\ast}[\xi]\quad\forall\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{N}\backslash\{0\}.

Therefore, we obtain the following result.

Proposition 4.3
.

The map
\displaystyle f\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
defined by

f(a,b)=a\cdot b

is an
\displaystyle(\mathcal{A}_{1},\mathcal{B}_{1}^{\ast})
-quasiaffine map.

Note that this result has a lot of implications for weak convergence in the context of compensated compactness (e.g.
[
Mur78
,
Mur81
,
Tar79
,
DP85
,
Rin14
,
GR19
]
). In particular, if
\displaystyle u_{n},v_{n}\in L^{2}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{d})
with
\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{1}u_{n}=0
and
\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{1}^{\ast}v_{n}=0
, then due to the Characterisation Theorem
A

(e)
we find that

u_{n}\rightharpoonup u,~v_{n}\rightharpoonup v\quad\Longrightarrow\quad u_{n}\cdot v_{n}\rightharpoonup u\cdot v\text{ in the sense of distributions.}

The two most prominent examples are the following. On the one hand, the operators

\mathcal{A}_{1}=\curl,\quad\mathcal{B}_{1}^{\ast}=\divergence,

both acting on
\displaystyle\mathbb{R}^{N\times d}
-matrices are a well-known example, which is the initial example of compensated compactness. Another example of this type is

\mathcal{A}_{1}=\curl\curl^{T},\quad\mathcal{B}_{1}^{\ast}=\divergence

on symmetric
\displaystyle N\times N
matrices, considered in the context of linear elasticity (e.g.
[
CMO18
]
.)
