Spread of infections in a heterogeneous moving population
Duncan Dauvergne, Allan Sly

Abstract
We consider a model where an infection moves through a collection of particles performing independent random walks. In this model, Kesten and Sidoravicius established linear growth of the infected region when infected and susceptible particles move at the same speed. In this paper we establish a linear growth rate when infected and susceptible particles move at different speeds, answering an open problem from their work. Our proof combines an intricate coupling of Poisson processes with a streamlined version of a percolation model of Sidoravicius and Stauffer.



1
Introduction

We consider an interacting particle system with two types of random walkers, labelled
{\bf S}
and
{\bf I}
for susceptible and infected (in other papers these are often called
A/B
or sometimes
X/Y
). When a susceptible particle meets an infected one, it too becomes infected. Special cases of this model include multi-particle Diffusion Limited Aggregation (MDLA) and the Frog Model.

Taking terminology from the epidemiology literature, we refer to this interacting particle system as an SI process. Formally, we define the SI process
X_{t}
as follows. The process is initialized with a sea of particles distributed according to a rate
\mu
Poisson process on
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
. All particles are initially
susceptible
and each susceptible particle performs a continuous-time rate-
D_{S}
simple random walk on
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
. At time
0
, a single
infected
particle is planted at the origin, which performs a continuous-time rate-
D_{I}
simple random walk. The particles interact as follows: when a susceptible particle is at the same vertex as an infected particle, it immediately becomes infected, at which point it starts performing a rate-
D_{I}
random walk. We let
{\bf S_{t}}
and
{\bf I_{t}}
denote the set of susceptible and infected particles at time
t
, respectively. For a particle
a
we will let
{\overline{a}}(t)
denote its position at time
t
.
Our main theorem states that for large
t
, every particle in a ball of radius
\gamma t
is infected.

Theorem 1.1
.

For any
\mu,D_{I},D_{S}>0
and any dimension
d\geq 1
there exists
\gamma(\mu,D_{I},D_{S},d)>0
and a random time
T
such that
T<\infty
almost surely and for all
t>T
,

\inf_{a\in{\bf S_{t}}}|{\overline{a}}(t)|\geq\gamma t.

Kesten and Sidoravicius
[
KS05
]
established Theorem
1.1
in the special case when
D_{I}=D_{S}
. Their proof made use of the special property that when the speeds of the two types are equal, the infection process does not affect the trajectories of the particles. In particular, the particles viewed without labels perform independent simple random walks and remain in equilibrium. Because of this, it was possible for them to study a Poisson process on the space of paths. They performed a multiscale analysis to show that regions with atypically few particles are rare and sparsely arranged, so the infection process can spread quickly. In the case
D_{I}\neq D_{S}
this analysis no longer works, as the infection process affects the trajectories and the two cannot be studied separately. In this setting, Kesten and Sidoravicius stated they could show that all partices in a ball of radius
t\log^{-C}(t)
are infected by time
t
, while omitting a proof. They left the case of unequal speeds as an open problem. Adding to the challenge, when
D_{I}\neq D_{S}
the infection process is not monotone in any of the parameters of the model
\mu,D_{I},D_{S}
. The process is not even monotone in the addition of new particles to the initial condition, as an additional particle may change the time of one infection, possibly delaying a later infection.

We note that the linear speed in Theorem
1.1
is the correct growth rate. Indeed,
[
KS05
]
showed the following result.

Theorem 1.2

(
[
KS05
]
, Theorem 1)
.

For any
\mu,D_{I},D_{S}>0
and
d\in{\mathbb{N}}
there exists
\gamma^{\prime}(\mu,D_{I},D_{S},d)>0
such that for large enough
t

\mathbb{P}\Big[\sup_{a\in{\bf I_{t}}}|{\overline{a}}(t)|\geq\gamma^{\prime}t\Big]\leq 2e^{-t}.

Remark 1.3
.

Our framework for proving Theorem
1.1
is quite robust and can accommodate variations of the setup. For example, the same proof also works if we modify the infection rule by only having susceptible particles become infected with probability
p\in(0,1)
each time they meet an infected particle, or if we allow infections to occur when susceptible particles enter a given radius of an infected particle. The proof also goes through verbatim if we start from any initial condition that stochastically dominates a Poisson initial condition, even though the SI process is not monotone in the initial condition.

1.1
Proof Sketch

The basic premise of the proof is that if the infection process is spreading linearly then it will keep visiting new regions of the plane with independently distributed particle configurations. It infects the particles in each new region, some of which spread out which continues the linear spread of the process. Of course, this is only approximately true and sometimes a new area will by chance only be sparsely populated with susceptible particles. Our proof is composed of two parts: an analysis of a competing growth model known as Sidoravicius–Stauffer percolation that will allow us to deal with sparsely populated regions, paired with a coupling of Poisson processes that can be thought of as a way to make rigorous the notion of constantly discovering new independent particles.

In order to preserve independence, we reveal the process only in a limited but growing portion of the plane. Partitioning the plane into blocks, we “colour” a block the first time an infected particle enters that block (red) or if a certain time has elapsed since a neighbouring block was coloured (blue); the blue colouring ensures that the colouring process grows with a linear speed. We only reveal particles when they are first in a coloured block. For each block we generate an independent Poisson process on the space of particle trajectories. When a new block is coloured we use this Poisson process to decide what particles are revealed. To account for the dependence in the original SI process, we only add particles whose past trajectories would not have previously been in a coloured region. This is the correct conditional distribution. Since our colouring process is moving at linear speed whereas the random walks are diffusive, the probability that a particle trajectory was previously in a coloured region does not increase with
t
, ensuring that we typically reveal a large number of particles in each block.

Using this construction we can “lower bound” the number of particles and the spread of the infection in each newly discovered coloured block. When a block is coloured red, an infected particle has entered and there will typically be a large number of newly discovered particles that it can infect in a short period of time. At least some of these particles will likely move to a neighbouring block rather quickly, propagating the infection. To make this precise, we define events
\mathcal{A}_{B}
for each block which have high probability, are almost independent (formally they are 1-dependent), and guarantee that if a block is red then all its neighbouring blocks will contain an infected particle after a short period of time. We have good control over
\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{A}_{B}]
. In our setup, we can guarantee that the probability of the events
\mathcal{A}_{B}
will increase to
1
as we use coarser blocks and slower growth rates for the blue process.

In order to show that most blocks are red and that the infection process is spreading quickly, we will relate the colouring process to
Sidoravicius–Stauffer Percolation
(SSP), introduced in
[
SS19
]

It was introduced with the somewhat unwieldy name First Passage Percolation in a Hostile Environment (FPPHE)
. This process, which was first used to study MDLA, is given by the competition between two i.i.d. first-passage percolation processes. The first of these is red, and is initiated at the origin at time
0
. As the red process tries to spread, it may encounter
blue seeds
at certain vertices. Whenever it attempts to invade a blue seed, the blue growth process becomes activated from that vertex and starts to spread, competing with the red process to annex vertices.
Sidoravicius and Stauffer used a multiscale construction to show that if the red growth rate is faster than the blue growth rate,
d\geq 2
, and the blue seeds are given by site percolation with small enough probability
p
, then with positive probability, most of
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
becomes red.

We construct a simplified version of SSP in which the blue process spreads along an edge
(u,v)
after a waiting time
\kappa(u,v)\gg 1
while the red process spreads in time at most
1
. This simplifies the multi-scale analysis as we need only consider the random locations of the blue seeds and can ignore the randomness in the passage times. This is important, since in our setting these passage times may have complicated dependencies. This simplification also allows us to have a caveat where the red process can potentially ‘tunnel’ through the blue process, a phenomenon that arises in our coupling with the SI model.

We take the blue seeds to be the blocks where
\mathcal{A}_{B}^{c}
holds and show that the blue blocks in the SI colouring process can be stochastically dominated by the blue set of our SSP. While the red component of the SSP is infinite only with positive probability, we can make this probability go to
1
by taking blocks with larger side-lengths. This, however, comes at the expense of a slower overall growth rate, and hence only shows Theorem
1.1
with a
random

\gamma>0
. To get around this, we consider how SSPs with smaller side-lengths and different values of
\kappa
are coupled to the same SI process. Analyzing the relationships between the spread of these processes allows us to conclude Theorem
1.1
with a
deterministic

\gamma>0
for all dimensions
d\geq 2
.

The tools of Sidoravicius-Stauffer percolation are no longer relevant in dimension
1
, where the red process can never go around a blue seed. However, in this setting we can use a simpler proof idea, still based on a coupling of the SI process with different Poisson processes, where we look at block sizes of varying scales. This Poisson framework for the proof is related to the framework used in the study of supercritical MDLA in dimension
1
in
[
Sly16
]
, though the details of the proofs are different.

1.2
Prior work

Two special cases of this model have received considerable attention. The Frog Model, also known as the Stochastic Combustion process, is the case
D_{A}=0
where susceptible particles do not move until they become infected. This substantially simplifies the analysis since a particle is always infected at its initial location. Consequently a great deal is know for the Frog Model including a shape theorem and large deviations
[
AMP02
,
RS04
,
CQR09
,
BR10
]
.

The other natural special case is
D_{B}=0
, also known as multi-particle Diffusion Limited Aggregation (MDLA). Here the infection rule must be modified slightly since otherwise all infected particles would be frozen on the same vertex. Instead, particles become infected just before they move onto an infected vertex and remain frozen in place there, along with any other susceptible vertices at the same location. This is similar to the well known Diffusion Limited Aggregation model where particles enter one at a time from infinity and perform a random walk until they hit the aggregate. Instead, in MDLA, there is a sea of susceptible particles which move as random walks until they hit the aggregate and freeze. Sidoravicius and Stauffer
[
SS19
]
showed that in a variant where particles perform exclusion processes that there is linear growth with positive probability if the density is close enough to
1
in dimension
2
and higher. In the random walk version of the model, linear growth with probability
1
was established in
[
Sly16
]
whenever the density is at least
\frac{5}{6}
. The question of a shape theorem remains open.
An unpublished conjecture of Eldan predicts that there is no phase transition. That is, there is linear growth for all positive initial densities in dimension 2 and higher.

In fact, a phase transition in MDLA is expected to occur only in dimension 1. Kesten and Sidoravicius
[
KS08a
]
showed that the diameter of the aggregate grows like order
t^{\frac{1}{2}}
when the initial density is less than 1 while
[
Sly16
]
showed that the aggregate grows linearly when the initial density is greater than 1. In the critical case Elboim, Nam and the second author
[
ENS20
]
showed that the speed is
t^{\frac{2}{3}}
and the scaling limit is given by the integral of a Bessel
(8/3)
process to the power of
-\frac{2}{3}
. A discontinuous scaling limit was found in a simplified
frictionless
version of the model by Dembo and Tsai
[
DT19
]
.

A natural question in any growth model is to establish a limiting shape theorem. In the case of a simple model like first passage percolation this is established by a subadditivity argument
[
Ric73
,
CD81
]
. This does not hold in the SI model. However, in the case
D_{S}=D_{I}
, Kesten and Sidoravicius
[
KS08b
]
were able to establish an approximate superconvolutivity property, roughly showing that superadditivity happens on average. In doing so they established a shape theorem. Their approach heavily uses monotonicity results that are not available when
D_{S}\neq D_{I}
, although we still expect them to be approximately true.

Our approach can be viewed as a synthesis of two main ideas. The first is that revealing the particles and their trajectories corresponds to revealing part of a Poisson process on the space of random walk paths and that the information revealed is a stopping set which leaves the remainder of the Poisson process independent. A simpler version of this approach was used to study MDLA
[
Sly16
,
ENS20
]
. With this perspective, we are able to achieve a certain amount of spatial independence which plays a crucial role in our proof.

The second main idea is to couple the block process with Sidoravicius–Stauffer Percolation for competing growth models.
The application of SSP in
[
SS19
]
required the case where the competing processes grow with almost equal speed (see also
[
FS20
]
). Our framework requires less from SSP. In particular, we can allow a large deterministic separation of the speeds of the competing growth processes. While our conditions are different from
[
SS19
]
and so we reprove those results, the proofs in that part of the paper are essentially simplified versions of theirs. In particular, the fact that the blue process grows deterministically means that we need only consider the randomness of the seed locations and not the passage times which substantially streamlines the argument.

The combination of the two approaches also lends itself to proving linear growth with probability 1. Our local analysis of the infection process holds with higher probability as we take the block size to infinity. This, however, comes at the expense of a slower overall growth rate. By coupling to SSPs at different scales, we show how the SI process can get a good start with larger blocks and then move down to smaller blocks and a faster growth rate.

2
Sidoravicius–Stauffer percolation

In this section we develop a deterministic theory of competing red and blue growth processes known as Sidoravicius–Stauffer percolation (SSP). Informally, in SSP, a set of red vertices grows outwards from the origin in
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
according to a set of edge weights, similarly to first passage percolation. Within
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
, there are a collection of blue seeds
\mathfrak{B}_{*}
which cannot be invaded by the red process. Whenever the red process attempts to invade a blue seed, the blue proces becomes activated. Once activated, the blue process will also grow outward from a blue seed at a slow, constant speed. The red and blue processes can only invade squares that are not already part of one of the two coloured processes.

Within this framework, we also allow for squares adjacent to the blue process to become activated at arbitrary times. When these squares are activated, they will turn red and join the red process unless they belong to the set of blue seeds, in which case they will turn blue.

2.1
Constructing the process

We will define a pair of coupled growth processes
\mathfrak{R},\mathfrak{B}:[0,\infty)\to\{S:S\subset\mathbb{Z}^{2}\}
. The growth of these processes will be governed according to weights on the set of directed edges

E:=\{(u,v)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}:\|u-v\|_{1}=1\}.

We will need the following data to define
\mathfrak{R},\mathfrak{B}
.

•

A function
X_{\mathfrak{R}}:E\to[0,1]
. We will think of
X_{\mathfrak{R}}
as a collection of clocks which govern the growth of the red process.

•

A collection of blue seeds
\mathfrak{B}_{*}\subset\mathbb{Z}^{2}
.

•

Functions
X_{\mathfrak{B}}:E\to[0,\infty)
and
\kappa:E\to(0,\infty)
such that
X_{\mathfrak{B}}\leq\kappa
. We will think of
X_{\mathfrak{B}}
as a collection of clocks which govern the growth of the blue process, and we call
\kappa
the
parameter
of the process.

Edges where
X_{\mathfrak{B}}(e)=\kappa(e)
will encourage the spread of the blue process, whereas other edges will encourage the spread of the red process. When thinking about the proofs in this section, it may be helpful for the reader to think of
\kappa
as constant. There will be no extra nuance for the case of variable
\kappa
.

•

We have allowed for the caveat that clocks may have a value of
0
. This corresponds to instantaneous invasion. To ensure that the process is still well-defined even when instantaneous invasion is allowed, we require that there are no directed cycles
(u_{0},u_{1},\dots u_{n}=u_{0})
with

X_{\mathfrak{R}}(u_{i-1},u_{i})\wedge X_{\mathfrak{B}}(u_{i-1},u_{i})=0

for all
i=1,\dots,n
.

We define
\mathfrak{R}
and
\mathfrak{B}
by recording a time
T(u)
when each vertex
u
gets added to the process, and a colour
C(u)
for that vertex. We initialize the process by setting
T(0)=0
, and setting
C(0)=\mathfrak{R}
if
0\notin B
and
C(0)=\mathfrak{B}
if
0\in\mathfrak{B}_{*}
. The rules for assigning the other times and colours are as follows.

For every edge
(u,v)\in E
, at time
T(u)+X_{C(u)}(u,v)
, the edge
(u,v)
will ring, and the process will update accordingly. If
T(v)<T(u)+X_{C(u)}(u,v)
, then nothing happens. This corresponds to the case when
v
has already been added to one of the processes by this time. Otherwise, we set
T(v)=T(u)+X_{C(u)}(u,v)
, and colour
v
according the following four rules.

1.

If
v\in\mathfrak{B}_{*}
, set
C(v)=\mathfrak{B}
.

2.

If
C(u)=\mathfrak{R}
and
v\notin\mathfrak{B}_{*}
, then set
C(v)=\mathfrak{R}
.

3.

If
C(u)=\mathfrak{B},v\notin\mathfrak{B}_{*}
, and
X_{\mathfrak{B}}(u,v)<\kappa(u,v)
, then set
C(v)=\mathfrak{R}
.

4.

If
C(u)=\mathfrak{B},v\notin\mathfrak{B}_{*}
and
X_{\mathfrak{B}}(u,v)=\kappa(u,v)
, then set
C(v)=\mathfrak{B}
.

One caveat with this set of rules, is if
T(u)+X_{C(u)}(u,v)=T(u^{\prime})+X_{C(u^{\prime})}(u^{\prime},v)
for two different vertices
u,u^{\prime}
. In this case, if the colours assigned to
v
via the two edges
(u,v)
and
(u,v^{\prime})
are different, we set
C(u)=\mathfrak{B}
. For
t\in[0,\infty)
, we set

\begin{split}\mathfrak{R}(t)&=\{u\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}:T(u)\leq t,C(u)=\mathfrak{R}\}\quad\text{and}\\
\quad\mathfrak{B}(t)&=\{u\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}:T(u)\leq t,C(u)=\mathfrak{B}\}.\end{split}

(1)

When working with SSPs, we will always assume that for any
t
, only finitely many squares
u
satisfy
T(u)<t
.
Under this
finite speed
assumption, the colouring process is well-defined. From now on, all processes we consider have finite speed.

The above construction defines an SSP on
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
driven by an initial red site at the origin at time
0
. We will also consider SSPs on certain connected subsets of vertices
V\subset\mathbb{Z}^{2}
, activated by an external red source at the
boundary

\partial V=\{u\in V:(u,v)\in E\text{ for some }v\notin V\}.

We assume
\partial V
is finite.
These processes are defined in the following way. Let
S:\partial V\to[0,\infty)
be any function such that
S(x)<\infty
for at least one

. We call

the

.
The evolution of the SSP on

2.2
An encapsulation theorem

The main goal of Section
2
is to show that under certain conditions on an SSP, if
\kappa
is bounded below by a sufficiently large constant, then we can find a set
\mathfrak{C}
depending only on the initial configuration of blue seeds such that
\mathfrak{B}(\infty)\subset\mathfrak{C}
. We will do this with a multiscale argument, and the set
\mathfrak{C}
will be built in terms of these scales. Note that the restriction on
\kappa
is purely to facilitate the proof; a version of the same result holds for any
\kappa>1
.

We first set up the series of required scales. Here and throughout the paper, various explicit constants appear. These have no meaning and are not chosen optimally – we only work with explicit constants to help orient the reader with the large number of different scales that must be balanced.

Let
r_{0}=1\leq r_{1}<r_{2}<\dots
be a sequence of integer scales satisfying

\gamma:=\prod_{i=0}^{\infty}\left(1+\frac{10^{12}r_{i}}{r_{i+1}}\right)<2,

(2)

and such that
r_{i+1}=(i+1)^{2}r_{i}
for all large enough
i
.
Condition (
2
) implies that
10^{12}r_{i}\leq r_{i+1}
for all
i
and the relationship between
r_{i},r_{i+1}
implies that there exists a constant
c>0
such that

c^{-1}\leq\frac{r_{n}}{(n!)^{2}}\leq c

(3)

for all
n
.
To define the sets used to form
\mathfrak{C}
we first set up some notation. For closed disks and annuli, we write

D(x,r)=\{y\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}:|y-x|\leq r\},\qquad A(x,r,R)=\{y\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}:r\leq|y-x|\leq R\},

where
|x-y|
denotes graph distance with the edge set
E
, or equivalently the
L^{1}
-distance. Here and throughout the paper, for two sets
A,B
, we use the notation

d(A,B)=\inf\{d(x,y):x\in A,y\in B\},

for the infimal distance between two sets, and write
d(x,A):=d(\{x\},A)
. We will also use the union-of-disks notation

D(A,r)=\{y\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}:d(y,A)\leq r\}=\bigcup_{x\in A}D(x,r).

Now, for a set of blue seeds
\mathfrak{B}_{\diamond}\subset\mathbb{Z}^{2}
, define

A_{1}(\mathfrak{B}_{\diamond}):=\{x\in\mathfrak{B}_{\diamond}:(\mathfrak{B}_{\diamond}\setminus\{x\})\cap D(x,r_{1}/3)=\emptyset\},

We then recursively define
\mathfrak{B}_{\diamond,k}=\mathfrak{B}_{\diamond}\setminus\cup_{i=1}^{k-1}A_{i}(\mathfrak{B}_{\diamond})
, and let

A_{k}(\mathfrak{B}_{\diamond}):=\{x\in\mathfrak{B}_{\diamond,k}:\mathfrak{B}_{\diamond,k}\cap A(x,r_{k-1},r_{k}/3)=\emptyset\}.

Finally, for a set
A\subset\mathbb{Z}^{2}
, we let
[A]
be the union of
A
and all bounded components of
A^{c}
. Our main theorem about SSPs is the following.

Theorem 2.1
.

Consider an SSP
(\mathfrak{R},\mathfrak{B})
on
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
initiated from a set of blue seeds
\mathfrak{B}_{*}
with parameter
\kappa
such that
\kappa(e)>4000
for all
e\in E
. Suppose that:

1.

There exists some connected set
V\subset\mathbb{Z}^{2}
such that
\partial V\subset\mathfrak{R}(\infty)
.

2.

Let
\mathfrak{B}_{\diamond}=\mathfrak{B}_{*}\cap V
. Then
\mathfrak{B}_{\diamond}=\bigcup_{k=1}^{\infty}A_{k}(\mathfrak{B}_{\diamond})
.

3.

[\mathfrak{D}]\subset V\setminus(\partial V\cup\{0\})
, where
\mathfrak{D}:=\bigcup_{k=1}^{\infty}D(A_{k}(\mathfrak{B}_{\diamond}),r_{k}/100)
.

Then
\mathfrak{B}(\infty)\subset[\mathfrak{C}]\cup V^{c}
, where
\mathfrak{C}:=\bigcup_{k=1}^{\infty}D(A_{k}(\mathfrak{B}_{\diamond}),100r_{k-1})
.

Inputs for Theorem
2.1

The proof of Theorem
2.1
is based on a fairly involved inductive argument. In this section, we prove a collection of basic inputs that are required for the induction. The first input is a structural lemma about
\mathfrak{C}
and
\mathfrak{D}
.
We first define
k
-scale approximations

\mathfrak{D}_{k}:=\bigcup_{i=1}^{k}D(A_{i}(\mathfrak{B}_{\diamond}),r_{i}/100)\qquad\text{ and }\qquad\mathfrak{C}_{k}:=\bigcup_{i=1}^{k}D(A_{i}(\mathfrak{B}_{\diamond}),100r_{i-1}).

We will also need to work with a slight enlargement of
\mathfrak{C}
. Define

\mathfrak{C}^{+}:=\bigcup_{i=1}^{\infty}D(A_{i}(\mathfrak{B}_{\diamond}),300r_{i-1}),

and similarly define
\mathfrak{C}^{+}_{k}
. We will use throughout that
\mathfrak{C}_{k}\subset\mathfrak{C}_{k}^{+}\subset\mathfrak{D}_{k}
, so when bounding the sizes of any of these sets, it will always suffice to bound
\mathfrak{D}_{k}
.

Lemma 2.3
.

For every
k
, all components of
\mathfrak{D}_{k},[\mathfrak{D}_{k}]
have diameter at most
r_{k}
.

Proof.

Filling in holes cannot increase the diameter of a set, so it suffices to prove the lemma for
\mathfrak{D}_{k}
. We prove this by induction on
k
. The definition of
A_{1}(\mathfrak{B}_{\diamond})
implies that all components of
\mathfrak{D}_{1}
are disks of radius
r_{1}/100
, which have diameter bounded above by
r_{1}
. Now suppose that the claim holds for
\mathfrak{D}_{k-1}
. Let
C
be a connected component of
\mathfrak{D}_{k}
. We can write

C=C_{k}\cup C_{k-1},

where
C_{k}\subset D(A_{k}(\mathfrak{B}_{\diamond}),r_{k}/100)
and
C_{k-1}\subset\mathfrak{D}_{k-1}
. If
C_{k}
has at least two distinct connected components, then there must be a connected component
C_{k-1}^{\prime}
of
C_{k-1}
, and connected components
C_{k,1},C_{k,2}\subset C_{k}
such that

C_{k,1}\cup C_{k,2}\cup C_{k-1}^{\prime}

is connected. In particular, there must be points
x_{1},x_{2}\in A_{k}(\mathfrak{B}_{\diamond})
such that

d(x_{1},C_{k-1}^{\prime}),d(x_{2},C_{k-1}^{\prime})\leq r_{k}/100+1.

Since
C_{k-1}^{\prime}
has diameter at most
r_{k-1}
by the inductive hypothesis, this implies

|x_{1}-x_{2}|\leq r_{k}/50+2+r_{k-1}.

On the other hand,
|x_{1}-x_{2}|\geq r_{k}/50,
since otherwise
D(x_{1},r_{k}/100)
and
D(x_{2},r_{k}/100)
would overlap and hence so would
C_{k,1},C_{k,2}
. Therefore

x_{2}\in A(x_{1},r_{k}/50,r_{k}/50+2+r_{k-1}).

The annulus above is contained in
A(x_{1},r_{k-1},r_{k}/3)
for all
k
since
10^{12}r_{k-1}\leq r_{k}
for all
k
, and
r_{0}=1
. This contradicts that both
x_{1},x_{2}\in A_{k}(\mathfrak{B}_{\diamond})
.

Therefore
C_{k}
consists of a single connected component. By the construction of
A_{k}(\mathfrak{B}_{\diamond})
, we than have

C_{k}=D(B^{*},r_{k}/100),

where
B^{*}
is a cluster of blue seeds satisfying
\text{diam}(B^{*})\leq r_{k-1}
. Therefore

\text{diam}(C_{k})\leq r_{k}/50+r_{k-1}.

Attaching
C_{k}
to connected components of
C_{k-1}
can only increase the diameter by
2r_{k-1}
by the inductive hypothesis, so
\text{diam}(C)\leq r_{k}/50+3r_{k-1}\leq r_{k},

as desired.
∎

A key part of the inductive analysis involves showing that the red and blue processes cannot be slowed down or sped up too much by the presence of lower-scale blue clusters. We handle this issue by bounding lengths of paths that are forced to avoid
[\mathfrak{C}_{k}^{+}]
, and by bounding lengths of paths that can pass through components of
[\mathfrak{C}_{k}^{+}]
at no cost.

For a subset
G\subset\mathbb{Z}^{2}
, let

|y-z|_{G}

be the graph distance between
y
and
z
in
G
, where the edge set is induced from the edge set
E
on
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
. Also let

|y-z|_{G+}

be the weighted graph distance between two points in
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
, where all edges in
E
have weight
1
, except for edges with at least one vertex in
G
, which get weight
0
. In the remainder of this section, we build up to the following proposition.

Proposition 2.4
.

Define

\gamma_{k}=\prod_{i=1}^{k}\left(1+\frac{10^{12}r_{i-1}}{r_{i}}\right),

For every
k\geq 1
, for every
x,y\in[\mathfrak{D}_{k}]^{c}
we have

\gamma_{k}^{-1}|x-y|\leq|x-y|_{[\mathfrak{C}^{+}_{k}]+}\leq|x-y|_{[\mathfrak{C}^{+}_{k}]^{c}}\leq\gamma_{k}|x-y|.

In the statement above, recall that
[G]
is equal to
G
with its holes filled in.

The proof of Proposition
2.4
relies on an inductive argument. Building to get the inductive step will require several lemmas. For these lemmas, we assume that we have the following setup. First, for a set
A\subset\mathbb{Z}^{2}
, define its
outer boundary

\partial^{o}A:=\partial A^{c}.

We can think of the usual boundary
\partial A
as an
inner
boundary.

1.

We have sets
C\subset D\subset\mathbb{Z}^{2}
and
C^{\prime}\subset D^{\prime}\subset\mathbb{Z}^{2}
with
\partial^{o}D^{\prime}\cap D=\emptyset
. Moreover, these sets have simply connected components.

2.

For some

, all components of
D\cup D^{\prime}
have diameter at most
r
.

3.

For some

, for any
x,y
in distinct components of
D^{\prime}
we have
|x-y|>r^{\prime}
.

4.

For all
x,y\notin D
, we have

\frac{1}{2}|x-y|\leq|x-y|_{C+}\leq|x-y|_{C^{c}}\leq 2|x-y|.

5.

For all

in the same connected component of
\partial^{o}D^{\prime}
, we have

|x-y|_{[C\cup C^{\prime}]^{c}}\leq 10|x-y|_{C^{c}}.

Lemma 2.5
.

Suppose that we are in the setup above, and that additionally,
D^{\prime}
consists of a single component.
Then for any
x,y\in[D(D^{\prime},m)\cup D]^{c}
, we have

|x-y|_{[C\cup C^{\prime}]^{c}}\leq\left(1+\frac{60r}{m}\right)|x-y|_{C^{c}}.

Similarly, for all
x,y\in[D(D^{\prime},m)\cup D]^{c}
we have

|x-y|_{[C\cup C^{\prime}]+}\geq\left(1-\frac{3r}{m}\right)|x-y|_{C+}.

Proof.

For the first implication, let
x,y\in[D(D^{\prime},m)\cup D]^{c}
and let
G(x,y)
be a
|\cdot|_{C^{c}}
-geodesic from
x
to
y
. If
G(x,y)\cap D^{\prime}=\emptyset
, then
|x-y|_{[C\cup C^{\prime}]^{c}}=|x-y|_{C^{c}}
, and the claim follows. Also, if

2|x-y|\leq d(x,D^{\prime})+d(D^{\prime},y),

then any path from
x
to
y
that goes through
D^{\prime}
is at least length
2|x-y|
which is at least
|x-y|_{C^{c}}
by assumption
4
. Hence
G(x,y)\cap D^{\prime}=\emptyset
in this case as well.

It just remains to prove the lemma when

2|x-y|\geq d(x,D^{\prime})+d(D^{\prime},y)

(4)

and
G(x,y)\cap D^{\prime}\neq\emptyset
. We can find
a\neq b\in\partial^{o}D^{\prime}\cap G(x,y)
such that
G(x,a)\cap D^{\prime}=\emptyset,G(b,y)\cap D^{\prime}=\emptyset
, where
G(x,a),G(b,y)
are the segments of
G(x,y)
from
x
to
a
and
b
to
y
. Now, by the triangle inequality we have that

\displaystyle|x-y|_{[C\cup C^{\prime}]^{c}}

\displaystyle\leq|x-a|_{C^{c}}+|a-b|_{[C\cup C^{\prime}]^{c}}+|b-y|_{C^{c}}.

(5)

Next, we bound
|a-b|_{[C\cup C^{\prime}]^{c}}
. First, since
a,b\in\partial^{o}D^{\prime}
, neither point lies in
D
by assumption
1
. Therefore we can apply the bound in assumption
4
. Combining this with the bound in assumption
5
gives that

|a-b|_{[C\cup C^{\prime}]^{c}}\leq 20|a-b|.

By assumption
2
, this is bounded above by
20(r+2)\leq 60r
, so (
5
) is bounded above by
|x-a|_{C^{c}}+|b-y|_{C^{c}}+60r
, which is in turn bounded above by

\displaystyle|x-y|_{C^{c}}+60r.

(6)

For this bound, we have used that

|x-y|_{C^{c}}=|x-a|_{C^{c}}+|a-b|_{C^{c}}+|b-y|_{C^{c}}\geq|x-a|_{C^{c}}+|b-y|_{C^{c}}.

Now, (
4
) and the fact that
x,y\notin D(D^{\prime},m)
implies that
|x-y|_{C^{c}}\geq m
. Combining this with (
6
) yields the bound.

For the second implication, let
G^{+}(x,y)
be a
|\cdot|_{[C\cup C^{\prime}]+}
-geodesic from
x
to
y
. Again, the bound is straightforward if
G^{+}(x,y)\cap D^{\prime}=\emptyset
, and if not we can find
a\neq b\in\partial^{o}D^{\prime}\cap G^{+}(x,y)
such that
G^{+}(x,a)\cap D^{\prime}=\emptyset,G^{+}(b,y)\cap D^{\prime}=\emptyset
. Therefore

\displaystyle|x-y|_{[C\cup C^{\prime}]+}

\displaystyle\geq|x-a|_{C+}+|b-y|_{C+}

Now, on the other hand the triangle inequality implies

\displaystyle|x-y|_{C+}

\displaystyle\leq|x-a|_{C+}+|a-b|_{C+}+|b-y|_{C+}

\displaystyle\leq|x-a|_{C+}+|a-b|+|b-y|_{C+}

\displaystyle\leq|x-a|_{C+}+r+2+|b-y|_{C+},

so setting
Z=|x-a|_{C+}+|b-y|_{C+}
, the ratio of
|x-y|_{[C\cup C^{\prime}]+}
and
|x-y|_{C+}
is at least
Z/(Z+r+2)
. Finally,
Z\geq m
since
x,y\notin D(D^{\prime},m)
, so
Z/(Z+r+2)\geq m/(m+r+2)\geq 1-(3r)/m
, yielding the bound.
∎

Lemma 2.6

2.5
Bounds for random blue seeds

So far, our study of SSPs has been purely deterministic. In the remainder of the paper, we want to consider random SSPs. Typically, the randomness on the edge weights will be quite complex. However, in our applications of Theorem

the configuration of blue seeds

will be always be stochastically dominated by a low-intensity i.i.d. process. In this section, we bound the behaviour of

Lemma 2.11

\mathfrak{B}_{\diamond,k}^{\prime}\setminus(A_{k}(\mathfrak{B}_{\diamond})\cap\mathfrak{B}_{\diamond,k}^{\prime})\subset\mathfrak{B}_{\diamond,k+1}.

This follows from the definition and the inductive hypothesis. Indeed,

\displaystyle A_{k}(\mathfrak{B}_{\diamond}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\{x\in\mathfrak{B}_{\diamond,k}^{\prime}:\mathfrak{B}_{\diamond,k}^{\prime}\cap A(x,r_{k-1},r_{k}/3)=\emptyset\}

\displaystyle=A_{k}(\mathfrak{B}_{\diamond})\cap\mathfrak{B}_{\diamond,k}^{\prime},

of Theorem

are independent at distances greater than

. Therefore the probability that this happens for a fixed
y
is
\rho_{k-1}^{2}
. Therefore by a union bound,

\rho_{k}\leq r_{k-1}^{2}\rho_{k-1}^{2}.

Using the initial bound

\rho_{1}\leq p,

we get that

\rho_{k}\leq p^{2^{k-1}}r_{1}^{2^{k-1}}r_{3}^{2^{k-2}}\cdots r_{k-1}^{2}.

Now,
r_{k}\leq ce^{2k\log k}
for all
k
for some
c>0
, so we have that

\rho_{k}\leq p^{2^{k-1}}c^{2^{k}}\exp\left(2^{k-1}\sum_{i=1}^{k}2^{-i}i\log i\right).

The sum
\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}2^{-i}i\log i
is convergent, so the above is bounded by

(cp)^{2^{k-1}}

for some universal
c>0
.
∎

We use Lemma

to get bounds on the engulfing set

Lemma 2.13
.

Again, assume that
\mathfrak{B}_{\diamond}
is stochastically dominated by an i.i.d. Bernoulli process with parameter

.
Fix a ball

(19)

In particular, for every

(20)

Moreover, for all

\mathbb{P}([\mathfrak{D}]\cap D(x,r)\neq[\mathfrak{D}_{k}]\cap D(x,r))\leq r^{2}(cp)^{2^{k-1}},

(21)

and for all sufficiently small

\mathbb{P}\left(|[\mathfrak{D}]\cap D(x,r)|\geq\frac{1}{3}|D(x,r)|\right)\leq\exp\left(\log(cp)\exp(\log r/(c\log\log r))\right)

(22)

for an absolute constants

The same bounds hold with
\mathfrak{C},\mathfrak{C}_{k}
in place of
\mathfrak{D},\mathfrak{D}_{k}
.

Proof.

First,
[\mathfrak{D}]=\bigcup_{k=1}^{\infty}[\mathfrak{D}_{k}]
. This follows since any bounded component of

is a bounded component of

2.3

and are not also components of
[\mathfrak{D}_{k-1}]
, we have

[\mathfrak{D}]\cap D(0,r)\subset\bigcup_{k=1}^{\infty}[\mathfrak{D}_{k}]_{r+r_{k}},

and

M(r)\leq r_{K},\text{ where }K=\sup\{k\geq 1:[\mathfrak{D}_{k}]_{r+r_{k}}\neq\emptyset\}.

Moreover, if the set above is empty, then
M(r)=0
. We estimate
\mathbb{P}([\mathfrak{D}_{k}]_{r+r_{k}}\neq\emptyset)
. This is bounded above by
\mathbb{P}(A_{k}(\mathfrak{B}_{\diamond})\cap D(x,r+r_{k})\neq\emptyset)
. We can bound this by Lemma
2.12
, a union bound, and the bound
r_{k}\leq c\exp(2k\log k)
. This yields (
19
). The bound (
21
) also follows from this proof, and the analogous bound for

follows analogously.

The inequality (
20

19
) and a union bound over
n\in\{n_{0},n_{0}+1,\dots\}

. Note that to go from (

.

For (

), we first bound the same probability with
[\mathfrak{D}_{k}]
in place of
[\mathfrak{D}]

) with

and translation invariance of each of the sets

\mathbb{E}|[\mathfrak{D}_{k}]\cap D(x,r)|\leq cp|D(x,r)|.

Moreover, the independence in Lemma

and the diameter bound in Lemma

implies that if points

apart, then the events

Taking

2.6
Interacting pairs

To prove the almost sure lower bound on the speed in Theorem

3
Linear growth in dimension

In this section we define carefully constructed events in order to apply SSP to the SI model.

3.1
A block construction for the SI process

If we change the rates of the susceptible and infected particles to
\theta D_{S}
and
\theta D_{I}
then it effectively speeds up the process by a time factor of
\theta
. Thus by a suitable rescaling, we can without loss of generality assume that
D_{S}=1
and let
\beta=D_{I}
.

Fix a side length
L=2^{k}
for some large
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
. We subdivide
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
into a collection of dyadic blocks

\mathscr{B}=\mathscr{B}_{L}:=\Big\{zL+\big\{-L/2,\ldots,L/2-1\big\}^{2}:z\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}\Big\}.

(26)

This induces a natural map
f=f_{L}:\mathbb{Z}^{2}\to\mathscr{B}_{L}
.
Via a somewhat complicated construction, we will apply Theorems
2.14
and
2.15
to show that the infection grows linearly. Given a side length
L
, for some small
\alpha>0
, let
\xi=\xi_{L}=\alpha L^{2}
. This will be the minimum speed at which the colouring of blocks spreads for blocks far away from the origin.

We will now define a

for blocks

. The colouring process will depend on a slow initial speed parameter
\chi\geq 8\xi
, a small radius parameter
r\geq 0
and a large radius parameter
R\geq 2r\chi/\xi
.

Definition 3.1
.

We will let
\tau_{B}
denote the (stopping) time when a block
B
is first coloured. For a vertex
u\in B
we will use
\tau_{u}
as shorthand for
\tau_{B}
. A block
B
becomes coloured at time

(a)

A neighbouring block
B^{\prime}
was coloured at time

and

,
or
a neighbouring block
B^{\prime}
was coloured at time

and

,
or
a neighbouring block
B^{\prime}
was coloured at time
\tau_{B^{\prime}}=t-\xi
and

.

(b)

An infected particle enters
B
for the first time at time
t
.

(c)

A block

becomes coloured according to rule (b) at time
t
and the infected particle that enters
B^{\prime}
is within distance
\alpha L
of
B
.

In case (c) we call this a multi-colouring, where up to three blocks may be coloured simultaneously.
In case (b) we call the infected particle that entered

of

B^{\prime}
. In cases (b) and (c) we say that the block

of

Moving forward, we say that a particle
a
is

the first time that it is in a coloured block, either because the block was coloured or because it entered a coloured block. We let

denote the process of coloured particles up to time

denote the
\sigma
-algebra generated by

. We let

A key element of our analysis is to regard the collection of random walks as given by a Poisson process on
\mathfrak{W}

\frac{1}{t}|w(t)|\to 0

over all time

with the same law based on a collection of Poisson processes

Case 1: A newly coloured block:

.

For

Proof.

A principal particle

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\bigg[\int_{0}^{T}I(a(t)=x(t),S(a,U)>t)dt>0\bigg],

\displaystyle r_{a}

\mathcal{X}_{n}=\bigcup_{B\in\mathscr{B}_{L_{p}}:B\cap D(0,2\gamma n)\neq\emptyset}\Big\{\mathcal{K}_{B}-\tau_{B}>\frac{n}{2}-1\Big\}.

Letting
\epsilon>0
, by Proposition
3.13
we have that

\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{X}_{n},\mathcal{E}_{\epsilon}]\leq Cn^{-100}

for a constant
C
that depends on
\epsilon
but not on
n
.
On the event
\mathcal{X}_{n}^{c}
all particles that start in
D(0,2\gamma n)^{c}
are infected before time
n-1
. If
(\mathcal{X}_{n}\cup\mathcal{Y}_{n})^{c}
holds then all particles in
D(0,\gamma t)
at time
t
are infected for times
t\in[n-1,n]
. By the above bounds and the Borel-Cantelli Lemma,
(\mathcal{X}_{n}\cup\mathcal{Y}_{n})^{c}
holds only finitely often a.s. on
\mathcal{E}_{\epsilon}
. Since
\epsilon>0
was arbitrary and
\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{E}_{\epsilon}]\geq 1-\epsilon
by Corollary
3.12
, this completes the proof.

4
Linear growth in dimension
d=1

In dimension
d=1
, we cannot appeal to the framework of Sidoravicius-Stauffer percolation to prove that the infection survives forever. However, unsurprisingly a simpler proof idea works in this case. The key is again to use a Poisson decomposition of the process with appropriate notions of red and blue (fast and slow) blocks. However, since the process cannot travel around slow blocks, if we encounter a slow block we will instead look for a fast block at a larger scale.

In this section, all constants will depend on the diffusion rates
D_{S},D_{I}
and the density
\mu
.
Fix a small constant
\delta>0
and a large constant
L\in{\mathbb{N}}
. How large or small we need to take these values will be made clear in the proof. As it turns out, we will need to choose a fixed
\delta>0
so that two of the steps in the forthcoming Lemma
4.3
go through, and then
L
will be chosen so that
L\delta^{2}
is larger than some big
D_{S},D_{I},\mu
-dependent constant.

For every
a\in\mathbb{Z}
, let
\tau_{a}
be the first time when an infected particle reaches site
a
, and let
\tau_{0}=0
. For
a,k\in{\mathbb{N}}
, let
\mathfrak{R}(a,k)
be the event where the following two conditions are satisfied.

(i)

At every time
t\in[\tau_{a},\tau_{a}+k^{2}L^{2}]
there are at least
\delta kL
total particles in the interval
(a,a+kL]
. Note that at time
\tau_{a}
, all of these particles are necessarily susceptible.

(ii)

\tau_{a+kL}\leq\tau_{a}+k^{2}L^{2}
.

We think of
\mathfrak{R}(a,k)
as a ‘red’ event where the process moves sufficiently quickly at scale
k
through the box
(a,a+kL]
. Letting
S_{0}=0,K_{0}=0
, we now recursively define scales and increments
K_{i},S_{i}
by

K_{i}=\inf\{k\in{\mathbb{N}},k\geq K_{i-1}-1:\mathfrak{R}(S_{i-1},k)\text{ holds}\},\quad S_{i}=S_{i-1}+LK_{i}=\sum_{j=1}^{i}LK_{j}

Also, let
\mathcal{F}_{i}
be the
\sigma
-algebra generated by all trajectories of all infected particles up to time
\tau_{S_{i}}
. Our goal is to show that the sizes of the
K_{i}
are well-controlled. The key to doing this is the following observation:

•

Let
Y(t)
be the location of the rightmost infected particle in the process. Conditionally on
\mathcal{F}_{i}
, the distribution of susceptible particles in the interval
(S_{i},\infty)
at time
\tau_{S_{i}}
is a Poisson process of varying intensity
\mu P^{b}_{i},b\in(S_{i},\infty)
, where

P^{b}_{i}=\mathbb{P}(X(t)+b-Y(\tau_{S_{i}}-t)>0\quad\text{ for all }t\in[0,\tau_{S_{i}}]\;|\;\mathcal{F}_{i}),

where
X
is an independent continuous time random walk of rate
D_{S}
started at
0
.

This observation is the analogue of the Poisson description of the SI process used in prior sections. The construction of the
K_{i},S_{i}
above allows us to get a lower bound on the probabilities
P^{b}_{i}
.

Lemma 4.1
.

There is a constant
c>0
depending only on
D_{S}
such that for
k\geq 2
we have

P^{b}_{k}\geq c\mathbf{1}(b-S_{k}\geq LK_{k}/8).

The key point in the proof of Lemma
4.1
is that

K_{j}\leq K_{i}+i-j\qquad\text{ for all }j\leq i\in{\mathbb{N}}.

(51)

To take advantage of this, we prove a simple lemma about sequences.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
s_{i}
be a sequence of natural numbers such that
s_{j}\leq s_{i}+i-j
for all
j\leq i\in{\mathbb{N}}
. Then

\sum_{i=1}^{k}s_{i}^{2}\geq t\qquad\implies\qquad\sum_{i=1}^{k}s_{i}\geq\frac{t}{2s_{k}}\wedge\frac{t^{2/3}}{2}.

(52)

Proof.

Let
s_{j}=M
be the largest element of
\{s_{1},\dots,s_{k}\}
. We have

{\raisebox{1.99997pt}{$\sum_{i=1}^{k}s_{i}$}\left/\raisebox{-1.99997pt}{$\sum_{i=1}^{k}s_{i}^{2}$}\right.}\geq\frac{1}{M},

(53)

so if
M\leq 2s_{k}
, (
53
) implies the lemma. Now suppose
M>2s_{k}
. Without loss of generality, we may assume that

\sum_{i=j+1}^{k}s_{i}^{2}\leq t,

since otherwise we could remove
s_{1},\dots,s_{j}
and decrease the right side sum in (
52
). Now, the conditions of the lemma guarantee that the interval
\{s_{k},s_{k}+1\dots,M-1\}
is contained in the set
\{s_{j+1},\dots,s_{k}\}
, so

\frac{1}{8}M^{3}\leq\frac{1}{2}(M-s_{k})((M-1)^{2}+s_{k}^{2})\leq\sum_{i=j+1}^{k}s_{i}^{2}\leq t.

(54)

For the first inequality in (
54
), we have used that
M>2s_{k}
, and hence also
M\geq 3
. Combining (
54
) and (
53
) then yields the result.
∎

Proof of Lemma

.

Let
\tilde{Y}(t)=Y(\tau_{S_{k}})-Y(\tau_{S_{k}}-t)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\Big(\forall s>0:\frac{X(L^{2}K_{k}^{2}s)}{LK_{k}}<\frac{1}{8}+\frac{s^{2/3}}{6}\mathbf{1}(1<s)\Big).

By standard random walk estimates, this probability is bounded below by a constant

since the scaling factor

is bounded away from

To complete the proof of the lemma, it just remains to show (

Let

\tilde{Y}(t)\geq S_{k}-S_{j(t)}=\sum_{i=j(t)+1}^{k}LK_{i},\quad\text{ where }\quad j(t)=\min\{j\in{\mathbb{N}}:\tau_{S_{j}}>\tau_{S_{k}}-t\}.

(56)

Next, observe that

t\leq\tau_{S_{k}}-\tau_{S_{j(t)-1}}=\sum_{i=j(t)}^{k}\tau_{S_{i}}-\tau_{S_{i-1}}\leq\sum_{i=j(t)}^{k}K^{2}_{i}L^{2}.

Since

. Combined with (

\sum_{i=j(t)+1}^{k}LK_{i}\geq\frac{1}{3}\sum_{i=j(t)}^{k}LK_{i}.

(58)

By looking at (

), (
57
), (
58
), to lower bound
\tilde{Y}(t)
, we see that we want to bound the right side of (

) below subject to the constraint that
\sum_{i=j(t)}^{k}K_{i}^{2}\geq t/L^{2}
. By (

), this puts us in the setting of Lemma

. Therefore

\tilde{Y}(t)\geq\frac{t}{6LK_{k}}\wedge\frac{t^{2/3}}{6L^{1/3}}\geq\frac{t^{2/3}}{6L^{1/3}K_{k}^{1/3}},

where in the final equality, we use that

∎

We use Lemma
4.1
to bound the
K_{i}
.

Lemma 4.3
.

There exists a constant
c>0
such that for
i\geq 2
and
n,m\in{\mathbb{N}}
with
n\geq m-1
we have

\mathbb{P}(K_{i}>n,K_{i-1}=m\mid\mathcal{F}_{i-1})\leq cn^{-4}\mathbf{1}(K_{i-1}=m).

In the proof, constants
c,c^{\prime},c^{\prime\prime}
depend only on
D_{S},D_{I},\mu
.

Proof.

We work conditionally on
\mathcal{F}_{i-1}
and on the event where
K_{i-1}=m
. The conditional probability in the lemma can then be bounded above by the conditional probability of
\mathfrak{R}(S_{i-1},n)^{c}
.

First, as long as
\delta
was chosen sufficiently small, since
n\geq m-1
, under this conditioning the number of particles that stay in the spatial interval
[S_{i-1}+nL/3,S_{i-1}+2nL/3]
throughout the time interval
[\tau_{S_{i-1}},\tau_{S_{i-1}}+n^{2}L^{2}]
stochastically dominates a Poisson random variable of mean at least
2\delta nL
. This uses the density bound in Lemma
4.1
.
Therefore condition (i) fails with probability at most
\exp(-c\delta nL)\leq e^{-n}
.

Now set
x=\sqrt{\delta}nL\log^{1/2}(n+1)
. Condition (ii) is implied by the following three events:

I.

At time
\tau_{S_{i-1}}+n^{2}L^{2}/2
, at least one infected particle is in the region
[S_{i-1}-x,\infty)
.

II.

At time
\tau_{S_{i-1}}+n^{2}L^{2}/2
, at least
\delta n^{1/2}L
particles that were uninfected at time
\tau_{S_{i-1}}
lie in the interval
J=(S_{i-1}-2x,S_{i-1}-x]
.

III.

Of these particles, at least one is in the region
[S_{i-1}+nL,\infty)
at time
\tau_{S_{i-1}}+n^{2}L^{2}
.

We estimate the probability of events I, II, and III failing. For I, by condition (i) for the event
\mathfrak{R}(S_{i-1},m)
, there are at least
\delta mL
infected particles in the region
(S_{i-1}-mL,S_{i-1}]
at time
\tau_{S_{i-1}}
. For the first event to fail, all of these need to move left by distance at least
x-mL
in the interval
[\tau_{S_{i-1}},\tau_{S_{i-1}}+n^{2}L^{2}/2]
. For large enough
n
, we have
x-mL\geq x/2=\sqrt{\delta}nL\log^{1/2}(n+1)/2
. For such
n
the probability that one individual particle moves left by at least
x-mL
is
n^{-c^{\prime}\delta}
for some absolute constant
c^{\prime}>0
. Therefore the probability that all particles move left by this much is at most
n^{-c^{\prime}m\delta^{2}L}
. As long as
\delta^{2}L
is sufficiently large, this probability is at most
O(n^{-4})
.

Next, we show the probability that event II fails also has an upper bound of order lower than
O(n^{-4})
. Since the event (i) fails with probability at most
e^{-n}
, we may assume this event holds.
Given the event in (i), there are at least
\delta nL
susceptible particles in the region
(S_{i-1},S_{i-1}+nL]
at time
\tau_{S_{i}}
. Each of these has probability at least

c^{\prime\prime}\exp(-c^{\prime}x^{2}/(n^{2}L^{2}))\geq c^{\prime\prime}n^{-c^{\prime}\delta}

of being in the interval
J
at time
\tau_{S_{i-1}}+n^{2}L^{2}/2
. Therefore the number of particles that were uninfected at time
\tau_{S_{i-1}}
that are in
J
at this time stochastically dominates a binomial random variable with
\delta nL
trials and success probability
n^{-c^{\prime}\delta}
. As long as
\delta
was chosen small enough so that
c^{\prime}\delta\leq 1/4
and
\delta L
is sufficiently large, a standard estimate on the lower tail of a binomial random variable then shows that event II fails with probability less than
O(n^{-4})
. A similar argument shows that the event III also fails with this probability. Putting all this together shows that condition (ii) fails with total probability
O(n^{-4})
.
∎

Lemma 4.4
.

Almost surely,
K_{1}<\infty
.

The proof of Lemma
4.4
is essentially the same as the proof of Lemma
4.3
, so we omit it. The only difference is that for bounding the probability of event I in the proof above, we can only assume the existence of
one
infected particle (rather than
\delta mL
). This reduces the upper bound on
\mathbb{P}(K_{1}>n)
from
O(n^{-4})
to
O(n^{-\epsilon})
for some
D_{S},D_{I},\mu
-dependent positive number

.

Proposition 4.5
.

There exists
c>0
depending on
D_{S},D_{I},\mu
such that almost surely,

\limsup_{|a|\to\infty}\frac{\tau_{a}}{a}\leq c<\infty.

Proof.

First, by symmetry it suffices to prove the bound as
a\to\infty
. Next, for
k\in{\mathbb{N}},k\geq 2
, we can write

\frac{\tau_{S_{k}}}{S_{k-1}}\leq\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{k}K_{i}^{2}L^{2}}{\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}K_{i}L}\leq\frac{L}{k-1}\sum_{i=1}^{k}K_{i}^{2}.

(59)

Here the first inequality uses condition (ii) in the definition of
\mathfrak{R}(a,k)
, and the second equality uses the trivial bound that
\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}K_{i}L\geq(k-1)L
.
By Lemma
4.3
,
the sequence
(K_{i},i\in{\mathbb{N}})
is stochastically dominated by a recursively defined sequence
L_{i},i\in{\mathbb{N}}
, where
L_{1}=M_{1}
and
L_{i+1}=(L_{i}-1)\vee M_{i+1}
for a sequence
M_{i},i\in{\mathbb{N}}
, where the

are i.i.d. with finite third moment. Now, in a coupling where

for all

\sum_{i=1}^{k}K_{i}^{2}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{k}L_{i}^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\max_{1\leq j\leq i}[(M_{j}-(i-j))^{+}]^{2}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{k}M_{i}^{3}.

This bound and the law of large numbers shows that the limsup of the right side of (

) is almost surely bounded above by some constant

, so

\limsup_{k\to\infty}\frac{\tau_{S_{k}}}{S_{k-1}}\leq c<\infty.

(60)

Finally, for every
a\in{\mathbb{N}}
we have
\tau_{a}/a\leq\tau_{S_{k(a)}}/S_{k(a)-1}
, where
k(a)
is such that
S_{k(a)-1}\leq a\leq S_{k(a)}
. Finally,
k(a)\to\infty
with

∎

5
Proof of coupling

In this section, we prove that the construction of the SI process given in Section
3.1
is valid. All notation is as in that section, and for the proof in this section,
L
is a fixed side length and all blocks belong to
\mathscr{B}=\mathscr{B}_{L}
. The parameters
r,R,\chi
are also fixed.

The natural construction of the SI process is built from the Poisson process
\mathscr{P}
on
\mathfrak{W}
along with a single ignition trajectory
W_{B_{0},\operatorname{ig}}
for the initially infected particle, where
B_{0}
is the block containing the origin. This is the construction of the SI process given in
[
KS05
]
. In the arXiv version
[
kesten2003spread
]
of that article they show that the construction gives a strong Markov process. By the strong Markov property, the following process is equal to the original SI process in distribution: run the SI process according to
\mathscr{P},W_{B_{0},\operatorname{ig}}
but at every time a particle becomes the ignition particle for a block
B
at a site
x
, replace its trajectory for
s\in[\tau_{B},\infty)
with
x+W_{B,\operatorname{ig}}(\beta(s-\tau_{B}))
. It is this latter process that we will couple with the more complex block construction.

For
t\in\mathbb{R}
we define the time shift function
\psi_{t}:\mathfrak{W}\to\mathfrak{W}
by
(\psi_{t}(w))(s)=w(s-t)
. For
A\subset\mathfrak{W}
we let
\psi_{t}(A):=\{\psi_{t}(w):w\in A\}
.
We view the Poisson processes
\mathscr{P}_{B},B\in\mathscr{B}
taken together as a Poisson process
\mathscr{P}^{*}
on
\mathscr{B}\times\mathfrak{W}
. For
A\subset\mathfrak{W}
we let
\mathscr{P}\mid_{A}
denote the restriction of
\mathscr{P}
to
A
. We will show that there is a natural coupling of
\mathscr{P}
and
\mathscr{P}^{*}
such that they generate the same SI process
X_{t}^{*}
when using the same set of ignition trajectories.

Let
C_{t}\subset\mathbb{Z}^{2}
be the set of vertices in coloured blocks at time
t
in the SI process generated by
\mathscr{P}
and all ignition trajectories. Let
\mathcal{G}_{0}
denote the
\sigma
-algebra generated by the ignition particle trajectories of all the blocks. Define the set of paths that have entered the coloured region by time
t
as

A_{t}=\{w\in\mathfrak{W}:\exists t^{\prime}\in[0,t),w(t^{\prime})\in C_{t^{\prime}}\}.

Let
\mathcal{G}_{t}
be the filtration generated by
\mathscr{P}\mid_{A_{t}}
and
\mathcal{G}_{0}
, which is independent of
\mathscr{P}
.
By construction, the map
t\mapsto A_{t}
is left continuous; we let
A_{t}^{+}=\bigcap_{t^{\prime}>t}A_{t^{\prime}}
be its right continuous version.
We partition
A_{t}
into sets

A_{B,t}=\{w\in A_{t}:t^{\prime}=\min\{s\in[0,t),w(s)\in C_{s}\},w(t^{\prime})\in B\}.

This is the set of paths which first enter the coloured region in block
B
. Our construction ensures that
C_{t}
is adapted to
\mathcal{G}_{t}
since
C_{t}
grows either by infected particles from previously coloured blocks entering new blocks or by its spread to neighbouring blocks after time
\xi
is elapsed. We define the random map

\Psi:\mathfrak{W}\to\mathscr{B}\times\mathfrak{W}

such that for
w\in A_{B}
,

\Psi(w)=(B,\psi_{\tau_{B}}(w)).

We will couple
\mathscr{P}
and
\mathscr{P}^{*}
such that
w\in\mathscr{P}
if and only if
\Psi(w)\in\mathscr{P}^{*}
. The remaining point process
\mathscr{P}^{*}\setminus\Psi(\mathfrak{W})
is not used in the construction and this can be set independently of
\mathscr{P}
in the coupling. To see that this gives the correct coupling note that a particle with trajectory
w(t)
is first coloured in box
B
corresponds to a particle with trajectory
w(t-\tau_{B})
in
\mathscr{P}_{B}
.

Now let us see that, given
\mathcal{G}_{0}
, two different Poisson processes
\mathscr{P}\neq\mathscr{P}^{\prime}
cannot give rise to the same
\mathscr{P}^{*}
in the coupling. Let
C_{t}^{\prime}
be the coloured region given by the process generated by
\mathscr{P}^{\prime}
and define
A_{t}^{\prime}
analogously. Define the stopping times

T_{p}=\inf_{t}\{t:\mathscr{P}\mid_{A_{t}}\neq\mathscr{P}^{\prime}\mid_{A^{\prime}_{t}}\},\qquad T_{c}=\inf_{t}\{t:C_{t}\neq C_{t}^{\prime}\}

For
t<T_{p}\wedge T_{c}
we have that the processes of coloured particles must be equal and so
X_{t}^{*}=X_{t}^{*\prime}
. Suppose that
T_{c}=T_{p}\wedge T_{c}
. The processes
C_{t},C_{t}^{\prime}
are right continuous so we must have
C_{T_{c}}\neq C_{T_{c}}^{\prime}
. Suppose that
B\subset C_{T_{c}}
but
B\not\subset C_{T_{c}}^{\prime}
. This can only happen if a particle coloured at some time
T_{a}<T_{c}
in
X_{t}^{*}
enters
B
at time
T_{c}
. Now, that particle must also be present in
X_{T_{a}}^{*\prime}
since
\mathscr{P}\mid_{A_{T_{a}}}=\mathscr{P}^{\prime}\mid_{A^{\prime}_{T_{a}}}
and so must make the same jump into
B
at the same time. This is a contradiction, so
T_{p}<T_{c}
. As a consequence of this,
A_{t}=A_{t}^{\prime}
for
t\leq T_{p}
and
A^{+}_{T_{p}}=A^{+\prime}_{T_{p}}
.

Since the set
\{t:w\in A_{t}\}
is open on the left for any path
w
, we have that
\mathscr{P}\mid_{A_{T_{p}}}=\mathscr{P}^{\prime}\mid_{A_{T_{p}}}=\mathscr{P}^{\prime}\mid_{A^{\prime}_{T_{p}}}
. So we must have
\mathscr{P}\mid_{A^{+}_{T_{p}}}\neq\mathscr{P}^{\prime}\mid_{A^{+}_{T_{p}}}
. Suppose
w
is some trajectory in
\mathscr{P}\mid_{A^{+}_{T_{p}}\setminus A_{T_{p}}}
but not in
\mathscr{P}^{\prime}\mid_{A^{+}_{T_{p}}\setminus A_{T_{p}}}
. It is coloured in some block

and since
B
was coloured before time

we have that
\tau_{B}=\tau^{\prime}_{B}
. But then
(B,\psi_{\tau_{B}}(w))\in\mathscr{P}^{*}
so if
\mathscr{P}^{\prime}
can also be coupled with
\mathscr{P}^{*}
then
w
must be in
\mathscr{P}^{\prime}
as well, which is a contradiction.

Therefore the map from
\mathscr{P}\mapsto\mathscr{P}^{*}
is one-to-one, and it follows that from the coupling we can reconstruct
\mathscr{P}
from
\mathscr{P}^{*}
. To complete the proof of the desired coupling, we just need to check that
\mathscr{P}^{*}
is indeed a Poisson process when defined via
\Psi(\mathscr{P})
.

Proposition 5.1
.

Under the coupling given by
\Psi

\mathscr{P}^{*}
is a Poisson process on
\mathscr{B}\times\mathfrak{W}
.

Proof.

We let
\mathscr{P}^{*}_{0}

\mathscr{B}\times\mathfrak{W}
which is independent of
\mathscr{P}

\mathscr{P}^{*}

\mathscr{P}^{*}_{0}
.
Let
\mathcal{G}^{+}_{t}
be the filtration generated by
\mathscr{P}\mid_{A_{t}^{+}}
and
\mathcal{G}_{0}
.
We define
0=T_{1}<T_{2}<\ldots
to be the times that
C_{t}
grows or new particles hit
C_{t}
. Formally we say that for
i\geq 1,
we have

T_{i+1}=\inf\{t>T_{i}:C_{t}\neq C_{T_{i}}\}\wedge\inf\{t>T_{i}:\mathscr{P}(A_{t})>\mathscr{P}(A_{T_{i}}^{+})\}.

We will define a sequence of point processes
\mathscr{P}^{*}_{i}
for
i\geq 0
such that

\mathscr{P}^{*}_{i}\mid_{\Psi(A^{+}_{T_{i}})}=\Psi(\mathscr{P})\mid_{\Psi(A^{+}_{T_{i}})},\qquad\mathscr{P}^{*}_{i}\mid_{\Psi(A^{+}_{T_{i}})^{c}}=\mathscr{P}^{*}_{0}\mid_{\Psi(A^{+}_{T_{i}})^{c}}

(61)

where
\Psi(\mathscr{P})\mid_{\Psi(A^{+}_{T_{i}})}(\Lambda):=\mathscr{P}(\Psi^{-1}(\Lambda))
for
\Lambda\subset\Psi(A^{+}_{T_{i}})
. That is, we couple only the set
A^{+}_{T_{i}}
and the remainder of
\mathscr{B}\times\mathfrak{W}
is given by
\mathscr{P}^{*}_{0}
. By setting
A_{T_{0}}^{+}:=\emptyset
we can make
\mathscr{P}^{*}_{0}
is trivially consistent with equation (
61

\mathscr{P}^{*}_{i}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\mathscr{P}^{*}_{0}.

(62)

on the set

. In particular, the left side of (

In particular, conditional on
