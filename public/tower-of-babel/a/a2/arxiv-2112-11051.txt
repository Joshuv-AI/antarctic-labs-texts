A Feynman-Kac approach for the spatial derivative of the solution to the Wick stochastic heat equation driven by time homogeneous white noise
Hyun-Jung Kim, Ramiro Scorolli

Abstract
. We consider the (unique) mild solution u ⁡ ( t , x ) u(t,x) of a 1-dimensional stochastic heat equation on [ 0 , T ] × ℝ [0,T]\times\mathbb{R} driven by time-homogeneous white noise in the Wick-Skorokhod sense. The main result of this paper is the computation of the spatial derivative of u ⁡ ( t , x ) u(t,x) , denoted by ∂ x u ⁡ ( t , x ) \partial_{x}u(t,x) , and its representation as a Feynman-Kac type closed form. The chaos expansion of ∂ x u ⁡ ( t , x ) \partial_{x}u(t,x) makes it possible to find its (optimal) Hölder regularity especially in space.

1.
Introduction

As an extension of the paper
[
26
]
, we will further investigate the (unique) mild solution of the 1-dimensional stochastic heat equation (SHE):

(1)

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}u(t,x)=\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\partial^{2}_{xx}u(t,x)+u(t,x)\diamond\dot{W}(x),\quad t\in(0,T],\ x\in\mathbb{R},\\
u(0,x)=u_{0}(x),\quad x\in\mathbb{R},\end{cases}

where
T>0
,
u_{0}
is a function satisfying certain conditions,
\dot{W}(x)
is a space-only Gaussian
white noise
on a complete probability space
\left(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P}^{W}\right)
, and
\diamond
stands for the
Wick product
. In other words, the corresponding stochastic integration in (
1
) is interpreted in the Wick-Skorokhod sense. We postpone all technical definitions to the following section.

Definition 1
.

We say
u:[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}\times\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
is said to be a
mild solution
of (
1
) if for any fixed
(t,x)\in[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}
,
u(t,x)\in L^{2}\left(\mathbb{P}^{W}\right)
and it satisfies

(2)

\displaystyle u(t,x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}p(t,x-y)u_{0}(y)dy+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}}p(t-s,x-y)u(s,y)\diamond\dot{W}(y)dyds,\ \mbox{$\mathbb{P}^{W}$-almost surely},

where
p(t,x):=\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi t}}e^{-\frac{x^{2}}{2t}}
is the Gaussian heat kernel.

Let
\left\{u(t,x)\right\}_{(t,x)\in[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}}
be a mild solution of (
1
). Then for
any fixed
(t,x)
, the random variable
u(t,x)
admits the following multiple Wiener chaos expansion (e.g.
[
9
]
,
[
11
]
or
[
28
]
):

(3)

\displaystyle u(t,x)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}I_{n}\left(F_{n}^{\mathtt{MW}}(t,x)\right),

where
I_{n}
is the
n
-th multiple Wiener integral with respect to
W
,

\displaystyle F_{0}^{\mathtt{MW}}(t,x)=\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}p(t,x-y)u_{0}(y)\;dy;

\displaystyle F_{n}^{\mathtt{MW}}(t,x;y_{1},\dots,y_{n})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n!}\int_{[0,t]^{n}}p\left(t-r_{\rho(n)},x-y_{\rho(n)}\right)\times\cdots\times p\left(r_{\rho(2)}-r_{\rho(1)},y_{\rho(2)}-y_{\rho(1)}\right)F_{0}^{\mathtt{MW}}(r_{\rho(1)},x_{\rho(1)})\;d\mathbf{r},\ n\geq 1,

and
\rho
denotes the permutation of
\{1,\dots,n\}
such that
0<r_{\rho(1)}<\cdots<r_{\rho(n)}<t
. For simplicity, we have denoted
d\mathbf{r}:=dr_{1}dr_{2}\cdots dr_{n}
.

To distinguish among different representations of the mild solution, let us call (
3
) the
multiple Wiener solution

u^{\mathtt{MW}}(t,x)
of (
1
). There are a few papers considering this representation:

(i)

The paper
[
28
, Theorem 3.1]
shows that
u^{\mathtt{MW}}
is the unique mild solution in

C\left([0,T];L^{2}(\mathbb{R});L^{2}(\Omega)\right)
if
u_{0}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
by showing,

\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}n!\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left\|F^{\mathtt{MW}}_{n}(t,x;\bullet)\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}^{2}dx\leq C\|u_{0}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}^{2}<\infty.

Note that
u_{0}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
does not cover
u_{0}\equiv 1
.

(ii)

When
u_{0}\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
,
[
9
, Section 4]
shows that

\sup_{(t,x)\in[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}n!\left\|F^{\mathtt{MW}}_{n}(t,x;\bullet)\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}^{2}\leq C\|u_{0}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})}^{2}<\infty.

Hence, we can say that
u^{\mathtt{MW}}
is the unique mild solution in

C\left([0,T]\times\mathbb{R};L^{2}(\Omega)\right)
if
u_{0}\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
.

There is an alternative chaos expansion of the mild solution
u
(e.g.
[
17
, Theorem 3.11]
):

(4)

\displaystyle u(t,x)=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{J}}u^{\mathtt{CS}}_{\alpha}(t,x)\xi_{\alpha},

where
\xi_{\alpha}
and
\mathcal{J}
are defined in Section
2.1.2
. Letting
\mathbb{T}^{n}_{[0,t]}:=\{0\leq s_{1}\leq\cdots\leq s_{n}\leq t\}
, we can write

u^{\mathtt{CS}}_{(\mathbf{0})}(t,x)=\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}p(t,x-y)u_{0}(y)dy
, and for
|\alpha|=n\geq 1
,

\displaystyle u^{\mathtt{CS}}_{\alpha}(t,x)=\sqrt{n!}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{n}_{[0,t]}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}p(t-s_{n},x-y_{n})\cdots p(s_{2}-s_{1},y_{2}-y_{1})u^{\mathtt{CS}}_{(\mathbf{0})}(s_{1},y_{1})\;\mathfrak{e}_{\alpha}(y_{1},\dots,y_{n})\;d\mathbf{s}d\mathbf{y},

for
\alpha\in\mathcal{J}_{n}:=\{\alpha\in\mathcal{J}:|\alpha|=n\}
,
and
\{\mathfrak{e}_{\alpha},\alpha\in\mathcal{J}_{n}\}
forms an orthonormal basis of the symmetric part of
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. We will call (
4
) the
chaos solution

u^{\mathtt{CS}}(t,x)
of (
1
). The existence and uniqueness of this representation can be proved by showing the following:

(i’)

We can prove that
u^{\mathtt{CS}}
is the unique mild solution in
C\left([0,T];L^{2}(\mathbb{R});L^{2}(\Omega)\right)
when
u_{0}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
by showing (c.f.
[
14
, Theorem 4.1]
)

(5)

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{J}_{n}}\left\|u_{\alpha}^{\mathtt{CS}}(t,\bullet)\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}^{2}\leq C\|u_{0}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}^{2}<\infty.

(ii’)

We can also show that
u^{\mathtt{CS}}
is the unique mild solution in
C\left([0,T]\times\mathbb{R};L^{2}(\Omega)\right)
if
u_{0}\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
(c.f.
[
14
, Theorem 4.3]
) by achieving

(6)

\displaystyle\sup_{(t,x)\in[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{J}_{n}}\left|u_{\alpha}^{\mathtt{CS}}(t,x)\right|^{2}\leq C\|u_{0}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})}^{2}<\infty.

Indeed, (
5
) and (
6
) can be easily obtained as follows:

(
5
)

To use the same argument as
[
14
, Theorem 4.1]
, it is enough to show

U_{0}:=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}p(s,y-z_{1})u_{0}(z_{1})dz_{1}\right)\cdot\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}p(s,y-z_{2})u_{0}(z_{2})dz_{2}\right)dy\leq\|u_{0}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})},

and it is clear by semigroup property and Hölder inequality,

\displaystyle U_{0}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}p(s+r,z_{1}-z_{2})u_{0}(z_{1})u_{0}(z_{2})dz_{1}dz_{2}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}p(s+r,z_{1})\int_{\mathbb{R}}u_{0}(z_{1}+z_{2})u_{0}(z_{2})dz_{2}dz_{1}\leq\|u_{0}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}.

(
6
)

To use the same argument as
[
14
, Theorem 4.3]
, it is enough to show
\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}}p(t,x-y)u_{0}(y)dy\right|\leq\|u_{0}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})}
, and it automatically follows from the fact
\int_{\mathbb{R}}p(t,x)dx=1
.

Then, it is not surprising that
u^{\mathtt{MW}}=u^{\mathtt{CS}}
if
u_{0}\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
since the mild solution is unique in
C\left([0,T]\times\mathbb{R};L^{2}(\Omega)\right)
.

We now discuss one more possible representation of the mild solution. In fact, the condition (
2
) is equivalent to the following because
\mathbb{D}^{1,2}
is dense in
L^{2}\left(\mathbb{P}^{W}\right)
. (e.g.
[
19
, Exercise 1.1.7 and Corollary 1.5.1]
): For any random variable
F\in\mathbb{D}^{1,2}
,

(7)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[F\cdot u(t,x)\right]=\mathbb{E}[F]\cdot\int_{\mathbb{R}}p(t,x-y)u_{0}(y)dy+\mathbb{E}\left[F\cdot\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}}p(t-s,x-y)u(s,y)\diamond\dot{W}(y)dyds\right].

Here, Malliavin derivative
D
and the Sobolev-Malliavin space
\mathbb{D}^{1,2}
are defined in Section
2.2
.

Moreover, it is known that (
7
) is equivalent to

(8)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[F\cdot u(t,x)\right]=\mathbb{E}[F]\cdot\int_{\mathbb{R}}p(t,x-y)u_{0}(y)dy+\mathbb{E}\left[\left\langle\int_{0}^{t}p(t-s,x-\bullet)u(s,\bullet)ds,D_{(\bullet)}F\right\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}\right],

if
\mathbb{E}\left(F\cdot\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}h(y)\diamond\dot{W}(y)dy\right)\right)=\mathbb{E}\left(\langle DF,h\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}\right)
for
h\in L^{2}\left(\mathbb{P}^{W};L^{2}(\mathbb{R})\right)
and
\int_{\mathbb{R}}h(y)\diamond\dot{W}(y)dy\in L^{2}\left(\mathbb{P}^{W}\right)
(e.g.
[
20
, Section 2.5]
).

Using (
8
) and a Wong-Zakai-type approximation, the paper
[
26
]
gives a Feynman-Kac representation of the unique mild solution of (
1
) when
u_{0}\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
. This is given by

\displaystyle u(t,x)=\mathbb{E}^{B}\left[u_{0}(B_{t}^{x})\exp\{\Psi_{t,x}\}\right],

where
\{B_{t}^{x}\}_{t\geq 0}
is a one-dimensional Brownian motion starting at
x
, and for fixed
(t,x)\in[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}
, the random variable
\Psi_{t,x}
is given by

\displaystyle\Psi_{t,x}:=\int_{\mathbb{R}}L_{y}^{x}(t)dW(y)-\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}}|L_{y}^{x}(t)|^{2}dy.

Here
L_{a}^{x}(t)
denotes the local time of
\left\{B_{s}^{x}\right\}_{s\geq 0}
at level
a
and time
t
. Let us call the Feynman-Kac representation the
Feynman-Kac solution

u^{\mathtt{FK}}(t,x)
of (
1
).

Combining all, as long as
u_{0}\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
, we can say

(9)

\displaystyle u:=u^{\mathtt{FK}}=u^{\mathtt{MW}}=u^{\mathtt{CS}}\in C\left([0,T]\times\mathbb{R};L^{2}(\Omega)\right).

In this paper,
we will provide an alternative proof for the equivalence (
9
) using a more direct approach.

The main motivation for the current article is as follows: As we stated above,
the equation (
1
) may have three possible representations for the unique mild solution
u
, namely (I) Feynman-Kac solution
u^{\mathtt{FK}}
, (II) multiple Wiener-Itô integral solution
u^{\mathtt{MW}}
, and (III) chaos solution
u^{\mathtt{CS}}
. Unfortunately, there is no enough discussion on Hölder regularity of the mild solution. In particular,

(I)

There is no Hölder regularity result for
u^{\mathtt{FK}}
in the existing literature.

(II)

For
u^{\mathtt{MW}}
,
[
28
, Theorem 4.1]
proves that
u^{\mathtt{MW}}\in\mathcal{C}^{1/2-\varepsilon,1/2-\varepsilon}([0,T],\mathbb{R})
for any small
\varepsilon>0
if
u_{0}\in C^{1}_{b}(\mathbb{R})\cap L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
. Here,
C^{1}_{b}(\mathbb{R})
denotes the space of all bounded differentiable functions on
\mathbb{R}
with bounded continuous derivatives.

(III)

On the one hand, no one discusses the regularity of
u^{\mathtt{CS}}
on the whole line. On the other hand, the paper
[
14
]
discuss the same equation as (
1
), but on a bounded domain, say
[0,\pi]
; the authors show that there exists a unique mild solution (using chaos expansion)
u_{b}^{\mathtt{CS}}\in C([0,T];L^{2}([0,\pi]);L^{2}(\Omega))
if
u_{0}\in L^{2}([0,\pi])
, and moreover
u_{b}^{\mathtt{CS}}\in\mathcal{C}^{3/4-\varepsilon,3/2-\varepsilon}([0,T]\times[0,\pi])
for any small
\varepsilon>0
if
u_{0}\in\mathcal{C}^{3/2}([0,\pi])
.

Since Hölder continuity is a local property, it is natural to expect that
u^{\mathtt{CS}}\in\mathcal{C}^{3/4-\varepsilon,3/2-\varepsilon}([0,T]\times\mathbb{R})
for any small
\varepsilon>0
(under a suitable initial condition on
u_{0}
) like the bounded case
u_{b}^{\mathtt{CS}}
. Furthermore, it is impossible that the other representations
u^{\mathtt{FK}}
and
u^{\mathtt{MW}}
have a different regularity from the one of
u^{\mathtt{CS}}
(by uniqueness).
In this sense, we would say that the existing Hölder regularity results of the mild solution on
\mathbb{R}
should be improved, and in this paper, we will suggest an idea of how to get the desired result. We emphasize that the regularity almost
3/4
in time and almost
3/2
in space is optimal in the classical PDE sense, since
\dot{W}
is understood to have regularity
-1/2-\varepsilon
for any small
\varepsilon>0
(c.f.
[
6
, Lemma 1.1]
).

The main feature of this paper is to find the optimal spatial regularity of the unique mild solution
u
. The first task is to find
\partial_{x}u
and check if it is well-defined. One can compute
\partial_{x}u
from
u^{\mathtt{CS}}
using the same argument as
[
14
]
, but we will focus on the Feynman-Kac representation and compute the spatial derivative of
u
using
u^{\mathtt{FK}}
as a main part of this paper. This approach allows us to obtain a Feynman-Kac-type closed formula for
\partial_{x}u
.
We remark that we can also derive
the chaos decomposition of
\partial_{x}u
using
u^{\mathtt{FK}}
, and it is exactly the same as the one after differentiating
u^{\mathtt{CS}}
with respect to
x
directly.
With this in hand, we can achieve the optimal Hölder regularity of
\partial_{x}u
that is almost
1/4
in time and almost
1/2
in space.

2.
Preliminaries

2.1.
Elements of white noise analysis

In this section, we will give a brief outline of the white noise tools which will be used in this article. The interested reader for more details is referred to
[
7
]
and
[
15
]
.

For
n\in\mathbb{N}
, let
\mathscr{S}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
be the Schwartz space of rapidly decreasing functions on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and
\mathscr{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
the dual of
\mathscr{S}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, which is called the space of tempered distributions. We further define the white noise probability space by
(\mathscr{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}),\mathcal{B},\mu)
, where
\mathcal{B}
is the Borel
\sigma
-algebra on
\mathscr{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R})
, i.e. the
\sigma
-algebra generated by the
cylindrical sets
, and
\mu
is the
standard Gaussian measure in
\mathscr{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R})
(see
[
15
, Section 3.1]
for more details). Specifically, the measure
\mu
satisfies

\displaystyle\int_{\mathscr{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R})}e^{i\langle\omega,\varphi\rangle}d\mu(\omega)=e^{-\frac{1}{2}|\varphi|_{0}^{2}},\quad\quad\varphi\in\mathscr{S}(\mathbb{R}),

where
|\bullet|_{0}
denotes the norm in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
.

Let
W
denote the canonical coordinate process (or Wiener integral) on
\mathscr{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R})
given by

W_{\phi}=\langle\omega,\phi\rangle,\;\phi\in\mathscr{S}(\mathbb{R}),\;\omega\in\mathscr{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}).

Here,
\langle\bullet,\bullet\rangle
denotes the dual pairing between
\mathscr{S}(\mathbb{R})
and
\mathscr{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R})
.

Note that we can extend
W
continuously in
L^{2}(\mu)
to
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
as

\displaystyle\langle\omega,\phi\rangle:=L^{2}(\mu)-\lim_{k\to\infty}\langle\omega,\phi_{k}\rangle,\quad\phi\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}),

where
\{\phi_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
is any sequence in
\mathscr{S}(\mathbb{R})
such that
\phi_{k}\to\phi
in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
.
In particular, if we define

(10)

\displaystyle W(x)(\omega):=\begin{cases}\langle\omega,\chi_{[0,x]}\rangle,\quad\mbox{if $x\geq 0,\ \omega\in\mathscr{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R})$};\\
-\langle\omega,\chi_{[x,0]}\rangle,\quad\mbox{if $x<0,\ \omega\in\mathscr{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R})$,}\end{cases}

it is easy to check that
\{W(x)\}_{x\in\mathbb{R}}
is a Brownian motion.

2.1.1.
Chaos expansion in terms of multiple Wiener integrals

It is well-known that any random variable
F\in L^{2}(\mu)
can be written as

\displaystyle F=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}I_{n}(f_{n}),

where
I_{n}
is the multiple Wiener integral of order
n
with respect to the Brownian motion in (
10
), and
f_{n}
is a symmetric element of
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. This is the Wiener-Itô-Segal isomorphism between square integrable
Brownian functionals
and the the symmetric Fock space
[
13
]
, i.e.

\displaystyle L^{2}(\mu)\cong\bigoplus_{n=0}^{\infty}\operatorname{Sym}L^{2}(\mathbb{R})^{\otimes n}.

It turns out that the following isometry property holds

\displaystyle\|F\|^{2}_{2}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}n!|f_{n}|_{0,n}^{2},

where
\|\bullet\|_{2}
denotes the norm in
L^{2}(\mu)
and
|\bullet|_{0,n}
denotes the norm in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n}),n\in\mathbb{N}
, whenever
n=1
we shall omit the second sub-index.

2.1.2.
Chaos expansion in terms of Hermite polynomials

Let
\{e_{j}\}_{j\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\mathscr{S}(\mathbb{R})
be the family of
Hermite functions
defined by

\displaystyle e_{j}(x):=(-1)^{j-1}\left(\sqrt{\pi}2^{j-1}(j-1)!\right)^{-1/2}e^{x^{2}/2}\frac{d^{(j-1)}}{dx^{(j-1)}}e^{-x^{2}},\ j\in\mathbb{N},

where
\displaystyle\frac{d^{0}}{dx^{0}}
is the identity operator.
It is known that
\{e_{j}\}_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
forms a complete orthonormal basis (CONB) of
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
.
We next let
\mathcal{J}:=(\mathbb{N}_{0}^{\mathbb{N}})_{c}
be the collection of multi-indices
\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\dots)
such that every
\alpha_{j}
is a non-negative integer and there are only finitely many non-zero components. In this case, we define a few notations:

•

(\mathbf{0})
is the multi-index with all zeroes;

•

\alpha^{-}_{(j)}:=\left(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\dots,\mbox{max}(\alpha_{j}-1,0),\alpha_{j+1},\dots\right)\in\mathcal{J}
;

•

\alpha^{+}_{(j)}:=(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\dots,\alpha_{j}+1,\alpha_{j+1},\dots)\in\mathcal{J};

•

|\alpha|:=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\alpha_{j}
;

•

\alpha!:=\prod_{j=1}^{\infty}\alpha_{j}!
.

We also define the collection of random variables
\Xi:=\{\xi_{\alpha},\alpha\in\mathcal{J}\}
by

\displaystyle\xi_{\alpha}:=\prod_{j=1}^{\infty}\left(\frac{H_{\alpha_{j}}(W_{e_{j}})}{\sqrt{\alpha_{j}!}}\right),

where

\displaystyle H_{n}(x)=(-1)^{n}e^{x^{2}/2}\frac{d^{n}}{dx^{n}}e^{-x^{2}/2}

is the Hermite polynomial of order
n
.

One of the famous Cameron and Martin’s theorems
[
1
]
states that the family
\Xi
forms an orthonormal basis in
L^{2}(\mu)
, and thus for
F\in L^{2}(\mu)
, we have the following chaos expansion

\displaystyle F=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{J}}F_{\alpha}\xi_{\alpha}\quad\mbox{and}\quad\|F\|_{2}^{2}=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{J}}F_{\alpha}^{2},\quad\mbox{where}\ F_{\alpha}:=\mathbb{E}[F\xi_{\alpha}].

In fact, both approaches to the chaos expansion for
F\in L^{2}(\mu)
in Sections
2.1.1
and
2.1.2
are equivalent, and we can go from one to the other without particular difficulties. For example, see Section
3.2
below.

2.1.3.
S-transform, Wick product, and generalized random variables

Now we introduce one of the main tools we will employ in our proofs, namely the S-transform. Let
F\in L^{2}(\mu)
and
\phi\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
. Then the S-transform of
F
is given by

\displaystyle S(F)(\phi):=\mathbb{E}\left[F\times\mathcal{E}(\phi)\right],\quad\mbox{where}\quad\mathcal{E}(\phi):=\exp\bigg\{W_{\phi}-\frac{1}{2}|\phi|_{0}^{2}\bigg\}.

Note that
\mathcal{E}(\phi)

is called
stochastic exponential
or
Wick exponential
(e.g.
[
8
]
).

In certain applications, one may be interested in spaces that are larger than
L^{2}(\mu)
, and one is naturally led to consider spaces of
generalized
random variables (see for instance
[
7
]
,
[
10
]
,
[
15
]
). In this article, we will solely consider the space of
Hida distributions
, and will briefly introduce them in the following.

Let
A
be the operator given by
A=-\displaystyle\frac{d^{2}}{dx^{2}}+x^{2}+1
, and for
F\in L^{2}(\mu)
with
F=\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}I_{n}(f_{n})
satisfying

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}n!|A^{\otimes n}f_{n}|_{0,n}^{2}<\infty,

we define
\Gamma(A)F\in L^{2}(\mu)
by

\displaystyle\Gamma(A)F:=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}I_{n}(A^{\otimes n}f_{n}).

Sometimes
\Gamma(A)
is referred to as the
second quantization
of the operator
A
.

Remark 2
.

It is known (e.g.
[
25
, Theorem 2]
) that if we define
|\bullet|_{p}:=|A^{p}\bullet|_{0}
, the family of
p-seminorms

\left\{|\bullet|_{p},\;p\geq 0\right\}
are equivalent to the usual seminorms in the Schwartz space
\mathscr{S}(\mathbb{R})
, i.e. they generate the same topology.

Definition 3
.

Let
(S_{p})^{*}
be the completion of
L^{2}(\mu)
with respect to the norm

\|\bullet\|_{-p}:=\left\|\Gamma(A^{-p})\bullet\right\|_{2},\;p>0.

Then the space of
Hida distributions
is given by

\displaystyle(S)^{*}:=\bigcup_{p\geq 0}(S_{p})^{*}.

Any element
\Phi\in(S)^{*}
can be represented as the formal series:

\displaystyle\Phi=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}I_{n}(F_{n}),\quad F_{n}\in\mathscr{S}^{\prime}_{sym}(\mathbb{R}^{n}):=\operatorname{Sym}\mathscr{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R})^{\otimes n}

such that

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}n!|(A^{-p})^{\otimes n}F_{n}|_{0,n}^{2}<\infty.

In this setting, we can give a proper meaning to the
white noise

\partial_{x}W(x)

as a Hida distribution and in an slight abuse of notation, we will denote

Here,

We note that

Remark 4
.

The S-transform can be extended naturally to the space of Hida distributions by a duality argument, i.e. for
\Phi\in(S)^{*}
, we define the S-transform of
\Phi
as

\displaystyle S(\Phi)(\phi):=\llangle\Phi,\mathcal{E}(\phi)\rrangle,\quad\phi\in\mathscr{S}(\mathbb{R}),

where
\llangle\bullet,\bullet\rrangle
stands for the bilinear dual paring between the space of Hida distributions
(S)^{*}
and its dual space, denoted by
(S)
. Note that
(S)
is called the space of Hida test functions (see

for further details).

Definition 5
.

A function
F:\mathscr{S}(\mathbb{R})\to\mathbb{C}
is called a U-functional if

(1)

For every
\phi,\varphi\in\mathscr{S}(\mathbb{R})
the mapping
\mathbb{R}\ni\lambda\mapsto F(\lambda\phi+\varphi)\in\mathbb{C}
has an entire extension to
z\in\mathbb{C}
.

(2)

There are constants

such that

|F(\varphi)|\leq K_{1}\exp\left(K_{2}|\varphi|_{p}^{2}\right),\quad\varphi\in\mathscr{S}(\mathbb{R}).

We are now ready to introduce a characterization result for the space
(S)^{*}
.

Theorem 6
.

[
22
, theorem 1.2]

The S-transform defines a bijection between the space
(S)^{*}
and the space of U-functionals.

It is well-known that we cannot in general define the product between generalized functions, and this impossibility obviously extends also to generalized random variables. However, we are still able to define a particular type of
renormalized
product called
Wick product
and denoted by
\diamond
in the following way.

Definition 7
.

[
15
, Definition 8.11]

Let
\Phi,\Psi\in(S)^{*}
be two Hida distributions. We define the
Wick product
between the two elements, denoted by
\Phi\diamond\Psi
, to be the unique Hida distribution satisfying

\displaystyle S(\Phi\diamond\Psi)(\phi)=S(\Phi)(\phi)\cdot S(\Psi)(\phi)

for every
\phi\in\mathscr{S}(\mathbb{R})
.

Remark 8
.

The Hida space
(S)^{*}
is an algebra with respect to the
Wick product
.

Remark 9
.

We can also give an alternative characterization of the
Wick product
between generalized random variables in terms of chaos decompositions (e.g.
[
7
, Corollary 4.22]
), namely if

\Phi,\Psi\in(S)^{*}
are given by the formal series

\displaystyle\Phi=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}I_{n}(F_{n}),\quad\Psi=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}I_{n}(G_{n}),

then the Wick product between
\Phi,\Psi\in(S)^{*}
is defined by the following chaos decomposition:

\displaystyle\Phi\diamond\Psi=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}I_{n}(H_{n}),

where
H_{n}=\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{n}F_{n-j}\widehat{\otimes}G_{j}
, and
\widehat{\otimes}
denotes the symmetric tensor product.

One of the most striking properties of the
Wick product
is its relation with stochastic integration of Skorokhod-Itô type. In particular if
\{Y(x)\}_{x\in\mathbb{R}}
is a Skorokhod integrable process, then we have that

\int_{\mathbb{R}}Y(x)\delta W(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}Y(x)\diamond\dot{W}(x)dx

where the right hand side must be understood as a Pettis integral in
(S)^{*}
(see for instance
[
8
]
or
[
15
, Section 13.3]
) and the left hand side is a Skorokhod integral (see
[
19
]
). This is the reason why in (
1
) we introduce the
Wick product

\diamond
and say that the corresponding stochastic integral should be interpreted in the Skorokhod-Itô sense.

Elements of Malliavin calculus

For the purpose of this article, we will need a few definitions regarding Malliavin calculus. The interested reader is referred to
[

]
and
[

for a compressive exposition of Malliavin calculus and to
[

for the particular case in which the underlying probability space is the white noise probability space.

Let
\mathcal{S}
denote the class of smooth random variables

having the form

\displaystyle F=f(W_{h_{1}},\dots,W_{h_{n}}),

where
h_{1},\dots,h_{n}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
, and

belong to

which stands for the set of all infinitely continuously differentiable functions such that each function together with all its derivatives has a polynomial growth. We will refer to
\mathcal{S}
as the family of

.

Definition 10
.

The Malliavin derivative of a smooth Brownian functional

is the
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
-valued random variable given by

DF=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\partial_{i}f(W_{h_{1}},\dots,W_{h_{n}})h_{i}.

In the same way, we can define the
k
-th derivative of
F
for any
k\in\mathbb{N}
, which will be a
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})^{\otimes k}
-valued random variable.

Definition 11
.

Let
\mathcal{S}
be the space of smooth Brownian functionals, and define the following semi-norm on
\mathcal{S}
for
k\geq 1
and
p\geq 1
,

\displaystyle|F|_{k,p}:=\left[\mathbb{E}\left(|F|^{p}\right)+\sum_{j=1}^{k}\mathbb{E}\left(|D^{j}F|_{0,n}^{p}\right)\right]^{1/p}.

We will denote by
\mathbb{D}^{k,p}
the completion of the family
\mathcal{S}
with respect to the seminorm
|\bullet|_{k,p}
and for any
F\in\mathbb{D}^{k,p}
, we will let

D^{k}F=\lim_{n\to\infty}D^{k}F_{n}\quad\mbox{in $L^{p}\left(\mu;L^{2}(\mathbb{R})^{\otimes k}\right)$},

where
(F_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\mathcal{S}
is any sequence converging to
F
in
L^{p}(\mu)
.

Remark 12
.

There exists an extension of the Malliavin derivative to an element of the Hida distribution space
(S)^{*}
called the
Hida-Malliavin derivative
(see for instance
[
3
]
).

Furthermore, we will use the following notation

\displaystyle\mathbb{D}^{\infty,2}:=\bigcap_{k\geq 1}\mathbb{D}^{k,2},

and the following lemma (e.g.
[
27
]
).

Lemma 13
.

Let
F\in\mathbb{D}^{{\infty},2}
have the following chaos decomposition

F=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}I_{n}(f_{n}).

Then it holds that
f_{n}(\bullet)=\displaystyle\frac{1}{n!}\mathbb{E}[D_{\bullet}^{n}F]
.

Finally we introduce the following space of test functions that was introduced for the first time in
[
23
]
.

Definition 14
.

For any
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
, let
\mathcal{G}_{\lambda}
be the closure of
L^{2}(\mu)
with respect to the norm
\left\|\Gamma(e^{\lambda}I)\;\bullet\right\|_{2},

where
I
stands for the identity operator.
More explicitly,

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{\lambda}:=\bigg\{F=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}I_{n}(f_{n})\in L^{2}(\mu):\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}n!e^{2\lambda n}|f_{n}|_{0,n}^{2}<\infty\bigg\},

and now we set

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}:=\bigcap_{\lambda\in\mathbb{R}}\mathcal{G}_{\lambda}.

In particular, it is not hard to see the following inclusions:

\displaystyle(S)\subset\mathcal{G}\subset L^{2}(\mu).

We note that if

can be written as
\displaystyle\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{J}}F_{\alpha}\xi_{\alpha}
(the chaos expansion in terms of Hermite polynomials), then one can show that

if

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}e^{2\lambda n}\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{J}_{n}}|F_{\alpha}|^{2}<\infty.

2.3.
Hölder spaces and classical Hölder regularity results

In this subsection, we first give a definition of Hölder spaces on
G\subseteq\mathbb{R}
. For
0<\gamma<1
, we let

\displaystyle[f]_{\gamma}:=\sup_{z_{1}\neq z_{2}\in G}\frac{\left|f(z_{1})-f(z_{2})\right|}{|z_{1}-z_{2}|^{\gamma}}.

We say that
f
is Hölder continuous with Hölder exponent
\gamma

(or Hölder
\gamma
continuous) on
G
if

\sup_{z\in G}|f(z)|+[f]_{\gamma}<\infty.

The collection of Hölder
\gamma
continuous functions on
G
is
denoted by

\mathcal{C}^{\gamma}\left(G\right)

with the norm

[[f]]_{\gamma}:=\sup_{z\in G}|f(z)|+[f]_{\gamma}.

For
k\in\mathbb{N}
, we say that
f
is a
k
times continuously differentiable function on
G

if the
m
-th derivative of
f
, denoted by
\partial^{m}f
, exists and is continuous for all
m\leq k
.
The collection of
k
times continuously differentiable functions on
G
such that
\partial^{k}f\in\mathcal{C}^{\gamma}(G)
with
0<\gamma<1
, is denoted by
\mathcal{C}^{k+\gamma}(G)

with the norm

[[f]]_{k+\gamma}:=\sum_{1\leq m\leq k}\sup_{z\in G}|\partial^{m}f(z)|+[\partial^{k}f]_{\gamma}<\infty.

In a similar manner, we can define the Hölder spaces on
[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}
for
T>0
as follows. For
0<\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}<1
, we define

\displaystyle[f]_{\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}}:=\displaystyle\sup_{(t,x)\neq(s,x)\in[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}}\frac{\left|f(t,x)-f(s,x)\right|}{|t-s|^{\gamma_{1}}}+\displaystyle\sup_{(t,x)\neq(t,y)\in[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}}\frac{\left|f(t,x)-f(t,y)\right|}{|x-y|^{\gamma_{2}}}.

Then,
f
is said to be Hölder
(\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2})
continuous on
[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}
if
\displaystyle\sup_{(t,x)\in[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}}|f(t,x)|+[f]_{\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}}<\infty
, and
the collection of Hölder
(\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2})
continuous functions on
[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}
is denoted by

\mathcal{C}^{\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}}\left([0,T]\times\mathbb{R}\right)

with the norm

[[f]]_{\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}}:=\sup_{(t,x)\in[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}}|f(t,x)|+[f]_{\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}}.

Let
k_{1},k_{2}\in\mathbb{N}
and
0<\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}<1
. The Hölder space, denoted by
\mathcal{C}^{k_{1}+\gamma_{1},k_{2}+\gamma_{2}}([0,T]\times\mathbb{R})
, is defined by the collection of all functions on
([0,T]\times\mathbb{R})
such that
f
is
k_{1}
times continuously differentiable in
t
and
k_{2}
times continuously differentiable in
x
and the norm

[[f]]_{k_{1}+\gamma_{1},k_{2}+\gamma_{2}}:=\sum_{0\leq i\leq k_{1},\ 0\leq j\leq k_{2}}\sup_{(t,x)\in[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}}\left|\partial^{i}_{t}\partial^{j}_{x}f(t,x)\right|+\left[\partial^{k_{1}}_{t}\partial^{k_{2}}_{x}f\right]_{\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}}<\infty.

Here,
\partial_{t}:=\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}

\left(\partial_{x}:=\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\right)
represents the differentiation operator with respect to
t
(resp.
x
).

Next, we state useful regularity results for the classical solutions of standard homogeneous and inhomogeneous heat equations on
[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}
.

Recall the Gaussian heat kernel
p(t,x)=\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi t}}e^{-\frac{x^{2}}{2t}}
. Let us define

(Pf)(t,x):=\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}p(t,x-y)f(y)dy,\quad(P\star f)(t,x):=\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}}p(t-s,x-y)f(s,y)dyds.

Lemma 15
.

[
16
, Chapter IV, Section 2]

Let
T>0
,
0<\gamma\notin\mathbb{N}
, and
n,m\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
. Then,

(1)

If
2n+m=\rho
and
f\in\mathcal{C}^{\gamma}(\mathbb{R})
,

[[\partial_{t}^{n}\partial_{x}^{m}(Pf)]]_{\frac{\gamma-\rho}{2},\gamma-\rho}\leq C[[f]]_{\gamma}

for some constant
C>0
.

(2)

Let
\lfloor\cdot\rfloor
be the greatest integer function. If
\rho=\lfloor\gamma\rfloor
,
2n+m=\rho+2
, and
f\in\mathcal{C}^{\frac{\gamma}{2},\gamma}([0,T]\times\mathbb{R})
,

[[\partial_{t}^{n}\partial_{x}^{m}(P\star f)]]_{\frac{\gamma-\rho}{2},\gamma-\rho}\leq C[[f]]_{\frac{\gamma}{2},\gamma},

for some constant
C>0
.

3.
Direct comparisons among
u^{\mathtt{FK}}
,
u^{\mathtt{MK}}
and
u^{\mathtt{CS}}

Let
\left(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P}^{W}\right)=\left(\mathscr{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}),\mathcal{B},\mu\right)
be our main probability space and introduce an auxiliary one
\left(\widetilde{\Omega},\widetilde{\mathcal{F}},\mathbb{P}^{B}\right)
carrying a one-dimensional Brownian motion
\{B_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
. Furthermore, let
\mathbb{E}^{W}
(
\mathbb{E}^{B}
) denote the expectation with respect to
\mathbb{P}^{W}
(resp.
\mathbb{P}^{B}
).

Moreover, we suppose that
u_{0}\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
so that we have by uniqueness

u^{\mathtt{FK}}=u^{\mathtt{MW}}=u^{\mathtt{CS}}\in C([0,T]\times\mathbb{R};L^{2}(\Omega)).

The aim of this section is to give an alternative and more direct proof to show
u^{\mathtt{FK}}=u^{\mathtt{MW}}=u^{\mathtt{CS}}
. For the sake of simplicity and consistency, we will denote, for
(t,x)\in[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}
,

u_{(\mathbf{0})}(t,x):=\int_{\mathbb{R}}p(t,x-y)u_{0}(y)\;dy.

3.1.
u^{\mathtt{FK}}
and
u^{\mathtt{MW}}

We recall the multiple Wiener solution of (
1
):

\displaystyle u^{\mathtt{MW}}(t,x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}I_{n}\left(F_{n}^{\mathtt{MW}}(t,x)\right),

where

\displaystyle F_{0}^{\mathtt{MW}}(t,x)=u_{(\mathbf{0})}(t,x);

\displaystyle F_{n}^{\mathtt{MW}}(t,x;y_{1},\dots,y_{n})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n!}\int_{[0,t]^{n}}p\left(t-r_{\rho(n)},x-y_{\rho(n)}\right)\times\cdots\times p\left(r_{\rho(2)}-r_{\rho(1)},y_{\rho(2)}-y_{\rho(1)}\right)u_{(\mathbf{0})}(r_{\rho(1)},x_{\rho(1)})\;d\mathbf{r},\ n\geq 1,

and

\rho
denotes the permutation of
\{1,\dots,n\}
such that
0<r_{\rho(1)}<\cdots<r_{\rho(n)}<t
.

Moreover, the Feynman-Kac solution is given by

\displaystyle u^{\mathtt{FK}}(t,x)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}^{B}\left[u_{0}(B_{t}^{x})\exp\{\Psi_{t,x}\}\right],

where

\{B_{t}^{x}\}_{t\geq 0}:=\{B_{t}+x\}_{t\geq 0}
, for fixed
(t,x)\in[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}
, and

(11)

\displaystyle\Psi_{t,x}(\omega;\widetilde{\omega})=\int_{\mathbb{R}}L_{y}^{x}(t;\widetilde{\omega})dW(y;\omega)-\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}}|L_{y}^{x}(t;\widetilde{\omega})|^{2}dy,\quad\mbox{$\mathbb{P}^{W}\otimes\mathbb{P}^{B}$-almost surely},

where
L_{a}^{x}(t)
denotes the local time of
\{B_{s}^{x}\}_{s\geq 0}
at level
a
and time
t
. Note that the stochastic integral in (
11
) is well-defined since the function
y\mapsto L_{y}^{x}(t;\widetilde{\omega})
is square integrable for each fixed
(t,x,\widetilde{\omega})
. From now on, we will omit the explicit dependence on
(\omega,\widetilde{\omega})
unless there is a risk of confusion.
Furthermore, we notice that by definition
\exp\{\Psi_{t,x}\}=\mathcal{E}(L^{x}(t))
, i.e. the stochastic exponential (e.g.
[
8
]
) of the Brownian local time.

Using Lemma
13
and the fact that
D\mathcal{E}(L^{x}(t))=\mathcal{E}(L^{x}(t))L^{x}(t)
and
\mathbb{E}^{W}\left[\mathcal{E}(L^{x}(t))\right]=1
, we can rewrite
u^{\mathtt{FK}}
as

\displaystyle u^{\mathtt{FK}}(t,x)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}I_{n}\left(F_{n}^{\mathtt{FK}}(t,x)\right),

where

\displaystyle F_{0}^{\mathtt{FK}}(t,x)

\displaystyle=u_{(\mathbf{0})}(t,x);

\displaystyle F_{n}^{\mathtt{FK}}(t,x;y_{1},\dots,y_{n})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n!}\mathbb{E}^{B}\left[(L^{x}(t))^{\otimes n}(y_{1},\dots,y_{n})u_{0}(B_{t}^{x})\right],\ n\geq 1.

We will directly prove
u^{\mathtt{FK}}=u^{\mathtt{MW}}
by showing
F_{n}^{\mathtt{FK}}=F_{n}^{\mathtt{MW}}
for all
n\geq 0
.

For each
t\in[0,T]
and
x\in\mathbb{R}
, it is clear that
F_{0}^{\mathtt{FK}}(t,x)=F_{0}^{\mathtt{MW}}(t,x)
.
We now let
n\geq 1
. Then, by Fubini lemma,

\displaystyle F_{n}^{\mathtt{FK}}(t,x;y_{1},\dots,y_{n})=\frac{1}{n!}\int_{[0,t]^{n}}\mathbb{E}^{B}\left[\delta_{0}(B_{s_{1}}^{x}-y_{1})\cdots\delta_{0}(B_{s_{n}}^{x}-y_{n})u_{0}(B_{t}^{x})\right]\;d\mathbf{s},

where
\delta_{x}(y):=\displaystyle\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}(\pi\varepsilon)^{-1/2}e^{-|x-y|^{2}/\varepsilon}
,
x,y\in\mathbb{R}
as the Dirac-delta function in the sense of distribution.
Let
\sigma
be the permutation of
\{1,\dots,n\}
such that
0<s_{\sigma(1)}<\cdots<s_{\sigma(n)}<t
. Then, we have

\displaystyle F_{n}^{\mathtt{FK}}

\displaystyle(t,x;y_{1},\dots,y_{n})=\frac{1}{n!}\int_{[0,t]^{n}}\mathbb{E}^{B}\left[\delta_{0}(B_{s_{\sigma(1)}}^{x}-y_{\sigma(1)})\cdots\delta_{0}(B_{s_{\sigma(n)}}^{x}-y_{\sigma(n)})u_{0}(B_{t}^{x})\right]\;d\mathbf{s},

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n!}\int_{[0,t]^{n}}\mathbb{E}^{B}\left[\delta_{0}(B_{s_{\sigma(1)}}^{x}-y_{\sigma(1)})\cdots\delta_{0}(B_{s_{\sigma(n)}}^{x}-y_{\sigma(n)})\mathbb{E}^{B}\left[u_{0}(B_{t}^{x})|\mathcal{F}_{s_{\sigma(n)}}\right]\right]\;d\mathbf{s},

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n!}\int_{[0,t]^{n}}\mathbb{E}^{B}\left[\delta_{0}(B_{s_{\sigma(1)}}^{x}-y_{\sigma(1)})\cdots\delta_{0}(B_{s_{\sigma(n)}}^{x}-y_{\sigma(n)})u_{(\mathbf{0})}\left(t-s_{\sigma(n)},B_{\sigma(n)}^{x}\right)\right]\;d\mathbf{s}.

We know that for any
t\geq s
and
f\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\delta_{0}(B_{t}^{x}-y)f(B_{t}^{x})|\mathcal{F}_{s}\right]

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}p\left(t-s,B_{s}^{x}-z\right)f(z)\delta_{0}(z-y)dz=p\left(t-s,B_{s}^{x}-y\right)f(y).

If we use the identity iteratively, we can obtain

\displaystyle F_{n}^{\mathtt{FK}}(t,x;y_{1},\dots,y_{n})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n!}\int_{[0,t]^{n}}p\left(s_{\sigma(1)},y_{\sigma(1)}-x\right)\times\cdots

(12)

\displaystyle\times p\left(s_{\sigma(n)}-s_{\sigma(n-1)},y_{\sigma(n)}-y_{\sigma(n-1)}\right)u_{(\mathbf{0})}(t-s_{\sigma(n)},x_{\sigma(n)})\;d\mathbf{s}.

Let
r_{i}=t-s_{i}
for

and
\rho(1)=\sigma(n)
,
\rho(2)=\sigma(n-1)
,
\dots
,
\rho(n)=\sigma(1)
. Then, it is clear that

,
and we can rewrite (

) as

\displaystyle F_{n}^{\mathtt{FK}}(t,x;y_{1},\dots,y_{n})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n!}\int_{[0,t]^{n}}p\left(t-r_{\rho(n)},x-y_{\rho(n)}\right)\times\cdots

\displaystyle\times p\left(r_{\rho(2)}-r_{\rho(1)},y_{\rho(2)}-y_{\rho(1)}\right)u_{(\mathbf{0})}(r_{\rho(1)},x_{\rho(1)})\;d\mathbf{r}=F_{n}^{\mathtt{MW}}(t,x;y_{1},\dots,y_{n}),

and thus
u^{\mathtt{FK}}(t,x)=u^{\mathtt{MW}}(t,x)
.

3.2.
u^{\mathtt{FK}}
and
u^{\mathtt{CS}}

Let us now show
u^{\mathtt{FK}}=u^{\mathtt{CS}}
directly. Recall

\displaystyle u^{\mathtt{CS}}(t,x)=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{J}}u^{\mathtt{CS}}_{\alpha}(t,x)\xi_{\alpha},

where
u^{\mathtt{CS}}_{(\mathbf{0})}(t,x)=u_{(\mathbf{0})}(t,x)
, and for

,

\displaystyle u^{\mathtt{CS}}_{\alpha}(t,x)=\sqrt{n!}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}F_{n}^{\mathtt{CS}}(t,x;y_{1},\dots,y_{n})\mathfrak{e}_{\alpha}(y_{1},\dots,y_{n})\;d\mathbf{y},\quad\alpha\in\mathcal{J}_{n}=\{\alpha\in\mathcal{J}:|\alpha|=n\},

\displaystyle F_{n}^{\mathtt{CS}}(t,x;y_{1},\dots,y_{n})=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{n}_{[0,t]}}p(t-s_{n},x-y_{n})\cdots p(s_{2}-s_{1},y_{2}-y_{1})u_{(\mathbf{0})}(s_{1},y_{1})\;d\mathbf{s},

\mathbb{T}^{n}_{[0,t]}=\{0\leq s_{1}\leq\cdots\leq s_{n}\leq t\}
,
and
\{\mathfrak{e}_{\alpha},\alpha\in\mathcal{J}_{n}\}
is an orthonormal basis of
L^{2}_{sym}(\mathbb{R}^{n}):=
the symmetric part of
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
.
Specifically,

(13)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{e}_{\alpha}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n!\alpha!}}\sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal{P}_{n}}e_{k_{\sigma(1)}}(y_{1})\cdots e_{k_{\sigma(n)}}(y_{n}),

where
k_{\alpha}=(k_{1},\dots,k_{n})
be its characteristic vector for any

(e.g.
[
14
, Section 2]
).

We have
u^{\mathtt{CS}}_{(\mathbf{0})}(t,x)=u^{\mathtt{FK}}_{(\mathbf{0})}(t,x)
for
(t,x)\in[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}
.
For
n\geq 1
, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{J}_{n}}u^{\mathtt{CS}}_{\alpha}(t,x)\mathfrak{e}_{\alpha}=\sqrt{n!}\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{J}_{n}}\left\langle F^{\mathtt{CS}}_{n}(t,x),\mathfrak{e}_{\alpha}\right\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\mathfrak{e}_{\alpha}.

In fact, it is equal to the orthogonal projection of
F^{\mathtt{CS}}_{n}
on

, and thus

\displaystyle\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{J}_{n}}u^{\mathtt{CS}}_{\alpha}(t,x)\frac{\mathfrak{e}_{\alpha}}{\sqrt{n!}}=\operatorname{Sym}\left(F^{\mathtt{CS}}_{n}(t,x)\right)=:\widetilde{F^{\mathtt{CS}}_{n}(t,x)}.

Note that

\displaystyle\widetilde{F^{\mathtt{CS}}_{n}(t,x)}=\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal{P}_{n}}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{n}_{[0,t]}}p(t-s_{n},x-y_{\sigma(n)})\cdots p(s_{2}-s_{1},y_{\sigma(2)}-y_{\sigma(1)})u_{(\mathbf{0})}(s_{1},y_{\sigma(1)})\;d\mathbf{s}.

Now we take the

on both sides to get

\displaystyle\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{J}_{n}}u^{\mathtt{CS}}_{\alpha}(t,x)I_{n}\left(\frac{\mathfrak{e}_{\alpha}}{\sqrt{n!}}\right)=I_{n}\left(\widetilde{F^{\mathtt{CS}}_{n}(t,x)}\right).

There’s a result due to Itô (see for instance
[

, equation 2.2.29]
) stating that

(15)

\displaystyle I_{n}\left(\displaystyle\operatorname{Sym}\bigotimes_{j=1}^{\infty}e_{j}^{\otimes\alpha_{j}}\right)=\prod_{j=1}^{\infty}H_{\alpha_{j}}\left(I_{1}(e_{j})\right)\quad\mbox{and thus}\quad I_{n}\left(\frac{\mathfrak{e}_{\alpha}}{\sqrt{n!}}\right)=\xi_{\alpha}.

Plugging this into (
14
), we obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{J}_{n}}u^{\mathtt{CS}}_{\alpha}(t,x)\xi_{\alpha}=I_{n}\left(\widetilde{F^{\mathtt{CS}}_{n}(t,x)}\right),

and thus

\displaystyle u^{\mathtt{CS}}(t,x)=u^{\mathtt{CS}}_{(\mathbf{0})}(t,x)+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{J}_{n}}u^{\mathtt{CS}}_{\alpha}(t,x)\xi_{\alpha}=u^{\mathtt{CS}}_{(\mathbf{0})}(t,x)+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}I_{n}\left(\widetilde{F^{\mathtt{CS}}_{n}(t,x)}\right).

To have that
u^{\mathtt{CS}}=u^{\mathtt{FK}}
, it only remains to show that
\widetilde{F^{\mathtt{CS}}_{n}(t,x)}=F^{\mathtt{FK}}_{n}(t,x)
for
n\geq 1
. Indeed,

\displaystyle F^{\mathtt{FK}}_{n}(t,x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n!}\int_{[0,t]^{n}}\mathbb{E}^{B}\left[\delta(B_{s_{1}}^{x}-y_{1})\cdots\delta(B_{s_{n}}^{x}-y_{n})u_{0}(B_{t}^{x})\right]\;d\mathbf{s}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n!}\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal{P}_{n}}\int_{[0,t]^{n}}\mathbb{E}^{B}\left[\delta\left(B_{s_{1}}^{x}-y_{\sigma(1)}\right)\cdots\delta\left(B_{s_{n}}^{x}-y_{\sigma(n)}\right)u_{0}(B_{t}^{x})\right]\;d\mathbf{s}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal{P}_{n}}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{n}_{[0,t]}}\mathbb{E}^{B}\left[\delta_{y_{\sigma(1)}}\widehat{\otimes}\cdots\widehat{\otimes}\delta_{y_{\sigma(n)}}(B_{s_{1}}^{x},\dots,B_{s_{n}}^{x})u_{0}(B_{t}^{x})\right]\;d\mathbf{s}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal{P}_{n}}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{n}_{[0,t]}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left[\delta_{y_{\sigma(1)}}\widehat{\otimes}\cdots\widehat{\otimes}\delta_{y_{\sigma(n)}}\right](y_{1},\dots,y_{n})p(s_{n}-s_{n-1},y_{n}-y_{n-1})\times\cdots

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\times p(s_{2}-s_{1},y_{2}-y_{1})p(s_{1},y_{1}-x)u_{(\mathbf{0})}(t-s_{n},y_{n})\;d\mathbf{y}\;d\mathbf{s}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal{P}_{n}}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{n}_{[0,t]}}p(s_{n}-s_{n-1},y_{\sigma(n)}-y_{\sigma(n-1)})\cdots p(s_{1},y_{\sigma(1)}-x)u_{(\mathbf{0})}(t-s_{n},y_{\sigma(n)})\;d\mathbf{s}

\displaystyle=\widetilde{F^{\mathtt{CS}}_{n}(t,x)}\quad\mbox{after rearranging the variables.}

4.
Basic regularity of
u

We again assume that
u_{0}\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
so that
u=u^{\mathtt{FK}}=u^{\mathtt{MW}}=u^{\mathtt{CS}}
and we will denote
\|\bullet\|_{\infty}:=\|\bullet\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})}
In this section,
we will provide a few basic regularity of
u
using the Feynman-Kac representation.

Theorem 16
.

For every
(t,x)\in[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}
,

u(t,x)\in\mathcal{G}.

Proof.

We have for
\phi\in\mathscr{S}(\mathbb{R})
,

\displaystyle S(u(t,x))(\phi)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}^{W}\left[u(t,x)\mathcal{E}(\phi)\right]=\mathbb{E}^{W}\mathbb{E}^{B}\left[u_{0}(B_{t}^{x})\mathcal{E}(L^{x}(t))\mathcal{E}(\phi)\right]

(16)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}^{B}\left[u_{0}(B_{t}^{x})\exp\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}L^{x}_{y}(t)\phi(y)dy\right)\right],

where the last equality comes from Fubini Lemma and
[
15
, Theorem 5.13]
.

Let
P_{m}:\mathscr{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R})\to\mathscr{S}(\mathbb{R})
be the orthogonal projection of
\mathscr{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R})
on
\operatorname{span}\{e_{1},\dots,e_{m}\}
,
m\geq 1
.
Then for
\eta\in\mathscr{S}_{\mathbb{C}}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}):=\mathscr{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R})\oplus i\mathscr{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R})
and
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
, we have

\displaystyle S(u(t,x))(\lambda P_{m}\eta)=\mathbb{E}^{B}\left[u_{0}(B_{t}^{x})\exp\bigg\{\int_{\mathbb{R}}\lambda P_{m}\eta(y)L_{y}^{x}(t)dy\bigg\}\right].

Using the fact that for
\phi,\eta\in\mathscr{S}^{\prime}_{\mathbb{C}}(\mathbb{R})
it holds that
\langle\eta,P_{m}\phi\rangle=\langle\phi,P_{m}\eta\rangle
, we can write

\displaystyle S(u(t,x))(\lambda P_{m}\eta)=\mathbb{E}^{B}\left[u_{0}(B_{t}^{x})\exp\bigg\{\lambda\int_{\mathbb{R}}\eta(y)(P_{m}L^{x}(t))(y)dy\bigg\}\right]

and

\displaystyle|S(u(t,x))(\lambda P_{m}\eta)|^{2}=\left|\mathbb{E}^{B}\left[u_{0}(B_{t}^{x})\exp\bigg\{\lambda\int_{\mathbb{R}}(\eta_{1}(y)+i\eta_{2}(y))(P_{m}L^{x}(t))(y)dy\bigg\}\right]\right|^{2}.

By Jensen’s inequality, we have

\displaystyle|S(u(t,x))(\lambda P_{m}\eta)|^{2}\leq\|u_{0}\|_{\infty}^{2}\mathbb{E}^{B}\left[\left|\exp\bigg\{\lambda\int_{\mathbb{R}}(\eta_{1}(y)+i\eta_{2}(y))(P_{m}L^{x}(t))(y)dy\bigg\}\right|^{2}\right].

Since
|z_{1}\cdot z_{2}|=|z_{1}|\cdot|z_{2}|
for any
z_{1},z_{2}\in\mathbb{C}
,

\displaystyle|S(u(t,x))(\lambda P_{m}\eta)|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\|u_{0}\|_{\infty}^{2}\mathbb{E}^{B}\left[\left|\exp\bigg\{\lambda\int_{\mathbb{R}}\eta_{1}(y)(P_{m}L^{x}(t))(y)dy\bigg\}\right|^{2}\left|\exp\bigg\{i\lambda\int_{\mathbb{R}}\eta_{2}(y)(P_{m}L^{x}(t))(y)dy\bigg\}\right|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\|u_{0}\|_{\infty}^{2}\mathbb{E}^{B}\left[\exp\bigg\{2\lambda\langle\eta_{1},P_{m}L^{x}(t)\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}\bigg\}\right].

Thus,

\displaystyle\int_{\mathscr{S}_{\mathbb{C}}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R})}|S(u(t,x))(\lambda P_{m}\eta)|^{2}\nu(d\eta)\leq\|u_{0}\|_{\infty}^{2}\mathbb{E}^{B}\left[\int_{\mathscr{S}_{\mathbb{C}}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R})}\exp\bigg\{2\lambda\langle\eta_{1},P_{m}L^{x}_{\bullet}(t)\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}\bigg\}\nu(d\eta)\right],

where the measure
\nu
is given by the product measure
\mu_{\frac{1}{2}}\otimes\mu_{\frac{1}{2}}
, where
\mu_{\frac{1}{2}}
is the measure on
(\Omega,\mathcal{B})
with the characteristic function given by:

\displaystyle\int_{\mathscr{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R})}e^{i\langle\omega,\varphi\rangle}d\mu(\omega)=e^{-\frac{1}{4}|\varphi|_{0}^{2}},\quad\quad\varphi\in\mathscr{S}(\mathbb{R}).

It is clear that for any
\varphi\in\mathscr{S}(\mathbb{R})
,
\mu_{\frac{1}{2}}\circ\left\langle\bullet,\frac{\varphi}{|\varphi|_{0}}\right\rangle^{-1}
is a centered Gaussian measure with variance
1/2
as in
[
8
, Lemma 2.1.2]
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\int_{\mathscr{S}_{\mathbb{C}}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R})}|S(u(t,x))(\lambda P_{m}\eta)|^{2}\nu(d\eta)

\displaystyle\leq\|u_{0}\|_{\infty}^{2}\mathbb{E}^{B}\left[\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{2\lambda y|P_{m}L^{x}(t)|_{0}}e^{-y^{2}}dy\right]

\displaystyle=\|u_{0}\|_{\infty}^{2}\mathbb{E}^{B}\left[e^{\lambda^{2}|P_{m}L^{x}(t)|^{2}_{0}}\right].

Finally, we obtain (by
[
4
, page 178]
)

\displaystyle\lim_{m\to\infty}\int_{\mathscr{S}_{\mathbb{C}}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R})}|S(u(t,x))(\lambda P_{m}\eta)|^{2}\nu(d\eta)\leq\|u_{0}\|_{\infty}^{2}\mathbb{E}\left[e^{\lambda^{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}}|L_{y}^{x}(t)|^{2}dy}\right]<\infty,\;\forall\lambda\in\mathbb{R},

which implies by
[
5
, Corollary 5.1]
,
u(t,x)
belongs to
\mathcal{G}
.
∎

Next, we state the basic Hölder regularity of
u
both in time and space.

Theorem 17
.

Let
0<\varepsilon<1/2
be arbitrary and
C
be a constant.

(i)

Assume that
u_{0}\equiv C
. Then,

u\in\mathcal{C}^{3/4-\varepsilon,1/2-\varepsilon}([0,T]\times\mathbb{R}).

(ii)

Assume that
u_{0}\not\equiv C
and
u_{0}\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
is (globally) Lipschitz continuous on
\mathbb{R}
.
Then,

u\in\mathcal{C}^{1/2-\varepsilon,1/2-\varepsilon}([0,T]\times\mathbb{R}).

Proof.

Let
\vvvert\bullet\vvvert_{p}:=\left(\mathbb{E}^{W}\mathbb{E}^{B}|\bullet|^{p}\right)^{1/p}
be the norm on the Banach space
L^{p}(\mathbb{P}^{W}\otimes\mathbb{P}^{B})
for
p\geq 1
. From
[
26
]
, we have

(17)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\sup_{(t,x)\in[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}}\vvvert\mbox{exp}\left\{\Psi_{t,x}\right\}\vvvert_{p}<\infty.

Also, (
11
) conditional on
B
, becomes

(18)

\displaystyle\Psi_{t,x}\sim N\left(-\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}}|L_{a}(t)|^{2}da,\int_{\mathbb{R}}|L_{a}(t)|^{2}da\right),

where
N\left(\mu,\sigma^{2}\right)
denotes the Gaussian random variable with mean
\mu
and variance
\sigma^{2}
.

(i) Let
u_{0}
be a constant. Then,
using the fact
|e^{x}-e^{y}|\leq(e^{x}+e^{y})|x-y|
for all
x,y\in\mathbb{R}
, Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, Minkowski inequality, (
17
), and (
18
), we can obtain for
p\geq 2
.

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}^{W}\left[|u(t,x)-u(s,y)|^{p}\right]

\displaystyle\leq c_{p}\bigg\{\mathbb{E}^{B}\mathbb{E}^{W}\left[|\Psi_{t,x}-\Psi_{s,y}|^{2}\right]^{1/2}\bigg\}^{p}=c_{p}\vvvert\Psi_{t,x}-\Psi_{s,y}\vvvert_{2}^{p},

for some
c_{p}>0
. Also, the triangular inequality implies

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}^{W}\left[|u(t,x)-u(s,y)|^{p}\right]\leq c_{p}\bigg\{\vvvert\Psi_{t,x}-\Psi_{t,y}\vvvert_{2}+\vvvert\Psi_{t,y}-\Psi_{s,y}\vvvert_{2}\bigg\}^{p}=:c_{p}\left(A_{1}^{1/2}+A_{2}^{1/2}\right)^{p}.

Let us now work with

\displaystyle A_{1}=\vvvert\Psi_{t,x}-\Psi_{t,y}\vvvert_{2}^{2}=\mathbb{E}^{B}\mathbb{E}^{W}\left[\Psi_{t,x}^{2}-2\Psi_{t,x}\Psi_{t,y}+\Psi_{t,y}^{2}\right].

By (
18
), we have

\displaystyle A_{1}=\mathbb{E}^{B}\left[2\int_{\mathbb{R}}|L_{a}(t)|^{2}da+\frac{1}{2}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}|L_{a}(t)|^{2}da\right)^{2}-2\mathbb{E}^{W}[\Psi_{t,x}\Psi_{t,y}]\right].

Recall the Dirac-delta function
\delta_{x}(y)=\displaystyle\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}(\pi\varepsilon)^{-1/2}e^{-|x-y|^{2}/\varepsilon}
,
x,y\in\mathbb{R}
.
Since

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}^{B}\mathbb{E}^{W}\left[\Psi_{t,x}\Psi_{t,y}\right]=\mathbb{E}^{B}\left[\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{t}\delta_{0}(B_{u}-B_{r}-(x-y))dudr+\frac{1}{4}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}|L_{a}(t)|^{2}da\right)^{2}\right],

we get

\displaystyle A_{1}=\mathbb{E}^{B}\left[2\int_{\mathbb{R}}|L_{a}(t)|^{2}da-2\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{t}\delta_{0}(B_{u}-B_{r}-(x-y))dudr\right].

The next step is done rigorously (See
[
10
]
for instance) by the translation invariant property of the Lebesgue measure:

\displaystyle A_{1}=\mathbb{E}^{B}\left[\int_{\mathbb{R}}|L_{a-x}(t)|^{2}da-2\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{t}\delta_{0}(B_{u}-B_{r}-(x-y))dudr+\int_{\mathbb{R}}|L_{a-y}(t)|^{2}da\right],

and this yields, by
[
10
, Proposition 9.2]
,

\displaystyle A_{1}=\mathbb{E}^{B}\left[\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left|L_{a-x}(t)-L_{a-y}(t)\right|^{2}da\right]=4t|x-y|+O(|x-y|^{2}),

which implies
u(t,\bullet)
is almost Hölder
1/2
continuous uniformly for all
t\in[0,T]
.

On the other hand, let us compute, for
0\leq s\leq t\leq T
,

\displaystyle A_{2}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}^{B}\mathbb{E}^{W}\left[\Psi_{t,y}^{2}-2\Psi_{t,y}\Psi_{s,y}+\Psi_{s,y}^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}^{B}\bigg[\int_{\mathbb{R}}|L_{a}(t)|^{2}da+\frac{1}{4}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}|L_{a}(t)|^{2}da\right)^{2}+\int_{\mathbb{R}}|L_{a}(s)|^{2}da+\frac{1}{4}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}|L_{a}(s)|^{2}da\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\quad-2\mathbb{E}^{W}\left[\Psi_{t,y}\Psi_{s,y}\right]\bigg].

Since

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}^{B}\mathbb{E}^{W}\left[\Psi_{t,y}\Psi_{s,y}\right]=\mathbb{E}^{B}\left[\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{s}\delta_{0}(B_{u}-B_{r})dudr+\frac{1}{4}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}|L_{a}(t)|^{2}da\right)\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}|L_{a}(s)|^{2}da\right)\right],

we have

\displaystyle A_{2}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}^{B}\left[\int_{\mathbb{R}}|L_{a}(t)|^{2}da-2\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{s}\delta_{0}(B_{r}-B_{z})drdz+\int_{\mathbb{R}}|L_{a}(s)|^{2}da\right]

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{4}\mathbb{E}^{B}\left[\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}|L_{a}(t)|^{2}da\right)^{2}-2\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}|L_{a}(t)|^{2}da\right)\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}|L_{a}(s)|^{2}da\right)+\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}|L_{a}(s)|^{2}da\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}^{B}\left[\int_{\mathbb{R}}|L_{a}(t)|^{2}da-2\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{s}\delta_{0}(B_{r}-B_{z})drdz+\int_{\mathbb{R}}|L_{a}(s)|^{2}da\right]

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{4}\mathbb{E}^{B}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left(|L_{a}(t)|^{2}-|L_{a}(s)|^{2}\right)da\right)^{2}.

We note that

\mathbb{E}^{B}\left[\int_{\mathbb{R}}|L_{a}(t)|^{2}da\right]=\mathbb{E}^{B}\left[\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{t}\delta_{0}(B_{r}-B_{z})drdz\right],

which implies

\displaystyle A_{2}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}^{B}\left[\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{t}\delta_{0}(B_{r}-B_{z})drdz-2\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{s}\delta_{0}(B_{r}-B_{z})drdz+\int_{0}^{s}\int_{0}^{s}\delta_{0}(B_{r}-B_{z})drdz\right]

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{4}\mathbb{E}^{B}\left(\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{t}\delta_{0}(B_{r}-B_{z})drdz-\int_{0}^{s}\int_{0}^{s}\delta_{0}(B_{r}-B_{z})drdz\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}^{B}\left[\int_{s}^{t}\int_{s}^{t}\delta_{0}(B_{r}-B_{z})drdz\right]+\frac{1}{4}\mathbb{E}^{B}\left(\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{t}\delta_{0}(B_{r}-B_{z})drdz-\int_{0}^{s}\int_{0}^{s}\delta_{0}(B_{r}-B_{z})drdz\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=:A_{3}+A_{4}.

We can easily compute
A_{3}
:

\displaystyle A_{3}

\displaystyle=\int_{s}^{t}\int_{s}^{t}(2\pi|r-z|)^{-1/2}drdz=C(t-s)^{3/2}\quad\mbox{for some $C>0$ independent of $x$}.

For
A_{4}
, we have

\displaystyle 4A_{4}

\displaystyle=\int_{s}^{t}\int_{s}^{t}\int_{s}^{t}\int_{s}^{t}\mathbb{E}^{B}\left(\delta_{0}(B_{z}-B_{r})\delta_{0}(B_{q}-B_{p})\right)dpdqdrdz

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{symmetry}}}{{=}}4!\int_{s}^{t}\int_{s}^{z}\int_{s}^{r}\int_{s}^{q}\mathbb{E}^{B}\left[\delta_{0}(B_{z}-B_{r})\right]\mathbb{E}^{B}\left[\delta_{0}(B_{q}-B_{p})\right]dpdqdrdz

\displaystyle=4!\int_{s}^{t}\int_{s}^{z}\int_{s}^{r}\int_{s}^{q}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi(z-r)}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi(q-p)}}dpdqdrdz

\displaystyle\leq 4!\int_{s}^{t}\int_{s}^{z}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi(z-r)}}\left(\int_{s}^{t}\int_{s}^{q}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi(q-p)}}\right)dpdqdrdz

\displaystyle=C(t-s)^{3}\quad\mbox{for some $C>0$ independent of $x$}.

Combining all together, we obtain

A_{2}\leq C|t-s|^{3/2},

which implies
u(\bullet,x)
is almost Hölder
3/4
continuous uniformly for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
.

(ii) If
u_{0}
is not a constant function on
\mathbb{R}
, then we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}^{W}\left[|u(t,x)-u(s,y)|^{p}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}^{W}\left|\mathbb{E}^{B}\left(u_{0}(x+B_{t})\mbox{exp}\left(\Psi_{t,x}\right)\right)-\mathbb{E}^{B}\left(u_{0}\left(y+B_{s}\right)\mbox{exp}(\Psi_{s,y})\right)\right|^{p}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}^{W}\Big|\mathbb{E}^{B}\left(\left(u_{0}(x+B_{t})-u_{0}(y+B_{s})\right)\mbox{exp}(\Psi_{t,x})\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\mathbb{E}^{B}\left(u_{0}(y+B_{s})\left(\mbox{exp}(\Psi_{t,x})-\mbox{exp}(\Psi_{s,y})\right)\right)\Big|^{p}.

Since
|f+g|^{p}\leq 2^{p-1}\left(|f|^{p}+|g|^{p}\right)
for
p\geq 1
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}^{W}\left[|u(t,x)-u(s,y)|^{p}\right]

\displaystyle\leq 2^{p-1}\bigg(\mathbb{E}^{W}\left|\mathbb{E}^{B}\left(\left(u_{0}\left(x+B_{t}\right)-u_{0}\left(y+B_{s}\right)\right)\mbox{exp}(\Psi_{t,x})\right)\right|^{p}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\mathbb{E}^{W}\left|\mathbb{E}^{B}\left(u_{0}\left(y+B_{s}\right)\left(\mbox{exp}(\Psi_{t,x})-\mbox{exp}(\Psi_{s,y})\right)\right)\right|^{p}\bigg)

\displaystyle=:2^{p-1}\left(\bar{A}_{1}+\bar{A}_{2}\right).

For
\bar{A}_{1}
, by Cauchy-Schwarz inequality,

\displaystyle\bar{A}_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\left(\mathbb{E}^{B}\left(u_{0}(x+B_{t})-u_{0}(y+B_{s})\right)^{2}\right)^{p/2}\cdot\mathbb{E}^{W}\left(\mathbb{E}^{B}\left(\mbox{exp}\left(2\Psi_{t,x}\right)\right)\right)^{p/2}.

Since
u_{0}
is Lipschitz continuous on
\mathbb{R}
, we have

\displaystyle\bar{A}_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\left(|t-s|+(x-y)^{2}\right)^{p/2}\cdot\mathbb{E}^{W}\left(\mathbb{E}^{B}\left(\mbox{exp}\left(2\Psi_{t,x}\right)\right)\right)^{p/2}

\displaystyle\leq\left(|t-s|^{1/2}+|x-y|\right)^{p}\cdot\mathbb{E}^{W}\left(\mathbb{E}^{B}\left(\mbox{exp}\left(2\Psi_{t,x}\right)\right)\right)^{p/2}.

By Minkowski inequality, Hölder inequality for
p\geq 2
, and (
17
), we also have

\displaystyle\bar{A}_{1}\leq\left(|t-s|^{1/2}+|x-y|\right)^{p}\cdot\mathbb{E}^{W}\mathbb{E}^{B}\left(\mbox{exp}\left(p\Psi_{t,x}\right)\right)<\infty.

For
\bar{A}_{2}
, since
u_{0}\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
, we can apply the same argument in (i). As a result, we can say that
u
is Hölder continuous almost
1/2
both in time and space.
∎

As we argued in the introduction, one expects that we can still improve the spatial regularity of
u
. We will derive our desired result in Section

.

5.
The spatial derivative of
u

As we anticipated in the introduction, we expect that
u(t,\bullet)\in C^{3/2-\varepsilon}(\mathbb{R})
for any small
\varepsilon>0

using the Feynman-Kac representation and then find its chaos expansion to see if it is well-defined in

Let us start with a useful Lemma. The following result will serve as a key idea for finding
\partial_{x}u(t,x)

Lemma 18
.

For fixed
(t,x)
the map
\widetilde{\omega}\ni\widetilde{\Omega}\mapsto\widetilde{\Phi}_{t,x}(\widetilde{\omega})\in(S)^{*}
given by

\displaystyle\widetilde{\Phi}_{t,x}(\widetilde{\omega})=\mathcal{E}(L^{x}(t;\widetilde{\omega}))\diamond\left[u_{0}^{\prime}(B_{t}^{x}(\widetilde{\omega}))+u_{0}(B_{t}^{x})I_{1}\left(\partial_{x}L^{x}(t;\widetilde{\omega})\right)\right],

is Bochner integrable in
(S)^{*}
. Here,
\partial_{x}L^{x}(t)\in\mathscr{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R})
denotes the pathwise distributional derivative of the local time of
\{B_{t}^{x}\}_{t\geq 0}
.

Proof.

This immediately follows from
[
7
, Theorem 4.51]
and the facts

(1)

S\left(\widetilde{\Phi}_{t,x}(\bullet)\right)(\phi)=\exp\left(\int_{0}^{t}\phi(B_{s}^{x}(\bullet))ds\right)\times\left[u_{0}^{\prime}(B_{t}^{x}(\bullet))+u_{0}(B_{t}^{x})\int_{0}^{t}\phi^{\prime}(B_{s}^{x}(\bullet))ds\right]
is measurable for any
\phi\in\mathscr{S}(\mathbb{R})
.

(2)

\left|S\left(\widetilde{\Phi}_{t,x}(\widetilde{\omega})\right)(\phi)\right|\leq\left(\|u_{0}^{\prime}\|_{\infty}+t\|u_{0}\|_{\infty}\|\phi^{\prime}\|_{\infty}\right)e^{t\|\phi\|_{\infty}}\leq e^{\|u_{0}^{\prime}\|_{\infty}+t(\|u_{0}\|_{\infty}\|\phi^{\prime}\|_{\infty}+\|\phi\|_{\infty})}\leq K_{1}e^{K_{2}|\phi|_{p}^{2}}

for some
p,K_{1},K_{2}\geq 0
. The last inequality comes from
the equivalence between standard seminorms in the Schwartz space and the system of
p
-
seminorms
.

∎

Recall the Feynman-Kac representation of
u
:

\displaystyle u(t,x)=\mathbb{E}^{B}\left[u_{0}(B_{t}^{x})\mathcal{E}(L^{x}(t))\right]

and recall
C^{1}_{b}(\mathbb{R})
is the space of all bounded differentiable functions on
\mathbb{R}
with bounded continuous derivative.

Theorem 19
.

Assume that
u_{0}\in C^{1}_{b}(\mathbb{R})
. Then,
for each
t>0
,
u(t,\bullet)
is weakly continuously differentiable in
(S)^{*}
, and

\displaystyle\partial_{x}u(t,x)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}^{B}\left[\mathcal{E}(L^{x}(t))\diamond\bigg\{u_{0}^{\prime}(B_{t}^{x})+u_{0}(B_{t}^{x})I_{1}\left(\partial_{x}L^{x}(t)\right)\bigg\}\right],

where
\partial_{x}L^{x}(t)\in\mathscr{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R})
is the pathwise distributional derivative of
L^{x}(t)
, and
\mathbb{E}^{B}
must be understood as a Bochner integral in
(S)^{*}
.

Proof.

To find
\partial_{x}u(t,x)
, we start by computing the S-transform of
u
. From (
4
), for
\phi\in\mathscr{S}(\mathbb{R})
, we have

(19)

\displaystyle S\left(u(t,x)\right)(\phi)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}^{B}\left[u_{0}(B_{t}^{x})\exp\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}L^{x}_{y}(t)\phi(y)dy\right)\right]=\mathbb{E}^{B}\left[u_{0}(B_{t}^{x})\exp\left(\int_{0}^{t}\phi(B_{s}^{x})ds\right)\right],

where the last equality follows by the occupation time formula.

It is clear that
x\in\mathbb{R}\mapsto S(u(t,x))(\phi)
is continuous for all
\phi\in\mathscr{S}(\mathbb{R})
, and
\left|S\left(u(t,x)\right)(\phi)\right|\leq K_{1}e^{K_{2}|\phi|_{p}^{2}}
for some
K_{1},K_{2},p>0
.
Then, by
[
18
, Lemma A.1.2]
, we can see that
u(t,x)=S^{-1}(S(u(t,x)))
is
weakly
continuous in
(S)^{*}
(with respect to the
x
variable).

In order to prove that
u(t,\bullet)
is
weakly
continuously differentiable in
(S)^{*}
we must first take the spatial derivative on both sides of (
19
). Using the fact
\phi,\phi^{\prime}\in\mathscr{S}(\mathbb{R})\subset L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
, by dominated convergence theorem (DCT), we obtain

\displaystyle\partial_{x}S(u(t,x))(\phi)=\mathbb{E}^{B}\left[u_{0}^{\prime}(B_{t}^{x})\exp\left(\int_{0}^{t}\phi(B_{s}^{x})ds\right)+u_{0}(B_{t}^{x})\exp\left(\int_{0}^{t}\phi(B_{s}^{x})ds\right)\times\int_{0}^{t}\phi^{\prime}(B_{s}^{x})ds\right],

and it is clear that the map
x\mapsto\partial_{x}S(u(t,x))(\phi)
is continuous for all
\phi\in\mathscr{S}(\mathbb{R})
.

We also need to show
\partial_{x}S\left(u(t,x)\right)
is a U-functional (see Definition
5
).
By direct computation, we can verify that, as in the proof of Lemma
18
,

\displaystyle\left|\partial_{x}S\left(u(t,x)\right)(\phi)\right|\leq K_{1}e^{K_{2}|\phi|_{p}^{2}},

where
K_{1},K_{2},p
are positive real constants.
Also, it is clear that the map
z\mapsto\partial_{x}S(u)(z\phi+\eta)
is entire for any
\phi,\eta\in\mathscr{S}(\mathbb{R})
and
z\in\mathbb{C}
. Hence,
\partial_{x}S\left(u(t,x)\right)
is indeed a U-functional, and thus there exists a unique
\Phi\in(S)^{*}
such that
\partial_{x}S(u(t,x))=S(\Phi)
; then from
[
18
, Lemma A.3]
, we can conclude that
u(t,\bullet)
is
weakly
continuously differentiable in the Hida distribution space
(S)^{*}
.
Following the aforementioned reference, the
weak
spatial derivative
\partial_{x}u
of
u
is defined as the unique element in
(S)^{*}
such that

\displaystyle S(\partial_{x}u(t,x))(\phi)=\partial_{x}S(u(t,x))(\phi).

Using Lemma
18
, we can see that
\partial_{x}S\left(u(t,x)\right)=\mathbb{E}^{B}\left(S\left(\widetilde{\Phi}_{t,x}\right)\right)
, and furthermore we have that
\mathbb{E}^{B}\left(S\left(\widetilde{\Phi}_{t,x}\right)\right)=S\left(\mathbb{E}^{B}\left(\widetilde{\Phi}_{t,x}\right)\right)
since the Bochner integral
\mathbb{E}^{B}
and the S-transform can be interchanged (e.g.
[
7
, Theorem 4.51]
).
Finally, by Theorem
6
, we can conclude

\displaystyle\partial_{x}u(t,x)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}^{B}\left[\mathcal{E}(L^{x}(t))\diamond\bigg\{u_{0}^{\prime}(B_{t}^{x})+u_{0}(B_{t}^{x})I_{1}\left(\partial_{x}L^{x}(t)\right)\bigg\}\right],

where
\mathbb{E}^{B}
must be understood as a Bochner integral in
(S)^{*}
.
∎

From this result, we can only say that
\partial_{x}u(t,x)\in(S)^{*}
for each
(t,x)\in[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}
. But, in the following subsection, we will show that
\partial_{x}u(t,x)\in\mathcal{G}
using its chaos decomposition, and furthermore, we will investigate its Hölder regularity.

5.1.
Chaos decomposition for
\partial_{x}u

Let us find the chaos expansion of

\displaystyle\partial_{x}u(t,x)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}^{B}\left[\mathcal{E}(L^{x}(t))\diamond\bigg\{I_{0}(u_{0}^{\prime}(B_{t}^{x}))+u_{0}(B_{t}^{x})I_{1}\left(\partial_{x}L^{x}(t)\right)\bigg\}\right],

and notice that in this context, the Wiener integral of order
0
equals the identity operator, but nonetheless we explicitly write
I_{0}
for notational convenience.

By Lemma
13
, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(L^{x}(t))=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n!}I_{n}\left(L^{x}(t)^{\otimes n}\right),\quad\mbox{convergent in $L^{2}\left(\mathbb{P}^{W}\right)$,}

and by the definition of
Wick product
, we see that

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(L^{x}(t))\diamond\big\{I_{0}(u_{0}^{\prime}(B_{t}^{x}))+u_{0}(B_{t}^{x})I_{1}\left(\partial_{x}L^{x}(t)\right)\big\}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}I_{n}(h_{n}(t,x)),\quad\mbox{convergent in $(S)^{*}$,}

where
h_{0}(t,x)=u_{0}^{\prime}\left(B_{t}^{x}\right)
, and

\displaystyle\mathscr{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\ni h_{n}(t,x;\bullet)=u_{0}(B_{t}^{x})\operatorname{Sym}\left[\left(\frac{L^{x}(t)^{\otimes(n-1)}}{(n-1)!}\right)\otimes\partial_{x}L^{x}(t)\right](\bullet)+u_{0}^{\prime}(B_{t}^{x})\left(\frac{L^{x}(t)^{\otimes n}}{n!}\right)(\bullet),\ n\geq 1.

It is known that (e.g.
[
15
, Chapter 13.3]
) if
\Psi(u)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}I_{n}(F_{n}(u))
is Bochner integrable on
(M,\sigma(M),m)
, then
F_{n}
is Bochner integrable on
(M,\sigma(M),m)
, and it holds that

\int_{M}\Psi(u)m(du)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}I_{n}\left(\int_{M}F_{n}(u)m(du)\right).

In our case, letting
(M,\sigma(M),m)=\left(\widetilde{\Omega},\widetilde{\mathcal{F}},\mathbb{P}^{B}\right)
, this would imply that

\displaystyle\partial_{x}u(t,x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}I_{n}\left(\mathbb{E}^{B}\left[h_{n}(t,x)\right]\right),

where
\mathbb{E}^{B}
should be understood as a Bochner integral in
\mathscr{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
.

We can easily check that the first term of
\partial_{x}u(t,x)
is
\mathbb{E}^{B}\left[u_{0}^{\prime}\left(B_{t}^{x}\right)\right]=\partial_{x}u_{(\mathbf{0})}(t,x)
. Let’s check the general
n
-th terms of
\partial_{x}u(t,x)
for
n\geq 1
.
Since
h_{n}(t,x;\bullet)
is a symmetric element of
\mathscr{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, we can expand it with respect to
\{\mathfrak{e}_{\alpha}:\alpha\in\mathcal{J}_{n}\}
as

(20)

\displaystyle h_{n}(t,x;\bullet)=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{J}_{n}}\left\langle h_{n}(t,x),\mathfrak{e}_{\alpha}\right\rangle_{n}\mathfrak{e}_{\alpha}(\bullet),\quad\mbox{convergence in $\mathscr{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{n})$},

where
\langle\bullet,\bullet\rangle_{n}
is the bilinear product between
\mathscr{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and
\mathscr{S}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, and

\mathfrak{e}_{\alpha}=\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{n!\alpha!}}\sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal{P}_{n}}e_{k_{\sigma(1)}(y_{1})}\cdots e_{k_{\sigma(n)}(y_{n})}

as defined in (
13
).

It is clear by direct calculations that
\langle\operatorname{Sym}f,\operatorname{Sym}g\rangle_{n}=\langle f,\operatorname{Sym^{2}}g\rangle_{n}=\langle f,\operatorname{Sym}g\rangle_{n}.

Then, we have

\displaystyle\left\langle h_{n}(t,x),\mathfrak{e}_{\alpha}\right\rangle_{n}

\displaystyle=\frac{u_{0}(B_{t}^{x})}{\sqrt{n!\alpha!}(n-1)!}\left\langle\left[{L^{x}(t)^{\otimes(n-1)}}\otimes\;\partial_{x}L^{x}(t)\right],\sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal{P}_{n}}\left[e_{k_{\sigma(1)}}\otimes\cdots{\otimes}e_{k_{\sigma(n)}}\right]\;\right\rangle_{n}

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{u_{0}^{\prime}(B_{t}^{x})}{\sqrt{n!\alpha!}n!}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}{L^{x}(t)^{\otimes(n)}}(y_{1},...,y_{n})\times\sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal{P}_{n}}\left[e_{k_{\sigma(1)}}\otimes\cdots{\otimes}e_{k_{\sigma(n)}}\right](y_{1},...,y_{n})d\mathbf{y}

\displaystyle=\frac{u_{0}(B_{t}^{x})}{\sqrt{n!\alpha!}(n-1)!}\sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal{P}_{n}}\int_{[0,t]^{n}}e_{k_{\sigma(1)}}\otimes\cdots{\otimes}e_{k_{\sigma(n)}}^{\prime}(B_{s_{1}}^{x},\dots,B_{s_{n}}^{x})\;d\mathbf{s}

(21)

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{u_{0}^{\prime}(B_{t}^{x})}{\sqrt{n!\alpha!}n!}\sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal{P}_{n}}\int_{[0,t]^{n}}e_{k_{\sigma(1)}}\otimes\cdots{\otimes}e_{k_{\sigma(n)}}(B_{s_{1}}^{x},\dots,B_{s_{n}}^{x})\;d\mathbf{s},

where in the last expression we used the occupation time formula and the fact that
-\partial_{x}L^{x}
equals distributional derivative of the Brownian local time.

Also, taking
\mathbb{E}^{B}
on both sides of (
20
), we have

(22)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}^{B}[h_{n}(t,x;\bullet)]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}^{B}\left[\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{J}_{n}}\left\langle h_{n}(t,x),\mathfrak{e}_{\alpha}\right\rangle_{n}\mathfrak{e}_{\alpha}(\bullet)\right].

At this point, we need the following Lemma to compute (
22
).

Lemma 20
.

[
2
, Lemma 11.45]

Let
f:M\to X
be Bochner integrable on
(M,\sigma(M),m)
in
X
and let
Y
be a Banach space. If
T:X\to Y
is a bounded operator, then
Tf:M\to Y
is Bochner integrable on
(M,\sigma(M),m)
in
Y
and it holds that

\displaystyle\int_{M}Tfdm=T\left(\int_{M}fdm\right).

In our case, we set
\mathscr{S}^{\prime}_{\mbox{sym}}(\mathbb{R}^{n}):=
the symmetric part of
\mathscr{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and
T:\mathscr{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\to\mathscr{S}^{\prime}_{\mbox{sym}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})

equals the orthogonal projection on
\mathscr{S}^{\prime}_{\mbox{sym}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, which is clearly a bounded linear operator.
Even though if it is well-known that the space of tempered distributions is not a Banach space, we can think of
\mathscr{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R})
as the inductive limit of a family of Hilbert spaces (e.g
[
15
, Section 3.2]
or
[
21
]
), and an analogous reasoning extends to the multi-dimensional case; thus the lemma above holds true by letting
Y
be some of those Hilbert spaces.

Then, (
22
) becomes

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}^{B}[h_{n}(t,x;\bullet)]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}^{B}\left[\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{J}_{n}}\langle h_{n}(t,x),\mathfrak{e}_{\alpha}\rangle_{n}\mathfrak{e}_{\alpha}(\bullet)\right]=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{J}_{n}}\left\langle\mathbb{E}^{B}\left[h_{n}(t,x)\right],\mathfrak{e}_{\alpha}\right\rangle_{n}\mathfrak{e}_{\alpha}(\bullet).

It is known that if a function is Bochner integrable, then its Pettis and Bochner integrals coincide (see for instance the discussion on page 80 of
[
12
]
). Therefore, by definition of the Pettis integral, we have

\displaystyle\left\langle\mathbb{E}^{B}\left[h_{n}(t,x)\right],\mathfrak{e}_{\alpha}\right\rangle_{n}=\mathbb{E}^{B}\left[\langle h_{n}(t,x),\mathfrak{e}_{\alpha}\rangle_{n}\right].

Hence, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}^{B}[h_{n}(t,x;\bullet)]=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{J}_{n}}\mathbb{E}^{B}\left[\langle h_{n}(t,x),\mathfrak{e}_{\alpha}\rangle_{n}\right]\mathfrak{e}_{\alpha}(\bullet).

Next we compute
\mathbb{E}^{B}\left[\left\langle h_{n}(t,x),\mathfrak{e}_{\alpha}\right\rangle_{n}\right]
, and so far from
(
5.1
), we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}^{B}\left[\left\langle h_{n}(t,x),\mathfrak{e}_{\alpha}\right\rangle_{n}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}^{B}\bigg[\frac{u_{0}(B_{t}^{x})}{\sqrt{n!\alpha!}(n-1)!}\int_{[0,t]^{n}}\sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal{P}_{n}}e_{k_{\sigma(1)}}\otimes\cdots{\otimes}e_{k_{\sigma(n)}}^{\prime}(B_{s_{1}}^{x},\dots,B_{s_{n}}^{x})\;d\mathbf{s}

(23)

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad+\frac{u_{0}^{\prime}(B_{t}^{x})}{\sqrt{n!\alpha!}n!}\sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal{P}_{n}}\int_{[0,t]^{n}}e_{k_{\sigma(1)}}\otimes\cdots{\otimes}e_{k_{\sigma(n)}}(B_{s_{1}}^{x},\dots,B_{s_{n}}^{x})\;d\mathbf{s}\bigg].

To simplify the expression in (
5.1
), we first observe

\displaystyle\partial_{x}\left[e_{k_{1}}(B_{s_{1}}^{x})\cdots e_{k_{n}}(B_{s_{n}}^{x})\right]=\underbrace{\left[e_{k_{1}}^{\prime}(B_{s_{1}}^{x})\cdots e_{k_{n}}(B_{s_{n}}^{x})\right]+\cdots+\left[e_{k_{1}}(B_{s_{1}}^{x})\cdots e_{k_{n}}^{\prime}(B_{s_{n}}^{x})\right]}_{\mbox{$n$ terms}}.

Since the Lebesgue measure is invariant under rotations, we see that for any
f:[0,t]^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
, it holds that

\displaystyle\int_{[0,t]^{n}}f(s_{1},\dots,s_{n})\;d\mathbf{s}=\int_{[0,t]^{n}}\operatorname{Sym}f(s_{1},\dots,s_{n})\;d\mathbf{s}.

Thus, for any permutation
\sigma
of
\{1,\dots,n\}
, we have

\displaystyle\int_{[0,t]^{n}}\frac{u_{0}(B_{t}^{x})}{(n-1)!}\sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal{P}_{n}}e_{k_{\sigma(1)}}\otimes\cdots{\otimes}e_{k_{\sigma(n)}}^{\prime}(B_{s_{1}}^{x},\dots,B_{s_{n}}^{x})\;d\mathbf{s}=\int_{[0,t]^{n}}u_{0}(B_{t}^{x})\partial_{x}\left[e_{k_{1}}(B_{s_{1}}^{x})\cdots e_{k_{n}}(B_{s_{n}}^{x})\right]\;d\mathbf{s},

and

\displaystyle\int_{[0,t]^{n}}\frac{u_{0}^{\prime}(B_{t}^{x})}{n!}\sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal{P}_{n}}e_{k_{\sigma(1)}}\otimes\cdots{\otimes}e_{k_{\sigma(n)}}(B_{s_{1}}^{x},\dots,B_{s_{n}}^{x})\;d\mathbf{s}=\int_{[0,t]^{n}}u_{0}^{\prime}(B_{t}^{x})e_{k_{1}}(B_{s_{1}}^{x})\cdots e_{k_{n}}(B_{s_{n}}^{x})\;d\mathbf{s},

which implies

(24)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}^{B}\left[\left\langle h_{n}(t,x),\mathfrak{e}_{\alpha}\right\rangle_{n}\right]=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n!\alpha!}}\mathbb{E}^{B}\left[\int_{\mathbb{T}^{n}_{[0,t]}}\partial_{x}\left[u_{0}(B_{t}^{x})e_{k_{1}}(B_{s_{1}}^{x})\cdots e_{k_{n}}(B_{s_{n}}^{x})\right]\;d\mathbf{s}\right],

where again
\mathbb{T}^{n}_{[0,t]}=\{0\leq s_{1}\leq\cdots\leq s_{n}\leq t\}
.

Furthermore, we notice that

\displaystyle\int_{[0,t]^{n}}\left[e_{k_{1}}(B_{s_{1}}^{x})\cdots e_{k_{n}}(B_{s_{n}}^{x})\right]\;d\mathbf{s}=\int_{[0,t]^{n}}\operatorname{Sym}\left[e_{k_{1}}(B_{s_{1}}^{x})\cdots e_{k_{n}}(B_{s_{n}}^{x})\right]\;d\mathbf{s}

\displaystyle=\int_{[0,t]^{n}}\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal{P}_{p}}\left[e_{k_{1}}(B_{s_{\sigma(1)}}^{x})\cdots e_{k_{p}}(B_{s_{\sigma(n)}}^{x})\right]\;d\mathbf{s}=\sqrt{\frac{\alpha!}{n!}}\int_{[0,t]^{n}}\partial_{x}\mathfrak{e}_{\alpha}(B_{s_{1}}^{x},\dots,B_{s_{n}}^{x})\;d\mathbf{s}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{{\alpha!n!}}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{n}_{[0,t]}}\partial_{x}\mathfrak{e}_{\alpha}(B_{s_{1}}^{x},\dots,B_{s_{n}}^{x})\;d\mathbf{s},

and similarly,

\displaystyle\int_{[0,t]^{n}}\partial_{x}\left[e_{k_{1}}(B_{s_{1}}^{x})\cdots e_{k_{n}}(B_{s_{n}}^{x})\right]\;d\mathbf{s}=\sqrt{{\alpha!n!}}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{n}_{[0,t]}}\partial_{x}\mathfrak{e}_{\alpha}(B_{s_{1}}^{x},\dots,B_{s_{n}}^{x})\;d\mathbf{s}.

Therefore, (
24
) becomes

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}^{B}\left[\left\langle h_{n}(t,x),\mathfrak{e}_{\alpha}\right\rangle_{n}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}^{B}\left[\int_{\mathbb{T}^{n}_{[0,t]}}\partial_{x}\left[u_{0}(B_{t}^{x})\mathfrak{e}_{\alpha}(B_{s_{1}}^{x},\dots,B_{s_{n}}^{x})\right]\;d\mathbf{s}\right]

(25)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{n}_{[0,t]}}\mathbb{E}^{B}\left[\partial_{x}\left[u_{0}(B_{t}^{x})\mathfrak{e}_{\alpha}(B_{s_{1}}^{x},\dots,B_{s_{n}}^{x})\right]\right]\;d\mathbf{s},

where the second equality holds true by Fubini lemma.

By conditioning iteratively on the filtration of
B
at the sites
s_{n},s_{n-1},\dots,s_{1}
, we can rewrite (
5.1
) as

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}^{B}\left[\left\langle h_{n}(t,x),\mathfrak{e}_{\alpha}\right\rangle_{n}\right]

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{n}_{[0,t]}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\partial_{x}\mathfrak{e}_{\alpha}(y_{1}+x,y_{2},\dots,y_{n})p(s_{n}-s_{n-1},y_{n}-y_{n-1})\times

\displaystyle\quad\quad\qquad\qquad\cdots\times p(s_{1},y_{1})u_{(\mathbf{0})}(t-s_{n},y_{n})d\mathbf{y}\;d\mathbf{s}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{n}_{[0,t]}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{p}}\partial_{y_{1}}\mathfrak{e}_{\alpha}(y_{1}+x,y_{2},\dots,y_{n})p(s_{n}-s_{n-1},y_{n}-y_{n-1})

\displaystyle\quad\quad\qquad\qquad\cdots\times p(s_{1},y_{1})u_{(\mathbf{0})}(t-s_{n},y_{n})d\mathbf{y}\;d\mathbf{s}

\displaystyle=-\int_{\mathbb{T}^{n}_{[0,t]}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\mathfrak{e}_{\alpha}(y_{1},y_{2},\dots,y_{n})p(s_{n}-s_{n-1},y_{n}-y_{n-1})

(26)

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\cdots\times\partial_{y_{1}}p(s_{1},y_{1}-x)u_{(\mathbf{0})}(t-s_{n},y_{n})d\mathbf{y}\;d\mathbf{s}.

Noticing that
-\partial_{y}p(s,y-x)=\partial_{x}p(s,x-y)
and letting
r_{i}=t-s_{n+1-i}
for
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
, the equation (
5.1
) becomes

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{T}^{n}_{[0,t]}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\mathfrak{e}_{\alpha}(y_{1},y_{2},\dots,y_{n})p(r_{2}-r_{1},y_{n}-y_{n-1})\times\cdots\times\partial_{x}p(t-r_{n},x-y_{1})u_{(\mathbf{0})}(r_{1},y_{n})d\mathbf{y}\;d\mathbf{r},

which yields, after relabeling the
y
’s,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}^{B}\left[\left\langle h_{n}(t,x),\mathfrak{e}_{\alpha}\right\rangle_{n}\right]=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{n}_{[0,t]}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}

\displaystyle\partial_{x}p(t-r_{n},x-y_{n})p(r_{n}-r_{n-1},y_{n}-y_{n-1})\times\cdots\times p(r_{2}-r_{1},y_{2}-y_{1})

\displaystyle\cdots\times\mathfrak{e}_{\alpha}(y_{1},y_{2},\dots,y_{n})u_{(\mathbf{0})}(r_{1},y_{1})d\mathbf{y}\;d\mathbf{r}.

Using the definition of
\mathfrak{e}_{\alpha}
, it equals

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha!}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{n!}}

\displaystyle\sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal{P}_{n}}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{n}_{[0,t]}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\partial_{x}p(t-r_{n},x-y_{n})p(r_{n}-r_{n-1},y_{n}-y_{n-1})\times\cdots\times p(r_{2}-r_{1},y_{2}-y_{1})

\displaystyle\cdots\times e_{k_{\sigma(1)}}(y_{1})\times\cdots\times e_{k_{\sigma(n)}}(y_{n})u_{(\mathbf{0})}(r_{1},y_{1})d\mathbf{y}\;d\mathbf{r}=:\frac{1}{\sqrt{n!}}\mathfrak{K}_{\alpha}(t,x),\quad|\alpha|=n\geq 1.

Putting all together, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}^{B}\left[h_{n}(t,x;\bullet)\right]=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{J}_{n}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{n!}}\mathfrak{K}_{\alpha}(t,x)\mathfrak{e}_{\alpha}(\bullet)

and

\displaystyle I_{n}\left(\mathbb{E}^{B}\left[h_{n}(t,x)\right]\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{J}_{n}}\mathfrak{K}_{\alpha}(t,x)I_{n}\left(\frac{\mathfrak{e}_{\alpha}}{\sqrt{n!}}\right)=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{J}_{n}}\mathfrak{K}_{\alpha}(t,x)\xi_{\alpha}\quad\mbox{by \eqref{eq:Inxia}.}

Finally, we have the chaos expansion of
\partial_{x}u(t,x)
as follows:

(27)

\displaystyle\partial_{x}u(t,x)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}I_{n}\left(\mathbb{E}^{B}\left[h_{n}(t,x)\right]\right)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{J}_{n}}\mathfrak{K}_{\alpha}(t,x)\xi_{\alpha}=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{J}}\mathfrak{K}_{\alpha}(t,x)\xi_{\alpha},

where
\mathfrak{K}_{(\mathbf{0})}(t,x)=\partial_{x}u_{(\mathbf{0})}(t,x)
, and for
|\alpha|=n\geq 1
,

\displaystyle\mathfrak{K}_{\alpha}(t,x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha!}}\sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal{P}_{n}}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{n}_{[0,t]}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}

\displaystyle\partial_{x}p(t-r_{n},x-y_{n})p(r_{n}-r_{n-1},y_{n}-y_{n-1})\times\cdots\times p(r_{2}-r_{1},y_{2}-y_{1})

\displaystyle\cdots\times e_{k_{\sigma(1)}}(y_{1})\times\cdots\times e_{k_{\sigma(n)}}(y_{n})u_{(\mathbf{0})}(r_{1},y_{1})d\mathbf{y}\;d\mathbf{r}.

Remark 21
.

Using the Feynman-Kac representation of
u(t,x)
, it was possible to compute the weak derivative of
u(t,x)
with respect to
x
in
(S)^{*}
as follows:

\partial_{x}u(t,x)=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{J}}\mathfrak{K}_{\alpha}(t,x)\xi_{\alpha},

where
\mathfrak{K}_{(\mathbf{0})}(t,x)=\partial_{x}u_{(\mathbf{0})}(t,x)
, and for
|\alpha|=n\geq 1
,

\displaystyle\mathfrak{K}_{\alpha}(t,x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha!}}\sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal{P}_{n}}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{n}_{[0,t]}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}

\displaystyle\partial_{x}p(t-r_{n},x-y_{n})p(r_{n}-r_{n-1},y_{n}-y_{n-1})\times\cdots\times p(r_{2}-r_{1},y_{2}-y_{1})

\displaystyle\cdots\times e_{k_{\sigma(1)}}(y_{1})\times\cdots\times e_{k_{\sigma(n)}}(y_{n})u_{(\mathbf{0})}(r_{1},y_{1})d\mathbf{y}\;d\mathbf{r}.

In fact, the weak spatial derivative is the usual spatial derivative in
L^{2}\left(\mathbb{P}^{W}\right)
sense (and hence
\mathbb{P}^{W}
almost sure sense). One can verify this assertion by computing
\partial_{x}u(t,x)
using
the chaos expansion of
u(t,x)
directly and by the uniqueness of mild solution.

Before we show
\partial_{x}u(t,x)\in\mathcal{G}
for each
t>0
and
x\in\mathbb{R}
, we first need the following lemma:

Lemma 22
.

Assume that
u_{0}\in C_{b}^{1}(\mathbb{R})
. Then,
for each
\alpha\in\mathcal{J}
,
t>0
and
x\in\mathbb{R}
,
\mathfrak{K}_{\alpha}(t,x)
is well-defined, and moreover, for
|\alpha|\geq 1
,

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}\mathfrak{K}_{\alpha}^{\epsilon}(t,x)=\mathfrak{K}_{\alpha}(t,x),

where

\displaystyle\mathfrak{K}_{\alpha}^{\epsilon}(t,x):=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha!}}\sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal{P}_{n}}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{n}_{[0,t-\epsilon]}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}

\displaystyle\partial_{x}p(t-r_{n},x-y_{n})p(r_{n}-r_{n-1},y_{n}-y_{n-1})\times\cdots\times p(r_{2}-r_{1},y_{2}-y_{1})

\displaystyle\cdots\times e_{k_{\sigma(1)}}(y_{1})\times\cdots\times e_{k_{\sigma(n)}}(y_{n})u_{(\mathbf{0})}(r_{1},y_{1})d\mathbf{y}\;d\mathbf{r}\qquad\mbox{for $\epsilon>0$},

with
\mathbb{T}^{n}_{[0,t-\epsilon]}:=\{0\leq s_{1}\leq\cdots\leq s_{n}\leq t-\epsilon\}
.

Proof.

We will decompose
\mathfrak{K}_{\alpha}(t,x)
as a finite sum of well-defined terms. Without loss of generality, we let
|\alpha|=n\geq 1
.
Notice that

\displaystyle\mathfrak{K}_{\alpha}(t,x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha!}}\sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal{P}_{n}}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{n}_{[0,t]}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\partial_{x}p(t-r_{n},x-y_{n})p(r_{n}-r_{n-1},y_{n}-y_{n-1})\times\cdots\times p(r_{2}-r_{1},y_{2}-y_{1})

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\quad\times e_{k_{\sigma(1)}}(y_{1})\times\cdots\times e_{k_{\sigma(n)}}(y_{n})u_{(\mathbf{0})}(r_{1},y_{1})d\mathbf{y}\;d\mathbf{r}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha!}}\sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal{P}_{n}}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{n}_{[0,t]}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\partial_{y_{n}}p(t-r_{n},x-y_{n})p(r_{n}-r_{n-1},y_{n}-y_{n-1})\times\cdots\times p(r_{2}-r_{1},y_{2}-y_{1})

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\quad\times e_{k_{\sigma(1)}}(y_{1})\times\cdots\times e_{k_{\sigma(n)}}(y_{n})u_{(\mathbf{0})}(r_{1},y_{1})d\mathbf{y}\;d\mathbf{r}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha!}}\sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal{P}_{n}}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{n}_{[0,t]}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}p(t-r_{n},x-y_{n})\partial_{y_{n}}\left(p(r_{n}-r_{n-1},y_{n}-y_{n-1})e_{k_{\sigma(n)}}(y_{n})\right)\times\cdots

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\quad\times p(r_{2}-r_{1},y_{2}-y_{1})e_{k_{\sigma(1)}}(y_{1})\times\cdots\times e_{k_{\sigma(n-1)}}(y_{n-1})u_{(\mathbf{0})}(r_{1},y_{1})d\mathbf{y}\;d\mathbf{r}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha!}}\sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal{P}_{n}}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{n}_{[0,t]}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}p(t-r_{n},x-y_{n})\partial_{y_{n}}p(r_{n}-r_{n-1},y_{n}-y_{n-1})e_{k_{\sigma(n)}}(y_{n})\times\cdots

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\quad\times p(r_{2}-r_{1},y_{2}-y_{1})e_{k_{\sigma(1)}}(y_{1})\times\cdots\times e_{k_{\sigma(n-1)}}(y_{n-1})u_{(\mathbf{0})}(r_{1},y_{1})d\mathbf{y}\;d\mathbf{r}

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha!}}\sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal{P}_{n}}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{n}_{[0,t]}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}p(t-r_{n},x-y_{n})p(r_{n}-r_{n-1},y_{n}-y_{n-1})e^{\prime}_{k_{\sigma(n)}}(y_{n})\times\cdots

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\quad\times p(r_{2}-r_{1},y_{2}-y_{1})e_{k_{\sigma(1)}}(y_{1})\times\cdots\times e_{k_{\sigma(n-1)}}(y_{n-1})u_{(\mathbf{0})}(r_{1},y_{1})d\mathbf{y}\;d\mathbf{r}

\displaystyle=:a_{1}+b_{1}.

We note that
b_{1}
is well-defined since
u_{0}\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
, and for
a_{1}
, we do a similar step as above:

\displaystyle a_{1}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha!}}\sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal{P}_{n}}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{n}_{[0,t]}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}p(t-r_{n},x-y_{n})\partial_{y_{n-1}}p(r_{n}-r_{n-1},y_{n}-y_{n-1})e_{k_{\sigma(n)}}(y_{n})\times\cdots

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\quad\times p(r_{2}-r_{1},y_{2}-y_{1})e_{k_{\sigma(1)}}(y_{1})\times\cdots\times e_{k_{\sigma(n-1)}}(y_{n-1})u_{(\mathbf{0})}(r_{1},y_{1})d\mathbf{y}\;d\mathbf{r}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha!}}\sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal{P}_{n}}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{n}_{[0,t]}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}p(t-r_{n},x-y_{n})p(r_{n}-r_{n-1},y_{n}-y_{n-1})e_{k_{\sigma(n)}}(y_{n})

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\quad\times\partial_{y_{n-1}}\left(p(r_{n-1}-r_{n-2},y_{n-1}-y_{n-2})e_{k_{\sigma(n-1)}}(y_{n-1})\right)\times\cdots

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\quad\times p(r_{2}-r_{1},y_{2}-y_{1})e_{k_{\sigma(1)}}(y_{1})\times\cdots\times e_{k_{\sigma(n-2)}}(y_{n-2})u_{(\mathbf{0})}(r_{1},y_{1})d\mathbf{y}\;d\mathbf{r}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha!}}\sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal{P}_{n}}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{n}_{[0,t]}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}p(t-r_{n},x-y_{n})p(r_{n}-r_{n-1},y_{n}-y_{n-1})e_{k_{\sigma(n)}}(y_{n})

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\quad\times\partial_{y_{n-1}}p(r_{n-1}-r_{n-2},y_{n-1}-y_{n-2})e_{k_{\sigma(n-1)}}(y_{n-1})\times\cdots

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\quad\times p(r_{2}-r_{1},y_{2}-y_{1})e_{k_{\sigma(1)}}(y_{1})\times\cdots\times e_{k_{\sigma(n-2)}}(y_{n-2})u_{(\mathbf{0})}(r_{1},y_{1})d\mathbf{y}\;d\mathbf{r}

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha!}}\sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal{P}_{n}}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{n}_{[0,t]}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}p(t-r_{n},x-y_{n})p(r_{n}-r_{n-1},y_{n}-y_{n-1})e_{k_{\sigma(n)}}(y_{n})

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\quad\times p(r_{n-1}-r_{n-2},y_{n-1}-y_{n-2})e^{\prime}_{k_{\sigma(n-1)}}(y_{n-1})\times\cdots

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\quad\times p(r_{2}-r_{1},y_{2}-y_{1})e_{k_{\sigma(1)}}(y_{1})\times\cdots\times e_{k_{\sigma(n-2)}}(y_{n-2})u_{(\mathbf{0})}(r_{1},y_{1})d\mathbf{y}\;d\mathbf{r}

\displaystyle=:a_{2}+b_{2}.

Again,
b_{2}
is well-defined. By iterating this process, we can get

\mathfrak{K}_{\alpha}(t,x)=a_{n-1}+\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}b_{i},

where
\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}b_{i}
is well-defined and

\displaystyle a_{n-1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha!}}\sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal{P}_{n}}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{n}_{[0,t]}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}p(t-r_{n},x-y_{n})p(r_{n}-r_{n-1},y_{n}-y_{n-1})\times\cdots\times p(r_{2}-r_{1},y_{2}-y_{1})

\displaystyle\times e_{k_{\sigma(1)}}(y_{1})\times\cdots\times e_{k_{\sigma(n)}}(y_{n})\partial_{y_{1}}u_{(\mathbf{0})}(r_{1},y_{1})d\mathbf{y}\;d\mathbf{r}.

Since
u_{(\mathbf{0})}(r_{1},y_{1})d\mathbf{y}=\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}p(r_{1},y_{1}-y_{0})u_{0}(y_{0})dy_{0}
,
a_{n-1}
becomes

\displaystyle a_{n-1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha!}}\sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal{P}_{n}}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{n}_{[0,t]}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n+1}}p(t-r_{n},x-y_{n})p(r_{n}-r_{n-1},y_{n}-y_{n-1})\times\cdots\times p(r_{2}-r_{1},y_{2}-y_{1})

\displaystyle\times e_{k_{\sigma(1)}}(y_{1})\times\cdots\times e_{k_{\sigma(n)}}(y_{n})p(r_{1},y_{1}-y_{0})u_{0}^{\prime}(y_{0})dy_{0}d\mathbf{y}\;d\mathbf{r},

which is clearly well-defined since
u_{0}^{\prime}\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
.

Moreover, one can show that
\displaystyle\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}\mathfrak{K}_{\alpha}^{\epsilon}(t,x)=\mathfrak{K}_{\alpha}(t,x)
easily by considering the same argument as
\mathfrak{K}_{\alpha}(t,x)
for
\mathfrak{K}_{\alpha}^{\epsilon}(t,x)
.
∎

Theorem 23
.

If
u_{0}\in C_{b}^{1}(\mathbb{R})
, for each
t>0
and
x\in\mathbb{R}
,

\partial_{x}u(t,x)\in\mathcal{G}.

Proof.

From (
27
), we have

\mathbb{E}|\partial_{x}u(t,x)|^{2}=|\mathfrak{K}_{(\mathbf{0})}(t,x)|^{2}+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{J}_{n}}|\mathfrak{K}_{\alpha}(t,x)|^{2}.

We note that
\mathfrak{K}_{(\mathbf{0})}(t,x)=\partial_{x}u_{(\mathbf{0})}(t,x)<\infty
for each
(t,x)\in[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}
.
For
|\alpha|=n\geq 1
,
we use
\mathfrak{K}_{\alpha}^{\epsilon}
:
For
\epsilon>0
, we notice that (by Fubini lemma)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{K}_{\alpha}^{\epsilon}(t,x)=\sqrt{n!}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{n}_{[0,t-\epsilon]}}

\displaystyle\partial_{x}p(t-r_{n},x-y_{n})p(r_{n}-r_{n-1},y_{n}-y_{n-1})\times\cdots\times p(r_{2}-r_{1},y_{2}-y_{1})

\displaystyle\cdots\times\mathfrak{e}_{\alpha}(y_{1},\dots,y_{n})u_{(\mathbf{0})}(r_{1},y_{1})\;d\mathbf{r}d\mathbf{y}.

Using Bessel’s inequality, we have that

\displaystyle\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{J}_{n}}|\mathfrak{K}_{\alpha}^{\epsilon}(t,x)|^{2}\leq n!\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\bigg(\int_{\mathbb{T}^{n}_{[0,t-\epsilon]}}\partial_{x}p(t-r_{n},x-y_{n})p(r_{n}-r_{n-1},y_{n}-y_{n-1})\times

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\cdots\times p(r_{2}-r_{1},y_{2}-y_{1})u_{(\mathbf{0})}(r_{1},y_{1})d\mathbf{r}\bigg)^{2}d\mathbf{y}

\displaystyle\leq n!\|u_{0}\|_{\infty}^{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{n}_{[0,t-\epsilon]}}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{n}_{[0,t-\epsilon]}}\partial_{x}p(t-r_{n},x-y_{n})\partial_{x}p(t-s_{n},x-y_{n})p(r_{n}-r_{n-1},y_{n}-y_{n-1})

\displaystyle\quad\times p(s_{n}-s_{n-1},y_{n}-y_{n-1})\cdots p(r_{2}-r_{1},y_{2}-y_{1})p(s_{2}-s_{1},y_{2}-y_{1})\;d\mathbf{r}d\mathbf{s}d\mathbf{y}.

Again using Fubini lemma and the semigroup property of
p
, we can write the last expression as

\displaystyle n!\|u_{0}\|_{\infty}^{2}(2\pi)^{-(n-1)/2}

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{T}^{n}_{[0,t-\epsilon]}}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{n}_{[0,t-\epsilon]}}\prod_{k=1}^{n-1}(s_{k+1}+r_{k+1}-s_{k}-r_{k})^{-1/2}

\displaystyle\quad\cdots\times\int_{\mathbb{R}}\partial_{x}p(t-r_{n},x-y_{n})\partial_{x}p(t-s_{n},x-y_{n})dy_{n}\;d\mathbf{r}d\mathbf{s}.

Since

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}\partial_{x}p(t-r_{n},x-y_{n})\partial_{x}p(t-s_{n},x-y_{n})dy_{n}=(2\pi)^{-1/2}(2t-s_{n}-r_{n})^{-3/2},

and
(a+b)^{-1/2}\leq 2^{-1/2}a^{-1/4}b^{-1/4}
for
a,b>0
, we can finally see that

\displaystyle\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{J}_{n}}|\mathfrak{K}_{\alpha}^{\epsilon}(t,x)|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq n!\|u_{0}\|_{\infty}^{2}(2\pi)^{-n/2}\left(\int_{\mathbb{T}^{n}_{[0,t-\epsilon]}}(t-s_{n})^{-3/4}\prod_{k=1}^{n-1}(s_{k+1}-s_{k})^{-1/4}d\mathbf{s}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq n!\|u_{0}\|_{\infty}^{2}(2\pi)^{-n/2}\left(\int_{\mathbb{T}^{n}_{[0,t]}}(t-s_{n})^{-3/4}\prod_{k=1}^{n-1}(s_{k+1}-s_{k})^{-1/4}d\mathbf{s}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\|u_{0}\|_{\infty}^{2}C^{n}t^{3n-4}n^{-n/2}\quad\mbox{for some $C>0$,}

where the last inequality follows from
[
14
, equation (4.10)]
.

For each
t>0
and
x\in\mathbb{R}
,
the convergence is uniform in
\epsilon
and
\mathfrak{K}_{\alpha}^{\epsilon}(t,x)\to\mathfrak{K}_{\alpha}(t,x)
as
\epsilon\to 0
by Lemma
22
,

\displaystyle\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{J}_{n}}|\mathfrak{K}_{\alpha}(t,x)|^{2}\leq\|u_{0}\|_{\infty}^{2}C^{n}t^{\frac{3n}{4}-1}n^{-n/2}\quad\mbox{for some constant $C>0$.}

Since
C^{n}t^{\frac{3n}{4}-1}n^{-n/2}e^{2\lambda n}
is summable in
n
for any
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
, the conclusion follows from Definition
14
.
∎

Remark 24
.

We have the following Feynman-Kac type formula for the spatial derivative of
u
:

\displaystyle\partial_{x}u(t,x)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}^{B}\left[\mathcal{E}(L^{x}(t))\diamond\bigg\{u_{0}^{\prime}(B_{t}^{x})+u_{0}(B_{t}^{x})I_{1}\left(\partial_{x}L^{x}(t)\right)\bigg\}\right],

where
\mathbb{E}^{B}
must be understood as a Bochner integral in
(S)^{*}
.
Notice that the integrand of
\mathbb{E}^{B}
on the right-hand side is in fact a Hida distribution. However, after taking the expectation
\mathbb{E}^{B}
, which is interpreted as a Bochner integral in
(S)^{*}
, we end up with a regular random variable in
\mathcal{G}
. This means that the
white noise integral
(or the Bochner integral in
(S)^{*}
) has a regularizing effect.

Theorem 25
.

Let
0<\varepsilon<1/2
be arbitrary and assume that
u_{0}\in C^{3/2}(\mathbb{R})
. Then, for each
t>0
,

\partial_{x}u(t,\bullet)\in C^{1/2-\varepsilon}(\mathbb{R}).

Proof.

Let
p>1
,
t>0
and
x\in\mathbb{R}
. By
[
14
, Proposition 2.1]
, we have

(28)

\displaystyle\left(\mathbb{E}\left|\partial_{x}u(t,x+h)-\partial_{x}u(t,x)\right|^{p}\right)^{1/p}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(p-1)^{n/2}\left(\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{J}_{n}}|\mathfrak{K}_{\alpha}(t,x+h)-\mathfrak{K}_{\alpha}(t,x)|^{2}\right)^{1/2}.

For
|\alpha|=0
, by Lemma
15
, we have

\mathfrak{K}_{(\mathbf{0})}(t,\bullet)=\partial_{x}u_{(\mathbf{0})}(t,\bullet)\in C^{1/2}(\mathbb{R}).

For
|\alpha|=n\geq 1
,
similarly to the proof of Theorem
23
, we can get

\displaystyle\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{J}_{n}}\left|\mathfrak{K}_{\alpha}^{\epsilon}(t,x+h)-\mathfrak{K}_{\alpha}^{\epsilon}(t,x)\right|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq n!\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\bigg(\int_{\mathbb{T}^{n}_{[0,t-\epsilon]}}\left(\partial_{x}p(t-r_{n},x+h-y_{n})-\partial_{x}p(t-r_{n},x-y_{n})\right)p(r_{n}-r_{n-1},y_{n}-y_{n-1})\times

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\cdots\times p(r_{2}-r_{1},y_{2}-y_{1})u_{(\mathbf{0})}(r_{1},y_{1})d\mathbf{r}\bigg)^{2}d\mathbf{y}

\displaystyle\leq n!\|u_{0}\|_{\infty}^{2}(2\pi)^{-(n-1)/2}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{n}_{[0,t-\epsilon]}}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{n}_{[0,t-\epsilon]}}\prod_{k=1}^{n-1}(s_{k+1}+r_{k+1}-s_{k}-r_{k})^{-1/2}

\displaystyle\quad\times\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left(\partial_{x}p(t-r_{n},x+h-y_{n})-\partial_{x}p(t-r_{n},x-y_{n})\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\quad\cdots\times\left(\partial_{x}p(t-s_{n},x+h-y_{n})-\partial_{x}p(t-s_{n},x-y_{n})\right)dy_{n}\;d\mathbf{r}d\mathbf{s}.

We next compute

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}\partial_{x}p(t_{1},x_{1}-z)\partial_{x}p(t_{2},x_{1}-z)dz

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi t_{1}^{3/2}t_{2}^{3/2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}(x_{1}-z)(x_{2}-z)e^{-\frac{(x_{1}-z)^{2}}{2t_{1}}-\frac{(x_{2}-z)^{2}}{2t_{2}}}dz

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi t_{1}^{3/2}t_{2}^{3/2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}z\left(z-(x_{1}-x_{2})\right)e^{-\frac{z^{2}}{2t_{1}}-\frac{(z-(x_{1}-x_{2}))^{2}}{2t_{2}}}dz

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{-\frac{(x_{1}-x_{2})^{2}}{2(t_{1}+t_{2})}}}{2\pi t_{1}^{3/2}t_{2}^{3/2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}(z^{2}-(x_{1}-x_{2})z)e^{-\frac{(t_{1}+t_{2})}{2t_{1}t_{2}}\left(z-\frac{(x_{1}-x_{2})t_{1}}{t_{1}+t_{2}}\right)^{2}}dz

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-\frac{(x_{1}-x_{2})^{2}}{2(t_{1}+t_{2})}}(t_{1}+t_{2})^{-3/2}\left(1-\frac{(x_{1}-x_{2})^{2}}{(t_{1}+t_{2})}\right).

The last equality can be verified using the mean and variance of a normal distribution
N\left(\frac{(x-y)t_{1}}{t_{1}+t_{2}},\frac{t_{1}t_{2}}{t_{1}+t_{2}}\right)
.
Then, we can easily check, using the fact
1-e^{-z}\leq z^{\gamma}
for any
z\geq 0
and
0<\gamma\leq 1
,

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left(\partial_{x}p(t-r_{n},x+h-y_{n})-\partial_{x}p(t-r_{n},x-y_{n})\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\quad\times\left(\partial_{x}p(t-s_{n},x+h-y_{n})-\partial_{x}p(t-s_{n},x-y_{n})\right)dy_{n}

(29)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}(2t-s-r)^{-3/2}\left(1-e^{-\frac{h^{2}}{2(2t-s-r)}}+\frac{h^{2}e^{-\frac{h^{2}}{2(2t-s-r)}}}{2t-s-r}\right)\leq Ch^{2\gamma}(2t-s-r)^{-3/2-\gamma}.

At this point, we restrict
0<\gamma<1/2
so that (
5.1
) is integrable both in
s
and
r
variables near
t
.

This leads to

\displaystyle\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{J}_{n}}\left|\mathfrak{K}_{\alpha}^{\epsilon}(t,x+h)-\mathfrak{K}_{\alpha}^{\epsilon}(t,x)\right|^{2}\leq h^{2\gamma}\|u_{0}\|_{\infty}^{2}C^{n}(\gamma)t^{\frac{3n}{4}-1-\frac{\gamma}{2}}n^{-n/2},

with
0<\gamma<1/2
for some constant
C(\gamma)>0
depending only on
\gamma
.

After taking
\epsilon\to 0
, the desired result follows from (
28
) and the Kolmogorov continuity theorem.
∎
