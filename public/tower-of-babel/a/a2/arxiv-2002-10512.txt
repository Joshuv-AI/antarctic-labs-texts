Sharp Constants of Approximation Theory. IV. Asymptotic Relations in General Settings
Michael I. Ganzburg

Abstract
. In this paper we first introduce the unified definition of the sharp constant that includes constants in three major problems of approximation theory, such as, inequalities for approximating elements, approximation of individual elements, and approximation on classes of elements. Second, we find sufficient conditions that imply limit relations between various sharp constants of approximation theory in general settings. Third, a number of examples from various areas of approximation theory illustrates the general approach.

In addition to (

by

2.
Definitions and Properties of Sharp Constants in Vector Spaces

Here, we first describe major spaces that are discussed in this paper. Next, we give
the unified definition of a
sharp constant in general settings and study some of its properties. Finally, we discuss
certain special cases of sharp constants of approximation theory and analysis.

2.1.
Spaces

Let
F
be a vector space over the field of complex numbers
{\mathbb{C}}

or real numbers
{\mathbb{R}}
. Let
F

be equipped with a functional

\|\cdot\|_{F}:F\to[0,{\infty})
, which is not necessarily a norm though we use the same notation.
Occasionally, we will refer to
\|\cdot\|_{F}
as a ”norm.” In particular,
\|\cdot\|_{F}
could be
a norm, a seminorm, or more generally, a
{\beta}
-norm or a
{\beta}
-seminorm.

Definition 2.1
.

[
53
, p. 1102]

We say that
F
is a
\beta
-normed space
,
\beta\in(0,1]
, if there exists
a
{\beta}
-
norm

\|\cdot\|_{F}
on
F
with the following properties:

(a)

\|f\|_{F}\geq 0
for all
f\in F
and
\|f\|_{F}=0
if and only if
f=0
.

(b)

\|\alpha f\|_{F}=|\alpha|\,\|f\|_{F}
for all
\alpha\in{\mathbb{C}}
(or
\alpha\in{\mathbb{R}}
).

(c)

\|f+g\|_{F}\leq\left(\|f\|_{F}^{\beta}+\|g\|_{F}^{\beta}\right)^{1/\beta}
for all
f\in F,\,g\in F
.

Occasionally, we will refer to (b) and (c) as the homogeneity property and
the
{\beta}
-triangle inequality, respectively.
In case of
{\beta}=1,\,\|\cdot\|_{F}
is a norm and
F
is a normed space.
Note that a
{\beta}
-norm is closely related to a
{\gamma}
-quasinorm
|||\cdot|||_{F}
satisfying
conditions (a), (b), and (c*)
|||f+g|||_{F}\leq{\gamma}(|||f|||_{F}+|||g|||_{F}),\,{\gamma}\geq 1;\,f\in F,\,g\in F.

Obviously, a
{\beta}
-norm is a
2^{1/{\beta}-1}
-quasinorm. Conversely, the Aoki-Rolewicz theorem

[
3
,
72
]
states that if for some
\beta\in(0,1],\,|||\cdot|||_{F}
is
a
2^{1/{\beta}-1}
-quasinorm, then there exists a
{\beta}
-norm
\|\cdot\|_{F}
, which
is equivalent to
|||\cdot|||_{F}
.
The best known examples of
\beta
-normed spaces
for
{\beta}\in(0,1)
are the sequence space
\ell_{\beta}
,
the function space
L_{\beta}
, and the Hardy space
H_{\beta}
.

If we replace property (a) with

(a*)

\|f\|_{F}\geq 0
for all
f\in F
,

then
\|\cdot\|_{F}
and
F
are called
a
{\beta}
-
seminorm
and a
\beta
-seminormed space
,
respectively.
For
\beta=1,\,\|\cdot\|_{F}

is a seminorm and
F
is a seminormed space.

However, in this paper
\|\cdot\|_{F}
is not necessarily a

\beta
-norm or a
\beta
-seminorm.
For example, the trivial functional

\|f\|_{F,\mathrm{triv}}:=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}0,&f=0,\\
1,&f\neq 0,\end{array}\right.\qquad f\in F,

(2.1)

satisfies property (a) and also property (c)
for any
\beta\in(0,1]

but does not satisfy property (b).
Meantime,
\|\cdot\|_{F,\mathrm{triv}}

satisfies the symmetry property

(b*)

\|-f\|_{F}=\|f\|_{F}
for all
f\in F
.

Note that a vector space
F
can be equipped with two (or more)
different ”norms;” we often denote them by
\|\cdot\|_{F}

and
\|\cdot\|_{F}^{*}
.
So the equality
F=G
for vector spaces
F
and
G
means that they
are equal as sets but can be equipped with different ”norms,”
while the equality
\|\cdot\|_{F}=\|\cdot\|_{G}
also means that
F=G
.

In this paper we also discuss sequences of ”norms”

\left\{\|\cdot\|_{F_{n}}\right\}_{n=1}^{\infty}

that possess either the continuity (C) or generalized continuity (GC) properties,
which can replace
the
{\beta}
-triangle inequality in certain problems.

Definition 2.2
.

We say that
\left\{\|\cdot\|_{F_{n}}\right\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
possesses the C-property if for any

h_{n}\in F_{n},\,g_{n}\in F_{n},\,n\in{\mathbb{N}}
, such that

\lim_{n\to{\infty}}\|g_{n}\|_{F_{n}}=0
,
the following relations hold true:

\displaystyle\liminf_{n\to{\infty}}\|h_{n}+g_{n}\|_{F_{n}}=\liminf_{n\to{\infty}}\|h_{n}\|_{F_{n}},

(2.2)

\displaystyle\limsup_{n\to{\infty}}\|h_{n}+g_{n}\|_{F_{n}}=\limsup_{n\to{\infty}}\|h_{n}\|_{F_{n}}.

(2.3)

Proposition 2.3
.

(i) If ”norms”
\|\cdot\|_{F_{n}},\,n\in{\mathbb{N}}
, satisfy the symmetry property (b*)
and the
{\beta}
-triangle inequality (c), where
{\beta}\in(0,1]
is independent of
n
, then

\left\{\|\cdot\|_{F_{n}}\right\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
possesses the C-property.

(ii) If
\|\cdot\|_{F_{n}}
is a
\beta
-norm or a
\beta
-seminorm,

n\in{\mathbb{N}},

where
{\beta}\in(0,1]
is independent of
n
, then

\left\{\|\cdot\|_{F_{n}}\right\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
possesses the C-property.

(iii) If
\|\cdot\|_{F_{n}}=\|\cdot\|_{F_{n},\mathrm{triv}},\,n\in{\mathbb{N}},

then

\left\{\|\cdot\|_{F_{n}}\right\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
possesses the C-property.

Proof.

Statement (i) follows immediately from the
{\beta}
-triangle inequalities

\displaystyle\|h_{n}\|_{F_{n}}^{\beta}-\|g_{n}\|_{F_{n}}^{\beta}\leq\|h_{n}+g_{n}\|_{F_{n}}^{\beta}\leq\|h_{n}\|_{F_{n}}^{\beta}+\|g_{n}\|_{F_{n}}^{\beta},\qquad n\in{\mathbb{N}},

while statements (ii) and (iii) are easy consequences of (i).
\Box

Definition 2.4
.

Let
F_{n}
be equipped with two ”norms”
\|\cdot\|_{F_{n}}
and

\|\cdot\|_{F_{n}}^{*},\,n\in{\mathbb{N}}
.
We say that
\left\{\|\cdot\|_{F_{n}}\right\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
possesses the GC-property
with respect to the sequence

\left\{\|\cdot\|_{F_{n}}^{*}\right\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
if for any

h_{n}\in F_{n},\,g_{n}\in F_{n},\,n\in{\mathbb{N}}
, such that

\lim_{n\to{\infty}}\|g_{n}\|_{F_{n}}^{*}=0
,
relations (
2.2
) and (
2.3
) hold true.

In case of
\|\cdot\|_{F_{n}}=\|\cdot\|_{F_{n}}^{*},\,n\in{\mathbb{N}}
,
the GC-property is reduced to the C-property. In most of our
applications we use sequences of
”norms” with the C-property. However, in the following
typical example (related to approximation theory)
we present a sequence of ”norms” that possesses the GC-property but does
not possess the C-property.

Example 2.5
.

Let
F_{n}\neq\{0\}
and let
\|\cdot\|_{F_{n}}^{*},\,n\in{\mathbb{N}},
satisfy property (b)
of Definition
2.1
.
Next, let

\left\{\|\cdot\|_{F_{n}}^{*}\right\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
possess the C-property. Let us set

\displaystyle\|f\|_{F_{n}}:=\|{\varphi}_{n}-f\|_{F_{n}}^{*},\qquad f\in F_{n},

where
{\varphi}_{n}
is a fixed element of
F_{n},\,n\in{\mathbb{N}}
.
Then
\left\{\|\cdot\|_{F_{n}}\right\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
possesses the GC-property
with respect to
\left\{\|\cdot\|_{F_{n}}^{*}\right\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
.
Indeed, for any

h_{n}\in F_{n},\,g_{n}\in F_{n},\,n\in{\mathbb{N}}
, such that

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to{\infty}}\|g_{n}\|_{F_{n}}^{*}=\lim_{n\to{\infty}}\|-g_{n}\|_{F_{n}}^{*}=0,

we have

\displaystyle\liminf_{n\to{\infty}}\|h_{n}+g_{n}\|_{F_{n}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\liminf_{n\to{\infty}}\|({\varphi}_{n}-h_{n})+(-g_{n})\|_{F_{n}}^{*}

(2.4)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\liminf_{n\to{\infty}}\|{\varphi}_{n}-h_{n}\|_{F_{n}}^{*}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\liminf_{n\to{\infty}}\|h_{n}\|_{F_{n}}.

Similar relations hold true if we replace
\liminf

in (
2.4
) with
\limsup
.
Hence (
2.2
) and (
2.3
) are valid.

On the other hand, since
F_{n}\neq\{0\},\,n\in{\mathbb{N}}
,
then by property (b) of Definition
2.1
, it is always possible to choose
{\varphi}_{n}\in F_{n},\,n\in{\mathbb{N}}
,
such that

\lim_{n\to{\infty}}\|{\varphi}_{n}\|_{F_{n}}^{*}=a
, where
a\in(0,{\infty})
is a fixed number.
Then for
h_{n}:=(1/3){\varphi}_{n}
and
g_{n}:=(1-1/n){\varphi}_{n},\,n\in{\mathbb{N}}
, we have

\lim_{n\to{\infty}}\|g_{n}\|_{F_{n}}=\lim_{n\to{\infty}}(1/n)\|{\varphi}_{n}\|_{F_{n}}^{*}=0

and

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to{\infty}}\|h_{n}+g_{n}\|_{F_{n}}=\lim_{n\to{\infty}}\|{\varphi}_{n}-h_{n}-g_{n}\|_{F_{n}}^{*}=a/3;\quad\lim_{n\to{\infty}}\|h_{n}\|_{F_{n}}=\lim_{n\to{\infty}}\|{\varphi}_{n}-h_{n}\|_{F_{n}}^{*}=2a/3.

These relations show that in general
\left\{\|\cdot\|_{F_{n}}\right\}_{n=1}^{\infty}

does not possess the C-property.

2.2.
Sharp Constants

For vector spaces
F
and
H
equipped with the corresponding ”norms” and for
an operator
L:F\to H
, we
define the ”norm” of
L
by the formula

\|L\|=\|L\|_{F\to H}:=\sup_{h\in F,\,\|h\|_{F}>0}\frac{\|L(h)\|_{H}}{\|h\|_{F}}.

(2.5)

If
B
is a subset of
F
, we
define the ”norm”
\|L\|
of an operator
L:B\to H
on
B
by the right hand side of
(
2.5
) with
h\in F
replaced by
h\in B
.

Let
F^{(j)}\neq\{0\}
be a vector space over
{\mathbb{C}}
or
{\mathbb{R}}
equipped with a ”norm”

\|\cdot\|_{F^{(j)}},\,j=1,\,2,
and let
B
be a nonempty subset of

F^{(2)}
. Next, let
D:B\to F^{(1)}
be an operator.
In case of the
imbedding
operator
D=I
, we assume that

F^{(1)}\cap F^{(2)}\neq\emptyset
and

B\subseteq F^{(1)}\cap F^{(2)}
.
Certainly, if
F^{(1)}=F^{(2)}
and
\|\cdot\|_{F^{(1)}}=\|\cdot\|_{F^{(2)}}
,
then
I
is the identity operator.

Let us define the following
sharp constant
:

{\mathcal{C}}={\mathcal{C}}\left(D,B,F^{(1)},F^{(2)}\right):=\sup_{f\in B,\,\|f\|_{F^{(2)}}>0}\frac{\|D(f)\|_{F^{(1)}}}{\|f\|_{F^{(2)}}},

(2.6)

which is the ”norm” of
D

on
B
. Clearly,
{\mathcal{C}}\in[0,{\infty}]
.

In certain cases the following formulae for
{\mathcal{C}}
are valid:

\displaystyle{\mathcal{C}}=\sup_{f\in B,\,\|f\|_{F^{(2)}}=1}\|D(f)\|_{F^{(1)}},

(2.7)

\displaystyle{\mathcal{C}}=\sup_{f\in B,\,\|f\|_{F^{(2)}}>0,\,\|D(f)\|_{F^{(1)}}=1}\frac{1}{\|f\|_{F^{(2)}}}.

(2.8)

In particular, it is easy to verify the following proposition.

Proposition 2.6
.

(i) Let
B
be a subspace of
F^{(2)},\,D
be a
1
-homogeneous operator
(that is,
D({\alpha}f)={\alpha}D(f),\,{\alpha}>0
), and ”norms”
\|\cdot\|_{F^{(j)}},\,j=1,\,2,

satisfy the homogeneity property (b). Then relations (
2.7
) and
(
2.8
) hold true.

(ii) If
\|\cdot\|_{F^{(2)}}=\|\cdot\|_{F^{(2)},\mathrm{triv}}
,
then (
2.7
) holds true.

(iii) If
\|\cdot\|_{F^{(1)}}=\|\cdot\|_{F^{(1)},\mathrm{triv}}
,
then (
2.8
) holds true.

2.3.
Sharp Constants in Approximation Theory and Analysis

It turns out that the problem of finding
{\mathcal{C}}={\mathcal{C}}\left(D,B,F^{(1)},F^{(2)}\right)

includes most classical problems
of approximation theory that are related to sharp constants. In addition, it includes
certain analysis problems as well. Below we first discuss three
major approximation problems A, B, and C as special cases of the problem
of finding
{\mathcal{C}}
.

A. Inequalities for Approximating Elements.

Let
F^{(j)}
be a
\beta_{j}
-normed functional space,
j=1,2;\,B
be
a nontrivial subspace of
F^{(2)}
; and

be a linear (often differential)
operator.
Then
{\mathcal{C}}
is the sharp constant in
various inequalities for approximating elements
that we call

inequalities of different metrics.
This is one of the most studied topics
of approximation theory. Several special problems are discussed below.

A1. Markov-Bernstein Type Inequalities for

\|\cdot\|_{F^{(1)}}=\|\cdot\|_{F^{(2)}}
.

A1.1. A. A. Markov and V. A. Markov type inequalities
for the set
B

of algebraic polynomials of one or several variables.

A1.2. Bernstein type inequalities
for the set
B

of trigonometric polynomials, splines, or entire functions of exponential type
of one or several variables.

A2. Inequalities of Different Metrics for

\|\cdot\|_{F^{(1)}}\neq\|\cdot\|_{F^{(2)}}

A2.1. Nikolskii type inequalities
for the set

of one or several variables.

A2.2. Schur type inequalities
for a nonweighted space

a weighted space
F^{(2)}
,
and the set

of trigonometric or algebraic polynomials or entire functions of exponential type
of one or several variables.

A2.3. Remez type inequalities
for a weighted space
F^{(2)}

when a weight vanishes outside of a measurable set
and for the set
B

of trigonometric, algebraic, or Müntz polynomials.

There are numerous publications related to all these inequalities, see,

B. Approximation of Individual Elements.

Let us define the operator
D:B\to F^{(1)}
arbitrarily with the only restriction that

D(f)\neq 0
for
f\in B
and let us choose

\|\cdot\|_{F^{(1)}}=\|\cdot\|_{F^{(1)},\mathrm{triv}}.

We denote by

F^{(2)}_{*}
the vector space
F^{(2)}
equipped with a certain
{\beta}_{2}
-norm

\|\cdot\|^{*}_{F^{(2)}}
on
F^{(2)},\,{\beta}_{2}\in(0,1]
.
For a fixed element
{\varphi}\in F^{(2)}_{*}\setminus B

we choose
\|f\|_{F^{(2)}}=\|{\varphi}-f\|^{*}_{F^{(2)}},\,f\in F^{(2)}_{*}
.
Then

{\mathcal{C}}=\sup_{f\in B,\,f\neq 0}\frac{\|f\|_{F^{(1)},\mathrm{triv}}}{\|{\varphi}-f\|^{*}_{F^{(2)}}}=\frac{1}{\inf_{f\in B,\,f\neq 0}\|{\varphi}-f\|^{*}_{F^{(2)}}}=\frac{1}{E\left({\varphi},B,F_{*}^{(2)}\right)},

(2.9)

6.1.
Notation

Let
{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
be the Euclidean
m
-dimensional space with elements

x=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{m}),\,y=(y_{1},\ldots,y_{m}),\,t=(t_{1},\ldots,t_{m})
,
the inner product
t\cdot y:=\sum_{j=1}^{m}t_{j}y_{j}
,
and the norm
|t|:=\sqrt{t\cdot t}
.
Next,
{\mathbb{C}}^{m}:={\mathbb{R}}^{m}+i{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
is the
m
-dimensional
complex space with elements

z=(z_{1},\ldots,z_{m})=x+iy

and the norm
|z|:=\sqrt{|x|^{2}+|y|^{2}}
;

{\mathbb{Z}}^{m}
denotes the set of all integral lattice points

k=(k_{1},\ldots,k_{m})
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
;
and
{\mathbb{Z}}^{m}_{+}
is a subset of
{\mathbb{Z}}^{m}

of all points with nonnegative coordinates.
We also use multi-indices
{\beta}=({\beta}_{1},\ldots,{\beta}_{m})\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{m}_{+}

and
{\alpha}=({\alpha}_{1},\ldots,{\alpha}_{m})\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{m}_{+}

with

\displaystyle|{\beta}|:=\sum_{j=1}^{m}{\beta}_{j},\qquad|{\alpha}|:=\sum_{j=1}^{m}{\alpha}_{j},\qquad y^{\beta}:=y_{1}^{{\beta}_{1}}\cdot\cdot\cdot y_{m}^{{\beta}_{m}},\qquad D^{\alpha}:=\frac{\partial^{{\alpha}_{1}}}{\partial y_{1}^{{\alpha}_{1}}}\cdot\cdot\cdot\frac{\partial^{{\alpha}_{m}}}{\partial y_{m}^{{\alpha}_{m}}}.

Let
V
be a centrally symmetric (with respect to the origin)
closed
convex body in
{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
and

V^{*}:=\{y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}:\forall\,t\in V,|t\cdot y|\leq 1\}

be the polar of
V
.
It is well known that
V^{*}
is a centrally symmetric (with respect to the origin)
closed
convex body in
{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
and
V^{**}=V
(see, e.g.,
[
71
, Sect. 14]
).
The set
V
generates the following dual norms
on
{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
and
{\mathbb{C}}^{m}
by

\displaystyle\|y\|_{V}^{*}:=\sup_{t\in V}|t\cdot y|,\quad y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m};\qquad\|z\|_{V}^{*}:=\sup_{t\in V}\left|\sum_{j=1}^{m}t_{j}z_{j}\right|,\quad z\in{\mathbb{C}}^{m}.

Note also that
V^{*}
is the unit ball in the norm
\|\cdot\|_{V}^{*}
on
{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
.
Examples of sets
V

and
V^{*}
and dual norms
\|\cdot\|_{V}^{*}
are given below.
Given
{\sigma}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m},\,{\sigma}_{j}>0,\,1\leq j\leq m
, and
M>0
, let

\Pi^{m}_{\sigma}:=\{t\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}:|t_{j}|\leq{\sigma}_{j},1\leq j\leq m\},\,Q^{m}_{M}:=\{t\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}:|t_{j}|\leq M,1\leq j\leq m\}
,
and

{\mathfrak{B}}^{m}_{M}:=\{t\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}:|t|\leq M\}

be the
m
-dimensional parallelepiped, cube, and ball, respectively.

For example, if
{\sigma}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m},\,{\sigma}_{j}>0,\,1\leq j\leq m
, and
V=\left\{t\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}:\left(\sum_{j=1}^{m}|t_{j}/{\sigma}_{j}|^{\mu}\right)^{1/\mu}\leq 1\right\}
, then
for
y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m},\,\|y\|_{V}^{*}=\left(\sum_{j=1}^{m}|{\sigma}_{j}y_{j}|^{\lambda}\right)^{1/{\lambda}}

and
V^{*}=\left\{y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}:\left(\sum_{j=1}^{m}|{\sigma}_{j}y_{j}|^{\lambda}\right)^{1/{\lambda}}\leq 1\right\}
,
where
\mu\in[1,{\infty}],\,{\lambda}\in[1,{\infty}]
, and
1/\mu+1/{\lambda}=1
.
In particular,

\|y\|_{\Pi^{m}_{\sigma}}^{*}=\sum_{j=1}^{m}{\sigma}_{j}|y_{j}|,\,\|y\|_{Q^{m}_{M}}^{*}=M\sum_{j=1}^{m}|y_{j}|
,
and
\|y\|_{{\mathfrak{B}}^{m}_{M}}^{*}=M|y|
.

We also use the floor function
\lfloor\cdot\rfloor
.

Function spaces

In the capacity of spaces
F^{(1)},\,F^{(2)},\,F^{(1)}_{n},
and

F^{(2)}_{n}
from Sections
3
,
4
, and
5

or their
\ast
-modifications from Section
5
,
we mostly use the following function spaces.

Let
\mu
be a
{\sigma}
-finite positive measure on a
{\sigma}
-algebra

\Sigma
over
{\mathbb{R}}^{m}

and let
S_{\mu}({\mathbb{R}}^{m})
be a vector space of all

\mu
-measurable functions

f:{\mathbb{R}}^{m}\to{\mathbb{C}}^{1}
.
Next, let
F({\mathbb{R}}^{m})=F({\mathbb{R}}^{m},\mu)
be a
{\beta}
-seminormed space,

{\beta}\in(0,1]
,
of functions from
S_{\mu}({\mathbb{R}}^{m})
.
We assume that the
{\beta}
-seminorm of
F({\mathbb{R}}^{m})
possesses
properties of monotonicity and
\boldsymbol{{\omega}}
-continuity.

Definition 6.1
.

We say that the
{\beta}
-seminorm
\|\cdot\|_{F({\mathbb{R}}^{m})}
is

monotone
if relations

g\in S_{\mu}({\mathbb{R}}^{m}),\,f\in F({\mathbb{R}}^{m})
,
and
|g(x)|\leq|f(x)|,\,x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
, imply

g\in F({\mathbb{R}}^{m})
and

\|g\|_{F({\mathbb{R}}^{m})}\leq\|f\|_{F({\mathbb{R}}^{m})}
.

Given a
\mu
-measurable set
{\Omega}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
and a space

F({\mathbb{R}}^{m})
,
we define a space
F({\Omega})
of the restrictions

f\left|{}_{\Omega}\right.

of functions
f\in S_{\mu}({\mathbb{R}}^{m})
to
{\Omega}
with the finite

{\beta}
-seminorm
\|f\left|{}_{\Omega}\right.\|_{F({\Omega})}:=\|f\cdot\chi({\Omega})\|_{F({\mathbb{R}}^{m})}
,
where
\chi({\Omega})
is the characteristic function of
{\Omega}
.
If there is no confusion, we use the notation
f

instead of
f\left|{}_{\Omega}\right.
.
For a monotone
{\beta}
-seminorm
\|\cdot\|_{F({\mathbb{R}}^{m})}
,

\|f\|_{F({\Omega})}\leq\|f\|_{F({\mathbb{R}}^{m})},\qquad f\in F({\mathbb{R}}^{m}),

(6.1)

so if

f\in F({\mathbb{R}}^{m})
, then
f=f\left|{}_{\Omega}\right.\in F({\Omega})
.
In a sense, the next definition allows us to reverse
the inequality sign in (
6.1
).

Definition 6.2
.

We say that the monotone
{\beta}
-seminorm
\|\cdot\|_{F({\mathbb{R}}^{m})}
is

\boldsymbol{{\omega}}
-
continuous
if for any increasing sequence of
\mu
-measurable
sets
\boldsymbol{{\omega}}:=\{{\Omega}_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}

in
{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
with
\cup_{n=1}^{\infty}{\Omega}_{n}={\mathbb{R}}^{m}
,
the following relation is valid:

\lim_{n\to{\infty}}\|f\|_{F({\Omega}_{n})}=\|f\|_{F({\mathbb{R}}^{m})},\qquad f\in F({\mathbb{R}}^{m}).

(6.2)

A typical example of a space
with a monotone and

\boldsymbol{{\omega}}
-continuous seminorm is
the seminormed space
C_{0}({\mathbb{R}}^{m})
of all complex-valued
continuous functions
f
on
{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
with the ”point” seminorm

\|f\|_{C_{0}({\mathbb{R}}^{m})}:=|f(0)|
.
For a compact set
{\Omega}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{m},\,0\in{\Omega},

we can also define the seminormed space
C_{0}({\Omega})
.
We use these seminorms in Examples
7.3
and
7.4
.

All spaces but
C_{0}(\Omega)
that we discuss in Chapter
7

are normed or
{\beta}
-normed.
Examples of spaces with monotone and

\boldsymbol{{\omega}}
-continuous norms include certain
Orlicz, Lorentz, and Marcinkiewicz spaces (see, e.g.

[
54
, Sect. 4.3]
). In particular, normed
separable spaces
F({\mathbb{R}}^{m})
or normed spaces,
satisfying the Fatou condition (named C-condition in

[
54
, Sect. 4.3]
), have
\boldsymbol{{\omega}}
-continuous norms.

A typical example of a space with a monotone and

\boldsymbol{{\omega}}
-continuous
{\beta}
-norm is
the space
L_{r,\mu}({\mathbb{R}}^{m}),r\in(0,{\infty}]
,
of all
\mu
-measurable complex-valued functions
f

on
{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
with the finite
{\beta}
-norm

\|f\|_{L_{r,\mu}({\mathbb{R}}^{m})}:=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\left(\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{m}}|f(x)|^{r}d\mu(x)\right)^{1/r},&0<r<{\infty},\\
\esssup_{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}}|f(x)|,&r={\infty},\end{array}\right.

(6.3)

where
{\beta}=\min\{1,r\}
.
For a
\mu
-measurable set
{\Omega}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
, we also use
the
{\beta}
-normed space
L_{r,\mu}({\Omega})
(
r\in(0,{\infty}],\,{\beta}=\min\{1,r\}
).

In all examples of Chapter
7
,

\mu
is a weighted measure on
{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
, i.e.,

d\mu(x)=W(x)dx,\,x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
, where
W
is a locally Lebesgue-integrable
weight on
{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
.
In particular, we use the weighted measure with a univariate
power weight

W(x)=|x|^{2\nu+1}
in Example
7.4
.
In case of the Lebesgue measure
\mu
(i.e.,
W=1
), we use the notation

L_{r}({\mathbb{R}}^{m})
and
L_{r}({\Omega}),\,r\in(0,{\infty}]
, in Examples

7.2
,
7.3
,
7.10
,
7.11
,

7.17
, and
7.18
.

In Example
7.12
we use a univariate exponential weight

W(x)=W_{r}(x):=\exp(-rQ(x)),\,r\in(0,{\infty})
,
where
Q:{\mathbb{R}}^{1}\to[0,{\infty})
is an even differentiable
convex function (see, e.g.,
[
60
,
36
]

for applications of
W_{r}
in analysis).
A typical example of
W_{r}
is
a Freud weight
W_{r,{\alpha}}(x):=\exp(-r|x|^{\alpha})),\,{\alpha}>1
.
The corresponding
{\beta}
-norm is a version of (
6.3
)
in the following form:

\|f\|_{L_{r}(W_{r},{\mathbb{R}}^{1})}:=\left(\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{1}}|f(x)\exp(-Q(x))|^{r}dx\right)^{1/r},\qquad 0<r<{\infty},

(6.4)

where
{\beta}=\min\{1,r\}
.
An extension of (
6.4
) to
r={\infty}
is given by

\|f\|_{L_{{\infty}}(W_{\infty},{\mathbb{R}}^{1})}:=\esssup_{x\in R^{1}}|f(x)\exp(-Q(x))|.

(6.5)

The space
L_{r}(W_{r},{\mathbb{R}}^{1})
(
r\in(0,{\infty}],\,{\beta}=\min\{1,r\}
) is the space
of all Lebesgue-measurable complex-valued functions
f

on
{\mathbb{R}}^{1}
with the finite
monotone and

\boldsymbol{{\omega}}
-continuous
{\beta}
-norms (
6.4
) and (
6.5
).
Note that norm (
6.5
) is different, compared with
norm (
6.3
) for
r={\infty}
.

Given
a>0
, the space of all functions
f
from
L_{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{m})

that are
2\pi a
-periodic in each variable is denoted by

\tilde{L}_{{\infty},2\pi a,m}
with the norm

\|f\|_{\tilde{L}_{{\infty},2\pi a,m}}=\|f\|_{L_{\infty}(R^{m})}=\|f\|_{L_{\infty}(Q^{m}_{\pi a})}
(see Example
7.17
).

6.3.
Function classes

In the capacity of sets
B
and

B_{n}
from Sections
3
,
4
, and
5
,
we use the following function classes.

Let
{\mathcal{P}}_{n,m}
be a set of all
polynomials
P(y)=\sum_{|{\beta}|\leq n}c_{\beta}y^{\beta}

in
m
variables of degree at most
n,\,n\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{1}_{+}
,
with complex coefficients.
Given
a>0
, the set of all trigonometric polynomials

Q(x)=\sum_{k\in aV\cap{\mathbb{Z}}^{m}}c_{k}e^{(ik)\cdot x}
with complex
coefficients is denoted by
{\mathcal{T}}_{aV}

and the set of all trigonometric polynomials

T(x)=\sum_{k\in aV\cap{\mathbb{Z}}^{m}}c_{k}e^{(ik)\cdot(x/a)}
with complex
coefficients is denoted by
{\mathcal{T}}_{a,V}
.

Definition 6.3
.

We say that an entire function
f:{\mathbb{C}}^{m}\to{\mathbb{C}}^{1}
has exponential type
V

if for any
{\varepsilon}>0
there exists a constant
C_{0}({\varepsilon},f)
such that
for all
z\in{\mathbb{C}}
,

|f(z)|\leq C_{0}({\varepsilon},f)\exp\left((1+{\varepsilon})\|z\|_{V}^{*}\right)
.

The set of all entire function of exponential type
V
is denoted
by
B_{V}
.
The set
B_{V}
was defined by Stein and Weiss

[
75
, Sect. 3.4]
.
For
V=\Pi^{m}_{\sigma},\,V=Q^{m}_{M},
and
V={\mathfrak{B}}^{m}_{M}
,
similar
sets of entire functions were
defined by Bernstein
[
14
]
and Nikolskii

[
68
]
,
[
69
, Sects. 3.1, 3.2.6]
, see also

[
24
, Definition 5.1]
.
Properties of functions from
B_{V}
have been investigated in numerous
publications (see, e.g.,
[
14
,
68
,
69
,
75
,
65
,
29
,
30
,
33
]
and
references therein).

Throughout the remaining sections of the paper,
if no confusion may occur, the same notation
is applied to functions

f\in B_{V},\,f\in{\mathcal{T}}_{aV},\,f\in{\mathcal{T}}_{a,V}
, and
f\in{\mathcal{P}}_{n,m}

and their restrictions to
{\mathbb{R}}^{m}

or to a subset
{\Omega}
of
{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
(e.g., in the form

f\in B_{V}\cap L_{p}({\mathbb{R}}^{m}))
.
In addition,
{\mathcal{T}}_{aV}\subset B_{aV}
and

{\mathcal{T}}_{a,V}\subset B_{V}
.

Let
M_{C,N}
be the set of all Lebesgue measurable functions
f
,
satisfying the inequality

\displaystyle|f(x)|\leq C(1+|x|)^{N}

for a. a.
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
,
where
C>0
and
N\geq 0
are constants independent of
x
.
In particular, we discuss classes
{\mathcal{T}}_{a,V}\cap M_{C,N}

in Examples
7.10
and
7.17
.

In addition, let
{\omega}:[0,{\infty})\to[0,{\infty})
be a modulus of continuity,
i.e.,
{\omega}(0)=0
and
{\omega}
is a continuous, nondecreasing, and
subadditive function on
[0,{\infty})
.
Next, we define the class
H_{\omega}(\Omega)

of all functions
f
on
\Omega\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
,
satisfying the inequality

\displaystyle|f(x)-f(y)|\leq{\omega}(|x-y|),\qquad x\in\Omega,\quad y\in\Omega.

We also need a subclass

H_{\omega,0}({\mathbb{R}}^{m}):=\{f\in H_{\omega}({\mathbb{R}}^{m}):f(0)=0\}
of
H_{\omega}({\mathbb{R}}^{m})
.
Note that

H_{\omega,0}({\mathbb{R}}^{m})\subseteq M_{{\omega}(2),1}.

(6.6)

Indeed, if
f\in H_{\omega,0}({\mathbb{R}}^{m})
, then

|f(x)|\leq{\omega}(|x|),\,x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
. Next,

\max_{\tau\in[0,2]}{\omega}(\tau)={\omega}(2)
, and
if
2^{k}<\tau\leq 2^{k+1},\,k\in{\mathbb{N}}
,
then
{\omega}(\tau)\leq{\omega}(2^{k+1})<\tau{\omega}(2)
. Therefore,

|f(x)|\leq{\omega}(2)(1+|x|),\,x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
,
and (
6.6
) holds true.

In case of
{\omega}(\tau)=\tau^{\lambda},\,\tau\in[0,{\infty}),\,0<{\lambda}\leq 1
, the class

H^{\lambda}(\Omega):=H_{\omega}(\Omega)
is called the Hölder class.
For
m=1
we also define the class
W^{s}H_{\omega}({\mathbb{R}}^{1})

of all
s
-differentiable functions on
{\mathbb{R}}^{1}
with

f^{(s)}\in H_{\omega}({\mathbb{R}}^{1}),\,s\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{1}_{+}
.

We also recall that the error of best approximation of

{\varphi}\in F({\Omega})
by elements from a subspace
B
of
F({\Omega})

is defined by

E({\varphi},B,F({\Omega})):=\inf_{f\in B}\|{\varphi}-f\|_{F({\Omega})}

(6.7)

(see (
2.9
)).

Certain properties of functions from defined classes
are discussed below. We first prove multivariate versions
of two known univariate results
(see, e.g.,
[
78
, Sects. 4.10.2 and 2.6.21]
).

Lemma 6.4
.

If a function
f\in B_{V}
has a period of
2\pi a
in each variable,
then
f\in{\mathcal{T}}_{a,V},\,a>0
.

Proof.

The entire function
f
coincides with its Fourier series

\sum_{k\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{m}}c_{k}e^{(ik)\cdot(x/a)}
.
Since
f\in L_{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{m})
,
this function is a tempered distribution on
{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
.
Then by the generalized Paley-Wiener theorem
[
65
, p. 13]
,
the support of the Fourier transform of
f
is a subset of
V
.
So
c_{k}=0
for
k\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{m}\setminus aV
.
\Box

Lemma 6.5
.

If
{\varphi}\in\tilde{L}_{{\infty},2\pi a,m}
, then

E({\varphi},B_{V},L_{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{m}))=E({\varphi},{\mathcal{T}}_{a,V},L_{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{m})).

(6.8)

Proof.

Since
{\mathcal{T}}_{a,V}\subseteq B_{V}
, we have

E({\varphi},B_{V},L_{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{m}))\leq E({\varphi},{\mathcal{T}}_{a,V},L_{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{m})).

(6.9)

Next given
{\varepsilon}\in(0,{\infty})
, let
g_{\varepsilon}\in B_{V}\cap L_{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{m})

satisfy the relation

\|{\varphi}-g_{\varepsilon}\|_{L_{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{m})}<E({\varphi},B_{V},L_{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{m}))+{\varepsilon}.

(6.10)

Then the function

\displaystyle G_{N}(x):=(2N+1)^{-m}\sum_{k\in Q_{N}^{m}}g_{\varepsilon}(x+2\pi ak),\qquad N\in{\mathbb{N}},

belongs to
B_{V}
and by (
6.10
),

\|{\varphi}-G_{N}\|_{L_{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{m})}<E({\varphi},B_{V},L_{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{m}))+{\varepsilon}.

(6.11)

Moreover,

\sup_{N\in{\mathbb{N}}}\|G_{N}\|_{L_{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{m})}\leq\|g_{\varepsilon}\|_{L_{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{m})}
.
Applying the compactness theorem
(see
[
42
, Lemma 2.3]
) to the sequence

\{G_{N}\}_{N=1}^{\infty}
we see that there exist a sequence
of natural numbers
\{N_{s}\}_{s=1}^{\infty}
and a function

G_{0}\in B_{V}
such that

\lim_{s\to{\infty}}G_{N_{s}}(x)=G_{0}(x)

(6.12)

uniformly on any compact
K\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
. Then
(
6.11
) and (
6.12
) imply that

\|{\varphi}-G_{0}\|_{L_{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{m})}\leq E({\varphi},B_{V},L_{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{m}))+{\varepsilon}.

(6.13)

Next, denoting by
e_{j}
the
j
th unit vector
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{m},\,1\leq j\leq m
,
we have

\max_{1\leq j\leq m}\left\|G_{N}(\cdot+2\pi ae_{j})-G_{N}(\cdot)\right\|_{L_{\infty}(R^{m})}\leq C_{7}/N,

(6.14)

where a constant
C_{7}>0
is independent of
N
. Setting
N=N_{s}
and
letting
s\to{\infty}
in (
6.14
),
we see from (
6.12
) and (
6.14
)
that
G_{0}
is
2\pi a
-periodic
in each variable.
Therefore,
G_{0}\in{\mathcal{T}}_{a,V}
by Lemma
6.4
,
and it follows from (
6.13
) that

E({\varphi},{\mathcal{T}}_{a,V},L_{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{m}))\leq\|{\varphi}-G_{0}\|_{L_{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{m})}\leq E({\varphi},B_{V},L_{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{m}))+{\varepsilon}.

(6.15)

Letting
{\varepsilon}\to 0+
in (
6.15
) and taking account of
(
6.9
), we arrive at (
6.8
).
\Box

We also need the following extension theorem.

Lemma 6.6
.

Given function
f:Q_{M}^{m}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{1},\,M>0
, there exists a function

\tilde{f}:{\mathbb{R}}^{m}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{1}
such that
\tilde{f}=f
on
Q_{M}^{m}
and

\tilde{f}
is
4M
-periodic in each variable. In addition,
if
f\in H_{\omega}(Q_{M}^{m})
, then
\tilde{f}\in H_{\omega}({\mathbb{R}}^{m})
.

Proof.

Let us set

h_{p}(x):=(-1)^{k_{p}}(x_{p}-2Mk_{p}),\quad x\in Q_{M}^{m}+2Mk,\quad 1\leq p\leq m,\quad k=(k_{1},\ldots,k_{m})\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{m}.

(6.16)

Then
h(x)=(h_{1}(x),\ldots,h_{m}(x)):{\mathbb{R}}^{m}\to Q_{M}^{m}

is a vector-valued function. Indeed, this statement is obviously
true for any interior point
x
of
Q_{M}^{m}+2Mk,\,k\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{m}
.
Let
x
belong to the boundary of
Q_{M}^{m}+2Mk,\,k\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{m}
.
Then without loss of generality we can assume that
there exists
s\in{\mathbb{N}},\,1\leq s\leq m
, such that

\displaystyle x_{p}=-M+2Mk_{p}=M+2M(k_{p}-1),\qquad 1\leq p\leq s,

\displaystyle x_{p}=x_{p}^{*}+2Mk_{p},\qquad|x_{p}^{*}|<M,\qquad s+1\leq p\leq m.

It follows from (
6.16
) that
h(x_{p})=(-1)^{k_{p}+1}M,\,1\leq p\leq s
, and
h(x_{p})

for
s+1\leq p\leq m
is uniquely defined by (
6.16
)
as well. Therefore,
h
is a function.

Next,
h
is
4M
-periodic in each variable.
Indeed, if
x\in Q_{M}^{m}+2Mk,\,k\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{m}
, then for every

l=(l_{1},\ldots,l_{m})\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{m}
,

\displaystyle h_{p}(x+4Ml)=(-1)^{k_{p}+2l_{p}}(x_{p}+4Ml_{p}-2M(k_{p}+2l_{p}))=h_{p}(x),\qquad 1\leq p\leq m.

In addition,
h(x)=x
on
Q_{M}^{m}
. Then

|h(x)-h(x^{*})|\leq|x-x^{*}|,\,x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m},\,x^{*}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}.

Indeed, it suffices to show that
y_{p}=h_{p}(x_{p})\in H^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{1}),\,1\leq p\leq m
.
Since
y_{p}
is
4M
-periodic on
{\mathbb{R}}^{1}
and the curve has
the slope of
1
on
[-M,M]
and the slope of
-1
on

[-2M,-M]
and
[M,2M]
, we see that
y_{p}\in H^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{1}),\,1\leq p\leq m
.

Finally, the function
\tilde{f}(x):=f(h(x))
satisfies
all conditions of the lemma.
\Box

Note that for
m=1
this result can be found in

[
78
, Sect. 3.5.71]
,
while for
m>1
Shvedov
[
73
, Proof of Theorem 4]

presented a geometric construction of
\tilde{f}

with no proof provided.

.

(i) If

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to{\infty}}\|R({\mathbb{R}}^{m},{\Omega}_{n})\,(h)\|_{F({\Omega}_{n})}=\|h\|_{F({\mathbb{R}}^{m})}.

In addition, for a set
A

Throughout the section

{\mathbb{R}}^{m}

7.1.
Inequalities for Approximating Elements
(Problem A)

Here, we discuss a special case of Corollary
5.1
and
three examples.

General result.

We assume that all assumptions of Section
5.1
are held
in the following special cases.
First,
we assume that
F^{(j)}=F^{(j)}({\mathbb{R}}^{m})=F^{(j)}({\mathbb{R}}^{m},\mu)
is
a
{\beta}_{j}
-seminormed functional space
with the monotone and

\displaystyle=\sup_{f\in H_{\omega}({\mathbb{R}}^{m})\cap L_{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{m})}E\left(f,B_{V}\cap L_{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{m}),L_{\infty}\left({\mathbb{R}}^{m}\right)\right).

(7.59)

The corresponding values of the sharp constants
in (
7.17
) were found only for
m=1

and for a concave modulus of continuity
{\omega}

(see
[
56
,
26
]
).
For the Hölder class
H^{\lambda}({\mathbb{R}}^{m})=H_{\omega}({\mathbb{R}}^{m})
with

{\omega}(\tau)=\tau^{\lambda},\,\tau\in(0,{\infty}),\,0<{\lambda}\leq 1
,
(
7.17
) immediately implies the relation

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to{\infty}}n^{\lambda}\sup_{f\in H^{\lambda}({\mathbb{R}}^{m})\cap\tilde{L}_{{\infty},2\pi,m}}E\left(f,{\mathcal{T}}_{nV},L_{\infty}\left(Q_{\pi}^{m}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\sup_{f\in H^{\lambda}({\mathbb{R}}^{m})\cap L_{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{m})}E\left(f,B_{V}\cap L_{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{m}),L_{\infty}\left({\mathbb{R}}^{m}\right)\right).

A more precise result with

H^{\lambda}({\mathbb{R}}^{m})\cap L_{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{m})
replaced by
H^{\lambda}({\mathbb{R}}^{m})

was obtained in
[
31
, Theorem 5.2]
.
The corresponding univariate result was proved by Bernstein

[
9
]
. Bernstein
[
12
]
attempted
to extend this result to the class
W^{s}H^{\lambda}({\mathbb{R}}^{1})

but his proof was incomplete (see also Example
7.18
).

Example 7.18
.

(Periodic approximation on
W^{s}H_{\omega}({\mathbb{R}}^{1})

The author

[

]
,

[

Remark 7.19
.

It appears that the most challenging and complicated
part of condition checking is checking Condition C3)
and its versions C3*), C3.1), C3.1.1), C3.2*), and
C3.3).
Despite a number of various examples presented
in Section
7

\left\{f_{n}\right\}_{n=1}^{\infty}

of exponential type from

f

by multivariate trigonometric polynomials

7.2

7.10

[
31
]

Condition C3.1.1) for approximation

7.3

and
[
11
,
29
]

Weighted polynomial approximation to

with

[
