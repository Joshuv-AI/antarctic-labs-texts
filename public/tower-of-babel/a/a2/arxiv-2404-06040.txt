Abstract
Based on m m -fold integrated empirical measures, we study three new classes of goodness-of-fits tests, generalizing Anderson-Darling, Cramér-von Mises, and Watson statistics, respectively, and examine the corresponding limiting stochastic processes. The limiting null distributions of the statistics all lead to explicitly solvable cases with closed-form expressions for the corresponding Karhunen-Loève expansions and covariance kernels. In particular, the eigenvalues are shown to be 1 k ( k + 1 ) ⋯ ( k + 2 m − 1 ) \frac{1}{k(k+1)\cdots(k+2m-1)} for the generalized Anderson-Darling, 1 ( π ​ k ) 2 ​ m \frac{1}{(\pi k)^{2m}} for the generalized Cramér-von Mises, and 1 2 ​ π ​ ⌈ k / 2 ⌉ 2 ​ m \frac{1}{2\pi\lceil k/2\rceil^{2m}} for the generalized Watson statistics, respectively. The infinite products of the resulting moment generating functions are further simplified to finite ones so as to facilitate efficient numerical calculations. These statistics are capable of detecting different features of the distributions and thus provide a useful toolbox for goodness-of-fit testing.

Introduction

1.1
Scope and summary

For more than seven decades, empirical processes have been one of
the main tools used in understanding the behaviors of many
goodness-of-fit tests defined via empirical distributions. We
explore in this paper the usage of the less addressed

iterated empirical measures
in generalizing the classical
goodness-of-fit tests, focusing on the rarely available

explicitly solvable cases
.

Three classical goodness-of-fit statistics.

Assume that
\{X_{1},\ldots,X_{N}\}
is an independent and identically
distributed (i.i.d.) sequence of random variables with a common
cumulative distribution function
F
. In the one sample setting, the
goodness-of-fit test is formulated as a test for the hypotheses

H_{0}:F=F_{0}\ \ \mbox{against}\ \ H_{1}:F\neq F_{0}.

When
F_{0}
is a continuous distribution, we may assume, without loss
of generality, that
F_{0}(x)=x
, the uniform distribution on
[0,1]
.
Since the empirical distribution function
F_{N}(x):=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{1\leqslant i\leqslant N}\mathds{1}_{\{X_{i}\leqslant x\}}
provides a natural estimator of

F(x)
with
\mathds{1}_{\{\cdot\}}
the indicator function, a good means of
measuring the discrepancy between
F
and
F_{0}
is to compute the
integrated squared difference between
F_{N}
and
F_{0}
with a suitable
weight function. Among the large number of such statistics, three
simple, effective and widely used ones are: the Cramér-von Mises
statistic

\displaystyle\omega_{N}=N\int_{0}^{1}(F_{N}(x)-x)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x

(
Cramér, 1928
;
von Mises, 1931
;
Smirnov, 1937
)
, the Anderson-Darling
statistic

\displaystyle A_{N}=N\int_{0}^{1}\frac{(F_{N}(x)-x)^{2}}{x(1-x)}\,\mathrm{d}x

(
Anderson and Darling, 1952
)
, and Watson’s (
U^{2}
-) statistic

\displaystyle U_{N}=N\int_{0}^{1}\biggl(F_{N}(x)-x-\int_{0}^{1}(F_{N}(t)-t)\,\mathrm{d}t\biggr)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x

(
Watson, 1961
)
. For historical and technical reasons (some
explained below), these are generally regarded as the
de facto

standards in the area of quadratic statistics based on empirical
distribution functions. Briefly, the Anderson-Darling statistic
has more power than the Cramér-von Mises statistic in the tail area
of the distribution due specially to the choice of the variance-based
weight function
\mathrm{Var}(F_{N}(x))=\frac{x(1-x)}{N}
under the null
hypothesis
H_{0}
. On the other hand, Watson’s statistic is designed
for testing the uniformity of periodic data with period 1 so that

X_{i}+1\equiv X_{i}
.

In addition to integral-type goodness-of-fit statistics, the
literature also abounds with other types such as the maximum-type
statistics, one representative being the Kolmogorov-Smirnov statistic,
defined as the maximum of the integrand of the Cramér-von Mises
statistic:

D_{N}=\sqrt{N}\sup_{x\in[0,1]}|F_{N}(x)-x|.

Similarly, the maximum of the integrand of the Anderson-Darling
statistic can be defined. In particular, the maximizer or the point
maximizing the statistic of these maximum-type statistics indicates
more precisely how discrepant the empirical distribution to the null
distribution is. Such test statistics have a similar structure to
estimating the change-point in regression analysis on the unit
interval, where the maximizer is an estimate of the change-point. We
will discuss briefly this viewpoint again later.

Explicit Karhunen-Loève expansions.

An important property possessed by the three classical statistics is
their
explicitly solvable eigenstructures
in the corresponding
limiting Gaussian processes of the signed square-root of the
integrand. For example, the stochastic process arising from the
Anderson-Darling statistic, defined as the limit of the map

x\mapsto\sqrt{N}\frac{F_{N}(x)-x}{\sqrt{x(1-x)}},

under the null hypothesis
H_{0}
, has the limiting Gaussian process
(
B(x)
being a Brownian bridge)

x\mapsto A(x):=\frac{B(x)}{\sqrt{x(1-x)}},

which has an explicit eigenfunction expansion referred to as the
Karhunen-Loève (KL) expansion

A(x)=\sum_{k\geqslant 1}\frac{1}{\sqrt{\lambda_{k}}}\,f_{k}(x)\xi_{k};

here
\lambda_{k}^{-1}
are the eigenvalues with
\lambda_{k}:=k(k+1)
,

f_{k}
are eigenfunctions expressible in terms of the Legendre
polynomials, and
\xi_{k}
are i.i.d. random variables with the
standard normal distribution
N(0,1)
; see Theorem
2.3
for
a more precise formulation.

The corresponding eigenvalues for the other two classical statistics
are given by
\lambda_{k}^{-1}
with
\lambda_{k}=(\pi k)^{2}

(Cramér-von Mises) and
\lambda_{k}=2\pi\bigl\lceil\frac{k}{2}\bigr\rceil^{2}
(Watson),
respectively, and the eigenfunctions are both expressible in terms of
trigonometric functions. Such explicit expansions proved to be
advantageous for most qualitative and numerical purposes, notably in
computing the density or the tail probabilities of the limiting
distribution (of the statistic); see the discussions after
Theorem
2.4
and (
45
). Also the feature of a few
leading eigenfunctions
f_{1},f_{2},\ldots
can be used to determine
the statistical power of the goodness-of-fit under local alternatives
(
Durbin and Knott (1972)
; see also Sec.
2.5
). In the words of

Deheuvels and Martynov (2008)
:

Unfortunately, for most Gaussian processes of interest
with respect to statistics, the values of the
\lambda_{k}
’s are
unknown, even though their existence remains guaranteed …The practical application of this theory to statistics is
therefore limited to a small number of particular cases.
…

In addition to the three statistics, some other fortunate examples of
tests whose KL expansions are explicitly identified are collected in
Sec.
1.2
.

On the other hand, the “small ball asymptotics” in probability
represents another context that is closely connected to the study of
goodness-of-fit statistics of integral-type. As in the context here,
explicit eigenstructures prove useful and informative; see e.g.,

Chen and Li (2003)
,
Gao et al. (2003)
,
Nikitin and Pusev (2013)
, and the references
therein.

In general, the goodness-of-fit statistics of integral-type are
constructed as positive-definite quadratic forms of the empirical
measure
\,\mathrm{d}F_{N}(x)
. Thus weighted infinite sums of chi-square
random variables of the form

\sum_{k\geqslant 1}\frac{\xi_{k}^{2}}{\lambda_{k}},\quad\xi_{k}\sim N(0,1)\;\mbox{i.i.d.}

(1)

appear naturally as the limit laws. However, to determine the
eigenstructure, we are led to the boundary-value problem involving an
integral equation whose covariance kernel is often not exactly
solvable. For example,
Lockhart and Stephens (1998)
mentioned that the limiting
distribution of
Shapiro and Wilk (1965)
’s statistic was of the form
(
1
), but its weights remained unknown in general.

On the other hand, it is well-known that the decay order of the
eigenvalues is determined by the smoothness of the covariance kernel,
so that even no closed-form expressions are available, one can often
deduce the required properties from smoothness of the covariance
kernel; see
Chang and Ha (1999)
;
Cochran and Lukas (1988)
for more information.

the following explicit form for the moment generating function (MGF)

(2)

and for the three classical statistics, the corresponding MGFs can be
further simplified as follows.

These representations in lieu of the infinite products
(

Some integrated empirical measures or Gaussian processes
in the literature.

Goodness-of-fit tests based on the one-time integrated empirical
measures was proposed by
Henze and Nikitin (2000)
in a way different
from this paper. They obtained the asymptotic distribution in terms
of one-time integrated Brownian bridge and the corresponding
eigenvalues
\lambda_{k}
are shown to be connected to the solutions of
the equation
\tan\lambda^{\frac{1}{4}}+\tanh\lambda^{\frac{1}{4}}=0
, the
corresponding eigenfunctions being expressible in terms of
trigonometric and hyperbolic functions. Similarly, Watson-type tests
and two-sample tests based on the integrated empirical measures were
studied in
Henze and Nikitin (2002)
and in
Henze and Nikitin (2003)
,
respectively. Recently
Durio and Nikitin (2016)
pointed out that some
integrated goodness-of fit tests have good statistical power for
skew alternative.

In connection with ball probabilities of stochastic processes,

Gao et al. (2003)
showed that the eigenvalues
\lambda_{k}
associated
with the squared integral of the one-time integrated Brownian
motion are expressible in terms of the real zeros of the equation

\cosh\lambda^{\frac{1}{4}}+\sec\lambda^{\frac{1}{4}}=0
, and provided the
moment generating function explicitly. In the case of multi-fold
integrated Brownian motion,
Tanaka (2008)
illustrated the use of
Fredholm determinant approach to deriving the KL expansion; see also
Chap. 5 of
Tanaka (2017)
.

Integrated processes and change-point analysis in regression

The detection of change-points is of fundamental interest in
statistical data analyses. In a typical setting, the change-point
analysis aims at detecting the change of the mean in time series
data. For that purpose, the difference in the sample mean before and
after a candidate of change-point is used as a scan statistic.
However, the changes to be detected are not necessarily limited to
the mean parameter.
MacNeill (1978a)
;
MacNeill (1978b)
discussed the
change of parameters when a polynomial regression is used to fit the
time series data. He proposed test statistics based on the
accumulated (integrated) residuals, and derived their asymptotic
distributions in terms of functionals of a Brownian motion. The same
idea was also examined in
Hirotsu (1986)
;
Hirotsu (2017)
. In the analysis
of ordered categorical data, he proposed to classify rows and/or
columns by detecting change-points among the rows/columns. He pointed
out that sometimes the change is observed as the change of
convexity/concavity of response curves, and the accumulated statistic
is useful for such detection purposes. The integrated empirical
processes proposed here are regarded as generalizations of their
statistics.

Organization of the paper.

In Sec.
1.2
, we give a brief historical account
relating to the statistics we propose. In particular, some
goodness-of-fit statistics of integral-type with explicit
eigenstructure are gathered there. In Sec.
2
, a class of
generalized Anderson-Darling statistics is proposed. From the
limiting null distribution of the integrand of the statistics, we
define and study the (generalized) Anderson-Darling process, with its
KL expansion and the covariance kernel given in explicit forms.
Sections
3
and
4
then deal with the generalized
Watson and Cramér-von Mises statistics and the associated
processes, and discuss the corresponding KL expansions, the
covariance kernels, and eigenfunction expansions. We also simplify
the infinite-product representations in all cases of the moment
generating functions of the limiting distributions, and compare the
statistical power of the statistics by Monte Carlo simulations.

Test statistics with explicit KL expansions.

We list some examples connected to goodness-of-fit and related tests
whose eigenstructures are explicitly known; this list is not intended
to be exhaustive or comprehensive, but for easier comparison with our
results.

As already stated,
MacNeill (1978b)
;
MacNeill (1978a)
proposed test
statistics in terms of an integrated empirical measure to detect the
change points in polynomial regressions. The eigenstructure of the
limiting Gaussian process is explicitly given; in particular, the
eigenvalues are expressible in terms of the zeros of spherical Bessel
functions, and the eigenfunctions in terms of associated Legendre
polynomials.

Deheuvels (1981)
proposed a statistic for testing independence in

m
-variate observations. An explicit KL expansion for the process
defined as the limit of the integral test is derived. The eigenvalue
has multiple indices
\lambda_{k_{1},\ldots,k_{m}}=\frac{2^{m/2}}{k_{1}\cdots k_{m}\pi^{m}}
(
k_{j}\geqslant 1
) with the corresponding
eigenfunctions given by
\prod_{1\leqslant j\leqslant m}\sin(k_{j}\pi u_{j})
.

Deheuvels and Martynov (2003)
defined the weighted Wiener process and Brownian
bridge with weight function
\varphi(t)=t^{\beta}
(
\beta>-1
), and
obtained their Karhunen-Loève expansions in terms of the zeros of
Bessel functions. A multivariate extension with weight function

\varphi(t)=\prod_{i}t_{i}^{\beta_{i}}
was given in
Deheuvels (2005)
.
In testing independence for a bivariate survival function,

Deheuvels and Martynov (2008)
proved that under the null hypothesis, the
proposed test statistic has an explicit Karhunen-Loève expansion
with eigenvalues
\frac{1}{k(k+1)(k+2)(k+3)}
(
k\geqslant 1
) and
eigenfunctions in terms of the modified Jacobi polynomials. Note that
this sequence of eigenvalues is the same as our generalized
Anderson-Darling statistic when
m=2
; see Table

1.1
.

Pycke (2003)
studied an
m
-variate extension of the
Anderson-Darling statistic. The eigenvalues now have the form

\lambda_{k}=\frac{m}{k(k+1)}
(
k\geqslant 1
) and the corresponding
eigenfunctions are expressible using the associated Legendre function.

Baringhaus and Henze (2008)
proposed a class of goodness-of-fit
statistics for exponentiality. The KL expansion appearing in the
limit law has eigenvalues in terms of the zeros of the Bessel
functions with Bessel eigenfunctions.

The generalized Anderson-Darling statistics

In this section, we propose our new generalized Anderson-Darling
statistics and examine their small-sample and large-sample
properties in detail.

2.1
Legendre polynomials and the template functions

We construct first the template functions needed using Legendre
polynomials.

Construction of the template functions.

The original Anderson-Darling statistic (
4
) can be written as

A_{N}=\int_{0}^{1}\frac{B_{N}(x)^{2}}{x(1-x)}\,\mathrm{d}x,\quad\mbox{where }\ B_{N}(x)=\sqrt{N}(F_{N}(x)-x).

(4)

Here we express
B_{N}(x)
in terms of a
template function

\tau_{1}(t;x)
:

B_{N}(x)=\sqrt{N}\int_{0}^{1}\tau_{1}(t;x)\,\mathrm{d}F_{N}(t),\quad\mbox{where }\ \tau_{1}(t;x):=\mathds{1}_{\{t\leqslant x\}}-x,

(5)

with
\mathds{1}_{\{t\leqslant x\}}=1
if
t\leqslant x
, and
0
otherwise.

The template function
t\mapsto\tau_{1}(t;x)
is a piecewise constant
function for
t\in[0,1]
with a breakpoint at
x
, and orthogonal to
any constant function, namely,
\int_{0}^{1}\tau_{1}(t;x)\,\mathrm{d}t=0
.

To extend this template function, we define
\tau_{2}(t;x)
for

t\in[0,1]
to be a function such that it is a continuous and
piecewise linear function with a change point at
x
, and orthogonal
to any linear function, namely,
\int_{0}^{1}\tau_{2}(t;x)(at+b)\,\mathrm{d}t=0
for all
a,b\in\mathbb{R}
.

In general, we define the
m
-th template function
\tau_{m}(t;x)
for

t\in[0,1]
to be a
C^{m-2}
and piecewise polynomial function of
degree
m-1
in
t
with a turning point at
x
, and orthogonal to any
polynomial function of degree
\leqslant m-1
, namely,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\tau_{m}(t;x)t^{j}\,\mathrm{d}t=0\qquad(j=0,1,\dots,m-1).

(6)

Definition 2.1

(Legendre polynomials
P_{k}(x)
)
.

The (shifted and
normalized) Legendre polynomials are defined to be the polynomials
of degree
k
satisfying the orthogonality relations

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}P_{k}(t)P_{l}(t)\,\mathrm{d}t=\mathds{1}_{\{k=l\}}\qquad(k,l\geqslant 0).

(7)

Note that the
P_{k}(x)
are nothing but the polynomials generated by
the Gram-Schdmit orthogonalization procedure in the unit interval
with the weight function
1
. They have the closed-form
expression

\displaystyle P_{k}(x)=\sqrt{2k+1}\sum_{0\leqslant l\leqslant k}\scalebox{0.8}{$\dbinom{k}{l}$}\scalebox{0.8}{$\dbinom{k+l}{l}$}(-1)^{k+l}x^{l},

(8)

and satisfy the relation
P_{k}(1-x)=(-1)^{k}P_{k}(x)
.
In particular,
P_{0}(x)=1
and

\displaystyle[x^{k}]P_{k}(x)=\scalebox{0.8}{$\dbinom{2k}{k}$}\sqrt{2k+1},

where
[x^{k}]f(x)
denotes the coefficient of
x^{k}
in the Taylor

f(x)
.

For convenience, define the
m

P_{k}(x)

\displaystyle P_{k}^{(-m)}(x)=\mathop{\int\cdots\int}_{0<t_{1}<\cdots<t_{m}<x}P_{k}(t_{1})\,\mathrm{d}t_{1}\cdots\,\mathrm{d}t_{m}=\int_{0}^{x}\frac{(x-t)^{m-1}}{(m-1)!}\,P_{k}(t)\,\mathrm{d}t.

(9)

The template functions we need are then given as follows.

Lemma 2.1
.

For
m\geqslant 1
and

, let

\displaystyle\tau_{m}(t;x)=\frac{(x-t)^{m-1}}{(m-1)!}\,\mathds{1}_{\{t\leqslant x\}}-\sum_{0\leqslant k<m}P_{k}(t)P_{k}^{(-m)}(x).

(10)

Then
\tau_{m}(t;x)\in C^{m-2}
for

, satisfies
(
6
) and is a piecewise polynomial function.

Proof.

Obviously, the template function
\tau_{m}
is of
C^{m-2}
. Only
(
6
) needs to be proved.
For that purpose, it suffices to show that

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{x}\tau_{m}(t;x)P_{j}(t)\,\mathrm{d}u=0\qquad(0\leqslant j\leqslant m-1).

(11)

By definition,

\int_{0}^{1}\tau_{m}(t;x)P_{j}(t)\,\mathrm{d}t=P_{j}^{(-m)}(x)-\sum_{0\leqslant k<m}P_{k}^{(-m)}(x)\int_{0}^{1}P_{j}(t)P_{k}(t)\,\mathrm{d}t,

which, by the orthogonality relation (
7
), yields
(
11
).
∎

2.2
The generalized Anderson-Darling statistic

With the template functions
\tau_{m}(t;x)
at hand, we now define,
similar to (
5
),

\displaystyle B^{[m]}_{N}(x)

\displaystyle:=\sqrt{N}\int_{0}^{1}\tau_{m}(t;x)\,\mathrm{d}F_{N}(t)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{N}\Biggl(\mathop{\int\cdots\int}_{0<t_{1}<\cdots<t_{m}<x}\,\mathrm{d}F_{N}(t_{1})\,\mathrm{d}t_{2}\cdots\,\mathrm{d}t_{m}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad-\sum_{0\leqslant k<m}P_{k}^{(-m)}(x)\int_{0}^{1}P_{k}(t)\,\mathrm{d}F_{N}(t)\Biggr).

(13)

Note that
B^{[1]}_{N}(x)=B_{N}(x)
.
Then, as in (
4
), we define
the generalized Anderson-Darling (GAD) statistic as

A^{[m]}_{N}:=\int_{0}^{1}\frac{B^{[m]}_{N}(x)^{2}}{(x(1-x))^{m}}\,\mathrm{d}x,

(14)

which equals the original Anderson-Darling statistic when
m=1
:

A^{[1]}_{N}=A_{N}
. However, unlike
A_{N}^{[1]}
, the variance of

B^{[m]}_{N}(x)
in (
13
) with
x
fixed is proportional to

(x(1-x))^{2m-1}
(see Theorem
2.2
), which is different
from the denominator
(x(1-x))^{m}
we used in (
14
) except
for the case
m=1
. The motivation of choosing such a weight will
become clear later.

On the other hand, it is less obvious whether the integral (
14
)
exists because of the seemingly singular factors in the denominator
at the end points. We now show that the GAD statistics
A^{[m]}_{N}

can be expressed in terms of the samples
X_{i}
’s,
1\leqslant i\leqslant N
.

Lemma 2.2
.

Assume
X_{i}\in(0,1)
for
1\leqslant i\leqslant N
. Then the GAD statistics

A^{[m]}_{N}
in (
14
) are well-defined for
m\geqslant 1
, and are
invariant with respect to the changes
X_{i}\mapsto 1-X_{i}
for all

i=1,\dots,N
.

Proof.

We first rewrite the template function
\tau_{m}(t;x)
. By
the completeness of the Legendre polynomial system and
the relation
P_{k}(1-x)=(-1)^{k}P_{k}(x)
,

\displaystyle\frac{(x-t)^{m-1}}{(m-1)!}

\displaystyle=\sum_{0\leqslant k<m}P_{k}(t)\biggl(\int_{x}^{1}+\int_{0}^{x}\biggr)\frac{(x-u)^{m-1}}{(m-1)!}P_{k}(u)\,\mathrm{d}u

\displaystyle=(-1)^{m-1}\sum_{0\leqslant k<m}P_{k}(1-t)P_{k}^{(-m)}(1-x)+\sum_{0\leqslant k<m}P_{k}(t)P_{k}^{(-m)}(x).

Substituting this into (
10
), we obtain

\displaystyle\tau_{m}(t;x)

\displaystyle=(-1)^{m-1}\sum_{0\leqslant k<m}P_{k}(1-t)P_{k}^{(-m)}(1-x)\mathds{1}_{\{t\leqslant x\}}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad-\sum_{0\leqslant k<m}P_{k}(t)P_{k}^{(-m)}(x)\mathds{1}_{\{t>x\}}.

(15)

Now expanding the square in (
14
) as a double integral, giving

A^{[m]}_{N}=\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}\biggl(\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\tau_{m}(t;x)\tau_{m}(u;x)}{(x(1-x))^{m}}\,\mathrm{d}x\biggr)\,\mathrm{d}F_{N}(t)\,\mathrm{d}F_{N}(u),

where the expression between the parentheses becomes, by
(
15
),

\displaystyle\sum_{0\leqslant k,l<m}P_{k}(1-t)P_{l}(1-u)\int_{t\vee u}^{1}\frac{P_{k}^{(-m)}(1-x)P_{l}^{(-m)}(1-x)}{(x(1-x))^{m}}\,\mathrm{d}x

(16)

\displaystyle-(-1)^{m-1}\sum_{0\leqslant k,l<m}P_{k}(1-t\wedge u)P_{l}(t\vee u)\int_{t\wedge u}^{t\vee u}\frac{P_{k}^{(-m)}(1-x)P_{l}^{(-m)}(x)}{(x(1-x))^{m}}\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle+\sum_{0\leqslant k,l<m}P_{k}(t)P_{l}(u)\int_{0}^{t\wedge u}\frac{P_{k}^{(-m)}(x)P_{l}^{(-m)}(x)}{(x(1-x))^{m}}\,\mathrm{d}x.

Since
P_{k}^{(-m)}(x)
and
P_{k}^{(-m)}(1-x)
contain factors of the
forms
x^{m+j}
and
(1-x)^{m+j}
for
j\geqslant 0
(see (
12
)),
respectively, the three integrals in (
16
) are
well-defined if
t\vee u<1
and
t\wedge u>0
. On the other hand,
(
16
) is invariant with respect to the change of
variables
(t,u)\mapsto(1-t,1-u)
.
∎

With more calculations, it is possible to derive a more precise
expression for
A_{N}^{[m]}
.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
a_{ij}=X_{i}\wedge X_{j}
and
b_{ij}=1-X_{i}\vee X_{j}
. Then

A_{N}^{[m]}=-\text{\scalebox{.9}{$\frac{1}{N}$}}\sum_{1\leqslant i,j\leqslant N}R_{m}(a_{ij},b_{ij})(\log a_{ij}+\log b_{ij})+\text{\scalebox{.9}{$\frac{1}{N}$}}\sum_{1\leqslant i,j\leqslant N}Q_{m}(a_{ij},b_{ij}),

(17)

where
Q_{m}(a,b)
and
R_{m}(a,b)
are symmetric polynomials in
a
and
b

of degree
2(m-1)
.

In particular,
R_{m}
is given explicitly by

\displaystyle R_{m}(a,b)

\displaystyle:=\frac{(1-a-b)^{m-1}}{\sqrt{2m-1}\,(m-1)!^{2}}\,P_{m}\left(\frac{-ab}{1-a-b}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{(-1)^{m-1}}{(m-1)!^{2}}\sum_{0\leqslant\ell<m}\binom{m-1}{\ell}\binom{m-1+\ell}{\ell}(ab)^{\ell}(1-a-b)^{m-1-\ell}.

(18)

But the expression for
Q_{m}
we obtained is very lengthy and
complicated. A proof of Lemma
2.3
is long and provided
in Appendix
A
.

In particular, the two statistics
A^{[1]}_{N}
and
A^{[2]}_{N}

have the following expressions in terms of the sample
X_{i}
’s. Let

a_{ij}=X_{i}\wedge X_{j}
and
b_{ij}=1-X_{i}\vee X_{j}
.

\displaystyle A^{[1]}_{N}

\displaystyle=\text{\scalebox{.9}{$\frac{1}{N}$}}\sum_{1\leqslant i,j\leqslant N}(-1-\log(1-a_{ij})-\log(1-b_{ij}))

\displaystyle=-N-\text{\scalebox{.9}{$\frac{1}{N}$}}\sum_{1\leqslant i\leqslant N}\bigl((2N-2i+1)\log(1-X_{(i)})+(2i-1)\log X_{(i)}\bigr),

where
X_{(1)}\leqslant\cdots\leqslant X_{(N)}
are the order statistics, and

\displaystyle A^{[2]}_{N}

\displaystyle=\text{\scalebox{.9}{$\frac{1}{N}$}}\sum_{1\leqslant i,j\leqslant N}\Bigl(\text{\scalebox{.9}{$\frac{1}{6}$}}(1+a_{ij}+b_{ij}+10a_{ij}b_{ij})

\displaystyle\qquad\quad+(1-a_{ij}-b_{ij}+2a_{ij}b_{ij})(\log(1-a_{ij})+\log(1-b_{ij}))\Bigr)

\displaystyle=\text{\scalebox{.9}{$\frac{1}{N}$}}\sum_{1\leqslant i,j\leqslant N}\Bigl(\text{\scalebox{.9}{$\frac{1}{6}$}}(2-|X_{i}-X_{j}|+10(X_{i}\wedge X_{j}-X_{i}X_{j}))

\displaystyle\qquad\quad+(|X_{i}-X_{j}|+2(X_{i}\wedge X_{j}-X_{i}X_{j}))\log(X_{i}\vee X_{j}-X_{i}X_{j})\Bigr).

A direct calculation of this double sum leads to quadratic time (in

N
) complexity. In terms of the order statistics, we can rearrange
the terms on the right-hand side and obtain a procedure with linear
time complexity:

\displaystyle A_{N}^{[2]}

\displaystyle:=\text{\scalebox{.9}{$\frac{1}{3}$}}N+\text{\scalebox{.9}{$\frac{1}{N}$}}\sum_{1\leqslant i\leqslant N}\Bigl({\text{\scalebox{.9}{$\frac{11}{3}$}}N-4i+2}\Bigr)X_{(i)}+\text{\scalebox{.9}{$\frac{2}{N}$}}\sum_{1\leqslant i\leqslant N}(N-i+X_{(i)})X_{(i)}\log X_{(i)}

\displaystyle\quad+\text{\scalebox{.9}{$\frac{2}{N}$}}\sum_{1\leqslant i\leqslant N}(i-X_{(i)})X_{(i)}\log(1-X_{(i)})+\text{\scalebox{.9}{$\frac{2}{N}$}}\Bigl({\sum_{1\leqslant i\leqslant N}X_{(i)}}\Bigr)\Bigl({\sum_{1\leqslant i\leqslant N}\log X_{(i)}}\Bigr)

\displaystyle\quad-\text{\scalebox{.9}{$\frac{5}{3N}$}}\Bigl({\sum_{1\leqslant i\leqslant N}X_{(i)}}\Bigr)^{2}-\text{\scalebox{.9}{$\frac{4}{N}$}}\Bigl({\sum_{1\leqslant i\leqslant N}X_{(i)}}\Bigr)\Bigl({\sum_{1\leqslant i\leqslant N}X_{(i)}\log X_{(i)}}\Bigr)

\displaystyle\quad+\text{\scalebox{.9}{$\frac{2}{N}$}}\sum_{1\leqslant i\leqslant N}(2X_{(i)}-1)(\log X_{(i)}-\log(1-X_{(i)}))\sum_{1\leqslant j<i}X_{(j)}.

Based on Lemma
2.3
, we now show that
A^{[m]}_{N}
can be
computed in time
O(N\log N)
.

Theorem 2.1
.

The GAD statistics
A_{N}^{[m]}
(
m\geqslant 1
) can be computed in
worst-case time complexity
O(N\log N)
. If in addition the
X_{i}
’s
are already sorted, then
A_{N}^{[m]}
can be computed in linear time.

Proof.

We may assume that the
X_{i}
’s are sorted in increasing order, for
otherwise, we can apply any standard sorting algorithm with
worst-case time complexity
O(N\log N)
. From the decomposition
(
17
), it suffices to examine sums of the form
(
a=1-X_{i}\wedge X_{j}
,
b=X_{i}\vee X_{j}
)

\displaystyle\sum_{1\leqslant i,j\leqslant N}(a^{k}b^{\ell}+a^{\ell}b^{k})

\displaystyle=2\sum_{1\leqslant i\leqslant N}X_{i}^{\ell}\sum_{1\leqslant j<i}(1-X_{j})^{k}+\sum_{1\leqslant i\leqslant N}X_{i}^{k}\sum_{1\leqslant j<i}(1-X_{j})^{\ell}

\displaystyle\qquad+\sum_{1\leqslant i\leqslant N}\bigl({(1-X_{i})^{k}X_{i}^{\ell}+(1-X_{i})^{\ell}X_{i}^{k}}\bigr)

Note that the sum

\displaystyle\sum_{1\leqslant i\leqslant N}X_{i}^{\ell}\sum_{1\leqslant j<i}(1-X_{j})^{k}

\displaystyle=\sum_{1\leqslant j\leqslant N}(1-X_{j})^{k}\sum_{j<i\leqslant N}X_{i}^{\ell}

\displaystyle=\sum_{1\leqslant j\leqslant N}(1-X_{j})^{k}\biggl({\sum_{1\leqslant i\leqslant N}X_{i}^{\ell}-\sum_{1\leqslant i<j}X_{i}^{\ell}-X_{j}^{\ell}}\biggr)

So all calculations can be reduced to computing sums of the forms

\sum_{1\leqslant i<j}X_{i}^{k}
, which can be pre-computed once and then
stored (in
O(N)
time). The computation of other sums of the forms

\sum_{1\leqslant i,j\leqslant N}a^{k}b^{\ell}\log a
and
\sum_{1\leqslant i,j\leqslant N}a^{k}b^{\ell}\log b
are similar. Except for sorting, all calculations can
be done in
O(N)
time. This proves the theorem.
∎

Sorting can be made more efficient on average if more
a priori

information of the data is known, for example, if
X_{i}
are regularly
distributed over the unit interval; see
(
Knuth, 1998
, Sec. 5.2.5)

or
Devroye (1986)
.

Remark 2.1
.

In the one-way ANOVA model,
Hirotsu (1986)
proposed two
statistics
\chi^{*2}
and
\chi^{\dagger 2}
for testing
the equality against the monotonicity of mean profile, and for
testing monotonicity of mean profile against the convexity,
respectively. These two tests correspond to
A^{[1]}_{N}
and

A^{[2]}_{N}
above.

2.3
The limiting GAD processes and their covariance
kernels

In this section, we examine the limiting distribution of the GAD
statistic under the null hypothesis
H_{0}
.

The limiting GAD processes.

Let
W(x)
be the Wiener
process on
[0,1]
, and let
B(x)=W(x)-xW(1)
be the Brownian
bridge. Define

\displaystyle B^{[m]}(x)

\displaystyle:=\int_{0}^{1}\tau_{m}(t;x)\,\mathrm{d}B(t)

\displaystyle=\mathop{\int\cdots\int}_{0<t_{1}<\cdots<t_{m}<x}B(t_{1})\,\mathrm{d}t_{1}\cdots\,\mathrm{d}t_{m-1}-\sum_{0\leqslant k<m}P_{k}^{(-m)}(x)\int_{0}^{1}P_{k}(t)\,\mathrm{d}B(t),

(19)

where
\tau_{m}
is defined in (
10
). Since the Brownian
bridge is continuous on the unit interval, namely,
B(\cdot)\in C^{0}[0,1]
, we see that
B^{[m]}(\cdot)\in C^{m-1}[0,1]
with
probability one.

The following lemma follows from a weak convergence argument in
L^{2}

(Sec. 1.8 and Example 1.8.6 of
van der Vaart and
Wellner (1996)
; see
also
Tsukuda and Nishiyama (2014)
).

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
\mu
be any finite measure on
[0,1]
. Then, as
N\to\infty
,

B^{[m]}_{N}(\cdot)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\to}}B^{[m]}(\cdot)
in

L^{2}([0,1],\mu)
and
A^{[m]}_{N}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\to}}A^{[m]}
, where

A^{[m]}=\int_{0}^{1}\frac{B^{[m]}(x)^{2}}{(x(1-x))^{m}}\,\mathrm{d}x.

(20)

Proof.

The stochastic map
x\mapsto B_{N}(x)=\sqrt{N}(F_{N}(x)-x)
converges
weakly to
B(\cdot)
in
L^{2}([0,1],\mu)
. Because of
\int_{0}^{1}P_{k}(t)\,\mathrm{d}B(t)=-\int_{0}^{1}P_{k}^{\prime}(t)B(t)\,\mathrm{d}t
,
B^{[m]}(\cdot)
in
(
19
) is continuous in
B(\cdot)
in
L^{2}([0,1],\mu)
.
Similarly,
A^{[m]}
is continuous in
B(\cdot)
. The convergences

B^{[m]}_{N}(\cdot)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\to}}B^{[m]}(\cdot)
and

A^{[m]}_{N}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\to}}A^{[m]}
then follow from the
continuous mapping theorem.
∎

We refer to the signed square-root of the integrand function in
(
20
)
x\mapsto\frac{B^{[m]}(x)}{(x(1-x))^{m/2}}
as the

generalized Anderson-Darling (GAD) processes
. We now clarify
the stochastic structure of such processes, starting first with
defining the required eigenfunctions, sketching a heuristic
derivation of the KL expansions and then proving rigorously all the
claims later.

Associated Legendre polynomials.

To pave way for deriving
the KL expansions, we introduce the following definition.

Definition 2.2
.

Define the normalized shifted associated Legendre
polynomials on
[0,1]
by

\displaystyle P^{[m]}_{k}(x):=(-1)^{m}\sqrt{\frac{(k-m)!}{(k+m)!}}\,(x(1-x))^{m/2}P_{k}^{(m)}(x),

(21)

where
P_{k}^{(m)}(x)
denotes the
m
-th derivative of
P_{k}(x)
with
respect to
x
.

For each
m
,
\{P^{[m]}_{k}(x)\}_{k\geqslant m}
forms an orthonormal
basis, that is,

\int_{0}^{1}P^{[m]}_{k}(x)P^{[m]}_{l}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x=\mathds{1}_{\{k=l\}}\qquad(k,l\geqslant 0).

These associated polynomials are connected to the integrated
Legendre polynomials
P_{k}^{(-m)}(x)
by the following relation.

Lemma 2.5
.

For
0\leqslant m\leqslant k

\displaystyle P_{k}^{(-m)}(x)=\sqrt{\frac{(k-m)!}{(k+m)!}}\,(x(1-x))^{m/2}P^{[m]}_{k}(x).

(22)

Proof.

The Legendre polynomial
P_{k}(x)
satisfies the following differential
equations, called Legendre’s differential equation when
m=1
.
For

1\leqslant m\leqslant k
,

\displaystyle\Bigl(\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x}\Bigr)^{m}\bigl({(x(1-x))^{m}P_{k}^{(m)}(x)}\bigr)-(-1)^{m}\frac{(k+m)!}{(k-m)!}P_{k}(x)=0.

(23)

To prove (
23
), let
q_{k-1}(x)
be an arbitrary polynomial
of degree
k-1
. We show that

\int_{0}^{1}q_{k-1}(x)\Bigl(\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x}\Bigr)^{m}\bigl({(x(1-x))^{m}P_{k}^{(m)}(x)}\bigr)\,\mathrm{d}x=0.

(24)

In fact, by integration by parts,

LHS of (
24
)

\displaystyle=(-1)^{m}\int_{0}^{1}q_{k-1}^{(m)}(x)(x(1-x))^{m}P_{k}^{(m)}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\Bigl(\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x}\Bigr)^{m}\bigl({q_{k-1}^{(m)}(x)(x(1-x))^{m}}\bigr)P_{k}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=0.

The last equality holds because the degree of the polynomial

\bigl(\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x}\bigr)^{m}\bigl({q_{k-1}^{(m)}(x)(x(1-x))^{m}}\bigr)
is less than or
equal to
k-1
. Since
q_{k-1}(x)
is arbitrary, there is a constant

M
such that

\Bigl(\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x}\Bigr)^{m}\bigl({(x(1-x))^{m}P_{k}^{(m)}(x)}\bigr)=MP_{k}(x).

By checking the leading term, we see that
M=(-1)^{m}\frac{(k+m)!}{(k-m)!}
. This proves (
23
), which,
together with the initial condition

\Bigl(\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x}\Bigr)^{j}\bigl({(x(1-x))^{m}P_{k}^{(m)}(x)}\bigr)\big|_{x=0}=0\qquad(0\leqslant j<m),

implies

(x(1-x))^{m}P_{k}^{(m)}(x)=(-1)^{m}\frac{(k-m)!}{(k+m)!}P_{k}^{(-m)}(x),

that is, (
22
) holds.
∎

Lemma
2.5
, coupling with (
21
) and (
22
),
yields

\int_{0}^{1}P_{k}^{(m)}(x)P_{\ell}^{(-m)}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x=(-1)^{m}\int_{0}^{1}P^{[m]}_{k}(x)P^{[m]}_{\ell}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x=(-1)^{m}\mathds{1}_{\{k=\ell\}},

(25)

for
0\leqslant m\leqslant\ell,k
.

Covariance kernel.

Let

K^{[m]}(x,y)=\mathrm{Cov}\biggl(\frac{B^{[m]}(x)}{(x(1-x))^{m/2}},\frac{B^{[m]}(y)}{(y(1-y))^{m/2}}\biggr)

be the covariance functions of the GAD processes.

Theorem 2.2
.

For
m\geqslant 1

\displaystyle K^{[m]}(x,y)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(m-1)!^{2}}\sum_{0\leqslant k<m}\frac{1}{m+k}\binom{m-1}{k}\frac{(x\wedge y-xy)^{m/2+k}|x-y|^{m-1-k}}{(x\vee y-xy)^{m/2}}.

(26)

When
x\neq y
, (
26
) can alternatively be expressed as

K^{[m]}(x,y)=\frac{(-1)^{m}}{(m-1)!^{2}}\,\text{Beta}_{m,m}\biggl(-\frac{x\wedge y-xy}{|x-y|}\biggr)\frac{|x-y|^{2m-1}}{(x(1-x)y(1-y))^{m/2}},

where
\text{Beta}_{a,b}
is the incomplete beta function with
parameters
(a,b)
. In particular, when
x=y
,

\displaystyle K^{[m]}(x,x)=\frac{(x(1-x))^{m-1}}{(2m-1)(m-1)!^{2}}.

The covariance functions
K^{[m]}(x,y)
for
m=1,2,3
are listed
as follows.

\displaystyle K^{[1]}(x,y)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{x\wedge y-xy}{x\vee y-xy}},

\displaystyle K^{[2]}(x,y)

\displaystyle=\frac{x\wedge y-xy}{x\vee y-xy}\cdot\frac{3(x\vee y)-(x\wedge y)+2xy}{6},

\displaystyle K^{[3]}(x,y)

\displaystyle=\frac{(x\wedge y-xy)^{\frac{3}{2}}}{(x\vee y-xy)^{\frac{3}{2}}}\biggl(\frac{\bigl({(x\vee y)-\frac{3}{4}xy}\bigr)^{2}}{12}+\frac{\bigl({(x\wedge y)+\frac{3}{2}xy}\bigr)^{2}}{120}-\frac{xy(3xy+8)}{192}\biggr).

Remark 2.2
.

Since
|x-y|=x\vee y-x\wedge y
and
xy=(x\wedge y)(x\vee y)
,

K^{[m]}(x,y)
in (
26
) is a function of
x\wedge y
and

x\vee y
. More precisely,
K^{[m]}(x,y)
is a symmetric function
in
x\wedge y
and
1-x\vee y
because of the invariance property
of
A^{[m]}_{N}
given in Lemma
2.2
.

We use a direct combinatorial proof, which is somewhat lengthy but
self-contained.

Proof of Theorem
2.2
.

It suffices to show that

\displaystyle\mathrm{Cov}\bigl({B^{[m]}(x),B^{[m]}(y)}\bigr)

\displaystyle\qquad=\frac{1}{(m-1)!^{2}}\sum_{0\leqslant k<m}\frac{1}{m+k}\binom{m-1}{k}(x\wedge y-xy)^{m+k}|x-y|^{m-1-k}.

Note that when
m=1
, the right-hand side becomes
x\wedge y-xy
,
which is the covariance function of the Brownian bridge. By definition

\displaystyle\mathrm{Cov}\bigl({B^{[m]}(x),B^{[m]}(y)}\bigr)

\displaystyle\qquad=\int_{0}^{1}\tau_{m}(t;x)\tau_{m}(t;y)\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\qquad=\int_{0}^{x\wedge y}\frac{(t-x)^{m-1}(t-y)^{m-1}}{(m-1)!^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}t-\sum_{0\leqslant k<m}P_{k}^{(-m)}(x)P_{k}^{(-m)}(y).

(27)

Without loss of generality, assume from now on
m\geqslant 1
and

0\leqslant x\leqslant y\leqslant 1
. Let

\displaystyle J_{m}(x,y):=\int_{0}^{x}\frac{(x-v)^{m-1}(y-v)^{m-1}}{(m-1)!^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}v-\sum_{0\leqslant k<m}P_{k}^{(-m)}(x)P_{k}^{(-m)}(y).

(28)

We show that

\displaystyle J_{m}(x,y)=\frac{(x(1-y))^{m}}{(m-1)!}\sum_{0\leqslant k<m}\frac{1}{m+k}\binom{m-1}{k}(x(1-y))^{k}(y-x)^{m-1-k},

(29)

starting with the first term on the right-hand side of (
28
),
which can be written as

\int_{0}^{x}\frac{(x-v)^{m-1}(y-v)^{m-1}}{(m-1)!^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}v=\frac{x^{m}}{(m-1)!^{2}}\int_{0}^{1}(1-v)^{m-1}(y-xv)^{m-1}\,\mathrm{d}v.

For the second term on the right-hand side of (
28
),
we have, by (
9
),

\displaystyle\sum_{0\leqslant k<m}P_{k}^{(-m)}(x)P_{k}^{(-m)}(y)

\displaystyle\qquad=\sum_{0\leqslant j,\ell<m}\frac{(-1)^{j+\ell}x^{m+j}{y^{m+\ell}}}{j!\ell!(m+j)!(m+\ell)!}\sum_{(j\vee\ell)\leqslant k<m}(2k+1)\frac{(k+j)!(k+\ell)!}{(k-j)!(k-\ell)!}.

By a simple induction on
m
, the inner sum equals

\sum_{(j\vee\ell)\leqslant k<m}(2k+1)\frac{(k+j)!(k+\ell)!}{(k-j)!(k-\ell)!}=\frac{(m+j)!(m+\ell)!}{(m-1-j)!(m-1-\ell)!(j+\ell+1)},

(30)

so that

\displaystyle\sum_{0\leqslant k<m}P_{k}^{(-m)}(x)P_{k}^{(-m)}(y)

\displaystyle\qquad=\sum_{0\leqslant j,\ell<m}\frac{(-1)^{j+\ell}x^{m+j}y^{m+\ell}}{j!\ell!(m-1-j)!(m-1-\ell)!(j+\ell+1)}

\displaystyle\qquad=\frac{1}{(m-1)!^{2}}\sum_{0\leqslant j,\ell<m}\binom{m-1}{j}\binom{m-1}{\ell}\frac{(-1)^{j+\ell}}{j+\ell+1}\,x^{m+j}y^{m+\ell}

\displaystyle\qquad=\frac{x^{m}y^{m}}{(m-1)!^{2}}\int_{0}^{1}(1-vx)^{m-1}(1-vy)^{m-1}\,\mathrm{d}v.

On the other hand,

\mbox{RHS of \eqref{Qmx}}=\frac{(x(1-y))^{m}}{(m-1)!^{2}}\int_{0}^{1}v^{m-1}(x(1-y)v+y-x)^{m-1}\,\mathrm{d}v.

Thus (
29
) will follow from the identity

\displaystyle\frac{x^{m}}{(m-1)!^{2}}\int_{0}^{1}(1-v)^{m-1}(y-xv)^{m-1}\,\mathrm{d}v

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad-\frac{x^{m}y^{m}}{(m-1)!^{2}}\int_{0}^{1}(1-vx)^{m-1}(1-vy)^{m-1}\,\mathrm{d}v

\displaystyle\qquad=\frac{(x(1-y))^{m}}{(m-1)!^{2}}\int_{0}^{1}v^{m-1}(x(1-y)v+y-x)^{m-1}\,\mathrm{d}v,

or, equivalently,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}v^{m-1}(y-x+xv)^{m-1}\,\mathrm{d}v-y^{m}\int_{0}^{1}(1-vx)^{m-1}(1-vy)^{m-1}\,\mathrm{d}v

(31)

\displaystyle=(1-y)^{m}\int_{0}^{1}v^{m-1}(x(1-y)v+y-x)^{m-1}\,\mathrm{d}v.

By multiplying both sides by
\frac{z^{m-1}}{(m-1)!}
and then
summing over all
m\geqslant 1
, it suffices only to prove that

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\bigl({e^{zv(y-x+xv)}-ye^{zy(1-vx)(1-vy)}-(1-y)e^{zv(x(1-y)v+y-x)}}\bigr)\,\mathrm{d}v=0.

(32)

Let

I(x):=\int_{0}^{x}e^{t^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}t,

which equals (up to a constant
\sqrt{\pi}/2
) the imaginary error
function. With this integral, all integrals in (
32
) are
of the form

\int_{0}^{1}e^{tv+wv^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}v=\frac{e^{-\frac{t^{2}}{4w}}}{\sqrt{w}}\biggl({I\biggl({\frac{t}{2\sqrt{w}}+\sqrt{w}}\biggr)-I\biggl({\frac{t}{2\sqrt{w}}}\biggr)}\biggr).

Applying this identity, we then have

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}e^{zv(y-x+xv)}\,\mathrm{d}v

\displaystyle\qquad=\frac{e^{-\frac{z(y-x)^{2}}{4x}}}{\sqrt{xz}}I\biggl({\frac{(x+y)\sqrt{z}}{2\sqrt{x}}}\biggr)-\frac{e^{-\frac{z(y-x)^{2}}{4x}}}{\sqrt{xz}}I\biggl({\frac{(y-x)\sqrt{z}}{2\sqrt{x}}}\biggr),

\displaystyle-y\int_{0}^{1}e^{zy(1-vx)(1-vy)}\,\mathrm{d}v

\displaystyle\qquad=-\frac{e^{-\frac{z(y-x)^{2}}{4x}}}{\sqrt{xz}}I\biggl({\frac{(y+x-2xy)\sqrt{z}}{2\sqrt{x}}}\biggr)+\frac{e^{-\frac{z(y-x)^{2}}{4x}}}{\sqrt{xz}}I\biggl({\frac{(y-x)\sqrt{z}}{2\sqrt{x}}}\biggr),

and

\displaystyle-(1-y)\int_{0}^{1}e^{zv({x(1-y)v+y-x})}\,\mathrm{d}v

\displaystyle\qquad=-\frac{e^{-\frac{z(y-x)^{2}}{4x}}}{\sqrt{xz}}I\biggl({\frac{(x+y)\sqrt{z}}{2\sqrt{x}}}\biggr)+\frac{e^{-\frac{z(y-x)^{2}}{4x}}}{\sqrt{xz}}I\biggl({\frac{(y+x-2xy)\sqrt{z}}{2\sqrt{x}}}\biggr).

Since the sum of the right-hand sides of the three relations is zero,
this proves (
32
) and thus (
31
).
∎

2.4
Eigenfunction expansions and KL expansions

Lemma 2.6
.

For
m\geqslant 1
, the following eigenequation

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}K^{[m]}(x,y)P^{[m]}_{k}(y)\,\mathrm{d}y=\frac{(k-m)!}{(k+m)!}P^{[m]}_{k}(x)\qquad(1\leqslant m\leqslant k)

(33)

holds.

Proof.

Because of (
21
) and (
22
), it suffices to show that

\int_{0}^{1}\mathrm{Cov}(B^{[m]}(x),B^{[m]}(y))P_{k}^{(m)}(y)\,\mathrm{d}y=(-1)^{m}P_{k}^{(-m)}(x).

By (
27
), we consider first the double integral

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}P_{k}^{(m)}(y)\int_{0}^{x\wedge y}\frac{(t-x)^{m-1}(t-y)^{m-1}}{(m-1)!^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}t\,\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle\qquad=\int_{0}^{x}\frac{(t-x)^{m-1}}{(m-1)!}\int_{t}^{1}\frac{(t-y)^{m-1}}{(m-1)!}P_{k}^{(m)}(y)\,\mathrm{d}y\,\mathrm{d}t.

Since the inner integral on the right-hand side is a polynomial in

t
of degree
k
, we have

\displaystyle\int_{t}^{1}\frac{(t-y)^{m-1}}{(m-1)!}P_{k}^{(m)}(y)\,\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle\qquad=\sum_{0\leqslant\ell\leqslant k}P_{\ell}(t)\int_{0}^{1}P_{\ell}(s)\int_{s}^{1}\frac{(s-y)^{m-1}}{(m-1)!}P_{k}^{(m)}(y)\,\mathrm{d}y\,\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\qquad=(-1)^{m-1}\sum_{0\leqslant\ell\leqslant k}P_{\ell}(t)\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{y}\frac{(y-s)^{m-1}}{(m-1)!}P_{\ell}(s)\,\mathrm{d}sP_{k}^{(m)}(y)\,\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle\qquad=(-1)^{m-1}\sum_{0\leqslant\ell\leqslant k}P_{\ell}(t)\int_{0}^{1}P_{\ell}^{(-m)}(y)P_{k}^{(m)}(y)\,\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle\qquad=-P_{k}(t),

where we used (
25
). Thus

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\mathrm{Cov}\bigl({B^{[m]}(x),B^{[m]}(y)}\bigr)P_{k}^{(m)}(y)\,\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle\qquad=\int_{0}^{x}\frac{(t-x)^{m-1}}{(m-1)!}\bigl(-P_{k}(t)\bigr)\,\mathrm{d}t-\sum_{0\leqslant\ell<m}P_{\ell}^{(-m)}(x)\int_{0}^{1}P_{\ell}^{(-m)}(y)P_{k}^{(m)}(y)\,\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle\qquad=(-1)^{m}P_{k}^{(-m)}(x).

This proves (
33
).
∎

Since Legendre polynomial system
\{P_{k}(t)\}_{k\geqslant 0}
is
complete, we have

\frac{(x-t)^{m-1}}{(m-1)!}\,\mathds{1}_{\{t\leqslant x\}}=\sum_{k\geqslant 0}P_{k}(t)\int_{0}^{x}\frac{(x-u)^{m-1}}{(m-1)!}P_{k}(u)\,\mathrm{d}u=\sum_{k\geqslant 0}P_{k}(t)P_{k}^{(-m)}(x).

Thus

\tau_{m}(t;x)=\sum_{k\geqslant m}P_{k}(t)P_{k}^{(-m)}(x),

(34)

which, together with Lemma
2.5
, yields

\frac{\tau_{m}(t;x)}{(x(1-x))^{m/2}}=\sum_{k\geqslant m}\sqrt{\frac{(k-m)!}{(k+m)!}}\,P_{k}(t)P^{[m]}_{k}(x)\qquad(m\geqslant 1).

(35)

Let

\xi_{k}=\int_{0}^{1}P_{k}(t)\,\mathrm{d}B(t)=\int_{0}^{1}P_{k}(t)\,\mathrm{d}W(t),

be a standard i.i.d. Gaussian sequence. Then
\mathbb{E}(\xi_{k}\xi_{l})=\int_{0}^{1}P_{k}(t)P_{l}(t)\,\mathrm{d}t=\mathds{1}_{\{k=l\}}
. By definition
(
13
) and (
35
), we expect that

\frac{B^{[m]}(x)}{(x(1-x))^{m/2}}=\sum_{k\geqslant m}\sqrt{\frac{(k-m)!}{(k+m)!}}\,P^{[m]}_{k}(x)\xi_{k}.

(36)

On the other hand, by (
33
), the following eigenfunction
expansion is also expected to be true

\displaystyle K^{[m]}(x,y)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\geqslant m}\frac{(k-m)!}{(k+m)!}\,P^{[m]}_{k}(x)P^{[m]}_{k}(y).

(37)

It then follows that

A^{[m]}=\sum_{k\geqslant m}\frac{(k-m)!}{(k+m)!}\,\xi_{k}^{2}.

(38)

We now justify all these expansions.

Theorem 2.3

(Expansions for
B^{[m]}(x)
,
K^{[m]}
and
A^{[m]}
)
.

If
m=1
, then the KL expansion (
36
) holds
uniformly in
x\in[\varepsilon,1-\varepsilon]
with probability
1
for any
\varepsilon>0
,
the eigenfunction expansion (
37
) holds uniformly in

x,y\in[\varepsilon,1-\varepsilon]
, and (
38
) converges with probability 1.

If
m\geqslant 2
, then (
36
) holds uniformly in
x\in[0,1]
with
probability 1, (
37
) holds uniformly in
x,y\in[0,1]
, and
(
38
) converges with probability 1.

Proof.

The results for
m=1
, namely, the original Anderson-Darling
statistic, are well-known;
see, for example, Chapter 5 of
Shorack and Wellner (1986)
and
Pycke (2003)
.

Assume now
m\geqslant 2
. Since
B^{[m]}(x)\in C^{m-1}[0,1]
and

\frac{\text{d}^{k}}{\text{d}x^{k}}B^{[m]}(x)\Bigl|_{x=0}=\frac{\text{d}^{k}}{\text{d}x^{k}}B^{[m]}(x)\Bigl|_{x=1}=0\qquad(0\leqslant k<m),

we define
\frac{B^{[m]}(x)}{(x(1-x))^{m/2}}
at the end points
x=0

and
1
to be
0
, so that
\frac{B^{[m]}(x)}{(x(1-x))^{m/2}}

becomes a continuous function in
x
on
[0,1]
. Also the covariance
function
K^{[m]}(x,y)
of the GAD process
\frac{B^{[m]}(x)}{(x(1-x))^{m/2}}
is continuous on
[0,1]\times[0,1]
by using
(
26
) of Theorem
2.2
.

Now, all required results will follow from standard arguments of
orthogonal decompositions of processes
(
Shorack and Wellner, 1986
, Ch. 5, Sec. 2)
. First, the kernel
K^{[m]}(x,y)
is symmetric
and positive-definite, and satisfies

\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}K^{[m]}(x,y)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}y<\infty.

Then, by Lemma
2.6
and Mercer’s theorem, (
37
)
holds uniformly in
x,y\in[0,1]
. On the other hand, by Kac and
Siegert’s theorem, the KL expansion (
36
) also holds for each

x\in[0,1]
in the mean-square convergence:

\mathbb{E}\Biggl(\frac{B^{[m]}(x)}{(x(1-x))^{m/2}}-\sum_{m\leqslant k\leqslant M}\sqrt{\frac{(k-m)!}{(k+m)!}}\,P^{[m]}_{k}(x)\xi_{k}\Biggr)^{2}\to 0\quad(M\to\infty).

Moreover, (
38
) holds with probability 1 (see Theorem 2 of

Shorack and Wellner (1986)
, pp. 210–211, for the details). The uniform
and almost sure convergence in (
36
) is a consequence of
Theorems of 3.7 and 3.8 of
Adler (1990)
; see also
Pycke (2003)
.
∎

2.5
Finite sample expansions and statistical power

The KL expansion (
36
) and the eigenfunction expansions
(
38
) describe the limiting distribution when the sample size

N
goes to infinity. In this section we obtain the counterparts of
these expansions when
N
is finite.
This is obtained by replacing

B(x)
by the “sample Brownian bridge”
B_{N}(x)=\sqrt{N}(F_{N}(x)-x)
.

First note that by direct calculations,
\tau_{m}(t;x)
in
(
10
) satisfies

\int_{0}^{1}\tau_{m}(t;x)P_{k}^{(m)}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x=(-1)^{m}P_{k}(t),

from which, together with the completeness of
P_{k}^{[m]}(x)
and
(
21
), we see that (
34
) holds for all
t
in

L^{2}(\,\mathrm{d}x|_{[0,1]})
. The integration with respect to the measure

\,\mathrm{d}B_{N}(x)
is nothing but a finite sum. Thus, substituting

t:=X_{i}
into (
34
), and summing up for
1\leqslant i\leqslant N
,
we have the sample version of the expansion

\frac{B^{[m]}_{N}(x)}{(x(1-x))^{m/2}}=\sum_{k\geqslant m}\sqrt{\frac{(k-m)!}{(k+m)!}}\,P^{[m]}_{k}(x)\,\widehat{\xi}_{k},

where

\widehat{\xi}_{k}=\int_{0}^{1}P_{k}(x)\,\mathrm{d}B_{N}(x)=\text{\scalebox{.9}{$\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}$}}\sum_{1\leqslant i\leqslant N}P_{k}(X_{i}).

This holds for each
(X_{i})_{1\leqslant i\leqslant N}\in[0,1]^{N}
in
L^{2}(\,\mathrm{d}x|_{[0,1]})
. For the generalized Anderson-Darling statistic
(
20
), we then have

A^{[m]}_{N}=\sum_{k\geqslant m}\frac{(k-m)!}{(k+m)!}\,\widehat{\xi}_{k}^{2}

for each
(X_{i})_{1\leqslant i\leqslant N}\in[0,1]^{N}
. In particular,

A^{[1]}_{N}
,
A^{[2]}_{N}
and
A^{[3]}_{N}
, when expressed in terms of the

\widehat{\xi}_{k}
’s, have the forms

\displaystyle A^{[1]}_{N}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\geqslant 1}\frac{\widehat{\xi}_{k}^{2}}{k(k+1)}=\frac{\widehat{\xi}_{1}^{2}}{2}+\frac{\widehat{\xi}_{2}^{2}}{6}+\frac{\widehat{\xi}_{3}^{2}}{12}+\cdots,

\displaystyle A^{[2]}_{N}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\geqslant 2}\frac{\widehat{\xi}_{k}^{2}}{(k-1)k(k+1)(k+2)}=\frac{\widehat{\xi}_{2}^{2}}{24}+\frac{\widehat{\xi}_{3}^{2}}{120}+\cdots,

\displaystyle A^{[3]}_{N}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\geqslant 3}\frac{\widehat{\xi}_{k}^{2}}{(k-2)(k-1)k(k+1)(k+2)(k+3)}=\frac{\widehat{\xi}_{3}^{2}}{720}+\frac{\widehat{\xi}_{4}^{2}}{5040}+\cdots,

where

\widehat{\xi}_{1}=2\sqrt{3N}m_{1}
,
\widehat{\xi}_{2}=6\sqrt{5N}\bigl({m_{2}-\frac{1}{12}}\bigr)
,

\widehat{\xi}_{3}=20\sqrt{7N}\bigl({m_{3}-\frac{3}{20}m_{1}}\bigr)

with
m_{k}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{1\leqslant i\leqslant N}\bigl({X_{i}-\frac{1}{2}}\bigr)^{k}
, the sample
k
th moment around
\frac{1}{2}
.
Since
\widehat{\xi}_{1}
and
\widehat{\xi}_{2}
are
linear functions of the sample mean and the sample variance,
respectively, they convey the information of the mean
and dispersion of the distribution, respectively. Thus,
one expects that
A^{[1]}_{N}

whose dominant component is
\widehat{\xi}_{1}^{2}
has
statistical power especially for mean-shift alternative, and

A^{[2]}_{N}
whose dominant component is
\widehat{\xi}_{2}^{2}

has statistical power for dispersion-shift alternative.
Similarly, since
\widehat{\xi}_{3}
contains the information of the skewness,

A^{[3]}_{N}
is expected to detect the skewness (or asymmetry)
of the distribution.

Note that when

N
is large and the distribution
F
of
X_{i}
is not too much away
from the null hypothesis
\mathrm{Unif}(0,1)
,
\widehat{\xi}_{k}^{2}
are
approximately distributed as the independent chi-square distributions with one degree of freedom and
the non-centrality parameter

\mathbb{E}\bigl[\widehat{\xi}_{k}\bigr]^{2}
with

\mathbb{E}\bigl[\widehat{\xi}_{k}\bigr]=\sqrt{N}\bigl(\int_{0}^{1}P_{k}(x)\,\mathrm{d}F(x)\bigr)
.
For example,
let
Y_{i}
be i.i.d. Gaussian random variables with
mean
\Delta\mu/\sqrt{N}
and
variance
1+\Delta\sigma^{2}/\sqrt{N}
,
and consider testing the null hypothesis that
the true distribution is
N(0,1)
.
Then,
F
is the distribution of
X_{i}=\Phi(Y_{i})
, where
\Phi
the distribution function of
N(0,1)
.
By simple calculations,

\mathbb{E}\bigl[\widehat{\xi}_{1}\bigr]\approx 0.977\Delta\mu,\qquad\mathbb{E}\bigl[\widehat{\xi}_{2}\bigr]\approx 0.616\Delta\sigma^{2},\qquad\mathbb{E}\bigl[\widehat{\xi}_{3}\bigr]\approx 0.183\Delta\mu

(39)

up to an error of
O(1)
.
The local property (
39
) is consistent with the interpretations of
\widehat{\xi}_{k}
stated above.

The decomposition of goodness-of-fit statistics is also discussed in

Durbin and Knott (1972)
based on the Cramér-von Mises statistic. While the
eigenfunctions for the Cramér-von Mises statistic are trigonometric
functions and different from our associate Legendre polynomials, the
interpretation of our components
\widehat{\xi}_{k}^{2}
is similar to that
given in
Durbin and Knott (1972)
.

In Figures

2.4

the test statistics
A_{N}^{[1]}
,
A_{N}^{[2]}
and
A_{N}^{[3]}

are compared based on Monte Carlo simulations.

H_{0}

F
is the normal distribution

N=100
samples

First, we assume that the true distribution is the normal

\mu

,
A_{N}^{[2]}

and
A_{N}^{[3]}

One sees that

2.6
Two-sample test statistic

For the original Anderson-Darling statistic,
Pettitt (1976)

proposed a two-sample statistic for testing equality of two empirical
distributions. Given two distribution functions
F^{(\ell)}
,

\ell=1,2
, assume that two i.i.d. samples
X^{(\ell)}_{1},\ldots,X^{(\ell)}_{N_{\ell}}
from
F^{(\ell)}
are available. The merged
dataset of
X^{(\ell)}_{j}
’s is denoted by
Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{N}
,

N=N_{1}+N_{2}
. Let

R^{(\ell)}_{j}=\sharp\left(i\mid Y_{i}\leqslant X^{(\ell)}_{j}\right)

be the rank of
X^{(\ell)}_{j}
in the whole dataset
Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{N}
.

Then, the
m
-fold integrated two-sample Anderson-Darling statistic
for testing
H_{0}:F^{(1)}=F^{(2)}
against
H_{1}:F^{(1)}\neq F^{(2)}
is given by

\widetilde{A}^{\,\,[m]}_{N_{1},N_{2}}=\frac{N_{1}N_{2}}{N^{2}}\sum_{1\leqslant i<N}\frac{\bigl({B^{(1)}_{i}-B^{(2)}_{i}}\bigr)^{2}}{\left(\frac{i}{N}\bigl({1-\frac{i}{N}}\bigr)\right)^{m}},

(41)

where

\displaystyle B^{(\ell)}_{i}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N_{\ell}}\sum_{1\leqslant j\leqslant N_{\ell}}\frac{\bigl({\frac{i}{N}-\frac{1}{N}R^{(\ell)}_{j}}\bigr)^{m-1}}{(m-1)!}\mathds{1}\bigl({R^{(\ell)}_{j}\leqslant i}\bigr)

\displaystyle\qquad-\frac{1}{N_{\ell}}\sum_{0\leqslant k<m}P_{k}^{(-m)}\bigl({\tfrac{i}{N}}\bigr)\sum_{1\leqslant j\leqslant N_{\ell}}P_{k}\Bigl({\tfrac{R^{(\ell)}_{j}}{N}}\Bigr).

To prove (
41
), note first that the
m
-fold GAD
statistic for testing

H_{0}:F=F_{0}\quad\mbox{vs.}\quad H_{1}:F\neq F_{0}

without assuming that
F_{0}
is the uniform distribution on
[0,1]

is given as

A^{[m]}_{N}=N\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\bigl({\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}B^{[m]}_{N}(x;F_{0};F_{N})}\bigr)^{2}}{\left(F_{0}(x)(1-F_{0}(x))\right)^{m}}\,\mathrm{d}F_{0}(x),

where

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\,B^{[m]}_{N}(x;F_{0};F_{N})=

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{x}\frac{(F_{0}(x)-F_{0}(t))^{m-1}}{(m-1)!}\,\mathrm{d}F_{N}(t)

\displaystyle-\sum_{0\leqslant k<m}P_{k}^{(-m)}(F_{0}(x))\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}P_{k}(F_{0}(x))\,\mathrm{d}F_{N}(x).

Let
F_{N_{\ell}}^{(\ell)}
be the underlying empirical distribution
function of
X^{(\ell)}_{1},\ldots,X^{(\ell)}_{N_{\ell}}
for
\ell=1,2
.
Denote by
F_{N}
the empirical distribution function of the merged
data
Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{N}
,
N=N_{1}+N_{2}
.
Obviously,

F_{N}(x)=\frac{N_{1}F^{(1)}_{N_{1}}(x)+N_{2}F^{(2)}_{N_{2}}(x)}{N}.

The two-sample Anderson-Darling statistic is defined by

\widetilde{A}^{\,\,[m]}_{N_{1},N_{2}}=\frac{N_{1}N_{2}}{N}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{N_{1}}}B^{[m]}_{N_{1}}\bigl({x;F_{N};F^{(1)}_{N_{1}}}\bigr)-\frac{1}{\sqrt{N_{2}}}B^{[m]}_{N_{2}}\bigl({x;F_{N};F^{(2)}_{N_{2}}}\bigr)\right)^{2}}{\left(F_{N}(x)(1-F_{N}(x))\right)^{m}}\,\mathrm{d}F_{N}(x).

Since
F_{N}(X^{(\ell)}_{j})=\frac{1}{N}R^{(\ell)}_{j}
, a simple
calculation then yields the result (
41
).

As
Pettitt (1976)
proved, the original Anderson-Darling
statistic has the same limiting null distribution as
the one-sided case. This holds for the general
m
as well, but
the details are omitted here.

2.7
The MGF of the limit law
A^{[m]}

By the expansion (
38
), we see that the MGF of the limit law

A^{[m]}
has the infinite product representation

\mathbb{E}\bigl({e^{sA^{[m]}}}\bigr)=\prod_{k\geqslant 1}\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-\frac{2s}{\lambda_{k}}}}\quad\text{where}\quad\lambda_{k}:=k(k+1)\cdots(k+2m+1).

This expression holds for complex
s
except for the branch-cut

[\frac{1}{2}\lambda_{1},\infty)
. It is known that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigl({e^{sA^{[1]}}}\bigr)=\sqrt{\frac{-2\pi s}{\cos\frac{\pi}{2}\sqrt{1+8s}}}.

(42)

We derive a similar representation for all
m\geqslant 1
.

Theorem 2.4
.

Let
\eta_{j}
(
0\leqslant j<m
) represent the zeros of the
polynomial equation (in
z
)

\displaystyle\prod_{0\leqslant j<m}\left(z-(j+\tfrac{1}{2})^{2}\right)-2s=0.

(43)

Then for
m\geqslant 1

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigl({e^{sA^{[m]}}}\bigr)=\sqrt{\frac{(-2\pi s)^{m}}{1!2!\cdots(2m-1)!}\prod_{0\leqslant j<m}\frac{1}{\cos\bigl({\pi\sqrt{\eta_{j}}}\bigr)}},

(44)

where the square-root denotes the principal branch.

The finite product representation (
44
) is preferable for
most analytic or numerical purposes. For example, the distribution
function of
A^{[m]}
can be computed by the following formula

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\bigl({A^{[m]}>x}\bigr)=\text{\scalebox{.9}{$\frac{1}{\pi}$}}\sum_{k\geqslant 1}(-1)^{k-1}\int_{\frac{1}{2}\lambda_{2k-1}}^{\frac{1}{2}\lambda_{2k}}\frac{e^{-xs}}{s\sqrt{\bigl|\mathbb{E}\bigl({e^{sA^{[m]}}}\bigr)\bigr|}}\,\mathrm{d}s,

(45)

which may be called
the Smirnov-Slepian formula
; see

Smirnov (1937)
;
Slepian (1958)
for the original papers where such
a technique was introduced. See also
Matsui and Takemura (2008)
and

Deheuvels and Martynov (2008)
for other applications. In the above
formula, we see that effective numerical procedures such as
(
44
) are crucial in computing the tails to high precision.

In particular, when
m=1
, the solution to
(
43
)
is
obviously given by
\eta_{0}=\frac{1}{4}+2s
, so we obtain (
42
).
When
m=2
, the solutions to (
43
) are

\frac{5}{4}\pm\sqrt{1+2s}
, so we obtain the identity

\begin{split}&\prod_{k\geqslant 1}\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-\frac{2s}{k(k+1)(k+2)(k+3)}}}\\
&\qquad=\frac{\pi s}{\sqrt{3\cos\left(\frac{\pi}{2}\sqrt{5-4\sqrt{1+2s}}\right)\cos\left(\frac{\pi}{2}\sqrt{5+4\sqrt{1+2s}}\right)}}.\end{split}

(46)

A very different construction based on Jacobi polynomials leading
to an MGF of the same form but with
s\mapsto 6s
can be found in

Deheuvels and Martynov (2008)
. For
m=3
,

\begin{split}&\prod_{k\geqslant 1}\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-\frac{2s}{k(k+1)(k+2)(k+3)(k+4)(k+5)}}}\\
&\qquad=\frac{(\pi s)^{3/2}}{\sqrt{-4320\cos\left(\pi\sqrt{\eta_{1}}\right)\cosh\left(\pi\sqrt{\eta_{2}}\right)\cosh\left(\pi\sqrt{\eta_{3}}\right)}}\end{split}

(47)

where
\eta:=\sqrt[3]{27s+80+3\sqrt{81s^{2}+480s-1728})}
and

\displaystyle\eta_{1}

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{12\eta}\left(4\eta^{2}+35\eta+112\right),

\displaystyle\eta_{2}

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{12\eta}\left(4e^{-\pi i/3}\eta^{2}-35\eta+112e^{\pi i/3}\right),

\displaystyle\eta_{3}

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{12\eta}\left(4e^{\pi i/3}\eta^{2}-35\eta+112e^{-\pi i/3}\right).

While the exact expressions readily become lengthy for higher
values of
m
, numerical evaluation is rather straightforward
once the value of
s
is specified.

Lemma 2.7
.

Let
\zeta_{j}(s)
,
0\leqslant j<2m
, denote the zeros of the polynomials

z(z+1)\cdots(z+2m-1)-2s
:

\displaystyle z(z+1)\cdots(z+2m-1)-2s=\prod_{0\leqslant j<2m}\left(z-\zeta_{j}(s)\right).

(48)

Then

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigl({e^{sA^{[m]}}}\bigr)=\sqrt{\prod_{0\leqslant j<2m}\frac{\Gamma(1-\zeta_{j}(s))}{j!}}.

(49)

Proof.

By (
48
)

1-\frac{2s}{k(k+1)\cdots(k+2m-1)}=\prod_{0\leqslant j<2m}\frac{k-\zeta_{j}(s)}{k+j}=\prod_{0\leqslant j<2m}\frac{1-\frac{\zeta_{j}(s)}{k}}{1+\frac{j}{k}}.

Note that

\displaystyle-\sum_{0\leqslant j<2m}\zeta_{j}(s)=[z^{2m-1}]\prod_{0\leqslant j<2m}(z+j)=m(2m-1)=\sum_{0\leqslant j<2m}j,

(50)

where
[z^{m}]f(z)
denotes the coefficient of
z^{m}
in the Taylor
expansion of
f(z)
. Using the identity (
50
) and
the infinite product representation of the Gamma function

\Gamma(1+s)e^{\gamma s}=\prod_{k\geqslant 1}\frac{e^{\frac{s}{k}}}{1+\frac{s}{k}},

we then have

\displaystyle\prod_{k\geqslant 1}\frac{1}{1-\frac{2s}{k(k+1)\cdots(k+2m-1)}}

\displaystyle=\prod_{k\geqslant 1}\prod_{0\leqslant j<2m}\frac{\left(1+\frac{j}{k}\right)e^{-\frac{j}{k}}}{\bigl({1-\frac{\zeta_{j}(s)}{k}}\bigr)e^{-\frac{\zeta_{j}(s)}{k}}}

\displaystyle=\prod_{0\leqslant j<2m}\frac{\Gamma(1-\zeta_{j}(s))e^{-\gamma\zeta_{j}(s)}}{\Gamma(j+1)e^{j\gamma}}

\displaystyle=\prod_{0\leqslant j<2m}\frac{\Gamma(1-\zeta_{j}(s))}{j!}.

This proves (
49
).
∎

Proof of Theorem
2.4
.

Note that the roots of the equation (
48
) are symmetric
with respect to
-m+\frac{1}{2}
, namely, if
-m+\frac{1}{2}+\zeta
is a
root of the equation (
48
), then
-m+\frac{1}{2}-\zeta
is
also a root. Thus we can regroup the zeros in pairs and use
the factorization

\displaystyle\Gamma\bigl({m+\tfrac{1}{2}-\zeta}\bigr)\Gamma\bigl({m+\tfrac{1}{2}+\zeta}\bigr)

\displaystyle\qquad=\Gamma\bigl({-m+\tfrac{1}{2}-\zeta}\bigr)\Gamma\bigl({m+\tfrac{1}{2}+\zeta}\bigr)\prod_{0\leqslant\ell<2m}\left(-m+\tfrac{1}{2}-\zeta+\ell\right),

which equals, by Euler’s reflection formula

\Gamma(s)\Gamma(1-s)=\frac{\pi}{\sin\pi s},

and (
48
), to

\Gamma\bigl({m+\tfrac{1}{2}-\zeta}\bigr)\Gamma\bigl({m+\tfrac{1}{2}+\zeta}\bigr)=\frac{2\pi s}{\sin\bigl({\pi(m+\tfrac{1}{2}+\zeta)}\bigr)}=\frac{(-1)^{m}2\pi s}{\cos(\pi\zeta)}.

Now if we replace
x
in the equation
x\cdots(x+2m-1)=2s
by

x\mapsto-m+\tfrac{1}{2}+z

\prod_{0\leqslant j<m}\left(z^{2}-(-m+\tfrac{1}{2}+j)^{2}\right)=2s,

which is equivalent to

\prod_{0\leqslant j<m}\left(z^{2}-(j+\tfrac{1}{2})^{2}\right)=2s.

Thus it suffices to consider the equation (
43
)

\prod_{0\leqslant j<m}\left(z-(j+\tfrac{1}{2})^{2}\right)=2s.

It then follows that

\prod_{0\leqslant j<2m}\Gamma(1-\zeta_{j}(s))=\prod_{0\leqslant j<m}\frac{(-1)^{m}2\pi s}{\cos\bigl({\pi\sqrt{\eta_{j}}}\bigr)}.

This proves (
44
).
∎

U^{[m]}_{N}=\int_{0}^{1}\,\vbox{\hrule height=0.6pt\kern 1.07639pt\hbox{\kern-1.99997pt$B$\kern-1.00006pt}

}^{[m]}_{N}(\{x-a\})^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x=U^{[m]}_{N},

where we used the relation

\displaystyle(\{X_{i}+a\}-x)-(X_{i}-\{x-a\})

\displaystyle=-(X_{i}+a-\{X_{i}+a\})-(x-a-\{x-a\})

\displaystyle=0\bmod 1.

On the other hand, if
X_{i}\mapsto\{-X_{i}\}=1-X_{i}
,
i=1,\ldots,N
,
then

\displaystyle\,\vbox{\hrule height=0.6pt\kern 1.07639pt\hbox{\kern-1.99997pt$B$\kern-1.00006pt}
}^{[m]}_{N}(x)=\text{\scalebox{.9}{$\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}$}}\sum_{1\leqslant i\leqslant N}\bar{\tau}_{m}(X_{i};x)\mapsto

\displaystyle\text{\scalebox{.9}{$\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}$}}\sum_{1\leqslant i\leqslant N}\bar{\tau}_{m}(1-X_{i};x)

\displaystyle=\text{\scalebox{.9}{$\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}$}}\sum_{1\leqslant i\leqslant N}(-1)^{m}\bar{\tau}_{m}(X_{i};1-x)

\displaystyle=(-1)^{m}\,\vbox{\hrule height=0.6pt\kern 1.07639pt\hbox{\kern-1.99997pt$B$\kern-1.00006pt}
}^{[m]}_{N}(1-x).

It follows that

U^{[m]}_{N}=\int_{0}^{1}\,\vbox{\hrule height=0.6pt\kern 1.07639pt\hbox{\kern-1.99997pt$B$\kern-1.00006pt}
}^{[m]}_{N}(x)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\mapsto\int_{0}^{1}\,\vbox{\hrule height=0.6pt\kern 1.07639pt\hbox{\kern-1.99997pt$B$\kern-1.00006pt}
}^{[m]}_{N}(1-x)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x=U^{[m]}_{N}.

The proof for
U_{N}^{*[m]}
is similar.
∎

3.2
The limiting GW processes: KL expansions and covariance kernel

In this section, we examine the limiting distribution of the GW
statistic under the null hypothesis
H_{0}
.

From their constructions,
U^{[m]}_{N}
and
U^{*[m]}_{N}
have
almost the same eigenstructure. In this subsection, we mainly deal
with the non-truncated version
U^{[m]}_{N}
. As in
Sec.
2.3
, we denote by
W(x)
and
B(x)

the Wiener process on
[0,1]
and the Brownian bridge,
respectively. Using the template functions
\bar{\tau}_{m}(t;x)

in (
58
) and
\bar{\tau}^{*}_{m}(t;x)
in (
62
),
we now define the limiting GW processes

\displaystyle\vbox{\hrule height=0.6pt\kern 1.07639pt\hbox{\kern-1.99997pt$B$\kern-1.00006pt}
}^{[m]}(x)=\int_{0}^{1}\bar{\tau}_{m}(t;x)\,\mathrm{d}W(t)=\int_{0}^{1}\bar{\tau}_{m}(t;x)\,\mathrm{d}B(t),

(63)

\displaystyle\vbox{\hrule height=0.6pt\kern 1.07639pt\hbox{\kern-1.99997pt$B$\kern-1.00006pt}
}^{*[m]}(x)=\int_{0}^{1}\bar{\tau}^{*}_{m}(t;x)\,\mathrm{d}W(t)=\int_{0}^{1}\bar{\tau}^{*}_{m}(t;x)\,\mathrm{d}B(t).

Since the Brownian bridge
B(\cdot)
is continuous on
[0,1]
,
both
\,\vbox{\hrule height=0.6pt\kern 1.07639pt\hbox{\kern-1.99997pt$B$\kern-1.00006pt}
}^{[m]}(\cdot)
and
\,\vbox{\hrule height=0.6pt\kern 1.07639pt\hbox{\kern-1.99997pt$B$\kern-1.00006pt}
}^{*[m]}(\cdot)
are
of the class
C^{m-1}
on
[0,1]
.

We now describe the weak convergence of these processes in the
functional spaces
L^{2}([0,1],\mu)
and
L^{\infty}([0,1])
, the space
of all essentially bounded functions on
[0,1]
.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
\mu
be any finite measure on
[0,1]
. As
N\to\infty
,

\,\vbox{\hrule height=0.6pt\kern 1.07639pt\hbox{\kern-2.04439pt$B$\kern-1.02228pt}
}^{[m]}_{N}(\cdot)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\to}}\,\vbox{\hrule height=0.6pt\kern 1.07639pt\hbox{\kern-2.04439pt$B$\kern-1.02228pt}
}^{[m]}(\cdot)
and
\,\vbox{\hrule height=0.6pt\kern 1.07639pt\hbox{\kern-2.04439pt$B$\kern-1.02228pt}
}^{*[m]}_{N}(\cdot)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\to}}\,\vbox{\hrule height=0.6pt\kern 1.07639pt\hbox{\kern-2.04439pt$B$\kern-1.02228pt}
}^{*[m]}(\cdot)
in
L^{2}([0,1],\mu)
and
in
L^{\infty}([0,1])
, and
U^{[m]}_{N}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\to}}U^{[m]}
and

U^{*[m]}_{N}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\to}}U^{*[m]}
, where

U^{[m]}=\int_{0}^{1}\,\vbox{\hrule height=0.6pt\kern 1.07639pt\hbox{\kern-2.04439pt$B$\kern-1.02228pt}
}^{[m]}(x)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\quad\text{and}\quad U^{*[m]}=\int_{0}^{1}\,\vbox{\hrule height=0.6pt\kern 1.07639pt\hbox{\kern-2.04439pt$B$\kern-1.02228pt}
}^{*[m]}(x)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x.

Proof.

The stochastic map
x\mapsto B_{N}(x)=\sqrt{N}(F_{N}(x)-x)
converges
weakly to
B(\cdot)
in both
L^{2}([0,1],\mu)
and
L^{\infty}([0,1])
.

B^{[m]}(\cdot)
and
B^{*[m]}(\cdot)
in (
63
) are continuous
in
B(\cdot)
in both
L^{2}([0,1],\mu)
and
L^{\infty}([0,1])
. The
rest of the proof is similar to that of Lemma
2.4
.
∎

We now clarify the covariances of the
\,\vbox{\hrule height=0.6pt\kern 1.07639pt\hbox{\kern-1.99997pt$B$\kern-1.00006pt}
}^{[m]}(\cdot)

and
\,\vbox{\hrule height=0.6pt\kern 1.07639pt\hbox{\kern-1.99997pt$B$\kern-1.00006pt}
}^{*[m]}(\cdot)
, which are centered Gaussian
processes on
[0,1]
.

Theorem 3.1
.

For
m\geqslant 1

\displaystyle\mathrm{Cov}\bigl({\,\vbox{\hrule height=0.6pt\kern 1.07639pt\hbox{\kern-2.04439pt$B$\kern-1.02228pt}
}^{[m]}(x),\,\vbox{\hrule height=0.6pt\kern 1.07639pt\hbox{\kern-2.04439pt$B$\kern-1.02228pt}
}^{[m]}(y)}\bigr)=(-1)^{m-1}b_{2m}(|x-y|)=2\sum_{k\geqslant 1}\frac{\cos(2k\pi(x-y))}{(2k\pi)^{2m}},

(64)

\displaystyle\mathrm{Cov}\bigl({\,\vbox{\hrule height=0.6pt\kern 1.07639pt\hbox{\kern-2.04439pt$B$\kern-1.02228pt}
}^{*[m]}(x),\,\vbox{\hrule height=0.6pt\kern 1.07639pt\hbox{\kern-2.04439pt$B$\kern-1.02228pt}
}^{*[m]}(y)}\bigr)=(-1)^{m-1}b_{2m}(|x-y|)-2\sum_{1\leqslant k<m}\frac{\cos(2k\pi(x-y))}{(2k\pi)^{2m}}.

(65)

In particular,

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}\bigl({\,\vbox{\hrule height=0.6pt\kern 1.07639pt\hbox{\kern-2.04439pt$B$\kern-1.02228pt}
}^{[m]}(x)}\bigr)=(-1)^{m-1}b_{2m}(0),

(66)

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}\bigl({\,\vbox{\hrule height=0.6pt\kern 1.07639pt\hbox{\kern-2.04439pt$B$\kern-1.02228pt}
}^{*[m]}(x)}\bigr)=(-1)^{m-1}b_{2m}(0)-2\sum_{1\leqslant k<m}\frac{1}{(2k\pi)^{2m}},

where the
(2m)!b_{2m}(0)
are Bernoulli numbers.

Proof.

Assume without loss of generality that
0\leqslant x\leqslant y\leqslant 1
. We use
again the generating function (
55
) of
b_{m}(x)
.
By the representation for
\bar{\tau}_{m}(t;x)
in (
61
), we
see that the covariance function satisfies

\displaystyle\mathrm{Cov}\bigl({\,\vbox{\hrule height=0.6pt\kern 1.07639pt\hbox{\kern-1.99997pt$B$\kern-1.00006pt}
}^{[m]}(x),\,\vbox{\hrule height=0.6pt\kern 1.07639pt\hbox{\kern-1.99997pt$B$\kern-1.00006pt}
}^{[m]}(y)}\bigr)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\bar{\tau}_{m}(t;x)\bar{\tau}_{m}(t;y)\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=[z^{m}w^{m}]\biggl(\int_{0}^{x}V(z;t-x+1)V(w;t-y+1)\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\int_{x}^{y}V(z;t-x)V(w;t-y+1)\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\int_{y}^{1}V(z;t-x)V(w;t-y)\,\mathrm{d}t\biggr)

\displaystyle=[z^{m}w^{m}]\frac{zw}{z+w}\left(\frac{e^{\Delta z}}{e^{z}-1}+\frac{e^{(1-\Delta)w}}{e^{w}-1}\right),

where
\Delta=y-x
. Now

\displaystyle\frac{zw}{z+w}\left(\frac{e^{\Delta z}}{e^{z}-1}+\frac{e^{(1-\Delta)w}}{e^{w}-1}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\geqslant 0}\frac{wb_{k}(\Delta)z^{k}+zb_{k}(1-\Delta)w^{k}}{z+w}

\displaystyle=zw\sum_{k\geqslant 1}b_{k}(\Delta)\frac{z^{k-1}-(-w)^{k-1}}{z-(-w)}+1

\displaystyle=zw\sum_{k\geqslant 2}b_{k}(\Delta)\frac{z^{k-1}+(-w)^{k-1}}{z-(-w)}+1

\displaystyle=zw\sum_{k\geqslant 2}b_{k}(\Delta)\sum_{0\leqslant j\leqslant k-2}z^{j}(-w)^{k-j-2}+1.

It follows that when
x\leqslant y
,
\mathrm{Cov}\bigl({\,\vbox{\hrule height=0.6pt\kern 1.07639pt\hbox{\kern-1.99997pt$B$\kern-1.00006pt}
}^{[m]}(x),\,\vbox{\hrule height=0.6pt\kern 1.07639pt\hbox{\kern-1.99997pt$B$\kern-1.00006pt}
}^{[m]}(y)}\bigr)=(-1)^{m-1}b_{2m}(\Delta)
. Similarly,
when
x<y
,

\mathrm{Cov}\bigl({\,\vbox{\hrule height=0.6pt\kern 1.07639pt\hbox{\kern-1.99997pt$B$\kern-1.00006pt}
}^{[m]}(x),\,\vbox{\hrule height=0.6pt\kern 1.07639pt\hbox{\kern-1.99997pt$B$\kern-1.00006pt}
}^{[m]}(y)}\bigr)=(-1)^{m-1}b_{2m}(-\Delta)
. We then conclude (
64
).

The proof of (
65
) is based on the identities

\int_{0}^{1}\bar{\tau}_{m}(t;x)\cos\Bigl(2k\pi(t-y)+\text{\scalebox{.9}{$\frac{m\pi}{2}$}}\Bigr)\,\mathrm{d}t=\frac{\cos(2k\pi(x-y))}{(2k\pi)^{m}},

and

\begin{split}&\int_{0}^{1}\cos\Bigl(2k\pi(t-x)+\text{\scalebox{.9}{$\frac{m\pi}{2}$}}\Bigr)\cos\Bigl(2\ell\pi(t-y)+\text{\scalebox{.9}{$\frac{m\pi}{2}$}}\Bigr)\,\mathrm{d}t\\
&\qquad=\begin{cases}\text{\scalebox{.9}{$\frac{1}{2}$}}\cos(2k\pi(x-y)),&k=\ell,\\
0,&k\neq\ell,\end{cases}\end{split}

(67)

whose proofs are similar and omitted.
∎

Let
\psi_{0}(x)=1
, and
\psi_{2k-1}(x)=\sqrt{2}\sin(2k\pi x)
,

\psi_{2k}(x)=\sqrt{2}\cos(2k\pi x)
for
k\geqslant 1
. Then,

\{\psi_{k}(x)\}_{k\geqslant 0}
is a complete orthonormal basis of

L^{2}[0,1]
. For each
m\geqslant 1
, let

\psi^{[m]}_{2k-1}(x)=\sqrt{2}\sin\Bigl(2k\pi x-\text{\scalebox{.9}{$\frac{m\pi}{2}$}}\Bigr)\quad\text{and}\quad\psi^{[m]}_{2k}(x)=\sqrt{2}\cos\Bigl(2k\pi x-\text{\scalebox{.9}{$\frac{m\pi}{2}$}}\Bigr).

Then
\{\psi^{[m]}_{k}(x)\}_{k\geqslant 0}
also form an orthonormal basis.
By expressing
\bar{\tau}_{m}(\cdot;x)
in (
58
) as
a Fourier series (
56
), and by expanding it on the basis

\psi_{k}(\cdot)
, we obtain the expansion

\displaystyle\bar{\tau}_{m}(t;x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\geqslant 1}\frac{1}{(2k\pi)^{m}}\left(\psi^{[m]}_{2k-1}(x)\psi_{2k-1}(t)+\psi^{[m]}_{2k}(x)\psi_{2k}(t)\right)

\displaystyle=2\sum_{k\geqslant 1}\frac{1}{(2k\pi)^{m}}\cos\Bigl(2k\pi(t-x)+\text{\scalebox{.9}{$\frac{m\pi}{2}$}}\Bigr),

(68)

and

\bar{\tau}^{*}_{m}(t;x)=\sum_{k\geqslant m}\frac{1}{(2k\pi)^{m}}\left(\psi^{[m]}_{2k-1}(x)\psi_{2k-1}(t)+\psi^{[m]}_{2k}(x)\psi_{2k}(t)\right).

By taking the stochastic integral
\int_{0}^{1}\,\mathrm{d}B(t)
on both sides,
we get the KL expansions

\displaystyle\,\vbox{\hrule height=0.6pt\kern 1.07639pt\hbox{\kern-1.99997pt$B$\kern-1.00006pt}
}^{[m]}(x)=\sum_{k\geqslant 1}\frac{1}{(2k\pi)^{m}}\left(\psi^{[m]}_{2k-1}(x)\xi_{2k-1}+\psi^{[m]}_{2k}(x)\xi_{2k}\right),

(69)

\displaystyle\,\vbox{\hrule height=0.6pt\kern 1.07639pt\hbox{\kern-1.99997pt$B$\kern-1.00006pt}
}^{*[m]}(x)=\sum_{k\geqslant m}\frac{1}{(2k\pi)^{m}}\left(\psi^{[m]}_{2k-1}(x)\xi_{2k-1}+\psi^{[m]}_{2k}(x)\xi_{2k}\right),

(70)

where

\xi_{j}=\int_{0}^{1}\psi_{j}(y)\,\mathrm{d}B(y)=\int_{0}^{1}\psi_{j}(y)\,\mathrm{d}W(y)\sim N(0,1)\ \ \rm i.i.d.

(71)

These KL expansions can again be justified by Mercer’s theorem.

Theorem 3.2
.

Let
\xi_{k}
,
k\geqslant 0
, be i.i.d. standard Gaussian random variables
defined by (
71
). For
m\geqslant 1
, (
69
) and (
70
)
hold uniformly in
x\in[0,1]
with probability 1, and both

U^{[m]}=\sum_{k\geqslant 1}\frac{\xi_{2k-1}^{2}+\xi_{2k}^{2}}{(2k\pi)^{2m}}\quad\text{and}\quad U^{*[m]}=\sum_{k\geqslant m}\frac{\xi_{2k-1}^{2}+\xi_{2k}^{2}}{(2k\pi)^{2m}}

(72)

converge with probability 1.

Proof.

Note that
\,\vbox{\hrule height=0.6pt\kern 1.07639pt\hbox{\kern-1.99997pt$B$\kern-1.00006pt}
}^{[m]}(\cdot)
is continuous on
[0,1]
. The
covariance function of
\,\vbox{\hrule height=0.6pt\kern 1.07639pt\hbox{\kern-1.99997pt$B$\kern-1.00006pt}
}^{[m]}(\cdot)
is given in
(
64
), which is continuous on
[0,1]\times[0,1]
.
Moreover, by (
66
),

\int_{0}^{1}\mathrm{Cov}(\,\vbox{\hrule height=0.6pt\kern 1.07639pt\hbox{\kern-1.99997pt$B$\kern-1.00006pt}
}^{[m]}(x),\,\vbox{\hrule height=0.6pt\kern 1.07639pt\hbox{\kern-1.99997pt$B$\kern-1.00006pt}
}^{[m]}(x))\,\mathrm{d}x<\infty.

In addition,
\mathrm{Cov}\bigl({\,\vbox{\hrule height=0.6pt\kern 1.07639pt\hbox{\kern-1.99997pt$B$\kern-1.00006pt}
}^{[m]}(x),\,\vbox{\hrule height=0.6pt\kern 1.07639pt\hbox{\kern-1.99997pt$B$\kern-1.00006pt}
}^{[m]}(y)}\bigr)

satisfies the eigenequation

\int_{0}^{1}\mathrm{Cov}\bigl({\,\vbox{\hrule height=0.6pt\kern 1.07639pt\hbox{\kern-1.99997pt$B$\kern-1.00006pt}
}^{[m]}(x),\,\vbox{\hrule height=0.6pt\kern 1.07639pt\hbox{\kern-1.99997pt$B$\kern-1.00006pt}
}^{[m]}(y)}\bigr)\psi^{[m]}_{k}(y)\,\mathrm{d}y=\frac{\psi^{[m]}_{k}(x)}{(2\lceil k/2\rceil\pi)^{2m}},\qquad k\geqslant 1.

The remaining proof then follows the same line as that of Theorem

2.3
. The required results for
\,\vbox{\hrule height=0.6pt\kern 1.07639pt\hbox{\kern-1.99997pt$B$\kern-1.00006pt}
}^{*[m]}(\cdot)
and

U^{*[m]}
are proved similarly.
∎

The covariance function for
B^{[m]}(\cdot)
when
m=1
is given
explicitly by

\mathrm{Cov}\bigl({\,\vbox{\hrule height=0.6pt\kern 1.07639pt\hbox{\kern-1.99997pt$B$\kern-1.00006pt}
}^{[1]}(x),\,\vbox{\hrule height=0.6pt\kern 1.07639pt\hbox{\kern-1.99997pt$B$\kern-1.00006pt}
}^{[1]}(y)}\bigr)=\text{\scalebox{.9}{$\frac{1}{12}$}}-\text{\scalebox{.9}{$\frac{1}{2}$}}|x-y|+\text{\scalebox{.9}{$\frac{1}{2}$}}(x-y)^{2},

(see also
(
Watson, 1961
, p. 111)
), and when
m=2

\mathrm{Cov}\bigl({\,\vbox{\hrule height=0.6pt\kern 1.07639pt\hbox{\kern-1.99997pt$B$\kern-1.00006pt}
}^{[2]}(x),\,\vbox{\hrule height=0.6pt\kern 1.07639pt\hbox{\kern-1.99997pt$B$\kern-1.00006pt}
}^{[2]}(y)}\bigr)=\text{\scalebox{.9}{$\frac{1}{720}$}}-\text{\scalebox{.9}{$\frac{1}{24}$}}(x-y)^{2}+\text{\scalebox{.9}{$\frac{1}{12}$}}|x-y|^{3}-\text{\scalebox{.9}{$\frac{1}{24}$}}(x-y)^{4};

see also
Henze and Nikitin (2002)
.

3.3
Statistic in terms of the samples

We now derive an expression for GW statistic in terms of the sample

X_{i}
’s, which turns out to be simpler than that of the GAD
statistic. Since

U^{[m]}_{N}=N\int_{0}^{1}\biggl(\int_{0}^{1}\bar{\tau}_{m}(t;x)\bar{\tau}_{m}(u;x)\,\mathrm{d}x\biggr)\,\mathrm{d}F_{N}(t)\,\mathrm{d}F_{N}(u),

(73)

it suffices to evaluate the integral within the parentheses above,
which can be done in essentially the same way as the proof of Theorem

3.1
.

Theorem 3.3
.

The generalized Watson statistics are expressed in terms of samples

X_{i}
’s as

U^{[m]}_{N}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{1\leqslant i,j\leqslant N}(-1)^{m-1}b_{2m}(|X_{i}-X_{j}|),

(74)

and

U^{*[m]}_{N}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{1\leqslant i,j\leqslant N}\biggl\{(-1)^{m-1}b_{2m}(|X_{i}-X_{j}|)-2\sum_{1\leqslant k<m}\frac{\cos(2k\pi(X_{i}-X_{j}))}{(2k\pi)^{2m}}\biggr\}.

(75)

Proof.

Let
\Delta=u-t
.
First assume that
0\leqslant t\leqslant u\leqslant 1
. The integral between
the large parentheses in (
73
) is

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\bar{\tau}_{m}(t;x)\bar{\tau}_{m}(u;x)\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=[z^{m}w^{m}]\biggl(\int_{u}^{1}V(z;t-x+1)V(w;u-x+1)\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\int_{t}^{u}V(z;t-x+1)V(w;u-x)\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\int_{0}^{t}V(z;t-x)V(w;u-x)\,\mathrm{d}t\biggr)

\displaystyle=[z^{m}w^{m}]\frac{zw}{z+w}\left(\frac{e^{(1-\Delta)z}}{e^{z}-1}+\frac{e^{\Delta w}}{e^{w}-1}\right).

(76)

By the same calculation in the proof of Theorem
3.1
, it holds that
(\ref{zw})=b_{m}(1-\Delta)=(-1)^{m}b_{m}(\Delta)
.
Similarly for
0\leqslant u\leqslant t\leqslant 1
,

(\ref{zw})=b_{m}(1+\Delta)=(-1)^{m}b_{m}(-\Delta)
.
In both cases,
(\ref{zw})=(-1)^{m}b_{m}(|\Delta|)
.
We then conclude (
74
).

For the proof of (
75
), we use the identities

\int_{0}^{1}\bar{\tau}_{m}(t;x)\cos\Bigl(2k\pi(u-x)+\text{\scalebox{.9}{$\frac{m\pi}{2}$}}\Bigr)\,\mathrm{d}x=\frac{\cos(2k\pi(t-u))}{(2k\pi)^{m}}

and (
67
).
∎

When
m=1
, the statistic is

\displaystyle U^{[1]}_{N}

\displaystyle=\text{\scalebox{.9}{$\frac{1}{2N}$}}\sum_{1\leqslant i,j\leqslant N}\Bigl(\text{\scalebox{.9}{$\frac{1}{6}$}}-|X_{i}-X_{j}|+(X_{i}-X_{j})^{2}\Bigr)

\displaystyle=\text{\scalebox{.9}{$\frac{1}{2N}$}}\sum_{1\leqslant i,j\leqslant N}\Bigl(-\text{\scalebox{.9}{$\frac{1}{12}$}}+\Bigl({|X_{i}-X_{j}|-\text{\scalebox{.9}{$\frac{1}{2}$}}}\Bigr)^{2}\Bigr).

This is equivalent to
N
times

\sum_{1\leqslant i\leqslant N}\Bigl(X_{(i)}-\text{\scalebox{.9}{$\frac{2i-1}{2N}$}}-\bar{X}+\text{\scalebox{.9}{$\frac{1}{2}$}}\Bigr)^{2}+\text{\scalebox{.9}{$\frac{1}{12N}$}},

where
\bar{X}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{1\leqslant i\leqslant N}X_{i}
represents the
sample mean; see
Watson (1961)
.

When
m=2
,

U^{[2]}_{N}=\text{\scalebox{.9}{$\frac{1}{N}$}}\sum_{1\leqslant i,j\leqslant N}\Bigl(\text{\scalebox{.9}{$\frac{1}{720}$}}-\text{\scalebox{.9}{$\frac{(X_{i}-X_{j})^{2}}{24}$}}+\text{\scalebox{.9}{$\frac{|X_{i}-X_{j}|^{3}}{12}$}}-\text{\scalebox{.9}{$\frac{(X_{i}-X_{j})^{4}}{24}$}}\Bigr),

and

U^{*[2]}_{N}=U^{[2]}_{N}-\text{\scalebox{.9}{$\frac{2}{N}$}}\sum_{1\leqslant i,j\leqslant N}\frac{\cos(2\pi(X_{i}-X_{j}))}{(2\pi)^{4}};

see also
Henze and Nikitin (2002)
.

3.5
Moment generating function

By the series representation (
72
), the MGF of the limiting
GW statistics now have the representations

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigl({e^{sU^{[m]}}}\bigr)=\prod_{k\geqslant 1}\frac{1}{1-\frac{2s}{\lambda_{k}}}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathbb{E}\bigl({e^{sU^{*[m]}}}\bigr)=\mathbb{E}\bigl({e^{sU^{[m]}}}\bigr)\prod_{1\leqslant k<m}\Bigl(1-\frac{2s}{\lambda_{k}}\Bigr),

(77)

where
\lambda_{k}:=(2k\pi)^{2m}
. The infinite series represents a
meromorphic function in the complex
s
-plane with simple poles at

s=\frac{1}{2}\lambda_{k}
for
k=1,2,\dots
. Similar to
Theorem
2.4
, the infinite product in

\mathbb{E}\bigl({e^{sU^{[m]}}}\bigr)
can be simplified.

Theorem 3.4
.

For
m\geqslant 1

\mathbb{E}\bigl({e^{sU^{[m]}}}\bigr)=\frac{\sqrt{2s}\,e^{\frac{m-1}{2}\pi i}}{2^{m}\prod_{0\leqslant j<m}\sin\bigl({\frac{1}{2}(2s)^{\frac{1}{2m}}e^{\frac{j\pi i}{m}}}\bigr)}.

(78)

Proof.

The proof is similar to but simpler than that of
Theorem
2.4
because the zeros of the polynomial
equation
(2\pi z)^{2m}=2s
have the closed-forms
\zeta_{j}(s)=\frac{1}{2\pi}(2s)^{\frac{1}{2m}}e^{\frac{j\pi i}{m}}
for
0\leqslant j<2m
.
So we first have the finite-product expression

\mathbb{E}\bigl({e^{sU^{[m]}}}\bigr)=\prod_{0\leqslant j<2m}\Gamma\bigl({1-\zeta_{j}(s)}\bigr).

Then we obtain (
78
) by rearranging the terms by
grouping the zeros in pairs and by Euler’s reflection formula.
Details are omitted here.
∎

In particular, with
\sigma_{m}:=\frac{1}{2}(2s)^{\frac{1}{2m}}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigl({e^{sU^{[1]}}}\bigr)

\displaystyle=\frac{\sigma_{1}}{\sin(\sigma_{1})},

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigl({e^{sU^{[2]}}}\bigr)

\displaystyle=\frac{\sigma_{2}^{2}}{\sin(\sigma_{2})\sinh(\sigma_{2})},

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigl({e^{sU^{[3]}}}\bigr)

\displaystyle=\frac{2\sigma_{3}^{3}}{\sin(\sigma_{3})\bigl({\cosh(\sqrt{3}\sigma_{3})-\cos(\sigma_{3})}\bigr)},

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigl({e^{sU^{[4]}}}\bigr)

\displaystyle=\frac{2\sigma_{4}^{4}}{\sin(\sigma_{4})\sinh(\sigma_{4})\bigl({\cosh(\sqrt{2}\sigma_{4})-\cos(\sqrt{2}\sigma_{4})}\bigr)}.

Since only simple poles instead of branch singularities are involved
in the infinite product representation (
77
) of

\mathbb{E}\bigl({e^{sU^{[m]}}}\bigr)
, we can easily invert the MGF by using
Laplace transform and obtain the following expressions.

Theorem 3.5
.

For
m\geqslant 1
, the density functions
f_{m}(x)
of
U^{[m]}

are given by

f_{m}(x)=m4^{m}\sum_{k\geqslant 1}\frac{(-1)^{k-1}(k\pi)^{3m-1}e^{\frac{m-1}{2}\pi i}}{\prod_{1\leqslant j<m}\sin\bigl({k\pi e^{\frac{j\pi i}{m}}}\bigr)}\,e^{-\frac{1}{2}(2k\pi)^{2m}x},

and the distribution functions by

1-2m\sum_{k\geqslant 1}\frac{(-1)^{k-1}(k\pi)^{m-1}e^{\frac{m-1}{2}\pi i}}{\prod_{1\leqslant j<m}\sin\bigl({k\pi e^{\frac{j\pi i}{m}}}\bigr)}\,e^{-\frac{1}{2}(2k\pi)^{2m}x},

for
x>0
.

Proof.

We start with the partial fraction expansion

\displaystyle\prod_{k\geqslant 1}\frac{1}{1-\frac{2s}{(2km)^{2m}}}=2m\sum_{k\geqslant 1}\frac{(-1)^{k-1}}{1-\frac{2s}{(2km)^{2m}}}\cdot\frac{(k\pi)^{m-1}e^{\frac{m-1}{2}\pi i}}{\prod_{1\leqslant j<m}\sin\bigl({k\pi e^{\frac{j\pi i}{m}}}\bigr)},

(79)

which is absolutely convergent for
m\geqslant 2
. This is proved by a
standard argument

\prod_{k\geqslant 1}\frac{1}{1-\frac{2s}{(2km)^{2m}}}=\sum_{k\geqslant 1}\frac{1}{1-\frac{2s}{(2km)^{2m}}}\prod_{j\neq k}\frac{1}{1-\frac{k^{2m}}{j^{2m}}},

where the inner product can be simplified by a similar method of
proof used in proving Theorem
3.4

\prod_{j\not=k}\frac{1}{1-\frac{k^{2m}}{j^{2m}}}=(-1)^{k-1}\frac{2m(k\pi)^{m-1}e^{\frac{m-1}{2}\pi i}}{\prod_{1\leqslant j<m}\sin\bigl(e^{\frac{j\pi i}{m}}k\pi\bigr)}\qquad(k\geqslant 1;m\geqslant 2).

For large
k
and
m\geqslant 2
, the terms in (
79
) decrease
in the order

2^{m}m(k\pi)^{m-1}\exp\biggl({-k\pi\sum_{1\leqslant j<m}\sin\frac{j\pi}{m}}\biggr)=2^{m}m(k\pi)^{m-1}\exp\left(-k\pi\cot\frac{\pi}{2m}\right).

Note that
\cot\frac{\pi}{2m}\sim\frac{2m}{\pi}
, we see that the
partial fraction expansion (
79
) converge absolutely
and very fast for
m\geqslant 2
.

Thus the decomposition (
79
) implies that
U^{[m]}
is
an infinite sum of exponential distributions, and the expressions for
the density and the distribution functions follow when
m\geqslant 2
, which
are also easily checked to hold when
m=1
(see
Watson (1961)
).
∎

In particular, the density function of
U^{[1]}
is given by

f_{1}(x)=4\sum_{k\geqslant 1}(-1)^{k-1}(k\pi)^{2}e^{-2(k\pi)^{2}x}\qquad(x>0);

as in
Watson (1961)
, and that of
U^{[2]}
by

f_{2}(x)=32\sum_{k\geqslant 1}\frac{(-1)^{k+1}(k\pi)^{5}}{\sinh(k\pi)}\,e^{-8(k\pi)^{4}x}\qquad(x\geqslant 0).

4
The generalized Cramér-von Mises statistics

In this section, we examine the corresponding generalized
Cramér-von Mises (GCvM) test statistics.

4.1
A new class of GCvM goodness-of-fit statistics

Recall that
F_{N}(x)
denotes the empirical distribution function of
the i.i.d. sequence of random variables
\{X_{1},\ldots,X_{N}\}
. The
Cramér-von Mises statistics is defined by

\omega_{N}=\int_{0}^{1}\widetilde{B}_{N}(x)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x,

where
\widetilde{B}_{N}(x)=\sqrt{N}(F_{N}(x)-x)=\sqrt{N}\int_{0}^{1}(\mathds{1}_{\{t\leqslant x\}}-x)\,\mathrm{d}F_{N}(t)
is as before. We
generalize the GCvM statistic by defining

\omega^{[m]}_{N}=\int_{0}^{1}\widetilde{B}^{[m]}_{N}(x)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\qquad(m\geqslant 1),

where
\widetilde{B}^{[1]}_{N}(x)=\widetilde{B}_{N}(x)
and

\widetilde{B}^{[m]}_{N}(x)=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\int_{0}^{x}\widetilde{B}^{[m-1]}_{N}(t)\,\mathrm{d}t,&\mbox{if $m$ is odd},\\
\displaystyle\int_{0}^{x}\widetilde{B}^{[m-1]}_{N}(t)\,\mathrm{d}t-\int_{0}^{1}(1-t)\widetilde{B}^{[m-1]}_{N}(t)\,\mathrm{d}t,&\mbox{if $m$ is even}.\end{cases}

As we will see in detail later, the correction term
-\int_{0}^{1}(1-t)\widetilde{B}^{[m-1]}_{N}(t)\,\mathrm{d}t
in the even case has the effect of
keeping the template functions invariant in the sense of
(
81
), which induces the invariance of the
statistics under the reflection of the data
X_{i}\mapsto 1-X_{i}
.

The iterated empirical measures
\widetilde{B}^{[m]}_{N}(x)
are also
expressible in terms of template functions. Let
b_{m}(y)
be a
normalized Bernoulli polynomial defined in (
53
).

Lemma 4.1
.

For
m\geqslant 1

\widetilde{B}^{[m]}_{N}(x)=\int_{0}^{1}\widetilde{\tau}_{m}(t;x)\,\mathrm{d}F_{N}(t),

where

\displaystyle\widetilde{\tau}_{m}(t;x)

\displaystyle=\frac{(x-t)^{m-1}}{(m-1)!}\mathds{1}_{\{t\leqslant x\}}-2^{m-1}\left(b_{m}\left(\text{\scalebox{.9}{$\frac{t+x}{2}$}}\right)+(-1)^{m}b_{m}\left(\text{\scalebox{.9}{$\frac{t-x}{2}$}}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=(-1)^{m-1}2^{m-1}b_{m}\left(\text{\scalebox{.9}{$\frac{t-x}{2}$}}+1\right)\mathds{1}_{\{t\leqslant x\}}+(-1)^{m-1}2^{m-1}b_{m}\left(\text{\scalebox{.9}{$\frac{t-x}{2}$}}\right)\mathds{1}_{\{t>x\}}

\displaystyle\qquad-2^{m-1}b_{m}\left(\text{\scalebox{.9}{$\frac{t+x}{2}$}}\right).

(80)

Following the same construction of the template functions used in
the generalizations of the AD and Watson statistics, we require the
template functions to be orthogonal to the eigenfunctions of lower
degrees. From the eigenfunction expansion (
89
)
given later, we also propose the truncated version of the template
functions

\widetilde{\tau}^{*}_{m}(t;x)=\widetilde{\tau}_{m}(t;x)-2\sum_{1\leqslant k<m}\frac{1}{(\pi k)^{m}}\cos\Bigl(k\pi x-\text{\scalebox{.9}{$\frac{m\pi}{2}$}}\Bigr)\cos(k\pi t)

and define the truncated version of the generalized CvM statistics

\omega^{*[m]}_{N}=\int_{0}^{1}\widetilde{B}^{*[m]}_{N}(x)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\quad\text{where}\quad\widetilde{B}^{*[m]}_{N}(x)=\int_{0}^{1}\widetilde{\tau}^{*}_{m}(t;x)\,\mathrm{d}F_{N}(x).

A graphical rendering of the first four template functions of

\widetilde{\tau}^{*}_{m}(t;x)
is given in Figure
4.1
.

Fig 4.1:
Template functions
\widetilde{\tau}^{*}_{m}(\cdot;x_{0})

for the generalized Cramér-von Mises statistics; here

x_{0}=\frac{\sqrt{5}-1}{2}
, the reciprocal of the golden ratio.

From the definitions, we obtain the following invariance properties.

Lemma 4.2
.

The template functions satisfy

\widetilde{\tau}_{m}(1-t;1-x)=(-1)^{m}\widetilde{\tau}_{m}(t;x)\quad\text{and}\quad\widetilde{\tau}^{*}_{m}(1-t;1-x)=(-1)^{m}\widetilde{\tau}^{*}_{m}(t;x).

(81)

The generalized CvM statistics are invariant under the reflection of
the data
X_{i}\mapsto 1-X_{i}
.

Proof of Lemma
4.1
.

Write

\widetilde{\tau}_{m}(t;x)=\frac{(x-t)^{m-1}}{(m-1)!}\,\mathds{1}_{\{0\leqslant t\leqslant x\}}-g_{m}(t;x),

where

g_{m}(t;x)=2^{m-1}\left(b_{m}\left(\text{\scalebox{.9}{$\frac{t+x}{2}$}}\right)+(-1)^{m}b_{m}\left(\text{\scalebox{.9}{$\frac{t-x}{2}$}}\right)\right).

Let
g_{0}(t;x)=x
. Then,
g_{m}(t;x)
satisfies, for
m\geqslant 2
,

\begin{split}g_{m}(t;x)=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\int_{0}^{x}g_{m-1}(t;v)\,\mathrm{d}v,&\mbox{if $m$ is odd},\\
\displaystyle\int_{0}^{x}g_{m-1}(t;v)\,\mathrm{d}v+\frac{(1-t)^{m}}{m!}\\
\displaystyle\qquad-\int_{0}^{1}(1-v)g_{m-1}(t;v)\,\mathrm{d}v,&\mbox{if $m$ is even}.\end{cases}\end{split}

(82)

To solve this recurrence relation, we write

g_{m}(t;x)=\sum_{0\leqslant k\leqslant m}g_{m,k}(t)x^{k}\qquad(m\geqslant 1).

Then we have
g_{1,1}=1
, and

g_{m,k}=\frac{g_{m-1,k-1}}{k}=\cdots=\frac{g_{m-k,0}}{k!}\qquad(m\geqslant 2;k\geqslant 0).

Thus it suffices to examine the sequence
c_{m}:=g_{m,0}
in more
detail. By (
82
), we see that
c_{m}=0
if
m
is odd, and

c_{m}=\frac{(1-t)^{m}}{m!}-\sum_{0\leqslant j<m}\frac{c_{m-1-j}}{(j+2)!}\qquad(m\geqslant 2),

if
m
is even, with
c_{0}:=1
. This sequence is easily solved by
considering the generating function
c(z):=\sum_{m\geqslant 0}c_{2m}z^{m}
,
which satisfies

c(z)=\frac{\sqrt{z}(e^{(2-t)\sqrt{z}}+e^{t\sqrt{z}})}{e^{2\sqrt{z}}-1},

and it follows that for even
m\geqslant 0

c_{m}=2^{m-1}\left(b_{m}\Bigl({1-\text{\scalebox{.9}{$\frac{t}{2}$}}}\Bigr)+b_{m}\Bigl({\text{\scalebox{.9}{$\frac{t}{2}$}}}\Bigr)\right).

Note that this holds also for odd
m
for which
c_{m}=0
. By the
known relation

\displaystyle b_{m}(1-x)=(-1)^{m}b_{m}(x),

(83)

we see that
c_{m}=2^{m}\,b_{m}\left(\text{\scalebox{.9}{$\frac{t}{2}$}}\right)
when
m
is
even. Consequently,

g_{m}(t;x)=\sum_{0\leqslant j\leqslant\left\lfloor m/2\right\rfloor}\frac{2^{2j}x^{m-2j}}{(m-2j)!}\,b_{2j}\left(\text{\scalebox{.9}{$\frac{t}{2}$}}\right)\qquad(m\geqslant 0).

This convolution sum can be simplified as follows.

\displaystyle g_{m}(t;x)

\displaystyle=[z^{m}]e^{xz}\left(\frac{ze^{tz}}{e^{2z}-1}+\frac{ze^{-tz}}{1-e^{-2z}}\right)

\displaystyle=[z^{m}]\frac{ze^{xz}\left(e^{tz}+e^{(2-t)z}\right)}{e^{2z}-1}

\displaystyle=2^{m-1}\left(b_{m}\left(\text{\scalebox{.9}{$\frac{x+t}{2}$}}\right)+b_{m}\left(1+\text{\scalebox{.9}{$\frac{x-t}{2}$}}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=2^{m-1}\left(b_{m}\left(\text{\scalebox{.9}{$\frac{x+t}{2}$}}\right)+(-1)^{m}b_{m}\left(\text{\scalebox{.9}{$\frac{t-x}{2}$}}\right)\right),

(84)

which completes the proof of the lemma.
∎

4.2
The limiting GCvM processes: KL expansions and covariance kernel

In this section, we examine the limiting distribution of the GCvM
statistic under the null hypothesis
H_{0}
. The arguments are almost
identical to those used for the GW statistics in Sec.
3.2
; thus some details are omitted.

Again the Wiener process on
[0,1]
and the Brownian bridge are
denoted by
W(x)
and
B(x)
, respectively. Using the template

\widetilde{\tau}_{m}(t;x)
given in (
80
), we define
the limiting GCvM processes

\displaystyle\widetilde{B}^{[m]}(x)=\int_{0}^{1}\widetilde{\tau}_{m}(t;x)\,\mathrm{d}W(t)=\int_{0}^{1}\widetilde{\tau}_{m}(t;x)\,\mathrm{d}B(t),

\displaystyle\widetilde{B}^{*[m]}(x)=\int_{0}^{1}\widetilde{\tau}^{*}_{m}(t;x)\,\mathrm{d}W(t)=\int_{0}^{1}\widetilde{\tau}^{*}_{m}(t;x)\,\mathrm{d}B(t).

Since the Brownian bridge
B(\cdot)
is of the class
C^{0}
on

[0,1]
, we have by induction that
\widetilde{B}^{[m]}(\cdot)
and

\widetilde{B}^{*[m]}(\cdot)
are of the class
C^{m-1}
on
[0,1]
.

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
\mu
be any finite measure on
[0,1]
. Then, as
N\to\infty
,

\widetilde{B}^{[m]}_{N}(\cdot)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\to}}\widetilde{B}^{[m]}(\cdot)
and
\widetilde{B}^{*[m]}_{N}(\cdot)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\to}}\widetilde{B}^{*[m]}(\cdot)
in
L^{2}([0,1],\mu)

and in
L^{\infty}([0,1])
, and
\omega^{[m]}_{N}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\to}}\omega^{[m]}
and
\omega^{*[m]}_{N}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\to}}\omega^{*[m]}
,
where

\omega^{[m]}=\int_{0}^{1}\widetilde{B}^{[m]}(x)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\quad\text{and}\quad\omega^{*[m]}=\int_{0}^{1}\widetilde{B}^{*[m]}(x)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x.

Proof.

The same as that for Lemma
3.2
.
∎

We now derive the covariance functions of the continuous centered
Gaussian processes
\widetilde{B}^{[m]}(\cdot)
on
[0,1]
.

Theorem 4.1
.

For
m\geqslant 1

\displaystyle\mathrm{Cov}\bigl({\widetilde{B}^{[m]}(x),\widetilde{B}^{[m]}(y)}\bigr)

\displaystyle=-2^{2m-1}\left(b_{2m}\Bigl({\text{\scalebox{.9}{$\frac{x+y}{2}$}}}\Bigr)+(-1)^{m}b_{2m}\Bigl({\text{\scalebox{.9}{$\frac{|x-y|}{2}$}}}\Bigr)\right)

\displaystyle=\displaystyle 2\sum_{k\geqslant 1}\frac{\cos\bigl({k\pi x-\frac{1}{2}m\pi}\bigr)\cos\bigl({k\pi y-\frac{1}{2}m\pi}\bigr)}{(k\pi)^{2m}},

(85)

\displaystyle\mathrm{Cov}\bigl({\widetilde{B}^{*[m]}(x),\widetilde{B}^{*[m]}(y)}\bigr)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Cov}\bigl({\widetilde{B}^{[m]}(x),\widetilde{B}^{[m]}(y)}\bigr)

(86)

\displaystyle-2\sum_{1\leqslant k<m}\frac{\cos\bigl({k\pi x-\frac{1}{2}m\pi}\bigr)\cos\bigl({k\pi y-\frac{1}{2}m\pi}\bigr)}{(k\pi)^{2m}}.

In particular,

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}\bigl({\widetilde{B}^{[m]}(x)}\bigr)=-2^{2m-1}\left(b_{2m}(x)+(-1)^{m}b_{2m}(0)\right),

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}\bigl({\widetilde{B}^{*[m]}(x)}\bigr)=\mathrm{Var}\bigl({\widetilde{B}^{[m]}(x)}\bigr)-2\sum_{1\leqslant k<m,\,k:\rm even}\frac{1}{(k\pi)^{2m}}.

The covariance functions (
85
) for
m=1,2
are given,
respectively, by

\displaystyle\mathrm{Cov}\bigl({\widetilde{B}^{[1]}(x),\widetilde{B}^{[1]}(y)}\bigr)

\displaystyle=x\wedge y-xy,

\displaystyle\mathrm{Cov}\bigl({\widetilde{B}^{[2]}(x),\widetilde{B}^{[2]}(y)}\bigr)

\displaystyle=\text{\scalebox{.9}{$\frac{1}{45}$}}-\text{\scalebox{.9}{$\frac{x^{2}+y^{2}}{6}$}}+\text{\scalebox{.9}{$\frac{(x\vee y)^{3}+3(x\wedge y)xy}{6}$}}-\text{\scalebox{.9}{$\frac{x^{4}+6x^{2}y^{2}+y^{4}}{24}$}}.

Proof of Theorem
4.1
.

The generating function of
g_{m}(t;x)
in (
84
) is

\displaystyle G(z;t,x)=\sum_{m\geqslant 1}g_{m}(t;x)z^{m-1}=\frac{e^{xz}\left(e^{tz}+e^{(2-t)z}\right)}{e^{2z}-1}-\frac{1}{z},

(87)

and

H(z;t,x)=\sum_{m\geqslant 1}\widetilde{\tau}_{m}(t;x)z^{m-1}=e^{(x-t)z}\mathds{1}_{\{0\leqslant t\leqslant x\}}-G(z;t,x).

Thus the covariance function of
\widetilde{B}^{[m]}(\cdot)
can be
represented as

\displaystyle K_{m}(x,y)

\displaystyle:=\mathrm{Cov}\bigl({\widetilde{B}^{[m]}(x),\widetilde{B}^{[m]}(y)}\bigr)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\widetilde{\tau}_{m}(t;x)\widetilde{\tau}_{m}(t;y)\,\mathrm{d}t=[z^{m-1}w^{m-1}]J(z,w;x,y),

where

\displaystyle J(z,w;x,y)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}H(z;t,x)H(w;t,y)\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{x\wedge y}e^{(x-t)z+(y-t)w}\,\mathrm{d}t-\int_{0}^{x}e^{(x-t)z}G(w;t,y)\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\quad-\int_{0}^{y}e^{(y-t)w}G(z;t,x)\,\mathrm{d}t+\int_{0}^{1}G(z;t,x)G(w;t,y)\,\mathrm{d}t.

Let
\Delta:=x+y
. Straightforward calculations using (
87
)
yield

\displaystyle J(z,w;x,y)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{z-w}\left(\frac{e^{w\Delta}}{e^{2w}-1}-\frac{e^{z\Delta}}{e^{2z}-1}\right)-\frac{1}{zw}

\displaystyle\qquad+\begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac{1}{z+w}\left(\frac{e^{(2-\delta)z}}{e^{2z}-1}+\frac{e^{\delta w}}{e^{2w}-1}\right),&\mbox{if }\delta:=y-x>0,\\
\displaystyle\frac{1}{z+w}\left(\frac{e^{\delta z}}{e^{2z}-1}+\frac{e^{(2-\delta)w}}{e^{2w}-1}\right),&\mbox{if }\delta:=x-y>0.\end{cases}

It follows that

\displaystyle[z^{m-1}w^{m-1}]\frac{1}{z-w}\left(\frac{e^{w\Delta}}{e^{2w}-1}-\frac{e^{z\Delta}}{e^{2z}-1}\right)-\frac{1}{2zw}

\displaystyle\qquad=[z^{m-1}w^{m-1}]\sum_{\ell\geqslant 2}b_{\ell}\left(\text{\scalebox{.9}{$\frac{\Delta}{2}$}}\right)\,2^{\ell-1}\frac{w^{\ell-1}-z^{\ell-1}}{z-w}

\displaystyle\qquad=-b_{2m}\left(\text{\scalebox{.9}{$\frac{\Delta}{2}$}}\right)\,2^{2m-1}.

Similarly, by (
83
),

\displaystyle[z^{m-1}w^{m-1}]\frac{1}{z+w}\left(\frac{e^{(2-\delta)z}}{e^{2z}-1}+\frac{e^{\delta w}}{e^{2w}-1}\right)-\frac{1}{2zw}

\displaystyle\qquad=[z^{m-1}w^{m-1}]\sum_{\ell\geqslant 2}b_{\ell}\left(\text{\scalebox{.9}{$\frac{\delta}{2}$}}\right)\,2^{\ell-1}\frac{w^{\ell-1}-(-z)^{\ell-1}}{z+w}

\displaystyle\qquad=(-1)^{m-1}b_{2m}\left(\text{\scalebox{.9}{$\frac{\delta}{2}$}}\right)\,2^{2m-1},

when
x<y
. The same expression also holds when
x>y
provided we
replace
\delta
by
x-y
. Thus in either case

K_{m}(x,y)=-2^{2m-1}\left(b_{2m}\left(\text{\scalebox{.9}{$\frac{x+y}{2}$}}\right)+(-1)^{m}b_{2m}\left(\text{\scalebox{.9}{$\frac{|x-y|}{2}$}}\right)\right).

Note that this identity implies another one by its decomposition

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{x\wedge y}\frac{(x-t)^{m-1}(y-u)^{m-1}}{(m-1)!^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}t-\int_{0}^{x}\frac{(x-t)^{m-1}}{(m-1)!}\,g_{m}(t;y)\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\qquad-\int_{0}^{y}\frac{(y-t)^{m-1}}{(m-1)!}\,g_{m}(t;x)\,\mathrm{d}t+\int_{0}^{1}g_{m}(t;x)g_{m}(t;y)\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=-2^{2m-1}\left(b_{2m}\Bigl({\text{\scalebox{.9}{$\frac{x+y}{2}$}}}\Bigr)+(-1)^{m}b_{2m}\left(\text{\scalebox{.9}{$\frac{|x-y|}{2}$}}\right)\right).

(88)

The proof of (

The former follows from the same generating function approach used

∎

Starting from the expansion

Theorem 4.2
.

The proof follows
mutatis mutandis
that of Theorem

\displaystyle K_{m}^{*}(s,t)

\displaystyle L_{m,3}

.

Consider first the integral

(-1)^{m+k+1}\int_{a}^{1}\frac{\phi_{m,k}(1-x)}{x^{m}}\,f_{m,\ell}(1-x)\,\mathrm{d}x=\int_{a}^{1}\frac{\phi_{m,k}(x)}{x^{m}}\,f_{m,\ell}(1-x)\,\mathrm{d}x.

We begin by deriving a more explicit expression for
\phi_{m,k}(x)

using (
8
):

\displaystyle\frac{\phi_{m,k}(x)}{\sqrt{2k+1}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(m-1)!\sqrt{2k+1}}\int_{0}^{1}(1-x-t)^{m-1}P_{k}(t)\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(m-1)!}\sum_{0\leqslant r<m}\binom{m-1}{r}(-x)^{m-1-r}

\displaystyle\hskip 71.13188pt\times\sum_{0\leqslant\ell\leqslant k}\binom{k+\ell}{\ell}\binom{k}{\ell}(-1)^{k+\ell}\int_{0}^{1}(1-t)^{r}t^{\ell}\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(m-1)!}\sum_{0\leqslant r<m}\binom{m-1}{r}(-x)^{m-1-r}

\displaystyle\hskip 71.13188pt\times\sum_{0\leqslant\ell\leqslant k}\binom{k+\ell}{\ell}\binom{k}{\ell}(-1)^{k+\ell}\frac{\ell!r!}{(r+\ell+1)!}

\displaystyle=\sum_{0\leqslant r<m}\frac{(-x)^{m-1-r}}{(m-1-r)!}\sum_{0\leqslant\ell\leqslant k}\frac{(k+\ell)!}{\ell!(k-\ell)!(r+\ell+1)!}\,(-1)^{k+\ell}.

By the identity

\sum_{0\leqslant\ell\leqslant k}\frac{(k+\ell)!(-1)^{k+\ell}}{\ell!(k-\ell)!(r+\ell+1)!}=\frac{(-1)^{k}r!}{(r-k)!(r+k+1)!}\qquad(r\geqslant k),

we then obtain

\frac{\phi_{m,k}(x)}{\sqrt{2k+1}}=\sum_{k\leqslant r<m}\frac{r!(-1)^{k+m-1-r}}{(m-1-r)!(r-k)!(r+k+1)!}\,x^{m-1-r}.

Consider now

\displaystyle\sum_{0\leqslant k<m}P_{k}(1-a)\phi_{m,k}(x)

\displaystyle\quad=(-1)^{m-1}\sum_{0\leqslant k<m}(2k+1)

\displaystyle\hskip 56.9055pt\times\sum_{0\leqslant r\leqslant k}\sum_{k\leqslant\ell<m}\frac{(k+r)!\ell!(-1)^{r+\ell}(1-a)^{r}x^{m-1-\ell}}{r!r!(k-r)!(m-1-\ell)!(\ell-k)!(\ell+k+1)!}

\displaystyle\quad=(-1)^{m-1}\sum_{0\leqslant r\leqslant\ell<m}\frac{\ell!(1-a)^{r}x^{m-1-\ell}}{r!r!(m-1-\ell)!}(-1)^{r+\ell}

\displaystyle\hskip 56.9055pt\times\sum_{r\leqslant k\leqslant\ell}\frac{(2k+1)(k-r)!}{(k-r)!(\ell-k)!(\ell+k+1)!}

\displaystyle\quad=(-1)^{m-1}\sum_{0\leqslant r\leqslant\ell<m}\frac{\ell!(1-a)^{r}x^{m-1-\ell}}{r!r!(m-1-\ell)!}(-1)^{r+\ell}{\frac{r!}{\ell!(\ell-r)!}}

\displaystyle\quad=(-1)^{m-1}\sum_{0\leqslant r\leqslant\ell<m}\frac{(1-a)^{r}x^{m-1-\ell}}{r!(m-1-\ell)!(\ell-r)!}(-1)^{r+\ell}

\displaystyle\quad=(-1)^{m-1}\frac{(x-a)^{m-1}}{(m-1)!}.

Now consider the partial sum

\displaystyle\sum_{0\leqslant k<m}P_{k}(1-a)f_{m,k}(1-x)

\displaystyle\quad=\sum_{0\leqslant k<m}(2k+1)\sum_{0\leqslant r_{1},r_{2}\leqslant k}\frac{(-1)^{r_{1}+r_{2}}(k+r_{1})!(k+r_{2})!(1-a)^{r_{1}}(1-x)^{r_{2}}}{r_{1}!r_{1}!(k-r_{1})!r_{2}!(m+r_{2})!(k-r_{2})!}

\displaystyle\quad=\sum_{0\leqslant r_{1},r_{2}<m}\frac{(-1)^{r_{1}+r_{2}}(1-a)^{r_{1}}(1-x)^{r_{2}}}{r_{1}!r_{1}!r_{2}!(m+r_{2})!}{\sum_{0\leqslant k<m}(2k+1)\frac{(k+r_{1})!(k+r_{2})!}{(k-r_{1})!(k-r_{2})!}}

\displaystyle\quad=\sum_{0\leqslant r_{1},r_{2}<m}\frac{(-1)^{r_{1}+r_{2}}(1-a)^{r_{1}}(1-x)^{r_{2}}}{r_{1}!r_{1}!r_{2}!(m+r_{2})!}\cdot{\frac{(m+r_{1})!(m+r_{2})!}{(r_{1}+r_{2}+1)(m-1-r_{1})!(m-1-r_{2})!}}

\displaystyle\quad=\sum_{0\leqslant r_{1}<m}\frac{(-1)^{r_{1}}(m+r_{1})!(1-a)^{r_{1}}}{r_{1}!r_{1}!(m-1-r_{1})!}\sum_{0\leqslant r_{2}<m}\frac{(-1)^{r_{2}}(1-x)^{r_{2}}}{r_{2}!(r_{1}+r_{2}+1)(m-1-r_{2})!},

where we used the same identity (
30
) as above.
Observe that

\displaystyle\sum_{0\leqslant r_{2}<m}\frac{(-1)^{r_{2}}(1-x)^{r_{2}}}{r_{2}!(r_{1}+r_{2}+1)(m-1-r_{2})!}

\displaystyle\qquad=\frac{1}{(m-1)!}\int_{0}^{1}u^{r_{1}}\left(1-(1-x)u\right)^{m-1}\,\mathrm{d}u

\displaystyle\qquad=\frac{1}{(m-1)!}\sum_{0\leqslant r_{2}<m}\binom{m-1}{r_{2}}x^{r_{2}}\int_{0}^{1}(1-u)^{m-1-r_{2}}u^{r_{1}+r_{2}}\,\mathrm{d}u

\displaystyle\qquad=\frac{1}{(m-1)!}\sum_{0\leqslant r_{2}<m}\binom{m-1}{r_{2}}\frac{(m-1-r_{2})!(r_{1}+r_{2})!}{(m+r_{1})!}\,x^{r_{2}}

\displaystyle\qquad=\sum_{0\leqslant r_{2}<m}\frac{(r_{1}+r_{2})!}{r_{2}!(m+r_{1})!}\,x^{r_{2}}.

It follows that

\displaystyle\sum_{0\leqslant k<m}P_{k}(1-a)f_{m,k}(1-x)

(101)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(m-1)!}\sum_{0\leqslant r_{2}<m}x^{r_{2}}\sum_{0\leqslant r_{1}<m}\binom{r_{1}+r_{2}}{r_{1}}\binom{m-1}{r_{1}}(-1)^{r_{1}}(1-a)^{r_{1}}.

In a similar way,

\displaystyle\sum_{0\leqslant\ell<m}P_{\ell}(b)f_{m,\ell}(1-x)

(102)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(m-1)!}\sum_{0\leqslant\ell_{2}<m}x^{\ell_{2}}\sum_{0\leqslant\ell_{1}<m}\binom{\ell_{1}+\ell_{2}}{\ell_{1}}\binom{m-1}{\ell_{1}}(-1)^{\ell_{1}}b^{\ell_{1}}.

Consequently,

\displaystyle\int_{a}^{1}\frac{1}{x^{m}}\sum_{0\leqslant k,\ell<m}P_{k}(1-a)P_{\ell}(b)\phi_{m,k}(x)f_{m,\ell}(1-x)\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\quad=\frac{(-1)^{m-1}}{(m-1)!^{2}}\int_{a}^{1}\frac{(x-a)^{m-1}}{x^{m}}\sum_{0\leqslant k,\ell<m}\binom{k+\ell}{\ell}\binom{m-1}{k}(-1)^{k}b^{k}x^{\ell}\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\quad=\frac{(-1)^{m-1}}{(m-1)!^{2}}\int_{a}^{1}\frac{(x-a)^{m-1}}{x^{m}}[z^{0}]\sum_{0\leqslant k,\ell<m}z^{-\ell}(1-z)^{-k-1}\binom{m-1}{k}(-1)^{k}b^{k}x^{\ell}\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\quad=\frac{(-1)^{m-1}}{(m-1)!^{2}}[z^{m-1}]\frac{(1-b-z)^{m-1}}{(1-z)^{m}}\int_{a}^{1}\frac{(x-a)^{m-1}}{x-z}\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\quad=\frac{1}{(m-1)!^{2}}[z^{m-1}]\frac{(1-b-z)^{m-1}}{(1-z)^{m}}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\times\sum_{0\leqslant\ell<m}\binom{m-1}{\ell}(-1)^{\ell}(a-z)^{m-1-\ell}\int_{a}^{1}(x-z)^{\ell-1}\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\quad=\frac{1}{(m-1)!^{2}}[z^{m-1}]\frac{(1-b-z)^{m-1}}{(1-z)^{m}}\left((a-z)^{m-1}\log\frac{1-z}{a-z}\right.

\displaystyle\qquad\left.+\sum_{1\leqslant\ell<m}\binom{m-1}{\ell}\frac{(-1)^{\ell}}{\ell}\left((1-z)^{\ell}(a-z)^{m-1-\ell}-(a-z)^{m-1}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\quad=\frac{1}{(m-1)!^{2}}[z^{m-1}]\Biggl\{\frac{(1-b-z)^{m-1}(a-z)^{m-1}}{(1-z)^{m}}\left(\log\frac{1-z}{1-\frac{z}{a}}-\log a\right)

\displaystyle\hskip 113.81102pt+\sum_{1\leqslant\ell<m}\binom{m-1}{\ell}\frac{(-1)^{\ell}}{\ell}\cdot\frac{(1-b-z)^{m-1}(a-z)^{m-1-\ell}}{(1-z)^{m-\ell}}

\displaystyle\hskip 113.81102pt-\frac{(1-b-z)^{m-1}(a-z)^{m-1}}{(1-z)^{m}}\sum_{1\leqslant\ell<m}\binom{m-1}{\ell}\frac{(-1)^{\ell}}{\ell}\Biggr\}.

Note that, by the change of variables
z\mapsto-au/(1-a-u)
, we
obtain

\displaystyle[z^{m-1}]\frac{(1-b-z)^{m-1}(a-z)^{m-1}}{(1-z)^{m}}

\displaystyle\qquad=(-1)^{m-1}[u^{m-1}]\frac{((1-a-b)(1-u)+ab)^{m-1}}{(1-u)^{m}}

\displaystyle\qquad=(-1)^{m-1}[u^{m-1}]\frac{((1-a-b)u+ab)^{m-1}}{(1-u)^{m}}

\displaystyle\qquad=(-1)^{m-1}\sum_{0\leqslant\ell<m}\binom{m-1}{\ell}(ab)^{\ell}(1-a-b)^{m-1-\ell}[u^{\ell}](1-u)^{-m}

\displaystyle\qquad=(-1)^{m-1}\sum_{0\leqslant\ell<m}\binom{m-1}{\ell}\binom{m-1+\ell}{\ell}(ab)^{\ell}(1-a-b)^{m-1-\ell}.

Similarly,

\displaystyle[z^{m-1}]\frac{(1-b-z)^{m-1}(a-z)^{m-1}}{(1-z)^{m}}\log\frac{1-z}{1-\frac{z}{a}}

\displaystyle\quad=(-1)^{m}\sum_{0\leqslant\ell<m}\binom{m-1}{\ell}\binom{m-1+\ell}{\ell}(ab)^{\ell}(1-a-b)^{m-1-\ell}\left(H_{m-1+\ell}-H_{\ell}\right).

Also

\sum_{1\leqslant\ell<m}\binom{m-1}{\ell}\frac{(-1)^{\ell}}{\ell}=-H_{m-1}.

This proves (
100
).
∎

We now examine the partial sum involving last integral in
(
99
).

\sum_{0\leqslant k,\ell<m}(-1)^{m+k}P_{k}(1-a)P_{\ell}(b)\int_{0}^{1}f_{m,k}(1-x)f_{m,\ell}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x.

By (
8
) and (
96
), this is already a
polynomial in
a
of degree
m-1
and in
b
of degree
m-1
. We can
derive a more precise but cumbersome expression for the coefficients.

Lemma A.4
.

\sum_{0\leqslant k,\ell<m}(-1)^{m+k}P_{k}(1-a)P_{\ell}(b)\int_{0}^{1}f_{m,k}(1-x)f_{m,\ell}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x=\sum_{0\leqslant k,\ell<m}d_{m,k,\ell}a^{k}b^{\ell},

where

d_{m,k,\ell}:=(-1)^{m+k+\ell}\frac{(k+\ell)!(H_{m+\ell}-H_{\ell})+\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leqslant r<m\\
r\neq\ell\end{subarray}}\frac{(k+r)!}{\ell-r}\left(\frac{(m+\ell)!}{(m+r)!}-\frac{\ell!}{r!}\right)}{k!^{2}\ell!^{2}(m-1-k)!(m-1-\ell)!}.

Proof.

We use the two expansions (
101
) and
(
102
), and obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{0\leqslant k,\ell<m}(-1)^{m+k}P_{k}(1-a)P_{\ell}(b)f_{m,k}(1-x)f_{m,\ell}(x)

\displaystyle\quad=(-1)^{m}\sum_{0\leqslant k,\ell<m}P_{k}(a)P_{\ell}(b)f_{m,k}(1-x)f_{m,\ell}(x)

\displaystyle\quad=\frac{(-1)^{m}}{(m-1)!^{2}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leqslant k_{2},\ell_{2}<m\\
0\leqslant k_{1},\ell_{1}<m\end{subarray}}\binom{k_{1}+k_{2}}{k_{1}}\binom{\ell_{1}+\ell_{2}}{\ell_{1}}\binom{m-1}{k_{1}}\binom{m-1}{\ell_{1}}

\displaystyle\hskip 142.26378pt\times(-1)^{k_{1}+\ell_{1}}a^{k_{1}}b^{\ell_{1}}x^{k_{2}}(1-x)^{\ell_{2}}

\displaystyle\quad=(-1)^{m}\hskip-14.22636pt\sum_{0\leqslant k_{1},k_{2},\ell_{1},\ell_{2}<m}\frac{(k_{1}+k_{2})!(\ell_{1}+\ell_{2})!(-1)^{k_{1}+\ell_{1}}a^{k_{1}}b^{\ell_{1}}}{k_{1}!k_{1}!k_{2}!\ell_{1}!\ell_{1}!\ell_{2}!(m—1-k_{1})!(m-1-\ell_{1})!}\,x^{k_{2}}(1-x)^{\ell_{2}}.

Thus

\displaystyle\sum_{0\leqslant k,\ell<m}(-1)^{m+k}P_{k}(1-a)P_{\ell}(b)\int_{0}^{1}f_{m,k}(1-x)f_{m,\ell}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\quad=(-1)^{m}\sum_{0\leqslant k_{1},k_{2},\ell_{1},\ell_{2}<m}\frac{(k_{1}+k_{2})!(\ell_{1}+\ell_{2})!(-1)^{k_{1}+\ell_{1}}a^{k_{1}}b^{\ell_{1}}}{k_{1}!k_{1}!\ell_{1}!\ell_{1}!(m-1-k_{1})!(m-1-\ell_{1})!(k_{2}+\ell_{2}+1)!}

\displaystyle\quad=(-1)^{m}\sum_{0\leqslant k_{1},\ell_{1}<m}\frac{(-1)^{k_{1}+\ell_{1}}a^{k_{1}}b^{\ell_{1}}}{k_{1}!k_{1}!\ell_{1}!\ell_{1}!(m-1-k_{1})!(m-1-\ell_{1})!}

\displaystyle\hskip 85.35826pt\times\sum_{0\leqslant k_{2},\ell_{2}<m}\frac{(k_{1}+k_{2})!(\ell_{1}+\ell_{2})!}{(k_{2}+\ell_{2}+1)!}.

Now

\sum_{0\leqslant\ell_{2}<m}\frac{(k_{1}+k_{2})!(\ell_{1}+\ell_{2})!}{(k_{2}+\ell_{2}+1)!}=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac{(k_{1}+k_{2})!}{\ell_{1}-k_{2}}\left(\frac{(m+\ell_{1})!}{(m+k_{2})!}-\frac{\ell_{1}!}{k_{2}!}\right),&\mbox{if }k_{2}\neq\ell_{1},\\
(k_{1}+k_{2})!(H_{m+\ell_{1}}-H_{\ell_{1}}),&\mbox{if }k_{2}=\ell_{1}.\end{cases}

This proves (
A.4
).
∎
