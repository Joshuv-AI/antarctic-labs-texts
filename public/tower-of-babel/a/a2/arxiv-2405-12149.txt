Generating large primordial fluctuations in single-field inflation for PBH formation
Jason Kristiano, and Jun’ichi Yokoyama

Abstract
In order to produce appreciable amount of primordial black holes (PBHs), the square amplitude of curvature perturbation must take a large value of 𝒪 ⁡ ( 0.01 ) \mathcal{O}(0.01) , namely, seven digits larger than the value observed by cosmic microwave background radiation (CMB) on large scales. Such a large fluctuation can be achieved by violating the slow-roll (SR) condition within a short duration. The best known of such possibilities is the ultraslow-roll (USR) inflation. We calculate the power spectrum of curvature perturbation in a simple single-field inflation model which evolves through the SR-USR-SR regimes so that both large-amplitude small-scale fluctuation for PBH formation and small-amplitude large-scale fluctuation as observed by CMB are realized. We further calculate the bispectrum and one-loop correction to the power spectrum induced by the third-order action of curvature perturbation as the beginning of precision cosmology on small scales. As a result, we show that single-field inflation model realizing PBH formation can be constrained by the quantum correction.

2
Two-point functions

The action of canonical inflation is given by

S=\frac{1}{2}\int\mathrm{d}^{4}x\sqrt{-g}\left[M_{\mathrm{pl}}^{2}R-(\partial_{\mu}\phi)^{2}-2V(\phi)\right],

(1)

where
M_{\mathrm{pl}}
is reduced Planck scale,
g=\mathrm{det}~g_{\mu\nu}
,
g_{\mu\nu}
and
R
are metric tensor and its Ricci scalar. Consider a spatially flat, homogeneous, and isotropic background,

\mathrm{d}s^{2}=-\mathrm{d}t^{2}+a^{2}(t)\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}^{2}=a^{2}(\tau)(-\mathrm{d}\tau^{2}+\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}^{2}),

(2)

where
\tau
is conformal time. Equations of motion for the scale factor
a(t)
and the homogeneous part of the inflaton
\phi(t)
are the Friedmann equations

H^{2}=\frac{1}{3M_{\mathrm{pl}}^{2}}\left(\frac{1}{2}\dot{\phi}^{2}+V(\phi)\right),~\dot{H}=-\frac{\dot{\phi}^{2}}{2M_{\mathrm{pl}}^{2}},

(3)

with
H=\dot{a}/{a}
being the Hubble parameter, and the Klein-Gordon equation

\ddot{\phi}+3H\dot{\phi}+\frac{\mathrm{d}V}{\mathrm{d}\phi}=0.

(4)

Here, a dot denotes time derivative.

During inflation, the evolution of the Hubble parameter is parameterized by the slow-roll (SR) parameters. The SR parameters are defined as

\epsilon_{n+1}=\frac{\dot{\epsilon}_{n}}{\epsilon_{n}H},~\epsilon_{1}\equiv-\frac{\dot{H}}{H^{2}},

(5)

where
n
is a positive integer. In case the Hubble parameter
H
is almost a constant, we have
\epsilon_{1}\ll 1
. This condition is called the quasi-de Sitter (quasi-dS) approximation. Thus, the scale factor can be approximated as

a\approx-\frac{1}{H\tau}\propto e^{Ht}.

(6)

Another stronger condition is called the SR approximation, which is defined as
|\epsilon_{n}|\ll 1
for every
n
. Therefore, the SR approximation implies a quasi-dS approximation, while the converse statement is not true. Violation of the SR approximation does not imply a breakdown of the quasi-dS approximation.

Figure 1:
Schematic picture of the inflaton potential realizing PBH formation from USR condition. When the inflaton is around
\phi_{\mathrm{CMB}}
, scales probed by CMB observations leave the horizon and it is in the SR regime. It enters an extremely flat region at
t=t_{s}
undergoing an USR period. It enters the SR period again at
t=t_{e}
until
\phi_{\mathrm{end}}
, the end of inflation.

Small perturbation from the homogeneous part,
\phi(t)
, of the inflaton
\phi(\bm{x},t)
and metric can be expressed as

\displaystyle\phi(\bm{x},t)=\phi(t)+\delta\phi(\bm{x},t),

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}s^{2}=-N^{2}\mathrm{d}t^{2}+\gamma_{ij}(\mathrm{d}x^{i}+N^{i}\mathrm{d}t)(\mathrm{d}x^{j}+N^{j}\mathrm{d}t),

(7)

where
\gamma_{ij}
is the three-dimensional metric on slices of constant
t
,
N
is the lapse function, and
N^{i}
is the shift vector. We choose comoving gauge condition

\delta\phi(\bm{x},t)=0,~\gamma_{ij}(\bm{x},t)=a^{2}(t)e^{2\zeta(\bm{x},t)}\delta_{ij},

(8)

where
\zeta(\bm{x},t)
is comoving curvature perturbation.
N
and
N^{i}
are obtained by solving the constraint equations. In this review, we do not consider the tensor perturbation.

Expanding the action (
1
) up to the second order of the curvature perturbation yields

S^{(2)}[\zeta]=M_{\mathrm{pl}}^{2}\int\mathrm{d}t~\mathrm{d}^{3}x~a^{3}\epsilon_{1}\left[\dot{\zeta}^{2}-\frac{1}{a^{2}}(\partial_{i}\zeta)^{2}\right].

(9)

Introducing the Mukhanov-Sasaki variable by
v=zM_{\mathrm{pl}}\zeta
, where
z=a\sqrt{2\epsilon_{1}}
, the action becomes canonically normalized

S^{(2)}_{\mathrm{can}}[v]=\frac{1}{2}\int\mathrm{d}\tau~\mathrm{d}^{3}x\left[(v^{\prime})^{2}-(\partial_{i}v)^{2}+\frac{z^{\prime\prime}}{z}v^{2}\right],

(10)

where a prime denotes derivative with respect to
\tau
. We can see that the quantity
z^{\prime\prime}/z
behaves as an effective time-dependent mass squared. In momentum space, quantization is performed by promoting the Mukhanov-Sasaki variable to an operator

\hat{v}_{\bm{k}}(\tau)=M_{\mathrm{pl}}z(\tau)\hat{\zeta}_{\bm{k}}(\tau)=v_{k}(\tau)\hat{a}_{\bm{k}}+v^{*}_{k}(\tau)\hat{a}_{-\bm{k}}^{\dagger},

(11)

where mode function
v_{k}(\tau)
satisfies the Mukhanov-Sasaki equation

v_{k}^{\prime\prime}+\left(k^{2}-\frac{z^{\prime\prime}}{z}\right)v_{k}=0.

(12)

In terms of the SR parameters, the effective mass term can be written as

\frac{z^{\prime\prime}}{z}=(aH)^{2}\left(2-\epsilon_{1}+\frac{3}{2}\epsilon_{2}-\frac{1}{2}\epsilon_{1}\epsilon_{2}+\frac{1}{4}\epsilon_{2}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\epsilon_{2}\epsilon_{3}\right).

(13)

From (
10
) we find that
\pi^{v}=v^{\prime}
is momentum conjugate to
v
, so canonical quantization imposes a commutator

[\hat{v}_{\bm{k}}(\tau),\hat{\pi}_{\bm{k}^{\prime}}^{v}(\tau)]=i\hbar\delta(\bm{k}-\bm{k}^{\prime}),

(14)

and the operators satisfy
[\hat{a}_{\bm{k}},\hat{a}_{\bm{k}^{\prime}}^{\dagger}]=(2\pi)^{3}\delta(\bm{k}-\bm{k}^{\prime})
under the normalization condition

v_{k}^{\prime*}v_{k}-v_{k}^{\prime}v_{k}^{*}=i\hbar.

(15)

The two-point functions of curvature perturbation and power spectrum can be written as

\displaystyle\left\langle\zeta_{\bm{k}}(\tau)\zeta_{\bm{k}^{\prime}}(\tau)\right\rangle\equiv(2\pi)^{3}\delta^{3}(\bm{k}+\bm{k}^{\prime})\left\langle\!\left\langle\zeta_{\bm{k}}(\tau)\zeta_{-\bm{k}}(\tau)\right\rangle\!\right\rangle,

(16)

\displaystyle\Delta^{2}_{s}(k,\tau)\equiv\frac{k^{3}}{2\pi^{2}}\langle\!\langle\zeta_{\bm{k}}(\tau)\zeta_{-\bm{k}}(\tau)\rangle\!\rangle,

(17)

the bracket
\langle\cdots\rangle=\bra{0}\cdots\ket{0}
denotes the vacuum expectation value (VEV), and
\Delta^{2}_{s}(k,\tau)
is the power spectrum multiplied by the phase space density.

2.1
Slow-roll approximation

With SR approximation, the effective mass becomes

\frac{z^{\prime\prime}}{z}=(aH)^{2}\left[2-\epsilon_{1}+\frac{3}{2}\epsilon_{2}+\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{2})\right].

(18)

Up to first-order correction in SR approximation,
aH
as function of
\tau
reads

aH=-\frac{1}{\tau(1-\epsilon_{1})}.

(19)

Then, the effective mass can be expressed in terms of the conformal time as

\frac{z^{\prime\prime}}{z}=\frac{1}{\tau^{2}}\left(2+3\epsilon_{1}+\frac{3}{2}\epsilon_{2}\right)=\frac{1}{\tau^{2}}\left(\nu^{2}-\frac{1}{4}\right),

(20)

where
\nu
is defined as

\nu=\frac{3}{2}+\delta=\frac{3}{2}+\epsilon_{1}+\frac{1}{2}\epsilon_{2}.

(21)

At early time,
\tau\rightarrow-\infty
, the Mukhanov-Sasaki equation approaches

v_{k}^{\prime\prime}+k^{2}v_{k}=0,

(22)

which is the equation of a simple harmonic oscillator. Requiring minimum energy state and normalization (
15
), solution of the Mukhanov-Sasaki equation at early time is given by

v_{k}(\tau\rightarrow-\infty)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2k}}e^{-ik\tau}.

(23)

This minimum energy state corresponds to the initial vacuum state called Bunch-Davies vacuum, defined as
\hat{a}_{\bm{k}}\ket{0}=0
.

At general time, solution of the Mukhanov-Sasaki equation is given by

v_{k}(\tau)=\sqrt{-k\tau}\left[c_{1}H_{\nu}^{(1)}(-k\tau)+c_{2}H_{\nu}^{(2)}(-k\tau)\right],

(24)

where
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
are coefficients to be fixed to a boundary condition. Given the asymptotic limit of Hankel functions

\lim_{x\rightarrow\infty}H_{\nu}^{(1,2)}(x)=\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi x}}\exp\left[\pm i\left(x-\frac{\nu\pi}{2}-\frac{\pi}{4}\right)\right],

(25)

we can match the general solution to the early time limit (
23
) that leads to

c_{1}=\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{4k}}e^{i\frac{\pi}{4}(2\nu+1)}~~\mathrm{and}~~c_{2}=0.

(26)

Then, the mode function of Mukhanov-Sasaki variable becomes

v_{k}(\tau)=\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{4}}e^{i\frac{\pi}{4}(2\nu+1)}\sqrt{-\tau}H_{\nu}^{(1)}(-k\tau).

(27)

To obtain the mode function of the curvature perturbation, we need to know the explicit function of
z(\tau)
. Starting from

\frac{z^{\prime}}{z}=aH\left(1+\frac{1}{2}\epsilon_{2}\right),

(28)

we can substitute (
19
) to obtain

\displaystyle\int\frac{\mathrm{d}z}{z}

\displaystyle=-\int\frac{\mathrm{d}\tau}{\tau}\left(1+\epsilon_{1}+\frac{1}{2}\epsilon_{2}\right)

\displaystyle\frac{z(\tau)}{z_{\star}}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{\tau}{\tau_{\star}}\right)^{-\left(1+\epsilon_{1}+\frac{1}{2}\epsilon_{2}\right)},

(29)

where
\tau_{\star}
is an arbitrary time reference and
z_{\star}=z(\tau_{\star})
. Choosing
\tau_{\star}
as horizon crossing time
k=a(\tau_{\star})H(\tau_{\star})
so

\displaystyle\tau_{\star}

\displaystyle=-\left[\frac{1}{aH(1-\epsilon_{1})}\right]_{\star}=-\frac{1}{k(1-\epsilon_{1})_{\star}},

(30)

\displaystyle z_{\star}

\displaystyle=\left(a\sqrt{2\epsilon_{1}}\right)_{\star}=\left(\frac{k}{H}\sqrt{2\epsilon_{1}}\right)_{\star},

(31)

then the explicit function of
z(\tau)
is

z(\tau)=\left(\frac{k}{H}\sqrt{2\epsilon_{1}}\right)_{\star}\left[-k\tau(1-\epsilon_{1})_{\star}\right]^{-\left(1+\epsilon_{1}+\frac{1}{2}\epsilon_{2}\right)}.

(32)

Then, the mode function of curvature perturbation reads

\zeta_{k}(\tau)=\frac{v_{k}(\tau)}{M_{\mathrm{pl}}z(\tau)}=\left(\frac{H}{M_{\mathrm{pl}}k\sqrt{2\epsilon_{1}}}\right)_{\star}\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{4}}(-k\tau)^{\left(1+\epsilon_{1}+\frac{1}{2}\epsilon_{2}\right)}e^{i\frac{\pi}{4}(2\nu+1)}\sqrt{-\tau}H_{\nu}^{(1)}(-k\tau).

(33)

With another asymptotic limit of Hankel function

\lim_{x\rightarrow 0}H_{\nu}^{(1)}(x)=-\frac{i}{\pi}\Gamma(\nu)\left(\frac{x}{2}\right)^{-\nu},

(34)

the curvature perturbation at the end of inflation
\tau=\tau_{0}(\rightarrow 0)
, approaches

\zeta_{k}(\tau_{0})=\left(\frac{iH}{M_{\mathrm{pl}}\sqrt{2k^{3}\epsilon_{1}}}\right)_{\star}\frac{1}{\sqrt{4\pi}}2^{3/2}\Gamma(3/2).

(35)

Also, the power spectrum reads

\Delta^{2}_{s}(k,\tau_{0})=\left(\frac{H^{2}}{8\pi^{2}M_{\mathrm{pl}}^{2}\epsilon_{1}}\right)_{\star},

(36)

with a weak
k
-dependence due to the horizon crossing condition manifested in the spectral tilt

n_{s}(k,\tau_{0})-1=\frac{\mathrm{d}\log\Delta_{s}^{2}(k,\tau_{0})}{\mathrm{d}\log k}=(-2\epsilon_{1}-\epsilon_{2})_{\star}~.

(37)

Another way to derive power spectrum in SR approximation is by looking at exact de Sitter case. In exact de Sitter, the effective mass is

\frac{z^{\prime\prime}}{z}=\frac{2}{\tau^{2}}.

(38)

Solution of the Mukhanov-Sasaki equation with Bunch-Davies initial vacuum is

v_{k}(\tau)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2k}}\left(1-\frac{i}{k\tau}\right)e^{-ik\tau}.

(39)

When we proceed to derive the curvature perturbation, we run into a trouble because in exact de Sitter we find
\epsilon_{1}=0
. However, as a first-order approximation of the SR parameters, we can simply consider the finite value of
\epsilon_{1}
. Also, the Hubble and SR parameters have a weak time dependence.

Numerical calculation of
\zeta_{k}(\tau)
shows that evaluating the proportionality coefficient
1/z
to
v_{k}(\tau)
at the horizon crossing time

\tau=\tau_{\star}
yields an excellent fit.
So the curvature perturbation (
33
) can be approximated as

\zeta_{k}(\tau)=\left(\frac{iH}{2M_{\mathrm{pl}}\sqrt{k^{3}\epsilon_{1}}}\right)_{\star}(1+ik\tau)e^{-ik\tau}.

(40)

Substituting
\tau\rightarrow 0
leads to the power spectrum (
36
).

The power spectrum (
36
) can be expressed as a function of potential. We can rewrite the Friedmann equation as

V=H^{2}M_{\mathrm{pl}}^{2}(3-\epsilon_{1}).

(41)

Taking derivative with respect to
\phi
yields

V_{,\phi}\equiv\frac{\mathrm{d}V}{\mathrm{d}\phi}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}H^{2}M_{\mathrm{pl}}(6-2\epsilon_{1}+\epsilon_{2}).

(42)

At leading order in the SR approximation, the first SR parameter can be written as

\epsilon_{1}\simeq\frac{M_{\mathrm{pl}}^{2}}{2}\left(\frac{V_{,\phi}}{V}\right)^{2}.

(43)

Therefore,
\epsilon_{1}
has an implicit time dependence because
\phi
depends on time.

From (
36
), we can see that the power spectrum at the wavenumber
p
corresponds to
\epsilon_{1}
evaluated at
\tau=-1/p
. This implies that observation of the power spectrum at the wavenumber
p
constrains
V_{,\phi}(\phi(\tau))
at
\tau=-1/p
. To solve the horizon problem, the fluctuations in the CMB must be around
60
e-folds before the end of inflation. Moreover, fluctuations are observed within the finite range of wavenumber
0.005\mathrm{Mpc}^{-1}<p<0.2\mathrm{Mpc}^{-1}
by CMB. This means that CMB observations constrain only a finite region of the potential around
60
e-folds before the end of inflation. Beyond such a region, there are much looser observational constraints on the potential. Therefore, it is possible to have an inflation model in which the SR approximation is violated after the horizon-crossing time of the CMB scales and before the end of inflation.

Comparison with observations can only be made if and only if we are convinced that quantum fluctuations generated during inflation behave as classical statistical fluctuations to leave observable traces in our universe. The following intuitive argument can be developed for the classicalization of curvature fluctuations.
In superhorizon regime,
k\ll aH
, the mode function behaves as

\zeta_{k}(\tau)=\left(\frac{iH}{2M_{\mathrm{pl}}\sqrt{k^{3}\epsilon_{1}}}\right)_{\star}\left[1+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{k^{2}}{a^{2}H^{2}}\right)\right],

(44)

which means
\zeta_{k}^{\ast}(\tau)=-\zeta_{k}(\tau)
to the leading order. So we find
\hat{\zeta}_{\bm{k}}(\tau)=\zeta_{k}(\tau)(\hat{a}_{\bm{k}}-\hat{a}_{-\bm{k}}^{\dagger})
and its conjugate
momentum
\hat{\pi}_{\bm{k}}^{\zeta}(\tau)=M_{\mathrm{pl}}^{2}z^{2}(\tau){\zeta}_{\bm{k}}^{\prime}(\tau)(\hat{a}_{\bm{k}}-\hat{a}_{-\bm{k}}^{\dagger})
have the same operator dependence, and apparently commute with each other, behaving classically
[
34
]
. This is why long-wave quantum fluctuations behave as if they are classical statistical fluctuations to provide the origin of large-scale structures and CMB anisotropy. The more precise statement, however, is that the quantum commutator

\left[\hat{\zeta}_{\bm{k}}(\tau),\hat{\pi}_{\bm{k}^{\prime}}^{\zeta}(\tau)\right]=i\hbar\delta(\bm{k}-\bm{k}^{\prime})

(45)

always holds, and it is the commutator

\left[\hat{\zeta}_{\bm{p}}(\tau),\hat{\zeta}_{\bm{q}}^{\prime}(\tau)\right]=\frac{i\hbar}{2M_{\mathrm{pl}}^{2}\epsilon_{1}(\tau)a^{2}(\tau)}\delta(\bm{p}-\bm{q})

(46)

that decreases exponentially during the standard slow-roll inflation.

Observation of the spectral tilt
n_{s}=0.9649\pm 0.0042
on the CMB scale implies that the SR parameters
\epsilon_{1},\absolutevalue{\epsilon_2}
at
\tau=-1/p
have a value
\mathcal{O}(0.01)
, based on (
37
). If the first SR parameter decreases rapidly at a later time
\tau\gg-1/p
, the power spectrum is amplified at wavenumber
k\gg p
. This can be achieved by violating the SR approximation at a later time. In the next subsections, we will show some inflation models that violate the SR approximation.

2.2
Ultraslow-roll inflation

In this subsection, we consider a regime where the potential is constant, called ultra-low-roll inflation (USR)
[
25
,
26
,
27
,
28
,
29
]
. Because
\mathrm{d}V/\mathrm{d}\phi=0
, the Klein-Gordon equation (
4
) becomes
\ddot{\phi}=-3H\dot{\phi}
, so
\dot{\phi}\propto a^{-3}
. This makes
\epsilon_{1}
strongly time-dependent and extremely small as

\epsilon_{1}=\frac{\dot{\phi}^{2}}{2M_{\mathrm{pl}}^{2}H^{2}}\propto a^{-6},

(47)

and the second SR parameter becomes

\epsilon_{2}\equiv\frac{\dot{\epsilon}_{1}}{\epsilon_{1}H}=2\epsilon_{1}+2\frac{\ddot{\phi}}{\dot{\phi}H}\simeq-6,

(48)

which breaks SR approximation. The effective mass can be written as

\frac{z^{\prime\prime}}{z}=(aH)^{2}\left[2+\frac{3}{2}\epsilon_{2}+\frac{1}{4}\epsilon_{2}^{2}+\mathcal{O}(\epsilon_{1})\right]=\frac{2}{\tau^{2}},

(49)

which is the same as in the exact de Sitter case. Thus, solution of the Mukhanov-Sasaki equation with Bunch-Davies initial vacuum is the same as (
39
). Then, the curvature perturbation is given by

\zeta_{k}(\tau)=\frac{iH}{2M_{\mathrm{pl}}\sqrt{k^{3}\epsilon_{1}(\tau)}}(1+ik\tau)e^{-ik\tau}.

(50)

Here,
\epsilon_{1}
has a strong dependence on time, which can be expressed as

\epsilon_{1}(\tau)=\epsilon_{1}(\tau_{\star})\left(\frac{a(\tau)}{a(\tau_{\star})}\right)^{-6},

(51)

where
\tau_{\star}
is the horizon-crossing time of a characteristic wavenumber
k_{\star}
. Therefore, the curvature perturbation becomes

\zeta_{k}(\tau)=\frac{iH}{2M_{\mathrm{pl}}\sqrt{k^{3}\epsilon_{1}(\tau_{\star})}}\left(\frac{a(\tau)}{a(\tau_{\star})}\right)^{3}(1+ik\tau)e^{-ik\tau}.

(52)

At the end of inflation, the power spectrum is

\Delta^{2}_{s}(k,\tau_{0})=\frac{H^{2}}{8\pi^{2}M_{\mathrm{pl}}^{2}\epsilon_{1}(\tau_{\star})}\left(\frac{a(\tau_{0})}{a(\tau_{\star})}\right)^{6}.

(53)

To solve the horizon problem, CMB-scale fluctuations must cross the horizon at
N_{\mathrm{CMB}}\approx 60
e-folds before the end of inflation. It means that power spectrum of CMB fluctuations can be written as

\Delta^{2}_{s}(p,\tau_{0})=\frac{H^{2}}{8\pi^{2}M_{\mathrm{pl}}^{2}\epsilon_{1}(\tau_{\star})}e^{6N_{\mathrm{CMB}}}.

(54)

To explain the observed
\Delta^{2}_{s}(p,\tau_{0})=2.1\times 10^{-9}
, for a reasonable value of
\epsilon_{1}(\tau_{\star})
, an extremely small value of
H/M_{\mathrm{pl}}
is required. Because
H/M_{\mathrm{pl}}
characterize the energy scale of inflation, an extremely small value of
H/M_{\mathrm{pl}}
can reach the energy scale of the Big Bang nucleosynthesis. Hence, inflation with only USR period cannot have the required e-folds number to solve the horizon problem.

2.3
Constant-roll inflation

We can generalize the USR condition to
\epsilon_{2}=\mathrm{constant}

[
30
,
31
,
32
]
. By taking reference at time
\tau_{\star}
, the first SR parameter can be written as

\epsilon_{1}(\tau)=\epsilon_{1}(\tau_{\star})\left(\frac{a(\tau)}{a(\tau_{\star})}\right)^{\epsilon_{2}},

(55)

Rewriting the second SR parameter as
\epsilon_{2}=-2(3+\alpha)
, the effective mass can be expressed as

\frac{z^{\prime\prime}}{z}=\frac{1}{\tau^{2}}(2+\alpha)(1+\alpha)=\frac{1}{\tau^{2}}\left(\nu^{2}-\frac{1}{4}\right),

(56)

where
\nu
is defined as

\nu=\absolutevalue{ \alpha+ \frac{3}{2} }.

(57)

Solution of the Mukhanov-Sasaki equation with Bunch-Davies initial condition is the same as (
27
). The power spectrum is given by

\Delta^{2}_{s}(k,\tau_{0})=\frac{H^{2}}{8\pi^{2}M_{\mathrm{pl}}^{2}\epsilon_{1}(\tau_{0})}\frac{1}{2\pi}2^{2\nu}[\Gamma(\nu)]^{2}\left(\frac{k}{aH}\right)^{3-2\nu}.

(58)

We can read that the spectral tilt is
n_{s}-1=3-2\nu
. Therefore, there are two possible solutions for
\alpha
, given by

\alpha_{\pm}=-\frac{3}{2}\pm\frac{1}{2}(4-n_{s}).

(59)

However, we have to choose a solution that does not grow outside the horizon. Recall the second-order action of
\zeta
that reads

S^{(2)}[\zeta]=M_{\mathrm{pl}}^{2}\int\mathrm{d}\tau~\mathrm{d}^{3}x~a^{2}\epsilon_{1}\left[(\zeta^{\prime})^{2}-(\partial_{i}\zeta)^{2}\right].

(60)

The equation of motion is given by

\zeta_{\bm{k}}^{\prime\prime}+\frac{(a^{2}\epsilon_{1})^{\prime}}{a^{2}\epsilon_{1}}\zeta_{\bm{k}}^{\prime}+k^{2}\zeta_{\bm{k}}=0

(61)

At superhorizon scale, we can neglect the last term in the equation of motion. Hence, evolution of the curvature perturbation outside the horizon is

\zeta_{k}(\tau_{0})\rightarrow A_{k}+B_{k}\int^{\tau_{0}}_{\tau_{\star}}\frac{\mathrm{d}\tau}{a^{2}(\tau)\epsilon_{1}(\tau)},

(62)

where
A_{k}
and
B_{k}
are time-independent coefficients. The second term determines whether
\zeta_{k}(\tau_{0})
grows or decays. Substituting (
55
), the integral becomes

\int^{\tau_{0}}_{\tau_{\star}}\frac{\mathrm{d}\tau}{a^{2}(\tau)\epsilon_{1}(\tau)}\sim\tau_{0}^{\epsilon_{2}+3}.

(63)

It shows that curvature perturbation grows outside the horizon if
\epsilon_{2}<-3
or equivalently
\alpha>-3/2
. It was pointed out in
[
24
]
that the curvature perturbation grows outside the horizon if
3-\epsilon_{1}+\epsilon_{2}<0
is satisfied. Clearly, USR condition satisfies this condition since
\epsilon_{2}=-6
.

From observation of spectral tilt
n_{s}=0.9649\pm 0.0042
on CMB scale, we obtain
\alpha_{+}\approx 0
and
\alpha_{-}\approx-3
. Solution
\alpha_{+}\approx 0
corresponds to
\epsilon_{2}\approx-6
, which approximately equal to USR inflation. As explained in the previous subsection, although it yields an almost scale-invariant power spectrum, this solution cannot explain the required number of e-folds of inflation because the curvature perturbation grows outside the horizon. Therefore, we have to choose
\alpha_{-}
as the solution because it corresponds to decaying curvature perturbation. With this solution, constant-roll inflation can explain the observed CMB fluctuations.

2.4
Ultraslow-roll to slow-roll transition

In this subsection, we consider an inflation model in which the second parameter SR evolves from the USR to the SR period at conformal time
\tau_{s}
. For
\tau<\tau_{s}
, the inflaton is in the USR period. At time
\tau\geq\tau_{s}
, the inflaton undergoes a transition from the USR period to the SR period. In the next part, we consider two different evolutions of the second SR parameter: smooth and sharp transitions. The former means that the second SR parameter evolves as a continuous function of time, while in the latter it evolves as a step function of time. We follow the parameterization of
\epsilon_{2}(\tau)
in
[
35
]
, which is also used in many literatures. The sketches of
\epsilon_{2}(\tau)
for both cases are shown by the gray and blue curves in Fig.
2
.

Sketch of
\epsilon_{2}(\tau)
for sharp and smooth transition from USR to SR period are shown by the gray and blue curve, respectively. Brown curve shows sharp transition from SR-USR-SR period, which will be discussed in Sec.

.

2.4.1
Smooth transition

At time
\tau>\tau_{s}
, consider evolution of the first and second SR parameter as

\displaystyle\epsilon_{1}(\tau)=\epsilon_{1}(\tau_{s})\frac{3\left[\left(\dfrac{\tau_{s}}{\tau}\right)^{s}(s-3)+s+3\right]^{2}}{2s^{2}(s+3)}\left(\frac{\tau}{\tau_{s}}\right)^{s+3},

(64)

\displaystyle\epsilon_{2}(\tau)=s-3+\frac{2s(s+3)}{\left(\dfrac{\tau_{s}}{\tau}\right)^{s}(s-3)+s+3},

(65)

where
s=\sqrt{9-12\eta_{V}}\simeq 3-2\eta_{V}
and
\eta_{V}\rightarrow 0
. The parameter
\eta_{V}
corresponds to the second derivative of the potential at the transition point. In this model, the second SR parameter evolves from USR period
\epsilon_{2}(\tau_{s})=-6
to SR period
\epsilon_{2}(\tau_{0})=-2\eta_{V}
. With this parametrization, the effective mass becomes

\frac{z^{\prime\prime}}{z}=\frac{1}{\tau^{2}}\left(2-3\eta_{V}\right)=\frac{1}{\tau^{2}}\left(\nu^{2}-\frac{1}{4}\right),

(66)

where

\nu^{2}=\frac{9}{4}-3\eta_{V}.

(67)

Because
\eta_{V}\rightarrow 0
, solution of the Mukhanov-Sasaki equation with Bunch-Davies initial vacuum is the same as (
39
). Then, the curvature perturbation is given by

\zeta_{k}(\tau)=\frac{iH}{2M_{\mathrm{pl}}\sqrt{k^{3}\epsilon_{1}(\tau)}}(1+ik\tau)e^{-ik\tau},

(68)

and power spectrum at the end of inflation becomes

\Delta^{2}_{s}(k,\tau_{0})=\frac{H^{2}}{8\pi^{2}M_{\mathrm{pl}}^{2}\epsilon_{1}(\tau_{0})}.

(69)

2.4.2
Sharp transition

Consider evolution of the second SR parameter as a step function of time

\epsilon_{2}(\tau)=-\Delta\epsilon_{2}\left[1-\theta(\tau-\tau_{s})\right],

(70)

where
\Delta\epsilon_{2}=6
. At time
\tau<\tau_{s}
, the inflaton is in the USR period with the Bunch-Davies initial vacuum. In this period, the curvature perturbation and its time derivative are given by

\displaystyle\zeta_{k}(\tau)

\displaystyle=\frac{iH}{2M_{\mathrm{pl}}\sqrt{k^{3}\epsilon_{1}(\tau)}}e^{-ik\tau}(1+ik\tau)

\displaystyle=\frac{iH}{2M_{\mathrm{pl}}\sqrt{k^{3}\epsilon_{1}(\tau_{s})}}\left(\frac{\tau_{s}}{\tau}\right)^{3}e^{-ik\tau}(1+ik\tau),

(71)

\zeta_{k}^{\prime}(\tau)=\frac{iH}{2M_{\mathrm{pl}}\sqrt{k^{3}\epsilon_{1}(\tau_{s})}}\left(\frac{\tau_{s}}{\tau}\right)^{3}e^{-ik\tau}\left(k^{2}\tau-\frac{3}{\tau}-3ik\right).

(72)

At time
\tau\geq\tau_{s}
, the inflaton is in the SR period. In this period, the curvature perturbation and its time derivative are given by

\zeta_{k}(\tau)=\frac{iH}{2M_{\mathrm{pl}}\sqrt{k^{3}\epsilon_{1}(\tau_{s})}}\left[\mathcal{A}_{k}e^{-ik\tau}(1+ik\tau)-\mathcal{B}_{k}e^{ik\tau}(1-ik\tau)\right],

(73)

\zeta_{k}^{\prime}(\tau)=\frac{iH}{2M_{\mathrm{pl}}\sqrt{k^{3}\epsilon_{1}(\tau_{s})}}k^{2}\tau\left(\mathcal{A}_{k}e^{-ik\tau}-\mathcal{B}_{k}e^{ik\tau}\right),

(74)

where
\mathcal{A}_{k}
and
\mathcal{B}_{k}
are general coefficients to be determined from boundary conditions
[
36
,
37
,
38
,
39
,
40
,
41
,
42
,
43
]
. This time, we have to consider both positive and negative frequency solutions because the boundary condition at
\tau=\tau_{s}
does not correspond to Bunch-Davies vacuum. Solutions of the coefficients by requiring continuity of
\zeta_{k}(\tau)
and
\zeta_{k}^{\prime}(\tau)
at transition
\tau=\tau_{s}
are

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{k}=1+\frac{3(1+k^{2}\tau_{s}^{2})}{2ik^{3}\tau_{s}^{3}},

(75)

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{k}=\frac{3(1+ik\tau_{s})^{2}}{2ik^{3}\tau_{s}^{3}}e^{-2ik\tau_{s}}.

(76)

At the end of inflation, the power spectrum becomes

\Delta^{2}_{s}(k,\tau_{0})=\frac{k^{3}}{2\pi^{2}}\absolutevalue{\zeta_k(\tau_0)}^{2}=\frac{H^{2}}{8\pi^{2}M_{\mathrm{pl}}^{2}\epsilon_{1}(\tau_{s})}\absolutevalue{\mathcal{A}_k - \mathcal{B}_k}^{2}.

(77)

For a very long-wavelength modes,
\absolutevalue{k \tau_s}\ll 1
, the power spectrum approaches

\Delta^{2}_{s}\left(k\ll\frac{1}{\absolutevalue{\tau_s}},\tau_{0}\right)=4\times\frac{H^{2}}{8\pi^{2}M_{\mathrm{pl}}^{2}\epsilon_{1}(\tau_{s})}.

(78)

3
Three-point functions

So far, we have considered the second-order action of the curvature perturbation that leads to two-point functions or the power spectrum at the tree level in the language of quantum field theory. Within the cosmological perturbation theory framework, higher-order correlation functions resulting from higher-order expansion of the action are expected to be small. However, because we are interested in PBH formation, where the curvature perturbation is large on certain small scales, higher-order correlation functions can be quite large.

In this section, we discuss the third-order action that yields three-point functions of the curvature perturbation.
Expanding (
1
) to third-order of
\zeta
yields the interaction action
[
44
]

S^{(3)}[\zeta]=S_{\mathrm{bulk}}[\zeta]+S_{\mathrm{B}}[\zeta]+M_{\mathrm{pl}}^{2}\int\mathrm{d}t~\mathrm{d}^{3}x~2f(\zeta)\left(\frac{\delta L}{\delta\zeta}\right)_{1}.

(79)

The bulk interaction
S_{\mathrm{bulk}}[\zeta]
reads

\displaystyle S_{\mathrm{bulk}}[\zeta]=M_{\mathrm{pl}}^{2}\int\mathrm{d}t~\mathrm{d}^{3}x~a^{3}

\displaystyle\left[\epsilon_{1}^{2}\dot{\zeta}^{2}\zeta+\frac{1}{a^{2}}\epsilon_{1}^{2}(\partial_{i}\zeta)^{2}\zeta-2\epsilon_{1}\dot{\zeta}\partial_{i}\zeta\partial_{i}\chi\right.

\displaystyle\left.-\frac{1}{2}\epsilon_{1}^{3}\dot{\zeta}^{2}\zeta+\frac{1}{2}\epsilon_{1}\zeta(\partial_{i}\partial_{j}\chi)^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\epsilon_{1}\dot{\epsilon}_{2}\dot{\zeta}\zeta^{2}\right],

(80)

where
\chi=\epsilon_{1}\partial^{-2}\dot{\zeta}
.

S_{\mathrm{B}}[\zeta]
is the surface action given by the total derivatives
[
45
,
46
]
. The last term is interaction proportional to the equation of motion in the lowest order,

\left(\frac{\delta L}{\delta\zeta}\right)_{1}=\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}(\epsilon_{1}a^{3}\dot{\zeta})-\epsilon_{1}a\partial^{2}\zeta.

(81)

The function
f(\zeta)
is explicitly given by

f(\zeta)=\frac{\epsilon_{2}}{4}\zeta^{2}+\frac{\dot{\zeta}}{H}\zeta+\frac{1}{4a^{2}H^{2}}[-(\partial_{i}\zeta)^{2}+\partial^{-2}\partial_{i}\partial_{j}(\partial_{i}\zeta\partial_{j}\zeta)]+\frac{1}{2H}[\partial_{i}\zeta\partial_{i}\chi-\partial^{-2}\partial_{i}\partial_{j}(\partial_{i}\zeta\partial_{j}\chi)].

(82)

Performing field redefinition
\zeta=\bm{\zeta}+f(\bm{\zeta})
generates third-order terms from the second-order action (
9
) that cancel relevant surface terms containing
\dot{\zeta}
.
As a result, we find a useful relation

[
44
,
45
,
46
]

S^{(2)}[\zeta]+S^{(3)}[\zeta]=S^{(2)}[\bm{\zeta}]+S_{\mathrm{bulk}}[\bm{\zeta}].

(83)

In terms of
\bm{\zeta}
, the total action is simply given by the second-order action (
9
) and bulk interaction (
80
).

Three-point functions of
\zeta
can be written schematically as

\langle\zeta_{\bm{k}_{1}}(\tau)\zeta_{\bm{k}_{2}}(\tau)\zeta_{\bm{k}_{3}}(\tau)\rangle=\langle\bm{\zeta}_{\bm{k}_{1}}(\tau)\bm{\zeta}_{\bm{k}_{2}}(\tau)\bm{\zeta}_{\bm{k}_{3}}(\tau)\rangle+\mathrm{redefinition~terms},

(84)

where the first term is generated by bulk interaction (
80
) and the second term is boundary contribution at
\tau
from field redefinition.
Higher-order correction to the expectation value of an operator
\mathcal{O}(\tau)
is calculated by the in-in perturbation theory

\langle\mathcal{O(\tau)}\rangle=\left\langle\left[\bar{\mathrm{T}}\exp\left(i\int_{-\infty}^{\tau}\mathrm{d}\tau^{\prime}H_{\mathrm{int}}(\tau^{\prime})\right)\right]\mathcal{\hat{O}}(\tau)\left[\mathrm{T}\exp\left(-i\int_{-\infty}^{\tau}\mathrm{d\tau^{\prime}}H_{\mathrm{int}}(\tau^{\prime})\right)\right]\right\rangle,

(85)

where
\mathrm{T}
and
\bar{\mathrm{T}}
denote time and antitime ordering. In this case, the interaction Hamiltonian is simply
H_{\mathrm{int}}=-\int\mathrm{d}^{3}x\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{bulk}}
, where
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{bulk}}
is the integrand of
S_{\mathrm{bulk}}
. For three-point functions, the operator is
\bm{\zeta}_{\bm{k}_{1}}(\tau)\bm{\zeta}_{\bm{k}_{2}}(\tau)\bm{\zeta}_{\bm{k}_{3}}(\tau)
. First-order expansion of (
85
) reads

\langle\mathcal{O(\tau)}\rangle=2\int_{-\infty}^{\tau}\mathrm{d}\tau_{1}~\mathrm{Im}\langle\mathcal{\hat{O}}(\tau)H_{\mathrm{int}}(\tau_{1})\rangle.

(86)

Bispectrum
\langle\!\langle\zeta_{\bm{k}_{1}}(\tau)\zeta_{\bm{k}_{2}}(\tau)\zeta_{\bm{k}_{3}}(\tau)\rangle\!\rangle
is defined as

\langle\zeta_{\bm{k}_{1}}(\tau)\zeta_{\bm{k}_{2}}(\tau)\zeta_{\bm{k}_{3}}(\tau)\rangle=(2\pi)^{3}\delta(\bm{k}_{1}+\bm{k}_{2}+\bm{k}_{3})\langle\!\langle\zeta_{\bm{k}_{1}}(\tau)\zeta_{\bm{k}_{2}}(\tau)\zeta_{\bm{k}_{3}}(\tau)\rangle\!\rangle.

(87)

A bispectrum has local type if it can be expressed as

\displaystyle\langle\!\langle\zeta_{\bm{k}_{1}}(\tau_{0})\zeta_{\bm{k}_{2}}(\tau_{0})\zeta_{\bm{k}_{3}}(\tau_{0})\rangle\!\rangle=\frac{6}{5}f_{\mathrm{NL}}

\displaystyle\left[\absolutevalue{\zeta_{k_1}(\tau_0)}^{2}\absolutevalue{\zeta_{k_2}(\tau_0)}^{2}+\absolutevalue{\zeta_{k_2}(\tau_0)}^{2}\absolutevalue{\zeta_{k_3}(\tau_0)}^{2}\right.

\displaystyle\left.+\absolutevalue{\zeta_{k_1}(\tau_0)}^{2}\absolutevalue{\zeta_{k_3}(\tau_0)}^{2}\right],

(88)

where
f_{\mathrm{NL}}
is a coefficient called nonlinear parameter.

3.1
Slow-roll approximation

In the SR approximation, the first line in cubic self-interaction (
80
) has
\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{2})
coefficients, while the second line has
\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{3})
coefficients. At the end of inflation, when the relevant modes are in the superhorizon limit, the leading term in the field redefinition (
82
) is the first term, because the other terms decay outside the horizon. Therefore, the third-order action becomes

S_{\mathrm{bulk}}[\bm{\zeta}]=M_{\mathrm{pl}}^{2}\int\mathrm{d}t~\mathrm{d}^{3}x~a^{3}\left[\epsilon_{1}^{2}\dot{\bm{\zeta}}^{2}\bm{\zeta}+\frac{1}{a^{2}}\epsilon_{1}^{2}(\partial_{i}\bm{\zeta})^{2}\zeta-2\epsilon_{1}^{2}\dot{\bm{\zeta}}\partial_{i}\bm{\zeta}\partial_{i}\partial^{-2}\dot{\bm{\zeta}}+\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{3})\right],

(89)

with
\zeta=\bm{\zeta}+\epsilon_{2}\bm{\zeta}^{2}/4
. With the in-in perturbation theory (
86
), we obtain bispectrum of
\bm{\zeta}
as

\displaystyle\langle\!\langle\bm{\zeta}_{\bm{k}_{1}}(\tau_{0})\bm{\zeta}_{\bm{k}_{2}}(\tau_{0})\bm{\zeta}_{\bm{k}_{3}}(\tau_{0})\rangle\!\rangle_{\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{2})}=

\displaystyle~\frac{H^{4}}{32M_{\mathrm{pl}}^{4}\epsilon_{1}^{2}(k_{1}k_{2}k_{3})^{3}}

(90)

\displaystyle\times\left[-\epsilon_{1\star}\sum_{i}k_{i}^{3}+\epsilon_{1\star}\sum_{i\neq j}k_{i}k_{j}^{2}+8\epsilon_{1\star}\frac{\sum_{i>j}k_{i}^{2}k_{j}^{2}}{\sum_{i}k_{i}}\right].

To arrive at bispectrum of
\zeta
, we have to include contribution from the field redefinition

\displaystyle\langle\!\langle\zeta_{\bm{k}_{1}}(\tau_{0})\zeta_{\bm{k}_{2}}(\tau_{0})\zeta_{\bm{k}_{3}}(\tau_{0})\rangle\!\rangle=\langle\!\langle\bm{\zeta}_{\bm{k}_{1}}(\tau_{0})\bm{\zeta}_{\bm{k}_{2}}(\tau_{0})\bm{\zeta}_{\bm{k}_{3}}(\tau_{0})\rangle\!\rangle

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{4}\epsilon_{2}(\tau_{0})\times 2\left[\absolutevalue{\zeta_{k_1}(\tau_0)}^{2}\absolutevalue{\zeta_{k_2}(\tau_0)}^{2}+\absolutevalue{\zeta_{k_2}(\tau_0)}^{2}\absolutevalue{\zeta_{k_3}(\tau_0)}^{2}+\absolutevalue{\zeta_{k_1}(\tau_0)}^{2}\absolutevalue{\zeta_{k_3}(\tau_0)}^{2}\right].

(91)

However, assuming
\epsilon_{3}
as a constant,
\epsilon_{2}(\tau_{0})
diverges as
\tau_{0}\rightarrow 0
. The explicit time dependence is given by

\epsilon_{2}(\tau_{0})=\epsilon_{2\star}\left(\frac{\tau_{0}}{\tau_{\star}}\right)^{-\epsilon_{3\star}}\simeq\epsilon_{2\star}\left(1-\epsilon_{3\star}\log\frac{\tau_{0}}{\tau_{\star}}\right).

(92)

At leading order of
\epsilon_{2}(\tau_{0})
, the bispectrum of
\zeta
is

\displaystyle\langle\!\langle\zeta_{\bm{k}_{1}}(\tau_{0})\zeta_{\bm{k}_{2}}(\tau_{0})\zeta_{\bm{k}_{3}}(\tau_{0})\rangle\!\rangle_{\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{2})}=

\displaystyle~\frac{H^{4}}{32M_{\mathrm{pl}}^{4}\epsilon_{1}^{2}(k_{1}k_{2}k_{3})^{3}}

(93)

\displaystyle\times\left[(\epsilon_{2}-\epsilon_{1})_{\star}\sum_{i}k_{i}^{3}+\epsilon_{1\star}\sum_{i\neq j}k_{i}k_{j}^{2}+8\epsilon_{1\star}\frac{\sum_{i>j}k_{i}^{2}k_{j}^{2}}{\sum_{i}k_{i}}\right].

In the squeezed limit, where one of the wavenumbers,
k_{1}
, is much smaller than the others,
k_{1}\rightarrow 0
, the bispectrum becomes

\langle\!\langle\zeta_{\bm{k}_{1}}(\tau_{0})\zeta_{\bm{k}_{2}}(\tau_{0})\zeta_{-\bm{k}_{2}}(\tau_{0})\rangle\!\rangle=(2\epsilon_{1}+4\epsilon_{2})_{\star}\frac{H^{4}}{32M_{\mathrm{pl}}^{4}\epsilon_{1}^{2}(k_{1}k_{2})^{3}}.

(94)

In terms of the mode function and spectral tilt, the squeezed bispectrum can be written as

\langle\!\langle\zeta_{\bm{k}_{1}}(\tau_{0})\zeta_{\bm{k}_{2}}(\tau_{0})\zeta_{-\bm{k}_{2}}(\tau_{0})\rangle\!\rangle=-(n_{s}(k_{2},\tau_{0})-1)\absolutevalue{\zeta_{k_1}(\tau_0)}^{2}\absolutevalue{\zeta_{k_2}(\tau_0)}^{2},

(95)

which is called Maldacena’s theorem. Here, we have shown Maldacena’s theorem by explicit derivation. It can also be proven by a model-independent method by considering the evolution of the small-scale perturbation in a rescaled dS background due to the long-wavelength perturbation
[
47
]
.

Another important point discussed in
[
44
]
is the bispectrum induced by the last term in (
80
)

S_{\mathrm{bulk}}[\bm{\zeta}]\supset\frac{1}{2}M_{\mathrm{pl}}^{2}\int\mathrm{d}t~\mathrm{d}^{3}x~a^{3}\epsilon_{1}\dot{\epsilon}_{2}\dot{\bm{\zeta}}\bm{\zeta}^{2}.

(96)

Although it is SR-suppressed compared to (
89
), such term induces bispectrum that evolves outside the horizon.

\langle\!\langle\bm{\zeta}_{\bm{k}_{1}}(\tau_{0})\bm{\zeta}_{\bm{k}_{2}}(\tau_{0})\bm{\zeta}_{\bm{k}_{3}}(\tau_{0})\rangle\!\rangle_{\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{3})}=\left(\epsilon_{2\star}\epsilon_{3\star}\log\frac{\tau_{0}}{\tau_{\star}}\right)\frac{H^{4}}{32M_{\mathrm{pl}}^{4}\epsilon_{1}^{2}(k_{1}k_{2}k_{3})^{3}}(k_{1}^{3}+k_{2}^{3}+k_{3}^{3}),

(97)

where
\star
denotes horizon crossing of the total wavenumber
k_{1}+k_{2}+k_{3}
. It shows that bispectrum of
\bm{\zeta}
evolves outside the horizon due to
\log\tau_{0}
dependence. However, for bispectrum of
\zeta
, there is an additional contribution from the field redefinition (
91
). After summing with the second term in (
92
), the
\log\tau_{0}
dependence cancels out. Therefore, bispectrum of
\zeta
does not evolve outside the horizon.

3.2
Ultraslow-roll inflation

During USR period,
\epsilon_{1}
becomes extremely small because it scales as

. Also,
\dot{\epsilon}_{2}=0
because

Factor

Ultraslow-roll to slow-roll transition

Sharp transition

\displaystyle\times\mathrm{Im}\left[\zeta_{k_{1}}(\tau_{0})\zeta_{k_{2}}(\tau_{0})\zeta_{k_{3}}(\tau_{0})\zeta_{k_{1}}^{*}(\tau)\zeta_{k_{2}}^{*}(\tau)\zeta_{k_{3}}^{\prime*}(\tau)+\mathrm{perm}\right].

\displaystyle\langle\!\langle\zeta_{\bm{k}_{1}}(\tau_{0})\zeta_{\bm{k}_{2}}(\tau_{0})\zeta_{-\bm{k}_{2}}(\tau_{0})\rangle\!\rangle

\displaystyle=\absolutevalue{\zeta_{k_1}(\tau_0)}^{2}\absolutevalue{\zeta_{k_2}(\tau_0)}^{2}\int_{\tau_{s}}^{\tau_{0}}\mathrm{d}\tau\sqrt{\frac{\epsilon_{1}(\tau_{0})}{\epsilon_{1}(\tau)}}\epsilon_{2}^{\prime}(\tau)\left(1+\frac{1}{2}\epsilon_{2}(\tau)\right)

\displaystyle=-2\eta_{V}\sqrt{\frac{\epsilon_{1}(\tau_{0})}{\epsilon_{1}(\tau_{s})}}\absolutevalue{\zeta_{k_1}(\tau_0)}^{2}\absolutevalue{\zeta_{k_2}(\tau_0)}^{2}.

(109)

Thus, we can read off the nonlinear parameter

\frac{12}{5}f_{\mathrm{NL}}=-2\eta_{V}\sqrt{\frac{\epsilon_{1}(\tau_{0})}{\epsilon_{1}(\tau_{s})}},

(110)

which is suppressed by SR parameter

[

[
55
]
, which makes the integrand of (

[

following
[

.

4
Generation of large fluctuation

In Sec.
2.2
, we have shown that violation of the SR approximation, especially the USR condition, leads to the growth of curvature perturbation outside the horizon
[
23
,
24
]
. Although the USR period cannot realize the required e-folds number of inflation, it can be useful for other purposes. If the inflaton undergoes USR period only for a short duration, the curvature perturbation will be enhanced only within that duration. At the end of inflation, it corresponds to amplification of the curvature perturbation at a range of wavenumbers that cross the horizon during the USR period.

Assuming sharp transitions, evolution of the second SR parameter corresponding to the potential in Fig.
1
is shown by the brown curve in Fig.
2
. The inflaton undergoes USR period in the flat region of potential from time
t_{s}
to
t_{e}
. In order to explain the observed CMB fluctuations, at early time,
t\lesssim t_{s}
, the inflation was in SR period with slightly tilted potential. At late time,
t\gtrsim t_{e}
, there is no observational constraint on the potential. A potential is viable as long as the curvature perturbation does not grow during the final period.

In this section, we study the power spectrum and bispectrum of curvature perturbation in such a setup.

4.1
Power spectrum

At early time,
t\lesssim t_{s}
, the inflaton was in SR period with Bunch-Davies initial vacuum, so the curvature perturbation is given by

\zeta_{k}(\tau)=\left(\frac{iH}{2M_{\mathrm{pl}}\sqrt{k^{3}\epsilon_{1s}}}\right)_{\star}e^{-ik\tau}(1+ik\tau),

(111)

where
\epsilon_{1s}
is the first SR parameter during this SR period.

At
t_{s}\lesssim t\lesssim t_{e}
, the inflaton is in the intermediate USR period. We define
\tau_{s}
and
\tau_{e}
as the conformal time corresponding to
t_{s}
and
t_{e}
, respectively. The first SR parameter can be written as
\epsilon_{1}(\tau)=\epsilon_{1s}(\tau/\tau_{s})^{6}
based on proportionality in (
47
). Therefore, the curvature perturbation becomes

\zeta_{k}(\tau)=\left(\frac{iH}{2M_{\mathrm{pl}}\sqrt{k^{3}\epsilon_{1s}}}\right)_{\star}\left(\frac{\tau_{s}}{\tau}\right)^{3}\left[\mathcal{A}_{k}e^{-ik\tau}(1+ik\tau)-\mathcal{B}_{k}e^{ik\tau}(1-ik\tau)\right],

(112)

where coefficients
\mathcal{A}_{k}
and
\mathcal{B}_{k}
are determined by matching to the SR solution (
111
) at the boundary. Solutions of the coefficients by requiring continuity of
\zeta_{k}(\tau)
and
\zeta_{k}^{\prime}(\tau)
at transition
\tau=\tau_{s}
are

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{k}=1-\frac{3(1+k^{2}\tau_{s}^{2})}{2ik^{3}\tau_{s}^{3}},

(113)

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{k}=-\frac{3(1+ik\tau_{s})^{2}}{2ik^{3}\tau_{s}^{3}}e^{-2ik\tau_{s}}.

(114)

At late time,
t\gtrsim t_{e}
, the inflaton goes back to SR dynamics. The curvature perturbation can be written as

\zeta_{k}(\tau)=\left(\frac{iH}{2M_{\mathrm{pl}}\sqrt{k^{3}\epsilon_{1s}}}\right)_{\star}\left(\frac{\tau_{s}}{\tau_{e}}\right)^{3}\left[\mathcal{C}_{k}e^{-ik\tau}(1+ik\tau)-\mathcal{D}_{k}e^{ik\tau}(1-ik\tau)\right],

(115)

where coefficients
\mathcal{C}_{k}
and
\mathcal{D}_{k}
are determined by matching to the USR solution (
112
) at the boundary. Solutions of the coefficients by requiring continuity of
\zeta_{k}(\tau)
and
\zeta_{k}^{\prime}(\tau)
at transition
\tau=\tau_{e}
are

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{k}=\frac{-1}{4k^{6}\tau_{s}^{3}\tau_{e}^{3}}

\displaystyle\left\{9(k\tau_{s}-i)^{2}(k\tau_{e}+i)^{2}e^{2ik(\tau_{e}-\tau_{s})}\right.

\displaystyle\left.-\left[k^{2}\tau_{s}^{2}(2k\tau_{s}+3i)+3i\right]\left[k^{2}\tau_{e}^{2}(2k\tau_{e}-3i)-3i\right]\right\},

(116)

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{k}=\frac{3}{4k^{6}\tau_{s}^{3}\tau_{e}^{3}}

\displaystyle\left\{e^{-2ik\tau_{s}}[3+k^{2}\tau_{e}^{2}(3-2ik\tau_{e})](k\tau_{s}-i)^{2}\right.

\displaystyle\left.+ie^{-2ik\tau_{e}}\left[3i+k^{2}\tau_{s}^{2}(2k\tau_{s}+3i)\right](k\tau_{e}-i)^{2}\right\}.

(117)

As before,
k_{s}
and
k_{e}
are wavenumbers which cross the horizon at
\tau_{s}
and
\tau_{e}
, respectively. At the end of inflation,
\tau_{0}~(\rightarrow 0)
, the tree-level power spectrum is

\Delta^{2}_{s(0)}(k,\tau_{0})=\frac{k^{3}}{2\pi^{2}}\absolutevalue{\zeta_k(\tau_0)}^{2}=\left(\frac{H^{2}}{8\pi^{2}M_{\mathrm{pl}}^{2}\epsilon_{1s}}\right)_{\star}\left(\frac{k_{e}}{k_{s}}\right)^{6}\absolutevalue{\mathcal{C}_k - \mathcal{D}_k}^{2}.

(118)

On large scale, the power spectrum approaches an almost scale-invariant limit

\Delta_{s(\mathrm{SR})}^{2}(k)\equiv\Delta^{2}_{s(0)}(k\ll k_{s},\tau_{0})=\left(\frac{H^{2}}{8\pi^{2}M_{\mathrm{pl}}^{2}\epsilon_{1s}}\right)_{\star}\left[1+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{k^{2}}{k_{s}^{2}}\right)\right],

(119)

which is consistent with CMB observation. Assuming
k_{e}\gg k_{s}
, the next order approximation is

\Delta^{2}_{s(0)}(k,\tau_{0})\simeq\left(\frac{H^{2}}{8\pi^{2}M_{\mathrm{pl}}^{2}\epsilon_{1s}}\right)_{\star}\left[1-\frac{8}{5}\left(\frac{k_{e}}{k_{s}}\right)^{3}\left(\frac{k}{k_{s}}\right)^{2}+\frac{24}{35}\left(\frac{k_{e}}{k_{s}}\right)^{3}\left(\frac{k}{k_{s}}\right)^{4}+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{k^{6}}{k_{s}^{6}}\right)\right].

(120)

The minus sign in the coefficient of
(k/k_{s})^{2}
is important because it can explain a dip in the power spectrum before amplification start. As
k
approaches
k_{s}
, the
k^{2}
-term grows until it reaches unity. At this point, the
k^{4}
-term is not large enough yet, so the power spectrum almost vanishes. This yields a dip in the power spectrum, which happens at the wavenumber

k_{\mathrm{dip}}\simeq k_{s}\left[\frac{5}{8}\left(\frac{k_{s}}{k_{e}}\right)^{3}\right]^{1/2}.

(121)

It has been argued that
this dip may lead to an interesting observational consequence
[
57
]
.

At
k_{\mathrm{dip}}<k<k_{s}
, the
k^{4}
-term grows while the
k^{6}
-term is still small, which explains the apparent
k^{4}
-growth in the power spectrum
[
38
]
. On a small scale with a larger wavenumber,
k_{s}\lesssim k\lesssim k_{e}
, the power spectrum is oscillating around

4\times\Delta_{s(\mathrm{PBH})}^{2}\equiv 4\times\Delta_{s(\mathrm{SR})}^{2}(k_{s})\left(\frac{k_{e}}{k_{s}}\right)^{6},

(122)

whose high-density peak may collapse into PBHs. Numerical coefficient 4 appears for the same reason as (
78
). It is amplified by a factor of
(k_{e}/k_{s})^{6}
compared to the CMB-scale power spectrum. To generate a significant abundance of PBHs, typically peak with
\mathcal{O}(0.01)
on small-scale power spectrum is required. It means that the power spectrum is amplified
\mathcal{O}(10^{7})
compared to CMB-scale. In terms of the number of e-folds during the USR period
N_{\mathrm{USR}}=\log[a(\tau_{e})/a(\tau_{s})]
, we need to have

N_{\mathrm{USR}}=\log\frac{\tau_{s}}{\tau_{e}}=\log\frac{k_{e}}{k_{s}}\simeq\frac{1}{6}\log 10^{7}=2.68,

(123)

to achieve a significant abundance of PBHs.

In this model, we can see that the power spectrum remains amplified even for
k>k_{e}
, which oscillates around
\Delta_{s(\mathrm{PBH})}^{2}
. This happens because the inflaton goes directly to the SR period after the USR period. In case the inflaton undergoes a constant-roll period with
\epsilon_{2}>0
after the USR period, the power spectrum at the end of inflation will decrease for
k>k_{e}
.

In this review, we choose an USR period for the intermediate phase as an example. In principle, the intermediate phase can be generalized to a constant-roll period as long as the curvature perturbation grows outside the horizon. The requirement was discussed below Eq. (
63
). However, for a general constant-roll period, the mode function becomes the Hankel function, which is given by (
24
) with (
57
). Technically, it becomes more difficult to match the coefficient of constant-roll period with the SR period because it involves derivative of Hankel function. In
[

]
, a method to approximate the mode function is proposed by utilizing the fact that the duration of the SR violating regime is extremely short compared to the total duration of inflation. An inflation model with two phases of SR violating period is also introduced in
[
40

that yields
k^{8}
growth in the power spectrum, which is much steeper than
k^{4}
growth.

4.2
Bispectrum

Consider the evolution of the second SR parameter shown by the brown curve in Fig.
2
. Its time derivative can be modelled as Dirac-delta function

\epsilon_{2}^{\prime}(\tau)=\Delta\epsilon_{2}[-\delta(\tau-\tau_{s})+\delta(\tau-\tau_{e})].

(124)

Substituting it to the time integral in (
103
) leads to

\displaystyle\langle\!\langle\zeta_{\bm{k}_{1}}(\tau_{0})\zeta_{\bm{k}_{2}}(\tau_{0})\zeta_{\bm{k}_{3}}(\tau_{0})\rangle\!\rangle=2M_{\mathrm{pl}}^{2}\epsilon_{1}(\tau_{s})a^{2}(\tau_{s})\Delta\epsilon_{2}\mathrm{Im}\left[\zeta_{k_{1}}(\tau_{0})\zeta_{k_{2}}(\tau_{0})\zeta_{k_{3}}(\tau_{0})\zeta_{k_{1}}^{*}(\tau_{s})\zeta_{k_{2}}^{*}(\tau_{s})\zeta_{k_{3}}^{\prime*}(\tau_{s})\right]

\displaystyle-2M_{\mathrm{pl}}^{2}\epsilon_{1}(\tau_{e})a^{2}(\tau_{e})\Delta\epsilon_{2}\mathrm{Im}\left[\zeta_{k_{1}}(\tau_{0})\zeta_{k_{2}}(\tau_{0})\zeta_{k_{3}}(\tau_{0})\zeta_{k_{1}}^{*}(\tau_{e})\zeta_{k_{2}}^{*}(\tau_{e})\zeta_{k_{3}}^{\prime*}(\tau_{e})\right]+\mathrm{perm}.

(125)

In squeezed limit, the bispectrum becomes

\displaystyle\langle\!\langle\zeta_{\bm{k}_{1}}(\tau_{0})\zeta_{\bm{k}_{2}}(\tau_{0})\zeta_{-\bm{k}_{2}}(\tau_{0})\rangle\!\rangle=-\left\{4\Delta\epsilon_{2}M_{\mathrm{pl}}^{2}\epsilon_{1}(\tau_{e})a^{2}(\tau_{e})\mathrm{Im}\left[\frac{\zeta_{k_{2}}^{2}(\tau_{0})}{\absolutevalue{\zeta_{k_2}(\tau_0)}^{2}}\zeta_{k_{2}}^{*}(\tau_{e})\zeta_{k_{2}}^{\prime*}(\tau_{e})\right]\right.

\displaystyle\left.-4\Delta\epsilon_{2}M_{\mathrm{pl}}^{2}\epsilon_{1}(\tau_{s})a^{2}(\tau_{s})\mathrm{Im}\left[\frac{\zeta_{k_{2}}^{2}(\tau_{0})}{\absolutevalue{\zeta_{k_2}(\tau_0)}^{2}}\zeta_{k_{2}}^{*}(\tau_{s})\zeta_{k_{2}}^{\prime*}(\tau_{s})\right]\right\}\absolutevalue{\zeta_{k_1}(\tau_0)}^{2}\absolutevalue{\zeta_{k_2}(\tau_0)}^{2}.

(126)

We define the term inside parenthesis as
C_{0}(k)
, so the bispectrum can be written as

\langle\!\langle\zeta_{\bm{k}_{1}}(\tau_{0})\zeta_{\bm{k}_{2}}(\tau_{0})\zeta_{-\bm{k}_{2}}(\tau_{0})\rangle\!\rangle=-C_{0}(k_{2})\absolutevalue{\zeta_{k_1}(\tau_0)}^{2}\absolutevalue{\zeta_{k_2}(\tau_0)}^{2}.

(127)

We can show that
C_{0}(k)=n_{s}(k,\tau_{0})-1
analytically, which confirms Maldacena’s theorem explicitly
[
10
]
.

102

\langle\!\langle\zeta_{\bm{k}_{1}}(\tau)\zeta_{\bm{k}_{2}}(\tau)\zeta_{-\bm{k}_{2}}(\tau)\rangle\!\rangle=-(n_{s}(k_{2},\tau)-1)\absolutevalue{\zeta_{k_1}(\tau)}^{2}\absolutevalue{\zeta_{k_2}(\tau)}^{2}.

(128)

We can read that spectral tilt of the short-scale perturbation, depends on time even at superhorizon scale, specifically around the transition time. Plot of the spectral tilt at the end of USR period and at the end of inflation are shown in Fig.

. Thus, in contrast to the case of the SR inflation in Sec.

where time dependence

In the presence of peaks and troughs in the power spectrum, Maldacena’s theorem is generalized in

. In addition, the consistency relation (

) fixes the sign of

at the peak, which has an implication for the abundances of PBH

4.3
Beyond tree-level contribution

So far, we have discussed two-point and three-point functions of the curvature perturbation at tree-level approximation. We have shown that the sharp transition of the second SR parameter generates a
\mathcal{O}(1)
contribution to the nonlinear parameter
f_{\mathrm{NL}}
. It is a large contribution to the bispectrum, which implies that a higher-order correction than first-order perturbation theory can also be large.

The second-order expansion of in-in perturbation theory reads

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{O(\tau)}\rangle_{(1)}=\langle\mathcal{O(\tau)}\rangle_{(0,2)}^{\dagger}+\langle\mathcal{O(\tau)}\rangle_{(1,1)}+\langle\mathcal{O(\tau)}\rangle_{(0,2)},

(129)

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{O(\tau)}\rangle_{(1,1)}=\int_{-\infty}^{\tau}\mathrm{d}\tau_{1}\int_{-\infty}^{\tau}\mathrm{d}\tau_{2}\left\langle H_{\mathrm{int}}(\tau_{1})\mathcal{\hat{O}}(\tau)H_{\mathrm{int}}(\tau_{2})\right\rangle,

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{O(\tau)}\rangle_{(0,2)}=-\int_{-\infty}^{\tau}\mathrm{d}\tau_{1}\int_{-\infty}^{\tau_{1}}\mathrm{d}\tau_{2}\left\langle\mathcal{\hat{O}}(\tau)H_{\mathrm{int}}(\tau_{1})H_{\mathrm{int}}(\tau_{2})\right\rangle.

In the presence of cubic self-interaction in the Hamiltonian, the second-order perturbation contributes to one-loop correction to the power spectrum and tree-level contribution to the trispectrum. For an inflation model with sharp transition of the second SR parameter, a one-loop correction to the large-scale power spectrum was first derived in
[
9
]
. In this case, the operator is
\zeta_{\bm{p}}(\tau)\zeta_{-\bm{p}}(\tau)
, where
\mathbf{p}
is the CMB scale wavevector, evaluated at
\tau=\tau_{0}~(\rightarrow 0)
.

At the end of inflation, there is no contribution from the field redefinition because it is evaluated at the SR period. Only the bulk interaction (
102
) contributes to the one-loop correction. Substituting the corresponding Hamiltonian of (
102
) to (
129
) yields

\displaystyle\langle\zeta_{\bm{p}}(\tau_{0})\zeta_{-\bm{p}}(\tau_{0})\rangle_{(1,1)}=

\displaystyle~\frac{1}{4}M_{\mathrm{pl}}^{4}\int_{-\infty}^{\tau_{0}}\mathrm{d}\tau_{1}~a^{2}(\tau_{1})\epsilon_{1}(\tau_{1})\epsilon_{2}^{\prime}(\tau_{1})\int_{-\infty}^{\tau_{0}}\mathrm{d}\tau_{2}~a^{2}(\tau_{2})\epsilon_{1}(\tau_{2})\epsilon_{2}^{\prime}(\tau_{2})

\displaystyle\times\int\prod_{a=1}^{6}\left[\frac{\mathrm{d}^{3}k_{a}}{(2\pi)^{3}}\right]\delta(\bm{k}_{1}+\bm{k}_{2}+\bm{k}_{3})\delta(\bm{k}_{4}+\bm{k}_{5}+\bm{k}_{6})

\displaystyle\times\left\langle\zeta_{\bm{k}_{1}}^{\prime}(\tau_{1})\zeta_{\bm{k}_{2}}(\tau_{1})\zeta_{\bm{k}_{3}}(\tau_{1})\zeta_{\bm{p}}(\tau_{0})\zeta_{-\bm{p}}(\tau_{0})\zeta_{\bm{k}_{4}}^{\prime}(\tau_{2})\zeta_{\bm{k}_{5}}(\tau_{2})\zeta_{\bm{k}_{6}}(\tau_{2})\right\rangle,

(130)

\displaystyle\langle\zeta_{\bm{p}}(\tau_{0})\zeta_{-\bm{p}}(\tau_{0})\rangle_{(0,2)}=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{4}M_{\mathrm{pl}}^{4}\int_{-\infty}^{\tau_{0}}\mathrm{d}\tau_{1}~a^{2}(\tau_{1})\epsilon_{1}(\tau_{1})\epsilon_{2}^{\prime}(\tau_{1})\int_{-\infty}^{\tau_{1}}\mathrm{d}\tau_{2}~a^{2}(\tau_{2})\epsilon_{1}(\tau_{2})\epsilon_{2}^{\prime}(\tau_{2})

\displaystyle\times\int\prod_{a=1}^{6}\left[\frac{\mathrm{d}^{3}k_{a}}{(2\pi)^{3}}\right]\delta(\bm{k}_{1}+\bm{k}_{2}+\bm{k}_{3})\delta(\bm{k}_{4}+\bm{k}_{5}+\bm{k}_{6})

\displaystyle\times\left\langle\zeta_{\bm{p}}(\tau_{0})\zeta_{-\bm{p}}(\tau_{0})\zeta_{\bm{k}_{1}}^{\prime}(\tau_{1})\zeta_{\bm{k}_{2}}(\tau_{1})\zeta_{\bm{k}_{3}}(\tau_{1})\zeta_{\bm{k}_{4}}^{\prime}(\tau_{2})\zeta_{\bm{k}_{5}}(\tau_{2})\zeta_{\bm{k}_{6}}(\tau_{2})\right\rangle.

(131)

Performing Wick contraction and summing both terms lead to

\displaystyle\langle\!\langle\zeta_{\bm{p}}(\tau_{0})\zeta_{-\bm{p}}(\tau_{0})\rangle\!\rangle_{(1)}=\frac{1}{4}M_{\mathrm{pl}}^{4}\epsilon_{1}^{2}(\tau_{e})a^{4}(\tau_{e})(\Delta\epsilon_{2})^{2}\int\frac{\mathrm{d}^{3}k}{(2\pi)^{3}}\left\{\zeta_{p}(\tau_{0})\zeta_{p}^{*}(\tau_{0})\left(4\zeta_{p}^{\prime}\zeta_{p}^{\prime*}\zeta_{q}\zeta_{q}^{*}\zeta_{k}\zeta_{k}^{*}\right.\right.

\displaystyle\left.\left.+8\zeta_{p}^{\prime*}\zeta_{p}\zeta_{q}^{\prime}\zeta_{q}^{*}\zeta_{k}\zeta_{k}^{*}+8\zeta_{p}^{\prime}\zeta_{p}^{*}\zeta_{q}^{\prime*}\zeta_{q}\zeta_{k}\zeta_{k}^{*}+16\zeta_{p}\zeta_{p}^{*}\zeta_{q}^{\prime}\zeta_{q}^{\prime*}\zeta_{k}\zeta_{k}^{*}\right)_{\tau=\tau_{e}}-\mathrm{Re}\left[\zeta_{p}(\tau_{0})\zeta_{p}(\tau_{0})\right.\right.

(132)

\displaystyle\left.\left.\times\left(4\zeta_{p}^{\prime*}\zeta_{p}^{\prime*}\zeta_{q}\zeta_{q}^{*}\zeta_{k}\zeta_{k}^{*}+8\zeta_{p}^{\prime*}\zeta_{p}^{*}\zeta_{q}^{\prime}\zeta_{q}^{*}\zeta_{k}\zeta_{k}^{*}+8\zeta_{p}^{\prime*}\zeta_{p}^{*}\zeta_{q}^{\prime*}\zeta_{q}\zeta_{k}\zeta_{k}^{*}+16\zeta_{p}^{*}\zeta_{p}^{*}\zeta_{q}^{\prime}\zeta_{q}^{\prime*}\zeta_{k}\zeta_{k}^{*}\right)_{\tau=\tau_{e}}\right]\right\}.

Here, we only show the leading contribution to the one-loop correction that comes from integral of Dirac-delta function at
\tau=\tau_{e}
. The contributions of
\tau=\tau_{s}
are subleading, which are explicitly evaluated in
[
10
]
.

For
p\ll k
, the one-loop correction can be simplified to

\langle\!\langle\zeta_{\bm{p}}(\tau_{0})\zeta_{-\bm{p}}(\tau_{0})\rangle\!\rangle_{(1)}=\frac{1}{4}M_{\mathrm{pl}}^{4}(\Delta\epsilon_{2})^{2}\absolutevalue{\zeta_p(\tau_0)}^{2}\int\frac{\mathrm{d}^{3}k}{(2\pi)^{3}}16\left[\epsilon_{1}^{2}a^{4}\absolutevalue{\zeta_k}^{2}\mathrm{Im}(\zeta_{p}\zeta_{p}^{\prime*})\mathrm{Im}(\zeta_{k}\zeta_{k}^{\prime*})\right]_{\tau=\tau_{e}}.

(133)

We find that the one-loop correction is exactly proportional to the quantum commutator (
46
), which implies the importance of decaying mode even for superhorizon mode function. If we only consider the constant mode of curvature perturbation at superhorizon scale, we will obtain an incorrect result
\left[\hat{\zeta}_{\bm{p}}(\tau),\hat{\zeta}_{\bm{q}}^{\prime}(\tau)\right]=0
. From commutator (
46
), we find

\mathrm{Im}(\zeta_{k}(\tau)\zeta_{k}^{\prime*}(\tau))=\frac{1}{4M_{\mathrm{pl}}^{2}\epsilon(\tau)a^{2}(\tau)},

(134)

which does not depend on wavenumber
k
. As we have already seen, in the standard SR inflation this quantity decreases exponentially as a function of conformal time.
However, during USR regime, since

\epsilon
decreases much more rapicly than
a^{-2}
, the right-hand-side of (
46
) and (
134
)
increases
exponentially, which makes the one-loop correction to the power spectrum large even on the large scale probed by CMB.

Then, substituting (
134
) into (
133
), we obtain a one-loop correction to the power spectrum

\Delta_{s(1)}^{2}(p,\tau_{0})=\frac{1}{4}(\Delta\epsilon_{2})^{2}\Delta_{s(0)}^{2}(p,\tau_{0})\int_{k_{\mathrm{IR}}}^{k_{\mathrm{UV}}}\frac{\mathrm{d}k}{k}\Delta_{s(0)}^{2}(k,\tau_{e}).

(135)

Because we are interested in the effect of large fluctuation on small scales related to PBH formation to the fluctuations on large scales observed by CMB, a natural choice of cutoffs are
k_{\mathrm{IR}}=k_{s}
and
k_{\mathrm{UV}}=k_{e}
. For
k_{e}/k_{s}\ll 1
, evaluating the wavenumber integral with small-scale mode function (
112
) yields

\Delta_{s(1)}^{2}(p,\tau_{0})=\frac{1}{4}(\Delta\epsilon_{2})^{2}\Delta_{s(0)}^{2}(p,\tau_{0})\Delta_{s(\mathrm{PBH})}^{2}\left(1.1+\log\frac{k_{e}}{k_{s}}\right).

(136)

Requiring the one-loop correction to be much smaller than tree-level contribution yields an upper-bound on small-scale power spectrum

\Delta_{s(\mathrm{PBH})}^{2}\ll\frac{1}{(\Delta\epsilon_{2})^{2}}\simeq 0.03,

(137)

where the numerical value is obtained by substituting
\Delta\epsilon_{2}=6
for transition from USR to SR period. Such a bound suggests that sharp transition of the second SR parameter cannot generate large fluctuation on small scales, otherwise perturbation theory breaks down. This result should be understood under caveat that there are other contributions from quartic Hamiltonian, both induced by the third-order action (
102
) and fourth-order action.

5.2
Summary

In this chapter, we have explained the mechanism to generate large primordial fluctuation in single-field inflation. In order to amplify the curvature perturbation, violation of the SR approximation is needed. We have discussed the simplest example of an inflation model that violates the SR approximation, called USR inflation. We have shown that USR inflation leads to growth of the curvature perturbation outside the horizon. The consequence is that USR inflation cannot explain the required e-folds number to solve the horizon problem.

Nevertheless, one can utilize the properties of the USR period for another purpose. If the USR period is sandwiched by two SR periods, the curvature perturbation grows temporary during the USR period. At the end of inflation, the power spectrum of the curvature perturbation is amplified on small scales corresponding to the time scale of the USR period, while keeping the small amplitude on large scale consistent with CMB observations.

Moreover, we have explained the nonlinear evolution of the curvature perturbation induced by cubic self-interaction, which yields three-point functions or bispectrum. The cubic self-interaction consists of bulk interaction and field redefinition. In USR inflation, only the field redefinition contributes to the bispectrum. When a transition from USR to SR period exists, the bulk interaction that is proportional to the time derivative of the second SR parameter becomes important. If the transition is sharp, the nonlinear parameter of the bispectrum has a
\mathcal{O}(1)
value. This means that the bispectrum is large enough, which indicates that the nonlinear effect cannot be neglected. At the same time, such a nonlinear effect gives backreaction to the power spectrum as a one-loop correction that leads to a constraint on the small-scale power spectrum associated with transition of the second SR parameter.
