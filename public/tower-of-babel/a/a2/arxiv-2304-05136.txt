Unified theory of quantum crystals and optical lattices with Bose-Einstein condensate


Abstract
When interactions between particles are strong, at low temperature, these particles can form self-organized quantum crystals, and when the particles interact weakly, periodic structures can be imposed by external fields, e.g. by optical lattices. These opposite cases usually are treated separately, dealing either with quantum crystals or with optical lattices. Here the unified theory is developed for arbitrary particle interaction strength, treating in the frame of the same model both the limiting cases of quantum crystals and optical lattices, as well as the states intermediate between these two limits. Bose particles are considered, hence at low temperature in optical lattices Bose-Einstein condensation can happen, while it seems to be prohibited in ideal quantum crystals that do not contain mesoscopic regions of disorder, such as dislocations and grain boundaries.

Introduction

Bose-Einstein condensation of dilute gas in optical lattices has been a topic
of high interest in the recent years

Bose gas in an optical lattice composes a periodic system, with the periodicity
superimposed by the externally created lattice. Dilute gas in optical lattices is
usually characterized by point-like interactions, because of which the interaction
between different lattice sites are small and do not play important role. The
structure of the system is basically due to the optical lattice. However, when particle

8

From the other side, it is known that sufficiently strong particle interactions lead
to the formation of solids with crystalline structure. At low temperature, there can
occur self-organized quantum crystals that practically are not influenced by an
external optical lattice. The description of quantum crystals requires a principally
different theoretical approach, as compared with optical lattices

[

,
11
,
12
,
13
]
.

The aim of this paper is to develop a unified theory of a system of particles
combining in the frame of the same model both the limiting cases of optical lattices
and quantum crystals as well as the states intermediate between these two limiting
cases. The occurrence of this or that state depends on the type of particle
interactions. Here bosons are considered, so that Bose-Einstein condensation can
arise, depending on the system parameters. Optical lattices with strong intersite
interactions have been considered in Refs.
[
14
,
15
]
. The
principal novelty of the present paper is the development of a unified model allowing
for taking account of Bose-Einstein condensation in an arbitrary spatially periodic
system, with particle interactions varying from weak atoms in an optical lattice to
strong interactions typical of self-organized quantum crystals.

Bose condensation of weakly interacting atomic gases in optical lattices is,
of course, well known

,

,

,

,

,

.
It is also known that in ideal quantum crystals, having no mesoscopic regions of

,

,

,

,

,

.
However, the general situation has not been studied, when in the frame of a unified

ideal lattices, containing no nanosize defects, such as dislocations or grain boundaries.
Taking account of such defects requires separate consideration

23

The layout of the paper is as follows. Section 2 starts with the description of a
spatially periodic system. Section 3 considers phonon collective excitations. The
inclusion of possible Bose-Einstein condensate is given in Sec. 4. Quasi-momentum
representation is introduced in Sec. 5. Atomic degrees of freedom in the presence
of Bose-Einstein condensate are treated in Sec. 6. In Sec. 7, the case of zero
temperature is considered and the restriction on the parameters when Bose condensation
in a spatially periodic system can develop is defined. Sec. 8 defines the critical
temperature at which the Bose condensation could happen, provided that a stable
condensed state is admissible. Section 9 concludes.

2
Breaking of translational symmetry

Let us start with the standard energy Hamiltonian of atoms

\hat{H}=\int\hat{\psi}^{\dagger}({\bf r})\;\hat{H}_{L}({\bf r})\;\hat{\psi}({\bf r})\;d{\bf r}\;+

+\;\frac{1}{2}\int\hat{\psi}^{\dagger}({\bf r})\;\hat{\psi}^{\dagger}({\bf r}^{\prime})\;\Phi({\bf r}-{\bf r}^{\prime})\;\hat{\psi}({\bf r}^{\prime})\;\hat{\psi}({\bf r})\;d{\bf r}d{\bf r}^{\prime}\;,

(1)

in which
\hat{\psi}
are boson field operators,
\Phi
is an interaction
potential, the lattice Hamiltonian is

\hat{H}_{L}({\bf r})=\frac{{\hat{\bf p}}^{2}}{2m}+U_{L}({\bf r})\qquad({\hat{\bf p}}\equiv-i\nabla)\;,

(2)

with the lattice potential usually
[
24
]
taken in the form

U_{L}({\bf r})=\sum_{\alpha=1}^{d}U_{\alpha}\sin^{2}(k_{0}^{2}r_{\alpha})\;.

The spatial dimension is denoted by
d
. Parameters
k_{0}^{\alpha}=\pi/a_{\alpha}

are prescribed by laser beams forming the optical lattice with the lattice
spacings
a_{\alpha}
, where
\alpha=1,2,\ldots,d
.

Following the scheme of Refs.
[
14
,
15
]
, we consider
the lowest-energy band corresponding to the well-localized Wannier functions

[
25
]
, keeping in mind that collective excitations will be
characterized by phonon degrees of freedom. Then the field operators can be
expanded over the well-localized Wannier functions,

\hat{\psi}({\bf r})=\sum_{j}\hat{c}_{j}w({\bf r}-{\bf r}_{j})\;,

(3)

where the index
j=1,2,\ldots,N_{L}
enumerates the lattice sites, whose number
is not necessarily equal to the number of atoms
N
. Then the filling factor

\nu\equiv\frac{N}{N_{L}}=\rho a^{d}\qquad\left(\rho\equiv\frac{N}{V}\right)

(4)

is not necessarily one. Here
a
is a mean distance between lattice sites,

a\equiv\left(\frac{V}{N_{L}}\right)^{1/d}=\left(\frac{\nu}{\rho}\right)^{1/d}\;.

(5)

The interaction potential
\Phi(\bf r)
can be represented by an effective
potential taking account of the influence of pair particle correlations, which
corresponds to the Kirkwood approximation
[
26
]
and makes this
potential integrable. As has been shown

[
27
,
28
,
29
,
30
]
, starting with the Kirkwood
approximation, it is possible to develop an iterative procedure for Green
functions containing no divergences. Note that another method of removing
divergences is by introducing an energy cut-off in the interaction

[
31
,
32
]
. The use of the pair correlation function for
smoothing a nonintegrable interaction potential

[
27
,
28
,
29
,
30
]
seems to be the most general
for elaborating an iterative procedure for Green functions (either at real or
at imaginary times
[
33
]
).

Following the standard procedure
[
4
]
, we come to the Hamiltonian

\hat{H}=-\sum_{i\neq j}J_{ij}\;\hat{c}_{i}^{\dagger}\;\hat{c}_{j}+\sum_{j}\left(\frac{{\bf p}_{j}^{2}}{2m}+U_{L}\right)\;\hat{c}_{j}^{\dagger}\;\hat{c}_{j}\;+

+\;\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j}U_{jj}\;\hat{c}_{j}^{\dagger}\;\hat{c}_{j}^{\dagger}\;\hat{c}_{j}\;\hat{c}_{j}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i\neq j}U_{ij}\;\hat{c}_{i}^{\dagger}\;\hat{c}_{j}^{\dagger}\;\hat{c}_{j}\;\hat{c}_{i}\;,

(6)

in which the tunneling parameter is

J_{ij}=-\int w^{*}({\bf r}-{\bf r}_{i})\;\hat{H}_{L}({\bf r})\;w({\bf r}-{\bf r}_{j})\;d{\bf r}\;,

(7)

the effective intersite interaction is

U_{ij}=\int|\;w({\bf r}-{\bf r}_{i})\;|^{2}\;\Phi({\bf r}-{\bf r}^{\prime})\;|\;w({\bf r}^{\prime}-{\bf r}_{j})\;|^{2}\;d{\bf r}d{\bf r}^{\prime}\;,

(8)

the average squared momentum at a site is

{\bf p}_{j}^{2}=\int w^{*}({\bf r}-{\bf r}_{j})\;{\hat{\bf p}}^{2}\;w({\bf r}-{\bf r}_{j})\;d{\bf r}\;,

(9)

and the lattice potential parameter is

U_{L}=\int|\;w({\bf r})\;|^{2}\;U_{L}({\bf r})\;d{\bf r}\;.

(10)

For instance, in the tight-binding approximation, where the Wannier functions are
represented by the Gaussians,

w({\bf r})=\prod_{\alpha}\left(\frac{1}{\pi l_{\alpha}^{2}}\right)^{1/4}\exp\left(-\;\frac{r_{\alpha}^{2}}{2l_{\alpha}^{2}}\right)\;,

(11)

with the effective radius

l_{\alpha}\equiv\frac{1}{\sqrt{m\varepsilon_{\alpha}}}\;,

(12)

the tunneling parameter reads as

J_{ij}=\sum_{\alpha}\frac{\varepsilon_{\alpha}}{8}\;\left[\left(\frac{r_{ij}^{\alpha}}{l_{\alpha}}\right)^{2}-2\right]\;\exp\left\{-\frac{1}{4}\sum_{\alpha}\left(\frac{r_{ij}^{\alpha}}{l_{\alpha}}\right)^{2}\right\}\;,

(13)

where

{\bf r}_{ij}\equiv{\bf r}_{i}-{\bf r}_{j}=\{r_{ij}^{\alpha}\}\;.

The effective frequency of particle oscillations inside a lattice site
\varepsilon_{\alpha}

can be defined by a self-consistent procedure that, in principle, does not require
the expansion of the interaction potential when anharmonic effects are important
(see e.g.
[
11
,
34
,
35
]
). Since our main aim is, first of
all, to develop a unified approach describing optical lattices as well as quantum
crystals, we shall not distract the reader by technical problems and will use the
simple self-consistent harmonic approximation

[
36
,
37
,
38
]
. Then the effective frequency
\varepsilon_{\alpha}

is defined in the frame of optimized perturbation theory

[
36
,
37
,
38
]
starting with a zero-order approximation
with a trial periodic potential
U_{0}({\bf r})
, such that the first approximation
for the statistical average of an operator
\hat{A}
be equal to the initial
approximation,

\langle\;\hat{A}\;\rangle_{1}=\langle\;\hat{A}\;\rangle_{0}\;.

(14)

This condition reduces to the equation

\int|\;w({\bf r}-{\bf r}_{j})\;|^{2}\;U_{0}({\bf r})\;d{\bf r}=\int|\;w({\bf r}-{\bf r}_{j})\;|^{2}\;U_{L}({\bf r})\;d{\bf r}\;+

+\;\int|\;w({\bf r}-{\bf r}_{j})\;|^{2}\;\Phi({\bf r}-{\bf r}^{\prime})\;\nu\sum_{i}|\;w({\bf r}-{\bf r}_{i}^{\prime})\;|^{2}\;d{\bf r}d{\bf r}^{\prime}\;,

(15)

which, due to the periodicity of the potentials
U_{0}({\bf r})
and
U_{L}({\bf r})
,
can be transformed into

\int|\;w({\bf r})\;|^{2}\;U_{0}({\bf r})\;d{\bf r}=\int|\;w({\bf r})\;|^{2}\;U_{L}({\bf r})\;d{\bf r}\;+

+\;\int|\;w({\bf r})\;|^{2}\;\Phi({\bf r}-{\bf r}^{\prime})\;\nu\sum_{i}|\;w({\bf r}-{\bf r}_{i})\;|^{2}\;d{\bf r}d{\bf r}^{\prime}\;.

(16)

In the vicinity of
{\bf r}\approx 0
, the trial and lattice potentials can be
represented as

U_{0}({\bf r})\cong u_{0}+\sum_{\alpha=1}^{d}\frac{m}{2}\;\varepsilon_{\alpha}^{2}r_{\alpha}^{2}\;,

U_{L}({\bf r})\cong\sum_{\alpha=1}^{d}\frac{m}{2}\;\omega_{\alpha}^{2}r_{\alpha}^{2}\;,

(17)

with the effective potential well at a site

u_{0}=\nu\sum_{j}\Phi({\bf r}_{j})\;.

(18)

Then Eq. (
16
) reduces to

\frac{1}{4}\sum_{\alpha}\left(\varepsilon_{\alpha}\;-\;\frac{\omega_{\alpha}^{2}}{\varepsilon_{\alpha}}\;-\;\frac{\Omega_{\alpha}^{2}}{\varepsilon_{\alpha}}\right)=0\;,

(19)

where

\Omega_{\alpha}^{2}\equiv\frac{2\nu}{m}\sum_{j}\frac{\partial^{2}\Phi({\bf r}_{j})}{\partial r_{j}^{\alpha}\partial r_{j}^{\alpha}}\;.

(20)

This yields the effective trial frequency

\varepsilon_{\alpha}=\sqrt{\omega_{\alpha}^{2}+\Omega_{\alpha}^{2}}

(21)

depending on both particle interactions and the optical lattice.

The optimized perturbation theory can also be realized by equating the zero-order
approximation for the energy per particle

E_{0}=u_{0}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\alpha}\varepsilon_{\alpha}

(22)

to its first-order approximation

E_{1}=\int w^{*}({\bf r})\;\hat{H}_{1}({\bf r})\;w({\bf r})\;d{\bf r}\;,

(23)

in which

\hat{H}_{1}({\bf r})=\frac{{\hat{\bf p}}^{2}}{2m}+U_{L}({\bf r})+\int\Phi({\bf r}-{\bf r}^{\prime})\;\nu\sum_{j}|\;w({\bf r}^{\prime}-{\bf r}_{j})\;|^{2}\;d{\bf r}^{\prime}\;.

(24)

The equation

E_{0}=E_{1}

(25)

leads to the same effective frequency (
21
).

3
Phonon excitations

According to the accepted picture
[
14
]
, the previous Sec. 2 describes
the state of atoms in the lowest energy band. Excitations above the lowest state,
characterizing atomic oscillations correspond to phonon degrees of freedom. These
are introduced as follows.

The atomic position is represented as

{\bf r}_{j}={\bf a}_{j}+{\bf u}_{j}\;,

(26)

where

{\bf a}_{j}\equiv\langle\;{\bf r}_{j}\;\rangle

(27)

is a fixed location of a lattice site and
{\bf u}_{j}
is an operator describing
the deviation from the site, such that

\langle\;{\bf u}_{j}\;\rangle=0\;.

(28)

The quantities
J_{ij}
and
U_{ij}
, entering the Hamiltonian, are functions of
the location (
26
),

J_{ij}\equiv J({\bf r}_{ij})\;,\qquad U_{ij}\equiv U({\bf r}_{ij})\;,\qquad U_{jj}=U(0)\equiv U\;,

(29)

where the notations are used:

{\bf r}_{ij}\equiv{\bf r}_{i}-{\bf r}_{j}={\bf a}_{ij}-{\bf u}_{ij}\;,\qquad{\bf a}_{ij}\equiv{\bf a}_{i}-{\bf a}_{j}\;,\qquad{\bf u}_{ij}\equiv{\bf u}_{i}-{\bf u}_{j}\;.

(30)

Expanding
J_{ij}
and
U_{ij}
in powers of the relative deviations, we have

J({\bf r}_{ij})\simeq J({\bf a}_{ij})+\sum_{\alpha}J_{ij}^{\alpha}u_{ij}^{\alpha}-\;\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\alpha\beta}J_{ij}^{\alpha\beta}u_{ij}^{\alpha}u_{ij}^{\beta}\;,

U({\bf r}_{ij})\simeq U({\bf a}_{ij})+\sum_{\alpha}U_{ij}^{\alpha}u_{ij}^{\alpha}-\;\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\alpha\beta}U_{ij}^{\alpha\beta}u_{ij}^{\alpha}u_{ij}^{\beta}\;,

(31)

where the first derivatives are

J_{ij}^{\alpha}\equiv\frac{\partial J({\bf a}_{ij})}{\partial a_{i}^{\alpha}}=\frac{\partial J({\bf a}_{ij})}{\partial a_{ij}^{\alpha}}\;,\qquad U_{ij}^{\alpha}\equiv\frac{\partial U({\bf a}_{ij})}{\partial a_{i}^{\alpha}}=\frac{\partial U({\bf a}_{ij})}{\partial a_{ij}^{\alpha}}\;,

and the second derivatives are

J_{ij}^{\alpha\beta}\equiv\frac{\partial^{2}J({\bf a}_{ij})}{\partial a_{i}^{\alpha}\partial a_{j}^{\beta}}=-\;\frac{\partial^{2}J({\bf a}_{ij})}{\partial a_{ij}^{\alpha}\partial a_{ij}^{\beta}}\;,

U_{ij}^{\alpha\beta}\equiv\frac{\partial^{2}U({\bf a}_{ij})}{\partial a_{i}^{\alpha}\partial a_{j}^{\beta}}=-\;\frac{\partial^{2}U({\bf a}_{ij})}{\partial a_{ij}^{\alpha}\partial a_{ij}^{\beta}}\;.

Atomic and phonon degrees of freedom can be decoupled by means of the conditions

u_{ij}^{\alpha}\;u_{ij}^{\beta}\;\hat{c}_{i}^{\dagger}\;\hat{c}_{j}^{\dagger}\;\hat{c}_{j}\;\hat{c}_{i}=\langle\;u_{ij}^{\alpha}u_{ij}^{\beta}\;\rangle\;\hat{c}_{i}^{\dagger}\;\hat{c}_{j}^{\dagger}\;\hat{c}_{j}\;\hat{c}_{i}+

+u_{ij}^{\alpha}\;u_{ij}^{\beta}\;\langle\;\hat{c}_{i}^{\dagger}\;\hat{c}_{j}^{\dagger}\;\hat{c}_{j}\;\hat{c}_{i}\;\rangle-\langle\;u_{ij}^{\alpha}\;u_{ij}^{\beta}\;\rangle\langle\;\hat{c}_{i}^{\dagger}\;\hat{c}_{j}^{\dagger}\;\hat{c}_{j}\;\hat{c}_{i}\;\rangle\;,

u_{ij}^{\alpha}\;u_{ij}^{\beta}\;\hat{c}_{i}^{\dagger}\;\hat{c}_{j}=\langle\;u_{ij}^{\alpha}\;u_{ij}^{\beta}\;\rangle\;\hat{c}_{i}^{\dagger}\;\hat{c}_{j}+u_{ij}^{\alpha}\;u_{ij}^{\beta}\;\langle\;\hat{c}_{i}^{\dagger}\;\hat{c}_{j}\;\rangle-\langle\;u_{ij}^{\alpha}\;u_{ij}^{\beta}\;\rangle\langle\;\hat{c}_{i}^{\dagger}\;\hat{c}_{j}\;\rangle\;,

{\bf p}_{j}^{2}\;\hat{c}_{j}^{\dagger}\;\hat{c}_{j}=\langle\;{\bf p}_{j}^{2}\;\rangle\;\hat{c}_{j}^{\dagger}\;\hat{c}_{j}+{\bf p}_{j}^{2}\;\langle\;c_{j}^{\dagger}\;\hat{c}_{j}\;\rangle-\langle\;{\bf p}_{j}^{2}\;\rangle\langle\;c_{j}^{\dagger}\;\hat{c}_{j}\;\rangle\;.

Then the tunneling renormalizes to

\widetilde{J}_{ij}\equiv J({\bf a}_{ij})-\;\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\alpha\beta}J_{ij}^{\alpha\beta}\langle\;u_{ij}^{\alpha}\;u_{ij}^{\beta}\;\rangle

(32)

and the interaction between lattice sites to

\widetilde{U}_{ij}\equiv U({\bf a}_{ij})-\;\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\alpha\beta}U_{ij}^{\alpha\beta}\langle\;u_{ij}^{\alpha}\;u_{ij}^{\beta}\;\rangle\;.

(33)

Also, let us introduce the renormalized dynamical matrix

\Phi_{ij}^{\alpha\beta}\equiv U_{ij}^{\alpha\beta}\langle\;\hat{c}_{i}^{\dagger}\;\hat{c}_{j}^{d}gr\;\hat{c}_{j}\;\hat{c}_{i}\;\rangle-2J_{ij}^{\alpha\beta}\langle\;\hat{c}_{i}^{\dagger}\;\hat{c}_{j}\;\rangle

(34)

and the force

F_{ij}^{\alpha}\equiv 2J_{ij}^{\alpha}\;\hat{c}_{i}^{\dagger}\;\hat{c}_{j}-U_{ij}^{\alpha}\;\hat{c}_{i}^{\dagger}\;\hat{c}_{j}^{\dagger}\;\hat{c}_{j}\;\hat{c}_{i}\;.

(35)

In that way, the Hamiltonian (
6
) becomes

\hat{H}=\hat{H}_{at}+\hat{H}_{vib}+\hat{H}_{def}+E_{N}\;.

(36)

Here the atomic Hamiltonian is

\hat{H}_{at}=-\sum_{i\neq j}\widetilde{J}_{ij}\;\hat{c}_{i}^{\dagger}\;\hat{c}_{j}+\frac{1}{2}\;U\sum_{j}\hat{c}_{j}^{\dagger}\;\hat{c}_{j}^{\dagger}\;\hat{c}_{j}\;\hat{c}_{j}\;+

+\;\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i\neq j}\widetilde{U}_{ij}\;\hat{c}_{i}^{\dagger}\;\hat{c}_{j}^{\dagger}\;\hat{c}_{j}\;\hat{c}_{i}+\sum_{j}\left(\frac{\langle{\bf p}_{j}^{2}\rangle}{2m}+U_{L}\right)\hat{c}_{j}^{\dagger}\;\hat{c}_{j}\;,

(37)

the Hamiltonian describing atomic vibrations is

\hat{H}_{vib}=\nu\sum_{j}\frac{{\bf p}_{j}^{2}}{2m}+\frac{1}{4}\sum_{i\neq j}\;\sum_{\alpha\beta}\Phi_{ij}^{\alpha\beta}u_{i}^{\alpha}u_{j}^{\beta}\;,

(38)

and the term responsible for deformation caused by force (
35
) is

\hat{H}_{def}=-\;\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j}\sum_{\alpha}\left[F_{j}^{\alpha}+(F_{j}^{\alpha})^{+}\right]\;u_{j}^{\alpha}\;.

(39)

The non-operator term reads as

E_{N}=\frac{1}{4}\sum_{i\neq j}\;\sum_{\alpha\beta}\Phi_{ij}^{\alpha\beta}\langle\;u_{i}^{\alpha}u_{j}^{\beta}\;\rangle-\nu\sum_{j}\frac{\langle{\bf p}_{j}^{2}\rangle}{2m}\;.

(40)

Due to the decoupling of atomic and phonon degrees of freedom

F_{j}^{\alpha}u_{j}^{\alpha}=F_{j}^{\alpha}\langle\;u_{j}^{\alpha}\;\rangle+\langle\;F_{j}^{\alpha}\;\rangle u_{j}^{\alpha}-\langle\;F_{j}^{\alpha}\;\rangle\langle\;u_{j}^{\alpha}\;\rangle=0\;,

(41)

the deformation term is zero,

\hat{H}_{def}=0\;.

(42)

Using the canonical transformation

{\bf u}_{j}=\frac{1}{2N}\sum_{ks}\sqrt{\frac{\nu}{m\omega_{ks}}}\;{\bf e}_{ks}\left(b_{ks}+b_{-ks}^{\dagger}\right)e^{i{\bf k}\cdot{\bf a}_{j}}\;,

{\bf p}_{j}=-\;\frac{i}{2N}\sum_{ks}\sqrt{\frac{m\omega_{ks}}{\nu}}\;{\bf e}_{ks}\left(b_{ks}-b_{-ks}^{\dagger}\right)e^{i{\bf k}\cdot{\bf a}_{j}}\;,

(43)

and denoting the phonon frequency by the relation

\omega_{ks}^{2}=\frac{\nu}{m}\sum_{j(\neq i)}\;\sum_{\alpha\beta}\Phi_{ij}^{\alpha\beta}\;e_{ks}^{\alpha}\;e_{ks}^{\beta}\;e^{i{\bf k}\cdot{\bf a}_{ij}}\;,

(44)

we reduce the Hamiltonian (
36
) to the form

\hat{H}=\hat{H}_{at}+\hat{H}_{ph}+E_{N}\;,

(45)

with the phonon Hamiltonian

\hat{H}_{ph}=\sum_{ks}\omega_{ks}\left(b_{ks}^{\dagger}b_{ks}+\frac{1}{2}\right)\;.

(46)

Here
s
is a polarization label and
{\bf e}_{ks}
are the polarization vectors.

Then, taking into account that

\langle\;u_{ij}^{\alpha}u_{ij}^{\beta}\;\rangle=2(1-\delta_{ij})\langle\;u_{j}^{\alpha}u_{j}^{\beta}\;\rangle\;,

it is straightforward to find the deviation-deviation correlation function

\langle\;u_{i}^{\alpha}u_{j}^{\beta}\;\rangle=\delta_{ij}\;\frac{\nu}{2N}\sum_{ks}\frac{e_{ks}^{\alpha}e_{ks}^{\beta}}{m\omega_{ks}}\;\coth\left(\frac{\omega_{ks}}{2T}\right)

(47)

and the mean kinetic energy per atom

\frac{\langle{\bf p}_{j}^{2}\rangle}{2m}=\frac{1}{4\nu N}\sum_{ks}\omega_{ks}\coth\left(\frac{\omega_{ks}}{2T}\right)\;.

(48)

4
Gauge symmetry breaking

When the considered system is composed of bosonic atoms, at low temperatures,
Bose-Einstein condensation can happen. The necessary and sufficient condition for
the occurrence of Bose-Einstein condensate is the global gauge symmetry breaking

[
39
,
40
,
41
,
42
]
. A convenient way of breaking
the gauge symmetry is by the use of the Bogolubov
[
43
,
44
]

shift of the field operator

\hat{\psi}({\bf r})=\eta({\bf r})+\psi_{1}({\bf r})\;.

(49)

Here the order parameter

\eta({\bf r})\equiv\langle\;\hat{\psi}({\bf r}\;\rangle

(50)

is the condensate wave function and
\psi_{1}
is the field operator of uncondensed
atoms, such that

\langle\;\psi_{1}({\bf r}\;\rangle=0\;.

(51)

The condensed and uncondensed atoms describe different degrees of freedom that are
orthogonal to each other,

\int\eta^{*}({\bf r})\;\psi_{1}({\bf r})\;d{\bf r}=0\;.

(52)

In terms of the operator
\hat{c}_{j}
, the Bogolubov shift reads as

\hat{c}_{j}=\eta+c_{j}\;.

(53)

Similarly to the Bogolubov shift (
49
), the order parameter is

\eta\equiv\langle\;\hat{c}_{j}\;\rangle\;,

(54)

with the operator of uncondensed atoms yielding

\langle\;c_{j}\;\rangle=0\;.

(55)

From the orthogonality relation (
52
), we have the condition

\sum_{j}c_{j}=0\;.

(56)

The expansions over Wannier functions for the condensate wave function read as

\eta({\bf r})=\sum_{j}\eta\;w({\bf r}-{\bf a}_{j})

(57)

and for the operator of uncondensed atoms, as

s\psi_{1}({\bf r})=\sum_{j}c_{j}\;w({\bf r}-{\bf a}_{j})\;.

(58)

In equilibrium, the condensate parameter
\eta
can be taken as real, so that the
number of condensed atoms is

N_{0}=\sum_{j}\eta^{2}=N_{L}\eta^{2}\;.

(59)

The operator of uncondensed atoms is

\hat{N}_{1}=\sum_{j}c_{j}^{\dagger}\;c_{j}\;.

(60)

Hence the number of these atoms is

N_{1}=\langle\;\hat{N}_{1}\;\rangle=\sum_{j}\langle\;c_{j}^{\dagger}\;c_{j}\;\rangle\;.

(61)

The related atomic fractions of condensed and uncondensed atoms, respectively,
are

n_{0}\equiv\frac{N_{0}}{N}\;,\qquad n_{1}\equiv\frac{N_{1}}{N}\;.

(62)

Then the condensate parameter can be written as

\eta=\sqrt{\nu n_{0}}

(63)

and the number of uncondensed atoms at a lattice site as

\langle\;c_{j}^{\dagger}\;c_{j}\;\rangle=\nu n_{1}\;.

(64)

Clearly, the number of all atoms at a lattice site is

\langle\;\hat{c}_{j}^{\dagger}\;\hat{c}_{j}\;\rangle=\eta^{2}+\nu n_{1}=\nu\;.

(65)

The total number of atoms

N=\langle\;\hat{N}\;\rangle=\sum_{j}\langle\;\hat{c}_{j}^{\dagger}\;\hat{c}_{j}\;\rangle=N_{0}+N_{1}

(66)

leads to the normalization condition

n_{0}+n_{1}=0\;.

(67)

The grand Hamiltonian of atoms has the form

H_{at}=\hat{H}_{at}-\mu_{0}N_{0}-\mu_{1}\hat{N}_{1}-\hat{\Lambda}\;,

(68)

in which the Lagrange multipliers
\mu_{0}
and
\mu_{1}
guarantee the validity
of normalizations (
59
) and (
61
), and the term

\hat{\Lambda}=\sum_{j}(\lambda_{j}c_{j}^{\dagger}+\lambda^{*}_{j}c_{j})

(69)

respects condition (
56
).

With the Bogolubov shift (
49
) or (
53
), the atomic grand Hamiltonian
becomes

H_{at}=H^{(0)}+H^{(1)}+H^{(2)}+H^{(3)}+H^{(4)}\;.

(70)

Here the first term does not contain the operators of uncondensed atoms,

H^{(0)}=\frac{1}{2}\;N\nu n_{0}^{2}\;(U+\widetilde{U})+Nn_{0}\;(h_{0}-\widetilde{J}-\mu_{0})\;,

(71)

where the notations

\widetilde{U}\equiv\sum_{i(\neq j)}\widetilde{U}_{ij}\;,\qquad\widetilde{J}\equiv\sum_{i(\neq j)}\widetilde{J}_{ij}\;,

(72)

and

h_{0}\equiv\langle\;\frac{{\bf p}_{j}^{2}}{2m}\;\rangle+U_{L}

(73)

are used. Employing condition (
56
), the part linear in
c_{j}
(and
c_{j}^{\dagger}
)
turns into zero,

H^{(1)}=0\;.

(74)

The term containing binary products of the operators
c_{j}
reads as

H^{(2)}=\sum_{i\neq j}(\nu n_{0}\widetilde{U}_{ij}-\widetilde{J}_{ij})\;c_{i}^{\dagger}\;c_{j}+(h_{0}+2\nu n_{0}U+\nu n_{0}\widetilde{U}-\mu_{1})\sum_{j}c_{j}^{\dagger}\;c_{j}\;+

+\;\frac{1}{2}\;\nu n_{0}U\sum_{j}(c_{j}^{\dagger}\;c_{j}^{\dagger}+c_{j}\;c_{j})+\frac{1}{2}\;\nu n_{0}\sum_{i\neq j}\widetilde{U}_{ij}(c_{i}^{\dagger}\;c_{j}^{\dagger}+c_{i}\;c_{j})\;.

(75)

The term with triple products of
c_{j}
is

H^{(3)}=\sqrt{\nu n_{0}}\;U\sum_{j}(c_{j}^{\dagger}\;c_{j}^{\dagger}\;c_{j}+c_{j}^{\dagger}\;c_{j}\;c_{j})+\sqrt{\nu n_{0}}\sum_{i\neq j}(c_{i}^{\dagger}\;c_{j}^{\dagger}\;c_{j}+c_{j}^{\dagger}\;c_{j}\;c_{i}).

(76)

And the last term is

H^{(4)}=\frac{1}{2}\;U\sum_{j}c_{j}^{\dagger}\;c_{j}^{\dagger}\;c_{j}\;c_{j}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i\neq j}\widetilde{U}_{ij}\;c_{i}^{\dagger}\;c_{j}^{\dagger}\;c_{j}\;c_{i}\;.

(77)

5
Momentum representation

To pass to the momentum representation, one has to make the Fourier transformation
for the operators

c_{j}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N_{L}}}\sum_{k}a_{k}e^{i{\bf k}\cdot{\bf a}_{j}}\;,\qquad a_{k}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N_{L}}}\sum_{j}c_{j}e^{-i{\bf k}\cdot{\bf a}_{j}}\;,

(78)

for the effective interaction potential

\widetilde{U}_{ij}=\sum_{k}\widetilde{U}_{k}e^{i{\bf k}\cdot{\bf a}_{ij}}\;,\qquad\widetilde{U}_{k}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i}\widetilde{U}_{ij}e^{-i{\bf k}\cdot{\bf a}_{ij}}\;,

(79)

and for the effective tunneling

\widetilde{J}_{ij}=\sum_{k}\widetilde{J}_{k}e^{i{\bf k}\cdot{\bf a}_{ij}}\;,\qquad\widetilde{J}_{k}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i}\widetilde{J}_{ij}e^{-i{\bf k}\cdot{\bf a}_{ij}}\;.

(80)

Then the term
H^{(0)}
does not change. The term
H^{(2)}
becomes

H^{(2)}=\sum_{k}[\;2(\nu n_{0}\widetilde{U}_{k}-\widetilde{J}_{k})+h_{0}+\nu n_{0}(2U+\widetilde{U})-\mu_{1}\;]a_{k}^{\dagger}a_{k}\;+

+\;\frac{1}{2}\;\nu n_{0}\sum_{k}(U+2\widetilde{U}_{k})(a_{k}^{\dagger}a_{-k}^{\dagger}+a_{-k}a_{k})\;.

(81)

The terms (
76
) and (
77
) read as

H^{(3)}=\sqrt{\frac{\nu n_{0}}{N_{L}}}\;\sum_{kp}(U+2\widetilde{U}_{k})(a_{k}^{\dagger}a_{p}^{\dagger}\;a_{k+p}+a_{k+p}^{\dagger}a_{p}^{\dagger}\;a_{k})\;,

(82)

and, respectively,

H^{(4)}=\frac{1}{2N}\sum_{kpq}(U+2\widetilde{U}_{k})a_{k}^{\dagger}a_{p}^{\dagger}\;a_{k+q}\;a_{p-q}\;.

(83)

Thus the grand Hamiltonian of the system can be represented as

H=H_{at}+\hat{H}_{ph}+E_{N}\;,\qquad H_{at}=H^{(0)}+H^{(2)}+H^{(3)}+H^{(4)}\;.

(84)

The grand thermodynamic potential is

\Omega=-T\ln{\rm Tr}e^{-\beta H}\qquad\left(\beta\equiv\frac{1}{T}\right)\;,

(85)

with
T
being temperature. The condensate chemical potential
\mu_{0}
is defined
by the equation

\frac{\partial\Omega}{\partial N_{0}}=\frac{1}{N}\;\left\langle\;\frac{\partial H}{\partial n_{0}}\;\right\rangle=0\;.

(86)

Recall that

\widetilde{U}_{ij}\equiv\widetilde{U}({\bf a}_{ij})\;,\qquad\widetilde{J}_{ij}\equiv\widetilde{J}({\bf a}_{ij})\;.

(87)

For the case of cubic lattice, in the nearest-neighbor approximation, we have

\widetilde{U}_{k}=\widetilde{U}({\bf a})\sum_{\alpha=1}^{d}\cos(k_{\alpha}a)\;,\qquad\widetilde{U}_{0}=\widetilde{U}({\bf a})d=\lim_{k\rightarrow 0}\widetilde{U}_{k}\;,

\widetilde{J}_{k}=\widetilde{J}({\bf a})\sum_{\alpha=1}^{d}\cos(k_{\alpha}a)\;,\qquad\widetilde{J}_{0}=\widetilde{J}({\bf a})d=\lim_{k\rightarrow 0}\widetilde{J}_{k}\;

(88)

and

\widetilde{U}=\sum_{i(\neq j)}\widetilde{U}({\bf a}_{ij})=z_{0}\widetilde{U}({\bf a})\;,\qquad\widetilde{J}=\sum_{i(\neq j)}\widetilde{J}({\bf a}_{ij})=z_{0}\widetilde{J}({\bf a})\;,

(89)

where
z_{0}=2d
is the number of the nearest neighbors and
{\bf a}
is the lattice
vector connecting the nearest neighbors.

An important quantity characterizing quantum crystal stability is the mean-square
deviation

r_{0}^{2}\equiv\sum_{\alpha=1}^{d}\langle\;u_{j}^{\alpha}\;u_{j}^{\alpha}\;\rangle\;.

(90)

The mechanical stability of a crystal becomes lost when the mean-square deviation
is large, so that atoms cannot be localized at their lattice sites. This is what
is called the Lindemann
[
45
]
criterion of stability. The Lindemann
stability criterion can be written as the inequality

\frac{r_{0}}{a}<\frac{1}{2}\;.

(91)

This criterion becomes broken under phonon instability, when atoms delocalize

[
8
,
9
,
14
,
15
]
.

If the quantum crystal is well localized, the mean-square deviation is small,
such that
r_{0}/a\ll 1
. Then the phonon renormalization of the effective atomic
interaction and of the effective tunneling is small, so that

\widetilde{U}({\bf a})\approx U({\bf a})\;,\qquad\widetilde{J}({\bf a})\approx J({\bf a})\;,

where

U({\bf a})=\int|\;w({\bf r})\;|^{2}\;\Phi({\bf r}-{\bf r}^{\prime})\;|\;w({\bf r}^{\prime}-{\bf a})\;|^{2}\;d{\bf r}d{\bf r}^{\prime}\;,

U\equiv U(0)=\int|\;w({\bf r})\;|^{2}\;\Phi({\bf r}-{\bf r}^{\prime})\;|\;w({\bf r}^{\prime})\;|^{2}\;d{\bf r}d{\bf r}^{\prime}\;,

(92)

and
J({\bf a})
is defined by (
13
). It is important to note that

U({\bf a})<0\;,\qquad J({\bf a})>0\;.

(93)

6
Hartree-Fock-Bogolubov approximation

For the products of the operators
a_{k}
, higher than two, we use the
Hartree-Fock-Bogolubov approximation (HFB) as is explained in Refs.

[
4
,
7
,
42
,
46
]
.

For the fraction of uncondensed atoms, we have

n_{1}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k}n_{k}\;,\qquad n_{k}=\langle\;a_{k}^{\dagger}a_{k}\;\rangle\;,

(94)

and the anomalous average is

\sigma=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k}\sigma_{k}\;,\qquad\sigma_{k}=\langle\;a_{-k}a_{k}\;\rangle\;.

(95)

In equilibrium,
\sigma_{k}
is real.

To simplify the following expressions, we may notice that
n_{k}
and
\sigma_{k}

are maximal when
k\rightarrow 0
. Then we may use the central-peak approximation

[
46
,
47
]
, according to which

\sum_{p}\widetilde{U}_{k+p}n_{p}=\widetilde{U}_{k}\sum_{p}n_{p}\;,\qquad\sum_{p}\widetilde{J}_{k+p}n_{p}=\widetilde{J}_{k}\sum_{p}n_{p}\;,

\sum_{p}\widetilde{U}_{k+p}\sigma_{p}=\widetilde{U}_{k}\sum_{p}\sigma_{p}\;,\qquad\sum_{p}\widetilde{J}_{k+p}\sigma_{p}=\widetilde{J}_{k}\sum_{p}\sigma_{p}\;.

(96)

In that way, we get
H^{(3)}=0
and

H^{(4)}=2\nu n_{1}\sum_{k}(U+\widetilde{U}_{0}+\widetilde{U}_{k})a_{k}^{\dagger}a_{k}+\frac{1}{2}\;\nu\sigma\sum_{k}(U+2\widetilde{U}_{k})(a_{k}^{\dagger}a_{-k}^{\dagger}+a_{-k}a_{k})\;-

-\;\frac{1}{2}\;\nu\sum_{k}[\;2n_{1}(U+\widetilde{U}_{0}+\widetilde{U}_{k})n_{k}+\sigma(U+2\widetilde{U}_{k})\sigma_{k}\;]\;.

(97)

The atomic Hamiltonian (
71
) takes the form

H_{at}=\sum_{k}\omega_{k}a_{k}^{\dagger}a_{k}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k}\Delta(a_{k}^{\dagger}a_{-k}^{\dagger}+a_{-k}a_{k})+E_{HFB}\;,

(98)

in which

\omega_{k}=2\nu U+2\nu\widetilde{U}_{k}+2\nu n_{1}\widetilde{U}_{0}+\nu n_{0}\widetilde{U}-2\widetilde{J}_{k}+h_{0}-\mu_{1}

(99)

and

\Delta_{k}=\nu(U+2\widetilde{U}_{k})(n_{0}+\sigma)\;,

(100)

while the nonoperator term is

E_{HFB}=H^{(0)}-\;\frac{1}{2}\;\nu\sum_{k}[\;2n_{1}(U+\widetilde{U}_{0}+\widetilde{U}_{k})n_{k}+\sigma(U+2\widetilde{U}_{k})\sigma_{k}\;]\;.

(101)

Accomplishing the Bogolubov canonical transformation (see details in

[
4
,
42
,
46
]
), we obtain the atomic Hamiltonian

H_{at}=\sum_{k}\varepsilon_{k}b_{k}^{\dagger}b_{k}+E_{B}\;,

(102)

with the spectrum

\varepsilon_{k}=\sqrt{\omega_{k}^{2}-\Delta_{k}^{2}}

(103)

and the nonoperator term

E_{B}=E_{HFB}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k}(\varepsilon_{k}-\omega_{k})\;.

(104)

Bose condensate exists
[
7
,
29
,
42
]
, provided that

\lim_{k\rightarrow 0}\varepsilon_{k}=0\;,\qquad{\rm Re}\;\varepsilon_{k}\geq 0\;.

(105)

This gives the chemical potential

\mu_{1}=h_{0}+\nu U(1+n_{1}-\sigma)+\nu n_{0}\widetilde{U}-2\widetilde{J}+2\nu\widetilde{U}_{0}(2n_{1}-\sigma)\;.

(106)

Taking this into account results in

\omega_{k}=\Delta_{0}+2(\widetilde{J}_{0}-\widetilde{J}_{k})-2\nu(\widetilde{U}_{0}-\widetilde{U}_{k})\;,\qquad\Delta_{k}=\Delta_{0}-2\nu(\widetilde{U}_{0}-\widetilde{U}_{k})(n_{0}+\sigma)\;,

(107)

where

\Delta_{0}\equiv\lim_{k\rightarrow 0}\Delta_{k}=\nu(U+2\widetilde{U}_{0})(n_{0}+\sigma)\;.

(108)

For a cubic lattice, we get

\omega_{k}=\Delta_{0}+4[\;\widetilde{J}({\bf a})-\nu\widetilde{U}({\bf a})\;]\sum_{\alpha=1}^{d}\sin^{2}\left(\frac{k_{\alpha}a}{2}\right)\;,

\Delta_{k}=\Delta_{0}-4\nu\widetilde{U}({\bf a})(n_{0}+\sigma)\sum_{\alpha=1}^{d}\sin^{2}\left(\frac{k_{\alpha}a}{2}\right)\;,

(109)

which at small momenta reads as

\omega_{k}\simeq\Delta_{0}+[\;\widetilde{J}({\bf a})-\nu\widetilde{U}({\bf a})\;](ka)^{2}\;,\qquad\Delta_{k}\simeq\Delta_{0}-\nu\widetilde{U}({\bf a})(n_{0}+\sigma)(ka)^{2}\;.

(110)

Spectrum (
103
) is

\varepsilon_{k}^{2}=8\left\{\Delta_{0}+2[\;\widetilde{J}({\bf a})-\nu\widetilde{U}({\bf a})(1+n_{0}+\sigma)\;]\;\sum_{\alpha}\sin^{2}\left(\frac{k_{\alpha}a}{2}\right)\right\}\;\times

\times\;[\;\widetilde{J}({\bf a})-\nu\widetilde{U}({\bf a})(1-n_{0}-\sigma)\;]\sum_{\alpha}\sin^{2}\left(\frac{k_{\alpha}a}{2}\right)\;.

(111)

At small momenta, we find the gapless spectrum

\varepsilon_{k}\simeq ck\qquad(k\equiv|\;{\bf k}\;|\rightarrow 0)\;,

(112)

with the sound velocity given by the expression

c^{2}=2\Delta_{0}a^{2}[\;\widetilde{J}({\bf a})-\nu\widetilde{U}({\bf a})(1-n_{0}-\sigma)\;]\;.

(113)

Summation over momenta reduces to the integration over the first Brillouin zone,

\sum_{k}\longmapsto V\int_{\mathbb{B}}\frac{d{\bf k}}{(2\pi)^{d}}\;,

(114)

such that the normalization be valid:

\sum_{k}1=N_{L}\;.

(115)

The latter transforms into

\frac{\nu}{\rho}\int_{\mathbb{B}}\frac{d{\bf k}}{(2\pi)^{d}}=1\qquad\left(\rho\equiv\frac{N}{V}=\frac{\nu}{a^{d}}\right)\;.

(116)

In this way, the fraction of uncondensed atoms, in the HFB approximation, becomes

n_{1}=\frac{1}{\rho}\int_{\mathbb{B}}n_{k}\frac{d{\bf k}}{(2\pi)^{d}}\;,

(117)

with the momentum distribution

n_{k}=\frac{\omega_{k}}{2\varepsilon_{k}}\;\coth\left(\frac{\varepsilon_{k}}{2T}\right)-\;\frac{1}{2}\;.

(118)

The anomalous average takes the form

\sigma=\frac{1}{\rho}\int_{\mathbb{B}}\sigma_{k}\;\frac{d{\bf k}}{(2\pi)^{d}}\;,

(119)

where

\sigma_{k}=-\;\frac{\Delta_{k}}{2\varepsilon_{k}}\;\coth\left(\frac{\varepsilon_{k}}{2T}\right)\;.

(120)

7
Zero temperature

Let us study the case of zero temperature. Setting
T=0
results in

n_{k}=\frac{\omega_{k}}{2\varepsilon_{k}}\;-\;\frac{1}{2}\;,\qquad\sigma_{k}=-\;\frac{\Delta_{k}}{2\varepsilon_{k}}\;.

(121)

In the long-wave limit,

n_{k}\simeq\frac{\Delta_{0}}{2ck}\;,\qquad\sigma_{k}=-\;\frac{\Delta_{0}}{2ck}\qquad(k\rightarrow 0)\;.

(122)

To simplify the consideration, let us use the Debye approximation. Then the
integration over the Brillouin zone is replaced by the integration over the
Debye sphere,

\int_{\mathbb{B}}\frac{d{\bf k}}{(2\pi)^{d}}\;\longmapsto\;\frac{2}{(4\pi)^{d/2}\Gamma(d/2)}\int_{0}^{k_{D}}k^{d-1}\;dk\;,

(123)

with the Debye radius defined by normalization (
116
) that acquires the
form

\frac{2a^{d}}{(4\pi)^{d/2}\Gamma(d/2)}\int_{0}^{k_{D}}k^{d-1}\;dk=1\;.

(124)

This gives

k_{D}=\frac{\sqrt{4\pi}}{a}\;\left[\;\frac{d}{2}\;\Gamma\left(\frac{d}{2}\right)\;\right]^{1/d}\;.

(125)

Then the replacement (
123
) reads as

\int_{\mathbb{B}}\frac{d{\bf k}}{(2\pi)^{d}}\;\longmapsto\;\frac{d}{(k_{D}a)^{d}}\int_{0}^{k_{D}}k^{d-1}\;dk\;.

(126)

The spectrum in the Debye approximation is taken in the long-wave form

\varepsilon_{k}=ck\qquad(0\leq k\leq k_{D})\;,

(127)

being limited by the Debye radius. The spectrum on the Debye sphere is

\varepsilon_{D}\equiv ck_{D}=k_{D}a\left\{2\nu[\;U+2\widetilde{U}({\bf a})(n_{0}+\sigma)\;]\;[\;\widetilde{J}({\bf a})-\nu\widetilde{U}({\bf a})(1-n_{0}-\sigma)\;]\right\}^{1/2}\;.

(128)

Let us introduce the dimensionless quantities

A\equiv\nu\;\frac{U+2\widetilde{U}({\bf a})d}{\varepsilon_{D}}\;(n_{0}+\sigma)\;,

B\equiv\frac{(k_{D}a)^{2}}{\varepsilon_{D}}\;[\;\widetilde{J}({\bf a})-\nu\widetilde{U}({\bf a})\;]\;,\qquad C\equiv-\;\frac{(k_{D}a)^{2}}{\varepsilon_{D}}\;\nu\widetilde{U}({\bf a})(n_{0}+\sigma)\;,

(129)

and the dimensionless momentum

x\equiv\frac{k}{k_{D}}\qquad(0\leq x\leq 1)\;.

(130)

With these notations, we have

n_{k}=\frac{A+Bx^{2}}{2x}\;-\;\frac{1}{2}\;,\qquad\sigma_{k}=-\;\frac{A+Cx^{2}}{2x}\;.

(131)

The fraction of uncondensed atoms takes the form

n_{1}=\frac{d}{2\nu}\;\left(\frac{A}{d-1}+\frac{B}{d+1}\right)-\;\frac{1}{2\nu}

(132)

and the anomalous average becomes

\sigma=-\;\frac{d}{2\nu}\;\left(\frac{A}{d-1}+\frac{C}{d+1}\right).

(133)

Hence the fraction of condensed atoms is

n_{0}=1+\frac{1}{2\nu}\;-\;\frac{d}{2\nu}\;\left(\frac{A}{d-1}+\frac{B}{d+1}\right)\;.

(134)

Under the existence of Bose-Einstein condensate, the sum

n_{0}+\sigma=1+\frac{1}{2\nu}\;-\;\frac{d}{2\nu}\;\left(\frac{2A}{d-1}+\frac{B}{d+1}+\frac{C}{d+1}\right)

becomes nonzero. These expressions show that there can be no condensate in
one-dimensional space, that is, at zero temperature, the condensate can exist only
for
d\geq 2
.

The condensate existence depends on the system parameters. Varying the parameters at
zero temperature can induce a quantum phase transition between a localized state and
a delocalized condensed state. We know well that a localized state of a quantum
crystal can perfectly exist at zero temperature

[
10
,
11
,
12
,
13
,
14
]
. To discover whether
there could happen the quantum phase transition between the Bose-condensed state
and a localized noncondensed state, we need to study whether there can exist a
relation between the system parameters, where the condensate fraction becomes zero.
The condensed state is characterized above, with the condensate fraction being given
by (
134
). At the point of the phase transition,
n_{0}
becomes zero, as a result
of which
\sigma
and
\Delta_{k}
tend to zero. Then energy (
128
) reads as

\varepsilon_{D}=k_{D}a\left\{2\nu U\;[\;\widetilde{J}({\bf a})-\nu\widetilde{U}({\bf a})\;]\;\right\}^{1/2}\;,

(135)

and for the quantities (
129
) we get

A=C=0\;,\qquad B=\frac{k_{D}a}{\sqrt{2\nu U}}\;\sqrt{\widetilde{J}({\bf a})-\nu\widetilde{U}({\bf a})}\;.

(136)

From the other side, setting in (
134
)
n_{0}=0
, we have

B=(1+2\nu)\;\frac{d+1}{d}\;.

(137)

Equating the latter expressions, with the use of the formulas (
72
) and
(
89
), results in the relation

U=\frac{(k_{D}a)^{2}d(\widetilde{J}-\nu\widetilde{U})}{4\nu(1+2\nu)^{2}(d+1)^{2}}\;.

(138)

Here, for different dimensionality, we have

k_{D}a=2\sqrt{\pi}=3.544908\qquad(d=2)\;,

k_{D}a=(6\pi^{2})^{1/3}=3.897777\qquad(d=3)\;.

For optical lattices, with contact-interacting atoms, we have

0<\widetilde{U}<\widetilde{J}
and
U>0
. Hence relation (
138
) can be
satisfied and a point of a quantum phase transition between localized state and
delocalized condensed state can exist.

In the case of a quantum crystal, with sufficiently long-range interactions, we have

\widetilde{U}<0
, since
\widetilde{J}>0
, we get

\widetilde{J}-\nu\widetilde{U}>0
. The term
U
describes self-interaction at
a lattice site. Keeping in mind an effective potential smoothed by a pair correlation
function
[
28
,
30
]
excludes self-interaction, implying
U=0
.
In such a case, relation (
138
) cannot be satisfied, which means that in an ideal
quantum crystal Bose-Einstein condensation cannot occur.

8
Temperature of Bose-Einstein condensation

Bose-Einstein condensation in a periodic structure, for
d>2
, can happen at finite
temperature. When temperature tends to the point of Bose-Einstein condensation,

T\rightarrow T_{c}
, then
n_{0}
,
\sigma
,
\Delta_{k}
, and
\Delta_{0}
all tend to zero, while
the fraction of uncondensed atoms tends to one,
n_{1}\rightarrow 1
. Therefore, at the
transition point

\varepsilon_{k}=\omega_{k}\qquad(T=T_{c})\;,

(139)

and the transition temperature is defined by the equation

\rho=\int_{\mathbb{B}}n_{k}\;\frac{d{\bf k}}{(2\pi)^{d}}\qquad(T=T_{c})\;,

(140)

where

n_{k}=\frac{1}{2}\;\coth\left(\frac{\omega_{k}}{2T_{c}}\right)-\;\frac{1}{2}\;.

(141)

The frequency
\omega_{k}
reads as

\omega_{k}=\frac{4}{z_{0}}\;(\widetilde{J}-\nu\widetilde{U})\sum_{\alpha=1}^{d}\sin^{2}\left(\frac{k_{\alpha}a}{2}\right)\;,

(142)

which in the long-wave limit gives

\omega_{k}\simeq\frac{1}{z_{0}}\;(\widetilde{J}-\nu\widetilde{U})\;(ka)^{2}\qquad(k\rightarrow 0)\;.

(143)

To approximately calculate integral (
140
), let us resort to the Debye
approximation and take into account that the main input from the integral of

\coth x
comes from small
x
. Then we find the condensation temperature

T_{c}=(1+2\nu)\;\frac{d-2}{4d^{2}}\;(k_{D}a)^{2}(\widetilde{J}-\nu\widetilde{U})\;.

(144)

This shows that in two dimensions, Bose-Einstein condensate can exist only at zero
temperature. For
d>2
, taking into account atomic interactions between different
lattice sites diminishes the condensation temperature, if
\widetilde{U}>0
and
increases it, if
\widetilde{U}<0
.

Formula (
144
) has to be understood in the sense that, if Bose-Einstein
condensation can occur in the system, it happens at the given
T_{c}
. But whether the
condensate can really appear depends on other system parameters, as is discussed in
the previous section. If the system parameters prohibit the existence of Bose-Einstein
condensate at low temperatures, formula (
144
) is not applicable. The condensate
existence, requiring that
0<n_{0}<1
, depends on the quantities
\widetilde{J}
and

\widetilde{U}
, describing tunneling and interactions of atoms at different
lattice sites, as well as includes the self-interaction term
U
of atoms at the same
lattice site.

9
Conclusion

A unified description of optical lattices and quantum crystals is developed, which
is applicable to both these limiting cases as well as to intermediate situations.
The possibility of Bose-Einstein condensation is taken into account. The main aim of
the paper is to demonstrate the principal scheme of such a unified description,
because of which simple approximations are employed. In particular, the lattice
structures of an optical lattice and of a quantum crystal are taken as equal.
General formulas are illustrated by means of a cubic lattice. Phonon excitations are
treated in a self-consistent harmonic approximation. Bose condensed state is described
using the self-consistent Hartree-Fock-Bogolubov approximation. Analysis shows that
for optical lattices, taking account of interactions between different lattice sites
increases the condensation temperature, when these interactions are attractive
(
\widetilde{U}<0
) and decreases it when the interactions are repuslive
(
\widetilde{U}>0
). Phonon excitations renormalize the tunneling term and
the intersite interactions, while they do not influence the self-interaction term for
the same lattice site. In the case of an ideal quantum crystal, with sufficiently strong
interactions, guaranteeing the crystal stability, Bose-Einstein condensation seems to be
impossible. More complicated systems, intermediate between typical optical lattices and
quantum crystals, which could be called soft quantum crystals, require a careful
numerical investigation in line with the developed approach.
