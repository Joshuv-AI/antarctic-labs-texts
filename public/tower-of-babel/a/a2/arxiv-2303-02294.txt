Abstract
. In this paper we solve several reverse isoperimetric problems in the class of λ \lambda -convex bodies, i.e., convex bodies whose curvature at each point of their boundary is bounded below by some λ > 0 \lambda>0 . We give an affirmative answer in ℝ 3 \mathbb{R}^{3} to a conjecture due to Borisenko which states that the λ \lambda -convex lens , i.e., the intersection of two balls of radius 1 / λ 1/\lambda , is the unique minimizer of volume among all λ \lambda -convex bodies of given surface area. Also, we prove a reverse inradius inequality : in model spaces of constant curvature and arbitrary dimension, we show that the λ \lambda -convex lens (properly defined in non-zero curvature spaces) has the smallest inscribed ball among all λ \lambda -convex bodies of given surface area. This solves a conjecture due to Bezdek on minimal inradius of isoperimetric ball-polyhedra in ℝ n \mathbb{R}^{n} .



1.
Introduction

1.1.
Classical and reverse isoperimetric problems

What body has the largest volume for a given surface area?

This classical
isoperimetric problem
has been studied, generalized and solved in a great variety of contexts, for example, in the spaces of constant curvature, etc. (see, e.g.,
[
Ro
]
). In the
n
-dimensional Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, for instance, it leads to the following fundamental inequality:

The Classical Isoperimetric Inequality
.

Let
n\geqslant 2
. Let
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a domain with surface area
|\partial K|
and volume
|K|
, and let
B\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a ball. If
|\partial K|=|\partial B|
, then
|K|\leqslant|B|
, and equality holds if and only if
K
is a ball.

At the same time, one may pose the following
reverse isoperimetric problem
:

What body has the smallest volume for a given surface area?

The reverse isoperimetric problem has received a great deal of attention in recent years. Observe that for a given surface area, the
n
-dimensional volume can be arbitrary small (think of a flat pancake, i.e., an oblong and thin domain). Hence, as stated, the reverse isoperimetric problem has a trivial solution. In his celebrated work, Ball
[
Bal1
,
Bal2
]
overcame this issue by restricting the problem to affine classes of convex bodies. In this setting, the problem turns out to be non-trivial, and Ball showed the following:

The Reverse Isoperimetric Inequality due to Ball
.

Let
n\geqslant 2.
Let
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a convex body and
T\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a regular simplex. Then there is an affine image
K^{\prime}
of
K
such that
|\partial K^{\prime}|=|\partial T|
and
|K^{\prime}|\geqslant|T|
.

The equality case in Ball’s reverse isoperimetric inequality was settled later by Barthe
[
Ba
]
. We remark that for the classical isoperimetric problem optimizers are balls, while this is no longer the case for the reverse problem.

1.2.
Reverse isoperimetric problem under curvature constraints

In this paper, we take a different approach and restrict the reverse isoperimetric problem to the class of convex bodies with
uniform curvature constraints
.
Namely, we require that the boundary of a convex body is either not too flat, or not too curved, in the following sense.

Let
K
be a convex body in an
n
-dimensional model space
M^{n}(c)
of constant curvature
c\in\mathbb{R}
,
n\geqslant 2
. We say that
K
is
\lambda
-convex
if there exists
\lambda>0
so that the principal curvatures
k_{i}
,
i\in\{1,\ldots,n-1\}
, with respect to inward pointing normals of the boundary
\partial K
satisfy

k_{i}(p)\geqslant\lambda\quad\text{ for each point }p\in\partial K.

If the boundary of
K
is not smooth, then the bound above is understood in the barrier sense, which we will make precise in Definition
2.1
(see also Figure
2
). In a similar way,
K
is called
\lambda
-concave
if the inequality

\lambda\geqslant k_{i}(p)\geqslant 0

holds at each point
p\in\partial K
and for every
i\in\{1,\ldots,n-1\}
.

It turns out that for
\lambda
-convex and
\lambda
-concave bodies the reverse isoperimetric problem is well-posed and has a non-trivial solution in spaces of constant curvature. In this paper, we will focus on its version in the Euclidean space. Despite the fact that the notions of
\lambda
-concavity and
\lambda
-convexity look dual, the progress of resolving the reverse isoperimetric problem for these classes is not uniform.

For
\lambda
-concave bodies in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, the reverse isoperimetric problem was completely solved by Chernov and the authors in
[
CDT
]
, where the following sharp result was shown as part of a whole range of
reverse quermassintegrals inequalities
:

The Reverse Isoperimetric Inequality for
\lambda
-concave Bodies

(
[
CDT
]
)
.

Let
n\geqslant 2
. Let
K~\subset~\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a
\lambda
-concave body, and let
S\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a
\lambda
-sausage
, i.e., the convex hull of two balls of radius
1/\lambda
. If
|\partial K|=|\partial S|
, then
|K|\geqslant|S|
, and equality holds if and only if
K
is a
\lambda
-sausage.

An alternative proof of this result was given later in
[
SY
]
using different techniques (see also
[
Na
]
for an elegant proof of a weaker version of the inequality).

Much less is known about the reverse isoperimetric problem for
\lambda
-convex bodies in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
n\geqslant 2
. The following conjecture is due to Borisenko (see
[
CDT
]
):

Conjecture

(The Reverse Isoperimetric Inequality for
\lambda
-convex bodies)
.

Let
n\geqslant 2
. Let
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a
\lambda
-convex body, and let
L\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a
\lambda
-convex
lens
, i.e., the intersection of two balls of radius
1/\lambda
(see Figure
1
). If
|\partial K|=|\partial L|
, then
|K|\geqslant|L|
, and equality holds if and only if
K
is a
\lambda
-convex lens.

For
n=2
, the conjecture was confirmed in
[
BD2
]
(see
[
FKV
]
for an alternative proof). Further progress made in
[
BD3
,
Dr1
,
Bor
]
fully resolved an analogous conjecture in all 2-dimensional spaces of constant curvature and their natural generalizations (e.g., Alexandrov spaces of curvature bounded below; see also
[
CFP
]
for a generalization of the inequality in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
with additional assumptions on convexity). For
n\geqslant 3
, partial progress was made so far only in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, where the conjecture was confirmed in a restrictive class of surfaces of revolution
[
Dr3
]
.

In this paper, we completely solve Borisenko’s conjecture in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. Our main result is

Theorem A

(The Reverse Isoperimetric Inequality for
\lambda
-convex bodies in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
)
.

Let
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
be a
\lambda
-convex body and
L\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
be a
\lambda
-convex lens. If
|\partial K|=|\partial L|
, then
|K|\geqslant|L|.
Moreover, equality holds if and only if
K
is a
\lambda
-convex lens.

Figure 1.
The
\lambda
-convex lens (or the UFO-body).

For the proof of Theorem
A
, we use some ideas of Nazarov
[
Naz
]
. Our approach is different from
[
Dr3
]
, where the optimal control theory and Pontryagin’s Maximum Principle was used to solve the corresponding constrained optimization problem. Our proof can be adapted to work also in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, as we illustrate in Appendix
A
. This gives an alternative proof of the result in
[
BD2
]
.

Recall that the
inradius
of a convex body is the radius of the largest ball contained in the body. As a by-product of the proof of Theorem
A
in Section
3
, we establish the inequality
r(K)\geqslant r(L)
between the inradii
r(K)
and
r(L)
of a
\lambda
-convex body
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
and the lens
L\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
satisfying
|\partial K|=|\partial L|
. This is an instance of a
reverse inradius inequality
. As the second main result of the paper (see Section
1.3
), we show that the same inequality holds in all constant curvature spaces of arbitrary dimension. In particular, this gives an indication that Borisenko’s conjecture might be true for
n\geqslant 4
.

We also refer the reader to
[
HTr
,
Ga
]
where some partial progress on the reverse isoperimetric problem under curvature constraints in the non-convex setting was made. (In the non-convex setting, the problem does not always have a well-defined solution. For example, in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, there exists a constant
a>0
so that for any arbitrary large
\ell>0
there is a domain
D
with area
a
such that
\partial D
is a simple closed curve of length
\ell
and curvature
|k|\leqslant 1
.)

1.3.
Reverse inradius inequality for
\lambda
-convex bodies

In the spirit of our discussion in Section
1.1
, one can ask the following question:

What convex body has the largest inradius for a given surface area?

In
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, the answer is a ball, and it is a unique body with such property. This is a classical result that immediately follows from the fact that the surface area of the inscribed ball is no greater than the surface area of the convex body in which it is inscribed. The reverse question

What convex body has the smallest inradius for a given surface area?

is well-defined and has a non-trivial solution for
\lambda
-convex bodies in spaces of constant curvature, while this is not the case for general convex bodies.

For
2
-dimensional
\lambda
-convex bodies in the Euclidean plane, i.e., the space of constant zero curvature, Milka
[
Mi2
]
proved that the
\lambda
-convex lens has the smallest inradius for a given length of the boundary. Much later, this result was generalized in
[
Dr4
]
to arbitrary
\lambda
-convex domains in
2
-dimensional Alexandrov spaces of curvature bounded below, thus closing this question in dimension
2
. Recently, Bezdek
[
Be1
]
showed that among all
\lambda
-convex bodies in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
n\geqslant 2
, the
\lambda
-convex lens has the smallest inradius for a given
volume
. Bezdek conjectured that the same result must be true if one considers
\lambda
-convex bodies of a given surface area
[
Be1
, Conjecture 5]
. We confirm this conjecture by establishing the following sharp and much more general result:

Theorem B

(The Reverse Inradius Inequality for
\lambda
-convex bodies)
.

Let
n\geqslant 2
. Let
K\subset M^{n}(c)
be a
\lambda
-convex body in a model space
M^{n}(c)
of constant curvature
c
, and let
L\subset M^{n}(c)
be a
\lambda
-convex lens. If
|\partial K|=|\partial L|
, then the inradii of the bodies satisfy
r(K)\geqslant r(L)
. Moreover, equality holds if and only if
K
is a
\lambda
-convex lens.

For the definition of a
\lambda
-convex lens in spaces of constant curvature, see Definition
2.1
.

The following result is an immediate corollary of Theorem
B
in the
2
-dimensional space of constant positive curvature that can be obtained using spherical duality (see
[
BD3
, Section 4]
):

Corollary 1.1

(The reverse outer radius inequality in
\mathbb{S}^{2}
)
.

Let
K\subset\mathbb{S}^{2}
be a
\lambda
-concave body and
S\subset\mathbb{S}^{2}
be a
\lambda
-sausage (i.e. the convex hull of two circles of geodesic curvature equal to
\lambda
). If
|K|=|S|
, then the circumradii
R(K),R(S)
of the bodies (i.e. the radii of the smallest geodesic disks containing the bodies) satisfy
R(K)\leqslant R(S)
. Moreover, equality holds if and only if
K
is a
\lambda
-sausage.∎

It is plausible that the same result holds for
\lambda
-concave bodies in spheres of arbitrary dimension, but some additional tools might be required for the proof.

Finally, we would like to point out that the reverse inradius problem in the class of
\lambda
-concave bodies in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
has a simple solution. Indeed,
r(K)\geqslant 1/\lambda
because every
\lambda
-concave body
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
contains a ball of radius
1/\lambda
. This inequality is the best possible and can be attained only by
\lambda
-sausages.

1.4.
Structure of the paper

In Section
2
, we give the definition of
\lambda
-convexity and provide some background on the notion. In Sections
3
and
4
we give the proof of Theorems
A
and
B
, respectively. Using our method, we provide a self-contained proof of the reverse isoperimetric inequality in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
in Appendix
A
. Finally, Appendix
B
contains an asymptotic computation in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
showing that, as
n\to\infty
, the
\lambda
-convex lens has smaller volume for given surface area compared to its most close ‘competitor’, a
\lambda
-convex spindle
(will be defined in Appendix
B
).

2.
Some background

We denote by
M^{n}(c)
a complete simply connected Riemannian manifold of dimension
n\geqslant 2
and of constant sectional curvature equal to
c\in\mathbb{R}
. We call these manifolds
model spaces
. It is well-known that
M^{n}(c)
is either the spherical, Euclidean, or hyperbolic space depending on whether, respectively,
c>0
,
c=0
, or
c<0
.

We will use the notation
|A|
for the
n
-dimensional volume of a measurable set
A
in
M^{n}(c)
. We will use the same notation
|\partial A|
for the
(n-1)
-dimensional volume of the boundary of
A
(provided this volume is well-defined).

As usual, a
convex body
in a model space is a compact, geodesically convex set with non-empty interior.

2.1.
Totally umbilical hypersurfaces in model spaces

For a given
\lambda>0
, let
\mathcal{S}_{\lambda}\subset M^{n}(c)
be a complete totally umbilical hypersurface of constant normal curvature equal to
\lambda
. We will use the notation
\mathcal{B}_{\lambda}
for the convex region bounded by
\mathcal{S}_{\lambda}
. Everywhere below we assume that
\mathcal{B}_{\lambda}
is closed.

The following classification of complete totally umbilical hypersurfaces is well-known (see, e.g.,
[
BZ
, Section 35.2.4]
):

(1)

If
c=0
, i.e., the ambient model space is the Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, then the sets
\mathcal{S}_{\lambda}
and
\mathcal{B}_{\lambda}
are, respectively, spheres and balls of radius
1/\lambda
.

(2)

If
c>0
, i.e.,
M^{n}(c)
is the sphere
\mathbb{S}^{n}(c)
of radius
1/\sqrt{c}
, then
\mathcal{S}_{\lambda}
is a geodesic sphere of radius
1/\sqrt{c}\cdot\cot^{-1}(\lambda/\sqrt{c})
and
\mathcal{B}_{\lambda}
is the corresponding ball.

(3)

The situation is more interesting if
c<0
, i.e., when the model space is the hyperbolic space
\mathbb{H}^{n}(c)
, and it depends on the relation between
c
and
\lambda
:

(a)

If
\lambda>\sqrt{-c}
, then
\mathcal{S}_{\lambda}
is a geodesic sphere of radius
1/\sqrt{-c}\cdot\coth^{-1}(\lambda/\sqrt{-c})
, and
\mathcal{B}_{\lambda}
is the geodesic ball. In this case,
\mathcal{B}_{\lambda}
is compact.

(b)

If
\lambda=\sqrt{-c}
, then
\mathcal{S}_{\lambda}
is a
horosphere
and
\mathcal{B}_{\lambda}
is a
horoball
. In this case,
\mathcal{B}_{\lambda}
is not compact (however, it can be compactified by adding a single point at infinity).

(c)

Finally, if
\lambda<\sqrt{-c}
, then
\mathcal{S}_{\lambda}
is an
equidistant hypersurface
(or
equidistant
for short), i.e., the set of points at a given distance from a totally geodesic hyperplane. By explicit computation, one can see that for each
\mathcal{S}_{\lambda}
there exists a unique hyperplane
\mathcal{H}\subset\mathbb{H}^{n}(c)
such that

\mathcal{S}_{\lambda}=\left\{x\in\mathbb{H}^{n}(c)\colon\dist_{\mathbb{H}^{n}(c)}(x,\mathcal{H})=\frac{1}{2\sqrt{-c}}\log\frac{\sqrt{-c}+\lambda}{\sqrt{-c}-\lambda}\right\}.

We will call
\mathfrak{r}_{\lambda}:=\frac{1}{2\sqrt{-c}}\log\frac{\sqrt{-c}+\lambda}{\sqrt{-c}-\lambda}
the
characteristic distance
for the equidistant
\mathcal{S}_{\lambda}\subset\mathbb{H}^{n}(c)
. In the equidistant case,
\mathcal{B}_{\lambda}
is not compact and there is a cone of directions to infinity within
\mathcal{B}_{\lambda}
.

In the Poincaré unit ball model of
\mathbb{H}^{n}(c)
, the hypersurfaces
\mathcal{S}_{\lambda}
are Euclidean spheres that: lie inside the unit ball if
\lambda>\sqrt{-c}
, touch the unit sphere if
\lambda=\sqrt{-c}
, and intersect the unit sphere at an angle different from
\pi/2
if
\lambda<\sqrt{-c}
. Note that when the angle of intersection tends to
\pi/2
, the corresponding equidistant hypersurface tends to a totally geodesic hyperplane.

2.2.
\lambda
-convex bodies

Fix some
\lambda>0
. Recall that for every totally umbilical hypersurface
\mathcal{S}_{\lambda}
in a model space, we denote by
\mathcal{B}_{\lambda}
the corresponding convex region bounded by
\mathcal{S}_{\lambda}
.

Definition 2.1

(
\lambda
-convex body)
.

A convex body
K\subset M^{n}(c)
is
\lambda
-convex
if for each
p\in\partial K
there exists a neighborhood
U_{p}\subset M^{n}(c)
and a totally umbilical hypersurface
\mathcal{S}_{\lambda}
that passes through
p
in such a way that

U_{p}\cap\partial K\subset\mathcal{B}_{\lambda}

(see Figure
2
).

Figure 2.
K
is a convex body,
\mathcal{S}_{\lambda}
is a totally umbilical hypersurface of constant normal curvature
\lambda>0
, e.g., a sphere.
K
is
\lambda
-convex
if this local picture holds in some neighborhood
U_{p}
around every point
p\in\partial K
. In this case, we say that the principal curvatures of
\partial K
are bounded below by
\lambda
in the
barrier sense
.

For a
\lambda
-convex body
K
, we call
\mathcal{B}_{\lambda}=\mathcal{B}_{\lambda}(p)
a
supporting convex set of curvature
\lambda
(passing through
p\in\partial K
). If
\mathcal{B}_{\lambda}
is a geodesic ball, we will call it a
supporting ball
(and its boundary
\mathcal{S}_{\lambda}
a
supporting sphere
) at the point
p
.

The following classical theorem of Blaschke turns the local condition in Definition
2.1
into a global one. It was first proven in
[
Bla
]
for
\lambda
-convex bodies with smooth boundary in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, and was later extended in
[
Ra
,
BrS
]
for general
\lambda
-convex domains in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and in
[
Ka
,
Mi1
,
Ho
,
BD4
,
Dr5
]
for
\lambda
-convex bodies in other model spaces (and more generally, in some Riemannian manifolds of bounded curvature; see
[
Dr5
]
and references therein).

Theorem 2.2

(Blaschke’s Rolling Theorem)
.

Let
K\subset M^{n}(c)
be a
\lambda
-convex body. Then for every
p\in\partial K
there exists a supporting convex set
\mathcal{B}_{\lambda}(p)
of curvature
\lambda
such that
K\subseteq\mathcal{B}_{\lambda}(p)
. ∎

Finally, let us point out an equivalent definition of
\lambda
-convexity which is, in some sense, dual to Definition
2.1
. The equivalence can be seen using Theorem
2.2
. A convex body
K\subset M^{n}(c)
is said to be
\lambda
-convex if and only if any pair of points in
K
can be connected by the shortest arc of a curve of constant geodesic curvature
\lambda
and this arc lies entirely in
K
. From this point of view,
\lambda
-convexity is a generalization of the standard notion of convexity where we say that a body is convex if and only if the line segment that connects any two points lies entirely inside the body.
It follows that the whole spindle hypersurface, that is the locus of all arcs connecting the two points, and the domain bounded by this hypersurface must lie inside the body (this justifies the name “spindle convexity” used by some authors; see Section
2.3
). Spindle hypersurfaces will appear in our discussion in Appendix
B
.

2.3.
\lambda
-convex polytopes and their properties

In this subsection, we define a special class of
\lambda
-convex bodies.

Definition 2.3

(
\lambda
-convex polytope)
.

A
\lambda
-convex polytope
in
M^{n}(c)
is a
\lambda
-convex body given as the intersection of finitely many convex regions
\mathcal{B}_{\lambda}
. Each
facet
of the
\lambda
-convex polytope is the intersection of the corresponding
\mathcal{B}_{\lambda}
with the boundary of the polytope. A facet is of dimension
n-1
. Two facets are either disjoint, or intersect. In the latter case, their intersection is called a (lower-dimensional)
face
. The faces of dimension
0
and
1
are called
vertices
and
edges
of the polytope, respectively.

Definition 2.4

(
\lambda
-convex lens)
.

A
\lambda
-convex polytope with exactly two facets is called a
\lambda
-convex lens
(or a
UFO body
) (see Figure
1
).

Note that for
n\geqslant 3
, a
\lambda
-convex lens has exactly one
(n-2)
-dimensional face and no faces of dimension less than
n-2
. For
n=2
, there are exactly two
0
-dimensional faces (i.e., the vertices of the
2
-dimensional lens). Each lens is reflection-symmetric with respect to the totally geodesic hyperplane passing through its
(n-2)
-dimensional face(s). In
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, a
\lambda
-convex lens is the intersection of two balls of radius
1/\lambda
.

We would like to point out a direct correspondence between
\lambda
-convex polytopes and
ball-polyhedra
, i.e., bodies that are obtained as intersections of finitely many geodesic balls of a fixed radius. These objects from discrete geometry have been intensively studied, see e.g.,
[
BLNP
]
and references therein. The notion of
\lambda
-convexity in the context of ball-polyhedra appears in a number of papers as “
r
-hyperconvexity”, “spindle convexity”, “ball convexity”, and “
r
-duality”, e.g.,
[
BLNP
,
Be2
,
FKV
,
LNT
]
. However, in our definition of
\lambda
-convexity in the hyperbolic space, we allow for supporting hypersurfaces to be not only balls, but also horospheres or equdistants. The former case is known as an instance of
h
-convexity
. From this point of view, motivated by some questions in geometric probability,
\lambda
-convexity was studied in Riemannian spaces, e.g., in
[
BGR
,
BM
]
.

The following proposition is a direct corollary of Blaschke’s rolling theorem (Theorem
2.2
):

Proposition 2.5

(Approximation by
\lambda
-convex polytopes)
.

Let
K\subset M^{n}(c)
be a
\lambda
-convex body. Then there exists a sequence
(P_{i})_{i=1}^{\infty}
of
\lambda
-convex polytopes such that
K\subseteq...\subseteq P_{2}\subseteq P_{1}
and
\lim\limits_{i\rightarrow\infty}P_{i}=K
in Hausdorff metric.
∎

Observe that an implicit assumption in Definition
2.3
is that the intersection of balls in question is compact. In many cases, this is automatic:

Lemma 2.6

(Compact intersection of balls)
.

Let
\mathcal{B}_{1},\mathcal{B}_{2}\subset M^{n}(c)
be a pair of convex bodies bounded by totally umbilical hypersurfaces of curvature
\lambda
. Assume
\mathcal{B}_{1}
is not contained in
\mathcal{B}_{2}
, and
\mathcal{B}_{2}
is not contained in
\mathcal{B}_{1}
.
If
c\geqslant 0
, or
c<0
and
\lambda\geqslant\sqrt{-c}
, then
\mathcal{B}_{1}\cap\mathcal{B}_{2}
is a compact set.

Proof.

It follows from the classification of totally umbilical hypersurfaces, see Section
2.1
.
∎

However, Lemma
2.6
is false in this generality in the hyperbolic equidistant case, i.e., when
\lambda<\sqrt{-c}
. Since each
\mathcal{B}_{i}
is unbounded and contains a cone of directions to infinity, this causes extra difficulty. Nonetheless, we can still get a compact intersection provided that sufficiently many equidistant balls are taken and that they are sufficiently ‘close’ to each other. More precisely, we have:

Lemma 2.7

(Compact intersection of equidistant balls)
.

Let
B\subset\mathbb{H}^{n}(c)
,
c<0
, be a geodesic ball, and let
\mathcal{B}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{B}_{m}
be a collection of
m
convex regions in
\mathbb{H}^{n}(c)
bounded by equidistants of curvature
\lambda\in(0,\sqrt{-c})
. Assume also that
B\subset\mathcal{B}_{i}
and
\partial B
touches
\partial\mathcal{B}_{i}
at some point
p_{i}
for each
i\in\{1,\ldots,m\}
. Suppose that the radius of
B
is less than the characteristic distance
\mathfrak{r}_{\lambda}
and that the set of touching points
{\bf p}=\{p_{1},\ldots,p_{m}\}
does not lie in any open half-space with respect to any hyperplane passing through the center of
B
. Then

\mathcal{B}_{\bf p}:=\bigcap_{i=1}^{m}\mathcal{B}_{i}

is a compact set.

Remark
.

Lemma
2.7
is false if
r\geqslant\mathfrak{r}_{\lambda}
(compare to Lemma
2.8
(
3
) below).

Proof of Lemma
2.7
.

For the proof, we need an auxiliary construction as follows. Let
\mathcal{H}\subset\mathbb{H}^{n}(c)
be a hyperplane passing through the center of
B
. Denote by
S=S(\mathcal{H}):=\partial B\cap\mathcal{H}
the corresponding great sphere. For
p\in S
, let
\mathcal{B}_{\lambda}(p)\supset B
be the convex region bounded by the equidistant of curvature
\lambda
that touches the ball
B
at
p
. Let
r
be the radius of
B
. The assumption
r<\mathfrak{r}_{\lambda}
is equivalent to the fact that

\mathcal{Y}:=\bigcap_{p\in S}\mathcal{B}_{\lambda}(p)

is a compact (convex) body. Equivalently, this means that for each such great sphere
S
, one can find a point, say
q=q(S)\in\mathbb{H}^{n}(c)
, such that each equidistant arc of curvature
\lambda
joining
q
with a point
q^{\prime}\in S
is perpendicular to
\mathcal{H}
at
q^{\prime}
. By symmetry, the point
q
lies on the geodesic line
\gamma
passing through the center of
B
perpendicular to
\mathcal{H}
. Moreover, there are exactly two such points, one per each half-space with respect to
\mathcal{H}
. Call these points
q^{+}=q^{+}(S)
and
q^{-}=q^{-}(S)
. Now, for a point
p\in\mathbb{H}^{n}(c)
, define
\mathcal{X}(B,p)
to be the intersection of all convex sets of the form
\mathcal{B}_{\lambda}
containing both
B
and
p
. In this notation,

\mathcal{X}(B,q^{+})=\mathcal{X}(B,q^{-})=\mathcal{Y}.

In general,
\mathcal{X}(B,p)
is not necessarily compact. However, since
\mathcal{X}(B,q^{\pm})
are compact, for every point
p
sufficiently close in the hyperbolic metric to either
q^{+}
or
q^{-}
, the set
\mathcal{X}(B,p)
is also compact. Moreover, if, additionally,
p
lies on the geodesic
\gamma
outside of the segment
[q^{-},q^{+}]
and closer to, say
q^{+}
, then both
\partial\mathcal{X}(B,p)\cap\partial B
and
q^{-}
are contained in the same open half-space with respect to
\mathcal{H}
. By symmetry, the intersection
\partial\mathcal{X}(B,p)\cap\partial B
lies in some hyperplane
\mathcal{I}(p)
. Let
\mathcal{I}^{+}(p)
be the open half-space with respect to
\mathcal{I}(p)
that contains
q^{+}
. Finally, set

\mathcal{Y}^{+}(p):=B\cup\left(\mathcal{X}(B,p)\cap\mathcal{I}^{+}(p)\right).

Let us return back to the proof of the lemma. Assume the contrary, and let
\gamma\subset\mathcal{B}_{\bf p}
be a geodesic ray that starts at the center of
B
and goes to infinity. Let
\mathcal{H}_{\gamma}
be the hyperplane passing through the center of
B
perpendicular to
\gamma
, and let
S_{\gamma}:=S(\mathcal{H}_{\gamma})
be the corresponding great sphere. Since
B\subset\mathcal{B}_{\bf p}
, Blaschke’s rolling theorem (Theorem
2.2
) guarantees that each equidistant arc of curvature
\lambda
that joins a point in
\gamma
with a point in
\partial B
, lies in
\mathcal{B}_{\bf p}
. In particular,
\mathcal{Y}^{+}(p)\subset\mathcal{B}_{\bf p}
for some
p\in\gamma
sufficiently close to
q^{+}(S_{\gamma})\in\gamma
. But
S_{\gamma}
lies in the interior of
\mathcal{Y}^{+}(p)
by construction. This is a contradiction since the open half-space with respect to
\mathcal{H}_{\gamma}
that contains
p
does not contain points in
{\bf p}
.
∎

2.4.
Inscribed balls and their properties

Let
K\subset M^{n}(c)
be a convex body. A geodesic ball of the largest possible radius contained in
K
is called an
inscribed ball for
K
. The
inradius of
K
, denoted by
r(K)
, is the radius of an inscribed ball for
K
. Note that, even though there might be several inscribed balls for
K
(even when
K
is strictly convex), they must have equal radii. Thus,
r(K)
is well-defined. The question of uniqueness is addressed in the following lemma:

Lemma 2.8

(On uniqueness of inscribed balls)
.

Let
K\subset M^{n}(c)
be a strictly convex body.

(1)

If
c\geqslant 0
, then
K
has a unique inscribed ball;

(2)

If
c<0
and
K
is
\lambda
-convex with
\lambda\geqslant\sqrt{-c}
, then
K
has a unique inscribed ball;

(3)

For each
c<0
and
\lambda^{\prime}\in(0,\sqrt{-c})
, there is a
\lambda^{\prime}
-convex body
K
such that
K
has exactly two inscribed balls; in particular, in the hyperbolic space, a
\lambda
-convex body can have several inscribed balls.

Proof.

The argument in the Euclidean space is well-known and goes, for example, as follows. If there are two distinct balls
B_{1},B_{2}\subset K\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
, necessarily of the same radius
r:=r(K)
, then the convex hull
H
of
B_{1}
and
B_{2}
lies entirely in
K
. Moreover, since
K
is strictly convex, any ball
B\subset H
of radius
r
different from
B_{1}
and
B_{2}
must lie in the interior of
K
. Hence, one can enlarge
B
(keeping its center the same) so that the larger ball still lies in
K
. This contradicts the maximality of
r
.

We can reinterpret this argument so that it also works in
\mathbb{S}^{n}
. Again, by contradiction, we assume that there are two different inscribed balls
B_{1}
and
B_{2}
for
K\subset\mathbb{S}^{n}
.

Let
H^{\prime}
be the locus of balls of radius
r
centered on the geodesic segment
I
connecting the centers of
B_{1}
and
B_{2}
. Equivalently,
H^{\prime}
is the set of points at distance at most
r
from
I
. In the Euclidean space,
H^{\prime}=H
. In the spherical case, we have that
H^{\prime}\subsetneq H\subset K
, and hence the argument above applied to
H^{\prime}
yields that a strictly convex body
K\subset\mathbb{S}^{n}
has the unique inscribed ball. This concludes (
1
).

Now we consider the hyperbolic case. For simplicity, assume
c=-1
and write
\mathbb{H}^{n}:=\mathbb{H}^{n}(-1)
. The inclusion
H^{\prime}\subsetneq H
does not hold in
\mathbb{H}^{n}
. Observe, however, that
\partial H^{\prime}\setminus(\partial B_{1}\cup\partial B_{2})
is
\mu
-convex, with
\mu=\tanh(2r)\in(0,1)
(i.e.,
r=\mathfrak{r}_{\mu}
, see (
3c
) in Section
2.1
). If we are in the assumption of case (
2
), then
\mu<\lambda
. By Blaschke’s rolling theorem it follows that
H^{\prime}\subsetneq K
, and hence we can reach a contradiction in the same way as in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\mathbb{S}^{n}
. This concludes (
2
).

Figure 3.
In the Poincaré disk model of
\mathbb{H}^{2}
: the body
H^{\prime\prime}
is the concatenation of two circular arcs on the boundary of disks
B_{1}
and
B_{2}
(in blue), and two green arcs of curves
\mathcal{S}_{\lambda^{\prime}}
of constant curvature
\lambda^{\prime}\in(0,1)
(in green). The outer parallel set
H^{\prime}
of the segment
I
is the concatenation of two orange arcs
\mathcal{S}_{\lambda}
and two blue circular arcs on the boundary of the disks. For comparison, the disk centered at the origin has the same radius as
B_{1}
and
B_{2}
. In this example,
B_{1}
and
B_{2}
are the only inscribed disks for
H^{\prime\prime}
.

In general, there is no guarantee that
H^{\prime}\subset K
. Also,
H^{\prime}
provides an example of a
\mu
-convex set, with
\mu\in(0,1)
, for which all balls of radius
r
inside
H^{\prime}
are inscribed, and there is a continuous family of such balls. To conclude (
3
), one can modify this example as follows (see Figure
3
for a construction in
\mathbb{H}^{2}
). Given
\lambda^{\prime}\in(0,1)
, pick a geodesic segment
I\subset\mathbb{H}^{n}
and let
H^{\prime}
be defined as above for
r>\mathfrak{r}_{\lambda^{\prime}}
. Consider all possible arcs of curves of curvature
\lambda^{\prime}
joining points in
B_{1}
and
B_{2}
. These arcs will sweep out a
\lambda^{\prime}
-convex body
H^{\prime\prime}
for which
B_{1}
and
B_{2}
are the only inscribed balls.
∎

The following proposition gives a necessary and some sufficient conditions under which a ball inside a convex body is an inscribed ball. Parts of this proposition are well-known (and are not the most general) and we outline the proof for completeness of exposition.

Proposition 2.9

(Necessary and sufficient conditions for inscribed balls)
.

Let
K\subset M^{n}(c)
be a convex body, and let
B\subset K
be a geodesic ball centered at some point
o\in K
.

(1)

If
B
is an inscribed ball for
K
, then
\partial B\cap\partial K
is not contained in any open half-space with respect to a hyperplane passing through
o
.

(2)

If
K
is a
\lambda
-convex polytope,
\partial B\cap\partial K
is not contained in any open half-space with respect to a hyperplane passing through
o
, and
B
touches all facets of
K
, then
B
is an inscribed ball for
K
. Moreover, it is unique.

Proof.

We start with part (
1
). Towards a contradiction, suppose that
S:=\partial B\cap\partial K
lies in some open half space
\mathcal{H}^{+}
. Write
\mathcal{H}:=\partial\mathcal{H}^{+}
,
o\in\mathcal{H}
. Let
\mathcal{H}^{-}:=M^{n}(c)\setminus\mathcal{H}^{+}
be the complement of
\mathcal{H}^{+}
. Pick a small
\varepsilon>0
, and consider a geodesic segment
I_{\varepsilon}\subset\mathcal{H}^{-}
of length
\varepsilon
, starting at
o
and perpendicular to
\mathcal{H}
. Let
r=r(K)
be the radius of
B
. Define
H^{\prime}_{\varepsilon}
to be the locus of balls of radius
r=r(K)
centered at
I_{\varepsilon}
. Since
B
and
K
are compact and
S
is contained in
\mathcal{H}^{+}
, the distance

\dist_{M^{n}(c)}\left(\partial K,\mathcal{H}^{-}\cap B\right)

is strictly positive. Therefore, there exists
\varepsilon
such that
H^{\prime}_{\varepsilon}\subset K
. To reach a contradiction, we can apply the argument that is similar to the one that was used in the proof of Lemma
2.8
(
1
).

Now we deal with part (
2
). We will give an argument in the hyperbolic case; in other cases, the argument is similar and we omit it.

Assume first that
\partial B\cap\partial K
contains at least
n+1
distinct points
p_{1},\ldots,p_{n+1}
such that the smallest totally geodesic subspace containing this set of points is equal to
M^{n}(c)
. By assumption, each
p_{i}
is the tangent point of
B
and some of the facets. In the Poincaré disk model of
\mathbb{H}^{n}(c)
, each
\mathcal{S}_{\lambda}(p_{i})
is a Euclidean sphere. Furthermore,
\partial B
is a Euclidean sphere too. But in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, there exists only one sphere that lies in the interior and touches any given collection of
n+1
mutually distinct spheres. If
B^{\prime}
is the inscribed ball for
K
, then
\partial B^{\prime}\cap\partial K
is not contained in any open half space with respect to the origin (part (
1
)). By the uniqueness described above,
B=B^{\prime}
.

In general, let
1\leqslant m<n
be the smallest dimension of a totally geodesic subspace
Y\subset M^{n}(c)
such that
o\in Y
and
\partial B\cap\partial K\subset Y
. Since
\partial B\cap\partial K
is not contained in any open half-space with respect to a hyperplane passing through
o
, the body
K
must be symmetric with respect to
Y
. Hence, any inscribed ball for
K
is also symmetric with respect to
Y
. Note that within
Y
, the points in
\partial B\cap\partial K
cannot lie in any open half-space of
Y
with respect to a plane of dimension
m-1
passing through
o
. By minimality of
m
, we can choose at least
m+1
distinct points
p_{1},\ldots,p_{m+1}
in
\partial B\cap\partial K
so that the geodesic rays
op_{1},\ldots,op_{m+1}
span
Y
. We can now apply a similar uniqueness argument as above. For every
m<n
, there exists a unique Euclidean sphere in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
that lies inside and touches any
m
given Euclidean spheres, and is symmetric with respect to an
m
-dimensional Euclidean plane (we can assume that
o
is the center of the unit ball model). The proposition now follows in the same way as in the case of
n+1
distinct points.
∎

The following proposition relates the extreme properties of the inradius to the structure of the boundary of a
\lambda
-convex body.

Proposition 2.10

(Extreme properties of the inradius)
.

Let
K\subset M^{n}(c)
be a
\lambda
-convex body. (In the case
c<0
and
\lambda<\sqrt{-c}
, we additionally assume that
r(K)<\mathfrak{r}_{\lambda}
.)

If
K\subset M^{n}(c)
is not a
\lambda
-convex polytope, then one can find a
\lambda
-convex polytope
K^{\prime}
such that

(2.1)

r(K^{\prime})=r(K)\quad\text{ and }\quad|\partial K^{\prime}|>|\partial K|.

Equivalently, there exists a
\lambda
-convex polytope
K^{\prime\prime}
such that

(2.2)

r(K^{\prime\prime})<r(K)\quad\text{ and }\quad|\partial K^{\prime\prime}|=|\partial K|.

Proof.

We start with (
2.1
).

Let
B
be an inscribed ball for
K
, and let
o
be its center. By Proposition
2.9
(
1
),
\partial B\cap\partial K
is not contained in any open half space with
o
on its boundary. Pick a subset
T\subset\partial B\cap\partial K
consisting of at most
n+1
points that have the same property, i.e., they do not lie in any open half space with respect to hyperplanes through
o
. Since
K
is
\lambda
-convex, for each
p\in T
there exists a supporting convex set
\mathcal{B}_{\lambda}(p)
of curvature
\lambda
passing through
p
. Define the following intersection:

K^{\prime}=\bigcap_{p\in T}\mathcal{B}_{\lambda}(p).

By Lemmas
2.6
and
2.7
,
K^{\prime}
is a
\lambda
-convex polytope. By Blaschke’s rolling theorem (Theorem
2.2
),
K^{\prime}\supset K
. Hence,
|\partial K^{\prime}|>|\partial K|
. This inequality is strict because
K
is not a
\lambda
-convex polytope by assumption. Furthermore,
B\subset K^{\prime}
and
T\subset\partial B
by construction. By part (
2
) of Proposition
2.9
,
B
is the inscribed ball for
K^{\prime}
. Therefore,
r(K^{\prime})=r(K)
. This concludes (
2.1
).

To prove (
2.2
), we use (
2.1
) and a monotonicity argument. Indeed, let
K^{\prime}
be the polytope that satisfies (
2.1
), and let
B
be its inscribed ball of radius
r=r(K)
. For
s\leqslant r
, let
B^{\prime}
be a ball of radius
s
concentric with
B
. For each
p\in T
, let
p^{\prime}
be the intersection of the geodesic ray
op
with
\partial B^{\prime}
. Let
T^{\prime}
be the set of all such
p^{\prime}
’s. For each
p^{\prime}
, let
\mathcal{S}_{\lambda}(p^{\prime})
be the
\lambda
-convex hypersurface that touches
B^{\prime}
at
p^{\prime}
in such a way that
\mathcal{B}_{\lambda}(p^{\prime})\supset B^{\prime}
. Finally, define

K^{\prime\prime}(s):=\bigcap_{p^{\prime}\in T^{\prime}}\mathcal{B}_{\lambda}(p^{\prime}).

Since
s\leqslant r
,
K^{\prime\prime}(s)
is a
\lambda
-convex polytope. By Proposition
2.9
(
2
),
r\left(K^{\prime\prime}(s)\right)=s
. By construction,
K^{\prime\prime}(r)=K^{\prime}
, and hence by assumption,
|\partial K^{\prime\prime}(r)|>|\partial K|
. By continuity, there exists
s_{0}<r
such that
|\partial K^{\prime\prime}(s_{0})|=|\partial K|
, and the proposition follows with
K^{\prime\prime}=K^{\prime\prime}(s_{0})
.
∎

Remark
.

We will use a similar monotonicity argument in the proof of Theorem
B
.

2.5.
Inner parallel bodies in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and their properties

Let
A
be a convex body in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. The
inner parallel body
at distance
t\geqslant 0
is defined as

A_{t}=A-t{\bm{B}}:=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:x+t{\bm{B}}\subset A\},

where
{\bm{B}}
is the unit Euclidean ball. The greatest number
t
for which
A_{t}
is not empty is the inradius
r(A)
of
A
. Observe that if
A\subset A^{\prime}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
are two convex bodies, then
A_{t}\subset A^{\prime}_{t}
.

The Hausdorff distance of convex bodies
A,A^{\prime}
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
is defined as

d_{H}(A,A^{\prime})=\min\{\mu\geqslant 0:\ A\subset A^{\prime}+\mu{\bm{B}},\ A^{\prime}\subset A+\mu{\bm{B}}\}.

For a convex body
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
0\leqslant\varepsilon\leqslant r(A)
, define

(2.3)

T_{A}(\varepsilon):=\min\left\{\mu\geqslant 0:A-\varepsilon{\bm{B}}+\mu{\bm{B}}\supset A\right\}.

Clearly,
T_{A}(\varepsilon)\geqslant\varepsilon
, but this inequality can be strict. Observe that
T_{A}(\varepsilon)=d_{H}(A,A_{\varepsilon})
.

Lemma 2.11

(Dependence on
\varepsilon
)
.

T_{A}(\varepsilon)\to 0\quad\text{as}\quad\varepsilon\searrow 0.

Proof.

For monotonically decreasing
\varepsilon
, the bodies in the sequence
A_{\varepsilon}=A-\varepsilon{\bm{B}}
are nested, i.e.,
A_{\varepsilon}\subsetneq A_{\varepsilon^{\prime}}
if
\varepsilon>\varepsilon^{\prime}
. Hence, by Blaschke’s Selection Theorem
[
Sch
, p. 63]
, there exists the Hausdorff limit of this sequence and it is equal to the convex body

A^{\prime}:=\Cl\left(\bigcup_{\varepsilon>0}A_{\varepsilon}\right),

where
\Cl
denotes the closure of a set. Note that the interior of
A^{\prime}
, denoted by
\inter A^{\prime}
, is equal to
\bigcup_{\varepsilon>0}A_{\varepsilon}
. Finally, observe that for each
x\in\inter A
there exists
\delta=\delta(x)
(which can be arbitrary small) so that
x+\delta{\bm{B}}\subset A
. For example,
\delta
can be chosen to be the half of the distance of
x
to
\partial A
. By definition of inner parallel bodies,
x\in A_{\delta}
. Thus,
x\in\inter A^{\prime}
for every
x\in\inter A
, which implies that
\inter A^{\prime}=\inter A
, and hence
A=A^{\prime}
. By construction,
d_{H}(A^{\prime},A_{\varepsilon})\to 0
as
\varepsilon\to 0
. Thus,
T_{A}(\varepsilon)=d_{H}(A,A_{\varepsilon})\to 0
as
\varepsilon\to 0
, which is the claim of the lemma.
∎

Lemma 2.12

(Dependence on
A
)
.

Let
A
and
A^{\prime}
be convex bodies in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that
A^{\prime}\supset A
. Then

T_{A^{\prime}}(\varepsilon)\leqslant T_{A}(\varepsilon)+d_{H}(A,A^{\prime}).

Proof.

We have the following implications:

\displaystyle A^{\prime}\supset A\ \Rightarrow\ A^{\prime}-\varepsilon{\bm{B}}\supset A-\varepsilon{\bm{B}}\ \Rightarrow\ A^{\prime}-\varepsilon{\bm{B}}+T_{A}(\varepsilon){\bm{B}}\supset A-\varepsilon{\bm{B}}+T_{A}(\varepsilon){\bm{B}}\supset A,

where the last inclusion follows by (
2.3
). Using the definition of the Hausdorff distance, we obtain

\displaystyle A^{\prime}-\varepsilon{\bm{B}}+T_{A}(\varepsilon){\bm{B}}\supset A\ \Rightarrow\ A^{\prime}-\varepsilon{\bm{B}}+T_{A}(\varepsilon){\bm{B}}+d_{H}(A,A^{\prime}){\bm{B}}\supset A+d_{H}(A,A^{\prime}){\bm{B}}\supset A^{\prime}.

Therefore,
A^{\prime}-\varepsilon{\bm{B}}+\left(T_{A}(\varepsilon)+d_{H}(A,A^{\prime})\right){\bm{B}}\supset A^{\prime}
, and hence
T_{A^{\prime}}(\varepsilon)\leqslant T_{A}(\varepsilon)+d_{H}(A,A^{\prime})
, as desired.
∎

Lemma 2.13

(Hausdorff limits of nested sequences)
.

If
A^{1}\supset A^{2}\supset...\supset C
is a nested sequence of convex bodies, then

\mathcal{A}:=\bigcap_{j=1}^{\infty}A^{j}

is a convex body and

d_{H}\left(A^{j},\mathcal{A}\right)\to 0\quad\text{ as }\quad j\to\infty.

Proof.

It is immediate that
\mathcal{A}
is a closed convex set. Since each
A^{j}
contains the convex body
C
, the interior of
\mathcal{A}
is not empty. Hence,
\mathcal{A}
is a convex body. The convergence follows by
[
Sch
, Lemma 1.8.2]
.
∎

Using the three lemmas above, we establish the following auxiliary result.

Lemma 2.14

(Approximation descends to inner parallel bodies)
.

If
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a convex body, and
(A^{j})_{j=1}^{\infty}
is a sequence of convex bodies such that

A^{j}\supset A^{j+1}\supset A\quad\text{ and }\quad\lim_{j\to\infty}d_{H}\left(A,A^{j}\right)=0,

then for the inner parallel bodies at distance
0\leqslant t<r(A)
, we have

\lim_{j\to\infty}d_{H}\left(A_{t},A_{t}^{j}\right)=0.

Proof.

Since
(A^{j})_{j=1}^{\infty}
is a nested sequence that approximates
A
, there is a monotone sequence
\varepsilon_{j}\searrow 0
such that

d_{H}(A,A^{j})=\varepsilon_{j}.

Hence,

A+\varepsilon_{j}{\bm{B}}\supset A^{j}\ \ \Rightarrow\ \ A\supset A^{j}-\varepsilon_{j}{\bm{B}}\ \ \Rightarrow\ \ A-t{\bm{B}}\supset A^{j}-\varepsilon_{j}{\bm{B}}-t{\bm{B}}.

In our notation, the last inclusion also reads as
A_{t}\supset A_{t}^{j}-\varepsilon_{j}{\bm{B}}
. Since by (
2.3
)

A_{t}^{j}-\varepsilon_{j}{\bm{B}}+T_{A_{t}^{j}}(\varepsilon_{j}){\bm{B}}\supset A_{t}^{j},

we conclude that

(2.4)

A_{t}+T_{A_{t}^{j}}(\varepsilon_{j}){\bm{B}}\supset A_{t}^{j}.

By assumption of the lemma,
A^{j}\supset A
, so
A^{j}_{t}\supset A_{t}
. Hence, (
2.4
) implies that

d_{H}\left(A_{t},A_{t}^{j}\right)\leqslant T_{A_{t}^{j}}(\varepsilon_{j}).

It remains to show that
T_{A_{t}^{j}}(\varepsilon_{j})\to 0
as
j\to\infty
.

Since the sequence
\left(A^{j}\right)_{j=1}^{\infty}
is decreasing, so is the sequence
\left(A^{j}_{t}\right)_{j=1}^{\infty}
. Moreover, each
A^{j}_{t}
contains
A_{t}
. The latter set is a (non-empty) convex body by our choice of
0\leqslant t<r(A)
. Hence, by Lemma
2.13
,

\mathcal{A}:=\bigcap_{j=1}^{\infty}A_{t}^{j}

is a convex body and is the Hausdorff limit of
A_{t}^{j}
as
j\rightarrow\infty
. Since
A_{t}^{j}\supset\mathcal{A}
, Lemma
2.12
guarantees that

T_{A_{t}^{j}}(\varepsilon_{j})\leqslant T_{\mathcal{A}}(\varepsilon_{j})+d_{H}(\mathcal{A},A_{t}^{j})

for each
j\geqslant 1
.
By Lemma
2.13
,

d_{H}(\mathcal{A},A_{t}^{j})\to 0\quad\text{ as }\quad j\to\infty.

Since
\varepsilon_{j}\searrow 0
as
j\to\infty
, Lemma
2.11
guarantees that

T_{\mathcal{A}}(\varepsilon_{j})\to 0\quad\text{ as }\quad j\to\infty.

Combining everything together, we get that
\lim\limits_{j\to\infty}T_{A_{t}^{j}}(\varepsilon_{j})=0
. This concludes the proof.

Proof of the reverse isoperimetric inequality in

(Theorem

The proof breaks into two steps. In the first step, we establish the theorem when

Proposition 3.1

(Areas of inner parallel bodies are smaller for lenses)

(Area of small triangles)

Proof.

By the triangle inequality,

For the normal coordinates centered at

Here,

Step II: General case

-polygons and proved the inequality for arbitrary
1
-convex bodies in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. Let us now conclude the equality case for general bodies.

Assume that
\partial K
has no vertices. It will be clear how to adopt the proof to the case when
\partial K
has vertices (in some sense, they only help).

Let
K^{j}
be a sequence of
1
-convex polygons approximating
K
, i.e.,
K^{j}\to K
as
j\rightarrow\infty
in Hausdorff metric. Let
L^{j}
be the corresponding sequence of
1
-convex lenses so that

|\partial K^{j}|=|\partial L^{j}|\quad\text{for every }j.

By continuity, it follows that
L^{j}\to L
, where
L
is the lens such that
|\partial K|=|\partial L|
.

Let
\gamma_{i}^{j}
be the angles of
K^{j}
,
\gamma_{*}^{j}
be the angle of
L^{j}
, and denote
\alpha^{j}:=\max\limits_{i}\gamma_{i}^{j}
. Since
\partial K
is smooth, we can choose an approximating sequence so that

\alpha^{j}\searrow 0.

We also have

\gamma_{*}^{j}\nearrow\gamma_{*}.

(Note that
\alpha^{j}\leqslant\gamma_{*}^{j}
.) Because of our choice of sequences, we can find
N>0
so that

(A.6)

\alpha^{j}<\frac{\gamma_{*}}{3}<\frac{2\gamma_{*}}{3}<\gamma_{*}^{j}\quad\text{ for all }\quad j\geqslant N.

We claim that for each
j\geqslant N
,

(A.7)

\left.\frac{d}{dt}\right|_{t=0^{+}}|\partial K^{j}_{t}|\geqslant\left.\frac{d}{dt}\right|_{t=0^{+}}|\partial L^{j}_{t}|+C,

where
C=C(\gamma_{*})>0
depends only on
\gamma_{*}
.
First observe that a more detailed analysis of (
A.5
) gives the following bound:

\left.\frac{d}{dt}\right|_{t=0^{+}}|\partial K^{j}_{t}|\geqslant\left.\frac{d}{dt}\right|_{t=0^{+}}|\partial L^{j}_{t}|+\mathcal{E}_{j},

where

\mathcal{E}_{j}=\gamma_{*}^{j}\cdot\left(\frac{\tan\frac{\gamma_{*}^{j}}{2}}{\frac{\gamma_{*}^{j}}{2}}-\frac{\tan\frac{\alpha^{j}}{2}}{\frac{\alpha^{j}}{2}}\right)

By the Mean Value Theorem applied to
f(x):=\tan(x)/x
and using (
A.6
), for some
\xi\in[\alpha^{j}/2,\gamma_{*}^{j}/2]
, we have the following uniform estimate for
j\geqslant N
:

\mathcal{E}_{j}=\gamma^{j}_{*}\cdot f^{\prime}(\xi)\cdot\frac{\gamma_{*}^{j}-\alpha^{j}}{2}\geqslant\frac{1}{9}(\gamma_{*})^{2}\cdot f^{\prime}(\xi).

The value
f^{\prime}(\xi)
coming from the Mean Value Theorem has its minimum on
[0,\gamma_{*}]
for our choice of
N
. This minimum is strictly positive and depends only on
\gamma_{*}
. Hence, again for our choice (
A.6
), there exists
C>0
, depending only on
\gamma_{*}
, such that

\mathcal{E}_{j}\geqslant C\quad\text{for all}\quad j\geqslant N.

This translates into the assertion of the claim.

Passing to the limit in (
A.7
), as
C
does not depend on
j
, we obtain that

\left.\frac{d}{dt}\right|_{t=0^{+}}|\partial K_{t}|\geqslant\left.\frac{d}{dt}\right|_{t=0^{+}}|\partial L_{t}|+C.

Therefore,
|K|>|L|
, which proves
strict
inequality for
1
-convex bodies with smooth boundaries. If
\partial K
has vertices, then we can choose an approximating sequence of
1
-convex polygons that does not change the vertices. Repeating the argument of the claim above, we will get a strict inequality unless
K
is a
1
-convex lens. This concludes the proof of Theorem
A.1
in the general case.
∎

Remark
.

The argument as in Step II of the proof of Theorem
A
can be also applied in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
to study the equality case. Above, we presented a different proof in order to illustrate another approach of how one can control inequalities under limits.

Remark
.

In
\mathbb{R}^{2}
and
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, it is possible to explicitly compute the volume of a
\lambda
-convex lens as a function of its surface area.
Using these explicit formulas, we obtain the following reverse isoperimetric inequalities (RIP) as corollaries to Theorem
A
and Theorem
A.1
:

Corollary A.2

(Explicit RIP in dimension 2)
.

If
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
is a
\lambda
-convex body, then

\quad|K|\geqslant\frac{|\partial K|}{2\lambda}-\frac{1}{\lambda^{2}}\sin\left(\frac{|\partial K|\lambda}{2}\right),

and equality holds if and only if
K
is a
\lambda
-convex lens.
∎

Corollary A.3

(Explicit RIP in dimension 3)
.

If
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
is a
\lambda
-convex body, then

\quad|K|\geqslant\frac{|\partial K|^{2}\lambda}{96\pi^{2}}\cdot\left(12\pi-|\partial K|\lambda^{2}\right),

and equality holds if and only if
K
is a
\lambda
-convex lens.
∎

Appendix B
Lenses vs. spindles, an asymptotic competition

Here we discuss an extra evidence in support of the Borisenko conjecture. By Theorem
A.1
, a
\lambda
-convex lens is the solution for the
2
-dimensional reverse isoperimetric problem in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. If one would try to conjecture a potential solution for dimension
n\geqslant 3
, a natural candidate would be a body obtained by rotating this lens. However, there are two ways one can rotate it in order to preserve convexity. The result of rotating the
2
-dimensional lens with respect to the axis of symmetry which realizes the smallest width is the
n
-dimensional lens. By Theorem
A
, this body is indeed the solution in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
.
On the other hand, one can rotate the lens with respect to the axis of symmetry which realizes its diameter. The resulting body, which we call a
spindle
and define more formally below, is a natural ‘competitor’ to the lens for the optimal shape. As we will see below, in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, the lens ‘wins’ this competition asymptotically as
n\to\infty
. Spindles also appear as solutions to other optimization problems with curvature constraints (see, e.g.,
[
Be1
,
BD1
]
).

We say that a convex body
\textup{Sp}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a
\lambda
-convex spindle
if
Sp
is the intersection of all balls of radius
1/\lambda
containing a pair of given points. The boundary of
Sp
is obtained by taking a smaller arc of a circle of radius
1/\penalty\lambda
in the
x_{1}x_{n}
-plane, with both endpoints on the
x_{1}
-axis, and rotating it in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
about the
x_{1}
-axis (here and below,
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}
are the Cartesian coordinates in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
).

The next proposition shows that asymptotically a lens has smaller volume than a spindle assuming that their surface areas are the same.

Proposition B.1

(Lens wins at infinity)
.

Let
L
be a
\lambda
-convex lens and
Sp
be a
\lambda
-convex spindle in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. If
|\partial L|=|\partial\textup{Sp}|
, then
|L|<|\textup{Sp}|
as
n\rightarrow\infty.

Proof.

Without loss of generality, assume
\lambda=1
. We start with computing surface areas of a lens and a spindle. Consider the circle of radius
1
in the
x_{1}x_{n}
-plane centered at the point
(0,\dots,0,-h_{1})
for
0<h_{1}<1
. Let
C_{1}
be the arc of this circle above the
x_{1}
-axis given by the following equation

x_{n}=\sqrt{1-x_{1}^{2}}-h_{1},

where
x_{1}
changes between
0
and
\sqrt{1-h_{1}^{2}}
.
By rotating
C_{1}
about the
x_{1}
-axis, we obtain the part of
Sp
in the hyperplane
x_{n}\geqslant 0
. Now we compute the surface area of
Sp
using the corresponding formula for the surface area of the body of revolution:

\displaystyle|\partial\textup{Sp}|=2\int_{0}^{\sqrt{1-h_{1}^{2}}}\left(\sqrt{1-x_{1}^{2}}-h_{1}\right)^{n-2}\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-x_{1}^{2}}}\,dx_{1}.

We will use Laplace’s method to obtain the asymptotic behavior of
|\partial\textup{Sp}|
as
n\rightarrow\infty
(see, e.g.,
[
BH
]
). We claim that

(B.1)

\displaystyle|\partial\textup{Sp}|

\displaystyle\approx\frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{\sqrt{2}}(1-h_{1})^{n-\frac{5}{2}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\left(1+O\left(\frac{1}{n}\right)\right).

The dominant contribution in the asymptotic expansion of the integral above comes from the neighborhood of
x_{1}=0
, so we can consider the integral over
(0,\,\infty)
.
Also, using the fact that
\sqrt{1-x_{1}^{2}}-h_{1}=(1-h_{1})\left(1-\frac{1}{2(1-h_{1})}x_{1}^{2}+O(x_{1}^{4})\right)
and that the integral over
(\sqrt{1-h_{1}^{2}},\,\infty)
is exponentially small, we have

\displaystyle|\partial\textup{Sp}|

\displaystyle\approx 2(1-h_{1})^{n-2}\int_{0}^{\infty}\left(1-\frac{1}{2(1-h_{1})}x_{1}^{2}+O(x_{1}^{4})\right)^{n-2}e^{\frac{1}{2}x_{1}^{2}+O(x_{1}^{4})}\,dx_{1}

\displaystyle=2(1-h_{1})^{n-2}\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-(n-2)\left(\frac{1}{2(1-h_{1})}x_{1}^{2}+O(x_{1}^{4})\right)}e^{\frac{1}{2}x_{1}^{2}+O(x_{1}^{4})}\,dx_{1}

\displaystyle=2(1-h_{1})^{n-2}\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{\frac{-(n-3)-h_{1}}{2(1-h_{1})}x_{1}^{2}-(n-3)O(x_{1}^{4})}\,dx_{1}

\displaystyle\approx 2(1-h_{1})^{n-2}\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-(n-3)\left(\frac{1}{2(1-h_{1})}x_{1}^{2}+O(x_{1}^{4})\right)}\,dx_{1}.

Making the change of variables
y^{2}=\frac{1}{2(1-h_{1})}x_{1}^{2}+O(x_{1}^{4})
in the last integral, and using that
y=\frac{x_{1}}{\sqrt{2(1-h_{1})}}\left(1+O(x_{1}^{2})\right)

and

dx_{1}=\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2(1-h_{1})}}+O(y^{2})\right)dy,

we obtain

\displaystyle|\partial\textup{Sp}|

\displaystyle\approx 2(1-h_{1})^{n-2}\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-(n-3)\left(\frac{1}{2(1-h_{1})}x_{1}^{2}+O(x_{1}^{4})\right)}\,dx_{1}

\displaystyle=2(1-h_{1})^{n-2}\left[\frac{1}{\sqrt{2(1-h_{1})}}\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}e^{-(n-3)y^{2}}dy+\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}e^{-(n-3)y^{2}}O(y^{2})dy\right].

The first integral is just the Gaussian integral

\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}e^{-(n-3)y^{2}}dy=\frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{2\sqrt{n-3}}.

The second integral we estimate using the change of variables
y=\sqrt{n-3}\,t
as follows

\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}e^{-(n-3)y^{2}}O(y^{2})dy=\frac{1}{(n-3)^{\frac{3}{2}}}\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}e^{-t^{2}}O(t^{2})dt.

Therefore, we get (
B.1
).

Now we can find the surface area of a lens
L
. For this, consider the circle of radius
1
in the
x_{1}x_{n}
-plane centered at the point
(-\sqrt{1-h_{2}^{2}},\dots,0,0)
for
0<h_{2}<1
. Let
C_{2}
be the arc of this circle given by the following equation

x_{n}=\sqrt{1-\left(x_{1}+\sqrt{1-h_{2}^{2}}\right)^{2}}

where
x_{1}
changes between
0
and
1-\sqrt{1-h_{2}^{2}}
. We claim that

(B.2)

\displaystyle|\partial L|

\displaystyle\approx 2\frac{h_{2}^{n-1}}{\sqrt{1-h_{2}^{2}}}\frac{1}{n}\left(1+O\left(\frac{1}{n}\right)\right).

Neglecting the exponentially small tails, the surface area of
L
is

\displaystyle|\partial L|

\displaystyle=2\int\limits_{0}^{1-\sqrt{1-h_{2}^{2}}}\left(1-\left(x_{1}+\sqrt{1-h_{2}^{2}}\right)^{2}\right)^{\frac{n-3}{2}}dx_{1}

\displaystyle\approx 2h_{2}^{n-3}\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}\left(1-\frac{x_{1}^{2}}{h_{2}^{2}}-2\frac{\sqrt{1-h_{2}^{2}}}{h_{2}^{2}}x_{1}\right)^{\frac{n-3}{2}}dx_{1}

\displaystyle=2h_{2}^{n-3}\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\frac{n-3}{2}\left(2\frac{\sqrt{1-h_{2}^{2}}}{h_{2}^{2}}x_{1}+O(x_{1}^{2})\right)}dx_{1}.

Making the change of variables
y=2\frac{\sqrt{1-h_{2}^{2}}}{h_{2}^{2}}x_{1}+O(x_{1}^{2})
and using that

\displaystyle dx_{1}=\left(\frac{h_{2}^{2}}{2\sqrt{1-h_{2}^{2}}}+O(y)\right)dy,

we get

\displaystyle|\partial L|

\displaystyle\approx 2\,h_{2}^{n-3}\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\frac{n-3}{2}y}\left(\frac{h_{2}^{2}}{2\sqrt{1-h_{2}^{2}}}+O(y)\right)dy.

By the argument similar to the one above, we obtain (
B.2
).

In the same manner, we obtain the expression for volumes:

\displaystyle|\textup{Sp}|

\displaystyle\approx\frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{\sqrt{2}}(1-h_{1})^{n-\frac{3}{2}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\left(1+O\left(\frac{1}{n}\right)\right);

\displaystyle|L|

\displaystyle\approx 2\frac{h_{2}^{n+1}}{\sqrt{1-h_{2}^{2}}}\frac{1}{n}\left(1+O\left(\frac{1}{n}\right)\right),

where
h_{1},h_{2}\in(0,1)
.

Since
|\partial\textup{Sp}|=|\partial L|
, we have

\displaystyle(1-h_{1})^{n-\frac{5}{2}}\left(1+O\left(\frac{1}{n}\right)\right)=\frac{2\sqrt{2}}{\pi}\frac{h_{2}^{n-1}}{\sqrt{1-h_{2}^{2}}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\left(1+O\left(\frac{1}{n}\right)\right).

This implies that
1-h_{1}=h_{2}
when
n\rightarrow\infty
. Thus,

\frac{|\textup{Sp}|}{|\partial\textup{Sp}|}=1-h_{1}>h_{2}^{2}=\frac{|L|}{|\partial L|}

as
n\rightarrow\infty
which confirms that a lens has smaller volume than a spindle.

∎
