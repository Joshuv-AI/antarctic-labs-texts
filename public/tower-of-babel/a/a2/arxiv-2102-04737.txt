Federated Learning with Local Differential Privacy: Trade-offs between Privacy, Utility, and Communication
Muah Kim, Onur Günlü, Rafael F. Schaefer

Abstract
Federated learning (FL) allows to train a massive amount of data privately due to its decentralized structure. Stochastic gradient descent (SGD) is commonly used for FL due to its good empirical performance, but sensitive user information can still be inferred from weight updates shared during FL iterations. We consider Gaussian mechanisms to preserve local differential privacy (LDP) of user data in the FL model with SGD. The trade-offs between user privacy, global utility, and transmission rate are proved by defining appropriate metrics for FL with LDP. Compared to existing results, the query sensitivity used in LDP is defined as a variable and a tighter privacy accounting method is applied. The proposed utility bound allows heterogeneous parameters over all users. Our bounds characterize how much utility decreases and transmission rate increases if a stronger privacy regime is targeted. Furthermore, given a target privacy level, our results guarantee a significantly larger utility and a smaller transmission rate as compared to existing privacy accounting methods.

Introduction

Differential privacy (DP) is widely used due to its strong privacy guarantees. DP tackles the privacy leakage about single data belonging to an individual in a dataset when some information from the dataset is publicly available. Common DP mechanisms add an independent random noise component to available data to provide privacy, which can be provided by using local sources of randomness such as physical unclonable functions (PUFs)
[

[
10

[
11

[

[

, with experimental evaluations
[

[

[

,

, an improved advanced composition theorem (AC2)

Unlike the composition theorems that directly compose the DP parameters, the MA approach circumvents the composition by converting DP into Rényi-differential privacy (RDP), whose composition has a simple linear form. The MA is shown to outperform the AC1 and the AC2 when a Gaussian noise mechanism is used
[
3

. The MA is further improved by using the optimal conversion from parameters of RDP to DP
[
19

. An FL model with stochastic gradient descent (FedSGD) with LDP is not yet investigated with a comprehensive theoretical analysis that considers privacy, utility, and communication jointly. This paper focuses on trade-offs between privacy, utility, and transmission rate, where LDP is provided to the FedSGD model by using a Gaussian mechanism. In
[

One main contribution of this paper is the privacy analysis for the FedSGD model by using an enhanced MA suggested in
[

Our utility bound considers system heterogeneity by allowing users to have distinct dataset sizes, data sampling probabilities, and target privacy levels. The transmission rate is considered as the differential entropy of the noisy gradients for lossless communications. We illustrate significant gains from our bounds in terms of the required noise power, the utility metric, and the transmission rate as compared to the existing methods.

System Model

2.1
Federated SGD (FedSGD)

Consider that the FedSGD method consists of a central server and
K
users. The
K
users are assumed to have their own neural networks with the same structure. At each time step
t
such that
t\in\{1,2,\dots,T\}
, the server distributes the aggregated weight values
\mathbf{w}^{(t)}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
to all users and the
K
users’ networks are initialized with those weight values. Then, user
k
randomly samples a dataset
\mathcal{J}_{k}^{(t)}
from its whole dataset
\mathcal{D}_{k}
with probability
q_{k}
and calculates the gradient
\mathbf{g}_{k}^{(t)}
from
\mathcal{J}_{k}^{(t)}
. In particular, we use a loss function
\ell(\mathbf{w}^{(t)},x)
defined for each data sample
x
and given weights
\mathbf{w}^{(t)}
, and we represent the local (per user) loss
\mathcal{L}_{k}(\mathbf{w}^{(t)},\mathcal{J}_{k}^{(t)})\in\mathbb{R}
as

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{k}(\mathbf{w}^{(t)},\mathcal{J}_{k}^{(t)})

\displaystyle\coloneqq\dfrac{1}{|\mathcal{J}_{k}^{(t)}|}\sum_{x\in\mathcal{J}_{k}^{(t)}}\ell(\mathbf{w}^{(t)},x)

(1)

where
|\mathcal{J}_{k}^{(t)}|
denotes the size of a set
\mathcal{J}_{k}^{(t)}
. Suppose the difference between the true loss and an empirical loss can be made negligible by using a sufficiently large dataset.
Each user’s local gradient
\mathbf{g}_{k}^{(t)}
is then represented as

\displaystyle\mathbf{g}^{(t)}_{k}(\mathcal{J}_{k}^{(t)})\!=\!\nabla_{\mathbf{w}}\mathcal{L}_{k}(\mathbf{w}^{(t)},\mathcal{J}^{(t)}_{k})\!=\!\dfrac{1}{|\mathcal{J}_{k}^{(t)}|}\!\sum_{x\in\mathcal{J}_{k}^{(t)}}\nabla_{\mathbf{w}}\ell(\mathbf{w}^{(t)},x)

where
\nabla_{\mathbf{w}}
represents the gradient along the weight vector
\mathbf{w}=(w_{1},w_{2},\dots,w_{d})
, i.e.,
\nabla_{\mathbf{w}}=(\frac{\partial}{\partial w_{1}},\frac{\partial}{\partial w_{2}},\dots,\frac{\partial}{\partial w_{d}})
. Let
\left\lVert\mathbf{x}\right\rVert
denote the
\ell_{2}
-norm of a
d
-dimensional vector
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{d})
, i.e.,
\left\lVert\mathbf{x}\right\rVert=\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{d}x_{i}^{2}}
. Assume that
G
is the maximum
\ell_{2}
-norm value of all possible gradients for any given weight vector
\mathbf{w}_{k}
and sampled dataset
\mathcal{J}_{k}
, i.e.,

G=\sup_{\mathbf{w}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathcal{J}_{k}\in\mathcal{D}_{k}}\mathbb{E}[\left\lVert\mathbf{g}_{k}(\mathcal{J}_{k})\right\rVert]
. Each user clips its local gradient by a clipping threshold value
C\in(0,G]
as

\displaystyle\bar{\mathbf{g}}_{k}^{(t)}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{g}^{(t)}_{k}\Big/\max\Big\{1,\left\lVert\mathbf{g}^{(t)}_{k}\right\rVert/C\Big\}.

(2)

The clipped gradients
\{\bar{\mathbf{g}}_{k}^{(t)}\}_{k=1}^{K}
are sent to the server and aggregated to obtain the updated weight vector
\mathbf{w}^{(t+1)}
. Suppose we use a learning rate
\eta_{t}
, then the weight update is

\displaystyle\mathbf{w}^{(t+1)}=\mathbf{w}^{(t)}-\eta_{t}\cdot\sum_{k=1}^{K}\dfrac{|\mathcal{J}_{k}^{(t)}|}{|\mathcal{J}^{(t)}|}\bar{\mathbf{g}}^{(t)}_{k}

(3)

where
\mathcal{J}^{(t)}=\cup_{k=1}^{K}\mathcal{J}_{k}^{(t)}
.
The global loss of the FL system is

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{w}^{(t)},\mathcal{J}^{(t)})

\displaystyle=\sum_{x\in\mathcal{J}^{(t)}}\frac{1}{|\mathcal{J}^{(t)}|}\ell(\mathbf{w}^{(t)},x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\frac{|\mathcal{J}_{k}^{(t)}|}{|\mathcal{J}^{(t)}|}\mathcal{L}_{k}(\mathbf{w}^{(t)},\mathcal{J}_{k}^{(t)})

(4)

which can be obtained by taking a weighted sum of the local losses defined in (
1
). Thus, the weighted average of local gradients is equivalent to the gradient of the global loss calculated with the whole sampled data, i.e., we have

\displaystyle\nabla_{\mathbf{w}}\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{w}^{(t)},\mathcal{J}^{(t)})

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{|\mathcal{J}^{(t)}|}\sum_{x\in\mathcal{J}^{(t)}}\nabla_{\mathbf{w}}\ell(\mathbf{w}^{(t)},x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\dfrac{|\mathcal{J}_{k}^{(t)}|}{|\mathcal{J}^{(t)}|}\sum_{x\in\mathcal{J}_{k}^{(t)}}\dfrac{1}{|\mathcal{J}_{k}^{(t)}|}\nabla_{\mathbf{w}}\ell(\mathbf{w}^{(t)},x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\dfrac{|\mathcal{J}_{k}^{(t)}|}{|\mathcal{J}^{(t)}|}\mathbf{g}^{(t)}_{k}(\mathbf{w}^{(t)},\mathcal{J}_{k}^{(t)}).

(5)

This implies that such an FL model results in the same weight update as the centralized model, so one global loss optimization problem can be divided into multiple local problems
[
7
]
.

2.2
Local differential privacy (LDP)

FL guarantees a certain level of privacy since the users do not directly send their data to the central server publicly
[
7
]
. However, a certain amount of information can still be inferred from the shared information of the local networks, so a privacy mechanism is still necessary to protect user data. We consider LDP to guarantee individual user privacy. A mechanism
\mathcal{M}_{k}
is
(\epsilon_{k},\delta_{k})-
LDP w.r.t. the user
k
’s dataset
\mathcal{D}_{k}
, if any two neighboring datasets
D\sim D^{\prime}\subseteq\mathcal{D}_{k}
satisfy for any
\mathcal{S}\subseteq\text{range}(\mathcal{M}_{k})
that

\displaystyle\Pr\left[\mathcal{M}_{k,D}(\mathbf{g}_{k})\in\mathcal{S}\right]

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\leq e^{\epsilon_{k}}\cdot\Pr\left[\mathcal{M}_{k,D^{\prime}}(\mathbf{g}_{k})\in\mathcal{S}\right]+\delta_{k}.

(6)

We assume that the local gradients
\mathbf{g}^{(t)}_{k}
are clipped and then LDP is satisfied by adding a Gaussian noise component
\mathbf{Z}_{k}
. Suppose the Gaussian noise variance of each dimension is proportional to
C^{2}
, i.e.,
\mathbf{Z}_{k}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},C^{2}\sigma_{k}^{2}\mathbf{I}_{d})
for some
\sigma_{k}^{2}>0
, where
\mathbf{I}_{d}
is the
d\times d
identity matrix. Denote the noisy gradients as
\tilde{\mathbf{g}}_{k}^{(t)}
, so the Gaussian LDP mechanism can be represented as
\tilde{\mathbf{g}}_{k}^{(t)}=\mathcal{M}_{k}(\bar{\mathbf{g}}^{(t)}_{k})=\bar{\mathbf{g}}^{(t)}_{k}+\mathbf{Z}_{k}\sim\mathcal{N}(\bar{\mathbf{g}}^{(t)}_{k},C^{2}\sigma_{k}^{2}\mathbf{I}_{d})
.
With such an LDP mechanism, the weight update equation (
3
) can be used by replacing
\bar{\mathbf{g}}_{k}^{(t)}
with
\tilde{\mathbf{g}}_{k}^{(t)}
for all
t=1,2,\dots,T
.

3
Trade-offs between Privacy, Utility, and Transmission Rate

We next characterize the Gaussian noise variance required to guarantee a target LDP level after
T
rounds of weight updates for FL with LDP, i.e.,
T
-fold composition
. Furthermore, we analyze the effects of the Gaussian noise on the utility and the transmission rate, given a target LDP level. With an example we illustrate that a tighter privacy composition bound can yield a significantly larger utility and smaller transmission rate for the same target LDP level.

3.1
Theoretical Analysis

We define utility
\mathcal{U}(T)
after
T
iterations as the multiplicative inverse of the convergence rate, i.e., we have

\mathcal{U}(T)=\dfrac{1}{\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{w}^{(T)},\mathcal{J}^{(T)})]-\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{w}^{*})}

(7)

where
\mathbf{w}^{*}
is the optimal weight vector that minimizes the global loss, i.e.,
\mathbf{w}^{*}={\arg\min}_{\mathbf{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{w},\cup_{k=1}^{K}\mathcal{D}_{k})
. This utility metric is used instead of accuracy to track the learning performance analytically. Consider that the transmission of a noisy gradient is lossless, and define the user
k
’s transmission rate
R_{\text{tr},k}
by the differential entropy of its noisy gradient, i.e.,
R_{\text{tr},k}\!=\!h(\tilde{\mathbf{g}}^{(t)}_{k})
for
t\!=\!1,2,\dots,T
. The transmission rate is measured by a differential entropy term for simplicity, which can be extended by allowing distortion. We remark that the transmission rate can be further reduced with quantization, as suggested, e.g., in
[
20
]
.

The following theorem provides the trade-offs between LDP parameters, utility, and the transmission rate for the assumed FL model; see Appendix
A
for its proof.

.

’s Gaussian mechanism
\mathcal{M}_{k}

4
Conclusion

Trade-offs between privacy, utility, and transmission rate of a FedSGD model with a Gaussian LDP mechanism were proved. We provided a noise variance bound that guarantees a given LDP level after multiple rounds of weight updates by using a tight composition theorem. The proposed utility bound allows distinct parameters for all users and allows the gradients to be clipped and noisy. The noise variance required is illustrated to be significantly smaller than the ones obtained by using existing privacy composition methods MA, AC1, and AC2. Similarly, our bounds lead to a significantly larger utility and a smaller transmission rate. In future work, we will illustrate gains from our bounds as compared to existing methods for large available datasets used for FL.

\mathcal{D}_{k}^{\prime}
to
\mathcal{D}_{k}
that only differs by a single data
D_{n}
, i.e.,
\mathcal{D}_{k}=\mathcal{D}^{\prime}_{k}\cup\{D_{n}\}
without loss of generality. Instead of considering the worst case that
\bar{\mathbf{g}}_{k}(\mathcal{D}_{k})=-\bar{\mathbf{g}}_{k}(\mathcal{D}^{\prime}_{k})
and
\left\lVert\bar{\mathbf{g}}_{k}(\mathcal{D}_{k})\right\rVert=C
, we assume
\bar{\mathbf{g}}_{k}(\mathcal{D}^{\prime}_{k})=\mathbf{0}
and
\bar{\mathbf{g}}_{k}(\mathcal{D}_{k})=2C\mathbf{e}_{1}
and analyze the Rényi divergence of two perturbed gradients without loss of generality. This assumption makes the problem one-dimensional because the neighboring datasets
\mathcal{D}_{k}
and
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}_{k}
result in the gradients that have different elements only in the first dimension. Let
\mu_{0}
denote the PDF of
\mathcal{N}(0,C^{2}\sigma_{k}^{2})
and
\mu_{1}
denote the PDF of
\mathcal{N}(2C,C^{2}\sigma_{k}^{2})
.
The noisy gradients of the Gaussian mechanism can be represented in one dimension as

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{k}(\mathcal{D}^{\prime}_{k})\sim\mu_{0}

(14)

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{k}(\mathcal{D}_{k})\sim\mu\coloneqq(1-q_{k})\mu_{0}+q_{k}\mu_{1}.

(15)

To observe
\gamma_{k}
for given
\mu
,
\mu_{0}
, and an integer
\alpha_{k}
, we start with the definition of RDP:

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{\alpha_{k}}\mathbb{E}_{z\sim\mu}[(\mu(z)/\mu_{0}(z))^{\alpha_{k}}]\leq\gamma_{k}

(16)

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{\alpha_{k}}\mathbb{E}_{z\sim\mu_{0}}[(\mu_{0}(z)/\mu(z))^{\alpha_{k}}]\leq\gamma_{k}.

(17)

Two inequalities can be shown by the same method, so we show only the second inequality here.
Changing the probability that the expectation is taken over from
\mu_{0}
to
\mu
and using the binomial expansion, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{z\sim\mu_{0}}[(\mu_{0}(z)/\mu(z))^{\alpha_{k}}]=\mathbb{E}_{z\sim\mu}[(\mu_{0}(z)/\mu(z))^{\alpha_{k}+1}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{z\sim\mu}[(1+(\mu_{0}(z)-\mu(z))/\mu(z))^{\alpha_{k}+1}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{\alpha_{k}+1}\dbinom{\alpha_{k}+1}{i}\mathbb{E}_{z\sim\mu}[((\mu_{0}(z)-\mu(z))/\mu(z))^{i}].

(18)

The first term of the summation coming from
i=0
simply becomes
1
. The second term when
i=1
is
0
by simple calculus. The third term with
i=2
can be bounded as

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{z\sim\mu}[((\mu_{0}(z)-\mu(z))/\mu(z))^{2}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{z\sim\mu}[((q_{k}\mu_{0}(z)-q_{k}\mu_{1}(z))/\mu(z))^{2}]

\displaystyle=q_{k}^{2}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}(\mu_{0}(z)-\mu_{1}(z))^{2}/\mu(z)dz

\displaystyle\leq\frac{q_{k}^{2}}{1-q_{k}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}(\mu_{0}(z)-\mu_{1}(z))^{2}/\mu_{0}(z)dz

\displaystyle=\frac{q_{k}^{2}}{1-q_{k}}\mathbb{E}_{z\sim\mu_{0}}[((\mu_{0}(z)-\mu_{1}(z))/\mu_{0}(z))^{2}].

(19)

The expected value of the above term can be further simplified and bounded as below:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{z\sim\mu_{0}}[((\mu_{0}(z)-\mu_{1}(z))/\mu_{0}(z))^{2}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{z\sim\mu_{0}}\left[\left(1-\exp\left(\frac{-z^{2}+4Cz-4C^{2}+z^{2}}{2C^{2}\sigma_{k}^{2}}\right)\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=1\!-\!2\mathbb{E}_{z\sim\mu_{0}}\left[\exp\left(\dfrac{4Cz\!-\!4C^{2}}{2C^{2}\sigma_{k}^{2}}\right)\right]

\displaystyle\qquad+\!\mathbb{E}_{z\sim\mu_{0}}\left[\exp\left(\dfrac{8Cz\!-\!8C^{2}}{2C^{2}\sigma_{k}^{2}}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=1\!-\!2\!+\!\exp\left(\frac{8C^{2}}{2C^{2}\sigma_{k}^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle=\exp\left(\frac{4}{\sigma_{k}^{2}}\right)\!-\!1\!=\!4/\sigma_{k}^{2}\!+\!\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{\sigma_{k}^{4}}\right).

(20)

The third term with
i=2
eventually becomes

\displaystyle\dbinom{\alpha_{k}+1}{2}\mathbb{E}_{z\sim\mu}\!\left[\left(\dfrac{\mu_{0}(z)-\mu(z)}{\mu(z)}\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\quad\leq\!\dfrac{4\alpha_{k}(\alpha_{k}+1)q_{k}^{2}}{2(1-q_{k})\sigma_{k}^{2}}+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{q_{k}^{2}\alpha_{k}^{2}}{\sigma_{k}^{4}}\right).

(21)

[
3
, Lemma 3]
shows that the other terms, i.e.,
(i\geq 3)
terms, are upper bounded by
\mathcal{O}\left(\dfrac{q_{k}^{3}\alpha_{k}^{3}}{\sigma_{k}^{3}}\right)
. Thus,
\gamma_{k}(\alpha_{k})=\dfrac{2q_{k}^{2}}{(1-q_{k})\sigma_{k}^{2}}(\alpha_{k}+1)+\mathcal{O}(\dfrac{q_{k}^{3}\alpha_{k}^{2}}{\sigma_{k}^{3}})
. Note that using
\gamma_{k}(\alpha_{k})
with
\sigma_{k}^{2}
is equivalent to using
\gamma_{0}(\alpha_{k})
with
\frac{(1-q_{k})\alpha_{k}}{4q_{k}^{2}(\alpha_{k}+1)}\sigma_{k}^{2}
because

\displaystyle\gamma_{k}(\alpha_{k})=\frac{2q_{k}^{2}(\alpha_{k}+1)}{(1-q_{k})\sigma_{k}^{2}}=\frac{\alpha_{k}}{2\left(\frac{(1-q_{k})\alpha_{k}}{4q_{k}^{2}(\alpha_{k}+1)}\sigma_{k}^{2}\right)}.

(22)

In accordance with an assumption that
\alpha_{k}
is optimally chosen to be
\alpha_{k}=2\log(\delta_{k}^{-1})/\epsilon_{k}
from
[
19
, Theorem 5]
, we obtain
\alpha_{k}/(\alpha_{k}+1)\approx 1
when
\delta_{k}
is sufficiently small. We can obtain the following inequality by directly using
[
19
, Theorem 5]
for the noise variance associated with
\gamma_{k}(\alpha_{k})
:

\displaystyle\frac{1-q_{k}}{4q_{k}^{2}}\sigma_{k}^{2}\approx\frac{(1-q_{k})\alpha_{k}}{4q_{k}^{2}(\alpha_{k}+1)}\sigma_{k}^{2}

\displaystyle\quad\!\geq\!\frac{2T}{\epsilon_{k}^{2}}\log\frac{1}{\delta_{k}}\!+\!\frac{T}{\epsilon_{k}}-\frac{2T}{\epsilon_{k}^{2}}\left(\log(2\log\delta_{k}^{-1})\!+\!1\!-\!\log\epsilon_{k}\right)

\displaystyle\hskip 50.0pt+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\log^{2}(\log\delta_{k}^{-1})}{\log\delta_{k}^{-1}}\right).

(23)

Dividing both hand sides by the coefficient of
\sigma_{k}^{2}
completes the proof of (
10
).

Due to the assumption that
\mathcal{L}
is
\lambda
-strongly convex, for all
\mathbf{w},\mathbf{w}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and any subgradient
\mathbf{g}
of
\mathcal{L}
at
\mathbf{w}
we have

\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{w}^{\prime})-\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{w})\geq\langle\mathbf{g},\mathbf{w}^{\prime}-\mathbf{w}\rangle+\frac{\lambda}{2}\left\lVert\mathbf{w}^{\prime}-\mathbf{w}\right\rVert^{2}

(24)

so that we obtain

\displaystyle\langle\mathbf{g}^{(t)}_{k},

\displaystyle\mathbf{w}^{(t)}-\mathbf{w}^{*}\rangle=-\langle\mathbf{g}^{(t)}_{k},\mathbf{w}^{*}-\mathbf{w}^{(t)}\rangle

\displaystyle\geq\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{w}^{(t)})-\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{w}^{*})+\frac{\lambda}{2}\left\lVert\mathbf{w}^{(t)}-\mathbf{w}^{*}\right\rVert^{2}\geq 0

(25)

and

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}

\displaystyle(\mathbf{w}^{(t)})-\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{w}^{*})\geq\langle\mathbf{g}^{*},\mathbf{w}^{(t)}-\mathbf{w}^{*}\rangle+\frac{\lambda}{2}\left\lVert\mathbf{w}^{(t)}-\mathbf{w}^{*}\right\rVert^{2}

\displaystyle\hskip 65.00009pt\geq\frac{\lambda}{2}\left\lVert\mathbf{w}^{(t)}-\mathbf{w}^{*}\right\rVert^{2},

(26)

where
\mathbf{g}^{*}
is the gradient at the optimum point
\mathbf{w}^{*}
calculated with an arbitrary subset
\mathcal{S}
of
\mathcal{D}
. The
\mu
-smoothness condition gives

\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{w}^{(t)})-\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{w}^{*})]\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{\mu}{2}\left\lVert\mathbf{w}^{(t)}-\mathbf{w}^{*}\right\rVert^{2}\right].

(27)

We want to next observe how the weight vector
\mathbf{w}^{(t)}
converges to its optimum
\mathbf{w}^{*}
by bounding the expected mean square error
\mathbb{E}\left[\left\lVert\mathbf{w}^{(t)}-\mathbf{w}^{*}\right\rVert^{2}\right]
. We first obtain a recurrence formula for
\left\lVert\mathbf{w}^{(t)}-\mathbf{w}^{*}\right\rVert^{2}
by using the weight update equation (
3
) with
\bar{\mathbf{g}}_{k}^{(t)}
replaced by
\tilde{\mathbf{g}}_{k}^{(t)}
as follows:

\displaystyle\left\lVert\mathbf{w}^{(t+1)}-\mathbf{w}^{*}\right\rVert^{2}=\left\lVert\mathbf{w}^{(t)}-\mathbf{w}^{*}-\eta_{t}\cdot\sum_{k=1}^{K}\frac{|\mathcal{J}_{k}^{(t)}|}{|\mathcal{J}^{(t)}|}\tilde{\mathbf{g}}^{(t)}_{k}\right\rVert^{2}

\displaystyle=\left\lVert\mathbf{w}^{(t)}-\mathbf{w}^{*}\right\rVert^{2}\!+\!\eta_{t}^{2}\left\lVert\sum_{k=1}^{K}\frac{|\mathcal{J}_{k}^{(t)}|}{|\mathcal{J}^{(t)}|}\tilde{\mathbf{g}}^{(t)}_{k}\right\rVert^{2}

\displaystyle\qquad-2\eta_{t}\langle\mathbf{w}^{(t)}-\mathbf{w}^{*},\sum_{k=1}^{K}\frac{|\mathcal{J}_{k}^{(t)}|}{|\mathcal{J}^{(t)}|}\tilde{\mathbf{g}}^{(t)}_{k}\rangle.

(28)

First, the second term can be decomposed into

\displaystyle\eta_{t}^{2}\left\lVert\sum_{k=1}^{K}\frac{|\mathcal{J}_{k}^{(t)}|}{|\mathcal{J}^{(t)}|}\tilde{\mathbf{g}}^{(t)}_{k}\right\rVert^{2}

\displaystyle=\eta_{t}^{2}\left\lVert\sum_{k=1}^{K}\frac{|\mathcal{J}_{k}^{(t)}|}{|\mathcal{J}^{(t)}|}(\bar{\mathbf{g}}^{(t)}_{k}+\mathbf{Z}_{k})\right\rVert^{2}

\displaystyle=\eta_{t}^{2}\left\lVert\sum_{k=1}^{K}\frac{|\mathcal{J}_{k}^{(t)}|}{|\mathcal{J}^{(t)}|}\bar{\mathbf{g}}^{(t)}_{k}+\mathbf{Z}\right\rVert^{2}

(29)

where
\mathbf{Z}=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\frac{|\mathcal{J}_{k}^{(t)}|}{|\mathcal{J}^{(t)}|}\mathbf{Z}_{k}
. The square of the
\ell_{2}
-norm can be expanded as

\displaystyle\eta_{t}^{2}\left\lVert\sum_{k=1}^{K}\frac{|\mathcal{J}_{k}^{(t)}|}{|\mathcal{J}^{(t)}|}\bar{\mathbf{g}}^{(t)}_{k}\right\rVert^{2}+

\displaystyle 2\eta_{t}^{2}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\frac{|\mathcal{J}_{k}^{(t)}|}{|\mathcal{J}^{(t)}|}\langle\bar{\mathbf{g}}^{(t)},\mathbf{Z}\rangle+\eta_{t}^{2}\left\lVert\mathbf{Z}\right\rVert^{2}.

It is straightforward to show that
\mathbf{Z}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},C^{2}\sigma^{2}\mathbf{I}_{d})
where
\sigma^{2}=\frac{\sum_{k=1}^{K}(|\mathcal{D}_{k}|q_{k}\sigma_{k})^{2}}{(\sum_{k=1}^{K}|\mathcal{D}_{k}|q_{k})^{2}}
.
The expected value of the second term can be obtained as follows.

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\eta_{t}^{2}\left\lVert\sum_{k=1}^{K}\frac{|\mathcal{J}_{k}^{(t)}|}{|\mathcal{J}^{(t)}|}\bar{\mathbf{g}}^{(t)}_{k}\right\rVert^{2}\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[\cancelto{0}{2\eta_{t}^{2}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\frac{|\mathcal{J}_{k}^{(t)}|}{|\mathcal{J}^{(t)}|}\langle\bar{\mathbf{g}},\mathbf{Z}\rangle}\right]

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\mathbb{E}[\eta_{t}^{2}\left\lVert\mathbf{Z}\right\rVert^{2}]

\displaystyle\leq\eta_{t}^{2}C^{2}+d\eta_{t}^{2}C^{2}\sigma^{2}=\eta_{t}^{2}C^{2}(1+d\sigma^{2})

(30)

which follows because every element of
\mathbf{Z}
is a zero-mean Gaussian, and taking the inner product is a linear transformation. Next, the third term of (
28
) can be decomposed into the inner products that can be locally calculated as

\displaystyle-2\eta_{t}\langle\mathbf{w}^{(t)}-\mathbf{w}^{*},\sum_{k=1}^{K}\frac{|\mathcal{J}_{k}^{(t)}|}{|\mathcal{J}^{(t)}|}\tilde{\mathbf{g}}^{(t)}_{k}\rangle

\displaystyle\qquad=-2\eta_{t}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\frac{|\mathcal{J}_{k}^{(t)}|}{|\mathcal{J}^{(t)}|}\langle\mathbf{w}^{(t)}-\mathbf{w}^{*},\tilde{\mathbf{g}}^{(t)}_{k}\rangle.

(31)

We bound the expected value of each summand in (
31
) as

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{|\mathcal{J}_{k}^{(t)}|}{|\mathcal{J}^{(t)}|}\langle\mathbf{w}^{(t)}-\mathbf{w}^{*},\tilde{\mathbf{g}}^{(t)}_{k}\rangle\right]

\displaystyle\!=\!\mathbb{E}\!\left[\!\frac{|\mathcal{J}_{k}^{(t)}|}{|\mathcal{J}^{(t)}|}\!\langle\mathbf{w}^{(t)}\!-\!\mathbf{w}^{*},\bar{\mathbf{g}}^{(t)}_{k}\rangle\!\right]\!+\!\cancelto{0}{\mathbb{E}\!\left[\!\frac{|\mathcal{J}_{k}^{(t)}|}{|\mathcal{J}^{(t)}|}\langle\!\mathbf{w}^{(t)}\!-\!\mathbf{w}^{*},\mathbf{Z}_{k}\rangle\!\right]\!}

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{|\mathcal{J}_{k}^{(t)}|}{|\mathcal{J}^{(t)}|}\frac{C}{G}\langle\mathbf{w}^{(t)}-\mathbf{w}^{*},\mathbf{g}^{(t)}_{k}\rangle\right].

(32)

And then we bound the expected value of (
31
) as follows:

\displaystyle-2\eta_{t}\mathbb{E}\left[\langle\mathbf{w}^{(t)}-\mathbf{w}^{*},\sum_{k=1}^{K}\frac{|\mathcal{J}_{k}^{(t)}|}{|\mathcal{J}^{(t)}|}\tilde{\mathbf{g}}^{(t)}_{k}\rangle\right]

\displaystyle\leq-2\eta_{t}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{|\mathcal{J}_{k}^{(t)}|}{|\mathcal{J}^{(t)}|}\frac{C}{G}\langle\mathbf{w}^{(t)}-\mathbf{w}^{*},\mathbf{g}^{(t)}_{k}\rangle\right]

\displaystyle\overset{(a)}{\leq}-2\eta_{t}\frac{C}{G}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{w}^{(t)})-\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{w}^{*})+\frac{\lambda}{2}\left\lVert\mathbf{w}^{(t)}-\mathbf{w}^{*}\right\rVert^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\overset{(b)}{\leq}-2\eta_{t}\frac{C}{G}\mathbb{E}\left[\lambda\left\lVert\mathbf{w}^{(t)}-\mathbf{w}^{*}\right\rVert^{2}\right]

(33)

where
(a)
follows by (
25
) and
(b)
follows by (
26
). Using (
30
) and (
33
), we can bound the expected value of (
28
) as

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left\lVert\mathbf{w}^{(t+1)}-\mathbf{w}^{*}\right\rVert^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\!\mathbb{E}\!\left[\left\lVert\mathbf{w}^{(t)}-\mathbf{w}^{*}\right\rVert^{2}\right]\!+\!\eta_{t}^{2}C^{2}(1+d\sigma^{2})

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad-2\eta_{t}\frac{C}{G}\lambda\mathbb{E}\left[\left\lVert\mathbf{w}^{(t)}-\mathbf{w}^{*}\right\rVert^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\!(1\!-\!2\eta_{t}\frac{C}{G}\lambda)\mathbb{E}\!\left[\!\left\lVert\mathbf{w}^{(t)}\!-\!\mathbf{w}^{*}\right\rVert^{2}\right]\!+\!\eta_{t}^{2}C^{2}(1\!+\!d\sigma^{2}).

(34)

Assume a learning rate of
\eta_{t}=\frac{G}{C\lambda t}
, so we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left\lVert\mathbf{w}^{(t+1)}-\mathbf{w}^{*}\right\rVert^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq(1-\frac{2}{t})\mathbb{E}\left[\left\lVert\mathbf{w}^{(t)}-\mathbf{w}^{*}\right\rVert^{2}\right]+\frac{G^{2}(1+d\sigma^{2})}{\lambda^{2}t^{2}}.

(35)

One can infer the following explicit bound for each term for some

from the above recurrence relation:

\mathbb{E}\left[\left\lVert\mathbf{w}^{(t)}-\mathbf{w}^{*}\right\rVert^{2}\right]\leq a_{1}\frac{G^{2}}{\lambda^{2}t}.

(36)

According to
[
21
, Lemma 2]
, the first term
\mathbb{E}\left[\left\lVert\mathbf{w}^{(1)}-\mathbf{w}^{*}\right\rVert^{2}\right]
can be bounded as
\mathbb{E}\left[\left\lVert\mathbf{w}^{(1)}-\mathbf{w}^{*}\right\rVert^{2}\right]\leq\frac{4G^{2}}{\lambda^{2}}
.
Thus, it is necessary to have
a_{1}\geq 4
. The next term with

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left\lVert\mathbf{w}^{(2)}-\mathbf{w}^{*}\right\rVert^{2}\right]\leq-\mathbb{E}\left[\left\lVert\mathbf{w}^{(1)}-\mathbf{w}^{*}\right\rVert^{2}\right]+\frac{G^{2}(1+d\sigma^{2})}{\lambda^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2G^{2}(1+d\sigma^{2})}{2\lambda^{2}}.

(37)

To include this term, we should satisfy
a_{1}\geq 2(1+d\sigma^{2})
. Similarly, the next term with
t=3
can be bounded as

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left\lVert\mathbf{w}^{(3)}-\mathbf{w}^{*}\right\rVert^{2}\right]\leq\frac{G^{2}(1+d\sigma^{2})}{4\lambda^{2}}

\displaystyle\;\leq\frac{\frac{3}{4}G^{2}(1+d\sigma^{2})}{3\lambda^{2}}.

Thus, we should satisfy also

. For all terms with

(1-2/t)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}

\displaystyle\left[\left\lVert\mathbf{w}^{(\tau+1)}-\mathbf{w}^{*}\right\rVert^{2}\right]\leq(1-\frac{2}{\tau})a_{1}\frac{G^{2}}{\lambda^{2}\tau}+\frac{G^{2}(1+d\sigma^{2})}{\lambda^{2}\tau^{2}}

\displaystyle=a_{1}\frac{G^{2}}{\lambda^{2}(\tau+1)}\frac{1}{\tau^{2}}((\tau+1)(\tau-2)+\frac{\tau+1}{a_{1}}(1+d\sigma^{2}))

\displaystyle\!=\!a_{1}\frac{G^{2}}{\lambda^{2}(\tau+1)}(1\!-\!\frac{1}{\tau}(1-\frac{1+d\sigma^{2}}{a_{1}})\!-\!\frac{1}{\tau^{2}}(2-\frac{1+d\sigma^{2}}{a_{1}}))

\displaystyle\overset{(a)}{\leq}a_{1}\frac{G^{2}}{\lambda^{2}(\tau+1)}

(39)

where

holds if

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{w}^{(t)})-\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{w}^{*})]\leq\dfrac{\mu}{2}\mathbb{E}\left[\left\lVert\mathbf{w}^{(t)}-\mathbf{w}^{*}\right\rVert^{2}\right]

(42)

The lower bound of (

Consider next the transmission rate bound, for which we denote the noisy gradient as a sum of the clipped gradient and noise to exploit their boundedness and statistical properties, respectively, as follows:

\displaystyle h(\tilde{\mathbf{g}}_{k}^{(t)})=h(\bar{\mathbf{g}}_{k}^{(t)}+\mathbf{Z}_{k})\leq h(\bar{\mathbf{g}}_{k}^{(t)})+h(\mathbf{Z}_{k})

\displaystyle h(\bar{\mathbf{g}}_{k}^{(t)})\leq

where

and

-th column as

\displaystyle\prod_{i=1}^{d}\mathbb{E}\big[(\bar{g}_{k,i}^{(t)})^{2}\big]

\displaystyle\leq\left(\frac{1}{d}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\mathbb{E}\big[(\bar{g}_{k,i}^{(t)})^{2}\big]\right)^{d}\leq\left(\frac{C^{2}}{d}\right)^{d}.

(46)

Combining (

\displaystyle h(\bar{\mathbf{g}}_{k}^{(t)})\leq\dfrac{d}{2}\log\Big(\frac{2\pi eC^{2}}{d}\Big).

Furthermore, the differential entropy of the noise

\displaystyle h(\mathbf{Z}_{k})=\dfrac{1}{2}\log\det(2\pi eC^{2}\sigma_{k}^{2}\mathbf{I}_{d})=\dfrac{d}{2}\log(2\pi eC^{2}\sigma_{k}^{2}).
