Keller and Lieb-Thirring estimates of the eigenvalues in the gap of Dirac operators


Abstract
Zusammenfassung We estimate the lowest eigenvalue in the gap of the essential spectrum of a Dirac operator with mass in terms of a Lebesgue norm of the potential. Such a bound is the counterpart for Dirac operators of the Keller estimates for the Schrödinger operator, which are equivalent to Gagliardo-Nirenberg-Sobolev interpolation inequalities. Domain, self-adjointness, optimality and critical values of the norms are addressed, while the optimal potential is given by a Dirac equation with a Kerr nonlinearity. A new critical bound appears, which is the smallest value of the norm of the potential for which eigenvalues may reach the bottom of the gap in the essential spectrum. The Keller estimate is then extended to a Lieb-Thirring inequality for the eigenvalues in the gap. Most of our result are established in the Birman-Schwinger reformulation.

Introduction and main results

In 1961, J.B. Keller established in
[
45
]
the expression of the potential which minimizes the lowest eigenvalue, or
ground state
,
\lambda_{S}(V)
of the Schrödinger operator
-\Delta-V
in dimension
d=1
, under a constraint on the Lebesgue norm

\|{V}\|_{p}=\left(\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}{|V|^{p}}\,{\mathrm{d}}x\right)^{1/p}

of exponent
p
of
V
. This estimate was later extended in
[
53
]
by E.H. Lieb and W. Thirring to higher dimensions and to a sum of the lowest eigenvalues. During the last forty years, various refinements were published. As an example, we quote stability results for
\lambda_{S}(V)
proved in
[
12
]
by E.A. Carlen, R.L. Frank, and E.H. Lieb. Although Dirac operators inherit many qualitative properties of Schrödinger operators, dealing with Dirac operators turns out to be a delicate issue.

If
\not{\mathcal{D}}_{m}
denotes the free Dirac operator and
V
is a non-negative valued function,
\not{\mathcal{D}}_{m}-V
is not bounded from below. One is actually interested in the lowest eigenvalue
\lambda_{D}(V)
in the essential gap
(-m\,c^{2},m\,c^{2})
, where
m
denotes the mass and
c
the speed of light. We shall speak of
\lambda_{D}(V)
as the
ground state
energy of
\not{\mathcal{D}}_{m}-V
. In the standard setting, it is expected that
\lambda_{D}(V)-m\,c^{2}
converges to
\lambda_{S}(V)
in the non-relativistic limit,
i.e.
, as
c\to+\infty
. It is therefore a natural question to estimate
\lambda_{D}(V)
in terms of
\|{V}\|_{p}
and identify the corresponding optimal potential. This question is the main purpose of our paper. A new
critical value
appears, which corresponds to the smallest value of
\|{V}\|_{p}
for which
\lambda_{D}(V)
reaches, for some potential
V\geq 0
, the lower end of the essential gap
-\,m\,c^{2}
. In a linear setting, a similar question has been raised in
[
35
,
34
]
, where the authors find a critical value
\nu_{1}
so that
\lambda_{D}\big(\mu*|\cdot|^{-1}\big)>-\,m\,c^{2}
for all positive measures
\mu
with
\mu({\mathbb{R}}^{3})<\nu_{1}
, with
2/\big(\pi/2+2/\pi\big)<\nu_{1}\leq 1
. Going back to
[
28
,
21
,
27
]
, it is known that
Hardy inequalities
play an essential role in the analysis of the spectrum of Dirac-Coulomb operators. In the present article, except for the case
p=d=1
, we rather find a nonlinear functional inequality of Gagliardo-Nirenberg-Sobolev nature, instead of a Hardy inequality (see comments in Appendix
C.2
).

It is possible to characterize the eigenvalues of
\not{\mathcal{D}}_{m}-V
in the gap by a
min-max principle
according to
[
29
,
28
,
30
]
but this raises delicate issues involving the domain of the operator and its self-adjoint extensions addressed respectively in
[
63
,
30
,
36
,
37
,
33
]
. Applied with a Coulombian potential
V
, the method gives rise, after the maximising step in the min-max method, to a lower bounded quadratic form which amounts to a kind of Hardy inequality for the upper component: see
[
27
,
21
,
10
]
for details. The same strategy applies to a general potential
V
under a constraint on
\|{V}\|_{p}
, except that the Keller type bound on
\lambda_{D}(V)
is given by an implicit condition: see Appendix
C
. The optimal potential solves a nonlinear Dirac equation with Kerr-type nonlinearity. For the two-dimensional case, this equation has been studied in
[
8
,
5
,
7
,
6
]
by W. Borrelli. In the one-dimensional case, the solution is explicit, which allows us to identify it as in the case of the Schrödinger operator studied in
[
45
]
. Alternatively to the min-max principle, the properties of the
Birman-Schwinger operator
corresponding to
\not{\mathcal{D}}_{m}-V
allows us to characterize
\lambda_{D}(V)
and, except in Appendix
C
, we will adopt this point of view.

The
Keller-Lieb-Thirring inequality for a Schrödinger operator
goes as follows. Let us assume that
q>2
, with
q<2^{*}:=2\,d/(d-2)
if
d\geq 3
, and let
\vartheta=d\,(q-2)/(2\,q)
. For any function
u\in\mathrm{H}^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
, the Gagliardo-Nirenberg-Sobolev inequality

\|{\nabla u}\|_{2}^{\vartheta}\,\|{u}\|_{2}^{1-\vartheta}\geq\mathscr{C}_{q}\,\|{u}\|_{q}

can be rewritten in the non-scale invariant form as

\forall\,(\lambda,u)\in(0,+\infty)\times\mathrm{H}^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})\,,\quad\|{\nabla u}\|_{2}^{2}+\lambda\,\|{u}\|_{2}^{2}\geq\mathsf{C}_{q}\,\lambda^{1-\vartheta}\,\|{u}\|_{q}^{2}

(1.1)

with an optimal constant
\mathsf{C}_{q}
such that
\mathscr{C}_{q}^{2}=\vartheta^{\vartheta}(1-\vartheta)^{1-\vartheta}\,\mathsf{C}_{q}
. The equivalence of the two forms can be recovered by optimizing on
\lambda
in (
1.1
). There is also an inequality which is dual of (
1.1
) and goes as follows. Consider a potential
V\in\mathrm{L}^{p}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
. Using Hölder’s inequality with exponents
p
and
q
such that
1/p+2/q=1
and
p>d/2
, and taking
\lambda
so that
\mathsf{C}_{q}\,\lambda^{1-\vartheta}=\|{V}\|_{p}
, we deduce from (
1.1
) that

\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}{|\nabla u|^{2}}\,{\mathrm{d}}x-\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}{V\,|u|^{2}}\,{\mathrm{d}}x\geq\|{\nabla u}\|_{2}^{2}-\|{V}\|_{p}\,\|{u}\|_{q}^{2}\geq-\left(\mathsf{C}_{q}^{-1}\,\|{V}\|_{p}\right)^{\frac{1}{1-\vartheta}}\,\|{u}\|_{2}^{2}\,.

This is the Keller-Lieb-Thirring estimate for
-\Delta-V
,
i.e.
,

\forall\,V\in\mathrm{L}^{p}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})\,,\quad 0\leq\lambda_{S}^{-}(V)\leq\mathsf{K}_{p}\,\|{V}\|_{p}^{\eta}

(1.2)

where
\eta:=1/(1-\vartheta)=2\,p/(2\,p-d)
and
\lambda^{-}:=\max(0,-\lambda)
denotes the negative part of
\lambda
. See
[
22
,
23
,
24
]
for details. An optimization on
V
shows that (
1.1
) and (
1.2
) are equivalent. The optimal constant in (
1.2
) is
\mathsf{K}_{p}=\mathsf{C}_{q}^{-\eta}
. In addition, for all
\lambda>0
, if
u
is a radial positive solution of

-\Delta u-u^{\frac{p+1}{p-1}}=-\,\lambda\,u\,,

(1.3)

then
(u,\lambda)
is an optimal pair for (
1.1
), and
V:=u^{q-2}=u^{2/(p-1)}
is an optimal potential for (
1.2
), which moreover satisfies
\lambda_{S}(V)=-\,\lambda
.
It turns out that the solution of (
1.3
) is unique up to translations according to
[
16
,
49
,
55
]
and can be explicitly computed if
d=1
: see
[
45
]
, or
[
23
]
and references therein for additional related results.

In order to state a
Keller-Lieb-Thirring inequality for the Dirac operator
, we need some definitions and preliminary properties. Let us start with the free Dirac operator on
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
. We refer to
[
66
]
for a comprehensive list of results and properties. For simplicity, we choose units in which
c=1
, except in Appendix
C
in which we consider the non-relativistic limit as
c\to+\infty
. Let
d\geq 1
and set
N:=2^{\lfloor(d+1)/2\rfloor}
where
\lfloor x\rfloor=\max\{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}\,:\,n\leq x\}
denotes the integer part of
x
. Let
\alpha_{1},\cdots,\alpha_{d}
and
\beta
be
N\times N
Hermitian matrices satisfying the following anti-commutation rules

\forall\,j,\,k=1,\dots,d\,,\quad\begin{cases}\alpha_{j}\,\alpha_{k}+\alpha_{k}\,\alpha_{j}=2\,\delta_{jk}\,{\mathbb{I}}_{N}\\
\alpha_{j}\,\beta+\beta\,\alpha_{j}=0\\
\beta^{2}={\mathbb{I}}_{N}\end{cases}

(1.4)

where
\delta_{jk}
denotes the Kronecker symbol and
{\mathbb{I}}_{N}
is the
N\times N
identity matrix. See,
e.g.
,
[
41
]
for an existence result for such matrices. The
free Dirac operator
in dimension
d
is defined by

\not{\mathcal{D}}_{m}:=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\alpha_{j}\,(-\,{\mathrm{i}}\,\partial_{j})+m\,\beta=\boldsymbol{\alpha}\cdot(-\,{\mathrm{i}}\,\nabla)+m\,\beta

where we consider Cartesian coordinates
(x_{1},\dots,x_{d})
,
\partial_{j}:=\partial/\partial x_{j}
and
\boldsymbol{\alpha}=(\alpha_{k})_{k=1,\dots,d}
. With the Pauli matrices

\textstyle\sigma_{1}:=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}\,,\quad\sigma_{2}:=\begin{pmatrix}0&-\,{\mathrm{i}}\\
{\mathrm{i}}&0\end{pmatrix}\quad\mbox{and}\quad\sigma_{3}:=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&-1\end{pmatrix}\,,

explicit expressions of
\not{\mathcal{D}}_{m}
are given

(i)

in dimension
d=1
, by
\boldsymbol{\alpha}=\sigma_{2}
and
\beta=\sigma_{3}
so that

\not{\mathcal{D}}_{m}:=\sigma_{2}\,(-\,{\mathrm{i}}\,\partial_{1})+m\,\sigma_{3}\,,

(ii)

in dimension
d=2
, by
\boldsymbol{\alpha}=(\sigma_{j})_{j=1,2}
and
\beta=\sigma_{3}
so that

\not{\mathcal{D}}_{m}:=\sum_{j=1}^{2}\sigma_{j}(-\,{\mathrm{i}}\,\partial_{j})+m\,\sigma_{3}\,,

(iii)

in dimension
d=3
, by
\boldsymbol{\alpha}=(\alpha_{k})_{k=1,2,3}
and
\beta
such that

\alpha_{k}:=\begin{pmatrix}0&\sigma_{k}\\
\sigma_{k}&0\end{pmatrix}\quad\mbox{and}\quad\beta:=\begin{pmatrix}{\mathbb{I}}_{2}&0\\
0&-{\mathbb{I}}_{2}\end{pmatrix}\,.

The free Dirac operator satisfies
\not{\mathcal{D}}_{m}^{2}=-\,\Delta+m^{2}
. It is self-adjoint on
\mathrm{L}^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d},{\mathbb{C}}^{N})
, with domain

\operatorname{Dom}(\not{\mathcal{D}}_{m})=\mathrm{H}^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{d},{\mathbb{C}}^{N})

and spectrum

\sigma(\not{\mathcal{D}}_{m})=\sigma_{\rm ess}(\not{\mathcal{D}}_{m})=(-\infty,-m]\cup[m,+\infty)\,.

Next we consider
Dirac operators
\not{\mathcal{D}}_{m}-V
with potentials

V\in\mathrm{L}^{p}({\mathbb{R}}^{d},{\mathbb{R}}^{+})
where the notation
\not{\mathcal{D}}_{m}-V
denotes
\not{\mathcal{D}}_{m}-V\mathbb{I}_{N}
. When switching on a potential
V
, we expect that some eigenvalues of
\not{\mathcal{D}}_{m}-V
emerge from the upper essential spectrum
[m,+\infty)
. We shall prove in Section
2
that
\not{\mathcal{D}}_{m}-V
can be defined as a self-adjoint operator with
essential spectrum

\sigma_{\rm ess}(\not{\mathcal{D}}_{m}-V)=\sigma_{\rm ess}(\not{\mathcal{D}}_{m})
. This allows us to define the
ground state

\lambda_{D}(V)
as the lowest eigenvalue in the gap
(-\,m,m)
.

Our first result states that the
ground state
is bounded by a function of
\|{V}\|_{p}
. Let

\Lambda_{D}(\alpha,p):=\inf\Big\{\lambda_{D}(V)\,:\,V\in\mathrm{L}^{p}({\mathbb{R}}^{d},{\mathbb{R}}^{+})\;\mbox{and}\;\|{V}\|_{p}=\alpha\Big\}\,.

(1.5)

Theorem 1.1
.

Assume that
p\geq d\geq 1
. There exists
\alpha_{\star}(p)>0
such that the map
\alpha\mapsto\Lambda_{D}(\alpha,p)
defined on
\big[0,\alpha_{\star}(p)\big)
is continuous, strictly decreasing, takes values in
(-\,m,m]
, and such that

\lim_{\alpha\to 0_{+}}\Lambda_{D}(\alpha,p)=m\quad\mbox{and}\quad\lim_{\alpha\to\alpha_{\star}(p)}\Lambda_{D}(\alpha,p)=-\,m\,.

Moreover, if
(p,d)\neq(1,1)
, the infimum (
1.5
) is attained on
\big(0,\alpha_{\star}(p)\big)
and

\forall\,\alpha\in\big(0,\alpha_{\star}(p)\big)\,,\quad\Lambda_{D}(\alpha,p)=\lambda_{D}(V_{\alpha,p})

where
V_{\alpha,p}=|\Psi|^{2/(p-1)}
, and
\Psi\in\mathrm{L}^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d},{\mathbb{C}}^{N})
solves the
nonlinear Dirac equation

\not{\mathcal{D}}_{m}\Psi-|\Psi|^{\frac{2}{p-1}}\,\Psi=\Lambda_{D}(\alpha,p)\,\Psi

(1.6)

and satisfies the constraint
\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}{|\Psi|^{2\,p/(p-1)}}\,{\mathrm{d}}x=\|{V_{\alpha,p}}\|_{p}^{p}=\alpha^{p}
.

The proof of Theorem
1.1
is given in Section
3
and relies on the properties of the inverse map of
\alpha\mapsto\Lambda_{D}(\alpha,p)
defined by

\alpha_{D}(\lambda,p):=\inf\Big\{\|{V}\|_{p}\,:\,V\in\mathrm{L}^{p}({\mathbb{R}}^{d},{\mathbb{R}}^{+})\;\mbox{and}\;\lambda_{D}(V)=\lambda\Big\}\,.

(1.7)

The critical value is
\alpha_{\star}(p)=\lim_{\lambda\to(-m)_{+}}\alpha_{D}(\lambda,p)
. It is such that

\lim_{\alpha\to\alpha_{\star}(p)_{-}}\lambda_{D}(V_{\alpha,p})=-\,m

and this limit is the upper bound of the lower essential spectrum
(-\infty,-m]
or, equivalently, the lower end of the gap. For sake of simplicity, we adopt the convention that
\alpha_{\star}(p)=\alpha_{D}(-\,m,p)
. In the subcritical range of potentials, a simple consequence of Theorem
1.1
is the following
Keller-Lieb-Thirring estimate
for the Dirac operator
\not{\mathcal{D}}_{m}-V
.

Corollary 1.2
.

Assume
p\geq d\geq 1
. For all
V\in\mathrm{L}^{p}({\mathbb{R}}^{d},{\mathbb{R}}^{+})
with
\|{V}\|_{p}<\alpha_{\star}(p)
, we
have the optimal bound

-\,m\leq\Lambda_{D}\left(\|{V}\|_{p},p\right)\leq\lambda_{D}(V)\leq m\,.

(1.8)

If
(p,d)\neq(1,1)
, then
V_{\alpha,p}
as in Theorem
1.1
realizes the equality case,
i.e.
,
\lambda_{D}(V_{\alpha,p})=\Lambda_{D}\left(\alpha,p\right)
.

The nonlinear Dirac equation (
1.6
) plays for the Dirac operator
\not{\mathcal{D}}_{m}-V
the same role as (
1.3

-\Delta+V

\Lambda_{D}(\alpha,V)

Nonlinear Dirac equations have been introduced to model extended fermions, as effective operators for nonlinear effects in graphene-like materials or Bose-Einstein condensates: see
[
32
, Section 1.6]
and
[
5
, Introduction]
for an introduction to the literature. Since the spinors in the Dirac equation have at least two components, many types of nonlinearities can be considered (see,
e.g.
,
[
59
]
and references therein) and give rise to various phenomena. For instance, localized solutions to a nonlinear equation of the form

\not{\mathcal{D}}_{m}\Psi-G(\Psi)=\lambda\,\Psi

for some function
G:{\mathbb{C}}^{N}\mapsto{\mathbb{C}}^{N}
correspond to solitary wave solutions to the time-dependent nonlinear Dirac equation and have attracted considerable attention: see,
e.g.
,
[
15
,
4
,
56
,
38
]
.

It is a common assumption to consider a nonlinearity that preserves Lorentz, or particle-hole, symmetry. Such a non-linearity takes the form

\not{\mathcal{D}}_{m}\Psi-F\big(\left\langle\Psi,\beta\,\Psi\right\rangle_{{\mathbb{C}}^{N}}\big)\,\Psi=\lambda\,\Psi

(1.9)

and is called the
Soler-type nonlinearity
. The Soler nonlinearity formally appears when minimizing the first positive eigenvalue of
\not{\mathcal{D}}_{m}-\beta\,V
but will not be studied in this paper. In contrast, the nonlinearity that appears in (
1.6
) is of the form
F(\langle\Psi,\Psi\rangle_{{\mathbb{C}}^{N}})\,\Psi
, which is sometimes called a
Kerr-type nonlinearity
as in
[
5
]
, apparently by extension of the cubic nonlinearity used in optics. Existence of localized solutions for (
1.6
) is studied in
[
5
]
in the critical exponent case
p=d=2
, and in
[
8
]
in the critical exponent case
p=d
for all dimensions

with

. Our results give an independent proof of the existence of a localized solution.

In
[
4
,
38
]
, the authors proved that equations of the form (

d\geq 2
by looking for solutions of (
1.9

Theorem 1.3

Let

\not{\mathcal{D}}_{m}\Psi-|\Psi|^{\frac{2}{p-1}}\,\Psi=\lambda\,\Psi\quad\mbox{with}\quad\not{\mathcal{D}}_{m}:=\begin{pmatrix}m&\partial_{x}\\

has a unique solution

Subcritical regime

With
\mathsf{A}:=\frac{p}{p-1}\,\big(m^{2}-\lambda^{2}\big)

If

\lim_{\lambda\to(-m)_{+}}\alpha_{D}(\lambda,1)=\lim_{p\to 1_{+}}\alpha_{\star}(p)=\pi\,.

Our results are not limited to estimates for the ground state and we also have a
Lieb-Thirring inequality
for the sum of eigenvalues
in the gap

(-\,m,m)
of Dirac operators of the form
\not{\mathcal{D}}_{m}-V
with
V\in\mathrm{L}^{p}({\mathbb{R}}^{d},{\mathbb{R}}^{+})
. We denote by
-\,m<\lambda_{1}\leq\lambda_{2}\leq\cdots<m
the possibly infinite sequence of eigenvalues in the gap
(-\,m,m)
, and write

e_{k}=e_{k}(m,V):=(m-\lambda_{k})>0\,,

so that
2\,m>e_{1}\geq e_{2}\geq\cdots>0
. The quantity
e_{k}
is the distance between the eigenvalue
\lambda_{k}
and the bottom of the upper essential spectrum
+\,m

Theorem 1.4
.

For all
\gamma>d/2
and
p\in(d,\gamma+d/2]
, there is a constant
L_{\gamma,d,p}>0
so that, for all
V\in\mathrm{L}^{p}({\mathbb{R}}^{d},{\mathbb{R}}^{+})
, and all
m>0
, we have

\sum_{k\geq 1}e_{k}^{\gamma}(m,V)\leq L_{\gamma,d,p}\,m^{\frac{d}{2}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}V_{m}^{\gamma+\frac{d}{2}-p}\,V^{p}\,{\mathrm{d}}x\quad\text{with}\quad V_{m}:=\min\left\{m,V\right\}\,.

(1.13)

If
V\in\mathrm{L}^{p}({\mathbb{R}}^{d},{\mathbb{R}}^{+})\cap\mathrm{L}^{\gamma+d/2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d},{\mathbb{R}}^{+})
, using the inequalities
V_{m}\leq m
and
V_{m}\leq V
gives respectively

\sum_{k\geq 1}e_{k}^{\gamma}\leq L_{\gamma,d,p}\,m^{\gamma+d-p}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\,V^{p}\,{\mathrm{d}}x\quad\text{and}\quad\sum_{k\geq 1}e_{k}^{\gamma}\leq L_{\gamma,d,p}\,m^{\frac{d}{2}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}V^{\gamma+\frac{d}{2}}\,{\mathrm{d}}x\,.

The inequality (
1.13
) is, in some sense, an interpolation between these two critical cases. In the proof, we use rough estimates: the method is constructive but there is a lot of space for improving on the constant
L_{\gamma,d,p}
.

This paper is organized as follows. In Section

2
Properties of Dirac operators

2.1
A self-adjoint realization

We assume that
V\in\mathrm{L}^{p}({\mathbb{R}}^{d},{\mathbb{R}}^{+})
is positive valued and deal with the self-adjoint extensions of
\not{\mathcal{D}}_{m}-V
.

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
p\geq d\geq 1
and
V\in\mathrm{L}^{p}({\mathbb{R}}^{d},{\mathbb{R}}^{+})
. Then the operator
\not{\mathcal{D}}_{m}-V
is self-adjoint with domain:

\textstyle\operatorname{Dom}(\not{\mathcal{D}}_{m}-V):=\Big\{\psi\in\mathrm{L}^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d},{\mathbb{C}}^{N}):\sqrt{V}\psi,\,(\not{\mathcal{D}}_{m}-V)\,\psi\in\mathrm{L}^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d},{\mathbb{C}}^{N})\Big\}\,.

This is the unique self-adjoint realisation verifying:

\mathrm{H}^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{d},{\mathbb{C}}^{N})\subseteq\operatorname{Dom}(\not{\mathcal{D}}_{m}-V)\subseteq\mathrm{H}^{\frac{1}{2}}({\mathbb{R}}^{d},{\mathbb{C}}^{N})\,.

Moreover, we have the following properties.

(i)

If
p
satisfies

\begin{cases}p\geq 2&\quad\mbox{if}\quad d=1\,,\\
p>2&\quad\mbox{if}\quad d=2\,,\\
p\geq d&\quad\mbox{if}\quad d\geq 3\,,\end{cases}

(2.1)

then
\operatorname{Dom}(\not{\mathcal{D}}_{m}-V)=\mathrm{H}^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{d},{\mathbb{C}}^{N})
.

(ii)

If
1<p\leq 2
and
d=1
, then
\operatorname{Dom}(\not{\mathcal{D}}_{m}-V)
is also included in
\mathrm{H}^{\frac{3}{2}-\frac{1}{p}}({\mathbb{R}},{\mathbb{C}}^{2})
, hence in
\mathrm{L}^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}},{\mathbb{C}}^{2})
.

We call the extension of Proposition
2.1
the
distinguished
extension, as it is the unique one whose domain is included in the formal form domain
\operatorname{Dom}(|\not{\mathcal{D}}_{m}|^{1/2})=\mathrm{H}^{1/2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d},{\mathbb{C}}^{N})
. We will consider only this extension in what follows, so that the operator
\not{\mathcal{D}}_{m}-V
is self-adjoint under the condition
p\geq d\geq 1
. The proof of the first part of Proposition
2.1
follows from
[
57
]
. For completeness, we provide a short proof using the associated Birman-Schwinger operator. Under Condition (
2.1
), the point
(i)
comes from the usual Kato-Rellich theorem
[
62
,
43
]
. The result in
(ii)
is derived by bootstrapping the Sobolev embedding theorem. See Section
2.3
for the proof of Proposition
2.1
.

Remark 2.2
.

For comparison, it is interesting to consider limit cases. The Coulomb potential
V(x)=1/|x|
in dimension
d=3
is in the weak Sobolev space
\mathrm{L}^{3}_{w}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
. The operator
\not{\mathcal{D}}_{m}-\kappa\,V
is essentially self-adjoint if
0\leq\kappa\leq\sqrt{3}/2
, it has a distinguished extension if
\sqrt{3}/2<\kappa\leq 1
, and no distinguished self-adjoint extension if
\kappa>1
: see
[
13
]
and references therein. Also see Remark
2.5
.

2.2
The Birman-Schwinger operator

The Birman-Schwinger operator is a powerful tool for analysing the spectral properties of
\not{\mathcal{D}}_{m}-V
when
V
belongs to a large class of perturbations.
In the relativistic case, Klaus in
[
46
]
used it extensively to characterize and study the first eigenvalue of Dirac operators when proving the existence of a distinguished self-adjoint extension. For non-Hermitian potentials
V
, it can be employed to locate the eigenvalues of
\not{\mathcal{D}}_{m}-V
, as shown for example by Cuenin, Laptev and Tretter in
[
18
]
, and by Fanelli and Krejčiřík in
[
39
]
.
Furthermore, it can be applied to discuss properties of the ground state of
\not{\mathcal{D}}_{m}-V
when
V
is a generalised Coulomb-type potential, see,
e.g.
,
[
46
,
14
,
34
,
35
]
.
Throughout this paper, following the approach by Kato
[
44
]
and by Konno and Kuroda
[
48
]
, the Birman-Schwinger operator is used to define the self-adjoint extension of the operator
\not{\mathcal{D}}_{m}-V
.
Then, with this rigorous definition at hand, we prove the existence of the optimization problem which defines the
ground state
by applying variational methods directly on the Birman-Schwinger reformulation of the problem.

For
z\notin\sigma(\not{\mathcal{D}}_{m})
, let

R_{0}(z):=(\not{\mathcal{D}}_{m}-z)^{-1}

(2.2)

denote the resolvent operator. Recall that we assume
V\geq 0
. We introduce the
Birman-Schwinger operator

K_{V}(z):=\sqrt{V}\,R_{0}(z)\,\sqrt{V}=\sqrt{V}\,\dfrac{1}{\not{\mathcal{D}}_{m}-z}\,\sqrt{V}\,.

(2.3)

Lemma 2.3
.

For all
p\geq d\geq 1
, all
V\in\mathrm{L}^{p}({\mathbb{R}}^{d},{\mathbb{R}}^{+})
and all
z\notin\sigma(\not{\mathcal{D}}_{m})
, the operator
K_{V}(z)
is compact (hence bounded). In addition,

\lim_{s\to\pm\infty}\left\|K_{V}({\mathrm{i}}\,s)\right\|_{\rm op}=0\,.

This result follows from the
Kato-Seiler-Simon inequality
: see proof in Section
2.3
. A consequence of Lemma
2.3
is the following result (also see Section
2.3
for the proof).

Proposition 2.4
.

Let
\not{\mathcal{D}}_{m}-V
be the distinguished self-adjoint extension defined as in Proposition
2.1
. Then

\sigma_{\rm ess}(\not{\mathcal{D}}_{m}-V)=\sigma_{\rm ess}(\not{\mathcal{D}}_{m})=(-\infty,-m]\cup[m,+\infty)\,.

Moreover the
Birman-Schwinger principle
holds: for all
\lambda\in(-\,m,m)
,
\lambda
is an eigenvalue of
\not{\mathcal{D}}_{m}-V
if and only if
1
is an eigenvalue of
K_{V}(\lambda)
.

Let us point out some differences with Birman-Schwinger operators associated with Schrödinger operators (see Figure

In the Schrödinger case, the Birman-Schwinger operator is of the form

\widetilde{K_{V}}(\lambda)=\sqrt{V}\,\dfrac{1}{-\Delta-\lambda}\,\sqrt{V}\,.

2.3
Proofs of Proposition
2.1
, Lemma
2.3
and Proposition
2.4

We start by establishing that
K_{V}
defined by (
2.3
) is a compact operator (Lemma
2.3
) before proving Propositions
2.1
and
2.4
.

Proof of Lemma
2.3
.

Assume first that
p>d
. We claim that, for
z\notin\sigma(\not{\mathcal{D}}_{m})
, the operator
K_{V}(z)
is compact. We have
R_{0}(z)=g_{z}(-{\mathrm{i}}\,\nabla)
with

g_{z}(k):=\dfrac{1}{|k|^{2}+m^{2}-z^{2}}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{d}\alpha_{j}\,k_{j}+m\,\beta+z\,{\mathbb{I}}_{N}\right)=:\sum_{j=1}^{d}g_{z}^{j}(k)+g_{z}^{m}(k)+g_{z}^{z}(k)\,,

with obvious notation. Let us focus on the
g_{z}^{1}(k)
term. We write
g_{z}^{1}(k)=g_{z}^{A}(k)\,g_{z}^{B}(k)
with

g_{z}^{A}(k):=\dfrac{\sqrt{|k_{1}|}\,{\rm sgn}(k_{1})}{\sqrt{|k|^{2}+m^{2}-z^{2}}}\,\alpha_{1}\quad\mbox{and}\quad g_{z}^{B}(k):=\dfrac{\sqrt{|k_{1}|}}{\sqrt{|k|^{2}+m^{2}-z^{2}}}\,\mathbb{I}_{d}\,.

All components of the functions
g_{z}^{A}
and
g_{z}^{B}
are in
\mathrm{L}^{q}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
for all
q>2\,d
. Since
\sqrt{V}
is in
\mathrm{L}^{2p}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
with
2\,p>2\,d
, we can use the Kato-Seiler-Simon inequality
[
65
, Chapter 4, Theorem 4.1]
and conclude that the operator

K_{V}^{1}(z):=\sqrt{V}\,g_{z}^{1}(-{\mathrm{i}}\,\nabla)\,\sqrt{V}

is in the Schatten class
{\mathfrak{S}}_{p}\big(\mathrm{L}^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d},{\mathbb{C}}^{N})\big)
with

\|K_{V}^{1}(z)\|_{{\mathfrak{S}}_{p}}\leq\left\|\sqrt{V(x)}\,g_{z}^{A}(-{\mathrm{i}}\,\nabla)\right\|_{{\mathfrak{S}}_{2p}}\,\left\|g_{z}^{B}(-{\mathrm{i}}\,\nabla)\,\sqrt{V(x)}\,\right\|_{{\mathfrak{S}}_{2p}}\leq C\,\|V\|_{p}\,\|g_{z}^{1}\|_{p}\,.

In addition, we have
\|g_{z=is}^{1}\|_{p}\to 0
as
s\to\pm\infty
. Similar computation for the other terms shows that
K_{V}
is in the Schatten class
{\mathfrak{S}}_{p}
, and that
\lim_{s\to\pm\infty}\|K_{V}({\mathrm{i}}\,s)\|_{\rm op}=0
.

In the case
p=d
with
d\geq 2
, we use that all components of the functions
g_{z}^{A}
and
g_{z}^{B}
are in the weak-Sobolev space
\mathrm{L}^{2\,d}_{w}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
. According to
[
65
, Chapter 4]
,
g_{z}^{A}(-{\mathrm{i}}\,\nabla)\,\sqrt{V}
and
\sqrt{V}\,g_{z}^{B}(-{\mathrm{i}}\,\nabla)
are in the weak Schatten class
{\mathfrak{S}}_{2d,w}
. In particular, they are compact operators. This already proves that
K_{V}
is compact as well. Note that
\|g_{z={\mathrm{i}}\,s}^{1}\|_{d,w}
does not converge to
0
as
s\to\pm\infty
.

For any
R>0
,
\sqrt{V_{R}}:=\min\big(\sqrt{V},R\big)
belongs to
\mathrm{L}^{d}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})\cap\mathrm{L}^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
. We have

\left\|g_{z}^{A}(-{\mathrm{i}}\,\nabla)\,\sqrt{V}\right\|_{\rm op}\leq\left\|g_{z}^{A}(-{\mathrm{i}}\,\nabla)\,\sqrt{V_{R}}\right\|_{\rm op}+\left\|g_{z}^{A}(-{\mathrm{i}}\,\nabla)\,\big(\sqrt{V}-\sqrt{V_{R}}\big)\right\|_{\rm op}\,.

For
R
large enough, the second term is small in the Schatten space
{\mathfrak{S}}_{2d,w}
, and for
z={\mathrm{i}}\,s
with
|s|
large, the first term is small in
{\mathfrak{S}}_{q}
with
q>p
. Hence
\lim_{s\to\pm\infty}\|K_{V}({\mathrm{i}}\,s)\|_{\rm op}=0
.

Let us finally assume
p=d=1
. In this case, with explicit computations, the kernel of the Birman-Schwinger operator
K_{V}(z)
is given by

\sqrt{V(x)}\cdot\frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix}\frac{z+m}{k}&\operatorname{sign}(x-y)\\
\operatorname{sign}(x-y)&\frac{z-m}{k}\end{pmatrix}{\mathrm{e}}^{-k\,|x-y|}\cdot\sqrt{V(y)}\in\mathrm{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{{\mathbb{R}}},\mathbb{C}^{2})

where
k=\sqrt{m^{2}-z^{2}}
is chosen with a positive real part. Thus,
K_{V}
is a Hilbert-Schmidt operator (hence it is compact) and by the dominated convergence theorem we can conclude that

\lim_{s\to\pm\infty}\|K_{V}({\mathrm{i}}\,s)\|_{\rm op}=0
.
∎

Proof of Proposition
2.1
.

We divide the proof in several steps.

Distinguished self-adjoint extension.

We define the domain of self-adjointness for the operator
\not{\mathcal{D}}_{m}-V
as a perturbation of
\not{\mathcal{D}}_{m}
by applying the method of G. Nenciu in
[
57
]
.
Using similar techniques as in the proof of Lemma
2.3
, one can show that the operators
R_{0}(z)\,\sqrt{V}
and
\sqrt{V}\,R_{0}(z)
can be extended into bounded linear operators on
\mathrm{L}^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d},{\mathbb{C}}^{N})
. These operators are compact operators, in the Schatten class
{\mathfrak{S}}_{2p}
. We are now in the setting of
[
57
]
. Let
\Omega:=\{z\in{\mathbb{C}}\,:\,1\notin\sigma\big(K_{V}(z)\big)\}
where
K_{V}
is defined by (
2.3
). The set

\Omega
is non-empty by Lemma
2.3
. For
z\in\Omega
, define

R(z):=R_{0}(z)+R_{0}(z)\,\sqrt{V}\,\big(1-K_{V}(z)\big)^{-1}\,\sqrt{V}\,R_{0}(z)\,.

According to
[
57
]
, the operator
\not{\mathcal{D}}_{m}-V
has a unique self-adjoint extension whose resolvent is the operator
R(z)
defined in (
2.2
). Its domain is
\operatorname{Dom}(\not{\mathcal{D}}_{m}-V):={\rm Ran}\,R(z)
, which is independent of
z\in\Omega
. This is the unique extension which is included in the formal form domain
\operatorname{Dom}(|\not{\mathcal{D}}_{m}|^{1/2})=\mathrm{H}^{1/2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d},{\mathbb{C}}^{N})
.

Domain of the distinguished extension.

Define the
maximal domain
as

\operatorname{Dom}_{\rm max}(\not{\mathcal{D}}_{m}-V):=\big\{\psi\in\mathrm{L}^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d},{\mathbb{C}}^{N})\,:\,(\not{\mathcal{D}}_{m}-V)\,\psi\in\mathrm{L}^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d},{\mathbb{C}}^{N})\big\}.

Then, the set

\left\{\psi\in\mathrm{L}^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d},{\mathbb{C}}^{N})\,:\,\sqrt{V}\,\psi,\;(\not{\mathcal{D}}_{m}-V)\psi\in\mathrm{L}^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d},{\mathbb{C}}^{N})\right\}

is also
\operatorname{Dom}_{\rm max}(\not{\mathcal{D}}_{m}-V)\cap\operatorname{Dom}\big(\sqrt{V}\big)
. We write
\psi\in\operatorname{Dom}(\not{\mathcal{D}}_{m}-V)
as
R(z)\,f
for some
f\in\mathrm{L}^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d},{\mathbb{C}}^{N})
.
Then

\sqrt{V}\,\psi=\sqrt{V}\,R_{0}(z)\,f+K_{V}\,\big(1-K_{V}(z)\big)^{-1}\sqrt{V}\,R_{0}(z)\,f\in\mathrm{L}^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d},{\mathbb{C}}^{N})\,.

This proves that
\operatorname{Dom}(\not{\mathcal{D}}_{m}-V)\subset\operatorname{Dom}_{\rm max}(\not{\mathcal{D}}_{m}-V)\cap\operatorname{Dom}\big(\sqrt{V}\big)
. For the opposite inclusion, consider
\psi\in\operatorname{Dom}_{\rm max}(\not{\mathcal{D}}_{m}-V)\cap\operatorname{Dom}\big(\sqrt{V}\big)
. We set
f:=(\not{\mathcal{D}}_{m}+V-z)\,\psi\in\mathrm{L}^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d},{\mathbb{C}}^{N})
and
\psi_{0}=R(z)\,f\in\operatorname{Dom}(\not{\mathcal{D}}_{m}-V)
. Note that
\sqrt{V}\,\psi_{0}\in\mathrm{L}^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d},{\mathbb{C}}^{N})
, since

\sqrt{V}\,R(z)=\sqrt{V}\,R_{0}(z)+K_{V}(z)\,\big(1-K_{V}(z)\big)^{-1}\,\sqrt{V}\,R_{0}(z)

is a bounded operator on
\mathrm{L}^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d},{\mathbb{C}}^{N})
. So
\phi:=\psi-\psi_{0}
is such that

\big(\not{\mathcal{D}}_{m}-V-z\big)\,\phi=0\quad\mbox{and}\quad\phi\in\operatorname{Dom}\big(\sqrt{V}\big)\,.

From the relation

\big(1-R_{0}(z)\big)^{-1}\,\sqrt{V}\,R_{0}(z)\,\big(\not{\mathcal{D}}_{m}-V-z\big)=\sqrt{V}\,,

we obtain
\sqrt{V}\,\phi=0
, and from the relation

R_{0}(z)\,\big(\not{\mathcal{D}}_{m}-V-z\big)=1-R_{0}(z)\,V=1-\big(R_{0}(z)\,\sqrt{V}\,\big)\,\sqrt{V}\,,

we finally get
\phi=0
, hence
\psi=\psi_{0}\in\operatorname{Dom}(\not{\mathcal{D}}_{m}-V)
.

Finally, if
p\geq d\geq 1
by the Hölder inequality and the Sobolev embedding theorem we get that
\mathrm{H}^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{d},{\mathbb{C}}^{N})\subseteq\operatorname{Dom}(\not{\mathcal{D}}_{m}-V)
and this concludes the first part of the proof.

Self-adjointness on
\mathrm{H}^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{d},{\mathbb{C}}^{N})
.

Let us prove
(i)
.
Assume that
p
satisfies (
2.1
). Thanks to (
1.4
) we have

\forall\,\psi\in\mathrm{H}^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d},{\mathbb{C}}^{N})\,,\quad\left\|\not{\mathcal{D}}_{m}\psi\right\|_{2}^{2}=\|\nabla\psi\|_{2}^{2}+m^{2}\,\|\psi\|_{2}^{2}\,.

This shows that the graph norm of
\not{\mathcal{D}}_{m}
is equivalent to the usual
\mathrm{H}^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{d},{\mathbb{C}}^{N})
norm. Set

q:=\begin{cases}\frac{2\,p}{p-2}&\quad\mbox{if}\quad p>2\\
+\infty&\quad\mbox{if $d=1$ and $p=2$}\end{cases}\quad\mbox{so that}\quad\frac{1}{p}+\frac{1}{q}=\frac{1}{2}\,.

We write
V=V_{1}+V_{2}
with
V_{1}:=V\,{\mathds{1}}_{V\geq R}
and
V_{2}:=V\,{\mathds{1}}_{V\leq R}
. We have

\displaystyle\|V\,\psi\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\|V_{1}\,\psi\|_{2}+\|V_{2}\,\psi\|_{2}\leq\|V_{1}\|_{p}\,\|\psi\|_{q}+\|V_{2}\|_{\infty}\,\|\psi\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq C_{S}\,\|V_{1}\|_{p}\,\|\psi\|_{\mathrm{H}^{1}}+\|V_{2}\|_{\infty}\,\|\psi\|_{2}

where, in the last inequality, we used Sobolev’s embedding
\mathrm{H}^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})\hookrightarrow\mathrm{L}^{q}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
and, according to (
2.1
),
q
satisfies

\begin{cases}2\leq q\leq+\infty&\quad\mbox{if}\quad d=1\,,\\
2\leq q<+\infty&\quad\mbox{if}\quad d=2\,,\\
2\leq q\leq\frac{2\,d}{d-2}&\quad\mbox{if}\quad d\geq 3\,.\end{cases}

We choose
R
large enough so that
C_{S}\,\|V_{1}\|_{p}<1
and conclude with the
Kato-Rellich theorem
(see
[
60
, Theorem X.12]
) that
\not{\mathcal{D}}_{m}-V
is self-adjoint with domain
\mathrm{H}^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{d},{\mathbb{C}}^{N})
.
Since any self-adjoint operator only admits trivial self-adjoint extensions, we can conclude that
\mathrm{H}^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{d},{\mathbb{C}}^{N})=\operatorname{Dom}(\not{\mathcal{D}}_{m}-V)
.

Regularity for
d=1
.

Let us focus on
(ii)
and assume that
d=1
and
1<p\leq 2
. Let us prove that
\operatorname{Dom}(\not{\mathcal{D}}_{m}-V)
is also included in
\mathrm{H}^{\frac{3}{2}-\frac{1}{p}}({\mathbb{R}},{\mathbb{C}}^{2})
. For any
\psi\in\operatorname{Dom}(\not{\mathcal{D}}_{m}-V)
, we have

(\not{\mathcal{D}}_{m}-V)\,\psi=:f\,\in\mathrm{L}^{2}({\mathbb{R}},{\mathbb{C}}^{2})\,,

hence
\not{\mathcal{D}}_{m}\,\psi=f+V\psi
. We recall the following negative Sobolev embeddings: for all
1<r\leq 2
, we have
\mathrm{L}^{r}({\mathbb{R}})\hookrightarrow\mathrm{H}^{-s}({\mathbb{R}})
for all
s\geq\frac{2-r}{2\,r}
and
\mathrm{L}^{2}({\mathbb{R}})\hookrightarrow\mathrm{H}^{-s}({\mathbb{R}})
for all
s\geq 0
, while

\forall\,s\geq\tfrac{1}{2\,p}\,,\quad V\,\psi=\sqrt{V}\,\underbrace{\big(\sqrt{V}\,\psi\big)}_{\in\mathrm{L}^{2}({\mathbb{R}},{\mathbb{C}}^{2})}\in\mathrm{L}^{\frac{2\,p}{p+1}}({\mathbb{R}},{\mathbb{C}}^{2})\hookrightarrow\mathrm{H}^{-s}({\mathbb{R}},{\mathbb{C}}^{2})\,.

We deduce that
\not{\mathcal{D}}_{m}\psi\in\mathrm{H}^{-\frac{1}{2\,p}}({\mathbb{R}},{\mathbb{C}}^{2})
, hence that
\psi\in\mathrm{H}^{1-\frac{1}{2\,p}}({\mathbb{R}},{\mathbb{C}}^{2})
. We now bootstrap the argument. For
p>1
, we have
1-\frac{1}{2\,p}>\frac{1}{2}
, so
\psi\in\mathrm{L}^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}},{\mathbb{C}}^{2})
by Sobolev’s embedding. This gives
V\,\psi\in\mathrm{L}^{p}({\mathbb{R}},{\mathbb{C}}^{2})\hookrightarrow\mathrm{H}^{\frac{p-2}{2\,p}}({\mathbb{R}},{\mathbb{C}}^{2})
. So
\not{\mathcal{D}}_{m}\psi=f-V\,\psi\in\mathrm{H}^{\frac{p-2}{2\,p}}({\mathbb{R}},{\mathbb{C}}^{2})
as well, and we obtain
\psi\in\mathrm{H}^{1+\frac{p-2}{2\,p}}({\mathbb{R}},{\mathbb{C}}^{2})
with
1+\frac{p-2}{2\,p}=\frac{3}{2}-\frac{1}{p}
, as wanted.
∎

3
The variational problem

In this section, we consider the minimization problem (
2.4
) and prove Theorem
1.1
in a reformulation which relies on the Birman-Schwinger operator associated to
\not{\mathcal{D}}_{m}-V
, as introduced in Section
2.2
. The proof of Theorem
1.1
is given below, right after the statement of Corollary
3.2
, as a simple consequence of previous results in the Birman-Schwinger framework.

3.1
An auxiliary maximization problem

First, we notice that, for all
t>0
, we have
K_{tV}(\lambda)=t\,K_{V}(\lambda)
, hence
\mu_{1}\big(K_{tV}(\lambda)\big)=t\,\mu_{1}\big(K_{V}(\lambda)\big)
. So, introducing the auxiliary problem

{\mathcal{N}}(\lambda,p):=\sup\Big\{\mu_{1}\big(K_{W}(\lambda)\big)\,:\,W\in\mathrm{L}^{p}({\mathbb{R}}^{d},{\mathbb{R}}^{+})\,,\;\|W\|_{p}=1\Big\}\,,

(3.1)

we deduce that

\alpha_{D}(\lambda,p)=\frac{1}{{\mathcal{N}}(\lambda,p)}\,.

(3.2)

If
W
is a maximizer for
{\mathcal{N}}(\lambda,p)
, then
V=W/{\mathcal{N}}(\lambda,p)
is a minimizer for
\alpha_{D}(\lambda,p)
.
In what follows, we study the maximization problem (
3.1
). We perform several changes of variables to study this problem. First, the min-max principle shows that
{\mathcal{N}}(\lambda,p)
equals

{\mathcal{N}}(\lambda,p)=\sup_{\begin{array}[]{c}W\in\mathrm{L}^{p}({\mathbb{R}}^{d},{\mathbb{R}}^{+})\\
\|W\|_{p}=1\end{array}}\sup_{\begin{array}[]{c}\phi\in\mathrm{L}^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d},{\mathbb{C}}^{N})\\
\|\phi\|_{2}=1\end{array}}\left\langle\phi,\sqrt{W}\,R_{0}(\lambda)\,\sqrt{W}\phi\right\rangle\,.

We make the change of variable

w:=\sqrt{W}\phi

so that, by Hölder’s inequality,
w\in\mathrm{L}^{\frac{2\,p}{p+1}}({\mathbb{R}}^{d},{\mathbb{C}}^{N})
, and, with the convention that
\|{w}\|_{r}=\|{|w|_{{\mathbb{C}}^{N}}}\|_{r}
,

\left\|w\right\|_{\frac{2\,p}{p+1}}\leq\|W\|_{p}^{\frac{1}{2}}\,\|\phi\|_{2}=1\,.

In addition, there is equality if and only if
\mathrm{W}^{p}
is proportional to
|\phi|^{2}
, both proportional to
|w|^{\frac{2\,p}{p+1}}
. With

q:=\frac{2\,p}{p+1}\in(1,2)\,,

this shows that
{\mathcal{N}}(\lambda,p)
is also solution to the optimization problem

{\mathcal{N}}(\lambda,p)=\sup\Big\{\big\langle w,R_{0}(\lambda)\,w\big\rangle\,:\,w\in\mathrm{L}^{q}({\mathbb{R}}^{d},{\mathbb{C}}^{N})\,,\;\|w\|_{q}=1\Big\}\,.

(3.3)

In addition, if
w\in\mathrm{L}^{q}({\mathbb{R}}^{d},{\mathbb{R}}^{+})
is an optimizer of (
3.3
), then the corresponding optimal
W
and
\phi
are given by

W=|w|^{\frac{q}{p}}=|w|^{\frac{2}{p+1}}\quad\mbox{and}\quad\phi=|w|^{\frac{q}{2}-1}\,w=|w|^{-\frac{1}{p+1}}\,w\,.

Thus, by showing the existence of an optimizer for (
3.3
), we solve problem (
3.1
), and by definition of the Birman-Schwinger operator, find an optimal potential and eigenfunction for our original problem (
1.7
).

Since
\alpha\mapsto\Lambda_{D}(\alpha,p)
is the inverse map of
\lambda\mapsto\alpha_{D}(\lambda,p)
according to (
3.2
), and since
\alpha_{D}(\lambda,p)=1/{\mathcal{N}}(\lambda,p)
, it is enough to focus on the properties of
{\mathcal{N}}(\cdot,p)
.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let us consider
{\mathcal{N}}
defined by (
3.3
).
For all
\lambda\in(-\,m,m)
and all
p>d
, we have
{\mathcal{N}}(\lambda,p)>0
. All maximizing sequences for (
3.3
) are precompact up to translations, hence (
3.3
) has maximizers. If
w
is such an optimizer, then
w
satisfies the Euler-Lagrange equation

R_{0}(\lambda)\,w=\tau\,|w|^{-\frac{2}{p+1}}w\quad\mbox{with}\quad\tau={\mathcal{N}}(\lambda,p)\,.

(3.4)

Finally, the map
\lambda\mapsto{\mathcal{N}}(\lambda,p)
is continuous, strictly increasing, and satisfies

\lim_{\lambda\to-m}{\mathcal{N}}(\lambda,p)=:{\mathcal{N}}_{c}(p)>0\quad\text{and}\quad\lim_{\lambda\to+m}{\mathcal{N}}(\lambda,p)=\infty.

The proof of the first part relies on the profile decomposition method (concentration-compactness) used by Lions
[
54
]
, and is given in the next section. Theorem
3.1
implies the existence of an optimal potential and an optimal spinor.

Corollary 3.2
.

Under the assumptions of Theorem
1.1
, the infimum (
2.4
) is attained for any
\lambda\in(-\,m,m)
by a potential
V=|\Psi|^{2/(p-1)}
, where
\Psi\in\mathrm{L}^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d},{\mathbb{R}}^{N})
solves the
nonlinear Dirac equation

\not{\mathcal{D}}_{m}\Psi-|\Psi|^{\frac{2}{p-1}}\,\Psi=\lambda\,\Psi\,,

(3.5)

such that
\lambda_{D}(V)=\lambda
and
\big(\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}{|\Psi|^{2\,p/(p-1)}}\,{\mathrm{d}}x\big)^{1/p}=\|{V}\|_{p}=\alpha_{D}(\lambda,p)=1/{\mathcal{N}}(\lambda,p)
.

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

Our main Theorem
1.1
is a direct consequence of Theorem
3.1
and Corollary
3.2
. Since
{\mathcal{N}}_{c}(p)>0
, we have indeed
\alpha_{c}(p):=1/{\mathcal{N}}_{c}(p)<\infty
.
∎

Proof of Corollary
3.2
.

First, we translate the Euler-Lagrange equation for
w
into an equation for the potential
V
and an eigenfunction (not normalized)
\Psi
. We set

\Psi=\tau^{\frac{1-p}{2}}\,|w|^{-\frac{2}{p+1}}\,w\quad\mbox{so that}\quad w=\tau^{\frac{p+1}{2}}\,|\Psi|^{\frac{2}{p-1}}\,\Psi\,.

Applying
\not{\mathcal{D}}_{m}-\lambda
to (
3.4
) shows that
\Psi
satisfies the nonlinear Dirac equation (
3.5
). The optimal potential
W
for the
{\mathcal{N}}(\lambda,p)
problem in (
3.1
) is
W=|w|^{\frac{2}{p+1}}=\tau\,|\Psi|^{\frac{2}{p-1}}
, and finally, the optimal potential
V
for the
\alpha_{D}(\lambda,p)
problem is, as wanted,

V=\frac{W}{{\mathcal{N}}(\lambda,p)}=|\Psi|^{\frac{2}{p-1}}\,.

We recover the value of
{\mathcal{N}}(\lambda,p)
and
\alpha_{D}(\lambda,p)
from the solution
\Psi
because

\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}{|\Psi|^{\frac{2\,p}{p-1}}}\,{\mathrm{d}}x=\tau^{-p}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}{|w|^{\frac{2\,p}{p+1}}}\,{\mathrm{d}}x=\tau^{-p}={\mathcal{N}}(\lambda,p)^{-p}=\alpha_{D}(\lambda,p)^{p}\,.

Among all solutions of (
3.5
),
\Psi
is the one with the smallest
\mathrm{L}^{\frac{2\,p}{p-1}}({\mathbb{R}}^{d},{\mathbb{C}}^{N})
norm so that
\lambda=\Lambda_{D}(\alpha,p)
and
\Psi
actually solves (
1.6
).
∎

3.2
Proof of Theorem
3.1

We now prove Theorem
3.1
. We consider a more general case, and study a general optimization problem.
In what follows, we use the notation

\big\langle w,K*w\big\rangle:=\iint_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\big\langle w(x),K(x-y)\,w(y)\big\rangle_{{\mathbb{C}}^{N}}\,{\mathrm{d}}x\,{\mathrm{d}}y

and define for any
s>0
the maximization problem

J(s):=\sup\left\{\big\langle w,K*w\big\rangle\,:\,w\in\mathrm{L}^{q}({\mathbb{R}}^{d},{\mathbb{C}}^{N})\,,\;\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}{|w|^{q}}\,{\mathrm{d}}x=s\right\}\,.

(3.6)

Here,
K
is a convolution operator, or equivalently a multiplication operator in Fourier space. In our case,
K(x-y)=R_{0}(\lambda)(x-y)
is the kernel of the Dirac resolvent, but we state a more general result.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
q\in(1,2)
, set
q^{\prime}:=q/(q-1)\in(2,+\infty)
and
r:=q^{\prime}/2\in(1,+\infty)
. Let
K:{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to{\mathcal{M}}_{N}({\mathbb{C}})
be a matrix-valued function satisfying
K(x)=K(-x)^{*}
, and such that one of the two properties holds:

(i)

either
K\in\mathrm{L}^{r}({\mathbb{R}}^{d},{\mathcal{M}}_{N}({\mathbb{C}}))
,

(ii)

or
K=R_{0}(\lambda)
is a Dirac resolvent for some
\lambda\in(-\,m,m)
.

Then the map
w\mapsto\big\langle w,K*w\big\rangle
is well-defined on
\mathrm{L}^{q}({\mathbb{R}}^{d},{\mathbb{C}}^{N})
and real valued.
Moreover, if
J(1)>0
, then (
3.6
) admits maximizers.

Before proving this result, we make several remarks.

Remark 3.4
.

Lemma
3.3
fails at the endpoint
q=2
. Indeed, by applying the Fourier transform we have

\big\langle w,K*w\big\rangle=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\left\langle\widehat{w}(k),\widehat{K}(k)\,\widehat{w}(k)\right\rangle_{{\mathbb{C}}^{N}}\,{\mathrm{d}}k\,.

This means that all optimizing sequences must concentrate on Dirac masses in Fourier space at locations where
k\mapsto\sup\operatorname{spec}\big(\widehat{K}(k)\big)
has maxima. Since the Fourier transform is an isometry on
\mathrm{L}^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
, we deduce that the maximization problem has no maximum in general. The same argument shows that the existence of optimizers is closely related to the fact that the Fourier transform is not a bijection between
\mathrm{L}^{q}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
and
\mathrm{L}^{q^{\prime}}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
if
1<q<2
.

Remark 3.5
.

In the case of the Dirac operator, one has an explicit expression for
K=R_{0}(\lambda)
, the fundamental solution of
\not{\mathcal{D}}_{m}-\lambda
. Using that

\left(\not{\mathcal{D}}_{m}-\lambda\right)^{-1}=\left(\not{\mathcal{D}}_{m}+\lambda\right)\dfrac{1}{-\Delta+m^{2}-\lambda^{2}}\,,

we first deduce that
R_{0}(\lambda)(\cdot)
is the Fourier transform of

g_{\lambda}(k)=\left(\sum_{j=1}^{d}\alpha_{j}\,k_{j}+m\,\beta+\lambda\,\mathbb{I}_{N}\right)\frac{1}{k^{2}+m^{2}-\lambda^{2}}\,.

The function
k\mapsto g_{\lambda}(k)
is analytic on
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
because there is no singularity in the denominator since
|\lambda|<m
, so its Fourier transform is exponentially decaying in
x
. Actually, we have

R_{0}(\lambda)(x)=\frac{c_{d,\lambda}}{|x|^{d/2-1}}\Bigg({\mathrm{i}}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\alpha_{j}\,\frac{x_{j}}{|x|}\,\sqrt{m^{2}-\lambda^{2}}\,K_{\frac{d}{2}}\Big(\sqrt{m^{2}-\lambda^{2}}\;|x|\Big)\\
+(m\,\beta+\lambda\,\mathbb{I}_{N})\,K_{\frac{d}{2}-1}\Big(\sqrt{m^{2}-\lambda^{2}}\;|x|\Big)\Bigg)

where
c_{d,\lambda}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\,\big(\frac{\sqrt{m^{2}-\lambda^{2}}}{2\pi}\big)^{d/2-1}
and
K_{\nu}
is the modified Bessel function of the second kind. In particular, there is
C\geq 0
so that

\big|R_{0}(\lambda)(x)\big|\leq\begin{cases}C\,|x|^{1-d}&\mbox{as}\,|x|\to 0\,,\\
C\,{\mathrm{e}}^{-\sqrt{m^{2}-\lambda^{2}}\;|x|}&\mbox{as}\,|x|\to+\infty\,.\end{cases}

So, in the Dirac case, we have
R_{0}(\lambda)\in\mathrm{L}^{r}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
for all
r<\frac{d}{d-1}
and
R_{0}(\lambda)\in\mathrm{L}^{\frac{d}{d-1}}_{w}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
. In particular, the case
(ii)
is not covered by
(i)
only in the case where
r=\frac{d}{d-1}
, which corresponds to the critical exponent case
p=d\geq 2
, that is,
q=\frac{2\,d}{d+2}
in (
3.3
).

Remark 3.6
.

Let us consider the case
s=1
in (
3.6
).
In order to see that
J(1)>0
in the Dirac case with
\lambda\in(-\,m,m)
, let
f\in\mathrm{L}^{q}({\mathbb{R}}^{d},{\mathbb{C}})
be a normalized function and let
\phi_{+}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{N}
be a normalized vector such that
\beta\,\phi_{+}=\phi_{+}
. We find that

(\not{\mathcal{D}}_{m}+\lambda)\,f\,\phi_{+}=(m+\lambda)\,f\phi_{+}+(-\,{\mathrm{i}}\,\nabla f)\cdot\alpha\,\phi_{+}\,.

Moreover, by (
1.4
), we have that
\big\langle\phi_{+},\alpha_{j}\,\phi_{+}\big\rangle_{{\mathbb{C}}^{N}}=0
. Thus:

\begin{split}J(1)\geq\big\langle f\,\phi_{+},R_{0}(\lambda)\,f\,\phi_{+}\big\rangle&=\big\langle f\,\phi_{+},\big(-\Delta+m^{2}-\lambda^{2}\big)^{-1}\,(\not{\mathcal{D}}_{m}+\lambda)\,f\,\phi_{+}\big\rangle\\
&=(m+\lambda)\,\big\langle f,\big(-\Delta+m^{2}-\lambda^{2}\big)^{-1}\,f\big\rangle_{\mathrm{L}^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d},{\mathbb{C}})}>0\,.\end{split}

(3.7)

Proof of Lemma
3.3
.

First, we note that the condition
K(x)=K(-x)^{*}
reads
\widehat{K}(k)=\widehat{K}(k)^{*}
, so the operator
K
is symmetric.

In the first part of the proof, we cover both cases
(i)
and
(ii)
by assuming

K\in\mathrm{L}^{r}_{w}\big({\mathbb{R}}^{d},{\mathcal{M}}_{N}({\mathbb{C}})\big)\cap\mathrm{L}^{r}\big({\mathcal{B}}_{1}^{c},{\mathcal{M}}_{N}({\mathbb{C}})\big)\quad\mbox{with}\quad{\mathcal{B}}_{R}:=\{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\,:\,|x|<R\}

(3.8)

with
\frac{2}{q}+\frac{1}{r}=2
. From the Hardy-Littlewood-Sobolev inequality, and since
K\in\mathrm{L}^{r}_{w}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
, we have

\forall\,w_{1}\,,\;w_{2}\in\mathrm{L}^{q}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})\,,\quad\big|\big\langle w_{1},K*w_{2}\big\rangle\big|\leq C\,\|w_{1}\|_{q}\,\|w_{2}\|_{q}\,\|K\|_{r,w}\,.

(3.9)

In particular,
w\mapsto\big\langle w,K*w\big\rangle
is well-defined and real valued on
\mathrm{L}^{q}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
.

Using the scaling
w_{s}=s^{1/q}\,w_{1}
, we obtain that

J(s)=s^{2/q}\,J(1)\,.

(3.10)

Since
J(1)>0
, we deduce first that
J(s)
is increasing. Also, since
2/q>1
,
J(s)
is convex and so we have the strong binding inequality

\forall\,s\,,\;s^{\prime}>0\,,\quad J(s+s^{\prime})>J(s)+J(s^{\prime})\,.

(3.11)

Let
(w_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
be a maximizing sequence for
J(1)
. Our argument relies on the concentration-compactness method for the sequence
(w_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
, following the approach of Lions
[
54
]
and using Levy’s functional. It differs from the concentration-compactness method used in
[
34
]
, as we work directly with the Birman-Schwinger operator instead of the min-max quadratic form. We set

Q(\rho):=\liminf_{n\to+\infty}Q_{n}(\rho)\quad\mbox{with}\quad Q_{n}(\rho):=\sup_{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\int_{{\mathcal{B}}(x,\rho)}|w_{n}|^{q}\,{\mathrm{d}}x\,.

It is clear from the definition that
\rho\mapsto Q(\rho)
is non-decreasing, and that
Q(\rho)\leq 1
for all
\rho>0
. We set

\mu:=\lim_{\rho\to+\infty}Q(\rho)\in[0,1]\,.

We divide the proof in the classical steps of the concentration-compactness method and start by discarding the cases
\mu=0
(vanishing) and
\mu<1
(dichotomy).

\bullet
Vanishing
.
Fix
\varepsilon:=J(1)/4>0
. Since
K\in\mathrm{L}^{r}({\mathcal{B}}_{1}^{c})
, there is
R>1
large enough so that

\|{K}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{r}({\mathcal{B}}_{R}^{c})}\leq\varepsilon\,.

By Young’s inequality, since
\frac{2}{q}+\frac{1}{r}=2
, we get that for all
w\in\mathrm{L}^{q}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
with
\|w\|_{q}=1
,

\big\langle w,(\mathbbm{1}_{{\mathcal{B}}_{R}^{c}}K)*w\big\rangle\leq\varepsilon\,\|{w}\|_{q}^{2}\leq\varepsilon\,.

We now estimate the contribution of
\mathbbm{1}_{{\mathcal{B}}_{R}}\,K
. For
z\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
, let
C_{z}
be the cube
z+[0,1]^{d}
, so that
\{C_{z}\}_{z\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}
covers
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
. For a function
w:{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{C}}^{N}
, we have

\displaystyle\big\langle w,\left(\mathbbm{1}_{{\mathcal{B}}_{R}}\,K\right)*w\,\big\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{z,z^{\prime}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}\iint_{C_{z}\times C_{z^{\prime}}}\big\langle w(x),(\mathbbm{1}_{{\mathcal{B}}_{R}}\,K)(x-y)\,w(y)\big\rangle_{{\mathbb{C}}^{N}}\,{\mathrm{d}}x\,{\mathrm{d}}y

\displaystyle\leq\|K\|_{\mathrm{L}^{r}_{w}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})}\sum_{z,z^{\prime}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}\|{w}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{q}(C_{z})}\,\|{w}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{q}(C_{z^{\prime}})}\,\mathbbm{1}_{\left\{|z-z^{\prime}|\leq R+2\,\sqrt{d}\right\}}

using again the Hardy-Littlewood-Sobolev inequality. The double sum can be seen as a discrete convolution, and we apply Young’s inequality with
z\mapsto\|w\|_{\mathrm{L}^{q}(C_{z})}\in\ell^{2}({\mathbb{Z}}^{d})
and
z\mapsto\mathbbm{1}_{\left\{|z|\leq R+2\,\sqrt{d}\right\}}\in\ell^{1}({\mathbb{Z}}^{d})
to bound

\big\langle w,(\mathbbm{1}_{{\mathcal{B}}_{R}}\,K)*w\big\rangle\leq C_{R}\sum_{z\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}\|{w}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{q}(C_{z})}^{2}\leq C_{R}\,\sup_{z\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}\|{w}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{q}(C_{z})}^{2-q}\,\|{w}\|_{q}^{q}

where
C_{R}
is a positive constant which is independent of
w
: for all
w\in\mathrm{L}^{q}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
with
\|w\|_{q}=1
, we have

\big\langle w,K*w\big\rangle\leq\varepsilon+C_{R}\,\sup_{z\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}\|{w}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{q}(C_{z})}^{2-q}\,.

Applying this estimate to a maximizing sequence
(w_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
for
J(1)=4\,\varepsilon
, we obtain that, up to a subsequence,

\tfrac{1}{2}\,J(1)\leq\left\langle w_{n},K*w_{n}\right\rangle\leq\tfrac{1}{4}\,J(1)+C_{R}\sup_{z\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}\|{w_{n}}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{q}(C_{z})}^{2-q}\,.

This implies

Q_{n}\left(\sqrt{d}\right)\geq\sup_{z\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}\|{w_{n}}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{q}(C_{z})}^{q}\geq\frac{J(1)}{\left(4\,C_{R})\right)^{\frac{q}{2-q}}}>0

and finally
\mu>0
, which discards the
vanishing
case of the concentration-compactness method.

\bullet
Dichotomy
.
By definition of
Q_{n}
, there are sequences of centers
x_{n}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
and radii
\rho_{n}>0
going to infinity so that

\lim_{n\to+\infty}\int_{{\mathcal{B}}(x_{n},\rho_{n})}|w_{n}|^{q}\,{\mathrm{d}}x=\mu\,.

Without loss of generality, by translating the functions
w_{n}
, we may assume
x_{n}=0
. In addition, up to a non-displayed subsequence, we have that for all
\varepsilon>0
, there is
n_{0}
large enough so that, for all
n\geq n_{0}
, we have

\int_{\rho_{n}<|x|<2\,\rho_{n}}|w_{n}|^{q}\,{\mathrm{d}}x<\varepsilon\quad\mbox{and}\quad\left|1-\mu-\int_{|x|>2\,\rho_{n}}|w_{n}|^{q}\,{\mathrm{d}}x\right|<\varepsilon\,.

We set

\begin{cases}w_{n}^{(1)}&:=w_{n}\,\mathbbm{1}_{\left\{|x|\leq\rho_{n}\right\}}\,,\\
w_{n}^{(2)}&:=w_{n}\,\mathbbm{1}_{\left\{\rho_{n}\leq|x|\leq 2\,\rho_{n}\right\}}\,,\\
w_{n}^{(3)}&:=w_{n}\,\mathbbm{1}_{\left\{|x|>2\,\rho_{n}\right\}}\,.\end{cases}

Introducing
E(w_{1},w_{2}):=\langle w_{1},K*w_{2}\rangle
and
{\mathcal{E}}(w):=E(w,w)
, we have

\begin{split}{\mathcal{E}}(w_{n})&={\mathcal{E}}\left(w_{n}^{(1)}\right)+{\mathcal{E}}\left(w_{n}^{(2)}\right)+{\mathcal{E}}\left(w_{n}^{(3)}\right)\\
&\quad+2\,{\rm Re}\left(E\left(w_{n}^{(1)},w_{n}^{(2)}\right)+E\left(w_{n}^{(1)},w_{n}^{(3)}\right)+E\left(w_{n}^{(2)},w_{n}^{(3)}\right)\right)\\
&\leq J(\mu)+J(\varepsilon)+J(1-\mu+\varepsilon)\\
&\quad+2\,{\rm Re}\left(E\left(w_{n}^{(1)},w_{n}^{(2)}\right)+E\left(w_{n}^{(1)},w_{n}^{(3)}\right)+E\left(w_{n}^{(2)},w_{n}^{(3)}\right)\right)\,.\end{split}

From (
3.9
), and the fact that
\|w_{n}^{(2)}\|_{q}\leq\varepsilon^{1/q}
, we get that

E\left(w_{n}^{(1)},w_{n}^{(2)}\right)\leq C\,\mu\,\varepsilon^{1/q}\quad\mbox{and}\quad E\left(w_{n}^{(2)},w_{n}^{(3)}\right)\leq C\,(1-\mu)\,\varepsilon^{1/q}\,.

Finally, we have

\displaystyle\left|E\left(w_{n}^{(1)},w_{n}^{(3)}\right)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\int_{|x|\leq\rho_{n}}\int_{|y|\geq 2\rho_{n}}\big|\big\langle w_{n}(x),K(x-y)\,w_{n}(y)\big\rangle_{{\mathbb{C}}^{N}}\big|\,{\mathrm{d}}x\,{\mathrm{d}}y

\displaystyle\leq\iint_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}|w_{n}(x)|\,|w_{n}(y)|\,(K\,\mathbbm{1}_{{\mathcal{B}}_{\rho_{n}}^{c}})(x-y)\,{\mathrm{d}}x\,{\mathrm{d}}y

\displaystyle\leq\left\|K\,\mathbbm{1}_{{\mathcal{B}}_{\rho_{n}}^{c}}\right\|_{r}\leq C\,\varepsilon

for
n
large enough, where in the last line we used Young’s inequality, and the fact that
\rho_{n}\to+\infty
.
Thanks to these facts, we can conclude that

J(1)\leq J(\mu)+J(1-\mu+\varepsilon)+J(\varepsilon)+C\,\varepsilon^{1/q}\,.

In the limit as
\varepsilon\to 0
, we obtain
J(1)\leq J(\mu)+J(1-\mu)
, which contradicts (
3.11
) if
\mu\neq 1
. So
\mu=1
, which discards the
dichotomy
case of the concentration-compactness method.

\bullet
Convergence for tight sequences
.
At this point, we proved that for all
\varepsilon>0
there is
\rho>0
and
n_{0}
large enough so that, for all
n>n_{0}
, and after appropriate translations and subsequences,

\|{\mathbbm{1}_{{\mathcal{B}}_{\rho}^{c}}\,w_{n}}\|_{q}\leq\varepsilon\,.

(3.12)

In other words, the sequence
(w_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
is tight in
\mathrm{L}^{q}({\mathbb{R}}^{d},{\mathbb{C}}^{N})
. The sequence
(w_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
is bounded in the reflexive Banach space
\mathrm{L}^{q}({\mathbb{R}}^{d},{\mathbb{C}}^{N})
. Hence, up to a non-displayed subsequence,
(w_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
converges weakly to some

, and we have
\|w\|_{q}\leq 1
.

Let us prove that
{\mathcal{E}}(w)=J(1)
. Let
\varepsilon>0
, and let
\rho>0
be large enough so that (
3.12
) holds. In particular, by Hardy-Littlewood-Sobolev, we have

\left|\langle w_{n}\,{\mathds{1}}_{{\mathcal{B}}_{\rho}^{c}},K*w_{n}\big\rangle\right|\leq C\,\|w_{n}\|_{q}\,\|w_{n}\,{\mathds{1}}_{{\mathcal{B}}_{\rho}^{c}}\|_{q}\leq C\,\varepsilon\,,

and we have a similar inequality with
w
instead of
w_{n}
. On the other hand, we have

\langle w_{n}\,{\mathds{1}}_{{\mathcal{B}}_{\rho}},K*w_{n}\big\rangle=\langle w_{n},Tw_{n}\big\rangle_{\mathrm{L}^{q},\mathrm{L}^{q^{\prime}}}\,,

where

is the operator from
\mathrm{L}^{q}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
to
\mathrm{L}^{q^{\prime}}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
with kernel

. The operator
T:\mathrm{L}^{q}({\mathbb{R}}^{d},{\mathbb{C}}^{N})\to\mathrm{L}^{q^{\prime}}({\mathbb{R}}^{d},{\mathbb{C}}^{N})
is bounded. We claim that
T
is a compact operator. In the Dirac case
(ii)
, this comes from the fact that
K*w_{n}\in\mathrm{W}^{1,q}
with
\|K*w_{n}\|_{\mathrm{W}^{1,q}}({\mathbb{R}}^{d},{\mathbb{C}}^{N})\leq C\,\|w_{n}\|_{q}
together with the Rellich-Kondrachov compact embedding theorem. In the case
(i)
, where
K\in\mathrm{L}^{r}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
, setting
\tau_{h}\,f(x):=f(x-h)
, we have

\big\|\tau_{h}(K*w)-K*w\big\|_{\mathrm{L}^{q^{\prime}}(B_{\rho})}=\big\|\left(\tau_{h}\,K-K\right)*w\big\|_{q^{\prime}}\leq\|\tau_{h}\,K-K\|_{r}\,\|w\|_{q}\,.

Since
K\in\mathrm{L}^{r}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
, we have
\|\tau_{h}\,K-K\|_{r}\to 0
as

, and we conclude with the Kolmogorov-Riesz-Fréchet theorem (see for instance
[
11

).

As a consequence,
(Tw_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
converges strongly to
Tw
in
\mathrm{L}^{q^{\prime}}({\mathbb{R}}^{d},{\mathbb{C}}^{N})
. In particular, we obtain that

\lim_{n\to+\infty}\big\langle w_{n}\,{\mathds{1}}_{{\mathcal{B}}_{\rho}},K*w_{n}\big\rangle=\big\langle w\,{\mathds{1}}_{{\mathcal{B}}_{\rho}},K*w\big\rangle.

Gathering the two inequalities gives

\displaystyle\left|\big\langle w_{n},K*w_{n}\big\rangle-\big\langle w,K*w\big\rangle\right|

\displaystyle\;\leq\left|\big\langle w_{n}\,{\mathds{1}}_{{\mathcal{B}}_{\rho}},K*w_{n}\big\rangle-\big\langle w{\mathds{1}}_{{\mathcal{B}}_{\rho}},K*w\big\rangle\right|+\left|\big\langle w_{n}\,{\mathds{1}}_{{\mathcal{B}}_{\rho}}^{c},K*w_{n}\big\rangle\right|+\left|\big\langle w\,{\mathds{1}}_{{\mathcal{B}}_{\rho}}^{c},K*w\big\rangle\right|

\displaystyle\;\leq\left|\big\langle w_{n}\,{\mathds{1}}_{{\mathcal{B}}_{\rho}},K*w_{n}\big\rangle-\big\langle w{\mathds{1}}_{{\mathcal{B}}_{\rho}},K*w\big\rangle\right|+2\,C\,\varepsilon.

Sending first
n
to
+\infty
, and then
\varepsilon
to
0
shows that
\langle w,K*w\rangle=J(1)
. Finally, since
\|w\|_{q}\leq 1
, by (

) we deduce that
\|w\|_{q}=1
. This proves that
(w_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
converges strongly to
w
in
\mathrm{L}^{q}({\mathbb{R}}^{d},{\mathbb{C}}^{N})
and that

is an optimizer.
∎

It is an open question to decide whether

is compact or not under the condition (

Proof of Theorem

.

In the setting of Theorem

, we take

. We have

The fact that

-\,m<\lambda^{\prime}<\lambda<m

be the optimizer for

R_{0}(\lambda^{\prime})=R_{0}(\lambda)-(\lambda-\lambda^{\prime})\,R_{0}(\lambda^{\prime})\,R_{0}(\lambda)\,,

we obtain

Using that

This proves that

is locally Lipschitz, hence continuous.

We now prove the bounds on
\lim_{\lambda\to\pm m}{\mathcal{N}}(\lambda,p)

) and take a function

, where

\mathcal{N}(\lambda,p)\geq L^{-2\,d/q}\,(m+\lambda)\,\Big\langle{g(\cdot/L)},{\big(-\Delta+m^{2}-\lambda^{2}\big)^{-1}\,g(\cdot/L)}\Big\rangle\,.

We bound the resolvent as

\big(-\Delta+m^{2}-\lambda^{2}\big)^{-1}\geq\big(m^{2}-\lambda^{2}\big)^{-1}\left(1+\big(m^{2}-\lambda^{2}\big)^{-1}\Delta\right)

and change variables to obtain

\mathcal{N}(\lambda,p)\geq L^{d\,(1-\frac{2}{q})}\,(m-\lambda)^{-1}\left(\|{g}\|_{2}^{2}-L^{-2}\,\big(m^{2}-\lambda^{2}\big)^{-1}\,\|{\nabla g}\|_{2}^{2}\right).

Since

, we may take

for any

.

Finally, to prove that
\lim_{\lambda\to-m}{\mathcal{N}}(\lambda,p)>0

there exists a function

and

,

(3.13)

where

{\mathcal{N}}(\lambda,p)\geq\langle w,R_{0}(\lambda)\,w\rangle=\left\langle w,\dfrac{P}{\not{\mathcal{D}}_{m}-\lambda}\,w\right\rangle+\left\langle w,\dfrac{P^{\perp}}{\not{\mathcal{D}}_{m}-\lambda}\,w\right\rangle.

The second term is null since

It remains to prove (

\not{\mathcal{D}}_{m}={\mathcal{F}}M(k)\,{\mathcal{F}}^{*}

denotes the Fourier transform and

is the

,

\sigma(M(k))=\big\{\pm\,(|k|^{2}+m^{2})^{1/2}\big\}

Let

be a smooth family of spinors from some open ball

to

M(k)\,v(k)=(|k|^{2}+m^{2})^{1/2}\,v(k)
. To construct such a local family of spinors, one can consider
v_{0}
a normalized eigenfunction of

, and set,

v(k):=\frac{P(k)\,v_{0}}{\|P(k)\,v_{0}\|_{2}}\,,\quad P(k):={\mathds{1}}(M(k)>0)\,.

Since
k\mapsto P(k)
is smooth locally around

(

can be written as a Cauchy integral

.
Let also

w:=\dfrac{\widetilde{w}}{\|\widetilde{w}\|_{q}}\quad\text{with}\quad\widetilde{w}:={\mathcal{F}}\big(\chi(k)\,v(k)\big)\,.

By construction, we have

which concludes the proof of (

Let

Let us first prove that

4
Lieb-Thirring inequality

This section contains the proof of Theorem
1.4
.
We closely follow the original proof by Lieb and Thirring
[
51
,
53
]
(see also
[
52
]
). This is possible since we are assuming
V\geq 0
. In the general case where
V
has no sign, some results can be found in the works of Cuenin
[
17
]
, and Frank-Simon
[
40
]
, where the authors control the Riesz-mean

\sum_{k}{\rm dist}\big(\lambda_{k},\sigma(\not{\mathcal{D}}_{m}-V)\big)^{\gamma}\,,

that is, the distance to the whole spectrum. Actually, without assuming a sign on
V
, one cannot expect to control the sums in (
1.13
), since, for
V\leq 0
small, the eigenvalues of
\not{\mathcal{D}}_{m}-V
emerge from the bottom essential spectrum (hence have a distance of order
2\,m>0
to the upper essential spectrum).
Here, since
V
is nonnegative, the eigenvalues emerge from the upper essential spectrum as the strength of the potential increases.

Proof of Theorem
1.4
.

It is sufficient to prove the result for
V
bounded and compactly supported. By the
Birman-Schwinger principle
introduced in Section
2.2
, we know that
\lambda
is an eigenvalue for
\not{\mathcal{D}}_{m}-V
acting on
\mathbb{C}^{N}
valued spinors if and only if
1
is an eigenvalue of
K_{V}(\lambda)
defined by (
2.3
): see Proposition
2.4
. We also proved that
\lambda\mapsto K_{V}(\lambda)
is operator increasing. In particular, if we set

N_{e}(V):=\text{number of eigenvalues of $\not{\mathcal{D}}_{m}-V$ in $[-\,m,m-e]$}

and

B_{e}(V):=\text{number of eigenvalues of $K_{V}(m-e)$ which are greater or equal than $1$}\,,

then we have
N_{e}(V)\leq B_{e}(V)
. We have equality if the highest eigenvalues of
K_{V}(\lambda)
gets strictly smaller than
1
as
\lambda\to-\,m
. This happens for instance if
\|V\|_{p}\leq\alpha_{*}(p)
.

With
R_{0}
defined by (
2.2
), using the operator inequality

R_{0}(\lambda)\leq\mathbbm{1}_{{\mathbb{C}}^{N}}\left(\sqrt{-\Delta+m^{2}}-\lambda\right)^{-1}\,,

we can estimate
B_{e}(V)
by
N\,B_{e}^{\rm pr}(V)
, where
B_{e}^{\rm pr}(V)
is the number of eigenvalues above
1
of the pseudo-relativistic Birman-Schwinger operator

K^{\rm pr}_{V}(m-e):=\sqrt{V}\left(\sqrt{-\Delta+m^{2}}-m+e\right)^{-1}\sqrt{V}\,.

In addition, with the definition

N_{e}^{\rm pr}(V):=\text{number of eigenvalues of $\left(\sqrt{-\Delta+m^{2}}-m\right)-V$ less or equal than $-\,e$}\,,

the usual Birman-Schwinger principle shows that
B_{e}^{\rm pr}(V)=N_{e}^{\rm pr}(V)
. To sum up, we have

N_{e}(V)\leq B_{e}(V)\leq N\,B_{e}^{\rm pr}(V)=N\,N_{e}^{\rm pr}(V)\,.

(4.1)

The operator
\sqrt{-\Delta+m^{2}}-m
is sometimes called the Chandrasekhar (or pseudo-relativistic) kinetic energy operator. It is a positive operator,
\sqrt{-\Delta+m^{2}}-m-V
is bounded from below, and the min-max formula applies. We can now repeat the usual arguments of Lieb and Thirring for the pseudo-relativistic operator.

First, for
\gamma>0
, the cake-layer representation gives

\sum_{k\geq 1}e_{k}^{\gamma}=\gamma\int_{0}^{2\,m}e^{\gamma-1}\,N_{e}(V)\,{\mathrm{d}}e\leq\gamma\,N\,\int_{0}^{2\,m}e^{\gamma-1}\,B_{e}^{\rm pr}(V)\,{\mathrm{d}}e\,.

(4.2)

Note that for the pseudo-relativistic model, if
-\,e_{1}^{\rm pr}\leq-\,e_{2}^{\rm pr}\leq\cdots<0
are the negative eigenvalues of
\left(\sqrt{-\Delta+m^{2}}-m\right)-V
, we have

\sum_{k\geq 1}(e_{k}^{\rm pr})^{\gamma}=\gamma\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{\gamma-1}\,N_{e}^{\rm pr}(V)\,{\mathrm{d}}e=\gamma\,\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{\gamma-1}\,B_{e}^{\rm pr}(V)\,{\mathrm{d}}e\,,

and the integral runs over
e\in{\mathbb{R}}^{+}
instead of
e\in(0,2\,m)
. Actually, the previous two inequalities together with (
4.1
) show that

\sum_{k\geq 1}e_{k}^{\gamma}\leq N\sum_{k\geq 1}(e_{k}^{\rm pr})^{\gamma}\,.

In other words, the Riesz-mean of the eigenvalues increases when one replaces the Dirac operator by the pseudo–relatisvistic one (up to the
N
factor). Lieb-Thirring inequalities for the last sum have been derived by Daubechies in
[
19
]
(and used,
e.g.
, in
[
50
]
). In what follows, we derive another inequality specifically for the Dirac operator. We use in particular the fact that the integral in (
4.2
) only runs for
e
in the bounded interval
(0,2\,m)
instead
of
{\mathbb{R}}^{+}
.

\bullet

Bound for
B_{e}^{\rm pr}(V)
. Assume
V\in\mathrm{L}^{p}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
with
d<p
. The number of eigenvalues above
1
of
K_{V}^{\rm pr}(m-e)
is bounded from above by
\|K_{V}^{\rm pr}(m-e)\|_{{\mathfrak{S}}^{p}}^{p}
. We estimate this norm using the Kato-Simon-Seiler inequality (see
[
65
, Theorem 4.2]
). Using a decomposition similar to the one in the proof of Lemma
2.3
, we obtain

B_{e}^{\rm pr}(V)\leq\|K_{V}^{\rm pr}(m-e)\,\|_{{\mathfrak{S}}_{p}}^{p}\leq C_{p}\left\|g_{m,e}\right\|_{p}^{p}\,\|V\|_{p}^{p}\,,

where we introduced the function

g_{m,e}(k):=\left(\sqrt{k^{2}+m^{2}}-(m-e)\right)^{-1}\,.

Note that
g_{m,e}\in\mathrm{L}^{p}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
since
p>d
, and

\|g_{m,e}\|_{p}^{p}=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\dfrac{{\mathrm{d}}k}{\left(\sqrt{k^{2}+m^{2}}-m+e\right)^{p}}=|{\mathbb{S}}^{d-1}|\int_{0}^{\infty}\dfrac{r^{d-1}\,{\mathrm{d}}r}{\left(\sqrt{r^{2}+m^{2}}-m+e\right)^{p}}\,.

To estimate this norm, we make the change of variable
X=\frac{1}{e}\,\big(\sqrt{r^{2}+m^{2}}-m\big)
, so that
r=\sqrt{(e\,X+m)^{2}-m^{2}}=\sqrt{e\,X\,(e\,X+2\,m)}
. We obtain

\|g_{m,e}\|_{p}^{p}=\frac{|{\mathbb{S}}^{d-1}|}{e^{p-\frac{d}{2}}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\dfrac{\big(X(e\,X+2\,m)\big)^{\frac{d}{2}-1}(e\,X+m)\,{\mathrm{d}}X}{\left(X+1\right)^{p}}\,.

The last integral is an increasing function of
e
(and has a finite value as
e\to 0
by the monotone convergence theorem). Since
e\in(0,2\,m)
, we can bound this integral by its value at
e=2\,m
. We deduce that there is a constant
C_{p,d}
such that

B_{e}^{\rm pr}(V)\leq C_{p,d}\,\|{V}\|_{p}^{p}\,\dfrac{m^{d/2}}{e^{p-d/2}}\,.

(4.3)

\bullet

Proof of the Lieb–Thirring estimate
. We now follow
[
51
,
53
,
52
]
. The min–max principle for the pseudo-relativistic operator shows that its eigenvalues are decreasing when
V
increases. Since
V\leq[V-e/2]_{+}+e/2
, we may bound

B_{e}^{\rm pr}(V)=N_{e}^{\rm pr}(V)\leq N_{e}^{\rm pr}\big([V-e/2]_{+}+e/2\big)\\
=N_{e/2}^{\rm pr}\big([V-e/2]_{+}\big)=B_{e/2}^{\rm pr}\big([V-e/2]_{+}\big)\,.

For any
p>d
, we can apply the bound in (
4.3
) to estimate
B_{e/2}^{\rm pr}\big([V-e_{2}]_{+}\big)
. Inserting this estimate into (
4.2
), we get

\sum_{k\geq 1}e_{k}^{\gamma}\leq N\,C_{p,d}\,\gamma\,m^{\frac{d}{2}}\int_{0}^{2\,m}(e/2)^{\gamma-1+\frac{d}{2}-p}\,\big\|[V-e/2]_{+}\big\|_{p}^{p}\,{\mathrm{d}}e\\
=C_{\gamma,d,p}\,m^{\frac{d}{2}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\int_{0}^{2\,m}e^{\gamma-1+\frac{d}{2}-p}\,\big[V(x)-e/2\big]_{+}^{p}\,{\mathrm{d}}e\,{\mathrm{d}}x\\
\leq C_{\gamma,d,p}\,m^{\frac{d}{2}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}V^{\gamma+\frac{d}{2}}(x)\int_{0}^{s^{*}(x)}s^{\gamma-1+\frac{d}{2}-p}\,(1-s)^{p}_{+}\,{\mathrm{d}}s\,{\mathrm{d}}x\,,

where
s^{*}(x):=\min\{m/V(x),1\}
, with the convention that
s^{*}(x)=1
if
V(x)=0
. The second integral converges whenever
p<\gamma+d/2
.
We can simply use the bound
(1-s)^{p}\leq 1
in the last integral, and finally obtain

\sum_{k\geq 1}e_{k}^{\gamma}\leq L_{\gamma,d,p}\,m^{\frac{d}{2}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}V_{m}^{\gamma+\frac{d}{2}-p}\,V^{p}\,{\mathrm{d}}x\quad\text{with}\quad V_{m}:=\min\left\{m,V\right\}\,.

(4.4)

This inequality is valid for all
d<p<\gamma+d/2
. Note that
C_{\gamma,d,p}
stays bounded in the limit as
p\to\gamma+d/2
, so a similar inequality also holds if
p=\gamma+d/2
. ∎

Remark 4.1
.

The result of Theorem
1.4
can be extended to the case of a potential
V\in\mathrm{L}^{p}({\mathbb{R}}^{d},{\mathbb{R}}^{+})+\mathrm{L}^{\gamma+d/2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d},{\mathbb{R}}^{+})
by noticing that the right-hand side of (
4.4
) is continuous for

in this space.

5
Explicit computations

5.1
The case
d=1
: proof of Theorem
1.3

In this section, we prove the uniqueness and the symmetry up to translations of the solution of the nonlinear Dirac equation (
3.5
). We also compute the map
\alpha_{D}(\lambda,p)
.

Proof of Theorem
1.3
.

In the one-dimensional case, Equation (
3.5
) can be rewritten for the components of
\Psi=:(\varphi,\chi)^{\top}
as

\begin{cases}\varphi^{\prime}=-\,\Big(\lambda+m+\left(|\chi|^{2}+|\varphi|^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{p-1}}\Big)\,\chi\,,\\
\chi^{\prime}=\Big(\lambda-m+\left(|\chi|^{2}+|\varphi|^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{p-1}}\Big)\,\varphi\,.\end{cases}

(5.1)

The corresponding potential is
V=\left(|\chi|^{2}+|\varphi|^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{p-1}}
. This system conserves

\begin{split}H(\varphi,\chi)&:=m\left(|\chi|^{2}-|\varphi|^{2}\right)+\lambda\left(|\chi|^{2}+|\varphi|^{2}\right)+\tfrac{p-1}{p}\,\left(|\chi|^{2}+|\varphi|^{2}\right)^{\frac{p}{p-1}}\,,\\
G(\varphi,\chi)&:=\bar{\chi}\,\varphi-\bar{\varphi}\,\chi\,.\end{split}

Since we are looking for solutions vanishing at
\pm\infty
, they satisfy
H\big(\varphi(x),\chi(x)\big)=0
,
G(\varphi(x),\chi(x))=0
for all
x\in{\mathbb{R}}
. This second condition shows that solutions can be chosen real valued. For real valued variables in the
(\varphi,\chi)
-plane, the level set
H(\varphi,\chi)=0
has the shape of an infinity sign. Among real valued functions, uniqueness up to translations follows from the phase plane analysis. We can choose the unique solution with
\chi(0)=0
,
\varphi(0)>0
, given. For this solution,
\varphi
is even and
\chi
is odd and positive on
{\mathbb{R}}^{+}
. Hence symmetry and uniqueness, up to translations and multiplication by a phase, are granted by elementary considerations. Next, we have

V^{\prime}=\tfrac{1}{p-1}\,\frac{(\chi^{2}+\varphi^{2})^{\prime}}{(\chi^{2}+\varphi^{2})^{\frac{p}{p-1}}}\quad\mbox{with}\quad\left(\chi^{2}+\varphi^{2}\right)^{\prime}=-\,4\,m\,\chi\,\varphi\,,

which proves that
V
is increasing in the quadrant
\{\chi<0,\varphi>0\}
and decreasing in the quadrant
\{\chi>0,\varphi>0\}
. Hence
V
is even and decreasing on
{\mathbb{R}}^{+}
, while on
{\mathbb{R}}^{+}
both
\chi
and
\varphi
are positive valued.

Now let us compute
\|{V}\|_{p}
. It is enough to do the computation on
{\mathbb{R}}^{+}
. First, the equation
H(\varphi,\chi)=0
can be rewritten as

2\,m\,\varphi^{2}=(m+\lambda)\,V^{p-1}+\tfrac{p-1}{p}\,V^{p}\,,

and so

\varphi=\sqrt{\tfrac{1}{2\,m}\,V^{p-1}\left(m+\lambda+\tfrac{p-1}{p}\,V\right)}\,.

Next, from the equation
V^{p-1}=\chi^{2}+\varphi^{2}
, we deduce that

\chi=\sqrt{V^{p-1}-\varphi^{2}}=\sqrt{\tfrac{1}{2\,m}\,V^{p-1}\left(m-\lambda-\tfrac{p-1}{p}\,V\right)}\,.

Finally, we have

(p-1)\,V^{p-2}\,V^{\prime}=\left(V^{p-1}\right)^{\prime}=\left(\chi^{2}+\varphi^{2}\right)^{\prime}=-\,4\,m\,\chi\,\varphi\,.

Collecting the three last equalities shows that
V
solves the autonomous differential equation

V^{\prime}=-\,\tfrac{2}{p-1}\,V\,\sqrt{\left(m-\lambda-\tfrac{p-1}{p}\,V\right)\left(m+\lambda+\tfrac{p-1}{p}\,V\right)}\,.

At
x=0
, we have
V^{\prime}(0)=0
, which implies

V(0)=\tfrac{p}{p-1}\,(m-\lambda)\,.

\bullet
Subcritical regime
\lambda>-\,m
.
The function

Z(x):=\tfrac{p-1}{p\,(m+\lambda)}\,V\big(\tfrac{p-1}{2\,(m+\lambda)}\,x\big)

satisfies

Z^{\prime}=-\,Z\,\sqrt{(z_{0}-Z)\,(1+Z)}\,,\quad Z(0)=z_{0}=\tfrac{m-\lambda}{m+\lambda}\,.

(5.2)

One can directly check that the solution of (
5.2
) is

Z(x)=\dfrac{2\,z_{0}}{(1+z_{0})\cosh\left(\sqrt{z_{0}}\,x\right)+1-z_{0}}\,.

This gives (
1.10
). The
\mathrm{L}^{p}({\mathbb{R}})
norm of
V
is computed as

\|{V}\|_{p}^{p}=\frac{p^{p}\,(m+\lambda)^{p-1}}{2\,(p-1)^{p-1}}\,\|{Z}\|_{p}^{p}\,.

Using that
Z
is even, monotone decreasing on
{\mathbb{R}}^{+}
, with the change of variable
z=Z(x)
and
t=z/z_{0}
, we obtain, using (
5.2
),

\displaystyle\|Z\|_{p}^{p}

\displaystyle=2\int_{0}^{+\infty}Z^{p}(x)\,{\mathrm{d}}x

\displaystyle=2\int_{0}^{z_{0}}\dfrac{z^{p-1}}{\sqrt{(z_{0}-z)\,(1+z)}}\,{\mathrm{d}}z=2\,z_{0}^{p-\frac{1}{2}}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{t^{p-1}}{\sqrt{(1-t)\,\big(1-(-z_{0})\,t\big)}}\,{\mathrm{d}}t

\displaystyle=2\,z_{0}^{p-\frac{1}{2}}\,B\left(\tfrac{1}{2},p\right)\,_{2}F_{1}\left(\tfrac{1}{2},p;p+\tfrac{1}{2};-z_{0}\right)\,.

See
[
1
, 15.3.1 p. 558]
for the last equality. This completes the computation of
\alpha_{D}(\lambda,p)
. By taking the limit as
p\to 1_{+}
, we obtain
\alpha_{D}(\lambda,1)=\arccos(\lambda/m)
.

\bullet
Critical case
\lambda=-\,m
. The function

Z(x):=\tfrac{p-1}{2\,m\,p}\,V\big(\tfrac{p-1}{2\,m}\,x\big)

solves

Z^{\prime}=-\,2\,Z^{3/2}\,\sqrt{1-Z}\,,\quad Z(0)=1

on
{\mathbb{R}}^{+}
. The solution is

\forall\,x\in{\mathbb{R}}\,,\quad Z(x)=\dfrac{1}{1+x^{2}}\,.

This gives (
1.11
), and the expression of
\alpha_{\star}(p)
follows from

\|{V}\|_{p}^{p}=\frac{p^{p}\,(2\,m)^{p-1}}{(p-1)^{p-1}}\,\|{Z}\|_{p}^{p}

with

\|Z\|_{p}^{p}=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}\dfrac{{\mathrm{d}}x}{\left(1+x^{2}\right)^{p}}=B\left(\tfrac{1}{2},p-\tfrac{1}{2}\right)

according to
[
42
, 8.380.3 p. 917]
. This concludes the proof of Theorem
1.3
.
∎

Notice that
\lim_{z_{0}\to+\infty}\sqrt{z_{0}}\,B\left(\tfrac{1}{2},p\right)\,_{2}F_{1}\left(\tfrac{1}{2},p;p+\tfrac{1}{2};-z_{0}\right)=B\left(\tfrac{1}{2},p-\tfrac{1}{2}\right)
, so that
\lim_{\lambda\to(-1)_{+}}\alpha_{D}(\lambda,p)=\alpha_{\star}(p)
.

5.2
The radial case in dimension
d=2

We now provide some numerical simulations to obtain upper bounds for the maps
\Lambda_{D}(\alpha,p)
.

First, we restrict the minimization problem (
1.5
) to radial potentials, that is, we compute

\Lambda_{D}^{\rm rad}(\alpha,p):=\inf\Big\{\lambda_{D}(V)\,:\,V\in\mathrm{L}^{p}({\mathbb{R}}^{d},{\mathbb{R}}^{+}),\;\mbox{$V$ radial and}\;\|{V}\|_{p}=\alpha\Big\}\,.

Below in Appendix
B
, we provide some numerical evidences that the optimal potentials are radial. We abusively write
V(x)=V(r)
with
r=|x|
,
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
, use polar coordinates
(x,y)=(r\cos\theta,r\sin\theta)
, and write

\partial_{x}=\cos\theta\,\partial_{r}-\frac{1}{r}\,\sin\theta\,\partial_{\theta}\quad\mbox{and}\quad\partial_{y}=\sin\theta\,\partial_{r}+\frac{1}{r}\,\cos\theta\,\partial_{\theta}\,.

In these coordinates, the Dirac operator becomes

\not{\mathcal{D}}_{m}=\begin{pmatrix}m&-\,{\mathrm{i}}\,\partial_{x}-\partial_{y}\\
-\,{\mathrm{i}}\,\partial_{x}+\partial_{y}&-\,m\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}m&{\mathrm{e}}^{-\,{\mathrm{i}}\,\theta}\left(-\,{\mathrm{i}}\,\partial_{r}-\frac{1}{r}\,\partial_{\theta}\right)\\
{\mathrm{e}}^{{\mathrm{i}}\,\theta}\left(-\,{\mathrm{i}}\,\partial_{r}+\frac{1}{r}\,\partial_{\theta}\right)&-\,m\end{pmatrix}.

This suggests to decompose a spinor
\Psi
in Fourier modes with the convention

\Psi(r,\theta)=\sum_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}\begin{pmatrix}\varphi_{n}(r)\,{\mathrm{e}}^{{\mathrm{i}}\,n\,\theta}\\
{\mathrm{i}}\,\chi_{n}(r)\,{\mathrm{e}}^{{\mathrm{i}}\,(n+1)\,\theta}\end{pmatrix}\,.

If
\Phi:=(\not{\mathcal{D}}_{m}-V)\,\Psi
with corresponding Fourier modes
\big((\widetilde{\varphi_{n}},\widetilde{\chi_{n}})^{\top}\big)_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}
, then we have

\begin{pmatrix}\widetilde{\varphi_{n}}\\
\widetilde{\chi_{n}}\end{pmatrix}=(\not{\mathcal{D}}_{m}^{(n)}-V)\begin{pmatrix}\varphi_{n}\\
\chi_{n}\end{pmatrix}\quad\mbox{with}\quad\not{\mathcal{D}}_{m}^{(n)}=\begin{pmatrix}m&\partial_{r}+\frac{n+1}{r}\\
-\,\partial_{r}+\frac{n}{r}&-\,m\end{pmatrix}\,.

The operator
\not{\mathcal{D}}_{m}^{(n)}
is self-adjoint in the Hilbert space
\mathrm{L}^{2}\big({\mathbb{R}}^{+}\times(0,2\pi),r\,{\mathrm{d}}r\,{\mathrm{d}}\theta\big)
because
(\partial_{r})^{*}=-\,\partial_{r}-\frac{1}{r}

denote the lowest eigenvalue of
\not{\mathcal{D}}_{m}^{(n)}-V
in the gap
(-\,m,m)
, and let

and

\Lambda_{D}^{{\rm rad}}(\alpha,p):=\inf_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}\Lambda_{D}^{{\rm rad},(n)}(\alpha,p)\,.

We have the estimates

\Lambda_{D}(\alpha,p)\leq\Lambda_{D}^{{\rm rad}}(\alpha,p)\leq\Lambda_{D}^{{\rm rad},(0)}(\alpha,p)\,.

(5.3)

A wavefunction
\Psi(r,\theta)=\big(\varphi(r)\,{\mathrm{e}}^{{\mathrm{i}}\,n\,\theta},{\mathrm{i}}\,\chi(r)\,{\mathrm{e}}^{{\mathrm{i}}\,(n+1)\,\theta}\big)^{\top}
solves the non-linear Dirac equation (

\begin{cases}\varphi^{\prime}-\frac{n}{r}\,\varphi=-\,\Big(\lambda+m+\left(|\chi|^{2}+|\varphi|^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{p-1}}\Big)\,\chi\,,\\
\chi^{\prime}+\frac{n+1}{r}\,\chi=\Big(\lambda-m+\left(|\chi|^{2}+|\varphi|^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{p-1}}\Big)\,\varphi\,.\end{cases}

(5.4)

This system with

is attained by

with

\left(\not{\mathcal{D}}_{m}^{\hbar,c}-W\right)\psi=\mu\,\psi\quad\mbox{where}\quad\not{\mathcal{D}}_{m}^{\hbar,c}:=-\,{\mathrm{i}}\,\hbar\,c\,\boldsymbol{\alpha}\cdot\nabla+m\,c^{2}\beta

\hbar=c=m=1
by the change of variables

\psi(x)=\Psi\left(\frac{m\,c}{\hbar}\,x\right)\,,\quad W(x)=m\,c^{2}\,V\left(\frac{m\,c}{\hbar}\,x\right)\,,\quad\mu=m\,c^{2}\,\lambda\,.

As a consequence, the
ground state

\lambda_{D}^{\hbar,c}(W)
of
\not{\mathcal{D}}_{m}^{\hbar,c}-W
defined as its lowest eigenvalue in the gap
\left(-m\,c^{2},m\,c^{2}\right)
and estimated by
\lambda_{D}(V)\geq\Lambda_{D}^{(m=1)}\left(\|{V}\|_{p},p\right)
according to the
Keller-Lieb-Thirring inequality for the Dirac operator
(
1.8
) becomes

\lambda_{D}^{\hbar,c}(W)\geq m\,c^{2}\,\Lambda_{D}^{(m=1)}\left(\hbar^{-\frac{d}{p}}\,m^{\frac{d}{p}-1}\,c^{\frac{d}{p}-2}\,\|{W}\|_{p},p\right)

(C.1)

using the above change of variables. Here
\Lambda_{D}^{(m=1)}
stands for
\Lambda_{D}
when we assume
m=1
in notations of Theorem
1.1
.

Proposition C.1
.

Let either
d\geq 1
and
p>1
if
d=1
, or
p\geq d
if
d\geq 2
. With
\Lambda_{D}^{(m=1)}(\alpha,p)
defined by (
1.5
),
\eta=2\,p/(2\,p-d)
and
\mathsf{K}_{p}
as in (
1.2
), we have

1-\Lambda_{D}^{(m=1)}(\alpha,p)=2^{\frac{d}{2\,p-d}}\,\mathsf{K}_{p}\,\alpha^{\eta}\,\big(1+o(1)\big)\quad\mbox{as}\quad\alpha\to 0_{+}\,.

If
d=1
, we obtain that

\mathsf{K}_{p}=\big(p^{p}\,(p-1)^{-(p-1)}\,B(\tfrac{1}{2},p)\big)^{-\frac{2}{2\,p-1}}

by expanding the expression of
\alpha_{D}(\lambda,p)
given in Theorem
1.3
as
\lambda\to 1_{-}
. This is consistent with
\mathsf{K}_{p}=\mathsf{C}_{q}^{-\eta}
and the expression of the explicit, optimal value of the constant
\mathsf{C}_{q}
in (
1.1
) if the dimension is
d=1
: we refer to
[
23
]
and references therein for details.

Beweis.

Let us consider the general case
d\geq 1
. The non-relativistic limit of the ground state
\lambda_{D}^{\hbar,c}(W)
of the Dirac operator
\not{\mathcal{D}}_{m}^{\hbar,c}-W
is, up to the mass energy
m\,c^{2}
, given by the ground state of the Schrödinger operator

-\,\tfrac{\hbar^{2}}{2\,m}\,\Delta-W

by standard results: see for instance
[
32
, Section 2.4]
. Hence

\lim_{c\to+\infty}\left(m\,c^{2}-\lambda_{D}^{\hbar,c}(W)\right)=\lambda_{S}^{-}(W_{\mu})\quad\mbox{where}\quad W_{\mu}(x):=W(\mu\,x)\quad\mbox{and}\quad\mu=\frac{\hbar}{\sqrt{2\,m}}\,.

Here
-\,\lambda_{S}^{-}(W_{\mu})
denotes, if it exists, the negative ground state of
the Schrödinger operator
-\,\Delta-W_{\mu}
. The factor
\mu=\hbar/\sqrt{2\,m}
arises from a scaling argument. By definition (
1.5
), we obtain

\lim_{c\to+\infty}m\,c^{2}\left(1-\Lambda_{D}^{(m=1)}\left(\hbar^{-\frac{d}{p}}\,m^{\frac{d}{p}-1}\,c^{\frac{d}{p}-2}\,\|{W}\|_{p},p\right)\right)\leq\mathsf{K}_{p}\,\|{W_{\mu}}\|_{p}^{\eta}=\mathsf{K}_{p}\,\mu^{-\frac{d\,\eta}{p}}\,\|{W}\|_{p}^{\eta}

but there is in fact equality if we use as test function an optimal function
W
for (
1.2
). Taking
\alpha=\hbar^{-\frac{d}{p}}\,m^{\frac{d}{p}-1}\,c^{\frac{d}{p}-2}\,\|{W}\|_{p}
in the limit as
c\to+\infty
concludes the proof.
∎

Proposition
C.1
is in fact equivalent to

\lim_{c\to+\infty}\left(m\,c^{2}-\lambda_{D}^{\hbar,c}(W)\right)\leq\mathsf{K}_{p}\left(\tfrac{2\,m}{\hbar^{2}}\right)^{\frac{d}{2\,p-d}}\,\|{W}\|_{p}^{\eta}

(C.2)

written with the physical constants. In other words, we recover a standard
Keller-Lieb-Thirring inequality for the Schrödinger operator
(
1.2
) in the non-relativistic limit. In dimension
d=1
, a tedious but elementary computation directly shows that the constant obtained by taking the non-relativistic limit in the Keller-Lieb-Thirring inequality for the Dirac operator written with optimal constant is the optimal constant in the Keller-Lieb-Thirring inequality for the Schrödinger operator, as it can be deduced for instance from
[
45
,
23
]
.

The definition (
1.7
) can be generalized to the case
(\hbar,c)\neq(1,1)
using the monotonicity of
\alpha\mapsto\Lambda_{D}^{(m=1)}(\alpha,p)
stated in Theorem
1.1
and (
C.1
). If
\alpha_{D}^{(m=1)}
denotes the inverse of
\alpha\mapsto\Lambda_{D}^{(m=1)}(\alpha,p)
, the condition

\|W\|_{p}\leq\alpha_{D}^{\hbar,c}(\lambda,p):=\hbar^{\frac{d}{p}}\,m^{1-\frac{d}{p}}\,c^{2-\frac{d}{p}}\,\alpha_{D}^{(m=1)}\!\left(\tfrac{\lambda}{m\,c^{2}},p\right)

(C.3)

guarantees that
\lambda_{D}^{\hbar,c}(W)\geq\lambda
. Notice that
p\geq d
implies that

\lim_{c\to\infty}\|W\|_{p}\leq\alpha_{D}^{\hbar,c}(\lambda,p)=\infty\,.

C.2
An interpolation inequality for the Dirac operator

Using a min-max principle as in
[
28
]
, it is possible to write an optimal interpolation inequality of Gagliardo-Nirenberg-Sobolev type which plays for the free Dirac operator the same role as (
1.1
). The inequality is somewhat involved, but Inequality (
1.1
) is recovered in the non-relativistic limit as
c\to+\infty
. For sake of simplicity, we consider only the case
d=1
.

Let us start by a short and formal summary of the
min-max principle
applied to the determination of the ground state of the Dirac operator. If
(\varphi,\chi)^{\top}
is an eigenspinor of the operator
\not{\mathcal{D}}_{m}^{\hbar,c}-V
with eigenvalue
\lambda\in(-\,m\,c^{2},\,m\,c^{2})
, then, as in (
5.1
) we have

\begin{cases}\hbar\,c\,\varphi^{\prime}&=-\,(\lambda+m\,c^{2}+V)\,\chi\,,\\
\hbar\,c\,\chi^{\prime}&=(\lambda-m\,c^{2}+V)\,\varphi\,.\end{cases}

The first line gives

\chi=-\,\hbar\,c\,\frac{\varphi^{\prime}}{\lambda+m\,c^{2}+V}

so that the problem amounts to solving

-\,(\hbar\,c)^{2}\left(\frac{\varphi^{\prime}}{\lambda+m\,c^{2}+V}\right)^{\prime}+\left(m\,c^{2}-\lambda-V\right)\varphi=0\,.

Multiplying by
\varphi
and integrating suggests to introduce the functional

\mathcal{E}[\mu,V,\phi]:=(\hbar\,c)^{2}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}{\frac{|\phi^{\prime}|^{2}}{\mu+m\,c^{2}+V}}\,{\mathrm{d}}x+\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}{\left(m\,c^{2}-\mu-V\right)|\phi|^{2}}\,{\mathrm{d}}x\,.

Clearly, we have
{\mathcal{E}}[\lambda,V,\varphi]=0
. In addition, for all fixed
V
and
\phi
, the map
\mu\mapsto{\mathcal{E}}[\mu,V,\phi]
is decreasing. It is proved in
[
63
, Lemma 2.4]
, that for all
-\,m<\mu<\lambda_{D}^{\hbar,c}(V)
, the quadratic map
\phi\mapsto{\mathcal{E}}[\mu,V,\phi]
is positive definite, and that, for
\mu=\lambda_{D}(V)
, we have
{\mathcal{E}}[\mu,V,\phi]=0
if and only if
\phi=\varphi
, up to a multiplicative constant. In particular, we have

\forall\,\phi\in C^{\infty}_{0}({\mathbb{R}})\,,\quad\forall\,V\in\mathrm{L}^{p}({\mathbb{R}})\,,\quad\|V\|_{p}\leq\alpha_{D}^{\hbar,c}(\lambda,p)\implies{\mathcal{E}}[\lambda,V,\phi]\geq 0\,.

Minimizing
{\mathcal{E}}[\lambda,W,\phi]
in
W
such that
\|W\|_{p}=\alpha\leq\alpha_{D}^{\hbar,c}(\lambda,p)
shows that the optimal
W
solves the Euler-Lagrange equation of the implicit form

\nu\,W^{p-1}=|\phi|^{2}+\dfrac{(\hbar\,c)^{2}\,|\phi^{\prime}|^{2}}{(\lambda+m\,c^{2}+W)^{2}}\,,

(C.4)

where
\nu\geq 0
is now the Lagrange multiplier for the constraint
\|W\|_{p}=\alpha
. Note that for all fixed
a
,
b
,
c\geq 0
, the equation

\nu\,X^{p-1}=a+\frac{b}{(c+X)^{2}}

has a unique solution in
X_{\nu}\geq 0
, as the left-hand side is an increasing function of
X
, while the right-hand side is decreasing, and that
\nu\mapsto X_{\nu}
is increasing. So for fixed
\nu\geq 0
, there is a unique
W=V_{\nu}[\phi]
satisfying (
C.4
) and the map
\nu\mapsto V_{\nu}
is pointwise decreasing, hence so is the map
\nu\mapsto\|V_{\nu}\|_{p}
. With
\alpha_{D}^{\hbar,c}(\lambda,p)
given by (
C.3
), we define

\nu_{*}(\lambda,p,\phi):=\inf\left\{\nu>0\,:\,\|V_{\nu}[\phi]\|_{p}\leq\alpha_{D}^{\hbar,c}(\lambda,p)\right\}\,.

Summarizing, we proved that for all
\phi\in C^{\infty}_{0}({\mathbb{R}})
and all
\nu\geq\nu_{*}(\lambda,p,\phi)
,

(\hbar\,c)^{2}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}{\frac{|\phi^{\prime}|^{2}}{\lambda+m\,c^{2}+V_{\nu}[\phi]}}\,{\mathrm{d}}x+\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}{\Big(m\,c^{2}-\lambda-V_{\nu}[\phi]\Big)\,|\phi|^{2}}\,{\mathrm{d}}x\geq 0\,,

(C.5)

which can be interpreted as a
Gagliardo–Nirenberg type inequality
for
\phi
alone. Such an inequality is known for a fixed, given potential
V
from
[
28
,
21
,
27
]
and it is then of Hardy-type, as for instance the new Hardy inequality in
[
35
]
, but the novelty in this paper is that we take
V=V_{\nu}[\phi]
thus making it a non-linear interpolation inequality.
While the form (
C.5
) is non-explicit, it allows to recover the usual Gagliardo–Nirenberg inequality in the non-relativistic limit as
c\to\infty
. By writing
\lambda=m\,c^{2}+E
for some
E<0
, (
C.5
) becomes

(\hbar\,c)^{2}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}{\frac{|\phi^{\prime}|^{2}}{2\,m\,c^{2}+E+V_{\nu}[\phi]}}\,{\mathrm{d}}x-\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}{\big(E+V_{\nu}[\phi]\big)\,|\phi|^{2}}\,{\mathrm{d}}x\geq 0\,.

Let us choose
\nu=\|\phi\|^{2}_{2\,p/(p-1)}
.
As
c\to\infty
, we get from (
C.4
) that
V_{\nu}[\phi]
converges to
\|\phi\|^{-2/(p-1)}_{2\,p/(p-1)}\,|\phi|^{2/(p-1)}
. Together with (
C.2
), we get that
\nu\geq\nu_{*}(\lambda,p,\phi)
in the limit
c\to\infty
whenever
|E|\geq\mathsf{K}_{p}\,(2\,m/\hbar^{2})^{d/(2\,p-d)}
. We obtain

\dfrac{\hbar^{2}}{2\,m}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}{|\phi^{\prime}|^{2}}\,{\mathrm{d}}x-\|\phi\|_{\frac{2\,p}{p-1}}^{2}\geq E\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}{|\phi|^{2}}\,{\mathrm{d}}x\,.

This inequality is the Gagliardo–Nirenberg inequality (
1.1
) written in non-scale invariant form, for an appropriate choice of the parameter
\lambda
in (
1.1
).

Anhang D
The case
p=d=1

This appendix deals with the limit case
p=1
of Theorem
1.3
devoted to the one-dimensional Keller estimates. We give a computation of
\alpha_{D}(\lambda,1)
which is not based on the limit as
p\to 1_{+}
of the nonlinear estimates and prove that any sequence of optimizing potentials concentrates into a Dirac
\delta
distribution.

Proposition D.1
.

If
d=1
, then
\alpha_{D}(\lambda,1)=\arccos(\lambda/m)
. More specifically, for all
\alpha\in(0,\pi)
, all
V\in\mathrm{L}^{1}({\mathbb{R}},{\mathbb{R}}^{+})
with
\|{V}\|_{1}=\alpha
, if
\lambda\in(-m,m)
is an eigenvalue of
\not{\mathcal{D}}_{m}-V
, then we have the strict inequality

m\,\cos\alpha<\lambda\,.

In addition, any sequence of nonnegative potentials
(V_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
with
\|{V_{n}}\|_{1}=\alpha
and eigenvalues
\lambda_{n}
approaching
m\,\cos\alpha
, converges as
n\to+\infty
to a Dirac
\delta
distribution.

According to
[
67
]
, “the method of directly solving the Dirac equation with a
\delta
-function potential and the method of obtaining the solution by first solving the Dirac equation with a short-range potential and afterward taking the
\delta
-function limit, lead to different results” [concerning the spectrum]. This issue is known as Klein’s paradox. Although the Keller-Lieb-Thirring (
1.12
) makes sense for any nonnegative potential
V\in\mathrm{L}^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
, it is a natural question to investigate by direct methods whether the bound is achieved in the larger set of bounded nonnegative measures and consider sequences of optimizing potentials.

Beweis.

We start with a calculation for a bounded and compactly supported potential
V
. In this case, the eigenvalue equation rewrites as

\Psi^{\prime}=\big({\mathrm{i}}\,\sigma_{2}\,(V+\lambda)+m\,\sigma_{1}\big)\,\Psi\,.

We decompose
\Psi
on the (not-orthonormal) basis given by the eigenvectors
e_{\pm}
of the matrix
{\mathrm{i}}\,\lambda\,\sigma_{2}-m\,\sigma_{1}
defined by

e_{\pm}:=\begin{pmatrix}\sqrt{m^{2}-\lambda^{2}}\,\\
\pm\,(m-\lambda)\end{pmatrix}\quad\text{such that}\quad\begin{pmatrix}0&m+\lambda\\
m-\lambda&0\end{pmatrix}e_{\pm}=\pm\,\sqrt{m^{2}-\lambda^{2}}\,e_{\pm}\,.

Decomposing
\Psi(x)=\mathsf{a}(x)\,e_{+}+\mathsf{b}(x)\,e_{-}
and using the identities

\displaystyle\langle{\mathrm{i}}\,\sigma_{2}\,e_{\pm},e_{\pm}\rangle=0\,,\quad

\displaystyle\langle{\mathrm{i}}\,\sigma_{2}\,e_{\pm},e_{\mp}\rangle=\pm\,2\,(m-\lambda)\,\sqrt{m^{2}-\lambda^{2}}\,,

\displaystyle\langle{\mathrm{i}}\,\sigma_{2}\,e_{\pm},{\mathrm{i}}\,\sigma_{2}\,e_{\pm}\rangle=2\,m\,(m-\lambda)\,,

\displaystyle\quad\langle{\mathrm{i}}\,\sigma_{2}\,e_{\pm},{\mathrm{i}}\,\sigma_{2}\,e_{\mp}\rangle=2\,\lambda\,(m-\lambda)\,,

gives, with
\mathsf{W}:=V/\sqrt{m^{2}-\lambda^{2}}
,

\displaystyle\mathsf{a}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\left(\sqrt{m^{2}-\lambda^{2}}-\lambda\,\mathsf{W}\right)\mathsf{a}-m\,\mathsf{W}\,\mathsf{b}\,,

\displaystyle\mathsf{b}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=-\left(\sqrt{m^{2}-\lambda^{2}}-\lambda\,\mathsf{W}\right)\mathsf{b}+m\,\mathsf{W}\,\mathsf{a}\,.

Since
V
(and
\mathsf{W}
) are compactly supported, a square-integrable solution must have
\mathsf{b}=0
in a neighborhood of
-\infty
and
\mathsf{a}=0
in a neighborhood of
+\infty
. Without loss of generality, we take a solution with
\mathsf{a}(x)>0
for
x
near
-\infty
.
Since

(\mathsf{a}\,\mathsf{b})^{\prime}=m\,\mathsf{W}\,(\mathsf{a}^{2}-\mathsf{b}^{2})

is nonnegative if
|\mathsf{a}|>|\mathsf{b}|
,
such a solution enters the first
(\mathsf{a},\mathsf{b})
quadrant and stays in the first quadrant until the first value of
x
such that
\mathsf{a}(x)=0
. We denote this value by
x_{1}
, with
x_{1}=+\infty
if
\mathsf{a}
does not change sign.
In the interval
(-\infty,x_{1})
, the ratio
\mathsf{t}:=\mathsf{b}/\mathsf{a}
is well-defined and satisfies

\mathsf{t}^{\prime}=\frac{1}{\mathsf{a}^{2}}\left(-\,2\,\mathsf{a}\,\mathsf{b}\left(\sqrt{m^{2}-\lambda^{2}}-\lambda\,\mathsf{W}\right)+m\,\mathsf{W}\left(\mathsf{a}^{2}+\mathsf{b}^{2}\right)\right)\\
=-\,2\,\sqrt{m^{2}-\lambda^{2}}\,\mathsf{t}+\mathsf{W}\left(m+m\,\mathsf{t}^{2}+2\,\lambda\,\mathsf{t}\right)\,.

We finally define the angle

\theta_{\lambda}(\mathsf{t}):=\arctan\left(\frac{m\,\mathsf{t}+\lambda}{\sqrt{m^{2}-\lambda^{2}}}\right)\,,

such that
\lim_{x\to x_{1}}\theta_{\lambda}(\mathsf{t}(x))=\pi/2
and

\frac{1}{\sqrt{m^{2}-\lambda^{2}}}\,(\theta_{\lambda}\circ\mathsf{t})^{\prime}=\frac{\mathsf{t}^{\prime}}{m+2\,\lambda\,\mathsf{t}+m\,\mathsf{t}^{2}}=\mathsf{W}-2\,\frac{\sqrt{m^{2}-\lambda^{2}}\,\mathsf{t}}{m+2\,\lambda\,\mathsf{t}+m\,\mathsf{t}^{2}}\,.

(D.1)

Integrating for
x\in(-\infty,x_{1})
, we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{\pi/2-\theta_{\lambda}(0)}{\sqrt{m^{2}-\lambda^{2}}}=\int_{-\infty}^{x_{1}}\mathsf{W}(s)\,{\mathrm{d}}s-2\int_{-\infty}^{x_{1}}\frac{\sqrt{m^{2}-\lambda^{2}}\,\mathsf{t}}{m+2\,\lambda\,\mathsf{t}+m\,\mathsf{t}^{2}}\,{\mathrm{d}}s<\frac{\alpha}{\sqrt{m^{2}-\lambda^{2}}}\,.

(D.2)

Since
\theta_{\lambda}(0)=\arcsin(\lambda/m)
, we obtain

\arccos(\lambda/m)<\alpha\quad\text{or}\quad\lambda>m\,\cos\alpha\,.

In order to approximate unbounded potentials, we need an estimate on the negative term in (
D.2
). Take any number
c>1
. Since
\mathsf{t}
is continuous, there is an interval
I_{V}(c)\subset(-\infty,x_{1}]
such that
\mathsf{t}(x)\in(1/c,c)
for all
x\in I_{V}(c)
.
We have the bound (note that the integrand is symmetric under
\mathsf{t}\mapsto 1/\mathsf{t}
)

\int_{I_{V}(c)}\frac{2\,(m^{2}-\lambda^{2})\,\mathsf{t}(s)}{m+m\,\mathsf{t}(s)^{2}+2\,\lambda\,\mathsf{t}(s)}\,{\mathrm{d}}s\geq\frac{2\,(m^{2}-\lambda^{2})\,c}{m+m\,c^{2}+2\,\lambda\,c}\,|I_{V}(c)|

and therefore

\arccos(\lambda/m)\leq\alpha-\frac{2\,(m^{2}-\lambda^{2})\,c}{m+m\,c^{2}+2\,\lambda\,c}\,|I_{V}(c)|\,.

(D.3)

To prove that
|I_{V}(c)|
cannot be arbitrarily small, we integrate (
D.1
) on
I_{V}(c)
, which gives

\displaystyle\theta_{\lambda}(c)-\theta_{\lambda}(1/c)\leq\int_{I_{V}(c)}V(s)\,{\mathrm{d}}s\leq Q_{V}\big(|I_{V}(c)|\big)

(D.4)

where we have defined

Q_{V}(r):=\sup_{x\in{\mathbb{R}}}\int_{x-r/2}^{x+r/2}V(s)\,{\mathrm{d}}s\,.

Now, assume that
(V_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
is a sequence of potentials with
\|{V_{n}}\|_{1}=\alpha
and eigenvalues
\lambda_{n}
converging to
\lambda:=m\,\cos\alpha
. Without loss of generality, we may assume that each
V_{n}
is bounded and compactly supported. By (
D.3
), in order to approach the equality case, we need that
|I_{V_{n}}(c)|
tends to zero for each
c>1
. We now use (
D.4
) to show that this implies the convergence (after suitable translations) to a Dirac
\delta
distribution.

Fix
\epsilon>0
and
r>0
. Fix
c>1
and
n_{0}
such that for all
n\geq n_{0}
, we have

\theta_{\lambda_{n}}(c)-\theta_{\lambda_{n}}(1/c)\geq\theta_{\lambda}(+\infty)-\theta_{\lambda}(0)-\epsilon=\alpha-\epsilon\,.

Upon increasing
n_{0}
, we can assume
|I_{V_{n}}(c)|\leq r
for all
n\geq n_{0}
. From (
D.4
), this gives

Q_{V_{n}}(r)\geq Q_{V_{n}}(|I_{V_{n}}(c)|)\geq\alpha-\epsilon\,.

Since
r
and
\epsilon
are arbirary, we have shown that
Q_{V_{n}}
converges pointwise to
\alpha
.
In the language of concentration-compactness, this excludes
vanishing
and
dichotomy
and implies that, after a sequence of translations,
V_{n}
converges to a measure of total mass
\alpha
supported at the origin, hence, to a Dirac
\delta
distribution.
∎
