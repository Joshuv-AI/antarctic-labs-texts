Real-time tracking of moving objects from scattering matrix in real-world microwave imaging
Seong-Ho Son, Kwang-Jae Lee, Won-Kwang Park

Abstract
The problem of the real-time microwave imaging of small, moving objects from a scattering matrix, whose elements are measured scattering parameters, without diagonal elements is considered herein. An imaging algorithm based on a Kirchhoff migration operated at single frequency is designed, and its mathematical structure is investigated by establishing a relationship with an infinite series of Bessel functions of integer order and antenna configuration. This is based on the application of the Born approximation to the scattering parameters of small objects. The structure explains the reason for the detection of moving objects via a designed imaging function and supplies its some properties. To demonstrate the strengths and weaknesses of the proposed algorithm, various simulations with real-data are conducted.

Most studies have focused on the development of detection algorithms for unknown objects located in a wide area; however, the detection of the movements of small objects or changes in permittivity/conductivity distributions in small or narrow regions has not been sufficiently investigated. Detecting small objects is not an easy problem to solve but can be applied to various real-world problems, such as the diagnoses of cerebral hemorrhages, imaging of crack propagations in walls or bridges, and through-wall imaging. In general, objects exhibit material properties (permittivities and conductivities) that are different from their background media; thus, most studies have focused on retrieving complete information about these properties. Unfortunately, owing to the intrinsic ill-posedness and nonlinearity of the inverse scattering problem, it cannot be successfully and satisfactorily resolved.

To solve this problem, various inversion techniques and corresponding computational environments have been investigated. The most popular and appropriate approaches in real-world applications are based on the Newton-type iteration scheme, which involves retrieving information about the shape, location, and material properties of unknown objects (minimizers); these unknown objects minimize the discrete norm (generally,

Herein, we apply Kirchhoff migration for a real-time tracking of moving small objects from the scattering matrix, whose elements are measured scattering parameters at a fixed frequency. In contrast to the traditional studies in inverse scattering problems, the diagonal elements of a scattering matrix cannot be determined from a microwave machine, i.e., scattering parameters cannot be obtained when the transmitting and receiving antennas are at the same location. Considering such a limitation, an imaging function based on the Kirchhoff migration for tracking moving objects is designed, and its mathematical structure was rigorously analyzed by establishing a relation with an infinite series of Bessel functions of integer order and antenna configurations. Based on this relation, we can demonstrate that designed tracking algorithm is fast and effective. Moreover, it is possible to guarantee unique determination of moving objects. To illustrate the feasibilities of the designed technique and to avoid committing

1
The forward problem and scattering parameters

In this section, the forward model and scattering parameters are briefly introduced in the presence of a set of objects with small diameter. Let
D_{m}(t)
,
m=1,2,\cdots,M
, be an (cross-sectional) object with location
\mathbf{r}_{m}(t)
at time
t
and
D(t)
denotes the collection of
D_{m}(t)
. Throughout this study, all
D_{m}(t)
are included in a homogeneous region of interest (ROI)
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
and surrounded by several transmitting and receiving antennas
\mathbf{A}_{n}
located at
\mathbf{a}_{n}
,
n=1,2,\cdots,N
, with
|\mathbf{a}_{n}|=R
. We denote
\mathcal{A}
as the collection of antennas
\mathbf{A}_{n}
and assume that all
D_{m}(t)
and
\Omega
are characterized by their dielectric permittivity and electric conductivity at a given angular frequency
\omega=2\pi f
, i.e., the magnetic permeability of all objects are constant such that
\mu(\mathbf{r},t)\equiv\mu_{\mathrm{b}}=4\pi\times${10}^{-7}\text{\,}\mathrm{H}$
,
\mathbf{r}\in\Omega
. We denote
\varepsilon(\mathbf{r},t)
and
\sigma(\mathbf{r},t)
as the piecewise constant permittivity and conductivity

\varepsilon(\mathbf{r},t)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{rcl}\varepsilon_{m}&\mbox{for}&\mathbf{r}\in D_{m}(t),\\
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{b}}&\mbox{for}&\mathbf{r}\in\Omega\backslash\overline{D(t)},\end{array}\right.\quad\mbox{and}\quad\sigma(\mathbf{r},t)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{rcl}\sigma_{m}&\mbox{for}&\mathbf{r}\in D_{m}(t),\\
\sigma_{\mathrm{b}}&\mbox{for}&\mathbf{r}\in\Omega\backslash\overline{D(t)},\end{array}\right.

respectively. With this, we further assume that the following relationships hold for
m=1,2,\cdots,M
,

\omega\varepsilon_{\mathrm{b}}\gg\sigma_{\mathrm{b}}\quad\text{and}\quad\left(\sqrt{\frac{\varepsilon_{m}}{\varepsilon_{\mathrm{b}}}}-1\right)\text{diam}(D_{m})<\frac{\text{wavelength}}{4},

(1)

where
\text{diam}(D_{m})
denotes the diameter of
D_{m}
.

Let
\mathbf{E}_{\inc}(\mathbf{a}_{q},\mathbf{r})
be the time-harmonic incident electric field in a homogeneous medium resulting from a point current density at
\mathbf{A}_{q}
. Then, based on the Maxwell equation,
\mathbf{E}_{\inc}(\mathbf{a}_{q},\mathbf{r})
satisfies

\nabla\times\mathbf{E}_{\inc}(\mathbf{a}_{q},\mathbf{r})=i\omega\mu_{\mathrm{b}}\mathbf{H}_{\inc}(\mathbf{a}_{q},\mathbf{r})\quad\mbox{and}\quad\nabla\times\mathbf{H}_{\inc}(\mathbf{a}_{q},\mathbf{r})=(\sigma_{\mathrm{b}}-i\omega\varepsilon_{\mathrm{b}})\mathbf{E}_{\inc}(\mathbf{a}_{q},\mathbf{r}).

Let
\mathbf{E}_{\tot}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{a}_{p})
be the corresponding total electric field due to the existence of
D(t)
measured at
\mathbf{A}_{p}
. Then,
\mathbf{E}_{\tot}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{a}_{p})
satisfies

\nabla\times\mathbf{E}_{\tot}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{a}_{p})=i\omega\mu_{\mathrm{b}}\mathbf{H}_{\tot}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{a}_{p})\quad\mbox{and}\quad\nabla\times\mathbf{H}_{\tot}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{a}_{p})=(\sigma(\mathbf{r},t)-i\omega\varepsilon(\mathbf{r},t))\mathbf{E}_{\tot}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{a}_{p})

with the transmission condition on
\partial D_{m}
,
m=1,2,\cdots,M
. Here, the time-harmonic dependence
e^{-i\omega t}
is assumed.

The scattering parameter (
S-
parameter)
S(p,q,t)
,
p,q=1,2,\cdots,N
, is defined as the ratio of the output voltage at the
\mathbf{A}_{p}
and the input voltage at the
\mathbf{A}_{q}
at the time
t
. Let
S_{\tot}(p,q,t)
and
S_{\inc}(p,q,t)
be the scattering parameter data in the presence and absence of
D(t)
, respectively, and denote
S_{\scat}(p,q,t)=S_{\tot}(p,q,t)-S_{\inc}(p,q,t)
as the scattered field
S
-parameter. Based on the simulation setup introduced in recent works
[
43
,
44
,
45
]
, only the
z
-components of
\mathbf{E}_{\inc}(\mathbf{a}_{q},\mathbf{r})
and
\mathbf{E}_{\tot}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{a}_{p})
can be handled so that by denoting
\mathrm{E}_{\inc}^{(z)}(\mathbf{a}_{q},\mathbf{r})
and
\mathrm{E}_{\tot}^{(z)}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{a}_{p})
as the
z
-component of incident and total fields, respectively,
S_{\scat}(p,q,t)
can be presented as the following integral equation formula:

S_{\scat}(p,q,t)=\frac{ik_{0}^{2}}{4\omega\mu_{\mathrm{b}}}\int_{\Omega}\left(\frac{\varepsilon(\mathbf{r}^{\prime},t)-\varepsilon_{\mathrm{b}}}{\varepsilon_{\mathrm{b}}}+i\frac{\sigma(\mathbf{r}^{\prime},t)-\sigma_{\mathrm{b}}}{\omega\varepsilon_{\mathrm{b}}}\right)\mathrm{E}_{\inc}^{(z)}(\mathbf{a}_{q},\mathbf{r}^{\prime})\mathrm{E}_{\tot}^{(z)}(\mathbf{r}^{\prime},\mathbf{a}_{p})d\mathbf{r}^{\prime},

(2)

where
k_{0}
denotes the lossless background wavenumber that satisfies
k_{0}^{2}=\omega^{2}\varepsilon_{\mathrm{b}}\mu_{\mathrm{b}}
, refer to
[
46
]
.

Kirchhoff migration for a real-time detection: introduction, analysis, and some properties

2.1
Introduction to imaging function of the Kirchhoff migration

Herein, we introduce the traditional imaging function of the Kirchhoff migration and apply it for a real-time mornitoring of moving objects
D_{m}(t)
generated from the scattering matrix
\mathbb{K}(t)
such that

\mathbb{K}(t)=\begin{bmatrix}S_{\scat}(1,1,t)&S_{\scat}(1,2,t)&\cdots&S_{\scat}(1,N,t)\\
S_{\scat}(2,1,t)&S_{\scat}(2,2,t)&\cdots&S_{\scat}(2,N,t)\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
S_{\scat}(N,1,t)&S_{\scat}(N,2,t)&\cdots&S_{\scat}(N,N,t)\end{bmatrix}.

(3)

Unfortunately, it is very difficult to apply (
2
) directly to design an imaging function because exact expression of
\mathrm{E}_{\tot}^{(z)}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{a}_{p})
is unknown. Since we already assumed the condition (
1
) holds, every
D_{m}(t)
can be regarded as an object with small diameter. Thus, based on
[
47
]
, it is possible to apply the Born approximation
\mathrm{E}_{\tot}^{(z)}(\mathbf{a}_{p},\mathbf{r}^{\prime})\approx\mathrm{E}_{\inc}^{(z)}(\mathbf{a}_{p},\mathbf{r}^{\prime})
so that (
2
) can be approximated as

S_{\scat}(p,q,t)\approx\frac{ik_{0}^{2}}{4\omega\mu_{\mathrm{b}}}\int_{D(t)}\left(\frac{\varepsilon(\mathbf{r}^{\prime},t)-\varepsilon_{\mathrm{b}}}{\varepsilon_{\mathrm{b}}}+i\frac{\sigma(\mathbf{r}^{\prime},t)-\sigma_{\mathrm{b}}}{\omega\varepsilon_{\mathrm{b}}}\right)\mathrm{E}_{\inc}^{(z)}(\mathbf{a}_{q},\mathbf{r}^{\prime})\mathrm{E}_{\inc}^{(z)}(\mathbf{a}_{p},\mathbf{r}^{\prime})d\mathbf{r}^{\prime}.

(4)

and correspondingly, applying (
4
) to (
3
),
\mathbb{K}(t)
can be decomposed as

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \mathbb{K}(t)&\approx\frac{ik_{0}^{2}}{4\omega\mu_{\mathrm{b}}}\int_{D(t)}\left(\frac{\varepsilon(\mathbf{r}^{\prime},t)-\varepsilon_{\mathrm{b}}}{\varepsilon_{\mathrm{b}}}+i\frac{\sigma(\mathbf{r}^{\prime},t)-\sigma_{\mathrm{b}}}{\omega\varepsilon_{\mathrm{b}}}\right)\begin{bmatrix}\vskip 3.0pt plus 1.0pt minus 1.0pt\mathrm{E}_{\inc}^{(z)}(\mathbf{a}_{1},\mathbf{r}^{\prime})\\
\mathrm{E}_{\inc}^{(z)}(\mathbf{a}_{2},\mathbf{r}^{\prime})\\
\vdots\\
\mathrm{E}_{\inc}^{(z)}(\mathbf{a}_{N},\mathbf{r}^{\prime})\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\mathrm{E}_{\inc}^{(z)}(\mathbf{a}_{1},\mathbf{r}^{\prime})&\cdots&\mathrm{E}_{\inc}^{(z)}(\mathbf{a}_{N},\mathbf{r}^{\prime})\end{bmatrix}d\mathbf{r}^{\prime}\\
&:=\frac{ik_{0}^{2}}{4\omega\mu_{\mathrm{b}}}\int_{D(t)}\mathcal{O}(\mathbf{r}^{\prime},t)\mathbf{G}(\mathbf{r}^{\prime},t)\mathbf{G}(\mathbf{r}^{\prime},t)^{\mathtt{T}}d\mathbf{r}^{\prime}.\end{aligned}

(5)

Based on the structure of the above decomposition, let us generate the following unit vector: for each
\mathbf{r}\in\Omega
,

\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{r})=\frac{\mathbf{W}(\mathbf{r})}{|\mathbf{W}(\mathbf{r})|},\quad\text{where}\quad\mathbf{W}(\mathbf{r})=\begin{bmatrix}\vskip 3.0pt plus 1.0pt minus 1.0pt\mathrm{E}_{\inc}^{(z)}(\mathbf{a}_{1},\mathbf{r})\\
\mathrm{E}_{\inc}^{(z)}(\mathbf{a}_{2},\mathbf{r})\\
\vdots\\
\mathrm{E}_{\inc}^{(z)}(\mathbf{a}_{N},\mathbf{r})\end{bmatrix}.

Now, let us define an inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\ell^{2}(\mathcal{A})}
on the Lebesgue space
\ell^{2}(\mathcal{A})
such that

\langle\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{r}),\mathbf{G}(\mathbf{r}^{\prime},t)\rangle_{\ell^{2}(\mathcal{A})}=\overline{\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{r})}^{\mathtt{T}}\mathbf{G}(\mathbf{r}^{\prime},t)=\frac{1}{|\mathbf{W}(\mathbf{r})|}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\overline{\mathrm{E}_{\inc}^{(z)}(\mathbf{a}_{n},\mathbf{r})}\mathrm{E}_{\inc}^{(z)}(\mathbf{a}_{n},\mathbf{r}^{\prime}).

(6)

Then, based on the orthogonal property of the
\ell^{2}(\mathcal{A})
, we can examine that the value of
\langle\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{r}),\mathbf{G}(\mathbf{r}^{\prime},t)\rangle_{\ell^{2}(\mathcal{A})}
will reach its maximum value when
\mathbf{r}=\mathbf{r}^{\prime}\in D(t)

\mathbf{r}\in\Omega

\mathfrak{F}_{\kir}(\mathbf{r},t)=|\overline{\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{r})}^{\mathtt{T}}\mathbb{K}(t)\overline{\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{r})}|.

(7)

Based on the (
5
) and (
6
), the value of
\mathfrak{F}_{\kir}(\mathbf{r},t)
is expected to reach its maximum value when
\mathbf{r}=\mathbf{r}^{\prime}\in D(t)

36
]

Contrary to the traditional simulation setup, each

for
n=1,2,\cdots,N

cannot be determined so that generated scattering matrix is of the following form

\mathbb{K}(t)=\begin{bmatrix}\text{unknown}&S_{\scat}(1,2,t)&\cdots&S_{\scat}(1,N,t)\\
S_{\scat}(2,1,t)&\text{unknown}&\cdots&S_{\scat}(2,N,t)\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
S_{\scat}(N,1,t)&S_{\scat}(N,2,t)&\cdots&\text{unknown}\end{bmatrix}.

For a related discussions, we recommend some references

,

\mathbb{G}(t)=\begin{bmatrix}0&S_{\scat}(1,2,t)&\cdots&S_{\scat}(1,N,t)\\
S_{\scat}(2,1,t)&0&\cdots&S_{\scat}(2,N,t)\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
S_{\scat}(N,1,t)&S_{\scat}(N,2,t)&\cdots&0\end{bmatrix}.

(8)

We refer to

for an explanation of why the diagonal elements of scattering matrix were set to zero. Although, the diagonal elements are missing, the following imaging function can be introduced with a similar way to (

\mathfrak{F}(\mathbf{r},t)=|\overline{\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{r})}^{\mathtt{T}}\mathbb{G}(t)\overline{\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{r})}|.

(9)

2.2
Structure of the imaging function

In order to explain the availability of real-time detection of moving object, mathematical structure of the designed imaging function is carefully explored by establishing a relationship with an infinite series of Bessel functions.

Theorem 2.1

(Structure of imaging function)
.

Let
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{n}=\mathbf{a}_{n}/R=(\cos\theta_{n},\sin\theta_{n})^{\mathtt{T}}
and
\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}^{\prime}=|\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}^{\prime}|(\cos\phi,\sin\phi)^{\mathtt{T}}
. If
|k_{\mathrm{b}}(\mathbf{a}_{n}-\mathbf{r})|\geq 0.25
for all
\mathbf{r}\in\Omega
, then
\mathfrak{F}(\mathbf{r},t)
can be represented as follows:

\mathfrak{F}(\mathbf{r},t)\approx\Bigg|\int_{D(t)}\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{r}^{\prime},t)\left(J_{0}(k_{\mathrm{b}}|\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}^{\prime}|)+\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\mathcal{E}(k_{\mathrm{b}}|\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}^{\prime}|,t)\right)^{2}d\mathbf{r}^{\prime}\\
-\frac{1}{N}\int_{D(t)}\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{r}^{\prime},t)\left(J_{0}(2k_{\mathrm{b}}|\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}^{\prime}|)+\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\mathcal{E}(2k_{\mathrm{b}}|\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}^{\prime}|,t)\right)d\mathbf{r}^{\prime}\Bigg|,

(10)

where
J_{s}
is a Bessel function of the order
s
of the first kind,

\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{r}^{\prime},t)=\frac{N\omega\varepsilon_{\mathrm{b}}}{32k_{\mathrm{b}}R\pi}\left(\frac{\varepsilon(\mathbf{r}^{\prime},t)-\varepsilon_{\mathrm{b}}}{\varepsilon_{\mathrm{b}}}+i\frac{\sigma(\mathbf{r}^{\prime},t)-\sigma_{\mathrm{b}}}{\omega\varepsilon_{\mathrm{b}}}\right),\quad\text{and}\quad\mathcal{E}(k_{\mathrm{b}}|\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}^{\prime}|,t)=\sum_{s=-\infty,s\neq 0}^{\infty}i^{s}J_{s}(k_{\mathrm{b}}|\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}^{\prime}|)e^{is(\theta_{n}-\phi)}.

Proof.

Since
|k_{\mathrm{b}}(\mathbf{a}_{n}-\mathbf{r})|\geq 0.25
for all
n=1,2,\cdots,N
, the following asymptotic form of the Hankel function holds (see
[
51
, Theorem 2.5]
, for instance)

H_{0}^{(1)}(k_{\mathrm{b}}|\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}^{\prime}|)\approx\frac{(1-i)e^{ik_{\mathrm{b}}|\mathbf{r}|}}{\sqrt{k_{\mathrm{b}}\pi|\mathbf{a}_{n}|}}e^{-ik_{\mathrm{b}}\boldsymbol{\theta}_{n}\cdot\mathbf{r}^{\prime}}.

(11)

Then, since

\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{r})\approx\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\bigg[e^{-ik_{\mathrm{b}}\boldsymbol{\theta}_{1}\cdot\mathbf{r}},e^{-ik_{\mathrm{b}}\boldsymbol{\theta}_{2}\cdot\mathbf{r}},\ldots,e^{-ik_{\mathrm{b}}\boldsymbol{\theta}_{N}\cdot\mathbf{r}}\bigg]^{\mathtt{T}}

and

\mathbb{G}(t)\approx C\begin{bmatrix}\vskip 6.0pt plus 2.0pt minus 2.0pt0&\displaystyle\int_{D(t)}\mathcal{O}(\mathbf{r}^{\prime},t)e^{-ik_{\mathrm{b}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{1}+\boldsymbol{\theta}_{2})\cdot\mathbf{r}^{\prime}}d\mathbf{r}^{\prime}&\cdots&\displaystyle\int_{D(t)}\mathcal{O}(\mathbf{r}^{\prime},t)e^{-ik_{\mathrm{b}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{1}+\boldsymbol{\theta}_{N})\cdot\mathbf{r}^{\prime}}d\mathbf{r}^{\prime}\\
\displaystyle\int_{D(t)}\mathcal{O}(\mathbf{r}^{\prime},t)e^{-ik_{\mathrm{b}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{2}+\boldsymbol{\theta}_{1})\cdot\mathbf{r}^{\prime}}d\mathbf{r}^{\prime}&0&\cdots&\displaystyle\int_{D(t)}\mathcal{O}(\mathbf{r}^{\prime},t)e^{-ik_{\mathrm{b}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{2}+\boldsymbol{\theta}_{N})\cdot\mathbf{r}^{\prime}}d\mathbf{r}^{\prime}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\displaystyle\int_{D(t)}\mathcal{O}(\mathbf{r}^{\prime},t)e^{-ik_{\mathrm{b}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{N}+\boldsymbol{\theta}_{1})\cdot\mathbf{r}^{\prime}}d\mathbf{r}^{\prime}&\displaystyle\int_{D(t)}\mathcal{O}(\mathbf{r}^{\prime},t)e^{-ik_{\mathrm{b}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{N}+\boldsymbol{\theta}_{2})\cdot\mathbf{r}^{\prime}}d\mathbf{r}^{\prime}&\cdots&0\\
\end{bmatrix},

we can derive

\overline{\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{r})}^{\mathtt{T}}\mathbb{G}(t)\approx\frac{C}{\sqrt{N}}\begin{bmatrix}\vskip 3.0pt plus 1.0pt minus 1.0pt\displaystyle\int_{D(t)}\mathcal{O}(\mathbf{r}^{\prime},t)e^{-ik_{\mathrm{b}}\boldsymbol{\theta}_{1}\cdot\mathbf{r}^{\prime}}\left(\sum_{n\in\mathcal{N}_{1}}e^{ik_{\mathrm{b}}\boldsymbol{\theta}_{n}\cdot(\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}^{\prime})}\right)d\mathbf{r}^{\prime}\\

(12)

\displaystyle\int_{D(t)}\mathcal{O}(\mathbf{r}^{\prime},t)e^{-ik_{\mathrm{b}}\boldsymbol{\theta}_{2}\cdot\mathbf{r}^{\prime}}\left(\sum_{n=1}^{N}e^{ik_{\mathrm{b}}\boldsymbol{\theta}_{n}\cdot(\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}^{\prime})}-e^{ik_{\mathrm{b}}\boldsymbol{\theta}_{2}\cdot(\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}^{\prime})}\right)d\mathbf{r}^{\prime}\\
\vdots\\
\displaystyle\int_{D(t)}\mathcal{O}(\mathbf{r}^{\prime},t)e^{-ik_{\mathrm{b}}\boldsymbol{\theta}_{N}\cdot\mathbf{r}^{\prime}}\left(\sum_{n=1}^{N}e^{ik_{\mathrm{b}}\boldsymbol{\theta}_{n}\cdot(\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}^{\prime})}-e^{ik_{\mathrm{b}}\boldsymbol{\theta}_{N}\cdot(\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}^{\prime})}\right)d\mathbf{r}^{\prime}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\vskip 3.0pt plus 1.0pt minus 1.0pte^{ik_{\mathrm{b}}\boldsymbol{\theta}_{1}\cdot\mathbf{r}}\\
e^{ik_{\mathrm{b}}\boldsymbol{\theta}_{2}\cdot\mathbf{r}}\\
\vdots\\

=\frac{C}{N}\begin{bmatrix}\vskip 3.0pt plus 1.0pt minus 1.0pt\displaystyle\int_{D(t)}\mathcal{O}(\mathbf{r}^{\prime},t)e^{-ik_{\mathrm{b}}\boldsymbol{\theta}_{1}\cdot\mathbf{r}^{\prime}}\left(NJ_{0}(k_{\mathrm{b}}|\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}^{\prime}|)+\sum_{n=1}^{N}\mathcal{E}(k_{\mathrm{b}}|\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}^{\prime}|,t)-e^{ik_{\mathrm{b}}\boldsymbol{\theta}_{1}\cdot(\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}^{\prime})}\right)d\mathbf{r}^{\prime}\\

2.3
Various properties of imaging function

On the basis of the result (
10
), we can explore some properties of imaging function as follows.

Property 2.1

(Availability of detection)
.

Since
J_{0}(0)=1
and
J_{s}(0)=0
for
s\neq 0
, map of
\mathfrak{F}(\mathbf{r},t)
will contain peaks of large magnitudes when
\mathbf{r}=\mathbf{r}^{\prime}\in D(t)
. Hence, the locations of the moving objects can be imaged via the map of
\mathfrak{F}(\mathbf{r},t)
. Furthermore, owing to the oscillating property of the Bessel functions, some artifacts will be included on the map of
\mathfrak{F}(\mathbf{r},t)
.

Property 2.2

(Dependence of the material properties)
.

If
\mathbf{r}=\mathbf{r}^{\prime}\in D_{m}(t)
then since
J_{0}(k_{\mathrm{b}}|\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}^{\prime}|)=1
and
\mathcal{E}(k_{\mathrm{b}}|\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}^{\prime}|,t)=\mathcal{E}(2k_{\mathrm{b}}|\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}^{\prime}|,t)=0
, we have

\mathfrak{F}(\mathbf{r},t)\approx\bigg|\int_{D_{m}(t)}\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{r}^{\prime},t)d\mathbf{r}^{\prime}-\frac{1}{N}\int_{D_{m}(t)}\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{r}^{\prime},t)d\mathbf{r}^{\prime}\bigg|=\frac{(N-1)\omega\varepsilon_{\mathrm{b}}}{32|k_{\mathrm{b}}|R\pi}\bigg|\frac{\varepsilon_{m}-\varepsilon_{\mathrm{b}}}{\varepsilon_{\mathrm{b}}}+i\frac{\sigma_{m}-\sigma_{\mathrm{b}}}{\omega\varepsilon_{\mathrm{b}}}\bigg|\text{area}(D_{m}(t)).

Hence, we can conclude that the value of
\mathfrak{F}(\mathbf{r},t)
significantly depends on the size, permittivity, and conductivity of the object. This means that if the size, permittivity, and conductivity of an object
D_{m}(t)
is considerably larger than those of the others, peaks of large magnitude will appear on the map of
\mathfrak{F}(\mathbf{r},t)
at the location of this object because
|\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{r},t)|>|\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{r}^{\prime},t)|
for
\mathbf{r}\in D_{m}(t)
and
\mathbf{r}^{\prime}\in D(t)\backslash\overline{D_{m}(t)}
.

Property 2.3

(Ideal conditions for a proper detection)
.

Based on the structure of the factor
\mathcal{E}(k_{\mathrm{b}}|\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}^{\prime}|,t)
, it disturbs the detection of objects. Thus, eliminating the factors
\mathcal{E}(k_{\mathrm{b}}|\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}^{\prime}|,t)
and
\mathcal{E}(2k_{\mathrm{b}}|\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}^{\prime}|,t)
will guarantee good results. Notice that if one can increase
N
as much as possible (
N\longrightarrow+\infty
) or apply extremely high frequency (
\omega\longrightarrow+\infty
), then their effects can be reduced but this is inappropriate for real-world applications.

Property 2.4

(Practical condition for a proper detection)
.

To reduce the adverse effect of
\mathcal{E}(k_{\mathrm{b}}|\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}^{\prime}|,t)
, a condition must be found to satisfy the following equation:

\sum_{n=1}^{N}\sum_{s=-\infty,s\neq 0}^{\infty}i^{s}J_{s}(k_{\mathrm{b}}|\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}^{\prime}|)e^{is(\theta_{n}-\phi)}=0.

Since we have no a priori information of objects,
i^{s}J_{s}(k_{\mathrm{b}}|\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}^{\prime}|)
cannot be eliminated. This means that we must find a condition
\theta_{n}
such that

\sum_{s=-\infty,s\neq 0}^{\infty}\sum_{n=1}^{N}e^{is(\theta_{n}-\phi)}=\sum_{s=-\infty,s\neq 0}^{\infty}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\cos\big(s(\theta_{n}-\phi)\big)+i\sin\big(s(\theta_{n}-\phi)\big)=0.

Based on the periodic property of the cosine and sine functions, the effect of
\mathcal{E}(k_{\mathrm{b}}|\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}^{\prime}|,t)
can be reduced when even number of antennas are uniformly distributed on a circular array and total number of antennas
N
is greater than
8
. This means that the array configuration of the antennas affects the imaging quality and this is the theoretical reason for the even number of antennas and their symmetric location with respect to the origin in general. We refer to
[
52
,
34
,
44
]
for a similar phenomenon in various imaging techniques.

Property 2.5

(On the imaging of objects close to an antenna)
.

On the imaging of objects, the distance between each objects or between object and antennas significantly influences the imaging performance because the integral equation formula (
2
) holds for well-separated objects and Theorem
2.1
holds when
|k_{\mathrm{b}}(\mathbf{a}_{n}-\mathbf{r})|\geq 0.25
. If an object
D_{m}(t)
is very close to an antenna
\mathbf{A}_{n}
at time
t
such that

0<|\mathbf{a}_{n}-\mathbf{r}_{m}(t)|\ll\frac{1}{|k_{\mathrm{b}}|}

then based on the asymptotic form of the Hankel function

H_{0}^{(1)}(k_{\mathrm{b}}|\mathbf{a}_{n}-\mathbf{r}^{\prime}|)\approx 1+\frac{2i}{\pi}\left\{\ln\left(\frac{|k_{\mathrm{b}}(\mathbf{a}_{n}-\mathbf{r}^{\prime})|}{2}\right)+\gamma\right\}\longrightarrow\text{blow up}\quad\text{as}\quad\mathbf{r}^{\prime}\longrightarrow\mathbf{a}_{n},

some elements of
\mathbb{G}(t)
will be very large valued so that unexpected imaging results (appearance of several artifacts with large magnitudes, invisible of objects, etc.) will be obtained. Here,
\gamma=0.57721\ldots
denotes the Euler–Mascheroni constant.

Finally, based on the Property
2.1
, we can derive following important result of the unique determination.

Corollary 2.1

(Unique determination of moving objects)
.

For given angular frequency
\omega
, the moving objects
D_{m}(t)
at each time
t
can be detected uniquely through the map of
\mathfrak{F}(\mathbf{r},t)
with the same condition of Theorem
2.1
.

4
Conclusion and perspectives

Owing to the existence of small, moving objects and the non-iterative Kirchhoff migration technique in inverse scattering problems, we designed a real-time algorithm for imaging the moving objects on the basis of the representation formula of the scattering parameters. To examine the feasibility and explore some properties of the designed algorithm, we proved that the imaging function could be represented as the total and array configuration of the antennas and an infinite series of Bessel functions of integer order.

Numerical simulations were performed using experimental data generated by the ETRI to demonstrate that the Kirchhoff migration is very effective for a real-time detection of moving objects in microwave imaging. However, the algorithm’s application is currently restricted to the detection of small objects; therefore, further applications to the detection of the movements of arbitrary shaped extended objects or the evolution of crack-like defects must be considered. Forthcoming studies will focus on designing appropriate imaging algorithms, performing related mathematical analyses, and conducting related simulations.
