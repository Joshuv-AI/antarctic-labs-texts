Teleparallel gravity is a theory of gravity which replaces the Levi-Civita connection by a teleparallel connection – a metric-compatible connection with vanishing curvature.
Teleparallel equivalent of general relativity (TEGR) is a special case from this class of theories, and its dynamics is governed by equations which are equivalent to Einstein field equations of general relativity.
In 2009 Lucas and Pereira presented the idea of a new operator that would allow us to express the action of TEGR in a form reminiscent of the Yang-Mills action for a gauge theory, for which torsion is the field strength.
The authors constructed the operator as a duality operator acting on torsion 2-forms and curvature 2-forms, taking into account all the contractions of these forms with the volume form of the spacetime manifold.
The definition of this operator for other forms, however, remained unclear.
In this thesis, we seek to give a mathematically consistent definition of the operator, and we follow the results published by Lucas and Pereira.
We show that the approach taken by Lucas and Pereira does not lead to a consistent definition of this operator, because it results in the operator with the desired properties either being non-unique or non-existent.

Keywords:

duality operator, teleparallel gravity, tetrad formalism, torsion

Chapter 1
Introduction

General relativity is an exceptionally successful theory of gravity. To this day, it stood several observational tests on almost all scales of physics and remains together with quantum field theory one of the pillars of modern physics.

Albert Einstein formulated the field equations of general relativity in 1915. The fundamental idea of the theory is that gravity is a manifestation of the curvature of spacetime. It entirely disposed of the notion of gravitational force and described the motion of small bodies in the gravitational field as the natural motion of the bodies in the curved spacetime. The source of the curvature of spacetime is energy and momentum, as expressed by the Einstein field equations.

In 1920s, Einstein – who, of course, could not have been at that time aware of the need to modify his newly-established theory of gravity – was attempting to develop a unified theory of gravity and electromagnetism based on the use of tetrads (orthonormal frames) and distant parallelism (teleparallelism). After publishing eight papers on the topic in the
Sitzungsberichte
of the Prussian Academy, he abandoned his idea because it did not lead to his ultimate goal of unification of electromagnetism and gravity
[
1
]
. Nevertheless, Einstein’s work involved several important novelties, namely using tetrads instead of the metric tensor and considering other than Riemannian geometries to describe gravity. In 1960s and 1970s it became clear that Einstein teleparallelism can be a successful theory of gravity only, i.e. without attempts to incorporate electromagnetism
[
2
]
.

The development of quantum mechanics in 1920s and then quantum field theory in the following years started to expose some of the limitations of general relativity. Another challenge for general relativity came with the observations of the accelerating expansion of the universe
[
3
]
. One possible approach to explain these phenomena is making modifications in the matter sector of the theory, which requires modifications of the Standard Model of particle physics. However, it may also be the case that it is not necessary to significantly restructure the Standard Model (the matter section), but that the gravitational section requires further revisiting. Thus, alternative theories of gravity are of considerable interest, because, instead of changing the matter content of the theory, an alternative theory of gravity may potentially provide an explanation for phenomena which within the current cosmological paradigm based on general relativity are ascribed to the presence of dark energy and dark matter
[
4
,
5
]
.

Our current understanding of elementary particles, which make up matter, and three of the four fundamental interactions of nature -- electromagnetic, strong and weak -- is captured by the Standard Model of particle physics. It is a quantum field theory in which all the interactions are mediated by so-called gauge bosons
1
1

1

The gauge bosons of Standard Model are photons (which mediate the electromagnetic interaction), eight gluons (for the strong interaction) and
W^{+},W^{-}
and
Z^{0}
bosons (they mediate the weak interaction).
. Standard Model is an example of a gauge theory.

Gauge theories have a special place among the field theories. One of the reasons is that they can be quantized. Gauge bosons are particles which correspond to gauge fields, which are fields that have to be introduced into a theory to make a global symmetry of this theory to be local.
In other words, gauge theories have some unphysical degrees of freedom. The same physical fields correspond to several different configurations of the gauge fields. This is a kind of redundancy in the theoretical description of reality.

Examples of gauge theories include the Standard Model and electrodynamics (classical, as well as quantum), which is a
U(1)
-gauge theory – which means that it is invariant under
U(1)
local transformations – and the gauge bosons of this theory are photons. Of great importance for particle physics are Yang-Mills theories, which are non-abelian gauge theories. An example of a Yang-Mills theory is quantum chromodynamics (QCD), which is a
SU(3)
-gauge theory.

General relativity is coordinate-independent, which means that the laws are invariant under changes of coordinates. In other words, the equations take the same form in any coordinates. This, however, does not make it a gauge theory from the mathematical point of view, because conceptually this is a different kind of redundancy in the physical model. On the other hand, all the other fundamental forces of physics are described by gauge theories, so it is natural to expect that gravity too can be described in the framework of a gauge theory.

There exist several modified theories of gravity, and also attempts to conceptualize general relativity as a gauge theory. An interesting possibility that has been gaining momentum in the recent decades is
teleparallel gravity
. The interest in teleparallelism was revived in 1960s and 1970s by the works of Møller concerning the definition of the energy-momentum for gravity
[
6
,
7
,
8
]
, and then by Pellegrini and Plebański
[
9
]
. Another impulse for revisiting Einstein’s teleparalellism came from the work of Hayashi and Nakano on gauge aspects of gravity
[
10
]
.

While in general relativity the gravitational interaction is realized via the curvature of the Levi-Civita connection, which is metric-compatible and torsion-free, in teleparallel gravity the interaction is an effect of torsion of a teleparallel connection, that is, a metric-compatible connection with zero curvature. Torsion in this case acts as a force, which similarly to the Lorentz force equation of electromagnetism appears as an effective force term on the right-hand side of the equation of motion of a freefalling particle
[
11
]
.

One of the many theories that this torsion-based approach has produced is the teleparallel equivalent of general relativity (TEGR), which is dynamically equivalent to general relativity, meaning that they cannot be distinguished through classical experiments. However, they are conceptually quite different and in scenarios such as quantum gravity the theories may exhibit different behaviors.

In 1967 Hayashi and Nakano published a paper
[
10

which discussed teleparallel gravity as gauge theory of translations only, instead of gauging the Lorentz or Poincaré group. The Lagrangian density of the theory is quadratic in torsion, which is identified as the field strength of the translational group. Later, Cho has shown

that requiring covariance of the field equations restricts the form of the translational Lagrangian to the Lagrangian of TEGR, which Einstein considered already in 1929

Chapter 2
Metric-affine geometries

The mathematical formalism of general relativity appears to only require a differentiable spacetime manifold with with one geometric object defined on it – the metric tensor
g
. A differentiable manifold endowed with a metric tensor is called Riemannian manifold. However, a closer investigation reveals that there is another geometric structure needed in order to define covariant derivative, which is necessary to define the curvature tensor. This structure is called affine connection. In general relativity, the choice of a connection is special in the sense that it is completely dependent on the metric, and nothing else. In a more general geometric context, however, a connection on the manifold is independent of the metric. Even non-Riemannian manifolds may have a connection defined on them.

2.1
Linear connection

For the basics of differential geometry we follow here the exposition and notation from the textbook by Marián Fecko
[
17
]
.

Definition 2.1.1
.

Affine connection

\nabla
on a differentiable manifold
\mathcal{M}
is a mathematical structure such that to any vector field
W
on
\mathcal{M}
it assigns an operator
\nabla_{W}
, the covariant derivative in the direction of
W
, with the following properties:

1.

it is a linear operator on the tensor algebra, and it preserves the type of the tensor

\displaystyle\nabla_{W}:\mathcal{T}^{p}_{q}\left(\mathcal{M}\right)\longrightarrow\mathcal{T}^{p}_{q}\left(\mathcal{M}\right),

\displaystyle\nabla_{W}\left(A+\lambda B\right)=\nabla_{W}A+\lambda\nabla_{W}B,

\displaystyle A,B\in\mathcal{T}^{p}_{q}\left(\mathcal{M}\right),\lambda\in\mathbb{R}.

2.

its behavior on the tensor product obeys Leibniz rule

\displaystyle\nabla_{W}\left(A\otimes B\right)=\left(\nabla_{W}A\right)\otimes B+A\otimes\left(\nabla_{W}B\right),

\displaystyle A\in\mathcal{T}^{p}_{q}\left(\mathcal{M}\right),B\in\mathcal{T}^{p^{\prime}}_{q^{\prime}}\left(\mathcal{M}\right).

3.

on type

\displaystyle\nabla_{W}\psi=W\psi,

\displaystyle\psi\in\mathcal{F}\left(\mathcal{M}\right)\equiv\mathcal{T}^{0}_{0}\left(\mathcal{M}\right).

4.

\displaystyle\nabla_{W}\circ C=C\circ\nabla_{W},

5.

it is
\mathcal{F}
-linear in

\displaystyle\nabla_{V+fW}=\nabla_{V}+f\nabla_{W},

\displaystyle V,W\in\mathcal{T}^{1}_{0}\left(\mathcal{M}\right),f\in\mathcal{F}\left(\mathcal{M}\right).

2.3
Connection decomposition

The Christoffel symbols of a RLC connection on

\displaystyle Q_{\mu\nu\rho}

\displaystyle=g_{\rho\nu,\mu}-\Gamma^{\lambda}_{\nu\mu}g_{\lambda\rho}-\Gamma^{\lambda}_{\rho\mu}g_{\nu\lambda},

\displaystyle Q_{\mu\nu\rho}+Q_{\nu\mu\rho}-Q_{\rho\nu\mu}

\displaystyle=2g_{\rho\lambda}\accentset{\circ}{\Gamma}^{\lambda}_{\mu\nu}-g_{\lambda\rho}\left(\Gamma^{\lambda}_{\mu\nu}+\Gamma^{\lambda}_{\nu\mu}\right)

\displaystyle+g_{\lambda\mu}\left(\Gamma^{\lambda}_{\nu\rho}-\Gamma^{\lambda}_{\rho\nu}\right)+g_{\lambda\nu}\left(\Gamma^{\lambda}_{\mu\rho}-\Gamma^{\lambda}_{\rho\mu}\right),

(2.11)

and finally, using (

\displaystyle Q_{\mu\nu\rho}+Q_{\nu\mu\rho}-Q_{\rho\nu\mu}

Chapter 3
Tetrads

General relativity is essentialy just geometry on a Riemannian manifold. In order to do any calculations on a manifold we need to use coordinates, although the results of such calculations remain independent of the choice of coordinates. In the standard formulation of general relativity, all tensors are expressed with respect to coordinate bases and Einstein field equations are also expressed using a coordinate basis. However, it is possible, and sometimes even convenient, to express them with respect to a non-coordinate basis.

3.1
Holonomic and anholonomic frames

Let

The condition of duality gives us

\accentset{\bullet}{K}^{\alpha}_{\phantom{\alpha}\beta\lambda}\accentset{\circ}{\Gamma}^{\lambda}_{\mu\nu}-\accentset{\bullet}{K}^{\alpha}_{\phantom{\alpha}\beta\lambda}\accentset{\circ}{\Gamma}^{\lambda}_{\nu\mu}
allows us to simplify:

\displaystyle\accentset{\bullet}{R}^{\alpha}_{\phantom{\alpha}\beta\mu\nu}

\displaystyle=\accentset{\circ}{R}^{\alpha}_{\phantom{\alpha}\beta\mu\nu}+\accentset{\bullet}{K}^{\alpha}_{\phantom{\alpha}\lambda\mu}\accentset{\bullet}{K}^{\lambda}_{\phantom{\lambda}\beta\nu}-\accentset{\bullet}{K}^{\alpha}_{\phantom{\alpha}\lambda\nu}\accentset{\bullet}{K}^{\lambda}_{\phantom{\lambda}\beta\mu}+

\displaystyle+\left(\partial_{\mu}\accentset{\bullet}{K}^{\alpha}_{\phantom{\alpha}\beta\nu}-\accentset{\bullet}{K}^{\alpha}_{\phantom{\alpha}\lambda\nu}\accentset{\circ}{\Gamma}^{\lambda}_{\beta\mu}+\accentset{\bullet}{K}^{\lambda}_{\phantom{\lambda}\beta\nu}\accentset{\circ}{\Gamma}^{\alpha}_{\lambda\mu}-\accentset{\bullet}{K}^{\alpha}_{\phantom{\alpha}\beta\lambda}\accentset{\circ}{\Gamma}^{\lambda}_{\nu\mu}\right)

\displaystyle-\left(\partial_{\nu}\accentset{\bullet}{K}^{\alpha}_{\phantom{\alpha}\beta\mu}-\accentset{\bullet}{K}^{\alpha}_{\phantom{\alpha}\lambda\mu}\accentset{\circ}{\Gamma}^{\lambda}_{\beta\nu}+\accentset{\bullet}{K}^{\lambda}_{\phantom{\lambda}\beta\mu}\accentset{\circ}{\Gamma}^{\alpha}_{\lambda\nu}-\accentset{\bullet}{K}^{\alpha}_{\phantom{\alpha}\beta\lambda}\accentset{\circ}{\Gamma}^{\lambda}_{\mu\nu}\right)

\displaystyle=\accentset{\circ}{R}^{\alpha}_{\phantom{\alpha}\beta\mu\nu}+\accentset{\bullet}{K}^{\alpha}_{\phantom{\alpha}\lambda\mu}\accentset{\bullet}{K}^{\lambda}_{\phantom{\lambda}\beta\nu}-\accentset{\bullet}{K}^{\alpha}_{\phantom{\alpha}\lambda\nu}\accentset{\bullet}{K}^{\lambda}_{\phantom{\lambda}\beta\mu}+\accentset{\circ}{\nabla}_{\mu}\accentset{\bullet}{K}^{\alpha}_{\phantom{\alpha}\beta\nu}-\accentset{\circ}{\nabla}_{\nu}\accentset{\bullet}{K}^{\alpha}_{\phantom{\alpha}\beta\mu}.

The curvature of teleparallel connection vanishes, which implies that the scalar curvature

0=\accentset{\circ}{R}+\accentset{\bullet}{K}^{\alpha}_{\phantom{\alpha}\lambda\alpha}\accentset{\bullet}{K}^{\lambda\beta}_{\phantom{\lambda\beta}\beta}-\accentset{\bullet}{K}^{\alpha}_{\phantom{\alpha}\lambda\beta}\accentset{\bullet}{K}^{\lambda\beta}_{\phantom{\lambda\beta}\alpha}+\accentset{\circ}{\nabla}_{\alpha}\accentset{\bullet}{K}^{\alpha\beta}_{\phantom{\alpha\beta}\beta}-\accentset{\circ}{\nabla}_{\beta}\accentset{\bullet}{K}^{\alpha\beta}_{\phantom{\alpha\beta}\alpha}.

(4.7)

Defining
torsion scalar

[
19
]

\accentset{\bullet}{T}=\accentset{\bullet}{K}^{\alpha}_{\phantom{\alpha}\lambda\alpha}\accentset{\bullet}{K}^{\lambda\beta}_{\phantom{\lambda\beta}\beta}-\accentset{\bullet}{K}^{\alpha}_{\phantom{\alpha}\lambda\beta}\accentset{\bullet}{K}^{\lambda\beta}_{\phantom{\lambda\beta}\alpha}

(4.8)

and noticing

\displaystyle\accentset{\bullet}{K}^{\alpha\beta}_{\phantom{\alpha\beta}\beta}-\accentset{\bullet}{K}^{\beta\alpha}_{\phantom{\alpha\beta}\beta}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{2}\left(\accentset{\bullet}{T}^{\beta\alpha}_{\phantom{\beta\alpha}\beta}+\accentset{\bullet}{T}^{\phantom{\beta}\alpha\beta}_{\beta\phantom{\alpha\beta}}-\accentset{\bullet}{T}^{\alpha\beta}_{\phantom{\alpha\beta}\beta}-\accentset{\bullet}{T}^{\alpha\beta}_{\phantom{\beta\alpha}\beta}-\accentset{\bullet}{T}^{\phantom{\beta}\beta\alpha}_{\beta\phantom{\alpha\beta}}+\accentset{\bullet}{T}^{\beta\alpha}_{\phantom{\alpha\beta}\beta}\right)

(4.9)

\displaystyle=-2\accentset{\bullet}{T}^{\phantom{\beta}\beta\alpha}_{\beta\phantom{\alpha\beta}},

we can express the scalar curvature of the RLC connection as

\accentset{\circ}{R}=-\accentset{\bullet}{T}-\accentset{\circ}{\nabla}_{\alpha}\left(\accentset{\bullet}{K}^{\alpha\beta}_{\phantom{\alpha\beta}\beta}-\accentset{\bullet}{K}^{\beta\alpha}_{\phantom{\beta\alpha}\beta}\right)=-\accentset{\bullet}{T}+2\accentset{\circ}{\nabla}_{\alpha}\accentset{\bullet}{T}^{\phantom{\beta}\beta\alpha}_{\beta}.

(4.10)

We will adopt the handy notation
h=\lvert\det(h^{a}_{\phantom{a}\mu})\rvert
. Looking at (
3.14
) we see that

\sqrt{-g}=\sqrt{-\det(g_{\mu\nu})}=\sqrt{-\det(\eta_{ab})\det(h^{a}_{\phantom{a}\mu})\det(h^{b}_{\phantom{a}\nu})}=\lvert\det(h^{a}_{\phantom{a}\mu})\rvert=h.

All this should serve as a good motivation for postulating the following:

Definition 4.1.1
.

The
action of teleparallel gravity
is

\accentset{\bullet}{\mathcal{S}}=-\dfrac{1}{2\kappa}\int_{\mathcal{M}}{\accentset{\bullet}{T}h\,d^{4}x}.

(4.11)

To be more precise, we should call this the action of
teleparallel equivalent of general relativity
(TEGR)
[
19
]
, but some authors (e.g. Aldrovandi and Pereira
[
16
]
) actually mean TEGR when they speak of teleparallel gravity.

The equations of motion may be obtained through the variation of this action. It is vitally important to realize that the dynamics of the theory with this action is by construction equivalent to the dynamics of general relativity, in which the dynamics is determined by the Einstein-Hilbert action. The reason is that
\left(2\kappa\right)^{-1}\int_{\mathcal{M}}{2\accentset{\circ}{\nabla}_{\alpha}\accentset{\bullet}{T}^{\phantom{\beta}\beta\alpha}_{\beta}\sqrt{-g}\,d^{4}x}
constitutes only a surface term, which is irrelevant when varying the action.
To show this, we use the formula
[
22
]

\accentset{\circ}{\nabla}_{\mu}V^{\mu}=\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{-g}}\partial_{\mu}\left(\sqrt{-g}V^{\mu}\right),

(4.12)

which allows us to write

\dfrac{1}{2\kappa}\int_{\mathcal{M}}{2\accentset{\circ}{\nabla}_{\alpha}\accentset{\bullet}{T}^{\phantom{\beta}\beta\alpha}_{\beta\phantom{\alpha\beta}}\sqrt{-g}\,d^{4}x}=\dfrac{1}{2\kappa}\int_{\mathcal{M}}{2\partial_{\alpha}\left(\sqrt{-g}\accentset{\bullet}{T}^{\phantom{\beta}\beta\alpha}_{\beta\phantom{\alpha\beta}}\right)d^{4}x}.

(4.13)

Now let us rewrite the torsion scalar in terms of the torsion
\accentset{\bullet}{T}^{\lambda}_{\alpha\beta}
. Using (
4.8
) and (
2.15
) we have

\displaystyle\accentset{\bullet}{T}

\displaystyle=\accentset{\bullet}{T}^{\alpha}_{\phantom{\alpha}\alpha\lambda}\accentset{\bullet}{T}^{\beta\lambda}_{\phantom{\mu\lambda}\beta}-\dfrac{1}{4}\left(\accentset{\bullet}{T}^{\phantom{\lambda}\alpha}_{\lambda\phantom{\alpha}\beta}+\accentset{\bullet}{T}^{\phantom{\beta}\alpha}_{\beta\phantom{\alpha}\lambda}-\accentset{\bullet}{T}^{\alpha}_{\phantom{\alpha}\lambda\beta}\right)\left(\accentset{\bullet}{T}^{\beta\lambda}_{\phantom{\beta\lambda}\alpha}+\accentset{\bullet}{T}^{\phantom{\alpha}\lambda\beta}_{\alpha\phantom{\lambda\beta}}-\accentset{\bullet}{T}^{\lambda\beta}_{\phantom{\lambda\beta}\alpha}\right)

\displaystyle=\accentset{\bullet}{T}^{\alpha}_{\phantom{\alpha}\alpha\lambda}\accentset{\bullet}{T}^{\beta\lambda}_{\phantom{\mu\lambda}\beta}-\dfrac{1}{4}\left(\accentset{\bullet}{T}_{\lambda\alpha\beta}+\accentset{\bullet}{T}_{\beta\alpha\lambda}-\accentset{\bullet}{T}_{\alpha\lambda\beta}\right)\left(\accentset{\bullet}{T}^{\beta\lambda\alpha}+\accentset{\bullet}{T}^{\alpha\lambda\beta}-\accentset{\bullet}{T}^{\lambda\beta\alpha}\right)

\displaystyle=\accentset{\bullet}{T}^{\alpha}_{\phantom{\alpha}\alpha\lambda}\accentset{\bullet}{T}^{\beta\lambda}_{\phantom{\mu\lambda}\beta}-\dfrac{1}{4}\accentset{\bullet}{T}_{\lambda\alpha\beta}\left(\accentset{\bullet}{T}^{\beta\lambda\alpha}+\accentset{\bullet}{T}^{\alpha\lambda\beta}-\accentset{\bullet}{T}^{\lambda\beta\alpha}\right)-

\displaystyle-\dfrac{1}{4}\accentset{\bullet}{T}_{\beta\alpha\lambda}\left(\accentset{\bullet}{T}^{\beta\lambda\alpha}+\accentset{\bullet}{T}^{\alpha\lambda\beta}-\accentset{\bullet}{T}^{\lambda\beta\alpha}\right)+\dfrac{1}{4}\accentset{\bullet}{T}_{\alpha\lambda\beta}\left(\accentset{\bullet}{T}^{\beta\lambda\alpha}+\accentset{\bullet}{T}^{\alpha\lambda\beta}-\accentset{\bullet}{T}^{\lambda\beta\alpha}\right).

Notice that

\accentset{\bullet}{T}_{\beta\alpha\lambda}\left(\accentset{\bullet}{T}^{\beta\lambda\alpha}+\accentset{\bullet}{T}^{\alpha\lambda\beta}-\accentset{\bullet}{T}^{\lambda\beta\alpha}\right)=\accentset{\bullet}{T}_{\lambda\alpha\beta}\left(\accentset{\bullet}{T}^{\lambda\beta\alpha}+\accentset{\bullet}{T}^{\alpha\beta\lambda}-\accentset{\bullet}{T}^{\beta\lambda\alpha}\right),

so the first two terms with the parantheses cancel. What remains is

\displaystyle\accentset{\bullet}{T}

\displaystyle=\accentset{\bullet}{T}^{\alpha}_{\phantom{\alpha}\alpha\lambda}\accentset{\bullet}{T}^{\beta\lambda}_{\phantom{\mu\lambda}\beta}+\dfrac{1}{4}\accentset{\bullet}{T}_{\alpha\lambda\beta}\left(\accentset{\bullet}{T}^{\beta\lambda\alpha}+\accentset{\bullet}{T}^{\alpha\lambda\beta}-\accentset{\bullet}{T}^{\lambda\beta\alpha}\right)

(4.14)

\displaystyle=\accentset{\bullet}{T}^{\alpha}_{\phantom{\alpha}\alpha\lambda}\accentset{\bullet}{T}^{\beta\lambda}_{\phantom{\beta\lambda}\beta}+\dfrac{1}{4}\accentset{\bullet}{T}_{\lambda\alpha\beta}\left(\accentset{\bullet}{T}^{\beta\alpha\lambda}+\accentset{\bullet}{T}^{\lambda\alpha\beta}-\accentset{\bullet}{T}^{\alpha\beta\lambda}\right)

(4.15)

\displaystyle=\accentset{\bullet}{T}^{\alpha}_{\phantom{\alpha}\alpha\lambda}\accentset{\bullet}{T}^{\beta\lambda}_{\phantom{\beta\lambda}\beta}+\dfrac{1}{4}\accentset{\bullet}{T}_{\lambda\alpha\beta}\left(\accentset{\bullet}{T}^{\beta\alpha\lambda}+\accentset{\bullet}{T}^{\lambda\alpha\beta}+\accentset{\bullet}{T}^{\beta\alpha\lambda}\right)

(4.16)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{4}\accentset{\bullet}{T}_{\lambda\alpha\beta}\accentset{\bullet}{T}^{\lambda\alpha\beta}+\dfrac{1}{2}\accentset{\bullet}{T}_{\lambda\alpha\beta}\accentset{\bullet}{T}^{\beta\alpha\lambda}-\accentset{\bullet}{T}^{\alpha}_{\phantom{\alpha}\lambda\alpha}\accentset{\bullet}{T}^{\beta\lambda}_{\phantom{\mu\lambda}\beta}

(4.17)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{4}\accentset{\bullet}{T}^{\lambda}_{\phantom{\lambda}\alpha\beta}\accentset{\bullet}{T}_{\lambda}^{\phantom{\lambda}\alpha\beta}+\dfrac{1}{2}\accentset{\bullet}{T}^{\lambda}_{\phantom{\lambda}\alpha\beta}\accentset{\bullet}{T}_{\phantom{\beta\alpha}\lambda}^{\beta\alpha}-\accentset{\bullet}{T}^{\alpha}_{\phantom{\alpha}\lambda\alpha}\accentset{\bullet}{T}^{\beta\lambda}_{\phantom{\mu\lambda}\beta}.

(4.18)

The Lagrangian of teleparallel gravity is therefore

\accentset{\bullet}{\mathcal{L}}=-\dfrac{h}{2\kappa}\left(\dfrac{1}{4}\accentset{\bullet}{T}^{\lambda}_{\phantom{\lambda}\alpha\beta}\accentset{\bullet}{T}_{\lambda}^{\phantom{\lambda}\alpha\beta}+\dfrac{1}{2}\accentset{\bullet}{T}^{\lambda}_{\phantom{\lambda}\alpha\beta}\accentset{\bullet}{T}_{\phantom{\beta\alpha}\lambda}^{\beta\alpha}-\accentset{\bullet}{T}^{\alpha}_{\phantom{\alpha}\lambda\alpha}\accentset{\bullet}{T}^{\beta\lambda}_{\phantom{\mu\lambda}\beta}\right).

(4.19)

4.2
Equations of motion

We are now ready to derive the equations determining the dynamics in teleparallel gravity. Before we can even approach the variation of the action, we need to clarify what the variables of the Lagrangian are. As we have mentioned in the first chapter, the fundamental variables on manifold
\mathcal{M}
are metric
g
and connection
\accentset{\bullet}{\nabla}
, therefore the Lagrangian naturally depends on the tetrad
h^{a}_{\phantom{a}\mu}
(from which we construct the metric by (
3.14
)) and the spin connection
\accentset{\bullet}{\omega}^{a}_{\phantom{a}b\mu}
. Writing the Euler-Lagrange equations

\partial_{\sigma}\dfrac{\partial\accentset{\bullet}{\mathcal{L}}}{\partial\left(\partial_{\sigma}h^{a}_{\phantom{a}\rho}\right)}-\dfrac{\partial\accentset{\bullet}{\mathcal{L}}}{\partial h^{a}_{\phantom{a}\rho}}=0

(4.20)

for the variation of the Lagrangian given in the section above with respect to the tetrad, we arrive at the equations

\partial_{\sigma}\left(h\accentset{\bullet}{S}_{a}^{\phantom{a}\rho\sigma}\right)-\kappa h\accentset{\bullet}{\mathcal{J}}_{a}^{\phantom{a}\rho}=\kappa h\Theta_{a}^{\phantom{a}\rho},

(4.21)

where
\Theta_{a}^{\phantom{a}\rho}=-\dfrac{1}{h}\dfrac{\delta\mathcal{L}_{s}}{\delta h^{a}_{\phantom{a}\rho}}
is the matter energy-momentum tensor and
\mathcal{L}_{s}
is the Lagrangian of a general source field
[
11
]
.
The other two objects appearing in eq. (
4.21
) are the so-called
superpotential

\accentset{\bullet}{S}_{a}^{\phantom{a}\rho\sigma}=\dfrac{1}{2}\left(\accentset{\bullet}{T}_{\phantom{\sigma\rho}a}^{\sigma\rho}+\accentset{\bullet}{T}_{a}^{\phantom{a}\rho\sigma}-\accentset{\bullet}{T}_{\phantom{\rho\sigma}a}^{\rho\sigma}\right)-h_{a}^{\phantom{a}\sigma}\accentset{\bullet}{T}_{\phantom{\theta\rho}\theta}^{\theta\rho}+h_{a}^{\phantom{a}\rho}\accentset{\bullet}{T}_{\phantom{\theta\sigma}\theta}^{\theta\sigma}

(4.22)

and the so-called
gauge current

\accentset{\bullet}{\mathcal{J}}_{a}^{\phantom{a}\rho}=\dfrac{1}{\kappa}h_{a}^{\phantom{a}\lambda}\accentset{\bullet}{S}_{c}^{\phantom{c}\nu\rho}\accentset{\bullet}{T}^{c}_{\phantom{c}\nu\lambda}+\dfrac{h_{a}^{\phantom{a}\rho}}{h}\accentset{\bullet}{\mathcal{L}}+\dfrac{1}{\kappa}\accentset{\bullet}{\omega}^{c}_{\phantom{c}a\sigma}\accentset{\bullet}{S}_{c}^{\phantom{c}\rho\sigma}.

(4.23)

Now half the work is done, but we have yet to execute the variation with respect to the other variable of the Lagrangian, the spin connection
\accentset{\bullet}{\omega}^{a}_{\phantom{a}b\mu}
. For this, we will resort to a dirty trick
[
23
,
24
]
. In the previous section, we have shown that

\accentset{\bullet}{\mathcal{L}}\left(h^{a}_{\phantom{a}\rho},\accentset{\bullet}{\omega}^{a}_{\phantom{a}b\mu}\right)=\accentset{\circ}{\mathcal{L}}\left(h^{a}_{\phantom{a}\rho}\right)-\dfrac{1}{\kappa}\partial_{\alpha}\left(h\accentset{\bullet}{T}^{\phantom{\beta}\beta\alpha}_{\beta\phantom{\alpha\beta}}\right),

(4.24)

where

\accentset{\circ}{\mathcal{L}}\left(h^{a}_{\phantom{a}\rho}\right)=\frac{1}{2\kappa}h\accentset{\circ}{R}\left(h^{a}_{\phantom{a}\rho}\right)\equiv\frac{1}{2\kappa}\sqrt{-g}\accentset{\circ}{R}\left(g_{\mu\nu}\right)

(4.25)

is the Einstein-Hilbert Lagrangian, which, as we know, does not depend on spin connection
\accentset{\bullet}{\omega}^{a}_{\phantom{a}b\mu}
, but only on the metric, and therefore (by (
3.14
)) on the tetrad
h^{a}_{\phantom{a}\rho}
. Due to the fact that the second term on the right is just a 4-divergence, it will only contribute a surface term in the action. However, surface terms are irrelevant when it comes to variation of the action, so variations of
\accentset{\bullet}{\mathcal{S}}
with respect to
\accentset{\bullet}{\omega}^{a}_{\phantom{a}b\mu}
are identically vanishing, hence they give no equation
[
11
]
.

4.3
Alternative form of action

Notice that the Lagrangian of teleparallel gravity may be rewritten in a different form
[
16
]
. First, we factor out
\accentset{\bullet}{T}^{\lambda}_{\phantom{\lambda}\mu\nu}

\displaystyle\accentset{\bullet}{\mathcal{L}}

\displaystyle=-\dfrac{h}{2\kappa}\left(\dfrac{1}{4}\accentset{\bullet}{T}^{\lambda}_{\phantom{\lambda}\mu\nu}\accentset{\bullet}{T}_{\lambda}^{\phantom{\lambda}\mu\nu}+\dfrac{1}{2}\accentset{\bullet}{T}^{\lambda}_{\phantom{\lambda}\mu\nu}\accentset{\bullet}{T}_{\phantom{\nu\mu}\lambda}^{\nu\mu}-\accentset{\bullet}{T}^{\lambda}_{\phantom{\lambda}\mu\lambda}\accentset{\bullet}{T}_{\phantom{\nu\mu}\nu}^{\nu\mu}\right)

(4.26)

\displaystyle=-\dfrac{h}{2\kappa}\accentset{\bullet}{T}^{\lambda}_{\phantom{\lambda}\mu\nu}\left(\dfrac{1}{4}\accentset{\bullet}{T}_{\lambda}^{\phantom{\lambda}\mu\nu}+\dfrac{1}{2}\accentset{\bullet}{T}_{\phantom{\nu\mu}\lambda}^{\nu\mu}-\delta^{\nu}_{\lambda}\accentset{\bullet}{T}_{\phantom{\rho\mu}\rho}^{\rho\mu}\right)

\displaystyle=-\dfrac{h}{2\kappa}\eta_{ab}h^{b}_{\phantom{b}\lambda}\accentset{\bullet}{T}^{a}_{\phantom{a}\mu\nu}\left(\dfrac{1}{4}\accentset{\bullet}{T}^{\lambda\mu\nu}+\dfrac{1}{2}\accentset{\bullet}{T}^{\nu\mu\lambda}-g^{\nu\lambda}\accentset{\bullet}{T}_{\phantom{\rho\mu}\rho}^{\rho\mu}\right)

\displaystyle\equiv-\dfrac{h}{2\kappa}\eta_{ab}h^{b}_{\phantom{b}\lambda}\accentset{\bullet}{T}^{a}_{\phantom{a}\mu\nu}\accentset{\bullet}{H}^{\mu\nu\lambda}.

Next, we use Kronecker deltas to change the indices on
\accentset{\bullet}{H}
and use the fact that
\accentset{\bullet}{T}^{\lambda}_{\phantom{\lambda}\mu\nu}=\accentset{\bullet}{T}^{\lambda}_{\phantom{\lambda}\left[\mu\nu\right]}

\displaystyle\accentset{\bullet}{\mathcal{L}}

\displaystyle=-\dfrac{h}{2\kappa}\eta_{ab}h^{b}_{\phantom{b}\lambda}\accentset{\bullet}{T}^{a}_{\phantom{a}\mu\nu}\delta^{\mu}_{\alpha}\delta^{\nu}_{\beta}\accentset{\bullet}{H}^{\alpha\beta\lambda}

(4.27)

\displaystyle=-\dfrac{h}{2\kappa}\eta_{ab}h^{b}_{\phantom{b}\lambda}\accentset{\bullet}{T}^{a}_{\phantom{a}\mu\nu}\delta^{\left[\mu\right.}_{\alpha}\delta^{\left.\nu\right]}_{\beta}\accentset{\bullet}{H}^{\alpha\beta\lambda}.

Using the identity
\varepsilon^{\mu\nu\gamma\sigma}\varepsilon_{\alpha\beta\gamma\sigma}=4\delta^{\left[\mu\right.}_{\alpha}\delta^{\left.\nu\right]}_{\beta}

[
17
]
this further leads to

\displaystyle\accentset{\bullet}{\mathcal{L}}

\displaystyle=-\dfrac{h}{2\kappa}\eta_{ab}h^{b}_{\phantom{b}\lambda}\accentset{\bullet}{T}^{a}_{\phantom{a}\mu\nu}\dfrac{1}{4}\varepsilon^{\mu\nu\gamma\sigma}\varepsilon_{\alpha\beta\gamma\sigma}\accentset{\bullet}{H}^{\alpha\beta\lambda}

(4.28)

\displaystyle=-\dfrac{h}{2\kappa}6\eta_{ab}h^{b}_{\phantom{b}\lambda}\accentset{\bullet}{T}^{a}_{\phantom{a}\mu\nu}\varepsilon_{\gamma\sigma\alpha\beta}\accentset{\bullet}{H}^{\alpha\beta\lambda}\dfrac{1}{4!}\varepsilon^{\mu\nu\gamma\sigma}

\displaystyle=-\dfrac{h}{2\kappa}\eta_{ab}h^{b}_{\phantom{b}\lambda}6\accentset{\bullet}{T}^{a}_{\phantom{a}\left[\mu\nu\right.}\varepsilon_{\left.\gamma\sigma\right]\alpha\beta}\accentset{\bullet}{H}^{\alpha\beta\lambda}\dfrac{1}{4!}\varepsilon^{\mu\nu\gamma\sigma}.

Interestingly,
\accentset{\bullet}{T}^{a}_{\phantom{a}\left[\mu\nu\right.}\varepsilon_{\left.\gamma\sigma\right]\alpha\beta}\accentset{\bullet}{H}^{\alpha\beta\lambda}
would almost look like the exterior product of the torsion forms
\accentset{\bullet}{T}^{a}
with some other form, were it not for the fact that at this point we do not know if
\varepsilon_{\gamma\sigma\alpha\beta}\accentset{\bullet}{H}^{\alpha\beta\lambda}

6=\dfrac{(2+2)!}{2!2!}
and define

\left(\mathbin{\mathchoice{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\displaystyle\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.77778}{$\displaystyle\bigcirc$}}}$\cr$\displaystyle\star$\cr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\textstyle\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.77778}{$\textstyle\bigcirc$}}}$\cr$\textstyle\star$\cr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\scriptstyle\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.77778}{$\scriptstyle\bigcirc$}}}$\cr$\scriptstyle\star$\cr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\scriptscriptstyle\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.77778}{$\scriptscriptstyle\bigcirc$}}}$\cr$\scriptscriptstyle\star$\cr}}}}\accentset{\bullet}{T}\right)^{b}_{\gamma\sigma}=h^{b}_{\phantom{b}\lambda}h\varepsilon_{\gamma\sigma\alpha\beta}\left(\dfrac{1}{4}\accentset{\bullet}{T}^{\lambda\alpha\beta}+\dfrac{1}{2}\accentset{\bullet}{T}^{\beta\alpha\lambda}-g^{\beta\lambda}\accentset{\bullet}{T}_{\phantom{\rho\alpha}\rho}^{\rho\alpha}\right).

(4.29)

Then

\displaystyle\accentset{\bullet}{\mathcal{L}}

\displaystyle=-\dfrac{1}{2\kappa}\eta_{ab}\dfrac{(2+2)!}{2!2!}\accentset{\bullet}{T}^{a}_{\phantom{a}\left[\mu\nu\right.}\varepsilon_{\left.\gamma\sigma\right]\alpha\beta}h^{b}_{\phantom{b}\lambda}h\accentset{\bullet}{H}^{\alpha\beta\lambda}\dfrac{1}{4!}\varepsilon^{\mu\nu\gamma\sigma}

(4.30)

\displaystyle\equiv-\frac{1}{2\kappa}\eta_{ab}\left(\accentset{\bullet}{T}^{a}\wedge\mathbin{\mathchoice{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\displaystyle\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.77778}{$\displaystyle\bigcirc$}}}$\cr$\displaystyle\star$\cr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\textstyle\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.77778}{$\textstyle\bigcirc$}}}$\cr$\textstyle\star$\cr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\scriptstyle\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.77778}{$\scriptstyle\bigcirc$}}}$\cr$\scriptstyle\star$\cr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\scriptscriptstyle\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.77778}{$\scriptscriptstyle\bigcirc$}}}$\cr$\scriptscriptstyle\star$\cr}}}}\accentset{\bullet}{T}^{b}\right)_{\mu\nu\gamma\sigma}\dfrac{1}{4!}\varepsilon^{\mu\nu\gamma\sigma}.

We see that
\accentset{\bullet}{H}
is a tensor, while

is the (metric-compatible) volume form (see Definition

\mathbin{\mathchoice{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\displaystyle\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.77778}{$\displaystyle\bigcirc$}}}$\cr$\displaystyle\star$\cr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\textstyle\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.77778}{$\textstyle\bigcirc$}}}$\cr$\textstyle\star$\cr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\scriptstyle\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.77778}{$\scriptstyle\bigcirc$}}}$\cr$\scriptstyle\star$\cr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\scriptscriptstyle\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.77778}{$\scriptscriptstyle\bigcirc$}}}$\cr$\scriptscriptstyle\star$\cr}}}}\accentset{\bullet}{T}^{b}

\wedge

As
\mathbin{\mathchoice{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\displaystyle\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.77778}{$\displaystyle\bigcirc$}}}$\cr$\displaystyle\star$\cr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\textstyle\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.77778}{$\textstyle\bigcirc$}}}$\cr$\textstyle\star$\cr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\scriptstyle\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.77778}{$\scriptstyle\bigcirc$}}}$\cr$\scriptstyle\star$\cr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\scriptscriptstyle\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.77778}{$\scriptscriptstyle\bigcirc$}}}$\cr$\scriptscriptstyle\star$\cr}}}}\accentset{\bullet}{T}^{b}
are 2-forms and the underlying manifold

is 4-dimensional, it must be that for any
a,b

\displaystyle\left(\accentset{\bullet}{T}^{a}\wedge\mathbin{\mathchoice{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\displaystyle\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.77778}{$\displaystyle\bigcirc$}}}$\cr$\displaystyle\star$\cr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\textstyle\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.77778}{$\textstyle\bigcirc$}}}$\cr$\textstyle\star$\cr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\scriptstyle\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.77778}{$\scriptstyle\bigcirc$}}}$\cr$\scriptstyle\star$\cr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\scriptscriptstyle\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.77778}{$\scriptscriptstyle\bigcirc$}}}$\cr$\scriptscriptstyle\star$\cr}}}}\accentset{\bullet}{T}^{b}\right)

\displaystyle\propto\omega,

\displaystyle\left(\accentset{\bullet}{T}^{a}\wedge\mathbin{\mathchoice{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\displaystyle\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.77778}{$\displaystyle\bigcirc$}}}$\cr$\displaystyle\star$\cr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\textstyle\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.77778}{$\textstyle\bigcirc$}}}$\cr$\textstyle\star$\cr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\scriptstyle\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.77778}{$\scriptstyle\bigcirc$}}}$\cr$\scriptstyle\star$\cr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\scriptscriptstyle\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.77778}{$\scriptscriptstyle\bigcirc$}}}$\cr$\scriptscriptstyle\star$\cr}}}}\accentset{\bullet}{T}^{b}\right)_{\mu\nu\gamma\sigma}

\displaystyle\equiv\left(\accentset{\bullet}{T}^{a},\accentset{\bullet}{T}^{b}\right)_{g}\omega_{\mu\nu\gamma\sigma}.

Recalling that
2
2

2

\sgn g
is the sign of
\det\left(g_{\mu\nu}\right)
.

\omega^{\mu\nu\gamma\sigma}=\dfrac{\sgn g}{h}\varepsilon^{\mu\nu\gamma\sigma}=-\dfrac{1}{h}\varepsilon^{\mu\nu\gamma\sigma}
, we can now rewrite the action

\displaystyle\accentset{\bullet}{\mathcal{S}}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{M}}{\accentset{\bullet}{\mathcal{L}}\,d^{4}x}

(4.47)

5.4.1
Possible contractions and parametrization

For the curvature tensor of a metric-compatible connection, the following holds:

\displaystyle R_{\alpha\beta\mu\nu}

\displaystyle=-R_{\alpha\beta\nu\mu},

(5.88)

\displaystyle R_{\alpha\beta\mu\nu}

\displaystyle=-R_{\beta\alpha\mu\nu},

(5.89)

hence the possible independent contractions allow us to parametrize

\displaystyle\left(\mathbin{\mathchoice{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\displaystyle\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.77778}{$\displaystyle\bigcirc$}}}$\cr$\displaystyle\star$\cr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\textstyle\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.77778}{$\textstyle\bigcirc$}}}$\cr$\textstyle\star$\cr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\scriptstyle\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.77778}{$\scriptstyle\bigcirc$}}}$\cr$\scriptstyle\star$\cr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\scriptscriptstyle\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.77778}{$\scriptscriptstyle\bigcirc$}}}$\cr$\scriptscriptstyle\star$\cr}}}}\Omega\right)^{ab}_{\phantom{ab}cd}=\omega_{cdef}

\displaystyle\left[\alpha R^{abef}+\beta\left(R^{aebf}-R^{beaf}\right)+\gamma\left(g^{ea}R^{lfb}_{\phantom{lfb}l}-g^{eb}R^{lfa}_{\phantom{lfa}l}\right)+\right.

(5.90)

\displaystyle+\left.\mu g^{ae}g^{bf}R^{lk}_{\phantom{lk}lk}+\lambda R^{efab}+\kappa\left(g^{ea}R^{lbf}_{\phantom{lbf}l}-g^{eb}R^{laf}_{\phantom{laf}l}\right)\right].

The terms in brackets are to ensure the pseudo-antisymmetry of
\left(\mathbin{\mathchoice{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\displaystyle\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.77778}{$\displaystyle\bigcirc$}}}$\cr$\displaystyle\star$\cr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\textstyle\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.77778}{$\textstyle\bigcirc$}}}$\cr$\textstyle\star$\cr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\scriptstyle\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.77778}{$\scriptstyle\bigcirc$}}}$\cr$\scriptstyle\star$\cr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\scriptscriptstyle\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.77778}{$\scriptscriptstyle\bigcirc$}}}$\cr$\scriptscriptstyle\star$\cr}}}}\Omega\right)^{a}_{\phantom{a}bcd}
in indices
a,b
.
In the case of RLC connection,

R_{\alpha\beta\mu\nu}=R_{\mu\nu\alpha\beta},

(5.91)

which makes the term with
\lambda

\alpha

\kappa
the same as the one with

[
14

15

\displaystyle\left(\mathbin{\mathchoice{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\displaystyle\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.77778}{$\displaystyle\bigcirc$}}}$\cr$\displaystyle\star$\cr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\textstyle\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.77778}{$\textstyle\bigcirc$}}}$\cr$\textstyle\star$\cr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\scriptstyle\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.77778}{$\scriptstyle\bigcirc$}}}$\cr$\scriptstyle\star$\cr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\scriptscriptstyle\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.77778}{$\scriptscriptstyle\bigcirc$}}}$\cr$\scriptscriptstyle\star$\cr}}}}\Omega\right)^{ab}_{\phantom{ab}cd}=\omega_{cdef}

\displaystyle\left[\alpha R^{abef}+\beta\left(R^{aebf}-R^{beaf}\right)+\right.

(5.92)

\displaystyle+\left.\kappa\left(g^{ea}R^{lbf}_{\phantom{lbf}l}-g^{eb}R^{laf}_{\phantom{laf}l}\right)+\mu g^{ae}g^{bf}R^{lk}_{\phantom{lk}lk}\right].

Chapter 7
Conclusions

The description of gravity in terms of torsion of a metric teleparallel connection and using the tetrad formalism proved to be a very clever idea. From the mathematical perspective, TEGR is an elegant theory of gravity, which can be formulated as a gauge theory of the group
\mathbb{T}_{4}
of translations in 4D, as we have seen in chapter
6
.

The main focus of this thesis was the search for a proper definition of a duality operator
\mathbin{\mathchoice{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\displaystyle\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.77778}{$\displaystyle\bigcirc$}}}$\cr$\displaystyle\star$\cr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\textstyle\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.77778}{$\textstyle\bigcirc$}}}$\cr$\textstyle\star$\cr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\scriptstyle\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.77778}{$\scriptstyle\bigcirc$}}}$\cr$\scriptstyle\star$\cr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\scriptscriptstyle\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.77778}{$\scriptscriptstyle\bigcirc$}}}$\cr$\scriptscriptstyle\star$\cr}}}}
, whose existence was proposed by Lucas and Pereira
[
14
]
.
We have inquired into the problem whether this
\mathbin{\mathchoice{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\displaystyle\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.77778}{$\displaystyle\bigcirc$}}}$\cr$\displaystyle\star$\cr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\textstyle\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.77778}{$\textstyle\bigcirc$}}}$\cr$\textstyle\star$\cr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\scriptstyle\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.77778}{$\scriptstyle\bigcirc$}}}$\cr$\scriptstyle\star$\cr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\scriptscriptstyle\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.77778}{$\scriptscriptstyle\bigcirc$}}}$\cr$\scriptscriptstyle\star$\cr}}}}
operator can be constructed consistently from the mathematical point of view. Our analysis of the results of Lucas and Pereira
[
14
]
, together with our motivation for
\mathbin{\mathchoice{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\displaystyle\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.77778}{$\displaystyle\bigcirc$}}}$\cr$\displaystyle\star$\cr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\textstyle\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.77778}{$\textstyle\bigcirc$}}}$\cr$\textstyle\star$\cr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\scriptstyle\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.77778}{$\scriptstyle\bigcirc$}}}$\cr$\scriptstyle\star$\cr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\scriptscriptstyle\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.77778}{$\scriptscriptstyle\bigcirc$}}}$\cr$\scriptscriptstyle\star$\cr}}}}
to be similar to the Hodge
\star
operator, led to our formulation of an algorithm for its construction in a general case, i.e. for the application of
\mathbin{\mathchoice{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\displaystyle\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.77778}{$\displaystyle\bigcirc$}}}$\cr$\displaystyle\star$\cr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\textstyle\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.77778}{$\textstyle\bigcirc$}}}$\cr$\textstyle\star$\cr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\scriptstyle\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.77778}{$\scriptstyle\bigcirc$}}}$\cr$\scriptstyle\star$\cr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\scriptscriptstyle\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.77778}{$\scriptscriptstyle\bigcirc$}}}$\cr$\scriptscriptstyle\star$\cr}}}}
on any

, which for torsion produced the result obtained in

]
, but for connection 1-forms and curvature 2-forms failed to provide any solution at all. This is contrary to the claims of Lucas and Pereira, who assert they have obtained a unique solution for the case of curvature (up to a sign).

Thus, to sum up our results, we showed that the naive approach taken by Lucas and Pereira does not lead to a consistent definition of this operator, because it results in the operator with the desired properties either being non-unique or non-existent.

The Hodge
\star
operator
“ignores”
the algebraic indices (see section
5.2.1
) of the forms. Thus, any attempt at a definition of the newly-proposed duality operator
\mathbin{\mathchoice{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\displaystyle\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.77778}{$\displaystyle\bigcirc$}}}$\cr$\displaystyle\star$\cr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\textstyle\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.77778}{$\textstyle\bigcirc$}}}$\cr$\textstyle\star$\cr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\scriptstyle\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.77778}{$\scriptstyle\bigcirc$}}}$\cr$\scriptstyle\star$\cr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\scriptscriptstyle\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.77778}{$\scriptscriptstyle\bigcirc$}}}$\cr$\scriptscriptstyle\star$\cr}}}}
that is based on utilizing the Hodge
\star
operator at some point, no matter how sophisticated and clever the precise prescription, formula or procedure is, is destined for failure.

For example, one approach to the construction might leverage the geometry of the frame bundle endowed with a connection
\omega
and use the canonical
\mathbb{R}^{n}
-valued 1-form
\theta
(which is the solder form)
[
17
]
and connection 1-forms to construct a (canonical) volume form and a (canonical) metric tensor, and use these in the definition of the Hodge
\star
operator for forms on the frame bundle. This
\star
operator could then be applied on forms on the frame bundle (because connection
\omega
, torsion
T=D\theta
and curvature
\Omega=D\omega
are forms defined on the frame bundle – see section
6.2
), and we could apply pull-back by a local section
\sigma
on the result, so that we could obtain a result on the base manifold of the bundle. However, the
\star
operator constructed and used in this way also
“ignores”
the algebraic indices.

For that reason, a deeper insight into the geometry underlying teleparallel gravity, frame bundles, soldered bundles or principal fiber bundles is necessary (but might not be sufficient) to obtain a proper coordinate-free definition of the operator, from which the geometric interpretation would be clear and such a definition would be canonical.
