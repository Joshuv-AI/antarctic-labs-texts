Abstract

We investigate the solvability of the Diophantine equation in the title, where d > 1 d>1 is a square-free integer, p , q p,q are distinct odd primes and x , y , a , b x,y,a,b are unknown positive integers with gcd ⁡ ( x , y ) = 1 \gcd(x,y)=1 . We describe all the integer solutions of this equation, and then use the main finding to deduce some results concerning the integers solutions of some of its variants. The methods adopted here are elementary in nature and are primarily based on the existence of the primitive divisors of certain Lehmer numbers.

2.1.
Lehmer numbers and existence of their primitive divisors

A pair
(\alpha,\beta)
of algebraic integers is said to be a Lehmer pair if
(\alpha+\beta)^{2}
and
\alpha\beta
are two non-zero coprime rational integers, and
\alpha/\beta
is not a root of unity. For a given positive integer
n
, the
n
-th Lehmer number corresponds to the pair
(\alpha,\beta)
is defined as

\mathcal{L}_{n}(\alpha,\beta)=\begin{cases}\dfrac{\alpha^{n}-\beta^{n}}{\alpha-\beta}&\text{ if $n$ is odd,}\\
\dfrac{\alpha^{n}-\beta^{n}}{\alpha^{2}-\beta^{2}}&\text{ if $n$ is even}.\end{cases}

Note that all Lehmer numbers are non-zero rational integers. The Lehmer pairs
(\alpha_{1},\beta_{1})
and
(\alpha_{2},\beta_{2})
are said to be equivalent if
\alpha_{1}/\alpha_{2}=\beta_{1}/\beta_{2}\in\{\pm 1,\pm\sqrt{-1}\}
. A prime divisor
p
of
\mathcal{L}_{n}(\alpha,\beta)
is said to be primitive if
p\nmid(\alpha^{2}-\beta^{2})^{2}\mathcal{L}_{1}(\alpha,\beta)\mathcal{L}_{2}(\alpha,\beta)\cdots\mathcal{L}_{n-1}(\alpha,\beta)
.
The following classical result concerning the existence of primitive divisors of Lehmer numbers was proved in
[
3
, Theorem 1.4]
.

Theorem A
.

For any integer
n>30
, the Lehmer numbers
\mathcal{L}_{n}(\alpha,\beta)
have primitive divisors.

Given a Lehmer pair
(\alpha,\beta)
, let
a=(\alpha+\beta)^{2}
and
b=(\alpha-\beta)^{2}

\alpha=(\sqrt{a}\pm\sqrt{b})/2

\beta=(\sqrt{a}\mp\sqrt{b})/2

(\alpha,\beta)

2^{p-1}q^{n}=\lambda_{1}\lambda_{2}\left(u^{p-1}d^{(p-1)/2}+p^{2m-2}T\right).

(2.11)

Since
p
is odd prime with

and
m-1=m_{1}>0
, so that reading (

) modulo

.
Thus we can conclude that

)
has no integer solutions provided

3.
Concluding Remark

Here, we look into a generalized version of Theorem
1.1
. We mainly consider the following Diophantine equation:

dx^{2}+p^{2m}q^{2n}=4y^{N},~~~~x,y,m,n,N\in\mathbb{N},p\mid N,\gcd(x,y)=1,\gcd(p,h(-d))=1,

(3.1)

where
d
is a square-free positive integer, and
p
and
q
are distinct odd primes.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
d,p
and
q
be as in Theorem
1.1
. Assume that
N
is a positive integer satisfying
p\mid N
and
\gcd(N,2h(-d))=1
. Then

(i)

if
d\equiv 1,2\pmod{4}
, then (
3.1
) has no integer solution;

(ii)

if
d\equiv 3\pmod{4}
, then (
3.1
) has no positive integer solution when
q^{n}\not\equiv\pm 1\pmod{p}
. Moreover when
q^{n}\equiv\pm 1\pmod{p}
, (
3.1
) has solutions only if
2^{\frac{N}{p}-1}p^{m-1}=|\mathcal{I}(d,u^{\prime},1,N/p)|
with
N/p
prime and
u^{\prime}\in\mathbb{N}
, and such solutions (if exist) are given by

(x,y)=\left(\frac{|u\mathcal{R}(d,u,p^{m-1},p)|}{2^{p-1}},\frac{u^{\prime 2}d+p^{2\delta(m-1)}}{4}\right),

where
u=\begin{cases}\dfrac{|u^{\prime}\mathcal{R}(d,u^{\prime},p^{m-1},N/p)|}{2^{\frac{N}{p}-1}},\text{ if }\delta=0;\\
u^{\prime},\text{ if }\delta=1;\end{cases}

with
u^{\prime}
a suitable positive integer.

Proof of Theorem
3.1
.

Let
(x,y,m,n,N)
be a positive integer solution of (
3.1
) for a given square integer
d>1
and a pair of distinct odd primes
p,q
. Then as in the proof of Theorem
1.1
,
x
is odd,
d\equiv 3\pmod{4}
and
\gcd(pq,dx)=1
.

As
p\mid N
, so that (
3.1
) can be written as

dx^{2}+p^{2m}q^{2n}=4Y^{p}

(3.2)

by putting
Y=y^{N/p}
. By Theorem
1.1
, (
3.2
) has no solution in positive integers provided
q^{n}\not\equiv\pm 1\pmod{p}
, and hence (
3.1
) too has no solution in positive integers.

We now consider
q^{n}\equiv\pm 1\pmod{p}
. Then by Theorem
1.1
, the possible solutions of (
3.2
) are given by

(x,Y)=\left(\frac{|u\mathcal{R}(d,u,p^{m-1},p)|}{2^{p-1}},\frac{u^{2}d+p^{2(m-1)}}{4}\right).

(3.3)

If
N/p>1
, then we can write
N=pt
for some odd integer
t\geq 3
and thus from (
3.3
), one gets

du^{2}+p^{2(m-1)}=4y^{t},~~u\geq 1,y>1,\gcd(u,y)=1,\gcd(t,2h(-d))=1.

(3.4)

By
[
8
, Theorem 2.1]
, if
t
has a prime factor
\ell
satisfying
2^{\ell-1}p^{m-1}\neq|\mathcal{I}(d,u^{\prime},1,\ell)|
for some suitable positive integer
u^{\prime}
, then (
3.4
) has no solution
(d,u,y,m,t)
and thus (
3.1
) too has no integer solution. Further, (
3.4
) has solutions only if
t
is prime satisfy
2^{t-1}p^{m-1}=|\mathcal{I}(d,u^{\prime},1,t)|
and those are given by

(u,y)=\left(\frac{u^{\prime}|\mathcal{R}(d,u^{\prime},1,t)|}{2^{t-1}},\frac{u^{\prime 2}d+1}{4}\right).

Thus the corresponding solutions of (
3.1
) are given by

(x,y)=\left(\frac{|u\mathcal{R}(d,u,p^{m-1},p)|}{2^{p-1}},\frac{u^{\prime 2}d+1}{4}\right),

where
u=\dfrac{u^{\prime}|\mathcal{R}(d,u^{\prime},1,N/p)|}{2^{\frac{N}{p}-1}}
and
u^{\prime}\geq 1
is a suitable integer.

Finally for
N/p=1
, one can conclude that the solutions of (
3.1
) are as follows:

(x,y)=\left(\frac{|u\mathcal{R}(d,u,p^{m-1},p)|}{2^{p-1}},\frac{u^{2}d+p^{2(m-1)}}{4}\right),

where
u\geq 1
is an odd integer.
This completes the proof.
