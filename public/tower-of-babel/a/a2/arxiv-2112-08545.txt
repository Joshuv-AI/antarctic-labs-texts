Simultaneous Sieve Inference for Time-Inhomogeneous Nonlinear Time Series Regression
Xiucai Ding, Zhou Zhou

Abstract
In this paper, we consider the time-inhomogeneous nonlinear time series regression for a general class of locally stationary time series. On one hand, we propose sieve nonparametric estimators for the time-varying regression functions which can achieve the min-max optimal rate as in Stone, 1982 . On the other hand, we develop a unified simultaneous inferential theory which can be used to conduct both structural and exact form testings on the functions. Our proposed statistics are powerful even under locally weak alternatives. We also propose a multiplier bootstrapping procedure for practical implementation. Our methodology and theory do not require any structural assumptions on the regression functions and we also allow the functions to be supported in an unbounded domain. We also establish sieve approximation theory for 2-D functions in unbounded domain and a Gaussian approximation result for affine and quadratic forms for high dimensional locally stationary time series, which can be of independent interest. Numerical simulations and a real financial data analysis are provided to support our results.

Introduction

In many scientific endeavors, researchers are interested in understanding the specific functional form of a relationship between variables. To name but a few, the estimation of household Engle curves
Bierens and Pott-Buter, 1990
, the estimation of demand functions
Blundell et al., 2017
, the estimation of an asset’s price
Chetty, 2006
;
Fan, 2005
;
Koo et al., 2021
, the understanding of the functional relation between genetics and diseases
Wittkowski and Song, 2010
and the relationship between social and economic factors in social studies
Rueda et al., 2013
. There are usually two main challenges associated with these applications. First, the underlying theory does not narrow down a specific functional or parametric form for the relationship. Second, the observations span long time horizons and constantly involving. Consequently, the relationship is unlikely to remain stable. To address these issues, a general time-inhomogeneous nonparametric regression model offers a more flexible framework.

Consider the following time-inhomogeneous nonlinear time series regression

\displaystyle Y_{i,n}=\sum_{j=1}^{r}m_{j}(t_{i},X_{j,i,n})+\epsilon_{i,n},\quad i=1,2,\cdots,n,

(1.1)

where
r>0
is some given fixed integer. Here
\{X_{j,i,n}\}
are the covariates which can be locally stationary and depend on each other,
t_{i}=i/n
and

\epsilon_{i,n}
is general locally stationary time series (c.f. Assumption
2.1
) whose covariance depend on both time and the covariates and satisfies that
\mathbb{E}(\epsilon_{i,n}|X_{j,i,n})=0.
Moreover,
m_{j}
’s are some smooth functions that map the time and covariates to the conditional mean. (
1.1
) has been employed in the literature, for instance, see
Karmakar et al., 2021
;
Sun et al., 2021
;
Vogt, 2012
;
Vogt, 2015
;
Zhang and Wu, 2015
. It provides a more flexible and general framework and a wide range of models can fit into it. For example, (
1.1
) has been studied extensively in the literature by imposing various structural assumptions on
m_{j}
’s. Assuming that
m_{j}
has a multiplicative separability form in the time
t
and the covariate, the time varying linear model has been studied in
Cai, 2007
;
Fan and Yao, 2003
;
Fan and Zhang, 2000
;
Huang et al., 2002
;
Ramsay and Silverman, 2005
;
Zhou, 2015
;
Zhou and Wu, 2010
among others and the general multiplicative form has been recently studied in
Chen et al., 2021
;
Hu et al., 2019
;
Zhang and Wang, 2015
. Moreover, when
X_{j,i,n}=Y_{i-j,n}
in (
1.1
), it becomes the nonparametric and nonlinear autoregressive process
(
Vogt, 2015
, Section 3.1)
.
Finally, we point out that (
1.1
) can be regarded as a discretized version of the Langevin equation

\mathrm{d}Y_{t}=\sum_{j=1}^{r}m_{j}(t,X_{j,t})\mathrm{d}t+\sigma(t,X_{1,t},\cdots,X_{r,t})\mathrm{d}B(t),

where
B(t)
is some martingale.

In what follows, we summarize some related results in the literature in Section
1.1
and provide an overview of our results and state the novelties in Section
1.2
.

1.1
Some related results

In this subsection, we summarize some related results. In the literature, the general model setup (
1.1
) has been studied based on the nonparametric kernel estimation. In
Vogt, 2012
, under the classical strong mixing conditions and using the locally stationary process as in
Dahlhaus et al., 2019
, the author proposed a Nadaraya-Watson (NW) type estimator utilizing a product kernel. Moreover, the author also established the convergence rates for their estimation on any compact set and the point-wise normality for their estimators. Moreover, the results and ideas have been used to study the structural changes problem in
Vogt, 2015
. Later on, in
Zhang and Wu, 2015
, the authors advanced the kernel estimation theory under a general setting using the physical representation
Wu, 2005
. They also applied their results to study the model selection problems. Moreover, recently, in
Karmakar et al., 2021
, under specific model setups, the authors considered the statistical inference for the time-varying parameters by analyzing the negative conditional Gaussian likelihoods with kernel estimations. They were able to establish the simultaneous inference results but only in the time domain without considering the covariates.

We also point out that (
1.1
) has also been studied in the literature provided some structural assumptions are imposed. On one hand, when the smooth functions are not time-varying, (
1.1
) has been extensively studied. Especially, the kernel based estimators and pointwise normality have been provided in the literature; see
Fan and Yao, 2003
;
Li and Racine, 2006
;
Linton and Wang, 2016
;
Zhao and Wu, 2008
among others, and the sieve least square estimators and asymptotic normality has also been studied; see
Belloni et al., 2015
;
Chen, 2007
;
Chen and Christensen, 2015
;
Hansen, 2014
among others. On the other hand, when the functions are time-varying, the kernel estimators and simultaneous inference have been studied for the time-varying coefficient linear regression model when the smooth functions are of the form
X_{j,i,n}\theta_{j}(t);
see
Cai, 2007
;
Fan and Zhang, 2000
;
Huang et al., 2002
;
Zhou, 2014b
;
Zhou, 2015
among others. Furthermore, researchers have generalized the linear regression model to the setting when
m_{j}=\rho_{j}(t)g(X_{j,i,n})
has a multiplicative separability structure. For instance, the kernel estimators and asymptotic normality have been established in
Chen et al., 2021
;
Pei et al., 2018
and the spline estimators and their asymptotic normality have been studied in
Zhang and Wang, 2015
for functional data and in
Hu et al., 2019
for locally stationary time series, among others.

In summary, most of the relevant literature focus on providing a kernel based estimator for the smooth functions in the general setting (
1.1
). It is known that due to modeling the variation with respect to both time and covariates, the convergence rates for the estimations can be slow. Moreover, the existing works only established convergence rates on a compact domain which may prohibit the applications when the covariates have unbounded supports. Finally, the literature has only focused on proving the point-wise convergence rates of the general smooth functions and they may not be useful for conducting inference on the smooth functions simultaneously.

Motivated by the above challenges, in the current paper, we study (
1.1
) in great generality and optimality. Inspired by the applications in the literature, we consider two scenarios of (
1.1
): (1). The time-inhomogeneous nonlinear time series model, i.e.,
X_{j,i,n}=Y_{i-j,n}
; (2). The general regression setting, i.e.,
X_{j,i,n}
are some locally stationary stationary predictors. On one hand, we provide sup-norm rate optimal nonparamteric sieve estimators for the functions
m_{j}^{\prime}
s. On the other hand, we develop simultaneously inferential theory for these functions. Our results and methodologies do not require any structural assumptions on
m_{j}
’s and we also allow the supports of these functions to be unbounded. We give an overview of our results in Section
1.2
.

1.2
Overview of our results and novelties

In this subsection, we give a heuristic overview of our main results. Throughout the paper, for the locally stationary time series, we use the general form of physical representation following
Ding and Zhou, 2020
;
Wu, 2005
;
Zhang and Wu, 2015
;
Zhou and Wu, 2009
(c.f. Assumption
2.1
) which covers a wide range of commonly used locally stationary time series (c.f. Examples
2.1
and
2.2
). The aim of this paper is to provide a systematic and unified estimation and inferential theory for the smooth functions in (
1.1
) without imposing additional structural assumption.

For the estimation part, we propose the sieve nonparamatric estimators for the smooth functions. However, unlike in the standard applications of sieve expansions where the smooth functions are supported on a bounded domain
Belloni et al., 2015
;
Chen, 2007
;
Chen and Christensen, 2015
;
Ding and Zhou, 2020
;
Ding and Zhou, 2021
;
Hansen, 2014
, the covariates in (
1.1
) are usually supported in
\mathbb{R}
and hence the sieve methods and the classic approximation theory cannot be applied directly. To address this issue, we use the mapped sieve basis functions
Boyd, 2009
;
Shen and Wang, 2009
which can monotonically and smoothly map the unbounded domain to a compact domain. Consequently, we can construct a hierarchical sieve basis functions as in (
3.7
) and establish the uniform approximation theory for the smooth functions on unbounded domain; see Theorem
3.1
for more details. Based on the approximation theory, it suffices to estimate the expansion coefficients using one ordinary least square to obtain the sieve estimators as in (
3.11
). The estimation procedure is computationally cheap and the proposed estimators are theoretically consistent. Especially, under mild regularity conditions, for short-range dependent locally stationary time series, our estimators achieve the min-max optimal rate as obtained by Stone in
Stone, 1982
; see Theorem
3.2
and Remark
3.1
for more details.

Once the estimation theory is established, we can develop the inferential theory. First, we provide the simultaneous confidence regions (SCR) based on our sieve estimators (c.f. (
4.6
)). Then we apply the SCR to conduct structural testings on the smooth functions. For example, we can test whether the functions are time-invariant (c.f. Example
4.1
) or have multiplicative separability structure (c.f. Example
4.2
). Second, we propose a
L_{2}
statistic to test whether the smooth functions are equal to some given functions (c.f. (
4.9
)). The key technical input is to establish a Gaussian approximation result for high dimensional locally stationary time series for the affine forms and quadratic forms. We prove such a result in Theorem
A.1
and it can be of independent interest. Especially, when the temporal relation of the locally stationary time series decays fast enough, the approximate rate matches that of
Fang, 2016
which is claimed to be the best known rate. Moreover, to obtain the critical values for the SCR, we establish the maximum deviation of a Gaussian process using the device of volume of tubes
Johansen and Johnstone, 1990
;
Knowles and Siegmund, 1989
;
Sun, 1993
. For practical implementation, we propose a multiplier bootstrap procedure as in
Chernozhukov et al., 2013
;
Zhou, 2013
which is both theoretically sound and empirically accurate and powerful; see Theorems
4.3
and
4.4
. Numerical simulations and real data analysis are provided to support our results and methodologies.

Finally, we point out that even though we focus on the object of the physical form of locally stationary time series, our methodologies and results can be applied to the locally stationary time series considered in
Dahlhaus et al., 2019
;
Vogt, 2012
or even the general non-stationary time series in
Ding and Zhou, 2021
, after some minor modifications. Before concluding this subsection, we summarize the contributions of the current paper as follows.

(1).

We propose the sieve estimators for the time-inhomogeneous nonlinear time series regression (
1.1
) using mapped sieve basis functions. Our methodology does not require any structural assumptions on the smooth functions and we allow the functions to be supported in an unbounded domain. Moreover, our estimation procedure only needs one OLS regression and our estimator achieves the min-max optimal rates under mild assumptions.

(2).

We establish a general and unified simultaneous inferential theory for the smooth functions in (
1.1
). Our theory can be used to conduct both structural and exact form testings on the smooth functions. Moreover, the proposed statistics are powerful even under weak alternatives.

(3).

We provide a multiplier bootstrap procedure to implement our methodologies in practice. The bootstrapping statistics can asymptotically mimic the distributions of the statistics proposed in the inferential theory. Moreover, our bootstrap method is robust and adaptive to the data set.

(4).

We establish a Gaussian approximation result for both affine and quadratic forms of high dimensional locally stationary time series. The result matches the best known rate under mild regularity assumptions. Moreover, we also calculate the critical values of the maxima of the Gaussian random field whose randomness arising from a locally stationary time series.

The paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
, we introduce the locally stationary time series, the concrete settings for (
1.1
) and some technical assumptions. In Section
3
, we propose the sieve estimators for the smooth functions and prove their theoretical optimality. In Section
4
, we develop the simultaneous inferential theory based on our sieve estimators and consider several important hypothesis testing problems. For practical applications, we propose a multiplier bootstrapping strategy. In Section
5
, we conduct extensive numerical simulations to support the usefulness of our estimators and proposed bootstrapping statistics for statistical inference. A real data analysis is provided in Section
6
. Technical proofs are deferred to Appendix
A
. Some auxiliary lemmas are collected in Appendix
B
and a list of commonly used sieve basis functions is provided in Appendix
C
.

The models and assumptions

In this section, we introduce the models, some related notations and assumptions. In the current paper, we consider the family of locally stationary time series for
{Y_{i},X_{j,i},\epsilon_{i}}
as considered in
Dahlhaus et al., 2019
;
Zhou and Wu, 2009
. As mentioned in Section
1
, we are primarily interested in two scenarios. We now state the models for both cases in Assumption
2.1
. For any random variable
Z\in\mathbb{R},
we denote its
q
-norm by

\|Z\|_{q}=\left(\mathbb{E}|X|^{q}\right)^{1/q}.

For simplicity, we write
\|Z\|\equiv\|Z\|_{2}.
For two sequences of real values
\{a_{n}\}
and
\{b_{n}\},
we write
a_{n}=\mathrm{O}(b_{n})
if
|a_{n}|\leq C|b_{n}|
for some constant
C
and
a_{n}=\mathrm{o}(b_{n})
if
|a_{n}|\leq\epsilon_{n}|b_{n}|
for some positive sequence
\{\epsilon_{n}\}
that
\epsilon_{n}\downarrow 0
as
n\rightarrow\infty.
Moreover, if
a_{n}=\mathrm{O}(b_{n})
and
b_{n}=\mathrm{O}(a_{n}),
we write
a_{n}\asymp b_{n}.
For a sequence of random variables
\{X_{n}\}
and a positive sequence of
\{C_{n}\},
we use the notation
X_{n}=\mathrm{O}_{\mathbb{P}}(C_{n})
to say that
X_{n}/C_{n}
is stochastically bounded, and
X_{n}=\mathrm{o}_{\mathbb{P}}(C_{n})
to say that
X_{n}/C_{n}
converges to

in probability. Throughout the paper, we omit the subscript
n
without causing further confusion.

Assumption 2.1
.

In this paper, we suppose the following assumptions hold for the regression (
1.1
):

(1). Time series regression setting:
X_{j,i}=Y_{i-j}
.
In this setting, we assume that both
\{X_{i}\}
and
\{\epsilon_{i}\}
are locally stationary time series such that

X_{i}=G_{1}\left(t_{i},\mathcal{F}_{i}\right),\ \epsilon_{i}=G_{2}\left(t_{i},\mathcal{F}_{i}\right),

(2.1)

where
\mathcal{F}_{i}=(\cdots,\eta_{i-1},\eta_{i})
and
\{\eta_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}
are i.i.d. random variables with
\mathbb{E}(\epsilon_{i}|\mathcal{F}_{i-1})=0
. Here
G_{1},G_{2}:\mathbb{R}^{\infty}\times\mathbb{R}^{\infty}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
are measurable functions such that for any fixed
t\in[0,1],

G_{k}(t,\mathcal{F}_{i}),k=1,2,
are properly defined random variables. Moreover, we assume that
G_{k}(t,\cdot)
are stochastic Lipschitz functions of

such that for some
q>2
, some constant
C>0
and any
s,t\in[0,1],

\sup_{i}\left\|G_{k}(s,\mathcal{F}_{i})-G_{k}(t,\mathcal{F}_{i})\right\|_{q}\leq C|s-t|,\ k=1,2.

(2.2)

(2). General non-stationary regression setting:
X_{j,i},1\leq j\leq r,
are locally stationary predictors.
In this setting, we assume that all
\{Y_{i}\},\{X_{i}\},\{\epsilon_{i}\}
are locally stationary time series such that

Y_{i}=G(t_{i},\mathcal{F}_{i}),\ X_{j,i}=H_{j}(t_{i},\mathcal{G}_{i}),\ \epsilon_{i}=D(t_{i},\mathcal{L}_{i}),

(2.3)

where
\mathcal{F}_{i}=(\cdots,\eta_{i-1},\eta_{i}),\ \mathcal{G}_{i}=(\cdots,\gamma_{i-1},\gamma_{i})
and
\mathcal{L}_{i}=(\cdots,(\eta_{i-1},\gamma_{i-1})^{\top},(\eta_{i-1},\gamma_{i-1})^{\top})
with
\mathbb{E}(\epsilon_{i}|{\cal G}_{i})=0.
Here we assume that
\{(\eta_{i},\gamma_{i})^{\top}\}_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}
are i.i.d. random elements,
G,H_{j},1\leq j\leq r,D
are measurable functions satisfying the stochastic Lipschitz continuous condition as in (
2.2
).

We remark that the physical form (
2.1
) is a very general representation in the sense that many locally stationary time series, linear or non-linear, can be written in this form. For illustration, we refer the readers to Examples
2.1
and
2.2
below. In the current paper, we focus on the practical setting that only one time series is available, i.e., only one realization of
\{X_{i}\}
for Case (1) and
\{(Y_{i},X_{i})\}
for Case (2) is observed. In this setting, it is natural to consider the time series with short-range temporal dependence. We employ the
physical dependence measure
to quantify the temporal dependence of the time series. For simplicity, we state the definition using
\{X_{i}\}
as in (
2.1
).

Definition 2.1

(Physical dependence measure)
.

Let
\{\eta_{i}^{\prime}\}
be an i.i.d. copy of
\{\eta_{i}\}.
Assume that for some
q>2,

\|X_{i}\|_{q}<\infty.

Then for
j\geq 0,
we define the physical dependence measure of
\{X_{i}\}
as

\delta_{1}(j,q)=\sup_{t}\|G_{1}(t,\mathcal{F}_{0}),G_{1}(t,\mathcal{F}_{0,j})\|_{q},

where
\mathcal{F}_{0,j}=(\mathcal{F}_{-j-1},\eta_{-j}^{\prime},\eta_{-j+1},\cdots,\eta_{0}).
For convenience, we denote
\delta_{1}(j,q)=0
when
j<0.

The physical dependence measure provides a convenient tool to study the temporal dependence of time series. On one hand, it quantifies the magnitude of change in the system’s output when the input of the system
j
steps ahead is replaced by an i.i.d. copy. On the other hand, the strong temporal dependence of the locally stationary time series can be controlled in terms of
\delta_{1}(j,q)
and the concentration inequalities (c.f. Lemmas
B.2
and
B.3
) can be established based on them. For more details on physical dependence measure, we refer the readers to
Liu and Lin, 2009
;
Liu et al., 2013
;
Wu, 2005
;
Wu, 2007
;
Wu and Zhou, 2011
. Moreover, for the purpose of illustration, in Examples
2.1
and
2.2
below, we explain how the physical dependence measure can be calculated easily for the commonly used examples of locally stationary time series.

Throughout the paper, we impose the following assumption to ensure that the temporal dependence decays fast enough so that the locally stationary time series has short-range dependence.

Assumption 2.2
.

Suppose that there exists some constant
\tau>0
and some constant
C>0
such that for Case (1) of Assumption
2.1

\max\{\delta_{1}(j,q),\delta_{2}(j,q)\}\leq Cj^{-\tau},\ \text{for all}\ j\geq 1,

(2.4)

where
\delta_{1},\delta_{2}
are the physical dependence measures for
\{X_{i}\}
and
\{\epsilon_{i}\},
respectively. Moreover, for Case (2) of Assumption
2.1
, we assume that

\max_{0\leq k\leq r+1}\delta_{k}(j,q)\leq Cj^{-\tau},\text{for all}\ j\geq 1,

where
\delta_{0}
is the physical dependence measure of
\{Y_{i}\},

\delta_{r+1}
is that of
\{\epsilon_{i}\}
and
\delta_{k},1\leq k\leq r,
are those of
\{X_{k,i}\},1\leq k\leq r.

Before concluding this subsection, we provide two examples of locally stationary time series.

Example 2.1

(Locally stationary linear time series)
.

Let
\{\epsilon_{i}\}
be i.i.d. random variables and
\{a_{j}(t)\}
be continuously differentiable functions on
[0,1].
Consider the locally stationary linear process such that

G(t,\mathcal{F}_{i})=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}a_{k}(t)\epsilon_{i-k}.

It is easy to see that the physical dependence measure of the above linear process, denoted as
\delta(j,q)
satisfies that

\delta(j,q)=\mathrm{O}(\sup_{t}|a_{j}(t)|).

Consequently, according to
(
Ding and Zhou, 2020
, Example 2.4)
, (
2.2
) and Assumption
2.2
are satisfied if the following conditions hold

\sup_{t}|a_{j}(t)|\leq Cj^{-\tau},\ \ \sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\sup_{t\in[0,1]}|a_{j}^{\prime}(t)|^{\min\{2,q\}}<\infty.

Example 2.2

(Locally stationary nonlinear time series)
.

Let
\{\epsilon_{i}\}
be i.i.d. random variables. Consider a locally stationary process as follows

G(t,\mathcal{F}_{i})=R(t,G(t,\mathcal{F}_{i-1}),\epsilon_{i}),

where
R
is some measurable function. The above expression is quite general such that many locally stationary time series can be written in the above form. For example, the threshold autoregressive model, exponential autoregressive model and bilinear autoregressive models. According to
(
Zhou and Wu, 2009
, Theorem 6)
, suppose that for some
x_{0},

\sup_{t}\|R(t,x_{0},\epsilon_{i})\|_{q}<\infty.
Denote

\chi:=\sup_{t}L(t),\ \text{where}\ L(t):=\sup_{x\neq y}\frac{\|R(t,x,\epsilon_{0})-R(t,y,\epsilon_{0})\|_{q}}{|x-y|}.

Note that
\chi<1
and
\delta(j,q)=\mathrm{O}(\chi^{j}).
Therefore, Assumption
2.2
is satisfied. Moreover, by
(
Zhou and Wu, 2009
, Proposition 4)
(
2.2
) holds if

\sup_{t\in[0,1]}\|M(G(t,\mathcal{F}_{0}))\|_{q}<\infty,\ \text{where}\ M(x):=\sup_{0\leq t<s\leq 1}\frac{\|R(t,x,\epsilon_{0})-R(s,x,\epsilon_{0})\|_{q}}{|t-s|}.

As a concrete example, we consider the time varying threshold autoregressive (TVTAR) model denoted as

G(t,\mathcal{F}_{i})=a(t)[G(t,\mathcal{F}_{i-1})]^{+}+b(t)[-G(t,\mathcal{F}_{i-1})]^{+}.

In this case, Assumption
2.2
and (
2.2
) will be satisfied if

a(t),b(t)\in\mathsf{C}^{1}([0,1]),\ \text{and}\ \sup_{t\in[0,1]}\left(|a(t)|+|b(t)|\right)<1.

3
Sieve estimation for time varying nonlinear regression

In this section, we propose a nonparametric approach to estimate the nonlinear functions
m_{j}(t,x),\ 1\leq j\leq r.

3.1
Sieve least square estimation

In this subsection, we use the sieve least square estimation approach to obtain the estimators for
m_{j}(t,x),1\leq j\leq r.
Due to similarity, we mainly focus on explaining Case (1) of Assumption
2.1
and only briefly discuss Case (2) from time to time; see Remark
3.2
below for some discussion. Our methodology is nonparametric and utilizes the sieve basis expansion; see
Chen, 2007
for a comprehensive review. We next provide a heuristic overview of the method and the details will be offered in Sections
3.1.1
and
3.1.2
.

Since we can only observe one realization of the time series, it is natural to impose some smoothness condition on
m_{j}(t,x)
(c.f. Assumption
3.1
). First, under these conditions, it suffices to consider
m_{j,c,d}(t,x)
(c.f. Theorem
3.1
) denoted as

m_{j,c,d}(t,x)=\sum_{\ell_{1}=1}^{c}\sum_{\ell_{2}=1}^{d}\beta_{j,\ell_{1},\ell_{2}}b_{\ell_{1},\ell_{2}}(t,x),

(3.1)

where
\{b_{\ell_{1},\ell_{2}}(t,x)\}
are the sieve basis functions and
\{\beta_{j,\ell_{1},\ell_{2}}\}
are the coefficients to be estimated. Here
c\equiv c(n),\ d\equiv d(n)
depend on the smoothness of
m_{j}(t,x).
For detailed discussion of the construction of sieve basis functions and the performance of (
3.1
), we refer the readers to Section
3.1.1
. Second, in view of (
3.1
), given a set of sieve basis functions, it suffices to estimate the coefficients. We will use the ordinary least square (OLS) to achieve this goal and obtain the estimators
\{\widehat{\beta}_{j,\ell_{1},\ell_{2}}\}
. This aspect will be discussed in Section
3.1.2
. Based on the above calculation, we can obtain our sieve least square estimation as follows

\widehat{m}_{j,c,d}(t,x)=\sum_{\ell_{1}=1}^{c}\sum_{\ell_{2}=1}^{d}\widehat{\beta}_{j,\ell_{1},\ell_{2}}b_{\ell_{1},\ell_{2}}(t,x).

(3.2)

3.1.1
Mapped hierarchical sieves for 2-D smooth function on unbounded domain

In this subsection, we discuss how to use sieve basis functions to approximate a smooth 2-D function. Note that for
m_{j}(t,x),1\leq j\leq r,
when
x
is defined on a compact domain in
\mathbb{R}
, the results have been established, for example, see
(
Chen, 2007
, Section 2.3.1)
for a comprehensive review. However, in the real applications, the domain of
x
is usually unbounded and mostly the whole real line. Therefore, we need to modify the commonly used sieves to accommodate for practical applications.

In the literature, there exist three different approaches to deal with the unbounded domain. The first method is to apply a finite interval method, such as Chebyshev polynomials, to an interval
x\in[-L,L]
where
L
is large but
finite. This method is also known as domain truncation. The second way is to use a basis that is intrinsic to the unbounded domain such as Hermite functions. The third approach is to map the infinite interval into a finite domain through a change of coordinate and then apply a finite interval method. We refer the readers to
Boyd, 2001
;
Boyd, 2009
;
Shen and Wang, 2009
for a review.

In the current paper, we employ the third method that we will apply some suitable mappings to map the unbounded domain to a compact one. The main reason is because the first and second methods usually result in slow approximate rates so that we need to use more basis functions in order to achieve certain accuracy. The mapping idea is popular in numerical PDEs for handling the boundary value problems. For a review of the mapping strategy, we refer the readers to
Boyd, 2009
;
Shen and Wang, 2009
. In what follows, without loss of generality, we assume that the domain for
x
is either
\mathbb{R}
or
\mathbb{R}_{+}.
The mappings will map the domain to a compact interval, say,
[-1,1].
We state the definitions of the mappings as follows.

Definition 3.1

(Mappings)
.

For some positive scaling factor
s>0,
consider a family of mappings of the form:

x=g(y;s),\ s>0,\ y\in I:=(-1,1),\ x\in\Lambda:=\mathbb{R}_{+}\ \text{or}\ \mathbb{R},

such that

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}x}{\mathrm{d}y}=g^{\prime}(y;s)>0,\ y\in I;

\displaystyle g(-1;s)=\begin{cases}0,&\Lambda=\mathbb{R}_{+}\\
-\infty,&\Lambda=\mathbb{R}\end{cases},\ g(1;s)=\infty.

Since the above mapping is invertible, we denote the inverse mapping by

y=g^{-1}(x;s)=u(x;s),\ x\in\Lambda,y\in I,s>0.

Based on the above definition, it is easy to see that
\frac{1}{2}u(x;s)+\frac{1}{2}

or

to

Before proceeding to construct the sieve basis functions,

Example 3.1

x=\frac{sy}{\sqrt{1-y^{2}}},\ y=\frac{x}{\sqrt{x^{2}+s^{2}}}.

In this example, we provide some mappings when

The second mapping is the following logarithmic mapping

3.1.2
OLS estimation for the coefficients

Armed with the basis expansion in Section

and the approximation result Theorem

in (

Since

which collects all these coefficients

in the order of the indices
\llbracket 1\mkern 1.5mu..\mkern 1.5mur\rrbracket\times\llbracket 1\mkern 1.5mu..\mkern 1.5muc\rrbracket\times\llbracket 1\mkern 1.5mu..\mkern 1.5mud\rrbracket;

where

as the design matrix whose entry satisfies that

(3.9)

where
\ell_{1}
and

are defined in

Furthermore, we denote

and

Recall that for Case (1),

Armed with these notations, the OLS estimator for

\widehat{\bm{\beta}}=(W^{\top}W)^{-1}W^{\top}\bm{Y}.

(3.10)

For

denote the diagonal matrix

where

when

and

otherwise, and

is the

be the collection of the basis functions

Then our proposed estimator for

3.2
Theoretical properties of the proposed estimators

In this subsection, we prove the properties of the proposed estimator (
3.11
). We first prepare some notations and assumptions. Let
\bm{w}_{i}=(w_{ik})_{\{1\leq k\leq rd\}}\in\mathbb{R}^{rd},r+1\leq i\leq n,
such that

w_{ik}=\varphi_{\ell_{2}(k)}(X_{i-\ell_{1}(k)-1}),

(3.12)

where
\ell_{1}(k)
and
\ell_{2}(k)
are defined as

\ell_{1}(k):=\left\lfloor\frac{k}{d}\right\rfloor,\ \ell_{2}(k)=k-\ell_{1}(k)d.

(3.13)

Moreover, we denote
\bm{x}_{i}=(x_{ik})_{\{1\leq k\leq rd\}}\in\mathbb{R}^{rd},r+1\leq i\leq n,
as

x_{ik}=w_{ik}\epsilon_{i}.

(3.14)

In the following lemma, we show that both the high dimensional time series
\{\bm{w}_{i}\}
and
\{\bm{x}_{i}\}
can be regarded as short-range dependent locally stationary stationary time series.

Lemma 3.1
.

Suppose (1) of Assumptions
2.1
and Assumption
2.2
hold. Then there exist measurable functions
\mathbf{V}(t,\cdot)=(V_{1}(t,\cdot),\cdots,V_{rd}(t,\cdot)),\ \mathbf{U}(t,\cdot)=(U_{1}(t,\cdot),\cdots,U_{rd}(t,\cdot))\in\mathbb{R}^{rd}
satisfying the stochastic Lipschitz continuity as in (
2.2
) such that
\{\bm{x}_{i}\}
is mean-zero and

\bm{w}_{i}=\mathbf{V}(t_{i},\mathcal{F}_{i}),\ \bm{x}_{i}=\mathbf{U}(t_{i},\mathcal{F}_{i}),\ t_{i}=\frac{i}{n}.

(3.15)

Moreover, denote their physical dependence measures as

\delta_{w}(j,q)=\sup_{1\leq k\leq rd}\sup_{t}\|V_{k}(t,\mathcal{F}_{0})-V_{k}(t,\mathcal{F}_{0,j})\|_{q},\ \delta_{x}(j,q)=\sup_{1\leq k\leq rd}\sup_{t}\|U_{k}(t,\mathcal{F}_{0})-U_{k}(t,\mathcal{F}_{0,j})\|_{q}.

(3.16)

Then we have that for some constant
C>0

\max\{\delta_{w}(j,q),\delta_{x}(j,q)\}\leq Cj^{-\tau},\ \text{for all}\ j\geq 1,

(3.17)

Similar results hold when (2) of Assumption
2.1
holds.

Next, we define the long-run covariance matrices of
\{\bm{w}_{i}\}
and
\{\bm{x}_{i}\}
as
\Pi(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{rd\times rd}
and
\Omega(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{rd\times rd},
respectively, as

\Pi(t):=\sum_{j=-\infty}^{+\infty}\operatorname{Cov}\left(\mathbf{V}(t,\mathcal{F}_{0}),\mathbf{V}(t,\mathcal{F}_{j})\right),\ \Omega(t):=\sum_{j=-\infty}^{+\infty}\operatorname{Cov}\left(\mathbf{U}(t,\mathcal{F}_{0}),\mathbf{U}(t,\mathcal{F}_{j})\right).

(3.18)

Throughout the paper, we will use the following short-hand notation

p:=rcd.

(3.19)

Using the above notations, denote the integrated long run covariance matrices
\Pi\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
and
\Omega\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
as

\Pi:=\int_{0}^{1}\Pi(t)\otimes(\mathbf{a}(t)\mathbf{a}^{\top}(t))\mathrm{d}t,\ \Omega:=\int_{0}^{1}\Omega(t)\otimes(\mathbf{a}(t)\mathbf{a}^{\top}(t))\mathrm{d}t,

(3.20)

where
\mathbf{a}(t)=(\phi_{1}(t),\cdots,\phi_{c}(t))^{\top}.

In the current paper, we will need the following regularity assumption on
\Pi
and
\Omega.
It is frequently used in the statistics literature, for instance, see
Cai et al., 2016
;
Chen et al., 2013
;
Ding and Zhou, 2020
;
Yuan, 2010
.

Assumption 3.2
.

For
\Pi
and
\Omega
in (
3.20
), we assume that there exists a universal constant
\kappa>0
such that

\min\{\lambda_{p}(\Pi),\lambda_{p}(\Omega)\}\geq\kappa,

where
\lambda_{p}(\cdot)
is the smallest eigenvalue of the given matrix.

Armed with the above notations and assumptions, we state the results regarding the consistency of our proposed estimator (
3.11
).
Denote

\xi_{c}:=\sup_{1\leq i\leq c}\sup_{t\in[0,1]}|\phi_{i}(t)|,\ \zeta=\sup_{t,x}\|\bm{b}\|,

(3.21)

where
\|\bm{b}\|\equiv\|\bm{b}\|_{2}
is the
L_{2}
norm of
\bm{b}.

Theorem 3.2
.

Suppose Assumptions
2.1
–
3.2
hold. Moreover, we assume

p\left(\frac{\xi^{2}_{c}}{\sqrt{n}}+\frac{\xi^{2}_{c}n^{\frac{2}{\tau+1}}}{n}\right)=\mathrm{o}(1).

(3.22)

Then we have that

\max_{j}\sup_{t\in[0,1],x\in\mathbb{R}}\left\|m_{j}(t,x)-\widehat{m}_{j,c,d}(t,x)\right\|=\mathrm{O}\left(\xi_{c}\zeta\sqrt{\frac{p}{n}}+c^{-\mathsf{m}_{1}}+d^{-\mathsf{m}_{2}}\right).

(3.23)

Remark 3.1
.

Theorem
3.2
implies that our proposed sieve least square estimator (
3.11
) is consistent under mild conditions. First,
\xi_{c}
and
\zeta
can be calculated for specific sieve basis functions and so does the convergence rate in (
3.23
). For example, when
\{\phi_{i}(t)\}
are chosen as the Fourier basis functions and
\{\varphi_{j}(t)\}
as the mapped Fourier basis functions, then
\xi_{c}=\mathrm{O}(1)
and
\zeta=\mathrm{O}(\sqrt{p}).
Additionally, if we assume that
\mathsf{m}_{1}=\mathsf{m}_{2}=\infty
such that
m(t,x)
is infinitely differentiable, by choosing

the rate on the right-hand side of (
3.23
) reads as
\mathrm{O}(n^{-1/2}\log^{2}n)
which matches the optimal uniform (i.e. sup-norm) convergence rate as obtained in
Chen and Christensen, 2015
;
Ding and Zhou, 2020
;
Stone, 1982
. For the magnitudes of
\xi_{c}
and
\zeta
for more general sieves, we refer the readers to Appendix
C
.

Second, the condition (
3.22
) ensures that
n^{-1}W^{\top}W
in the OLS estimator
\widehat{\bm{\beta}}
in (
3.10
) will converge to the integrated long run covaraince matrix
\Pi,
which guarantees the regularity behavior of
\widehat{\bm{\beta}}
; see (
A.9
) for more details. In fact (
3.22
) can be easily satisfied. For example, when
\xi_{c}=\mathrm{O}(1)
and

we only require
\tau>1
for the physical dependence measure as in (
2.4
).

Remark 3.2
.

We point out that the general non-stationary setting, i.e., (2) of Assumption
2.1
can be handled similarly with some minor modifications. For example, in (
3.9
),
X_{i-(l+1)}
should be replaced by
X_{l+1,i},
and in (
3.12
),
X_{i-\ell_{1}(k)-1}
should be replaced by

We omit the details since the main differences are just notational.

4
Statistical inference for the nonlinear regression

In this section, we infer the nonlinear functions
m_{j}(t,x),1\leq j\leq r,
based on our proposed sieve least square estimator (
3.11
). As in Section
3
, we focus our discussion on Case (1) of Assumption
2.1
and only briefly explain how to handle Case (2) without providing extra details in Remark
4.3
. Throughout this section, we assume that for the error in (
3.23
)

c^{-\mathsf{m}_{1}}+d^{-\mathsf{m}_{2}}=\mathrm{O}(n^{-\gamma}),\ \gamma>\frac{1}{2}.

(4.1)

Note that (
4.1
) is a mild assumption by choosing reasonably large values of
c
and
d
. For example, if
\mathsf{m}_{1}=\mathsf{m}_{2}=\infty,
it can be easily satisfied by choosing
c,d\asymp\log n.
In general, it will be satisfied if we choose

c\asymp n^{C/\mathsf{m}_{1}},\ d\asymp n^{C/\mathsf{m}_{2}},

(4.2)

for some large constant
C>\frac{1}{2}.
This shows that we only need a relatively small amount of basis functions once
\mathsf{m}_{k},k=1,2,
are reasonably large.

4.1
Problems setup and proposed statistics

In this subsection, we introduce several commonly concerned problems and provide our proposed statistics utilizing (
3.11
).

For
1\leq j\leq r,
our first goal is to construct a
(1-\alpha)
simultaneous confidence region (SCR) of
m_{j}(t,x)
based on our estimator (
3.11
), denoted as
\{\Upsilon_{\alpha}(t,x),0\leq t\leq 1,x\in\mathbb{R}\}.
The SCR is defined as follows

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\mathbb{P}\left\{m_{j}(t,x)\in\Upsilon_{\alpha}(t,x),\ \forall\ 0\leq t\leq 1,\ \forall\ x\in\mathbb{R}\right\}=1-\alpha,

(4.3)

which generalizes the definition of simultaneous confidence band as in
Fan and Zhang, 2000
;
Zhou and Wu, 2010
. For
0\leq t\leq 1
and
x\in\mathbb{R},
denote

h_{j}(t,x)=\sqrt{\bm{l}_{j}^{\top}\Omega\bm{l}_{j}},\ 1\leq j\leq r,

(4.4)

where
\bm{l}_{j}\equiv\bm{l}_{j}(t,x):=\Pi^{-1}(\bm{b}\otimes\mathbf{I}_{r})\mathsf{I}_{j}
and
\Pi,\Omega
are defined in (
3.20
). Recall (
3.11
). Denote

\mathsf{T}_{1j}(t,x)=\widehat{m}_{j,c,d}(t,x)-m_{j}(t,x).

(4.5)

As we will see in Theorem
4.1
,
h_{j}^{2}(t,x)
is the asymptotic variance of
\sqrt{n}\mathsf{T}_{1j}.
Therefore, given the nominal level
\alpha,
the SCR can be constructed as follows

\hat{m}_{j,c,d}(t,x)\pm\frac{c_{\alpha}}{\sqrt{n}}h_{j}(t,x),\quad t\in[0,1],x\in\mathbb{R},

(4.6)

where
c_{\alpha}
is the critical value which follows that

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\mathbb{P}(\sup_{t,x}\left|\frac{\sqrt{n}\mathsf{T}_{1j}}{h_{j}(t,x)}\right|\leq c_{\alpha})=1-\alpha.

As discussed in
(
Yang and Zhou, 2020
, Section 5.2)
, the SCR can serve as a foundation for inferring the structure of
m_{j}(t,x).
The general idea can be described as follows. Under some specific structural assumptions (see Examples
4.1
and
4.2
below), the functions
m_{j}(t,x)
can be estimated with faster convergence rates than those estimated using the general approach (
3.11
). For example, in Example
4.1
, under the assumption that
m_{j}
is stationary (i.e., independent of time), instead of using
\mathrm{O}(cd)
hierarchical basis functions as in (
3.7
), we only need
\mathrm{O}(d)
mapped sieve basis functions so that we will have a better rate compared to use all the basis functions as illustrated in (
3.23
). Similarly, assuming
m_{j}(t,x)
is separable in
t,x
as in Example
4.2
, we can estimate them separately so that we only need
\mathrm{O}(c+d)
basis functions.

Based on the above observations, if the functions
m_{j}(t,x)
under the null hypothesis can be estimated with faster convergence rates than those estimated using the general approach (
3.11
), to test the structure of
m_{j}(t,x)
under some specific null hypothesis, it suffices to check whether the estimated functions under the null hypothesis (with faster convergence rates) can be embedded into the SCR constructed generally using (
3.11
). This general procedure achieves asymptotic power one as long as the alternatives deviate from the null hypothesis with a rate large than the order of the width of the SCR. In what follows, we present two important examples.

Example 4.1

(Testing time-homogeneity)
.

We consider the structural assumption that

\mathbf{H}_{0}:\ m_{j}(t,x)\equiv m_{j}(x),\ \ \forall t\in[0,1],

(4.7)

for some function
m_{j}(\cdot).
Under the null assumption of (
4.7
), we can estimate the function only using the basis
\{\varphi_{j}(x)\}
instead of using (
3.7
). Under the assumption of (
4.1
), by an argument similar to Theorem
3.2
, let
\zeta_{d}:=\sup_{x}\|(\varphi_{1}(x),\cdots,\varphi_{d}(x))^{*}\|,
we can obtain a convergence rate
\zeta_{d}(d/n)^{1/2}
which is faster than the rate in (
3.23
).

Example 4.2

(Testing separability)
.

We consider the structural assumption that

\mathbf{H}_{0}:m_{j}(t,x)=f_{j}(t)g_{j}(x),\ \forall\ t\in[0,1]\ \text{and}\ x\in\mathbb{R},

(4.8)

for some functions
f_{j}(\cdot)
and
g_{j}(\cdot).
Under the null assumption of (
4.8
), we can estimate
f_{j}(\cdot)
and
g_{j}(\cdot)
separately using the basis functions
\{\phi_{j}(t)\}
and
\{\varphi_{j}(x)\},
respectively. Under the assumption of (
4.1
), we can obtain a convergence rate
\zeta_{c}(c/n)^{1/2}+\zeta_{d}(d/n)^{1/2}
which is faster than the rate in (
3.23
).

Our second task is to conduct hypothesis on the functions
m_{j}(t,x),1\leq j\leq r.
In this problem, we want to test the exact forms of the functions instead of understanding their structure as discussed earlier. In this setting, generally, we cannot obtain an estimator with a faster convergence rate and SCR may not be applicable. Instead, we propose a
L_{2}
test. Specifically, we are interested in testing the following null hypothesis

\mathbf{H}_{0}:\ m_{j}(t,x)\equiv m_{j,0}(t,x),

(4.9)

where
m_{j,0}(t,x)
is some pre-given function. To test (
4.9
), we propose the following
L_{2}
statistic

\mathsf{T}_{2j}=\int_{[0,1]}\int_{\mathbb{R}}[\hat{m}_{j,c,d}(t,x)-m_{j,0}(t,x)]^{2}\mathrm{d}t\mathrm{d}x.

(4.10)

Note that under the null hypothesis (
4.9
),
\mathsf{T}_{2j}
should be small.

Remark 4.1
.

Even though we focus our arguments for each specific
j,
our discussion can be easily generalized to the multiple case. We take the second task as an example. Instead of considering (
4.9
), one may be interested in testing

\mathbf{H}_{0}:\ m_{j}(t,x)\equiv m_{j,0}(t,x),\ \text{for all}\ 1\leq j\leq r.

In this setting, we shall use the statistic

\mathsf{T}_{2}=\sum_{j=1}^{r}\int_{[0,1]}\int_{\mathbb{R}}[\hat{m}_{j,c,d}(t,x)-m_{j,0}(t,x)]^{2}\mathrm{d}t\mathrm{d}x.

(4.11)

As will be seen in Remark
4.3
below, the distribution of
\mathsf{T}_{2}
can be obtained in a similar fashion as
\mathsf{T}_{2j}.
Similar arguments hold for the SCR.

and

First, for the SCR (

Together with Theorems

Numerical simulations

In this section, we conduct extensive numerical simulations to illustrate the usefulness of our results. As before, we focus our discussion on Case (1) of Assumption

.

5.1
Parameter selection

In this subsection, we discuss how to choose the important parameters in practice using the data driven approach as in
Bishop, 2013
.

We first discuss how to choose
c
and
d.
For a given integer
l,
say
l=\lfloor 3\log_{2}n\rfloor,
we divide the time series into two parts: the training part
\{X_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n-l}
and the validation part
\{X_{i}\}_{i=n-l+1}^{n}.
With some preliminary initial pair
(c,d)
, we propose a sequence of candidate pairs
(c_{i},d_{j}),\ i=1,2,\cdots,u,\ j=1,2,\cdots,v,
in an appropriate neighborhood of
(c,d)
where
u,v
are some given large integers. For each pair of the choices
(c_{i},d_{j}),
we estimate
\widehat{m}_{j,c,d}(t,x)
as in (
3.11
). Then using the fitted model, we forecast the time series in the validation part of the time series. Let
\widehat{X}_{n-l+1,ij},\cdots,\widehat{X}_{n,ij}
be the forecast of
X_{n-l+1},...,X_{n},
respectively using the parameter pair
(c_{i},d_{j})
. Then we choose the pair
(c_{i_{0}},d_{j_{0}})
with the minimum sample MSE of forecast, i.e.,

({i_{0}},{j_{0}}):=\mathop{\mbox{argmin}}_{((i,j):1\leq i\leq u,1\leq j\leq v)}\frac{1}{l}\sum_{k=n-l+1}^{n}(X_{k}-\widehat{X}_{k,ij})^{2}.

Then we discuss how to choose
m
for practical implementation. In
Zhou, 2013
, the author used the minimum volatility (MV) method to choose the window size
m
for the scalar covariance function. The MV method does not depend on the specific form of the underlying time series dependence structure and hence is robust to misspecification of
the latter structure
Politis et al., 1999
. The MV method utilizes the fact that the covariance structure of
\widehat{\Omega}
becomes stable when the
block size
m
is in an appropriate range, where
\widehat{\Omega}=E[\Phi\Phi^{*}|(X_{1},\cdots,X_{n})]=
is defined as

\widehat{\Omega}:=\frac{1}{(n-m-r+1)m}\sum_{i=b+1}^{n-m}\Big[\Big(\sum_{j=i}^{i+m}\widehat{\bm{x}}_{i}\Big)\otimes\Big(\mathbf{a}(\frac{i}{n})\Big)\Big]\times\Big[\Big(\sum_{j=i}^{i+m}\widehat{\bm{x}}_{i}\Big)\otimes\Big(\mathbf{a}(\frac{i}{n})\Big)\Big]^{\top}.

(5.1)

Therefore, it desires to minimize the standard errors of the latter covariance structure in a suitable range of candidate
m
’s.

In detail, for a give large value
m_{n_{0}}
and a neighbourhood control parameter
h_{0}>0,
we can choose a sequence of window sizes
m_{-h_{0}+1}<\cdots<m_{1}<m_{2}<\cdots<m_{n_{0}}<\cdots<m_{n_{0}+h_{0}}
and obtain
\widehat{\Omega}_{m_{j}}
by replacing
m
with
m_{j}
in (
5.1
),
j=-h_{0}+1,2,\cdots,n_{0}+h_{0}.
For each
m_{j},j=1,2,\cdots,m_{n_{0}},
we calculate the matrix norm error of
\widehat{\Omega}_{m_{j}}
in the
h_{0}
-neighborhood, i.e.,

\mathsf{se}(m_{j}):=\mathsf{se}(\{\widehat{\Omega}_{m_{j+k}}\}_{k=-h_{0}}^{h_{0}})=\left[\frac{1}{2h_{0}}\sum_{k=-h_{0}}^{h_{0}}\|\overline{\widehat{\Omega}}_{m_{j}}-\widehat{\Omega}_{m_{j}+k}\|^{2}\right]^{1/2},

where
\overline{\widehat{\Omega}}_{m_{j}}=\sum_{k=-h_{0}}^{h_{0}}\widehat{\Omega}_{m_{j}+k}/(2h_{0}+1).

Therefore, we choose the estimate of
m
using

\widehat{m}:=\mathop{\mbox{argmin}}_{m_{1}\leq m\leq m_{n_{0}}}\mathsf{se}(m).

Note that in
Zhou, 2013
the author used
h_{0}=3
and we also use this choice in the current paper.

5.2
Simulations setup

In this subsection, we introduce our simulation setup in the time series setting. We consider that
r=1
and consider the following specifications of
m(t,x)
which have been studied in the literature. Consider that

X_{i}=m(t,X_{i-1})+\sigma(t,X_{i-1})\epsilon_{i}.

Note that we will choose
\epsilon_{i}
to be a locally stationary time series so that the variance of
\sigma(t,X_{i-1})\epsilon_{i}
depends on both
X_{i-1}
and

The settings of
m(t,x),\sigma(t,x)
and
\{\epsilon_{i}\}
are listed below.

In what follows, we consider that
m(t,x),\sigma(t,x)
take the following forms. For some

(1).

m(t,x)=5t+4\cos(2\pi tx),\ \sigma(t,x)=1.5\exp(-x^{2}/2)\left(2+\sin(2\pi t)\right).

(2).

m(t,x)=(\delta\sin(2\pi t)+1)\exp(-x^{2}/2),
and
\sigma(t,x)
is defined as

\sigma(t,x)=\frac{1.5\exp(x)}{1+\exp(x)}\left(0.5\cos(2\pi tx)+1\right).

(3).

m(t,x)=4t(\delta\cos(2\pi tx)-\frac{1}{2}\exp(-x^{2}/2)),
and
\sigma(t,x)
is defined as

\sigma(t,x):=\begin{cases}0.7(1+x^{2}),&|x|\leq 1;\\
1.4,&|x|>1\ \text{and}\ 0\leq t<0.5,\\
2,&|x|>1\ \text{and}\ 0.5\leq t<1.\end{cases}

Moreover, for the locally stationary time series
\{\epsilon_{i}\}
, denote

a_{1}(\frac{i}{n})\equiv 0.4,\ a_{2}(\frac{i}{n})=0.4\sin(2\pi\frac{i}{n}),

and

are i.i.d. standard Gaussian random variables, we consider the following settings

(a).

Time-varying linear AR(2) model

\epsilon_{i}=\sum_{j=1}^{2}a_{j}(\frac{i}{n})\epsilon_{i-j}+\eta_{i}.

(b).

Self-exciting threshold auto-regressive (SETAR) model

\epsilon_{i}=\begin{cases}a_{1}(\frac{i}{n})\epsilon_{i-1}+\eta_{i},&\epsilon_{i-1}\geq 0,\\
a_{2}(\frac{i}{n})\epsilon_{i-1}+\eta_{i},&\epsilon_{i-1}<0.\end{cases}

6
Real data analysis

In this section, we apply our methodologies to study the shape of the monthly risk premium for S
\&
P 500 index as in
(
Chen et al., 2021
, Section 6.2)
. Let
\mu_{t}
and
\sigma_{t}
be the conditional mean and conditional volatility of the excess return of the market portfolio, respectively. Our goal is to uncover how
\mu_{t}
relates to
\sigma_{t}.
Especially, we want to understand the functional relation that
\mu_{t}=m(t,\sigma_{t}^{2})
for some unknown function
m.
Such a problem has been studied extensively in the literature of financial economics. In the seminal work
Merton, 1973
, Merton modeled that
\mu_{t}=\gamma\sigma_{t}^{2}
for some constant
\gamma
representing the risk aversion of the agent. Recently, it has been argued in
Bollerslev et al., 2013
;
Brandt and Wang, 2010
;
Chaieb et al., 2021
;
Ghysels et al., 2014
;
Gu et al., 2020
that a general nonlinear and time-varying function will facilitate the modeling and interpretation of the shape of the market risk premium.

Very recently, in
Chen et al., 2021
, the authors modeled the relationship between
\mu_{t}
and
\sigma_{t}
using
\mu_{t}=\rho(t)g(\sigma_{t})
for some unknown functions
\rho
and
g.
They also estimated these functions using kernel methods. Moreover, on one hand, they found their estimation were consistent with real observations empirically and statistically analysis theoretically. On the other hand, they provided some insights for the shape of the monthly risk premium for S
\&
P 500 Index based on their analysis. However, they did not justify why a separable structure was valid for modeling the risk premium. In what follows, we use our proposed multiplier bootstrap statistics to justify the separability assumption, i.e., testing (
4.8
).

We follow the setting of
(
Chen et al., 2021
, Section 6.2)
. In the notation of our model (
1.1
),
r=1
and we set
Y_{t}
to be
\mu_{t}=r_{mt}-r_{ft}
which is the excess return on the S
\&
P 500 Index calculated as the difference between the monthly continuously compounded cumulative return on the index minus the monthly return on 30-day Treasury Bills. For the conditional volatility
\sigma_{t},
we used the realized volatility (RV) measure from Oxford Man Realized Library (
https://realized.oxford-man.ox.ac.uk/
). To be more precise, let
RV_{t}
denote the daily annualized RV during the
t
th month. Then we obtain one-month-ahead RV forecast
\mathbb{E}_{t-1}(RV_{t})
using the HAR-RV model as in
Corsi, 2009
, which is our
X_{t}.
Our data covers the period of 31 January 2001–31 December 2018. Under the nominal level
0.05,
using the Daubechies-9 basis functions with
c=4,d=3
and
m=7,
we find that the
p
-value is
0.38
so we can conclude that the separable structural assumption is reasonable for modeling the risk premium. This supports the analysis of
Chen et al., 2021
.

Finally, we estimate the functions
\rho(\cdot)
and
g(\cdot)
using our sieve estimators. Due to separability, as discussed in Example
4.2
, we can estimate them separability instead of using the hierarchical sieve basis functions. We also provide the simultaneous confidence bands for these functions. In Figure
3
blow, we report the results. We find that when the RV is fixed, the risk premium changes in a nonlinear way of time which suggests the existence of a time-varying risk aversion. Moreover, for the
g(\cdot)
function, it is more flat and seems to have a monotone pattern. Our findings are consistent with those in
Chen et al., 2021
; see Figure 7 and the discussion therein.

A.2

In this subsection, we prove Theorem

Proof of Theorem
3.2
.

Since
r
is finite, without loss of generality, we assume
r=1.
For general
r,
the proof can be modified verbatim with only additional notational complicatedness. In what follows, we omit the subscript
j,
i.e.,
m\equiv m_{1}
and
\widehat{m}_{c,d}\equiv\widehat{m}_{1,c,d}.
The starting point of our proof is the following decomposition

\displaystyle\|\widehat{m}_{c,d}(t,x)-m(t,x)\|

\displaystyle\leq\|\widehat{m}_{c,d}(t,x)-m_{c,d}(t,x)\|+|m_{c,d}(t,x)-m(t,x)|.

(A.3)

Since the second term of the right-hand side of (
A.3
) can be bounded using Theorem
3.1
, it suffices to control the first term. Moreover, using the definitions (
3.1
), (
3.2
) and (
3.11
), by Cauchy-Schwarz inequality,

\|\widehat{m}_{c,d}(t,x)-m_{c,d}(t,x)\|\leq\|\widehat{\bm{\beta}}-\bm{\beta}\|\zeta.

(A.4)

The rest of the proof leaves to control
\widehat{\bm{\beta}}-\bm{\beta}.
Recall that

\widehat{\bm{\beta}}-\bm{\beta}=(W^{\top}W)^{-1}W^{\top}\bm{\epsilon}.

(A.5)

Therefore, we need to control
\|\widehat{\bm{\beta}}-\bm{\beta}\|
which satisfies

\|\widehat{\bm{\beta}}-\bm{\beta}\|\leq\left\|\frac{1}{\lambda_{p}((n^{-1}W^{\top}W))}\right\|\|n^{-1}W^{\top}\bm{\epsilon}\|.

(A.6)

First, we establish the convergence results for the matrix
W^{\top}W.
Denote
W_{i}
as the
i
th column of
W.
It suffices to analyze the terms

Without loss of generality, we focus our discussion on the terms
W_{1}^{\top}W_{1}
and
W_{1}^{\top}W_{2}.
The general cases of
i,j
can be handled similarly. We start with the term

). We have

\frac{1}{n}W_{1}^{\top}W_{1}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=2}^{n}\phi_{1}(t_{k})^{2}\varphi_{1}(X_{k-1})^{2}.

3.1
, we can show that
\mathfrak{h}_{k}:=\phi_{1}(t_{k})^{2}\varphi_{1}(X_{k-1})^{2},2\leq k\leq n,

\delta_{\mathfrak{h}}(j,q)\leq C\xi_{c}^{2}j^{-\tau},\ \text{for some constant}\ C>0.

, we obtain that

\left\|\frac{1}{n}W_{1}^{\top}W_{1}-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=2}^{n}\phi_{1}(t_{k})^{2}\mathbb{E}\varphi_{1}(X_{k-1})^{2}\right\|_{q}\leq\frac{C\xi_{c}^{2}}{\sqrt{n}},

, we have that

\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=2}^{n}\phi_{1}(t_{k})^{2}\mathbb{E}\varphi_{1}(X_{k-1})^{2}-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=2}^{n}\phi_{1}(t_{k})^{2}\Pi_{11}(t_{k})\right|\leq C\xi_{c}^{2}n^{-1+\frac{2}{\tau+1}},

\Pi

\left\|\frac{1}{n}W^{\top}W-\Pi\right\|_{\mathrm{op}}=\mathrm{O}_{\mathbb{P}}\left(p\left(\frac{\xi^{2}_{c}}{\sqrt{n}}+\frac{\xi^{2}_{c}n^{\frac{2}{\tau+1}}}{n}\right)\right),

3.19

3.2

3.22

\left\|\frac{1}{\lambda_{p}(n(W^{\top}W)^{-1})}\right\|=\mathrm{O}(1).

(A.10)

Second, we control the error term

Without loss of generality, we focus on its first entry and consider

\frac{\left[W^{\top}\bm{\epsilon}\right]_{11}}{n}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=2}^{n}\phi_{1}(t_{k})\varphi_{1}(X_{k-1})\epsilon_{k}.

(A.11)

3.1

(A.13)

Appendix C
A brief summary of sieve spaces approximation theory

In this section, we give a brief overview on the sieve approximation theory
Chen, 2007
for compact domain and the mapped sieve approximation theory for the unbounded domain
Boyd, 2001
;
Boyd, 2009
;
Shen and Wang, 2009
. We start with introducing some commonly used sieve basis functions.

Example C.1

(Trigonometric and mapped trigonometric polynomials)
.

For
x\in[0,1],
we consider the following trigonometric polynomials

\left\{1,\sqrt{2}\cos(2k\pi x),\sqrt{2}\sin(2k\pi x),\cdots\right\},\ k\in\mathbb{N}.

The above basis functions form an orthonormal basis
of
L_{2}([0,1]).
Note that the classic trigonometric basis function is well suited for approximating periodic functions on
[0,1].

When
x\in\mathbb{R},
let
u(x):\mathbb{R}\rightarrow[0,1]
be constructed following Example
3.1
. Since
u(x)
in Example
3.1
satisfies that
u^{\prime}(x)\in L_{2}(\mathbb{R})
, the following mapped trigonometric polynomials form an orthonomal basis for
L_{2}(\mathbb{R})

\left\{\sqrt{u^{\prime}(x)},\ \sqrt{2u^{\prime}(x)}\cos(2k\pi u(x)),\ \sqrt{2u^{\prime}(x)}\sin(2k\pi u(x)),\cdots\right\},\ k\in\mathbb{N},

where we used inverse function theorem.

Example C.2

(Jacobi and mapped Jacobi polynomials)
.

For
I=(-1,1)
and some constants
\alpha,\beta>-1,
denote the Jacobi weight function as

\omega^{\alpha,\beta}(y)=(1-y)^{\alpha}(1+y)^{\beta},

and associated weighted
\mathcal{L}^{2}
space as
\mathcal{L}^{2}_{\omega^{\alpha,\beta}}(I).
With the above notations, the Jacobi polynomials are the orthogonal polynomials
\{J_{n}^{\alpha,\beta}(y)\}
such that

\int_{I}J_{n}^{\alpha,\beta}(y)J_{m}^{\alpha,\beta}(y)\omega^{\alpha,\beta}(y)\mathrm{d}y=\gamma_{n}^{\alpha,\beta}\delta_{n,m},

where
\delta_{n,m}
is the Kronecker function, and

\gamma_{n}^{\alpha,\beta}=\frac{2^{\alpha+\beta+1}\Gamma(n+\alpha+1)\Gamma(n+\beta+1)}{(2n+\alpha+\beta+1)\Gamma(n+1)\Gamma(n+\alpha+\beta+1)}.

More explicitly, the Jacobi polynomials can be characterized using the Rodrigues’ formula
Szegö, 1939

J_{n}^{\alpha,\beta}(x)=\frac{(-1)^{n}}{2^{n}n!}(1-x)^{-\alpha}(1+x)^{-\beta}\frac{\mathrm{d}^{n}}{\mathrm{d}x^{n}}\left((1-x)^{\alpha}(1+x)^{\beta}(1-x^{2})^{n}\right).

Then for
x\in[0,1],
the following sequence forms an orthonormal basis for
L_{2}([0,1])

\left\{\mathsf{J}^{\alpha,\beta}_{k}(x):=\sqrt{\frac{2\omega^{\alpha,\beta}(2x-1)}{\gamma_{k}^{\alpha,\beta}}}J_{k}^{\alpha,\beta}(2x-1)\right\},\ k\in\mathbb{N}.

We mention that when
\alpha=\beta=0,
the Jacobi polynomial reduces to the Legendre polynomial and when
\alpha=\beta=-0.5,
the Jacobi polynomial reduces to the Chebyshev polynomial of the first kind.

When
x\in\mathbb{R},
let
u(x)
be the mapping as in Example
C.1
. Then the following sequence provides an orthonormal basis for
L_{2}(\mathbb{R})
(see
(
Shen and Wang, 2009
, Section 2.3)
)

\left\{\sqrt{u^{\prime}(x)}\mathsf{J}_{k}^{\alpha,\beta}(u(x))\right\}.

Example C.3

(Wavelet and mapped Wavelet basis)
.

For
N\in\mathbb{N},
a Daubechies (mother) wavelet of class
D-N
is a function
\psi\in L_{2}(\mathbb{R})
defined by

\psi(x):=\sqrt{2}\sum_{k=1}^{2N-1}(-1)^{k}h_{2N-1-k}\varphi(2x-k),

where
h_{0},h_{1},\cdots,h_{2N-1}\in\mathbb{R}
are the constant (high pass) filter coefficients satisfying the conditions

\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}h_{2k}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}=\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}h_{2k+1},

as well as, for
l=0,1,\cdots,N-1

\sum_{k=2l}^{2N-1+2l}h_{k}h_{k-2l}=\begin{cases}1,&l=0,\\
0,&l\neq 0.\end{cases}

And
\varphi(x)
is the scaling (father) wavelet function is supported on
[0,2N-1)
and satisfies the recursion equation

\varphi(x)=\sqrt{2}\sum_{k=0}^{2N-1}h_{k}\varphi(2x-k),
as well as the normalization
\int_{\mathbb{R}}\varphi(x)dx=1
and

\int_{\mathbb{R}}\varphi(2x-k)\varphi(2x-l)dx=0,\ k\neq l.

Note that the filter coefficients can be efficiently computed as listed in
Daubechies, 1992
. The order
N
, on the one hand, decides the support of our wavelet; on the other hand, provides the regularity condition in the sense that

\int_{\mathbb{R}}x^{j}\psi(x)dx=0,\ j=0,\cdots,N,\ \text{where}\ N\geq d.

We will employ Daubechies wavelet with a sufficiently high order when forecasting in our simulations and data analysis. In the present paper, to construct a sequence of orthogonal wavelet, we will follow the dyadic construction of
Daubechies, 1988
. For a given
J_{n}
and
J_{0},
we will consider the following periodized wavelets on
[0,1]

\Big\{\varphi_{J_{0}k}(x),\ 0\leq k\leq 2^{J_{0}}-1;\psi_{jk}(x),\ J_{0}\leq j\leq J_{n}-1,0\leq k\leq 2^{j}-1\Big\},\ \mbox{ where}

(C.1)

\varphi_{J_{0}k}(x)=2^{J_{0}/2}\sum_{l\in\mathbb{Z}}\varphi(2^{J_{0}}x+2^{J_{0}}l-k),\ \psi_{jk}(x)=2^{j/2}\sum_{l\in\mathbb{Z}}\psi(2^{j}x+2^{j}l-k),

or, equivalently
Meyer, 1990

\Big\{\varphi_{J_{n}k}(x),\ 0\leq k\leq 2^{J_{n}-1}\Big\}.

(C.2)

When
x\in\mathbb{R},
let
u(x)
be the mapping as in Example
C.1
. Then using (
C.2
), the following sequence provides an orthonormal basis for
L_{2}(\mathbb{R})

\left\{\sqrt{u^{\prime}(x)}\varphi_{J_{n}k}(u(x))\right\}.

Similarly, we can construct the mapped wavelet functions using (
C.1
).

Next, we collect some important properties of the above basis functions and some useful results on the approximation errors.

–

.

.

Suppose Assumption

denote
