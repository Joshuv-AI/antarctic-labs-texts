A Bias-Accuracy-Privacy Trilemma for Statistical Estimation Authors are ordered alphabetically.
Gautam Kamath, Argyris Mouzakis, Matthew Regehr, Vikrant Singhal, Thomas Steinke, Jonathan Ullman

Abstract
Differential privacy (DP) is a rigorous notion of data privacy, used for private statistics. The canonical algorithm for differentially private mean estimation is to first clip the samples to a bounded range and then add noise to their empirical mean. Clipping controls the sensitivity and, hence, the variance of the noise that we add for privacy. But clipping also introduces statistical bias. This tradeoff is inherent: we prove that no algorithm can simultaneously have low bias, low error, and low privacy loss for arbitrary distributions. Additionally, we show that under strong notions of DP (i.e., pure or concentrated DP), unbiased mean estimation is impossible, even if we assume that the data is sampled from a Gaussian. On the positive side, we show that unbiased mean estimation is possible under a more permissive notion of differential privacy (approximate DP) if we assume that the distribution is symmetric.



1
Introduction

While the goal of statistical inference and machine learning is to learn about a population, most statistical and learning algorithms reveal information specific to their sample, raising concerns about the
privacy
of the individuals contributing their data.
Differential privacy
(DP)
[
43
]
has emerged as the standard framework for addressing privacy concerns. Informally, a differentially private algorithm guarantees that no attacker can infer much more about an individual in the sample than they could have inferred if that person’s data was never collected. There is a rich literature of differentially private algorithms for various statistical inference and machine learning tasks, many of which are now deployed.

Adding the constraint of differential privacy to a statistical inference or machine learning task can, and often does, incur an inherent cost
[
27
,
46
,
75
,
66
]
, and there has been a large body of work pinning down these costs for a variety of tasks. The costs are typically studied via the two-way tradeoff between privacy and error, as measured by some loss function. However, in many applications, we have multiple desiderata for the estimator, not all of which can be captured by a single loss function.

In this work, we study the
statistical bias
of differentially private mean estimators, which adds an extra dimension to the tradeoff betweeen privacy and error. More precisely, given
n
independent samples
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}\in\mathbb{R}
from an unknown univariate distribution
P
, we estimate the mean
\mu(P)\coloneqq\mathbb{E}_{X\leftarrow P}[X]
, subject to the constraint that the estimator
\hat{\mu}
be differentially private. Without a privacy constraint, the empirical mean
\bar{X}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}
is both unbiased and provides optimal error bounds for all the settings we consider. Research on private mean estimation has also pinned down the optimal mean squared error (MSE)
\mathbb{E}[(\hat{\mu}(X)-\mu(P))^{2}]
for a variety of families of distributions, such as sub-Gaussian distributions
[
75
,
25
]
and distributions satisfying bounded moment conditions
[
14
,
73
]
. Unfortunately, these estimators can be very biased. Estimators with little or no bias are desirable because error due to variance can be averaged out by combining multiple estimates, whereas error due to bias can be difficult to eliminate.

Contributions

Our main contribution is to show that privacy inherently leads to statistical bias, by establishing a

Furthermore, Theorem

We also identify

Implicitly, all of our results apply to datasets of fixed, publicly known size. An extension of our results to setting where the dataset size is itself private can be found in Appendix

1.3
Overview of Results

There are a variety of methods for private mean estimation, all of which introduce bias. To understand the source of bias, it is useful to review one common approach – the
noisy clipped mean

M(X)
, which we define as follows. First, it clips the samples to some bounded range
[a,b]
, defined by

\mathsf{clip}_{[a,b]}(x)\coloneqq\min\{\max\{x,a\},b\}.

Next, it computes the empirical mean of the clipped samples
\hat{\mu}_{[a,b]}(X)\coloneqq\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathsf{clip}_{[a,b]}(X_{i})
. Finally, it perturbs the clipped mean with random noise whose variance is calibrated to the
sensitivity
of the clipped mean – i.e., the width of the clipping interval. Specifically, to ensure
\varepsilon

\varepsilon

M(X):=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathsf{clip}_{[a,b]}(X_{i})+\mathsf{Lap}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{b-a}{\varepsilon n}}}\right),

where

denotes the Laplace distribution, which has mean

and variance

Since the Laplace distribution has mean

\mathbb{E}[M(X)]=\mathbb{E}[\mathsf{clip}_{[a,b]}(X_{i})]

Can we obtain unbiased private estimators by making stronger assumptions on the distribution?
Our subsequent results show that the answer depends on what assumptions we are willing to make. Namely, we show that a generalization of the lower bound in Theorem
1.2

Generalization to Higher Moment Bounds.
If the distribution
P
has bounded interval support, then unbiased private mean estimation is possible as clipping to this support has no effect.
More generally, if
P
is more tightly concentrated, then we can clip more aggressively and obtain better bias-accuracy-privacy tradeoffs for the noisy clipped mean.

We consider the class of distributions that satisfy the stronger assumption
{{\mathbb{E}}_{X\leftarrow P}[|X-\mu|^{\lambda}]}\leq 1

\lambda>2

For bias
\beta

{\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[(M(X)-\mu(P))^{2}}}\right]}\leq O\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{n}+\beta^{2}+\frac{1}{n^{2}\cdot\varepsilon^{2}\cdot\beta^{2/(\lambda-1)}}}}\right).

Note that, although we can achieve a lower MSE for the same bias, this tradeoff is still qualitatively similar to the case of Theorem
1.2
in that estimators with optimal MSE must have large bias.
We prove an analogue of Theorem
1.2
showing that this tradeoff is tight for this class of distributions, for every
\lambda>2

1.4
Our Techniques

We provide two different methods for proving lower bounds on the MSE of low-bias private estimators, such as Theorem
1.2
and its generalization to higher moments. While this is redundant, we hope that offering multiple perspectives can provide deeper insight.

Negative Results via the Fingerprinting Method (Theorem
1.2
, §
2
).

The fingerprinting method
[
27
,
46
]
(alternatively called “tracing attacks” or “membership-inference attacks”) is the main approach for proving optimal lower bounds on the error of differentially private estimation. Our proof of Theorem
1.2
is based on a refinement of this method that separately accounts for the bias and mean squared error of the estimator, and thus allows for us to prove tradeoffs between these two parameters.

To give intuition for the argument, we consider the case where
M
is an
unbiased
estimator, in which case the argument is similar to the proof of the Cramér-Rao bound. Assume that we have a suitable family of distributions
P_{\mu}
with mean
{{\mathbb{E}}_{X\leftarrow P_{\mu}}[X]}=\mu
and an unbiased estimator
M
such that
{{\mathbb{E}}_{X\leftarrow P_{\mu}^{n}}[M(X)]}=\mu
. As in the proof of the Cramér-Rao bound, we take the derivative of the unbiasedness constraint, which gives

1=\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\mu}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{\underset{X\leftarrow P_{\mu}^{n},M}{\mathbb{E}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[M(X)}}\right]}}}\right]=\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\underset{X\leftarrow P_{\mu}^{n},M}{\mathbb{E}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[M(X)\cdot\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\mu}\log P_{\mu}(X_{i})}}\right]},

where
P_{\mu}(x)
denotes the probability mass or density function of
P_{\mu}
evaluated at
x
.
The
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-differential privacy guarantee of
M
says that
M(X)
and
X_{i}
are close to being independent where
\varepsilon
and
\delta
quantify the distance from independence. Moreover, a straightforward calculation shows that
{{\mathbb{E}}_{X\leftarrow P_{\mu}}[\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\mu}\log P_{\mu}(X)]}=0
. Thus, for all
i\in[n]
, we have

{\underset{X\leftarrow P_{\mu}^{n},M}{\mathbb{E}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[M(X)\cdot\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\mu}\log P_{\mu}(X_{i})}}\right]}\approx_{\varepsilon,\delta}{\underset{X\leftarrow P_{\mu}^{n},M}{\mathbb{E}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[M(X)}}\right]}\cdot{\underset{X\leftarrow P_{\mu}^{n}}{\mathbb{E}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\mu}\log P_{\mu}(X_{i})}}\right]}=0.

Intuitively, this leads to the contradiction

1=\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\underset{X\leftarrow P_{\mu}^{n},M}{\mathbb{E}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[M(X)\cdot\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\mu}\log P_{\mu}(X_{i})}}\right]}\approx_{\varepsilon,\delta}\sum_{i=1}^{n}0.

To make this argument precise, we must exactly quantify the approximation
\approx_{\varepsilon,\delta}
, which depends both on the privacy parameters
\varepsilon
and
\delta
, as well as on the variances of
M(X)
and of
\smash{\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\mu}\log P_{\mu}(X_{i})}
. The variance of
M(X)
is the quantity that we are trying to bound. The variance of
\smash{\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\mu}\log P_{\mu}(X_{i})}
(which is known as the Fisher information) is something we control by choosing the distribution

to be a distribution supported on two points.

The above proof sketch applies to the unbiased case (
\beta=0
). The general case (
\beta>0
) introduces some additional complications to the proof. In particular, we cannot simply consider a single fixed value of the mean parameter
\mu
, as we must rule out the pathological algorithm that ignores its input sample and outputs
\mu
, which has somehow been hardcoded into the algorithm. This pathological algorithm trivially satisfies privacy and is unbiased for the single distribution
P_{\mu}
. To rule out this algorithm, we consider a distribution over the parameter
\mu
and average over this distribution, where the distribution’s support is wider than the allowable bias
\beta
. While we can no longer assume that

1=\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\mu}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{\underset{X\leftarrow P_{\mu}^{n},M}{\mathbb{E}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[M(X)}}\right]}}}\right],

we can still argue that the derivative must be
\geq\Omega(1)

on average
over the choice of
\mu
.

Negative Results via Amplification (Theorem
1.2
Revisited, §
2.2
).

We present an alternative approach for proving MSE lower bounds on low-bias private estimators. While this approach gives slightly weaker bounds than Theorem
1.2

This method is a proof by contradiction: We start by assuming the existence of a private estimator and we show that running such an algorithm on independent datasets and averaging the results would violate existing lower bounds on the mean squared error of private mean estimators

We start with an

To complete the proof, we can apply

The estimator we construct in the proof of Theorem
1.3
combines two well-known techniques: the noisy-clipped-mean that we already discussed and the so-called name-and-shame algorithm. As discussed above, the noisy-clipped-mean satisfies pure DP, but it leads to a tradeoff between bias and accuracy.

Name-and-shame is a pathological algorithm, which with probability
\delta n
, outputs one random sample in the dataset without any privacy protection, and otherwise outputs nothing. This algorithm satisfies
(0,\delta)
-DP and can be used as the basis for an unbiased estimator. Specifically, given a sample
X\leftarrow P^{n}
, with probability
\delta n
, output
X_{I}/\delta n
for a uniformly random
I\in[n]
, and otherwise output
0
. This estimator is unbiased, but the variance scales with
1/\delta n
, which is impractically large for reasonable values of
\delta
.

Intuitively, our estimator breaks the distribution into the tail portion (far from the mean) and the head portion (close to the mean), and uses the noisy-clipped mean on the head and then uses name-and-shame on the tail to correct the bias. That is, we estimate
\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathsf{clip}_{[a,b]}(X_{i})
with noise addition. Then we estimate the tail
\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(X_{i}-\mathsf{clip}_{[a,b]}(X_{i})))
using name-and-shame. Combining these two estimates yields an unbiased estimator, such that the variance of name-and-shame is greatly reduced because
X_{i}-\mathsf{clip}_{[a,b]}(X_{i})=0
with high probability.

Unbiased Mean Estimation for Symmetric Distributions (Theorem
1.4
, §
4
).

Our unbiased private estimator for symmetric distributions is based on the estimator of
[
75
]
, with some modifications to ensure unbiasedness, so we begin by reviewing the key ideas in their estimator. Their estimator has three steps: First, obtain a coarse estimate
\widetilde{\mu}
. Second, use this coarse estimate to compute a clipped mean
\widehat{\mu}\coloneqq\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathsf{clip}_{[\widetilde{\mu}-c,\widetilde{\mu}+c]}(X_{i})
. Finally, add noise to this fine estimate
\widehat{\mu}
, so
M(X)=\widehat{\mu}+\mathsf{Lap}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{2c}{n\varepsilon}}}\right)
.

The coarse estimate only needs to satisfy a minimal accuracy guarantee; roughly, we require
|\widetilde{\mu}-\mu(P)|\leq O(\sigma)
with high probability, where
\sigma^{2}
is the variance of
P
. We can compute such an estimate using a histogram where we split the real line into intervals of length
O(\sigma)
and pick an interval that contains many samples. With high probability, there is at least one interval containing many samples and the midpoint of any such interval is a good coarse estimate. We can privately select such an interval with high probability – even though there are infinitely many intervals to choose from – using standard techniques in DP
[
65
,
97
]
.

Intuitively, we modify the algorithm of Karwa and Vadhan so that the symmetry of the distribution
P
is preserved in our estimates
\widetilde{\mu}
and
\widehat{\mu}
of the mean. The only part of the algorithm that breaks the symmetry is when we split the real line into intervals. We fix this by adding a random offset to the intervals. That is, up to scaling, our intervals are of the form
\{[\ell+T,\ell+1+T):\ell\in\mathbb{Z}\}
, where
T\in[-1/2,+1/2)
is a uniformly random offset. Note that the distribution of this set is equivariant under translation, so we can proceed with the analysis as if we had translated it to be symmetric around the unknown mean.

The key observation for the analysis is that, if the coarse estimate
\widetilde{\mu}
has a symmetric distribution with center
\mu
, then the clipping does not introduce bias. This is because the clipping is equally likely to introduce positive bias or negative bias and this averages out. There are two additional technicalities in the algorithm: First, we must ensure that the coarse estimate
\widetilde{\mu}
is independent by using independent samples for the two stages. Second, the coarse estimation procedure may fail to produce an estimate
\widetilde{\mu}
because no interval contains enough samples. In this case, we fall back on a version of the name-and-shame algorithm that is unbiased but has high variance
O(\mu^{2}/\delta)
. Since the probabiltiy of needing this fallback will be
O(\delta^{2})
, this case does not contribute much to the overall variance.

Negative Result for Pure DP Unbiased Mean Estimation (Theorem
1.5
, §
5
).

Suppose, for the sake of contradiction, that
M
is an
\varepsilon
-DP unbiased estimator for
\mu\in(a,b)
given samples from
\mathcal{N}(\mu,1)
.
Consider the function

g(\mu)\coloneqq{\underset{X\leftarrow\mathcal{N}(\mu,1)^{n},M}{\mathbb{E}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[M(X)}}\right]}.

We first observe that, if
M
is unbiased for
\mu\in(a,b)
, then it must be unbiased for all
\mu\in\mathbb{R}
.
Intuitively, this holds because of the smoothing induced by the Gaussian distribution – if
M(X)
were biased when the data is drawn from
\mathcal{N}(\mu^{*},1)
for some
\mu^{*}
, then the same data could occur under any other distribution
\mathcal{N}(\mu,1)
with some tiny but non-zero probability, and thus
M(X)
would also be biased for
\mu\in(a,b)
, as well.
Formally, we show
g
is analytic. Thus, its value on the whole real line can be determined from the interval
(a,b)
.

Second we observe that, if
M(X)
has bounded mean absolute deviation
{{\mathbb{E}}_{X\leftarrow\mathcal{N}(\mu^{*},1)^{n}}[|M(X)-\mu^{*}|]}
for some
\mu^{*}
, then
{\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[|M(x)|}}\right]}
must be uniformly bounded for all inputs
x
. Namely,

|g(\mu)|\leq{\underset{X\leftarrow\mathcal{N}(\mu,1)^{n}}{\mathbb{E}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[|M(X)|}}\right]}\leq|\mu^{*}|+{\underset{X\leftarrow\mathcal{N}(\mu,1)^{n}}{\mathbb{E}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[|M(X)-\mu^{*}|}}\right]}\leq|\mu^{*}|+\exp(\varepsilon n){\underset{X\leftarrow\mathcal{N}(\mu^{*},1)^{n}}{\mathbb{E}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[|M(X)-\mu^{*}|}}\right]}

for all
\mu
and some fixed
\mu^{*}
that does not depend on
\mu
. This follows from the strong
group privacy
property of
(\varepsilon,0
)-DP algorithms – changing the entire sample
X\leftarrow\mathcal{N}(\mu^{*},1)^{n}
to any other sample
x
can only change the distribution of
M(X)
by a pointwise multiplicative factor of
\exp(\varepsilon n)
.

The first observation shows that
g(\mu)=\mu
on the entire real line
\mu\in\mathbb{R}
, and the second observation shows that
g(\mu)
is uniformly bounded on the entire real line. This is a contradiction.

This result extends beyond the Gaussian distribution to exponential families. Furthermore, it extends from pure
(\varepsilon,0)
-DP to concentrated DP
[
45
,
24
]
and other variants of differential privacy that satisfy a strong group privacy property. However, it does not apply to approximate
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-DP because the group privacy guarantee underpinning the second observation breaks when
\delta>0
.

We now prove our main negative result, which is to show that this tradeoff is inherent and not unique to clip-and-noise. Informally, we show that if an algorithm is differentially private and has low bias, then it must have high error. There are, of course, other parameters that arise in the analysis, such as bounds on the tails of the unknown distribution
P
.

We provide two different proofs, which give slightly different results. The first proof directly applies the fingerprinting technique for lower bounds on differentially private estimation
[
27
]
, while the second proof is a “black-box” reduction. The black-box reduction yields a slightly weaker result, though the proof technique may be of more general interest.

2.1
Negative Result via Fingerprinting

We begin by stating our general result and, before giving the proof, we provide some remarks and corollaries to help interpret the result.

Theorem 2.1

(Bias-Accuracy-Privacy Tradeoff)
.

Let
\varepsilon,\delta,\beta,\alpha,\tau\geq 0
and
\lambda>1
.
Let
M:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
be an
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-DP algorithm that satisfies the following bias and accuracy properties.
For any distribution
P
on
\mathbb{R}
with
\mu(P)\coloneqq{\underset{X\leftarrow P}{\mathbb{E}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[X}}\right]}\in[0,1]
and
{\underset{X\leftarrow P}{\mathbb{E}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[|X-\mu(P)|^{\lambda}}}\right]}\leq 1
, we have the following:

\displaystyle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|{\underset{X\leftarrow P^{n},M}{\mathbb{E}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[M(X)}}\right]}-\mu(P)}}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\beta,

\displaystyle{\underset{X\leftarrow P^{n},M}{\mathbb{E}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|M(X)-\mu(P)}}\right|}}\right]}

\displaystyle\leq\alpha,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\min\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\delta,{\underset{X\leftarrow P^{n},M}{\mathbb{P}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[|M(X)-\mu(P)|>x}}\right]}}}\right\}~\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\leq\alpha\cdot\tau.

If
16\beta\leq\gamma\leq 1/5
, then

\alpha\geq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(32n\cdot\sinh(\varepsilon)\cdot\gamma^{1/(\lambda-1)}+16n\cdot\tau\cdot\gamma^{-1}}}\right)^{-1}
.

Note that, for small values of
\varepsilon
,
\sinh(\varepsilon)\approx\varepsilon
, but, for large
\varepsilon
,
\sinh(\varepsilon)\approx\frac{1}{2}\exp(\varepsilon)
.
Since this is the “usual” dependence on
\varepsilon
in many such bounds under the constraint of DP,
\sinh
allows us to capture behaviour in both regimes with a single function.

The first two accuracy conditions are not hard to interpret: The parameter
\beta
bounds the bias of the algorithm, while
\alpha
bounds the mean absolute deviation. By Jensen’s inequality,

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|{\underset{X\leftarrow P^{n},M}{\mathbb{E}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[M(X)}}\right]}-\mu(P)}}\right|\leq{\underset{X\leftarrow P^{n},M}{\mathbb{E}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|M(X)-\mu(P)}}\right|}}\right]}\leq\sqrt{{\underset{X\leftarrow P^{n},M}{\mathbb{E}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(M(X)-\mu(P)}}\right)^{2}}}\right]}}.

Thus, we can assume that
\beta\leq\alpha
.
Furthermore, if an estimator
M(X)
has a bound on the mean squared error of
\alpha^{2}
, it consequently also has a mean absolute error of at most
\alpha
.
Thus, our somewhat unconventional assumption controlling the mean absolute error only broadens the class of estimators against which our lower bound holds: for interpretability, one could instead replace this with
\alpha^{2}
being the mean squared error of
M(X)
.

The third property and the parameter
\tau
is somewhat harder to interpret.
We note that this condition is implied by a bound on the MSE of the estimator via the following lemma.
It applies in more general circumstances as well, when we may have bounds on higher or lower moments of the estimator’s error.

Lemma 2.2

(Setting
\tau=\delta^{1-1/\kappa}
in Theorem
2.1
)
.

Let
\alpha,\delta\geq 0
and
\kappa>1
.
Let

be a random variable satisfying
{\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|Y}}\right|^{\kappa}}}\right]}\leq\alpha^{\kappa}
.
Then
\int_{0}^{\infty}\min\{\delta,{\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[|Y|>x}}\right]}\}~\mathrm{d}x\leq\alpha\cdot\delta^{1-1/\kappa}
.

2.2
.

We assume, without loss of generality, that
\delta<1
.
Suppose, for now, there exists

such that
{\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[|Y|>c}}\right]}=\delta
.
If

, then
\min\{\delta,{\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[|Y|>x}}\right]}\}={\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[|Y|>x}}\right]}={\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[|Y|\cdot\mathbb{I}[|Y|>c]>x}}\right]}
.
Likewise, if

, then
\min\{\delta,{\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[|Y|>x}}\right]}\}=\delta={\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[|Y|\cdot\mathbb{I}[|Y|>c]>x}}\right]}
.
Thus,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\min\{\delta,{\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[|Y|>x}}\right]}\}\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}{\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[|Y|\cdot\mathbb{I}[|Y|>c]>x}}\right]}

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[|Y|\cdot\mathbb{I}[|Y|>c]}}\right]}

\displaystyle\leq{\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[|Y|^{\kappa}}}\right]}^{\frac{1}{\kappa}}\cdot{\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathbb{I}[|Y|>c]^{\frac{\kappa}{\kappa-1}}}}\right]}^{\frac{\kappa-1}{\kappa}}

.
∎

In general, Theorem

\gamma=\mathsf{clip}_{[16\beta,1/5]}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{(\lambda-1)\tau}{2\sinh(\varepsilon)}}}\right)^{1-1/\lambda}}}\right).

2.7
) and post-processing (Lemma
2.9
), imply that
A_{m}\circ\Pi
is
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\varepsilon^{\prime}\coloneqq O(\varepsilon\sqrt{\log(1/\delta_{1})/m}),\delta^{\prime}\coloneqq\delta_{1}+O(\delta m)}}\right)
-DP for all
\delta_{1}\in[2\exp(-\frac{m}{16\exp(\varepsilon)}),1]
.

Now, we apply Theorem
2.6

[
73
, Theorem 3.8]
to
A_{m}
:
There exists a distribution
P
with mean and variance bounded by
1
, such that

{\underset{(X_{1},\cdots,X_{m})\leftarrow(P^{n})^{m},A_{m}}{\mathbb{E}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(A_{m}(X_{1},\cdots,X_{m})-\mu(P)}}\right)^{2}}}\right]}\geq\Omega\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{nm(\varepsilon^{\prime}+\delta^{\prime})}}}\right).

Combining all these inequalities gives

\displaystyle\beta^{2}+\frac{\alpha^{2}}{m}

\displaystyle\geq{\underset{(X_{1},\cdots,X_{m})\leftarrow(P^{n})^{m}}{\mathbb{E}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(A_{m}(X_{1},\cdots,X_{m})-\mu(P)}}\right)^{2}}}\right]}

\displaystyle\geq\Omega\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{nm(\varepsilon^{\prime}+\delta^{\prime})}}}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\Omega\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{nm(\varepsilon\sqrt{\log(1/\delta_{1})/m}+\delta_{1}+\delta m)}}}\right).

This rearranges to

\alpha^{2}\geq\Omega\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{n\varepsilon\sqrt{\log(1/\delta_{1})/m}+n\delta_{1}+nm\delta}}}\right)-m\beta^{2}.

It only remains to set
m\in\mathbb{N}
and
\delta_{1}\in[2\exp(-\frac{m}{16\exp(\varepsilon)}),1]
to maximize this lower bound.

Now, we assume that
\delta_{1}\leq O(\varepsilon/\sqrt{m})
and
\delta\leq O(\varepsilon/m^{3/2})
. Then the first term in the denominator dominates and we have

\alpha^{2}\geq\Omega\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{n\varepsilon\sqrt{\log(1/\delta_{1})/m}}}}\right)-m\beta^{2}.

Then setting
m=\Theta\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{n^{2}\varepsilon^{2}\beta^{4}\log(1/\delta_{1})}}}\right)
optimizes the expression giving

\alpha^{2}\geq\Omega\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{n^{2}\varepsilon^{2}\beta^{2}\log(1/\delta_{1})}}}\right).

We set
\delta_{1}=n\varepsilon^{2}\beta^{2}\leq O(\varepsilon/\sqrt{m})
. This satisfies
\delta_{1}\in[2\exp(-\frac{m}{16\exp(\varepsilon)}),1]
as long as
\beta^{2}\leq 1/n\varepsilon^{2}
and
m=\Theta\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\tfrac{1}{n^{2}\varepsilon^{2}\beta^{4}\log(1/n\varepsilon^{2}\beta^{2})}}}\right)\geq O(\exp(\varepsilon)\log(1/n\varepsilon^{2}\beta^{2}))
. The latter constraint rearranges to
\beta^{2}\log(1/n\varepsilon^{2}\beta^{2})\leq\Omega\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\tfrac{\exp(-\varepsilon)}{n\varepsilon}}}\right)
.
To conclude, we note that the assumption
\delta\leq O(\varepsilon/m^{3/2})
is implied by
\delta\leq O(n^{3}\varepsilon^{4}\beta^{6})
.
∎

for

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[(L_{m}\otimes\dots\otimes L_{1}\circ\Pi)(x)\in E}}\right]}

\displaystyle={\underset{\Pi_{-1}}{\mathbb{E}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[(L_{m}\otimes\dots\otimes L_{1}\circ\Pi_{1})(\Pi_{-1}(x))\in E\mid\Pi_{-1}}}\right]}}}\right]}

\displaystyle\leq{\underset{\Pi_{-1}}{\mathbb{E}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\delta^{\prime}+\exp(\varepsilon^{\prime}){\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[(L_{m}\otimes\dots\otimes L_{1}\circ\Pi_{1})(\Pi_{-1}(x^{\prime}))\in E\mid\Pi_{-1}}}\right]}}}\right]}

\displaystyle=\delta^{\prime}+\exp(\varepsilon^{\prime}){\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[(L_{m}\otimes\dots\otimes L_{1}\circ\Pi)(x^{\prime})\in E}}\right]}

∎

3
Low-Bias Estimators for General Distributions

In this section, we describe and analyze algorithms for private estimation with low or no bias.
We give three algorithms: an
(\varepsilon,0)
-DP algorithm based on the clipped mean (Proposition
3.5
), a
(0,\delta)
-DP algorithm based on a variant of the “name-and-shame” algorithm (Proposition
3.6
), and an
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-DP algorithm obtained by combining the two (Proposition
3.7
).
By taking the best of the three resulting bounds, we get Theorem
1.3
.

3.1
Lemmata

We will require the following technical lemmata in our analysis.

Lemma 3.1
.

\forall\lambda>1~\forall c>0~\forall t\in\mathbb{R},~~\max\{0,t-c\}\leq\frac{(\lambda-1)^{\lambda-1}}{\lambda^{\lambda}\cdot c^{\lambda-1}}\cdot|t|^{\lambda}.

Proof.

If
t=0
, the claim holds as an equality. Now, we assume that
t\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
.
Define
f:\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}\to\mathbb{R}
by
f(t)=|t|^{\lambda}
. Then
f^{\prime}(t)=\lambda\cdot|t|^{\lambda-1}\cdot\mathsf{sign}(t)
and
f^{\prime\prime}(t)=\lambda(\lambda-1)\cdot|t|^{\lambda-2}\geq 0
for all
t\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
. Since
f
is convex,

\forall a\geq 0~\forall t\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\},~~f(t)\geq f(a)+f^{\prime}(a)\cdot(t-a)=a^{\lambda}+\lambda\cdot a^{\lambda-1}\cdot(t-a)=\lambda\cdot a^{\lambda-1}\cdot\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t-\frac{\lambda-1}{\lambda}\cdot a}}\right).

Taking the maximum over
a=0
and
a=\frac{c\lambda}{\lambda-1}
and rearranging yields the result.
∎

The following lemma decomposes the mean squared error of the clipped mean into the sum of the sampling error and the (squared) population bias introduced (which is further bounded).

Lemma 3.2
.

Fix
\lambda>1
and
a<b
.
Let
P
be a distribution with mean
\mu(P)\in(a,b)
.
Let
\mu_{[a,b]}(P)\coloneqq{\underset{X\leftarrow P}{\mathbb{E}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathsf{clip}_{[a,b]}(X)}}\right]}\in[a,b]
.
Let
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
be independent samples from
P
.
Then

{\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i}^{n}\mathsf{clip}_{[a,b]}(X_{i})-\mu(P)}}\right)^{2}}}\right]}\leq\frac{{\underset{X\leftarrow P}{\mathbb{E}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[(X-\mu(P))^{2}}}\right]}}{n}+(\mu_{[a,b]}(P)-\mu(P))^{2}

and

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\mu_{[a,b]}(P)-\mu(P)}}\right|\leq\frac{(\lambda-1)^{\lambda-1}}{\lambda^{\lambda}}\cdot\frac{{\underset{X\leftarrow P}{\mathbb{E}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[|X-\mu(P)|^{\lambda}}}\right]}}{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\min\{\mu(P)-a,b-\mu(P)\}}}\right)^{\lambda-1}}\leq\frac{1}{\lambda}\cdot\frac{{\underset{X\leftarrow P}{\mathbb{E}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[|X-\mu(P)|^{\lambda}}}\right]}}{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\min\{\mu(P)-a,b-\mu(P)\}}}\right)^{\lambda-1}}.

Proof.

We have

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i}^{n}\mathsf{clip}_{[a,b]}(X_{i})-\mu(P)}}\right)^{2}}}\right]}

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i}^{n}\mathsf{clip}_{[a,b]}(X_{i})-\mu_{[a,b]}(P)}}\right)^{2}}}\right]}+(\mu_{[a,b]}(P)-\mu(P))^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{i}^{n}{\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathsf{clip}_{[a,b]}(X_{i})-\mu_{[a,b]}(P)}}\right)^{2}}}\right]}+(\mu_{[a,b]}(P)-\mu(P))^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{i}^{n}{\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathsf{clip}_{[a,b]}(X_{i})-\mu(P)}}\right)^{2}}}\right]}+(\mu_{[a,b]}(P)-\mu(P))^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{i}^{n}{\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(X_{i}-\mu(P)}}\right)^{2}}}\right]}+(\mu_{[a,b]}(P)-\mu(P))^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{{\underset{X\leftarrow P}{\mathbb{E}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[(X-\mu(P))^{2}}}\right]}}{n}+(\mu_{[a,b]}(P)-\mu(P))^{2}.

The first inequality follows from the fact that
{\underset{X\leftarrow P}{\mathbb{E}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[(X-\mu(P))^{2}}}\right]}=\inf_{u\in\mathbb{R}}{\underset{X\leftarrow P}{\mathbb{E}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[(X-u)^{2}}}\right]}
.
The second inequality follows from the fact that
\mu(P)\in[a,b]
and, hence,
(\mathsf{clip}_{[a,b]}(x)-\mu(P))^{2}\leq(x-\mu(P))^{2}
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
.

It remains to bound
\mu_{[a,b]}(P)-\mu(P)
. We have

\displaystyle\mu_{[a,b]}(P)-\mu(P)

\displaystyle={\underset{X\leftarrow P}{\mathbb{E}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathsf{clip}_{[a,b]}(X)-X}}\right]}

\displaystyle={\underset{X\leftarrow P}{\mathbb{E}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathbb{I}[X>b](b-X)+\mathbb{I}[X<a](a-X)}}\right]}

\displaystyle={\underset{X\leftarrow P}{\mathbb{E}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\max\{a-X,0\}}}\right]}-{\underset{X\leftarrow P}{\mathbb{E}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\max\{X-b,0\}}}\right]}.

By Lemma
3.1
,

\displaystyle 0\leq{\underset{X\leftarrow P}{\mathbb{E}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\max\{X-b,0\}}}\right]}

\displaystyle={\underset{X\leftarrow P}{\mathbb{E}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\max\{(X-\mu(P))-(b-\mu(P)),0\}}}\right]}

\displaystyle\leq{\underset{X\leftarrow P}{\mathbb{E}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\frac{(\lambda-1)^{\lambda-1}}{\lambda^{\lambda}\cdot(b-\mu(P))^{\lambda-1}}\cdot|X-\mu(P)|^{\lambda}}}\right]}.

Similarly,

0\leq{\underset{X\leftarrow P}{\mathbb{E}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\max\{a-X,0\}}}\right]}\leq\frac{(\lambda-1)^{\lambda-1}}{\lambda^{\lambda}\cdot(\mu(P)-a)^{\lambda-1}}\cdot{\underset{X\leftarrow P}{\mathbb{E}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[|X-\mu(P)|^{\lambda}}}\right]}.

Thus,

\frac{(\lambda-1)^{\lambda-1}}{\lambda^{\lambda}\cdot(b-\mu(P))^{\lambda-1}}\cdot{\underset{X\leftarrow P}{\mathbb{E}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[|X-\mu(P)|^{\lambda}}}\right]}\leq\mu_{[a,b]}(P)-\mu(P)\leq\frac{(\lambda-1)^{\lambda-1}}{\lambda^{\lambda}\cdot(\mu(P)-a)^{\lambda-1}}\cdot{\underset{X\leftarrow P}{\mathbb{E}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[|X-\mu(P)|^{\lambda}}}\right]}.

Finally, note that
\frac{(\lambda-1)^{\lambda-1}}{\lambda^{\lambda}}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-\frac{1}{\lambda}}}\right)^{\lambda-1}\cdot\frac{1}{\lambda}\leq\frac{\exp(-1+1/\lambda)}{\lambda}\leq\frac{1}{\lambda}
.
∎

Finally, we will also require standard properties of the well-known Laplace mechanism.

Definition 3.3

(Sensitivity)
.

Let
f:\mathcal{X}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
be a function, its

is

\Delta_{f}\coloneqq\sup_{x\sim x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}^{n}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|f(x)-f(x^{\prime})}}\right|.

Lemma 3.4

(Laplace Mechanism)
.

Let
f:\mathcal{X}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
be a function
with sensitivity
\Delta_{f}
.
Then, denoting by
\mathsf{Lap}(b)
the Laplace distribution with location
0
and scale parameter
b
, the Laplace mechanism

M(x)\coloneqq f(x)+\mathsf{Lap}(\Delta_{f}/\varepsilon)

-DP. Furthermore,

{\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|M(x)-f(x)}}\right|\geq\frac{\Delta_{f}\cdot\log(1/\beta)}{\varepsilon}}}\right]}\leq\beta.

3.2
Algorithms

We first have a positive result based on clipping and adding noise, which satisfies pure DP.
The clipped and noised mean is folklore in differential privacy.
Analyzing such a procedure with bounded moments has been done in a few works
[
41
,
14
,
73
]
.
These works generally set algorithm parameters to achieve a prescribed bias, towards the goal of minimizing the overall error.
As our goal is to explicitly quantify the bias, we leave it as a free variable.

Proposition 3.5

(
\varepsilon
-DP Algorithm)
.

Fix any
\varepsilon,\beta>0
,
a<b
,
\lambda\geq 2
, and
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
Consider the following
\varepsilon
-DP mechanism
M:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
:

M(x)\coloneqq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i}^{n}\mathsf{clip}_{[\hat{a},\hat{b}]}(x_{i})}}\right)+\mathsf{Lap}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\hat{b}-\hat{a}}{\varepsilon n}}}\right),

where
\hat{a}\coloneqq a-\beta^{-1/(\lambda-1)}
and
\hat{b}\coloneqq b+\beta^{-1/(\lambda-1)}
.
Then, for any distribution
P
on
\mathbb{R}
with
\mu(P)\coloneqq{\underset{X\leftarrow P}{\mathbb{E}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[X}}\right]}\in[a,b]
and
{\underset{X\leftarrow P}{\mathbb{E}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[|X-\mu(P)|^{\lambda}}}\right]}\leq 1
, we have

\displaystyle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|{\underset{X\leftarrow P^{n},M}{\mathbb{E}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[M(X)}}\right]}-\mu(P)}}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\beta,

\displaystyle{\underset{X\leftarrow P^{n},M}{\mathbb{E}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[(M(X)-\mu(P))^{2}}}\right]}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{n}+\beta^{2}+\frac{2}{\varepsilon^{2}n^{2}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(b-a+\frac{2}{\beta^{1/(\lambda-1)}}}}\right)^{2}.

Proof of Proposition
3.6
.

Since
A
satisfies local
(0,\delta)
-DP,
M
satisfies
(0,\delta)
-DP.
Since
A
is unbiased (i.e.,
\forall x\in\mathbb{R}~~{\underset{A}{\mathbb{E}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[A(x)}}\right]}=x
), so is
M
.
Finally, we calculate the mean squared error:

\displaystyle{\underset{X\leftarrow P^{n},M}{\mathbb{E}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[(M(X)-\mu(P))^{2}}}\right]}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}{\underset{X\leftarrow P,A}{\mathbb{E}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[(A(X)-\mu(P))^{2}}}\right]}

\displaystyle=\frac{{\underset{X\leftarrow P,A}{\mathbb{E}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[A(X)^{2}}}\right]}-\mu(P)^{2}}{n}

\displaystyle=\frac{{\underset{X\leftarrow P}{\mathbb{E}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[0+\delta\cdot(X/\delta)^{2}}}\right]}-\mu(P)^{2}}{n}

\displaystyle=\frac{{\underset{X\leftarrow P}{\mathbb{E}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[X^{2}}}\right]}-\delta\cdot\mu(P)^{2}}{\delta\cdot n}

\displaystyle=\frac{{\underset{X\leftarrow P}{\mathbb{E}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[(X-\mu(P))^{2}}}\right]}+(1-\delta)\cdot\mu(P)^{2}}{\delta\cdot n}.

We have the required result.
∎

Next, we give an algorithm based on the folklore “name-and-shame” procedure, which is
(0,\delta)
-DP.
The name-and-shame procedure is generally phrased as randomly selecting a point from a dataset and outputting it, sans any further privacy protection.
It is commonly used as an illustration of which values of
\delta
are meaningful when it comes to informal uses of the word “privacy”, and not as a serious algorithm.
However, we note that such a procedure gives an exactly unbiased estimate of the mean, which the previous
(\varepsilon,0)
-DP was unable to do.
We thus use it to design an unbiased algorithm for mean estimation, albeit at a high price in the dependence on
\delta
, which we recall is usually chosen to be very small.

Proposition 3.6

(
(0,\delta)
-DP Algorithm)
.

Fix any
\delta\in(0,1]
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
Consider the following
(0,\delta)
-DP algorithm

where where each instantiation of

is independent and

is the randomized algorithm

A(x)\coloneq\begin{cases}0&\textrm{with probability }1-\delta\\
\frac{x}{\delta}&\textrm{with probability }\delta\end{cases}.

M
satisfies the following bias and accuracy properties.
For any distribution
P
on
\mathbb{R}
,

\displaystyle{\underset{X\leftarrow P^{n},M}{\mathbb{E}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[M(X)}}\right]}=\mu(P),

(i.e.,
M
is unbiased)

\displaystyle{\underset{X\leftarrow P^{n},M}{\mathbb{E}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[(M(X)-\mu(P))^{2}}}\right]}=\frac{{\underset{X\leftarrow P}{\mathbb{E}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[(X-\mu(P))^{2}}}\right]}+(1-\delta)\cdot\mu(P)^{2}}{\delta\cdot n}.

Proof of Proposition
3.6
.

Since
A
satisfies local
(0,\delta)
-DP,
M
satisfies
(0,\delta)
-DP.
Since
A
is unbiased (i.e.,
\forall x\in\mathbb{R}~~{\underset{A}{\mathbb{E}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[A(x)}}\right]}=x

M
.
Finally, we calculate the mean squared error:

\displaystyle{\underset{X\leftarrow P^{n},M}{\mathbb{E}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[(M(X)-\mu(P))^{2}}}\right]}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}{\underset{X\leftarrow P,A}{\mathbb{E}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[(A(X)-\mu(P))^{2}}}\right]}

\displaystyle=\frac{{\underset{X\leftarrow P,A}{\mathbb{E}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[A(X)^{2}}}\right]}-\mu(P)^{2}}{n}

\displaystyle=\frac{{\underset{X\leftarrow P}{\mathbb{E}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[0+\delta\cdot(X/\delta)^{2}}}\right]}-\mu(P)^{2}}{n}

\displaystyle=\frac{{\underset{X\leftarrow P}{\mathbb{E}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[X^{2}}}\right]}-\delta\cdot\mu(P)^{2}}{\delta\cdot n}

\displaystyle=\frac{{\underset{X\leftarrow P}{\mathbb{E}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[(X-\mu(P))^{2}}}\right]}+(1-\delta)\cdot\mu(P)^{2}}{\delta\cdot n}.

We have the required result.
∎

We can combine both of these methods into a new algorithm for

Essentially, it decomposes a sample into non-tail and tail components, releasing the former via

4
Unbiased Estimators for Symmetric Distributions

We now present our unbiased private mean
estimation algorithm for
symmetric
distributions
over
\mathbb{R}
that are weakly concentrated, i.e.,
those that have a bounded second moment.

Definition 4.1

(Symmetric Distribution)
.

We say that a distribution
P
on
\mathbb{R}
is
symmetric
if there exists some
\mu(P)\in\mathbb{R}
, such that
\forall x\in\mathbb{R},~{\underset{X\leftarrow P}{\mathbb{P}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[X-\mu(P)\leq x}}\right]}={\underset{X\leftarrow P}{\mathbb{P}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mu(P)-X\leq x}}\right]}
.
The value
\mu(P)
is called the
center
of the distribution
P
.

Note that the center of the distribution is unique and coincides with the mean and the median (whenever these two quantities are well-defined).

Our algorithm is based on the approach of
KV 18
, but with some modifications to ensure unbiasedness. First, we obtain a coarse estimate of the mean, and then we use this coarse estimate to perform clipping to obtain a precise estimate via noise addition.
The key observation is that, if the coarse estimate we use for clipping is unbiased and symmetric (and also independent from the data used in the second step), then the clipping does not introduce bias.
We obtain the coarse estimate via a DP histogram, where each bucket in the histogram is an interval on the real line. To ensure that this is unbiased and symmetric, we simply need to apply a random offset to the bucket intervals.

4.1
Coarse Unbiased Estimation

Our coarse estimator (Algorithm
1
) is similar to that of
[
75
]
.
The key modification to ensure unbiasedness is adding a random offset to the histogram bins.
We define
\mathsf{round}_{\mathbb{Z}}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{Z}
to be the function that rounds real numbers to the nearest integer, i.e., for any
x\in\mathbb{R}
, we have
x\in[\mathsf{round}_{\mathbb{Z}}(x)-1/2,\mathsf{round}_{\mathbb{Z}}(x)+1/2)
.

Input:

Dataset
x=(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Output:

Estimate
\widetilde{\mu}\in\mathbb{R}\cup\{\bot\}
.

Let
T
be uniform on the interval
[-1/2,+1/2]
.

Let
K=\{\mathsf{round}_{\mathbb{Z}}(x_{i}-T):i\in[n]\}\subset\mathbb{Z}
.

For each
k\in K
, sample
\xi_{k}\leftarrow\mathsf{Lap}(2/\varepsilon)
independently.

If
\max_{k\in K}|\{i\in[n]:\mathsf{round}_{\mathbb{Z}}(x_{i}-T)=k\}|+\xi_{k}\leq 2+\frac{2\log(1/\delta)}{\varepsilon}
, output
\bot
.

Otherwise, output
T+\argmax_{k\in K}|\{i\in[n]:\mathsf{round}_{\mathbb{Z}}(x_{i}-T)=k\}|+\xi_{k}
.

Algorithm 1

Unbiased DP Coarse Estimator
\textsc{DPUCoarse}_{\varepsilon,\delta}(x)

First, it is easy to verify that Algorithm

Proposition 4.2
.

We recall the group privacy property of differential privacy, which quantifies the privacy guaranteed by a DP algorithm for a group of individuals within a dataset.

Lemma 4.3

4.2

We will prove that

By post-processing, we can consider an algorithm that outputs more information. Specifically, we can assume that for each

The output distributions on the neighboring inputs are the same except for one

It will be easier to proceed by rewriting Algorithm

•

\displaystyle\widehat{p}_{r}(x+c)=\frac{1}{n}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\{i\in[n]:x_{i}+c\in[r\pm 1/2)\}}}\right|=\frac{1}{n}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\{i\in[n]:x_{i}\in[r-c\pm 1/2)\}}}\right|=\widehat{p}_{r-c}(x),

\displaystyle R(x+c)

\displaystyle=\{r\in\mathbb{Z}+T:\widehat{p}_{r}(x+c)>0\}

\displaystyle=\{r\in\mathbb{Z}+T-c:\widehat{p}_{r}(x)>0\}+c

\displaystyle R^{*}(x+c)

Proof of Proposition
4.7
.

By Lemma
4.5
, we assume, without loss of generality, that
\mu(P)=0
.

Let

Recall, from Algorithm

and that

.

with high probabiliy.

Define

In other words, it suffices for us to show that, with high probability,

or

are large and all other

values are small.

For any

, we have
\mathsf{round}_{\mathbb{Z}}(x-T)\in\{k_{-},k_{+}\}
. Thus

C_{k_{-}}+C_{k_{+}}\geq\sum_{i}^{n}\mathbb{I}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[X_{i}\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(-\frac{1}{2},+\frac{1}{2}}}\right)}}\right].

Due to our assumption that
{\underset{X\leftarrow P}{\mathbb{E}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[(X-\mu(P))^{2}}}\right]}<1/64
, Chebyshev’s inequality yields
{\underset{X\leftarrow P}{\mathbb{P}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[X\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\pm\frac{1}{2}}}\right)}}\right]}\geq\frac{15}{16}
. Furthermore, by Hoeffding’s inequality, we have

{\underset{X\leftarrow P^{n}}{\mathbb{P}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\sum_{i}^{n}\mathbb{I}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[X_{i}\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(-\frac{1}{2},+\frac{1}{2}}}\right)}}\right]\geq\frac{15}{16}n-s}}\right]}\geq 1-\exp(-2s^{2}/n)

for all

.
In particular,

{\underset{X\leftarrow P^{n}}{\mathbb{P}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\sum_{i}^{n}\mathbb{I}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[X_{i}\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(-\frac{1}{2},+\frac{1}{2}}}\right)}}\right]\geq\frac{7}{8}n}}\right]}\geq 1-\exp(-n/128).

This means
{\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[C_{k_{-}}+C_{k_{+}}\geq\frac{7}{8}n}}\right]}\geq 1-\exp(-n/128)
.

(breaking ties arbitrarily). If

, then

, while

for all

. Thus

{\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[C_{k_{*}}\geq\frac{7}{16}n\wedge\max_{k\in K\setminus\{k_{+},k_{-}\}}C_{k}\leq\frac{1}{8}n}}\right]}\geq 1-\exp(-n/128).

The next step is to analyze the noise. For all

and

{\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\xi_{k}\geq r}}\right]}={\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\xi_{k}\leq-r}}\right]}=\frac{1}{2}\exp(-r\varepsilon/2)
. Note that
|K|\leq n
. Setting

and taking a union bound over

{\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\xi_{k_{*}}\geq\frac{-n}{8}\wedge\max_{k\in K\setminus\{k_{+},k_{-}\}}\xi_{k}\leq\frac{n}{8}}}\right]}\geq 1-\frac{n}{2}\exp(-n\varepsilon/16).

{\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[C_{k_{*}}+\xi_{k_{*}}\geq\frac{5}{16}n\wedge\max_{k\in K\setminus\{k_{+},k_{-}\}}C_{k}+\xi_{k}\leq\frac{1}{4}n}}\right]}\geq 1-\exp(-n/128)-\frac{n}{2}\exp(-n\varepsilon/16).

Finally, we bound
{\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathbb{I}[\widetilde{\mu}\neq\bot]\cdot|\widetilde{\mu}-\mu(P)|^{\lambda}}}\right]}
.
Observe that
\widetilde{\mu}=T+k
for some

and

for some

|\widetilde{\mu}-X_{i}|\leq 1/2
for some

and, hence,
|\widetilde{\mu}-\mu(P)|\leq 1/2+\max_{i\in[n]}|X_{i}-\mu(P)|

It follows that

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathbb{I}[\widetilde{\mu}\neq\bot]\cdot|\widetilde{\mu}-\mu(P)|^{\lambda}}}\right]}

\displaystyle\leq{\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{2}+\max_{i\in[n]}|X_{i}-\mu(P)|}}\right)^{\lambda}}}\right]}

\displaystyle\overset{\textrm{(A)}}{\leq}{\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\frac{1}{2}+2^{\lambda-1}\cdot\max_{i\in[n]}|X_{i}-\mu(P)|^{\lambda}}}\right]}

\displaystyle\overset{\textrm{(B)}}{\leq}\frac{1}{2}+2^{\lambda-1}\cdot\sum_{i\in[n]}{\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[|X_{i}-\mu(P)|^{\lambda}}}\right]}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}+n\cdot 2^{\lambda-1}\cdot\psi^{\lambda},

where Inequality A follows from the fact that
\forall p\geq 1~\forall x,y\geq 0,~~(x+y)^{p}\leq(x^{p}+y^{p})\cdot 2^{p-1}
, and Inequality B holds because the maximum among a set of non-negative real numbers should be at most the sum of those numbers.
This completes our proof.
∎

4.2
Final Algorithm

Now, we present our main algorithm (Algorithm
2
) for unbiased mean estimation of symmetric distributions under (approximate) DP.
The idea is simple: invoke our coarse estimator (Algorithm
1
) to get a symmetric, unbiased, rough estimate of the mean privately; then apply the standard clip-average-noise technique.
The second step will not create any new bias because the clipping is performed around a symmetric, unbiased estimate that is independent of the data we are clipping and averaging, and the added noise has mean
0
.
There is an additional hiccup though: the coarse estimator may fail. In this case, we fall back to a
(0,\delta)
-DP algorithm that does not require a coarse estimate, as in Proposition
3.6
.

. Clipping & Scale:

Else

5
Impossibility of Pure DP Unbiased Estimation

In Section
4
, we showed that it is possible to perform unbiased mean estimation for symmetric distributions. However, this result only provides approximate DP (i.e.,
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-DP with
\delta>0
). We now show that this is inherent.
We show that unbiased estimation is impossible under pure DP (i.e.,
(\varepsilon,0)
-DP) for a large class of sufficiently smooth distributions.

For technical expedience, we focus on the case when the data come from an
exponential family
, though the following results can be proved in a more general setting. We refer to the calculations in Subsection
5.1
for the recipe to generalize beyond exponential families. In any case, exponential families include a wide range of common distributions, including Gaussians, exponential distributions, Laplace distributions with fixed mean, and Gamma distributions. A notable exception is the Laplace distribution with unknown mean. It is an open and interesting question to investigate whether our impossibility result applies to mean-estimation on Laplacian data. We do not expect our method to be applicable.

Definition 5.1

(Exponential Family)
.

Let
D\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
,

, and
T\colon D\to\mathbb{R}^{k}
. The exponential family with

and
sufficient statistic

h
and
T
respectively is the collection of probability measures
P_{\eta}
with density

f_{T,h,\eta}(x)=h(x)\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\eta^{\top}T(x)-Z(\eta)}}\right),

where
Z(\eta)=\log\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\int\limits_{S}h(x)\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\eta^{\top}T(x)}}\right)\,dx}}\right)
is called the
log-partition function
of the family. The family is defined over all values
\eta\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
for which
Z(\eta)<\infty
. The set of these values, which we denote
U
, is called the family’s

.

Theorem 5.2

(Impossibility of Pure DP Unbiased Estimation for Exponential Families)
.

Let

be an interval of infinite length, let
\{P_{\eta}:\eta\in U\}
be an exponential family, and let

be any interval of positive length. Then, for any

satisfying
{{\mathbb{E}}_{X\leftarrow P_{\eta}^{n},M}[M(X)]}=\eta

\eta\in I

We remark that the interval over which the algorithm is unbiased must have positive length. It is easy to construct a pathological estimator that is unbiased at a single point

. On the other hand, the theorem implies that there can be no pure DP estimator that gives an unbiased estimate for the mean of

for all
\lambda\in(0,0.1)

The range of natural parameters having infinite length is also essential to our analysis. We emphasize that this is a property of the distribution and not of the algorithm; that is, the algorithm does not need to “know” about

Note that the family of distributions
\{\mathsf{Bernoulli}(p):p\in[0,1]\}

Proving this result requires tools from complex analysis and measure theory, a review of which can be found in Appendices

5.1
Locally Unbiased Estimators Are Globally Unbiased

We first argue that the expectation of the estimator
{{\mathbb{E}}_{X\leftarrow P_{\eta}}[\phi(X)]}
must be an analytic function of the parameter
\eta
.
A function being analytic means that its Taylor series provides an exact representation of the function.

Proposition 5.3

(Analyticity under Exponential Families)
.

Let
\{P_{\eta}:\eta\in U\}
be an exponential family on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
in canonical form (recall Definition
5.1
) and let
\phi:\mathcal{X}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
be any well-defined estimator for
\{P_{\eta}:\eta\in U\}
, i.e.,
{{\mathbb{E}}_{X\leftarrow P_{\eta}}[|\phi(X)|]}
is finite for all
\eta\in U
. Then
g:U\to\mathbb{R}
defined by
g(\eta)\coloneqq{{\mathbb{E}}_{X\leftarrow P_{\eta}}[\phi(X)]}
is an analytic function.

An immediate and important consequence if Proposition
5.3
is that any estimator for the parameter of an exponential family that is locally unbiased is also globally unbiased. That is, if we have an unbiased estimator for a restricted range of parameter values (of nonzero length), then we have an unbiased estimator for the entire range.

Proposition 5.4

(Locally Unbiased Implies Globally Unbiased)
.

Let
\phi:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
be any well-defined estimator for an exponential family
\{P_{\eta}:\eta\in U\}
. Let
I\subseteq U
be an interval of nonzero length. If
{{\mathbb{E}}_{X\leftarrow P_{\eta}}[\phi(X)]}=\eta
for all
\eta\in I
, then
{{\mathbb{E}}_{X\leftarrow P_{\eta}}[\phi(X)]}=\eta
for all
\eta\in U
.

Intuitively, the result holds because if an analytic function is linear in some nontrivial interval, we can compute the Taylor series at an interior point of that interval to deduce that the function is linear globally. In any case, the result follows immediately by applying the identity theorem (Theorem
B.4
) to Proposition
5.3

Now, before we prove Proposition
5.3

is analytic, as well.
∎

5.2
Pure DP Estimators Are Uniformly Bounded

We now show by strong group privacy that, if the expectation of a pure DP estimator is bounded locally, then it is uniformly bounded globally.

Proposition 5.7

(Pure DP Estimators Are Uniformly Bounded)
.

Let
A:\mathcal{X}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
be a randomized algorithm. If
A
is
(\varepsilon,0)
-DP, then for all
x,x^{*}\in\mathcal{X}^{n}
,

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|{\underset{A}{\mathbb{E}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[A(x)}}\right]}}}\right|\leq\exp(\varepsilon n){\underset{A}{\mathbb{E}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|A(x^{*})}}\right|}}\right]}
.

Intuitively, this holds because modifying a single entry of
x
changes the distribution of
A(x)
by at most a factor of
\exp(\varepsilon)
, so modifying all
n
points should result in a change by a factor of at most
\exp(\varepsilon n)
.
We emphasize that the bound on
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|{\underset{A}{\mathbb{E}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[A(x)}}\right]}}}\right|
is
uniform
because the result holds for any
x,x^{*}
.

Proof of Proposition
5.7
.

Recall that
{\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[Y}}\right]}=\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}{\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[Y\geq t}}\right]}\,dt
for any non-negative random variable
Y
.
For any
x\in\mathcal{X}^{n}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|{\underset{A}{\mathbb{E}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[A(x)}}\right]}}}\right|\overset{\mathrm{(a)}}{\leq}{\underset{A}{\mathbb{E}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|A(x)}}\right|}}\right]}=\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}{{\underset{A}{\mathbb{P}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|A(x)}}\right|\geq t}}\right]}\,dt}\overset{\mathrm{(b)}}{\leq}\exp(\varepsilon n)\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}{{\underset{A}{\mathbb{P}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|A(x^{*})}}\right|\geq t}}\right]}\,dt}=\exp(\varepsilon n){\underset{A}{\mathbb{E}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|A(x^{*})}}\right|}}\right]},

where inequalities (a) and (b) follow from Jensen’s inequality and group privacy (Lemma
4.3
), respectively.
∎

Our impossibility result for exponential families now follows by stringing together the tools we have collected so far.

Proof of Theorem
5.2
.

Suppose, for the sake of contradiction, there exist
\varepsilon\geq 0
,
n\geq 0
, and an
\varepsilon
-DP algorithm
M:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
for which
{{\mathbb{E}}_{X\leftarrow P_{\eta}^{n},M}[M(X)]}=\eta
when
\eta\in I
. By Proposition
5.5
,
\{P_{\eta}^{n}:\eta\}
is an exponential family, such that for every
P\in\{P_{\eta}:\eta\}
, the natural parameter of
P^{n}
is the same as that of
P
. Therefore, by Proposition
5.4
, we have
{{\mathbb{E}}_{X\leftarrow P_{\eta}^{n},M}[{M}(X)]}=\eta
for all
\eta\in U
. In particular, since
U
is unbounded,
{{\mathbb{E}}_{X\leftarrow P_{\eta}^{n},M}[{M}(X)]}
must be an unbounded function of
\eta
, which contradicts Proposition
5.7
.
∎

Discussion and Future Work

Comparing our lower and upper bounds gives strong evidence that the bias-accuracy-privacy tradeoff bifurcates around
\delta\approx\beta^{4}\varepsilon^{2}
. In the regime where
\delta
is small relative to the bias, namely
\delta\ll\beta^{4}\varepsilon^{2}
, Theorems
1.2
and
1.3
yield matching bounds and thus the trilemma is tight. However, a technical gap in our results emerges when
\delta\gg\beta^{4}\varepsilon^{2}
, in which case our lower and upper bounds differ by a factor of
\sqrt{n}
. A direction for future work is to close this gap and determine whether crossing the threshold impacts the dependence on
n
.

Another direction is to consider higher-order moment estimation problems such as covariance estimation. We expect a similar tradeoff to hold if light distributional assumptions are imposed. However, just as symmetry enables unbiased private mean estimation, we conjecture that there are mild conditions that lead to unbiased private covariance estimation.

A final direction to consider is the algorithmic implications of our impossibility result (Theorem
5.2
).
The rates of certain tasks, such as stochastic gradient descent, depend on the bias (see, e.g., discussion in
[
67
]
).
Thus it is prudent to investigate implications of our results for privatizing these tasks.

Appendix A
Non-Private Error of Mean Estimation

Given independent samples
X_{1},\cdots,X_{n}\in\mathbb{R}
from an unknown distribution
P
, the empirical mean
\hat{\mu}(X)\coloneqq\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i}^{n}X_{i}
is an unbiased estimator of the distribution mean
\mu(P)\coloneqq{\underset{X\leftarrow P}{\mathbb{E}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[X}}\right]}
and its mean squared error is

\alpha^{2}\coloneqq{\underset{X\leftarrow P^{n}}{\mathbb{E}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\hat{\mu}(X)-\mu(P)}}\right)^{2}}}\right]}=\frac{{\underset{X\leftarrow P}{\mathbb{E}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[(X-\mu(P))^{2}}}\right]}}{n}=O(1/n).

This mean squared error is asymptotically optimal in a minimax sense and is optimal for the univariate Gaussian case
P=\mathcal{N}(\mu,\sigma^{2})
.

We have the following well-known result which shows that the empirical mean is asymptotically optimal even for the simple case of Bernoulli data.

Proposition A.1
.

Let
M:\{0,1\}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
be an estimator satisfying

\forall p\in[0,1]~~{\underset{X\leftarrow\mathsf{Bernoulli}(p)^{n}}{\mathbb{E}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[(M(X)-p)^{2}}}\right]}\leq\alpha^{2}.

Then
\alpha^{2}\geq\frac{1}{6(n+2)}
.

The empirical mean attains MSE
{\underset{X\leftarrow\mathsf{Bernoulli}(p)^{n}}{\mathbb{E}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[(\hat{\mu}(X)-p)^{2}}}\right]}=\frac{p(1-p)}{n}
. However, this is not the minimax optimal estimator of the mean of a Bernoulli distribution, rather it is the biased estimator

\check{\mu}(X)\coloneqq\frac{1}{n+\sqrt{n}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\sqrt{n}}{2}+\sum_{i}^{n}X_{i}}}\right),

which has MSE

{\underset{X\leftarrow\mathsf{Bernoulli}(p)^{n}}{\mathbb{E}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[(\check{\mu}(X)-p)^{2}}}\right]}=\frac{1}{4(\sqrt{n}+1)^{2}}

for all
p\in[0,1]

[
60
]
.

Proof of Proposition
A.1
.

Let
P\in[0,1]
be uniform and, conditioned on
P
, let
X\leftarrow\mathsf{Bernoulli}(P)^{n}
be
n
independent bits, each with conditional expectation
P
. Note that the marginal distribution of
\sum_{i}^{n}X_{i}
is uniform on
\{0,1,\cdots,n\}
.

Given
X=x
, the conditional distribution of
P
is

P|_{X=x}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\mathsf{Beta}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1+\sum_{i}^{n}x_{i},1+\sum_{i}^{n}(1-x_{i})}}\right).

In terms of mean squared error, the best estimator of
P
is simply the mean of this conditional distribution. That is, the function
f:\{0,1\}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
that minimizes
{\underset{P,X}{\mathbb{E}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[(P-f(X))^{2}}}\right]}
is the conditional expectation
f(x)={\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[P\mid X=x}}\right]}
.
Indeed, this is the

of conditional expectation in the general measure theoretic setting.
Consequently, the best possible mean squared error of an estimator of
P
given
X
is the variance of this conditional distribution
P|X
.

The distribution
\mathsf{Beta}(a,b)
has mean
\frac{a}{a+b}
and variance
\frac{ab}{(a+b)^{2}(a+b+1)}
.
Now we have

\displaystyle\alpha^{2}

\displaystyle\geq{\underset{P\leftarrow[0,1],X\leftarrow\mathsf{Bernoulli}(P)^{n}}{\mathbb{E}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[(M(X)-P)^{2}}}\right]}

\displaystyle\geq{\underset{X}{\mathbb{E}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{\underset{P}{\mathbb{E}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({\underset{P}{\mathbb{E}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[P\mid X}}\right]}-P}}\right)^{2}\mid X}}\right]}}}\right]}

\displaystyle={\underset{P\leftarrow[0,1],X\leftarrow\mathsf{Bernoulli}(P)^{n}}{\mathbb{E}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1+\sum_{i}^{n}X_{i}}{2+n}-P}}\right)^{2}}}\right]}

\displaystyle={\underset{P\leftarrow[0,1],X\leftarrow\mathsf{Bernoulli}(P)^{n}}{\mathbb{E}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\frac{(1+\sum_{i}^{n}X_{i})(1+\sum_{i}^{n}(1-X_{i}))}{(n+2)^{2}(n+3)}}}\right]}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n+1}\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{(1+k)(1+n-k)}{(n+2)^{2}(n+3)}=\frac{1}{(n+1)(n+2)^{2}(n+3)}\sum_{k=0}^{n}(1+n)+n\cdot k-k^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(n+1)(n+2)^{2}(n+3)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left((1+n)\cdot(n+1)+n\cdot\frac{n(n+1)}{2}-\frac{n(n+1)(2n+1)}{6}}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{6(n+1)^{2}+3n^{2}(n+1)-n(n+1)(2n+1)}{6(n+1)(n+2)^{2}(n+3)}=\frac{6(n+1)+3n^{2}-n(2n+1)}{6(n+2)^{2}(n+3)}

∎

If we change the distribution of

Appendix B
Background on Complex Analysis

The primary objects of interest in complex analysis are the
holomorphic
functions in the complex plane, namely those functions
f:U\to\mathbb{C}
that are differentiable at every point
z\in U
. Many familiar functions, such as the polynomials, are in fact holomorphic or may be extended to a holomorphic function. Note that when
U=\mathbb{C}
, i.e.,
f
is differentiable on the whole complex plane, we say that
f
is an
entire
function.

A basic result of complex analysis asserts that a function
f:U\to\mathbb{C}
is holomorphic exactly when it is
analytic
, i.e., its Taylor series expansion around any point
z_{0}\in U
converges to
f
in some neighborhood of
z_{0}
. For this reason, holomorphic functions are typically referred to as analytic functions.
We consider analyticity in our work as there exist useful mathematical tools to check when functions are analytic, and even more useful tools for constraining functions that we have established to be analytic.

For our purposes, we define a
closed contour
in a region
D\subseteq\mathbb{C}
to be a continuously differentiable map
\gamma:[0,1]\to D
with
\gamma(0)=\gamma(1)
. Informally, we say that a region in the plane is
simply connected
if it contains no holes. For instance, the disk
\{z\in\mathbb{C}:\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|z}}\right|\leq 3\}
is simply connected, whereas the annulus
\{z\in\mathbb{C}:\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|z}}\right|\in[1,3]\}
is not.

A thorough review of the language of complex analysis with the precise definitions of the above (which are not necessary for the understanding of our application) is outside the scope of this work, so we recommend the textbook by
[
5
]
for a more comprehensive background.

A useful property of analytic functions is that their closed contour integrals vanish in simply connected regions. The following theorem characterises this more formally.

Theorem B.1

(Cauchy’s Theorem)
.

Let
U
be an open, simply connected subset of
\mathbb{C}
and let
f:U\to\mathbb{C}
be analytic. Then, for any closed contour
\gamma
in
U
, we have
\oint\limits_{\gamma}f(z)\,dz=0
.

The converse is true, as well, and is a convenient technique for establishing analyticity.

Theorem B.2

(Morera’s Theorem)
.

Let
U\subseteq\mathbb{C}
be open and let
f:U\to\mathbb{C}
be continuous. Suppose that, for all simply connected
D\subseteq U
and any closed contour
\gamma
in
D
, we have
\oint\limits_{\gamma}f(z)\,dz=0
. Then
f
is analytic.

Next, for functions
f_{1},f_{2}:U\to\mathbb{C}
and any
L\subseteq U
, we write
f_{1}|L\equiv f_{2}|L
, if for all
x\in L
,
f_{1}(x)=f_{2}(x)
. Additionally, we write
f_{1}\equiv f_{2}
, if
f_{1}|U\equiv f_{2}|U
. Finally, we define the
limit points
of a set.

Definition B.3

(Limit Point of a Set)
.

Given a topological space
\mathcal{X}
and
S\subseteq\mathcal{X}
, we say that
x\in\mathcal{X}
is a limit point of
S
, if for every neighbourhood
B\subseteq\mathcal{X}
of
x
(with respect to the topology of
\mathcal{X}
), there exists a point
y\in B
, such that
y\in S
and
y\neq x
.

In other words, a limit point
x
of
S
can be “approximated by points in
S
.”
The main property of analytic functions that we exploit is the fact that any two analytic functions that agree locally must, in fact, agree globally, as we show next.

Theorem B.4

(Identity Theorem)
.

Let
U\subseteq\mathbb{C}
be open, and
f_{1},f_{2}:U\to\mathbb{C}
be analytic. Suppose there is a set
L\subseteq U
with a limit point in
U
, such that
f_{1}|L\equiv f_{2}|L
. Then
f_{1}\equiv f_{2}
.

Background on Measure Theory

Recall that a
measure space
is the combination of a set
\mathcal{X}
with a collection
\Sigma
of subsets of
\mathcal{X}
, which are closed under complement and countable unions, as well as a function
\mu:\Sigma\to[0,\infty]
satisfying
\mu(\emptyset)=0
and
\mu\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\bigcup_{i=1}^{\infty}A_{i}}}\right)=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\mu(A_{i})
for disjoint
A_{1},A_{2},\dots\in\Sigma
. The subsets making up
\Sigma
are called the
measurable subsets
of
\mathcal{X}
and
\mu
is called a
measure
on
\mathcal{X}
. We say that
\mathcal{X}
is
\sigma
-finite
when it can be decomposed as
\mathcal{X}=\bigcup_{i=1}^{\infty}A_{i}
where
A_{1},A_{2},\dots\in\Sigma
are all of finite measure
\mu(A_{i})<\infty
. A function
f:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{C}
is said to be
measurable
if
f^{-1}(U)
is a measurable subset of
\mathcal{X}
for any open
U\subseteq\mathbb{C}
. In this case, we say that
f:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
is
\mu
-integrable
if
\int_{\mathcal{X}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|f}}\right|\,d\mu
, the Lebesgue integral of
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|f}}\right|
with respect to
\mu
, exists and is finite.

Now, in order to apply Morera’s theorem, we will require some standard integral-limit interchange theorems. The first is the dominated convergence theorem, which asserts that pointwise convergence of a sequence of functions may be interchanged with integration, provided that the sequence is uniformly bounded by an integrable function.

Theorem C.1

(Dominated Convergence Theorem)
.

Let
\mathcal{X}
be a measure space. Suppose that
(f_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is a sequence of measurable functions
\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{C}
converging pointwise to some
f
, i.e.,
f_{n}(x)\to f(x)
for all
x\in\mathcal{X}
as
n\to\infty
. Suppose further that there is some measurable
G:\mathcal{X}\to[0,\infty)
such that
\int\limits_{\mathcal{X}}G\,d\mu<\infty
and
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|f_{n}(x)}}\right|\leq G(x)
for all
x\in\mathcal{X}
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Then
f
is integrable such that

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\int\limits_{\mathcal{X}}f_{n}\,d\mu=\int\limits_{\mathcal{X}}f\,d\mu.

Switching the order of integration is a very useful operation that is permitted under fairly general measure-theoretic conditions. We describe it as follows.

Theorem C.2

(Fubini’s Theorem)
.

Let
\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{Y}
be
\sigma
-finite measure spaces and suppose that
f:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}\to\mathbb{R}
is measurable such that

\displaystyle\int\limits_{\mathcal{X}}\int\limits_{\mathcal{Y}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|f(x,y)}}\right|\,dy\,dx<\infty.

Then

\displaystyle\int\limits_{\mathcal{X}}\int\limits_{\mathcal{Y}}f(x,y)\,dy\,dx=\int\limits_{\mathcal{Y}}\int\limits_{\mathcal{X}}f(x,y)\,dx\,dy.

We extend the impossibility result for unbiased estimation under pure DP in Section

But first we briefly introduce concentrated DP.

Concentrated DP is a variant of DP that has particularly nice composition properties. It was introduced by
[
45

[
24
]
(see also
[
89
]

Definition D.1

(Concentrated DP)
.

\forall t>0~~~\mathrm{D}_{t+1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(M(x)\middle\|M(x^{\prime})}}\right)\coloneqq\frac{1}{t}\log\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({\underset{Y\leftarrow M(x)}{\mathbb{E}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{{\underset{M}{\mathbb{P}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[M(x)=Y}}\right]}}{{\underset{M}{\mathbb{P}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[M(x^{\prime})=Y}}\right]}}}}\right)^{t}}}\right]}}}\right)\leq(t+1)\rho.

. The above definition applies when the distributions of

Appendix E
Unknown Dataset Size

Our results consider the setting in which the dataset size
n
is known and does not need to be kept private.
In particular, this means we assume neighboring datsets are the same size (see Definition
1.1
), so that we replace an element, rather than adding or removing an element.
The differential privacy literature often glosses over this distinction, as it is usually not important. But the distinction can matter, such as in privacy amplification by subsampling
[
89
, §6.2]
, and it could also matter when we are concerned about bias.

Our negative results automatically extend to the unknown dataset size setting, as this setting is only more general.
We remark that if we define differential privacy with respect to addition or removal of an element, then this implies differential privacy with respect to replacement. That is, if an algorithm is
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-DP with respect to addition or removal, then it is
(2\varepsilon,(1+\exp{\varepsilon})\delta)
-DP with respect to replacement. This is because one replacement can be achieved by the combination of one removal and one addition and thus we can apply group privacy (Lemma
4.3
). Hence, our negative results apply in the more general setting, up to a small loss in parameters.

If we define differential privacy with respect to replacement, then we can perform a removal by simply replacing the removed element with some default or null value. However, this default value could introduce bias. Thus, our positive results do not automatically extend to the unknown dataset size setting.
Nevertheless, we show that our positive results do extend to the more general setting where the dataset size is unknown and neighboring datasets are allowed to add, remove, or replace elements. Throughout this section, we use our “
\sim
” notation to denote neighboring datasets, but this time, two datasets could be neighboring if they differ on at most one entry via addition or removal or replacement of a point, as opposed to just replacement.

E.1
Generic Reduction from Unknown to Known Dataset Size

We first give a generic reduction that takes an algorithm
M_{n}:\mathcal{X}^{n}\to\mathcal{Y}
that works for a known dataset size
n
and produces an algorithm
M_{*}:\mathcal{X}^{*}\to\mathcal{Y}
that works for unknown dataset size.
9
9

9

Here
\mathcal{X}^{*}=\bigcup_{n=0}^{\infty}\mathcal{X}^{n}
.
The reduction preserves the bias and accuracy properties, but the privacy parameters degrade, and we also sacrifice some of the dataset.

The natural approach to perform such a reduction is to first have
M_{*}
privately obtain an estimate
N
of the value of
n
, and then run
M_{N}
on the dataset.
This is essentially how our method works, but special care is needed to ensure that
0\leq N\leq n
. And we also need to ensure that this does not introduce bias; e.g., even if
N
is an unbiased estimate of
n
, computing
\tfrac{1}{N}\sum_{i}^{n}X_{i}
could be a biased estimate of the mean.

Proposition E.1
.

For each
n\geq n_{0}
, let
M_{n}:\mathcal{X}^{n}\to\mathcal{Y}
be an
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-DP algorithm (with respect to replacement of one element of its input).
Then there exists an algorithm
M_{*}:\mathcal{X}^{*}\to\mathcal{Y}
that is
(2\varepsilon,2\delta)
-DP (with respect to addition, removal, or replacement of one element of its input) and has the following form.
For any input
x\in\mathcal{X}^{n}
of size
n
,
M_{*}(x)=M_{N}(\widehat{x})
, where
N
is random with
n\geq N\geq n-2\lceil\log(2/\delta)/\varepsilon\rceil
and
\widehat{x}\in\mathcal{X}^{N}
is a uniformly random subset of
N
elements of
x
; but, if
N<n_{0}
, then
M_{*}
simply outputs
\bot
.

Recall that, for distributions
P
and
Q
over a set
\mathcal{X}
, we say that
P
and
Q
are
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-indistinguishable (denoted by
P\sim_{\varepsilon,\delta}Q
), if for all measurable

E.2
Pure DP Algorithm for Bounded Distributions

The reduction in the previous subsection inherently requires approximate DP because we use truncated noise.
If we use un-truncated Laplace noise, then there is a non-zero probability that
N>n
or
N<n_{0}
and hence the reduction fails to provide an unbiased estimator.
Thus, it is natural to wonder whether the unknown dataset size setting inherently requires approximate DP to ensure unbiasedness even for bounded distributions. (Recall that Theorem
1.5
shows that approximate DP is necessary to ensure unbiasedness for Gaussian data.) We show that this is not the case.

Instead of a generic reduction like Proposition
E.1
, we extend the noisy clipped mean approach to the unknown dataset size setting in a manner that does not add bias and remains pure DP.
This allows us to extend all our positive results for pure DP to the unknown dataset size setting.

A natural approach would be to estimate
n
and
\sum_{i}^{n}x_{i}
separately, and output the ratio of the estimates. However, the expectation of a ratio is, in general, not the same as the ratio of the expectations; thus, this natural approach can be biased.
Instead, we use the
smooth sensitivity
framework of
[
82
]
to allow us to add unbiased noise directly to
\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i}x_{i}
that scales with
\tfrac{1}{n}
even though this value is unknown.

[
83
, §A]
provide a similar algorithm for this task, but their approach does not satisfy pure DP because it is based on the propose-test-release framework.

We first provide some background on the smooth sensitivity framework, starting with the idea of
local sensitivity
. Unlike global sensitivity, local sensitivity is pertinent to a fixed individual dataset, and is the worst-case change in the value of a function when we add or remove or replace one point in that particular dataset.

Definition E.2

(Local Sensitivity)
.

Next, we define a category of functions that upper bounds the local sensitivity of a function in a

Definition E.3

(

\displaystyle\forall x\in\mathcal{X}^{*},~~~S(x)
