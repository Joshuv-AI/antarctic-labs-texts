Evaluation of Gaussian integrals for the modeling of two-dimensional quantum systems
Øyvind Sigmundson Schøyen, Håkon Emil Kristiansen, Alfred Alocias Mariadason

Abstract
We have developed a McMurchie-Davidson-like recursion formula for efficient evaluation of the Coulomb attraction and interaction matrix elements between two-dimensional primitive Cartesian Gaussian type orbitals. We also present recurrence schemes for combined position and differential operator integrals, and three-center Gaussian integrals. The Cartesian Gaussian orbitals are isotropic in the exponent, but with arbitrary centers and angular momentum.



I
Introduction

Two-dimensional many-particle quantum mechanical systems are routinely
studied both theoretically and experimentally.
Systems such as lateral quantum dots and rings,
Burkard et al. 1999
;
Hu and Das Sarma 2000
;
Nielsen et al. 2010
;
Reimann and Manninen 2002
;
Chwiej and Szafran 2008
;
Yuan et al. 2017
;
Popsueva et al. 2007
and
electrons on liquid helium
Platzman and Dykman 1999
are confined to
planar motion and can be treated as two-dimensional structures.
They are seen as promising candidate models for qubits, and can be used
to build quantum computers.

Solving these systems theoretically requires the use of approximations
for all but the simplest problems.
In particular for the case of multiparticle problems and arbitrary
potential geometries.
A large number of
ab initio
many-body methods are formulated in
terms of a known single-particle basis.
For some problems the eigenstates of the single-particle Hamiltonian
can be used, but these states can make the solution of the interaction
terms more involved.
In three dimensions the usage of Cartesian Gaussian type orbitals have
been the prevalent choice for
ab initio
methods applied to atomic
and molecular systems
Helgaker et al. 2014
.
The main reason for this is that there exists several efficient
algorithms
McMurchie and Davidson 1978
;
Obara and Saika 1986
for the evaluation of matrix
elements between Gaussian type functions and relevant one- and two-body
operators.
In particular, the computationally expensive, non-separable Coulomb
attraction and interaction integrals can be expressed in terms of fast
recursion relations for arbitrary angular momentum values.

However, due to the non-separability of the Coulomb integrals, the
results for three dimensions do not generalize to lower dimensions.
In particular, the computationally most intensive task of computing the
two-body Coulomb interaction integrals are completely different in the
two-dimensional case compared to the three-dimensional one.
A common basis in two dimensions are the polar harmonic oscillator
eigenstates, or the Fock-Darwin states, as the Coulomb interaction
integrals can be solved analytically
Girvin and Jach 1983
;
Anisimovas and Matulis 1998
.
These are well suited for a single potential well located at the origin.
In more complicated geometries with several well-centers, e.g., a double
quantum dot, the quality of this basis rapidly deteriorates.

We propose the use of primitive Cartesian Gaussian type orbitals in
two-dimensions as they are much more flexible in their shape and
placement allowing for a wider range of potential geometries.
In two dimensions the Coulomb integrals between
s
-Gaussians, i.e.,
spherical charge distributions, have a closed-form solution in terms of
a modified Bessel function
Nielsen et al. 2010
.
Previous work has often utilized several
s
-Gaussian orbitals to
create a good enough single-particle basis
Hu and Das Sarma 2000
;
Nielsen et al. 2010
.
Using
p
,
d
, and higher-order Gaussians gives a better
single-particle basis and lowers the number of states needed to build a
sufficiently good representation of the problem.
In this article, we have extended these solutions by introducing
McMurchie-Davidson-like recursion relations for the evaluation of
Coulomb integrals between Gaussian type orbitals of arbitrary angular
momentum.
This creates a general framework for studying two-dimensional systems
using
ab initio
methods.

In
section II
we define the primitive Cartesian Gaussian type
orbitals and their properties.
We also demonstrate how they can be expanded in Hermite-Gaussian
functions and list the overlap integral between two Gaussian orbitals.
We proceed to present recursion relations for the calculation of matrix
elements between two Gaussian orbitals and the combined position and
differential operator

Theory

A two-dimensional primitive unormalized Cartesian Gaussian orbital may

\displaystyle E^{i+1,j}_{t}=\frac{1}{2p}E^{ij}_{t-1}+X_{PA}E^{ij}_{t}+(t+1)E^{ij}_{t+1},

\displaystyle s_{\alpha\beta}=\innerproduct{g_{\alpha}}{g_{\beta}}=\innerproduct{g_{ik}}{g_{jl}}=s_{ij}s_{jl}.

II.1
Combined position and differential operator integral

The second integral we wish to solve using arbitrary primitive
Gaussian functions is the combined position and differential
operator.
We derive this expression for the differential operator instead of
the momentum operator to ensure that all integrals are real.
To ensure full generality we allow for arbitrary centers of the
position operator, and arbitrary exponents for both the position
and differential operator.
The integral we wish to solve is thus

\displaystyle L^{ef}_{ij}

\displaystyle=\matrixelement{g_i(a, A)}{\hat{x}^{e}_C \dv[f]{}{x}}{g_j(b, B)},

(10)

with extensions to two dimensions by taking the product of a
similar integral with
x\to y
.
There are two downward recursion formulas – one for the position
term and another for the differential operator – for this
integral.
They are given by

\displaystyle L^{e+1,f}_{ij}=L^{ef}_{i+1,j}+X_{AC}L^{ef}_{ij},

(11)

\displaystyle L^{e,f+1}_{ij}=jL^{ef}_{i,j-1}-2bL^{ef}_{i,j+1},

(12)

where
X_{AC}\equiv A-C
.
The base case of the recursion is the overlap integral, i.e.,

L^{00}_{ij}=s_{ij}
from
Equation 8
.
See appendix
C
for the derivation – which
is based on the work of
Tellgren et al. 2008
– of these matrix
elements.
As an example, from
Equation 10
we can build the kinetic
operator matrix elements in two dimensions by

\displaystyle T_{\alpha\beta}

\displaystyle=\matrixelement{g_{\alpha}}{
\left(\dv[2]{}{x} + \dv[2]{}{y}\right)
}{g_{\beta}}

\displaystyle=L^{02}_{ij}s_{kl}+s_{ij}L^{02}_{kl},

(13)

where
\alpha=(i,k)
,
\beta=(j,l)
, and we have inserted the
one-dimensional overlap integral between the Cartesian components
that are not dependent on the derivative operator.

II.2
Gaussian well integral

When modeling
n
-well systems it is useful to tune the placement
and shape of the various wells.
One of the easiest potential wells that allow for this flexibility
are Gaussian potential wells.
This results in three-center integrals
Obara and Saika 1986
.
For the sake of full generality we therefore define the
one-dimensional Gaussian potential well to be on the form

\displaystyle\hat{v}_{k}(x;c,C,w)=-wx_{C}^{k}\exp(-c x_C^2),

(14)

that is, the Gaussian well is itself a primitive Gaussian with an
exponent
c>0
and
w>0
giving a negative weight.
Extending to higher dimensions results in a product between two
one-dimensional wells.
The matrix elements for this operator is given by

\displaystyle G^{k}_{ij}

\displaystyle=\matrixelement{g_i(a, A)}{\hat{v}_k}{g_j(b, B)}=-w\sum_{t=0}^{i+j}E^{ij}_{t}P^{t}_{k0},

(15)

where
E^{ij}_{t}
are the Hermite-Gaussian expansion coefficients
between
g_{i}(x;a,A)
and
g_{j}(x;b,B)
, and
P^{t}_{kl}
can be
found from the recurrence scheme

\displaystyle P^{t}_{kl}

\displaystyle=\matrixelement{g_k(c, C)}{\pdv[t]{}{P}}{g_l(p, P)}

\displaystyle=2pP^{t-1}_{k,l+1}-lP^{t-1}_{k,l-1},

(16)

with the base case
P^{0}_{kl}=\innerproduct{g_k(c, C)}{g_l(p, P)}=s_{kl}
, i.e., the overlap integral.
It is worth noting which Gaussians are included in the recurrence
scheme for
P^{t}_{kl}
as this is a two-center integral.
The
g_{l}(x;p,P)
Gaussian is the result from the product of

g_{i}(x;a,A)
and
g_{j}(x;b,B)
with
p
as the total exponent
and
P
the center of mass from the product.
The Gaussian
g_{k}(x;c,C)
is the potential well.
The derivation of this expression can be found in appendix

D
.

II.3
Coulomb attraction integral

Next, we look at the Coulomb attraction integral in two dimensions.
This integral is not separable in the two Cartesian directions and
we have to treat the integral in full.
We wish to compute

\displaystyle v_{\alpha\beta}

\displaystyle=\matrixelement{g_{\alpha}(a, \vb{A})}{
\frac{1}{r_C}
}{g_{\beta}(b, \vb{B})}=\int\differential[2]{\vb{r}}\frac{\Omega_{\alpha\beta}(\mathbf{r})}{r_{C}},

(17)

where
r_{C}\equiv\norm{\vb{r} - \vb{C}}
, and
\alpha=(i,k)

and
\beta=(j,l)
are the compound indices of the
two-dimensional Gaussians.
We have set
e=(4\pi\epsilon_{0})^{-1}=1
to keep the
expressions as clean as possible.
We expand the Gaussian overlap distribution in Hermite-Gaussian
functions and move the derivative operators in front of the
integral.
This gives

\displaystyle v_{\alpha\beta}

\displaystyle=\sum_{tu}E^{\alpha\beta}_{tu}\partialderivative[t]{P_x}\partialderivative[u]{P_y}\int\differential[2]{\vb{r}}\frac{\exp(-p r^{2}_{P})}{r_{C}},

(18)

where
\sum_{tu}\equiv\sum_{t=0}^{i+j}\sum_{u=0}^{k+l}
.
The closed form expression for the two-dimensional integral over the
spherical Gaussian is derived in appendix

E

\displaystyle\int\differential[2]{\vb{r}}\frac{\exp(-p r^{2}_{P})}{r_{C}}=\pi\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{p}}\tilde{i}_{0}\!\left(-p\Sigma^{2}/2\right),

(19)

where
\tilde{i}_{n}(z)\equiv\exp(z)I_{n}(z)
is the

exponentially scaled, modified Bessel function of the first
kind
, and
\mathbf{\Sigma}=\mathbf{P}-\mathbf{C}
.
Inserted back into
Equation 18

\displaystyle v_{\alpha\beta}

\displaystyle=\pi\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{p}}\sum_{tu}E^{\alpha\beta}_{tu}\tilde{I}_{tu}\!\left(-p\Sigma^{2}/2\right),

(20)

where we have defined

\displaystyle\tilde{I}_{tu}\!\left(-p\Sigma^{2}/2\right)

\displaystyle\equiv\partialderivative[t]{P_x}\partialderivative[u]{P_y}\tilde{i}_{0}\!\left(-p\Sigma^{2}/2\right),

(21)

Now the task is to find a recurrence formula for the evaluation of

Equation 21

We therefore introduce the intermediate integrals

\displaystyle\tilde{I}^{n}_{tu}\!\left(-p\Sigma^{2}/2\right)\equiv\partialderivative[t]{P_x}\partialderivative[u]{P_y}\tilde{i}_{n}\!\left(-p\Sigma^{2}/2\right),

(22)

where
\tilde{I}_{tu}(z)\equiv\tilde{I}^{0}_{tu}(z)

\tilde{I}^{n}_{00}(z)\equiv\tilde{i}_{n}(z)

Incrementing

\displaystyle\tilde{I}^{n}_{t+1,u}

\displaystyle=-\frac{p}{2}\Biggl[t\left(\tilde{I}^{n-1}_{t-1,u}+2\tilde{I}^{n}_{t-1,u}+\tilde{I}^{n+1}_{t-1,u}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad+\Sigma_{x}\left(\tilde{I}^{n-1}_{tu}+2\tilde{I}^{n}_{tu}+\tilde{I}^{n+1}_{tu}\right)\Biggr],

(23)

\displaystyle\tilde{I}^{n}_{t,u+1}

\displaystyle=-\frac{p}{2}\Biggl[u\left(\tilde{I}^{n-1}_{t,u-1}+2\tilde{I}^{n}_{t,u-1}+\tilde{I}^{n+1}_{t,u-1}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad+\Sigma_{y}\left(\tilde{I}^{n-1}_{tu}+2\tilde{I}^{n}_{tu}+\tilde{I}^{n+1}_{tu}\right)\Biggr],

(24)

where we have removed the function arguments (they are the same as
in
Equation 20

These expressions apply for

and with
\tilde{i}_{-1}(z)=\tilde{i}_{1}(z)
.
The recursion schemas are derived in

F
.

The Coulomb attraction potential is seldom used in two-dimensional
systems.
They are similar to the Gaussian wells in that they are flexible in
their placement and strength.
We have included it in this article as recursion relations can be
completely reused in the two-body interaction integral.

II.4
Coulomb interaction integral

We are now in a position to set up the Coulomb interaction matrix
elements between two two-dimensional primitive Cartesian Gaussian
functions.
The Coulomb interaction operator is given by

\displaystyle\hat{u}(\mathbf{r}_{1},\mathbf{r}_{2})=\frac{1}{\norm{\vb{r}_1 - \vb{r}_2}},

(25)

where we again have set all physical constants to unity, and we
define
r_{12}\equiv\norm{\vb{r}_1 - \vb{r}_2}
.
The matrix elements are then given by

\displaystyle u^{\alpha\beta}_{\gamma\delta}

\displaystyle\equiv\matrixelement{g_{\alpha}(a, \vb{A}) g_{\beta}(b, \vb{B})}{
\hat{u}
}{g_{\gamma}(c, \vb{C}) g_{\delta}(d, \vb{D})}

\displaystyle=\int\differential[2]{\vb{r}_1}\differential[2]{\vb{r}_2}\frac{\Omega_{\alpha\gamma}(\mathbf{r}_{1})\Omega_{\beta\delta}(\mathbf{r}_{2})}{r_{12}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{tu,\tau\nu}E^{\alpha\gamma}_{tu}E^{\beta\delta}_{\tau\nu}\int\differential[2]{\vb{r}_1}\differential[2]{\vb{r}_2}\frac{\Lambda_{tu}(\mathbf{r}_{1})\Lambda_{\tau\nu}(\mathbf{r}_{2})}{r_{12}}.

(26)

For the integrals over the Hermite-Gaussians (sans the expansion
coefficients) we have

\displaystyle V_{tu;\tau\nu}

\displaystyle=\partialderivative[t]{P_x}\partialderivative[u]{P_y}\partialderivative[\tau]{Q_x}\partialderivative[\nu]{Q_y}V_{0},

(27)

where we have denoted the integral over the two spherical Gaussian
charge distributions by
V_{0}
.
This integral has a closed form solution given by

\displaystyle V_{0}

\displaystyle=\int\differential[2]{\vb{r}_1}\differential[2]{\vb{r}_2}\frac{\exp(-p\vb{r}^2_{1P})\exp(-q\vb{r}^2_{2Q})}{r_{12}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\pi^{2}}{pq}\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{4\sigma}}\tilde{i}_{0}\!\left(-\Delta^{2}/(8\sigma)\right),

(28)

with
\mathbf{r}_{1P}\equiv\mathbf{r}_{1}-\mathbf{P}
and similarly for

\mathbf{r}_{2Q}
,
\sigma=(p+q)/(4pq)
, and
\mathbf{\Delta}=\mathbf{Q}-\mathbf{P}

Nielsen et al. 2010
.
This derivation is demonstrated in appendix

G
.
Except for the function argument and the prefactor, the Coulomb
interaction integral between two spherical Gaussian orbitals is
identical to the Coulomb attraction integral.
To be able to reuse the recursion formula in

Equation 23
we look at the function argument to
the exponentially scaled Bessel function.
Defining
f\equiv-\Delta^{2}/(8\sigma)
for the function argument,
we have that

\displaystyle\partialderivative[]{f}{Q_i}=-\frac{\Delta_{i}}{4\sigma}=-\partialderivative[]{f}{P_i},\quad\partialderivative[2]{f}{Q_i}=\partialderivative[2]{f}{P_i}=-\frac{1}{4\sigma}.

(29)

This lets us write the integral over the two Hermite-Gaussian
functions as

\displaystyle V_{tu;\tau\nu}

\displaystyle=(-1)^{t+u}\frac{\pi^{2}}{pq}\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{4\sigma}}\tilde{I}_{t+\tau,u+\nu}\!\left(-\Delta^{2}/(8\sigma)\right),

(30)

where
\tilde{I}_{t+\tau,u+\nu}
is the same as in

Equation 21
with
P_{i}\to Q_{i}
in the center
derivatives shown in
Equation 22

3
3

3

This is because
\mathbf{\Delta}=\mathbf{Q}-\mathbf{P}
, i.e.,
\mathbf{Q}

is the positive term in the center difference for the Coulomb
interaction.
For the Coulomb attraction integral this definition is reversed
with
\mathbf{\Sigma}=\mathbf{P}-\mathbf{C}
.

.
We therefore find the same recursion formula as in

Equation 23
, but with
p\to(4\sigma)^{-1}
and

\Sigma_{i}\to\Delta_{i}
.
This leaves us with the full expression for the Coulomb interaction
matrix elements

\displaystyle u^{\alpha\beta}_{\gamma\delta}

\displaystyle=\frac{\pi^{2}}{pq}\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{4\sigma}}\sum_{tu}(-1)^{t+u}E^{\alpha\gamma}_{tu}

\displaystyle\quad\times\sum_{\tau\nu}E^{\beta\delta}_{\tau\nu}\tilde{I}_{t+\tau,u+\nu}\!\left(-\Delta^{2}/(8\sigma)\right).

(31)

III
Summary remarks

In this report, we have derived recursion formulas for the evaluation of
atomic integrals using two-dimensional primitive Gaussian orbitals.
The formulas are an extension of the three-dimensional
McMurchie-Davidson method to two-dimensional systems.
These integrals allow for more flexibility in the construction of basis
sets for
ab initio
methods applied to two-dimensional systems,
e.g., lateral quantum dots.

In this paper, we have only been concerned with the technicalities of
computing matrix elements, but studies as to how the Gaussian basis
elements should be chosen, e.g., placement of the centers, width of the
peak, is of interest.
Much can be learned from previous work on contracted primitive Gaussian
orbitals in the three-dimensional case.

Hermite-Gaussian expansion coefficients

\displaystyle=\sum_{t=1}^{i+j+1}\frac{1}{2p}E^{ij}_{t-1}\Lambda_{t}+\sum_{t=-1}^{i+j-1}(t+1)E^{ij}_{t+1}\Lambda_{t}

\displaystyle\qquad+\sum_{t=0}^{i+j}X_{PA}E^{ij}_{t}\Lambda_{t},

\displaystyle\Omega_{i+1,j}

\displaystyle=\sum_{t=0}^{i+j+1}E^{i+1,j}_{t}\Lambda_{t}

\displaystyle=\sum_{t=0}^{i+j+1}\left[\frac{1}{2p}E^{ij}_{t-1}+(t+1)E^{ij}_{t+1}+X_{PA}E^{ij}_{t}\right]\Lambda_{t}.

(38)

Equating terms at each order of Hermite-Gaussian then gives the
recurrence scheme for the expansion coefficients at every step in the
sum.
We are then left with

\displaystyle E^{i+1,j}_{t}=\frac{1}{2p}E^{ij}_{t-1}+X_{PA}E^{ij}_{t}+(t+1)E^{ij}_{t+1},

(39)

\displaystyle E^{i,j+1}_{t}=\frac{1}{2p}E^{ij}_{t-1}+X_{PB}E^{ij}_{t}+(t+1)E^{ij}_{t+1},

(40)

where
E^{00}_{0}=\exp(-\mu X^{2}_{AB})
, and
E^{ij}_{t}=0
for
t<0
and
t>i+j
.

Appendix B
Overlap integral between two Gaussian orbitals

Here we derive the overlap integral – as seen in

Equation 8
– between two one-dimensional
primitive Gaussians.

\displaystyle s_{ij}

\displaystyle=\innerproduct{g_i(a, A)}{g_j(b, B)}=\int\differential{x}g_{i}(x)g_{j}(x)

\displaystyle=\int\differential{x}\Omega_{ij}(x)=\sum_{t=0}^{i+j}E^{ij}_{t}\int\differential{x}\Lambda_{t}(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{t=0}^{i+j}E^{ij}_{t}\partialderivative[t]{P}\int\differential{x}\exp(-p x^2_P)

\displaystyle=\sum_{t=0}^{i+j}E^{ij}_{t}\partialderivative[t]{P}\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{p}}=\sum_{t=0}^{i+j}E^{ij}_{t}\delta_{t0}\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{p}}

\displaystyle=E^{ij}_{0}\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{p}},

(41)

where
p
is the total exponent from the product of the two Gaussian functions.
The overlap between two two-dimensional primitive Gaussians will be a
product of the overlap between each Cartesian direction.
That is,

\displaystyle s_{\alpha\beta}

\displaystyle=\innerproduct{g_{\alpha}}{g_{\beta}}=\innerproduct{g_{ik}}{g_{jl}}=s_{ij}s_{kl}.

(42)

Appendix C
Combined position and differential operator integrals

We derive the recursion formulas for the combined position and
differential operator integrals shown in
Equation 11

and
Equation 12
.
We start by finding a relation for the position term on a single
Gaussian orbital.
That is,

\displaystyle g_{i}(x;a,A)x_{C}

\displaystyle=x^{i}_{A}\exp(-a x^2_A)\left(x-C-A+A\right)

\displaystyle=x^{i}_{A}\exp(-a x^2_A)\left(x_{A}+X_{AC}\right)

\displaystyle=g_{i+1}(x)+X_{AC}g_{i}(x).

(43)

The downward recurrence scheme for the position is then

\displaystyle L^{ef}_{ij}

\displaystyle=\matrixelement*{g_i}{\hat{x}^{e}_{C} \dv[f]{}{x}}{g_j}

\displaystyle=\Bigl[\bra*{g_{i + 1}}+X_{AC}\bra*{g_{i}}\Bigr]\hat{x}^{e-1}_{C}\derivative[f]{x}\!\ket*{g_j}

\displaystyle=L^{e-1,f}_{i+1,j}+X_{AC}L^{e-1,f}_{ij},

(44)

which corresponds to
Equation 11
.
The action of a derivative operator on a primitive one-dimensional
Gaussian orbital is

\displaystyle\derivative[]{x}g_{j}(x;b,B)

\displaystyle=\derivative[]{x_B}\left[x^{i}_{B}\exp(-b x^2_B)\right]

\displaystyle=jg_{j-1}(x)-2bg_{j+1}(x).

(45)

We thus find
Equation 12
from

\displaystyle L^{ef}_{ij}

\displaystyle=\matrixelement*{g_i}{\hat{x}^{e}_{C} \dv[f]{}{x}}{g_j}

\displaystyle=\bra*{g_i}\!\hat{x}^{e}_{C}\derivative[f - 1]{x}\Bigl[j\ket*{g_{j - 1}}-2b\ket*{g_{j + 1}}\Bigr]

\displaystyle=jL^{e,f-1}_{i,j-1}-2bL^{e,f-1}_{i,j+1}.

(46)

Appendix D
Gaussian well integral

The matrix element between two primitive Gaussians over a Gaussian well
is given by

\displaystyle G^{k}_{ij}

\displaystyle=\matrixelement{g_i(a, A)}{\hat{v}_{k}(c, C, w)}{g_j(b, B)}

\displaystyle=-w\int\differential{x}x_{C}^{k}\exp(-cx_{C}^{2})\Omega_{ij}(x)

\displaystyle=-w\sum_{t=0}^{i+j}E^{ij}_{t}\int\differential{x}g_{k}(x)\Lambda_{t}(x)

\displaystyle=-w\sum_{t=0}^{i+j}E^{ij}_{t}\matrixelement{g_k(c, C)}{\pdv[t]{}{P}}{g_0(p, P)}

\displaystyle=-w\sum_{t=0}^{i+j}E^{ij}_{t}P^{t}_{k0}.

(47)

Here it is worth noting that the choice of creating an overlap
distribution between
g_{i}(x;aA)
and
g_{j}(x;b,B)
is somewhat
arbitrary.
This can very well be done between the well and any of the two.
However, this requires some care when manipulating the indices for the
expansion coefficients and the matrix elements
P^{t}_{kl}
.
We observe that
P^{0}_{kl}=\innerproduct{g_k}{g_l}=s_{kl}
.
For
t>0
we get

\displaystyle P^{t}_{kl}

\displaystyle=\matrixelement{g_k(c, C)}{\pdv[t]{}{P}}{g_l(p, P)}

\displaystyle=\bra*{g_k}\!\partialderivative[t - 1]{P}\Bigl[2p\ket*{g_{l + 1}}-l\ket*{g_{l - 1}}\Bigr]

\displaystyle=2pP^{t-1}_{k,l+1}-lP^{t-1}_{k,l-1}.

(48)

Appendix E
Coulomb attraction integral over a spherical Gaussian charge
distribution

We derive a closed-form expression for the Coulomb attraction integral
over a two-dimensional spherical Gaussian charge distribution.
We have

\displaystyle v_{0}(C)

\displaystyle=\int\differential[2]{\vb{r}}\frac{\exp(-pr^2_P)}{r_{C}}

\displaystyle=\int\differential[2]{\vb{r}}\exp(-pr^2_P)\int\differential{t}\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}}\exp(-r^2_{C} t^2),

(49)

where we in the last equation have used that

\displaystyle\int\differential{t}\exp(-r^2_{C} t^2)=\frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{r_{C}},

(50)

for
\real(r_{C})>0
, in reverse.
We now use the Gaussian product rule

\displaystyle\exp(-p r^2_{P})\exp(-r^2_{C} t^2)

\displaystyle=\exp(-\nu\Sigma^2)\exp(-s r^2_{S}),

(51)

where we have introducd
s=p+t^{2}
,
\nu=pt^{2}/s
,
S=(p\mathbf{P}+t^{2}\mathbf{C})/(p+t^{2})
, and
\mathbf{\Sigma}=\mathbf{P}-\mathbf{C}
.
Inserting this back into the integral we can now solve the integral over

\differential[2]{\vb{r}}
for the last term in the Gaussian product.
This leaves us with

\displaystyle v_{0}(C)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}}\int\differential{t}\exp(-\frac{pt^2}{p + t^2} \Sigma^2)\frac{\pi}{p+t^{2}}.

(52)

This integral is symmetric for
t\in(-\infty,\infty)
.
We therefore shift the limits of integration to
t\in[0,\infty)
and
multiply with a factor of
2
.
Then

\displaystyle v_{0}(C)

\displaystyle=\frac{2\sqrt{\pi}}{p}\int_{0}^{\infty}\differential{t}\frac{p}{p+t^{2}}\exp(-\frac{pt^2}{p + t^2} \Sigma^2),

(53)

where we have moved a factor of
p
into the numerator of the integral.
Next, we perform a variable substitution introducing

\displaystyle u^{2}=\frac{t^{2}}{p+t^{2}}\implies u=\frac{t}{\sqrt{p+t^{2}}},

(54)

where
t\geq 0
from the integration limits and
\real(p)>0
.
The new integration limits are
\lim_{t\to\infty}u(t)=1
and
u(0)=0
.
We also have

\displaystyle\derivative[]{u}{t}=\frac{\left(1-u^{2}\right)^{3/2}}{\sqrt{p}}\implies\differential{t}=\frac{\sqrt{p}}{\left(1-u^{2}\right)^{3/2}}\differential{u},

(55)

for the variable substitution.
Inserted back into
v_{0}(C)
we are then left with

\displaystyle v_{0}(C)

\displaystyle=\frac{2\sqrt{\pi}}{p}\int_{0}^{1}\differential{u}\frac{\sqrt{p}\left(1-u^{2}\right)}{\left(1-u^{2}\right)^{3/2}}\exp(-p u^2 \Sigma^2)

\displaystyle=\pi\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{p}}\exp(-\frac{p \Sigma^{2}}{2})I_{0}\!\left(-\frac{p\Sigma^{2}}{2}\right),

(56)

where
I_{0}(z)
is the modified Bessel function of the first kind.
Note that
I_{0}(z)=I_{0}(-z)
making the choice of sign in the argument
arbitrary.
However, when we create a recursion formula for higher-order Hermite
Gaussians we benefit from using the same sign in the exponential
function and the Bessel function.

Appendix F
Recursion formula for the Coulomb attraction integral

Defining
z\equiv-p\Sigma^{2}/2
as the argument of the exponential
function and the Bessel function in

Equation 19
, we find the derivative of this
quantity with respect to
P_{x}
and
P_{y}
to be

\displaystyle\partialderivative[]{z}{P_i}=-p\Sigma_{i},\quad\partialderivative[2]{z}{P_i}=-p.

(57)

Furthermore, we define the
exponentially scaled modified Bessel
function of the first kind
to be

\displaystyle\tilde{i}_{n}(z)\equiv\exp(z)I_{n}(z),

(58)

where
n
denotes the order of the modified Bessel function.
The derivative of this function is

\displaystyle\left.\derivative[]{\extbes_n}{z}\right\rvert_{z}

\displaystyle=\tilde{i}_{n}(z)+\frac{1}{2}\left[\tilde{i}_{n-1}(z)+\tilde{i}_{n+1}(z)\right],

(59)

where
n\geq 0
and
\tilde{i}_{-1}(z)=\tilde{i}_{1}(z)
.
Using the intermediate integrals defined in

Equation 22
and in the text below, we compute

\displaystyle\tilde{I}^{n}_{t+1,u}(p,\mathbf{\Sigma})

\displaystyle=\partialderivative[t]{P_x}\partialderivative[u]{P_y}\left.\partialderivative[]{\extbes_{n}}{P_x}\right\rvert_{z=-p\Sigma^{2}/2}

\displaystyle=\partialderivative[t]{P_x}\partialderivative[u]{P_y}\partialderivative[]{z}{P_x}\left.\partialderivative[]{\extbes_{n}}{z}\right\rvert_{z=-p\Sigma^{2}/2}

\displaystyle=\partialderivative[t]{P_x}\partialderivative[u]{P_y}\left(-p\Sigma_{x}\right)\frac{1}{2}\Bigl[\tilde{i}_{n-1}

\displaystyle\qquad+2\tilde{i}_{n}+\tilde{i}_{n+1}\Bigr],

(60)

where we for brevity have removed the function arguments to the
exponentially scaled modified Bessel functions.
To proceed from here we note that the derivative with respect to
P_{y}

“passes through” the term in front of the Bessel functions.
For the derivatives with respect to
P_{x}
we have that

\displaystyle\left[\partialderivative[t]{P_x},\Sigma_{x}\right]

\displaystyle=t\partialderivative[t - 1]{P_x}.

(61)

We then have

\displaystyle\tilde{I}^{n}_{t+1,u}(p,\mathbf{\Sigma})

\displaystyle=-\frac{p}{2}\left[t\partialderivative[t - 1]{P_x}+\Sigma_{x}\partialderivative[t]{P_x}\right]

\displaystyle\qquad\times\left[\tilde{I}^{n-1}_{0u}+2\tilde{I}^{n}_{0u}+\tilde{I}^{n+1}_{0u}\right]

\displaystyle=-\frac{p}{2}\Biggl[t\left(\tilde{I}^{n-1}_{t-1,u}+2\tilde{I}^{n}_{t-1,u}+\tilde{I}^{n+1}_{t-1,u}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad+\Sigma_{x}\left(\tilde{I}^{n-1}_{tu}+2\tilde{I}^{n}_{tu}+\tilde{I}^{n+1}_{tu}\right)\Biggr].

(62)

For the
y
-direction we have a similar relation given by

\displaystyle\tilde{I}^{n}_{t,u+1}(p,\mathbf{\Sigma})

\displaystyle=-\frac{p}{2}\Biggl[u\left(\tilde{I}^{n-1}_{t,u-1}+2\tilde{I}^{n}_{t,u-1}+\tilde{I}^{n+1}_{t,u-1}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad+\Sigma_{y}\left(\tilde{I}^{n-1}_{tu}+2\tilde{I}^{n}_{tu}+\tilde{I}^{n+1}_{tu}\right)\Biggr].

(63)

Appendix G
Coulomb interaction between two spherical Gaussian charge
distributions

We derive the closed-form expression for the Coulomb interaction
integral between two two-dimensional spherical Gaussian charge
distributions.
This is based on the work done by
Nielsen et al. 2010

Nielsen et al. 2010
.
The integral we wish to solve is on the form

\displaystyle V_{0}

\displaystyle=\int\differential[2]{\vb{r}_1}\differential[2]{\vb{r}_2}\frac{\exp(-p r_{1P}^{2})\exp(-q r_{2Q}^{2})}{r_{12}},

(64)

where we have defined
\mathbf{r}_{1P}\equiv\mathbf{r}_{1}-\mathbf{P}
and
similarly
\mathbf{r}_{2Q}
.
We also have
r_{12}\equiv\norm{\vb{r}_1 - \vb{r}_2}
.
To proceed we use the momentum representation of the exponential
functions and the Coulomb operator.
They are given by

\begin{gathered}\exp(-p r^{2}_{1P})=\frac{\pi}{p}\int\frac{\differential[2]{\vb{k}}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\exp(-\frac{k^2}{4p})\exp(-i\vb{k}\cdot\vb{r}_{1P}),\\
\frac{1}{r_{12}}=\int\frac{\differential[2]{\vb{k}}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\frac{2\pi}{k}\exp(-i\vb{k}\cdot\vb{r}_{12}).\end{gathered}

(65)

Inserted into the full integral we have

\displaystyle V_{0}

\displaystyle=\frac{\pi^{2}}{pq}\int\differential[2]{\vb{r}_1}\differential[2]{\vb{r}_2}\frac{\differential[2]{\vb{k}_1}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\frac{\differential[2]{\vb{k}_2}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\frac{\differential[2]{\vb{k}_3}}{(2\pi)^{2}}

\displaystyle\qquad\times\frac{2\pi}{k_{2}}\exp(-i\vb{k}_2\cdot\vb{r}_{12})

\displaystyle\qquad\times\exp(-\frac{k_1^2}{4p})\exp(-i\vb{k}_1\cdot\vb{r}_{1P})

\displaystyle\qquad\times\exp(-\frac{k_3^2}{4q})\exp(-i\vb{k}_3\cdot\vb{r}_{2Q}).

(66)

We collect all the
\mathbf{r}
-terms in the complex exponentials, and
integrate over
\mathbf{r}_{1}
and
\mathbf{r}_{2}
yielding delta functions for
the momentum integrals.
We can then set
\mathbf{k}\equiv-\mathbf{k}_{1}=\mathbf{k}_{2}=\mathbf{k}_{3}
, and
this lets us write

\displaystyle V_{0}

\displaystyle=\frac{\pi^{2}}{pq}\int\frac{\differential[2]{\vb{k}}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\frac{2\pi}{k}\exp(-\sigma k^2)\exp(i\vb{k}\cdot\vb{\Delta}),

(67)

where we have defined
\sigma=(p+q)/(4pq)
and
\mathbf{\Delta}=\mathbf{Q}-\mathbf{P}
.
We now transform to polar coordinates and choose the
x
-axis to be
along the
\mathbf{\Delta}
such that we can write the scalar product
between
\mathbf{k}
and
\mathbf{\Delta}
as
\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{\Delta}=k\Delta\cos(\phi)
, in the normal way.
We let
k\in(-\infty,\infty)
and
\phi\in[0,\pi)
as this
covers the same area as the conventional limits.
The integral can now be written

\displaystyle V_{0}

\displaystyle=\frac{\pi}{2pq}\int_{0}^{\pi}\differential{\phi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\differential{k}\exp(-\sigma k^2)\exp(ik\Delta\cos(\phi)),

(68)

where the Coulomb momentum term has been cancelled by the Jacobian from
changing to polar coordinates.
Completing the square in the exponentials we get

\displaystyle-\sigma k^{2}+ik\Delta\cos(\phi)

\displaystyle=-\sigma\left(k-\frac{i\Delta\cos(\phi)}{2\sigma}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\quad-\frac{\Delta^{2}\cos[2](\phi)}{4\sigma},

(69)

where only the squared term is dependent on
k
.
We can then solve the
k
-integral leaving us with

\displaystyle V_{0}

\displaystyle=\frac{\pi}{pq}\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{4\sigma}}\int_{0}^{\pi}\differential{\phi}\exp(-\frac{\Delta^2\cos[2](\phi)}{4\sigma}).

(70)

Next we rewrite the argument in the exponential using a trigonometric
identity for double angles.
That is

\displaystyle-\frac{\Delta^{2}\cos[2](\phi)}{4\sigma}=-\frac{\Delta^{2}}{8\sigma}\bigl(1+\cos(2\phi)\bigr),

(71)

and we move the constant term outside the integral.
Defining
\theta=2\phi
we have
\differential{\theta}/2=\differential{\phi}
and a
doubling of the integral limits.
We are then left with

\displaystyle V_{0}

\displaystyle=\frac{\pi}{pq}\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{4\sigma}}\exp(-\frac{\Delta^2}{8\sigma})\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{\differential{\phi}}{2}\exp(-\frac{\Delta^2}{8\sigma}\cos(\theta)).

(72)

The last term in the integral is symmetric over the integration
interval.
This then gives

\displaystyle V_{0}

\displaystyle=\frac{\pi}{pq}\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{4\sigma}}\exp(-\frac{\Delta^2}{8\sigma})\pi I_{0}\!\left(-\frac{\Delta^{2}}{8\sigma}\right),

(73)

with
I_{0}(z)
being the modified Bessel function of the first kind.
Collecting terms we are then left with

\displaystyle V_{0}

\displaystyle=\frac{\pi^{2}}{pq}\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{4\sigma}}\tilde{i}_{0}\!\left(-\frac{\Delta^{2}}{8\sigma}\right),

(74)

where we have inserted the definition of the exponentially scaled Bessel
function from
Equation 58
.
