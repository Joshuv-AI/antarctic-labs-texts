Quasi-invariant theorem on the Gaussian path space Supported in part by NNSFC (12071085).
Qinpin Chen, Jian Sun, Bo Wu

Abstract
In this article, we will first introduce a class of Gaussian processes, and prove the quasi-invariant theorem with respect to the Gaussian Wiener measure, which is the law of the associated Gaussian process. In particular, it includes the case of the fractional Brownian motion. As applications, we will establish the integration by parts formula and Bismut-Elworthy-Li formula on the Gaussian path space, and by which we derive some logarithmic Sobolev inequalities. Moreover, we will also provides some applications in the field of financial mathematics.



Keywords:
F
-Gaussian process; Quasi-invariant theorem; Integration by parts formula; Logarithmic Sobolev inequality

In mathematical finance area, people usually uses Brownian motion to model asset prices. This approach achived hugh successes in derivatives pricing which cultivated in 1997 Nobel Economcs prize to the works of Black, Scholes and Merton who developed a complete theory of option pricing formula. The theory is complete but still has a serious unsolved discrepancies between the constant volatility used in the model and realistic market data. In the Black-Scholes-Merton theory underlying price has a constant volatility which is independent of the option strikes but market option prices implies a volatility curve with respect to strikes. This phenomina called for more models afterwards among which stochastic volatility model played a key roles in applications, for example

2
F
-Gaussian process

Let
(\Omega,\mathscr{F},\mathbb{P})
be a complete probability space and
\{B_{t}:t\geq 0\}
be the standard Brownian
motion on
\mathbb{R}
. Let
F:\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is a continuous function of two variables.
In the following, we will mainly consider the stochastic process defined by

(2.1)

B_{t}^{F}:=\int_{0}^{t}F(t,s)\text{\rm{d}}B_{s},\quad t\geq 0.

To ensure that
B^{F}
is a Gaussian process, we need to add some reasonable conditions for the function
F
:

(2.2)

F\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})~~~\text{ and}~~\int_{0}^{t\wedge s}F(t,r)F(s,r)\text{\rm{d}}r\neq 0,\quad\forall~t,s>0

and for all
t\geq 0
,

(2.3)

\varphi_{t}(\cdot):=F(t,\cdot)\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}).

Under the above conditions (
2.3
) and (
2.4
), it is easy to show that
B^{F}
is a centered Gaussian process with covariance
R_{F}(t,s):=\mathbb{E}(B_{t}^{F}B_{s}^{F})
and

(2.4)

R_{F}(t,s)=\int_{0}^{t\wedge s}F(t,r)F(s,r)\text{\rm{d}}r.

Here, we will call
B^{F}
as the
F
-Gaussian process.

In particular, when
F(t,s)\equiv 1,(t,s)\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}
, then
B_{t}^{F}
is the standard Brownian motion on
\mathbb{R}
; when we choose
F(t,s)=K_{H}(t,s),(t,s)\in[0,\infty)\times[0,\infty)
, where
K_{H}(t,s)
is the non-negative square integrable kernel defined on
[0,\infty)\times[0,\infty)
given by

(2.5)

K_{H}(t,s)=\begin{cases}&c_{H}s^{\frac{1}{2}-H}\int_{s}^{t}(u-s)^{H-\frac{3}{2}}u^{H-\frac{1}{2}}\text{\rm{d}}u,\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad~~H\in(\frac{1}{2},1),\\
&1_{[0,t]}(s),\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad~~H=\frac{1}{2},\\
&b_{H}\left(\left(\frac{t(t-s)}{s}\right)^{H-\frac{1}{2}}-\left(H-\frac{1}{2}\right)s^{\frac{1}{2}-H}\int_{s}^{t}(u-s)^{H-\frac{1}{2}}u^{H-\frac{3}{2}}\text{\rm{d}}u\right),\quad H\in(0,\frac{1}{2}),\end{cases}

where
t>s
, and
c_{H},b_{H}
are independent with respect to
t,s
,
B^{K_{H}}
is exactly the fractional Brownian motion with Hurst index
H
in
[
44
,
8
]
.

For each fixed
T>0
, let
\mu^{T}
be the law of
B^{F}_{[0,T]}
, which is a probability measure on the path space
W_{T}:=C([0,T];\mathbb{R})
. Usually,
\mu^{T}
is also called the
F
-Gaussian Wiener measure.

Throughout the article, we assume that the conditions (
2.3
) and (
2.4
) hold. In fact, they are also the necessary conditions for
B^{F}
to be a Gaussian process.

3
Integrals for
F
-Gaussian process

In the section, we will introduce stochastic integrals with respect to
F
-Gaussian process
B^{F}
by using its Gaussianity. They may be expressed in terms of an integral with respect to the standard Brownian motion. Before going on, we first introduce two operators
K_{F}^{*}
and
(K_{F}^{*})^{-1}
, which are very critical for the quasi-invariant theorem in the next section.

3.1
K_{F}^{*}
and
(K_{F}^{*})^{-1}
operators

For any fixed
T>0
, we define by the classical Cameron-Martin space for the standard Brownian motion
B_{t}
:

(3.1)

\mathbb{H}=\left\{h\in C^{1}([0,T];\mathbb{R}):h(0)=0,\|h\|^{2}_{\mathbb{H}}:=\int_{0}^{T}|h^{\prime}(s)|^{2}\text{\rm{d}}s<\infty\right\},

which is a separable Hilbert space under the inner product

\langle h,g\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}:=\langle h^{\prime},g^{\prime}\rangle_{L^{2}([0,T])}=\int_{0}^{T}h^{\prime}(s)g^{\prime}(s)\text{\rm{d}}s,\quad h,g\in\mathbb{H}.

For a fixed function
F\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
satisfying with (
2.3
) and (
2.4
), we will introduce the Cameron-Martin space for the
F
-Gaussian process
B_{t}^{F}
. To do that, we need to define an operator
K_{F}
on
L^{2}([0,T];\mathbb{R})
given by

(3.2)

K_{F}h(t):=\int_{0}^{t}F(t,s)h(s)\text{\rm{d}}s,\quad h\in L^{2}([0,\infty)).

Let

(3.3)

\mathbb{H}^{F}:=\left\{h^{F}:=K_{F}\dot{h}\Big|h\in\mathbb{H}\right\}.

Then
\mathbb{H}^{F}
is a inner product space under the following inner product:

(3.4)

\langle h^{F},g^{F}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}^{F}}=\int_{0}^{T}h^{\prime}(s)g^{\prime}(s)\text{\rm{d}}s

for any
h^{F},g^{F}\in\mathbb{H}^{F}
with the forms
h^{F}:=K_{F}\dot{h},~g^{F}:=K_{F}\dot{g},~h,g\in\mathbb{H}
. Moreover, we may easily obtain

\langle K_{F}\dot{h},K_{F}\dot{g}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}^{F}}:=\langle h,g\rangle_{\mathbb{H}},\quad\forall h,g\in\mathbb{H}.

Since

\|K_{F}h\|_{L^{2}}\leq\|F\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}\|h\|_{L^{2}},

K_{F}
is a bounded linear operator from
L^{2}([0,T])
to
\mathbb{H}^{F}
. Thus
\mathbb{H}^{F}
is also a Hilbert space under the inner product
\langle h^{F},g^{F}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}^{F}}
.

Let
K_{F}^{*}
be the adjoint of
K_{F}
in the following sense:

\int_{0}^{T}(K_{F}^{*}g)(s)h(s)\text{\rm{d}}s=\int_{0}^{T}g(s)\frac{\text{\rm{d}}(K_{F}h)(s)}{\text{\rm{d}}s},\quad g,h\in L^{2}([0,T];\mathbb{R}),

where
g,h\in L^{2}([0,T])
. By exchanging the order of the double integral, we immediately obtain that

(3.5)

(K_{F}^{*}g)(s)=F(s,s)g(s)+\int_{s}^{T}\frac{\partial F(t,s)}{\partial t}g(t)\text{\rm{d}}t.

Let
\mathscr{K}_{F}^{*}
be the adjoint of
K_{F}
in the
L^{2}
sense:

\int_{0}^{T}(\mathscr{K}_{F}^{*}g)(t)h(t)\text{\rm{d}}t=\int_{0}^{T}g(t)K_{F}h(t)\text{\rm{d}}t,\quad g,h\in L^{2}([0,T];\mathbb{R}),

where
g,h\in L^{2}([0,T])
. Moreover, we get easily

(3.6)

\mathscr{K}_{F}^{*}g(t)=\int_{t}^{T}F(s,t)g(s)\text{\rm{d}}s.

Remark 3.1
.

Assume that
F\in L^{2}([0,T]^{2};\mathbb{R})
, then the following hold

(1)

K_{F}
and
\mathscr{K}_{F}^{*}:L^{2}([0,T];\mathbb{R})\rightarrow\mathbb{H}^{F}
are bounded linear operators;

(2)
Further assume that
F\in C^{1}([0,T];\mathbb{R})
and
F(s,s)=0,\forall s\in[0,T]
, then
K_{F}^{*}:L^{2}([0,T];\mathbb{R})\rightarrow\mathbb{H}^{F}
is a bounded linear operator, and
\mathscr{K}_{F}^{*}=K_{F}^{*}\circ\mathscr{K}_{1_{\{s\leq t\}}}^{*}.

By the definition of
B^{F}
, we may take actually the
F
-Gaussian process as the functional of the standard Brownian motion, and combining this with the quasi-invariant property of the Wiener measure, we will derive the quasi-invariant theorem with respect to the
F
-Gaussian Wiener measure. To obtain that, we firstly establish the essential connection between the standard Brownian motion and
F
-Gaussian process. Thus, in addition to the above assumption of
F
that
B^{F}
is a Gaussian process, we need to further assume that:

(A1)

For a.s.
t\in L^{2}[0,T],F_{1}(t,s):=\frac{\partial F(t,s)}{\partial t}\in L^{2}([0,T]^{2};\mathbb{R})
.

(A2)

There exists
\Phi\in L^{2}([0,T]^{2};\mathbb{R})\cap C^{1}((0,T)^{2};\mathbb{R})
such that for any
0\leq r\leq t\leq T
,

\xi(r):=\int_{r}^{t}\Phi(t,s)F(s,r)\text{\rm{d}}s~~\text{and}~~\xi,\frac{1}{\xi}\in L^{2}([0,T];\mathbb{R}).

(A3)

Assume
F\in C([0,T]^{2};\mathbb{R})
and there exists
\Theta\in L^{2}([0,T]^{2};\mathbb{R})
such that for any
0\leq t\leq r\leq T
,

\zeta(r):=\Theta(r,t)F(r,r)+\int_{t}^{r}\Theta(s,t)\frac{\partial F(r,s)}{\partial r}\text{\rm{d}}s~~\text{and}~~\zeta,\frac{1}{\zeta}\in L^{2}([0,T];\mathbb{R}).

In the following Theorem
3.2
, we will present representations of inverse operators
K_{F}^{-1}
and
(K_{F}^{*})^{-1}
under above certain conditions. This is the crucial point for the proof of the quasi-invariant theorem in the next section.

Theorem 3.2
.

Suppose
(A1)
holds.

(1)
If
F
satisfies condition
(A2)
, then
K_{F}
is invertible and

(3.7)

K_{F}^{-1}g(t):=\frac{1}{\xi(t)}\frac{\text{\rm{d}}}{\text{\rm{d}}t}K_{\Phi}g(t)=\frac{1}{\xi(t)}\Big[\Phi(t,t)g(t)+\int^{t}_{0}\frac{\text{\rm{d}}\Phi(t,s)}{\text{\rm{d}}t}g(s)\text{\rm{d}}s\Big],\quad\forall~g\in\mathbb{H}^{F}.

(2)
If
F
satisfies condition
(A3)
, then
K_{F}^{*}
is invertible and

(3.8)

(K_{F}^{*})^{-1}g(t):=-\frac{1}{\zeta(t)}\frac{\text{\rm{d}}}{\text{\rm{d}}t}\mathscr{K}_{\Theta}^{*}g(t)=\frac{1}{\zeta(t)}\Big[\Theta(t,t)g(t)-\int^{T}_{t}\frac{\text{\rm{d}}\Theta(s,t)}{\text{\rm{d}}t}g(s)\text{\rm{d}}s\Big],\quad\forall~g\in\mathbb{H}^{F}.

Remark 3.3
.

(1)
Usually, all derivatives
\frac{\text{\rm{d}}}{\text{\rm{d}}t}g
in the above theorem
3.2
are in the
L^{2}
-sense.

(2)
When
F=K_{H}
, i.e.
B^{F}
is the fBm,
K_{F}^{-1}
and
(K_{F}^{*})^{-1}
were introduced by Decreusefond-Üstünel in
[
20
]
.

Proof of Theorem
3.2

(1)

(a)
Suppose that
h\in L^{2}([0,T];\mathbb{R})
, then by (
2.3
) and (
3.2
), we know that

g(t):=K_{F}h\in\mathbb{H}^{F}.

Now, it suffices to only show that

K_{F}^{-1}K_{F}h(t)=h(t),\quad h\in L^{2}([0,T];\mathbb{R})~~\text{and}~~K_{F}K_{F}^{-1}g(t)=g(t),\quad g\in\mathbb{H}^{F}.

Firstly, it is easy to obtain that

K_{F}^{-1}K_{F}h(t)=\frac{1}{\xi(t)}\frac{\text{\rm{d}}}{\text{\rm{d}}t}K_{\Phi}K_{F}h=h(t).

In fact, using (
3.2
) again,

\displaystyle K_{\Phi}K_{F}h(t)=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\Phi(t,s)\left(\int_{0}^{s}F(s,r)h(r)\text{\rm{d}}r\right)\text{\rm{d}}s

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{s}\Phi(t,s)F(s,r)h(r)\text{\rm{d}}r\text{\rm{d}}s

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\int_{r}^{t}\Phi(t,s)F(s,r)h(r)\text{\rm{d}}s\text{\rm{d}}r

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\left(\int_{r}^{t}\Phi(t,s)F(s,r)\text{\rm{d}}s\right)h(r)\text{\rm{d}}r

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\xi(r)h(r)\text{\rm{d}}r,

where the last equality is due to
(A2)
. Since
\xi
is continuous, we obtain

\frac{\text{\rm{d}}}{\text{\rm{d}}t}(K_{\Phi}K_{F}h)(t)=\xi(t)h(t)---L^{2}-{sense},

which is equivalent to

h(t)=\frac{1}{\xi(t)}\frac{\text{\rm{d}}}{\text{\rm{d}}t}K_{\Phi}K_{F}h(t).

(b)
Next, we will prove that

K_{F}K_{F}^{-1}g(t)=g(t),\quad g\in\mathbb{H}^{F}.

By the definition of
\mathbb{H}^{F}
, we know that there exists a
h_{1}\in L^{2}([0,T];\mathbb{R})
such that
g(t)=K_{F}h_{1}
. Then by (a), we have

K_{F}K_{F}^{-1}g(t)=K_{F}K_{F}^{-1}K_{F}h_{1}(t)=K_{F}h_{1}(t)=g(t).

(2)
Proof of
(2)
is similar to that of
(1)
, for the convenience of the reader, in the following we will give the detailed proof. We need to only show that

\begin{cases}&(K_{F}^{*})^{-1}K_{F}^{*}h(t)=h(t),\quad h\in L^{2}([0,T];\mathbb{R}),\\
&K_{F}^{*}(K_{F}^{*})^{-1}g(t)=g(t),\quad g\in K_{F}^{*}(L^{2}([0,T];\mathbb{R})).\end{cases}

By (
3.5
) and
(A3)
, we have

\displaystyle\mathscr{K}^{T}_{\Theta}K_{F}^{*}h(t)=

\displaystyle\int_{t}^{T}\Theta(s,t)\left(F(s,s)h(s)+\int_{s}^{T}\frac{\partial F(r,s)}{\partial r}h(r)\text{\rm{d}}r\right)\text{\rm{d}}s

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{t}^{T}\Theta(r,t)F(r,r)h(r)\text{\rm{d}}r+\int_{t}^{T}\int_{s}^{T}\Theta(s,t)\frac{\partial F(r,s)}{\partial r}h(r)\text{\rm{d}}r\text{\rm{d}}s

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{t}^{T}\Theta(r,t)F(r,r)h(r)\text{\rm{d}}r+\int_{t}^{T}\int_{t}^{r}\Theta(s,t)\frac{\partial F(r,s)}{\partial r}h(r)\text{\rm{d}}s\text{\rm{d}}r

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{t}^{T}\left(\Theta(r,t)F(r,r)+\int_{t}^{r}\Theta(s,t)\frac{\partial F(r,s)}{\partial r}\text{\rm{d}}s\right)h(r)\text{\rm{d}}r

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{t}^{T}\zeta(r)h(r)\text{\rm{d}}r.

Since
\zeta
is continuous, we have

\frac{\text{\rm{d}}}{\text{\rm{d}}t}\mathscr{K}^{T}_{\Theta}K_{F}^{*}h(t)=-\zeta(t)h(t)---L^{2}~{sense}.

Thus,

(K_{F}^{*})^{-1}K_{F}^{*}h(t)=-\frac{1}{\zeta(t)}\frac{\text{\rm{d}}}{\text{\rm{d}}t}\mathscr{K}^{T}_{\Theta}K_{F}^{*}h(t)=h(t).

For any
h\in L^{2}([0,T];\mathbb{R})
, let
g=K_{F}^{*}h
. By (
3.5
) and
{\bf(A3)}
, we know that

g(t)=K_{F}^{*}h(t)=F(t,t)h(t)+\int_{t}^{T}\frac{\partial F(s,t)}{\partial s}h(s)\text{\rm{d}}s\in L^{2}([0,T];\mathbb{R}).

Then by using the above equality, we obtain

K_{F}^{*}(K_{F}^{*})^{-1}g(t)=K_{F}^{*}(K_{F}^{*})^{-1}K_{F}^{*}h(t)=K_{F}^{*}h(t)=g(t).~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~\square

In theorem
3.2
, we only show that
K_{F},K_{F}^{*}
is invertible on the subspace
\mathbb{H}^{F}
of
L^{2}([0,T];\mathbb{R})
. However, to introduce the integrals with respect to
F
-Gaussian process and the quasi-invariant theorem in the next section, it requires that
K_{F}^{*}
is invertible on the whole
L^{2}([0,T];\mathbb{R})
. To do that, we need to add the following stronger condition
(A4)
:

(A4)

Assume
F\in C([0,T]^{2};\mathbb{R})
and there exists functions
\Theta\in L^{2}([0,T]^{2};\mathbb{R})\cap C^{1}((0,T)^{2};\mathbb{R})
and
0<\zeta_{1},\zeta_{2}\in L^{2}([0,T];\mathbb{R})
with
\frac{1}{\zeta_{1}},\frac{1}{\zeta_{2}}\in L^{2}([0,T];\mathbb{R})
such that for any
0\leq t\leq r\leq T
,

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\frac{\Theta(r,t)}{\zeta_{1}(r)}F(r,r)+\int_{t}^{r}\frac{\Theta(s,t)}{\zeta_{1}(s)}\frac{\partial F(r,s)}{\partial r}\text{\rm{d}}s=\zeta_{2}(r),\\
\frac{\Theta(r,t)}{\zeta_{2}(t)}F(t,t)+\int_{t}^{r}\frac{\partial F(s,t)}{\partial s}\frac{\Theta(r,s)}{\zeta_{2}(s)}\text{\rm{d}}s=\zeta_{1}(t),\\
\frac{\partial\Theta(r,t)}{\partial r}+\frac{\partial\Theta(r,t)}{\partial t}\equiv 0.\end{array}\right.

Remark 3.4
.

In the condition
(A4)
, if
\frac{\Theta(s,t)}{\zeta_{1}(s)}
and
\zeta_{2}
are replaced by
\Theta(s,t)
and
\zeta
respectively, then the first equation in the condition
(A4)
implies condition
(A3)
.

Theorem 3.5
.

Suppose
(A1)
and
(A4)
hold. Then we have

(3.9)

\left(K_{F}^{*}\right)^{-1}g(t)=-\frac{1}{\zeta_{2}(t)}\frac{\text{\rm{d}}}{\text{\rm{d}}t}\int_{t}^{T}\frac{\Theta(s,t)}{\zeta_{1}(s)}g(s)\text{\rm{d}}s,\quad g\in L^{2}([0,T];\mathbb{R}),

where the derivative
\frac{\text{\rm{d}}}{\text{\rm{d}}t}
is in the
L^{2}
-sense.

Proof.

It suffices to prove that

(3.10)

\left(K_{F}^{*}\right)^{-1}K_{F}^{*}g(t)=g(t),\quad K_{F}^{*}\left(K_{F}^{*}\right)^{-1}g(t)=g(t),\quad g\in L^{2}([0,T];\mathbb{R}).

According to Theorem
3.2
, we know that
\left(K_{F}^{*}\right)^{-1}K_{F}^{*}g(t)=g(t)
holds for
g\in L^{2}([0,T];\mathbb{R})
, and
K_{F}^{*}\left(K_{F}^{*}\right)^{-1}g(t)=g(t)
holds for
g\in\mathbb{H}^{F}
. Thus, we only need to show that

(3.11)

K_{F}^{*}\left(K_{F}^{*}\right)^{-1}g(t)=g(t),\quad\forall g\in L^{2}([0,T];\mathbb{R}).

By
(A4)
, we know that
\frac{\Theta(s,t)}{\zeta_{1}(s)}
is continous with respect to
s\in[0,T]
. By repeating the above argument in the Theorem
3.2
, we may get

(3.12)

\displaystyle\frac{\text{\rm{d}}}{\text{\rm{d}}t}\int_{t}^{T}\frac{\Theta(s,t)}{\zeta_{1}(s)}g(s)=

\displaystyle-\frac{\Theta(t,t)}{\zeta_{1}(t)}g(t)+\int_{t}^{T}\frac{\partial\Theta(s,t)}{\partial t}\frac{g(s)}{\zeta_{1}(s)}\text{\rm{d}}s

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{\Theta(T,t)}{\zeta_{1}(t)}g(t)+\int_{t}^{T}\left(\frac{\partial\Theta(s,t)}{\partial t}\frac{g(s)}{\zeta_{1}(s)}+\frac{\partial\Theta(s,t)}{\partial s}\frac{g(t)}{\zeta_{1}(t)}\right)\text{\rm{d}}s

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{\Theta(T,t)}{\zeta_{1}(t)}g(t)+\int_{t}^{T}\frac{\partial\Theta(s,t)}{\partial s}\left(\frac{g(t)}{\zeta_{1}(t)}-\frac{g(s)}{\zeta_{1}(s)}\right)\text{\rm{d}}s----L^{2}~\text{sense},

where the second equal is due to the third equation in
(A4)
. Combining this with (
3.9
),

(3.13)

\displaystyle K_{F}^{*}\left(K_{F}^{*}\right)^{-1}g(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{F(t,t)}{\zeta_{2}(t)}\left(-\frac{\Theta(T,t)}{\zeta_{1}(t)}g(t)+\int_{t}^{T}\frac{\partial\Theta(s,t)}{\partial s}\left(\frac{g(t)}{\zeta_{1}(t)}-\frac{g(s)}{\zeta_{1}(s)}\right)\text{\rm{d}}s\right)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\int_{t}^{T}\frac{\partial F(s,t)}{\partial s}\frac{1}{\zeta_{2}(s)}\left(\frac{\Theta(T,s)}{\zeta_{1}(s)}g(s)-\int_{s}^{T}\frac{\partial\Theta(r,s)}{\partial r}\left(\frac{g(s)}{\zeta_{1}(s)}-\frac{g(r)}{\zeta_{1}(r)}\right)\text{\rm{d}}r\right)\text{\rm{d}}s

Next, we will finish the proof by dicussing different case of
g
.

(a) Firstly, we consider the case of
g(t)=1_{[0,a]}(t)\zeta_{1}(t)
for some
a\in(0,T]
. From (
3.13
), we obtain

\displaystyle K_{F}^{*}\left(K_{F}^{*}\right)^{-1}g(t)=\frac{\Theta(a,t)}{\zeta_{2}(t)}F(t,t)+\int_{t}^{a}\frac{\partial F(s,t)}{\partial s}\frac{\Theta(a,s)}{\zeta_{2}(s)}\text{\rm{d}}s=\zeta_{1}(t)=g(t),\quad t\leq a,

where the last second equality is due to the condition
{\bf(A4)}
. When
t>a
, we know that

K_{F}^{*}\left(K_{F}^{*}\right)^{-1}g(t)=0.

(b) For general case of

. According the conditions

, we know that

, thus it only suffices to show that

\displaystyle K_{F}^{*}\left(K_{F}^{*}\right)^{-1}g(t)=g(t),\quad\forall~g(t)=h(t)\eta_{1}(t),h\in L^{2}([0,T];\mathbb{R}).

Since

is a linear operator, by the dominated convergence theorem and
(a)
, it is easy to get the above conclusion. Thus, we finish the proof of this theorem. ∎

Lemma 3.10

Proof.

Combining this with (

\mathbb{E}(B_{t}^{F}B_{s}^{F})=\int_{0}^{t\wedge s}F(t,r)F(s,r)\text{\rm{d}}r=\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{s}\left(\int_{0}^{u\wedge v}\frac{\partial F(u,r)}{\partial u}\frac{\partial F(v,r)}{\partial v}\text{\rm{d}}r\right)\text{\rm{d}}u\text{\rm{d}}v.

thus

By (

), for any
m,n\in\mathbb{N}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(\Sigma_{n}\Sigma_{m})=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left(\sum_{i=0}^{l(n)-1}c_{i}^{(n)}(B_{t_{i+1}^{(n)}}^{F}-B_{t_{i}^{(n)}}^{F})\right)\left(\sum_{j=0}^{l(m)-1}c_{j}^{(m)}(B_{t_{j+1}^{(m)}}^{F}-B_{t_{j}^{(m)}}^{F})\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{l(n)-1}\sum_{j=0}^{l(m)-1}c_{i}^{(n)}c_{j}^{(m)}\mathbb{E}(B_{t_{i+1}^{(n)}}^{F}-B_{t_{i}^{(n)}}^{F})(B_{t_{j+1}^{(m)}}^{F}-B_{t_{j}^{(m)}}^{F})

n\geq 1
,

\mathscr{E}(F_{\gamma}^{(n)},F_{\gamma}^{(n)})\leq\max_{1\leq k\leq n}\mathscr{E}(F_{\gamma,k},F_{\gamma,k})\leq T,

Since
F_{\gamma}^{(n)}\uparrow F_{\gamma}
as
n\uparrow\infty
and
F_{\gamma}\in L^{2}(W_{T},\mu^{F})
, it follows
[
43
, Lemma I-2.12]
that
F_{\gamma}\in\mathscr{D}(\mathscr{E})
and (
4.22
) holds as we select
C\equiv T
.

(2) Now let
\{\gamma_{k}:k\geq 1\}
be a countable dense subset of
W_{T}
. For any
n\geq 1
, let

F_{n}:=\inf_{1\leq k\leq n}F_{\gamma_{k}}.

Similar to the above argument we obtain from (
4.22
) that
\{F_{n}\}\subset\mathscr{D}(\mathscr{E})
and

\mathscr{E}(F_{n},F_{n})\leq C,\quad n\geq 1.

Since the sequence
\{F_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
satisfies
F_{n}\geq 0,F_{n}\geq F_{n+1},n\in\mathbb{N}
and
F_{1}\in L^{2}(W_{T},\mu^{F})
, we obtain

\|F_{n}\|_{L^{2}(W_{T},\mu^{F})}\leq\|F_{1}\|_{L^{2}(W_{T},\mu^{F})},\quad n\geq 1.

Therefore,

\mathscr{E}(F_{n},F_{n})\leq C+\|F_{1}\|_{L^{2}(W_{T},\mu^{F})}=:C_{2},\quad n\geq 1.

Since
\{\gamma_{i}:i\geq 1\}\subset W_{T}
is a dense subset, we have
F_{n}\downarrow 0
as
n\uparrow\infty
.

(3) Now, following the line of
[
24
]
(see pages 606 and 607 therein), we need to find out a sequence of closed subsets
\{K_{m}\subset W_{T}:m\in\mathbb{N}\}
, such that
F_{n}\rightarrow 0
uniformly on every
K_{m}
and
\lim\limits_{m\rightarrow\infty}\text{\rm Cap}(W_{T}\backslash K_{m})=0
. Since the sequence
\{F_{n}\}
is uniformly bounded in (2), there exists a subsequence of
\{F_{n}\}
(denoted again by
\{F_{n}\}
) such that

\mathscr{E}(\bar{F_{n}},\bar{F_{n}})\rightarrow 0,\quad n\uparrow\infty,

where
\bar{F_{n}}:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}F_{i},n\in\mathbb{N}
.
According to
[
43
, Propositions 3.5]
, there exists a subsequence of
\{\bar{F_{n}}\}
(denoted again by
\{\bar{F_{n}}\}
) and an
\mathscr{E}
-nest
\{K_{m}\},m\in\mathbb{N}
, such that on every
K_{m}
,
\bar{F_{n}}
converges uniformly to zero as
n\uparrow\infty
. Since
\{F_{n}\}
is a decreasing sequence,
F_{n}\leq\bar{F_{n}}
. So
F_{n}\rightarrow 0
uniformly on every
K_{m}
as well. As in
[
51
]
, proof of Proposition 3.1, or in
[
24
]
, proof of Proposition 5, it follows now from the definition of
F_{n}
that every
K_{m}
is totally bounded. Thus,
\{K_{m}\}
is an
\mathscr{E}
-nest consisting of compact sets, which is our desired sequence.
\hfill\square

5
Logarithmic Sobolev inequalities

In this section, we will establish Logarithmic Sobolev inequalities with respect to Dirichlet forms
(\mathscr{E},\mathscr{D}(\mathscr{E}))
,
(\mathscr{E}_{OU},\mathscr{D}(\mathscr{E}_{OU}))
and
(\mathscr{E}_{L^{2}},\mathscr{D}(\mathscr{E}_{L^{2}}))
respectively.
First we will present the Clark-Ocone-Haussmann formula for the Gaussian process
B^{F}
. Let
\mathscr{F}_{t}^{F}
be the
\sigma
-algebra generated by
B^{F}
, then we know that

\mathscr{F}_{t}^{F}:=\sigma\big(B_{s}^{F},s\leq t\big)=\sigma\big(B_{s},s\leq t\big)=\mathscr{F}_{t}.

Theorem 5.1
.

[Clark-Ocone-Haussmann formula]

Assume that
F
satisfies
(A1), (A2)
and
(A4)
, then we have

(5.1)

G\big(B^{F}\big)=\mathbb{E}\big[G\big(B^{F}\big)\big]+\int_{0}^{T}H_{t}^{G}\text{\rm{d}}B_{t}^{F},\quad~G\in\mathscr{D}(\mathscr{E})),

where

(5.2)

H_{t}^{G}=(K_{F}^{*})^{-1}\mathbb{E}\big[K_{F}^{-1}\nabla^{F}G\big(B^{F}\big)(t)|\mathscr{F}_{t}^{F}\big].

Proof.

Since
\mathscr{F}C_{b}^{OU}
is dense in
\mathscr{D}(\mathscr{E})
, it is enough to prove that (
5.1
) holds for each
G\in\mathscr{F}C_{b}^{OU}
.
By the classical Clark-Ocone formula of the Brownian motion
B_{t}
, we know that

(5.3)

G\big(B\big)=\mathbb{E}\big[G\big(B\big)\big]+\int_{0}^{T}\mathbb{E}\big[DG\big(B\big)|\mathscr{F}_{t}\big]\text{\rm{d}}B_{t}(\omega),\quad\forall~G\in\mathscr{D}.

By the definition of
B^{F}
, we know that
B^{F}
may be looked as the functional of
B
, i.e.
B^{F}_{\cdot}(\omega)=\int^{\cdot}_{0}F(\cdot,s)\text{\rm{d}}B_{s}(\omega):=\Phi\circ B(\omega)
. Then
\Phi\in\mathscr{D}
. Now replace
G
by
G\circ\Phi
in (
5.3
), we have

(5.4)

G\circ\Phi\big(B\big)=\mathbb{E}\big[(G\circ\Phi)\big(B\big)\big]+\int_{0}^{T}\mathbb{E}\big[D(G\circ\Phi)\big(B\big)|\mathscr{F}_{t}\big]\text{\rm{d}}B_{t}.

Notice that for each
h\in\mathbb{H}
,

\displaystyle\big\langle D(G\circ\Phi)(B),\dot{h}\big\rangle_{L^{2}[0,T]}

\displaystyle=D_{h}(G\circ\Phi)(B)=D_{K_{F}\dot{h}}G(\Phi(B))

\displaystyle=D_{K_{F}\dot{h}}G(B^{F})=\big\langle\nabla G(B^{F}),K_{F}\dot{h}\big\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}DG(B^{F})\text{\rm{d}}K_{F}\dot{h}(t)

\displaystyle=\big\langle K_{F}^{*}(DG)(B^{F}),\dot{h}\big\rangle_{L^{2}[0,T]}.

Since the arbitrariness of
h
, we obtain

(5.5)

D(G\circ\Phi)(B)=K_{F}^{*}(DG)(B^{F}).

By remark
4.6
, we have

\displaystyle G\big(B^{F}\big)=G\circ\Phi\big(B\big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[G\circ\Phi\big(B\big)\big]+\int_{0}^{T}\mathbb{E}\big[D(G\circ\phi)\big(B\big)(t)|\mathscr{F}_{t}\big]\text{\rm{d}}B_{t}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[G\big(B^{F}\big)\big]+\int_{0}^{T}\mathbb{E}\big[K_{F}^{*}(DG)\big(B^{F}\big)(t)|\mathscr{F}_{t}^{F}\big]\text{\rm{d}}B_{t}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[G\big(B^{F}\big)\big]+\int_{0}^{T}(K_{F}^{*})^{-1}\mathbb{E}\big[K_{F}^{*}(R_{F})^{-1}(\nabla^{F}G)\big(B^{F}\big)(t)|\mathscr{F}_{t}^{F}\big]\text{\rm{d}}B_{t}^{F}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[G\big(B^{F}\big)\big]+\int_{0}^{T}(K_{F}^{*})^{-1}\mathbb{E}\big[K_{F}^{-1}(\nabla^{F}G)\big(B^{F}\big)(t)|\mathscr{F}_{t}^{F}\big]\text{\rm{d}}B_{t}^{F}.

Thus we finish the proof.
∎

By theorem
5.1
, we may derive the following damped Logarithmic Sobolev inequality.

Theorem 5.2
.

[Damped Logarithmic Sobolev inequality]

Assume that
F
satisfies
(A1), (A2)
and
(A4)
, we have

(5.6)

\text{\rm{Ent}}_{\mu^{F}}(G^{2}):=\mathbb{E}_{\mu^{F}}\left(G^{2}\log\frac{G^{2}}{\mathbb{E}_{\mu^{F}}(G^{2})}\right)\leq 2\mathscr{E}(G,G),\quad G\in\mathscr{D}(\mathscr{E}).

Proof.

By the Clark-Ocone-Haussmann formula in theorem
5.1
, we know that

(5.7)

\displaystyle G\big(B^{F}\big)=\mathbb{E}\big[G\big(B^{F}\big)\big]+\int_{0}^{T}H_{t}^{G}\text{\rm{d}}B_{t}^{F}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[G\big(B^{F}\big)\big]+\int_{0}^{T}\mathbb{E}\big[K_{F}^{-1}(\nabla^{F}G)\big(B^{F}\big)(t)|\mathscr{F}_{t}^{F}\big]\text{\rm{d}}B_{t}.

Without loss of generality, we always assume that
G
is bounded and
G>0
. Consider the right continuous version of martingale

(5.8)

M_{t}:=\mathbb{E}\big[G\big(B^{F}\big)|\mathscr{F}_{t}^{F}\big],~~\quad 0\leq t\leq T,

then by (
5.7
) we have

M_{t}=\mathbb{E}\big[G\big(B^{F}\big)\big]+\int_{0}^{t}\mathbb{E}\big[K_{F}^{-1}(\nabla^{F}G)\big(B^{F}\big)(t)|\mathscr{F}_{t}^{F}\big]\text{\rm{d}}B_{t}

and

(5.9)

\text{\rm{d}}M_{t}=\mathbb{E}\big[K_{F}^{-1}(\nabla^{F}G)\big(B^{F}\big)(t)|\mathscr{F}_{t}^{F}\big]\text{\rm{d}}B_{t}.

Consider the function
f(x):=x\log x
. Then

f^{\prime}(x)=\log x+1,\quad f^{\prime\prime}(x)=x^{-1},\quad x>0.

By the Itô’s formula, we have

(5.10)

\displaystyle f(M_{T})-f(M_{0})

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}(\log(M_{t})+1)\text{\rm{d}}M_{t}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{T}\frac{\left(\mathbb{E}\big[K_{F}^{-1}(\nabla^{F}G)\big(B^{F}\big)(t)|\mathscr{F}_{t}^{F}\big]\right)^{2}}{M_{t}}\text{\rm{d}}t.

Taking expectation on both side in the above equation with respect to
\mathbb{E}
, we get

(5.11)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[G(B^{F})\log G(B^{F})\big]-\mathbb{E}\big[G(B^{F})\big]\log\mathbb{E}\big[G(B^{F})\big]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}\bigg[\int_{0}^{T}\frac{\left(\mathbb{E}\big[K_{F}^{-1}(\nabla^{F}G)\big(B^{F}\big)(t)|\mathscr{F}_{t}^{F}\big]\right)^{2}}{\mathbb{E}\big[G\big(B^{F}\big)|\mathscr{F}_{t}^{F}\big]}\text{\rm{d}}t\bigg].

By using the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality and
G
is replaced by
G^{2}
, we get

\displaystyle\left(\mathbb{E}\big[K_{F}^{-1}(\nabla^{F}G^{2})\big(B^{F}\big)(t)|\mathscr{F}_{t}^{F}\big]\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=\left(\mathbb{E}\big[2G\big(B^{F}\big)K_{F}^{-1}(\nabla^{F}G)\big(B^{F}\big)(t)|\mathscr{F}_{t}^{F}\big]\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 4\mathbb{E}\big[G\big(B^{F}\big)^{2}|\mathscr{F}_{t}^{F}\big]\mathbb{E}\big[(K_{F}^{-1}(\nabla^{F}G)\big(B^{F}\big)(t))^{2}|\mathscr{F}_{t}^{F}\big]

Therefore

\displaystyle\text{\rm{Ent}}_{\mu^{F}}(G^{2})=

\displaystyle\text{\rm{Ent}}_{\mathbb{P}}\big[G\big(B^{F}(\omega)\big)^{2}\big]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2\mathbb{E}\int_{0}^{T}\mathbb{E}\big[(K_{F}^{-1}(\nabla^{F}G)\big(B^{F}(\omega)\big)(t))^{2}|\mathscr{F}_{t}^{F}\big]\text{\rm{d}}t

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2\mathbb{E}\int_{0}^{T}(K_{F}^{-1}(\nabla^{F}G)\big(B^{F}(\omega)\big)(t))^{2}\text{\rm{d}}t

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\mathbb{E}\left\|(\nabla^{F}G)\big(B^{F}(\omega)\big)\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{F}}^{2}=2\mathbb{E}_{\mu^{F}}\left\|(\nabla^{F}G)\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{F}}^{2}.

∎

From the damped logarithmic Sobolev inequality in Theorem
5.2
, we may derive the following Logarithmic Sobolev inequality with respect to Ornstein-Uhlenbeck Dirichlet form
(\mathscr{E}_{OU},\mathscr{D}(\mathscr{E}_{OU}))
and
L^{2}
-Dirichlet form
(\mathscr{E}_{L^{2}},\mathscr{D}(\mathscr{E}_{L^{2}}))
respectively.

Theorem 5.3
.

[Logarithmic Sobolev inequality(O-U case)]

Under the same conditions in Theorem
5.2
, for any
\delta>0
we have

(5.12)

\text{\rm{Ent}}_{\mu^{F}}(G^{2})\leq C\mathscr{E}_{OU}(G,G),\quad\forall~G\in\mathscr{D}(\mathscr{E}_{OU}),

where

(5.13)

C=2(1+\delta)\sup_{t\in[0,T]}F(t,t)+2\frac{1+\delta}{\delta}\left\|\frac{\partial F(t,s)}{\partial t}\right\|_{L^{2}([0,T]^{2};\mathbb{R})}.

Proof.

By Theorem
5.2
, we know that

(5.14)

\text{\rm{Ent}}_{\mu^{F}}(G^{2})\leq 2\mathscr{E}(G,G)=2\int_{W_{T}}\left\|\nabla^{F}G\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{F}}^{2}\text{\rm{d}}\mu^{F}.

For each
h^{F}\in\mathbb{H}^{F}
, we get

\displaystyle\|\nabla^{F}G\|_{\mathbb{H}^{F}}^{2}=\sup_{\|h^{F}\|_{\mathbb{H}^{F}}\leq 1}\big\langle\nabla^{F}G,h^{F}\big\rangle_{\mathbb{H}^{F}}^{2}=\sup_{\|h^{F}\|_{\mathbb{H}^{F}}\leq 1}(D_{h^{F}}G)^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sup_{\|h^{F}\|_{\mathbb{H}^{F}}\leq 1}\big\langle\nabla G,h^{F}\big\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}^{2}\leq\sup_{\|h^{F}\|_{\mathbb{H}^{F}}\leq 1}\|h^{F}\|_{\mathbb{H}}^{2}\|\nabla G\|_{\mathbb{H}}^{2}.

In the following, we need to estimate
\sup_{\|h^{F}\|_{\mathbb{H}^{F}}\leq 1}\|h^{F}\|_{\mathbb{H}}^{2}
.
Noticing that for any
h^{F}\in\mathbb{H}^{F}
, we can find
\dot{h}\in L^{2}([0,T];\mathbb{R})
such that
h^{F}=K_{F}\dot{h}
. Thus by equation (
3.4
) we obtain

\big\|h^{F}\big\|_{\mathbb{H}^{F}}=\big\|K_{F}\dot{h}\big\|_{\mathbb{H}^{F}}=\big\|\dot{h}\big\|_{L^{2}([0,T];\mathbb{R})}.

For any
\delta>0
and real numbers
x,y
, we have

(x+y)^{2}\leq(1+\delta)x^{2}+\frac{1+\delta}{\delta}y^{2}.

Applying the above inequality and (
3.5
), we have

\displaystyle\big\|K_{F}\dot{h}\big\|^{2}_{\mathbb{H}}=

\displaystyle\left\|\frac{\text{\rm{d}}}{\text{\rm{d}}t}K_{F}\dot{h}\right\|^{2}_{L^{2}([0,T];\mathbb{R})}=\left\|\frac{\text{\rm{d}}}{\text{\rm{d}}t}\int_{0}^{t}F(t,s)\dot{h}(s)\text{\rm{d}}s\right\|^{2}_{L^{2}([0,T];\mathbb{R})}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\int^{T}_{0}\Big[F(t,t)\dot{h}(t)+\int_{0}^{t}\frac{\partial F(t,s)}{\partial t}\dot{h}(s)\text{\rm{d}}s\Big]^{2}dt

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle(1+\delta)\int^{T}_{0}\Big[F(t,t)\dot{h}(t)\Big]^{2}dt+\frac{1+\delta}{\delta}\int^{T}_{0}\Big[\int_{0}^{t}\frac{\partial F(t,s)}{\partial t}\dot{h}(s)\text{\rm{d}}s\Big]^{2}dt

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle(1+\delta)\sup_{t\in[0,T]}F(t,t)\|\dot{h}\|^{2}_{L^{2}([0,T];\mathbb{R})}+\frac{1+\delta}{\delta}\left\|\frac{\partial F(t,s)}{\partial t}\right\|^{2}_{L^{2}([0,T]^{2};\mathbb{R})}\|\dot{h}\|_{L^{2}([0,T];\mathbb{R})}

Therefore

(5.15)

\displaystyle\sup_{\|h^{F}\|_{\mathbb{H}^{F}}\leq 1}\|h^{F}\|_{\mathbb{H}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\sup_{\|\dot{h}\|_{L^{2}([0,T];\mathbb{R})}\leq 1}\|K_{F}\dot{h}\|^{2}_{\mathbb{H}}

\displaystyle\leq(1+\delta)\sup_{t\in[0,T]}F(t,t)+\frac{1+\delta}{\delta}\left\|\frac{\partial F(t,s)}{\partial t}\right\|_{L^{2}([0,T]^{2};\mathbb{R})}.

∎

Theorem 5.4
.

[Logarithmic Sobolev inequality(
L^{2}
-case)]

Under conditions in Theorem
5.2
, we get

(5.16)

\text{\rm{Ent}}_{\mu^{F}}(G^{2})\leq C\mathscr{E}_{L^{2}}(G,G),

for
G\in\mathscr{D}(\mathscr{E}_{L^{2}})
, where

(5.17)

C=2\|F\|_{L^{2}([0,T]^{2};\mathbb{R})}^{2}=2\int_{0}^{T}\int_{0}^{T}F(t,s)^{2}\text{\rm{d}}s\text{\rm{d}}t.

Proof.

Since
\mathscr{F}C_{b}^{L^{2}}\subset\mathscr{D}(\mathscr{E}_{OU})
, (
5.6
) holds for each
G\in\mathscr{F}C_{b}^{L^{2}}
. Next,
this proof is similar to the proof of Theorem
5.4
. For the convenience of readers’ reading, here we will provide the complete proof. For any
h\in\mathbb{H}^{F}
, there exists a
\dot{h}\in L^{2}([0,T];\mathbb{R})
such that
h^{F}=K_{F}\dot{h}
, thus we have

(5.18)

\displaystyle\big\|\nabla^{F}G\big\|_{\mathbb{H}^{F}}^{2}=

\displaystyle\sup_{\big\|h^{F}\big\|_{\mathbb{H}^{F}}\leq 1}\big\langle\nabla^{F}G,h^{F}\big\rangle_{\mathbb{H}^{F}}^{2}=\sup_{\|h^{F}\|_{\mathbb{H}^{F}}\leq 1}\big|D_{h^{F}}G\big|^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sup_{\big\|\dot{h}\big\|_{L^{2}}\leq 1}\big|D_{K_{F}\dot{h}}G\big|^{2}=\sup_{\|\dot{h}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\leq 1}\big\langle DG,K_{F}\dot{h}\big\rangle_{L^{2}([0,T];\mathbb{R})}|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\big\|DG\big\|_{L^{2}([0,T];\mathbb{R})}^{2}\sup_{\|\dot{h}\|_{L^{2}}\leq 1}\big\|K_{F}\dot{h}\big\|_{L^{2}([0,T];\mathbb{R})}^{2}.

Now we need to estimate
\sup_{\|\dot{h}\|_{2}\leq 1}\|K_{F}\dot{h}\|_{L^{2}([0,T];\mathbb{R})}^{2}
. In fact,

\displaystyle\|K_{F}\dot{h}\|_{2}^{2}=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\left(\int_{0}^{t}F(t,s)\dot{h}(s)\text{\rm{d}}s\right)^{2}\text{\rm{d}}t

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\left(\int_{0}^{t}F(t,s)^{2}\text{\rm{d}}s\int_{0}^{t}\dot{h}(s)^{2}\text{\rm{d}}s\right)\text{\rm{d}}t

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\left(\int_{0}^{T}F(t,s)^{2}\text{\rm{d}}s\int_{0}^{T}\dot{h}(s)^{2}\text{\rm{d}}s\right)\text{\rm{d}}t

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\int_{0}^{T}\int_{0}^{T}F(t,s)^{2}\text{\rm{d}}s\text{\rm{d}}t\right)\left(\int_{0}^{T}\dot{h}(s)^{2}\text{\rm{d}}s\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\|F\|_{L^{2}([0,T]^{2};\mathbb{R})}^{2}\|\dot{h}\|_{L^{2}([0,T];\mathbb{R})}^{2}.

This implies conclusion holds.
∎

6
Bismut-Elworthy-Li’s formula

In this section, based on the quasi-invariant theorem
4.1
, we will establish Bismut-Elworthy-Li’s formula with respect to Gaussian Wiener measure.

For each
x\in\mathbb{R}
, define

B_{t}^{F,x}=B_{t}^{F}+x.

Let
\mu
be the distributions of stochastic processes
B_{\cdot}^{F,x}
.

Theorem 6.1
.

[Bismut-Elworthy-Li’s formula]

Suppose
F
satisfies conditions
(A1), (A2)
and
(A4)
. Then for each
T>0
and
g\circ B_{T}^{F,x}\in L^{1}(\mathbb{P})
,

(6.1)

\frac{\text{\rm{d}}}{\text{\rm{d}}x}\mathbb{E}\left(g\big(B_{T}^{F,x}\big)\right)=\frac{1}{T_{0}}\mathbb{E}\left(g\big(B_{T}^{F,x}\big)\left(\int_{0}^{T}(R_{F})^{-1}\left(\int_{0}^{t}F(t,s)\text{\rm{d}}s\right)\text{\rm{d}}B_{t}^{F,x}\right)\right).

where

T_{0}:=\int_{0}^{T}F(T,s)\text{\rm{d}}s.

Proof.

According to the condition
(A2)
, we know that
T_{0}>0
. For each
\varepsilon\geq 0
and
t\geq 0
, define

h^{F,\varepsilon}_{t}:=K_{F}\Big(\frac{\varepsilon}{T_{0}}\Big)(t)=\int^{t}_{0}F(t,s)\frac{\varepsilon}{T_{0}}ds=\frac{\varepsilon}{T_{0}}\int^{t}_{0}F(t,s)ds.

Then
h^{F,\varepsilon}\in\mathbb{H}^{F}
. Denote

B_{t}^{F,x,\varepsilon}=B_{t}^{F,x}-h_{t}^{F,\varepsilon},\quad t\geq 0.

Let
\mu^{\varepsilon}
be the distributions of stochastic processes
B_{\cdot}^{F,x,\varepsilon}
. According to
4.2
, we have

(6.2)

\displaystyle\frac{\text{\rm{d}}\mu^{\varepsilon}}{\text{\rm{d}}\mu}

\displaystyle=\exp\left\{-\int_{0}^{T_{0}}(R_{F})^{-1}h^{F,\varepsilon}\text{\rm{d}}B_{t}^{F}-\frac{1}{2}\|h^{F,\varepsilon}\|_{\mathbb{H}^{F}}^{2}\right\}

\displaystyle=\exp\left\{-\int_{0}^{T_{0}}(R_{F})^{-1}h^{F,\varepsilon}\text{\rm{d}}B_{t}^{F}-\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{2{T_{0}}}\right\}.

In additional, for any
T_{1}>0
, the law of
B_{[0,T_{1}]}^{F,x+\varepsilon,\varepsilon}
under the probability measure
\mu^{\varepsilon}
is the same as
B_{[0,T_{1}]}^{F,x+\varepsilon}
under
\mu
, i.e.

(6.3)

\int_{\Omega}G\big(B_{[0,T_{1}]}^{F,x+\varepsilon,\varepsilon}\big)\frac{\text{\rm{d}}\mu^{\varepsilon}}{\text{\rm{d}}\mu}\text{\rm{d}}\mathbb{P}=\int_{\Omega}G\big(B_{[0,T_{1}]}^{F,x+\varepsilon}\big)\text{\rm{d}}\mathbb{P},\quad\forall~G\in L^{1}(\mu).

Since

\displaystyle B_{T}^{F,x+\varepsilon,\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=B_{T}^{F,x+\varepsilon}-h_{T}^{F,\varepsilon}=x+\varepsilon+B_{T}^{F}-h_{T}^{F,\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=x+\varepsilon+B_{T}^{F}-\frac{\varepsilon}{T_{0}}\int^{T}_{0}F(T,s)ds

\displaystyle=x+\varepsilon+B_{T}^{F}-\varepsilon

\displaystyle=B_{T}^{F,x},

Combing all the above arguments, in particular, taking
G(\gamma)=g(\gamma_{T})
, we obtain that

\int_{\Omega}g\big(B_{T}^{F,x}\big)\frac{\text{\rm{d}}\mu^{\varepsilon}}{\text{\rm{d}}\mu}\text{\rm{d}}\mathbb{P}=\int_{\Omega}g\big(B_{T}^{F,x+\varepsilon,\varepsilon}\big)\frac{\text{\rm{d}}\mu^{\varepsilon}}{\text{\rm{d}}\mu}\text{\rm{d}}\mathbb{P}=\int_{\Omega}g\big(B_{T}^{F,x+\varepsilon}\big)\text{\rm{d}}\mathbb{P}

Thus,

(6.4)

\int_{\Omega}g\big(B_{T}^{F,x+\varepsilon}\big)\text{\rm{d}}\mathbb{P}=\int_{\Omega}g\big(B_{T}^{F,x}\big)\exp\left\{-\int_{0}^{T_{0}}(R_{F})^{-1}h^{F,\varepsilon}\text{\rm{d}}B_{t}^{F}-\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{2{T_{0}}}\right\}\text{\rm{d}}\mathbb{P}.

Taking the derivative with respect to
\varepsilon
on both sides of the above equation, and let
\varepsilon=0
, we immediately get

(6.5)

\frac{\text{\rm{d}}}{\text{\rm{d}}x}\mathbb{E}\left(g(B_{T}^{F,x})\right)=\frac{1}{T_{0}}\mathbb{E}\left(g(B_{T}^{F,x})\left(\int_{0}^{T}(R_{F})^{-1}\left(\int_{0}^{t}F(t,s)\text{\rm{d}}s\right)\text{\rm{d}}B_{t}^{F,x}\right)\right).

∎

.

When
B^{F}
is the fractional Brownian motion, Theorem
6.1
cover Theorem 3.1 in
[
27
]
.

7
Martingales with respect to F-Gaussian Process

In general, the
F
-Gaussian process
(B_{t}^{F})_{t\geq 0}
is not a martingale. A natural question: Does there exists a measurable function
h(t,s)
such that the stochastic integral

M(t):=\int_{0}^{t}h(t,s)\text{\rm{d}}B_{s}^{F}

is a martingale? The following theorem
7.1
gives the positive answer.

Theorem 7.1
.

Assume that
F
satisfies conditions
(A1)
and
(A4)
, then

(7.1)

M(t)=\int_{0}^{t}\frac{\Theta(t,s)}{\zeta_{2}(s)}\text{\rm{d}}B_{s}^{F}

is a martingale with respect to the filtration generated by the
F
-Gaussian process, where
\Theta
and
\zeta_{2}
are coincide with that in the condition
(A4)
.

Proof.

According to (
7.1
) and (
3.34
) we get

\displaystyle M(t)=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}K_{F}^{*}\left(\frac{\Theta(t,s)}{\zeta_{2}(s)}1_{[0,t]}(s)\right)\text{\rm{d}}B_{s}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\left(F(s,s)\frac{\Theta(t,s)}{\zeta_{2}(s)}1_{[0,t]}(s)+\int_{s}^{T}\frac{\partial F(u,s)}{\partial u}\frac{\Theta(t,u)}{\zeta_{2}(u)}1_{[0,t]}(u)\text{\rm{d}}u\right)\text{\rm{d}}B_{s}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\left(F(s,s)\frac{\Theta(t,s)}{\zeta_{2}(s)}+\int_{s}^{t}\frac{\partial F(u,s)}{\partial u}\frac{\Theta(t,u)}{\zeta_{2}(u)}\text{\rm{d}}u\right)\text{\rm{d}}B_{s}.

By the cndition
(A4)
, we know

(7.2)

F(s,s)\frac{\Theta(t,s)}{\zeta_{2}(s)}+\int_{s}^{t}\frac{\partial F(u,s)}{\partial u}\frac{\Theta(t,u)}{\zeta_{2}(u)}\text{\rm{d}}u:=\zeta_{1}(s)

only depend on the variable
s
and is
L^{2}
-integrable. This implies that

M(t):=\int_{0}^{t}\zeta_{1}(s)\text{\rm{d}}B_{s}

is a martingale.
∎

Example 7.2
.

(1)
When
F(t,s)=K_{H}(t,s)
.
Then

M(t)=\int_{0}^{t}(t-s)^{\frac{1}{2}-H}s^{\frac{1}{2}-H}\text{\rm{d}}B_{s}^{H}

is a martingale.

(2)
When

F(t,s)=\frac{1}{\Gamma(\alpha)}f_{1}(s)\int_{s}^{t}(u-s)^{\alpha-1}f_{2}(u)\text{\rm{d}}u,

Then

M(t)=\int_{0}^{t}(t-s)^{-\alpha}f_{2}(s)^{-1}\text{\rm{d}}B_{s}^{F}

is a martingale.

(3)
When

F(t,s)=f(s),

then for any
\zeta(s)\in L^{2}([0,T])
,

M(t)=\int_{0}^{t}\zeta(s)\text{\rm{d}}B_{s}^{F}

is a martingale. In fact,
F
is independent with respect to
t
, hence

K_{F}^{*}\zeta(s)=f(s)\zeta(s)

is independent with respect to
t
.

Proof.

We only prove
(2)
. By the proof Corollary
3.7
, we know that
F
satisfies
(A1)
and
(A4)
. we obtain easily that

\Theta(t,s)=(t-s)^{-\alpha},\quad\zeta_{2}(s)=f_{2}(s),\quad\zeta_{1}(s)=\Gamma(1-\alpha)f_{1}(s).

Hence conclusions are from Theorem
7.1
.
∎

Remark 7.3
.

When
F(t,s)=K_{H}(t,s)
, Norros-Valkeila-Virtamo
[
49
]
proved that
M(t)
is a martingale by using the covariance of
M(s),M(t)
, and Hu-Nualart-Song
[

]
used
M(t)
to prove some properties of fractional martingales.

8
Applications to Mathematical Finance

In this section, we will apply the techiques developed in previous sections to Mathematical finance. It is well known that in financial derivatives pricing theory,
we use geometric Brownian motion to model stock processes. Let
S_{t}
be the stock process and satisfies the following stochastic differential equation

(8.1)

\text{\rm{d}}S_{t}=\mu S_{t}\text{\rm{d}}t+\sigma S_{t}\text{\rm{d}}B_{t},\quad S_{0}=s\in\mathbb{R}.

where
B_{t}
is the standard Brownian motion and
\mu\in\mathbb{R}
is the drift coefficient and
\sigma>0
is the diffusion
coefficient also called volatility, both are constants. By Itô formula, we know that it has a solution

(8.2)

S_{t}=S_{0}\exp\bigg\{\Big(\mu-\frac{1}{2}\sigma^{2}\Big)t^{2}+\sigma B_{t}\bigg\}.

Furthermore we consider the European call option with payoff function

(8.3)

P(S_{T})=\max(S_{T}-K,0)

where
S_{T}
is the stochastic process
S_{t}
at time
T
and constant
K\in\mathbb{R}
is the strike price. By the Black-Scholes theory, to price the derivative, we have to use the risk neutral measure and work with risk free interest rate
r
. Then we can price this derivative by the following formula

(8.4)

V(0,S_{0},K)=S_{0}N(d_{1})e^{-rT}-KN(d_{2}),

where

N(x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int^{x}_{-\infty}e^{-\frac{t^{2}}{2}dt}

and

\displaystyle d_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{\log\frac{S_{0}}{K}+rT+\frac{1}{2}\sigma^{2}T}{\sigma\sqrt{T}}

\displaystyle d_{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{\log\frac{S_{0}}{K}+rT-\frac{1}{2}\sigma^{2}T}{\sigma\sqrt{T}}.

However, derivative pricing under this assumption does not match the observations from market data. In option market, we find that option with strike price
K
has an implied volatility
\sigma
depends on
K
where in our model, the volatility
\sigma
does not depend on the strike price
K
. This dependence can be characterized as a curve called volatility skew or volatility smile. Therefore new models are being called to explain this phenomina. One of the models is the stochastic volatility model. In the stochastic volatility model, instead of using one source of uncertainty
B_{t}^{(1)}
we introduce another source of uncertainty
B_{t}^{(2)}

\displaystyle\text{\rm{d}}S_{t}

\displaystyle=rS_{t}+\sqrt{v_{t}}S_{t}\text{\rm{d}}B_{t}^{(1)},

\displaystyle\text{\rm{d}}v_{t}

\displaystyle=a(v_{t})dt+b(v_{t})\text{\rm{d}}B_{t}^{(2)},

where
a,b
are two functions to characterize the variance process of
v_{t}
,
B_{t}^{(1)},B_{t}^{(2)}
are two Brownian motions with certain correlation.

Besides the traditional Brownian motion, recently people starts to use Frational Brownian motion as the driver for
v_{t}
, namely rough volatility models. In the rough volatility model, they use fractional Brownian motion to characterize variance

(8.5)

v_{t}=v_{0}\exp\left(B^{H}_{t}\right),

where
B^{H}
is the fractional Brownian motion with respect to Hurst index of
H
,
v_{t}
starts from
v_{0}
.

Using the technique developed in this paper, we can further extend the above model

(8.6)

v_{t}=v_{0}\exp\left(B^{F}_{t}\right),

where

(8.7)

B^{F}_{t}=\int_{0}^{t}F(t,s)\text{\rm{d}}B_{s}.

Here
F(t,s)
is one of the permissible kernels defined in previous sections. We now list some of the properties of the variance processes.

Theorem 8.1
.

The variance process defined in (
8.6
) has mean

(8.8)

E(v_{t})=\exp\left\{\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}F^{2}(t,s)\text{\rm{d}}s\right\}

and has Malliavin Derivative

(8.9)

D_{s}v_{t}=v_{t}F(t,s).

And further more we have

(8.10)

D_{s}\left(\frac{1}{v_{t}}\right)=-\frac{1}{v_{t}}F(t,s)

Proof.

For each fixed
t>0
, consider the stochastic process

M_{u}:=\int_{0}^{u}F(t,s)\text{\rm{d}}B_{s},\quad 0\leq u\leq t.

Then
\{M_{u}:0\leq u\leq t\}
is a martingale under the filtraction generated by standard Brownian process
\{B_{u}:0\leq u\leq t\}
. Then

\exp\left\{\int_{0}^{u}F(t,s)\text{\rm{d}}B_{s}-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{u}F^{2}(t,s)\text{\rm{d}}s\right\},\quad 0\leq u\leq t

is the exponential martingale of
\{M_{u}:0\leq u\leq t\}
. Therefore for any
0\leq u\leq t
,

\mathbb{E}\left(\exp\left\{\int_{0}^{u}F(t,s)\text{\rm{d}}B_{s}-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{u}F^{2}(t,s)\text{\rm{d}}s\right\}\right)=1.

In particular, when
t=u
, that is

\mathbb{E}(v_{t})=\exp\left\{\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}F^{2}(t,s)\text{\rm{d}}s\right\}.

For the Malliavin Derivative, for any
h\in\mathbb{H}
, we have

\displaystyle D_{h}(v_{t})=

\displaystyle\frac{\text{\rm{d}}}{\text{\rm{d}}\varepsilon}\bigg|_{\varepsilon=0}\exp\left(\int_{0}^{t}F(t,s)\text{\rm{d}}(B_{s}+\varepsilon h_{s})\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lim\limits_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0}\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\left(\exp\Big(B_{t}^{F}+\varepsilon\int_{0}^{t}F(t,s)\dot{h}(s)\text{\rm{d}}s\Big)-\exp(B_{t}^{F})\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\exp(B_{t}^{F})\int_{0}^{t}F(t,s)\dot{h}(s)\text{\rm{d}}s

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}v_{t}F(t,s)\dot{h}(s)\text{\rm{d}}s=\int_{0}^{t}D_{s}(v_{t})\dot{h}(s)\text{\rm{d}}s.

That is

D_{s}(v_{t})=v_{t}F(t,s).

Similarly, we have

\displaystyle D_{h}\bigg(\frac{1}{v_{t}}\bigg)=

\displaystyle\frac{\text{\rm{d}}}{\text{\rm{d}}\varepsilon}\exp\left(-\int_{0}^{t}F(t,s)\text{\rm{d}}(B_{s}+h_{s})\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\int_{0}^{t}\frac{1}{v_{t}}F(t,s)\dot{h}(s)\text{\rm{d}}s=\int_{0}^{t}D_{s}\Big(\frac{1}{v_{t}}\Big)\dot{h}(s)\text{\rm{d}}s.

That is

D_{s}\Big(\frac{1}{v_{t}}\Big)=-\frac{1}{v_{t}}F(t,s).

Thus we have proved equations (
8.8
), (
8.9
) and (
8.10
).
∎

Consider now a derivative on variance whose payoff is
f(v_{t})
. In finance the function
f
is usually a continuous function with reasonable smoothness. For example in the case of standard call option, the function
f
takes the following form

f(x)=\max(c-x,0),c\in\mathbb{R}

We now want to price its value at time
0
. Let it’s value be called

(8.11)

P(v_{0})=\mathbb{E}\left(f(v_{t})\right),

here we want to emphasis the dependence of the function
P
on the
v_{0}
. When
f\in C^{1}_{b}
, its derivative with respect to initial value
v_{0}
can be calculated directly as

(8.12)

\frac{\text{\rm{d}}P}{\text{\rm{d}}v_{0}}=\mathbb{E}\left(f^{\prime}(v_{t})\frac{v_{t}}{v_{0}}\right).

By using integration by parts formula and Theorem
8.1
we have

(8.13)

Df(v_{t})=f^{\prime}(v_{t})Dv_{t}=f^{\prime}(v_{t})v_{t}F(t,s),

hence we have

(8.14)

f^{\prime}(v_{t})v_{t}=Df(v_{t})\frac{1}{F(t,s)}.

Usian the integration by parts formula again, we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left(f^{\prime}(v_{t})v_{t}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}(Df(v_{t})\frac{1}{F(t,s)})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left(f(v_{t})\int_{0}^{t}\frac{1}{F(t,s)}\text{\rm{d}}B_{s}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left(f(v_{t})B^{1/F}_{t}\right)

For general case, when
f
is not a
C^{1}_{b}
function, we can use the previous Bismut’s formula and can still get a nice expression of the expectation which is friendly to numerical computation.

Theorem 8.2
.

For each fixed
t>0
, we have

(8.15)

\frac{\text{\rm{d}}P}{\text{\rm{d}}v_{0}}=\frac{1}{v_{0}}\mathbb{E}\big[f(v_{t})B^{1/F}_{t}\big]

for any function
f
satisfying with
f\circ\exp(B^{F,x}_{t})\in L^{1}(\mathbb{P}).

Proof.

According to (
8.6
) and Theorem
6.1

\displaystyle\frac{\text{\rm{d}}P}{\text{\rm{d}}v_{0}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\text{\rm{d}}}{\text{\rm{d}}v_{0}}\mathbb{E}\Big(f\Big(v_{0}\exp\left(B^{F}_{t}\right)\Big)\Big)=\frac{\text{\rm{d}}}{\text{\rm{d}}v_{0}}\mathbb{E}\Big[f\Big(e^{\log v_{0}+B_{t}^{F}}\Big)\Big]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{v_{0}}\frac{\text{\rm{d}}}{\text{\rm{d}}x}\mathbb{E}\Big[f(e^{x+B_{t}^{F}})\Big]~~\Big(\text{here}~x=\log v_{0}\Big)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{v_{0}}\mathbb{E}\Big[f(v_{t})B^{1/F}_{t}\Big]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{v_{0}}\mathbb{E}\big[f(v_{t})B^{1/F}_{t}\big].

∎

This proof can reduce the smoothness requirements for function
f
which is essential for a lot of application in finance. For example, the plain vanilla call, put, digital or barrier options all have payoff functions fall into this category.
