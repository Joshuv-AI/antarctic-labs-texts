The Stringy S-matrix Bootstrap: Maximal Spin and Superpolynomial Softness
Kelian Häring, and, Alexander Zhiboedov

Abstract
We explore the space of meromorphic amplitudes with extra constraints coming from the shape of the leading Regge trajectory. This information comes in two guises: it bounds the maximal spin of exchanged particles of a given mass; it leads to sum rules obeyed by the discontinuity of the amplitude, which express the softness of scattering at high energies. We assume that the leading Regge trajectory is linear, and we derive bounds on the low-energy Wilson coefficients using the dual and primal approaches. For the graviton-graviton scattering in four dimensions, the maximal spin constraint leads to slightly more stringent bounds than those that follow from general constraints of analyticity, crossing, and unitarity. The exponential softness at high energies is manifest in our primal approach and is not used in our implementation of the dual approach. Nevertheless, we observe the agreement between the bounds obtained from both. We conclude that high-energy superpolynomial softness does not leave an obvious imprint on the low-energy observables. We exhibit a unitary three-parameter deformation of the Veneziano amplitude for the open string case. It has a novel, exponentially soft behavior at high energies and fixed angles. We generalize the previous analysis of this regime and present a stringy version of the lower bound on high-energy, fixed-angle scattering by Cerulus and Martin.

One of the manifestations of locality in quantum field theory is a polynomial behavior of scattering amplitudes at high energies

This paper explores the possible effects of the amplitudes non-polynomiality at high energies on the low-energy observables, such as the Wilson coefficients.

Open string case

The dual approach for the open string case leads to results very similar to the closed string case. Assumptions and constraints

Let us start by reviewing the standard assumptions satisfied by the tree-level (or meromorphic) scattering amplitudes. We then explain the extra constraints imposed in this work in more detail.

Here we list properties of tree-level two-to-two scattering amplitudes of massless scalar particles in four spacetime dimensions. The Mandelstam variables satisfy

Closed strings: MHV scattering of gravitons

In this section, we consider graviton scattering, which corresponds to the closed string case

Next, we want to impose that the amplitude satisfies the well-known linear Regge behavior of string theory (

(15)

As explained in Section

To explore the space of Wilson coefficients when infinitely many Regge sum rules (or superpolynomial softness) are imposed, we will turn to the primal method, where these constraints are built-in. c^{++}_{n,J}\geq 0\,,\quad c^{+-}_{n,J}\geq 0\,\ .

(21)

These constraints restrict the allowed space of
\alpha
’s. The space of amplitudes defined in this way is still infinite-dimensional and we will study its projection on the space of a few leading low-energy Wilson coefficients.

In order to explore the space of amplitudes numerically, we consider a truncated ansatz

\displaystyle f_{N_{\text{max}}}(s|t,u)

\displaystyle=-8\pi G_{N}\frac{\Gamma(-s)\Gamma(-t)\Gamma(u)}{\Gamma(1+s)\Gamma(1+t)\Gamma(1+u)}{}

\displaystyle+\sum_{c_{s},c_{tu}=0}^{N_{\text{max}}}\sum_{d_{s},d_{tu}=1}^{2N_{\text{max}}}\theta_{\alpha_{\text{ind}}}(\alpha)\,\alpha_{c_{s},c_{tu},d_{s},d_{tu}}\frac{\Gamma(c_{s}-s)\Gamma(c_{tu}-t)\Gamma(c_{tu}-u)}{\Gamma(d_{s}+s)\Gamma(d_{tu}+t)\Gamma(d_{tu}+u)}\,,

(22)

where the limit of the sum is chosen such that the residues are polynomial and the Regge limit satisfies (
15
). This requires additional constraints on
\alpha
’s that we impose by inserting
\theta_{\alpha_{\text{ind}}}(\alpha)
which is
1
when
\alpha\in\alpha_{\text{ind}}
and zero otherwise, it removes remaining dependent terms, see Appendix
B
. This is performed in two steps. First, we realized that terms with
c_{t}\neq c_{u}
and
d_{t}\neq d_{u}
are redundant and thus can be removed from the sum as in (
22
). Second, we solve for the remaining dependence order by order in
N_{\text{max}}
. All in all, this ansatz contains
3N_{\text{max}}^{2}+N_{\text{max}}-2
free parameters. In what follows, we will explore
primal
bound on Wilson coefficients numerically. This procedure will be explained in detail next in Section
3.2
.

However, before going further, let us remind the reader that already in string theory, there exists a nontrivial
\alpha
solution for
f(s|t,u)
in (
3.1
), namely the scattering of gravitons in heterotic string theory
Kawai:1985xq
, where the MHV amplitude is

f^{(hs)}(s|t,u)=-8\pi G_{N}\frac{\Gamma(-s)\Gamma(-t)\Gamma(-u)}{\Gamma(1+s)\Gamma(1+t)\Gamma(1+u)}\left(1-\frac{tu}{1+s}\right)

(23)

which is simply generated from the ansatz with one nontrivial term
\alpha_{0,(1,1),2,(1,1)}=8\pi G_{N}
. We know that this amplitude satisfies all the constraints, the space of
\alpha
’s is thus nontrivial.

3.2
Primal algorithm

Here we describe the numerical primal algorithm used to find extremal stringy gravitational amplitudes and derive bounds on Wilson coefficients.

Provided the ansatz (
22
), it is clear that the low-energy Wilson coefficients are linear combinations of the
\alpha
’s. Explicitly they take the form

a_{k,j}=-x_{0}8\pi G_{N}+\sum_{c_{s},c_{tu}=0}^{N_{\text{max}}}\sum_{d_{s},d_{tu}=1}^{2N_{\text{max}}}x_{c_{s},c_{tu},d_{s},d_{tu}}\alpha_{c_{s},c_{tu},d_{s},d_{tu}}\,,

(24)

where
x_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
and can be computed by expanding the ansatz at low energy. Similar relations hold for
|\beta_{R^{3}}|^{2}
and
|\beta_{\phi}|^{2}
. The same is true for the partial wave coefficients
c^{++}_{n,J},\,c^{+-}_{n,J}
. The procedure of bounding Wilson coefficients can thus be efficiently implemented using Linear Programming, and in practice, we used SDPB
Simmons-Duffin:2015qma
;
Landry:2019qug
.
6
6

6

We also tried linear solvers such as GLPK and Gurobi
gurobi
. However, we observed that as we increase
N_{\text{max}}
, high precision was needed and we turned to SDPB, where arbitrary precision can be used.
When imposing unitarity, this cannot be done numerically for all
n,J
, therefore we truncate the number of constraints by imposing

c^{++}_{n,J}\geq 0\,,\quad c^{+-}_{n,J}\geq 0\,,\quad\forall\,n\leq n_{\text{max}}\,,\,\forall J\,.

(25)

As we will see, in practice, the convergence in
n_{\text{max}}
is fast. We impose the constraint for all spins, and due to the linearity of the Regge trajectory, the number of constraints scales as
\mathcal{O}(n_{\text{max}}^{2})
. In practice, we computed the coefficients
c_{n,J}^{++},\,c_{n,J}^{+-}
for each term in (
22
) using (
19
) and (
20
).

We now give an example of the procedure of maximizing the quantity
A
(
A
is any ratio of Wilson coefficients and in the gravitational case we can normalize everything to
8\pi G_{N}
)

(a)

At fixed
N_{\text{max}}
for the ansatz (
22
), we maximize
A
by increasing the number of unitarity constraints
n_{\text{max}}
. Experimentally, the extremal value converges to a plateau for
n_{\text{max}}\gtrsim 2N_{\text{max}}+10
.

(b)

We extremize
A
for increasing size of the ansatz
N_{\text{max}}
. For each fixed
N_{\text{max}}
, the resulting amplitude satisfies all the constraints.

(c)

We fit the extremal
A
vs
N_{\text{max}}
and when possible, we extrapolate to
N_{\text{max}}\to\infty
. As we will see, is it not always clear that
A
converges to a finite value.
The converged value is then a
primal
bound on
A
.

It is straightforward to extend this algorithm to explore a higher-dimensional space of parameters. This is done by fixing
(A_{1},\dots,A_{n})
and maximizing
A_{0}
.

3.3
Bounds on Wilson coefficients

In this section, we present bounds on various ratios of Wilson coefficients.

In section Section

we consider bounds on the Wilson coefficients normalized to
G_{N}
.
Some of the bounds of this type are known to suffer from IR divergencies in four dimensions. The simplest example concerns the correction to the graviton three-point coupling which is bounded as

\frac{|\beta_{R^{3}}|^{2}}{8\pi G_{N}}\lesssim\frac{\log(M_{\text{HS}}L_{\text{IR}})}{M_{\text{HS}}^{4}}\,,

Then we consider various bounds on Wilson coefficients

; in the Regge limit, it takes the form (
1

(37)

where as before we set

The main difference compared to the case of closed strings is that we find that unitarity excludes any ansatz with a finite number of satellite terms, see Appendix
D
. Due to this fact, we were not able to set up a systematic primal bootstrap scheme to derive bounds on Wilson coefficients for the open string case.

There are however nontrivial solutions with infinitely many satellite terms which satisfy unitarity.
We find a three-parameter family of such unitarity amplitudes which can be represented through a simple worldsheet integral. They exhibit novel high-energy, fixed-angle behavior.

4.1
Ansatz

As before we consider the amplitudes with exactly equidistant spectrum
m^{2}=n
, polynomial residues, that satisfy crossing. For the open string case where the amplitude only has poles in the
s
- and
t
-channels we get the following ansatz

\displaystyle T(s,t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{c_{s},c_{t}=0}^{\infty}\sum_{c_{u}=\max(c_{s},c_{t})}^{c_{s}+c_{t}}\alpha_{c_{s},c_{t},c_{u}}\frac{\Gamma(c_{s}-s)\Gamma(c_{t}-t)}{\Gamma(c_{u}-s-t)}\,,\quad\alpha_{c_{s},c_{t},c_{u}}=\alpha_{c_{t},c_{s},c_{u}}

(38)

where the lower bound on
c_{u}
comes from imposing the Regge behavior
s^{t+(c_{s}-c_{u})}
and the upper bound from imposing that residues are polynomials.

Not all the terms in the ansatz above are independent. Eliminating the redundancies, we can write a simpler ansatz

\displaystyle T(s,t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\sum_{k=0}^{i}b_{ik}\frac{\Gamma(i-s)\Gamma(i-t)}{\Gamma(i+k-s-t)},

(39)

which was considered by Khuri in
khuri1969derivation
.

The term
k=i=0
corresponds to the Veneziano amplitude. Notice that the amplitude (
39
) automatically satisfies the maximal spin constraint. The reason is that taking the discontinuity in
s
automatically truncates the sum over
i
and one can trivially check that
T_{s}\sim s^{t}
. In Appendix
E
, we argue that this ansatz is complete for amplitudes with an equidistant spectrum and linear trajectories.

4.2
Regge sum rules

Checking RSR for the amplitude (
39
) is more subtle. Here we can distinguish two cases: when the sum over
i
truncates to
\leq N_{\text{max}}
; when the sum over
i
goes all the way to infinity.

Let us first discuss the case
i\leq N_{\text{max}}
. In this case, superpolynomial softness is trivial because for given
i
and
k
, the amplitude behaves in the Regge limit as
T(s,t)\sim s^{t-k}
.
However, we show in Appendix
D
that all such amplitudes violate unitarity. Thus, we conclude that
no unitary deformations of the Veneziano amplitude with
i\leq N_{\text{max}}
exist.
This makes the method used to derive bounds on Wilson coefficients in the previous section inapplicable, see Section
3.2
.

Next, we consider the case when the sum over
i
goes all the way to infinity. In this case, a class of unitary deformation was recently found by Cheung and Remmen in
Cheung:2023adk
.
In particular, they found an amplitude depending on the parameter
r

T_{\text{CR}}(s,t)=\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}{1\over i!}{r\over r+i}\frac{\Gamma(i-s)\Gamma(i-t)}{\Gamma(i-s-t)}=\frac{\Gamma(-s)\Gamma(-t)}{\Gamma(-s-t)}{}_{3}F_{2}(-s,-t,r;-s-t,1+r;1)

(40)

which for
r=0
reduces to the Veneziano amplitude. Unitarity imposes an additional constraint on
r
. For example, in
d=4
, it requires that
r\geq-1/2
. In the Regge limit, this deformation takes the form

\displaystyle T_{\text{CR}}(s,t)=s^{t}+{r\over(1+t)s}+...\ ,

(41)

where the second term explicitly violates RSR. The mechanism by which this term emerges is interesting: the Regge limit and sum over
i
above do not commute. Therefore even though each term in the sum (
40
) satisfies RSR, the full amplitude given by an infinite sum does not.

To the best of our knowledge, the existence of unitary deformations of the Veneziano amplitudes that satisfy RSR has not been explicitly demonstrated so far, and it is what we will show next. We will not try to be exhaustive and it would be very interesting to classify all such deformations. We leave this problem for future work.

Unitary amplitudes

High-energy, fixed-angle scattering

Here we consider high-energy

The result (

•

The asymptotic Regge limit assumption

•

We see therefore that already at the level of amplitudes with equidistant spectrum and exactly linear Regge trajectories, the result of

5
Conclusions

Charting out the space of stringy tree-level amplitudes is largely an open problem. Among other things, this space is important because it contains large
N
QCD and weakly coupled UV completions of Einstein gravity.

In this paper, we developed the S-matrix approach to this problem. We utilized extra knowledge about the leading Regge trajectory which we considered to be linear. On one hand, it puts an upper bound on the maximal spin of exchanged particles at a given mass.
On the other hand, scattering amplitudes in this class exhibit superpolynomial softness: they decay faster than a polynomial at high energies and fixed angles. This condition can be conveniently restated as an infinite set of Regge sum rules (
12
) that the discontinuity of the amplitude has to obey.

Our basic conclusion is that superpolynomial softness does not lead to any obvious low-energy imprint as exhibited by the low-energy Wilson coefficients. In contrast to that, the maximal spin constraint leads to slightly more stringent bounds compared to the standard bootstrap scheme based on causality and unitarity.
13
13

13

Strictly speaking, to strengthen this conclusion it would be desirable to extend our primal ansatzes both for the closed and open string cases. For the closed string case, it would be interesting to construct amplitudes that do not satisfy RSR more systematically. For example, including terms considered in
Veneziano:2017cks
would be an obvious way to do it. For the open string case, on the opposite, we would like to have a better understanding of the landscape of the amplitudes that do satisfy RSR, beyond the example considered in the present paper.

Let us provide a simple, intuitive explanation of these results. The low-energy Wilson coefficients are dominated by the contribution from the lightest degrees of freedom that were integrated out. The maximal spin constraint puts a bound on the maximal spin of these lightest massive degrees of freedom and thus affects the low-energy Wilson coefficients. The UV softness, however, can kick in at energies
s_{\text{UV}}\gg m_{\text{gap}}^{2}
and therefore leave very little imprint on the low-energy observables. It is not obvious that it is possible to construct amplitudes with the separation of these two scales (the mass gap, and the energy scale at which the UV soft behavior becomes visible). For example, in the standard string amplitudes, the UV softness can be already seen at energies
s_{\text{UV}}\sim m_{\text{gap}}^{2}
and not just asymptotically. Our primal ansatz achieves precisely that: it delays the kick-in of the UV soft behavior to higher energies and thus effectively hides it from a low-energy experimentalist.

In the statements above, we effectively assumed that properties of the leading Regge trajectory
j(t)
for positive and negative
t
are unrelated. It is quite probable that this is not the case. For example, in all known examples,
j(t)
is a convex function of
t
.

For the closed string case, we focused on the MHV scattering of gravitons in four dimensions with the leading Regge trajectory

We put forward a primal bootstrap scheme, see Section

We also observed an approximate agreement between the primal and the dual bounds. This fact is interesting because in our dual implementation, only a finite number of RSR could be added and they are not used in the numerics. Our primal ansatz, on the other hand, satisfies infinitely many RSR.

For the open string case, we considered the scattering of massless scalars and took the leading Regge trajectory to be

D

A.1
Dispersion relation and Wilson coefficients

We start by writing a dispersion relation for

\begin{split}f(s|t,u)&=\oint\frac{ds^{\prime}}{2\pi i}\frac{f(s^{\prime}|t,-s^{\prime}-t)}{s-s^{\prime}}=\frac{8\pi G_{N}}{stu}+|\beta_{R^{3}}|^{2}\frac{tu}{s}-|\beta_{\phi}|^{2}\frac{1}{s}\\

&\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\left.+\sum_{J=4}^{\infty}\frac{\rho_{J}^{+-}(m^{2})d_{4,4}^{J}\left(1+\frac{2t}{m^{2}}\right)}{(t+m^{2})^{4}(-s-t-m^{2})}\right)\end{split}

(61)

where we recall that the spectral densities

are the imaginary part of the partial amplitude

\displaystyle\operatorname{Im}T_{++--}(s,t,u)

\displaystyle=\sum_{J=0}^{\infty}\frac{1+(-1)^{J}}{2}\rho_{J}^{++}(m^{2})d_{0,0}^{J}\left(1+\frac{2t}{s}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{J=4}^{\infty}\rho_{J}^{+-}(m^{2})d_{4,4}^{J}\left(1+\frac{2t}{s}\right)\,,

(63)

and from unitarity

. In writing the dispersion relation for

and thus

and the arc at infinity can be dropped.

By expanding the dispersion relation (

), it is straightforward to obtain dispersive representation for the

\displaystyle a_{k,0}

\displaystyle=\left<\frac{1}{(m^{2})^{4+k}}\right>_{++}+\left<\frac{(-1)^{k}}{(m^{2})^{4+k}}\right>_{+-}

\displaystyle a_{2,1}

\displaystyle=\left<\frac{\mathcal{J}}{m^{14}}\right>_{++}+\left<\frac{22-\mathcal{J}}{m^{14}}\right>_{+-}

\displaystyle a_{4,1}

\displaystyle\left<(\dots)\right>_{+-}

(69)

to represent the moment with positive measures. It is also convenient to name the function inside the brackets

Clearly, for even

A.2
Crossing symmetry and null constraints

The function

is symmetric in
t-u
. However, the dispersion relation (
61
) is performed at fixed

\begin{split}&\left<\frac{d_{0,0}^{J}\left(1+\frac{2t}{m^{2}}\right)}{m^{6}(s-m^{2})}\right>_{++}+\left<\frac{m^{2}\,d_{4,4}^{J}\left(1+\frac{2t}{m^{2}}\right)}{(t+m^{2})^{4}(u-m^{2})}\right>_{+-}\\

By expanding (

in (

They are yet another set of null constraints. Indeed, the same function also appears in a third amplitude

\displaystyle T_{+-+-}(s,t,u)=([13]\langle 24\rangle)^{4}f(t|s,u)=t^{4}f(t|s,u)

A.3
Dual bootstrap algorithm

To derive dual bounds, we start by writing the
bootstrap equation
. Without loss of generality, we will explain the case where the Wilson coefficients are normalized by
a_{0,0}
and we set
m_{\text{gap}}=1
for clarity. We will consider here carving out a 2d region
\{g,\lambda\}
where
g,\lambda
can by any of the

. Let us define the vectors

\displaystyle\vec{v}^{++}(m^{2},J)

\displaystyle=\left(a_{0,0}^{++}(m^{2},J),g^{++}(m^{2},J),\lambda^{++}(m^{2},J),\vec{n}^{++}(m^{2},J)\right)

(77)

\displaystyle\vec{v}^{+-}(m^{2},J)

\displaystyle=\left(a_{0,0}^{+-}(m^{2},J),g^{+-}(m^{2},J),\lambda^{+-}(m^{2},J),\vec{n}^{+-}(m^{2},J)\right)

(78)

\displaystyle\vec{v}_{o}

\displaystyle=(-1,0,0,\vec{0})

(79)

\displaystyle\vec{v}_{g}

\displaystyle=(0,-1,0,\vec{0})

(80)

\displaystyle\vec{v}_{\lambda}

\displaystyle=(0,0,-1,\vec{0})

(81)

where
\vec{n}(m^{2},J)
is a vector of null constraint
\vec{n}=(\vec{\mathcal{X}},\vec{\mathcal{Y}})
and thus

\vec{0}=\left<\vec{n}^{++}(m^{2},J)\right>_{++}+\left<\vec{n}^{+-}(m^{2},J)\right>_{+-}\,.

(82)

We denote the number of null constraints used by
k_{\text{max}}
, the highest value of
k
in (
72
).
We can then write the
bootstrap equation

0=a_{0,0}\,\vec{v}_{0}+g\,\vec{v}_{g}+\lambda\,\vec{v}_{\lambda}+\left<\vec{v}^{++}(m^{2},J)\right>_{++}+\left<\vec{v}^{+-}(m^{2},J)\right>_{+-}\,.

(83)

The corresponding bootstrap problem is to find a functional
\vec{\alpha}
such that

•

\vec{\alpha}
is normalized by
\vec{\alpha}\cdot\vec{v}_{g}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}+1&,\,\text{ for upper bound}\\
-1&,\,\text{ for lower bound}\\
\end{array}\right.

•

\vec{\alpha}
maximize
\vec{\alpha}\cdot\left(\vec{v}_{o}+\frac{\lambda}{a_{0,0}}\vec{v}_{\lambda}\right)
. We call the result of this optimization
A(\lambda)
.

•

\vec{\alpha}
is positive on the spectrum:

\begin{array}[]{ll}\vec{\alpha}\cdot\vec{v}^{++}(m^{2},J)\geq 0&\text{for all }(m,J)\in\text{ spectrum}\\
\vec{\alpha}\cdot\vec{v}^{+-}(m^{2},J)\geq 0&\text{for all }(m,J)\in\text{ spectrum}\,.\end{array}

(84)

In this case, without specific spectrum assumption
(A)

\text{spectrum}:\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}(++)\text{ channel}:&~~~m\geq m_{\text{gap}},J=0,2,\dots\\
(+-)\text{ channel}:&~~~m\geq m_{\text{gap}},J=4,5,\dots\end{array}\right.

(85)

For
\vec{\alpha}
solution to the bootstrap problem, applying the functional
\vec{\alpha}
to the bootstrap equation and using linearity of the average
\left<\dots\right>
we obtain

\vec{\alpha}\cdot\left(\vec{v}_{o}+\frac{\lambda}{a_{0,0}}\vec{v}_{\lambda}\right)\pm\frac{g}{a_{0,0}}\leq 0

(86)

which leads to the two-sided bound

A^{-}(\lambda)\leq\frac{g}{a_{0,0}}\leq-A^{+}(\lambda)\,.

(87)

This procedure can be efficiently implemented in SDPB
Simmons-Duffin:2015qma
;
Landry:2019qug
. It led to all the dual bounds using only causality, unitarity, and crossing symmetry presented in this work (gray regions in our plots). This procedure can be extended to carve a 3d region as shown in Figure
9
.

In practice, we need to truncate the number of constraints in spin. However, as already observed in
Caron-Huot:2020cmc
, the convergence in spin is fast and we truncated at
J_{\text{max}}=100
.

Let us explain next how the maximal spin constraint
(B)

\sum_{J}^{\infty}\to\sum_{J}^{j(m^{2})}\,.

This change propagates all the way to the definition of the averages (

It has the effect of changing the spectrum in the constraint (

\begin{gathered}\text{spectrum with}\\[-6.0pt]
\text{the maximal spin constraint}\end{gathered}:\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}(++)\text{ channel}:&~~~m\geq m_{\text{gap}},J=0,2,\dots,j(m^{2})\\

and can be efficiently implemented in SDPB. To do so, we invert the relation

With these extra constraints on the spectrum, the convergence in the number of spin constraints is slower than by considering (

A.5
RSR as null constraints

Here we will discuss how RSR can be added to the dual method described above and why it does not change the bound in the present formalism. For simplicity, we will describe here the case of the open string but the same argument applies for closed string.

Following the same procedure as above, the Wilson coefficients can be written using dispersion relations

g_{n,\ell}=\left<\frac{2^{\ell}}{\ell!}\frac{P_{J}^{(\ell)}(1)}{m^{2n}}\right>

(91)

where
P_{J}^{(\ell)}(x)
is the
\ell
-derivative of the Legendre polynomial and the average is defined via

\displaystyle T_{s}(s,t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{J=0}^{\infty}\rho_{J}(s)P_{J}\left(1+\frac{2t}{s}\right)\,,

(92)

\displaystyle\left<\dots\right>

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\pi}\sum_{J=0}^{\infty}\int_{m_{\text{gap}}^{2}}^{\infty}\frac{dm^{2}}{m^{2}}\rho_{J}(m^{2})(\dots)\,.

(93)

Null constraints that follow crossing symmetry are easily obtained using
g_{n,\ell}=g_{n,n-\ell}
and we denote them
\mathcal{X}_{n,\ell}
.
21
21

21

See for example
Albert:2022oes
for detailed expressions. A second set of null constraints can also be obtained but does not influence the argument in this section.

Let us now write the RSR in a similar form. Starting from (
12
), we obtain

\text{RSR:}\quad\mathcal{R}_{n}(t)\equiv\left<m^{2n}P_{J}\left(1+\frac{2t}{m^{2}}\right)\right>=0\,,~~j(t)<-n,\,n\geq 2\,.

(94)

For
n=0,1
, one would pick the constant and pole of the amplitude at
s=0
. This is a new family of null constraints. Let us focus on the linear trajectory
j(t)=t
, which implies that
t<-n
. Looking at the argument of the Legendre polynomials, we see that

1+\frac{2t}{m^{2}}<-1\,,\quad\text{for}\quad-t<m^{2}<m_{\text{gap}}^{2}\,,

(95)

this region always exists for
t<-n
and
m_{\text{gap}}=1
. Using properties of the Legendre polynomials, it implies that at large
J
and any fixed
m_{*}
in this interval

\left[\mathcal{R}_{n}(t)\right](m_{*}^{2},J)\sim m_{*}^{2n}\,c^{J}(-1)^{J}\,,~~~c>1\,,

(96)

and thus grows exponentially with an oscillating sign. This contrasts with the sum rules for the Wilson coefficients (and thus also the usual null constraints
\mathcal{X}
), which grows as a polynomial in
J
. Let us see what it implies for the bootstrap algorithm. As in Appendix
A.3
, we build the vector
\vec{v}
and add one RSR constraint

\vec{v}(m^{2},J)=\left(g_{1,0}(m^{2},J),g_{n,\ell}(m^{2}.J),\vec{\chi}(m^{2},J),[\mathcal{R}_{n}(t)](m^{2},J)\right)

(97)

at large
J
, it behaves as

\vec{v}(m^{2},J)\sim(0,0,\vec{0},c^{J}(-1)^{J})+O(J^{k})\,.

(98)

Thus, the constraints on the functional
\vec{\alpha}\cdot\vec{v}(m^{2},J)\geq 0
at large
J
imposes that the last coefficient of
\vec{\alpha}
is set to zero. Thus, the constraint
\mathcal{R}_{n}(t)=0
is not used by the dual algorithm. The conclusion does not change for any finite number of RSR constraints included as the coefficient
c
in (
96
) depends on
t,m_{*}
.

When supplemented by the maximal spin constraint
(B)
, the argument presented above fails as one forbids arbitrary large spin at fixed
m_{*}
. However, it is easy to see how a single RSR cannot be used in this case as well. Let us consider the large mass behavior of (
97
). Clearly, from (
91
), all Wilson coefficients and null constraints decay at large
m^{2}
. In contrast, the RSR (
94
) grows and we get

\vec{v}(m^{2},J)\underset{m^{2}\gg 1}{\sim}\left(0,0,\vec{0},m^{2n}P_{J}\left(1+\frac{2t}{m^{2}}\right)\right)+O(m^{-2})\,.

(99)

For large but finite mass, the argument of the Legendre polynomial is
x=1-\delta<1
and oscillates in
J
. Provided that
j(m^{2})
is larger than this oscillation period, the constraint on the functional
\vec{\alpha}\cdot\vec{v}(m^{2},J)\geq 0
will also set the last coefficient of
\vec{\alpha}
to zero. Note that this mechanism is ‘softer’ as the growth is polynomial and not exponential as in (
98
). However, a single constraint still cannot be used. It is less clear that a large or infinite number of constraints (
94
) could not be used, for example, by using a single
n
and various
t
.

With infinitely many constraints included, the argument above fails, and it might be that the sum rules could be used. It is also possible that by applying a ‘smart’ functional to this constraint, they could be included (for example, by ‘smearing’ in
t
?). We leave this investigation to future work. Instead, in the present paper, we use a primal approach and build an ansatz that satisfies all constraints
R_{n}(t)
by construction.

Appendix B
Constraints on the closed string sum

In this section, we present constraints on the

22
). First, we impose that all the residues are polynomials. This implies that

\displaystyle c_{s}+c_{tu}\geq d_{tu},~~~2c_{tu}\geq d_{s}\ .

The minimal solution to these inequalities, namely

,

, and

Comment on the Regge behavior

Finally, let us comment on the Regge behavior of amplitudes presented in Table
2
. Two of the functions for the MHV amplitude presented above grow too fast in the Regge limit, namely they have the Regge intercept
j_{0}=3
. For the
tu
-pole amplitude, this can be cured by considering the triple product with different mass
\frac{1}{(M^{2}-s)(m^{2}-t)(m^{2}-u)}
. Indeed, performing the same analysis as above shows that for
M\geq m
, this amplitude is unitary. Thus we can define the improved
tu
-pole amplitude by

f_{tu-\text{pole}}^{\text{improved}}=\frac{g}{m^{2}}\frac{1}{(M^{2}-s)(m^{2}-t)(m^{2}-u)}\,,\quad M\gg m\,.

(114)

As
M\gg m
, the corrections to the Wilson coefficients are suppressed by
O(m/M)
. This amplitude now has the Regge intercept
j_{0}=2
. Since this amplitude is unitary only for
M\geq m
, a similar improvement cannot be performed for the spin
0
exchange amplitude.

It is sometimes possible to add a contact term to cure the Regge behavior (see for example
Haring:2022sdp
, Appendix A). Here, for the spin
0
, one would need to add
+\frac{\lambda^{2}}{M^{6}s}
which corresponds to the massless scalar exchange amplitude
|\beta_{\phi}|^{2}
, but taken with the wrong sign and is thus not unitary. We do not know how to ‘improve’ the spin
0
amplitude such that it satisfies unitarity and has
j_{0}\leq 2
.
How is it possible then that this amplitude lies at the boundary of the allowed region in the dual approach? First, while we have not found it, it is possible that an improvement exists such that it does not change the value of the Wilson coefficient by adding a tower of particles of mass
M\gg m
. Second, in the dual formalism, this amplitude satisfies all the sum rules written and hence is not excluded.

Appendix D
Unitarity of open string finite sums

In this section, we extend the argument of Sivers and Yellin
Sivers:1971ig
to show that any finite ansatz (
39
) cannot satisfy unitarity. The truncated ansatz reads

T_{N_{\text{max}}}(s,t)=\sum_{i=0}^{N_{\text{max}}}\sum_{k=0}^{i}b_{ik}\frac{\Gamma(i-s)\Gamma(i-t)}{\Gamma(i+k-s-t)}\,,

(115)

where by assumption
b_{ik}
are finite real coefficients.

The residue of a single term in the ansatz is

\frac{\Gamma(i-s)\Gamma(i-t)}{\Gamma(i+k-s-t)}\underset{s\to n}{\sim}-\frac{R_{n}^{(i,k)}(t)}{s-n}

(116)

where

R_{n}^{(i,k)}(t)=\frac{(-1)^{i+n}\Gamma(i-t)}{(n-i)!\Gamma(i+k-n-t)}

(117)

n-k
in

As a first step, we show that a single term with
i\neq 0
does not satisfy unitarity. To this end, we will show that in the partial wave expansion

R_{n}^{(i,k)}(t)=\sum_{J=0}^{n-k}c^{(i,k)}_{n,J}P_{J}\left(1+\frac{2t}{n}\right)\,

(118)

and
J=n-k-1

. Using, (
117

(n-i)!R_{n}^{(i,k)}(t)\Big|_{t=\frac{n}{2}(x-1)}=(-1)^{i+k}\left(\frac{n}{2}\right)^{n-k}\left[x^{n-k}+\frac{n-k}{n}(1-k-2i)x^{n-k-1}+\dots\right]\,.

Importantly,
(1-k-2i)<0
for all

and

have opposite signs. Moreover, we know that the Legendre polynomials

are expansion in odd/even powers of

for odd/even

and
x^{n-k-1}
contributes to different spin. Moreover, using that the coefficient of

\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}c^{(i,k)}_{n,n-k}&=(-1)^{i+k}\left(\frac{n}{4}\right)^{n-k}\frac{\sqrt{\pi}\Gamma(n-k+1)}{(n-i)!\Gamma\left(n-k+\frac{1}{2}\right)}\\

(120)

which makes it clear that they have opposite signs. This concludes the proof showing that a single term cannot satisfy unitarity as it requires
c^{(i,k)}_{n,J}\geq 0
.

Let us go back to a finite sum (
115
), (
117
) implies that at large

n!\,R_{n}^{(i,k)}(t)\sim n^{i}\,t^{n-k}\,,

(121)

and thus the terms with
\max(i)

(122)

(123)

We turn now to the residue at

Appendix E
Completeness argument for the open string case

Here we would like to comment on the completeness of the ansatz (
39
) following Khuri
khuri1969derivation
.
The fact that all particles live on equidistant linear Regge trajectories translates to the following statement

\displaystyle T(s,t)\sim{1\over\Gamma(1+t)\sin\pi t}\Big(a_{0}(t)(-s)^{t}+a_{1}(t)(-s)^{t-1}+...\Big)+...,

(128)

where the last
...
stands for the RSR violating contributions
{1\over s^{n}}
that vanish in the
s\to\infty
limit. The first observation is that

a_{k}(t)
are entire functions. Moreover, if we consider the residue at
t=n
it should become polynomial,
therefore

\displaystyle a_{k}(n)=0,~~~k>n.

(129)

We now consider the ansatz

\displaystyle\tilde{T}(s,t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\sum_{k=0}^{i}b_{ik}\frac{\Gamma(i-s)\Gamma(i-t)}{\Gamma(i+k-s-t)}.

(130)

The basic idea is that by choosing
b_{ik}
we can reproduce a given set of entire functions
a_{k}(t)
. For
example, for the leading one we get the following equation

\displaystyle a_{0}(t)=\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}b_{i0}{\Gamma(i-t)\over\Gamma(-t)},

(131)

where in writing (
131
) we expanded each term under the series. Eq. (
131
) expresses the entire function

a_{0}(t)
in terms of Newton polynomials with interpolating points chosen to be nonnegative integer
t=n
.

A sufficient condition for convergence for such an expansion was derived by Buck
buck1948interpolation
. Let us introduce the growth indicator of an entire function
f(t)

\displaystyle h(\theta,f)=\lim_{r\to\infty}\sup{1\over r}\log|f(re^{i\theta})|.

(132)

Then Buck has proven that the expansion (
131
) converges if

\displaystyle h(\theta,f)<\cos\theta\log(2\cos\theta)+\theta\sin\theta,~~~|\theta|<\pi/2\ .

(133)

Similar conditions hold for subleading trajectories.

We expect that (
133
) follows from consistency in the semiclassical limit
s,t\gg 1
as discussed in
Caron-Huot:2016icg
. Note that in all known examples
h(\theta,f)\leq 0
for
|\theta|<\pi/2
and therefore the bound (
133
) is trivially satisfied. Assuming this is the case, we consider next the difference

\displaystyle\delta T(s,t)=T(s,t)-\tilde{T}(s,t).

(134)

It is an entire function that vanishes at infinity. Therefore
\delta T(s,t)=0
. We do not have an analogous argument for the closed string ansatz.

Appendix F
Bound on the asymptotic form of the amplitude

We consider a stringy amplitude at large
s,t\to\infty
. We focus on the discontinuity that takes the form

\displaystyle T_{s}(s,t)=\sum_{i}\delta(s-m_{i}^{2})\sum_{J}^{j(s)}c_{i,J}P_{J}\left(1+{2t\over m_{i}^{2}}\right)\ .

(135)

The RHS is a polynomial that is characterized by a set of zeros. If we now perform an average over many poles, for example, by considering
T(s(1+i\epsilon),t(1+i\epsilon))
, it was argued in
Caron-Huot:2016icg
that it is these zeros, called the excess zeros, that control the amplitude asymptotically.

It is convenient to introduce a distribution of zeros
\rho(z,\bar{z})
and write for the asymptotic form of the amplitude

\displaystyle\log T(s,t)=c_{0}t^{k}\int d^{2}z\rho(z,\bar{z})\log\left(1-{s\over tz}\right),

(136)

where we assumed the asymptotic form of the Regge trajectory takes the form
j_{\text{asy}}(s)=c_{0}s^{k}
. Our task is then
to find the distribution of zeros that arises from the sum over Legendre polynomials with positive coefficients,
such that

\displaystyle\int d^{2}z\rho(z,\bar{z})=1,~~~\rho(z,\bar{z})\geq 0,

(137)

which satisfies crossing that takes the following form

\displaystyle\int d^{2}z\rho(z,\bar{z})\left(\beta^{k}\log\left(1-{1\over\beta z}\right)+\log\left(1-{\beta\over z}\right)\right)=0\ ,~~~\beta>0,

(138)

where we introduced
\beta=t/s
.

Moreover, the distribution of zeros should come from a positive sum of Legendre polynomials and should
correctly reproduce the Regge limit behavior. Introducing the ‘electric field’ produced by the excess zeros

\displaystyle f(\beta)\equiv\int d^{2}z{\rho(z,\bar{z})\over\beta-z},

(139)

one can show that

\displaystyle 0\leq\sqrt{\beta(1+\beta)}f(\beta)

\displaystyle<1,

(140)

\displaystyle\partial_{\beta}\left(\sqrt{\beta(1+\beta)}f(\beta)\right)

\displaystyle\geq 0\ .

(141)

In addition to that, consistency with the Regge limit implies that

\displaystyle f(\beta)=-k\log\beta\beta^{k-1}+(k+1)M_{1}\beta^{k}+...,

(142)

where
M_{1}=-\int d^{2}z\rho(z,\bar{z})z
is the dipole moment of the distribution. The asymptotic above is only
consistent with the formulas above for
k>1/2
.

F.1
Support of the distribution of excess zeros

To make further progress
Caron-Huot:2016icg
had to make an assumption on the support of the distribution of zeros
\rho(z,\bar{z})
that arises from the
sum over Legendre polynomials with positive coefficients. The first, rather weak, assumption that zeros are localized for
{\rm Re}z\leq 0

leads to an additional constraint

\displaystyle k\leq 1.

(143)

Making a stronger assumption that the zeros are located inside an ellipse that touches the real axis at
{\rm Re}z=0,-1
,
Caron-Huot:2016icg
then argued
that
k=1
and that the amplitude is given by the asymptotic limit of the Veneziano amplitude.

F.2
Extending the support of the distribution

It is clear from the results of this paper that the assumption above about the effective support of zeros is too restrictive. Let us consider the generalized Veneziano amplitude
T_{c_{0},c_{1},\lambda}
with
c_{1}\neq 0
. There is a nontrivial range of parameters for which it satisfies unitarity and crossing and takes the following
form in the asymptotic region
s,t\to\infty

\displaystyle\log T_{c_{0},c_{1},\lambda}

\displaystyle=(s+t)\log(s+t)-s\log s-t\log t{}

\displaystyle+c_{1}\Big(t\log{1\over 2}\Big(1-\tilde{\lambda}{s-t\over s+t}+\sqrt{1-\tilde{\lambda}}\sqrt{1-\tilde{\lambda}{(s-t)^{2}\over(s+t)^{2}}}\Big)+\{s\leftrightarrow t\}\Big),{}

where the second line vanishes for
\tilde{\lambda}\equiv 4\lambda(1-\lambda)=0
.

In this case the amplitude can be written as follows

\displaystyle\log T_{c_{0},c_{1},\lambda}=t\int_{{\sqrt{\tilde{\lambda}}+1\over\sqrt{\tilde{\lambda}}-1}}^{0}dx\rho(x)\log\left(1-{s\over tx}\right).

(144)

In particular, we have

\displaystyle\lim_{\tilde{\lambda}\to 1}{\sqrt{\tilde{\lambda}}+1\over\sqrt{\tilde{\lambda}}-1}\to\infty.

(145)

Therefore, we see that positive sums over Legendre polynomials consistent with the Regge limit can generate distributions of zeros that ‘spills’ arbitrarily far beyond
-1\leq x\leq 0
considered in
Caron-Huot:2016icg
.

F.3
Maximal value of the amplitude

To make some progress it is interesting to consider a dual formulation of
Caron-Huot:2016icg
. We consider the case of a linear Regge trajectory
j(t)=\alpha_{\text{asy}}^{\prime}t
,

, and we ask the following question: what is the maximal value that the amplitude can attain at the crossing-symmetric point
\log T(s,s)\leq\alpha_{{\rm max}}s

We assume that all zeros are localized along the negative real axis parameterized by
z\leq 0
and we set

so that everything only depends on
\beta={t\over s}
. Let us quickly demonstrate that such a bound exists. To do it we introduce a set of ‘null constraints’ by expanding the crossing equation around

\displaystyle\beta\log\left(1-{1\over\beta z}\right)+\log\left(1-{\beta\over z}\right)\Big|_{\beta=1-\epsilon}=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}n_{i}(z)\epsilon^{i},

(146)

such that

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{0}dz\rho(z)n_{i}(z)=0.

(147)

One can check that not all of the null constraints are linearly independent. We find that a convenient choice is to consider
(n_{1},n_{2},n_{3},n_{5},n_{7},...)

\displaystyle=\log\left(1-{1\over z}\right)-{2\over 1-z}\ ,

(148)

\displaystyle n_{2}(z)

\displaystyle={1\over 6}{1+3z\over(z-1)^{3}}\ .

(149)

\displaystyle\log T(1,1)\leq\alpha,

\displaystyle 1-{1\over\alpha}\log\left(1-{1\over z}\right)+\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}d_{i}n_{i}(z)\geq 0,~~~~z\leq 0.

(151)

Indeed, imagine that we have found a functional with this property. We can then integrate the equation above against the density of zeros to get

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{0}dz\ \rho(z)\Bigg(1-{1\over\alpha}\log\left(1-{1\over z}\right)+\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}d_{i}n_{i}(z)\Bigg)=1-{1\over\alpha}\log T(1,1)\geq 0,

It is not immediately obvious that functionals with the property (

\displaystyle 1-{1\over 2}\log\left(1-{1\over z}\right)+{1\over 2}\left(\log\left(1-{1\over z}\right)-{2\over 1-z}\right)={-z\over 1-z}\geq 0,~~~z\leq 0,

\displaystyle\log T(s,s)\leq 2s.

(154)

\displaystyle\log\prod_{i=1}^{j(t)}(s-z_{i}(t))=\int d^{2}z\rho(t,z,\bar{z})\left(\log(z-s)-\log z\right).

\displaystyle\log T(s,t)\simeq j_{\text{asy}}(t)\int d^{2}z\rho_{\text{asy}}(z,\bar{z})\log\left(1-{1\over\beta z}\right),~~~\beta={t\over s}.

\displaystyle\int d^{2}z\rho_{\text{asy}}(z,\bar{z})=1.

\displaystyle\beta\int d^{2}z\rho_{\text{asy}}(z,\bar{z})\log\left(1-{1\over\beta z}\right)=\int d^{2}z\rho_{\text{asy}}(z,\bar{z})\log\left(1-{\beta\over z}\right).

\displaystyle\log T(1,\beta)=\int d^{2}z\rho_{\text{asy}}(z,\bar{z})\log\left(1-{\beta\over z}\right).

and the discontinuity of
\partial_{\beta}\log T(1,\beta)

Appendix G
Stringy Cerulus-Martin bound

The Cerulus-Martin bound on the high-energy scattering at fixed angles
Cerulus:1964cjb
;
Tourkine:2023xtu
;
Buoninfante:2023dyd
effectively expresses Mandelstam analyticity in the following form

\displaystyle{\rm Regge}\leq({\rm Fixed\ real\ angle})\times({\rm Fixed\ complex\ angle}).

(169)

It is usually presented as a lower bound on scattering as follows

\displaystyle({\rm Fixed\ real\ angle})\geq{{\rm Regge}\over({\rm Fixed\ complex\ angle})}\ .

(170)

In nonperturbative QFT, for example in QCD, we do not have a bound on scattering at complex angles,
therefore it is not a rigorous lower bound in this case.

We would like next derive a lower bound on the scattering at physical fixed scattering angle for stringy amplitudes. Our input will be the following: an upper bound on the amplitude discussed in the section above, and the polynomial nature of the Regge limit.

We consider fixed-angle scattering so that

\displaystyle t=-{s\over 2}(1-z),

(171)

and we would like to derive a lower bound of the following type

\displaystyle\max_{|z|\leq z_{0}}|T(s,z)|\geq T_{0}(s,z).

(172)

We first start with the following simple observation

\displaystyle\max_{|z|\leq z_{0}}|T(s,z)|\geq\max_{|z|\leq z_{0}}|{\rm Im}T(s,z)|=\max_{|z|\leq z_{0}}|T_{s}(s,z)|.

(173)

Next, we notice that the discontinuity of the amplitude is simply a polynomial (as described above in (
135
))

\displaystyle T_{s}(s,z)\sim\sum_{J=0}^{j(s)}c_{i,J}P_{J}(z),~~~c_{i,J}\geq 0\ ,

(174)

and thus, it is an analytic function in the
z
-plane. To derive a lower bound, we consider the following mapping

\displaystyle w(z)={z+\sqrt{z^{2}-z_{0}^{2}}\over z_{0}}.

(175)

Under this mapping, the real-line segment
-z_{0}\leq z\leq z_{0}
is mapped into a unit circle in the
w
-plane.

We now consider three circles in the
w
-plane:
|w|=1
,
|w|=r_{2}
and
|w|=r_{3}
, such that
r_{3}>r_{2}>1
. The discontinuity of the amplitude is an analytic function in the annulus
1\leq|w|\leq r_{3}
.
We also introduce the following notation

\displaystyle M_{r}=\max_{|w|=r}|T_{s}(s,z)|.

(176)

We then have the three-circle theorem that states the following. For a function analytic inside the annulus and bounded on its boundary, we have

\displaystyle M_{r_{2}}\leq M_{1}^{1-{\log r_{2}\over\log r_{3}}}M_{r_{3}}^{{\log r_{2}\over\log r_{3}}}\ .

(177)

We choose
r_{2}
at fixed
t
such that the circle includes the Regge limit of the amplitude, which is
\sim 1
, where the equivalence relation means
‘modulo powers’. We then have

\displaystyle 1\leq M_{1}^{1-{\log w(1)\over\log r_{3}}}M_{r_{3}}^{{\log w(1)\over\log r_{3}}},

(178)

where we set
r_{2}=w(1)
which is its leading large
s
behavior.

We next rewrite this bound as follows

\displaystyle M_{1}\geq(M_{r_{3}})^{-{\log w(1)\over\log r_{3}}/(1-{\log w(1)\over\log r_{3}})},

(179)

where the LHS is related to fixed-angle scattering for physical angles, whereas the RHS is related to scattering at complex angles.

We next notice the following simple fact

\displaystyle\Big|\sum_{J}^{j(s)}c_{i,J}P_{J}(z)\Big|\leq\sum_{J}^{j(s)}c_{i,J}\Big|P_{J}(z)\Big|\leq\sum_{J}^{j(s)}c_{i,J}P_{J}(\sqrt{|z|^{2}+1}),~~~|z|\geq 1,

(180)

where we used that
c_{i,J}\geq 0
. We then get

\displaystyle M_{r_{3}}\leq T_{s}(s,\sqrt{z_{*}^{2}+1}),

(181)

where

\displaystyle z_{*}\equiv\max_{|w|=r_{3}}|z(w)|=z_{0}{1+r_{3}^{2}\over 2r_{3}}.

(182)

Next, we use the bound discussed in the previous section (
155
) to get

\displaystyle T_{s}(s,z)

\displaystyle\lesssim e^{\alpha_{\text{asy}^{\prime}}sf_{\text{Ven}}(z)},{}

\displaystyle f_{\text{Ven}}(z)

\displaystyle\equiv{z+1\over 2}\log{z+1\over 2}-{z-1\over 2}\log{z-1\over 2},

(183)

where we are working modulo power-like corrections.

Combining all the inequalities above, we get that for any
r_{3}

\displaystyle\max_{|z|\leq z_{0}}|T(s,z)|\geq(e^{\alpha_{\text{asy}^{\prime}}sf_{\text{Ven}}(\sqrt{z_{*}^{2}+1})})^{-{\log w(1)\over\log r_{3}}/(1-{\log w(1)\over\log r_{3}})}.

(184)

To optimize the bound, we would like to maximize the RHS. We find that the maximum is attained at
r_{3}=\infty
which finally gives

\displaystyle\max_{|z|\leq z_{0}}|T(s,z)|\gtrsim e^{-\alpha_{\text{asy}^{\prime}}s\log{1+\sqrt{1-z_{0}^{2}}\over z_{0}}}\ .

(185)

This constitutes a stringy generalization of the Cerulus-Martin lower bound on scattering at fixed angles. In the main text, we discussed the relationship between the asymptotic Regge trajectory
j_{\text{asy}}\simeq\alpha_{\text{asy}}t
, and the leading Regge trajectory
j(t)\simeq\alpha^{\prime}t
, and argued that
\alpha^{\prime}_{\text{asy}}\leq\alpha^{\prime}
. We can, therefore, write the bound in terms of the leading Regge trajectory

\displaystyle\max_{|z|\leq z_{0}}|T(s,z)|\gtrsim e^{-\alpha^{\prime}s\log{1+\sqrt{1-z_{0}^{2}}\over z_{0}}}\ ,

(186)

which is the bound we quoted in the main text.

Let us comment on the following technical subtlety in the argument above. Strictly speaking, the imaginary part we considered above in (
174
) is
\propto\delta(s-m^{2})
, and therefore our bound above directly applies to the residues of the amplitude only. The standard way to cure this problem (as well as to use the bound (
186
)) is to take the high-energy limit away from the real axis
s\to s(1+i\epsilon)
. We expect that in this limit, the imaginary part of the amplitude is still effectively given by (
174
), see
Caron-Huot:2016icg
for the discussion of this point, and therefore our arguments apply.
