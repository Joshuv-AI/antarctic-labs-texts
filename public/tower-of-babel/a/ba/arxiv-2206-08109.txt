Viscous Flow in a 1D Spin-Polarized Fermi Gas: the Role of Integrability on Viscosity
Jeff Maki, Shizhong Zhang

Abstract
The transport properties of one-dimensional Fermi gases at low-temperatures are often described by the Luttinger liquid (LL) model. However, to study dissipation one needs to examine interactions beyond the LL model. In this work we provide a simple model which allows for a direct microscopic calculation of the bulk viscosity, namely the one dimensional spin polarized p-wave Fermi gas. We calculate the bulk viscosity in both the high- and low-temperature limits. We find that the bulk viscosity is finite and consistent with the requirement of scale symmetry, in spite of the inherent integrability of the microscopic model. We argue how integrability does not forbid a finite bulk viscosity, and compare our work to previous kinetic theory calculations.

Comparison to Traditional Kinetic Theory.—

Eqs. (
8
-
11
) were calculated using the microscopic definition in Eq. (
2
). As one can see these results are consistent with conformal symmetry and provide sensible results at finite and zero temperature. However, our results disagree with previous calculations using kinetic theory
Matveev17
;
DeGottardi20
.

In the kinetic theory approach, the bulk viscosity is calculated by examining the effects of three-body interactions in a 1D spin polarized Fermi gas subjected to a slowly varying velocity gradient. According to standard arguments, the two-body scattering only leads to forward and backward scattering and can’t relax the momentum, hence three-body processes are needed
Khodas07
;
Ristivojevic16
. This argument leads to a bulk viscosity
\zeta\propto k_{F}^{5}\ell^{-2}T^{-3}
at low-temperatures. The kinetic theory then predicts a divergent bulk viscosity in the weakly interacting limit at low-temperatures which is inconsistent with the requirement of conformal symmetry
Son07
. A similar analysis at high-temperatures for zero-range p-wave interactions gives a vanishing bulk viscosity for arbitrary scattering volume because the real part of the quasiparticle self-energy leads only to the renormalization of the chemical potential and the effective mass.

It is then important to ask why the previous kinetic theory calculations are inadequate for evaluating the bulk viscosity. Recently studies have shown that the kinetic approach is deficient in calculating the bulk viscosity, while it is accurate at describing the other transport quantities
Fujii20
. The kinetic theory approach misses an important piece of the bulk-viscosity due to the correlation of pairs, as indicated explicitly in Eq. (
2
). Such correlation effects do not occur at the single-particle level, and is absent in the standard kinetic theory calculation
Enss_Private
.

A Modified Kinetic Theory Approach.—

From our response function calculations, we know the bulk viscosity arises solely from the two-body interactions,
V_{+}
, which has been previously ignored

. In principle, one may ask whether a kinetic theory calculation including

can produce qualitatively correct results. Consider the following Hamiltonian of fermionic quasiparticles from the LL model:

\displaystyle H_{0}

\displaystyle=\sum_{r=\pm 1}\int dx:\psi^{\dagger}_{r}(x)\left(-irv\partial_{x}\right)\psi_{r}(x)

\displaystyle+\frac{\tilde{\eta}_{-}}{2}\partial_{x}\psi^{\dagger}(x)\partial_{x}\psi(x)\colon

(12)

\displaystyle V_{+}

\displaystyle=\tilde{\eta}_{+}\int dx\sum_{r}\colon ir\psi^{\dagger}_{r}(x)\overleftrightarrow{\partial_{x}}\psi_{r}(x)\psi_{\bar{r}}^{\dagger}(x)\psi_{\bar{r}}(x)\colon

(13)

where

We then follow the calculation in Ref.

is the fermionic quasiparticle dispersion,
\Delta\mu=\mu(T)-\mu(0)
is the residual chemical potential at finite

\dot{n}_{p}^{0}(t)
is formally of order
\partial_{x}u

Now, the crucial question is to determine the value of

\frac{1}{\tau}\approx A\frac{\tilde{\eta}_{+}^{2}T^{3}}{v^{4}}

is some constant and we have ignored the

Given

\dot{n}_{p}^{0}=n_{p}^{0}(1-n_{p}^{0})\frac{3v^{2}p^{2}-\pi^{2}T^{2}}{6\tilde{\eta}_{-}^{-1}v^{2}T}\left(n\partial_{n}\ln(\nu)\right)\partial_{x}u

and

\zeta\approx Cmk_{F}\ell^{2}T+O(T^{2})

for some constant

This result is encouraging because it is consistent with scale symmetry. However, we have assumed that the two-body scattering can lead to a relaxation of the quasiparticle distribution function. It is still unknown how these results could arise from a proper kinetic theory calculation, especially since the bulk viscosity is determined by multi-particle correlations. On the other hand, it is also important to understand within the response function formalism, how three-body interactions can affect the bulk viscosity.

Discussions.—

We have shown in this Letter that there is indeed a bulk viscosity for a Galilean invariant integrable model, the spin polarized Fermi gas in 1D. This is interesting as naively one associates integrability with divergent transport physics. A natural question is to ask whether the dual of this model, the Lieb Liniger model of interacting bosons
Girardeau60
;
Cheon99
;
Lieb63
;
Valiente21
, also has a finite bulk viscosity.

From the virial expansion we can establish explicit duality relations between the fermionic bulk viscosity
\zeta_{F}(\ell)
and the bosonic counterpart
\zeta_{B}(a)
at high-temperatures (see SM):

\zeta_{F}(\ell)=\zeta_{B}\left(\frac{1}{a}\right).

(18)

For these two models, the duality is of the strong-to-weak variety, mapping the strongly interacting bulk viscosity of one gas to the weakly interacting limit of the other. We do not expect the temperature to affect the duality relation. As a result, we can deduce that the bulk viscosity of the Lieb-Liniger model near resonance and at low-temperatures is given by Eq. (
8
) with the replacement of
\ell
with
a^{-1}
via Eq. (
18
). Similarly, the bulk viscosity for the spin-polarized Fermi gas near resonance and at low-temperatures can be determined by evaluating the bulk viscosity for a weakly interacting Bose gas in 1D. This gives a bulk viscosity consistent with Eq. (
9
) where
\bar{\zeta}(x)=x^{4}
, i.e. the bulk viscosity near resonance diverges as
T^{-4}

Tanaka22b
.

(35)

This leads us to the final result that the bulk viscosity spectral function is given by:

\zeta(\omega)=\frac{\chi_{\Pi\Pi}(\omega)-\chi_{\Pi\Pi}(0)}{i\omega}

(36)

Linearization and Bosonization of the Spin-Polarized Fermi Gas

In order to describe the low-temperature and low-energy properties of the system, we follow the standard procedure of linearizing the fermionic operators around the Fermi surface and bosonizing the result
Giamarchi

\displaystyle\psi(x)

\displaystyle H_{LL}

In this representation the density fluctuations are given by:

n(x)=-\sqrt{\frac{\mathcal{K}}{2\pi}}\sum_{r=\pm 1}r\partial_{x}\varphi_{r}(x)

Similarly, we define the current and stress-tensor according to:

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\sum_{r}-\sqrt{\frac{\mathcal{K}}{2\pi}}\left[v\partial_{x}\varphi_{r}(x)\right.

\lim_{\ell\to 0}\Pi=\lim_{\ell\to 0}\int dx\Pi(x)=\frac{2H}{m}.

V
Calculation of the Bulk Viscosity via Stress-Tensor

Next we calculate the bulk viscosity Eq. (
36
) in both the low- and high-temperature limits. At low-temperatures, the physics is described by the LL model presented in Eqs. (
54
-
58
). In order to calculate the bulk viscosity, we consider the trace of the stress-tensor:

\displaystyle\Pi(t)=\sqrt{\frac{\mathcal{K}}{2\pi}}

\displaystyle\left[\frac{4\sqrt{2\pi}}{6}\left(3\tilde{\eta}_{-}+\tilde{\eta}_{+}\right)H\right.

\displaystyle\left.-\frac{2v\sqrt{2\pi}\tilde{\eta}_{+}}{6}\sum_{r}\int dx\left(\partial_{x}\varphi_{r}(x,t)\partial_{x}\varphi_{\bar{r}}(x,t)\right.\right.

\displaystyle\left.\left.-\left(\partial_{x}\varphi_{r}(x,t)\right)^{2}\right)\right]

(64)

Since the commutator between the Hamiltonian and any operator vanishes in thermal equilibrium, one only needs to worry about the second line of Eq. (
64
) in calculating Eq. (
36
). To explicitly calculate Eq. (
36
) we use finite temperature field theory. In doing so we replace the retarded correlation function in Eq. (
26
) with the imaginary time-ordered Green’s function and evaluate it to leading order in perturbation theory. The result is:

\displaystyle\chi_{\Pi,\Pi}(q,i\omega_{n})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{K}\frac{v^{2}\tilde{\eta}_{+}^{2}}{9}\sum_{r}\frac{1}{\beta}\sum_{i\eta_{n}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{dp}{2\pi}

\displaystyle\left[D_{r}(p,i\eta_{n})D_{\bar{r}}(-p,-i\eta_{n}+i\omega_{n})\right.

\displaystyle\left.+3D_{r}(p,i\eta_{n})D_{r}(-p,-i\eta_{n}+i\omega_{n})\right]

(65)

where the bare bosonic propagator,
D_{r}(q,i\omega_{n})
is given by:

D_{r}(q,i\omega_{n})=\frac{-rq}{i\omega_{n}-rvq}

(66)

After performing the frequency summation in Eq. (
65
) only one term survives:

\displaystyle\chi_{\Pi,\Pi}(q,i\omega_{n})

\displaystyle=-\mathcal{K}\frac{v^{2}\tilde{\eta}_{+}^{2}}{9}\sum_{r}

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{dp}{2\pi}\frac{p^{2}}{i\omega_{n}-2rvp}\left(1+2n_{B}(rvp)\right)

(67)

where
n_{b}(x)=\left[\exp(\beta x)-1\right]^{-1}
is the Bose-Einstein distribution.

The bulk viscosity can now be evaluated by substituting the result of Eq. (
67
) into Eq. (
36
) and then performing the analytic continuation,
i\omega_{n}\to\omega+i\delta
. This procedure gives the following expression for the bulk viscosity:

\zeta(\omega)\approx\mathcal{K}\frac{\tilde{\eta}_{+}^{2}}{72v}\frac{\omega}{\tanh\left(\frac{\beta\omega}{4}\right)}

(68)

In the weakly interacting limit this gives:

\zeta(\omega)\approx\frac{m\ell^{2}k_{F}}{8\pi^{2}}\frac{\omega}{\tanh\left(\frac{\omega}{4T}\right)}

(69)

where we note that the definition of
\Pi
used in this work, Eq. (
62

m
. Thus we have added an additional factor of
m^{2}
, which is correct to leading order in
k_{F}\ell
.

VI
Calculation of the Bulk Viscosity Using Microscopic Theory

In this section we present a derivation of the bulk viscosity in the high temperature limit by using the full microscopic theory. We begin with Eq. (
36
). Although the bulk viscosity is defined in terms of the stress-tensor correlation function, we note that the commutator of the Hamiltonian with any operator vanishes in thermal Equilibrium. Following Eq. (
28
), the bulk viscosity can also be defined in terms of the the contact correlation function
Nishida19
;
Maki20b
;
Enss20
;
Hofmann20
:

\zeta(\omega)=\frac{Im\left[\chi_{C,C}(\omega)-\chi_{C,C}(0)\right]}{\ell^{2}\omega}

(70)

where:

\chi_{C,C}(\omega)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dte^{i(\omega+i\delta)t}i\theta(t)\langle\left[C_{\ell}(t),C_{\ell}(0)\right]\rangle

(71)

and the contact operator
C_{\ell}
is given by Eq. (
29
).

The evaluation of Eq. (
71
) can be calculated using finite temperature field theory. According to the virial expansion, the leading contribution to Eq. (
70
) comes from the pair-pair propagator:

\displaystyle\zeta(\omega)\approx\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}

\displaystyle\frac{dQ}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{dx}{\pi}\frac{n_{B}(x)-n_{B}(x+\omega)}{\ell^{2}\omega}

\displaystyle Im\left[\frac{T(Q,x-i\delta)}{4}\right]Im\left[\frac{T(Q,x+\omega-i\delta)}{4}\right]

(72)

with
n_{B}(x)
is the Bose-Einstein distribution at temperature
T
, and
T(Q,z)
is the T-matrix, Eq. (
20
).

In the high temperature limit the chemical potential is large and negative, and an expansion in terms of the fugacity,
z=e^{\beta\mu}
, is permissible. In the high-temperature limit one finds:

\displaystyle\zeta(\omega)

\displaystyle\approx z^{2}\frac{1-e^{-\beta\omega}}{\ell^{2}\omega}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{dQ}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{dx}{\pi}e^{-\beta\frac{Q^{2}}{4}-\beta x}

\displaystyle Im\left[\frac{T(x-i\delta)}{4}\right]Im\left[\frac{T(x+\omega-i\delta)}{4}\right]

(73)

and

\displaystyle\left(\frac{T(x-i\delta)}{4}\right)^{-1}=\frac{1}{\ell}+\sqrt{-x+i\delta}

(74)

In the weakly interacting limit one finds:

\zeta(\omega\to 0)\approx\frac{2}{\pi^{5/2}}\left(k_{F}\ell\right)^{2}T^{1/2}

(75)

To obtain Eq. (
75
) we have used the equation of state to relate the fugacity to the density,
n
, and the Fermi momentum,
k_{F}
:

\displaystyle z

\displaystyle=n\sqrt{2\pi\beta}

\displaystyle n

\displaystyle=\frac{k_{F}}{\pi}

(76)

Conversely, in the resonantly interacting limit,
\ell^{-1}=0
, one obtains:

\displaystyle\lim_{\ell^{-1}\to 0}\lim_{\omega\to 0}\zeta(\omega)

\displaystyle\approx 2\frac{k_{F}^{2}}{\pi^{5/2}}\frac{1}{\ell^{2}}\frac{1}{T^{3/2}}\ln\left(m\ell^{2}T\right)

(77)

\displaystyle\lim_{\omega\to 0}\lim_{\ell^{-1}\to 0}\zeta(\omega)

\displaystyle\approx 2\frac{k_{F}^{2}}{\pi^{5/2}}\frac{1}{\ell^{2}}\frac{1}{T^{3/2}}\ln\left(\frac{4T}{\omega}\right)

(78)

We briefly note that Eq. (
72
) also describes the low-temperature bulk viscosity to leading order in perturbation theory. A direct evaluation of Eq. (
72
) using the microscopic model at low-temperatures gives results consistent with Eq. (
69
) in the zero-frequency limit. However, one needs to use the two-body T-matrix in the presence of the many-body background. We won’t provide the details here, but provide a similar analysis for the Lieb-Liniger model below. This further confirms that the LL model provides an accurate evaluation of the bulk viscosity.

VII
Calculation of the Scattering rate in Kinetic Theory

In this section we consider using a pseudo kinetic theory to evaluate the bulk viscosity. In this approach we examine the response of Fermionic quasiparticles to a two-body dephasing process beyond the LL model. Although these processes do not physically relax the momentum, we find results consistent with the more microscopic definition of the bulk viscosity. This approach is in contrast to previous studies where the bulk viscosity is explicitly related to three-body processes which do relax the momentum of the system
Matveev17
;
DeGottardi20
, but provide answers inconsistent with conformal symmetry
Son07
.

Consider the following Hamiltonian for fermionic quasiparticles:

\displaystyle H

\displaystyle=H_{0}+V_{+}

\displaystyle H_{0}

\displaystyle=\sum_{r}\int dx\colon\psi^{\dagger}_{r}(x)\left(-irv\partial_{x}\right)\psi_{r}(x)+\frac{1}{\tilde{m}}\partial_{x}\psi^{\dagger}(x)\partial_{x}\psi(x)\colon

\displaystyle V_{+}

\displaystyle=\int dx\sum_{r}\colon ir\eta_{+}\psi^{\dagger}_{r}(x)\frac{\overleftarrow{\partial_{x}}-\overrightarrow{\partial_{x}}}{2}\psi_{r}(x)\psi_{\bar{r}}^{\dagger}(x)\psi_{\bar{r}}(x)\colon

(79)

In Eq. (
79
) we have already included the LL renormalization effects and the non-perturbative summation of the
V_{-}
interactions in order to define quasiparticles with velocity,
v
, Eq. (
51
), and effective mass
\tilde{m}^{-1}=\tilde{\eta}_{-}
, Eq. (
57
). The interactions beyond the LL model couple right and left moving fermions together with an interaction strength
\eta_{+}
from Eq. (
58
).

To examine the bulk viscosity we subject the system to a slowly changing velocity field
u
. Because of the interactions, there will necessarily be power dissipated. In the limit of small velocity gradients, one expects the power dissipated to be of the form:

W=\zeta L(\partial_{x}u)^{2}

(80)

where
\zeta
is the zero-frequency limit of the bulk viscosity spectral function.

From standard thermodynamic arguments the power dissipated is
W=T\dot{S}
, where
S
is the entropy. The entropy can be explicitly calculated using the fermionic statistics of the quasiparticles:

S=-\sum_{r,p}\left[n_{r,p}\ln(n_{r,p})+(1-n_{r,p})\ln(1-n_{r,p})\right]

(81)

where
n_{r,p}
is the time-dependent distribution function of a
r
-moving quasiparticle with momentum
p
. Assuming the velocity gradient is weak enough, the momentum distribution can be expanded as
n_{r,p}=n_{r,p}^{0}+\delta n_{r,p}
, with
\delta n_{r,p}\propto\partial_{x}u
. The equilibrium distribution function can be written as:

n_{r,p}^{0}=\left(\exp\left[\beta\left(rvp+\frac{p^{2}}{2\tilde{m}}-\Delta\mu\right)\right]+1\right)^{-1}.

(82)

In defining Eq. (
82
), we note that
\Delta\mu
is the shift in the chemical potential away from its zero-temperature value:
\Delta\mu=\mu(T)-\mu(T=0)
, as the zero-temperature chemical potential is used in writing down the linearized Hamiltonian. From the Sommerfeld expansion one finds:

\Delta\mu=\frac{\pi^{2}T^{2}}{12\tilde{m}v^{2}}.

(83)

Then expanding
W
to linear order in
\delta n_{r,p}
gives:

W=-T\sum_{r,p}\frac{\dot{n}_{r,p}^{0}\delta n_{r,p}}{n_{r,p}^{0}(1-n_{r,p}^{0})}.

(84)

where
\dot{n}_{r,p}^{0}
represents the total time derivative of
n_{r,p}^{0}
.

To proceed further we need to evaluate the time-dependence of the equilibrium distribution function:
\dot{n}_{r,p}^{0}
. Following Refs.
Matveev17
;
DeGottardi20
we note that the velocity gradient can be imagined as a box with a size
L(t)
expanding such that:
\partial_{x}u=\dot{L}(t)/L(t)
. In this picture the time dependence of the density and momentum
p
are trivial:

\displaystyle\frac{\dot{n}}{n}

\displaystyle=-\partial_{x}u

\displaystyle\frac{\dot{p}}{p}

\displaystyle=-\partial_{x}u

(85)

In order to obtain the time-dependence of the temperature and the chemical potential, we note that the quasi-particle collisions preserve the quasi-particle number and energy:

\displaystyle\sum_{p}\dot{n}_{r,p}(t)

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\sum_{p}\epsilon_{r}(p)\dot{n}_{r,p}(t)

\displaystyle=0

(86)

where
\epsilon_{r}(p)\approx rvp
. Requiring the equilibrium distribution function satisfy Eq. (
86
), one finds the following time-dependences for
\beta=1/T
and
\Delta\mu
:

\displaystyle\frac{\dot{\beta}}{\beta}

\displaystyle=\left[n\partial_{n}\ln(nv)\right]\partial_{x}u

\displaystyle\frac{\dot{\Delta\mu}}{\Delta\mu}

\displaystyle=\left[n\partial_{n}\ln\left(\frac{\tilde{m}}{n^{2}}\right)\right]\partial_{x}u

(87)

which is valid to first order in
1/(\beta\tilde{m}v^{2})
. Finally we can obtain the time-derivative of the distribution function as:

\dot{n}_{r,p}^{0}=n_{r,p}^{0}(1-n_{p}^{0})\frac{6v^{2}p^{2}-\pi^{2}T^{2}}{12\tilde{m}v^{2}T}\left(n\partial_{n}\ln(\nu)\right)\partial_{x}u

(88)

where
\nu=\frac{\pi n}{\tilde{m}v}
.

Next we need to calculate
\delta n_{r,p}
. This is done using the relaxation time-approximation:
\delta n_{r,p}=-\tau_{r,p}\dot{n}^{0}_{r,p}
, where
\tau_{r,p}
is the scattering time associated with the two-body interactions beyond the LL model,
V_{+}
. Using the relaxation time-approximation the bulk viscosity is found to be:

\zeta=\frac{T}{L}\sum_{p}\frac{(\dot{n}_{p}^{0})^{2}}{n_{p}^{0}(1-n_{p}^{0})}\tau.

(89)

The definition of
\tau
follows from Fermi’s golden rule. Consider the scattering time for a right moving fermion with momentum
p
.

\frac{1}{\tau}=\sum_{k_{1},k_{2},k_{1}^{\prime},k_{2}^{\prime}}\sum_{r^{\prime}}\delta_{p,k_{1}}W_{1,2}^{1^{\prime},2^{\prime}}n_{L}(k_{2})(1-n_{r^{\prime}}(k_{1}^{\prime}))(1-n_{\bar{r}^{\prime}}(k_{2}^{\prime}))

(90)

where
\bar{r}=-r
and

\displaystyle W_{1,2}^{1^{\prime},2^{\prime}}=2\pi

\displaystyle\delta\left(\epsilon_{r}(k_{1})+\epsilon_{\bar{r}}(k_{2})-\epsilon_{r^{\prime}}(k_{1}^{\prime})-\epsilon_{\bar{r}^{\prime}}(k_{2}^{\prime})\right)

\displaystyle|\langle k_{1}^{\prime}\ r_{1}^{\prime},k_{2}^{\prime}\ r_{2}^{\prime}|V_{+}|k_{1}R,k_{2}L\rangle|^{2}.

(91)

The matrix element due to
V_{+}
is given by:

\displaystyle\langle k_{1}^{\prime}\ r_{1}^{\prime},k_{2}^{\prime}\ r_{2}^{\prime}|

\displaystyle V_{+}|k_{1}R,k_{2}L\rangle=

\displaystyle\frac{\eta_{+}}{L}\left[(q+q^{\prime})\delta_{R,r_{1}^{\prime}}+(q-q^{\prime})\delta_{R,\bar{r}_{1}^{\prime}}\right].

(92)

A straightforward calculation provides:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\tau}=\frac{4\eta_{+}^{2}}{v}\int\frac{dq}{2\pi}q^{2}n_{L}^{0}(p-2q)(1-n_{L}^{0}(p-2q))(1-n_{R}^{0}(p)),

(93)

In order to evaluate Eq. (
93
) we need to perform the normal ordering, which essentially subtracts off the
T=0
contribution. However, the
T=0
contribution is identically zero. Therefore the relaxation time is finally given by:

\frac{1}{\tau}=\frac{\eta_{+}^{2}T^{3}}{3v^{4}}

(94)

Substituting Eqs. (
88
) and Eq. (
94
) into Eq. (
89
) gives the final result for the bulk viscosity:

\zeta\approx\frac{1}{24\pi}\frac{T}{v\tilde{m}^{2}\eta_{+}^{2}}\left[n\partial_{n}\ln(\nu)\right]^{2}.

(95)

From the definition of
\nu=\pi n/\tilde{m}v
, one can show that:

\displaystyle n\partial_{n}\ln(\nu)=-\frac{5}{4}\left(\frac{2k_{F}\ell}{\pi}\right)^{2}.

(96)

This gives us the final expression for the bulk viscosity:

\displaystyle\zeta

\displaystyle\approx\frac{25}{6\pi^{4}}mk_{F}\ell^{2}T

(97)

Eq. (
97
) has the same functional form as the LL calculation using the microscopic definition of the bulk viscosity.

The main point to note is that one has to go beyond the standard kinetic theory calculation to produce results consistent with the microscopic evaluation of the bulk viscosity.

VIII
Bulk Viscosity for the Lieb-Liniger Model

Let us now consider the Lieb-Liniger model of interacting bosons:

\displaystyle H

\displaystyle=\int dx\ \frac{1}{2m}\partial_{x}\phi^{\dagger}(x)\partial_{x}\phi(x)

\displaystyle+\int dx\frac{g}{4}\phi^{\dagger}(x)\phi^{\dagger}(x)\phi(x)\phi(x)

(98)

where
\phi(x)
is the bosonic operator,
m
is the atomic mass and
g=-4/a
relates the interaction strength to the scattering length,
a
.

In the high temeprature limit, the two-body T-matrix has the form:

\displaystyle\left(\frac{T(x-i\delta)}{4}\right)^{-1}=-a+\frac{1}{\sqrt{-x+i\delta}}

(99)

where
\xi_{k}=\frac{k^{2}}{2m}-\mu
, and
n_{B}(x)=\left(e^{\beta x}-1\right)^{-1}
is the Bose-Einstein distribution.

We can again define the stress-tensor according to:

\Pi=2H+aC_{a}

(100)

where the contact operator is:

C_{a}=\frac{\partial H}{\partial a}=\int dx\ \frac{g}{16}\phi^{\dagger}(x)\phi^{\dagger}(x)\phi(x)\phi(x).

(101)

From Eq. (
25
) the bulk viscosity for the Lieb-Liniger model is defined as:

\zeta(\omega)=\frac{\chi_{\Pi\Pi}(\omega)-\chi_{\Pi\Pi}(0)}{i\omega}+\frac{2a\langle C_{a}\rangle}{i\omega}

(102)

The extra term in Ref. (
102
) arises from the behaviour of the contact operator under a scale transformation. Since
g=-4/a
, the contact operator does not simply transform according to its scaling dimension of
-3
but obeys the modified relation:

\left.\frac{dC_{a}}{db}\right|_{b=0}=-C_{a}.

(103)

This leads to the following behavior of
\Pi
under a scale transformation:

\left.\frac{d\Pi_{b}}{db}\right|_{b=0}=-2\Pi-aC_{a}.

(104)

This ought to be compared to how the expectation value
\langle\Pi\rangle
changes under a scale transformation:

\left.\frac{d}{db}\langle\Pi\rangle_{b}\right|_{b=0}=-2\langle\Pi\rangle-3a\langle C_{a}\rangle-a^{2}\frac{\partial\langle C_{a}\rangle}{\partial a}

(105)

The last term can again be identified as
\chi_{\Pi\Pi}(0)
via linear response theory, similar to the Fermionic case. This substitution brings the original definition of the bulk viscosity, Eq. (
25
), into the form of Eq. (
102
). The presence of the Drude peak is still consistent with scale symmetry, but suggests a divergent bulk viscosity spectral function at zero-frequency for fixed arbitrary interaction strengths. If one is considered with the real part of the bulk viscosity spectral function, the Drude peak only appears at zero-frequency. In this work, we always assume that the frequency is finite but vanishingly small, so as to ignore the Drude peak.

At leading order the bulk viscosity can be written as:

\displaystyle\zeta(\omega)\approx\frac{a^{2}}{\pi\beta}

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{dx}{\pi}\frac{n_{B}(x)-n_{B}(x+\omega)}{\omega}

\displaystyle Im\left[\frac{T(x-i\delta)}{4}\right]Im\left[\frac{T(x+\omega-i\delta)}{4}\right]

(106)

where
T(x-i\delta)
is the two-body T-matrix defined in the vacuum. Eq. (
106
) is valid in the high-temperature limit for arbitrary interaction strengths. Near resonance
na\ll 1
the bulk viscosity can be analytically evaluated:

\zeta(\omega\to 0)\approx\frac{2}{\pi^{5/2}}\left(k_{F}a\right)^{2}T^{1/2}

(107)

where we have used
n=k_{F}/\pi
, and similarly for the weakly interacting limit
na\gg 1
:

\lim_{\omega\to 0}\lim_{a^{-1}\to 0}\zeta(\omega)\approx 2\frac{k_{F}^{2}}{\pi^{5/2}}\frac{1}{a^{2}}\frac{1}{T^{3/2}}\ln\left(\frac{4T}{\omega}\right)

(108)

Upon inspection of Eqs. (
107
-
108
), one finds that they are equivalent to Eqs. (
75
,
78
) in the opposing limit. This allows one to establish the duality relations:

\zeta_{F}(\ell)=\zeta_{B}\left(\frac{1}{a}\right).

(109)

Hence the bulk viscosity for a strongly (weakly) interacting Fermi gas is equal to the bulk viscosity of a weakly (strongly) interacting Bose gas.

Figure 2:
Bulk viscosity of a spin-polarized Fermi gas near resonance. At high temperatures, Eq. (
78
), the bulk viscosity vanishes like
\ell^{-2}n^{2}T^{-3/2}
, while for low temperatures the bulk viscosity diverges as:
\ell^{-2}n^{7}T^{-4}
. From the duality relations, this plot also describes the bulk viscosity for a weakly interacting 1D bose gas.

In the low-temperature limit the bulk viscosity can be evaluated at low-temperatures. The only required modification to Eq. (
106
) is to use the T-matrix calculated within the many-body background. To leading order in perturbation theory one can write the many-body T-matrix as:

\displaystyle Im\left[\frac{T(Q,x-i\delta)}{4}\right]

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle\frac{1}{a^{2}}\int\frac{dk}{\pi}

\displaystyle\left[1+n_{B}\left(\xi_{\frac{Q}{2}+k}\right)+n_{B}\left(\xi_{\frac{Q}{2}-k}\right)\right]

\displaystyle\times\pi\delta(x-\xi_{\frac{Q}{2}+k}-\xi_{\frac{Q}{2}-k}-i\delta)

(110)

From this expression, one can show that the integrand is dominated near
\epsilon\approx 0
and
Q\approx 0
. Expanding near this point and evaluating the resulting integrals give:

\zeta_{B}(\omega\to 0)\approx\frac{16}{\pi^{2}}\frac{n^{7}}{a^{2}T^{4}}\log\left(16\frac{T^{2}}{n^{2}\omega}\right)

(111)

In order to obtain Eq. (
111
) we have used the non-interacting equation of state:

n=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\beta}}Li_{1/2}\left(e^{\beta\mu}\right)

(112)

where
Li_{s}(x)
is the polylogarithm function. As one approaches the zero-temperature limit for fixed density, one can show
\sqrt{2\beta}n=(-\beta\mu)^{-1/2}
, i.e.
\mu\to 0^{-}
as
T\to 0
.

From Eqs. (
78
) and (
111
) one can construct the bulk viscosity for arbitrary temperatures near resonance, as shown in Fig. (
2
). From the duality relations Figs. (
1
) and (
2
) describe the bulk viscosity for a 1D Bose gas near resonance and for weak interactions respectively.
