Apéry-Like Sums and Colored Multiple Zeta Values
Ce Xu and Jianqiang Zhao[1mm] a. School of Mathematics and Statistics, Anhui Normal University, Wuhu 241002, PRCb. Department of Mathematics, The Bishop’s School, La Jolla, CA 92037, USA[5mm] Dedicated to Professor Zhi-Wei Sun on the occasion of his 60th birthday

Abstract
In this article we shall survey some recent progress on the study of Apéry-like sums which are multiple variable generalizations of the two sums Apéry used in his famous proof of the irrationality of ζ ⁡ ( 2 ) \zeta(2) and ζ ⁡ ( 3 ) \zeta(3) . We only allow the central binomial coefficients to appear in these infinite sums but they can appear either on the numerator or on the denominator. Special values of both types are closely related to the colored multiple zeta values and have played important roles in the calculations of the ε {\varepsilon} -expansion of multiloop Feynman diagrams. We will summarize several different approaches to computing these sums and prove a few conjectural identities of Z.-W. Sun as corollaries along the way.



Keywords
: Apéry-like sum, central binomial coefficient, colored multiple zeta value, iterated integral, unital function, regularization.

11M32, 11B65, 33B30.

Multiple zeta values

1.1
Historical background

The study of the Riemann zeta function
\zeta(z)
and its special values has been one of the major driving forces in contemporary and modern number theory. More than a century before Riemann defined his namesake function for complex variable
z
, at the young age of 28, Euler already achieved his stardom status by finding the exact value of
\zeta(2)
, the renowned Basel problem at that time. In fact he gave three different proofs of
\zeta(2)=\pi^{2}/6
in
[
32
]
and presented his result in December of 1735 at St. Petersburg Academy of Sciences. Seven years later, Goldbach wrote to Euler from Moscow and referred him to some curious double sums which prompted Euler to begin his study of the double zeta values. Euler later returned a few more times to the same subject during his legendarily long math career.

In modern notation, the multiple zeta function of complex variables
z_{1},\dotsc,z_{d}
is defined by

\zeta(z_{1},\dotsc,z_{d}):=\sum\limits_{n_{1}>\cdots>n_{d}>0}\frac{1}{n_{1}^{z_{1}}\cdots n_{d}^{z_{d}}}.

(1)

It converges if
\rre(z_{1}+\dotsm+z_{j})>j
for all

and similarly to
\zeta(z)
,
it can be analytically continued to a memomorphic function to
\mathbb{C}^{d}
with points of indeterminacy explicitly described
(see
[
4
,
64
]
). Its special value
\zeta({\textbf{{k}}})
at positive integer arguments
{\textbf{{k}}}=(k_{1},\dotsc,k_{d})

is called a
multiple zeta value
(MZV). It converges if and only if
k_{1}\geq 2
in which case
k
is called
admissible
.
Conventionally,

is called the
depth
and
|{\textbf{{k}}}|=k_{1}+\dotsm+k_{d}

of the MZV, respectively,.
A celebrated result of Euler states that if the weight

is odd then the double zeta value
\zeta(a,b)
can be expressed
as a
\mathbb{Q}
-linear combination of
\zeta(w)
and products of Riemann zeta values
\zeta(k)\zeta(w-k),2\leq k\leq w-2

(see
[

for a modern constructive proof). The special case when

, often dubbed as Euler’s identity,
is particularly enticing:

\zeta(3)=\zeta(2,1).

(2)

After Euler, the study of MZVs went into dormancy for more than 200 years. Two influential papers appeared
during the last decade of 20th century. Around 1990, D. Zagier at Max Planck Institute for Mathematics in Germany
started to consider the relations between MZVs, the mixed Tate motives over
\mathbb{Z}
,
and the Vassiliev knot invariants and published his ideas in the seminal paper
[
62
]

(see
[
14

[
35

for more recent results). In particular, he pointed out the crucial representation of MZVs by Chen’s iterated integrals first noticed by M. Kontsevitch.

Across the Atlantic, around almost the same time period, M. Hoffman at the US Naval Academy
began to study the rational relations among the MZVs in
[

]

and then discovered the algebraic structures hidden underneath them

[
37

in 1997. The foundation laid down in this paper
has since led to a series of important progresses in the study of not only MZVs themselves but also
their various generalizations in recent years.

Chen’s theory of iterated integrals

For

\int_{\alpha}{\phi}_{1}\cdots{\phi}_{r}:=\int_{0}^{1}f_{1}(t)\,dt\cdots f_{r}(t)\,dt.

1.3
Double shuffle relations of MZVs

The most fundamental question concerning MZVs is their relations over the rational number field
\mathbb{Q}
. Euler’s proof of
\zeta(2)=\pi^{2}/6

implies that
\zeta(2)
is transcendental due to the transcendence of
\pi
. His idea can be easily extended to show that every
Riemann zeta value at positive even integer
\zeta(2n)
is a rational multiple of
\pi^{2n}
.
On the other hand, even though it is widely believed that
\zeta(2n+1)
is transcendental for every
n\geq 1
not a single case has
been proved so far. In 1978, Apéry
[
6
]
shocked the math world by showing that
\zeta(3)
is irrational by a careful
and ingenious manipulation of the following series

\zeta(3)=\frac{5}{2}\sum_{n\geq 1}\frac{(-1)^{n-1}}{n^{3}\binom{2n}{n}}.

(5)

It is therefore natural to ask, more generally, whether MZVs are all transcendental or not. As this is out of reach at the moment, we are led to the next problem: how can we find as many rational relations between MZVs as possible? The answer is given conjecturally by the so-called regularized double shuffle relations which, roughly speaking, are consequences of multiplying MZVs in two different ways.

When we multiply two MZVs, first we can apply their defining series and express the product as a
\mathbb{Z}
-linear combination of MZVs of the same weight. For example,

\displaystyle\zeta(m)\zeta(n)=\sum_{k\geq 1}\frac{1}{k^{m}}\sum_{l\geq 1}\frac{1}{l^{n}}=

\displaystyle\,\sum_{k>l\geq 1}\frac{1}{k^{m}l^{n}}+\sum_{k=l\geq 1}\frac{1}{k^{m}l^{n}}+\sum_{l>k\geq 1}\frac{1}{k^{m}l^{n}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,\zeta(m,n)+\zeta(m+n)+\zeta(n,m)

for all positive integers
m,n\geq 2
. This clearly generalizes to MZVs of arbitrary depths. We call the terms such as the first and the third in the last line above “shuffle terms” and the others “stuffing terms” (i.e., obtained by some argument merges). This way of expanding the product is called the
stuffle product
, which was first coined in
[
12
]
although similar structures appeared implicitly already in Cartier’s work
[
22
]
in early 1970s. For example, using stuffle product we see that

\zeta(2)^{2}=2\zeta(2,2)+\zeta(4).

(6)

The second way of multiplying the MZVs comes from their iterated integral expression (
3
).
For this we need to apply Chen’s Lemma
1.1
(iii) using its shuffle product structure. For example, setting

{\texttt{x}}_{0}=\frac{dt}{t},\qquad{\texttt{x}}_{1}=\frac{dt}{1-t}

(7)

we have

\displaystyle\zeta(2)^{2}=\int_{0}^{1}{\texttt{x}}_{0}{\texttt{x}}_{1}\shuffle{\texttt{x}}_{0}{\texttt{x}}_{1}=

\displaystyle\,2\int_{0}^{1}{\texttt{x}}_{0}{\texttt{x}}_{1}{\texttt{x}}_{0}{\texttt{x}}_{1}+4\int_{0}^{1}{\texttt{x}}_{0}{\texttt{x}}_{0}{\texttt{x}}_{1}{\texttt{x}}_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,2\zeta(2,2)+4\zeta(3,1).

(8)

Combining (
1.3
) with (
6
) we then derive a
finite double shuffle relation
(DBSF)

\zeta(4)=4\zeta(3,1).

We see clearly that any finite DBSF must have weight at least four.

1.4
Regularization and renormalization of MZVs

A natural question now arises: is it possible to prove Euler’s identity (
2
)
by extending the above idea? This amounts to the question of how to define “
\zeta(1)
” properly by generalizing both the series definition (
1
) and the integral expression (
3
) to include all non-admissible MZVs. This was done
by K. Ihara, M. Kaneko and D. Zagier in their ground-breaking work
[
39
]
.

For any
{\textbf{{k}}}=(k_{1},\dots,k_{d})\in\mathbb{N}^{d}
one defines
{\bf w}({\textbf{{k}}}):={\texttt{x}}_{0}^{k_{1}-1}{\texttt{x}}_{1}\dotsm{\texttt{x}}_{0}^{k_{d}-1}{\texttt{x}}_{1}
as its associated word.
Using Hoffman’s algebraic set-up laid out in
[
37
]
, Ihara et al. showed that one can extend the shuffle product
\shuffle
of words for admissible MZVs to that of non-admissible ones after suitable regularization. Further, one can define a stuffle product
\ast

on words reflecting the stuffle product of admissible MZVs and then extend it to non-admissible ones.

To begin with, by abuse of notation, setting
\zeta({\bf w}({\textbf{{k}}}))=\zeta({\textbf{{k}}})
and linearly extending
\zeta
over admissible words,
we have, for admissible
k
and
l
,

\zeta({\textbf{{k}}})\zeta({\textbf{{l}}})=\zeta\big({\bf w}({\textbf{{k}}})\shuffle{\bf w}({\textbf{{l}}})\big)=\zeta\big({\bf w}({\textbf{{k}}})\ast{\bf w}({\textbf{{l}}})\big).

(9)

This is nothing but the finite DBSF. The key observation here is that for any
\ell\geq 0
and

{\textbf{{k}}}=(1_{\ell},k_{\ell+1},\dots,k_{d})\in\mathbb{N}^{d}
(
k_{\ell+1}\geq 2
if
\ell<d
), one can find the asymptotic
expansion of the
regularized iterated integral

\displaystyle\zeta^{({\varepsilon})}({\textbf{{k}}}):=\int_{0}^{1-{\varepsilon}}{\bf w}({\textbf{{k}}})=P_{\ell}(\log{\varepsilon})+O({\varepsilon}\log^{d}{\varepsilon})\quad\text{as }{\varepsilon}\to 0^{+},

and the
multiple harmonic sums

\displaystyle H_{M}({\textbf{{k}}}):=\sum\limits_{M\geq n_{1}>\cdots>n_{d}>0}\frac{1}{n_{1}^{k_{1}}\cdots n_{d}^{k_{d}}}=Q_{\ell}(\log M+\gamma)+O(M^{-1}\log^{d}M)

as
M\to\infty
, where
\gamma
is Euler’s constant,
P_{\ell}(T)
and
Q_{\ell}(T)
are polynomials in
T
of degree
\ell
whose coefficients
of
T^{j}
, if nonzero, are
\mathbb{Q}
-linear combinations MZVs of weight
|{\textbf{{k}}}|-j
. Moreover, if
\ell=0
then
P_{\ell}(T)=Q_{\ell}(T)=\zeta({\textbf{{k}}}).

Definition 1.1
.

The
\shuffle
- and
\ast
-
regularization
of
\zeta({\textbf{{k}}})

are defined, respectively, by

\zeta_{\shuffle}({\textbf{{k}}}):=P_{\ell}(T)\quad\text{and}\quad\zeta_{\ast}({\textbf{{k}}}):=Q_{\ell}(T).

(10)

Furthermore, the value
P_{\ell}(0)
is called the
renormalization
or
renormalized value

of the divergent integral
\int_{0}^{1}{\bf w}({\textbf{{k}}})

representing the non-admissible MZV
\zeta({\textbf{{k}}})
, denoted by

\int_{0}^{1}\hskip-14.2083pt{\rm R}\hskip 6.45831pt{\bf w}({\textbf{{k}}}).

(11)

Ihara et al. showed that for any words
{\bf u}
and
{\bf v}

not ending with
{\texttt{x}}_{0}
(representing either admissible or non-admissible MZVs)

\zeta_{\shuffle}({\bf u})\zeta_{\shuffle}({\bf v})=\zeta_{\shuffle}({\bf u}\shuffle{\bf v})\quad\text{and}\quad\zeta_{\ast}({\bf u})\zeta_{\ast}({\bf v})=\zeta_{\ast}({\bf u}\ast{\bf v}).

Moreover, there is an
\mathbb{R}
-linear map
\rho:\mathbb{R}[T]\to\mathbb{R}[T]
defined by

\rho(\exp(Tu))=\exp\Big(\sum_{n\geq 2}\frac{(-1)^{n}}{n}\zeta(n)u^{n}\Big)\exp(Tu)

for a formal variable
u
, such that
\rho\circ\zeta_{\ast}=\zeta_{\shuffle}
. This relation is called the

regularized
(or
extended
) DBSF.

As an application of the regularized DBSF we can now prove Euler’s identity (
2
). Indeed, from the stuffle product
of
H_{M}(1)H_{M}(2)
we have

\displaystyle\zeta_{\ast}(1)\zeta(2)=\zeta_{\ast}({\texttt{x}}_{0}{\texttt{x}}_{1}\ast{\texttt{x}}_{1})

\displaystyle\,=\zeta_{\ast}({\texttt{x}}_{1}{\texttt{x}}_{0}{\texttt{x}}_{1}+{\texttt{x}}_{0}{\texttt{x}}_{1}{\texttt{x}}_{1}+{\texttt{x}}_{0}^{2}{\texttt{x}}_{1})

\displaystyle\,=\zeta_{\ast}(1,2)+\zeta(2,1)+\zeta(3).

On the other hand, from the shuffle product of
\zeta^{({\varepsilon})}(1)\zeta^{({\varepsilon})}(2)
, we see that

\zeta_{\shuffle}(1)\zeta(2)=\zeta_{\shuffle}({\texttt{x}}_{0}{\texttt{x}}_{1}\shuffle{\texttt{x}}_{1})=\zeta_{\shuffle}({\texttt{x}}_{1}{\texttt{x}}_{0}{\texttt{x}}_{1}+2{\texttt{x}}_{0}{\texttt{x}}_{1}{\texttt{x}}_{1})=\zeta_{\shuffle}(1,2)+2\zeta(2,1).

Hence the regularized DBSF implies that

\zeta(2)T-2\zeta(2,1)=\zeta_{\shuffle}(1,2)=\rho(\zeta_{\ast}(1,2))=\zeta(2)T-\zeta(2,1)-\zeta(3),

since
\rho(T)=T
. Thus Euler’s identity
\zeta(2,1)=\zeta(3)
follows immediately. Incidentally, we find that
the renormalized value of “
\zeta(1,2)
” is

\int_{0}^{1}\hskip-14.2083pt{\rm R}\hskip 6.45831pt{\texttt{x}}_{1}{\texttt{x}}_{0}{\texttt{x}}_{1}=\zeta_{\shuffle}(1,2)\big|_{T=0}=-2\zeta(2,1).

Multiple polylogarithm and colored MZVs

2.1
Motivation

Many mathematicians and physicists have been attracted to the study of MZVs because of the recent advancement in
computer science which has enabled them to compute enormous integrals produced from complicated Feynman diagrams.
It turns out that what comes out of these computations are not only MZVs but also some special values of
multiple polylogarithms. Fix
{\textbf{{k}}}=(k_{1},\dotsc,k_{d})\in\mathbb{N}^{d}

For convenience, if

As in the logarithm case, the multiple polylogarithm (

\int_{0}^{1}\frac{1}{x}\Li_{\textbf{{k}}}(U_{N}(x))\,dx\in\mathsf{CMZV}_{|{\textbf{{k}}}|+1}^{N}.

To prove this we need the concept of
N
-unital functions (see §
4
).

3
Apéry-like sums

In his celebrated proof of irrationality of
\zeta(2)
and
\zeta(3)
in 1979, Apéry used crucially the following two identities

\zeta(2)=3\sum_{n\geq 1}\frac{1}{n^{2}{\binom{2n}{n}}}\quad\text{and}\quad\zeta(3)=\frac{5}{2}\sum_{n\geq 1}\frac{(-1)^{n-1}}{n^{3}{\binom{2n}{n}}}.

(18)

These were extended to formulas expressing other Riemann zeta values in terms of series similar to those on the right-hand side of (
18
) (see
[
5
,
10
,
43
]
).

Also motivated by Apéry’s proof, Leshchiner
[
44
]
generalized these identities to higher weight (alternating)
Riemann zeta values and their odd-indexed analogs. At arbitrary depth, one may define two types of generalizations with the binomial coefficients appearing on either the numerator or the denominator. One may further introduce other binomial coefficients such as

\binom{3n}{n}
and
\binom{4n}{2n}
, which we will not consider in this paper.

If the central binomial coefficients appear on the numerator (resp. denominator) then we call the sum a
Apéry-type binomial series
(resp.
inverse binomial series
).
For example, for any
d\in\mathbb{N}
,
{\textbf{{s}}}=(s_{1},\ldots,s_{d})\in\mathbb{N}^{d}
, and a complex variable
z

we can define a class of Apéry-type inverse binomial series by

\sigma({\textbf{{s}}};z):=\sum_{n_{1}>n_{2}>\cdots>n_{d}>0}{\binom{2n_{1}}{n_{1}}}^{-1}\frac{(2z)^{2n_{1}}}{(2n_{1})^{s_{1}}\cdots(2n_{d})^{s_{d}}}.

(19)

Here we have renormalized the series so that its odd-indexed variation behaves similarly. By Stirling’s formula

\displaystyle 4^{n}{{\binom{2n}{n}}}^{-1}\sim\sqrt{\pi n}.

We will again call the sum
|{\textbf{{s}}}|:=s_{1}+\dots+s_{d}
the
weight
of the series
\sigma({\textbf{{s}}};z)
and
d
the
depth
.
We see that
\sigma({\textbf{{s}}};z)
converges for all
|z|<1
and, if
s
is admissible,
\sigma({\textbf{{s}}};z)
converges even for
|z|=1
.
In view of (
18
), one naturally wonders if such series are related to CMZVs for some special values of
z
.
More specifically, we would like to answer the following question.

Question 1.
Can we express
\sigma({\textbf{{s}}};z)
and its alternating version in terms of CMZVs for
z=\sqrt{j}/2
for
j=1,2,4
?

Another equally important motivation to study Apéry-like sums comes from theoretical physics. It turns out that
such series, like CMZVs, play significant roles in the evaluation of Feynman integrals. Many experimental work emerged
around the beginning of this century, e.g., see
[
13
,
30
,
31
]
for inverse
binomial series and
[
40
]
for binomial series. In fact, odd variations of both types already appeared implicitly
in
[
31
, (1.1)]
and
[
40
, (A.25)]
, respectively. By “odd variations” we mean
the series obtained from (
19
) by changing the even parity of some indices
2n_{j}
to odd ones
2n_{j}+1
.

In the theoretical framework of MZVs and their alternating version, considering their odd-indexed variations has been proved to be a very fruitful idea (see
[
38
,
42
,
59
]
). These are essentially some level two CMZVs.

Question 2.
Can we express odd variations of
\sigma({\textbf{{s}}};z)
and its alternating version in terms of CMZVs?

Many different approaches have been devised to evaluate the Apéry-like sums and their odd variants.
Ablinger
[
1
]
first converts many Apéry-type inverse binomial series into
iterated integrals, which are then computed using so-called cyclotomic harmonic polylogarithms.
Chu
[
26
,
27
]
and his collaborators have applied hypergeometric series transformation to
express many elegant
\mathbb{Q}
-linear combinations of these sums in terms of Riemann zeta values and proved
quite a few conjectures of Z.-W. Sun. However, his method has been applied to Apéry-type inverse binomial series only.
Campbell and his collaborators
[
18
,
19
,
20
]
use the idea of Fourier-Legendre series and the
properties of the complete elliptic integral of the first kind to derive many compact formulas for Apéry-type binomial series but few inverse ones. Moreover, some multiple integrals associated with
N
-posets can also be used to prove some relations among the Apéry-like sums
[
61
]
.

In the rest of this paper, we consider five more methods of evaluating these sums. The main tools are (i) single variable multiple polylogathms composed with unital functions, (ii) the Clausen functions, (iii) the trigonometric iterated integrals, (iv) the fibration method for iterated integrals which can be regarded as an extension of (i), and (v) the contour integrals, respectively.

N
-unital functions

Let

For example, it is not hard to show that
\mathsf{U}_{1}=\langle x\rangle_{6}

\langle f\rangle_{6}:=\left\{f,1-f,\frac{1}{f},\frac{f}{f-1},\frac{f-1}{f},\frac{-1}{f-1}\right\}.

.
For small levels this is given by the next result.

.

(

The proof is by an explicit computation of

Precisely, there are

4.2
Iterated integral involving
N
-unital functions

We will see that the following conjecture due to Au is crucial in the evaluation of Apéry-like sums via unital functions.

Conjecture 4.3
.

Let
{\text{{k}}}\in\mathbb{N}^{d}
. For all
U(x)\in\mathsf{U}_{N}
, the renormalized value

\int_{0}^{1}\hskip-14.2083pt{\rm R}\hskip 5.59721pt\frac{1}{x}\Li_{\text{{k}}}(U(x))\,dx\in\mathsf{CMZV}_{|{\text{{k}}}|+1}^{N}.

First, we should explain how the regularization is defined in this context. We start by considering

\int_{\eta}^{1-{\varepsilon}}\frac{1}{x}\Li_{\textbf{{k}}}(U(x))\,dx

for arbitrarily small numbers
\eta,{\varepsilon}>0
. The main philosophy is that under ideal situations like ours, we may rewrite the
integrand as a finite sum of “well-behaved” functions so that the integral of each part can be expressed as
a polynomial of
\log\eta
and
\log{\varepsilon}
modulo
O(\eta\log^{J}\eta)
and
O({\varepsilon}\log^{J}{\varepsilon})
where
J=\dep({\textbf{{k}}})+1
.
This process, which is completely similar
to that for obtaining the regularized DBSF of MZVs, is again called
regularization
.
Although each summand may diverge as
\eta,{\varepsilon}\to 0
, we can just consider the constant term of each summand
since we know the total sum itself has finite limit.
This constant term is then called the
renormalization
of the corresponding summand, completely
similar to the renormalizations of (
11
) and (
16
).
We again denote this regularized and then renormalized value by

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\hskip-14.2083pt{\rm R}\hskip 5.59721pt\frac{1}{x}\Li_{\textbf{{k}}}(U(x))\,dx.

Using the notation from the last section, if
U(x)\in\mathsf{U}_{N}
then by (
14
)

\Li_{\textbf{{k}}}(U(x))=\int_{0}^{U(x)}{\phi}_{1}\dotsm{\phi}_{w},

where
w=|{\textbf{{k}}}|
is the weight and the 1-forms
{\phi}_{j}
are either
d\log t
or
-d\log(1-t)
.
Then Lemma
1.1
(i) immediately yields that

\int_{0}^{1}\hskip-14.2083pt{\rm R}\hskip 5.59721pt\frac{1}{x}\Li_{\textbf{{k}}}(U(x))\,dx=\sum_{j=1}^{w}\left(\int_{0}^{1}\hskip-14.2083pt{\rm R}\hskip 5.59721pt\frac{1}{x}\int_{U(0)}^{U(x)}{\phi}_{1}\dotsm{\phi}_{j}\,dx\right)\left(\int_{0}^{U(0)}\hskip-26.69434pt{\rm R}\hskip 15.49997pt{\phi}_{j+1}\dotsm{\phi}_{w}\right).

(21)

Here we have removed the
j=0
term since it vanishes after regularization and renormalization.

It is clear to see that under the change of variables
t\to U(t)
the first factor in the products
above is reduced to an iterated integral over
[0,x]
of 1-forms of the shape
d\log U(x)
and
d\log(1-U(x))
.
Then the renormalized value

\int_{0}^{1}\hskip-14.2083pt{\rm R}\hskip 5.59721pt\frac{dx}{x}\int_{U(0)}^{U(x)}[d\log t,d\log(1-t)]_{j}=\int_{0}^{1}\hskip-14.2083pt{\rm R}\hskip 5.59721pt\frac{1}{x}\int_{0}^{x}[d\log U(x),d\log(1-U(x))]_{j}

must lie in
\mathsf{CMZV}_{j+1}^{N}
by the definition of
N
-unitality.

However, it is much trickier to treat the second factor in (
21
),
which amounts to finding the set
\mathsf{U}_{N}
of all possible
N
-unital functions
U(x)

and then prove that the iterated integrals
\int_{0}^{U(0)}{\phi}_{j+1}\dotsm{\phi}_{w}
lie in the correct space.

Theorem 4.4
.

Let
{\mathcal{C}}^{N}
be defined by (
20
). For any composition of positive integers
k
and
U(x)\in\mathsf{U}_{N}
,
if
U(0)\in{\mathcal{C}}^{N}
then

\int_{0}^{1}\hskip-14.2083pt{\rm R}\hskip 5.59721pt\frac{1}{x}\Li_{\text{{k}}}(U(x))\,dx\in\mathsf{CMZV}_{|{\text{{k}}}|+1}^{N}.

Proof.

By (
21
) it suffices to prove the claim that for all weight
w
and
N
-unital function
U(x)
with
U(0)\in{\mathcal{C}}^{N}
,

\int_{0}^{U(0)}\hskip-26.69434pt{\rm R}\hskip 15.49997pt[d\log t,d\log(1-t)]_{w}\in\mathsf{CMZV}_{w}^{N}.

If
U(0)=0
or
1
then the claim is trivial. If
U(0)=\infty
, then the claim follows immediately from (
16
).
This complete the proof of
N=1
case.

Assume
N\geq 2
. For
U(0)\neq 0,1,\infty
since

\mu_{j}^{-1}
is still an
N

we may just assume

for some

.

(1)

. The claim follows quickly from the substitution

.

(2)

. Applying Lemma
1.1
(i) again we get

\int_{0}^{1-\mu}\hskip-25.40266pt{\rm R}\hskip 14.2083pt[{\texttt{x}}_{0},{\texttt{x}}_{1}]_{w}=\sum_{j=0}^{w}\int_{1}^{1-\mu}\hskip-25.40266pt{\rm R}\hskip 14.2083pt[{\texttt{x}}_{0},{\texttt{x}}_{1}]_{j}\cdot\int_{0}^{1}\hskip-14.2083pt{\rm R}\hskip 5.59721pt[{\texttt{x}}_{0},{\texttt{x}}_{1}]_{w-j}.

Then by change of variables

(so that

) in the first factor we reduce it to Case 1)
by Lemma
1.1
(ii) (notice the order of integration is reversed by this change of variables).

(3)

\int_{0}^{\frac{1}{1-\mu}}\hskip-25.61795pt{\rm R}\hskip 15.28468pt[{\texttt{x}}_{0},{\texttt{x}}_{1}]_{w}=\sum_{j=0}^{w}\int_{1}^{\frac{1}{1-\mu}}\hskip-25.61795pt{\rm R}\hskip 15.28468pt[{\texttt{x}}_{0},{\texttt{x}}_{1}]_{j}\cdot\int_{0}^{1}\hskip-14.2083pt{\rm R}\hskip 5.59721pt[{\texttt{x}}_{0},{\texttt{x}}_{1}]_{w-j}.

4.3
Evaluation of Apéry-like sums via
N
-unital functions

As Apéry showed us that central binomial series such as (
5
) can play very important roles in number theory. Many works on these have appeared in recent years even though mathematicians studied them much earlier. In this section, using a key observation of Au
[
7
]
relating such series to CMZVs via unital functions we will present, for each level
N\geq 2
, a family of such series.

Fix
N\geq 2
and
1\leq j\leq N-1
. Put
\theta=\theta_{j}^{(N)}:=2j\pi/N
,
\mu=\mu_{j}^{(N)}:=e^{i\theta}
, and

a=\mu+{\bar{\mu}}=2\cos\theta
. Define

\displaystyle f_{n}=f_{n}^{(N,j)}:=\int_{0}^{1}\frac{1}{x}\left(\frac{(2-a)x}{x^{2}-ax+1}\right)^{n}\,dx,

\displaystyle g_{n}=g_{n}^{(N,j)}:=\int_{0}^{1}\left(\frac{(2-a)x}{x^{2}-ax+1}\right)^{n}\,dx.

By direct computation we see that

\displaystyle f_{1}=

\displaystyle\frac{(2-a)}{2\sin\theta}(\pi-\theta)=(\pi-\theta)\tan\frac{\theta}{2},\quad f_{2}=\frac{2-a}{4\sin^{2}\theta}(af_{1}+2-a),

\displaystyle g_{0}=1,\quad g_{1}=

\displaystyle(2-a)\log\Big(2\sin\frac{\theta}{2}\Big)+\frac{a(2-a)}{4\sin\theta}(\pi-\theta),\quad g_{2}=\frac{1}{a}(2f_{2}-2+a).

The only exception is when
N=2
and
j=1
in which case we can compute directly that
g_{1}=4\log 2-2
.
Assuming
n\geq 1
, by integration by parts we have

\displaystyle f_{n}=

\displaystyle\,\left.\frac{(2-a)^{n}x^{n}}{n(x^{2}-ax+1)^{n}}\right|_{0}^{1}+\int_{0}^{1}\frac{(2-a)^{n}x^{n}(2x-a)}{(x^{2}-ax+1)^{n+1}}\,dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,\frac{1}{n}+\frac{2}{2-a}g_{n+1}-\frac{a}{2-a}f_{n+1}.

Similarly,

\displaystyle g_{n}=

\displaystyle\,\left.\frac{(2-a)^{n}x^{n+1}}{(n+1)(x^{2}-ax+1)^{n}}\right|_{0}^{1}+\frac{n}{n+1}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{(2-a)^{n}x^{n+1}(2x-a)}{(x^{2}-ax+1)^{n+1}}\,dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,\frac{1}{n+1}+\frac{n}{n+1}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{(2-a)^{n}[2\cdot(x^{n+2}-ax^{n+1}+x^{n})+ax^{n+1}-2x^{n}]}{(x^{2}-ax+1)^{n+1}}\,dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,\frac{1}{n+1}+\frac{n}{n+1}\left(2g_{n}+\frac{a}{2-a}g_{n+1}-\frac{2}{2-a}f_{n+1}\right).

Therefore we get the second order recurrence for both
\{f_{n}\}
and
\{g_{n}\}
: for all
n\geq 2

\displaystyle(a+2)nf_{n+1}-a(2n-1)f_{n}-(2-a)(n-1)f_{n-1}=0,

\displaystyle(a+2)n(n-1)g_{n+1}-a(2n-1)(n-1)g_{n}-(2-a)n(n-2)g_{n-1}=

\displaystyle 2-a.

Theorem 4.6
.

Let
N\geq 2
,
1\leq j<N
and
\theta=2j\pi/N
. Set

F_{1}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}1,&\hbox{if $N=2$;}\\
(\pi-\theta)\csc\theta,&\hbox{if $N\geq 3$,}\end{array}\right.\quad F_{2}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}4/3,&\hbox{if $N=2$;}\\
(\cos\theta F_{1}+1)\csc^{2}\theta,&\hbox{if $N\geq 3$,}\end{array}\right.

and

n\sin^{2}\theta F_{n+1}-(2n-1)\cos\theta F_{n}-(n-1)F_{n-1}=0\quad\forall n\geq 2.

Then we have

\displaystyle\sum_{n\geq 1}\frac{H_{n-1}({\text{{k}}})}{n^{l}}(1-\cos\theta)^{n}F_{n}\in\mathsf{CMZV}_{|{\text{{k}}}|+l+1}^{N}.

In particular, when
l=1
and
2
we have

\displaystyle\sum_{n\geq 1}\frac{(1-\cos\theta)^{n}F_{n}}{n}=

\displaystyle\,2\zeta(2)-\Li_{2}(\mu)-\Li_{2}({\bar{\mu}})\in\mathsf{CMZV}_{2}^{N},

\displaystyle\sum_{n\geq 1}\frac{(1-\cos\theta)^{n}F_{n}}{n^{2}}=

\displaystyle\,\sum_{\eta=\mu,{\bar{\mu}}}\Big(2\Li_{2,1}(\eta,\bar{\eta})-\Li_{2,1}(\eta,1)-\Li_{2,1}(\eta,\bar{\eta}^{2})\Big)

\displaystyle\,+2\zeta(3)-\Li_{3}(\mu)-\Li_{3}({\bar{\mu}})\in\mathsf{CMZV}_{3}^{N}.

Proof.

It is easy to see that
f_{n}=(1-\cos\theta)^{n}F_{n}
. Put

U(x):=\frac{(2-a)x}{x^{2}-ax+1}=\frac{(2-a)x}{(x-\mu)(x-{\bar{\mu}})},\quad 1-U(x)=\frac{(x-1)^{2}}{(x-\mu)(x-{\bar{\mu}})}\in\mathsf{U}_{N}.

5.1
Some conjectures of Z.-W. Sun

Since late 1980s Z.-W. Sun has discovered many beautiful and highly nontrivial identities of infinite series, often involving interesting objects such as Fibonacci numbers, Bernoulli numbers, Euler numbers, etc. In
[

53
,
54

he proposed a few families of conjectures of such identities by generalizing the Lucas numbers to Lucas sequences. We strongly recommend his recent book
[

even though it is in Chinese because the readers can still understand most of the 820 conjectures even if they do not know a single Chinese character.

Let
A
and
B
be integers. Sun defines the more general
Lucas sequence

\{v_{n}(A,B)\}_{n\geq 0}
as follows (see
[
51

\displaystyle v_{0}(A,B):=2,\quad v_{1}(A,B):=A,

\displaystyle v_{n+1}(A,B)=Av_{n}(A,B)-Bv_{n-1}(A,B)\quad\forall n\geq 1.

The characteristic equation
x^{2}-Ax+B=0
of the sequence
\{v_{n}\}_{n\geq 0}
has two roots

\alpha=\frac{A+\sqrt{A^{2}-4B}}{2}\quad\text{and}\quad\beta=\frac{A-\sqrt{A^{2}-4B}}{2}.

It is well known that for any integer

we have

\displaystyle v_{n}(A,B)=\alpha^{n}+\beta^{n}.

For examples, setting
\phi=\frac{\sqrt{5}+1}{2}
we have

\displaystyle v_{n}(3,1)=L_{2n}=\phi^{2n}+\phi^{-2n},

\displaystyle v_{n}(4,1)=(2-\sqrt{3})^{n}+(2+\sqrt{3})^{n},

\displaystyle v_{n}(4,2)=(2-\sqrt{2})^{n}+(2+\sqrt{2})^{n},

\displaystyle v_{n}(5,5)=(\sqrt{5}\phi)^{n}+(\sqrt{5}\phi^{-1})^{n}.

Define
H_{0}:=0
and the

Proof of Theorem

by Clausen function

Recall that for any positive integer

the Clausen function
\cl_{m}(\theta)
is defined by

\displaystyle{\cl}_{2m-1}(\theta):=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\cos(n\theta)}{n^{2m-1}}\quad\text{and}\quad{\cl}_{2m}(\theta):=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\sin(n\theta)}{n^{2m}}\qquad\forall\theta\in[0,\pi].

Many special values of the Clausen function are closely related to the Riemann zeta values. For example,
\cl_{2m-1}(0)=\zeta(2m-1)
,

\cl_{2m-1}(\pi)=(2^{2-2m}-1)\zeta(2m-1),\quad\text{and}\quad\cl_{2m-1}\Big(\frac{\pi}{2}\Big)=\frac{\cl_{2m-1}(\pi)}{2^{2m-1}}.

(30)

The following relations among special values of the Clausen function are well known (see, e.g.,
[
45
, Eq. (6.51)]
):
for any positive integer
r
we have

\displaystyle\cl_{3}\big(\tfrac{\pi}{r}\big)+\cl_{3}\big(\tfrac{3\pi}{r}\big)+\cdots+\cl_{3}\big(\tfrac{(2r-1)\pi}{r}\big)=-\tfrac{3}{4r^{2}}\zeta(3),

(31)

\displaystyle\cl_{3}\big(\tfrac{2\pi}{r}\big)+\cl_{3}\big(\tfrac{4\pi}{r}\big)+\cdots+\cl_{3}\big(\tfrac{(2r-2)\pi}{r}\big)=-\big(1-\tfrac{1}{r^{2}}\big)\zeta(3).

(32)

Setting
r=4,5,6
in (
31
) and
r=5
in (
32
) one obtains

\displaystyle\cl_{3}\big(\tfrac{\pi}{4}\big)+\cl_{3}\big(\tfrac{3\pi}{4}\big)=-\tfrac{3}{128}\zeta(3),\qquad\cl_{3}\big(\tfrac{\pi}{5}\big)+\cl_{3}\big(\tfrac{3\pi}{5}\big)=\tfrac{9}{25}\zeta(3),

(33)

\displaystyle\cl_{3}\big(\tfrac{\pi}{6}\big)+\cl_{3}\big(\tfrac{5\pi}{6}\big)=\tfrac{1}{12}\zeta(3),\qquad\cl_{3}\big(\tfrac{2\pi}{5}\big)+\cl_{3}\big(\tfrac{4\pi}{5}\big)=-\tfrac{12}{25}\zeta(3),

(34)

since
\cl_{3}(\theta)=\cl_{3}(2\pi\pm\theta)
,
\cl_{3}(\pi)=-\tfrac{3}{4}\zeta(3)
and
\cl_{3}\big(\tfrac{\pi}{2}\big)=-\tfrac{3}{32}\zeta(3)
by (

On the other hand, Davydychev and Kalmykov have shown that
(see
[

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n^{3}\binom{2n}{n}}u^{n}=2\cl_{3}(\theta)+2\theta\cl_{2}(\theta)-2\zeta(3)+\theta^{2}\log\Big(2\sin\frac{\theta}{2}\Big),

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{H_{n-1}}{n^{2}\binom{2n}{n}}u^{n}=4\cl_{3}(\pi-\theta)-2\theta\cl_{2}(\pi-\theta)+3\zeta(3),

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{H_{2n-1}}{n^{2}\binom{2n}{n}}u^{n}=-2\cl_{3}(\theta)+4\cl_{3}(\pi-\theta)-2\theta\cl_{2}(\pi-\theta)-\theta\cl_{2}(\theta)+5\zeta(3),

where

for any

.
From the above three equations we get

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{u^{n}}{n^{2}\binom{2n}{n}}\left(H_{2n}-H_{n-1}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{u^{n}}{n^{2}\binom{2n}{n}}\left(H_{2n-1}-H_{n-1}+\frac{1}{2n}\right)

\displaystyle=\zeta(3)-\cl_{3}(\theta)+\frac{\theta^{2}}{2}\log\Big(2\sin\frac{\theta}{2}\Big).

(35)

Taking special values of

we obtain

\displaystyle u=\Big(\frac{\sqrt{5}\pm 1}{2}\Big)^{2}=4\sin^{2}\frac{\theta}{2}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad

\displaystyle\sin\frac{\theta}{2}=\frac{\sqrt{5}\pm 1}{4}\quad\text{and}\quad\theta=\frac{2\pi}{5}\pm\frac{\pi}{5},

\displaystyle u=\frac{5\pm\sqrt{5}}{2}=4\sin^{2}\frac{\theta}{2}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad

\displaystyle\sin\frac{\theta}{2}=\sqrt{\frac{5\pm\sqrt{5}}{8}}\quad\text{and}\quad\theta=\frac{3\pi}{5}\pm\frac{\pi}{5},

\displaystyle u=2\pm\sqrt{3}=4\sin^{2}\frac{\theta}{2}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad

\displaystyle\sin\frac{\theta}{2}=\frac{\sqrt{2\pm\sqrt{3}}}{2}\quad\text{and}\quad\theta=\frac{\pi}{2}\pm\frac{\pi}{3},

\displaystyle u=2\pm\sqrt{2}=4\sin^{2}\frac{\theta}{2}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad

\displaystyle\sin\frac{\theta}{2}=\frac{\sqrt{2\pm\sqrt{2}}}{2}\quad\text{and}\quad\theta=\frac{\pi}{2}\pm\frac{\pi}{4}.

Therefore, (

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{L_{2n}}{n^{2}\binom{2n}{n}}\left(H_{2n}-H_{n-1}\right)

\displaystyle=2\zeta(3)-\cl_{3}\big(\tfrac{3\pi}{5}\big)-\cl_{3}\big(\tfrac{\pi}{5}\big)+\frac{4\pi^{2}}{25}\log(\phi)

\displaystyle=\frac{41\zeta(3)+4\pi^{2}\log(\phi)}{25},

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{v_{n}(5,5)}{n^{2}\binom{2n}{n}}\left(H_{2n}-H_{n-1}\right)

\displaystyle=2\zeta(3)-\cl_{3}\big(\tfrac{2\pi}{5}\big)-\cl_{3}\big(\tfrac{4\pi}{5}\big)+\frac{\pi^{2}\log\left(5^{5}\phi^{6}\right)}{50}

\displaystyle=\frac{124\zeta(3)+\pi^{2}\log\left(5^{5}\phi^{6}\right)}{50},

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{v_{n}(4,1)}{n^{2}\binom{2n}{n}}\left(H_{2n}-H_{n-1}\right)

6
Apéry-like sums via trigonometric integrals

6.1
Akhilesh’s
n
-tails of Apéry-type inverse binomial series

In the two sequential papers
[
2
,
3
]
Akhilesh discovered some very intriguing and surprising connections between
MZVs and the Apéry-type inverse binomial series (
19
), with a slightly different normalization.
His ingenious idea is to study the
n
-tails (and more generally, double tails) of such series. After obtaining the explicit
formula for the depth one case one can then iterate this process to arbitrary depth.
To make it more transparent we reformulate one of his key results as follows. Set
\binom{0}{0}=1
,

\displaystyle b_{n}(z)=\frac{4^{n}z^{2n}}{{\binom{2n}{n}}}\quad\text{and}\quad b_{n}=b_{n}(1)=\frac{4^{n}}{{\binom{2n}{n}}}\quad\forall n\geq 0.

Define

\displaystyle g_{s}(t)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\tan t\,dt,&\hbox{if $s=1$;}\\
dt\circ(\cot t\,dt)^{s-2}\circ dt,&\hbox{if $s\geq 2$,}\end{array}\right.

and their non-trigonometric counterpart

G_{s}(t)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}{\omega}_{2}&\hbox{if $s=1$;}\\
{\omega}_{1}{\omega}_{0}^{s-2}{\omega}_{1}&\hbox{if $s\geq 2$,}\end{array}\right.

(38)

where

\displaystyle{\omega}_{0}:=

\displaystyle\,\frac{dt}{t},\quad

\displaystyle\,{\omega}_{1}:=

\displaystyle\,\frac{dt}{\sqrt{1-t^{2}}},\quad

\displaystyle\,{\omega}_{2}:=

\displaystyle\,\frac{t\,dt}{1-t^{2}}.

(39)

Theorem 6.1
.

(cf.
[
3
, Theorem 4]
)

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
,
{\textbf{{s}}}=(s_{1},\dots,s_{d})\in\mathbb{N}^{d}
,
y\in(-\pi/2,\pi/2)
if
s_{1}=1
and
y\in[-\pi/2,\pi/2]
if
s_{1}\geq 2
.
Then the
n
-tail of
\sigma({\textbf{{s}}};\sin y)

\displaystyle\sigma({\textbf{{s}}};\sin y)_{n}:=

\displaystyle\,\sum_{n_{1}>\cdots>n_{d}>n}\frac{b_{n_{1}}(\sin y)}{(2n_{1})^{s_{1}}\cdots(2n_{d})^{s_{d}}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,\frac{d}{dy}\int_{0}^{y}g_{s_{1}}\circ\cdots\circ g_{s_{d}}\circ b_{n}(\sin t)\,dt.

Using non-trigonometric 1-forms, for all
z\in(-1,1)
we have

\displaystyle\sigma({\textbf{{s}}};z)_{n}:=

\displaystyle\,\sum_{n_{1}>\cdots>n_{d}>n}\frac{b_{n_{1}}(z)}{(2n_{1})^{s_{1}}\cdots(2n_{d})^{s_{d}}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,\sqrt{1-z^{2}}\frac{d}{dz}\int_{0}^{z}G_{s_{1}}\circ\cdots\circ G_{s_{d}}\circ b_{n}(t){\omega}_{1}.

We now provide a new proof of the following result conjectured by Sun
[
52
, (3.10)]
first proved
by Ablinger
[
1
, (5-56)]
.

Theorem 6.2
.

We have

\sum_{n\geq 1}\frac{3^{n}}{n^{2}\binom{2n}{n}}\left(H_{n}+\frac{1}{2n}\right)=\frac{\pi^{2}}{3}\log 3.

(40)

Proof.

First, since

we can rewrite (

) as

\displaystyle 8\sigma(2,1;\sqrt{3}/2)+12\sigma(3;\sqrt{3}/2)=\frac{\pi^{2}}{3}\log 3.

By Akhilesh’s Theorem
6.1
it suffices to show

8A+12B=\frac{\pi^{2}}{3}\log 3,

(41)

where

\displaystyle A=

\displaystyle\,\int_{0}^{\pi/3}dt(\tan t\,dt)\,dt,\quad B=\int_{0}^{\pi/3}\frac{dt}{\tan t}\,dt\,dt=\int_{0}^{\pi/3}(t^{2}\cot t)dt.

By integrating by parts, we get

\displaystyle A=\int_{0}^{\pi/3}\left(\int_{0}^{t}(x\tan x)dx\right)dt=

\displaystyle\,\left.t\int_{0}^{t}(x\tan x)dx\right|_{0}^{\pi/3}-\int_{0}^{\pi/3}(t^{2}\tan t)dt

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,\frac{\pi}{3}\int_{0}^{\pi/3}(x\tan x)dx-\int_{0}^{\pi/3}(t^{2}\tan t)dt.

We can verify by differentiation that (see also
[

, Lemma 1]

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{z}(t\tan t)dt=

\displaystyle\,\frac{i}{2}\Big(z^{2}+\Li_{2}(-e^{2iz})+\frac{1}{2}\zeta(2)\Big)-z\log(1+e^{2iz}),

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{z}(t^{2}\tan t)dt=

\displaystyle\,\frac{i}{3}z^{3}-z^{2}\log(1+e^{2iz})+iz\Li_{2}(-e^{2iz})-\frac{1}{2}\Li_{3}(-e^{2iz})-\frac{3}{8}\zeta(3),

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{z}(t^{2}\cot t)dt=

\displaystyle\,-\frac{i}{3}z^{3}+z^{2}\log(1+e^{iz})+z^{2}\log(1-e^{iz})-2iz\Li_{2}(e^{iz})

\displaystyle\,-2iz\Li_{2}(-e^{iz})+2\Li_{3}(e^{iz})+2\Li_{3}(-e^{iz})-\frac{1}{2}\zeta(3),

where we have used the fact that
\Li_{2}(-1)=\zeta(\bar{2})=-\frac{1}{2}\zeta(2)=-\frac{\pi^{2}}{12}
and
\Li_{3}(-1)=\zeta(\bar{3})=-\frac{3}{4}\zeta(3)
.
Setting
\eta=e^{2\pi i/3}
, after a lot of simplification and cancelations we obtain

\displaystyle 8A+12B=\frac{\pi^{2}\log 3}{3}-\frac{2\pi i}{3}\left(\frac{\pi^{2}}{9}+2\Li_{2}(-\eta)+3\Li_{2}(\eta)\right).

(42)

Noting that by the series definition

\Li_{2}(-\eta)+\Li_{2}(\eta)=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-\eta)^{k}+\eta^{k}}{k^{2}}=\frac{1}{2}\Li_{2}(\eta^{2}),

(43)

we know the quantity in the parenthesis in (

\displaystyle\frac{\pi^{2}}{9}+2\Li_{2}(-\eta)+3\Li_{2}(\eta)=\frac{\pi^{2}}{9}+\Li_{2}(\eta^{2})+\Li_{2}(\eta)

(44)

is real (invariant under conjugation) so that it must be zero

is real. Thus (

We can also prove directly (

\displaystyle 2\Li_{2}(-\eta)+3\Li_{2}(\eta)+\Li_{2}(1)=

\displaystyle\,\Li_{2}(\eta^{2})+\Li_{2}(\eta)+\Li_{2}(1)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{\eta^{2k}+\eta^{k}+1}{k^{2}}=\frac{1}{3}\Li_{2}(1)

since

This proves (

6.3

It turns out Akhilesh’s method of using iteration to express Apéry-type inverse binomial series can be generalized

{\textbf{{s}}}=(s_{1},\dots,s_{d})\in\mathbb{N}^{d}
and
{\boldsymbol{\eta}}=(\eta_{1},\dots,\eta_{d})\in\{\pm 1\}^{d}
.
Let
l_{j}(n)=2n
or
l_{j}(n)=2n\pm 1
for all
1\leq j\leq d
. Suppose
z=\sqrt{j}/2
for
j=1,2,4
. Is there a level
N
such that

\displaystyle\sum_{n_{1}\succ n_{2}\succ\,\cdots\succ n_{d}\succ\,0}\frac{a_{n_{1}}z^{2n_{1}}\eta_{1}^{n_{1}}\cdots\eta_{d}^{n_{d}}}{l_{1}(n_{1})^{s_{1}}\cdots l_{d}(n_{d})^{s_{d}}}\in\mathsf{CMZV}^{N}\otimes\mathbb{Q}[\exp(2\pi i/N)],

\displaystyle\sum_{n_{1}\succ n_{2}\succ\,\cdots\succ n_{d}\succ\,0}\frac{b_{n_{1}}z^{2n_{1}}\eta_{1}^{n_{1}}\cdots\eta_{d}^{n_{d}}}{l_{1}(n_{1})^{s_{1}}\cdots l_{d}(n_{d})^{s_{d}}}\in\mathsf{CMZV}^{N}\otimes\mathbb{Q}[\exp(2\pi i/N)]

if the sums converge? Here “
\succ
” can be either “
\geq
” or “
>
”. One may also ask the same question when

a_{n}
and
b_{n}
are replaced by their squares while adding an extra factor of
1/\pi
on the
right-hand side for
a_{n}^{2}
version. Available evidence seems to suggest that
N
is at least 120.

7
Apéry-like sums via fibrations of iterated integrals

In
[
21
]
, Campbell et al. solved an open problem posed by H. Chen in his book
[
23
]
by evaluating the following two Apéry-like sums explicitly and showed that

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(2n+1)^{3}\binom{2n}{n}}\in i\mathsf{CMZV}_{3}^{12},\qquad\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{n}}{(2n+1)^{3}\binom{2n}{n}}\in\mathsf{CMZV}_{3}^{10}.

In fact, they were able to show that

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{4^{n}\sin^{2n+1}(2m\pi/N)}{(2n+1)^{k}\binom{2n}{n}}\in i\mathsf{CMZV}_{k}^{\rm{lcm}(2,N)}\quad\forall k\geq 2,N\geq 3,m\in\mathbb{Z}.

The main idea to evaluate these is use a variation of (
22
) in the form of

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\bigg(\frac{x}{1+x^{2}}\bigg)^{2n+1}\frac{dx}{1+x^{2}}=\frac{1}{4(2n+1)\binom{2n}{n}}

together with

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{z^{2n+1}}{(2n+1)^{k}}=\frac{\Li_{k}(z)-\Li_{k}(-z)}{2},

to derive (for any
k\geq 3
and
w
such that
\Im(iw)>0
and
\Im(i/w)>0
)

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{n}}{(2n+1)^{k}\binom{2n}{n}}\biggl(\frac{1-w^{2}}{w}\biggr)^{2n+1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{}2i\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\Li_{k-1}\bigl(\frac{1-w^{2}}{iw}\frac{x}{1+x^{2}}\bigr)-\Li_{k-1}\bigl(-\frac{1-w^{2}}{iw}\frac{x}{1+x^{2}}\bigr)}{1+x^{2}}\,dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{}\frac{2i}{(k-2)!}\int_{iw}^{i}\frac{\log^{k-2}\bigl(\frac{1-w^{2}}{iw}\frac{z}{1+z^{2}}\bigr)}{1+z^{2}}\log\frac{z}{i}\,dz+\frac{2i}{(k-2)!}\int_{i}^{i/w}\frac{\log^{k-2}\bigl(-\frac{1-w^{2}}{iw}\frac{z}{1+z^{2}}\bigr)}{1+z^{2}}\log\frac{z}{i}\,dz.

This last expression can be treated using the generalized polylogarithms
[
33
]
(also called hyperlogarithms in
[
49
]
) when
w=\exp(2\pi mi/N)
after noticing that

\displaystyle{}\log^{k}\bigg(\frac{1-w^{2}}{w}\bigg)\in\mathsf{CMZV}_{k}^{{\rm lcm}(2,N)}.

The key step in the process of computing the generalized polylogarithms is to convert various complicated iterated integrals to those that can be seen easily to lie inside
\mathsf{CMZV}
, which is called the fibration in
[
55
]
since one of the major programs in Panzer’s package
[
48
,
49
]
is named fibrationBasis which is partially motivated by the ideas of Brown
[
15
,
16
]
.

Combining with Au’s algorithm
[
7
]
, Campbell et al. further proved the following in
[
21
]
.

Theorem 7.1
.

For positive integer
k\geq 2
, set

\displaystyle{\mathcal{S}}_{k}(z):=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{z^{n}}{(2n+1)^{k}\binom{2n}{n}}.

Then the following relations hold true:

\displaystyle{\mathcal{S}}_{k}\biggl(-\frac{9}{4}\biggr)\in

\displaystyle\mathsf{CMZV}_{k}^{6},

\displaystyle\quad{\mathcal{S}}_{k}(-4)\in

\displaystyle\mathsf{CMZV}_{k}^{8},

\displaystyle\quad{\mathcal{S}}_{k}\biggl(-\frac{1}{2}\biggr)\in

\displaystyle\sqrt{2}\mathsf{CMZV}_{k}^{8},

\displaystyle{\mathcal{S}}_{k}(-1)\in

\displaystyle\mathsf{CMZV}_{k}^{10},

\displaystyle\quad{\mathcal{S}}_{k}\biggl(-\frac{16}{5}\biggr)\in

\displaystyle\sqrt{5}\mathsf{CMZV}_{k}^{10},

\displaystyle\quad{\mathcal{S}}_{k}\biggl(-\frac{4}{3}\biggr)\in

\displaystyle\sqrt{3}\mathsf{CMZV}_{k}^{12}.

By applying similar ideas, Y. Zhou
[
67
]
showed that for all
k\in\mathbb{N}

\displaystyle\sum_{n_{1}\geq n_{2}\geq 0}\frac{\binom{2n_{1}}{n_{1}}}{8^{n_{1}}n_{2}^{k}}\in

\displaystyle\sqrt{2}\mathsf{CMZV}_{k}^{8},\qquad

\displaystyle\sum_{2n_{1}\geq n_{2}\geq 0}\frac{\binom{2n_{1}}{n_{1}}}{8^{n_{1}}n_{2}^{k}}\in

\displaystyle\sqrt{2}\mathsf{CMZV}_{k}^{8},

\displaystyle\sum_{n_{1}\geq n_{2}\geq 0}\frac{\binom{2n_{1}}{n_{1}}}{16^{n_{1}}n_{2}^{k}}\in

\displaystyle\sqrt{3}\mathsf{CMZV}_{k}^{12},\qquad

\displaystyle\sum_{2n_{1}\geq n_{2}\geq 0}\frac{\binom{2n_{1}}{n_{1}}}{16^{n_{1}}n_{2}^{k}}\in

\displaystyle\sqrt{3}\mathsf{CMZV}_{k}^{12}.

We remark that the method of fibration can be applied to some other Apéry-like sums involving binomial coefficients

\binom{3n}{n}
,
\binom{4n}{2n}
and
\binom{6n}{3n}
. We refer the interested readers to
[
8
,
21
,
55
,
67
]
for more details.

8
Apéry-like sums via contour integrals

Let
H_{n}^{(p)}
be the generalized
n
th-harmonic number of order
p
defined by

H_{n}^{(p)}:=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{1}{k^{p}},\quad H_{n}\equiv H_{n}^{(1)}\quad\text{and}\quad H_{0}^{(p)}:=0.

Motivated by Flajolet and Salvy’s paper
[
34
]
. Flajolet and Salvy
[
34
]
introduced and used the method of contour integrals and residue computations to study the following kind of special Dirichlet series that involve harmonic numbers

\displaystyle{S_{{\bf p},q}}:=\sum\limits_{n=1}^{\infty}{\frac{{H_{n}^{\left({{p_{1}}}\right)}H_{n}^{\left({{p_{2}}}\right)}\cdots H_{n}^{\left({{p_{r}}}\right)}}}{{{n^{q}}}}},

(70)

we call them
generalized (non-alternating) Euler sums
. Moreover, if
r>1
in (
70
), they were called
nonlinear Euler sums
. Here
{\bf p}:=(p_{1},p_{2},\ldots,p_{r})\ (r,p_{i}\in\mathbb{N},i=1,2,\ldots,r)
with
p_{1}\leq p_{2}\leq\ldots\leq p_{r}
and
q\geq 2
. The quantity
w:={p_{1}}+\cdots+{p_{r}}+q
is called the weight and the quantity
r
is called the degree (order).
By the well-known “harmonic product" (also called “quasi-shuffle product")
[
37
]
, we see that the generalized Euler sums
{S_{{\bf p},q}}
can be expressed as a rational linear combinations of multiple zeta values. See
[
58
]
for the explicit formula of generalized Euler sums via multiple zeta values.

Flajolet and Salvy
[
34
]
defined a kernel function
\xi\left(s\right)
by the two requirements: 1. it is meromorphic on the whole complex plane. 2. it satisfies
\xi\left(s\right)=o(s)
over an infinite collection of circles
\left|s\right|={\rho_{k}}
with
{\rho_{k}}\to\infty
. Applying these two conditions of kernel
function
\xi\left(s\right)
, Flajolet and Salvy discovered the following residue lemma.

Lemma 8.1
.

(cf.
[
34
]
)

Let
\xi\left(s\right)
be a kernel function and let
r(s)
be a rational function which is
O(s^{-2})
at infinity. Then

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{\alpha\in O}{{\mathop{\rm Res}}{{({r\left(s\right)\xi\left(s\right)},\alpha)}}}+\sum\limits_{\beta\in S}{{\mathop{\rm Res}}{{({r\left(s\right)\xi\left(s\right)},\beta)}}}=0,

(71)

where
S
is the set of poles of
r(s)
and
O
is the set of poles of
\xi\left(s\right)
that are not poles
r(s)
. Here
{\mathop{\rm Re}\nolimits}s{\left[{r\left(s\right)},s=\alpha\right]}
denotes the residue of
r(s)
at
s=\alpha
.

Flajolet and Salvy considered the contour integration involving classical digamma function and used the residue computations to establish more explicit reductions of generalized Euler sums to Euler sums with lower degree.
In particular, they proved the famous theorem that a nonlinear Euler sum
S_{p_{1}p_{2}\cdots p_{r},q}
reduces to a combination of sums of lower orders whenever the weight
p_{1}+p_{2}+\cdots+p_{r}+q
and the order
r
are of the same parity by considering the kernels

\xi_{r}(s):=\pi\cot(\pi s)\frac{\psi^{(p_{1}-1)}(-s)\cdots\psi^{(p_{r}-1)}(-s)}{(p_{1}-1)!\cdots(p_{r}-1)!}

and the base function
r(s)=s^{-q}
.

8.1
Main Methods

By extending Flajolet and Salvy’s idea it is not too difficult to discover the following fact that any
polynomial form in

\pi\cot\pi s,\quad\frac{\pi}{\sin\pi s},\quad\psi^{(j)}(\pm s),\quad\frac{\Gamma^{2}(s+1)}{\Gamma(2s+1)}4^{s}

is itself a kernel function with poles at a subset of the integers. Because by the Legendre duplication formula

\Gamma(s)\Gamma\left(s+\frac{1}{2}\right)=\sqrt{\pi}\cdot 2^{1-2s}\Gamma(2s)

and the asymptotic expansion for the ratio of two gamma functions

\frac{\Gamma(s+a)}{\Gamma(s+b)}=s^{a-b}\left(1+O\left(\frac{1}{s}\right)\right)\,,\quad\text{for }|\arg(s)|\leq\pi-\varepsilon\,,\ \varepsilon>0\,,\ |s|\to\infty

(see
[
41
, Sections 2.3 and 2.11]
), we have

\displaystyle\frac{\Gamma^{2}(s+1)}{\Gamma(2s+1)}4^{s}=\sqrt{\pi s}\left(1+O\left(\frac{1}{s}\right)\right)=o(s)\,,\quad|s|\to\infty\,.

(72)

The purpose of this section is precisely to investigate the power of such kernels in connection with summation formulas and Apéry-like sums. We need the following lemmas.

Lemma 8.2
.

(cf.
[
57
]
)

For
|s|<1
, the following identities hold:

\Gamma(s+1)\,\mathrm{e}^{\gamma s}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}C_{n}\frac{s^{n}}{n!}\,,\quad\{\Gamma(s+1)\,\mathrm{e}^{\gamma s}\}^{-1}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}D_{n}\frac{s^{n}}{n!}\,,

where
\gamma:=\lim_{n\to\infty}(H_{n}-\log n)
is the Euler-Mascheroni constant, and

\displaystyle C_{n}:=Y_{n}(0,1!\zeta(2),-2!\zeta(3),\ldots,(-1)^{n}(n-1)!\zeta(n))\,,

\displaystyle D_{n}:=Y_{n}(0,-1!\zeta(2),2!\zeta(3),\ldots,(-1)^{n-1}(n-1)!\zeta(n))\,.

Here
Y_{n}
denote
exponential complete Bell polynomials
(see
[
28
, Section 3.3]
and
[
50
, Section 2.8]
) defined by

\exp\left(\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}x_{k}\frac{t^{k}}{k!}\right)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}Y_{n}(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n})\frac{t^{n}}{n!}\,,

(73)

and satisfy the recurrence

Y_{n}(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n})=\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\binom{n-1}{j}x_{n-j}Y_{j}(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{j})\,,\quad n\geq 1.

(74)

According to Lemma
8.2
, it is clear that
C_{k}
and
D_{k}
are rational linear combinations of products of zeta values. In particular, by (
74
), we have

(C_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}=(1,0,\zeta(2),-2\zeta(3),\frac{27}{2}\zeta(4),\ldots)
and
(D_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}=(1,0,-\zeta(2),2\zeta(3),\frac{3}{2}\zeta(4),\ldots)
.
Furthermore, by the relations

\displaystyle\frac{\Gamma(s-n)}{\Gamma(s+1)}=\prod_{k=0}^{n}\frac{1}{s-k}=\frac{(-1)^{n}}{n!}\frac{1}{s}\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\zeta_{n}^{\star}(\{1\}_{m})s^{m}\,,

\displaystyle\frac{\Gamma(s+1)}{\Gamma(s-n)}=\prod_{k=0}^{n}(s-k)=(-1)^{n}n!s\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{m}\zeta_{n}(\{1\}_{m})s^{m}\,,

for
n\geq 0
, the next lemma can be established.

Lemma 8.3
.

For nonnegative integer
n
, when
s\to-n
, we have

\displaystyle\Gamma(s)\,\mathrm{e}^{\gamma(z-1)}=\frac{(-1)^{n}}{n!}\,\mathrm{e}^{-\gamma(n+1)}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}A_{k}(n)(s+n)^{k-1}\,,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\Gamma(s)\,\mathrm{e}^{\gamma(s-1)}}=(-1)^{n}n!\,\mathrm{e}^{\gamma(n+1)}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}B_{k}(n)(s+n)^{k+1}\,,

where

A_{k}(n):=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k_{1}+k_{2}=k\\
k_{1},k_{2}\geq 0\end{subarray}}\zeta_{n}^{\star}(\{1\}_{k_{1}})\frac{C_{k_{2}}}{k_{2}!}\,,\quad B_{k}(n):=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k_{1}+k_{2}=k\\
k_{1},k_{2}\geq 0\end{subarray}}(-1)^{k_{1}}\zeta_{n}(\{1\}_{k_{1}})\frac{D_{k_{2}}}{k_{2}!}\,.

Here for positive integers
k_{1},k_{2},\ldots,k_{r}
, the
multiple harmonic sums
(MHSs)
\zeta_{n}(k_{1},k_{2},\ldots,k_{r})
are defined by

\displaystyle\zeta_{n}({\textbf{{k}}}):=\sum_{n\geq n_{1}>n_{2}>\cdots>n_{r}\geq 1}\frac{1}{n_{1}^{k_{1}}n_{2}^{k_{2}}\cdots n_{r}^{k_{r}}}\,.

(75)

Additionally, similarly to MHSs, denote the
multiple harmonic star sums

\zeta_{n}^{\star}(k_{1},k_{2},\ldots,k_{r})
by

\displaystyle\zeta_{n}^{\star}({\textbf{{k}}}):=\sum_{n\geq n_{1}\geq n_{2}\geq\cdots\geq n_{r}\geq 1}\frac{1}{n_{1}^{k_{1}}n_{2}^{k_{2}}\cdots n_{r}^{k_{r}}}\,.

(76)

Here when
n<r
, then
{\zeta_{n}}({\bf k}):=0
, and
{\zeta_{n}}(\emptyset)={\zeta^{\star}_{n}}(\emptyset):=1
. When taking the limit
n\rightarrow\infty
in (
75
) and (
76
) with
k_{1}>1
, we get the so-called the multiple zeta values and the multiple zeta star values, respectively.

Now, we use the Lemmas
8.1
and
8.3
to establish some explicit relations of Apéry-like sums.
For more details we refer the readers to our paper
[
57
]
.

8.2
Evaluations of some Apéry-like sums via contour integrals

Firstly, denote

G(\vec{k}):=G(k_{1},k_{2},k_{3},k_{4})=\frac{2^{k_{3}+k_{4}}\log^{k_{4}}(2)C_{k_{1}}C_{k_{2}}D_{k_{3}}}{k_{1}!k_{2}!k_{3}!k_{4}!}\,.

(77)

Then we have a new closed form expression.

Theorem 8.4
.

For integer
q\geq 2
, we have

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\binom{2n}{n}}{4^{n}n^{q-1}}=(-1)^{q}\sum_{k_{1}+k_{2}+k_{3}+k_{4}=q-1,\atop k_{1},\ldots,k_{4}\geq 0}G(k_{1},k_{2},k_{3},k_{4})\,.

Proof.

Consider the function

F_{0}(s):=\frac{\Gamma^{2}(s+1)}{\Gamma(2s+1)}\frac{4^{s}}{s^{q}}\,.

Applying (
72
) yields

F_{0}(s)=\frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{s^{q-1/2}}\left(1+O\left(\frac{1}{s}\right)\right)\,,\quad|s|\to\infty\,,

which implies that the integral
\oint_{(\infty)}F_{0}(s)\,\mathrm{d}s=0
, where
\oint_{(\infty)}
denotes integration along large circles, that is, the limit of integrals
\oint_{|s|=\rho_{k}}
. Hence, we can use the Lemma
8.1
to obtain an identity of infinite series.
On the other hand, the function
F_{0}(s)
has poles only at all non-positive integers. For a negative integer
-n
, by Lemma
8.3
, if
s\to-n
, we have

F_{0}(s)=-\frac{4^{s}}{s^{q}}\frac{(2n-1)!}{(n-1)!^{2}}\sum_{k_{1},k_{2},k_{3}\geq 0}2^{k_{3}+1}A_{k_{1}}(n-1)A_{k_{2}}(n-1)B_{k_{3}}(2n-1)(s+n)^{k_{1}+k_{2}+k_{3}-1}\,.

Hence, the residue is

{\rm Res}(F_{0}(s),-n)=(-1)^{q+1}\frac{\binom{2n}{n}}{4^{n}n^{q-1}}\,,\quad\text{for }n=1,2,\ldots\,.

Similarly, if
s\to 0
, we have

F_{0}(s)=\frac{4^{s}}{s^{q}}\sum_{k_{1},k_{2},k_{3}\geq 0}\frac{2^{k_{3}}C_{k_{1}}C_{k_{2}}D_{k_{3}}}{k_{1}!k_{2}!k_{3}!}s^{k_{1}+k_{2}+k_{3}}\,,

and the residue of the pole of order
q
at 0 is

{\rm Res}(F_{0}(s),0)=\sum_{|k|_{4}=q-1}G(k_{1},k_{2},k_{3},k_{4})\,.

Using Lemma
8.1
and summing these two contributions gives the statement of the theorem.
∎

Theorem 8.5
.

For positive integer
q>1
,

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{n}{(n-1/2)^{q}}\cdot\frac{\binom{2n}{n}}{4^{n}}=\frac{2}{\pi}\sum_{k_{1}+\cdots+k_{4}+2k_{5}=q-2,\atop k_{1},\ldots,k_{5}\geq 0}G(k_{1},k_{2},k_{3},k_{4})\widetilde{t}(2k_{5}+2),

(78)

where
{\widetilde{t}(k)}:=2^{k}\sl\frac{1}{(2n-1)^{k}}=(2^{k}-1)\zeta(k)
for
k>1
.

Proof.

Similarly to Theorem
8.4
, we consider the contour integral

\displaystyle\oint_{(\infty)}F_{1}(s)ds:=\oint_{(\infty)}\pi\tan(\pi s)F_{0}(s)ds=0.

The function
F_{1}(s)
has poles only at
0
and
n-1/2\ (n=1,2,3,\ldots)
. By straightforward calculations, we obtain

\displaystyle{\rm Res}(F_{1}(s),n-1/2)=-\pi\frac{n}{(n-1/2)^{q}}\frac{\binom{2n}{n}}{4^{n}}

and

\displaystyle{\rm Res}(F_{1}(s),0)=2\sum_{k_{1}+\cdots+k_{4}+2k_{5}=q-2,\atop k_{1},\ldots,k_{5}\geq 0}\frac{C_{k_{1}}C_{k_{2}}D_{k_{3}}2^{k_{3}+k_{4}}(\log(2))^{k_{4}}{\widetilde{t}(2k_{5}+2)}}{k_{1}!k_{2}!k_{3}!k_{4}!}.

Hence, we complete the proof of Theorem
8.5
.
∎

It is possible that some other identities of Apéry-like sums can be proved using
techniques of the present section. For example, considering the function

F_{2}(s):=(\psi(-s)+\gamma)F_{0}(s)
, which has poles only at all integers. By residue computations, we obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{4^{n}}{n^{q}\binom{2n}{n}}=(-1)^{q}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{H_{n-1}\binom{2n}{n}}{4^{n}n^{q-1}}+\sum_{k_{1}+k_{2}+\cdots+k_{5}=q-2,\atop k_{1},\ldots,k_{5}\geq 0}G(k_{1},k_{2},k_{3},k_{4})\zeta(k_{5}+2)\,.

(79)
