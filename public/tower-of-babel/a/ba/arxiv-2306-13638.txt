Some new primality criteria based on Lucas sequences.
Gaitanas Konstantinos

Abstract




Abstract:
In this paper, we provide some novel results concerning the behavior of
\frac{U_{kn}}{U_{k}}
modulo
{U_{n}}
, where
(U_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is the Lucas sequence of the first kind. As a consequence, we obtain some primality criteria which do not seem to appear in the literature.

Keywords:
Lucas sequences, prime numbers.

1
Introduction

Let
a_{n}(P,Q)
be numerical sequences, satisfying the recurrence relation

\displaystyle a_{n+1}=P\cdot a_{n}-Q\cdot a_{n-1}\quad P,Q\in\mathbb{Z}.

The two special cases with initial conditions
(a_{0},a_{1})=(0,1)
and
(a_{0},a_{1})=(2,P)
are the
Lucas sequences
of the first and second kind and will be denoted by
U_{n}(P,Q)
and
V_{n}(P,Q)
, respectively. Since the Lucas sequences satisfy a linear recurrence relation with constant coefficients, it is easy to obtain explicit expressions (Binet forms) from the characteristic polynomial
p(x)=x^{2}-Px+Q
. If the discriminant of
p(x)
is non-zero and
\alpha,\beta
are its roots, then

\displaystyle U_{n}=\frac{\alpha^{n}-\beta^{n}}{\alpha-\beta},\quad V_{n}=\alpha^{n}+\beta^{n}.

Many popular examples are recovered as particular instances of Lucas sequences, which are important for their own sake. Probably the two most popular are the Fibonacci and Lucas numbers (
“The two shining stars in the vast array of integer sequences”
[
1
]
) which are obtained from
U_{n}(1,-1)
and
V_{n}(1,-1)
, respectively. Other well-known examples include the Pell, Mersenne, Jacobsthal and Balancing numbers. For more material that might satisfy the reader’s curiosity we refer to the book of Paulo Ribenboim
“My Numbers, My Friends”

[
2
]
. The main purpose of this paper is to present some congruence relations which hold among the terms of
(U_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
and eventually provide some primality criteria which are aimed to serve mostly for theoretical purposes. We emphasize that the congruences we obtain throughout this paper seem to be of independent interest
1
1

1

It is possible that some of these congruences have already appeared somewhere in the vast mathematical literature. For the convenience of the reader, we provide detailed proofs to make the paper self-contained.

Notation and Preliminaries

This section is a quick-reference guide to the notation and background information that will be assumed throughout this paper.

We denote by
\varphi(n)
Euler’s totient function, which counts the positive integers not greater than
n
, that are relatively prime to
n
. We will also use without proof the fact that the number of positive integers
k
less than
n
with

, is exactly
\varphi(\frac{n}{d})
. Legendre’s symbol is defined as

\displaystyle\left(\frac{a}{p}\right)=\begin{cases}1,&\text{if }a\text{ is a quadratic residue modulo }p\text{ and }a\not\equiv 0\pmod{p},\\
-1,&\text{if }a\text{ is a quadratic nonresidue modulo }p,\\
0,&\text{if }a\equiv 0\pmod{p}.\end{cases}.

In addition, we mention some well-known facts about Lucas sequences. For the sake of brevity, we omit their proofs since they are obtained without much effort and can be found in various mathematical contexts, such as
[

.

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
D=P^{2}-4Q
. The following hold true:

2.1.1.

U_{m+n}=U_{m}V_{n}-Q^{n}U_{m-n}
.

2.1.2.

V_{n}=2U_{n+1}-PU_{n}
.

2.1.3.

V_{n}^{2}-DU_{n}^{2}=4Q^{n}
.

Moreover, for the rest of the paper we will assume that
P
and
Q
are coprime. In this way, we ensure that
(U_{n})
is a
strong divisibility sequence
which means that
\gcd(U_{x},U_{y})=U_{\gcd(x,y)}
. This evidently implies that if
y\mid x
, then
U_{y}\mid U_{x}
. This is something which will be frequently used. As for other prerequisites, the reader is expected to be familiar only with basic congruence rules and Bézout’s identity, which states that if
\gcd(x,y)=d
, then there are
s,q\in\mathbb{Z}
such that
sx+qy=d
.

Two important lemmas.

In this section, we provide two lemmas which will be the key tools for the proof of Theorem (
4.1
). We note that a version of the first lemma already appears in

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
k\in\mathbb{N^{*}}
. The following holds true:

\frac{U_{kn}}{U_{k}}\equiv\begin{cases}n\cdot Q^{\frac{k(n-1)}{2}}\pmod{U_{k}},&\text{if}\quad n\equiv 1\pmod{2},\\
nU_{k+1}\cdot Q^{\frac{k(n-2)}{2}}\pmod{U_{k}},&\text{if}\quad n\equiv 0\pmod{2}.\\
\end{cases}

(1)

Proof.

We prove this by applying induction on
n
. We note that (
1
) obviously holds true for
n=0
and
n=1
. Suppose that it holds true
for every natural number not greater than
n
, with
n\geq 2
. From (
2.1.1
) for
m=kn
and
n=k
, we obtain

\frac{U_{k(n+1)}}{U_{k}}=\frac{U_{kn}}{U_{k}}V_{k}-Q^{k}\frac{U_{k(n-1)}}{U_{k}}.

(2)

Assume that
n+1
is odd. Then
n
is even and
n-1
is also odd. Hence, using the induction hypothesis the last equation yields

\displaystyle\frac{U_{k(n+1)}}{U_{k}}\equiv nU_{k+1}Q^{\frac{k(n-2)}{2}}V_{k}-Q^{k}(n-1)Q^{\frac{k(n-2)}{2}}\pmod{U_{k}}.

From (
2.1.2
),
V_{k}\equiv 2U_{k+1}\pmod{U_{k}}
. Thus,
nU_{k+1}V_{k}\equiv\frac{n}{2}\cdot 2U_{k+1}V_{k}\equiv\frac{n}{2}V_{k}^{2}\equiv 2nQ^{k}\pmod{U_{k}}
. The last congruence holds, since from (
2.1.3
),
V_{k}^{2}\equiv 4Q^{k}\pmod{U_{k}}
. It follows that

\displaystyle\frac{U_{k(n+1)}}{U_{k}}\equiv 2nQ^{k}Q^{\frac{k(n-2)}{2}}-(n-1)Q^{\frac{k(n-2)}{2}}Q^{k}\equiv(n+1)Q^{\frac{kn}{2}}\pmod{U_{k}}

which proves the claim if
n+1
is odd.

If
n+1
is even, then
n
is odd and
n-1
is even. From the induction hypothesis, (
2
) yields

\displaystyle\frac{U_{k(n+1)}}{U_{k}}\equiv n\cdot Q^{\frac{k(n-1)}{2}}V_{k}-Q^{k}(n-1)U_{k+1}Q^{\frac{k(n-3)}{2}}\pmod{U_{k}}.

But
V_{k}\equiv 2U_{k+1}\pmod{U_{k}}
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\frac{U_{k(n+1)}}{U_{k}}\equiv 2n\cdot Q^{\frac{k(n-1)}{2}}U_{k+1}-Q^{k}(n-1)U_{k+1}Q^{\frac{k(n-3)}{2}}\equiv(n+1)U_{k+1}Q^{\frac{k(n-1)}{2}}\pmod{U_{k}}

which proves the claim if
n+1
is even.
∎

We use similar arguments to obtain our second result.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
k\in\mathbb{N}
. The following holds true:

U_{kn+1}\equiv\begin{cases}U_{k+1}Q^{\frac{k(n-1)}{2}}\pmod{U_{k}},&\text{if}\quad n\equiv 1\pmod{2},\\
Q^{\frac{kn}{2}}\pmod{U_{k}},&\text{if}\quad n\equiv 0\pmod{2}.\\
\end{cases}

(3)

Proof.

We prove this again by applying induction on
n
. It is evident that (
3
) holds true for
n=0
and
n=1
. Suppose that it holds true
for every natural number not greater than
n
, with
n\geq 2
. From (
2.1.1
) for
m=kn
and
n=k+1
, we obtain

U_{k(n+1)+1}=U_{kn}V_{k+1}-Q^{k+1}U_{k(n-1)-1}\equiv-Q^{k+1}U_{k(n-1)-1}\mod{U_{k}}

(4)

since
U_{k}\mid U_{kn}
. From definition,
U_{k(n-1)+1}=PU_{k(n-1)}-QU_{k(n-1)-1}
. But
U_{k}\mid U_{k(n-1)}
, which implies that
-QU_{k(n-1)-1}\equiv U_{k(n-1)+1}\pmod{U_{k}}
. Therefore, from (
4
) we deduce that

U_{k(n+1)+1}\equiv Q^{k}U_{k(n-1)+1}\pmod{U_{k}}.

(5)

Assume that
n+1
is even. Then
n-1
is also even and from the induction hypothesis the last congruence yields
U_{k(n+1)+1}\equiv Q^{k}Q^{\frac{k(n-1)}{2}}\equiv Q^{\frac{k(n+1)}{2}}\pmod{U_{k}}
.

If
n+1
is odd, then
n-1
is also odd. From the induction hypothesis and (
5
) we deduce that
U_{k(n+1)+1}\equiv Q^{k}U_{k+1}Q^{\frac{k(n-2)}{2}}\equiv U_{k+1}Q^{\frac{kn}{2}}\pmod{U_{k}}
. This completes the proof.
∎

For the sake of brevity, it will be convenient to present the previous results without taking into consideration the parity of

. Therefore, we introduce a function

\displaystyle a(x)=\begin{cases}0,&\text{if}\quad x\equiv 1\pmod{2},\\

In this way, we obtain the following Corollary.

Corollary 3.3
.

The following hold true:

\frac{U_{kn}}{U_{k}}\equiv n\left(U_{k+1}\right)^{a(n)}\cdot Q^{\frac{k(n-a(n)-1)}{2}}\pmod{U_{k}}.

(6)

U_{kn+1}\equiv\left(U_{k+1}\right)^{1-a(n)}\cdot Q^{\frac{k(n+a(n)-1)}{2}}\pmod{U_{k}}.

(7)

The previous Corollary is interesting on its own; we briefly describe two applications:

From (
6
) we easily obtain that

\displaystyle U_{kn}=U_{k}\cdot n\left(U_{k+1}\right)^{a(n)}\cdot Q^{\frac{k(n-a(n)-1)}{2}}\pmod{U_{k}^{2}}.

From this, it is straightforward to see that if

, then

. This result has already appeared several times in the literature (for example, in §3 of

In addition, from the identity
U_{m+n}=U_{m+1}U_{n}-QU_{m}U_{n-1}
for

and

, we obtain that
U_{kn+r}=U_{kn+1}U_{r}-QU_{kn}U_{r-1}
. From (
7
) and the fact that
U_{k}\mid U_{kn}
, we deduce the more general congruence:

\displaystyle U_{kn+r}\equiv U_{r}\left(U_{k+1}\right)^{1-a(n)}\cdot Q^{\frac{k(n+a(n)-1)}{2}}\pmod{U_{k}}.

4
Main results.

In this section, we state our main results and provide some key proof techniques and insights. We recall that the Lucas sequence of the first kind is a strong divisibility sequence, which means that
\gcd(U_{n},U_{k})=U_{\gcd(n,k)}
.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
k\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
and
\gcd(n,k)=d
. Then

\displaystyle\frac{U_{kn}}{U_{k}}\equiv\begin{cases}dU_{d+1}\cdot\frac{U_{n}}{U_{d}}\cdot Q^{\frac{kn-k-n}{2}}\pmod{U_{n}},&\text{if}\quad n,k\quad\text{are even and}\quad\frac{n}{d},\frac{k}{d}\quad\text{are odd},\\
d\cdot\frac{U_{n}}{U_{d}}\cdot Q^{\frac{kn-k-n+d}{2}}\pmod{U_{n}},&\text{otherwise}.\\
\end{cases}

Proof.

Suppose that

\frac{U_{kn}}{U_{k}}=r+M\cdot U_{n}

(8)

where
0\leq r\leq U_{n}-1
and
M\in\mathbb{N}
. This is equivalent to
U_{kn}=rU_{k}+MU_{n}U_{k}
. Since
U_{n}\mid U_{kn}
and
U_{n}\mid kMU_{n}U_{k}
, we obtain
U_{n}\mid rU_{k}
. But
\gcd(U_{n},U_{k})=U_{d}
, which implies that
\gcd(\frac{U_{n}}{U_{d}},\frac{U_{k}}{U_{d}})=1
. Thus,
\frac{U_{n}}{U_{d}}\mid r
, so
r=c\cdot\frac{U_{n}}{U_{d}}
for some
c\in\mathbb{N}
. Hence,

\frac{U_{kn}}{U_{k}}=c\cdot\frac{U_{n}}{U_{d}}+MU_{n}.

(9)

From (
7
) for
k=d
and
n=\frac{k}{d}
, we obtain

U_{k+1}\equiv(U_{d+1})^{1-a(k/d)}\cdot Q^{\frac{d(k/d+a(k/d)-1)}{2}}\pmod{U_{k}}.

(10)

Thus, (
6
) implies that

\frac{U_{kn}}{U_{k}}\equiv n(U_{d+1})^{a(n)(1-a(k/d))}\cdot Q^{\frac{da(n)a(k/d)-da(n)+kn-k}{2}}\pmod{U_{k}}.

(11)

Also from (
6
), for
k=d
and
n=\frac{n}{d}
, we obtain

\frac{U_{n}}{U_{d}}\equiv\frac{n}{d}(U_{d+1})^{a(n/d)}\cdot Q^{\frac{d(n/d-a(n/d)-1)}{2}}\pmod{U_{d}}.

(12)

Since
n\equiv k\equiv 0\pmod{d}
, then
U_{n}\equiv U_{k}\equiv 0\pmod{U_{d}}
. Thus, (
9
), (
11
) and (
12
) imply that

\displaystyle n(U_{d+1})^{a(n)(1-a(k/d))}\cdot Q^{\frac{da(n)a(k/d)-da(n)+kn-k}{2}}\equiv c\frac{n}{d}(U_{d+1})^{a(n/d)}\cdot Q^{\frac{d(n/d-a(n/d)-1)}{2}}\pmod{U_{d}}.

Furthermore, we observe

U_{d+1}=P\cdot U_{d}-Q\cdot U_{d-1}

\delta\mid U_{d}
and
\delta\mid Q

\delta\mid U_{d+1}

(13)

Moving forward, we multiply both sides of (

\displaystyle\frac{U_{kn}}{U_{n}}=c\frac{U_{k}}{U_{d}}+MU_{k}.

Following the same steps, (by exchanging the roles of

\frac{U_{kn}}{U_{n}}\equiv k(U_{d+1})^{a(k)(1-a(n/d))}\cdot Q^{\frac{da(k)a(n/d)-da(k)+kn-n}{2}}\pmod{U_{d}}.

(14)

and

\frac{U_{k}}{U_{d}}\equiv\frac{k}{d}(U_{d+1})^{a(k/d)}\cdot Q^{\frac{d(k/d-a(k/d)-1)}{2}}\pmod{U_{d}}.

(15)

Again, since

(16)

The previous results indicate that the rest of the proof falls naturally into four cases:

Case 1

The number

\displaystyle da(k)a(n/d)-da(k)+da(k/d)=d(-a(k)+a(k/d))=0.

.

The number

\displaystyle a(n)(1-a(k/d))-a(n/d)=1-\left(a(k/d)+a(n/d)\right)=0,

(17)

\displaystyle\frac{U_{kn}}{U_{k}}\equiv d\cdot\frac{U_{n}}{U_{d}}\cdot Q^{\frac{kn-k-n+d}{2}}\pmod{U_{n}}.

.

Both

\displaystyle\frac{2^{kn}-1}{2^{k}-1}\equiv d\cdot\frac{2^{n}-1}{2^{d}-1}\pmod{2^{n}-1}.

\displaystyle\frac{2^{kn}-1}{2^{k}-1}\equiv d\cdot\frac{2^{n}-1}{2^{d}-1}2^{\frac{kn-k-n+d}{2}}\equiv d\cdot\frac{2^{n}-1}{2^{d}-1}\pmod{2^{n}-1}.

This completes the proof.

4.1
Criteria of Primality.

Theorem 4.3
.

The congruence

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\frac{2^{kn}-1}{2^{k}-1}\equiv 0\pmod{2^{n}-1}

holds true, if and only if
n
is prime.

Proof.

From (
22
), we observe that

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\frac{2^{kn}-1}{2^{k}-1}\equiv\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\gcd(n,k)=d\\
1\leq k<n\end{subarray}}d\cdot\frac{2^{n}-1}{2^{d}-1}\pmod{2^{n}-1}.

The number of positive integers
k
less than
n
with
\gcd(n,k)=d
, is exactly
\varphi(\frac{n}{d})
. Thus,

\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\frac{2^{kn}-1}{2^{k}-1}\equiv(2^{n}-1)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d\mid n\\
1\leq d<n\end{subarray}}\frac{d\cdot\varphi(\frac{n}{d})}{2^{d}-1}\pmod{2^{n}-1}.

(23)

It suffices to prove that the last sum is an integer if and only if
n
is prime.

If
n
is prime, there is only one divisor
d<n
of
n
, namely
d=1
and the sum is equal to

If

), we know that

, unless

Remark 1
.

We pause now to record an observation that follows readily from the computations made above; if
n\equiv 3\pmod{4}
, then (
25
) holds true, regardless of whether
n
is prime. To see this, observe that
i^{n-1}=-1
, thus

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\gcd(n,k)=d\end{subarray}}(i^{n-1})^{(k-1)}=-\sum_{\gcd(n,k)=d}(-1)^{d\cdot\frac{k}{d}}=-\sum_{\gcd(\frac{n}{d},\frac{k}{d})=1}(-1)^{\frac{k}{d}}.

The last equality holds, since
d
is odd. In addition, we note that
\frac{k}{d}
denotes a number which is coprime to
\frac{n}{d}
. If
\gcd(\frac{k}{d},\frac{n}{d})=1
, then
\gcd(\frac{n}{d}-\frac{k}{d},\frac{n}{d})=1
. But
\frac{n}{d}-\frac{k}{d}
and
\frac{k}{d}
have opposite parity since their sum is
\frac{n}{d}\equiv 1\pmod{2}
, which means that
(-1)^{\frac{n}{d}-\frac{k}{d}}+(-1)^{\frac{k}{d}}=0
. Hence, for every
d
we have a sum of pairs which vanishes, and the result follows.
