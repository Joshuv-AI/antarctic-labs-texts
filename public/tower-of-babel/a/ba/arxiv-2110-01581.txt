Quickest Change Detection with Non-Stationary Post-Change Observations
Yuchen Liang, Alexander G. Tartakovsky, Venugopal V. Veeravalli

Abstract
The problem of quickest detection of a change in the distribution of a sequence of independent observations is considered. The pre-change observations are assumed to be stationary with a known distribution, while the post-change observations are allowed to be non-stationary with some possible parametric uncertainty in their distribution. In particular, it is assumed that the cumulative Kullback-Leibler divergence between the post-change and the pre-change distributions grows in a certain manner with time after the change-point. For the case where the post-change distributions are known, a universal asymptotic lower bound on the delay is derived, as the false alarm rate goes to zero. Furthermore, a window-limited Cumulative Sum (CuSum) procedure is developed, and shown to achieve the lower bound asymptotically. For the case where the post-change distributions have parametric uncertainty, a window-limited (WL) generalized likelihood-ratio (GLR) CuSum procedure is developed and is shown to achieve the universal lower bound asymptotically. Extensions to the case with dependent observations are discussed. The analysis is validated through numerical results on synthetic data. The use of the WL-GLR-CuSum procedure in monitoring pandemics is also demonstrated.

Introduction

The problem of quickest change detection (QCD) is of fundamental importance in a variety of applications and has been extensively studied in mathematical statistics (see, e.g.,
[
1
,
2
,
3
,
4

for overviews). Given a sequence of observations whose distribution changes at some unknown change point, the goal is to detect the change in distribution as quickly as possible after it occurs, while not making too many false alarms.

In the classical formulations of the QCD problem, it is assumed that observations are independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) with known pre- and
post-change distributions. In many practical situations, while it is reasonable to assume that we can accurately estimate the pre-change distribution, the post-change distribution is rarely completely known. Furthermore, in many cases, it is reasonable to assume that the system is in a steady state before the change point and produces i.i.d. observations, but in the post-change mode the observations may be substantially non-identically distributed, i.e., non-stationary.
For example, in the pandemic monitoring problem, the distribution of the number of people infected daily might have achieved a steady (stationary) state before the start of a new wave, but after the onset of a new wave, the post-change observations may no longer be stationary. Indeed, during the early phase of the new wave, the mean of the post-change distribution grows approximately exponentially. We will address the pandemic monitoring problem in detail in Section
V
.

II
Information Bounds and Optimal Detection

Let
\{X_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
be a sequence of independent random variables (generally vectors), and let
\nu
be a change point.
Assume that
X_{1},\dots,X_{\nu-1}
all have density
p_{0}
with respect to some non-degenerate, sigma-finite measure
\mu
and
that
X_{\nu},X_{\nu+1},\dots
have densities
p_{1,\nu,\nu},p_{1,\nu+1,\nu},\ldots
, respectively, with respect to
\mu
.
Note that the observations are allowed to be non-stationary after the change point and the post-change distributions may generally depend on the change point.

Let
({\cal F}_{n})_{n\geq 0}
be the filtration, i.e.,
{\cal F}_{0}=\{\Omega,\varnothing\}
and
{\cal F}_{n}=\sigma\left\{X_{\ell},1\leq\ell\leq n\right\}

is the sigma-algebra generated by the vector of
n
observations
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
and let
{\cal F}_{\infty}=\sigma(X_{1},X_{2},\dots)
.
In what follows we denote by
\mathbb{P}_{\nu}
the probability measure on the entire sequence of observations when the change-point is
\nu
. That is,
under
\mathbb{P}_{\nu}
the random variables
X_{1},\dots,X_{\nu-1}
are i.i.d. with the common (pre-change) density
p_{0}

and
X_{\nu},X_{\nu+1},\dots
are independent with (post-change) densities
p_{1,\nu,\nu},p_{1,\nu+1,\nu},\ldots
.
Let
\mathbb{E}_{\nu}
denote the corresponding expectation. For
\nu=\infty
this distribution will be denoted by
\mathbb{P}_{\infty}
and the corresponding expectation by
\mathbb{E}_{\infty}
. Evidently, under
\mathbb{P}_{\infty}
the random variables
X_{1},X_{2},\dots
are i.i.d. with density
p_{0}
. In the sequel, we denote by
\tau
Markov (stopping) times with respect to the filtration
({\cal F}_{n})_{n\geq 0}
, i.e., the event
\{\tau=n\}
belongs to
{\cal F}_{n}
.

The change-time
\nu
is assumed to be unknown but deterministic.
The problem is to detect the change quickly while not causing too many false alarms. Let
\tau
be a stopping time defined on the observation sequence associated with the detection rule, i.e.,
\tau
is the time at which we stop taking observations and declare that the change has occurred. The problem is to detect the change quickly, minimizing the delay to detection
\tau-\nu
, while not causing too many false alarms.

II-A

Classical Results under i.i.d. Model

A special case of the model described above is where both the pre- and post-change observations are i.i.d., i.e.,
p_{1,n,\nu}\equiv p_{1}
for all
n\geq\nu\geq 1
. In this case, Lorden
[
5
]
proposed solving the following optimization problem to find the best stopping time
\tau
:

\inf_{\tau\in\mathcal{C}_{\alpha}}\mathrm{WADD}\left(\tau\right)

(1)

where

\mathrm{WADD}\left(\tau\right):=\sup_{\nu\geq 1}\esssup\mathbb{E}_{\nu}\left[\left(\tau-\nu+1\right)^{+}|{\cal F}_{\nu-1}\right]

(2)

characterizes the worst-case expected delay, and
\esssup
stands for essential supremum. The constraint set is

\mathcal{C}_{\alpha}:=\left\{\tau:\mathrm{FAR}\left(\tau\right)\leq\alpha\right\}

(3)

with

\mathrm{FAR}\left(\tau\right):=\frac{1}{\mathbb{E}_{\infty}\left[\tau\right]}

(4)

which guarantees that the false alarm rate of the algorithm does not exceed
\alpha
. Recall that
\mathbb{E}_{\infty}\left[\cdot\right]
is the expectation operator when the change never happens, and we use the conventional notation
(\cdot)^{+}:=\max\{0,\cdot\}
for the nonnegative part. The mean time to a false alarm (MTFA)

\mathbb{E}_{\infty}\left[\tau\right]
is sometimes referred to as the average run length to false alarm.

Lorden also showed that Page’s CuSum detection algorithm
[
15
]

solves the problem in (
1
) asymptotically as
\alpha\to 0
,
which is given by:

\tau_{\text{Page}}\left(b\right):=\inf\left\{n:\max_{1\leq k\leq n+1}\sum_{i=k}^{n}\log\frac{p_{1}(X_{n})}{p_{0}(X_{n})}\geq b\right\}.

(5)

It was shown by Moustakides
[
16
]
that the CuSum algorithm is exactly optimal for the problem in (
1
) if threshold
b=b_{\alpha}

is selected so that
\mathrm{FAR}\left(\tau_{\text{Page}}\left(b_{\alpha}\right)\right)=\alpha
. If threshold
b_{\alpha}
is selected in a special way that accounts for
the overshoot of
W(n)
over
b_{\alpha}
at stopping, which guarantees the approximation
\mathrm{FAR}\left(\tau_{\text{Page}}\left(b_{\alpha}\right)\right)\sim\alpha

as
\alpha\to 0
, then we have the following third-order asymptotic approximation (as
\alpha\to 0
) for the worst-case expected detection delay of the optimal procedure:

\displaystyle\inf_{\tau\in\mathcal{C}_{\alpha}}\mathrm{WADD}\left(\tau\right)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{WADD}\left(\tau_{\text{Page}}(b_{\alpha})\right)+o(1),

\displaystyle\mathrm{WADD}\left(\tau_{\text{Page}}(b_{\alpha})\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{{D(p_{1}||p_{0})}}(\left|\log\alpha\right|-\mathsf{const}+o(1))

(see, e.g.,
[
1
]
), which also implies the first-order asymptotic approximation (as
\alpha\to 0
):

\inf_{\tau\in\mathcal{C}_{\alpha}}\mathrm{WADD}\left(\tau\right)\sim\mathrm{WADD}\left(\tau_{\text{Page}}\left(\left|\log\alpha\right|\right)\right)=\frac{\left|\log\alpha\right|}{D(p_{1}||p_{0})}(1+o(1))

(6)

where
Y_{\alpha}\sim G_{\alpha}
is equivalent to
Y_{\alpha}=G_{\alpha}(1+o(1))
. Here
D(p_{1}||p_{0})
is the Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence between
p_{1}
and
p_{0}
. Also, in the following we use a standard notation

o(x)
as
x\to x_{0}
for the function
f(x)
such that
f(x)/x\to 0
as
x\to x_{0}
, i.e.,
o(1)\to 0
as
\alpha\to 0
, and
O(x)
for the function
f(x)
such that

f(x)/x
is bounded as
x\to 0
, i.e.,
O(1)
is a finite constant.

Along with Lorden’s worst average detection delay
\mathrm{WADD}\left(\tau\right)
, defined in (
2
),
we can also consider the less pessimistic Pollak’s performance measure
[
17
]
:

\mathrm{SADD}\left(\tau\right):=\sup_{\nu\geq 1}\mathbb{E}_{\nu}\left[\tau-\nu+1|\tau\geq\nu\right].

Pollak suggested the following minimax optimization problem in class
\mathcal{C}_{\alpha}
:

\inf_{\tau\in\mathcal{C}_{\alpha}}\mathrm{SADD}\left(\tau\right).

(7)

An alternative to CuSum is the Shiryaev-Roberts (SR) change detection procedure
\tau_{\text{SR}}
based not on the maximization of the likelihood ratio over the unknown change point but on summation of likelihood ratios (i.e., on averaging over the uniform prior distribution). As shown in
[
18
]
, the SR procedure is second-order asymptotically minimax with respect to Pollak’s measure:

\inf_{\tau\in\mathcal{C}_{\alpha}}\mathrm{SADD}\left(\tau\right)=\mathrm{SADD}\left(\tau_{\text{SR}}\right)+O(1)\quad\text{as}~\alpha\to 0.

The CuSum procedure with a certain threshold
b_{\alpha}
also has a second-order optimality property with respect to the risk
\mathrm{SADD}\left(\tau\right)
. A detailed numerical comparison of CuSum and SR procedures for i.i.d. models was performed in
[
19
]
.

II-B

Information Bounds for Non-stationary Post-Change Observations

In the case where both the pre- and post-change observations are independent and the post-change observations are non-stationary, the log-likelihood ratio is:

Z_{n,k}=\log\frac{p_{1,n,k}(X_{n})}{p_{0}(X_{n})}

(8)

where
n\geq k\geq 1
. Here
k
is a hypothesized change-point and
X_{n}
is drawn from the true distribution
\mathbb{P}_{\nu}
(
\nu\in[1,\infty)
or
\nu=\infty
).

In the classical i.i.d. model described in Section
II-A
, the cumulative KL-divergence after the change point increases linearly in the number of observations. We generalize this condition as follows.
Let
g_{\nu}:\mathbb{R}^{+}\to\mathbb{R}^{+}
be an increasing and continuous function, which we will refer to as
growth function
. Note that the inverse of
g_{\nu}
, denoted by
g_{\nu}^{-1}
, exists and is also increasing and continuous. We assume that the expected sum of the log-likelihood ratios under
\mathbb{P}_{\nu}
, which corresponds to the cumulative KL-divergence for our non-stationary model, matches the value of the growth function at all positive integers, i.e.,

g_{\nu}(n)=\sum_{i=\nu}^{\nu+n-1}\mathbb{E}_{\nu}\left[Z_{i,\nu}\right],\forall n\geq 1

(9)

Furthermore, we assume that
\mathbb{E}_{\nu}\left[Z_{i,\nu}\right]>0
for all
i\geq\nu

and that for each
x>0

g^{-1}(x):=\sup_{\nu\geq 1}g_{\nu}^{-1}(x)

(10)

exists. Note that
g^{-1}
is also increasing and continuous. We also assume that
g_{\nu}(n)
diverges for all
\nu\geq 1
, and thus

g^{-1}(x)
is properly defined on the entire positive real line. In the special case where the post-change distribution is invariant to the change-point
\nu
, i.e., for
j\geq 0
,
p_{1,\nu+j,\nu}
is not a function of
\nu
, we have
g\equiv g_{\nu}
and
g^{-1}\equiv g_{\nu}^{-1}
for all
\nu\geq 1
.

In order for change detection procedures to perform well it is necessary for the cumulative KL divergence between post- and pre-change distributions to grow sufficiently fast with
n
, e.g., faster than
\log n
. That is, the inverse
g^{-1}(x)
cannot grow too fast. This fact was
discussed in
[
9
]
for the hypothesis testing problem where it was shown that if
g(n)\sim\log n
, then the performance of Wald’s SPRT is extremely poor. The same is true for change detection problems.

The following key assumption on
g^{-1}(x)
guarantees the asymptotic optimality solution to the minimax problem in which we are interested:

\log g^{-1}(x)=o(x)\quad\text{as}~x\to\infty.

(11)

To better understand this condition, we provide three special cases below:

1.

The post-change observations are independent and stationary (as in the classical case). Here
g^{-1}(x)
is linear in
x
. Thus,
\log g^{-1}(x)=O(\log x)=o(x)
, and condition (
11
) is always satisfied.

2.

The KL divergence between the post- and pre-change distributions always increases (or increases asymptotically). Intuitively, this means that the post-change distributions increasingly drift away from that of the pre-change over time, as in Example
II.1
below and in the pandemic monitoring example in Section
V-D
. In this case,
g^{-1}(x)
grows at a slower than linear rate, and thus condition (
11
) is always satisfied.

3.

The KL divergence between the post- and pre-change distributions gradually decreases to 0. Intuitively, this means that the post-change distributions gradually recovers that of the pre-change over time. Condition (
11
) guarantees detection for slow enough recovery, specifically, when the post-change KL divergence satisfies
\mathbb{E}_{\nu}\left[Z_{n,\nu}\right]\sim n^{-\theta}
with
\theta<1
as the decay factor. In this case,
\log g^{-1}(x)=O((1-\theta)^{-1}\log x)=o(x)
. Obviously, condition (
11
) fails if
\mathbb{E}_{\nu}\left[Z_{n,\nu}\right]\sim n^{-1}
, i.e., when
g(n)\sim\log n
and hence
\log g^{-1}(x)\sim x
. We provide an example and some simulations for diminishing KL divergence between the post- and pre-change distributions in Section
V-B
.

We should note that such a growth function
g(n)
has been adopted previously in sequential hypothesis testing with non-stationary observations
[
1
, Sec. 3.4]
, but not in QCD problem formulations such as the one considered here.

The proof of asymptotic optimality is performed in two steps. First, we derive a first-order asymptotic (as
\alpha\to 0
) lower bound for the maximal expected detection delays
\inf_{\tau\in\mathcal{C}_{\alpha}}\mathrm{WADD}\left(\tau\right)
and
\inf_{\tau\in\mathcal{C}_{\alpha}}\mathrm{SADD}\left(\tau\right)
. To this end, we need the following
right-tail condition for the log-likelihood ratio process:

\sup_{\nu\geq 1}\mathbb{P}_{\nu}\left\{\max_{t\leq n}\sum_{i=\nu}^{\nu+t-1}Z_{i,\nu}\geq(1+\delta)g_{\nu}(n)\right\}\xrightarrow{n\to\infty}0\quad\forall\delta>0,

(12)

assuming that for all
\nu\geq 1

\frac{\sum_{i=\nu}^{\nu+t-1}Z_{i,\nu}}{g_{\nu}(t)}\xrightarrow[t\to\infty]{\text{in}~\mathbb{P}_{\nu}\text{-probability}}1.

At the second stage, we show that this lower bound is attained for the WL-CuSum procedure under the following left-tail condition

\max_{t\geq\nu\geq 1}\mathbb{P}_{\nu}\left\{\sum_{i=t}^{t+n-1}Z_{i,t}\leq(1-\delta)g_{\nu}(n)\right\}\xrightarrow{n\to\infty}0\quad\forall\delta\in(0,1).

(13)

The following lemma provides sufficient conditions under which conditions (
12
) and (
13
)
hold for the sequence of independent and non-stationary observations. Hereafter we use the notation
\mathrm{Var}_{\nu}\left(Y\right)=\mathbb{E}_{\nu}\left[Y^{2}-\mathbb{E}_{\nu}\left[Y\right]^{2}\right]

for variance of the random variable
Y
under distribution
\mathbb{P}_{\nu}
.

Lemma II.1
.

Consider the growth function
g_{\nu}(n)
defined in (
9
). Suppose that the sum of variances of the log-likelihood ratios satisfies

\sup_{t\geq\nu\geq 1}\frac{1}{g_{\nu}^{2}(n)}\sum_{i=t}^{t+n-1}\mathrm{Var}_{\nu}\left(Z_{i,t}\right)\xrightarrow{n\to\infty}0

(14)

Then condition (
12
) holds.

If, in addition, for all
\nu\geq 1
and all positive integers
\Delta
,

\mathbb{E}_{\nu}\left[Z_{i,\nu}\right]\leq\mathbb{E}_{\nu}\left[Z_{i+\Delta,\nu+\Delta}\right],

(15)

then condition (
13
) holds.

The proof is given in the appendix.

Remark
.

One can generalize condition (
15
) in a way that either
\mathbb{E}_{\nu}\left[Z_{i,\nu}\right]\leq\mathbb{E}_{\nu}\left[Z_{i+\Delta,\nu+\Delta}\right]
or

\frac{1}{g_{\nu}(n)}\sum_{i=\nu}^{\nu+n-1}\left(\mathbb{E}_{\nu}\left[Z_{i,\nu}\right]-\mathbb{E}_{\nu}\left[Z_{i+\Delta,\nu+\Delta}\right]\right)=o(1)

holds for all positive integers
\Delta
.

Example II.1
.

Consider the following Gaussian exponential mean-change (GEM) detection problem. Denote by
{\cal N}(\mu_{0},\sigma_{0}^{2})
the Gaussian distribution with mean
\mu_{0}
and variance
\sigma_{0}^{2}
. Let
X_{1},\dots,X_{\nu-1}
be distributed as
{\cal N}(\mu_{0},\sigma_{0}^{2})
, and for all
n\geq\nu
let
X_{n}
be distributed as
{\cal N}(\mu_{0}e^{\theta(n-\nu)},\sigma_{0}^{2})
. Here
\theta
is some positive fixed parameter. The log-likelihood ratio is given by:

\displaystyle Z_{n,t}=\log\frac{p_{1,n,t}(X_{n})}{p_{0}(X_{n})}

\displaystyle=-\frac{(X_{n}-\mu_{0}e^{\theta(n-t)})^{2}}{2\sigma_{0}^{2}}+\frac{(X_{n}-\mu_{0})^{2}}{2\sigma_{0}^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mu_{0}}{\sigma_{0}^{2}}(e^{\theta(n-t)}-1)X_{n}-\frac{\mu_{0}^{2}(e^{2\theta(n-t)}-1)}{2\sigma_{0}^{2}}.

(16)

Now, the growth function can be calculated as

g_{\nu}(n)=\sum_{i=\nu}^{\nu+n-1}\mathbb{E}_{\nu}\left[Z_{i,\nu}\right]=\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\frac{\mu_{0}^{2}}{2\sigma_{0}^{2}}(e^{\theta i}-1)^{2}.

(17)

Since the post-change distribution is invariant to the change-point
\nu
,
g^{-1}(n)=g_{1}^{-1}(n)=O(\log n)\implies\log g^{-1}(n)=o(n)
, which satisfies (
11
). Also, the sum of variances of the log-likelihood ratios is

\sum_{i=t}^{t+n-1}\mathrm{Var}_{\nu}\left(Z_{i,t}\right)=\sum_{i=t}^{t+n-1}\frac{\mu_{0}^{2}}{\sigma_{0}^{4}}(e^{\theta(i-t)}-1)^{2}\mathrm{Var}_{\nu}\left(X_{i}\right)=\frac{2}{\sigma_{0}^{2}}g_{\nu}(n)=o(g_{\nu}^{2}(n))

for all
t\geq\nu
, which establishes condition (
14
).
Further, for any
i\geq\nu
and
\Delta\geq 1
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\nu}\left[Z_{i+\Delta,\nu+\Delta}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{\mu_{0}}{\sigma_{0}^{2}}(e^{\theta(i-\nu)}-1)\mathbb{E}_{\nu}\left[X_{i+\Delta}\right]-\frac{\mu_{0}^{2}(e^{2\theta(i-\nu)}-1)}{2\sigma_{0}^{2}}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\mu_{0}}{\sigma_{0}^{2}}(e^{\theta(i-\nu)}-1)\mathbb{E}_{\nu}\left[X_{i}\right]-\frac{\mu_{0}^{2}(e^{2\theta(i-\nu)}-1)}{2\sigma_{0}^{2}}=\mathbb{E}_{\nu}\left[Z_{i,\nu}\right]

which establishes condition (
15
).

The following theorem gives a lower bound on the worst-case average detection delays as
\alpha\to 0
in class
\mathcal{C}_{\alpha}
.

Theorem II.1
.

For
\delta\in(0,1)
let

h_{\delta}(\alpha):=g^{-1}((1-\delta)|\log\alpha|).

(18)

Suppose that
g^{-1}(x)
satisfies (
11
). Then for all
\delta\in(0,1)
and some

\nu\geq 1

\lim_{\alpha\to 0}\sup_{\tau\in\mathcal{C}_{\alpha}}\mathbb{P}_{\nu}\left\{\nu\leq\tau<\nu+h_{\delta}(\alpha)\right\}=0

(19)

and as
\alpha\to 0
,

\inf_{\tau\in\mathcal{C}_{\alpha}}\mathrm{WADD}\left(\tau\right)\geq\inf_{\tau\in\mathcal{C}_{\alpha}}\mathrm{SADD}\left(\tau\right)\geq g^{-1}(\left|\log{\alpha}\right|)(1+o(1)).

(20)

Proof.

Obviously, for any Markov time
\tau
,

\mathrm{WADD}\left(\tau\right)\geq\mathrm{SADD}\left(\tau\right)\geq\mathbb{E}_{\nu}\left[(\tau-\nu)^{+}\right].

Therefore, to prove the asymptotic lower bound (
20
) we have to show that as
\alpha\to 0
,

\sup_{\nu\geq 1}\mathbb{E}_{\nu}\left[(\tau-\nu)^{+}\right]\geq g^{-1}(|\log{\alpha}|)(1+o(1)),

(21)

where the
o(1)
term on the right-hand side does not depend on
\tau
, i.e., uniform in
\tau\in\mathcal{C}_{\alpha}
.

To begin, let the stopping time
\tau\in{\cal C}_{\alpha}
and note that by Markov’s inequality,

\mathbb{E}_{\nu}\left[(\tau-\nu)^{+}\right]\geq h_{\delta}(\alpha)\mathbb{P}_{\nu}\left\{(\tau-\nu)^{+}\geq h_{\delta}(\alpha)\right\}.

Hence, if assertion (
19
) holds, then for some
\nu\geq 1

\inf_{\tau\in\mathcal{C}_{\alpha}}\mathbb{P}_{\nu}\left\{(\tau-\nu)^{+}\geq h_{\delta}(\alpha)\right\}=1-o(1)\quad\text{as}~\alpha\to 0.

This implies the asymptotic inequality

\inf_{\tau\in\mathcal{C}_{\alpha}}\mathbb{E}_{\nu}\left[(\tau-\nu)^{+}\right]\geq h_{\delta}(\alpha)(1+o(1)),

(22)

which holds for an arbitrary
\delta\in(0,1)
and some
\nu
. Since by our assumption the function
h_{\delta}(\alpha)
is continuous,
taking the limit
\delta\to 0
and maximizing over
\nu\geq 1
yields inequality (
21
).

It remains to prove (
19
).
Changing the measure
\mathbb{P}_{\infty}\to\mathbb{P}_{\nu}
and using Wald’s likelihood ratio identity, we obtain the following chain of equalities and inequalities
for any
C>0
and
\delta\in(0,1)
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\infty}\left\{\nu\leq\tau<\nu+h_{\delta}(\alpha)\right\}=\mathbb{E}_{\nu}\left[\mathds{1}\{0\leq\tau-\nu<h_{\delta}(\alpha)\}\exp\left(-\sum_{i=\nu}^{\tau}Z_{i,\nu}\right)\right]

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{E}_{\nu}\left[\mathds{1}\{0\leq\tau-\nu<h_{\delta}(\alpha),\sum_{i=\nu}^{\tau}Z_{i,\nu}<C\}\exp\left(-\sum_{i=\nu}^{\tau}Z_{i,\nu}\right)\right]

\displaystyle\geq e^{-C}\mathbb{P}_{\nu}\left\{0\leq\tau-\nu<h_{\delta}(\alpha),\max_{0\leq n-\nu<h_{\delta}(\alpha)}\sum_{i=\nu}^{n}Z_{i,\nu}<C\right\}

\displaystyle\geq e^{-C}\left(\mathbb{P}_{\nu}\left\{0\leq\tau-\nu<h_{\delta}(\alpha)\right\}-\mathbb{P}_{\nu}\left\{\max_{0\leq n<h_{\delta}(\alpha)}\sum_{i=\nu}^{\nu+n}Z_{i,\nu}\geq C\right\}\right),

where the last inequality follows from the fact that
\Pr({\cal A}\cap{\cal B})=\Pr({\cal A})-\Pr({\cal B}^{c})
for any events
{\cal A}
and
{\cal B}
, where

{\cal B}^{c}
is the complement event of
{\cal B}
. Setting
C=g(h_{\delta}(\alpha))(1+\delta)=(1-\delta^{2})|\log\alpha|
yields

\mathbb{P}_{\nu}\left\{\nu\leq\tau<\nu+h_{\delta}(\alpha)\right\}\leq\kappa^{(\nu)}_{\delta,\alpha}(\tau)+\sup_{\nu\geq 1}\beta^{(\nu)}_{\delta,\alpha},

(23)

where

\kappa^{(\nu)}_{\delta,\alpha}(\tau)=e^{(1-\delta^{2})|\log\alpha|}\mathbb{P}_{\infty}\left\{0\leq\tau-\nu<h_{\delta}(\alpha)\right\}

and

\beta^{(\nu)}_{\delta,\alpha}=\mathbb{P}_{\nu}\left\{\max_{0\leq n<h_{\delta}(\alpha)}\sum_{i=\nu}^{\nu+n}Z_{i,\nu}\geq(1+\delta)g(h_{\delta}(\alpha))\right\}.

Since
g(h_{\delta}(\alpha))\to\infty
as
\alpha\to 0
, by condition (
12
),

\sup_{\nu\geq 1}\beta^{(\nu)}_{\delta,\alpha}\to 0.

(24)

Next we turn to the evaluation of the term
\kappa^{(\nu)}_{\delta,\alpha}(\tau)
for any stopping time
\tau\in{\cal C}_{\alpha}
.
It follows from Lemma 2.1 in
[
2
, page 72]
that for any
M<\alpha^{-1}
, there exists some
\ell\geq 1
(possibly depending on
\alpha
) such that

\mathbb{P}_{\infty}\left\{\ell\leq\tau<\ell+M\right\}\leq\mathbb{P}_{\infty}\left\{\tau<\ell+M|\tau\geq\ell\right\}<M\,\alpha,

(25)

so for some
\nu\geq 1
,

\kappa^{(\nu)}_{\delta,\alpha}(\tau)\leq M\alpha e^{(1-\delta^{2})|\log\alpha|}=M\alpha^{\delta^{2}}.

If we choose
M\leq M_{\alpha}=\left\lfloor h_{\delta}(\alpha)^{2}\right\rfloor\Big|_{\delta=0}=\left\lfloor(g^{-1}(|\log\alpha|))^{2}\right\rfloor
, then for all sufficiently small
\alpha
,

\log M\leq 2\log g^{-1}(|\log\alpha|)=o(|\log\alpha|)

so that the condition (
11
) is satisfied. Furthermore,

M_{\alpha}\,\alpha^{p}\xrightarrow{\alpha\to 0}0\quad\text{as}~\alpha\to 0

for any
p>0
. To see this, assume for purpose of contradiction that there exists some
p_{0}>0
and
c_{0}>0
such that

\lim_{\alpha\to 0}M_{\alpha}\alpha^{p_{0}}=c_{0}
. Then, since
\lim_{\alpha\to 0}\alpha^{-p_{0}}\neq 0
,

\lim_{\alpha\to 0}\log M_{\alpha}=p_{0}\lim_{\alpha\to 0}|\log\alpha|+\log c_{0}
and thus
\log M_{\alpha}\neq o(|\log\alpha|)
. Hence, it follows that for some

\nu\geq 1
, which may depend on
\alpha
, as
\alpha\to 0

\inf_{\tau\in\mathcal{C}_{\alpha}}\kappa^{(\nu)}_{\delta,\alpha}(\tau)\leq M_{\alpha}\alpha^{\delta^{2}}\to 0.

(26)

Combining (
23
), (
24
), and (
26
) we obtain that for some
\nu\geq 1

\mathbb{P}_{\nu}\left\{\nu\leq\tau<\nu+h_{\delta}(\alpha)\right\}\leq M_{\alpha}\alpha^{\delta^{2}}+\sup_{\nu\geq 1}\beta^{(\nu)}_{\delta,\alpha}=o(1),

where the
o(1)
term is uniform over all
\nu\geq 1
. This yields assertion (
19
), and the proof is complete.
∎

II-C

Asymptotically Optimal Detection for Non-stationary Post-Change Observations with Known Distributions

Recall that under the classical setting, Page’s CuSum procedure (in (
5
)) is optimal and has the following structure:

\tau_{\text{Page}}\left(b\right)=\inf\left\{n:\max_{1\leq k\leq n+1}\sum_{i=k}^{n}Z_{i}\geq b\right\}

(27)

where
Z_{i}
is the log-likelihood ratio when the post-change distributions are stationary. When the post-change distributions are potentially non-stationary, the CuSum stopping rule is defined similarly as:

\tau_{C}\left(b\right):=\inf\left\{n:\max_{1\leq k\leq n+1}\sum_{i=k}^{n}Z_{i,k}\geq b\right\}

(28)

where
Z_{i,k}
represents the log-likelihood ratio between densities
p_{1,i,k}
and
p_{0}
for observation
X_{i}
(defined in (
8
)). Here
i
is the time index and
k
is the hypothesized change point. Note that if the post-change distributions are indeed stationary, i.e.,
p_{1,i,k}\equiv p_{1}
, we would get
Z_{i,k}\equiv Z_{i}
for all
k\leq i
, and thus
\tau_{C}\equiv\tau_{\text{Page}}
.

Page’s classical CuSum algorithm admits a recursive way to compute its test statistic. Unfortunately, despite having independent observations, the test statistic in (
28
) cannot be computed recursively, even for the special case where the post-change distribution is invariant to the change-point as in Example
II.1
.

Example II.2
.

Consider the Gaussian Exponential Mean-Change problem defined in Example
II.1
. Suppose
\mu_{0}=\sigma_{0}^{2}=\theta=1
. Then, the log-likelihood ratio is given by

Z_{n,t}=(e^{n-t}-1)X_{n}-\frac{e^{2(n-t)}-1}{2}.

Note that
Z_{n,t}
is a (linear) function of
X_{n}
. Consider the following realization:

X_{1}=1,\quad X_{2}=0,\quad X_{3}=10.

It can be verified that

\arg\max_{1\leq k\leq 3}\sum_{i=k}^{2}Z_{i,k}=2,~\text{and}~\arg\max_{1\leq k\leq 4}\sum_{i=k}^{3}Z_{i,k}=1.

Note that maximizer
k^{*}
goes backward in time in this case, in contrast to what happens when both the pre- and post-change observations follow i.i.d. models. The test statistic at time
n=2
is a function of only
X_{2}
, and this is insufficient to construct the test statistic at time
n=3
, which is a function of
X_{1}
, in addition to being a function of
X_{2}
, and
X_{3}
.

For computational tractability we therefore consider a window limited version of the CuSum procedure in (
28
):

\tilde{\tau}_{C}\left(b\right):=\inf\left\{n:\max_{n-m\leq k\leq{n+1}}\sum_{i=k}^{n}Z_{i,k}=:W(n)\geq b\right\}

(29)

where
m
is the window size. For
n<m
maximization is performed over
1\leq k\leq n
. In the asymptotic setting,
m=m_{\alpha}
depends on
\alpha
and should go to infinity as
\alpha\to 0
with certain appropriate rate.
Specifically, following a similar condition that Lai
[
8
]
used in the asymptotically stationary case, we shall require that
m_{\alpha}\to\infty
as
\alpha\to 0

in such a way that

\liminf_{\alpha\to 0}m_{\alpha}/g^{-1}(\left|\log\alpha\right|)>1.

(30)

Since the range for the maximum is smaller in
\tilde{\tau}_{C}(b)
than in
\tau_{C}(b)
, given any realization of
X_{1},X_{2},\ldots
, if the test statistic of
\tilde{\tau}_{C}(b)
crosses the threshold
b
at some time
n
, so does that of
\tau_{C}(b)
. Therefore, for any fixed threshold
b>0
,

\tau_{C}(b)\leq\tilde{\tau}_{C}(b)

(31)

almost surely.

In the following, we first control the asymptotic false alarm rate of
\tilde{\tau}_{C}(b)
with an appropriately chosen threshold in Lemma
II.2
. Then we obtain asymptotic approximation of the expected detection delays of
\tilde{\tau}_{C}(b)
in Theorem
II.2
. Finally, we combine these two results and provide an asymptotically optimal solution to the problem in (
1
) in Theorem
II.3
.

Lemma II.2
.

Suppose that
b_{\alpha}=\left|\log\alpha\right|
. Then

\mathrm{FAR}\left(\tilde{\tau}_{C}(b_{\alpha})\right)\leq\alpha\quad\text{for all}~\alpha\in(0,1),

(32)

i.e.,
\tilde{\tau}_{C}(b_{\alpha})\in\mathcal{C}_{\alpha}
.

Proof.

Define the statistic

R_{n}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\exp\left(\sum_{i=k}^{n}Z_{i,k}\right),\quad R_{0}=0

and the corresponding stopping time
T_{b}:=\inf\{n:R_{n}\geq e^{b}\}
. We now show that

\mathbb{E}_{\infty}\left[T_{b}\right]\geq e^{b}
, which implies that
\mathbb{E}_{\infty}\left[\tilde{\tau}_{C}(b)\right]\geq e^{b}
for any
b>0
since, evidently,

\tilde{\tau}_{C}(b)\geq T_{b}
for any
b>0
. Recall that
{\cal F}_{n}=\sigma(X_{\ell},1\leq\ell\leq n)
denotes a sigma-algebra generated by
(X_{1},\dots,X_{n})
.
Since
\mathbb{E}_{\infty}\left[e^{Z_{n,k}}|{\cal F}_{n-1}\right]=1
, it is easy to see that

\mathbb{E}_{\infty}\left[R_{n}|{\cal F}_{n-1}\right]=1+R_{n-1}\quad\text{for}~n\geq 1.

Consequently, the statistic
\{R_{n}-n\}_{n\geq 1}
is a zero-mean
(\mathbb{P}_{\infty},{\cal F}_{n})
-martingale. It suffices to assume that
\mathbb{E}_{\infty}\left[T_{b}\right]<\infty

since otherwise the statement is trivial. Then,
\mathbb{E}_{\infty}\left[R_{T_{b}}-T_{b}\right]
exists and also

\liminf_{n\to\infty}\int_{\{T_{b}>n\}}|R_{n}-n|\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}_{\infty}=0

since
0\leq R_{n}<e^{b}
on the event
\{T_{b}>n\}
. Hence, we can apply the optional sampling theorem
(see, e.g.
[
1
, Th 2.3.1, page 31]
), which yields

\mathbb{E}_{\infty}\left[R_{T_{b}}\right]=\mathbb{E}_{\infty}\left[T_{b}\right]
. Since
R_{T_{b}}\geq e^{b}
it follows that
\mathbb{E}_{\infty}\left[\tilde{\tau}_{C}(b)\right]\geq\mathbb{E}_{\infty}\left[T_{b}\right]\geq e^{b}
.

Now, setting
b_{\alpha}=\left|\log\alpha\right|
implies the inequality

\mathbb{E}_{\infty}\left[\tilde{\tau}_{C}(b_{\alpha})\right]\geq e^{b_{\alpha}}=\frac{1}{\alpha}

(33)

(for any
m_{\alpha}\geq 1
), and therefore (
32
) follows.
∎

The following result establishes asymptotic performance of the WL-CuSum procedure given in (
29
) for large threshold values.

Theorem II.2
.

Fix
\delta\in(0,1)
and let
N_{b,\delta}:=\lfloor g^{-1}(b/(1-\delta))\rfloor
.
Suppose that in the WL-CuSum procedure the size of the window
m=m_{b}
diverges (as
b\to\infty
) in such a way that

m_{b}\geq N_{b,\delta}(1+o(1)).

(34)

Further, suppose that conditions (
12
) and (
13
) hold for
Z_{n,k}
when
n\geq k\geq 1
. Then, as
b\to\infty
,

\mathrm{SADD}\left(\tilde{\tau}_{C}(b)\right)\sim\mathrm{WADD}\left(\tilde{\tau}_{C}(b)\right)\sim g^{-1}(b).

(35)

Proof.

Since
\mathrm{FAR}\left(\tilde{\tau}_{C}(b)\right)\leq e^{-b}
, the WL-CuSum procedure
\tilde{\tau}_{C}(b)
belongs to class
\mathcal{C}_{\alpha}
with
\alpha=e^{-b}
.
Hence, replacing
\alpha
by
e^{-b}
in the asymptotic lower bound (
20
)
in Theorem
II.1
, we obtain that under condition (
12
) the following asymptotic lower bound holds:

\liminf_{b\to\infty}\frac{\mathrm{WADD}\left(\tilde{\tau}_{C}(b))\right)}{g^{-1}(b)}\geq\liminf_{b\to\infty}\frac{\mathrm{SADD}\left(\tilde{\tau}_{C}(b))\right)}{g^{-1}(b)}\geq 1.

(36)

Thus, to establish (
35
) it suffices to show that under condition (
13
) as
b\to\infty

\mathrm{WADD}\left(\tilde{\tau}_{C}(b))\right)\leq g^{-1}(b)(1+o(1)).

(37)

Note that we have the following chain of equalities and inequalities:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\nu}\left[(\tilde{\tau}_{C}(b))-\nu)^{+}|{\cal F}_{\nu-1}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\int_{\ell N_{b,\delta}}^{(\ell+1)N_{b,\delta}}\mathbb{P}_{\nu}\left\{\tilde{\tau}_{C}(b)-\nu>t|{\cal F}_{\nu-1}\right\}\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\leq N_{b,\delta}+\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}\int_{\ell N_{b,\delta}}^{(\ell+1)N_{b,\delta}}\mathbb{P}_{\nu}\left\{\tilde{\tau}_{C}(b)-\nu>t|{\cal F}_{\nu-1}\right\}\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\leq N_{b,\delta}+\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}\int_{\ell N_{b,\delta}}^{(\ell+1)N_{b,\delta}}\mathbb{P}_{\nu}\left\{\tilde{\tau}_{C}(b)-\nu>\ell N_{b,\delta}|{\cal F}_{\nu-1}\right\}\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=N_{b,\delta}\left(1+\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}_{\nu}\left\{\tilde{\tau}_{C}(b)-\nu>\ell N_{b,\delta}|{\cal F}_{\nu-1}\right\}\right).

(38)

Define
\lambda_{n,k}:=\sum_{i=k}^{n}Z_{i,k}
and
K_{n}:=\nu+nN_{b,\delta}
. We have
W(n)=\max_{n-m_{b}<k\leq n}\lambda_{k,n}
.
Since by condition (
34
)
m_{b}>N_{b,\delta}
(for a sufficiently large
b
), for any
n\geq 1
,

W(\nu+nN_{b,\delta})\geq\lambda_{K_{n},K_{n-1}}

and we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\nu}\left\{\tilde{\tau}_{C}(b)-\nu>\ell N_{b,\delta}|{\cal F}_{\nu-1}\right\}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}_{\nu}\left\{W(1)<b,\dots,W(\nu+\ell N_{b,\delta})<b|{\cal F}_{\nu-1}\right\}

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}_{\nu}\left\{W(\nu+N_{b,\delta})<b,\dots,W(\nu+\ell N_{b,\delta})<b|{\cal F}_{\nu-1}\right\}

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}_{\nu}\left\{\lambda_{K_{1},K_{0}}<b,\dots,\lambda_{K_{\ell},K_{\ell-1}}<b|{\cal F}_{\nu-1}\right\}

\displaystyle=\prod_{n=1}^{\ell}\mathbb{P}_{\nu}\left\{\lambda_{K_{n},K_{n-1}}<b\right\},

(39)

where the last equality follows from independence of the increments of
\{\lambda_{t,n}\}_{n\geq t}
.

By condition (
13
), for a sufficiently large
b
there exists a small
\varepsilon_{b}
such that

\mathbb{P}_{\nu}\left\{\lambda_{K_{n},K_{n-1}}<b\right\}\leq\varepsilon_{b},\quad\forall n\geq 1.

Therefore, for any
\ell\geq 1
,

\mathbb{P}_{\nu}\left\{\tilde{\tau}_{C}(b)-\nu>\ell N_{b_{\alpha},\delta}|{\cal F}_{\nu-1}\right\}\leq\varepsilon_{b}^{\ell}.

Combining this inequality with (
II-C
) and using the fact that
\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}\varepsilon_{b}^{\ell}=\varepsilon_{b}(1-\varepsilon_{b})^{-1}
,
we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\nu}\left[(\tilde{\tau}_{C}(b)-\nu)^{+}|{\cal F}_{\nu-1}\right]\leq N_{b,\delta}\left(1+\frac{\varepsilon_{b}}{1-\varepsilon_{b}}\right)=\frac{\lfloor g^{-1}(b/(1-\delta))\rfloor}{1-\varepsilon_{b}}.

(40)

Since the right-hand side of this inequality does not depend on
\nu
,
g^{-1}(b/(1-\delta))\to\infty
as
b\to\infty
and
\varepsilon_{b}
and
\delta
can be arbitrarily small numbers, this implies the upper bound (
37
). The proof is complete.
∎

Using Lemma
II.2
and Theorem
II.2
, we obtain the following asymptotic result which establishes asymptotic optimality of the WL-CuSum procedure and its asymptotic operating characteristics.

Theorem II.3
.

Suppose that threshold
b_{\alpha}
is so selected that
b_{\alpha}\sim|\log\alpha|
as
\alpha\to 0
, in particular as
b_{\alpha}=|\log\alpha|
.
Further, suppose that left-tail (
12
) and right-tail (
13
) conditions hold for
Z_{n,k}
when
n\geq k\geq 1
.
Then, the WL-CuSum procedure in (
29
) with the window size
m_{\alpha}
that satisfies the condition

m_{\alpha}\geq g^{-1}(|\log\alpha|)(1+o(1))\quad\text{as}~\alpha\to 0

(41)

solves the problems (
1
) and (
7
) asymptotically to first order as
\alpha\to 0
, i.e.,

\begin{split}\inf_{\tau\in\mathcal{C}_{\alpha}}\mathrm{WADD}\left(\tau\right)&\sim\mathrm{WADD}\left(\tilde{\tau}_{C}(b_{\alpha})\right),\\
\inf_{\tau\in\mathcal{C}_{\alpha}}\mathrm{SADD}\left(\tau\right)&\sim\mathrm{SADD}\left(\tilde{\tau}_{C}(b_{\alpha})\right)\end{split}

(42)

and

\mathrm{SADD}\left(\tilde{\tau}_{C}(b_{\alpha})\right)\sim\mathrm{WADD}\left(\tilde{\tau}_{C}(b_{\alpha})\right)\sim g^{-1}(\left|\log{\alpha}\right|).

(43)

Proof.

Let
b_{\alpha}
be so selected that
\mathrm{FAR}\left(\tilde{\tau}_{C}(b_{\alpha})\right)\leq\alpha
and
b_{\alpha}\sim|\log\alpha|
as
\alpha\to 0
. Then by
Theorem
II.2
, as
\alpha\to 0

\mathrm{SADD}\left(\tilde{\tau}_{C}(b_{\alpha})\right)\sim\mathrm{WADD}\left(\tilde{\tau}_{C}(b_{\alpha})\right)\sim g^{-1}(|\log\alpha|).

Comparing these asymptotic equalities with the asymptotic lower bound (
20
) in
Theorem
II.1
immediately yields asymptotics (
42
) and (
43
).
In particular, if
b_{\alpha}=\left|\log\alpha\right|
, then by Lemma
II.2

\mathrm{FAR}\left(\tilde{\tau}_{C}(b_{\alpha})\right)\leq\alpha
, and therefore the assertions hold.∎

Remark
.

Clearly, the asymptotic optimality result still holds in the case where no window is applied, i.e.,
m_{\alpha}=n-1
.

Example II.3
.

Consider the same setting as in Example
II.1
. We have shown that conditions (
14
) and (
15
) hold in this setting, and thus (
12
) and (
13
) also hold by Lemma
II.1
. Considering the growth function
g_{\nu}(n)
given in (
17
), as
n\to\infty
, we obtain

g_{\nu}(n)=\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\frac{\mu_{0}^{2}}{2\sigma_{0}^{2}}(e^{\theta i}-1)^{2}=\frac{\mu_{0}^{2}}{2\sigma_{0}^{2}}e^{2\theta(n-1)}(1+o(1)).

Thus, as
y\to\infty
,

g^{-1}(y)=\frac{1}{2\theta}\log\left(\frac{2\sigma_{0}^{2}}{\mu_{0}^{2}}y\right)(1+o(1))

and if
b_{\alpha}=|\log\alpha|
or more generally
b_{\alpha}\sim|\log\alpha|
as
\alpha\to 0
we obtain

\displaystyle\mathrm{WADD}\left(\tilde{\tau}_{C}(b_{\alpha})\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\theta}\log\left(\frac{2\sigma_{0}^{2}}{\mu_{0}^{2}}\left|\log\alpha\right|\right)(1+o(1))

\displaystyle=O\left(\frac{1}{2\theta}\log(\left|\log\alpha\right|)\right).

(44)

III
Asymptotically Optimum Procedure for Non-Stationary Post-Change Observations with Parametric Uncertainty

We now study the case where the evolution of the post-change distribution is parametrized by an unknown but deterministic parameter
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Let
X_{\nu},X_{\nu+1},\dots

each have density
p_{1,0}^{\theta},p_{1,1}^{\theta},\dots
, respectively, with respect to the common non-degenerate measure
\mu
, when post-change parameter is
\theta
. Let
\mathbb{P}_{k,\theta}
and
\mathbb{E}_{k,\theta}
denote, respectively, the probability measure on the entire sequence of observations and expectation when the change point is
\nu=k<\infty
and the post-change parameter is
\theta
. Let
\Theta\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be an open and bounded set of parameter values. For any
n\geq k
and
\theta\in\Theta
the log-likelihood ratio process is given by

Z_{n,k}^{\theta}=\log\frac{p_{1,n,k}^{\theta}(X_{n})}{p_{0}(X_{n})}.

(45)

Also, the growth function in (
9
) is redefined as

g_{\nu,\theta}(n)=\sum_{i=\nu}^{\nu+n-1}\mathbb{E}_{\nu,\theta}\left[Z^{\theta}_{i,\nu}\right],\forall n\geq 1

(46)

and it is assumed that
g_{\theta}^{-1}(x)=\sup_{\nu\geq 1}g_{\nu,\theta}^{-1}(x)
exists. It is also assumed that

\log g_{\theta}^{-1}(x)=o(x)\quad\text{as}~x\to\infty.

(47)

The goal in this section is to solve the optimization problems (
1
) and (
7
) asymptotically as
\alpha\to 0
under parameter uncertainty. More specifically, for
\theta\in\Theta
, define Lorden’s and Pollak’s worst-case expected detection delay measures

\mathrm{WADD}_{\theta}\left(\tau\right):=\esssup\sup_{\nu\geq 1}\mathbb{E}_{\nu,\theta}\left[(\tau-\nu+1)^{+}|{\cal F}_{\nu-1}\right]

and

\mathrm{SADD}_{\theta}\left(\tau\right):=\sup_{\nu\geq 1}\mathbb{E}_{\nu,\theta}\left[\tau-\nu+1|\tau\geq\nu\right]

and the corresponding asymptotic optimization problems: find a change detection procedure
\tau^{*}
that minimizes these measures to first order in class

\mathcal{C}_{\alpha}
, i.e., for all
\theta\in\Theta
,

\lim_{\alpha\to 0}\frac{\inf_{\tau\in\mathcal{C}_{\alpha}}\mathrm{WADD}_{\theta}\left(\tau\right)}{\mathrm{WADD}_{\theta}\left(\tau^{*}\right)}=1,\quad\lim_{\alpha\to 0}\frac{\inf_{\tau\in\mathcal{C}_{\alpha}}\mathrm{SADD}_{\theta}\left(\tau\right)}{\mathrm{SADD}_{\theta}\left(\tau^{*}\right)}=1.

(48)

Consider the following WL-GLR-CuSum change detection procedure

\tilde{\tau}_{G}\left(b\right):=\inf\left\{n:\max_{n-m_{b}\leq k\leq{n+1}}\sup_{\theta\in\Theta_{b}}\sum_{i=k}^{n}Z_{i,k}^{\theta}\geq b\right\},

(49)

where
\Theta_{b}\nearrow\Theta
as
b\nearrow\infty
. For
n<m_{b}
maximization is performed over
1\leq k\leq n
. Therefore, it is guaranteed that
\theta\in\Theta_{b}
for all large enough
b
. Since we are interested in class

\mathcal{C}_{\alpha}=\{\tau:\mathrm{FAR}\left(\tau\right)\leq\alpha\}
, in which case both threshold
b=b_{\alpha}
and window size
m_{b}=m_{\alpha}
are the functions of
\alpha
, we will write

\Theta_{b}=\Theta_{\alpha}
and suppose that
\Theta_{\alpha}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
is compact for each
\alpha
. Hereafter we omit the dependency of

\hat{\theta}_{n,k}
on
\alpha
for brevity. In this paper, we focus on the case where
\Theta_{\alpha}
is continuous for all
\alpha
’s.
The discrete case is simpler and will be considered elsewhere.

The following assumption is made to guarantee the existence of an upper bound on FAR.

Assumption III.1
.

There exists
\varepsilon>0
such that for any large enough

\mathbb{P}_{\infty}\left\{\max_{(k,n):k\leq n\leq k+m_{b}}\sup_{\theta:\left\lVert\theta-\hat{\theta}_{n,k}\right\rVert<b^{-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}}}\lambda_{\text{max}}\left(-\nabla_{\theta}^{2}\sum_{i=k}^{n}Z_{i,k}^{\theta}\right)\leq 2b^{\varepsilon}\right\}\geq 1-\xi_{b}

where
\lambda_{\text{max}}\left(A\right)
represents the maximum absolute eigenvalue of a symmetric matrix
A
and
\xi_{b}\searrow 0
as
b\nearrow\infty
.

Example III.1
.

Consider again the Gaussian exponential mean-change detection problem in Example
II.1
. Now we consider the case where the exact value of the post-change exponent coefficient
\theta
is unknown and belongs to
\Theta=[\Theta_{\text{min}},\Theta_{\text{max}}]
. Note that
\theta
characterizes the entire post-change evolution rather than a single post-change distribution.
We shall verify Assumption
III.1
below.

Recalling the definition of log-likelihood ratio given in (
II.1
), for any
\theta\in\Theta
and
k\leq i\leq n
where
n-k\leq m_{b}
, we have

\displaystyle-\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\theta^{2}}Z^{\theta}_{i,k}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\theta^{2}}\left(\frac{\mu_{0}}{\sigma_{0}^{2}}(e^{\theta(i-k)}-1)X_{i}-\frac{\mu_{0}^{2}(e^{2\theta(i-k)}+1)}{2\sigma_{0}^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle=-\frac{\mu_{0}}{\sigma_{0}^{2}}(i-k)^{2}e^{\theta(i-k)}X_{i}+2(i-k)^{2}\frac{\mu_{0}^{2}e^{2\theta(i-k)}}{\sigma_{0}^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mu_{0}}{\sigma_{0}^{2}}(i-k)^{2}e^{\theta(i-k)}(2\mu_{0}e^{\theta(i-k)}-X_{i}).

(51)

Therefore,

\displaystyle\max_{(k,n):k\leq n\leq k+m_{b}}\sup_{\theta\in\Theta}\left|-\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\theta^{2}}\sum_{i=k}^{n}Z^{\theta}_{i,k}\right|

\displaystyle=\sup_{\theta\in\Theta}\max_{(k,n):k\leq n\leq k+m_{b}}\frac{\mu_{0}}{\sigma_{0}^{2}}\left|\sum_{i=k}^{n}(i-k)^{2}e^{\theta(i-k)}(2\mu_{0}e^{\theta(i-k)}-X_{i})\right|

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{\theta\in\Theta}\frac{\mu_{0}}{\sigma_{0}^{2}}m_{b}^{2}e^{\theta m_{b}}\left(2\mu_{0}m_{b}e^{\theta m_{b}}+\max_{(k,n):k\leq n\leq k+m_{b}}\left|\sum_{i=k}^{n}X_{i}\right|\right)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(*)}}{{\leq}}\sup_{\theta\in\Theta}\frac{4\mu_{0}^{2}}{\sigma_{0}^{2}}m_{b}^{3}e^{2\theta m_{b}}\leq\frac{4\mu_{0}^{2}}{\sigma_{0}^{2}}m_{b}^{3}e^{2\Theta_{\text{max}}m_{b}}

(52)

where
(*)
is true provided that

\max_{(k,n):k\leq n\leq k+m_{b}}\left|\sum_{i=k}^{n}X_{i}\right|<2\mu_{0}m_{b}e^{\theta m_{b}}.

Since
X_{i}
’s are i.i.d. under
\mathbb{P}_{\infty}
,
\sum_{i=k}^{n}X_{i}
has a Gaussian distribution with mean
\leq(m_{b}+1)\mu_{0}
and variance
\leq(m_{b}+1)\sigma_{0}^{2}
.
Therefore, for any
\theta\in\Theta

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\infty}\left\{\max_{(k,n):k\leq n\leq k+m_{b}}\left|\sum_{i=k}^{n}X_{i}\right|>2\mu_{0}m_{b}e^{\theta m_{b}}\right\}

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}_{\infty}\left\{\left|\sum_{i=1}^{m_{b}}X_{i}\right|>2\mu_{0}m_{b}e^{\theta m_{b}}\right\}

\displaystyle=2Q\left(\frac{2\mu_{0}m_{b}e^{\theta m_{b}}-m_{b}\mu_{0}}{\sigma_{0}\sqrt{m_{b}+1}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq 2\exp\left(-\frac{2\mu_{0}^{2}m_{b}^{2}(e^{\theta m_{b}}-1)^{2}}{\sigma_{0}^{2}(m_{b}+1)}\right)\searrow 0~\text{as $b\to\infty$}

where

Recalling the condition in (

) for the worst-case expected delay, we obtain that if we set

Then Assumption

holds when

Note that
\mathrm{WADD}_{\theta}\left(\tilde{\tau}_{G}(b)\right)\leq\mathrm{WADD}_{\theta}\left(\tilde{\tau}_{C}(b)\right)

IV
Extensions to Pointwise Optimality and Dependent Non-homogeneous Models

The measure of FAR that we have used in this paper (see (
4
)) is the inverse of the MTFA. However, the MTFA is a good measure of the FAR if, and only if, the pre-change distributions of the WL-CuSum stopping time
\tilde{\tau}_{C}(b)
and the WL-GLR-CuSum stopping time
\tilde{\tau}_{G}(b)
are approximately geometric.
While this geometric property can be established for i.i.d. data models (see, e.g., Pollak and Tartakovsky
[
20
]
and Yakir
[
21
]
), it is not neccessarily true for non-homogeneous and dependent data, as discussed in Mei
[
22
]
and Tartakovsky
[
23
]
.
Therefore, in general, the MTFA is not appropriate for measuring the FAR. In fact, large values of MTFA may not necessarily guarantee small values of the probability of false alarm as discussed in detail in
[
23
,
1
]
. When the post-change model is Gaussian non-stationary as defined in Example
II.1
, the MTFA
may still be an appropriate measure for false alarm rate, as shown in the simulation study in Section
V-C
. Based on this result we conjecture that the MTFA-based FAR constraint may be suitable for other independent and non-stationary data models as well. However, in general, this may not be the case, and
a more appropriate measure of the FAR in the general case may be the maximal (local) conditional probability of false alarm in the time interval
(k,k+m]
defined as
[
1
]
:

\mathrm{SPFA}_{m}(\tau)=\sup_{k\geq 0}\mathbb{P}_{\infty}\left\{\tau\leq k+m|\tau>k\right\}.

Then the constraint set in (
3
) can be replaced by set

\mathbb{C}_{\beta,m}=\{\tau:\mathrm{SPFA}_{m}(\tau)\leq\beta\}

of procedures for which the SPFA does not exceed a prespecified value
\beta\in(0,1)
.

Pergamenschtchikov and Tartakovsky
[
24
,
14
]
considered general stochastic models of dependent and
nonidentically distributed observations
but asymptotically homogeneous (i.e.,
g(n)=n
). They proved not only minimax optimality but also asymptotic pointwise optimality as
\beta\to 0

(i.e., for all change points
\nu\geq 1
) of the Shiryaev-Roberts (SR)
procedure for the simple post-change hypothesis, and the mixture SR for the composite post-change hypothesis in class
\mathbb{C}_{\beta,m}
, when

m=m_{\beta}
depends on
\beta
and goes to infinity as
\beta\to 0
at such a rate that
\log m_{\beta}=o(|\log\beta|)
.

The results of

[
24
,
14
]
can be readily extended to the asymptotically non-homogeneous case where the function
g(n)
increases with

n
faster than
\log n
. In particular, using the developed in
[
24
,
14
]
techniques based on embedding class
\mathbb{C}_{\beta,m}

in the Bayesian class with a geometric prior distribution for the change point and the upper-bounded weighted PFA,
it can be shown that the WL-CuSum
procedure (
29
) with
m_{\alpha}
replaced by
m_{\beta}
is first-order pointwise asymptotically optimal in class

\mathbb{C}_{\beta,m_{\beta}}=\mathbb{C}_{\beta}
as long as the uniform complete version
of the strong law of large numbers for the log-likelihood ratio holds, i.e., for all
\delta>0

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\sup_{\nu\geq 1}\mathbb{P}_{\nu}\left\{\left|\frac{1}{g_{\nu}(n)}\sum_{i=\nu}^{\nu+n-1}Z_{i,\nu}-1\right|>\delta\right\}<\infty,

where in the general non-i.i.d. case
the partial LLR
Z_{i,\nu}
is

Z_{i,\nu}=\log\frac{p_{1,i,\nu}(X_{i}|X_{1},\dots,X_{i-1})}{p_{0,i}(X_{i}|X_{1},\dots,X_{i-1})}.

Specifically, it can be established that for all fixed
\nu\geq 1
, as
\beta\to 0
,

\displaystyle\inf_{\tau\in\mathbb{C}_{\beta}}\mathrm{ADD}_{\nu}(\tau)\sim\mathrm{ADD}_{\nu}(\tilde{\tau}_{C}(b_{\alpha}))\sim g^{-1}(|\log\beta|),

where we used the notation
\mathrm{ADD}_{\nu}(\tau)=\mathbb{E}_{\nu}\left[\tau-\nu|\tau\geq\nu\right]
for the conditional average delay to detection. Similar results also hold
for the maximal average detection delays
\mathrm{WADD}\left(\tau\right)
and
\mathrm{SADD}\left(\tau\right)=\sup_{\nu\geq 1}\mathrm{ADD}_{\nu}(\tau)
.

It is worth noting that it follows from the proof of Theorem
II.1
that under condition (
12
) the following asymptotic lower bound holds for the average detection delay

\mathrm{ADD}_{\nu}(\tau)
uniformly for all values of the change point in class
\mathbb{C}_{\beta}
:

\inf_{\tau\in\mathbb{C}_{\beta}}\mathrm{ADD}_{\nu}(\tau)\geq g^{-1}(|\log\beta|)(1+o(1)),\quad\forall\nu\geq 1~\text{as}~\beta\to 0.

In the case where the post-change observations have parametric uncertainty, sufficient conditions for the optimality of the WL-GLR-CuSum procedure are more sophisticated – a probability in the vicinity of the true post-change parameter should be involved
[
14
]
.

Further details and the proofs are omitted and will be given elsewhere.

The problem is modeled as one of detecting a change in the mean of a Beta distribution as in

We assumed a start date of June 15th, 2021 for the monitoring, at which time the pandemic appeared to be in a steady state with incremental cases staying relatively flat. We observe that the WL-GLR-CuSum statistic significantly and persistently crosses the detection threshold around late July in all counties, which is strong indication of a new wave of the pandemic. More importantly, unlike the raw observations which are highly varying, the WL-GLR-CuSum statistic shows a clear dichotomy between the pre- and post-change settings, with the statistic staying near zero before the purported onset of the new wave, and taking off very rapidly (nearly vertically) after the onset.

VI
Conclusion

We considered the problem of the quickest detection of a change in the distribution of a sequence of independent observations, assuming that the pre-change observation are stationary with known distribution, while the post-change observations are non-stationary with possible parametric uncertainty. Specifically, we assumed that the cumulative KL divergence between the post-change and the pre-change distributions grows at least logarithmically after the change point. We derived a universal asymptotic lower bound on the worst-case expected detection delay under a constraint on the false alarm rate in this non-stationary setting, which had been previously derived only in the asymptotically stationary setting. We showed that the developed WL-CuSum procedure for known post-change distribution, as well as the developed WL-GLR-CuSum procedure for the unknown post-change parameters, asymptotically achieve the lower bound on the worst-case expected detection delay, as the false alarm rate goes to zero. We validated these theoretical results through numerical Monte-Carlo simulations. We also demonstrated that the proposed WL-GLR-CuSum procedure can be effectively used in monitoring pandemics. some possible avenues for future research, in particular, those allowing for dependent observations and more general false alarm constraints.
