Contextuality and inductive bias in quantum machine learning
Joseph Bowles, Victoria J Wright, Máté Farkas, Nathan Killoran, Maria Schuld

Abstract
Generalisation in machine learning often relies on the ability to encode structures present in data into an inductive bias of the model class. To understand the power of quantum machine learning, it is therefore crucial to identify the types of data structures that lend themselves naturally to quantum models. In this work we look to quantum contextuality—a form of nonclassicality with links to computational advantage—for answers to this question. We introduce a framework for studying contextuality in machine learning, which leads us to a definition of what it means for a learning model to be contextual. From this, we connect a central concept of contextuality, called operational equivalence, to the ability of a model to encode a linearly conserved quantity in its label space. A consequence of this connection is that contextuality is tied to expressivity: contextual model classes that encode the inductive bias are generally more expressive than their noncontextual counterparts. To demonstrate this, we construct an explicit toy learning problem—based on learning the payoff behaviour of a zero-sum game—for which this is the case. By leveraging tools from geometric quantum machine learning, we then describe how to construct quantum learning models with the associated inductive bias, and show through our toy problem that they outperform their corresponding classical surrogate models. This suggests that understanding learning problems of this form may lead to useful insights about the power of quantum machine learning.

Indeed, an analogous perspective is commonplace in quantum computation, where computational advantages are expected only for specific problems that happen to benefit from the peculiarities of quantum logic.

To repeat a common quip: many problems in machine learning are NP-hard, but neural networks don’t know that so they solve them anyway.

Quantum foundations is predominantly concerned with understanding the frontier between the quantum and classical world, and typically values a clear qualitative understanding of a phenomenon over purely mathematical knowledge. In the final part of the work, we study the performance of quantum models for problems that involve our contextuality-inspired bias (Section

In our numerical study, we show that a quantum model class that encodes our contextuality-inspired bias achieves a lower generalisation error than the corresponding surrogate model classes at a specific learning task, even after allowing for regularisation in the surrogate model. We argue that this is due to the fact that the bias cannot be easily encoded into the surrogate model class, which therefore cannot exploit this information during learning.

In Section
9

Many of these areas are classical in nature, and therefore suggests that quantum machine learning may be suited to tackling classical learning problems with a specific structure. Finally, in Section

Overall, we hope our approach and framework will lead to a new way of thinking about quantum machine learning, and ultimately lead to the identification of impactful problems where the specific structure of quantum theory makes quantum models the machine learning models of choice.

2.1
Procedures, preparations and effects

The simplest and most commonly studied scenario of contextuality is the prepare-and-measure scenario (see Figure
2
A). For our purposes, we imagine a user with access to a machine learning model, who can prepare the model in a specific state by inputting data into it. In contextuality, this is called a
preparation
, denoted
\textbf{S}\in\texttt{Preps}
(the letter
S
is used since a preparation can be thought of as a state), and is described via a
procedure
, denoted
L
. A procedure is simply a list of actions describing in words what the user does, e.g., ‘input the data point
\boldsymbol{x}
into the model’. We also assume that the user can query the model for different tasks and observe the corresponding predictions. These events are described by
effects
in contextuality. An effect, denoted
\textbf{E}=\omega|L\in\texttt{Effects}
is a procedure
L
together with a specific observation
\omega\in W_{L}
that signals success, where
W_{L}
is the set of all observations that may follow
L
. For example ‘query the model for task 1’ (procedure) and ‘observe the label +1’ (observation). An effect can therefore be thought of as a binary yes/no question related to a specific procedure
3
3

3

Whereas preparations can be thought of as the analogue of quantum states, effects can be thought of as the analogue of measurement operators.
.

Note that at this level of description we have not specified anything about how the model works (it could be quantum or classical for example), and are concerned only with descriptions of the actions and observations of the user that interacts with it. For this reason, this approach is often called an
operational
approach; hence the common appearance of the term ‘operational’ in the proceeding sections.

2.2
Operational statistics

For every
\textbf{S}\in\texttt{Preps}
and every
\textbf{E}\in\texttt{Effects}
, we assume there is a corresponding probability
P(\textbf{E}|\textbf{S})
that describes the likelihood of the successful outcome of
E
given
S
, and we call the set of all
P(\textbf{E}|\textbf{S})
the
operational statistics
. In our case,
P(\textbf{E}|\textbf{S})
is simply the probability to observe a particular label for some task (given by
E
) conditioned on certain data being input into the model (given by
\textbf{S})
. In addition to the effects that correspond to the possible predictions of the model, we also include the trivial effect
\Omega
that always occurs for any preparation,

\displaystyle P(\Omega|\textbf{S})=1\quad\forall\,\textbf{S}\in\texttt{Preps},

(1)

and the null effect
\emptyset
that never occurs,

\displaystyle P(\emptyset|\textbf{S})=0\quad\forall\,\textbf{S}\in\texttt{Preps}.

(2)

Operationally speaking, these effects correspond to the user simply ignoring the model and declaring either a successful or failed observation. We naturally assume that probabilities of effects corresponding to two mutually incompatible observations are additive. For example, if
\textbf{E}_{0\lor 1}=0\lor 1|L
is an effect corresponding to observing a label prediction
0
or
1
and
\textbf{E}_{0}=0|L
and
\textbf{E}_{1}=1|L
are those for labels

and
1
independently, then

\displaystyle P(\textbf{E}_{0\lor 1}|\textbf{S})=P(\textbf{E}_{0}|\textbf{S})+P(\textbf{E}_{1}|\textbf{S}).

(3)

With this, we can now define a measurement

\displaystyle\textbf{M}=\{\textbf{E}_{1},\textbf{E}_{2},\cdots,\textbf{E}_{d}\}

(4)

to be a collection of effects corresponding to the same procedure
L_{\textbf{M}}
with mutually incompatible observations

whose probabilities sum to 1:

\displaystyle\textbf{E}_{i}=\omega_{i}|L_{\textbf{M}},\quad\sum_{i}P(\textbf{E}_{i}|\textbf{S})=1\quad\forall\,\textbf{S}\in\texttt{Preps}.

(5)

we call the triple

\{\texttt{Preps},\texttt{Effects},P(\textbf{E}|\textbf{S})\}

of possible preparations, effects and operational statistics, an
operational scenario

4

4

This is analogous to the concept of ‘operational theory’ encountered in the generalized contextuality literature; here we use the word ‘scenario’ instead since we are not concerned with an entire physical theory.
.

2.3
Convex mixtures

A key ingredient of generalised contextuality is the ability to perform convex mixtures; i.e., the user is assumed to have access to a trusted source of randomness that they can use to probabilistically mix procedures. For example, given two preparations
\textbf{S}_{1}
and
\textbf{S}_{2}
with procedures
L_{1}
and
L_{2}
, the user may flip a biased coin (with bias
p
) and perform either
L_{1}
or
L_{2}
. We call the resulting preparation a
preparation density
, written

\displaystyle s=p\textbf{S}_{1}+(1-p)\textbf{S}_{2},

(6)

which results in a corresponding mixture of operational statistics,

\displaystyle P(\textbf{E}|p\textbf{S}_{1}+(1-p)\textbf{S}_{2})=pP(\textbf{E}|\textbf{S}_{1})+(1-p)P(\textbf{E}|\textbf{S}_{2})\quad\forall\textbf{E}\in\texttt{Effects}.

(7)

Similarly given two effects
\textbf{E}_{1}=\omega_{1}|L_{1}
and
\textbf{E}_{2}=\omega_{2}|L_{2}
, the user can flip a biased coin and realise the
effect density

\displaystyle e=p\textbf{E}_{1}+(1-p)\textbf{E}_{2},

(8)

such that

\displaystyle P(p\textbf{E}_{1}+(1-p)\textbf{E}_{2}|\textbf{S})=pP(\textbf{E}_{1}|\textbf{S})+(1-p)P(\textbf{E}_{2}|\textbf{S})\quad\forall\textbf{S}\in\texttt{Preps}.

(9)

By allowing arbitrary probabilistic mixtures of preparations and effects, we can therefore prepare any preparation density
s\in\text{conv}(\texttt{Preps})
and any effect density
e\in\text{conv}(\texttt{Effects})
, where
conv
denotes the convex hull of a set.

2.4
Operational equivalences

At the heart of generalised contextuality is the notion of operational equivalence, which relates to the possibility of distinguishing between pairs of preparation or effect densities. We say that two preparation densities
s_{1},s_{2}
are
operationally equivalent
, denoted
\sim
, iff they give identical predictions for all effects,

\displaystyle s_{1}\sim s_{2}\iff P(\textbf{E}|s_{1})=P(\textbf{E}|s_{2})\quad\forall\,\textbf{E}\in\texttt{Effects}.

(10)

Similarly, two effect densities
e_{1},e_{2}
are operationally equivalent iff they have the same probability of success for all preparations,

\displaystyle e_{1}\sim e_{2}\iff P(e_{1}|\textbf{S})=P(e_{2}|\textbf{S})\quad\forall\,\textbf{S}\in\texttt{Preps}.

(11)

Thus, a pair of preparations or effects are operationally equivalent if and only if they are indistinguishable with respect to any of the possible procedures and observations in the operational scenario.

Ontological models

An ontological model is a specific causal model (see Figure

5

One could also call these hidden variables.

The probability of an effect given some preparation is causally mediated through the variable

Each effect

\displaystyle P(\textbf{E}|\textbf{S})=\int_{\Lambda}\text{d}\lambda\,\mu_{\textbf{S}}(\lambda)\xi_{\textbf{E}}(\lambda).

Ontic state distributions and ontic response functions for preparation and effect densities are defined using the same additivity relations as (

2.6
Generalised noncontextuality of ontological models

Generalised noncontextuality is a constraint, called
ontological equivalence
, that is imposed on ontological models. Within a given ontological model of some operational scenario, we say that two preparation densities
s_{1}
and
s_{2}
are ontologically equivalent if

\displaystyle\mu_{s_{1}}(\lambda)=\mu_{s_{2}}(\lambda)\quad\forall\,\lambda,

(14)

i.e., they result in the same probability density over ontic states. Similarly, two effect densities
e_{1}
and
e_{2}
are ontologically equivalent if they have the same ontic response function:

\displaystyle\xi_{e_{1}}(\lambda)=\xi_{e_{2}}(\lambda)\quad\forall\,\lambda.

(15)

Generalised noncontextuality can now be understood as the following principle, which is often motivated by Leibniz’s principle of the identity of indiscernibles
[
51
]
:

operational equivalence
\iff
ontological
equivalence,

or equivalently

\displaystyle s_{1}\sim s_{2}

\displaystyle\iff\mu_{s_{1}}(\lambda)=\mu_{s_{2}}(\lambda)\quad\forall\,\lambda,

(16)

\displaystyle e_{1}\sim e_{2}

\displaystyle\iff\xi_{e_{1}}(\lambda)=\xi_{e_{2}}(\lambda)\quad\forall\,\lambda.

(17)

In other words,
if two preparation of effect densities are indistinguishable, then they should be described in the same way in the ontological model
. At this point it can be useful to make a link to Kochen-Specker noncontextuality for those that are familiar. In Kochen-Specker noncontextuality, one assigns the same deterministic response function to projectors that appear in different measurement contexts. This follows from an application of (
16
) and (
17
), where the projector that appears in two different measurements is understood as two distinct but operationally equivalent effects, and the determinism of the response functions is a consequence of preparation noncontextuality
[
52
]
. As we will see however, there are other operational equivalences that are not of this form. As a result, the framework is applicable to scenarios beyond the Kochen-Specker setting; hence the name
generalised
contextuality.

A noncontextual ontological model of an operational scenario can now be defined as follows.

A noncontextual ontological model of an operational scenario
\{\texttt{Preps},\texttt{Effects},P(\textbf{E}|\textbf{S})\}
is a triple

\displaystyle\{\Lambda,\mu_{\textbf{S}}(\lambda),\xi_{\textbf{E}}(\lambda)\}

(18)

of ontic states, ontic state distributions and ontic response functions satisfying the constraints (
16
) and (
17
) such that

\displaystyle P(\textbf{E}|\textbf{S})=\int_{\Lambda}\text{d}\lambda\,\mu_{\textbf{S}}(\lambda)\xi_{\textbf{E}}(\lambda)\quad\forall\textbf{S}\in\texttt{Preps},\;\forall\textbf{E}\in\texttt{Effects}.

(19)

In prepare-and-measure scenarios where operational equivalences exist, the constraints (
16
) and (
17
) limit the set of operational statistics that admit a noncontextual ontological model to a subset of all possible statistics. If the operational statistics lie outside of this set, they are therefore called
contextual
.

Contextuality of multi-task machine learning models

We now have all the necessary ingredients we need to define a notion of contextuality for machine learning. As in the previous section, we consider a scenario that involves a user with access to a learning model, who can input data
\boldsymbol{x}

\displaystyle h(\theta)=\{\mathcal{P}_{\theta}^{1}(y|\boldsymbol{x}),\mathcal{P}_{\theta}^{2}(y|\boldsymbol{x}),\cdots,\mathcal{P}_{\theta}^{m}(y|\boldsymbol{x})\}.

Here,

\displaystyle H=\{h(\theta)\}_{\theta}=\{\{\mathcal{P}_{\theta}^{1}(y|\boldsymbol{x}),\mathcal{P}_{\theta}^{2}(y|\boldsymbol{x}),\cdots,\mathcal{P}_{\theta}^{m}(y|\boldsymbol{x})\}\}_{\theta},

Since the model

In this section we focus on multi-task models to define our notion of contextuality. The reason for this is that this paradigm most naturally fits the existing contextuality literature, where the relevant scenarios involve a choice of measurements that we map to separate tasks. corresponds to a procedure whereby the user queries the model for task

and observes the label
y

\Omega
and
\emptyset
are the trivial and null effects respectively. The operational statistics are given by the model predictions
P(\textbf{E}^{k}_{y}|\textbf{S}_{\boldsymbol{x}})=\mathcal{P}_{\theta}^{k}(y|\boldsymbol{x})
.

This choice of operational scenario then leads to a natural definition of noncontextuality for multi-task models.

A multi-task model

\displaystyle h(\theta)=\{\mathcal{P}_{\theta}^{1}(y|\boldsymbol{x}),\mathcal{P}_{\theta}^{2}(y|\boldsymbol{x}),\cdots,\mathcal{P}_{\theta}^{m}(y|\boldsymbol{x})\}

(22)

is noncontextual iff the operational statistics
P(\textbf{E}|\textbf{S})
of the associated operational scenario (given by Definition
3
) admits a noncontextual ontological model. If such an ontological model does not exist, the learning model is said to be contextual.

With this definition, we now define a noncontextual model class to be any class that contains only noncontextual multi-task models.

A multi-task model class

\displaystyle H=\{\{\mathcal{P}_{\theta}^{1}(y|\boldsymbol{x}),\mathcal{P}_{\theta}^{2}(y|\boldsymbol{x}),\cdots,\mathcal{P}_{\theta}^{m}(y|\boldsymbol{x})\}\}_{\theta}

(23)

is noncontextual if every multi-task model in the class is noncontextual in the sense of Definition
3
.

We remark that the theory-independent approach we have taken above is different to the common perspective of generalised contextuality, where the operational scenario corresponds to the entire underlying physical theory. In Appendix
A
we expand on this point and explain why our approach is necessary to arrive at a reasonable definition of contextuality for machine learning.

5.1
The game

The data set is based on the well known rock, paper, scissors (RPS) game. Our game features three players that play a variant of the RPS game via a referee (Figure
5

\mathtt{R}
), paper (
\mathtt{P}
) or scissors (
\mathtt{S}
), and we denote the choice of actions of the three players by the vector
\boldsymbol{a}\in\{\mathtt{R},\mathtt{P},\mathtt{S}\}^{3}
. Each player also has a special action whose role we will describe shortly. For Player 1, the special action is
\mathtt{R}
, for Player 2 it is
\mathtt{P}
and for Player 3 it is
\mathtt{S}
. Following the actions, each player either receives receives €1 or must pay €1 to the referee, which we refer to as their pay-off, and denote the three payoffs as
\boldsymbol{y}=(\pm 1,\pm 1,\pm 1)
. The payoffs are decided probabilistically based on the actions of the players. More precisely, the payoff of Player
k
is such that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[Y^{(k)}]=\frac{\ell_{k}(\boldsymbol{a})}{2},

(60)

where
\ell_{k}(\boldsymbol{a})\in\{-2,-1,0,1,2\}
is equal to the number of players that Player
k
beat in that round minus the number of players that beat Player
k
. This rule ensures that if a player beats (or loses to) all others, they are sure to win (or lose) the round.

The rules for who beats whom are as follows. If two players choose different actions, the rule is the well-known rule:

\displaystyle\mathtt{R}_{k}>\mathtt{S}_{l},\quad\mathtt{P}_{k}>\mathtt{R}_{l},\quad\mathtt{S}_{k}>\mathtt{P}_{l}\quad\forall\;k,l,

(61)

where
\mathtt{R}_{k}>\mathtt{S}_{l}
means “Player
k
plays rock beats Player
l
plays scissors”. If two players play the same action, one player beats the other if the common action is their special action. For example, if players 1 and 2 both play
\mathtt{R}
, then Player 1 beats Player 2 since
\mathtt{R}
is Player 1’s special action. These rules can be summarised as

\displaystyle\mathtt{R}_{1}>\mathtt{R}_{k},\;\;k=2,3\quad\quad\mathtt{P}_{2}>\mathtt{P}_{k},\;\;k=1,3\quad\quad\mathtt{S}_{3}>\mathtt{S}_{k},\;\;k=1,2.

(62)

In any other case it is a draw. Note that this game satisfies a zero-sum condition. Mathematically, since we must have
\sum_{k}\ell_{k}(\boldsymbol{a})=0
it follows from (
60
) that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[Y^{1}|\boldsymbol{a}]+\mathbb{E}[Y^{2}|\boldsymbol{a}]+\mathbb{E}[Y^{3}|\boldsymbol{a}]=0.

(63)

5.2
The data set

In the zero-sum game literature, it is common to introduce the concept of a
strategy
. A strategy
\boldsymbol{x}(k)
for Player
k
is simply a list of probabilities that the player performs each possible action,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{x}(k)=(P(A^{(k)}=\texttt{R}),P(A^{(k)}=\texttt{P}),P(A^{(k)}=\texttt{S})).

(64)

We denote by
\boldsymbol{x}\in D_{\boldsymbol{x}}
the
3\times 3
matrix whose
k^{\text{th}}
row is
\boldsymbol{x}(k)
, where
D_{\boldsymbol{x}}
is the space of valid strategy matrices, i.e. positive-valued matrices whose rows sum to
1
. Some of these strategies correspond to the six extremal behaviours in Figure
3
. Consider for example the strategy

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{x}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0\\
0&0&1\\
0&0&1\end{pmatrix},

(65)

i.e. a deterministic strategy where Player 1 plays
R
and Player 2 and 3 play
S
. Following the rules of the game, the probabilities of a positive payoff for the three players are given by the vector
\boldsymbol{v}_{1}=(1,0,\frac{1}{2})
, which is one of the extremal behaviours in Figure
3
. The other five extremal behaviours
\boldsymbol{v}_{i}
are obtained by the deterministic strategies that correspond to the deterministic choice of actions
\texttt{SPP},\texttt{RPR},\texttt{SRS},\texttt{PPS}
and
RRP
.

The data set consists of pairs of strategies
\boldsymbol{x}
and payoffs
\boldsymbol{y}
. We sample a number of strategies
\{\boldsymbol{x}_{i}\}
following a distribution
\mathcal{D}(\boldsymbol{x})
that corresponds to generating a random positive matrix and normalising the rows (see Appendix
F
for more details). The corresponding payoffs
\boldsymbol{y}_{i}
are sampled according to the rules of the game given the strategy
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}
. That is, a choice of actions is sampled according to
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}
and the corresponding pay-offs
\boldsymbol{y}_{i}
is sampled via (
60
). We therefore have the conditional distribution

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}(\boldsymbol{y}|\boldsymbol{x})=\sum_{\boldsymbol{a}\in\{\texttt{R},\texttt{P},\texttt{S}\}^{3}}P(\boldsymbol{a}|\boldsymbol{x})P(\boldsymbol{y}|\boldsymbol{a})

(66)

which defines our data distribution
\mathcal{D}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})=\mathcal{D}(\boldsymbol{x})\mathcal{D}(\boldsymbol{y}|\boldsymbol{x})
. Note that the actions of the players are not explicit in the data, only the strategies
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}
. For a fixed
\boldsymbol{x}
, from (
66
) the conditional distribution
\mathcal{D}(\boldsymbol{y}|\boldsymbol{x})
can be seen as a probabilistic mixture over the distributions
P(\boldsymbol{y}|\boldsymbol{a})
. It follows from (
63
) that the data distribution
\mathcal{D}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})
satisfies the bias

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[Y^{1}|\boldsymbol{x}]+\mathbb{E}[Y^{2}|\boldsymbol{x}]+\mathbb{E}[Y^{3}|\boldsymbol{x}]=0,

(67)

for all
\boldsymbol{x}
, and is of the form described in Theorem
4.1
. Since this follows from the zero-sum property of the game, any data set constructed from a zero-sum game in the same fashion will satisfy (
67
).

5.3
The learning problem

We now describe a concrete problem that features the RPS data set. Consider a learning algorithm that has access to the RPS data
\{\boldsymbol{x}_{i},\boldsymbol{y}_{i}\}
and knows only that it was generated via a zero-sum game scenario, i.e. the bias (
67
) is assumed but the underlying game is unknown. The task we consider is to learn the pay-off behaviour of the game in the following sense.

Consider a data set

\displaystyle\chi=\{\boldsymbol{x}_{i},\boldsymbol{y}_{i}\}

(68)

of strategies
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}\in D_{x}
and corresponding payoffs
\boldsymbol{y}_{i}\in\{\pm 1\}^{3}
sampled from a data distribution
\mathcal{D}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})
that respects the zero-sum bias (
67
). Construct a multi-task model

\displaystyle\{\mathcal{P}_{\theta}^{1}(y|\boldsymbol{x}),\mathcal{P}_{\theta}^{2}(y|\boldsymbol{x}),\mathcal{P}_{\theta}^{3}(y|\boldsymbol{x})\}

(69)

that samples from the marginal payoff distributions
\mathcal{D}(y^{(k)}|\boldsymbol{x})
for unseen strategies
\boldsymbol{x}
.

We can now interpret Theorem
4.1
in the context of this learning problem. A multi-task model class that encodes the zero-sum bias contains models that are restricted to behaviours in the yellow hexagon of Figure
3
B. Furthermore, if the model class is noncontextual, then the convex hull of these behaviours cannot contain the dashed hexagon. Thus, no noncontextual multi-task model that satisfies the zero-sum bias will be able to sample the correct payoff distributions for all possible strategies. In particular, if a test data set contains strategies close to the deterministic strategies corresponding to the choice of actions
\texttt{RSS},\texttt{SPP},\texttt{RPR},\texttt{SRS},\texttt{PPS}
and
RRP
, then, as described in Figure
3
B, it will necessarily fail at sampling the correct payoff distribution for some strategies in the set, thus contributing to the expected generalisation error. We note that it is not too difficult to construct other data sets with the same structure by considering different scenarios in which a similar bias is present. In section
9
we outline a number of these in more detail.

6
Contextuality of general learning models

Starting from the multi-task case, in this section we describe how one can arrive at a consistent definition of noncontextuality for single-task models (Definition
6.2
). We argue that in order to do this, it is more natural to think of contextuality relative to a particular bias of the model, rather than a property of the model as a whole. This is quite a subtle point that is important if trying to use our framework beyond this work. However, it is not necessary for understanding the rest of the paper, and some readers may wish to skip the section and return to it at a later time.

6.1
Connecting multi-task and single-task models

The definition of a noncontextual multi-task learning model (Definition
3
) considers all preparations and effects related to a user that interacts with the model at inference time. While this is a suitable perspective for multi-task problems such as
5.3
, if one wishes to define a consistent notion of noncontextuality that applies to more general, single-task models, considering the full set of preparations and effects can be overkill. To understand this issue, consider the modification to Problem
4.1
for
m=3
, in which the task is to learn the joint distribution of labels rather than the marginal distributions.

Consider a data set
\chi=\{\boldsymbol{x}_{i},\boldsymbol{y}_{i}\}
of input data
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and label vectors
\boldsymbol{y}_{i}\in\{\pm 1\}^{3}
sampled from a data distribution
\mathcal{D}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})
satisfying

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[Y^{(1)}|\boldsymbol{x}]+\mathbb{E}[Y^{(2)}|\boldsymbol{x}]+\mathbb{E}[Y^{(3)}|\boldsymbol{x}]=0.

(70)

Construct a model

\mathcal{P}_{\theta}(\boldsymbol{y}|\boldsymbol{x})

that samples from the distribution
\mathcal{D}(\boldsymbol{y}|\boldsymbol{x})
for unseen
\boldsymbol{x}
.

We will call the model
\mathcal{P}_{\theta}(\boldsymbol{y}|\boldsymbol{x})
a
joint model
in order to contrast it to the multi-task model that is concerned only with the marginal statistics. A joint model can clearly be used to construct an associated multi-task model
\{\mathcal{P}_{\theta}^{1}(y|\boldsymbol{x}),\mathcal{P}_{\theta}^{2}(y|\boldsymbol{x}),\mathcal{P}_{\theta}^{3}(y|\boldsymbol{x})\}

via three coarse-grainings that associate each element of the output label
\boldsymbol{y}
to different tasks. In this way, a sufficiently expressive joint model can trivially simulate any multi-task model, and in practice one can use the joint model class to tackle Problem
4.1
. Suppose that a multi-task model constructed in this way satisfies the same operational equivalences (
43
) and (
39
) as before, and is contextual by virtue of violating the inequality (
44
). Since this multi-task model is contextual and is obtained via a trivial post-processing of the joint model, it is natural to conclude that there is some form of contextuality present in the original joint model. However, if we blindly adopt Definition
3
, this is not necessarily the case.

To see this, consider a user with access to the joint model
\mathcal{P}(\boldsymbol{y}|\boldsymbol{x})
. In the corresponding operational scenario the preparations are the same as before, however since we have a single task the effects are now

\displaystyle\{\textbf{E}_{+++},\textbf{E}_{++-},\textbf{E}_{+-+},\textbf{E}_{+--},\textbf{E}_{-++},\textbf{E}_{-+-},\textbf{E}_{--+},\textbf{E}_{---}\}\cup\{\Omega,\emptyset\},

(71)

corresponding to the eight possible values of the label vector
\boldsymbol{y}
. Since the coarse-grained multi-task model satisfies the operational equivalence (
39
) there is an operational equivalence between the coarse-grained effects

\displaystyle\textbf{E}_{\pm}^{1}=\sum_{i,j\in\{+,-\}}\textbf{E}_{\pm ij},\quad\textbf{E}_{\pm}^{2}=\sum_{i,j\in\{+,-\}}\textbf{E}_{i\pm j},\quad\textbf{E}_{\pm}^{3}=\sum_{i,j\in\{+,-\}}\textbf{E}_{ij\pm}

(72)

that correspond to observing a label
\pm 1
for each element of
\boldsymbol{y}
. We therefore have

\displaystyle\textbf{E}_{+}^{1}+\textbf{E}_{+}^{2}+\textbf{E}_{+}^{3}\sim\textbf{E}_{-}^{1}+\textbf{E}_{-}^{2}+\textbf{E}_{-}^{3}

(73)

as before.

The issue arises when one considers the preparation equivalences
(
43
)
.
Since the coarse-grained multi-task model satisfies
(
43
)
, it follows that

\displaystyle P(\textbf{E}_{\pm}^{k}|\scalebox{0.9}{$\frac{1}{2}$}s_{1}+\scalebox{0.9}{$\frac{1}{2}$}s_{2})=P(\textbf{E}_{\pm}^{k}|\scalebox{0.9}{$\frac{1}{2}$}s_{3}+\scalebox{0.9}{$\frac{1}{2}$}s_{4})=P(\textbf{E}_{\pm}^{k}|\scalebox{0.9}{$\frac{1}{2}$}s_{5}+\scalebox{0.9}{$\frac{1}{2}$}s_{6})

(74)

for
k=1,2,3
, i.e., the mixtures of preparations are indistinguishable if one observes marginal statistics only. However, in order to be operationally equivalent, the preparations must give identical statistics for all effects:

\displaystyle P(\textbf{E}_{ijk}|\scalebox{0.9}{$\frac{1}{2}$}s_{1}+\scalebox{0.9}{$\frac{1}{2}$}s_{2})=P(\textbf{E}_{ijk}|\scalebox{0.9}{$\frac{1}{2}$}s_{3}+\scalebox{0.9}{$\frac{1}{2}$}s_{4})=P(\textbf{E}_{ijk}|\scalebox{0.9}{$\frac{1}{2}$}s_{5}+\scalebox{0.9}{$\frac{1}{2}$}s_{6}),\quad i,j,k\in\{\pm 1\}.

(75)

Even if (
74
) is satisfied, it may be the case that (
75
) is not. This will be the case, for example, when the preparation densities
\frac{1}{2}s_{1}+\frac{1}{2}s_{2}
,
\frac{1}{2}s_{3}+\frac{1}{2}s_{4}
, and
\frac{1}{2}s_{5}+\frac{1}{2}s_{6}
have identical marginal statistics, but differ in their joint statistics (see Appendix
C
for an explicit example). In such cases we cannot conclude that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}s_{1}+\frac{1}{2}s_{2}\sim\frac{1}{2}s_{3}+\frac{1}{2}s_{4}\sim\frac{1}{2}s_{5}+\frac{1}{2}s_{6}.

(76)

As a result, the ontological model will lack the constraints implied by these equivalences, and we may be unable to prove contextuality
8
8

8

In fact, in quantum models, if one cannot identify any operational equivalences related to the preparations, there is always a noncontextual model
[
20
]
.
. Considering the full set of preparations and effects therefore seems problematic in order to arrive at a consistent notion of contextuality across the single-task and multi-task settings.

6.2
Contextuality relative to a bias

The moral of this story is that it may be more natural to think about contextuality relative to a particular bias of the model, rather than as a property of the model as a whole. Indeed, when studying contextuality it is always necessary to specify the procedures and observations that most accurately reflect the spirit of the nonclassicality one is wishing to probe, and different choices can lead to different conclusions
[
30
]
. The solution to this conundrum therefore comes from considering contextuality with respect to those preparations and effects that play a role in a specific bias. In this way, one can identify a type of contextuality present in the operational statistics that is related to the non-existence of a noncontextual ontological model for these preparations and effects only.

In particular, we may consider a general learning model
\mathcal{P}_{\theta}(\boldsymbol{y}|\boldsymbol{x})
with associated operational scenario
\{\texttt{Preps},\texttt{Effects},P(\textbf{E}|\textbf{S})\}
for which there exists an inductive bias that can be written as

\displaystyle\sum_{\textbf{E}_{i}\in\mathfrak{E}_{1}}p_{i}P(\textbf{E}_{i}|\textbf{S}_{\boldsymbol{x}})=\sum_{\textbf{E}_{j}\in\mathfrak{E}_{2}}q_{j}P(\textbf{E}_{j}|\textbf{S}_{\boldsymbol{x}})\quad\forall\textbf{S}_{\boldsymbol{x}}

(77)

where
\mathfrak{E}_{1},\mathfrak{E}_{2}\subseteq\texttt{Effects}
and
p_{i},q_{j}
are convex weights. This implies the operational equivalence

\displaystyle\sum_{\textbf{E}_{i}\in\mathfrak{E}_{1}}p_{i}\textbf{E}_{i}\sim\sum_{\textbf{E}_{j}\in\mathfrak{E}_{1}}q_{j}\textbf{E}_{j}.

(78)

We can now define noncontextuality relative to the bias as follows.

Consider a learning model
\mathcal{P}(\boldsymbol{y}|\boldsymbol{x})
with associated operational scenario

\displaystyle\{\texttt{Preps},\texttt{Effects},P(\textbf{E}|\textbf{S})\}

(79)

that satisfies an inductive bias of the form (
77
). The learning model is noncontextual relative to the bias (
77
) if the operational statistics of the operational scenario

\displaystyle\{\texttt{Preps},\mathfrak{E}_{1}\cup\mathfrak{E}_{2},P(\textbf{E}|\textbf{S})\}

(80)

admits a noncontextual ontological model.

Using this definition, if the coarse-grained multi-task model is contextual in the sense of Definition
3
, the joint model is contextual relative to the bias (
28
), which unifies the multi-task and single-task approaches. We note that one could also consider biases that are satisfied by a subset of preparations, and consider a similar definition to the above involving these preparations only, but we do not consider this in this work.

7
Encoding inductive bias into quantum learning models

Until now, we have only been concerned with abstract representations of learning models in the form of preparations and effects of an operational scenario. In this section we present methods to construct classes of multi-task quantum machine learning models
\{\{\mathcal{P}^{k}_{\theta}(y|\boldsymbol{x})\}_{k=1,\cdots,m}\}_{\theta}
with
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
y\in\{-1,+1\}
that encode the inductive bias

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[Y^{(1)}|\boldsymbol{x}]+\mathbb{E}[Y^{(2)}|\boldsymbol{x}]+\cdots+\mathbb{E}[Y^{(m)}|\boldsymbol{x}]=0\quad\forall\boldsymbol{x}

(81)

for all parameters
\theta
in the class. We consider a bias of the above form due to its simplicity and relevance to this work, but much of what we say can be straightforwardly extended to biases expressed as general linear combinations of the probabilities
\mathcal{P}_{\theta}^{k}(y|\boldsymbol{x})
for arbitrary finite label dimension, and even to certain forms of nonlinear biases (as we explain in Section
7.3
). We remind the reader that since quantum theory is known to be contextual in the generalised sense
[
20
,
54
]
, the constraints on noncontextual models related to this bias do not apply to quantum models.

The type of quantum models we consider correspond to parameterised quantum circuits
[
55
]
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}^{k}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(y|\boldsymbol{x})=\bra{\psi_{0}}U^{\dagger}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})\mathcal{M}_{y|k}U(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})\ket{\psi_{0}},

(82)

where
\ket{\psi_{0}}
is some
n
-qubit initial state,
U
is a parameterised unitary that depends on the input data and some trainable parameters
\boldsymbol{\theta}
, and
\{\{\mathcal{M}_{+1|k},\mathcal{M}_{-1|k}\}\}_{k}
is a set of two-outcome measurements that sample the label for task
k
. Denoting by
\mathcal{O}_{k}=\mathcal{M}_{+1|k}-\mathcal{M}_{-1|k}
the corresponding observable for task
k
, from (
81
) our aim is to find choices of
\mathcal{O}_{k}
,
\ket{\psi_{0}}
and
U
such that

\displaystyle\sum_{k}\bra{\psi_{0}}U^{\dagger}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})\mathcal{O}_{k}U(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})\ket{\psi_{0}}=\bra{\psi_{0}}U^{\dagger}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})\mathcal{H}U(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})\ket{\psi_{0}}=0\quad\forall\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta},

(83)

where we call
\mathcal{H}=\sum_{k}\mathcal{O}_{k}
the
bias operator
. Note that from the above,
\mathcal{H}
has to have at least one non-positive eigenvalue. We explore two methods that we call the state-based and measurement-based approaches. As we will see, the linearity of quantum theory plays an important role, and provides a rather natural way of encoding such biases.

Figure 6:
Two methods for encoding the inductive bias (
81
) into a quantum machine learning model. A. The state-based approach. The first unitary creates states that satisfy the bias for the chosen measurement operators
\mathcal{M}_{y|k}
. The second unitary is a bias-invariant layer that performs rotations that preserve the bias for all input states. B. The measurement-based approach. Here, an arbitrary input state and unitary can be chosen, but the measurement observables are chosen to have a specific structure that enforces the bias.

7.1
State-based approach

The first step of this approach is to fix a choice of observables
\{\mathcal{O}_{k}\}
. A simple choice we will use in the next section is to take
\mathcal{O}_{k}=\sigma_{z}^{(k)}
, so that a computational basis measurement on the
k^{\text{th}}
qubit samples the label for task
k
. Given such a choice, the question then is how to construct
U
and
\ket{\psi_{0}}
such that (
83
) holds. The method we propose is to build the quantum model from a sequence of two subcircuits
V(\boldsymbol{\theta})
and
W(\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{x},\theta})
(see Figure
6
A). The first circuit is a parameterised unitary that we call the
input preparation unitary
, whose role is to prepare a state for the next circuit that satisfies the bias, i.e. such that
\langle\mathcal{H}\rangle=0
. The second circuit is a
bias-invariant layer
, meaning that it preserves the quantity
\langle\mathcal{H}\rangle
for arbitrary input states from the first layer.

We first focus on the second circuit
W(\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{x},\theta})
. In order to be bias-invariant, unitaries in this circuit must belong to the set

\displaystyle G=\{U\;|\;\bra{\psi}U^{\dagger}\mathcal{H}U\ket{\psi}=\bra{\psi}\mathcal{H}\ket{\psi}\;\forall\ket{\psi}\},

(84)

which forms a matrix Lie group under matrix multiplication. The associated matrix Lie algebra
9
9

9

Here we use the ‘physicist’ convention where we include a factor of
i
so that the matrices
X
are Hermitian; in the mathematics literature the
i
is usually absorbed into the matrix
X
.
is equivalent to the vector space of matrices that exponentiate to elements of
G
:

\displaystyle\mathfrak{g}=\{X\;|\;\exp{(itX)}\in G\;\forall t\in\mathbb{R}\}.

(85)

For the group
G
, this is just equal to those Hermitian operators that commute with
\mathcal{H}
,

\displaystyle\mathfrak{g}=\{X\;|\;X^{\dagger}=X,[X,\mathcal{H}]=0\}.

(86)

It follows that the bias
\langle\mathcal{H}\rangle
is conserved by any data-encoding unitary of the form
W(\boldsymbol{x})=\exp{(iXf(\boldsymbol{x}}))
and any trainable unitary of the form
W(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\exp{(iXg(\boldsymbol{\theta}))}
, where
X\in\mathfrak{g}
and
f
,
g
are arbitrary real functions. Any number of unitaries of this form can therefore be combined in sequence to construct a bias-invariant circuit
W(\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{x},\theta})
. The main challenge here is determining the set (
86
) and judiciously choosing the generators
X
from which
W
is defined. For choices of
\mathcal{O}_{k}
with a lot of structure this may be relatively easy however. For example, for the choice
\mathcal{O}_{k}=\sigma_{z}^{(k)}
, one finds that any generator
X
that is diagonal in the computational basis, as well as those of the form
X=\sigma_{x}^{(i)}\sigma_{x}^{(j)}+\sigma_{y}^{(i)}\sigma_{y}^{(j)}
,
i,j\in\{1,\cdots,m\},i\neq j
commute with
\mathcal{H}
. These may be combined linearly to create new generators that also commute with
\mathcal{H}
.

We now turn to the input preparation circuit
V(\boldsymbol{\theta})
. This should prepare a state that satisfies the bias, i.e. it consists of unitaries in the set

\displaystyle G^{\prime}=\{U\;|\;\bra{\psi_{0}}U^{\dagger}\mathcal{H}U\ket{\psi_{0}}=0\}.

(87)

In general this does not form a group (since it is not closed) and therefore may be more difficult to characterise than
G
. Note that even if
\ket{\psi_{0}}
satisfies the bias, unitaries in
G^{\prime}
do not necessarily commute with
\mathcal{H}
. An example of such a unitary for the case
\mathcal{O}_{k}=\sigma_{z}^{(k)}
and
\ket{\psi_{0}}=\ket{+}^{\otimes n}
is

\displaystyle V(\theta)=\exp{(i\sigma_{x}^{(1)}\theta)}\exp{(-i\sigma_{x}^{(2)}\theta)}

(88)

for any
\theta\in(0,\pi]
. We are unaware of general methods to construct parameterised unitaries in
G^{\prime}
, so at the moment we rely on ad-hoc approaches like the above.

7.2
Measurement-based approach

The second approach (Figure
6
B) is based on encoding the bias into the structure of the measurement operators rather than the parameterised unitary. In this case, one can consider an arbitrary input state
\ket{\psi_{0}}
and arbitrary parameterised unitary
U(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})
. The observables however must be chosen so that

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}_{1}+\mathcal{O}_{2}+\cdots+\mathcal{O}_{m}=\mathcal{H}=0.

(89)

From the linearity of the trace it follows that this choice ensures (
81
) is satisfied. An example of observables that satisfy (
89
) for a qubit and
m=3
are any three equally spaced ‘trine’ observables in a plane of the Bloch sphere, e.g.,
\mathcal{O}_{k}=\boldsymbol{v}_{k}\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma}
with
\boldsymbol{v}_{k}=(\cos\theta_{k},\sin\theta_{k},0)
and
\theta_{k}=2k\pi/3
. However, to the best of our knowledge, observables of this form for larger dimension are not known and further research is required in this direction. Here, one may expect that mathematical tools from other constructions of symmetric measurements such as mutually unbiased bases and SIC-POVMs to be useful
[
56
]
. In Appendix
D
we give a previously unknown construction for the case
m=3
for all even dimensions that satisfies (
89
).

Finally we comment that for both the state and measurement based approaches, it may be beneficial to construct models that sample from classical probabilistic mixtures of such circuits by combining them with stochastic neural network models (see e.g.
[
57
]
), but we do not consider this here.

7.3
Nonlinear biases

The same techniques can also be used to encode nonlinear biases if they can be expressed through a bias operator as
\langle\mathcal{H}\rangle=0
in the quantum model. Consider for example a bias of the form

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[f_{1}(Y^{(1)})|\boldsymbol{x}]+\mathbb{E}[f_{2}(Y^{(2)})|\boldsymbol{x}]+\cdots+\mathbb{E}[f_{m}(Y^{(m)})|\boldsymbol{x}]=0\quad\forall\boldsymbol{x}

(90)

where the
f_{k}
are potentially nonlinear functions of the labels. If the observables
\mathcal{O}_{k}
are of the form
\mathcal{O}_{k}=\sum_{y}y\mathcal{M}_{y|k}
with
\mathcal{M}_{y|k}
the projector corresponding to label
y
of task
k
, then the left hand side of the above is given by (
83
) where

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}=f_{1}(\mathcal{O}_{1})+f_{2}(\mathcal{O}_{2})+\cdots+f_{m}(\mathcal{O}_{m}),

(91)

and
f_{k}(\mathcal{O}_{k})=\sum_{y}f_{k}(y)\mathcal{M}_{y|k}
. Thus, one can use the state-based or measurement-based approach described with this bias operator in order to construct model classes that encode (
90
). Of course, the difficult task to find the corresponding generators
X
in (
86
) or a collection of measurements satisfying (
89
) for a given nonlinear bias. It would be interesting to understand which biases admit efficient model constructions in this way.

8
Outperforming classical surrogates

In this section we construct a class of quantum models that encode the bias (
81
) for
m=3
, using the state-based approach of Section
7.1
. We then train the model class using the RPS data set described in Section
5
. In order to investigate the effect of the encoded bias, we compare the quantum models to a class of classical surrogate models recently introduced in
[
11
]
. As we will see, the quantum model class achieves a lower generalisation error than its associated surrogate model class, which we claim to be due to the ability of the quantum class to encode the relevant inductive bias. We stress that we are not claiming that this is evidence of ‘quantum advantage’ since the quantum models we consider are small enough to be easily simulated on a laptop computer. Code used to generate the plots of this section can be found at
https://github.com/XanaduAI/Contextual-ML
.

8.1
The learning problem

The learning problem we consider is that of learning the payoff behaviour of an unknown zero-sum game, as described in Problem
5.3
. We use the RPS data set

\displaystyle\chi=\{\boldsymbol{x}_{i},\boldsymbol{y}_{i}\},

(92)

containing
1500
strategies
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}\in D_{x}
and payoffs
\boldsymbol{y}_{i}
. Since from normalisation the last entry of each row of
\boldsymbol{x}
is linearly dependent on the first two, we compress each
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}
into a matrix of size
3\times 2
, and scale the data by
\frac{\pi}{2}
so that the input data points correspond to the matrix

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{x}=\frac{\pi}{2}\begin{pmatrix}P(A_{1}=\texttt{R})&P(A_{1}=\texttt{P})-P(A_{1}=\texttt{S})\\
P(A_{2}=\texttt{P})&P(A_{2}=\texttt{S})-P(A_{2}=\texttt{R})\\

(93)

8.2
The quantum model class

We consider a simple class of three-qubit quantum models, described in Figure
7
, which encode the bias using the state-based method of section
7
. This class contains models of the form
\mathcal{P}_{\theta}(\boldsymbol{y}|\boldsymbol{x})
which we view as a multi-task model
\{\mathcal{P}^{1}_{{\theta}}(y|\boldsymbol{x}),\mathcal{P}^{2}_{{\theta}}(y|\boldsymbol{x}),\mathcal{P}^{3}_{{\theta}}(y|\boldsymbol{x})\}
by associating each element of
\boldsymbol{y}
with a separate task. Following
[
44
]
, the expectation value of the final
\sigma_{z}
measurement on any of the qubits is equivalent to a truncated Fourier series of the form

\displaystyle f_{\theta}(\boldsymbol{x})=\sum_{\boldsymbol{\omega}\in\Omega}c_{\boldsymbol{\omega}}(\theta)e^{i\boldsymbol{\omega}\cdot\boldsymbol{z}(\boldsymbol{x})},

(94)

where
\boldsymbol{z}(\boldsymbol{x})=(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},x_{4}x_{5},x_{4}x_{6},x_{5}x_{6}).
The frequency spectrum
\Omega
is determined by the eigenspectra of the data-encoding gates. In our case one finds

\displaystyle\Omega_{L}=\{(\omega_{1},\omega_{2},\cdots,\omega_{6})\;|\;\omega_{i}\in\{-L,-L+1,\cdots,L-1,L\}\;\forall i\},

(95)

giving a total of
(2L+1)^{6}
vectors
\omega
. The coefficients
c_{\boldsymbol{\omega}}(\theta)
are determined by the structure of the rest of the circuit; note that we have
c_{\boldsymbol{-\omega}}=c^{*}_{\boldsymbol{\omega}}
so that
f
is real-valued. The model therefore samples labels
\boldsymbol{y}
such that

\displaystyle(\mathbb{E}[Y^{(1)}|\boldsymbol{x}],\mathbb{E}[Y^{(2)}|\boldsymbol{x}],\mathbb{E}[Y^{(3)}|\boldsymbol{x}])=(f^{1}_{\theta}(\boldsymbol{x}),f^{2}_{\theta}(\boldsymbol{x}),f^{3}_{\theta}(\boldsymbol{x})),

(96)

where each
f^{k}_{\theta}
is of the form (
94
). Note that each of the generators of the gates in the circuit commute with the bias operator
\mathcal{H}=\sigma_{z}^{(1)}+\sigma_{z}^{(2)}+\sigma_{z}^{(3)}
and that the input preparation unitary
V_{\theta}
always prepares a state such that
\langle\mathcal{H}\rangle=0
. The values of the coefficients of the three Fourier series
f^{k}_{\theta}(\boldsymbol{x})
are therefore correlated by the circuit structure in such a way that the zero-sum bias is always satisfied.

8.3
Classical surrogates of quantum models

The idea of constructing a classical surrogate model of a quantum model was recently introduced in
[
11
]
to study the inductive bias of quantum models for regression tasks, i.e. models of the form
f_{\theta}(\boldsymbol{x})\in\mathbb{R}
. A surrogate model class is simply a linear model in the same Fourier features of the quantum model. For example, consider a quantum model class of the form (
94
). The surrogate class is the parameterised truncated Fourier series

\displaystyle g_{\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{\beta}}(\boldsymbol{x})=\sum_{\boldsymbol{\omega}\in\Omega}\alpha_{\boldsymbol{\omega}}\cos(\boldsymbol{\omega}\cdot\boldsymbol{z(\boldsymbol{x}}))+\beta_{\boldsymbol{\omega}}\sin(\boldsymbol{\omega}\cdot\boldsymbol{z}(\boldsymbol{x})),

(97)

where the
\alpha_{\boldsymbol{\omega}}
and
\beta_{\boldsymbol{\omega}}
are real-valued trainable parameters and
\Omega
is the same as in (
95
). We have chosen this parameterisation rather than the exponential form since we know the model must produce a real number and can therefore avoid using complex numbers. The surrogate model class is larger than the quantum model class since it contains all models in (
94
), however it lacks the specific bias of the quantum class that is encoded in the structure of the Fourier coefficients. Interestingly, surrogate model classes often perform better than generic quantum models for regression on a range of data sets
[
11
]
.

Since our problem is not a regression problem but involves sampling the payoff labels, we need to define a natural notion of a classical surrogate to compare the quantum model (
96
) against. The logic we will follow is as above: view each
f^{k}_{\theta}(\boldsymbol{x})
of the quantum model as a truncated Fourier series and construct a surrogate model by ignoring the structure in the coefficients. To this end, we consider a collection of three linear models

\displaystyle(g^{1}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{1},\boldsymbol{\beta}_{1}}(\boldsymbol{x}),g^{2}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{2},\boldsymbol{\beta}_{2}}(\boldsymbol{x}),g^{3}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{3},\boldsymbol{\beta}_{3}}(\boldsymbol{x})),

(98)

where each
g^{k}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{k},\boldsymbol{\beta}_{k}}(\boldsymbol{x})
is of the form (
97
) and has the spectrum
\Omega_{L}
. In analogy to (
96
) we would like to interpret these values as expectation values
\mathbb{E}[Y^{(k)}|\boldsymbol{x}]
, however this is not immediately possible since
g^{k}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{k},\boldsymbol{\beta}_{k}}
is not bounded in
[-1,1]
. To remedy this, we use a HardTanh function

\displaystyle h(x)=\begin{cases}-1&\text{if }x<-1\\
x&\text{if }-1\leq x\leq 1\\
1&\text{if }x>1\end{cases}

(99)

so that payoffs are sampled from the model as

\displaystyle(\mathbb{E}[Y^{(1)}|\boldsymbol{x}],\mathbb{E}[Y^{(2)}|\boldsymbol{x}],\mathbb{E}[Y^{(3)}|\boldsymbol{x}])=(h(g^{1}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{1},\boldsymbol{\beta}_{1}}(\boldsymbol{x})),h(g^{2}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{2},\boldsymbol{\beta}_{2}}(\boldsymbol{x})),h(g^{3}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{3},\boldsymbol{\beta}_{3}}(\boldsymbol{x}))).

(100)

This surrogate model class has access to the same frequency spectra as the quantum model class and by virtue of being less constrained, contains the quantum class as a specific subset of its parameters. The surrogate model does not however encode the zero-sum bias (
81
). This is because the constraint is related in a complex manner to the weights
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{k},\boldsymbol{\beta}_{k}
and it is therefore not clear how to enforce it. Moreover, attempting to enforce the constraint via a postprocessing of the
g^{k}
’s seems cumbersome, and we were unable to identify a general method that would scale to larger label dimensions, unlike in the quantum case. We therefore expect that it is infeasible to encode this kind of bias into a linear Fourier features model, and indeed it is this difference that we expect to lead to a lower generalisation error in the quantum model.

8.4
Training and evaluation

To train the models we minimise the negative log likelihood of the labels given the strategies

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}=-\sum_{(\boldsymbol{x}_{i},\boldsymbol{y}_{i})\in\chi}\left(\log(\mathcal{P}^{1}_{\theta}(y_{i}^{(1)}|\boldsymbol{x}_{i}))+\log(\mathcal{P}^{2}_{\theta}(y_{i}^{(2)}|\boldsymbol{x}_{i}))+\log(\mathcal{P}^{3}_{\theta}(y_{i}^{(3)}|\boldsymbol{x}_{i}))\right)

(101)

using the Adam gradient-descent optimiser. Full details on the data sampling and training can be found in Appendix
F
. We acknowledge that this training method does not scale well for quantum models since it requires the ability to efficiently estimate log probabilities, which becomes infeasible for large circuits. Indeed, a crucial challenge of quantum machine learning is to develop scalable methods that perform well on generative tasks, but we do not focus on this problem in this work.

To evaluate the trained models we consider the sum of the Kullback–Leibler divergences between the trained models
\mathcal{P}^{k}_{\theta}(y|\boldsymbol{x})
and the true data distributions
\mathcal{D}_{k}(y|\boldsymbol{x})\equiv\mathcal{D}(y^{(k)}|\boldsymbol{x})
, averaged over
\mathcal{D}(\boldsymbol{x})
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}(\boldsymbol{x})}\left[\frac{1}{3}\sum_{k=1}^{3}D_{\text{KL}}(\mathcal{D}_{k}(Y|\boldsymbol{x})||\mathcal{P}^{k}_{\theta}(Y|\boldsymbol{x}))\right]=\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}(\boldsymbol{x})}\left[\frac{1}{3}\sum_{k=1}^{3}\left(\sum_{y=\pm 1}\mathcal{D}_{k}(y|\boldsymbol{x})\log\left(\frac{\mathcal{D}_{k}(y|\boldsymbol{x})}{\mathcal{P}^{k}_{\theta}(y|\boldsymbol{x})}\right)\right)\right].

(102)

We sample
N=10000
strategies
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{\text{test}}
via
\mathcal{D}(\boldsymbol{x})
and estimate the average (
102
) with the sum

\displaystyle\frac{1}{3N}\sum_{\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{\text{test}}}\sum_{k=1}^{3}D_{\text{KL}}(\mathcal{D}_{k}(Y|\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{\text{test}})||\mathcal{P}_{k}(Y|\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{\text{test}})),

(103)

which we calculate to machine precision using symbolic expressions of the model probabilities.

8.5
Results

In our study we consider four model classes: (i) The biased quantum model given in Figure
7
with
L=2
and
B=2
, (ii) a generic quantum model with approximately the same number of parameters, the details of which we give in appendix
E
, (iii) a surrogate model corresponding to a
L=1
quantum model, (iv) a surrogate corresponding to a
L=2
quantum model. The results are given in Figure
8
. As has been observed before
[
11
]
, the surrogate models are able to outperform a generic quantum model class. The best performance is achieved by the biased quantum model class however, which we conclude is due to the ability of the quantum model to naturally encode the zero-sum bias. Note that the surrogate models suffer severely from over-fitting, as can be seen from the decrease in the negative log likelihood and increase of generalisation error. This is due to the large number of parameters the surrogate models have relative to the quantum models. To try and counter this, we also optimised the surrogate models using a
L2
weight penalty term added to the cost (
101
). Although this alleviates the problem of overfitting, we did not find that it improved generalisation performance (see Figure
9
for the case
L=2
and Figure
11
of the appendix for
L=1
). This suggests that the lower generalisation error of the quantum model over the surrogate originates from the inductive bias rather than overfitting in the surrogate model.

9
Avenues for contextuality-inspired inductive bias

In this work we have focused on a single, simple inductive bias and shown that contextuality plays a role in learning. Our ultimate goal however is to identify complex problems of practical interest for which contextuality is crucial, and for which the structure of quantum models makes them the model of choice. In this section, we suggest a number of scenarios with this goal in mind, and finish by discussing possible extensions to our framework.

9.1
Zero-sum games with larger payoff dimension

Most real-life zero-sum game scenarios involve payoffs that take more values than only
\pm 1
as we considered here. For example, one could consider a zero-sum game scenario with
N
players and conditional payoff distributions
\mathcal{P}(\boldsymbol{y}|\boldsymbol{x})
with each element of
\boldsymbol{y}
in
[-C,\cdots,C]
for some integer
C
. From the zero-sum condition it follows that

\sum_{k=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}(Y^{(k)}|\boldsymbol{x})=0

\forall\;\boldsymbol{x}

as before. To see how this leads to a constraint on noncontextual models, we first shift the values of the payoffs by
C
so that they are in the range
[0,2C]
. This does not change the nature of the problem since it is just a shift of the label data. The zero-sum condition then becomes

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{N}\sum_{y=0}^{2C}\mathcal{P}^{k}_{\theta}(y|\boldsymbol{x})y=C\quad\forall\boldsymbol{x}\implies\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\sum_{y=0}^{2C}p_{y}\mathcal{P}^{k}_{\theta}(y|\boldsymbol{x})=\frac{1}{N(2C+1)}\quad\forall\boldsymbol{x},

(104)

where
p_{y}=y/\sum_{y}y=\frac{y}{C(2C+1)}
is a probability distribution.

The right-hand side of the above allows us to identify an operational equivalence in the corresponding operational scenario of a multi-task model. Consider the following two effect densities: (
e_{1}
) A player is chosen at random. A
2C
-sided die with probabilities
p_{i}
is rolled with outcome
y
and the payoff of Player
k
is observed, and success corresponds to the model returning the payoff value
y
; (
e_{2}
) The model is ignored and success is declared with probability
q=1/(N(2C+1))
. These two procedures correspond to the left and right sides of the equality in the right hand side of (
104
). It follows that the two measurement procedures give identical statistics for all strategies. Since
e_{2}=q\Omega+(1-q)\emptyset
this implies the operational equivalence

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\sum_{y=1}^{2C}p_{y}\textbf{E}_{y|k}\sim q\Omega+(1-q)\emptyset,

(105)

where
\textbf{E}_{y|k}
is the effect corresponding to observing payoff
y
of Player
k
. This leads to the constraint

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{N}\sum_{y=1}^{2C}\xi_{\textbf{E}_{y|k}}(\lambda)p_{y}=\frac{1}{2C+1}\quad\forall\;\lambda

(106)

on the noncontextual ontological model. This will limit the expressivity of the model as before, however a clear picture of the structure of these constraints and how they affect learning is needed. We note that one need not necessarily consider only zero-sum games; similar constraints can be found in the same manner in any constant-sum game as well.

9.2
Conserved substances in networks

Biases of the form (
81
) become relevant when modelling the behaviour of conserved substances in networks, which may have relevance to problems in biology (e.g. a network of cells sharing a conserved substance), population modelling (a network of locations sharing a conserved number of people/animals), manufacturing logistics (a network of factories sharing a conserved number of resources). To formalise these settings, consider a network consisting of
N
cells that share some substance. We denote the amount of the substance in cell
j
by
y_{j}
, which we assume to take discrete values. We also consider some parameters
\boldsymbol{x}
(e.g. time, network connectivity) that affect the exchange of the substance throughout the network, and assume that the total amount of substance is conserved on average:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[Y_{1}|\boldsymbol{x}]+\mathbb{E}[Y_{2}|\boldsymbol{x}]+\cdots+\mathbb{E}[Y_{N}|\boldsymbol{x}]=C.

(107)

Consider a multi-task model whose aim is to model the amount of substance in a given cell given parameters
\boldsymbol{x}
. That is, it is a probabilistic model
\mathcal{P}^{k}_{\theta}(y|\boldsymbol{x})
that from (
107
) satisfies

\displaystyle\sum_{k}\sum_{y}\mathcal{P}^{k}_{\theta}(y|\boldsymbol{x})y=C.

(108)

This is of the same from as the left hand side of (
104
), and so an analogous constraint to (
106
) can be derived on the corresponding noncontextual ontological model.

9.3
Quantum control under a conserved quantity

Consider a quantum control scenario where a multipartite intial state
\rho_{0}
is evolved to a final state
\rho(\boldsymbol{x})
and one of a number of measurements
\{\textbf{E}_{y|k}\}
is made with outcome
y=0,1,\cdots,M
. Here,
\boldsymbol{x}
are some parameters of the control scenario, for example strengths of various interactions and interaction time. Suppose there exists an operator of the form

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}=\sum_{yk}\textbf{E}_{y|k}g_{y}

(109)

with
g_{y}\geq 0
that is conserved during the control process, i.e.
\langle\mathcal{O}\rangle=C\;\forall\boldsymbol{x}
. For example,
\mathcal{O}
could be the energy of the system, and control operations are known to conserve energy. If we wish to construct a multi-task model to learn the experimental behaviour, this conservation law translates to the desirable inductive bias

\displaystyle\sum_{y,k}\mathcal{P}^{k}_{\theta}(y|\boldsymbol{x})g_{y}=C\implies\frac{1}{N}\sum_{y,k}\mathcal{P}^{k}_{\theta}(y|\boldsymbol{x})p_{y}=\frac{C}{NG}

(110)

where
G=\sum_{y}g_{y}
,
p_{y}=g_{y}^{k}/G
. Similarly to the examples above, this allows us to identify an operational equivalence that leads to a constraint

\displaystyle\sum_{k}\sum_{y}\xi_{\textbf{E}_{y|k}}(\lambda)p_{y}=\frac{C}{G}\quad\forall\;\lambda,

(111)

on a noncontextual learning model.

9.4
Possible extensions to our framework

A significant challenge of this work involved deciding on how to cast machine learning in the language of contextuality, and the framework we present here is by no means the only option, nor the most general framework possible. For example, it is possible to define a notion of contextuality for transformations
[
20
]
, in addition to the preparations and effects we consider in this work. This notion of contextuality may be more suited, for example, to studying the non-classically of layers in models with a sequential structure. Another option would be to consider a definition of contextuality that is defined with respect to the full model class, rather than a single model in the class as in Definition
3
. Doing this, one could study operational equivalences across different models in the class, although care would be needed to connect these equivalences to relevant concepts in learning. It is also worth stating clearly that the noncontextual constraints we consider in this work are qualitatively different from the constraints in Kochen-Specker type contextuality
[
49
]
, which is studied in most works connecting quantum computation to contextuality. Kocken-Specker type contextuality can still exist within our framework, but follows from qualitatively different operational equivalences (that we briefly described in Section
2.6
). For this reason our work is something of a departure from the usual perspective of contextuality and could potentially find applications in the wider field of quantum computation.

Generalised contextuality has also recently been connected to the framework of generalised probabilistic theories
[
58
,
59
,
60
,
61
]
(see also
[
62
]
for an application to associative memories). This framework is commonly used in foundations research and allows one to transform any probabilistic theory, such as classical and quantum theory, into a simple linear structure in which the states and effects are represented as vectors in a vector space
10
10

10

this is essentially what we have done in the geometric picture of section
4.2
and Figure
3
.
. In the generalised probability theory framework, noncontextuality becomes equivalent to a simple geometric condition, called simplex embedability
[
60
]
, which relates to the possibility of embedding the state space into a higher dimensional simplex, and the effect space into its dual. Understanding this structural limitation in the context of learning models may therefore lead to general insights about the structural difference between contextual and noncontextual learners, and could potentially be used to define a measure of contextuality that goes beyond the binary contextual/noncontextual framework we assume.

10
Outlook

Although quantum machine learning has progressed in many ways, it nevertheless remains unclear why a machine learning practitioner would reach for the quantum toolbox amongst the vast array of classical tools on offer. In order to transform quantum machine learning into a science that has real-world impact, it is therefore crucial that we identify the features of quantum models that make them a powerful paradigm for learning. Historically, this power has often been viewed through the lens of quantum computation, and this has led to a program of searching for quantum advantage in terms of asymptotic complexity-theoretic analysis.

Whereas it is true that some form of computational hardness is required to go beyond the capabilities of classical models, taking this perspective as a starting point may be overly restrictive due to the general difficulty of proving complexity-theoretic separations, and the highly specific and complex mathematical structures they use. Our work offers a fresh perspective however: perhaps an alternative way to view the value of quantum machine learning is not in terms of computation, but through the ability to parameterise expressive model classes that encode certain data structures. As we have shown, contextuality offers an interesting perspective with which to view this question, and has led us to a specific data structure that can be naturally encoded in quantum models. In order to make further progress in this direction, we believe that greater effort is needed to

i

Identify mathematical structures that are characteristic of quantum theory and understand how these connect to structures present in data;

ii

Determine which of these structures are connected to non-classicality and thus are likely to be difficult to emulate classically;

iii

Understand the kinds of learning problem where these non-classical structures plays a useful role.

The nascent field of geometric quantum machine learning
[
40
,
41
,
42
]
—the study of encoding symmetry structures into quantum models—may be a useful framework in which to tackle the first of these points.
Other insights may also be possible by borrowing ideas from the quantum foundations literature, particularly for point (ii). For example, how does our framework of contextuality (or extensions thereof) connect to the notion of invariance and equivariance in geometric machine learning? What other common structures in contextuality (such as Kochen-Specker sets
[
49
]
and antidistinguishability
[
63
]
) can be connected to biases in data? Is the non-signalling multi-partite structure of Bell nonlocality useful for learning certain data sets? Do concepts in quantum foundations suggest how to build a non-classical ‘quantum neuron’? Answers to these questions will generally not lead to statements of quantum advantage in the language of complexity theory. What they reveal in terms of understanding and intuition may however be valuable in the search for practical uses for quantum machine learning models.

Perhaps the most pertinent question regarding this particular work is whether the linearly conserved quantities we consider (for potentially larger label dimension) can be usefully encoded in classical models using a tractable amount of resources. The fact that neural networks are nonlinear functions of the input data is actually problematic here, since it becomes difficult to impose linear constraints on the nonlinear output variables
[
64
]
. The linear structure of quantum theory is particularly useful however, as it provides a model in which the output label probabilities are nonlinear in the input data, but the linear bias is nevertheless enforced. Furthermore, classical mechanics is already used as a framework to encode conserved quantities in continuous-valued data
[
65
,
66
]
. Since quantum theory can be seen as a generalisation of classical mechanics that deals with discrete variables (quanta), this does suggest that quantum theory is a very natural structure in which to encode conserved quantities of discrete data.
