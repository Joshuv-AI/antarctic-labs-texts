A Generic Strategy Improvement Method for Simple Stochastic Games
David Auger, Xavier Badin de Montjoye, Yann Strozecki

Abstract
We present a generic strategy improvement algorithm (GSIA) to find an optimal strategy of simple stochastic games (SSG). We prove the correctness of GSIA, and derive a general complexity bound, which implies and improves on the results of several articles. First, we remove the assumption that the SSG is stopping, which is usually obtained by a polynomial blowup of the game. Second, we prove a tight bound on the denominator of the values associated to a strategy, and use it to prove that all strategy improvement algorithms are in fact fixed parameter tractable in the number r \displaystyle r of random vertices. All known strategy improvement algorithms can be seen as instances of GSIA, which allows to analyze the complexity of converge from below by Condon [ 13 ] and to propose a class of algorithms generalising Gimbert and Horn’s algorithm [ 15 , 16 ] . These algorithms terminate in at most r ! \displaystyle r! iterations, and for binary SSGs, they do less iterations than the current best deterministic algorithm given by Ibsen-Jensen and Miltersen [ 17 ] .

Introduction

A
simple stochastic game
, or SSG, is a two-player turn-based zero-sum game with perfect information introduced by Condon
[
12
]
. It is a simpler version of
stochastic games
, previously defined by Shapley
[
22
]
. An SSG is played by two players
max
and
min
moving a pebble on a graph. Vertices are divided into
min
vertices,
max
vertices, random vertices and a target vertex for
max
. When the pebble reaches a
min
or
max
vertex, corresponding players move the pebble to a neighbouring vertex of their choice. If it reaches a random vertex, the next vertex is chosen at random following some probability law. Finally, when the pebble reaches the target vertex,
min
pays
\displaystyle 1
to
max
. The goal of
min
is to minimise the probability to reach the target vertex while
max
must maximise this probability.

We study the algorithmic problem of
solving
an SSG, i.e. finding a pair of optimal strategies in an SSG,
or equivalently the optimal value vector of the optimal probabilities for
max
to reach the sink from each vertex. There are always optimal strategies for both players that are positional
[
12
]
, i.e. stationary and deterministic, but the number of positional strategies is exponential in the size of the game. Consequently, finding a pair of optimal strategies is a problem not known to be in
FP
, but it is in
PPAD

[
19
]
, a class included in
FNP
.

Simple Stochastic Games can be used to simulate many classical games such as parity games, mean or discounted payoff games
[
2
,
8
]
. Moreover, stochastic versions of these games are equivalent to SSGs
[
2
]
, which underlines that SSGs are an important model to study. SSGs have applications in different domains such as model checking of modal
\displaystyle\mu
-calculus
[
23
]
, or modelling autonomous urban driving
[
10
]
.

There are three known methods to solve SSGs: strategy improvement, value iteration and quadratic programming. A strategy improvement algorithm (SIA) starts with a strategy for one player and improves it until it is optimal, whereas value iteration algorithms (VIA) update a value vector by elementary operations, which converges to the optimal value vector of the game. Implementations of those algorithms have been written and compared in
[
20
]
.

Denote by
\displaystyle n
be the number of
max
vertices and
\displaystyle r
be the number of random vertices in an SSG.
For SSGs with
max
vertices of outdegree
\displaystyle 2
, the best known deterministic algorithm is an SIA which makes at worst
\displaystyle\displaystyle O\left(2^{n}/n\right)
iterations (see
[
24
]
), and the best known randomised algorithm is a SIA described by Ludwig in
[
21
]
, which runs in
\displaystyle\displaystyle 2^{O\left(\sqrt{n}\right)}
.

Gimbert and Horn give an SIA in
[
15
]
, running in
\displaystyle\displaystyle O^{*}\left(r!\right)
iterations, namely a superpolynomial dependency in
\displaystyle r
only (
\displaystyle O^{*}
omits polynomial factors in
\displaystyle r
and
\displaystyle n
). For SSGs where random vertices have a probability distribution
\displaystyle(1/2,1/2)
(coin toss), Ibsen-Jensen and Miltersen present a VIA of complexity in
\displaystyle\displaystyle O^{*}\left(2^{r}\right)

[
17
]
. It turns out that all SIA runs in
\displaystyle\displaystyle O^{*}\left(2^{r}\right)
on SSGs with probability distribution
\displaystyle(1/2,1/2)
, as we prove in this article. The same complexity of
\displaystyle O^{*}\left(2^{r}\right)
is obtained for general SSGs with a more involved randomised algorithm in
[
4
]
.

Most of the aforementioned algorithms rely on the game being stopping, meaning that it structurally ends in a sink with probability
\displaystyle 1
. This condition is not restrictive since any SSG can be transformed into a stopping SSG while keeping the same optimal strategies. However, this transformation incurs a quadratic blow-up of the game and cannot be used in real life application. In this paper we give bounds in
\displaystyle O\left(2^{r}Poly(n)\right)
computational time for some kinds of algorithm. Using the stopping transformation would have induced a
\displaystyle O\left(2^{nr^{2}}Poly(n)\right)
complexity. The stopping restriction has been lifted for quadratic programming in
[
20
]
and before that for SIA and VIA in
[
9
,
7
]
.

Contributions

We introduce GSIA, a new meta-algorithm to solve SSGs in Sec.
3
. This algorithm proves simultaneously the correctness of multiple algorithms (
[
13
,
15
,
14
,
24
,
17
,
4
]
). In Sec.
4
, we give a general complexity bound that matches or improves on previous bounds obtained by ad-hoc methods. We show that all these algorithms are fixed-parameter tractable in the number of random vertices. Moreover, we do not rely on the fact that the game is stopping, which was commonly used in the aforementioned papers.
The proof of correctness relies on a notion of concatenation for strategies and an analysis of absorbing sets in the game, while the complexity bound is derived from a new and tight characterisation of the values of an SSG. Finally, in Sec.
5
, we show how GSIA can be used to derive new algorithms, generalising classical ones. In particular, we exhibit a class of algorithms which generalise Gimbert and Horn’s algorithm
and use less iterations than Ibsen-Jensen and Miltersen’s algorithm.

We emphasise that our goal here is not to define a new
algorithm that would have a better –but still exponential– complexity bound than the state of the art (a sub-exponential algorithm for SSGs, like the ones found for parity games
[
5
,
11
]
, would already be a significant improvement),
but rather to wrap-up a lot of previous research
by showing that all known SIA for SSGs, despite having emerged in different contexts and having ad-hoc proofs of convergence, are in fact instances of a general pattern that can be
further expanded, and actually share the
best known complexity bounds.

Simple Properties of Simple Stochastic Games

2.1
Simple Stochastic Games

We give a generalised definition of
Simple Stochastic Game
, a two-player zero-sum game with turn-based moves and perfect information introduced by Anne Condon
[
13
]
.

Definition 1
.

A Simple Stochastic Game (SSG) is a directed graph
\displaystyle G
, together with:

1.

A partition of the vertex set
\displaystyle V
in four parts
\displaystyle V_{\text{\sc max}}
,
\displaystyle V_{\text{\sc min}}
,
\displaystyle V_{R}
and
\displaystyle V_{S}
(all possibly empty, except
\displaystyle V_{S}
), satisfying the following conditions:

(a)

every vertex of
\displaystyle V_{\text{\sc max}}
,
\displaystyle V_{\text{\sc min}}
or
\displaystyle V_{R}
has at least one outgoing arc;

(b)

every vertex of
\displaystyle V_{S}
has exactly one outgoing arc which is a loop on itself.

2.

For every
\displaystyle x\in V_{R}
, a probability distribution
\displaystyle p_{x}(\cdot)
with rational values, on the outneighbourhood of
\displaystyle x
.

3.

For every
\displaystyle x\in V_{S}
, a value
\displaystyle\mbox{Val}(x)
which is a rational number in the closed interval
\displaystyle[0,1]
.

In the article, we denote
\displaystyle|V_{\text{\sc max}}|
by
\displaystyle n
and
\displaystyle|V_{R}|
by
\displaystyle r
.
Vertices from
\displaystyle V_{\text{\sc max}}
,
\displaystyle V_{\text{\sc min}}
,
\displaystyle V_{R}
and
\displaystyle V_{S}
are respectively called
max
vertices,
min
vertices, random vertices and sinks. For
\displaystyle x\in V
, we denote by
\displaystyle N^{+}(x)
the set of outneighbours of
\displaystyle x
. We assume that for every
\displaystyle x\in V_{R}
and
\displaystyle y\in V
,
\displaystyle y\in N^{+}(x)
if and only if
\displaystyle p_{x}(y)>0
.

The game is played as follows. The two players are named
max
and
min
. A token is positioned on a starting vertex
\displaystyle x
. If
\displaystyle x
is in
\displaystyle V_{\text{\sc max}}
(resp.
\displaystyle V_{\text{\sc min}}
) the
max
player (resp. the
min
player) chooses one of the outneighbours of
\displaystyle x
to move the token to. If
\displaystyle x
is in
\displaystyle V_{R}
, the token is randomly moved to one of the outneighbours of
\displaystyle x
according to the probability distribution
\displaystyle p_{x}(\cdot)
, independently of everything else. This process continues until the token reaches a sink
\displaystyle s
and then, player
min
has to pay
\displaystyle\mbox{Val}(s)
to player
max
and the game stops.
The problem we study is to
find the best possible strategies
for
min
and
max
, and the expected value that
min
has to pay to
max
while following those strategies.

We consider a slightly restricted class of SSGs where the probability distribution on each random vertex has a given precision and the value of the sinks are
\displaystyle 0
and
\displaystyle 1
.

Definition 2
.

For
\displaystyle q
a positive integer, we say that an SSG is a
\displaystyle q
-SSG if there are only two sinks of value
\displaystyle 0
and
\displaystyle 1
, and for all
\displaystyle x\in V_{R}
, there is an integer
\displaystyle q_{x}\leq q
such that the probability distribution
\displaystyle p_{x}(\cdot)
can be written as
\displaystyle p_{x}(x^{\prime})=\displaystyle\frac{\ell_{x,x^{\prime}}}{q_{x}}
for all
\displaystyle x^{\prime}
where
\displaystyle\ell_{x,x^{\prime}}
is a natural number.

As an example, let
\displaystyle x
be a random vertex of a
\displaystyle 2
-SSG, and let
\displaystyle u\in N^{+}(x)
, then
\displaystyle p_{x}(u)
can be equal to
\displaystyle 0
,
\displaystyle 1/2
or
\displaystyle 1
. The case
\displaystyle p_{x}(u)=0
is forbidden by definition, and if
\displaystyle p_{x}(u)=1
, then
\displaystyle x
is of degree one and can be removed (by redirecting arcs entering
\displaystyle x
directly to
\displaystyle u
), without changing anything about the outcome of the game.
Hence, we suppose without loss of generality that each random vertex of a
\displaystyle 2
-SSG has degree
\displaystyle 2
and has probability distribution
\displaystyle(1/2,1/2)
. This definition matches the one of a
binary SSG
, given by Condon and used in most articles on SSGs, except that we allow here
max
and
min
vertices to have an outdegree larger than
\displaystyle 2
.

Play, History and Strategies

Definition 3
.

A

in
\displaystyle G

\displaystyle X=(x_{0},x_{1},x_{2},\cdots)

\displaystyle t\geq 0

A

.

A

\displaystyle\sigma(h)

\displaystyle x_{k}

(resp.
\displaystyle\Sigma_{gen}^{\text{\sc min}}
).

For
\displaystyle\sigma\in\Sigma_{gen}^{\text{\sc max}}
and
\displaystyle\tau\in\Sigma_{gen}^{\text{\sc min}}
, given a starting vertex
\displaystyle x_{0}

\displaystyle X=(X_{0},X_{1},\cdots)
of
\displaystyle G
in the following way. At
\displaystyle t=0

\displaystyle X_{0}=x_{0}
, and for
\displaystyle t\geq 0

\displaystyle X_{t}\in V_{\text{\sc max}}

\displaystyle X_{t+1}=\sigma(X_{0},X_{1},\cdots,X_{t})

•

\displaystyle X_{t}\in V_{\text{\sc min}}

\displaystyle X_{t+1}=\tau(X_{0},X_{1},\cdots,X_{t})

The corresponding expected value and conditional expected values are denoted by
\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}^{x_{0}}_{\sigma,\tau}\left(\cdot|\cdot\right)

We now define

Definition 5
.

A general
max

\displaystyle\sigma
(resp.

\displaystyle x
(resp.

\displaystyle\sigma(h)=\sigma((x))

\displaystyle(x)

is the history containing only

\displaystyle\Sigma^{\text{\sc min}}

2.3
Values in an SSG

Definition 6
.

Let
\displaystyle G
be an SSG and let
\displaystyle(\sigma,\tau)
be a pair of
max
and
min
strategies, the value vector
\displaystyle v^{G}_{\sigma,\tau}

\displaystyle|V|
defined by, for any
\displaystyle x_{0}\in V
,

This value represents the expected gains for player
max

\displaystyle(\sigma,\tau)
and the game starts in vertex
\displaystyle x_{0}
.

As before, the superscript
\displaystyle G

To compare value vectors, we use the pointwise order: we say that
\displaystyle v\geq v^{\prime}
if for all vertices
\displaystyle x\in V

\displaystyle v(x)\geq v^{\prime}(x)
. Moreover, we say that
\displaystyle v>v^{\prime}
if
\displaystyle v\geq v^{\prime}
and there is some
\displaystyle x
such that
\displaystyle v(x)>v^{\prime}(x)
.
Given a
max
strategy
\displaystyle\sigma
, a
best response
to
\displaystyle\sigma

is a
min

\displaystyle\tau
such that
\displaystyle v_{\sigma,\tau}\leq v_{\sigma,\tau^{\prime}}

for all
min

\displaystyle\tau^{\prime}
.

Proposition 7

(
[
13
]
)
.

A positional strategy admits a positional best response, which can be found in polynomial time using linear programming.

The set of positional best responses to
\displaystyle\sigma
is denoted by
\displaystyle BR(\sigma)
. Similarly, for a
min

\displaystyle\tau
, we define the notion of best response to
\displaystyle\tau

\displaystyle BR(\tau)
. Except explicitly stated otherwise (in Sec.

), all considered strategies are positional.

We denote by
\displaystyle\tau(\sigma)
a positional best response to
\displaystyle\sigma
.
For a
max
strategy
\displaystyle\sigma
and
\displaystyle\tau\in BR(\sigma)
, we write
\displaystyle v_{\sigma}
for
\displaystyle v_{\sigma,\tau}
.
For a
min
strategy
\displaystyle\tau
and
\displaystyle\sigma\in BR(\tau)
, we write
\displaystyle v_{\tau}
for
\displaystyle v_{\sigma,\tau}
. The vector
\displaystyle v_{\sigma}
is called the value vector of strategy
\displaystyle\sigma

\displaystyle\sigma^{\prime}\underset{G}{>}\sigma

\displaystyle v^{G}_{\sigma^{\prime}}>v^{G}_{\sigma}

It is well known (see

) that there is a pair of deterministic positional strategies
\displaystyle(\sigma^{*},\tau^{*})
called optimal strategies, that satisfies for all
\displaystyle x
,

\displaystyle v^{*}=v_{\sigma^{*},\tau^{*}}=v_{\sigma^{*}}=v_{\tau^{*}}

\displaystyle\sigma^{*}

\displaystyle\tau^{*}

2.4
Optimality Conditions

The next two lemmas give characterisations of (optimal) value vectors under a pair of strategies. They are fundamental to all algorithms finding optimal strategies. Proofs of similar results can be found in
[
12
]
; we add here a fifth condition to make the characterisation hold when the game is not stopping.

For any SSG, the vertices with value
\displaystyle 0
under optimal strategies can be found in linear time by a simple graph traversal computing its complementary, the set of
max
vertices which can access a sink of positive value, regardless of the choice of the
min
player. Let
\displaystyle K^{G}
be the set of vertices with value
\displaystyle 0
under optimal strategies. For a
max
strategy
\displaystyle\sigma
of
\displaystyle G
and a
min
strategy
\displaystyle\tau
, we call
\displaystyle K^{G}_{\sigma,\tau}
the set of vertices with value zero under the pair of strategies
\displaystyle\sigma,\tau
, and
\displaystyle K^{G}_{\sigma}
the set of vertices with value zero under
\displaystyle\sigma,\tau
when
\displaystyle\tau
is a best response to
\displaystyle\sigma
.

Lemma 8
.

Given positional strategies
\displaystyle(\sigma,\tau)
and a real
\displaystyle|V|
-dimensional vector
\displaystyle v
, one has
equality between
\displaystyle v
and
\displaystyle v_{\sigma,\tau}
if and only if the following conditions are met:

{romanenumerate}

For
\displaystyle s\in V_{S}
,
\displaystyle v(s)=\mbox{Val}(s)

For
\displaystyle r\in V_{R}
,
\displaystyle v(r)=\sum\limits_{y\in N^{+}(r)}p_{r}(y)v(y)

For
\displaystyle x\in V_{\text{\sc min}}
,
\displaystyle v(x)=v(\tau(x))

For
\displaystyle x\in V_{\text{\sc max}}
,
\displaystyle v(x)=v(\sigma(x))

For any
\displaystyle x\in V
,
\displaystyle v(x)=0
, if and only if
\displaystyle x\in K^{G}_{\sigma,\tau}

Moreover,
\displaystyle\tau\in BR(\sigma)
if and only if for any
\displaystyle x
in
\displaystyle V_{\text{\sc min}}
,
\displaystyle v(x)=\min\limits_{y\in N^{+}(x)}v(y)=v(\tau(x))
and the last condition is modified into
\displaystyle v(x)=0
if and only if
\displaystyle x\in K^{G}_{\sigma}
.

Lemma 9

(Optimality conditions)
.

Given positional strategies
\displaystyle(\sigma,\tau)
and denoting
\displaystyle v=v_{\sigma,\tau}
,
\displaystyle(\sigma,\tau)
are optimal strategies if and only if:

{romanenumerate}

For
\displaystyle s\in V_{S}
,
\displaystyle v(s)=\mbox{Val}(s)

For
\displaystyle r\in V_{R}
,
\displaystyle v(r)=\sum\limits_{y\in N^{+}(r)}p_{r}(y)v(y)

For
\displaystyle x\in V_{\text{\sc min}}
,
\displaystyle v(x)=\min\limits_{y\in N^{+}(x)}v(y)

For
\displaystyle x\in V_{\text{\sc max}}
,
\displaystyle v(x)=\max\limits_{y\in N^{+}(x)}v(y)

For any
\displaystyle x\in V
,
\displaystyle v(x)=0
, if and only if
\displaystyle x\in K^{G}

The conditions of Lemma
9
imply that
\displaystyle(\sigma,\tau)
is a certificate of optimality that can be checked in polynomial time: compute
\displaystyle v_{\sigma,\tau}
by solving the linear system of Lemma

\displaystyle K^{G}

\displaystyle s\in V^{G}_{S}\cap R^{G}_{\sigma,\tau}(x)

3.2
The Algorithm

An SSG is
stopping
if under every pair of strategies, a play eventually reaches a sink with probability
\displaystyle 1
. Most algorithms in the literature depend on the game being stopping.
It is usually not seen as a limitation since it is possible to transform every SSG into a stopping SSG, but the transformation makes the game polynomially larger by adding
\displaystyle O(nr)
random vertices, which is bad from a complexity point of view, especially for algorithm with parametrized complexity in the number of random vertices.
We strengthen the classical order on strategies, to get rid of the stopping condition in the generic strategy improvement algorithm presented in this section.

Definition 13
.

Let
\displaystyle\sigma
and
\displaystyle\sigma^{\prime}
be two
max
strategies, then
\displaystyle\sigma^{\prime}\underset{G}{\succ}\sigma
if
\displaystyle\sigma^{\prime}\underset{G}{>}\sigma
and for every
max
vertex
\displaystyle x
, if
\displaystyle v^{G}_{\sigma^{\prime}}(x)=v^{G}_{\sigma}(x)
, then
\displaystyle\sigma^{\prime}(x)=\sigma(x)
.

Algorithm
1
is a classical strategy improvement algorithm with two twists:
the improvement is for
the stricter order
\displaystyle\succ
and it is guaranteed in
the transformed game
rather than in the original game. We call Algorithm
1
the Generic Strategy Improvement Algorithm, or GSIA.

Data:

\displaystyle G
a stopping SSG

Result:

\displaystyle(\sigma,\tau)
a pair of optimal strategies

begin

1

select an initial
max
strategy
\displaystyle\sigma

2

while

\displaystyle(\sigma,\tau(\sigma))
are not optimal strategies of
\displaystyle G

do

3

choose a subset
\displaystyle A
of arcs of
\displaystyle G

4

find
\displaystyle\sigma^{\prime}
such that
\displaystyle\sigma^{\prime}\underset{G[A,\sigma]}{\succ}\sigma
.

5

\displaystyle\sigma\longleftarrow\sigma^{\prime}

6

7

return

\displaystyle(\sigma,\tau(\sigma))

8

Algorithm 1

GSIA

Algorithm
1
is a generic algorithm (or meta-algorithm) because neither the selection of an initial strategy
\displaystyle\sigma
at line
\displaystyle 2

\displaystyle A
at line
\displaystyle 4
, nor the way of finding
\displaystyle\sigma^{\prime}
at line
\displaystyle 5
, are specified. A choice of implementation for these three parts is an
instance
of GSIA, that is a concrete strategy improvement algorithm.
Note that if
\displaystyle\sigma^{\prime}\underset{G[A,\sigma]}{>}\sigma
is found,
it is easy to find
\displaystyle\sigma^{\prime\prime}
with
\displaystyle\sigma^{\prime\prime}\underset{G[A,\sigma]}{\succ}\sigma
: define
\displaystyle\sigma^{\prime\prime}
as equal to
\displaystyle\sigma^{\prime}
, except for
max
vertices
\displaystyle x
such that
\displaystyle v^{G}_{\sigma^{\prime}}(x)=v^{G}_{\sigma}(x)
and
\displaystyle\sigma^{\prime}(x)\neq\sigma(x)
where
\displaystyle\sigma^{\prime\prime}(x)
is defined as
\displaystyle\sigma(x)
.

When we prove some property of GSIA in this article, it means that the property is true for all instances of GSIA, that is regardless of the selection of the initial strategy, the set
\displaystyle A
and the method for selecting
\displaystyle\sigma^{\prime}
.

In order to prove the correctness of GSIA, we need to prove two points:

1.

If
\displaystyle\sigma
is not optimal in
\displaystyle G
, then
\displaystyle\sigma
is not optimal in
\displaystyle G[A,\sigma]
.

2.

If
\displaystyle\sigma^{\prime}\underset{G[A,\sigma]}{\succ}\sigma
then
\displaystyle\sigma^{\prime}\underset{G}{>}\sigma
.

The first point is proved in the following lemma, while the second one is harder to obtain and is the subject of the next two subsections.

Lemma 14
.

For an SSG
\displaystyle G
and a subset of arcs
\displaystyle A
, a
max

\displaystyle\sigma
is optimal in
\displaystyle G
if and only if it is optimal in
\displaystyle G[A,\sigma]

Proof.

Except on
\displaystyle A
-sinks, the value vectors of
\displaystyle\sigma
in
\displaystyle G
and
\displaystyle G[A,\sigma]
are equal by Lemma

. Furthermore, by Lemma
11

\displaystyle K^{G}_{\sigma}=K^{G[A,\sigma]}_{\sigma}
;
hence
\displaystyle\sigma
satisfies the optimality conditions of Lemma

in
\displaystyle G
if and only if it satisfies them in
\displaystyle G[A,\sigma]
.
∎

3.3
Concatenation of Strategies

As a tool for proving the correctness of Algorithm
1
, we introduce the notion of concatenation of strategies which produces non-positional strategies even if both concatenated strategies are positional. The idea of using a sequence of concatenated strategies to interpolate between two strategies has been introduced in
[
16
]
.

Definition 15
.

For two
max
strategies
\displaystyle\sigma
,
\displaystyle\sigma^{\prime}
and a subset of arcs
\displaystyle A

\displaystyle\sigma^{\prime}|_{A}\sigma

\displaystyle\sigma^{\prime}
until an arc of
\displaystyle A

\displaystyle\sigma
until the end of the game. We let
\displaystyle\sigma^{\prime}|^{0}_{A}\sigma=\sigma
and for all
\displaystyle i\geq 0

\displaystyle\sigma^{\prime}|^{i+1}_{A}\sigma=\sigma^{\prime}|_{A}(\sigma^{\prime}|^{i}_{A}\sigma)

When
\displaystyle A

\displaystyle\sigma^{\prime}|^{i}\sigma

Strategy
\displaystyle\sigma^{\prime}|^{i}_{A}\sigma
is the strategy that plays like
\displaystyle\sigma^{\prime}
until
\displaystyle i
arcs from
\displaystyle A
have been crossed, and then plays like
\displaystyle\sigma
. Hence, we can relate the strategy
\displaystyle\sigma^{\prime}|_{A}\sigma

to a positional strategy in
\displaystyle G[A,\sigma]
as shown in the next lemma.

Lemma 16
.

For two
max
strategies
\displaystyle\sigma
,
\displaystyle\sigma^{\prime}
and a subset of arcs
\displaystyle A
, we have:
\displaystyle v^{G}_{\sigma^{\prime}|_{A}\sigma}=v^{G[A,\sigma]}_{\sigma^{\prime}}

Proof.

In
\displaystyle G
, after crossing an arc from
\displaystyle A
, by definition of
\displaystyle\sigma^{\prime}|_{A}\sigma
,
max
plays according to
\displaystyle\sigma
. The game being memoryless, from this point, the best response for
min
is to play like
\displaystyle\tau(\sigma)\in BR(\sigma)
. Thus, there is a best response to
\displaystyle\sigma^{\prime}|\sigma
of the form
\displaystyle\tau^{\prime}|\tau(\sigma)
with
\displaystyle\tau^{\prime}
a
min
strategy not necessarily positional. Let us consider a play following
\displaystyle(\sigma^{\prime}|\sigma,\tau|\tau(\sigma))
with
\displaystyle\tau
any
min

\displaystyle A

\displaystyle(\sigma^{\prime},\tau)
in
\displaystyle G[A,\sigma]

\displaystyle A

\displaystyle v^{G}_{\sigma^{\prime}|\sigma,\tau|\tau(\sigma)}=v^{G[A,\sigma]}_{\sigma^{\prime},\tau}.

∎

The following proposition is the core idea of GSIA: a strategy which improves on

\displaystyle\sigma

The proof relies on a precise analysis of the set of vertices which cannot reach a sink, to deal with the fact that the game is not stopping. We prove that, if

\displaystyle G

We analyse the algorithmic complexity of GSIA, by lower bounding the values

from
\displaystyle\sigma

4.1
Values of q-SSGs

In a
\displaystyle 2

4.2
Bounding the Number of Iterations of GSIA

GSIA produces a sequence of strictly increasing positional
max
strategies. The number of positional
max
strategies is bounded by
\displaystyle\left|\Sigma^{\text{\sc max}}\right|=\prod\limits_{x\in V_{\text{\sc max}}}\deg(x)
, hence the number of iterations of GSIA is bounded by this value. If we consider the case of a binary SSG (all vertices of outdegree
\displaystyle 2
), we have the classical bound of
\displaystyle|\Sigma^{\text{\sc max}}|=2^{n}
iterations. The best known bound for a deterministic algorithm is
\displaystyle 2^{n}/n
iterations obtained for Hoffman-Karp algorithm
[
24
]
, which is not far from the trivial bound of
\displaystyle 2^{n}
iterations.

We give a bound for
\displaystyle q
-SSG, which depends on
\displaystyle q
and
\displaystyle r
the number of random vertices.
The difference of two values written as
\displaystyle a/b
and
\displaystyle c/d
, with
\displaystyle a
and
\displaystyle b
less than
\displaystyle q^{-r}

is more than
\displaystyle q^{-2r}
. Hence, if a value increases in GSIA, it increases at least by
\displaystyle q^{-2r}
.
Using the classical notion of switch and anti-switch
[
24
]
, recalled bellow, we can prove
that all vertices which have their value increased by a step of GSIA, are increased by at least
\displaystyle q^{-r}
.

Theorem 26
.

For
\displaystyle G
a
\displaystyle q
-SSG with
\displaystyle r
random vertices and
\displaystyle n

max
vertices, the number of iterations of GSIA is at most
\displaystyle nq^{r}
.

We introduce the notion of switch and anti-switch, to prove that the improvement is at least
\displaystyle q^{-r}
rather than
\displaystyle q^{-2r}
.

Definition 27
.

A switch (resp. an anti-switch) of a
max
strategy
\displaystyle\sigma

with switched set
\displaystyle S~\subseteq~V_{\text{\sc max}}
is a strategy
\displaystyle\sigma_{S}
defined by
\displaystyle\sigma_{S}(x)=\sigma(x)
for
\displaystyle x\notin S
, and
satisfying

\displaystyle v_{\sigma}(\sigma(x))~<~v_{\sigma}(\sigma_{S}(x))
(resp.
\displaystyle v_{\sigma}(\sigma(x))~>~v_{\sigma}(\sigma_{S}(x))
)
for
\displaystyle x\in S
(hence
\displaystyle\sigma_{S}(x)\neq\sigma(x)
).

A common tool to solve SSGs is the fact that a switch increases the value of a strategy, while an anti-switch decreases it. Within our framework of transformed game, it is extremely simple to prove.

Lemma 28
.

If
\displaystyle\sigma_{S}
is a switch of
\displaystyle\sigma
, then
\displaystyle\sigma_{S}>\sigma
.
If
\displaystyle\sigma_{S}
is an anti-switch of
\displaystyle\sigma
, then
\displaystyle\sigma_{S}<\sigma
.

Proof.

Consider
\displaystyle G[A,\sigma]
the game obtained from
\displaystyle G
, where
\displaystyle A
is the set of all arcs of
\displaystyle G
. Let us consider
\displaystyle x
a vertex switched in
\displaystyle\sigma^{\prime}
, that is with
\displaystyle v^{G}_{\sigma}(\sigma(x))<v^{G}_{\sigma}(\sigma^{\prime}(x))
. Then, because all arcs are in
\displaystyle A
, we have
\displaystyle v^{G[A,\sigma]}_{\sigma}(x)=v^{G}_{\sigma}(\sigma(x))
and

\displaystyle v^{G[A,\sigma]}_{\sigma^{\prime}}(x)=v^{G}_{\sigma}(\sigma^{\prime}(x))
. Hence,
\displaystyle v^{G[A,\sigma]}_{\sigma}(x)<v^{G[A,\sigma]}_{\sigma^{\prime}}(x)
and for
\displaystyle v^{G}_{\sigma}(\sigma(x))\geq v^{G}_{\sigma}(\sigma^{\prime}(x))
,
\displaystyle\sigma(x)=\sigma^{\prime}(x)
,
which implies
\displaystyle\sigma^{\prime}\underset{G[A,\sigma]}{\succ}\sigma
. Prop.
18
proves
\displaystyle\sigma^{\prime}\underset{G}{>}\sigma
.

The proof is the same for an anti-switch, since
\displaystyle\sigma\underset{G[A,\sigma]}{\succ}\sigma^{\prime}\Rightarrow\sigma\underset{G}{>}\sigma^{\prime}
(which can be proved similarly as Prop.
18
, while keeping in mind that in the decreasing case, creating absorbing set lowers the value).
∎

We use the previous lemma to prove Th.
26
.

Proof.

Let us consider
\displaystyle\sigma
the strategy computed at some point by GSIA and
\displaystyle\sigma^{\prime}
the next strategy. By Prop.
18
,
\displaystyle\sigma<\sigma^{\prime}
.
Hence, by Lemma
28
,
\displaystyle\sigma^{\prime}
cannot be an anti-switch of
\displaystyle\sigma
. Thus, there is a
max
vertex
\displaystyle x
such that
\displaystyle v_{\sigma}(\sigma(x))<v_{\sigma}(\sigma^{\prime}(x))
. We recall that
\displaystyle\sigma^{\prime}(x)
denotes the successor of
\displaystyle x
under strategy
\displaystyle\sigma^{\prime}
.

Since
\displaystyle\sigma<\sigma^{\prime}
, we have
\displaystyle v_{\sigma}(x)=v_{\sigma}(\sigma(x))<v_{\sigma}(\sigma^{\prime}(x))\leq v_{\sigma^{\prime}}(\sigma^{\prime}(x))=v_{\sigma^{\prime}}(x)
.
We now evaluate
\displaystyle v_{\sigma}(\sigma^{\prime}(x))-v_{\sigma}(\sigma(x))
.
In the game
\displaystyle G
, under the strategies
\displaystyle\sigma,\tau(\sigma)
, Th.
23
implies that for some
\displaystyle t\leq q^{r}
,
\displaystyle v_{\sigma}(\sigma(x))=p/t
and

\displaystyle v_{\sigma}(\sigma^{\prime}(x))=p^{\prime}/t
. We have
\displaystyle p/t<p^{\prime}/t
, thus
\displaystyle p^{\prime}/t-p/t\geq 1/t\geq 1/q^{r}
. Hence, the value of some
max
vertex increases by
\displaystyle 1/q^{r}
in each iteration of GSIA. Since there are
\displaystyle n

max
vertices and their values are bounded by
\displaystyle 1
, there are at most
\displaystyle nq^{r}
iterations.
∎

The complexity of GSIA is the number of iterations given by Th.
26
, multiplied by the complexity of an iteration. In an iteration, there are two sources of complexity: constructing the game
\displaystyle G[A,\sigma]
and finding an improving strategy
\displaystyle\sigma^{\prime}
in
\displaystyle G[A,\sigma]
. To construct the game,
\displaystyle v_{\sigma}
is computed by solving a linear program of size
\displaystyle m
up to precision
\displaystyle p=q^{r}
. Let
\displaystyle C_{1}(m,p)
be the complexity of computing
\displaystyle v_{\sigma}
, then the best bound is currently in
\displaystyle O(m^{\omega}\log(p))

[
18
]
, with
\displaystyle\omega
the current best bound on the matrix multiplication exponent. Let
\displaystyle C_{2}(n,r,q)
be the complexity of computing
\displaystyle\sigma^{\prime}
, the complexity of an iteration is in
\displaystyle O(nq^{r}(C_{1}(n+r,q^{r})+C_{2}(n,r,q))
.

We obtain a better complexity, when
\displaystyle C_{2}(n,r,q)=O(C_{1}(n,q^{r})r/n)
, which is the case for most instances of GSIA mentionned in this article. The number of iterations is only
\displaystyle rq^{r}
if we can guarantee that a random vertex increases its value at each step. When no random vertex is improved, the cost of computing
\displaystyle G[A,\sigma]
can be made smaller, which yields the following theorem.

Theorem 29
.

Let
\displaystyle G
be a
\displaystyle q
-SSG with
\displaystyle r
random vertices and
\displaystyle n

max
vertices. If
\displaystyle C_{2}(n,r,q)=O(C_{1}(n,q^{r})r/n)
, then the complexity of GSIA is in
\displaystyle O(rq^{r}C_{1}(n,q^{r}))
.

Proof.

We assume that
\displaystyle r<n
, otherwise the theorem is trivial.
Let
\displaystyle\sigma^{\prime}
be the strategy computed by GSIA at some point, improving on the strategy
\displaystyle\sigma
. GSIA must compute
\displaystyle G[A,\sigma^{\prime}]
, and thus
\displaystyle v_{\sigma^{\prime}}
and we explain a method to do so efficiently.

We assume that the order of the values (in
\displaystyle G
) of the random vertices is the same for
\displaystyle\sigma
and
\displaystyle\sigma^{\prime}
. Then, knowing this order and
\displaystyle\sigma^{\prime}
, it is easy to compute
\displaystyle\tau(\sigma^{\prime})
a best response to
\displaystyle\sigma^{\prime}
in
\displaystyle O(r\log(r)+n)
time
[
1
]
. Then, we can compute the values
\displaystyle v_{\sigma^{\prime},\tau(\sigma^{\prime})}
in time
\displaystyle O(C_{1}(r,q^{r}))
, since it is done by solving a linear system of dimension
\displaystyle r
with precision
\displaystyle q^{r}
, a task which is simpler than solving a linear program. Since
\displaystyle C_{1}(r,q^{r})
is at least quadratic in
\displaystyle r
, then
\displaystyle C_{1}(r,q^{r})<C_{1}(n,q^{r})r/n
and by hypothesis
\displaystyle C_{2}(n,r,q)=O(C_{1}(n,q^{r})r/n)
, hence a step is of complexity at most
\displaystyle O(C_{1}(n,q^{r})r/n)
. There are at most
\displaystyle nq^{r}
such steps, for a total complexity of
\displaystyle O(rq^{r}C_{1}(n,q^{r}))
.

We need to detect when the assumption that the values of the random vertices are the same for
\displaystyle\sigma
and
\displaystyle\sigma^{\prime}
is false. If
\displaystyle v_{\sigma^{\prime},\tau(\sigma^{\prime})}
satisfies the optimality conditions at the
min
vertices, then
\displaystyle\tau(\sigma^{\prime})
is a best response. Otherwise, we compute the best response by solving a linear program in time
\displaystyle C(n,q^{r})
. In that case, the order of the random vertices has changed:
there are two vertices
\displaystyle x_{1}
and
\displaystyle x_{2}
such that
\displaystyle v_{\sigma}(x_{1})<v_{\sigma}(x_{2})
and
\displaystyle v_{\sigma^{\prime}}(x_{1})>v_{\sigma^{\prime}}(x_{2})
. Hence,
\displaystyle v_{\sigma^{\prime}}(x_{1})>v_{\sigma}(x_{2})
, which implies that
\displaystyle v_{\sigma^{\prime}}(x_{1})-v_{\sigma}(x_{1})>v_{\sigma}(x_{2})-v_{\sigma}(x_{1})>q^{-r}
.

We have proved that when the random order changes, the value of some random vertex increases by at least
\displaystyle q^{-r}
, hence there are at most
\displaystyle rq^{r}
such steps. The complexity from these steps is bounded by
\displaystyle O(rq^{r}C_{1}(n,q^{r}))
, which proves the theorem.
∎

5
Two Instances of GSIA

As previously mentioned, all known strategy improvement algorithms can be viewed as particular instances of GSIA. This includes e.g. switch-based algorithms, like Hoffman-Karp algorithm
[
13
,
24
]
or Ludwig’s recursive algorithm
[
21
]
. With the help of GSIA it also becomes very easy to derive new algorithms, by transforming the game into polynomial time solvable instances, such as almost acyclic games
[
3
]
. We detail all these old and new algorithms in Section
6
.

In this section, we focus instead on two particular instances (or family of instances) of GSIA, for which we obtain new complexity bounds using the results of the previous sections.

5.1
GSIA and f-strategies

The strategy improvement algorithm proposed by Gimbert and Horn in
[
15
]
(denoted by GHA) can be viewed as an instance of GSIA where the set
\displaystyle A
of fixed arcs is the set
\displaystyle R
of all arcs going out of random vertices, and the improvement step in the subgame
\displaystyle G[R,\sigma]
consists in taking an optimal strategy.
In this case, the subgame
\displaystyle G[R,\sigma]
is deterministic (random vertices are connected to sinks only and can be replaced by sinks), hence optimal values in
\displaystyle G[R,\sigma]
depend only on the relative ordering of the values
\displaystyle v_{\sigma}(x)
for sink and random vertices
\displaystyle x
of
\displaystyle G
.
These values can be computed in
\displaystyle O(r\log(r)+n)
time
[
1
]
. In the original paper
[
15
]
, the algorithm is proposed in a context where the number
of sinks is two, but we generalise their definitions to our context.

Consider a total ordering
\displaystyle f
on
\displaystyle V_{R}\cup V_{S}
,
\displaystyle f:x_{1}<x_{2}<\cdots<x_{r+s}
,
where
\displaystyle s
is the number of sinks. An

\displaystyle f
-strategy corresponding to this ordering is an optimal
max
strategy in the game where
the
\displaystyle s+r
vertices above are replaced by sinks with new values satisfying
\displaystyle\mbox{Val}(x_{1})<\mbox{Val}(x_{2})<\cdots<\mbox{Val}(x_{r+s}).
Clearly, this strategy does not depend on the actual values given but only on
\displaystyle f
. Note that if several
\displaystyle f
-strategies exist for a given
\displaystyle f
,
they share the same values on all vertices.

Algorithm GHA produces an improving sequence of
\displaystyle f
-strategies, and the two sinks of value zero and one are always first and last in the order, hence its number of iterations is bounded by
\displaystyle r!
, the total number of possible orderings of the random vertices. We extend this result to a large class of instances of GSIA: let us call Optimal-GSIA (Opt-GSIA), the meta algorithm obtained from Algorithm
1
with two additional constraints:

•

the set
\displaystyle A
of fixed arcs is the same at each step of Algorithm
1
;

•

at line
\displaystyle 5
, the improving strategy
\displaystyle\sigma^{\prime}
is the optimal strategy in
\displaystyle G[A,\sigma]
.

All classical algorithms captured by GSIA, or new ones presented in this article are in fact instances of Opt-GSIA. We now show that Opt-GSIA has an iteration number similar to GHA. Since we have proved a bound of
\displaystyle nq^{r}
iterations, by Th.
26
, Opt-GSIA has essentially the best known number of iterations, for
\displaystyle q
small and large (the latter being interesting in the case of random vertices with large degree and arbitrary probability distributions).

Theorem 30
.

Consider an SSG
\displaystyle G
and a set of arcs
\displaystyle A
containing
\displaystyle k
arcs out of
max
or
min
vertices.
Then Algorithm Opt-GSIA runs in at most
\displaystyle\min((r+k)q^{r},(r+k)!)
iterations.

Proof.

Let
\displaystyle\sigma
be one of the iterated
max
strategies obtained by an instance of Opt-GSIA,
and
\displaystyle\sigma^{\prime}
be an optimal strategy in
\displaystyle G[A,\sigma]
. Then
\displaystyle\sigma^{\prime}
is
consequently an
\displaystyle f
-strategy in
\displaystyle G[A,\sigma]
, where
\displaystyle f
is the ordering on
\displaystyle V_{R}\cup V_{A}
(where
\displaystyle V_{A}
is the set of
\displaystyle A
-sinks) which is induced by the value vector
\displaystyle v^{G[A,\sigma]}_{\sigma^{\prime}}
(if vertices have the same value, just arbitrarily decide
their relative ordering in
\displaystyle f
).

Since strategies produced by the algorithm strictly increase in values by Prop.
18
, they must be all distinct. Hence, the order

\displaystyle f
must be distinct at each step of the algorithm, which proves that Opt-GSIA does at most
\displaystyle(r+k)!
iterations.

Moreover, at every step the value in
\displaystyle G
of at least one vertex in
\displaystyle V_{R}\cup V_{A}
must improve, by at least
\displaystyle q^{-r}
because of Th.
23
. Since the value of these vertices is bounded by
\displaystyle 1
, the number of iterations of Opt-GSIA is bounded by
\displaystyle(r+k)q^{r}
.
∎

5.2
Generalised Gimbert and Horn’s Algorithm

Th.
30
gives a competitive bound on the number of iterations for a strategy improvement algorithm, but the algorithmic complexity of an instance of Opt-GSIA also depends on how we find an optimal solution in
\displaystyle G[A,\sigma]
. We now present a class of instances of Opt-GSIA generalising GHA, with two interesting properties: there is a simple condition on
\displaystyle A
, which guarantees that
\displaystyle G[A,\sigma]

is solvable in polynomial time, and they can be precisely compared to Ibsen-Jensen and Miltersen’s algorithm (denoted by IJMA)
[
17
]
, which is the current best deterministic algorithm for
\displaystyle 2
-SSGs.

Let us describe IJMA, restated in our framework, and generalised to
\displaystyle q
-SSGs.
IJMA is not a strategy improvement algorithm but a
value iteration algorithm
, which keeps a vector of values for random vertices, here denoted by
\displaystyle v^{\text{IJMA}}_{i}
at step
\displaystyle i
. This vector is updated in the following way:

•

first, an optimal
\displaystyle f
-strategy is computed in the deterministic game
\displaystyle G[R,v^{\text{IJMA}}_{i}]
, and we denote the values of this game by
\displaystyle v^{\prime\text{IJMA}}_{i}
(remember that
\displaystyle R
is the set of arcs going out of random vertices in
\displaystyle G
) ;

•

second, the vector
\displaystyle v^{\text{IJMA}}_{i}
is updated on every random vertex by

v^{\text{IJMA}}_{i+1}(x)=\sum\limits_{y\in N^{+}(x)}p_{x}(y)\cdot v^{\prime\text{IJMA}}_{i}(y).

As can be seen, IJMA has an almost linear update complexity
since
\displaystyle G[R,\sigma]
is a deterministic game and can be solved in
\displaystyle O(r\log(r)+n)
time.

As noted in Sec.
5.1
, recall that Optimal-GSIA with
\displaystyle R
as a fixed set of arcs is equivalent to Gimbert and Horn’s algorithm (GHA).
When
\displaystyle A\subseteq R
in Opt-GSIA, we obtain a generalisation of GHA,
which can be precisely compared to IJMA as shown in the next theorem.

Theorem 31
.

Opt-GSIA, with
\displaystyle A\subseteq R
needs less iterations than IJMA to find the optimal values on any input.

Proof.

We denote by
\displaystyle\sigma_{i}
the strategy obtained after
\displaystyle i
steps of an instance of Opt-GSIA, where
\displaystyle A\subseteq R
.
We prove by induction on
\displaystyle i
that
\displaystyle v_{\sigma_{i}}\geq v^{\text{IJMA}}_{i}
(on random vertices).
In IJMA, the value vector is initialised to
\displaystyle 0
at the first step, hence any choice of initial strategy for Opt-GSIA guarantees a larger value on random vertices and satisfies the induction hypothesis.

Now assume that
\displaystyle v_{\sigma_{i}}\geq v^{\text{IJMA}}_{i}
for some
\displaystyle i
.
First, we have

v_{\sigma_{i+1}}=v^{G[A,\sigma_{i+1}]}_{\sigma_{i+1}}

(1)

by Lemma
12
and

v^{G[A,\sigma_{i+1}]}_{\sigma_{i+1}}\geq v^{G[A,\sigma_{i}]}_{\sigma_{i+1}}

(2)

since
\displaystyle\sigma_{i+1}<\sigma_{i}
by Proposition
18
. Now

\displaystyle v^{G[A,\sigma_{i}]}_{\sigma_{i+1}}
is, by definition of Opt-GSIA, an optimal value vector of
\displaystyle G[A,\sigma_{i}]
, but
optimal values are larger in
\displaystyle G[A,\sigma_{i}]
than in
\displaystyle G[R,\sigma_{i}]

since
\displaystyle A\subseteq R
, and on the other hand, optimal values of
\displaystyle G[R,\sigma_{i}]
are larger than those of

\displaystyle G[R,v^{\text{IJMA}}_{i}]
, using Lemma
17
and the induction hypothesis,
the latter being
\displaystyle v^{\prime\text{IJMA}}
by definition of IJMA. Putting these together,
we have proved that

v_{\sigma_{i+1}}\geq v^{\prime}_{\text{IJMA}}.

(3)

Consider now a random vertex
\displaystyle x
. Considering
the optimality conditions of Lemma
8

for
\displaystyle v_{\sigma_{i+1}}
and the definition of
\displaystyle v^{\text{IJMA}}_{i+1}(x)
,
we see that

\displaystyle\displaystyle v_{\sigma_{i+1}}(x)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{y\in N^{+}(x)}p_{x}(y)v_{\sigma_{i+1}}(y)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\geq\sum\limits_{y\in N^{+}(x)}p_{x}(y)v^{\prime\text{IJMA}}_{i+1}(y)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=v^{\text{IJMA}}_{i+1}

This concludes the induction and the result follows.
∎

We have proved that on every game, instances of Opt-GSIA with
\displaystyle A\subseteq R
make less iteration than IJMA; and it can need dramatically less of them. Indeed, the analysis of IJMA
[
17
]
relies on finding an extremal input for the algorithm, which happens to have no
max
nor
min
vertices. This extremal input is solved in one iteration of Opt-GSIA with the help of the ”best response” step, and Opt-GSIA is then exponentially faster. We have yet no result to quantify how faster Opt-GSIA is in the general case, but we suspect the number of iterations of Opt-GSIA to be much smaller in many cases.

While the number of iterations of Opt-GSIA is better than the number of iterations of IJMA, one should take into account the complexity of a single iteration.
In general, there is no algorithm for solving an iteration of Opt-GSIA
in polynomial time, since when

\displaystyle A=\emptyset
it is equivalent to solving any SSG; but
let us consider a mild condition ensuring that it will be the case.
Suppose that
\displaystyle A
contains at least one arc with transition
probability at least
\displaystyle 1/n
out of every random vertex of

\displaystyle G
. This is not a very restrictive property, since there is a least one such arc for each random vertex, and thus at least
\displaystyle 2^{r}
sets
\displaystyle A
have this property. For
\displaystyle q
-SSGs, with
\displaystyle q
fixed, the condition can be simplified into saying that
\displaystyle A
contains at least an arc out of each random vertex.
In this case, in the subgame
\displaystyle G[A,\sigma]
, there is a probability of stopping on a sink of a least
\displaystyle 1/n
when going through a random vertex. Hence, for this game, the value iteration algorithm
(as presented in
[
17
]
), converges in polynomial time in
\displaystyle n
to a value vector which is close enough to the optimal value vector so that we can recover it.
Then, at the end of an iteration of Opt-GSIA, a best response must be computed, in time
\displaystyle O(rn^{\omega})
. This should be compared to IJMA, whose iterations only requires an almost linear time.

To remedy this, we propose an hybrid version between Opt-GSIA and IJMA that
combines the good properties of both algorithms:
do the same value iteration algorithm as IJMA, but once every

\displaystyle rn^{\omega-1}
iterations, compute a best response, in time
\displaystyle O\left(rn^{\omega-1}\right)
, to update the values as in Opt-GSIA rather than doing a value propagation. This hybrid version enjoys the same complexity as IJMA since the overhead from the best response computation is in constant time per iteration. Moreover, the proof of Th.
31
shows that it needs less iterations than
IJMA, and exponentially so for the extremal input which is a cycle of random vertices.

6
Algorithms Derived from GSIA

We show that all known strategy improvement algorithms can be expressed as instances of GSIA and we also propose several new algorithms, derived from choices of
\displaystyle A
which make the transformed game polynomial time solvable.
The only algorithms which are not instances of GSIA are based on values rather than strategies:
value propagation
[
9
,
13
,
17
]
, quadratic programming
[
13
,
20
]
and dichotomy
[
3
]
.

6.1
Hoffman-Karp Algorithms

The most classical method to solve an SSG, called the Hoffman-Karp algorithm, repeatedly applies switches to the strategy until finding the optimal one. It is also a generic algorithm, since the choice of the set of vertices to switch at each step is not specified nor the choice of the initial strategy. Many details on these algorithms can be found in
[
13
]
or
[
24
]
.

Hoffman-Karp algorithms are instances of GSIA, where
\displaystyle A
is the set of all arcs of the SSG. Indeed, as proved in Lemma
28
, a switch
\displaystyle\sigma^{\prime}
of
\displaystyle\sigma
satisfies
\displaystyle\sigma\underset{G[A,\sigma]}{\succ}\sigma^{\prime}
. Interpreting Hoffman-Karp algorithms as instances of GSIA proves that they work on non-stopping games, while in most article the stopping condition is required. Moreover, it shows that their number of iterations is
\displaystyle O(nq^{r})
on
\displaystyle q
-SSGs, a complexity exponential in
\displaystyle r
only, which was known only for algorithms specially designed for this purpose
[
16
,
14
,
17
,
4
]
.

Ludwig’s Algorithm
[
21
]
, which is the best randomised algorithm to solve SSGs, can be seen as an Hoffman-Karp algorithm using Bland’s rule as shown in
[
4
]
: a random order on the vertices is drawn, and at each step, the first switchable vertex in the order is switched. Two other Hoffman-Karp algorithms are presented in
[
24
]
: switching all switchable vertices at each step or switching a random subset. Seeing these three algorithms as instances of GSIA yields
\displaystyle O(nq^{r})
as a deterministic bound on their number of iterations, which was unknown. However, the analysis of
[
21
,
24
]
is required to obtain a good complexity in
\displaystyle n
for these algorithms.

6.2
Selection of the Initial Strategy

In
[
14
]
, Dai and Ge give a randomised improvement of GHA simply by choosing a better initial strategy. To do so, they choose randomly
\displaystyle\sqrt{r!}\log(r!)
strategies and choose the one with the highest value. This ensures, with high probability, that at most
\displaystyle\sqrt{r!}
iterations will be done in GHA. Thus, their algorithm runs in
\displaystyle O(\left(\sqrt{r!}\right)
iterations. This algorithm is also captured by GSIA by selecting the initial strategy in the same way, however it seems hard to combine the gain made by the random selection of the strategy and the bound in
\displaystyle O(q^{r})
of GSIA, since even a strategy close to the optimal one may have values far from it. Remark that it is trivial to extend this method to any instance of Opt-GSIA to improve on the complexity of Th.
30
.

6.3
New Algorithms

We can use GSIA to design many strategy improvement algorithms. We present three of them, all based on a choice of
\displaystyle A
which makes
\displaystyle G[A,\sigma]
solvable in polynomial time. The initial strategy can be anything
and
\displaystyle\sigma^{\prime}
is always chosen to be the optimal strategy in
\displaystyle G[A,\sigma]
. Most of them can be seen as generalisations of known algorithms.

1.

Let
\displaystyle A
be a feedback arc set of
\displaystyle G
, then
\displaystyle G[A,\sigma]
is acyclic and it can be solved in linear time. It seems intuitively appealing to think that this algorithm will be faster if the feedback arc set
is small but we have no proof to sustain such a proposition.

2.

A
max
acyclic SSG is an SSG such that that every
max
vertex has at most one outgoing arc in a cycle.
max
acyclic SSG can be solved in polynomial time, see
[
3
]
. If we let
\displaystyle A
be a set of arc that contains all but one outgoing arcs of each
max
vertex, then
\displaystyle G[A,\sigma]
is
max
acyclic and can be solved in polynomial time. Moreover, such a game can be solved by strategy improvement in at most
\displaystyle n
iterations. This can be seen as a generalisation of Hoffman-Karp algorithm, in which
\displaystyle A
contains
all
outgoing arcs of
max
vertices.

3.

As an intermediate between acyclic games and
max
acyclic games, we may consider almost acyclic games, where all vertices have at most one outgoing arc in a cycle. Almost acyclic SSGs can be solved in linear time
[
3
]
.
