Cross-constraint basis theorems and products of partitions
Julien Cervelle, William Gaudelier, Ludovic Levy Patey

Abstract
We both survey and extend a new technique from Lu Liu to prove separation theorems between products of Ramsey-type theorems over computable reducibility. We use this technique to show that Ramsey’s theorem for n n -tuples and three colors is not computably reducible to finite products of Ramsey’s theorem for n n -tuples and two colors.



1
Introduction

In this article, we consider mathematical theorems as problems
\mathsf{P}
, formulated in terms of
instances
and
solutions
.
For example, König’s lemma states that every infinite, finitely branching tree admits an infinite path. Here, an instance of
\mathsf{KL}
is an infinite, finitely branching tree
T\subseteq\mathbb{N}^{<\mathbb{N}}
, and a solution to
T
is an infinite path
P\in[T]
.

There are many ways to compare the strength of mathematical problems. The most well-known approach is proof-theoretic, using
reverse mathematics
.
It uses subsystems of second-order arithmetic, with a base theory,
\mathsf{RCA}_{0}
, capturing
computable mathematics
. Then, if
\mathsf{RCA}_{0}\vdash\mathsf{Q}\rightarrow\mathsf{P}
, then
\mathsf{Q}
is at least as strong as
\mathsf{P}
, in the sense that one can solve
\mathsf{P}
using multiple applications of
\mathsf{Q}
as a black box, with only computable manipulations. There exist other approaches, more computability-theoretic, such as the
Weihrauch reduction
and
computable reduction
. A problem
\mathsf{P}
is
computably reducible
to
\mathsf{Q}
(written
\mathsf{P}\leq_{c}\mathsf{Q}
) if for every
\mathsf{P}
-instance
X
, there exists an
X
-computable
\mathsf{Q}
-instance
\widehat{X}
such that, for every
\mathsf{Q}
-solution
\widehat{Y}
of
\widehat{X}
,
X\oplus\widehat{Y}
computes a
\mathsf{P}
-solution to
X
. Weihrauch reduction is a uniform variant of computable reduction.

The proof-theoretic and computability-theoretic approaches are related, in that when restricting reverse mathematics to
\omega
-models
, that is, models whose first-order part consists of the standard integers together with the usual operations, then an implication over
\mathsf{RCA}_{0}
is a generalization of computable reduction, in which multiple successive applications are allowed. There are similar links between intuitionistic reverse mathematics and the Weihrauch reduction. From this viewpoint, computable reduction is “ressource-sensitive”, in that only one application of
\mathsf{Q}
is allowed to solve
\mathsf{P}
. Each approach has its own interest, as it reveals a different aspect of the relation between
\mathsf{P}
and
\mathsf{Q}
.

1.1
Ramsey’s theorem

In this article, we consider one particular family of problems, based on
Ramsey’s theorem
. Given a set
X\subseteq\mathbb{N}
, we write
[X]^{n}
for the set of all
n
-element subsets of
X
. Given a coloring
f:[\mathbb{N}]^{n}\to k
, a set
H\subseteq\mathbb{N}
is
f
-homogeneous
if
f
is constant on
[H]^{n}
. Ramsey’s theorem for
n
-tuples and
k
-colors (
\mathsf{RT}^{n}_{k}
) states the existence, for every coloring
f:[\mathbb{N}]^{n}\to k
, of an infinite
f
-homogeneous set. The statement
\mathsf{RT}^{n}_{k}
has two parameters.

The computational power of
\mathsf{RT}^{n}_{k}
depending on
n
is well-understood for computable instances. For
n=1
, there is always a computable solution. For
n\geq 2
, Jockusch
[
17
, Theorem 5.1]
proved the existence of a computable instance of
\mathsf{RT}^{n}_{2}
with no
\Sigma^{0}_{n}
solution. He also showed
[
17
, Theorem 5.5]
that every computable instance of
\mathsf{RT}^{n}_{k}
admits a
\Pi^{0}_{n}
solution. Cholak, Jockusch, and Slaman
[
5
, Theorem 12.1]
proved that for every computable instance of
\mathsf{RT}^{n}_{k}
, every PA degree over
\emptyset^{(n-2)}
computes the jump of a solution. Seetapun
[
27
, Theorem 2.1]
proved that for every computable instance of
\mathsf{RT}^{n}_{k}
and for every non
\Delta^{0}_{n-1}
set
C
, there exists a solution that does not compute
C
. Liu
[
21
, Theorem 1.5]
proved that every computable instance of
\mathsf{RT}^{2}_{k}
admits a solution of non-PA degree. On the other hand, for
n\geq 3
, Hirschfeldt and Jockusch
[
15
, Corollary 2.2]
proved the existence of a computable instance of
\mathsf{RT}^{n}_{2}
such that every solution is of PA degree over
\emptyset^{(n-2)}
.

Translated in terms of reverse mathematics,
\mathsf{RT}^{1}_{2}
is provable over
\mathsf{RCA}_{0}
,
\mathsf{RT}^{2}_{2}
is strictly in between
\mathsf{RCA}_{0}
and
\mathsf{ACA}_{0}
, and incomparable with
\mathsf{WKL}_{0}
, and
\mathsf{RT}^{n}_{2}
is equivalent to
\mathsf{ACA}_{0}
over
\mathsf{RCA}_{0}
for every
n\geq 3
. See Simpson
[
28
]
for a presentation of
\mathsf{RCA}_{0}
,
\mathsf{WKL}_{0}
and
\mathsf{ACA}_{0}
. The number
k
of colors is not relevant when it is standard, using a color-blindness argument. Indeed, given an instance of
\mathsf{RT}^{n}_{k^{2}}
, one can create an instance of
\mathsf{RT}^{n}_{k}
by grouping the colors into blocks of size
k
, and, given a solution, define another instance of
\mathsf{RT}^{n}_{k}
by working on the domain of the first solution. For this reason, the power of
\mathsf{RT}^{n}_{k}
depending on
k
was not studied until recently.

The color blindness argument shows that
\mathsf{RCA}_{0}\vdash\mathsf{RT}^{n}_{k}\rightarrow\mathsf{RT}^{n}_{k^{2}}
by two successive applications of
\mathsf{RT}^{n}_{k}
. This kind of argument does not hold for a resource-sensitive notion such as computable reduction. Patey
[
24
, Corollary 3.14]
proved that for every
n\geq 2
and every
k
,
\mathsf{RT}^{n}_{k+1}\not\leq_{c}\mathsf{RT}^{n}_{k}
.

1.2
Products of problems

There exist various operators on mathematical problems, coming essentially from the study of Weihrauch degrees. Among these, we shall consider two operators:

•

The
star
of a problem
\mathsf{P}
is the problem
\mathsf{P}^{*}
whose instances are finite tuples
(X_{0},\dots,X_{n-1})
of instances of
\mathsf{P}
for some
n\in\mathbb{N}
and whose solutions are finite tuples
(Y_{0},\dots,Y_{n-1})
such that
Y_{i}
is a
\mathsf{P}
-solution to
X_{i}
for every
i<n
.

•

The
parallelization
of a problem
\mathsf{P}
is the problem
\widehat{\mathsf{P}}
whose instances are infinite sequences of
\mathsf{P}
-instances
X_{0},X_{1},\dots
and whose solutions are infinite sequences
Y_{0},Y_{1},\dots
such that for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
Y_{n}
is a
\mathsf{P}
-solution to
X_{n}
.

When considering a reduction
\mathsf{P}\leq_{c}\mathsf{Q}^{*}
, one is allowed to use an arbitrarily large, but finite number
n
of instances of
\mathsf{Q}
to solve an instance
X
of
\mathsf{P}
, where
n
depends on
X
. However, the instances of
\mathsf{Q}
must be simultaneously chosen, in that they are not allowed to depend on each others’ solutions. This simultaneity prevents from using the standard color blindness argument, and motivates the following question:

Question 1.1
.

Given
n,k\geq 1
, is
\mathsf{RT}^{n}_{k+1}\leq_{c}(\mathsf{RT}^{n}_{k})^{*}
?

The case
n=1
is not interesting since
\mathsf{RT}^{n}_{k}
is computably true, but was studied in the context of Weihrauch degrees by Dorais et al
[
8
]
, Hirschfeldt and Jockusch
[
15
]
and Dzhafarov and al
[
10
]
. The first interesting case for computable reduction is then
n=2
. Let us first illustrate why the technique used to prove that
\mathsf{RT}^{2}_{k+1}\not\leq_{c}\mathsf{RT}^{2}_{k}
fails when considering products. Patey
[
24
]
used an analysis based on preservation of hyperimmunities. A function
g:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}
is
hyperimmune
if it is not dominated by any computable function. An infinite set
A
is hyperimmune if its principal function is hyperimmune, where the
principal function

p_{A}
of a set
A\coloneqq\{x_{0}<x_{1}<\ldots\}
is the function
n\mapsto x_{n}
. Patey proved
[
24
, Theorem 3.11]
that for every
k\geq 1
, every
(k+1)
-tuple of hyperimmune functions
g_{0},\dots,g_{k}
, and every computable instance
X
of
\mathsf{RT}^{n}_{k}
, there exists a solution
Y
such that at least two among the hyperimmune functions remain
Y
-hyperimmune, but that it is not the case for computable instances of
\mathsf{RT}^{n}_{k+1}
. This property fails when considering the star operator, as the following lemma shows:

Lemma 1.2

(Cholak et al
[
4
]
)
.

There exist 4 hyperimmune functions
g_{0},\dots,g_{3}
and two computable colorings
f_{0},f_{1}:[\mathbb{N}]^{2}\to 2
such that for every infinite
f_{0}
-homogeneous set
H_{0}
and every infinite
f_{1}
-homogeneous set
H_{1}
, at most one
g_{i}
is
H_{0}\oplus H_{1}
-homogeneous.

Proof.

Indeed, consider a
\Delta^{0}_{2}
4-partition
A_{0}\sqcup\dots\sqcup A_{3}=\mathbb{N}
such that for every
i<4
,
\overline{A}_{i}
is hyperimmune.
For every
i<4
,
g_{i}
is the principal function of
\overline{A}_{i}
.
Let
f_{0},f_{1}
be computable instances of
\mathsf{RT}^{2}_{2}
such that for every
x
,
\lim_{y}f_{0}(x,y)=1
iff
x\in A_{0}\cup A_{1}
and
\lim_{y}f_{1}(x,y)=1
iff
x\in A_{0}\cup A_{2}
. Every infinite
f_{0}
-homogeneous set
H_{0}
is either included in
A_{0}\cup A_{1}
or in
A_{2}\cup A_{3}
, and every infinite
f_{1}
-homogeneous set
H_{1}
is either included in
A_{0}\cup A_{2}
or in
A_{1}\cup A_{3}
.

Note that if a set
H\subseteq A_{i}\cup A_{j}
for some
i<j<4
, then
p_{H}
dominates each
g_{k}
for
k\in\{0,1,2,3\}-\{i,j\}
,
in which case none of those
g_{k}
are
H
-hyperimmune. Thus, either
g_{0}
and
g_{1}
or
g_{2}
and
g_{3}
are not
H_{0}
-hyperimmune, and either
g_{0}
and
g_{2}
, or
g_{1}
and
g_{3}
are not
H_{1}
-hyperimmune. It follows that at most one of the
g_{i}
is
H_{0}\oplus H_{1}
-hyperimmune.
∎

The instances of
\mathsf{RT}^{2}_{2}
created in
Lemma

1.2
are called
stable
, that is, for every
x
,
\lim_{y}f(x,y)
exists. Let
\mathsf{SRT}^{2}_{k}
denote the restriction of
\mathsf{RT}^{2}_{k}
to stable instances.
Liu
[
22
]
proved that
\mathsf{SRT}^{2}_{3}\not\leq_{c}(\mathsf{SRT}^{2}_{2})^{*}
, answering a question by Cholak et al
[
4
]
.
His proof involved completely new combinatorics, which will be presented in this article. We will also extend his result to give a complete answer to
Question

1.1
and prove that
\mathsf{RT}^{n}_{3}\not\leq_{c}(\mathsf{RT}^{n}_{2})^{*}
for every
n\geq 2
.
Before presenting Liu’s approach, note that the reduction holds when considering parallelization, but for a completely different reason.

Lemma 1.3
.

For every
n,k\geq 1
,
\mathsf{RT}^{n+1}_{k}\leq_{c}\widehat{\mathsf{RT}^{n}_{2}}
.

Proof.

By Brattka and Rakotoniaina
[
3
, Corollary 3.30]
,
\mathsf{WKL}^{(n)}\leq_{c}\widehat{\mathsf{RT}^{n}_{2}}
, where
\mathsf{WKL}^{(n)}
is the problem whose instances are
\Delta^{0}_{n+1}
approximations of infinite binary trees, and whose solutions are infinite paths through the trees. It follows that for every set
X
, there is an
X
-computable instance of
\widehat{\mathsf{RT}^{n}_{2}}
such that every solution is of PA degree over
X^{(n)}
. By Cholak, Jockusch and Slaman
[
5
]
, for every instance
X
of
\mathsf{RT}^{n+1}_{k}
, every PA degree over
X^{(n)}
computes the jump of a solution to
X
.
∎

1.3
Definitions and notation

We assume familiarity with computability theory (see Soare
[
29
]
) and reverse mathematics (see any of Simpson
[
28
]
, Hirschfeldt
[
13
]
or Dzhafarov and Mummert
[
12
]
).

We identify an integer
k\in\mathbb{N}
with the set
\{0,\dots,k-1\}
. The set
[X]^{n}
of
n
-element subsets of
X
is in one-to-one correspondence with the set of increasing ordered
n
-tuples over
X
.
Thus, we shall write for example
f:[\mathbb{N}]^{n}\to k
for
f:[\mathbb{N}]^{n}\to\{0,\dots,k-1\}
, and
f(x_{0},\dots,x_{n-1})
for
f(\{x_{0},\dots,x_{n-1}\})
, assuming
x_{0}<\dots<x_{n-1}
.

Given
k\in\mathbb{N}
, let
k{N}
be the set of all infinite
k
-valued sequences, and
k^{<\mathbb{N}}
be the set of all finite
k
-valued strings.
We use Greek letters
\sigma,\tau,\mu,\rho,\dots
to denote finitary strings, capital Latin letters
X,Y,Z
to denote finite or infinite sets of integers, or infinite
k
-valued sequences. Given
k,n\in\mathbb{N}
,
k^{=n}
denotes the set of
k
-valued strings of length exactly
n
. We define
k^{<n}
and
k^{\leq n}
accordingly.

Given two strings
\sigma,\tau
, we let
|\sigma|
denote the length of
\sigma
, and
\sigma\preccurlyeq\tau
means that
\sigma
is a (non-strict) prefix of
\tau
. We also write
\sigma\prec X
to mean that
\sigma
is an initial segment of
X
. We let
[\sigma]\coloneqq\{X\in k{N}:\sigma\prec X\}
. The choice of
k
will be clear from the context. The letter
\varepsilon
denotes the empty string.

In the whole document, we fix
r\in\mathbb{N}
, and use the following notations. For
N\in\mathbb{N}
and
u\in\{\mathbb{N},{<}\mathbb{N},{\leqslant}N,{=}N\}
, we let
\mathcal{X}_{u}(0)\coloneqq 3^{u}
,
\mathcal{X}_{u}(1)\coloneqq(2^{u})^{r}
and
\mathcal{X}_{u}\coloneqq\mathcal{X}_{u}(0)\times\mathcal{X}_{u}(1)
. For simplicity, when
u
is omitted it means
u\coloneqq\mathbb{N}
, i.e.
\mathcal{X}\coloneqq 3{N}\times(2{N})^{r}
.

Then, in

\Sigma^{0}_{1}

2
Core ideas

In this section, we give the general picture of a proof for separating a theorem from another over computable reducibility.
Then, we specialize the idea to the particular question of
\mathsf{RT}^{2}_{k+1}\leq_{c}(\mathsf{RT}^{2}_{k})^{*}
and explain the core ideas of Liu’s technique. Note that the terminology has been freely altered from Liu’s original article.

2.1
Separating theorems

Given two problems
\mathsf{P}
and
\mathsf{Q}
, in order to prove that
\mathsf{P}\not\leq_{c}\mathsf{Q}
, one needs to construct an instance
X_{\mathsf{P}}
of
\mathsf{P}
whose solutions are difficult to compute, and, for every
X_{\mathsf{P}}
-computable instance
X_{\mathsf{Q}}
of
\mathsf{Q}
, a solution
Y_{\mathsf{Q}}
to
X_{\mathsf{Q}}
that does not
X_{\mathsf{P}}
-compute any solution to
X_{\mathsf{P}}
. The framework was used in its whole generality by Lerman, Solomon, and Towsner
[
20
]
, but all the currently known separations over reverse mathematics or over computable reduction can be done by constructing a computable instance of
\mathsf{P}
.

The construction of the instance of
\mathsf{P}
can be done either by a priority construction or by an effectivization of a forcing construction. In many cases, it is constructed using the finite extension method, that is, an effectivization of Cohen forcing. This will be the case in our article (see
Proposition

5.13
).

Given a computable instance
X_{\mathsf{Q}}
of
\mathsf{Q}
, the solution
Y_{\mathsf{Q}}
is usually built by forcing, in a forcing notion
(\mathbb{P}_{X_{\mathsf{Q}}},\leq)
.
The solution has to satisfy two types of properties:

•

Structural properties
: being a solution to
X_{\mathsf{Q}}
. These properties are generally ensured by the very definition of the notion of forcing.

•

Computational properties
: not computing a solution to
X_{\mathsf{P}}
. These properties are divided into countably many requirements, by considering each Turing functional individually. Given a requirement
\mathcal{R}_{e}
, one must prove that the set of conditions forcing
\mathcal{R}_{e}
is dense.

There is often a tension between the structural properties which provide some computational strength, and the computational properties which require some weakness. There is however some degree of freedom in the computational properties, as they are parameterized by the instance
X_{\mathsf{P}}
of
\mathsf{P}
on which we have the hand.

The idea, coming from Lerman, Solomon and Towsner
[
20
]
, consists in building the instance
X_{\mathsf{P}}
considering each tuple
(X_{\mathsf{Q}},c,\mathcal{R}_{e})
at a time, where
X_{\mathsf{Q}}
is a computable instance of
\mathsf{Q}
,
c
is a forcing condition in
(\mathbb{P}_{X_{\mathsf{Q}}},\leq)
, and
\mathcal{R}_{e}
is a requirement. Given a partial approximation of
X_{\mathsf{P}}
and a tuple
(X_{\mathsf{Q}},c,\mathcal{R}_{e})
, ask whether there is an extension
d\leq c
forcing
\Phi_{e}^{Y_{\mathsf{Q}}}
to output enough bits of information. If so, complete
X_{\mathsf{P}}
so that it diagonalizes against the functional. Otherwise, there is an extension
d\leq c
forcing
\Phi_{e}^{Y_{\mathsf{Q}}}
to be partial. The counter-intuitive part of this approach is that the satisfaction of the requirements is ensured by the construction of
X_{\mathsf{P}}
instead of the construction of the solutions
Y_{\mathsf{Q}}
to computable instances of
\mathsf{Q}
.

As explained by Patey
[
25
]
, one can polish the previous construction, by abstracting the construction steps of
X_{\mathsf{Q}}
to consider every operation with the same definitional properties, yielding some kind of genericity property. For example, the separation of the Erdős-Moser theorem and the Ascending Descending Sequence from Ramsey’s theorem for pairs
[
20
]
were polished in
[
25
]
and
[
26
]
to obtain hyperimmunity and dependent hyperimmunity, respectively. In this article, the polishing step yields
\Gamma
-hyperimmunity (see
Definition

5.11
).

2.2
Separating Ramsey-type theorems

In the particular case of Ramsey-type theorems, there exists a well-established sub-scheme of construction. Many Ramsey-type theorems are of the form “For every
k
-coloring of the
n
-tuples of an infinite structure, there exists an infinite isomorphic substructure over which all the
n
-tuples satisfy some properties”. In the case of Ramsey’s theorem, the infinite structure is
(\mathbb{N},<)
, and the property is homogeneity, but one can consider weaker properties, such as transitivity, in which case one obtains the Erdős-Moser theorem. One can also consider tree structures, yielding the tree theorem
[
6
]
or Milliken’s tree theorem
[
1
]
. These theorems are usually proven by induction, by constructing so-called pre-homogeneous substructures. In the case of Ramsey’s theorem, an infinite set
X
is
pre-homogeneous
for a coloring
f:[\mathbb{N}]^{n+1}\to k
if for every
\vec{x}\in[X]^{n}
and every larger
y_{0},y_{1}\in X
,
f(\vec{x},y_{0})=f(\vec{x},y_{1})
.

Although pre-homogeneity is the natural notion to consider from a combinatorial viewpoint, the computability-theoretic practice has shown the interest of a weaker notion of “delayed pre-homogeneity” called cohesiveness. Each Ramsey-type theorem has its own notion of cohesiveness. In the case of Ramsey’s theorem, this yields the following definition.

Definition 2.1
.

An infinite set
C
is
cohesive
for an infinite sequence of sets
R_{0},R_{1},\dots
if for every
n
,
C\subseteq^{*}R_{n}
or
C\subseteq^{*}\overline{R}_{n}
, where
\subseteq^{*}
means “included up to finite changes”.
\mathsf{COH}
is the statement “Every infinite sequence of sets admits an infinite cohesive set.”

Given a coloring
f:[\mathbb{N}]^{n+1}\to k
, one can consider the sequence of sets
\vec{R}\coloneqq\langle R_{\vec{x},z}:\vec{x}\in[\mathbb{N}]^{n},z<k\rangle
defined by
R_{\vec{x},z}\coloneqq\{z\in\mathbb{N}:f(\vec{x},z)=y\}
. Given an infinite
\vec{R}
-cohesive set
C
, the coloring
f
restricted to
[C]^{n+1}
is
stable
, that is, for every
\vec{x}\in[\mathbb{N}]^{n}
,
\lim_{y\in C}f(\vec{x},y)
exists. This induces a
\Delta^{0}_{2}(C)
coloring
\widehat{f}:[C]^{n}\to k
. Cohesiveness is therefore a bridge between computable instances of
\mathsf{RT}^{n+1}_{k}
and
\Delta^{0}_{2}
instances of
\mathsf{RT}^{n}_{k}
.

Cohesiveness has almost no computational power. Indeed, by delaying pre-homogeneity, the statement becomes about the jump of sets. More precisely,
\mathsf{COH}
is computably equivalent to the statement “For every
\Delta^{0}_{2}
infinite binary tree, there exists a
\Delta^{0}_{2}
path” (see Belanger
[
2
]
). Most of the properties used to separate Ramsey-type statements are preserved by
\mathsf{COH}
. This phenomenon can be explained by the fact that every set can be made
\Delta^{0}_{2}
without affecting too much the ground model (see Towsner
[
30
]
). This will be again the case in our article (
Theorem

5.16
). Because of this, the question of the separation
\mathsf{RT}^{2}_{k+1}\not\leq_{c}(\mathsf{RT}^{2}_{k})^{*}
becomes a question about separating
\Delta^{0}_{2}
instances of
\mathsf{RT}^{1}_{k+1}
from finite products of
\Delta^{0}_{2}
instances of
\mathsf{RT}^{1}_{k}
.

More generally, given two Ramsey-type statements
\mathsf{P}^{n}
,
\mathsf{Q}^{n}
parameterized by the dimension of the
n
-tuples, the question of
\mathsf{P}^{n+1}\leq_{c}\mathsf{Q}^{n+1}
is often reduced to the corresponding question with
\Delta^{0}_{2}
instances of
\mathsf{P}^{n}
and
\mathsf{Q}^{n}
. The experience shows that almost all the known separations consist in actually constructing a
\Delta^{0}_{2}
instance of
\mathsf{P}^{n}
which defeats not only all the
\Delta^{0}_{2}
instances of
\mathsf{Q}^{n}
, but
all
the instances of
\mathsf{Q}^{n}
, with no effectiveness restriction (see
[
24
,
23
]
for examples). The previous remark about Towsner’s work shows that this apparently stronger diagonalization is often without loss of generality. This will be again the case in our article, and the
\Delta^{0}_{2}
instance of
\mathsf{RT}^{1}_{k+1}
will defeat all the finite products of instances of
\mathsf{RT}^{1}_{k}
(
Theorem

3.13
).

Building a single instance of
\mathsf{RT}^{1}_{k}
which defeats simultaneously uncountably many instances of
(\mathsf{RT}^{1}_{k+1})^{*}
raises new difficulties, as the sequence of all tuples
(X_{\mathsf{Q}},c,\mathcal{R}_{e})
is not countable anymore. Thankfully, we shall see that there exists a single countable notion of forcing
(\mathbb{P},\leq)
such that
\mathbb{P}_{X}\subseteq\mathbb{P}
for every
\mathsf{Q}
-instance
X
. Moreover, given a condition
c\in\mathbb{P}
, the class
I(c)
of all
\mathsf{Q}
-instances
X
such that
c\in\mathbb{P}_{X}
is a compact class. One will exploit this compactness to defeat all
\mathsf{Q}
-instances
X\in I(c)
simultaneously.

2.3
Cross-constraint techniques

The setting is therefore the following: in order to prove that
\mathsf{P}^{n+1}\not\leq_{c}\mathsf{Q}^{n+1}
, one builds a
\Delta^{0}_{2}
instance
X
of
\mathsf{P}^{n}
such that for every instance
\widetilde{X}
of
\mathsf{Q}^{n}
, there is a
\mathsf{Q}^{n}
-solution
\widetilde{Y}
to
\widetilde{X}
that does not compute any
\mathsf{P}^{n}
-solution to
X
.

The instance
X
of
\mathsf{Q}^{n}
is built by an effectivization of Cohen forcing. For example, in this article, to prove that
\mathsf{RT}^{2}_{k+1}\not\leq_{c}(\mathsf{RT}^{2}_{k})^{*}
, we will build a
\Delta^{0}_{2}
instance
f
of
\mathsf{RT}^{1}_{k+1}
using an increasing sequence of
(k+1)
-valued strings
\sigma_{0}\prec\sigma_{1}\prec\dots
and let
f
be the limit of this sequence.

Let
(\mathbb{P},\leq)
be a countable notion of forcing used to build solutions to every instance of
\mathsf{Q}^{n}
. At stage
s
, assuming the Cohen condition
\sigma_{s}
has been defined, consider the next pair
(c,\mathcal{R}_{e})
where
c\in\mathbb{P}
and
\mathcal{R}_{e}
is a requirement saying that
\Phi_{e}^{G}
is not a solution to the
\mathsf{P}^{n}
-instance. Consider the class
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\operatorname{dom}\mathsf{P}^{n}\times I(c)
of all pairs
(X,\widetilde{X})
such that there is an extension
d\leq c
with
\widetilde{X}\in I(c)
forcing
\Phi_{e}^{G}
to be partial or a
\mathsf{P}^{n}
-solution to
X
. There are two cases:

•

Case 1: the class
\mathcal{C}
is
left-full
below
\sigma_{s}
, that is, for every instance
X
of
\mathsf{P}^{n}
extending
\sigma_{s}
, there exists a
\mathsf{Q}^{n}
-instance
\widetilde{X}
such that
(X,\widetilde{X})\in\mathcal{C}
. Then, by some appropriate basis theorem which depends on the combinatorics of
\mathsf{P}^{n}
and
\mathsf{Q}^{n}
, there exist multiple pairs
(X_{0},\widetilde{X}_{0}),\dots,(X_{k-1},\widetilde{X}_{k-1})
in
\mathcal{C}
such that
X_{0},\dots,X_{k-1}
are
incompatible
, in the sense that there is no set which is a solution to all these
\mathsf{P}^{n}
-instances simultaneously, while
\widetilde{X}_{0},\dots,\widetilde{X}_{k-1}
are compatible as
\mathsf{Q}^{n}
-instances. Then, by building a solution to the
\mathsf{Q}^{n}
-instance which will be simultaneously a solution to
\widetilde{X}_{0},\dots,\widetilde{X}_{k-1}
, this forces
\Phi_{e}^{G}
to be partial, hence to satisfy
\mathcal{R}_{e}
.

•

Case 2: the class
\mathcal{C}
is not left-full below
\sigma_{s}
. Then, there exists a
\mathsf{P}^{n}
-instance
X
extending
\sigma_{s}
such that, for every
\mathsf{Q}^{n}
-instance
\widetilde{X}\in I(c)
,
c
forces
\Phi_{e}^{G}
not to be a
\mathsf{P}^{n}
-solution to
X
. By compactness of
I(c)
, an initial segment
\sigma_{s+1}\prec X
is sufficient to witness this diagonalization, hence to satisfy
\mathcal{R}_{e}
.

The general idea of cross-constraint techniques takes its roots in Liu’s proof of separation of Ramsey’s theorem for pairs from weak König’s lemma
[
21
]
, in a slightly different setting. Indeed,
\mathsf{P}^{n+1}
was
\mathsf{WKL}
, which is known to admit a maximally difficult instance, so only
\mathsf{Q}^{n}
was built. In that article, he considered the class
\mathcal{C}
of all pairs
(f,\widetilde{X})
such that
f
is a partial function with finite support, and
\widetilde{X}\in I(c)
is an instance of
\mathsf{RT}^{1}_{2}
.

3
General framework

In this section, we define the fundamental notions of left-full cross-tree, and prove the main theorems parameterized by the cross-constraint ideals. The basis theorems proven in
Section

4
will show the existence of cross-constraint ideals with various computability-theoretic properties, and will be used to answer the main question in
Section

5.2
.

3.1
Cross-trees

When considering
\Pi^{0}_{1}
-classes for the space
\mathcal{X}
, it is natural to consider cross-trees which play a role analogous to binary trees in the case of the Cantor space.

Definition 3.1
.

We extend the
prefix relation
on strings
\preccurlyeq
to tuples of strings, in the natural way. More precisely, for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
, given integers
k_{0},\ldots,k_{n-1}
, and two tuples
\sigma\coloneqq(\sigma_{0},\ldots,\sigma_{n-1}),\tau\coloneqq(\tau_{0},\ldots,\tau_{n-1})\in\prod_{i<n}k_{i}^{<\mathbb{N}}
, we have

\sigma\preccurlyeq\tau
if and only if
\forall i<n,\sigma_{i}\preccurlyeq\tau_{i}
. For any
k\in\mathbb{N}
, the
empty string
of
k^{<\mathbb{N}}
is denoted by
\varepsilon
, and we abuse the notation to also denote any tuple
(\varepsilon,\ldots,\varepsilon)
.

Moreover, we define the set of its
infinite extensions
by
[\sigma]\coloneqq[\sigma_{0}]\times\ldots\times[\sigma_{n-1}]
, and its
length
by
|\sigma|\coloneqq\max\{|\sigma_{i}|:i<n\}
.

Definition 3.2
.

A class
\mathcal{P}\subseteq\mathcal{X}
is
\Pi^{0}_{1}
if there is a c.e. set
W\subseteq\mathcal{X}_{<\mathbb{N}}
such that

\overline{\mathcal{P}}=\bigcup_{\chi\in W}[\chi]

Definition 3.3
.

A
cross-tree
is a set
T\subseteq\mathcal{X}_{<\mathbb{N}}
which is downward-closed for the prefix relation
\preccurlyeq
, and such that
\forall(\rho,\sigma)\in T,\forall i{<}j{<}r,|\sigma_{i}|=|\sigma_{j}|\text{ and }|\sigma|\leqslant|\rho|
. The
height
of
T
is
h(T)\coloneqq\max\{|\chi|:\chi\in T\}

The class of its (infinite)
paths
is defined as

[T]\coloneqq\big\{(X,Y)\in\mathcal{X}:\forall n,(X\!{\upharpoonright}_{n},Y\!{\upharpoonright}_{n})\in T\big\}

Moreover, given a string
\rho\in\mathcal{X}_{<\mathbb{N}}(0)
, we define the tree
T[\rho]\coloneqq\{\sigma\in\mathcal{X}_{<\mathbb{N}}(1):(\rho,\sigma)\in T\}
, which is finite since
|\sigma|\leqslant|\rho|
. A cross-tree
T
is said to be
right-pruned
if
\forall\rho\in\mathcal{X}_{<\mathbb{N}}(0),T[\rho]
is pruned, i.e. all the leaves of
T[\rho]
have length
|\rho|
. Finally, for any
N\in\mathbb{N}
we define
T\!{\upharpoonright}_{N}\coloneqq\{\chi\in T:|\chi|\leqslant N\}
.

Lemma 3.4
.

A class
\mathcal{P}\subseteq\mathcal{X}
is
\Pi^{0}_{1}
if and only if there is a computable cross-tree
T\subseteq\mathcal{X}_{<\mathbb{N}}
such that
[T]=\mathcal{P}
.

Proof.

Let
T\subseteq\mathcal{X}_{<\mathbb{N}}
be a computable cross-tree. The set
[T]
is
\Pi^{0}_{1}
as its complement is the set
\bigcup_{\chi\in W}[\chi]
where
W\coloneqq\mathcal{X}_{<\mathbb{N}}\smallsetminus T
is computable.

Now let
\mathcal{P}
be a
\Pi^{0}_{1}
class whose complement is
\bigcup_{(\rho,\sigma)\in W}[\rho]\times[\sigma]
. Consider the cross-tree
T
such that

(\rho,\sigma)\in T\iff\forall\mu\preccurlyeq\rho,\forall\tau\preccurlyeq\sigma,(\mu,\tau)\notin W[|\rho|]\text{ and }|\rho|\geqslant|\sigma|
. It is computable and its paths are exactly the elements of
\mathcal{P}
.
∎

Remark 3.5
.

In the rest of the document, every notion or proposition related to a class
\mathcal{P}\subseteq\mathcal{X}
, also holds for a computable cross-tree
T\subseteq\mathcal{X}_{<\mathbb{N}}
instead, by considering its associated class
[T]
.

3.2
Left-fullness

The notion of left-fullness below is a sufficient notion of largeness such that any left-full
\Pi^{0}_{1}
-class contains multiple members satisfying some constraints. A key factor also lies in the fact that the complexity of this notion is only
\Pi^{0}_{1}
.

Definition 3.6
.

A class
\mathcal{P}\subseteq\mathcal{X}
is
left-full
below
(\rho,\sigma)\in\mathcal{X}_{<\mathbb{N}}
if

\forall X\in[\rho],\exists Y\in[\sigma],(X,Y)\in\mathcal{P}

Moreover, for any integer
N\in\mathbb{N}
, we say that a finite tree
T\subseteq\mathcal{X}_{\leqslant N}
is
left-full
below
(\rho,\sigma)\in T
if for every leaf
(\mu,\tau)\in T
,
|\mu|=N
, and for every
\mu\in\mathcal{X}_{N}(0)
extending
\rho
, there is some
\tau\in\mathcal{X}_{N}(1)
extending
\sigma
, such that
(\mu,\tau)\in T
.

We simply say “left-full” to signify “left-full below
(\varepsilon,\varepsilon)
”.

The above definition for finite trees is motivated by the following lemma. In particular it shows that
T
is left-full below
(\rho,\sigma)
if and only if for any
N\in\mathbb{N}
,
T\!{\upharpoonright}_{N}
is left-full below
(\rho,\sigma)
.

Lemma 3.7
.

Let
(\rho,\sigma)\in\mathcal{X}_{<\mathbb{N}}
, and
\mathcal{P}\subseteq\mathcal{X}
be a
\Pi^{0}_{1}
class, whose associated computable cross-tree is
T
.
The statement

\mathcal{P}\text{ is left-full below }(\rho,\sigma)

(a)

is equivalent to

\forall\mu\succcurlyeq\rho,\exists\tau\succcurlyeq\sigma,|\tau|=|\mu|\text{ and }(\mu,\tau)\in T

(b)

Moreover, if
T
is right-pruned, then the statement is equivalent to

\forall\mu\succcurlyeq\rho,(\mu,\sigma)\in T

(c)

Proof.

We first show (
a
)
\implies
(
b
). Let
\mu\succcurlyeq\rho
. Consider
X\in[\mu]\subseteq[\rho]
. By (
a
), there is
Y\in[\sigma]
such that
(X,Y)\in\mathcal{P}
. Thus, in particular, we have that
(\mu,Y\!{\upharpoonright}_{|\mu|})\in T
. Hence
\tau\coloneqq Y\!{\upharpoonright}_{|\mu|}
is the desired witness.

For the converse, let
X\in[\rho]
. We want to find
Y\in[\sigma]
such that
(X,Y)\in\mathcal{P}
. Consider the set
S\coloneqq\{\tau\succcurlyeq\sigma:(X\!{\upharpoonright}_{|\tau|},\tau)\in T\}
. Since
T
is a cross-tree,
S
is a finitely-branching cross-tree, with root
\sigma
. Moreover, it is infinite because of (
b
). Thus, by König’s lemma, there is
Y\in 2{N}
such that
\forall\ell,(X\!{\upharpoonright}_{\ell},Y\!{\upharpoonright}_{\ell})\in T
. Hence
(X,Y)\in\mathcal{P}
.

Last, we show (
c
)
\Leftrightarrow
(
b
). For all
\mu\succcurlyeq\rho
, if there is
\tau\succcurlyeq\sigma
such that
|\tau|=|\mu|\text{ and }(\mu,\tau)\in T
, then in particular
(\mu,\sigma)\in T
since
T
is a cross-tree.
As for the converse, consider
\mu\succcurlyeq\rho
. We have
(\mu,\sigma)\in T
, and since the cross-tree
\{\tau:(\mu,\tau)\in T\}
is pruned, it means there is
\tau\succcurlyeq\sigma
in it of size
|\mu|
and such that
(\mu,\tau)\in T
.
∎

The following lemma shows how left-fullness is preserved when extending or shortening the stems. Note that the first and second components do not play a symmetric role.

Lemma 3.8
.

Let
T
be a computable cross-tree left-full below
(\rho,\sigma)\in\mathcal{X}_{<\mathbb{N}}
. Then

(a)

\forall\widehat{\rho}\succcurlyeq\rho,\forall\widehat{\sigma}\preccurlyeq\sigma,T\text{ is left-full below }(\widehat{\rho},\widehat{\sigma})

(b)

For every
n\in\mathbb{N}
, there are some
\widehat{\rho}\succcurlyeq\rho
and
\widehat{\sigma}\succcurlyeq\sigma

such that
|\widehat{\sigma}|=n
and
T
is left-full below
(\widehat{\rho},\widehat{\sigma})

Proof.

Item
(a)
can be proven directly from the definition of left-full.
For Item
(b)
, we can suppose WLOG that
|\rho|\geq n
and
|\sigma|\leq n
, thanks to the previous item. Consider all the extensions of
\sigma
of length
n
, denoted
\sigma_{0},\ldots,\sigma_{k-1}
for some
k\in\mathbb{N}
, and define
\rho_{0}\coloneqq\rho
. If
T
is left-full below
(\rho_{0},\sigma_{0})
then the assertion is proven. Otherwise, by
Lemma

3.7
, it means
\exists\rho_{1}\succcurlyeq\rho_{0},\forall\tau\succcurlyeq\sigma_{0},|\tau|=|\rho_{1}|\implies(\rho_{1},\tau)\notin T
. Now we consider the pair
(\rho_{1},\sigma_{1})
to see if
T
is left-full below it. In case it is not, use
Lemma

3.7
as in the previous case. Proceed inductively like this for every
\sigma_{j}
, where
j<k
. If at some point
T
is left-full below
(\rho_{j},\sigma_{j})
then the assertion is proven. Otherwise, it means we have built a sequence of string
\rho_{0}\preccurlyeq\ldots\preccurlyeq\rho_{k}
such that

\forall j<k,\forall\tau\succcurlyeq\sigma_{j},|\tau|=|\rho_{j+1}|\implies(\rho_{j+1},\tau)\notin T
, and since
T
is a tree we even have
\forall j<k,\forall\tau\succcurlyeq\sigma_{j},|\tau|=|\rho_{k}|\implies(\rho_{k},\tau)\notin T
. But the latter statement contradicts the fact that
T
is left-full below
(\rho,\sigma)
, by considering the string
\rho_{k}
in
Lemma

3.7
.
∎

3.3
Parameterized theorems

Most of the computability-theoretic constructions of solutions to Ramsey-type theorems are done by variants of Mathias forcing, with reservoirs belonging to some Turing ideal containing only weak sets. The combinatorics of the statement usually require some closure properties on this ideal. For example, to construct solutions to computable instances of Ramsey’s theorem for pairs, or to arbitrary instances of the pigeonhole principle, one requires the ideal to be a Scott ideal, that is, a model of
\mathsf{WKL}
(see
[
11
,
27
]
). One must then prove some basis theorem for
\mathsf{WKL}
to construct Scott ideals with only weak sets.

In our case, we shall need another closure property, yielding the notion of cross-constraint ideal. The main constructions of this section will be parameterized by cross-constraint ideals, whose existence will be proven in
Section

4
.

Definition 3.9
.

Let
X
be an infinite set. A pair of instances
(f,g)
of
\mathsf{RT}^{1}_{k}
is
finitely compatible on
X
if for all color
i<k
the set
X\cap f^{-1}(i)\cap g^{-1}(i)
is finite. Whenever
X=\mathbb{N}
, we simply say that
(f,g)
is finitely compatible. Also, note that the negation of “finitely compatible” is “infinitely compatible”

Definition 3.10
.

The
cross-constraint problem
, denoted
\mathsf{CC}
, is the problem whose instances are left-full cross-trees
T\subseteq\mathcal{X}_{<\mathbb{N}}
, and a solution to
T
is a pair of paths
(X^{i},Y^{i})_{i<2}
such that

(X^{0},X^{1})
is finitely compatible, and for all
s<r
,
(Y^{0}_{s},Y^{1}_{s})
is infinitely compatible.

The following notion of cross-constraint ideal simply says that
\mathcal{M}
is an
\omega
-model of
\mathsf{CC}
, where an
\omega
-model is a structure of second-order arithmetic in which the first-order part consists of the standard integers. An
\omega
-model is then fully characterized by its second-order part, and is therefore often identified to it. The second-order part of an
\omega
-structure is a model of
\mathsf{RCA}_{0}
iff it is a Turing ideal.

Definition 3.11
.

A
cross-constraint ideal
is a Turing ideal
\mathcal{M}\subseteq\mathfrak{P}(\mathbb{N})
such that, any instance
T\in\mathcal{M}
of
\mathsf{CC}
has a solution
(X^{i},Y^{i})_{i<2}
such that
(X^{0},Y^{0})\oplus(X^{1},Y^{1})\in\mathcal{M}
.

Last, we define a notion of hyperimmunity for
k
-colorings
f
, which is an intermediate notion between Cohen genericity and hyperimmunity. It implies in particular that for every
j<k
,
\{x:f(x)\neq j\}
is hyperimmune, but in a dependent way.

Definition 3.12
.

An instance
f
of
\mathsf{RT}^{1}_{k}
is
hyperimmune
relative to
D\subseteq\mathbb{N}
if for every
D
-computable array of
k
-tuples of mutually disjoint finite sets
\{\vec{F_{n}}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
such that
\min\bigcup_{j<k}F_{n,j}>n
, there is
N\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\forall j<k,F_{N,j}\subseteq f^{-1}(j)

In other words, an instance
f
is hyperimmune relative to
D
if for every
D
-computable sequence
g_{0},g_{1},\dots
of partial
k
-valued functions with finite support, such that
\forall n\in\mathbb{N},\min\operatorname{dom}g_{n}>n
, then
f
is a completion of some
g_{n}
.
We are now ready to prove our first parameterized theorem.

Theorem 3.13

(Liu
[
22
, Theorem 2.1]
)
.

Let
\mathcal{M}
be a countable cross-constraint ideal and let
f\in\mathcal{X}(0)
be hyperimmune relative to any element of
\mathcal{M}
, then for any
g\in\mathcal{X}(1)
there is a solution
\vec{G}
of
g
which, for any
Z\in\mathcal{M}
, does not
Z
-compute any solution of
f
.

Proof.

The set
\vec{G}
is constructed by a variant of Mathias forcing, using conditions of the form
\big((\vec{F_{\alpha}})_{\alpha\in 2^{r}},\vec{A}\big)
where

•

\vec{F_{\alpha}}
is an
r
-tuple of finite sets
g
-homogeneous for the colors
\alpha
, i.e.
\forall s<r,F_{\alpha,s}\subseteq g_{s}^{-1}(\alpha(s))

•

\vec{A}
is an
r
-tuple of infinite sets in
\mathcal{M}
such that
\forall\alpha\in 2^{r},\forall s<r,\min A_{s}>\max F_{\alpha,s}

The idea is that we do not know in advance what the colors of homogeneity will be for the solution being constructed, so we build all the possibilities in parallel, with
\alpha
indicating the colors, i.e. for any
i<r
, the set
F_{\alpha,i}
is
g_{i}
-homogeneous for the color
\alpha(i)
.

A condition
\big((\vec{E_{\alpha}})_{\alpha\in 2^{r}},\vec{B}\big)

extends
another
\big((\vec{F_{\alpha}})_{\alpha\in 2^{r}},\vec{A}\big)
if, for every
\alpha\in 2^{r}
and every
s<r
, we have
E_{\alpha,s}\supseteq F_{\alpha,s}
,
B_{s}\subseteq A_{s}
, and
E_{\alpha,s}-F_{\alpha,s}\subseteq A_{s}
.

Every sufficiently generic filter
\mathcal{F}
for this notion of forcing induces a family of sets
(G^{\mathcal{F}}_{\alpha,s})_{\alpha\in 2^{r},s<r}
defined by

G^{\mathcal{F}}_{\alpha,s}=\bigcup\{F_{\alpha,s}:\big((\vec{F_{\alpha}})_{\alpha\in 2^{r}},\vec{A}\big)\in\mathcal{F}\}

Given an initial segment
F_{\alpha,s}
of a condition
\big((\vec{F_{\alpha}})_{\alpha\in 2^{r}},\vec{A}\big)
, it is not necessarily possible to find an extension
\big((\vec{E_{\alpha}})_{\alpha\in 2^{r}},\vec{B}\big)
with
|E_{\alpha,s}|>|F_{\alpha,s}|
,
since it might be the case that
A_{s}\cap g^{-1}_{s}(\alpha(s))
is empty. Thus, for any sufficiently generic filter
\mathcal{F}
, the set
G^{\mathcal{F}}_{\alpha,s}
might not be infinite. However, there must necessarily exist some
\alpha\in 2^{r}
such that every
G^{\mathcal{F}}_{\alpha,s}
,
s<r
is infinite.
Given a condition
c=\big((\vec{F_{\alpha}})_{\alpha\in 2^{r}},\vec{A}\big)
and a coloring
h\in\mathcal{X}(1)
, define the set

V_{h}(c)\coloneqq\{\alpha\in 2^{r}:\forall s<r,A_{s}\cap h^{-1}_{s}(\alpha(s))\neq\emptyset\}

of “valid” combinations.
Note that if
d\leqslant c
, then
V_{h}(d)\subseteq V_{h}(c)
. Moreover,
V_{g}(c)\neq\emptyset
for every condition
c
. A “valid” combination of a condition
c
allows us to find an extension, as the following lemma shows.

Lemma 3.14
.

For all conditions
c\coloneqq\big((\vec{F_{\alpha}})_{\alpha\in 2^{r}},\vec{A}\big)
, all
\beta\in V_{g}(c)
and all
s<r
, there is an extension
\big((\vec{E_{\alpha}})_{\alpha\in 2^{r}},\vec{B}\big)
such that
|E_{\beta,s}|>|F_{\beta,s}|
.

Proof.

Let
\beta\in V_{g}(c)
and
s<r
. By definition of
\beta
consider
n\in A_{s}\cap g^{-1}_{s}(\alpha(s))
. Define
(\vec{E_{\alpha}})_{\alpha\in 2^{r}}
to be equal to
(\vec{F_{\alpha}})_{\alpha\in 2^{r}}
, except for
E_{\beta,s}\coloneqq F_{\beta,s}\cup\{n\}
. Accordingly, define
\vec{B}
to be equal to
\vec{A}
, except for
B_{s}\coloneqq A_{s}-\{0,1,\dots,n\}
. Then
\big((\vec{E_{\alpha}})_{\alpha\in 2^{r}},\vec{B}\big)
is a condition which satisfies the lemma.
∎

Fix an enumeration of Turing functionals
(\Psi_{e})_{e\in\mathbb{N}}
. For any
e\in\mathbb{N}
,
j<3
and
Z\in\mathcal{M}
let

\mathcal{R}_{e,j}^{Z}\coloneqq\Psi_{e}^{Z\oplus\vec{G}}\text{ is not an infinite subset of }f^{-1}(j)

Lemma 3.15
.

For any
2^{r}
-tuple of integers
(e_{\alpha})_{\alpha\in 2^{r}}
, any
2^{r}
-tuple of colors
(j_{\alpha})_{\alpha\in 2^{r}}
, any
Z\in\mathcal{M}
, and any condition
c
, there is an extension forcing
\bigvee_{\alpha\in V_{g}(c)}\mathcal{R}_{e_{\alpha},j_{\alpha}}^{Z}
.

Proof.

Let
c\coloneqq\big((\vec{F_{\alpha}})_{\alpha\in 2^{r}},\vec{A}\big)
be a condition, and define
U(c)
to be the
\Pi^{0}_{1}(\vec{A})
-class whose elements are colorings
\widetilde{g}
such that

\forall s<r,\forall j<3,A_{s}\cap g_{s}^{-1}(j)=\emptyset\implies A_{s}\cap\widetilde{g_{s}}^{-1}(j)=\emptyset

in other words
U(c)\coloneqq\{\widetilde{g}:V_{\widetilde{g}}(c)=V_{g}(c)\}
. Note that it is non-empty since
g\in U(c)
.
This will ensure that the
\widetilde{g}
we consider later have the same behavior as
g
regarding
\vec{A}
.

Moreover, given
\alpha\in 2^{r}
, an
r
-tuple of finite sets
\vec{G}

satisfies

(\vec{F_{\alpha}},\vec{A})
if

4
Cross-constraint basis theorems

As mentioned before, the two main theorems of
Section

3
are parameterized by cross-constraint ideals, which are themselves built using iterated applications of the cross-constraint principle (
\mathsf{CC}
).
In this section, we prove various basis theorems for
\mathsf{CC}
, namely, the
\Delta^{0}_{2}
, low, cone avoidance, and non-
\Sigma^{0}_{1}
preservation basis theorems. The
\Delta^{0}_{2}
and cone avoidance basis theorems for
\mathsf{CC}
were previously proven by Liu
[
22
]
, but we give a new proof of the cone avoidance basis theorem which resembles more its classical counterpart for
\Pi^{0}_{1}
classes.

4.1
Conditions

The most famous basis theorems for
\Pi^{0}_{1}
classes are all proven using effective versions of forcing with binary trees. Similarly, all the basis theorems for
\mathsf{CC}
in this section will be proven with effective variants of the same notion of forcing that we now describe.

Definition 4.1
.

For
k\in\mathbb{N}
and
m\leqslant n\in\mathbb{N}
, given chains
\rho^{0},\rho^{1}\in k^{m}
,
\mu^{0},\mu^{1}\in k^{n}
, and
\tau^{0},\tau^{1}\in k^{n-m}
such that
\forall i<2,\mu^{i}=\rho^{i}\cdot\tau^{i}
.
We say that
(\mu^{0},\mu^{1})
is
completely incompatible over
(\rho^{0},\rho^{1})
if
\forall i<n-m,\tau^{0}(i)\neq\tau^{1}(i)
.
When
(\rho^{0},\rho^{1})=(\varepsilon,\varepsilon)
, we simply say
completely incompatible
.

Definition 4.2
.

A
condition-tuple
for a class
\mathcal{P}\subseteq\mathcal{X}
is a tuple
(\rho^{i},\sigma^{i})_{i<2}\in\mathcal{X}_{<\mathbb{N}}^{2}
such that
\mathcal{P}
is left-full below
(\rho^{i},\sigma^{i})
for both
i<2
, and
|\rho^{0}|=|\rho^{1}|
.
A condition-tuple
(\widehat{\rho}^{i},\widehat{\sigma}^{i})_{i<2}

extends
another
(\rho^{i},\sigma^{i})_{i<2}
, written
(\widehat{\rho}^{i},\widehat{\sigma}^{i})_{i<2}\leq(\rho^{i},\sigma^{i})_{i<2}
, if

1.

for both
i<2
,
(\widehat{\rho}^{i},\widehat{\sigma}^{i})\succcurlyeq(\rho^{i},\sigma^{i})

2.

(\widehat{\rho}^{0},\widehat{\rho}^{1})
is completely incompatible over
(\rho^{0},\rho^{1})

Remark 4.3
.

A pair
(\rho^{i},\sigma^{i})
of a condition-tuple is seen as a finite approximation of some
(X^{i},Y^{i})\in\mathcal{P}
we wish to build, i.e. of an element in
\mathcal{P}\cap\left[(\rho^{i},\sigma^{i})\right]
, such that
(X^{0},X^{1})
is completely incompatible over
(\rho^{0},\rho^{1})
.

4.2
Cross-constraint
\Delta^{0}_{2}
basis theorem

The following
\Delta^{0}_{2}
basis theorem is an effective analysis of the simplest known combinatorial proof of the cross-constraint problem. It was proven by Liu
[
22
]
. We provide the original proof as a warmup for the next basis theorems and for the sake of completeness.

Theorem 4.4

(Liu
[
22
, Lemma 2.2]
)
.

Any left-full computable instance
T
of
\mathsf{CC}
has a solution
(X^{i},Y^{i})_{i<2}
such that
(X^{0},Y^{0})\oplus(X^{1},Y^{1})\leqslant_{T}\emptyset^{\prime}
.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{P}\coloneqq[T]
.
We build a
\emptyset^{\prime}
-computable sequence of condition-tuples
(\rho^{i}_{0},\sigma^{i}_{0})_{i<2}\geq(\rho^{i}_{1},\sigma^{i}_{1})_{i<2}\geq\ldots
, such that, for
i<2
the functions
X^{i}:=\bigcup_{t}\rho^{i}_{t}
and
Y^{i}:=\bigcup_{t}\sigma^{i}_{t}
are the desired witnesses. To simplify notation, we simply say condition for condition-tuple.

Given a condition
(\rho^{i}_{t},\sigma^{i}_{t})_{i<2}
, we want to extend it to
(\rho^{i}_{t+1},\sigma^{i}_{t+1})_{i<2}
such that for all
s<r
,
(\sigma^{0}_{t+1,s},\sigma^{1}_{t+1,s})
is not completely incompatible over
(\sigma^{0}_{t,s},\sigma^{1}_{t,s})
. We proceed as follows:
say
(\rho^{i}_{t},\sigma^{i}_{t})_{i<2}
has been constructed, search for an extension
(\rho^{i}_{t+1},\sigma^{i}_{t+1})_{i<2}
such that the property above holds. By
Lemma

3.7
, this search requires the use of
\emptyset^{\prime}
to check whether
\mathcal{P}
is left-full below
(\rho^{i}_{t+1},\sigma^{i}_{t+1})
or not (for each i).

If there is always such an extension, then the construction is complete. Otherwise, for some condition
(\rho^{i}_{t},\sigma^{i}_{t})_{i<2}
, there is some
s<r
such that all extensions are completely incompatible over
(\sigma^{0}_{t,s},\sigma^{1}_{t,s})
. Such a condition is said to
exclude component
s<r
. Nevertheless, by considering the following lemma, the construction can be completed.

Lemma 4.5
.

If a condition
(\rho^{i},\sigma^{i})_{i<2}
excludes component
s<r
, then for every
i<2
, there is a coloring
Y\in 2{N}
such that for every
(\widehat{\rho},\widehat{\sigma})\leqslant(\rho^{i},\sigma^{i})
, if
\mathcal{P}
is left-full below
(\widehat{\rho},\widehat{\sigma})
, then
\widehat{\sigma}_{s}
is compatible with
Y
.

Proof.

Fix
(\rho^{i},\sigma^{i})_{i<2}
such that it excludes component
s<r
and fix
i<2
.

Suppose first that for cofinitely many
n\in\mathbb{N}
, there exists a
k_{n}<2
such that for every
(\rho^{\prime},\sigma^{\prime})\leq(\rho^{i},\sigma^{i})
for which
\mathcal{P}
is left-full, if
|\sigma^{\prime}_{s}|>n
then
\sigma^{\prime}_{s}(n)=k_{n}
. Then let
Y(n)\coloneqq k_{n}
. This is well-defined since by
Lemma

3.8
, there exist suitable extensions of every length.

Suppose now that for infinitely many
n\in\mathbb{N}
, for every
k<2
, there is a
(\rho^{\prime},\sigma^{\prime})\leq(\rho^{i},\sigma^{i})
for which
\mathcal{P}
is left-full and such that
\sigma^{\prime}_{s}(n)\neq k
. Fix
n>|\sigma^{i}_{s}|
, and for each
k<2
, let
(\rho_{k},\sigma_{k})\leq(\rho^{i},\sigma^{i})
be a condition extension such that
\sigma_{k,s}(n)\neq k
.

Since we are working in a 3-valued realm, there exists a
\widetilde{\rho}\succcurlyeq\rho^{1-i}
of length greater than
\max(|\rho_{0}|,|\rho_{1}|)
such that both
(\widetilde{\rho},\rho_{0})
and
(\widetilde{\rho},\rho_{1})
are completely incompatible over
(\rho^{1-i},\rho^{i})
.
Pick any pair
(\widehat{\rho}^{1-i},\widehat{\sigma}^{1-i})\leq(\widetilde{\rho},\sigma^{1-i})
for which
\mathcal{P}
is left-full and such that
|\widehat{\sigma}_{s}^{1-i}|>n
, this is possible by
Lemma

3.8
. Let
k=\widehat{\sigma}_{s}^{1-i}(n)
. Then
(\widehat{\rho}^{1-i},\widehat{\sigma}^{1-i},\rho_{k},\sigma_{k})
is an extension contradicting the fact that
(\rho^{i},\sigma^{i})_{i<2}
excludes component
s<r

∎

If at some point in the construction the condition
(\rho^{i}_{t},\sigma^{i}_{t})_{i<2}
excludes the components of

∎

Lemma 4.10

(Liu
[
22

For any

\varphi:3^{n}\to 3^{m}

\varphi(\rho)=\widehat{\rho}

Proof.

To give an intuition, if we just wanted to show the existence of a map
\varphi:3^{n}\to 3^{m}
preserving incompatibility over
(\varepsilon,\varepsilon)
, we could have taken the function which maps
\eta\in 3^{n}
to
\eta\cdot a^{m-n}
where

.

Fix
\rho

and

.
For every

\tau_{\rho(0)}=\tau
and for every

\tau_{a}
and

Let
\varphi

Lemma 4.11

(Liu
[

Let

(\varepsilon,\varepsilon)

\mathcal{R}_{\nu,\rho}\coloneqq\forall\widehat{\rho}\succcurlyeq\varphi(\rho),\nu\in f(\widehat{\rho})\text{ or }\nu\notin f(\varphi(\rho))

and

.

\eta

\varphi_{s}(\rho)

\varphi_{s}

\mathcal{R}_{\nu,\rho}

Finally, define
m\coloneqq n_{p}
and
\varphi\coloneqq\varphi_{p}
. By the hypothesis made on the function

for some

\forall\widehat{\rho}\succcurlyeq\varphi(\rho),\nu\in f(\widehat{\rho})

∎

The following definition should be understood in the light of the first-jump control for
\Pi^{0}_{1}
classes. When trying to construct an infinite path through an infinite binary tree
T\subseteq 2^{<\omega}
, one must ensure that at every step, the node is
extensible
, that is, the branch below the node is infinite. Being extensible is a
\Pi^{0}_{1}
property, and therefore to obtain a good first-jump control, one must resort to an overapproximation: Given a set
A\subseteq 2^{<\omega}
, instead of asking whether there is an extensible node in
T\cap A
, one will ask whether there is a level in the tree such that every node at this level belongs to
A
. Among the nodes at that level, at least one must be extendible. If
A
is
\Sigma^{0}_{1}
, then the former question is
\Sigma^{0}_{2}
, while the latter is
\Sigma^{0}_{1}
.

One can use a different technique, and ask whether the
\Pi^{0}_{1}
class
\mathcal{P}
of infinite subtrees of
T
disjoint from
A
is empty. In particular, by considering
S\subseteq T
, the pruned subtree of
T
containing only extendible nodes, since
S\not\in\mathcal{P}
, there is an extendible node in
S\cap A
. Here again, this overapproximation is
\Sigma^{0}_{1}
.

In the case of cross-constraint problems, a node
(\rho,\sigma)
is extensible in a cross-tree
T
if
T
is left-full below
(\rho,\sigma)
. The notion of
T
-sufficiency below is therefore the counterpart of the
\Sigma^{0}_{1}
question above, mutatis mutandis.

Definition 4.12

(Liu
[
22
, Definition 4.22]
)
.

Given a tree
T\subseteq\mathcal{X}
, and a tuple
(\rho^{i},\sigma^{i})_{i<2}\in\mathcal{X}_{<\mathbb{N}}^{2}
, a set

is

-sufficient over
(\rho^{i},\sigma^{i})_{i<2}
if, for every infinite subtree

for which
(\rho^{i},\sigma^{i})_{i<2}
is a condition-tuple, there is a tuple

such that
\forall i<2,(\widehat{\rho}^{i},\widehat{\sigma}^{i})\succcurlyeq(\rho^{i},\sigma^{i})
, and
(\widehat{\rho}^{0},\widehat{\rho}^{1})
is completely incompatible over

.

Note that if
A
is
\Sigma^{0}_{1}
, then the statement “

is
T
-sufficient over
(\rho^{i},\sigma^{i})_{i<2}
” is
\Sigma^{0}_{1}(T)
. The combinatorics for the cross-constraint problem are more complicated than the ones for weak König’s lemma, and one cannot simply consider the pruned tree containing only extensible nodes. However, the following lemmas show that one can consider a weakly pruned tree in which every node is almost extensible, in the sense that every node can be extended into a node below which the cross-tree is left-full.

The following lemma uses compactness to give a finite cross-tree version of
T
-sufficiency.
Recall that the notion of left-fullness was extended to finite trees, which induces a notion of condition-tuple.
Given a set

Lemma 4.13
.

Let

\forall i<2,(\widehat{\rho}^{i},\widehat{\sigma}^{i})\succcurlyeq(\rho^{i},\sigma^{i})

(\widehat{\rho}^{0},\widehat{\rho}^{1})

Proof.

Consider the class

.
It forms a tree for the relation where

The following lemma is the desired combinatorial lemma.

(Liu

Let

.

\displaystyle\tau

4.4
Cross-constraint cone avoidance basis theorem

We now prove our first cross-constraint basis theorem which requires some sort of first-jump control, using the combinatorics developed in
Section

4.3
. This basis theorem was first proven by Liu
[
22
, Lemma 4.5]
using a different argument. Our new proof follows more closely the standard proof of the cone avoidance basis theorem for
\Pi^{0}_{1}
classes.

Theorem 4.15

(Cross-constraint cone avoidance, Liu
[
22
, Lemma 4.5]
)
.

Let
C
be a non-computable set.
Any left-full computable instance
T
of
\mathsf{CC}
has a solution
(X^{i},Y^{i})_{i<2}
such that
(X^{0},Y^{0})\oplus(X^{1},Y^{1})\not\geqslant_{T}C
.

Proof.

To prove the theorem, we use forcing with conditions of the form
\left((\rho^{i},\sigma^{i})_{i<2},U,B\right)
, where

•

U
is a
B
-computable cross-subtree of
T

•

(\rho^{i},\sigma^{i})_{i<2}
is a condition-tuple for
[U]

•

B\subseteq\mathbb{N}
and
B\not\geqslant_{T}C

We will satisfy the following requirements for each
e\in\mathbb{N}
:

\mathcal{R}_{e}:\Phi_{e}^{(X^{0},Y^{0})\oplus(X^{1},Y^{1})}\neq C

A condition
\left((\rho^{i},\sigma^{i})_{i<2},U,B\right)

forces

\mathcal{R}_{\Psi}
, if
\mathcal{R}_{\Psi}
holds for all
(X^{i},Y^{i})\in[U]
extending
(\rho^{i},\sigma^{i})
for each
i<2
.

Lemma 4.16
.

For every condition
c\coloneqq\left((\rho^{i},\sigma^{i})_{i<2},U,B\right)
and every
e\in\mathbb{N}
, there is an extension of
c
that forces
\mathcal{R}_{e}
.

Proof.

For all
x\in\mathbb{N},v<2
, define

A_{x,v}\coloneqq\{(\widehat{\rho}^{i},\widehat{\sigma}^{i})_{i<2}\in\mathcal{X}_{<\mathbb{N}}^{2}:\Phi_{e}^{(\widehat{\rho}^{0},\widehat{\sigma}^{0})\oplus(\widehat{\rho}^{1},\widehat{\sigma}^{1})}(x)\downarrow=v\}

The set
A_{x,v}
is upward-closed, and
\Sigma^{0}_{1}
uniformly in
x,v
. Consider the following
\Sigma^{0}_{1}(B)
set:

Q=\{(x,v):A_{x,v}\mbox{ is }U\mbox{-sufficient over }(\widehat{\rho}^{i},\widehat{\sigma}^{i})_{i<2}\}

Case 1.

(x,C(x))\not\in Q
for some
x\in\mathbb{N}
. Let
\mathscr{L}
be the
\Pi^{0}_{1}(B)
class of cross-trees
S
witnessing that
A_{x,C(x)}
is not
U
-sufficient over
(\rho^{i},\sigma^{i})_{i<2}
. By the cone avoidance basis theorem, there is some cross-tree
S
such that
S\oplus B\not\geqslant_{T}C
.
The condition
d\coloneqq\big((\rho^{i},\sigma^{i})_{i<2},S,S\oplus B\big)
is the extension we are looking for. Indeed, it forces
\mathcal{R}_{e}
, because for
(X^{i},Y^{i})_{i<2}\in[d]
, if
\Psi^{(X^{0},Y^{0})\oplus(X^{1},Y^{1})}
is total, then it is different from
C
on input
x
.

Case 2.

(x,1-C(x))\in Q
for some
x\in\mathbb{N}
. Unfolding the definition,
A_{x,1-C(x)}
is
U
-sufficient
(\rho^{i},\sigma^{i})_{i<2}
, so by
Lemma

4.14
, there is a condition-tuple
(\widehat{\rho}^{i},\widehat{\sigma}^{i})_{i<2}\in A_{x,1-C(x)}
extending
(\rho^{i},\sigma^{i})_{i<2}
. Thus the
condition
\big((\widehat{\rho}^{i},\widehat{\sigma}^{i})_{i<2},U,B\big)
is the desired extension, as it forces
\mathcal{R}_{e}
.

Case 3.
Neither Case 1 nor Case 2 holds. Then
Q
is the
\Sigma^{0}_{1}(B)
graph of the characteristic function of
C
, so
C\leq_{T}B
. Contradiction.

∎

We are now ready to prove
Theorem

4.15
. Let
c_{0}=\left((\rho^{i},\sigma^{i})_{i<2},U,B\right)
be a condition that excludes a maximal number of components and let
\mathcal{F}
be a sufficiently generic filter for this notion of forcing, containing
c_{0}
. For
i<2
, let
X^{i}=\bigcup\{\rho^{i}:\left((\rho^{i},\sigma^{i})_{i<2},U,B\right)\in\mathcal{F}\}
and
Y^{i}=\bigcup\{\sigma^{i}:\left((\rho^{i},\sigma^{i})_{i<2},U,B\right)\in\mathcal{F}\}
. By
Lemma

3.8
,
X^{i}
and
Y^{i}
are both infinite sequences. By
Lemma

4.5
, for every
s<r
,
Y^{0}_{s}\cap Y^{1}_{s}
is infinite. By
Lemma

4.16
,
(X^{0},Y^{0})\oplus(X^{1},Y^{1})\not\geqslant_{T}C
. This completes the proof of
Theorem

4.15
.
∎

Corollary 4.17
.

For any non-computable set
C\subseteq\mathbb{N}
, there is a cross-constraint ideal that does not contain
C
.

Proof.

We construct a sequence of sets
Z_{0}\leqslant_{T}Z_{1}\leqslant_{T}\ldots
such that for any integer
n=\langle k,e\rangle
,
Z_{n}\not\geq_{T}C
, and if
\Phi_{e}^{Z_{k}}
is an instance of
\mathsf{CC}
, then
Z_{n+1}
computes a solution.

Define
Z_{0}\coloneqq\emptyset
. Suppose
Z_{n}
has been defined and let
n=\langle k,e\rangle
. If
\Phi_{e}^{Z_{k}}
is not a left-full cross-tree, then
Z_{n+1}\coloneqq Z_{n}
. Otherwise, by
Theorem

4.15
relativized to
Z_{n}
, there is a pair of paths
P_{0}
and
P_{1}
, such that

. In which case

.

By construction, the class
\mathcal{M}\coloneqq\{X\in 2{N}:\exists n,X\leqslant_{T}Z_{n}\}
is a cross-constraint ideal containing only sets avoiding the cone above

, in particular

is not in the ideal.
∎

4.5

\Sigma^{0}_{1}

We now prove a second cross-constraint basis theorem, about preservation of non-

This basis theorem for

4.6

Definition 5.1

A tree

•

In other words, a one-step variation of a tree consists of either extending a leaf with finitely many immediate children, or backtracking by removing a node, and making all its siblings leaves again. This is a non-reflexive relation. The notion of one-step variation can be reminiscent of the Hydra game
[

Definition 5.3

By induction, we define the partial orders

•

Lemma 5.4

(Liu
[

By induction on

Since the function in a computation path is an embedding into

Indeed, the “or” case can turn an inner node back into a leaf, but it can only do so finitely many times, because it strictly decreases the cardinal of the set of direct successors of the node it is applied to. Hence, there can only be finitely many computation paths.

5.1
Preservation of
\Gamma

We now prove that
\mathsf{COH}
admits preservation of
\Gamma

Theorem 5.16
.

Let
g\in 3{N}
be a
\Gamma
-hyperimmune function and
R_{0},R_{1},\dots
be a uniformly computable sequence of sets.
Then there is an
\vec{R}
-cohesive set
G
such that
g
is
\Gamma
-hyperimmune relative to
G
.

Proof.

We proceed by forcing, using Mathias conditions
(F,X)
such that
X
is computable.

For a
\Gamma_{m}
-functional
\Psi
, define the requirement
\mathcal{R}_{\Psi,m}\coloneqq
there is
n\in\mathbb{N}
such that
g
is compatible with
\lim_{s}\Psi^{G}(n,s)
.

Lemma 5.17
.

For each condition
(F,X)
,
m\in\mathbb{N}
, and
\Gamma_{m}
-functional
\Psi
, there is an extension forcing
\mathcal{R}_{\Psi,m}

Proof.

We define a computable
\Gamma_{m}
-approximation
f:\mathbb{N}^{2}\to\Gamma_{m}
as follows.
First, for every
n
,
f(n,0)\coloneqq\zeta_{m}
.
Suppose that at step
s
, we have defined
f(n,s)
for every
n
.
Then for each
n
, if there is some
F^{\prime}\subseteq X
with
\max F^{\prime}<s
and some
t\leqslant s
such that
\Psi^{F\cup F^{\prime}}(n,t)\downarrow
and
\Psi^{F\cup F^{\prime}}(n,t)\succ f(n,s)
, then let
f(n,s+1)\coloneqq\Psi^{F\cup F^{\prime}}(n,t)
. Otherwise, let
f(n,s+1)\coloneqq f(n,s)
. Then go to the next stage.

By construction,
f
is a
\Gamma_{m}
-approximation. Since
g
is
\Gamma
-hyperimmune, there is
n\in\mathbb{N}
such that
g
is compatible with
\lim_{s}f(n,s)
. Now by definition of
f
, there is a finite (possibly empty)
F^{\prime}\subseteq X
and
t\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\lim_{s}f(n,s)=\Psi^{F\cup F^{\prime}}(n,t)
. We claim that
(F\cup F^{\prime},X-\{0,\dots,\max F^{\prime}\})
forces
\mathcal{R}_{\Psi,m}
.
Indeed, by construction of
f
and since we considered the limit, we have that, for every
F^{\prime\prime}\subseteq X-\{0,\dots,\max F^{\prime}\}
and cofinitely many
t^{\prime}\in\mathbb{N}
,
\Psi^{F\cup F^{\prime}\cup F^{\prime\prime}}(n,t^{\prime})=\Psi^{F\cup F^{\prime}}(n,t)
.
∎

Let
\mathcal{F}
be a sufficiently generic filter for computable Mathias forcing and let
G=\bigcup_{(F,X)\in\mathcal{F}}F
. By genericity,
G
is
\vec{R}
-cohesive, since given a condition
(F,X)
and a computable set
R_{x}
, either
(F,X\cap R_{x})
or
(F,X\cap\overline{R}_{x})
is a valid extension. By
Lemma

5.17
, for every
m
and
\Gamma_{m}
-functional
\Gamma
,
g
diagonalizes against
\Gamma^{G}
. Thus, by
Lemma

5.9
,
g
is
\Gamma
-hyperimmune relative to
G
.
∎

Corollary 5.18
.

For every
\Gamma
-hyperimmune function
f:\mathbb{N}\to 3
, there exists a cross-constraint ideal
\mathcal{M}\models\mathsf{COH}

such that
f
is
\Gamma
-hyperimmune relative to every element of
\mathcal{M}
.

Proof.

We construct a sequence of sets
Z_{0}\leqslant_{T}Z_{1}\leqslant_{T}\ldots
such that for any integer
n=\langle i,k,e\rangle
,

•

f
is
\Gamma
-hyperimmune relative to
Z_{n}
.

•

if
n=\langle 0,k,e\rangle
, and if
\Phi_{e}^{Z_{k}}
is an instance of
\mathsf{CC}
, then
Z_{n+1}
computes a solution.

•

if
n=\langle 1,k,e\rangle
, and if
\Phi_{e}^{Z_{k}}
is an instance of
\mathsf{COH}
, then
Z_{n+1}
computes a solution.

Define
Z_{0}\coloneqq\emptyset
. Suppose
Z_{n}
has been defined.
If
n=\langle 0,k,e\rangle
and
\Phi_{e}^{Z_{k}}

Z_{n+1}\coloneqq Z_{n}
. Otherwise, by

relativized to

, there is a pair of paths

and

, such that

\Gamma
-hyperimmune relative to

.

and

is not a countable sequence of sets, then
Z_{n+1}\coloneqq Z_{n}
. Otherwise, by
Theorem

5.16
relativized to

, there is a
\Phi_{e}^{Z_{k}}
-cohesive set

, such that

is
\Gamma
-hyperimmune relative to

By construction, the class

5.2
Reducibility results

Definition 5.19
.

A problem
\mathsf{P}
is
strongly omnisciently computably reducible
to a problem
\mathsf{Q}
, noted
\mathsf{P}\leqslant_{soc}\mathsf{Q}
, if for every
\mathsf{P}
-instance
X
, there exists a
\mathsf{Q}
-instance
\widehat{X}
such that, for every
\mathsf{Q}
-solution
\widehat{Y}
of
\widehat{X}
,
\widehat{Y}
computes a
\mathsf{P}
-solution to
X
.

Contrary to computable reduction, no effectiveness is imposed on the complexity of the
\mathsf{Q}
-instance
\widehat{X}
to solve the
\mathsf{P}
-instance
X
. Moreover, the solution to the
\mathsf{P}
-instance has to be computable from the solution
\widehat{Y}
to the
\mathsf{Q}
-instance
\widehat{X}
, without the help of
X
.

Theorem 5.20

(Liu
[
22
, Theorem 2.1]
)
.

\mathsf{RT}^{1}_{3}\not\leqslant_{\text{soc}}(\mathsf{RT}^{1}_{2})^{*}

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{M}
be a countable cross-constraint ideal (such an ideal exists thanks to
Corollary

4.7
), and let
f\in\mathcal{X}(0)
be hyperimmune relative to any set in
\mathcal{M}
. For any
g\in\mathcal{X}(1)
, by
Theorem

3.13
, there are sets
\vec{G}
witnessing the inequality
\mathsf{RT}^{1}_{3}\not\leqslant_{\text{soc}}(\mathsf{RT}^{1}_{2})^{*}
.
∎

Liu
[
22
, Theorem 4.1]
proved that
\mathsf{SRT}^{2}_{3}\not\leqslant_{c}(\mathsf{SRT}^{2}_{2})^{*}
. We strengthen his result by proving that it holds even for non-stable instances of

.

Theorem 5.21
.

\mathsf{SRT}^{2}_{3}\not\leqslant_{c}(\mathsf{RT}^{2}_{2})^{*}

Proof.

By
proposition

5.13
there exists a
\Delta^{0}_{2}
coloring
f:\mathbb{N}\to 3
which is
\Gamma
-hyperimmune.
Using Shoenfield’s limit lemma, there is a stable computable function
h:[\mathbb{N}]^{2}\to 3
such that for every
x

\lim_{y}h(x,y)=f(x)
. Consider
h
as a computable instance of
\mathsf{SRT}^{2}_{3}
. Fix any
r
-tuple of computable colorings
g_{0},\ldots,g_{r-1}:[\mathbb{N}]^{2}\to 2
for some
r\in\mathbb{N}
. It suffices to show the existence of
g_{i}
-homogeneous sets
H_{i}
for every
i<r
such that
\bigoplus_{j<r}H_{j}
does not compute any infinite
h
-homogeneous set.

By
Corollary

5.18
, there is a countable cross-constraint ideal
\mathcal{M}\models\mathsf{COH}
for which
f
is
\Gamma
-hyperimmune relative to any set in
\mathcal{M}
. In particular, since
g_{0},\dots,g_{r-1}
are computable, they belong to
\mathcal{M}
. Moreover, by
Lemma

5.12
,

is hyperimmune relative to every element of
\mathcal{M}
. By

, there exists
g_{i}
-homogeneous sets
H_{i}
for every

, such that
\bigoplus_{j<r}H_{j}
does not compute any infinite

-homogeneous set. Since any
h
-homogeneous set is
f
-homogeneous, then
\bigoplus_{j<r}H_{j}
does not compute any infinite
h
-homogeneous set.
∎

Definition 5.22
.

A set
H\subseteq\mathbb{N}
is
pre-homogeneous
for a coloring
f:[\mathbb{N}]^{n+1}\to k
if
\forall\sigma\in[H]^{n},\forall z{>}y{>}\max\sigma,f(\sigma,y)=f(\sigma,z)

Jockusch
[
17
, Lemma 5.4]
proved that for every computable coloring
f:[\mathbb{N}]^{n+1}\to k
, every PA degree relative to
\emptyset^{\prime}
computes an infinite pre-homogeneous set for

. Moreover, for
n\geq 2
, Hirschfeldt and Jocksuch
[
15

proved a reversal.

Theorem 5.23
.

For every
n\geq 2
,

\mathsf{SRT}^{n}_{3}\not\leqslant_{c}(\mathsf{RT}^{n}_{2})^{*}

Proof.

We prove by induction on
n\geq 2
that for every set
P
, there exists a
\Delta^{0}_{n}(P)
coloring
f:\mathbb{N}\to 3

such that for every
r\geq 1
every
r
-tuple of
P
-computable colorings
g_{0},\dots,g_{r-1}:[\mathbb{N}]^{n}\to 2
,
there are infinite
\vec{g}
-homogeneous sets
G_{0},\dots,G_{r-1}
such that
\vec{G}\oplus P
does not compute any infinite
f
-homogeneous set.

The case

corresponds to a relativized form of
Theorem

5.21
. Now suppose the hypothesis holds for some
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Fix some set
P
, and let
Q\gg P^{\prime}
be such that
Q^{\prime}\leqslant_{T}P^{\prime\prime}
(which exists by relativization of the low basis theorem (Jockusch and Soare
[
18
]
)).

By induction hypothesis relativized to
Q
, there exists a
\Delta^{0}_{n}(Q)
(i.e.
\Delta^{0}_{n+1}(P)
) coloring
f:\mathbb{N}\to 3
such that for every
r\geq 1
every
r

Q
-computable colorings
g_{0},\dots,g_{r-1}:[\mathbb{N}]^{n}\to 2

\vec{g}
-homogeneous sets
G_{0},\dots,G_{r-1}
such that
\vec{G}\oplus Q
does not compute any infinite
f
-homogeneous set.

Now, consider an
r
-tuple of
P
-computable colorings
h_{0},\dots,h_{r-1}:[\mathbb{N}]^{n+1}\to 2
.
By Jockusch
[
17

,

C_{0},\dots,C_{r-1}\subseteq\mathbb{N}
pre-homogeneous for
\widetilde{h}_{0},\dots,\widetilde{h}_{r-1}
. For

be the

-computable coloring defined by

By choice of

Finally, by Shoenfield’s limit lemma, for every

∎
