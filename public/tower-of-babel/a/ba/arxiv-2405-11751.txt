Asymptotic theory of in-context learning by linear attention
Yue M. Lu, Mary I. Letey, Jacob A. Zavatone-Veth, Anindita Maiti, Cengiz Pehlevan

Abstract
Transformers have a remarkable ability to learn and execute tasks based on examples provided within the input itself, without explicit prior training. It has been argued that this capability, known as in-context learning (ICL), is a cornerstone of Transformers’ success, yet questions about the necessary sample complexity, pretraining task diversity, and context length for successful ICL remain unresolved. Here, we provide a precise answer to these questions in an exactly solvable model of ICL of a linear regression task by linear attention. We derive sharp asymptotics for the learning curve in a phenomenologically-rich scaling regime where the token dimension is taken to infinity; the context length and pretraining task diversity scale proportionally with the token dimension; and the number of pretraining examples scales quadratically. We demonstrate a double-descent learning curve with increasing pretraining examples, and uncover a phase transition in the model’s behavior between low and high task diversity regimes: In the low diversity regime, the model tends toward memorization of training tasks, whereas in the high diversity regime, it achieves genuine in-context learning and generalization beyond the scope of pretrained tasks. These theoretical insights are empirically validated through experiments with both linear attention and full nonlinear Transformer architectures.

Since their introduction by Vaswani et al. in 2017

The technological advancements enabled by Transformers have inspired a substantial body of research aimed at understanding their working principles. One key observation is that language models gain new behaviors and skills as their number of parameters and the size of their training datasets grow
[

11
,
12

in-context learning
(ICL), which describes the model’s ability to learn and execute tasks based on the context provided within the input itself, without the need for explicit prior training on those specific tasks. To give an example from natural language processing, a pretrained large language model might be able to successfully translate English to Italian after being prompted with a few example translations, even if it has not been specifically pretrained on that translation task
[
7
]
. ICL enables language models to perform new, specialized tasks without retraining, which is arguably a key reason for their general-purpose abilities.

Despite recent progress in understanding ICL, fundamental questions about when and how ICL emerges in large language models remain unresolved. These models are typically trained or pretrained using a next-token prediction objective, but the impact of various algorithmic and hyperparameter choices during pretraining on ICL performance is still not well understood. What mechanisms do Transformers implement to facilitate ICL? How many pretraining examples are necessary for ICL capabilities to arise? Furthermore, how many examples must be provided within the input context for the model to successfully perform an in-context task? Another important question is the degree of task diversity in the training data: how diverse must the training tasks be to enable ICL for truly novel tasks that the model has not encountered before?

In this paper, we address these questions by investigating the ICL capabilities of a linear attention module for linear regression tasks. This simplified model setting, which exhibits the minimal architectural features to perform ICL for linear regression, enables us to derive an asymptotically precise theory of ICL performance, elucidating its exact dependence on various hyperparameters. In the remainder of this section, we first provide an overview of related work on ICL. We then summarize our main contributions.

ICL in Transformer architectures.

Empirical studies of synthetic ICL tasks.

Though ICL in NLP is both impressive and useful, these natural data do not allow precise experimental control and study. Towards a fine-grained understanding of the conditions required for ICL, many recent works have explored ICL of parametrically-controllable synthetic tasks, notably linear regression and classification. A motivating objective of our work is to provide a theoretical account of the empirical findings made by Raventós

I.2
Summary of contributions

We now summarize the main contributions of our paper relative to the prior art reviewed above. Building on recent literature, we focus on a simplified model of a Transformer that captures its key architectural motif: the linear self-attention module
[
27
,
28
,
31
,
30
,
29
,
25
]
. Linear attention includes the quadratic pairwise interactions between inputs that lie at the heart of softmax attention, but it omits the normalization steps and fully connected layers. This simplification makes the model more amenable to theoretical analysis. Our main result is a sharp asymptotic analysis of ICL for linear regression using linear attention, leading to a more precisely predictive theory than previous population risk analyses or finite-sample bounds
[
29
,
28
]
. The main contributions of our paper are structured as follows:

1.

We begin in §
II
by developing a simplified parameterization of linear self-attention that allows pretraining on the ICL linear regression task to be performed using ridge regression.

2.

Within this simplified model, we identify a phenomenologically rich scaling limit in which the ICL performance can be analyzed (§
III
). As the token dimension tends to infinity, we allow the number of pretraining examples scale quadratically with the token dimension, while the context length and pretraining task diversity scale linearly. In this joint limit, we compute sharp asymptotics for ICL performance using random matrix theory.

3.

The asymptotically precise theory curves we derive reveal several interesting phenomena (§
III
). First, we observe double-descent in the model’s ICL generalization performance as a function of pretraining dataset size, reflecting our assumption that it is pretrained to interpolation. Second, we study the non-monotonic dependence of ICL performance on context length. Last, we uncover a transition from memorization to in-context learning as the pretraining task diversity increases. This transition recapitulates the empirical findings of
[
20
]
in full Transformer models.

4.

In §
IV
, we demonstrate through numerical experiments that the insights from our theory, derived using the simplified linear attention model, transfer to full Transformer models with softmax self-attention. In particular, the scaling of pretraining sample complexity and task diversity with token dimension required for successful ICL is consistent.

II
Problem formulation

We begin by describing the setting of our study.

II.1
ICL of linear regression

In an ICL task, the model takes as input a sequence of tokens
\{x_{1},y_{1},x_{2},y_{2},\ldots,x_{\ell},y_{\ell},x_{\ell+1}\}
, and outputs a prediction of
y_{\ell+1}
. We will often refer to an input sequence as a “context.”
The pairs
\{x_{i},y_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\ell+1}
are i.i.d. samples from a
context-dependent
joint distribution
P(x,y)
. Hence, the model needs to gather information about
P(x,y)
from the first
\ell
examples and use this information to predict
y_{\ell+1}
from
x_{\ell+1}
. We will refer to
\ell
as the “context length”.

In this work, we focus on an approximately linear mapping between
x_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
y_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
:

y_{i}=\langle x_{i},w\rangle+\epsilon_{i},

(2)

where
\epsilon_{i}
is a Gaussian noise
w\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is referred to as a task vector. We note that the task vector
w
is fixed within a context, but can change between different contexts. The model has to learn
w
from the
\ell
pairs presented within the context, and use it to predict
y_{\ell+1}
from
x_{\ell+1}
.

II.2
Linear self-attention

The model that we will analytically study is the linear self-attention block
[
33
]
. Linear self-attention takes as input an embedding matrix
Z
, whose columns hold the sequence tokens. The mapping of sequences to matrices is not unique. Here, following the convention in
[
29
,
30
,
33
]
, we will embed the input sequence
\{x_{1},y_{1},x_{2},y_{2},\ldots,x_{\ell},y_{\ell},x_{\ell+1}\}
as:

\displaystyle Z=\left[\begin{array}[]{ccccc}x_{1}&x_{2}&\ldots&x_{\ell}&x_{\ell+1}\\
y_{1}&y_{2}&\ldots&y_{\ell}&0\end{array}\right]\in\mathbb{R}^{(d+1)\times(\ell+1)},

where
0
in the lower-right corner is a token that prompts the missing value
y_{\ell+1}
to be predicted.

For value matrix
V\in\mathbb{R}^{(d+1)\times(d+1)}
and key and query matrices
K,Q
such that
K^{\top}Q\in\mathbb{R}^{(d+1)\times(d+1)}
, the output of a linear-attention block
[
34
,
35
,
33
]
is given by

\displaystyle A\coloneqq Z+\frac{1}{\ell}VZ(KZ)^{\top}(QZ).

(5)

The output
A
is a matrix while our goal is to predict a scalar,
y_{\ell+1}
. Following the choice of positional encoding in (
II.2
), we will take
A_{d+1,\ell+1}
, the element of
A
corresponding to the
0
prompt, as the prediction for
y_{\ell+1}
:

\displaystyle\hat{y}\coloneqq A_{d+1,\ell+1}.

(6)

II.3
Pretraining data

The model is pretrained on
n
sample sequences, where the
\mu
th sample is a collection of
\ell+1
vector-scalar pairs
\{x_{i}^{\mu}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},y_{i}^{\mu}\in\mathbb{R}\}_{i=1}^{\ell+1}
related by the approximate linear mapping in (
2
):
y_{i}^{\mu}=\langle x_{i}^{\mu},w^{\mu}\rangle+\epsilon_{i}^{\mu}
. Here,
w^{\mu}
denotes the task vector associated with the
\mu
th sample. We make the following statistical assumptions:

1.

x_{i}^{\mu}
are
d
-dimensional random vectors, sampled i.i.d. over both
i
and
\mu
from an isotropic Gaussian distribution
\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d}/d)
.

2.

At the beginning of training, construct a finite set with
k
task vectors, denoted by

\Omega_{k}=\left\{w_{1},w_{2},\ldots,w_{k}\right\}.

(7)

The elements of this set are independently drawn once from

w_{i}\sim_{\text{i.i.d.}}\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d}).

(8)

For
1\leq\mu\leq n
, the task vector
w^{\mu}
associated with the
\mu
th sample context is uniformly sampled from
\Omega_{k}
. Note that the variable
k
controls the task diversity in the pretraining dataset. Importantly,
k
can be less than
n
, in which case the same task vector from
\Omega_{k}
will be repeated multiple times.

3.

The noise terms
\epsilon_{i}^{\mu}
are i.i.d. over both
i
and
\mu
, and drawn from a normal distribution
\mathcal{N}(0,\rho)
.

We denote a sample from this distribution by
(Z,y_{\ell+1})\sim\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{train}}
.

II.4
Parameter reduction

Before specifying the training procedure, it is insightful to first examine the prediction mechanism of the linear attention module for the ICL task. This proves to be a fruitful exercise, shedding light on key questions: Can linear self-attention learn linear regression in-context? If so, what do the model parameters learn from the data to solve this ICL problem? By closely analyzing these aspects, we can also formulate a simplified problem that lends itself to analytical study.

We start by rewriting the output of the linear attention module, (
6
), in an alternative form. Following
[
29
]
, we define

\displaystyle V=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}V_{11}&v_{12}\\
v_{21}^{\top}&v_{22}\end{array}\right],\quad M=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}M_{11}&m_{12}\\
m_{21}^{\top}&m_{22}\end{array}\right]\coloneqq K^{\top}Q,

where
V_{11}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
,
v_{12},v_{21}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
v_{22}\in\mathbb{R}
,
M_{11}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
,
m_{12},m_{21}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and
m_{22}\in\mathbb{R}
. We assume that the inner dimension of
K^{\top}Q
is greater than equal to
d+1
so that matrix
M
can achieve full rank. From (
5
) and (
6
), one can check that

\displaystyle\hat{y}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\ell}\big\langle x_{\ell+1},v_{22}M_{11}^{\top}\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}y_{i}x_{i}+v_{22}m_{21}\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}y_{i}^{2}+M_{11}^{\top}\sum_{i=1}^{\ell+1}x_{i}x_{i}^{\top}v_{21}+m_{21}\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}y_{i}x_{i}^{\top}v_{21}\big\rangle,

(13)

where
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
stands for the standard inner product.

This expression reveals several interesting points, including how this model could express a solution to the ICL task. First, not all parameters in (
II.4
) contribute to the output: we can discard all the parameters except for the last row of
V
and the first
d
columns of
M
.
Second, the first term

\frac{1}{\ell}v_{22}M_{11}^{\top}\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}y_{i}x_{i}

(14)

offers a hint about how the linear attention module might be solving the task. The sum
\frac{1}{\ell}\sum_{i\leq\ell}y_{i}x_{i}
is a noisy estimate of
\mathbb{E}[xx^{\top}]w
for that context. Hence, if the parameters of the model are such that
v_{22}M_{11}^{\top}
is approximately
\mathbb{E}[xx^{\top}]^{-1}
, this term alone makes a good prediction for the output. Third, the third term,
M_{11}^{\top}\sum_{i=1}^{\ell+1}x_{i}x_{i}^{\top}v_{21}
does not depend on outputs
y
, and thus does not directly contribute to the ICL task that relies on the relationship between
x
and
y
. Finally, the fourth term,
m_{21}\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}y_{i}x_{i}^{\top}v_{21}
, only considers a one dimensional projection of
x
onto
v_{21}
. Because the task vectors
w
and
x
are isotropic in the statistical models that we consider, there are no special directions in the problem. Consequently, we expect the optimal
v_{21}
to be approximately zero by symmetry considerations.

Motivated by these observations, and for analytical tractability, we study the linear attention module with the constraint
v_{21}=0
. In this case, collecting the remaining parameters in a matrix

\displaystyle\Gamma\coloneqq v_{22}\begin{bmatrix}M_{11}^{\top}/d&m_{21}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times(d+1)}

(15)

and the input sequence in another matrix
H_{Z}
, defined as

\displaystyle H_{Z}\coloneqq x_{\ell+1}\begin{bmatrix}\frac{d}{\ell}\sum_{i\leq\ell}y_{i}x_{i}^{\top}&\frac{1}{\ell}\sum_{i\leq\ell}y_{i}^{2}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times(d+1)},

(16)

we can rewrite the predicted label as

\hat{y}=\langle\Gamma,H_{Z}\rangle.

(17)

The
1/d
scaling of
M_{11}
in
\Gamma
is chosen so that the columns of
H_{Z}
scale similarly; it does not affect the final predictor
\hat{y}
.

We note that
[
29
]
provides an analysis of the population risk (whereas we focus on empirical risk) for a related reduced model in which both
v_{21}
and
m_{21}
are set to
0
. Consequently, the predictors considered in
[
29
]
slightly differ from ours in (
15
)–(
17
) by an additive term (due to
m_{21}
). The authors of
[
29
]
justify this reduced model through an optimization argument: if these parameters
v_{21}
and
m_{21}
are initialized to zero, they remain zero under gradient descent optimization of the population risk.

In the remainder of this paper, we will examine the ICL performance of the reduced model given in (
16
) and (
17
), except when making comparisons to a full, nonlinear Transformer architecture. Henceforth, unless explicitly stated otherwise, we will refer to this reduced model as the linear attention module.

II.5
Model pretraining

The parameters of the linear attention module are learned from
n
samples of input sequences,

\displaystyle\{x_{1}^{\mu},y_{1}^{\mu},\ldots,x_{\ell+1}^{\mu},y_{\ell+1}^{\mu}\},\qquad\mu=1,\ldots,n.

(18)

We estimate model parameters by minimizing MSE loss on next-output prediction with ridge regularistion, giving

\displaystyle\Gamma^{\ast}

\displaystyle=\underset{\Gamma}{\arg\,\min}\,\sum_{\mu=1}^{n}\left(y_{\ell+1}^{\mu}-\langle\Gamma,H_{Z^{\mu}}\rangle\right)^{2}+\frac{n}{d}\lambda\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\Gamma\right\rVert}_{\mathrm{F}}^{2},

(19)

where
\lambda>0
is a regularization parameter, and
H_{Z^{\mu}}
refers to the input matrix (
16
) populated with the
\mu
th sample sequence. The factor
n/d
in front of
\lambda
makes sure that, when we take the
d\to\infty
or
n\to\infty
limits later, there is still a meaningful ridge regularization when
\lambda>0
. The solution to the optimization problem in (
19
) can be expressed explicitly as

\displaystyle\text{vec}(\Gamma^{\ast})=\left(\frac{n}{d}\lambda I+\sum_{\mu=1}^{n}\text{vec}(H_{Z^{\mu}})\text{vec}(H_{Z^{\mu}})^{\top}\right)^{-1}\sum_{\mu=1}^{n}y_{\ell+1}^{\mu}\text{vec}(H_{Z^{\mu}}),

(20)

where
\vecop(\cdot)
denotes the vectorization operation. Throughout this paper, we adopt the
row-major
convention. Thus, for a
d_{1}\times d_{2}
matrix
A
,
\vecop(A)
is a vector in
\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}d_{2}}
, formed by stacking the rows of
A
together.

II.6
Evaluation

For a given set of parameters
\Gamma
, the model’s generalization error is defined as

e(\Gamma)\coloneqq\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{test}}}\left[\left(y_{\ell+1}-\langle\Gamma,H_{Z}\rangle\right)^{2}\right],

(21)

where
(Z,y_{\ell+1})\sim\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{test}}
is a new sample drawn from the probability distribution of the test dataset. We consider two different test data distributions
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{test}}
:

1.

ICL task:

x_{i}
and
\epsilon_{i}
are i.i.d. Gaussians as in the pretraining case. However, each task vector
w^{\text{test}}
associated with a test input sequence of length
\ell
is drawn independently from
\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d})
. We will denote the test error under this setting by
e^{\mathrm{ICL}}(\Gamma)
.

2.

In-distribution generalization (IDG) task:
The test data are generated in exactly the same manner as the training data,
i.e.
,
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{test}}=\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{train}}
, hence the term in-distribution generalization. In particular, the set of unique task vectors
\{w_{1},\ldots,w_{k}\}
is identical to that used in the pretraining data. We will denote the test error under this setting by
e^{\text{IDG}}(\Gamma)
. This task can also be referred to as in-weight learning.

The ICL task evaluates the true in-context learning performance of the linear attention module. The task vectors in the test set differ from those seen in training, requiring the model to infer them from context. The IDG task assesses the model’s performance on task vectors it has previously encountered during pretraining. High performance on the IDG task but low performance on the ICL task indicates that the model memorizes the training task vectors. Conversely, high performance on the ICL task suggests that the model can learn genuinely new task vectors from the provided context.

To understand the performance of our model on both ICL and IDG tasks, we will need to evaluate these expressions for the pretrained attention matrix
\Gamma^{\ast}
given in (
20
). An asymptotically precise prediction of
e^{\mathrm{ICL}}(\Gamma^{\ast})
and
e^{\mathrm{IDG}}(\Gamma^{\ast})
will be a main result of this work.

II.7
Baselines: Bayes-optimal within-context estimators

Following
[
20
]
, it is useful to compare the predictions made by the trained linear attention to optimal estimators that use only the current context information. These estimators rely solely on the data within the given context for their predictions, but need oracle knowledge of the full statistical models underlying the data. Under the mean square loss, the optimal Bayesian estimator
\hat{y}_{\text{Bayes}}=\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{test}}}[y_{\ell+1}|x_{1},y_{1},x_{2},y_{2},\ldots,x_{\ell},y_{\ell},x_{\ell+1}]
in our setting has the form

\displaystyle\hat{y}_{\text{Bayes}}=(w_{\text{Bayes}})^{\top}x_{\ell+1},

(22)

where
w_{\text{Bayes}}
is the Bayes estimator of the task vector
w
.

For the ICL task, the Bayes-optimal ridge regression estimator is given by

\displaystyle w_{\text{Bayes}}^{\text{ridge}}\coloneqq\left(\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}x_{i}x_{i}^{\top}+\rho I_{d}\right)^{-1}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}y_{i}x_{i}\right),

(23)

where the ridge parameter is set to the noise variance
\rho
. We will refer to it as the
ridge estimator
.
For the IDG task, the Bayes-optimal estimator is given by

\displaystyle w_{\text{Bayes}}^{\text{dMMSE}}\coloneqq\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{k}w_{j}e^{-\frac{1}{2\rho}\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\left(y_{i}-w_{j}^{\top}x_{i}\right)^{2}}}{\sum_{j=1}^{k}e^{-\frac{1}{2\rho}\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\left(y_{i}-w_{j}^{\top}x_{i}\right)^{2}}}.

(24)

Here, we assume that the training task vectors
\{w_{1},\ldots,w_{k}\}
are known to the estimator. Following
[
20
]
, we will refer to this estimator as the
discrete minimum mean squared error (dMMSE) estimator
.

The test performance of these estimators are calculated by

e^{\text{Bayes}}_{\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{test}}}=\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{test}}}\left[\left(y_{\ell+1}-(w_{\text{Bayes}})^{\top}x_{\ell+1}\right)^{2}\right],

(25)

where
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{test}}
can be associated with either the ICL or the IDG task, and
w_{\text{Bayes}}
can be the ridge or the dMMSE estimator. To avoid possible confusion, we emphasize that we will sometimes plot the performance of an estimator on a task for which it is not optimal. For example, we will test the dMMSE estimator, which is Bayes-optimal for the pretraining distribution, on the ICL task, where it is not optimal. This will be done for benchmarking purposes.

III
Theoretical results

To answer the questions raised in the introduction, we provide a precise asymptotic analysis of the learning curves of the linear attention module for ICL of linear regression. We then focus on various implications of these equations, and verify through simulations that our insights gained from this theoretical analysis extend to more realistic nonlinear Transformers.

III.1
Joint asymptotic limit

We have now defined both the structure of the training data as well as the parameters to be optimized. For our theoretical analysis, we consider a joint asymptotic limit in which the input dimension
d
, the pretraining dataset size
n
, the context length
\ell
, and the number of task vectors in the training set
k
, go to infinity together such that

\displaystyle\frac{\ell}{d}\coloneqq\alpha=\Theta(1),\quad\frac{k}{d}\coloneqq\kappa=\Theta(1),\quad\frac{n}{d^{2}}\coloneqq\tau=\Theta(1).

(26)

Identification of these scalings constitutes one of the main results of our paper.
As we will see, the linear attention module exhibits rich learning phenomena in this limit.

The intuition for these scaling parameters can be seen as follows. Standard results in linear regression
[
36
,
37
,
38
]
show that to estimate a
d
-dimensional task vector
w
from the
\ell
samples within a context, one needs at least
\ell=\Theta(d)
. The number of unique task vectors that must be seen to estimate the covariance matrix of the true
d
-dimensional task distribution
\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d})
should also scale with
d
,
i.e.

k=\Theta(d)
.
Finally, we see from (

\Theta(d^{2})
. This suggests that the number of individual contexts the model sees during pretraining should scale similarly,
i.e.
,

.

III.2
Learning curves for ICL of linear regression by a linear attention module

Our theoretical analysis, explained in detail in the Supplementary Information, leads to asymptotically precise expressions for the generalization errors under the two test distributions being studied. Specifically, our theory predicts that, as
d,n,\ell,k\to\infty
in the joint limit given in (
26
),

\displaystyle e^{\mathrm{ICL}}(\Gamma^{\ast})\longrightarrow e^{\mathrm{ICL}}(\tau,\alpha,\kappa,\rho,\lambda)\qquad\text{almost surely},

(27)

and

\displaystyle e^{\mathrm{IDG}}(\Gamma^{\ast})\longrightarrow e^{\mathrm{IDG}}(\tau,\alpha,\kappa,\rho,\lambda)\qquad\text{almost surely},

(28)

where
e^{\mathrm{ICL}}(\tau,\alpha,\kappa,\rho,\lambda)
and
e^{\mathrm{IDG}}(\tau,\alpha,\kappa,\rho,\lambda)
are two deterministic functions of the parameters
\tau
,
\alpha
,
\kappa
,
\rho
and
\lambda
. The exact expressions of these two functions can be found in Section
E
of the SI. For simplicity, we only present in what follows the ridgeless limit (
i.e.
,
\lambda\to 0^{+}
) of the asymptotic generalization errors.

Result 1

(ICL generalization error in the ridgeless limit)
.

Let

q^{\ast}\coloneqq\frac{1+\rho}{\alpha},\quad m^{\ast}\coloneqq\mathcal{M}_{\kappa}\left({q^{\ast}}\right),\quad\mu^{\ast}\coloneqq q^{\ast}\mathcal{M}_{\kappa/\tau}(q^{\ast}),

(29)

where
\mathcal{M}_{\kappa}(\cdot)
, defined in Section
H
of the supplementary, is a function related to the Stieltjes transform of the Marchenko-Pastur law. Then

\displaystyle e^{\mathrm{ICL}}_{\mathrm{ridgeless}}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\lim_{\lambda\to 0^{+}}e^{\mathrm{ICL}}(\tau,\alpha,\kappa,\rho,\lambda)

(30)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}{\frac{\tau(1+q^{\ast})}{1-\tau}\left[1-\tau(1-\mu^{\ast})^{2}+\mu^{\ast}(\rho/q^{\ast}-1)\right]}{-2\tau(1-\mu^{\ast})+(1+\rho)}&\tau<1\\
\left(q^{\ast}+1\right)\left(1-2q^{\ast}m^{\ast}-(q^{\ast})^{2}\mathcal{M}^{\prime}_{\kappa}(q^{\ast})+\frac{(\rho+q^{\ast}-(q^{\ast})^{2}m^{\ast})m^{\ast}}{\tau-1}\right)-2(1-q^{\ast}m^{\ast})+(1+\rho)&\tau>1\end{cases},

where
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}_{\kappa}(\cdot)
means the derivative of
\mathcal{M}_{\kappa}(q)
with respect to
q
.

Result 2

(IDG generalization error in the ridgeless limit)
.

Let
q^{\ast}
,
m^{\ast}
, and
\mu^{\ast}
be the scalars defined in (
29
). We have

\displaystyle e^{\mathrm{IDG}}_{\mathrm{ridgeless}}\coloneqq\lim_{\lambda\to 0^{+}}e^{\mathrm{IDG}}(\tau,\alpha,\kappa,\rho,\lambda)=

(31)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\frac{\tau}{1-\tau}\left(\frac{\rho+q^{\ast}-2q^{\ast}(1-\tau)({q^{\ast}}/{\xi^{\ast}}+1)}{1-p^{\ast}(1-\tau)}+\frac{\tau\mu^{\ast}(q^{\ast}+\xi^{\ast})^{2}}{q^{\ast}}\right)&\tau<1\\
\frac{\tau}{\tau-1}[\rho+q^{\ast}(1-q^{\ast}m^{\ast})]&\tau>1\end{cases},

(32)

where
\xi^{\ast}=\frac{(1-\tau)q^{\ast}}{\tau\mu^{\ast}}
and
p^{\ast}=\big(1-{\kappa}\big(\frac{\kappa\xi^{\ast}}{1-\tau}+1\big)^{-2}\big)^{-1}
.

We will discuss various implications of these equations in the next sections.

We derived these results using techniques from random matrix theory. The full setup and technical details are presented in the Supplementary Information. The computations involve analysis of the properties of the finite-sample optimal parameter matrix
\Gamma^{*}
(see (
20
)). A key technical component of our analysis involves characterizing the spectral properties of the sample covariance matrix of
n=\Theta(d^{2})
i.i.d. random vectors in dimension
\Theta(d^{2})
. Each of these vectors is constructed as the vectorized version of the matrix in (
16
). Related but simpler versions of this type of random matrices involving the tensor product of i.i.d. random vectors have been studied in recent work
[
39
]
. Some of our derivations are based on non-rigorous yet technically plausible heuristics. We support these predictions with numerical simulations and discuss in the Supplementary Information the steps required to achieve a fully rigorous proof.

III.5
Learning transition with increasing pretraining task diversity

It is important to quantify if and when a given model is actually learning in-context,
i.e.
, solving a new regression problem by adapting to the specific structure of the task rather than relying solely on memorized training task vectors. We refer to this phenomenon as
task generalization
.

We expect pretraining task diversity (
k
) to play a key role in task generalization. To provide some motivation for this, suppose the model was pretrained on only a single task vector (
k=1
). It is reasonable to expect that the model will memorize this task vector and make predictions under the prior that the task vector in the test examples is identical to that particular pretraining task vector. On the other extreme, suppose the model was pretrained by drawing a fresh task vector for every training sequence. Here, the expectation would be that the model achieves task generalization. Indeed, results from previous computational studies on the linear regression task
[
20
]
suggest that transformers are better in-context learners if they have seen a greater task diversity in the training set, and further the models transition from a task memorization phase to a task generalization phase as task diversity increases. Hence, we study the task generalization behavior of the linear transformer as the task diversity increases and precisely quantify the task diversity scale at which such transition happens.

First, we need to discuss how to measure task generalization. To motivate our solution, we take a closer look at the ridge estimator (
w_{\text{Bayes}}^{\text{ridge}}
as in
eq.

23
), which is the Bayes-optimal estimator given the ICL task structure. We analytically characterize
e_{\text{ICL}}
and
e_{\text{IDG}}
errors for this estimator in SI Section
F
, giving

e^{\text{ridge}}_{\text{ICL}}=\rho\left(1+\frac{1}{\alpha}\mathcal{M}_{\alpha}\left(\frac{\rho}{\alpha}\right)\right)=e^{\text{ridge}}_{\text{IDG}}.

Note that the performance of the ridge estimator is identical on the ICL and IDG test distributions, meaning that it does not rely at all on memorizing task vectors.

Motivated by these arguments, we propose to measure the task generalization capability of a model by studying the difference between its performance on the ICL test distribution and its performance on the IDG test distribution. Specifically, we consider the quantity
g_{\text{task}}=e_{\text{ICL}}-e_{\text{IDG}}
for a given model or estimator. This difference being large implies the model, performing better on training tasks, has not learned the true task distribution and is not generalizing in task. Conversely, a small difference between ICL error and IDG error suggests that the model is leveraging the underlying structure of the task distribution rather than overfitting to and interpolating specific task instances seen in training.

Simulations of
g_{\text{task}}
as a function of task diversity,
\kappa=k/d
, are shown in
Figure

4
for two inference models: dMMSE estimator, and the linear transformer. The dMMSE estimator is included as a benchmark quantifying the performance of a “pure memorizer”, as it is the Bayes-optimal estimator that assumes task vectors only seen in the training test. We see that, as the task diversity parameter
\kappa
increases,
g_{\text{task}}
falls for both estimators but at very different rates.

The
g_{\text{task}}
for the dMMSE estimator falls very slowly as a function of
\kappa
. This is expected because the explicit form of
w_{\text{Bayes}}^{\text{dMMSE}}
, given in
eq.

24
, is that of a kernel smoother employing a Gaussian kernel as the weighting function around each task vector. Known results about this class of estimators show the performance of the dMMSE estimator on the ICL task suffers from the “curse of dimensionality”
[
42
,
43
,
44
]
, i.e. the required number of training samples
k=d\kappa
would need to be exponential in
d
to outperform some given
e_{\text{ICL}}
error tolerance. Intuitively, the IDG task distribution
\mathcal{P}_{\text{test}}=\mathcal{P}_{\text{train}}=\text{Unif}\{w_{1},\cdots,w_{k}\},w_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d})
and the ICL task distribution
\mathcal{P}_{\text{test}}=\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d})
are indistinguishable (by appropriate measures) only if
k
is exponentially large in
d
.
We present more formal arguments in SI Section
F
.

On the other hand, the
g_{\text{task}}
for the linear transformer estimator first behaves similarly to the dMMSE estimator, but starts falling sharply after around
\kappa=1
. How quickly does
g^{\text{LT}}_{\text{task}}
limit to 0 as

from (

32

we have the leading asymptotic behavior given by

c_{2}&\tau>1\\
\end{cases}+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{\kappa^{2}}\right)

for constants
c_{1},c_{2}
dependent on
\alpha,\tau,\rho
, provided in SI Section
F

\kappa
in the solution learned by the linear attention mechanism,
consider the regime where
\tau,\alpha\to\infty
with

\lim_{\begin{subarray}{c}\tau\to\infty\\
\alpha\to\infty\end{subarray}}g^{\text{LT}}_{\text{task}}=\begin{cases}(1-\kappa)\left(1+\frac{\rho}{1+\rho}c^{\ast}\right)\qquad&\kappa<1\\
0&\kappa>1\end{cases}\,.

(35)

This change in analytical behavior indicates a phase transition at

We conclude that the linear transformer model is a much more efficient task generalizer than the dMMSE estimator which is the Bayes-optimal memorizer, as shown in

V
Discussion

In this paper, we computed sharp asymptotics for the in-context learning (ICL) performance in a simplified model of ICL for linear regression using linear attention. This exactly solvable model demonstrates a transition from memorizing pretraining task vectors to generalizing beyond them as the diversity of pretraining tasks increases, echoing empirical findings in full Transformers
[
20
]
. Additionally, we observe a sample-wise double descent as the amount of pretraining data increases. Our numerical experiments show that full, nonlinear Transformers exhibit qualitatively similar behavior in the scaling regime relevant to our solvable model. Understanding the mechanistic underpinnings of ICL of well-controlled synthetic tasks in solvable models is an important prerequisite to understanding how it emerges from pretraining on natural data
[
21
]
.

Our paper falls within a broader program of research that seeks sharp asymptotic characterizations of the performance of machine learning algorithms. This program has a long history in statistical physics
[
46
,
47
,
41
]
, and has in recent years attracted substantial attention in machine learning
[
38
,
48
,
49
,
50
,
51
,
52
,
53
,
41
,
54
]
. For simplicity, we have assumed that the covariates in the in-context regression problem are drawn from an isotropic Gaussian distribution. However, our technical approach could be extended to anisotropic covariates, and, perhaps more interestingly, to featurized linear attention models in which the inputs are passed through some feature map before linear attention is applied
[
35
,
34
]
. This extension would be possible thanks to an appropriate form of
Gaussian universality
: for certain classes of regression problems, the asymptotic error coincides with that of a model where the true features are replaced with Gaussian features of matched mean and covariance
[
38
,
48
,
49
,
50
,
51
,
52
,
53
,
39
,
55
]
. This would allow for a theoretical characterization of ICL for realistic data structure in a closer approximation of full softmax attention, yielding more precise predictions of how performance scales in real Transformers.

In our analysis, we have assumed that the model is trained to interpolation on a fixed dataset. This allows us to cast our simplified form of linear attention pretraining as a ridge regression problem, which in turn enables our random matrix analysis. In contrast, Transformer-based large language models are usually trained in a nearly-online setting, where each gradient update is estimated using fresh examples with no repeating data
[
56
]
. Some of our findings, such as double-descent in the learning curve as a function of the number of pretraining examples, are unlikely to generalize to the fully-online setting. It will be interesting to probe these potential differences in future work.

Finally, our results have some bearing on the broad question of what architectural features are required for ICL
[
7
,
13
,
21
]
. Our work shows that a full Transformer—or indeed even full linear attention—is not required for ICL of linear regression. However, our simplified model retains the structured quadratic pairwise interaction between inputs that is at the heart of the attention mechanism. It is this quadratic interaction that allows the model to solve the ICL regression task, which it does essentially by reversing the data correlation (see §
II.4
). One would therefore hypothesize that our model is minimal in that further simplifications within this model class would impair its ability to solve this ICL task. In the specific context of regression with isotropic data, a simple point of comparison would be to fix
\Gamma=I_{d}
, which gives a pretraining-free model that should perform well when the context length is very long. However, this further-reduced model would perform poorly if the covariates of the in-context task are anisotropic. Generally, it would be interesting to investigate when models lacking this precisely-engineered quadratic interaction can learn linear regression in-context
[
57
]
, and if they’re less sample-efficient than the attention-based models considered here.

Stochastic order notation
: In our analysis, we use a concept of high-probability bounds known as

. This notion, first introduced in
[

,
59
]
, provides a convenient way to account for low-probability exceptional events where some bounds may not hold. Consider two families of nonnegative random variables:

We also use the notation

to indicate that two families of random variables

are asymptotically equivalent. Precisely,

\mathbb{P}\left[\,\mathinner{\!\left\lvert X-Y\right\rvert}>d^{-\varepsilon}\right]\leq d^{-D}

(40)

Appendix B
Moment Calculations and Generalization Errors

For a given set of parameters
\Gamma
, its generalization error is defined as

e(\Gamma)=\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{test}}}\left[\left(y_{\ell+1}-\langle\Gamma,H_{Z}\rangle\right)^{2}\right],

(41)

where
(Z,y_{\ell+1})\sim\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{test}}
is a new sample drawn from the distribution of the test data set. Recall that
Z
is the input embedding matrix defined by

\displaystyle Z=\left[\begin{array}[]{ccccc}x_{1}&x_{2}&\ldots&x_{\ell}&x_{\ell+1}\\
y_{1}&y_{2}&\ldots&y_{\ell}&0\end{array}\right]\in\mathbb{R}^{(d+1)\times(\ell+1)},

and
y_{\ell+1}
denotes the missing value to be predicted. The goal of this section is to derive an expression for the generalization error
e(\Gamma)
.

Note that the test distribution
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{test}}
crucially depends on the probability distribution of the task vector
w
used in the linear model
y=w^{\top}x+\eta
. For the ICL task, we have
w\sim\mathsf{Unif}(\mathcal{S}^{d-1}(\sqrt{d}))
, the uniform distribution on the sphere
\mathcal{S}^{d-1}(\sqrt{d})
. For the IDG task,
w
is sampled uniformly from the set
\left\{w_{1},w_{2},\ldots,w_{k}\right\}
, where these
k
vectors are the same as those used in the training data. In what follows, we slightly abuse the notation by writing
w\sim\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{test}}
to indicate that
w
is sampled from the task vector distribution associated with
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{test}}
.

You will note that the distribution
w\sim\mathsf{Unif}(\mathcal{S}^{d-1}(\sqrt{d}))
chosen above does not match the distribution
w\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d}).
For analytical tractibility and convenience, the remaining calculations will have
w\sim\mathsf{Unif}(\mathcal{S}^{d-1}(\sqrt{d}))
not
w\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d}).
For large
d
(in practice, this seems to be any
d>80
), simulations show that these choices are indistinguishable.

Let
w
be the task vector used in the input matrix
Z
. Throughout the paper, we use
\mathbb{E}_{w}\left[\cdot\right]
to denote the conditional expectation with respect to the randomness in the data vectors
\left\{x_{i}\right\}_{i\in[\ell+1]}
and the noise
\left\{\epsilon_{i}\right\}_{i\in[\ell+1]}
, with the task vector
w
kept fixed. We have the following expressions for the first two
conditional
moments of
(H_{Z},y_{\ell+1})
.

Lemma 1

(Conditional moments)
.

Let the task vector
w\in\mathcal{S}^{d-1}(\sqrt{d})
be fixed. We have

\mathbb{E}_{w}\left[y_{\ell+1}\right]=0,\quad\text{and}\quad\mathbb{E}_{w}\left[H_{Z}\right]=0.

(44)

Moreover,

\mathbb{E}_{w}\left[y_{\ell+1}H_{Z}\right]=\frac{1}{d}w\begin{bmatrix}w^{\top},&1+\rho\end{bmatrix}

(45)

and

\mathbb{E}_{w}\left[\vecop(H_{Z})\vecop(H_{Z})^{\top}\right]=\frac{(1+\rho)}{d}I_{d}\otimes\begin{bmatrix}\frac{d}{\ell}I_{d}+(1+\ell^{-1})(1+\rho)^{-1}ww^{\top}&(1+2\ell^{-1})w\\
(1+2\ell^{-1})w^{\top}&(1+2\ell^{-1})(1+\rho)\end{bmatrix}.

(46)

Proof.

Using the equivalent representations in (
229
) and (
230
), it is straightforward to verify the estimates of the first (conditional) moments in (
44
). To show (
45
), we note that

H_{Z}=(d/\ell)z_{a}z_{b}^{\top},

(47)

where

z_{a}=M_{w}\begin{bmatrix}s\\
u\end{bmatrix}\qquad\text{and}\qquad z_{b}=\begin{bmatrix}M_{w}h\\
(\theta_{w}a/\sqrt{d}+\theta_{\epsilon})^{2}/\sqrt{d}+\theta_{q}^{2}/\sqrt{d}\end{bmatrix}.

(48)

Using the representation in (
230
), we have

\mathbb{E}_{w}\left[y_{\ell+1}H_{Z}\right]=(d/\ell)\mathbb{E}_{w}\left[y_{\ell+1}z_{a}\right]\mathbb{E}_{w}\left[z_{b}^{\top}\right].

(49)

Computing the expectations
\mathbb{E}_{w}\left[y_{\ell+1}z_{a}\right]
and
\mathbb{E}_{w}\left[z_{b}^{\top}\right]
then gives us (
45
). Next, we show (
46
). Since
z_{a}
and
z_{b}
are
independent
,

\mathbb{E}\left[\vecop(H_{Z})\vecop(H_{Z})^{\top}\right]=(d/\ell)^{2}\,\mathbb{E}\left[z_{a}z_{a}^{\top}\right]\otimes\mathbb{E}\left[z_{b}z_{b}^{\top}\right].

(50)

The first expectation on the right-hand side is easy to compute. Since
M_{w}
is an orthonormal matrix,

\mathbb{E}_{w}\left[z_{a}z_{a}^{\top}\right]=I_{d}

(51)

To obtain the second expectation on the right-hand side of the above expression, we can first verify that

\mathbb{E}_{w}\left[M_{w}hh^{\top}M_{w}\right]=\frac{\ell}{d^{2}}\left[(1+\rho)I_{d}+\frac{(\ell+1)}{d}ww^{\top}\right].

(52)

Moreover,

\mathbb{E}_{w}\left[M_{w}h\left((a/\sqrt{d}+\theta_{\epsilon})^{2}/\sqrt{d}+\theta_{q}^{2}/\sqrt{d}\right)\right]=\frac{\ell(\ell+2)(1+\rho)}{d^{3}}w

(53)

and

\mathbb{E}_{w}\left[\left((a/\sqrt{d}+\theta_{\epsilon})^{2}/\sqrt{d}+\theta_{q}^{2}/\sqrt{d}\right)^{2}\right]=\frac{\ell(\ell+2)(1+\rho)^{2}}{d^{3}}.

(54)

Combining (
52
), (
53
), and (
54
), we have

\mathbb{E}\left[z_{b}z_{b}^{\top}\right]=\frac{(\ell/d)^{2}(1+\rho)}{d}\begin{bmatrix}\frac{d}{\ell}I_{d}+(1+\ell^{-1})(1+\rho)^{-1}ww^{\top}&(1+2\ell^{-1})w\\
(1+2\ell^{-1})w^{\top}&(1+2\ell^{-1})(1+\rho)\end{bmatrix}.

(55)

Substituting (
51
) and (
55
) into (
46
), we reach the formula in (
46
).
∎

Proposition 1

(Generalization error)
.

For a given weight matrix
\Gamma
, the generalization error of the linear transformer is

\displaystyle e(\Gamma)

\displaystyle=\frac{1+\rho}{d}\tr\left(\Gamma\begin{bmatrix}\frac{d}{\ell}I_{d}+(1+\ell^{-1})(1+\rho)^{-1}R_{\mathrm{test}}&(1+2\ell^{-1})b_{\mathrm{test}}\\
(1+2\ell^{-1})b_{\mathrm{test}}^{\top}&(1+2\ell^{-1})(1+\rho)\end{bmatrix}\Gamma^{\top}\right)

(56)

\displaystyle-\frac{2}{d}\tr\left(\Gamma\begin{bmatrix}R_{\mathrm{test}}\\
(1+\rho)b_{\mathrm{test}}^{\top}\end{bmatrix}\right)+1+\rho,

where

b_{\mathrm{test}}\coloneqq\mathbb{E}_{w\sim\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{test}}}\left[w\right]\quad\text{and}\quad R_{\mathrm{test}}\coloneqq\mathbb{E}_{w\sim\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{test}}}\left[ww^{\top}\right].

(57)

Remark 1
.

We use
w\sim\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{test}}
to indicate that
w
is sampled from the task vector distribution associated with
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{test}}
. For the ICL task,
w\sim\mathsf{Unif}(\mathcal{S}^{d-1}(\sqrt{d}))
. It is straightforward to check that, in this case,

\textrm{(ICL)}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\qquad b_{\mathrm{test}}=0\quad\text{and}\quad R_{\mathrm{test}}=I_{d}.

(58)

For the IDG task, we have

\text{(IDG)}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\qquad b_{\mathrm{test}}=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i}w_{i}\quad\text{and}\quad R_{\mathrm{test}}=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i\in[k]}w_{i}w_{i}^{\top},

(59)

where
\left\{w_{i}\right\}_{i\in[k]}
is the set of fixed task vectors used in the training data.

Proof.

Recall the definition of the generalization error in (
41
). We start by writing

\displaystyle e(\Gamma)=\vecop(\Gamma)^{\top}\mathbb{E}\left[\vecop(H_{Z})\vecop(H_{Z})^{\top}\right]\vecop(\Gamma)-2\vecop(\Gamma)^{\top}\vecop(\mathbb{E}\left[y_{N+1}H_{Z}\right])+\mathbb{E}\left[y_{\ell+1}^{2}\right],

(60)

where
H_{Z}
is a matrix in the form of

\displaystyle H_{Z}\coloneqq x_{\ell+1}\begin{bmatrix}\frac{d}{\ell}\sum_{i\leq\ell}y_{i}x_{i}^{\top}&\frac{1}{\ell}\sum_{i\leq\ell}y_{i}^{2}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times(d+1)},

(61)

and
H_{Z}
is independent of
\Gamma
. Since
y_{\ell+1}=x_{\ell+1}^{\top}w+\epsilon
, with
\epsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\rho)
denoting the noise, it is straightforward to check that

\mathbb{E}\left[y_{\ell+1}^{2}\right]=1+\rho.

(62)

Using the moment estimate (
46
) in Lemma
1
and the identity (
36
), we have

\displaystyle\vecop(\Gamma)^{\top}\mathbb{E}\left[\vecop(H_{Z})\vecop(H_{Z})^{\top}\right]\vecop(\Gamma)

(63)

\displaystyle=\frac{1+\rho}{d}\tr\left(\Gamma\begin{bmatrix}\frac{d}{\ell}I_{d}+(1+\ell^{-1})(1+\rho)^{-1}R_{\mathrm{test}}&(1+2\ell^{-1})b_{\mathrm{test}}\\
(1+2\ell^{-1})b_{\mathrm{test}}^{\top}&(1+2\ell^{-1})(1+\rho)\end{bmatrix}\Gamma^{\top}\right).

Moreover, by (
45
),

\vecop(\Gamma)^{\top}\vecop\left(\mathbb{E}\left[y_{\ell+1}H_{Z}\right]\right)=\frac{1}{d}\tr\left(\Gamma\begin{bmatrix}R_{\mathrm{test}}\\
(1+\rho)b_{\mathrm{test}}^{\top}\end{bmatrix}\right).

(64)

∎

Corollary 1
.

For a given set of parameters
\Gamma
, its generalization error can be written as

e(\Gamma)=\frac{1}{d}\tr\left(\Gamma B_{\mathrm{test}}\Gamma^{\top}\right)-\frac{2}{d}\tr\left(\Gamma A_{\mathrm{test}}^{\top}\right)+(1+\rho)+\mathcal{E},

(65)

where

A_{\mathrm{test}}\coloneqq\begin{bmatrix}R_{\mathrm{test}}&(1+\rho)b_{\mathrm{test}}\end{bmatrix},

(66)

B_{\mathrm{test}}\coloneqq\begin{bmatrix}\frac{1}{\alpha}(1+\rho)I_{d}+R_{\mathrm{test}}&(1+\rho)b_{\mathrm{test}}\\
(1+\rho)b_{\mathrm{test}}^{\top}&(1+\rho)^{2}\end{bmatrix},

(67)

and
R_{\mathrm{test}},b_{\mathrm{test}}
are as defined in (
57
). Moreover,
\mathcal{E}
denotes an “error” term such that

\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\mathcal{E}\right\rvert}\leq\frac{C_{\alpha,\rho}\max\left\{\mathinner{\!\left\lVert R_{\mathrm{test}}\right\rVert}_{\mathsf{op}},\mathinner{\!\left\lVert b_{\mathrm{test}}\right\rVert},1\right\}\left(\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\Gamma\right\rVert}_{\mathsf{F}}^{2}/d\right)}{d},

(68)

where
C_{\alpha,\rho}
is some constant that only depends on
\alpha
and
\rho
.

Proof.

Let

\Delta=\begin{bmatrix}\frac{d}{\ell}(1+\rho)I_{d}+(1+\ell^{-1})R_{\mathrm{test}}&(1+2\ell^{-1})(1+\rho)b_{\mathrm{test}}\\
(1+2\ell^{-1})(1+\rho)b_{\mathrm{test}}^{\top}&(1+2\ell^{-1})(1+\rho)^{2}\end{bmatrix}-B_{\mathrm{test}}.

(69)

It is straightforward to check that

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{d}\tr\left(\Gamma\Delta\Gamma^{\top}\right)

(70)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{d}\vecop(\Gamma)^{\top}(I_{d}\otimes\Delta)\vecop(\Gamma)

(71)

\displaystyle\leq\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\Delta\right\rVert}_{\mathsf{op}}\frac{\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\Gamma\right\rVert}_{\mathsf{F}}^{2}}{d}.

(72)

The bound in (
68
) follows from the estimate that
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\Delta\right\rVert}_{\mathsf{op}}\leq C_{\alpha,\rho}\max\left\{\mathinner{\!\left\lVert R_{\mathrm{test}}\right\rVert}_{\mathsf{op}},\mathinner{\!\left\lVert b_{\mathrm{test}}\right\rVert},1\right\}/d
.
∎

Remark 2
.

Consider the optimal weight matrix
\Gamma^{\ast}
obtained by solving the ridge regression problem in

\displaystyle\Gamma^{\ast}

\displaystyle=\underset{\Gamma}{\arg\,\min}\,\sum_{\mu=1}^{n}\left(y_{\ell+1}^{\mu}-\langle\Gamma,H_{Z^{\mu}}\rangle\right)^{2}+\frac{n}{d}\lambda\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\Gamma\right\rVert}_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}\,.

(73)

Since
\Gamma^{\ast}
is the optimal solution of (
73
), we must have

\frac{n}{d}\lambda\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\Gamma^{\ast}\right\rVert}_{\mathsf{F}}^{2}\leq\sum_{\mu\in[n]}(y_{\ell+1}^{\mu})^{2},

(74)

where the right-hand side is the value of the objective function of (
73
) when we choose
\Gamma
to be the all-zero matrix. It follows that

\frac{\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\Gamma^{\ast}\right\rVert}_{\mathsf{F}}^{2}}{d}\leq\frac{\sum_{\mu\in[n]}(y_{\ell+1}^{\mu})^{2}}{\lambda\,n}.

(75)

By the law of large numbers,
\frac{\sum_{\mu\in[n]}y_{\mu}^{2}}{n}\to 1+\rho
as
n\to\infty
. Thus,
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\Gamma^{\ast}\right\rVert}_{\mathsf{F}}^{2}/{d}
is asymptotically bounded by the constant
(1+\rho)/\lambda
. Furthermore, it is easy to check that
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert R_{\mathrm{test}}\right\rVert}_{\mathsf{op}}=\mathcal{O}(1)
and
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert b_{\mathrm{test}}\right\rVert}=\mathcal{O}(1)
for both ICL [see (
58
)] and IDG [see (
59
)]. It then follows from Corollary
1
that the generalization error associated with the optimal parameers
\Gamma^{\ast}
is asymptotically determined by the first three terms on the right-hand side of (
65
).

Appendix C
Analysis of Ridge Regression: Extended Resolvent Matrices

We see from Corollary
1
and Remark
2
that the two key quantities in determining the generalization error
e(\Gamma^{\ast})
are

\frac{1}{d}\tr(\Gamma^{\ast}A_{\mathrm{test}}^{\top})\qquad\text{and}\qquad\frac{1}{d}\tr(\Gamma^{\ast}B_{\mathrm{test}}(\Gamma^{\ast})^{\top}),

(76)

where
A_{\mathrm{test}}
and
B_{\mathrm{test}}
are the matrices defined in (
66
) and (
67
), respectively. In this section, we show that the two quantities in (
76
) can be obtained by studying a parameterized family of extended resolvent matrices.

To start, we observe that the ridge regression problem in (
73
) admits the following closed-form solution:

\vecop(\Gamma^{\ast})=G\left(\textstyle\sum_{\mu\in[n]}y_{\mu}\vecop({H_{\mu}})\right)/d,

(77)

where
G
is a resolvent matrix defined as

G=\left(\textstyle\sum_{\mu\in[n]}\vecop({H_{\mu}})\vecop({H_{\mu}})^{\top}/d+\tau\lambda I\right)^{-1}.

(78)

For our later analysis of the generalization error, we need to consider a more general, “parameterized” version of
G
, defined as

G(\pi)=\left(\textstyle\sum_{\mu\in[n]}\vecop({H_{\mu}})\vecop({H_{\mu}})^{\top}/d+\pi\Pi+\tau\lambda I\right)^{-1},

(79)

where
\Pi\in\mathbb{R}^{(d^{2}+d)\times(d^{2}+d)}
is a symmetric positive-semidefinite matrix and
\pi
is a nonnegative scalar. The original resolvent
G
in (
78
) is a special case, corresponding to
\pi=0
.

The objects in (
77
) and (
79
) are the submatrices of an
extended
resolvent matrix, which we construct as follows. For each
\mu\in[n]
, let

z_{\mu}=\begin{bmatrix}y_{\mu}/d\\
\vecop(H_{\mu})/\sqrt{d}\end{bmatrix}

(80)

be an
(d^{2}+d+1)
-dimensional vector. Let

\Pi_{e}=\begin{bmatrix}0&\\
&\Pi\end{bmatrix},

(81)

where
\Pi
is the
(d^{2}+d)\times(d^{2}+d)
matrix in (
79
).
Define an extended resolvent matrix

G_{e}(\pi)=\frac{1}{\sum_{\mu\in[n]}z_{\mu}z_{\mu}^{\top}+\pi\Pi_{e}+\tau\lambda I}.

(82)

By block-matrix inversion, it is straightforward to check that

G_{e}(\pi)=\begin{bmatrix}c(\pi)&-c(\pi)q^{\top}(\pi)\\
-c(\pi)q(\pi)&G(\pi)+c(\pi)q(\pi)q^{\top}(\pi)\end{bmatrix},

(83)

where

q(\pi)\coloneqq\frac{1}{d^{3/2}}G(\pi)\left(\textstyle\sum_{\mu\in[n]}y_{\mu}\vecop({H_{\mu}})\right)

(84)

is a vector in
\mathbb{R}^{d(d+1)}
, and

is a scalar such that

\frac{1}{c(\pi)}=\frac{1}{d^{2}}\sum_{\mu\in[n]}y_{\mu}^{2}+\tau\lambda-\frac{1}{d^{3}}\sum_{\mu,\nu\in[n]}y_{\mu}y_{\nu}\vecop(H_{\mu})^{\top}G(\pi)\vecop(H_{\nu}).

(85)

By comparing (
84
) with (
77
), we see that

\vecop(\Gamma^{\ast})=\sqrt{d}\,q(0).

(86)

Moreover, as shown in the following lemma, the two key quantities in (
76
) can also be obtained from the extended resolvent
G_{e}(\pi)
.

Lemma 2
.

For any matrix
A\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times(d+1)}
,

\frac{1}{d}\tr(\Gamma^{\ast}A^{\top})=\frac{-1}{c(0)\sqrt{d}}\begin{bmatrix}0&\vecop(A)^{T}\end{bmatrix}G_{e}(0)e_{1},

(87)

where
e_{1}
denotes the first natural basis vector in
\mathbb{R}^{d^{2}+d+1}
. Moreover, for any symmetric and positive semidefinite matrix
B\in\mathbb{R}^{(d+1)\times(d+1)}
, if we set

\Pi=I_{d}\otimes B

(88)

in (
81
), then

\frac{1}{d}\tr(\Gamma^{\ast}B(\Gamma^{\ast})^{\top})=\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\pi}\left(\frac{1}{c(\pi)}\right)\biggr|_{\pi=0}.

(89)

Proof.

The identity (
87
) follows immediately from the block form of
G_{e}(\pi)
in (
83
) and the observation in (
86
). To show (
89
), we take the derivative of
1/c(\pi)
with respect to
\pi
. From (
85
), and using the identity

\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\pi}G(\pi)=-G(\pi)\Pi G(\pi),

(90)

we have

\displaystyle\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\pi}\left(\frac{1}{c(\pi)}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{d^{3}}\sum_{\mu,\nu\in[n]}y_{\mu}y_{\nu}\vecop(H_{\mu})^{\top}G(\pi)\Pi G(\pi)\vecop(H_{\nu})

(91)

\displaystyle=q^{\top}(\pi)\Pi q(\pi).

(92)

Thus, by (
86
),

\displaystyle\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\pi}\left(\frac{1}{c(\pi)}\right)\biggr|_{\pi=0}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{d}\left(\vecop(\Gamma^{\ast})\right)^{\top}\Pi\vecop(\Gamma^{\ast})

(93)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{d}\left(\vecop(\Gamma^{\ast})\right)^{\top}\left(I_{d}\otimes B\right)\vecop(\Gamma^{\ast}).

(94)

Applying the identity in (
36
) to the right-hand side of the above equation, we reach (
89
).
∎

Remark 3
.

To lighten the notation, we will often write

[resp.

[resp.

Remark 4
.

In light of (

Remark 5

F.3
Asymptotics of the dMMSE estimator in
\kappa

To study the slowness of the dMMSE estimator more explicitly, consider the

e^{-\frac{1}{2\rho}\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\left(y_{i}-w_{j}^{\top}x_{i}\right)^{2}}\to e^{-\frac{\ell}{2\rho}\left(\frac{1}{d}\|w^{\ast}-w_{j}\|^{2}+\rho\right)}\,,

and these weightings exponentially favor choosing
\omega_{j}
that minimizes
\|w^{\ast}-w_{j}\|^{2}
over the set of
k
training tasks

. It’s immediately clear that

in this limit as the minimal value of

when
w^{\ast}\in\{w_{1},\cdots,w_{k}\}
is 0. Taking

we have

\displaystyle g^{\text{dMMSE}}_{\text{task}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{d}\mathbb{E}_{w^{\ast}\sim\mathcal{P}_{\text{test}}}\left[\mathbb{E}_{w_{i}\sim\mathcal{P}_{\text{train}}}\left[\min_{i\in[k]}\|w^{\ast}-w_{i}\|^{2}\right]\right].

(210)

It is equivalent in high-dimensions to work with
w^{\ast},w_{i}
sampled uniformly on the sphere
\mathcal{S}^{d-1}(\sqrt{d}).
For analytical tractability, we will proceed with this. We exploit spherical symmetry in both
w^{\ast}
and
w_{i}
to simplify

\displaystyle\|w^{\ast}-w_{i}\|^{2}

\displaystyle=2d-2\sqrt{d}x_{i}\qquad\text{ for $x_{i}$ a single component of vector $w_{i}\sim\text{Unif}(\mathcal{S}^{d-1}(\sqrt{d}))$}

(211)

\displaystyle=2d-2\sqrt{d}(2\sqrt{d}\,b_{i}-\sqrt{d})\qquad\text{for $b_{i}\sim\text{Beta}\left(\frac{d-1}{2},\frac{d-1}{2}\right)$}

(212)

\displaystyle=4d-4db_{i}

(213)

This gives

\displaystyle g_{\text{task}}

\displaystyle=\rho+4-4\,\mathbb{E}_{b_{i}\sim\text{Beta}\left(\frac{d-1}{2},\frac{d-1}{2}\right)}\left[\max_{i\in[k]}b_{i}\right].

(214)

We wish to study the behavior of this expected maximum of a set of
k
Beta variables as
k
grows large. We are thus interested in calculating

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{b_{i}\sim\text{Beta}\left(\frac{d-1}{2},\frac{d-1}{2}\right)}\left[\max_{i\in[k]}b_{i}\right],

(215)

Let
D=\frac{d-1}{2}
. Following the standard procedure for deriving the PDF for the distribution of the extreme value, have

\displaystyle p(b_{i}=x)=\frac{1}{B(D)}(1-x)^{D-1}x^{D-1}

(216)

where

\displaystyle B(D)=\frac{\Gamma(D)^{2}}{\Gamma(2D)}.

(217)

Then

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(b_{i}\leq M,\forall i\in[k])=\left(\frac{1}{B(D)}\int_{0}^{M}dx(1-x)^{D-1}x^{D-1}\right)^{k}

(218)

and

\displaystyle p(\max_{i\in[k]}b_{i}=M)=\frac{k}{B(D)}M^{D-1}(1-M)^{D-1}\left(\frac{1}{B(D)}\int_{0}^{M}dx\,(1-x)^{D-1}x^{D-1}\right)^{k-1}

(219)

and

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{b_{i}\sim\text{Beta}\left(\frac{d-1}{2},\frac{d-1}{2}\right)}\left[\max_{i\in[k]}b_{i}\right]=\frac{k}{B(D)}\int_{0}^{1}dMM^{D}(1-M)^{D-1}\left(\frac{1}{B(D)}\int_{0}^{M}dx\,(1-x)^{D-1}x^{D-1}\right)^{k-1}

(220)

Note that the integral can be written as

\displaystyle\frac{k}{B(D)}\int_{0}^{1}dM\,M^{D}(1-M)^{D-1}\exp\left((k-1)\log\frac{1}{B(D)}\int_{0}^{M}dx\,(1-x)^{D-1}x^{D-1}\right)

(221)

To proceed, let’s consider the variable substitution

\displaystyle e^{-t}=\frac{1}{B(D)}\int_{0}^{M}dx\,(1-x)^{D-1}x^{D-1}.

(222)

By this definition,
t(M=0)=\infty
,
t(M=1)=0

Plugging in

\displaystyle k\int_{0}^{\infty}dt\,M(t)e^{-kt}.

(223)

Naively, it looks like one could get a simple series expansion in
1/k
through integration by parts, but this will fail because
\left.\frac{dM}{dt}\right|_{t=0}=\infty
for
D>1
. One can see this by differentiating (
222
) with respect to
t
. Instead, have

\displaystyle k\int_{0}^{\infty}dt\,M(t)e^{-kt}=\int_{0}^{\infty}dt\,M(t/k)e^{-t}=1-\int_{0}^{\infty}dt\,\epsilon(t/k)e^{-t}

(224)

where we defined

\displaystyle\epsilon(t/k)\equiv 1-M(t/k).

(225)

Now, we can expand
\epsilon(t/k)
and integrate. We have to find an appropriate series expansion from the implicit definition of
\epsilon(t/k)
.

\displaystyle e^{-t/k}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{B(D)}\int_{0}^{1-\epsilon(t/k)}dx\,(1-x)^{D-1}x^{D-1}

(226)

To get the leading behavior, one proceeds by expanding in leading order to
1-M
or the right and to

on the left

\displaystyle 1-\frac{t}{k}=1-\frac{\epsilon(t/k)^{D}}{B(D)}\quad\implies\epsilon\approx\left(\frac{B(D)t}{k}\right)^{1/D}

(227)

which in turn implies the leading behavior of the expectation we are looking for is

\displaystyle 1-\frac{B(D)^{1/D}\Gamma(1/D+1)}{k^{1/D}}

(228)

From this calculation we recover that the decrease of
g_{\text{task}}^{\text{dMMSE}}
towards 0 is explicitly dimensionally cursed at
\alpha\to\infty
. We expect a similar rate to hold for finite
\alpha
by the curse-of-dimensionality arguments given previously.
