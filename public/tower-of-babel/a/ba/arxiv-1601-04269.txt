Co-Poisson structures on polynomial Hopf algebras
Qi Lou, Quanshui Wu

Abstract
. The Hopf dual H ∘ H^{\circ} of any Poisson Hopf algebra H H is proved to be a co-Poisson Hopf algebra provided H H is noetherian. Without noetherian assumption, unlike it is claimed in literature, the statement does not hold. It is proved that there is no nontrivial Poisson Hopf structure on the universal enveloping algebra of a non-abelian Lie algebra. So the polynomial Hopf algebra, viewed as the universal enveloping algebra of a finite-dimensional abelian Lie algebra, is considered. The Poisson Hopf structures on polynomial Hopf algebras are exactly linear Poisson structures. The co-Poisson structures on polynomial Hopf algebras are characterized. Some correspondences between co-Poisson and Poisson structures are also established.

1.
Introduction

Poisson structure naturally appears in classical/quantum mechanics, in
mathematical physics, and
in deformation theory. It is an important algebra structure in Poisson
geometry, algebraic geometry and non-commutative geometry. There are lots of research in the related subjects.

Co-Poisson structure is a dual concept of Poisson structure in
categorial point of view. It arises also in mathematics and mathematical physics naturally as explained in the next two paragraphs.

Let
G
be a Lie group and
\mathcal{O}(G)
be its algebra of functions.
A Lie group
G
is said to be a Poisson Lie group if
\mathcal{O}(G)
is a Poisson Hopf algebra.
It is well-know that the category of connected and simply-connected Lie groups is equivalent to the category of finite-dimensional Lie algebras.
In this case,
\mathcal{O}(G)
is identified with the Hopf dual
U(\mathfrak{g})^{\circ}
of the universal enveloping algebra
U(\mathfrak{g})
, where
\mathfrak{g}

is the corresponding Lie algebra of
G
.
The Poisson counterpart of this fact holds also, namely, the category of connected and simply-connected
Poisson Lie groups is equivalent to the category of finite-dimensional Lie bialgebras (
[
KS
, Theorem 3.3.1]
or
[
Dr
, Theorem 1]
).
However, the Lie bialgebra structures on any Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
is in one-to-one correspondence with the co-Poisson Hopf structures on
U(\mathfrak{g})
(
[
CP
, Proposition 6.2.3]
).

On the other hand, to quantize a Lie group or Lie algebra one should equip it with an extra structure, namely, a Poisson Lie group structure or Lie bialgebra structure, respectively.
Therefore co-Poisson structure naturally appears in the theory of quantum groups and in mathematical physics.

If
G
is a connected and simply-connected Poisson Lie group and
\mathfrak{g}
is the corresponding Lie bialgebra,
then the Poisson Hopf structure on
U(\mathfrak{g})^{\circ}\cong\mathcal{O}(G)
is the dual of the co-Poisson Hopf structures on
U(\mathfrak{g})
.
In
[
OP
]
, the authors proved that the dual Hopf algebra
U(\mathfrak{g})^{\circ}
of
U(\mathfrak{g})
is a Poisson Hopf algebra for any finite-dimensional
Lie bialgebra
\mathfrak{g}
. In fact, in general, as stated in
[
KS
, Proposition 3.1.5]
earlier, the dual Hopf algebra of any co-Poisson Hopf algebra is a Poisson Hopf algebra.
A complete proof is given in a recent paper by Oh
[
Oh
, Theorem 2.2]
. The dual proposition that the dual Hopf algebra of any Poisson Hopf algebra is a co-Poisson Hopf algebra is also stated in
[
KS
, Proposition 3.1.5]
.
Unfortunately, this claim is not true in general as showed in our Example
3.6
. Under an additional assumption that the algebra is noetherian,
we prove the statement is true in Proposition
3.5
.

We prove that there is no nontrivial Poisson Hopf structure on the universal enveloping algebra of a non-abelian Lie algebra in Proposition
2.5
.
So, we turn to consider in latter sections the abelian case, i.e., the polynomial Hopf algebra
A=k[x_{1},x_{2},\cdots,x_{d}]
, viewed as the universal enveloping algebra
of an abelian Lie algebra of dimension
d
.
The Poisson Hopf structures on
A=k[x_{1},x_{2},\cdots,x_{d}]

are exactly linear Poisson structures on
A
(see Proposition
5.3
).
By establishing a reciprocity law between two linear maps of
A\to A\otimes A
for
A=k[x_{1},x_{2},\cdots,x_{d}]
(see Proposition
4.3
),
all co-Poisson coalgebra and co-Poisson Hopf algebra structures on
A=k[x_{1},x_{2},\cdots,x_{d}]

are described in Theorem
4.7
and Proposition
5.4
respectively. In particular, the co-Poisson coalgebra structures on
A=k[x,y]
are given by the linear maps
I:A\to k(x\otimes y-y\otimes x)
(see Proposition
4.8
). By using the algebra of divided power series, the co-Poisson coalgebra structures on
A=k[x_{1},x_{2},\cdots,x_{d}]
are showed to be in one-to-one correspondence with the Poisson algebra structures on
\tilde{A}=k[[x_{1},x_{2},\cdots,x_{d}]]
, the algebra of formal power series in Theorem
5.8
.

The paper is organized as follows. The definitions of (co-)Poisson (co)algebras are recalled in Section 2. Some preliminary results and examples are also given in Section 2.
In Section 3, we establish some dual properties between co-Poisson structures and Poisson structures.
In Section 4, we characterize co-Poisson coalgebra structures on polynomial Hopf algebra.
In Section 5, we characterize co-Poisson Hopf structures on polynomial Hopf algebras.

2.
Poisson structures and co-Poisson structures

2.1.
Poisson algebras and Poisson Hopf algebras

Definition 2.1
.

[
Li
,
Wei
]

An algebra
A
equipped with a linear map
\{-,-\}:A\otimes A\to A
is called a
Poisson algebra
if

(1)

A
with
\{-,-\}:A\otimes A\to A
is a Lie algebra.

(2)

\{-,c\}:A\to A
is a derivation with respect to the multiplication of
A
for all
c\in A
, that is,

\{ab,c\}=a\{b,c\}+\{a,c\}b
for all
a,b\in A
.

It should be noted that we don’t assume that
A
is commutative in general.
As showed in
[
FL
, Theorem 1.2]
, if
A
is prime and not commutative, then any nontrivial
Poisson structure
\{-,-\}
on
A
is the commutator bracket
[-,-]
up to some scalar
\alpha
in the center of the Martindale right quotient ring of
A
,
where
\alpha
is essentially determined by a bimodule morphism from some nonzero ideal to

.

Proposition 2.2
.

[
FL
, Theorem 1.2]

Let
A
be a Poisson algebra with
\{A,A\}\neq 0
and
[A,A]\neq 0
.
If
A
is prime, then

(1)

for any
a,b\in A
, there is an isomorphism
of
A
-bimodules

f_{(a,b)}:A[a,b]A\to A\{a,b\}A,[a,b]\mapsto\{a,b\};

(2)

in the Martindale right quotient ring of
A
,
\{-,-\}=\alpha[-,-]
, where
\alpha
is represented by

f_{(a,b)}
for any
a,b\in A
such that
[a,b]\neq 0.

Proof.

(1)
By using Leibniz rule and calculating
\{ac,bd\}
in both variables (see
[
Vo
, Theorem 1]
or
[
FL
, Lemma 1.1]
), it follows that,
for all
a,b,c,d\in A
,

[a,b]\{c,d\}=\{a,b\}[c,d].

(2.1)

Then, for all
a,b,c,d,e\in A
,

[a,b]\{ec,d\}=\{a,b\}[ec,d]
, which implies that

[a,b]e\{c,d\}=\{a,b\}e[c,d].

(2.2)

Since
[A,A]\neq 0
,
\{A,A\}\neq 0
and
A
is
prime, then
[a,b]=0
if and only if
\{a,b\}=0
for any
a,b\in A
.

Suppose
[a,b]\neq 0
. If
\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}[a,b]y_{i}=0
, then
for any
c\in A
,

0=\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\{a,b\}c)x_{i}[a,b]y_{i}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}[a,b]c(x_{i}\{a,b\}y_{i}).

Then
\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}\{a,b\}y_{i}=0
as
A
is prime and
[a,b]\neq 0
. This shows that the map

f_{(a,b)}:A[a,b]A\to A\{a,b\}A,~~\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}[a,b]y_{i}\mapsto\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}\{a,b\}y_{i}

is well-defined.
So,
f_{(a,b)}
is an

A
-bimodule morphism, and is in fact an isomorphism.

(2) If
[a,b]\neq 0
, then the bimodule morphism
f_{(a,b)}:A[a,b]A\to A,[a,b]\mapsto\{a,b\}

represents an element in the center of the Martindale right quotient ring of
A
(
[
MR
, 10.3.5]
or
[
He
, Chapter 1, §3]
).
It follows from (
2.2
) that
f_{(a,b)}
and
f_{(c,d)}
represent the same element, say
\alpha
, in the Martindale right quotient ring of
A
if both
[a,b]
and
[c,d]

are nonzero. Then
\{-,-\}=\alpha[-,-]
in the Matindale right quotient ring of
A
.
∎

By Proposition
2.2
, any
Poisson algebra structure
\{-,-\}
on the Weyl algebra
A_{n}(k)
or the matrix algebra
M_{n}(k)
is
\alpha[-,-]
for some
\alpha\in k
.

Commutative Poisson algebras appear naturally in geometry and algebra.
For more examples of commutative Poisson algebras, see
[

]
,
[

]

]

Definition 2.3
.

(
[
CP
, Definition 6.2.1]
)
Let
(H,\mu,\eta,\Delta,\varepsilon,S)
be a Hopf algebra with a
linear map
\{-,-\}:H\otimes H\to H
. Then
H
is called a
Poisson Hopf algebra
if

(1)

(H,\{-,-\})
is a Poisson algebra.

(2)

The structures are compatible in the sense that, for all
a,b\in H
,

\displaystyle\Delta\left(\{a,b\}\right)=\sum\{a_{1},b_{1}\}\otimes a_{2}b_{2}+\sum a_{1}b_{1}\otimes\{a_{2},b_{2}\}.

(2.3)

Let
A
and
B
be two Poisson algebras. An algebra morphism
f:A\to B
is called a
Poisson algebra morphism
if
f\left(\{a,b\}_{A}\right)=\{f(a),f(b)\}_{B}
for all
a,b\in A
.

Equation (
2.3
) means that
\Delta:H\to H\otimes H
is a Poisson
algebra morphism when
H
is commutative by the following lemma.

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
A
and
B
be two commutative Poisson algebras. There is
naturally a Poisson structure
\{-,-\}_{A\otimes B}
on the tensor
product algebra
A\otimes B
given by

\{a\otimes b,a^{\prime}\otimes b^{\prime}\}_{A\otimes B}=\{a,a^{\prime}\}_{A}\otimes bb^{\prime}+aa^{\prime}\otimes\{b,b^{\prime}\}_{B}.

It is easy to see that there is no nontrivial Poisson Hopf algebra structure on any group algebra
k[G]
.
There is also no nontrivial Poisson Hopf structure on the universal enveloping algebra
U(\mathfrak{g})

if
\mathfrak{g}
is a non-abelian Lie algebra.

Proposition 2.5
.

Let
\mathfrak{g}
be a non-abelian Lie algebra over a field of characteristic
\neq 2
. Then there is no nontrivial Poisson Hopf structure on
U(\mathfrak{g})
.

Proof.

Suppose
\{-,-\}:U(\mathfrak{g})\otimes U(\mathfrak{g})\to U(\mathfrak{g})
is a non-trivial Poisson Hopf structure on
U(\mathfrak{g})
.
Since
\mathfrak{g}
is non-abelian and
U(\mathfrak{g})
is prime, then by Proposition
2.2
, for any
a,b\in\mathfrak{g}
,
[a,b]=0
if and only if
\{a,b\}=0
.
Recall the compatible condition (
2.3
), i.e.,
for all
x,y\in U(\mathfrak{g})
,

\Delta\{x,y\}=x_{1}y_{1}\otimes\{x_{2},y_{2}\}+\{x_{1},y_{1}\}\otimes x_{2}y_{2}.

This implies that if both
x
and
y
are primitive then so is
\{x,y\}
.
If we take
x=ab
and
y=cd
for
a,b,c,d\in\mathfrak{g}
, the compatible condition (
2.3
) will imply that

[b,c]\otimes\{a,d\}+[b,d]\otimes\{a,c\}

is skew-symmetric for all
a,b,c,d\in\mathfrak{g}
.
So,
[b,c]\otimes\{a,b\}
is skew-symmetric by taking
d=b
.
Suppose
[b,c]\neq 0
as
[\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{g}]\neq 0
. Since both
[b,c]
and
\{a,b\}
are primitive,
then
\{a,b\}=0
for all
a\in\mathfrak{g}
as
\charac k\neq 2
. In particular,
\{c,b\}=0
, and so,
[c,b]=0
, which is a contradiction.
∎

As pointed out in
[

, Remark 3.1.4]
, if

is a commutative
Poisson Hopf algebra, then the counit
\varepsilon:H\to k
is a
Poisson algebra morphism, and the antipode

is a Poisson
algebra anti-morphism. Here is a proof of the facts (see also
[

, Lemma 4.2]
).

Lemma 2.6
.

Let
H
be a Poisson Hopf algebra.
Then the counit
\varepsilon:H\to k
is a Poisson algebra morphism.
The antipode
S:H\to H
is a Poisson algebra anti-morphism provided
H
is commutative.

Proof.

We need to show
\varepsilon\left(\{a,b\}\right)=0
and
S\left(\{a,b\}\right)=\{S(b),S(a)\}
for all
a,b\in H
.
Since
\varepsilon(h)=\varepsilon(h_{1})\varepsilon(h_{2})
for all
h\in H
, by (
2.3
),

\varepsilon\left(\{a,b\}\right)=\varepsilon\left(\{a_{1},b_{1}\}\right)\varepsilon(a_{2}b_{2})+\varepsilon(a_{1}b_{1})\varepsilon\left(\{a_{2},b_{2}\}\right)=2\varepsilon\left(\{a,b\}\right).

Thus
\varepsilon\left(\{a,b\}\right)=0
. For the second assertion,

\displaystyle\{S(b),S(a)\}=

\displaystyle\{S(b_{1}),S(a)\}b_{2}S(b_{3})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-S(b_{1})\{b_{2},S(a)\}S(b_{3})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-S(b_{1})\{b_{2},S(a_{1})\}a_{2}S(a_{3})S(b_{3})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle S(b_{1})S(a_{1})\{b_{2},a_{2}\}S(a_{3})S(b_{3})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-S(b_{1})S(a_{1})b_{2}a_{2}S\left(\{b_{3},a_{3}\}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle S\left(\{a,b\}\right),

where the second last
``="
follows from the fact
\{a_{1},b_{1}\}S(a_{2}b_{2})+a_{1}b_{1}S\left(\{a_{2},b_{2}\}\right)=0
for all
a,b\in H
, and the last
``="
follows from the commutativity of
H
.
∎

2.2.
Co-Poisson coalgebras and co-Poisson Hopf algebras

The concepts of co-Poisson coalgebra and co-Poisson Hopf algebra are
introduced in
[
CP
, Chapter 6]
to study quantizations of Lie
bialgebras. They are dual to Poisson algebra and Poisson Hopf algebra.

Definition 2.7
.

(
[
CP
, Definition 6.2.2]
)
A coalgebra
(C,\Delta,\varepsilon)
equipped with a linear map
q:C\to C\otimes C
is
called a
co-Poisson coalgebra
if

(1)

C
with
q:C\to C\otimes C
is a Lie coalgebra, that is,

\displaystyle(1+t_{2})\circ q

\displaystyle=0,

(skew-symmetric)

\displaystyle(1+t_{3}+t_{3}^{2})\circ(q\otimes 1)\circ q

\displaystyle=0.

(co-Jacobi
identity)

(2)

(\Delta\otimes 1)q=(1\otimes q)\Delta-t_{3}^{2}(q\otimes 1)\Delta
.      (co-Leibniz rule)

In co-Poisson coalgebra
C
, we use the sigma notations

\Delta(c)=\sum c_{1}\otimes c_{2},\,q(c)=\sum c_{(1)}\otimes c_{(2)}\,\,\textrm{and}\,\,(q\otimes 1)q(c)=\sum c_{(1)}\otimes c_{(2)}\otimes c_{(3)}

where
\sum
is often omitted in the computations. Then,

(1\otimes q)q(c)=(1\otimes q)(-c_{(2)}\otimes c_{(1)})=-c_{(3)}\otimes c_{(1)}\otimes c_{(2)}=c_{(3)}\otimes c_{(2)}\otimes c_{(1)}.

Remark 2.8
.

By using the sigma notation, the co-Leibniz rule reads as

\sum c_{(1)1}\otimes c_{(1)2}\otimes c_{(2)}=\sum c_{1}\otimes c_{2(1)}\otimes c_{2(2)}-\sum c_{1(2)}\otimes c_{2}\otimes c_{1(1)}

for all
c\in C
. It is equivalent to

(1\otimes\Delta)q=(q\otimes 1)\Delta-t_{3}(1\otimes q)\Delta.

(2.4)

If the coalgebra
C
is cocommutative, then the co-Leibniz rule is
also equivalent to

(\Delta\otimes 1)q=(1-t_{3})(1\otimes q)\Delta.

(2.5)

The cocommutator
\Delta^{\prime}
gives a co-Poisson structure on any coalgebra
(C,\Delta,\varepsilon)
.

It follows from
the co-Leibniz rule that there is no nontrivial co-Poisson coalgebra structure on any group coalgebra
k[G]
.

Definition 2.9
.

Let
(C,\Delta,\varepsilon)
be a coalgebra. A linear map
d:C\to C
is called a

if, for all

,

\Delta(d(c))=d(c_{1})\otimes c_{2}+c_{1}\otimes d(c_{2}).

Example 2.10
.

Let
C
be a cocommutative coalgebra,

and

be two coderivations of

such that
d_{1}d_{2}=d_{2}d_{1}
. Let

be the map

, where

q(c)=d_{1}(c_{1})\otimes d_{2}(c_{2})-d_{2}(c_{1})\otimes d_{1}(c_{2}).

Then

is a co-Poisson coalgebra.

Dual to the fact
\{a,-\}:A\to A
is a derivation in any Poisson algebra

is the following.

Let

Definition 2.13
.

[

(2.6)

Co-Poisson Hopf structures on

[

On the other hand, by the co-Leibniz rule,

\displaystyle h_{1}\otimes h_{2}\otimes h_{3(1)1}\otimes h_{3(1)2}\otimes h_{3(2)}\otimes h_{4}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle h_{1}\otimes h_{2}\otimes h_{3}\otimes h_{4(1)}\otimes h_{4(2)}\otimes h_{5}-h_{1}\otimes h_{2}\otimes h_{3(2)}\otimes h_{4}\otimes h_{3(1)}\otimes h_{5}.

By Lemma

Thus

\displaystyle S(h_{1})h_{3(1)}S(h_{5})\otimes S(h_{2})h_{3(2)}S(h_{4})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle S(h_{1})h_{4(2)}S(h_{5})\otimes S(h_{2})h_{3}S(h_{4(1)})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle S(h_{1})h_{2(2)}S(h_{3})\otimes S(h_{2(1)})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle S(h_{(1)})\otimes S(h_{(2)}).

It remains to show that
S(h_{1})h_{3(2)}S(h_{5})\otimes S(h_{2})h_{3(1)}S(h_{4})=q\left(S(h)\right)
.
Since
0=q(h_{1}S(h_{2}))=q(h_{1})\Delta\left(S(h_{2})\right)+\Delta(h_{1})q\left(S(h_{2})\right)
for any
h\in H
, then

h_{1(1)}S(h_{3})\otimes h_{1(2)}S(h_{2})+h_{1}S(h_{3})_{(1)}\otimes h_{2}S(h_{3})_{(2)}=0.

Hence

h_{1}\otimes h_{3}S(h_{5})_{(1)}\otimes h_{2}\otimes h_{4}S(h_{5})_{(2)}+h_{1}\otimes h_{3(1)}S(h_{5})\otimes h_{2}\otimes h_{3(2)}S(h_{4})=0.

Thus

\displaystyle S(h_{1})h_{3(2)}S(h_{5})\otimes S(h_{2})h_{3(1)}S(h_{4})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle S(h_{1})h_{3}S(h_{5})_{(1)}\otimes S(h_{2})h_{4}S(h_{5})_{(2)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle S(h_{1})h_{2}S(h_{5})_{(1)}\otimes S(h_{3})h_{4}S(h_{5})_{(2)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle S(h)_{(1)}\otimes S(h)_{(2)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle q\left(S(h)\right),

where the cocommutativity is used for the second
``="
.
∎

The dual form of (
2.1
) for co-Poisson coalgebras is the following
proposition.

Proposition 2.17
.

Let
(C,\Delta,\varepsilon)
be a co-Poisson coalgebra with co-Poisson structure
q
. Then

(q\otimes\Delta^{\prime})\circ\Delta=(\Delta^{\prime}\otimes q)\circ\Delta.

Proof.

It follows by using the co-Leibniz rule to calculate

(\Delta\otimes\Delta)q(a)=(\Delta\otimes 1\otimes 1)(1\otimes\Delta)q(a)=(1\otimes 1\otimes\Delta)(\Delta\otimes 1)q(a).

∎

The dual form of (

Corollary 2.18

3.
Dual properties between Poisson and co-Poisson Hopf algebras

As the vector space dual of any coalgebra is an algebra, the dual of
any co-Poisson coalgebra is a Poisson algebra.

Proposition 3.1
.

(1)

Suppose
(C,\Delta,\varepsilon)
is a coalgebra,
q:C\to C\otimes C

is a linear map. Then
(C,q)
is a co-Poisson coalgebra if and only
if
(C^{*},q^{*})
is a Poisson algebra.

(2)

Suppose
\sigma:C\to D
is a linear map between co-Poisson coalgebras. Then
\sigma:C\to D
is a
co-Poisson coalgebra morphism if and only if
\sigma^{*}:D^{*}\to C^{*}
is a Poisson algebra morphism.

Proof.

(1) Note that
q^{*}
is the map

q^{*}:C^{*}\otimes C^{*}\subseteq(C\otimes C)^{*}\to C^{*},f\otimes g\mapsto\{f,g\}
, where
\{f,g\}\in C^{*}
is the map
C\to k,\,c\mapsto(f\otimes g)q(c)=f(c_{(1)})g(c_{(2)})
.

Note that
(\{f,g\}+\{g,f\})(c)=(f\otimes g)(1+t_{2})q(c)
. It is easy to see that
\{-,-\}_{C^{*}}:C^{*}\times C^{*}\to C^{*}
is skew-symmetric if and only if

q
is skew-symmetric.

Since
\{\{f,g\},h\}(c)=(f\otimes g\otimes h)(q\otimes 1)q(c)
, then

\circlearrowleft\{\{f,g\},h\}(c)=(f\otimes g\otimes h)(1+t_{3}+t_{3}^{2})(q\otimes 1)q(c).

It follows that
\{-,-\}_{C^{*}}
satisfies the Jacobi identity if and
only if
q
satisfies the co-Jacobi identity.

Similarly, since
\{f*g,h\}(c)=(f\otimes g\otimes h)\left((\Delta\otimes 1)q(c)\right)
and

((f*\{g,h\})-(\{h,f\}*g))(c)=(f\otimes g\otimes h)((1\otimes q)\Delta-t_{3}^{2}(q\otimes 1)\Delta)(c),

then
\{f*g,h\}=f*\{g,h\}+\{f,h\}*g
if and only
if
(\Delta\otimes 1)q=(1\otimes q)\Delta-t_{3}^{2}(q\otimes 1)\Delta
.

(2) It is well known that
\sigma:C\to D
is a coalgebra morphism
if and only if
\sigma^{*}:D^{*}\to C^{*}
is an algebra morphism. Since

(\sigma^{*}\{f,g\}_{D^{*}}-\{\sigma^{*}f,\sigma^{*}g\}_{C^{*}})(c)=(f\otimes g)(\Delta_{D}\sigma-(\sigma\otimes\sigma)\Delta_{C})(c),

then
\sigma^{*}\{-,-\}_{D^{*}}=\{-,-\}_{C^{*}}(\sigma^{*}\otimes\sigma^{*})
if and only if
\Delta_{D}\sigma=(\sigma\otimes\sigma)\Delta_{C}
. The proof is finished.
∎

If
A
is an algebra, then
A^{\circ}=\{f\in A^{*}\mid\ker f\,\textrm{contains a cofinite ideal of}\,A\}
is a coalgebra, which
is called the finite dual of
A
. Suppose
(A,p=\{-,-\})
is a
Poisson algebra. Example
3.2
shows that

(A^{\circ},q=p^{*})
may not be a co-Poisson coalgebra because
p^{*}:A^{*}\to(A\otimes A)^{*}
may not be restricted to a map
p^{*}_{A^{\circ}}:A^{\circ}\to A^{\circ}\otimes A^{\circ}
.

Example 3.2
.

Let
A=k[x_{1},x_{2},\cdots,x_{n},\cdots]
be a polynomial algebra
with infinitely many variables
\{x_{i}\mid i\geq 1\}
. Let
p(x_{i}\otimes x_{j})=\{x_{i},x_{j}\}=1
for all
i<j
. Then
p
gives a Poisson algebra structure
on
A
. Suppose
\varepsilon:A\to k,x_{i}\mapsto 0
, is the
augmentation map. Then
\varepsilon\in A^{\circ}
, but

p^{*}(\varepsilon)=\varepsilon p\notin(A\otimes A)^{\circ}
.

In fact, if
\varepsilon p\in(A\otimes A)^{\circ}
, then there is a
cofinite ideal
I
of
A
such that
I\otimes A+A\otimes I\subseteq\ker(\varepsilon p)
. Then,
\{I,A\}\subseteq\ker\varepsilon
.
Since
A/I
is finite-dimensional, there exists a nonzero linear
polynomial in
I
, which will imply that
1\in\{I,A\}
. It
contradicts to
\{I,A\}\subseteq\ker\varepsilon
.

But if
A
is left or right noetherian, then we
have the following positive conclusion.

Proposition 3.3
.

Let
(A,p=\{-,-\})
be a Poisson algebra, and
f:A\to B
be a Poisson algebra morphism.

(1)

If
A
is a (left or right) noetherian algebra, then
(A^{\circ},q=p^{*})
is a co-Poisson
coalgebra.

(2)

If both
A
and
B
are (left or right) noetherian, then
f^{*}:B^{\circ}\to A^{\circ}
is a co-Poisson coalgebra morphism.

Proof.

It suffices to show that
p^{*}:A^{*}\to(A\otimes A)^{*}
restricts
to a map

p^{*}|_{A^{\circ}}:A^{\circ}\to A^{\circ}\otimes A^{\circ}\cong(A\otimes A)^{\circ}.

Suppose
f\in A^{\circ}
and
I\subseteq\ker f
is a cofinite ideal of

A
. Since
A
is left or right noetherian, then
I/I^{2}
is a finitely
generated left or right
A/I
-module.
Hence
\dim_{k}I/I^{2}<\infty
as
\dim_{k}A/I<\infty
. So,
I^{2}
is also a cofinite ideal of

A
. Let
J=I^{2}\otimes A+A\otimes I^{2}
. Then

is a cofinite

A\otimes A
, and

p^{*}(f)(J)=fp(J)\subseteq f(I)=\{0\}.

It follows that
p^{*}(f)\in(A\otimes A)^{\circ}
.
∎

Note that
A^{\circ}=0
for the Weyl algebra

. So, even in noetherian case

may not be a Poisson algebra
when
(A^{\circ},q=p^{*})
is a co-Poisson
coalgebra.

, the authors prove that the Hopf dual

of a

is an

. In fact, this is true in general

[

. We give a proof here. Oh
gives a proof also in a recent paper (

Proposition 3.4
.

Let
(H,\mu,\eta,\Delta,\varepsilon,S,q)
be a co-Poisson Hopf algebra. Then the Hopf dual
H^{\circ}

is a Poisson Hopf algebra.

Proof.

It is well-know that
H^{\circ}

is a Hopf algebra (
[
Sw
, Section 6.2]
,
[
Mo
, Theorem
9.1.3]
). By Proposition
3.1
, to show
H^{\circ}
is
a Poisson algebra it suffices to show that
q^{*}:(H\otimes H)^{*}\to H^{*}
restricts to a map
q^{\circ}:H^{\circ}\otimes H^{\circ}\cong(H\otimes H)^{\circ}\to H^{\circ},
that is,
q^{*}\left((H\otimes H)^{\circ}\right)\subseteq H^{\circ}
.

Suppose
F\in(H\otimes H)^{\circ}
and
J\subseteq\ker F
is a
cofinite ideal in
H\otimes H
. Since
\Delta
is an
algebra morphism,
\Delta^{-1}(J)
is an ideal of
H
. It follows
from
q(ab)=q(a)\Delta(b)+\Delta(a)q(b)
that
I=q^{-1}(J)\cap\Delta^{-1}(J)
is an ideal of
H
. Since the linear map

H/I\to\frac{H\otimes H}{J}\oplus\frac{H\otimes H}{J},\quad\overline{h}\mapsto\left(\overline{q(h)},\overline{\Delta(h)}\right)

is injective,
I
is a cofinite ideal
of
H
. Note that
q^{*}(F)(I)=F(q(I))\subseteq F(J)=0
, i.e.,
I\subseteq\ker q^{*}(F)
. It follows that
q^{*}(F)\in H^{\circ}
.

To finish the proof, we need to show that
\mu^{*}:H^{\circ}\to H^{\circ}\otimes H^{\circ}
satisfies the compatible condition (
2.3
), that is, for all
f,g\in H^{\circ}
,

\mu^{*}(\{f,g\}_{H^{\circ}})=\{f_{1},g_{1}\}_{H^{\circ}}\otimes(f_{2}*g_{2})+(f_{1}*g_{1})\otimes\{f_{2},g_{2}\}_{H^{\circ}}.

This is true, because, for any

x,y\in H
, on one hand, by (
2.6
),

\displaystyle\mu^{*}(\{f,g\}_{H^{\circ}})(x\otimes y)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\{f,g\}_{H^{\circ}}(xy)=(f\otimes g)q(xy)=f((xy)_{(1)})g((xy)_{(2)})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle f((x_{(1)}y_{1})g((x_{(2)}y_{2})+f((x_{1}y_{(1)})g((x_{2}y_{(2)}),

on the other hand,

\displaystyle(\{f_{1},g_{1}\}_{H^{\circ}}\otimes(f_{2}*g_{2})+(f_{1}*g_{1})\otimes\{f_{2},g_{2}\}_{H^{\circ}})(x\otimes y)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\{f_{1},g_{1}\}_{H^{\circ}}(x)\,(f_{2}*g_{2})(y)+(f_{1}*g_{1})(x)\,\{f_{2},g_{2}\}_{H^{\circ}}(y)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle f_{1}(x_{(1)})g_{1}(x_{(2)})f_{2}(y_{1})g_{2}(y_{2})+f_{1}(x_{1})g_{1}(x_{2})f_{2}(y_{(1)})g_{2}(y_{(2)})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle f((x_{(1)}y_{1})g((x_{(2)}y_{2})+f((x_{1}y_{(1)})g((x_{2}y_{(2)}).

∎

4.
Co-Poisson coalgebra structures on
k[x_{1},x_{2},\cdots,x_{d}]

Suppose
\mathfrak{g}=kx_{1}\oplus kx_{2}\oplus\cdots\oplus kx_{d}
is
a
d
-dimensional Lie algebra. The co-Poisson Hopf structures on
U(\mathfrak{g})

are in one-to-one correspondence with the Lie bialgebra structures on
\mathfrak{g}
.
If
\mathfrak{g}
is non-abelian, then there is no nontrivial Poisson Hopf structure on
U(\mathfrak{g})
(see Proposition
2.5
).
So, we turn to consider the case when
\mathfrak{g}
is abelian from now on. Then

A=U(\mathfrak{g})=k[x_{1},\cdots,x_{d}]
. We first characterize
co-Poisson coalgebra structures on
k[x_{1},x_{2},\cdots,x_{d}]
in this section.

Suppose
(C,\Delta,\varepsilon)
is a coalgebra. Let
P(C)
be the subspace of
C
consisting of
all primitive elements of
C
. Assume
P(C)=\oplus_{i\in I}ke_{i}

as a vector space, with
(I,<)
being well
ordered by using Well Ordering Principle. Let

\mathcal{I}=\bigoplus_{i,j\in I,i<j}k(e_{i}\otimes e_{j}-e_{j}\otimes e_{i}).

Lemma 4.1
.

Retain the notations above. Suppose
X\in C\otimes C
. Then
X\in\mathcal{I}
if and only if

(1+t_{2})(X)=0\,\textrm{ and }\,(\Delta\otimes 1)(X)=(1-t_{3})(1\otimes X).

Proof.

“
\Rightarrow
” Trivial.

“
\Leftarrow
” Assume
0\neq X=\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}\otimes b_{i}

with
n
minimal. Then
\{a_{1},\cdots,a_{n}\}
and

\{b_{1},\cdots,b_{n}\}
are both linearly independent. Since

(1+t_{2})(X)=0
, i.e.,
\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}\otimes b_{i}=-\sum_{i=1}^{n}b_{i}\otimes a_{i}
, then

(1-t_{3})(1\otimes X)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}(1\otimes a_{i}\otimes b_{i}-b_{i}\otimes 1\otimes a_{i})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}(1\otimes a_{i}+a_{i}\otimes 1)\otimes b_{i}.

Since

(\Delta\otimes 1)(X)=(1-t_{3})(1\otimes X)
, then
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Delta(a_{i})\otimes b_{i}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}(1\otimes a_{i}+a_{i}\otimes 1)\otimes b_{i}
. Thus
a_{i}\in P(C)
by the independence of the

b_{i}
’s.

By using
\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}\otimes b_{i}=-\sum_{i=1}^{n}b_{i}\otimes a_{i}

and a similar discussion, we have
b_{i}\in P(C)
as well. The
assertion follows.
∎

The following facts are obvious.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
B
be a bialgebra. Then

(1)

If
X\in B\otimes B
is skew-symmetric, then so is
X\Delta(x)
for any
x\in P(B)
.

(2)

For any
a\in B
and
X\in B\otimes B
,

(\Delta\otimes 1)(X\Delta(a))=(\Delta\otimes 1)(X)\cdot\Delta^{(2)}(a).

In the following,
A=U(\mathfrak{g})=k[x_{1},x_{2},\cdots,x_{d}]
.
Let
\mathcal{H}(A)

be the set of all monic monomials of
A
. For any
a\in\mathcal{H}(A)
,
\Delta(a)=\sum a_{1}\otimes a_{2}
is always assumed to be
the expression by the standard
k
-basis of
k[x_{1},x_{2},\cdots,x_{d}]
. For any
a\in\mathcal{H}(A)
,
|a|
is the degree of
a
.

First, we establish a reciprocity law for two linear maps from
A
to
A\otimes A
, which is a key step to characterize
the co-Poisson structures.

Proposition 4.3
.

Let
q:A\to A\otimes A
and
I:A\to A\otimes A
be two linear maps. Then
I(a)=(-1)^{|a_{2}|}q(a_{1})\Delta(a_{2})
for all
a\in A
if and only if

q(a)=I(a_{1})\Delta(a_{2})
for all
a\in A
.

Proof.

First note that in our case (the algebra is generated by primitive elements), for any
1\neq a\in\mathcal{H}(A)
,

(-1)^{|a_{2}|}\Delta(a_{1}a_{2})=0.

(4.1)

Since
a\otimes 1
is one of the terms of
\Delta(a)
for any
a\in\mathcal{H}(A)
, then

\sum_{a_{2}\neq 1}(-1)^{|a_{2}|+1}\Delta(a_{1}a_{2})=\Delta(a).

(4.2)

“
\Rightarrow
” Obviously,
I(1)=q(1)
, and so
q(a)=I(a_{1})\Delta(a_{2})
for
a=1

\displaystyle I(ax)=(-1)^{|a_{2}|}q(a_{1}x)\Delta(a_{2})+(-1)^{|a_{2}|+1}q(a_{1})\Delta(a_{2}x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle q(ax)+\sum_{a_{3}\neq 1}(-1)^{|a_{3}|}(I(a_{1}x)\Delta(a_{2})+I(a_{1})\Delta(a_{2}x))\Delta(a_{3})

\displaystyle+(-1)^{|a_{3}|+1}I(a_{1})\Delta(a_{2})\Delta(a_{3}x),

then

Thus

4.2
).

“
\Leftarrow
” Obviously,
I(a)=(-1)^{|a_{2}|}q(a_{1})\Delta(a_{2})

holds for

by

suffices to show that if

then for any

∎

To prove Proposition
4.5
, we need the following lemma.

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
A=U(\mathfrak{g})=k[x_{1},\cdots,x_{d}]
. Then for any linear map
q:A\to A\otimes A
and

(-1)^{|a_{2}|}(1\otimes q)\Delta(a_{1})\cdot\Delta^{(2)}(a_{2})=(-1)^{|a_{2}|}1\otimes q(a_{1})\Delta(a_{2}).

Proof.

We claim first for any
a\in\mathcal{H}(A)
,

\sum(-1)^{|a_{1}|+|a_{2}|}a_{1}a_{3}\otimes a_{2}\otimes a_{4}=\sum(-1)^{|a_{1}|}1\otimes a_{1}\otimes a_{2}.

(4.3)

It is obviously true for
a=1

4.3
) holds
for
a
. We show (
4.3
) holds for
ax
for any

.

Since
\Delta^{(3)}(x)=1\otimes 1\otimes 1\otimes x+1\otimes 1\otimes x\otimes 1+1\otimes x\otimes 1\otimes 1+x\otimes 1\otimes 1\otimes 1,
then

\displaystyle(-1)^{|(ax)_{1}|+|(ax)_{2}|}(ax)_{1}(ax)_{3}\otimes(ax)_{2}\otimes(ax)_{4}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(-1)^{|a_{1}|+|a_{2}|}a_{1}a_{3}\otimes a_{2}\otimes a_{4}x+(-1)^{|a_{1}|+|a_{2}|}a_{1}a_{3}x\otimes a_{2}\otimes a_{4}

\displaystyle-(-1)^{|a_{1}|+|a_{2}|}a_{1}a_{3}\otimes a_{2}x\otimes a_{4}-(-1)^{|a_{1}|+|a_{2}|}a_{1}xa_{3}\otimes a_{2}\otimes a_{4}

\displaystyle(-1)^{|a_{1}|+|a_{2}|}a_{1}a_{3}\otimes a_{2}\otimes a_{4}x-(-1)^{|a_{1}|+|a_{2}|}a_{1}a_{3}\otimes a_{2}x\otimes a_{4}

\displaystyle(-1)^{|a_{1}|}1\otimes a_{1}\otimes a_{2}x-(-1)^{|a_{1}|}1\otimes a_{1}x\otimes a_{2}

\displaystyle(-1)^{|(ax)_{1}|}1\otimes(ax)_{1}\otimes(ax)_{2}.

Hence (
4.3
) holds for all

.
By applying

), then

(-1)^{|a_{1}|+|a_{2}|}a_{1}a_{3}\otimes q(a_{2})\otimes\Delta(a_{4})=(-1)^{|a_{1}|}1\otimes q(a_{1})\otimes\Delta(a_{2}).

Thus

∎

Proposition 4.5
.

Let
A=U(\mathfrak{g})=k[x_{1},\cdots,x_{d}]
and
\mathcal{I}=\oplus_{i<j}k(x_{i}\otimes x_{j}-x_{j}\otimes x_{i}).
Then, for any linear map

q:A\to A\otimes A
, the following are equivalent.

(1)

q
is skew-symmetric and satisfies the co-Leibniz rule.

(2)

I(a)=(-1)^{|a_{2}|}q(a_{1})\Delta(a_{2})\in\mathcal{I}
for all
a\in A
.

Proof.

“(1)
\Rightarrow
(2)” By Lemma
4.1
, it suffice to show

(1+t_{2})\left((-1)^{|a_{2}|}q(a_{1})\Delta(a_{2})\right)=0
and

(\Delta\otimes 1)((-1)^{|a_{2}|}q(a_{1})\Delta(a_{2}))=(1-t_{3})\left(1\otimes(-1)^{|a_{2}|}q(a_{1})\Delta(a_{2})\right).

Note that
A
is generated by primitive elements. It follows from
Lemma
4.2
that
(1+t_{2})((-1)^{|a_{2}|}q(a_{1})\Delta(a_{2}))=0

for all
a\in\mathcal{H}(A)
.

By Lemmas
4.2
,
4.4
and the co-Leibniz rule (
2.5
),

\displaystyle(\Delta\otimes 1)((-1)^{|a_{2}|}q(a_{1})\Delta(a_{2}))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(-1)^{|a_{2}|}(\Delta\otimes 1)q(a_{1})\cdot\Delta^{(2)}(a_{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(1-t_{3})(-1)^{|a_{2}|}(1\otimes q)\Delta(a_{1})\cdot\Delta^{(2)}(a_{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(1-t_{3})\left((-1)^{|a_{2}|}(1\otimes q)\Delta(a_{1})\cdot\Delta^{(2)}(a_{2})\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(1-t_{3})\left(1\otimes(-1)^{|a_{2}|}q(a_{1})\Delta(a_{2})\right).

“(2)
\Rightarrow
(1)” Suppose
(-1)^{|a_{2}|}q(a_{1})\Delta(a_{2})\in\mathcal{I}
for all
a\in\mathcal{H}(A)
. Then
q(1)\in\mathcal{I}
.
We check the skew symmetric property of
q
and the co-Leibniz rule

(\Delta\otimes 1)q(a)=(1-t_{3})(1\otimes q)\Delta(a)

by induction on the degree of

a
.
They are true for
a=1
by Lemma
4.1
. Assume they are true
for
a
of degree no more than
n
. Now for any
a
with
\deg a=n+1
.
Since
I(a)=(-1)^{|a_{2}|}q(a_{1})\Delta(a_{2})\in\mathcal{I}
,

q(a)=\sum_{a_{2}\neq 1}(-1)^{|a_{2}|+1}q(a_{1})\Delta(a_{2})+I(a).

Since
q(a_{1})
is skew symmetric for
a_{2}\neq 1
by induction
hypothesis and
I(a)\in\mathcal{I}
, then
q(a)
is skew
symmetric by Lemma
4.2
. By Lemma
4.4
,

\displaystyle(\Delta\otimes 1)(q(a))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\Delta\otimes 1)\left(\sum_{a_{2}\neq 1}(-1)^{|a_{2}|+1}q(a_{1})\Delta(a_{2})+I(a)\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(-1)^{|a_{2}|+1}\sum_{a_{2}\neq 1}(\Delta\otimes 1)q(a_{1})\cdot\Delta^{(2)}(a_{2})+(\Delta\otimes 1)(I(a))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(1-t_{3})\left(\sum_{a_{2}\neq 1}(-1)^{|a_{2}|+1}(1\otimes q)\Delta(a_{1})\right)\cdot\Delta^{(2)}(a_{2})+(1-t_{3})(1\otimes I(a))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(1-t_{3})\left(\sum_{a_{2}\neq 1}(-1)^{|a_{2}|+1}(1\otimes q)\Delta(a_{1})\cdot\Delta^{(2)}(a_{2})\right)

\displaystyle+(1-t_{3})\left(\sum(-1)^{|a_{2}|}(1\otimes q(a_{1})\Delta(a_{2}))\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(1-t_{3})\left((1\otimes q)\Delta(a)\right).

The proof is finished.
∎

Proposition 4.6
.

For any
a\in\mathcal{H}(A)
,
let
I(a)=\sum_{1\leq i,j\leq d}\lambda_{a}^{ij}x_{i}\otimes x_{j}\in\mathcal{I}
with
(\lambda_{a}^{ij})_{d\times d}\in M_{d}(k)

skew-symmetric. Then the linear map
q:A\to A\otimes A,a\mapsto I(a_{1})\Delta(a_{2})
defines a co-Poisson structure on the coalgebra

A
if and only if for all
1\leq i<j<k\leq d
and

,

\sum_{s=1}^{d}\left(\lambda_{a_{1}}^{sk}\lambda_{x_{s}a_{2}}^{ij}+\lambda_{a_{1}}^{si}\lambda_{x_{s}a_{2}}^{jk}+\lambda_{a_{1}}^{sj}\lambda_{x_{s}a_{2}}^{ki}\right)=0.

(4.4)

Proof.

By Proposition
4.5
, we need only to care for the co-Jacobi identity.
For any
a\in\mathcal{H}(A)
,

\displaystyle(q\otimes 1)q(a)=(q\otimes 1)(I(a_{1})\Delta(a_{2}))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(q\otimes 1)(\sum_{s,t}\lambda_{a_{1}}^{st}(x_{s}\otimes x_{t})\Delta(a_{2}))=\sum_{s,t}\lambda_{a_{1}}^{st}q(x_{s}a_{2})\otimes x_{t}a_{3}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{s,t}\lambda_{a_{1}}^{st}(I(x_{s}a_{2})\Delta(a_{3})+I(a_{2})\Delta(x_{s}a_{3}))\otimes x_{t}a_{4}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{s,t,i,j}\lambda_{a_{1}}^{st}\lambda_{x_{s}a_{2}}^{ij}(x_{i}\otimes x_{j}\otimes x_{t})(a_{3}\otimes a_{4}\otimes a_{5})

\displaystyle+\sum_{s,t,i,j}\lambda_{a_{1}}^{st}\lambda_{a_{2}}^{ij}(x_{i}x_{s}\otimes x_{j}\otimes x_{t}+x_{i}\otimes x_{j}x_{s}\otimes x_{t})(a_{3}\otimes a_{4}\otimes a_{5})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle U+V,

where
U
is the first term, i.e.,

and

is the second term,

Then, by the skew-symmetric property of

\displaystyle\circlearrowleft V=

\displaystyle\sum_{s,t,i,j}(\lambda_{a_{1}}^{st}\lambda_{a_{2}}^{ij}+\lambda_{a_{1}}^{ij}\lambda_{a_{2}}^{ts})(x_{i}x_{s}\otimes x_{j}\otimes x_{t})(a_{3}\otimes a_{4}\otimes a_{5})

\displaystyle+\sum_{s,t,i,j}(\lambda_{a_{1}}^{st}\lambda_{a_{2}}^{ij}+\lambda_{a_{1}}^{ji}\lambda_{a_{2}}^{st})(x_{i}\otimes x_{j}x_{s}\otimes x_{t})(a_{3}\otimes a_{4}\otimes a_{5})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0.

So, the co-Jacobi identity holds if and only if
\circlearrowleft U=0
.
Note that

\displaystyle\circlearrowleft U=

\displaystyle\sum_{s,t,i,j}\lambda_{a_{1}}^{st}\lambda_{x_{s}a_{2}}^{ij}(x_{i}\otimes x_{j}\otimes x_{t}+x_{t}\otimes x_{i}\otimes x_{j}+x_{j}\otimes x_{t}\otimes x_{i})(a_{3}\otimes a_{4}\otimes a_{5})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i,j,k}\sum_{s}\left(\lambda_{a_{1}}^{sk}\lambda_{x_{s}a_{2}}^{ij}+\lambda_{a_{1}}^{si}\lambda_{x_{s}a_{2}}^{jk}+\lambda_{a_{1}}^{sj}\lambda_{x_{s}a_{2}}^{ki}\right)(x_{i}\otimes x_{j}\otimes x_{k})(a_{3}\otimes a_{4}\otimes a_{5}).

If

in

and

.

Conversely, if

.
∎

In summary, we have the following result.

Theorem 4.7
.

Let
A=U(\mathfrak{g})=k[x_{1},\cdots,x_{d}]
. Then a linear map

gives a co-Poisson coalgebra structure on
A
if and only if there is a linear map
I:A\to A\otimes A
such that for all
a\in\mathcal{H}(A)
,

(1)

q(a)=I(a_{1})\Delta(a_{2})
.

(2)

I(a)=\sum_{1\leq i,j\leq d}\lambda_{a}^{ij}x_{i}\otimes x_{j}\in\mathcal{I}

with
(\lambda_{a}^{ij})_{d\times d}\in M_{d}(k)

skew-symmetric.

(3)

For all
1\leq i<j<k\leq d

\sum_{s=1}^{d}\left(\lambda_{a_{1}}^{sk}\lambda_{x_{s}a_{2}}^{ij}+\lambda_{a_{1}}^{si}\lambda_{x_{s}a_{2}}^{jk}+\lambda_{a_{1}}^{sj}\lambda_{x_{s}a_{2}}^{ki}\right)=0.

The following proposition shows that the co-Jacobi identity holds trivially in two variables case.
So, the co-Poisson coalgebra structures on
k[x,y]
are given by the linear maps
I:k[x,y]\to k(x\otimes y-y\otimes x)
.

Proposition 4.8
.

Let
A=k[x,y]
. Then there is an one-to-one correspondence between
the co-Poisson coalgebra structures on
A
and the linear maps
A\to\mathcal{I}=k(x\otimes y-y\otimes x)

(q:A\to A\otimes A)\mapsto(I:A\to\mathcal{I},a\mapsto(-1)^{|a_{2}|}q(a_{1})\Delta(a_{2}),

with the inverse map

(I:A\to\mathcal{I})\mapsto(q:A\to A\otimes A,a\mapsto I(a_{1})\Delta(a_{2})).

Proof.

We only need to show the co-Jacobi identity always holds in this
case. Assume
I(a)=l_{a}(x\otimes y-y\otimes x)
with
l_{a}\in k
.
For any
a\in A
,

q(a)=I(a_{1})\Delta(a_{2})=l_{a_{1}}(x\otimes y-y\otimes x)(a_{2}\otimes a_{3})=l_{a_{1}}(xa_{2}\otimes ya_{3}-ya_{2}\otimes xa_{3}).

Then

\circlearrowleft(q\otimes 1)q(a)=\circlearrowleft l_{a_{1}}\left(q(xa_{2})\otimes ya_{3}-q(ya_{2})\otimes xa_{3}\right).
Now

\displaystyle\circlearrowleft l_{a_{1}}q(xa_{2})\otimes ya_{3}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\circlearrowleft l_{a_{1}}\left(I(xa_{2})\Delta(a_{3})+I(a_{2})\Delta(xa_{3})\right)\otimes ya_{4}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\circlearrowleft l_{a_{1}}l_{xa_{2}}(x\otimes y-y\otimes x)(a_{3}\otimes a_{4})\otimes ya_{5}

\displaystyle+\circlearrowleft l_{a_{1}}l_{a_{2}}(x\otimes y-y\otimes x)(xa_{3}\otimes a_{4}+a_{3}\otimes xa_{4})\otimes ya_{5}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\circlearrowleft l_{a_{1}}l_{xa_{2}}(xa_{3}\otimes ya_{4}\otimes ya_{5}-ya_{3}\otimes xa_{4}\otimes ya_{5})

\displaystyle+\circlearrowleft l_{a_{1}}l_{a_{2}}(x^{2}a_{3}\otimes ya_{4}\otimes ya_{5}+xa_{3}\otimes xya_{4}\otimes ya_{5}

\displaystyle-xya_{3}\otimes xa_{4}\otimes ya_{5}-ya_{3}\otimes x^{2}a_{4}\otimes ya_{5})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\circlearrowleft l_{a_{1}}l_{a_{2}}(xa_{3}\otimes xya_{4}\otimes ya_{5}-xya_{3}\otimes xa_{4}\otimes ya_{5}).

Similarly,

\circlearrowleft l_{a_{1}}q(ya_{2})\otimes xa_{3}=\circlearrowleft l_{a_{1}}l_{a_{2}}(ya_{3}\otimes xya_{4}\otimes xa_{5}-xya_{3}\otimes ya_{4}\otimes xa_{5}).

Thus
\circlearrowleft(q\otimes 1)q(a)=0
for all

.
∎

5.
(Co-)Poisson Hopf structures on
k[x_{1},x_{2},\cdots,x_{d}]

5.1.
Poisson Hopf structures

As proved in
[
LPV
, Proposition 1.8]
, any Poisson structure on the polynomial algebra
A=k[x_{1},x_{2},\cdots,x_{d}]
is
given by
\{x_{i},x_{j}\}=f_{ij}
where
\{f_{ij}\}_{d\times d}
is a skew-symmetric matrix over
A
such that
for all
1\leq i<j<k\leq d
,

\sum_{l=1}^{d}\left(f_{lk}\frac{\partial f_{ij}}{\partial x_{l}}+f_{li}\frac{\partial f_{jk}}{\partial x_{l}}+f_{lj}\frac{\partial f_{ki}}{\partial x_{l}}\right)=0.

(5.1)

If
A=U(\mathfrak{g})=k[x_{1},x_{2},\cdots,x_{d}]
is viewed as a Hopf algebra, then the Poisson algebra structures on
A
can be described in a form dual to
Theorem
4.7
. The following is a reciprocity law for linear maps
A\otimes A\to A
.

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
p:A\otimes A\to A
and
J:A\otimes A\to A
be two linear maps. Then
p(a\otimes b)=J(a_{1}\otimes b_{1})a_{2}b_{2}
for all
a,b\in A
if and only if

J(a\otimes b)=(-1)^{|a_{2}|+|b_{2}|}p(a_{1}\otimes b_{1})a_{2}b_{2}
for all
a,b\in A
.

Proof.

Similar to that of Proposition
4.3
.
∎

Proposition 5.2
.

Let
A=U(\mathfrak{g})=k[x_{1},\cdots,x_{d}]
. Then a linear map

p:A\otimes A\to A
gives a Poisson structure on
A
if and only if there is a linear map
J:A\otimes A\to A
such that for all
a,b\in A
,

(1)

p(a\otimes b)=J(a_{1}\otimes b_{1})a_{2}b_{2}.

(2)

J
is skew-symmetric, and
J(a\otimes b)=0
except both
a
and
b
are of degree
1
.

(3)

The Jacobi identity holds for
J
.

Actually, Proposition
5.2
is exactly
[
LPV
, Proposition 1.8]
. If
p:A\otimes A\to A
is a Poisson algebra structure on
A
, and
J(a\otimes b)=(-1)^{|a_{2}|+|b_{2}|}p(a_{1}\otimes b_{1})a_{2}b_{2}
,
then
J
satisfies conditions (2) and (3) in Proposition
5.2
. In this case, condition (1) is the same as

\{f,g\}=\sum_{1\leq i,j\leq d}\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{i}}\frac{\partial g}{\partial x_{j}}\{x_{i},x_{j}\}.

The Poisson Hopf structures on
A
are classified in the following
proposition. They are exactly linear Poisson structures on
A
.

Proposition 5.3
.

Any Poisson Hopf structure on
A=k[x_{1},x_{2},\cdots,x_{d}]
is given by

\{x_{i},x_{j}\}=\sum_{l=1}^{d}\lambda^{ij}_{l}x_{l}\qquad(1\leq i,j\leq d),

where
\lambda^{ij}_{l}=-\lambda^{ji}_{l}
,
subject to the relations, for all
1\leq i<j<k\leq d
and all
1\leq s\leq d
,

\sum_{l=1}^{d}\left(\lambda^{ij}_{l}\lambda^{lk}_{s}+\lambda^{jk}_{l}\lambda^{li}_{s}+\lambda^{ki}_{l}\lambda^{lj}_{s}\right)=0.

Proof.

Suppose
\{-,-\}
is a Poisson Hopf structure on
A
. Then, by (
2.3
),

\Delta\left(\{x_{i},x_{j}\}\right)=1\otimes\{x_{i},x_{j}\}+\{x_{i},x_{j}\}\otimes 1,

that is,
\{x_{i},x_{j}\}
is a primitive element of
A
. Hence
\{x_{i},x_{j}\}=\sum_{l=1}^{d}\lambda^{ij}_{l}x_{l}
for some
\lambda^{ij}_{l}\in k
such that
\lambda^{ij}_{l}=-\lambda^{ji}_{l}
for all
l
.
Then (
5.1
) is equivalent to, for all
1\leq i<j<k\leq d

and all
1\leq s\leq d
,

\sum_{l=1}^{d}\left(\lambda^{ij}_{l}\lambda^{lk}_{s}+\lambda^{jk}_{l}\lambda^{li}_{s}+\lambda^{ki}_{l}\lambda^{lj}_{s}\right)=0.

Conversely, any such a Poisson algebra structure is in fact a Poisson Hopf structure on
A
.
We need to check that
\Delta\left(\{a,b\}\right)=a_{1}b_{1}\otimes\{a_{2},b_{2}\}+\{a_{1},b_{1}\}\otimes a_{2}b_{2}
for all
a,b\in\mathcal{H}(A)
, which can be done by induction.
∎

5.2.

(2)

(3)

For all

\sum_{l=1}^{d}\left(\lambda_{s}^{lk}\lambda_{l}^{ij}+\lambda_{s}^{li}\lambda_{l}^{jk}+\lambda_{s}^{lj}\lambda_{l}^{ki}\right)=0

Proof.

“
\Rightarrow
” Let
I:A\to A\otimes A
be the map
I(a)=(-1)^{|a_{2}|}q(a_{1})\Delta(a_{2})
for all
a\in A
. Then, by Proposition

5.3.
Dual (co-)Poisson structures

It is well-known that the dual algebra
A^{*}
of the coalgebra
A=k[x_{1},x_{2},\cdots,x_{d}]
is the algebra of formal divided power series (it is called Hurwitz series in
[
Ke
, Proposition 2.4]
in one variable), which is isomorphic to the
algebra of formal power series
k[[x_{1},x_{2},\cdots,x_{d}]]
. By Cartier-Gabriel-Kostant-Milnor-Moore Theorem (
[
Sw
, Theorem 8.1.5]
and
[
MM
,
\lx@sectionsign
6]
),
the finite dual
A^{\circ}
of the polynomial Hopf algebra
A=k[x_{1},x_{2},\cdots,x_{d}]
is isomorphic to

A\ltimes k^{n}
as Hopf algebras,
where
k^{n}
carries the additive
group structure (the group-like elements in the Hopf dual). Similar to
[
LPV
, Proposition 1.8]
, the following lemma holds.

Lemma 5.7
.

Let
\tilde{A}=k[[x_{1},\cdots,x_{d}]]
be the algebra of formal power series. Any Poisson structure on
\tilde{A}
is given by

\{x_{i},x_{j}\}=f_{ij}
, where
(f_{ij})_{d\times d}
is a skew-symmetric matrix over
\tilde{A}
such that for all
1\leq i<j<k\leq d
,

\{\{x_{i},x_{j}\},x_{k}\}+\{\{x_{j},x_{k}\},x_{i}\}+\{\{x_{k},x_{i}\},x_{j}\}=0.

In this case, for all
f,g\in\tilde{A}
,

\{f,g\}=\sum_{i,j=1}^{d}\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{i}}\frac{\partial g}{\partial x_{j}}f_{ij}.

Theorem 5.8
.

Suppose
\charac k=0
. Let
\tilde{A}=k[[x_{1},x_{2},\cdots,x_{d}]]
be the algebra of formal power series
and
A=k[x_{1},x_{2},\cdots,x_{d}]
. Then there is an one-to-one correspondence between the Poisson algebra structures on
\tilde{A}

and the co-Poisson coalgebra structures on
A
.

Proof.

Suppose

\{x_{i},x_{j}\}=f_{ij}=\sum_{a\in\mathcal{H}(A)}\lambda^{ij}_{a}a\,(1\leq i,j\leq d)

gives a Poisson algebra structure on
\tilde{A}
. Let
\alpha^{ij}_{a}=a!\lambda^{ij}_{a}
and

I(a)=\sum_{1\leq i,j\leq d}\alpha^{ij}_{a}x_{i}\otimes x_{j},\quad q(a)=I(a_{1})\Delta(a_{2})\,\textrm{for all}\,a\in\mathcal{H}(A).

Then
q:A\to A\otimes A
is a co-Poisson coalgebra structure on
A
.
In fact, for all
1\leq i<j<k\leq d
,

\{\{x_{i},x_{j}\},x_{k}\}=\sum_{a,b\in\mathcal{H}(A)}\sum_{l=1}^{d}\lambda^{ij}_{a}\lambda^{lk}_{b}\frac{\partial a}{\partial x_{l}}b=\sum_{c\in\mathcal{H}(A)}\sum^{ab=c}_{a,b\in\mathcal{H}(A)}\sum_{l=1}^{d}(a^{(l)}+1)\lambda^{ij}_{ax_{l}}\lambda^{lk}_{b}c.

Then
\circlearrowleft\{\{x_{i},x_{j}\},x_{k}\}=0

if and only if for all
c\in\mathcal{H}(A)
and all
1\leq i<j<k\leq d
,

\sum^{ab=c}_{a,b\in\mathcal{H}(A)}\sum_{l=1}^{d}(a^{(l)}+1)\left(\lambda^{ij}_{ax_{l}}\lambda^{lk}_{b}+\lambda^{jk}_{ax_{l}}\lambda^{li}_{b}+\lambda^{ki}_{ax_{l}}\lambda^{lj}_{b}\right)=0.

Note that
\alpha^{ij}_{a}=a!\lambda^{ij}_{a}
. It is equivalent to

\sum^{ab=c}_{a,b\in\mathcal{H}(A)}\sum_{l=1}^{d}\frac{1}{a!b!}\left(\alpha^{ij}_{ax_{l}}\alpha^{lk}_{b}+\alpha^{jk}_{ax_{l}}\alpha^{li}_{b}+\alpha^{ki}_{ax_{l}}\alpha^{lj}_{b}\right)=0.

By multiplying it with
c!
, it is easy to see that it is equivalent to

\sum_{l=1}^{d}\left(\alpha^{ij}_{c_{1}x_{l}}\alpha^{lk}_{c_{2}}+\alpha^{jk}_{c_{1}x_{l}}\alpha^{li}_{c_{2}}+\alpha^{ki}_{c_{1}x_{l}}\alpha^{lj}_{c_{2}}\right)=0

for all
c\in\mathcal{H}(A)
and
1\leq i<j<k\leq d
.

On the other hand, suppose
q:A\to A\otimes A
is a co-Poisson coalgebra structure on
A
.
Let

I(a)=(-1)^{|a_{2}|}q(a_{1})\Delta(a_{2}):=\alpha^{ij}_{a}x_{i}\otimes x_{j}
,
and
\lambda^{ij}_{a}=\frac{1}{a!}\alpha^{ij}_{a}
. Then

\{x_{i},x_{j}\}=f_{ij}:=\sum_{a\in\mathcal{H}(A)}\lambda^{ij}_{a}a.

gives a Poisson algebra structure on
\tilde{A}
.
∎

Remark 5.9
.

A co-Poisson coalgebra structure
q
on
A

I:A\to A\otimes A

rational
