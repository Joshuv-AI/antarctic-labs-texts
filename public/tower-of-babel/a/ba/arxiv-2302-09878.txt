Optimal polynomial feedback laws for finite horizon control problems
Karl Kunisch karl.kunisch@uni-graz.at, Donato Vásquez-Varas donato.vasquez-varas@ricam.oeaw.ac.at

Abstract
A learning technique for finite horizon optimal control problems and its approximation based on polynomials is analyzed. It allows to circumvent, in part, the curse dimensionality which is involved when the feedback law is constructed by using the Hamilton-Jacobi-Bellman (HJB) equation. The convergence of the method is analyzed, while paying special attention to avoid the use of a global Lipschitz condition on the nonlinearity which describes the control system. The practicality and efficiency of the method is illustrated by several examples. For two of them a direct approach based on the HJB equation would be unfeasible.

Introduction

The synthesis of optimal feedback laws for non-linear control problems remains to be a difficult task. The main approach for obtaining feedback laws relies on the dynamical programming and it involves solving a Hamilton-Jacobi-Bellman (HJB) equation. It is well known that this equation suffers from the curse of dimensionality, which makes a direct approach infeasible. In the last years many attempts were made in order to alleviate this obstacle. In the present work we analyze a learning approach to obtain optimal feedbacks laws for finite horizon, non-linear optimal control problems. This is an extension of the developments made for infinite horizon control problems in
Kunisch et al. 2022
and
Kunisch and Walter 2021
, and for finite horizon problems in
Kunisch and Walter 2022
. While in
Kunisch and Walter 2021
and
Kunisch and Walter 2022
we focus on approximations and numerical realization by neural networks, in
Kunisch et al. 2022
we started to use monomials as approximating ansatz functions for the feedback operators to be learned. In the analysis part of this paper the optimality conditions are analysed and a relation between them and the HJB equation is established, which allows to compare the value function of the control problem and the one which is obtained by the learning approach. Compared to
Kunisch and Walter 2022
we choose a simpler cost functional here, which
only involves the value function itself, wereas in
Kunisch and Walter 2022
we used also first and second order derivatives.
In the numerical part, we demonstrate the efficiency of the approach by diverse applications. Let us mention that we also tested approximation of the time-dependent component of the value
feedback function by Legendre polynomials rather than polynomials. In numerical practice the latter outperformed the former. For this reason we focus here on the approximation by monomials in space as well as in time.

The proposed learning technique results in an infinite dimensional optimization problem. For practical realization it must be discretized. For this purpose, we specify a rather general framework for which convergence of discretization schemes can be established. Due to their simplicity and the encouraging results obtained in
Kunisch et al. 2022
, we use polynomials as ansatz functions for our concrete numerical realization. In this case the general assumptions for convergence hold true, provided that the value function is sufficiently smooth. We point out that differently from
Kunisch et al. 2022
, the technique used here to prove convergence of the method utilizes that the value function solves the HJB equation. This, together with the fact that we consider a finite time horizon allows us to get a better convergence order than the one obtained in
Kunisch et al. 2022

2
Control theory and dynamical programming

We aim for an optimal feedback law for the following finite horizon problem

(P)\quad\min_{u}J(t_{0},y_{0},u):=\int_{t_{0}}^{T}\left(\ell(t,y)+\frac{\beta}{2}|u|^{2}\right)dt+g(y(T))

(1)

s.t.\quad y^{\prime}(t)=f(t,y(t))+Bu(t),\quad y(t_{0})=y_{0}

(2)

where
T>0
,
t_{0}\in(0,T)
,
y_{0}\in\Omega
,
\beta>0
,
u\in L^{2}((t_{0},T);\mathbb{R}^{M})
,
f\in C([0,T];C^{1}(\overline{\Omega};\mathbb{R}^{d}))
,
B\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times M}
, and
\ell\in C([0,T];C^{1}(\overline{\Omega}))
and
g\in C^{1}(\overline{\Omega})
are bounded from below by 0. Further

\Omega
is an open, convex, and bounded subset of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
For this problem, we define the associated value function by

V(t_{0},y_{0})=\min_{u}J(t_{0},y_{0},u).

(3)

If the value function
V
is in
C^{1}([0,T]\times\Omega)
, then it satisfies the Hamilton-Jacobi-Bellman equation

\frac{dV}{dt}(t,y)+\ell(y)+\min_{u\in\mathbb{R}^{M}}\left\{\nabla_{y}V(t,y)^{\top}(f(y)+Bu)+\frac{\beta}{2}|u|^{2}\right\}=0\mbox{ in }[0,T]\times\Omega,

(4)

\quad V(T,y)=g(y)\mbox{ in }\Omega.

(5)

Moreover, if for an initial condition
y_{0}\in\Omega
the optimal solution of (
1
) is such that the respective state
y^{*}
satisfies
y^{*}(t)\in\Omega
for all
t\in[t_{0},T]
, then we can define a feedback law by evaluating

{{u^{*}(t,y)=-\frac{1}{\beta}B^{\top}\nabla_{y}V(t,y)}}

(6)

along
y=y^{*}(t)
,
and
y^{*}
solves the closed loop problem

\frac{d}{dt}y^{*}(t)=f(y^{*})+Bu^{*}(t,y^{*}(t)),\quad y^{*}(t_{0})=y_{0}.

(7)

This implies that once we solve the HJB equation we can obtain an optimal feedback law by using (
6
). However, it is well known that the HJB equation suffers from the curse of dimensionality, which makes this approach infeasible for large
d
. Instead we propose a learning approach that we will describe in the following section.

3
Statement of the learning problem

To start we introduce the relevant hypotheses and definitions. Let
\omega\subset\Omega
be a open subset of
\Omega
such that
\overline{\omega}\subset\Omega
. For
v\in C([0,T];C^{2}(\overline{\Omega}))
, we define
U_{v}(t,y)
and
F_{v}(t,y)
by

U_{v}(t,y)=-\frac{1}{\beta}B^{\top}\nabla_{y}v(t,y)\mbox{ and }F_{v}(t,y)=f(t,y)+BU_{v}(t,y)\mbox{ for all }(t,y)\in[0,T]\times\overline{\Omega}.

We note that
U_{v}(t,y)
satisfies

U_{v}(t,y)=\argmin_{U\in\mathbb{R}^{m}}\left\{\frac{\beta}{2}|u|^{2}+\nabla_{y}v(t,y)^{\top}(f(t,y)+BU)\right\}\mbox{ for all }(t,y)\in[0,T]\times\overline{\Omega}.

(8)

Clearly,
U_{v}
is a
C^{1}
function with respect to
y
. Combining this with the fact that
\overline{\Omega}
is a convex and compact subset of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we have that
U_{v}
and
F_{v}
are Lipschitz functions in their second argument, namely, defining

L_{U_{v}}=\frac{|B|}{\beta}\left\lVert\nabla_{y}^{2}v\right\rVert_{C([0,T]\times\overline{\Omega};\mathbb{R}^{d\times d})},\quad L_{F_{v}}=L_{f}+|B|L_{U_{v}},

we have

|U_{v}(t,y_{1})-U_{v}(t,y_{2})|\leqslant L_{U_{v}}|y_{1}-y_{2}|,\quad|F_{v}(t,y_{1})-F_{v}(t,y_{2})|\leqslant L_{F_{v}}|y_{1}-y_{2}|,

(9)

for all
t\in[0,T]
and
y_{1},y_{2}\in\overline{\Omega}
. In the following we assume that
v
is such that for all
(t_{0},y_{0})\in[0,T]\times\omega
, there exists a unique
y\in C^{1}([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{d})
satisfying

y^{\prime}(t)=F_{v}(t,y(t)),\quad\mbox{for all }t\in[t_{0},T],\quad y(t_{0})=y_{0}.

(10)

We extend
y
as follows

\tilde{y}(t)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\displaystyle y(t)&\mbox{ if }t\in[t_{0},T]\\
\vskip 6.0pt plus 2.0pt minus 2.0pt\cr\displaystyle y_{0}+\int_{t_{0}}^{t}F_{v}(s,y_{0})ds&\mbox{ if }t\in[0,t_{0}]\end{array}\right.

(11)

which is a continuously differentiable function in
[0,T]
. Associated to
v
, we define

\mathcal{G}(v):=\{(t,\tilde{y}(t;t_{0},y_{0})):\ (t_{0},y_{0})\in(0,T)\times\omega,\ t\in[t_{0},T)\}\subset(0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{d},

(12)

\mathcal{R}(v):=\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\ \exists t\in(0,T),\ (t,y)\in\mathcal{G}(v)\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d},

(13)

where
\tilde{y}(t;t_{0},y_{0})
is defined in (
11
). Due to the fact that for
t_{0}\in(0,T)
and
t\in[t_{0},T]
the function
y_{0}\in\omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}\mapsto\tilde{y}(t;t_{0},y_{0})
onto its range in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a diffeomorphism, we know that
\mathcal{G}(v)
and
\mathcal{R}(v)
are open sets.

Additionally, we define
\mathcal{D}
as a subset of
C([0,T],C^{2}(\overline{\Omega}))
of functions such that problem (
10
) has a solution and the closure of the union of all the trajectories with
(t_{0},y_{0})\in[0,T]\times\omega
is contained in
\Omega
, i.e.,

\mathcal{D}=\left\{v\in C([0,T],C^{2}(\overline{\Omega})):\ \begin{array}[]{l}\displaystyle\mbox{ problem }\eqref{closedloop2}\mbox{ has a solution }\forall(t_{0},y_{0})\in[0,T]\times\omega\\
\vskip 6.0pt plus 2.0pt minus 2.0pt\cr\displaystyle\mbox{ and }\overline{\mathcal{R}(v)}\subset\Omega\end{array}\right\}.

We define
\Phi
as the solution mapping of (
11
), that is,

\begin{array}[]{c}\Phi:[0,T]\times\omega\times\mathcal{D}\mapsto C([0,T];\overline{\Omega})\\
(t_{0},y_{0},v)\in[0,T]\times\omega\times\mathcal{D}\mapsto\Phi(t_{0},y_{0},v)=\tilde{y}\in C([0,T];\overline{\Omega})\end{array}

(14)

where
\tilde{y}
is the function given by (
11
) and the domain is endowed with the norm of
\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\times C([0,T],C^{2}(\overline{\Omega}))
.

We formulate the following learning problem

\displaystyle\min_{v\in\overline{\mathcal{D}}}\mathcal{J}(v):=\frac{1}{T|\omega|}\int_{\omega}\int_{0}^{T}\mathcal{V}(t_{0},y_{0},v)dy_{0}dt_{0}

(15)

where
\mathcal{V}:[0,T]\times\omega\times\mathcal{D}
is defined by

\mathcal{V}(t_{0},y_{0},v):=\int_{t_{0}}^{T}\left(\ell(t,y(t))+\frac{\beta}{2}|U_{v}(t,y(t))|^{2}\right)dt+g(y(T)),

(16)

with
y(t)=\Phi(t_{0},y_{0},v)(t)
. We shall prove that the function
\Phi
,
\mathcal{V}
, and
\mathcal{J}
are continuously differentiable functions.

Remark 1
.

We shall verify that if
V\in\mathcal{D}
, then there exists a non-trivial neighborhood of
V
in
C([0,T];C^{1}(\overline{\Omega}))
which is contained in
\mathcal{D}
. Moreover,
\mathcal{V}(t_{0},y_{0},v)\geqslant V(t_{0},y_{0})
for all
v\in C([0,T];C^{1}(\overline{\Omega}))
, which implies that the value function is a solution for the learning problem. Further, if
v^{*}
is an optimal solution of (
15
), then

\int_{\Omega}\int_{0}^{T}|\mathcal{V}(t_{0},y_{0},v^{*})-V(t_{0},y_{0})|dt_{0}dy_{0}=\int_{\Omega}\int_{0}^{T}\left(\mathcal{V}(t_{0},y_{0},v^{*})-V(t_{0},y_{0})\right)dt_{0}dy_{0}\leqslant 0

and by the continuity of
\mathcal{V}
and
V
, we obtain that
\mathcal{V}(t_{0},y_{0},v^{*})=V(t_{0},y_{0})
for all
(t_{0},y_{0})\in[0,T]\times\omega
. Hence, by the dynamical programming principle
the feedback
U_{v^{*}}
is optimal in
\mathcal{G}(v^{*})
.

4
Optimality conditions

Now, we study the necessary and sufficient optimality conditions for the learning problem (
15
). To this end, we first analyze the smoothness of the functions
\Phi
,
\mathcal{V}
and
\mathcal{J}
.

Lemma 1
.

Let
v\in\mathcal{D}
. Then, there exists
\delta>0
such that if for
\tilde{v}\in C([0,T];C^{2}(\overline{\Omega}))
it holds
\left\lVert\tilde{v}-v\right\rVert_{C([0,T],C^{1}(\overline{\Omega}))}<\delta
, then
\tilde{v}\in\mathcal{D}
. Further,
\mathcal{D}
is an open subset of
C([0,T];C^{2}(\overline{\Omega}))
and

\left\lVert\Phi(t_{0},y_{0},v)-\Phi(t_{0},y_{0},\tilde{v})\right\rVert_{C([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{d})}\leqslant C\left\lVert\tilde{v}-v\right\rVert_{C([0,T];C^{1}(\overline{\Omega}))},\ \forall(t_{0},y_{0},\tilde{v})\in[0,T]\times\omega\times\mathcal{D}

(17)

where
C
depends on
B,f,T
and
v
.

Proof.

Denote by
y
the solution of (
10
) for
v
. By (
9
) and the local existence Theorem, there exists an existence time
\bar{T}>t_{0}
and a function
\tilde{y}\in C([0,\tilde{T}];\mathbb{R}^{d})
such that

\tilde{y}^{\prime}(t)=F_{\tilde{v}}(t,\tilde{y}(t))\mbox{ for all }t\in(t_{0},\tilde{T})\mbox{ and }\tilde{y}(t_{0})=y_{0}.

We assume that
\tilde{T}
is the maximum time that satisfies
\tilde{y}(t)\in\Omega
for all
t\in(t_{0},\tilde{T})
. We shall prove that
\tilde{T}\geqslant T
by contradiction. Assume that
\tilde{T}<T
. This implies that
\tilde{y}(\tilde{T})\in\partial\Omega
, otherwise
\tilde{y}(\tilde{T})\in\Omega
and we could use the existence and uniqueness Theorem to extend
\tilde{y}
to some
\bar{T}>\tilde{T}
with
\tilde{y}(t)\in\Omega
for all
t\in(t_{0},\bar{T})
, which is a contradiction. Set
z=y-\tilde{y}
, then for
t\in(0,\tilde{T})
we have

\begin{array}[]{ll}\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}|z(t)|^{2}&\displaystyle=(F_{v}(t,y(t))-F_{\tilde{v}}(t,\tilde{y}(t)))z(t)\\
\vskip 6.0pt plus 2.0pt minus 2.0pt\cr&\displaystyle\leqslant(F_{v}(t,y(t))-F_{v}(t,\tilde{y}(t))+F_{v}(t,\tilde{y}(t))-F_{\tilde{v}}(t,\tilde{y}(t)))z(t)\\
\vskip 6.0pt plus 2.0pt minus 2.0pt\cr&\displaystyle\leqslant L_{F_{v}}|z(t)|^{2}+\frac{1}{\beta}|B|^{2}|z(t)|\left\lVert\tilde{v}-v\right\rVert_{C([0,T];C^{1}(\overline{\Omega}))},\end{array}

(18)

By a generalized Gronwall inequality (see Theorem 21 in
Dragomir 2003
) this estimates implies

|y(t)-\tilde{y}(t)|=|z(t)|\leqslant\frac{|B|^{2}}{\beta L_{F_{v}}}(\exp(L_{F_{v}}(T-t_{0}))-1)\left\lVert\tilde{v}-v\right\rVert_{C([0,T];C^{1}(\overline{\Omega}))}

for all
t\in(t_{0},\tilde{T})
. In particular, if
\tilde{v}\in\mathcal{D}
, then we have
\tilde{T}\geqslant T
and the last inequality implies (
17
). By the definition of
\mathcal{D}
there exists
\varepsilon>0
such that

\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:dist(x,\overline{\mathcal{R}(v)})<\varepsilon\}\subset\Omega

and we can choose
\delta
such that
|z(t)|\leqslant\frac{\varepsilon}{2}
for all
t\in[t_{0},\tilde{T}]
. By the continuity of
\tilde{y}
, this implies that
\tilde{y}(\tilde{T})\in\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon}\in\Omega
, which is a contradiction and therefore
\tilde{T}\geqslant T
. Moreover, since this holds uniformly in
(t_{0},y_{0})\in(0,T)\times\omega
, we have that
\mathcal{R}(\tilde{v})\subset\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon}\subset\Omega
. Since
\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon}
is close, we have
\overline{\mathcal{R}(\tilde{v})}\subset\Omega
, which implies that
\tilde{v}\in D
and thus
\mathcal{D}
is open.

Proposition 1
.

The functional
\Phi
is locally Lipschitz in
[0,T]\times\omega\times\mathcal{D}
and continuously differentiable. Further, setting
z_{1}=D_{t_{0}}\Phi(t_{0},y_{0},v)\in C([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{d})
,
z_{2}=D_{y_{0}}\Phi(t_{0},y_{0},v)\in C([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{d\times d})
, and
z_{3}=D_{v}\Phi(t_{0},y_{0},v)\cdot\phi\in C([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{d})
for
\phi\in C([0,T],C^{2}(\overline{\Omega}))
,

and
(t_{0},y_{0})\in(0,T)\times\omega
, we have

\displaystyle z_{1}(t)

\displaystyle=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\displaystyle-F_{v}(t_{0},y_{0})+\int_{t_{0}}^{t}D_{y}F_{v}(s,y(s))z_{1}(s)ds&\mbox{for }t\in(t_{0},T],\\
\vskip 6.0pt plus 2.0pt minus 2.0pt\cr\displaystyle-F_{v}(t_{0},y_{0})&\mbox{for }t\in[0,t_{0}],\end{array}\right.

\displaystyle z_{2}(t)

\displaystyle=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\displaystyle I+\int_{t_{0}}^{t}D_{y}F_{v}(s,y(s))z_{2}(s)ds&\mbox{for }t\in(t_{0},T],\\
\vskip 6.0pt plus 2.0pt minus 2.0pt\cr\displaystyle I+\int_{t_{0}}^{t}D_{y}F_{v}(s,y_{0})z_{2}(s)ds&\mbox{for }t\in[0,t_{0}],\end{array}\right.

\displaystyle z_{3}(t)

\displaystyle=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\displaystyle\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\left(D_{y}F_{v}(s,y(s))z_{3}(s)+BU_{\phi}(s,y(s))\right)ds&\mbox{for }t\in(t_{0},T],\\
\vskip 6.0pt plus 2.0pt minus 2.0pt\cr\displaystyle\int_{t_{0}}^{t}BU_{\phi}(s,y_{0})ds&\mbox{for }t\in[0,t_{0}],\end{array}\right.

where

Proof.

By

1

\Phi
is locally Lipchitz with respect to

\Psi(t_{0},y)(t)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\displaystyle y(t)-\int_{t_{0}}^{t}F_{v}(s,y(s))ds-y_{0}&\mbox{for }t\in[t_{0},T],\\
\vskip 6.0pt plus 2.0pt minus 2.0pt\cr\displaystyle y(t)-\int_{t_{0}}^{t}F_{v}(s,y_{0})ds-y_{0}&\mbox{for }t\in[0,t_{0}].\end{array}\right.

(25)

Its partial derivatives are given by

(D_{y}\Psi(t_{0},y)\!\cdot\!z)(t)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\displaystyle z(t)-\int_{t_{0}}^{t}D_{y}F_{v}(s,y(s))z(s)ds&\mbox{for }t\in[t_{0},T],\\

(26)

for

\frac{d}{dt_{0}}\Psi(t_{0},y)(t)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\displaystyle F_{v}(t_{0},y(t_{0}))&\mbox{for }t\in[t_{0},T],\\
\vskip 6.0pt plus 2.0pt minus 2.0pt\cr\displaystyle F_{v}(t_{0},y_{0})&\mbox{for }t\in[0,t_{0}].\end{array}\right.

Thus

This implies (

(55)

for all

6
Optimization algorithm

In this section we describe the optimization algorithm that we use to solve (
44
). To this end, we consider
X=\mathbb{R}^{N}
and a differentiable injection
\mathcal{I}:X\mapsto C([0,T];C^{2}(\overline{\Omega}))
. With the last assumption, the function
\mathcal{J}\circ\mathcal{I}
is differentiable. Nevertheless, the objective function of problem (
44
) includes a
\ell_{1}
penalty term to promote the sparcity, which is non differentiable. Taking this into account, we decided to use a proximal gradient method. Additionally, we choose the Barzilai Borwein step size along with a non-monotone backtracking line search, which proved to be efficient for high dimensional problems (see
Azmi and Kunisch 2020
,
Barzilai and Borwein 1988
, and
Raydan 1997
for a convergence analysis in the smooth case).

We now describe the algorithm that we use to solve (
44
). We set
\tilde{\mathcal{J}}=\mathcal{J}\circ\mathcal{I}
and we denote the
k-
th element of the sequence produced by the algorithm by
\theta^{k}\in X
, the step size by
s^{k}
, and we set at each iteration

d^{k}:=\nabla\tilde{\mathcal{J}}(\theta^{k})+\gamma(1-r)\theta^{k}.

(57)

We use the proximal point update rule as is described in section 10.2 in
Beck 2017
, namely we choose
\theta^{k+1}
such that

\theta^{k+1}=\argmin_{\vartheta\in X}\left\{d^{k}\cdot\left(\vartheta-\theta^{k}\right)+\frac{1}{2s^{k}}|\theta^{k}-\vartheta|_{2}^{2}+\gamma r|\vartheta|_{1}\right\}.

(58)

To choose the step size
s^{k}
we use the non-monotone backtracking line search described in
Wright et al. 2009
, starting from an initial guess
s_{0}^{k}
.
That is, for
\kappa\in(0,1)
,
n
a non negative integer number and
\beta\in(0,1)
, we take
s^{k}=s_{0}^{k}\beta^{i}
such that
i
is the smallest natural number which satisfies

\tilde{\mathcal{J}}(\theta^{+})\leqslant\max_{i\in\{\max(0,k-n),\ldots,n\}}\tilde{\mathcal{J}}(\theta^{k-i})-\frac{\kappa}{s_{0}^{k}\beta^{i}}|\theta^{k}-\theta^{+}|^{2},

(59)

for
\theta^{+}>0
and set
\theta^{k+1}=\theta^{+}
.
The Barzilai-Borwein step size is used as initial guess, namely we take
s_{0}^{k}
as

s_{0}^{k}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\displaystyle\big[(\theta_{k}-\theta_{k-1})\cdot(d_{k}-d_{k-1})\big]/|d_{k}-d_{k-1}|^{2}&\mbox{ if }k\mbox{ is }odd,\\
\vskip 6.0pt plus 2.0pt minus 2.0pt\cr\displaystyle|\theta_{k}-\theta_{k-1}|^{2}/\big[(\theta_{k}-\theta_{k-1})\cdot(d_{k}-d_{k-1})\big]&\mbox{ if }k\mbox{ is }even.\end{array}\right.

(60)

We use non-monotone stopping criterion, namely, defining for
k>0

J_{k}:=\tilde{\mathcal{J}}(\theta^{k})+\gamma\left(\frac{1-r}{2}|\theta^{k}|^{2}_{2}+r|\theta^{k-i}|_{1}\right)\mbox{ and }\quad J^{max}_{k}:=\min_{i\in\{\max(0,k-n),\ldots,n\}}J_{k-i},

we iterate until
\left(J^{max}_{k-1}-J_{k}\right)\leqslant J^{max}_{k-1}\cdot tol
.

We summarize the algorithm as follows:

7
Polynomial learning problem

In order to introduce the polynomial ansatz we need some notation. Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
d\in\mathbb{N}
, where
\mathbb{N}=\{0,1,2,\ldots\}
. For a multi-index
\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{d})\in\mathbb{N}^{d}
we define a monomial
\phi_{\alpha}
by

\phi_{\alpha}(y)=\prod_{j=1}^{d}y_{j}^{\alpha_{j}},\quad y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

(61)

We denote by
\Lambda_{n}
the set of multi-indexes such the sum of all its elements is lower or equal than
n
and by
\mathcal{B}_{n}
the set of all the monomials with total degree lower or equal to
n
, that is

\Lambda_{n}=\left\{\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{d}:\ \sum_{j=1}^{d}\alpha_{j}\leqslant n\right\},\quad\mathcal{B}_{n}=\{\phi_{\alpha}:\ \alpha\in\Lambda_{n}\}

(62)

We further denote the
hyperbolic cross
multi-index set by
\Gamma_{n}
and we also introduce a subset
\mathcal{S}_{n}
of
\mathcal{B}_{n}
composed by the elements of the subset
\mathcal{B}_{n}
associated to the multi-indexes in
\Gamma_{n}
, i.e.

\Gamma_{n}=\Big\{\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{d})\in\mathbb{N}^{d}:\quad\prod_{j=1}^{d}(\alpha_{j}+1)\leqslant n+1\Big\},\quad\mathcal{S}_{n}=\{\phi_{\alpha}:\ \alpha\in\Gamma_{n}\}.

(63)

It is important to observe that the cardinality of
\Lambda_{n}
is
\sum_{j=1}^{n}\binom{d+j+1}{j}
, on the other hand the cardinality of
\Gamma_{n}
is bounded by
\min\{2n^{3}4^{d},e^{2}n^{2+\log_{2}(n)}\}
(see
Adcock et al. 2017
). Hence, for high
d
the cardinality of the hyperbolic cross is smaller than the cardinality of
\Lambda_{n}
. For this reason,
\Lambda_{n}
is more suitable for high dimensional problems.

We point out that we only need to consider the elements in either
\Lambda_{n}
or
\Gamma_{n}
that satisfy
B^{\top}\nabla\phi_{\alpha}(y)\neq 0
for all
y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. By Lemma 1 in
Kunisch et al. 2022
, we know that these elements are characterized by the condition
B^{\top}e_{i}\neq 0
for all
i=1,\ldots,d
such that
\alpha_{i}>0
. For a set
S
of multi-indexes we define

S(B)=\{\alpha\in S:\ B^{\top}e_{i}\neq 0\mbox{ for all }i=1,\ldots,d\mbox{ such that }\alpha_{i}>0\}.

(64)

With this definition, we only consider either
\Gamma_{n}(B)
or
\Lambda_{n}(B)
.

We assume that
\Gamma_{n}(B)
and
\Lambda_{n}(B)
are ordered, namely
\Lambda_{n}(B)=\{\alpha^{tot}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{|\Lambda_{n}(B)|}
and
\Gamma_{n}(B)=\{\alpha_{i}^{hc}\}_{i=1}^{|\Gamma_{n}(B)|}
. For
j\in\{0,1,\ldots,m\}
and
t\in\mathbb{R}
we define

q_{j}(t)=t^{j}
. Then, for
m\in\mathbb{N}
we define
\mathcal{I}^{tot}_{m,n}(\theta)
and

\mathcal{I}^{hc}_{m,n}(\theta)
by

\mathcal{I}^{tot}_{m,n}(\theta)(t,y)=\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}\sum_{i=1}^{|\Lambda_{n}(B)|}\theta_{j,i}q_{j}(t)\phi_{\alpha^{tot}_{i}}(y),\ \mathcal{I}^{hc}_{m,n}(\theta)(t,y)=\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}\sum_{i=1}^{|\Gamma_{n}(B)|}\theta_{j,i}q_{j}(t)\phi_{\alpha^{hc}_{i}}(y),

(65)

for all
(t,y)in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and for
\theta
in
\mathbb{R}^{m}\times\mathbb{R}^{|\Lambda_{n}(B)|}
or
\mathbb{R}^{m}\times\mathbb{R}^{|\Gamma_{n}(B)|}
, respectively.

Following the previous notation, the total degree polynomial learning problem and the hyperbolic cross polynomial learning problem are respectively given by

\min_{\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{m}\times\mathbb{R}^{|\Lambda_{n}(B)|}}\mathcal{J}(\mathcal{I}^{tot}_{m,n}(\theta))+\gamma\left(\frac{1-r}{2}|\theta|^{2}_{2}+r|\theta|_{1}\right)

(66)

and

\min_{\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{m}\times\mathbb{R}^{|\Gamma_{n}(B)|}}\mathcal{J}(\mathcal{I}^{hc}_{m,n}(\theta))+\gamma\left(\frac{1-r}{2}|\theta|^{2}_{2}+r|\theta|_{1}\right)

(67)

For these problem we say that
m
is the time degree and
n
is the space degree.

Regarding the convergence and the existence of solutions of problems (
66
) and (
67
), we known by Theorem 9 in
(
Hájek and Johanis 2014
, Section 7.2)
that hypothesis (
45
) is satisfied as
m
and
n
tend to infinity. Hence, by
1
there exist
(m_{0}^{tot},n_{0}^{tot})
and
(m_{0}^{tot},n_{0}^{hc})
such that problem (
66
) has an optimal solution if
m\geqslant m_{0}^{tot}
and
n\geqslant n_{0}^{tot}
and problem (
67
) has an optimal solution if
m\geqslant m_{0}^{hc}
and
n\geqslant n_{0}^{hc}
.

Numerical Examples

We implement
Algorithm

1
to solve problem (
44
) for three experiments. In all them
\mathcal{J}
is approximated by means of the Monte-Carlo method. We choose uniformly at random a training set
\{(t_{0}^{i},y_{0}^{i})\}_{i=1}^{N}
of initial times and initial conditions and approximate
\mathcal{J}(v)
by

\mathcal{J}(v)\approx\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathcal{V}(t_{0}^{i},y_{0}^{i},v).

In each case we shall specify the sets of initial conditions and initial times for training and testing, and the discretization method used. To avoid numerical issues we normalize the arguments of the monomials, i.e., we redefine
\phi_{\alpha}(y)
by

\phi_{\alpha}(y)=\prod_{i=1}^{d}\left(\frac{y}{l}\right)^{\alpha_{i}}
and
q_{j}^{m}(t)
by
q_{j}^{m}(t)=\left(\frac{t}{T}\right)^{j}
, where
T
is the time horizon of the control problem and
l
is a positive constant chosen for each specific problem.

The performance of our approach is measured by comparing
\hat{u}_{i}(t)=U_{\hat{v}}(t,\Phi(t_{0}^{i},y_{0}^{i},\hat{v})(t))
to the corresponding optimal control
u^{*}
obtained by solving the open loop problem, for every initial condition in the test set given by
\hat{u}_{i}(t)=U_{\hat{v}}(t,\Phi(t_{0}^{i},y_{0}^{i},\hat{v})(t))
, and subsequently computing the normalized squared error in
L^{2}((0,T);\mathbb{R}^{m})
given by

MNSE_{u}(\{\hat{u}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N},\{u_{i}^{*}\}_{i=1}^{N})=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\int_{t_{0}^{i}}^{T}|\hat{u_{i}}-u_{i}^{*}|^{2}dt\Big/\sum_{i=1}^{N}\int_{t_{0}^{i}}^{T}|u_{i}^{*}|^{2}dt,

and analogously the error
MNSE_{y}(\{\hat{y}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N},\{y_{i}^{*}\}_{i=1}^{N})
for the states.
We also compare the optimal value of the open loop problem with the objective function of (
1
) evaluated in
\hat{u}
by computing the mean normalized absolute error

MNAE_{J}(\{\hat{u}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N},\{u_{i}^{*}\}_{i=1}^{N})=\sum_{i=1}^{N}|J(t_{0}^{i},u_{i}^{*},y_{0}^{i})-J(t_{0}^{i},\hat{u}_{i},y_{0}^{i})|\Big/\sum_{i=1}^{N}J(t_{0}^{i},u_{i}^{*},y_{0}^{i}).

The optimal control for the open loop problem is computed by a gradient descent algorithm with a backtracking line-search.

8.3
Collision-Avoiding multi-agent control problem

In this example we consider a set of
N_{a}\geqslant 2
agents with states
\{(x^{a}_{i})\}^{N_{a}}_{i=1}\subset\mathbb{R}^{p}
, a set of targets
\{(x^{t}_{i})\}^{N_{a}}_{i=1}\subset\mathbb{R}^{p}
to be reached by each agent and a set of
N_{o}\geqslant 1
obstacles denoted by
\{(x^{o}_{i})\}^{N_{a}}_{i=1}\subset\mathbb{R}^{p}
. The control is on the velocity of the agents, i.e.,

(x_{i}^{a})^{\prime}(t)=u_{a}(t)
for
t\in(t_{0},T)
. The running cost is given by

\ell(x_{1}^{a},\ldots,x_{N_{a}}^{a})=\sigma_{1}Q(x_{1}^{a},\ldots,x_{N_{a}}^{a})+\sigma_{2}W(x_{1}^{a},\ldots,x_{N_{a}}^{a})

where

Q(x_{1}^{a},\ldots,x_{N_{a}}^{a})=\frac{1}{N_{a}\cdot N_{o}}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{a}}\sum_{j=1}^{N_{o}}\exp(-\frac{1}{2r_{o}^{2}}|x_{i}^{a}-x_{j}^{o}|^{2})\frac{1}{r_{o}^{q}}

and

W(x_{1}^{a},\ldots,x_{N_{a}}^{a})=\frac{2}{N_{a}\cdot(N_{a}-1)}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{a}}\sum_{j=i+1}^{N_{a}}\exp(-\frac{1}{2r_{a}^{2}}|x_{i}^{a}-x_{j}^{a}|^{2})

with
r_{o},r_{a}>0
. The term
Q
accounts for collision between agents and obstacles, and
W
accounts for the collision among agents. To enforce the agents to reach the targets, the following terminal cost is used

g(x_{1}^{a},\ldots,x_{N_{a}}^{a})=\frac{\sigma_{3}}{2N_{a}}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{a}}|x_{i}^{a}-x_{i}^{t}|^{2}.

The resulting optimal control problem is given by

\begin{array}[]{c}\displaystyle\min_{u_{i}\in L^{2}((t_{0},T);\mathbb{R}^{p})}\int_{t_{0}}^{T}\ell(x_{1}^{a}(t),\ldots,x_{N_{a}}^{a}(t))dt+\sum_{i=1}^{N_{a}}\frac{\beta}{2N_{a}}\int_{t_{0}}^{T}|u_{i}(t)|^{2}dt+g(x_{1}^{a}(T),\ldots,x_{N_{a}}^{a}(T)),\\
\vskip 6.0pt plus 2.0pt minus 2.0pt\cr\displaystyle\frac{dx_{i}^{a}}{dt}(t)=u_{a}(t),\quad t\in(t_{0},T),\quad x_{a}^{i}(0)=x_{a,0}^{i},\quad i\in\{1,\ldots,N_{a}\}.\end{array}

(71)

This formulation is similar to those given in
Onken et al. 2021
and
Onken et al. 2023
.

For our implementation we choose
p=2
,
N_{a}=10
,
N_{o}=4
,
r_{o}=0.2
,
r_{a}=0.1
,
\sigma_{1}=10
,
\sigma_{2}=10
and
\sigma_{3}=100
. The obstacles and targets are given by
x_{i}^{o}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\cos\left(\frac{2(i-1)\pi}{N_{o}}\right),\sin\left(\frac{2(i-1)\pi}{N_{o}}\right)\right)
for i in
\{1,\ldots,N_{o}\ldots\}
and
x_{i}^{t}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\cos\left(\frac{2(i-1)\pi}{N_{a}}\right),\sin\left(\frac{2(i-1)\pi}{N_{a}}\right)\right)
for i in
\{1,\ldots,N_{a}\}
. Both the targets and the obstacles are depicted in
Figure

5
.

For the learning problem we consider
\omega=\omega_{1}\times\omega_{2}\ldots\times\omega_{N_{a}}
, where

\omega_{i}=\left\{(\rho\cos(\theta),\rho\sin(\theta))\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:\ 0.8\leqslant\rho\leqslant 2,\ \theta\in\left(\frac{2\pi}{N_{a}}(i-1),\frac{2\pi}{N_{a}}i\right)\right\}.

The cardinality of the training and test sets are 10 and 500 respectively. For the learning problem we set the time degree to 15,
l=2
, and the sparsity coefficient to
r=0.5
. For the learning algorithm we set
n=5
and
tol=10^{-4}

9
Conclusion

In this work a learning approach based on a polynomial ansatz for the synthesis of feedback laws of finite horizon control problem was studied. The performance of this approach was tested on 3 different problems. In all the experiments, the feedback law synthesised by the presented approach was capable of approximating the solutions of the corresponding open loop problems. Further, our experiments show that by tuning the penalty coefficients and the degree of the polynomial ansatz it is possible to find sparse polynomial feedback laws.
