Adversarial Robustness in Unsupervised Machine Learning: A Systematic Review CCS: Computing methodologies Unsupervised learning CCS: Security and privacy Software security engineering
Mathias Lundteigen Mohus, Jingyue Li

Abstract
. As the adoption of machine learning models increases, ensuring robust models against adversarial attacks is increasingly important. With unsupervised machine learning gaining more attention, ensuring it is robust against attacks is vital. This paper conducts a systematic literature review on the robustness of unsupervised learning, collecting 86 papers. Our results show that most research focuses on privacy attacks, which have effective defenses; however, many attacks lack effective and general defensive measures. Based on the results, we formulate a model on the properties of an attack on unsupervised learning, contributing to future research by providing a model to use.



1.
Introduction

As technologies mature, hardware capabilities increase, and legislation is introduced, adopting machine learning technologies in various applications seems inevitable, with the technology generally divided into three types: Supervised Learning (SL), Reinforcement Learning (RL), and Unsupervised Learning. SL focuses on approaches where the training data is labeled, i.e., a data sample maps to a correct value. A variety of cases use this, e.g., object detection
(
Ali and Shah 2005
)
, image classification
(
Peña et al. 2014
)
, and voice recognition
(
Ali et al. 2021
)
. RL is an approach where the model trains in the working environment, rewarding or punishing the model based on its performance. Use cases for this are bug detection in software
(
Rani et al. 2023
)
, computing resource optimization
(
Shi et al. 2020
)
, and control of cyber-physical systems
(
Liu et al. 2019c
)
. UL performs training of its models without guidance; pure data are used as the basis for training. Use cases for this is data generation
(
Devaranjan et al. 2020
)
, e.g.,
GANs
; encoding/decoding
(
Srivastava et al. 2015
)
, e.g.,
autoencoders
, and data clustering
(
Chander and Vijaya 2021
)
.

By its lack of need for guided information, UL can enable more data to be usable. In a world that generates a large amount of data (around 2.5 quintillion bytes each day
(
Dihuni 2020
)
), methods for using unsupervised data would be invaluable. However, where technology is used, adversaries inevitably attempt to exploit vulnerabilities in these technologies. With the increased use of ML in various fields, it is crucial to explore the adversarial robustness of ML models, particularly the use of UL, as this field has received less focus than supervised and reinforcement learning.

Liu et al. 2018
summarized attacks on machine learning models broadly, while
Guan et al. 2018
cover machine learning within cybersecurity.
Zhang et al. 2020
outline privacy vulnerabilities in machine learning, which could reveal sensitive information used to train synthetic data generators in the context of UL. At the same time,
Martins et al. 2020
cover
adversarial examples
relating to detection techniques, where the input to an ML model is perturbed in a specific way in order to disrupt the functionality of the model.

In contrast with
Liu et al. 2018
,
Guan et al. 2018
and
Zhang et al. 2020
, where parts of UL adversarial robustness are covered, we aim to conduct a Systematic Literature Review (SLR) on the topic of adversarial robustness within the whole field of unsupervised machine learning. The papers focus on answering the three research questions below:

•

RQ1
: What types of attacks exist against UL-based systems, and what are the common attributes of the attacks?

•

RQ2
: What defensive countermeasures exist for attacks against UL-based systems?

•

RQ3
: What are the challenges to defending against possible attacks targeting UL-based systems?

The SLR follows the guidelines outlined by
Keele et al. 2007
and identified 86 papers relevant to adversarial robustness in unsupervised learning. Thematic analysis was then used on these papers to summarize the papers according to the RQs systematically.

The results of SLR reveal that:

•

GANs
are mostly targeted by
privacy
attacks.

•

AutoEncoders
are mostly targeted by
adversarial examples
.

•

Poisoning
attacks are used against most types of UL.

•

Most attacks utilize
distance metrics
in limiting how much the attack alters samples for the attack.

•

Several defensive methods that work in a supervised or reinforcement context are not applicable in an unsupervised context. For example,
fine pruning
and
output activation clustering
are not suitable defenses for generative models.

•

The adversary’s knowledge of the target system greatly affects the attack’s performance.

•

There is a noticeable lack of defensive methods used in UL, except for
differential privacy
.

The contributions of the study are:

•

We provide a complete and novel summary of the state-of-the-art adversarial robustness of unsupervised learning relating to attributes of the attack, target, and defense.

•

We have summarized and outlined the main challenges facing the adversarial robustness of unsupervised learning.

•

We create a model of the attributes associated with attacks against unsupervised machine learning to analyze and compare adversarial attacks against UL.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. Section 2 covers the basics of unsupervised learning. Section 3 covers surveys related to the security and robustness of machine learning. Section 4 explains our research method. Sections 5 to 7 present the results. Section 8 discusses the results. Section 9 concludes and proposes future work.

2.
Background

Within UL, two types of technologies generally exist: discriminatory and generative.
Discriminatory
covers models where data are input into the model, producing a specific output based on its training. Examples of these are
clustering

(
Grira et al. 2004
;
Hahne et al. 2008
)
,
AutoEncoders

(
Bank et al. 2020
;
Pinaya et al. 2020
)
and
super-resolution networks

(
Anwar et al. 2020
)
.

•

Clustering
refers to a broad family of methods that defines clusters of data points based on certain heuristics. Unsupervised clustering finds these clusters based on distance metrics between data points to find which data points are mathematically close enough to belong to the same cluster. Clustering is often used for data analysis in data-heavy fields, such as gene information
(
Sumedha and Weigt 2008
)
.

•

AutoEncoders
is a method for training a neural network to create an inner representation of the training data, since the model trains to output the same data as the input. In most cases, AutoEncoders force a dimensionality reduction on the inner representation by limiting the number of hidden nodes in the network, where the first part of the network training is to create the encoding, and the last part of the network training is to decode the representation into the original data. One application of AutoEncoders is denoising data
(
Bank et al. 2020
)
where data is passed through the AutoEncoder to remove noise.

•

Super-resolution networks
are neural networks that work to increase the dimensionality of the data, by training the model to output a higher-dimensional piece of data, based on a lower-dimensional piece of data, e.g. a low-resolution image. Super-resolution is often used in image technologies to upscale the resolution of an image
(
Anwar et al. 2020
)
.

Generative
models cover models which do not directly use input data but are used to create new data with properties tied to the properties of the training set. Examples of this are
Variational AutoEncoders

(
Kingma et al. 2019
)
and
Generative Adversarial Networks

(
Creswell et al. 2018
;
Wang et al. 2017
;
Goodfellow et al. 2020
)
.

•

Variational AutoEncoder
is a method to use AutoEncoder to generate data by forcing constraints on the inner representation of the AutoEncoder, in turn making it possible to input random noise into the decoder part of the AutoEncoder and producing an output that is statistically similar to the training set used to train the AutoEncoder. This model is one of the main components of generating
deepfake
images by supplanting some of the encoded features of an image with another, generating an image with features from both images
Rocco 2021
.

•

Generative adversarial networks
consist of two models, the generator and the discriminator. The generator is a neural network with random noise as input, while the discriminator is a classifier trained to differentiate between the output from the generator and the training data. The generator and the discriminator are, in turn, trained against each other based on the performance of the discriminator. GANs are used for various synthetic data generation, e.g., medical data generation
(
Han et al. 2018
)
.

3.
Related Work

As the field of machine learning security has matured, several papers have been published that survey the state of the art within both broad and narrow fields within ML security.

Liu et al. 2018
use the taxonomy from
Fredrikson et al. 2015
to analyze 42 papers on attacks against machine learning models. The survey group the papers based on 1) The targeted algorithm, with the types being: Naïve Bayes/Logistic Regression/Decision Tree, Support Vector Machines (SVM), Principal Component Analysis (PCA)/Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO), Clustering, and Deep Neural Network (DNN), and 2) The type of attack, with the types being: Poisoning Attack, Evasion Attack, Impersonation Attack, and Inversion Attack.
Liu et al. 2018
also focus explicitly on comparing types of Adversarial Examples attacks, comparing the techniques, advantages, disadvantages, and formations of the adversarial examples. Additionally,
Liu et al. 2018
summarize defensive techniques for ML, outlining how its surveyed papers assess the security of their ML models and which defensive mechanisms are put in place: proactive or reactive. Defenses in training are also discussed, such as Data Sanitation, Bootstrap Aggregating, Random Subspace Method, and designing more robust algorithms. For defenses in the testing/inferring phase,
Liu et al. 2018
state that “countermeasures in the testing/inferring phase mainly focus on the improvement of learning algorithms’ robustness,” describing techniques like Game Theory, Adversarial Training, Defensive Distillation, Dimension Reduction, as well as ensemble methods. Lastly,
Liu et al. 2018
also cover the privacy of data used in ML, including Differential Privacy and Homomorphic Encryption.

Martins et al. 2020
perform a survey on the use of adversarial examples targeted towards detection techniques, particularly malware (11 papers) and intrusion detection (nine papers), surveyed from mainly IEEE Xplore, Springer, arXiv, ScienceDirect, Research Gate, and Google, using the keywords “Intrusion Detection,” “Malware Detection” and “Adversarial Machine Learning.” The paper compares several features recurring in its literature: Attack Techniques, Classifiers, Datasets, Metrics, and Results and Conclusions.
Martins et al. 2020
also cover defensive techniques against adversarial examples: Adversarial Training, Gradient Masking, Defensive Distillation, Feature Squeezing, Universal Perturbation Defense Method, and MagNet.

Guan et al. 2018
cover two related topics: ML’s impact on cybersecurity and AI systems’ robustness, presenting 14 papers. The paper labels attributes covering the Research Direction, whether AI for cybersecurity or security of AI, points of innovation in the paper, and a summary of the idea for each paper. The paper also synthesizes common threats and solutions in AI security, such as Data Sanitation and Learning Algorithm Improvement being the solution to Poisoning attacks, Adversarial Training and Output Smoothing being solutions to Evasion and Impersonation Attacks, and Differential Privacy and Homomorphic Encryption being the solution to Inversion Attacks.

Zhang et al. 2020
present a survey on privacy threats and mitigations within ML. The paper outlines a taxonomy for attacks, covering the Stage (where the attack happens), Goal, Knowledge, Capability, and Strategy of the attack. The authors summarize privacy threats against models as Parameter/Function, Hyperparameter, and Architecture, with defensive measures being Confidence Perturbation and Malicious Query Behaviour Detection. Privacy threats against data are summarized as Data Memorization attacks, Model Inversion Attacks, and Membership Inference attacks, with defensive measures being Hierarchical Model Stacking Techniques, Attribute Correlation with Adversarial Perturbations, Meta-Classifier, Collaborative Learning, Homomorphic Encryption, and Differential Privacy.

In summary, there is an evident lack of a systematic and comprehensive survey focusing on attacks targeting all kinds of ULs and corresponding defenses. Considering the potential importance of unsupervised learning in environments with a large amount of unlabeled data, a survey to cover the adversarial robustness of unsupervised machine learning would provide the industry with insight into the shortcomings of UL technologies and academia with knowledge on the state-of-the-art attacks and defenses that are covered in the field of adversarial robustness within UL.

4.
Research Design and Implementation

4.1.
Systematic Literature Review outline

In computer science,
Keele et al. 2007
show one framework for conducting an SLR within software engineering and related fields. This SLR uses the method outlined in
Keele et al. 2007
as a base, which includes 1) Creating the search query based on inclusion criteria, 2) Filtering based on exclusion criteria, and 3) Validating the quality of papers.

4.2.
Creating the search query

Based on the research questions and research focus, we generate the search query using a modified version of PICOC (Population, Intervention, Comparison, Outcome, Context) from
Petticrew and Roberts 2008
, without the
Comparison
section, as it was considered not applicable to the context of this SLR. While the PIOC framework groupings guide the query’s creation, the specific interpretation in
Keele et al. 2007
do not map perfectly to the chosen tokens, as described below.

•

Population:
Relating to the security of unsupervised machine learning, the population will cover general areas of technologies that are related to or specifically UL.

•

Intervention:
The intervention relates here to general descriptions of how an attacker might exploit vulnerabilities in UL systems.

•

Outcome:
Outcome relates to the desired data or information that a piece of research produces through their papers, specifically finding the vulnerabilities and defensive methods of UL methods.

•

Context:
The context relates to the context in which the research is explored, specifically which part the UL is considered, e.g., the technology versus human error.

As
Keele et al. 2007
do not outline a framework for evaluating the quality of the search query, other than following the PICOC framework, while
Denyer et al. 2009
only state “it is often wise to spend significant time on constructing the search strings, for this investment can improve the efficiency of the search”, we formulate here a method for evaluating the query. First, a list of 13 papers relating to security in UL was created, based on an ad-hoc search, in addition to previous knowledge of specific papers. We then search the IEEE Xplore, ACM Digital Library, arXiv, and Scopus databases using the search query and note how many of the papers in the ad-hoc list are present in the resulting list. While the ad-hoc nature of the list is not ideal, we consider this method better than the alternative, i.e., not having a baseline of comparison, as we know the papers in the ad-hoc list should ideally be present in the resulting list. The second consideration metric was the number of papers produced by the search query. For this, we would only accept queries that produced between 1000 and 10 000 papers to ensure enough relevant papers were included and ensure the following filtration process would not take too long. The results of the query testing are summarized in Table

. Rounds 1 and 2 were good examples of casting a too-narrow and too-wide net, highlighted by the matching percentage to the ad-hoc list and the total number of papers being very low and very high, respectively. Round 3 produced the correct number of papers but was considered not precise enough, with a matching score of 15%. Round 4 also produced the correct number of papers, increased the matching rate to the ad-hoc list, and was therefore chosen as the query for the SLR.

4.3.
Paper filtering

The initial list of papers was generated using the query in IEEE Xplore, ACM Digital Library, arXiv, and Scopus. Including unpublished papers in this review follows from the recommendation from
Denyer et al. 2009
“extensive searches are conducted to include both published and unpublished studies.” The initial list was imported into EndNote, automatically removing any duplicates. We used the inclusion criterion for filtering the papers: the paper must cover attacks against UL technologies. We show the exclusion criteria in Table

We performed several rounds of filtering. The first round focused on removing irrelevant papers from the list. In this round, the papers were considered only based on the title and evaluated on whether 1) It does not relate to security, 2) It does not relate to UL, or 3) The title singularly refers to supervised or reinforcement learning.
The second round focused on further limiting the number of irrelevant papers from the list. The evaluation criteria remain the same as for filtration round 1. Still, we give the evaluation for each paper more time, and we document each decision to filter in or out.
The third round limits the number of irrelevant papers even further, where we use the same criteria as filtration rounds 1 and 2, use more time for each evaluation and consider the paper’s abstract. Here, the evaluator can also consider a paper in its entirety to confidently ascertain whether it is relevant or not, according to the criteria referred to here as round 3.5.

4.4.
Quality Assessment

To ensure the papers contributing to the SLR have a certain quality,
Keele et al. 2007
also outline the importance of quality assessment (QA). Our QA consists of 11 questions based on the approach presented by
Keele et al. 2007
, scoring 0, 0.5, or 1 point per question. For any paper scoring lower than 5.5 points, we exclude the paper. Additionally, to ensure internal validity, a random sample of papers will be analyzed using the Cohen Kappa statistic
(
Warrens 2015
)
, measured between the primary researcher doing the QA, and a separate researcher, to ensure the QA scores are accurately applied. The questions used for the QA are in Appendix
A
.

4.5.
Snowballing

After the initial round of search, filtration, and QA, we also perform two backward and forward snowballing rounds to ensure we include as many relevant papers as possible in this SLR. For the first round of snowballing, the final list of papers after the QA is used as a base, and for the second round, we use the list of papers after QA from the first round of snowballing.
Backward snowballing
(BS) is performed by compiling a list of all the references used in each paper. In contrast,
forward snowballing
(FS) is performed using
Semantic Scholar
to find papers referencing each paper. After one round of initial search and two rounds of forward and backward snowballing, we combine the papers into a single list of papers to be analyzed.

4.7.
Data analysis and synthesis

Thematic analysis (TA) is a widely used method for analyzing qualitative data
(
Braun and Clarke 2006
)
, as it “offers an accessible and theoretically flexible approach to analyzing qualitative data.” Performing a TA consists of coding sections of the qualitative data based on its content and categorizing the codes into different themes, which in turn is used for describing and comparing data. Two main ways of performing the TA are either by grounding it in the content or using predetermined codes. In grounding the TA in the content, the codes and themes emerge from the data itself instead of being based on a theory of which codes should exist
(
Walker and Myrick 2006
)
. The grounded codes can, in turn, be used for creating hypotheses or theories based on the data. Using predetermined codes revolves around codes and themes emerging from predetermined assumptions, hypotheses, and knowledge, on the subject, and the codes and themes used within this for research based on these preconceptions. Grounded and predetermined codes can be combined into a hybrid process, with specific predetermined codes and themes complemented by grounded codes to generate the finished set of codes and themes.

5.1.
Attacks targeting AutoEncoder-based systems

We found 25 papers (29%) that cover
AutoEncoder
models in the SLR.

5.1.1.
Attack Method:

Attacks against
autoencoders
include
adversarial examples
,
inference and extraction
, and
poisoning
, which are covered by 60%, 16%, and 16% of the papers on
autoencoders
, respectively, and one paper
(
Tantipongpipat et al. 2021
)
cover the implementation of a differentially private
autoencoder
-
GAN
hybrid generator, aimed to protect against
privacy
attacks generally.

Adversarial Examples
can work at two points, i.e., perturbations of the actual input
(
Kim et al. 2020
;
Tabacof et al. 2016
;
Gondim-Ribeiro et al. 2018
;
Sun et al. 2021
;
Condessa and Kolter 2020
;
Willetts et al. 2019
;
Cemgil et al. 2019
;
Barrett et al. 2022
;
Camuto et al. 2021
;
Kos et al. 2018
;
Kuzina et al. 2021
;
Kuzina et al. 2022
;
Sun et al. 2020
)
, or on the latent code passed to the decoder
(
Albaseer et al. 2020
;
Deng and Sang 2020
;
Sadeghi and Larsson 2019
;
Sun et al. 2021
;
Kos et al. 2018
;
Kuzina et al. 2021
)
. The attacks appear to rely on where an attacker could have reasonable access to the input data. In contexts where the entire AutoEncoder is used as a single step, e.g., removing noise, attacks do not focus directly on the latent code but rather construct an attack that tricks the encoder into creating different latent codes, changing the output. Similarly, in contexts where the adversary has direct access to the latent code, e.g., physical transmission or generation, the focus is to create perturbations that trick the decoder.

Inference and extraction
attacks are limited to
membership inference

(
Chen et al. 2020b
;
Bernau et al. 2022
;
Chandrasekaran et al. 2021
;
Hilprecht et al. 2019
)
, where the adversary aims to extract information on whether the training used a specific sample.

Poisoning
attacks can be placed into three categories, depending on how and what the adversary does.
Injection
attacks
(
Sun et al. 2021
;
Nkashama et al. 2022
)
aims to disrupt a model by introducing new samples into the training set,
manipulation
attacks
(
Ding et al. 2019
;
Sun et al. 2021
;
Creswell et al. 2017
)
changes existing samples, and
logic corruption

(
Salem et al. 2020
;
Sun et al. 2021
)
aims to introduce a backdoor into the model, which can later be abused.

5.1.2.
Attack Knowledge:

We separate an adversary’s knowledge into
white box
,
grey box
, and
black box
, which are covered by 60%, 40%, and 28% of the papers on
autoencoders
, respectively.

White box
enable the attacker to craft their attack specifically to the model to enable their objective. For certain attacks like
membership inference

(
Chen et al. 2020b
;
Sun et al. 2021
)
,
adversarial examples

(
Tabacof et al. 2016
;
Gondim-Ribeiro et al. 2018
;
Condessa and Kolter 2020
;
Cemgil et al. 2019
;
Kos et al. 2018
;
Kuzina et al. 2021
;
Kuzina et al. 2022
)
,
poisoning

(
Creswell et al. 2017
;
Nkashama et al. 2022
;
Salem et al. 2020
)
, and
PGD

(
Cemgil et al. 2019
)
, white box knowledge enables a more optimized attack. In a physical system, knowledge of the AutoEncoder enables the adversary to perform a general attack
(
Albaseer et al. 2020
;
Sadeghi and Larsson 2019
)
. Additionally, white box attacks appear easier and more successful than scenarios of less knowledge
(
Chen et al. 2020b
;
Albaseer et al. 2020
;
Kim et al. 2020
;
Sadeghi and Larsson 2019
)
.

Grey box
attacks mean the adversary has incomplete knowledge of the AutoEncoder and has to make a sub-optimal attack. One such knowledge is the mapping between input and output values, whether the input is the latent code
(
Chen et al. 2020b
;
Sun et al. 2021
;
Camuto et al. 2021
)
or the actual data
(
Ding et al. 2019
;
Sun et al. 2021
;
Willetts et al. 2019
;
Barrett et al. 2022
;
Camuto et al. 2021
;
Sun et al. 2020
)
. Other attacks, like
adversarial examples

(
Deng and Sang 2020
)
, create substitute networks from the available information to craft universal perturbations, enabling attacks against any AutoEncoder. For attacks like
membership inference

(
Chandrasekaran et al. 2021
;
Bernau et al. 2022
)
, the adversary utilizes available training and test data to infer whether the training used specific samples. Grey box attacks also appear more successful than a black box and less than white box attacks
(
Chen et al. 2020b
)
.

Black box
attacks rely on the known vulnerabilities of AutoEncoders and make assumptions on the model and data to create the attack. For generative AutoEncoders like VAE, the adversary would only have direct access to the generated samples
(
Chen et al. 2020b
;
Sun et al. 2021
;
Kuzina et al. 2021
)
. In other systems like physical communication
(
Albaseer et al. 2020
)
,
adversarial examples

(
Kim et al. 2020
;
Sadeghi and Larsson 2019
)
, and
membership inference

(
Hilprecht et al. 2019
)
, the adversary uses an existing model as a substitute for the actual model, which enables the adversary to turn a
black box
attack into a kind of
white box
attack against the target AutoEncoder.

One paper
(
Tantipongpipat et al. 2021
)
focuses on implementing
differential privacy
, which is commonly used for
membership inference
attacks and would be most helpful in the context of limited knowledge.

5.1.3.
Attack Goal:

We separate an adversary’s goal into
integrity
,
privacy
, and
availability
, which are covered by 68%, 20%, and 12% of the papers on
autoencoders
, respectively.

Integrity
attacks aim to introduce new functionality, change existing functionality, or avoid the existing functionality of an AutoEncoder. Attacks often focus on maximization of distortion of the model’s output, on all, or subsets, of the input data
(
Gondim-Ribeiro et al. 2018
;
Sun et al. 2021
)
. For attacks like
adversarial examples

(
Kim et al. 2020
;
Tabacof et al. 2016
;
Condessa and Kolter 2020
;
Creswell et al. 2017
;
Willetts et al. 2019
;
Cemgil et al. 2019
;
Barrett et al. 2022
;
Camuto et al. 2021
;
Kos et al. 2018
;
Kuzina et al. 2021
;
Kuzina et al. 2022
;
Sun et al. 2020
)
, the adversary aim to force a change in the latent representation of the
autoencoder
, in turn producing different output from what the input data intended to produce. Similarly,
poisoning
attacks focus on introducing unintended functionality into the model, e.g., backdoors on specific input
(
Ding et al. 2019
;
Salem et al. 2020
)
, or prevent intended functionality of the model
(
Nkashama et al. 2022
)
.

Privacy
attacks have as a goal to extract some private information from the
autoencoder

(
Tantipongpipat et al. 2021
)
.
Membership inference

(
Chen et al. 2020b
;
Hilprecht et al. 2019
;
Chandrasekaran et al. 2021
;
Bernau et al. 2022
)
is the primary type of privacy attack against AutoEncoders, aiming to determine if a specific sample was used in training by analyzing the output from the model.

Availability
attacks aim to prevent the intended functionality of the model by performing the attack. In physical communication
(
Albaseer et al. 2020
;
Deng and Sang 2020
;
Sadeghi and Larsson 2019
)
, using
adversarial examples
causes a jamming attack on the decoder, preventing the model from decoding the encoded message.

5.1.4.
Metrics to measure attack success:

We separate the metrics used by an adversary to determine attack success into
distance metrics
, and
true/false positive/negative
based, which are covered by 60%, and 56% of the papers on
autoencoders
, respectively.

Distance metrics
refer to using a mathematical formula comparing two or two sets of samples to determine the amount of difference between them. Some metrics, like
Structured Similarity Index Measure
(SSIM),
Peak Signal-to-Noise Ratio
(PSNR),
Mean Square Deviation
(MSD), and
Root Mean Square Deviation
(RMSD) are used for comparing whether there is a noticeable difference between malicious and clean samples
(
Chen et al. 2020b
;
Tabacof et al. 2016
;
Hilprecht et al. 2019
;
Gondim-Ribeiro et al. 2018
;
Ding et al. 2019
;
Willetts et al. 2019
;
Kos et al. 2018
;
Kuzina et al. 2021
;
Kuzina et al. 2022
;
Salem et al. 2020
)
, with
Evidence Lower BOund
(ELBO)
(
Condessa and Kolter 2020
)
denoting the performance of the model in regards to the quality.
Area Under Distortion-Distortion Curve
(AUDDC) is used to measure how well an attack can influence the output of the model
(
Sun et al. 2020
)
. Other metrics, like
L_{2}
, are used as a threshold of perturbations in the input
(
Sun et al. 2021
;
Creswell et al. 2017
;
Barrett et al. 2022
;
Kos et al. 2018
)
.

True/False Positive/Negative based metrics
are used to determine the rate of success and failure by the attackers and determine how many measured instances are successes (true positive and true negative) and how many are failures (false positive and false negative). One way of using these metrics is to measure the performance of the attacked system with and without the influence of the attacker, e.g., detection systems
(
Nkashama et al. 2022
)
, classifiers using output from UL systems
(
Albaseer et al. 2020
;
Kim et al. 2020
;
Gondim-Ribeiro et al. 2018
;
Kuzina et al. 2022
)
, and physical communication
(
Albaseer et al. 2020
;
Deng and Sang 2020
;
Sadeghi and Larsson 2019
)
. In
adversarial examples
,
success rate
is used for determining if the attack works, both in a targeted and untargeted manner
(
Kos et al. 2018
)
. For other attacks like
membership inference

(
Chen et al. 2020b
;
Hilprecht et al. 2019
;
Chandrasekaran et al. 2021
;
Cemgil et al. 2019
;
Bernau et al. 2022
)
, the success is measured by how well their attack can differentiate if a sample was used for training by the
autoencoder
.

Additionally, two papers do not contribute with metrics to determine attack success, as they cover implementation of
differential privacy

(
Tantipongpipat et al. 2021
)
, or an analysis on adversarial robustness to
adversarial examples

(
Camuto et al. 2021
)
.

Attack focus:

We separate where the adversary focuses into
production
and
training
, which are covered by 92%, and 12% of the papers on
autoencoders
, respectively.

Production
models exist within their respective working environments, exposing them somewhat to the outside world.
Privacy
attacks like
membership inference

(
Chen et al. 2020b
;
Hilprecht et al. 2019
;
Chandrasekaran et al. 2021
;
Bernau et al. 2022
)
mainly only focus on production models, as the data pursued are no longer directly used by the model. Defenses like
differential privacy

(
Tantipongpipat et al. 2021
)
also would only be applicable for protecting a production model.
Adversarial examples

(
Albaseer et al. 2020
;
Deng and Sang 2020
;
Kim et al. 2020
;
Sadeghi and Larsson 2019
;
Tabacof et al. 2016
;
Gondim-Ribeiro et al. 2018
;
Sun et al. 2021
;
Condessa and Kolter 2020
;
Creswell et al. 2017
;
Willetts et al. 2019
;
Cemgil et al. 2019
;
Kos et al. 2018
;
Kuzina et al. 2021
;
Kuzina et al. 2022
;
Sun et al. 2020
)
is another type of attack mainly used on production models, where the adversary somehow has direct access to the input to the model, and can introduce perturbations to the input samples. Some papers cover generalized increases of
autoencoder
robustness to perturbations
(
Barrett et al. 2022
;
Camuto et al. 2021
)
, while others cover uses of
differential privacy
to enable privacy guarantees on the training data

Tantipongpipat et al. 2021

.

Training
models are models in the context of training for a specific purpose.
Poisoning
attacks
(
Ding et al. 2019
;
Creswell et al. 2017
;
Nkashama et al. 2022
;
Salem et al. 2020
)
is the only attack targeting
autoencoder
in training by modifying the training data itself to modify the training of the model in a specific direction.

Summary of attacks on
AutoEncoders
:

For
autoencoders
, the main source of attacks stems from
adversarial examples
, being usable against both
discriminatory
and
generative
models, often in a limited knowledge scenario in order to disrupt the output from the model. There are also attacks focusing on extracting information about the training data, which often are performed in minimum knowledge scenarios.
Poisoning
attacks are also used to disrupt or introduce new functionality to the model. An adversary targeting
autoencoders
also often relies on
distance metrics
to determine the difference between clean and malicious data in order to avoid simple detection techniques, while
true/false positive/negative
metrics are used for determining how successful the attack is.

against UL-supported classifiers means that the adversary crafts perturbations, which affect the UL system to the degree that the classification is also affected. An

.

Attack Knowledge:

We separate an adversary’s knowledge into
white box
and

, which are covered by 75% and 75% of the papers on

White box
attacks, in the use of
AutoEncoder
, corresponds to improved accuracy under

(

)
. In
semi-supervised

(

)
and

(

Black box
attacks against

with an

has a decreased performance with

(

)
. Additionally,
semi-supervised

(
Liu et al. 2019b
;

Attack Goal:

is

in 100% of the papers.

Integrity
attacks like

against

and

can construct poisoned samples that decrease the performance of the resulting classifier, while

(

)

5.2.4.
Metrics to measure attack success:

We separate the metrics used by an adversary to determine attack success into
distance metrics
, and
true/false positive/negative
based, which is covered by 50% and 75% of the papers on
UL supported classification
, respectively.

Distance metrics
have several different ways of measurement. One measurement of success is the degree to which the attacker can influence the results of the classifier through an unsupervised model, like with
semi-supervised

(
Liu et al. 2019b
)
, where the
RMSE
of the classification is used to determine success. When joined with an

accuracy
is used to determine the success of an attack
(

. Another measurement is the degree to which the attacker perturbs the input, as unrestricted perturbation would be visible to statistical analysis or human evaluation. For this, the
L_{2}
norm
(

can be used to determine perturbations in a

attack.

True/False Positive/Negative
-based metrics, like how well the classifier can function under attack, is one way of measuring attack success, e.g.,
adversarial examples

(
Kim et al. 2020
)
and

(

both utilizing the

of the classifier.

Attack focus:

Where the adversary focuses their attack is separated into

and

, which are covered by 75% and 25% of the papers on

, respectively.

Training
systems have adversaries that focus on disrupting classifier functionality by performing a

attack on

(

)
and

(

models.

Production
systems have adversaries where the focus is on disrupting the functionality of a classifier in a real-world environment, specifically using an

, as it is targeted by

Summary of attacks on

:

Attacks on

are mainly

, where the adversary perturbs data to influence the UL part, in turn influencing the classification; and

which influence the performance of the resulting classifier. Attacks appear to be most successful in a high-knowledge scenario. The measurements of success mainly focus on the classifier’s performance, e.g.,

, with

5.3.
Attacks targeting clustering

We found ten papers (12%) covering unsupervised clustering.

5.3.1.
Attack Method:

Attacks against
clustering
include
poisoning
,
evasion
, and
adversarial examples
, which are covered by 60%, 50%, and 10% of the papers on
clustering
, respectively.

Poisoning
attacks work by inserting crafted samples that compromise the cluster’s functionality. One such attack is the
bridging
attack
(
Biggio et al. 2013
;
Biggio et al. 2014a
;
Biggio et al. 2014b
;
Sabottke et al. 2019
)
, where the adversary crafts samples that "bridges" gaps between clusters, in turn altering the resulting clusters. Other approaches involve manipulating nodes and edges of
graph-based clustering

(
Chen et al. 2017
)
to disrupt the system or to alter existing samples from the training set according to a specified maximum total perturbation
(
Cinà et al. 2022
)
.

Evasion
attacks consist of crafting samples with features overlapping with the actual sample set to conceal the sample and avoid detection without significantly altering the clustering functionality
(
Banerjee et al. 2018
;
Biggio et al. 2013
;
Biggio et al. 2014a
;
Biggio et al. 2014b
;
Anindya and Kantarcioglu 2018
)
.

Adversarial examples
work by optimizing the perturbation relating to the maximization of the clustering results
(
Yang et al. 2020
)
.

One paper
(
Rösner and Schmidt 2018
)
covers the construction of a privacy constraint enforced in training to prevent private sample information from being inferred to protect against privacy attacks.

5.3.2.
Attack Knowledge:

We separate an adversary’s knowledge into
white box
,
grey box
, and
black box
, which are covered by 50%, 50%, and 10% of the papers on
clustering
, respectively.

White box
attacks can optimize
poisoning

(
Biggio et al. 2013
;
Biggio et al. 2014a
;
Biggio et al. 2014b
;
Sabottke et al. 2019
;
Chen et al. 2017
)
attacks, manipulate the resulting cluster, and optimize
obfuscation

(
Biggio et al. 2013
)
attacks by crafting adversarial samples to hide samples in existing clusters.

Grey box
means adversaries would have access to a limited data mapping with its resulting cluster.
Bridging
attacks
(
Biggio et al. 2013
)
can be used to gradually discover samples that disrupt the function of the resulting cluster, or for
evasion

(
Anindya and Kantarcioglu 2018
)
and
adversarial examples

(
Yang et al. 2020
)
, use the mapping between samples and resulting clusters to optimize perturbation needed to avoid detection by the clustering. Some methods of
poisoning
attacks
(
Cinà et al. 2022
)
rely on constructing a general perturbation based on the mapping between samples and resulting clusters by using a
genetic algorithm
to discover optimizations iteratively. Other limited knowledge attacks rely on using approximate models based on open-sourced data and performing
white box
attacks against these approximate models to construct their attack
(
Chen et al. 2017
)
.

Black box
attacks include adversaries who can employ an
evasion
attack
(
Banerjee et al. 2018
)
by slowly enforcing drift in the clustering towards a specific point, which does not require any information of the model.

One paper
(
Rösner and Schmidt 2018
)
enforces privacy constraints in training and would likely be most applicable in limited knowledge scenarios.

5.3.3.
Attack Goal:

We separate an adversary’s goal into
integrity
,
privacy
, and
availability
, which are covered by 68%, 20%, and 12% of the papers on
clustering
, respectively.

Availability
attacks work by preventing or disrupting the general functionality of the clustering.
Poisoning
attacks
(
Biggio et al. 2013
;
Biggio et al. 2014a
;
Biggio et al. 2014b
;
Cinà et al. 2022
;
Sabottke et al. 2019
;
Chen et al. 2017
)
work by introducing specific samples to the training to influence the shape of the resulting clusters, to the degree that prevents the clustering from working as intended.

Integrity
attacks focus on influencing the clustering results regarding specific samples.
Evasion
attacks aim to hide specific samples in existing clusters by overlapping their features with those of actual samples
(
Banerjee et al. 2018
;
Biggio et al. 2013
)
or by slowly introducing modified samples to drift the clustering in a specific direction
(
Anindya and Kantarcioglu 2018
)
.
Adversarial examples

(
Yang et al. 2020
)
aim to modify samples in a way that results in the misclustering of samples.

Privacy
attacks cover theoretical constraints on clusters to ensure the privacy of the samples used to train the model
(
Rösner and Schmidt 2018
)
.

5.3.4.
Metrics to measure attack success:

We separate the metrics used by an adversary to determine attack success into
true/false positive/negative
,
distance metrics
,
number of clusters
, and
objective function
, which are covered by 50%, 40%, 40%, and 30% of the papers on
clustering
, respectively.

True/false positive/negative
metrics, like
accuracy
used in detection
(
Banerjee et al. 2018
;
Anindya and Kantarcioglu 2018
)
to determine its performance against
evasion
attacks. In
poisoning
attacks,
F-measure

(

)
and

(

)
are used for determining the quality of the clusters produced under attack. In
adversarial examples

(

,

measures how well the clusters fit the labels.

are used in

attacks
(

)
to craft samples to avoid simple detection. Metrics like

,

, and

(

)
to limit the amount any single feature can be modified. For
adversarial examples
,
Normalized Mutual Information
(NMI) is used to compare actual and predicted cluster labels, and
distortion
measures the perturbation of the sample
(
Yang et al. 2020
)
.

Number of clusters
is used to compare the performance of attacks and their capability of reducing or increasing the number of clusters the model produces.
Poisoning
attacks
(
Biggio et al. 2013
;
Biggio et al. 2014a
;
Biggio et al. 2014b
;
Sabottke et al. 2019
)
use this metric to determine how well the attack works.

Objective function
based metrics are used in
(
Biggio et al. 2013
;
Biggio et al. 2014a
;
Biggio et al. 2014b
)
and are crafted for the specific attack and model.

One paper
(
Rösner and Schmidt 2018
)
does not describe concrete attacks and, as such, does not outline any metrics for the success of an attack.

5.3.5.
Attack focus:

We separate the adversary focus into
production
and
training
, which are covered by 50%, and 50% of the papers on
clustering
, respectively.

Production
systems are targeted by attacks like

(

)
and

(

, where the aim is to reduce the functionality of specific samples while keeping the general clustering functionality intact, while other attacks like

(

aim to disrupt the functionality of existing

. Additionally, papers, like
(

attacks
(

;

;
Biggio et al. 2014b
;
Cinà et al. 2022
;
Sabottke et al. 2019

, modifying or injecting malicious samples to the training set, which can significantly reduce the functionality of the clustering systems.

Summary of attacks on
clustering
:

Poisoning
attacks on clustering involve introducing samples to disrupt the cluster functionality, merge, or increase the number of clusters found. Evasion attacks work by hiding specific samples within clusters to avoid detection without altering the cluster’s functionality. Most attacks on clustering rely on increased knowledge, as the adversary has a hard time accomplishing their goals without knowing the data samples used for training.
True/False Positive/Negative
metrics are used by the adversary to determine how good the resulting cluster is, measuring how well the cluster fits the labels. In contrast,
distance metrics
limit the amount of perturbation a sample can have. The

for

in order to facilitate sharing models and datasets without risk of privacy violations

;
Beaulieu-Jones et al. 2019

, context-dependent implementations of privacy guarantees to enable private sharing

Inference and extraction
attacks, like
membership inference
attacks, are methods of attacks to determine whether specific samples were used during the training of the GAN model
(
Frigerio et al. 2019
;
Lin et al. 2021
)
. Methods for this include using distance metrics between the sample and generated samples
(
Chen et al. 2020b
;
Hilprecht et al. 2019
;
Mukherjee et al. 2021
;
Sun et al. 2021
;
Hassanzadeh and Tillman 2022
)
, using discriminator confidence
(
Hayes et al. 2017
;
Sun et al. 2021
;
Hassanzadeh and Tillman 2022
)
, or training a classifier
(
Lin et al. 2020
;
Sun et al. 2021
;
Kunar et al. 2021
;
Zhou et al. 2021
)
, in order to determine whether the training used a sample. Other methods determine membership by controlling the latent code of the generator and determining membership based on the reconstruction error of the generator when trying to generate specific samples
(
Sum Liu et al. 2018
)
.
Attribute inference
is another way of extracting private attributes from GANs by looking at publicly known features, collecting generated samples, and using regression to predict the private features
(
Sun et al. 2021
;
Kunar et al. 2021
)
.
Model extraction
is a method for cloning an existing model based on the generated samples of a target GAN, like
accuracy extraction

(
Sun et al. 2021
;
Hu and Pang 2021
)
, where the adversary measures the difference in the distribution of the cloned and actual model; and
fidelity extraction

(
Sun et al. 2021
;
Hu and Pang 2021
)
compares the distribution of the cloned model to the actual training data set. The cloned model can subsequently be used as the substitute model in a
white-box

membership inference
attack
(
Hayes et al. 2017
;
Hu et al. 2021
)
.

Poisoning
attacks is, among other, used against federated GAN training, where the adversary can contribute to the global model as one or multiple participant(s) and subsequently modify the information that it contributes, and affect the performance of the global model
(
Zhang et al. 2019
;
Jin and Li 2022
)
. Other methods modify the training data directly, either by injecting new samples
(
Sun et al. 2021
)
, modifying existing samples to disrupt the model
(
Sun et al. 2021
)
, or modifying samples to introduce new by-products in the generated samples
(
Ding et al. 2019
;
Rawat et al. 2021
;
Sun et al. 2021
;
Jin and Li 2022
;
Salem et al. 2020
)
.

Adversarial examples
attack relies on access to the latent code used by the generator and perturbing some input into it to generate a completely different sample from what would have been generated without the perturbation.
Adversarial examples
can be used during training for
adversarial training

(
Liu and Hsieh 2019
;
Sajeeda and Hossain 2022
)
, increasing the robustness of the model. Other papers focus on implementing changes to how the
GAN
works in order to be theoretically robust against
adversarial examples

(
Chrysos et al. 2020
)
. Additionally, metrics commonly used to determine sample quality, e.g.,
Fréchet Inception Distance
(FID) and
Inseption Score
(IS), are also explored and are determined to be insufficient in discovering
adversarial examples

(
Alfarra et al. 2022
)
.

Evasion
attacks focus on finding minimal perturbations on the latent codes, which produce unsatisfactory samples, compared to what is produced by the unperturbed latent code
(
Sun et al. 2021
)
, in order to generate unsatisfactory samples, but still following the statistical distribution of the training samples.

)

)

and

Training

Summary of attacks against
GANs
:

The primary attacks against
GANs
revolve around extracting information from the model based on the generated samples they produce, including:
membership inference
, where the adversary tries to find if the training used a specific sample,
attribute inference
to extract the features of the training data, and
model extraction
, where one clone the model, in turn giving the adversary access to optimize attacks on the cloned model to attack the original model subsequently.
Poisoning
attacks are also used, where the adversary crafts samples to be used in training, either to decrease the utility of the generated models or to introduce backdoors into the generated samples. Knowledge of the model is also essential, as more knowledge enables optimized attacks. However,
membership inference
attacks are made with only knowledge of the generated samples.
Distance metrics
are used for determining the model’s performance by analyzing the clean and attacked generated samples. In contrast,
true/false positive/negative
determines the degree to which the attack was successful.

5.5.
Super Resolution

We found three papers (3%) covering super-resolution models.

5.5.1.
Attack Method:

Attacks against
super resolution
only include
adversarial examples
.

Adversarial example
attacks perturb the input to the model to alter the result significantly
(
Choi et al. 2019
;
Castillo et al. 2021
;
Yue et al. 2021
)
. One paper also produces general perturbations for super-resolution
(
Choi et al. 2019
)
, i.e., the perturbation is independent of the model’s training.

5.5.2.
Attack Knowledge:

The adversary’s knowledge is only covered by
white box
attacks.

White-box
attacks revolve around knowing a mapping between input and output, and, with limited perturbations on the input, being able to produce large perturbations on the output
(
Choi et al. 2019
;
Castillo et al. 2021
;
Yue et al. 2021
)
. For this attack, an adversary would require access to the model to know the perturbed output. The perturbation is iteratively fine-tuned, which also applies to the general perturbation
(
Choi et al. 2019
)
.

5.5.3.
Attack Goal:

Integrity
attacks are the only goal covered for
super-resolution
.

Integrity
attacks, like
adversarial examples
, have as a goal to insert imperceptible perturbation on the input to the model, in turn causing an extensive deterioration of the output from the model
(
Choi et al. 2019
;
Castillo et al. 2021
;
Yue et al. 2021
)
.

5.5.4.
Metrics to measure attack success:

distance metrics
are the only metrics an adversary uses to determine attack success for
super-resolution
.

Distance metrics
include the difference between perturbed and unperturbed input and output measurements. The metrics used for this include
PSNR

(
Choi et al. 2019
;
Castillo et al. 2021
;
Yue et al. 2021
)
measuring noise,
SSIM

(
Castillo et al. 2021
;
Yue et al. 2021
)
measuring perceptual differences, and
Learned Perceptual Image Patch Similarity
(LPIPS)
(
Castillo et al. 2021
)
measuring the difference in activation in the model on two inputs.

5.5.5.
Target Focus:

The adversary only focuses their attack on
production
systems.

Production
systems under
adversarial examples
mean an adversary makes perturbations to the input to the model
(
Choi et al. 2019
;
Castillo et al. 2021
;
Yue et al. 2021
)
, in order to cause large perturbations in the output.

Summary of attacks against
super-resolution
:

Against
super resolution
,
adversarial examples
is the only attack, focusing on disrupting the model’s output by perturbing the input, requiring complete knowledge of the model to perform the attack. The attack focuses mainly on using
distance metrics
to measure the difference between clean and adversarial input and output.

6.1.
Defenses against attacks targeting AutoEncoder

A summary of the relationship between the attack type and defensive measure for autoencoders is shown in Figure
9
.

Adversarial training
consists of using adversaries’ strategy to generate samples to be learned by the model, making the model more robust against the specific attack used to generate the samples. This defense has certain drawbacks, like reducing performance on clean samples
(
Kim et al. 2020
;
Sadeghi and Larsson 2019
)
or lacking generalizability on other perturbations
(
Sadeghi and Larsson 2019
)
.

Smoothing
, also referred to as
gradient masking
, works by preventing adversaries from inferring information on the internal gradients of the model
(
Kim et al. 2020
;
Sun et al. 2021
;
Willetts et al. 2019
;
Cemgil et al. 2019
;
Barrett et al. 2022
;
Sun et al. 2020
)
, providing noticeable robustness increase against
adversarial examples
in
white-box
settings
(
Kim et al. 2020
)
, and marginal robustness increase in
black-box
settings
(
Kim et al. 2020
)
.

Robustness constraints
work by enforcing a guarantee on the lower bounds of when perturbations in the input would affect the output. In particular, this is targeted as a general defense against
adversarial examples

(
Condessa and Kolter 2020
;
Camuto et al. 2021
)
.

Markov Chain Monte Carlo
(MCMC) is employed on the latent code from the encoder, where the variables are updated before being used by the decoder
(
Kuzina et al. 2022
)
. This method provides robustness against
adversarial examples
and higher quality data than
autoencoder
under attack.

Differential privacy
ensures that, under certain constraints, an adversary cannot determine if a specific sample was used in training, providing slight
(
Chen et al. 2020b
;
Chandrasekaran et al. 2021
)
to large
(
Bernau et al. 2022
)
amount of privacy protection, depending on the privacy budget
(
Bernau et al. 2022
)
. Attacks like
membership inference

(
Chen et al. 2020b
;
Sun et al. 2021
;
Bernau et al. 2022
)
,
attribute inference

(
Sun et al. 2021
)
, and
model extraction

(
Sun et al. 2021
)
can be mitigated by using this method. Differential privacy is also used as a way of ensuring the privacy of synthetic data
(
Tantipongpipat et al. 2021
)
. However, this method requires more data resources and causes a decrease in the quality of the data generated
(
Chen et al. 2020b
;
Chandrasekaran et al. 2021
;
Tantipongpipat et al. 2021
;
Bernau et al. 2022
)
.

Privacy Constraints
is another method for ensuring the privacy of training data when releasing synthetic data
(
Chandrasekaran et al. 2021
)
. Here though, differential privacy is implemented with a causal model, where the utility of the generated samples is higher than non-causal differential privacy implementations for the same privacy budget.

Dropout
works on neural networks by dropping random neurons during training to prevent the model from overfitting the data. While the method is effective against
membership inference

(
Hilprecht et al. 2019
;
Sun et al. 2021
)
, it also results in a quality decrease of generated samples and requires more computer resources
(
Hilprecht et al. 2019
;
Sun et al. 2021
)
.

Weight normalization
works by periodically re-parameterizing the neural network weights. In turn, the re-parametrizing can increase the model’s generalizability and provide some protection against
membership inference
attacks
(
Sun et al. 2021
)
.

Fine pruning
works in neural networks by removing specific neurons that are not improving the performance on clean samples. The method is not, however, proven effective for autoencoders, as "fine-pruning does not remove the by-product task" introduced by
poisoning
attacks
(
Ding et al. 2019
;
Sun et al. 2021
)
. Additionally, fine pruning also decreases the quality of generated samples and requires more computing resources
(
Ding et al. 2019
;
Sun et al. 2021
)
.

Activation Output Clustering
works by analyzing a hidden layer of a neural network to discover different distributions between clean and adversarial input. The defense does not work for generative models. However, the strategy struggles to differentiate between real and adversarial input
(
Ding et al. 2019
;
Sun et al. 2021
)
.

Lastly, several papers do not describe defensive measures against their respective adversarial attack, like
adversarial examples

(
Albaseer et al. 2020
;
Deng and Sang 2020
;
Tabacof et al. 2016
;
Gondim-Ribeiro et al. 2018
;
Kos et al. 2018
;
Kos et al. 2018
;
Kuzina et al. 2021
)
, and
poisoning

(
Creswell et al. 2017
;
Nkashama et al. 2022
)
.

6.2.
Defenses against attacks targeting classification

Adversarial training
for defending UL-supported classification is done by adversarially training an
autoencoder
and the classifier
(
Kim et al. 2020
)
, with adversarial robustness increasing or decreasing depending on the training set.
Kim et al. 2020
conclude that adversarial robustness using an
autoencoder
and
adversarial training
provide the same effect, differing from the robustness provided by
gradient masking
.

Relabeling
entails calculating the influence a specific sample would have on the training in case of
poisoning

(
Franci et al. 2022
)
, and re-labeling the sample, in turn decreasing the performance of the
poisoning attack
.

6.3.
Defenses against attacks targeting clustering

A summary of the relationship between the attack type and defensive measure for clustering is shown in Figure
11
.

Game theory
is proposed as a general defensive strategy for clustering. The general strategy involves the target employing defensive measures depending on specified conditions
(
Banerjee et al. 2018
)
or modeling the attacker-target system as an adversarial game to determine optimal defenses
(
Biggio et al. 2013
)
for a variety of attacks.

Adversarial training
is employed with adversarial strategies like
adversarial examples

(
Yang et al. 2020
)
,
noise injection

(
Chen et al. 2017
)
, and
small community

(
Chen et al. 2017
)
, which ensures the clustering trains to be more robust against these specific attacks. The drawback of this method, however, is the potential reduction of the overall
accuracy
of the models
(
Chen et al. 2017
)
, though some results indicate that
adversarial training
does improve the performance
(
Yang et al. 2020
)
.

Robustness enhancement
constructs feature sets for the clustering consisting of binaries for important features and numerical values for secondary features
(
Sabottke et al. 2019
)
, preventing adversarial attacks from merging clusters.

Privacy enhancement
applies privacy constraints to the clustering process for the anonymity of samples belonging to a specific cluster to be ensured
(
Rösner and Schmidt 2018
)
.

Lastly, several papers do not describe defensive measures against adversarial attacks, like
poisoning

(
Biggio et al. 2014a
;
Biggio et al. 2014b
;
Cinà et al. 2022
)
and
evasion

(
Biggio et al. 2013
;
Anindya and Kantarcioglu 2018
)
.

6.4.
Defenses against attacks targeting GAN

A summary of the relationship between the attack type and defensive measure for GANs is shown in Figure
11
.

Differential privacy
is used as a defensive method in 27 papers. The research in
(
Xie et al. 2018
;
Zhang et al. 2018
;
Beaulieu-Jones et al. 2019
;
Jordon et al. 2018
;
Xu et al. 2019
;
Chen et al. 2020a
;
Zhang et al. 2021
;
Rajotte and Ng 2020
;
Kossen et al. 2022
;
Tantipongpipat et al. 2021
;
Fan 2020
;
Long et al. 2021
;
Badu-Marfo et al. 2020
;
Chen et al. 2022
;
Gupta et al. 2020
;
Liu et al. 2019a
;
Tinsley et al. 2021
;
Torfi et al. 2022
)
all cover theoretical applications of differential privacy. The defense is also applied specifically to counter
membership inference
in
(
Chen et al. 2020b
;
Lin et al. 2020
;
Frigerio et al. 2019
;
Hayes et al. 2017
;
Mukherjee et al. 2021
;
Sun et al. 2021
;
Hu et al. 2021
;
Kunar et al. 2021
;
Lin et al. 2021
)
, providing slight
(
Kunar et al. 2021
;
Chen et al. 2020b
;
Hu et al. 2021
)
to large
(
Frigerio et al. 2019
;
Mukherjee et al. 2021
;
Hu et al. 2021
)
privacy protection, depending on the privacy budget
(
Hayes et al. 2017
;
Mukherjee et al. 2021
;
Hu et al. 2021
)
and training set size
(
Frigerio et al. 2019
)
. Differential privacy can also cause quality decreases in generated samples
(
Hayes et al. 2017
;
Mukherjee et al. 2021
)
and cause groups of data to become over-represented by the generator
(
Mukherjee et al. 2021
)
.

Privacy Enchancement
are methods for ensuring the privacy of training samples without implementing differential privacy. In
(
Hassanzadeh and Tillman 2022
)
, the model trains to generate samples independent of the set of samples used for training to protect the model from
membership inference
attacks. In
(
Oleszkiewicz et al. 2018
)
, the model trains the discriminator to determine whether two samples belong to the same identity, which, through the adversarial min-max game between the generator and the discriminator, forces the generator to produce private samples.

Weight normalization
recalculates the parameters of the weight in the model in order to improve generalizability
(
Sun et al. 2021
)
. While the method can reduce the accuracy of
memberhsip inference
attacks
(
Hayes et al. 2017
;
Sun et al. 2021
)
, it also can result in instability between the discriminator and the generator
(
Hayes et al. 2017
;
Sun et al. 2021
)
.

Dropout
works by removing random neurons in a neural network to prevent overfitting by the model and might be viable for GANs, as it decreases the accuracy of
membership inference
attacks
(
Hayes et al. 2017
;
Hilprecht et al. 2019
)
, but at the same time reduces the quality of the generated samples
(
Hayes et al. 2017
;
Hilprecht et al. 2019
;
Sun et al. 2021
)
.

Data perturbation
is a method to perturb the output data produced by the model in order to protect against
model extraction
attacks
(
Sun et al. 2021
;
Hu and Pang 2021
)
, by adding different kinds of noise, e.g., Gaussian Filtering and JPEG compression.

Adversarial training
is used by
Liu and Hsieh 2019
, with an additional classifier to increase the generator’s robustness and generalizability. In contrast,
Sajeeda and Hossain 2022
find other research covering adversarial training for the discriminator and how adversarially trained GAN can be robust against perturbations.

Robustness enhancement
is a method for forcing the generator to generate samples within the target space, in particular samples under adversarial and non-adversarial noise
(
Chrysos et al. 2020
)
, in order to avoid lousy quality data from being generated under noise.

Fine pruning
works by removing neurons in a neural network that does not provide good quality generated samples but did not remove the introduced by-products from
poisoning
attacks and decreased the performance of the model
(
Sun et al. 2021
)
.

Activation Output Clustering
analyzes one of the layers of a neural network and clusters the values from these, with the hope of differentiating between actual and malicious data from a
poisoning
attack
(
Ding et al. 2019
)
.

Inspection
relies on analyzing the model parameters, i.e.,
static model inspection

(
Rawat et al. 2021
)
; analyzing the model behavior in action, i.e.,
dynamic model inspection

(
Rawat et al. 2021
)
; or analyzing the output of the model to compare with known adversarial data
(
Rawat et al. 2021
)
. The defensive measure appears unreliable in analyzing the output, and model inspection methods require knowledge of the adversary to be effective.

Lastly, some papers cover adversarial attacks that are not covered by any defensive measures, like
membership inference

(
Zhou et al. 2021
;
Sum Liu et al. 2018
)
,
poisoning

(
Zhang et al. 2019
;
Jin and Li 2022
;
Salem et al. 2020
)
, and
adversarial examples

(
Alfarra et al. 2022
)
.

6.5.
Defenses against attacks targeting super-resolution:

As shown in Section
5.5
, all attacks on
super-resolution
consists of
adversarial examples
.

Data pre-processing
is one such defense, covered by
Choi et al. 2019
, where the data size is reduced by one pixel and subsequently resized to the original size, resulting in an increase of the measured
PSNR
on the adversarial input.

Ensamble
methods covered by
Choi et al. 2019
use a
geometric self-ensemble
method, where the original images are geometrically transformed and passed through the model, subsequently inversely transformed and combined to create the final upscaled image. This method increases the adversarial inputs measured by
PSNR
.

Adversarial training
is performed by including
adversarial examples
in the re-training of the super-resolution model, which increases model generalizability, with the model performing better against
adversarial examples

(
Castillo et al. 2021
;
Yue et al. 2021
)
.

7.
Results of RQ3: Challenges to secure UL-based systems

7.1.
Challenges to secure systems using AutoEncoders

As autoencoders are applicable for several different purposes, there are also different challenges depending on the use. For generative autoencoders,
privacy

(
Chen et al. 2020b
;
Hilprecht et al. 2019
;
Chandrasekaran et al. 2021
;
Bernau et al. 2022
)
attack is a problem, particularly when an adversary has access to the model parameters
(
Chen et al. 2020b
)
. Though there are defenses like
differential privacy

(
Chen et al. 2020b
;
Tantipongpipat et al. 2021
;
Bernau et al. 2022
)
,
fine pruning

(
Ding et al. 2019
)
,
activation output clustering

(
Ding et al. 2019
)
, the impact on generated samples is notable. Autoencoder systems for encoding/decoding, or noise reduction, are also vulnerable, particularly against
adversarial examples
in the input
(
Tabacof et al. 2016
;
Mukherjee et al. 2021
)
, or latent space
(
Albaseer et al. 2020
;
Creswell et al. 2017
)
. Defensive measures for these kinds of attacks on autoencoders are also not very well researched
(
Sadeghi and Larsson 2019
)
.

7.2.
Challenges to secure UL supported classifications

We can divide the adversarial robustness of classification into two groups. Firstly is the use of UL systems to provide adversarial robustness of the classifier, e.g., using an
autoencoder
in front of the classifier
(
Kim et al. 2020
)
. Secondly is the exploration of adversarial robustness in systems employing UL in tandem with classification, e.g., using
clustering
to separate malicious and legitimate data for classification
(
Banerjee et al. 2018
)
, training classifiers on generated samples
(
Zhang et al. 2019
;
Rajotte and Ng 2020
;
Oleszkiewicz et al. 2018
;
Kunar et al. 2021
;
Bernau et al. 2022
;
Chen et al. 2022
;
Gupta et al. 2020
)
, or employing a classifier in addition to discriminator in a
GAN

(
Liu and Hsieh 2019
)
. In this case, while there exist several attacks, like
poisoning

(
Liu et al. 2019b
;
Zhang et al. 2019
;
Franci et al. 2022
;
Wu and He 2021
)
, defensive measures in the literature is mainly theoretical, like
game theory

(
Banerjee et al. 2018
)
.

7.3.
Challenges to secure clustering

While clustering as a concept relies on finding optimal groups of samples based on some distance metric, how the distance is calculated impacts the vulnerabilities and adversarial robustness of clustering. It is speculated that the specific
poisoning
attacks by
Biggio et al. 2013
might only apply to
single-linkage hierarchical
clustering, while other clustering methods would not. Additionally, known vulnerabilities of clustering are still not explored enough in the literature, as most attacks focus on specific clustering methods
(
Biggio et al. 2013
;
Biggio et al. 2014a
;
Biggio et al. 2014b
;
Rösner and Schmidt 2018
)
. Defending clustering methods is also not very well explored
(
Biggio et al. 2013
)
, with methods like
adversarial simulation

(
Biggio et al. 2013
)
and general privacy constraints
(
Rösner and Schmidt 2018
)
left as future work in the surveyed papers.

Adversarial robustness for GAN models is covered by various papers, with many limitations. One of the major limitations is the compromise between GAN utility and the implementation of defensive measures.

8.1.
Comparison with related work

Liu et al. 2018

Zhang et al. 2020
do not outline their method of discovering the list of papers used for their analysis, and
Martins et al. 2020
only outline their search engines and keywords. This study follows a more SLR systematic approach, and the papers covered by this study are, therefore, more complete and representative of the state of the field.

Table

The most notable difference between this paper, and the other papers, is that this paper has not found any defenses using
homomorphic encryption

homomorphic encryption
, 2) unsupervised learning does not work well with

homomorphic encryption
has not yet been widely used as a defensive measure for unsupervised learning. Doing a surface search on
Google Scholar

homomorphic encryption
might be a viable future endeavor.
Other defensive measures that are present in the other papers but not in ours, like
reject on negative impact
,
defensive distillation
,
universal perturbation defense method

reverse poisoning

query distribution detection
,
hierarchical model stacking

alternating training
,
defensive perturbation

8.2.
Implications to academia

Our study gives a structured and comprehensive summary of the state-of-the-art adversarial robustness of unsupervised learning and how this can be used for further research.
Attack knowledge
is one of the core parts of the attack, and while specific attacks like
adversarial examples
and
membership inference
can work with minimal knowledge, all attacks perform noticeably better with an increase in knowledge of the system. In this regard, our paper aims at structuring and summarizing the performance of attacks and defenses depending on the amount of knowledge, which could be used in future research to make comparisons easier. Specific attacks also employ substitute models trained to mimic the actual model and perform full-knowledge attacks on these, which can transfer to the actual model. Future research should therefore consider a broader range of knowledge scenarios in their analysis, as a
black box
attack could be turned into a
white box
attack using such methods.

Another critical attack aspect is the
attack goal
. On the one hand, our paper shows that most attacks focus on infringing on the
privacy
of the target, which in turn motivates future research into finding methods for ensuring privacy since this is the most popular attack vector. Additionally, we believe our findings that
differential privacy
impact model performance should motivate future research into providing privacy guarantees with less impact on performance. On the other hand, our paper also shows that
integrity
attacks are effective. However, their defenses are not given as much focus in the research, which is something we wish to motivate further research in doing.

Future research would also benefit from this paper concerning the kinds of attacks leveraged against specific technologies. We can, for example, see that
poisoning
attacks target most technologies; or that there are no
privacy
attacks on
clustering
, which future research could use as motivation for studying these topics.

We also believe the summaries on
metrics
can enable future research with both motivations for selecting specific metrics and categorizing which metrics are used by which papers, making comparisons between papers easier.

Regarding
defenses
, we believe our paper provides future research with several significant findings. We categorize how each defensive measure is used, for which technology, and against which attack they are leveraged. We hope to motivate future research to utilize this survey in finding defenses and make for easier comparisons when researching defensive measures. We have also presented how each defense performs in terms of effectiveness against specific attacks and, where applicable, how they impact the model’s performance. In turn, we hope to motivate future research into less performance-reducing defenses. Lastly, while many papers cover complete knowledge scenarios to defend against a worst-case attack, only focusing on one level of knowledge might also be a problem, as defensive measures in different attacker knowledge scenarios are not necessarily transferable. Therefore, as mentioned in regards to
attack knowledge
, we believe future research should consider how the defense is impacted when employed against attacks in different knowledge scenarios.

One of the main challenges facing adversarially robust UL is the general need for more focus on constructing defensive measures against attacks. Much research focuses primarily on the vulnerability of a specific model without formulating defenses. Additionally, when research does focus on defensive measures, it is mostly against specific attacks. While exploring specific defenses is good, there is a noticeable lack of more general defensive measures, with the big exception of
differential privacy
, which has seen the most focus out of any defense in UL. We, therefore, believe that future research would benefit from focusing on more generalizable defenses.

We believe our model in Figure

of analyzing adversarial robustness, i.e., looking at

,

,

,

,

Future research also needs to be performed in a manner that enables easy comparisons between results by explicitly defining these different categories.

8.3.
Implication to industry

Those in the industry who utilize any of the technologies presented, e.g., clustering for anomaly detection or synthetic data generation, can use the results in this paper to better evaluate their use of this technology, e.g., in risk assessment. Additionally, since a significant part of this paper explicitly outlines the state-of-the-art concerning defensive measures for these technologies, this paper would be a good resource for finding and implementing defensive measures to protect their UL models. Lastly, our focus on finding vulnerabilities in these technologies could motivate the industry to consider the security of their models and not just use unsupervised models for their utility.

8.4.
Threats to validity

We strictly followed the SLR guideline to ensure the method was unambiguous and considered biases that could influence the results’ quality. However, we could still identify some possible validity threats related to this study.

The researchers’ knowledge of adversarial robustness in unsupervised learning would inevitably impact the search query. It would impact the specific words and the decision for when the query is good enough. The researchers have tried to mitigate this impact by 1) Documenting the specific papers that served as a basis for what the search should produce and 2) The initial search query would only be accepted if it produced between 1000 and 10 000 papers.

Another possible threat to validity is the research databases used for finding papers. This study covered as many relevant databases as possible, including IEEE Xplore, ACM Digital Library, arXiv, and Scopus.

When filtering the papers, for the first round of filtration, where papers were excluded based on the title alone, the researchers had to decide to exclude instead of include a paper explicitly. The reasoning is that deciding to exclude requires more thought than an inclusive decision, making the researcher less likely to exclude relevant papers. From the second round onward, the researchers documented their reasoning for exclusion or inclusion and produced documentation that could be scrutinized.

Regarding the quality assurance of the papers, there are also several possible biases to consider. First, there is the bias in choosing which categories contribute to the overall quality score. For this, we considered four different papers
(

;

;

)

Secondly, there is a bias in how the papers were graded based on these questions. To combat this, after the QA of the remaining 28 papers in the initial round, we conducted a kappa analysis on the scoring between the two authors to indicate whether the scoring was externally valid. The two authors scored a random sample of eight papers, and a weighted kappa score was calculated, producing a weighted kappa score of 0.68.

9.
Conclusion and future work

The existing literature on adversarial robustness in machine learning needs more coverage on unsupervised machine learning technologies. We have therefore performed a systematic literature review to summarize adversarial attacks, defenses, and research gaps related to unsupervised machine learning. From an initial list of 9129 papers, we have identified a list of 86 primary studies. We summarized the attack types, properties of targeted systems, metrics used for attack success, and the impact of the attacks. For each
target type
, we summarized and analyzed the papers in relation to 1)
attack type
,
attack goal
,
attack focus
,
attack knowledge
, and
metrics
, 2) summarized and analyzed the papers relating to
defense
, and 3) summarized and analyzed the current challenges in research into adversarial robustness of UL.

One of the notable findings is that, except for
differential privacy
, the field of unsupervised learning has a noticeable lack of focus on effective defensive methods, as several papers also conclude that defensive methods used in other fields of machine learning do not work in the context of unsupervised learning. Additionally, despite the fact that unsupervised learning systems are fairly diverse in function and form, the metrics used to determine whether attacks are successful are primarily shared, and many adversaries use distance metrics to determine the strength of the attack to avoid detection. Knowing this, crafting more sophisticated detection methods that would not be affected by distance-metric limitations might be worthwhile.

As mentioned in the discussion, further research into poisoning attacks is a worthwhile direction due to both the applicability of poisoning attacks against, seemingly, any unsupervised machine learning (and perhaps all types of machine learning) and due to the amount of data that is used in UL, which could contain poisoned samples since the data could be sourced from public databases or data that anyone can contribute to.

Target types

: ML consisting of two models, a generator, and a discriminator, training by competing against each other

: Neural network which is trained on the input and output being the same value

•

: Here, classification methods are models which have the output from a UL system as its input

•

Super Resolution
: ML method for increasing the dimensionality of a sample

Attack types

•

Adversarial Examples
: Specific perturbation in input to cause disruption

•

Poisoning
: Modification or addition of specifically crafted training samples

•

Membership Inference
: Extraction of knowledge about training by using the output of a model

•

Evasion
: Attacks crafted to avoid detection techniques

•

Obfuscation
: Modification of input to be hidden in existing groups or clusters

•

Bridging
: Addition of crafted samples to bridge between groups or clusters, causing disruption

•

Saturation
: Flooding the system with an excess of data

•

: Extraction of the model functions and parameters

•

Backdoor
: Inclusion of a specific trigger in a model causing disruption

•

Attribute Inference
: Extraction of attributes of training data

•

Reconstruction
: Extraction of statistical features of the training set for a model

•

Accuracy Extraction
: Extraction of a model through comparison to generated samples

•

Fidelity Extraction
: Extraction of a model through comparison to training samples

: Measurement of how many samples were correctly identified

Defenses

•

Differential Privacy:
Using mathematical proofs in training to force the constraint that a single sample cannot be known to be used for training

•

Adversarial Training:
The method of using an adversarial method for constructing samples the model otherwise would be vulnerable to, and subsequently training using these samples

•

Gradient Masking:
A method of obfuscating the gradients of the output of a model, to prevent useful information from being obtained

•

Game Theory:
The employment of defensive methods according to a strategy using a multitude of parameters

•

Differentially Private Stochastic Gradient Descent (DPSGD):
The application of DP to the training of GANs

•

Weight Normalization:
Reparametrization of the weight vectors in an already existing NN

•

Bootstrapping:
The use of existing robust models as the base of training a model

•

Fine Pruning:
The removal of neurons in a NN which does not improve the performance

Dropout:
Random removal of neurons during training
