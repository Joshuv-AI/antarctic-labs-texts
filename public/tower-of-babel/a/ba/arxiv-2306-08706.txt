Exit-problem for a class of non-Markov processes with path dependency
Ashot Aleksian, Aline Kurtzmann, Julian Tugaut

Abstract
We study the exit-time of a self-interacting diffusion from an open domain G ⊂ ℝ d G\subset\mathbb{R}^{d} . In particular, we consider the equation d X t = − ( ∇ V ​ ( X t ) + 1 t ​ ∫ 0 t ∇ F ​ ( X t − X s ) ​ d s ) ​ d t + σ ​ d W t . \differential{X_t}=-\left(\nabla V(X_{t})+\frac{1}{t}\int_{0}^{t}\nabla F(X_{t}-X_{s})\differential{s}\right)\differential{t}+\sigma\differential{W_t}. We are interested in the small-noise ( σ → 0 \sigma\to 0 ) behaviour of the exit-time from the potentials’ domain of attraction. In this work rather weak assumptions on the potentials V V and F F , and on the domain G G are considered. In particular, we do not assume V V nor F F to be either convex or concave, which covers a wide range of self-attracting and self-repelling stochastic processes possibly moving in a complex multi-well landscape. The Large Deviation Principle for the Self-interacting diffusion with generalized initial conditions is established. The main result of the paper states that, under some assumptions on the potentials V V and F F , and on the domain G G , the Kramers’ type law for the exit-time holds. Finally, we provide a result concerning the exit-location of the diffusion.

1.1
State of the art

Similar systems with path-interaction behaviour have already been studied by numerous researchers for more than 30 years. In most of the papers, the long-time behaviour of the process is considered. One of the first mathematical descriptions of such a process under the name of Self-avoiding random walk is presented by J.R. Norris, L.C.G. Rogers, and D. Williams in
Norris et al 1987
. Some stochastic properties of the process along with some long-time behaviour results in a particular setting were shown. The main difference between the system considered there and ours is that in
Norris et al 1987
there is no renormalization of the interaction term with time. In
Durrett and Rogers 1992
R.T. Durrett and L.C.G. Rogers introduce a system that is similar to the one presented in
Norris et al 1987
and aims to model the shape of a growing polymer:

\differential{X_t}=\left(\int_{0}^{t}f(X_{t}-X_{s})\differential{s}\right)\differential{t}+\differential{W_t}.

Given the physical interpretation of the process, the authors called this model “the Brownian Polymers”. In this paper, asymptotic bounds in dimension
d=1
with some assumptions on the interaction function were presented, along with some conjectures on more precise long-time behaviour. Among those, the authors conjectured that, in the symmetrical,
repulsive
case with limited interaction (
f(-x)=-f(x)
,
xf(x)\geq 0
, and
f
has compact support), one can show that
X_{t}/t\xrightarrow{}0
a.s. This repulsive case was later studied in the paper
Tarrès et al 2012
, where the conjecture was partially proved (with an additional assumption on smoothness of
f
).

Another branch of models with path-interaction, but this time via the renormalized-with-time occupation measure
\mu_{t}=\frac{1}{t}\int_{0}^{t}\delta_{X_{s}}\differential{s}
living on a compact manifold, started to develop with a paper of M. Benaïm, M. Ledoux, and O. Raimond
Benaïm et al 2002
. This work was continued in
Benaïm and Raimond 2003
,
Benaïm and Raimond 2005
, and others. In the first paper, the authors showed that the asymptotic behaviour of the empirical measure of the process
\mu_{t}
can be linked to some deterministic dynamical flow. In the following paper
Benaïm and Raimond 2003
, the authors used these techniques to describe some convergence in law properties of the process. Later, in
Benaïm and Raimond 2005
, assuming the interaction to be symmetric, the authors gave an almost sure convergence result for the occupation measure
\mu_{t}
and described its limit points.

The first step towards studying this process in the non-compact setting, which was
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, was done by A. Kurtzmann in
Kurtzmann 2010
. The author considers the following model with an interaction that depends on the empirical measure of the process.

\differential{X_t}=-\left(\nabla V(X_{t})+\frac{1}{t}\int_{0}^{t}\nabla_{x}F(X_{t},X_{s})\differential{s}\right)\differential{t}+\differential{W_t},

where
F
is regular and
V
is convex at infinity. In this paper, the ergodic properties of
X
were studied, as well as certain conditions on
V
and
F
that guarantee almost sure convergence of
\mu_{t}
. This work generalizes the previous ones of Benaïm and co-authors.

In the paper of V. Kleptsyn and A. Kurtzmann
Kleptsyn and Kurtzmann 2012
, the dynamic of the form (
1.2
) with the constant
\sigma=\sqrt{2}
is considered. The authors proved that, in the case of a convex confinement
V
with
\lim_{|x|\to+\infty}V(x)=+\infty
, an attractive and symmetrical interaction (the potential
F
is uniformly convex and spherically symmetrical, i.e.
F(x)=F(|x|)
), and some other regularity assumptions, the occupation measure
\mu_{t}
converges (in *-weakly sense) almost surely to a density
\rho_{\infty}
. It means that there exists a unique density
\rho_{\infty}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
such that

\mu_{t}=\frac{1}{t}\int_{0}^{t}\delta_{X_{s}}\differential{s}\xrightarrow[t\to\infty]{*-\text{weakly}}\rho_{\infty}(x)\differential{x}\quad\text{a.s.}

Moreover,
\rho_{\infty}
is a function satisfying the following functional equation:

\rho_{\infty}=\Pi(\rho_{\infty}):=\frac{\exp{-V - F*\rho_\infty}}{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\exp{-V(y) - F*\rho_\infty(y)}\differential{y}}.

That brings us to the second part of the paper, the exit-time problem. The main goal of this work is to study first the exit-time of
X^{\sigma}
, given by (
1.2
), from some bounded regular domain
G\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, i.e., the following stopping time
\tau_{G}^{\sigma}:=\inf\{t\geq 0:X_{t}^{\sigma}\notin G\}
. In particular, we are interested in exits driven by the Brownian motion with a small noise
\sigma>0
. A similar problem for Itô diffusion was introduced and solved by M. Freidlin and A. Wentzell. The techniques that they used in a number of papers, starting with
Ventcel’ and Freĭdlin 1970
and presented in their book
Freidlin and Wentzell 2012
, are known under the name of the Freidlin-Wentzell theory (see also
(
Dembo and Zeitouni 2010
, §5.6)
). Consider an Itô diffusion in the gradient form:

\differential{X_t}=-\nabla V(X_{t})\differential{t}+\sigma\differential{W_t}.

Then, the main result of the Freidlin-Wentzell theory is that, given a domain
G\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
with only one attraction point of the gradient field
-\nabla V
, named
a\in G
, and some other regularity assumptions on
G
and
V
, the so-called Kramers’ type law for the exit-time
\tau_{G}^{\sigma}
can be established. Namely, if we denote
H:=\inf_{z\in\partial G}\{V(z)-V(a)\}
, then for any starting point
x_{0}\in G
and for any
\delta>0

\lim_{\sigma\to 0}\mathbf{P}_{x_{0}}(e^{\frac{2(H-\delta)}{\sigma^{2}}}<\tau^{\sigma}_{G}<e^{\frac{2(H+\delta)}{\sigma^{2}}})=1.

(1.5)

It means that, with decreasing
\sigma>0
, the exit-time from a stable (or positively invariant) by
-\nabla V
domain
G
grows exponentially with a rate that depends on the height
H
of the potential
V
inside the domain
G
.

A similar question was posed by S. Herrmann, P. Imkeller, and D. Peithmann for McKean-Vlasov type diffusion in
Herrmann et al 2008
. In particular, they considered a so-called
Self-stabilizing diffusion
(SSD) of the form

\begin{cases}\differential{X^\sigma_t}\!\!\!\!&=-\nabla V(X^{\sigma}_{t})\differential{t}-\nabla F*\nu^{\sigma}_{t}(X^{\sigma}_{t})\differential{t}+\sigma\differential{W_t},\\
\nu^{\sigma}_{t}&=\mathcal{L}(X_{t}^{\sigma}),\\
X^{\sigma}_{0}&=x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\;\text{a.s.},\end{cases}

(1.6)

where
\mathcal{L}(X^{\sigma}_{t})
denotes the law of random variable
X_{t}^{\sigma}
. Apart from the obvious parallels that can be drawn between equations (
1.2
) and (
1.6
) that define SID and SSD respectively, the following fact makes the self-stabilizing case interesting for the current paper. As was shown in
Cattiaux et al 2008
, in the case of convex confinement and interaction and under some other regularity assumptions, the law
\nu_{t}^{\sigma}
converges to the unique probability measure that is the solution of
\nu=\Pi(\nu)
, which is the same behaviour that was established in
Kleptsyn and Kurtzmann 2012
.

Following the Freidlin-Wentzell theory techniques, in
Herrmann et al 2008
the authors first establish a large deviation principle for SSD with general assumptions on
V
and
F
. After that, they had to restrict themselves to the case of convex confinement and convex interaction potentials in order to achieve, under some stability and regularity assumptions on
G
, the Kramers’ type law (
1.5
). Assuming
F(0)=0
, the rate
H
in this case have the form
H=\inf_{z\in\partial G}\{U_{a}(z)-U_{a}(a)\}
.

In
Tugaut 2012
and
Tugaut 2016
, J. Tugaut studies the exit-time problem for SSD in a convex landscape with a convex interaction, as it was presented in
Herrmann et al 2008
. In these papers he establishes the Kramers’ type law, avoiding using the large deviation principle. Instead, the author uses various analytical and coupling methods to deal with the problem, thus simplifying calculations. This work was continued in
Tugaut 2018
and
Tugaut 2019
where the same techniques were used to establish the Kramers’ type law in the case of the confinement potential
V
that is of the double-well form. However, the exit-time for the SSD with general assumptions (as in the Freidlin-Wentzell theory for Itô diffusions) is still an open problem.

The exit-time problem was also studied for the case of the self-interacting diffusion. In
Aleksian et al 2024
, A. Aleksian, P. Del Moral, A. Kurtzmann, and J. Tugaut prove Kramers’ type law for SID in which both the interaction and the confinement potentials
V
and
F
are convex. This nice property of the potentials was used by the authors in order to prove this exit-time result by applying analytical and coupling techniques similar to the ones used in
Tugaut 2016
. Since the goal of the current paper is to establish some exit-time result for SID with more general, than in
Aleksian et al 2024
, assumptions, we have to use a different approach. In our case, that consists in proving the Large Deviation Principle and restoring the logic of the Freidlin-Wentzell theory for SID.

1.2
Some remarks on dynamics and initial condition

Let us introduce some notation and notions that will be useful to describe laconically later derivations in this paper.

The process
X^{\sigma}
itself is non-Markov, yet the triple
(t,\mu^{\sigma}_{t},X^{\sigma}_{t})
is. In order to see this, we will describe the notion of the previous path using three parameters.
t_{0}\geq 0
will represent its time-length,
\mu_{0}\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
will represent the occupation measure of the past trajectory and
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
its end point starting from which we continue the dynamic. We contract initial conditions into a vector
\mathbf{x}=(t_{0},\mu_{0},x_{0})\in\mathfrak{X}=[0,\infty]\!\times\!\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\!\times\!\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and, in order to be able to operate with the three components of
\mathbf{x}
separately, we introduce the following projection mappings. Let
\mathrm{T}:\mathfrak{X}\to[0,\infty]
be the projection on the first coordinate, returning initial time
t_{0}
. For the initial empirical measure, let
\mathrm{M}:\mathfrak{X}\to\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
be the mapping that returns the second coordinate of
\mathbf{x}\in\mathfrak{X}
. Finally, for the starting point of the process, respectively, define the mapping
\mathrm{P}:\mathfrak{X}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
that returns the third coordinate of
\mathbf{x}\in\mathfrak{X}
.

Following the classical notation for diffusions, we introduce the following system of equations:

\begin{cases}\differential{\X^{\x, \sigma}_t}\!\!\!\!&=-\nabla V(\boldsymbol{X}^{\mathbf{x},\sigma}_{t})\differential{t}-\nabla F*\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\mathbf{x},\sigma}_{t}(\boldsymbol{X}^{\mathbf{x},\sigma}_{t})\differential{t}+\sigma\differential{W_t},\\
\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\mathbf{x},\sigma}_{t}&=\frac{\mathrm{T}{\mathbf{x}}}{\mathrm{T}{\mathbf{x}}+t}\mathrm{M}{\mathbf{x}}+\frac{1}{\mathrm{T}{\mathbf{x}}+t}\int_{0}^{t}\delta_{\boldsymbol{X}^{\mathbf{x},\sigma}_{s}}\differential{s},\\
\boldsymbol{X}^{\mathbf{x},\sigma}_{0}&=\mathrm{P}{\mathbf{x}}\;\text{a.s.}\end{cases}

(1.7)

Hereafter, we will drop the initial conditions from the superscript if it is clear which
\mathbf{x}\in\mathfrak{X}
is meant. Though, to avoid confusion, we will always use the superscript
\sigma
, which can be equal to
0
in the deterministic case. For
\mathbf{x}
such that
\mathrm{T}{\mathbf{x}}=\infty
we naturally extend the definition of the processes (
1.7
) as

\begin{cases}\differential{\X^{\x, \sigma}_t}\!\!\!\!&=-\nabla V(\boldsymbol{X}^{\mathbf{x},\sigma}_{t})\differential{t}-\nabla F*\mathrm{M}{\mathbf{x}}(\boldsymbol{X}^{\mathbf{x},\sigma}_{t})\differential{t}+\sigma\differential{W_t},\\
\boldsymbol{X}^{\mathbf{x},\sigma}_{0}&=\mathrm{P}{\mathbf{x}}\;\text{a.s.}\end{cases}

(1.8)

Despite that the introduction of
\mathbf{x}
aims to describe a notion of a “previous path” of the process, the set
\mathfrak{X}
is defined to be more general. Firstly, we allow
t_{0}
to be equal to infinity. Second,
\mu_{0}
belongs to
\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, which, in general, does not restrict it to be an empirical measure of any path.

Note that the topology naturally defined on
[0,\infty]
by open intervals in usual sense and intervals of the form
[0,x)
,
(x,\infty]
is metrizable. Let us denote
d_{[0,\infty]}
some metric on this space. We also equip the set
\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
with Wasserstein-2 metric (see e.g.
(
Villani 2009
, Definition 6.1)
). Thus, the Cartesian product
\mathfrak{X}
is a complete separable metric space with the following metric

d_{\mathfrak{X}}(\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{x}_{2})=\max(d_{[0,\infty]}(\mathrm{T}{\mathbf{x}_{1}},\mathrm{T}{\mathbf{x}_{2}});\mathbb{W}_{2}(\mathrm{M}{\mathbf{x}_{1}};\mathrm{M}{\mathbf{x}_{2}});|\mathrm{P}{\mathbf{x}_{1}}-\mathrm{P}{\mathbf{x}_{2}}|),

where
\mathbb{W}_{2}
is the Wasserstein distance (see references in §
2.1
).

Existence and uniqueness result for stochastic differential equations that depend on their path is standard for the case of Lipschitz continuous drift term (see e.g.
(
Rogers and Williams 2000
, Theorem 11.2)
). It is worth noting that introducing
\mathbf{x}\in\mathfrak{X}
does not complicate matters in any way, as long as
\nabla V
and
\nabla F
remain Lipschitz continuous. Assumption
A-1
ensures that we are working in this framework. Using the standard argument for linear diffusion processes, it can be shown that the stochastic process
t\mapsto(\mathrm{T}\mathbf{x}+t,\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\mathbf{x},\sigma}_{t},\boldsymbol{X}_{t}^{\mathbf{x},\sigma})
is Markov and, moreover, being continuous, it is also a strong Markov process that takes its values in
\mathfrak{X}
.

2
Large Deviation Principle

In this paper, the Large Deviations techniques are widely used. Here, by the Large Deviation Principle (LDP) we mean the following asymptotic behaviour of measures.

Definition 1
.

Family of measures
(\nu^{\sigma})_{\sigma>0}
defined on some Banach space
B
equipped with Borel sigma-algebra
\mathcal{B}
is said to satisfy the Large Deviation Principle with a good rate function
I
if for any measurable set
\Gamma\in\mathcal{B}
:

-\inf_{x\in\accentset{\circ}{\Gamma}}I(x)\leq\liminf_{\sigma\xrightarrow{}0}\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\log\nu^{\sigma}(\Gamma)\leq\limsup_{\sigma\xrightarrow{}0}\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\log\nu^{\sigma}(\Gamma)\leq-\inf_{x\in\overline{\Gamma}}I(x),

where
I:B\to[0,\infty]
is a lower-semicontinuous function (this property defines
rate function
) whose level sets
\{x:I(x)\leq\alpha\}
are compact subsets of
B
for any
0\leq\alpha<\infty
(which means by definition that the rate function is
good
).

For more information about the LDP, see
Dembo and Zeitouni 2010
. Note that our definition of the LDP by its appearance deviates from the conventional one, but they are equivalent up to multiplication of the “conventional” rate function by
1/2
.

The following lemma generalizes the large deviation principle for the case of converging initial conditions. Under assumptions

Lemma 2.3
.

For any family

For any open
\Psi\subset C([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{d})

\liminf_{\begin{subarray}{c}\sigma\to 0\end{subarray}}\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\log\mathbf{P}_{\mathbf{x}_{\sigma}}(\boldsymbol{X}^{\sigma}\in\Psi)\geq-\inf_{\phi\in\Psi}I_{T}^{\mathbf{x}}(\phi).

We will show that the families of measures
(\nu^{\mathbf{x}_{\sigma},\sigma})_{\sigma>0}
and
(\nu^{\mathbf{x},\sigma})_{\sigma>0}

and
\boldsymbol{X}^{\mathbf{x},\sigma}
respectively, are exponentially equivalent (see
(
Dembo and Zeitouni 2010

) for any
\{\mathbf{x}_{\sigma}\}_{\sigma>0}
such that
\mathbf{x}_{\sigma}\xrightarrow[\sigma\to 0]{d_{\mathfrak{X}}}\mathbf{x}
.

Indeed, if we define
Z^{\sigma}_{t}=\boldsymbol{X}^{\mathbf{x}_{\sigma},\sigma}_{t}-\boldsymbol{X}^{\mathbf{x},\sigma}_{t}

\boldsymbol{X}^{\mathbf{x}_{\sigma},\sigma}_{t}
and

are driven by the same Brownian motion, then

\displaystyle|Z_{t}^{\sigma}|

\displaystyle\leq|\mathrm{P}{\mathbf{x}_{\sigma}}-\mathrm{P}{\mathbf{x}}|+\int_{0}^{t}|\nabla V(\boldsymbol{X}_{s}^{\mathbf{x}_{\sigma},\sigma})-\nabla V(\boldsymbol{X}_{s}^{\mathbf{x},\sigma})|\differential{s}

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{t}|\frac{\mathrm{T}{\mathbf{x}_{\sigma}}}{\mathrm{T}{\mathbf{x}_{\sigma}}+s}\nabla F*\mathrm{M}{\mathbf{x}_{\sigma}}(\boldsymbol{X}_{s}^{\mathbf{x}_{\sigma},\sigma})-\frac{\mathrm{T}{\mathbf{x}}}{\mathrm{T}{\mathbf{x}}+s}\nabla F*\mathrm{M}{\mathbf{x}}(\boldsymbol{X}_{s}^{\mathbf{x},\sigma})|\differential{s}

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{s}\big|\frac{1}{\mathrm{T}{\mathbf{x}_{\sigma}}+s}\nabla F(\boldsymbol{X}_{s}^{\mathbf{x}_{\sigma},\sigma}-\boldsymbol{X}_{z}^{\mathbf{x}_{\sigma},\sigma})-\frac{1}{\mathrm{T}{\mathbf{x}}+s}\nabla F(\boldsymbol{X}_{s}^{\mathbf{x},\sigma}-\boldsymbol{X}_{z}^{\mathbf{x},\sigma})\big|\differential{z}\differential{s}.

\frac{\mathrm{T}{\mathbf{x}_{\sigma}}}{\mathrm{T}{\mathbf{x}_{\sigma}}+s}\nabla F*\mathrm{M}{\mathbf{x}}(\boldsymbol{X}_{s}^{\mathbf{x},\sigma})

\frac{1}{\mathrm{T}{\mathbf{x}_{\sigma}}+s}\nabla F(\boldsymbol{X}_{s}^{\mathbf{x},\sigma}-\boldsymbol{X}_{z}^{\mathbf{x},\sigma})

\nabla V
and
\nabla F
are Lipschitz continuous, we get

\displaystyle|Z_{t}^{\sigma}|

\displaystyle\leq(1+\text{Lip}_{\nabla F})d_{\mathfrak{X}}(\mathbf{x}_{\sigma},\mathbf{x})+(\text{Lip}_{\nabla V}+\text{Lip}_{\nabla F})\int_{0}^{t}|Z_{s}^{\sigma}|\differential{s}

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left|\left(\frac{\mathrm{T}{\mathbf{x}_{\sigma}}}{\mathrm{T}{\mathbf{x}_{\sigma}}+s}-\frac{\mathrm{T}{\mathbf{x}}}{\mathrm{T}{\mathbf{x}}+s}\right)\nabla F(\boldsymbol{X}_{s}^{\mathbf{x},\sigma}-z)\right|\mathrm{M}{\mathbf{x}}(\differential{z})\differential{s}

By definition, rate functions are lower semicontinuous, i.e. for any
\mathbf{x}\in\mathfrak{X}
, the inequality
\liminf_{\phi\to\phi_{0}}I^{\mathbf{x}}_{T}(\phi)\geq I^{\mathbf{x}}_{T}(\phi_{0})

I^{\mathbf{x}}_{T}
are achieved over compact sets. The following lemma extends this lower semicontinuity property of our rate functions to the case of converging initial conditions besides the argument of
I_{T}

I_{T}
as a function of two arguments

still possesses lower semicontinuity property.

\displaystyle 2\int_{0}^{T}\Big|\frac{\mathrm{T}{\mathbf{x}_{n}}}{\mathrm{T}{\mathbf{x}_{n}}+t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\nabla F(\phi_{n}(t)-u)\mathrm{M}{\mathbf{x}_{n}}(\differential{u})-\frac{\mathrm{T}{\mathbf{x}}}{\mathrm{T}{\mathbf{x}}+t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\nabla F(\phi_{n}(t)-u)\mathrm{M}{\mathbf{x}}(\differential{u})\Big|^{2}\differential{t}

\displaystyle+2\int_{0}^{T}\Big|\frac{1}{\mathrm{T}{\mathbf{x}_{n}}+t}\int_{0}^{t}\nabla F(\phi_{n}(t)-\phi_{n}(u))\differential{u}-\frac{1}{\mathrm{T}{\mathbf{x}}+t}\int_{0}^{t}\nabla F(\phi_{n}(t)-\phi_{n}(u))\differential{u}\Big|^{2}\differential{t}

\displaystyle=:2\text{I}\text{I}_{1}+2\text{I}\text{I}_{2}.

Let us apply dominated convergence theorem to both
\text{I}\text{I}_{1}
and
\text{I}\text{I}_{2}
. In order to do so, we prove that integrands are uniformly bounded.

First, consider
\text{I}\text{I}_{1}
. Of course, fractions
\frac{\mathrm{T}{\mathbf{x}_{n}}}{\mathrm{T}{\mathbf{x}_{n}}+t}
and
\frac{\mathrm{T}{\mathbf{x}}}{\mathrm{T}{\mathbf{x}}+t}
are bounded by
1
. As for
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\nabla F(\phi_{n}(t)-u)|\mathrm{M}{\mathbf{x}_{n}}(\differential{u})
, we introduce the following decomposition

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}

\displaystyle|\nabla F(\phi_{n}(t)-u)|\mathrm{M}{\mathbf{x}_{n}}(\differential{u})

(2.4)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\nabla F(\phi_{n}(t)-u)-\nabla F(\phi(t)-u)+\nabla F(\phi(t)-u)|\mathrm{M}{\mathbf{x}_{n}}(\differential{u})

\displaystyle\leq\text{Lip}_{\nabla F}|\phi_{n}(t)-\phi(t)|+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\nabla F(\phi(t)-u)|\mathrm{M}{\mathbf{x}_{n}}

\displaystyle\leq\text{Lip}_{\nabla F}\|\phi_{n}-\phi\|_{\infty}+C_{\nabla F}\left(\max_{t\in[0,T]}|\phi(t)|+\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|u|^{2}\mathrm{M}{\mathbf{x}_{n}}\right)^{1/2}\right),

and since
\int|u|^{2}\mathrm{M}{\mathbf{x}_{n}}\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}\int|u|^{2}\mathrm{M}{\mathbf{x}}
(see
(
Villani 2009
, Theorem 6.9)
), the integral
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\nabla F(\phi_{n}(t)-u)|\mathrm{M}{\mathbf{x}_{n}}(\differential{u})
and, as a consequence,
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\nabla F(\phi_{n}(t)-u)|\mathrm{M}{\mathbf{x}}(\differential{u})
are uniformly (in
t
) bounded.

For
\text{I}\text{I}_{2}
, we also use lipschitzness of
\nabla F
when needed, convergence of
\phi_{n}
in uniform norm topology towards
\phi
, as well as bounds of the form
|\phi(t)|\leq\max_{t\in[0,T]}|\phi(t)|
. That easily gives us uniform (in
t
) boundedness of the integrand of the integral
\text{I}\text{I}_{2}
.

Thus, to calculate
\lim_{n\to\infty}\textbf{I}
, we can use the dominated convergence theorem and pass the limit inside both of the integrals
\text{I}\text{I}_{1}
and
\text{I}\text{I}_{2}
.

Since
\nabla F
is continuous and since the uniform boundedness in
t
and in
u

[0,T]
of
\nabla F(\phi_{n}(t)-\phi_{n}(u))
can be easily established,
\lim_{n\to\infty}\text{I}\text{I}_{2}
is clearly equal to
0
. Limits of components of
\text{I}\text{I}_{1}
are also obvious, except for
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\nabla F(\phi_{n}(t)-u)|\mathrm{M}{\mathbf{x}_{n}}(\differential{u})
that needs some attention. Let us show that expression
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\nabla F(\phi(t)-u)|\mathrm{M}{\mathbf{x}}(\differential{u})
is actually its limit with

. Similarly to computations in (
2.4
), we get

\displaystyle\Big|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\nabla F(\phi_{n}(t)-u)\mathrm{M}{\mathbf{x}_{n}}(\differential{u})-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\nabla F(\phi(t)-u)\mathrm{M}{\mathbf{x}}(\differential{u})\Big|

\displaystyle\leq\Big|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\nabla F(\phi(t)-u)\mathrm{M}{\mathbf{x}_{n}}(\differential{u})-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\nabla F(\phi(t)-u)\mathrm{M}{\mathbf{x}}(\differential{u})\Big|+\text{Lip}_{\nabla F}\|\phi_{n}-\phi\|_{\infty}.

As was pointed out before, convergence of measures in Wasserstein distance gives convergence of respective integrals, since
\nabla F
is Lipschitz continuous
(
Villani 2009
, Theorem 6.9)
.

This is the last remark needed to observe that
\lim_{n\to\infty}\textbf{I}=0
. Thus, the lemma is proved by (
2.3
).
∎

\lim_{t\to\infty}\inf_{\psi\in\Psi_{t}}I_{t}(\psi)=\infty

(3.21)

where
\mathbf{x}^{n}_{k}\in\mathfrak{X}

\displaystyle\mathrm{T}{\mathbf{x}_{k}^{n}}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{T}{\mathbf{x}_{0}}+kT_{1}^{\rho},

\displaystyle\mathrm{P}{\mathbf{x}_{k}^{n}}

\displaystyle=\psi^{n,k-1}(kT_{1}^{\rho}),

\displaystyle\mathrm{M}{\mathbf{x}_{k}^{n}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathrm{T}{\mathbf{x}_{0}}}{\mathrm{T}{\mathbf{x}_{0}}+kT_{1}^{\rho}}\mathrm{M}{\mathbf{x}_{0}}+\frac{1}{\mathrm{T}{\mathbf{x}_{0}}+kT_{1}^{\rho}}\int_{0}^{kT_{1}^{\rho}}\delta_{\psi_{s}^{n}}\differential{s};

By Lemma

\displaystyle+\text{Lip}_{\nabla F}\Big(\int_{0}^{t}|\boldsymbol{X}_{s}^{\sigma}-\mathrm{P}{\mathbf{x}}|\differential{s}+\int_{0}^{t}\log(\frac{\TP{\x} + t}{\TP{\x} + s})|\boldsymbol{X}_{s}^{\sigma}-\mathrm{P}{\mathbf{x}}|\differential{s}\Big).

Finally, introducing
R:=\sup_{z\in\partial G}|z-a|
and using the fact that
\mathbf{x}
is assumed, in the lemma, to belong to
\mathcal{C}^{1}_{\varepsilon,\rho}
, we get the bound:

\displaystyle|\boldsymbol{X}_{t}^{\sigma}-\mathrm{P}{\mathbf{x}}|

\displaystyle\leq\sigma|W_{t}|+\Big((\text{Lip}_{\nabla V}+\text{Lip}_{\nabla F})R+\text{Lip}_{\nabla F}(1+2\varepsilon)\rho\Big)t

\displaystyle+\left(\text{Lip}_{\nabla V}+2\text{Lip}_{\nabla F}+\log(\frac{\TP{\x} + t}{\TP{\x}})\right)\int_{0}^{t}|\boldsymbol{X}_{s}^{\sigma}-\mathrm{P}{\mathbf{x}}|\differential{s}.

We apply Grönwall’s inequality and the fact that, by the chose of
\rho
and
\varepsilon
, we have
(1+2\varepsilon)\rho<3
, and get:

\displaystyle|\boldsymbol{X}_{t}^{\sigma}-

\displaystyle\mathrm{P}{\mathbf{x}}|\leq\Big(\sigma|W_{t}|+\Big((\text{Lip}_{\nabla V}+\text{Lip}_{\nabla F})R+3\text{Lip}_{\nabla F}\Big)t\Big)

(3.33)

\displaystyle\times\exp{(\text{Lip}_{\nabla V} + 2\text{Lip}_{\nabla F})t + \TP{\x}\frac{\TP{\x} + t}{\TP{\x}}\log(\frac{\TP{\x} + t}{\TP{\x}}) - t}.

Thus, it follows that, for the absolute value of the Brownian motion itself,

\sigma|W_{t}|\geq-C_{1}t+C_{2}(t)|\boldsymbol{X}_{t}^{\sigma}-\mathrm{P}{\mathbf{x}}|,

where, for simplicity of further derivations, we introduced a positive constant
C_{1}:=(\text{Lip}_{\nabla V}+\text{Lip}_{\nabla F})R+3\text{Lip}_{\nabla F}
and a function
C_{2}(t)
that is one over the exponent that appears in the equation (
3.33
). For our purposes, it is not the form of
C_{2}
itself that is important, but rather its following properties:
C_{2}(t)\geq 0
for any
t\geq 0
and
C_{2}(t)\xrightarrow[t\to 0]{}1
.

The infimum of
C_{2}
over any time interval
[0,T]
, for
T<1
, is either equal to
C_{2}(T)
, if
\text{Lip}_{\nabla V}+2\text{Lip}_{\nabla F}\geq 1
, or can be bounded by
C_{2}(T)\exp\{\text{Lip}_{\nabla V}+2\text{Lip}_{\nabla F}-1\}
otherwise. This observation can be expressed in the following form:

\inf_{t\in[0,T]}C_{2}(t)\geq\min\{C_{2}(T);C_{2}(T)\exp{\text{Lip}_{\nabla V} + 2\text{Lip}_{\nabla F} - 1}\}.

Moreover, it is easy to check that
\lim_{T\to 0}C_{2}^{2}(T)/T=\infty
.

Taking into account these remarks, we can now use the Schilder theorem
(
Dembo and Zeitouni 2010
, Lemma 5.2.1)
that provides the LDP for the path of the Brownian motion. Hence, for some fixed
\zeta
and
0<T<1
:

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}_{\mathbf{x}}(\sup_{t\in[0,T]}|\boldsymbol{X}^{\sigma}_{t}-\mathrm{P}{\mathbf{x}}|\geq\zeta)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbf{P}_{\mathbf{x}}(\sigma\sup_{t\in[0,T]}|W_{t}|\geq-C_{1}t+C_{2}(t)\zeta)

\displaystyle\leq 4d\exp{-\frac{(-C_1 t + \inf_{t \in[0, T]}C_2(t)\zeta)^2}{4dT} \cdot\frac{2}{\sigma^2}},

where
d
is the dimension of the space.

Note that for any
\zeta>0
the following limit holds:

\frac{\big(-C_{1}T+\inf_{t\in[0,T]}C_{2}(t)\zeta\big)^{2}}{T}\xrightarrow[T\to 0]{}\infty.

Thus, for any given in advance
c
and
\zeta

to be small enough, such that the rate
-\big(-C_{1}T(\zeta,c)+\inf_{t\in[0,T(\zeta,c)]}C_{2}(t)\zeta\big)^{2}/\big(4dT(\zeta,c)\big)
will be less or equal to
-c
, which completes the proof.

4
Generalization

In this section we present the possible generalisation of Assumptions
A-1
and
A-2
as well as the exit-time result for this more general process. In this paper we did not talk in details about the problem of existence and uniqueness of the self-interacting diffusion. While in the Lipschitz case (Assumption
A-1
) these questions are standard, locally Lipschitzness of the potentials add some complication. In
Aleksian et al 2024
, the authors provide a proof for existence and uniqueness of self-interacting diffusion under the following assumptions that we take as a baseline:

Assumptions A-1
′
.

1.

(regularity) Potentials
V
and
F
belong to the space
C^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\mathbb{R})
.

2.

(growth) There exist a constant
C_{\text{gr}}
and an order
n>0
such that for any
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
|\nabla V(x)-\nabla V(y)|\leq C_{\text{gr}}|x-y|(1+|x|^{2n}+|y|^{2n})
as well as
|\nabla F(x)-\nabla F(y)|\leq C_{\text{gr}}|x-y|(1+|x|^{2n}+|y|^{2n})
.

3.

(confinement at infinity)
\underset{|x|\rightarrow\infty}{\lim}V(x)=+\infty
,
\underset{|x|\rightarrow\infty}{\lim}\frac{|\nabla V(x)|^{2}}{V(x)}=+\infty
and there exists
\alpha>0
such that
\Delta V(x)\leq\alpha V(x)
.

It is worth noting that Assumption
A-1′
.
2
is a combination of locally Lipschitz and polynomial growth conditions. Assumption
A-1′
.
3
has been introduced in
Aleksian et al 2024
to control the Lyapunov functional of the diffusion process to ensure that there is no explosion within a finite time. It is important to mention that, if required, this assumption can be replaced by another one that ensures the process’s global existence and uniqueness without any impact on the exit-time result.

We can also relax Assumptions
A-2
. Indeed, the domain
G
does not necessarily have to be bounded, but it is necessary to add another assumption on the level sets instead. Consider:

Assumptions A-2
′
.

1.

(domain
G
)
G\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
is an open connected set such that
\partial G=\partial\overline{G}
. The boundary
\partial G
is a smooth
(d-1)
-dimensional hypersurface.

2.

(bounded sublevel set) Define
L^{-}_{H}:=\{x\in U_{a}(x)\leq H\}
the sublevel set of the height
H:=\inf_{x\in\partial G}\{U_{a}(x)-U_{a}(a)\}
. Then
L^{-}_{H}\cap G
is a bounded connected set.

3.

(stability of
\overline{G}
under the effective potential) Let
\phi
be defined as
\phi_{t}^{x}=x-\int_{0}^{t}\nabla U_{a}(\phi_{s}^{x})\differential{s}
. For any
x\in\overline{G}
,
\{\phi^{x}_{t}\}_{t>0}\subset G
and
\phi^{x}_{t}\xrightarrow[t\to\infty]{}a
. Moreover,
\nabla U_{a}(a)=0
.

4.

(strong attraction around
a
) There exist
\Delta_{\mu},\Delta_{x}>0
small enough and constants
0<K_{2}<K_{1}<\infty
, such that for any
\mu
satisfying
\mathbb{W}_{2}(\mu;\delta_{a})\leq\Delta_{\mu}
and for any

, we have

\langle\nabla V(x)+\nabla F*\mu(x);x-a\rangle\geq K_{1}|x-a|^{2}-K_{2}|x-a|\mathbb{W}_{2}\left(\mu;\delta_{a}\right).

Under these assumptions, we can obtain the Kramers’ type law and the exit-location result. Consider

Corollary 4.1
.

Let Assumptions
A-1′
and
A-2′
be fulfilled. Let the process

be the unique strong solution of the system (

\lim_{\sigma\to 0}\mathbf{P}_{x_{0}}\left(e^{\frac{2(H-\delta)}{\sigma^{2}}}<\tau_{G}^{\sigma}<e^{\frac{2(H+\delta)}{\sigma^{2}}}\right)=1;

(4.1)

\lim_{\sigma\to 0}\mathbf{P}_{x_{0}}(X^{\sigma}_{\tau_{G}^{\sigma}}\in N)=0.

(4.2)

We do not prove this corollary rigorously but rather give an idea of the proof. The main insight comes from the proof of the exit-location result—Section

and
\overline{F}
are modifications of

and

respectively defined in the following way. Let

. First, we let
\overline{V}(x)=V(x)
and
\overline{F}(x)=F(x)
for any
x\in B_{\text{mod}}
, Then, we extend
\overline{V}
and
\overline{F}
on other points of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that in the end the potentials
\overline{V}
and
\overline{F}
are Lipschitz continuous and belong to
C^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\mathbb{R})
.

It means that
\overline{V}
,
\overline{F}
and
B_{\text{mod}}\cap G
satisfy Assumptions
A-1
and
A-2
and we can establish the Kramers’ type law for
\overline{\tau}^{\sigma}:=\inf\{t\geq 0:Y^{\sigma}_{t}\notin B_{\text{mod}}\cap G\}
and exit-location result for
Y^{\sigma}
. Note also that for any
t\geq 0
we have:

X_{t\wedge\overline{\tau}^{\sigma}}^{\sigma}=Y_{t\wedge\overline{\tau}^{\sigma}}^{\sigma}\text{ a.s.}

The last observation that one needs to do in order to prove the corollary is the following. By the exit-location result, the probability that
Y^{\sigma}
exits the domain
B_{\text{mod}}\cap G
around
\partial G\cap L_{H}
tends to one as
\sigma
tends to zero. It means that the probability that
\tau_{G}^{\sigma}=\overline{\tau}^{\sigma}
should also tend to one as
\sigma
tends to zero, since leaving the domain
B_{\text{mod}}\cap G
on the boundary
\partial G
also means leaving
G
itself.
