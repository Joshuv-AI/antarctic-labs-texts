Noisy Accelerated Power Method for Eigenproblems with Applications
Vien V. Mai and Mikael Johansson

Abstract
This paper introduces an efficient algorithm for finding the dominant generalized eigenvectors of a pair of symmetric matrices. Combining tools from approximation theory and convex optimization, we develop a simple scalable algorithm with strong theoretical performance guarantees. More precisely, the algorithm retains the simplicity of the well-known power method but enjoys the asymptotic iteration complexity of the powerful Lanczos method . Unlike these classic techniques, our algorithm is designed to decompose the overall problem into a series of subproblems that only need to be solved approximately. The combination of good initializations, fast iterative solvers, and appropriate error control in solving the subproblems lead to a linear running time in the input sizes compared to the superlinear time for the traditional methods. The improved running time immediately offers acceleration for several applications. As an example, we demonstrate how the proposed algorithm can be used to accelerate canonical correlation analysis, which is a fundamental statistical tool for learning of a low-dimensional representation of high-dimensional objects. Numerical experiments on real-world data sets confirm that our approach yields significant improvements over the current state-of-the-art.



1
Introduction

Computing the leading eigenvectors of a matrix is one of the most fundamental problems in scientific computing. The problem has been studied extensively in a classical setting and is used in countless applications.
For generalized eigenvector problems, standard iterative methods such as the
power method
and the Lanczos method require multiple matrix-vector products of the form
\mathbf{B}^{-1}\bm{x}
at each iteration
[
22
, Section 9.2.6]
, which becomes prohibitively expensive in high dimensions. The poor scalability of the standard techniques make them unfit for many applications in machine learning and data science. In these applictions, the ability to deal efficiently with large-scale data is the main concern, while machine precision accuracy is no longer essential since the problem data is often uncertain and subject to noise. Novel algorithms for scalable and approximate eigenvalue computations are therefore urgently needed.

In this paper, we consider the problem of finding the solution
(\bm{w},\lambda)
to the following equation

\displaystyle\mathbf{A}\bm{w}=\lambda\mathbf{B}\bm{w}

(1)

where
\mathbf{A}
and
\mathbf{B}
are given real, symmetric matrices and
\mathbf{B}
is positive definite.
Such eigenproblems appear in a wide range of machine learning tasks, such as finding a set of directions in the
data-embedding space which contains the maximum amount of variance (principal components analysis), finding a hyperplane that separates
two classes of data (Fisher discriminant),
and finding correlations between two different representations of the same data
(canonical correlation analysis), among many others.

Let
\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y}\in\mathbbmss{R}^{n\times d}
be two (potentially large) sets of data with empirical covariance matrices
\mathbf{\Sigma}_{11}=\frac{1}{n}\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{X}
,
\mathbf{\Sigma}_{22}=\frac{1}{n}\mathbf{Y}^{\top}\mathbf{Y}
, and
\mathbf{\Sigma}_{12}=\frac{1}{n}\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{Y}
.
The canonical correlation analysis (CCA) problem is concerned with finding the features
\mathbf{x}
and
\mathbf{y}
that best encapsulate the similarity of
\mathbf{X}
and
\mathbf{Y}
. In particular, CCA maximizes the empirical cross-correlation between
\mathbf{X}
and
\mathbf{Y}
by solving the following problem

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\underset{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}}{\text{maximize}}&&\bm{x}^{\top}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{12}\bm{y}\\
&\text{subject to}&&\bm{x}^{\top}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{11}\bm{x}=\bm{y}^{\top}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{22}\bm{y}=1.\end{aligned}

(2)

Generalized eigenvalue (GEV) problems, on the other hand, compute features that maximize discrepancies between the data sets as measured by the the quantities

\displaystyle\underset{\mathbf{x}}{\max}\frac{\bm{x}^{\top}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{11}\bm{x}}{\bm{x}^{\top}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{22}\bm{x}}\quad\text{and}\quad\underset{\mathbf{y}}{\max}\frac{\bm{y}^{\top}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{22}\bm{y}}{\bm{y}^{\top}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{11}\bm{y}}.

Both problems can be reduced to performing principal component analysis (PCA) on complicated matrices, e.g.,
\mathbf{\Sigma}_{22}^{-1/2}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{12}^{\top}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{11}^{-1}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{12}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{22}^{-1/2}
for CCA and
\mathbf{\Sigma}_{22}^{-1/2}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{11}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{22}^{-1/2}
for GEV. However, this approach is not scalable, as the formation of
\mathbf{\Sigma}_{11}^{-1/2}
and
\mathbf{\Sigma}_{22}^{-1/2}
becomes prohibitively expensive for large data sets.

Related Work

While there have been significant recent progress in understanding PCA, efficient algorithms and theoretical guarantees remain limited for generalized eigenvalue problems and CCA.
Below, we summarize recent work in optimization and approximation theory that have influenced the developments in this paper.

1.1.2
Approximation theory and acceleration

Approximation theory is ubiquitous in machine learning, optimization, and numerical linear algebra. This is because many problems in these areas can be reduced to finding a faster way to compute primitives such as
x^{s}
,
x^{-1}
, or
e^{x}

[
23
]
. For example, for the solution to linear systems of equations of the form
Ax=b
with
A\in\mathbbmss{R}^{n\times n}
, Chebyshev iteration improves the iteration complexity from
\mathcal{O}\left(n\kappa\log\frac{1}{\epsilon}\right)
of the Richardson iteration to
\mathcal{O}\left(n\sqrt{\kappa}\log\frac{1}{\epsilon}\right)
, where
\kappa
is the condition number of
A

[
11
, Chapter 11]
. This improved rate is similar to the speed-ups by Nesterov’s fast gradient method over the classical gradient descent
[
18
]
, which was recently discussed in terms of polynomial approximations in
[
24
]
. For eigenproblems, the Lanczos method can be considered as an accelerated version of the power method and improves the iteration complexity from
\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{\Delta}\log\frac{1}{\epsilon}\right)
to
\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{\Delta}}\log\frac{1}{\epsilon}\right)
. Here,
\Delta
is the relative difference between the two eigenvalues of largest magnitude of the relevant matrix, which tends to be very small for many practical data sets. Although polynomial approximation is not explicit in the Lancoz method, its convergence proof is heavily based on Chebyshev polynomials
[
22
]
. We remark that the notion of
noisy
Chebyshev iterations was studied for linear system of equations in
[
10
]
; however, the proof technique is not directly applicable to eigenproblems.

1.2
Contributions

We propose a
linearly convergent
algorithm for the GEV and CCA problems which combines the simplicity of the power method with the asymptotic performance of the Lanczos method.
Our method—called
NAPI
—builds on an acceleration technique inspired by polynomial approximation and an efficient
inexact
matrix-vector product computation via advanced convex optimization methods. Our work is motivated by the work in
[
10
,
9
,
21
]
, and the main contributions are summarized as follows:

•

We explicitly characterize the convergence properties of
NAPI
. Our result matches the asymptotic iteration complexity of the Lanczos method but with much lower computational cost per iteration. In particular,
NAPI
attains the worst-case complexity bound
\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{\Delta}}\log\frac{1}{\epsilon}\right)
which is not improvable in terms of the eigenvalue gap
\Delta
. While the Lanczos method can not operate in a stochastic setting, each iteration of
NAPI
involves solving a least squares problem inexactly, hence can be handled very efficiently by some of the most advanced convex optimization solvers. By appropriately controlling the errors in solving the least squares problem, and by using a well-designed
initialization
,
NAPI
obtains a
linear
running time in the input-size which allows it to operate on large-scale data sets.

•

Our result can be used to obtain improved running time for several downstream applications. We demonstrate how
NAPI
can be applied to the CCA problem, resulting in practical and theoretical improvements over the state-of-the-art algorithms (see, Table
1
). Experiments on real-world data sets confirm the effectiveness of our method.

1.4
Preliminaries

Principal angles are a useful tool for studying the convergence of subspaces in iterative eigenvalue methods. We rely on the following definition:

Definition 1

(Principal Angles
[
33
]
)
.

Let
\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{Y}
be subspaces of
\mathbbmss{R}^{d}
of dimension at least
k
. The principal angles
0\leq\theta^{(1)}\leq\theta^{(2)}\cdots\leq\theta^{(k)}\leq\pi/2
between
\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{Y}
are defined recursively via:

\displaystyle\cos\theta^{(i)}\left(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y}\right)=\max_{\bm{x}\in\mathcal{X}}\max_{\bm{y}\in\mathcal{Y}}\;\bm{x}^{\top}\mathbf{B}\bm{y}=\left|{\bm{x}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{B}\bm{y}_{i}}\right|,

subject to
\left\|{\bm{x}}\right\|_{\mathbf{B}}=\left\|{\bm{y}}\right\|_{\mathbf{B}}=1
,
\bm{x}^{\top}\mathbf{B}\bm{x}_{j}=0
,
\bm{y}^{\top}\mathbf{B}\bm{y}_{j}=0
,
j=1,\ldots,i-1
. The vectors
\{\bm{x}_{1},\bm{x}_{2},\ldots,\bm{x}_{k}\}
and
\{\bm{y}_{1},\bm{y}_{2},\ldots,\bm{y}_{k}\}
are called the principal vectors.
For matrices
\mathbf{X}
and
\mathbf{Y}
, we use
\theta^{(i)}\left(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y}\right)
to denote the
i
th principal angle between their ranges.

Since we are only interested in the largest principal angle, we make slight abuse of notation and write
\theta\left(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y}\right)
instead of
\theta^{(k)}\left(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y}\right)
.

Proposition 1
.

Let
\mathbf{X}
and
\mathbf{Y}
be orthogonal bases for subspaces
\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{Y}
w.r.t
\mathbf{B}
, respectively. Let
\mathbf{X}_{\bot}
be an orthogonal basis w.r.t
\mathbf{B}
for the orthogonal complement of
\mathcal{X}
. Then,

\displaystyle\cos\theta\left(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y}\right)=\sigma_{k}\left(\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{B}\mathbf{Y}\right)\,\,\text{and}\,\,\,\sin\theta\left(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y}\right)=\left\|{\mathbf{X}_{\bot}^{\top}\mathbf{B}\mathbf{Y}}\right\|.

If, in addition,
\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{B}\mathbf{Y}
is invertible, then

\displaystyle\tan\theta\left(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y}\right)=\left\|{\mathbf{X}_{\bot}^{\top}\mathbf{B}\mathbf{Y}\left(\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{B}\mathbf{Y}\right)^{-1}}\right\|.

The following lemma presents a nice connection between the principal angles and the distances between subspaces.
The proof of this lemma when
\mathbf{B}=\mathbf{I}
can be found in
[
29
, Section II.4]
; its generalization to general positive definite matrices is straightforward, hence omitted here.

Lemma 1

(Distance Between Subspaces)
.

Let
\mathbf{X}
and
\mathbf{Y}
be orthogonal bases for subspaces
\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{Y}
w.r.t
\mathbf{B}
, respectively. Let
\mathbf{Q}
be an orthogonal matrix, then

\displaystyle\left\|{\mathbf{X}\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{B}-\mathbf{Y}\mathbf{Y}^{\top}\mathbf{B}}\right\|

\displaystyle=\sin\theta\left(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y}\right),

\displaystyle\min_{\mathbf{Q}^{\top}\mathbf{Q}=\mathbf{I}_{k}}\left\|{\mathbf{B}^{1/2}\mathbf{X}-\mathbf{B}^{1/2}\mathbf{Y}\mathbf{Q}}\right\|

\displaystyle\leq 2\sin\frac{\theta\left(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y}\right)}{2}.

2
Polynomial Approximation, Least Squares Solvers and Eigenproblems

2.1
What does polynomial approximation have to do with eigenvalues?

The
power method

[
11
]
is one of the oldest techniques for computing the largest eigenvalue of a matrix, and the conceptual starting point for many contemporary numerical algorithms. It works as follows: choose an initial vector
\bm{x}_{0}\in\mathbbmss{R}^{d}
uniformly at random from the unit sphere and multiply it repeatedly by the symmetric matrix
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbbmss{R}^{d\times d}
. This generates a sequence of iterates
\{\bm{x}_{s}\}
, related via
\bm{x}_{s}=\mathbf{A}\bm{x}_{s-1}=\mathbf{A}^{s}\bm{x}_{0}
.
Under certain conditions, this sequence, normalized appropriately, will converge to the leading eigenvector of
A
, i.e, the one associated with the eigenvalue of largest magnitude.

The power method can be viewed as applying of a degree-
s
polynomial, namely
p_{s}\left(z\right)=z^{s}
, to
\mathbf{A}
, and then multiplying it by the initial vector
\bm{x}_{0}
. The polynomial
p_{s}\left(z\right)
evaluates
1
to
1
and moves every
z
with
\left|{z}\right|<1
closer to
0
at the rate determined by
\left|{z}\right|
. Let
\mathbf{A}=\mathbf{U}\mathbf{\Lambda}\mathbf{U}^{\top}
be the eigenvalue value decomposition of
\mathbf{A}
, where
\mathbf{\Lambda}=\mathrm{diag}\left({\lambda_{1}},{\ldots},{\lambda_{d}}\right)
is a matrix of eigenvalues satisfying
\left|{\lambda_{1}}\right|>\left|{\lambda_{2}}\right|\geq\ldots\geq\left|{\lambda_{d}}\right|
. Then, the polynomial
p_{s}\left(z\right)
applied to
\mathbf{A}
is simply

\displaystyle p_{s}\left(\mathbf{A}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{U}\begin{bmatrix}p_{s}\left(\lambda_{1}\right)&0&\ldots&0\\[3.00003pt]
0&p_{s}\left(\lambda_{2}\right)&\ldots&0\\[3.00003pt]
\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&0\\[3.00003pt]
0&\ldots&0&p_{s}\left(\lambda_{d}\right)\end{bmatrix}\mathbf{U}^{\top}=\mathbf{U}\begin{bmatrix}\lambda_{1}^{s}&0&\ldots&0\\[3.00003pt]
0&\lambda_{2}^{s}&\ldots&0\\[3.00003pt]
\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&0\\[3.00003pt]
0&\ldots&0&\lambda_{2}^{s}\end{bmatrix}\mathbf{U}^{\top}.

Since
\left|{\lambda_{1}}\right|>\left|{\lambda_{2}}\right|
,
\mathbf{A}^{s}/\lambda_{1}^{s}
converges to
\bm{u}_{1}\bm{u}_{1}^{\top}
, where
\bm{u}_{1}
is the first column of
\mathbf{U}
. This means that an estimate of
\bm{u}_{1}
can be found by simply picking the first column of
\mathbf{A}^{s}
and normalizing it to have a unit norm. If we suppose that
\left|{\lambda_{2}}\right|=\left(1-\Delta\right)\left|{\lambda_{1}}\right|
, then it will take roughly
s=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{\Delta}\log\frac{1}{\epsilon}\right)
iterations until
|\lambda_{2}|^{s}\leq\epsilon|\lambda_{1}|^{s}
. Since the eigengap
\Delta
tends to be very small for practical large-scale matrices, the power method converges very slowly. Fortunately, the next proposition indicates that it is possible to to improve the running time from
\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{\Delta}\log\frac{1}{\epsilon}\right)
to
\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{\Delta}}\log\frac{1}{\epsilon}\right)
by a simple modification of the power method.

Proposition 2
.

[
23
, Theorem 3.3]

For any positive integers
s
and
d
, there is a degree-
d
polynomial
p_{s,d}\left(x\right)
satisfying

\displaystyle\sup_{x\in\left[-1,1\right]}\left|{p_{s,d}\left(x\right)-x^{s}}\right|\leq 2e^{-d^{2}/2s}.

The result implies that it is possible to approximate
x^{s}
to any accuracy
\delta>0
i.e.

\sup_{x\in\left[-1,1\right]}\left|{p_{s,d}\left(x\right)-x^{s}}\right|\leq\delta.

using a polynomial
p_{s,d}(x)
of degree
d=\lceil\sqrt{2\ln\left(2/\delta\right)s}\rceil
.
This suggests that it should be possible to develop an approximate power iteration which reduces the number of matrix multiplications by
A
from
s
to
\sqrt{s}
.
Of course, the existence result in Proposition
2
is only useful if there is an efficient way to construct the approximating polynomial. The proof of the proposition relies on Chebyshev polynomials, which are ubiquitous in numerical optimization.
We also note that the result above is essentially optimal in the sense that polynomial approximations of
x^{s}
over
\left[-1,1\right]
require degree
\Omega(\sqrt{s})

[
23
, Chapter 5]
.

2.2
Stochastic optimization of least squares

Over the past few years, there has been a great attention in developing stochastic algorithms for solving
empirical risk minimization
problems in machine learning. Specifically, one is interested in the unconstrained minimization of

\displaystyle f\left(x\right):=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}f_{i}\left(x\right),

(3)

where
f_{1},\ldots,f_{n}
is
L
-smooth and convex functions, and
f
is
\mu
-strongly convex function. Solving this problem using standard first order methods such as gradient descent requires
\mathcal{O}\left(n\kappa\log\frac{1}{\epsilon}\right)
passes over the data set to achieve an
\epsilon
-optimal solution, where the condition number
\kappa=L/\mu
can be as large as
\Omega\left(n\right)
in machine learning applications
[
4
]
. This running time can be further improved to
\mathcal{O}\left(n\sqrt{\kappa}\log\frac{1}{\epsilon}\right)
if one uses Nesterov’s accelerated scheme
[
18
]
. Many
linearly convergent
stochastic methods, such as SDCA
[
25
]
, SAGA
[
6
]
, or SVRG
[
15
]
, have been introduced and shown to outperform the standard first order methods under mild assumptions. These methods only access to the gradient of one individual component function
f_{i}
at each step of the algorithm, instead of the full gradient as in standard first order methods. This results in an improved computational complexity of
\mathcal{O}\left(\left(n+\kappa\right)\log\frac{1}{\epsilon}\right)
passes over the data set to achieve an
\epsilon
-optimal solution in expectation. When these methods are combined with Nesterov acceleration, the expected complexity becomes
O\left((n+\sqrt{n\kappa})\log(1/\epsilon)\right)
(see, e.g.,
[
7
]
and
[
1
]
).

In this work, we approximately compute the matrix-vector product
\mathbf{B}^{-1}\bm{b}
by minimizing the least-squares loss

\displaystyle f\left(\bm{w}\right):=\frac{1}{2}\bm{w}^{\top}\mathbf{B}\bm{w}-\bm{w}^{\top}\bm{b},

over
\bm{w}
. This problem has the unique solution is
\bm{w}^{\star}=\mathbf{B}^{-1}\bm{b}
. In our applications of interest,
\mathbf{B}
is usually an empirical covariance matrix of the form
\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\bm{x}_{i}\bm{x}_{i}^{\top}
, hence
f\left(\bm{w}\right)
can be expressed as a finite-sum as in (
3
) with

\displaystyle f_{i}(\bm{w})

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{2}\bm{w}^{\top}\bm{x}_{i}\bm{x}_{i}^{\top}\bm{w}-\bm{w}^{\top}\bm{b}

Thus, we can find an approximate solution to
\mathbf{B}^{-1}\bm{b}
using either standard first-order methods or more advanced stochastic solvers. As we will see, using the advanced solvers together with good initializations will lead to a significant improvement in iteration complexity of our method.

2.3
The top-k generalized eigenvector problem

Definition 2

(Top-
k
generalized eigenvectors)
.

Given symmetric matrices
\mathbf{A}
and
\mathbf{B}
where
\mathbf{B}
is positive definite, the top-
k
generalized eigenvectors
\bm{w}_{1},\ldots,\bm{w}_{k}
are defined by

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \bm{w}_{i}\in\,&\underset{\bm{w}}{\argmax}&&|\bm{w}^{\top}\mathbf{A}\bm{w}|\\
&\mbox{subject to}&&\bm{w}^{\top}\mathbf{B}\bm{w}=1\\
&&&\bm{w}^{\top}\mathbf{B}\bm{w}_{j}=0\quad\forall j\in\{1,2,\ldots,i-1\}.\end{aligned}

(4)

Problem (
4
) can be reduced to the problem of computing the top-
k
eigenvectors associated with
k
eigenvalues of largest magnitude of
\mathbf{M}=\mathbf{B}^{-1/2}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}^{-1/2}
and then multiplying by
\mathbf{B}^{-1/2}
(see, Lemma 2). Let us use the simple power method for this task. Let
\bm{y}=\mathbf{B}^{-1/2}\mathbf{M}^{T}\mathbf{x}
be the result of
T
iterations of the power method followed by multiplying with
\mathbf{B}^{-1/2}
, we can write
\bm{y}=\left(\mathbf{B}^{-1}\mathbf{A}\right)^{T}\mathbf{B}^{-1/2}\bm{x}
. Since the power method converges from a random initial vector
\bm{x}
with high probability, one can ignore
\mathbf{B}^{-1/2}
and compute instead
\bm{y}=\left(\mathbf{B}^{-1}\mathbf{A}\right)^{T}\bm{x}
. Thus, we can compute our desired eigenvectors by simply alternating between applying
\mathbf{A}
and
\mathbf{B^{-1}}

3
Computing the Leading Generalized Eigenvector

In this section, we introduce
NAPI
, an noisy accelerated power method for solving (
1

3.1
Description of the algorithm

Our algorithm is designed to run in an inner-outer fashion and it hinges upon: (i) an acceleration technique inspired by Chebyshev polynomials for approximating matrix powers; and (ii) an efficient inexact matrix-vector product computation. In particular, the acceleration technique in the outer loop allows to reduce the number of outer iterations, while the subproblems in the inner loop are efficiently solved by some of the most advanced convex optimization solvers. These solvers are capable of exploiting sparsity and problem structure and scale to truly large-scale data sets. The last critical component of our algorithm is a well-designed warm start procedure which reduces the practical running times even further.

3.2
Deriving acceleration through Chebyshev magic

As discussed above, the power method can be accelerated by finding a good low-degree polynomial approximation to
x^{T}
. However, to evaluate a polynomial of degree
T
, we may need to store up to
T
terms before adding them up. Fortunately, the next result demonstrates that we can construct a Chebyshev polynomial recursively using only the two previous terms.

Definition 3

(Chebyshev Polynomials)
.

For a nonnegative integer
t
, the degree
t
Chebyshev polynomial of the first kind,
T_{t}\left(z\right)
, is defined recursively as follows:

\displaystyle T_{0}\left(z\right)

\displaystyle=1,\,\,\,T_{1}\left(z\right)=z,

\displaystyle T_{t}\left(z\right)

\displaystyle=2zT_{t-1}\left(z\right)-T_{t-2}\left(z\right)\quad\text{for}\quad t\geq 2.

To motivate our algorithm, let us focus on the unconstrained version of Problem (
4
), where we relax the condition that the solution should have unit
\mathbf{B}
-norm.
Our goal is to find the leading eigenvector of
\mathbf{B}^{-1}\mathbf{A}
by approximating
\left(\mathbf{B}^{-1}\mathbf{A}\right)^{T}
using a Chebyshev polynomial. Using Definition
3
with
z=\mathbf{B}^{-1}\mathbf{A}
and letting
\bm{y}_{t}=T_{t}\left(\mathbf{B}^{-1}\mathbf{A}\right)\bm{w}_{0}
, we can approximate
\left(\mathbf{B}^{-1}\mathbf{A}\right)^{T}\bm{w}_{0}
by recursively building a new iterate using only the previous two iterates:

\displaystyle\bm{y}_{t+1}=2\mathbf{B}^{-1}\mathbf{A}\bm{y}_{t}-\bm{y}_{t-1}.

(5)

Note that the result in Proposition
2
is stated for approximating over the interval
[-1,1]
. Therefore, we will consider a scaled Chebyshev polynomial of the form
C_{t}(\mathbf{B}^{-1}\mathbf{A})=T_{t}\left(\frac{\mathbf{B}^{-1}\mathbf{A}}{c}\right)
for some constant
c
. Letting
\bm{x}_{t}=C_{t}(\mathbf{B}^{-1}\mathbf{A})\bm{w}_{0}
, we will thus study the recursion

\displaystyle\bm{x}_{t+1}=\mathbf{B}^{-1}\mathbf{A}\bm{x}_{t}-\beta\bm{x}_{t-1},

(6)

where
\beta
is a constant depending on
c
. As we shall see, determining the best possible
\beta
leads to an improved convergence rate for our method.

Returning to the constrained case, we wish to use a (scaled) Chebyshev polynomial to get an improved convergence rate while ensuring that the iterates have unit
\mathbf{B}
-norm. To this end, in our algorithm, we normalize the intermediate iterate
\tilde{\bm{w}}_{t+1}
to have unit
\mathbf{B}
-norm and rescale the current iterate
\bm{w}_{t}
by the same factor as that for
\tilde{\bm{w}}_{t+1}
. The iterates generated by Algorithm
1
can be written as

\displaystyle\tilde{\bm{w}}_{t+1}:=\mathbf{B}^{-1}\mathbf{A}\bm{w}_{t}-\beta\bm{w}_{t-1},\,\,\gamma_{t+1}=\left\|{\tilde{\bm{w}}_{t+1}}\right\|_{\mathbf{B}}

(7)

\displaystyle\bm{w}_{t}:=\bm{w}_{t}\gamma_{t+1}^{-1},\,\,\bm{w}_{t+1}:=\tilde{\bm{w}}_{t+1}\gamma_{t+1}^{-1},

(8)

and the latest iterate
\bm{w}_{T}
can be expressed as

\displaystyle\bm{w}_{T}=\frac{C_{T}\left(\mathbf{B}^{-1}\mathbf{A}\right)\bm{w}_{0}}{\left\|{C_{T}\left(\mathbf{B}^{-1}\mathbf{A}\right)\bm{w}_{0}}\right\|_{\mathbf{B}}},

where
C_{T}
is a scaled Chebyshev polynomial of degree
T
. That is, the output vector obtained by applying (
7
)-(
8
) recursively is equivalent to performing only recursion (
6
) without normalizing the iterates at each step, but only at the last step. To see that, suppose that
\bm{w}_{-1}=\bm{x}_{-1}=0
and
\bm{w}_{0}=\bm{x}_{0}
, then we will show by induction that
\bm{w}_{t}=\bm{x}_{t}\alpha_{t}^{-1}
for all
t\geq 1
, where
\alpha_{t}=\gamma_{t}\gamma_{t-1}\ldots\gamma_{0}>0
. Assume that
\bm{w}_{i}=\bm{x}_{i}\alpha_{i}^{-1}
for all
i\leq t
, the sequence generated by (
7
)-(
8
) can be written as

\displaystyle\bm{w}_{t+1}=\left(\mathbf{B}^{-1}\mathbf{A}\bm{w}_{t}-\beta\bm{w}_{t-1}\gamma_{t}^{-1}\right)\gamma_{t+1}^{-1}.

(9)

It follows that

\displaystyle\bm{w}_{t+1}

\displaystyle=\left(\mathbf{B}^{-1}\mathbf{A}\bm{x}_{t}\alpha_{t}^{-1}-\beta\bm{x}_{t-1}\alpha_{t-1}^{-1}\gamma_{t}^{-1}\right)\gamma_{t+1}^{-1}

\displaystyle=\left(\mathbf{B}^{-1}\mathbf{A}\bm{x}_{t}\alpha_{t}^{-1}-\beta\bm{x}_{t-1}\alpha_{t}^{-1}\right)\gamma_{t+1}^{-1}

\displaystyle=\left(\mathbf{B}^{-1}\mathbf{A}\bm{x}_{t}-\beta\bm{x}_{t-1}\right)\alpha_{t}^{-1}\gamma_{t+1}^{-1}

\displaystyle=\bm{x}_{t+1}\alpha_{t+1}^{-1},

as desired. The following result, whose proof can be found in
[
21

, characterizes the convergence rate of Algorithm
1
in the absence of noise.

Proposition 3

(Convergence of the exact method)
.

Let
\lambda_{i}
be the eigenvalues of
\mathbf{B}^{-1}\mathbf{A}
satisfying
\left|{\lambda_{1}}\right|>\left|{\lambda_{2}}\right|\geq\ldots\geq\left|{\lambda_{d}}\right|
, and let
\Delta=1-\left|{\lambda_{2}}\right|/\left|{\lambda_{1}}\right|
be its relative eigenvalue gap. If the least squares subproblems in Step 4 of Algorithm

are solved exactly and the parameter
\beta
satisfies
\left|{\lambda_{2}}\right|\leq 2\sqrt{\beta}<\left|{\lambda_{1}}\right|
, then

\displaystyle\sin^{2}\theta\left(\bm{w}_{T},\bm{u}_{1}\right)\leq 4\left(\frac{2\sqrt{\beta}}{\left|{\lambda_{1}}\right|+\sqrt{\lambda_{1}^{2}-4\beta}}\right)^{2T}\tan^{2}\theta\left(\bm{w}_{0},\bm{u}_{1}\right).

Moreover, if
\beta=\lambda_{2}^{2}/4
, then one can achieve an
\epsilon
-optimal solution, i.e,
\sin\theta\left(\bm{w}_{T},\bm{u}_{1}\right)\leq\epsilon
, after at most
T=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{\Delta}}\log\frac{\tan\theta_{0}}{\epsilon}\right)
iterations.

The above result matches the iteration complexity of the Lanczos method, a significantly more complex algorithm
[
11
]
. However, note that the result requires exact computation of
\mathbf{B}^{-1}\mathbf{A}\bm{w}
for some vector
\bm{w}
, which (as already discussed) is prohibitively expensive for large scale data sets.

3.3
Warm-start

Initialization plays a critical role in designing an efficient method, cf. the work on the standard power iteration in
[
9
]
. Recall that the matrix-vector product
\mathbf{B}^{-1}\mathbf{A}\bm{w}_{t}
is computed approximately by solving the least squares problem

\displaystyle\min_{\bm{w}\in\mathbbmss{R}^{d}}f\left(\bm{w}\right):=\frac{1}{2}\bm{w}^{\top}\mathbf{B}\bm{w}-\bm{w}^{\top}\mathbf{A}\bm{w}_{t},

whose unique solution is
\bm{w}^{\star}=\mathbf{B}^{-1}\mathbf{A}\bm{w}_{t}
. As the iterates converge, the solutions to the least squares problems in consecutive iterations become increasingly similar. It is natural to use a scaled version of the previous iterate as initialization. This gives the following initial objective

\displaystyle f\left(\alpha\bm{w}_{t}\right)=\frac{1}{2}\alpha^{2}\bm{w}_{t}^{\top}\mathbf{B}\bm{w}_{t}-\alpha\bm{w}_{t}^{\top}\mathbf{A}\bm{w}_{t}.

Minimizing
f\left(\alpha\bm{w}_{t}\right)
over
\alpha
gives us the optimal scaling
\alpha_{t}^{\star}=\bm{w}_{t}^{\top}\mathbf{A}\bm{w}_{t}/\bm{w}_{t}^{\top}\mathbf{B}\bm{w}_{t}
.
As will be shown later, with this initialization, we only need to run the least squares solver until the initial error has been reduced by a constant factor, independent of the final suboptimality required.

3.4
Convergence argument

In this subsection, we will show that as long as the errors in the matrix-vector multiplications are controlled in an appropriate way,
NAPI
enjoys the same convergence rate as the idealized method where subproblems are solved exactly.

Due to the inexact solution of subproblems, the output of Algorithm
1
is no longer equivalent to applying the Chebyshev polynomial to an initial vector, hence the proof of Proposition
3
is not applicable anymore. However, the iterates defined by Algorithm

\displaystyle\bm{\vartheta}_{t+1}:=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{w}_{t+1}\\

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\gamma_{t+1}}\left(\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{B}^{-1}\mathbf{A}&-\beta\mathbf{I}\\[3.00003pt]

\displaystyle\mathbf{C}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{B}^{-1}\mathbf{A}&-\beta\mathbf{I}\\[3.00003pt]

\displaystyle\mathbf{W}_{T}=\mathbf{U}\mathbf{\Lambda}(\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{\Lambda}^{2})^{-1/2}\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{G}_{T}^{-1}+\mathbf{E}\mathbf{G}_{T}^{-1}=\big(\mathbf{U}+\mathbf{E}\mathbf{M}^{-1}\big)\mathbf{M}\mathbf{G}_{T}^{-1},

4
Application to CCA

Our main result can readily lead to improvement of several downstream applications. In this section, we will discuss the benefits of our proposed method in solving the CCA problem.

In CCA, we are provided with pairs of data points from two views
(\bm{x}_{1},\bm{y}_{1}),\ldots,(\bm{x}_{n},\bm{y}_{n})
where
\bm{x}_{i}\in\mathbbmss{R}^{1\times d_{1}}
,
\bm{y}_{i}\in\mathbbmss{R}^{1\times d_{2}}
, and
n
is the size of the training set. For example, the views can be the visual image data and audio data in a video of people speaking. Let
\mathbf{X}=\left[\bm{x}_{1},\ldots,\bm{x}_{n}\right]^{\top}\in\mathbbmss{R}^{n\times d_{1}}
and
\mathbf{Y}=\left[\bm{y}_{1},\ldots,\bm{y}_{n}\right]^{\top}\in\mathbbmss{R}^{n\times d_{2}}
be the data matrices for each view, respectively. We define the relevant empirical covariance matrices as
\mathbf{\Sigma}_{11}=\frac{1}{n}\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{X}+\gamma_{1}\mathbf{I}
,
\mathbf{\Sigma}_{22}=\frac{1}{n}\mathbf{Y}^{\top}\mathbf{Y}+\gamma_{2}\mathbf{I}
, and
\mathbf{\Sigma}_{12}=\frac{1}{n}\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{Y}
, where
\gamma_{1}
and
\gamma_{2}
are regularization parameters.
The goal is to find a linear transformation of the points in each view that retains as much as the redundant information between the views. More specifically, CCA finds the solution of the following problem

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \left(\bm{\phi}_{i},\bm{\varphi}_{i}\right)&\in\underset{\bm{\phi},\bm{\varphi}}{\argmax}&&\hskip-5.69046pt\bm{\phi}^{\top}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{12}\bm{\varphi}\\
&\hskip-5.69046pt\text{subject to}&&\hskip-5.69046pt\bm{\phi}^{\top}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{11}\bm{\phi}=\bm{\varphi}^{\top}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{22}\bm{\varphi}=1\\
&&&\hskip-5.69046pt\bm{\phi}^{\top}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{11}\bm{\phi}_{j}=\bm{\varphi}^{\top}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{22}\bm{\varphi}_{j}=0,\,\,\forall j\leq i-1.\end{aligned}

(11)

It is easy to check that any stationary point
\left(\bm{\phi}_{i},\bm{\varphi}_{i}\right)
of Problem (
11
) satisfies

\displaystyle\mathbf{\Sigma}_{12}\bm{\varphi}_{i}=\lambda_{i}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{11}\bm{\phi}_{i}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathbf{\Sigma}_{12}^{\top}\bm{\phi}_{i}=\lambda^{\prime}_{i}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{22}\bm{\varphi}_{i},

(12)

where
\lambda_{i}
and
\lambda^{\prime}_{i}
are two constants. Using the constraints, it can be verified that
\lambda_{i}=\lambda^{\prime}_{i}
. The system (
12
) above can therefore be cast into the following generalized eigenproblem

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{0}&\mathbf{\Sigma}_{12}\\[0.0pt]
\mathbf{\Sigma}_{12}^{\top}&\mathbf{0}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\bm{\phi}_{i}\\
\bm{\varphi}_{i}\end{bmatrix}=\lambda_{i}\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{11}&\mathbf{0}\\[0.0pt]
\mathbf{0}&\mathbf{\Sigma}_{22}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\bm{\phi}_{i}\\
\bm{\varphi}_{i}\end{bmatrix}.

(13)

The solution to this problem has
d_{1}+d_{2}
eigenvalues
\lambda_{1}>\lambda_{2}>\ldots>-\lambda_{2}>-\lambda_{1}
; for each eigenvalue
\lambda_{i}
with the corresponding eigenvector

[\bm{\phi}_{i}^{\top},\bm{\varphi}_{i}^{\top}]^{\top}
,
-\lambda_{i}
is also an eigenvalue with the corresponding eigenvector

[\bm{\phi}_{i}^{\top},-\bm{\varphi}_{i}^{\top}]^{\top}
.
Let
\mathbf{A}
and
\mathbf{B}
be the matrices on the left and right in (
13
), respectively, then the eigenvalue gap of
\mathbf{B}^{-1}\mathbf{A}
is zero. Thus, iterative methods for finding the leading eigenvector of
\mathbf{B}^{-1}\mathbf{A}
may not converge. Since the top
2k
-dimensional eigen-space of
\mathbf{B}^{-1}\mathbf{A}
is the subspace spanned by
[\bm{\phi}_{i}^{\top},\bm{\varphi}_{i}^{\top}]^{\top}
and

[\bm{\phi}_{i}^{\top},-\bm{\varphi}_{i}^{\top}]^{\top}
,
i=1,\ldots,k
, one can solve the above problem for the top-
2k
eigenvector problem and then choose any orthogonal basis that spans the output subspace and a random
k
-dimensional projection of those vectors. Finally, to fulfil the constraints in CCA problem, we perform the last orthogonalization steps w.r.t
\mathbf{\Sigma}_{11}
and
\mathbf{\Sigma}_{22}

6
Conclusion

We have proposed and analyzed a simple algorithm for finding the dominant generalized eigenvectors of a pair of symmetric matrices. The algorithm admits a linear convergence rate and was shown to outperform the current state-of-the-art algorithms. To achieve that result, our algorithm exploits the problem structure and decomposes the overall problem into a sequence of subproblems. The approximation theory is used to accelerate the overall process which helps to achieve the asymptotic iteration complexity of the Lanczos method, while each of the subproblem can be solved efficiently by advanced iterative solvers and with a well-design initialization. This translates into a
linear
time algorithm in the input sizes, suitable for many large-scale applications in machine learning and data science.

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\left\|{\mathrm{P}^{\perp}_{\bm{v}_{1}}\mathbf{C}\bm{\vartheta}_{t}}\right\|_{\mathbf{\mathcal{B}}}+\left\|{\mathrm{P}^{\perp}_{\bm{v}_{1}}\bm{\xi}_{t}}\right\|_{\mathbf{\mathcal{B}}}}{\left\|{\mathrm{P}_{\bm{v}_{1}}\mathbf{C}\bm{\vartheta}_{t}}\right\|_{\mathbf{\mathcal{B}}}-\left\|{\mathrm{P}_{\bm{v}_{1}}\bm{\xi}_{t}}\right\|_{\mathbf{\mathcal{B}}}}.

(14)

Since

\displaystyle\geq\left|{\mu_{1}}\right|\left\|{\mathrm{P}_{\bm{v}_{1}}\bm{\vartheta}_{t}}\right\|_{\mathbf{\mathcal{B}}}

\displaystyle\left\|{\mathrm{P}^{\perp}_{\bm{v}_{1}}\mathbf{C}\bm{\vartheta}_{t}}\right\|_{\mathbf{\mathcal{B}}}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\bm{\vartheta}_{t}^{\top}\mathbf{C}^{\top}(\mathrm{P}^{\perp}_{\bm{v}_{1}})^{\top}\mathbf{\mathcal{B}}\mathrm{P}^{\perp}_{\bm{v}_{1}}\mathbf{C}\bm{\vartheta}_{t}}\leq\left|{\mu_{2}}\right|\left\|{\mathrm{P}^{\perp}_{\bm{v}_{1}}\bm{\vartheta}_{t}}\right\|_{\mathbf{\mathcal{B}}},

it follows that

\displaystyle\tan\theta\left(\bm{\vartheta}_{t+1},\bm{v}_{1}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\left\|{\mathrm{P}^{\perp}_{\bm{v}_{1}}\bm{\vartheta}_{t}}\right\|_{\mathbf{\mathcal{B}}}}{\left\|{\mathrm{P}_{\bm{v}_{1}}\bm{\vartheta}_{t}}\right\|_{\mathbf{\mathcal{B}}}}\times\frac{\left|{\mu_{2}}\right|+\frac{\left\|{\mathrm{P}^{\perp}_{\bm{v}_{1}}\bm{\xi}_{t}}\right\|_{\mathbf{\mathcal{B}}}}{\left\|{\mathrm{P}^{\perp}_{\bm{v}_{1}}\bm{\vartheta}_{t}}\right\|_{\mathbf{\mathcal{B}}}}}{\left|{\mu_{1}}\right|-\frac{\left\|{\mathrm{P}_{\bm{v}_{1}}\bm{\xi}_{t}}\right\|_{\mathbf{\mathcal{B}}}}{\left\|{\mathrm{P}_{\bm{v}_{1}}\bm{\vartheta}_{t}}\right\|_{\mathbf{\mathcal{B}}}}}

\displaystyle\hskip 8.5359pt\leq\tan\theta\left(\bm{\vartheta}_{t},\bm{v}_{1}\right)\times\frac{\left|{\mu_{2}}\right|+\frac{\left\|{\mathrm{P}^{\perp}_{\bm{v}_{1}}\bm{\xi}_{t}}\right\|_{\mathbf{\mathcal{B}}}}{\left\|{\mathrm{P}^{\perp}_{\bm{v}_{1}}\bm{\vartheta}_{t}}\right\|_{\mathbf{\mathcal{B}}}}}{\left|{\mu_{1}}\right|-\frac{\left\|{\mathrm{P}_{\bm{v}_{1}}\bm{\xi}_{t}}\right\|_{\mathbf{\mathcal{B}}}}{\left\|{\mathrm{P}_{\bm{v}_{1}}\bm{\vartheta}_{t}}\right\|_{\mathbf{\mathcal{B}}}}}

\displaystyle\hskip 8.5359pt=\tan\theta\left(\bm{\vartheta}_{t},\bm{v}_{1}\right)\times\frac{\left|{\mu_{2}}\right|+\frac{\left\|{\bm{\xi}_{t}}\right\|_{\mathbf{\mathcal{B}}}}{\left\|{\bm{\vartheta}_{t}}\right\|_{\mathbf{\mathcal{B}}}\sin\theta\left(\bm{\vartheta}_{t},\bm{v}_{1}\right)}}{\left|{\mu_{1}}\right|-\frac{\left\|{\bm{\xi}_{t}}\right\|_{\mathbf{\mathcal{B}}}}{\left\|{\bm{\vartheta}_{t}}\right\|_{\mathbf{\mathcal{B}}}\cos\theta\left(\bm{\vartheta}_{t},\bm{v}_{1}\right)}}.

(15)

To get a geometric rate of convergence, it suffices to choose the noise level such that the rightmost term in (
A.2
) is less than 1. Since

yields

(16)

(18)

\beta

Note that the right-hand side of (

\beta
, hence achieving its optimal solution at

Combining (
B
) and (

\displaystyle\sin^{2}\theta\left(\bm{\vartheta}_{t},\bm{v}_{1}\right)\leq\tan^{2}\theta\left(\bm{\vartheta}_{t},\bm{v}_{1}\right)\leq\left(\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\sqrt{\beta}}{\left|{\lambda_{1}}\right|+\sqrt{\lambda_{1}^{2}-4\beta}}\right)^{2t}\tan^{2}\theta\left(\bm{\vartheta}_{0},\bm{v}_{1}\right).

(19)

We now pay attention to the running time of Algorithm
1
required to achieve an
\epsilon
-optimal solution, when the parameter
\beta
is chosen optimally. For
\beta=\left|{\lambda_{2}}\right|^{2}/4
, we have

\displaystyle 1-\frac{\left|{\mu_{2}}\right|}{\left|{\mu_{1}}\right|}

\displaystyle=1-\frac{2\sqrt{\beta}}{\left|{\lambda_{1}}\right|+\sqrt{\lambda_{1}^{2}-4\beta}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\left|{\lambda_{1}}\right|-\left|{\lambda_{2}}\right|+\sqrt{\lambda_{1}^{2}-\lambda_{2}^{2}}}{\left|{\lambda_{1}}\right|+\sqrt{\lambda_{1}^{2}-\lambda_{2}^{2}}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{\left|{\lambda_{1}}\right|-\left|{\lambda_{2}}\right|}\left(\sqrt{\left|{\lambda_{1}}\right|+\left|{\lambda_{2}}\right|}-\sqrt{\left|{\lambda_{1}}\right|-\left|{\lambda_{2}}\right|}\right)}{\left|{\lambda_{2}}\right|}

(20)

k

\displaystyle t\geq\frac{2\left|{\mu_{1}}\right|}{\left|{\mu_{1}}\right|-\left|{\mu_{2}}\right|}\log\frac{\tan\theta\left(\bm{\vartheta}_{0},\bm{v}_{1}\right)}{\epsilon}\geq\frac{2\sqrt{2}}{\sqrt{\Delta}}\log\frac{\tan\theta\left(\bm{\vartheta}_{0},\bm{v}_{1}\right)}{\epsilon}.

\displaystyle\textsf{r}\left(\bm{w}\right)=\left\|{\bm{w}-\mathbf{B}^{-1}\mathbf{A}\bm{w}_{t}}\right\|_{\mathbf{B}}^{2}=2\left(f\left(\bm{w}\right)-f\left(\mathbf{B}^{-1}\mathbf{A}\bm{w}_{t}\right)\right).

\displaystyle\frac{\textsf{r}_{\mathrm{des}}}{\textsf{r}_{\mathrm{init}}}\leq\frac{\left(\left|{\mu_{1}}\right|-\left|{\mu_{2}}\right|\right)^{2}}{16\lambda_{1}^{2}}\frac{\min\left\{\sin^{2}\theta\left(\bm{\vartheta}_{t},\bm{v}_{1}\right),\cos^{2}\theta\left(\bm{\vartheta}_{t},\bm{v}_{1}\right)\right\}}{\sin^{2}\theta\left(\bm{w}_{t},\bm{u}_{1}\right)}.
