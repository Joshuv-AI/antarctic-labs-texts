Identifiability of Product of Experts Models
Spencer L. Gordon, Manav Kant, Eric Ma, Leonard J. Schulman, Andrei Staicu

Abstract
Product of experts (PoE) are layered networks in which the value at each node is an AND (or product) of the values (possibly negated) at its inputs. These were introduced as a neural network architecture that can efficiently learn to generate high-dimensional data which satisfy many low-dimensional constraints—thereby allowing each individual expert to perform a simple task. PoEs have found a variety of applications in learning. We study the problem of identifiability of a product of experts model having a layer of binary latent variables, and a layer of binary observables that are iid conditional on the latents. The previous best upper bound on the number of observables needed to identify the model was exponential in the number of parameters. We show: (a) When the latents are uniformly distributed, the model is identifiable with a number of observables equal to the number of parameters (and hence best possible). (b) In the more general case of arbitrarily distributed latents, the model is identifiable for a number of observables that is still linear in the number of parameters (and within a factor of two of best-possible). The proofs rely on root interlacing phenomena for some special three-term recurrences.

Introduction

Product of experts models.

In modeling complex, high-dimensional data, it is often necessary to combine various simple distributions to produce a more expressive distribution. One way of doing this is the mixture model, or weighted sum of distributions. Alone, however, this still requires quite expressive components, which is a hindrance for modeling data in a high-dimensional space. Product of experts (PoE) were introduced in the neural networks literature as an antidote to this problem: the distribution is factorized into a set of independent lower-dimensional distributions
[
11
,
12
]
. Equivalently, the overall distribution is an AND over the factor distributions (which may themselves be mixture models).

PoEs have recently been applied to solve diverse problems requiring the generation of data that simultaneously satisfy numerous sets of constraints. For example, the PoE-GAN framework has advanced the state of the art in multimodal conditional image synthesis, generating images conditioned on all or some subset of text, sketch, and segmentation inputs
[
15
]
. In the field of language generation, a PoE with two factors, a pre-trained language model and a combination of a toxicity expert with an anti-expert, was used to steer a language model away from offensive outputs
[
17
]
.

However, fundamental questions about even the simplest PoE models remain unresolved. In this paper, we study a PoE in which each observable random variable (rv)
X
depends upon statistically independent latent rv’s
{\mathcal{U}}=(U_{1},\dotsc,U_{\ell})
. The
model
here is the prior distributions on the
U_{j}
, and the statistics are the likelihoods
\Pr(X=x|{\mathcal{U}})
.

We investigate the well-studied class of instances in which the
X_{i}
and the
U_{j}
are binary and
\Pr(X=1|{\mathcal{U}})
can be expressed as a product over the
U_{j}
’s, namely, for some coefficients
\alpha
,

\Pr(X=1\mid{\mathcal{U}}=u)=\prod_{j=1}^{\ell}\alpha_{j,u_{j}}

(1)

It is worth noting the latent symmetry present in this class, in that the distribution of
X
conditional on
\mathcal{U}
is invariant to permutation of the
\mathcal{U}_{j}
’s.

The question with which we are concerned is: what is the minimum
n
needed so that instances of this class can be identified from the statistics of
n
independent samples
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
? It is necessary to sample at least as many observable variables as there are parameters. However, the previous best upper bound on this value was
exponential
in the number of parameters.

We show that identifiability holds in the least possible number of observables, in the case of (identical) uniform priors on the
U_{j}
’s; and in twice the least possible in the case of general priors. This resolves the previous exponential gap that existed for this problem.

The only prior upper bound for the for the number of observables required for model identification, followed by treating

We close this exponential gap, both for uniform and for general priors. We introduce a general method for showing local identifiability of these latent symmetric models. Our main results are:

Theorem
1
: In the uniform-prior case, the model can be identified with

Theorem
17
:
In the general-prior case, the model can be identified with

These theorems are logically incomparable. Their proofs share a basic structure, but the second involves some surprising ingredients that are not foreshadowed in the first.

This result opens up the possibility of a fast algorithm for the identification of PoEs belonging to the given class, since the running time of any such algorithm is bounded from below by the number of moments required for identification. We envision a research program culminating in a fast algorithm for robust identification of product of experts models.

Related work: undirected graphical models.

The
conditional distributions
on
X
that occur in our model (

]

(a kind of Markov random field model), and particularly the “harmonium”

{\mathcal{U}}
, one layer of observable random variables

An RBM is often written in the following form:

\Pr(({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{U}})=(x,u))=\frac{1}{Z}\exp(-xWu^{\dagger}-xb^{\dagger}-cu^{\dagger})

(2)

where

Z=\sum_{x,u}\exp(-xWu^{\dagger}-xb^{\dagger}-cu^{\dagger})
. The dependence of

on

can be expressed:

\Pr(x|u)=\exp(-xWu^{\dagger}-xb^{\dagger}-cu^{\dagger}-d)

(3)

where
d=-\sum_{i,j}\log(1-\alpha_{i,j,0});\;b_{i}=-\sum_{j}\log\frac{\alpha_{i,j,0}}{1-\alpha_{i,j,0}};\;c_{j}=-\sum_{i}\log\frac{1-\alpha_{i,j,1}}{1-\alpha_{i,j,0}};\;W_{ij}=-\log\frac{\alpha_{i,j,1}(1-\alpha_{i,j,0})}{\alpha_{i,j,0}(1-\alpha_{i,j,1})}.
This is referred to as an undirected graphical model because each coefficient

is regarded as an energy associated with the unordered pair of sites
\{i,j\}
(
i
observable,
j
latent). The conditional distributions

in (
3
) have
the same form as in our model (
1
) (or its more general version in which the

are only conditionally independent)—but (

) does not allow imposition of
a chosen product distribution on
{\mathcal{U}}
as the prior, and in fact, generally the

will not be independent in the distribution (
2
).

The conditional distributions of RBMs enable expressive statistical models with relatively few parameters. For this reason and because of the connection to layered networks, RBMs have been extensively studied in the neural networks and algebraic statistics literatures (citations above). An interesting recent (and independent) contribution in this literature concerns identifiability
[
23
]
, but there are no obvious implications in either direction between the works.
(The main thing to note is that it relies for identifiability on the Kruskal condition; that condition does gives an upper bound
on the number of observables required but if one works out the bound, one sees
that it cannot be less than

2^{\ell+1}-1
. But the slightly better bound of
2^{\ell}
was the exponentially-large bound that we set out to rectify. Second, one should note that that work addresses a somewhat more general class of models, but then relies upon a general-position assumption about the input, an assumption we do not make, and that is also not valid in our setting of conditionally-iid
X_{i}
.)

Identifying models with uniform priors

2.1
Preliminaries

The model.

In this section we treat the case that the prior on each
U_{j}
is uniform.
Then the model has
2\ell
parameters
\alpha_{jb}
for
j=1,\dotsc,\ell
and
b=0,1
;
and (
1
) yields that

\displaystyle\begin{split}\Pr(X=1)&=\mathbold\mu_{1}\quad\quad\text{ where }\\
\mathbold\mu_{1}&\coloneqq\prod_{j=1}^{\ell}\frac{\alpha_{j0}+\alpha_{j1}}{2}\end{split}

(4)

and more generally

\displaystyle\begin{split}\Pr(X_{1}=\ldots=X_{t}=1)&=\mathbold\mu_{t}\quad\quad\text{ where }\\
\mathbold\mu_{t}&\coloneqq\prod_{j=1}^{\ell}\frac{\alpha_{j0}^{t}+\alpha_{j1}^{t}}{2}.\end{split}

(5)

Hadamard products of Hankel matrices.

It was observed in
[
3
]
that the RBM model under consideration there, was a Hadamard product of simpler models, each having only a single latent variable. A similar phenomenon occurs in the directed model we study.

For each
j\in[\ell]
, define
\Han(n,j)
to be the
(n+1)\times(n+1)
matrix
with entries

\Han(n,j)_{ab}=(\alpha_{j0}^{a+b}+\alpha_{j1}^{a+b})/2\quad\quad(0\leq a,b\leq n).

This is a Hankel matrix of rank
2
. The Hadamard (or entrywise) product of these matrices, ranging over
1\leq j\leq\ell
, is

H(n)_{ab}=\mathbold\mu_{a+b}\quad\quad(0\leq a,b\leq n).

If we had access to each individual Hankel matrix
\Han(2,j)
, we could apply the classical method of Prony or similar methods
[
4
,
8
,
16
]
to determine
\alpha_{j0}
and

\alpha_{j1}
; this method works (for an arbitrary prior on
U_{j}
)
provided the Hankel matrix is rank-deficient.

However, what we have access to is only
H(n)
and not the individual
\Han(n,j)
’s. One may consider
H(n)
itself as a Hankel matrix, by ignoring the product structure on the
U_{j}
’s and regarding
U
as a single latent variable with range
[2^{\ell}]
; but then in order to have rank deficiency, one requires the very large Hadamard matrix
H(2^{\ell})
, and consequently, the Prony method is only applicable to identifying the model if we obtain
n=2^{\ell}
observables
X_{i}
. This is exponentially larger than the number of degrees of freedom of the model, which as we see from the model (
5
) is merely linear in
\ell
. As noted earlier, this exponential gap was the motivation for our investigation.
We show that the linear answer is correct: the model is locally identifiable as soon as
n
matches the number of degrees of freedom of the model (after quotienting out a continuous symmetry which we shortly describe).

Symmetries of the model.

The model has both discrete and continuous symmetries. The moments
\mathbold\mu_{t}
are invariant to:

1.

Discrete symmetries.
The wreath product
S_{2}\wr S_{\ell}
(a.k.a. hyperoctahedral group):

(a)

For any
j
, exchange
\alpha_{j0}
and
\alpha_{j1}
.

(b)

Exchange any
j
and
j^{\prime}
. That is, for
j\neq j^{\prime}
, exchange

\alpha_{j0}
with
\alpha_{j^{\prime}0}
, and
\alpha_{j1}
with
\alpha_{j^{\prime}1}
.

2.

Continuous symmetries.
For any
j\neq j^{\prime}
and
\lambda>0
, the gauge transformation

\displaystyle\begin{cases}(\alpha_{j0},\alpha_{j1})&\mapsto(\lambda\alpha_{j0},\lambda\alpha_{j1}),\\
(\alpha_{j^{\prime}0},\alpha_{j^{\prime}1})&\mapsto(\lambda^{-1}\alpha_{j^{\prime}0},\lambda^{-1}\alpha_{j^{\prime}1})\end{cases}

(6)

The model can of course be identified only up to these symmetries. We can therefore, w.l.o.g., take advantage of the gauge symmetries to scale the parameters
\alpha_{jb}
so that for all
j\in[\ell]
,

\alpha_{j0}+\alpha_{j1}=2\mathbold\mu_{1}^{1/\ell}=:\gamma

(7)

We see now that the model has only
\ell
genuine continuous degrees of freedom.

If
\mathbold\mu_{1}=0
the model is trivial, so in the sequel we assume
\gamma>0
.

To solve for
\alpha_{j0}
and
\alpha_{j1}
, up to the symmetry between them, it suffices, in view of (
7
), to solve for

{a}_{j}\coloneqq\alpha_{j0}\alpha_{j1}.

This transformation results in a family of polynomials

q_{t}
in
{a}_{1},\ldots,{a}_{\ell}
such that
\mathbold\mu_{t}=q_{t}({a}_{1},\ldots,{a}_{\ell})
; fixing any
m
, the mapping
(q_{1},\ldots,q_{m}
) of

\{{a}_{1},\ldots,{a}_{\ell}\}
to
(\mathbold\mu_{1},\ldots,\mathbold\mu_{m})

carries
\mathbb{R}^{\ell}
into a variety of dimension at most
\ell
. If the dimension is
\ell
, then for all but a set of measure
0
, specifically at all regular points of the mapping, the image has finitely many pre-images.

Our main result in this section is:

Theorem 1
.

The mapping
\{a_{1},\ldots,a_{\ell}\}\mapsto(\mathbold\mu_{1},\dotsc,\mathbold\mu_{\ell})
is a.e. locally identifiable.

2.2
The polynomial sequence

We require a more detailed understanding of the probabilities
\mu_{t}
. Observe that

\alpha_{j0}^{2}+\alpha_{j1}^{2}=(\alpha_{j0}+\alpha_{j1})^{2}-2\alpha_{j0}\alpha_{j1}=\gamma^{2}-2a_{j}

so that

\mathbold\mu_{2}=\prod_{j=1}^{\ell}\frac{\gamma^{2}-2a_{j}}{2}.

Similarly,
\alpha_{j0}^{3}+\alpha_{j1}^{3}=(\alpha_{j0}+\alpha_{j1})^{3}-3(\alpha_{j0}^{2}\alpha_{j1}+\alpha_{j0}\alpha_{j1}^{2})=\gamma^{3}-3\gamma a_{j}
, so that
\mathbold\mu_{3}
has an analogous expression. This continues. In the remainder of this Section the subscript ‘

Lemma 2

(Three-term recurrence)
.

Letting
a=\alpha_{0}\alpha_{1}
,

(\alpha_{0}^{m}+\alpha_{1}^{m})/2

can be written as a polynomial
\mathbold p_{m}(a)

satisfying the three-term recurrence

\displaystyle\mathbold p_{0}(a)

\displaystyle=1,

\displaystyle\mathbold p_{1}(a)

\displaystyle=\gamma/2,

\displaystyle\mathbold p_{m}(a)

\displaystyle=\gamma\mathbold p_{m-1}-a\mathbold p_{m-2},\quad m\geq 2.

(8)

Proof.

This is the Newton identity relating power and elementary symmetric functions, specialized to the two-variable case;
\gamma
is the first, and
a
is the second, elementary symmetric function of
\alpha_{0}
and
\alpha_{1}
.

\Box

The recurrence (
8
) resembles the
familiar recurrence of orthogonal polynomials, but differs significantly in that the variable (here

) multiplies
\mathbold p_{m-2}
rather than
\mathbold p_{m-1}
. In particular the polynomials
\mathbold p_{m}
are not of incrementing degree in

(We note however that at the particular value

, these polynomials

Observation 3
.

The polynomials
\mathbold p_{m}
defined above satisfy the following:

1.

The degree of
\mathbold p_{m}(a)
is
\left\lfloor m/2\right\rfloor
.

2.

\mathbold p_{m}(0)=\gamma^{m}/2
for
m\geq 1
.

3.

The leading term of
\mathbold p_{m}(a)
has a negative sign if
\left\lfloor m/2\right\rfloor
is odd, and a positive sign if
\left\lfloor m/2\right\rfloor
is even.

Proof.

By induction over

.

\Box

Proposition 4

(Interlacing)
.

\mathbold p_{0}
and
\mathbold p_{1}
are positive constants. For any
m\geq 2
:

1.

The roots of
\mathbold p_{m}
are simple and contained in the interval
(0,\infty)
; denote them

\beta_{m,1}<\dotsc<\beta_{m,{\left\lfloor m/2\right\rfloor}}
.

2.

0<\beta_{m,1}<\beta_{m-1,1}
and
\beta_{m-1,i-1}<\beta_{m,i}<\beta_{m-1,i}
for
i=2,\dotsc,\left\lfloor m/2\right\rfloor
. If
\mathbold p_{m}
has degree greater than
\mathbold p_{m-1}
then
\beta_{m-1,{\left\lfloor(m-1)/2\right\rfloor}}<\beta_{m,{\left\lfloor m/2\right\rfloor}}
.

(For
m=2
this requires the convention
\beta_{1,0}=0,\beta_{1,1}=\infty
.)

Proof.

We induct on
m
. The Proposition holds for
m=0,1
. Now
\mathbold p_{2}=-a+\gamma^{2}/2
which has a root at
\gamma^{2}/2
and
\mathbold p_{3}=\gamma(-a+\gamma^{2}/2)-a\gamma/2=-3\gamma a/2+\gamma^{3}/2
which has a root at
\gamma^{2}/3
.

Now fix any
m\geq 2
. Let
d=\deg(\mathbold p_{m-2})
, so
d+1=\deg(\mathbold p_{m})
.
By the inductive hypothesis,
0<\beta_{m-1,1}<\beta_{m-2,1}<\beta_{m-1,2}<\dotsm<\beta_{m-1,d}<\beta_{m-2,d}
.
If
\deg(\mathbold p_{m-1})=d+1>\deg(\mathbold p_{m})
, then there is an additional root
\beta_{m-1,d+1}
of
\mathbold p_{m-1}
with
\beta_{m-2,d}<\beta_{m-1,d+1}
.
Since we’ve accounted for every root of
\mathbold p_{m-1}
and
\mathbold p_{m-2}
,
the value of
\mathbold p_{m-1}
must alternate between strictly positive and strictly negative on the sequence of open intervals
(-\infty,\beta_{m-1,1})
,
(\beta_{m-1,1},\beta_{m-1,2})
,
(\beta_{m-1,2},\beta_{m-1,3}),\dotsc,

(\beta_{m-1,{\left\lfloor(m-1)/2\right\rfloor}},\infty)
,
and
\mathbold p_{m-2}
alternates in sign on the intervals
(-\infty,\beta_{m-2,1})
,
(\beta_{m-2,1},\beta_{m-2,2}),\dotsc,

(\beta_{m-2,d},\infty)
.
Now we compute

\displaystyle\mathbold p_{m}(\beta_{m-1,1})

\displaystyle=\gamma\mathbold p_{m-1}(\beta_{m-1,1})-\beta_{m-1,1}\mathbold p_{m-2}(\beta_{m-1,1})

\displaystyle=-\beta_{m-1,1}\mathbold p_{m-2}(\beta_{m-1,1})

\displaystyle<0;

\displaystyle\mathbold p_{m}(\beta_{m-2,1})

\displaystyle=\gamma\mathbold p_{m-1}(\beta_{m-2,1})-\beta_{m-2,1}\mathbold p_{m-2}(\beta_{m-2,1})

\displaystyle=\gamma\mathbold p_{m-1}(\beta_{m-2,1})

\displaystyle<0.

By Observation
3
,
\mathbold p_{m}(0)=\gamma^{m}/2>0
so there must be a root of
\mathbold p_{m}
in
(0,\beta_{m-1,1})
.

For
1<i<d
,
\beta_{m-1,i}\in(\beta_{m-2,i-1},\beta_{m-2,i})
and
\beta_{m-2,i}\in(\beta_{m-1,i},\beta_{m-1,i+1})
.
Moreover,
\mathbold p_{m}(\beta_{m-1,i})=-\beta_{m-1,i}\mathbold p_{m-2}(\beta_{m-1,i})
and
\mathbold p_{m}(\beta_{m-2,i})=\gamma\mathbold p_{m-1}(\beta_{m-2,i})
, so
\sign(\mathbold p_{m}(\beta_{m-1,i}))=\sign(\mathbold p_{m}(\beta_{m-2,i}))=-\sign(\mathbold p_{m}(\beta_{m-1,i+1}))
. We conclude that there is a root of
\mathbold p_{m}
in the interval
(\beta_{m-2,i},\beta_{m-1,i+1})
for
1<i<d
.

We’ve shown that there are roots of
\mathbold p_{m}
in each of the intervals

(0,\beta_{m-1,1})
,
(\beta_{m-2,1}
,
\beta_{m-1,2})
,

(\beta_{m-2,2},\beta_{m-1,3}),\dotsc,

(\beta_{m-2,d-1}
,

.

and

It suffices to show that there is a point at which the Jacobian of the mapping is nonsingular.

We now construct a sequence of rational functions

First, we set

Inductively we construct

By construction,

Define
s_{i}(y)=\prod_{j=1}^{\ell}r_{i}(y_{j})
for
i=1,\dotsc,\ell
so that
s_{i}
is the product of
r_{i}
evaluated at each indeterminate, just as
q_{i}
is the product of
\mathbold p_{i}
evaluated at each indeterminate. In fact, we have

s_{i}=q_{i}\prod_{i^{\prime}=1}^{i-1}s_{i^{\prime}}^{-M(\mathbold p_{i},\eta_{i^{\prime}})}.

Let
Q
and
S
be the following mappings:

(y_{1},\dotsc,y_{\ell})\xmapsto{Q}(q_{1},\dotsc,q_{\ell})\xmapsto{S}(s_{1},\dotsc,s_{\ell}).

Consider the Jacobian of
S\circ Q
, evaluated at the point
\eta=(\eta_{1},\dotsc,\eta_{\ell})
.
By construction

\frac{\partial s_{i}}{\partial y_{j}}(\eta)=\left(\prod_{j^{\prime}\neq j}r_{i}(\eta_{j^{\prime}})\right)r^{\prime}_{i}(\eta_{j}).

(10)

Now

\prod_{j^{\prime}\neq j}r_{i}(\eta_{j^{\prime}})\neq 0\iff i=j

since
r_{i}(\eta_{i})=0
and
M(r_{i},\eta_{j})=0
for any
j\neq i
. Moreover,
r^{\prime}_{i}(\eta_{i})\neq 0
since
\eta_{i}
is a simple root of
r_{i}
, so we conclude that
\frac{\partial s_{i}}{\partial y_{j}}(\eta)\neq 0\iff i=j
. Thus, the Jacobian is a diagonal matrix with non-zero diagonal entries and is therefore invertible.
The Proposition follows.

\Box

Proof of Theorem
1
.

Apply Prop.
5
with the polynomials
\mathbold p
being as defined in Lemma
2
, and
the root
\eta_{m}
of
\mathbold p_{m}
being the point
\beta_{m,1}
provided by Prop.
4
.

\Box

3
Identifying models with general priors

3.1
Preliminaries

We now treat the more general setting where
U_{j}
have arbitrary priors, specified by the parameters

\pi_{j}=\Pr(U_{j}=1).

It will be convenient to also use the notation
\pi_{j}(u)
, with
\pi_{j}=\pi_{j}(1)=1-\pi_{j}(0)
.

Because the
U_{j}
’s are not uniformly sampled, the moments will take on a different form than before (we call the
n
th moment
q_{n}
):

\displaystyle q_{n}\coloneqq\Pr(X_{1}=\cdots=X_{n}=1)

\displaystyle=\sum_{u\in\{0,1\}^{\ell}}\prod_{j=1}^{\ell}\pi_{j}(u_{j})\alpha_{j,u_{j}}^{n}=\prod_{j=1}^{\ell}\left(\pi_{j}(0)\alpha_{j,0}^{n}+\pi_{j}(1)\alpha_{j,1}^{n}\right)

\displaystyle=\prod_{j=1}^{\ell}\left((1-\pi_{j})\alpha_{j,0}^{n}+\pi_{j}\alpha_{j,1}^{n}\right)

\displaystyle=\prod_{j=1}^{\ell}r_{n}(\alpha_{j,0},\alpha_{j,1},\pi_{j})

(11)

where we define
r_{n}(\alpha_{0},\alpha_{1},\pi)=\pi\alpha_{1}^{n}+(1-\pi)\alpha_{0}^{n}
. We may view
r_{n}(\alpha_{j,0},\alpha_{j,1},\pi_{j})
as the contribution of
U_{j}
to the moment
q_{n}
.

Symmetries of the model.

The model has discrete and continuous symmetries as before. The moments
q_{t}
are invariant to:

1.

Discrete symmetries (hyperoctahedral).

(a)

For any
j
, exchange
\alpha_{j0}
and
\alpha_{j1}
, and replace
\pi_{j}
with
1-\pi_{j}
.

(b)

Exchange any
j
and
j^{\prime}
. That is, for
j\neq j^{\prime}
, exchange

\alpha_{j0}
with
\alpha_{j^{\prime}0}
,
\alpha_{j1}
with
\alpha_{j^{\prime}1}
, and
\pi_{j}
with
\pi_{j^{\prime}}
.

2.

Continuous symmetries.
For any
j\neq j^{\prime}
and
\lambda>0
, the gauge transformation

\displaystyle\begin{cases}(\alpha_{j0},\alpha_{j1})&\mapsto(\lambda\alpha_{j0},\lambda\alpha_{j1}),\\
(\alpha_{j^{\prime}0},\alpha_{j^{\prime}1})&\mapsto(\lambda^{-1}\alpha_{j^{\prime}0},\lambda^{-1}\alpha_{j^{\prime}1})\end{cases}.

Of course, the model may only be identified up to these symmetries. Therefore, as before, we use the gauge symmetries to scale our parameters such that for all
j\in[\ell]
, letting
\gamma\coloneqq\Pr(X_{1}=1)^{1/\ell}
, we have that
r_{1}(\alpha_{j,0},\alpha_{j,1},\pi_{j})=\gamma
. As in Sec.
2
, the model is trivial if
\gamma=0
, so we assume throughout that
\gamma\neq 0
.

3.2
Polynomial sequences

It will be convenient to make the change of variables
\sigma_{j}=2\pi_{j}-1
.
Due to the factorization (
11
), we can while studying the polynomials
r
,
focus on an arbitrary
j
, and drop the indices
j
until we return to treating the polynomials
q
. We can now write

\displaystyle r_{n}=\pi\alpha_{1}^{n}+(1-\pi)\alpha_{0}^{n}=\frac{\alpha_{1}^{n}+\alpha_{0}^{n}}{2}+\sigma\frac{\alpha_{1}^{n}-\alpha_{0}^{n}}{2}.

Let
d=(\alpha_{1}-\alpha_{0})/{2}
.

Proposition 6
.

r_{n}=2(\gamma-\sigma d)r_{n-1}-[(\gamma-\sigma d)^{2}-d^{2}]r_{n-2}
for all
n>1
.

Proof.

Observe that
r_{0}=1
and recall that we have set
r_{1}=\gamma
. Note that
2(\gamma-\sigma d)=\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{0}
and
(\gamma-\sigma d)^{2}-d^{2}=\alpha_{1}\alpha_{0}
. So

\displaystyle 2(\gamma-\sigma d)r_{n-1}-[(\gamma-\sigma d)^{2}-d^{2}]r_{n-2}

\displaystyle=(\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{0})r_{n-1}-\alpha_{0}\alpha_{1}r_{n-2}

\displaystyle=(\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{0})\left(\frac{\alpha_{1}^{n-1}+\alpha_{0}^{n-1}}{2}+\sigma\frac{\alpha_{1}^{n-1}-\alpha_{0}^{n-1}}{2}\right)

\displaystyle\quad-\alpha_{0}\alpha_{1}\left(\frac{\alpha_{1}^{n-2}+\alpha_{0}^{n-2}}{2}+\sigma\frac{\alpha_{1}^{n-2}-\alpha_{0}^{n-2}}{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha_{1}^{n}+\alpha_{0}^{n}}{2}+\sigma\frac{\alpha_{1}^{n}-\alpha_{0}^{n}}{2}=r_{n}

\Box

We make a final change of variables to replace
(\sigma,d)
by
(x,y)
:
x=\gamma-\sigma d
and
y=d^{2}
. (This is invertible after quotienting by the hyperoctahedral symmetry of the model.) This yields the following three-term recurrence:

\displaystyle r_{0}(x,y)

\displaystyle=1

\displaystyle r_{1}(x,y)

\displaystyle=\gamma

\displaystyle r_{n}(x,y)

\displaystyle=2xr_{n-1}-(x^{2}-y)r_{n-2}.

It is helpful to define the following family of polynomials
(p_{n})
, which are very closely related to the polynomials
(r_{n})
. In particular, we will see in the following proposition that they are the “coefficient polynomials” of the
r_{n}
.

\displaystyle p_{-1}(x,y)

\displaystyle=1

\displaystyle p_{0}(x,y)

\displaystyle=2x

\displaystyle p_{n}(x,y)

\displaystyle=2xp_{n-1}-(x^{2}-y)p_{n-2}

Proposition 7
.

For any
0\leq k\leq n-1
,

\displaystyle r_{n}

\displaystyle=p_{k}r_{n-k-1}-(x^{2}-y)p_{k-1}r_{n-k-2}

\displaystyle p_{n}

\displaystyle=p_{k}p_{n-k-1}-(x^{2}-y)p_{k-1}p_{n-k-2}

Proof.

Fix
n
, and induct on
k
.
k=0
is immediate from the definitions. The proofs for
r_{n}
and
p_{n}
are essentially identical as they rely only on the three-term recurrences (which are the same) and on the initial conditions
p_{-1}
and
p_{0}
. We write out the argument for
r_{n}
: it amounts to showing that the expression for
k
equals that for
k+1
:

\displaystyle p_{k}r_{n-k-1}-(x^{2}-y)p_{k-1}r_{n-k-2}

\displaystyle=p_{k}(2xr_{n-k-2}-(x^{2}-y)r_{n-k-3})-(x^{2}-y)p_{k-1}r_{n-k-2}

\displaystyle=r_{n-k-2}(2xp_{k}-(x^{2}-y)p_{k-1})-(x^{2}-y)p_{k}r_{n-k-3}

\displaystyle=p_{k+1}r_{n-k-2}-(x^{2}-y)p_{k}r_{n-k-3}.

\Box

In analogy to Section
2.2
, where we studied the roots of the univariate polynomials
\mathbold p_{n}
, we now need some understanding of where each
r_{n}
(which is bivariate, in variables
x,y
) is zero.

Notice that
r_{i}(0,0)=0
for all
i\geq 2
. Since this is a common zero for all
r_{i}
,
i\geq 2
, we call this the trivial zero.

Lemma 8
.

For
i\geq 2

the only zero of
r_{i}
on the curves
x=0
and
x^{2}=y
is the trivial zero.

Proof.

First, for
x=0
the recursion takes the form
r_{n}(0,y)=yr_{n-2}
, and so:

\displaystyle r_{n}(0,y)=\begin{cases}\gamma y^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}&\text{if }n\equiv 0\mod 2\\
y^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}&\text{if }n\equiv 1\mod 2\end{cases}

This implies that if
r_{n}(0,y)=0
, then
y=0
. (Notice that this also forces
n\geq 2
.)

Second, for
x^{2}=y
: here the recursion takes the form
r_{n}(x,x^{2})=2xr_{n-1}(x,x^{2})
, and therefore we have
r_{n}(x,x^{2})=\gamma(2x)^{n-1}
. Therefore if
r_{n}(x,x^{2})=0
for
n\geq 2
then
x=0
.

\Box

3.3
Common Zeros

A new phenomenon that we encounter, unlike in Section
2
, is that we need to identify
common zeros
of
r_{i},r_{j}
for
i\neq j
.
First we make the following observation.

Lemma 9
.

For no
i
is there a nontrivial zero shared by
r_{i}
and
r_{i+1}
, or by
r_{i}
and
r_{i+2}
.

Proof.

Pick the smallest such
i
for which either claim fails, and let
(x_{0},y_{0})
be a nontrivial zero. We know from Lemma
8
that
x_{0}\neq 0
and
x_{0}^{2}\neq y_{0}
. If the claim fails because

r_{i}=r_{i+2}=0
, then writing
r_{i+2}=2x_{0}r_{i+1}-(x_{0}^{2}-y_{0})r_{i}
, we see that necessarily also
r_{i+1}=0
. Then

\displaystyle r_{i+1}

\displaystyle=2x_{0}r_{i}-(x_{0}^{2}-y_{0})r_{i-1}

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=(x_{0}^{2}-y_{0})r_{i-1}

Since
x_{0}^{2}\neq y_{0}
, it follows that
r_{i-1}=0
, which contradicts the minimality of
i
.

\Box

The structure of pairwise-common roots in the
(x,y)
plane is complex and has two especially interesting regions: the
line
x=\gamma/2
and the parabola
y=x^{2}-\gamma^{2}
. The remainder of our analysis relies on common roots within the first of these regions.

3.4
Restricting to the line
x=\gamma/2

On this line we have the recurrence
r_{n}(\gamma/2,y)=\gamma r_{n-1}-(\gamma^{2}/4-y)r_{n-2}
. Since
p_{0}(\gamma/2,y)=\gamma
, the initial conditions for the
p
polynomials and
r
polynomials are identical evaluated on the line:

\displaystyle p_{-1}(\gamma/2,y)=r_{0}(\gamma/2,y)

\displaystyle p_{0}(\gamma/2,y)=r_{1}(\gamma/2,y)

Also since we have
r_{n}(\gamma/2,y)=\gamma r_{n-1}-(\gamma^{2}/4-y)r_{n-2}
and
p_{n}(\gamma/2,y)=\gamma p_{n-1}-(\gamma^{2}/4-y)p_{n-2}
, then it must be true that
r_{n}(\gamma/2,y)=p_{n-1}(\gamma/2,y)
. Now consider the univariate polynomials defined by the following recursion:

\displaystyle s_{0}(y)

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle s_{1}(y)

\displaystyle=1

\displaystyle s_{n}(y)

\displaystyle=\gamma s_{n-1}-(\gamma^{2}/4-y)s_{n-2}

Notice that
r_{n-1}(\gamma/2,y)=p_{n-2}(\gamma/2,y)=s_{n}(y)
, thus we will turn our attention to the zeros of the
s
polynomials. From Proposition
7
we have the following corollary:

Corollary 10
.

For all
2\leq k\leq n-1
,
s_{n}=s_{k}s_{n-k+1}-(\gamma^{2}/4-y)s_{k-1}s_{n-k}
.

We can now prove the following, which has no analogue in the uniform-priors case but is key to the general-priors case.

Theorem 11
.

\gcd(s_{i},s_{j})=s_{\gcd(i,j)}
.

(Note, this is
\gcd
in the ring
\mathbb{R}[y]
.)

Proof.

The theorem will follow from showing that:

If
j>i
then
\gcd(s_{i},s_{j})=\gcd(s_{i},s_{j-i}).

(12)

To show (
12
): In Corollary
10
, since
j>i
, we can substitute
n=j
and
k=i+1
to obtain:

\displaystyle s_{j}=s_{i+1}s_{j-i}-(\gamma^{2}/4-y)s_{i}s_{j-i-1}

This shows
\gcd(s_{i},s_{j-i})\mid s_{j}
, and therefore
\gcd(s_{i},s_{j-i})\mid\gcd(s_{i},s_{j})
.

It also shows that
\gcd(s_{i},s_{j})\mid s_{i+1}s_{j-i}
. From Lemma
9
,
s_{i}
and
s_{i+1}
are relatively prime, it follows that
\gcd(s_{i},s_{j})\mid s_{j-i}
.
Consequently
\gcd(s_{i},s_{j})\mid\gcd(s_{i},s_{j-i})
.

\Box

3.5
Simple roots of
s_{n}
polynomials

We aim to show here that the roots of
s_{n}
are simple and real. We start with some useful properties. Recall that
\gamma\in(0,1]
.

Lemma 12
.

For every
n\geq 1
,

1.

The leading coefficient of
s_{n}
is positive.

2.

s_{n}(0)=n(\frac{\gamma}{2})^{n-1}>0
.

3.

The degree of
s_{n}
is
\lfloor\frac{n-1}{2}\rfloor
.

Proof.

Clearly, these statements are true of
s_{1}
and
s_{2}
. Now suppose
n>2
. For Part
1
, observe that the leading coefficient of
\gamma s_{n-1}
is positive by the inductive hypothesis, and the same is true of
ys_{n-2}-(\gamma^{2}/4)s_{n-2}
. Thus the leading coefficient of
s_{n}
is positive.
For Part
2
, observe:

\displaystyle s_{n}(0)

\displaystyle=\gamma s_{n-1}(0)-\frac{\gamma^{2}}{4}s_{n-2}(0)=\gamma(n-1)(\frac{\gamma}{2})^{n-2}-\frac{\gamma^{2}}{4}(n-2)(\frac{\gamma}{2})^{n-3}=n(\frac{\gamma}{2})^{n-1}

For Part
3
, first suppose
n
is odd. Then
s_{n-1},s_{n-2}
have the same degree and so
s_{n}
has the same degree as
ys_{n-2}
, which is
(n-1)/2
. If
n
is even, then
s_{n-1}
has degree
(n-2)/2
and
s_{n-2}
has degree
(n-4)/2
. Since the leading coefficients of
\gamma s_{n-1}
and

(y-\gamma^{2}/4)s_{n-2}
have the same sign by the inductive hypothesis, the degree of
s_{n}
is
(n-2)/2
.

\Box

With the aid of Lemma
12
we exhibit an interlacing property of the polynomials
s_{n}
.

Lemma 13
.

For
k\geq 3
,
s_{k}
has real roots
\beta_{k,1},\dots,\beta_{k,\lfloor(k-1)/2\rfloor}
, satisfying the following:
(a) For
n>1
,
\beta_{2n+1,1}<\beta_{2n,1}<\cdots<\beta_{2n,n-1}<\beta_{2n+1,n}<0
.
(b) For
n\geq 1
,
-\infty<\beta_{2n+1,1}<\beta_{2n+2,1}<\cdots<\beta_{2n+1,n}<\beta_{2n+2,n}<0
.

In particular, each
s_{k}
has only simple roots.

Proof.

It is easy to check that
\beta_{3,1}=-\frac{3}{4},\beta_{4,1}=-\frac{1}{4}
, so
\beta_{3,1}<\beta_{4,1}
, and they are both contained in the interval
(-\infty,0)
.

We proceed by induction for all
n>1
, treating (a), (b) separately.

(a) Observe that for
i\in[n-1]
,

\displaystyle s_{2n+1}(\beta_{2n,i})=-\left(\frac{\gamma^{2}}{4}-\beta_{2n,i}\right)s_{2n-1}(\beta_{2n,i}).

By the inductive hypothesis,
\beta_{2n,i}<0
, so
\frac{\gamma^{2}}{4}-\beta_{2n,i}
is positive, and for convenience we will denote it by
c_{i}
. Now note as we range over all
i
, the sign of
s_{2n-1}(\beta_{2n,i})
changes every time we increment
i
because
s_{2n-1}
interlaces
s_{2n}
by the inductive hypothesis. By the Intermediate Value Theorem, we have found
n-2
roots in the intervals
(\beta_{2n,i},\beta_{2n,i+1})
for
i=1,\dots,n-2
. We have two more roots to account for. Note

\sign(s_{2n+1}(\beta_{2n,1}))=-\sign(s_{2n-1}(\beta_{2n,1}))=\sign\left(\lim_{v\to-\infty}s_{2n-1}(v)\right)=-\sign\left(\lim_{v\to-\infty}s_{2n+1}(v)\right).

The first equality holds by the recurrence relation; the second equality holds because
\beta_{2n-1,1}<\beta_{2n,1}
, and the third equality holds because the degrees of
s_{2n-1},s_{2n+1}
are different by 1. Thus,
s_{2n+1}
has an odd number of roots, and therefore one root, in the interval
(-\infty,\beta_{2n,1})
. Finally, observe that
s_{2n-1}(\beta_{2n,n-1})>0
because
\beta_{2n-1,n-1}<\beta_{2n,n-1}
and
s_{2n-1}
has positive leading coefficient by the previous lemma. Thus,
s_{2n+1}(\beta_{2n,n-1})<0
. Since
s_{2n+1}(0)>0
,
s_{2n+1}
has a root in the interval
(\beta_{2n,n-1},0)
. Thus we’ve accounted for all
n
roots of
s_{2n+1}
, and shown that they are all negative and interlace the roots of
s_{2n}
.

(b)
We now show that the roots of
s_{2n+2}
interlace those of
s_{2n+1}
and
0
. First, observe that
s_{2n+2}(\beta_{2n+1,i})=-c_{i}s_{2n}(\beta_{2n+1,i})
for
i=1,\dots,n
, where we have made the obvious change of definition for
c_{i}>0
. Since
s_{2n}(\beta_{2n+1,i})
changes sign every time we increment
i
, by the Intermediate Value Theorem,
s_{2n+2}
has at least one root each in
(\beta_{2n+1,i},\beta_{2n+1,i+1})
for
i=1,\dots,n-1
. Finally, we can see that
s_{2n}(\beta_{2n+1,n})>0
because
\beta_{2n,n-1}<\beta_{2n+1,n}
and
s_{2n}
has positive leading coefficient. Thus,
s_{2n+2}(\beta_{2n+1,n})<0
, so
s_{2n+2}
has a root in the interval
(\beta_{2n+1,n},0)
. We have now accounted for all
n
roots of
s_{2n+2}
.

\Box

3.6
Decomposition of the polynomials
s_{n}

Theorem
11
and Lemma
13
actually imply that the polynomials
s_{n}
have considerably more structure than already revealed. This will be essential to our results.

Lemma 14
.

There are real polynomials
h_{n}
such that
h_{n}
has only simple real roots,
\gcd(h_{n},h_{m})=1
for
n\neq m
, and for every
n
,

s_{n}(y)=\prod_{d\mid n}h_{d}(y).

(13)

Proof.

We prove this by induction on
n
. For the
\gcd
claim, while treating
n
we address only
m
s.t.
n\nmid m
.

Since
s_{1}=1
, the factorization and real-roots claims hold for
n
prime, with
h_{n}=s_{n}
; the
\gcd
claim follows from Theorem
11
.

For
n
composite, we know
from Theorem
11
that
s_{n}
shares the roots of every
s_{d},\,d\mid n
;
by the inductive hypothesis this is equivalent to saying that
s_{n}
is divisible by
\prod_{d\mid n,d<n}h_{d}(y)
.
Since
s_{n}
has only simple roots, any remaining factors of
s_{n}
cannot be shared with any
h_{d}
for
d\mid n,d<n
.
Set
h_{n}=s_{n}/\left(\prod_{d\mid n,d<n}h_{d}(y)\right)
. Again, since
s_{n}
has only simple roots,
h_{n}
is relatively prime to every
h_{d},d\mid n
. It remains to show that
h_{n}
is relatively prime to
h_{m}
for
n\nmid m
. Since
\gcd(s_{n},s_{m})=s_{\gcd(n,m)}
, which does not include any factors of
h_{m}
, we know that
h_{m}
is relatively prime to
s_{n}
, and therefore to
h_{n}
.

\Box

We will refer to the
h_{n}
as “atomic polynomials” to recognize that they are what compose the
s_{n}
polynomials. Next we wish to work out their degrees, which we denote
f(n)=\deg h_{n}
.

Lemma 15
.

f(1)=0
,
f(2)=0
, and for
n>2
,

f(n)=\frac{n}{2}\prod_{q\text{ prime, }q\mid n}(1-1/q)>0
.

Proof.

From (
13
),
\deg s_{n}=\sum_{d\mid n}f(d).
Next perform Möbius inversion in the division lattice to obtain an expression for
f(n)
in terms of
F(d)\coloneq\deg s_{d}=\lfloor(d-1)/2\rfloor
: that is,
for
\mu
the Möbius function of the division lattice,
f(n)=\sum_{d\mid n}F(d)\mu(n/d)
. Letting the prime factorization of
n
be
n=q_{1}^{\beta_{1}}\cdots q_{k}^{\beta_{k}}
with
q_{i}<q_{i+1}
, this expression simplifies to
f(n)=\sum_{S\subseteq[k]}(-1)^{|S|}F(n/q^{S})
where
q^{S}\coloneq\prod_{i\in S}q_{i}
. Now consider three cases.

First, suppose
n
is odd. Then
\lfloor(n-1)/2\rfloor=(n-1)/2
, and
n/q^{S}
is odd for any
S
. Observe

\displaystyle f(n)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{S\subseteq[k]}(-1)^{|S|}\left(\frac{n}{q^{S}}-1\right)=\frac{n}{2}\sum_{S\subseteq[k]}(-1)^{|S|}\frac{1}{q^{S}}=\frac{n}{2}\prod_{i=1}^{k}\left(1-\frac{1}{q_{i}}\right).

The second equality follows because
S
has as many even-sized as odd-sized subsets.

Second, suppose that
4\mid n
. Now

n/q^{S}
is even for any
S
because
q^{S}
contains at most one factor of
2
.
For even
n
,
\lfloor(n-1)/2\rfloor=(n-2)/2
. The argument now follows the pattern for
n
odd.

Third, suppose that
n=2m
,
m>1
odd. Now
q_{1}=2,\beta_{1}=1
.
So for
S\subset[k]
if
1\notin S
then
F(n/q^{S})=\frac{n}{q^{S}}-2
, and if

1\in S
then
F(n/q^{S})=\frac{n}{q^{S}}-1
.

\displaystyle f(n)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{1\notin S}(-1)^{|S|}\left(\frac{n}{q^{S}}-2\right)+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{1\in S}(-1)^{|S|}\left(\frac{n}{q^{S}}-1\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{n}{2}\sum_{1\notin S}(-1)^{|S|}\frac{1}{q^{S}}-\frac{n}{2}\sum_{1\notin S}(-1)^{|S|}\frac{1}{2q^{S}}=\frac{n}{4}\prod_{i=2}^{k}\left(1-\frac{1}{q_{i}}\right)=\frac{n}{2}\prod_{i=1}^{k}\left(1-\frac{1}{q_{i}}\right).

\Box

For every

h_{n}
possesses a nonempty set of simple roots, called the atomic roots of
h_{n}
or
s_{n}
; these are roots of
s_{m}
if and only if

. See Fig.

.

3.7
Identifiability: the Jacobian at perturbed common roots

In this section we finally show how to generalize the method of Sec.
2.3
, by leveraging roots shared between pairs of
s_{n}
’s. Unlike in the basic method introduced in Sec.
2.3
, it will not work to evaluate the Jacobian of the mapping exactly at a point specified by these common roots; instead, it will be necessary to slightly perturb the evaluation point.

For any pair
r_{i}
and
r_{2i+1}
, let
J_{i}
be the Jacobian of the map
(x,y)\mapsto(r_{i}(x,y),r_{2i+1}(x,y))
. Since we have
r_{2i+1}=p_{i-1}r_{i+1}-(x^{2}-y)p_{i-2}r_{i}
we can write the partial derivatives of
r_{2i+1}
as follows:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial r_{2i+1}}{\partial x}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial p_{i-1}}{\partial x}r_{i+1}+\frac{\partial r_{i+1}}{\partial x}p_{i-1}-(x^{2}-y)\left(\frac{\partial p_{i-2}}{\partial x}r_{i}+\frac{\partial r_{i}}{\partial x}p_{i-2}\right)-2xp_{i-2}r_{i}

(14)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial r_{2i+1}}{\partial y}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial p_{i-1}}{\partial y}r_{i+1}+\frac{\partial r_{i+1}}{\partial y}p_{i-1}-(x^{2}-y)\left(\frac{\partial p_{i-2}}{\partial y}r_{i}+\frac{\partial r_{i}}{\partial y}p_{i-2}\right)+p_{i-2}r_{i}

(15)

We define auxiliary polynomials
F_{i}
and
G_{i}
:

\displaystyle F_{i}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial r_{i}}{\partial x}\frac{\partial r_{i+1}}{\partial y}-\frac{\partial r_{i}}{\partial y}\frac{\partial r_{i+1}}{\partial x}

(16)

\displaystyle G_{i}

\displaystyle=p_{i-2}\left(\frac{\partial r_{i}}{\partial x}+2x\frac{\partial r_{i}}{\partial y}\right)-(x^{2}-y)\left(\frac{\partial r_{i}}{\partial x}\frac{\partial p_{i-2}}{\partial y}-\frac{\partial r_{i}}{\partial y}\frac{\partial p_{i-2}}{\partial x}\right)

(17)

Using these expressions above, we have that the determinant of
J_{i}
simplifies.

\displaystyle\det(J_{i})

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial r_{i}}{\partial x}\frac{\partial r_{2i+1}}{\partial y}-\frac{\partial r_{i}}{\partial y}\frac{\partial r_{2i+1}}{\partial x}=r_{i+1}\left(\frac{\partial r_{i}}{\partial x}\frac{\partial p_{i-1}}{\partial y}-\frac{\partial r_{i}}{\partial y}\frac{\partial p_{i-1}}{\partial x}\right)+p_{i-1}F_{i}+r_{i}G_{i}

(18)

Lemma 16
.

On the line
x=\gamma/2
,
\det(J_{i})=-2s_{i+2}s_{i}\frac{\partial s_{i+1}}{\partial y}+s_{i+1}(F_{i}+G_{i})

Proof.

Pick any point on the line
v=(\gamma/2,v_{0})
. Notice that we can rewrite
r_{i}
and
p_{i-1}
as follows:

\displaystyle p_{i-1}=2xp_{i-2}-(x^{2}-y)p_{i-3}

\displaystyle r_{i}=\gamma p_{i-2}-(x^{2}-y)p_{i-3}

Writing the partials of both equations we see:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial r_{i}}{\partial y}

\displaystyle=\gamma\frac{\partial p_{i-2}}{\partial y}-(x^{2}-y)\frac{\partial p_{i-3}}{\partial y}+p_{i-3}

\displaystyle\frac{\partial r_{i}}{\partial x}

\displaystyle=\gamma\frac{\partial p_{i-2}}{\partial x}-(x^{2}-y)\frac{\partial p_{i-3}}{\partial x}-2xp_{i-3}

\displaystyle\frac{\partial p_{i-1}}{\partial y}

\displaystyle=2x\frac{\partial p_{i-2}}{\partial y}-(x^{2}-y)\frac{\partial p_{i-3}}{\partial y}+p_{i-3}

\displaystyle\frac{\partial p_{i-1}}{\partial x}

\displaystyle=2x\frac{\partial p_{i-2}}{\partial x}-(x^{2}-y)\frac{\partial p_{i-3}}{\partial x}-2xp_{i-3}+2p_{i-2}

Since we are only interested in the solutions on the line
x=\frac{\gamma}{2}
, we can now rewrite the following partials:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial p_{i-1}}{\partial y}(v)

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial r_{i}}{\partial y}(v)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial p_{i-1}}{\partial x}(v)

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial r_{i}}{\partial x}(v)+2s_{i}(v_{0})

Finally we see that:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial p_{i-1}}{\partial x}(v)\frac{\partial r_{i}}{\partial y}(v)-\frac{\partial p_{i-1}}{\partial y}(v)\frac{\partial r_{i}}{\partial x}(v)

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{\partial r_{i}}{\partial x}(v)+2s_{i}(v_{0})\right)\frac{\partial r_{i}}{\partial y}(v)-\frac{\partial r_{i}}{\partial y}(v)\frac{\partial r_{i}}{\partial x}(v)

\displaystyle=2s_{i}(v_{0})\frac{\partial r_{i}}{\partial y}(v)=2s_{i}(v_{0})\frac{\partial s_{i+1}}{\partial y}(v_{0})

We know on the line
s_{i+1}=r_{i}=p_{i-1}
for all
i
, so plugging this back into Equation
18
, we get the following expression as desired:

\displaystyle\det(J_{i})(v)=-2s_{i+2}(v_{0})s_{i}(v_{0})\frac{\partial s_{i+1}}{\partial y}(v_{0})+s_{i+1}(v_{0})(F_{i}(v)+G_{i}(v))

\Box

Now we can begin proving the main theorem of this section.

Theorem 17
.

The map
(x_{1},y_{1},\ldots,x_{\ell},y_{\ell})\mapsto(q_{2},q_{5},\ldots,q_{2n},q_{4n+1},\ldots,q_{2\ell},q_{4\ell+1})

. Let us make the following observations about this set:

Observation 18
.

For all

,
j\leq n
. Moreover,

if and only if

Suppose

and it follows

.

\Box

We know that each root of

({\partial s_{2n+1}}/{\partial y})(c_{i})\neq 0
. Furthermore, since

and
s_{2n+2}(c_{n})\neq 0
. Lastly, notice that from the above observation,

if and only if

. Together with Theorem

this implies that there exists some

such that for all

, we have that
s_{2n}
,
s_{2n+2}
,
\partial s_{2n+1}/\partial y
, and

for all

Clearly

\displaystyle\bigcup_{n=1}^{\ell}\left\{|r_{2n}|,|r_{2n+1}|,|r_{2n-1}|,|\frac{\partial r_{2n}}{\partial x}|,|\frac{\partial r_{2n}}{\partial y}|,|\frac{\partial r_{4n+1}}{\partial x}|,|\frac{\partial r_{4n+1}}{\partial y}|,|F_{2n}|,|G_{2n}|\right\}

to be the maximum over the maximums of these functions and 1.

We now pick some small
\epsilon>0
to be specified later. Define the set
D_{j}=\{n\mid j\in R_{n}\}
. We will pick our points as follows, for all
1\leq i\leq\ell
, we pick

. If

, we define

\displaystyle\epsilon_{i+1}=\min\left(\bigcup_{k\in D_{i}}\{|s_{2k+1}(d_{i})|,|s_{4k+2}(d_{i})|\}\right)

Notice that this process results in a set of points
\{d_{i}\}
where for all
n
and
k\in R_{n}
if

then
s_{2n+1}(d_{n})<\epsilon_{n}<s_{2n+1}(d_{k})
and
s_{4n+2}(d_{n})<\epsilon_{n}<s_{4n+2}(d_{k})
. Therefore for all

\displaystyle\left|\frac{s_{2n+1}(d_{n})}{s_{2n+1}(d_{k})}\right|\leq 1

\displaystyle\left|\frac{s_{4n+2}(d_{n})}{s_{4n+2}(d_{k})}\right|\leq 1

(19)

We now evaluate the Jacobian at the point

. We scale the rows corresponding to

and

by the following two non-zero values respectively:

\displaystyle s_{2n+1}(d_{n})\prod_{k\in R_{n}}\frac{1}{s_{2n+1}(d_{k})}

\displaystyle s_{4n+2}(d_{n})\prod_{k\in R_{n}}\frac{1}{s_{4n+2}(d_{k})}

We call the resulting matrix

\displaystyle B=\begin{pmatrix}N_{1,1}&\ldots&N_{1,\ell}\\
\vdots&&\vdots\\
N_{\ell,1}&\ldots&N_{\ell,\ell}\end{pmatrix}

Notice that each

\displaystyle N_{n,m}=\begin{pmatrix}\frac{s_{2n+1}(d_{n})}{s_{2n+1}(d_{m})}\prod_{k\notin R_{n}}s_{2n+1}(d_{k})&0\\
0&\frac{s_{4n+2}(d_{n})}{s_{4n+2}(d_{m})}\prod_{k\notin R_{n}}s_{4n+2}(d_{k})\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\frac{\partial r_{2n}}{\partial x}(\frac{\gamma}{2},d_{m})&\frac{\partial r_{2n}}{\partial y}(\frac{\gamma}{2},d_{m})\\
\frac{\partial r_{4n+1}}{\partial x}(\frac{\gamma}{2},d_{m})&\frac{\partial r_{4n+1}}{\partial y}(\frac{\gamma}{2},d_{m})\end{pmatrix}

Lemma 19
.

Suppose
b_{i,j}\in N_{n,m}
. If
m\in R_{n}
then
|b_{i,j}|\leq M^{\ell}
, otherwise
|b_{i,j}|\leq\epsilon M^{\ell-1}

Proof.

First we will make the following observation, assume that
i
and
j
are odd, since

we have that:

\displaystyle\left|\frac{\partial r_{2n}}{\partial x}(d_{m})\right|\prod_{k\notin R_{n}}\left|s_{2n+1}(d_{k})\right|<M^{\ell}

(21)

If
i
is even we replace
2n
with
4n+1
, and if
j
is even we replace

with
y
. Notice that the same argument works in all of those cases. If
m\in R_{n}
, then we know that Equation
19
implies that both
|s_{2n+1}(d_{n})/s_{2n+1}(d_{m})|\leq 1
and
|s_{4n+2}(d_{n})/s_{4n+2}(d_{m})|\leq 1
. Together with Equation
21
this implies that
|b_{i,j}|<M^{\ell}
as desired.

Suppose that
m\notin R_{n}
, we will do the following analysis assuming both
i
and
j
are odd, but an identical argument works for either
i
and
j
even.

\displaystyle\frac{\partial r_{2n}}{\partial x}(\gamma/2,d_{m})\cdot\frac{s_{2n+1}(d_{n})}{s_{2n+1}(d_{m})}\prod_{k\notin R_{n}}s_{2n+1}(d_{k})=\frac{\partial r_{2n}}{\partial x}(\gamma/2,d_{m})\cdot s_{2n+1}(d_{n})\prod_{k\notin R_{n}\cup\{m\}}s_{2n+1}(d_{k})

Notice
|s_{2n+1}(d_{n})|<\epsilon_{n}<\epsilon
and all other terms are bounded above in magnitude by
M
, since there are at most

of those terms and

we have that
|b_{i,j}|<\epsilon M^{\ell-1}
as desired.

\Box

The intuition for the rest of the proof is as follows. We know that for a block lower-triangular matrix, the determinant is the product of its diagonal blocks. This is because any permutation
\pi
which selects an element of the lower-left block must also select an element from the zero block and so the term associated to
\pi
contributes nothing to the determinant.

Similar to this, we will show that the product of the determinant of the diagonal blocks,
N_{n,n}
, contains a term which is not dependent on
\epsilon
. In the following lemma we will show that all
\pi
which pick elements of off-diagonal blocks scale with
\epsilon
. This will imply for sufficiently small
\epsilon
this matrix must be non-singular.

Let
H\subset S_{2\ell}
be the subgroup generated by the
\ell
transpositions
(2n-1,2n)
for
n\in[\ell]
.

Lemma 20
.

For all
\pi\in S_{2\ell}\setminus H
:

\displaystyle\left|\sign(\pi)\prod_{i=1}^{\ell}b_{i,\pi(i)}\right|<\epsilon M^{\ell^{2}-1}

Proof.

Pick any
\pi\in S_{2\ell}
, notice that if for all
i\in\{1,\ldots,\ell\}
,
\pi(2i)\leq 2i
and
\pi(2i-1)\leq 2i
then
\pi\in H
. Thus since we pick
\pi\in S_{2\ell}\setminus H
, then for some
i
either
\pi(2i)>2i
or
\pi(2i-1)>2i
, in both cases we have that for some
j
,
b_{j,\pi(j)}
lies in
N_{n,m}
for
m>n
.

From Observation
18
, we noted that if
m>n
, then
m\notin R_{n}
and thus from Lemma
19

|b_{j,\pi(j)}|<\epsilon M^{\ell-1}
and for all other
k\in\{1,\ldots,\ell\}\setminus\{j\}
,
|b_{k,\pi(k)}|<M^{\ell}
. From this, the inequality follows trivially.

\Box

Now we turn our attention to the portion of the determinant contributed by all the permutations in
H
.

\displaystyle\sum_{\pi\in H}\text{sign}(\pi)\prod_{i=1}^{\ell}b_{i,\pi(i)}=\prod_{n=1}^{\ell}\det(N_{n,n})=\prod_{n=1}^{\ell}\det(J_{2n}(d_{n}))\left(\prod_{k\notin R_{n}}s_{2n+1}(d_{k})\cdot s_{4n+2}(d_{k})\right)

(22)

Notice that using the definitions for
M
and
A
, we can bound the magnitude of the following product from above and below.

\displaystyle A^{2\ell^{2}-2(\alpha_{1}+\cdots+\alpha_{\ell})}\leq\left|\prod_{n=1}^{\ell}\prod_{k\notin R_{n}}s_{2n+1}(d_{k})\cdot s_{4n+2}(d_{k})\right|\leq M^{2\ell^{2}}

(23)

Lemma 21
.

\displaystyle\left|\prod_{n=1}^{\ell}\det(J_{2n}(d_{n}))\right|\geq 2^{\ell}A^{3\ell}-\epsilon 2^{\ell}(2^{\ell}-1)M^{3\ell-2}

Proof.

From Lemma
16
:

\displaystyle\prod_{n=1}^{\ell}\det(J_{2n}(d_{n}))=\prod_{n=1}^{\ell}(-2s_{2n+2}(d_{n})s_{2n}(d_{n})\frac{\partial s_{2n+1}}{\partial y}(d_{n})+s_{2n+1}(d_{n})(F_{2n}(\gamma/2,d_{n})+G_{2n}(\gamma/2,d_{n})))

We will first bound the magnitude of the term of this product that results from picking the left term of each factor. From the definition of
A
we get the following bound.

\displaystyle\left|\prod_{n=1}^{\ell}-2s_{2n+2}(d_{n})s_{2n}(d_{n})\frac{\partial s_{2n+1}}{\partial y}(d_{n})\right|\geq 2^{\ell}A^{3\ell}

All of the rest of the terms must include
s_{2n+1}(d_{n})
for some
n
, and we know that
|s_{2n+1}(d_{n})|<\epsilon
for all
n
. Using this fact and the definition of

\displaystyle|s_{2n+1}(d_{n})(F_{2n}(\gamma/2,d_{n})+G_{2n}(\gamma/2,d_{n})|

\displaystyle<\epsilon(2M)

\displaystyle|-2s_{2n+2}(d_{n})s_{2n}(d_{n})\frac{\partial s_{2n+1}}{\partial y}(d_{n})|

\displaystyle<2M^{3}

\Box

Using Lemma

from below.

\displaystyle\left|\det(B)\right|

\displaystyle\geq\left|\sum_{\pi\in H}\sign(\pi)\prod_{i=1}^{\ell}b_{i,\pi(i)}\right|-\left|\sum_{\pi\in S_{2\ell}\setminus H}\sign(\pi)\prod_{i=1}^{\ell}b_{i,\pi(i)}\right|

\displaystyle\geq\left(\prod_{k\notin R_{n}}s_{2n+1}(d_{k})\cdot s_{4n+2}(d_{k})\right)\left(2^{\ell}A^{3\ell}-\epsilon 2^{\ell}(2^{\ell}-1)M^{3\ell-2}\right)-\epsilon(2\ell)!M^{\ell^{2}-1}

and therefore the Jacobian of our desired map is non-singular. This implies that our desired map is locally identifiable, concluding the proof.

\Box

Open questions

A fundamental issue is whether there is an algorithm for
efficiently
identifying the model from its statistics. Settling this in the positive would be the ideal way also of proving full identifiability.

A natural question is whether the product in Eqn. (
5
) can be replaced by other symmetric functions; even more generally, one may consider the situation in which the effect of the latent variables
U_{1},\ldots,U_{\ell}
on the observable variables is invariant not under the permutation group
S_{\ell}
but under, say, a transitive subgroup of
S_{\ell}
.

A separate direction to pursue is, under what conditions can the model be identified if it has
n
observables
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
which are independent, but not necessarily iid, conditional on
{\mathcal{U}}
. (This is commonly the assumed setting for RBM’s.) That is, Eqn. (
5
) would be replaced by

\mathbold\mu_{S}=\prod_{j=1}^{\ell}\frac{\prod_{i\in S}\alpha_{i,j,0}+\prod_{i\in S}\alpha_{i,j,1}}{2}

for each
S\subseteq[n]
. As a positive indication, for the case
\ell=1
, but with an arbitrary finite range for
{\mathcal{U}}
, identification was shown
[
10
]
to be possible under such conditions, without necessitating any increase in
n
.

A full understanding of this family of problems requires also extending to non-binary
X_{i}
and
U_{j}
. The former is likely straightforward following the lead of
[
6
]
, but non-binary
U_{j}
will demand replacing our two-dimensional space
(x,y)
by a higher-dimensional space and, perhaps, generalizing our approach through pairwise-common zeros, to zeros shared by larger assemblies of polynomials.

Appendix A
General condition for applying root and pole information

Observe that the process (
9
) is effectively Gaussian elimination on the rows of the matrix
M
, which starts out lower-triangular with
1
’s on the diagonal. Carried further this yields:

Theorem 22
.

Let
\mathbold p_{1},\dotsc,\mathbold p_{\ell}
be univariate
rational functions and let
q_{i}(y)\coloneqq\prod_{j=1}^{\ell}\mathbold p_{i}(y_{j})
for
i=1,\dotsc,\ell
. Let
\eta_{1},\dotsc,\eta_{L}
be the points which are

\mathbold p_{i}
.

(y_{1},\dotsc,y_{\ell})\mapsto(q_{1}(y),\dotsc,q_{\ell}(y))

\ell\times L
matrix
M(\mathbold p,\eta)
with
(i,j)
’th entry

, has rank
\ell
over
\mathbb{Q}
.

Proof.

Only If:
Let
v\in\mathbb{Z}^{\ell}
be a linear dependence of the rows,
v\cdot M(\mathbold p,\eta)=0
. Then

\prod_{i^{\prime}=1}^{\ell}q_{i^{\prime}}(y)^{v_{i^{\prime}}}
factors as

\prod_{j}\prod_{i^{\prime}=1}^{\ell}\mathbold p_{i^{\prime}}(y_{j})^{v_{i^{\prime}}}
. By construction
\prod_{i^{\prime}=1}^{\ell}\mathbold p_{i^{\prime}}(\eta_{j})^{v_{i^{\prime}}}
is nonzero and finite for every
1\leq j\leq L
.
Furthermore
\prod_{i^{\prime}=1}^{\ell}\mathbold p_{i^{\prime}}(x)^{v_{i^{\prime}}}
is nonzero and finite for all

because for such
x
every
\mathbold p_{i^{\prime}}(x)
is nonzero and finite. Thus,
\prod_{i^{\prime}=1}^{\ell}\mathbold p_{i^{\prime}}^{v_{i^{\prime}}}
is a rational function without finite roots
or poles, and therefore a nonzero constant. So

\prod_{i^{\prime}=1}^{\ell}q_{i^{\prime}}(y)^{v_{i^{\prime}}}
is a nonzero constant. Consequently, the parameterized variety

q(y)=(q_{1}(y),\ldots,q_{\ell}(y))
has
codimension at least
1
in
\mathbb{C}^{\ell}
.

If:
Without loss of generality suppose the submatrix of
M(\mathbold p,\eta)
in columns
1,\ldots,\ell
is nonsingular.
Let
N
be a matrix with integer entries such that

N\cdot M(\mathbold p,\eta)=(D\mid B)
where
D
is a diagonal matrix with positive integer entries on the diagonal, and

B
is any
\ell\times(L-\ell)
matrix;
\mid
denotes concatenation. Define the rational functions

r_{i}=\prod_{i^{\prime}=1}^{\ell}\mathbold p_{i^{\prime}}^{N_{ii^{\prime}}}

(24)

By construction, for
j\leq\ell
,
M(r_{i},\eta_{j})=D_{ij}
.
Define
s_{i}(y)\coloneqq\prod_{j=1}^{\ell}r_{i}(y_{j})
for
i=1,\dotsc,\ell
so that
s_{i}
is the product of
r_{i}
evaluated at each indeterminate, just as
q_{i}
is the product of
\mathbold p_{i}
