Algorithmic Gaussianization through Sketching: Converting Data into Sub-gaussian Random Designs
Michał Dereziński

Abstract
Algorithmic Gaussianization is a phenomenon that can arise when using randomized sketching or sampling methods to produce smaller representations of large datasets: For certain tasks, these sketched representations have been observed to exhibit many robust performance characteristics that are known to occur when a data sample comes from a sub-gaussian random design, which is a powerful statistical model of data distributions. However, this phenomenon has only been studied for specific tasks and metrics, or by relying on computationally expensive methods. We address this by providing an algorithmic framework for gaussianizing data using sparse sketching operators, proving that it is possible to efficiently construct data sketches that are nearly indistinguishable (in terms of total variation distance) from sub-gaussian random designs. In particular, relying on a recently introduced sketching technique called Leverage Score Sparsified (LESS) embeddings, we show that one can construct an n × d n\times d sketch of an N × d N\times d matrix 𝐀 \mathbf{A} , where n ≪ N n\ll N , that is nearly indistinguishable from a sub-gaussian design, in time O ⁡ ( nnz ⁡ ( 𝐀 ) ​ log ⁡ N + n ​ d 2 ) O(\mathrm{nnz}(\mathbf{A})\log N+nd^{2}) , where nnz ⁡ ( 𝐀 ) \mathrm{nnz}(\mathbf{A}) is the number of non-zero entries in 𝐀 \mathbf{A} . As a consequence, strong statistical guarantees and precise asymptotics available for the estimators produced from sub-gaussian designs (e.g., for least squares and Lasso regression, covariance estimation, low-rank approximation, etc.) can be straightforwardly adapted to our sketching framework. We illustrate this with a new approximation guarantee for sketched least squares, among other examples. The key technique that enables our analysis is a novel variant of the Hanson-Wright inequality on the concentration of random quadratic forms, which we establish for random vectors that arise from sparse sketches.

Introduction

In a standard statistical learning setup, we are given a sample of
i.i.d. points
(\mathbf{x}_{1},y_{1}),...,(\mathbf{x}_{n},y_{n})
, and our
goal is
to perform an estimation or prediction task. For example, in linear
regression, we aim to learn a linear model
\mathbf{w}^{*}
from labels/responses
y_{i}=\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{w}^{*}+\xi_{i}
, where
\xi_{i}
represents the noise. Naturally, the
performance of prediction models for linear regression, such as
ordinary least squares (OLS) and
Lasso, depends greatly on the properties of the sample distribution, as well
as on the distribution of the noise
\xi
, e.g., whether they are
heavy-tailed or not. To that end, there is extensive
literature (see Section
5.2
) which provides precise analysis of statistical and machine
learning models under strong distributional assumptions on the data,
one of the most common assumptions being
Gaussianity and sub-gaussianity. When, the data and noise exhibit small sub-gaussian tails
(e.g., by having a constant sub-gaussian Orlicz norm), then estimators such as OLS and Lasso, as
well as model selection methods such as cross-validation, exhibit
provably good, even optimal, performance. In modern learning tasks these
distributional assumptions are rarely met. On the other hand, we can often benefit
from a great abundance of cheap data available in many
domains. To that end, we ask: Can we leverage this data abundance to
algorithmically introduce sub-gaussianity into a data
distribution, and when is it practical?

A key motivation in this context is randomized sketching,
which is useful in situations when running a learning/estimation algorithm
directly on the entire dataset is computationally
prohibitive. Instead, we produce a smaller sketch of
the data, e.g., via importance sampling or random projections, and use
it as a surrogate data sample. To illustrate this, suppose that our
goal is to solve a least squares regression task
(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{b})
:

\displaystyle\text{Find}\qquad\mathbf{w}^{*}=\argmin_{\mathbf{w}}L(\mathbf{w})\qquad\text{for}\quad L(\mathbf{w})=\|\mathbf{A}\mathbf{w}-\mathbf{b}\|^{2},

where
\mathbf{A}
is a large tall
N\times d
matrix and
\mathbf{b}
is an

N

tractable instance is via the so-called Gaussian embedding: We apply a
randomized linear transformation to both

\mathbf{b}
, where the

Main result: Hanson-Wright inequality for sparse sketches

In this section, we present our main result, showing that
certain sparse sketching operators produce data sketches whose
distributions are
nearly indistinguishable from sub-gaussian random designs.

As there are a number of closely related notions of sub-gaussianity, we
clarify this here, with a more detailed discussion in
Section
5

3

We say that a variable
X

K
-sub-gaussian if its
corresponding sub-gaussian Orlicz norm, i.e.,
\|X\|_{\psi_{2}}=\inf\{t>0:\,\mathbb{E}\,\exp(X^{2}/t^{2})\leq 2\}
, is bounded by
K
.
For a random vector, perhaps the simplest and most popular model of
sub-gaussianity is to assume that it has independent sub-gaussian
entries. However, entry-wise independence is a fairly strong
assumption. The multivariate sub-gaussian norm, i.e.,

\|\mathbf{x}\|_{\psi_{2}}=\sup_{\mathbf{v}:\|\mathbf{v}\|=1}\|\mathbf{v}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{x}\|_{\psi_{2}}
, is a popular
relaxation that allows for sub-gaussian vectors with dependent
entries. However, this
notion is too weak for some applications, e.g., in high-dimensional
statistics
[
4
]
, or to obtain our least squares result (Theorem
3
). These settings
often rely on a stronger property,
called the Hanson-Wright inequality,
which has seen significant interest in the literature
[
30

42

1

47

5

In the classical version given below
[
42

established for vectors with independent sub-gaussian entries, however, in
our main results, we demonstrate that it is applicable far beyond that
setting. In the following statement,

Lemma 1
(Hanson-Wright inequality)

Let

K

inequality with constant

\displaystyle\mathrm{Pr}\Big\{|\mathbf{x}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{B}\mathbf{x}-\mathrm{tr}(\mathbf{B})|\geq t\Big\}\leq 2\exp\bigg(-c\min\Big\{\frac{t^{2}}{K^{4}\|\mathbf{B}\|_{F}^{2}},\frac{t}{K^{2}\|\mathbf{B}\|}\Big\}\bigg)\qquad\text{for any \ $\mathbf{B}$}.

(2)

3
Applications: Least squares and Lasso regression

We next present some of the implications of Theorem
1
. In
Section
3.1
, we present our main application, giving a
precise characterization of the expected approximation error of the sketched
least squares estimator with LESS embeddings. Here, this is not
merely a corollary of our main result, but rather a result of
independent interest, and to our knowledge, it was not previously
known even for dense sub-gaussian sketches. Then, in
Section
3.2
, we demonstrate how our results imply new efficient
algorithms for approximately solving Lasso regression and other
constrained optimization tasks. Further applications to low-rank
approximation, as well as corollaries for the subspace embedding and
Johnson-Lindenstrauss properties of LESS embeddings, are given in Appendix
A
.

3.1
Sketched least squares with LESS embeddings

As a motivating application of our theory, we study the approximation
properties of the sketched least
squares estimator, showing that, when the sketch is a LESS embedding,
then it achieves nearly the same expected approximation error as for a
Gaussian embedding, even down to constant factors.

Consider an
N\times d
data matrix
\mathbf{A}
with full column rank, where
N\gg d
, and an

N
-dimensional vector
\mathbf{b}
. Recall that our goal is to approximately
solve the least squares task
(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{b})
,
i.e., find:

\displaystyle\mathbf{w}^{*}=\argmin_{\mathbf{w}}L(\mathbf{w})=(\mathbf{A}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{A})^{-1}\mathbf{A}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{b},\quad\text{where}\quad L(\mathbf{w})=\|\mathbf{A}\mathbf{w}-\mathbf{b}\|^{2}.

Given an
n\times N
sketching matrix

\mathbf{S}
, the sketched least squares estimator
\widehat{\mathbf{w}}
for this model is the
solution of the sketched version of the problem, i.e.,
(\mathbf{S}\mathbf{A},\mathbf{S}\mathbf{b})
.
If
\mathbf{S}
is a Gaussian embedding, then via a reduction to a Gaussian
random design, the expected error of this estimator is given by the
exact formula (
1
), but the cost of constructing such a
sketch is
O(Ndn)
, which is prohibitively expensive. If we let
\mathbf{S}
be a LESS embedding matrix with

d
non-zeros per row (see Remark
1
), then
the cost of computing this estimator is
O({\mathrm{nnz}}(\mathbf{A})\log N+nd^{2})
,
which includes the cost of approximating the leverage scores, sketching
the data, and computing
\widehat{\mathbf{w}}
from the sketch. We show that the
resulting estimator enjoys nearly the same expected error guarantee as the
Gaussian embedding. To avoid numerical precision issues which may
occur when computing the exact expectation (there is a small but
non-zero probability that
\mathbf{S}\mathbf{A}
is very ill-conditioned), we assume
that: the LESS embedding matrix is constructed so that the random
variables
r_{i}
from Definition
1
are
random
\pm 1
sign variables, and the probability distribution satisfies

p_{i}\approx_{O(1)}\ell_{i}(\mathbf{A})/d+1/N
. We also restrict
the entries of the sketched solution
\widehat{\mathbf{w}}
to a finite
range
\mathcal{R}=[-D,D]
, for a sufficiently large

D={\mathrm{poly}}(N,\kappa(\mathbf{A}),\|\mathbf{A}\|,\|\mathbf{b}\|)
which is absorbed by the
logarithmic factors in the result (see Appendix
E
for details).

Theorem 3

Fix
\mathbf{A}
of rank
d
and let
\mathbf{S}

be a LESS embedding as above, with
n\geq\tilde{O}(d)
rows and
k=d

non-zeros per row.
For any
\mathbf{b}
, the estimator

\widehat{\mathbf{w}}=\argmin_{\mathbf{w}\in\mathcal{R}^{d}}\|\mathbf{S}(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{w}-\mathbf{b})\|^{2}
satisfies:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[L(\widehat{\mathbf{w}})-L(\mathbf{w}^{*})\big]\,\approx_{1+\epsilon}\frac{d}{n-d-1}\cdot L(\mathbf{w}^{*})\qquad\text{for}\qquad\epsilon=\tilde{O}(1/\sqrt{d}\,),

where
L(\mathbf{w})=\|\mathbf{A}\mathbf{w}-\mathbf{b}\|^{2}
and
\mathbf{w}^{*}=\argmin_{\mathbf{w}}L(\mathbf{w})
.

The result easily extends to any sketching method covered by
Remark
1
, as long as the entries of
\mathbf{S}
are almost surely
bounded, as well as to dense random
\pm 1
matrices, affecting
only the polylogarithmic factors hidden in
\tilde{O}
.

Remark 3

The obtained expression for the expected multiplicative
approximation error, i.e.,

\frac{d}{n-d-1}
, is completely independent of the data matrix
\mathbf{A}

or the regression vector
\mathbf{b}
, and it matches the formula
(
1
) for the Gaussian embedding.
Comparable non-asymptotic guarantees for other fast sketching methods, such as SJLT, SRHT,
or leverage score sampling, take the form of
\tilde{O}(d/n)\cdot L(\mathbf{w}^{*})
, where the precise constant and logarithmic terms are problem-dependent

[
49
,
10
, e.g., see]
. To our knowledge, this result
was not previously known even for dense sub-gaussian sketches.

Proof sketch

The proof, given in Appendix
E
, proceeds differently from
the existing bounds for sketched least squares. Our approach is
inspired by the asymptotic analysis of the OLS estimator in
high-dimensional statistics
[
28
, e.g.,]
, but we
obtain a non-asymptotic bound and we avoid any statistical
assumptions on the label noise. We start by rewriting
the expected loss
\mathbb{E}[L(\widehat{\mathbf{w}})]
as the expectation of the
leave-one-out cross-validation estimator,

L_{{\mathrm{CV}}}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\tilde{\mathbf{a}}_{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\widehat{\mathbf{w}}^{-i}-\tilde{b}_{i})^{2}
, where

(\tilde{\mathbf{a}}_{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}},\tilde{b}_{i})
is the
i
th row of the sketched problem

(\mathbf{S}\mathbf{A},\mathbf{S}\mathbf{b})
and
\widehat{\mathbf{w}}^{-i}
is computed by
excluding this row from the sketch. We then use the Hanson-Wright inequality via
Theorem
1
to show that all of the leverage
scores of the sketch are with high probability very close to
uniform (Lemma
7
):

\displaystyle\ell_{i}(\mathbf{S}\mathbf{A})\approx_{1+\epsilon}\frac{d}{n}\quad\text{ with
}\quad\epsilon=\tilde{O}(1/\sqrt{d}\,)\quad\text{ for all $i$.}

Using a standard short-cut formula, this allows us to approximate
the expectation of
L_{{\mathrm{CV}}}
as follows:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[L_{{\mathrm{CV}}}]\approx_{1+\epsilon^{\prime}}\big(1-\tfrac{d}{n}\big)^{-2}\mathbb{E}\big[\|\mathbf{S}(\mathbf{A}\widehat{\mathbf{w}}-\mathbf{b})\|^{2}\big]\quad\text{with
}\quad\epsilon^{\prime}=\tilde{O}(\sqrt{d}/n).

Finally, our key contribution in the analysis is a new approximate expectation
formula for the sketch of the so-called
hat matrix
,

\mathbf{H}=\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{A}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{A})^{-1}\mathbf{A}
(i.e., the orthogonal projection onto the
column-span of
\mathbf{A}
), which arises when deriving the optimum least
squares loss:
L(\mathbf{w}^{*})=\mathbf{b}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{H})\mathbf{b}
.
Specifically, in Lemma
8
we
show that the hat
matrix
\widehat{\mathbf{H}}
of the sketch
\mathbf{S}\mathbf{A}
satisfies:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[\,\mathbf{S}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}(\mathbf{I}-\widehat{\mathbf{H}})\mathbf{S}\,\big]\approx_{1+\epsilon^{\prime}}\big(1-\tfrac{d}{n}\big)\cdot(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{H})\quad\text{with}\quad\epsilon^{\prime}=\tilde{O}(\sqrt{d}/n),

where
\approx_{1+\epsilon}
is defined in terms of the psd matrix
ordering, and the formula holds exactly when
\mathbf{S}
is a Gaussian
embedding. The result then follows because
\mathbb{E}[\|\mathbf{S}(\mathbf{A}\widehat{\mathbf{w}}-\mathbf{b})\|^{2}]=\mathbf{b}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{S}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}(\mathbf{I}-\widehat{\mathbf{H}})\mathbf{S}]\mathbf{b}
.

Experiments

In Appendix
B
we empirically show that our estimate
for the expected approximation error of sketched OLS with a LESS
embedding is even more accurate than suggested by the theory, on a
range of benchmark regression tasks. Our experiments also show that
this problem-independent expected approximation error is not always shared
by other fast sketching methods that are considered to have strong
guarantees for least squares, such as SRHTs.

3.2
Lasso and constrained least squares

In this section, we show how our results can be used to extend
existing sketching guarantees in
constrained least squares optimization from dense sub-gaussian sketches to LESS embeddings. This general problem
setting includes such standard tasks as Lasso regression, sparse
recovery and training a support vector machine.

We consider the following constrained optimization task for

\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times d}
and
\mathbf{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
:

\displaystyle\text{find}\qquad\mathbf{w}^{*}=\argmin_{\mathbf{w}\in\mathcal{C}}L(\mathbf{w}),\qquad\text{for}\qquad L(\mathbf{w})=\|\mathbf{A}\mathbf{w}-\mathbf{b}\|^{2},

where
\mathcal{C}
is some convex subset of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Our goal is to
approximately solve this task by solving a sketched version of the problem:

\widehat{\mathbf{w}}=\argmin_{\mathbf{w}\in\mathcal{C}}\|\mathbf{S}(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{w}-\mathbf{b})\|^{2}
,
for an
n\times N
sketching matrix
\mathbf{S}
. When the sketching matrix is
a sub-gaussian embedding, then
[
41
]
showed
that the sketch size
n
needed for
\widehat{\mathbf{w}}
to achieve a

(1+\epsilon)
-approximation, i.e.,
L(\widehat{\mathbf{w}})\leq(1+\epsilon)\cdot L(\mathbf{w}^{*})
, is controlled by the so-called Gaussian width, which can be
viewed as a measure of the degrees of freedom for the constrained
optimization task (in particular, it can be much smaller than the
actual dimension
d
), and is defined as follows:

\displaystyle\mathbb{W}:=\mathbb{W}(\mathbf{A}\mathcal{K})=\mathbb{E}_{{\mathbf{g}}}\Big[\sup_{\mathbf{u}\in\mathbf{A}\mathcal{K}\cap\mathcal{S}^{N-1}}|{\mathbf{g}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{u}|\Big],

where
{\mathbf{g}}
has i.i.d. standard Gaussian entries,
\mathcal{S}^{N-1}
is the unit sphere in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
, and
\mathcal{K}
is the tangent cone of the constraint set
\mathcal{C}
at the
optimum
\mathbf{w}^{*}
, i.e., the closed convex hull of

\{\Delta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\mid\Delta(\mathbf{w}-\mathbf{w}^{*})\text{ for }t\geq 0,\mathbf{w}\in\mathcal{C}\}
. Specifically, they showed that if
\mathbf{S}
consists of
i.i.d. mean zero isotropic sub-gaussian rows and

n\geq O(\mathbb{W}^{2}/\epsilon^{2})
, then with high probability

\widehat{\mathbf{w}}
is a
(1+\epsilon)
-approximation of

\mathbf{w}^{*}
. We use Theorem
1
to extend this result to LESS
embeddings (see Appendix
F
).

Theorem 4

If
\mathbf{S}
is a LESS embedding matrix for
\mathbf{A}
, with size
n\geq\tilde{O}(\mathbb{W}^{2}/\epsilon^{2})
, then with high probability
\widehat{\mathbf{w}}
is a

(1+\epsilon)
-approximation of
\mathbf{w}^{*}
.

We illustrate this claim with
a corollary for Lasso regression, where the constraint set is an

\ell_{1}
ball, i.e.,
\mathcal{C}=\{\mathbf{w}:\|\mathbf{w}\|_{1}\leq R\}
. Crucially, here the
sketch size needed to obtain a good approximation scales
with the sparsity of the solution
\mathbf{w}^{*}
, rather than with the
dimension of the data.
First, we
formulate an
\ell_{1}
-restricted condition number of
\mathbf{A}
, which is
always smaller than the actual condition number, and which naturally arises in
bounding the Gaussian width:

\displaystyle\kappa=\max_{i}\|\mathbf{A}_{:,i}\|/\gamma_{s}^{-}(\mathbf{A}),\qquad\text{where}\qquad\gamma_{s}^{-}(\mathbf{A})=\min_{\|\mathbf{v}\|=1,\,\|\mathbf{v}\|_{1}\leq 2\sqrt{s}}\|\mathbf{A}\mathbf{v}\|.

Corollary 1

Suppose that the Lasso solution
\mathbf{w}^{*}
is
s
-sparse. Then, using a LESS
embedding of size
n\geq\tilde{O}(\kappa^{2}s/\epsilon^{2})
, we can
obtain a
(1+\epsilon)
-approximation of
\mathbf{w}^{*}
in time

O({\mathrm{nnz}}(\mathbf{A})\log N+nd^{2})
.

Finally, we note that similar results for approximately solving
Lasso and other constrained least squares problems can be obtained for SRHT sketches.
However, there are two key differences: (1) the bounds
obtained for SRHT are weaker than the ones achieved for
LESS or sub-gaussian sketches, e.g., in addition to the Gaussian
width, they also scale with other
problem-dependent quantities such as the Rademacher width

[
41
, see Theorem 2 of]
; and (2) the cost of
SRHT is
O(Nd\log N)
, which is higher than the cost of LESS when
{\mathrm{nnz}}(\mathbf{A})\ll Nd
.

4
Key technical result: A Hanson-Wright limit theorem

In this section, we present the key technical result that we use to
prove Theorem
1
. This result shows that, by relying
on the central limit phenomenon, we can establish that a sum of
bounded random vectors gets very close, in terms of the total
variation distance
d_{{\mathrm{tv}}}
, to a random vector that
satisfies the Hanson-Wright inequality (
2
) with a
small constant
K
, even though the sum may not
satisfy this property directly. There has been extensive literature dedicated to extending and
adapting the Hanson-Wright inequality

[
30
,
1
,
47
,
5
, e.g.,]
,
and our result is of independent interest in the context of this line
of works.

Theorem 5

Let
\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{x}_{2},...
be i.i.d. random vectors, where

\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{x}_{i}\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}]=\mathbf{\Sigma}
is full-rank and

\|\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1/2}\mathbf{x}_{i}\|_{2}
is
M
-sub-gaussian. Define a
k
-gaussianized sample

\tilde{\mathbf{x}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{k}}\sum_{i=1}^{k}r_{i}\mathbf{x}_{i}
,
where
r_{i}
are independent mean zero unit variance
R
-sub-gaussian random
variables. For any
\delta>0
, there is
L=O\big(1+M\log(M/\delta)/\sqrt{k}\big)
, a
d\times d
psd matrix
\widetilde{\mathbf{\Sigma}}
, and a mean zero isotropic vector

\mathbf{z}
satisfying the Hanson-Wright inequality (
2
) with
K=LR
, such that:

\displaystyle d_{{\mathrm{tv}}}\big(\,\tilde{\mathbf{x}},\,\widetilde{\mathbf{\Sigma}}^{1/2}\mathbf{z}\,\big)\leq\delta\qquad\text{and}\qquad\widetilde{\mathbf{\Sigma}}\approx_{1+\delta}\mathbf{\Sigma}.

Remark 4

Note that
M^{2}\geq\mathbb{E}[\|\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1/2}\mathbf{x}_{i}\|_{2}^{2}]=d
, where
d
is the dimension of
\mathbf{x}_{i}
.
If we make a stronger assumption that
\|\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1/2}\mathbf{x}_{i}\|_{2}\leq M
almost surely, then we can obtain a slightly sharper result:

L=O(1+M\sqrt{\log(M/\delta)/k})
, which is what we use to establish
Theorem
1
. Either of the boundedness assumptions includes
all distributions over finite populations in general position.

The proof, which can be found in Appendix
D
, is briefly
summarized here. We start by
defining a high-probability event
\mathcal{E}
ensuring that the sample covariance
matrix
\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i}\mathbf{x}_{i}\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}
is sufficiently well bounded. In the
process, we establish a bound on the matrix moments of the random
covariates
\mathbf{x}_{i}\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}
(Lemma
5
). Then, we
construct the vector
\mathbf{z}
so that it is coupled with
\tilde{\mathbf{x}}

when the event
\mathcal{E}
holds. This requires introducing a correction
term designed to minimize
the distortion of the covariance matrix of
\mathbf{z}
, so as to ensure that
\widetilde{\mathbf{\Sigma}}\approx_{1+\delta}\mathbf{\Sigma}
. We then establish the Hanson-Wright
inequality for
\mathbf{z}
in three steps: First, analyzing the error coming
from the
r_{i}
’s; then accounting for the randomness in
\mathbf{x}_{i}
’s; and
finally, bounding the noise coming from the correction term.

At a high level, Theorem
5
states that as
k
goes to infinity, the total
variation distance of
\tilde{\mathbf{x}}
from a certain family of sub-gaussian distributions
goes to zero. Using the total variation distance to
measure central limit behavior (instead of, say, the Wasserstein
distance) is unusual because of how strong of a guarantee it requires (see
Section
5.2
for discussion). This is possible here because
we are comparing to a sub-gaussian distribution, instead of directly to a Gaussian.

Note that the lower bound we provide in Theorem
2
for

p
-sparsified sketches also constitutes a lower bound for
Theorem
5
. In particular, Theorem
2
implies that there are
distributions of
\mathbf{x}_{i}
such that
M=\Theta(\sqrt{d})
, for which

K=\tilde{\Omega}(1+M/\sqrt{k})
.
This, up to logarithmic factors,
matches our upper bound of
K=\tilde{O}(1+M/\sqrt{k})
samples, and can
be easily extended to
M\gg\sqrt{d}
.

Proof of Theorem
1

We now briefly explain how Theorem
1
follows
from Theorem
5
.
Consider an
N\times d
matrix
\mathbf{A}
with rank
d
, and let

\mathbf{\Sigma}=\mathbf{A}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{A}
. Suppose that the random vectors
\mathbf{x}_{i}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{p_{I_{i}}}}\mathbf{a}_{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}
are defined as scaled row samples from

\mathbf{A}
, drawn using the index distribution
I_{i}\sim p
and scaled so that

\mathbf{\Sigma}:=\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{x}_{i}\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}]=\mathbf{A}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{A}
. Now, let us construct an

n\times d
random matrix
\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}

consisting of rows
\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{1},...,\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{n}
which are independent

k
-gaussianized samples defined as in Theorem
5
. This
sketch can be obtained by applying a
p
-sparsified sketching
matrix
\sqrt{n}\mathbf{S}
to
\mathbf{A}
, where each row of
\sqrt{n}\mathbf{S}
is given by

\sum_{i=1}^{k}\frac{r_{i}}{\sqrt{kp_{I_{i}}}}\mathbf{e}_{I_{i}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}
.
Notice that
\|\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1/2}\mathbf{x}_{i}\|_{2}\leq\sqrt{\ell_{I_{i}}(\mathbf{A})/p_{I_{i}}}\leq\sqrt{\mu}
(as defined in Theorem
1
), so we can let the constant
M
in
Theorem
5
be
\sqrt{\mu}
, where recall that
\ell_{i}(\mathbf{A})
is the

i
th leverage score of matrix
\mathbf{A}
, i.e.,

\ell_{i}(\mathbf{A})=\mathbf{a}_{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}(\mathbf{A}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{A})^{-1}\mathbf{a}_{i}=\|\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1/2}\mathbf{a}_{i}\|^{2}
. Note that we
can rely here on the stronger boundedness condition
as in Remark
4
, so that we obtain

d_{{\mathrm{tv}}}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{i},\widetilde{\mathbf{\Sigma}}^{1/2}\mathbf{z})\leq\delta
for each
i
, with

K=O(1+\sqrt{\mu\log(\mu/\delta)/k})
. Recall that small total
variation distance implies that we can couple
\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{i}
with a
corresponding
\widetilde{\mathbf{\Sigma}}^{1/2}\mathbf{z}_{i}
, where
\mathbf{z}_{i}
is an independent
sample of
\mathbf{z}
, so that they are identical with probability
1-\delta
. It
remains to apply a union bound
over the
n
random vectors
\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{1},...,\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{n}
and their
corresponding coupled samples
\mathbf{z}_{1},...,\mathbf{z}_{n}
, to show the total variation distance of
n\delta
between the
entire sketch
\sqrt{n}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{A}
and the corresponding sub-gaussian design

\mathbf{Z}\widetilde{\mathbf{\Sigma}}^{1/2}
. Replacing
\delta
with
\delta/n
, we obtain the
claim of Theorem
1
.

5.1
Sub-gaussian concentration hierarchy for random vectors

While the notion of sub-gaussian concentration for scalar random
variables can be naturally represented by the Orlicz norm

\|\cdot\|_{\psi_{2}}
, the landscape of sub-gaussian concentration
becomes more complex when we consider multivariate distributions. As
discussed in Section
2
, the natural multivariate extension
of the Orlicz norm, i.e., the sub-gaussian norm

\|\mathbf{x}\|_{\psi_{2}}=\sup_{\mathbf{v}:\|\mathbf{v}\|=1}\|\mathbf{v}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{x}\|_{\psi_{2}}
, while
useful for some applications (such as Theorem
4
), does
not suffice for other Gaussian-like guarantees (such as Theorem

3
).
A more general approach to quantifying sub-gaussianity of random
vectors is to analyze the concentration of real-valued functions of
these vectors. In this context, let
\mathcal{F}
be some subset of functions

f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
with a bounded Lipschitz constant defined as:

\|f\|_{{\mathrm{Lip}}}:=\sup_{\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}}|f(\mathbf{u})-f(\mathbf{v})|/\|\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{v}\|
.

Definition 2

We say that a
d
-dimensional random
vector
\mathbf{x}
has the concentration property over
\mathcal{F}
with constant
K

if
\|f(\mathbf{x})-\mathbb{E}\,f(\mathbf{x})\|_{\psi_{2}}\leq K\cdot\|f\|_{{\mathrm{Lip}}}
for all

f\in\mathcal{F}
.

For the sake of simplicity, we focus on
random vectors that are mean zero,
\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{x}]\!=\!\mathbf{0}
, and
isotropic, i.e.,
\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}]\!=\!\mathbf{I}
. Gaussian vectors (as well as,
e.g., random vectors on the sphere) satisfy the concentration
property over all Lipschitz functions, with an absolute constant
K
.
However, as this condition is very restrictive, smaller function
families have been considered. If we restrict
\mathcal{F}
to
convex functions, then vectors with independent bounded entries
satisfy the concentration property (but vectors with i.i.d. sub-gaussian entries
may not). On the other end of the concentration hierarchy, illustrated in
Figure
3
, is the family of
linear functions, i.e.,
\mathcal{F}=\{f:f(\mathbf{x})\!=\!|\mathbf{v}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{x}|,\text{ for some
}\mathbf{v}\!\in\!\mathbb{R}^{d}\}
, for which the concentration constant of
\mathbf{x}
becomes
simply its sub-gaussian norm. Of primary interest to this work is
the family of Euclidean functions
[
47
]
,
defined as
\mathcal{F}=\{f:f(\mathbf{x})=\sqrt{\mathbf{x}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{B}\mathbf{x}}\ \text{ for psd }\,\mathbf{B}\}
,
which falls between convex and linear in the hierarchy, and can be
used to characterize vectors with i.i.d. sub-gaussian entries.

Proposition 1

[
46
, Section 6.3]

If an isotropic mean zero vector satisfies the
Hanson-Wright inequality (
2
),
then it has
O(K)
sub-gaussian norm and
O(K^{2})
-Euclidean concentration.

consists of independent rows
which have either bounded sub-gaussian norm or
i.i.d. sub-gaussian entries, or more generally, satisfy a
concentration property for a family of Lipschitz functions

[
35
, e.g.,]
. Our gaussianization framework opens the
possibility of extending many of these results to sketching.

Our results are related to the study of the rates of convergence in
the multivariate central limit theorem (CLT), with the key difference that we relax
our notion of gaussianity, which allows us to use a stronger notion
of distance (i.e., the total variation distance, together with the
Hanson-Wright constant). Nevertheless, it is helpful to compare
our approach with the convergence rates for the multivariate CLT in
terms of the Wasserstein distance

[
9
,
7
,
26
]
. Using the setup from our key
technical result,
Theorem
5
, for a random vector
\mathbf{x}
with covariance matrix
\mathbf{\Sigma}=\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}]
that satisfies

\|\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1/2}\mathbf{x}\|\leq M
almost surely, the currently best known
Wasserstein CLT of order 2 yields
W_{2}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}},\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{\Sigma}))=O(M\sqrt{d\log(k)/k})

[
25
]
, whereas our bound on
the Hanson-Wright constant is
O(1+M\sqrt{\log(M)/k})
. The guarantees are not directly comparable,
but one could argue that both bounds are useful primarily when
k
is
large enough to absorb the dependence on
M
and
d
, i.e.,

k=O(M^{2}d\log(M))
for the Wasserstein bound and
k=O(M^{2}\log(M))

for our result.
Also, while the Wasserstein distance can be
used to bound the difference between expectations of Lipschitz
functions, it is non-trivial to effectively bound the Lipschitz
constant for most of the quantities considered in this work, such as
the least squares approximation error, and using
this approach would require a more complex case-by-case analysis.

6
Conclusions and open questions

We provided the first general characterization of
Algorithmic Gaussianization, which refers to
the phenomenon that many algorithmic techniques used to construct
small random
representations (sketches) of large datasets produce data samples that
are more Gaussian-like than the original data. In our main
result, we showed that a sparse sketching matrix can be used to
produce a sample that is nearly
indistinguishable from a sub-gaussian random design. We used this to
show a reduction between a fast sketching technique called LESS
embeddings and a sub-gaussian random matrix whose rows satisfy the
classical Hanson-Wright inequality. Our lower bound
showed that the result is nearly tight up to logarithmic factors, and
that the sub-gaussian reduction is not possible for leverage score sampling.
We demonstrated how our techniques can
be used to provide improved guarantees for sketched estimators
in least squares, Lasso regression, and low-rank approximation.
More broadly, our results point to new open questions
related to the complexity of generating repeated samples from a product
between a matrix and a Gaussian-like random vector:

Open question.

N\times d
matrix
\mathbf{A}
and a function
family
\mathcal{F}
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
(as in Figure

complexity of producing
n
independent samples of a
d

random vector that is, up to total variation

\delta
, distributed according to
\mathbf{A}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{z}
, where

\mathbf{z}

\mathcal{F}
with
constant
K=O(1)
, as given by Definition

Appendix A
More applications: Randomized SVD and low-distortion
embeddings

In this section, we provide further examples of how Theorem
1
can be applied in
combination with existing results for sub-gaussian random
designs. In each of these examples, we can extend existing
results from sub-gaussian embeddings (which are dense, and
therefore not very efficient) to analogous results for LESS embeddings
(which are sparse, and thus can be implemented much more efficiently).

A.1
Randomized SVD

An important application of randomized sketching is low-rank approximation, where
our goal is to estimate a small number of top principal directions
of an
N\times d
data matrix
\mathbf{A}
. One of the most popular techniques
in this area is the Randomized SVD algorithm

[
27
]
, where we construct the approximation by
projecting the dataset onto a subspace spanned by the rows of the
sketch
\mathbf{S}\mathbf{A}
, with
\mathbf{S}
denoting the
n\times N
sketching matrix,
and
n\ll d
. The error of such an approximation is often measured by the
sum of squared lengths of the residuals from the projection:

\|\mathbf{A}-\mathbf{A}\cdot\mathrm{Proj}_{\mathbf{S}\mathbf{A}}\|_{F}^{2}
, where

\mathrm{Proj}_{\mathbf{S}\mathbf{A}}
denotes the projection onto the row-span of

\mathbf{S}\mathbf{A}
and
\|\cdot\|_{F}
is the Frobenius norm. Prior work has shown strong approximation guarantees for
Gaussian embeddings in this context, such as
the following, given by
[
27
]
, comparing the expected error to the best rank
k

approximation, for some
k<n-2
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Big[\big\|\mathbf{A}-\mathbf{A}\cdot\mathrm{Proj}_{\mathbf{S}\mathbf{A}}\big\|_{F}^{2}\Big]\leq\Big(1+\frac{k}{n-k-1}\Big)\cdot\min_{\mathbf{B}:\,\mathrm{rank}(\mathbf{B})=k}\|\mathbf{A}-\mathbf{B}\|_{F}^{2}.

Those guarantees were later extended (and, in some regimes, improved) for sub-gaussian embeddings
[
16
]
and
Determinantal Point Processes
[
14
]
.
The results for Gaussian and sub-gaussian embeddings rely heavily on the
Hanson-Wright property of the rows of the sketching
matrices. Thus, our techniques can be used to extend them to LESS
embeddings by using
Theorem
1
. Consequently, we can obtain bounds for the
expected approximation
error of Randomized SVD with LESS embeddings that, in a certain regime
of small sketch sizes, nearly match those achieved by Gaussian embeddings.

Corollary 2

Consider an
N\times d
matrix
\mathbf{A}
with stable rank

r=\|\mathbf{A}\|_{F}^{2}/\|\mathbf{A}\|^{2}
.
Given a LESS embedding
\mathbf{S}
of size
n\leq r/2
, and a
Gaussian embedding
\widetilde{\mathbf{S}}
of the same size, we have:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Big[\big\|\mathbf{A}-\mathbf{A}\cdot\mathrm{Proj}_{\mathbf{S}\mathbf{A}}\big\|_{F}^{2}\Big]\approx_{1+\epsilon}\mathbb{E}\Big[\big\|\mathbf{A}-\mathbf{A}\cdot\mathrm{Proj}_{\widetilde{\mathbf{S}}\mathbf{A}}\big\|_{F}^{2}\Big]\quad\text{with}\quad\epsilon=\tilde{O}(1/\sqrt{r}).

Proof

We rely on Theorem 2 of
[
16
]
,
characterizing the expected approximation error for a sub-gaussian
embedding of size
n
via an implicit analytic formula:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Big[\big\|\mathbf{A}-\mathbf{A}\cdot\mathrm{Proj}_{\widetilde{\mathbf{S}}\mathbf{A}}\big\|_{F}^{2}\Big]\approx_{1+\epsilon}n\lambda_{n},\quad\text{where}\quad\lambda_{n}=f_{\mathbf{A}}^{-1}(n)\quad\text{for}\quad f_{\mathbf{A}}(\lambda)=\mathrm{tr}\,\mathbf{A}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{A}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{A}+\lambda\mathbf{I})^{-1}.

(3)

Here,
f_{\mathbf{A}}^{-1}(n)
denotes the function inverse of

f_{\mathbf{A}}
at
n
. The function
f_{\mathbf{A}}(\lambda)
is known in the literature as

\lambda
-statistical dimension, a notion of degrees of freedom that
arises in the analysis of ridge regression, e.g., see
[
17
]
. Naturally, their
result applies to the Gaussian embedding
\widetilde{\mathbf{S}}\mathbf{A}
, but also to any
random design
\mathbf{Z}\mathbf{\Sigma}^{1/2}
(where
\mathbf{\Sigma}=\mathbf{A}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{A}
) such
that the rows satisfy the Hanson-Wright inequality with a constant

K
(with
\epsilon
having a polynomial dependence on
K
). So, we can
apply our reduction to LESS embeddings by coupling
\sqrt{n}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{A}

with
\mathbf{Z}\widetilde{\mathbf{\Sigma}}^{1/2}
for some

\widetilde{\mathbf{\Sigma}}\approx_{1+\delta}\mathbf{\Sigma}
, as in
Theorem
1
. Letting

\mathrm{Er}(\mathbf{S}\mathbf{A})
denote
\|\mathbf{A}-\mathbf{A}\cdot\mathrm{Proj}_{\mathbf{S}\mathbf{A}}\|_{F}^{2}
and

\mathcal{E}
be the
(1-\delta)
-probability event where
\sqrt{n}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{A}=\mathbf{Z}\widetilde{\mathbf{\Sigma}}^{1/2}
, we have:

\displaystyle\big|\mathbb{E}\big[\mathrm{Er}(\mathbf{S}\mathbf{A})\big]-\mathbb{E}\big[\mathrm{Er}(\mathbf{Z}\widetilde{\mathbf{\Sigma}}^{1/2})\big]\big|

\displaystyle=\delta\cdot\big|\mathbb{E}\big[\mathrm{Er}(\mathbf{S}\mathbf{A})-\mathrm{Er}(\mathbf{Z}\widetilde{\mathbf{\Sigma}}^{1/2})\mid\neg\mathcal{E}\big]\big|\leq\delta\cdot\|\mathbf{A}\|_{F}^{2}.

Since
\|\mathbf{A}\|_{F}^{2}\leq r\cdot\|\mathbf{A}\|^{2}\leq\frac{r}{r-n}\cdot\min_{\mathbf{Z}}\mathrm{Er}(\mathbf{Z}\widetilde{\mathbf{\Sigma}}^{1/2})
, setting
\delta\leq\epsilon/2
, we get

\mathbb{E}\big[\mathrm{Er}(\mathbf{S}\mathbf{A})\big]\approx_{1+\epsilon}\mathbb{E}\big[\mathrm{Er}(\mathbf{Z}\widetilde{\mathbf{\Sigma}}^{1/2})\big]
. Next,
since
\widetilde{\mathbf{\Sigma}}\approx_{1+\delta}\mathbf{\Sigma}
, we can use the implicit
error formula for sketching
\widetilde{\mathbf{\Sigma}}^{1/2}
with
\mathbf{Z}
, and relate that back
to the original problem as follows:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[\mathrm{Er}(\mathbf{Z}\widetilde{\mathbf{\Sigma}}^{1/2})\big]\approx_{1+\delta}\mathbb{E}\big[\|\widetilde{\mathbf{\Sigma}}^{1/2}(\mathbf{I}-\mathrm{Proj}_{\mathbf{Z}\widetilde{\mathbf{\Sigma}}})\|_{F}^{2}\big]\approx_{1+\epsilon}n\tilde{\lambda}_{n},

where
\tilde{\lambda}_{n}
is defined as in (
3
), but with

\mathbf{A}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{A}
replaced by
\widetilde{\mathbf{\Sigma}}
. To close the loop, we must relate
the implicit analytic expression based on
\widetilde{\mathbf{\Sigma}}
back to

\lambda_{n}
. This can be done easily by bounding the derivative of

f_{\mathbf{A}}^{-1}
, showing that
\tilde{\lambda}_{n}\approx_{1+O(\delta)}\lambda_{n}
.
Now, it remains to observe that since both
the LESS embedding error
\mathbb{E}\big[\mathrm{Er}(\mathbf{S}\mathbf{A})\big]
and the
Gaussian embedding error
\mathbb{E}\big[\mathrm{Er}(\widetilde{\mathbf{S}}\mathbf{A})\big]
are
approximated by the same quantity
n\lambda_{n}
up to a
1+O(\epsilon)

factor, they are also approximated by each other, concluding the proof.

A.2
Low-distortion embeddings

Another important property of sub-gaussian random matrices is that
they can be used to construct low-dimensional embeddings that preserve the
geometry of a high-dimensional space, such as the subspace embedding
property and the Johnson-Lindenstrauss property, which are central to many
applications of sketching
[
49
, e.g., see]
. We briefly mention some
classical examples and discuss how they can be extended to
gaussianized sketches, including LESS embeddings.

We say that an
n\times N
matrix
\mathbf{S}
is an
\epsilon
-low-distortion embedding
for some set of points
\mathcal{X}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{N}
if there is a fixed
scalar
\alpha>0
such that
\|\alpha\mathbf{S}\mathbf{v}\|\approx_{1+\epsilon}\|\mathbf{v}\|

for all
\mathbf{v}\in\mathcal{X}
,
i.e., the embedding approximately preserves the Euclidean norm of

\mathbf{v}
. When
\mathbf{S}
is a sketching matrix applied to an
N\times d
data
matrix
\mathbf{A}
, we are typically interested in vectors
\mathbf{v}
from the
column span of
\mathbf{A}
(i.e., such that
\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}
for some
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
),
denoted by
\mathrm{span}(\mathbf{A})
.
Relying on Theorem
1
and standard
properties of sub-gaussian random matrices
[
46
]
, we can establish the
following low-distortion embedding properties for LESS embeddings.

Corollary 3

Let
\mathbf{A}
be
N\times d
, and
\mathbf{S}
be a LESS embedding for

\mathbf{A}
of size
n
with
d\log(nd/(\epsilon\delta))
non-zeros per
row. Then, there is an absolute constant
C
such that the following claims are true:

1.

(Johnson-Lindenstrauss property)
For any finite set

\mathcal{X}\subseteq\mathrm{span}(\mathbf{A})
, if
n\geq C\log(|\mathcal{X}|/\delta)/\epsilon^{2}
, then with probability
1-\delta
,
matrix
\mathbf{S}
is an
\epsilon
-low-distortion embedding for
\mathcal{X}
.

2.

(Subspace embedding property)
If
n\geq C(d+\log(1/\delta))/\epsilon^{2}
, then with probability

1-\delta
, matrix
\mathbf{S}
is an
\epsilon
-low-distortion embedding for

\mathcal{X}=\mathrm{span}(\mathbf{A})
.

Proof

Whenever,
\mathcal{X}\subseteq\mathrm{span}(\mathbf{A})
, the low-distortion property can be
formulated as a property of the sketch
\mathbf{S}\mathbf{A}
, treated as a linear
transformation: for any
\mathbf{v}\in\mathrm{span}(\mathbf{A})
, we have
\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}

for some
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and we need to ensure that
\|\alpha\mathbf{S}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}\|\approx_{1+\epsilon}\|\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}\|
.
Here, we can once again rely on Theorem
1
,
coupling
\sqrt{n}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{A}
with a sub-gaussian design
\mathbf{Z}\widetilde{\mathbf{\Sigma}}^{1/2}

for some
\widetilde{\mathbf{\Sigma}}\approx_{1+\delta}\mathbf{A}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{A}
. If
\mathbf{Z}
is an

\epsilon
-low-distortion embedding for
\widetilde{\mathcal{X}}=\{\widetilde{\mathbf{\Sigma}}^{1/2}\mathbf{x}:\ \mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}\}
with constant

\alpha
, then with probability
1-\delta
(specifically, when

\sqrt{n}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{A}=\mathbf{Z}\widetilde{\mathbf{\Sigma}}^{1/2}
), for any
\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}
, we have:

\displaystyle\|\alpha\sqrt{n}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}\|=\|\alpha\mathbf{Z}\widetilde{\mathbf{\Sigma}}^{1/2}\mathbf{x}\|\approx_{1+\epsilon}\|\widetilde{\mathbf{\Sigma}}^{1/2}\mathbf{x}\|\approx_{1+\delta}\|\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}\|,

where in the last step we used that

\widetilde{\mathbf{\Sigma}}\approx_{1+\delta}\mathbf{A}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{A}
. Thus, as long as
\delta\leq\epsilon
, we reduced the problem to showing the corresponding
property for
\mathbf{Z}
. Both the Johnson-Lindenstrauss and subspace
embedding properties can be shown for sub-gaussian embeddings (with
sizes as given in the statement) using standard arguments (e.g., see
the proof of Theorem 4.6.1 in
[
46
]
), thus
concluding the proof.

We note that low-distortion embedding properties have been shown for
other sketching operators, including SRHTs
[
44
]
and other sparse sketching
matrices like CountSketch
[
11
]
and SJLTs

[
40
]
. These results often circumvent
sub-gaussian analysis by relying on matrix concentration inequalities
such as the matrix Bernstein inequality (Lemma

3
) or other more involved arguments, however
this inevitably incurs additional
overhead factors in the required sketch size
n
. For example, an SRHT
requires sketch size of at least
n\geq O(d\log(d)/\epsilon^{2})
to
establish the subspace embedding property, instead of
O(d/\epsilon^{2})

shown above (the additional log factor is a well-known limitation of
the matrix concentration-style analysis). The

O(d\log(d)/\epsilon^{2})
guarantee was also previously shown for LESS
embeddings with
d
non-zeros per row, also using matrix concentration
inequalities instead of sub-gaussian concentration

[
18
, Lemma 12]
. Thus, the above corollary suggests
that, when requiring a subspace embedding, we can trade an additional
log-factor in the sketch size for a log-factor in the density of a
LESS embedding matrix. This could be a useful trade-off when we wish
to minimize the storage space of the sketched data.

Matrix concentration inequalities.

In the proof of our main results, we use the following versions of the matrix Bernstein concentration
inequality for the sums of independent symmetric random matrices.

Lemma 2
(Sub-exponential matrix Bernstein)

[
45
, Theorem 6.2]

For
i=1,2,...
, consider a finite sequence
\mathbf{M}_{i}
of
d\times d

independent and symmetric random matrices such that

\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{M}_{i}]=\mathbf{0},\quad\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{M}_{i}^{p}]\preceq\frac{p!}{2}\cdot R^{p-2}\mathbf{A}_{i}^{2}\quad\textmd{for}\quad p=2,3,...

Then, defining the variance parameter
\sigma^{2}=\|\sum_{i}\mathbf{A}_{i}^{2}\|
, for any

we have:

\displaystyle\mathrm{Pr}\bigg\{\lambda_{\max}\Big(\sum\nolimits_{i}\mathbf{M}_{i}\Big)\geq t\bigg\}

\displaystyle\leq d\cdot\exp\left(\frac{-t^{2}/2}{\sigma^{2}+Rt}\right).

Lemma 3
(Bounded matrix Bernstein)

[
45
, Theorem 6.1]

For
i=1,2,...
, consider a finite sequence
\mathbf{M}_{i}
of

independent and symmetric random matrices such that

\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{M}_{i}]=\mathbf{0},\quad\lambda_{\max}(\mathbf{M}_{i})\leq R\qquad\text{almost surely.}

\sigma^{2}=\|\sum_{i}\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{M}_{i}^{2}]\|

\displaystyle\mathrm{Pr}\bigg\{\lambda_{\max}\Big(\sum\nolimits_{i}\mathbf{M}_{i}\Big)\geq t\bigg\}

Linear algebraic identities.

Our proof of the
least squares approximation guarantee for LESS embeddings relies on
the following standard rank-one update formula for the matrix inverse.

Lemma 4
(Sherman-Morrison formula)

For a matrix
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
and
\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}

such that both
\mathbf{A}
and
\mathbf{A}+\mathbf{u}\mathbf{v}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}
are invertible, we have:

(\mathbf{A}+\mathbf{u}\mathbf{v}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}})^{-1}=\mathbf{A}^{-1}-\frac{\mathbf{A}^{-1}\mathbf{u}\mathbf{v}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{A}^{-1}}{1+\mathbf{v}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{A}^{-1}\mathbf{u}}.

In particular, it follows that:

(\mathbf{A}+\mathbf{u}\mathbf{v}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}})^{-1}\mathbf{u}=\frac{\mathbf{A}^{-1}\mathbf{u}}{1+\mathbf{v}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{A}^{-1}\mathbf{u}}.

Appendix D
Hanson-Wright limit theorem: Proof of Theorem
5

In this section, we prove our main result, Theorem
5
,
showing that a
k
-gaussianized sample
\tilde{\mathbf{x}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{k}}\sum_{i=1}^{k}r_{i}\mathbf{x}_{i}
is close in total variation distance to a
random vector that satisfies the Hanson-Wright inequality
with a small constant.

We start by using the matrix Bernstein inequality to verify how well
the sample covariance
\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\mathbf{x}_{i}\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}
can be
bounded in terms of the true covariance
\mathbf{\Sigma}
. For this we
establish the following lemma, proven in Appendix

H
. The lemma is used to show the matrix
moment condition required by the sub-exponential matrix Bernstein
(Lemma
2
).

Lemma 5

There is an absolute constant
C
such that any
d
-dimensional random vector
\mathbf{x}
with covariance

\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}]=\mathbf{\Sigma}
, such that
\|\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1/2}\mathbf{x}\|
is

M
-sub-gaussian, satisfies the following sub-exponential matrix moment bound:

\displaystyle\bigg\|\mathbb{E}\Big[\Big(\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1/2}\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1/2}-\mathbf{I}\Big)^{p}\Big]\bigg\|\leq\big(CM^{2}(p+\log M)\big)^{p-1}.

We can now use Lemma
5
in conjunction with the
sub-exponential matrix Bernstein inequality (Lemma
2
) by

Appendix E
Sketched least squares: Proof of Theorem
3

In this section, we prove Theorem
3
, giving a precise
estimate for the expected approximation error for sketched least
squares with LESS embeddings.
Recall that we are given an
N\times d
data matrix
\mathbf{A}
and an

N
-dimensional vector
\mathbf{b}
. We let
L(\mathbf{w})=\|\mathbf{A}\mathbf{w}-\mathbf{b}\|^{2}
and
\mathbf{w}^{*}=\argmin_{\mathbf{w}}L(\mathbf{w})
. Given an
n\times N
LESS embedding
matrix
\mathbf{S}
, we define the sketched least squares estimator as:

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathbf{w}}=\argmin_{\mathbf{w}\in\mathcal{R}^{d}}\|\mathbf{S}(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{w}-\mathbf{b})\|^{2},

where
\mathcal{R}=[-D,D]
is only used to protect against the corner
cases where
\mathbf{S}\mathbf{A}
is very ill-conditioned, which arise with a small but non-zero probability when
computing the exact expectation of the loss.
In the proof, we will use our reduction between a LESS embedding and a
sub-gaussian design to define an event that holds with a
high
1-\delta
probability (Lemma
7
). This event ensures certain
regularity conditions on the sketch, such as near-uniformity of the
leverage scores, and avoids the aforementioned corner cases. We then proceed to analyze the conditional expectation,
conditioned on the high-probability event. To obtain the final
unconditional expectation, we must choose
\delta
sufficiently small
to absorb the worst-case loss that might occur if the high-probability
event fails. This is where we incur a dependence on the range diameter
D
,
which is in general unavoidable when computing the expected loss, but it only affects the
logarithmic factors in the result.

Let us briefly comment on those logarithmic dependencies. If we let the LESS embedding have
d
non-zeros per row, as
recommended by
[
18
]
, then our reduction to a
sub-gaussian sketch (which is used in Lemma
7
) incurs a
logarithmic dependence in the Hanson-Wright constant, i.e.,

K=O(\sqrt{\log(nd/\delta)})
, and also, the lower bound on the sketch size
required for the claim becomes
n\geq O(d\log(d/\delta))
. This also
results in a polylogarithmic factor in the error
\epsilon
. We note
that these logarithmic terms can be avoided if we slightly
increase the density of the sketch, from
d
to
d\log(nd/\delta)

non-zeros per row. Then, the result can be established for

n\geq O(d)
.

Instead of computing the expectation of
L(\widehat{\mathbf{w}})
directly, we will
define an unbiased estimate of the expected loss via leave-one-out
cross-validation (CV):

\displaystyle L_{{\mathrm{CV}}}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\big(\mathbf{s}_{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{A}\widehat{\mathbf{w}}^{-i}-\mathbf{s}_{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{b}\big)^{2},\qquad\text{for}\quad\widehat{\mathbf{w}}^{-i}=\argmin_{\mathbf{w}\in\mathcal{R}^{d}}\|\mathbf{S}_{-i}(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{w}-\mathbf{b})\|^{2},

where
\mathbf{s}_{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}
​ is the
i
th row of
\mathbf{S}
and
\mathbf{S}_{-i}
denotes
\mathbf{S}
without the
i
th row. We use the following
standard “shortcut formula” for the CV estimate, which holds as
long as the scalar range
\mathcal{R}=[-D,D]
is large enough so that
each
\widehat{\mathbf{w}}^{-i}
is the same as the unconstrained least squares solution (we include
the proof in Appendix

Lemma 6

Suppose that
(\mathbf{A}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{S}_{-i}\mathbf{S}_{-i}\mathbf{A})^{-1}\preceq O(1)\cdot(\mathbf{A}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{A})^{-1}
for all
i
. Then, for sufficiently

, the sketched estimator is given by

\widehat{\mathbf{w}}=(\mathbf{A}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{S}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{A})^{-1}\mathbf{A}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{S}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{b}
, and the leave-one-out cross-validation loss can be computed as:

\displaystyle L_{{\mathrm{CV}}}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\bigg(\frac{\mathbf{s}_{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{A}\widehat{\mathbf{w}}-\mathbf{s}_{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{b}}{1-\ell_{i}(\mathbf{S}\mathbf{A})}\bigg)^{2},

where
\ell_{i}(\mathbf{S}\mathbf{A})=\mathbf{s}_{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{A}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{S}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{A})^{-1}\mathbf{A}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{s}_{i}

Crucially, the CV loss is an unbiased estimate of the expected loss
based on the sketch of size

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[L_{{\mathrm{CV}}}]=\mathbb{E}[L(\widehat{\mathbf{w}}^{-n})],

Thus, for the rest of the proof, we will actually estimate

\mathbb{E}[L(\widehat{\mathbf{w}}^{-n})]
rather than
\mathbb{E}[L(\widehat{\mathbf{w}})]
, but the difference
between the estimates is sufficiently small to be absorbed by

\epsilon

\frac{d}{n-d}\approx_{1+\epsilon}\frac{d}{n-d-1}

A key part of the analysis is showing that the leverage scores of
the sketched matrix are nearly uniform with high probability. This

Lemma 7

\displaystyle(\mathbf{A}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{S}_{-i}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{S}_{-i}\mathbf{A})^{-1}\preceq O(1)\cdot(\mathbf{A}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{A})^{-1}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mathbf{s}_{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{A}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{S}_{-i}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{S}_{-i}\mathbf{A})^{-1}\mathbf{A}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{s}_{i}\,\approx_{1+\epsilon}\frac{d}{n-d},

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}\ =\ \Big[(\mathbf{A}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{S}_{-i}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{S}_{-i}\mathbf{A})^{-1}\preceq O(1)\cdot(\mathbf{A}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{A})^{-1},\quad\mathbf{s}_{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{A}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{S}_{-i}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{S}_{-i}\mathbf{A})^{-1}\mathbf{A}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{s}_{i}\,\approx_{1+\epsilon}\frac{d}{n-d}\quad\forall_{i}\Big].

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{E}}[L_{{\mathrm{CV}}}]

\displaystyle\approx_{1+\epsilon^{\prime}}\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{E}}\bigg[\sum_{i=1}^{n}\bigg(\frac{\mathbf{s}_{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{A}\widehat{\mathbf{w}}-\mathbf{s}_{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{b}}{1-d/n}\bigg)^{2}\bigg]=\Big(\frac{n}{n-d}\Big)^{2}\cdot\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{E}}\big[\|\mathbf{S}(\mathbf{A}\widehat{\mathbf{w}}-\mathbf{b})\|^{2}\big].

Thus, it remains to approximate

\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{E}}\big[\min_{\mathbf{w}}\|\mathbf{S}(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{w}-\mathbf{b})\|^{2}\big]

of the similar form of the two quantities, in that they can be both
written as squared distance between a vector and a subspace. In the case of

L(\mathbf{w}^{*})
, it is the squared distance between
\mathbf{b}
and the column-span
of
\mathbf{A}
, and it can be written as:

\displaystyle L(\mathbf{w}^{*})=\mathbf{b}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{H})\mathbf{b},\qquad\text{for}\quad\mathbf{H}=\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{A}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{A})^{-1}\mathbf{A},

where
\mathbf{H}
is the so-called hat matrix, an orthogonal projection onto
the column-span of
\mathbf{A}
. In the sketched version, we get a squared
distance between
\mathbf{S}\mathbf{b}
and the column-span of
\mathbf{S}\mathbf{A}
, which can be
written similarly, using a sketched version of the hat matrix:

\displaystyle\min_{\mathbf{w}}\|\mathbf{S}(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{w}-\mathbf{b})\|^{2}=\mathbf{b}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{S}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}(\mathbf{I}-\widehat{\mathbf{H}})\mathbf{S}\mathbf{b},\qquad\text{for}\quad\widehat{\mathbf{H}}=\mathbf{S}\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{A}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{S}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{A})^{-1}\mathbf{A}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{S}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}.

Thus, both quantities are quadratic forms applied to the vector

\mathbf{b}
. We relate these two quadratic forms in expectation through the following result.

Lemma 8

The sketched hat matrix
\widehat{\mathbf{H}}
satisfies the following approximate
expectation formula:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{E}}\big[\mathbf{S}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}(\mathbf{I}-\widehat{\mathbf{H}})\mathbf{S}\big]\approx_{1+\epsilon^{\prime}}\Big(1-\frac{d}{n}\Big)\cdot(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{H}),\qquad\text{for}\quad\epsilon^{\prime}=\tilde{O}(\sqrt{d}/n).

Remark 5

Here,
\approx_{1+\epsilon}
refers to upper/lower bounds in terms of the
positive semidefinite ordering. This result extends an exact
expectation formula that holds when
\mathbf{S}
is a Gaussian embedding.

Putting everything together, we obtain the following approximation of
the expected CV loss conditioned on
\mathcal{E}
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{E}}[L_{\mathrm{CV}}]

\displaystyle\approx_{1+\epsilon^{\prime}}\Big(\frac{n}{n-d}\Big)^{2}\mathbf{b}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{E}}\big[\mathbf{S}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}(\mathbf{I}-\widehat{\mathbf{H}})\mathbf{S}\big]\mathbf{b}

\displaystyle\approx_{1+\epsilon^{\prime}}\Big(\frac{n}{n-d}\Big)^{2}\Big(1-\frac{d}{n}\Big)\cdot L(\mathbf{w}^{*})=\frac{n}{n-d}\cdot L(\mathbf{w}^{*}).

From this it follows that

\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{E}}[L_{{\mathrm{CV}}}]-L(\mathbf{w}^{*})\approx_{1+\epsilon}\frac{d}{n-d}\cdot L(\mathbf{w}^{*})\approx_{1+\epsilon}\frac{d}{n-d-1}\cdot L(\mathbf{w}^{*}),

for
\epsilon=\tilde{O}(1/\sqrt{d})
. Note that for this we have to
switch from
\epsilon^{\prime}=\tilde{O}(\sqrt{d}/n)
to
\epsilon=\tilde{O}(1/\sqrt{d})
, because
the right-hand side gets scaled by
d/n
. Finally, we convert from
conditional expectation as follows:

\displaystyle(1-\delta)\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{E}}[L_{{\mathrm{CV}}}]\leq\mathbb{E}[L_{{\mathrm{CV}}}]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{E}}[L_{{\mathrm{CV}}}]+\delta\sup_{\mathbf{S}}L_{{\mathrm{CV}}}

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{E}}[L_{{\mathrm{CV}}}]+\delta\cdot n\cdot\sup_{\mathbf{S}}\|\mathbf{s}_{n}\|^{2}\|\mathbf{A}\widehat{\mathbf{w}}^{-n}-\mathbf{b}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{E}}[L_{{\mathrm{CV}}}]+\delta\cdot{\mathrm{poly}}(N,D),

where in the last step, to avoid the corner cases where sketch
\mathbf{S}\mathbf{A}

is extremely ill-conditioned, we used that
\widehat{\mathbf{w}}
, as well as
\mathbf{A}
and

\mathbf{b}
, have entries in
[-D,D]
, and that, by our assumption on the
sparsifying distribution
p
, a LESS
embedding matrix
\mathbf{S}
has entries uniformly bounded by
O(\sqrt{Nk})
.
Choosing sufficiently small
\delta\leq\epsilon L(\mathbf{w}^{*})/{\mathrm{poly}}(N,D)
, we can obsorb that bound
into a logarithmic factor that appears in the upper bound on

\epsilon
(if we use
d
non-zeros per row in LESS). Alternatively, we can increase the
number of non-zeros per row in LESS to
d\log(nd/\delta)
, in which
case, we avoid the dependence of
\epsilon
on
D
.
We note that another simple way to impose a strict bound on the
worst-case loss is to use a small
\ell_{2}
regularizer when computing the
sketched least squares, ensuring
that the sketched problem is never ill-conditioned. Extending our
analysis to the regularized least squares setting is an interesting
direction for future work.

Proof of Lemma

Lemma 9

\mathbf{X}

Next, we proceed with the following decomposition of the trace

\mathbf{x}_{1},...,\mathbf{x}_{i}

\mathcal{E}

\displaystyle F(\mathbf{X})-\tilde{F}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{n}[\mathrm{tr}\mathbf{B}\mathbf{Q}\mid\mathcal{E}]-\mathbb{E}_{0}[\mathrm{tr}\mathbf{B}\mathbf{Q}\mid\mathcal{E}]=\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\mathbb{E}_{i}-\mathbb{E}_{i-1})[\mathrm{tr}\mathbf{B}\mathbf{Q}\mid\mathcal{E}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\mathbb{E}_{i}-\mathbb{E}_{i-1})[\mathrm{tr}\mathbf{B}(\mathbf{Q}-\mathbf{Q}_{-i})\mid\mathcal{E}]+(\mathbb{E}_{i}-\mathbb{E}_{i-1})[\mathrm{tr}\mathbf{B}\mathbf{Q}_{-i}\mid\mathcal{E}].

We proceed to bound each of the two terms. The latter can be
bounded straightforwardly. Note that one of
\mathcal{E}_{1}
or
\mathcal{E}_{2}
is
independent of
\mathbf{x}_{i}
. Without loss of generality suppose that it
is
\mathcal{E}_{1}
. Then, we have:

\displaystyle\big|(\mathbb{E}_{i}-\mathbb{E}_{i-1})[\mathrm{tr}\mathbf{B}\mathbf{Q}_{-i}\mid\mathcal{E}]\big|\leq\frac{\delta}{1-\delta}\cdot\big|(\mathbb{E}_{i}-\mathbb{E}_{i-1})[\mathrm{tr}\mathbf{B}\mathbf{Q}_{-i}\mid\mathcal{E}_{1}\wedge\neg\mathcal{E}_{2}]\big|\lesssim\delta\cdot\mathrm{tr}\,\mathbf{M}.

To bound the first term, we use the Sherman-Morrison rank-one
update formula (Lemma
4
), observing that:

\displaystyle|\mathrm{tr}\mathbf{B}(\mathbf{Q}-\mathbf{Q}_{-i})|=\frac{\frac{1}{n}\mathrm{tr}\,\mathbf{B}\mathbf{Q}_{-i}\mathbf{x}_{i}\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{Q}_{-i}}{1+\frac{1}{n}\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{Q}_{-i}\mathbf{x}_{i}}\leq\frac{1}{n}\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{Q}_{-i}\mathbf{B}\mathbf{Q}_{-i}\mathbf{x}_{i}=\frac{1}{n}\mathbf{z}_{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\tilde{\mathbf{M}}_{i}\mathbf{z}_{i},

where we let
\mathbf{z}_{i}=\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1/2}\mathbf{x}_{i}
and

\tilde{\mathbf{M}}_{i}=\mathbf{\Sigma}^{1/2}\mathbf{Q}_{-i}\mathbf{B}\mathbf{Q}_{-i}\mathbf{\Sigma}^{1/2}
. Now,
since
\mathbf{z}_{i}
satisfies Hanson-Wright inequality with constant
K
, it
also satisfies Euclidean concentration with constant
O(K^{2})
(see
Proposition
1
), and in
particular, letting
Z_{i}=(\mathbf{z}_{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\tilde{\mathbf{M}}_{i}\mathbf{z}_{i})^{1/2}
and conditioning on
\tilde{\mathbf{M}}_{i}
we have:

\|Z_{i}-\mathbb{E}[Z_{i}\mid\tilde{\mathbf{M}}_{i}]\|_{\psi_{2}}\lesssim K^{2}\|\tilde{\mathbf{M}}_{i}\|^{1/2}
. Thus,
defining
M_{i}=\mathbb{E}[Z_{i}\mid\tilde{\mathbf{M}}_{i}]\leq(\mathrm{tr}\tilde{\mathbf{M}}_{i})^{1/2}
, we have:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[|\mathbf{z}_{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\tilde{\mathbf{M}}_{i}\mathbf{z}_{i}|^{p}\mid\mathcal{E}\big]^{1/p}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{1-\delta}\mathbb{E}\big[|\mathbf{z}_{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\tilde{\mathbf{M}}_{i}\mathbf{z}_{i}|^{p}\mid\mathcal{E}_{1}\big]^{1/p}=\frac{1}{1-\delta}\mathbb{E}\big[(M_{i}-M_{i}+Z_{i})^{2p}\mid\mathcal{E}_{1}]^{1/p}

\displaystyle\lesssim\mathbb{E}\big[\mathbb{E}[M_{i}^{2p}\mid\tilde{\mathbf{M}}_{i}]\mid\mathcal{E}_{1}\big]^{1/p}+\mathbb{E}\big[\mathbb{E}[|Z_{i}-M_{i}|^{2p}\mid\tilde{\mathbf{M}}_{i}]\mid\mathcal{E}_{1}\big]^{1/p}

\displaystyle\lesssim\mathbb{E}\big[(\mathrm{tr}\tilde{\mathbf{M}}_{i})^{p}\mid\mathcal{E}_{1}\big]^{1/p}+\mathbb{E}\big[(\sqrt{p}K^{2}\|\tilde{\mathbf{M}}_{i}\|^{1/2})^{2p}\mid\mathcal{E}_{1}\big]^{1/p}

\displaystyle\lesssim\mathrm{tr}\mathbf{M}+pK^{4}\|\mathbf{M}\|.

where the last step follows because conditioned on
\mathcal{E}_{1}
we have

\mathrm{tr}\,\tilde{\mathbf{M}}_{i}\lesssim\mathrm{tr}\,\mathbf{M}
and
\|\tilde{\mathbf{M}}_{i}\|\lesssim\|\mathbf{M}\|
.
Using Burkholder’s inequality
[
29
, e.g., see]
for the martingale difference
sequence
X_{i}=(\mathbb{E}_{i}-\mathbb{E}_{i-1})[\mathrm{tr}\mathbf{B}\mathbf{Q}\mid\mathcal{E}]

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[|F(X)-\tilde{F}|^{p}\big]^{1/p}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\bigg[\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}\Big)^{p}\bigg]^{1/p}\leq Cp\cdot\Bigg(\mathbb{E}\bigg[\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}_{i-1}[X_{i}^{2}]\Big)^{p/2}\bigg]+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\big[X_{i}^{p}\big]\Bigg)^{1/p}

Applying Markov’s inequality with

Corollary 4

Suppose that
\mathbf{X}
consists of rows

\displaystyle\mathrm{Pr}

Lower bound: Proof of Theorem

Without loss of generality, assume that

is a multiple of

(otherwise we can pad the matrix with zeros), and consider matrix
\mathbf{A}

consisting of stacked
d\times d
identity matrices, scaled by
\sqrt{d/N}

so that
\mathbf{A}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{A}=\mathbf{I}

, in which case the
sketching matrix
\mathbf{S}
and the design matrix
\mathbf{Z}
both consist of
single random vectors
\mathbf{s}
and
\mathbf{z}

We will in fact show a strictly stronger claim that

\|\mathbf{z}\|_{\psi_{2}}\geq\min\big\{1,c\sqrt{\frac{d/k}{\log d}}\big\}

c>0
. This claim is stronger because,
by Proposition
1
, the Hanson-Wright constant

upper
bounds the sub-gaussian norm as follows:
\|\mathbf{z}\|_{\psi_{2}}=O(K)

\mathbf{z}

\|\mathbf{z}\|_{\psi_{2}}\geq 1

Let
\tilde{\mathbf{x}}=\mathbf{A}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{s}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{k}}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\frac{r_{i}}{\sqrt{p_{I_{i}}}}\mathbf{a}_{I_{i}}

\tilde{\mathbf{z}}=\mathbf{\Sigma}^{1/2}\mathbf{z}
be the random vector coupled with

\tilde{\mathbf{x}}
so that there is an event
\mathcal{E}
with probability at least

1/2
such that, conditioned on
\mathcal{E}

\tilde{\mathbf{z}}=\tilde{\mathbf{x}}

Next, in the joint probability space of
(\tilde{\mathbf{x}},\tilde{\mathbf{z}})

\mathcal{A}_{j}
, which holds when exactly one of the

\mathbf{a}_{I_{1}},...,\mathbf{a}_{I_{k}}
has a non-zero
j
th coordinate. Also, let

\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{A}_{1}\vee...\vee\mathcal{A}_{d}
. Let us start by showing the

. Each row of
\mathbf{A}
has only one non-zero

are the

with a non-zero in a

C

apprioriately, we can ensure that this probability is at least

2/3
. Thus, we showed that
\mathrm{Pr}\{\mathcal{A}\}\geq 2/3
.

Next, our goal is to show that one of the events
\mathcal{A}_{j}
has a
positive intersection with the event
\mathcal{E}
(i.e., when
\tilde{\mathbf{x}}
coincides
with
\tilde{\mathbf{z}}

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{d}\mathrm{Pr}\{\mathcal{A}_{j}\wedge\mathcal{E}\}\geq\mathrm{Pr}\{\mathcal{A}\wedge\mathcal{E}\}\geq\mathrm{Pr}\{\mathcal{A}\}-\mathrm{Pr}\{\neg\mathcal{E}\}\geq 2/3-1/2\geq 1/6,

denotes the

\tilde{\mathbf{x}}

\displaystyle\|\tilde{\mathbf{x}}\cdot\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{E}}\|_{\psi_{2}}\geq\big\||\tilde{x}_{j}|\cdot\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{E}}\big\|_{\psi_{2}}\geq\Big\|c\sqrt{d/k}\cdot\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{A}_{i}\wedge\mathcal{E}}\Big\|_{\psi_{2}}=\Omega\Big(\sqrt{\tfrac{d/k}{\log d}}\,\Big),

where we used a simple lower bound for the sub-gaussian norm of a
Bernoulli random variable. This immediately implies the same bound
for
\tilde{\mathbf{z}}
since

\|\tilde{\mathbf{z}}\|_{\psi_{2}}\geq\|\tilde{\mathbf{x}}\cdot\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{E}}\|_{\psi_{2}}
. We
can finally return to

. Using

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{z}\|_{\psi_{2}}

\displaystyle=\sup_{\mathbf{v}:\|\mathbf{v}\|=1}\|\mathbf{v}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\widetilde{\mathbf{\Sigma}}^{-1/2}\tilde{\mathbf{z}}\|_{\psi_{2}}=\sup_{\mathbf{v}:\|\mathbf{v}\|=1}\|\mathbf{v}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\widetilde{\mathbf{\Sigma}}^{-1/2}\|\bigg\|\frac{\mathbf{v}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\widetilde{\mathbf{\Sigma}}^{-1/2}}{\|\mathbf{v}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\widetilde{\mathbf{\Sigma}}^{-1/2}\|}\tilde{\mathbf{z}}\bigg\|_{\psi_{2}}

\displaystyle=\Omega(1)\sup_{\mathbf{v}:\|\mathbf{v}\|=1}\bigg\|\frac{\mathbf{v}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\widetilde{\mathbf{\Sigma}}^{-1/2}}{\|\mathbf{v}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\widetilde{\mathbf{\Sigma}}^{-1/2}\|}\tilde{\mathbf{z}}\bigg\|_{\psi_{2}}=\Omega\big(\|\tilde{\mathbf{z}}\|_{\psi_{2}}\big)=\Omega\Big(\sqrt{\tfrac{d/k}{\log d}}\,\Big).

, as well as
\widehat{\mathbf{w}}
, can be computed using the standard
formulas for the unconstrained least squares, without restricting to
\mathcal{R}^{d}=[-D,D]^{d}
.

Proof of the shortcut formula.

This is a standard
derivation, which we include for completeness. As shorthands, we let
\mathbf{X}=\mathbf{S}\mathbf{A}
,
\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{S}\mathbf{b}
, and
\ell_{i}=\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}(\mathbf{X}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{x}_{i}
. From
Sherman-Morrison (Lemma
4
), we have:

\displaystyle(\mathbf{X}_{-i}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{X}_{-i})^{-1}\mathbf{x}_{i}=\frac{(\mathbf{X}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{x}_{i}}{1-\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}(\mathbf{X}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{x}_{i}}=\frac{(\mathbf{X}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{x}_{i}}{1-\ell_{i}},

where
\mathbf{X}_{-i}
is
\mathbf{X}
without the
i
th row.
Next, we use that to obtain:

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathbf{w}}^{-i}

\displaystyle=(\mathbf{X}_{-i}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{X}_{-i})^{-1}\mathbf{X}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{y}-(\mathbf{X}_{-i}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{X}_{-i})^{-1}\mathbf{x}_{i}y_{i}

\displaystyle=\bigg((\mathbf{X}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{X})^{-1}+\frac{(\mathbf{X}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{x}_{i}\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}(\mathbf{X}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{X})^{-1}}{1-\ell_{i}}\bigg)\mathbf{X}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{y}-\frac{(\mathbf{X}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{x}_{i}y_{i}}{1-\ell_{i}}

\displaystyle=\widehat{\mathbf{w}}+\frac{(\mathbf{X}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{x}_{i}}{1-\ell_{i}}\Big(\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}(\mathbf{X}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{y}-y_{i}\Big)=\widehat{\mathbf{w}}+\frac{\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\widehat{\mathbf{w}}-y_{i}}{1-\ell_{i}}(\mathbf{X}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{x}_{i}.

Finally, we plug this into the
i
th component of the leave-one-out cross-validation estimate:

\displaystyle\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\widehat{\mathbf{w}}^{-i}-y_{i}=\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\widehat{\mathbf{w}}+\frac{\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\widehat{\mathbf{w}}-y_{i}}{1-\ell_{i}}\cdot\ell_{i}-y_{i}=\frac{\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\widehat{\mathbf{w}}-y_{i}}{1-\ell_{i}}.

Squaring and summing the components concludes the proof.
