Coarse-graining master equation for periodically driven systems
Ronja Hotz 1, Gernot Schaller 1,2

Abstract
We analyze Lindblad-Gorini-Kossakowski-Sudarshan-type generators for selected periodically driven open quantum systems. All these generators can be obtained by temporal coarse-graining procedures, and we compare different coarse-graining schemes. Similar as for undriven systems, we find that a dynamically adapted coarse-graining time, effectively yielding non-Markovian dynamics by interpolating through a series of different but invididually Markovian solutions, gives the best results among the different coarse-graining schemes, albeit at highest computational cost.



I
Introduction

While the propagation of undriven closed quantum systems via Schrödingers equation may be challenging for many degrees of freedom, it can be calculated with standard methods.
This changes when the Hamiltonian is subject to an external driving, when time-ordering becomes relevant.
Even for the simple case of periodic driving, where Floquet theory
Floquet 1883
;
Shirley 1965
applies, the exact calculation of the unitary propagator may be notoriously difficult
Grifoni and Hänggi 1998
;
Eckardt and Anisimovas 2015
;
Bukov et al. 2015
.
In addition, it is then often hidden that the time-dependent variation of external parameters requires that the system is coupled in some way to an outside world, which to be complete would actually require to model the system as open.
While open quantum systems in absence of driving are well-studied and interesting-subject on their own
Weiss 1993
;
Breuer and Petruccione 2002
;
Schlosshauer 2007
, the joint discussion of the effects of system-reservoir coupling and periodic driving is challenging.

However, with in particular periodic types of driving being experimentally quite feasible with current standards
Chu 1991
;
Rice and Zhao 2000
;
Aidelsburger et al. 2013
;
Atala et al. 2014
;
Jotzu et al. 2014
, the derivation of proper dissipators for periodically driven open systems is of great concern.
In principle, it is formally possible to perform the same approximations that lead to Lindblad-Gorini-Kossakowski-Sudarshan (LGKS) master equations
Lindblad 1976
;
Gorini et al. 1976
for an undriven system also for its driven version
Kohler et al. 1997
;
Grifoni and Hänggi 1998
;
Gelbwaser-Klimovsky et al. 2013
;
Szczygielski et al. 2013
;
Szczygielski 2014
.
In the interaction picture, the application of Born-, Markov-, and secular approximations generically leads to LGKS master equations that come in handy due to their stability and – despite the lack of thermalization
Shirai et al. 2015
;
Shirai et al. 2016
– their transparent thermodynamic interpretation
Cuetara et al. 2015
.
However, it has also frequently been noted that in particular the involved secular approximation may lead to strong artifacts
Restrepo et al. 2019
;
Luo et al. 2020
.
An intuitive explanation for this is that the level spacing in the extended (Floquet) space may become very small, which conflicts with the secular approximation
Hone et al. 2009
.
Therefore, traditional Floquet-LGKS approaches have been questioned in many works
Restrepo et al. 2019
;
Schnell et al. 2020
.

Already for undriven systems, it can be argued that the (non-LGKS) Redfield equation applied in the proper regime only leads to small violations of density matrix properties
in trade for a closer description of physical reality
Hartmann and Strunz 2020
.
As a practical benefit, improper density matrices can then be used as an indicator for leaving the region of validity of such perturbative schemes, whereas such a witness is missing for LGKS-based approaches.

. For dynamical coarse-graining (DCG) (purple) the coarse-graining time is chosen dynamically as the physical time. For

Another viewpoint is to preserve the formal LGKS form of the generator while improving in some ways the involved approximations
Whitney 2008
.
There are various routes to achieve this, e.g. formally restoring the LGKS form by discarding its subspace with negative eigenvalues
Kiršanskas et al. 2018
;
Ptaszyński and Esposito 2019
, by performing only a partial secular approximation
Farina and Giovannetti 2019
;
Cattaneo et al. 2020
, or by taking the effects of the reservoir on the system pointer basis into account
Schultz and von
Oppen 2009
;
Schultz 2010
;
Teretenkov 2021
;
Trushechkin 2021
.
While these approaches apply to undriven systems, we would like to focus on an approach that can be easily combined with periodic driving.

Such an approach that can also be well-motivated using a microscopic derivation is temporal coarse-graining
Vacchini 2000
;
Lidar et al. 2001
;
Schaller and Brandes 2008
;
Schaller et al. 2009
;
Benatti et al. 2009
;
Benatti et al. 2010
;
Royer 1996
;
Schaller et al. 2009
;
Majenz et al. 2013
;
Rivas 2017
;
Rivas 2019
;
Hartmann and Strunz 2020
;
Mozgunov and Lidar 2020
.
Intuitively, it can be understood as an approximate Markovian representation of the reduced exact solution over a chosen coarse-graining time interval.
The freedom in choosing the coarse-graining interval is exemplified in Fig.
1
and shall be the subject of this paper with a focus on periodically driven systems.
We begin with a brief introduction into the method in Sec.
II
and then compare various dissipators for three examples of driven open systems in Secs.
III
,
IV
, and
V
.

II
Coarse-graining methods for periodically driven open systems

The starting point of our considerations is a representation of the Hamiltonian of the full universe in the form

\displaystyle H(t)=H_{S}(t)+\sum_{\alpha}A_{\alpha}\otimes B_{\alpha}+H_{B}

(1)

with periodically driven system Hamiltonian
H_{S}(t+T)=H_{S}(t)
, system and bath coupling operators
A_{\alpha}
and
B_{\alpha}
, respectively, and reservoir Hamiltonian
H_{B}
.
We do not necessarily require the coupling operators to be individually hermitian, but the total interaction Hamiltonian is hermitian
H_{I}=\sum_{\alpha}A_{\alpha}\otimes B_{\alpha}=H_{I}^{\dagger}
.
Furthermore, we assume the system coupling operators
A_{\alpha}
to be normalized with respect to some norm, such that unit and strength of the interaction is carried by the bath coupling operators
B_{\alpha}
.

Since system and bath operators act on different Hilbert spaces, we assume from the beginning that
[H_{S},H_{B}]=0
and also
[A_{\alpha},B_{\beta}]=0
,
[A_{\alpha},H_{B}]=0
,
[B_{\alpha},H_{S}]=0
.
In an interaction picture with respect to
H_{0}=H_{S}(t)+H_{B}
(marked by bold symbols), we can approximate the time evolution operator
\mbox{\boldmath$U$}(t_{0}+\tau,t_{0})
.
The coarse-graining approach originates from the attempt to match the reduced evolution of the open quantum system with a time-local master equation

\displaystyle e^{\mbox{\boldmath${\cal L}_{\tau}$}\tau}\rho_{S}^{0}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle!}}{{=}}{\rm Tr_{B}}\left\{\mbox{\boldmath$U$}(t_{0}+\tau,t_{0})\rho_{S}^{0}\otimes\rho_{B}^{0}\mbox{\boldmath$U^{\dagger}$}(t_{0}+\tau,t_{0})\right\}\,.

(2)

Here, we have already put an index
{\cal L}_{\tau}
to the dissipation superoperator to highlight the fact that with a constant superoperator
{\cal L}
it will in general not be possible to capture the exact reduced dynamics for all times, which would require a Kraus representation
Kraus 1971
.
For weak system-reservoir interaction (or small propagation times
\tau
) we can use that
\mbox{\boldmath$U$}(t_{0}+\tau,t_{0})
is close to the identity and
\mbox{\boldmath${\cal L}$}_{\tau}
is small.
One can then expand the expressions on both sides and assuming furthermore that
{\rm Tr}\left\{B_{\alpha}\rho_{B}^{0}\right\}=0
, one arrives at a defining equation for the coarse-graining generator, see App.
A.1
for details.
Choosing for simplicity
t_{0}=0
it reads

{\cal L}_{\tau}
\rho_{S}

\displaystyle=-\mathrm{i}\Big[\frac{1}{2\mathrm{i}\tau}\int\limits^{\tau}_{0}dt_{1}dt_{2}\sum_{\alpha\bar{\alpha}}C_{\alpha\bar{\alpha}}(t_{1},t_{2})

(3)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\times{\rm sgn}(t_{1}-t_{2})\boldsymbol{A}_{\alpha}(t_{1})\boldsymbol{A}_{\bar{\alpha}}(t_{2}),\boldsymbol{\rho_{S}}\Big]

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{1}{\tau}\int\limits^{\tau}_{0}dt_{1}dt_{2}\sum_{\alpha\bar{\alpha}}C_{\alpha\bar{\alpha}}(t_{1},t_{2})

\displaystyle\qquad\times\Big[\boldsymbol{A}_{\bar{\alpha}}(t_{2})\boldsymbol{\rho_{S}}\boldsymbol{A}_{\alpha}(t_{1})-\frac{1}{2}\{\boldsymbol{A}_{\alpha}(t_{1})\boldsymbol{A}_{\bar{\alpha}}(t_{2}),\boldsymbol{\rho_{S}}\}\Big]\,,

with bath correlation functions
C_{\alpha\bar{\alpha}}(t_{1},t_{2})={\rm Tr_{B}}\{\boldsymbol{B_{\alpha}}(t_{1})\boldsymbol{B_{\bar{\alpha}}}(t_{2})\rho_{B}^{0}\}={\rm Tr}\left\{\mbox{\boldmath$B_{\alpha}(t_{1}-t_{2})$}B_{\beta}\rho_{B}^{0}\right\}
, where the last equality holds when
[H_{B},\rho_{B}^{0}]=0
.
Here, the interaction picture representation of the system coupling operators is formally given by

\mbox{\boldmath$A_{\alpha}$}(t)=U_{S}^{\dagger}(t)A_{\alpha}U_{S}(t)
with
U_{S}(t)=\mathcal{T}\left\{e^{-\mathrm{i}\int_{0}^{t}H_{S}(t^{\prime})dt^{\prime}}\right\}
and
\mathcal{T}
denoting the time ordering operator.
One can show that for any kind of driving and also for any fixed choice of
t_{0}
and
\tau
, the above coarse-graining dissipator
{\cal L}_{\tau}
is of LGKS form
Mozgunov and Lidar 2020
;
Schaller and Ablaßmayer 2020
.
This contrasts the above generator from other coarse-graining approaches that arise from a temporal averaging of Redfield equations, which require subsequent secular-type approximations to reach an LGKS form
Elouard et al. 2020
.

Thus, we can generally split the generator into a unitary part and a dissipative one

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\dot{\rho}_{S}}

\displaystyle=-\mathrm{i}\left[\mbox{\boldmath$H_{\rm LS}^{\tau}$},\boldsymbol{\rho_{S}}\right]+\mbox{\boldmath$\mathcal{D}^{\tau}$}\mbox{\boldmath$\rho_{S}$}\,,

(4)

which specifically for periodic driving have the single-integral expressions

H_{\rm LS}^{\tau}

\displaystyle=\int d\omega\sum_{\alpha\bar{\alpha}}\sigma_{\alpha\bar{\alpha}}(\omega)\frac{1}{2\mathrm{i}}\sum_{nn^{\prime}}\sum_{abcd}

\displaystyle\qquad\times f_{0}^{\tau}(\bar{E}_{a}-\bar{E_{b}}+n\Omega,\bar{E}_{c}-\bar{E}_{d}+n^{\prime}\Omega,\omega)

\displaystyle\qquad\times A_{\alpha,ab}^{n}A_{\bar{\alpha},dc}^{-n^{\prime}}L_{ab}L_{dc}\,,

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath$\mathcal{D}^{\tau}$}\boldsymbol{\rho_{S}}

\displaystyle=\int d\omega\sum_{\alpha\bar{\alpha}}\gamma_{\alpha\bar{\alpha}}(\omega)\sum_{nn^{\prime}}\sum_{abcd}

\displaystyle\qquad\times f_{0}^{\tau}(\bar{E}_{a}-\bar{E}_{b}+n\Omega,\bar{E}_{c}-\bar{E}_{d}+n^{\prime}\Omega,\omega)

\displaystyle\qquad\times A_{\alpha,ab}^{+n}A_{\bar{\alpha},dc}^{-n^{\prime}}

\displaystyle\qquad\times{\left[L_{dc}\boldsymbol{\rho_{S}}L_{ab}-\frac{1}{2}\left\{L_{ab}L_{dc},\boldsymbol{\rho_{S}}\right\}\right]}\,.

(5)

Here, the first term converges for large
\tau
to the LGKS Lambshift term, and the second term denotes the dissipative influence of the reservoir.
The functions
\sigma_{\alpha\bar{\alpha}}(\omega)
as well as
\gamma_{\alpha\bar{\alpha}}(\omega)
denote odd and even Fourier transforms of the reservoir correlation functions (
A.1
),
f_{0}^{\tau}(E_{a},E_{b},\omega)
is a nascent
\delta
-function, the
A_{\alpha,ab}^{n}
are Fourier components of the system coupling operator matrix elements, the
quasienergies
\bar{E}_{a}\in[-\Omega/2,+\Omega/2)
are chosen in the first Brillouin zone defined by the period of the driving
\Omega=2\pi/T
,
and the Lindblad operators
L_{ab}
generate transitions between Floquet Hamiltonian eigenstates, see App
A.1
for the precise definitions.

The different coarse-graining schemes sketched in Fig.
1
may lead to different LGKS-type dissipators.

For later reference, we coin the coarse-graining schemes
dynamical coarse-graining

Schaller and Brandes 2008
(DCG) with
t_{0}=0
and
\tau=t
and

period-coarse-graining
(PCG) with
t_{0}=0
and
\tau=T
.
Details for these schemes are found in Appendices
A.3
and
A.4
, respectively.
Furthermore, we remark that the limit of very large coarse-graining times
\tau\to\infty
allows for significant simplifications, see App.
A.5
, which however do not always coincide with the frequently-used Born-Markov-secular (BMS) Floquet master equation exposed in App.
A.6
and a further simplified variant thereof – the Born-Markov-ultrasecular (BMU) master equation – in App.
A.7
.

Our problem is defined by three physical timescales.
We will be concerned with driven two-level systems, where the natural internal time scale of the system dynamics is defined by their level splitting
\Delta E
via the relation
\tau_{\rm int}=2\pi/\Delta E
.
Then, we have the obvious timescale given by the period of the external driving
\tau_{\rm drv}=2\pi/\Omega=T
.
Finally, the reservoir timescales are reflected in the decay timescale of the reservoir correlation function
\tau_{\rm dec}=2\pi/\omega_{\rm c}
, where
\omega_{\rm c}
is a cutoff in the reservoir spectral density.
We are going to investigate how the technical timescale – the coarse-graining time
\tau
– can be optimally chosen in dependence of the physical ones.

In what follows, we will compare the solutions to various periodically driven problems that are based on these different coarse-graining approaches, i.e., for the DCG approach we evaluate Eq. (
II
) with
\tau=t
, for the PCG approach we use Eq. (
II
) with
\tau=T
, whereas for the BMS and BMU results we use Eqns. (
A.6
) and (
A.7
), respectively.
Furthermore, in our calculations we will for simplicity neglect the Hermitian correction term to the Hamiltonian
H_{\rm LS}^{\ldots}
throughout.
Whereas for the particular pure-dephasing model considered below this term has no effect anyways, in general it can only be neglected in the weak-coupling regime where
{\left|H_{\rm LS}^{\ldots}\right|}\ll{\left|H_{S}\right|}
.

III
Pure-dephasing models

By the term pure-dephasing models we summarize models where the interaction commutes with the system Hamiltonian at all times, i.e., system and reservoir cannot exchange energy.
The Hamiltonian of a general driven-system pure-dephasing model with a reservoir of bosonic oscillators is then given by:

\displaystyle H(t)

\displaystyle=H_{S}(t)+A\otimes\sum_{k}\left(h_{k}b_{k}+h_{k}^{*}b_{k}^{\dagger}\right)+\sum_{k}\omega_{k}b_{k}^{\dagger}b_{k}\,,

\displaystyle\left[H_{S}(t),A\right]=0\,.

(6)

The assumption of commuting coupling operator
A
and system Hamiltonian
H_{S}(t)
allows to solve the dynamics exactly, see App.
B
.

III.1
Exact solution

Specifically for a driven two level system with
H_{S}=\sigma^{z}\left[\frac{\Delta}{2}+\lambda\cos(\Omega t)\right]
with the single system coupling operator
A=\sigma^{z}
we obtain that the populations in the
\sigma^{z}
eigenbasis remain constant

\displaystyle\rho_{S,00}(t)=\rho_{S,00}^{0}\,,\qquad\rho_{S,11}(t)=\rho_{S,11}^{0}\,,

(7)

whereas the coherences decay oscillatorily according to

\displaystyle\rho_{S,10}(t)

\displaystyle=e^{+\mathrm{i}\left(\Delta\cdot t+2\frac{\lambda}{\Omega}\sin(\Omega t)\right)}

(8)

\displaystyle\qquad\times\exp\left\{-\frac{4}{\pi}\int\limits_{-\infty}^{\infty}d\omega\gamma(\omega)\frac{\sin^{2}\left(\frac{\omega t}{2}\right)}{\omega^{2}}\right\}\rho_{S,10}^{0}\,.

Here, the Fourier transform of the single reservoir correlation function is
\gamma(\omega)=\Gamma(\omega)[1+n_{B}(\omega)]
with spectral density
\Gamma(\omega)\equiv 2\pi\sum_{k}{\left|h_{k}\right|}^{2}\delta(\omega-\omega_{k})
(analytically continued via
\Gamma(-\omega)=-\Gamma(\omega)
to the complete real axis) and the Bose distribution is
n_{B}(\omega)=[e^{\beta\omega}-1]^{-1}
.
In absence of driving (
\lambda\to 0
), this falls back to the known pure-dephasing solution of the spin-boson model, see e.g. Ref.
Lidar et al. 2001
.
Additionally, we remark that one may also obtain the above result from the undriven model by a gauge transform.

III.2
Master equation solutions

We can now compare this exact solution with the various approximate approaches discussed before by dropping the indices as above, i.e., using
A_{1}=A=\sigma^{z}

and
\gamma_{11}(\omega)=\gamma(\omega)=\Gamma(\omega)[1+n_{B}(\omega)]
for coupling operator and Fourier transform of the reservoir correlation function.
Notably, the matrix elements of the coupling operators become rather trivial

A_{1,ab}^{n}=\delta_{n,0}\delta_{ab}\bra{\bar{a}}\sigma^{z}\ket{\bar{a}}
,
where
\ket{\bar{a}}
are Floquet Hamiltonian eigenstates.
To compute them, it suffices to realize that for this simple problem the system Floquet Hamiltonian is just
\bar{H}=\frac{\Delta}{2}\sigma^{z}
, such that its eigenstates are trivial.
All master equations capture the constant populations (
7
).

The coarse-graining solution for fixed coarse-graining time
\tau
predicts for the coherences a decay according to:

\displaystyle\rho_{cg,10}(t)

\displaystyle=e^{+\mathrm{i}\left(\Delta\cdot t+2\frac{\lambda}{\Omega}\sin(\Omega t)\right)}

(9)

\displaystyle\qquad\times\exp\left\{-\frac{4}{\pi}{\frac{t}{\tau}}\int\limits_{-\infty}^{\infty}\gamma(\omega)\frac{\sin^{2}(\frac{\omega{\tau}}{2})}{\omega^{2}}d\omega\right\}\rho^{0}_{10}\,,

where the PCG approach is recovered for
\tau=2\pi/\Omega
.

Clearly, for DCG (
\tau=t
), the above equation exactly reproduces the exact solution (
8
).

Furthermore, for this model the BMS and BMU solutions coincide:

\displaystyle\rho_{\rm BM,10}(t)=e^{+\mathrm{i}\left(\Delta\cdot t+2\frac{\lambda}{\Omega}\sin(\Omega t)\right)}e^{-2\gamma(0)t}\rho^{0}_{10}\,,

(10)

which can also be obtained by virtue of (
40
) from Eq. (
9
) in the limit
\tau\to\infty
.

Since pure-dephasing-type models are a very specific model class, we consider models that allow the exchange of energy between system and reservoir below.

IV
Circular driving

As a second model, we consider a periodically driven open two level system of the form

\displaystyle H(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{\Delta}{2}\sigma^{z}+P\sigma^{+}e^{-\mathrm{i}\Omega t}+P^{*}\sigma^{-}e^{+\mathrm{i}\Omega t}

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{k}(\sigma^{+}h_{k}b_{k}+\sigma^{-}h_{k}^{*}b_{k}^{\dagger})+\sum_{k}\omega_{k}b_{k}^{\dagger}b_{k}\,,

(11)

where
P
denotes a driving amplitude.
For
P=0
and a vacuum reservoir, the model is exactly solvable
Garraway 1997
;
Breuer and Petruccione 2002
, but we are here interested in the case of finite driving amplitudes but weak system-reservoir coupling.
We therefore interpret the DCG solution as benchmark for this model, see App.
A.2
.

Before treating the model with our standard approaches, we demonstrate that the model can be mapped to a different frame where the Hamiltonian becomes time-independent.
In this time-independent frame, a standard master equation can be derived that is equivalent to the Floquet (BMS) master equation in the original frame.

IV.1
Mapping to a time-independent frame

By employing the unitary gauge transform

\displaystyle V(t)=\exp\left[-\mathrm{i}\left(\frac{\Omega t}{2}\sigma^{z}+\Omega t\sum_{k}b_{k}^{\dagger}b_{k}\right)\right]

(12)

one can see from the observations

\displaystyle V^{\dagger}(t)\sigma^{z}V(t)

\displaystyle=\sigma^{z}\,,\qquad V^{\dagger}(t)\sigma^{\pm}V(t)=\sigma^{\pm}e^{\pm\mathrm{i}\Omega t}\,,

\displaystyle V^{\dagger}(t)b_{k}V(t)

\displaystyle=e^{-\mathrm{i}\Omega t}b_{k}\,,\qquad V^{\dagger}(t)b_{k}^{\dagger}V(t)=e^{+\mathrm{i}\Omega t}b_{k}^{\dagger}\,,

(13)

that the total Hamiltonian becomes time-independent under the transformation

\displaystyle V^{\dagger}(t)H(t)V(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{\Delta}{2}\sigma^{z}+P\sigma^{+}+P^{*}\sigma^{-}

(14)

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{k}(\sigma^{+}h_{k}b_{k}+\sigma^{-}h_{k}^{*}b_{k}^{\dagger})+\sum_{k}\omega_{k}b_{k}^{\dagger}b_{k}\,.

Accordingly, by transforming into the corresponding picture, the expectation value of an observable
O
can be written as

\displaystyle\left<O\right>={\rm Tr}\left\{OU(t)\rho_{0}U^{\dagger}(t)\right\}\equiv{\rm Tr}\left\{\tilde{O}(t)\tilde{U}(t)\rho_{0}\tilde{U}^{\dagger}(t)\right\}

(15)

with the effective time evolution operator
\tilde{U}(t)=V^{\dagger}(t)U(t)
and transformed observable

\tilde{O}(t)=V^{\dagger}(t)OV(t)
.
The effective time evolution operator evolves according to a time-independent picture

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\tilde{U}(t)

\displaystyle=-\mathrm{i}\tilde{H}\tilde{U}(t)\,,

(16)

\displaystyle\tilde{H}

\displaystyle=V^{\dagger}(t)H(t)V(t)+\mathrm{i}\dot{V}V

\displaystyle=\frac{\Delta-\Omega}{2}\sigma^{z}+P\sigma^{+}+P^{*}\sigma^{-}

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{k}(\sigma^{+}h_{k}b_{k}+\sigma^{-}h_{k}^{*}b_{k}^{\dagger})+\sum_{k}(\omega_{k}-\Omega)b_{k}^{\dagger}b_{k}\,,

such that we can write
\tilde{U}(t)=e^{-\mathrm{i}\tilde{H}t}
.

Therefore, under the gauge transform (
14
), the time evolution is just that of a time-independent open two-level system.
In this picture, we can derive the BMS master equation for an undriven system.
Formally, this master equation looks like Eq. (
A.6
) with keeping only the
n=0
term and instead of the Floquet Hamiltonian eigenstates and eigenvalues using the eigenstates and eigenvalues of

\tilde{H}_{S}=\frac{\Delta-\Omega}{2}\sigma^{z}+P\sigma^{+}+P^{*}\sigma^{-}
.

In the last line of the above equation, we can see that the reservoir single-particle energies are shifted, which eventually also affects the Kubo-Martin-Schwinger
Kubo 1957
;
Martin and Schwinger 1959
;
Kossakowski et al. 1977
relation obeyed by the reservoir correlation function.
Therefore, the resulting master equation does not thermalize even in the time-independent picture but reaches some nonequilibrium steady state, which – after transforming back to the original picture – exhibits a time-dependence with the period of the driving.
This becomes more evident in a simpler long-term analysis:
For non-degenerate eigenvalues of the effective system Hamiltonian
\tilde{H}_{S}
, the populations decouple from the coherences and the evolution is given by a simple rate equation:

\dot{\tilde{\rho}}_{S,aa}=\sum_{b}\gamma_{ab,ab}\tilde{\rho}_{S,bb}-\sum_{b}\gamma_{ba,ba}\tilde{\rho}_{S,aa}

with transition rates from energy level
b
to
a
given by

\gamma_{ab,ab}
.
The steady state solution is then given by

\displaystyle\bar{\tilde{\rho}}_{S}

\displaystyle=P_{-}\ket{-}\bra{-}+(1-P_{-})\ket{+}\bra{+}\,,

\displaystyle P_{-}

\displaystyle=\frac{\gamma_{-+,-+}}{\gamma_{-+,-+}+\gamma_{+-,+-}}\,,

(17)

where
\ket{-}
and
\ket{+}
denote the ground and excited state of
\tilde{H}_{S}
, respectively.
For our example the two transition rates read

\displaystyle\gamma_{-+,-+}

\displaystyle=\tilde{\gamma}_{12}(E_{+}-E_{-})|\bra{-}\sigma^{-}\ket{+}|^{2}

\displaystyle\qquad+\tilde{\gamma}_{21}(E_{+}-E_{-})|\bra{-}\sigma^{+}\ket{+}|^{2}\,,

\displaystyle\gamma_{+-,+-}

\displaystyle=\tilde{\gamma}_{12}(E_{-}-E_{+})|\bra{+}\sigma^{-}\ket{-}|^{2}

\displaystyle\qquad+\tilde{\gamma}_{21}(E_{-}-E_{+})|\bra{+}\sigma^{+}\ket{-}|^{2}\,,

(18)

with
\tilde{\gamma}_{12}(\omega)=\Theta(\omega+\Omega)\Gamma(\omega+\Omega)[1+n_{B}(\omega+\Omega)]

where for large times we can insert

We find numerically that the solutions derived in this way fully agree with the BMS solutions in the original frame (solid red curves in Figs.

This is not surprising as the gauge transformation that we used should not change the general outcome.
In addition, we checked that the simplified long-term dynamics agrees with the full time-dependent solution for large times.

V
Fast-Driving solution

As a last example, we consider systems where the driving breaks the pure-dephasing character of the system

\displaystyle H(t)

\displaystyle=H_{S}^{0}+\lambda\cos(\Omega t)C+A\otimes\sum_{k}(h_{k}b_{k}+h_{k}^{*}b_{k}^{\dagger})

\displaystyle\qquad+\sum_{k}\omega_{k}b_{k}^{\dagger}b_{k}\,,

(21)

with system Hamiltonian
H_{S}^{0}
and system coupling operator
A=A^{\dagger}
fulfilling only the constraint
[H_{S}^{0},A]=0
and system driving operator
C=C^{\dagger}
with in general
[H_{S}^{0},C]\neq 0
.
Under a naive rotating wave approximation (RWA), the contribution of the driving vanishes completely, and in case of a qubit the exact solution (
8
) from the previous section in absence of driving (
\lambda=0
) applies for all values of the system-reservoir coupling strength.
However, to go beyond the naive RWA, we transform into an interaction picture with respect to the driving

\displaystyle U_{1}(t)=e^{-\mathrm{i}\lambda/\Omega\sin(\Omega t)C}\,,

(22)

and in this picture (marked by a tilde e.g. via
\tilde{A}(t)=U_{1}^{\dagger}(t)AU_{1}(t)
) the transformed Hamiltonian assumes a time-dependent pure-dephasing form

\displaystyle\tilde{H}(t)

\displaystyle=\tilde{H}_{S}^{0}(t)+\tilde{A}(t)\otimes\sum_{k}(h_{k}b_{k}+h_{k}^{*}b_{k}^{\dagger})+\sum_{k}\omega_{k}b_{k}^{\dagger}b_{k}\,,

(23)

where we still have the equal-time commutation
\left[\tilde{H}_{S}^{0}(t),\tilde{A}(t)\right]=U_{1}^{\dagger}(t)\left[H_{S}^{0},A\right]U_{1}(t)=0
.
Nevertheless, we cannot apply the naive polaron treatment of App.
B
, since now also the coupling operator has picked up a periodic time-dependence.
Then, the polaron transform would become time-dependent as well, leading to an additional term in the effective Hamiltonian, such that the system-reservoir interaction would just be moved elsewhere.

However, depending on the problem one may have the situation that also the time averages of the operators

\displaystyle\bar{H}_{S}^{0}\equiv\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}\tilde{H}_{S}^{0}(t)dt\,,\qquad\bar{A}\equiv\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}\tilde{A}(t)dt

(24)

commute with each other
[\bar{H}_{S}^{0},\bar{A}]=0
, and in this case the Hamiltonian after an RWA in the interaction picture

\displaystyle\bar{H}_{\rm tot}=\bar{H}_{S}^{0}+\bar{A}\otimes\sum_{k}(h_{k}b_{k}+h_{k}^{*}b_{k}^{\dagger})+\sum_{k}\omega_{k}b_{k}^{\dagger}b_{k}

(25)

is of pure-dephasing type and can be solved with standard methods, see App.
C
for details.

The transformation (
22
) is of course not equivalent to the interaction picture representation.
However, under the RWA in this transformed frame, the time evolution operator of the system becomes

U_{S}(t)\approx U_{1}(t)e^{-\mathrm{i}\bar{H}_{S}^{0}t}
, such that we can identify approximations to kick operator
U_{\rm kick}(t)\approx U_{1}(t)
and system Floquet Hamiltonian
\bar{H}\approx\bar{H}_{S}^{0}
, respectively.

V.1
Fast driving benchmark

Specifically, for a two level system with

\displaystyle H_{S}^{0}=\frac{\Delta}{2}\sigma^{z}\,,\quad A=\sigma^{z}\,,\quad C=\sigma^{x},

(26)

we obtain with the Fourier decomposition

\displaystyle\tilde{A}(t)

\displaystyle=U_{1}^{\dagger}(t)\sigma^{z}U_{1}(t)

\displaystyle=\sigma^{z}\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}{\cal J}_{2n}\left(\frac{2\lambda}{\Omega}\right)e^{\mathrm{i}2n\Omega t}

VI
Conclusions

Our intention in this study was to find an improved time-independent Lindblad (Markovian
Breuer 2012
) master equation applicable to periodically driven systems.
For this, we analyzed driven qubit systems coupled to thermal reservoirs with coarse-graining approaches.
All methods used had the formal advantage of being in LGKS form, which unconditionally preserves all density matrix properties and leads to a stable numerical propagation.
The negative result is that the analyzed coarse-graining approach over one period of the driving (PCG) did not match the expectations, i.e., it was inferior to the DCG approach, even in the special case of pure dephasing.

On the positive side, we found that the BMS approach can yield quite reliable results in the long term limit provided the secular approximation is performed in a proper way.
The DCG approach performed at least as good as the BMS variant in the long-term limit and approaches the exact solution by construction in the short-term limit.
As the corresponding systematic expansion converges in the weak-coupling regime (see App.
A.2
), we consider it as the most accurate second-order perturbative solution among the schemes tested.
Its drawback is the computational cost, since for each time a suitable dissipator needs to be calculated numerically.
To avoid this, we note that by using spectral densities with a simpler polynomial structure, the involved integrals in (
II
) can be performed analytically.
The fact that it is not possible to capture the full dynamics faithfully with a single coarse-graining time could be taken as a hint that the restriction to LGKS dynamics is too severe.
Using a Redfield approach however may be highly unstable for driven systems even in the weak-coupling limit, such that one should rather aim at deriving Kraus representations from first principles.

A.1
General coarse-graining derivation

To evaluate the r.h.s. of the defining equation (

\mbox{\boldmath$U$}(t_{0},t_{0})=\mbox{\boldmath$1$}

(32)

where the nested integration bounds make the time-ordering explicit and the

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath$H$}_{I}(t,t_{0})

\displaystyle\qquad\otimes e^{+\mathrm{i}H_{B}(t-t_{0})}B_{\alpha}e^{-\mathrm{i}H_{B}(t-t_{0})}

Applied to the periodically-driven Schrödinger equation, Floquet theory predicts that the time evolution operator can be expressed as a product of a unitary and periodic kick operator (that has the period of the driving) and the exponential of a Floquet Hamiltonian

\displaystyle U_{S}(t)

\displaystyle D

\displaystyle\equiv{\left|\int d\omega g(\omega)f_{t_{0}}^{\tau}(\omega_{\alpha},\omega_{\beta},\omega)\right|}

\displaystyle\leq\int d\omega{\left|g(\omega)f_{t_{0}}^{\tau}(\omega_{\alpha},\omega_{\beta},\omega)\right|}

\displaystyle\leq\left[{\rm ess\;sup}_{\omega}{\left|g(\omega)\right|}\right]\int{\left|f_{t_{0}}^{\tau}(\omega_{\alpha},\omega_{\beta},\omega)\right|}d\omega

\displaystyle\leq\left[{\rm ess\;sup}_{\omega}{\left|g(\omega)\right|}\right]\sqrt{\int\frac{\tau}{2\pi}{\rm sinc}^{2}\left[(\omega_{\alpha}-\omega)\frac{\tau}{2}\right]d\omega}\times

\displaystyle\qquad\times\sqrt{\int\frac{\tau}{2\pi}{\rm sinc}^{2}\left[(\omega_{\beta}-\omega)\frac{\tau}{2}\right]d\omega}

\displaystyle={\rm ess\;sup}_{\omega}{\left|g(\omega)\right|}\,.

(41)

Here, in the second inequality we have used Hölders inequality for the
1
-norm and the
\infty
-norm, and in the third inequality we have first inserted (
39
) and then used Hölders inequality for the
2
-norm.
Thus, if the maximum of even and odd Fourier transforms of the correlation functions (that enter as
g(\omega)
) is small, i.e., in the weak-coupling limit, we may expect convergence of the series for all
\tau
.

For the bosonic reservoirs that we consider, a finite Fourier transform of the reservoir correlation function requires at least ohmic spectral densities.
If, for example, the Fourier transform of the reservoir correlation function is given by
\gamma(\omega)=\Gamma_{0}\omega e^{-{\left|\omega\right|}/\omega_{c}}[1+1/(e^{\beta\omega}-1)]
, we find at zero temperatures
\gamma(\omega)\leq\Gamma_{0}\omega_{\rm c}e^{-1}
and at high temperatures
\gamma(\omega)\leq\Gamma_{0}/\beta
.

A.3
Dynamical coarse-graining (DCG)

For dynamical coarse-graining, the time-dependent system density matrix is expressed as

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath$\rho_{S}$}(t)=\exp\left\{\mbox{\boldmath${\cal L}_{t}$}\cdot t\right\}\mbox{\boldmath$\rho_{S}$}(t_{0})\,.

(42)

This density matrix then formally evolves according to

\frac{d}{dt}\mbox{\boldmath$\rho_{S}$}=\left[\left(\frac{d}{dt}e^{\mbox{\boldmath${\cal L}_{t}$}\cdot t}\right)e^{-\mbox{\boldmath${\cal L}_{t}$}\cdot t}\right]\mbox{\boldmath$\rho_{S}$}(t)
, where the term in square brackets can be seen as a non-Markovian generator of the dynamical coarse-graining evolution
Rivas 2017
.
The full term in square brackets is not of LGKS form.
Nevertheless, the scheme preserves all density matrix properties, which follows as
{\cal L}_{t}
is of LGKS form for fixed
t
, such that the scheme (
42
) is an interpolation through physically valid density matrices.

A.4
Initial-period coarse-graining (PCG)

Coarse-graining only over the initial period (
t_{0}=0
and
\tau=T
), the PCG-coarse-graining generator is obtained from (
II
)
by inserting

\displaystyle f_{0}^{T}(\omega_{1},\omega_{1},\omega)

\displaystyle=\frac{T}{2\pi}e^{\mathrm{i}(\omega_{1}-\omega_{2})T/2}

(43)

\displaystyle\qquad\times{\rm sinc}\left[(\omega_{1}-\omega)\frac{T}{2}\right]{\rm sinc}\left[(\omega_{2}-\omega)\frac{T}{2}\right]\,.

The generator then still contains an integration over
\omega
, which – depending on the form of the spectral density – may need to be solved numerically.

A.5
Long-term coarse-graining

When
\tau\to\infty
, we get from Eq. (
40
) that the remaining integral in (
II
) can be fully resolved, leading to

\displaystyle{\bf H_{\rm LS}^{\infty}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\mathrm{i}}\sum_{\alpha\bar{\alpha}}\sum_{nn^{\prime}}\sum_{abcd}\sigma_{\alpha\bar{\alpha}}(\bar{E}_{a}-\bar{E}_{b}+n\Omega)

\displaystyle\qquad\times\delta_{\bar{E}_{a}-\bar{E}_{b}+n\Omega,\bar{E}_{c}-\bar{E}_{d}+n^{\prime}\Omega}A_{\alpha,ab}^{n}A_{\bar{\alpha},dc}^{-n^{\prime}}{L_{ab}L_{dc}}\,,

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}^{\infty}\boldsymbol{\rho_{S}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\alpha\bar{\alpha}}\sum_{nn^{\prime}}\sum_{abcd}\gamma_{\alpha\bar{\alpha}}(\bar{E}_{a}-\bar{E}_{b}+n\Omega)

\displaystyle\qquad\times\delta_{\bar{E}_{a}-\bar{E}_{b}+n\Omega,\bar{E}_{c}-\bar{E}_{d}+n^{\prime}\Omega}A_{\alpha,ab}^{+n}A_{\bar{\alpha},dc}^{-n^{\prime}}

\displaystyle\qquad\times{\left[L_{dc}\boldsymbol{\rho_{S}}L_{ab}-\frac{1}{2}\left\{L_{ab}L_{dc},\boldsymbol{\rho_{S}}\right\}\right]}\,.

(44)

Thus, the corresponding generator can be readily evaluated, and in particular the long-term results from App.
A.3
will coincide with the long-term results from the equation above.

A.6
Floquet-BMS master equation

In Eq. (
A.5
), the evaluation of the resonance condition resulting from the Kronecker-
\delta

\displaystyle\bar{E}_{a}-\bar{E}_{b}+n\Omega=\bar{E}_{c}-\bar{E}_{d}+n^{\prime}\Omega

(45)

may require some care.
The typical argument is that for fast driving, the above resonance can only be met if separately
n^{\prime}=n
and
\bar{E}_{a}-\bar{E}_{b}=\bar{E}_{c}-\bar{E}_{d}
.
However, the applicability of this argument critically depends on the Floquet spectrum.
If for a two-level system we by chance have Floquet energies well within the first Brillouin zone
\bar{E}_{a}\in\{-\Omega/4,+\Omega/4\}
, this generates energy differences
\bar{E}_{a}-\bar{E}_{b}\in\{-\Omega/2,0,+\Omega/2\}
.
In this case, the above resonance condition can also be met with
\bar{E}_{a}-\bar{E}_{b}=+\Omega/2
,
\bar{E}_{c}-\bar{E}_{d}=-\Omega/2
and
n^{\prime}=n+1
, demonstrating that in general, the long-term limit of periodically driven coarse-graining master equation need not coincide with the significantly simpler Floquet-BMS master equation that results from demanding the resonance separately (i.e., by setting
n^{\prime}=n
and
\bar{E}_{a}-\bar{E}_{b}=\bar{E}_{c}-\bar{E}_{d}
)

H_{\rm LS}^{\rm BMS}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\mathrm{i}}\sum_{\alpha\bar{\alpha}}\sum_{n}\sum_{abcd}\sigma_{\alpha\bar{\alpha}}(\bar{E}_{a}-\bar{E}_{b}+n\Omega)

\displaystyle\qquad\times\delta_{\bar{E}_{a}-\bar{E}_{b},\bar{E}_{c}-\bar{E}_{d}}A_{\alpha,ab}^{n}A_{\bar{\alpha},dc}^{-n}{L_{ab}L_{dc}}\,,

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}^{\rm BMS}\boldsymbol{\rho_{S}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\alpha\bar{\alpha}}\sum_{n}\sum_{abcd}\gamma_{\alpha\bar{\alpha}}(\bar{E}_{a}-\bar{E}_{b}+n\Omega)

\displaystyle\qquad\times\delta_{\bar{E}_{a}-\bar{E}_{b},\bar{E}_{c}-\bar{E}_{d}}A_{\alpha,ab}^{+n}A_{\bar{\alpha},dc}^{-n}

\displaystyle\qquad\times{\left[L_{dc}\boldsymbol{\rho_{S}}L_{ab}-\frac{1}{2}\left\{L_{ab}L_{dc},\boldsymbol{\rho_{S}}\right\}\right]}\,.

(46)

A.7
The ultra-secular limit (BMU)

Even when (
A.6
) is applicable, we note that the remaining Kronecker-
\delta
leaves many terms that are often neglected.
For example, the above resonance can always be trivially fulfilled with
\bar{E}_{a}=\bar{E}_{b}
and
\bar{E}_{c}=\bar{E}_{d}
, even for the undriven case.
For many models, one has that
\gamma_{\alpha\bar{\alpha}}(0)\to 0
, such that such terms would not contribute anyways for undriven systems, where
\Omega\to 0
.
But in general they will have to be kept.
For comparison we therefore also state the ultra-secular approximation (BMU), where only terms with
a=c
and
b=d
are kept

\displaystyle{\bf H_{\rm LS}^{\rm BMU}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\mathrm{i}}\sum_{\alpha\bar{\alpha}}\sum_{n}\sum_{ab}\sigma_{\alpha\bar{\alpha}}(\bar{E}_{a}-\bar{E}_{b}+n\Omega)

\displaystyle\quad\times A_{\alpha,ab}^{n}A_{\bar{\alpha},ba}^{-n}{L_{ab}L_{ba}}\,,

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}^{\rm BMU}\boldsymbol{\rho_{S}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\alpha\bar{\alpha}}\sum_{n}\sum_{ab}\gamma_{\alpha\bar{\alpha}}(\bar{E}_{a}-\bar{E}_{b}+n\Omega)A_{\alpha,ab}^{+n}A_{\bar{\alpha},ba}^{-n}

\displaystyle\quad\times\left[L_{ba}\boldsymbol{\rho_{S}}L_{ab}-\frac{1}{2}\left\{L_{ab}L_{ba},\boldsymbol{\rho_{S}}\right\}\right]\,.

(47)

Appendix B
Exact pure-dephasing solution

B.1
General solution

For a driven Hamiltonian of pure dephasing form (
III
), the exact dynamics can be calculated by using a polaron (or Lang-Firsov
Lang and Firsov 1963
) transform:

\displaystyle U_{\rm p}=e^{A\otimes\left(\frac{h_{k}}{\omega_{k}}b_{k}-\frac{h_{k}^{*}}{\omega_{k}}b_{k}^{\dagger}\right)}.

(48)

Applying this transformation to system and bath operators yields

\displaystyle U_{\rm p}^{\dagger}H_{S}(t)U_{\rm p}

\displaystyle=H_{S}(t)\,,\qquad U_{\rm p}^{\dagger}AU_{\rm p}=A\,,

(49)

\displaystyle U_{\rm p}^{\dagger}b_{k}U_{\rm p}

\displaystyle=b_{k}-\frac{h^{*}_{k}}{\omega_{k}}A\,,\qquad U_{\rm p}^{\dagger}b_{k}^{\dagger}U_{\rm p}=b_{k}^{\dagger}-\frac{h_{k}}{\omega_{k}}A\,,

which leads to decoupled system and bath Hamiltonians

\displaystyle U_{\rm p}^{\dagger}H(t)U_{\rm p}

\displaystyle=H_{S}(t)-\left(\sum_{k}\frac{|h_{k}|^{2}}{\omega_{k}}\right)A^{2}+\sum_{k}\omega_{k}b_{k}^{\dagger}b_{k}

\displaystyle\equiv\bar{H}_{S}(t)+\sum_{k}\omega_{k}b_{k}^{\dagger}b_{k},

(50)

with
\bar{H}_{S}(t)
denoting an effective system Hamiltonian. Hence, the time evolution operator in the polaron picture
\tilde{U}(t)
is given by the system and bath evolution separately

\displaystyle\tilde{U}(t)=\mathcal{T}\left\{e^{-\mathrm{i}\int_{0}^{t}\bar{H}_{S}(t^{\prime})dt^{\prime}}\right\}e^{-\mathrm{i}\sum_{k}\omega_{k}b_{k}^{\dagger}b_{k}t}\,,

(51)

where
\mathcal{T}
denotes the time-ordering operator of the system.
For a general unitary transformation
V(t)
into another frame via
\ket{\Psi(t)}=V(t)\ket{\tilde{\Psi}(t)}
, the time evolution operator in the original frame
U(t)
can be expressed in terms of the time evolution operator in the new frame
\tilde{U}(t)
as
U(t)=V(t)\tilde{U}(t)V^{\dagger}(0)
.
Thus, for the time-independent polaron transform this relation yields

\displaystyle U(t)=U_{\rm p}\tilde{U}(t)U_{\rm p}^{\dagger}.

(52)

From this, the expectation value of any system observable
O_{S}
,

\displaystyle\left<O_{S}\right>

\displaystyle={\rm Tr}\left\{(O_{S}\otimes\mbox{\boldmath$1$}_{B})\rho(t)\right\}

\displaystyle={\rm Tr}\left\{U^{\dagger}(t)(O_{S}\otimes\mbox{\boldmath$1$}_{B})U(t)\rho^{0}\right\},

(53)

can be calculated as:

\displaystyle\left<O_{S}\right>={\rm Tr}\left\{U_{\rm p}\tilde{U}^{\dagger}(t)U_{\rm p}^{\dagger}(O_{S}\otimes\mbox{\boldmath$1$}_{B})U_{\rm p}\tilde{U}(t)U_{\rm p}^{\dagger}\rho^{0}\right\}\,.

(54)

B.2
Two-level system

For the example of a two level system,

\displaystyle H_{S}(t)=\frac{\Delta}{2}\sigma^{z}{+\lambda\cos\Omega t}\,,\qquad A=\sigma^{z},

(55)

the populations
\rho_{S,00}(t)=\frac{1}{2}\langle\mathds{1}+\sigma^{z}\rangle
and
\rho_{S,11}(t)=\frac{1}{2}\langle\mathds{1}-\sigma^{z}\rangle
are constant as
\mathds{1}
and
\sigma^{z}
commute with
U_{\rm p}
and
\tilde{U}(t)
,
and we obtain (
7
) in the main text.

For the coherences
\rho_{01}=\left<\sigma^{-}\right>
and
\rho_{10}=\left<\sigma^{+}\right>
, the calculations are more involved. Using that

\displaystyle U_{\rm p}^{\dagger}\sigma^{\pm}U_{\rm p}=e^{\pm 2\sum_{k}\left(\frac{h_{k}^{*}}{\omega_{k}}b_{k}^{\dagger}-\frac{h_{k}}{\omega_{k}}b_{k}\right)}\sigma^{\pm},

(56)

and organising bath and system parts together, one gets

\displaystyle\left<\sigma^{\pm}\right>

\displaystyle={\rm Tr}\Big\{U_{\rm p}U_{B}^{\dagger}(t)e^{\pm 2\sum_{k}\left(\frac{h_{k}^{*}}{\omega_{k}}b_{k}^{\dagger}-\frac{h_{k}}{\omega_{k}}b_{k}\right)}U_{B}(t)U_{S}^{\dagger}(t)\sigma^{\pm}

\displaystyle\qquad\times U_{S}(t)U_{\rm p}^{\dagger}\rho^{0}\Big\},

(57)

with
U_{B}(t)=e^{-\mathrm{i}\sum_{k}\omega_{k}b_{k}^{\dagger}b_{k}t}
and
U_{S}(t)=e^{-\mathrm{i}\left(\frac{\Delta}{2}t+\frac{\lambda}{\Omega}\sin(\Omega t)\right)\sigma^{z}}
.
Now, one can use that

\displaystyle e^{\mathrm{i}\sum_{k}\omega_{k}b_{k}^{\dagger}b_{k}t}e^{\pm 2\sum_{k}\left(\frac{h_{k}^{*}}{\omega_{k}}b_{k}^{\dagger}-\frac{h_{k}}{\omega_{k}}b_{k}\right)}e^{-\mathrm{i}\sum_{k}\omega_{k}b_{k}^{\dagger}b_{k}t}

\displaystyle\qquad=e^{\pm 2\sum_{k}\left(\frac{h_{k}^{*}}{\omega_{k}}b_{k}^{\dagger}e^{\mathrm{i}\omega_{k}t}-\frac{h_{k}}{\omega_{k}}b_{k}e^{-\mathrm{i}\omega_{k}t}\right)}\,,

\displaystyle e^{\mathrm{i}x\sigma^{z}}\sigma^{\pm}e^{-\mathrm{i}x\sigma^{z}}=e^{\pm 2\mathrm{i}x}\sigma^{\pm}\,,

(58)

which leads by inserting the identity
U_{\rm p}^{\dagger}U_{\rm p}=\mathds{1}
to:

\displaystyle\left<\sigma^{\pm}\right>

\displaystyle=e^{\pm 2\mathrm{i}\left(\frac{\Delta}{2}t+\frac{\lambda}{\Omega}\sin(\Omega t)\right)}

(59)

\displaystyle\quad\times{\rm Tr}\left\{U_{\rm p}e^{\pm 2\sum_{k}\left(\frac{h_{k}^{*}}{\omega_{k}}b_{k}^{\dagger}e^{\mathrm{i}\omega_{k}t}-{\rm h.c.}\right)}U_{\rm p}^{\dagger}U_{\rm p}\sigma^{\pm}U_{\rm p}^{\dagger}\rho^{0}\right\}\,.

Next, the polaron transformation is applied to the system and bath parts separately:

\displaystyle U_{\rm p}e^{\pm 2\sum\limits_{k}\frac{h_{k}^{*}}{\omega_{k}}b_{k}^{\dagger}e^{\mathrm{i}\omega_{k}t}-{\rm h.c.}}U_{\rm p}^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=e^{\pm 2\sum\limits_{k}\frac{h_{k}^{*}}{\omega_{k}}\left(b_{k}^{\dagger}+\frac{h_{k}}{\omega_{k}}\sigma^{z}\right)e^{\mathrm{i}\omega_{k}t}-{\rm h.c.}}\,,

\displaystyle U_{\rm p}\sigma^{\pm}U_{\rm p}^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=e^{\mp 2\sum_{k}\left(\frac{h_{k}^{*}}{\omega_{k}}b_{k}^{\dagger}-\frac{h_{k}}{\omega_{k}}b_{k}\right)}\sigma^{\pm}.

(60)

Separating system and bath parts and inserting the initial condition
\rho^{0}=\rho^{0}_{S}\otimes\bar{\rho}_{B}
yields

\displaystyle\left<\sigma^{\pm}\right>

\displaystyle=e^{\pm 2\mathrm{i}\left(\frac{\Delta}{2}t+\frac{\lambda}{\Omega}\sin(\Omega t)\right)}

\displaystyle\qquad\times{\rm Tr}\left\{e^{\pm 4\mathrm{i}\sum_{k}\frac{|h_{k}|^{2}}{\omega_{k}^{2}}\sin(\omega_{k}t)\sigma^{z}}\sigma^{\pm}\rho_{S}^{0}\right\}B_{\pm}(t)\,,

\displaystyle B_{\pm}(t)

\displaystyle={\rm Tr}\Big\{e^{\pm 2\sum_{k}\left(\frac{h_{k}^{*}}{\omega_{k}}b_{k}^{\dagger}e^{\mathrm{i}\omega_{k}t}-\frac{h_{k}}{\omega_{k}}b_{k}e^{-\mathrm{i}\omega_{k}t}\right)}

\displaystyle\qquad\times e^{\mp 2\sum_{k}\left(\frac{h_{k}^{*}}{\omega_{k}}b_{k}^{\dagger}-\frac{h_{k}}{\omega_{k}}b_{k}\right)}\bar{\rho}_{B}\Big\}\,.

(61)

The trace over the bath parts
B_{\pm}(t)
gives:

\displaystyle B_{\pm}(t)

\displaystyle=\exp\left\{\mp 4\mathrm{i}\sum_{k}\frac{|h_{k}|^{2}}{\omega_{k}^{2}}\sin(\omega_{k}t)\right\}

(62)

\displaystyle\times\exp\left\{-4\sum_{k}\frac{|h_{k}|^{2}}{\omega_{k}^{2}}[1-\cos(\omega_{k}t)][1+2n_{B}(\omega_{k})]\right\}\,.

Here,
n_{B}(\omega_{k})=\frac{1}{e^{\beta\omega_{k}}-1}
denotes the Bose distribution. Plugging the expression for
B_{\pm}(t)
into Eq. (
61
) and using

\displaystyle{\rm Tr}\left\{e^{\pm 4\mathrm{i}\sum_{k}\frac{|h_{k}|^{2}}{\omega_{k}^{2}}\sin(\omega_{k}t)\sigma^{z}}\sigma^{\pm}\rho_{S}^{0}\right\}

\displaystyle=e^{\pm 4i\sum_{k}\frac{|h_{k}|^{2}}{\omega_{k}^{2}}\sin(\omega_{k}t)}

\displaystyle\qquad\times{\rm Tr}\left\{\sigma^{\pm}\rho_{S}^{0}\right\},

(63)

yields – after inserting the spectral coupling density
\Gamma(\omega)=2\pi\sum_{k}|h_{k}|^{2}\delta(\omega-\omega_{k})
–
for the expectation value of
\left<\sigma^{+}\right>=\rho_{10}
Eq. (
8
) in the main text.

Appendix C
Analytic fast-driving approximation

We consider the simplified form of Eq. (
V
) with
H_{S}^{0}=\frac{\Delta}{2}A
.
First, we move into an interaction picture with respect to the driving by performing the transformation
U_{1}(t)=e^{-\mathrm{i}\frac{\lambda}{\Omega}\sin(\Omega t)C}
,
which yields after applying the rotating wave approximation

\displaystyle\tilde{H}_{\rm RWA}

\displaystyle=\tilde{A}\left(\frac{\Delta}{2}+\sum_{k}(h_{k}b_{k}+h_{k}^{*}b_{k}^{\dagger})\right)+\sum_{k}\omega_{k}b_{k}^{\dagger}b_{k}\,,

\displaystyle\tilde{A}

\displaystyle=\frac{\Omega}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{\frac{2\pi}{\Omega}}U_{1}^{\dagger}(t)AU_{1}(t)dt\,.

(64)

This is the Hamiltonian of a simple pure dephasing system, see Eq. (
III
), with time independent system Hamiltonian.

Applying the polaron transform (
48
), system and bath part of the Hamiltonian can be decoupled and (
50
) becomes

\displaystyle H_{\rm p}

\displaystyle=U_{\rm p}^{\dagger}\tilde{H}_{\rm RWA}U_{\rm p}

\displaystyle=\frac{\Delta}{2}\tilde{A}-\sum_{k}\tfrac{|h_{k}|^{2}}{\omega_{k}}\tilde{A}^{2}+\sum_{k}h_{k}b_{k}^{\dagger}b_{k}\,,

(65)

which yields for the time evolution operator in this picture:

\displaystyle\tilde{U}(t)\approx e^{-\mathrm{i}\left(\frac{\Delta}{2}\tilde{A}-\sum_{k}\tfrac{|h_{k}|^{2}}{\omega_{k}}\tilde{A}^{2}\right)t}e^{-\mathrm{i}\sum_{k}h_{k}b_{k}^{\dagger}b_{k}t}.

(66)

In an anologous way as for the pure dephasing system, we can write the expectation value of an observable as:

\displaystyle\langle O_{S}\rangle={\rm Tr}\left\{U_{\rm p}\tilde{U}^{\dagger}(t)U_{\rm p}^{\dagger}U_{1}^{\dagger}(O_{S}\otimes\mbox{\boldmath$1$}_{B})U_{1}U_{\rm p}\tilde{U}(t)U_{\rm p}^{\dagger}\rho^{0}\right\}.

(67)

For a two level system Eq. (
26
), we get

\displaystyle\tilde{A}=\mathcal{J}_{0}\left(\tfrac{2\lambda}{\Omega}\right)\sigma^{z},

(68)

with
\mathcal{J}_{n}(x)
denoting the Bessel function of the first kind, which is defined as the solution of the differential equation
x^{2}\mathcal{J}_{n}^{\prime\prime}(x)+x\mathcal{J}_{n}^{\prime}(x)-(z^{2}+n^{2})\mathcal{J}_{n}(x)=0
, eventually leading to (
V.1
) in the main text.
