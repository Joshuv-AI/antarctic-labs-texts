Replicable Bandits for Digital Health Interventions
Kelly W. Zhanglabel=e1]kelly.zhang@imperial.ac.uk, Nowell Closserlabel=e2]nowellclosser@g.harvard.edu, Anna L. Trellalabel=e3]annatrella@g.harvard.edu, Susan A. Murphylabel=e4]samurphy@g.harvard.edu

Abstract
Adaptive treatment assignment algorithms, such as bandit algorithms, are increasingly used in digital health intervention clinical trials. Frequently the data collected from these trials is used to conduct causal inference and related data analyses to decide how to refine the intervention, and whether to roll-out the intervention more broadly. This work studies inference for estimands that depend on the adaptive algorithm itself; a simple example is the mean reward under the adaptive algorithm. Specifically, we investigate the replicability of statistical analyses concerning such estimands when using data from trials deploying adaptive treatment assignment algorithms. We demonstrate that many standard statistical estimators can be inconsistent and fail to be replicable across repetitions of the clinical trial, even as the sample size grows large. We show that this non-replicability is intimately related to properties of the adaptive algorithm itself. We introduce a formal definition of a ”replicable bandit algorithm” and prove that under such algorithms, a wide variety of common statistical estimators are guaranteed to be consistent and asymptotically normal. We present both theoretical results and simulation studies based on a mobile health oral health self-care intervention. Our findings underscore the importance of designing adaptive algorithms with replicability in mind, especially for settings like digital health where deployment decisions rely heavily on replicated evidence. We conclude by discussing open questions on the connections between algorithm design, statistical inference, and experimental replicability.

Introduction

Adaptive treatment assignment algorithms, e.g., bandit
[
50
]
and more general reinforcement learning (RL) algorithms
[
83
]
, are increasingly used in clinical trials, social science experiments, and by tech companies to personalize treatment decisions to individuals
[
24
,
80
,
87
,
86
,
54
,
66
,
3
,
95
,
57
]
.
These algorithms are adaptive because they learn during the course of the trial, i.e., they are updated periodically using accruing data collected during the trial.
After an adaptive algorithm is deployed, various statistical analyses are needed to answer questions such as: What was the average reward under the deployed algorithm? What was the effect of treatment on other outcomes of interest? What about in a particular state? Further, statistical analyses can be used to inform the design of the adaptive algorithm for the next implementation, e.g., design choices regarding the state space, action space, and/or choice of reward.

In this work, we investigate the replicability of statistical analyses for data from trials deploying adaptive treatment assignment algorithms.
We focus on digital health intervention clinical trials; replicability of clinical trials is particularly critical as these trials are intended to provide evidence regarding future implementation on the broader population of such individuals. We use the term replicability in the spirit of
Reproducibility and Replicability in Science

[
65
]
, where they say that replicability means “obtaining
consistent
results across studies aimed at answering the same scientific question, each of which has obtained its own data.” The definition of replicability in clinical trials refers to replications of the same study design yielding similar results with high probability. This typically involves:

•

Using the same methodology:
Identical procedures, inclusion/exclusion criteria, outcome measures, and data curation and analysis techniques are applied. This also means the same bandit
or reinforcement learning algorithm (with the same hyperparameters) in adaptive experiments.

•

New, independent sample from the same population:
Repetitions of the trial conducted on different samples of individuals from the same population.

•

Same or different researchers:
Replication can be done by the original team or independent researchers.

Replicability ensures that other scientists will reach similar conclusions, i.e., have similar post-trial data analysis results, if they implement the same adaptive algorithm with a different group of individuals sampled from the same population. Replicability is closely related to the concept of “Stability” in Vertical Data Science (Predictability, Computability, and Stability) from
Yu and Kumbier [101]
.

There are many notions of replicability discussed in the literature. In the reinforcement learning literature there are a variety of definitions of what it means for the
algorithm
or the policies learned by the algorithm to be replicable
[
43
,
18
,
46
,
20
]
. At a high level, algorithmic replicability involves repeated use of the algorithm on inputs, sampled from the same underlying distribution, leading to the same or similar outputs.
In contrast, we aim for “inferential” replicability. This is replicability of the statistical inferential methods across trial replications
[
29
]
. This work will begin to shed light on the ways that these two types of replicability are related.

The main focus of this work is on how to ensure the replicability of statistical analyses for digital health intervention experiments that utilize adaptive algorithms for treatment assignment. While several works have shown that valid statistical inference is possible, they generally assume idealized settings, e.g., each individual’s responsiveness to treatment does not change over time and/or the
post-trial inference uses a correctly specified
outcome model.
In this work, we discuss how basic notions of replicability of statistical analyses can fail when the true environment begins to deviate from these idealized settings. In health, the true environment is highly likely to differ from these idealized setting as the environment concerns complex human behaviors.

In particular, we show that on adaptively collected data, the replicability of many standard statistical estimators
is intimately related to the replicability of the policies formed by the adaptive algorithm used to collect the data (see Section
4
for the formal definition of replicable adaptive algorithms).
This is true specifically for estimators of quantities, like the expected value of a health outcome on average across individuals in the trial, which depends on the treatment assignments made by the adaptive algorithm.
We show that standard statistical estimators for such quantities can be inconsistent and fail to be replicable across repetitions of an experiment, if the policies formed by the adaptive algorithm are “non-replicable”. For example, we show in simple settings that common treatment effect estimators will be positive (strictly bounded above zero) for a constant fraction of trial replications and negative (strictly bounded below zero) for another constant fraction of replications; moreover, this is the case even as the number of individuals in each replicated trial goes to infinity.
Furthermore, we show that many standard bandit algorithms, like
\epsilon
-greedy and Thompson sampling
[
78
]
, can fail to satisfy such replicability requirements in these very simple settings.

This work underscores the importance of ensuring inferential replicability when using adaptive algorithms. Inferential replicability is critical for the wider adoption and application of these algorithms in settings like digital health and experimental design. Furthermore, we argue that researchers in the area of adaptive experimentation must prioritize the development of algorithms and methodologies that ensure inferential replicability.

Generally the above works assume that the environment is stationary and that outcome models are correctly specified.
In this paper, we investigate inference on adaptively collected data in the digital health setting, where these assumptions may fail to hold, specifically under nonstationary environments and misspecified outcome models.
See the next section for a discussion of why misspecified outcome models are common in digital health. Classically, estimators formed by minimizing a loss function based on a misspecified model will be consistent none-the-less for the minimizers of the loss function (e.g., negative log likelihood,

In this work, we show that on adaptively collected data, under these types of deviations in the environment (non-stationarity and outcome model misspecification), standard estimators can be
inconsistent
for the parameters that minimize the associated loss function. This is a very severe consequence of using adaptive algorithms, which has significant consequences for inferential replicability.

Another line of work uses randomization-based methods for inference on adaptively collected data
[
63
,
26
]
.
Freidling et al. [26]
only consider inference for finite populations, which is not the focus of this work.
Nair and Janson [63]
provide a variety of methods (e.g. conditional independence testing in Markov decision processes, prediction intervals) and the one that is closest to our work considers inference for a fixed treatment effect in a contextual bandit setting. This differs from our work in that (i) they do not accommodate longitudinal data, (ii) their estimand cannot depend on the data collection algorithm (e.g. cannot be used to infer the average reward under the algorithm), and (iii) their method only applies to location and scale family outcomes (e.g. does not work for binary outcomes).

Finally, there are works that formalize the replicability of a decision-making rule or algorithm
[
43
,
18
,
46
,
20
,
69
]
. Many of these works build on the framework introduced in
Impagliazzo et al. [39]
, which formalizes what it means for a randomized algorithm to be replicable (these works are discussed further in Section
4.2
).
In this work, we focus on the replicability of statistical analyses, rather than on ensuring replicability of the adaptive algorithm; however, we show that the replicability of post-trial statistical analyses is often closely related to the replicability of the adaptive data collection algorithm (see Sections
3
and
4

Common characteristics of digital health intervention trials

The motivation for this work is the increasing use of adaptive algorithms in digital intervention experiments. These interventions are designed to encourage individuals to engage in healthy behaviors. These interventions focus on a variety of health areas—including physical activity

In digital health interventions, the adaptive algorithm itself is often considered a component of the entire intervention package. Thus the effectiveness of the intervention package will depend on the quality of the algorithm and the decisions it makes.

Example

(Oralytics)
.

In this work, we use the series of Oralytics clinical trials as a running example
[

,

,

. Oralytics is a dental health intervention designed to augment standard dental care by providing additional support over a 10-week period. In the Oralytics trial, individuals are given a Bluetooth-enabled toothbrush and asked to download the Oralytics mobile app. During the the trial, individuals may receive engagement messages encouraging toothbrushing via the app before their usual morning and evening brushing windows (i.e., up to twice daily). An adaptive decision-making algorithm (e.g., a contextual bandit) is used to optimize (personalize) whether or not to deliver an engagement message. Individual’s toothbrushing behaviors are automatically detected with the Bluetooth-enabled toothbrush.

Oralytics involves a series of clinical trials,
where each trial uses a contextual bandit algorithm. Between trials, the contextual bandit algorithm is redeveloped
[
86
,
87
,
62
,
88
]
.
At this time, the Oralytics algorithm is being revised for deployment in a two-arm trial for late spring 2025. In this two-arm trial, individuals will be randomized between the standard of care versus standard of care augmented with the digital intervention delivered using the revised contextual bandit algorithm. The primary outcome in this trial will be the difference in measures of overall dental health between the two groups at the end of the trial period. Further Oralytics digital intervention trials are in the planning stages.

We now highlight some common characteristics of digital health intervention trials that use adaptive algorithms (including Oralytics).

1.

Multiple decision-times over time per individual:
Digital health interventions involve making multiple decisions on the same individual over time. For example, treatment decisions could be whether or not to deliver engagement messages to encourage healthy behavior, as is the case in Oralytics. Typically the number of decision times per individual are fixed and particular to the domain, e.g., twice per day over a 70-day period to improve brushing behaviors.

2.

Data-sharing bandit algorithms:
Digital intervention settings often have low signal relative to the amount of noise in outcomes. This is because treatments are delivered to individuals as they go about their everyday lives, as opposed to in a clinic. Moreover, clinical trials generally only involve a limited number of individuals.
Thus, in the digital health setting developers of adaptive algorithms must carefully trade-off bias and variance due to the high variance in outcomes typical in digital health. As a result, contextual bandit algorithms (simple RL algorithms) are most often used
[
54
,
70
,
24
,
59
,
7
,
55
,
100
,
51
,
62
]
.

To further trade-off bias and variance, it is also common for bandit algorithms to combine or “pool” data across heterogeneous individuals in the trial to improve learning, i.e., the algorithm may adapt the treatment decisions for one individual based on previous treatment outcomes from others
[
100
,
24
,
70
,
62
]
. These data-sharing or pooling algorithms reduce noise and potentially learn faster.

3.

The primary data analysis is prespecified in clinical trials:
All National Institute of Health (NIH) funded clinical trials, including the post-trial statistical analyses, are required to be preregistered. This means that the analyses that will be performed cannot be changed without jeopardizing the validity of the trial.

In this work, we consider digital health settings in which adaptive treatment assignment algorithms pool data over individuals to learn. Further, we make the simplifying assumption that individuals are all recruited into the trial all at once (which is common in trials that recruit on the internet). In general, digital health intervention trials typically recruit individuals into the trial incrementally or in batches over time. Though we do not explicitly handle incremental recruitment in this paper, we conjecture that the key ideas below will generalize to that setting.

Post-trial analyses

2.3.1
Potential outcomes.

To discuss the post-trial statistical analyses (including causal analyses), we use potential outcome notation. Note that the potential outcomes model we consider for the post-trial analyses does not necessarily match the reward model used by the adaptive treatment assignment algorithm.
The post-trial statistical analyses needs to be interpretable, replicable, and valid even if the the adaptive algorithm makes simplifying modeling assumptions and its reward model is misspecified. Recall from Section
2.1
that simplified contextual bandit algorithms are commonly used in order to reduce variance, so it is not surprising that the algorithm’s model may be misspecified.

Let

\mathcal{A}^{t}
denotes the

]

X_{i,T}(a_{1:T-1}),Y_{i,T}(a_{1:T}):a_{1:T}\in\{0,1\}^{T}\big\}.

(2)

2.3.2
Estimands under population-deployed policies.

When bandit algorithms pool or combine data across individuals to learn, the policies
\{\pi_{t}(\,\cdot\,;\hat{\beta}_{t-1}^{(n)})\}_{t=1}^{T}
that the bandit algorithm learns depend on
n
, the number of individuals in the trial (as seen in display (
1
) and Figure
1
). This means that quantities like the expected average reward per individual (averaged over time)
under a bandit policy can also depend on
n
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}^{(n)}\left[\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}R(Y_{i,t})\right].

Above, we use
\mathbb{E}^{(n)}
to denote expectations under the deployed bandit algorithm; the expectation averages over the distribution of the potential outcomes of the
n
individuals, the stochasticity in the bandit algorithm itself, and the treatment decisions the algorithm makes. Formally, the expectation
\mathbb{E}^{(n)}
is indexed by
n
, the number of individuals in the trial because
the observed outcomes
Y_{i,t}
depend on the distribution of the treatment selection policies
\{\hat{\pi}_{t}^{(n)}\}_{t=1}^{T}

formed by the bandit algorithm, which can change with the number of individuals in the trial
.

The dependence of quantities like the expected reward on the number of individuals in the trial requires careful thought about estimands. In this work, we consider estimands under
population-deployed policies
: estimands consistent with deployment of the intervention package in the population. Indeed, the goal of many digital health trials using adaptive algorithms is to evaluate the performance of the intervention package (of which the contextual bandit algorithm is one component) if deployed on the wider population. For this reason, we are particularly interested in statistical inference under the policy that would be learned under the limit as the number of individuals in the trial grows (
n\to\infty
). For example, we might consider the following estimand, which is the expected reward:

\displaystyle\theta^{*}=\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{E}^{(n)}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}R(Y_{i,t})\right].

(4)

The above quantity can be interpreted as the expected reward if the bandit algorithm was deployed on the entire population of interest. This type of estimand is relevant for the two-arm trial phase of Oralytics (see Example in Section
Example
). We provide formal conditions under which the limit in display (
4
) exists, later in Proposition
1
.

2.3.3
Replicable post-trial analyses.

After the trial concludes, we are interested in performing statistical analyses using the dataset
\mathcal{H}_{1:n,T}
collected by the adaptive algorithm. We consider two properties that help ensure replicable statistical analyses; the first directly concerns the replicability of an estimator’s value across trial replications, while the second, though distinct from replicability itself, concerns being able to characterize the variability of that estimator across hypothetical replications.

After the trial concludes, we will form an estimator
\hat{\theta}^{(n)}\triangleq f(\mathcal{H}_{1:n,T})
using some real-valued function
f
. For example, to estimate (
4
), we could use
f(\mathcal{H}_{1:n,T})=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}R(Y_{i,t})
. We say this estimator is replicable if its value is “close” with high probability across independent replications of the clinical trial. Moreover, as the number of individuals in the trial
n
grows, the estimates across replications must get closer. We formalize this in Definition
1
below.

Definition 1

(Replicable estimator)
.

Let
\mathcal{H}_{1:n,T}
and
\tilde{\mathcal{H}}_{1:n,T}
be histories associated with independent replications of a trial. Let
\hat{\theta}^{(n)}\triangleq f(\mathcal{H}_{1:n,T})
and
\tilde{\theta}^{(n)}\triangleq f(\tilde{\mathcal{H}}_{1:n,T})
for a real-valued function
f
. We say
f
is a replicable estimator if for each
\epsilon>0
, as
n\to\infty
,

\displaystyle\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}}\mathbb{P}_{P}^{(n)}\big(\big|\hat{\theta}^{(n)}-\tilde{\theta}^{(n)}\big|>\epsilon\big)\to 0.

In our definition above, we take the supremum over the class of outcome distributions
P\in\mathcal{P}
. The use of the supremum in the definition means that, replicability as defined here, holds
uniformly
across a range of possible data-generating distributions, including the worst cases.

The second property that we would like for our post-trial statistical analyses is that we are able to use
\mathcal{H}_{1:n,T}
to form a consistent estimate for the variance of our estimator
\hat{\theta}^{(n)}
. Having an accurate estimate of the variance ensures that using data from our
single
trial run,
\mathcal{H}_{1:n,T}
, we are able to characterize the variability of
\hat{\theta}^{(n)}
across many hypothetical replications of the trial. Accurate variance estimation is typically required for using asymptotic approximations (e.g. asymptotic normal approximations) to form confidence intervals for estimands of interest. In Definition
2
below, we formally define what we mean by consistently estimating the variance of
\hat{\theta}^{(n)}
.

Definition 2

(Consistent variance estimator)
.

Let
\mathcal{H}_{1:n,T}
be the data collected in an adaptive trial and let
\hat{\theta}^{(n)}\triangleq f(\mathcal{H}_{1:n,T})
be an estimator defined by a real-valued function
f
. We say that
v(\mathcal{H}_{1:n,T})
is a consistent estimator for the variance of
\hat{\theta}^{(n)}
, i.e.,
\textnormal{Var}(\hat{\theta}^{(n)})
, if for any
\epsilon>0
, as
n\to\infty
,

\displaystyle\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}}\mathbb{P}_{P}^{(n)}\bigg(\bigg|\frac{\textnormal{Var}(\hat{\theta}^{(n)})}{v(\mathcal{H}_{1:n,T})}-1\bigg|>\epsilon\bigg)\to 0.

In our definition above, we ensure that the ratio of the variance estimate and the true variance is close to
1
.
Additionally, just as in Definition
1
, we take a supremum over the class of outcome distributions
P\in\mathcal{P}
, which ensures our variance estimator is consistent even for worst-case possible distributions.

3
Two Examples of Non-Replicability

This section aims to demonstrate the need to think carefully about replicability when using bandit algorithms in digital health. We consider two examples in which we attempt to replicate statistical analyses when using common bandit algorithms in digital health trials. In these examples, the estimator
averaged across replications
converges to the desired estimand. However, in any particular replication, the estimator is
inconsistent
and does not converge to the desired estimand as the number of individuals
n\to\infty
. These examples are disturbing since each digital health scientist only has access to their trial and not a population of hypothetical replications.

The first example considers estimation of the mean reward based on data collected by a bandit using a misspecified reward model. In particular, the true conditional mean of the reward is changing with time (non-stationary), but the bandit’s reward model is stationary.
In the second example, both the reward model of the bandit algorithm used to make adaptive treatment assignments during the trial, as well as the outcome model used in the statistical analysis after the trial, are misspecified.
For simplicity and clarity, the examples consider a
T=2
setting. These simplistic examples capture the main problems that one may encounter in real digital health intervention trials (we examine settings with
T>2
in simulations in Section
6
).

3.1
Mean reward in a misspecified non-stationary environment

In this example, we are interested in estimating the average reward under the bandit algorithm when deployed on the population (under the
population-deployed policy
from Section
2.3.2
)
as described in (
4
):

\displaystyle\theta^{*}=\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{E}^{(n)}\left[\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}R(Y_{i,t})\right].

(5)

It is assumed for now that the above limit is well-defined; we prove this for the setting we consider below (see Proposition
3.1
).

A natural estimator is the empirical average reward, i.e.,

\displaystyle\hat{\theta}^{(n)}\triangleq\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}R(Y_{i,t}).

(6)

Next we describe a simple environment, with
T=2
and with a non-stationary mean reward in which
\hat{\theta}^{(n)}
does not converge in probability to
\theta^{*}
and fails to be replicable in the sense of Definition

1
. We also show empirically that the sample variance
\hat{s}^{2}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\big(\frac{1}{T}\sum_{i=1}^{T}R(Y_{i,t})-\hat{\theta}^{(n)}\big)^{2}
fails to be an accurate estimate for variance of
\hat{\theta}^{(n)}
across trial replications.

3.1.1
Nonstationary data generating environment.

It is well known that in health settings the impact of treatment may change over time
[
59
,
82
]
. For example, individuals may initially be more responsive earlier in the trial, but become increasingly disengaged with the intervention over time
[
2
,
93
,
68
]
.
In this example, the underlying environment is non-stationary, but the bandit algorithm’s model of the environment is stationary, and thus misspecified.

Consider a trial with just
T=2
decision times. Actions are binary, so
\mathcal{A}=\{0,1\}
. Suppose the rewards are generated as follows:

\displaystyle R\big(Y_{i,t}\big)=\mu_{0}+\Delta_{t}A_{i,t}+\epsilon_{i,t},

where
\epsilon_{i,t}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
i.i.d. across
i
and
t
.

In the following,
\mu_{0}=0
and to mimic disengagement with the intervention over time, there is a decreasing trend in the treatment effect
\Delta_{t}
:
\Delta_{1}=0
and
\Delta_{2}=-0.25
.

3.1.2
Sample mean is non-replicability under common bandit algorithms.

We consider two simple, stationary multi-armed
bandit algorithms (there are no context features): (i)
\epsilon
-greedy, and (ii) Thompson sampling with a conjugate Gaussian-Gaussian model. We choose these algorithms not because the conclusions are limited to these two algorithms, but rather because these algorithms are often used in practice
[
62
,
24
,
54
,
55
]
.
In both the (i)
\epsilon
-greedy and (ii) Thompson sampling examples, the
\{A_{i,1}\}_{i=1}^{n}
at time
t=1
are i.i.d. Bernoulli (
0.5
).

Example

(
\epsilon
-greedy multi-armed bandit)
.

Given
\epsilon\in(0,0.5)
, the
\epsilon
-greedy bandit algorithm selects treatment at time
t=2
with probability:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\big(A_{i,t}=1\mid\hat{\beta}_{t-1}^{(n)}\big)=\begin{cases}1-\epsilon&\textnormal{if}~\hat{\beta}_{t-1}^{(n)}>0\\
\epsilon&\textnormal{otherwise}\end{cases},

for
\hat{\beta}_{t-1}^{(n)}=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{t^{\prime}=1}^{t-1}A_{i,t^{\prime}}R(Y_{i,t^{\prime}})}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{t^{\prime}=1}^{t-1}A_{i,t^{\prime}}}-\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{t^{\prime}=1}^{t-1}(1-A_{i,t^{\prime}})R(Y_{i,t^{\prime}})}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{t^{\prime}=1}^{t-1}(1-A_{i,t^{\prime}})}
.

Example

(Thompson sampling multi-armed bandit)
.

The Thompson sampling bandit algorithm
[
78
]
forms a Bayesian model of the rewards:

\displaystyle R(Y_{i,t})=\mu_{a}+\epsilon_{i,t},

with prior
\mu_{a}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
independently for each
a\in\{0,1\}
and error,
\epsilon_{i,t}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
i.i.d. across
i
and
t
. The algorithm selects treatment using:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\big(A_{i,t}=1\mid\mathcal{H}_{1:n,t-1}\big)=\mathbb{P}\big(\mu_{1}>\mu_{0}\mid\mathcal{H}_{1:n,t-1}\big).

The right hand side probability above averages over the posterior draw of
\mu_{1},\mu_{0}
given the collective user history
\mathcal{H}_{1:n,t-1}
.
Recall that
\mathcal{H}_{1:n,t-1}\triangleq\{\mathcal{H}_{i,t-1}\}_{i=1}^{n}
for individual user histories
\mathcal{H}_{i,t-1}\triangleq\{X_{i,t^{\prime}},A_{i,t^{\prime}},Y_{i,t^{\prime}}\}_{t^{\prime}=1}^{t-1}
.

Figure
2
below shows empirically that under these common bandit algorithms in the non-stationary environment described above,
\hat{\theta}^{(n)}
does not converge in probability to a constant,
as
n\to\infty
(this is formalized in Proposition
3.1
below).
Since at
t=1
, both the
\epsilon
-greedy and Thompson sampling algorithms select
A_{i,t=1}=1
with probability
0.5
and furthermore there is no treatment effect at time
t=1
(
\Delta_{1}=0
), one might expect that these two algorithms would perform much like a baseline policy

\pi^{\textnormal{baseline}}
that selects
A_{i,t}=1
with probability
0.5
for all
i,t
. The expected average (over
T
) reward under this baseline policy is
\mathbb{E}_{\pi^{\textnormal{baseline}}}\left[\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}R(Y_{i,t})\right]=-0.0625
.
If one uses the either of the two bandit algorithms in the trial, and estimates the expected average (over
T
) reward using the estimator (
6
), a constant fraction of replication trials will have
\hat{\theta}^{(n)}>0.0625+\delta
and another constant fraction of replications will have
\hat{\theta}^{(n)}<-0.0625-\delta
for some
\delta>0
; this is true even as
n\to\infty
.

Proposition 3.1

(Non-replicability under non-stationarity)
.

\epsilon
-greedy (for any
\epsilon<0.5
) or Gaussian-Gaussian Thompson sampling (with standard normal priors) is deployed in the non-stationary environment described above.

In this setting,
\theta^{*}
from (
5
) is well-defined. Moreover,
\hat{\theta}^{(n)}
does not converge in probability to
\theta^{*}
as
n\to\infty
.
Additionally, there exists a
\delta>0
for which
\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}^{(n)}\big(\hat{\theta}^{(n)}>-0.0625+\delta\big)>0
and

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}^{(n)}\big(\hat{\theta}^{(n)}<-0.0625+\delta\big)>0
. We characterize the limiting distribution of
\hat{\theta}^{(n)}
in Appendix
A.1

.

We now describe the intuition behind this inferential non-replicability phenomenon.
Since at the first decision time
(t=1)
the true treatment effect
\Delta_{1}=0
, the bandit algorithm should not learn to favor selecting action
1
or action
0
on average across trial replications. Indeed, under common bandit algorithms, including
\epsilon
-greedy and Thompson sampling the action selection probabilities
\mathbb{P}(A_{i,2}=1\mid\mathcal{H}_{1:n,1})
on average across trial replications equal
0.5
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}^{(n)}\big[\mathbb{P}(A_{i,2}=1\mid\mathcal{H}_{1:n,1})\big]=0.5

for each
n
. However, the treatment probability
\mathbb{P}(A_{i,2}=1\mid\mathcal{H}_{1:n,1})
in any
individual
trial does not concentrate, that is,
\mathbb{P}(A_{i,2}=1\mid\mathcal{H}_{1:n,1})

does not
converge in probability to
0.5
as
n\to\infty
. This occurs because when the treatment effect is zero, the algorithm has no reason to favor one treatment over the other (formalized in Appendix
A.1

). In other words, the bandit algorithm’s treatment propensities are not replicable across trials, which leads to the variability in the average reward estimator
\hat{\theta}^{(n)}
, from display (
6
), in this non-stationary environment.

3.1.3
Sample variance underestimates the true variance under common bandit algorithms.

Building on the experimental setup from Section
3.1.1
, we now show the issues that
can occur regarding variance estimation, even when
\Delta_{1}
does not exactly equal
0
. We use the same algorithms (Thompson sampling and
\epsilon
-greedy), and keep most hyperparameters the same (
\mu_{0}=0
,
\Delta_{2}=-0.25
,
T=2
). We only vary
\Delta_{1}
and the number of individuals
n
in each trial.

In each simulated trial we compute the mean estimator
\hat{\theta}^{(n)}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}R(Y_{i,t})
. We also compute a variance estimator for
\hat{\theta}^{(n)}
using the sample variance:

\displaystyle\hat{s}^{2}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\bigg(\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}R(Y_{i,t})-\hat{\theta}^{(n)}\bigg)^{2}.

Typically, we expect that
\frac{\textnormal{Var}(\hat{\theta}^{(n)})}{\hat{s}^{2}/n}\approx 1
.
Note that across all our simulations (
1000
replications for each value of
\Delta_{1}
and
n
), the variance estimate,
\hat{s}^{2}
, took values in the range
[0.45,0.55]
. In other words,
\hat{s}^{2}
was very stable across a variety of different environments with different values of
\Delta_{1}
and
n
; we used values
\Delta_{1}\in[-0.2,0.2]
and
n\in[200,5000]
.

We compare
\hat{s}^{2}/n
to the true variance
\textnormal{Var}(\hat{\theta}^{(n)})
. We form our approximate estimate
\widehat{\textnormal{Var}}(\hat{\theta}^{(n)})
by taking the sample variance of
\hat{\theta}^{(n)}

across
1000
trial replications
; we call this estimate
\widehat{\textnormal{Var}}(\hat{\theta}^{(n)})
. Note that while
\hat{s}^{2}/n
is an approximate variance estimate based on just a
single
trial, the estimate
\widehat{\textnormal{Var}}(\hat{\theta}^{(n)})
is formed across many trial replications.

In Figure
3
, below we plot
\frac{\widehat{\textnormal{Var}}(\hat{\theta}^{(n)})}{\hat{s}^{2}/n}
under Thompson sampling,
\epsilon
-greedy (
\epsilon=0.05
), and a non-adaptive algorithm that selects actions with a constant
0.5
probability. Under the adaptive algorithms,
\frac{\widehat{\textnormal{Var}}(\hat{\theta}^{(n)})}{\hat{s}^{2}/n}
is about
1
when the value of
n
is large relative to values of
|\Delta_{1}|\not=0
.
However, when
|\Delta_{1}|
is small relative to the number of users
n
, the variance
\widehat{\textnormal{Var}}(\hat{\theta}^{(n)})
is sometimes more than ten times greater than
\hat{s}^{2}/n
!
Notably, this phenomenon does not occur when using a non-adaptive data collection algorithm (selects actions with constant probability
0.5
).

In summary, even when
\Delta_{1}
is not exactly zero, the standard variance estimate
\hat{s}^{2}/n
can
severely
underestimate the true variance of
\hat{\theta}^{(n)}
across trial replications. We expect variance estimation issue occur in this example whenever the signal-to-noise ratio
\frac{|\Delta_{1}|}{\sigma}
is small relative to the sample size
n
. Note that small signal-to-noise ratios are small in practice because effect sizes and sample sizes are often small (e.g.,
<100
).

3.2
Misspecified linear contextual bandit

In the previous section, we discussed how non-replicability can occur in a nonstationary multi-armed bandit setting. We now consider a
stationary contextual
bandit setting in which the reward model used by the bandit algorithm is misspecified. This reflects the common practice in which simple RL/bandit algorithms are favored, to better trade-off bias and variance in these noisy settings (as discussed in Section
2.1
). We consider a post-trial inferential target which is a marginal loss minimizer; note in digital health, it is common to consider inferring causal effects
[
11
,
62
,
71
]
that are marginal over (a subset of) the features used in the bandit algorithm.

For clarity, here we consider a toy version in which both the bandit algorithm and the post-trial inference use a model for the mean of the reward, conditional on features, that is not correctly specified for the underlying true conditional mean of the reward.
Specifically, in the post-trial analysis, we are interested in inference for
\theta^{*}
, which is the minimizer of a least squares criterion, i.e,
\theta^{*}
solves:

\displaystyle 0=\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{E}^{(n)}\left[\dot{\ell}_{\theta}(\mathcal{H}_{i,T})\right]\bigg|_{\theta=\theta^{*}},

(7)

where
\dot{\ell}_{\theta}
is the derivative of
\ell_{\theta}
with respect to
\theta=[\theta_{0},\theta_{1}]
where

\ell_{\theta}(\mathcal{H}_{i,T})\\
=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\big\{R(Y_{i,t})-\phi_{\textnormal{inf}}(X_{i,t})^{\top}\theta_{0}-A_{i,t}\phi_{\textnormal{inf}}(X_{i,t})^{\top}\theta_{1}\big\}^{2}.

(8)

It is assumed for now that the limit in display (
7
) is well-defined. Above,
\phi_{\textnormal{inf}}(X_{i,t})=[1,X_{i,t}]
is a featurization of the covariates
X_{i,t}
we use for inference. In the true data generating distribution, the mean reward will be a linear function of a different featurization of the state
\phi_{\textnormal{gen}}(X_{i,t})
; see display (
10
).

The post-trial inference is based on the least squares estimator:

\displaystyle\hat{\theta}^{(n)}=\textnormal{argmin}_{\theta_{0},\theta_{1}\in\real^{2}}~\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ell_{\theta}(\mathcal{H}_{i,T}).

(9)

3.2.1
Data generating environment.

Consider the following simple environment. Actions are binary, so
\mathcal{A}=\{0,1\}
. The time-varying covariate features

X_{i,t}\sim\textnormal{Uniform}[0,1]
are i.i.d. over
i
and
t
. The expected reward is linear in a featurization of the state
\phi_{\textnormal{gen}}(X_{i,t})=[1,X_{i,t},X_{i,t}^{2}]
:

\displaystyle R(Y_{i,t})=\phi_{\textnormal{gen}}(X_{i,t})^{\top}\alpha_{0}+A_{i,t}\phi_{\textnormal{gen}}(X_{i,t})^{\top}\alpha_{1}+\epsilon_{i,t},

(10)

where noise
\epsilon_{i,t}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
are i.i.d. over
i
and
t
. Specifically,
\alpha_{0}=[0.1,0.1,0]
and
\alpha_{1}=[\frac{1}{3},-2,2]
.
With these values of
\alpha_{0},\alpha_{1}
along with featurization vectors
\phi_{\textnormal{gen}}
from (
10
), the linear model used by the post-trial inference model, which uses
\phi_{\textnormal{inf}}
from (
8
), is misspecified.

3.2.2
Non-replicability under (contextual)

\epsilon
-greedy.

Suppose an
\epsilon
-greedy (or greedy) based contextual bandit algorithm is used to select treatments during the trial.
At the first decision time
(t=1)
, the algorithm randomizes with
\mathbb{P}\big(A_{i,1}=1\mid X_{i,1}=x\big)=0.5
for all
x
. At the second decision time, the algorithm forms action selection probabilities as follows:

\mathbb{P}\big(A_{i,2}=1\mid\mathcal{H}_{1:n,1},X_{i,2}\big)\\
=\begin{cases}1-\epsilon&\textnormal{if}~\phi_{\textnormal{alg}}(X_{i,2})^{\top}\hat{\beta}_{1,1}^{(n)}>0\\
\epsilon&\textnormal{otherwise}\end{cases}

(11)

where
\hat{\beta}_{1,1}^{(n)}
is the least squares estimator using the featurization function
\phi_{\textnormal{alg}}(X_{i,t})=\phi_{\textnormal{inf}}(X_{i,t})=[1,X_{i,t}]
:

\displaystyle\big[\hat{\beta}_{1,0}^{(n)},\hat{\beta}_{1,1}^{(n)}\big]=\textnormal{argmin}_{\beta_{0},\beta_{1}}~\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\tilde{\ell}_{(\beta_{0},\beta_{1})}(\mathcal{H}_{i,1}),

where

As seen in Figure

(the first dimension of

individuals in each replication trial. Furthermore, the non-replicability does not improve substantially by increasing the sample size

, a constant fraction of replication trials will have

for some

4
Replicable Bandits

The non-replicable statistical analyses examples we presented in the previous section are fundamentally due to the non-replicability of the bandit algorithms used to make treatment decisions (see the discussion below Proposition

3.1
for additional intuition behind this). In this section, we show that if the bandit algorithm itself satisfies certain “replicability” properties, then a variety of statistical analyses after the experiment will be replicable (we discuss the types of statistical analyses we are interested in detail later in Section
5
).
In other words, if the bandit algorithm is replicable, then the non-replicable statistical analyses phenomena from the previous section will no longer occur. These observations lead to a variety of open questions regarding how to design bandit algorithms with replicability in mind, which we list in Section
7
.

4.1
Definition

Informally, we say that a bandit algorithm is replicable if across repetitions of the trial, the learned policies become close in with high probability
as the sample size,
n
, increases.
Below, for any real-valued function of
x\in\mathcal{X},a\in\mathcal{A}
we use the notation
\|f\|_{\infty}\triangleq\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X},a\in\mathcal{A}}|f(x,a)|
. In the definition below, we allow policies to depend on the entire collective history
\mathcal{H}_{1:n,t-1}
. Note that this is a strict generalization of allowing the bandit algorithm to depend on particular statistics
\hat{\beta}_{t-1}^{(n)}
formed using
\mathcal{H}_{1:n,t-1}
. Thus
\pi\big(x,a;\hat{\beta}_{t-1}^{(n)}\big)
is replaced by
\pi_{t}(x,a;\mathcal{H}_{1:n,t-1})
in the definition.

Definition 3

(Replicable bandit algorithm)
.

Let
\mathcal{H}_{1:n,T}
and
\tilde{\mathcal{H}}_{1:n,T}
be histories associated with independent replications of a trial. A bandit algorithm is replicable if for each
t\in[1\colon T-1]
and each
\epsilon>0
, as
n\to\infty

\hskip-14.22636pt\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}}\mathbb{P}_{P}^{(n)}\left(\big\|\pi_{t}\big(\,\cdot\,;\mathcal{H}_{1:n,t-1}\big)-\pi_{t}\big(\,\cdot\,;\tilde{\mathcal{H}}_{1:n,t-1}\,\big)\big\|_{\infty}>\epsilon\right)\\
\to 0,

(12)

Furthermore, we say that a bandit algorithm is replicable at a non-random, monotonically increasing rate
\{r^{(n)}\}_{n\geq 1}
if for each
t\in[1\colon T-1]
and each
\epsilon>0
, as
n\to\infty

\hskip-14.22636pt\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}}\mathbb{P}_{P}^{(n)}\left(r^{(n)}\big\|\pi_{t}\big(\,\cdot\,;\mathcal{H}_{1:n,t-1}\big)-\pi_{t}\big(\,\cdot\,;\tilde{\mathcal{H}}_{1:n,t-1}\,\big)\big\|_{\infty}>\epsilon\right)\\
\to 0.

(13)

Above,
\mathbb{P}_{P}^{(n)}
averages over the randomness in forming the histories across replications
\mathcal{H}_{1:n,t-1}
and
\tilde{\mathcal{H}}_{1:n,t-1}
; this randomness includes the draw of potential outcomes from
P\in\mathcal{P}
as described in (
3
) and any randomness in how the algorithm forms policies and selects actions. These definitions of replicable bandit algorithms require that the policies learned by the algorithm must be “close” with high probability across replications of the trial. Additionally, as the number of people in each trial
n
grows, the policies learned across replications must get closer.

The supremum over the class of outcome distributions
P\in\mathcal{P}
ensures that replicability, as defined here,
holds
uniformly
across a range of possible data generating distributions, including the worst cases. Further this definition of replicability assists in proving that convergence of the estimator is uniform in the underlying distribution (see Chapter 2.8 in
Van Der Vaart and Wellner [91]
for definition).
It is well-known in classical statistics that the use of asymptotic approximations with non-regular estimators whose convergence is not uniform in the underlying distribution can result in very poor statistical inference approximations
[
75
,
48
,
12
,
61
,
56
,
32
]
.

In our earlier simulations from Section
3.1
, we found that the distribution of the mean reward estimator under Thompson sampling and
\epsilon
-greedy varied greatly with

\Delta_{1}
the margin in the first timestep. This demonstrates non-uniform convergence since whether the mean reward estimator converges depends on the particular data generating environment
P
. Ensuring the algorithm is replicable uniformly over a range of data-generating distributions helps us ensure that the post-trial estimator
\hat{\theta}^{(n)}
for the mean reward also converges uniformly in the underlying distribution.

4.2
Relationship to existing definitions of replicable decision-making.

In the literature on replicable bandits and reinforcement learning, there are other definitions of replicability.
Eaton et al. [18]
and
Karbasi et al. [43]
define a reinforcement learning algorithm as replicable if it learns the
same
policy with high probability across repetitions. Formally, this means for some
\rho_{n}
that converges to
1
at some rate,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\big\|\pi_{t}\big(\,\cdot\,;\mathcal{H}_{1:n,t-1},\xi\big)-\pi_{t}\big(\,\cdot\,;\tilde{\mathcal{H}}_{1:n,t-1},\xi\big)\|_{\infty}=0\right)\geq\rho_{n}.

(14)

Above the policy also depends on noise
\xi
, which is independent of
\mathcal{H}_{1:n,t-1}
, which allows for randomness in how the policy is formed.
Note that they explicitly assume that the random noise
\xi
is the same across replicated trials.
Our definition differs in that we allow for policies formed to be close but not
exactly
equal with high probability. Additionally, the above formulation does not consider replicability that is uniform over the data-generating distribution
P\in\mathcal{P}
, i.e., for worst-case data-generating distributions.

A different work on replicable bandits aims to ensure that the
entire sequence of actions
taken by the bandit algorithm across replications is the same with high probability
[
20
]
. However, their formulation holds constant across replications, any randomness in how the algorithm forms policies and selects actions. This essentially means that their definition of replicability only encompasses the case in which replications are forced to use the same initial random seed.

Finally,
Parmigiani [69]
defines a notion of replicability of a decision-making rule with respect to a particular utility function, e.g., total expected reward. Their formulation of replicability allows for variation in the decision-making rule as long as it maximizes the utility function of interest. In contrast, we require a much stronger notion of replicability of the decision-making algorithm; our definition of replicability will allow replicability of estimators for a variety of quantities, rather than a single pre-specified utility function. We discuss the types of post-trial statistical analyses we are interested in later in Section
5
.

4.3
Example bandit algorithms that do not satisfy replicability

As we saw in the non-replicability examples in Section
3
,
\epsilon
-greedy and Thompson sampling
[
78
,
42
]
fail to satisfy Definition
3
.
Many common bandit algorithms fail to satisfy the replicable algorithm condition when there is no unique optimal action (or the difference between the best action and the second best is close to zero or decreasing).
This non-replicability is related to taking discontinuous functions (e.g., argmax) of the data to form the policy
[
103
,
56
]
. This problem also occurs if the function used to form the policy is smooth but approaches a discontinuous function in the limit, e.g., a softmax function with a temperature that goes to zero as
n
grows.
Note that discontinuous/non-smooth functions of data are problematic for asymptotic approximations in many areas of statistics
[
48
,
49
,
52
,
75
]
.

While the non-replicability of the bandit algorithm does not lead to inferential non-replicability when the post-trial inferential model is correctly specified
[
104
,
105
]
, once there is misspecification in the inferential model and/or bandit algorithm, non-replicability issues can become a severe problem.

4.4
Example bandit algorithms that generally satisfy replicability

In this section, we discuss several examples of bandit algorithms that will generally satisfy replicability from Definition
3
.

4.4.1
Boltzmann sampling.

Boltzmann sampling (or softmax exploration)
[
83
, Chpt 2.8]
is a type of bandit algorithm that effectively is a “softmax” version of the
\epsilon
-greedy. Boltzmann sampling with (with a temperature parameter that does not go to zero as the sample size increases) satisfies the bandit replicability definition, and this type of algorithm has been used in several digital health trials
[
100
,
51
]
. Below, we describe a simple instantiation of Boltzmann sampling.

At each decision time, the algorithm forms
\hat{\beta}_{t}^{(n)}=[\hat{\beta}_{t,0}^{(n)},\hat{\beta}_{t,1}^{(n)}]
, which is the minimizer of a least squares criterion with a ridge regularization:

\textnormal{argmin}_{\beta}~\lambda\|\beta\|_{2}^{2}\\
+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{t^{\prime}=1}^{t}\left\{R(Y_{i,t^{\prime}})-\big[\phi_{\textnormal{alg}}(X_{i,t^{\prime}}),A_{i,t^{\prime}}\phi_{\textnormal{alg}}(X_{i,t})\big]^{\top}\beta\right\}^{2}.

(15)

\epsilon
-greedy uses
\hat{\beta}_{t}^{(n)}
to form action selection probabilities using a thresholding function (see the formula from (
11
)). In contrast, Boltzmann sampling forms treatment probabilities using a softmax function:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\big(A_{i,t}=a\mid\hat{\beta}_{t-1}^{(n)},X_{i,t}\big)=\frac{1}{1+\exp(-s\cdot\phi_{\textnormal{alg}}(X_{i,t})^{\top}\hat{\beta}_{t-1,1}^{(n)})}.

(16)

Above the hyperparameter
s>0
changes the steepness or “temperature” of the softmax. We discuss considerations for choosing this hyperparameter later in Section
4.4.4
.

4.4.2
Smoothed Thompson sampling.

Several recent digital health intervention trials (including Oralytics)
[
62
,
27
,
88
]
use a modified version of Thompson sampling that ensures replicability of the algorithm in the sense of Definition
3
. We first discuss a simple instantiation of a standard Thompson sampling algorithm
[
78
,
84
]
and then describe the modified version.

A classical linear Thompson sampling algorithm posits a Bayesian model where a latent parameter
\alpha=[\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}]
is sampled from a known, multivariate Gaussian prior distribution
\alpha\sim N(\mu,\Sigma)
. Then rewards are modeled as Gaussian conditional on
\alpha
and the covariate
X_{i,t}
:

R(Y_{i,t})\mid X_{i,t},\alpha\\
\sim N\big(\phi_{\textnormal{alg}}(X_{i,t})^{\top}\alpha_{0}+A_{i,t}\phi_{\textnormal{alg}}(X_{i,t})^{\top}\alpha_{1},\sigma^{2}\big).

(17)

Standard Thompson sampling selects action
1
according to the posterior probability that action
1
is optimal (i.e., the posterior probability that the treatment effect
X_{i,t}^{\top}\alpha_{1}
is positive):

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\big(A_{i,t}=1\mid\hat{\beta}_{t-1}^{(n)},X_{i,t}\big)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\big(\phi_{\textnormal{alg}}(X_{i,t})^{\top}\alpha_{1}>0\mid\mathcal{H}_{1:n,t-1},X_{i,t}\big)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\big[\boldsymbol{1}_{\phi_{\textnormal{alg}}(X_{i,t})^{\top}\alpha_{1}>0}\mid\mathcal{H}_{1:n,t-1},X_{i,t}\big].

Above, the probability on the right-hand side averages over the randomness in the draw of
\alpha_{1}
according to the posterior distribution given
\mathcal{H}_{1:n,t-1}
. The second equality above holds because probabilities can be written as expectations of indicators.

In the smoothed version of Thompson sampling, we replace the indicator function with a smoothed function
\rho
:

\mathbb{P}\big(A_{i,t}=1\mid\hat{\beta}_{t-1}^{(n)},X_{i,t}\big)\\
=\mathbb{E}\big[\rho\big(\phi_{\textnormal{alg}}(X_{i,t})^{\top}\alpha_{1}\big)\mid\mathcal{H}_{1:n,t-1},X_{i,t}\big].

(18)

For example, we can choose
\rho
to be a softmax function
\rho(x)=\big(1+e^{-s\cdot x}\big)^{-1}
, where
s
controls the temperature of the softmax.

4.4.3
Covariate-Adjusted Response Adjusted randomization (CARA).

Many of the covariate-adjusted adaptive randomization algorithms from the adaptive clinical trial literature also satisfy our definition of replicability
[
76
,
36
,
106
,
4
]
. Interestingly, many of the CARA algorithms described in the literature are essentially generalizations of the Boltzmann sampling from Section
4.4.1
, e.g., see Chapter 9 of
Rosenberger et al. [76]
. Variants include fitting a logistic regression model instead of a linear regression model for binary rewards
[
106
]
and replacing the softmax function with other smooth functions like a Gaussian cumulative density function
[
4
]
.

4.4.4
Choosing the allocation function.

The replicable algorithms we describe above require choosing an “allocation function”, which maps a given estimated treatment effect to an action selection probability. For example, in Boltzmann sampling from (
16
), the allocation function is a softmax function with a temperature parameter
s
that we need to choose. In smoothed Thompson sampling from (
18
), the allocation function is
\rho
.
In choosing the allocation function, there are multiple (competing) objectives, specifically, reward maximization and power maximization. See Chapter 2.2 of
Hu and Rosenberger [36]
and
Simchi-Levi and Wang [81]
for further discussion of the tradeoff between reward maximization and power maximization.

If the environment is a bandit environment,
4
4

4

In standard stochastic contextual bandit environments, the distribution of future contexts
X_{i,t+1}
is independent of previous actions
A_{i,1:t}
given previous contexts
X_{i,1:t}
; that is
X_{i,t+1}\mathrel{\perp\!\!\!\perp}A_{i,1:t}\mid X_{i,1:t}
. In contrast, in general Markov decision processes, the next context/state may depend on the past action.
then in the limit, rewards are maximized if the allocation function approaches an indicator function, e.g., for smoothed Thompson sampling, this means
\rho
will approach
\rho(x)=\boldsymbol{1}_{x>0}
and in Boltzmann sampling
s
should approach positive infinity. Note, though, that if an indicator allocation function is used, the algorithm will fail to be replicable. In contrast, to maximize the power to detect a treatment effect, it is optimal for the allocation function to approach the Neyman allocation
[
64
]
; in simple bandit environments where the reward noise variance is the same for all actions, the Neyman allocation selects all actions with equal probability.

In other words, the choice of the allocation function will simultaneously affect the algorithm’s ability to maximize rewards and the power of statistical analyses to detect a treatment effect. Though we do not explore it thoroughly in this work, we believe that the allocation function can be chosen as a part of the clinical trial design by extending methods from standard power analyses
[
16
,
53
]
, which specify that for a standardized effect size of interest (e.g.,
0.1
or
0.2
) the power must be sufficiently high (e.g.,
\geq 80\%
). We discuss further open questions related to this point later in Section
7
.

5
Statistical Analyses after using Replicable Bandits

In this section, we discuss post-trial statistical analyses of interest and present our theoretical guarantees for these statistical analyses on data collected by replicable bandit algorithms.
In most clinical trials, particularly behavioral health and digital health clinical trials, both the pre-specified and non-pre-specified data analyses concern inference for population parameters. These parameters are defined via the population version of estimating functions or a loss criterion and estimated using Z or M estimators
[
91
]
. Specifically, we are interested in constructing confidence intervals for a loss minimizer
\theta^{*}(P)
that solves the following under the population-deployed policies
5
5

5

Recall we introduced population-deployed policies in Section
2.3.2
:

\displaystyle 0=\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{E}_{P}^{(n)}\left[\dot{\ell}_{\theta}\big(\mathcal{H}_{i,T}\big)\right]\bigg|_{\theta=\theta^{*}(P)},

(19)

for a least squares criterion

\displaystyle\ell_{\theta}\big(\mathcal{H}_{i,T}\big)=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left\{\mathcal{Y}_{i,t}-\phi_{\textnormal{inf}}\big(X_{i,t},A_{i,t},\mathcal{H}_{i,t-1}\big)^{\top}\theta\right\}^{2}.

(20)

Above, we use
\mathcal{Y}_{i,t}\triangleq m(Y_{i,t})\in\real
, where
m
is a known mapping that takes
Y_{i,t}
and outputs a scalar (e.g.,
m
could output a dimension of the vector
Y_{i,t}
or output the reward
R(Y_{i,t})
).

\dot{\ell}_{\theta}
is the derivative of
\ell_{\theta}
with respect to
\theta
and
\phi_{\textnormal{inf}}
is a vector of bounded feature mapping functions.
We formalize that the limit in (
19
) exists in Proposition
1
.

For different choices of
\mathcal{Y}_{i,t}
and
\phi_{\textnormal{inf}}
, we can represent inference for different quantities of interest. For example, for
\mathcal{Y}_{i,t}=R(Y_{i,t})
and
\phi_{\textnormal{inf}}=1
, then
\theta^{*}(P)
is the mean reward. Alternatively, if we were interested in inferring a treatment effect, we could consider choosing

\displaystyle\phi_{\textnormal{inf}}(X_{i,t},A_{i,t},\mathcal{H}_{i,t-1})=[g(X_{i,t},\mathcal{H}_{i,t-1}),A_{i,t}f(X_{i,t})]

for some functions
g
and
f
that take values on
\real{}^{d_{g}}
and
\real{}^{d_{f}}
respectively. In this case, the last
\real{}^{d_{f}}
dimensions of
\theta^{*}(P)
will represent parameters in a treatment effect that is moderated by
X_{i,t}
. In the longitudinal digital health literature, these types of treatment effects are called
causal excursion effects
. See
Boruvka et al. [11]
and
Qian et al. [72]
for further discussion of causal excursion effects.

To estimate
\theta^{*}(P)
we consider an empirical loss minimizer
\hat{\theta}^{(n)}
that solves

\displaystyle 0=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\dot{\ell}_{\theta}\big(\mathcal{H}_{i,T}\big)\bigg|_{\theta=\hat{\theta}^{(n)}}.

(21)

We saw earlier in Section
3
that if the bandit algorithm collecting the data is non-replicable, then standard estimators like
\hat{\theta}^{(n)}
can fail to be replicable. In this section, we assume the bandit algorithm is replicable according to Definition
3
and show that
\hat{\theta}^{(n)}
is replicable and that we can construct confidence intervals for
\theta^{*}(P)
.

The theoretical results we present in the following sections for this estimator on data collected by replicable bandit algorithms are two-fold. First, in Section
5.1
we show consistency, i.e., that the estimator
\hat{\theta}^{(n)}
converges to
\theta^{*}(P)
in probability. This ensures that for any replication of the trial, as the sample size
n
grows,
\hat{\theta}^{(n)}
will converge to the quantity of interest
\theta^{*}(P)
; this implies
\hat{\theta}^{(n)}
is replicable in the sense of Definition
1
. Then in Section
5.2
we prove that the estimator
\hat{\theta}^{(n)}
is asymptotically normal, i.e.,
\sqrt{n}\big(\hat{\theta}^{(n)}-\theta^{*}(P)\big)\overset{D}{\to}N\big(0,\Sigma(P)\big)
for some covariance matrix
\Sigma(P)
which we can estimate; this will ensure we are able to characterize the variability of
\hat{\theta}^{(n)}
across replications in the sense of Definition
2
. With the asymptotic normality result, we are able to construct asymptotically valid confidence intervals (and confidence regions) for
\theta^{*}(P)
.

5.1
Consistency

In this section, we show that the estimator
\hat{\theta}^{(n)}
from (
21
) converges to the quantity of interest
\theta^{*}(P)
. Formally, we show that for each
\epsilon>0
,

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\,\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}}\,\mathbb{P}_{P}^{(n)}\left(\big\|\hat{\theta}^{(n)}-\theta^{*}(P)\big\|_{2}>\epsilon\right)=0.

(22)

The above ensures that the estimator
\hat{\theta}^{(n)}
is replicable, i.e., across trial replications, the proportion for which
\big\|\hat{\theta}^{(n)}-\theta^{*}(P)\big\|_{2}>\epsilon
goes to zero as the sample size in each trial grows. Note that the above ensures that
\hat{\theta}^{(n)}
is replicable in the sense of Definition
1
.
Above, the supremum over possible data generating distributions
P\in\mathcal{P}
ensures that convergence is uniform in the underlying distribution (see Section
4.1
for further discussion of the need for uniform convergence).

We now discuss the assumptions we use to ensure (
22
). The first, Assumption
1
, below, helps ensure that
\theta^{*}
from (
19
) is well-defined (see Proposition
1
). Specifically, we assume there exists a population-deployed policy. This is the
expected
policy that would be learned on average across trial replications as the number of individuals in each trial
n\to\infty
. Note that Assumption
1
display (
23
) below is very mild and holds even for bandit algorithms that are not replicable in the sense of Definition
3
. This assumption rules out edge-case bandit algorithms that may continually oscillate, e.g., switch between two different policies indefinitely depending on whether

is even or odd. Display (
24
) generalizes the assumption to hold at a particular rate.

Assumption 1

(Population-deployed policies)
.

For each
t\in[2\colon T]
, there exists a policy
\pi_{t}^{*}
that may depend on

P\in\mathcal{P}
such that

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\,\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}}\,\left\|\mathbb{E}_{P}^{(n)}\big[\pi\big(\,\cdot\,;\hat{\beta}_{t-1}^{(n)}\big)\big]-\pi_{t}^{*}(\,\cdot\,;P)\big)\right\|_{\infty}=0.

(23)

Furthermore, we say this assumption holds for a non-random, monotonically increasing rate

if

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\,\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}}\,r^{(n)}\left\|\mathbb{E}_{P}^{(n)}\big[\pi\big(\,\cdot\,;\hat{\beta}_{t-1}^{(n)}\big)\big]-\pi_{t}^{*}(\,\cdot\,;P)\big)\right\|_{\infty}=0.

(24)

Assumption
2
below concerns the second derivative of the population loss minimization criterion:
\ddot{\ell}_{\theta}\big(\mathcal{H}_{i,T}\big)\triangleq\phi_{\textnormal{inf}}\big(X_{i,t},A_{i,t},\mathcal{H}_{i,t-1}\big)\phi_{\textnormal{inf}}\big(X_{i,t},A_{i,t},\mathcal{H}_{i,t-1}\big)^{\top}

is unique. Note that this type of assumption is standard in the literature, and similar assumptions are needed even for i.i.d. data settings

Assumption 2

(Invertible)

For some
\lambda>0
and all sufficiently large

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{P}^{(n)}\left[\phi_{\textnormal{inf}}\big(X_{i,t},A_{i,t},\mathcal{H}_{i,t-1}\big)\phi_{\textnormal{inf}}\big(X_{i,t},A_{i,t},\mathcal{H}_{i,t-1}\big)^{\top}\right].

Assumption
3
below ensures that the outcomes

Assumption 3

(Moments)

For each
P\in\mathcal{P}

\displaystyle\max_{a_{1:T}\in\mathcal{A}^{1:T}}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\big|\mathcal{Y}_{i,t}(a_{1:t})\big|\leq Y,\quad P\textnormal{-almost surely}

Before we state our consistency result for

5.2
Asymptotic normality

In this section we discuss when the estimator

\displaystyle\sqrt{n}\big(\hat{\theta}^{(n)}-\theta^{*}\big)\overset{D}{\to}\mathcal{N}\left(0,\ddot{L}_{P}^{-1}\Sigma_{P}\ddot{L}_{P}^{-1}\right).

Simulations

In this section, we examine replicability experimentally in a synthetic environment and an environment based on Oralytics
[
62
,
88
]
(see the Example in Section
Example
). In both these settings, we are interested in the average outcome under the population-deployed policy (similar to (
4
)):

\displaystyle\theta^{*}=\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{E}^{(n)}\left[\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathcal{Y}_{i,t}\right].

We estimate
\theta^{*}
above with the empirical average outcome:

\displaystyle\hat{\theta}^{(n)}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathcal{Y}_{i,t}.

In each of our two experimental settings, we have binary actions (
\mathcal{A}=\{0,1\}
) and we consider the smoothed Thompson sampling algorithm and Boltzmann sampling from Section
4.4
, as well as an
\epsilon
-greedy contextual bandit algorithm. See Appendix
D

for full details on algorithms. Note that smoothed Thompson sampling and Boltzmann sampling satisfy our replicable bandit definition, i.e., (
12
), and
\epsilon
-greedy does not. As a result, we find in our simulations that
\epsilon
-greedy can fail to be replicable in some environments, while Boltzmann sampling is consistently replicable across various environments.

6.1
Synthetic simulations

6.1.1
Simulation environment.

We consider the synthetic simulation environment that was used in
Zhang et al. [105]
. In this environment,
T=50
and the mean reward is dependent on the recent “dosage”, i.e., an exponentially discounted average of the number of previous treatments. In other words, in this setting, the sequence of previous treatments can impact an individual’s future responsiveness. Specifically, the outcome of interest
\mathcal{Y}_{i,t}=Y_{i,t}
where

\displaystyle Y_{i,t}=\alpha_{0}+\alpha_{1}A_{i,t}\frac{1}{c_{\gamma}}\sum_{t^{\prime}=1}^{t-1}\gamma^{t-1-t^{\prime}}A_{i,t^{\prime}}+\alpha_{2}A_{i,t}+\epsilon_{i,t}.

(27)

Above, we use

\epsilon_{i,t}

for each individual

The Boltzmann sampling, Thompson sampling, and
\epsilon
-greedy algorithms use the reward

and use previous decision outcomes,

, as the context.
Specifically, the algorithms use the following working model for the expected reward:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[R(Y_{i,t})\mid X_{i,t},A_{i,t}]=\beta_{0}^{\top}\phi_{\textnormal{alg}}(X_{i,t})+\beta_{1}^{\top}A_{i,t}\phi_{\textnormal{alg}}(X_{i,t})

where
\phi_{\textnormal{alg}}(X_{i,t})=[1,R(Y_{i,t-1})]
. The Boltzmann sampling and smoothed Thompson sampling (s-TS) algorithms use a steepness of
s=5
; see displays (
16
) and (
18
) respectively. The smoothed Thompson sampling algorithm use a multivariate Gaussian prior with mean zero and an identity covariance matrix. For
\epsilon
-greedy, we use

; see display (
11
).

6.2
Oralytics simulations

Oralytics involves a series of oral health clinical trials, each using a contextual bandit algorithm to personalize the delivery of intervention prompts encouraging oral self-care behaviors for individuals at-risk for dental disease. For each individual, the Oralytics algorithm makes decisions every day at two decision points (i.e., once in the morning and once in the evening). The action space is binary, representing sending an intervention prompt (
A_{i,t}=1
) or not (
A_{i,t}=0
). In the upcoming Oralytics trial
[
62
]
, individuals are each in the trial for
10
weeks, which means a total of
T=140
decision times. We use
n=100
in our simulations.

6.2.1
Simulation environment.

To build the simulation environment, we use data from the pilot phase of the Oralytics study
[
62
,
88
]
. The pilot phase ran in spring 2023 for 35 days. All individuals were recruited and started the study at the same time (no incremental recruitment). For each individual in the pilot study, we fit an outcome model, which is used to simulate rewards. To make the simulation environment more realistic, we also modeled the negative delayed effects of actions and individual app opening behavior. See Appendix
D.2.1

for full details.

In Oralytics, a variety of brushing outcomes are collected passively via the blue-tooth enabled toothbrushes. These include
B_{i,t}
, the number of seconds the individual brushes, and
P_{i,t}
, the number of seconds the user brushes with too much pressure, following the
t^{\textnormal{th}}
decision time. Brushing quality is the true behavioral outcome that the scientific team aims to improve for each individual to achieve long-term oral health. Our working definition of brushing quality is the number of seconds the individual brushes total minus the number of seconds of over-pressure brushing:

\displaystyle\mathcal{Y}_{i,t}=\max(180,B_{i,t}-P_{i,t})

(28)

Note above that we truncate by
180
to avoid optimizing for over-brushing.

Although the main outcome of interest
\mathcal{Y}_{i,t}
is the brushing quality, the reward is a design choice that can be modified in order to improve the algorithm’s learning. In Oralytics we use
R(Y_{i,t})=\mathcal{Y}_{i,t}-C_{i,t}
, where
C_{i,t}
is a cost term that represents a crude approximation of the delayed effects of actions on future contexts and rewards. This allows us to approximate a full reinforcement learning algorithm with a contextual bandit algorithm.
For more details on the reward design see Appendix
D.2.2

and
Trella et al. [87]
.
The featurization used by the contextual bandit algorithms,
\phi_{\textnormal{inf}}(X_{i,t})\in\real^{5}
, consists of an intercept, time of day (morning vs. evening), brush times over the past week, “dosage” (i.e., number of promps sent) in the past week, and a prior day app engagement indicator.

6.2.2
Contextual bandit algorithms.

The Boltzmann sampling, Thompson sampling, and
\epsilon
-greedy algorithms all use the same reward definition and context features. The Boltzmann sampling and smoothed Thompson sampling (s-TS) algorithms use a steepness of
s=0.025
; see displays (
16
) and (
18
) respectively. We set
s=0.025\approx\frac{1}{38.83}
, where
38.83
is the standard deviation of the reward noise
[
88
]
. The smoothed Thompson sampling algorithm uses a prior based on that used in the Oralytics trial
[
88
]
. For
\epsilon
-greedy, we use
\epsilon=0.2
; see display (
11
).

6.2.3
Results.

As seen in Table
2
, the expected outcome under Boltzmann sampling, smoothed Thompson sampling (s-TS), and
\epsilon
-greedy in the Oralytics environment is comparable. Similar to the synthetic simulation setting, we find that under Boltzmann sampling and smoothed Thompson sampling, using a normal approximation for
\hat{\theta}^{(n)}
with the adaptive sandwich variance estimator of the limiting variance leads to accurate confidence intervals. However, in contrast to the synthetic simulation setting, we find that under
\epsilon
-greedy, the naive approach of using a normal approximation for
\hat{\theta}^{(n)}
and estimating the limiting variance using the standard sandwich variance estimators empirically works well in this setting.

These simulation results support the idea that Boltzmann sampling can better ensure inferential replicability across a variety of possible data-generating environments
P\in\mathcal{P}
, while the inferential replicability under
\epsilon
-greedy may depend on properties of the environment itself, properties that may not be known a priori to deploying the algorithm.

7
Discussion and Open Questions

In this work, we connect inferential replicability to replicability of the underlying bandit algorithm used to make treatment decisions. We show that in a variety of misspecified environments (non-stationary and misspecified reward model), when common bandit algorithms are used to assign treatments, standard estimators can be inconsistent and thus non-replicable across trials. We discuss how this non-replicability relates to the non-replicability of the underlying policies formed by the bandit algorithm across trials.
We then provide a definition of what it means for a bandit algorithm to be replicable. We prove that under replicable bandit algorithms, a variety of standard estimators are consistent and asymptotically normal.

There are several open questions given this work, which we summarize below:

Replicability and regret minimization:

What are the consequences of ensuring replicability of the bandit algorithm on regret
[
42
]
? Is it possible to ensure that a bandit algorithm is both replicable and minimizes regret optimally? We found in our experiments in Section
6.1
that the algorithm that was not replicable lead to a bit higher rewards than an algorithm that was replicable. What about algorithms that are designed to optimally balance regret minimization and the power of a particular pre-specified post-trial primary analysis
[
19
,
36
,
81
,
73
,
47
]
?

Replicability and robustness:
Several papers have found that algorithms that minimize regret aggressively can be less robust to model misspecification
[
22
,
21
,
96
]
. Could there be a relationship between replicability and ensuring the algorithm minimizes regret robustly across many environments?

Lemma A.1
.

Under the environment described in Section

, for any bandit algorithm that uses treatment assignment probabilities

and
\hat{\pi}_{2}(1)=\mathbb{P}(A_{i,2}=1\mid\mathcal{H}_{1:n,1})

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(R(Y_{i,1})+R(Y_{i,2})\right)-\Delta_{2}\hat{\pi}_{2}(1)\overset{P}{\to}0.

Proof.

Since

\{\epsilon_{1,i}\}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}R(Y_{i,1})=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mu_{0}+\Delta_{1}A_{i,1}+\epsilon_{i,1}\overset{P}{\to}\mu_{0}+\Delta_{1}\pi_{1}(1)=0.

(29)

The last equality above holds because in the environment
\mu_{0}=0
and
\Delta_{1}=0

We now show that
\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}R(Y_{i,2})-\Delta_{2}\hat{\pi}_{2}(1)\overset{P}{\to}0

29
), will be sufficient to prove this Lemma.
Note that for any
\alpha>0

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}R(Y_{i,2})-\Delta_{2}\hat{\pi}_{2}(1)\right|>\alpha\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\alpha^{2}n^{2}}\mathbb{E}^{(n)}\left[\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\big\{\Delta_{2}\big(A_{i,2}-\hat{\pi}_{2}(1)\big)+\epsilon_{i,2}\big\}\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\underbrace{=}_{(b)}\frac{1}{\alpha^{2}n^{2}}\mathbb{E}^{(n)}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{n}\big\{\Delta_{2}\cdot\big(A_{i,2}-\hat{\pi}_{2}(1)\big)+\epsilon_{i,2}\big\}^{2}\right]

2.

Minimum Exploration:
For a constant
\pi_{\min}
,

\displaystyle\inf_{\beta\in\real^{d_{\beta}}}\pi(a,x;\beta)>\pi_{\min}.

3.

Lipschitz Policy Function:
There exists a non-negative real-valued function

such that
\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}}\mathbb{E}_{P,\pi^{*}}\left[\|\dot{\pi}(a,s;P)\|_{1}^{2+\alpha}\right]<\infty
for some

Square-root rate of parameters:

\displaystyle\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}}\big(\hat{\beta}_{1:T-1}^{(n)}-\beta_{1:T-1}^{*}(P)\big)=\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}}O_{P}(1/\sqrt{n}).

We now verify that the above conditions hold:

1.

Bounded Moments:
For any dimension
k
,

\displaystyle\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}}\mathbb{E}_{P,\pi^{*}}\left[\left|\dot{\ell}_{\theta^{*}}(\mathcal{H}_{i,T})_{[k]}+Q_{P,2:T}\psi_{\beta_{1:T-1}^{*}}(\mathcal{H}_{i,T-1})_{[k]}\right|^{2+\alpha}\right]

\displaystyle\leq 2^{1+\alpha}\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}}\mathbb{E}_{P,\pi^{*}}\left[\left|\dot{\ell}_{\theta^{*}}(\mathcal{H}_{i,T})_{[k]}\right|^{2+\alpha}+\left|Q_{P,2:T}\psi_{\beta_{1:T-1}^{*}}(\mathcal{H}_{i,T-1})_{[k]}\right|^{2+\alpha}\right]<\infty.

The first inequality above holds because by Jensen’s inequality
\big(\frac{|a+b|}{2}\big)^{2+\alpha}\leq\big(\frac{|a|+|b|}{2}\big)^{2+\alpha}\leq\frac{|a|^{2+\alpha}+|b|^{2+\alpha}}{2}
.

The final inequality above holds by Assumptions
3

and
5

.

2.

Minimum Exploration:
This condition holds since our bandit algorithm has a constant exploration rate
\{\pi_{\min}^{(n)}\}_{n\geq 1}
where
\pi_{\min}^{(n)}=\pi_{\min}
for a constant
\pi_{\min}>0
.

3.

Lipschitz Policy Function:
This holds since by Assumption

\pi(x,a;\beta)
is differentiable with respect to
\beta
. Also for each
\beta\in\real^{d_{\beta}}

\|\dot{\pi}(\,\cdot\,;\beta)\|_{\infty}<\infty
.

4.

By Assumption

\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}}\big(\hat{\beta}_{1:T-1}^{(n)}-\beta_{1:T-1}^{*}(P)\big)=\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}}O_{P}(1/\sqrt{n})
.

By Slutsky’s Theorem, continuous mapping theorem, (
53

55

\displaystyle\sqrt{n}\big(\hat{\theta}^{(n)}-\theta^{*}\big)

\displaystyle=-\ddot{L}_{P}^{-1}\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\pi_{i,2:T}^{*}}{\hat{\pi}_{i,2:T}^{(n)}}\left(\dot{\ell}_{\theta^{*}}(\mathcal{H}_{i,T})-Q_{P,2:T}\psi_{\beta_{1:T-1}^{*}}(\mathcal{H}_{i,T-1})\right)+\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}}o_{P}(1)

uniformly over

Outcome Generating Process.

We use a zero-inflated Poisson to model each individual’s outcome generating process because of the zero-inflated nature of brushing quality found in the Oralytics pilot data set. Each individual’s outcome generating process is as follows:

Z\sim\text{Bernoulli}\bigg(1-\mathrm{sigmoid}\big(g(X_{i,t})^{\top}w_{i,b}-A_{i,t}\cdot\max\big[\Delta_{i,B}^{\top}g(X_{i,t}),0\big]\big)\bigg)

S\sim\text{Poisson}\big(\exp\big(g(X_{i,t})^{\top}w_{i,p}+A_{i,t}\cdot\max\big[\Delta_{i,N}^{\top}g(X_{i,t}),0\big]\big)\big)

\mathcal{Y}_{i,t}=ZS

w_{i,b},w_{i,p}
are individual-specific baseline weight vectors,
\Delta_{i,B},\Delta_{i,N}
are individual-specific advantage (or effect size) weight vectors.
g(X_{i,t})
is the baseline feature vector and the feature vector that interacts with the effect size as described in the subsequent section, and
\mathrm{sigmoid}(x)=\frac{1}{1+e^{-x}}
.

The individual’s intent to brush is modeled by the Bernoulli outcome
Z
and the individual’s brushing quality when they intend to brush is modeled by the Poisson outcome
S
. Notice that the models for
Z,S
means the effect size on
\mathcal{Y}
must be non-negative. Otherwise, not sending an engagement prompt could yield a higher immediate brushing quality than sending an engagement prompt, which is nonsensical in this oral health setting. We ensure that
\max\big[\Delta_{i,B}^{\top}h(X_{i,t}),0\big]
and
\max\big[\Delta_{i,N}^{\top}h(X_{i,t}),0\big]
are non-negative to prevent the effect size from switching signs and having a negative effect on brushing quality.

We jointly fit weights
w_{i,b},w_{i,p},\Delta_{i,B},\Delta_{i,N}
for both the Bernoulli and the Poisson components using each individual’s data. Specifically, we used MAP with prior
w_{i,b},w_{i,p},\Delta_{i,B},\Delta_{i,N}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I)
as a form of regularization because we have sparse data for each individual. Finalized weight values were chosen by running random restarts and selecting the weights with the highest log posterior density.

Responsivity to Actions.

We model delayed effects of actions by shrinking individuals’ responsiveness to actions, i.e., their initial treatment effect sizes. We shrink individuals’ effect sizes by a factor
E=0.5
when a certain criterion is met. The choice of
0.5
was chosen in discussion with domain experts. Specifically, the criterion if either of the two scenarios holds: (a)
\mathbb{I}[\bar{B}_{i,t}>b]
(individual brushes well) and
\mathbb{I}[\bar{A}_{i,t}>a_{1}]
(individual was sent too many engagement prompts for a healthy brusher), or (b)
\mathbb{I}[\bar{A}_{i,t}>a_{2}]
(the individual has been sent too many engagement prompts). See display (
58
) for definitions of
\bar{B}
and
\bar{A}
; we define the values of hyperparamters
b,a_{1},a_{2}
in the next section (under the subheading “Reward”). Notice that the same definitions and values of
\bar{B},\bar{A},b,a_{1},a_{2}
used in the environment models are the same as will be used the reward definition.

The first time an individual’s criterion has been met, the individual’s future effect sizes
\Delta_{i,B},\Delta_{i,N}
starting at time
t+1
will be scaled down proportionally by
E=0.5
(the individual is less responsive to treatment). Namely, the effect sizes will be shrunken to
E\cdot\Delta_{i,B},E\cdot\Delta_{i,N}
. Then after a week, at time
t+14
, we will check the criterion again. If the criterion is met again, the effect sizes will be further shrunk by a factor of
E

E^{2}\cdot\Delta_{i,B},E^{2}\cdot\Delta_{i,N}

starting at time

This procedure continues until the individual finishes the study. Notice that this means the individual can only have their effect size shrunk at most once a week. This procedure simulates how the individual may experience a reduction in responsiveness, but after a week, if the RL algorithm does not intervene too much, the individual may recover their prior responsivity.

App Engagement Behavior.

We simulate individual app opening as follows. For each individual in the pilot study, we calculated

The algorithm decided between sending a prompt

or not

. If

Reward.

The reward is a function of brushing quality
\mathcal{Y}_{i,t}
.
R(Y_{i,t}):=\mathcal{Y}_{i,t}-C_{i,t}
where
C_{i,t}
is a cost term that represents a crude approximation approximation of the delayed effects of actions. We now discuss in detail about the design of the cost term
C_{i,t}
. The inclusion of cost term
C_{i,t}
allows the bandit algorithm to optimize for immediate healthy brushing behavior, while simultaneously considering the delayed effect of the current action on the effectiveness of future actions. The cost term can be interpreted as a function (with parameters
\xi_{1},\xi_{2}
) which takes in current covariates
X_{i,t}
and action
A_{i,t}
and returns the delayed negative effect of currently sending an engagement prompt.

Recall in the time-varying covariate features, we defined
\bar{B}_{i,t}:=c_{\gamma}\sum_{j=1}^{14}\gamma^{j-1}\mathcal{Y}_{i,t-j}
and
\bar{A}_{i,t}:=c_{\gamma}\sum_{j=1}^{14}\gamma^{j-1}A_{i,t-j}
. Note that in the algorithm time-varying covariate features,
\bar{B},\bar{A}
are normalized, but in the cost term below they are not. We set
\gamma=\frac{13}{14}
to represent looking back 14 decision point points and scale each sum by constant
c_{\gamma}=\frac{1-\gamma}{1-\gamma^{14}}
so that the weights sum to 1. Notice that our choice of
\gamma
and the scaling constant means
0\leq\bar{B}_{i,t}\leq 180
and
0\leq\bar{A}_{i,t}\leq 1
.

\bar{B}_{i,t}
captures the individual’s exponentially discounted brushing quality in the past week.
\bar{A}_{i,t}
captures the number of actions that were sent over the past week.
Both terms are exponentially discounted because we expect that actions sent and individual brushing in the near past will be more predictive of the delayed impact of the actions (i.e., affecting a individual’s responsivity to future actions) than those in the further past.

We define the cost of sending an engagement prompt (i.e. captures individual burden in sending a prompt) as:

C_{i,t}:=\begin{cases}\xi_{1}\mathbb{I}[\bar{B}_{i,t}>b]\mathbb{I}[\bar{A}_{i,t}>a_{1}]&\\
\hskip 28.45274pt+\xi_{2}\mathbb{I}[\bar{A}_{i,t}>a_{2}]&\smash{\raisebox{6.88889pt}{if $A_{i,t}=1$}}\\
0&\hskip 0.0pt\mathrm{if~}A_{i,t}=0\end{cases}

(57)

Notice that the algorithm only incurs a cost if the current action is to send an action, i.e.,
A_{i,t}=1
. The first term
\xi_{1}\mathbb{I}[\bar{B}_{i,t}>b]\mathbb{I}[\bar{A}_{i,t}>a_{1}]
encapsulates the belief that if a high-performing individual was sent too many engagement prompts within the past week, then we want to penalize the reward. The second term
\xi_{2}\mathbb{I}[\bar{A}_{i,t}>a_{2}]
encapsulates the belief that regardless of individual performance, if they received too many engagement prompts within the past week, then we also want to penalize the reward.
b,a_{1},a_{2}
are chosen by domain experts. Notice that
a_{1}<a_{2}
because we believe a high-performing individual will have a lower threshold of being burdened by an engagement prompt. The scientific team decided to set the following values:

•

b=111
, is set to the 50th-percentile of individual brushing durations in an earlier data set ROBAS 2.

9

9

ROBAS 2 data is publicly accessible in GitHub
here
.

•

a_{1}=0.5
, represents a rough approximation of the individual getting an engagement prompt 50% of the time (rough approximation because we are using an exponential average mean)

•

a_{2}=0.8
, represents a rough approximation of the individual getting an engagement prompt 80% of the time (rough approximation because we are using an exponential average mean)

\xi_{1},\xi_{2}
are non-negative hyperparameters that are set to
\xi_{1}=100,\xi_{2}=100

Outcome Generating Process.

We use a zero-inflated Poisson to model each individual’s outcome generating process because of the zero-inflated nature of brushing quality found in the Oralytics pilot data set. Each individual’s outcome generating process is as follows:

Z\sim\text{Bernoulli}\bigg(1-\mathrm{sigmoid}\big(g(X_{i,t})^{\top}w_{i,b}-A_{i,t}\cdot\max\big[\Delta_{i,B}^{\top}g(X_{i,t}),0\big]\big)\bigg)

S\sim\text{Poisson}\big(\exp\big(g(X_{i,t})^{\top}w_{i,p}+A_{i,t}\cdot\max\big[\Delta_{i,N}^{\top}g(X_{i,t}),0\big]\big)\big)

\mathcal{Y}_{i,t}=ZS

w_{i,b},w_{i,p}
are individual-specific baseline weight vectors,
\Delta_{i,B},\Delta_{i,N}
are individual-specific advantage (or effect size) weight vectors.
g(X_{i,t})
is the baseline feature vector and the feature vector that interacts with the effect size as described in the subsequent section, and
\mathrm{sigmoid}(x)=\frac{1}{1+e^{-x}}
.

The individual’s intent to brush is modeled by the Bernoulli outcome
Z
and the individual’s brushing quality when they intend to brush is modeled by the Poisson outcome
S
. Notice that the models for
Z,S
means the effect size on
\mathcal{Y}
must be non-negative. Otherwise, not sending an engagement prompt could yield a higher immediate brushing quality than sending an engagement prompt, which is nonsensical in this oral health setting. We ensure that
\max\big[\Delta_{i,B}^{\top}h(X_{i,t}),0\big]
and
\max\big[\Delta_{i,N}^{\top}h(X_{i,t}),0\big]
are non-negative to prevent the effect size from switching signs and having a negative effect on brushing quality.

We jointly fit weights
w_{i,b},w_{i,p},\Delta_{i,B},\Delta_{i,N}
for both the Bernoulli and the Poisson components using each individual’s data. Specifically, we used MAP with prior
w_{i,b},w_{i,p},\Delta_{i,B},\Delta_{i,N}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I)
as a form of regularization because we have sparse data for each individual. Finalized weight values were chosen by running random restarts and selecting the weights with the highest log posterior density.

Responsivity to Actions.

We model delayed effects of actions by shrinking individuals’ responsiveness to actions, i.e., their initial treatment effect sizes. We shrink individuals’ effect sizes by a factor
E=0.5
when a certain criterion is met. The choice of
0.5
was chosen in discussion with domain experts. Specifically, the criterion if either of the two scenarios holds: (a)
\mathbb{I}[\bar{B}_{i,t}>b]
(individual brushes well) and
\mathbb{I}[\bar{A}_{i,t}>a_{1}]
(individual was sent too many engagement prompts for a healthy brusher), or (b)
\mathbb{I}[\bar{A}_{i,t}>a_{2}]
(the individual has been sent too many engagement prompts). See display (
58
) for definitions of
\bar{B}
and
\bar{A}
; we define the values of hyperparamters
b,a_{1},a_{2}
in the next section (under the subheading “Reward”). Notice that the same definitions and values of
\bar{B},\bar{A},b,a_{1},a_{2}
used in the environment models are the same as will be used the reward definition.

The first time an individual’s criterion has been met, the individual’s future effect sizes
\Delta_{i,B},\Delta_{i,N}
starting at time
t+1
will be scaled down proportionally by
E=0.5
(the individual is less responsive to treatment). Namely, the effect sizes will be shrunken to
E\cdot\Delta_{i,B},E\cdot\Delta_{i,N}
. Then after a week, at time
t+14
, we will check the criterion again. If the criterion is met again, the effect sizes will be further shrunk by a factor of
E
down to
E^{2}\cdot\Delta_{i,B},E^{2}\cdot\Delta_{i,N}
starting at time
t+15

\Delta_{i,B},\Delta_{i,N}
starting at time
t+15
.
This procedure continues until the individual finishes the study. Notice that this means the individual can only have their effect size shrunk at most once a week. This procedure simulates how the individual may experience a reduction in responsiveness, but after a week, if the RL algorithm does not intervene too much, the individual may recover their prior responsivity.

App Engagement Behavior.

We simulate individual app opening as follows. For each individual in the pilot study, we calculated
p^{\text{app}}_{i}

Then at the end of each day in the simulation study, we independently sample from a Bernoulli distribution with probability
p^{\text{app}}_{i}
for every individual

Reward.

The reward is a function of brushing quality
\mathcal{Y}_{i,t}
.
R(Y_{i,t}):=\mathcal{Y}_{i,t}-C_{i,t}
where
C_{i,t}
is a cost term that represents a crude approximation approximation of the delayed effects of actions. We now discuss in detail about the design of the cost term
C_{i,t}
. The inclusion of cost term
C_{i,t}
allows the bandit algorithm to optimize for immediate healthy brushing behavior, while simultaneously considering the delayed effect of the current action on the effectiveness of future actions. The cost term can be interpreted as a function (with parameters
\xi_{1},\xi_{2}
) which takes in current covariates
X_{i,t}
and action
A_{i,t}
and returns the delayed negative effect of currently sending an engagement prompt.

Recall in the time-varying covariate features, we defined
\bar{B}_{i,t}:=c_{\gamma}\sum_{j=1}^{14}\gamma^{j-1}\mathcal{Y}_{i,t-j}
and
\bar{A}_{i,t}:=c_{\gamma}\sum_{j=1}^{14}\gamma^{j-1}A_{i,t-j}
. Note that in the algorithm time-varying covariate features,
\bar{B},\bar{A}
are normalized, but in the cost term below they are not. We set
\gamma=\frac{13}{14}
to represent looking back 14 decision point points and scale each sum by constant
c_{\gamma}=\frac{1-\gamma}{1-\gamma^{14}}
so that the weights sum to 1. Notice that our choice of
\gamma
and the scaling constant means
0\leq\bar{B}_{i,t}\leq 180
and
0\leq\bar{A}_{i,t}\leq 1
.

\bar{B}_{i,t}
captures the individual’s exponentially discounted brushing quality in the past week.
\bar{A}_{i,t}
captures the number of actions that were sent over the past week.
Both terms are exponentially discounted because we expect that actions sent and individual brushing in the near past will be more predictive of the delayed impact of the actions (i.e., affecting a individual’s responsivity to future actions) than those in the further past.

We define the cost of sending an engagement prompt (i.e. captures individual burden in sending a prompt) as:

C_{i,t}:=\begin{cases}\xi_{1}\mathbb{I}[\bar{B}_{i,t}>b]\mathbb{I}[\bar{A}_{i,t}>a_{1}]&\\
\hskip 28.45274pt+\xi_{2}\mathbb{I}[\bar{A}_{i,t}>a_{2}]&\smash{\raisebox{6.88889pt}{if $A_{i,t}=1$}}\\
0&\hskip 0.0pt\mathrm{if~}A_{i,t}=0\end{cases}

(59)

Notice that the algorithm only incurs a cost if the current action is to send an action, i.e.,
A_{i,t}=1
. The first term
\xi_{1}\mathbb{I}[\bar{B}_{i,t}>b]\mathbb{I}[\bar{A}_{i,t}>a_{1}]
encapsulates the belief that if a high-performing individual was sent too many engagement prompts within the past week, then we want to penalize the reward. The second term
\xi_{2}\mathbb{I}[\bar{A}_{i,t}>a_{2}]
encapsulates the belief that regardless of individual performance, if they received too many engagement prompts within the past week, then we also want to penalize the reward.
b,a_{1},a_{2}
are chosen by domain experts. Notice that
a_{1}<a_{2}
because we believe a high-performing individual will have a lower threshold of being burdened by an engagement prompt. The scientific team decided to set the following values:
