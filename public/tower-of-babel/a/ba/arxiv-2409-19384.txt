Abstract
. This is an introduction to Hall algebras from the perspective of 2 2 -Segal spaces or decomposition spaces, as introduced by Dyckerhoff and Kapranov and Gálvez-Carrillo, Kock and Tonks, respectively. We explain how linearizations of the 2 2 -Segal space arising as the Waldhausen 𝒮 ∙ \mathcal{S}_{\bullet} -construction of a proto-exact category recover various previously known Hall algebras. We use the 2 2 -Segal perspective to study functoriality of the Hall algebra construction and explain how relative variants of 2 2 -Segal spaces lead naturally to representations of Hall algebras.

Introduction

A heroic mathematician once explained to the authors that a Hall algebra is
“something like a quantum group.” Here we present a different
perspective: a Hall algebra is “a linearization of a
2
-Segal space.”
Realizing the hero’s interpretation can be hard work, as speaking precisely about Hall algebras
necessitates many minor variations and technicalities. Their interpretation is often
justified by the observation that one can tell the story in such a way so as
to conclude with an intrinsic construction of a quantum group. On the
other hand, a wimpy mathematician who defies a litany of technical hurdles
through abstraction, may miss the trees for the forest, but sometimes gains
new and often valuable insights by having chosen a different path.

2
-Segal spaces were introduced by Dyckerhoff and Kapranov in

[
DK19
]
and decomposition spaces by Gálvez-Carrillo, Kock
and Tonks in
[
GCKT18a
]
. These notions are equivalent, something
unsurprising considering that, in each case, the authors made their
discoveries through a careful study of the associativity relation which is
satisfied by Hall algebras and incidence algebras, respectively.
The connection between Hall algebras and
2
-Segal spaces occurs when the
2
-Segal space arises from Waldhausen’s
\mathcal{S}_{\bullet}
-construction.

For a suitable category
\mathcal{C}
, the

\mathcal{S}_{\bullet}
-construction is—roughly speaking—a simplicial object
\mathcal{S}_{\bullet}(\mathcal{C})
,
whose
n
-simplices correspond to composition series of length
n
in

\mathcal{C}

[
Wal85
]
. The homotopy groups of the corresponding geometric realization agree
with the algebraic
K
-theory of
\mathcal{C}
:

K_{n}(\mathcal{C})\simeq\pi_{n+1}|\mathcal{S}_{\bullet}(\mathcal{C})|.

For Waldhausen, an important goal was to show that a broad collection of
categories
\mathcal{C}
, now known as Waldhausen categories, could be considered
suitable, thereby extending Quillen’s definition of algebraic
K
-theory to a much
wider setting. For our purposes, the crucial
observation of the aforementioned authors is that the simplicial object

\mathcal{S}_{\bullet}(\mathcal{C})
is a
2
-Segal space. Waldhausen’s

\mathcal{S}_{\bullet}
-construction, once equipped with the
2
-Segal structure,
contains in its shadow the Hall algebra and its friends.

The story of Hall algebras can be told in a way which
allows these
2
-Segal structures to appear organically. The Hall
algebra of a suitable category
\mathcal{C}
is a ring which is
spanned by the isomorphism classes of objects in
\mathcal{C}
together with a
product which is determined by the combinatorics of extensions between these
objects. The first Hall algebra, discovered independently by Steinitz
[
Ste01
]
and Hall

[
Hal59
]
, arises when
\mathcal{C}
is the category of finite
p
-abelian groups (
p
prime). Steinitz and Hall interpreted this
Hall algebra in terms of the ring of symmetric functions. Perhaps the
most common definition of Hall algebras found in the literature today,
introduced by Ringel
[
Rin90a
]
, concerns abelian categories
\mathcal{C}

with finiteness properties similar to those of the category of
p
-abelian
groups. Ringel’s main examples concerned quivers
Q
and their abelian categories of finite dimensional representations
\mathsf{rep}_{\mathbb{F}_{q}}(Q)
over a finite field
\mathbb{F}_{q}
. It was known from Gabriel’s theorem
[
Gab72
]
that, for
connected quivers
Q
, the category
\mathsf{rep}_{\mathbb{F}_{q}}(Q)
has finitely many isomorphism classes of indecomposable objects if and only if the underlying graph of
Q
is a Dynkin diagram from the list
A_{n}
,
D_{n}
or
E_{6}
,
E_{7}
,
E_{8}
.
In each case, Ringel identified the Hall algebra of
\mathcal{C}=\mathsf{rep}_{\mathbb{F}_{q}}(Q)
with the positive part of the quantum
group associated to
Q

[
Rin90b
]
:

H(\mathsf{rep}_{\mathbb{F}_{q}}(Q))\simeq U_{\sqrt{q}}^{+}(\mathfrak{g}_{Q}).

This construction established the Hall algebra as a central object in the field of geometric representation theory. Later, Green introduced a coproduct on the Hall algebra and extended Ringel’s isomorphism to one of bialgebras
[
Gre95
]
. Moreover, taking
Q
to be the Jordan (one loop) quiver recovers the original Hall algebra of Steinitz and Hall. In his study of quantum groups, Lusztig built magnificently on
these ideas, viewing the Hall algebra as a convolution algebra of
locally constant functions on the moduli stack of the objects of
\mathcal{C}

[
Lus10
]
. These ideas were also central to Nakajima’s construction of representations of quantum affine algebras
[
Nak01
]
.

The convolution algebra incarnation of the Hall algebra sees only very
coarsely the structure of the moduli stacks involved in its
definition. Barwick proved that the Waldhausen

\mathcal{S}_{\bullet}
-construction
\mathcal{S}_{\bullet}(\mathcal{C})
occurs as the
Goodwillie derivative of the functor which associates to
\mathcal{C}

its moduli space of objects
[
Bar16
]
. From the perspective of this
paper, the central observation of Dyckerhoff–Kapranov and
Gálvez-Carrillo–Kock–Tonks is that the Waldhausen

\mathcal{S}_{\bullet}
-construction, seen as a
2
-Segal space, suffices to
encode the Hall algebra. The geometric definition of Lusztig factors through the
\mathcal{S}_{\bullet}
-construction, as evidenced by
Section
2
, which produces the Hall algebra of Ringel as a linearization of the
2
-Segal space
associated to the category.

That the ideas of Steinitz, 123 years ago, have a home within an additive
linearization of a Goodwillie derivative of the most complicated object
(moduli stack of objects) that we can associate to the simplest non-trivial
object (the Jordan quiver) is a heroic novelty of sorts.

What is included in this article

Section
1
contains brief accounts of
2
-Segal
spaces and the Waldhausen
\mathcal{S}_{\bullet}
-construction. We work in the generality of proto-exact categories, a non-additive generalization of exact categories which allows for the realization of Hall algebras of so-called
\mathbb{F}_{1}
-linear categories. Section
2
constructs from a
2
-Segal space
X_{\bullet}
an associative algebra object in spans. Various linearizations are then discussed and it is explained, via examples, how this includes a number of Hall algebras which appear in the literature. This includes Ringel’s Hall algebra of
\mathbb{F}_{q}
-linear abelian categories, their
\mathbb{F}_{1}
-analogues introduced by Szczesny and the cohomological and motivic Hall algebras of Kontsevich–Soibelman and Joyce.
The remainder of the article is concerned with using
2
-Segal spaces as an organizational tool for the study of Hall algebras. Section
3
contains a discussion of the functoriality of the Hall algebra construction while Section
4
explains the role of relative variants of
2
-Segal spaces in the representation theory of Hall
algebras, two topics for which this abstraction yields constructive
approaches.

What is not included in this article

Being a narrow discussion of a subject containing many technicalities, this article

Sch12a
]
, who

, who takes the
2
-Segal

]
. The role of Hall algebras in geometric representation theory is discussed only indirectly; see
[
Sch12b
]
for an introduction in this direction. Similarly, we do not discuss higher categorical generalizations of Hall algebras, such as Hall monoidal categories or algebra objects in the
(\infty,2)
-category of spans

Dyc18

DK19
]
. We do not discuss other ideas which interface the
2
-Segal/decomposition space perspectives of the articles

DK19

1.
Recollections on

We recall the definition of
2
-Segal spaces in a form convenient for applications to Hall algebras. The theory of
2

[

]
. See

1.1.
2
-Segal spaces

Let
\Delta
be the category of non-empty finite ordinals and their monotone maps. We often use without mention the canonical equivalence of
\Delta
with its full subcategory on the standard ordinals
[n]=\{0,\dots,n\}
,
n\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
.

Let
\mathscr{S}
be a Quillen model category. We refer to homotopy pullbacks and pushouts simply as pullbacks and pushouts, respectively, and similarly for Cartesian and coCartesian diagrams. We write
pt
for the final object of
\mathscr{S}
. The primary example to keep in mind is the model category of small groupoids, in which weak equivalences are equivalences of categories and cofibrations are functors which are injective on objects. With minor changes,
\mathscr{S}
can also be taken to be an
\infty
-category with finite limits.

As a warm up and for later reference, we recall the definition of a
1
-Segal space.

Definition 1.1

(
[
Rez01
]
)
.

A simplicial object
X_{\bullet}:\Delta^{\textup{\text{op}}}\rightarrow\mathscr{S}
is
1
-Segal
if, for every

and

, the square

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X_{n}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X_{\{i,\dots,n\}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X_{\{0,\dots,i\}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X_{\{i\}}}

is Cartesian.

In the above diagram, we have written
X_{n}
for
X_{[n]}
and all morphisms are induced by the obvious inclusions of ordinals. Similar comments apply in what follows.

Definition 1.2

(
[
DK19
]
)
.

A simplicial object
X_{\bullet}:\Delta^{\textup{\text{op}}}\rightarrow\mathscr{S}
is
2
-Segal
if, for every
n\geq 3
and
0\leq i<j\leq n
, the square

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X_{n}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X_{\{i,\dots,j\}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X_{\{0,\dots,i,j,\dots,n\}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X_{\{i,j\}}}

is Cartesian.

Definition 1.3

(
[
DK19
]
)
.

A
2
-Segal simplicial object

is

and

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X_{n-1}}

1.2.
Proto-exact categories

In order to treat various flavours of Hall algebras which appear in the literature, we work with the following non-additive generalization of Quillen’s exact categories
[

]

Definition 1.5

(
[

)

A

(i)

and the morphism

is in
\mathfrak{D}
.

(ii)

Each class
\mathfrak{I}
and

is closed under composition and contains all isomorphisms.

(iii)

A commutative square of the form

(1.1)

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces U}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces V}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces W}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X}

is a Cartesian if and only if it is coCartesian.

(iv)

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces V}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces W}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X}

can be completed to a biCartesian square of the form (

(v)

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces U}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces V}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces W}

Examples of proto-exact categories include abelian categories, where

are the monomorphisms and epimorphisms, respectively, and exact categories, where

Example 1.6

(
[

]
)
.

The

2.3.
Hall algebras of finitary categories

We specialize the results of Section

to recover the theory of Hall algebras, as formulated by Ringel

Given a small groupoid
\mathcal{A}
, let
\ell(\mathcal{A})=\textup{\text{Fun}}_{c}(\pi_{0}\mathcal{A},\mathbb{C})
be the vector space of finitely supported complex valued functions on the set
\pi_{0}\mathcal{A}
of isomorphism classes of objects of
\mathcal{A}
. Let
\mathsf{Sm}
consist of the weakly proper morphisms, that is, morphisms of groupoids

for which the induced map
\pi_{0}f:\pi_{0}\mathcal{A}\rightarrow\pi_{0}\mathcal{B}
is finite-to-one. In this case, there is an obvious pullback morphism
f^{*}:\ell(\mathcal{B})\rightarrow\ell(\mathcal{A})
. Let
\mathsf{P}
consist of the locally proper morphisms, that is, morphisms of groupoids
f:\mathcal{A}\rightarrow\mathcal{B}
for which, for each
a\in\mathcal{A}

f:\textup{\text{Aut}}_{\mathcal{A}}(a)\rightarrow\textup{\text{Aut}}_{\mathcal{B}}(f(a))
has finite kernel and cokernel. In this case, there is a pushforward

given by

(f_{*}\phi)(b)=\int_{Rf^{-1}(b)}\phi_{|Rf^{-1}(b)},

where
Rf^{-1}(b)
denotes the homotopy fibre of
f
over
b\in\mathcal{B}
. Here, for a small groupoid
\mathcal{G}
for which each group
\textup{\text{Aut}}_{\mathcal{G}}(x)

, is finite with function

\int_{\mathcal{G}}\phi=\sum_{x\in\pi_{0}\mathcal{G}}\frac{1}{|\textup{\text{Aut}}_{\mathcal{G}}(x)|}\phi(x).

This data defines a
\mathsf{V}\mathsf{ect}_{\mathbb{C}}

Lemma 2.5
.

(1)

The morphism
(\partial_{2},\partial_{0})
is weakly proper, that is,
(\partial_{2},\partial_{0})\in\mathsf{Sm}
.

(2)

The morphism
\partial_{1}
is locally proper, that is,
\partial_{1}\in\mathsf{P}
.

Proof.

(1)

This follows from the assumption that each set
\textup{\text{Hom}}_{\mathcal{C}}(W,U)
is finite.

(2)

This follows from the assumption that each set
\textup{\text{Ext}}^{1}_{\mathcal{C}}(W,U)
is finite. ∎

It follows from Theorem
2.3
that there is a unital associative algebra

H(\mathcal{C}):=H(\mathcal{S}_{\bullet}(\mathcal{C});\ell).

The algebra
H(\mathcal{C})
is the
Hall algebra of
\mathcal{C}
, originally defined by Ringel
[
Rin90a
]
. The vector space
H(\mathcal{C})
has a canonical basis
\{1_{U}\}_{U\in\pi_{0}\mathcal{C}}
labelled by isomorphism classes of objects of
\mathcal{C}
in which the multiplication reads

1_{U}\cdot 1_{W}=\sum_{V\in\pi_{0}\mathcal{C}}c_{U,W}^{V}1_{V}.

Here
c_{U,W}^{V}
is the cardinality of the set

\{U^{\prime}\subset V\mid U^{\prime}\simeq U,\;V/\penalty U^{\prime}\simeq W\}.

Alternatively, we can write

c_{U,W}^{V}=\frac{|\textup{\text{Aut}}_{\mathcal{C}}(V)|}{|\textup{\text{Aut}}_{\mathcal{C}}(U)||\textup{\text{Aut}}_{\mathcal{C}}(W)|}\frac{|\textup{\text{Ext}}^{1}_{\mathcal{C}}(W,U)_{V}|}{|\textup{\text{Hom}}_{\mathcal{C}}(W,U)|},

where
\textup{\text{Ext}}^{1}_{\mathcal{C}}(W,U)_{V}\subset\textup{\text{Ext}}^{1}_{\mathcal{C}}(W,U)
denotes the subset of extensions (seen as isomorphism classes of conflations) whose middle term is isomorphic to
V
. In this form, it is apparent that the finitary assumption on
\mathcal{C}
ensures that
c_{U,W}^{V}
is finite and, for fixed
U,W\in\pi_{0}\mathcal{C}
, is non-zero for only finitely many
V\in\pi_{0}\mathcal{C}
.

We work out various instances of the above construction. Let
\mathbb{F}_{q}
be a finite field of cardinality
q
.

Example 2.6
.

Let
\mathsf{vect}_{\mathbb{F}_{q}}
be the finitary abelian category of finite dimensional vector spaces over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
. The Hall algebra

H(\mathsf{vect}_{\mathbb{F}_{q}})\simeq\bigoplus_{n\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}}\mathbb{C}\cdot 1_{n}

has multiplication determined by

1_{n}\cdot 1_{m}=\left[\begin{matrix}n+m\\
n\end{matrix}\right]_{q}1_{n+m},

where our conventions for quantum integers read

[n]_{q}=\frac{q^{n}-1}{q-1},\qquad[n]_{q}!=\prod_{i=1}^{n}[i]_{q}\qquad\left[\begin{matrix}n+m\\
n\end{matrix}\right]_{q}=\frac{[n+m]_{q}!}{[n]_{q}![m]_{q}!}.

The structure constant
\left[\begin{smallmatrix}n+m\\
n\end{smallmatrix}\right]_{q}
is the number of
\mathbb{F}_{q}
-rational points of the Grassmannian
\textnormal{Gr}(n,n+m)
.

Example 2.7
.

Let
\mathbb{F}_{q}[x]{\text{-}}\mathsf{mod}^{\mathsf{nil}}
be the finitary abelian category of finite dimensional nilpotent
\mathbb{F}_{q}[x]
-modules. Jordan canonical form induces a bijection from the set of isomorphism classes of objects of
\mathbb{F}_{q}[x]{\text{-}}\mathsf{mod}^{\mathsf{nil}}
to the set
\mathcal{P}
of partitions, so that we may identify

H(\mathbb{F}_{q}[x]{\text{-}}\mathsf{mod}^{\mathsf{nil}})\simeq\bigoplus_{\lambda\in\mathcal{P}}\mathbb{C}\cdot 1_{\lambda}.

The structure constants
c_{\lambda,\mu}^{\nu}
are rather complicated and do not admit a closed form expression. However, studying these coefficients when
\mu
is of the form
(1^{r})
allows one to prove that
H(\mathbb{F}_{q}[x]{\text{-}}\mathsf{mod}^{\mathsf{nil}})
is a polynomial algebra on the countably infinite variables
1_{(1^{r})}
,
r\geq 1
.
Using this, it is not difficult to show that
H(\mathbb{F}_{q}[x]{\text{-}}\mathsf{mod}^{\mathsf{nil}})
is isomorphic to Macdonald’s ring of symmetric functions
[
Mac95
]
, an important algebra in combinatorics.

Taking
q
to be a prime
p
, the algebra
H(\mathbb{F}_{p}[x]{\text{-}}\mathsf{mod}^{\mathsf{nil}})
is isomorphic to the original Hall algebra, introduced by Steinitz in the context of the combinatorics of the lattice of finite
p
-abelian groups
[
Ste01
]
.

Example 2.8

(
[
Rin90b
,
Gre95
]
)
.

Let
Q
be an acyclic quiver, that is, a finite oriented multigraph without cycles. Let
\mathsf{rep}_{\mathbb{F}_{q}}(Q)
be the category of finite dimensional representations of
Q
over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
. Concretely, a representation of
Q
is the data of a finite dimensional vector space

over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
for each vertex

of

and a
\mathbb{F}_{q}
-linear map
V_{i}\rightarrow V_{j}
for each arrow

of

. The category
\mathcal{C}
is finitary abelian. Denote by
S_{i}
the one dimensional representation which is supported only at the vertex
i
. Any simple object of
\mathcal{C}
is isomorphic to a unique
S_{i}
.

Let
\mathfrak{g}_{Q}
be the complex generalized Kac̆–Moody algebra whose generalized Dynkin diagram is the underlying multigraph of
Q
. There is a bijection between the vertices of
Q
and the set
\Pi^{+}
of positive simple roots of
\mathfrak{g}_{Q}
. Let
U_{v}(\mathfrak{g}_{Q})
be the De Concini–Kač quantum group at quantum parameter
v\in\mathbb{C}^{\times}
. The algebra
U_{v}(\mathfrak{g}_{Q})
has a standard presentation by generators and relations; Chevalley generators
E_{i},F_{i}
,
i\in\Pi^{+}
, and group-like Cartan generators
K^{\pm 1}_{\alpha}
,
\alpha
a simple coroot. Let
U_{v}^{+}(\mathfrak{g}_{Q})
be the subalgebra of
U_{v}(\mathfrak{g}_{Q})
generated by
E_{i}
; its only relations are the quantum Serre relations.

It was proved by Ringel and Green that the assignment
E_{i}\mapsto 1_{S_{i}}
extends to an algebra embedding

U_{\sqrt{q}}^{+}(\mathfrak{g}_{Q})\hookrightarrow H(\mathsf{rep}_{\mathbb{F}_{q}}(Q))

which is an isomorphism if and only if

is of finite representation type, that is,
Q
is an orientation of a simply laced Dynkin diagram
[
Rin90b
,
Gre95
]
. The algebra
H(\mathsf{rep}_{\mathbb{F}_{q}}(Q))
itself is in fact a quantization of a
q
-dependent Borcherds algebra
[
SVdB01
]
. For a general discussion of the relationship between Hall algebras of finitary exact categories and quantum groups, see
[
BG16
]
.

More generally, if
Q
has cycles (including loops), one can consider the category of representations of
Q
with nilpotency conditions on morphisms associated to cycles. For example, the category
\mathsf{rep}^{\mathsf{nil}}_{\mathbb{F}_{q}}(Q_{\text{{Jor}}})
of nilpotent representations of the Jordan quiver

(a single vertex with a loop) is equivalent to the category
\mathbb{F}_{q}[x]{\text{-}}\mathsf{mod}^{\mathsf{nil}}
considered in Example

. In this way, one can expand the class of generalized quantum Kac̆–Moody algebras realized by Hall algebras
[
KS06
]
.

The theory of Hall algebras of quivers is rich and interesting. A detailed introduction can be found in
[
Sch12a
]
.

Example 2.9

(
[
Kap97
]
)
.

Let
X
be a smooth projective irreducible curve over a finite field
\mathbb{F}_{q}
. The category
\mathsf{coh}(X)
of coherent sheaves on
X
is finitary abelian and hence has an associated Hall algebra
H(X):=H(\mathsf{coh}(X))
.

The case of
X=\mathbb{P}^{1}
, the projective line over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
, was studied by Kapranov
[
Kap97
]
. See
[
BK01
]
for an expository account. To explain the main result, note that the full subcategory
\mathsf{tor}(\mathbb{P}^{1})\subset\mathsf{coh}(\mathbb{P}^{1})
of torsion sheaves is abelian and closed under extensions. Using functoriality of the Hall algebra construction, as discussed in Section
3
, this translates to define a subalgebra
H(\mathsf{tor}(\mathbb{P}^{1}))
of
H(\mathbb{P}^{1})
. Consideration of the support of a torsion sheaf leads to a decomposition

\mathsf{tor}(\mathbb{P}^{1})=\bigoplus_{x\in\mathbb{P}^{1}}\mathsf{tor}(\mathbb{P}^{1})_{x}

labelled by the closed points of
\mathbb{P}^{1}
and hence an isomorphism of algebras

H(\mathsf{tor}(\mathbb{P}^{1}))\simeq\bigotimes_{x\in\mathbb{P}^{1}}H(\mathsf{tor}(\mathbb{P}^{1})_{x}).

The abelian equivalence
\mathsf{tor}(\mathbb{P}^{1})_{x}\simeq\mathsf{rep}^{\mathsf{nil}}_{\mathbb{F}_{q^{\deg x}}}(Q_{\text{{Jor}}})
yields, via Example
2.7
, a description of
H(\mathsf{tor}(\mathbb{P}^{1}))
in terms of algebras of symmetric functions. Similarly, the subcategory
\mathsf{vect}(\mathbb{P}^{1})\subset\mathsf{coh}(\mathbb{P}^{1})
of vector bundles and their morphisms of constant rank is exact and closed under extensions. The resulting subalgebra
H(\mathsf{vect}(\mathbb{P}^{1}))
of

can be interpreted as the algebra of unramified automorphic forms for the groups
GL_{n}
,
n\geq 0
, with parabolic induction as the product. Finally, that the pair
(\mathsf{tor}(\mathbb{P}^{1}),\mathsf{vect}(\mathbb{P}^{1}))
is a torsion theory for
\mathsf{coh}(\mathbb{P}^{1})
translates to the statement that
H(\mathbb{P}^{1})
is a semi-direct product of
H(\mathsf{tor}(\mathbb{P}^{1}))
and
H(\mathsf{vect}(\mathbb{P}^{1}))
with respect to the Hecke action of the former on the latter. By taking a subalgebra
\tilde{H}(\mathsf{tor}(\mathbb{P}^{1}))
of
H(\mathsf{tor}(\mathbb{P}^{1}))
obtained as a suitable average of
H(\mathsf{tor}(\mathbb{P}^{1}))_{x}
,

, it is proved that there is an algebra isomorphism

\tilde{H}(\mathsf{tor}(\mathbb{P}^{1}))\rtimes H(\mathsf{vect}(\mathbb{P}^{1}))\simeq U_{\sqrt{q}}^{D,+}(\widehat{\mathfrak{sl}_{2}(\mathbb{C})})

and hence a non-surjective algebra embedding

U_{\sqrt{q}}^{D,+}(\widehat{\mathfrak{sl}_{2}(\mathbb{C})})\hookrightarrow H(\mathbb{P}^{1}).

Here
U_{\sqrt{q}}^{D,+}(\widehat{\mathfrak{sl}_{2}(\mathbb{C})})
denotes the positive part of the quantum affine algebra
U_{v}(\widehat{\mathfrak{sl}_{2}(\mathbb{C})})
in Drinfeld’s new realization
[
Dri87
]
, specialized to
v=\sqrt{q}
. This realization is particularly natural when
\widehat{\mathfrak{sl}_{2}(\mathbb{C})}
is constructed from the loop algebra of
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}(\mathbb{C})
.

On the other hand, denoting by

the Kronecker quiver (vertex set
\{1,2\}
with two arrows from

to

contains as a subalgebra the positive part of the standard Chevalley presentation of

. To explain this connection, note that while the abelian categories

and

are not equivalent—the latter is finite length, the former is not—Beilinson proved that there is an equivalence of their derived categories

D^{b}(\mathsf{coh}(\mathbb{P}^{1}_{/\penalty\mathbb{F}_{q}}))\simeq D^{b}(\mathsf{rep}_{\mathbb{F}_{q}}(K_{2})).

This leads to the idea that there should exist a derived generalization of the Hall algebra construction which, when applied to the category (

, whereas the hearts

and

realize only (non-conjugate) positive parts thereof. See

Hall algebras of elliptic curves have also been studied in detail; see

]
.

Remark 2.10
.

In many of the examples above, the Hall algebras arise from
\mathbb{F}_{q}
-reductions of a category
\mathcal{C}_{\mathbb{Z}}
defined over
\mathbb{Z}
. What is often called the Hall algebra is an algebra
A
over the Laurent polynomial ring which interpolates between the reduction:

H(\mathcal{C}_{\mathbb{Z}}\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}\mathbb{F}_{q})\simeq A\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}[q,q^{-1}]}\mathbb{C}.

One important subtlety in the subject is that the existence of such an algebra
A
is not guaranteed.

We turn to examples of Hall algebras arising from non-exact categories.

Example 2.11
.

The proto-exact category
\mathsf{vect}_{\mathbb{F}_{1}}
of finite dimensional vector spaces over
\mathbb{F}_{1}
, as defined in Example
1.6
, is finitary. The Hall algebra

H(\mathsf{vect}_{\mathbb{F}_{1}})\simeq\bigoplus_{n\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}}\mathbb{C}\cdot 1_{n}

has multiplication

1_{n}\cdot 1_{m}=\left(\begin{matrix}n+m\\
n\end{matrix}\right)1_{n+m}.

This algebra is sometimes called the
divided power algebra
because of the identification with the integral subring
\mathbb{Z}\langle\frac{x^{n}}{n!}:n\geq 0\rangle\subset\mathbb{C}[x]
. The structure constant
\left(\begin{smallmatrix}n+m\\
n\end{smallmatrix}\right)
is the Euler characteristic of the complex Grassmannian
\mbox{Gr}(n,n+m)
. In particular, this example is a
q\rightarrow 1
limit of Example
2.6
in a rather obvious sense.

Example 2.12

(
[
Szc12
]
)

3.
Functoriality between Hall algebras

One advantage of using

algebras is that clearer statements concerning functorality of the Hall algebra construction can be

to questions of functoriality, providing some details and highlights which
may be missing, but otherwise sourcing existent literature.
The results in this section were understood by some of the experts at the conference which gave rise to this collection of surveys. This section was added because the statements were not contained the literature at the time.
A summary of statements pertaining to Hall (co)algebras is found in Theorem

ELY22a

Example 4.4
.

We consider a symplectic counterpart of Example
2.6
. Give
\mathsf{vect}_{\mathbb{F}_{q}}
the standard
\mathbb{F}_{q}
-linear duality functor
P=\textup{\text{Hom}}_{\mathbb{F}_{q}}(-,\mathbb{F}_{q})
and double dual isomorphism
\Theta_{U}=-\text{{ev}}_{U}
the negative of the canonical evaluation isomorphism. Symmetric forms are then symplectic vector spaces. The resulting Hall module is

M(\mathsf{vect}_{\mathbb{F}_{q}})=\bigoplus_{m=0}^{\infty}\mathbb{C}\cdot 1_{2m}^{P},

where
1_{2m}^{P}
denotes the characteristic function of the symplectic vector space of dimension
2m
. The module structure is

1_{n}\star 1_{2m}^{P}=q^{\frac{n(n+1)}{2}}\left[\begin{matrix}n+m\\
n\end{matrix}\right]_{q}1^{P}_{n+m},

where now the structure constant counts the number of
\mathbb{F}_{q}
-rational points of the isotropic Grassmannian
\textnormal{IGr}(n,2(m+n))
.

Taking instead
\Theta_{U}=\text{{ev}}_{U}
results in symmetric forms which are orthogonal vector spaces. The resulting Hall module
M(\mathsf{vect}_{\mathbb{F}_{q}})
is a direct sum of
H(\mathsf{vect}_{\mathbb{F}_{q}})
-modules labelled by the Witt group, that is, the parity of the dimension of orthogonal vector spaces, and has structure constants which count
\mathbb{F}_{q}
-rational points of orthogonal Grassmannians.

(
[

)
.

We consider a relative counterpart of Example
2.8
. Let
\sigma
be an anti-involution of a quiver
Q
, that is,
\sigma
is self-bijections of the sets of vertices and arrows of
Q

\alpha:i\rightarrow j
is an arrow in

, then so too is
\sigma(\alpha):\sigma(j)\rightarrow\sigma(i)
. Define a duality structure on
\mathsf{rep}_{\mathbb{F}_{q}}(Q)
by

, where

denotes vertex-wise
\mathbb{F}_{q}
-linear duality and
\Theta
is the vertex-wise evaluation isomorphism. Symmetric forms in
\mathsf{rep}_{\mathbb{F}_{q}}(Q)
are orthogonal counterparts of quiver representations. See
[

]

Q
. By incorporating the coalgebra and comodule structures of
H(Q)
and

(the latter has been neglected in this article), one checks that the

-module

\sigma
-analogue
of the quantum group
U_{\sqrt{q}}(\mathfrak{g}_{Q})

[
EK08
]
. With this interpretation,

decomposes into highest weight modules labelled by the cuspidal elements of

Example 4.6

(
[
You20
]
)
.

We consider a relative counterpart of Section
2.4
. Give
\mathsf{rep}_{\mathbb{C}}(Q)
a duality structure as in Example
4.5
. The resulting
cohomological Hall module

M(Q)=M(\mathcal{R}_{\bullet}(\mathsf{rep}_{\mathbb{C}}(Q));H^{\bullet}(-;\mathbb{Q}))
plays a central role in the orientifold Donaldson–Thomas theory of
Q
, that is, the mixed orthogonal/symplectic generalization of Donaldson–Thomas theory
[
You15
,
You20
]
. Again, we restrict attention to the
m
-loop quiver
Q_{m}
. Fix the duality
P=\textup{\text{Hom}}_{\mathbb{C}}(-,\mathbb{C})
and double dual isomorphism
\Theta=-\text{{ev}}
. There is an isomorphism of stacks

\mathcal{R}_{0}(\mathsf{rep}_{\mathbb{C}}(Q_{m}))\simeq\bigsqcup_{e=0}^{\infty}[\mathfrak{sp}_{2e}(\mathbb{C})^{\oplus m}/\penalty\textnormal{Sp}_{2e}(\mathbb{C})]

so that the underlying
\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{Z}
-graded vector space of
M(Q_{m})
is

M(Q_{m})=\bigoplus_{e=0}^{\infty}M_{2e}(Q_{m}),

where

M_{2e}(Q_{m})\simeq H^{\bullet}(B\textnormal{Sp}_{2e}(\mathbb{C}))\simeq\mathbb{Q}[z_{1}^{2},\dots,z_{e}^{2}]^{\mathfrak{S}_{e}},\qquad|z_{i}|=2.

Keeping the notation of Section
2.4
, the action of
f\in H_{d}(Q_{m})
on
g\in M_{2e}(Q_{m})
is

\displaystyle f\star g=2^{(m-1)d}\sum_{\pi\in\mathfrak{sh}_{d,e}^{\sigma}}\pi\Big[

\displaystyle f(x_{1},\dots,x_{d})g(z_{1},\dots,z_{e})\times

\displaystyle\Big(\prod_{i=1}^{d}(-x_{i})\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq d}(-x_{i}-x_{j})\prod_{i=1}^{d}\prod_{j=1}^{e}(x_{i}^{2}-z_{j}^{2})\Big)^{m-1}\Big],

where
\mathfrak{sh}^{\sigma}_{d,e}=\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{d}\times\mathfrak{sh}_{d,e}
is the set of signed-shuffles of type
(d,e)
. There is an analogue of Efimov’s theorem in this relative setting. Namely, define a
2\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}\times\mathbb{Z}
-graded vector space by

W_{Q_{m}}^{\textnormal{prim}}=M(Q_{M})/\penalty(H(Q_{m})_{+}\star M(Q_{m})),

where
H(Q_{m})_{+}
is the augmentation ideal of
H(Q_{m})
, and pick a graded splitting so as to view
W^{\textnormal{prim}}_{Q_{m}}
as a subspace of
M(Q_{m})
. The vector space
W_{Q_{m}}^{\textnormal{prim}}
is the
cohomological orientifold Donaldson–Thomas invariant of
Q
. Then, for each

, there is a
\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}\times\mathbb{Z}
-graded subalgebra
H(Q_{m})(e)\subset H(Q_{m})
such that the action map

(4.4)

\star:\bigoplus_{e\in 2\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}}H(Q_{m})(e)\otimes_{\mathbb{Q}}W^{\textnormal{prim}}_{Q_{m},e}\rightarrow M(Q_{m})

is an isomorphism of graded vector spaces. Moreover,

can be described with sufficient understanding of
V_{Q_{m}}^{\textnormal{prim}}
. This isomorphism can be used to inductively determine
W^{\textnormal{prim}}_{Q_{m},e}
. While the isomorphism (
4.4
) remains conjectural for general symmetric quivers, it is known that

is isomorphic to the Chow group of the moduli space of stable symmetric representations of

[
FY18
]
.

4.3.
Hall modules via framing

Example 4.8
.

We work with the stable framed
\mathcal{S}_{\bullet}
-construction of Example
4.2
. Subject to smoothness and properness conditions, for each phase
\phi\in[0,1)
there results a module
M^{\textnormal{st}\mathchar 45\relax\textnormal{fr}}_{\phi}(\mathcal{C}):=M(\mathcal{S}_{\bullet}^{\textnormal{st}\mathchar 45\relax\textnormal{fr}}(\mathcal{C}^{\textnormal{ss}}_{\phi});\ell)
over the Hall algebra
H^{\textnormal{ss}}_{\phi}(\mathcal{C}):=H(\mathcal{S}_{\bullet}(\mathcal{C}^{\textnormal{ss}}_{\phi});\ell)
.

For example, let
\mathcal{C}=\mathsf{rep}_{\mathbb{C}}(Q)
. Sending a representation to its dimension vector defines a surjective group homomorphism
K_{0}(\mathsf{rep}_{\mathbb{C}}(Q))\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}^{Q_{0}}
. A tuple
\zeta=(\zeta_{i})_{i\in Q_{0}}\in\mathbb{H}_{+}^{Q_{0}}
defines a function
\mathbb{Z}^{Q_{0}}\rightarrow\mathbb{C},d\mapsto\sum_{i\in Q_{0}}\zeta_{i}d_{i}
, which induces a stability function
Z
on
\mathsf{rep}_{\mathbb{C}}(Q)
. For any
f\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{Q_{0}}
, the functor
\Phi:U\mapsto\bigoplus_{i\in Q_{0}}\textup{\text{Hom}}_{\mathbb{C}}(\mathbb{C}^{f_{i}},U_{i})
is a framing. Working in the context of Section
2.4
and Example
4.6
, the resulting cohomological
H^{\textnormal{ss}}_{\phi}(Q)
-modules
M^{\textnormal{st}\mathchar 45\relax\textnormal{fr}}_{\phi}(Q)
play an important role in Donaldson–Thomas theory
[
Soi16
,
Fra16
]
. With knowledge of the structure of the algebra
H^{\textnormal{ss}}_{\phi}(Q)
, for example as described in Section
2.4
, the module
M^{\textnormal{st}\mathchar 45\relax\textnormal{fr}}_{\phi}(Q)
can be used to inductively determine topological invariants of the smooth moduli space of stable framed representations of
Q
, also known as non-commutative Hilbert schemes. From a different perspective,
M^{\textnormal{st}\mathchar 45\relax\textnormal{fr}}_{\phi}(Q)
can be seen to approximate
H^{\textnormal{ss}}_{\phi}(Q)
in the limit
f\rightarrow\infty
. This has the technical benefit that, for sufficiently large
f
, the underlying vector space of
M^{\textnormal{st}\mathchar 45\relax\textnormal{fr}}_{\phi}(Q)
is the cohomology of a smooth variety, as opposed to a stack. This idea is used to great effect to study
H^{\textnormal{ss}}_{\phi}(Q)
in
[
DM20
]
. Similar ideas can be applied in the context of moduli spaces of sheaves over a smooth projective curve
[
MR15
]
or surface
[
DPS22
]
.
