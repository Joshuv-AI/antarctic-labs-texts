Spectral instability of peakons in the b b -family of the Camassa–Holm equations
Stéphane Lafortune, Dmitry E. Pelinovsky

Abstract
. We prove spectral instability of peakons in the b b -family of Camassa–Holm equations in L 2 ​ ( ℝ ) L^{2}(\mathbb{R}) that includes the integrable cases of b = 2 b=2 and b = 3 b=3 . We start with a linearized operator defined on functions in H 1 ​ ( ℝ ) ∩ W 1 , ∞ ​ ( ℝ ) H^{1}(\mathbb{R})\cap W^{1,\infty}(\mathbb{R}) and extend it to a linearized operator defined on weaker functions in L 2 ​ ( ℝ ) L^{2}(\mathbb{R}) . For b ≠ 5 2 b\neq\frac{5}{2} , the spectrum of the linearized operator in L 2 ​ ( ℝ ) L^{2}(\mathbb{R}) is proved to cover a closed vertical strip of the complex plane. For b = 5 2 b=\frac{5}{2} , the strip shrinks to the imaginary axis, but an additional pair of real eigenvalues exists due to projections to the peakon and its spatial translation. The spectral instability results agree with the linear instability results in the case of the Camassa-Holm equation for b = 2 b=2 .



1.
Introduction

We consider the following
b
-family of Camassa–Holm equations (which we call
b
-CH),

u_{t}-u_{xxt}+(b+1)uu_{x}=bu_{x}u_{xx}+uu_{xxx}.

(1)

The family generalizes the classical cases of the Camassa–Holm (CH) equation for
b=2
and the Degasperis–Procesi (DP) equation for
b=3
.

The CH equation has first appeared in the study of bi-Hamiltonian structure
of the Korteweg–de Vries (KdV) equation
[
13
]
. It was later introduced by Camassa and Holm
[
2
]
in hydrodynamical
applications as a model for unidirectional wave propagation
on shallow water. The hydrodynamical relevance of the CH
equation as a model for shallow water waves was discussed in

[
3
,
21
,
5
]
.

The DP equation can also be regarded as a model for nonlinear shallow water dynamics with its asymptotic accuracy equal to the CH equation
[
10
]
. The
b
-CH family of equations was introduced in
[
11
,
9
]
by using transformations of the integrable KdV equation within the same asymptotic accuracy.

One of the most intriguing properties of the
b
-CH equations is
the occurrence of wave breaking when the solutions stay bounded
but their slope develops a singularity in a finite time.
Related to the wave breaking is the existence of peaked travelling waves
called
peakons
. The exact peakon solution is given by

u(x,t)=ce^{-|x-ct|}\qquad x\in\mathbb{R}.

(2)

This solution is related to the reformulation
of the
b
-CH equation (
1
) in the weaker form

\vskip-5.69046ptu_{t}+uu_{x}+\frac{1}{4}\phi^{\prime}\ast\left[bu^{2}+(3-b)u_{x}^{2}\right]=0,

(3)

where
\ast
denotes convolution and
\phi(x)=e^{-|x|}
is Green’s
function satisfying
(1-\partial_{x}^{2})\phi=2\delta_{0}

with
\delta_{0}
being the Dirac delta distribution centered at
x=0
.

According to (
2
), the exact peakon solution in the form

u(x,t)=c\phi(x-ct)
satisfies the integral equation (
3
).
We can assume without loss of generality that
c=1

because the scaling transformation
u(x,t)\rightarrow au(x,at)
with arbitrary
a\in\mathbb{R}
leaves (
3
) invariant. It can then be checked directly
that
\phi(x)=e^{-|x|}
satisfies the integral equation

-\phi+\frac{1}{2}\phi^{2}+\frac{1}{4}\phi\ast\left[b\phi^{2}+(3-b)(\phi^{\prime})^{2}\right]=0

(4)

piecewisely on both sides from the peak at
x=0
, where the integral equation (
4
) arises after substituting the travelling wave reduction
to the
b
-CH equation (
3
) and integrating in
x
with zero conditions at infinity.

Stability of peakons has been considered in the literature. Numerical simulations in
[
18
,
19
]
showed that the peakons of the
b
-CH equation are likely to be unstable for
b<1
. This conjecture was
recently illustrated in
[
4
]
with the analysis of the linearized operator at the peakon solution and additional numerical experiments.
In the case of
b<-1
, the numerical results of
[
18
,
19
]
suggested that arbitrary initial data moves to the left and asymptotically separates out into a number of smooth time-independent solitary waves. Smooth time-independent solitary wave solutions were shown to be orbitally stable for
b<-1
in
[
20
]
. Smooth travelling solitary wave solutions in a regularized version of the b-CH equation were shown to be orbitally stable for
b=2
in
[
8
]
and for
b=3
in
[
22
]
.

For
b>1
, numerical simulations in
[
18
,
19
]
showed that
arbitrary initial data asymptotically resolves into a number of peakons.
Orbital stability of peakons in the energy space
H^{1}(\mathbb{R})
was shown
for the CH equation
(b=2)
in
[
6
,
7
]
by using
conservation of two energy integrals. This method was extended
in
[
23
]
, where the authors showed orbital stability of
peakons for the DP equation (
b=3
) in the energy space
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})\cap L^{3}(\mathbb{R})
.
Since solutions of the initial-value problem
for the
b
-CH equation with
b>1
are ill-posed in
H^{s}(\mathbb{R})
for
s<\frac{3}{2}

[
17
]
(and in
H^{\frac{3}{2}}(\mathbb{R})
for
1<b\leq 3

[
16
]
) due to the lack of continuous dependence and norm inflation, smooth solutions to the CH and DP equations
were considered in
[
7
]
and
[
23
]
being close to the
peakons in the energy space.

The largest class of initial data for which the initial-value problem
is well-posed for the
b
-CH equation is given by the space
H^{1}(\mathbb{R})\cap W^{1,\infty}(\mathbb{R})

[
24
]
.
It was recently shown in
[
26
]
for the CH equation
(b=2)

that although the peakons are orbitally stable in
H^{1}(\mathbb{R})
, they are unstable with respect to perturbations in
W^{1,\infty}(\mathbb{R})

in the sense that the
W^{1,\infty}
norm of the peaked perturbations may grow in time and may reach infinity in a finite time leading to the wave breaking of the solution. This analysis was performed
by using the method of characteristics in the nonlinear evolution
of the CH equation.

Previous studies of stability avoid the question of the linearized
stability of peakons because it was believed that “the nonlinearity plays a dominant role rather than being a higher-order correction” and that “the passage from the linear to the nonlinear theory is not an easy task, and may even be false”
[
7
]
. The first study of the linearized evolution
of peaked perturbations in
[
26
]
gave a valid evidence
to this concern since it was found that the
H^{1}
norm of the peaked perturbations grow within the linearized approximation exponentially as
e^{\frac{1}{2}t}
, while it does not grow in the full nonlinear evolution.
It was recently clarified in
[
25
]
in the setting of peaked periodic waves of the CH equation
(b=2)
that both the growth of the
H^{1}
norm of perturbations in the linearized approximation and its boundedness
in the full nonlinear evolution of the CH equation are related to the same two conserved energies.

These preliminary results raised an open question of whether the instability
of the peakons can be understood from the spectral stability
theory, where the instability of travelling waves follow
from the presence of the spectrum
of a linearized operator in the right half plane of the complex plane.

The main purpose of this work is to give a definitive answer to this question with rigorous analysis of the spectral instability of peakons
in the
b
-CH equation for any
b
.

Since the local well-posedness of the initial-value problem
holds in the space

H^{1}(\mathbb{R})\cap W^{1,\infty}(\mathbb{R})

which include peakons and their peaked perturbations
[
24
,
26
]
,
we first introduce the linearized operator acting on functions in this space.
However, this space is restrictive for the spectral stability theory,
hence we use projections to the peakon and its spatial translation
in order to extend the linearized operator in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
with a suitable
defined domain, similarly to the recent studies of peaked periodic waves
in the reduced Ostrovsky equation
[
14
,
15
]
.

We then analyze the spectrum of the linearized operator in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
.
We prove that for
b\neq\frac{5}{2}
, the spectrum covers a closed vertical strip of the complex plane. The half-width of the strip is exactly
\frac{1}{2}

for
b=2
which coincides with the exponential growth
e^{\frac{1}{2}t}
of the
H^{1}
norm of the perturbations obtained in
[
26
]
. We also note that the half-width of the strip for
b<\frac{5}{2}
agrees with the observation made in
Remark 3.6 in
[
4
]
in their analysis of the differential operator
at the peakon of the
b
-CH equation.

For
b=\frac{5}{2}
, the strip shrinks to the imaginary axis, but we show that the projections to the peakon and its spatial translation also grow exponentially according to a system of two first-order differential equations. This additional instability suggests that the peakons of the
b
-CH equation (
3
) are spectrally unstable in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
for every
b
.

The paper is organized as follows. Section
2
explains the
derivation of the linearized operator acting on functions in
H^{1}(\mathbb{R})\cap W^{1,\infty}(\mathbb{R})
and its extension to
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
with a suitable defined domain,
after which the main result is formulated. Section
3
gives the proof of the main result with the analysis of spectral properties
of the linearized operator in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
.
Section
4
describes the time evolution of the linearized
equation in connection
to the spectral properties of the linearized operator in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
.

2.
Linearized evolution

We recall from
[
26
]
that if
u\in C([0,T),H^{1}(\mathbb{R})\cap W^{1,\infty}(\mathbb{R}))
is a weak solution to the
b
-CH equation (
3
) such that
u(t,\cdot+\xi(t))\in C^{1}(-\infty,0)\cap C^{1}(0,\infty)
for
t\in[0,T)
, then the single peak at
x=\xi(t)
moves
along the local characteristic curve with
\xi^{\prime}(t)=u(t,\xi(t))
.
Therefore, we decompose the solution near the peakon
\phi(x)=e^{-|x|}

travelling with the unit speed into the following sum:

u(t,x)=\phi(x-t-a(t))+v(t,x-t-a(t)),

(5)

where
v(t,\cdot)\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R})\cap W^{1,\infty}(\mathbb{R})

is the peaked perturbation such that
v(t,\cdot)\in C^{1}(-\infty,0)\cap C^{1}(0,\infty)

and
a(t)
is the deviation of the peak position of the perturbed peakon
from its unperturbed position satisfying
a^{\prime}(t)=v(t,0)
.
Substituting (
5
) into (
3
) and using
the stationary equation (
4
) yields the following linearized equation:

v_{t}=(1-\phi)v_{\xi}+(v_{0}-v)\phi^{\prime}-\frac{1}{2}\phi^{\prime}\ast\left[b\phi v+(3-b)\phi^{\prime}v_{\xi}\right],

(6)

where
v_{0}(t):=v(t,0)
and
\xi:=x-t-a(t)
is the new spatial coordinate
in the travelling frame. By using an elementary identity proven in
[
26
]
,

\phi^{\prime}\ast(\phi^{\prime}v^{\prime})=\phi\ast(\phi^{\prime}v)-\phi^{\prime}\ast(\phi v)+2(v_{0}-v)\phi^{\prime},\quad\forall v\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}),

we can rewrite the linearized equation (
6
) in the equivalent form:

v_{t}=(1-\phi)v_{\xi}+(b-2)(v_{0}-v)\phi^{\prime}+Q(v),

(7)

where

Q(v):=\frac{1}{2}(b-3)\phi\ast(\phi^{\prime}v)-\frac{1}{2}(2b-3)\phi^{\prime}\ast(\phi v).

(8)

Using another elementary identity from
[
26
]
,

\phi\ast(\phi^{\prime}v)+\phi^{\prime}\ast(\phi v)+2\phi v_{-1}=0,\quad\forall v\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}),\quad v_{-1}(\xi):=\int_{0}^{\xi}v(\xi^{\prime})d\xi,

one can rewrite
Q(v)
into the following two equivalent forms:

Q(v)=\frac{3}{2}(b-2)\phi\ast(\phi^{\prime}v)+(2b-3)\phi v_{-1}=-\frac{3}{2}(b-2)\phi^{\prime}\ast(\phi v)+(3-b)\phi v_{-1}.

(9)

The following lemma shows that the linear operator
Q
is compact in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
.

Lemma 2.1
.

The operator
Q:L^{2}(\mathbb{R})\mapsto L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
is compact.

Proof.

Each term in either (
8
) or (
9
) can
be written as an integral operator of the form

\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}K(\xi,\xi^{\prime})v(\xi^{\prime})\,d\xi^{\prime},

for some kernel
K\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
. As such, each of those terms defines a Hilbert-Schmidt integral operator, known to be compact (see
[
27
]
, p. 262). To be more specific,
\phi\ast(\phi^{\prime}v)
corresponds to the kernel

K_{1}=-{\mbox{sgn}}\!\left(\xi^{\prime}\right)e^{-|\xi-\xi^{\prime}|-|\xi^{\prime}|},

while
\phi v_{-1}
corresponds to

K_{2}=\begin{cases}\displaystyle{{\mbox{sgn}}\!\left(\xi\right)e^{-|\xi|}\;\;\rm{for}\;\;0\leq|\xi^{\prime}|\leq|\xi|},\\
\displaystyle{0\;\;{\rm{otherwise}},}\end{cases}

for which we obtain

\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|K_{1}(\xi,\xi^{\prime})|^{2}d\xi d\xi^{\prime}=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\left(|\xi|+\frac{1}{2}\right)e^{-2|\xi|}d\xi=1

and

\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|K_{2}(\xi,\xi^{\prime})|^{2}d\xi d\xi^{\prime}=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|\xi|e^{-2|\xi|}d\xi=\frac{1}{2}.

Hence,
K\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
and
Q
is the compact Hilbert–Schmidt operator in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
.
∎

The linearized equation (
7
) with
Q(v)
given by either (
8
) or (
9
) is well-defined
in
v(t,\cdot)\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R})\cap W^{1,\infty}(\mathbb{R})
.
This can be shown by using either the local well-posedness theory
[
24
]
or the method of characteristical curves
[
26
]
.
The linearized evolution depends on the value

v_{0}(t)=v(t,0)
which is well-defined due to Sobolev embedding of

H^{1}(\mathbb{R})
into the space of bounded and continuous functions.

Next, we extend the linearized equation to the larger space
{\rm Dom}(L)\subset L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
associated with the linearized operator

L:=(1-\phi)\partial_{\xi}+(2-b)\phi^{\prime}+Q.

(10)

Since
Q
is a compact operator in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
by Lemma
2.1

and
\phi\in W^{1,\infty}(\mathbb{R})
, the domain of
L

in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
is defined by

{\mbox{Dom}}(L)=\left\{v\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}):\quad(1-\phi)v^{\prime}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})\right\}.

(11)

It follows from the bound
\|(1-\phi)v^{\prime}\|_{L^{2}}\leq\|v^{\prime}\|_{L^{2}}
that
H^{1}(\mathbb{R})
is continuously embedded into
{\rm Dom}(L)
.
However,
H^{1}(\mathbb{R})
is not equivalent to
{\rm Dom}(L)
because

\phi^{\prime}\in{\rm Dom}(L)
but
\phi^{\prime}\notin H^{1}(\mathbb{R})
since
\xi\delta_{0}(\xi)\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
but
\phi\notin C^{1}(\mathbb{R})
. Generally,
functions in
{\rm Dom}(L)
do not have to be continuous across the peak at
x=0
, therefore,
v_{0}
may not be defined if
v\in{\rm Dom}(L)
but
v\notin H^{1}(\mathbb{R})
.

In order to extend the linearized equation (
7
) in
{\rm Dom}(L)
, we are going to use another equivalent reformulation of the linearized
equation (
7
) for
v(t,\cdot)\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R})\cap W^{1,\infty}(\mathbb{R})
. This is described in Lemma
2.3
after the proof of the following elementary property of
L
.

Lemma 2.2
.

For
L:{\rm Dom}(L)\subset L^{2}(\mathbb{R})\mapsto L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
, it is true for every
b
that

L\phi=(2-b)\phi^{\prime}{\mbox{ and }}L\phi^{\prime}=0.

(12)

Proof.

By using the first expression in (
9
), we obtain for every
\xi\neq 0

Q(\phi)=\frac{3}{2}(b-2)\phi\ast(\phi\phi^{\prime})+(2b-3)\phi\int_{0}^{\xi}\phi(\xi^{\prime})d\xi^{\prime}=(1-b)(\phi^{\prime}-\phi\phi^{\prime})

and

Q(\phi^{\prime})=\frac{3}{2}(b-2)\phi\ast(\phi^{\prime}\phi^{\prime})+(2b-3)\phi\int_{0}^{\xi}\phi^{\prime}(\xi^{\prime})d\xi^{\prime}=-\phi+(b-1)\phi^{2},

which yields (
12
) after substituting into (
10
). Note that

\phi,\phi^{\prime}\in{\rm Dom}(L)
and that the singularity at
\xi=0
does not result in any contribution in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
because
\xi\delta_{0}(\xi)=0
in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
.
∎

Lemma 2.3
.

Consider the class of functions in

X:=C(\mathbb{R},H^{1}(\mathbb{R})\cap W^{1,\infty}(\mathbb{R}))\cap C^{1}(\mathbb{R},L^{2}(\mathbb{R})\cap L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})).

Then,
v\in X
is a solution
to the linearized equation (
7
) if and only if
\tilde{v}:=v-v_{0}\phi\in X
satisfying
\tilde{v}(t,0)=0
is a solution of the linearized equation

\tilde{v}_{t}=L\tilde{v}-\frac{3}{2}(b-2)\langle\phi\phi^{\prime},\tilde{v}\rangle\phi,

(13)

where the inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
is defined in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
.

Proof.

Substituting
v(t,x)=\tilde{v}(t,x)+v_{0}(t)\phi(x)
into (
7
) yields

\tilde{v}_{t}+v_{0}^{\prime}(t)\phi=L\tilde{v}+v_{0}L\phi+(b-2)v_{0}\phi^{\prime}.

The last two terms cancel out due to the first identity (
12
) in Lemma
2.2
. On the other hand, taking the limit
\xi\to 0

into (
7
) in the class of functions

v\in X
yields

v_{0}^{\prime}(t)=\lim_{\xi\to 0}Q(v)(\xi)=\frac{3}{2}(b-2)\langle\phi\phi^{\prime},v\rangle,

where either representation in (
9
) can be used together with the spatial symmetry of
\phi
. Since
\langle\phi\phi^{\prime},\phi\rangle=0
,
it follows that
\langle\phi\phi^{\prime},v\rangle=\langle\phi\phi^{\prime},\tilde{v}\rangle
so that the two equations yield (
13
). Since
v_{0}(t)=v(t,0)
, it is true that
\tilde{v}(t,0)=0
. This constraint is preserved in the time evolution of (
13
) since
\lim\limits_{\xi\to 0}L\tilde{v}=\frac{3}{2}(b-2)\langle\phi\phi^{\prime},\tilde{v}\rangle
for
\tilde{v}\in X
.

The proof in the opposite direction from (
13
) to (
7
) is identical.
∎

The equivalent evolution problem (
13
) is still defined
for
\tilde{v}(t,\cdot)\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R})\cap W^{1,\infty}(\mathbb{R})
. However, the right-hand side is now well defined
if
\tilde{v}(t,\cdot)\in{\rm Dom}(L)\subset L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
. This enables us to define linear stability of the peakons as follows.

Definition 2.4
.

The peakon solution
u(t,x)=\phi(x-t)
of the
b
-CH equation (
3
) is said to be linearly stable if for every

\tilde{v}_{0}\in{\rm Dom}(L)\subset L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
, there exists a positive constant
C
and a unique solution
\tilde{v}\in C(\mathbb{R},{\rm Dom}(L))
to the linearized equation (
13
) with
\tilde{v}(0,\xi)=\tilde{v}_{0}(\xi)
such that

\|\tilde{v}(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}}\leq C\|{v_{0}}\|_{L^{2}},\quad t>0.

Otherwise, it is said to be linearly unstable.

In order to prove that the peakons are linearly unstable in the sense of Definition
2.4
for all
b\in\mathbb{R}
, we reduce the linearized equation (
13
) in
{\rm Dom}(L)\subset L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
to two parts, where one is defined by the linearized operator
L
in (
10
)–(
11
) and the other one is defined by a system of two first-order differential equations. This task is achieved with the secondary decomposition described in the following lemma.

Lemma 2.5
.

Consider the class of functions in

Y:=C(\mathbb{R},{\rm Dom}(L))\cap C^{1}(\mathbb{R},L^{2}(\mathbb{R})).

Then,
\tilde{v}\in Y
is a solution
to the linearized equation (
13
) if
w:=\tilde{v}-\alpha\phi-\beta\phi^{\prime}\in Y
is a solution of the linearized equation

\frac{dw}{dt}=Lw,

(14)

with
\alpha
and
\beta
satisfying the system

\frac{d\alpha}{dt}=(2-b)\beta+\frac{3}{2}(2-b)\langle\phi\phi^{\prime},w\rangle,\quad\frac{d\beta}{dt}=(2-b)\alpha.

(15)

Proof.

Substituting
\tilde{v}(t,x)=\alpha(t)\phi(x)+\beta(t)\phi^{\prime}(x)+w(t,x)
into (
13
) yields

\alpha^{\prime}(t)\phi+\beta^{\prime}(t)\phi^{\prime}+w_{t}=(2-b)\alpha\phi^{\prime}+Lw+(2-b)\beta\phi+\frac{3}{2}(2-b)\langle\phi\phi^{\prime},w\rangle\phi,

where we have used
\langle\phi,(\phi^{\prime})^{2}\rangle=\frac{2}{3}
,

\langle\phi\phi^{\prime},\phi\rangle=0
, and the identities (
12
) in Lemma
2.2
. Separating
\phi
,
\phi^{\prime}
and the rest yields (
14
) and (
15
).
∎

Corollary 2.6
.

The peakon is linearly unstable in
Y
for every
b\neq 2
.

Proof.

Setting
w=0
in (
14
) and (
15
) gives the second-order homogeneous system

\frac{d\alpha}{dt}=(2-b)\beta,\quad\frac{d\beta}{dt}=(2-b)\alpha,

where the linear instability with the exponential growth
e^{|2-b|t}

exists for every
b\neq 2
. By Definition
2.4
, the peakon is linearly unstable in
Y
.
∎

Remark 2.7
.

In order to ensure the uniqueness of the decomposition
\tilde{v}(t,x)=\alpha(t)\phi(x)+\beta(t)\phi^{\prime}(x)+w(t,x)
, we compute the adjoint
operator
L^{*}:{\rm Dom}(L)\subset L^{2}(\mathbb{R})\mapsto L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
with respect to
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
:

L^{*}v:=(\phi-1)v_{\xi}+(3-b)\phi^{\prime}v+\frac{1}{2}(b-3)\phi^{\prime}(\phi\ast v)+\frac{1}{2}(2b-3)\phi(\phi^{\prime}\ast v).

(16)

With straightforward computations, we obtain

\displaystyle L^{*}1=0,

\displaystyle L^{*}{\rm sgn}=3(b-2)\phi^{2},

\displaystyle L^{*}\phi^{2}=2(b-3)\phi\phi^{\prime}+\frac{8}{3}(3-b)\phi^{2}\phi^{\prime},

\displaystyle L^{*}\phi\phi^{\prime}=(b-4)\phi^{2}+\frac{8}{3}(3-b)\phi^{3},

where
{\rm sgn}(\xi)=1
if
\xi>0
and
{\rm sgn}(\xi)=-1
if
\xi<0
.

If
b=2
, the constraints
\langle 1,w\rangle
and
\langle{\rm sgn},w\rangle
are preserved in the time evolution of
w\in Y
. One can uniquely define
\alpha(t)
and
\beta(t)
by the orthogonality conditions

\langle 1,w\rangle=0
and
\langle{\rm sgn},w\rangle=0

so that
\alpha=\frac{1}{2}\langle 1,\tilde{v}\rangle
and
\beta=-\frac{1}{2}\langle{\rm sgn},\tilde{v}\rangle
, where we have used
\langle 1,\phi\rangle=-\langle{\rm sgn},\phi^{\prime}\rangle=2
and
\langle 1,\phi^{\prime}\rangle=\langle{\rm sgn},\phi\rangle=0
.

If
b=3
, the constraints
\langle\phi^{2},w\rangle
and

\langle{\rm sgn}+3\phi\phi^{\prime},w\rangle
are preserved in the time evolution of
w\in Y
. One can uniquely define
\alpha(t)
and
\beta(t)
by the orthogonality conditions

\langle\phi^{2},w\rangle=\langle{\rm sgn}+3\phi\phi^{\prime},w\rangle=0
and
\langle\phi^{2},\phi^{\prime}\rangle=\langle{\rm sgn}+3\phi\phi^{\prime},\phi\rangle=0
.

In Appendix
A
, we derive an antisymmetric bounded function
v_{b}
that satisfies
L^{*}v_{b}=0
for all
b>3

and thus also provides a way to uniquely define
\alpha(t)
and
\beta(t)
by the orthogonality conditions

\langle 1,w\rangle=\langle v_{b},w\rangle=0
and
\langle 1,\phi^{\prime}\rangle=\langle v_{b},\phi\rangle=0

Definition 2.8

Let

(1)

(2)

The spectrum

(a)

the point spectrum

\sigma_{\rm p}(A)=\{\lambda\in\sigma(A):\;\;{\rm Ker}(A-\lambda I)\neq\{0\}\},

(b)

the residual spectrum

\sigma_{\rm r}(A)=\{\lambda\in\sigma(A):\;\;{\rm Ker}(A-\lambda I)=\{0\},\;\;{\rm Ran}(A-\lambda I)\neq X\},

(c)

the continuous spectrum

\sigma_{\rm c}(A)=\{\lambda\in\sigma(A):\;\;{\rm Ker}(A-\lambda I)=\{0\},\;\;{\rm Ran}(A-\lambda I)=X,\;\;(A-\lambda I)^{-1}:X\to X\text{ is unbounded}\}.

3.1.
Spectrum of
L_{0}

The spectrum of
L_{0}
is described by the following theorem.

Theorem 3.1
.

The spectrum of the linear operator
L_{0}:{\rm Dom}(L)\subset L^{2}(\mathbb{R})\mapsto L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
defined by (
18
) is given by

\sigma(L_{0})=\left\{\lambda\in\mathbb{C}:\;\;|\operatorname{\sf Re}\!\left(\lambda\right)|\leq\left|\frac{5}{2}-b\right|\right\}.

Moreover, the point spectrum
is located for
0<|\operatorname{\sf Re}\!\left(\lambda\right)|<\frac{5}{2}-b

if
b<\frac{5}{2}
and the residual spectrum is located
for
0<|\operatorname{\sf Re}\!\left(\lambda\right)|<b-\frac{5}{2}

if
b>\frac{5}{2}
, whereas the continuous spectrum is located for

\operatorname{\sf Re}\!\left(\lambda\right)=0
and
\operatorname{\sf Re}\!\left(\lambda\right)=\pm\left|\frac{5}{2}-b\right|

in both cases.

Proof.

Given simplicity of the definition of
L_{0}
, the proof is obtained by computing the point, residual, and continuous spectrum of
L_{0}
explicitly.

Point spectrum of
L_{0}
.
We solve the differential equation

(1-\phi)\frac{dv}{d\xi}+(2-b)\phi^{\prime}v=\lambda v,\quad\xi\in\mathbb{R}.

(19)

The differential equation (
19
) is solved separately for
\xi>0
and
\xi<0
with the following general solution:

v(\xi)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\displaystyle{v_{+}\frac{(e^{\xi}-1)^{\lambda}}{(1-e^{-\xi})^{b-2}}},&\quad\xi>0,\\
\displaystyle{v_{-}\frac{1}{(e^{-\xi}-1)^{\lambda}{(1-e^{\xi})^{b-2}}}},&\quad\xi<0,\end{array}\right.

(20)

where
v_{-}
and
v_{+}
are arbitrary constants.

The differential equation (
19
) has the following symmetry: if
\lambda=\lambda_{0}
is an eigenvalue with the eigenfunction
v=v_{0}(\xi)
, then
\lambda=-\lambda_{0}
is an eigenvalue with the eigenfunction
v=v_{0}(-\xi)
. Therefore, it is sufficient to consider the case of
\operatorname{\sf Re}\!\left(\lambda\right)\geq 0
.

Since
v(\xi)\sim v_{+}e^{\lambda\xi}
as
\xi\to+\infty
, then

v\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
for
\operatorname{\sf Re}\!\left(\lambda\right)\geq 0
is satisfied by the only choice of
v_{+}=0
. Since
v(\xi)\sim v_{-}e^{\lambda\xi}
as
\xi\to-\infty
, then
v\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
is satisfied for arbitrary
v_{-}

if and only if
\operatorname{\sf Re}\!\left(\lambda\right)>0
. Since
v(\xi)\sim v_{-}|\xi|^{2-b-\lambda}
as
x\to 0^{-}
, then
v\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
is satisfied for arbitrary
v_{-}
if and only if
\operatorname{\sf Re}\!\left(\lambda\right)+b-2<\frac{1}{2}
,
that is for
\operatorname{\sf Re}\!\left(\lambda\right)<\frac{5}{2}-b
. In all other cases, we have to set
v_{-}=0
for
v\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
so that the zero function is the only solution of (
19
) in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
.
Summarizing and using the symmetry above,
\sigma_{p}(L_{0})
exists if
b<\frac{5}{2}
and is located for
0<|\operatorname{\sf Re}\!\left(\lambda\right)|<\frac{5}{2}-b
.

Residual spectrum of
L_{0}
.
By Lemma 6.2.6 in
[
1
]
,
if
\sigma_{p}(L_{0})
is an empty set, then
\sigma_{r}(L_{0})=\sigma_{p}(L_{0}^{*})
,
where

L_{0}^{*}=-\partial_{\xi}(1-\phi)+(2-b)\phi^{\prime}=-(1-\phi)\partial_{\xi}+(3-b)\phi^{\prime}

is the adjoint operator to
L_{0}
in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
. The differential equation

-(1-\phi)\frac{dv}{d\xi}+(3-b)\phi^{\prime}v=\lambda v,\quad\xi\in\mathbb{R}

(21)

becomes (
19
) after the transformation:
\lambda\mapsto-\lambda
and

b-2\mapsto 3-b
. Therefore, we obtain the following general solution
by applying this transformation to (
20
):

v(\xi)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\displaystyle{v_{+}\frac{(e^{\xi}-1)^{-\lambda}}{(1-e^{-\xi})^{3-b}}},&\quad\xi>0,\\
\displaystyle{v_{-}\frac{1}{(e^{-\xi}-1)^{-\lambda}{(1-e^{\xi})^{3-b}}}},&\quad\xi<0,\end{array}\right.

(22)

where
v_{-}
and
v_{+}
are arbitrary constants. Proceeding similarly with the limits
\xi\to\pm\infty
and
\xi\to 0^{\pm}
shows
that nonzero solutions of (
21
) exist in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})

if and only if
b>\frac{5}{2}
and
0<|\operatorname{\sf Re}\!\left(\lambda\right)|<\frac{5}{2}-b
. This yields
\sigma_{r}(L_{0})
since
\sigma_{p}(L_{0})
is an empty set for
b>\frac{5}{2}
.

Resolvent set of
L_{0}
.
Let us consider the resolvent equation

L_{0}v-\lambda v=f,

(23)

where
f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
is arbitrary and
\operatorname{\sf Re}\!\left(\lambda\right)\geq 0
is assumed without loss of generality. We multiply both sides of (
23
) by
\bar{v}
and integrate over
\mathbb{R}
. Using the definition of
L_{0}
given in
(
18
), one finds

\begin{split}\left<\left((1-\phi)v\right)^{\prime},\,v\right>+(3-b)\left<\phi^{\prime}v,\,v\right>-\lambda\|v\|^{2}=\left<f,\,v\right>.\end{split}

(24)

By integration by parts, since
\lim\limits_{\xi\to\pm\infty}v(\xi)=0
for
v\in{\rm Dom}(L)
, we
have that

\left<\left((1-\phi)v\right)^{\prime},\,v\right>=-\left<v,\,\left((1-\phi)v\right)^{\prime}\right>-\left<\phi^{\prime}v,\,v\right>,

thus

\operatorname{\sf Re}\!\left(\left<((1-\phi)v)^{\prime},\,v\right>\right)=-\frac{1}{2}\left<\phi^{\prime}v,\,\bar{v}\right>.

(25)

Taking the real part of (
24
), multiplying by -1, and using (
25
), we get

\left(b-\frac{5}{2}\right)\left<\phi^{\prime}v,\,v\right>+\operatorname{\sf Re}\!\left(\lambda\right)\|v\|^{2}=-\operatorname{\sf Re}\!\left(\left<f,\,v\right>\right),

(26)

where

-\|v\|^{2}\leq\left<\phi^{\prime}v,\,v\right>\leq\|v\|^{2}.

(27)

Using the upper bound of (
27
) in (
26
) in the case
b\leq\frac{5}{2}
, we find that

\left(\operatorname{\sf Re}\!\left(\lambda\right)+b-\frac{5}{2}\right)\|v\|^{2}\leq\left|\operatorname{\sf Re}\!\left(\left<f,\,v\right>\right)\right|\leq\|f\|\|v\|,

where the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality has been used. Hence, for every
\operatorname{\sf Re}\!\left(\lambda\right)>\frac{5}{2}-b
, there exists
C_{\lambda}
such that

\|v\|\leq C_{\lambda}\|f\|
so that this
\lambda
belongs to
\rho(L_{0})
.

In a very similar way, using the lower bound of (
27
) in (
26
)
in the case
b\geq\frac{5}{2}
, we find that

\begin{split}\left(\operatorname{\sf Re}\!\left(\lambda\right)-b+\frac{5}{2}\right)\|v\|^{2}\leq\left|\operatorname{\sf Re}\!\left(\left<f,\,v\right>\right)\right|\leq\|f\|\|v\|,\end{split}

hence
\operatorname{\sf Re}\!\left(\lambda\right)>b-\frac{5}{2}
belongs to
\rho(L_{0})
.

Continuous spectrum of
L_{0}
.
By Theorem 4 in
[
12

\xi>0
and
\xi<0
.

For
\xi>0

by

which follows from the method of characteristics. Then,

satisfies the resolvent equation

where
\mathfrak{L}_{0}^{+}:=\partial_{z}+(b-2)(1+e^{z})^{-1}
and

. Since

\sigma_{c}(\mathfrak{L}_{0}^{+})

and

as

,
\sigma_{c}(\mathfrak{L}_{0}^{+})
in

is located at
\operatorname{\sf Re}\!\left(\lambda\right)=0
. Since

\mathfrak{L}_{0}^{+}=\partial_{z}+(b-2)-(b-2)e^{z}(1+e^{z})^{-1}
and

is bounded and decaying exponentially to

as

\sigma_{c}(\mathfrak{L}_{0}^{+})
in

is located at

\operatorname{\sf Re}\!\left(\lambda\right)=b-\frac{5}{2}
.

Similarly, for
\xi<0
, we use the transformation
(-\infty,0)\ni\xi\mapsto z\in\mathbb{R}
by

\mathfrak{L}_{0}^{-}\mathfrak{v}-\lambda\mathfrak{v}=\mathfrak{f},

where
\mathfrak{L}_{0}^{-}:=\partial_{z}+(2-b)(1+e^{-z})^{-1}

\mathfrak{f}(z):=f(\xi)
. Since

\sigma_{c}(\mathfrak{L}_{0}^{-})
in

By Theorem 4 in

\sigma_{c}(L_{0})

∎

Remark 3.4

Some ideas of the proof of Lemma

are similar to what was done recently in

-CH equation was used in

Proof.

The spectral problem (

as the differential equation (

is an eigenvalue with the eigenfunction

, then
\lambda=-\lambda_{0}
is an eigenvalue with the eigenfunction

. Therefore, it is sufficient to consider the case
\operatorname{\sf Re}\!\left(\lambda\right)\geq 0
.

Applying the operator

to (

\xi<0
and
\xi>0
yields the following differential equation

\lambda(v-v^{\prime\prime})=(1-\phi)(v^{\prime}-v^{\prime\prime\prime})-b\phi^{\prime}(v-v^{\prime\prime}).

Indeed, for

\displaystyle(1-\partial_{\xi}^{2})\phi\ast(\phi^{\prime}v)=(1-\partial_{\xi}^{2})\left[\int_{-\infty}^{\xi}e^{-\xi+2\eta}v(\eta)d\eta+\int_{\xi}^{0}e^{\xi}v(\eta)d\eta+\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{\xi-2\eta}v(\eta)d\eta\right]=2e^{\xi}v

\displaystyle(1-\partial_{\xi}^{2})\phi v_{-1}=(1-\partial_{\xi}^{2})e^{\xi}\int_{0}^{\xi}v(\eta)d\eta=-2e^{\xi}v-e^{\xi}v^{\prime},

which yields (
29

\xi<0
after adding all terms. Computations for
\xi>0

Let
m:=v-v^{\prime\prime}
. The differential equation (

(1-\phi)\frac{dm}{d\xi}-b\phi^{\prime}m=\lambda m,

(30)

which coincides with the differential equation (

by

. Therefore, the exact solution is given by the corresponding transformation of the solution (

(31)

where
m_{-}
and
m_{+}
are arbitrary constants. The eigenfunction

is recovered from solutions of the second-order equation

m_{-}e^{\lambda\xi}(1-e^{\xi})^{-\lambda-b},&\quad\xi<0.\end{array}\right.

(32)

If

, then

, the dual space of

. Since

as

for

and

is arbitrary. Similarly,

as
\xi\to-\infty

If
\operatorname{\sf Re}\!\left(\lambda\right)=0

v\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
exists with
the only choice
m_{-}=0
and
v(\xi)=c_{-}e^{\xi}
, where

is arbitrary. In this case,

with

It remains to consider the case

,
m_{-}\neq 0

\operatorname{\sf Re}\!\left(\lambda\right)>0

\displaystyle+(\lambda+b-2)(1-e^{\xi})(2f^{\prime}+(3-2b)f)+(\lambda+b-2)(\lambda+b-1)f=-1.

The homogeneous part of the second-order (

f_{p}(\xi)\sim\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}1+\mathcal{O}(|\xi|),&\quad\lambda+b\neq\{1,2\},\\
\log|\xi|+\mathcal{O}(|\xi|\log|\xi|),&\quad\lambda+b=\{1,2\},\end{array}\right.\quad\mbox{\rm as}\quad\xi\to 0^{-},

(35)

which yields the corresponding behavior of
v_{p}(\xi)
from (
33
):

{{v_{p}}}(\xi)\sim\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}|\xi|^{2-b-\lambda},&\quad\lambda+b\neq\{1,2\},\\
\log|\xi|,&\quad\lambda+b=2,\\
|\xi|\log|\xi|,&\quad\lambda+b=1,\end{array}\right.\quad\mbox{\rm as}\quad\xi\to 0^{-}.

(36)

Hence, (
32
) has a solution
v\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
if and only if
\operatorname{\sf Re}\!\left(\lambda\right)+b-2<\frac{1}{2}
, for
0<\operatorname{\sf Re}\!\left(\lambda\right)<\frac{5}{2}-b
.

Summarizing and using the symmetry above,
\sigma_{p}(L)
exists if
b<\frac{5}{2}
and is located for
0<|\operatorname{\sf Re}\!\left(\lambda\right)|<\frac{5}{2}-b
.
∎

4.
Time evolution of the linearized system

Due to the difference in the spectral properties of the linearized operator
L
in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
for
b<\frac{5}{2}
and
b>\frac{5}{2}
in Theorem
2.9
, the growth of the
L^{2}
norm of the peaked perturbations is different due to the point spectrum for
b<\frac{5}{2}
and due to the residual spectrum for
b>\frac{5}{2}
, as follows from the balance equation (
17
).
We illustrate this difference here by studying exact solutions to the initial-value problem

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}v_{t}=(1-\phi)v_{\xi}+(2-b)\phi^{\prime}v,&\quad t>0,\\
v|_{t=0}=v_{0},&\end{array}\right.

(38)

where
v_{0}\in{\rm Dom}(L)
.
The initial-value problem coincides with the linearized equation
for the truncated operator
L_{0}
. By Theorem
3.1
, the unstable spectrum of the operator
L_{0}
is the point spectrum for
b<\frac{5}{2}
and the residual spectrum for
b>\frac{5}{2}
.

The initial-value problem (
38
) can be solved exactly by using the method of characteristics also used in
[
26
,
25
,
14
]
. The following lemma gives the bounds obtained from the exact solution of the initial-value problem (
38
).

Lemma 4.1
.

For every
v_{0}\in{\rm Dom}(L)
, the initial-value problem (
38
) admits the unique solution
v\in C(\mathbb{R},{\rm Dom}(L))
satisfying the following properties:

•

If
b=\frac{5}{2}
, then
\|v(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}}=\|v_{0}\|_{L^{2}}
.

•

If
b>\frac{5}{2}
, then
\|v(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}(-\infty,0)}\leq\|v_{0}\|_{L^{2}(-\infty,0)}
and there is a positive constant
C_{0}
that depends on
v_{0}
such that
\|v(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}(0,\infty)}\geq C_{0}e^{(b-\frac{5}{2})t}
.

•

If
b<\frac{5}{2}
, then
\|v(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}(0,\infty)}\leq\|v_{0}\|_{L^{2}(0,\infty)}
and there exists
v_{0}\in{\rm Dom}(L)
such that
\|v(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}(-\infty,0)}=e^{\lambda_{0}t}\|v_{0}\|_{L^{2}(-\infty,0)}
with
\lambda_{0}\in(0,\frac{5}{2}-b)
.

Proof.

By using the method of characteristics, we introduce
the characteristic curves
\xi=X(t,s)
satisfying
X_{t}=\phi(X)-1

with
X|_{t=0}=s
. The exact solution for
X(t,s)
is readily available
(see, e.g.
[
26
]
):

X(t,s)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\log\left[1+(e^{s}-1)e^{-t}\right],&\quad s>0,\\
-\log\left[1+(e^{-s}-1)e^{t}\right],&\quad s<0.\end{array}\right.

(39)

Along the characteristic curves, the function
V(t,s):=v(t,\xi=X(t,s))

satisfies

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}V_{t}=(2-b)\phi^{\prime}(X(t,s))V,&\quad t>0,\\
V|_{t=0}=v_{0}(s),\end{array}\right.

(40)

which is uniquely solved separately for
s>0
and
s<0
by

V(t,s)=v_{0}(s)\left[\frac{\partial X}{\partial s}\right]^{2-b}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}v_{0}(s)\left[1+(e^{t}-1)e^{-s}\right]^{b-2},&\quad s>0,\\
v_{0}(s)\left[1+(e^{-t}-1)e^{s}\right]^{b-2},&\quad s<0.\end{array}\right.

(41)

By the chain rule, we obtain

\|v(t,\cdot)\|^{2}_{L^{2}(0,\infty)}=\int_{0}^{\infty}|v_{0}(s)|^{2}\left[1+(e^{t}-1)e^{-s}\right]^{2b-5}ds

(42)

and

\|v(t,\cdot)\|^{2}_{L^{2}(-\infty,0)}=\int_{-\infty}^{0}|v_{0}(s)|^{2}\left[1+(e^{-t}-1)e^{s}\right]^{2b-5}ds.

(43)

If
b=\frac{5}{2}
, the linear evolution is
L^{2}
-preserving in time both for
s>0
and
s<0
.

If
b>\frac{5}{2}
, then it follows from (
42
) and (
43
) that

\|v(t,\cdot)\|^{2}_{L^{2}(0,\infty)}\geq(e^{t}-1)^{2b-5}\int_{0}^{\infty}|v_{0}(s)|^{2}e^{-(2b-5)s}ds,\quad\|v(t,\cdot)\|^{2}_{L^{2}(-\infty,0)}\leq\int_{-\infty}^{0}|v_{0}(s)|^{2}ds.

This proves that the
L^{2}
norm of the perturbation grows exponentially in time on the right side of the peak at
\xi=0
, while that on the left side of the peak stays bounded in time. Moreover, the growth rate of the
L^{2}
norm is exactly
e^{(b-\frac{5}{2})t}
.

If
b<\frac{5}{2}
, then it follows from (
42
) and (
43
) that

\|v(t,\cdot)\|^{2}_{L^{2}(0,\infty)}\leq\int_{0}^{\infty}|v_{0}(s)|^{2}ds,

so that the
L^{2}
norm of the perturbation stays bounded in time on the right side of the peak at
\xi=0
. It may first look like the
L^{2}
norm
on the left side of the peak stays bounded too with the formal limit

\lim_{t\to+\infty}\|v(t,\cdot)\|^{2}_{L^{2}(-\infty,0)}=\int_{-\infty}^{0}|v_{0}(s)|^{2}(1-e^{s})^{2b-5}ds.

(44)

However, the limit may not exist if
v_{0}(0)\neq 0
. If
v_{0}
is the eigenfunction of the point spectrum of
L_{0}
for the eigenvalue
\lambda_{0}\in(0,\frac{5}{2}-b)
, which follows from (
20
), that is,

v_{0}(s)=\frac{e^{\lambda_{0}s}}{(1-e^{s})^{\lambda_{0}+b-2}},\quad s<0,

(45)

then after elementary transformations, we obtain for
s<0

V(t,s)=v_{0}(s)\left[1+(e^{-t}-1)e^{s}\right]^{b-2}=\frac{e^{\lambda_{0}t+\lambda_{0}X(t,s)}}{(1-e^{X(t,s)})^{\lambda_{0}+b-2}}=v_{0}(X(t,s))e^{\lambda_{0}t},

so that
\|v(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}(-\infty,0)}=\|v_{0}\|_{L^{2}(-\infty,0)}e^{\lambda_{0}t}
grows exponentially in time. Due to the weak singularity of
v_{0}(s)
as
s\to 0^{-}
, which is allowed in
{\rm Dom}(L)\subset L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
, the integral of
|v_{0}(s)|^{2}(1-e^{s})^{2b-5}
in (
44
) diverges.
∎
