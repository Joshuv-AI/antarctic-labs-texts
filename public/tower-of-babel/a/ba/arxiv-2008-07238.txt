Philipp Grohs and Lukas Liehr

Abstract

Introduction

The phase retrieval problem concerns the reconstruction of a given signal
f\in\mathcal{C}
in a signal class
\mathcal{C}
contained in a given Banach space
\mathcal{B}
from phaseless linear measurements. These measurements are of the form
\{|\Phi_{\lambda}(f)|:\lambda\in X\}
with
(\Phi_{\lambda})_{\lambda\in X}\subseteq\mathcal{B}^{\prime}
constituting a family of linear functionals on
\mathcal{B}
. Problems of this kind arise in a large number of applications, most notably in coherent diffraction imaging and audio processing where the measurements
(\Phi_{\lambda}(f))_{\lambda\in X}\subseteq{\mathbb{C}}
typically arise as (samples of) the Fourier- or Gabor transform of a signal
f
, see for example the recent surveys
[
7
,
9
]
and the references therein. The question of when any given function
f\in\mathcal{C}
is uniquely determined from its phaseless linear measurements
up to a global phase
, meaning that
|\Phi_{\lambda}(f)|=|\Phi_{\lambda}(h)|
for all
\lambda\in X
and some
f,h\in\mathcal{C}
implies that there exists a global phase factor
\tau\in{\mathbb{T}}\coloneqq\{z\in{\mathbb{C}}:|z|=1\}
with
f=\tau h
, in general constitutes a difficult mathematical problem, especially when
\mathcal{B}
is infinite-dimensional and
X
is discrete as it would be the case in most practical applications. In the present paper, we study the phase retrieval problem arising from measurements of the Gabor transform. The Gabor transform
\mathcal{G}:{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}})}\to L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{2})
is defined by

\mathcal{G}f(x,\omega)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(t)\varphi(t-x)e^{-2\pi it\omega}\,dt,

where
\varphi
is the Gaussian window function
\varphi(t)=e^{-\pi t^{2}}
. If not specified otherwise, all functions
f\in{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}})}
are assumed to be complex-valued. For a subset
X\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
of the time-frequency plane we are interested in reconstructing
f\in\mathcal{C}\subseteq L^{2}({\mathbb{R}})
from

\{|\mathcal{G}f(x,\omega)|:(x,\omega)\in X\}
,
the corresponding spectrogram measurements of the Gabor transform given on
X
. Such problems arise, for instance, in ptychography
[
6
]
and audio processing
[
25
]
. In the language above, the linear functionals
\Phi_{\lambda}
are the maps
\Phi_{\lambda}(f)\coloneqq\langle f,T_{x}M_{\omega}\varphi\rangle
with
\lambda=(x,\omega)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
,
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
the
L^{2}
-inner product and
T_{x},M_{\omega}
the translation and modulation operator, defined by
T_{x}\psi(t)=\psi(\cdot-x)
and
M_{\omega}\psi(t)=e^{2\pi i\omega t}\psi(t)
, respectively. In order to work with a concise notion of uniqueness in the absence of phase information we introduce the following condition.

Definition 1.1
.

Let
\mathcal{C}\subseteq{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}})}
and
X\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
. We say that the pair
(\mathcal{C},X)
satisfies condition
(U)
if every
f\in\mathcal{C}
is determined up to a global phase by the values
\{|\mathcal{G}f(z)|:z\in X\}
. In formulas,

\forall f,h\in\mathcal{C}:(\,|\mathcal{G}f(z)|=|\mathcal{G}h(z)|\ \forall z\in X\implies\exists\tau\in{\mathbb{T}}:f=\tau h\,).

1.1
Previous work

In the following, we provide a list of pairs
(\mathcal{C},X)
which are known to satisfy condition
(\textbf{U})
.

1.

It is well-known that every
f\in{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}})}
is determined up to a global phase by the measurements
\{|\mathcal{G}f(x,\omega)|:(x,\omega)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}\}
and therefore
({L^{2}({\mathbb{R}})},{\mathbb{R}}^{2})
satisfies condition
(U)
. This is a direct consequence of the ambiguity-relation

{\mathcal{F}}(|\mathcal{G}f|^{2})(x,\xi)=A\hat{f}(x,\xi)\,\overline{A\varphi(x,\xi)},

and the fact that the ambiguity function
A\varphi
does not vanish on
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}

[
11
, Theorem A.2]
.
In the previous identity, the operator
{\mathcal{F}}:L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})\to L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
denotes the Fourier transform, defined on
L^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})\cap L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
via

{\mathcal{F}}f(\omega)=\hat{f}(\omega)=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}f(x)e^{-2\pi ix\cdot\omega}\,dx

and extends to a unitary operator on
L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
.
In practice, the measurements
|\mathcal{G}f(x,\omega)|
are never given on whole
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
and one seeks to prove similar uniqueness results where
X
is ”small” in the sense that
X
is, for instance, discrete or a set of measure zero.

2.

Assume that
e^{i\alpha_{1}}{\mathbb{R}},e^{i\alpha_{2}}{\mathbb{R}}\subseteq{\mathbb{C}}
are two lines which satisfy the condition
\alpha_{1}-\alpha_{2}\notin\pi{\mathbb{Q}}
. A result due to Jaming shows that every entire function of finite order is determined up to a global phase from its modulus on
e^{i\alpha_{1}}{\mathbb{R}}\cup e^{i\alpha_{2}}{\mathbb{R}}

[
17
, Theorem 3.3]
. This result was recently extended by Perez
[
22
]
. The Gabor transform is, except for a non-zero weighting factor and a reflection, an entire function. This follows from the relation

\mathcal{G}f(x,-\omega)=e^{\pi ix\omega}Bf(z)e^{-\frac{\pi}{2}|z|^{2}}

where
z=x+i\omega
and
Bf
is the Bargmann transform of
f

[
12
, Proposition 3.4.1]
. The Bargmann transform of a function
f\in{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}})}
is an entire function of order less or equal than two
[
14
, Theorem 2]
. As a direct consequence, Jaming’s theorem yields the following choice of
\mathcal{C}
and
X
.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}\in[0,2\pi)
with
\alpha_{1}-\alpha_{2}\notin\pi{\mathbb{Q}}
. Set
\mathcal{C}={L^{2}({\mathbb{R}})}
and
X=e^{i\alpha_{1}}{\mathbb{R}}\cup e^{i\alpha_{2}}{\mathbb{R}}
. Then
(\mathcal{C},X)
satisfies condition
(U)
.

Jaming’s result is based on Hadamard’s factorization theorem and its proof demonstrates that the two lines
e^{i\alpha_{1}}{\mathbb{R}},e^{i\alpha_{2}}{\mathbb{R}}
can be replaced by sets
A\subseteq e^{i\alpha_{1}}{\mathbb{R}},B\subseteq e^{i\alpha_{2}}{\mathbb{R}}
where
A,B
are sets of uniqueness of entire functions of finite order (e.g.
A
and
B
contain a limit point)
[
17
, Remark 3.4]
. In this case,
({L^{2}({\mathbb{R}})},A\cup B)
satisfies condition
(U)
as well.

3.

It follows readily from Theorem
1.2
that
({L^{2}({\mathbb{R}})},\mathcal{O})
satisfies condition
(U)
whenever
\mathcal{O}
is an open subset of
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
. For if
\mathcal{O}\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{2}\simeq{\mathbb{C}}
is open then we can find two lines
e^{i\alpha_{1}}{\mathbb{R}},e^{i\alpha_{2}}{\mathbb{R}}\subseteq{\mathbb{C}}
satisfying
\alpha_{1}-\alpha_{2}\notin\pi{\mathbb{Q}}
such that
A=e^{i\alpha_{1}}{\mathbb{R}}\cap\mathcal{O}
and
B=e^{i\alpha_{2}}{\mathbb{R}}\cap\mathcal{O}
contain a limit point.

4.

An example where the sampling set
X
is fully discrete can be found in
[
2
]
. In this article the authors show that if
\mathcal{C}=PW_{b}^{2}({\mathbb{R}},{\mathbb{R}})
is the class of real-valued Paley-Wiener functions,

PW_{b}^{2}({\mathbb{R}},{\mathbb{R}})=\{f\in L^{2}({\mathbb{R}},{\mathbb{R}}):{\mathrm{supp}}(\hat{f})\subseteq[-\tfrac{b}{2},\tfrac{b}{2}]\},

(1)

then the choice

satisfies condition
(U)

[

. We will return to this example in Section

To summarize,
1
,
2
and
3
above consider non-discrete sampling sets. In practice, however, the spectrogram can only be sampled on a discrete set
X
.
The only result applying to this case is
4
which is, however, only valid for the restricted class of real-valued and band-limited signals, whereas applications in diffraction imaging would rather require results for more general function classes.
In particular, it is unknown which classes of complex-valued functions (if any) can be uniquely recovered from discrete samples
X\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
of their spectrograms. In the present article we are primarily interested in the setting where

is a lattice in the time-frequency plane, i.e.

with

to a proper subspace of

Theorem 1.3
.

Let

Preliminaries

This section collects several preliminary results which will be used throughout the paper. In Section
2.1
we provide uniqueness results for Paley-Wiener and shift-invariant spaces, Section
2.2
discusses density results for translates of functions, and Section
2.3

2.1
Uniqueness sets

Our uniqueness results make use of uniqueness theorems for Paley-Wiener spaces and shift-invariant spaces with Gaussian generator. The notion of a uniqueness set is defined as follows.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
Q\subseteq C({\mathbb{R}})
be a class of real- or complex-valued, continuous functions. A set
\Lambda\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}
is called uniqueness set for
Q
if the implication

\left(f,h\in Q:f(\lambda)=h(\lambda)\ \forall\lambda\in\Lambda\right)\implies f(t)=h(t)\ \forall t\in{\mathbb{R}}

is satisfied.

Sufficient conditions on a set
\Lambda
to be a uniqueness set for Paley-Wiener spaces and shift-invariant spaces are given by means of their effective Beurling-Malliavin density and lower Beurling density, respectively. In the following we give a brief summary of the results relevant for the upcoming proofs.

2.1.1
Paley-Wiener spaces

For
b>0
we denote by
PW_{b}^{2}({\mathbb{R}})
the Paley-Wiener space which is defined by

PW_{b}^{2}({\mathbb{R}})=\{f\in L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}):{\mathrm{supp}}(\hat{f})\subseteq[-\tfrac{b}{2},\tfrac{b}{2}]\}.

The classical sampling theorem of Shannon, Whittaker and Kotel’nikov implies that every
f\in PW_{b}^{2}({\mathbb{R}})
is determined by its samples at
b^{-1}{\mathbb{Z}}

[
15
, Theorem 6.13]
. In particular,
f
can be recovered by the cardinal sine series

f(t)=\sum_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}f\left(\frac{n}{b}\right)\mathrm{sinc}(bt-n)

and
\Lambda=b^{-1}{\mathbb{Z}}
is a uniqueness set for
PW_{b}^{2}({\mathbb{R}})
. Clearly, if
f\in PW_{b}^{2}({\mathbb{R}})
then it follows from the representation

f(x)=\int_{-b/2}^{b/2}\hat{f}(\omega)e^{2\pi i\omega x}\,d\omega

that a set of real numbers
\Lambda\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}
is a uniqueness set for
PW_{b}^{2}({\mathbb{R}})
if and only if the complex exponentials

\mathcal{E}_{\Lambda}=\{e^{2\pi i\lambda x}:\lambda\in\Lambda\}

are complete in
L^{2}[-\tfrac{b}{2},\tfrac{b}{2}]
. Determining assumptions on
\Lambda
which imply completeness of
\mathcal{E}_{\Lambda}
is commonly known as the completeness problem of complex exponentials. In this context, a quantity of interest is the radius of completeness of
\Lambda
which is defined by

R(\Lambda)=\sup\{a\geq 0:\mathcal{E}_{\Lambda}\text{ is complete in }L^{2}[-\tfrac{a}{2},\tfrac{a}{2}]\}.

A formula for the radius of completeness in terms of a suitable density was derived by Beurling and Malliavin
[
4
,
5
]
. To state the corresponding theorem, we start by defining the so-called effective Beurling-Malliavin density. Several equivalent definitions of this density are known. We follow the approach given in
[
24
, Chapter 5]
.
For an interval
I\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}
denote by
|I|
its length and by
\mathrm{dist}(0,I)
its distance to the origin. A sequence
\{I_{n}:n\in{\mathbb{Z}}\}
of disjoint intervals on
{\mathbb{R}}
is called
long
if

\sum_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}\frac{|I_{n}|^{2}}{1+\mathrm{dist}^{2}(0,I_{n})}=\infty.

For a sequence
\Lambda\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}
of real numbers, the
effective Beurling-Malliavin density (effective BM density)
is defined as

D^{*}(\Lambda)\coloneqq\sup\{d\geq 0:\exists\text{ long }(I_{n})_{n}\text{ such that }\#(\Lambda\cap I_{n})\geq d|I_{n}|\ \forall n\},

where
\#(\Lambda\cap I_{n})
denotes the number of elements in
\Lambda\cap I_{n}
.
The Beurling-Malliavin theorem states that
D^{*}(\Lambda)
coincides with
R(\Lambda)

[
24
, Theorem 26]
.

Theorem 2.2

(Beurling-Malliavin)
.

If
\Lambda
is a discrete real sequence then
R(\Lambda)=D^{*}(\Lambda).
Consequently, if
a<D^{*}(\Lambda)<b
for some
0<a<b
then
\mathcal{E}_{\Lambda}
is complete in
L^{2}[-\tfrac{a}{2},\tfrac{a}{2}]
and incomplete in
L^{2}[-\tfrac{b}{2},\tfrac{b}{2}]
.

2.1.2
Shift-invariant spaces

Recent developments in sampling theory due to Gröchenig, Romero and Stöckler show that assumptions on a suitable density of a sequence can be used to obtain uniqueness and sampling results for shift-invariant spaces with totally positive generators of Gaussian type
[
13
]
. We start by defining shift-invariant spaces. Let
1\leq p\leq\infty
and let
\beta>0
be a step size. The shift-invariant space
V_{\beta}^{p}(\varphi)
, generated by the Gaussian
\varphi(t)=e^{-\pi t^{2}}
, is defined by

V_{\beta}^{p}(\varphi)\coloneqq\{f\in L^{p}({\mathbb{R}}):f=\sum_{k\in{\mathbb{Z}}}c_{k}\varphi(\cdot-\beta k),\ c\in\ell^{p}({\mathbb{Z}})\}.

(2)

Note that functions in
V_{\beta}^{p}(\varphi)
are continuous and if
1\leq p\leq q\leq\infty
then one has the inclusion

V_{\beta}^{1}(\varphi)\subseteq V_{\beta}^{p}(\varphi)\subseteq V_{\beta}^{q}(\varphi)

[
3
, Corollary 2.5]
.
Clearly, shift-invariant spaces can be defined in a more general fashion by replacing the Gaussian generator
\varphi
with a different generator
g
. In particular, the choice
g(t)=\mathrm{sinc}(bt)
for some
b>0
implies that
V_{1/b}^{2}(g)=PW_{b}^{2}({\mathbb{R}})
and we are in the situation of Section
2.1.1
. For a general introduction to shift-invariant spaces we refer to the survey
[
28
]
.
In order to obtain uniqueness results for shift-invariant spaces, we introduce the
lower Beurling density
.
If
\Lambda\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}
then the lower Beurling density of
\Lambda
is defined by

D^{-}(\Lambda)\coloneqq\liminf_{r\to\infty}\left(\inf_{a\in{\mathbb{R}}}\frac{\#(\Lambda\cap[a,a+r))}{r}\right).

In the situation where the generator is the Gaussian
\varphi
, we have the following uniqueness theorem
[
13
, Theorem 4.4]
.

Theorem 2.3
.

If
\Lambda\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}
has lower Beurling density
D^{-}(\Lambda)>\beta^{-1}
then
\Lambda
is a uniqueness set for
V_{\beta}^{\infty}(\varphi)
.

2.2
Müntz-Szász type approximations

We aim at combining the above-stated uniqueness theorems with results concerning the density of discrete translates which are based on the classical Müntz-Szász theorem
[
18
, Theorem 6.1]
.

Theorem 2.4

(Müntz-Szász)
.

Let
[a,b]
be a compact interval such that
a>0
and let
\Lambda\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}
be a sequence of distinct real numbers. Let
Q
be one of the spaces
C[a,b]
or
L^{p}[a,b]
with
1\leq p<\infty
. Then the sequence of powers
\{p_{\lambda}:\lambda\in\Lambda\},p_{\lambda}(t)=t^{\lambda},
is complete in
Q
if and only if

\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda}{}^{{}^{\prime}}\frac{1}{|\lambda|}=\infty,

where the symbol
\sum^{\prime}
indicates that the sum runs over all terms with nonvanishing denominator.

The Müntz-Szász theorem can be used to investigate under which assumptions a function space can be spanned by discrete translates of a single function
\psi
. Recall that for
\lambda\in{\mathbb{R}}
the shift-operator
T_{\lambda}
is defined by
T_{\lambda}\psi=\psi(\cdot-\lambda)
.

Definition 2.5
.

Let
I\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}
be an interval and let
\Lambda\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}
be a set of real numbers. We say that a function
\psi\in{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}})}
is a
\Lambda
-generator for
L^{2}(I)
if

{\mathrm{span}\,}\{T_{\lambda}\psi:\lambda\in\Lambda\}

is dense in
L^{2}(I)
.

For instance, Wiener’s Tauberian theorem
[
31
, Theorem 1]
implies that every
\psi
which has an a.e. non-vanishing Fourier transform is an
{\mathbb{R}}
-generator for
{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}})}
. It is natural to ask if
\Lambda
can be chosen to be discrete. It then depends on
\Lambda
and the topology of
I
whether or not there exists a function that generates
L^{2}(I)
. For example, there exists no
{\mathbb{Z}}
-generator for
{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}})}
but an arbitrary perturbation of
{\mathbb{Z}}
of the form

\Lambda=\{n+a_{n}:a_{n}\neq 0,a_{n}\to 0\}

admits a
\Lambda
-generator for
{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}})}

[
20
]
. If
\psi
is a Gaussian then the following result due to Zalik provides a Müntz-type condition on
\Lambda
such that
\psi
is a
\Lambda
-generator for
L^{2}(I)
whenever
I
is a compact interval
[
32
, Theorem 4]
.

Theorem 2.6

(Zalik)
.

Let
I\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}
be a compact interval and let
\psi(t)=ae^{-b^{2}(t-c)^{2}}
be a Gaussian,
a,b>0,c\in{\mathbb{R}}
. Further, let
\Lambda\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}
be a sequence of distinct real numbers. Then
\psi
is a
\Lambda
-generator for
L^{2}(I)
if and only if the series
\sum^{\prime}_{\lambda\in\Lambda}|\lambda|^{-1}
diverges.

2.3
Fractional Fourier transform

Let
\{h_{n}:n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}\}\subseteq{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}})}
be the Hermite basis functions for
{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}})}
,

h_{n}(t)=\frac{2^{1/4}}{\sqrt{n!}}\left(-\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\right)^{n}e^{\pi t^{2}}\left(\frac{d}{dt}\right)^{n}e^{-2\pi t^{2}}.

For
\theta\in{\mathbb{R}}
, the operator
{\mathcal{F}}_{\theta}:{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}})}\to{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}})}
, defined by

{\mathcal{F}}_{\theta}f=\sum_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}}e^{-i\theta n}\langle f,h_{n}\rangle h_{n},

is called the fractional Fourier transform of order
\theta
. Denoting by
u_{\theta}:{\mathbb{R}}\to{\mathbb{C}}
the chirp modulation function
u_{\theta}(t)=e^{-i\pi|t|^{2}\cot\theta}
then the fractional Fourier transform satisfies the relation

{\mathcal{F}}_{\theta}f(\omega)=c_{\theta}u_{\theta}(\omega){\mathcal{F}}(u_{\theta}f)(\tfrac{\omega}{\sin\theta})

where
c_{\theta}=\sqrt{1-i\cot\theta}
is a normalization factor and
{\mathcal{F}}:{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}})}\to{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}})}
denotes the ordinary Fourier transform. If we define, in addition, the rotation matrix
R_{\theta}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2\times 2}
by

R_{\theta}\coloneqq\left({\begin{array}[]{cc}\cos\theta&-\sin\theta\\
\sin\theta&\cos\theta\\
\end{array}}\right)

(3)

then the operator
{\mathcal{F}}_{\theta}
has the following properties
[
16
,
21
]
.

Lemma 2.7
.

Let
\varphi(t)=e^{-\pi t^{2}}
be the standard Gaussian and let
\theta,\eta\in{\mathbb{R}}
. Then the fractional Fourier transform enjoys the following properties.

1.

{\mathcal{F}}_{\theta}\varphi=\varphi
for every
\theta\in{\mathbb{R}}
whenever
\varphi
is the Gaussian
\varphi(t)=e^{-\pi t^{2}}

2.

{\mathcal{F}}_{\theta}{\mathcal{F}}_{\eta}={\mathcal{F}}_{\theta+\eta}
for every
\theta,\eta\in{\mathbb{R}}

3.

{\mathcal{F}}_{\frac{\pi}{2}}={\mathcal{F}}
,
{\mathcal{F}}_{-\frac{\pi}{2}}={\mathcal{F}}^{-1}
and
{\mathcal{F}}_{0}=\mathrm{Id}

4.

A(f,g)(R_{\theta}z)=A({\mathcal{F}}_{\theta}f,{\mathcal{F}}_{\theta}g)(z)
for every
z\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
,
\theta\in{\mathbb{R}}
and every
f,g\in{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}})}
where
A(f,g)
denotes the cross-ambiguity function of
f
and
g
,

A(f,g)(x,\omega)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(t+\tfrac{x}{2})\overline{g(t-\tfrac{x}{2})}e^{-2\pi it\omega}\,dt.

3
Main results

In this section, we prove the main results of the present paper. In Section
3.1
we derive uniqueness results for compactly-supported functions followed by a comparison of our results to the real-valued setting. Section
3.3
establishes analogous results for Gaussian shift-invariant spaces.

3.1
Compactly-supported functions

For a function
h:{\mathbb{R}}\to{\mathbb{C}}
and a value
\tau\in{\mathbb{R}}
we introduce the shorthand notation

h_{\tau}\coloneqq(T_{\tau}h)\overline{h},

(4)

where
T_{\tau}
is the translation operator.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
b,c>0
and
\mathcal{C}=L^{4}[-\tfrac{c}{2},\tfrac{c}{2}]
. Then the choice

X=\tfrac{1}{b}{\mathbb{Z}}\times\tfrac{1}{2c}{\mathbb{Z}}

implies that the pair
(\mathcal{C},X)
satisfies condition
(U)
.
In other words, every
f\in L^{4}[-\tfrac{c}{2},\tfrac{c}{2}]
is determined up to a global phase by the measurements

\{|\mathcal{G}f(x,\omega)|\,:\,(x,\omega)\in\tfrac{1}{b}{\mathbb{Z}}\times\tfrac{1}{2c}{\mathbb{Z}}\}.

Proof.

We denote by
\varphi(t)=e^{-\pi t^{2}}
the Gaussian window function. Further, we define
p_{x}(t)\coloneqq f(t+\frac{x}{2})\overline{\varphi(t-\frac{x}{2})}
. From the definition of the cross-ambiguity function it follows that

A(f,\varphi)(x,\omega)\overline{A(f,\varphi)(x,\omega)}={\mathcal{F}}p_{x}(\omega)\overline{{\mathcal{F}}p_{x}(\omega)}={\mathcal{F}^{-1}}({\mathcal{F}}^{2}p_{x}*\overline{p_{x}})(\omega).

The convolution
{\mathcal{F}}^{2}p_{x}*\overline{p_{x}}
can be written as

({\mathcal{F}}^{2}p_{x}*\overline{p_{x}})(\omega)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}T_{\omega}f(k)\,\overline{f(k)}\,T_{x}[\overline{T_{\omega}\varphi(k)}\,\varphi(k)]\,dk=\langle f_{\omega},T_{x}\varphi_{\omega}\rangle,

where we used the change of variables
k\mapsto k-\frac{x}{2}
in the first equality as well as the notation as defined in equation (
4
). From the fact that
\mathcal{G}f(x,\omega)=e^{-\pi ix\omega}A(f,\varphi)(x,\omega)
we obtain the identity

{\mathcal{F}}_{2}(|\mathcal{G}f|^{2})(x,\omega)=\langle f_{\omega},T_{x}\varphi_{\omega}\rangle,

(5)

where
{\mathcal{F}}_{2}
denotes the Fourier transform with respect to the second argument. Observe that
f_{\omega}=0
a.e. whenever
|\omega|>c
. Consequently, for every
x\in{\mathbb{R}}
we have
|\mathcal{G}f(x,\cdot)|^{2}\in PW_{2c}^{2}({\mathbb{R}})
. Now suppose that a function
h\in L^{4}[-\tfrac{c}{2},\tfrac{c}{2}]
satisfies

|\mathcal{G}f(z)|=|\mathcal{G}h(z)|

for every
z\in\frac{1}{b}{\mathbb{Z}}\times\frac{1}{2c}{\mathbb{Z}}
. By Shannon’s sampling theorem we know that
\frac{1}{2c}{\mathbb{Z}}
is a uniqueness set for
PW_{2c}^{2}({\mathbb{R}})
. Consequently, the functions
|\mathcal{G}f(\lambda,\cdot)|^{2}
and
|\mathcal{G}h(\lambda,\cdot)|^{2}
agree for ever
\lambda\in\frac{1}{b}{\mathbb{Z}}
. Taking the Fourier transform with respect to the second argument and consulting equation (
5
) gives the relation

\langle f_{\omega}-h_{\omega},T_{\lambda}\varphi_{\omega}\rangle=0

which holds for every
\omega\in{\mathbb{R}}
and every
\lambda\in\frac{1}{b}{\mathbb{Z}}
. Observe, that for a fixed
\omega
, the function
\varphi_{\omega}
is a Gaussian of the form
\varphi_{\omega}(t)=ae^{-b^{2}(t-d)^{2}}
for some
a,b>0,d\in{\mathbb{R}}
. Clearly, the series

\sum_{\lambda\in\frac{1}{b}{\mathbb{Z}}}{}^{{}^{\prime}}\frac{1}{|\lambda|}=b\sum_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}\setminus\{0\}}|n|^{-1}

is divergent and it follows from Zalik’s theorem that the system of translates
\{T_{\lambda}\varphi_{\omega}:\lambda\in\frac{1}{b}{\mathbb{Z}}\}
is complete in
L^{2}[-\tfrac{c}{2},\tfrac{c}{2}]
. Note that
f_{\omega}
and
h_{\omega}
have support in
[-\tfrac{c}{2},\tfrac{c}{2}]
and by Hölder’s inequality
f_{\omega}-h_{\omega}\in L^{2}[-\tfrac{c}{2},\tfrac{c}{2}]
. Therefore,
f_{\omega}=h_{\omega}
for every
\omega\in{\mathbb{R}}
. The choice
\omega=0
shows that

|f|^{2}=|h|^{2}

(6)

and
h=0
whenever
f=0
. If
f\neq 0
, select a point
p\in{\mathbb{R}}
for which
f(p)\neq 0
. Then
f(t)\overline{f(p)}=h(t)\overline{h(p)}
for every
t
. Hence, there exists a
\tau\in{\mathbb{C}}
such that
f=\tau h
. Equation (
6
) implies
\tau\in{\mathbb{T}}
.
∎

In Theorem
3.1
the sampling set
X=\frac{1}{b}{\mathbb{Z}}\times\frac{1}{2c}{\mathbb{Z}}
is a lattice,
X=A{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}
with
A=\mathrm{diag}(\frac{1}{b},\frac{1}{2c})
. This lattice was chosen in such a way that both Shannon’s sampling theorem and Zalik’s theorem are applicable. In view of Theorem
2.2
and the assumptions of Zalik’s theorem, the lattice
A
can be replaced by more general sampling sets as the following result shows.

Theorem 3.2
.

Suppose that
A,B\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}
are two sequences of real numbers with the following properties:

1.

the elements of
A
are distinct and the series of reciprocals
\sum^{\prime}_{a\in A}|a|^{-1}
diverges,

2.

B
is a uniqueness set for
PW_{2c}^{2}({\mathbb{R}})
for some
c>0
.

Then every
f\in L^{4}[-\tfrac{c}{2},\tfrac{c}{2}]
is determined up to a global phase by the values
|\mathcal{G}f(A\times B)|
. In particular, the pair
(L^{4}[-\tfrac{c}{2},\tfrac{c}{2}],A\times B)
satisfies condition
(U)
.

Proof.

Arguing in a similar fashion as in the proof of Theorem
3.1
, we observe that
|\mathcal{G}f(x,\cdot)|^{2},|\mathcal{G}h(x,\cdot)|^{2}\in PW_{2c}^{2}({\mathbb{R}})
for every
x\in{\mathbb{R}}
provided that
f,h\in L^{4}[\frac{c}{2},\frac{c}{2}]
. The assumption on
B
being a uniqueness set for
PW_{2c}^{2}({\mathbb{R}})
shows that for every
a\in A
the implication

\begin{split}&|\mathcal{G}f(a,b)|^{2}=|\mathcal{G}h(a,b)|^{2}\ \ \forall b\in B\\
\implies&|\mathcal{G}f(a,\omega)|^{2}=|\mathcal{G}h(a,\omega)|^{2}\ \ \ \ \forall\omega\in{\mathbb{R}}\end{split}

holds true. Using identity (
5
) and applying the Fourier transform results in the equation

\langle f_{\omega},T_{a}\varphi_{\omega}\rangle=\langle h_{\omega},T_{a}\varphi_{\omega}\rangle

which holds for every
a\in A
and every
\omega\in{\mathbb{R}}
. Since
\varphi_{\omega}
is a Gaussian,
f_{\omega}-h_{\omega}\in L^{2}[-\frac{c}{2},\frac{c}{2}]
and
\sum^{\prime}_{a\in A}|a|^{-1}=\infty
, Zalik’s theorem implies that
f_{\omega}=h_{\omega}
for every
\omega\in{\mathbb{R}}
. This gives
f=\tau h
for some
\tau\in{\mathbb{T}}
which concludes the proof of the statement.
∎

Note that in Theorem
3.2
the assumption on
B
being a uniqueness set for
PW_{2c}^{2}({\mathbb{R}})
is satisfied if one requires that
B
has effective Beurling-Malliavin density
D^{*}(B)>2c
.
For instance, we could choose
B=b+\beta{\mathbb{Z}}
where
b\geq 0
is a non-negative real number and
0<\beta\leq\frac{1}{2c}
. Further, if
A
takes the form
A=a+\alpha{\mathbb{Z}}
with
a\geq 0
and
\alpha>0
then
A
satisfies condition 1 of Theorem
3.2
which implies that the pair
(L^{4}[-\tfrac{c}{2},\tfrac{c}{2}],(a+\alpha{\mathbb{Z}})\times(b+\beta{\mathbb{Z}}))
satisfies condition
(U)
. This sampling set is a shifted lattice and is visualized in Figure
1
.

Figure 1:
Lattice in the time-frequency plane. Functions in
L^{4}[-\tfrac{c}{2},\tfrac{c}{2}]
are determined from measurements given on lattices of the form
(a+\alpha{\mathbb{Z}})\times(b+\beta{\mathbb{Z}})
where
a,b\geq 0
and
\alpha>0
are arbitrary and
\beta
satisfies
0<\beta\leq\frac{1}{2c}
.

Observe further that in Theorem
3.2
the uniqueness set
B
does not need to be a priori fixed. In fact, for every single
a\in A
we can choose a different uniqueness set
S^{a}\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}
.

Theorem 3.3
.

Let
c>0
and let
A\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}
be a sequence of distinct real numbers with the property that the series of reciprocals
\sum^{\prime}_{a\in A}|a|^{-1}
diverges. For every
a\in A
let
S^{a}
be a uniqueness set for
PW_{2c}^{2}({\mathbb{R}})
. Set

X=\bigcup_{a\in A}\{(a,s):s\in S^{a}\}

and
\mathcal{C}=L^{4}[-\tfrac{c}{2},\tfrac{c}{2}]
. Then
(\mathcal{C},X)
satisfies condition
(U)
.

Proof.

Suppose that
f,h\in L^{4}[-\frac{c}{2},\frac{c}{2}]
are such that

|\mathcal{G}f(z)|=|\mathcal{G}h(z)|\ \ \forall z\in X.

With an analogous argument as in the proof of Theorem
3.2
we infer from the assumption on
S^{a}
being a uniqueness set for
PW_{2c}^{2}({\mathbb{R}})
that

|\mathcal{G}f(a,\omega)|=|\mathcal{G}h(a,\omega)|\ \ \forall a\in A\ \forall\omega\in{\mathbb{R}}.

An application of the Fourier transform together with the identity (
5
) shows that

\langle f_{\omega}-h_{\omega},T_{a}\varphi_{\omega}\rangle=0\ \ \forall a\in A\ \forall\omega\in{\mathbb{R}}.

By assumption, the series of reciprocals
\sum^{\prime}_{a\in A}|a|^{-1}
diverges. Therefore, Zalik’s theorem implies that
f_{\omega}=h_{\omega}
for every
\omega\in{\mathbb{R}}
which in turn yields
f=\tau h
for some
\tau\in{\mathbb{T}}
.
∎

Consulting Section
2.3
, new uniqueness results can be obtained via a phase-space rotation. In particular, all of the previous theorems can be stated in an analogous manner for band-limited functions by means of a rotation of the lattice by 90 degrees.

Proposition 3.4
.

Let
\theta\in{\mathbb{R}}
and let
R_{\theta}
be the rotation matrix as defined in equation
3
. Further, let
b>0
and
\mathcal{C}_{\theta}\coloneqq{\mathcal{F}}_{-\theta}L^{4}[-\tfrac{c}{2},\tfrac{c}{2}]
. If

X_{\theta}\coloneqq R_{\theta}(\tfrac{1}{b}{\mathbb{Z}}\times\tfrac{1}{2c}{\mathbb{Z}})\coloneqq\{R_{\theta}z:z\in\tfrac{1}{b}{\mathbb{Z}}\times\tfrac{1}{2c}{\mathbb{Z}}\}

then
(\mathcal{C}_{\theta},X_{\theta})
satisfies condition
(U)
. In particular, all band-limited functions in the space

\mathcal{C}_{\frac{\pi}{2}}={\mathcal{F}^{-1}}L^{4}[-\tfrac{c}{2},\tfrac{c}{2}]=\{f\in PW_{c}^{2}({\mathbb{R}}):\hat{f}\in L^{4}[-\tfrac{c}{2},\tfrac{c}{2}]\}

are determined up to a global phase by the measurements

\{|\mathcal{G}f(z)|:z\in\tfrac{1}{2c}{\mathbb{Z}}\times\tfrac{1}{b}{\mathbb{Z}}\}.

Proof.

Suppose that
f,h\in\mathcal{C}_{\theta}
satisfy

|\mathcal{G}f(R_{\theta}z)|=|\mathcal{G}h(R_{\theta}z)|

for every
z\in\frac{1}{2c}{\mathbb{Z}}\times\frac{1}{b}{\mathbb{Z}}
. From the properties of the fractional Fourier transform (see Lemma
2.7
) it follows that

|\mathcal{G}({\mathcal{F}}_{\theta}f)(z)|=|\mathcal{G}({\mathcal{F}}_{\theta}h)(z)|

for every
z\in\frac{1}{b}{\mathbb{Z}}\times\frac{1}{2c}{\mathbb{Z}}
. By assumption,
{\mathcal{F}}_{\theta}f
and
{\mathcal{F}}_{\theta}h
are elements of
L^{4}[-\tfrac{c}{2},\tfrac{c}{2}]
. Hence, the first part of the statement follows from Theorem
3.1
. Since
{\mathcal{F}}_{-\frac{\pi}{2}}={\mathcal{F}^{-1}}
and

R_{\frac{\pi}{2}}=\left({\begin{array}[]{cc}0&-1\\
1&0\\
\end{array}}\right)

the second part of the statement is a special case of the first.
∎

3.2
Real-valued maps with compact support

In the previous section, we derived uniqueness theorems for complex-valued functions under a support condition. We wish to elaborate on these results by providing a comparison to the setting where besides a support condition an additional real-valuedness property holds. To that end, suppose that
f\in L^{2}[-\frac{c}{2},\frac{c}{2}]
has a real-valued Fourier transform.
Since the Gabor transform satisfies the relation
|\mathcal{G}f(x,\omega)|=|\mathcal{G}\hat{f}(-\omega,x)|
whenever
f\in{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}})}
and
(x,\omega)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
, the uniqueness problem for compactly-supported functions with a real-valued Fourier transform is equivalent to the uniqueness problem in
PW_{c}^{2}({\mathbb{R}},{\mathbb{R}})
, the class of real-valued Paley-Wiener functions. This is precisely the setting mentioned in Section
1.1
and considered in
[
2
]
where it was shown that if
\mathcal{C}=PW_{c}^{2}({\mathbb{R}},{\mathbb{R}})
and
X=\frac{1}{2c}{\mathbb{Z}}\times\{0\}
then
(\mathcal{C},X)
satisfies condition
(U)
. Note that this result is an application of the fact that a function in
PW_{c}^{2}({\mathbb{R}},{\mathbb{R}})
is determined up to a global phase by samples of its modulus
[
29
]
.

Theorem 3.5

(Thakur)
.

Let
p,q\in PW_{c}^{2}({\mathbb{R}},{\mathbb{R}})
such that
|p(x)|=|q(x)|
for every
x\in\frac{1}{2c}{\mathbb{Z}}
. Then there exists a constant
\tau\in\{1,-1\}
such that
p=\tau q
.

The corresponding uniqueness result in
PW_{c}^{2}({\mathbb{R}},{\mathbb{R}})
from spectrogram samples follows readily: since
|\mathcal{G}f(x,0)|=|f*\varphi(x)|
, the assumption that

|\mathcal{G}f(z)|=|\mathcal{G}h(z)|\ \forall z\in\tfrac{1}{2c}{\mathbb{Z}}\times\{0\}

for some
f,h\in PW_{c}^{2}({\mathbb{R}},{\mathbb{R}})
is equivalent to the relation

|f*\varphi(x)|=|h*\varphi(x)|\ \forall x\in\tfrac{1}{2c}{\mathbb{Z}}.

Since
f*\varphi,h*\varphi\in PW_{c}^{2}({\mathbb{R}},{\mathbb{R}})
, an application of Thakur’s theorem and the fact that
\hat{\varphi}
does not vanish yields the existence of a
\tau\in\{1,-1\}\subseteq{\mathbb{T}}
so that
f=\tau h
. In contrast, if
f
is complex-valued then
f*\varphi
is a complex-valued band-limited function in
PW_{c}^{2}({\mathbb{R}})
. But functions in
PW_{c}^{2}({\mathbb{R}})
are
not
determined up to a global phase by samples of their moduli, even if the samples are given on the entire real line.

Example 3.6
.

Suppose that the Fourier transform of
f\in PW_{c}^{2}({\mathbb{R}})
has support in the interval
[-\frac{c}{4},\frac{c}{4}]
. For
\varepsilon\in[-\frac{c}{4},\frac{c}{4}]\setminus\{0\}
define

f_{\varepsilon}(t)=e^{2\pi i\varepsilon t}f(t).

Then the Fourier transform of
f_{\varepsilon}
has support in
[-\frac{c}{2},\frac{c}{2}]
which gives
f_{\varepsilon}\in PW_{c}^{2}({\mathbb{R}})
. Since
\varepsilon\neq 0
the support of
{\mathcal{F}}f
and
{\mathcal{F}}(f_{\varepsilon})
do not coincide. In particular, there exists no
\tau\in{\mathbb{T}}
so that
f=\tau f_{\varepsilon}
. On the other hand,
|f(t)|=|f_{\varepsilon}(t)|
for every
t\in{\mathbb{R}}
.

The fact that complex-valued band-limited signals are not determined by their moduli produces a natural obstacle in generalizing the proof above which is based on Thakur’s theorem from the real-valued setting to the complex-valued setting. However, it yields the new insight that Gabor phase retrieval under a real-valuedness assumption is possible via sampling on a 1-dimensional lattice whereas uniqueness for complex-valued maps is achieved by an extension to a 2-dimensional lattice in the time-frequency plane. This is summarized in

Theorem 3.7
.

Let
b,c>0
and consider the class of functions in
L^{4}[-\tfrac{c}{2},\tfrac{c}{2}]
which have a real-valued Fourier transform,

\mathcal{C}_{\mathbb{R}}=\{f\in L^{4}[-\tfrac{c}{2},\tfrac{c}{2}]:{\mathcal{F}}f\ \text{is real-valued}\,\}.

If
X_{\mathbb{R}}=\{0\}\times\frac{1}{2c}{\mathbb{Z}}
then
(\mathcal{C}_{\mathbb{R}},X_{\mathbb{R}})
satisfies condition
(U)
. On the other hand, if

\mathcal{C}_{\mathbb{C}}=L^{4}[-\tfrac{c}{2},\tfrac{c}{2}]

and
X_{\mathbb{C}}
is the extended lattice
X_{\mathbb{C}}=\frac{1}{b}{\mathbb{Z}}\times\frac{1}{2c}{\mathbb{Z}}
then
(\mathcal{C}_{\mathbb{C}},X_{\mathbb{C}})
satisfies condition
(U)
.

Proof.

Suppose that
f,h\in\mathcal{C}_{\mathbb{R}}
are such that

|\mathcal{G}f(z)|=|\mathcal{G}h(z)|

(7)

for every
z\in\{0\}\times\frac{1}{2c}{\mathbb{Z}}
. Since
|\mathcal{G}u(x,\omega)|=|\mathcal{G}\hat{u}(-\omega,x)|
for every
(x,\omega)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
and every
u\in{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}})}
it follows that equation (
7
) is equivalent to the validity of the identity

|\mathcal{G}\hat{f}(x,0)|=|\mathcal{G}\hat{h}(x,0)|

for every
x\in\frac{1}{2c}{\mathbb{Z}}
. The inclusion
L^{4}[-\frac{c}{2},\frac{c}{2}]\subseteq L^{2}[-\frac{c}{2},\frac{c}{2}]
implies that
\hat{f},\hat{h}\in PW_{c}^{2}({\mathbb{R}},{\mathbb{R}})
. Further, we have

|\mathcal{G}\hat{f}(x,0)|=|\hat{f}*\varphi(x)|

with
\hat{f}*\varphi\in PW_{c}^{2}({\mathbb{R}},{\mathbb{R}})
and the same holds true when
\hat{f}
gets replaced by
\hat{h}
. Hence, the modulus of the two functions
\hat{f}*\varphi,\hat{h}*\varphi\in PW_{c}^{2}({\mathbb{R}},{\mathbb{R}})
agrees on
\frac{1}{2c}{\mathbb{Z}}
. Thakur’s theorem (Theorem
3.5
) yields the existence of a constant
\tau\in\{1,-1\}\subseteq{\mathbb{T}}
so that
\hat{f}*\varphi=\tau(\hat{h}*\varphi)
. By Fourier uniqueness and the fact that
\varphi
does not vanish, the previous identity implies that
f
and
h
agree up to a global phase. The second statement of the theorem is the content of Theorem
3.1
.
∎

3.3
Shift-invariant spaces with Gaussian generator

All of the theorems in the previous chapter used the fact that our signal space
\mathcal{C}
consists of functions which are compactly-supported in the time domain or the frequency domain (or more general: their fractional Fourier transform has compact support). We wish to make use of the uniqueness Theorem
2.3
which holds for shift-invariant spaces with Gaussian generator to determine sets
X\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
consisting of separated points such that
(V_{\beta}^{1}(\varphi),X)
satisfies condition
(U)
. Recall that a set
X\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
is called separated if

\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}x,x^{\prime}\in X\\
x\neq x^{\prime}\end{subarray}}|x-x^{\prime}|>0

where
|\cdot|
denotes the Euclidean distance. We first discuss the general case where the considered signals are complex-valued. In analogy to Theorem
3.7
we then proceed by discussing the real-valued case and show that as in the case of compactly-supported functions, real-valued maps in Gaussian shift-invariant spaces are determined by samples on a 1-dimensional lattice whereas uniqueness in the complex case is achieved by an extension to a 2-dimensional lattice. To tackle the complex case we start with an algebraic lemma.

Lemma 3.8
.

Let
\beta>0
and
f=\sum_{k\in{\mathbb{Z}}}c_{k}\varphi(\cdot-\beta k)\in V_{\beta}^{1}(\varphi)
. Then the modulus of the Gabor transform of
f
can be written as

|\mathcal{G}f(x,\omega)|^{2}=\sum_{k\in{\mathbb{Z}}}\sum_{j\in{\mathbb{Z}}}c_{k}\overline{c_{j}}a(j,k,\beta)M_{\frac{\beta}{2}(j-k)}\varphi(\omega)T_{\frac{\beta}{2}(j+k)}\varphi(x),

where
a(j,k,\beta)=\frac{1}{2}e^{-\frac{\pi\beta^{2}}{4}(k-j)^{2}}
.

Proof.

If
f=\sum_{k\in{\mathbb{Z}}}c_{k}\varphi(\cdot-\beta k)
then it follows from the properties of the Gabor transform that

\mathcal{G}f(x,\omega)=\sum_{k\in{\mathbb{Z}}}c_{k}e^{-2\pi i\beta k\omega}\mathcal{G}\varphi(x-\beta k,\omega).

Note that the Gabor transform of the Gaussian
\varphi
is given by
\mathcal{G}\varphi(x,\omega)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}e^{-\pi ix\omega}e^{-\frac{\pi}{2}(x^{2}+\omega^{2})}
and therefore

\mathcal{G}f(x,\omega)=\sum_{k\in{\mathbb{Z}}}\frac{c_{k}}{\sqrt{2}}e^{-2\pi i\beta k\omega}e^{-\pi i(x-\beta k)\omega}e^{-\frac{\pi}{2}((x-\beta k)^{2}+\omega^{2})}.

Consequently, we obtain the identity

|\mathcal{G}f(x,\omega)|^{2}=\sum_{k\in{\mathbb{Z}}}\sum_{j\in{\mathbb{Z}}}\frac{c_{k}\overline{c_{j}}}{2}e^{\pi i\beta(j-k)\omega}e^{-\frac{\pi}{2}((x-\beta k)^{2}+(x-\beta j)^{2}+2\omega^{2})}.

But

e^{-\frac{\pi}{2}((x-\beta k)^{2}+(x-\beta j)^{2}+2\omega^{2})}=e^{-\frac{\pi\beta^{2}}{4}(k-j)^{2}}\varphi(\omega)\varphi(x-\tfrac{\beta}{2}(j+k))

which yields the statement.
∎

The previous representation of
|\mathcal{G}f(x,\omega)|^{2}
will be used to provide a condition on the step size
\beta
such that every function in
V_{\beta}^{1}(\varphi)
is determined up to a global phase from lattice measurements. This is the content of

Theorem 3.9
.

Let
\beta\in{\mathbb{R}}_{>0}\setminus{\mathbb{Q}}_{>0}
and let
\Lambda\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}
be a sequence with lower Beurling density
D^{-}(\Lambda)>\frac{2}{\beta}
. Then every
f\in V_{\beta}^{1}(\varphi)
is determined up to a global phase by the measurements

\{|\mathcal{G}f(x,\omega)|:(x,\omega)\in\Lambda\times{\mathbb{Z}}\}.

In particular, for every
\varepsilon>0
and every
\beta\in{\mathbb{R}}_{>0}\setminus{\mathbb{Q}}_{>0}
the pair
(V_{\beta}^{1}(\varphi),\frac{\beta}{2+\varepsilon}{\mathbb{Z}}\times{\mathbb{Z}})
satisfies condition
(U)
.

Proof.

Suppose that
f=\sum_{k\in{\mathbb{Z}}}c_{k}\varphi(\cdot-\beta k)\in V_{\beta}^{1}(\varphi)
with
(c_{k})_{k}\in\ell^{1}({\mathbb{Z}}).
Let
a(j,k,\beta)
be defined as in Lemma
3.8
. Define a sequence
(A_{n})_{n}\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}
via

A_{n}=\sum_{j,k\in{\mathbb{Z}},\,j+k=n}c_{k}\overline{c_{j}}a(j,k,\beta)M_{\frac{\beta}{2}(j-k)}\varphi(\omega)

where
\omega\in{\mathbb{R}}
is fixed. Moreover, for any fixed
x\in{\mathbb{R}}
define the sequence
(B_{n})_{n}\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}
via

B_{n}=\sum_{j,k\in{\mathbb{Z}},\,j-k=n}c_{k}\overline{c_{j}}a(j,k,\beta)T_{\frac{\beta}{2}(j+k)}\varphi(x).

It follows from Lemma
3.8
that

|\mathcal{G}f(x,\omega)|^{2}=\sum_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}A_{n}T_{\frac{\beta}{2}n}\varphi(x)=\sum_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}B_{n}M_{\frac{\beta}{2}n}\varphi(\omega).

Since
|a(j,k,\beta)|\leq 1
for every
j,k\in{\mathbb{Z}}
and every
\beta>0
we have
\left\|A\right\|_{{\ell^{1}({\mathbb{Z}})}}\leq\varphi(\omega)\left\|c\right\|_{{\ell^{1}({\mathbb{Z}})}}^{2}
and
\left\|B\right\|_{{\ell^{1}({\mathbb{Z}})}}\leq\left\|c\right\|_{{\ell^{1}({\mathbb{Z}})}}^{2}
. We conclude that for every
\omega\in{\mathbb{R}}
, the map
x\mapsto|\mathcal{G}f(x,\omega)|^{2}
is an element of the shift-invariant space
V_{\frac{\beta}{2}}^{1}(\varphi)
. In addition, for every
x\in{\mathbb{R}}
the map

\omega\mapsto\frac{|\mathcal{G}f(x,\omega)|^{2}}{\varphi(\omega)}

is continuous and
\frac{2}{\beta}
-periodic. Now suppose that
h\in V_{\beta}^{1}(\varphi)
satisfies

|\mathcal{G}f(\lambda,n)|=|\mathcal{G}h(\lambda,n)|

for every
(\lambda,n)\in\Lambda\times{\mathbb{Z}}
. According to the previous arguments, we have
|\mathcal{G}f(\cdot,n)|^{2},|\mathcal{G}h(\cdot,n)|^{2}\in V_{\frac{\beta}{2}}^{1}(\varphi)
. By Theorem
2.3
,
\Lambda
is a uniqueness set for
V_{\frac{\beta}{2}}^{1}(\varphi)
and therefore

|\mathcal{G}f(x,n)|=|\mathcal{G}h(x,n)|

for every
x\in{\mathbb{R}}
and every
n\in{\mathbb{Z}}
. Fix
x\in{\mathbb{R}}
. It follows that

\frac{|\mathcal{G}f(x,\omega)|^{2}}{\varphi(\omega)}=\frac{|\mathcal{G}h(x,\omega)|^{2}}{\varphi(\omega)}

(8)

whenever
\omega\in{\mathbb{Z}}
. By periodicity, the left- and right-hand side of equation (
8
) agree for every
\omega\in D
where

D\coloneqq\left\{n\ \mathrm{mod}\ \frac{2}{\beta}:n\in{\mathbb{Z}}\right\}=\frac{2}{\beta}\left\{\frac{\beta}{2}n\ \mathrm{mod}\ 1:n\in{\mathbb{Z}}\right\}.

Since
\beta
is irrational, it follows from Weyl’s equidistribution theorem that the sequence
\{\frac{\beta}{2}n\ \mathrm{mod}\ 1:n\in{\mathbb{Z}}\}
is uniformly distributed in
[0,1]
(see
[
30
, Satz 2]
). Hence, both sides of equation (
8
) agree on a dense subset of
[0,\frac{2}{\beta}]
. By periodicity and continuity, we conclude that equation (
8
) holds for every
\omega\in{\mathbb{R}}
. Since
x
was arbitrary it follows that the modulus of the Gabor transform of
f
agrees with the modulus of the Gabor transform of
h
on whole
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
. Consequently, there exists a
\tau\in{\mathbb{T}}
such that
f=\tau h
(see Section
1.1
). The second statement follows from the fact that the lower Beurling density of
\tfrac{\beta}{2+\varepsilon}{\mathbb{Z}}
satisfies

D^{-}(\tfrac{\beta}{2+\varepsilon}{\mathbb{Z}})=\tfrac{2+\varepsilon}{\beta}>\tfrac{2}{\beta}.

∎

In the previous theorem, the step size
\beta
was chosen in such a way that the sequence
\{n\ \mathrm{mod}\ \frac{2}{\beta}:n\in{\mathbb{Z}}\}
is dense in
[0,\frac{2}{\beta}]
. Clearly, we can drop the assumption on
\beta
to be irrational and instead work with a sampling set
\Lambda\times B
where
B=\{b_{n}:n\in{\mathbb{Z}}\}\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}
is chosen in such a way that
\{b_{n}\ \mathrm{mod}\ \frac{2}{\beta}:n\in{\mathbb{Z}}\}
is dense in
[0,\frac{2}{\beta}]
.

Theorem 3.10
.

Let
\beta>0
and suppose that
\Lambda\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}
and
B=\{b_{n}:n\in{\mathbb{Z}}\}\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}
are real sequences with the following properties:

1.

the sequence
\Lambda
has lower Beurling density
D^{-}(\Lambda)>\frac{2}{\beta}
,

2.

the sequence
\{b_{n}\ \mathrm{mod}\ \frac{2}{\beta}:n\in{\mathbb{Z}}\}
is dense in the interval
[0,\frac{2}{\beta}]
.

Then
(V_{\beta}^{1}(\varphi),\Lambda\times B)
satisfies condition
(U)
. In particular, both
\Lambda
and
B
can be chosen to be separated. In this case,
\Lambda\times B
constitutes a separated subset of
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
.

Proof.

Arguing in a similar fashion as in the proof of Theorem
3.9
, we have
|\mathcal{G}f(\cdot,b_{n})|^{2},|\mathcal{G}h(\cdot,b_{n})|^{2}\in V_{\frac{\beta}{2}}^{1}(\varphi)
whenever
n\in{\mathbb{Z}}
and
f,h\in V_{\beta}^{1}(\varphi)
. Since
\Lambda
has lower Beurling density
D^{-}(\Lambda)>\frac{2}{\beta}
, it follows from Theorem
2.3
that
|\mathcal{G}f(x,b_{n})|^{2}=|\mathcal{G}h(x,b_{n})|^{2}
for every
x\in{\mathbb{R}}
and every
n\in{\mathbb{Z}}
. In particular,

\frac{|\mathcal{G}f(x,b_{n})|^{2}}{\varphi(b_{n})}=\frac{|\mathcal{G}h(x,b_{n})|^{2}}{\varphi(b_{n})}

(9)

for every
x\in{\mathbb{R}}
and every
n\in{\mathbb{Z}}
. Since both the left-hand side and the right-hand side of equation (
9
) are continuous and
\frac{2}{\beta}
-periodic, the density of the set
B
in the interval
[0,\frac{2}{\beta}]
implies that
|\mathcal{G}f(x,\omega)|^{2}=|\mathcal{G}h(x,\omega)|^{2}
for every
(x,\omega)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
. Hence,
f
and
h
agree up to a global phase (see Section
1.1
).
∎

Comparing the previous generalization of Theorem
3.9
with the results in
[
1
,
10
]
implies that Theorem
3.10
is sharp with respect to the density condition on the sequence
\{b_{n}\ \mathrm{mod}\ \frac{2}{\beta}:n\in{\mathbb{Z}}\}
.

Remark 3.11

(On the sharpness of Theorem
3.10
)

Let
\beta>0
and
\{c_{k}\}\in c_{00}({\mathbb{Z}})
where
c_{00}({\mathbb{Z}})
denotes the space of all complex sequences with finitely many non-zero components. Further, let

f=\sum_{k\in{\mathbb{Z}}}c_{k}T_{\beta k}\varphi

be a linear combination of
\beta{\mathbb{Z}}
-shifts of
\varphi
. Clearly, we have
f\in V_{\beta}^{1}(\varphi)
. Define a second function
\tilde{f}\in V_{\beta}^{1}(\varphi)
by

\tilde{f}=\sum_{k\in{\mathbb{Z}}}\overline{c_{k}}T_{\beta k}\varphi,

i.e.
\tilde{f}
arises from
f
via complex conjugation of the sequence
\{c_{k}\}
. It was shown in
[
10
, Theorem 3.1]
that the above choice of
f
and
\tilde{f}
implies that

|\mathcal{G}f(z)|=|\mathcal{G}\tilde{f}(z)|\ \ \forall z\in{\mathbb{R}}\times\tfrac{1}{\beta}{\mathbb{Z}}.

Moreover, it was shown that if the sequence
\{c_{k}\}
is not contained in a line in the complex plane passing through the origin, i.e.

\nexists\alpha\in{\mathbb{R}}:\{c_{k}\}\subseteq e^{i\alpha}{\mathbb{R}},

then
f
and
\tilde{f}
do not agree up to a global phase. Hence, uniqueness in
V_{\beta}^{1}(\varphi)
is not achieved from the sampling set
\Lambda\times B\coloneqq{\mathbb{R}}\times\tfrac{1}{\beta}{\mathbb{Z}}
. Note that the set
B=\{b_{n}:n\in{\mathbb{Z}}\}=\frac{1}{\beta}{\mathbb{Z}}
with
b_{n}=\frac{1}{\beta}n
, has the property that

\{b_{n}\ \mathrm{mod}\ \tfrac{2}{\beta}:n\in{\mathbb{Z}}\}=\{\tfrac{1}{\beta}n\ \mathrm{mod}\ \tfrac{2}{\beta}:n\in{\mathbb{Z}}\}

is not dense in the interval
[0,\frac{2}{\beta}]
since it is a set of isolated points. Therefore, condition (2) of Theorem
3.10
is violated. In particular, the previous construction shows that, in general, the uniqueness property does not hold anymore if one drops condition (2) of Theorem
3.10
. Observe further, that every rectangular lattice
a{\mathbb{Z}}\times\frac{1}{\beta}{\mathbb{Z}},a,\beta>0,
is contained in the set
{\mathbb{R}}\times\frac{1}{\beta}{\mathbb{Z}}
. This shows additionally that the pair

(V_{\beta}^{1}(\varphi),\tfrac{\beta}{2+\varepsilon}{\mathbb{Z}}\times\tfrac{1}{\beta}{\mathbb{Z}})

does not satisfy condition
(U)
for every
\varepsilon>0
and every
\beta>0
. In contrast, Theorem
3.9
states that the pair

(V_{\beta}^{1}(\varphi),\tfrac{\beta}{2+\varepsilon}{\mathbb{Z}}\times{\mathbb{Z}})

satisfies condition
(U)
for every
\varepsilon>0
and every
\beta\in{\mathbb{R}}_{>0}\setminus{\mathbb{Q}}_{>0}
. Hence, uniqueness is achieved by making the sampling rate in frequency direction independent of the step-size
\beta
of the shift-invariant space
V_{\beta}^{1}(\varphi)
.

Similar to the case of compactly-supported functions, a rotation of the time-frequency plane yields uniqueness results for functions which have the property of being the fractional Fourier transform of a function in
V_{\beta}^{1}(\varphi)
. More precisely we have

Proposition 3.12
.

Let
\theta\in{\mathbb{R}}
,
\beta\in{\mathbb{R}}_{>0}\setminus{\mathbb{Q}}_{>0}
and suppose that
\Lambda\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}
has lower Beurling density
D^{-}(\Lambda)>\frac{2}{\beta}
. Then every
f\in{\mathcal{F}}_{-\theta}V_{\beta}^{1}(\varphi)
is determined up to a global phase by the measurements

\{|\mathcal{G}f(z)|:z\in R_{\theta}(\Lambda\times{\mathbb{Z}})\},

where
R_{\theta}
denotes the rotation matrix as defined in equation
3
.

Proof.

Let
f,h\in{\mathcal{F}}_{-\theta}V_{\beta}^{1}(\varphi)
, i.e. there exist functions
u,v\in V_{\beta}^{1}(\varphi)
such that
f={\mathcal{F}}_{-\theta}u
and
h={\mathcal{F}}_{-\theta}v
. Suppose that
z=R_{\theta}z^{\prime}
with
z^{\prime}\in\Lambda\times{\mathbb{Z}}
. Then by Lemma
2.7
we observe that the equality
|\mathcal{G}f(z)|=|\mathcal{G}h(z)|
is equivalent to
|\mathcal{G}u(z^{\prime})|=|\mathcal{G}v(z^{\prime})|
. Since
z^{\prime}\in\Lambda\times{\mathbb{Z}}
was arbitrary, it follows that the spectrograms of
u
and
v
agree on
\Lambda\times{\mathbb{Z}}
. Therefore, Theorem
3.9
implies the existence of a
\tau\in{\mathbb{T}}
such that
u=\tau v
. In particular,
{\mathcal{F}}_{-\theta}u=\tau{\mathcal{F}}_{-\theta}v
which yields
f=\tau h
.
∎

If
f
is the product of a periodic function and the Gaussian
\varphi
then a rotation by 90 degrees combined with Proposition
3.12
implies the following result.

Corollary 3.13
.

Assume that
f\in{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}})}
factors as
f=\varphi u
where
u\in C^{1}({\mathbb{R}})
is
\beta
-periodic. Let
\Lambda
be a sequence in
{\mathbb{R}}
with lower Beurling density
D^{-}(\Lambda)>2\beta
. If
\beta\in{\mathbb{R}}_{>0}\setminus{\mathbb{Q}}_{>0}
then
f
is determined up to a global phase by the measurements

\{|\mathcal{G}f(x,\omega)|:(x,\omega)\in{\mathbb{Z}}\times\Lambda\}.

Proof.

By assumptions, we can write
u
as a Fourier series

u(t)=\sum_{k\in{\mathbb{Z}}}c_{k}e^{2\pi i\frac{1}{\beta}k}
,
where the sequence
(c_{k})_{k}
is in
\ell^{1}({\mathbb{Z}})
. Consequently, we have

{\mathcal{F}}(\varphi u)=\sum_{k\in{\mathbb{Z}}}c_{k}T_{\frac{1}{\beta}k}\varphi\in V_{\frac{1}{\beta}}^{1}(\varphi).

The statement follows from Theorem
3.9
, since
|\mathcal{G}f(x,\omega)|=|\mathcal{G}\hat{f}(\omega,-x)|
.
∎

3.4
Real-valued maps in Gaussian shift-invariant spaces

In Section
3.2
we have outlined how uniqueness for compactly-supported maps from samples on a 1-dimensional lattice follows under a real-valuedness assumption. This result was a consequence of the fact that a real-valued band-limited map is determined from samples of its absolute value. An extension of the 1-dimensional lattice to a 2-dimensional lattice yielded injectivity for complex-valued maps. In the present section, we show that an analogous statement is valid in the Gaussian shift-invariant setting. The starting point is the following theorem of Gröchenig
[
8
, Theorem 1]
which has the flavor of Thakur’s theorem. For
\gamma>0
we denote by
\phi_{\gamma}
the Gaussian
\phi_{\gamma}(t)=e^{-\gamma t^{2}}
.

Theorem 3.14

(Gröchenig)
.

Assume that
\Lambda\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}
is separated and that
D^{-}(\Lambda)>\frac{2}{\beta}
. If
\gamma,\beta>0
and
f,h\in V_{\beta}^{\infty}(\phi_{\gamma})
are such that

|f(\lambda)|=|h(\lambda)|\ \ \forall\lambda\in\Lambda

then there exists a constant
\tau\in\{1,-1\}
such that
f=\tau h
.

We notice that Romero generalized Theorem
3.14
to the setting where the Gaussian generator is a totally positive function of Gaussian type
[
27
]
. Similar to Section
3.2
we can deduce that uniqueness from Gabor measurements under a real-valuedness assumption is achieved from samples on a 1-dimensional lattice. To do so we denote by
V_{\beta}^{p}(\varphi,{\mathbb{R}})
the class of real-valued maps in
V_{\beta}^{p}(\varphi)
, where
\varphi(t)=e^{-\pi t^{2}}
denotes the standard Gaussian.

Corollary 3.15
.

Let
\varepsilon,\beta>0
and let
\mathcal{C}=V_{\beta}^{\infty}(\varphi,{\mathbb{R}})
. Suppose that
\Lambda\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}
is separated with lower Beurling density
D^{-}(\Lambda)>\tfrac{2}{\beta}
. If

X=\Lambda\times\{0\}

then
(\mathcal{C},X)
satisfies condition
(U)
.

Proof.

Let
n\in{\mathbb{Z}}
. A calculation shows that the convolution of
\varphi
with
T_{\beta n}\varphi
is given by

\varphi*T_{\beta n}\varphi=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}T_{\beta n}\phi_{\frac{\pi}{2}}.

Hence, if
f=\sum_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}c_{n}T_{\beta n}\varphi
with a real sequence
\{c_{n}\}\in\ell^{\infty}({\mathbb{Z}})
then

\mathcal{G}f(x,0)=\varphi*f=\sum_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}c_{n}(\varphi*T_{\beta n}\varphi)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\sum_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}c_{n}T_{\beta n}\phi_{\frac{\pi}{2}}\in V_{\beta}^{\infty}(\phi_{\frac{\pi}{2}},{\mathbb{R}}).

(10)

Therefore, the identity

|\mathcal{G}f(z)|=|\mathcal{G}h(z)|\ \ \forall z\in X

holds if and only if

|\varphi*f(x)|=|\varphi*h(x)|\ \ \forall x\in\Lambda.

By equation (
10
), both convolutions satisfy
\varphi*f,\varphi*h\in V_{\beta}^{\infty}(\phi_{\frac{\pi}{2}},{\mathbb{R}})
. Since
D^{-}(\frac{2}{\beta+\varepsilon}{\mathbb{Z}})>\frac{2}{\beta}
it follows from Gröchenig’s theorem that
\varphi*f=\tau(\varphi*h)
for some
\tau\in\{1,-1\}
. Since the Fourier transform of
\varphi
does not vanish we have
f=\tau h
, concluding the proof of the statement.
∎

Comparing the real-valued case with the complex-valued case implies the following statement.

Theorem 3.16
.

Let
\varepsilon,\beta>0
. If the signal class
\mathcal{C}_{\mathbb{R}}
and the sampling set
X_{\mathbb{R}}
is chosen as

\mathcal{C}_{\mathbb{R}}=V_{\beta}^{1}(\varphi,{\mathbb{R}}),\ X_{\mathbb{R}}=\frac{2}{\beta+\varepsilon}{\mathbb{Z}}\times\{0\}

then
(\mathcal{C}_{\mathbb{R}},X_{\mathbb{R}})
satisfies condition
(U)
. Further, if
\beta
is irrational then the choice

\mathcal{C}_{\mathbb{C}}=V_{\beta}^{1}(\varphi),\ X_{\mathbb{C}}=\frac{2}{\beta+\varepsilon}{\mathbb{Z}}\times{\mathbb{Z}}

implies that
(\mathcal{C}_{\mathbb{C}},X_{\mathbb{C}})
satisfies condition
(U)
.

Proof.

The set
\Lambda=\frac{2}{\beta+\varepsilon}{\mathbb{Z}}
has lower Beurling density
D^{-}(\Lambda)>\tfrac{2}{\beta}
Hence, the first part of the statement follows from Corollary
3.15
and the inclusion
V_{\beta}^{1}(\varphi,{\mathbb{R}})\subseteq V_{\beta}^{\infty}(\varphi,{\mathbb{R}})
. The second part was shown in Theorem
3.9
.
∎

4
Concluding remarks

We conclude the article with several remarks related to our results from Section
3
and compare them with the previous work mentioned in Section
1.1
.

1.

Let
s\in\{-1,1\}
and
a\in{\mathbb{Z}}
. In both Theorem
3.1
and Theorem
3.9
the lattice
{\mathbb{Z}}\times(2c)^{-1}{\mathbb{Z}}
and
\frac{\beta}{2+\varepsilon}{\mathbb{Z}}\times{\mathbb{Z}}
can be replaced by
(s{\mathbb{N}}+a)\times(2c)^{-1}{\mathbb{Z}}
and
\frac{\beta}{2+\varepsilon}{\mathbb{Z}}\times(s{\mathbb{N}}+a)
, respectively. Consequently, compactly-supported functions and functions in shift-invariant spaces with Gaussian generator are determined by spectrogram samples lying in a half-plane.

2.

In the proof of Theorem
3.9
we started by sampling the spectrogram
|\mathcal{G}f(x,\omega)|
with respect to the time variable
x
. The desired result then followed via an application of Weyl’s equidistribution theorem. On the other hand, after applying the sampling Theorem
2.3
we could use the identity
\mathcal{G}f(x,\omega)=e^{-2\pi ix\omega}\mathcal{G}\hat{f}(\omega,-x)
and then apply the Fourier transform with respect to the second argument as we did in Theorem
3.1
, the case of compactly-supported signals. This approach would yield the identity

{\mathcal{F}}|\mathcal{G}\hat{f}(\omega,\cdot)|^{2}(x)=\langle(\hat{f})_{x},T_{\omega}\varphi_{x}\rangle.

Since
f
is an element of
V_{\beta}^{1}(\varphi)
, the function
(\hat{f})_{x}
is certainly not compactly-supported and Zalik’s theorem, Theorem
2.6
, is not applicable. At this point, one could use the following generalization of Zalik’s theorem
[
32
, Theorem 2]
.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
\psi(t)=ae^{-b^{2}(t-c)^{2}}
be a Gaussian where
a,b>0
and
c\in{\mathbb{R}}
. Let
\Lambda\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}
be a sequence of distinct real numbers and denote by
S(\varepsilon)
the series

\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda}{}^{{}^{\prime}}\frac{1}{|\lambda|^{2+\varepsilon}}.

The divergence of
S(\varepsilon)
for some
\varepsilon>0
suffices for the sequence
\{T_{\lambda}\psi:\lambda\in\Lambda\}
to be complete in
{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}})}
, whereas the divergence of
S(0)
constitutes a necessary condition for completeness of
\{T_{\lambda}\psi:\lambda\in\Lambda\}
in
{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}})}
.

However, an application of this result is not meaningful for our purposes. For if
\Omega
is separated then
S(\varepsilon)
certainly converges for every
\varepsilon>0
. Therefore, this approach would not yield sampling sets consisting of separated points.

3.

At the beginning of the present paper it was shown that if
e^{i\alpha_{1}}{\mathbb{R}},e^{i\alpha_{2}}{\mathbb{R}}\subseteq{\mathbb{C}}
are two lines which satisfy the condition
\alpha_{1}-\alpha_{2}\notin\pi{\mathbb{Q}}
then the pair
({L^{2}({\mathbb{R}})},e^{i\alpha_{1}}{\mathbb{R}}\cup e^{i\alpha_{2}}{\mathbb{R}})
satisfies condition
(U)
. This was a consequence of Jaming’s theorem
[
17
]
. A crucial step in the proof of Jaming’s theorem lies in the observation that if
f
is a non-zero entire function then for arbitrary
z\in{\mathbb{C}}\setminus\{0\}
the orbit
D=\{ze^{2i(\alpha_{1}-\alpha_{2})n}:n\in{\mathbb{Z}}\}
cannot be contained in the zero set of
f
. For if
\alpha_{1}-\alpha_{2}\notin\pi{\mathbb{Q}}
then by Weyl’s equidistribution theorem the set
D
contains a limit point. In Theorem
3.9
, Weyl’s equidistribution theorem was used to obtain uniqueness results from fully discrete measurements.
