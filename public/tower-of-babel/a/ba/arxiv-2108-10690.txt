On a Low-Frequency and Contrast Stabilized Full-Wave Volume Integral Equation Solver for Lossy Media
Clément Henry, IEEE, Adrien Merlini, Lyes Rahmouni

Abstract
In this paper we present a new regularized electric flux volume integral equation (D-VIE) for modeling high-contrast conductive dielectric objects in a broad frequency range. This new formulation is particularly suitable for modeling biological tissues at low frequencies, as it is required by brain epileptogenic area imaging, but also at higher ones, as it is required by several applications including, but not limited to, transcranial magnetic and deep brain stimulation (TMS and DBS, respectively). When modeling inhomogeneous objects with high complex permittivities at low frequencies, the traditional D-VIE is ill-conditioned and suffers from numerical instabilities that result in slower convergence and in less accurate solutions. In this work we address these shortcomings by leveraging a new set of volume quasi-Helmholtz projectors. Their scaling by the material permittivity matrix allows for the re-balancing of the equation when applied to inhomogeneous scatterers and thereby makes the proposed method accurate and stable even for high complex permittivity objects until arbitrarily low frequencies. Numerical results, canonical and realistic, corroborate the theory and confirm the stability and the accuracy of this new method both in the quasi-static regime and at higher frequencies.



I
Introduction

The electromagnetic modeling of human tissues has numerous applications that include brain source localization
[
1
]
, dosimetry
[
2
]
, deep brain stimulation (DBS)
[
3
]
, transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS)
[
4
]
, electric impedance tomography
[
5
]
, and hyperthermic cancer therapy
[
6
]
. All these procedures require an accurate modeling of the interactions between electromagnetic fields and the human body. Depending on the application, numerical solvers can be either full-wave solvers directly derived from Maxwell’s equations or static solvers based on Poisson’s equation, which are only valid in the quasi-static regime
[
7
]
. While static solvers are sufficient to model resistive effects occurring in biological tissues, full-wave solvers are required when capacitive, inductive, and propagation effects should also be taken into consideration
[
7
,
8
]
. For instance, full-wave models are needed when the source has a larger spectral content (e.g. the magnetic pulse emitted by TMS coils
[
9
]
or the electrical current injected by an electrode in neurostimulation
[
10
]
). For these applications, the modeling should be done at various frequencies and, hence, the solver used should be able to perform accurately at arbitrary frequency.

Two main families of numerical solvers are widely employed for frequency domain bio-electromagnetic modeling: integral equation (IE) and differential equation solvers. In particular, integral equation solvers can be either surfacic (SIE) or volumic (VIE) depending on the nature of the tissue to model
[
11
]
. Although these solvers give rise to dense matrices, the higher computational cost incurred can be significantly reduced using acceleration techniques such as the fast multipole method (FMM)
[
12
]
or the adaptive cross approximation (ACA)
[
13
]
. Moreover, since IE formulations automatically enforce radiation conditions, no discretization is required outside the object. Unfortunately, however, bio-electromagnetic modeling is a challenging task for solvers based on integral equations because of the high complex permittivity contrast between the different tissues and their background
[
14
]
. Indeed, both a high-contrast object and/or a low operating frequency introduce a severe ill-conditioning in the discretized IE operator, which yields a slower convergence and a loss of accuracy in the solution
[
15
]
. These two phenomena are often referred to as the low-frequency (LF) and the high-contrast (HC) breakdowns respectively
[
16
,
17
,
18
,
19
]
.
In addition to the conditioning issue, the ill-scaling between the different components of the solution of the discretized system causes the latter to have fewer digits of accuracy due to finite machine precision
[
20
]
.

The high-contrast breakdown in piecewise homogeneous scatterers has been cured in the case of the Poggio-Miller-Chang-Harrington-Wu-Tsai (PMCHWT) formulation
[
21
]
and in a novel single-source integral equation
[
18
]
by leveraging the Calderón identities while the low-frequency breakdown of the PMCHWT was tackled by its preconditioning with quasi-Helmholtz projectors
[
22
]
. While these stabilized surface formulations have numerous advantages, they are limited to piecewise homogeneous models of biological tissues. Volume integral equations, instead, can model objects with a high degree of inhomogeneity. Unfortunately, as their surface counterparts, they suffer from the HC breakdown
[
23
,
24
,
25
,
19
,
26
,
27
,
28
]
and fail to converge rapidly in applications with high-permittivity contrast scatterers. Another limitation of traditional VIE is that, even though they are immune from the low-frequency breakdown in purely dielectric objects
[
29
]
, a frequency ill-scaling between the different parts of the VIE can occur when the object under study has a complex permittivity which depends on the frequency
[
30
]
. Therefore, in these cases, the LF breakdown can be considered an intrinsic part of the HC breakdown in the VIE. These limitations prevent the standard volume formulations to perform well in realistic biomedical applications where the modeling of high-contrast conductive tissues from the quasi-static regime to the microwave regime is required.

Regularization techniques have been introduced for solving the LF and the HC problems in the electric current VIE (J-VIE), the electric field VIE (E-VIE), and the electric flux VIE (D-VIE). The LF breakdown has been cured in the J-VIE for anisotropic and inhomogeneous scatterers using a Loop-Star-Facet decomposition for re-scaling properly the unknown
[
31
]
. However, solving this problem using a Helmholtz discretization deteriorates the dense discretization behavior of the VIE, i.e., it causes the conditioning of the system matrix to deteriorate when the average edge length
h
of the discretized geometry decreases
[
32
]
. Instead, the quasi-Helmholtz projectors
[
15
]
allow for the removal of the ill-scaling in the formulation while keeping its dense mesh behavior unchanged. These projectors have been adapted to the J-VIE and used for curing the HC limitations of this equation for isotropic inhomogeneous scatterers
[
33
]
. Another approach to solve the HC problem is presented in
[
25
]
, where the E-VIE is regularized using symbol calculus and the Calderón identities. Its application to the J-VIE is discussed in
[
34
]
. While they are free from the HC breakdown, the two above-mentioned methods do not consider the numerical stability of the J-VIE or the E-VIE at low frequencies when modeling lossy dielectric objects, which is an important feature for a solver operating in low-frequency biomedical applications.
Regarding the D-VIE, an effective solution to both the LF and HC breakdowns has been proposed in
[
35
,
36
]
where an additional surface integral equation is used to adjust the background permittivity and lower the dielectric contrast. This technique is quite effective when the main problem is the contrast between the background and the object but it does not remove directly the internal contrasts between the different media within the simulated object, and an ill-conditioning thus remains for inhomogeneous objects.

The contribution of this paper is twofold, on the one hand we propose a new set of quasi-Helmholtz projectors that, differently from those proposed in the past, are the first of their kind to be oblique and to be particularly suited to manipulate the solenoidal and non-solenoidal parts the D-VIE. On the other hand, we leverage these new projectors to obtain a new regularized D-VIE which is immune from both ill-conditioning and the loss of accuracy occurring at low frequencies and in high-contrast objects.
More specifically, the new projectors, built from a weighted graph Laplacian matrix, when combined with the appropriate re-scaling, allow for the re-balancing the D-VIE in both the LF and HC regimes. The regularized D-VIE obtained is free from both the HC and the LF breakdowns and exhibits a solution that is valid until arbitrarily low frequencies, unlike standard full-wave solvers. This versatility makes it an appropriate formulation for biomedical applications where solvers that can operate in a broad frequency range and in high-contrast objects are required. The reader should however note that, although in our numerical validations we focus on biomedical applications and brain modeling in particular, the solver we propose is a completely general purpose one and several other application scenarios such as the modeling of lossy interconnects in printed circuit boards
[
30
]
could benefit from its use. Very preliminary results of this work have been presented in the conference contribution
[
37
]
.

Low-frequency and High-contrast Analyses of the D-VIE

In this section we will first present an analysis of the low-frequency problems of the D-VIE. This will then be followed by an analysis of the high-contrast problems of the D-VIE. Leveraging these analyses and the nature of the criticalities observed, a cure for both problems will then be presented in

.

III-A

Low-frequency Analysis of the D-VIE

We analyze here the low-frequency behavior of the D-VIE when modeling lossy dielectric objects surrounded by free space. At low frequencies VIEs are subject to the low-frequency breakdown due to the frequency dependence of the complex permittivity of the object
[
30
]
. In this subsection we will confirm this with an analysis coherent with our framework that will then be used in the next section to construct the new formulation we propose in this work.
Here we will also show that for the discretized D-VIE, the equation ill-scaling not only causes the ill-conditioning of the associated linear system matrix, but also the loss of significant digits in the solution coefficients.
Our strategy will be to expose the low-frequency behavior of the D-VIE through a suitably normalized Loop-Star decomposition.

We propose here a new generalization of the Loop-Star decomposition, that differently from the standard decomposition, is not coefficient orthogonal, but rather an oblique decomposition. In its normalized form, it is defined as follows

\boldsymbol{\mathsf{B^{A^{-1}}_{\Lambda\Sigma}}}=\left[\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\Lambda}}({\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\Lambda}}}^{\mathrm{T}}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\Lambda}})^{-\frac{1}{2}},\boldsymbol{\mathsf{A}}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\tilde{\Sigma}}}({\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\tilde{\Sigma}}}}^{\mathrm{T}}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{A}}^{2}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\tilde{\Sigma}}})^{-\frac{1}{2}}\right]\,,

(13)

where
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{A}}
is an invertible real symmetric matrix and for any invertible matrix
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{U}}
,
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{U}}^{-\frac{1}{2}}
is the inverse of one of the (potentially many) square root matrices of
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{U}}
. The fact that
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{B^{A^{-1}}_{\Lambda\Sigma}}}
is invertible is proven in Appendix
B

In this work, however, the decomposition will only be used as a theoretical tool in the conditioning analysis and, subsequently, as the starting point for defining our new oblique projectors. In other words, the computation of (
13

III-B

High-Contrast Analysis of the D-VIE

In the following we analyze the high-contrast behavior of the D-VIE for complex permittivity objects with dominant imaginary part (
\sigma/(\omega\epsilon_{0}\epsilon_{r}^{\prime})\gg 1
) at low frequencies. In this regime, the dielectric contrast
\kappa
between the object and the background is approximately
1
and the Green’s function
G_{0}
can be bounded from above by a frequency-independent term.

In our theoretical treatment, the scatterer
\Omega
is supposed to be a piecewise homogeneous object composed of a region
\Omega_{M}
which has the maximum conductivity
\sigma_{\text{max}}
of
\Omega
(associated to the complex permittivity
\epsilon_{\text{max}}
) while the conductivity is bounded in its complementary domain
\Omega\backslash\Omega_{M}
. In the following, the boundary between
\Omega_{M}
and the rest of the object is denoted by
\partial\Omega_{M}
and the maximum conductivity ratio is defined as
r_{\sigma}=\sigma_{\text{max}}/\sigma_{\text{min}}
with
\sigma_{\text{min}}
being the minimum conductivity in
\Omega
. The following high-contrast analysis will be performed for
r_{\sigma}
going toward infinity with
\sigma_{\text{min}}
fixed. Note that this scenario represents the high internal contrast occurring in lossy dielectric objects in several application scenarios.

Similarly to the low-frequency breakdown, the HC problem originates from the solenoidal part of
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{Z}}
that is ill-scaled due to the permittivity scaling of the Gram matrix
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{G_{\epsilon}}}
. While the approach carried out in the low-frequency analysis only required the determination of the frequency scaling of
\|\boldsymbol{\mathsf{G_{\epsilon}}}\|
, some additional considerations on the material dependence of the minimum singular value of the Gram matrix are needed in the HC analysis. To this aim, we leverage the block structure of
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{G_{\epsilon}}}
for the above-mentioned scatterer. The entries of
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{G_{\epsilon}}}
can be written as

\left[\boldsymbol{\mathsf{G_{\epsilon}}}\right]_{mn}=\int_{T_{m}^{+}}\boldsymbol{f_{m}}\cdot\epsilon^{-1}\boldsymbol{f_{n}}\,\mathrm{d}v+\int_{T_{m}^{-}}\boldsymbol{f_{m}}\cdot\epsilon^{-1}\boldsymbol{f_{n}}\,\mathrm{d}v\,,

with
T^{\pm}_{m}
being the tetrahedra on which
\boldsymbol{f_{m}}
is defined. In the case of a half basis function, only
T^{+}_{m}
is required.
From this definition, we can build the following block matrix

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathsf{G_{\epsilon}}}=\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{G_{M}}}&{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{B_{M}}}}^{\mathrm{T}}\\
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{B_{M}}}&\boldsymbol{\mathsf{G_{R}}}\end{bmatrix}\,,\text{ in which}

(44)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathsf{G_{M}}}=\boldsymbol{\mathsf{G_{\epsilon}}}[a_{1},\dots,a_{N_{FM}};a_{1},\dots,a_{N_{FM}}]\,,

(45)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathsf{B_{M}}}=\boldsymbol{\mathsf{G_{\epsilon}}}[a_{N_{FM}+1},\dots,a_{N_{F}};a_{1},\dots,a_{N_{FM}}]\,,

(46)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathsf{G_{R}}}=\boldsymbol{\mathsf{G_{\epsilon}}}[a_{N_{FM}+1},\dots,a_{N_{F}};a_{N_{FM}+1},\dots,a_{N_{F}}]\,,

(47)

with
\{a_{1},\dots,a_{N_{FM}}\}
being the indices of the rows (and columns) of
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{G_{\epsilon}}}
corresponding to the
N_{FM}
SWG basis functions that have both of their supporting tetrahedra in
\Omega_{M}
and
\{a_{N_{FM}+1},\dots,a_{N_{F}}\}
being the
{N_{F}-N_{FM}}
indices of the remaining SWG basis functions.

Using the block structure given in (
44
) and the fact
\Re(1/\epsilon_{\text{max}})=\mathcal{O}(r_{\sigma}^{-2})
,
\Im(1/\epsilon_{\text{max}})=\mathcal{O}(r_{\sigma}^{-1})
,
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{G_{R}}}=\mathcal{O}(1/\epsilon)
(bounded complex permittivity),
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{B_{M}}}=\mathcal{O}(1/\epsilon_{\text{max}})
, and
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{G_{M}}}=\mathcal{O}(1/\epsilon_{\text{max}})
when
r_{\sigma}\rightarrow\infty
, we obtain the following scalings for the blocks of
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{G_{\epsilon}}}
when the contrast goes to infinity

\displaystyle\Re\left(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{G_{\epsilon}}}\right)\mathrel{\mathop{=}\limits_{r_{\sigma}\to\infty}}\begin{bmatrix}\mathcal{O}(1/r_{\sigma}^{2})&\mathcal{O}(1/r_{\sigma}^{2})\\
\mathcal{O}(1/r_{\sigma}^{2})&\mathcal{O}(1)\end{bmatrix}\,,

(48)

\displaystyle\Im\left(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{G_{\epsilon}}}\right)\mathrel{\mathop{=}\limits_{r_{\sigma}\to\infty}}\begin{bmatrix}\mathcal{O}(1/r_{\sigma})&\mathcal{O}(1/r_{\sigma})\\
\mathcal{O}(1/r_{\sigma})&\mathcal{O}(1)\end{bmatrix},

(49)

which, according to the Gershgorin circle theorem, show that
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{G_{\epsilon}}}
suffers from ill-conditioning in objects with high internal contrast. This HC problem in the Gram matrix can be a source of ill-conditioning in the discretized D-VIE, and hence should be considered when regularizing the D-VIE for high-contrast.

Just like the orthogonal quasi-Helmholtz projectors

15

So starting from the decomposition described in (
13

\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\Sigma}}
instead of
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\tilde{\Sigma}}}

(50)

in which as before
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{A}}
is an invertible symmetric matrix.
Note that (
50
) is a valid Helmholtz decomposition, as demonstrated in Appendix
A
.
We can now obtain a new set of projectors by solving for
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\Lambda}}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\tilde{l}}}
and for
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{A}}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\Sigma}}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\tilde{s}}}
separately. To this end, we first multiply (
50
) by
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\Lambda}}^{\mathrm{T}}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{A}}^{-1}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\Sigma}}^{\mathrm{T}}
, and we obtain the following equations

\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\Lambda}}^{\mathrm{T}}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{A}}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\alpha}}=\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\Lambda}}^{\mathrm{T}}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{A}}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\Lambda}}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\tilde{l}}};~\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\Sigma}}^{\mathrm{T}}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\alpha}}=\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\Sigma}}^{\mathrm{T}}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{A}}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\Sigma}}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\tilde{s}}}\,,

(51)

in which we used the properties that
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\Lambda}}^{\mathrm{T}}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{A}}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{A}}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\Sigma}}=\boldsymbol{\mathsf{0}}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\Sigma}}^{\mathrm{T}}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\Lambda}}=\boldsymbol{\mathsf{0}}

51

(52)

where

\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\Sigma}}^{\mathrm{T}}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{A}}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\Sigma}}

The next step is to obtain the solenoidal and non-solenoidal parts of
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\alpha}}
in terms of
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\alpha}}
itself by applying
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\Lambda}}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{A}}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\Sigma}}
to the equations in (

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\Lambda}}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\tilde{l}}}=\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\Lambda}}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\Lambda}}^{\mathrm{T}}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{A}}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\Lambda}})^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\Lambda}}^{\mathrm{T}}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{A}}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\alpha}}\,,

(53)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathsf{A}}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\Sigma}}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\tilde{s}}}=\boldsymbol{\mathsf{A}}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\Sigma}}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\Sigma}}^{\mathrm{T}}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{A}}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\Sigma}})^{+}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\Sigma}}^{\mathrm{T}}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\alpha}}\,.

(54)

What we have obtained is a family of two complementary oblique projectors

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathsf{P^{\Lambda}_{A^{-1}}}}=\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\Lambda}}\left(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\Lambda}}^{\mathrm{{\mathrm{T}}}}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{A}}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\Lambda}}\right)^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\Lambda}}^{\mathrm{{\mathrm{T}}}}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{A}}^{-1}\,,

(55)

V
The New Regularized Volume Integral Equation

To find the correct parameters of a regularizer for the D-VIE based on the new oblique quasi-Helmholtz projectors just introduced, we will employ an approach often and successfully used with projectors at low-frequency: first a regularization is obtained by determining the parameters of a, impractical-to-implement but theoretically useful, normalized Loop-Star decomposition; then the same coefficients are used for linearly combining solenoidal and non-solenoidal projectors. In the present context, however, we not only have to regularize the low-frequency breakdown, but also the high-contrast breakdown. Thus, after finding the frequency-regularizing coefficients with a normalized Loop-Star analysis we will propose a formulation that also solves the high-contrast breakdown by using an ansatz whose effectiveness will then be proved in the following section.

Consider the following normalized and re-scaled Loop-Star decomposition of the D-VIE

) holds, we thus obtain lower bounds for the minimum singular values of
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{M_{\Lambda\Lambda}}}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{M_{\Sigma\Sigma}}}
((

) and (

)) which translates into the non-singularity of
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{M_{D}}}
in (
83
). In that case, identity (
86
) can be applied to (
83
) to find a lower bound for the minimum singular value of
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{M_{B}}}
. Before applying (
86
), we also need to enforce that the norms of
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{M_{\Lambda\Sigma}}}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{M_{\Sigma\Lambda}}}
and the minimum singular values of
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{M_{\Lambda\Lambda}}}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{M_{\Sigma\Sigma}}}
respect inequality (
85
). Using a similar approach as before, the off-diagonal blocks
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{M_{\Lambda\Sigma}}}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{M_{\Sigma\Lambda}}}
can be bounded as

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{\mathsf{M_{\Lambda\Sigma}}}\|\leq r_{\sigma}\omega\tau_{\Lambda\Sigma A}\,,

(101)

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{\mathsf{M_{\Sigma\Lambda}}}\|\leq\omega\tau_{\Sigma G}+\omega^{2}\tau_{\Sigma\Lambda A}+\omega\tau_{\Sigma\Lambda\Phi}\,,\text{ in which}

(102)

\displaystyle\tau_{\Lambda\Sigma A}=\gamma_{\Lambda}\sigma_{\text{min}}/c_{0}^{2}\|\boldsymbol{\mathsf{G}}^{-1}\|\|\boldsymbol{\mathsf{Z^{1}_{A}}}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{Q_{\Sigma R}}}\|\,,

(103)

\displaystyle\tau_{\Sigma\Lambda A}=\gamma_{\Sigma}/c_{0}^{2}\|{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{Q_{\Sigma L}}}}^{\mathrm{T}}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{G}}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{Z^{1}_{A}}}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{Q_{\Lambda R}}}\|\,,

(104)

\displaystyle\tau_{\Sigma\Lambda\Phi}=\gamma_{\Sigma}\sigma_{\text{min}}^{-1}\|{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{Q_{\Sigma L}}}}^{\mathrm{T}}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{G}}^{-1}\|\|\boldsymbol{\mathsf{Z^{1}_{\Phi,1\epsilon}}}\|\,,

(105)

such that
\|\boldsymbol{\mathsf{Z_{\Phi,1\epsilon}}}\|\leq\omega/\sigma_{\text{min}}\|\boldsymbol{\mathsf{Z^{1}_{\Phi,1\epsilon}}}\|
in which
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{Z^{1}_{\Phi,1\epsilon}}}
is a permittivity-independent matrix that follows the relation
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{Z_{\Phi,1\epsilon}}}=\boldsymbol{\mathsf{Z^{1}_{\Phi,1\epsilon}}}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\Upsilon_{\mathcal{\delta\kappa}}}}
with
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\Upsilon_{\mathcal{\delta\kappa}}}}
being a diagonal matrix filled as
[\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\Upsilon_{\mathcal{\delta\kappa}}}}]_{nn}=\epsilon_{0}^{-1}\mathcal{\delta\kappa}_{n}
. This last inequality can be obtained by observing that
\|\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\Upsilon_{\mathcal{\delta\kappa}}}}\|\leq\omega/\sigma_{\text{min}}
.

Finally, we can enforce the conditions (

) and (

) using the bounds derived in (

), (

), (

), (

), (

), and (

). These conditions being enforced, the resulting condition number of

can be bounded from above as

\begin{split}\>\text{cond}\left(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{M_{B}}}\right)&\leq\>\text{cond}\left(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{M_{D}}}\left(\bf{I}+\boldsymbol{\mathsf{M_{D}}}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{M_{O}}}\right)\right)\\
&\leq\>\text{cond}\left(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{M_{D}}}\right)\frac{1+\|\boldsymbol{\mathsf{M_{D}}}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{M_{O}}}\|}{1-\|\boldsymbol{\mathsf{M_{D}}}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{M_{O}}}\|}\\

(106)

in which the condition (

) was employed. An upper bound for the condition number of
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{M}}
can then be obtained from (

) using (

).

The variables in these bounds are the frequency and the maximum conductivity ratio in the object

which are dictated by the electromagnetic problem to solve, and the parameter

) and (

) are met regardless of the maximum conductivity ratio

and of the parameter

(

) and the upper bound for the condition number of

and

and

is bounded when

) that in the static limit the condition number of
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{M}}
is bounded as the maximum conductivity ratio increases. Away from that limit,
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{M}}
will still be properly conditioned as long as the bounds above-mentioned are enforced. To show the proper behavior of this formulation beyond the static limit, some values of

and the frequency for which the bounds are respected are given in the numerical results section. VII
Implementation Related Details

This section presents the details related to the implementation of the proposed preconditioner. Starting from an existing D-VIE, the quasi-Helmholtz projectors must be computed efficiently not to deteriorate the overall complexity of the solver. The main difficulty to build the projectors resides in the inversion of the stiffness matrix
{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\Sigma}}}^{\mathrm{T}}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{G_{\epsilon}}}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\Sigma}}
, which has a condition number that grows when the discretization of the geometry is refined. To remedy this issue, this operation can be done leveraging the algebraic multigrid (AMG) method as preconditioner
[
45
]
together with an iterative solver. Besides, since the Gram matrix
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{G_{\epsilon}}}
is sparse and symmetric, its direct inverse can be obtained in an efficient manner using a multifrontal solver
[
46
]
.

In addition to this, however, we have found also a very effective strategy that does not require the inversion of
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{G_{\epsilon}}}
, resulting in less computational and implementation efforts. In fact, we observed numerically that using the real part or the imaginary part of the diagonal of the Gram matrix in the stiffness matrix also makes the formulation stable. The set of projectors in this case is defined as follows

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathsf{P^{\Sigma}_{D}}}=\boldsymbol{\mathsf{D}}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\Sigma}}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\Sigma}}^{\mathrm{T}}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{D}}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\Sigma}})^{+}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\Sigma}}^{\mathrm{T}}\,,

(107)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathsf{P^{\Lambda}_{D}}}=\bf{I}-\boldsymbol{\mathsf{P^{\Sigma}_{D}}}=\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\Lambda}}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\Lambda}}^{\mathrm{T}}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{D}}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\Lambda}})^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\Lambda}}^{\mathrm{T}}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{D}}\,,

(108)

where
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{D}}\in\mathbb{C}^{N_{F}\times N_{F}}
is a diagonal matrix constructed as

\boldsymbol{\mathsf{D}}=\begin{cases}\phantom{\mathrm{j}}\Re\left(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{D_{G}}}\right)&\text{ if }\|\Re\left(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{D_{G}}}^{-1}\right)\|>\|\Im\left(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{D_{G}}}^{-1}\right)\|\\
\mathrm{j}\Im\left(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{D_{G}}}\right)&\text{ if }\|\Re\left(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{D_{G}}}^{-1}\right)\|<\|\Im\left(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{D_{G}}}^{-1}\right)\|\,,\end{cases}

(109)

with
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{D_{G}}}
being a diagonal matrix filled with the diagonal of
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{G_{\epsilon}}}
. Similarly as in (
70
), the preconditioned D-VIE with
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{P^{\Sigma}_{D}}}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{P^{\Lambda}_{D}}}
reads

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathsf{L_{D}}}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{Z}}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\alpha}}=\boldsymbol{\mathsf{L_{D}}}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{v}}\,,\text{ in which}

(110)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathsf{L_{D}}}=\frac{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{P^{\Lambda}_{D}}}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{D}}^{-1}}{\|\boldsymbol{\mathsf{P^{\Lambda}_{D}}}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{D}}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{G_{\epsilon}}}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{P^{\Lambda}_{D}}}\|}\ +\frac{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{P^{\Sigma}_{D}}}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{D_{0}}}^{-1}}{\|\boldsymbol{\mathsf{P^{\Sigma}_{D}}}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{D_{0}}}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{Z_{\Phi}}}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{P^{\Sigma}_{D}}}\|}\,,

(111)

and
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{D_{0}}}
is a diagonal matrix filled with the diagonal of
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{G}}
.

This new preconditioner gives rise to a slightly higher condition number than with the preconditioner obtained from
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{G_{\epsilon}}}
, but has been numerically verified to be stable in a broad frequency range and for high permittivity objects, as illustrated in next section.
Moreover the stiffness matrix
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\Sigma}}^{\mathrm{T}}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{D}}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\Sigma}}
, which is also a weighted graph Laplacian matrix, can be inverted efficiently using an aggregation-based AMG method from
[
47
,
48
]
together with a conjugate gradient (CG) algorithm.

IX
Conclusion

We introduced a novel volume integral equation for modeling dielectric and conductive materials with high-contrast in a broad frequency range. The new D-VIE scheme leverages scaled volume quasi-Helmholtz projectors to cure both its high-contrast and low-frequency ill-conditioning without deteriorating its dense discretization behavior. The scaling of the oblique quasi-Helmholtz projectors allows us to re-scale the equation when applied to inhomogeneous objects with high complex permittivities. Numerical examples illustrate the stability and accuracy of this new method. The preconditioned D-VIE shows good accuracy in the biomedical applications presented in this paper, both in the quasi-static regime and at higher frequencies.

Appendix A
Validity of the Scaled Loop-Star Decomposition

In this appendix, it is shown that

any SWG coefficient vector
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\alpha}}
can be decomposed with the scaled Loop-Star decomposition

\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\alpha}}=\boldsymbol{\mathsf{A}}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\Sigma}}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\tilde{s}}}+\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\Lambda}}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\tilde{l}}}\,,

(112)

where
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{A}}\in\mathbb{C}^{N_{F}\times N_{F}}
is an invertible complex symmetric matrix with one of the (potentially many different) square root matrices denoted by
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{A}}^{\frac{1}{2}}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\tilde{l}}}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\tilde{s}}}
are the coefficient vectors of the solenoidal and non-solenoidal basis functions in this new decomposition.
Without loss of generality, we show in the following that
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\alpha}}
can be (uniquely) decomposed as follows

\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\alpha}}=\boldsymbol{\mathsf{A}}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\tilde{\Sigma}}}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\bar{s}}}+\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\Lambda}}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\tilde{l}}}\,,

(113)

in which we recall that
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\tilde{\Sigma}}}
is obtained by removing one column (i.e. one star function) from
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{{\Sigma}}}
and we introduce
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\bar{s}}}
, the coefficient vector of the Star part in this decomposition.

To prove (
113
), we first show that
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{A}}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\alpha}}
can be orthogonally decomposed as

\boldsymbol{\mathsf{A}}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\alpha}}=\boldsymbol{\mathsf{A}}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\Lambda}}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\tilde{l}}}+\boldsymbol{\mathsf{A}}^{\frac{1}{2}}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\tilde{\Sigma}}}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\bar{s}}}\,,

(114)

the existence (and unicity) of which is equivalent to the existence (and unicity) of (
113
) since
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{A}}^{\frac{1}{2}}
is invertible. Since
\left(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{A}}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\Lambda}}\right)^{\mathrm{T}}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{A}}^{\frac{1}{2}}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\tilde{\Sigma}}}=\boldsymbol{\mathsf{0}}
, the decomposition is an orthogonal one and these two scaled transformation matrices have linearly independent column vectors. Then we need to show that the rank of the sum of
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{A}}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\Lambda}}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{A}}^{\frac{1}{2}}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\tilde{\Sigma}}}
is equal to the number of SWG basis functions
N_{F}
. Since
\text{rank}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{A}}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\Lambda}})=\text{rank}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\Lambda}})
(
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{A}}^{\frac{1}{2}}
is invertible),
\text{rank}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{A}}^{\frac{1}{2}}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\tilde{\Sigma}}})=\text{rank}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\tilde{\Sigma}}})
,
\text{rank}(\left[\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\tilde{\Sigma}}}~\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\Lambda}}\right])=N_{F}
, and
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{A}}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\Lambda}}
and

\lambda_{\text{min}}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{G}})=\min_{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{F}}}~({{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{x}}}^{\mathrm{T}}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{G}}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{x}}})/({{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{x}}}^{\mathrm{T}}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{x}}})
(i.e. Rayleigh principle) allows rewriting (
139
) as

\frac{{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{x}}}^{\mathrm{T}}\Im\left(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{G_{\epsilon}}}\right)\boldsymbol{\mathsf{x}}}{{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{x}}}^{\mathrm{T}}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{x}}}\geq\lambda_{\text{min}}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{G}})\frac{{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{x}}}^{\mathrm{T}}\Im\left(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{G_{\epsilon}}}\right)\boldsymbol{\mathsf{x}}}{{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{x}}}^{\mathrm{T}}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{G}}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{x}}}\geq\lambda_{\text{min}}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{G}})\frac{1}{\Im\left(\epsilon_{\text{max}}\right)}\,,

(140)

and thus
\lambda_{\text{min}}\left(\Im\left(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{G_{\epsilon}}}\right)\right)=\min_{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{F}}}~({{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{x}}}^{\mathrm{T}}\Im\left(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{G_{\epsilon}}}\right)\boldsymbol{\mathsf{x}}})/({{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{x}}}^{\mathrm{T}}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{x}}})\geq\lambda_{\text{min}}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{G}})/{\Im\left(\epsilon_{\text{max}}\right)}
.
Since
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{G_{\epsilon}}}
is symmetric, the inequality
s_{\text{min}}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{G_{\epsilon}}})\geq{s_{\text{min}}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{G}})}\omega/{\sigma_{\text{max}}}
also holds true.

Similarly, an upper bound for the maximum singular value of
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{G_{\epsilon}}}
can be derived. Instead of
l_{\text{min}}
,
l_{\text{max}}
is introduced

l_{\text{max}}=\max_{\boldsymbol{x}\in L^{2}(\Omega)^{3}}\langle\boldsymbol{x}(\boldsymbol{r}),\frac{\boldsymbol{x}(\boldsymbol{r})}{\Im(\epsilon(\boldsymbol{r}))}\rangle_{\Omega}/\langle\boldsymbol{x}(\boldsymbol{r}),{\boldsymbol{x}(\boldsymbol{r})}\rangle_{\Omega}\,.

(141)

Then, following the same procedure that was used to derive
s_{\text{min}}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{G_{\epsilon}}})
, we obtain
s_{\text{max}}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{G_{\epsilon}}})\leq{s_{\text{max}}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{G}})}\omega/{\sigma_{\text{min}}}
.

Appendix F
Bound for the Minimum Singular Value of a Sum of Two Matrices

Given two matrices
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{A}}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{B}}
, with
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{A}}
being invertible, if
\|\boldsymbol{\mathsf{B}}\|<s_{\text{min}}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{A}})
, the condition number of
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{A}}+\boldsymbol{\mathsf{B}}
can be bounded from above as

\begin{split}\text{cond}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{A}}+\boldsymbol{\mathsf{B}})&\leq\text{cond}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{A}})\text{cond}(\bf{I}+\boldsymbol{\mathsf{A}}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{B}})\\
&\leq\text{cond}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{A}})\frac{1+\|\boldsymbol{\mathsf{A}}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{B}}\|}{1-\|\boldsymbol{\mathsf{A}}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{B}}\|}\,.\end{split}

(142)

Then using the fact that
\|\boldsymbol{\mathsf{A}}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{B}}\|\leq\|\boldsymbol{\mathsf{B}}\|/s_{\text{min}}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{A}})
, inequality (
142
) becomes

\text{cond}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{A}}+\boldsymbol{\mathsf{B}})\leq\text{cond}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{A}})\frac{1+\|\boldsymbol{\mathsf{B}}\|/s_{\text{min}}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{A}})}{1-\|\boldsymbol{\mathsf{B}}\|/s_{\text{min}}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{A}})}=\gamma\,.

Since
\text{cond}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{A}}+\boldsymbol{\mathsf{B}})={\|\boldsymbol{\mathsf{A}}+\boldsymbol{\mathsf{B}}\|}/{s_{\text{min}}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{A}}+\boldsymbol{\mathsf{B}})}
, the next step is to find a lower bound for
\|\boldsymbol{\mathsf{A}}+\boldsymbol{\mathsf{B}}\|/\gamma
, which can be expressed as
s_{\text{min}}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{A}}+\boldsymbol{\mathsf{B}})\geq\|\boldsymbol{\mathsf{A}}+\boldsymbol{\mathsf{B}}\|/\gamma\geq|(\|\boldsymbol{\mathsf{A}}\|-\|\boldsymbol{\mathsf{B}}\|)|/\gamma
. Subsequently, leveraging the fact that
\|\boldsymbol{\mathsf{B}}\|\leq s_{\text{min}}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{A}})
, we can bound
|(\|\boldsymbol{\mathsf{A}}\|-\|\boldsymbol{\mathsf{B}}\|)|
from below with
\|\boldsymbol{\mathsf{A}}\|(1-1/\text{cond}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{A}}))
.
Finally, the bound for
s_{\text{min}}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{A}}+\boldsymbol{\mathsf{B}})
reads

s_{\text{min}}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{A}}+\boldsymbol{\mathsf{B}})\geq\|\boldsymbol{\mathsf{A}}\|\frac{\text{cond}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{A}})-1}{\text{cond}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{A}})\gamma}
.
