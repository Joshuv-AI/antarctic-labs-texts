Equilibrium and nonequilibrium Gibbs states on sofic groups
Christopher Shriver

Abstract
Recent work of Barbieri and Meyerovitch has shown that, for very general spin systems indexed by sofic groups, equilibrium (i.e. pressure-maximizing) states are Gibbs. The main goal of this paper is to show that the converse fails in an interesting way: for the Ising model on a free group, the free-boundary state typically fails to be equilibrium as long as it is not the only Gibbs state. For every temperature between the uniqueness and reconstruction thresholds a typical sofic approximation gives this state finite but non-maximal pressure, and for every lower temperature the pressure is non-maximal over every sofic approximation. We also show that, for more general interactions on sofic groups, the local limit of Gibbs states over a sofic approximation Σ \Sigma , if it exists, is a mixture of Σ \Sigma -equilibrium states. We use this to show that the plus- and minus-boundary-condition Ising states are Σ \Sigma -equilibrium if Σ \Sigma is any sofic approximation to a free group. Combined with a result of Dembo and Montanari, this implies that these states have the same entropy over every sofic approximation.



1
Overview

Consider a shift system
\mathtt{A}^{\Gamma}
, where
\mathtt{A}
is a finite set and
\Gamma
is a countable sofic group acting on
\mathtt{A}^{\Gamma}
by the “shift” action

(g\cdot\mathbf{x})_{h}=\mathbf{x}_{g^{-1}h}.

Suppose
u\colon\mathtt{A}^{\Gamma}\to\mathbb{R}
is a continuous function, which we think of as specifying for each
\mathbf{x}\in\mathtt{A}^{\Gamma}
the “specific energy at the identity
e\in\Gamma
.” Also let
\h_{\Sigma}\colon\Prob^{\Gamma}(\mathtt{A}^{\Gamma})\to[-\infty,\log\lvert\mathtt{A}\rvert]
denote the sofic entropy with respect to some sofic approximation
\Sigma
to
\Gamma
.

We consider two standard notions of what it means for a probability measure
\mu\in\Prob^{\Gamma}(\mathtt{A}^{\Gamma})
to describe the state of a system in statistical equilibrium. First, we say that
\mu
is a
\Sigma
-
equilibrium state
if it maximizes the
sofic pressure

\press_{\Sigma}(\mu)=\h_{\Sigma}(\mu)-\int u\,d\mu.

Definitions of similar quantities often include a factor of temperature or inverse temperature on one of the terms; we absorb this factor into the function
u
. See Section
2
for more precise definitions.

The second notion is that of a
Gibbs state
. Briefly, a measure
\mu\in\Prob(\mathtt{A}^{\Gamma})
is a Gibbs state with respect to some potential if it has certain local conditional probabilities specified by that potential. The collection of these conditional probabilities is called a specification, or the DLR equations, after Dobrushin, Lanford, and Ruelle who initially developed the concept. They can be interpreted as a kind of local equilibrium criterion. We will not provide a precise definition of “Gibbs state” here; instead we will work with specific standard examples. For precise definitions see for example
[
Geo11
]
. When we say a measure “is Gibbs” or “is a Gibbs state,” we always mean with respect to a particular potential.

When
\Gamma
is amenable and the energy function
u
is “nice enough,” the set of equilibrium states is the same as the set of Gibbs states. The first versions of this theorem are due to Dobrushin, Lanford, and Ruelle. For
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
-indexed finite-alphabet shift systems with
u
defined by an “absolutely summable potential,” see
[
Geo11
, Chapter 15]
. For general amenable groups, see
[
Tem84
]
.

One direction of this equivalence has been extended to actions of sofic groups: Barbieri and Meyerovitch have recently shown that every equilibrium state is Gibbs as long as
\Gamma
is sofic and
u
satisfies a somewhat technical (but very general) criterion
[
BM22
]
.

The most natural version of a converse would be that every Gibbs state is equilibrium for “nice enough” interactions. It is not clear what “nice enough” should mean exactly, but it is natural to assume it should include the Ising model: this is generally seen as the simplest nontrivial interaction. We will consider, then, whether every Gibbs state for an Ising model on the Cayley graph of a free group
\Gamma
(
i.e.
an infinite regular tree) must be an equilibrium state.

But, if the temperature is low enough, there exists a sofic approximation
\Sigma
to the free group
\Gamma
such that the free-boundary Ising state
\mu^{\mathrm{FB}}
(which is Gibbs) has
\h_{\Sigma}(\mu^{\mathrm{FB}})=-\infty
, so
\press_{\Sigma}(\mu^{\mathrm{FB}})=-\infty
. Intuitively, this is because a good model for
\mu^{\mathrm{FB}}
on a sofic approximation is a bipartition, which has very small cut density if the temperature is low. Therefore a typical sofic approximation does not admit good models for
\mu^{\mathrm{FB}}
. But any Bernoulli shift, for example, will have finite
\press_{\Sigma}(\cdot)
, so
\mu^{\mathrm{FB}}
is a Gibbs state which is not
\Sigma
-equilibrium.

This sort of degenerate behavior of sofic entropy, where
\Sigma
is simply incompatible with
\mu^{\mathrm{FB}}
, is well-known and does not provide a very satisfying counterexample. The same example can be used to show that there are two sofic approximations which give
\mu^{\mathrm{FB}}
different sofic entropy values. But it is still an open question whether a measure can have different
finite
sofic entropy values.

This suggests looking for a partial converse: that every Ising Gibbs state with
finite

\Sigma
-entropy is
\Sigma
-equilibrium. In this paper we show that this is not true. We show that for all temperatures below the uniqueness threshold (and no external field) the free-boundary state is
\Sigma
-non-equilibrium for some
\Sigma
for which
\h_{\Sigma}(\mu^{\mathrm{FB}})>-\infty
. For temperatures above the reconstruction threshold, this is true for a “typical”
\Sigma
.

Furthermore, we show that for low enough temperatures
\mu^{\mathrm{FB}}
is nonequilibrium over
every

\Sigma
. This also rules out the possibility that some additional assumption on the sofic approximation could make the converse hold, or that every Gibbs state is equilibrium over
some
sofic approximation.

Specifically, for free groups of rank at least 2, we establish that
\mu^{\mathrm{FB}}
is non-equilibrium over
typical

\Sigma
for all temperatures between the uniqueness and reconstruction thresholds (Theorem
A
). The temperature being below either the reconstruction threshold or half the uniqueness threshold is a sufficient condition for
\mu^{\mathrm{FB}}
to be non-equilibrium over
every

\Sigma
(Theorem
B
); it is unclear whether this is true up to the uniqueness threshold. See Figure
1
.

Figure 1:
Temperature ranges where equilibrium status of the free boundary state
\mu^{\mathrm{FB}}
is established. The horizontal axis is
r
, the rank of the free group (and half the degree of the Cayley tree), and the vertical axis is inverse temperature or interaction strength.

In the upper region,
\mu^{\mathrm{FB}}
is never equilibrium. In the middle region, it is nonequilibrium over some sofic approximations. In the lower region it is the only Gibbs state, so must be equilibrium.

We also develop a method for establishing that some states are equilibrium (Theorem
6.5
). We use it (and
[
MMS12
]
) to show that the plus and minus states (with no external field) are equilibrium over every sofic approximation to a free group. Combined with
[
DM10
]
, this implies that the plus and minus Ising states on a free group have the same sofic entropy over every sofic approximation.

1.1
Statements of main results

The proof of Theorem
A
, that
\mu^{\mathrm{FB}}
is nonequilibrium over a typical sofic approximation, is based on the following “first moment” calculation:

Proposition 1.1
.

At every temperature below the uniqueness threshold, the free boundary state is
\f
-nonequilibrium.

By “
\f
-equilibrium” we mean that a state maximizes the pressure where the entropy quantity is the
\f
invariant. As shown in
[
Bow10b
]
, the
\f
invariant is the sofic entropy over a particular random sofic approximation: a sequence of uniformly random homomorphisms. In random graph terminology, this corresponds to the permutation model.

We prove Proposition
1.1
using a natural one-parameter family
\{\mu_{t}\,:\,t\in\mathbb{R}\}
of Markov chains in which
t=0
gives the free boundary state. The uniqueness threshold is exactly where the second derivative of the
\f
-pressure at
t=0
becomes positive. Since the free boundary state is a local minimum of the pressure within this family, it must be nonequilibrium.

This alone is not a satisfying counterexample because it is not obvious whether
\mu^{\mathrm{FB}}
being
\f
-nonequilibrium is simply due to the degenerate behavior mentioned above: at low enough temperatures the
\f
invariant, and hence the
\f
-pressure, is affected by the possibility of the random sofic approximation supporting no good models for
\mu^{\mathrm{FB}}
at all. What we really want is a deterministic
\Sigma
such that
\mu^{\mathrm{FB}}
has finite
\h_{\Sigma}
but is not
\Sigma
-equilibrium.

Moreover, Seward’s ergodic decomposition formula for the
\f
-invariant
[
Sew16
]
prevents any non-ergodic measure from being
\f
-equilibrium. If the ergodic decomposition of
\mu
has Shannon entropy
\delta
, then (since the average specific energy is affine)
\press_{\f}(\mu)
is
\delta
less than the average of
\press_{\f}(\cdot)
over the ergodic decomposition, so cannot be maximal. This is not a concern for
\mu^{\mathrm{FB}}
, since this state is ergodic at every temperature, but it is another reason why the converse can fail for somewhat degenerate reasons with random sofic approximations.

As long as the free-boundary state is tail-trivial (i.e. when the temperature is above the reconstruction threshold), we can use
[
Shr21
]
to show that a “typical” sofic approximation has the desired property:

Theorem A
.

For every temperature between the uniqueness and reconstruction thresholds, for a “typical” sofic approximation
\Sigma
the free boundary Ising state has finite
\Sigma
-entropy but is
\Sigma
-nonequilibrium.

More precisely, there is a sequence of sets of homomorphisms
\{S_{n}\subset\Hom(\mathbb{F}_{r},\Sym(n))\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
with
\mathbb{P}(S_{n})\to 1
such that if
\Sigma=\{\sigma_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
is a sequence of maps with
\sigma_{n}\in S_{m_{n}}
for some
m_{n}\to\infty
then

1.

\Sigma
is a sofic approximation to
\mathbb{F}_{r}

2.

\press_{\Sigma}(\mu^{\mathrm{FB}})=\press_{\f}(\mu^{\mathrm{FB}})<\press_{\f}(\mu^{+})=\press_{\Sigma}(\mu^{+})

The probability measure on
\Hom(\mathbb{F}_{r},\Sym(n))
is the uniform distribution.

Remarks
:

1.

This result says something about the Boltzmann distribution over a typical large random regular graph: while there are microstates corresponding to the free-boundary state, the total probability of the set of these microstates is exponentially small at intermediate temperatures. At low enough temperatures, there are typically no free-boundary microstates. An analogous result for the Potts model is proven in
[
CGG+23
]
. There, this phenomenon is called “phase coexistence.”

2.

The main theorem of
[
NSZ22
]
implies that
\mu^{\mathrm{FB}}
has good models over every sofic approximation, at all temperatures down to some multiple of the reconstruction threshold. In particular, the
\f
invariant is unaffected by the possibility of having no good models. The second-moment method we use here instead applies over a wider range of temperatures, and also tells us that the
\Sigma
-pressure is exactly the
\f
-pressure for typical
\Sigma
.

We then show that, for an overlapping range of temperatures, the free boundary state is never equilibrium over any sofic approximation:

Theorem B
.

For every temperature below either half the uniqueness threshold or below the reconstruction threshold, the free boundary state is
\Sigma
-nonequilibrium for every
\Sigma
.

In the condition “half the uniqueness threshold,” the constant
\frac{1}{2}
is not optimal. The hypothetical ideal constant would be
1
, but numerically it appears the the method used here can only achieve up to
\frac{1}{1.65}\approx 0.61
. This is discussed further following the proof at the end of Section
5
.

We also have a positive result showing that the plus and minus states are always equilibrium:

Theorem C
.

With zero external field, the plus and minus Ising states are equilibrium over every sofic approximation to a free group. These two states are the only
\f
-equilibrium states.

This is a consequence of a more general result (Theorem
6.5
) which shows that, for any continuous energy function
u
, the local limit of finitary Gibbs states over a sofic approximation
\Sigma
(if it exists) is a mixture of
\Sigma
-equilibrium measures.

Theorem
C
implies uniqueness of sofic entropy for the plus/minus states:

Corollary 1.2
.

With zero external field, the plus and minus Ising states have the same sofic entropy over every sofic approximation.

Proof.

[
DM10
, Theorem 2.4]
shows that the sofic topological pressure is the same over any deterministic sofic approximation. Since, by Theorem
C
, the plus and minus states attain the supremum in Chung’s variational principle
[
Chu13
]
for every sofic approximation, they always have the same sofic pressure. Since the energy is independent of the sofic approximation, so is the sofic entropy.
∎

1.2
Some related work

The paper
[
BPS95
]
, written before the introduction of sofic entropy, show that “Gibbs” and “equilibrium” are not equivalent on trees when entropy is defined by the limit of normalized Shannon entropies of marginals on finite subtrees. They show that for some interactions there is a unique equilibrium state, which is not Gibbs, and for the Ising model with zero external field the free boundary state is the unique equilibrium measure. This is very different from what happens with sofic entropy.

The earlier paper
[
FS77
]
also noted that Gibbs states on trees can fail to minimize relative entropy density, and introduced an “inner” relative entropy density for which the variational principle does hold.

The question is worth revisiting in the context of sofic entropy: as the authors of
[
BPS95
]
point out, the source of some of the problems with trees is their nonamenability. Sofic entropy solves some of these difficulties: most famously, it is a measure conjugacy invariant
[
Bow10c
,
Bow10d
]
. In the context of statistical physics, sofic pressure is nondecreasing under Glauber dynamics
[
Shr22
]
and, as mentioned above, it is generally true with sofic entropy that equilibrium measures are Gibbs
[
BM22
]
.

1.2.1
Related results on the Potts model

Recent independent work of Coja-Oghlan et al.
[
CGG+23
]
has established analogous behavior for the Potts model on random regular graphs, but the terminology is different. In this section we compare the present work to
[
CGG+23
]
.

The result closest to the present paper is “phase coexistence”: a typical large random regular graph has some labelings which correspond to the free-boundary state (which they call paramagnetic) but exponentially fewer than the number which correspond to constant-boundary-conditions states (which they call ferromagnetic). The respective exponential growth rates are Bethe free energies of the states – in this case, this is the same as the
\f
invariant. Their correspondence between labelings of a finite graph and infinite-volume measures is slightly different form ours: they only require the labelings to use individual colors with the correct frequencies, while we require the average local statistics to match. However, in the temperature range of interest (non-reconstruction/tail-triviality), most of the labelings with the correct color frequencies should be good models for the correct Markov chains, so this should not make a difference. This is because, as discussed below, Markov chains maximize the
\f
invariant and tail-triviality implies that the
\f
invariant is the typical entropy value (for Gibbs states of nearest-neighbor interactions – see Proposition
4.1
).

There is also an interesting connection with the main metastability result of
[
CGG+23
]
. Their phase coexistence result is proven by using “strong non-reconstruction” to avoid involved second-moment optimization. They then use that the paramagnetic state is a local
maximizer
of the Bethe free energy to argue that there is an exponentially small bottleneck keeping Glauber dynamics near paramagnetic microstates for an exponentially long amount of time. We also use non-reconstruction via Proposition
4.1
to avoid doing a second-moment optimization by hand. This result is based on an earlier approach from
[
Shr21
]
that used a weaker but more general metastability result for Glauber evolutions of Gibbs microstates. This does not require the Gibbs states to be local maximizers of the pressure, but it only shows that Glauber dynamics is trapped for an amount of time which grows linearly in the size of the graph (or, in continuous-time Glauber dynamics, for a constant amount of time). The metastability result of
[
Shr21
]
is also more general in that it applies to arbitrary locally tree-like graphs, while
[
CGG+23
]
relies on a counting estimate which holds for typical random graphs.

Note that the Ising free boundary state, which we study in the present paper, is actually a local
minimum
of the
\f
-pressure within the parametrization
\mu_{t}
(Proposition
1.1
) so the bottleneck metastability approach of
[
CGG+23
]
will not work. Moreover, the coupling argument for “strong non-reconstruction”
[
CGG+23
, Proposition 2.6]
fails for the Ising model outside the uniqueness regime, so the “phase coexistence” result of the present paper (Theorem
A
) does not simply follow from
[
CGG+23
]
by taking
q=2
.

See Figures
2
,
3
for plots of the
\f
-pressures of the families of Markov chains which arise for the Potts model.

We also emphasize that neither the present paper nor
[
CGG+23
]
seems able to completely address phase coexistence when the free-boundary state is reconstructible. Our Theorem
B
states that, for all temperatures below the reconstruction threshold, the set of free-boundary microstates is given exponentially small weight by the Boltzmann measure on any locally tree-like graph. But we do not know whether this weight is nonzero for a typical random regular graph. At low enough temperatures it must be zero (since free-boundary microstates would give bisections with very small cut density, which don’t typically exist
[
DMS17
]
), but it seems to be unknown what happens just below the reconstruction threshold.

q=5

q-1=4

t=0

2
Definitions

Although the main results are stated for the Ising model on a tree, some results below (for example, Theorem
6.5
) are proven in greater generality. Let
\Gamma
be a countable sofic group and
\mathtt{A}
be a finite set.

2.1
Sofic entropy and sofic groups

We give a brief summary here to establish notation. The concept of sofic entropy is originally due to Lewis Bowen
[
Bow10d
]
but we follow the notation of
[
Aus16
]
for shift systems.

If
V
is a finite set, given
\mathbf{x}\in\mathtt{A}^{V}
,
v\in V
,
\sigma\colon\Gamma\to\Sym(V)
we define the
pullback name

\Pi_{v}^{\sigma}\mathbf{x}\in\mathtt{A}^{\Gamma}
by

\Pi_{v}^{\sigma}\mathbf{x}(\gamma)=\mathbf{x}\big(\sigma(\gamma)(v)\big).

The
empirical distribution
of
\mathbf{x}
over
\sigma
is

P_{\mathbf{x}}^{\sigma}=\big(v\mapsto\Pi_{v}^{\sigma}\mathbf{x}\big)_{*}\Unif(V)=\frac{1}{\lvert V\rvert}\sum_{v\in V}\delta_{\Pi_{v}^{\sigma}\mathbf{x}}.

If
\mathcal{O}
is a weak*-open subset of
\Prob(\mathtt{A}^{\Gamma})
, we define

\Omega(\sigma,\mathcal{O})=\{\mathbf{x}\in\mathtt{A}^{V}\,:\,P_{\mathbf{x}}^{\sigma}\in\mathcal{O}\}.

If
\mathcal{O}
is a small neighborhood of some
\mu
then we can think of any
\mathbf{x}\in\Omega(\sigma,\mathcal{O})
as a “good model” for
\mu
over
\sigma
in that its “local statistics” are close to
\mu
.

If
\Sigma=\big(\sigma_{n}\colon\Gamma\to\Sym(V_{n})\big)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is a sequence of maps then the sofic entropy of
\mu\in\Prob(\mathtt{A}^{\Gamma})
over
\Sigma
is defined by

\h_{\Sigma}(\mu)=\inf_{\mathcal{O}\ni\mu}\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{\lvert V_{n}\rvert}\log\left\lvert\Omega(\sigma_{n},\mathcal{O})\right\rvert.

This is the (upper) exponential growth rate of the number of good models for
\mu
over
\Sigma
.

Given
F\Subset\Gamma
and
\delta>0
, a map
\sigma\colon\Gamma\to\Sym(V)
is called
(F,\delta)
-
sofic
if

\frac{1}{\lvert V\rvert}\left\lvert\big\{v\in V\,:\,\sigma(\gamma)(v)\neq v\ \forall\gamma\in F\setminus\{1_{\Gamma}\}\big\}\right\rvert>1-\delta\quad\text{(almost free)}

and

\frac{1}{\lvert V\rvert}\left\lvert\big\{v\in V\,:\,\sigma(\gamma_{1}\gamma_{2})(v)=\sigma(\gamma_{1})\big(\sigma(\gamma_{2})(v)\big)\ \forall\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}\in F\big\}\right\rvert>1-\delta\quad\text{(almost action)}.

A sequence
\Sigma=\{\sigma_{n}\colon\Gamma\to\Sym(V_{n})\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
is a sofic approximation (to
\Gamma
) if
\lvert V_{n}\rvert\to\infty
and for every
F,\delta
, the map
\sigma_{n}
is
(F,\delta)
-sofic for all large enough
n
.

A countable group
\Gamma
is called sofic if there is at least one sofic approximation to
\Gamma
.

If
\Sigma
is a sofic approximation then
\h_{\Sigma}(\cdot)
is a measure-conjugacy invariant
[
Bow10d
]
, and
\h_{\Sigma}(\mu)=-\infty
if
\mu
is not invariant under the shift action of
\Gamma
.

2.2
Random sofic approximations,
\f
-entropy

Suppose we have a sequence
\Sigma=(\sigma_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of
random
maps
\sigma_{n}\colon\Gamma\to\Sym(V_{n})
. Then
\Sigma
is called a (random) sofic approximation if for any
(F,\delta)
and any
c>0
we have

\mathbb{P}(\sigma_{n}\text{ is }(F,\delta)\text{-sofic})>1-e^{-cn}

for all large enough
n
. The sofic entropy of
\mu
over a random
\Sigma
is

\h_{\Sigma}(\mu)=\inf_{\mathcal{O}\ni\mu}\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{\lvert V_{n}\rvert}\log\EE\left\lvert\Omega(\sigma_{n},\mathcal{O})\right\rvert.

The expectation is taken over the random
\sigma_{n}
.

In the case of a free group
\Gamma=\mathbb{F}_{r}=\langle s_{1},\ldots,s_{r}\rangle
, one natural choice is to let
\sigma_{n}
be a uniformly random
homomorphism
in
\Hom(\Gamma,\Sym(n))
. We call this sequence, which is a random sofic approximation,
\f
. In
[
Bow10b
]
it is shown that
\h_{\f}(\mu)
is equal to the “
\f
invariant”
\f(\mu)
, a measure-conjugacy invariant for free-group actions introduced in
[
Bow10c
]
. For general systems, it should be noted that the two are equal only when there is a finite-entropy generating partition, but this is satisfied for finite-alphabet shift systems. More recently,
\f(\mu)
has sometimes been called “RS entropy” (where RS stands for “replica-symmetric”) or “annealed entropy” to emphasize analogy with related ideas in statistical physics
[
Bow19
,
BBS22
]
.

Still in the case of a free group, if
\mu\in\Prob^{\Gamma}(\mathtt{A}^{\Gamma})
is a Markov chain then there is an easy-to-compute formula for
\f(\mu)
. For
\gamma\in\Gamma
let
\pi_{\gamma}\colon\mathtt{A}^{\Gamma}\to\mathtt{A}
denote projection onto the
\gamma
coordinate. Then, as shown in
[
Bow10a
]
,

\f(\mu)=(1-r)\shent_{\mu}(\pi_{e})+\sum_{i=1}^{r}\shent_{\mu}(\pi_{e}\mid\pi_{s_{i}}).

2.3
Equilibrium measures and pressure

We sometimes want to work with measures supported on some closed, shift-invariant subset
\mathcal{X}\subset\mathtt{A}^{\Gamma}
, called a
subshift
. For example, independent sets can be considered as labelings of
\Gamma
by elements of
\{0,1\}
such that no two
1
’s are adjacent (in the Cayley graph for some fixed generating set).

The same approach to sofic pressure on a subshift appears in
[
BM22
, Section 3.4]
.

Let
u\colon\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
be a continuous function. This will play the role of a “specific energy” or “energy per site” functional without having to perform a spatial average (which is not available for nonamenable groups).

Since
\mathtt{A}^{\Gamma}
, and hence
\mathcal{X}
, is compact,
u
has minimum and maximum values
u^{\min}
and
u^{\max}
.

For
\mu\in\Prob^{\Gamma}(\mathcal{X})
, we define the
energy density
of
\mu
by

u(\mu)=\int_{\mathcal{X}}u(x)\,\mu(dx).

The
pressure
of
\mu
over some (random or deterministic) sofic approximation
\Sigma
is then defined by

\press_{\Sigma}(\mu)=\h_{\Sigma}(\mu)-u(\mu).

Any
\mu
maximizing the pressure over
\Sigma
is called
\Sigma
-equilibrium. At least one such
\mu
always exists by semicontinuity, but in general there can be many.

We will also need to work with pressure for measures on finite “model spaces”
\mathtt{A}^{V}
, but this requires dealing with a small issue: if
\sigma\colon\Gamma\to\Sym(V)
is any map, then we would want to define the total energy of a configuration
\mathbf{x}\in\mathtt{A}^{V}
by summing up the energy contributions from each vertex, each of which can be determined by pulling back to a configuration on
\Gamma
:

U^{\sigma}(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{v\in V}u(\Pi_{v}^{\sigma}\mathbf{x})=\lvert V\rvert\cdot\int_{\mathcal{X}}u(x)\,P_{\mathbf{x}}^{\sigma}(dx).

In the case of the Ising model, this is fine. But in general we cannot be sure that every pullback name
\Pi_{v}^{\sigma}\mathbf{x}
is in the subshift
\mathcal{X}
(i.e. the domain of
u
). So we need to pick a continuous extension
\tilde{u}\colon\mathtt{A}^{\Gamma}\to\mathbb{R}
and define

\tilde{U}^{\sigma}(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{v\in V}\tilde{u}(\Pi_{v}^{\sigma}\mathbf{x})=\lvert V\rvert\cdot\int_{\mathtt{A}^{\Gamma}}\tilde{u}(x)\,P_{\mathbf{x}}^{\sigma}(dx).

If
\mathbf{x}
is a good model for some
\mu\in\Prob^{\Gamma}(\mathcal{X})
, then
\frac{1}{\lvert V\rvert}\tilde{u}(\mathbf{x})\approx\tilde{u}(\mu)=u(\mu)
.

For
\zeta\in\Prob(\mathtt{A}^{V})
, we then define the average energy

\tilde{U}^{\sigma}(\zeta)=\int_{\mathtt{A}^{V}}\tilde{U}^{\sigma}(\mathbf{x})\,\zeta(d\mathbf{x})=\lvert V\rvert\cdot\int_{\mathtt{A}^{\Gamma}}\tilde{u}(\mathbf{x})\,P_{\zeta}^{\sigma}(d\mathbf{x})

and the pressure

\widetilde{\Press}^{\sigma}(\zeta)=\shent(\zeta)-\tilde{U}^{\sigma}(\zeta).

It is straightforward to show using Lagrange multipliers that the unique
\zeta
maximizing
\widetilde{\Press}^{\sigma}(\cdot)
is the measure
\tilde{\xi}_{\sigma}
with
\tilde{\xi}_{\sigma}\{\mathbf{x}\}=\tilde{Z}_{\sigma}^{-1}\exp(-\tilde{U}^{\sigma}(\mathbf{x}))
, where the normalizing constant
\tilde{Z}_{\sigma}=\sum_{\mathbf{x}}\exp(-\tilde{U}^{\sigma}(\mathbf{x}))
is called the
partition function
. It is also straightforward to check that
\widetilde{\Press}^{\sigma}(\xi_{\sigma})=\log\tilde{Z}_{\sigma}
.

But this
\tilde{\xi}_{\sigma}
may depend on the choice of extension
\tilde{u}
, and it may not be supported on good models for measures on
\mathcal{X}
. To fix this, given an open neighborhood
\mathcal{U}\supset\Prob^{\Gamma}(\mathcal{X})
let
\tilde{\xi}_{\sigma}^{\mathcal{U}}
be the measure supported on
\Omega(\sigma,\mathcal{U})
with maximal
\tilde{P}^{\sigma}
. Again using Lagrange multipliers, we get that

\tilde{\xi}_{\sigma}^{\mathcal{U}}\{\mathbf{x}\}=\tilde{Z}_{\sigma,\mathcal{U}}^{-1}\exp(-\tilde{U}^{\sigma}(\mathbf{x}))\mathbf{1}_{\mathbf{x}\in\Omega(\sigma,\mathcal{U})}

where

\tilde{Z}_{\sigma,\mathcal{U}}=\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in\Omega(\sigma,\mathcal{U})}\exp(-\tilde{U}^{\sigma}(\mathbf{x})).

The
topological pressure
in this setting can be defined by

\press_{\Sigma}=\inf_{\mathcal{U}\supset\Prob^{\Gamma}(\mathcal{X})}\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log\EE_{\sigma_{n}}\tilde{Z}_{\sigma_{n},\mathcal{U}}.

The infimum over neighborhoods
\mathcal{U}
of
\Prob^{\Gamma}(\mathcal{X})
eliminates the dependence on the extension
\tilde{u}
.

The sofic, deterministic version of this quantity was introduced in
[
Chu13
]
, where it was also shown that, for deterministic
\Sigma
,

\press_{\Sigma}=\sup\{\press_{\Sigma}(\mu)\,:\,\mu\in\Prob^{\Gamma}(\mathcal{X})\}.

In other words,
\press_{\Sigma}
is the value of
{\press}_{\Sigma}(\mu)
for every
\Sigma
-equilibrium
\mu
.

2.4
The Ising Model

In this paper we mostly work with the ferromagnetic Ising model. For a free group
\mathbb{F}_{r}
with generating set
\langle s_{1},\ldots,s_{r}\rangle
the Ising model can be defined by setting

u(\mathbf{x})=-\frac{J}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{r}\big(\mathbf{x}(e)\mathbf{x}(s_{i})+\mathbf{x}(e)\mathbf{x}(s_{i}^{-1})\big)

for
\mathbf{x}\in\{-1,+1\}^{\mathbb{F}_{r}}
, where
J>0
is some fixed “interaction strength.” We can think of each edge with the same label on either end as having energy
-J
and each edge with opposite labels as having energy
+J
: we then assign half the energy of each edge to each of the vertices it’s incident on. Then the energy assigned to the identity
e
is given by the formula above.

Often a parameter called “inverse temperature”
\beta
is included in addition to or instead of
J
(usually not in the definition of
u
, but rather as a multiplicative factor often appearing along with
u
), but mathematically this is redundant in this setting. Here we follow the notation of
[
Geo11
]
. But we do use the standard terminology of “low temperature” (equivalent to large
J
) and “high temperature” (small
J
).

Depending on
J
and
r
, there are up to three completely homogeneous Markov chains which are Gibbs measures for the Ising model. Following
[
Geo11
, Section 12.2]
, for each
t\in\mathbb{R}
, let

\alpha(t)=\frac{e^{-2J}+e^{-2t}}{2e^{-2J}+2\cosh(2t)}

and

\beta(t)=\frac{1}{e^{2(J+t)}+1}.

Then let
\mu_{t}
be the completely homogeneous (i.e. invariant under the full automorphism group of the Cayley graph) Markov chain on
\{-1,+1\}^{\Gamma}
with single-site marginal the row vector

\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\mathbb{P}\{-1\}&\mathbb{P}\{+1\}\end{array}\right)=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\alpha(t)&1-\alpha(t)\end{array}\right)

and transition matrix

\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\mathbb{P}\{-1\to-1\}&\mathbb{P}\{-1\to+1\}\\
\mathbb{P}\{+1\to-1\}&\mathbb{P}\{+1\to+1\}\end{array}\right)=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}1-\beta(-t)&\beta(-t)\\
\beta(t)&1-\beta(t)\end{array}\right).

In
[
Geo11
, Section 12.2]
it is shown that the Gibbs measures for the Ising model on
\Gamma=\mathbb{F}_{r}
which are completely homogeneous Markov chains are exactly the measures
\mu_{t}
where
t
is a solution to

t=\frac{2r-1}{2}\log\frac{\cosh(t+J)}{\cosh(t-J)}.

(Note that
d
in
[
Geo11
]
denotes the branching factor, not the degree.)

For any
J,r
we have a solution
t=0
, which is called the
free boundary
Gibbs state
\mu^{\mathrm{FB}}
. For
J>J_{uniq}(r)\coloneqq\arctanh(\frac{1}{2r-1})
, there is a pair of solutions
t_{+},t_{-}
which give two additional states called the plus and minus boundary condition states, respectively. In fact
t_{+}=-t_{-}
. We write
\mu^{+}=\mu_{t^{+}}
for the plus state and
\mu^{-}=\mu_{t^{-}}
for the minus state. If
J\leq J_{uniq}(r)
then
t=0
is the only solution, and in fact
\mu_{0}=\mu^{\mathrm{FB}}
is the only Gibbs measure. For that reason
J_{uniq}(r)
is called the uniqueness threshold.

There is a second threshold called the reconstruction threshold,
J_{rec}(r)=\arctanh\frac{1}{\sqrt{2r-1}}
. The free boundary state is tail-trivial if and only if
0<J\leq J_{rec}(r)

[
BRZ95
]
. Tail-triviality is equivalent to being an extreme point in the convex set of all (not necessarily shift-invariant) Gibbs measures. Many authors also use the term “pure.”

3
The free boundary state is
\f
-nonequilibrium

In this section we prove Proposition
1.1
. Throughout, the external field is assumed to be zero.

We prove Proposition
1.1
by showing that
t=0
is a local minimum of

(where
\mu_{t}
is as defined in Section

3.1
Specific energy

In this subsection we find an expression for the specific energy
u(\mu_{t})
.

The energy of the configuration
\mathbf{x}\in\{\pm 1\}^{\Gamma}
due to interactions with the identity
e\in\Gamma
is

u(\mathbf{x})=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{v\text{ adj. to }e}-J\mathbf{x}(e)\mathbf{x}(v)=-\frac{J}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{r}[\mathbf{x}(e)\mathbf{x}(s_{i})+\mathbf{x}(e)\mathbf{x}(s_{i}^{-1})].

The factor of
\frac{1}{2}
appears because each edge is involved in the interaction of two particles, so we assign half the energy of the edge to each. The corresponding definition in
[
Geo11
]
is Equation (15.22).

Each of the
2r
products of adjacent spins has the same distribution under
\mu_{t}
, and

\displaystyle\EE_{\mathbf{x}\sim\mu_{t}}\mathbf{x}(e)\mathbf{x}(s_{i})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\{\mathbf{x}(e)=\mathbf{x}(s_{i})\}-\mathbb{P}\{\mathbf{x}(e)\neq\mathbf{x}(s_{i})\}

\displaystyle=\left[\alpha(t)(1-\beta(-t))+(1-\alpha(t))(1-\beta(t))\right]-\left[\alpha(t)\beta(-t)+(1-\alpha(t))\beta(t)\right]

\displaystyle=\left[\alpha(t)-\alpha(t)\beta(-t)+1-\beta(t)-\alpha(t)+\alpha(t)\beta(t)\right]-\left[\alpha(t)\beta(-t)+\beta(t)-\alpha(t)\beta(t)\right]

\displaystyle=1-2\alpha(t)\beta(-t)-2\beta(t)+2\alpha(t)\beta(t)

\displaystyle=1+2\alpha(t)[\beta(t)-\beta(-t)]-2\beta(t).

So the specific energy is

u(\mu_{t})\coloneqq\EE_{\mathbf{x}\sim\mu_{t}}u(\mathbf{x})=-Jr\left(1+2\alpha(t)[\beta(t)-\beta(-t)]-2\beta(t)\right).

3.2
\f
invariant

In this subsection we find an expression for
\f(\mu_{t})
.

Since
\mu_{t}
is a Markov chain,
\f(\mu_{t})=F(\mu_{t})

[
Bow10a
]
.

\displaystyle F(\mu_{t})

\displaystyle=(1-r)\shent_{\mathbf{x}\sim\mu_{t}}(\mathbf{x}(e))+\sum_{i=1}^{r}\shent_{\mathbf{x}\sim\mu_{t}}(\mathbf{x}(s_{i})|\mathbf{x}(e))

\displaystyle=(1-r)\shent(\alpha(t))+r\left[\alpha(t)\shent(\beta(-t))+(1-\alpha(t))\shent(\beta(t))\right]

where in the second line we use
\shent
to denote the binary entropy function
\shent(p)=-p\log p-(1-p)\log(1-p)
.

3.3
Proof of Proposition
1.1

We have shown so far that

\displaystyle\press_{\f}(\mu_{t})

\displaystyle=\f(\mu_{t})-u(\mu_{t})

\displaystyle=Jr\left(1+2\alpha(t)[\beta(t)-\beta(-t)]+2\beta(t)\right)

\displaystyle\hskip 72.26999pt+(1-r)\shent(\alpha(t))-r\left[\alpha(t)\shent(\beta(-t))+(1-\alpha(t))\shent(\beta(t))\right].

The derivative is quite complicated, but using Mathematica
[
Wol21
]
, for example, we can then calculate
\left.\frac{d}{dt}\right|_{t=0}\press_{\f}(\mu_{t})=0
and

Since

for all

So for all temperatures below the uniqueness threshold, the free boundary state
\mu_{0}
is a local minimum of

, so cannot be
\f
-equilibrium.

4
Pressure over a typical sofic approximation (Proof of Theorem
A
)

Suppose that two Gibbs states
\mu,\nu
are both tail-trivial but have different
\f
-pressures. For the Ising model, by
[
Shr21
, Corollary 16]
this implies that
\mu,\nu
both satisfy a standard kind of “second moment” criterion. For
\mu
, for example, this means that if
\lambda
is any self-joining of
\mu
(that is,
\lambda
is a shift-invariant measure on
(\{+1,-1\}^{2})^{\mathbb{F}_{r}}
with both
\{+1,-1\}^{\mathbb{F}_{r}}
-marginals equal to
\mu
) then
\f(\lambda)\leq 2\f(\mu)
.

The next proposition shows that this second-moment criterion implies that for a “typical” sofic approximation
\Sigma
, the
\Sigma
-entropy of both measures are the same as their
\f
-invariants. Combined with Proposition
1.1
, this proves Theorem
A
, since
\mu^{\mathrm{FB}}
is tail-trivial at temperatures down to the reconstruction threshold and
\mu^{+},\mu^{-}
are always tail-trivial.

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
\Gamma=\mathbb{F}_{r}
denote the rank-
r
free group. If
\mu\in\Prob^{\Gamma}(\mathtt{A}^{\Gamma})
is such that
\f(\lambda)\leq 2\f(\mu)
for any self-joining
\lambda
of
\mu
, there exists a sequence
\{S_{n}\subset\Hom(\Gamma,\Sym(n)\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
of sets of homomorphisms with
\mathbb{P}(S_{n})\to 1
such that if
\sigma_{n}\in S_{n}
for each
n
then

1.

\Sigma=\{\sigma_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
is a sofic approximation and

2.

\h_{\Sigma}(\mu)=\f(\mu)
.

4.1
Concentration

For
h\in[0,\log|\mathtt{A}|]
and
\sigma\in\Hom(\Gamma,\Sym(n))
, let

\rho_{\mu}(\sigma,h)=\inf\left\{\varepsilon>0\,:\,\frac{1}{n}\log\left\lvert\Omega(\sigma,\mu,\varepsilon)\right\rvert\geq h\right\}.

Note that
\rho_{\mu}(\sigma,h)\leq\diam(\mathtt{A}^{n})
. For fixed
h
and
\mu
, this quantity exhibits exponential concentration as a function of a random homomorphism
\sigma
:

Lemma 4.2
.

If
\sigma_{n}\sim\Unif(\Hom(\Gamma,\Sym(n)))
then

\mathbb{P}\left\{\left\lvert\rho_{\mu}(\sigma_{n},h)-\EE[\rho_{\mu}(\sigma_{n},h)]\right\rvert>t\right\}\leq 2\exp\left(\frac{-nt^{2}}{8r}\right).

Proof.

This is a consequence of a standard kind of martingale concentration argument using “switchings”; a version for the permutation model appears in
[
Shr21
]
. If two homomorphisms
\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2}
differ by a “switching” then for any microstate, its empirical distributions over
\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2}
are distance at most
2/n
apart. So the minimum distance from
\mu
to get any specified number of microstates changes by at most
2/n
. Hence

\mathbb{P}\left\{\left\lvert\rho_{\mu}(\sigma_{n},h)-\EE[\rho_{\mu}(\sigma_{n},h)]\right\rvert>t\right\}\leq 2\exp\left(\frac{-t^{2}}{2nr(2/n)^{2}}\right).\qed

It is not immediately clear whether
\EE[\rho_{\mu}(\sigma_{n},h)]
converges as
n\to\infty
, but we can define the upper limit

\overline{\rho}_{\mu}(h)=\limsup_{n\to\infty}\EE[\rho_{\mu}(\sigma_{n},h)].

This is a nondecreasing function of
h
.

Lemma 4.3
.

If the product self-joining
\mu\times\mu
has maximal
\f
invariant, then
\overline{\rho}_{\mu}(h)=0
for every
h<\f(\mu)
.

Proof.

By the Paley-Zygmund inequality, for any
\theta\in(0,1)

\mathbb{P}\left\{\left\lvert\Omega(\sigma_{n},\mu,\varepsilon)\right\rvert\geq\theta\EE\left\lvert\Omega(\sigma_{n},\mu,\varepsilon)\right\rvert\right\}\geq(1-\theta)^{2}\frac{\big(\EE\left\lvert\Omega(\sigma_{n},\mu,\varepsilon)\right\rvert\big)^{2}}{\EE\!\big[\left\lvert\Omega(\sigma_{n},\mu,\varepsilon)\right\rvert^{2}\big]}.

Now by a standard compactness argument

\inf_{\varepsilon>0}\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log\EE\!\big[\left\lvert\Omega(\sigma_{n},\mu,\varepsilon)\right\rvert^{2}\big]=\sup\{\f(\lambda)\,:\,\lambda\text{ self-joining of $\mu$}\},

which by assumption is equal to
\f(\mu\times\mu)=2\f(\mu)
. So for any
\delta>0
, if
\varepsilon>0
is small enough then for all large enough
n

\EE\!\big[\left\lvert\Omega(\sigma_{n},\mu,\varepsilon)\right\rvert^{2}\big]\leq\exp\big(n(2\f(\mu)+\delta)\big).

It is a consequence of
[
Bow10a
]
that

\f(\mu)=\inf_{\varepsilon>0}\liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log\EE\left\lvert\Omega(\sigma_{n},\mu,\varepsilon)\right\rvert;

whether a limsup or liminf is used, the method of
[
Bow10a
]
shows that this coincides with the earlier information-theoretic formula for
\f(\mu)
in
[
Bow10b
]
. In particular, for any
\delta>0
, for all
\varepsilon>0
we have

\EE\left\lvert\Omega(\sigma_{n},\mu,\varepsilon)\right\rvert\geq\exp\big(n(\f(\mu)-\delta)\big)

for all large enough
n
.

So given
\delta>0
, if
\varepsilon
is small enough then for all large
n

\mathbb{P}\left\{\left\lvert\Omega(\sigma_{n},\mu,\varepsilon)\right\rvert\geq\theta\EE\left\lvert\Omega(\sigma_{n},\mu,\varepsilon)\right\rvert\right\}\geq(1-\theta)^{2}\frac{\big(\exp\big(n(\f(\mu)-\delta)\big)\big)^{2}}{\exp\big(n(2\f(\mu)+\delta)\big)}=(1-\theta)^{2}\exp(-3\delta n).

Now fix some
h<\f(\mu)
. Pick some
\delta>0
such that
\f(\mu)-\delta>h
; then for any
\varepsilon>0
and
\theta\in(0,1)
for all large enough
n
we have

\theta\EE\left\lvert\Omega(\sigma_{n},\mu,\varepsilon)\right\rvert\geq\theta\exp(n(\f(\mu)-\delta))\geq\exp(nh).

So for any
\delta>0
, if
\varepsilon>0
is small enough then for all large enough

\mathbb{P}\{\rho_{\mu}(\sigma_{n},h)\leq\varepsilon\}\geq\mathbb{P}\{\tfrac{1}{n}\log\left\lvert\Omega(\sigma_{n},\mu,\varepsilon)\right\rvert\geq h\}\geq(1-\theta)^{2}\exp(-3\delta n).

Since
\varepsilon,\delta>0
can be as small as we want, this is incompatible with the exponential concentration in Lemma
4.2
unless
\overline{\rho}_{\mu}(h)=0
.
∎

5
Nonequilibrium over every sofic approximation

In this section, energy and pressure functions will refer to an Ising model with interaction strength

In this section we establish that, for low enough temperatures, the free-boundary state on the rank-

\mathbb{F}_{r}

We use a
\Sigma

\Sigma

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
\Sigma
be any sofic approximation to the free group
\mathbb{F}_{r}=\langle s_{1},\ldots,s_{r}\rangle

\mu\in\Prob^{\mathbb{F}_{r}}(\mathtt{A}^{\mathbb{F}_{r}})

\mu_{k}\in\Prob^{\mathbb{Z}}(\mathtt{A}^{\mathbb{Z}})
be the marginal of
\mu
on the copy of
\mathbb{Z}
in
\mathbb{F}_{r}
generated by

\h_{\Sigma}(\mu)\leq\h^{\mathrm{KS}}(\mu_{k}).

Here
\h^{\mathrm{KS}}
refers to the standard Kolmogorov-Sinai entropy rate of a
\mathbb{Z}
-system.

This is very similar to
[

Recall that if
\sigma\colon\Gamma\to\Sym(V)
is a map and
\mathcal{O}\subset\Prob(\mathtt{A}^{\Gamma})
is any set then we defined
\Omega(\sigma,\mathcal{O})=\{\mathbf{x}\in\mathtt{A}^{V}\,:\,P_{\mathbf{x}}^{\sigma}\in\mathcal{O}\}
to be the set of “microstates” (labelings of

by symbols in
\mathtt{A}

\mathcal{O}
(which is typically an open neighborhood of some measure

of interest).

Similarly, if
\zeta\in\Prob(\mathtt{A}^{V})
is the law of a random microstate then we can define the average empirical distribution

P_{\zeta}^{\sigma}=\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathtt{A}^{V}}P_{\mathbf{x}}^{\sigma}\cdot\zeta\{\mathbf{x}\}

and define

to be the set of laws of random microstates whose average empirical distribution is in

. Note that we do

to be supported on microstates whose individual empirical distributions are in

: in some cases below there may be no such microstates.

Definition 6.1
.

Suppose
\Sigma=\{\sigma_{n}\colon\Gamma\to\Sym(V_{n})\}
is a random sofic approximation to
\Gamma
. For each

and open neighborhood
\mathcal{U}\supset\mathcal{X}
, let
\xi_{n}^{\mathcal{U}}=\widetilde{\xi}_{\sigma_{n}}^{\mathcal{U}}\in\Prob(\mathtt{A}^{V_{n}})
denote the restricted finitary Gibbs states for a potential

(with some continuous extension
\tilde{u}\colon\mathtt{A}^{\Gamma}\to\mathbb{R}
if necessary).

We say that
\mu
is the local weak limit over
\Sigma
of the finitary Gibbs states for

, or, more briefly
\lwlim(u,\Sigma)=\mu
, if for every open neighborhood
\mathcal{O}\ni\mu

\limsup_{\mathcal{U}\downarrow\Prob^{\Gamma}(\mathcal{X})}\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{\lvert V_{n}\rvert}\log\mathbb{P}\{\xi_{n}^{\mathcal{U}}\not\in\mathbf{\Omega}(\sigma_{n},\mathcal{O})\}=-\infty.

The
\limsup
over

here is over the directed set of open neighborhoods of
\Prob^{\Gamma}(\mathcal{X})
.

Note that some call this notion “local convergence on average.” We require a superexponentially small probability of the empirical measure not being close to
\mu
since even exponentially small events can affect the average pressure; see the proof of the third part of Lemma

.

Lemma 6.2
.

For any fixed

\lwlim(u,\Sigma)
exists for every deterministic sofic approximation

\lwlim(u,\Sigma)=\bar{\mu}

Proof.

Suppose there are distinct

In the deterministic case, “

So for every open

Definition 6.3

Sofic approximations without this property do exist. The following example was suggested by Lewis Bowen:

Example 6.4

Let
\mu_{1}

Theorem 6.8

Since

\Phi(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{x}

\mu^{+}

\mu^{-}

has barycenter
\bar{\mu}
,

It was also claimed in Theorem
C
that, with zero external field,
\mu^{+}
and
\mu^{-}
are the only
\f
-equilibrium states. This follows from the following lemma, Proposition
1.1
, and the fact that

,

, and

are the only Ising Gibbs states which are completely homogeneous Markov chains
[
Geo11
, Section 12.2]
. A tree-indexed Markov chain is called completely homogeneous if the transition kernel is the same across every edge.

The lemma is stated in slightly further generality than is required for the Ising model. This requires making one more definition: we say that a subshift
\mathcal{X}\subset\mathtt{A}^{\mathbb{F}_{r}}
is a
topological Markov chain
if there is some
\{0,1\}
-valued
\lvert\mathtt{A}\rvert\times\lvert\mathtt{A}\rvert

M
such that
\mathcal{X}
is the set of all
\mathbf{x}\in\mathtt{A}^{\mathbb{F}_{r}}
such that if
\gamma\in\mathbb{F}_{r}
and
i\in[r]
then
M_{\mathbf{x}(\gamma),\mathbf{x}(s_{i}\gamma)}=1
. In other words, there is some set of pairs of symbols in
\mathtt{A}
(encoded by
M
) which are allowed to appear on either end of a directed edge, and
\mathcal{X}
is the set of
all
microstates for which only allowed pairs appear.

Some authors refer to this as a “subshift of finite type.”

Lemma 6.9
.

Let
\mathcal{X}
be a subshift of some
\mathtt{A}^{\mathbb{F}_{r}}
which is a topological Markov chain. Suppose that
\mu\in\Prob^{\mathbb{F}_{r}}(\mathcal{X})
is
\f
-equilibrium for some “nearest-neighbor” energy function
u\colon\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
, i.e. one of the form

u(\mathbf{x})=B(\mathbf{x}(e))+\sum_{i=1}^{r}J(\mathbf{x}(e),\mathbf{x}(s_{i}))

for functions
B\colon\mathtt{A}\to\mathbb{R}
and
J\colon\mathtt{A}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
.

Then
\mu
is a completely homogeneous Markov chain.

Proof.

Let
\mu^{1}
be the Markov approximation to
\mu
. Note that since
\mathcal{X}
is a topological Markov chain,
\mu^{1}
is still supported on
\mathcal{X}
. By
[
Bow10a
, Theorem 11.1]
,
\f(\mu^{1})\geq\f(\mu)
, with equality if and only if
\mu=\mu^{1}
, i.e.
\mu
is a Markov chain. Since
u(\mu)
only depends on the single-site and single-edge marginals,
u(\mu)=u(\mu^{1})
. So
\press_{\f}(\mu^{1})\geq\press_{\f}(\mu)
, with equality if and only if
\mu
is a Markov chain. In particular, if
\mu
is
\f
-equilibrium then it is a Markov chain.

We have assumed that
\mu
is shift-invariant, but we need to explain why it is completely homogeneous: a priori there may be a different Markov transition kernel for each generator
s_{i}
(i.e. each edge type). To see that this is not possible for an equilibrium measure, note that (since
\mu
is Markov) if we let
X_{e},X_{1},\ldots X_{r}
denote random spins at the sites
e,s_{1},\ldots s_{r}\in\mathbb{F}_{r}
distributed according to
\mu
then

\displaystyle\press_{\f}(\mu)

\displaystyle=\f(\mu)-u(\mu)

\displaystyle=\left[(1-2r)\shent(X_{e})+\sum_{i=1}^{r}\shent(X_{e},X_{i})\right]-\EE\left[B(X_{e})+\sum_{i=1}^{r}J(X_{e},X_{r})\right]

\displaystyle=\left[(1-2r)\shent(X_{e})-\EE B(X_{e})\right]+\sum_{i=1}^{r}\left[\shent(X_{e},X_{i})-\EE J(X_{e},X_{i})\right].

So whichever
i\in[r]
corresponds to the largest term in the sum, we should just use that transition kernel for every edge type to maximize the pressure.
∎

Corollary 6.10
.

If
\lwlim(u,\f)=\bar{\mu}
, then
\bar{\mu}
is a mixture of shift-invariant Markov chains which are Gibbs with respect to
u
.

Proof.

The limit
\bar{\mu}
is a mixture of
\f
-equilibrium states by Theorem

. These must be Markov chains by the previous lemma. They must be Gibbs since

is an invariant Gibbs measure, and hence cannot be a mixture of non-Gibbs invariant measures
[
Geo11
, Theorem 14.15(c)]
(note this theorem is stated for actions of

but the same proof works for any countable group).
∎

Note that the assumption of the previous result holds in particular if the local limit exists over every deterministic sofic approximation, by Lemma

[

]
that it is possible to identify the limit of Ising models on locally tree-like graphs, not just prove that it exists: as long as the limit does exist (and is independent of the sofic approximation), we have shown here that it must be something simple.
