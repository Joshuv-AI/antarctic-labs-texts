The Challenge of Using LLMs to Simulate Human Behavior: A Causal Inference Perspective
George Gui, Olivier Toubia

Abstract
Large Language Models (LLMs) have demonstrated impressive potential to simulate human behavior. Using a causal inference framework, we empirically and theoretically analyze the challenges of conducting LLM-simulated experiments, and explore potential solutions. In the context of demand estimation, we show that variations in the treatment included in the prompt (e.g., price of focal product) can cause variations in unspecified confounding factors (e.g., price of competitors, historical prices, outside temperature), introducing endogeneity and yielding implausibly flat demand curves. We propose a theoretical framework suggesting this endogeneity issue generalizes to other contexts and won’t be fully resolved by merely improving the training data. Unlike real experiments where researchers assign pre-existing units across conditions, LLMs simulate units based on the entire prompt, which includes the description of the treatment. Therefore, due to associations in the training data, the characteristics of individuals and environments simulated by the LLM can be affected by the treatment assignment. We explore two potential solutions. The first specifies all contextual variables that affect both treatment and outcome, which we demonstrate to be challenging for a general-purpose LLM. The second explicitly specifies the source of treatment variation in the prompt given to the LLM (e.g., by informing the LLM that the store is running an experiment). While this approach only allows the estimation of a conditional average treatment effect that depends on the specific experimental design, it provides valuable directional results for exploratory analysis.

Introduction

The rise of large language models (LLMs) like GPT has sparked interest across disciplines (including marketing, computer science, economics, psychology and political science) in leveraging these tools to simulate how humans would respond to questions or stimuli in different contexts
(
Aher et al., 2023
;
Brand et al., 2023
;
Dillion et al., 2023
;
Goli and Singh, 2023
;
Horton, 2023
;
Park et al., 2023
)
. If these LLM-simulated experiments accurately approximate real human behavior, the implications for both researchers and firms are substantial. Researchers could use LLMs for pilot experiments to pinpoint stimuli with significant impact, thus improving the efficiency of theory development and experimental design. Firms interested in understanding consumer demand could leverage these realistic simulations to explore different ideas and strategies, thereby improving product development and optimizing marketing mix variables.

However, a key question is whether we can interpret the patterns simulated by an LLM-simulated experiment as causal and how to better design these LLM-simulated experiments to answer causal questions. For example, if we ask LLM-simulated participants the same question framed in different ways and get different answers, when can we interpret this difference as being caused by framing? If LLM-simulated customers are more likely to make a purchase when presented with lower prices, when can we attribute this change to price sensitivity? If we find LLM-simulated individuals keep making the same choice over and over again in different contexts, how can we interpret the causes for this persistence? While the success of using LLMs to answer these questions depends on the quality of the training data and the accuracy of the information, it also hinges on whether the simulated results are indeed driven by the
variation in the treatment of interest
, and not by other confounding factors.

To set the stage, in Section
2
we first review how social science researchers conduct between-subject experiments to study causal questions and how LLMs can be used to mimic or simulate these experiments. We then demonstrate that the classic challenge of distinguishing correlation from causation applies to LLM experiments. Although the variations in the answers are causally driven by
variations in the LLM prompts
, these variations in prompts do not necessarily correspond to the variations the experimenter intends to induce. To illustrate this distinction, we consider an experimenter interested in studying the demand for a focal product at different prices. The experimenter can test different LLM prompts that vary only on the price of the product, in order to simulate purchase decisions. Although purchase decisions are causally driven by
variations in prompts
, we document that they are not necessarily driven exclusively by variations in the
price
of the product. This is because variations in the price listed in the prompt might cause variations in other unspecified confounding factors (e.g., competing prices) that the experimenter intended to keep constant. Therefore, the experiment can be invalid in the sense that the cause-and-effect relationship between the treatment of interest (e.g., price) and the outcome (e.g., sales) can be explained by other unspecified factors.

Sections
3
and
4
then respectively explore two approaches for addressing this challenge. One approach consists in adding more detail to control for confounding factors. While this approach is appealing when all confounders are accounted for, we document that it is particularly challenging to achieve in an LLM simulation, because simulated individuals and environments do not pre-exist. Rather, they are “constructed on the fly” (figuratively) by the LLM based on the entire prompt, which includes the treatment. Therefore, the characteristics of individuals and environments simulated by the LLM can be affected by the treatment assignment. We find that blindly specifying too much detail may also sometimes backfire, especially in the absence of domain knowledge. Another approach consists in specifying to the LLM that the source of variation is experimental (e.g., instruct the LLM that the price of the product is set randomly by the store). Although this approach can only estimate a conditional average treatment effect that depends on the experimental design (e.g., the range of the distribution from which price is randomly drawn), it can still be informative when the experimental design is plausible, and researchers can test the robustness of the effect by testing different experimental designs.

Finally, in Section
5
we provide a stylized theoretical framework to explore whether future improvements in training data and LLMs might resolve the challenges that we identify, and shed light on potential solutions. Section
6
discusses broader implications and contexts, and Section
7
concludes.

2
Motivating Example

Between-subject experiments are conducted by assigning different groups of participants to different treatments and then comparing the outcomes across these groups to study the treatment effects. For example, if researchers are interested in studying how consumers respond to price changes, they can randomly assign consumers to different prices and compare purchase decisions. A key feature of between-subject experiments is that they are not subject to carryover effects, where exposure to one treatment affects the response to a subsequent treatment. Section
6
extends our discussion to within-subject experiments.

One possible way to mimic between-subject experiments is to conduct “between-session” experiments using LLMs. In this approach, different sessions, each involving a unique interaction with an LLM, stand in for the “subjects” used in a traditional between-subjects design. For example, to mimic the aforementioned between-subject price experiment, researchers can randomly assign the price presented to LLM-simulated customers in different sessions and compare their purchase decisions by asking those customers if they are willing to purchase a product at a given price
(
Brand et al., 2023
, e.g.,)
.

However, while the definition of the treatment and its source of variation are often clear in a traditional between-subject experiment, the treatment can be ambiguous in a simulated “between-session” LLM experiment. For example, suppose in one LLM session we ask if a customer is willing to purchase a 12-pack of Coca-Cola for a price of $4, and in another LLM session we ask if another customer is willing to purchase a 12-pack of Coca-Cola for a price of $6. What causes the price variation? One possibility, as intended by researchers, is that the store is conducting an experiment that randomizes the price holding everything else constant, which is an ideal setting to study price sensitivity. Another possibility, however, is that the price is higher in one scenario because the customer has higher income, is less price sensitive, or is shopping in a store in which all products tend to be more expensive.

To illustrate this ambiguity, we first demonstrate an example of demand simulation using this approach for a 12-can package of regular Coca-Cola, one of the top-selling consumer packaged goods. We use the Open AI API to ensure that the LLM (GPT 4 in our case) can give answers in the desired format, and we “pin” the model to gpt4-0613, to make sure that the exact same model is used for all our simulations. In particular, on the API we have the ability to manipulate not just the input from the “User,” but also the “System” which gives meta-instructions to GPT. (In ChatGPT, by default “System” is set to “You are a helpful assistant,” and all input is attributed to “User.”) Prompt

These findings are not surprising in hindsight: in real observational data, the price of the focal product should be informative of the price of the competing products, especially if we do not know anything about the context, the type of store, and how the price for a product is set. An LLM trained on such data would also naturally make such association. This association can be realistic and useful if researchers intend to use LLMs to simulate competing prices based on the focal product price. However, this same association is problematic when researchers intend to estimate the impact of changing the focal price, while holding everything else constant. Because the competing price is not constant in this simulation, and the competing price may affect the purchase probability, the researchers cannot identify the causal effect of interest, a challenge well known as confounding or endogeneity. There are several ways to address this endogeneity problem when working with real-world data. One observational approach is to control for additional covariates. In LLM, an analog of this approach would be adding more details to the prompt. Another approach is to leverage an experimental sample that randomizes the treatment of interest. In LLM, an analog of this approach is to explicitly specify that the price is set randomly.
The following two sections explore the benefits and challenges associated with each of these two approaches.

3
Potential Solution 1: Adding More Detail in the Prompt

When working with real-world data, a common approach researchers use for causal identification is to control for covariates. If researchers have controlled for all confounding covariates that affect both the treatment and outcome, the conditional independence assumption, or
ignorability
, is satisfied. If sufficient variation remains after controlling for covariates, then researchers can identify the causal effect of interest. In LLM-simulated experiments, adding detailed information about individuals and their environment is analogous to controlling for covariates. These covariates can range from demographic information, location, preferences, budget, etc. In demand estimation, these covariates can also include the price of competing products in the category, which can be used as a proxy for the type of stores that the customer is visiting and the competitive environment that the focal product is facing. Given the strong correlation between focal and competing prices documented in Figure
3(b)
, this section uses competing price as an example to discuss the benefit and challenges of this approach of controlling for covariates. Supplementary Appendix
C
discusses running the experiment on a “silicon sample” of personas, following
Argyle et al., 2023

5
sheds light on insights that can be generalized to other covariates and contexts.

However, a key challenge of using this method is determining whether all confounders have been accounted for. For example, Figure
2
and
3(b)
illustrate that, in hindsight, changing the price of a focal product may inadvertently affect the unspecified competing prices. But what about other unspecified potential confounders? Should researchers also specify other factors such as customer income, exact location, weather, exact date, hour of the day, or even second of the day? Where should researchers draw the line? While this is a common issue when working with real-world observational data, this challenge is amplified with LLM simulated experiments due to the fluid nature of the LLM-simulated individuals. Real-world experiments are based on pre-existing individuals. Even if the datasets are limited such that researchers do not directly observe all the characteristics of an individual (such as income, preference, and location), these characteristics objectively exist, and most likely won’t be suddenly changed by an experimental manipulation. In comparison, an LLM-simulated individual does not pre-exist. The characteristics of this simulated individual also do not pre-exist. Unless these characteristics are explicitly specified in the LLM-simulation, they are subject to change due to associations with the treatment variable that are unobserved to the researcher.

To illustrate the scope of this issue, we extend our analysis to also ask about past prices of the focal product, prices in neighboring stores, and outside temperature, while manipulating the price of Coca-Cola. See Prompt
A
in Supplementary Appendix
A
for the detailed prompt used this analysis. Figure
5
demonstrates that, although a researcher may be only interested in manipulating the current Coca-Cola price, the manipulation inadvertently affects related variables such as past Coca-Cola prices, Coca-Cola prices at nearby stores, and even the outside temperature. Again, in hindsight, this finding is not surprising, because the current Coca-Cola price may indeed be correlated with these other variables in the observational data used to train the LLM. However, this pattern illustrates the challenge in deciding the set of covariates to control for: any unspecified variable, even if it is a pretreatment variable (e.g., past price) that is supposed to stay constant, or a variable (e.g., weather) that by nature should not be affected by the experimental manipulation, can be inadvertently affected in an LLM-simulation.

One tempting method to address the concern of inadequate control is to specify as much detail as possible in an LLM simulation. This approach is analogous to the kitchen-sink method used for real-world data analysis, in which researchers try to control for all observed covariates to address the confounding issue. However, this covariate-selection approach does not guarantee that including more covariates will always improve the results
(
Bhattacharya and Vogt, 2007

Potential Solution 2: Communicating the Experimental Design to the LLM

Another common approach to address endogeneity in real-world data is to focus on a subset of observations with experimental variation. This experimental variation ensures that the treatment is randomly assigned such that there are no unobserved confounding factors. An analogue of this method in LLM simulation is to explicitly state that the variation in the treatment is generated under a specific experimental design, in the hope that the variation in the treatment will not change other unspecified factors that are supposed to stay constant. Prompt
4
provides an example of such experimental design, in which we explicitly state that the price is drawn randomly from
-60\%
to
60\%
relative to the baseline price.

Second, here the researcher reveals to LLM that an experiment is being conducted, which differs from standard experimental settings where the participant are usually unaware that they are in an experiment. 5
Theoretical Framework

So far, we have empirically illustrated some challenges related to the use of LLMs in simulating consumer purchase behavior using a specific LLM (GPT4, pinned on 06/13/23). Are the challenges documented here likely to generalize to other types of human behavior, and hold with other types of LLMs? Under what conditions should the potential solutions we have explored be able to solve the problems we have identified? Will these problems cease to exist as LLMs become more and more powerful, trained on ever-growing datasets? To answer these questions, it is essential to develop a systematic and theoretical understanding of the challenges discussed in this paper. To that end, in this section we formalize our experimental exercise, by explicitly couching our discussion within a causal inference framework.

In order to understand which limitations are unlikely to be addressed by future LLMs with better training data, for the sake of our theoretical exercise we consider a hypothetical situation in which the training data is accurate and unlimited, in the sense that it contains large samples of accurate data that cover any possible scenario and are sufficient to answer any question. In this limit case, rather than generating a synthetic response, the LLM could potentially correctly answer any question by looking up all observations in the training data that match the scenario described in a prompt. Therefore, the challenge lies in whether the prompt will be able to instruct, directly or indirectly, the LLM to look up the right subsample to answer a question.

\displaystyle ATE\equiv E[Y_{i}(D_{H})]-E[Y_{i}(D_{L})]

(1)

To arrive at this quantity, the retailer may ask the LLM to simulate customer purchase decisions as in Prompt
2
and then summarize the purchase decisions at different prices.

Prompt 7:

You, AI, are a customer interested in {FocalProduct}. The {FocalProduct} is priced at $d, are you willing to purchase it?

This approach seems appealing because it resembles how researchers usually conduct between-subject surveys or lab studies, in which different participants are exposed to different stimuli. One important benefit of the between-subject design is that participants are less likely to learn what researchers are really interested in. However, depending on how the LLM processes the prompt, it may or may not answer the question the researcher has in mind. For example, an unintended interpretation of Prompt
5
could be that the retailer is interested in the best prediction of customer purchase when the product
happens to be priced
at
d
. This prediction may be generated by focusing on all subsets of observations that satisfy
D_{i}=d
to calculate
E[Y_{i}|D_{i}=d]
. The difference of these expectations at the two different prices can be denoted as:

\displaystyle\widehat{ATE^{O}}\equiv E[Y_{i}|D_{i}=D_{H}]-E[Y_{i}|D_{i}=D_{L}]

(2)

This difference captures the
realized outcomes
for units that are actually assigned to
D_{H}
vs.
D_{L}
, which is different from
ATE
that refers to the difference in
potential outcomes
. The distribution of the realized outcomes and the potential outcomes are different when the assignment of
D_{i}
is not exogenous, such that:

\displaystyle(Y_{i}(D_{H}),Y_{i}(D_{L}))\not\perp D_{i},

(3)

i.e., the realized treatment is not orthogonal to the potential treatment effect in the training data.

For example, suppose that in the training data, retailers have stores that are either low type or high type. When
R_{i}=0
, for the low type stores, not only the price of the focal product is low, but the prices of competing products are also low. Thus,
D_{i}=D_{L}
is often associated with
X_{i}=X_{L}
, and
D_{i}=D_{H}
is often associated with
X_{i}=X_{H}
. In this scenario, this difference
\widehat{ATE^{O}}
fails to capture the average treatment effect of changing price from
D_{L}
to
D_{H}
. Instead, it may capture the difference in store types.

Following the standard causal inference framework, one solution discussed in Section
3
is to control for more covariates so that a conditional independence assumption can be satisfied. With domain expertise, researchers may suspect that store type is a relevant latent covariate in the training data. To avoid endogeneity bias, researchers may incorporate the competing price into the prompt, with the implicit assumption that the competing price is a good proxy for store type and the competitive environment.

Prompt 8:

You, AI, are a customer interested in {FocalProduct}. The {FocalProduct} is priced at $D and the competing product is priced at $X, are you willing to purchase the {FocalProduct}?

And then, for each value of
X
, one can calculate the conditional difference:

\displaystyle\widehat{CATE}(X)\equiv E[Y_{i}|D_{i}=D_{H},X_{i}=X]-E[Y_{i}|D_{i}=D_{L},X_{i}=X]

(4)

If the standard conditional independence assumption is satisfied such that:

\displaystyle(Y_{i}(D_{H}),Y_{i}(D_{L}))\perp D_{i}|X_{i}

(5)

then
\widehat{CATE}(X)
can accurately identify the CATE conditional on X. The ATE can be identified by taking the expectation of this
\widehat{CATE}(X)
over the distribution of
X
:

\displaystyle\widehat{ATE}^{Covariate}\equiv E[\widehat{CATE}(X)]

(6)

However, if this conditional independence assumption is violated such that:

\displaystyle(Y_{i}(D_{H}),Y_{i}(D_{L}))\not\perp D_{i}|X_{i}

(7)

then
\widehat{ATE}^{Covariate}
may not correctly identify the object of interest. For a general-purpose LLM simulator that is designed to answer a wide range of causal questions, this conditional independence assumption may be difficult to satisfy for all questions of interest. In particular, the researcher needs to consider whether the prompt, including the focal treatment and the added detail, will direct the LLM to the appropriate subset of the training data in order to answer the question of interest.

For example, suppose that there is another variable
S
that captures store type. When asking Prompt
5
with the focal price equal to
D_{L}
vs.
D_{H}
, the researcher may be trying to answer one of at least two potential research questions:

1.

For a subset of stores that
typically set
the price of the competing product to be
X
, what is the effect if we
change
the price of the focal product from
D_{L}
to
D_{H}
?

2.

For an average store, what is the effect if we
change
the price of the focal product from
D_{L}
to
D_{H}
while
setting
the price of the competing product to be
X
?

These two questions imply different relevant distributions of
S
, i.e., the researcher would implicitly like the LLM to focus on different subsets of stores in the two cases. When asking Prompt
5
with
D_{L}
vs.
D_{H}
, there is a risk that the distribution of
S
might be different across the two prompts. If
S
varies systematically with
D
conditional on
X
and if
S
impacts
Y
, then the conditional independence assumption would be violated.

Even if the distribution of
S
happens to be the same across the two prompts and if the conditional independence assumption is satisfied, there is a risk that this distribution might be different from the one assumed by the researcher, in which case the researcher would be measuring a conditional average treatment effect different from the one they believe they are measuring. In other words, if the answers to the two research questions above are different, then even when the training dataset contains the correct information to answer both of these questions, no LLM can answer these two questions correctly at the same time. If Prompt
5
is “interpreted” by an LLM to answer the first question correctly, then when researchers are interested in the second question and use the same prompt, they will get an incorrect answer, and vice versa. Therefore, by adding more detail, a general LLM may happen to answer some of the causal questions as intended by the researcher, but it can be challenging to answer other questions as intended.

One obvious solution would be to be more explicit in the prompt, e.g., include additional information about the type of store, to guide the LLM to focus on relevant subsets of the training data, or to be more clear about how the additional detail, e.g., “you are in a store that
typically sets the price of the competing product at $X
”. However, as we illustrated in Section
3
, it is challenging to include
all
relevant controls in the prompt, and the effect of price on demand could still be confounded with unexpected factors such as outside temperature. Moreover, Figure
6
showed how additional detail may trigger further associations, which may lead to unrealistic outcomes such as upward-sloping demand curves for products like Coca-Cola.

The second approach we consider is being more explicit about the treatment of interest or be more precise about the reason behind a variation. For example, the researchers may ask:

Prompt 9:

The store is running an experiment that randomizes the price of the focal product between
D_{L}
and
D_{H}
. You, AI, are a customer interested in {FocalProduct}. The {FocalProduct} is priced at $D, are you willing to purchase the {FocalProduct}?

In this prompt, the researcher reveals the reason behind the variation: the price is
D_{H}
for some customers and
D_{L}
for other customers because the store is running an experiment. This statement suggests that the LLM should not use the subsample where
R_{i}=0
, because this sample does not have experimental variation. This statement also suggests that the LLM should not use the subsample where
R_{i}=2
, because it does not match the exact experiment description. Therefore, the LLM may be able to simulate the purchase results by focusing on the subsample that matches the description of the prompt:
R_{i}=1
, and calculate the purchase probability under different prices given this probability:
E[Y_{i}|D_{i}=d,R_{i}=1]
. Due to randomization,

\displaystyle(Y_{i}(D_{H}),Y_{i}(D_{L}))\perp D_{i}|R_{i}=1

(8)

and the researcher can estimate:

\displaystyle\widehat{CATE}(R_{i}=1)\equiv E[Y_{i}|D_{i}=1,R_{i}=1]-E[Y_{i}|D_{i}=0,R_{i}=1]

(9)

Under the independence assumption,
CATE^{E}(R_{i}=1)
can correctly estimate the conditional average treatment effect for
R_{i}=1
.

\displaystyle CATE(R_{i}=1)\equiv E[Y_{i}(1)|R_{i}=1]-E[Y_{i}(0)|R_{i}=1]

(10)

If the customers for whom
R_{i}=1
in the training data are randomly selected in a way that is orthogonal to the treatment effect, such that:

\displaystyle(Y_{i}(D_{H}),Y_{i}(D_{L}))\perp R_{i}

(11)

then this
\widehat{CATE}(R_{i}=1)
can correctly identify the
ATE
.

However, note that this method is not without caveats. First, if there are latent variables that correlate both with
R
and with the treatment effect, then we are only identifying a conditional average treatment effect for a population that is subject to this kind of experiment. It also means that when the experiment design is different, the result may also change, because different types of stores may be interested in running different kinds of experiments. As documented in Figure
9
, results can be sensitive to the experimental design. For example,
S
, which captures the type of store, may be correlated with
R
and impact
Y
.
That is, the independence assumption may not be satisfied, if for example only certain types of stores that cater to certain types of customers would run the experiment described in the prompt:

\displaystyle(Y_{i}(D_{H}),Y_{i}(D_{L}))\not\perp R_{i}

(12)

In that case,
CATE(R_{i}=1)\neq ATE
. However, similar to many surveys or lab studies that focus on a particular set of participants, this CATE may still be useful in informing researchers about the direction of the effect and can be used as a pilot study.

Note that the covariate approach of adding more detail and the experimental approach of explain the reason behind the variation are not mutually exclusive. The researchers can include more detail as well as explaining the reasons for variation at the same time, which can identify the ATE (after integrating over
X
) under the less restrictive assumption that the selection of units is random after conditioning on the controlled covariates:

\displaystyle(Y_{i}(D_{H}),Y_{i}(D_{L}))\perp R_{i}|X_{i}=x

(13)

Therefore, researchers can consider adding more detail as well as specifying the source of variation to improve LLM simulations.

6
Discussion

Our theoretical framework suggests that the challenges identified in this paper can extend from between-subject experiments to within-subject experiments. Suppose that instead of conducting a between-subject experiment across multiple LLM sessions, a researcher conducts a “within-subject” experiment within a single session. In this session, the researcher first asks the LLM-simulated customer their purchase probability under a high price. After getting their responses, the researcher then asks the customer’s purchase probability under a low price. Prompts
D
in Supplementary Appendix
D
gives an example of such within-subject experiment. Such within-subject design is conceptually similar to the approach of specifying the source of variation in Section
4
. In both cases, the experimental design is revealed, and in both cases the result is sensitive to the experimental design. For example, the order of treatment may affect the results. When the customer is first presented with high price, the LLM may make implicit assumptions about the unspecified competing prices, past prices, and neighboring prices, which in turn affect their first purchase decision. This first purchase decision, combined with the follow-up prices presented to the customer, will be used to make further implicit assumptions that in turn affect the subsequent purchase decisions.

The challenges identified in this paper are also relevant to the study of other causal problems. For example, when studying the effect of language styles, one possible way to conduct the experiment is to express a question in different ways across prompts and then compare the answers. However, the LLM may “infer” additional information based on the language style, e.g., whether the language is formal or concise may influence the context or identity of the user who asked the question.
Similarly, if an LLM-simulated customer is observed making the same purchase over and over again, a natural question is whether this implies that past purchases causally induce future purchases, or whether the repeated purchase pattern is driven by a strong preference for the product which affects both the current and future purchases.

We also note that there are situations in which the ability of LLMs to capture latent associations might be desirable to the researcher. For example, a researcher might be interested in measuring the total effect of a price discount, where the total effect is defined as the sum of the marginal utility from saving money plus a signaling effect which operates through a change in consumer beliefs about factors such as contemporaneous and future actions by the focal firms and its competitors, or other factors capturing attributes of the product. For example, offering a discount might create a perception of lower quality, which might hurt sales. Because these factors might be relevant to predicting actual sales, the researcher might value the LLM’s ability to capture such associations. However, based on our findings, a challenge with this approach is ensuring that the set of latent factors captured by the LLM are exactly those relevant to the researcher. For example, if the LLM “assumes” that outside temperature is higher when Coca-Cola offers a discount, this would limit the usefulness of the experiment if the researcher is interested in the total impact of running a discount on a given day/context.

7
Conclusion

This paper evaluates the challenge of using large language models (LLMs) to simulate human behavior. This challenge arises because LLM-simulated individuals and environments do not pre-exist, and any of their characteristics can be influenced by the treatment assignment. The LLM’s ability to produce responses that capture associations present in the training data, while generally desirable, also implies that changing a variable in the prompt can unintentionally affect other unspecified variables that are supposed to stay constant. This makes it difficult to interpret whether the simulated cause-and-effect relationship is indeed driven by the treatment of interest.

We then explore two methods to address this challenge: adding detailed context to control for potential confounders and specifying the experimental design. Both approaches are useful but have limitations. The former is appealing when all confounders can be accounted for. However, our results show that this can be challenging in an LLM simulation. The other approach of specifying that the source of variation is driven by experiments can only estimate a conditional average treatment effect that depends on the experimental design. Nevertheless, it can still be informative when the experimental design is plausible, and researchers can examine the robustness of the effect by testing different experimental designs. Finally, we provide a theoretical framework that demonstrates that the challenges documented in this paper are unlikely to be fully resolved by merely improving the training data. The framework also sheds light on potential solutions, and we hope it will encourage the development of future methods that more effectively account for confounding in order to improve LLM simulation of human behavior.
