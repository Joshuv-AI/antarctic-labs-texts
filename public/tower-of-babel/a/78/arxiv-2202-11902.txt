A PTAS for Packing Hypercubes into a Knapsack
Klaus Jansen, Arindam Khan, Marvin Lira, K. V. N. Sreenivas

Abstract
We study the d d -dimensional hypercube knapsack problem ( d d -D Hc-Knapsack ) where we are given a set of d d -dimensional hypercubes with associated profits, and a knapsack which is a unit d d -dimensional hypercube. The goal is to find an axis-aligned non-overlapping packing of a subset of hypercubes such that the profit of the packed hypercubes is maximized. For this problem, Harren (ICALP’06) gave an algorithm with an approximation ratio of ( 1 + 1 / 2 d + ε ) (1+1/2^{d}+\varepsilon) . For d = 2 d=2 , Jansen and Solis-Oba (IPCO’08) showed that the problem admits a polynomial-time approximation scheme (PTAS); Heydrich and Wiese (SODA’17) further improved the running time and gave an efficient polynomial-time approximation scheme (EPTAS). Both the results use structural properties of 2-D packing, which do not generalize to higher dimensions. For d > 2 d>2 , it remains open to obtain a PTAS, and in fact, there has been no improvement since Harren’s result. We settle the problem by providing a PTAS. Our main technical contribution is a structural lemma which shows that any packing of hypercubes can be converted into another structured packing such that a high profitable subset of hypercubes is packed into a constant number of special hypercuboids, called 𝒱 \mathcal{V} -Boxes and 𝒩 \mathcal{N} -Boxes. As a side result, we give an almost optimal algorithm for a variant of the strip packing problem in higher dimensions. This might have applications for other multidimensional geometric packing problems.

Introduction

Multidimensional geometric packing problems are well-studied natural generalizations of the classical knapsack and bin packing problems.
In the
d
-dimensional geometric knapsack problem (
d
-D Gen-Knapsack
),
where
d
is a fixed constant parameter,
we are given a set of
n
items
\mathcal{I}:=\{1,2,\dots,n\}
.
Each item
i\in[n]
is a
d
-dimensional (
d
-D) hypercuboid with side length
s_{k}(i)\in(0,1]
along the
k^{\mathrm{th}}
dimension and profit
p(i)\in\mathbb{Q}_{>0}
.
The goal is to pack a subset of hypercuboids into a
d
-D unit knapsack (i.e.,
[0,1]^{d}
) such that the profit is maximized.
We need the packing of the hypercuboids to be axis-aligned and non-overlapping.
In this paper, we study
d
-dimensional hypercube knapsack (
d
-D Hc-Knapsack
), a special case of
d
-D Gen-Knapsack
, where all the items
are hypercubes, i.e., for all
i\in[n]
, the
i^{\mathrm{th}}
hypercuboid is a hypercube of side length
s(i)
.

d
-D Gen-Knapsack
generalizes the classical (1-D) knapsack problem
[
34
]
and thus is NP-hard.
It finds numerous applications in scheduling, ad placement, and cutting stock
[
18
]
.
For
2
-D Gen-Knapsack
, Jansen and Zhang
[
26
]
gave a
(2+\varepsilon)
-approximation algorithm.
Gálvez et al.
[
18
]
gave a 1.89-approximation and
(3/2+\varepsilon)
-approximation for the cardinality version (i.e., all items have the same profit).
Adamaszek and Wiese
[
3
]
gave a QPTAS when the input size is quasi-polynomially bounded. Gálvez et al.
[
19
]
later gave a pseudo-polynomial time
(4/3+\varepsilon)
-approximation.
For
3
-D Gen-Knapsack
, the present best approximation ratio is
7+\varepsilon

[
14
]
.
For
d\geq 4
, Sharma
[
40
]
has given a
(1+\varepsilon)3^{d}
-approximation algorithm.
Interestingly for
d\geq 2
, unlike
d
-D Gen-Knapsack
,
d
-D Hc-Knapsack
is not a generalization of 1-D knapsack.
Leung et al.
[
35
]
showed
2
-D Hc-Knapsack
is strongly NP-hard, using a reduction from the 3-partition problem.
The NP-hardness status of
d
-D Hc-Knapsack
for
d>2
, was open for a long time.
Recently, Lu et al.
[
36
,
37
]
settled the status by
showing that
d
-D Hc-Knapsack
is also NP-hard for
d>2
.

A related problem is
d
-D Hc-Binpacking
where we are given
d
-D hypercubes
and the goal is to pack them into the minimum number of unit hypercubes. Back in 2006, Bansal et al.
[
5
]
gave an APTAS for this problem.
Their algorithm starts by classifying the input set into small, medium, and large items.
They first pack the large items (
O(1)
in number) near-optimally by brute force and then pack the small items using Next Fit Decreasing Height (NFDH)
[
11
]
in the gaps left in between the large items.
The remaining unpacked (small and medium) items are packed into additional bins using NFDH.
However, this constructive approach can not be used to devise a PTAS for
d
-D Hc-Knapsack
.
Specifically, after packing the large items, the total space left to pack
the small items can be very small and this space can be fragmented among several voids in between the large items.
This renders packing the small items difficult, especially if the small items occupy a small volume in the optimal solution and carry a lot of profit. This turns out to be a bottleneck case. Otherwise, if the volume of small items
in an optimal solution is significant, or if their profit is not significant, or if
the empty space in the optimal solution is significant, we can easily devise a PTAS. In fact, the algorithm in
[
5
]
can be adapted to devise PTASes for the special cases of
d
-D Hc-Knapsack
: (i) cardinality case, and (ii) when each item has the profit:volume ratio in the range
[1,r]
for a fixed constant
r
.
However, the case of arbitrary profits remains very difficult to handle.

For
d
-D Hc-Knapsack
, Harren
[
21
]
gave a
(1+{1}/{2^{d}}+\varepsilon)
-approximation algorithm, by removing a least profitable large item and using the empty space to pack the small items. Interestingly, the approximation ratio gets smaller as
d
grows. He also studied
d
-D Hc-StripPacking
, where, given a set of hypercubes and a strip with a
(d-1)
-dimensional base and unbounded height, the goal is to pack all the hypercubes while minimizing the height. He gave an APTAS for the special case of
d
-D Hc-StripPacking
when the ratio between the shortest and longest sides of the base is bounded by a constant.

Jansen and Solis-Oba
[
25
]
gave a PTAS for
2
-D Hc-Knapsack
, by overcoming the above-mentioned bottleneck.

They consider an optimal packing and categorize the packed items into
small, medium,
and
large
items.
Then, by extending the edges of the large items,
the remaining space is divided into
O(1)
number of rectilinear regions,
where each region is classified as either
large
or
elongated
or
small
,
based on the largest item intersecting or completely contained in it.
Then, to make sure that a region has no items partially intersecting it,
they repack all the items as follows.
The
large
region is rectangular and its dimensions are much larger
compared to the largest item intersecting it. Here NFDH can repack a high profitable subset of the items.
An
elongated
region is again rectangular and it has one dimension much longer than the other. Here
an algorithm for strip packing
[
27
]
is used to repack the items.
Finally, the
small
regions are handled by applying the above transformations recursively as they can have complicated shapes (they may not be rectangular).
Recently, Heydrich and Wiese
[
23
]
gave an EPTAS, effectively achieving the best-possible approximation.
However, a PTAS for
d
-D Hc-Knapsack
for
d>2
remains elusive as it is difficult to find such transformations for higher dimensions.
Even for 3-D, the best-known approximation ratio remains 9/8
[
21
]
. In 2-D, many structural theorems
[
4
,
18
]
show that a near-optimal solution exists where all items are packed into
O(1)
-number of rectangular regions where items are either packed as a stack or packed using NFDH. But these results do not generalize to higher dimensions.
E.g., while extending the approach of
large
and
elongated
blocks of
[
25
]
to
d>2
, we may not obtain a near-optimal structure packed in
d
-D hypercuboids; rather, we might obtain complicated rectilinear regions. However, prior to our work, there exist no algorithms which considered packing in such rectilinear regions.

1.1
Our Contributions

For the
d
-D Hc-Knapsack
problem, we design a PTAS, thus settling the problem.
Our main structural result intuitively says that any packing, by incurring a loss of only
\varepsilon
-fraction of the profit, can be transformed into
O(1)
number of special hypercuboidal regions such that each region either contains a single large item, or contains items very small compared to its dimensions (
\mathcal{V}
-Box, see
\cref
fig:v-box), or contains items placed along a multidimensional grid (
\mathcal{N}
-Box, see
\cref
fig:n-box). We then provide an algorithm that guesses the arrangement of these hypercuboids and uses results of
[
25
,
34
]
to find a near-optimal packing of items in these special boxes.

((a))

An
\mathcal{V}
-Box is just a huge cuboid when compared to its size parameter
\hat{s}
. Each item has side length at most
\hat{s}
and the item set assigned
to it can be packed by NFDH.

((b))

An
\mathcal{N}
-Box is a multidimensional grid. An item set assigned to an
\mathcal{N}
-Box can be packed by placing each item in a cell.

Figure 1
:
\mathcal{V}
-Box and
\mathcal{N}
-Box

For our structural theorem, we start with an optimal solution.
First, we divide the items in the optimal packing into large, medium, and small items such that the medium items have a tiny profit and can be discarded.
Then, by extending the facets of the large items, we create a non-uniform grid with
O(1)

hypercuboidal cells.
However, some items may intersect the facets of these cells.
We consider the facets of all the cells and identify (based on the intersecting items) each of the facets as either
good
or

bad
.
Intuitively, if we only have good facets then we can repack items so that no items intersect the facets.
To get rid of the bad facets, we merge some cells so that they form composite cells,
which we call
collections
(See
\cref
3d-blocks-motivation).
Now we need to repack the items in the
collections
which may have complex rectilinear shapes.
If the collection has
k\in\{0,1,\dots,d\}
long dimensions then we call it a
k
-elongated collection.
Note that we can have
d+1
types of collections.

Our structural theorem can be viewed as a generalization of

[
25
,
21
]
; however, due to the complicated shape of collections, we had to overcome several technical obstacles in the process. For simplicity, we explain the intuition for
d=3
, where we obtain a packing of items into four types of rectilinear regions (collections) as in
\cref
3d-blocks-motivation.

Figure 2
:
Repacking items inside the left-most collection is easy (using NFDH). The right-most collections are handled recursively. However, repacking
the items in the other two collections is challenging (we needed a novel strip packing algorithm).

The first collection (3-elongated) is a
\mathcal{V}
-Box, i.e., all its three dimensions are long compared to the largest item packed in it. The simple NFDH algorithm suffices to repack the items inside it while losing only a few items with a small profit.
Although repacking in large collections can be easily handled by NFDH, there exist no previous algorithms to repack items in the remaining
types of collections.
Now for
k\in[d-1]
,
collections are long in at least one dimension and thus can be viewed as a type of strip (though the base may not be rectangular).
Harren
[
21
]
showed how to repack items almost optimally in a
d
-dimensional strip with a rectangular base with
a bounded aspect ratio. However, their approach doesn’t trivially extend to the 1- and 2-elongated collections in

\cref
3d-blocks-motivation, as the base can be non-rectangular or it may not satisfy the property of bounded aspect ratio even though it is rectangular.
In this paper, we circumvent this bottleneck by designing a PTAS (under resource augmentation) for
d
-dimensional strip packing to pack items on such complicated bases. Now we explain how we use the strip packing algorithm to pack these collections.

In the second collection (2-elongated), one dimension (marked in red) is reasonably short compared to the largest item intersecting it and the other dimensions (in green) are long.
We first divide the items in the collection into large, medium, and small items and ensure that the total volume of medium items is marginal.
We then consider the large items and round their sizes to up to
O(1)
types using linear grouping
[
27
]
.
The number of (rounded) large items that can fit on along the short dimension is
O(1)
.
So, similar to
[
27
]
, we solve an LP that represents the fractional strip packing with (rounded) large items where the items
are allowed to be sliced along
the long dimensions. We then convert this into an integral packing of
large items using resource augmentation along the long dimensions. Then we pack as many small items as possible in the gaps left. The rest of the small items, together with the medium items, are packed on the top.

The third collection (1-elongated), though complicated, is ensured (by our construction) to have a base with a bounded aspect ratio
1
1

1

More accurately, the minimal cuboid, which completely contains the base, has bounded-aspect ratio.
.
Moreover, it can be viewed as a union of

O(1)
number of smaller disjoint rectangles (which we call
base cells
).
We again classify each item in this collection as large, medium, or small. As above, we round the large items to
O(1)
types.
Note that the number of large items that can fit on the
base of the strip is only a constant (due to the bounded aspect ratio). We pack them integrally by first
solving the fractional LP where items are allowed to be sliced along the long dimension.
Our main novelty, in this case, is the way in which we pack the small items on the top of the strip.
Unlike
[
21
]
, we can’t use NFDH directly to pack the small items as the base isn’t rectangular.
Instead, we first merge a few base cells to form a larger base cell which is
rectangular (we show that this is indeed possible) and is large enough to accommodate the largest item (and thus any item).
Now, having at least one large base cell, we distribute the small and medium items
among all the base cells so that when they are packed using NFDH in strips on these base cells,
the maximum height is minimized.
Observe that if we pack the items on a single base cell, we may not efficiently use the entire area of the base and this can lead to a very tall strip.
Thus this process of distributing the items among all the base cells is important.

We use our near-optimal strip packing algorithm on these 1- or 2-elongated collections to repack the items while losing only a small profit.

Interestingly, the strip packing algorithm for both kinds of bases (in general, for any
k
-elongated collection where
k\in[d-1]
) is the same; the only change is the number of
short dimensions of the base that is given as a parameter to the algorithm. We provide the
algorithm for
d
-dimensional strip packing in
\cref
sec:strip packing.

Finally, for 0-elongated collections, we apply the above process recursively.
Using a shifting argumentation, we show that
O_{\varepsilon}(1)
steps of recursion is sufficient.
We also show that the resulting packing is a packing into
O(1)
number of
\mathcal{V}
-Boxes and
\mathcal{N}
-Boxes.
With more
technical ingenuity, these ideas can be extended to the case of
d>3
.

We believe that our techniques will be helpful for other multidimensional geometric packing problems involving rectilinear regions that are not hypercuboids.

Notations and Preliminaries

Let
[n]:=\Set{1,2,\dots,n}
.
For any set of items
\mathcal{I}
,
we define
\hat{s}(\mathcal{I}):=\max_{i\in\mathcal{I}}s(i)
.
For any
k
-dimensional region
R
, we denote its volume by
\operatorname{VOL}_{k}\left(R\right)
.
We extend this notation to an item
i
or a set of items
\mathcal{I}
, i.e.,

\operatorname{VOL}_{d}\left(i\right)=(s(i))^{d}
, and
\operatorname{VOL}_{d}\left(\mathcal{I}\right)=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}\operatorname{VOL}_{d}\left(i\right)
.
Also, the profit of a packing is the sum of the profits of the packed items
\mathcal{Q}
:
p\left(\mathcal{Q}\right):=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{Q}}p\left(i\right)
.

Consider a set of points
X
in
[0,1]^{d}
where each point

x\in X
can be represented as
(x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{d})
. For any set of dimensions

\mathcal{D}\subseteq[d]
, we define the projection of
X
onto
\mathcal{D}

as the set
\Set{\left(x_{d_{1}},\dots,x_{d_{\left|\mathcal{D}\right|}}\right)}{\left(x_{1},\dots,x_{k}\right)\in X}
,
where
\Set{d_{1},\dots,d_{\left|\mathcal{D}\right|}}=\mathcal{D}
and
d_{i}<d_{i+1}
for all
i\in[\left|\mathcal{D}\right|-1]
.

We only consider axis-aligned packing of hypercuboids and hypercubes.
Thus, a
d
-D hypercuboid
C
is given by the position of its lower corner
\left(x_{1},\dots,x_{d}\right)\in\real^{d}

and side lengths
\ell_{1},\dots,\ell_{d}\in\real_{+}
.
Then,
C
is the cartesian product of the intervals
\prod_{i=1}^{d}\left[x_{i},x_{i}+\ell_{i}\right]
, and
\operatorname{VOL}_{d}\left(C\right):=\prod_{i=1}^{d}\ell_{i}

and its surface area is defined as the sum of the volumes of its
\left(d-1\right)
-dimensional facets, i.e.,

\operatorname{SURF}_{d}\left(C\right):=2\sum_{i=1}^{d}\prod_{j=1,j\neq i}^{d}\ell_{j}=2\sum_{i=1}^{d}\operatorname{VOL}_{d}\left(C\right)/\ell_{i}
.
A packing is a subset
\mathcal{Q}\subseteq\mathcal{I}
of items
with positions
\operatorname{pos}:\mathcal{Q}\longrightarrow\left[0,1\right]^{d}
.
It is valid, if each item
i\in\mathcal{Q}
with the lower corner positioned at
\operatorname{pos}\left(i\right)
is completely included in the unit hypercube

[0,1]^{d}
and each pair of items
i,j\in\mathcal{Q}
is positioned non-overlapping.

Consider a set of items lying in
d
dimensional space. For any region in the
d
dimensional space,
we define the profit of that region as the profit of the set of items
completely
contained
in that region.

We will use the higher dimensional variant of the well-known Next Fit Decreasing Height (NFDH)
algorithm extensively. For the sake of completeness, we provide the details of the algorithm
in
\cref
app:nfdh.

Harren
[
21
]
gave a surface area based efficiency guarantee of NFDH
algorithm.

Lemma 1

(
[
21
]
)
.

Consider a set
S
of hypercubes with side lengths at most
\delta
and a hypercuboid
C
.
The NFDH algorithm either packs all items from
S
into
C

or the total volume left free inside of
C
is at most
\delta\operatorname{SURF}_{d}\left(C\right)/2
.

Now we define
\mathcal{V}
-Box and
\mathcal{N}
-Box.

Definition 2

(
\mathcal{V}
-Box and
\mathcal{N}
-Box)
.

Let
B
be a
d
-dimensional hypercuboid,
\mathcal{Q}
be a set of
items packed in it, and let
\hat{s}
be an upper bound on the
side lengths of the items in
\mathcal{Q}
.
We say that
B
is a
\mathcal{V}
-Box if its side lengths can be written
as
n_{1}\hat{s},n_{2}\hat{s},\dots,n_{d}\hat{s}
where

n_{1},n_{2},\dots,n_{d}\in\mathbb{N}_{+}
and the volume of the packed items is at most
\operatorname{VOL}_{d}\left(B\right)-\hat{s}\frac{\operatorname{SURF}_{d}\left(B\right)}{2}
.
We say that
B
is an
\mathcal{N}
-Box if its side lengths can be written
as
n_{1}\hat{s},n_{2}\hat{s},\dots,n_{d}\hat{s}
where

n_{1},n_{2},\dots,n_{d}\in\mathbb{N}_{+}
and the number of
packed items is at most
\prod_{i=1}^{d}n_{i}
.

As the side lengths are integral multiples of
\hat{s}
, the number of distinct

\mathcal{V}
-Boxes and
\mathcal{N}
-Boxes in the near-optimal structure will be polynomially bounded.
The following corollary follows from
\cref
nfdh.

Corollary 3
.

Let
B
be a
\mathcal{V}
-Box with the item set
\mathcal{Q}
packed in it. Then these items can be repacked into
B
using NFDH.

Proof.

We can either pack all the items in
\mathcal{I}
into
B
using NFDH, or, by
\cref
nfdh,
the free volume inside of
B
is at most
\hat{s}\operatorname{SURF}_{d}\left(B\right)/2
.
The latter possibility however implies that we packed items with a volume of at least
\operatorname{VOL}_{d}\left(B\right)-\hat{s}\operatorname{SURF}_{d}\left(B\right)/2
,
which is an upper bound on

\operatorname{VOL}_{d}\left(\mathcal{I}\right)

by the definition of a
\mathcal{V}
-Box.
∎

Observation 4
.

Let
B
be an
\mathcal{N}
-Box with the item set
\mathcal{Q}
packed in it.
As
\left|\mathcal{Q}\right|\leq\prod_{i=1}^{d}n_{i}
, we can divide
B
into

\prod_{i=1}^{d}n_{i}
cells such that each cell has length
\hat{s}
in each of the

in one cell.

Lemma 6
.

Let
C
be a
d
-D grid with

3.1
Structure of a Nearly Optimal Solution

First, we prove that
given an optimal packing
\operatorname{OPT}_{\mathrm{knapsack}}\left(\mathcal{I}\right)
of the input set
\mathcal{I}
, there exists another packing
containing a subset of the items packed in a simple structure
which can be searched for, in polynomial time. For this,
we modify the optimal packing to obtain a near-optimal packing in which all the
items are packed into either
\mathcal{V}
-Boxes and
\mathcal{N}
-Boxes except for a constant number of large items. The total number of
these boxes is

and their sizes come from a set whose cardinality is
polynomial in
\left|\mathcal{I}\right|
. Hence, this near-optimal packing can be
searched for, in polynomial time.

Theorem 7
.

For each
0<\varepsilon<1/2^{d+2}
, there is a packing with the following properties:

(i)

It consists of
\mathcal{N}
-Boxes and
\mathcal{V}
-Boxes whose total number is bounded by
a constant
C_{\mathrm{boxes}}\left(d,\varepsilon\right)
, which depends only on
\varepsilon
and

.

(ii)

The number of items in the packing that are not packed in these boxes is bounded
by a constant
C_{\mathrm{large}}\left(d,\varepsilon\right)
, which depends only on
\varepsilon
and
d
.

(iii)

The profit of the packing is at least
\left(1-2^{d+2}\varepsilon\right)\operatorname{OPT}_{\mathrm{knapsack}}\left(\mathcal{I}\right)

To prove the theorem, first, in

\cref
sec:knapsack:structure:classification, we consider a packing in an arbitrary grid
(note that the knapsack can be viewed as a grid
containing a single cell) and merge some
of its cells into collections based on the packing of the items into the grid.

The reason for choosing an arbitrary grid is that, as we will see,
we recursively divide the knapsack
into grids (which can have arbitrary shapes) and consider each of them separately.
Then in

\cref
sec:knapsack:structure:repacking, we show how to repack the items in a collection
using NFDH or by a strip packing algorithm described in
\cref

Before we proceed to repack the items inside of those collections,

Previously, we reordered the dimensions for a collection to have the

The differences between our algorithm and Harren’s algorithm
[
21
]
can be summed up as follows:
Due to the complex nature of
A
, we have multiple hypercuboids, each having a bounded aspect ratio.
This changes the analysis of the algorithm. It also changes the details like packing the medium and small items on the top,
because we have to split one strip into multiple smaller strips
(having hypercuboidal bases) and distribute the items among them. The addition of very long
sides in form of
B
, like in
\cref
fig:strip packing:2d base:2, breaks the
bounded aspect ratio property in a more crucial way. Harren’s
algorithm first packs the large items on the base and then extends
this packing along the height of the strip. This relies on the fact that,
due to bounded aspect ratio, not many large items can be packed on the base,
and thus we have to consider only a constant number of so-called

configurations
. If we have additional long sides in the base in form of
B
,
we don’t have such a bound on the number of large items
that can fit in the base. For example, in
\cref
fig:strip packing:2d base:2,
an item can be large with respect to
A
(shown in green), but the number of
such items that can fit on the base
A\times B
can’t be bounded by a constant
since the side shown in red can be arbitrarily long.

We work around this problem as follows: First, we consider
(d-k)
dimensional packings,
i.e.,
configurations
of the items, on
A
.
Then we not only extend these configurations along the height of the strip but
also along each of the
(k-1)
dimensions of
B
.
For example, in
\cref
fig:strip packing:2d base:2, we first create one-dimensional
packings on
A
and then extend each of these packings
both in the rightward direction and along the height of the strip.

3.1.3
Repacking a Collection

Depending on the type of the collection,
we can simplify the packing of the items assigned to each collection.
Let
\mathcal{C}
be a
k
-elongated collection and let
\mathcal{Q}
be the
set of items assigned to
\mathcal{C}
.
Let
\hat{s}:=\hat{s}\left(\mathcal{Q}\right)
.
We consider the cases

First of all, if
\mathcal{C}
is a
d
-elongated collection, then it consists of only a single cell as it has no bad facets and hence can not be merged with any other cell.
Thus we can repack (after removing only a small profit subset) it efficiently using NFDH
because it is a hypercuboidal region that is large in all dimensions.

Lemma 16
.

If
\mathcal{C}
is a
d
-elongated collection, then it can be transformed into a single

\mathcal{V}
-Box with a loss of profit at most

For a
k
-elongated collection with

\cref
knapsack:structure:strip packing
as shown in the next lemma.

Lemma 17
.

A
k
-elongated collection with

can be transformed into a constant number of
\mathcal{V}
-Boxes and

-Boxes by losing profit at most

\left(k+7+\max\left(6,2^{k}\right)\right)\varepsilon p\left(\mathcal{Q}\right)
where
\mathcal{Q}
is the set of items assigned to it.

Proof.

We begin by shrinking the collection to compensate for the enlargement that the strip packing algorithm will cause.
Define one of the long dimensions of the collection to be its height and let
h
be the length in this dimension.
Let
c:=4+\max\left(3,2^{k-1}\right)
.
We assumed
\varepsilon<1/2^{(d+2)}<1/(2c)
.
Divide the strip into
\left\lfloor 1/\left(c\varepsilon\right)\right\rfloor
slices along the height.
After that each slice has a height of at least

\displaystyle c\varepsilon h

\displaystyle=\left(4+\max\left(3,2^{k-1}\right)\right)\varepsilon h

\displaystyle\geq\left(2+\max\left(3,2^{k-1}\right)\right)\varepsilon h+2\varepsilon\alpha_{k}\hat{s}

(since
h\geq\alpha_{k}\hat{s}
)

\displaystyle{{=}\left(2+\max\left(3,2^{k-1}\right)\right)\varepsilon h+4\left(C_{\mathrm{configs}}\left(d,k,\alpha_{k+1},N,\varepsilon\right)+3\right)\hat{s}}

(by definition of
\alpha_{k}
)

\displaystyle\geq\left(2+\max\left(3,2^{k-1}\right)\right)\varepsilon h+2\left(C_{\mathrm{configs}}\left(d,k,\alpha_{k+1},N,\varepsilon\right)+3\right)\hat{s}+6\hat{s}.

By clearing the slice with the lowest profit of items completely contained in it, we leave a gap with a height of

\left(2+\max\left(3,2^{k-1}\right)\right)\varepsilon h+2\left(C_{\mathrm{configs}}\left(d,k,\alpha_{k+1},N,\varepsilon\right)+3\right)\hat{s}+4\hat{s}
.
This causes a loss of profit at most

\frac{1}{\left\lfloor 1/\left(c\varepsilon\right)\right\rfloor}p\left(\mathcal{Q}\right)\leq\frac{1}{1/\left(c\varepsilon\right)-1}p\left(\mathcal{Q}\right)\leq\frac{1}{1/\left(c\varepsilon\right)-1/\left(2c\varepsilon\right)}p\left(\mathcal{Q}\right)=2c\varepsilon p\left(\mathcal{Q}\right).

The items intersecting the bottom or top facet of the collection fit in a gap of height
2\hat{s}
.
We reduce the height of the collection to

h^{\prime}:=h-\left(2+\max\left(3,2^{k-1}\right)\right)\varepsilon h-2\left(C_{\mathrm{configs}}\left(d,k,\alpha_{k+1},N,\varepsilon\right)+3\right)\hat{s}

by shifting the items intersecting the (removed) region at the top into a gap of height

\left(2+\max\left(3,2^{k-1}\right)\right)\varepsilon h+2\left(C_{\mathrm{configs}}\left(d,k,\alpha_{k+1},N,\varepsilon\right)+3\right)\hat{s}+\hat{s}
.

For each other large dimension we create
\left\lceil 1/\varepsilon\right\rceil
slices and clear the slice with the lowest profit,
losing at most
\varepsilon p\left(\mathcal{Q}\right)

and create a gap of width at least

\frac{1}{\left\lceil 1/\varepsilon\right\rceil}\alpha_{k}\hat{s}-2\hat{s}\geq\frac{1}{1/\varepsilon+1}\alpha_{k}\hat{s}-2\hat{s}\geq\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\alpha_{k}\hat{s}-2\hat{s}\geq 4\hat{s}.

Now we can shift the items intersecting the orthogonal facets into that gap
and reduce the length of the collection in this direction by
\hat{s}

After this shifting process, we will be left with a subset of items

For this, we create a

We define

Now our base can be represented as the cartesian product

As a last preparation step, we scale the whole collection and the items in it by

Thus, we can use the strip packing algorithm
\cref
knapsack:structure:strip packing using the bound on the height by

knapsack:structure:strip packing:exact to compute a packing of height at most

\displaystyle\left(1+\left(2+\max\left(3,2^{k-1}\right)\right)\varepsilon\right)\operatorname{OPT}_{\mathrm{strip}}\left(Q^{\prime},A,B\right)+2\left(C_{\mathrm{configs}}\left(d,k,\alpha_{k+1},N,\varepsilon\right)+3\right)\hat{s}f

\displaystyle\leq\left(1+\left(2+\max\left(3,2^{k-1}\right)\right)\varepsilon\right)h^{\prime}f+2\left(C_{\mathrm{configs}}\left(d,k,\alpha_{k+1},N,\varepsilon\right)+3\right)\hat{s}f

\displaystyle=hf.

After scaling back, the packing fits into our collection.
∎

Repacking
0
-elongated Collections.
For
a
0
-elongated collection, we first distinguish the items into large, medium, and small items such that the profit of
medium items is very small so that they can be discarded. We then further distinguish between the cases when (i) there is
a large item of very small profit or (ii) every large item has a significant profit. In the first case, we remove the large item
to make enough space to repack the small items. In the second case, we use the fact that the number of large items can only be

O(1)
. So, we partition the grid into smaller grids and solve these recursively. We finally prove that we only require
O(1)
number of recursive
steps that we require are only a constant in number.

From now on, lets assume that
\mathcal{C}
is a
0
-elongated collection.
To partition
\mathcal{Q}
into large, medium, and small items, we define the following values.

\rho^{\prime}_{0}:=1,\rho^{\prime}_{i+1}:=\left(\frac{\left(\rho_{i}^{\prime}\right)^{d}}{4dN\alpha_{1}^{d}}\right)^{d+1}
and

\rho_{i}:=\rho_{i}^{\prime}\hat{s}
for all
i\geq 0
.
Let
\eta\in{\rm Nature}_{+}
be minimal such that
p\left(\Set{x\in\mathcal{Q}}{\rho_{\eta}<s\left(x\right)<\rho_{\eta-1}}\right)\leq\varepsilon p\left(\mathcal{Q}\right)
.
Note that
\eta\leq 1/\varepsilon
.
We now partition
\mathcal{Q}
into sets

\mathcal{S}:=\Set{x\in\mathcal{Q}}{s\left(x\right)\leq\rho_{\eta}}
,

\mathcal{M}:=\Set{x\in\mathcal{Q}}{\rho_{\eta}<s\left(x\right)<\rho_{\eta-1}}
and

\mathcal{L}:=\Set{x\in\mathcal{Q}}{\rho_{\eta-1}\leq s\left(x\right)}
.

We call
\mathcal{S}
(resp.
\mathcal{M},\mathcal{L}

Note that since

Suppose

In this case, we have at most

\left(1-\varepsilon\right)\operatorname{OPT}_{\mathrm{knapsack}}\left(\mathcal{I}\right)

with a running time which is polynomial in
\left|\mathcal{I}\right|
.

Proof.

Using
\cref
knapsack:structure with an accuracy of
2^{-d-3}\varepsilon

we know that there is a packing with a simple structure and profit at least
\left(1-\varepsilon/2\right)\operatorname{OPT}_{\mathrm{knapsack}}\left(\mathcal{I}\right)
.
Let
\mathcal{B}
be the set of
\mathcal{N}
-Boxes and
\mathcal{V}
-Boxes in this
structure,
\mathcal{L}\subseteq\mathcal{I}
the set of items packed outside of those boxes and
\mathcal{S}\subseteq\mathcal{I}\backslash\mathcal{L}
the set of items packed inside of them.

As the number of items in
\mathcal{L}
is constant,
there are at most
\left|\mathcal{I}\right|^{C_{\mathrm{large}}\left(d,\varepsilon\right)}
choices for this subset
\mathcal{L}\subseteq\mathcal{I}
.
There are at most
C_{\mathrm{boxes}}\left(d,\varepsilon\right)+1
choices for the number of boxes in
\mathcal{B}

and for each box there are at most:

•

2
choices whether it is a
\mathcal{V}
-Box or an
\mathcal{N}
-Box,

•

\left|\mathcal{I}\right|
choices for the size parameter
\hat{s}
of the box,

•

\left|\mathcal{I}\right|^{d}
choices for the side lengths of the box.

All of these choices are polynomial in the number of items
\left|\mathcal{I}\right|
.
Thus, by iterating over all possible choices,
we can assume at this point that we know the set of items
\mathcal{L}

and the set
\mathcal{B}
of
\mathcal{V}
-Boxes and
\mathcal{N}
-Boxes of that nearly optimal solution.
As both
\left|\mathcal{L}\right|
and
\left|\mathcal{B}\right|
are bounded by a constant,
we can find a packing of those items and boxes in the unit hypercube in constant time.
The last step left to do is to pack items from
\mathcal{I}\backslash\mathcal{L}
in the boxes with a nearly optimal profit.
We can do this by solving a special variant of the Generalized Assignment Problem (GAP)
[
41
]
.
In GAP, we are given a set of one-dimensional knapsacks with a capacity each and a set of items that may have a possibly different size and profit for each knapsack.
The goal in this problem is to find a feasible packing with maximal profit.
We will consider our
O(1)
number of
\mathcal{V}
-Boxes and
\mathcal{N}
-Boxes to be knapsacks.
Consider a
\mathcal{V}
-Box
B_{V}
with size parameter
\hat{s}
. We set its capacity to be
\operatorname{VOL}_{d}\left(B\right)-\hat{s}\left(\operatorname{SURF}_{d}\left(B\right)/2\right)
.
For any item
i
, we set its size with respect to
B_{V}
as
\operatorname{VOL}_{d}\left(i\right)
if
s(i)\leq\hat{s}
and
\infty
otherwise.
On the other hand, consider an
\mathcal{N}
-Box
B_{N}
with size parameter
\hat{s}
and
\{n_{i}\}_{i\in{d}}
denoting the number of cells in each dimension.
We set its capacity to be
n_{1}n_{2}\dots n_{d}
.
For any item
i
, we set its size with respect to
B_{N}
as
1
if
s(i)\leq\hat{s}
and
\infty
otherwise.
The profit of any item
i
with respect to any box is just set as
p\left(i\right)
.
This boils down to a variant of GAP with
O(1)
number of knapsacks,
which admits a PTAS
[
18
]
.
Thus by solving this instance, we get a subset
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathcal{I}\backslash\mathcal{L}
with profit at least
p\left(\mathcal{S}^{\prime}\right)\geq\left(1-\varepsilon/2\right)p\left(\mathcal{S}\right)
.
Using
\cref
n-box and
\cref
v-box we can ensure to pack those items inside the boxes.
The total profit packed is

∎

4
Conclusion

We have designed a PTAS for a variant of the knapsack problem (
d
-D Hc-Knapsack
),
where the items are
d
-dimensional hypercubes with arbitrary profits.
En route, we have also developed a near-optimal algorithm for a variant of the
d
-dimensional hypercube strip packing problem, where the base need not be hypercuboidal, but we are
allowed to extend some of the sides of the base (the long dimensions) by a small amount.
Extending the techniques of
[
23
]
, we believe that it might be possible to design
an EPTAS for
d
-D Hc-Knapsack
.

The knapsack problem for squares, cubes, and hypercubes is thus almost settled.
Whether there exists a PTAS for the knapsack variant where items are rectangles
is still open. It is also interesting to improve the current best approximation
ratios (
(5+\varepsilon)
with rotations and
(7+\varepsilon)
without rotations

[
14
]
) for the knapsack
problem where items are cuboids (3D) because of its practical relevance.
Another interesting task of theoretical importance would be to improve the
current best approximation ratio (which is
(3^{d}+\varepsilon)
due to
[
40
]
) for the knapsack
problem where items are
d
-dimensional hypercuboids.

Next Fit Decreasing Height (NFDH) is a greedy algorithm introduced in

\mathcal{R}

1

Now we can bound the number of large items in the transformed packing.

\displaystyle C_{\mathrm{large}}\left(d,\varepsilon\right)

C.1
Classification of the Items

We assume that the accuracy parameter
\varepsilon
is at most
1/3
.
We partition the input set
\mathcal{I}
of hypercubes into sets of
large
\mathcal{L}:=\Set{x\in\mathcal{I}}{\rho_{\eta}\leq s\left(x\right)}
,

\mathcal{M}:=\Set{x\in\mathcal{I}}{\rho_{\eta+1}<s\left(x\right)<\rho_{\eta}}

and small
\mathcal{S}:=\Set{x\in\mathcal{I}}{s\left(x\right)\leq\rho_{\eta+1}}

\displaystyle\rho_{1}:=\frac{\varepsilon\hat{s}}{2\left(d-1\right)N\left(\alpha\hat{s}\right)^{d-k}}\textup{ and }\rho_{i+1}:=\rho_{1}\frac{\rho_{i}^{\left(d-k\right)^{2}}}{2^{d-k}}\textup{ for all }i\geq 1

and
\eta\in{\rm Nature}_{+}
is the smallest index such that the total volume of the items in

\mathcal{M}
is at most
\varepsilon\operatorname{VOL}_{d}\left(\mathcal{I}\right)
.
Clearly
\eta
is at most
\left\lceil 1/\varepsilon\right\rceil
.

C.2
Packing the Large Items

We start by creating a packing for the large items into the strip.
The rough procedure for this is the following:
First, we reduce the number of item sizes through rounding.
This creates a constant bound on the number of subsets of rounded large items that can be packed into
A
, as the items with the same size can be considered to be identical.
For each of those subsets, we create a
(d-k)
-dimensional packing into the region of
A
.
Each of those packings will be assigned a
k
-dimensional volume
which will be used to extend this packing to a
d
-dimensional one.
This extension will be accomplished by transforming a
(d-k)
-dimensional item placed in
A
into an
\mathcal{N}
-Box with enough space for many items of the same size.
As the last step, we stack those packings on top of each other into the strip.

Now we describe the rounding procedure in detail.
We round the sizes of the large items up using the linear grouping
technique
[
12
]
such that the number of
distinct side lengths is a constant
N_{\mathrm{sizes}}:=\left\lceil 1/\left(\varepsilon\rho_{\eta}^{d}\right)\right\rceil
.
To do this, we partition the large items
\mathcal{L}
into groups
\mathcal{G}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{G}_{N_{\mathrm{sizes}}}
as follows.
We first sort the items in decreasing order of their side lengths.
Let
l=N_{\mathrm{sizes}}-\left|\mathcal{L}\right|\%N_{\mathrm{sizes}}
where
\%
is the modulo operator.
The first group
\mathcal{G}_{1}
contains the first
m:=\left\lfloor\left|\mathcal{L}\right|/N_{\mathrm{sizes}}\right\rfloor
items,

G_{2}
contains the next
m
items, and so on until group
G_{l}
.
Groups
G_{l+1}
through
G_{N_{\mathrm{sizes}}}
contain
m+1
items each.

The side length of every item in group
\mathcal{G}_{i}
for each
i\in[N_{\mathrm{sizes}}]

is increased to
\hat{s}(\mathcal{G}_{i})
.
Let the set of rounded up items be denoted by
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{up}}
.
The next lemma shows that this rounding only has a limited effect on the volume and the height of an optimal packing for the large items.

Let’s now define a

{\rm Nature}_{0}^{N_{\mathrm{sizes}}}
as follows:
A configuration

\mathcal{G}_{i}

for all

.
We call a configuration

if the projection of the items represented by it in the first
(d-k)

\bigcup_{a\in A}a
;
otherwise, it is invalid.
As each large item has size at least
\rho_{\eta}
, we know that every configuration with
any tuple value

is invalid since the volume of

is one unit.
Thus, the number of valid configurations is upper bounded by

\left(1/\rho_{\eta}^{d-k}+1\right)^{N_{\mathrm{sizes}}}
which is a constant.
Further, each of those configurations contains only a constant number of items;
Thus, we can find in a constant time a lower-justified packing for its items into
\bigcup_{a\in A}a
to verify that it is valid or detect that no such packing exists and the configuration is invalid.

Lower-justified packing denotes a packing where each item is pushed downward in each dimension
until it is tangent to a lower item or a lower border of
A
.
Thus, we can verify if a configuration is valid or not in constant time.

Let

be the number of valid configurations and

be the number of items from group

\displaystyle\min

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{N_{\mathrm{configs}}}x_{i}

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{N_{\mathrm{configs}}}x_{i}c_{i,j}=\left|\mathcal{G}_{j}\right|\hat{s}\left(\mathcal{G}_{j}\right)^{k}\quad\textrm{for all }j\in N_{\mathrm{sizes}}

\displaystyle x_{i}\geq 0

So, there exists an optimal solution and we denote it with

for

.
For each valid configuration number

does not exceed the optimal height for packing the large items.

C.3
Packing Small Items in the Gaps

We now focus on the gaps inside of each layer
L_{i}
that are not reserved for the
\mathcal{N}
-Boxes that contain the large items.
We want to pack small items in those gaps using NFDH.
Additionally, we want those gaps to match the requirements for
\mathcal{V}
-Boxes, therefore we will also perform some rounding on their size.

As there are at most
{1}/{\rho_{\eta}^{d-k}}
large items packed into
\bigcup_{a\in A}a
by the configuration from which this layer was created,
we can split the free space in each individual
a\in A
into a grid with at most

\cref

Thus, this grid contains at most
2^{d-k}/\rho_{\eta}^{\left(d-k\right)^{2}}
hypercuboidal cells which we
will call gaps. This way, we split the entire
(d-k)
-dimensional region
A
into a set
G_{i}
of
at most
N2^{d-k}/\rho_{\eta}^{\left(d-k\right)^{2}}
hypercuboidal gaps.
This method also ensures that each gap is contained in some
a\in A
and therefore of a limited side length of
\alpha\hat{s}
.
We can define a function
\operatorname{ext}
that extends each gap
g\in G_{i}
to a
d
-dimensional gap

g\times B\times\left[0,h_{i}\right]
.
Note, that we assume here for simplicity that each layer starts at a height of

and reaches a height of

L_{1}^{\prime},\dots,L_{i-1}^{\prime}
.
By the way in which the layer was constructed the gaps
\Set{\operatorname{ext}\left(g\right)}{g\in G_{i}}
partition the empty space of the layer
L_{i}
.
Consider
\cref
fig:strip packing:large packing.
The packing into
A
in
\cref

1
-dimensional gap on top of the items.
The cells of
A
are not visualized in this figure, so it might be the case that this gap is divided into multiple gaps because of the boundaries of the cells, but for simplicity let us suppose this gap is not divided.
In
\cref

3
-dimensional gap in the same way as the packed items were extended to
\mathcal{N}
-Boxes and it covers the whole empty space of the layer.

We can also define another function
\operatorname{ext}^{\prime}
that rounds the side lengths of each
g\in G_{i}
down to the largest
multiple of
\hat{s}\left(\mathcal{S}\right)
before applying
\operatorname{ext}
.
The following lemma shows that we don’t lose too much of the packable volume in the gap by rounding.

Lemma 27
.

Let

be a gap in layer

can be bounded by

\displaystyle\operatorname{VOL}_{d}\left(\operatorname{ext}\left(g\right)\right)-\operatorname{VOL}_{d}\left(\operatorname{ext}^{\prime}\left(g\right)\right)

\displaystyle\leq\rho_{\eta+1}\left(d-k\right)x_{i}^{*}\left(\alpha\hat{s}\right)^{d-k-1}.

Proof.

The hypercuboidal region
\operatorname{ext}^{\prime}\left(g\right)
is obtained from
\operatorname{ext}\left(g\right)
by decreasing the
side lengths in
(d-k)
dimensions by a value of at most
\hat{s}\left(\mathcal{S}\right)
. Let

g_{1},g_{2},\dots,g_{d-k}
be the lengths of
\operatorname{ext}\left(g\right)
in those dimensions. Then

\displaystyle\operatorname{VOL}_{d}\left(\operatorname{ext}^{\prime}\left(g\right)\right)

\displaystyle\geq\operatorname{VOL}_{k-1}\left(B\right)h_{i}\prod_{j=1}^{d-k}(g_{j}-\hat{s}\left(\mathcal{S}\right))

\displaystyle=x_{i}^{*}\prod_{j=1}^{d-k}(g_{j}-\hat{s}\left(\mathcal{S}\right))

(definition of
h_{i}
)

\displaystyle\geq x_{i}^{*}\left(\prod_{j=1}^{d-k}g_{j}-\hat{s}\left(\mathcal{S}\right)\sum_{j=1}^{d-k}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}k\in[d-k]\\
k\neq j\end{subarray}}g_{k}\right)

(inductive argument over
(d-k)
)

\displaystyle\geq x_{i}^{*}\left(\prod_{j=1}^{d-k}g_{j}-(d-k)\hat{s}\left(\mathcal{S}\right)(\alpha\hat{s})^{d-k-1}\right)

(since for all
i\in[d-k]
,
g_{i}\leq\alpha\hat{s}
)

\displaystyle\geq x_{i}^{*}\prod_{j=1}^{d-k}g_{j}-\rho_{\eta+1}\left(d-k\right)x_{i}^{*}\left(\alpha\hat{s}\right)^{d-k-1}

(since
\hat{s}\left(\mathcal{S}\right)\leq\rho_{\eta+1}
)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{VOL}_{d}\left(\operatorname{ext}\left(g\right)\right)-\rho_{\eta+1}\left(d-k\right)x_{i}^{*}\left(\alpha\hat{s}\right)^{d-k-1}

\displaystyle\;\;\qed

For each gap
g\in G_{i}
we now assign a maximal set of small items
\mathcal{I}_{i,g}
such that the volume of these items does not exceed

\operatorname{VOL}_{d}\left(\operatorname{ext}^{\prime}\left(g\right)\right)-\rho_{\eta+1}\frac{\operatorname{SURF}_{d}\left(\operatorname{ext}^{\prime}\left(g\right)\right)}{2}

By doing so, we ensure that the items in
\mathcal{I}_{i,g}
can be packed in
\operatorname{ext}^{\prime}(g)
using
\cref
v-box.
We can now distinguish between two cases, where we either (i) pack all small items in this way
or (ii) we ensure that small items are packed into each gap
\operatorname{ext}^{\prime}(g)
with a volume of at least

\operatorname{VOL}_{d}\left(\mathcal{I}_{i,g}\right)>\operatorname{VOL}_{d}\left(\operatorname{ext}^{\prime}\left(g\right)\right)-\rho_{\eta+1}\frac{\operatorname{SURF}_{d}\left(\operatorname{ext}^{\prime}\left(g\right)\right)}{2}-\rho_{\eta+1}^{d}

as
\rho_{\eta+1}^{d}
is an upper bound on the volume of a small item.
In the second case, we can show that each layer is sufficiently packed, i.e., the wasted space is small. We show this
in the next lemma.

Lemma 28
.

For a layer
L_{i}
, let
\operatorname{FVOL}_{d}\left(L_{i}\right)
denote the volume of the empty space in
L_{i}
left after packing small items in the set of gaps
\{\operatorname{ext}^{\prime}(g)|g\in G_{i}\}
.
If there are still small items left unpacked, then we have the following inequality.

\displaystyle\operatorname{FVOL}_{d}\left(L_{i}\right)\leq\frac{3}{2}\varepsilon x_{i}^{*}+\frac{\varepsilon}{d-1}\operatorname{VOL}_{k-1}\left(B\right)\hat{s}.

Proof.

By using the upper bound
\alpha\hat{s}
for the side length of any gap
g\in G_{i}
in the first
(d-k)
dimensions
and since
b_{j}\geq 1
we can bound the surface of the rounded gaps.
Let
g_{1},g_{2},\dots,g_{d-k}
denote the side lengths of
\operatorname{ext}^{\prime}(g)
.

\displaystyle\quad\frac{\operatorname{SURF}_{d}\left(\operatorname{ext}^{\prime}\left(g\right)\right)}{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\operatorname{SURF}_{d}\left(\operatorname{ext}\left(g\right)\right)}{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{\operatorname{VOL}_{d}\left(\operatorname{ext}^{\prime}(g)\right)}{2}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}\frac{1}{b_{j}}+\sum_{j=1}^{d-k}\frac{1}{g_{j}}+\frac{\operatorname{VOL}_{k-1}\left(B\right)}{x_{i}^{*}}\right)

(by definition of
\operatorname{SURF}_{d}\left(\cdot\right)
)

\displaystyle=\frac{g_{1}g_{2}\dots g_{d-k}x_{i}^{*}}{2}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}\frac{1}{b_{j}}+\sum_{j=1}^{d-k}\frac{1}{g_{j}}+\frac{\operatorname{VOL}_{k-1}\left(B\right)}{x_{i}^{*}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\left(d-k\right)x_{i}^{*}\left(\alpha\hat{s}\right)^{d-k-1}+\left(\alpha\hat{s}\right)^{d-k}x_{i}^{*}\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}\frac{1}{b_{j}}+\left(\alpha\hat{s}\right)^{d-k}\operatorname{VOL}_{k-1}\left(B\right)

(as each
g_{i}\leq\alpha\hat{s}
)

\displaystyle\leq\left(d-k\right)x_{i}^{*}\left(\alpha\hat{s}\right)^{d-k-1}+\left(\alpha\hat{s}\right)^{d-k}\left(x_{i}^{*}\left(k-1\right)+\operatorname{VOL}_{k-1}\left(B\right)\right)

(since each
b_{i}\geq 1
)

Now we can bound the free volume inside of a gap
g\in G_{i}
:

\displaystyle\quad\operatorname{VOL}_{d}\left(\operatorname{ext}\left(g\right)\right)-\operatorname{VOL}_{d}\left(\mathcal{I}_{i,g}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\operatorname{VOL}_{d}\left(\operatorname{ext}^{\prime}\left(g\right)\right)-\operatorname{VOL}_{d}\left(\mathcal{I}_{i,g}\right)+\rho_{\eta+1}\left(d-k\right)x_{i}^{*}\left(\alpha\hat{s}\right)^{d-k-1}

(by
\cref
strip packing:gaps:rounding)

\displaystyle<\rho_{\eta+1}\frac{\operatorname{SURF}_{d}\left(\operatorname{ext}^{\prime}\left(g\right)\right)}{2}+\rho_{\eta+1}^{d}+\rho_{\eta+1}\left(d-k\right)x_{i}^{*}\left(\alpha\hat{s}\right)^{d-k-1}

(using the lower bound on
\operatorname{VOL}_{d}\left(\mathcal{I}_{i,g}\right)
)

\displaystyle\leq 2\rho_{\eta+1}\left(d-k\right)x_{i}^{*}\left(\alpha\hat{s}\right)^{d-k-1}+\rho_{\eta+1}\left(\alpha\hat{s}\right)^{d-k}\left(x_{i}^{*}\left(k-1\right)+\operatorname{VOL}_{k-1}\left(B\right)\right)+\rho_{\eta+1}^{d}.

Finally, we add the free volume of all the gaps in layer
L_{i}

and use the bound
\left|G_{i}\right|\leq N2^{d-k}/\rho_{\eta}^{\left(d-k\right)^{2}}
:

\displaystyle\quad\operatorname{FVOL}_{d}\left(L_{i}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{g\in G_{i}}\left(\operatorname{VOL}_{d}\left(\operatorname{ext}\left(g\right)\right)-\operatorname{VOL}_{d}\left(\mathcal{I}_{i,g}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{N{2^{d-k}}}{\rho_{\eta}^{\left(d-k\right)^{2}}}\rho_{\eta+1}\left(2\left(d-k\right)x_{i}^{*}\left(\alpha\hat{s}\right)^{d-k-1}+\left(\alpha\hat{s}\right)^{d-k}\left(x_{i}^{*}\left(k-1\right)+\operatorname{VOL}_{k-1}\left(B\right)\right)+\rho_{\eta+1}^{d-1}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\varepsilon\hat{s}}{2\left(d-1\right)\left(\alpha\hat{s}\right)^{d-k}}\left(2\left(d-k\right)x_{i}^{*}\left(\alpha\hat{s}\right)^{d-k-1}+\left(\alpha\hat{s}\right)^{d-k}\left(x_{i}^{*}\left(k-1\right)+\operatorname{VOL}_{k-1}\left(B\right)\right)+\rho_{\eta+1}^{d-1}\right)

(by definition of
\rho_{\eta+1}
and
\rho_{1}
)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{\alpha}x_{i}^{*}+\frac{\varepsilon\hat{s}}{2}x_{i}^{*}+\frac{\varepsilon\hat{s}}{2\left(d-1\right)}\operatorname{VOL}_{k-1}\left(B\right)+\frac{\varepsilon\hat{s}}{2\left(d-1\right)\left(\alpha\hat{s}\right)^{d-k}}\rho_{\eta+1}^{d-1}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{3}{2}\varepsilon x_{i}^{*}+\frac{\varepsilon}{2\left(d-1\right)}\operatorname{VOL}_{k-1}\left(B\right)\hat{s}+\frac{\varepsilon}{2\left(d-1\right)\alpha^{d-k}}\rho_{\eta+1}^{k-1}\hat{s}

(since
\alpha\geq 1
and
\rho_{\eta+1}\leq\hat{s}\leq 1
)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{3}{2}\varepsilon x_{i}^{*}+\frac{\varepsilon}{d-1}\operatorname{VOL}_{k-1}\left(B\right)\hat{s}

(since
\rho_{\eta+1}\leq 1
,
\alpha\geq 1
and
\operatorname{VOL}_{k-1}\left(B\right)\geq 1
)

∎

Because we enlarged the layers at the end of
\cref
sec:strip packing:large in the long dimensions,
we can enlarge each gap
in the long dimensions and along the height of the strip
such that each side length is a multiple of
\hat{s}\left(S\right)
and the gap is thus a valid
\mathcal{V}
-Box.

C.4
Packing Medium and the Remaining Small Items on the Top

The remaining unpacked small items are packed together with the medium items in a new layer
L_{\text{top}}
on top of the strip using NFDH.
We use
\hat{s}\left(\mathcal{M}\right):=\hat{s}\left(\mathcal{S}\right)
in the special case where there are no medium items.
Contrary to
[
21
]
, where a single hypercuboid forms the base of the strip
and the items of the additional layer are just packed using NFDH,
our base is formed by multiple hypercuboids.
Each of those hypercuboids forms its own strip and we have to decide for each item in which strip to pack it to maintain a low height.
Moreover, it might be the case that the largest medium item does not fit in any of those individual strips and we have to combine strips to pack this item.
Let us first discuss how to pack items into a strip with a hypercuboid as the base.

Lemma 29
.

Let
c
be a
(d-1)
-dimensional hypercuboid and
\mathcal{I}
be a set of
d
-dimensional hypercubes of size at most
\rho_{\eta}
.
Let
2\operatorname{VOL}_{d-1}\left(c\right)>\rho_{\eta}\operatorname{SURF}_{d-1}\left(c\right)
.
The items can then be packed using NFDH into a strip with base
c
and height

\displaystyle h=\frac{\operatorname{VOL}_{d}\left(\mathcal{I}\right)+\rho_{\eta}\operatorname{VOL}_{d-1}\left(c\right)}{\operatorname{VOL}_{d-1}\left(c\right)-\rho_{\eta}\operatorname{SURF}_{d-1}\left(c\right)/2}.

Proof.

We know by
\cref
nfdh, that we can either pack all items into the hypercuboid defined by
g:=c\times\left[0,h\right]
or we can pack a subset
\mathcal{I}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathcal{I}
of the items leaving free space inside of this hypercuboid of volume

\displaystyle\operatorname{VOL}_{d-1}\left(c\right)h-\operatorname{VOL}_{d}\left(\mathcal{I}^{\prime}\right)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{VOL}_{d}\left(g\right)-\operatorname{VOL}_{d}\left(\mathcal{I}^{\prime}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\rho_{\eta}\frac{\operatorname{SURF}_{d}\left(g\right)}{2}

\displaystyle=\rho_{\eta}\left(\frac{\operatorname{SURF}_{d-1}\left(c\right)}{2}h+\operatorname{VOL}_{d-1}\left(c\right)\right).

We may restate this inequality as

\displaystyle\left(\operatorname{VOL}_{d-1}\left(c\right)-\rho_{\eta}\frac{\operatorname{SURF}_{d-1}\left(c\right)}{2}\right)h\leq\operatorname{VOL}_{d}\left(\mathcal{I}^{\prime}\right)+\rho_{\eta}\operatorname{VOL}_{d-1}\left(c\right),

and that is equivalent to

\displaystyle\operatorname{VOL}_{d}\left(\mathcal{I}\right)+\rho_{\eta}\operatorname{VOL}_{d-1}\left(c\right)\leq\operatorname{VOL}_{d}\left(\mathcal{I}^{\prime}\right)+\rho_{\eta}\operatorname{VOL}_{d-1}\left(c\right).

This finally yields
\operatorname{VOL}_{d}\left(\mathcal{I}\right)\leq\operatorname{VOL}_{d}\left(\mathcal{I}^{\prime}\right)
by using the definition of
h

and since
\mathcal{I}^{\prime}
is a subset of
\mathcal{I}
, we also have
\operatorname{VOL}_{d}\left(\mathcal{I}\right)\geq\operatorname{VOL}_{d}\left(\mathcal{I}^{\prime}\right)
and thus all items are packed.
∎

Next, we want to ensure that we can use the packing method described in
\cref
strip packing:top layer,
meaning for each item there is a strip that is large enough to pack the item into.

To do this, we temporarily pack a
(d-k)
dimensional cube of side length
\hat{s}
on
A

(see
\cref
fig:strip packing:top layer:1)
and take the set of all intersected hypercuboids
A_{\text{merged}}\subseteq A
(see
\cref
fig:strip packing:top layer:2).
Those hypercuboids form a larger hypercuboid with side lengths at least
\hat{s}
in all
(d-k)
dimensions.

This is because
A
is assumed to be a grid.
If we assume, that the grid boundaries are named
g_{i,j}
for
i\in[d]
and some values of
j
, then we find for each dimension
i
a maximal index
j_{i,-}
and a minimal index
j_{i,+}>j_{i,-}
such that
g_{i,j_{i,-}}
is smaller than the lowest point of the packed large item in the
i^{\mathrm{th}}
dimension and
g_{i,j_{i,+}}
is larger than the highest point of the packed large item in the
i^{\mathrm{th}}
dimension because the item is packed inside of the grid.
Furthermore the cells
\Set{\prod_{i=1}^{d}\left[g_{i,j_{i}},g_{i,j_{i}+1}\right]}{j_{i,-}\leq j_{i}<j_{i,+}\text{ for all }i\in[d]}
are exactly the cells of
A
that are intersected by the packed item.
Those cells have to be part of the grid
A
because otherwise the large item would not be packed into the grid.
Combining those cells creates the hypercuboid
\prod_{i=1}^{d}\left[g_{i,j_{i,-}},g_{i,j_{i,+}}\right]

Next, we round the side lengths of each other hypercuboid in
A\backslash A_{\text{merged}}
down to the next multiple of
\hat{s}\left(\mathcal{M}\right)

and we round the side lengths of the new hypercuboid
\bigcup_{a\in A_{\text{merged}}}a
down to the next multiple of
\hat{s}\left(\mathcal{M}\right)
to create rounded instances for the hypercuboids in
A_{\text{merged}}
.
This creates a rounded set
A^{\prime}
of hypercuboids where
A_{\text{merged}}^{\prime}\subseteq A^{\prime}
are the rounded instances from the hypercuboids in
A_{\text{merged}}
(see
\cref
fig:strip packing:top layer:3).
Note that each hypercuboid in
A^{\prime}
is formed from a unique hypercuboid in
A
by reducing the size in each dimension by at most
\hat{s}\left(\mathcal{M}\right)
,
this is also true for the hypercuboids in
A_{\text{merged}}^{\prime}
.
This allows us to use similar dependencies between the rounded and non-rounded instances concerning volume and surface,
as we used for the gaps in
\cref
sec:strip packing:gaps.
As a last step, we replace each
\left(d-k\right)
-dimensional hypercuboid
a
inside of
A
or
A^{\prime}
by its
\left(d-1\right)
-dimensional
extension
a\times\prod_{j=1}^{k-1}[0,b_{j}]

and define
A^{\prime\prime}:=A^{\prime}\backslash A_{\text{merged}}^{\prime}\cup\Set{\bigcup_{a\in A_{\text{merged}}^{\prime}}a}
by merging the hypercuboids in
A_{\text{merged}}^{\prime}
to a larger hypercuboid as previously announced (see
\cref
fig:strip packing:top layer:4).
Now we know that there is at least one base for a strip,
where we can pack any of the medium and small items in.

Lemma 30
.

There exists a hypercuboid
c\in A^{\prime\prime}
, such that
2\operatorname{VOL}_{d-1}\left(c\right)>\rho_{\eta}\operatorname{SURF}_{d-1}\left(c\right)
.

Proof.

Let
c=\left(\bigcup_{a\in A_{\text{merged}}^{\prime}}a\right)
be the hypercuboid we got through merging.
Let
c_{1},\dots,c_{d-1}
be the side lengths of this hypercuboid.
Because of the creation and rounding process, each of those lengths is at least
\hat{s}-\rho_{\eta}
.
We have
\operatorname{VOL}_{d-1}\left(c\right)=\prod_{j=1}^{d-1}c_{j}
. So

\displaystyle\operatorname{SURF}_{d-1}\left(c\right)=2\operatorname{VOL}_{d-1}\left(c\right)\sum_{j=1}^{d-1}\frac{1}{c_{j}}.

Thus, we only have to show that
1>\rho_{\eta}\sum_{j=1}^{d-1}1/c_{j}
. It holds that

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{d-1}\frac{\rho_{\eta}}{c_{j}}<\sum_{j=1}^{d-1}\frac{\rho_{\eta}}{\hat{s}-\rho_{\eta}}=\sum_{j=1}^{d-1}\frac{1}{\hat{s}/\rho_{\eta}-1}\leq\sum_{j=1}^{d-1}\frac{1}{\hat{s}/\rho_{1}-1}<\sum_{j=1}^{d-1}\frac{1}{d-1}=\frac{d-1}{d-1}=1.

Now we mark each hypercuboid
c\in A^{\prime\prime}
for which
2\operatorname{VOL}_{d-1}\left(c\right)>\rho_{\eta}\operatorname{SURF}_{d-1}\left(c\right)
holds, knowing that there is at least one, and set it as the base of a strip.
For each base
a\in A^{\prime\prime}
we assign a subset
\mathcal{I}_{\text{top},a}
of the remaining small and medium items and set its height
h_{\text{top},a}
according to
\cref
strip packing:top layer.

That is, we initialize
h_{\text{top},a}
as

\displaystyle h_{\text{top},a}=\frac{\rho_{\eta}\operatorname{VOL}_{d-1}\left(c\right)}{\operatorname{VOL}_{d-1}\left(c\right)-\rho_{\eta}\operatorname{SURF}_{d-1}\left(c\right)/2}

Initially all sets
\mathcal{I}_{\text{top},a}
are empty.
Note that this still leads to a nonzero initial height for each strip.
For each item
x
that needs to be assigned, we assign it to a marked base
a\in A^{\prime\prime}
for which the new height
h_{\mathrm{top},a}^{\prime}=h_{\mathrm{top},a}+\operatorname{VOL}_{d}\left(x\right)/\left(\operatorname{VOL}_{d-1}\left(c\right)-\rho_{\eta}\operatorname{SURF}_{d-1}\left(c\right)/2\right)
would be minimal to ensure that those heights stay balanced.

After all items are assigned, we define
h_{\mathrm{top}}
to be the maximal height of any strip where we packed an item in
and define
\operatorname{ext}_{\mathrm{top}}
as the extension of a
\left(d-1\right)
-dimensional hypercuboid
a
to the
d
-dimensional hypercuboid
a\times\left[0,h_{\mathrm{top}}\right]
of height
h_{\mathrm{top}}
.
We pack
\mathcal{I}_{top,a}
into
\operatorname{ext}_{\mathrm{top}}\left(a\right)
using NFDH for all
a\in A^{\prime\prime}
and create this way a new layer
L_{\mathrm{top}}
of height
h_{\mathrm{top}}
.
Now we can analyze the free volume of each individual box
\operatorname{ext}_{\mathrm{top}}\left(a\right)
.

Lemma 31
.

For all
a\in A^{\prime\prime}
the free volume inside of the box
\operatorname{ext}_{\mathrm{top}}\left(a\right)
is at most

\displaystyle\operatorname{FVOL}_{d}\left(\operatorname{ext}_{\mathrm{top}}\left(a\right)\right)\leq\rho_{\eta}\frac{\operatorname{SURF}_{d}\left(\operatorname{ext}_{\mathrm{top}}\left(a\right)\right)}{2}+\rho_{\eta}^{d}

Proof.

For the unmarked hypercuboids
a\in A^{\prime\prime}
the statement is trivial,
as they have
\operatorname{VOL}_{d-1}\left(a\right)\leq\rho_{\eta}\frac{\operatorname{SURF}_{d-1}\left(a\right)}{2}
by definition.

Therefore, let
a\in A^{\prime\prime}
be marked.
In this case the strip was packed using the method explained above.
Thus, we know that it is packed to a large height.
More precisely, we know through our balancing method,
that adding an item with side length
\rho_{\eta}

(which is the upper bound for the side length of a medium item)
would increase the height assigned to that strip through
\cref
strip packing:top layer to a larger value than
h_{\mathrm{top}}
.

\displaystyle h_{\mathrm{top}}

\displaystyle\leq h_{\mathrm{top},a}+\frac{\rho_{\eta}^{d}}{\operatorname{VOL}_{d-1}\left(a\right)-\rho_{\eta}\operatorname{SURF}_{d-1}\left(a\right)/2}

\displaystyle=\frac{\operatorname{VOL}_{d}\left(\mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{top},a}\right)+\rho_{\eta}\operatorname{VOL}_{d-1}\left(a\right)+\rho_{\eta}^{d}}{\operatorname{VOL}_{d-1}\left(a\right)-\rho_{\eta}\operatorname{SURF}_{d-1}\left(a\right)/2}

(by definition of
h_{\mathrm{top},a}
)

Hence, we obtain that

\displaystyle\operatorname{VOL}_{d-1}\left(a\right)h_{\mathrm{top}}-\rho_{\eta}\frac{\operatorname{SURF}_{d-1}\left(a\right)}{2}h_{\mathrm{top}}\leq\operatorname{VOL}_{d}\left(\mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{top},a}\right)+\rho_{\eta}\operatorname{VOL}_{d-1}\left(a\right)+\rho_{\eta}^{d}

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow\operatorname{FVOL}_{d}\left(\operatorname{ext}_{\mathrm{top}}\left(a\right)\right)\leq\rho_{\eta}\left(\frac{\operatorname{SURF}_{d-1}\left(a\right)}{2}h_{\mathrm{top}}+\operatorname{VOL}_{d-1}\left(a\right)\right)+\rho_{\eta}^{d}

By unfolding the definition for the surface we get

\displaystyle\frac{\operatorname{SURF}_{d}\left(\operatorname{ext}_{\mathrm{top}}\left(a\right)\right)}{2}=\frac{\operatorname{SURF}_{d-1}\left(a\right)}{2}h_{\mathrm{top}}+\operatorname{VOL}_{d-1}\left(a\right).

This concludes the proof.
∎

Now we can consider the free volume inside of the whole layer.

Lemma 32
.

The free volume in layer
L_{\mathrm{top}}
is at most

\displaystyle\operatorname{FVOL}_{d}\left(L_{\mathrm{top}}\right)\leq\frac{3}{2}\varepsilon h_{\mathrm{top}}\operatorname{VOL}_{k-1}\left(B\right)+\frac{\varepsilon}{d-1}\operatorname{VOL}_{k-1}\left(B\right)\hat{s}.

Proof.

We can make a proof similar to the one for
\cref
strip packing:gaps:fvol.
The layer
L_{\mathrm{top}}
can be split into the hypercuboids
\operatorname{ext}_{\mathrm{top}}\left(a\right)
for
a\in A
.
We start by relating the hypercuboids in
A
from our splitting to the volume of the hypercuboids in
A^{\prime\prime}
which we used for packing.
For the cardinalities we know
\left|A^{\prime\prime}\right|\leq\left|A^{\prime}\right|=\left|A\right|\leq N
.

Also, note that since
A^{\prime\prime}
is obtained from
A^{\prime}
by merging a few hypercuboids,

\sum_{a\in A^{\prime\prime}}\operatorname{VOL}_{d}\left(\operatorname{ext}_{\mathrm{top}}\left(a\right)\right)=\sum_{a\in A^{\prime}}\operatorname{VOL}_{d}\left(\operatorname{ext}_{\mathrm{top}}\left(a\right)\right)
.
However,
\sum_{a\in A^{\prime\prime}}\operatorname{SURF}_{d}\left(\operatorname{ext}_{\mathrm{top}}\left(a\right)\right)\leq\sum_{a\in A^{\prime}}\operatorname{SURF}_{d}\left(\operatorname{ext}_{\mathrm{top}}\left(a\right)\right)
.

\displaystyle\sum_{a\in A}\operatorname{VOL}_{d}\left(\operatorname{ext}_{\mathrm{top}}\left(a\right)\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{a\in A^{\prime}}\left(\operatorname{VOL}_{d}\left(\operatorname{ext}_{\mathrm{top}}\left(a\right)\right)+\rho_{\eta}\left(d-k\right)h_{\mathrm{top}}\left(\alpha\hat{s}\right)^{d-k-1}\operatorname{VOL}_{k-1}\left(B\right)\right)

(similar to
\cref
strip packing:gaps:rounding but with
\rho_{\eta}
and
h_{\mathrm{top}}\operatorname{VOL}_{k-1}\left(B\right)
instead of
\rho_{\eta+1}
and
x_{i}^{*}
)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle N\rho_{\eta}\operatorname{VOL}_{k-1}\left(B\right)\left(d-k\right)h_{\mathrm{top}}\left(\alpha\hat{s}\right)^{d-k-1}+\sum_{a\in A^{\prime\prime}}\operatorname{VOL}_{d}\left(\operatorname{ext}_{\mathrm{top}}\left(a\right)\right)

Similar to how we upper-bounded
\operatorname{SURF}_{d}\left(\operatorname{ext}^{\prime}(g)\right)/2
in
\cref
strip packing:gaps:fvol, instead of using
x_{i}^{*}
,
if we use
h_{\mathrm{top}}\operatorname{VOL}_{k-1}\left(B\right)
, we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{\operatorname{SURF}_{d}\left(\operatorname{ext}_{\mathrm{top}}\left(a\right)\right)}{2}\leq\operatorname{VOL}_{k-1}\left(B\right)\left(\left(d-k\right)h_{\mathrm{top}}\left(\alpha\hat{s}\right)^{d-k-1}+\left(\alpha\hat{s}\right)^{d-k}\left(h_{\mathrm{top}}\left(k-1\right)+1\right)\right)

By applying those two inequalities and
\cref
strip packing:top layer:fvol:single we can bound the free volume to:

\displaystyle\quad\operatorname{FVOL}_{d}\left(L_{\mathrm{top}}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{a\in A}\operatorname{VOL}_{d}\left(\operatorname{ext}_{\mathrm{top}}\left(a\right)\right)-\sum_{a\in A^{\prime\prime}}\operatorname{VOL}_{d}\left(\mathcal{I}_{top,a}\right)

\displaystyle\leq N\rho_{\eta}\operatorname{VOL}_{k-1}\left(B\right)\left(d-k\right)h_{\mathrm{top}}\left(\alpha\hat{s}\right)^{d-k-1}+\sum_{a\in A^{\prime\prime}}\left(\operatorname{VOL}_{d}\left(\operatorname{ext}_{\mathrm{top}}\left(a\right)\right)-\operatorname{VOL}_{d}\left(\mathcal{I}_{top,a}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\leq N\rho_{\eta}\operatorname{VOL}_{k-1}\left(B\right)\left(d-k\right)h_{\mathrm{top}}\left(\alpha\hat{s}\right)^{d-k-1}+\sum_{a\in A^{\prime\prime}}\left(\rho_{\eta}\frac{\operatorname{SURF}_{d}\left(\operatorname{ext}_{\mathrm{top}}\left(a\right)\right)}{2}+\rho_{\eta}^{d}\right)

(
\cref
strip packing:top layer:fvol:single)

\displaystyle\leq N\rho_{\eta}\operatorname{VOL}_{k-1}\left(B\right)\left(d-k\right)h_{\mathrm{top}}\left(\alpha\hat{s}\right)^{d-k-1}+\sum_{a\in A^{\prime}}\left(\rho_{\eta}\frac{\operatorname{SURF}_{d}\left(\operatorname{ext}_{\mathrm{top}}\left(a\right)\right)}{2}+\rho_{\eta}^{d}\right)

\displaystyle\leq N\rho_{\eta}\operatorname{VOL}_{k-1}\left(B\right)\left(2\left(d-k\right)h_{\mathrm{top}}\left(\alpha\hat{s}\right)^{d-k-1}+\left(\alpha\hat{s}\right)^{d-k}\left(h_{\mathrm{top}}\left(k-1\right)+1\right)\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad+N\rho_{\eta}^{d}

Now we can use the fact that
\rho_{\eta}\leq\rho_{1}
which is
\varepsilon\hat{s}/\left(2\left(d-1\right)N\left(\alpha\hat{s}\right)^{d-k}\right)
.
We use this to simplify the individual summands of this inequality.

\displaystyle N\rho_{\eta}\operatorname{VOL}_{k-1}\left(B\right)2\left(d-k\right)h_{\mathrm{top}}\left(\alpha\hat{s}\right)^{d-k-1}\leq\varepsilon h_{\mathrm{top}}\operatorname{VOL}_{k-1}\left(B\right)

\displaystyle N\rho_{\eta}\operatorname{VOL}_{k-1}\left(B\right)\left(\alpha\hat{s}\right)^{d-k}h_{\mathrm{top}}\left(k-1\right)\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\frac{k-1}{d-1}\hat{s}h_{\mathrm{top}}\operatorname{VOL}_{k-1}\left(B\right)\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{2}h_{\mathrm{top}}\operatorname{VOL}_{k-1}\left(B\right)

\displaystyle N\rho_{\eta}\operatorname{VOL}_{k-1}\left(B\right)\left(\alpha\hat{s}\right)^{d-k}\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{2\left(d-1\right)}\hat{s}\operatorname{VOL}_{k-1}\left(B\right)

\displaystyle N\rho_{\eta}^{d}\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{2\left(d-1\right)}\frac{1}{\alpha^{d-k}}\frac{\rho_{\eta}^{d-k}}{\hat{s}^{d-k}}\rho_{\eta}^{k-1}\hat{s}\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{2\left(d-1\right)}\hat{s}\operatorname{VOL}_{k-1}\left(B\right)

This sums up to the inequality

\displaystyle\operatorname{FVOL}_{d}\left(L_{\mathrm{top}}\right)\leq\frac{3}{2}\varepsilon h_{\mathrm{top}}\operatorname{VOL}_{k-1}\left(B\right)+\frac{\varepsilon}{d-1}\operatorname{VOL}_{k-1}\left(B\right)\hat{s}.

Now, that all items are packed, we extend the size of the top layer in the long dimensions by
\hat{s}
.
This way, we gain enough space to also increase the size of the boxes inside of the top layer in those dimensions, such that their side lengths are multiples of
\hat{s}\left(\mathcal{M}\right)
and they are thus
\mathcal{V}
-Boxes.

C.5
Analysis

Now we can analyze the total height
h_{\mathrm{total}}
of the packing.
We have to distinguish between the case where all of the small items are packed in the gaps and the case where some of them were packed in the top layer.
If the gaps were all filled with a large volume of small items, we can use volume arguments to find a bound on the height
h_{\mathrm{total}}
.

Lemma 33
.

If not all small items were packed into the gaps of the layers
L_{1},\dots,L_{N_{\mathrm{configs}}}
, then we have a height of

\displaystyle h_{\mathrm{total}}\leq\left(1+5\varepsilon\right)\operatorname{OPT}_{\mathrm{strip}}\left(\mathcal{I},A,B\right)+2\left(N_{\mathrm{configs}}+2\right)\hat{s}.

Proof.

Let
\operatorname{VOL}_{d-k}\left(P_{i}\right):=\sum_{j=1}^{N_{\mathrm{sizes}}}c_{i,j}\hat{s}\left(\mathcal{G}_{j}\right)^{d-k}
be the volume of the
\left(d-k\right)
-dimensional packing
P_{i}
associated with configuration
i
.
By
\cref
strip packing:large:rounding we can bound the volume of the large items:

\displaystyle\operatorname{VOL}_{d}\left(\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{up}}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{N_{\mathrm{sizes}}}\left|\mathcal{G}_{j}\right|\hat{s}\left(\mathcal{G}_{j}\right)^{d}=\sum_{j=1}^{N_{\mathrm{sizes}}}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{\mathrm{configs}}}x_{i}^{*}c_{i,j}\hat{s}\left(\mathcal{G}_{j}\right)^{d-k}=\sum_{i=1}^{N_{\mathrm{configs}}}\operatorname{VOL}_{d-k}\left(P_{i}\right)x_{i}^{*}

\displaystyle\operatorname{VOL}_{d}\left(\mathcal{L}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\operatorname{VOL}_{d}\left(\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{up}}\right)-\varepsilon\operatorname{VOL}_{d}\left(\mathcal{L}\right)-\operatorname{VOL}_{k-1}\left(B\right)\hat{s}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{N_{\mathrm{configs}}}\operatorname{VOL}_{d-k}\left(P_{i}\right)x_{i}^{*}-\varepsilon\operatorname{VOL}_{d}\left(\mathcal{L}\right)-\operatorname{VOL}_{k-1}\left(B\right)\hat{s}.

We can bound the volume
V_{1}
of the small items that were packed between the large items using
\cref
strip packing:gaps:fvol:

\displaystyle V_{1}

\displaystyle:=\sum_{i=1}^{N_{\mathrm{configs}}}\left(\operatorname{VOL}_{d}\left(L_{i}\right)-\operatorname{FVOL}_{d}\left(L_{i}\right)-\operatorname{VOL}_{d-k}\left(P_{i}\right)x_{i}^{*}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{i=1}^{N_{\mathrm{configs}}}\left(x_{i}^{*}-\frac{3}{2}\varepsilon x_{i}^{*}-\frac{\varepsilon}{d-1}\operatorname{VOL}_{k-1}\left(B\right)\hat{s}-\operatorname{VOL}_{d-k}\left(P_{i}\right)x_{i}^{*}\right)

(using
\operatorname{VOL}_{d}\left(L_{i}\right)=x_{i}^{*}
)

\displaystyle=\left(1-\frac{3}{2}\varepsilon\right)\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N_{\mathrm{configs}}}h_{i}\right)\operatorname{VOL}_{k-1}\left(B\right)-N_{\mathrm{configs}}\frac{\varepsilon}{d-1}\operatorname{VOL}_{k-1}\left(B\right)\hat{s}-\sum_{i=1}^{N_{\mathrm{configs}}}\operatorname{VOL}_{d-k}\left(P_{i}\right)x_{i}^{*}

(using
h_{i}=x_{i}^{*}/\operatorname{VOL}_{k-1}\left(B\right)
)

\displaystyle>\left(1-\frac{3}{2}\varepsilon\right)\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N_{\mathrm{configs}}}h_{i}\right)\operatorname{VOL}_{k-1}\left(B\right)-N_{\mathrm{configs}}\frac{3}{2}\varepsilon\operatorname{VOL}_{k-1}\left(B\right)\hat{s}-\sum_{i=1}^{N_{\mathrm{configs}}}\operatorname{VOL}_{d-k}\left(P_{i}\right)x_{i}^{*}.

The volume
V_{2}
of the remaining small and medium items is located in the top layer and we can bound it using
\cref
strip packing:top layer:fvol:

\displaystyle V_{2}

\displaystyle:=\operatorname{VOL}_{d}\left(L_{\mathrm{top}}\right)-\operatorname{FVOL}_{d}\left(L_{\mathrm{top}}\right)

\displaystyle\geq h_{\mathrm{top}}\operatorname{VOL}_{k-1}\left(B\right)-\frac{3}{2}\varepsilon h_{\mathrm{top}}\operatorname{VOL}_{k-1}\left(B\right)-\frac{\varepsilon}{d-1}\operatorname{VOL}_{k-1}\left(B\right)\hat{s}

\displaystyle=\left(1-\frac{3}{2}\varepsilon\right)h_{\mathrm{top}}\operatorname{VOL}_{k-1}\left(B\right)-\frac{\varepsilon}{d-1}\operatorname{VOL}_{k-1}\left(B\right)\hat{s}

\displaystyle>\left(1-\frac{3}{2}\varepsilon\right)h_{\mathrm{top}}\operatorname{VOL}_{k-1}\left(B\right)-\frac{3}{2}\varepsilon\operatorname{VOL}_{k-1}\left(B\right)\hat{s}.

Recall that
h_{\mathrm{total}}=\sum_{i=1}^{N_{\mathrm{configs}}}\left(h_{i}+\hat{s}\right)+h_{\mathrm{top}}+\hat{s}
.
The bound on the total volume of small and medium items is therefore:

\displaystyle\quad\operatorname{VOL}_{d}\left(\mathcal{S}\right)+\operatorname{VOL}_{d}\left(\mathcal{M}\right)

\displaystyle=V_{1}+V_{2}

\displaystyle>\left(1-\frac{3}{2}\varepsilon\right)\left(h_{\mathrm{total}}-\left(N_{\mathrm{configs}}+1\right)\hat{s}\right)\operatorname{VOL}_{k-1}\left(B\right)

\displaystyle\quad-\left(N_{\mathrm{configs}}+1\right)\frac{3}{2}\varepsilon\operatorname{VOL}_{k-1}\left(B\right)\hat{s}-\sum_{i=1}^{N_{\mathrm{configs}}}\operatorname{VOL}_{d-k}\left(P_{i}\right)x_{i}^{*}

\displaystyle=\left(1-\frac{3}{2}\varepsilon\right)h_{\mathrm{total}}\operatorname{VOL}_{k-1}\left(B\right)-\left(N_{\mathrm{configs}}+1\right)\left(1-\frac{3}{2}\varepsilon\right)\operatorname{VOL}_{k-1}\left(B\right)\hat{s}

\displaystyle\quad-\left(N_{\mathrm{configs}}+1\right)\frac{3}{2}\varepsilon\operatorname{VOL}_{k-1}\left(B\right)\hat{s}-\sum_{i=1}^{N_{\mathrm{configs}}}\operatorname{VOL}_{d-k}\left(P_{i}\right)x_{i}^{*}

\displaystyle=\left(1-\frac{3}{2}\varepsilon\right)h_{\mathrm{total}}\operatorname{VOL}_{k-1}\left(B\right)-\left(N_{\mathrm{configs}}+1\right)\operatorname{VOL}_{k-1}\left(B\right)\hat{s}-\sum_{i=1}^{N_{\mathrm{configs}}}\operatorname{VOL}_{d-k}\left(P_{i}\right)x_{i}^{*}.

Thus, we have a bound on the volume of all items:

\displaystyle\left(1+\varepsilon\right)\operatorname{VOL}_{d}\left(\mathcal{I}\right)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{VOL}_{d}\left(\mathcal{L}\right)+\operatorname{VOL}_{d}\left(\mathcal{S}\right)+\operatorname{VOL}_{d}\left(\mathcal{M}\right)+\varepsilon\operatorname{VOL}_{d}\left(\mathcal{I}\right)

\displaystyle>\sum_{i=1}^{N_{\mathrm{configs}}}\operatorname{VOL}_{d-k}\left(P_{i}\right)x_{i}^{*}-\varepsilon\operatorname{VOL}_{d}\left(\mathcal{L}\right)-\operatorname{VOL}_{k-1}\left(B\right)\hat{s}

\displaystyle\quad+\left(1-\frac{3}{2}\varepsilon\right)h_{\mathrm{total}}\operatorname{VOL}_{k-1}\left(B\right)-\left(N_{\mathrm{configs}}+1\right)\operatorname{VOL}_{k-1}\left(B\right)\hat{s}

\displaystyle\quad-\sum_{i=1}^{N_{\mathrm{configs}}}\operatorname{VOL}_{d-k}\left(P_{i}\right)x_{i}^{*}+\varepsilon\operatorname{VOL}_{d}\left(\mathcal{I}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\left(1-\frac{3}{2}\varepsilon\right)h_{\mathrm{total}}\operatorname{VOL}_{k-1}\left(B\right)-\left(N_{\mathrm{configs}}+2\right)\operatorname{VOL}_{k-1}\left(B\right)\hat{s}.

This gives us the claimed bound on the height. It holds that

\displaystyle h_{\mathrm{total}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1+\varepsilon}{1-\frac{3}{2}\varepsilon}\frac{\operatorname{VOL}_{d}\left(\mathcal{I}\right)}{\operatorname{VOL}_{k-1}\left(B\right)}+\frac{1}{1-\frac{3}{2}\varepsilon}\left(N_{\mathrm{configs}}+2\right)\hat{s}

\displaystyle\leq\left(1+5\varepsilon\right)\operatorname{OPT}_{\mathrm{strip}}\left(\mathcal{I},A,B\right)+2\left(N_{\mathrm{configs}}+2\right)\hat{s}.

For the last step we use
2\left(1+\varepsilon\right)/\left(2-3\varepsilon\right)\leq 1+5\varepsilon
for
\varepsilon\leq 1/3

and the fact that

\displaystyle\operatorname{OPT}_{\mathrm{strip}}\left(\mathcal{I},A,B\right)\geq\frac{\operatorname{VOL}_{d}\left(\mathcal{I}\right)}{\operatorname{VOL}_{d-k}\left(A\right)\operatorname{VOL}_{k-1}\left(B\right)}=\frac{\operatorname{VOL}_{d}\left(\mathcal{I}\right)}{\operatorname{VOL}_{k-1}\left(B\right)}.

If all small items were packed in the gaps, the top layer only contains items of medium size.
By choice of
\eta
, we know that the medium items have a small total volume.
Thus we can argue that the height of
h_{\mathrm{top}}
of the top layer is small, which also gives us a good bound on
h_{\mathrm{total}}
.

Lemma 34
.

If all small items were packed into the gaps of the layers
L_{1},\dots,L_{N_{\mathrm{configs}}}
, then we have a height of

\displaystyle h_{\mathrm{total}}\leq\left(1+\left(2^{k-1}+2\right)\varepsilon\right)\operatorname{OPT}_{\mathrm{strip}}\left(\mathcal{I},A,B\right)+\left(N_{\mathrm{configs}}+3\right)\hat{s}.

Proof.

By the choice of
\mathcal{M}
and
\cref
strip packing:top layer:fvol we have

\displaystyle\varepsilon\operatorname{VOL}_{d}\left(\mathcal{I}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\operatorname{VOL}_{d}\left(\mathcal{M}\right)=\operatorname{VOL}_{d}\left(L_{\mathrm{top}}\right)-\operatorname{FVOL}_{d}\left(L_{\mathrm{top}}\right)

\displaystyle\geq h_{\mathrm{top}}\operatorname{VOL}_{k-1}\left(B\right)-\frac{3}{2}\varepsilon h_{\mathrm{top}}\operatorname{VOL}_{k-1}\left(B\right)-\frac{\varepsilon}{d-1}\operatorname{VOL}_{k-1}\left(B\right)\hat{s}.

This gives us a bound on the height of the top layer:

\displaystyle h_{\mathrm{top}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{1-\frac{3}{2}\varepsilon}\frac{\operatorname{VOL}_{d}\left(\mathcal{I}\right)}{\operatorname{VOL}_{k-1}\left(B\right)}+\frac{\varepsilon}{1-\frac{3}{2}\varepsilon}\frac{1}{d-1}\hat{s}

\displaystyle\leq 2\varepsilon\operatorname{OPT}_{\mathrm{strip}}\left(\mathcal{I},A,B\right)+\frac{2}{3\left(d-1\right)}\hat{s}

\displaystyle<2\varepsilon\operatorname{OPT}_{\mathrm{strip}}\left(\mathcal{I},A,B\right)+\hat{s}.

By applying this, we can prove the claimed bound on the total height:

\displaystyle h_{\mathrm{total}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{N_{\mathrm{configs}}}h_{i}+h_{\mathrm{top}}+\left(N_{\mathrm{configs}}+1\right)\hat{s}

\displaystyle\leq\operatorname{OPT}_{\mathrm{strip}}\left(\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{up}},A,B\right)+2\varepsilon\operatorname{OPT}_{\mathrm{strip}}\left(\mathcal{I},A,B\right)+\left(N_{\mathrm{configs}}+2\right)\hat{s}

(using
\cref
strip packing:large:lp)

\displaystyle\leq\left(1+\left(2+2^{k-1}\right)\varepsilon\right)\operatorname{OPT}_{\mathrm{strip}}\left(\mathcal{I},A,B\right)+\left(N_{\mathrm{configs}}+3\right)\hat{s}

(using
\cref
strip packing:large:rounding and
\mathcal{L}\subseteq\mathcal{I}
)

∎

The only part missing for the proof of
\cref
knapsack:structure:strip packing is a constant bound on
N_{\mathrm{configs}}
.

Lemma 35
.

The numbers
\rho_{\eta}
,
N_{\mathrm{sizes}}
and
N_{\mathrm{configs}}
can be bounded by constants that are only dependent on
d
,
k
,
\alpha
,
N
and
\varepsilon
.

Proof.

In the following we will define new constants formally as functions over the constants
d
,
k
,
\alpha
,
N
and
\varepsilon
received from the problem definition to show their dependencies.
As those values never change throughout this proof, we will however skip those arguments when using the newly defined constants to gain clearer formulas.
First of all we can bound
\rho_{1}
in the following way:

\displaystyle\rho_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{\varepsilon\hat{s}}{2\left(d-1\right)N\left(\alpha\hat{s}\right)^{d-k}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\varepsilon}{2\left(d-1\right)N\alpha^{d-k}\hat{s}^{d-k-1}}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\varepsilon}{2\left(d-1\right)N\alpha^{d-k}}

(using
\hat{s}\leq 1
)

\displaystyle=:C_{\mathrm{\rho,1}}\left(d,k,\alpha,N,\varepsilon\right).

Now we can bound
\rho_{i}\geq C_{\mathrm{\rho,i}}
with

\displaystyle C_{\mathrm{\rho,i}}\left(d,k,\alpha,N,\varepsilon\right):=\frac{C_{\mathrm{\rho,1}}^{i\left(d-k\right)^{2i-2}}}{2^{\left(i-1\right)\left(d-k\right)^{2i-3}}}.

We prove it by induction. For
i=1
the new definition of
C_{\mathrm{\rho,1}}
is consistent with the old one and thus the bound is already shown above.
For
i+1
we can prove the bound with

\displaystyle\rho_{i+1}

\displaystyle=\rho_{1}\frac{\rho_{i}^{\left(d-k\right)^{2}}}{2^{d-k}}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{C_{\mathrm{\rho,1}}}{2^{d-k}}\left(\frac{C_{\mathrm{\rho,1}}^{i\left(d-k\right)^{2i-2}}}{2^{\left(i-1\right)\left(d-k\right)^{2i-3}}}\right)^{\left(d-k\right)^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{C_{\mathrm{\rho,1}}}{2^{d-k}}\frac{C_{\mathrm{\rho,1}}^{i\left(d-k\right)^{2i}}}{2^{\left(i-1\right)\left(d-k\right)^{2i-1}}}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{C_{\mathrm{\rho,1}}^{\left(i+1\right)\left(d-k\right)^{2i}}}{2^{i\left(d-k\right)^{2i-1}}}

\displaystyle=C_{\mathrm{\rho,i+1}}.

Using
\eta\leq\left\lceil 1/\varepsilon\right\rceil
we get

\rho_{\eta}\geq C_{\mathrm{\rho,\left\lceil 1/\varepsilon\right\rceil}}=:C_{\mathrm{\rho}}\left(d,k,\alpha,N,\varepsilon\right)
.
With this bound we can bound the number of sizes to which we round the large items to

\displaystyle N_{\mathrm{sizes}}=\left\lceil\frac{1}{\varepsilon\rho_{\eta}^{d}}\right\rceil\leq\frac{1}{\varepsilon C_{\mathrm{\rho}}^{d}}+1=:C_{\mathrm{sizes}}\left(d,k,\alpha,N,\varepsilon\right).

The number of configurations can now be bounded by

\displaystyle N_{\mathrm{configs}}\leq\left(\frac{1}{\rho_{\eta}^{d-k}}+1\right)^{N_{\mathrm{sizes}}}\leq\left(\frac{1}{C_{\mathrm{\rho}}^{d-k}}+1\right)^{C_{\mathrm{sizes}}}=:C_{\mathrm{configs}}\left(d,k,\alpha,N,\varepsilon\right).

∎

Note, that we have the following property which is important in
\cref
sec:knapsack.

Lemma 36
.

C_{\mathrm{configs}}>1/C_{\mathrm{\rho,1}}>N\alpha^{d-k}\geq\alpha
.

Proof.

\displaystyle C_{\mathrm{configs}}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{1}{C_{\mathrm{\rho}}^{d-k}}+1\right)^{C_{\mathrm{sizes}}}

(From definition of
C_{\mathrm{configs}}
)

\displaystyle\geq{1}/{C_{\mathrm{\rho}}}

(As
C_{\mathrm{\rho}}\leq 1,C_{\mathrm{sizes}}\geq 1
)

\displaystyle\geq 1/C_{\mathrm{\rho,1}}

(As
C_{\mathrm{\rho,1}}\geq C_{\mathrm{\rho}}
)

\displaystyle=2(d-1)N\alpha^{d-k}/\varepsilon

(From definition of
C_{\mathrm{\rho,1}}
)

\displaystyle>N\alpha^{d-k}

(As
d\geq 2,\varepsilon<1
)

\displaystyle\geq\alpha

(As
N\geq 1,\alpha\geq 1
)

∎

Lemma 37
.

The strip packing algorithm of
\cref
sec:strip packing packs all items in
\mathcal{N}
-Boxes and
\mathcal{V}
-Boxes.
The number of
\mathcal{N}
-Boxes is bounded by

C_{\mathrm{configs}}/C_{\rho}^{d-k}

and the number of
\mathcal{V}
-Boxes is bounded by

C_{\mathrm{configs}}N2^{d-k}/C_{\mathrm{\rho}}^{\left(d-k\right)^{2}}+N
.

Proof.

Note that the strip packing algorithm creates
\mathcal{N}
-Boxes only at the end of
\cref
sec:strip packing:large.
In this step, one
\mathcal{N}
-Box is created for each large item in each configuration.
As mentioned before, the volume of
A
is
1
and each large item has a side length of at least
\rho_{\eta}
.
Thus, there are at most
1/C_{\mathrm{\rho}}^{d-k}
large items per configuration and there are at most
C_{\mathrm{configs}}
configurations.
This results in an upper bound of
C_{\mathrm{configs}}/C_{\rho}^{d-k}
for the number of
\mathcal{N}
-Boxes.

\mathcal{V}
-Boxes are created in two steps of the algorithm.
The first step is stated in
\cref
sec:strip packing:gaps where we create a
\mathcal{V}
-Box for each gap in each layer that was created for the large items.
There is one layer for each configuration and in the beginning of
\cref
sec:strip packing:gaps, the number of gaps per layer is bounded by
2^{d-k}/\rho_{\eta}^{\left(d-k\right)^{2}}
.
Thus, there are at most
C_{\mathrm{configs}}N\left(2^{d-k}/C_{\mathrm{\rho}}^{\left(d-k\right)^{2}}\right)

\mathcal{V}
-Boxes created in this step.
The second step where
\mathcal{V}
-Boxes are created is in
\cref
sec:strip packing:top where we place the remaining small and medium items in an additional layer.
There, the number of
\mathcal{V}
-Boxes is bounded by the number of cells in
A
which is bounded by
N
.
This concludes our proof.
∎
