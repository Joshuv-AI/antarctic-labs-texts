Thresholds for the monochromatic clique transversal game
Csilla Bujtás, Pakanun Dokyeesun, Sandi Klavžar

Abstract
We study a recently introduced two-person combinatorial game, the ( a , b ) (a,b) -monochromatic clique transversal game which is played by Alice and Bob on a graph G G . As we observe, this game is equivalent to the ( b , a ) (b,a) -biased Maker-Breaker game played on the clique-hypergraph of G G . Our main results concern the threshold bias a 1 ​ ( G ) a_{1}(G) that is the smallest integer a a such that Alice can win in the ( a , 1 ) (a,1) -monochromatic clique transversal game on G G if she is the first to play. Among other results, we determine the possible values of a 1 ​ ( G ) a_{1}(G) for the disjoint union of graphs, prove a formula for a 1 ​ ( G ) a_{1}(G) if G G is triangle-free, and obtain the exact values of a 1 ​ ( C n ​ □ ​ C m ) a_{1}(C_{n}\,\square\,C_{m}) , a 1 ​ ( C n ​ □ ​ P m ) a_{1}(C_{n}\,\square\,P_{m}) , and a 1 ​ ( P n ​ □ ​ P m ) a_{1}(P_{n}\,\square\,P_{m}) for all possible pairs ( n , m ) (n,m) .

Introduction

The
monochromatic transversal game
(MT game), as introduced by Mendes et al.
[
16
]
, is a positional game on a hypergraph
{\cal H}
. The two players alternately choose (play) an unplayed vertex and Alice colors it red while Bob colors the chosen vertex blue. Alice wins if she obtains a red transversal (vertex cover) in
{\cal H}
and Bob wins if he colors all vertices of a hyperedge with blue. It is called Alice-start game or Bob-start game, respectively, if Alice or Bob is the first to play. The
monochromatic clique-transversal game
(MCT game) is played on the clique-hypergraph
{\cal H}_{G}
of a graph
G
. For the sake of simplicity, we will usually refer to an MCT game as being played on
G
instead of
{\cal H}_{G}
. The
(a,b)
-monochromatic clique-transversal game
(
(a,b)
-MCT game) follows the rules of the MCT game, but Alice (resp. Bob) colors
a
vertices (resp.
b
vertices) in each of her (resp. his) turn.

The introductory paper
[
16
]
studied the
(a,b)
-MCT game on the clique-hypergraphs of powers of cycles and paths, observing also some basic properties of the game.

1.1
Standard definitions

A
hypergraph

{\cal H}
is a set system over the vertex set
V({\cal H})
. The hyperedge set
E({\cal H})
of
{\cal H}
may contain any nonempty subset of
V({\cal H})
i.e.,
E({\cal H})\subseteq 2^{V({\cal H})}\setminus\{\emptyset\}
. It is clear that every simple graph can be considered as a (2-uniform) hypergraph. A hypergraph is
simple
, if no hyperedge
e\in{\cal H}
is a subset of a different
e^{\prime}\in E({\cal H})
. The
maximum vertex degree
\Delta({\cal H})
of
{\cal H}
is the maximum number of hyperedges incident to a vertex in
{\cal H}
. A
singleton
is a hyperedge of cardinality
1
.

A set
S\subseteq V({\cal H})
is a
transversal
(also called vertex cover or hitting set) in the hypergraph
{\cal H}
, if
S
contains at least one vertex from each hyperedge
e\in E({\cal H})
. Further,
S
is a
minimal transversal
if it contains no transversal as a proper subset. The
transversal hypergraph

Tr({\cal H})
of
{\cal H}
is defined on the vertex set
V({\cal H})
and the hyperedges correspond to the minimal transversals of
{\cal H}
. As it has been stated already in Berge’s fundamental book
[
2
]
, every simple hypergraph
{\cal H}
satisfies
{\cal H}=Tr(Tr({\cal H}))
.

In a graph
G
, a
clique
is a complete subgraph that is inclusion-wise maximal.
The
clique-hypergraph

{\cal H}_{G}
of
G
is defined on the same vertex set as
G
while its hyperedges correspond to the vertex sets of the cliques in
G
, cf.
[
4
,
13
]
. Note that every isolated vertex in
G
corresponds to a singleton in
{\cal H}_{G}
. Moreover, if
G
is triangle-free and contains no isolated vertices, then
{\cal H}_{G}\cong G
.

For a non-negative integer
k
, a
k
-independent set

S\subseteq V(G)
is a set of vertices in a graph
G
such that the maximum degree of the subgraph induced by
S
is at most
k

[
5
,
6
,
14
]
. The
k
-independence number
of
G
is the maximum cardinality of a
k
-independent set in
G
, and is denoted by
\alpha_{k}(G)
. The
0
-independence number
\alpha_{0}(G)
is the independence number
\alpha(G)
of the graph.

The
Cartesian product

G\,\square\,H
of graphs
G
and
H
is defined on the vertex set
V(G)\times V(H)
such that two vertices
(g,h)
and
(g^{\prime},h^{\prime})
are adjacent if either
gg^{\prime}\in E(G)
and
h=h^{\prime}
, or
g=g^{\prime}
and
hh^{\prime}\in E(H)
. If
h\in V(H)
, then the subgraph of
G\,\square\,H
induced by the vertex set
\{(g,h):\ g\in V(G)\}
is isomorphic to
G
, called a
G
-layer
, and denoted with
G^{h}
. Analogously the
H
-layers are defined and denoted with
{}^{g}\!H
for a fixed vertex
g\in V(G)
. (See
[
12
]
for more details on Cartesian products.)

The
disjoint union

G\mathbin{\mathchoice{\vtop{\halign{\hfil$\m@th\displaystyle#$\hfil\cr\cup\cr\cdot\crcr}}}{\vtop{\halign{\hfil$\m@th\textstyle#$\hfil\cr\cup\cr\cdot\crcr}}}{\vtop{\halign{\hfil$\m@th\scriptstyle#$\hfil\cr\cup\cr\cdot\crcr}}}{\vtop{\halign{\hfil$\m@th\scriptscriptstyle#$\hfil\cr\cup\cr\cdot\crcr}}}}H
of (disjoint) graphs
G
and
H
has the vertex set
V(G)\cup V(H)
and the edge set
E(G)\cup E(H)
. The
domination number

\gamma(G)
of a graph
G
is the cardinality of a smallest dominating set, that is, a set
X\subseteq V(G)
such that each vertex from
V(G)\setminus X
has a neighbor in
X
.
P_{n}
and
C_{n}
respectively stand for the path and cycle graph on
n
vertices. The order of the graph
G
will be denoted by
n(G)
. The open neighborhood and the closed neighborhood of a vertex
v
in
G
will be respectively denoted by
N(v)
and
N[v]
. For a set
S\subseteq V(G)
we define
N[S]=\bigcup_{v\in S}N[v]
. For every positive integer
k
,
[k]
stands for the set
\{1,\ldots,k\}
.

1.2
Games

The
Maker-Breaker game,
introduced by Erdős and Selfridge
[
8
]
, is played by Maker and Breaker on a hypergraph
{\cal H}
. (For different instances of the Maker-Breaker game see
[
7
,
9
,
10
,
18
]
.) The vertex set
V({\cal H})
is called the
board
and the hyperedges of
{\cal H}
are the
winning sets
. The two players alternately choose (i.e., play) an unplayed vertex. We say that a player
claims
the set
S\subseteq V({\cal H})
if he (or she) plays all vertices from
S
. It is a
Maker-start game
(resp.
Breaker-start game
) if Maker (resp. Breaker) is the first player.

Maker wins
the game if he claims a winning set, while
Breaker wins
if she can prevent Maker from doing this. The latter equivalently means that Breaker plays at least one vertex from each winning set, that is, she claims a minimal transversal of
{\cal H}
. The following observation was stated in
[
3
]
:

Observation 1.1

A Maker-Breaker game on
{\cal H}
with a player A as Maker and a player B as Breaker is the same as the Maker-Breaker game on
Tr({\cal H})
where the roles of A and B are switched.

By comparing the definitions, it is easy to see that an MT game on
{\cal H}
can always be considered as a Maker-Breaker game on
Tr({\cal H})
where Alice is Maker and Bob is Breaker. By Observation
1.1
, we may infer the following statements that give us a useful approach in our study.

Observation 1.2

(i)

An MT game on a hypergraph
{\cal H}
is the same as the Maker-Breaker game on
{\cal H}
where Maker corresponds to Bob and Breaker corresponds to Alice.

(ii)

An
(a,b)
-MCT game on
G
is the same as the
(b,a)
-biased Maker-Breaker game on
{\cal H}_{G}
where Maker is Bob and Breaker is Alice.

For a graph
G
and a fixed positive integer
\ell
, the
threshold bias
(or shortly threshold)
a_{\ell}(G)
(resp.
a_{\ell}^{\prime}(G)
) denotes the smallest positive integer
a
such that Alice can win in the Alice-start (resp. Bob-start)
(a,\ell)
-MCT game. By definition, if
a<a_{\ell}(G)
, then Bob has a winning strategy in the Alice-start
(a,\ell)
-MCT game on
G
. It is also known (see e.g., Exercise 3.6.2 in
[
11
]
) that for every
a\geq a_{\ell}(G)
, Alice wins in the Alice-start
(a,\ell)
-MCT game. The same is true for the threshold
a_{\ell}^{\prime}(G)
that is, Alice wins in the Bob-start
(a,\ell)
-MCT game on
G
if and only if
a\geq a_{\ell}^{\prime}(G)
.

It can be proved via different simple approaches (see e.g., Proposition 2.1.6 in

If there exists a winning strategy for Alice (resp. Bob) in the Bob-start (resp. Alice-start)

-MCT game on a graph

.

Observation

and the definition of the threshold bias directly implies the following relation.

Observation 1.4

It holds for every graph
G
that

a_{1}^{\prime}(G)\geq a_{1}(G)\,.

(1)

In the rest of the paper we will also refer to the following statements.

Observation 1.5

(i)

If a hypergraph
{\cal H}
contains no singletons, then Breaker has a winning strategy in the
(1,\Delta({\cal H}))
-biased Maker-Breaker game on
{\cal H}
no matter who is the first player.

(ii)

Let

be an isolate-free graph. If each vertex belongs to at most

-MCT game on

(iii)

If

a_{1}(G)\leq a_{1}^{\prime}(G)\leq k
.

Proof.
In the

-biased Maker-Breaker game on
{\cal H}
, Breaker may reply to Maker’s every move

(and some additional vertices if necessary). This strategy ensures that whenever Maker plays a second vertex from some hyperedge, at least one vertex from this hyperedge has been already played by Breaker. Then, as

By Observation

2
Thresholds for triangle-free graphs

In this section we focus on triangle-free graphs that do not contain isolated vertices. For such a graph
G
, the clique hypergraph
{\cal H}_{G}
is isomorphic to
G
and therefore, by Observation
1.2
(ii), Bob wins the
(a,b)
-MCT game on
G
if and only if he claims two adjacent vertices.

Theorem 2.1

If
G
is a triangle-free graph that contains no isolated vertex, then

\displaystyle(i)\quad

\displaystyle a_{1}(G)=\min_{k\geq 0}\max\{k,n(G)-\alpha_{k}(G)\}=\min_{X\subseteq V(G)}\max\{\Delta(G-X),|X|\}\,,

\displaystyle(ii)\quad

\displaystyle a_{1}^{\prime}(G)=\Delta(G)\,.

Proof.
To establish (i), we are going to prove three claims.

Claim 1

If
\displaystyle a\geq\min_{k\geq 0}\max\{k,n(G)-\alpha_{k}(G)\}
, then Alice wins in the
(a,1)
-MCT game.

Proof.

Alice chooses a maximum
k^{*}
-independent set
Y
such that

\max\{k^{*},n(G)-\alpha_{k^{*}}(G)\}=\min_{k\geq 0}\max\{k,n(G)-\alpha_{k}(G)\}.

Observe that, by our condition, the set
X=V(G)\setminus Y
contains at most
a
vertices. In the first turn, Alice plays all vertices from
X
and further
a-|X|
arbitrarily chosen vertices if
a>|X|
. Since
Y
is a
k^{*}
-independent set,
\Delta(G[Y])\leq k^{*}\leq a
; that is each vertex played by Bob in his first turn or later would have at most
a
unplayed neighbors. Alice thus can reply to each move
v
of Bob by claiming all the unplayed neighbors of
v
(and some further vertices if
a
is big enough). With this strategy, Alice ensures that Bob can never claim two adjacent vertices and Alice’s moves form a vertex cover that is a clique transversal in the isolate-free graph
G
.
(
\Box
)

Claim 2

If
a<\displaystyle\min_{X\subseteq V(G)}\max\{\Delta(G-X),|X|\}
, then Bob wins in the
(a,1)
-MCT game.

Proof.
Let
A_{1}
be the set of the
a
vertices played by Alice in the first turn. Under the present condition,
a<\max\{\Delta(G-A_{1}),|A_{1}|\}
. As
|A_{1}|=a
, we may infer
1\leq a<\Delta(G-A_{1})
. Consequently, in his first turn, Bob can play a vertex
v
such that
\deg_{G-A_{1}}(v)>a
. Then, Alice cannot claim all the unplayed neighbors of
v
in her next turn and Bob wins in his second turn by claiming an unplayed neighbor of
v
.
(
\Box
)

Claim 3

For every graph
G

\min_{X\subseteq V(G)}\max\{\Delta(G-X),|X|\}=\min_{k\geq 0}\max\{k,n(G)-\alpha_{k}(G)\}.

Proof.
Let
k^{*}
be the smallest integer such that

\max\{k^{*},n(G)-\alpha_{k^{*}}(G)\}=\min_{k\geq 0}\max\{k,n(G)-\alpha_{k}(G)\}.

We select a maximum
k^{*}
-independent set
Y^{*}
and define
X^{*}=V(G)\setminus Y^{*}
. By the choice of
k^{*}
and
Y^{*}
, we have
|X^{*}|=n(G)-\alpha_{k^{*}}(G)
and
\Delta(G-X^{*})=k^{*}
. This gives

\max\{\Delta(G-X^{*}),|X^{*}|\}=\max\{k^{*},n(G)-\alpha_{k^{*}}(G)\}=\min_{k\geq 0}\max\{k,n(G)-\alpha_{k}(G)\}

and, as
X^{*}\subseteq V(G)
, we conclude

\min_{X\subseteq V(G)}\max\{\Delta(G-X),|X|\}\leq\min_{k\geq 0}\max\{k,n(G)-\alpha_{k}(G)\}.

To prove the other direction, let
X^{\prime}
be a set of vertices in
G
such that

\max\{\Delta(G-X^{\prime}),|X^{\prime}|\}=\min_{X\subseteq V(G)}\max\{\Delta(G-X),|X|\}.

(2)

From the possible candidates for the role of
X^{\prime}
we choose one of minimum cardinality. This ensures that
Y^{\prime}=V(G)\setminus X^{\prime}
is a maximum
k^{\prime}
-independent set with
k^{\prime}=\Delta(G-X^{\prime})
. Note that

|X^{\prime}|=n(G)-\alpha_{k^{\prime}}(G)
also follows and (
2
) remains true. We therefore obtain

\min_{X\subseteq V(G)}\max\{\Delta(G-X),|X|\}=\max\{k^{\prime},n(G)-\alpha_{k^{\prime}}(G)\}\geq\min_{k\geq 0}\max\{k,n(G)-\alpha_{k}(G)\}

that completes the proof of the claim.
(
\Box
)

Claims
1

3
directly imply that the threshold stated in (i) holds for every triangle- and isolate-free graph.

To prove (ii), we consider the Bob-start
(a,1)
-MCT game on
G
. As the graph is isolate-free, Bob cannot win in his first turn. If
a<\Delta(G)
, Bob plays a vertex

, Alice cannot claim all vertices from

in her response and hence, Bob can win the game in his second turn by playing an unplayed vertex which is adjacent to

Under the present conditions, every clique of

(iii) implies

Case 3:

Torus graphs

In this subsection we prove:

Theorem 4.1

If

, then

\displaystyle a_{1}(C_{n}\,\square\,C_{m})=\begin{cases}1;&n=m=3,\\
2;&n=3,m=4,\\
3;&n=3,m\geq 5,\\
4;&m\geq n\geq 4,\end{cases}

and

\displaystyle a_{1}^{\prime}(C_{n}\,\square\,C_{m})=\begin{cases}1;&n=m=3,\\
3;&n=3,m\geq 4,\\
4;&m\geq n\geq 4.\end{cases}

Proof.
Consider first the case
m\geq n\geq 4
and recall that
V(C_{n}\,\square\,C_{m})=\{(i,j):\ i\in[n],j\in[m]\}
. Then
C_{n}\,\square\,C_{m}
is a 4-regular, triangle-free graph, hence Theorem
2.1
(ii) implies
a^{\prime}_{1}(C_{n}\,\square\,C_{m})=4
. Thus by (
1
),
a_{1}(C_{n}\,\square\,C_{m})\leq a^{\prime}_{1}(C_{n}\,\square\,C_{m})=4
.

It remains to show that
a_{1}(C_{n}\,\square\,C_{m})\geq 4
. For this sake consider the following winning strategy for Bob in the Alice-start
(3,1)
-MCT game on
C_{n}\,\square\,C_{m}
. Let
A_{1}
be the set of three vertices played by Alice in her first move. Since we have assumed that
m\geq n\geq 4
, there exist
i\in[n]
and
j\in[m]
such that
A_{1}\cap V((C_{n})^{j})=\emptyset
and
A_{1}\cap V(^{i}(C_{m}))=\emptyset
. Then Bob plays
(i,j)
in his first move. Since
(i,j)
is adjacent to four unplayed vertices, Alice cannot claim all these vertices in her next turn. Therefore Bob can win by playing an unplayed neighbor of
(i,j)
. This finishes the proof for
m\geq n\geq 4
.

In the rest of the proof we assume that
n=3
and consider the
(a,1)
-MCT game on
C_{3}\,\square\,C_{m}
.

Case 1:

m=3
.

Consider the Bob-start
(1,1)
-MCT game on
C_{3}\,\square\,C_{3}
. By symmetry we may assume without loss of generality that the first move of Bob is
(1,1)
. Then Alice replies by the move
(2,1)
. Then, using symmetry again, we need to consider four cases for a possible second move of Bob:
(1,2)
,
(2,2)
,
(3,2)
, and
(3,1)
. If Bob plays
(1,2)

. Whatever Bob plays next, Alice replies in the layer
(C_{3})^{2}
and later she can finish the game with a move in
{}^{3}(C_{3})
. In all the other possible second moves of Bob, Alice replies by playing
(1,2)
and then the only layers in which Alice has not played yet are
(C_{3})^{3}
and
{}^{3}(C_{3})
. Then it is straightforward to see that in all the cases Alice can win the game. We conclude that

Case 2:

Since each vertex is incident with three cliques (one

Consider the Bob-start

Next we consider the Alice-start
(2,1)

. Then Bob replies by playing

Case 3:

By Observation
1.5
(iii),
a_{1}(C_{3}\,\square\,C_{m})\leq 3

To show that

Assume that Alice plays two vertices in her first move. Then there are two consecutive layers

and

(1,k-1)
and

(1,k-1)

Lemma 4.7

and

(\Leftarrow)

(i,j)
