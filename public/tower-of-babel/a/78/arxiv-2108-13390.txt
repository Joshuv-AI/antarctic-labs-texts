Introduction

Background and main result

Complex hyperbolic
n

\mathbb{C}H^{n}
is the model space uniformizing Kähler manifolds of complex dimension

with constant negative holomorphic sectional curvature.
\mathbb{C}H^{n}

\mathbb{C}^{n}

[

[

We begin by explaining an alternative description of complex hyperbolic cusps in terms of the Calabi ansatz
[
8
,
9
]

be any Hermitian metric on

. This scaling freedom can also be understood as a consequence of the natural action of

on the total space of

as a smooth function on the total space which is homogeneous of degree

along every fiber, we define

and
\omega_{h}

=

Write

In this paper, we determine the precise asymptotics of the Datar-Fu-Song solutions

Main Theorem
.

Fix a Hermitian metric

L
as above. Let

be a complete Kähler-Einstein metric on

such that for all

\displaystyle|\nabla^{k}_{\omega_{h}}(u+(n+1)\log(1+cx))|_{\omega_{h}}=O_{k}\left(x^{-\frac{n}{2}+\frac{1}{4}-\frac{k}{2}}e^{-\frac{2\sqrt{\lambda_{1}}}{\sqrt{x}}}\right)\;\,\text{as}\;\,x\to 0^{+}.

(1.1)

Here

In particular,

Overview of the proof

The function
x
is the unique decaying radial solution to the homogeneous linearized Kähler-Einstein equation. We expand
u
according to powers of
x
, which results in a formal power series solution differing from
u
by
O(x^{\infty})
. This is similar to asymptotic expansion arguments as in
[
12
,
16
,
18
,
19
,
25
]
, with our proof being closest in spirit to
[
12
]
. The coefficients of our expansion are actually constants, the first coefficient determines all the others, and the formal series converges to
-(n+1)\log(1+cx)
for some
c\in\mathbb{R}
. We think of this expression as the “tangent cone” of
u
.

Asymptotic expansion methods
[
12
,
16
,
18
,
19
,
25
]
stop at the statement that the difference between
u
and some formal (in this case, convergent) power series in
x
is
O(x^{\infty})
. A new point here is that we go further and prove an explicit estimate, (
1.1
), for this difference, which is actually completely sharp as evidenced by the next generation of solutions to the homogeneous linearized PDE. This estimate is exponential in
x
. In situations with conical or cylindrical geometry, i.e., with an asymptotic scaling or translation symmetry, this kind of statement is reminiscent of the unique tangent cone theorem of
[
1
]
; see
[
20
, p.270]
for a simplification of
[
1
]
using rescaling arguments, and see
[
4
]
for an application of
[
20
]
in the context of Einstein metrics. There are no useful scaling symmetries in our setting because our geometry is cuspidal (with two different collapsing scales) rather than conical or cylindrical.

We therefore develop a more effective version of the unique tangent cone argument, which is possible in our situation because we already have an explicit decay rate,
O(x^{\infty})
, and our goal is to boost this to the decay rate of the next generation of solutions,
O(e^{-\delta/\sqrt{x}})
. The two main ingredients are (A) an explicit integral operator in terms of Bessel functions, which serves as a right inverse to the linearized operator, and which we need to bound with respect to weighted norms that are piecewise polynomial and piecewise exponential in
x
(this is the most novel ingredient), and (B) uniform Schauder estimates on metric balls of radius
\sim 1
. These Schauder estimates are nontrivial because the geometry scale of
\omega_{h}
, where Schauder estimates follow from the standard theory on balls in
\mathbb{C}^{n}
, is only
\sim x
. Since
D
is a torus, we can work around this problem in a fairly standard manner by using local universal covers, or quasi-coordinates
[
6
]
, although in principle there is now a way of proving uniform Schauder estimates at large scales even if the local fundamental groups are trivial at all scales
[
11
]
.

Organization of the paper

In Section
2

2.1.
The model metric

Let

\displaystyle\sigma:=-{\log h},\;\,x:=\frac{1}{\sigma},\;\,\rho:=-\sqrt{\frac{n+1}{2}}\log x.

(2.1)

With this understood, we define the well-known Kähler-Einstein model metric

\displaystyle\omega_{h}:=-(n+1)i\partial\overline{\partial}\log\sigma

(2.2)

on the region
\{0<h<1\}
. See
[
8
,
9
]

Lemma 2.1
.

In any local holomorphic chart
(z^{\prime},z_{n})

\displaystyle\omega_{h}=(n+1)[-ix\partial\overline{\partial}\varphi+ix^{2}(\partial\varphi-\partial\log z_{n})\wedge(\overline{\partial}\varphi-\overline{\partial}\log\bar{z}_{n})].

Thus, under the convention that

\displaystyle\omega_{h}=i\sum g_{j\bar{k}}dz^{j}\wedge d\bar{z}^{k},

the metric coefficients are given by

\displaystyle g_{j\bar{k}}=-i\omega_{h}(\partial_{j},\partial_{\bar{k}})=(n+1)\left[-x\varphi_{j\bar{k}}+x^{2}\left(\varphi_{j}-\frac{\delta_{jn}}{z_{n}}\right)\left(\varphi_{\bar{k}}-\frac{\delta_{kn}}{\bar{z}_{n}}\right)\right].

\nabla_{r\partial_{r}}(r\partial_{r})=-\nabla_{\partial_{\theta}}\partial_{\theta}
, so it suffices to prove that
\langle\nabla_{\partial_{\theta}}\partial_{\theta},\mathbf{t}\rangle=0
for all vectors
\mathbf{t}
tangent to
Y_{\varepsilon}
. This can be done as follows. Since
\partial_{\theta}
is a Killing vector field,

\displaystyle\langle\nabla_{{\partial_{\theta}}}\partial_{\theta},\mathbf{t}\rangle=-\langle\nabla_{\mathbf{t}}\partial_{\theta},\partial_{\theta}\rangle=-\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{t}\langle\partial_{\theta},\partial_{\theta}\rangle=-(n+1)\mathbf{t}(\sigma^{2})=0,

(2.24)

as desired, using also (
2.10
). This proves that
\nabla_{\mathbf{n}}\mathbf{n}=0
.

Next, we compute the mean curvature of
Y_{\varepsilon}
. The unoriented area element of
Y_{\varepsilon}
is

\displaystyle dA=\sqrt{\det(g_{h}|_{Y_{\varepsilon}})}\,d\theta\,dx_{\alpha}\,dy_{\alpha}=(n+1)^{\frac{n-1}{2}}\varepsilon^{n-1}\sqrt{\det(g_{\varepsilon})}\,d\theta\,dx_{\alpha}\,dy_{\alpha}.

(2.25)

By (
2.8
) and column operations,
\det(g_{\varepsilon})
equals the determinant of

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}-\frac{1}{2}(\varphi_{x_{\alpha}x_{\beta}}+\varphi_{y_{\alpha}y_{\beta}})&-\frac{1}{2}(\varphi_{x_{\alpha}y_{\beta}}-\varphi_{y_{\alpha}x_{\beta}})&\varepsilon^{2}\varphi_{y_{\alpha}}\\
-\frac{1}{2}(\varphi_{y_{\alpha}x_{\beta}}-\varphi_{x_{\alpha}y_{\beta}})&-\frac{1}{2}(\varphi_{y_{\alpha}y_{\beta}}+\varphi_{x_{\alpha}x_{\beta}})&-\varepsilon^{2}\varphi_{x_{\alpha}}\\
0&0&2\varepsilon^{2}\end{pmatrix},

(2.26)

which equals
\varepsilon^{2}f(x_{\alpha},y_{\alpha})
with
f
smooth and independent of
\varepsilon
. Thus,

\displaystyle\frac{\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{n}}dA}{dA}=\frac{\mathbf{n}(\sigma^{-\frac{n}{2}})}{\sigma^{-\frac{n}{2}}}=\frac{\frac{n}{2}\cdot(r\partial_{r})(\sigma)}{\sqrt{2(n+1)}}=-\frac{n}{\sqrt{2(n+1)}},

(2.27)

and this is the mean curvature of
Y_{\varepsilon}
with respect to
\mathbf{n}
.
∎

2.2.
The model operator

We now compute the linearization of the Monge-Ampère operator at our model metric
\omega_{h}
. With
(g_{j\bar{k}})
as in (
2.5
), this is the operator

\displaystyle L_{h}(u):=\frac{d}{dt}\biggr|_{t=0}\frac{(\omega_{h}+i\partial\overline{\partial}(tu))^{n}}{e^{tu}\omega_{h}^{n}}=g^{\bar{k}j}u_{j\bar{k}}-u.

(2.28)

Fix any local holomorphic chart
(z^{\prime},z_{n})
on the total space as in Section
2.1
, so that
h=e^{-\varphi(z^{\prime})}|z_{n}|^{2}
. Consider the associated smooth chart
(x_{\alpha},y_{\alpha},x,\theta)
, where
x=\frac{1}{\sigma}
. In this chart, we can still define the complex partials
u_{\alpha}=\partial_{z_{\alpha}}u
and
u_{\bar{\alpha}}=\partial_{\bar{z}_{\alpha}}u
in the usual way for all
\alpha\in\{1,\ldots,n-1\}
.

Lemma 2.5
.

In any chart
(x_{\alpha},y_{\alpha},x,\theta)
as above, the linearized operator is given by

\displaystyle\begin{split}L_{h}(u)=\frac{1}{n+1}(x^{2}u_{xx}+(n+1)xu_{x}-(n+1)u-x^{-1}\varphi^{\bar{\beta}\alpha}u_{\alpha\bar{\beta}}+(2x)^{-2}u_{\theta\theta})+F,\\
F=F(x_{\alpha},y_{\alpha},x^{-1}u_{\theta\alpha},x^{-1}u_{\theta\bar{\alpha}},x^{-1}u_{\theta\theta}),\end{split}

(2.29)

where
F
is smooth in
x_{\alpha},y_{\alpha}
and linear homogeneous in the other arguments.

Proof.

In the chart
(z^{\prime},z_{n})
, the definition (
2.28
) of
L_{h}
can be expanded into

\displaystyle L_{h}(u)=g^{\bar{k}j}u_{j\bar{k}}-u=g^{\bar{\beta}\alpha}u_{\alpha\bar{\beta}}+2{\rm Re}(g^{\bar{\alpha}n}u_{\bar{\alpha}n})+g^{\bar{n}n}u_{n\bar{n}}-u.

(2.30)

With
Q:=\varphi^{\bar{\beta}\gamma}\varphi_{\bar{\beta}}\varphi_{\gamma}
, the inverse of the metric tensor is given for
\alpha,\beta\in\{1,\ldots,n-1\}
by

\displaystyle g^{\bar{\beta}\alpha}=-\frac{\varphi^{\bar{\beta}\alpha}}{(n+1)x},\;\,g^{\bar{\alpha}n}=-\frac{\varphi^{\bar{\alpha}\gamma}\varphi_{\gamma}z_{n}}{(n+1)x},\;\,g^{\bar{n}n}=\frac{r^{2}(1-Qx)}{(n+1)x^{2}}.

(2.31)

This can be checked by direct multiplication, using (
2.5
).

Consider the change of coordinates
(z^{\prime},z_{n})\mapsto(x_{\alpha},y_{\alpha},x,\theta)
. A simple calculation shows that

\displaystyle\partial_{z_{\alpha}}\mapsto\partial_{z_{\alpha}}-x^{2}\varphi_{\alpha}\partial_{x},\;\,\partial_{z_{n}}\mapsto\frac{x^{2}}{z_{n}}\partial_{x}-\frac{i}{2z_{n}}\partial_{\theta}.

(2.32)

Thus, under the new coordinates
(x_{\alpha},y_{\alpha},x,\theta)
,

\displaystyle\begin{split}(n+1)g^{\bar{\beta}\alpha}u_{\alpha\bar{\beta}}&=-x^{-1}\varphi^{\bar{\beta}\alpha}(\partial_{z_{\alpha}}-x^{2}\varphi_{\alpha}\partial_{x})(u_{\bar{\beta}}-x^{2}\varphi_{\bar{\beta}}u_{x})\\
&=-x^{-1}\varphi^{\bar{\beta}\alpha}u_{\alpha\bar{\beta}}+(n-1)xu_{x}+\boxed{x\varphi^{\bar{\beta}\alpha}(\varphi_{\alpha}u_{x\bar{\beta}}+\varphi_{\bar{\beta}}u_{x\alpha})}-\underline{\underline{Q(x^{3}u_{xx}+2x^{2}u_{x})}}.\end{split}

(2.33)

Similarly, with
{F}_{1}
as in the statement of the lemma,

\displaystyle\begin{split}(n+1)g^{\bar{\alpha}n}u_{n\bar{\alpha}}&=-\frac{z_{n}}{x}\varphi^{\bar{\alpha}\gamma}\varphi_{\gamma}\left(\frac{x^{2}}{z_{n}}\partial_{x}-\frac{i}{2z_{n}}\partial_{\theta}\right)\left(u_{\bar{\alpha}}-x^{2}\varphi_{\bar{\alpha}}u_{x}\right)\\
&=\boxed{-x\varphi^{\bar{\alpha}\gamma}\varphi_{\gamma}u_{x\bar{\alpha}}}+\underline{\underline{Q(x^{3}u_{xx}+2x^{2}u_{x})}}-\frac{i}{2}Qxu_{x\theta}+{F}_{1}(x_{\alpha},y_{\alpha},x^{-1}u_{\theta\bar{\alpha}}).\end{split}

(2.34)

The last contribution is slightly more complicated:

\displaystyle\begin{split}(n+1)g^{\bar{n}n}u_{n\bar{n}}&=\frac{r^{2}}{x^{2}}(1-Qx)\left(\frac{x^{2}}{z_{n}}\partial_{x}-\frac{i}{2z_{n}}\partial_{\theta}\right)\left(\frac{x^{2}}{\bar{z}_{n}}u_{x}+\frac{i}{2\bar{z}_{n}}u_{\theta}\right)\\
&=x^{2}u_{xx}+2xu_{x}+(2x)^{-2}u_{\theta\theta}-\underline{\underline{Q(x^{3}u_{xx}+2x^{2}u_{x})}}+{F}_{2}(x_{\alpha},y_{\alpha},x^{-1}u_{\theta\theta}).\end{split}

(2.35)

The delicate part here is the coefficients of
u_{x}
and
u_{\theta}
, which arise as follows:

\displaystyle\frac{r^{2}}{x^{2}}\left(\frac{x^{2}}{z_{n}}\partial_{x}-\frac{i}{2z_{n}}\partial_{\theta}\right)\left(\frac{x^{2}}{\bar{z}_{n}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{r^{2}}{x^{2}}\partial_{z_{n}}\left(\frac{x^{2}}{\bar{z}_{n}}\right)=\frac{r^{2}}{x^{2}}\frac{1}{\bar{z}_{n}}\partial_{z_{n}}(\varphi-\log|z_{n}|^{2})^{-2}=2x

(2.36)

for
u_{x}
, and similarly the coefficient of
u_{\theta}
vanishes.

When we add the contributions of (
2.33
), (
2.34
) and (
2.35
) according to (
2.30
), the boxed and the underlined terms cancel. Cancellation of the boxed terms will be important in Section
4
.
∎

2.3.
Results of Datar-Fu-Song

In the setting of the Main Theorem, consider a member
h
of the canonical
1
-parameter family of Hermitian metrics on
L\to D
and its associated model metric
\omega_{h}
. For any tubular neighborhood
V
of the zero section
D\subset L
of the form
V=\{h\leqslant\delta\}
for some
\delta\in(0,1)
, a special case of Datar-Fu-Song
[
7
, Thm 4.1]
says that for all
\beta\in C^{\infty}(\partial V)
, the Dirichlet problem

\displaystyle\begin{split}(\omega_{h}+i\partial\overline{\partial}u)^{n}=e^{u}\omega_{h}^{n}\;\,\text{on}\;\,V\setminus D,\\
u=\beta\;\,\text{on}\;\,\partial V,\end{split}

(2.37)

has a smooth solution
u
such that the Kähler-Einstein metric
\omega_{h}+i\partial\overline{\partial}u
on
V\setminus D
is complete.

Conversely assume that for some closed tubular neighborhood
U
of the zero section, there exists a Kähler-Einstein metric
\omega
on
U\setminus D
such that
{\rm Ric}(\omega)=-\omega
. Thanks to the Kähler-Einstein equation, we have that
\omega=\omega_{h}+i\partial\overline{\partial}u
, where
u
is the log volume ratio of the two metrics. Equivalently,

\displaystyle(\omega_{h}+i\partial\overline{\partial}u)^{n}=e^{u}\omega_{h}^{n}.

(2.38)

Then the following result is a special case of
[
7
, Thm 1.3]
. This still holds in the unbounded curvature setting where
D
is a non-flat Calabi-Yau manifold.

Theorem 2.6
.

If
\omega
is complete, then there exists a constant
C
such that for all
0<\eta\leqslant 1
we have that
|u(p)|\leqslant C\eta
for all
p\in U\setminus D
such that
{\rm dist}_{\omega_{h}}(p,\partial U)\geqslant\eta^{-1}
and
{\rm dist}_{\omega}(p,\partial U)\geqslant\eta^{-1}
.

In particular, the maximum principle can now be applied to prove that
u
is the
unique
solution to the Dirichlet problem (
2.37
) such that
\omega_{h}+i\partial\overline{\partial}u
is complete, where
\beta
is defined to be the restriction of
u
to
\partial V
for any
V
as above such that
V\subset U
. This completeness assumption cannot be dropped, and (
2.38
) does not guarantee by itself that
u
is bounded, as the following example shows.

Example 2.7
.

We use the Calabi ansatz to construct Kähler-Einstein metrics on
V\setminus D=\{0<h\leqslant\delta\}
for some
\delta>0
. Let
\omega=i\partial\overline{\partial}\psi(-\sigma)
, where
\psi:(-\infty,\log\delta]\to\mathbb{R}
is smooth. Then
\omega
is positive definite if and only if
\psi^{\prime}>0
and
\psi^{\prime\prime}>0
, and the equation
{\rm Ric}(\omega)=-\omega
is equivalent to

\displaystyle(\psi^{\prime})^{n-1}\psi^{\prime\prime}=e^{\psi+a}

(2.39)

for some constant
a\in\mathbb{R}
. Our standard solutions
\omega=\omega_{\lambda h}
(
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
) in this paper correspond to

\displaystyle\psi(t)=-(n+1)\log(-(t+\log\lambda)),\;\,e^{a}=(n+1)^{n},

(2.40)

for
\delta\in(0,\frac{1}{\lambda})
. However, there do exist other solutions which are defined on all of
V\setminus D
.

In fact, by multiplying (
2.39
) by
\psi^{\prime}>0
and integrating, we see that (
2.39
) is equivalent to

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n+1}(\psi^{\prime})^{n+1}=e^{\psi+a}+b

(2.41)

for some constant
b\in\mathbb{R}
. This is in turn equivalent to

\displaystyle\int_{\psi(t)}^{\psi(t_{0})}(e^{\xi+a}+b)^{-\frac{1}{n+1}}d\xi=(n+1)^{\frac{1}{n+1}}(t_{0}-t)

(2.42)

for all
t\leqslant t_{0}
such that
\psi
exists on
[t,t_{0}]
and
e^{\psi+a}+b
is strictly positive. If
b=0
, then (
2.42
) implies (
2.40
) up to an additive constant. For
b<0
, the integral in (
2.42
) is uniformly bounded independently of
\psi(t)
, so the interval of allowed
t
-values has a finite lower bound. For
b>0
, this obstruction does not arise, so
\psi
exists on
(-\infty,\log\delta]
for some
\delta>0
and
\psi^{\prime}

>

c:=((n+1)b)^{1/(n+1)}>0
and
\psi^{\prime\prime}>0
.

The property
\psi^{\prime}>c
together with the mean value theorem tells us that
\psi(t)\leqslant\psi(t_{0})+c(t-t_{0})
for all
t\leqslant t_{0}
. Then (
2.39
) implies
\psi^{\prime\prime}(t)=O(e^{ct})
and (
2.41
) implies
\psi^{\prime}(t)-c=O(e^{ct})
as
t\to-\infty
. Using this, one can check that
(V\setminus D,\omega)
has finite diameter. In fact,
\omega
defines a singular metric on
V
with conical singularities along
D
, where
D
has diameter proportional to
\sqrt{c}
and the cone angle is
2\pi c
. To see that the angle is exactly
2\pi c
, notice that
\psi^{\prime}(t)=c+O(e^{ct})
implies
\psi(t)=ct+d+O(e^{ct})
for some constant
d\in\mathbb{R}
, and then
\psi^{\prime\prime}(t)
is asymptotic to a constant multiple of
e^{ct}
by (
2.39
).

Corollary 2.8
.

If
\omega
is complete and if in addition
\sup_{U\setminus D}|\omega|_{\omega_{h}}<\infty
, then
\omega
is uniformly equivalent to
\omega_{h}
and we have that
|u|=O((-{\log x})^{-1})
as
x\to 0^{+}
.

Proof.

By Theorem
2.6
,
u
is bounded, i.e., the volume forms
\omega_{h}^{n}
and
\omega^{n}
are uniformly comparable to each other. Since
|\omega|_{\omega_{h}}
is bounded, it follows that
\omega
and
\omega_{h}
are themselves uniformly equivalent. Then
|u(p)|=O((-{\log x(p)})^{-1})
follows for all
p\in U\setminus D
by taking
\eta
in Theorem
2.6
to be comparable to
(-{\log x(p)})^{-1}
because the gradient of
-{\log x(\cdot)}
has constant
\omega_{h}
-length by Lemma
2.4
.
∎

If
D
is a finite quotient of a complex torus, then the bound
\sup_{U\setminus D}|\omega|_{\omega_{h}}<\infty
can actually be proved
[
7
,
14
]
, which is also the reason why the Dirichlet problem (
2.37
) can be proved to admit a complete solution in this case
[
7
]
. Applying the standard local regularity theory of the complex Monge-Ampère equation in quasi-coordinates
[
5
]
, we thus obtain the following slight refinement of
[
7
, Thm 1.4]
.

Theorem 2.9
.

If
D
is a finite quotient of a torus and if
\omega
is complete, then
\omega
is uniformly equivalent to
\omega_{h}
and we have for all
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
that

|\nabla^{k}_{\omega_{h}}u|_{\omega_{h}}=O_{k}((-{\log x})^{-1})
as
x\to 0^{+}
.

3.
Expansion according to powers of
x

Assume the same setup as in the Main Theorem. Since
\omega_{h}
and
\omega
are Kähler-Einstein with the same Einstein constant, it follows that
\omega=\omega_{h}+i\partial\overline{\partial}u
, where
u
is the log volume ratio. Since
\omega
is complete, it follows from Theorem
2.9
that
|u|=O((-{\log x})^{-1})
with all derivatives. Also, by definition,

\displaystyle(\omega_{h}+i\partial\overline{\partial}u)^{n}=e^{u}\omega_{h}^{n}.

(3.1)

In this section we prove the following theorem, which is our first step towards the Main Theorem.

Theorem 3.1
.

There exists a constant
c\in\mathbb{R}
such that

\displaystyle u+(n+1)\log(1+cx)={O}(x^{\infty}).

(3.2)

The proof will be given in Section
3.2
after some preliminary estimates in Section
3.1
.

Note that if (
3.2
) holds in the pointwise sense, then it automatically holds for all derivatives. This follows from the classical regularity theory of the Monge-Ampère equation. For this it actually suffices to work at the scale of the injectivity radius because the constants in the elliptic estimates only blow up like fixed negative powers of
x
even at this scale. However, during the proof of Theorem
3.1
, it is helpful to work with a more specific
O
notation, which we will now define; cf.
[
12
, Thm 1.1]
.

Definition 3.2
.

After shrinking
U
if necessary, choose a finite cover of
U\setminus D
by charts
(x_{\alpha},y_{\alpha},x,\theta)
as in Sections
2.1
–
2.2
. Given this, we say that a function
f\in C^{\infty}(U\setminus D)
is
\mathbf{O}(x^{k})
for some
k\in\mathbb{R}
if, under all local charts of this cover, for all
c\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
there exists a constant
C
such that

\displaystyle|x^{c}\partial^{c}_{x}f|\leqslant Cx^{k},

(3.3)

and for all
a,b,c,d,N\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
with
a+b+d\geqslant 1
there exists a constant
C^{\prime}
such that

\displaystyle|\partial^{a}_{x^{\alpha}}\partial^{b}_{y^{\alpha}}\partial^{c}_{x}\partial^{d}_{\theta}f|\leqslant C^{\prime}x^{N}.

(3.4)

3.1.
Preliminary estimates

We begin by proving that
|u|=O(x)
as
x\to 0^{+}
. This bound is sharp because
x
is the unique decaying radial solution to the homogeneous linearized PDE, as one can easily check using Lemma
2.5
. Our proof uses a barrier argument as in
[
12
, Lemma 2.2]
, and this argument would go through for an arbitrary compact Calabi-Yau manifold
D
.

Lemma 3.3
.

Assuming only that
|u|=o(1)
as
x\to 0^{+}
, we obtain that
|u|=O(x)
.

Proof.

Define
U_{\delta}=\{0<x\leqslant\delta\}\subset L\setminus D
with
\delta\in(0,1)
small enough so that
U_{\delta}\subset U\setminus D
. We will prove the lemma in two steps, using a suitable barrier function in each step. The key is that
x^{1-\varepsilon}
is a supersolution of the linearized operator
L_{h}
for all
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
(in fact, for all
\varepsilon\in(0,n+2)
, but we are only interested in functions that go to zero as
x\to 0^{+}
) and
x^{1+\varepsilon}
is a subsolution for all
\varepsilon>0
.

In the first step, we consider the barrier
B:=x^{1-\varepsilon}
for an arbitrary but fixed
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
. Let

\displaystyle a_{\delta}:=\max_{\partial U_{\delta}}\frac{|u|}{B}=o(\delta^{\varepsilon-1})\;\,\text{as}\;\,\delta\to 0.

(3.5)

Then
u-a_{\delta}B\leqslant 0
on
\partial U_{\delta}
. We now prove that
u-a_{\delta}B\leqslant 0
on
U_{\delta}
if
\delta
is small. If this was false, then, because
u=o(1)
as
x\to 0^{+}
, for arbitrarily small values of
\delta
there would exist a point
p_{\delta}\in{\rm int}\,U_{\delta}
such that
u-a_{\delta}B
attains a positive global maximum at
p_{\delta}
. Thus, at
p_{\delta}
,

\displaystyle 0=\log\left(\frac{(\omega_{h}+i\partial\overline{\partial}u)^{n}}{\omega_{h}^{n}}\right)-u<\log\left(\frac{(\omega_{h}+i\partial\overline{\partial}(a_{\delta}B))^{n}}{\omega_{h}^{n}}\right)-a_{\delta}B.

(3.6)

Now
|i\partial\overline{\partial}B|_{\omega_{h}}=cB
for some constant
c=c(n,\varepsilon)
, so by (
3.5
) we have
|i\partial\overline{\partial}(a_{\delta}B)|_{\omega_{h}}=o(\delta^{\varepsilon-1})B=o(1)
uniformly on
U_{\delta}
as
\delta\to 0
. This allows us to linearize the right-hand side of (
3.6
) if
\delta
is small enough. Thus, there exists a constant
C=C(n)
such that if
\delta
is small, then, uniformly on
U_{\delta}
,

\displaystyle\begin{split}\log\left(\frac{(\omega_{h}+i\partial\overline{\partial}(a_{\delta}B))^{n}}{\omega_{h}^{n}}\right)-a_{\delta}B&\leqslant L_{h}(a_{\delta}B)+C|i\partial\overline{\partial}(a_{\delta}B)|_{\omega_{h}}^{2}\\
&=-\varepsilon(n+2-\varepsilon)(a_{\delta}B)+c^{2}C(a_{\delta}B)^{2}.\end{split}

(3.7)

Again because
\sup_{U_{\delta}}(a_{\delta}B)=o(1)
as
\delta\to 0
, this is eventually nonpositive on all of
U_{\delta}
, contradicting (
3.6
) at
p_{\delta}
. Thus, fixing
\delta
small enough depending on
u,n,\varepsilon
, we have that
u\leqslant a_{\delta}x^{1-\varepsilon}
on
U_{\delta}
.

A lower bound can be proved in the same way. The only difference is that the uniform smallness of
a_{\delta}B
on
U_{\delta}
is then also needed to ensure that
\omega_{h}-i\partial\overline{\partial}(a_{\delta}B)>0
. Notice also that, in the proof of the upper bound, the quadratic terms on the right-hand side of (
3.7
) were actually unnecessary because the Monge-Ampère operator is concave, but they are necessary in the proof of the lower bound.

In the second step, we carry out the same argument using
B:=x-x^{1+\varepsilon}
as a barrier for any fixed
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
. This is a positive supersolution to
L_{h}
on
U_{\delta}
for all
\varepsilon>0
because
x
is a solution and
x^{1+\varepsilon}
is a subsolution. The only difference to the first step is that the good linear term on the right-hand side of (
3.7
) is now comparable to
x^{\varepsilon}(a_{\delta}B)
, but the nonlinearities are still only bounded by
(a_{\delta}B)^{2}
. Thus, we can complete the argument only if we know that
\sup_{U_{\delta}}(x^{-\varepsilon}(a_{\delta}B))=o(1)
as
\delta\to 0
. But this is true as long as
\varepsilon<1
. The reason is that
|u|=O(x^{\mu})
as
x\to 0^{+}
for all
\mu\in(0,1)
from the first step, which implies
a_{\delta}=\max_{\partial U_{\delta}}(|u|/B)=O(\delta^{\mu-1})
as
\delta\to 0
. Thus, it suffices to choose
\mu\in(\varepsilon,1)
.
∎

Sharp higher-order bounds for
u
are now almost immediate as in
[
12
, Lemma 2.4]
. Here we use the flatness of
D
although it seems likely that this can be avoided using the methods of
[
11
]
.

Lemma 3.4
.

Assuming only that
|u|=O(x)
as
x\to 0^{+}
and that
\omega
is uniformly equivalent to
\omega_{h}
, we obtain that
|\nabla^{k}_{\omega_{h}}u|_{\omega_{h}}=O_{k}(x)
for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
.

Proof.

Shrinking
U
slightly if necessary, we can assume that
\partial U=\{h=\delta\}
for some
\delta\in(0,1)
.

Consider a local chart
(x_{\alpha},y_{\alpha},x,\theta)
on
U\setminus D
as in Sections
2.1
–
2.2
. Thus, for some
p\in D\subset U
, we have local holomorphic coordinates
(z^{\prime},z_{n})
on
U
mapping
p
to
0\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
such that the bundle projection
\pi
is given by
z\mapsto z^{\prime}=(z_{1},\ldots,z_{n-1})
. In terms of these coordinates,
x_{\alpha}={\rm Re}(z_{\alpha})
and
y_{\alpha}={\rm Im}(z_{\alpha})
for all
\alpha\in\{1,\ldots,n-1\}
, and
z_{n}=re^{i\theta}
and
x=(-{\log h})^{-1}=(\varphi(z^{\prime})-\log r^{2})^{-1}
.

Because
D
is flat, there exists a holomorphic covering map
\mathbb{C}^{n-1}\to D
. The line bundle
L
becomes holomorphically trivial after pulling back under this map. We can then assume that
z^{\prime}
is the standard coordinate vector on
\mathbb{C}^{n-1}
and that
L
has been trivialized in such a way that
\varphi(z^{\prime})
is a homogeneous quadratic polynomial. Moreover, after passing to the universal cover of the set
\{z_{n}\neq 0\}
, we may view
\theta
as a globally defined function. In this way, we realize the universal cover of
U\setminus D
as a subset of
\mathbb{C}^{n}
with holomorphic coordinates
(z^{\prime},w)
, where
w:=\log r+i\theta
. We also get a smooth chart
(x_{\alpha},y_{\alpha},x,\theta)
identifying the universal cover of
U\setminus D
with
\mathbb{R}^{2n-2}\times(0,x_{\delta}]\times\mathbb{R}
, where
x_{\delta}:=(-{\log\delta})^{-1}
.

Fix
q\in U\setminus D
such that
c_{n}x(q)<x_{\delta}
, where
c_{n}:=\exp(\sqrt{2/(n+1)})
. Let
B_{1}(q)
be the Riemannian unit ball with respect to
\omega_{h}
centered at
q
. Then, using our distance function
\rho
from (
2.1
),

\displaystyle B_{1}(q)\subset\{\rho(q)-1<\rho<\rho(q)+1\}=\{c_{n}^{-1}x(q)<x<c_{n}x(q)\}\subset U\setminus D.

(3.8)

On the universal cover of
U\setminus D
, we define a new chart via

\displaystyle(\check{x}_{\alpha},\check{y}_{\alpha},\check{x},\check{\theta}):=(x(q)^{\frac{1}{2}}x_{\alpha},x(q)^{\frac{1}{2}}y_{\alpha},x(q)^{-1}x,x(q)\theta).

(3.9)

Then, on the preimage of
B_{1}(q)
, uniformly as
x(q)\to 0^{+}
, the Riemannian metric associated with
\omega_{h}
is
C^{\infty}
equivalent to the standard Euclidean metric in the chart
(\check{x}_{\alpha},\check{y}_{\alpha},\check{x},\check{\theta})
. This is easy to check using Lemmas
2.2
–
2.3
, equation (
3.8
), and the fact that
\varphi(z^{\prime})
is a homogeneous quadratic polynomial.

We also introduce new holomorphic coordinates

\displaystyle\check{z}_{\alpha}:=x(q)^{\frac{1}{2}}z_{\alpha}=\check{x}_{\alpha}+i\check{y}_{\alpha},\;\,\check{w}:=x(q)w=\frac{1}{2}\left(\varphi(\check{z}^{\prime})-\frac{1}{\check{x}}\right)+i\check{\theta}.

(3.10)

Using indices with respect to
(\check{z}^{\prime},\check{w})
, the Monge-Ampère equation (
3.1
) can be written as

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}(\omega_{h}+ti\partial\overline{\partial}u)^{\bar{k}j}dt\cdot\partial_{j}\partial_{\bar{k}}u=u

(3.11)

on a fixed ball in
\mathbb{C}^{n}
, where the coefficient matrix of
\omega_{h}
is uniformly comparable to the identity to all orders. Since
\omega=\omega_{h}+i\partial\overline{\partial}u
is uniformly equivalent to
\omega_{h}
and
|u|
is in particular
O(1)
, the standard theory of the Monge-Ampère equation now tells us that
u
is bounded to all orders. Using this and the fact that
|u|=O(x)
, the lemma follows from Schauder theory applied to (
3.11
).
∎

By interpolation, we will now show that all derivatives of
u
with at least one
x_{\alpha},y_{\alpha},\theta
-direction are actually
O(x^{\infty})
because all of these directions are small under our metric
\omega_{h}
. For this and for the rest of the paper, it is helpful to directly prove an even stronger estimate in the
\theta
-directions. We do this using an argument similar to
[
10
, Prop 2.9(i)]
, which does not rely on the flatness of
D
.

Lemma 3.5
.

Assuming only that
|\nabla_{\omega_{h}}u|_{\omega_{h}}=O(x)
as
x\to 0^{+}
and that
\omega
is uniformly equivalent to
\omega_{h}
, we obtain an
\varepsilon>0
such that
|\nabla_{\omega_{h}}^{k}(\mathcal{L}_{\partial_{\theta}}u)|_{\omega_{h}}=O_{k}(e^{-\varepsilon/x})
for all
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
.

Proof.

Recall that
\partial_{\theta}
is a globally defined holomorphic vector field with
\mathcal{L}_{\partial_{\theta}}\omega_{h}=0
. Define
v=\mathcal{L}_{\partial_{\theta}}u
. Applying the operator
\mathcal{L}_{\partial_{\theta}}
to the Monge-Ampère equation (
3.1
), we thus obtain that

\displaystyle\Delta_{\omega}v=v,

(3.12)

where
\omega=\omega_{h}+i\partial\overline{\partial}u
is by assumption uniformly equivalent to
\omega_{h}
.

The first step is to prove that
v\in H^{1}(U\setminus D,\omega_{h})
. We have
|v|=O(x^{2})
because
|\nabla_{\omega_{h}}u|_{\omega_{h}}=O(x)
and
|\partial_{\theta}|_{\omega_{h}}=O(x)
. Thus,
v\in L^{2}(U\setminus D,\omega_{h})
because
{\rm Vol}(U\setminus D,\omega_{h})<\infty
. It is also not difficult to estimate the gradient of
v
with respect to
\omega_{h}
. For example, this can easily be done using quasi-coordinates as in the proof of Lemma
3.4
. The following method also works if
D
is not flat. Assume that
\partial U=\{\rho=\rho_{0}\}
for some
\rho_{0}>0
. For
k\in\mathbb{N}
,
k\geqslant\rho_{0}+1
, take a cutoff function
\eta_{k}=\eta_{k}(\rho)
that equals
1
for
\rho_{0}\leqslant\rho\leqslant k
and
0
for
\rho\geqslant k+1
, with
|\nabla_{\omega_{h}}\eta_{k}|_{\omega_{h}}\leqslant 2
. Then, by (
3.12
),

\displaystyle\int_{U\setminus D}\eta_{k}^{2}v\Delta_{\omega}v\,dV_{\omega}=\int_{U\setminus D}\eta_{k}^{2}v^{2}dV_{\omega}.

(3.13)

By integration by parts,

\displaystyle\int_{U\setminus D}\eta_{k}^{2}v\Delta_{\omega}v\,dV_{\omega}=-\int_{U\setminus D}\eta_{k}^{2}|\nabla_{\omega}v|_{\omega}^{2}\,dV_{\omega}-\int_{U\setminus D}2\eta_{k}v\langle\nabla_{\omega}\eta_{k},\nabla_{\omega}v\rangle_{\omega}\,dV_{\omega}.

(3.14)

After combining (
3.13
)–(
3.14
) and applying Cauchy-Schwarz in the obvious way, we can replace
\omega
by
\omega_{h}
because
\omega
is uniformly equivalent to
\omega_{h}
. Letting
k\to\infty
, we obtain the desired
H^{1}
bound.

For the main step of the proof, define
U_{\rho}=\{q\in{U\setminus D}:\rho(q)\geqslant\rho\}
. Then, by (
3.12
),

\displaystyle E(\rho):=\int_{U_{\rho}}(|\nabla_{\omega}v|_{\omega}^{2}+v^{2})\,dV_{\omega}=-\int_{\partial U_{\rho}}v\mathbf{n}_{\omega}(v)\,dA_{\omega},

(3.15)

where
\mathbf{n}_{\omega}
denotes the
\omega
-unit normal of
\partial U_{\rho}
pointing towards the cuspidal end. Thus,

\displaystyle E(\rho)\leqslant\left(\int_{\partial U_{\rho}}v^{2}dA_{\omega}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(\int_{\partial U_{\rho}}(\mathbf{n}_{\omega}(v))^{2}dA_{\omega}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

(3.16)

We will now develop a Poincaré inequality to deal with the first factor.

By Lemma
2.2
, the restriction of
g_{h}
to
Y:=\partial U_{\rho}
is given by

\displaystyle g_{h}|_{Y}=2(n+1)x(g_{D}+x\alpha^{2}),

(3.17)

where
\rho=-\sqrt{(n+1)/2}\log x
as usual,
g_{D}
is a fixed metric on
D
pulled back under the
S^{1}
-orbit map
Y\to D
, and
\alpha:=d\theta-\frac{1}{2}d^{c}\varphi
is an
S^{1}
-connection form, i.e.,
\alpha(\partial_{\theta})=1
and
\mathcal{L}_{\partial_{\theta}}\alpha=0
. The
S^{1}
-action preserves the metric
g:=g_{D}+x\alpha^{2}
, so all eigenspaces of the Laplacian of
g
are
S^{1}
-invariant. Thus, by the representation theory of
S^{1}
, every eigenspace
E=\{\phi\in L^{2}(Y,\mathbb{R}):\Delta_{g}\phi=-\lambda\phi\}
can be written as the
L^{2}
-orthogonal sum of a space
E_{0}
of
S^{1}
-invariant functions and of planes
\mathbb{R}\phi_{1}+\mathbb{R}\phi_{2}
, where
\phi_{1},\phi_{2}
are
L^{2}
-orthonormal and
\mathcal{L}_{\partial_{\theta}}\phi_{1}=-k\phi_{2}
,
\mathcal{L}_{\partial_{\theta}}\phi_{2}=k\phi_{1}
for some
k\in\mathbb{N}_{\geqslant 1}
. Then, for all
\phi\in E
,

\displaystyle\lambda\int_{Y}\phi^{2}=\int_{Y}|\nabla\phi|^{2}\geqslant\int_{Y}(\mathcal{L}_{x^{-1/2}\partial_{\theta}}\phi)^{2}=x^{-1}\int_{Y}(\mathcal{L}_{\partial_{\theta}}\phi)^{2}\geqslant x^{-1}\int_{Y}({\rm proj}_{E_{0}^{\perp}}\phi)^{2},

(3.18)

where all metric quantities are understood in terms of
g
. The first inequality holds because
x^{-1/2}\partial_{\theta}
is a
g
-unit vector, and the second inequality follows by splitting
\phi
into its components in
E_{0}
and in the planes of frequency
k\in\mathbb{N}_{\geqslant 1}
described above. See
[
15
, pp.447–448]
for a related argument.

In our situation,
v=\mathcal{L}_{\partial_{\theta}}u
is
L^{2}
-orthogonal to all
S^{1}
-invariant functions on
Y
. Thus, if
\phi={\rm proj}_{E}v
, then
\phi={\rm proj}_{E_{0}^{\perp}}\phi
, so either
\phi=0
or
\lambda\geqslant x^{-1}
by (
3.18
). Thus, because
g_{h}|_{Y}=2(n+1)xg
,

\displaystyle\int_{\partial U_{\rho}}v^{2}dA_{\omega_{h}}\leqslant 2(n+1)x^{2}\int_{\partial U_{\rho}}(|\nabla_{\omega_{h}}v|_{\omega_{h}}^{2}-(\mathbf{n}_{\omega_{h}}(v))^{2})\,dA_{\omega_{h}}.

(3.19)

We now drop the
(\mathbf{n}_{\omega_{h}}(v))^{2}
term from (
3.19
), replace
\omega_{h}
by
\omega
as the reference metric, plug the resulting inequality into (
3.16
), and trivially bound the
(\mathbf{n}_{\omega}(v))^{2}
term in (
3.16
) above by
|\nabla_{\omega}v|_{\omega}^{2}
. Thus,

\displaystyle E(\rho)\leqslant Cx\int_{\partial U_{\rho}}|\nabla_{\omega}v|_{\omega}^{2}\,dA_{\omega}\leqslant-Cx\left(\sup_{\partial U_{\rho}}|\nabla_{\omega}\rho|_{\omega}\right)E^{\prime}(\rho)\leqslant-CxE^{\prime}(\rho)

(3.20)

for some uniform constant
C
, where the second inequality follows from the coarea formula. Dividing both sides of
(
3.20
)
by
E(\rho)
and integrating from
\rho_{0}
to
\rho
, we derive that for some
\varepsilon>0
,

\displaystyle E(\rho)\leqslant E(\rho_{0})\exp\left(-\frac{1}{C}\int_{\rho_{0}}^{\rho}\frac{d\rho}{x(\rho)}\right)\leqslant Ce^{-\varepsilon/x}.

(3.21)

This decay rate is so fast that it now suffices to use elliptic regularity at the scale of the injectivity radius. Indeed, Moser iteration applied to (
3.12
) on
\omega_{h}
-balls of radius
x
yields a pointwise bound for
v
which is
O(x^{-N}e^{-\varepsilon/x})
for some
N>0
, and
O(x^{-N}e^{-\varepsilon/x})=O(e^{-(\varepsilon/2)/x})
. Then higher-order estimates for
v
follow from Schauder theory applied to (
3.12
) on balls of radius
x
. Here we also need higher-order estimates for the coefficient metric
\omega
, which follow from the standard regularity theory of the Monge- Ampère equation on
\omega_{h}
-balls of radius
x
. Indeed, by assumption,
|\nabla_{\omega_{h}}u|_{\omega_{h}}
decays exponentially fast in terms of the distance function
\rho
, so
u
converges to a constant exponentially fast in terms of
\rho
, and
\omega
is uniformly equivalent to
\omega_{h}
, so
|\nabla^{k}_{\omega_{h}}u|_{\omega_{h}}=O_{k}(x^{-k})
from the standard theory.
∎

Finally, we obtain the following self-improvement of Lemma
3.4
. This is similar to
[
12
, Lemma 2.5]
, although in
[
12
]
this improvement only applies to the
\theta
-derivatives because the
x_{\alpha},y_{\alpha}
-directions have length
O(1)
, and here we already have a better estimate for the
\theta
-derivatives.

Lemma 3.6
.

Assuming the conclusions of Lemmas
3.4
and
3.5
, we obtain that
u=\mathbf{O}(x)
.

The
\mathbf{O}
notation is as in Definition
3.2
. For this we need to choose a finite cover of
U\setminus D
by charts
(x_{\alpha},y_{\alpha},x,\theta)
, and we will use the ones constructed in the proof of Lemma
3.4
using the flatness of
D
. It again seems likely that, using
[
11
]
, the proof can be made to work even if
D
is not flat.

Proof.

Fix any point
q\in U\setminus D
. Using the rescaled chart
(\check{x}_{\alpha},\check{y}_{\alpha},\check{x},\check{\theta})
of (
3.9
) on the preimage of the set
\{c_{n}^{-1}x(q)<x<c_{n}x(q)\}\supset B_{1}(q)
, which makes the pullback of
\omega_{h}
Euclidean to all orders uniformly as
x(q)\to 0^{+}
, we can state the results of Lemmas
3.4
–
3.5
as follows: for all
a,b,c,d\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
,

\displaystyle|\partial_{\check{x}_{\alpha}}^{a}\partial_{\check{y}_{\alpha}}^{b}\partial_{\check{x}}^{c}\partial_{\check{\theta}}^{d}u|=\begin{cases}O_{a,b,c}(x(q))&\text{if}\;d=0\\
O_{a,b,c,d}(x(q)^{-d}e^{-\varepsilon/x(q)})&\text{if}\;d\geqslant 1\end{cases}\quad\text{as}\;x(q)\to 0^{+}.

(3.22)

Converting these estimates back to the unrescaled chart
(x_{\alpha},y_{\alpha},x,\theta)
, we gain a factor of
x(q)^{\frac{a+b}{2}-c+d}
on the right-hand side. This implies property (
3.3
) for
k=1
as well as property (
3.4
) for
d\geqslant 1
. Thus, it remains to verify (
3.4
) for
d=0
and
a+b\geqslant 1
. For this it suffices to prove the implication

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\;\forall A,B,C,D\in\mathbb{N}_{0}:|\partial_{x_{\alpha}}^{A}\partial_{y_{\alpha}}^{B}\partial_{x}^{C}\partial_{\theta}^{D}u|=O_{A,B,C}(x^{\frac{A+B}{2}-C})\cdot\begin{cases}O(x)&\text{if}\;D=0\\
O_{D}(e^{-\varepsilon/x})&\text{if}\;D\geqslant 1\end{cases}\\
\Longrightarrow&\;\forall a,b,c\in\mathbb{N}_{0}:|\partial_{x_{\alpha}}^{a}\partial_{y_{\alpha}}^{b}\partial_{x}^{c}u|=O_{a,b,c}(x^{\infty})\;\,\text{if}\;a+b\geqslant 1.\end{split}

(3.23)

To prove (
3.23
), first note that
v:=\partial_{x_{\alpha}}^{a}\partial_{y_{\alpha}}^{b}\partial_{x}^{c}u
satisfies
|\partial_{\theta}v|=O(e^{-\varepsilon^{\prime}/x})
for any
0<\varepsilon^{\prime}<\varepsilon
thanks to the left-hand side of (
3.23
). Thus, if
\bar{v}
denotes the average of
v
with respect to
\theta
over
[0,2\pi]
, then
|v-\bar{v}|=O(e^{-\varepsilon^{\prime}/x})
, so it is actually enough to prove that
|\bar{v}|=O(x^{\infty})
. Let
\bar{u}
denote the average of
u
with respect to
\theta
over
[0,2\pi]
, so that
\bar{v}=\partial_{x_{\alpha}}^{a}\partial_{y_{\alpha}}^{b}\partial_{x}^{c}\bar{u}
. Fixing the
x
-variable, we can view
\partial_{x}^{c}\bar{u}
as a function on the flat torus
D
. Similarly, we can view
\partial_{x_{\alpha}},\partial_{y_{\alpha}}
as commuting Killing vector fields on
D
. Because
a+b\geqslant 1
and
D
is a compact manifold without boundary, it follows that the average of
\bar{v}
over
D
is zero, so
\bar{v}(o)=0
for some point
o\in D
. Without loss,
o
is the image of
0
under the covering map
\mathbb{C}^{n-1}\to D
. Then, for a fixed convex fundamental domain
\Omega\ni 0
of the deck group action,

\displaystyle\sup_{z^{\prime}\in\Omega}|\bar{v}(z^{\prime})|=\sup_{z^{\prime}\in\Omega}\left|\int_{0}^{1}\frac{d}{dt}\bar{v}(tz^{\prime})\,dt\right|\leqslant{\rm diam}(\Omega)\left(\sup_{\Omega}|\partial_{x_{\alpha}}\bar{v}|+\sup_{\Omega}|\partial_{y_{\alpha}}\bar{v}|\right).

(3.24)

Iterating this argument
k
times and applying the left-hand side of (
3.23
) with
A+B=a+b+k
(and
C=c
,
D=0
), we obtain that
|\bar{v}|=O_{k}(x^{k_{0}+(k/2)})
for all
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, where
k_{0}:=\frac{a+b}{2}-c+1
.
∎

3.2.
Proof of Theorem
3.1

Throughout this section, assuming the conclusion of Lemma
3.6
,
D
can again be an arbitrary compact Calabi-Yau manifold rather than a flat complex torus.

The following definition is modeled after
[
12
, Defn 5.1]
.

Definition 3.7
.

After shrinking
U
if necessary, consider the finite cover of
U\setminus D
by local charts of the form
(x_{\alpha},y_{\alpha},x,\theta)
that was used in Lemma
3.6
via Definition
3.2
. For any
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
,
we say that a function
f\in C^{\infty}(U\setminus D)
has an expansion of order
x^{k}
if, under all charts of this cover,

\displaystyle f=\sum_{i=0}^{k}C_{i}x^{i}+R_{k},

(3.25)

where the
C_{i}
are constants and there exists an
\varepsilon>0
such that
R_{k}=\mathbf{O}(x^{k+\varepsilon})
.

Thus, Lemma
3.6
implies that
u
has an expansion of order
x^{0}
with
C_{0}=0
and with
\varepsilon=1
.

Lemma 3.8
.

If
\psi
is a
C^{2}
function which only depends on
x
, then

\displaystyle\frac{(\omega_{h}+i\partial\overline{\partial}\psi)^{n}}{\omega_{h}^{n}}=\frac{(n+1+x\psi^{\prime})^{n-1}(n+1+x(x\psi)^{\prime\prime})}{(n+1)^{n}}.

(3.26)

Proof.

Consider the holomorphic chart
(z^{\prime},z_{n})
underlying the given smooth chart
(x_{\alpha},y_{\alpha},x,\theta)
as in Section
2.1
. Then, by (
2.5
), the metric coefficients of
\omega_{h}
are given by

\displaystyle\begin{split}g_{\alpha\bar{\beta}}&=(n+1)(-x\varphi_{\alpha\bar{\beta}}+x^{2}\varphi_{\alpha}\varphi_{\bar{\beta}}),\\
g_{\alpha\bar{n}}&=-(n+1)\frac{x^{2}}{\bar{z}_{n}}\varphi_{\alpha},\\
g_{n\bar{n}}&=(n+1)\frac{x^{2}}{|z_{n}|^{2}}.\end{split}

(3.27)

On the other hand, using (
2.32
) we compute

\displaystyle\begin{split}\psi_{\alpha\bar{\beta}}&=x^{3}(2\psi^{\prime}+x\psi^{\prime\prime})\varphi_{\alpha}\varphi_{\bar{\beta}}-x^{2}\psi^{\prime}\varphi_{\alpha\bar{\beta}},\\
\psi_{\alpha\bar{n}}&=-\frac{x^{3}}{\bar{z}_{n}}(2\psi^{\prime}+x\psi^{\prime\prime})\varphi_{\alpha},\\
\psi_{n\bar{n}}&=\frac{x^{3}}{|z_{n}|^{2}}(2\psi^{\prime}+x\psi^{\prime\prime}).\end{split}

(3.28)

With this, we can now compute the ratio of
\det(g_{j\bar{k}}+\psi_{j\bar{k}})
and
\det(g_{j\bar{k}})
. In both of these determinants, we can take out common factors of
\bar{z}_{n}^{-1}
in the
n
-th column and
z_{n}^{-1}
in the
n
-th row. Then, by adding a
\varphi_{\bar{\beta}}
multiple of the
n
-th column of each matrix to its
\beta
-th column, we create a block triangular matrix in each case. A simple calculation concludes the proof of the lemma.
∎

We now rewrite the complex Monge-Ampère equation (
3.1
) as

\displaystyle M_{h}(u):=\log\left(\frac{(\omega_{h}+i\partial\overline{\partial}u)^{n}}{\omega_{h}^{n}}\right)-u=0,

(3.29)

and we decompose
M_{h}
into its linearization at
\omega_{h}
and its nonlinear parts via

\displaystyle M_{h}=L_{h}+Q_{h}.

(3.30)

Here,
L_{h}
was already defined in (
2.28
), and
Q_{h}
is a convergent power series in
i\partial\overline{\partial}u
without constant or linear terms. Using Lemma
2.5
, we can further rewrite this as

\displaystyle x^{2}u_{xx}+(n+1)xu_{x}-(n+1)u=G

(3.31)

in every local chart of our chosen cover of
U\setminus D
, where

\displaystyle\begin{split}G:=x^{-1}\varphi^{\bar{\beta}\alpha}u_{\alpha\bar{\beta}}-(2x)^{-2}u_{\theta\theta}-(n+1)(F+Q_{h}(u)),\\
F=F(x_{\alpha},y_{\alpha},x^{-1}u_{\theta\alpha},x^{-1}u_{\theta\bar{\alpha}},x^{-1}u_{\theta\theta}),\end{split}

(3.32)

and
F
is smooth in
x_{\alpha},y_{\alpha}
and linear homogeneous in the remaining arguments.

We have the following lemma, which is analogous to
[
12
, Lemma 5.1]
.

Lemma 3.9
.

For all
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, if
u
has an expansion of order
x^{k}
, with
\varepsilon>\frac{1}{2}
if
k=0
, then
G
has an expansion of order
x^{k+1}
.

Proof.

Those contributions to
G
that are linear in
u
can be written in terms of tangential derivatives of
u
. Thus, by Lemma
3.6
, they are
\mathbf{O}(x^{\infty})
, i.e.,
\mathbf{O}(x^{N})
for any given
N\in\mathbb{N}
. In particular, they have an expansion to any given order, and the polynomial part of this expansion is zero.

It remains to discuss the term
Q_{h}(u)
. For this we need to use our assumption that
u=\psi_{k}+R_{k}
, where
\psi_{k}
is a degree
k
polynomial in
x
and the remainder
R_{k}
is
\mathbf{O}(x^{k+\varepsilon})
. We first observe that

\displaystyle R_{k}=\rho_{k}(x)+\mathbf{O}(x^{\infty}),

(3.33)

where
\rho_{k}(x)=\mathbf{O}(x^{k+\varepsilon})
is a smooth radial function. Indeed, for
0<t\ll 1
we can simply define
\rho_{k}(t)
to be the average of
R_{k}
over the level set
\{x=t\}
with respect to the restriction of the Riemannian metric associated with
\omega_{h}
. Then the properties
\rho_{k}(x)=\mathbf{O}(x^{k+\varepsilon})
and (
3.33
) follow from the fact that
R_{k}=\mathbf{O}(x^{k+\varepsilon})
, which implies in particular that all tangential derivatives of
R_{k}
are
\mathbf{O}(x^{\infty})
.

Now, also by definition,

\displaystyle Q_{h}(u)=\log\left(\frac{(\omega_{h}+i\partial\overline{\partial}u)^{n}}{\omega_{h}^{n}}\right)-\frac{d}{dt}\biggr|_{t=0}\log\left(\frac{(\omega_{h}+ti\partial\overline{\partial}u)^{n}}{\omega_{h}^{n}}\right).

(3.34)

Writing
u=\psi_{k}+\rho_{k}+\mathbf{O}(x^{\infty})
in this formula, using Lemma
3.8
, and defining

\displaystyle\mathbf{q}(t):=\log\left(1+\frac{t}{n+1}\right)-\frac{t}{n+1},

(3.35)

we obtain that

\displaystyle Q_{h}(u)=(n-1)\mathbf{q}(x\psi_{k}^{\prime}+x\rho_{k}^{\prime})+\mathbf{q}(x(x\psi_{k})^{\prime\prime}+x(x\rho_{k})^{\prime\prime})+\mathbf{O}(x^{\infty}).

(3.36)

From this we can now deduce that
Q_{h}(u)
has an expansion of order
x^{k+1}
.

Indeed,
\mathbf{q}
is a convergent power series without constant or linear terms. Thus, if we expand (
3.36
) as a convergent power series in the four variables
a=x\psi_{k}^{\prime}
,
b=x\rho_{k}^{\prime}
,
c=x(x\psi_{k})^{\prime\prime}
and
d=x(x\rho_{k})^{\prime\prime}
, then each monomial will be divisible by
a^{2}
,
ab
,
b^{2}
,
c^{2}
,

or
d^{2}
. Now observe that
a,c
are polynomials in
x
without constant terms while
b,d
are smooth functions of
x
which are
\mathbf{O}(x^{k+\varepsilon})
. Thus, all monomials in
a,b,c,d
that appear in the expansion of (

) are either polynomials in
x
or
\mathbf{O}(x^{\min\{1+k+\varepsilon,2k+2\varepsilon\}})
smooth functions of
x
. Because
\varepsilon>\frac{1}{2}
if

and because the sum of these monomials converges as a power series in
a,b,c,d
, it follows that
Q_{h}(u)
has an expansion of order
x^{k+1}
, as desired.
∎

Since we know from Lemma
3.6
that
u
has an expansion of order
x^{0}
with
\varepsilon=1
, Lemma
3.9
tells us that
G
has an expansion of order
x^{1}
. More precisely,

G=\mathbf{O}(x^{2})
by the proof of Lemma
3.9
.

The following lemma is analogous to but much simpler than
[
12
, Proposition 5.1]
.

Lemma 3.10
.

Let
\ell\in\mathbb{N}_{\geqslant 1}
.
If
G
has an expansion of order
x^{\ell}
, then
u
has an expansion of order
x^{\ell}
.

Proof.

Fix any chart. Fix an arbitrary point
(x_{\alpha},y_{\alpha},\theta)\in D\times S^{1}
and let
0<x_{0}\ll 1
. View (
3.31
) as a second-order ODE for
u(x_{\alpha},y_{\alpha},x,\theta)
with initial values at
x=x_{0}
. The corresponding homogeneous ODE has the two linearly independent solutions
x
and
x^{-n-1}
. Using variation of parameters and the fact that
u=O(1)
and
G=O(x^{1+\varepsilon})
for some
\varepsilon>0
, we derive that

\displaystyle\begin{split}u(x)=\left(\frac{u(x_{0})}{x_{0}}+\frac{x_{0}^{-n-2}}{n+2}\int_{0}^{x_{0}}t^{n}G(t)\,dt\right)x-\frac{x^{-n-1}}{n+2}\int_{0}^{x}t^{n}G(t)\,dt-\frac{x}{n+2}\int_{x}^{x_{0}}t^{-2}G(t)\,dt.\end{split}

(3.37)

Evaluation at
(x_{\alpha},y_{\alpha},\theta)
is implicit in this formula.

If
G
is a degree
\ell
polynomial in
x
without constant or linear terms, then (
3.37
) implies that
u
is a degree
\ell
polynomial in
x
without constant terms.

If
G=\mathbf{O}(x^{\ell+\varepsilon})
, then we can deduce from (
3.37
) that

\displaystyle u(x)=\left(\frac{u(x_{0})}{x_{0}}+\frac{x_{0}^{-n-2}}{n+2}\int_{0}^{x_{0}}t^{n}G(t)\,dt-\frac{1}{n+2}\int_{0}^{x_{0}}t^{-2}G(t)\,dt\right)x+\mathbf{O}(x^{\ell+\varepsilon})

(3.38)

by splitting the last integral in (
3.37
) into an integral from
0
to
x_{0}
and an integral from
0
to
x
. The term in parentheses in (
3.38
) is by definition independent of
x
, and all of its tangential derivatives are
\mathbf{O}(x^{\infty})
by Lemma
3.6
. Thus, this term is constant, and so
u=const\cdot x+\mathbf{O}(x^{\ell+\varepsilon})
.
∎

Iterating Lemmas
3.9
–
3.10
, we obtain that
u
has an expansion to any order, i.e., for all
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
there exists a degree
k
polynomial
\psi_{k}(x)
without a constant term such that

u=\psi_{k}(x)+\mathbf{O}(x^{k+\varepsilon})
for some
\varepsilon>0
. In fact, the above arguments show that this holds for
\varepsilon=1
. Moreover, the polynomials
\psi_{k}(x)
obviously stabilize in the sense that
\psi_{k}(x)=\psi_{k-1}(x)+C_{k}x^{k}
for all
k\in\mathbb{N}_{\geqslant 1}
. Thus, we may pass to a limit
\psi=\lim_{k}\psi_{k}
in the ring of all formal power series in
x
without constant terms.

By construction,
\psi
is a formal power series solution to the complex Monge-Ampère equation (
3.1
). Equivalently, by (
3.31
)–(
3.32
) and (
3.36
),
\psi
formally solves the equation

\displaystyle x^{2}\psi^{\prime\prime}+(n+1)x\psi^{\prime}-(n+1)\psi=-(n+1)[(n-1)\mathbf{q}(x\psi^{\prime})+\mathbf{q}(x(x\psi)^{\prime\prime})],

(3.39)

where
\mathbf{q}(t)
as in (
3.35
) is a convergent power series in
t
without constant or linear terms. Any formal solution
\psi(x)=C_{1}x+C_{2}x^{2}+\cdots
to (
3.39
) is uniquely determined by its leading coefficient
C_{1}
. Indeed, comparing terms of order
x^{k}
in (
3.39
) yields a coefficient
(k-1)(k+n+1)C_{k}
on the left side and some polynomial in
C_{1},\ldots,C_{k-1}
on the right. For
k=1
this is a tautology, and for
k\geqslant 2
we can solve this equation uniquely for
C_{k}
in terms of
C_{1},\ldots,C_{k-1}
. However, instead of actually solving this system of equations, it is simpler to observe that if
C_{1}=-(n+1)c
, then the function
-(n+1)\log(1+cx)
has the right leading term and solves (
3.39
), so that
\psi(x)=-(n+1)\log(1+cx)
.

Theorem
3.1
has been proved.

4.
Improvement of the remainder

In this section, we complete the proof of the Main Theorem by improving the remainder
O(x^{\infty})
of Theorem
3.1
to the decay rate of the next generation of solutions of the homogeneous linearized PDE. This is done using a continuity argument. For this we first construct a right inverse to the model linear operator, which leads to a useful representation formula (Proposition
4.2
). As in Section
3
, the proof of this uses ODE arguments, although unlike in Section
3
we now need to work globally on the cross- section and consider the spectrum of the Laplacian (but it suffices to use
S^{1}
-invariant eigenfunctions). Using this formula, we then carry out the continuity argument in Proposition
4.4
.

4.1.
A representation formula

Assume the same setup as in the Main Theorem. By Theorem
3.1
, there exists a
c\in\mathbb{R}
such that if we define
\psi(x):=-(n+1)\log(1+cx)
, then the function
u^{*}:=u-\psi
satisfies
u^{*}=O(x^{\infty})
along with all of its
\omega_{h}
-derivatives. Recall that we had originally defined
\psi
to be the unique formal power series solution to
(\omega_{h}+i\partial\overline{\partial}\psi)^{n}=e^{\psi}\omega_{h}^{n}
whose leading coefficient agrees with the Green’s function coefficient of an asymptotic expansion of
u
. Using
\omega_{h}^{*}:=\omega_{h}+i\partial\overline{\partial}\psi
as a reference metric instead of
\omega_{h}
, we can therefore rewrite our equation as

\displaystyle(\omega_{h}^{*}+i\partial\overline{\partial}u^{*})^{n}=e^{u^{*}}(\omega_{h}^{*})^{n}.

(4.1)

A crucial observation at this point is that
\omega_{h}^{*}=\omega_{h^{*}}
for
h^{*}=e^{-c}h
. The radial coordinate associated with this new Hermitian metric is
x^{*}=(x^{-1}+c)^{-1}
. Passing from
\omega_{h},x
to
\omega_{h^{*}},x^{*}
makes no difference to the statement of the Main Theorem. Thus, we can assume without loss that
c=0
. In fact:

Reduction 4.1
.

To prove the Main Theorem, it is enough to prove that

\text{if}\;\,(\omega_{h}+i\partial\overline{\partial}u)^{n}=e^{u}\omega_{h}^{n}\;\,\text{and}\;\,|u|=O(x^{\infty}),\,\text{then}\;\,|u|=O\left(x^{-\frac{n}{2}+\frac{1}{4}}e^{-\frac{2\sqrt{\lambda_{1}}}{\sqrt{x}}}\right).

(4.2)

The desired derivative decay follows from standard elliptic regularity applied on the universal covers of
\omega_{h}
-geodesic balls of radius
\varepsilon\sqrt{t}
centered at points of height
x=t
for some small but fixed
\varepsilon>0
. The point is that
\sqrt{t}
is the biggest order of magnitude such that functions of the form
e^{-\delta/\sqrt{x}}
vary by bounded factors over such balls. At the same time, these balls have fundamental group
\mathbb{Z}
, and balls of radius
\varepsilon\sqrt{t}
in their universal covers satisfy uniform geometry bounds at their own scale.

We now go back to the ODE version (
3.31
) of the Monge-Ampère equation, i.e.,

\displaystyle\begin{split}x^{2}u_{xx}+(n+1)xu_{x}-(n+1)u=G,\\
G=x^{-1}\varphi^{\bar{\beta}\alpha}u_{\alpha\bar{\beta}}-(2x)^{-2}u_{\theta\theta}-(n+1)(F+Q_{h}(u)),\end{split}

(4.3)

where
F
is smooth in
x_{\alpha},y_{\alpha}
and linear homogeneous in
x^{-1}u_{\theta\alpha},x^{-1}u_{\theta\bar{\alpha}},x^{-1}u_{\theta\theta}
and where
Q_{h}(u)
is a power series in
i\partial\overline{\partial}u
without constant or linear terms. We move the Laplacian in the
D
-directions out of the inhomogeneity,
G
, and average with respect to
\theta\in[0,2\pi]
. This yields

\displaystyle\begin{split}x^{2}\mathring{u}_{xx}+(n+1)x\mathring{u}_{x}-(n+1)\mathring{u}-x^{-1}\varphi^{\bar{\beta}\alpha}\mathring{u}_{\alpha\bar{\beta}}=\mathring{G},\\
\mathring{u}:=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}u\,d\theta,\;\,\mathring{G}:=-(n+1)\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}Q_{h}(u)\,d\theta.\end{split}

(4.4)

Crucially,
\mathring{u}
and
\mathring{G}
are globally well-defined on
(0,\delta]\times D
for some
\delta>0
, not just chart by chart. This is because the operator
Q_{h}
is global and, for all global functions
v
, the integral
\int_{0}^{2\pi}v\,d\theta
in each chart can be identified with the
S^{1}
-invariant global function
\int_{S^{1}}g^{*}v\,dg
, where
(g^{*}v)(p)=v(g\cdot p)
(using the given global
S^{1}
-action) and where
dg
denotes the standard measure on
S^{1}
. Another key point is that
|u-\mathring{u}|=O(e^{-\varepsilon/x})
by Lemma
3.5
, so
u
satisfies the estimates in (
4.2
) if and only if
\mathring{u}
does.

Let
\lambda_{\ell},\varphi_{\ell}
denote the eigenvalues and
L^{2}
-orthonormalized eigenfunctions of the operator
\varphi^{\bar{\beta}\alpha}\partial_{\alpha}\partial_{\bar{\beta}}
on
D
, where
\ell\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and
0=\lambda_{0}<\lambda_{1}\leqslant\lambda_{2}\leqslant\ldots
We will use the Fourier decompositions

\displaystyle\mathring{u}=\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\mathring{u}_{\ell}(x)\varphi_{\ell},\;\,\mathring{G}=\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\mathring{G}_{\ell}(x)\varphi_{\ell},

(4.5)

which converge in
C^{\infty}_{\rm loc}
as usual. Then (
4.4
) decomposes into the ODEs

\displaystyle x^{2}\mathring{u}_{\ell}^{\prime\prime}(x)+(n+1)x\mathring{u}_{\ell}^{\prime}(x)-(n+1)\mathring{u}_{\ell}(x)-\lambda_{\ell}x^{-1}\mathring{u}_{\ell}(x)=\mathring{G}_{\ell}(x).

(4.6)

We already treated the case
\ell=0
in Section
3
, although in Section
3
we were able to work pointwise on the cross-section with no need to integrate in any direction.

Denote
\alpha=n+2
. For all
\ell\in\mathbb{N}_{\geqslant 1}
denote

\displaystyle H_{1,\ell}(x)={\frac{{1}}{x^{n/2}}{{\sl I}_{\alpha}\left(2\,{\frac{\sqrt{\lambda_{\ell}}}{\sqrt{x}}}\right)}},\,\,H_{2,\ell}(x)={\frac{{1}}{x^{n/2}}{{\sl K}_{\alpha}\left(2\,{\frac{\sqrt{\lambda_{\ell}}}{\sqrt{x}}}\right)}},

(4.7)

where
I_{\alpha}(s)
is the modified Bessel function of the first kind and
K_{\alpha}(s)
is the modified Bessel function of the second kind. It is known that as
s\to\infty
there exist asymptotic expansions

\displaystyle I_{\alpha}(s)

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{s}}{\sqrt{2\pi s}}\left(1-\frac{4\alpha^{2}-1}{8s}+\frac{(4\alpha^{2}-1)(4\alpha^{2}-9)}{2!(8s)^{2}}-\frac{(4\alpha^{2}-1)(4\alpha^{2}-9)(4\alpha^{2}-25)}{3!(8s)^{3}}+\cdots\right),

(4.8)

\displaystyle K_{\alpha}(s)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2s}}e^{-s}\left(1+\frac{4\alpha^{2}-1}{8s}+\frac{(4\alpha^{2}-1)(4\alpha^{2}-9)}{2!(8s)^{2}}+\frac{(4\alpha^{2}-1)(4\alpha^{2}-9)(4\alpha^{2}-25)}{3!(8s)^{3}}+\cdots\right).

(4.9)

In particular, if
\alpha=n+2
is fixed, then for all
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
there exists a universal
s_{\varepsilon}>0
depending only on
\varepsilon
such that the two factors in parentheses in (
4.8
)–(
4.9
) are in the interval
[1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon]
for
s\geqslant s_{\varepsilon}
. This remark is useful because it provides uniform estimates for
H_{1,\ell}(x)
and
H_{2,\ell}(x)
as
x\to 0^{+}
, where the constants depend on
\ell

but the estimates hold for all
x\in(0,\delta]
with
\delta
independent of
\ell
.

We are now able to state the main result of this section. A similar representation formula was used in a different setting in
[
15
, Section 6]
. We will say more about this at the end of the paper.

Proposition 4.2
.

Assume only that
u=\mathbf{O}(x^{M})
as
x\to 0^{+}
for some
M>1
rather than
|u|={O}(x^{\infty})
. Denote the restriction of a function
v
on the total space to the slice
\{x=t\}
by
v(t)
. Fix
0<x_{0}\ll 1
. Then, with all of the infinite sums converging in
C^{0}_{\rm loc}
, it holds on
(0,x_{0}]\times D
that

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathring{u}(x)&=-\frac{x^{-n-1}}{n+2}\int_{0}^{x}t^{n}\mathring{G}_{0}(t)\,dt+\frac{x}{n+2}\int_{0}^{x}t^{-2}\mathring{G}_{0}(t)\,dt\\
&+\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}\left(\frac{u_{\ell}(x_{0})}{H_{2,\ell}(x_{0})}+\frac{2H_{1,\ell}(x_{0})}{H_{2,\ell}(x_{0})}\int_{0}^{x_{0}}t^{n-1}H_{2,\ell}(t)\mathring{G}_{\ell}(t)\,dt\right)H_{2,\ell}(x)\varphi_{\ell}\\
&-\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}\left(2H_{1,\ell}(x)\int_{0}^{x}t^{n-1}H_{2,\ell}(t)\mathring{G}_{\ell}(t)\,dt\right)\varphi_{\ell}-\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}\left(2H_{2,\ell}(x)\int_{x}^{x_{0}}t^{n-1}H_{1,\ell}(t)\mathring{G}_{\ell}(t)\,dt\right)\varphi_{\ell}.\end{split}

(4.10)

Proof.

Consider an ODE of the general form

\displaystyle x^{2}v^{\prime\prime}(x)+(n+1)xv^{\prime}(x)-(n+1)v(x)-\lambda x^{-1}v(x)=f(x)

(4.11)

for some constant
\lambda>0
.
A function of the form
v(x)=x^{-n/2}F(2\sqrt{\lambda/x})
is a homogeneous solution of this ODE if and only if
F(s)
satisfies the modified Bessel equation

\displaystyle s^{2}F^{\prime\prime}(s)+sF^{\prime}(s)-(\alpha^{2}+s^{2})F(s)=0

(4.12)

for
\alpha=n+2
.
Thus, the two homogeneous solutions of (
4.11
) are

\displaystyle H_{1}(x)={\frac{{1}}{x^{n/2}}{{\sl I}_{\alpha}\left(2\,{\frac{\sqrt{\lambda}}{\sqrt{x}}}\right)}},\,\,H_{2}(x)={\frac{{1}}{x^{n/2}}{{\sl K}_{\alpha}\left(2\,{\frac{\sqrt{\lambda}}{\sqrt{x}}}\right)}}

(4.13)

as above.
By Abel’s formula and by (
4.8
)–(
4.9
),

\displaystyle I_{\alpha}(s)K_{\alpha}^{\prime}(s)-I_{\alpha}^{\prime}(s)K_{\alpha}(s)=-\frac{1}{s}.

(4.14)

By variation of parameters, the general solution to (
4.11
) is

\displaystyle v(x)=c_{1}H_{1}(x)+c_{2}H_{2}(x)

\displaystyle+H_{1}(x)\int_{x}^{x_{0}}\frac{H_{2}(t)f(t)}{t^{2}W(t)}\,dt-H_{2}(x)\int_{x}^{x_{0}}\frac{H_{1}(t)f(t)}{t^{2}W(t)}\,dt,

(4.15)

where thanks to (
4.14
) the Wronskian
W(t)
is given by

\displaystyle W(t)=H_{1}(t)H_{2}^{\prime}(t)-H_{1}^{\prime}(t)H_{2}(t)=-\sqrt{\lambda}t^{-n-\frac{3}{2}}(I_{\alpha}K_{\alpha}^{\prime}-I_{\alpha}^{\prime}K_{\alpha})(2\sqrt{\lambda/t})=\frac{1}{2t^{n+1}}.

(4.16)

Thus, (
4.15
) simplifies to

\displaystyle v(x)=c_{1}H_{1}(x)+c_{2}H_{2}(x)

\displaystyle+2H_{1}(x)\int_{x}^{x_{0}}t^{n-1}H_{2}(t)f(t)\,dt-2H_{2}(x)\int_{x}^{x_{0}}t^{n-1}H_{1}(t)f(t)\,dt.

(4.17)

To solve out the constants
c_{1},c_{2}
, we first take
x=x_{0}
, so that

\displaystyle v(x_{0})=c_{1}H_{1}(x_{0})+c_{2}H_{2}(x_{0}).

(4.18)

Next we divide (
4.17
) by
H_{1}(x)
and let
x\rightarrow 0^{+}
. Notice that
H_{1}(x)\rightarrow\infty
and
H_{2}(x)\rightarrow 0
as
x\rightarrow 0^{+}
. We claim that if
|v|=O(1)
and
|f|=O(1)
, then it follows that

\displaystyle 0=c_{1}+2\int_{0}^{x_{0}}t^{n-1}H_{2}(t)f(t)\,dt,

(4.19)

so we can solve for
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
. This is clear except for the limit of the second integral term from (
4.17
). To see that this is zero, we can assume that
|{\int_{x}^{x_{0}}t^{n-1}H_{1}(t)f(t)\,dt}|
goes to
\infty
because otherwise (
4.19
) follows after passing to a subsequence. Then L’Hôpital’s rule requires us to check that

\displaystyle\frac{\frac{d}{dx}\int_{x}^{x_{0}}t^{n-1}H_{1}(t)f(t)\,dt}{\frac{d}{dx}\frac{H_{1}(x)}{H_{2}(x)}}=\frac{x^{n-1}H_{1}(x)f(x)}{\frac{W(x)}{H_{2}(x)^{2}}}\to 0,

(4.20)

and we can easily see that this is true by using (
4.16
) and (
4.8
)–(
4.9
). In sum, if
|f|=O(1)
as
x\to 0^{+}
and if
v
solving (
4.11
) remains uniformly bounded as well, then

\displaystyle\begin{split}v(x)&=\left(\frac{v(x_{0})}{H_{2}(x_{0})}+\frac{2H_{1}(x_{0})}{H_{2}(x_{0})}\int_{0}^{x_{0}}t^{n-1}H_{2}(t)f(t)\,dt\right)H_{2}(x)\\
&-2H_{1}(x)\int_{0}^{x}t^{n-1}H_{2}(t)f(t)\,dt-2H_{2}(x)\int_{x}^{x_{0}}t^{n-1}H_{1}(t)f(t)\,dt.\end{split}

(4.21)

To apply this result to our situation, we now let
\lambda=\lambda_{\ell}
,
f=\mathring{G}_{\ell}
and
v=\mathring{u}_{\ell}
for all
\ell\geqslant 1
. Thanks to our assumption that
u=\mathbf{O}(x^{M})
for some
M>1
, it is obvious that
v
and
f
are
O(1)
, so (
4.21
) holds. In fact, only
M\geqslant 0
is needed for this. For the missing case
\ell=0
we use the analogous representation formula (
3.37
), and here we only require that
M>\frac{1}{2}
. Thus, ignoring convergence issues,

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathring{u}(x)&=\boxed{\left(\frac{\mathring{u}_{0}(x_{0})}{x_{0}}+\frac{x_{0}^{-n-2}}{n+2}\int_{0}^{x_{0}}t^{n}\mathring{G}_{0}(t)\,dt\right)x}\\
&-\frac{x^{-n-2}}{n+2}\int_{0}^{x}t^{n}\mathring{G}_{0}(t)\,dt-\boxed{\frac{x}{n+2}\int_{x}^{x_{0}}t^{-2}\mathring{G}_{0}(t)\,dt}\\
&+\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}\left(\frac{\mathring{u}_{\ell}(x_{0})}{H_{2,\ell}(x_{0})}+\frac{2H_{1,\ell}(x_{0})}{H_{2,\ell}(x_{0})}\int_{0}^{x_{0}}t^{n-1}H_{2,\ell}(t)\mathring{G}_{\ell}(t)\,dt\right)H_{2,\ell}(x)\varphi_{\ell}\\
&-\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}\left(2H_{1,\ell}(x)\int_{0}^{x}t^{n-1}H_{2,\ell}(t)\mathring{G}_{\ell}(t)\,dt\right)\varphi_{\ell}-\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}\left(2H_{2,\ell}(x)\int_{x}^{x_{0}}t^{n-1}H_{1,\ell}(t)\mathring{G}_{\ell}(t)\,dt\right)\varphi_{\ell}.\end{split}

(4.22)

The following claim, which is not optimal but sufficient, makes this rigorous.

Claim
: The three infinite sums in (
4.22
) converge in
C^{0}_{\rm loc}
. Moreover, for all
N>0
there exists a small number
x_{0,N}>0
such that if
x_{0}\leqslant x_{0,N}
, then each of these sums is pointwise
O(x^{N})
as
x\to 0^{+}
.

Assume for a moment that the Claim is true. Note that
|t^{n}\mathring{G}_{0}(t)|=O(t^{n+2M})
because
u=\mathbf{O}(x^{M})
. Thus, the first un-boxed term on the right-hand side of (
4.22
) is
O(x^{2M-1})=o(x)
because
M>1
. By writing
\int_{x}^{x_{0}}=\int_{0}^{x_{0}}-\int_{0}^{x}
, we can split the second box in (
4.22
) into a constant multiple of
x
plus a term which is
{O}(x^{2M})=o(x)
because
M>\frac{1}{2}
. Then the constant multiple of
x
must cancel the first box in (
4.22
) to make
\mathring{u}(x)=o(x)
, so the proposition follows. Thus, it remains to prove the Claim.

We begin with two preliminary estimates. First, there is a constant
C
such that for all
\ell\geqslant 1
,

\displaystyle\|\varphi_{\ell}\|_{L^{\infty}(D)}\leqslant C\ell^{\frac{1}{2}}.

(4.23)

This follows from a standard estimate for eigenfunctions of the Laplacian on a closed manifold together with Weyl’s law. Second, for all
x\in(0,1]
, all
\ell\geqslant 1
and all
N_{1},N_{2}>0
,

\displaystyle|\mathring{u}_{\ell}(x)|+|\mathring{G}_{\ell}(x)|\leqslant C_{N_{1},N_{2}}\ell^{-N_{1}}x^{N_{2}}.

(4.24)

Indeed, for any fixed
x
, integration by parts over
D
shows as usual that
\mathring{u}_{\ell}(x),\mathring{G}_{\ell}(x)
decay faster than any power of
\ell
, and the constants in these estimates can be bounded by the norms of sufficiently high tangential derivatives of
\mathring{u}(x),\mathring{G}(x)
. Since
u=\mathbf{O}(x^{M})
, any tangential derivative of
\mathring{u}(x)
is
O(x^{\infty})
. The same holds for
\mathring{G}(x)
because as in the proof of Lemma
3.9
, we can decompose
u
into an
\mathbf{O}(x^{M})
radial part plus
\mathbf{O}(x^{\infty})
, and this shows that
\mathring{G}(x)
decomposes into an
\mathbf{O}(x^{2M})
radial part plus
\mathbf{O}(x^{\infty})
.

We now apply (
4.23
)–(
4.24
) to each term of the three infinite sums in (
4.22
). Then we can see that in order to prove the Claim, it will be enough to prove a bound of the form

\displaystyle\frac{H_{2,\ell}(x)}{H_{2,\ell}(x_{0})}+H_{1,\ell}(x)\sup_{0<t\leqslant x}\biggl(t^{\bar{N}}H_{2,\ell}(t)\biggr)+H_{2,\ell}(x)\sup_{x\leqslant t\leqslant x_{0}}\biggl(t^{\bar{N}}H_{1,\ell}(t)\biggr)\leqslant C_{x_{0},\bar{N}}\ell^{N_{0}}x^{\bar{N}-N_{0}}

(4.25)

for some universal
N_{0}>0
and for all
\bar{N}\geqslant N_{0}
. This bound needs to hold for all
x\in(0,x_{0}]
and
\ell\geqslant 1
, but we can assume that
x_{0}
is less than some small number
x_{0,\bar{N}}>0
depending only on
\bar{N}
.

By the remark after (
4.8
)–(
4.9
), for all
\varepsilon>0
there exists an
x_{0,\varepsilon}>0
independent of
\ell
such that for all
0<x\leqslant x_{0}\leqslant x_{0,\varepsilon}
, all
\ell\geqslant 1
and all
\bar{N}>0
, the left-hand side of (
4.25
) is bounded by

\displaystyle\begin{split}&(1+\varepsilon)\left(\frac{x}{x_{0}}\right)^{-\frac{n}{2}+\frac{1}{4}}x^{\bar{N}}\sup_{0<t\leqslant x_{0}}\left(t^{-\bar{N}}e^{2\sqrt{\lambda_{\ell}}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{x_{0}}}-\frac{1}{\sqrt{t}}\right)}\right)^{\small\textcircled{1}}\\

fixed, and with
\alpha\to 0
,

fixed. However, for us, it will sometimes be beneficial (see
\textcircled{3}
) or at least harmless (see
\textcircled{4}
–
\textcircled{5}
) to choose
r\ll\frac{1}{\delta\alpha}t^{\alpha}
.

\textcircled{2}
Here we have
\alpha=\frac{1}{2}
because the domain of integration is
t\in[x_{*},x_{0}]
. Thus, our only option is to set
r\sim\sqrt{t}
in (
4.37
). More precisely, we let
r=\sqrt{t}/2
. Then we claim that, as expected,

\sup\nolimits_{A_{r}(t)}|u|\leqslant C_{0}CH(t)\;\,\text{for all}\;\,t\in[x_{*},x_{0}].

(4.44)

Indeed, if
A_{r}(t)\subset\{x_{*}\leqslant x\leqslant x_{0}\}
, this follows from the first line of (
4.34
) (with
x=x_{*}
) precisely by the above argument motivating the choice
r\lesssim\sqrt{t}
. For
t
so close to
x_{*}
that
A_{r}(t)\not\subset\{x_{*}\leqslant x\leqslant x_{0}\}
, we also need to use the second line of (
4.34
) and the trivial fact that
\sup_{A_{r}(t)\cap\{x<x_{*}\}}CH(x_{*})(\frac{x}{x_{*}})^{N}=CH(x_{*})
. Similary, for
t
so close
x_{0}
that
A_{r}(t)\not\subset\{x_{*}\leqslant x\leqslant x_{0}\}
, we moreover need to use our assumption that
|u|\leqslant C_{M}x^{M}
on
A_{r}(t)\cap\{x_{0}<x\leqslant 1\}
and choose
C
large enough so that

C_{M}(x_{0}(1+\sqrt{x_{0}}/2))^{M}\leqslant CH(x_{0}).

(4.45)

Having proved (
4.44
), we now combine this with (
4.37
) to obtain that

\begin{split}\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}\textcircled{2}&\leqslant\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}O(\ell^{-\infty})\,x_{*}^{-\frac{n}{2}+\frac{1}{4}}e^{-\frac{2\sqrt{\lambda_{\ell}}}{\sqrt{x_{*}}}}\int_{x_{*}}^{x_{0}}t^{n-1}t^{-\frac{n}{2}+\frac{1}{4}}e^{\frac{2\sqrt{\lambda_{\ell}}}{\sqrt{t}}}t^{\eta-1}CH(t)\,dt\\
&\leqslant\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}O(\ell^{-\infty})\,x_{*}^{-\frac{n}{2}+\frac{1}{4}}e^{-\frac{2\sqrt{\lambda_{\ell}}}{\sqrt{x_{*}}}}\int_{x_{*}}^{x_{0}}Ct^{-\frac{3}{2}+\eta}e^{\frac{2\sqrt{\lambda_{\ell}}-2\sqrt{\lambda_{1}}}{\sqrt{t}}}dt.\end{split}

(4.46)

This holds for any given
\eta\in(0,M-1)
if
x_{0}\leqslant x_{0,\eta}
for some absolute number
x_{0,\eta}>0
.

We now choose
\eta>\frac{1}{2}
(this is possible because
M>\frac{3}{2}
), evaluate the integral directly if
\lambda_{\ell}=\lambda_{1}
, and estimate it using Lemma
4.3
if
\lambda_{\ell}>\lambda_{1}
. Fixing
\varepsilon=1
in Lemma
4.3
and also assuming that

3\eta\sqrt{x_{0}}\leqslant\min\{\sqrt{\lambda_{\ell}}:\ell\geqslant 2,\lambda_{\ell}>\lambda_{1}\}-\sqrt{\lambda_{1}},

(4.47)

we thus obtain the following estimate:

\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}\textcircled{2}\leqslant C\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}\left[O(\ell^{-\infty})\,x_{*}^{-\frac{n}{2}+\frac{1}{4}}e^{-\frac{2\sqrt{\lambda_{\ell}}}{\sqrt{x_{*}}}}\begin{cases}x_{0}^{\eta-\frac{1}{2}}/(2\eta-1)&\text{if}\;\,\lambda_{\ell}=\lambda_{1}\\
x_{*}^{\eta}e^{\frac{2\sqrt{\lambda_{\ell}}-2\sqrt{\lambda_{1}}}{\sqrt{x_{*}}}}&\text{if}\;\,\lambda_{\ell}>\lambda_{1}\end{cases}\right]\leqslant\frac{C_{0}x_{0}^{\eta-\frac{1}{2}}\cdot CH(x_{*})}{\min\{1,2\eta-1\}}.

(4.48)

This will be bounded by
CH(x_{*})/20
if we decrease the absolute number
x_{0,\eta}>0
.

\textcircled{3}
While shorter than
\textcircled{2}
, this step may be more confusing because the domain is now
t\in(0,x_{*}]
, so (
4.34
) bounds
|u|
in terms of a polynomial rather than an exponential, but we are also still integrating this against the exponential-type function
H_{2,\ell}(t)
. Thus, it may not be obvious how
r
should be chosen (from
r\sim\sqrt{t}
for exponentials to
r\sim 1
for polynomials). However, we will see that the “easy” choice,
r\sim\sqrt{t}
, which does not require us to assume that
D
is flat, is actually better and sufficient.

First, while the analog of (
4.44
) would still hold for
A_{r}(t)\subset\{x\leqslant x_{*}\}
with
r\sim 1
, it would be false for
A_{r}(t)\not\subset\{x\leqslant x_{*}\}
with
r\sim 1
because the barrier function of (
4.34
) is exponential on
(x_{*},x_{0}]
. It is however true in both cases with
r\sim\sqrt{t}
. Thus, setting
r=\sqrt{t}/2
,

\sup_{A_{r}(t)}|u|\leqslant C_{0}CH(x_{*})\left(\frac{t}{x_{*}}\right)^{N}\text{for all}\;\,t\in(0,x_{*}],

(4.49)

where the case
A_{r}(t)\not\subset(0,x_{*}]
follows from the fact that

t(1+\sqrt{t}/2)>x_{*}\Longrightarrow\sup\{H(y):y\in(x_{*},t(1+\sqrt{t}/2)]\}\leqslant C_{0}H(x_{*})\leqslant C_{0}H(x_{*})\left(\frac{t}{x_{*}}\right)^{N}.

(4.50)

Second, by combining (
4.37
) and (
4.49
), we obtain for all
\eta\in(0,M-1)
that if
x_{0}\leqslant x_{0,\eta}
, then

\begin{split}\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}\textcircled{3}&\leqslant\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}O(\ell^{-\infty})\,x_{*}^{-\frac{n}{2}+\frac{1}{4}}e^{\frac{2\sqrt{\lambda_{\ell}}}{\sqrt{x_{*}}}}\int_{0}^{x_{*}}t^{n-1}t^{-\frac{n}{2}+\frac{1}{4}}e^{-\frac{2\sqrt{\lambda_{\ell}}}{\sqrt{t}}}t^{\eta-1}CH(x_{*})\left(\frac{t}{x_{*}}\right)^{N}dt\\
&\leqslant\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}O(\ell^{-\infty})\,x_{*}^{-\frac{n}{2}+\frac{1}{4}-N}e^{\frac{2\sqrt{\lambda_{\ell}}}{\sqrt{x_{*}}}}\int_{0}^{x_{*}}t^{\frac{n}{2}-\frac{7}{4}+\eta+N}e^{-\frac{2\sqrt{\lambda_{\ell}}}{\sqrt{t}}}dt\cdot CH(x_{*}).\end{split}

(4.51)

We can now apply Lemma
4.3
directly and without assuming that
\eta>\frac{1}{2}
:

\frac{1}{CH(x_{*})}\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}\textcircled{3}\leqslant\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}O(\ell^{-\infty})\,x_{*}^{-\frac{n}{2}+\frac{1}{4}-N}e^{\frac{2\sqrt{\lambda_{\ell}}}{\sqrt{x_{*}}}}x_{*}^{\frac{n}{2}-\frac{1}{4}+\eta+N}e^{-\frac{2\sqrt{\lambda_{\ell}}}{\sqrt{x_{*}}}}\leqslant C_{0}x_{*}^{\eta}.

(4.52)

\textcircled{4}
Here and in
\textcircled{5}
, the upper barrier for
|u|
from (
4.34
) and the function against which we need to integrate this barrier over the interval
(0,x_{*}]
in (
4.41
) are polynomials. Away from
t=x_{*}
this suggests that we should apply Claim 2 with
r\sim 1
. It is actually possible to work with
r\sim t^{\alpha}
for any
\alpha\in[0,\frac{1}{2})
, but choosing
\alpha>0
makes the algebra longer while in the proof of Claim 2 only the Schauder estimates for
\alpha=\frac{1}{2}
are easy and the ones for
\alpha\in[0,\frac{1}{2})
are all equally difficult, requiring us to assume that
D
is flat. Thus, for simplicity, we will stick to the choice
r\sim 1
. However, as
t
approaches
x_{*}
we do need to choose
r
smaller than this because the right-hand side of (
4.34
) is exponential rather than polynomial on
(x_{*},x_{0}]
. As it turns out, the following straightforward choice will be good enough:

r=r(t):=\max\left\{\frac{1}{2}\left(1-\frac{t}{x_{*}}\right),\frac{1}{2}x_{*}^{\frac{1}{2}}\right\}\,\text{for all}\;\,t\in(0,x_{*}].

(4.53)

To see that this makes (
4.49
) true for all
t\in(0,x_{*}]
, first observe that (
4.34
) trivially implies (
4.49
) if the
\sup
is taken over
A_{r}(t)\cap\{x\leqslant x_{*}\}
. Now
A_{r}(t)\not\subset\{x\leqslant x_{*}\}
is possible only if
t\in(x
∗
-\,x
{}_{*}^{3/2}
,x_{*}]
as otherwise the max in (

by computation. But if
t\in(x
∗
-\,x
{}_{*}^{3/2}
,x_{*}]
, then

, so we can deal with the sup over
A_{r}(t)\cap\{x_{*}<x\}
(if nonempty) by replacing
\sqrt{t}/2
with
\sqrt{t}
in (

As in
\textcircled{3}
, given (
4.49
), we can proceed using (
4.37
): for all
\eta\in(\frac{1}{2},M-1)
, if
x_{0}\leqslant x_{0,\eta}
, then

\frac{\textcircled{4}}{CH(x_{*})}\leqslant x_{*}^{-n-1}\int_{0}^{x_{*}}t^{n}\left[\frac{t^{\eta}}{r(t)^{2}}\left(\frac{t}{x_{*}}\right)^{N}\right]dt\leqslant x_{*}^{\eta-1}\int_{0}^{x_{*}}\frac{dt}{r(t)^{2}}.

(4.54)

Here we have simply estimated all powers of
t
by powers of
x_{*}
.
We split the integral in (

0
to
x
∗
-\,x
{}_{*}^{3/2}
(part I) and from
x
∗
-\,x

(part II). Then

\eqref{eq-Genius}_{\rm I}\leqslant 4x_{*}^{\eta-1}\int_{0}^{x_{*}-x_{*}^{3/2}}\left(1-\frac{t}{x_{*}}\right)^{-2}dt\leqslant 4x_{*}^{\eta-\frac{1}{2}},\;\,\eqref{eq-Genius}_{\rm II}\leqslant 4x_{*}^{\eta-1}x_{*}^{\frac{3}{2}}x_{*}^{-1}=4x_{*}^{\eta-\frac{1}{2}}.

(4.55)

\textcircled{5}
We can proceed as in
\textcircled{4}

are positive. Thus, even though we know that the integral converges because

so that all integrals along the way have an acceptable finite bound. This is where the condition

comes in: it lets us choose an
\eta\in(\frac{1}{2},M-1)
such that
-2+\eta+N>-1
.
With this, and avoiding bounding powers of
t
by powers of
x_{*}
if the exponents are negative, we obtain for

that

\begin{split}\frac{\textcircled{5}}{CH(x_{*})}\leqslant x_{*}^{1-N}\int_{0}^{x_{*}}t^{-2+\eta+N}\frac{dt}{r(t)^{2}}.\end{split}

(4.56)

If

\textcircled{4}
. Otherwise we split this integral into three parts: from

to
x_{*}/2
(part I), where

, so we can drop

and integrate out the

x

{}_{*}^{3/2}

\textcircled{4}

\textcircled{4}
, and part I is even smaller:

\begin{split}\eqref{eq-Genius2}_{\rm I}\leqslant 16x_{*}^{1-N}\int_{0}^{\frac{x_{*}}{2}}t^{-2+\eta+N}\,dt\leqslant\frac{16(\frac{1}{2})^{\eta+N-1}}{\eta+N-1}x_{*}^{\eta}.\end{split}

(4.57)

This completes the proof of the proposition, and hence of the Main Theorem.
∎

To conclude this paper, we comment on a different geometric problem where the same method can be used to prove sharp estimates of solutions. This concerns the complete Calabi-Yau metrics of
[
24
, Thm 4.2]
, which also play a role in the recent papers
[
3
,
21
]
mentioned in the Introduction.

Interestingly, in the Calabi-Yau setting, scaling the Hermitian metric on the underlying line bundle changes the Calabi model potential by terms that are not
O(1)
, and there are no polynomially decaying solutions or supersolutions to the relevant PDE. This means in particular that barrier arguments and expansion methods as in
[
12
,
16
,
18
,
19
,
25
]
cannot be used. However, it was proved in
[
15
, Cor 1.2]
via separation of variables that bounded solutions converge to constants at infinity faster than any power of the metric distance
\rho
. Independently, using an energy decay argument, it was proved in
[
10
, p.366]
that this convergence rate is
O(\exp(-\delta\rho^{\frac{n}{n+1}}))
for some
\delta>0
in dimension
n
.

In the present paper, all of the above methods (barriers, energy decay, separation of variables) were combined together and fed into a new continuity argument to obtain a very precise description of the solutions in the cuspidal Kähler-Einstein setting. It is then also possible to go back to the Calabi-Yau setting and use a similar approach (energy decay, separation of variables, continuity) to get a sharper estimate of the convergence rate there. In fact, one can now prove that this rate is

\displaystyle O(\rho^{-\frac{n-2}{2(n+1)}}\exp(-\delta(n)\sqrt{\lambda_{1}}\rho^{\frac{n}{n+1}})),

(4.58)

with
\lambda_{1}
the first eigenvalue of the Calabi-Yau metric on the divisor, as conjectured in
[
10
, p.366]
.
