On the asymptotic assumptions for Milne-like spacetimes
Eric Ling, Annachiara Piubello

Abstract
Milne-like spacetimes are a class of hyperbolic FLRW spacetimes which admit continuous spacetime extensions through the big bang, τ = 0 \tau=0 . The existence of the extension follows from writing the metric in conformal Minkowskian coordinates and assuming that the scale factor satisfies a ⁡ ( τ ) = τ + o ⁡ ( τ 1 + ε ) a(\tau)=\tau+o(\tau^{1+\varepsilon}) as τ → 0 \tau\to 0 for some ε > 0 \varepsilon>0 . This asymptotic assumption implies a ⁡ ( τ ) = τ + o ⁡ ( τ ) a(\tau)=\tau+o(\tau) . In this paper, we show that a ⁡ ( τ ) = τ + o ⁡ ( τ ) a(\tau)=\tau+o(\tau) is not sufficient to achieve an extension through τ = 0 \tau=0 , but it is necessary provided its derivative converges as τ → 0 \tau\to 0 . We also show that the ε \varepsilon in a ⁡ ( τ ) = τ + o ⁡ ( τ 1 + ε ) a(\tau)=\tau+o(\tau^{1+\varepsilon}) is not necessary to achieve an extension through τ = 0 \tau=0 .

Milne-like spacetimes are a class of hyperbolic FLRW spacetimes which admit continuous spacetime extensions through the big bang,
\tau=0

The scale factor for a Milne-like spacetime satisfies, by definition,
a(\tau)=\tau+o(\tau^{1+\varepsilon})

The asymptotic assumption
a(\tau)=\tau+o(\tau^{1+\varepsilon})

\lim_{\tau\to 0}a^{\prime}(\tau)
exists

Our conventions will follow

Let

curve such that
\gamma^{\prime}(t)
is future directed timelike for all

, including its break points and endpoints (understood as one-sided derivatives).
Past directed timelike curves
are defined time-dually.

Suppose

spacetime,

Let

C^{k}
spacetime
(M,g)
with respect to
\phi\colon M\to M_{\text{{\rm ext}}}
. The topological boundary of
M

is denoted by

. A future directed timelike curve
\gamma\colon[a,b]\to M_{\text{{\rm ext}}}

.
Past terminating timelike curves
are defined time-dually. The

of

are

\displaystyle\partial^{+}M\,

\displaystyle=\,\{p\in\partial M\mid\text{there is a future terminating timelike curve for $p$}\}

\displaystyle\partial^{-}M\,

\displaystyle=\,\{p\in\partial M\mid\text{there is a past terminating timelike curve for $p$}\}.

Example.

.

3
The necessity of
a(\tau)=\tau+o(\tau)
for past boundaries

In the previous section, we saw that the global coordinate system
\phi\colon M\to\phi(M)\subset\mathbb{R}^{4}
given by eq. (
2.6
) admits a past boundary for Milne-like spacetimes. By definition, the scale factor for a Milne-like spacetime satisfies
a(\tau)=\tau+o(\tau^{1+\varepsilon})
for some
\varepsilon>0
. Note that this implies that
a(\tau)=\tau+o(\tau)
. In this section, we show that this condition on
a(\tau)
is necessary for an extension provided
\lim_{\tau\to 0}a^{\prime}(\tau)
exists. First, we generalize the properties necessary for
\phi=(t,x,y,z)
to admit a past boundary for
M
.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
(M,g)
be a
C^{0}
hyperbolic FLRW spacetime satisfying
a(\tau)\to 0
as
\tau\to 0
. Then,
\phi(M)
is given by eq.
(
2.7
)
and
\phi
admits a past boundary for
(M,g)
if and only if
b(\tau)/a(\tau)
converges to a nonzero finite number as
\tau\to 0
.

Proof.

Suppose
b(\tau)/a(\tau)\to c\in(0,\infty)
as
\tau\to 0
. Then there exists a
\delta>0
such that
|b(\tau)/a(\tau)-c|<1
for all
0<\tau<\delta
. Therefore
(c-1)a(\tau)<b(\tau)<(c+1)a(\tau)
for all
0<\tau<\delta
. Since
a(\tau)\to 0
as
\tau\to 0
, the squeeze theorem implies that
b(\tau)\to 0
as
\tau\to 0
. Consequently,
\phi(M)
is given by (
2.7
). Thus, by eq. (
2
), a continuous extension,
(M_{\text{{\rm ext}}},g_{\text{{\rm ext}}})
, of
(M,g)
is given by defining
g_{\text{{\rm ext}}}=(1/c^{2})\eta
for points
t\leq r
and
g_{\text{{\rm ext}}}=g
for points
t>r
, where
\eta
is the Minkowski metric. Consequently,
\phi
admits a past boundary for
(M,g)
with the past boundary coinciding with the lightcone
t=r
.

Conversely, suppose
\phi(M)
is given by (
2.7
) and
\phi
admits a past boundary for
(M,g)
. By definition there is a continuous extension
(M_{\text{{\rm ext}}},g_{\text{{\rm ext}}})
of
(M,g)
with
\phi(M)\subset M_{\text{{\rm ext}}}\subset\mathbb{R}^{4}
. Define
C=\{(t,x,y,z)\mid t=r\}
and
D=\{(t,x,y,z)\mid t^{2}-r^{2}=\lim_{\tau\to\tau_{\rm max}}b^{2}(\tau)\}
. (If
b^{2}(\tau)\to\infty
as
\tau\to\tau_{\rm max}
, then take
D=\emptyset
.) Since
M_{\text{{\rm ext}}}\subset\mathbb{R}^{4}
, the topological boundary of
\phi(M)
within
M_{\text{{\rm ext}}}
satisfies
\partial\phi(M)\subset C\cup D.

Since
\phi
admits a past boundary for
(M,g)
, there is a point
p\in C\cup D
and a future directed timelike curve
\gamma\colon[0,1]\to M_{\text{{\rm ext}}}
with
\gamma(0)=p
and
\gamma\big((0,1]\big)\subset\phi(M)
. Therefore
p\notin D
since continuous extensions preserve time orientations. Thus
p\in C
. Let
g_{\mu\nu}
denote the components of the metric with respect to the coordinates
(t,x,y,z)
. Since we have a continuous extension, there is a Lorentzian metric
g_{\mu\nu}(p)
for
T_{p}M_{\text{{\rm ext}}}
such that
g_{\mu\nu}(q)\to g_{\mu\nu}(p)
as
q\to p
. Applying this to points
q\in M
, it follows that
g_{xx}(p)=g_{yy}(p)=g_{zz}(p)=-g_{tt}(p)>0
and all other components are zero. Put
k=\sqrt{g_{xx}(p)}
and
\Omega(\tau)=a(\tau)/b(\tau)
. We want to show
\Omega(\tau)\to k
as
\tau\to 0
. Seeking a contradiction, suppose this is not the case. Then there is an
\varepsilon>0
and a sequence of points
\tau_{n}\to 0
such that
|\Omega(\tau_{n})-k|\geq\varepsilon
. For this choice of
\varepsilon
, there is a neighborhood
U\subset M_{\text{{\rm ext}}}
of
p
such that
|\sqrt{g_{xx}(q)}-k|<\varepsilon
for all
q\in U
. Since
b(\tau)\to 0
as
\tau\to 0
and the hyperboloids
H_{\tau}=\{(t,x,y,z)\mid t>0\text{ and }t^{2}-r^{2}=b^{2}(\tau)\}
foliate
\phi(M)
, there is an
n
large enough such that
H_{\tau_{n}}
intersects
U
. For this choice of
n
, let
q\in H_{\tau_{n}}\cap U
. Then we have
\sqrt{g_{xx}(q)}=\Omega(\tau_{n})
. This gives the contradiction
\varepsilon\leq|\Omega(\tau_{n})-k|<\varepsilon
.
∎

The proof of (1) in Theorem
1.1
now follows from the following proposition.

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
(M,g)
be a
C^{1}
hyperbolic FLRW spacetime satisfying
a(\tau)\to 0
as
\tau\to 0
. Suppose
\phi(M)
is given by
(
2.7
)
and
\phi
admits a past boundary for
(M,g)
. If
\lim_{\tau\to 0}a^{\prime}(\tau)
exists, then
a(\tau)=\tau+o(\tau)
.

Proof.

Lemma
3.1
implies that
b(\tau)/a(\tau)
converges to a nonzero finite number as
\tau\to 0
. We have

\ln\left(\frac{b(\tau)}{a(\tau)}\right)\,=\,\int_{\tau_{0}}^{\tau}\frac{1}{a}-\ln\big(a(\tau)\big)\,=\,\int_{\tau_{0}}^{\tau}\frac{1-a^{\prime}}{a}+\ln\big(a(\tau_{0})\big).

(3.8)

Consequently,
b/a
converges to a nonzero finite number as
\tau\to 0
if and only if the improper integral
\int_{0}^{\tau_{0}}\frac{1-a^{\prime}}{a}:=\lim_{\tau\to 0}\int_{\tau}^{\tau_{0}}\frac{1-a^{\prime}}{a}
satisfies
\int_{0}^{\tau_{0}}\frac{1-a^{\prime}}{a}\in\mathbb{R}
.

Suppose
\lim_{\tau\to 0}a^{\prime}(\tau)=k\in[-\infty,\infty]
. An application of l’Hôpital’s rule
[
33
, Thm. 5.13]
shows that

\lim_{\tau\to 0}\frac{a(\tau)}{\tau}\,=\,\lim_{\tau\to 0}\frac{a^{\prime}(\tau)}{1}\,=\,k.

(3.9)

Now we show
k=1
. Since
a(\tau)>0
, it follows that
k\in[0,\infty]
. First, consider
0\leq k<1
. Set
\varepsilon=(1-k)/2
. There is a
\delta>0
such that
a(\tau)<(k+\varepsilon)\tau
and
a^{\prime}(\tau)<k+\varepsilon
for
0<\tau<\delta
. Therefore

\int_{0}^{\delta}\frac{1-a^{\prime}}{a}\,>\,\int_{0}^{\delta}\frac{1-(k+\varepsilon)}{(k+\varepsilon)\tau}d\tau\,=\,\left(\frac{2}{k+1}-1\right)\int_{0}^{\delta}\frac{1}{\tau}d\tau\,=\,\infty,

which contradicts
\int_{0}^{\tau_{0}}\frac{1-a^{\prime}}{a}\in\mathbb{R}
. Similarly, if
1<k\leq\infty
, one obtains a similar contradiction. Thus
k=1
. Then eq. (
3.9
) implies
a(\tau)=\tau+o(\tau)
.
∎

We briefly allude to why the assumptions in Proposition
3.2
are not optimal. Let
\bar{a}\colon[0,\infty)\to[0,\infty)
be the continuous extension of
a(\tau)
defined by
\bar{a}(0)=0
and
\bar{a}(\tau)=a(\tau)
for
\tau>0
. The proof of Proposition
3.2
shows that
\bar{a}
is
C^{1}
and
\bar{a}^{\prime}(0)=1
. However, this is more than necessary to obtain
a(\tau)=\tau+o(\tau)
. It would have been sufficient to simply show
\overline{a}^{\prime}
is differentiable at
\tau=0
and
\overline{a}^{\prime}(0)=1
.
For example, consider the scale factor
a(\tau)=\tau+\tau^{2}\sin(1/\tau)
. Clearly
a(\tau)=\tau+o(\tau)
and hence
\overline{a}^{\prime}(0)=1
. Moreover,
a(\tau)=\tau+o(\tau^{3/2})
so the corresponding hyperbolic FLRW spacetime is Milne-like, and thus
\phi
admits a past boundary. However,
\overline{a}(\tau)
is not
C^{1}
at
\tau=0
since
a^{\prime}(\tau)
diverges as
\tau\to 0
even though
\overline{a}^{\prime}(0)=1
.

Proposition
3.2
raises a natural question:

\emph{\text{Under what conditions does }}\lim_{\tau\to 0}a^{\prime}(\tau)\emph{\text{ exist? }}

(3.10)

We will explore this question for the remainder of this section.

By equation (
3.8
), we are motivated to define the continuous function
p\colon(0,\tau_{0}]\to\mathbb{R}
via

p(\tau)\,=\,\frac{1-a^{\prime}(\tau)}{a(\tau)},

(3.11)

and we see that
b(\tau)/a(\tau)
converges to a nonzero finite number if and only if
p
is integrable,
\int_{0}^{\tau}p\in\mathbb{R}
. Note that this is an improper integral, i.e.
\int_{0}^{\tau}p:=\lim_{\tau\to 0}\int_{\tau}^{\tau_{0}}p
.

Consider the following boundary value problem for
a\colon(0,\tau_{0}]\to(0,\infty)
:

\displaystyle\begin{cases}&a^{\prime}+pa\,=\,1,\\
&a(\tau)\to 0\text{ as }\tau\to 0,\\
&p\colon(0,\tau_{0}]\to\mathbb{R}$ is continuous and $\int_{0}^{\tau_{0}}p\in\mathbb{R}.\end{cases}

(3.12)

A
C^{1}
solution to this boundary value problem is

a(\tau)\,=\,\frac{1}{\mu(\tau)}\int_{0}^{\tau}\mu\>\>\>\>\text{ where }\>\>\>\>\mu(\tau)\,=\,e^{\int_{0}^{\tau}p}.

(3.13)

Remark.

\mu
is well defined since
\int_{0}^{\tau_{0}}p\in\mathbb{R}
. To see that (
3.13
) is a solution to the boundary value problem (
3.12
), note that the fundamental theorem of calculus still holds for improper integrals; this can be verified by breaking up the integral:
\int_{0}^{\tau}p=\int_{0}^{\varepsilon}p+\int_{\varepsilon}^{\tau}p
. The same trick can be used to show that
a(\tau)\to 0
as
\tau\to 0
.

As shown in eq. (
3.8
), the assumption that
b/a
converges to a nonzero finite number is equivalent to
\int_{0}^{\tau_{0}}p\in\mathbb{R}
which means that
p
is integrable as an improper integral. If
\int_{0}^{\tau_{0}}|p|<\infty
, then we say
p
is
absolutely integrable
as an improper integral; this is the same as saying
p\in L^{1}
. The following lemma shows that solution (
3.13
) is unique provided we make the slightly stronger assumption that
p
is absolutely integrable. An example of a function
p(\tau)
that satisfies
\int_{0}^{1}p\in\mathbb{R}
but
p\notin L^{1}
is
p(\tau)=\frac{1}{\tau}\sin\left(\frac{1}{\tau}\right)
.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
p\colon(0,\tau_{0}]\to\mathbb{R}
be continuous. If
p\in L^{1}
, then
(
3.13
)
is the unique
C^{1}
solution to the boundary value problem
(
3.12
)
.

Proof.

Since this is not an initial-value problem, one cannot simply invoke the standard uniqueness theorem from ODEs. Nevertheless, the proof uses similar ideas; we include it for completeness.

Suppose
a_{1}
and
a_{2}
are two different
C^{1}
solutions to (
3.12
). Since
p\in L^{1}
, there is a
\tau_{1}>0
such that
\int_{0}^{\tau_{1}}|p|<1
. Let
f\colon[0,\tau_{1}]\to\mathbb{R}
be the continuous function defined by
f(0)=0
and
f(\tau)=a_{1}(\tau)-a_{2}(\tau)
for
\tau\in(0,\tau_{1}]
.

We first show that
a_{1}=a_{2}
on
(0,\tau_{1}]
. Seeking a contradiction, suppose this is not the case. Since
|f|
is continuous, it obtains its maximum at some
\tau_{2}\in[0,\tau_{1}]
, and since
a_{1}
and
a_{2}
are different, we have
|f(\tau_{2})|>0
.
If a
C^{1}
function
a(\tau)
satisfies
a^{\prime}+pa=1
and
a(\tau)\to 0
as
\tau\to 0
, then it satisfies the (improper) integral equation
a(\tau)=\int_{0}^{\tau}(1-pa)
. Applying this to
a_{1}
and
a_{2}
, it follows that

|f(\tau_{2})|\,=\,\left|\int_{0}^{\tau_{2}}(1-pa_{1})-\int_{0}^{\tau_{2}}(1-pa_{2})\right|\,\leq\,\int_{0}^{\tau_{2}}|p(a_{1}-a_{2})|\,\leq\,|f(\tau_{2})|\int_{0}^{\tau_{2}}|p|<|f(\tau_{2})|,

which yields a contradiction. Thus
a_{1}=a_{2}
on
(0,\tau_{1}]
.

Now we show
a_{1}=a_{2}
on
(0,\tau_{0}]
. Let
A=\{\tau\in(0,\tau_{0}]\mid a_{1}(\tau)=a_{2}(\tau)\}
. The above paragraph shows that
A
is nonempty. Therefore, by connectedness, it suffices to show that
A
is both open and closed. That
A
is open follows from the standard existence and uniqueness theorem of ordinary differential equations. That
A
is closed follows from continuity of
a_{1}-a_{2}
.
∎

The following proposition provides an answer to question (
3.10
).

Proposition 3.4
.

Let
(M,g)
be a
C^{2}
hyperbolic FLRW spacetime satisfying
a(\tau)\to 0
as
\tau\to 0
. Let
p(\tau)
be given by eq.
(
3.11
)
. Assume one of the following two assumptions hold

(1)

p
is bounded in a neighborhood of
0
, or

(2)

\displaystyle\lim_{\tau\to 0}|p(\tau)|=\infty
and
\displaystyle\lim_{\tau\to 0}\frac{p^{2}(\tau)}{p^{\prime}(\tau)}
exists.

If
p\in L^{1}
, then
\lim_{\tau\to 0}a^{\prime}(\tau)
exists.

Proof.

We show
a^{\prime}(\tau)\to 1
as
\tau\to 0
. Recall that
a(\tau)
solves
a^{\prime}+pa=1
. Therefore, it suffices to show

\lim_{\tau\to 0}p(\tau)a(\tau)\,=\,0.

If
p(\tau)
is bounded in a neighborhood of 0, then the above limit follows from the squeeze theorem.

Now suppose
(2)
holds. By Lemma
3.3
, we have

\lim_{\tau\to 0}p(\tau)a(\tau)\,=\,\lim_{\tau\to 0}p(\tau)e^{-\int_{0}^{\tau}p}\int_{0}^{\tau}e^{\int_{0}^{t}p}dt.

(3.14)

Using l’Hôpital rule’s, we have

\lim_{\tau\to 0}\frac{\int_{0}^{\tau}e^{\int_{0}^{t}p}dt}{p^{-1}(\tau)}\,=\,-\lim_{\tau\to 0}\frac{e^{\int_{0}^{\tau}p}}{p^{\prime}(\tau)p^{-2}(\tau)}.

(3.15)

From assumption, we have

\lim_{\tau\to 0}\frac{p^{2}(\tau)}{p^{\prime}(\tau)}\,=\,c,

for some
-\infty\leq c\leq\infty
. We show that integrability of
p
implies that
c=0
. Indeed, another application of l’Hôpital’s rule gives

c\,=\,\lim_{\tau\to 0}\frac{p(\tau)}{p^{\prime}(\tau)p^{-1}(\tau)}\,=\,\lim_{\tau\to 0}\frac{\int_{0}^{\tau}p}{\ln|p(\tau)|}\,=\,0.

Thus

\lim_{\tau\to 0}\frac{p^{2}(\tau)}{p^{\prime}(\tau)}\,=\,0.

(3.16)

Combining (
3.14
), (
3.15
), and (
3.16
), we have
p(\tau)a(\tau)\to 0
as
\tau\to 0
. This completes the proof.
∎

Remark.
The assumption
p\in L^{1}
was only used to ensure uniqueness of solution (
3.13
) to the boundary-value problem (
3.12
). Without this assumption, we would have not been able to guarantee the equality in eq. (
3.14
).

Example.
Let
p(\tau)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\tau}}
. Then
p\in L^{1}\big([0,1]\big)
and satisfies (2) in Proposition
3.4
. The solution to the boundary value problem (
3.12
) is
a(\tau)=\frac{1}{2}e^{2\sqrt{x}}-\sqrt{x}-\frac{1}{2}
. Expanding
a(\tau)
around
\tau=0
shows that
a(\tau)=\tau+o(\tau)
.

The following corollary generalizes the above example and summarizes our treatment of the boundary-value problem within the context of finding a continuous spacetime extension through
\tau=0
.

Corollary 3.5
.

Assume the hypotheses of Proposition
3.4
. Then
\phi(M)
is given by
(
2.7
)
,
\phi
admits a past boundary for
(M,g)
,
a(\tau)
is given by
(
3.13
)
, and lastly
a(\tau)=\tau+o(\tau)
.

Proof.

If
p\in L^{1}
, then clearly
\int_{0}^{\tau_{0}}p\in\mathbb{R}
. Therefore
b(\tau)/a(\tau)
converges to a nonzero finite number as
\tau\to 0
. By Lemma
3.1
, it follows that
\phi(M)
is given by (
2.7
) and
\phi
admits a past boundary for
(M,g)
. Lemma
3.3
implies that
a(\tau)
is given by (
3.13
). Since
\lim_{\tau\to 0}a^{\prime}(\tau)
exists, Proposition
3.2
implies
a(\tau)=\tau+o(\tau)
.
∎

We make some final remarks on what happens when the hyperbolic slices have sectional curvature
-\kappa^{2}
instead of
-1
. In this case, the metric is
-d\tau^{2}+a^{2}(\tau)h/\kappa^{2}
where
h
is the hyperbolic metric with sectional curvature
-1
. Our theorems in this section apply to the function
\widetilde{a}(\tau)=a(\tau)/\kappa
. Consequently, we find that the scale factor must satisfy
a(\tau)=\kappa\tau+o(\tau)
. Similarly, if we restore the speed of light
c
in the metric, then we find that
a(\tau)
satisfies
a(\tau)=c\kappa\tau+o(\tau)
.

4
a(\tau)=\tau+o(\tau)
is not sufficient for past boundaries

In the previous section, Proposition
3.2
shows that
a(\tau)=\tau+o(\tau)
is necessary for
\phi
to admit a past boundary for
(M,g)
provided
a(\tau)
is
C^{1}
and
\lim_{\tau\to 0}a^{\prime}(\tau)
exists. In this section we show that
a(\tau)=\tau+o(\tau)
is not sufficient for
\phi
to admit a past boundary. But first we show that it is sufficient for the hyperboloids to foliate
\phi(M)
all the way down to the lightcone.

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
(M,g)
be a
C^{0}
hyperbolic FLRW spacetime. If
a(\tau)=\tau+o(\tau)
, then
\phi(M)
is given by
(
2.7
)
.

Proof.

It suffices to show
b(\tau)\to 0
as
\tau\to 0
. Since
b(\tau)=e^{\int_{\tau_{0}}^{\tau}\frac{1}{a}}
, it suffices to show
\int_{0}^{\tau_{0}}\frac{1}{a}=\infty
. This follows from
a(\tau)=\tau+o(\tau)
by a simple
\varepsilon
-
\delta
argument.
∎

Now we show
a(\tau)=\tau+o(\tau)
is not sufficient for
\phi
to admit a past boundary. The proof of (2) in Theorem
1.1
follows from the following proposition.

Proposition 4.2
.

Let
(M,g)
be the
C^{\infty}
hyperbolic FLRW spacetime with
a(\tau)=\tau-\tau/\ln(\tau)
for
\tau\in(0,1)
. Then
a(\tau)=\tau+o(\tau)
but
\phi
does not admit a past boundary for
(M,g)
.

Proof.

That
a(\tau)=\tau+o(\tau)
follows from
-1/\ln(\tau)\to 0
as
\tau\to 0
. Fix
\tau_{0}\in(0,1)
. Then

\ln\big(b(\tau)\big)\,=\,\int_{\tau_{0}}^{\tau}\frac{1}{t-\frac{t}{\ln t}}dt\,=\,\ln\big[\tau(1-\ln\tau)\big]-\ln\big[\tau_{0}(1-\ln\tau_{0})\big].

It follows that

\displaystyle\ln\left(\frac{b(\tau)}{a(\tau)}\right)\,

\displaystyle=\,-\int_{\tau}^{\tau_{0}}\frac{1}{t-\frac{t}{\ln t}}dt-\ln\left(\tau-\frac{\tau}{\ln\tau}\right)

\displaystyle=\,\ln|\ln\tau|-\ln\big[\tau_{0}(1-\ln\tau_{0})\big].

Thus
b(\tau)/a(\tau)\to\infty
as
\tau\to 0
. Now Lemma
3.1
implies that
\phi
does not admit a past boundary for
(M,g)
.
∎

Milne-like spacetimes satisfy
a(\tau)=\tau+o(\tau^{1+\varepsilon})
for some
\varepsilon>0
. In
[
26
, Thm. 3.4]
it was shown that for this scale factor,
b(\tau)/a(\tau)
converges to a nonzero finite number as
\tau\to 0
and so
\phi
admits a past boundary for Milne-like spacetimes by Lemma
3.1
. The following proposition shows that the
\varepsilon
is not necessary to achieve a past boundary and proves (3) in Theorem
1.1
.

Proposition 4.3
.

Let
(M,g)
be the
C^{\infty}
hyperbolic FLRW spacetime with scale factor
a(\tau)=\tau+\tau e^{-\sqrt{|\ln\tau|}}
for
\tau\in(0,1)
. Then

a(\tau)\,=\,\tau+o(\tau)\>\>\>\>\text{ but }\>\>\>\>a(\tau)\neq\tau+o(\tau^{1+\varepsilon})\text{ for any }\varepsilon>0,

and
\phi
admits a past boundary for
(M,g)
.

Proof.

Set
f(\tau)=e^{-\sqrt{|\ln\tau|}}
.
That
a(\tau)=\tau+o(\tau)
follows from
f(\tau)\to 0
as
\tau\to 0
. To show
a(\tau)\neq\tau+o(\tau^{1+\varepsilon})
for any
\varepsilon>0
, it suffices to show
f(\tau)/\tau^{\varepsilon}\to\infty
as
\tau\to 0
. Indeed

\lim_{\tau\to 0}\left[\ln\left(\frac{f(\tau)}{\tau^{\epsilon}}\right)\right]\,=\,\lim_{\tau\to 0}\left[-\sqrt{|\ln\tau|}-\epsilon\ln\tau\right]\,=\,\lim_{\tau\to 0}\left[\sqrt{|\ln\tau|}(-1+\epsilon\sqrt{|\ln\tau|})\right]\,=\,\infty.

Lastly, we want to show
b/a
converges to a nonzero finite number as
\tau\to 0
. We have

\displaystyle\ln\left(\frac{b(\tau)}{a(\tau)}\right)\,

\displaystyle=\,-\int_{\tau}^{\tau_{0}}\frac{1}{t+tf(t)}dt-\ln(\tau+\tau f(\tau))

\displaystyle=\,-\int_{\tau}^{\tau_{0}}\frac{1}{t+tf(t)}dt+\int_{\tau}^{\tau_{0}}\frac{1+f(t)+tf^{\prime}(t)}{t+tf(t)}dt-\ln(\tau_{0}+\tau_{0}f(\tau_{0}))

\displaystyle<\,\int_{\tau}^{\tau_{0}}\frac{f(t)+tf^{\prime}(t)}{t}dt-\ln(\tau_{0}+\tau_{0}f(\tau_{0}))

\displaystyle=\,\int_{\tau}^{\tau_{0}}\frac{e^{-\sqrt{|\ln(t)|}}}{t}dt+f(\tau_{0})-f(\tau)-\ln(\tau_{0}+\tau_{0}f(\tau_{0}))

\displaystyle=\,f(\tau_{0})[2\sqrt{|\ln(\tau_{0})|}+3]-f(\tau)[2\sqrt{|\ln(\tau)|}+3]-\ln(\tau_{0}+\tau_{0}f(\tau_{0})),

where in the second line we used the fundamental theorem of calculus, in the third the positivity of
f
and
f^{\prime}
, and in the last one integration by parts. Using l’Hôpital’s rule, we have

\lim_{\tau\to 0}f(\tau)\sqrt{|\ln\tau|}\,=\,\lim_{\tau\to 0}\frac{\sqrt{|\ln\tau|}}{e^{\sqrt{|\ln\tau|}}}\,=\,\lim_{\tau\to 0}\frac{1}{e^{\sqrt{|\ln\tau|}}}\,=\,0.

This shows

\lim_{\tau\to 0}\frac{b(\tau)}{a(\tau)}\,<\,\infty.

Since
b/a
is positive, it suffices to show that
b/a
is decreasing. Indeed, computing the derivative gives
(b/a)^{\prime}=-ba^{-2}(f+\tau f^{\prime})<0
.
∎

Remark
. The choice of scale factors in Propositions
4.2
and
4.3
could not have been analytic. Any analytic scale factor
a(\tau)=\sum_{0}^{\infty}c_{n}\tau^{n}
satisfying

a(\tau)=\tau+o(\tau)
implies
c_{0}=0
and
c_{1}=1
;
hence
a(\tau)=\tau+\sum_{2}^{\infty}c_{n}\tau^{n}
. Choosing
\varepsilon=1/2
, it’s clear that
a(\tau)=\tau+o(\tau^{3/2})
and hence the corresponding spacetime is Milne-like.
