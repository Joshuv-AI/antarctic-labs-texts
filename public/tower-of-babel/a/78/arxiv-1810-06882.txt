Free rational curves on low degree hypersurfaces and the circle method
Tim Browning, Will Sawin

Abstract
. We use a function field version of the Hardy–Littlewood circle method to study the locus of free rational curves on an arbitrary smooth projective hypersurface of sufficiently low degree. On the one hand this allows us to bound the dimension of the singular locus of the moduli space of rational curves on such hypersurfaces and, on the other hand, it sheds light on Peyre’s reformulation of the Batyrev–Manin conjecture in terms of slopes with respect to the tangent bundle.

14H10 (11D45, 11P55, 14G05, 14J70)

1.
Introduction

Let
X\subset\mathbb{P}^{n-1}
be a smooth hypersurface of degree
d\geqslant 3
, over a field
K

whose characteristic exceeds
d
if it is positive.
This paper has two aspects. On the one hand, motivated by questions in algebraic geometry, we shall be interested in the locus of points corresponding to free rational curves inside the
moduli space
\mathscr{M}_{0,0}(X,e)

of degree
e
rational curves on
X
. On the other hand, by working over a finite field, we shall
establish
a function field analogue of a recent conjecture due to Peyre
[
18
]
about
the distribution of “sufficiently free” rational points of bounded height on Fano varieties.

1.1.
Geometry

The expected dimension
of
\mathscr{M}_{0,0}(X,e)
is
(n-d)e+n-5
, a fact that is known to hold
for generic
X
if
n\geqslant d+3
,
thanks to Riedl and Yang
[
19
]
.
It follows from work of Browning and Vishe
[
4
]
that

\mathscr{M}_{0,0}(X,e)

is irreducible and
has the expected dimension
for any smooth
X
, provided that

n>(5d-4)2^{d-1}.

Our first result strengthens this.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
d\geqslant 3
, let
e\geqslant 1
and let
n>(2d-1)2^{d-1}
. Then

\mathscr{M}_{0,0}(X,e)

is an irreducible locally complete intersection of the expected dimension.

We can also bound the dimension of the singular locus of
\mathscr{M}_{0,0}(X,e)
,
as follows.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
d\geqslant 3
, let
e\geqslant 1
and let

n>3(d-1)2^{d-1}.

Then the
space
\mathscr{M}_{0,0}(X,e)
is smooth outside a set of codimension at least

\left(\frac{n}{2^{d-2}}-6(d-1)\right)\left(1+\left\lfloor\frac{e+1}{d-1}\right\rfloor\right).

In particular, whenever these inequalities are satisfied, it is generically smooth and reduced.

For
n\geqslant 2d+1
and generic
X
of degree
d\geqslant 3
, Harris, Roth and Starr
[
9
]
have also shown that
\mathscr{M}_{0,0}(X,e)
is generically smooth.
Note that, provided
n>3(d-1)2^{d-1}
, the codimension goes to
\infty

in Theorem
1.2

when either
e
or
n
does, with
d
fixed. Moreover, when both
e
and
n
are large with respect to
d
, the codimension is at least approximately
\frac{1}{2^{d-2}(d-1)}
of the total dimension.

Our work
addresses some questions of Eisenbud and Harris
[
7
, §6.8.1]

concerning the Fano variety of lines
F_{1}(X)=\mathscr{M}_{0,0}(X,1)
associated to
a smooth hypersurface

X\subset\mathbb{P}^{n-1}
of degree
d
.
Specifically, their question (a) asks whether
F_{1}(X)
is reduced and irreducible if
n>d+1
and
(b) asks whether the dimension of the singular locus of
F_{1}(X)
can be bounded in terms of
d
alone.
Theorems
1.1
and
1.2
answer the first question affirmatively for
n>3(d-1)2^{d-1}
and
give some weak evidence in support of the second question, by showing that it grows with
n
more slowly than the dimension of the whole space. Furthermore, we handle the analogous conjectures with higher degree curves, with no loss in the dependence on
n
, meaning that for large enough

e
we do better than their predicted bound
d\leqslant n/e
.

By comparison, Starr
[
20
]
has proved that if
n\geqslant d+e
and
X
is generic, then
\mathscr{M}_{0,0}(X,e)
has canonical singularities, which implies in particular that it is smooth outside a set of codimension at least
2
.
It does not seem possible that our method will prove that
\mathscr{M}_{0,0}(X,e)
has canonical singularities. By
Mustatǎ
[
16
]
and Lang-Weil
[
14
]
this is
equivalent to the conjunction of an infinite sequence of Diophantine estimates (in the spirit of Definition
3.7
),
but for fixed
n,d
and
e
it seems unlikely that the circle method is able to handle
more than finitely many of them.
In unpublished work, Starr and Tian use a bend-and-break approach to produce a less restrictive lower bound for the codimension of the singular locus for a general hypersurface
X\subset\mathbb{P}^{n-1}

of degree
d
. However, their method never proves a lower bound for the codimension greater than
n
, whereas our work achieves this if
e
is sufficiently large.

Comparing the various results, we see that Theorem
1.2
holds for a much more restricted range of
n
(unless
e
is very large relative to
d
) but it is valid for an arbitrary smooth hypersurface, rather than just a general one.

It should be possible to adapt our strategy to prove results about moduli spaces of genus
g
curves on
X
. However, the codimension we obtain for the whole moduli space will not be any better than the codimension we can prove for the space of maps from a fixed genus
g
curve to
X
.
In particular the codimension will shrink as
g
grows,
so the bound obtained would only be suitable for
e
sufficiently large with respect to
g
.

Let
\mathscr{T}_{X}
be the tangent bundle associated to the smooth hypersurface
X\subset\mathbb{P}^{n-1}
(as defined in
[
10
, p. 180]
, for example).
Our remaining result deals specifically with free curves and so we recall the definition here.

Definition 1.3
.

Let
c:\mathbb{P}^{1}\to X
be a rational curve and let
\varrho\in\mathbb{Z}
. We
say that
c
is
\varrho
-free if
c^{*}\mathscr{T}_{X}\otimes\mathscr{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{1}}(-\varrho)
is globally generated.

We shall follow common convention and say that
c
is free if it is
0
-free, and very free if it is
1
-free. One easily checks that this agrees with the standard definition that
c
is free if
c^{*}\mathscr{T}_{X}
is globally generated and very free if
c^{*}\mathscr{T}_{X}
is ample.
The definition of free curves goes back to pioneering work of Kollár–Miyaoka–Mori
[
13
, § 1]
on rational connectedness for Fano varieties, and they feature heavily in work of
Kollár
[
11
, § II.3]
.
We have taken Definition
1.3
from work of Debarre
[
6
, Def. 4.5]
,
which appears to be the first time that the notion of being
\varrho
-free occurs, for varying
\varrho\in\mathbb{Z}
.

Remark 1.4
.

If
c
is a
\varrho
-free rational curve on
X
then it follows from Definition
1.3
that

\deg(c^{*}\mathscr{T}_{X})\geqslant\rank(c^{*}\mathscr{T}_{X})\varrho
.
In general, the pull-back of the tangent bundle has rank
n-2
and degree
e(n-d)
. In this way we see that no degree
e
rational curve on
X
is ever
(\lfloor\frac{e(n-d)}{n-2}\rfloor+1)
-free. If
d\geqslant 2
then this implies that
\varrho\leqslant e
, for any
\varrho
-free rational curve
\mathbb{P}^{1}\to X
.

We let
U_{\varrho}\subset\mathscr{M}_{0,0}(X,e)

be the Zariski open set
that parameterises degree
e
maps from
\mathbb{P}^{1}
to
X
that are
\varrho
-free. We write

Z_{\varrho}=\mathscr{M}_{0,0}(X,e)\setminus U_{\varrho}
for the complement.
This is the closed set parameterising
degree
e
maps
\mathbb{P}^{1}\to X
that are not
\varrho
-free.
We shall prove the following bound for its dimension.

Theorem 1.5
.

Let
d\geqslant 3
and
n>3(d-1)2^{d-1}
.
Assume that
\varrho\geqslant-1
and

(1.1)

e\geqslant(\varrho+1)\left(2+\frac{1}{d-2}\right).

Then

(1.2)

\begin{split}\dim Z_{\varrho}\leqslant~&(n-d)e+n-5+2(d-1)\left\lfloor\frac{\varrho+1}{2}\right\rfloor\\
&-\left(\frac{n}{2^{d-2}}-6(d-1)\right)\left(1+\left\lfloor\frac{e-\varrho}{d-1}\right\rfloor-\left\lfloor\frac{\varrho+1}{2}\right\rfloor\right).\end{split}

The notion of free rational curves was originally introduced as a tool to study uniruled and rational connectedness properties of varieties. Taking
\varrho=1
it follows from Theorems
1.1
and
1.5
that
U_{1}\neq\emptyset
if
K
is algebraically closed and
e
is sufficiently large. Hence, by appealing to
[
6
, Cor. 4.17]
, we deduce that any smooth hypersurface
X\subset\mathbb{P}^{n-1}
of degree
d
is
rationally connected if
d\geqslant 3
and

n>3(d-1)2^{d-1}
.
This recovers a weak form of
the well-known result, independently
due
to Campana
[
5
]
and
Kollár–Miyaoka–Mori
[
12
]

that Fano varieties are rationally connected.
In fact both proofs use reduction to characteristic
p
, but they use different properties of characteristic
p
varieties, with
[
12
]
relying on Frobenius pull-back and our work using the Lang–Weil estimates.

Theorem
1.2
is derived from
Theorem
1.5
, which is proved using analytic number theory and builds on an approach
employed by Browning and Vishe
[
4
]
. (Theorem
1.1
uses essentially the same approach as
[
4
]
, with one improvement to a key lemma.)
One begins by working over a finite field
K=\mathbb{F}_{q}
of characteristic
>d
. We bound the dimension of
Z_{\varrho}
by counting the number of points defined over a finite extension of
\mathbb{F}_{q}

that lie in it. In §
3
, we will give an explicit description of this locus in terms
of a system of two Diophantine equations defined over the function
field
\mathbb{F}_{q}(T)
. Let
f\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
be a non-singular form of
degree
d
that defines the hypersurface
X\subset\mathbb{P}^{n-1}
.
Given
\varrho\in\mathbb{Z}
, we shall see that the primary counting function of interest to us, denoted

N_{\varrho}(q,e,f)
, is the one that counts vectors
(\mathbf{g},\mathbf{h})\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[T]^{2n}
, where

g_{1},\dots,g_{n}
have degree at most
e
and no common zero,
with at least one of degree exactly
e
,
and where
h_{1},\dots,h_{n}
have degree at most
e-1-\varrho
, such that

(1.3)

f(g_{1},\dots,g_{n})=0\quad\text{
and }\quad\sum_{i=1}^{n}h_{i}\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{i}}(g_{1},\dots,g_{n})=0.

Since each partial derivative of
f
is a degree
d-1
polynomial,
we obtain a linear equation for
\mathbf{h}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[T]^{n}

where the coefficients have degree at most
(d-1)e
in
T
.
Standard heuristics lead us to expect that, for typical
\mathbf{g}
, the number of available

\mathbf{h}
is
q^{(e-\varrho)(n-1)-(d-1)e}=q^{e(n-d)-\varrho(n-1)}
. (In fact, we shall see in Lemmas
3.4
and
3.5
that this is true only if the map
\mathbb{P}^{1}\to X
represented by

\mathbf{g}
is
\varrho
-free.)
Thus we expect that
N_{\varrho}(q,e,f)
is approximated by
q^{e(n-d)-\varrho(n-1)}N(q,e,f)
, where

N(q,e,f)
is the number of vectors
\mathbf{g}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[T]^{n}
such that
f(\mathbf{g})=0
, where

g_{1},\dots,g_{n}
have degree at most
e
and no common zero,
with at least one of degree exactly
e
.

In §
4
, we apply the function field version of the Hardy–Littlewood circle method to study the system of degree
d
equations (
1.3
), expressing the number of solutions as an integral of an exponential sum. We shall show that the major arc contribution to this integral cancels almost exactly with the expected approximation

q^{e(n-d)-\varrho(n-1)}N(q,e,f)
.
In §
5
, we prove an upper bound on all other arcs, taking special care to make
all of our implied constants depend explicitly on the size of the finite field
q
.
The standard way of proceeding involves
d-1
applications of Weyl differencing, a process
that would ultimately
require

n>3(d-1)2^{d}
variables overall. We shall gain a 50% reduction in the number of variables
by exploiting the special shape of the Diophantine system (
1.3
).
Finally, we bring everything together and apply the Lang–Weil estimates
[
14
]
to turn the bound for
\#Z_{\varrho}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
into a bound for the dimension of
Z_{\varrho}
. An application of spreading-out shows that the dimension bound holds over an arbitrary base field
K
such that
\mathrm{char}(K)>d
if it is positive.

1.2.
Arithmetic

In our geometric investigation of
Z_{\varrho}
we take the point of view that
e
and
\varrho
are fixed and
q\to\infty
. In this subsection we assume that the finite field is fixed, but we allow the parameters
e
and
\varrho
to tend to infinity appropriately.

Suppose that
V
is a smooth projective geometrically integral Fano variety
defined over a number field
K
.
For suitable Zariski open subsets
U\subset V
the Batyrev–Manin conjecture
[
8
]

makes a precise prediction about the asymptotic behaviour of the counting function

N_{U}(B)=\#\{x\in U(K):H_{\omega_{V}^{-1}}(x)\leqslant B\},

as
B\to\infty
, where
H_{\omega_{V}^{-1}}:V(K)\to\mathbb{R}
is an anticanonical height function. These conjectures are flawed, however, since it has been discovered
that the presence of Zariski dense thin sets in
V(K)
may skew the expected asymptotics. Recently, Peyre
[
18
]
has embarked on an ambitious programme to repair the conjecture by associating a measure of “freeness”
\ell(x)\in[0,1]
to any
x\in V(K)
and only counting those rational points for which
\ell(x)\geqslant\varepsilon_{B}
, where
\varepsilon_{B}
is a function of
B
decreasing to zero sufficiently slowly. (See
[
18
, Def. 6.11]
for a precise statement.) Peyre’s function
\ell(x)
is defined using
Arakelov geometry and
the theory of slopes associated to the tangent bundle
\mathscr{T}_{V}
.

We can lend support to Peyre’s freedom prediction
[
18
, §6]
by studying smooth hypersurfaces of low degree in the setting of global fields of positive characteristic. Let

X\subset\mathbb{P}^{n-1}
be a smooth hypersurface of degree
d
defined over a finite field
\mathbb{F}_{q}
whose characteristic exceeds
d.
We put

(1.4)

N_{X}^{\varepsilon\text{-free}}(B)=\#\left\{x\in X(K):\ell(x)\geqslant\varepsilon,~H_{-\omega_{X}}(x)\leqslant q^{B}\right\},

where
K=\mathbb{F}_{q}(T)
is the rational function field and

\ell(x)
will be defined in
§
6
. The expectation is that for a suitable range of
\varepsilon
,

N_{X}^{\varepsilon\text{-free}}(B)
should have the same asymptotic behaviour as the usual counting function

N_{X}(B)
, as
B\to\infty
.
The following result
confirms this and will be proved in §
6
.

Theorem 1.6
.

Let
d\geqslant 3
, let
n>3(d-1)2^{d-1}
and let

0\leqslant\varepsilon<\frac{n-1}{(n-d)(d-1)^{2}2^{d-1}}.

Then there exists
\delta>0
such that

N_{X}^{\varepsilon\text{-free}}(B)=c_{X}q^{B}+O\left(q^{(1-\delta)B}\right),

as
B\to\infty
,
where
c_{X}
is the
function field analogue of the
constant predicted by Peyre
[
17
]
.
Furthermore,
the implied constant only depends on
q
and
f
.

Note that this result does not require
\varepsilon_{B}
to decrease to zero, but only to stay below some fixed constant. This may be because the hypersurface
X
has Picard rank one, since Peyre
has shown in
[
18
, §7.2]
that for the product

\mathbb{P}^{1}\times\mathbb{P}^{1}
one requires
\varepsilon_{B}\to 0
for the asymptotic formula to be true.
Finally, one can see from the arguments in Theorem
1.6
that we can take the upper bound for
\varepsilon
to be significantly greater than
\frac{n-1}{(n-d)(d-1)^{2}2^{d-1}}
when
n
is large.
(In fact, the cutoff is allowed to approach
\frac{1}{d+1}
as
n\to\infty
.)

With appropriate adjustments to the proof of Theorem
1.6
, it is also possible to handle the
corresponding result for smooth hypersurfaces of low degree defined over
\mathbb{Q}
, with Poisson summation taking the place of the Riemann–Roch arguments that feature in §
3
. This is the object of our concurrent work
[
2
]
.

2.
Examples

As usual,
X\subset\mathbb{P}^{n-1}
is assumed to be a smooth hypersurface of degree
d\geqslant 3
, over a field
K
whose characteristic is either
0
or
>d
.
While the latter condition arises very naturally in our argument (as explained in Remark
5.5
),
the following result shows that
the statement of Theorem
1.5
is actually false when it is dropped.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
K=\overline{\mathbb{F}}_{p}
for a prime
p
and let
X\subset\mathbb{P}^{n-1}
be the
Fermat hypersurface

x_{1}^{d}+\dots+x_{n}^{d}=0.

Assume that
p\nmid d
and
d\neq ap^{r}-1

for any
r\in\mathbb{N}
and
a\in\{0,\dots,p-1\}
.
Then
X
is smooth, none of the curves in
\mathscr{M}_{0,0}(X,1)
are
(-1)
-free, and

\dim\mathscr{M}_{0,0}(X,1)>2n-d-5
.

Proof.

The moduli space of
n
-tuples of polynomials of degree
\leqslant 1
satisfying the equation
x_{1}^{d}+\dots+x_{n}^{d}=0
is a
\mathrm{GL}_{2}
-bundle over the moduli stack
\mathscr{M}_{0,0}(X,1)
parameterising lines in
X
, because for each line we can choose any basis of the corresponding two-dimensional vector space. Thus its dimension is equal to
4+\dim\mathscr{M}_{0,0}(X,1)
. This space is is cut out by
d+1
equations in
2n
variables, where
\binom{d}{i}
divides all coefficients of the
i
th equation, for
0\leqslant i\leqslant d
.
By Lucas’ theorem it follows that
p\mid\binom{d}{i}
if and only if at least one of the base
p
digits of
i
is greater than the corresponding base
p
digit of
d
. In this way we see that
p\mid\binom{d}{i}
for some
0\leqslant i\leqslant d
if and only if
d
does not take the form
ap^{r}-1
for some

a\in\{0,\dots,p-1\}
.
But then the space is cut out by fewer than
d+1
equations in
2n
variables. This implies that it has dimension greater than
2n-d-1
, whence
\dim\mathscr{M}_{0,0}(X,1)>2n-d-5
.
Furthermore,
since the dimension near each curve is greater than the expected dimension, it follows from Lemma
3.1
that they are not
(-1)
-free. Finally,
the Fermat hypersurface is smooth over
K
if and only if
p\nmid d
.
∎

This example generalises a discussion of Debarre
[
6
, §2.15]
. It shows
that for typical
p<d
the
statements of Theorems
1.1
and
1.5
are false for fields of characteristic
p
.

Returning to the general setting, the following result provides examples
of curves that are not
\varrho
-free.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
d,m,n\in\mathbb{N}
with
d\geqslant 3
and
m\leqslant n/2
. Let
K
be an infinite field. There exists a non-singular form
f(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
over
K
of degree
d
, such that

f(x_{1},\dots,x_{m},0,\dots,0)=\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{j}}(x_{1},\dots,x_{m},0\dots,0)=0

for all
x_{1},\dots,x_{m}
and all
j\leqslant n-m
.
For such a polynomial, every map
c:\mathbb{P}^{1}\to X
of degree
e
that factors through
\mathbb{P}^{m-1}\subseteq X\subseteq\mathbb{P}^{n-1}
fails to be
(\lfloor\frac{e(m-d)}{m-1}\rfloor+1)
-free. The moduli
space of such rational curves has dimension
m(e+1)-4
.

Let
X\subset\mathbb{P}^{n-1}
be a smooth hypersurface with underlying polynomial
f
, as in the lemma.
Taking
m=d
and
\varrho=0
, we see that when
n>2d

the space
Z_{1}
of non-very-free rational curves
\mathbb{P}^{1}\to X
of degree
e
has dimension
at least
d(e+1)-4.

Proof of Lemma
2.2
.

Without the non-singularity condition,
the space of such polynomials is linear. The singular polynomials form a closed subset. To prove the existence, it is sufficient to show that this subset has codimension
1
. The set of singular polynomials is the projection from the product of this linear space with
\mathbb{P}^{n-1}
of the set of pairs of a point and a polynomial singular at that point. For elements in
\mathbb{P}^{m-1}\subseteq\mathbb{P}^{n-1}
, the space of polynomials singular at that point has codimension
m
, as it is defined by the

m

independent conditions
\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{j}}(x_{1},\dots,x_{m},0\dots,0)=0
for

n-m+1\leqslant j\leqslant n
.
For all other elements, we claim that the
n
conditions
\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{j}}(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})=0
for
1\leqslant j\leqslant n
define a codimension
n
subspace.
To see this we may take a linear form

l

in the last
n-m
coordinates that is nonzero at that point.
Then the
n
-dimensional space of polynomials generated by

x_{j}l^{d-1}

for
1\leqslant j\leqslant n
lie in the linear subspace, since
d-1\geqslant 2
. But only the zero element in that subspace satisfies all
n
conditions.
It follows that the singular locus is the union of the projection of a codimension

m

bundle on
\mathbb{P}^{m-1}
and a codimension
n
bundle on its complement in
\mathbb{P}^{n-1}
. Thus the singular locus has codimension at least one, as desired.

For the freeness, we use the Euler exact sequence

(2.1)

0\to\mathscr{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{n-1}}\to\mathscr{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{n-1}}(1)^{n}\to\mathscr{T}_{\mathbb{P}^{n-1}}\to 0.

Consider the map
\mathscr{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{n-1}}(1)^{n}\to\mathscr{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{n-1}}(1)^{m}
given by projection onto the last
m
factors. Because
m\leqslant n/2
the composition of this projection with the map
\mathscr{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{n-1}}\to\mathscr{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{n-1}}(1)^{n}
vanishes on
\mathbb{P}^{m-1}
. So over
\mathbb{P}^{m-1}
, we obtain a map
\mathscr{T}_{\mathbb{P}^{n-1}}\to\mathscr{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{n-1}}(1)^{m}
.

Next consider the exact sequence
0\to\mathscr{T}_{\mathbb{P}^{n-1}}\to\mathscr{T}_{X}\to\mathscr{O}_{X}(d)\to 0
on
X
. The second map of this sequence is the dot product with the derivative of
f
. By assumption on
f
, restricted to
\mathbb{P}^{m-1}
, this map factors through the projection onto the last
m
vectors. Hence we obtain an exact sequence

0\to\mathscr{V}\to\mathscr{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{m-1}}(1)^{m}\to\mathscr{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{m-1}}(d)\to 0

whose kernel
\mathscr{V}
is a vector bundle on
\mathbb{P}^{m-1}
of degree
m-d
, which arises as a quotient of
\mathscr{T}_{X}
.

For
c:\mathbb{P}^{1}\to X
a map of degree
e
whose image lies in
\mathbb{P}^{m-1}
,
c^{*}\mathscr{V}
is a vector bundle of degree
e(m-d)
on
\mathbb{P}^{1}
which arises as a quotient of
c^{*}\mathscr{T}_{X}
. Because
c^{*}\mathscr{V}
splits as a direct sum of
m-1
line bundles, it must contain some line bundle summand of degree at most
\frac{e(m-d)}{m-1}
, and we can round down to the nearest integer. Hence
c^{*}\mathscr{T}_{X}
has some line bundle summand of degree at most
\lfloor\frac{e(m-d)}{m-1}\rfloor
and hence
c
is not
(\lfloor\frac{e(m-d)}{m-1}\rfloor+1)
-free.

The dimension estimate is the standard calculation for the moduli space of rational curves in projective space.
∎

Even for a general hypersurface there are some non-very-free curves. Indeed, for such a variety, the moduli space of lines has dimension
2n-d-5
, and each line admits a
(2e+1)
-dimensional moduli space of degree
e
maps from
\mathbb{P}^{1}
to that line. Because the pull-back of the tangent bundle to a line has rank
n-2
and degree
n-d
, it contains some summand of degree at most
0
as soon as
d\geqslant 2
, and so every pull-back of it has a summand of the same degree, and so these degree
e
coverings of lines fail to be
1
-free. Hence, for a general hypersurface

X\subset\mathbb{P}^{n-1}
of degree
d,
we have

\dim Z_{1}\geqslant 2(n+e)-d-7
.

These examples show that the dimension of the moduli space of non-very-free curves can grow linearly in
n
and it can grow linearly in
e
. We do not know if it can grow linearly in
ne
, as the dimension of

\mathscr{M}_{0,0}(X,e)
does.

3.
Vector bundles on
\mathbb{P}^{1}

Let
f
be a homogeneous polynomial of degree
d
in
n
variables over a field
K
and let
X\subset\mathbb{P}^{n-1}
be its projective zero locus. Assume that
X
is smooth and let
\mathscr{T}_{X}
be its tangent bundle. In this section we investigate the geometry of
\varrho
-free rational curves
c:\mathbb{P}^{1}\to X
, in the sense of Definition
1.3
.
It turns out that there is a natural characterization of the
(-1)
-free curves, which we recall here.

Lemma 3.1
.

A rational curve

c:\mathbb{P}^{1}\to X
of degree
e
is
(-1)
-free if and only if, in a neighborhood of
c
, the moduli space of rational curves on
X
is smooth of dimension
(n-d)e+n-5
.

Under the assumptions of Theorem
1.1
or
[
19
]
,
\mathscr{M}_{0,0}(X,e)
has dimension
(n-d)e+n-5
, so this is simply equivalent to
\mathscr{M}_{0,0}(X,e)
being smooth at
c
.

Proof of Lemma
3.1
.

The tangent space of the moduli space of rational curves at
c
is

H^{0}(\mathbb{P}^{1},c^{*}\mathscr{T}_{X})/H^{0}(\mathbb{P}^{1},\mathscr{T}_{\mathbb{P}^{1}}).

Note that

H^{0}(\mathbb{P}^{1},\mathscr{T}_{\mathbb{P}^{1}})
has dimension
3
. By Riemann–Roch,

\displaystyle\dim H^{0}(\mathbb{P}^{1},c^{*}\mathscr{T}_{X})-\dim H^{1}(\mathbb{P}^{1},c^{*}\mathscr{T}_{X})

\displaystyle=\dim(c^{*}\mathscr{T}_{X})+\deg(c^{*}\mathscr{T}_{X})

\displaystyle=n-2+e(n-d).

Hence if
c
is a smooth point on a component of dimension
n-5+e(n-d)
then
H^{0}(\mathbb{P}^{1},c^{*}\mathscr{T}_{X})
has dimension
n-2+e(n-d)
and so
H^{1}(\mathbb{P}^{1},c^{*}\mathscr{T}_{X})
vanishes. Thus
[
6
, Remark 4.6]
implies that
c
is (
-1
)-free.

Conversely if
c
is
(-1)
-free then
H^{1}(\mathbb{P}^{1},c^{*}\mathscr{T}_{X})
vanishes by
[
6
, Remark 4.6]
, so deformations are unobstructed. Thus the moduli space is smooth at
c
, and the dimension of the tangent space to the moduli space is
n-5+e(n-d)
.
∎

Let
\hat{\mathscr{T}}_{X}
be the inverse image of
\mathscr{T}_{X}\subseteq\mathscr{T}_{\mathbb{P}^{n-1}}
under the map
\mathscr{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{n-1}}(1)^{n}\to\mathscr{T}_{\mathbb{P}^{n-1}}
in the Euler sequence (
2.1
).
This yields

0\to\mathscr{O}_{X}\to\hat{\mathscr{T}}_{X}\to\mathscr{T}_{X}\to 0,

so that in particular
\hat{\mathscr{T}}_{X}
is a vector bundle of rank
n-1
on
X
.
With this in mind, we refine Definition
1.3
as follows.

Definition 3.2
.

We say that
c:\mathbb{P}^{1}\to X
is strongly
\varrho
-free if
c^{*}\hat{\mathscr{T}}_{X}\otimes\mathscr{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{1}}(-\varrho)
is globally generated.

We thank Paul Nelson for asking a question that suggested the above definition, and which turns out to simplify our argument compared to studying the tangent bundle directly.

Lemma 3.3
.

If
c
is strongly
\varrho
-free, then it is
\varrho
-free.

Proof.

This follows from the fact that
\mathscr{T}_{X}
is a quotient of
\hat{\mathscr{T}}_{X}
and if a vector bundle is globally generated then every quotient is globally generated. ∎

Lemma 3.4
.

We have

\dim H^{0}(\mathbb{P}^{1},c^{*}\hat{\mathscr{T}}_{X}\otimes\mathscr{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{1}}(-1-\varrho))\geqslant e(n-d)-\varrho(n-1)

with equality if and only if
c
is strongly
\varrho
-free.

Proof.

Because
\mathscr{T}_{X}
is the kernel of the map
df:\mathscr{T}_{\mathbb{P}^{n-1}}\to\mathscr{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{n-1}}(d)
,
\hat{\mathscr{T}}_{X}
is the kernel of a map
\mathscr{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{n-1}}(1)^{n}\to\mathscr{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{n-1}}(d)
and hence has degree
n-d
. Thus
c^{*}\hat{\mathscr{T}}_{X}
has degree
e(n-d)
. Because it has rank
n-1
, its tensor product with with
\mathscr{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{1}}(-1-\varrho)
has degree

e(n-d)-\varrho(n-1)-(n-1)
.
Hence by Riemann–Roch, the dimension of its space of global sections is

\displaystyle\dim H^{0}(\mathbb{P}^{1},c^{*}\hat{\mathscr{T}}_{X}

\displaystyle\otimes\mathscr{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{1}}(-1-\varrho))

\displaystyle=e(n-d)-\varrho(n-1)+\dim H^{1}(\mathbb{P}^{1},c^{*}\hat{\mathscr{T}}_{X}\otimes\mathscr{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{1}}(-1-\varrho)).

It now suffices to show that
H^{1}(\mathbb{P}^{1},c^{*}\hat{\mathscr{T}}_{X}\otimes\mathscr{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{1}}(-1-\varrho)
vanishes if and only if
c^{*}\hat{\mathscr{T}}_{X}\otimes\mathscr{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{1}}(-\varrho)
is globally generated.
We can assume that

c^{*}\hat{\mathscr{T}}_{X}=\bigoplus_{i=1}^{n-1}\mathscr{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{1}}(k_{i}).

Then
H^{1}(\mathbb{P}^{1},c^{*}\hat{\mathscr{T}}_{X}\otimes\mathscr{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{1}}(-1-\varrho))=0
if and only if
k_{i}-1-\varrho\geqslant-1
for all
i
, which happens if and only if
k_{i}-\varrho\geqslant 0
for all
i
, which occurs if and only if
c^{*}\hat{\mathscr{T}}_{X}\otimes\mathscr{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{1}}(-\varrho)
is globally generated. ∎

Vector notation such as
{\bf g}
or
{\bf h}
will denote
n
-tuples of polynomials in
T
.
Let
{\bf g}
be an
n
-tuple of polynomials in
T
of degree at most
e
, at least one of degree
e
, with no common zero, and such that
f({\bf g})=0
. These conditions ensure that
(g_{1}:\dots:g_{n})
defines a degree
e
map
c:\mathbb{P}^{1}\to X
.

Lemma 3.5
.

H^{0}(\mathbb{P}^{1},c^{*}\hat{\mathscr{T}}_{X}\otimes\mathscr{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{1}}(-1-\varrho))
is isomorphic to the space of
n
- tuples
{\bf h}
of polynomials in
T
of degree
\leqslant e-1-\varrho
, such that
\nabla f({\bf g})\cdot{\bf h}=0
.

Proof.

In this proof it will be convenient to set

\mathscr{B}=c^{*}\hat{\mathscr{T}}_{X}\otimes\mathscr{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{1}}(-1-\varrho)
.
We have an exact sequence
0\to\hat{\mathscr{T}}_{X}\to\mathscr{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{n-1}}(1)^{n}\to\mathscr{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{n-1}}(d)\to 0
, with the last map given by multiplication by the gradient of
f
. Thus we obtain an exact sequence

0\to\mathscr{B}\to c^{*}\mathscr{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{n-1}}(1)^{n}\otimes\mathscr{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{1}}(-1-\varrho)\to c^{*}\mathscr{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{n-1}}(d)\otimes\mathscr{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{1}}(-1-\varrho)\to 0

which simplifies to

0\to\mathscr{B}\to\mathscr{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{1}}(e-1-\varrho)^{n}\to\mathscr{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{1}}(de-1-\varrho)\to 0,

because
c
has degree
e
.
Applying the cohomology long exact sequence, we see that

H^{0}(\mathbb{P}^{1},\mathscr{B})
is the kernel of the natural map

H^{0}(\mathbb{P}^{1},\mathscr{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{1}}(e-1-\varrho)^{n})\to H^{0}(\mathbb{P}^{1},\mathscr{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{1}}(de-1-\varrho)),

given by multiplication by the gradient of
f
.
Since
H^{0}(\mathbb{P}^{1},\mathscr{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{1}}(e-1-\varrho)^{n})=H^{0}(\mathbb{P}^{1},\mathscr{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{1}}(e-1-\varrho))^{n}
is the space of
n
-tuples of polynomials of degree at most
e-1-\varrho
, this is exactly the stated space.
∎

We now assume
K=\mathbb{F}_{q}
is a finite field. Thus
f\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
is a non-singular form of degree
d\geqslant 3
. We assume throughout that
\mathrm{char}(\mathbb{F}_{q})>d
.

Definition 3.6
.

Let
N(q,e,f)
be the number of tuples of
n
polynomials
g_{1},\dots,g_{n}
over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
, of degree at most
e
, at least one of degree exactly
e
, with no common zero, such that
f(g_{1},\dots,g_{n})=0
.

Definition 3.7
.

For each integer
\varrho
, let
N_{\varrho}(q,e,f)
be the number of pairs of a tuple of polynomials
g_{1},\dots,g_{n}
over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
, of degree at most
e
, at least one of degree exactly
e
, with no common zero and a tuple of polynomials
h_{1},\dots,h_{n}
over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
, of degree at most
e-1-\varrho
, such that (
1.3
) holds.

Proposition 3.8
.

(1)

The number of
\mathbb{F}_{q}
-points on
\mathscr{M}_{0,0}(X,e)
is

\frac{N(q,e,f)}{(q-1)(q^{3}-q)}.

(2)

The number of
\mathbb{F}_{q}
-points on
Z_{\varrho}
is at most

\frac{N_{\varrho}(q,e,f)q^{\varrho(n-1)-e(n-d)}-N(q,e,f)}{(q-1)^{2}(q^{3}-q)}.

Proof.

Each point of
\mathscr{M}_{0,0}(X,e)
corresponds to
|\mathrm{PGL}_{2}(\mathbb{F}_{q})|=q^{3}-q
distinct maps
\mathbb{P}^{1}\to X
. Thus in (1) we will count the number of maps
\mathbb{P}^{1}\to X
, and in (2) we will count the number of maps
\mathbb{P}^{1}\to X
that are not
\varrho
-free, and in each case then divide by
q^{3}-q
.

For (1), it is sufficient to note that for any such tuple
{\bf g}
,
(g_{1}:\dots:g_{n})
are the projective coordinates of a degree
e
map
\mathbb{P}^{1}\to X
. All such maps arise this way, and two tuples define the same map if and only if one is the multiple of the other by a non-zero scalar.

For (2), it follows from Lemma
3.3
that it suffices to consider the space of degree
e
maps
c:\mathbb{P}^{1}\to X
that are not strongly
\varrho
-free.
Note that
N_{\varrho}(q,e,f)
is the sum over tuples of polynomials
(g_{1},\dots,g_{n})
, defining maps
c
, of
q
raised to the dimension of the vector space of possible
h_{1},\dots,h_{n}
. By Lemma
3.5
this exponent is

\dim H^{0}(\mathbb{P}^{1},c^{*}\hat{\mathscr{T}}_{X}\otimes\mathscr{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{1}}(-1-\varrho)).

By Lemma
3.4
,
q
to the power of this dimension is equal to
q^{e(n-d)-\varrho(n-1)}
if
c
is strongly
\varrho
-free and is at least
q^{e(n-d)-\varrho(n-1)+1}
otherwise. Hence

\displaystyle N_{\varrho}(q,e,f)q^{\varrho(n-1)-e(n-d)}

\displaystyle\geqslant\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{g}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[T]^{n}\\
|\mathbf{g}|=e\\
\text{$c$ not strongly $\varrho$-free}\end{subarray}}\hskip-8.5359ptq+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{g}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[T]^{n}\\
|\mathbf{g}|=e\\
\text{$c$ strongly $\varrho$-free}\end{subarray}}\hskip-8.5359pt1

\displaystyle=N(q,e,f)+(q-1)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{g}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[T]^{n}\\
|\mathbf{g}|=e\\
\text{$c$ not strongly $\varrho$-free}\end{subarray}}\hskip-8.5359pt1.

The proposition follows on noting that there are
(q-1)
tuples
\mathbf{g}
for each map
c:\mathbb{P}^{1}\to X
.
∎

4.
The circle method: identification of major arcs

For
e\geqslant 1

we have

N(q,e,f)=\#\{\mathbf{g}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[T]^{n}:|\mathbf{g}|=q^{e},~f(\mathbf{g})=0,~\gcd(g_{1},\dots,g_{n})=1\},

where
\mathbf{g}=(g_{1},\dots,g_{n})
and
|\mathbf{g}|=\max_{1\leqslant i\leqslant n}|g_{i}|
.
In particular only non-zero vectors
\mathbf{g}
occur.
Similarly, we may write

N_{\varrho}(q,e,f)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{g}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[T]^{n}\\
|\mathbf{g}|=q^{e}\\
f(\mathbf{g})=0\\
\gcd(g_{1},\dots,g_{n})=1\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{h}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[T]^{n}\\
|\mathbf{h}|<q^{e-\varrho}\\
\mathbf{h}.\nabla f(\mathbf{g})=0\end{subarray}}1,

where once again we note that only non-zero vectors
\mathbf{g}
occur.
We may use
the function field analogue of the
Möbius function

\mu:\mathbb{F}_{q}[T]\to\{0,\pm 1\}
to detect the coprimality condition

\gcd(g_{1},\dots,g_{n})=1
. This gives

\displaystyle N_{\varrho}(q,e,f)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[T]\\
\text{$k$ monic}\end{subarray}}\mu(k)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{g}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[T]^{n}\\
0<|\mathbf{g}|=q^{e}/|k|\\
f(\mathbf{g})=0\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{h}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[T]^{n}\\
|\mathbf{h}|<q^{e-\varrho}\\
\mathbf{h}.\nabla f(\mathbf{g})=0\end{subarray}}1

\displaystyle=\sum_{j\geqslant 0}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[T]\\
|k|=q^{j}\\
\text{$k$ monic}\end{subarray}}\mu(k)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{g}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[T]^{n}\\
0<|\mathbf{g}|=q^{e-j}\\
f(\mathbf{g})=0\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{h}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[T]^{n}\\
|\mathbf{h}|<q^{e-\varrho}\\
\mathbf{h}.\nabla f(\mathbf{g})=0\end{subarray}}1.

In view of the elementary identity

(4.1)

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[T]\\
|k|=q^{j}\\
\text{$k$ monic}\end{subarray}}\mu(k)=\begin{cases}1&\text{ if $j=0$,}\\
-q&\text{ if $j=1$,}\\
0&\text{ if $j>1$,}\end{cases}

it readily follows that

N_{\varrho}(q,e,f)=\sum_{j\geqslant 0}c_{j}N(e-j+1,e-\varrho),

where

(4.2)

c_{j}=\begin{cases}1&\text{ if $j=0$,}\\
-(q+1)&\text{ if $j=1$,}\\
q&\text{ if $j=2$,}\\
0&\text{ if $j>2$}\end{cases}

and

N(u,v)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{g}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[T]^{n}\\
0<|\mathbf{g}|<q^{u}\\
f(\mathbf{g})=0\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{h}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[T]^{n}\\
|\mathbf{h}|<q^{v}\\
\mathbf{h}.\nabla f(\mathbf{g})=0\end{subarray}}1,

for any integers
u,v\geqslant 1
.

We have

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{h}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[T]^{n}\\
|\mathbf{h}|<q^{e-\varrho}\\
\mathbf{h}.\nabla f(\mathbf{g})=0\end{subarray}}1=\int_{\mathbb{T}}S(\beta)\mathrm{d}\beta,

where

S(\beta)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{h}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[T]^{n}\\
|\mathbf{h}|<q^{e-\varrho}\end{subarray}}\psi(\beta\mathbf{h}.\nabla f(\mathbf{g})).

Here the integral is over the space
\mathbb{T}
of formal Laurent series in
T^{-1}
of degree less than
0
, against the Haar measure with total mass
1
, and
\psi
is the additive character of
\mathbb{F}_{q}((T^{-1}))
that sends a formal Laurent series in
T^{-1}
to a fixed non-trivial additive character of
\mathbb{F}_{q}
applied to the coefficient of
T^{-1}
.
With this notation we now have

(4.3)

N_{\varrho}(q,e,f)=\sum_{j\geqslant 0}c_{j}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{g}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[T]^{n}\\
0<|\mathbf{g}|<q^{e-j+1}\\
f(\mathbf{g})=0\end{subarray}}\int_{\mathbb{T}}S(\beta)\mathrm{d}\beta.

Our plan will be to define a set of major arcs whose total contribution to

q^{\varrho(n-1)-e(n-d)}N_{\varrho}(q,e,f)
is matched by
N(q,e,f).

We note that the sum over
\mathbf{g}
is empty unless
e\geqslant j
, so we will be able to assume this whenever dealing with this sum.

In what follows we shall frequently make use of the basic orthogonality property

(4.4)

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}b\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[T]\\
|b|<q^{B}\end{subarray}}\psi(\gamma b)=\begin{cases}q^{B}&\text{ if $\|\gamma\|<q^{-B}$,}\\
0&\text{ otherwise,}\end{cases}

which is valid for any integer
B\geqslant 0
and any
\gamma\in\mathbb{F}_{q}((T^{-1})).

Here
we recall that

\|\gamma\|=|\sum_{i\leqslant-1}b_{i}T^{i}|
for any
\gamma=\sum_{i\leqslant N}b_{i}T^{i}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}((T^{-1}))
.

Let
\mathbf{g}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[T]^{n}
be a non-zero vector such that
f(\mathbf{g})=0
.
The next result is the first step towards defining the relevant set of major arcs for our problem.

Lemma 4.1
.

Suppose that
\beta=a/r+\theta
for coprime polynomials
a,r\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[T]
such that
|a|<|r|\leqslant q^{e-\varrho}
.
Assume that
|r\theta|<q^{-(d-1)(e-j)}
.
Then

S(\beta)=\begin{cases}q^{n(e-\varrho)}&\text{ if $r\mid\gcd(g_{1},\dots,g_{n})^{d-1}$ and $|\theta|<q^{\varrho-e}/|\mathbf{g}|^{d-1}$,}\\
0&\text{ otherwise}.\end{cases}

Proof.

We break the sum into residue classes modulo
r
, by writing

\mathbf{h}=\mathbf{u}+r\mathbf{v}
for
|\mathbf{u}|<|r|
and
|\mathbf{v}|<q^{e-\varrho}/|r|
.
Then

S(\beta)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[T]^{n}\\
|\mathbf{u}|<|r|\end{subarray}}\psi(\beta\mathbf{u}.\nabla f(\mathbf{g}))\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[T]^{n}\\
|\mathbf{v}|<q^{e-\varrho}/|r|\end{subarray}}\psi(r\theta\mathbf{v}.\nabla f(\mathbf{g}))

Since

|r\theta|<q^{-(d-1)(e-j)}
we have

|r\theta\nabla f(\mathbf{g})|\leqslant|r\theta|q^{(d-1)(e-j)}<1
. Thus

\|r\theta\nabla f(\mathbf{g})\|=|r\theta\nabla f(\mathbf{g})|
and
it follows from (
4.4
) that

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[T]^{n}\\
|\mathbf{v}|<q^{e-\varrho}/|r|\end{subarray}}\psi(r\theta\mathbf{v}.\nabla f(\mathbf{g}))=\begin{cases}|r|^{-n}q^{n(e-\varrho)}&\text{ if $|\theta\nabla f(\mathbf{g})|<q^{\varrho-e}$,}\\
0&\text{ otherwise}.\end{cases}

We claim that

|\nabla f(\mathbf{g})|=|\mathbf{g}|^{d-1}
.
To see this suppose that

|\mathbf{g}|=q^{m}
for a non-negative integer
m
and let

\mathbf{g}^{*}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
be the (non-zero) leading coefficient of
\mathbf{g}
.
In particular
f(\mathbf{g}^{*})=0
since
f(\mathbf{g})=0
.
Since
f
has degree
d
it follows that
the coefficient of
T^{m(d-1)}
in

\nabla f(\mathbf{g})
is

\nabla f(\mathbf{g}^{*})\neq\mathbf{0}
, since

f
is non-singular.

Our argument so far shows that

S(\beta)=\begin{cases}|r|^{-n}q^{n(e-\varrho)}T(\beta)&\text{ if $|\theta|<q^{\varrho-e}/|\mathbf{g}|^{d-1}$,}\\
0&\text{ otherwise},\end{cases}

where

T(\beta)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[T]^{n}\\
|\mathbf{u}|<|r|\end{subarray}}\psi(\beta\mathbf{u}.\nabla f(\mathbf{g})).

When
|\theta|<q^{\varrho-e}/|\mathbf{g}|^{d-1}
it follows that

|\theta\mathbf{u}.\nabla f(\mathbf{g})|\leqslant q^{-1}|\theta r\nabla f(\mathbf{g})|\leqslant q^{-2+\varrho-e}|r|\leqslant q^{-2},

since
|r|\leqslant q^{e-\varrho}
. Hence, since
a
and
r
are coprime, we deduce that

T(\beta)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[T]^{n}\\
|\mathbf{u}|<|r|\end{subarray}}\psi\left(\frac{a\mathbf{u}.\nabla f(\mathbf{g})}{r}\right)=\begin{cases}|r|^{n}&\text{ if $r\mid\nabla f(\mathbf{g})$,}\\
0&\text{ otherwise},\end{cases}

Since
f
is a non-singular form, the statement of the lemma follows on noting that

r\mid\nabla f(\mathbf{g})
if and only if
r\mid\gcd(g_{1},\dots,g_{n})^{d-1}
.
∎

Lemma 4.2
.

Suppose that
e\geqslant\varrho
and

\frac{a_{1}}{r_{1}}+\theta_{1}=\frac{a_{2}}{r_{2}}+\theta_{2},

with
r_{1},r_{2}\mid\gcd(g_{1},\dots,g_{n})^{d-1}
and
|\theta_{1}|,|\theta_{2}|<q^{\varrho-e}/|\mathbf{g}|^{d-1}.

Then in fact
\frac{a_{1}}{r_{1}}=\frac{a_{2}}{r_{2}}
(and so
\theta_{1}=\theta_{2}
).

Proof.

By clearing denominators, we may assume
r_{1}=r_{2}=\gcd(g_{1},\dots,g_{n})^{d-1}
. Then

a_{1}-a_{2}=\gcd(g_{1},\dots,g_{n})^{d-1}(\theta_{2}-\theta_{1})
, so that

|a_{1}-a_{2}|<q^{\varrho-e}\frac{\gcd(g_{1},\dots,g_{n})^{d-1}}{|\mathbf{g}|^{d-1}}\leqslant q^{\varrho-e}\leqslant 1.

This implies that
a_{1}=a_{2}
, as required.
∎

We take as major arcs the union

(4.5)

\mathfrak{N}_{j}=\bigcup_{\begin{subarray}{c}r\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[T]\text{ monic}\\
|r|\leqslant q^{e-\varrho}\end{subarray}}\bigcup_{\begin{subarray}{c}|a|<|r|\\
\gcd(a,r)=1\end{subarray}}\left\{\beta\in\mathbb{F}_{q}((T^{-1})):\left|r\beta-a\right|<q^{-(d-1)(e-j)}\right\},

for
j\geqslant 0
.
It follows from Lemma
4.1
that
S(\beta)
is non-zero for
\beta\in\mathfrak{N}_{j}
if and only if there is some pair
(a/r,\theta)
such that
\beta=a/r+\theta
and all the conditions

|r|\leqslant q^{e-\varrho},\quad|\theta|<|r|^{-1}q^{-(d-1)(e-j)},\quad|a|<|r|,\quad\gcd(a,r)=1,

and

r\mid\gcd(g_{1},\dots,g_{n})^{d-1},\quad|\theta|<q^{\varrho-e}/|\mathbf{g}|^{d-1}

are satisfied. By Lemma
4.2
, pairs satisfying these conditions
(or even the last three conditions)
are unique. Hence we can rewrite the integral over the major arcs as

\displaystyle\int_{\mathfrak{N}_{j}}S(\beta)\mathrm{d}\beta

\displaystyle=q^{n(e-\varrho)}\hskip-8.5359pt\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}|r|\leqslant q^{e-\varrho}\\
\text{$r$ monic}\\
r\mid\gcd(g_{1},\dots,g_{n})^{d-1}\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}|a|<|r|\\
\gcd(a,r)=1\end{subarray}}\hskip-8.5359pt\int_{|\theta|<\min\{q^{\varrho-e}/|\mathbf{g}|^{d-1},~|r|^{-1}q^{-(d-1)(e-j)}\}}\mathrm{d}\theta

\displaystyle=q^{n(e-\varrho)}\hskip-8.5359pt\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}|r|\leqslant q^{e-\varrho}\\
\text{$r$ monic}\\
r\mid\gcd(g_{1},\dots,g_{n})^{d-1}\end{subarray}}\hskip-8.5359pt\varphi(r)\int_{|\theta|<\min\{q^{\varrho-e}/|\mathbf{g}|^{d-1},~|r|^{-1}q^{-(d-1)(e-j)}\}}\mathrm{d}\theta,

for any non-zero vector
\mathbf{g}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[T]^{n}
such that
f(\mathbf{g})=0
,
where

\varphi(r)
is the function field analogue of the Euler totient function.
We want to replace the integral over
\theta
by

\int_{|\theta|<q^{\varrho-e}/|\mathbf{g}|^{d-1}}\mathrm{d}\theta=\frac{q^{\varrho-e}}{|\mathbf{g}|^{d-1}}.

The error in doing this is at most this volume multiplied by the indicator function for the inequality

|r|^{-1}q^{-(d-1)(e-j)}<q^{\varrho-e}/|\mathbf{g}|^{d-1}.

Since
r\mid\gcd(g_{1},\dots,g_{n})^{d-1}
this inequality implies that

(4.6)

q^{j+D+1}|\mathbf{g}|\leqslant|\gcd(g_{1},\dots,g_{n})|q^{e},

where

(4.7)

D=\left\lfloor\frac{e-\varrho}{d-1}\right\rfloor.

At this point we observe that

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\text{$r\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[T]$ monic}\\
r\mid\gcd(g_{1},\dots,g_{n})^{d-1}\end{subarray}}\hskip-8.5359pt\varphi(r)=|\gcd(g_{1},\dots,g_{n})|^{d-1},

since
\mathbf{g}\neq\mathbf{0}
.
Note that when

r\mid\gcd(g_{1},\dots,g_{n})^{d-1}
and
|r|>q^{e-\varrho}
we must have

(4.8)

|\gcd(g_{1},\dots,g_{n})|\geqslant q^{D+1},

with
D
as above.
Putting everything together it follows that

(4.9)

\int_{\mathfrak{N}_{j}}S(\beta)\mathrm{d}\beta=\frac{q^{(n-1)(e-\varrho)}|\gcd(g_{1},\dots,g_{n})|^{d-1}}{|\mathbf{g}|^{d-1}}\left(1+\epsilon_{j}\mathbf{1}_{j}(\mathbf{g})\right)

for
\epsilon_{j}\in[-1,1]
,
where

\mathbf{1}_{j}(\mathbf{g})=\begin{cases}1&\text{ if \eqref{eq:error-1} or \eqref{eq:error-2} hold,}\\
0&\text{ otherwise}.\end{cases}

Let
N_{\varrho}^{\text{major}}(q,e,f)
denote the contribution
to the right hand side
of (
4.3
) from (
4.9
) for each
j
.
We now see that

\displaystyle N_{\varrho}^{\text{major}}(q,e,f)

\displaystyle=q^{(n-1)(e-\varrho)}\sum_{j\geqslant 0}c_{j}\hskip-8.5359pt\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{g}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[T]^{n}\\
0<|\mathbf{g}|<q^{e-j+1}\\
f(\mathbf{g})=0\end{subarray}}\frac{|\gcd(g_{1},\dots,g_{n})|^{d-1}}{|\mathbf{g}|^{d-1}}\left(1+\epsilon_{j}\mathbf{1}_{j}(\mathbf{g})\right).

On noting that

(n-1)(e-\varrho)-e(d-1)=e(n-d)-\varrho(n-1)
,
the main term is seen to be

\displaystyle q^{e(n-d)-\varrho(n-1)}\left(\tilde{N}(e)-q^{d}\tilde{N}(e-1)\right),

where for
u\geqslant 0
we set

\displaystyle\tilde{N}(u)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{g}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[T]^{n}\\
|\mathbf{g}|=q^{u}\\
f(\mathbf{g})=0\end{subarray}}|\gcd(g_{1},\dots,g_{n})|^{d-1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[T]\\
|k|\leqslant q^{u}\\
\text{$k$ monic}\end{subarray}}|k|^{d-1}N(q,u-\deg(k),f)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=0}^{u}q^{d\ell}N(q,u-\ell,f),

in the notation of Definition
3.6
.
Hence

\displaystyle\tilde{N}(e)-q^{d}\tilde{N}(e-1)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=0}^{e}q^{d\ell}N(q,e-\ell,f)-\sum_{\ell=0}^{e-1}q^{d(\ell+1)}N(q,e-1-\ell,f)

\displaystyle=N(q,e,f).

Remark 4.3
.

The cancellation here is not miraculous. The terms corresponding to
\mathbf{g}
with
|\mathbf{g}|<q^{e}
or
|\gcd(g_{1},\dots,g_{n})|>1
disappear precisely because
c_{j}
were the coefficients defined
in (
4.2
)
to sieve out these terms in the first place.

Turning to the error term we can combine (
4.6
) and (
4.8
)
to deduce that
\gcd(g_{1},\dots,g_{n})\geqslant q^{D+1}\min(1,q^{j-e}|\mathbf{g}|)=q^{D+1+j-e}|\mathbf{g}|
whenever

\mathbf{1}_{j}(\mathbf{g})=1
.
Hence

N_{\varrho}^{\text{major}}(q,e,f)-q^{e(n-d)-\varrho(n-1)}N(q,e,f)\leqslant q^{(n-1)(e-\varrho)}\sum_{j\geqslant 0}|c_{j}|E_{j},

where

\displaystyle E_{j}

\displaystyle=\sum_{0\leqslant u\leqslant e-j}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[T]\text{ monic}\\
|k|\geqslant q^{D+1+j-e+u}\end{subarray}}\frac{|k|^{d-1}}{q^{u(d-1)}}\#\left\{\mathbf{g}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[T]^{n}:\begin{array}[]{l}|\mathbf{g}|=q^{u},~f(\mathbf{g})=0\\
k=\gcd(g_{1}\dots,g_{n})\end{array}\right\}

\displaystyle=\sum_{0\leqslant u\leqslant e-j}\sum_{\ell\geqslant D+1+j-e+u}\frac{q^{\ell}}{q^{(u-\ell)(d-1)}}N(q,u-\ell,f).

Invoking
[
3
, Lemma 2.8]
, we deduce that

N(q,e,f)=O_{f}(q^{(e+1)(n-1)})

for any
n\geqslant 3
, where the implied constant depends at most on
f
. Hence,
since we may clearly assume that
n>d+1
,
it follows that

(4.10)

\begin{split}E_{j}&\ll_{f}\sum_{0\leqslant u\leqslant e-j}q^{u(n-d)+n-1}\sum_{\ell\geqslant D+1+j-e+u}q^{-\ell(n-d-1)}\\
&\ll_{f}q^{-(D+1)(n-d-1)}\sum_{0\leqslant u\leqslant e-j}\frac{q^{u(n-d)+n-1}}{q^{(u-e+j)(n-d-1)}}\\
&\ll_{f}q^{(e-j)(n-d)+n-1-(D+1)(n-d-1)}.\end{split}

The implied constant in this estimate depends only on
f
and not on
q
.
Thus

q^{(n-1)(e-\varrho)}\sum_{j\geqslant 0}|c_{j}|E_{j}\ll_{f}q^{2e(n-d)-\varrho(n-1)+de-e+n-1-(D+1)(n-d-1)}.

Putting everything together, we may conclude as follows.

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
\varrho\in\mathbb{Z}
and assume that
e\geqslant\varrho
. Then

N_{\varrho}^{\text{major}}(q,e,f)=q^{e(n-d)-\varrho(n-1)}\left(N(q,e,f)+O_{f}(q^{(e+1)(n-1)-(D+1)(n-d-1)})\right),

where

D
is given by (
4.7
).

5.
The circle method: minor arcs

It remains to study the quantity

(5.1)

N_{\varrho}^{\text{minor}}(q,e,f)=\sum_{j\geqslant 0}c_{j}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{g}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[T]^{n}\\
0<|\mathbf{g}|<q^{e-j+1}\\
f(\mathbf{g})=0\end{subarray}}\int_{\mathfrak{n}_{j}}S(\beta)\mathrm{d}\beta,

where
\mathfrak{n}_{j}
is the complement
in
\mathbb{T}=\{\beta\in\mathbb{F}_{q}((T^{-1})):|\beta|<1\}

of the major arcs
\mathfrak{N}_{j}
that we defined in (
4.5
).
Indeed, in view of Proposition
3.8
(2),
the following result is now a direct consequence of (
4.3
) and Lemma
4.4
.

Lemma 5.1
.

Assume that
d\geqslant 3
and

e\geqslant\varrho
.
Then

\#Z_{\varrho}(\mathbb{F}_{q})\leqslant\frac{q^{\varrho(n-1)-e(n-d)}}{(q-1)^{2}(q^{3}-q)}N_{\varrho}^{\text{minor}}(q,e,f)+O_{f}(q^{(e+1)(n-1)-5-(D+1)(n-d-1)}),

where
D
is given by (
4.7
).

We have

(5.2)

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{g}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[T]^{n}\\
0<|\mathbf{g}|<q^{e-j+1}\\
f(\mathbf{g})=0\end{subarray}}\int_{\mathfrak{n}_{j}}S(\beta)\mathrm{d}\beta=\int_{\mathbb{T}}\int_{\mathfrak{n}_{j}}(S(\alpha,\beta)-q^{n(e-\varrho)})\mathrm{d}\alpha\mathrm{d}\beta,

where

(5.3)

S(\alpha,\beta)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{g}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[T]^{n}\\
|\mathbf{g}|<q^{e-j+1}\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{h}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[T]^{n}\\
|\mathbf{h}|<q^{e-\varrho}\end{subarray}}\psi(\alpha f(\mathbf{g})+\beta\mathbf{h}.\nabla f(\mathbf{g})).

Viewed as polynomials in the
2n
variables
(\mathbf{g},\mathbf{h})
the pair of polynomials

f(\mathbf{g})
and
\mathbf{h}.\nabla f(\mathbf{g})
are homogeneous of degree
d
.
The obvious thing to do at this point is to apply Weyl differencing
d-1
times in the spirit of Birch. This requires one to work with a simultaneous Diophantine approximation of
\alpha
and
\beta
, which is somewhat
wasteful. It bears fruit provided that

2n-\dim V^{*}>3(d-1)2^{d},

where
V^{*}
is the (affine) “Birch singular locus”. In this setting
V^{*}
is the locus of
(\mathbf{g},\mathbf{h})\in\mathbb{A}^{2n}
such that the pair of vectors
(\nabla f(\mathbf{g}),\mathbf{0})
and

(\mathbf{h}.\nabla^{2}f(\mathbf{g}),\nabla f(\mathbf{g}))
are proportional.
Since
f
is non-singular, it follows that
V^{*}
is the set of
(\mathbf{g},\mathbf{h})\in\mathbb{A}^{2n}
such that
\mathbf{g}=\mathbf{0}
, so that
\dim V^{*}=n
. In this way we see that the standard approach would require

n>3(d-1)2^{d}
variables overall, although there are additional difficulties associated to having lopsided boxes.
In our work we shall exploit the special shape of our polynomials in such a way that our estimates are only sensitive to the Diophantine approximation properties of
\alpha
or
\beta
independently.
This allows us to handle half the number of variables when dealing with the sum
S(\alpha,\beta).

In what follows it will be convenient to define the monomials

P_{0}(T)=T^{e-j},\quad P(T)=T^{e-j+1}\quad\text{ and }\quad Q(T)=T^{e-\varrho}.

Let

(5.4)

\mathfrak{M}(J)=\bigcup_{\begin{subarray}{c}r\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[T]\text{ monic}\\
|r|\leqslant q^{J}\end{subarray}}\bigcup_{\begin{subarray}{c}|a|<|r|\\
\gcd(a,r)=1\end{subarray}}\left\{\alpha\in\mathbb{F}_{q}((T^{-1})):\left|r\alpha-a\right|<q^{J}|P_{0}|^{-d}\right\},

for any integer
J
.
Note that
\mathfrak{M}(-1)=\emptyset
. Let

(5.5)

M=\left\lceil\frac{d(e-j)}{2}\right\rceil.

According to the function field version
of Dirichlet’s approximation theorem
any element of
\mathbb{T}
has a representation

a/r+\theta
with
|a|<|r|\leqslant q^{M}
and
|r\theta|<q^{-M}
. Hence we can cover
\mathbb{T}
by a union of arcs

\mathfrak{M}(J+1)\setminus\mathfrak{M}(J)
for integers
J
such that

-1\leqslant J\leqslant M-1
.

Next, let

(5.6)

\mathfrak{N}(K)=\bigcup_{\begin{subarray}{c}r\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[T]\text{ monic}\\
|r|\leqslant q^{K}\end{subarray}}\bigcup_{\begin{subarray}{c}|a|<|r|\\
\gcd(a,r)=1\end{subarray}}\left\{\beta\in\mathbb{F}_{q}((T^{-1})):\left|r\beta-a\right|<\frac{q^{K}}{|P_{0}|^{d-1}|Q|}\right\},

for any integer
K
.
We note that
\mathfrak{N}(e-\varrho)=\mathfrak{N}_{j}
, in the notation of (
4.5
).
Let

(5.7)

N=\left\lceil\frac{(e-j)(d-1)+e-\varrho}{2}\right\rceil.

It now follows from Dirichlet’s approximation theorem that
the minor arcs

\mathfrak{n}_{j}
can be covered by
the union of arcs

\mathfrak{N}(K+1)\setminus\mathfrak{N}(K)
for integers
K
such that

e-\varrho\leqslant K\leqslant N-1
.

Observe in particular that if any minor arcs exist then
e-\varrho<N
so

(5.8)

(d-1)(e-j)>e-\varrho.

We may thus assume (
5.8
) when dealing with the minor arcs. Keeping the assumptions
d\geqslant 3
and
e\geqslant\varrho
, we see in particular that

|P|,|Q|\geqslant 1.

Our plan is to produce two estimates for
S(\alpha,\beta)
: one for when

\alpha
belongs to
\mathfrak{M}(J+1)\setminus\mathfrak{M}(J)
and one for when

\beta
belongs to
\mathfrak{N}(K+1)\setminus\mathfrak{N}(K)
.
Before proceeding further we note that

(5.9)

\meas\left(\mathfrak{M}(J)\right)\leqslant q^{2J}|P_{0}|^{-d}

and

(5.10)

\meas\left(\mathfrak{N}(K)\right)\leqslant q^{2K}|P_{0}|^{-d+1}|Q|^{-1},

for any integers
J,K\geqslant 0
.

Suppose that

f(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{i_{1},\dots,i_{d}=1}^{n}c_{i_{1},\dots,i_{d}}x_{i_{1}}\dots x_{i_{d}},

with symmetric coefficients
c_{i_{1},\dots,i_{d}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
.
Associated to
f
are the multilinear forms

(5.11)

\Psi_{i}(\mathbf{x}^{(1)},\dots,\mathbf{x}^{({d-1})})=d!\sum_{i_{1},\dots,i_{d-1}=1}^{n}c_{i_{1},\dots,i_{d-1},i}x_{i_{1}}^{(1)}\dots x_{i_{d-1}}^{(d-1)},

for
1\leqslant i\leqslant n
.
Our first estimate for
S(\alpha,\beta)
involves
summing trivially over
\mathbf{h}
and then applying
Weyl differencing

d-1
times to the sum over
\mathbf{g}
. This eliminates the effect of the lower degree term
\beta\mathbf{h}.\nabla f(\mathbf{g})
and leads one to a family of linear exponential sums
with phase vectors

(\alpha\Psi_{1}(\underline{\mathbf{g}}),\dots,\alpha\Psi_{n}(\underline{\mathbf{g}}))
, for

\underline{\mathbf{g}}=(\mathbf{g}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{g}_{d-1})\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[T]^{(d-1)n}
.
This approach closely parallels

[
4
]
.

An alternative estimate for
S(\alpha,\beta)
is obtained by applying Weyl differencing
d-2
times to the sum over
\mathbf{g}
. After a further application of Cauchy–Schwarz one then brings the
\mathbf{h}
-sum inside, giving a family of linear exponential sums with
phase vectors
(\beta\Psi_{1}(\underline{\mathbf{g}}),\dots,\beta\Psi_{n}(\underline{\mathbf{g}}))
, for

\underline{\mathbf{g}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[T]^{(d-1)n}
. This brings the Diophantine properties of
\beta
into play but extra difficulties arise from the fact that
P
and
Q
need not have the same degree.

5.1.
Geometry-of-numbers redux

We shall need to begin by revisiting a function field lattice point counting result that played a key role in
[
4
]
.
A
lattice in
\mathbb{F}_{q}((T^{-1}))^{N}
is a set of points of the form
{\bf x}=\Lambda\bf u
where
\Lambda
is an
N\times N
invertible matrix over
\mathbb{F}_{q}((T^{-1}))
and
{\bf u}
runs over elements of
\mathbb{F}_{q}[T]^{n}
. Given a lattice
\Lambda
, the adjoint lattice is defined as the lattice associated to the inverse transpose matrix
\Lambda^{-T}
.

Remark 5.2
.

We can view lattices as vector bundles on
\mathbb{P}^{1}
by viewing the matrix
\Lambda
as giving gluing data for gluing the trivial vector bundle on
\mathbb{A}^{1}
and the trivial vector bundle on a formal neighborhood of
\infty
, using the Beauville–Laszlo theorem. The adjoint lattice corresponds to the dual vector bundle, and the geometry-of-numbers computations in this section could instead be stated in this language.

Bearing our notation in mind we recall a
version of the “shrinking lemma” that is proved in
[
1
, Lemma 6.4]
.

Lemma 5.3
.

Let
\gamma
be a symmetric
n\times n
matrix with entries in
\mathbb{F}_{q}((T^{-1}))
.
Let
a,c,s\in\mathbb{Z}
such that
c>0
and
s\geqslant 0
.
Let
N_{\gamma,a,c}
be the number of
{\bf x}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[T]^{n}
such that
|{\bf x}|<q^{a}
and
\|\gamma{\bf x}\|<q^{-c}
. Then

\frac{N_{\gamma,a,c}}{N_{\gamma,a-s,c+s}}\leqslant q^{ns+n\max(\lfloor\frac{a-c}{2}\rfloor,0)}.

For any

\alpha\in\mathbb{F}_{q}((T^{-1}))
and any
r>0
, we set

(5.12)

N(\alpha;r)=\#\left\{\underline{\mathbf{g}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[T]^{(d-1)n}:\begin{array}[]{l}|\mathbf{g}_{1}|,\dots,|\mathbf{g}_{d-1}|<|P|\\
\|\alpha\Psi_{i}(\underline{\mathbf{g}})\|<q^{-r}~(\forall i\leqslant n)\end{array}{}\right\}.

Furthermore, for an integer
s\geqslant 0
, we put

N_{s}(\alpha;r)=\#\left\{\underline{\mathbf{g}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[T]^{(d-1)n}:\begin{array}[]{l}|\mathbf{g}_{1}|,\dots,|\mathbf{g}_{d-1}|<|P|/q^{s}\\
\|\alpha\Psi_{i}(\underline{\mathbf{g}})\|<q^{-r-(d-1)s}~(\forall i\leqslant n)\end{array}{}\right\}.

We can use the shrinking lemma to bound the ratio of these two quantities as follows.

Lemma 5.4
.

For
r>0
and

{s}\geqslant\max(0,e-j+1-r)
,
we have

\frac{N(\alpha,r)}{N_{s}(\alpha,r)}\leqslant q^{(d-1)ns+n\max(0,\lfloor\frac{e-j+1-r}{2}\rfloor)}.

Proof.

For each
v\in\{0,\dots,d-1\}
,
let
N^{(v)}(\alpha,r)
be the number of vectors
\underline{\mathbf{g}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[T]^{(d-1)n}

such that

(5.13)

|\mathbf{g}_{1}|,\dots,|\mathbf{g}_{v}|<|P|/q^{s},\quad|\mathbf{g}_{v+1}|,\dots,|\mathbf{g}_{d-1}|<|P|

and

\|\alpha\Psi_{i}(\underline{\mathbf{g}})\|<q^{-r-v},

for
1\leqslant i\leqslant n
.
Thus we have
N^{(0)}(\alpha,r)=N(\alpha,r)
and
N^{(d-1)}(\alpha,r)=N_{s}(\alpha,r)
.

Fix a choice of
v\in\{1,\dots,d-1\}
and let
\mathbf{g}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{g}_{v-1},\mathbf{g}_{v+1},\dots,\mathbf{g}_{d-1}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[T]^{n}
such that (
5.13
) holds.
We consider the linear forms

\displaystyle L_{i}(\mathbf{g})

\displaystyle=\alpha\Psi_{i}(\mathbf{g}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{g}_{v-1},\mathbf{g},\mathbf{g}_{v+1},\dots,\mathbf{g}_{d-1}),

for
1\leqslant i\leqslant n
.
These form an
n\times n
matrix. Because
\Psi_{i}
is the dualization in one variable of a symmetric
d
-linear form, this
n\times n
matrix is symmetric. The contribution to
N^{(v-1)}(\alpha,r)
from tuples with the chosen
\mathbf{g}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{g}_{v-1},\mathbf{g}_{v+1},\dots,\mathbf{g}_{d-1}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[T]^{n}
is
N_{\gamma,e-j+1,r+(v-1)s}
while the contribution to

N^{(v)}(\alpha,r)

from tuples of the same form is
N_{\gamma,e-j+1-s,r+vs}
. Note that
r+(v-1)s\geqslant r>0
for
v\geqslant 1
and so Lemma
5.3
is applicable. We
deduce that

\frac{N^{(v-1)}(\alpha,r)}{N^{(v)}(\alpha,r)}\leqslant q^{ns+n\max(\lfloor\frac{e-j+1-r-(v-1)s}{2}\rfloor,0)}

for
1\leqslant v\leqslant d-1
.

We take the product of this inequality over all
v
from
1
to
d-1
. The first term
in the exponent
contributes
(d-1)ns
. The second contributes
n\max(\lfloor\frac{e-j+1-r}{2}\rfloor,0)
for
v=1
and
0
for all other values of
v
, on assuming that
s\geqslant e-j+1-r
. Thus we get the stated bound. ∎

5.2.
Weyl differencing

Our fundamental tool for estimating

S(\alpha,\beta)
is Weyl differencing.
We recall first that
|P|,|Q|\geqslant 1
in this exponential sum.
Appealing to
[
4
, Eq. (5.2)]
first, Weyl differencing
d-1
times gives

|S(\alpha,\beta)|\leqslant|P|^{n}|Q|^{n}\left(|P|^{-(d-1)n}N(\alpha,e-j+1)\right)^{1/2^{d-1}},

in the notation of (
5.12
). Note that as
N(\alpha,e-j+1)\geqslant 1
and
2^{d-1}\geqslant(d-1)
, the right side is
\geqslant|Q|^{n}
.
Thus we have

(5.14)

|S(\alpha,\beta)-q^{n(e-\varrho)}|\leqslant 2|P|^{n}|Q|^{n}\left(|P|^{-(d-1)n}N(\alpha,e-j+1)\right)^{1/2^{d-1}},

We can also obtain an upper bound for
S(\alpha,\beta)
that only uses information about

\beta
.
Let us put

T(\mathbf{h})=\sum_{|\mathbf{g}|<|P|}\psi(\alpha f(\mathbf{g})+\beta\mathbf{h}.\nabla f(\mathbf{g})),

so that

S(\alpha,\beta)=\sum_{|\mathbf{h}|<|Q|}T(\mathbf{h}),

with
P,Q
are as before.
It follows from Cauchy–Schwarz that

(5.15)

|S(\alpha,\beta)|^{2^{d-2}}\leqslant|Q|^{(2^{d-2}-1)n}\sum_{|\mathbf{h}|<|Q|}|T(\mathbf{h})|^{2^{d-2}}.

After
d-3
applications of Weyl differencing we obtain

|T(\mathbf{h})|^{2^{d-3}}\leqslant|P|^{(2^{d-3}-d+2)n}\sum_{\mathbf{g}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{g}_{d-3}}\left|\sum_{\mathbf{g}}\psi\left(D(\mathbf{g})\right)\right|,

where
D(\mathbf{g})=D_{\mathbf{g}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{g}_{d-3}}(\alpha f(\mathbf{g})+\beta\mathbf{h}.\nabla f(\mathbf{g}))
and

D_{\mathbf{g}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{g}_{d-3}}
is the usual differencing operator. Here
\mathbf{g}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{g}_{d-3},\mathbf{g}
each run over vectors in
\mathbb{F}_{q}[T]^{n}
formed from polynomials of degree less than
e-j+1
.
A further application of Cauchy–Schwarz now yields

|T(\mathbf{h})|^{2^{d-2}}\leqslant|P|^{(2^{d-2}-d+1)n}\sum_{\mathbf{g}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{g}_{d-3}}\left|\sum_{\mathbf{g}}\psi\left(D(\mathbf{g})\right)\right|^{2}.

Differencing once more therefore leads to the expression

\left|\sum_{\mathbf{g}}\psi\left(D(\mathbf{g})\right)\right|^{2}=\sum_{\mathbf{g}_{d-2},\mathbf{g}_{d-1}}\psi\left(D_{\mathbf{g}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{g}_{d-2}}(\alpha f(\mathbf{g}_{d-1})+\beta\mathbf{h}.\nabla f(\mathbf{g}_{d-1})\right),

where

D_{\mathbf{g}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{g}_{d-2}}(\mathbf{h}.\nabla f(\mathbf{g}_{d-1})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}h_{i}\Psi_{i}(\mathbf{g}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{g}_{d-1}),

in the notation of (
5.11
).
Returning to (

)
we ignore the Diophantine approximation properties of
\alpha
and instead execute the linear exponential sum over
\mathbf{h}
. This leads to the expression

|S(\alpha,\beta)|\leqslant|P|^{n}|Q|^{n}\left(|P|^{-(d-1)n}N(\beta,e-\varrho)\right)^{1/2^{d-2}},

in the notation of (
5.12
). Again,
N(\beta,e-\varrho)\geqslant 1
and
2^{d-2}\geqslant(d-1)
so the right side is
\geqslant|Q|^{n}
, whence

(5.16)

|S(\alpha,\beta)-q^{n(e-\varrho)}|\leqslant 2|P|^{n}|Q|^{n}\left(|P|^{-(d-1)n}N(\beta,e-\varrho)\right)^{1/2^{d-2}}.

Remark 5.5
.

When
\mathrm{char}(\mathbb{F}_{q})\leqslant d
the polynomials
\Psi_{i}
are identically zero for
1\leqslant i\leqslant n
, so that
(

) and
(

) give nothing beyond the trivial bound for the exponential sum

.

Recall the definitions (
5.4
) and
(
5.6
) of

\mathfrak{M}(J)
and

\mathfrak{N}(K)
, respectively.
We want to bound the size of

when
\alpha\not\in\mathfrak{M}(J)

and
\beta\not\in\mathfrak{N}(K)
. To do this it will be convenient to introduce two parameters

and

.
Associated to these are the quantities

l_{1}=e-j+1-s_{1}\quad\text{ and }\quad l_{2}=e-j+1-s_{2}.

We can use our geometry-of-numbers shrinking result to establishing the following pair of estimates.

Lemma 5.6
.

Let
\alpha\not\in\mathfrak{M}(J)

and let

l_{1}\in\mathbb{Z}
be such that

l_{1}\leqslant 1+\frac{J}{d-1}\quad\text{ and }\quad l_{1}\leqslant e-j+1.

Then
there exists a constant

such that.

N(\alpha,e-j+1)\leqslant c_{d,n}q^{-nl_{1}}|P|^{(d-1)n}.

Proof.

It follows from Lemma
5.4
that

N(\alpha,e-j+1)
is at most

q^{(d-1)ns_{1}}\#\left\{\underline{\mathbf{g}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[T]^{(d-1)n}:\begin{array}[]{l}|\mathbf{g}_{1}|,\dots,|\mathbf{g}_{d-1}|<|P|/q^{s_{1}}\\
\|\alpha\Psi_{i}(\underline{\mathbf{g}})\|<|P|^{-1}q^{-(d-1)s_{1}}~(\forall i\leqslant n)\end{array}{}\right\},

for any
s_{1}\geqslant 0
.
Note that
|P|/q^{s_{1}}=q^{l_{1}}
and
q^{s_{1}}=|P_{0}|/q^{l_{1}-1}
.
Suppose that

|\mathbf{g}_{1}|,\dots,|\mathbf{g}_{d-1}|<|P|/q^{s_{1}}

and

\|\alpha\Psi_{i}(\underline{\mathbf{g}})\|<|P|^{-1}q^{-(d-1)s_{1}}

but
\Psi_{i}(\underline{\mathbf{g}})\neq 0
. Let
r=\Psi_{i}(\underline{\mathbf{g}})
and let

be the integer part of
\alpha\Psi_{i}(\underline{\mathbf{g}})
, each divided through by any common factors that they might share.
Then
|r|\leqslant q^{(d-1)(l_{1}-1)}

This contradicts the assumption that
\alpha\not\in\mathfrak{M}(J)
,
if

.
Hence, if
J\geqslant(d-1)(l_{1}-1)
and
\alpha\not\in\mathfrak{M}(J)
,
we have

N(\alpha,e-j+1)\leqslant q^{(d-1)ns_{1}}\#\left\{\underline{\mathbf{g}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[T]^{(d-1)n}:\begin{array}[]{l}|\mathbf{g}_{1}|,\dots,|\mathbf{g}_{d-1}|<q^{l_{1}}\\
\Psi_{i}(\underline{\mathbf{g}})=0~(\forall i\leqslant n)\end{array}{}\right\}.

The statement of the lemma follows on noting that
the remaining cardinality is
O(q^{(d-2)nl_{1}})
for dimensionality reasons,
where the implied constant depends only on

and

.
∎

Lemma 5.7
.

Let
\beta\not\in\mathfrak{N}(K)

and let

l_{2}\leqslant 1+\frac{K}{d-1}\quad\text{ and }\quad l_{2}\leqslant e-j+1-\max(0,\varrho-j+1).

such that.

N(\beta,e-\varrho)\leqslant c_{d,n}q^{-nl_{2}+n\max(0,\lfloor\frac{\varrho-j+1}{2}\rfloor)}|P|^{(d-1)n}.

Proof.

This time we take
r=e-\varrho
in Lemma
5.4
and deduce that

\displaystyle N(\beta,e-\varrho)

\displaystyle\leqslant~q^{(d-1)ns_{2}+n\max(0,\lfloor\frac{\varrho-j+1}{2}\rfloor)}

\displaystyle\times\#\left\{\underline{\mathbf{g}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[T]^{(d-1)n}:\begin{array}[]{l}|\mathbf{g}_{1}|,\dots,|\mathbf{g}_{d-1}|<q^{l_{2}}\\
\|\beta\Psi_{i}(\underline{\mathbf{g}})\|<|Q|^{-1}q^{-(d-1)s_{2}}~(\forall i\leqslant n)\end{array}{}\right\},

for any
s_{2}\geqslant\max(0,\varrho-j+1)
.
Arguing as in the previous result it is simple to check that we must in fact have

\Psi_{i}(\underline{\mathbf{g}})=0
for all
1\leqslant i\leqslant n
whenever

\beta\not\in\mathfrak{N}(K)
and
K\geqslant(d-1)(l_{2}-1)
.
But then there are

O(q^{(d-2)nl_{2}})
possible vectors

that contribute. The statement of the lemma follows.
∎

In our work we shall take

(5.17)

l_{1}=1+\left\lfloor\frac{J}{d-1}\right\rfloor,\quad l_{2}=1+\left\lfloor\frac{K}{d-1}\right\rfloor.

We need to check that the remaining conditions on
l_{1}
and
l_{2}
are satisfied in Lemmas
5.6
and
5.7
.
To begin with we note that

J\leqslant\left\lceil\frac{d(e-j)}{2}\right\rceil-1\leqslant\frac{d(e-j)}{2}-\frac{1}{2}

Hence for

5.6
to be applicable it suffices to have

But this is equivalent to
0\leqslant 1+(d-2)(e-j)
which follows from (
5.8
).
Next, we note that

K\leqslant\left\lceil\frac{(e-j)(d-1)+e-\varrho}{2}\right\rceil-1\leqslant\frac{(e-j)(d-1)+e-\varrho}{2}-\frac{1}{2},

so that Lemma
5.7

e-\varrho-1\leqslant(d-1)(e-j-2\max(0,\varrho-j+1)).

Thus it suffices to have

(5.18)

e-\varrho-1\leqslant(d-1)(e-j)

and

(5.19)

e-\varrho-1\leqslant(d-1)(e+j-2\varrho-2).

However, (
5.18
) follows from (
5.8

5.19
) holds.

Inserting
Lemmas
5.6
and
5.7
into our Weyl differencing bounds
(
5.14
) and (
5.16
), we deduce that there exists a constant

such that

\displaystyle|S(\alpha,\beta)-q^{n(e-\varrho)}|

\displaystyle\leqslant c_{d,n}|P|^{n}|Q|^{n}\min\left(q^{-nl_{1}/2^{d-1}},q^{-n(l_{2}-n\max(0,\lfloor\frac{\varrho-j+1}{2}\rfloor))/2^{d-2}}\right)

\displaystyle=c_{d,n}|P|^{n}|Q|^{n}/\max\left(q^{l_{1}},q^{2l_{2}-2\max(0,\lfloor\frac{\varrho-j+1}{2}\rfloor)}\right)^{n/2^{d-1}},

whenever
(\alpha,\beta)\in\mathfrak{M}(J+1)\setminus\mathfrak{M}(J)\times\mathfrak{N}(K+1)\setminus\mathfrak{N}(K)
and (
5.19
) holds.
We shall proceed under the assumption that the parameter
l_{2}
satisfies

(5.20)

l_{2}-\max\left(0,\left\lfloor\frac{\varrho-j+1}{2}\right\rfloor\right)\geqslant 0.

This is precisely the circumstance under which our
\beta

Assume that

5.20

Thus it follows that

\displaystyle|S(\alpha,\beta)-q^{n(e-\varrho)}|

\displaystyle\leqslant c_{d,n}\frac{q^{2(d-1)(2l_{2}-l_{1})-4(d-1)\max(0,\lfloor\frac{\varrho-j+1}{2}\rfloor)}|P|^{n}|Q|^{n}}{q^{(l_{2}-\max(0,\lfloor\frac{\varrho-j+1}{2}\rfloor))n/2^{d-2}}}.

Returning to (

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{g}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[T]^{n}\\
0<|\mathbf{g}|<q^{e-j+1}\\
f(\mathbf{g})=0\end{subarray}}\int_{\mathfrak{n}_{j}}S(\beta)\mathrm{d}\beta\leqslant\sum_{J=-1}^{M-1}\sum_{K=e-\varrho}^{N-1}E(J,K)

The measure of all

by (

Let us consider the total contribution

E_{l_{1},l_{2}}=\sum_{J=\max((d-1)(l_{1}-1),-1)}^{(d-1)l_{1}-1}\sum_{K=\max((d-1)(l_{2}-1),e-\varrho)}^{(d-1)l_{2}-1}E_{J,K},

from

Then

\displaystyle E_{l_{1},l_{2}}

\displaystyle\ll\frac{q^{6(d-1)l_{2}}|P|^{n}|Q|^{n-1}|P_{0}|^{-2d+1}q^{-4(d-1)\max(0,\lfloor\frac{\varrho-j+1}{2}\rfloor)}}{q^{(l_{2}-\max(0,\lfloor\frac{\varrho-j+1}{2}\rfloor))n/2^{d-2}}}

\displaystyle=q^{\Delta_{j}-l_{2}(n/2^{d-2}-6(d-1))+\max(0,\lfloor\frac{\varrho-j+1}{2}\rfloor)\left(n/2^{d-2}-4(d-1)\right)},

Because

l_{2}\geqslant 1+\left\lfloor\frac{e-\varrho}{d-1}\right\rfloor.

In particular our condition (

Furthermore, assuming

Since there are
O(e)

our work has therefore shown that

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{g}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[T]^{n}\\
0<|\mathbf{g}|<q^{e-j+1}\\
f(\mathbf{g})=0\end{subarray}}\int_{\mathfrak{n}_{j}}S(\beta)\mathrm{d}\beta

\displaystyle\Gamma_{j}=~

\displaystyle-\left(\frac{n}{2^{d-2}}-4(d-1)\right)\max\left(0,\left\lfloor\frac{\varrho-j+1}{2}\right\rfloor\right)

\displaystyle=~

\displaystyle\left(\frac{n}{2^{d-2}}-6(d-1)\right)\left(1+\left\lfloor\frac{e-\varrho}{d-1}\right\rfloor-\max\left(0,\left\lfloor\frac{\varrho-j+1}{2}\right\rfloor\right)\right)

\displaystyle-2(d-1)\max\left(0,\left\lfloor\frac{\varrho-j+1}{2}\right\rfloor\right).

Thus we certainly require (

We summarise our argument in the following result.

Lemma 5.8
.

Let
d\geqslant 3
and
n>3(d-1)2^{d-1}
.
Assume that

and

(5.22)

Then

N_{\varrho}^{\text{minor}}(q,e,f)\ll eq^{\Delta_{0}-\Gamma_{0}}

where

\Delta_{0}=2e(n-d)-\varrho(n-1)+n

and

\Gamma_{0}=\left(\frac{n}{2^{d-2}}-6(d-1)\right)\left(1+\left\lfloor\frac{e-\varrho}{d-1}\right\rfloor-\left\lfloor\frac{\varrho+1}{2}\right\rfloor\right)-2(d-1)\left\lfloor\frac{\varrho+1}{2}\right\rfloor.

Proof.

Recall (
5.1
) and note that

\Delta_{j}=\Delta_{0}-j(n-2d+1)
. Hence for the range of

in which we are interested we deduce from (
4.2
) that

|c_{j}|q^{\Delta_{j}-\Gamma_{j}}\ll q^{\Delta_{0}-\Gamma_{0}},

for all

. Moreover,
\Gamma_{0}
takes the value recorded in the statement of the lemma when

and the condition
(

) on

(

)
and (

)
both hold
for every

∎

5.3.
Deduction of Theorem
1.1

We assume that

n>(2d-1)2^{d-1}
.
We revisit the argument deployed in
[
3
]
to establish the irreducibility
and dimension of
\mathscr{M}_{0,0}(X,e)
.
This is based on a counting argument over a finite field
\mathbb{F}_{q}
whose characteristic is greater than the degree
d
of the non-singular form
f\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
that defines
X
.
According to
[
3
, Eq. (3.3)]
,
in order to deduce that

\mathscr{M}_{0,0}(X,e)
is irreducible and of the expected dimension it suffices to show that

(5.23)

\lim_{q\to\infty}q^{-(n-d)e-n+1}\hat{N}(q,e,f)\leqslant 1,

where
\hat{N}(q,e,f)
is the number of
\mathbf{g}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[T]^{n}
such that

|\mathbf{g}|<q^{e+1}
and
f(\mathbf{g})=0
.

We have

\hat{N}(q,e,f)=\int_{\mathbb{T}}S_{\text{BV}}(\alpha)\mathrm{d}\alpha,

where

S_{\text{BV}}(\alpha)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{g}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[T]^{n}\\
|\mathbf{g}|<q^{e+1}\end{subarray}}\psi(\alpha f(\mathbf{g}))=q^{-n(e-\varrho)}S(\alpha,0),

in the notation of (
5.3
), with
j=0
.
Take
j=0
in the major arcs

\mathfrak{M}(J)

that were
defined in
(
5.4
). A straightforward calculation shows that the contribution from
the major arc around
0
is

\int_{\mathfrak{M}(0)}S_{\text{BV}}(\alpha)\mathrm{d}\alpha=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{g}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[T]^{n}\\
|\mathbf{g}|<q^{e+1}\end{subarray}}\int_{|\theta|<q^{-de}}\psi(\theta f(\mathbf{g}))\mathrm{d}\theta=q^{ne-de}\left(q^{n-1}+O(q^{n/2})\right).

In order to complete the proof of (
5.23
) it therefore suffices to show that

\lim_{q\to\infty}q^{-(n-d)e-n+1}\sum_{J=0}^{M-1}\int_{\mathfrak{M}(J+1)\setminus\mathfrak{M}(J)}|S_{\text{BV}}(\alpha)|\mathrm{d}\alpha<1

where

M=\lceil\frac{de}{2}\rceil

is given by (
5.5
).
To do this we may
apply our previous work.
Thus it follows from
(
5.14
) and
Lemma
5.6

that

S_{\text{BV}}(\alpha)\ll|P|^{n}q^{-nl_{1}/2^{d-1}},

if
\alpha\not\in\mathfrak{M}(J)
and
l_{1}
is any integer such that

l_{1}\leqslant 1+J/(d-1)
and

l_{1}\leqslant e+1
.
The choice
l_{1}=1+\lfloor J/(d-1)\rfloor

is acceptable since
J\leqslant\lceil\frac{de}{2}\rceil-1\leqslant\frac{de-1}{2}
, whence

l_{1}\leqslant 1+\frac{de-1}{2(d-1)}=1+e,

for
d\geqslant 3
. Since
J\geqslant 0
we are clearly only interested in integers
l_{1}\geqslant 1
.
Appealing to (
5.9
) to estimate the volume of
\mathfrak{M}(J+1)
, we deduce that for given

the total
associated
contribution is

\displaystyle\sum_{J=(d-1)(l_{1}-1)}^{(d-1)l_{1}-1}\int_{\mathfrak{M}(J+1)\setminus\mathfrak{M}(J)}|S_{\text{BV}}(\alpha)|\mathrm{d}\alpha

\displaystyle\ll\sum_{J=(d-1)(l_{1}-1)}^{(d-1)l_{1}-1}q^{2J+2-de}.|P|^{n}q^{-nl_{1}/2^{d-1}}

\displaystyle\ll q^{-de+n(e+1)+(2(d-1)-n/2^{d-1})l_{1}}.

This is decreasing with

if

and we may therefore sum over

to finally deduce that

q^{-(n-d)e-n+1}\sum_{J=0}^{M-1}\int_{\mathfrak{M}(J+1)\setminus\mathfrak{M}(J)}|S_{\text{BV}}(\alpha)|\mathrm{d}\alpha\ll q^{1+2(d-1)-n/2^{d-1}}.

The exponent of

is negative if

, which thereby concludes the proof of (

\mathscr{M}_{0,0}(X,e)
is indeed irreducible and of the expected dimension. It follows from the same method used in
[
9

that
\mathscr{M}_{0,0}(X,e)
is locally a complete intersection. Indeed, since
\mathscr{M}_{0,0}(X,e)
is locally the intersection of

equations in

, a smooth stack of dimension

, it is a locally complete intersection if and only if its dimension is
(n-d)e+n-5
.

5.4.
Deduction of Theorem
1.5

Assume that
d\geqslant 3
,
n>3(d-1)2^{d-1}
,
\varrho\geqslant-1
, and
e\geqslant(\varrho+1)\left(2+\frac{1}{d-2}\right)
. In particular, this implies that
e\geqslant\varrho
, which is needed for Lemma
5.1
. In view of Theorem
1.1
, the stated bound is trivial unless
1+\lfloor\frac{e-\varrho}{d-1}\rfloor-\left\lfloor\frac{\varrho+1}{2}\right\rfloor>0
, so we may assume that

\lfloor\frac{e-\varrho}{d-1}\rfloor-\left\lfloor\frac{\varrho+1}{2}\right\rfloor\geqslant 0

and thus
e\geqslant\varrho+(d-1)\left\lfloor\frac{\varrho+1}{2}\right\rfloor
. Hence we may assume that (
5.22
) holds.

Combining
Lemmas
5.1
and

5.8
we deduce that

(5.24)

\#Z_{\varrho}(\mathbb{F}_{q})\ll eq^{e(n-d)+n-5-\min(\mu_{1}(n),\mu_{2}(n))},

with

\displaystyle\mu_{1}(n)=\left(\frac{n}{2^{d-2}}-6(d-1)\right)\left(1+D-\left\lfloor\frac{\varrho+1}{2}\right\rfloor\right)-2(d-1)\left\lfloor\frac{\varrho+1}{2}\right\rfloor

and

\mu_{2}(n)=(1+D)(n-d-1)-de+e+1.

Here we recall that
D
is given by (
4.7
) as
\lfloor\frac{e-\varrho}{d-1}\rfloor
.

We claim that
\mu_{1}(n)\leqslant\mu_{2}(n)
. They are both increasing affine functions of
n
, with
\mu_{1}(n)
of lesser slope than
\mu_{2}(n)
. Hence to check that
\mu_{1}(n)
is the minimum, it suffices to check that
\mu_{2}(n)\geqslant 0
and
\mu_{1}(n)\leqslant 0
when
n=3(d-1)2^{d-1}
. In other words, we must show that

3(d-1)2^{d-1}\geqslant d+1+\frac{e(d-1)-1}{1+D}.

To do this, observe that because
e\geqslant\varrho+(d-1)\left\lfloor\frac{\varrho+1}{2}\right\rfloor
we have
e\geqslant\frac{d+1}{2}\varrho
, so that

1+D\geqslant\frac{e+1-\varrho}{d-1}\geqslant\frac{e+1-\frac{2}{d+1}e}{d-1}\geqslant\frac{e}{d+1}.

Thus

d+1+\frac{e(d-1)-1}{1+D}\leqslant d+1+\frac{e(d-1)}{e/(d+1)}=d(d+1),

so it suffices to check

3(d-1)2^{d-1}\geqslant d(d+1).

But it is clear that
this
holds for all
d\geqslant 3
, whence
\mu_{2}(n)\geqslant\mu_{1}(n)
.

By Lang–Weil
[
14
]
, it now follows from (
5.24
) that

\dim Z_{\varrho}\leqslant e(n-d)+n-5-\mu_{1}(n)

for any smooth hypersurface defined over a finite field. For a general hypersurface, we can spread it out to a family defined over a ring finitely-generated over
\mathbb{Z}
. The dimension of
Z_{\varrho}
in this family is manifestly constant on some open subset of the spectrum of this ring, which must contain a finite-field valued point, so
\dim Z_{\varrho}
is at most
e(n-d)+n-5-\mu_{1}(n)
for the generic point and thus for the original hypersurface. This completes the proof of Theorem
1.5
.

5.5.
Deduction of Theorem
1.2

We consider the effect of taking
\varrho=-1
in Theorem
1.5
.
Clearly (
1.1
) is equivalent to
e\geqslant 0
and can be ignored.
Note that
Z_{-1}
contains the singular locus of
\mathscr{M}_{0,0}(X,e)

by
[
6
, Thm. 2.6]
. Thus the codimension of the singular locus is at least
\dim\mathscr{M}_{0,0}(X,e)-\dim Z_{-1}
. Theorem
1.2
therefore follows from applying Theorem
1.1
to calculate
\dim\mathscr{M}_{0,0}(X,e)
and Theorem
1.5
to bound
\dim Z_{-1}
.

Because the lower bound for the codimension of the singular locus is strictly positive, the moduli space is generically smooth. Any generically smooth locally complete intersection scheme is reduced, which thereby completes the proof of Theorem
1.1
.

6.
Peyre’s freedom counting function

In this section we prove the asymptotic formula in Theorem

),
by piecing together our work above and

[

. We have

(6.1)

N_{X}^{\varepsilon\text{-free}}(B)=N_{X}(B)-E_{\varepsilon}(B),

where
E_{\varepsilon}(B)
counts the number of
x\in X(\mathbb{F}_{q}(T))
with
H_{\omega_{V}^{-1}}(x)\leqslant q^{B}

such that
\ell(x)<\varepsilon
.

Let us begin by studying
N_{X}(B)
. As usual we suppose that
X
is defined by a non-singular form

f\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
of degree
d\geqslant 3
.
It follows from the proof of part (1) of Proposition
3.8
that

N_{X}(B)=\frac{1}{q-1}\#\left\{\mathbf{g}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[T]^{n}:\begin{array}[]{l}\gcd(g_{1},\dots,g_{n})=1\\
|\mathbf{g}|^{n-d}<q^{B+1},~f(\mathbf{g})=0\end{array}\right\}.

Using the Möbius function to detect the coprimality condition we obtain

\displaystyle N_{X}(B)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{q-1}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[T]\\
\text{$k$ monic}\end{subarray}}\mu(k)\#\left\{\mathbf{g}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[T]^{n}:0<|k\mathbf{g}|^{n-d}<q^{B+1},~f(\mathbf{g})=0\right\}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{q-1}\sum_{j\geqslant 0}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[T]\\
|k|=q^{j}\\
\text{$k$ monic}\end{subarray}}\mu(k)\#\left\{\mathbf{g}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[T]^{n}:\begin{array}[]{l}0<|\mathbf{g}|^{n-d}<q^{B+1-j(n-d)}\\
f(\mathbf{g})=0\end{array}\right\}.

Put

m=n-(d-1)2^{d}
and assume that
m>0
.
Then, on
appealing to Lee’s thesis
[
15
, Thm. 4.1.1]
,
it follows that

(6.2)

\begin{split}\#\big\{\mathbf{g}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[T]^{n}:&~0<|\mathbf{g}|^{n-d}<q^{R+1},~f(\mathbf{g})=0\big\}\\
&=q^{R}\left(c_{f}+O(q^{-mR/(2^{d+1}(d-1)(n-d))})\right),\end{split}

for any
R>0
,
where
c_{f}
is the usual product of singular series and singular integral.
Using (
4.1
) to handle the sum over
j
and
k
, it now follows from (
6.2
)
that there exists
\delta>0
such that

\displaystyle N_{X}(B)

\displaystyle=\frac{c_{f}}{(q-1)\zeta_{\mathbb{F}_{q}(T)}(n-d)}q^{B}+O\left(q^{(1-\delta)B}\right),

where

\zeta_{\mathbb{F}_{q}(T)}(s)=(1-q^{1-s})^{-1}
is the rational zeta function.
Arguing along standard lines (as in Peyre
[
17
, §5.4]
, for example), one readily confirms that this agrees with the Batyrev–Manin–Peyre prediction for the hypersurface
X
.

It remains to produce an upper bound for the quantity
E_{\varepsilon}(B)
in (
6.1
).
Let
x\in X(\mathbb{F}_{q}(T))
and suppose that it defines
a map
c:\mathbb{P}^{1}\to X
of degree
e
.
Then it follows from

[
18
, Notation 5.7]
that

\ell(x)=\frac{(n-1)\varrho}{e(n-d)}

if and only if
c
is
\varrho
-free but not
(\varrho+1)
-free.
(In particular, Remark
1.4
implies that
\ell(x)\in[0,1]
.)
We deduce that
E_{\varepsilon}(B)
is at most the number of rational maps from
\mathbb{P}^{1}\to X
with degree at most
B/(n-d)

which are not
\varrho
-free, with

(6.3)

\varrho=\left\lfloor\frac{\varepsilon B}{n-1}\right\rfloor+2.

We may therefore appeal to the proof of Proposition
3.8
(2) to estimate this quantity, finding that

E_{\varepsilon}(B)\leqslant\frac{N_{\varrho}(q,B/(n-d),f)q^{\varrho(n-1)-B}-N(q,B/(n-d),f)}{(q-1)^{2}},

with
\varrho
given by (
6.3
).
In what follows
it will be convenient to set
e=B/(n-d)
and to assume that
e\in\mathbb{N}
.
All of the implied constants that follow are allowed to depend on
q
and
f
, but not on
e
or
\varrho
.
We seek conditions on
n
and
\varrho
under which we can deduce that there exists
\delta>0
such that

E_{\varepsilon}(B)=O(q^{(1-\delta)e(n-d)})
.

First we improve our treatment of Lemma
4.4
slightly by revisiting the
argument (
4.10
). Since we no longer care about a dependence on the finite field, rather than invoking a trivial bound we may apply (
6.2
) to deduce that

N(q,u-\ell,f)\ll q^{(u-\ell)(n-d)}

if
n>(d-1)2^{d}
. But then (
4.10
) can be replaced by the bound

\displaystyle E_{j}

\displaystyle\ll_{f}\sum_{0\leqslant u\leqslant e-j}q^{u(n-2d+1)}\sum_{\ell\geqslant D+1+j-e+u}q^{-\ell(n-2d)}

\displaystyle\ll_{f}q^{(e-j)(n-2d+1)-(D+1)(n-2d)},

where
D
is given by (
4.7
),
whence

\displaystyle q^{(n-1)(e-\varrho)}\sum_{j\geqslant 0}|c_{j}|E_{j}

\displaystyle\ll_{f}q^{2e(n-d)-\varrho(n-1)-(D+1)(n-2d)}

\displaystyle\ll_{f}q^{2e(n-d)-\varrho(n-1)-(e-\varrho)(n-2d)/(d-1)}.

It now follows from (
4.3
) and our modified version of Lemma
4.4
that

E_{\varepsilon}(B)\ll q^{e(n-d)-(e-\varrho)(n-2d)/(d-1)}+q^{-e(n-d)+\varrho(n-1)}N_{\varrho}^{\text{minor}}(q,e,f),

provided that

e\geqslant\varrho
.
Note that
\Gamma_{0}=\gamma_{0}+O_{d,n}(1)
,
with

\gamma_{0}=\left(\frac{n}{2^{d-2}}-6(d-1)\right)\left(\frac{e-\varrho}{d-1}-\frac{\varrho}{2}\right)-(d-1)\varrho.

Appealing now to Lemma
5.8
we therefore deduce that

E_{\varepsilon}(B)\ll q^{e(n-d)-(e-\varrho)(n-2d)/(d-1)}+eq^{e(n-d)-\gamma_{0}}

if (
5.22
) holds.

Recall that
n>3(d-1)2^{d-1}
.
Then
n/2^{d-2}-6(d-1)\geqslant 2^{-d+2}
and we can ensure that

\gamma_{0}\geqslant\delta e
for a small parameter
\delta>0
(that depends only on
d
)
provided that

(6.4)

e\geqslant(d-1)^{2}2^{d-1}\varrho.

This is also enough to ensure
that
(e-\varrho)(n-2d)/(d-1)\geqslant\delta e
.
This inequality is clearly much stronger than (
5.22
).
The statement of Theorem
1.6
now follows on taking
e=B/(n-d)
and noting that the hypothesis on
\varepsilon
in the theorem is enough to ensure that
(
6.4
) holds when
\varrho

is given by
(
6.3
) and
B
is sufficiently large.
