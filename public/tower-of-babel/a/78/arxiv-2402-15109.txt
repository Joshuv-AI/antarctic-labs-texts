Abstract
Machine unlearning (MU) is to forget data from a well-trained model, which is practically important due to the “right to be forgotten”. The unlearned model should approach the retrained model, where the forgetting data are not involved in the training process and hence do not contribute to the retrained model. Considering the forgetting data’s absence during retraining, we think unlearning should withdraw their contribution from the pre-trained model. The challenge is that when tracing the learning process is impractical, how to quantify and detach sample’s contribution to the dynamic learning process using only the pre-trained model. We first theoretically discover that sample’s contribution during the process will reflect in the learned model’s sensitivity to it. We then practically design a novel method, namely MU-Mis (Machine Unlearning by Minimizing input sensitivity), to suppress the contribution of the forgetting data. Experimental results demonstrate that MU-Mis can unlearn effectively and efficiently without utilizing the remaining data. It is the first time that a remaining-data-free method can outperform state-of-the-art (SoTA) unlearning methods that utilize the remaining data.



I
Introduction

Memorization of training data is revealed to be a recipe for the good generalization of deep neural networks (DNNs)
[
1
,
2
,
3
]
. However, the stored information of data can be reproduced by privacy attacks
[
4
,
5
]
, raising data privacy concerns. To address this issue, the “right to be forgotten” (RTBF
[
6
]
) is introduced to safeguard user privacy. This entails removing the data not only from the training dataset but also from the trained DNN, ensuring that the DNN performs as if the data were never involved in the training.

Ideally, one can retrain the model without the data that need to be removed. However, this is practically unfeasible because training DNNs is very expensive in terms of both time and energy. Consequently, a new and important task called “Machine Unlearning” (MU)
[
7
]
, has emerged. The goal of MU is to fine-tune the
pre-trained model
to approach the
retrained model
as closely as possible, where the forgetting data were not involved in the training. The fundamental difference when data are involved in the training or not lies in their contribution to the learning process: as training data, they contribute to the learning, while as test data, their predictions benefit from the training data’s contribution. This implies that the roles played by the forgetting data in the pre-trained model and the retrained model vary from “contributor” to “bystander”. In light of this transition, unlearning should withdraw the forgetting data’s contribution to the learning process.

Learning is a dynamic process that gradually remembers and assimilates data, while unlearning, which is the reverse process that gradually removes data information, requires backtracking the training trajectory
[
8
]
. Nevertheless, such tracking contradicts the efficiency demands of unlearning, making it preferable to approximate sample contribution with only the pre-trained model. Unfortunately, quantifying sample’s contribution with only the pre-trained model is quite challenging, as the learned model parameters are co-contributed by all the training data.

Consequently, existing MU methods turn to unlearning by confusing the model’s knowledge of the forgetting data, e.g. random-relabeling
[
9
,
8
,
10
]
or knowledge-distillation from useless teacher
[
11
,
12
]
. Such confusion brings about
catastrophe unlearning

[
13
]
, i.e. severe damage to the model utility on the remaining data, necessitating extra maintenance with the remaining data. However, the remaining data might not be available in practice and such additional maintenance seriously slows down MU efficiency in large-scale dataset. Therefore, reducing the reliance on the remaining data is greatly desired. This appeals to reasonable unlearning operations grounded in the machine learning process, rather than introducing incorrect information.

Instead of approximating the historical contributed gradient update during training, we identify the clue of contribution by considering another view: derivative of the training process w.r.t a training sample. Abstractly, once the learning algorithm
\cal A
is determined, encompassing the structure, scheme, and parameters, the learned model
f
becomes a function of the training data. In other words, the learning process is a mapping from the training set
\mathcal{D}=\{(\boldsymbol{x}_{i},\boldsymbol{y}_{i})\}
to a function
f
: denoted as
f={\cal A}(\mathcal{D})
. Therefore, the training sample
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}
contributes to the output:
\partial{\cal A}/\partial\boldsymbol{x_{i}}\neq 0
while a sample out of the training set does not. A simple yet enlightening example lies in the support vector machine
[
14
,
15
]
, where only the training data can act as support vectors that impact the decision boundary. Thus, withdrawing the sample contribution can be achieved by suppressing
\partial{\cal A}/\partial\boldsymbol{x}_{i}
.

When designing a MU algorithm, we encounter a significant challenge:
\cal A
is determined by a dynamic training process that does not have an explicit expression. To overcome this, we theoretically reveal that
\partial{\cal A}/\partial{\boldsymbol{x}_{i}}
could be approximated by the learned model’s sensitivity to its input, i.e.
\partial{f(\boldsymbol{x})}/\partial\boldsymbol{x}
with
f=\cal A(\mathcal{D})
in Section
III-B
. For proper and delicate machine unlearning, we need to find a guideline that reflects the property of the retrained model meanwhile easy for optimization. Thus we delve deeper into the input sensitivity of different logits. Through empirical investigations under the machine learning (Section
III-C
) and machine unlearning (Section
III-D
) scenarios, we reveal that sample’s contribution to the learning process leads to an excess of the target class logit relative to the irrelevant classes in the input sensitivity. Motivated by these findings, we introduce a novel MU method called MU-Mis (Machine Unlearning by Minimizing Input Sensitivity), which suppresses sample contribution by reducing the magnitude gap in sensitivity norm between the target class and irrelevant class logits.

We conduct comprehensive experiments to evaluate the effectiveness and efficiency of our proposed MU-Mis, including 3 standard unlearning tasks across 6 datasets, in comparison with 11 competitive unlearning methods. We also compare with 4 existing remaining-data-free methods, underscoring our substantial advancements in disregarding the remaining data. The results demonstrate that MU-Mis can unlearn effectively meanwhile well preserve model utility on the remaining data
without utilizing the remaining data
. Beyond unlearning utility, we further assess the resilience of unlearning methods through full class and sub-class sequential unlearning. In sequential unlearning, MU-Mis exhibits commendable stability and effectiveness, whereas SoTA MU methods are exposed to have three distinct issues after multiple executions. This further highlights the reliability of MU-Mis for practical applications. Our contributions can be summarized as follows:

•

We point out that machine unlearning should withdraw the forgetting data’s contribution. To avoid tracing the training process, we theoretically illustrate that sample’s contribution will manifest in the learned model’s sensitivity to them.

•

We reveal that the sample’s contribution amplifies the sensitivity magnitude gap between the target class logit and the irrelevant classes’ through empirical investigations under the machine learning and unlearning scenarios.

•

Based on the above analysis and findings, we propose a novel unlearning method, called MU-Mis, to suppress the sample’s contribution by minimizing the sensitivity magnitude gap for the forgetting data.

•

Evaluations across 6 datasets and comparisons with 11 SoTA unlearning methods that utilize the remaining data demonstrate that MU-Mis can unlearn effectively and efficiently without the remaining data. Comparisons with 4 existing remaining-data-free methods highlight the significant advancement of MU-Mis in disregarding the remaining data. Noteworthily, it is the first time that a remaining-data-free method can outperform SoTA remaining-data-dependent methods.

II
Related Work

The primary goal of machine unlearning (MU)
[
16
,
17
,
18
]
is to remove the influence of specific data points on a pre-trained model, protecting data privacy. MU can be categorized into two types
[
16
]
: exact MU and approximate MU. Exact MU approaches parameters of the retrained model and guarantees the privacy risk statistically, extensively studied within convex settings and linear models
[
19
,
20
,
21
,
22
,
23
]
. However, exact MU for over-parameterized deep models requires retraining the model from scratch, which is impractical due to significant time and computation overhead. Therefore, approximate MU is proposed to balance the unlearning completeness and efficiency. Approximate MU approaches the output distribution of the retrained model, reducing privacy risks exposed by threats like membership inference attacks (MIA)
[
24
,
25
,
26
]
and model inversion attacks (MIA)
[
5
,
27
]
. In this paper, we concentrate on approximate unlearning, as it is more practical in large-scale models and situations with limited time and resources.

II-A

MU by Gradient-Based Update

One straightforward way to retrieve sample contribution is to keep and utilize the historical information (e.g. parameters and gradients) during the training process. Graves et al.
[
8
]
withdraw gradient updates of related batches, and Wu et al.
[
28
]
utilize intermediate checkpoints and quasi-newton method for rapid retraining. However, since machine learning is inherently a progressive process, the naive withdrawal of past updates will inevitably affect model’s performance on the remaining data. Also, the requirement of storing historical information also raises memory concerns. Another line of work estimates contribution of the forgetting data on learned model parameters instead of explicitly recording it. One commonly used estimation is the influence function
[
23
]
, initially introduced to unlearning by Guo et al.
[
19
]
. However, calculating the inverse Hessian in influence function is computationally expensive for DNNs and follow-up studies are devoted to reducing the computation
[
29
,
30
,
31
]
. While influence-based unlearning shows potential, the withdrawal still hurts performance on the remaining data. Wu et al.
[
32
]
attempted to mitigate this damage by re-weighting the remaining data in the loss. Unfortunately, the influence function is recently revealed to be fragile in DNNs
[
33
,
34
,
35
]
due to its reliance on the assumptions of convexity and optimality. Existing data influence estimations for DNNs
[
36
,
37
,
38
,
35
]
all require retracing the training trajectory and cannot be optimized and applied to MU. In this paper, we theoretically indicate that sample contributions will be approximately reflected in the sensitivity of the pre-trained model to input samples, opening up a new perspective to view sample contribution in DNNs.

II-B

MU by Loss Guided Re-optimization

Above gradient-based unlearning methods suffer from practical limitations for DNNs. Generally, practical MU methods unlearn by fine-tuning the model to optimize a proposed loss. They typically follow two design ideas: one is to make model’s behavior on the forgetting data similar to that on unseen data through knowledge distillation
[
11
,
39
,
12
]
or label confusion
[
8
,
10
]
, the other is to suppress the part of parameters that are responsible for predictions of the forgetting data
[
40
,
41
,
10
]
. However, both the confusion and suppression approaches inadvertently damage model’s utility on the remaining data. As a result, unlearning is done either in an “impair-then-repair” regime
[
42
]
or through specifically designed mechanisms
[
43
,
44
,
10
]
to alleviate the damage. These empirical insights failed to model and capture the actual characteristics of the retrained model, leading to substandard guidelines for approximate MU. We tackle this challenge by investigating the input sensitivity of the forgetting data in the pre-trained and retrained model, establishing a connection among machine learning, machine unlearning and input sensitivity. This enables us to guide MU process convincingly, effectively and efficiently and even eliminate the need for additional maintenance on model utility.

II-C

Remaining-data-free MU Methods

In practice, the remaining data might not be available for MU due to data ownership and privacy. Also, the efficiency demand of MU necessitates reducing dependency on the remaining data. Therefore, remaining-data-free MU method is more in line with the essence and practical application of MU. Existing SoTA MU methods are unable to preserve model utility in the absence of remaining data, as their forgetting operations unavoidably hurt model utility. We notice that recently, two concurrent works also delved into solving this issue. JiT
[
41
]
proposes to smooth the output around the forgetting data by minimizing local Lipschitz value. While SCAR
[
45
]
distills knowledge from the pre-trained model and utilize Out-of-distribution (OOD) data as a surrogate to preserve model utility. However, both approaches have an obvious performance gap to SoTA MU methods, and SCAR still relies on additional OOD data. In contrast, our proposed method does not require any extra data meanwhile outperforming them significantly.

III
Machine Learning, Machine Unlearning and Input Sensitivity

III-A

Problem Formulation

Machine Learning (ML)
is to learn a mapping from the input space
\mathcal{X}
to the output space
\mathcal{Y}
, denoted as the function
f(\cdot):\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathcal{Y}
. The output of
f
is C-dimension in a C-categories classification problem. Learning is performed by a
training algorithm

\mathcal{A}
, which generally takes in a training dataset
\mathcal{D}
and returns the learned function
f
. In this case, the outcome of
\mathcal{A}
varies with different training datasets. To investigate sample-wise influence on the learning process, in this paper, we consider
\mathcal{A}
in a broader sense. We distinguish different training processes by the
training dataset

\mathcal{D}=\{(\boldsymbol{x}_{i},\boldsymbol{y}_{i})\}_{i=1}^{m}
. That is to say, we have a family of the training algorithm
\mathcal{A}_{\mathcal{D}}
. Each
\mathcal{A}_{\mathcal{D}}
is a multivariate function that takes all the samples
\{\boldsymbol{x}_{j}\in\mathcal{X}\}
as input, regardless of whether they are in the training dataset
\mathcal{D}
. In this case, the output of
\mathcal{A}_{\mathcal{D}}
does not vary with each input variable, but only varies with the change of the training data
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}\in\mathcal{D}
, thereby making no response to the change of samples out of the training set.

Machine Unlearning (MU)
is to forget a subset of training data
\mathcal{D}_{f}\subset\mathcal{D}
, which is called the
forgetting data
, from a
pre-trained model

w_{p}
. The rest of training data
\mathcal{D}_{r}=\mathcal{D}\backslash{\mathcal{D}}_{f}
is called the
remaining data
. In practice, the learned function
f
is parameterized by parameters
w\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and learning is completed by performing on
w
. To instantiate the learned function, we have
\mathcal{A}_{\mathcal{D}}\mapsto f(\boldsymbol{x};w)
, where
\boldsymbol{x}
is input variable of function
f
. Machine unlearning mechanism
\mathbf{U}
responses at forgetting request and returns a
sanitized model

w_{u}=\mathbf{U}(\mathcal{D}_{r},\mathcal{D}_{f},w_{p})
. A good approximate unlearning mechanism should efficiently and effectively transform
w_{p}
into a sanitized model
w_{u}
, such that the difference in output distribution between
w_{u}
and the
retrained model

w_{r}
remains minimal. To facilitate the understanding of the objectives in our analysis, we only bold the input variables of training algorithm
\mathcal{A}_{\mathcal{D}}
and learned function
f(\boldsymbol{x})
, which are respectively
\boldsymbol{x_{i}}
and
\boldsymbol{x}
in the following analysis.

III-B

Theoretical analysis connecting sample contribution and input sensitivity

As previously discussed, machine unlearning is to withdraw sample’s contribution to the learning process, and an efficient unlearning method should explore the contribution directly from the pre-trained model. To detach per-sample contribution with the pre-trained model, we propose to identify the clue of contribution from the derivative of training mapping
\mathcal{A}_{\mathcal{D}}
to training sample
\boldsymbol{x_{i}}
, i.e.
\partial{\mathcal{A}}_{\mathcal{D}}/\partial\boldsymbol{x_{i}}
. Recall that
{\mathcal{A}}_{\mathcal{D}}
is determined by the training dataset
\mathcal{D}=\{(\boldsymbol{x_{i}},\boldsymbol{y}_{i})\}_{i=1}^{m}
and outputs the learned function
f(\boldsymbol{x};w_{p})
. Then
\partial{\mathcal{A}}_{\mathcal{D}}/\partial\boldsymbol{x_{i}}
is to compute
\partial f(\boldsymbol{x};w_{p})/\partial\boldsymbol{x_{i}}
. However, there is no explicit expression for this derivative. Therefore, in this part, we reflect on the learning dynamics to seek a surrogate with the pre-trained model.

After
T
iterations training updates in the parameter space, we have pre-trained model parameter
w_{p}=w_{0}+\sum_{k=1}^{T}\Delta w_{k}
, where
w_{0}
is randomly initialized model parameters and
\Delta w_{k}=w_{k+1}-w_{k}
is
k^{\text{th}}
parameter update. Specifically, when training loss
\mathcal{L}
and gradient descent with step size
\eta
are used, we have

\displaystyle\Delta w_{k}

\displaystyle=-\eta\sum_{i=1}^{m}\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{x_{i}})}{\partial w}\big|_{w=w_{k}}

\displaystyle=-\eta\sum_{i=1}^{m}\frac{\partial f(\boldsymbol{x_{i}};w)}{\partial w}\big|_{w=w_{k}}\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{x_{i}})}{\partial f}.

(1)

Viewing machine learning from the function space with first-order Taylor expansion on parameters, correspondingly we have
f=f_{0}+\sum_{k=1}^{T}\Delta f_{k}
, where
f_{0}=f(\boldsymbol{x},w_{0})
is initial function and
\Delta f_{k}
is induced by parameter update
\Delta w_{k}
. The evolution in function induced by parameter update is:

\displaystyle\Delta f_{k}(\boldsymbol{x};w)

\displaystyle\approx\frac{\partial f(\boldsymbol{x};w)}{\partial w}\big|_{w=w_{k}}^{\top}\Delta w_{k}

\displaystyle=-\eta\sum_{i=1}^{m}\frac{\partial f(\boldsymbol{x};w)}{\partial w}\big|_{w=w_{k}}^{\top}\frac{\partial f(\boldsymbol{x_{i}};w)}{\partial w}\big|_{w=w_{k}}\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{x_{i}})}{\partial f}.

(2)

To make it more clear, we make some simplifications: (a) Note that
\frac{\partial f(\cdot;w)}{\partial w}\big|_{w=w_{k}}
is the mapping from model input
\boldsymbol{x}
to the induced backpropagation gradient with parameters
w_{k}
. We abbreviate this mapping as
g_{k}(\boldsymbol{x}):\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d\times C}
and its derivative to input
\boldsymbol{x}
as
g_{k}^{\prime}
, where
d
is total number of model parameters. (b) In classification problem with cross-entropy loss as
\mathcal{L}
, we have
\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{x_{i}})}{\partial f}=e_{c}-p(\boldsymbol{x_{i}})
, where
e_{c}
is a one-hot vector with only
c^{\text{th}}
element equals to 1, and
p
is the probability vector of
\boldsymbol{x_{i}}
. The final learned function
f
is

\displaystyle f(\boldsymbol{x};w_{p})

\displaystyle=f(\boldsymbol{x};w_{0})+\sum_{k=1}^{T}\Delta f_{k}(\boldsymbol{x},w)

\displaystyle=f(\boldsymbol{x};w_{0})-\eta\sum_{k=1}^{T}\underbrace{\vphantom{g^{\top}_{k}(\boldsymbol{x_{i}})(e_{c}-p(\boldsymbol{x_{i}}))}g^{\top}_{k}(\boldsymbol{x})}_{(1)}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\underbrace{g_{k}(\boldsymbol{x_{i}})(e_{c}-p(\boldsymbol{x_{i}}))}_{(2)}.

(3)

Notice that term
(1)
is related to the
forward inference process
while term
(2)
is related to the
machine learning process
. Derivative of
f
w.r.t
\boldsymbol{x_{i}}
indicates how the learned function
f
varies when the training sample
\boldsymbol{x_{i}}
varies, while derivative w.r.t
\boldsymbol{x}
indicates how the prediction of
f
varies with its input
\boldsymbol{x}
at inference time. The former implies the training sample’s influence on learning, and the latter is the learned model’s sensitivity to its input. Next, we take the derivative of
f
w.r.t
\boldsymbol{x_{i}}
and
\boldsymbol{x}
respectively to view their relationship. Note that
p
is a probability vector determined by
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}
. Due to softmax activation, we consider
p(\boldsymbol{x_{i}})
hardly changes around
\boldsymbol{x_{i}}
, and omit its derivative term w.r.t
\boldsymbol{x_{i}}
. The difference in mapping
g_{k}
when
\boldsymbol{x_{i}}
changes is also omitted.

\hskip-9.24994pt\left\{\begin{aligned} \frac{\partial f(\boldsymbol{x};w_{p})}{\partial\boldsymbol{x_{i}}}&=-\eta\sum_{k=1}^{T}\underbrace{g^{\prime}_{k}(\boldsymbol{x_{i}})g_{k}(\boldsymbol{x})(e_{c}-p(\boldsymbol{x_{i}}))}_{=:\mathcal{C}_{k}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x_{i}})},\\
\frac{\partial f(\boldsymbol{x};w_{p})}{\partial\boldsymbol{x}}&=\frac{\partial f(\boldsymbol{x};w_{0})}{\partial\boldsymbol{x}}-\eta\sum_{k=1}^{T}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\underbrace{g^{\prime}_{k}(\boldsymbol{x})g_{k}(\boldsymbol{x_{i}})(e_{c}-p(\boldsymbol{x_{i}}))}_{=:\mathcal{S}_{k}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x_{i}})}.\end{aligned}\right.

(4)

\mathcal{C}_{k}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x_{i}})
determines the prediction change on
\boldsymbol{x}
when
\boldsymbol{x_{i}}
changes, and
\mathcal{S}_{k}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x_{i}})
stands for the part of model’s sensitivity to
\boldsymbol{x}
contributed by training sample
\boldsymbol{x_{i}}
. Note that
\frac{\partial f(\boldsymbol{x_{i}};w_{p})}{\partial\boldsymbol{x_{i}}}
is similar to the definition of memorization, which is framed as
self-influence

[
1
,
2
]
. To be more specific, memorization of a sample is defined as the prediction difference in itself when training with or without it. Similarly, the self-influence here is the prediction difference on
\boldsymbol{x_{i}}
when it slightly changes, i.e.
\frac{\partial f(\boldsymbol{x_{i}};w_{p})}{\partial\boldsymbol{x_{i}}}
. Thus we consider
\frac{\partial f(\boldsymbol{x_{i}};w_{p})}{\partial\boldsymbol{x_{i}}}
as the reflection of sample
\boldsymbol{x_{i}}
’s contribution. From the formulation, we have
\mathcal{S}_{k}(\boldsymbol{x_{i}},\boldsymbol{x_{i}})=\mathcal{C}_{k}(\boldsymbol{x_{i}},\boldsymbol{x_{i}})
. For a specific training sample
\hat{x}\in\mathcal{D}
, the learned model’s sensitivity to it can be further decomposed as

\displaystyle\frac{\partial f(\boldsymbol{x};w_{p})}{\partial\boldsymbol{x}}\big|_{\boldsymbol{x}=\hat{x}}=\frac{\partial f(\boldsymbol{x};w_{0})}{\partial\boldsymbol{x}}\big|_{\boldsymbol{x}=\hat{x}}-\eta\sum_{k=1}^{T}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mathcal{S}_{k}(\hat{x},\boldsymbol{x_{i}})

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial f(\boldsymbol{x},w_{0})}{\partial\boldsymbol{x}}\big|_{\boldsymbol{x}=\hat{x}}-\eta\sum_{k=1}^{T}\left[\mathcal{S}_{k}(\hat{x},\hat{x})+\sum_{\tilde{x}\in{\mathcal{D}/\hat{x}}}\mathcal{S}_{k}(\hat{x},\tilde{x})\right]

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\vphantom{\frac{\partial f(\boldsymbol{x};w_{0})}{\partial\boldsymbol{x}}\big|_{\boldsymbol{x}=\hat{x}}-\eta\sum_{k=1}^{T}\sum_{\tilde{x}\in{\mathcal{D}/\hat{x}}}\mathcal{S}_{k}(\hat{x},\tilde{x})}-\eta\sum_{k=1}^{T}\mathcal{S}_{k}(\hat{x},\hat{x})}_{\text{Contribution Term}}+\underbrace{\frac{\partial f(\boldsymbol{x},w_{0})}{\partial\boldsymbol{x}}\big|_{x=\hat{x}}-\eta\sum_{k=1}^{T}\sum_{\tilde{x}\in{\mathcal{D}/\hat{x}}}\mathcal{S}_{k}(\hat{x},\tilde{x})}_{\text{Residual Term}}.

(5)

The randomly initialized function
f_{0}
is generally quite insensitive to input change. Thus, the first term of the above residual term is very small. The second term is related to the correlation between the gradient on
\tilde{x}
and the sensitivity of the gradient on
\hat{x}
. We use a simple MLP model to illustrate the insight of
\mathcal{S}_{k}(\hat{x},\tilde{x})<<\mathcal{S}_{k}(\hat{x},\hat{x})
with
\hat{x}\neq\tilde{x}
. Assume the
l^{\text{t}h}
layer output of model is
x^{l}=\phi(\theta^{l}x^{l-1})
, where
\theta^{l}
refers to
l^{\text{t}h}
layer parameter and
\phi
refers to activation function. Then,

\displaystyle g_{k}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{\partial f_{k}}{\partial\theta^{l}}=\dfrac{\partial f_{k}}{\partial(\theta^{l}x^{l-1})}x^{{l-1}^{T}},

(6)

\displaystyle g^{\prime}_{k}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{\partial f_{k}}{\partial\theta^{l}\partial x}=\dfrac{\partial f_{k}}{\partial(\theta^{l}x^{l-1})}\dfrac{\partial x^{{l-1}^{T}}}{\partial x}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{\partial f_{k}}{\partial(\theta^{l}x^{l-1})}\phi^{\prime}(\theta^{l-1}x^{l-2})\theta^{l^{T}}\dfrac{\partial{x^{l-2}}}{\partial x}.

(7)

Thereby, the inner-dot
g^{\prime}_{k}(\hat{x})g_{k}(\tilde{x})\propto\tilde{x}^{l-1}\phi^{\prime}(\theta^{l-1}\hat{x}^{l-2})
. If ReLU activation is used, where
\phi^{\prime}(x)=1
if
x>0
else
\phi^{\prime}(x)=0
,
\mathcal{S}_{k}(\hat{x},\tilde{x})\propto g^{\prime}_{k}(\hat{x})g_{k}(\tilde{x})
will be quite small. The conclusion here is that the residual term is relatively smaller than the contribution term. Therefore, the contribution of a training sample to the training process would be approximately reflected in the pre-trained model’s output sensitivity to the sample.

This could also be verified numerically: we validate the contribution to learning by comparing
\|\nabla_{\boldsymbol{x}}f\|_{F}
of the training data before and after training in Figure
1
. For a randomly initialized model, there is little response to input changes, only about
10^{-4}
. After training, there is a significant order of magnitude growth to
10^{3}
, indicating an increased attention of the trained model to training data’s variations. This observation means that during the learning process, the training data contribute to model performance, and such efforts include promoting the model’s sensitivity to them. Thereby, we use
\partial f(\boldsymbol{x};w_{p})/\partial\boldsymbol{x}
as a surrogate for sample contribution
\partial{\cal A}/\partial\boldsymbol{x_{i}}
.

. Right: In well-trained model

. After training, the model exhibits significantly increased sensitivity to the training data. Such an increase reflects the training data’s contribution during training.

III-C

Input sensitivity of the target and irrelevant class logit

Above analysis illustrates that the input sensitivity of model output logits reflects sample’s contribution to the learned model. For more delicate control on such contribution, we have a closer look at different logits of
f(\boldsymbol{x})\in\mathbb{R}^{C}
for further understanding. During the learning process, the semantic meaning is fed via approaching the sample label. Consequently, contribution of training samples to different logits vary. It could be expected that the input sensitivity of the target class logit is more pronounced compared to that of irrelevant classes.

Let
f_{c}
denotes the logit output of the target class and
f_{c^{\prime}}
denotes the logit output of irrelevant classes. Figure
2
shows the distribution of
\|\nabla_{\boldsymbol{x}}f_{c}\|_{F}
and
\frac{1}{C-1}\sum_{c^{\prime}\neq c}\nolimits\|\nabla_{\boldsymbol{x}}f_{c^{\prime}}\|_{F}
(denoted as
\|\nabla_{\boldsymbol{x}}f_{c^{\prime}}\|_{F}
for brevity in the following) of training data before and after training. As expected, these two quantities are of comparative magnitude in the randomly initialized model, but
\|\nabla_{\boldsymbol{x}}f_{c}\|_{F}
becomes much larger than
\|\nabla_{\boldsymbol{x}}f_{c^{\prime}}\|_{F}
after training. This observation implies that the model amplifies
\|\nabla_{\boldsymbol{x}}f_{c}\|_{F}
to surpass
\|\nabla_{\boldsymbol{x}}f_{c^{\prime}}\|_{F}
during training, thereby generating a discernible difference in whether a sample has been learned to be correctly classified. Another perspective that supports our findings is the generative view of classification models.
The softmax-based discriminative classifier is revealed to be implicitly a density model which learns data distribution
[
46
,
47
]
. From this viewpoint, the logits
f(\boldsymbol{x})
of standard classifiers are un-normalized log-densities, and corresponding input-gradients
\nabla_{\boldsymbol{x}}f_{i}(\boldsymbol{x})
are log-gradients of a class-conditional density model. In other words, we have
\nabla_{\boldsymbol{x}}\log p_{\theta}(\boldsymbol{x}|y=i)=\nabla_{\boldsymbol{x}}f_{i}(\boldsymbol{x})
in the classification model, providing a rationale for the observed discrepancy.

III-D

Input sensitivity of samples present and absent in training

Above analysis implies that the input sensitivity perspective offers us a descent way to capture sample contribution during the machine learning process. To design an unlearning method, we should find an optimization objective that could guide the unlearning process towards the retrained model. Therefore, in the following part, we examine the forgetting data’s contribution by empirically comparing the difference in their input sensitivity under MU scenarios (introduced in Section
V-A
).

For each forgetting sample, we compute the difference
\Delta
between the retrained and the pre-trained model’s sensitivity to it, where the sensitivity including
\|\nabla_{\boldsymbol{x}}f_{c}\|_{F},\|\nabla_{\boldsymbol{x}}f_{c^{\prime}}\|_{F}
and
\|\nabla_{\boldsymbol{x}}f_{c}\|_{F}-\|\nabla_{\boldsymbol{x}}f_{c^{\prime}}\|_{F}
. Aiming for a light-weight unlearning algorithm, we prefer an optimization direction rather than modeling a distribution or specifying a target value for each sample. Hence, we focus on the sign of
\Delta
rather than the exact magnitude. Thus we count the ratio of rise and fall of
\Delta
, examining the overall trend. The experiments are conducted on three unlearning tasks and the results are presented in Figure
3
. For full class and random subset unlearning, the result is averaged over 3 different classes (subsets). For sub-class unlearning, we investigate three cases where the retrained model exhibits varying degrees of generalization ability on the unlearned sub-class.

From left to right in Figure
3
,
\Delta
is the sample-wise difference between the retrained and pre-trained model on
\|\nabla_{\boldsymbol{x}}f_{c}\|_{F},\|\nabla_{\boldsymbol{x}}f_{c^{\prime}}\|_{F}
and

\|\nabla_{\boldsymbol{x}}f_{c}\|_{F}-\|\nabla_{\boldsymbol{x}}f_{c^{\prime}}\|_{F}
. For each quantity, there is a consistent trend across different unlearning settings. Generally,
f_{c}
of the retrained model exhibits lower sensitivity and
f_{c^{\prime}}
exhibits higher sensitivity to the forgetting data than the pre-trained model. Specifically, in cases where the retrained model demonstrates good generalization on the forgetting data, e.g. subclass-CIFAR-20-sea, the trend of change in
\|\nabla_{\boldsymbol{x}}f_{c}\|_{F}
across the forgetting data is less pronounced, with a ratio around 50%. While the trend in
\|\nabla_{\boldsymbol{x}}f_{c^{\prime}}\|_{F}
is significant with a ratio around 80%. Therefore, we suspect the promotion of
\|\nabla_{\boldsymbol{x}}f_{c}\|_{F}
is mainly contributed by the shared information within the class, while the suppression of
\|\nabla_{\boldsymbol{x}}f_{c^{\prime}}\|_{F}
is mainly driven by the private information unique to each sample. In general, sample contribution to learning involves amplifying the sensitivity of the target-class logit to itself while simultaneously suppressing the sensitivity of irrelevant-class logits, thereby enlarging the relative sensitivity magnitude gap between different class logits.

IV
Proposed Method

IV-A

MU-Mis: Machine Unlearning by Minimizing Input Sensitivity

Sample’s contribution to learning includes promoting the sensitivity of the target-class logit and suppressing the sensitivity of irrelevant-class logits, thereby enlarging the magnitude gap between them. The magnitude gap encompasses both class-level and sample-level behaviors, aligning with the unlearning objective of erasing both public and private information from the pre-trained model. Therefore, we propose to withdraw the sample’s contribution by reducing such enhancement on the sensitivity magnitude gap. Minimizing this loss guides the pre-trained model to roll back
\|\nabla_{x}f_{c}\|_{F}
and pick up
\|\nabla_{x}f_{c^{\prime}}\|_{F}
. Mathematically, our proposed unlearning loss is:

\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{D}_{f};w)=\dfrac{1}{N_{f}}\sum_{x\in\mathcal{D}_{f}}(\|\nabla_{\boldsymbol{x}}f_{c}(x,w)\|^{2}_{F}-\|\nabla_{\boldsymbol{x}}f_{c^{\prime}}(x,w)\|^{2}_{F})\\

(8)

where
N_{f}
is number of the forgetting data,
c
represents the target class of sample
x
and
c^{\prime}\neq c
denotes an irrelevant class. For each forgetting sample, a new
c^{\prime}
is randomly selected every time the loss is computed.

Stopping Guideline.
The optimization should cease once the withdrawl is completed, requiring a stopping guideline for practical use. We monitor both the optimization objective and unlearning metrics during minimizing the MU-Mis loss Eq.(
8
) in Figure
4
, using the example of unlearning with ResNet-18 on fullclass-CIFAR100-rocket. In Figure
4
, different colors represent different learning rates and the purple dashed line represents the accuracy of the retrained model. A consistent trend on accuracy change during unlearning is observed across different learning rates: as the optimization of MU-Mis loss progresses, the accuracy of the forgetting data (FA) gradually decreases, the accuracy of the remaining (RA) and test data (TA) first decrease slightly and then grow up with the recovery of
\|\nabla_{x}f_{c^{\prime}}(x,w_{p})\|_{F}
. Notably, when
\|\nabla_{x}f_{c^{\prime}}(x,w)\|_{F}
recovers close to the level of its initial value
\|\nabla_{x}f_{c^{\prime}}(x,w_{p})\|_{F}
, RA approaches the retrained model across different learning rates. There exists a clear relationship between the unlearning progress and model performance, allowing for effective unlearning by stopping the optimization timely. To this end, we introduce a stopping threshold ratio
\delta
to regulate the time of stopping. We record the minimal value of irrelevant class logit sensitivity as
\epsilon
and terminate unlearning process when
\|\nabla_{x}f_{c^{\prime}}(\mathcal{D}_{f},w)\|_{F}>\epsilon
and
\frac{\|\nabla_{x}f_{c^{\prime}}(\mathcal{D}_{f},w)\|_{F}}{\|\nabla_{x}f_{c^{\prime}}(\mathcal{D}_{f},w_{p})\|_{F}}>\delta
. The overall algorithm is outlined in Algorithm
1
.

IV-B

Dicussion on input sensitivity view for machine unlearning

For machine unlearning task, the researchers mainly face three challenges: the stochasticity of training, incrementality of training, and catastrophe of unlearning
[
13
]
. In this paper, we illustrate that the input sensitivity perspective can capture sample contribution during training process. Through extensive experiments, we show that unlearning through the input sensitivity view has rare damage to model utility, eliminating the need for additional maintenance with the remaining data. Beyond these advantages, we raise the attention that the input sensitivity perspective possesses a profound connection to MU. MU emerges from model’s memorization of the training data and seeks to safeguard the privacy of training data. Recent studies by Garg et al.
[
48
]
and Ravikumar et al.
[
49
]
reveal the intrinsic relationship between memorization, privacy and sample’s input curvature. Also, Mo et al.
[
50
]
demonstrates that the input sensitivity of model gradient is the underlying cause of information leakage exposed by Model Inversion Attack (MIA)
[
5
,
27
]
. Collectively, these works further underscore the inherent advantages of adopting an input sensitivity perspective for machine unlearning.

Evaluation Metrics.
MU methods should be assessed from three aspects:
utility
,
privacy
, and
efficiency
[
59
]
. Beyond that, in practice, where the unlearning requests are made constantly, the unlearning
resilience
should be assessed, i.e. subsequent unlearning should not spoil previous unlearning efforts. For
utility

FA
), remaining data accuracy (
RA
), and test data accuracy (
TA
) of the unlearned model. FA and RA are computed on the valid set in class-wise unlearning and on the train set in random subset unlearning. We compute the average gap (
Avg. Gap
) between the retrained model and the unlearned model on accuracy-related metrics, including FA, RA and TA to illustrate the utility disparity. In terms of
resilience
, we evaluate on sequential unlearning tasks. We compute the train (
FGTA
) and valid (
FGVA
) accuracy on the forgotten classes and quantify the unlearning resilience with the average of their disparity to the retrained model (
Resilience Avg. Gap
). To further examine the indistinguishability between the retrained and unlearned model, we compute the
KL divergence
between their output distributions over the entire dataset. The retrained model is an oracle of approximate MU, therefore, above disparity metrics should be as small as possible. Regarding the
privacy
guarantee, we use membership inference attack (
MIA
)
[
11
]
to probe the remaining information of the forgetting data. The MIA success rate indicates how many samples in
\mathcal{D}_{f}
are predicted as membership samples of the unlearned model. From a privacy perspective, a lower MIA value implies less information leakage in the unlearned model and is preferred
[
40
]
. For
efficiency

RTE
) in
seconds
to indicate timeliness.

Comparison with Other Remaining-data-free Unlearning Methods.
In practice, the remaining data may not be available due to data ownership or privacy. Existing methods utilize the remaining data because their forgetting operations unavoidably damage model performance. They have no choice but to unlearn in an impair-then-repair regime

[

Therefore, developing unlearning methods that could reduce reliance on the maintenance with the remaining data is greatly desired and heads towards the essence of MU. We notice that very recently, several works also delved into solving this issue. We compare our methods with 4 remaining-data-free methods, including two baselines NG

•

SCAR

[

•

JiT

V-C

Unlearning Resilience: Sequential Machine Unlearning

In practical unlearning scenarios, the unlearning requests may arrive sequentially. This requires multiple executions of the unlearning method, exposing its accumulated effects on model performance. As pointed out by Wang et al.
[
56
]
, sequential unlearning greatly challenges the memorization management ability of unlearning methods due to underlying associations among unlearned classes. Moreover, the
privacy union effect

[
55
]
, which reveals that
removing the “layer” of outlier points that are most vulnerable to a privacy attack exposes a new layer of previously-safe points
, challenges the resilience of MU methods. While the retrained model can well manage the knowledge, the unlearned model might break down due to disordered forgetting operations.

Therefore, beyond
unlearning utility
, a good approximate unlearning algorithm should also have
unlearning resilience
, bringing as little damage to previous unlearning efforts during subsequent unlearning. In this part, we mainly focus on full class and sub-class unlearning tasks, where there is an apparent removal of high-level knowledge at each request. For full class unlearning, we iteratively unlearn 5 quite distinct classes (label 0-4, corresponding to Apple, Fish, Baby, Bear and Beaver) of CIFAR-100 in line with
[
56
]
. For sub-class unlearning, we iteratively unlearn 5 sub-classes (Orchid (54), Poppy (62), Rose (70), Sunflower (82), Tulip (92)) within a superclass “flower” on CIFAR-20. We compare with 4 competitive baselines according to the results of unlearning utility, including BT, FT, SSD and Salun. In sequential MU, we select the set of hyper-parameter that yields the best unlearning utility for each method and each request. Then we examine the impact of subsequent requests on previous unlearning efforts during each iteration. The disparities in accuracy-related MU metrics to the retrained model by different unlearning methods are shown in Figure
5
, where the left and the right figures correspond to full class and sub-class sequential unlearning tasks.

From Figure
5
, we can see that there are three kinds of deficiencies in existing SoTA MU methods:

1.

Performance Recovery:
The performance on the forgotten classes stages a recovery during subsequent unlearning request. This is evident in BT and Salun unlearned model, indicated by the above zero FGTA and FGVA in full class task. This suggests that retargeting model’s outputs of the forgetting data does not completely remove associated knowledge, posing a substantial risk since the concealed information might still be exploited by privacy attackers. Such a performance recovery is also observed when unlearning with AN, BE, BS and SCRUB, as illustrated in Figure
A1
.

2.

Knowledge Residue:
The unlearning method is unable to eliminate the knowledge of the forgetting data. The FT method, which removes information relying on the mechanism of “catastrophic forgetting”
[
66
]
, fails to unlearn effectively in sub-class task due to the resemblance between the forgetting and remaining data.

3.

Utility Breakdown:
The utility of unlearned model deteriorates significantly after multiple executions of the unlearning method. In sub-class task, we find that SSD exhibits a marked decline in utility after the last unlearning request, demonstrated by the final RA of
76.33\%
. In contrast, the RA in the retrained model and the MU-Mis unlearned model are respectively
84.83\%
and
84.59\%
. Such plummet implies a potential risk of model utility breakdowns when the magnitude of parameters is continuously scaled.

To facilitate an intuitive assessment in terms of utility and resilience, we compute the utility Avg. Gap and resilience Avg. Gap for each iteration in Figure
6
. The utility Avg. Gap is averaged over FTA, FVA, RA and TA, and the resilience Avg. Gap is averaged over FGTA and FGVA. From Figure
6
, it is evident that MU-Mis and SSD are significantly better than BT, FT, and Salun, demonstrating a notably small disparity to the retrained model regarding both the utility and resilience Avg. Gap across the full class and sub-class tasks. Importantly, MU-Mis achieves these results without relying on the remaining data, which are required by SSD.

of models before and after applying MU-Mis on PinsFaceRecognition dataset in Figure

V-E

Underlying reasons for model utility preservation

We suggest the favorable preservation on model utility might be attributed to two factors:

•

Conceptual motivations
: The forgetting operation in MU-Mis differs fundamentally from previous methods. While previous methods involved relabelling the forgetting data
[
40
,
41
,
10
]
or knowledge distillation from a useless teacher
[
11
,
39
,
12
]
, which unlearn by introducing incorrect information to spoil original knowledge, MU-Mis unlearns by solely withdrawing the contribution of forgetting data. In Figure
4
, we show that RA and TA stay at a high level throughout the withdrawal process.

•

Empirical investigation
:
Current gradient-based unlearning methods
[
40
,
41
,
10
,
28
,
8
,
21
,
64
]
all employ cross-entropy loss gradient
\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}(w,x)
to unlearn. However, the intra-class gradients in a well-trained model are quite similar
[
69
,
70
]
. While the gradient of input sensitivity norm w.r.t parameters is double back-propagation
[
71
]
, enhancing sample-specificity by first back-propagating to the input samples before reaching the parameters. Our pairwise analysis of cosine similarity among intra-class and inter-class samples, detailed in Appendix
B
, reveals a distinctive orthogonality in input sensitivity gradients, spontaneously reducing the interference between the forgetting and remaining data.

V-F

Hyper-parameter study

We show that there is a clear relationship between the norm ratio and model performance during unlearning in Figure
4
. The stopping threshold ratio
\delta
controls the magnitude ratio of final irrelevant class sensitivity norm
\|\nabla_{x}f_{c^{\prime}}(x,w)\|_{F}
to initial one
\|\nabla_{x}f_{c^{\prime}}(x,w_{p})\|_{F}
. In this part, we investigate the sensitivity of unlearning performance to
\delta
in Figure
9
, taking fullclass-CIFAR100-Rocket with ResNet-18 as an example. Different colors represent different
\delta
, ranging from
70\%
to
100\%
. The green dashed line indicates the performance of the retrained model. Notably, under a specific learning rate, the Avg. Gap exhibits minimal variation with changes in
\delta
. Generally, MU-Mis demonstrates resilience against variations in hyper-parameters
\delta
. In summary, the hyper-parameter tuning for MU-Mis is effortless, resilient and well-guided. This advantage in hyper-tuning indirectly enhances the efficiency of unlearning and facilitates straightforward application of MU-Mis, making it valuable for practical applications of MU methods.

VI
Conclusion

In this paper, we tackle two of the three primary challenges in machine unlearning: the incrementality of training and the catastrophe of unlearning. We theoretically establish a link between sample’s contribution during training and the pre-trained model’s sensitivity to it, allowing approximation of sample contribution with only the pre-trained model. To identify an effective metric for guiding unlearning optimization, we empirically investigate the input sensitivity norms under machine learning and unlearning scenarios. Our experiments reveal that when samples are involved in the training, they contribute to amplify the sensitivity magnitude gap between target and irrelevant class logits. Consequently, we propose MU-Mis, a method that unlearns by suppressing the forgetting data’s contributions. Extensive experiments confirm that MU-Mis unlearns effectively and efficiently without compromising the utility of remaining data, eliminating the need for additional maintenance. Beyond effective unlearning utility, MU-Mis also demonstrates remarkable unlearning resilience when subjected to sequential unlearning requests, showcasing the importance of a reasonable forgetting operation. Overall, MU-Mis is well-grounded, lightweight, remaining-data-free, and hyper-tune friendly, making it more practical than existing machine unlearning methods.

Unlearning resilience

We show the accuracy metrics of methods AN, BE and BS during full class sequential MU in Figure
A1
. For each unlearning request, their FA are consistently close to zero, signifying a successful and complete unlearning. However, starting
from the second iteration, their FGVA are notably high, indicating a performance recovery on previously unlearned classes. Therefore, the forgetting operations used by these three methods fail to manage the knowledge of DNNs well, bringing potential underlying damage.

Accuracy during fullclass sequential unlearning of methods AN, BE and BS and SCRUB.

Appendix B
Pairwise Gradient Similarity

One great advantage of our method is that we don’t need the remaining data.
In this part, we demonstrate such an advantage by showing the inherent orthogonality from the perspective of input sensitivity.

We calculate the pairwise cosine similarity within a class and between classes of the derivatives of four metrics w.r.t. parameters in a well-trained model. They are
\nabla_{w}\mathcal{L},\nabla_{w}f_{c},\nabla_{w}\|\nabla_{x}f_{c}\|_{F},
and
\nabla_{w}\sum_{c^{\prime}\neq c}\|\nabla_{x}f_{c^{\prime}}\|_{F}
(denoted as
\nabla_{w}\|\nabla_{x}f_{c^{\prime}}\|_{F}
in the following for brevity). The first two metrics are commonly used in current unlearning methods, and the last two are utilized in MU-Mis. The pair-wise similarity results are shown in Figure
A2
. Derivatives of all four metrics are approximately orthogonal between samples from different classes. However, intra-class similarities differ across four metrics. We can see that both
\nabla_{w}\mathcal{L}
and
\nabla_{w}f_{c}
bear a resemblance within a class, with cosine similarity centering around
0.3
and reaching up to
0.6
. On the other hand,
\nabla_{w}\|\nabla_{x}f_{c}\|_{F}
centers around
0.1
with intra-class similarity scarcely exceeding
0.3
. Directly taking the derivative of output w.r.t parameters preserves within-class similarity of the output, but such similarity is reduced when output first takes the derivative to the input and then back to parameters.

Interestingly,
\nabla_{w}\sum_{c^{\prime}\neq c}\|\nabla_{x}f_{c^{\prime}}\|_{F}
seems to be pairwise similar. However, we speculate that there are two reasons why this portion of our loss function does not significantly harm the performance of the remaining data. Firstly, as shown in Figure
2
,
\sum_{c^{\prime}\neq c}\|\nabla_{x}f_{c^{\prime}}\|_{F}
is significantly smaller than
\|\nabla_{x}f_{c}\|_{F}
on well-trained models. Therefore, in the early stages of optimizing the relative magnitudes of input sensitivities, the contribution of
\nabla_{w}\sum_{c^{\prime}\neq c}\|\nabla_{x}f_{c^{\prime}}\|_{F}
to the optimization direction can be neglected. Secondly,
\sum_{c^{\prime}\neq c}\|\nabla_{x}f_{c^{\prime}}\|_{F}
for the forgetting data actually corresponds to the target class of the remaining data. The inter-class similarity of
\nabla_{w}\sum_{c^{\prime}\neq c}\|\nabla_{x}f_{c^{\prime}}\|_{F}
implies that when we increase the input sensitivity of the forgetting data for the other irrelevant classes, we also increase the input sensitivity of the remaining data of the corresponding classes, thus preserving their sample contributions.

Fig. A2:
Inter-class and intra-class cosine similarity of four different metrics w.r.t parameters.
From left to right:
\nabla_{w}\mathcal{L}
,
\nabla_{w}f_{c}
,
\nabla_{w}\|\nabla_{x}f_{c}\|_{F}
, and
\nabla_{w}\|\nabla_{x}f_{c^{\prime}}\|_{F}
. Directly taking the derivative of output w.r.t parameters bears a resemblance across samples, but such similarity is reduced when output first takes derivative to input and then back to parameters.

C-D

Reweighted MU-Mis Loss

The TC (Target Class) and OC (Other Classes) terms in Eq. (
8
) can be re-weighted to enhance the optimization process of the MU-Mis loss. The ablation study in Table
A6
implies that the TC term promotes the unlearning and the OC term contributes to the recovery of performance on the remaining data. As shown in Figure
4
, TC term is much larger than OC and dominants the MU-Mis loss at the beginning of optimization. To facilitate the unlearning and subsequent recovery, we amplify the significance of the TC term when its magnitude is bigger than OC term, and conversely prioritize the OC term when the TC term’s influence diminishes in comparison. The parameter
\tau
serves as an indicator of this dynamic re-weighting switch, while
\kappa
quantifies the strength of such adjustment. Mathematically, the reweighted MU-Mis loss has the following formulation:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(w,\mathcal{D}_{f})

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{N}\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{D}f}[\alpha_{c}\cdot\|\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}f_{c}(w,\mathbf{x})\|^{2}_{F}-\alpha_{c^{\prime}}\cdot\|\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}f_{c^{\prime}}(w,\mathbf{x})\|^{2}_{F}],

(A1)

where

\displaystyle\alpha_{c}

\displaystyle=(\kappa-1)\tau+1,

\displaystyle\alpha_{c^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=(1-\kappa)\tau+\kappa,

\displaystyle\tau

\displaystyle=\mathbb{I}(\|\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}f_{c}(w,\mathbf{x})\|^{2}_{F}>\|\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}f_{c^{\prime}}(w,\mathbf{x})\|^{2}_{F}).

Thereby, we have

\begin{cases}\alpha_{c}=\kappa,\alpha_{c^{\prime}}=1\,\text{if $\|\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}f_{c}(\mathbf{x})\|^{2}_{F}\geq\|\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}f_{c^{\prime}}(\mathbf{x})\|^{2}_{F}$,}\\
\alpha_{c}=1,\alpha_{c^{\prime}}=\kappa\,\text{if $\|\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}f_{c}(\mathbf{x})\|^{2}_{F}<\|\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}f_{c^{\prime}}(\mathbf{x})\|^{2}_{F}$.}\end{cases}

The original MU-Mis loss Eq. (
8
) corresponds to
\kappa=1
. We apply this strategy in sub-class sequential MU task and Tiny-ImageNet dataset. We set
\kappa=80
for iterations
1\sim 3
and
\kappa=1
for iterations
4\sim 5
in sequential sub-class, and
\kappa=80
for Tiny-ImageNet.

C-E

KL Divergence

The KL divergence between two distributions is:

D_{\text{KL}}(p_{z}(w_{r})||p_{z}(w_{u}))=\int p_{z}(w_{r})\log[{p_{z}(w_{r})}/{p_{z}(\theta)}]\mathrm{d}\mathcal{D}

(A2)

We calculate empirical KL divergence with the entire dataset (including both the train and valid set). We first collect the predicted class probabilities from both the unlearned and retrained models of each sample, then we compute the output KL divergence as follows:

D_{\text{KL}}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{c=1}^{C}p_{c}(x_{i};w_{r})\log{\frac{p_{c}(x_{i};w_{r})}{p_{c}(x_{i};w_{u})}},

(A3)

where
N
is total number of dataset,
C
denotes the total number of classes,
w_{u}
is the unlearned model parameter and
w_{r}
is the retrained model parameter.
p_{c}(x_{i},w)
represents the
c
-th posterior probability of
i
-th sample in model
w
.
