Abstract
. This study focuses on constructing efficient rank-1 lattices that enable the exact integration and reconstruction of functions within Chebyshev spaces, based on finite lower index sets. We establish the equivalence of different reconstruction plans under specific conditions for certain lower sets. Furthermore, we introduce a heuristic component-by-component (CBC) algorithm that efficiently identifies admissible generating vectors and suitable numbers of nodes n n , optimizing both memory usage and computational time.

1.
Introduction

Function integration and reconstruction are fundamental problems in computational mathematics, with applications ranging from numerical analysis and machine learning to engineering and the physical sciences. Achieving accurate and efficient approximations, especially in high-dimensional settings, is challenging due to the exponential growth of computational resources required—a phenomenon often referred to as the curse of dimensionality
[
2
]
. Rank-1 lattices have emerged as a powerful framework to address these challenges, offering structured sampling schemes that facilitate efficient and accurate approximation of multivariate functions.

A rank-1 lattice rule approximates the
d
-dimensional integral over the unit cube
[0,1]^{d}
by evaluating the integrand at a carefully chosen set of lattice points:

(1.1)

I_{d}(f)=\int_{[0,1]^{d}}f({\boldsymbol{x}})\,\mathrm{d}{\boldsymbol{x}}\approx Q_{n,{\boldsymbol{z}}}(f)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}f\left(\frac{i{\boldsymbol{z}}}{n}\mod 1\right),

where
{\boldsymbol{z}}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
is the generating vector, and
n
is the number of nodes. The selection of
{\boldsymbol{z}}
and
n
is critical for ensuring the accuracy and efficiency of the approximation. Optimal rank-1 lattices minimize the number of nodes
n
while satisfying exactness conditions for specific function spaces, thereby balancing computational resources with approximation fidelity.

Previous works have extensively explored algorithms for constructing rank-1 lattices tailored to various function spaces. For instance,
[
7
]
introduced weighted degrees of exactness for anisotropic integrands and presented CBC strategies for their construction. Subsequent works, such as
[
11
]
, advanced these methodologies by developing deterministic algorithms for multiple rank-1 lattices, enhancing their applicability in high-dimensional approximation tasks. Additionally,
[
8
]
extended CBC approaches to deterministic settings, focusing on exact integration within truncated Fourier spaces.

Chebyshev spaces, characterized by their optimal convergence properties and prevalence in spectral methods
[
4
]
, present unique opportunities and challenges for rank-1 lattice constructions. Unlike Fourier spaces, Chebyshev spaces require handling polynomial bases with specific orthogonality properties, necessitating tailored reconstruction strategies. Lower index sets underpin these function spaces by ensuring that all necessary lower-order terms are included for accurate function representation
[
3
]
.

The primary contributions of this paper are threefold:

•

Theoretical Lower Bounds:
We establish lower bounds on the minimal number of nodes
n
required for exact reconstruction in Chebyshev spaces, and derive closed-form expressions for specific classes of lower index sets.

•

Equivalence of Reconstruction Plans:
We investigate different reconstruction strategies—denoted as Plans A, B, and C—and establish conditions under which these plans are equivalent for certain lower sets. This equivalence allows for flexibility in algorithm design, enabling the selection of the most computationally efficient plan without compromising reconstruction accuracy.

•

Heuristic CBC Algorithm:
We introduce a heuristic component-by-component (CBC) algorithm specifically designed for lower sets in Chebyshev spaces. This algorithm leverages the structural properties of lower sets to efficiently generate optimal generating vectors and determine suitable numbers of nodes
n
, significantly reducing both memory usage and computational time compared to exhaustive search methods.

The paper is organized as follows. Section
2
introduces notation, cubature rules, rank-1 lattice properties and lower sets. Section
3
provides lower bounds on nodes for reconstruction plans in Chebyshev expansions. Section
4
presents our main theoretical findings, including equivalence results for reconstruction plans. Section
5
describes the proposed heuristic CBC algorithms, their complexity, and implementation details. Section
6
showcases numerical experiments comparing the algorithms’ efficiency and accuracy. Section
7
summarizes our findings and proposes future research directions. Appendix
A
details some properties of lower sets, including their operations and characterizations of the so-called maximal and extremal elements.
A cardinality comparison result of broad interest is given in
A.2
.

2.1.
Approximation requirements

For the sake of clarity, in this section we will employ a unified
notation for the spaces and bases we have introduced. We use notation

(\alpha_{{\boldsymbol{k}}})_{{\boldsymbol{k}}}
for the multi-variate complete orthonormal
systems and use conjugate notation
\overline{\alpha_{\boldsymbol{k}}}
.
The integral
I(f)
and inner product
\langle f,g\rangle=I(f\overline{g})

are understood from the context. We will also use
{\mathcal{F}}_{\Lambda}

for truncated spaces, without specifying Fourier, cosine or Chebyshev.

Placing ourselves in one of the three spaces, a cubature (or quadrature)
is simply defined by
Q_{n}(f)=\sum_{j=1}^{n}w_{j}f({\boldsymbol{t}}_{i})
for given
weights
w_{j}>0
and given nodes
{\boldsymbol{t}}_{i}
belonging to the underlying domain.
One can be interested in the following properties

•

Integral exactness
. To demand that the cubature is
exact for all functions which are supported solely on
\Lambda
, i.e.,

Q_{n}(f)=I(f)
for all
f\in{\mathcal{F}}_{\Lambda}
. This holds if and only if

(2.5)

Q_{n}(\alpha_{\boldsymbol{k}})=I(\alpha_{\boldsymbol{k}})=\delta_{{\boldsymbol{k}},{\boldsymbol{0}}},\quad\quad\quad{\boldsymbol{k}}\in\Lambda,

where
\delta
is the Kronecker symbol, i.e. the cubature formula integrates exactly all basis functions

\alpha_{\boldsymbol{k}}
for
{\boldsymbol{k}}\in\Lambda
.

•

Function reconstruction
. To reconstruct all functions supported on a given lattice
\Lambda
, we define the approximation operator using
Q_{n}
as

f^{a}({{\boldsymbol{x}}})=\sum_{{\boldsymbol{k}}\in\Lambda}\widehat{f}_{\boldsymbol{k}}^{a}\,\alpha_{\boldsymbol{k}}({{\boldsymbol{x}}}),

where the coefficients
\widehat{f}_{\boldsymbol{k}}^{a}
are given by the cubature formula

\widehat{f}_{\boldsymbol{k}}^{a}=Q_{n}\left(f\overline{\alpha_{\boldsymbol{k}}}\right),

and approximate the exact coefficients

\widehat{f}_{\boldsymbol{k}}=\langle f,\alpha_{\boldsymbol{k}}\rangle=I\left(f\overline{\alpha_{\boldsymbol{k}}}\right),

where
I
represents the integral operator and
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
denotes the inner product.

To ensure accurate reconstruction, we impose the
non-aliasing condition
:

\widehat{f}_{\boldsymbol{k}}^{a}=\widehat{f}_{\boldsymbol{k}},\quad\forall\,{\boldsymbol{k}}\in\Lambda\text{ and }f\in\mathcal{F}_{\Lambda},

where
\mathcal{F}_{\Lambda}
is the space of functions supported solely on the lattice
\Lambda
. This condition ensures that the operator
f\in\mathcal{F}\mapsto f^{a}\in\mathcal{F}_{\Lambda}
is a valid reconstruction operator. If contributions from
\widehat{f}_{{\boldsymbol{k}}^{\prime}}
for
{\boldsymbol{k}}^{\prime}\notin\Lambda
affect
\widehat{f}_{\boldsymbol{k}}^{a}
, this introduces
aliasing
.

The non-aliasing condition holds if and only if the cubature formula satisfies:

(2.6)

Q_{n}(\alpha_{\boldsymbol{k}}\overline{\alpha_{{\boldsymbol{k}}^{\prime}}})=\delta_{{\boldsymbol{k}},{\boldsymbol{k}}^{\prime}}=\langle\alpha_{\boldsymbol{k}},\alpha_{{\boldsymbol{k}}^{\prime}}\rangle,\quad\quad\quad{\boldsymbol{k}},{\boldsymbol{k}}^{\prime}\in\Lambda.

where
\delta_{{\boldsymbol{k}},{\boldsymbol{k}}^{\prime}}
is the Kronecker delta. This ensures the orthogonality of the basis functions
\alpha_{\boldsymbol{k}}
, eliminating aliasing errors.

•

Function reconstruction and bi-orthonormality
.
Reconstruction using cubature formulas can be approached through the framework of bi-orthonormality. Let
(\beta_{\boldsymbol{k}})_{{\boldsymbol{k}}\in\Lambda}
be a set of functions in
\mathcal{F}_{\Lambda}
that is bi-orthonormal to
(\alpha_{\boldsymbol{k}})_{{\boldsymbol{k}}\in\Lambda}
, satisfying:

\langle\alpha_{\boldsymbol{k}},\beta_{{\boldsymbol{k}}^{\prime}}\rangle=\delta_{{\boldsymbol{k}},{\boldsymbol{k}}^{\prime}},\quad\forall\,{\boldsymbol{k}},{\boldsymbol{k}}^{\prime}\in\Lambda,

where
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
is the inner product and
\delta_{{\boldsymbol{k}},{\boldsymbol{k}}^{\prime}}
is the Kronecker delta.

Given this bi-orthonormal basis, we define the approximation:

(2.7)

f^{b}({{\boldsymbol{x}}})=\sum_{{\boldsymbol{k}}\in\Lambda}\widehat{f}_{\boldsymbol{k}}^{b}\,\alpha_{\boldsymbol{k}}({{\boldsymbol{x}}}),\quad\widehat{f}_{\boldsymbol{k}}^{b}=\frac{Q_{n}\left(f\overline{\beta_{{\boldsymbol{k}}}}\right)}{c_{\boldsymbol{k}}},

where
Q_{n}
is a cubature rule,
c_{\boldsymbol{k}}\neq 0
are nonzero coefficients, and
\widehat{f}_{\boldsymbol{k}}^{b}
are determined using
\beta_{\boldsymbol{k}}
instead of
\alpha_{\boldsymbol{k}}
. This scheme reproduces
\mathcal{F}_{\Lambda}
—or equivalently the basis functions
\alpha_{\boldsymbol{k}}
—if and only if:

(2.8)

Q_{n}\left(\alpha_{\boldsymbol{k}}\overline{\beta_{{\boldsymbol{k}}^{\prime}}}\right)=c_{\boldsymbol{k}}\delta_{{\boldsymbol{k}},{\boldsymbol{k}}^{\prime}},\quad\forall\,{\boldsymbol{k}},{\boldsymbol{k}}^{\prime}\in\Lambda.

The reproduction criterion in (
2.8
) aligns with the duality framework. For integral exactness, one seeks a linear form
\alpha_{\mathbf{0}}^{*}:f\mapsto\alpha_{\mathbf{0}}^{*}(f)
satisfying:

\alpha_{\mathbf{0}}^{*}(\alpha_{\boldsymbol{k}})=\delta_{\mathbf{0},{\boldsymbol{k}}},\quad\forall\,{\boldsymbol{k}}\in\Lambda,

which guarantees
\alpha_{\mathbf{0}}(f)=\widehat{f}_{\mathbf{0}}=I(f)
for any
f\in\mathcal{F}_{\Lambda}
. Similarly, reconstruction requires a family of linear forms
\alpha_{\boldsymbol{h}}^{*}:f\mapsto\alpha_{\boldsymbol{h}}^{*}(f)
indexed by
\Lambda
, such that:

\alpha_{\boldsymbol{h}}^{*}(\alpha_{\boldsymbol{k}})=\delta_{{\boldsymbol{h}},{\boldsymbol{k}}},\quad\forall\,{\boldsymbol{h}},{\boldsymbol{k}}\in\Lambda.

This family ensures that the operator
\sum_{{\boldsymbol{h}}\in\Lambda}\alpha_{\boldsymbol{h}}^{*}(f)\alpha_{\boldsymbol{h}}
reconstructs all functions in
\mathcal{F}_{\Lambda}
.

The forms
\alpha_{\boldsymbol{h}}^{*}
may not depend explicitly on
\Lambda
. Notable examples include
f\mapsto\widehat{f}_{\boldsymbol{h}}^{\square}
for
\square\in\{a,b,c\}
, as introduced in
[
12
]
. These approaches provide robust tools for reconstruction in high-dimensional settings.

2.2.
Rank-1 lattices rules

Rank-1 lattices are extensively studied for integration, reconstruction, and approximation in the Fourier space. Given a generating vector
{\boldsymbol{z}}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, the
n
points of a rank-1 lattice are defined as:

(2.9)

\mathbf{t}_{i}=\frac{i{\boldsymbol{z}}\bmod n}{n}\in[0,1[^{d},\quad\text{for }i=0,\ldots,n-1.

For a function
f
in Fourier space (or other spaces such as Cosine or Chebyshev), the cubature rules can be expressed as:

(2.10)

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}f\left({\boldsymbol{t}}_{i}\right),\quad\quad\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}f\left(\varphi_{\text{tent}}({\boldsymbol{t}}_{i})\right),\quad\quad\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}f\left(\cos\left(2\pi{\boldsymbol{t}}_{i}\right)\right),

where the first formula corresponds to a standard rank-1 lattice rule, the second to a tent-transformed lattice rule, and the third to a tent-transformed then cosine-transformed lattice rule. The tent transformation
\varphi_{\text{tent}}:[0,1]^{d}\to[0,1]^{d}
is defined as

\varphi_{\text{tent}}(\mathbf{t})=(1-|2t_{1}-1|,\dots,1-|2t_{d}-1|)
.
In view of the symmetry rank-1 lattices i.e. (
{\boldsymbol{t}}_{n-i}={\boldsymbol{1}}-{\boldsymbol{t}}_{i}

\forall i\in\{1,\dots,n-1\}
),
and that of tent and cosine functions, only half the points are needed for transformed rules
(see
[
6
,
12
]
). More precisely, at most

\lfloor n/2+1\rfloor
function evaluations are needed.

The first formula corresponds to a rank-1 lattice rule, which is used to approximate the integral

I(f)=\int_{[0,1]^{d}}f({{\boldsymbol{x}}})\,\mathrm{d}{{\boldsymbol{x}}},\quad\text{for }f\in\mathcal{F}^{\text{Four}}.

Rank-1 lattices exhibit an important property, known as the
character property
, which states that the cubature sum of exponential basis functions can only take the values 1 or 0. Specifically:

(2.11)

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}e^{\mathrm{i}2\pi i\,{\boldsymbol{h}}\cdot{\boldsymbol{z}}/n}=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }{\boldsymbol{h}}\cdot{\boldsymbol{z}}\equiv_{n}0,\\
0&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

This property, combined with the relation
e_{\boldsymbol{k}}\overline{e_{{\boldsymbol{k}}^{\prime}}}=e_{{\boldsymbol{k}}-{\boldsymbol{k}}^{\prime}}
, enables a reformulation of the integral exactness and reconstruction requirements discussed earlier.

2.3.
Lower sets

Definition 2.4
.

An index set
\Lambda\subset\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}
is called
lower
2
2

2

also called downward closed
if it satisfies the following property:

(2.23)

{\boldsymbol{k}}\in\Lambda\quad\text{and}\quad{\boldsymbol{h}}\leq{\boldsymbol{k}}\quad\implies\quad{\boldsymbol{h}}\in\Lambda.

Here, the comparison

is understood component-wise, meaning

for all

A lower set

can also be characterized by the equivalent property:

(2.24)

{\boldsymbol{k}}\in\Lambda\quad\text{and}\quad k_{i}\geq 1\quad\implies\quad{\boldsymbol{k}}-\mathbf{e}_{i}\in\Lambda,

where
\mathbf{e}_{i}
denotes the standard unit vector in the

. Notably, any lower set must include the null multi-index

. For a lower set

, we can write:

\Lambda=\bigcup_{{\boldsymbol{k}}\in\Lambda}\mathcal{B}_{{\boldsymbol{k}}},

where
\mathcal{B}_{{\boldsymbol{k}}}:=\{{\boldsymbol{h}}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}:{\boldsymbol{h}}\leq{\boldsymbol{k}}\}
is the block-type set associated with

Examples of Lower Sets

Typical examples of lower sets include the isotropic sets

\left\{{\boldsymbol{k}}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}:k_{j}\leq r\penalty\ \forall j\right\}

\{{\boldsymbol{k}}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}:\sum_{j=1}^{d}k_{j}\leq r\}

We specifically refer lower sets of

Maximal and Extremal Elements

An element
{\boldsymbol{k}}\in\Lambda
is called a
maximal element
of the lower set
\Lambda
if it is maximal with respect to the partial order
\leq
. Formally,
{\boldsymbol{k}}
is maximal if:

{\boldsymbol{h}}\in\Lambda\quad\text{and}\quad{\boldsymbol{k}}\leq{\boldsymbol{h}}\quad\implies\quad{\boldsymbol{h}}={\boldsymbol{k}}.

Equivalently,
{\boldsymbol{k}}
is maximal if
{\boldsymbol{k}}+\mathbf{e}_{i}\not\in\Lambda
for all
i=1,\dots,d
, where
\mathbf{e}_{i}
denotes the standard unit vector in the
i
-th direction.

An element
{\boldsymbol{k}}\in\Lambda
is called an
extremal element
of
\Lambda
if it cannot be expressed as the midpoint of two distinct elements of
\Lambda
. Formally,
{\boldsymbol{k}}
is extremal if:

(2.26)

2{\boldsymbol{k}}={\boldsymbol{h}}+{\boldsymbol{h}}^{\prime}\quad\text{and}\quad{\boldsymbol{h}},{\boldsymbol{h}}^{\prime}\in\Lambda\quad\implies\quad{\boldsymbol{h}}={\boldsymbol{h}}^{\prime}={\boldsymbol{k}}.

The null multi-index
\mathbf{0}
is always an extremal element. However, if
{\boldsymbol{k}}+\mathbf{e}_{j}\in\Lambda
for some
j\in\supp({\boldsymbol{k}})
, then
{\boldsymbol{k}}
is not extremal in
\Lambda
. We denote by
{\mathcal{E}}(\Lambda)
(resp.
{\mathcal{E}}_{\leq}(\Lambda)
) the set of extremal
(resp. maximal) elements of
\Lambda
. Here,
\mathrm{supp}(\boldsymbol{k})
denote the support of
\boldsymbol{k}
, defined as the subset of
\{1,\dots,d\}
where
k_{j}\neq 0
.

A maximal element is not necessarily extremal, e.g.

(1,1)
is maximal in
\{{\boldsymbol{k}}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{2}:k_{1}+k_{1}\leq 2\}

but not extremal since it is in the middle between

(2,0)
and
(0,2)
.
However, if
{\boldsymbol{k}}
is maximal and
\#(\supp({\boldsymbol{k}}))=1
, then
{\boldsymbol{k}}
is extremal. Moreover, any finite lower set has at least one maximal element. The set
\Lambda
is completely determined by its maximal elements:

(2.27)

\Lambda=\bigcup_{{\boldsymbol{k}}\text{ maximal in }\Lambda}\mathcal{B}_{{\boldsymbol{k}}}.

Necessary Conditions for Exact Reconstruction

In this section, we derive lower bounds on the minimal number of rank-1 lattice points

3.1.
Lower Bounds for Plans A, B, and C

The requirement in (
2.19
) ensures that the mapping:

{\boldsymbol{z}}\mapsto{\boldsymbol{h}}\cdot{\boldsymbol{z}}\pmod{n},

is injective over
\mathcal{M}(\Lambda)
. Similarly, the requirement in (
2.20
) enforces injectivity over
\Lambda\cup-\Lambda
and the requirement in (

) enforces injectivity over
\Lambda

Thus, the following holds

(3.1)

n\geq\begin{cases}\#\mathcal{M}(\Lambda)&\text{(Plan A admissibility)},\\
\#(\Lambda\cup-\Lambda)&\text{(Plan B admissibility)},\\
\#\Lambda&\text{(Plan C admissibility)}.\end{cases}

If
{\boldsymbol{h}}\cdot{\boldsymbol{z}}\equiv_{n}0
, then
{\boldsymbol{h}}\cdot{\boldsymbol{z}}\equiv_{n}-{\boldsymbol{h}}\cdot{\boldsymbol{z}}
.
Assuming
\mathbf{0}\notin\Lambda
and
{\boldsymbol{z}}\mapsto{\boldsymbol{h}}\cdot{\boldsymbol{z}}\pmod{n}

is injective over
\mathcal{M}(\Lambda)
, the remainder 0 is excluded.
The same holds for
\Lambda\cup-\Lambda

are slightly improved to:

n\geq\#\mathcal{M}(\Lambda)+1_{\mathbf{0}\notin\Lambda}\quad\text{(Plan A admissibility)},

n\geq\#(\Lambda\cup-\Lambda)+1_{\mathbf{0}\notin\Lambda}\quad\text{(Plan B admissibility)}.

The lower bounds can be implied from reconstruction properties directly. For instance,

(n,{\boldsymbol{z}})
is admissible for Plan A if and only if the rank-1 lattice rule

satisfies (

(e_{\boldsymbol{k}})_{{\boldsymbol{k}}\in\mathcal{M}(\Lambda)}

\frac{1}{n}\Phi^{\top}\Phi=I_{\#\mathcal{M}(\Lambda)},

.
This implies
\Phi

n\geq\#\mathcal{M}(\Lambda).

In general, for
\Lambda\subset\mathbb{Z}^{d}

(\alpha_{\boldsymbol{k}})_{{\boldsymbol{k}}\in\Lambda}

\Phi_{\alpha}^{\top}W\Phi_{\beta}=\text{diag}\left(c_{\boldsymbol{k}}\right)_{{\boldsymbol{k}}\in\Lambda},

Equivalence between Admissibility Requirements for lower sets

In this section, we establish equivalence properties between the admissibility requirements (

4.1.
Equivalences for General Lower Sets

We begin with the following result:

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
\Lambda\subset\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}
be a lower set,
n\in\mathbb{N}
, and
{\boldsymbol{z}}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
. The following statements are equivalent:

•

(n,{\boldsymbol{z}})
is admissible for Plan 0 for
\Lambda\oplus\Lambda
,

•

(n,{\boldsymbol{z}})
is admissible for Plan A for
\Lambda
.

This equivalence follows from Proposition
A.1
, which states that:

\mathcal{M}(\Lambda\oplus\Lambda)=\mathcal{M}(\Lambda)\oplus\mathcal{M}(\Lambda)=\mathcal{M}(\Lambda)\ominus\mathcal{M}(\Lambda),

where

\Lambda\ominus\Lambda^{\prime}\;=\;\{{\boldsymbol{k}}-{\boldsymbol{k}}^{\prime}\,:\,{\boldsymbol{k}}\in\Lambda,\,{\boldsymbol{k}}^{\prime}\in\Lambda^{\prime}\},
is the
difference set
.
In particular, the condition
{\boldsymbol{h}}\cdot{\boldsymbol{z}}\not\equiv_{n}0
for all
{\boldsymbol{h}}\in\mathcal{M}(\Lambda\oplus\Lambda)\setminus\{\mathbf{0}\}
is equivalent to
{\boldsymbol{h}}\cdot{\boldsymbol{z}}\not\equiv_{n}{\boldsymbol{h}}^{\prime}\cdot{\boldsymbol{z}}
for all
{\boldsymbol{h}},{\boldsymbol{h}}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{M}(\Lambda)
with
{\boldsymbol{h}}\neq{\boldsymbol{h}}^{\prime}
.

Let us discuss a few specific cases to illustrate these equivalences:

•

d=2
and
\Lambda=\mathcal{C}_{{\boldsymbol{k}}}
: we have that

{\mathcal{B}}_{\boldsymbol{k}}\subset{\mathcal{C}}_{\boldsymbol{k}}\oplus{\mathcal{C}}_{\boldsymbol{k}}
, hence

{\mathcal{B}}_{\boldsymbol{k}}\ominus{\mathcal{B}}_{\boldsymbol{k}}={\mathcal{M}}({\mathcal{B}}_{\boldsymbol{k}})\subset{\mathcal{M}}({\mathcal{C}}_{\boldsymbol{k}}\oplus{\mathcal{C}}_{\boldsymbol{k}})
.
As a result, if
(n,{\boldsymbol{z}})
is admissible for plan A for
{\mathcal{C}}_{\boldsymbol{k}}

then

{\boldsymbol{h}}\cdot{\boldsymbol{z}}\not\equiv_{n}{\boldsymbol{h}}^{\prime}\cdot{\boldsymbol{z}}
for
{\boldsymbol{h}}\neq{\boldsymbol{h}}^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{B}}_{\boldsymbol{k}}
.
This implies that
n\geq\#({\mathcal{B}}_{\boldsymbol{k}})
.
We actually have

(4.1)

n_{A}^{*}(\mathcal{C}_{{\boldsymbol{k}}})=(k_{1}+1)(k_{2}+1)+1.

The reason why
n_{A}^{*}(\mathcal{C}_{{\boldsymbol{k}}})\neq\#({\mathcal{B}}_{\boldsymbol{k}})
is
explained in the Appendix
A.4
.

•

d\geq 2
,
\Lambda=\mathcal{B}_{{\boldsymbol{k}}}
:
we have that
\mathcal{B}_{2{\boldsymbol{k}}}\ominus\mathcal{B}_{2{\boldsymbol{k}}}=\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{B}_{2{\boldsymbol{k}}})=\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{B}_{{\boldsymbol{k}}}\oplus\mathcal{B}_{{\boldsymbol{k}}})
. Thus,
(n,{\boldsymbol{z}})
is admissible for Plan A for
\mathcal{B}_{{\boldsymbol{k}}}
if and only if

{\boldsymbol{h}}\cdot{\boldsymbol{z}}\not\equiv_{n}{\boldsymbol{h}}^{\prime}\cdot{\boldsymbol{z}}
for all

{\boldsymbol{h}}\neq{\boldsymbol{h}}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{B}_{2{\boldsymbol{k}}}
.
We recover again
n\geq\#(\mathcal{B}_{2{\boldsymbol{k}}})=\#(\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{B}_{{\boldsymbol{k}}}))
. The

(n,{\boldsymbol{z}})
with

and

, where

and

for

, yields Plan A
admissibility. Indeed,
{\boldsymbol{h}}\mapsto{\boldsymbol{h}}\cdot{\boldsymbol{z}}
is one-to-one from

onto

\{0,\dots,\#(\mathcal{B}_{2{\boldsymbol{k}}})-1\}
. We therefore have

(4.2)

•

\mathcal{S}_{{\boldsymbol{w}},2u-\max_{i}(w_{i})}\subset\mathcal{S}_{{\boldsymbol{w}},u}\oplus\mathcal{S}_{{\boldsymbol{w}},u}\subset\mathcal{S}_{{\boldsymbol{w}},2u}.

Therefore, if
(n,{\boldsymbol{z}})
is admissible for Plan 0 for

(n,{\boldsymbol{z}})
is admissible for Plan A for
\mathcal{S}_{{\boldsymbol{w}},u}

(4.3)

Proposition 4.2
.

Let
\Lambda\subset\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}
be a lower set,
n\in\mathbb{N}
, and
{\boldsymbol{z}}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
. Then:

(4.4)

{\boldsymbol{h}}\cdot{\boldsymbol{z}}\not\equiv_{n}{\boldsymbol{h}}^{\prime}\cdot{\boldsymbol{z}}\quad\forall{\boldsymbol{h}}\neq{\boldsymbol{h}}^{\prime}\in\Lambda\quad\implies\quad{\boldsymbol{h}}\cdot{\boldsymbol{z}}\not\equiv_{n}0\quad\forall{\boldsymbol{h}}\in\mathcal{M}(\Lambda)\setminus\{\mathbf{0}\}.

An equivalence holds if
\Lambda=\mathcal{B}_{{\boldsymbol{k}}}
for some
{\boldsymbol{k}}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}
.

Proof.

For any
{\boldsymbol{h}}\in\mathcal{M}(\Lambda)\setminus\{\mathbf{0}\}
, we can decompose it as
{\boldsymbol{h}}={\boldsymbol{h}}^{+}-{\boldsymbol{h}}^{-}
, where
{\boldsymbol{h}}^{+}=\max({\boldsymbol{h}},\mathbf{0})
and
{\boldsymbol{h}}^{-}=\max(-{\boldsymbol{h}},\mathbf{0})
. Since
{\boldsymbol{h}}^{+},{\boldsymbol{h}}^{-}\leq|{\boldsymbol{h}}|
, and
|{\boldsymbol{h}}|\in\Lambda
, the lower property ensures
{\boldsymbol{h}}^{+},{\boldsymbol{h}}^{-}\in\Lambda

{\boldsymbol{h}}^{-}\cdot{\boldsymbol{z}}\not\equiv_{n}{\boldsymbol{h}}^{+}\cdot{\boldsymbol{z}}\quad\implies\quad{\boldsymbol{h}}\cdot{\boldsymbol{z}}\not\equiv_{n}0.

If
\Lambda=\mathcal{B}_{{\boldsymbol{k}}}
, note that

is equivalent to
\mathbf{0}\neq({\boldsymbol{h}}-{\boldsymbol{h}}^{\prime})\in\mathcal{M}(\Lambda)
.

Equivalence Between Plans B and C

The following results address the equivalence between Plans B and C, i.e., requirements (
2.20
) and (
2.21
). The techniques rely on those used earlier but are more intricate due to additional constraints.

Proposition 4.3
.

Let
\Lambda\subset\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}
be a lower set,
n\in\mathbb{N}
, and
{\boldsymbol{z}}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
. The following statements are equivalent:

•

(n,{\boldsymbol{z}})
is admissible for Plan B,

•

(n,{\boldsymbol{z}})
is admissible for Plan C and
2{\boldsymbol{h}}\cdot{\boldsymbol{z}}\not\equiv_{n}0
for all
{\boldsymbol{h}}\in\mathcal{E}(\Lambda)\setminus\{\mathbf{0}\}
.

Moreover, if
n
is odd, the additional condition
2{\boldsymbol{h}}\cdot{\boldsymbol{z}}\not\equiv_{n}0
is not required.

Proof.

It is straightforward that Plan B admissibility implies Plan C admissibility and that
{\boldsymbol{h}}\cdot{\boldsymbol{z}}\not\equiv_{n}-{\boldsymbol{h}}\cdot{\boldsymbol{z}}
for all
{\boldsymbol{h}}\in\Lambda\setminus\{\mathbf{0}\}
, as noted in Table
1
.

For the converse, assume
(n,{\boldsymbol{z}})
is admissible for Plan C and satisfies the additional condition, but is not admissible for Plan B. By Table
1
, there exists
{\boldsymbol{h}}\in\Lambda\setminus\{\mathbf{0}\}
and
\sigma\in\mathcal{S}_{{\boldsymbol{h}}}^{*}
such that

{\boldsymbol{h}}\cdot{\boldsymbol{z}}\equiv_{n}\sigma({\boldsymbol{h}})\cdot{\boldsymbol{z}}.

We have
\sigma({\boldsymbol{h}})={\boldsymbol{h}}^{+}-{\boldsymbol{h}}^{-}
and
{\boldsymbol{h}}={\boldsymbol{h}}^{+}+{\boldsymbol{h}}^{-}
, where

{\boldsymbol{h}}^{+}=\max(\sigma({\boldsymbol{h}}),\mathbf{0})
,

{\boldsymbol{h}}^{-}=\max(-\sigma({\boldsymbol{h}}),\mathbf{0})
, both in
\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}
. Note that
{\boldsymbol{h}}^{-}\neq\mathbf{0}
, as
{\boldsymbol{h}}^{-}=\mathbf{0}
implies
\sigma=\text{id}
, which is excluded.

The condition
{\boldsymbol{h}}\cdot{\boldsymbol{z}}\equiv_{n}\sigma({\boldsymbol{h}})\cdot{\boldsymbol{z}}
reformulates as

2{\boldsymbol{h}}^{-}\cdot{\boldsymbol{z}}\equiv_{n}0\equiv_{n}{\boldsymbol{0}}\cdot{\boldsymbol{z}}.

If
2{\boldsymbol{h}}^{-}\in\Lambda
, this contradicts Plan C admissibility. In general, since
\Lambda

is lower, then
{\boldsymbol{h}}^{-}\in\Lambda
. If
{\boldsymbol{h}}^{-}\not\in\mathcal{E}(\Lambda)
, then
2{\boldsymbol{h}}^{-}={\boldsymbol{k}}+{\boldsymbol{k}}^{\prime}
for some
{\boldsymbol{k}}\neq{\boldsymbol{k}}^{\prime}\in\Lambda
. This implies

{\boldsymbol{k}}\cdot{\boldsymbol{z}}\equiv_{n}-{\boldsymbol{k}}^{\prime}\cdot{\boldsymbol{z}},

contradicting Plan C admissibility. Thus, the equivalence is established.

If
n
is odd,

5.
A Specialized CBC Construction for Lower Sets

In this section, we present a
specialized component-by-component (CBC)
algorithm for constructing rank-1 lattices, targeting lower index sets
\Lambda\subset\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}
for Chebyshev expansions. The algorithm identifies a generator
{\boldsymbol{z}}
and modulus
n
that satisfy admissibility criteria for
\Lambda
under plans
0,A,B,C
. While it does not guarantee the minimal
n
, it provides a practical, polynomial-time solution for high-dimensional problems and large index sets.

The approach exploits the hierarchical structure of lower sets, enabling incremental admissibility checks across dimensions. By maintaining dictionaries to track intermediate results, the algorithm reduces redundant aliasing checks and improves computational efficiency.

Our approach shares conceptual similarities with certain CBC strategies presented in the literature; for instance, Algorithm 3.8 in
[
9
]
or the one proposed in
[
5
]
also iterate dimension by dimension. However, we exploit the
lower-set
structure of
\Lambda
more explicitly to reduce collision checks, permit partial or fully deferred decisions on
n
, and unify Plans 0, A, B, and C within a single framework. These features distinguish our algorithm and make it suitable for moderate to large
\Lambda
.

Notation.

A set
\Lambda\subset\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}
is called
lower
if, for every
{\boldsymbol{k}}\in\Lambda
, any
{\boldsymbol{h}}
with
0\leq{\boldsymbol{h}}\leq{\boldsymbol{k}}
(componentwise) also belongs to
\Lambda
. We denote by
{\mathcal{M}}(\Lambda)
the
mirrored version
of
\Lambda
, i.e., all indices in
\Lambda
with all possible sign patterns in each coordinate. We seek
(n,{\boldsymbol{z}})\in\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{Z}^{d}
such that
(n,{\boldsymbol{z}})
avoids collisions (aliasing) on
\Lambda
under one of the Plans
\{0,A,B,C\}
(see Table
1
).

Definition 5.1

(Admissibility sets,
{\mathcal{P}}_{\square}(\Lambda)
)
.

For a plan
\square\in\{0,A,B,C\}
, we say
(n,{\boldsymbol{z}})\in{\mathcal{P}}_{\square}(\Lambda)
if it satisfies the collision-free requirements of plan-
\square
on
\Lambda
. Specifically:

•

Plan 0:
For all nonzero
{\boldsymbol{h}}\in{\mathcal{M}}(\Lambda)
, we must have
{\boldsymbol{h}}\cdot{\boldsymbol{z}}\not\equiv 0\pmod{n}
.

•

Plan A:
For all distinct
{\boldsymbol{h}},{\boldsymbol{h}}^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{M}}(\Lambda)

{\boldsymbol{h}}\cdot{\boldsymbol{z}}\not\equiv{\boldsymbol{h}}^{\prime}\cdot{\boldsymbol{z}}\pmod{n}

•

These are less restrictive regarding sign flips (see Table

Our goal is to minimize

using known estimates (e.g., from Equation (

if

,
and so forth for lower dimensions. This observation allows admissibility checks to be built incrementally:

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
\Lambda\subset\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}
be lower, and
(n,{\boldsymbol{z}})\in\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{Z}^{d}
. Then

(n,{\boldsymbol{z}})\in\mathcal{P}_{\square}(\Lambda)\implies(n,{\boldsymbol{z}}_{[d-1]})\in\mathcal{P}_{\square}(\Lambda_{[d-1]})\implies\dots\implies(n,z_{1})\in\mathcal{P}_{\square}(\Lambda_{[1]}).

Iterative Verification

Using Lemma
5.2
, we verify
(n,{\boldsymbol{z}})\in\mathcal{P}_{\square}(\Lambda)
iteratively across dimensions:

(1)

First iteration:
Check if
(n,z_{1})\in\mathcal{P}_{\square}(\Lambda_{[1]})
.

(2)

Subsequent iterations:
For
j=2,\dots,d
, verify if
(n,{\boldsymbol{z}}_{[j]})\in\mathcal{P}_{\square}(\Lambda_{[j]})
, assuming that
(n,{\boldsymbol{z}}_{[j-1]})\in\mathcal{P}_{\square}(\Lambda_{[j-1]})
.

Efficient Admissibility Checks

Each iteration involves verifying
(n,{\boldsymbol{z}}_{[j]})
for
\Lambda_{[j]}
.
In the first iteration, this amounts to:

•

for
\square=0

7.
Conclusion

In this paper, we have demonstrated the equivalence of various reconstruction plans for rank-1 lattices under specific conditions pertaining to lower sets in Chebyshev spaces. We introduced a heuristic component-by-component (CBC) algorithm that effectively optimizes the selection of generating vectors and the number of nodes, achieving significant improvements in both memory usage and computational efficiency, backed by numerical evidence. These advancements pave the way for more efficient and scalable approaches in the integration and approximation of multivariate functions, with potential applications extending to diverse fields such as machine learning and engineering.

Appendix A
Lower sets

A.1.
Sum of Lower Sets

It is evident that the lower set structure is preserved under union and intersection. Additionally, we have the following property:

Proposition A.1
.

Let
\Lambda,\Lambda^{\prime}\subset\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}
be lower sets. Then the sum

\Lambda\oplus\Lambda^{\prime}=\{\boldsymbol{h}+\boldsymbol{k}:\boldsymbol{h}\in\Lambda,\boldsymbol{k}\in\Lambda^{\prime}\}

is also a lower set, and

\mathcal{M}(\Lambda\oplus\Lambda^{\prime})=\mathcal{M}(\Lambda)\oplus\mathcal{M}(\Lambda^{\prime}).

Proof.

First, observe that
\mathcal{B}_{\boldsymbol{h}}\oplus\mathcal{B}_{\boldsymbol{k}}=\mathcal{B}_{\boldsymbol{h}+\boldsymbol{k}}
. The operation
\oplus
distributes over
\cup
, so we have

\Lambda\oplus\Lambda^{\prime}=\left(\bigcup_{\boldsymbol{h}\in\Lambda}\mathcal{B}_{\boldsymbol{h}}\right)\oplus\left(\bigcup_{\boldsymbol{k}\in\Lambda^{\prime}}\mathcal{B}_{\boldsymbol{k}}\right)=\bigcup_{\boldsymbol{h}\in\Lambda,\boldsymbol{k}\in\Lambda^{\prime}}\mathcal{B}_{\boldsymbol{h}+\boldsymbol{k}},

which is indeed a lower set.

Now consider the mirrored sets. For any
\boldsymbol{h}+\boldsymbol{k}\in\Lambda\oplus\Lambda^{\prime}
and
s\in\{-1,1\}^{d}
, the multi-index

\boldsymbol{\ell}=s\otimes(\boldsymbol{h}+\boldsymbol{k})=\big(s_{1}(h_{1}+k_{1}),\dots,s_{d}(h_{d}+k_{d})\big)

satisfies
\boldsymbol{\ell}=(s\otimes\boldsymbol{h})+(s\otimes\boldsymbol{k})\in\mathcal{M}(\Lambda)\oplus\mathcal{M}(\Lambda^{\prime})
. Thus,
\mathcal{M}(\Lambda\oplus\Lambda^{\prime})\subset\mathcal{M}(\Lambda)\oplus\mathcal{M}(\Lambda^{\prime})
.

Conversely, let
\boldsymbol{\ell}\in\mathcal{M}(\Lambda)
and
\boldsymbol{\ell}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{M}(\Lambda^{\prime})
. Define
\boldsymbol{h},\boldsymbol{h}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}
as follows:

•

If
\mathrm{sign}(\ell_{j})=\mathrm{sign}(\ell_{j}^{\prime})
, set
h_{j}=|\ell_{j}|
and
h_{j}^{\prime}=|\ell_{j}^{\prime}|
.

•

Otherwise, set
h_{j}=|\ell_{j}+\ell_{j}^{\prime}|
and
h_{j}^{\prime}=0
if
|\ell_{j}|\geq|\ell_{j}^{\prime}|
, or
h_{j}=0
and
h_{j}^{\prime}=|\ell_{j}+\ell_{j}^{\prime}|
if
|\ell_{j}|<|\ell_{j}^{\prime}|
.

By construction,
|\boldsymbol{\ell}+\boldsymbol{\ell}^{\prime}|=\boldsymbol{h}+\boldsymbol{h}^{\prime}
, with
\boldsymbol{h}\leq|\boldsymbol{\ell}|
and
\boldsymbol{h}^{\prime}\leq|\boldsymbol{\ell}^{\prime}|
. Since
\Lambda
and
\Lambda^{\prime}
are lower sets,
\boldsymbol{h}\in\Lambda
and
\boldsymbol{h}^{\prime}\in\Lambda^{\prime}
, implying
|\boldsymbol{\ell}+\boldsymbol{\ell}^{\prime}|\in\Lambda\oplus\Lambda^{\prime}
. Thus,
\boldsymbol{\ell}+\boldsymbol{\ell}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{M}(\Lambda\oplus\Lambda^{\prime})
, completing the proof.
∎

The proof additionally shows that the maximal elements satisfy:

\mathcal{E}_{\leq}(\Lambda\oplus\Lambda^{\prime})\subset\mathcal{E}_{\leq}(\Lambda)\oplus\mathcal{E}_{\leq}(\Lambda^{\prime}).

Sum of Specific Types of Sets

The sum is straightforward for block-type sets:

\mathcal{B}_{\boldsymbol{h}}\oplus\mathcal{B}_{\boldsymbol{k}}=\mathcal{B}_{\boldsymbol{h}+\boldsymbol{k}}.

For cross-type sets, the sum is given by:

\mathcal{C}_{\boldsymbol{h}}\oplus\mathcal{C}_{\boldsymbol{k}}=\bigcup_{1\leq i,j\leq d}\mathcal{B}_{h_{i}\boldsymbol{e}_{i}+k_{j}\boldsymbol{e}_{j}}.

For simplex-type sets, we have:

\mathcal{S}_{\boldsymbol{w},u_{1}+u_{2}-\max_{i}(w_{i})}\subset\mathcal{S}_{\boldsymbol{w},u_{1}}\oplus\mathcal{S}_{\boldsymbol{w},u_{2}}\subset\mathcal{S}_{\boldsymbol{w},u_{1}+u_{2}}.

The inclusion
\mathcal{S}_{\boldsymbol{w},u_{1}+u_{2}}\supset\mathcal{S}_{\boldsymbol{w},u_{1}}\oplus\mathcal{S}_{\boldsymbol{w},u_{2}}
is obvious. For the other inclusion, let
\boldsymbol{h}\in\mathcal{S}_{\boldsymbol{w},u_{1}+u_{2}-\max_{i}(w_{i})}
. Then

\boldsymbol{h}
belongs to either

\mathcal{S}_{\boldsymbol{w},u_{1}}
or

\mathcal{S}_{\boldsymbol{w},u_{2}}
, or

\langle\boldsymbol{h},\boldsymbol{w}\rangle:=\sum_{j=1}^{d}h_{j}w_{j}>\ \max(u_{1},u_{2})
.
In the latter case, we decompose
\boldsymbol{h}=\boldsymbol{k}+(\boldsymbol{h}-\boldsymbol{k})
, where
\boldsymbol{k}
is chosen s.t.
\boldsymbol{k}\leq\boldsymbol{h}
and
\langle\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{w}\rangle
is closest to
u_{1}
from below. Using
\langle\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{w}\rangle>u_{1}-\max_{i}(w_{i})
, we get
\langle\boldsymbol{h}-\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{w}\rangle\leq u_{2}
. This reasoning also gives:

\mathcal{S}_{\boldsymbol{1},k_{1}}\oplus\mathcal{S}_{\boldsymbol{1},k_{2}}=\mathcal{S}_{\boldsymbol{1},k_{1}+k_{2}},\quad\forall k_{1},k_{2}\in\mathbb{N}.

A.2.
{\mathcal{M}}(\Lambda)
is Smaller than
\Lambda\oplus\Lambda

Proposition A.2
.

Let
\Lambda\subset\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}
be a finite lower set. Then:

\#({\mathcal{M}}(\Lambda))=\sum_{\boldsymbol{k}\in\Lambda}2^{|\boldsymbol{k}|_{0}}\leq\#(\Lambda\oplus\Lambda),

where equality holds if and only if
\Lambda=\mathcal{B}_{\boldsymbol{k}}
for some
\boldsymbol{k}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}
.

Proof.

For a multi-index
\boldsymbol{k}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}
, let
\mathrm{supp}(\boldsymbol{k})
denote the support of
\boldsymbol{k}
, defined as the subset of
\{1,\dots,d\}
where
k_{j}\neq 0
. Define
\mathcal{D}_{\boldsymbol{k}}
as the set of
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}\in\{0,1\}^{d}
such that
\mathrm{supp}(\boldsymbol{\varepsilon})\subset\mathrm{supp}(\boldsymbol{k})
.

We define the set-valued map

F:\boldsymbol{k}\longmapsto\{2\boldsymbol{k}-\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}\}_{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}\in\mathcal{D}_{\boldsymbol{k}}}.

This map satisfies
F(\boldsymbol{k})\cap F(\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime})=\emptyset
for
\boldsymbol{k}\neq\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}
. To verify this, suppose
F(\boldsymbol{k})\cap F(\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime})\neq\emptyset
. Then there exist
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}\in\mathcal{D}_{\boldsymbol{k}}
and
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{D}_{\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}}
such that

2\boldsymbol{k}-\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}=2\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}-\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}^{\prime}.

This implies
2(\boldsymbol{k}-\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime})=\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}-\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}^{\prime}
, which is possible only if
\boldsymbol{k}=\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}
, a contradiction.

For any
\boldsymbol{k}\in\Lambda
and any
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}\in\mathcal{D}_{\boldsymbol{k}}
, we have

2\boldsymbol{k}-\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}=\boldsymbol{k}+(\boldsymbol{k}-\boldsymbol{\varepsilon})\in\Lambda\oplus\Lambda.

Thus

\bigcup_{\boldsymbol{k}\in\Lambda}F(\boldsymbol{k})\subset\Lambda\oplus\Lambda.

Combining this with the disjoint property of
F(\boldsymbol{k})
, we obtain:

\#(\Lambda\oplus\Lambda)\geq\sum_{\boldsymbol{k}\in\Lambda}\#\{2\boldsymbol{k}-\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}\}_{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}\in\mathcal{D}_{\boldsymbol{k}}}=\sum_{\boldsymbol{k}\in\Lambda}\#\mathcal{D}_{\boldsymbol{k}}=\sum_{\boldsymbol{k}\in\Lambda}2^{|\boldsymbol{k}|_{0}}=\#({\mathcal{M}}(\Lambda)).

To achieve equality, note that

F(\Lambda):=\bigcup_{\boldsymbol{k}\in\Lambda}F(\boldsymbol{k})=\bigcup_{\boldsymbol{k}\in\Lambda}\mathcal{B}_{2\boldsymbol{k}}=\bigcup_{\boldsymbol{k}\in\Lambda\text{ maximal }}\mathcal{B}_{2\boldsymbol{k}}.

On the other hand

\Lambda\oplus\Lambda=\bigcup_{\boldsymbol{h},\boldsymbol{k}\in\Lambda}\mathcal{B}_{\boldsymbol{h}+\boldsymbol{k}}=\bigcup_{\boldsymbol{h},\boldsymbol{k}\in\Lambda\text{ maximal }}\mathcal{B}_{\boldsymbol{h}+\boldsymbol{k}}.

Equality holds if and only if
\Lambda
is of block type, i.e.,
\Lambda=\mathcal{B}_{\boldsymbol{k}}
for some
\boldsymbol{k}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}
, where

F(\mathcal{B}_{\boldsymbol{k}})=\mathcal{B}_{2\boldsymbol{k}}=\mathcal{B}_{\boldsymbol{k}}\oplus\mathcal{B}_{\boldsymbol{k}}.

In general, it seems unlikely that for all pairs
({\boldsymbol{i}},{\boldsymbol{j}})
of maximal elements
of
\Lambda
, one has
{\boldsymbol{i}}+{\boldsymbol{j}}\subset F(\Lambda)
. We give a
proof to this observation.

We let
\Lambda
be lower not of block-type and assume

\Lambda\oplus\Lambda=F(\Lambda)
. Let

{\boldsymbol{i}}^{(1)},\dots,{\boldsymbol{i}}^{(m)}
be its pairwise different maximal elements
ordered in lexicographical order,
{\boldsymbol{h}}\leq_{L}{\boldsymbol{k}}
if and only if

h_{1}<k_{1}
or (
h_{1}=k_{1}
and
\widehat{\boldsymbol{h}}\leq_{L}\widehat{\boldsymbol{k}}
)
where
\widehat{\boldsymbol{x}}=(x_{2},\dots,x_{d})
. By the assumption

\Lambda\oplus\Lambda=F(\Lambda)
, we must have

{\boldsymbol{i}}^{(m-1)}+{\boldsymbol{i}}^{(m)}\leq 2{\boldsymbol{i}}^{(n)}
for some
n\in\{1,\dots,m\}
.
We cannot have
n=m-1
or
n=m
since since then either

{\boldsymbol{i}}^{(m)}
or
{\boldsymbol{i}}^{(m-1)}
cannot be maximal elements which is in contradiction to the assumption. If
m=2
, we reach already a contradiction. We assume that

m\geq 3
and use notation
\nu={\boldsymbol{i}}^{(m-1)}
,
\mu={\boldsymbol{i}}^{(m)}
,

\lambda={\boldsymbol{i}}^{(n)}
, hence
\nu+\mu\leq 2\lambda
. Using the
lexicographical order assumption, at position
1
we must have

\lambda_{1}\leq\nu_{1}
and
\lambda_{1}\leq\mu_{1}
, hence
2\lambda_{1}\leq\nu_{1}+\mu_{1}
.
If one of the first inequalities is strict, we get
2\lambda_{1}<\nu_{1}+\mu_{1}

contradicting
\nu+\mu\leq 2\lambda
. Re-iterating this argument shows that
we must have
\lambda_{j}=\nu_{j}
and
\lambda_{j}=\mu_{j}
for all
j
.
We again reach a contradiction. The proof is complete.
∎

With minimal additional effort, we can identify a map that embeds
{\mathcal{M}}(\Lambda)
into
\Lambda\oplus\Lambda
. The map:

\boldsymbol{k}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}\mapsto 2|\boldsymbol{k}|-\mathbf{1}_{\boldsymbol{k}<\mathbf{0}}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d},

where
|\boldsymbol{k}|
is the componentwise absolute value of
\boldsymbol{k}
and
\mathbf{1}_{\boldsymbol{k}<\mathbf{0}}
is the indicator vector with ones in positions where
k_{j}<0
, achieves this embedding.

A.3.
Examples of maximal and extremal elements of lower sets

Maximal elements for block-, cross-, and simplex-type lower sets can be explicitly characterized. Specifically:

(A.1)

\begin{array}[]{c}{\mathcal{E}}_{\leq}({\mathcal{B}}_{\boldsymbol{k}})=\big\{{\boldsymbol{k}}\big\},\quad{\mathcal{E}}_{\leq}({\mathcal{C}}_{\boldsymbol{k}})=\big\{k_{j}{\boldsymbol{e}}_{j}:j\penalty\ s.t.\penalty\ k_{j}\geq 1\big\},\\
{\mathcal{E}}_{\leq}({\mathcal{S}}_{{\boldsymbol{w}},u})=\left\{{\boldsymbol{h}}:u-\min_{i}(w_{i})<\sum_{j=1}^{d}w_{j}h_{j}\leq u\right\}.\end{array}

We now turn to extremal elements. For any
\Lambda
lower:

(A.2)

{\mathcal{E}}(\Lambda)=\{{\boldsymbol{0}}\}\cup{\mathcal{E}}^{(1)}(\Lambda)\cup{\mathcal{E}}^{(2)}(\Lambda),

where
{\mathcal{E}}^{(1)}(\Lambda)
consists of elements with exactly one non-zero component, and
{\mathcal{E}}^{(2)}(\Lambda)
consists of elements with at least two non-zero components. Specifically:

{\mathcal{E}}^{(1)}(\Lambda)=\{m_{j}\boldsymbol{e}_{j}:j\in\mathrm{supp}(\Lambda)\},\quad m_{j}=\max_{\boldsymbol{k}\in\Lambda}(k_{j}),

where
\boldsymbol{e}_{j}
are unit multi-indices. The identification of
{\mathcal{E}}^{(2)}(\Lambda)
is less direct.

For block- and cross-type sets, extremal elements are straightforward:

(A.3)

\begin{array}[]{c}{\mathcal{E}}({\mathcal{B}}_{\boldsymbol{k}})=\big\{{\boldsymbol{h}}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}:h_{j}=0\penalty\ {\rm or}\penalty\ h_{j}=k_{j}\big\},\quad\quad{\mathcal{E}}({\mathcal{C}}_{\boldsymbol{k}})=\big\{{\boldsymbol{0}}\big\}\cup{\mathcal{E}}_{\leq}({\mathcal{C}}_{\boldsymbol{k}}),\end{array}

and have cardinalities
2^{s}
and
s+1
respectively where

s=\#\mathrm{supp}(\boldsymbol{k})
.

For sets of simplex type,

{\mathcal{E}}^{(1)}({\mathcal{S}}_{{\boldsymbol{w}},u})=\{\lfloor u/w_{j}\rfloor{\boldsymbol{e}}_{j}:j\penalty\ s.t.\penalty\ w_{j}\leq u\}
.
The characterization of
{\mathcal{E}}^{(2)}({\mathcal{S}}_{\boldsymbol{w},u})
is more nuanced, but it satisfies the following inclusion for
{\boldsymbol{h}}\in{\mathcal{E}}^{(2)}({\mathcal{S}}_{{\boldsymbol{w}},u})
:

(A.4)

u-\delta<\sum_{j=1}^{d}w_{j}h_{j}\leq u,\quad\quad\quad\delta=\max_{i}(w_{i})-\min_{i}(w_{i}).

Indeed, if
{\boldsymbol{h}}\in\mathbb{N}^{d}
has two non-zero components, say
h_{i},h_{j}\geq 1
,
we can write
{\boldsymbol{h}}={\boldsymbol{k}}+{\boldsymbol{k}}^{\prime}
where
{\boldsymbol{k}}={\boldsymbol{h}}+({\boldsymbol{e}}_{i}-{\boldsymbol{e}}_{j})
and
{\boldsymbol{k}}^{\prime}={\boldsymbol{h}}+({\boldsymbol{e}}_{j}-{\boldsymbol{e}}_{i})
.
We have that
{\boldsymbol{k}}\neq{\boldsymbol{k}}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{N}^{d}
and

\langle{\boldsymbol{w}},{\boldsymbol{k}}\rangle=\langle{\boldsymbol{w}},{\boldsymbol{h}}\rangle+(w_{i}-w_{j})
,

\langle{\boldsymbol{w}},{\boldsymbol{k}}^{\prime}\rangle=\langle{\boldsymbol{w}},{\boldsymbol{h}}\rangle+(w_{j}-w_{i})
. In particular, if

\langle{\boldsymbol{w}},{\boldsymbol{h}}\rangle\leq u-\delta
, then
\langle{\boldsymbol{w}},{\boldsymbol{k}}\rangle,\langle{\boldsymbol{w}},{\boldsymbol{k}}^{\prime}\rangle\leq u
,
implying that
{\boldsymbol{h}}
is not extremal in
{\mathcal{S}}_{{\boldsymbol{w}},u}
. We note that

\delta
in (
A.4
) can be refined to
\delta_{\boldsymbol{h}}=\min_{i\neq j}(|w_{i}-w_{j}|)
with minimization over
i,j\in\mathrm{supp}(\boldsymbol{h})
.

The above discussions applied to isotropic simplex-type set,
for which
{\rm min}_{i}(w_{i})=1
and
\delta=0
, implies

(A.5)

{\mathcal{E}}_{\leq}({\mathcal{S}}_{{\boldsymbol{1}},k})=\{{\boldsymbol{h}}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}:\sum_{j=1}^{d}h_{j}=k\},

(A.6)

{\mathcal{E}}({\mathcal{S}}_{{\boldsymbol{1}},k})=\{{\boldsymbol{0}}\}\cup{\mathcal{E}}^{(1)}({\mathcal{S}}_{{\boldsymbol{w}},u})=\{{\boldsymbol{0}}\}\cup\{k{\boldsymbol{e}}_{j}:j=1,\dots,d\}.

A.4.
Necessary Condition for Plan A Admissibility of Cross-Type Sets

Proposition A.3
.

Let
{\boldsymbol{k}}=(k_{1},k_{2})\in\mathbb{N}^{2}
and
n\leq\#(\mathcal{B}_{\boldsymbol{k}})=(k_{1}+1)(k_{2}+1)
. Then, for any
{\boldsymbol{z}}\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
, the admissibility condition

{\boldsymbol{h}}\cdot{\boldsymbol{z}}\not\equiv_{n}{\boldsymbol{h}}^{\prime}\cdot{\boldsymbol{z}},\quad\forall{\boldsymbol{h}}\neq{\boldsymbol{h}}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{C}_{\boldsymbol{k}}),

cannot hold.

Proof.

For any
{\boldsymbol{z}}=(z_{1},z_{2})
, consider the set:

\mathcal{S}=\{{\boldsymbol{h}}\cdot{\boldsymbol{z}}\mod n:{\boldsymbol{h}}\in\mathcal{B}_{{\boldsymbol{k}}}\}.

If
n<\#(\mathcal{B}_{{\boldsymbol{k}}})
, by the pigeonhole principle, there must exist distinct
{\boldsymbol{h}},{\boldsymbol{h}}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{B}_{{\boldsymbol{k}}}
such that

{\boldsymbol{h}}\cdot{\boldsymbol{z}}\equiv_{n}{\boldsymbol{h}}^{\prime}\cdot{\boldsymbol{z}}.

This implies:

{\boldsymbol{l}}^{\prime}\cdot{\boldsymbol{z}}\equiv_{n}{\boldsymbol{l}}\cdot{\boldsymbol{z}},

where
{\boldsymbol{l}}^{\prime}=(h_{1}^{\prime}-h_{1},0)
and
{\boldsymbol{l}}=(0,h_{2}-h_{2}^{\prime})
, with
{\boldsymbol{l}}\neq{\boldsymbol{l}}^{\prime}
and
{\boldsymbol{l}},{\boldsymbol{l}}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{C}_{{\boldsymbol{k}}})
. Thus, the admissibility condition fails.

Now consider the case
n=\#(\mathcal{B}_{{\boldsymbol{k}}})
. Either we encounter the same situation as above, or all the residues
0,\dots,n-1
are taken by
({\boldsymbol{h}}\cdot{\boldsymbol{z}}\mod n)
for
{\boldsymbol{h}}\in\mathcal{B}_{{\boldsymbol{k}}}
. In this case, there exists
(a,b)\in\mathcal{B}_{{\boldsymbol{k}}}
such that:

az_{1}+bz_{2}\equiv_{n}1.

Moreover, there exist
{\boldsymbol{h}},{\boldsymbol{h}}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{B}_{{\boldsymbol{k}}}
such that

h_{1}z_{1}+h_{2}z_{2}\equiv_{n}(k_{1}+1)z_{1}\text{ and }h_{1}^{\prime}z_{1}+h_{2}^{\prime}z_{2}\equiv_{n}(k_{2}+1)z_{2}.

These equations can be reformulated as

h_{2}z_{2}\equiv_{n}(k_{1}+1-h_{1})z_{1}\text{ and }h_{1}^{\prime}z_{1}\equiv_{n}(k_{2}+1-h_{2}^{\prime})z_{2}.

•

If
1\leq h_{1}\leq k_{1}
:
We have

{\boldsymbol{l}}\cdot{\boldsymbol{z}}\equiv_{n}{\boldsymbol{l}}^{\prime}\cdot{\boldsymbol{z}},

where
{\boldsymbol{l}}=(k_{1}+1-h_{1},0)
and
{\boldsymbol{l}}^{\prime}=(0,h_{2})
, with
{\boldsymbol{l}}\neq{\boldsymbol{l}}^{\prime}
and
{\boldsymbol{l}},{\boldsymbol{l}}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{C}_{{\boldsymbol{k}}})
. Thus, admissibility fails.

•

If
1\leq h_{2}^{\prime}\leq k_{2}
:
Similarly, we have

{\boldsymbol{l}}\cdot{\boldsymbol{z}}\equiv_{n}{\boldsymbol{l}}^{\prime}\cdot{\boldsymbol{z}},

where
{\boldsymbol{l}}=(h_{1}^{\prime},0)
and
{\boldsymbol{l}}^{\prime}=(0,k_{2}+1-h_{2}^{\prime})
, with
{\boldsymbol{l}}\neq{\boldsymbol{l}}^{\prime}
and
{\boldsymbol{l}},{\boldsymbol{l}}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{C}_{{\boldsymbol{k}}})
. Again, admissibility fails.

•

If
h_{1}=h_{2}^{\prime}=0
:
In this case, the equations become:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}h_{2}z_{2}\equiv_{n}(k_{1}+1)z_{1},\\
h_{1}^{\prime}z_{1}\equiv_{n}(k_{2}+1)z_{2}.\end{array}\right.

Multiplying these equations by
(k_{2}+1)
and
(k_{1}+1)
, respectively, gives:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}h_{2}h_{1}^{\prime}z_{1}\equiv_{n}nz_{1}\equiv_{n}0,\\
h_{2}h_{1}^{\prime}z_{2}\equiv_{n}nz_{2}\equiv_{n}0.\end{array}\right.

Combining these with
az_{1}+bz_{2}\equiv_{n}1
, we obtain

h_{2}h_{1}^{\prime}\equiv_{n}h_{2}h_{1}^{\prime}(az_{1}+bz_{2})\equiv_{n}0.

This implies that
n
divides
h_{2}h_{1}^{\prime}
. Since
n=(k_{1}+1)(k_{2}+1)
and
|h_{2}h_{1}^{\prime}|\leq k_{2}k_{1}
, it follows that
h_{2}=0
or
h_{1}^{\prime}=0
. Substituting back, we find

{\boldsymbol{l}}^{\prime}\cdot{\boldsymbol{z}}\equiv_{n}{\boldsymbol{l}}\cdot{\boldsymbol{z}},

where
{\boldsymbol{l}}=(-1,0)
,
{\boldsymbol{l}}^{\prime}=(k_{1},0)
, or
{\boldsymbol{l}}=(0,-1)
,
{\boldsymbol{l}}^{\prime}=(0,k_{2})
. In both cases,
{\boldsymbol{l}}\neq{\boldsymbol{l}}^{\prime}
and
{\boldsymbol{l}},{\boldsymbol{l}}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{C}_{{\boldsymbol{k}}})
, violating admissibility.

Thus, we conclude that the admissibility condition cannot hold for
n\leq\#(\mathcal{B}_{{\boldsymbol{k}}})
.
∎
