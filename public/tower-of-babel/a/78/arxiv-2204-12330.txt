Abstract
. Twin-width is a recently introduced graph parameter with applications in algorithmics, combinatorics, and finite model theory. For graphs of bounded degree, finiteness of twin-width is preserved by quasi-isometry. Thus, through Cayley graphs, it defines a group invariant. We prove that groups which are abelian, hyperbolic, ordered, solvable, or with polynomial growth, have finite twin-width. Twin-width can be characterised by excluding patterns in the self-action by product of the group elements. Based on this characterisation, we propose a strengthening called uniform twin-width , which is stable under constructions such as group extensions, direct products, and direct limits. The existence of finitely generated groups with infinite twin-width is not immediate. We construct one using a result of Osajda on embeddings of graphs into groups. This implies the existence of a class of finite graphs with unbounded twin-width but containing 2 O ⁡ ( n ) ⋅ n ! 2^{O(n)}\cdot n! graphs on vertex set { 1 , … , n } \{1,\dots,n\} , settling a question asked in a previous work.

Introduction

Twin-width is an invariant of graphs and matrices introduced in
[
6

,
with applications in algorithmics, logic, enumerative combinatorics
[

Finite twin-width is known to be a group invariant of Cayley graphs

Our main result is the construction of groups with infinite twin-width,

Theorem 1.1
.

There is a finitely generated group with infinite twin-width.

This disproves the ‘small conjecture’ proposed in

,
related to the Stanley-Wilf conjecture proved by Marcus and Tardos

It is also related to the problem of finding graphs with bounded degree and infinite twin-width, see

1.1.
Growth of classes excluding substructures

Marcus and Tardos proved that any
n\times n
0–1 matrix with more than
c_{k}n
1-entries contains all
k\times k
permutation matrices as submatrices,
for an appropriate
c_{k}
function of
k
only
[
24
]
.
A major corollary is the Stanley-Wilf conjecture: For any fixed permutation
\sigma
,
the class of permutations avoiding
\sigma
as pattern has single-exponential growth—as opposed to the class of all permutations which has factorial growth.
Guillemot and Marx built on the result of Marcus and Tardos by giving a characterisation of classes of permutations which exclude some pattern
[
19
]
:
they have bounded
width
, which is defined by representing permutations with points in the plane,
iteratively
contracting
these points down to a single one
while measuring a number of
conflicts
throughout this contraction sequence,
and minimizing the number of conflicts over all choices of contraction sequence.

A natural generalisation of the Stanley-Wilf conjecture to arbitrary 0–1 matrices is the following:
Any class of matrices
\mathcal{C}
closed under taking submatrices has growth at most single-exponential or at least factorial.
This was established in
[
5
]
, by proving that classes of matrices with exponential growth
are exactly the classes with bounded
twin-width
, a natural generalisation of Guillemot and Marx’s width to graphs and matrices proposed in
[
6
]
.
Matrices with bounded twin-width are also characterised by excluding
grids of high rank
, which can be seen as a generalisation of excluding patterns.

The logical next question in this line of work is the generalisation to graphs.
One may think of graphs as essentially the same as a 0–1 matrix, through adjacency matrices.
But a major difference is that matrices have an innate order, which is lost in graphs.
For graphs, the sensible equivalent of classes with exponential growth is
small classes
:
classes of graphs which contain at most
n!\cdot c^{n}
graphs on vertices
1,\dots,n
, for some constant
c
.
The additional
n!
compensates for the choice of a permutation of the vertices.
Well known examples of small classes of graphs include trees, planar graphs,
and more generally proper minor-closed classes
[
26
]
.
These examples are generalised by the following.

Theorem 1.2

(
[
3
]
)
.

Classes of graphs with bounded twin-width are small.

With this result, and the cases of matrices and permutations in mind,
the following very natural conjecture was raised.

Conjecture 1.3

(Small conjecture
[
3
]
)
.

A class of graphs closed under induced subgraphs is small if and only if it has bounded twin-width.

We disprove
1.3
using an approach suggested in
[
3
]
:
if
G
is a Cayley graph of some group
\Gamma
,
then the class of finite subgraphs of
G
is small
[
3
, Lemma 8.1]
.
If
\Gamma
is a group with infinite twin-width, then this class furthermore has unbounded twin-width,
hence is a counterexample to
1.3
.
Thus
Theorem

1.1
gives:

Corollary 1.4
.

There is a small class of graphs closed under induced subgraphs and with unbounded twin-width.

1.2.
Properties of twin-width of groups

We prove that for bounded degree graphs, finiteness of twin-width is preserved by
quasi-isometries
,
i.e. maps which preserve distances up to some affine lower and upper bounds,
and more generally by
coarse
and
regular
embeddings.
This is a generalisation of
[
3
, Lemma 8.2]
,
which proved that finite twin-width is a group invariant of Cayley graphs:
given a group
\Gamma
, all Cayley graphs of
\Gamma
are quasi-isometric,
hence either all or none of them have finite twin-width.
In relation with coarse geometry, we remark that Gromov-hyperbolic groups have finite twin-width,
because they embed into finite products of trees with bounded degree
[
7
]
.

Groups with finite twin-width have a remarkable second characterisation:
a group
\Gamma
has finite twin-width if and only if there is a total order
<
on
\Gamma

such that for any
x\in\Gamma
, the action of
x
on
(\Gamma,<)
by product
is a permutation with finite
width
(in the sense of Guillemot and Marx
[
19
]
),
or equivalently a permutation which avoids some pattern.
For example, if
<
is a right-invariant order on
\Gamma
,
the action of any
x
is an increasing permutation,
i.e. a permutation which excludes a decreasing sequence of length 2 as pattern.
This characterisation extends to non-finitely generated groups,
and also suggests a natural strengthening called
uniform twin-width
:
in the above statement, we require a uniform bound on the width of actions of elements of
\Gamma
,
or equivalently require all these actions to avoid the same pattern.
Having finite uniform twin-width implies finite twin-width, while the converse does not a priori hold.

Uniform twin-width is preserved by a number of group operations.

Lemma 1.5

(
Lemmas

6.5
,
6.14
,
6.2
,
6.15
and
6.13
in the text)
.

The following constructions preserve finiteness of uniform twin-width:

(1)

Group extensions (and thus Cartesian or semidirect products);

(2)

Infinite direct products and direct sums;

(3)

Direct limits;

(4)

Taking supergroups with finite index;

(5)

Quotienting by finite subgroups;

(6)

Wreath products.

From this and a few other results, we obtain many examples of groups with finite uniform twin-width:
abelian groups, solvable groups, groups with polynomial growth, lamplighter groups, automorphism groups of trees.

We conjecture that uniform twin-width is strictly stronger than twin-width,
i.e. there are groups with finite twin-width and infinite uniform twin-width.
A candidate is the group of finitely supported permutations on
\mathbb{Z}
:
it has finite twin-width, and we think it is unlikely to have finite uniform twin-width
as this would imply a universal bound on the uniform twin-width of finite groups,
and more generally elementary amenable groups.

1.3.
Twin-width of bounded degree graphs

Bounded degree graphs are an interesting and not very well understood special case in which to consider twin-width.
It is known that graphs with bounded degree can have infinite twin-width by the counting argument of the previous section:
it is easy to verify that the class of 3-regular graphs is not small,
hence it contains finite graphs with arbitrarily large twin-width by
Theorem

1.2
—in fact, for any
k
, almost all 3-regular graphs have twin-width more than
k
.
On the other hand, no explicit or deterministic construction
for bounded degree graphs with unbounded twin-width is known.
Finding such graphs could significantly help our understanding of twin-width.
Bilu-Linial expanders
[
1
]
are an interesting example:
they are 3-regular expanders, hence seemingly complex graphs,
but with a structured construction which allows to prove that
they have twin-width at most 6
[
3
, Section 5]
.

If
\Gamma
is a group with infinite twin-width, the class of subgraphs of some Cayley graph of
\Gamma

has bounded degree and unbounded twin-width, which could solve the aforementioned problem.
Unfortunately, the group constructed in
Theorem

1.1
does not help in this regard:
its construction starts from a sequence of bounded degree graphs
with unbounded twin-width in the first place, and relies largely on probabilistic methods.
Thus the same question remains for groups:
can we find a more explicit construction of a group with infinite twin-width,
or one with a direct proof that twin-width is infinite?

1.4.
Other graph parameters for groups

This paper proposes to consider twin-width on groups.
It is natural to ask whether the same can be done with other classical graph parameters.

Tree-width—arguably the most important graph ‘width’ parameter—is a group invariant, studied by Kuske and Lohrey
[
21
]
.
Using that graphs with bounded tree-width and bounded degree can be partitioned
into parts of bounded size so that the quotient is a tree
[
10
]
,
they prove the equivalence between finite tree-width, virtually free,
context-free word problem, and the Cayley graphs having decidable monadic second-order theory.

Stack and queue number are two other graph parameters, sitting between tree-width and twin-width:
bounded tree-width implies bounded stack and queue number
[
14
,
11
]
,
either of which implies bounded twin-width
[
3
, Theorem 7.4]
.
Eppstein et al. recently proved that the 3-dimensional grid with diagonals
has infinite stack number
[
12
]
.
This is the first known explicit construction of bounded degree graphs with unbounded stack number
(probabilistic constructions were previously known
[
23
]
).
This implies that stack number is not a group invariant,
because the 3-dimensional grid
without
diagonals has finite stack number,
and both variants of the grid are Cayley graphs of
\mathbb{Z}^{3}
.

On the other hand, queue number can be shown to be a group invariant,
and a surprisingly large part of the results of this paper can be adapted to queue number.
The group of
Theorem

1.1
of course has infinite queue number,
since finite queue number implies finite twin-width.
Queue number is characterised by excluding decreasing sequences in the adjacency matrix
for some choice of vertex ordering.
This somewhat resembles the matrix characterisation of twin-width,
and allows to define a
uniform
queue number for groups.
Most operations of
Lemma

1.5
also preserve finite uniform queue number.
However, unlike for twin-width, products and extensions preserve finiteness of uniform queue number,
but not the exact bound on the latter.
Thus, we still obtain for instance that finitely generated abelian groups have finite uniform queue number,
but the proof does not give a uniform bound on the latter,
and thus does not go through direct limits to reach non-finitely generated abelian groups.
Despite this, we do not have examples of groups with finite twin-width and infinite queue number
(or similarly for the uniform variants).
Separating twin-width and queue number on sparse graphs is an open problem.

1.5.
Organisation of the paper

Section

2
summarises basic notions and notations used in this paper.

Section

3
presents the definition of twin-width, and known results used in this work.
Because this paper focuses solely on graphs of bounded degree,
we give a more restricted definition than the one of
[
6
]
,
which slightly simplifies several of these results.

Section

4
presents twin-width as a quasi-isometric invariant property of Cayley graph,
giving some basic examples, as well as examples related to geometric group theory.

Section

5
introduces a second characterisation of twin-width of groups using matrices,
and a natural strengthening: uniform twin-width.

Section

6
proves a number of stability results for (uniform) twin-width under common group operations,
and uses these results to show that several well-known classes of groups have finite twin-width.

Section

7
constructs a finitely generated group with infinite twin-width,
by embedding a well-chosen sequence of graphs of bounded degree into a group.
This construction gives a recursive presentation, with decidable word problem.
It follows that there are finitely presented groups with infinite twin-width.
This can be read independently of
Sections

5
and
6
.

Finally,
Section

8
discusses the relation of twin-width and queue number in groups.
We show that many results of this paper also hold when replacing twin-width with queue number,
and we point out the points where this replacement fails.

2.
Preliminaries and notations

2.1.
Graphs

We consider undirected graphs, without loops nor multiple edges, and which may be finite or countably infinite.
A graph is denoted as a pair
G=(V,E)
with
V
and
E
the sets of vertices and edges respectively,
and we also write
V(G)
for
V
, and
E(G)
for
E
.

If
G,H
are graphs,
H
is a
subgraph
of
G
if
V(H)\subset V(G)
and
E(H)\subset E(G)
.
It is an
induced subgraph
if
V(H)\subset V(G)
,
and
E(H)
consists exactly of the edges in
E(G)
with endpoints in
V(H)
.
A
class
of graphs is a collection of graphs which is closed under isomorphism.
A class is
hereditary
if it is also closed under taking induced subgraphs.

If
v\in V(G)
, its degree
d(v)
is the number of edges incident to
v
.
We denote by
\Delta(G)
the maximum degree of vertices in
G
.
A graph is
k
-
regular
if all its vertices have degree
k
.
A
cubic
graph is a 3-regular graph.

The
diameter
of a connected graph
G
, denoted
\textsf{diam}(G)
, is the maximum distance between two vertices of
G
.
The
girth
of a graph
G
, denoted
\textsf{girth}(G)
, is the minimum length of a cycle in
G
.

2.2.
Matrices

We consider binary, possibly infinite matrices,
and pay particular attention to the order of their rows and columns.
That is, if
(X,<_{X})
,
(Y,<_{Y})
are two totally ordered, possibly infinite sets,
an
X\times Y
binary matrix is a map
M:X\times Y\to\{0,1\}
.
Equivalently, it is a binary relation between ordered sets.
We call
X
, respectively
Y
, the set of column, respectively row, indices.
The matrix
M
is finite if
X,Y
are finite.
A
submatrix
of
M
is obtained by restricting
M
to
X^{\prime}\times Y^{\prime}
for some
X^{\prime}\subset X,Y^{\prime}\subset Y
.
By analogy with graphs, we call degree of a column or a row of a matrix the number of 1-entries it contains.

Given
f:X\to Y
, the matrix
M_{f}
of
f
is defined by
M_{f}(x,y)=1
if and only if
f(x)=y
.
We will primarily consider this construction when
f
is bijective,
in which case we call
M_{f}
a
bijection matrix
.
Bijection matrices are exactly the matrices with all rows and columns of degree 1.
In the finite case, they are more commonly called permutation matrices,
but this would be abusive in general since
X
and
Y
need not be isomorphic as orders.

Given a graph
G=(V,E)
and a total order
<
on
V
,
the adjacency matrix
A_{<}(G)
is the
(V,<)\times(V,<)
matrix
such that
A_{<}(G)(x,y)=1
if and only if
x,y
are adjacent in
G
.

2.2.1.
Divisions of matrices

If
M
is an
X\times Y
matrix, a
k\times l
-division
of
M
consists of
partitions of rows and columns into intervals;
i.e. it is given by
\mathcal{X}=\{X_{1},\dots,X_{k}\}
partition of
X
,
\mathcal{Y}=\{Y_{1},\dots,Y_{l}\}
partition of
Y
,
such that every
X_{i}
or
Y_{j}
is an interval, and with
X_{1}<_{X}\dots<_{X}X_{k}
and
Y_{1}<_{Y}\dots<_{Y}Y_{l}
.
The
zone

(i,j)
of the division
(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y})
is the submatrix
M
restricted to
X_{i}\times Y_{j}
.
The
quotient
matrix
M/(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y})
is the
\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}
matrix
with a ‘1’ at position
X_{i},Y_{j}
if and only if the corresponding zone
X_{i}\times Y_{j}
of
M

is non-zero (i.e. contains at least a ‘1’).

2.3.
Groups

Unless stated otherwise, we consider Cayley graphs, group actions, etc. on the right.
If
\Gamma
is a group and
S\subset\Gamma
a finite generating set, its Cayley graph is

\text{Cay}(\Gamma,S)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\left(\Gamma,\textstyle\left\{xy\ |\ x^{-1}y\in S\cup S^{-1}\right\}\right).

Thus edges go from
x
to
y
by multiplying on the right by a generator.

A group presentation consists of a set
S
of generators, and a set
R
of relators
which are finite words over
S\uplus S^{-1}
, where
S^{-1}
denotes formal inverses of elements of
S
.
The group associated to this presentation, denoted
\left\langle S;R\right\rangle
,
is the free group over
S
quotiented by the normal subgroup generated by
R
.
A group
G
is said to admit
\left\langle S;R\right\rangle
as presentation if it is isomorphic to
\left\langle S;R\right\rangle
.
A group is finitely presented if it admits a presentation with finite sets of generators and of relators.

For a group
\Gamma
finitely generated by
S
, the word problem is the following decision problem:
given
w
a word over
S\cup S^{-1}
, is
w
interpreted as an element of
\Gamma
equal to the neutral element?
This problem can be undecidable even for finitely presented groups.
Decidability of the word problem is well known to be independent of the choice of
S
.

2.4.
Coarse geometry

In coarse geometry, distances in metric spaces are considered up to some possibly large error.
The fundamental notion is the quasi-isometry.
For
\lambda\geqslant 0
, a map
f:X\to Y
between metric spaces is
a
\lambda
-quasi-isometric embedding
if for all
x,x^{\prime}\in X
,

\lambda^{-1}d_{X}(x,x^{\prime})-\lambda\leqslant d_{Y}\left(f(x),f(x^{\prime})\right)\leqslant\lambda d_{X}(x,x^{\prime})+\lambda

Thus quasi-isometric embeddings are maps which preserve distances up to affine lower and upper bound.
The map
f
is a
\lambda
-quasi-isometry
if it is also
\lambda
-cobounded
,
i.e. every
y\in Y
is at distance at most
\lambda
of
f(X)
.
The spaces
X
and
Y
are said to be quasi-isometric
if there is a
\lambda
-quasi-isometry
X\to Y
for some
\lambda
.
This is equivalent to having back and forth quasi-isometric embeddings between
X
and
Y

whose compositions are within a bounded distance of identity maps.
Quasi-isometry is an equivalence relation.

In a graph
G
, the vertex set
V(G)
is equipped with the shortest path distance
d_{G}(x,y)
.
This defines a metric space when
G
is connected.
Remark that this metric space is quasi-isometric to the geometric realisation of
G
,
in which one replaces each edge of
G
by a path isometric to the real segment
[0,1]
.

Given a group
\Gamma
and
S,S^{\prime}
two finite generating sets of
\Gamma
,
it is easy to verify that
\text{Cay}(\Gamma,S)
and
\text{Cay}(\Gamma,S^{\prime})
are quasi-isometric.
This is the foundation of coarse geometry on groups.

Regular embeddings
are another notion of embedding in coarse geometry,
which is particularly interesting for graphs seen as metric spaces.
A map
f:X\to Y
is a
\lambda
-regular embedding if it is
\lambda
-Lipschitz
and for all
y\in Y
,
|{f^{-1}(y)}|\leqslant\lambda
.
If all balls of
X
have cardinality bounded by a function of their radius,
then any quasi-isometric embedding
X\to Y
is also a regular embedding.
In particular, this is the case when
X
is a graph with bounded degree.

2.5.
Direct limits

Let
S
be some structure (in our case, a group or a graph),
and let
(S_{i})_{i\in I}
be a family of substructures of
S
.
The family
(S_{i})_{i\in I}
is
directed
if for all
i,j\in I
,
there exists
k\in I
with
S_{i}\cup S_{j}\subseteq S_{k}
.
The structure
S
is said to be direct limit of
(S_{i})_{i\in I}

if
(S_{i})_{i\in I}
is directed and
S=\bigcup_{i\in I}S_{i}
.
For instance, chains are a very special case of directed families,
a graph is always direct limit of all its finite induced subgraphs,
and similarly a group is direct limit of its finitely generated subgroups.

The following is a folklore lemma which we use to show that twin-width goes through direct limits.
We give a proof with ultrafilter arguments.
When the directed family is isomorphic to
\mathbb{N}
as order,
one can also prove it using Kőnig’s theorem, or an induction and the axiom of countable choice.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
S
be direct limit of
(S_{i})_{i\in I}
,
and for each
i\in I
let
<_{i}
be a total order on
S_{i}
.
Then there exists a total order
<
on
S
such that
for any finite
X\subset S
, there is some
i\in I
with
X\subseteq S_{i}

and such that
<
and
<_{i}
coincide on
X
.

Proof.

Order
I
by
i\leqslant j
if
S_{i}\subseteq S_{j}
.
For
i\in I
, define its upward closure
\uparrow\!i=\textstyle\left\{j\ |\ j\geqslant i\right\}
.
Consider the family of non-empty and eventually upward-closed subsets

\mathcal{F}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\textstyle\left\{J\subseteq I\ |\ \exists i\in I,~\uparrow\!i\subseteq J\right\}.

If
A\in\mathcal{F}
and
A\subset B
, then clearly
B\in\mathcal{F}
.
Furthermore, given
A_{1},A_{2}\in\mathcal{F}
, consider
i_{1},i_{2}
such that
\uparrow\!i_{j}\subseteq A_{j}
.
Since
(S_{i})_{i\in I}
is directed, there exists
k\in I
such that
i_{1},i_{2}\leqslant k
.
Then
\uparrow\!k\subseteq A_{1}\cap A_{2}
, and thus
A_{1}\cap A_{2}\in\mathcal{F}
.
This proves that
\mathcal{F}
is a proper filter,
hence there is an ultrafilter
\mathcal{U}
containing
\mathcal{F}
.

For
x\neq y\in S
, consider the indices which order
x
before
y
.

I_{xy}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\textstyle\left\{i\in I\ |\ x<_{i}y\right\}

The limit order on
S
in then defined as
x<y
if and only if
I_{xy}\in\mathcal{U}
.
Since
S
is direct limit of
(S_{i})_{i\in I}
, for any
x,y\in S
, there is some
i\in I
such that
x,y\in S_{i}
.
Assuming
x\neq y
, it is clear that
\uparrow\!i\subseteq I_{xy}\cup I_{yx}
:
for any
j\geqslant i
,
x
and
y
must be comparable by
<_{j}
.
Since
\mathcal{U}
is an ultrafilter, it follows that either
I_{xy}\in\mathcal{U}
or
I_{yx}\in\mathcal{U}

(but not both as they are disjoint).
Thus
<
is a total, irreflexive and antisymmetric relation.
Let us prove the condition on
<
required by the lemma—transitivity follows from it.

Given
X\subseteq S
any finite subset, there is some
i\in I
with
X\subseteq S_{i}
.
Define

A\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}(\uparrow\!i)\cap\bigcap_{\begin{subarray}{c}x,y\in X\\
x<y\end{subarray}}I_{xy}

Then
A
is a finite intersection of elements of
\mathcal{U}
,
hence
A\in\mathcal{U}
and in particular it is non-empty.
Choose
j\in A
.
Then
j\geqslant i
, hence
X\subseteq S_{j}
.
Furthermore for any
x<y
in
X
, we have
j\in I_{xy}
hence
x<_{j}y
.
Since both
<
and
<_{j}
are total, irreflexive, and antisymmetric on
X
,
this implies that they coincide on
X
as desired.
∎

3.
Twin-Width of graphs and matrices

This section recalls the definition of twin-width
[
6
]
, and some fundamental results.
We will present a simplified definition specialised for graphs of bounded degree, called
strict twin-width
.
The two notions are equivalent on graphs of bounded degree,
in the sense that they differ by at most the maximum degree of the graph.
When considering matrices, strict twin-width is closely related to the work of Guillemot and Marx
who first introduced twin-width for permutation matrices
[
19
]
.
The reader may refer to
[
6
]

3.1.
Definitions and characterisations

3.1.1.
Twin-width of graphs

Let
G=(V,E)
be a finite graph and
\mathcal{P}
be a partition of
V(G)
.
The
quotient graph

G/\mathcal{P}
has vertex set
\mathcal{P}
,
and its edges are all
XY
with
X,Y\in\mathcal{P}
such that there exists an edge

of
G
with

and

.
A

is a sequence
\mathcal{P}_{n},\dots,\mathcal{P}_{1}
of partitions of

such that
\mathcal{P}_{n}=\textstyle\left\{\{v\}\ |\ v\in V\right\}
is the partition into singletons,

\mathcal{P}_{1}=\{V\}
is the trivial partition,
and each
\mathcal{P}_{i-1}
is obtained by merging two parts of
\mathcal{P}_{i}
.
The

of this partition sequence is

\max_{i=1}^{n}\Delta(G/\mathcal{P}_{i})
.
The

of

, denoted
\stww(G)
,
is the minimum width of a partition sequence starting with

.

For a partition sequence
\mathcal{P}_{n},\dots,\mathcal{P}_{1}
,
one may also see the quotients graphs
G/\mathcal{P}_{n},\dots,G/\mathcal{P}_{1}
as obtained
by successive contractions of arbitrary pairs of vertices,
starting from
G
and ending with a single vertex left, see
Fig.

1
.
Here, we insist that contracting pairs of non-adjacent vertices is allowed.

Remark that

.
Strict twin-width is also non-increasing when taking subgraphs.

(1)

Twin-width is continuously extended to an infinite graph

3.1.4.

Given a finite graph

\gv(G)
,
is the minimum of
\gv(A_{<}(G))
over all choices of
<

Similarly to matrices, the grid number of an infinite graph
is the supremum of grid number over finite induced subgraphs,
but here an additional argument is needed to order the infinite graph.

(

For a graph

For each finite induced subgraph

.
Recall that

is direct limit of its finite induced subgraphs,

, there is an order

on

coincides with

on

for some finite induced subgraph

containing

.

We claim that

.
Indeed, suppose on the contrary that

has a

-grid in this order.
By

Lemma 3.5

Twin-width is invariant by substitution in bijection matrices

\stww(M_{f[a:=g_{a}]_{a\in A}})=\max\left(\stww(M_{f}),\max_{a\in A}\stww(M_{g_{a}})\right).

Lemma 3.6
.

Let
(I,<_{I})
be a well-ordered set of indices, and for each

(X_{i},<_{X_{i}})
,
(Y_{i},<_{Y_{i}})
two totally ordered sets, and

Consider the products
X\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\prod_{i\in I}X_{i}
,
Y\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\prod_{i\in I}Y_{i}
.
They are totally ordered by lexicographic order, i.e. for

Let

\stww(M_{f})=\sup_{i\in I}\stww(M_{f_{i}}).

Proof.

Let

be a finite submatrix of

on rows

and columns

.
It suffices to bound
\stww(M^{\prime})
by the right-hand side of the lemma.
For each pair of distinct elements of

and let

Define

Z^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Z}_{i}
is a neighbour of
Y^{\prime}

if there exists
y\in Y^{\prime}
with
g(y)\in Z^{\prime}
.
Again, the number of neighbours of
Y^{\prime}
in
\mathcal{Z}_{i}
is bounded by the degree of
Y^{\prime}
in
M/(\mathcal{R}_{i},\mathcal{C}_{i})
.
Now, if
Z^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Z}_{i}
is such that there exists
x\in X^{\prime}
with
g(f(x))\in Z^{\prime}
,
then
Z^{\prime}
is a neighbour in
\mathcal{Z}_{i}
of a neighbour in
\mathcal{C}_{i}
of
X^{\prime}
.
It follows that the degree of
X^{\prime}
in
M_{\tau\circ\sigma}/(\mathcal{X}_{i},\mathcal{Z}_{i})

is bounded by the square of the maximum degree in
M/(\mathcal{R}_{i},\mathcal{C}_{i})
, which is still
2^{O(t)}
.
The same holds for the degree of a part
Z^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Z}_{i}
.

Thus we have constructed a division sequence for
M_{\tau\circ\sigma}
of width
2^{O(t)}
.
∎

The former proof could be adapted to show that the composition of
k

bijection matrices of twin-width
t
has twin-width
2^{O(tk)}
,
instead of the tower of exponentials obtained repetitively applying the former lemma.

4.
Twin-width as a geometric group invariant

In
[
3
, Lemma 8.2]
, we already proved finite twin-width is a group invariant.
This can be stated as a more general result on regular embeddings.

Lemma 4.1
.

If
f:H\to G
is a
\lambda
-regular embedding, then

\stww(H)\leqslant\lambda\stww(G)^{\lambda}.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{P}=\textstyle\left\{f^{-1}(v)\ |\ v\in V(G)\right\}
be the preimage partition of
V(H)
.
Since
f
is
\lambda
-regular, parts of
\mathcal{P}
have size at most
\lambda
.
One can see
f
as a map
f:H/\mathcal{P}\to G
, which is injective and
\lambda
-Lipschitz.
This implies that
H/\mathcal{P}
is isomorphic to a subgraph of
G^{(\lambda)}
.
Then, by
Lemmas

3.9
and
3.10
,

\stww(H)\leqslant\lambda\stww(H/\mathcal{P})\leqslant\lambda\stww(G)^{\lambda}.

∎

Since quasi-isometries between graphs of bounded degree are regular embeddings,
it follows that having finite twin-width is invariant under quasi-isometry for graphs of bounded degree.
In particular, whether or not a Cayley graph
\text{Cay}(\Gamma,S)
has finite twin-width
depends only on the group
\Gamma
, not on the generating set
S
.
This defines the notion of finite twin-width for finitely generated groups.

Let us give some simple examples.

(1)

Finite groups trivially have finite twin-width.

(2)

It is easy to see that the strict twin-width of a tree is exactly its maximum degree.
Thus, the free group with
k
generators has finite twin-width, since it admits the
k
-regular tree as Cayley graph.

(3)

A cartesian product of groups corresponds to the cartesian product of Cayley graphs,
which is known to preserve finite strict twin-width (see
[
3
, Theorem 7.2]
).

(4)

By (
1
) and (
2
), all cyclic groups have finite twin-width,
and thus by (
3
), all finitely generated abelian groups have finite twin-width.

Finite twin-width being closed under quasi-isometry,
it is natural to wonder if other well-known properties of groups preserved by quasi-isometry imply finite twin-width.
Hyperbolicity in the sense of Gromov
[
17
]

is arguably the most important quasi-isometric invariant of groups.
By a remarkable result of Buyalo, Dranishnikov, and Schroeder,
hyperbolic groups—and more generally, hyperbolic graphs of bounded degree—are quasi-isometric to finite Cartesian products of trees of bounded degree
[
7
]
.
Since trees of bounded degree have finite twin-width, and Cartesian products preserve it, we obtain the following.

Proposition 4.2
.

Gromov-hyperbolic groups and graphs of bounded degree have finite twin-width.

5.
Matrix characterisation

In this section, we give a second characterisation of twin-width through matrices.
This allows to generalise it beyond finitely generated groups,
and suggests a natural strengthening, called
uniform twin-width
.

5.1.
Twin-Width of group actions

Let
\Gamma
be a group acting on
X
by
\phi:\Gamma\to\Perm_{X}
,
where
\Perm_{X}
is the group of permutations on
X
.
Given a total order
<
on
X
and
g\in\Gamma
,
the (strict) twin-width of

acting on
(X,<)
is

\stww_{(X,<)}(\phi(g))\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\stww\left(M_{\phi(g)}\right),

where the
X\times X
bijection matrix
M_{\phi(g)}
is ordered according to
<
.

The action of
\Gamma
through
\phi
is said to have finite twin-width with regards to
<

if
\stww_{(X,<)}(\phi(g))
is finite for every
g\in\Gamma
—but possibly arbitrarily large.
This action is said to have finite twin-width
it has finite twin-width with regards to some total order on
X
.
Finite grid number for a group action is defined similarly.

Lemma 5.1
.

For a finitely generated group
\Gamma
, the following are equivalent.

(1)

Some (equivalently, every) Cayley graph of
\Gamma

has finite twin-width (equivalently, finite grid number).

(2)

The action of
\Gamma
on itself by right product
has finite twin-width (equivalently, finite grid number).

Proof.

In the statement, equivalences of finite twin-width and finite grid number
are from
Theorems

3.2
and
3.4
.
For
x\in\Gamma
and
<
a total order on
\Gamma
,
we denote by
M^{<}_{x}
the matrix of the action of
x
on
(\Gamma,<)
by right product.

Suppose that the action of
\Gamma
on itself has finite grid number,
witnessed by some total order
<
on
\Gamma
.
Let us prove that
\text{Cay}(\Gamma,S)
has finite grid number for an arbitrary finite generating set
S
.
Let
M
be the adjacency matrix of
\text{Cay}(\Gamma,S)
with regards to
<
.
Then
M
is the superposition

M=\biglor_{s\in S\cup S^{-1}}M^{<}_{s}

Any single
M^{<}_{x}
has finite grid number by hypothesis, and
S\cup S^{-1}
is finite,
thus it follows from
Lemma

3.7
that
M
has finite grid number.

Conversely, assume that
\text{Cay}(\Gamma,S)
has finite grid number for some finite generating set
S
.
This is witnessed by a total order
<
on
\Gamma
such that
the adjacency matrix
M
of
\text{Cay}(\Gamma,S)
with regards to
<
has finite twin-width.
For any
s\in S\cup S^{-1}
, one easily verifies that
M^{<}_{s}
is contained in

,
in the sense that
M^{<}_{s}(x,y)=1
implies
M(x,y)=1
.
It follows that
M^{<}_{s}
has finite twin-width.
If
x\in\Gamma
is now an arbitrary element,
x
can be decomposed as some product
x=s_{1}\dots s_{n}

with
s_{i}\in S\cup S^{-1}
, and the action of
x
is the composition of the actions of
s_{1},\dots,s_{n}
.
Since all
M^{<}_{s_{i}}
have finite twin-width,
it follows from
Lemma

3.11
that
M^{<}_{x}
has finite twin-width.
∎

Thus we can take as alternative definition that
\Gamma
has finite twin-width
if its action on itself by right product has finite twin-width.
This definition immediately generalises to non-finitely generated groups.

5.2.
Uniform twin-width

There is a natural strengthening of the former characterisation.
Consider an action of
\Gamma
on
X
given by
\phi:\Gamma\to\Perm_{X}
.
Given a total order
<
on
X
, the
uniform twin-width
of
\phi
with regards to
<
is

\utww_{(X,<)}(\phi)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\sup_{g\in\Gamma}\stww_{(X,<)}(\phi(g)).

Then, the uniform twin-width of
\phi
, denoted by
\utww(\phi)
,
is the minimum of
\utww_{(X,<)}(\phi)
over all choices of
<
total order on
X
.
The uniform twin-width of a group
\Gamma
, denoted
\utww(\Gamma)
,
is defined as the uniform twin-width of its action on itself by right product.

Unlike twin-width, the uniform twin-width of a group or group action is a well defined number (or infinity).
For some fixed ordering, having finite uniform twin-width
requires a uniform bound on the twin-width of the actions of each element,
whereas having finite twin-width only requires the twin-width of each of these actions to be finite.
Thus finite uniform twin-width implies finite twin-width, while the converse is not a priori true.

5.3.
Examples

Let us refine the examples of
Section

4
.
A
right-invariant
order on a group
\Gamma
is a total order
<

such that for all
x,y,z\in\Gamma
,
x<y
implies
xz<yz
.
A group is
(right-)orderable
if it admits a right-invariant total order.
Groups of finite twin-width can be seen as a vast generalisation of orderable groups.
Indeed, if
<
is a right-invariant order on
\Gamma
,
then the action of any
x\in\Gamma
by right product is monotone,
hence its matrix has strict twin-width 2 (the minimum possible value for non-trivial matrices).

Proposition 5.2
.

Right-orderable groups have uniform twin-width 2.

Finitely generated free groups are known to be orderable, hence have uniform twin-width 2.
It is easy to see that finite cyclic groups, with the natural order, also have uniform twin-width 2.
Using
Lemma

3.5
, we obtain that Cartesian product preserves uniform twin-width, i.e.

\utww(\Gamma_{1}\times\Gamma_{2})=\max(\utww(\Gamma_{1}),\utww(\Gamma_{2})).

This is witnessed by a lexicographic order on
\Gamma_{1}\times\Gamma_{2}
.
Therefore all finitely generated abelian groups have uniform twin-width 2.

6.
Groups with finite twin-width

In this section, we prove that uniform twin-width is preserved by a number of group constructions,
and use these results to provide examples of groups with finite twin-width.
Here, unlike the rest of the paper, we use
G,H
etc. to denote groups and not graphs.

Let us start with the trivial remark that twin-width is monotone under taking subgroups.
It is used for lower bounds in results of this section.

Lemma 6.1
.

If

x\in a
with
a
fixed, because
a
is finite.
The same arguments show that
\stww(M^{G/N}_{a})
is finite, hence
G/N
has finite twin-width.
∎

6.3.
Infinite products and wreath products

Given
(G_{i})_{i\in I}
a family of groups, their
direct product
is denoted by
\prod_{i\in I}G_{i}
.
Their
direct sum

\bigoplus_{i\in I}G_{i}
is the subgroup of the direct product
consisting of finitely supported tuples, i.e. the
(x_{i})_{i\in I}
with
x_{i}\in G_{i}

such that all but finitely many
x_{i}
are the neutral element.
When
I
is finite, the two notions coincide.

Lemma

6.5
of course implies that finite products preserve twin-width and uniform twin-width.
This can be generalised to infinite direct sum, and for uniform twin-width only, to infinite direct products.

Lemma 6.14
.

Given
(G_{i})_{i\in I}
a possibly infinite family of groups,
consider
S=\bigoplus_{i\in I}G_{i}
their direct sum, and
P=\prod_{i\in I}G_{i}
their direct product.
Then
S
has finite twin-width if and only if all
G_{i}
do, and

\utww(S)=\utww(P)=\sup_{i\in I}\utww(G_{i}).

Proof.

Choose some well-order on
I
, a total order
<_{i}
on each
G_{i}
witnessing its twin-width,
and order
S
and
P
lexicographically.
Assuming that
\utww(G_{i})\leqslant k
is witnessed by
<_{i}
,

Lemma

3.6
immediately gives
\utww(P)\leqslant k
, and a fortiori
\utww(S)\leqslant k
.
This proves the equalities on uniform twin-width.

For the claim on non-uniform twin-width, assume that all
G_{i}
have finite twin-width witnessed by
<_{i}
,
and let
x=(x_{i})_{i\in I}\in S
.
Again the matrix of
x
acting on
(S,<)
is the tensor product of the matrices of
x_{i}
acting on
(G_{i},<_{i})
.
Since
x
is finitely supported, all but finitely many of these matrices are identity,
while the remaining ones have finite twin-width.
Thus there is a universal bound on the twin-width of these matrices,
and we conclude using
Lemma

3.6
once again.
∎

Given any groups
G,H
, one can define the (complete)
wreath product

G\wr H\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}G^{H}\rtimes H

where
H
acts by permuting indices by right product,
i.e. for
h,h^{\prime}\in H
and
x\in G^{H}
, the action of

is

(x\cdot h)(h^{\prime})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}x(h^{\prime}h).

One may also consider the restricted wreath product, with a direct sum replacing the direct product,
and the same action of
H
.

G\wr_{r}H\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\left(\bigoplus_{h\in H}G\right)\rtimes H.

It is a subgroup of the complete wreath product.

Lemma 6.15
.

\utww(G\wr H)=\max(\utww(G),\utww(H)).

Proof.

By
Lemmas

6.14
and
6.7
.
∎

Interesting groups can be constructed through wreath products,
the most common example being the lamplighter group
(\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z})\wr_{r}\mathbb{Z}
,
which therefore has uniform twin-width 2.

6.4.
Group actions

The twin-width of a group is defined through its self-action by product.
In this section, we consider the twin-width of other actions,
and show a relation to the twin-width of the group.

Lemma 6.16
.

Let
(X,<_{X})
be a well-ordered set.
Then there exists a total order
<_{\Perm_{X}}
on the permutation group
\Perm_{X}

such that for
\sigma\in\Perm_{X}

\stww_{\Perm_{X}}(\sigma)\leqslant\stww_{X}(\sigma)

where the right-hand side is the twin-width of
\sigma
acting on
(X,<_{X})
,
whereas the left-hand side is the twin-width of
\sigma
acting on
(\Perm_{X},<_{\Perm_{X}})
by product.

Proof.

To respect the convention that twin-width is defined with regards to right product,
we will use postfix notations for
\Perm_{X}
, i.e. for
\sigma,\tau\in\Perm_{X}
and
x\in X
,
we write
x\cdot\sigma=\sigma(x)
and
\sigma\tau=\tau\circ\sigma
.

We order
X^{X}
lexicographically, i.e. for any
f\neq g:X\to X
,
define
m_{f,g}=\min\textstyle\left\{x\in X\ |\ x\cdot f\neq x\cdot g\right\}

and

f<_{X^{X}}g\quad\iff\quad m_{f,g}\cdot f<_{X}m_{f,g}\cdot g.

The group
\Perm_{X}
acts on
X^{X}
by post-composition,
that is
(f\cdot\sigma)(x)=f(x)\cdot\sigma
.
We define
<_{\Perm_{X}}
by restricting the former order from
X^{X}
to
\Perm_{X}
,
while the restriction of this action is simply right product.

For
\sigma\in\Perm_{X}
, let
M_{\sigma}
be the matrix of
\sigma
acting on
(X,<_{X})
,
let
M^{X}_{\sigma}
be the matrix of
\sigma
acting on
(X^{X},<_{X^{X}})
by post-composition,
and let
M^{\Perm_{X}}_{\sigma}
be the matrix of
\sigma
acting on
(\Perm_{X},<_{\Perm_{X}})
by right product.
Then
M^{X}_{\sigma}
is a tensor product of
M_{\sigma}
with itself,
hence by
Lemma

3.6
, we have
\stww(M^{X}_{\sigma})=\stww(M_{\sigma})
.
Furthermore
M^{\Perm_{X}}_{\sigma}
is clearly a submatrix of
M^{X}_{\sigma}
, thus

\stww_{\Perm_{X}}(\sigma)=\stww(M^{\Perm_{X}}_{\sigma})\leqslant\stww(M^{X}_{\sigma})=\stww(M_{\sigma})=\stww_{X}(\sigma).

∎

Corollary 6.17
.

Let
(X,<_{X})
be a well-ordered set, and
G
a group acting faithfully on
X
on the right.
If this action has finite twin-width with regards to
<_{X}
, then
G
has finite twin-width.
Furthermore if this action has uniform twin-width

with regards to
<_{X}
, then
\utww(G)\leqslant k
.

Proof.

Through the faithful action,

is a subgroup of
\Perm_{X}
.
∎

Let
T
be the infinite rooted binary tree.
Let
\Aut(T)
be the group of automorphisms of

,
i.e. bijections on nodes of

which preserve the root and the parent relation.
The group

notably the Grigorchuk group

.

Proposition 6.18
.

The group

has uniform twin-width 2.

Proof.

Let

be the breadth-first search order on

,
which orders first by increasing depth, then left-to-right at each level.
This is a well-order, hence by
Corollary

6.17

it suffices to prove that the action of

on

with regards to

has uniform twin-width 2.

Let

, and for

let

be the action of

restricted to nodes of depth

.
Then the bijection matrix

is obtained by substituting
the 2-by-2 diagonal and anti-diagonal matrices inside

that

(These are

6.5.
Uniform and non-uniform twin-width

Any group with finite uniform twin-width also has finite twin-width.
We conjecture that the converse does not hold,
and we think the following is a good candidate for a counterexample.

Let
\Perm_{\mathbb{Z}}
be the group of permutations on
\mathbb{Z}
,
and let
\Perm_{\mathbb{Z}}^{f}
be the subgroup consisting of finitely supported permutations.
Furthermore, let
T=\textstyle\left\{x\mapsto x+c\ |\ c\in\mathbb{Z}\right\}
the subgroup of translations.
While
\Perm_{\mathbb{Z}}^{f}
is not finitely generated,
\Perm_{\mathbb{Z}}^{f}\rtimes T
is.
The latter is sometimes called lampshuffler group.

Consider the well-order
0,1,-1,2,-2,\dots
on
\mathbb{Z}
.
The transposition of
0
and
1
and the translation by
1

are two permutations of
\mathbb{Z}
which generate
\Perm_{\mathbb{Z}}^{f}\rtimes T
,
and whose action on
\mathbb{Z}
clearly has finite twin-width.
Thus, by
Lemma

3.11
, the action of the lampshuffler on
\mathbb{Z}
has finite twin-width,
and by
Corollary

6.17
, so does the lampshuffler group itself.

We conjecture that the lampshuffler has infinite uniform twin-width,
and thus separates uniform and non-uniform twin-width.
A justification for this conjecture is that the lampshuffler having finite uniform twin-width
would imply a universal bound on the uniform twin-width of finite groups,
and more generally of elementary amenable groups
(the groups obtained from finite groups and abelian groups by taking
subgroups, quotients, extensions, and direct limits).
This would be surprising.

Proposition 6.19
.

The following are equivalent for any
k\in\mathbb{N}
.

(1)

For all finite group
G
,
\utww(G)\leqslant k
.

(2)

\utww(\Perm_{\mathbb{Z}}^{f}\rtimes T)\leqslant k
.

(3)

For all elementary amenable group
G
,
\utww(G)\leqslant k
.

Proof.

The lampshuffler
\Perm_{\mathbb{Z}}^{f}\rtimes T
is elementary amenable and contains all finite groups,
hence (
3
) implies (
2
) which implies (
1
).
Suppose now that all finite groups have uniform twin-width at most
k
,
and recall that abelian groups have uniform twin-width 2.
Remark that
k\geqslant 2
, because only the trivial group has twin-width 1.
It is known that all elementary amenable groups can be obtained from finite groups and abelian groups
using only extensions and direct limits
[
8
]
.
It follows by
Corollaries

6.6
and
6.2
that all elementary amenable groups have twin-width at most
k
.
∎

One could replace
\Perm_{\mathbb{Z}}^{f}\rtimes T
with just
\Perm_{\mathbb{Z}}^{f}
in the result:
they have the same uniform twin-width by
Corollary

6.7
.

Remark that the previous result means that if all elementary amenable groups have finite uniform twin-width,
then there is in fact a universal bound on their uniform twin-width,
because the lampshuffler is elementary amenable.

Constructing groups with infinite twin-width

This section constructs a finitely generated group with infinite twin-width.

7.1.
Embedding graphs of high twin-width

The construction embeds an appropriate sequence of graphs with bounded degree and unbounded twin-width in a Cayley graph.
The existence of such graphs is given by counting arguments (see

Theorem 7.1

Then there exists a group
\Gamma
finitely generated by
S
such that
every
G_{n}
isometrically embeds in
\text{Cay}(\Gamma,S)

To obtain a finitely generated group of infinite twin-width,

Precisely, the sequence we construct satisfies the following

Logarithms in this section are in base 2.

(1)

\textsf{girth}(G_{n})>\frac{1}{4}\log|{V(G_{n})}|

For

For

Lemma 7.2

For any constant

and any sufficiently large even

Proof.

The class

Define

Let us show that at least half of

Lemma 7.3

If

Proof.

Let

When choosing a random perfect matching

But the fact that each color class of

Now if these already known edges are part of

Thus the probability that

Therefore the expected total number of cycles of length at most

7.2.
Computability considerations

In this section, we argue that the proof of
Theorem

1.1
is effective,
and in particular that it can be turned into an algorithm to decide the word problem in the resulting group.

Theorem 7.6
.

There exists a group with infinite twin-width given by a presentation
\left\langle S;R\right\rangle
,
with a finite set
S
of generators and a recursive set
R
of relators.
Furthermore, this group has a decidable word problem.

Using Higman embedding theorem
[
20
]
, we obtain the following.

Corollary 7.7
.

There is a finitely presented group with infinite twin-width.

Note that this finitely presented group is a supergroup of the one constructed in
Theorem

7.6
,
and might not have a decidable word problem.

To prove
Theorem

7.6
,
we split the proof of
Theorem

1.1
in three steps,
and prove that each of them is effective.

(1)

Constructing the sequence of graphs
(G_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
,
with appropriate conditions on degree, girth, diameter, and twin-width (
Section

7.1
).

(2)

Adding labels to this sequence satisfying the small cancellation conditions.
This is the core of Osajda’s proof, see
[
28
, Section 2]
.

(3)

Obtaining a group from this small cancellation graph labelling.
This construction, attributed to Gromov
[
18
]
,
is based on the well-known techniques of small cancellation theory
(see e.g.
[
22
, Chapter V]
).

7.2.1.
Computing the sequence of graphs

Lemma 7.8
.

There exists a computable sequence of graphs
(G_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
,
satisfying the hypotheses of
Theorem

7.1
, and with unbounded twin-width.

Proof.

By
Lemmas

7.5
and
1.2
,
one can find such a sequence by choosing a

appropriately, for some

function of

.
It is easy to obtain an explicit formula for

from
the proofs of
Lemmas

,
7.4
and
7.5
.
This yields a naive algorithm: Enumerate all graphs of size

until finding one in

with sufficiently large twin-width.

However, the proofs give a natural and much more efficient randomized algorithm.
One chooses a graph uniformly at random in

Edge editions are performed according to

and the process is aborted should it require more than
4\cdot 6^{g}
edge deletions—which by
Lemma

7.3
happens with probability less than
1/2
.
In case of success, the resulting graph
G_{k}
is in
\mathcal{C}_{2}(n_{k})
.
Although the distribution of
G_{k}
over
\mathcal{C}_{2}(n_{k})
is not uniform,
the proof of
Lemma

7.5
can be adapted to show that
for any constant
c
and any subset
X\subset\mathcal{C}_{2}(n_{k})
of size
|{X}|\leqslant n!\cdot c^{n}
,
the probability that
G_{k}
is in
X
tends to 0 as
n_{k}
grows.
Applying this to the subset
X
of graphs with twin-width at most
t
for any fixed
t
,
we obtain by
Theorem

1.2
that the sequence
(G_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
has unbounded twin-width with probability 1.
Thus, this probabilistic algorithm does not even need to compute the twin-width of any graph.

This algorithm chooses
G_{k}
in expected time polynomial in its size
n_{k}
.
However
n_{k}
needs to be at least exponential in
k
,
since we want
G_{k}
to have girth at least linear in
k
, but at most logarithmic in its size.
∎

7.2.2.
Computing the small cancellation labelling

Osajda (
[
28
, Section 2]
) equips the sequence
(G_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}

with an edge labelling satisfying the small cancellation condition:
if
p,p^{\prime}
are two distinct paths in
G_{k},G_{k^{\prime}}
respectively,
with the same length and edge labelling,
then
|p|<\lambda\cdot\textsf{girth}(G_{k})
, and
|p^{\prime}|<\lambda\cdot\textsf{girth}(G_{k^{\prime}})
,
where
\lambda>0
is some fixed constant (the small cancellation parameter).
Said shortly: Any two paths with the same labelling must be short, relative to the girth.

The proof makes large use of Lovász Local Lemma.
Assuming some appropriate labellings of
G_{0},\dots,G_{k-1}
have been chosen inductively,
one proves that a random labelling of
G_{k}
is ‘good’ with non-zero probability.
Crucially, this is an incremental process:
to choose the labelling of
G_{k}
, one only needs to consider
G_{0},\dots,G_{k}
.
It will always be possible to later extend this sequence with an appropriate labelling of
G_{k+1}
.
This readily gives a naive algorithm, which enumerates all labellings of
G_{k}

and chooses the first ‘good’ one, before continuing with
G_{k+1}
.
Effective algorithms for Lovász Local Lemma such as
[
25
]

may be helpful to improve the complexity.

7.2.3.
Deciding the word problem

The former labelling of
(G_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
uses labels in
S\uplus S^{-1}
,
where
S
is some finite set, and
S^{-1}
denotes the set of formal inverses of
S
.
The group constructed by
Theorem

7.1
is then

\Gamma=\left\langle S;R\right\rangle

where the set
R
of relators contains all words on
S\uplus S^{-1}
read along some cycle of some
G_{k}
.
This set
R
is recursive: given some word
w
over
S\uplus S^{-1}
,
to decide if
w\in R
, one must test if
w
appears as the labelling of some cycle of some
G_{k}
.
This can only happen if
\textsf{girth}(G_{k})\leqslant|w|
.
Since
\textsf{girth}(G_{k})
increases at least linearly with
k
,
it suffices to check a finite number of graphs in the sequence.
This proves the first half of
Theorem

7.6
.

Decidability of the word problem uses a classical result of small cancellation theory,
stated below for the case of finite graphical presentations.

Lemma 7.9

(Greendlinger’s lemma
[
27
]
)
.

Let
G
be a finite graph equipped with an edge labelling on
S\uplus S^{-1}

satisfying the small cancellation condition.
Let
R
be the set of words read along the cycles of
G
, and
\Gamma=\left\langle S;R\right\rangle
.
If
w
is a word over
S\uplus S^{-1}
which represents the identity of
\Gamma
,
then some relator
r\in R
can be applied to
w
to reduce it to
w^{\prime}
, with
|{w^{\prime}}|<|{w}|
.

Although the above is stated for a finite graph, it generalises to a sequence of finite graphs.
Suppose a word
w
over
S\uplus S^{-1}
represents the identity of
\Gamma
.
Then there is finite sequence of relators in
R
which can be applied to
w
to reduce it to the empty word.
These relators are found in finitely many graphs in the sequence
(G_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
, say
G_{0},\dots,G_{s}
.
Then
w
is also equal to
1
in the group
\left\langle S;R^{\prime}\right\rangle
where the relators
R^{\prime}

are the ones read along some cycle of
G_{0}\cup\dots\cup G_{s}
.
We conclude by applying
Lemma

7.9
to
\left\langle S;R^{\prime}\right\rangle
.

Where Greendlinger’s lemma applies, the word problem can be decided using Dehn’s algorithm:
given a word
w
on
S\cup S^{-1}
, try to rewrite it into a strictly smaller word
by applying some relator of
R
, and repeat.
If this fails before reaching the empty word, Greendlinger’s lemma proves
that the word is not equivalent to the neutral element in
\Gamma
.
This algorithm works for
\Gamma
despite the set of relators
R
being infinite.
Indeed, given
w
of length
k
, only relators of length less than
2k
need to be considered in Dehn’s algorithm.
There are only finitely many such relators, and they can be enumerated.
This concludes the proof of
Theorem

7.6

8.
Remarks on queue number

This final section draws some remarks on the relation between twin-width and queue number in the context of groups.
Many results of this paper hold when replacing twin-width with queue number.
We point out the proofs which fail for queue number, but without counterexamples:
separating bounded twin-width and bounded queue number on sparse graphs remains an open question.

8.1.
Definition

Consider a graph
G
with a total order
<
on
V(G)
.
Two edges
xy
and
uv
are said to be
nested
if
x<u<v<y
, up to symmetries.
The graph
G
is said to be a
queue
with regards to
<
if it has no nested edges,
and a strict queue if furthermore it is a matching (no two edges share an endpoint).
A (strict)
t
-queue-layout
of
G
is a choice of order
<
on
V(G)

and a partition
E(G)=\biguplus_{i=1}^{t}E_{i}
of its edges such that for each
i
,
the graph formed by the edges
E_{i}
is a (strict) queue with regards to
<
, see
Fig.

7
.
The (strict)
queue number
of
G
, denoted
\qn(G)
(resp.
\sqn(G)
)
is the smallest
t
such that
G
admits a (strict)
t
-queue-layout.
Remark that the strict queue number is at least the maximum degree.
Furthermore, if
G
has maximum degree
\Delta
, given a
t
-queue-layout,
refining the partition of edges with a proper edge coloring gives a strict
(\Delta+1)t
-queue layout
using Vizing’s theorem. Thus

(12)

\qn(G)\leqslant\sqn(G)\leqslant(\Delta+1)\qn(G),

i.e. the two numbers are equivalent for graphs of bounded degree up to a constant factor.
We will focus on the strict queue number.

partitioned in solid and dashed edges.

8.2.
Matrix characterisation

Say that an
X\times Y
matrix
M
is increasing if it is the matrix of an increasing partial map,
i.e. whenever there are 1-coefficients at positions
(x_{1},y_{1})
and
(x_{2},y_{2})
,
either
x_{1}<x_{2}
and
y_{1}<y_{2}
, or
x_{2}<x_{1}
and
y_{2}<y_{1}
.
In particular, each row and column contains at most a single ‘1’.

Call strict queue number of
M
the minimum
k

such that
M
is equal to the superposition of
k
increasing matrices.
If
G
is a graph and
<
an order on
V(G)
witnessing the strict queue number of
G
,
then one verifies

(13)

\sqn(G)\leqslant\sqn(A_{<}(G))\leqslant 2\sqn(G).

The first inequality is simple, and the second is obtained by using up to
\sqn(G)
increasing matrices
to cover the 1-entries above the diagonal, and as many for the 1-entries below the diagonal.
Hence the strict queue number of
G
is within a factor 2 of
the strict queue number of its adjacency matrix, assuming an optimal choice of order on
V(G)
.

8.3.
Queue number of groups

If one considers the matrix product, with addition interpreted as logical ‘or’ and multiplication as logical ‘and’,
it is clear that a product of increasing matrices is itself increasing.
Furthermore, the adjacency matrix of
G^{(k)}
is equal to
(M+I)^{k}-I

where
M
is the adjacency matrix of
G
and
I
is the identity.
Matrix product distributes over matrix superposition, thus assuming that
\sqn(M)\leqslant t
, we have that
(M+I)
is superposition of
t+1
increasing matrices,
and after distributing
(M+I)^{k}
is superposition of at most
(t+1)^{k}
increasing matrices.
Thus we obtain a result similar to
Lemma

3.10
:

\sqn(G^{(k)})
is bounded by a function of
\sqn(G)
and
k
.
With a few other arguments, one can reproduce the proof of
Lemma

4.1

to prove that regular embeddings preserve finite strict queue number, which is therefore a group invariant.
All examples of
Section

4
can easily be shown to have finite queue number
(finitely generated abelian groups, finite Cartesian products of groups with finite queue number, hyperbolic groups).

The characterisation of groups with finite queue number through matrices
and the generalisation to infinitely generated groups work without any issue.
The uniform queue number of a group
G
is defined as

\uqn(G)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\min_{\text{$<$ order on $G$}}\ \sup_{x\in G}\ \sqn(M_{x}^{<})

with
M_{x}^{<}
the matrix of
x
acting on
(G,<)
.
Groups with uniform queue number 1 are exactly right-orderable groups.
As in
Lemma

6.2
, direct limits preserve uniform queue number.

8.4.
Group constructions

Cartesian products—and more generally group extensions—is where the first difference with twin-width appears.
Twin-width on permutation matrices is remarkable in that it is exactly preserved by substitution,
in the sense that the twin-width of the resulting matrix is
the maximum of the twin-width of the input matrices (see
Lemma

3.5
).
This fails for queue number: if
M,N
are permutation matrices
and
M[N]
is obtained by substituting
N
into every 1-coefficient of
M
,
then

(14)

\sqn(M[N])=\sqn(M)\sqn(N).

Thus, while Cartesian products (or group extensions) still preserve having finite uniform queue number,
they do not a priori preserve the exact bound.
Precisely, the lexicographic ordering used in
Lemma

6.5

gives the bound
\uqn(G\times H)\leqslant\uqn(G)\uqn(H)
by (
14
),
and this bound is tight for this specific choice of order.
However, there might be a better choice of order on
G\times H

giving
\uqn(G\times H)=\max(\uqn(G),\uqn(H))
in some cases.

For example, all finitely generated abelian groups have finite uniform queue number,
but it is unclear whether there is a universal bound on the latter.
In turn, infinitely generated abelian groups (and thus solvable groups) might have infinite uniform queue number.

Summarizing, proofs of finite twin-width from this paper also give finite queue number in the following cases.

(1)

Hyperbolic groups have finite queue number.

(2)

Right-orderable groups have uniform queue number 1, and this is a characterisation.

(3)

Finitely generated abelian groups have finite uniform queue number,
but a priori without a universal bound.

(4)

Polycyclic groups, which are defined like solvable groups except
requiring that the iterated quotients be finitely generated and abelian,
have finite uniform queue number.

(5)

Groups of polynomial growth have finite uniform queue number,
since they are virtually nilpotent, and nilpotent groups are polycyclic.

On the other hand, we conjecture that arbitrary abelian groups, and a fortiori solvable groups,
can have infinite queue number (uniform and non-uniform).
As a simple example, we do not know whether an infinite countable product of
\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
with itself has finite queue number.

We also conjecture that the group of tree automorphisms
considered in
Proposition

6.18
has infinite queue number.
This group is related to the Bilu-Linial expanders
[
1
]
,
which are known to have twin-width at most 6
[
3
, Section 5]
,
and are conjectured to have unbounded queue number.

Indeed, these expanders can be described as follows.
Let
G_{n}
be the group of permutations on
2^{n}
elements obtained by
restricting automorphisms of the infinite binary tree
T
to nodes at depth
n
in
T
, ordered left to right.
Equivalently, permutations in
G_{n}
are the ones obtained from a permutation
\sigma\in G_{n-1}

by substituting each ‘1’-coefficient of
\sigma
by either the diagonal or antidiagonal 2-by-2 matrix,
the choice being done independently for all ‘1’-coefficients.
Then Bilu-Linial expanders have a partition of vertices into
V_{1},V_{2},V_{3},V_{4}
of size
2^{n}
each,
and a choice of orders
<_{i}
on
V_{i}
such that the adjacency matrix of
(V_{i},<_{i})
versus
(V_{j},<_{j})

is the matrix of a permutation in
G_{n}
.
Precisely, a random choice of permutations in
G_{n}
in this description yields an expander with high probability,
while Bilu and Linial give a derandomized construction.

From this description, it is simple to see that if the action of
\Aut(T)
on
T
has finite uniform queue number,
then Bilu-Linial expanders have bounded queue number.
The converse, and the relation with the queue number of
\Aut(T)
are not clear,
but it is likely that an answer to either question would give significant insight for the other.
