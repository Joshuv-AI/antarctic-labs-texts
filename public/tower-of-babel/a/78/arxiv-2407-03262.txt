Abstract
The ℓ p \ell_{p} subspace approximation problem is an NP-hard low rank approximation problem that generalizes the median hyperplane problem ( p = 1 p=1 ), principal component analysis ( p = 2 p=2 ), and the center hyperplane problem ( p = ∞ p=\infty ). A popular approach to cope with the NP-hardness of this problem is to compute a strong coreset , which is a small weighted subset of the input points which simultaneously approximates the cost of every k k -dimensional subspace, typically to ( 1 + ε ) (1+\varepsilon) relative error for a small constant ε \varepsilon . We obtain an algorithm for constructing a strong coreset for ℓ p \ell_{p} subspace approximation of size O ~ ( k ε − 4 / p ) \tilde{O}(k\varepsilon^{-4/p}) for p < 2 p<2 and O ~ ​ ( k p / 2 ​ ε − p ) \tilde{O}(k^{p/2}\varepsilon^{-p}) for p > 2 p>2 . This offers the following improvements over prior work: • We construct the first strong coresets with nearly optimal dependence on k k for all p ≠ 2 p\neq 2 . In prior work, [ SW18 ] constructed coresets of modified points with a similar dependence on k k , while [ HV20 ] constructed true coresets with polynomially worse dependence on k k . • We recover or improve the best known ε \varepsilon dependence for all p p . In particular, for p > 2 p>2 , the [ SW18 ] coreset of modified points had a dependence of ε − p 2 / 2 \varepsilon^{-p^{2}/2} and the [ HV20 ] coreset had a dependence of ε − 3 ​ p \varepsilon^{-3p} . Our algorithm is based on sampling by root ridge leverage scores, which admits fast algorithms, especially for sparse or structured matrices. Our analysis completely avoids the use of the representative subspace theorem [ SW18 ] , which is a critical component of all prior dimension-independent coresets for ℓ p \ell_{p} subspace approximation. Our techniques also lead to the first nearly optimal online strong coresets for ℓ p \ell_{p} subspace approximation with similar bounds as the offline setting, resolving a problem of [ WY23a ] . All prior approaches lose poly ⁡ ( k ) \mathrm{poly}(k) factors in this setting, even when allowed to modify the original points.

Introduction

1.1
The
\ell_{p}
subspace approximation problem

Consider a set of
n
points
\{\mathbf{a}_{1},\mathbf{a}_{2},\dots,\mathbf{a}_{n}\}
in
d
-dimensional space. A natural problem is to determine the “cost” of moving these
n
points to lie on a low-dimensional subspace
F\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
of rank
k
. This is an old and important question that has been studied independently in varied fields such as operations research, statistics, and computational geometry.

We measure the cost of moving a single point to a subspace
F
by the Euclidean distance, given by

\cost(\mathbf{a}_{i},F)=\min_{\mathbf{x}\in F}\lVert\mathbf{a}_{i}-\mathbf{x}\rVert_{2}=\lVert\mathbf{a}_{i}^{\top}(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{P}_{F})\rVert_{2}

where
\mathbf{I}
denotes the
d\times d
identity and
\mathbf{P}_{F}
denotes the orthogonal projection matrix onto
F
. This cost is rotationally invariant, which is important for accurately modeling real world data
[
DZHZ06
]
. To measure the cost of the entire set of
n
points, we aggregate the cost of the individual points via the
\ell_{p}
loss
, which is parameterized by some exponent
p\geq 1
and is given by

\cost^{p}(\{\mathbf{a}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n},F)=\left[\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lVert\mathbf{a}_{i}^{\top}(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{P}_{F})\rVert_{2}^{p}\right]^{1/p}.

For smaller values of
p
, this cost tends to capture the average cost of a point
\mathbf{a}_{i}
, while for larger values of
p
, this cost tends to capture the maximum cost of a point
\mathbf{a}_{i}
. The problem of minimizing this objective over rank
k
subspaces
F
is known as the
\ell_{p}
subspace approximation problem
. To simplify the notation, we will write the objective as simply
\lVert\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{P}_{F})\rVert_{p,2}
, where
\mathbf{A}
is the
n\times d
matrix containing
\mathbf{a}_{i}
in the
i
-th row and
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{p,2}
is the
(p,2)
-norm
, which is defined for a
n\times d
matrix
\mathbf{Y}
as
\lVert\mathbf{Y}\rVert_{p,2}\coloneqq[\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lVert\mathbf{e}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{Y}\rVert_{2}^{p}]^{1/p}
. We will denote the optimal value of this problem as

\mathsf{OPT}\coloneqq\min_{F\in\mathcal{F}_{k}}\lVert\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{P}_{F})\rVert_{p,2}^{p}

where
\mathcal{F}_{k}
denotes the set of all rank
k
subspaces
F
. We will let
\mathbf{P}^{*}
denote the projection matrix for a rank
k
subspace achieving this optimum.

Several special cases of this problem are particularly well-studied. The most popular special case is the sum of squared costs, or the
\ell_{2}
loss
, which is the usual
principal component analysis (PCA) problem
and is one of the most popular dimension reduction techniques in statistics and data analysis. In modern machine learning and statistical practice, the
\ell_{1}
subspace approximation problem has been studied intensely as a natural variant of the PCA that retains rotational invariance yet offers solutions which are robust to outliers
[
SW87
,
DZHZ06
,
GZAL14
,
YHY17
,
NNSS20
]
. In transportation theory, the
\ell_{1}
subspace approximation cost for
k=1
is studied as the problem of solving for the cheapest way to lay out a railroad (i.e., a 1-dimensional subspace) to serve
n
cities
[
MB72
,
MN80
]
. In computational geometry, the
\ell_{1}
subspace approximation problem is also known as the
median hyperplane problem

[
MS01
]
, and also has a rich history
[
KM12
,
Sch99
,
MS01
]
. A closely related problem is the regression problem, for which
[
SL24
]
motivates the study of
\ell_{p}
loss for
p
strictly between
1
and
2
, i.e.,
1<p<2
, due to a combination of its robustness and efficiency properties.

In the other extreme, the
\ell_{\infty}
subspace approximation problem is particularly well-studied in the computational geometry literature, where it is known as the
center hyperplane problem

[
MS01
]
, and generalizes a variety of
containment
or
extent
problems such as minimum enclosing balls and minimum enclosing cylinders
[
GK94
,
HV04
,
AHV04
]
. However, the
\ell_{\infty}
norm poses various computational challenges
[
DTV11
,
GRSW12
]
and may be too sensitive to extreme values, and the
\ell_{p}
norm for large values of
p
are often considered as useful relaxations of the
\ell_{\infty}
norm problem that behave more smoothly
[
SV12
]
. For related regression problems, values of
p
strictly between
2
and
\infty
, i.e.,
2<p<\infty
, offer a compromise between robustness and accuracy, and find applications in the design of
polynomial finite impulse response filters
[
BBS94
,
Dum07
]
.

The flexibility of allowing for arbitrary values of
p
is also crucial for tuning the behavior of the optimization problem to a specific input distribution. As a toy example, consider an input matrix
\mathbf{A}=\mathbf{B}+\mathbf{G}
, where
\mathbf{B}
is a
n\times n
diagonal matrix with
k
ones and
n-k
zeros on the diagonal, and
\mathbf{G}
is i.i.d. Gaussian noise with variance
\sigma^{2}
. Then, the
(p,2)
-norm of
\mathbf{G}
to the power
p
is roughly
n^{p/2+1}\sigma^{p}
while the
(p,2)
-norm of
\mathbf{A}
to the power
p
is
k((1+\sigma)^{2}+(n-1)\sigma^{2})^{p/2}+(n-k)n^{p/2}\sigma^{p}
, that is, larger by roughly an additive
k
. Thus, in order for the
\ell_{p}
subspace approximation problem to output a good approximation to
\mathbf{B}
,
p
must be tuned such that
k=\Omega(n^{p/2+1}\sigma^{p})
; otherwise, the
0
matrix is already a constant factor approximation. We stress that
any setting of
p
could be important here, depending on the variance
\sigma^{2}
of
\mathbf{G}
. For instance, if
\sigma^{2}=k^{2/3}/n^{5/3}
, then we should set
p=3
, and if
\sigma^{2}=k^{1/2}/n^{3/2}
, then we should set
p=4
. Note that we also do not want to set
p
larger than we have to, as we shall see that the complexity of the problem increases for large
p
.

1.2
Sparsification and coresets

In big data settings, the number of points
n
is often prohibitively large.
Sparsification
and
coresets
are a class of techniques which have been extremely successful in handling such parameter regimes. Sparsification generally refers to the approximation of a large object by a sparse object, and has been studied extensively in settings such as graphs
[
BK96
,
ST04
,
BSS12
]
and hypergraphs
[
BST19
,
KKTY21
,
JLS23
,
Lee23
]
as well as a large variety of decomposable loss functions such as clustering
[
Che09
,
FL11
,
BFL16
,
FSS20
,
HV20
,
BJKW21
,
CSS21
,
CLSS22
,
CLS+22
,
HLW22
]
, submodular functions
[
RY22
,
JLLS23b
]
,
\ell_{p}
regression
[
DMM06a
,
Cla05
,
DDH+09
,
CP15
,
WY23b
]
, and generalized linear models
[
MMR21
,
MOP22
,
MMWY22
,
JLLS23a
]
. When the object being sparsified is a dataset, then its sparse approximation is often called a coreset. Coresets can often be used as a substantially smaller drop-in replacement of the original dataset, and leads to both time- and space-efficient algorithms.

Coresets have been a particularly important tool in the study of algorithms for
\ell_{p}
subspace approximation, due to the fact that the problem is known to be NP-hard for any
p\neq 2

[
DTV11
,
GRSW12
,
CW15
]
. In fact, the best known
(1+\varepsilon)
approximation algorithms for this problem proceed exactly by reducing the input size via coreset techniques, and then applying an exponential time algorithm on the reduced instance
[
FMSW10
,
CW15
]
. Thus, the best known running time of
\ell_{p}
subspace approximation is almost completely determined by how small the coreset can be made to be. Various coreset algorithms for
\ell_{p}
subspace approximation have been developed for both
p=2

[
DMM06b
,
DMM08
,
CEM+15
,
CMM17
]
and
p\neq 2
with increasingly improved sizes, running times, and guarantees
[
DV07
,
FL11
,
SV12
,
VX12
,
SW18
,
FSS20
,
HV20
,
FKW21
]
, generalized loss functions
[
CW15
,
MMWY22
]
, and in information-limited settings such as streaming
[
FMSW10
,
LSW18
,
MRWZ20
,
DP22
]
and online
[
BLVZ19
,
BDM+20
,
WY23a
]
models.

1.2.1
Strong coresets for
\ell_{p}
subspace approximation

While several different coreset guarantees have been studied, we focus on
strong coresets
, which are a weighted subset of points
\mathbf{a}_{i}
such that the weighted cost of any rank
k
subspace
F
on the coreset approximates the cost on the original dataset, up to a factor of
(1+\varepsilon)
.

Definition 1.1

(Strong coresets for
\ell_{p}
subspace approximation)
.

Let
1\leq p<\infty
and
0<\varepsilon<1
. Let
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
. Then, a diagonal matrix
\mathbf{S}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
is a
(1\pm\varepsilon)
strong coreset
for
\ell_{p}
subspace approximation if

\left\lVert\mathbf{S}\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{P}_{F})\right\rVert_{p,2}^{p}=(1\pm\varepsilon)\left\lVert\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{P}_{F})\right\rVert_{p,2}^{p}

for every
F\in\mathcal{F}_{k}
. We refer to the number of nonzero entries
\nnz(\mathbf{S})
of
\mathbf{S}
as the size of the coreset.

The guarantee of Definition
1.1
can also be viewed as a natural generalization of
projection cost-preserving sketches

[
CEM+15
,
CMM17
,
MM20
]
to the
\ell_{p}
subspace approximation setting.

Strong coresets can be used to reduce the size of the input instance to at most
\nnz(\mathbf{S})
points in
\nnz(\mathbf{S})
dimensions by rotational invariance. In particular, strong coresets of size
\nnz(\mathbf{S})=\poly(k/\varepsilon)
immediately remove the dependence of this problem on
n
and
d
, making this a powerful tool in the design of fast algorithms.

It is useful to compare the guarantee of Definition
1.1
with other popular coreset guarantees. One weaker guarantee is a
weak coreset
, which requires that if
\tilde{F}
is the optimal solution to the subspace approximation problem for
\mathbf{S}\mathbf{A}
, then it is also a
(1+\varepsilon)
-optimal solution for
\mathbf{A}

[
FL11
,
HV20
]
. Another further weaker guarantee is a
spanning coreset
, which requires that there is a rank
k
subspace
\tilde{F}
in the row span of
\mathbf{S}\mathbf{A}
that is a
(1+\varepsilon)
-optimal solution
[
DV07
,
SV12
,
CW15
,
WY24
]
. The guarantee of Definition
1.1
immediately achieves both of these guarantees, and offers further benefits that cannot be realized by these other guarantees, such as applications to solving
constrained
versions of
\ell_{p}
subspace approximation, or solving
\ell_{p}
subspace approximation in distributed and streaming models via the merge-and-reduce technique
[
BDM+20
,
CWZ23
]
. Known algorithms for a number of constrained low rank approximation problems such as binary matrix factorization
[
VVWZ23
]
, sparse PCA
[
Pia23
]
, projective non-negative matrix factorization
[
YO05
]
, and constrained subspace estimation
[
SVK+17
]
all start by first computing a coreset, as a coreset preserves all possible solutions, and then use a problem-dependent algorithm on the coreset whose running time is proportional to the coreset size. By reducing the coreset size for
\ell_{p}
-subspace approximation, we can improve the time complexity for all of these constrained problems.

1.2.2
Prior work

While it has long been known that strong coresets of size
\poly(k,d,\varepsilon^{-1})
independent of
n
exist
[
FL11
]
, the first dimension-independent result, i.e., a strong coreset of size only
\poly(k,\varepsilon^{-1})
independent of
d
, was achieved by the work of
[
SW18
]
. This result achieves a coreset of size of
\nnz(\mathbf{S})=\tilde{O}(k)\varepsilon^{-O(1)}
for
p<2
and
\nnz(\mathbf{S})=\tilde{O}(k^{p/2})\varepsilon^{-O(p^{2})}
for
p>2
, which is in fact nearly optimal with respect to
k

[
LWW21
]
. However, this result has several drawbacks. First, the
\varepsilon
dependence is poor, with an
O(p^{2})
dependence in the exponent which makes this result intractable for even moderately large
p
. Second, it does not quite satisfy Definition
1.1
, due to the fact that the coreset is a weighted subset of points
with an appended coordinate
which must be treated separately from the original coordinates, rather than a weighted subset of the original data points themselves. This is unfavorable for a number of reasons:

•

The use of an appended coordinate deviates from the traditional definition of coresets, which is the standard for a vast majority of the coreset literature.

•

Coreset size lower bounds against row subsets no longer apply to these constructions, which are often substantially easier to establish than general data structure lower bounds. For instance, there are several coreset lower bounds which are specific to row subsets such as
[
HV20
,
CLSS22
,
HLW22
]
for clustering. Another example is
[
LWW21
]
, which obtains nearly optimal (row subset) coreset lower bounds for
\ell_{p}
subspace embeddings, and looser lower bounds for general data structures.

•

Downstream algorithms for
\ell_{p}
subspace approximation must be modified to handle the additional coordinate, which may not always be simple. Algorithms for subspace approximation are highly varied, ranging from convex relaxation and rounding
[
DTV11
]
to power iterations
[
DZHZ06
,
NNSS20
]
to polynomial solvers
[
CW15
]
, and it is undesirable to adapt all of these analyses and implementations.

•

In the online coreset model, in which the coreset must be constructed “on the fly” as the input points
\mathbf{a}_{i}
arrive, this approach leads to suboptimal bounds
[
WY23a
]
(see Section
1.3.1
).

Furthermore, the algorithm of
[
SW18
]
runs in exponential time. The running time was reduced to polynomial time in a follow-up work of
[
FKW21
]
, but this result loses
\poly(k)
factors in the coreset size and still has the
\varepsilon^{-O(p^{2})}
dependence and the extra appended coordinate.

Among purely row subset coresets following Definition
1.1
, the work of
[
HV20
]
gives the first dimension-independent strong coresets using a technique known as sensitivity sampling, which can be implemented in input sparsity time
[
FKW21
,
WY23a
]
. However, the coreset size in this work, while dimension-independent, is not optimal, and loses
\poly(k)
factors in the coreset size
\nnz(\mathbf{S})
. Thus, there is a
\poly(k,\varepsilon^{-1})
factor gap in our understanding of the complexity of strong coresets for
\ell_{p}
subspace approximation, and it is a central question in this literature to pin down the dependence of
k
. In particular, it is natural to conjecture that the
[
SW18
]
result can be matched with a purely row subset coreset.

Question 1.2
.

Do strong coresets for
\ell_{p}
subspace approximation of size
\tilde{O}(k)\poly(\varepsilon^{-1})
for
p<2
and
\tilde{O}(k^{p/2})\poly(\varepsilon^{-1})
for
p>2
exist? Can such coresets be constructed efficiently?

1.3
Our contributions

The main result of this work is a positive resolution to Question
1.2
for all
1\leq p<\infty
. For
1\leq p<2
, we obtain the first row subset strong coreset with a nearly linear dependence on
k
. In particular, for
p=1
, we obtain strong coresets of size
\tilde{O}(k/\varepsilon^{4})
for the median hyperplane problem, which is off by only a
1/\varepsilon^{2}
factor off from the lower bound of
\tilde{\Omega}(k/\varepsilon^{2})

[
LWW21
,
WY23a
]
, and improves the previous upper bound of
\tilde{O}(k^{4}/\varepsilon^{6})
due to
[
HV20
]
. For
p=2
, the work of
[
CMM17
]
already gives a bound of
\tilde{O}(k/\varepsilon^{2})
for ridge leverage score sampling. However, we offer a new proof of this fact that is arguably much simpler.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
1\leq p<2
. Let
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
. Then, there is an algorithm running in
\tilde{O}\left\lparen\nnz(\mathbf{A})+d^{\omega}\right\rparen
time which, with probability at least
1-\delta
, constructs a strong coreset
\mathbf{S}
of size

\nnz(\mathbf{S})=\frac{k}{\varepsilon^{4/p}}(\log(k/\varepsilon\delta))^{O(1)}

satisfying Definition
1.1
, that is,

\left\lVert\mathbf{S}\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{P}_{F})\right\rVert_{p,2}^{p}=(1\pm\varepsilon)\left\lVert\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{P}_{F})\right\rVert_{p,2}^{p}\qquad\mbox{for every subspace $F\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}$ of rank at most $k$}.

For
2<p<\infty
, we obtain a bound that scales as
k^{p/2}
. While this is not nearly linear, this dependence on
k
is necessary for this range of
p
, and matches a lower bound of
\tilde{\Omega}(k^{p/2}\varepsilon^{-1}+k\varepsilon^{-2})

[
LWW21
,
WY23a
]
.

Theorem 1.4
.

Let
2<p<\infty
. Let
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
. Then, there is an algorithm running in
\tilde{O}\left\lparen\nnz(\mathbf{A})+d^{\omega}\right\rparen
time which, with probability at least
1-\delta
, constructs a strong coreset
\mathbf{S}
of size

\nnz(\mathbf{S})=\frac{k^{p/2}}{\varepsilon^{p}}(\log(k/\varepsilon\delta))^{O(p^{2})}

satisfying Definition
1.1
, that is,

\left\lVert\mathbf{S}\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{P}_{F})\right\rVert_{p,2}^{p}=(1\pm\varepsilon)\left\lVert\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{P}_{F})\right\rVert_{p,2}^{p}\qquad\mbox{for every subspace $F\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}$ of rank at most $k$}.

We give several remarks concerning our results. First, we are the first to establish even the
existence
of a weighted subset of points with nearly optimal dependence on
k
, and we are able to construct such a subset in nearly input sparsity time. We also note that for
p<2
, the fact that we achieve nearly linear size for the strong coreset guarantee implies that we simultaneously achieve the first nearly optimal size guarantees for other weaker yet popular coreset guarantees, including weak coresets
[
FL11
,
HV20
]
. That is, for
p<2
, we are in fact the first to resolve Question
1.2
even for weaker notions of coresets, both for existence and efficient constructions. For
p>2
, we obtain the best known construction for weak coresets and the best known efficient construction for spanning coresets. Finally, as we see in more depth later, our algorithm is based off of sampling rows by using scores known as
ridge leverage scores
, which is a primitive known to be have highly efficient algorithms, especially when the underlying matrix has additional structure such as efficient matrix-vector products
[
SW19
]
or positive semidefiniteness
[
MW17
]
.

1.3.1
Nearly optimal online coresets for
\ell_{p}
subspace approximation

Despite the several drawbacks discussed in Section
1.2.1
, strong coresets that use an additional coordinate as in
[
SW18
,
FKW21
]
may still be acceptable for applications to time- and space-efficient algorithms. This is especially true since we have shown that these approaches can be implemented in input sparsity time with nearly optimal dependence on
k
on the coreset size in Appendix
C
. However, we discuss one application of our results which requires our new approach to this problem, which is an application to sampling strong coresets for
\ell_{p}
subspace approximation in the
online coreset model
.

The literature of online algorithms studies algorithms which must make irrevocable decisions as its input arrives incrementally. In online machine learning and data analysis, a typical setting is for new data points to arrive one by one. The algorithm must then immediately make a decision about this data point, such as assigning them to clusters
[
Mey01
]
or outputting a prediction
[
Haz16
]
. The online coreset model, introduced by work of
[
CMP16
]
, studies the question of implementing coreset algorithms in such online settings. More specifically, the rows of our design matrix
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
arrive one by one in a stream, and we must sample the rows of the coreset in an online fashion, that is, we must decide whether or not to keep a row
i\in[n]
when it arrives, and we cannot discard rows that we keep, or retrieve rows that we discard. This model was originally studied in the context of coresets for
\ell_{2}
linear regression
[
CMP16
]
, and has subsequently been analyzed for a variety of problems in data analysis, computational geometry, and numerical linear algebra
[
BLVZ19
,
BDM+20
,
WY22
,
WY23b
,
WY23a
,
MMO23
]
.

For
\ell_{p}
subspace approximation in particular, the works of
[
BLVZ19
,
BDM+20
]
studied the case of
p=2
and achieved a nearly optimal coreset size of
\tilde{O}(k\varepsilon^{-2})
, while
[
WY23a
]
studied the case of
p\neq 2
, achieving a coreset size of roughly
\tilde{O}(k^{p+O(1)})\varepsilon^{-\Theta(p)}
by analyzing an algorithm based on sensitivity sampling
[
HV20
]
.

\tilde{O}(\cdot)

n

\kappa^{\mathsf{OL}}

One of the main open questions left in

Question 1.5
.

Are there online coreset algorithms for constructing strong coresets for
\ell_{p}
subspace approximation of size
\tilde{O}(k)\poly(\varepsilon^{-1})
for
p<2
and
\tilde{O}(k^{p/2})\poly(\varepsilon^{-1})
for
p>2
?

In the online coreset setting, all prior techniques for dimension-free coresets
[
SW18
,
HV20
,
FKW21
]
face a key challenge. The problem is that all of these techniques are based on computing a fixed low-dimensional subspace
S
which is used to construct the coreset. In the online coreset setting, this is a major obstruction, due to the fact that this low-dimensional subspace
S
can evolve as more inputs arrive. In the recent work of
[
WY23a
]
, it was shown that this problem can be circumvented by bounding the number of times that a subspace can undergo a significant change, using the so-called
online Lewis weights

[

]
. However, this technique increases the coreset size by the number of times this subspace changes, which is a factor of
\tilde{O}(k^{\max\{1,p/2\}})
, leading to a suboptimal overall bound of
\tilde{O}(k^{p+O(1)})\poly(\varepsilon^{-1})
. In particular, the extra factor of
k^{p/2}
is a glaring gap in our understanding of this problem, as pointed out by
[
WY23a
]
.

Despite these challenges, our work gives a positive resolution of Question
1.5

\ell_{p}
subspace approximation. One of the key aspects of our techniques will be to avoid a “commitment” to a specific low dimensional subspace via the use of regularization, and will allow us to seamlessly handle the online coreset setting. The technical details are given in Section
4.1

1.4
Technical overview

We will now give a technical overview of our results. In the following discussion,
\mathbf{S}
will always be constructed as a
\ell_{p}
sampling matrix
, with varying choices of sampling probabilities
q_{i}
.

Definition 1.6

(
\ell_{p}
sampling matrix)
.

Let
p\geq 1
. A random diagonal matrix
\mathbf{S}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
is an
\ell_{p}
sampling matrix with sampling probabilities
\{q_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
if for each
i\in[n]
, the
i
th diagonal entry is independently set to be

\mathbf{S}_{i,i}=\begin{cases}1/q_{i}^{1/p}&\text{with probability $q_{i}$}\\
0&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

1.4.1
Pitfalls in prior work

The central technique of
[
SW18
]
is a structural result which shows the existence of a
representative subspace

S\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
s=O(k)\poly(\varepsilon^{-1})
dimensions such that for any
k
-dimensional subspace
F\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

\left\lVert\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{P}_{F})\right\rVert_{p,2}^{p}=(1\pm\varepsilon)\left\lVert[\mathbf{A}\mathbf{P}_{S}(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{P}_{F}),\mathbf{b}_{S}]\right\rVert_{p,2}^{p}

where
\mathbf{b}_{S}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is the vector with
i
-th entry given by
\mathbf{b}_{S}(i)=\left\lVert\mathbf{a}_{i}^{\top}(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{P}_{S})\right\rVert_{2}
, and
[\mathbf{A}\mathbf{P}_{S}(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{P}_{F}),\mathbf{b}_{S}]
is the
n\times(d+1)
matrix formed by the concatenation of
\mathbf{A}\mathbf{P}_{S}(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{P}_{F})
and
\mathbf{b}_{S}
.
3
3

3

We improve the analysis of this result by removing a
1/\varepsilon^{3}
factor from
s
in Appendix
B
.
That is, the
\ell_{p}
subspace approximation cost of
F
can be approximated by the projection cost onto the subspace
S
, plus the additional projection cost of the lower dimensional points
\mathbf{A}\mathbf{P}_{S}
to the query subspace
F
. This reduces the subspace approximation problem in
d
dimensions to a similar problem in
s+1
dimensions. In turn, this lower dimensional problem can be solved using dimension-dependent techniques, since the dimension is now only
s+1=O(k)\poly(\varepsilon^{-1})
. Then to analyze sampling algorithms,
[
SW18
]
show that Dvoretzky’s theorem
[
Dvo61
,
FLM77
,
PVZ17
]
can be applied to convert the problem of approximating the
(p,2)
-norm to a problem of approximating the
(p,p)
-norm, i.e. the entrywise
\ell_{p}
norm, which can then be handled by sampling techniques for approximating
\ell_{p}
norms of vectors in a subspace
[
CP15
,
WY23b
]
. This shows that

\left\lVert\mathbf{S}[\mathbf{A}\mathbf{P}_{S}(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{P}_{F}),\mathbf{b}_{S}]\right\rVert_{p,2}^{p}=(1\pm\varepsilon)\left\lVert[\mathbf{A}\mathbf{P}_{S}(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{P}_{F}),\mathbf{b}_{S}]\right\rVert_{p,2}^{p}

when
\mathbf{S}
is constructed as a subspace embedding for the column space of
[\mathbf{A}\mathbf{P}_{S},\mathbf{b}_{S}]
. Ideally, we would like to apply the same reasoning as before to argue that

\left\lVert\mathbf{S}\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{P}_{F})\right\rVert_{p,2}^{p}=(1\pm\varepsilon)\left\lVert\mathbf{S}[\mathbf{A}\mathbf{P}_{S}(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{P}_{F}),\mathbf{b}_{S}]\right\rVert_{p,2}^{p}

to complete the chain of approximations. Unfortunately, it is unclear whether this step goes through when
\mathbf{S}
is constructed as a subspace embedding of
[\mathbf{A}\mathbf{P}_{S},\mathbf{b}_{S}]
. The intuition for this is that
S
is constructed to be a representative subspace for
\mathbf{A}
using information about
\mathbf{A}

outside
of the column space of
[\mathbf{A}\mathbf{P}_{S},\mathbf{b}_{S}]
, and there is no guarantee that a sampling matrix
\mathbf{S}
that preserves
[\mathbf{A}\mathbf{P}_{S},\mathbf{b}_{S}]
also preserves this information.

On the other hand, the result of
[
HV20
]
takes a different approach based on the classic
sensitivity sampling
technique
[
LS10
,
FL11
,
VX12
]
, and uses the representative subspace constructed
[
SW18
]
in an
existential
manner rather than an algorithmic one. For sensitivity sampling, one first defines
sensitivity scores

\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{i}(\mathbf{A})\coloneqq\sup_{F\in\mathcal{F}_{k}}\frac{\left\lVert\mathbf{a}_{i}^{\top}(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{P}_{F})\right\rVert_{2}^{p}}{\left\lVert\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{P}_{F})\right\rVert_{p,2}^{p}}

(1)

for each row
i\in[n]
which represent the largest fraction of the cost occupied by the
i
-th coordinate, ranging over all queries
F\in\mathcal{F}_{k}
. Then, by Bernstein bounds, it follows that for any fixed
F\in\mathcal{F}_{k}
, sampling the rows
i\in[n]
proportionally to the sensitivity scores preserves
\left\lVert\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{P}_{F})\right\rVert_{p,2}^{p}
up to
(1\pm\varepsilon)
factors. We can then apply this result to every
F
in a net over the space of rank
k
subspaces
F
, which has size roughly
\exp(dk)
, to construct coresets of size
\poly(d,k,\varepsilon^{-1})
. The work of
[
HV20
]
improves this argument by showing that the representative subspace
S
constructed by
[
SW18
]
allows one to derive a strong coreset guarantee from a weak coreset guarantee, which in turn admit dimension-independent bounds
[
FL11
]
. However, the weak coreset argument of
[
FL11
]
loses
\poly(k)
factors.

1.4.2
Ridge leverage scores

Our algorithmic technique takes a drastically different approach compared to the prior works of
[
SW18
,
HV20
,
FKW21
]
. Our starting point is a result of
[
CMM17
]
, which resolves Question
1.2
for the much simpler case of
p=2
. For
p=2
, finding an explicit rank
O(k)\poly(\varepsilon^{-1})
subspace with properties similar to the representative subspace
S
is not difficult due to the singular value decomposition (SVD)
[
DMM06b
,
DMM08
,
CEM+15
,
CMM17
]
. However, as noted by
[
CMM17
]
, while this gives a polynomial time algorithm for low rank approximation for
p=2
, finding these scores is already as hard as low rank approximation itself. Thus, this defeats the purpose of finding the coreset if the goal is to design faster algorithms. To address this problem,
[
CMM17
]
make use of the following alternative scores for a sampling-based algorithm, known as the
ridge leverage scores
.

Definition 1.7

(Ridge leverage scores
[
AM15
,
CMM17
]
)
.

Let
\lambda>0
and
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
. Then, for each
i\in[n]
, the
i
th ridge leverage score is defined as

\boldsymbol{\tau}_{i}^{\lambda}(\mathbf{A})\coloneqq\mathbf{a}_{i}^{\top}(\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A}+\lambda\mathbf{I})^{-1}\mathbf{a}_{i}=\sup_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{[\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}](i)^{2}}{\lVert\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}\rVert_{2}^{2}+\lambda\lVert\mathbf{x}\rVert_{2}^{2}}.

Note that ridge leverage scores can be approximated up to
O(1)
factors in just
\tilde{O}(\nnz(\mathbf{A})+d^{\omega})
time, where
\omega
is the exponent of matrix multiplication
[
SS11
,
DMMW12
,
CW13
,
CLM+15
]
.

The main result of
[
CMM17
]
establishes that if we set
\lambda=\left\lVert\mathbf{A}-\mathbf{A}_{k}\right\rVert_{F}^{2}/k
where
\mathbf{A}_{k}
is the best rank
k
approximation in the Frobenius norm, then sampling
\tilde{O}(k/\varepsilon^{2})
rows
\mathbf{a}_{i}
of
\mathbf{A}
proportionally to their ridge leverage scores yields a strong coreset
\mathbf{S}
of nearly optimal size satisfying Definition
1.1
for
p=2
. Furthermore, the scores
\boldsymbol{\tau}_{i}^{\lambda}(\mathbf{A})
only depend on a constant factor approximation to the
value
of the optimal low rank approximation, which can be obtained more readily than a subspace which (approximately) witnesses this value. Unfortunately, the analysis of
[
CMM17
]
is highly specific to the
\ell_{2}
norm, and makes heavy use of the structural properties of the SVD and the fact that the
(p,2)
-norm is an entrywise norm for
p=2
, and thus does not apply to
p\neq 2
. Nonetheless, several key ideas still do carry over to the setting of
p\neq 2
, which will be crucial to our analysis.

1.4.3
Reduction to embedding low rank matrices

We start with a conceptual simplification of the problem. Our goal is to preserve the objective function
\lVert\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{P}_{F})\rVert_{p,2}^{p}
for all rank
k
subspaces
F
. We will write this as
\lVert\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{P}^{*}-\mathbf{P}_{F})+\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{P}^{*})\rVert_{p,2}^{p}
where
\mathbf{P}^{*}
is the optimal rank
k
projection. That is, we think the objective as the sum of a “low rank” component and a “small fixed” component. Due to the bound on
\lVert\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{P}^{*})\rVert_{p,2}^{p}
and the fact that it is fixed, adding the “small fixed” component turns out to be relatively straightforward; technically, it amounts to replacing guarantees for embedding subspaces to embedding affine spaces. As shown in prior work
[
MMWY22
,
WY24
]
, this can be done even when the affine component is unknown, as long as the sampling probabilities are large enough. We thus focus on preserving the “low rank” component
\lVert\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{P}^{*}-\mathbf{P}_{F})\rVert_{p,2}^{p}
via sampling, that is, we focus on the guarantee that

\lVert\mathbf{S}\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{P}^{*}-\mathbf{P}_{F})\rVert_{p,2}^{p}=(1\pm\varepsilon)\lVert\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{P}^{*}-\mathbf{P}_{F})\rVert_{p,2}^{p}.

In fact, we will only need to use the fact that
\mathbf{P}^{*}-\mathbf{P}_{F}
has low rank and bounded operator norm. Thus, we generalize this to the guarantee that

\lVert\mathbf{S}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{X}\rVert_{p,2}^{p}=(1\pm\varepsilon)\lVert\mathbf{A}\mathbf{X}\rVert_{p,2}^{p}

for every
\mathbf{X}
with rank at most
2k
and operator norm at most
2
. Finally, a crucial observation is that our original objective function is always at least
\mathsf{OPT}
, so we can afford an
additive
error of
\varepsilon\mathsf{OPT}
. That is, it suffices to show the guarantee that

\lVert\mathbf{S}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{X}\rVert_{p,2}^{p}=(1\pm\varepsilon)\lVert\mathbf{A}\mathbf{X}\rVert_{p,2}^{p}\pm\varepsilon\mathsf{OPT}

(2)

in our setting. Thus for the rest of this technical overview, we will focus on showing (
2
).

Note that at this point, our approach already differs from the prior dimension-independent coreset results of
[
SW18
]
and
[
HV20
]
, in that we
directly
tackle the problem of approximating the original objective function via sampling, rather than approximating the objective by a lower-dimensional objective via a representative subspace. This is a critical step: rather than relying on the representative subspace to sample small coresets, we instead rely on the allowance of a small additive error of
\varepsilon\mathsf{OPT}
in sampling primitives, which in turn allow for smaller sample sizes along with other benefits.

Remark 1.8
.

In an earlier version of this work, the guarantee of (
2
) was used in a different way which ensured that (1) the coreset was a subspace embedding for a fixed subspace
S
of dimension
k/\varepsilon^{\max\{2,p\}}
, and (2) the coreset guaranteed that
\lVert\mathbf{S}\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{P}_{S\cup F}-\mathbf{P}_{S})\rVert_{p,2}^{p}=\lVert\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{P}_{S\cup F}-\mathbf{P}_{S})\rVert_{p,2}^{p}\pm\varepsilon\mathsf{OPT}
for every rank-
k
subspace
F
. However, requiring the guarantee of (1) causes the
\varepsilon
dependence to be much worse, and in particular by plugging in the dimension of
k/\varepsilon^{\max\{2,p\}}
into known subspace embedding dimension requirements
[
CP15
,
WY23b
]
, the coreset would need to be of a much larger size of at least
k^{p/2}/\varepsilon^{p^{2}/2}
for
p>2
.

1.4.4
Idea 1: additive-multiplicative
\ell_{p}
subspace embeddings via root ridge leverage scores

We begin by applying Dvoretzky’s theorem, which gives a linear map
\mathbf{H}
such that
\lVert\mathbf{H}^{\top}\mathbf{x}\rVert_{p}^{p}=(1\pm\varepsilon)\lVert\mathbf{x}\rVert_{2}^{p}
for every
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, so that
\lVert\mathbf{A}\mathbf{X}\rVert_{p,2}^{p}=(1\pm\varepsilon)\lVert\mathbf{A}\mathbf{X}\mathbf{H}\rVert_{p,p}^{p}
. This decouples the norm of the columns, reducing our problem to preserving the
\ell_{p}
norm of vectors of the form
\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}
, i.e., we wish to show
\lVert\mathbf{S}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}\rVert_{p}^{p}\approx\lVert\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}\rVert_{p}^{p}
. If the approximation guarantee is purely multiplicative, i.e.
\lVert\mathbf{S}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}\rVert_{p}^{p}=(1\pm\varepsilon)\lVert\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}\rVert_{p}^{p}
, then this guarantee is known in the literature as an
\ell_{p}
subspace embedding

[
CP15
,
WY23b
]
. However, this guarantee is too strong, and requires coreset size depending polynomially on
d
. We thus need a relaxation of this guarantee to achieve small coresets.

The first new ingredient in our analysis is to adapt the
additive-multiplicative
\ell_{2}
subspace embedding
idea of
[
CMM17
]
. In this result,
[
CMM17
]
show that a sampling matrix
\mathbf{S}
with probabilities proportional to the ridge leverage scores
\boldsymbol{\tau}_{i}^{\lambda}(\mathbf{A})
with
\lambda=\lVert\mathbf{A}-\mathbf{A}_{k}\rVert_{F}^{2}/k
satisfies the additive-multiplicative guarantee

\left\lVert\mathbf{S}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}=(1\pm\varepsilon)\left\lVert\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\pm\varepsilon\lambda\lVert\mathbf{x}\rVert_{2}^{2}\qquad\mbox{for every $\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}$}

with only
\tilde{O}(k/\varepsilon^{2})
samples. This fact immediately follows from applying the more standard guarantee for
leverage score sampling
on a concatenated matrix
[\mathbf{A};\sqrt{\lambda}\mathbf{I}]\in\mathbb{R}^{(n+d)\times d}
, where
\mathbf{I}
is the
d\times d
identity.

For an
\ell_{p}
version of this result, a recent result of
[
WY23c
]
shows an analysis of
root leverage score sampling
for nearly optimal
\ell_{p}
subspace embeddings for
p<2
, where the sampling probabilities are taken to be proportional to
\boldsymbol{\tau}_{i}(\mathbf{A})^{p/2}
, i.e., the
(p/2)
-th roots of the leverage score
\boldsymbol{\tau}_{i}(\mathbf{A})
. Although the row sample size is roughly
\tilde{O}(\varepsilon^{-2}n^{1-p/2}d^{p/2})
, which depends on
n
, this result can be applied recursively for
O(\log\log n)
applications to reduce the sample complexity down to
\tilde{O}(\varepsilon^{-4/p}d)
, which is nearly linear in
d
and thus nearly optimal. We show that a similar analysis in fact also works for
p>2
, where we can reduce the number of rows to
\tilde{O}(\varepsilon^{-2}n^{1-2/p}d)
in one round and
\tilde{O}(\varepsilon^{-p}d^{p/2})
rows by applying this result recursively.

With the
\ell_{p}
subspace embedding theorem in hand, we can now apply a similar trick as
[
CMM17
]
. We set
\lambda=\left\lVert\mathbf{A}-\mathbf{A}_{k}\right\rVert_{F}^{2}/k
and compute a subspace embedding for the matrix formed by concatenating
\mathbf{A}
with
\sqrt{\lambda}\mathbf{I}
via root leverage score sampling. This is equivalent to root ridge leverage score sampling for the matrix
\mathbf{A}
, whose scores sum to
k
. Thus, in each step of the recursive sampling, the number of rows sampled is
\tilde{O}(\varepsilon^{-2}n^{1-p/2}k^{p/2})
for
p<2
and
\tilde{O}(\varepsilon^{-2}n^{1-2/p}k)
for
p>2
. After
O(\log\log n)
steps of recursion, the final sample size is
\tilde{O}(\varepsilon^{-4/p}k)
rows for
p<2
and
\tilde{O}(\varepsilon^{-p}k^{p/2})
rows for
p>2
. We note that coreset sizes we obtain in Theorems
1.3
and
1.4
are a direct result of this recursive sampling scheme. Furthermore, the resulting additive-multiplicative subspace embedding guarantee is

\left\lVert\mathbf{S}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}\right\rVert_{p}^{p}=(1\pm\varepsilon)\left\lVert\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}\right\rVert_{p}^{p}\pm\varepsilon\lambda^{p/2}\left\lVert\mathbf{x}\right\rVert_{p}^{p}.

Now, we can apply the above
\ell_{p}
additive-multiplicative subspace embedding guarantees for the sampling matrix
\mathbf{S}
on each column of
\left\lVert\mathbf{A}\mathbf{X}\mathbf{H}\right\rVert_{p,p}^{p}
to obtain the approximation guarantee

\left\lVert\mathbf{S}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{X}\mathbf{H}\right\rVert_{p,p}^{p}=(1\pm\varepsilon)\left\lVert\mathbf{A}\mathbf{X}\mathbf{H}\right\rVert_{p,p}^{p}\pm\varepsilon\lambda^{p/2}\left\lVert\mathbf{X}\mathbf{H}\right\rVert_{p,p}^{p}.

Now by applying Dvoretzky’s theorem again to map the
(p,p)
-norm back to the
(p,2)
-norm, we obtain

\left\lVert\mathbf{S}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{X}\right\rVert_{p,2}^{p}=(1\pm\varepsilon)\left\lVert\mathbf{A}\mathbf{X}\right\rVert_{p,2}^{p}\pm\varepsilon\lambda^{p/2}\left\lVert\mathbf{X}\right\rVert_{p,2}^{p}.

Finally, it remains to bound
\lambda^{p/2}\left\lVert\mathbf{X}\right\rVert_{p,2}^{p}
, but here we will encounter some problems.

1.4.5
Problems when bounding the additive error

To bound the additive error
\lambda^{p/2}\left\lVert\mathbf{X}\right\rVert_{p,2}^{p}
, we will case on
p<2
and
p>2
. We may assume without loss of generality that
\mathbf{X}
has at most
n
rows, by restricting to the row span of
\mathbf{A}
. Then for
p<2
,
\lambda^{p/2}
is at most

\lambda^{p/2}=\frac{\lVert\mathbf{A}-\mathbf{A}_{k}\rVert_{F}^{p}}{k^{p/2}}\leq\frac{\lVert\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{P}^{*})\rVert_{F}^{p}}{k^{p/2}}\leq\frac{\lVert\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{P}^{*})\rVert_{p,2}^{p}}{k^{p/2}}=\frac{\mathsf{OPT}}{k^{p/2}}

(3)

by the monotonicity of
\ell_{p}
norms, while for
p>2
,
\lambda^{p/2}
is at most

\lambda^{p/2}=\frac{\left\lVert\mathbf{A}-\mathbf{A}_{k}\right\rVert_{F}^{p}}{k^{p/2}}\leq\frac{\left\lVert\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{P}^{*})\right\rVert_{F}^{p}}{k^{p/2}}\leq\frac{n^{p/2-1}\left\lVert\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{P}^{*})\right\rVert_{p,2}^{p}}{k^{p/2}}=\frac{n^{p/2-1}\mathsf{OPT}}{k^{p/2}}.

(4)

Furthermore,

\lVert\mathbf{X}\rVert_{p,2}^{p}\leq\begin{cases}n^{1-p/2}\lVert\mathbf{X}\rVert_{2,2}^{p}&\text{if $p<2$}\\
\lVert\mathbf{X}\rVert_{2,2}^{p}&\text{if $p>2$}\end{cases}\leq\begin{cases}n^{1-p/2}k^{p/2}&\text{if $p<2$}\\
k^{p/2}&\text{if $p>2$}\end{cases}

by using that
\rank(\mathbf{X})\leq k
and
\lVert\mathbf{X}\rVert_{2}\leq 1
. Then overall, we obtain a bound of

\lambda^{p/2}\lVert\mathbf{X}\rVert_{p,2}^{p}\leq\begin{cases}n^{1-p/2}\mathsf{OPT}&\text{if $p<2$}\\
n^{p/2-1}\mathsf{OPT}&\text{if $p>2$}\end{cases}

which are off by
\poly(n)
factors from
\mathsf{OPT}
, which is the error that we can tolerate. In order to fix this problem and improve our analysis by
\poly(n)
factors, we will use two different types of “flattening” tricks, one for
p<2
and one for
p>2
, which we discuss in the next two sections.

1.4.6
Idea 2: splitting rows for sharper additive error bounds for
p<2

To overcome the previous issue for
p<2
, we will sharpen the bound of (
3
). The loose bound that we will tighten is bounding the Frobenius norm loss
\lVert\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{P}^{*})\rVert_{F}^{p}
by the
(p,2)
-norm loss
\lVert\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{P}^{*})\rVert_{p,2}^{p}
. For general matrices, this bound is indeed tight since the rows of
\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{P}^{*})
could be imbalanced so that most of the mass is concentrated on a few rows. However, this bound is loose when the rows are flat, in which case there can be a
\poly(n)
factor separation in the two quantities. We will show how to recover this separation.

A classic result of
[
VX12
]
shows that the sensitivity scores (
1
) for
\ell_{p}
subspace approximation sum to at most
O(k)
for
p<2
. Then, a standard flattening argument shows that by replacing rows
\mathbf{a}_{i}
with large sensitivity with
l
copies of the scaled row
\mathbf{a}_{i}/l^{1/p}
, we obtain a new matrix
\mathbf{A}^{\prime}
with
n^{\prime}\leq 2n
rows that are each just scaled copies of rows of
\mathbf{A}
, such that
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{i^{\prime}}(\mathbf{A}^{\prime})=O(k/n)
for every row
i^{\prime}\in[n^{\prime}]
and
\lVert\mathbf{A}^{\prime}(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{P}_{F})\rVert_{p,2}^{p}=\lVert\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{P}_{F})\rVert_{p,2}^{p}
for every
F\in\mathcal{F}_{k}
. Because this matrix is now flat, it can be shown that

\lVert\mathbf{A}^{\prime}(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{P}^{*})\rVert_{F}^{2}\lesssim\left\lparen k/n\right\rparen^{2/p-1}\mathsf{OPT}^{2/p}.

Thus by replacing
\mathbf{A}
with
\mathbf{A}^{\prime}
, we obtain a matrix formed by the rows of
\mathbf{A}
that gives the same objective function, yet has a much smaller additive error when bounding
\lambda
, giving

\lambda^{p/2}=\frac{\lVert\mathbf{A}^{\prime}-\mathbf{A}_{k}^{\prime}\rVert_{F}^{p}}{k^{p/2}}\leq\frac{\lVert\mathbf{A}^{\prime}(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{P}^{*})\rVert_{F}^{p}}{k^{p/2}}\leq(k/n)^{1-p/2}\frac{\lVert\mathbf{A}^{\prime}(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{P}^{*})\rVert_{p,2}^{p}}{k^{p/2}}=(k/n)^{1-p/2}\frac{\mathsf{OPT}}{k^{p/2}}.

rather than the original bound in (
3
). We note, however, that this argument is still lossy, since the standard sensitivity-based flattening argument would flatten
any
matrix, whereas we only need this result for a single constant factor approximate subspace
\tilde{F}
. Thus, we instead explicitly compute a constant factor bicriteria solution, which can be done very quickly
[
DTV11
,
FKW21
,
WY23a
]
(see Lemma
2.3
), and flatten this particular solution nearly optimally, so that we instead get the bound

\lambda^{p/2}=\frac{\lVert\mathbf{A}^{\prime}-\mathbf{A}_{k}^{\prime}\rVert_{F}^{p}}{k^{p/2}}\leq\frac{\lVert\mathbf{A}^{\prime}(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{P}_{\tilde{F}})\rVert_{F}^{p}}{k^{p/2}}\leq(1/n)^{1-p/2}\frac{\lVert\mathbf{A}^{\prime}(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{P}_{\tilde{F}})\rVert_{p,2}^{p}}{k^{p/2}}=n^{p/2-1}\frac{O(\mathsf{OPT})}{k^{p/2}}.

Thus, we recover the extra factor of
n^{1-p/2}
lost when converting from the
\ell_{p}
norm to the
\ell_{2}
norm. This completes our proof sketch for
p<2
.

1.4.7
Idea 3: Dvoretzky’s theorem for sharper additive error bounds for
p>2

To improve our argument for
p>2
, we note that we have an additional degree of freedom when choosing to concatenate
\mathbf{A}
with
\sqrt{\lambda}\mathbf{I}
when analyzing the ridge leverage score sampling algorithm. Indeed, as long as we concatenate
\mathbf{A}
with
\sqrt{\lambda}\mathbf{U}
for any orthonormal matrix
\mathbf{U}
, then the leverage scores of
\mathbf{A}
concatenated with
\sqrt{\lambda}\mathbf{U}
will have leverage scores which coincide with the ridge leverage scores of
\mathbf{A}
, since

\mathbf{a}_{i}^{\top}(\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A}+\lambda\mathbf{U}^{\top}\mathbf{U})^{-1}\mathbf{a}_{i}=\mathbf{a}_{i}^{\top}(\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A}+\lambda\mathbf{I})^{-1}\mathbf{a}_{i}.

The resulting guarantee is that

\left\lVert\mathbf{S}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}\right\rVert_{p}^{p}=(1\pm\varepsilon)\left\lVert\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}\right\rVert_{p}^{p}\pm\varepsilon\lambda^{p/2}\left\lVert\mathbf{U}\mathbf{x}\right\rVert_{p}^{p},

(5)

so we may select
\mathbf{U}
to be an orthonormal matrix which makes this additive error as small as possible. We will choose
\mathbf{U}
to be a random
n\times d
orthonormal matrix
\mathbf{G}
scaled by
n^{-1/2}
, which has the advantage of flattening the mass of
\mathbf{x}
and thus minimizing the
\ell_{p}
norm.

By Dvoretzky’s theorem
[
Dvo61
,
FLM77
,
PVZ17
]
, it follows that as long as
n
is at least
\tilde{O}(k^{p/2})\poly(\varepsilon^{-1})=\tilde{O}(k^{p/2})\poly(\varepsilon^{-1})
, then for any
\mathbf{x}
in a fixed
k
-dimensional subspace, we will have that

\frac{1}{n^{p/2}}\left\lVert\mathbf{G}\mathbf{x}\right\rVert_{p}^{p}=(1\pm\varepsilon)n^{1-p/2}\left\lVert\mathbf{x}\right\rVert_{2}^{p}.

(6)

This cancels out with the factor of
n^{p/2-1}
that we lost in (
4
), giving us a sharp enough additive error. It may be tempting to reduce the additive error even further by choosing
\mathbf{G}
to have
m\gg n
rows rather than just
n
. However, this would affect the total number of rows sampled, since we would then need to oversample the root ridge leverage scores by a factor of
m^{p/2-1}
, which would increase the sample complexity. Note also that once the additive error is sufficiently small, (
5
) would give the purely multiplicative subspace embedding guarantee
\lVert\mathbf{S}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}\rVert_{p}^{p}=(1\pm\varepsilon)\lVert\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}\rVert_{p}^{p}
, for which there is a sample complexity lower bound of
\Omega(d^{p/2}/\varepsilon)

[
LWW21
]
.

Although we have fixed the
n^{p/2-1}
factor, we must now address a subtle issue. The above analysis works for a fixed rank
k
subspace specified by the low rank matrix
\mathbf{X}
. However, if we want this guarantee for every rank
k
matrix
\mathbf{X}
with operator norm
1
as we need, then we run into problems, since for any fixed embedding
\mathbf{G}
of dimension only
\tilde{O}(k^{p/2})\poly(\varepsilon^{-1})
, there exists a choice of
\mathbf{X}
which causes (
6
) to fail. To fix our final problem, we consider sampling
\poly(n)
many random matrices
\mathbf{U}
and use the fact that our sampling theorem will succeed for all of these
\mathbf{U}
by a union bound, with only a
\poly\log n
overhead in the sample complexity. Then for any fixed
\mathbf{X}
, the probability that the previous analysis fails for all of the copies of
\mathbf{U}
is at most
\exp(-\poly(n))
, so this success probability is high enough to union bound over a discretization of all rank
k
matrices
\mathbf{X}
. This completes our proof sketch for
p>2
.

1.4.8
Technical summary

Our analysis introduces major new ideas involving ridge leverage scores, giving the first understanding of their behavior for norms other than the very algebraic case of
p=2
. We achieve low-error additive-multiplicative affine embeddings using ridge leverage scores for these norms via novel flattening techniques both for
p<2
and for
p>2
. Our techniques significantly reduce the regularization term, which may be of independent interest given the wide applicability of ridge leverage scores. Our additive-multiplicative affine embeddings are then used to embed the
(p,2)
-norm of matrices of the form of a “low rank” component plus a “small fixed” component, which exactly captures the
\ell_{p}
subspace approximation objective function. In particular, ridge leverage scores enable us to avoid resorting to representative subspaces which would yield suboptimal bounds.

1.5
Corollaries

1.5.1
Streaming algorithms

A simple corollary of our nearly optimal constructions for strong coresets is that we immediately obtain similar results in
streaming
model of computation. In the streaming model, the rows
\mathbf{a}_{i}
of the input matrix
\mathbf{A}
arrive one at a time, and we wish to maintain a strong coreset for
\mathbf{A}
. In this setting, the classic
merge-and-reduce
technique (see, e.g.,
[
BDM+20
]
for a discussion) shows that a construction for a coreset of size
\tilde{O}(k^{c})\poly(\varepsilon^{-1})
can be converted into a streaming implementation of size
\tilde{O}(k^{c})\poly(\varepsilon^{-1}\log n)
by setting the accuracy parameter to
\varepsilon^{\prime}=\varepsilon/\log n
and composing the coreset construction in a binary tree fashion. Recent work of
[
CWZ23
]
shows that this argument can in fact be sharpened to a
\poly(\log\log n)
factor overhead rather than
\poly(\log n)
, by first computing an online coreset (see Section
1.3.1
), as we show in Section
4.2
.

1.5.2
Entrywise
\ell_{p}
low rank approximation

For
p<2
, our nearly optimal coresets for
\ell_{p}
subspace approximation imply new algorithms for the related problem of
entrywise
\ell_{p}
low rank approximation
.

Definition 1.9
.

Let
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
and let
k
be a rank parameter. Let
1\leq p<\infty
. Then, the
entrywise
\ell_{p}
low rank approximation
problem is the problem of minimizing the objective function

\lVert\mathbf{A}-\mathbf{X}\rVert_{p,p}^{p}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\left\lvert(\mathbf{A}-\mathbf{X})_{i,j}\right\rvert^{p}

among all rank
k
matrices
\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
.

This problem is another computationally difficult variant of the low rank approximation problem, and approximation algorithms and hardness have been studied in a long line of work
[
SWZ17
,
CGK+17
,
DWZ+19
,
MW21
,
JLL+21
,
WY23a
]
. The works of
[
JLL+21
,
WY23a
]
show that for
p<2
, if we multiply
\mathbf{A}
on the right by a dense matrix
\mathbf{G}
of
p
-stable random variables
[
Nol20
]
and then compute an
\ell_{p}
subspace approximation coreset
\mathbf{S}
of
\mathbf{A}\mathbf{G}
of size
\tilde{O}(k)
, then there exists a rank
k
matrix
\mathbf{V}
such that

\lVert\mathbf{A}-\mathbf{V}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{A}\rVert_{p,p}^{p}\leq\tilde{O}(k^{1/p-1/2})\min_{\rank(\mathbf{X})\leq k}\lVert\mathbf{A}-\mathbf{X}\rVert_{p,p}^{p}.

Among subset selection algorithms, this approximation guarantee is nearly optimal
[
MW21
]
. Furthermore, because
\mathbf{S}
is constructed based on sketching and coresets for
\ell_{p}
subspace approximation, this algorithm can be implemented in streaming and distributed settings, and previously discussed. However, these prior results had drawbacks. The result of
[
JLL+21
]
relied on the coreset construction of
[
SW18
]
, and thus does not give a true row subset, meaning that the subset selection lower bounds of
[
MW21
]
do not apply. In the work of
[
WY23a
]
, this idea was applied in the setting of online coresets, but their online coreset required a size of at least
k^{4}
, which resulted in a suboptimal approximation factor of at least
k^{4(1/p-1/2)}
. Our result fixes both of these problems, by giving a true row subset implementation of this result, as well as the first online coreset algorithm which selects
\tilde{O}(k)
rows with a
\tilde{O}(k^{1/p-1/2})
distortion.

Preliminaries

For a matrix
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
, we write
\mathbf{A}=\mathbf{U}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\mathbf{V}^{\top}
for the singular value decomposition (SVD) of
\mathbf{A}
. For a rank parameter
k
, we let
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k}
denote the matrix
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
with all but the top
k
singular values zeroed out,
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\setminus k}
for the matrix
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
with all but the bottom
d-k
singular values zeroed out, and
\mathbf{A}_{k}=\mathbf{U}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k}\mathbf{V}^{\top}
for the optimal rank
k
approximation of
\mathbf{A}
under the Frobenius norm.

For a subspace
F
, we let
\mathbf{V}_{F}
denote some orthonormal basis for the subspace.

2.1
Facts about Gaussians

2.1.1
Dvoretzky’s theorem

A classic result of Dvoretzky and Milman
[
Dvo61
,
Mil71
]
shows that a random subspace of a normed space is approximately Euclidean. We will need the following version of this result for
\ell_{p}
norms:

Theorem 2.1

(Dvoretzky’s theorem for
\ell_{p}
norms
[
FLM77
,
PVZ17
]
)
.

Let
1\leq p<\infty
and
0<\varepsilon<1/p
. Let
n\geq O(\max\{\varepsilon^{-2}k,\varepsilon^{-1}k^{p/2}\})
, and let
\mathbf{G}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times k}
be an i.i.d. random Gaussian matrix. Then,

\Pr\left\{\mbox{for all $\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}$, }\left\lVert\mathbf{G}\mathbf{x}\right\rVert_{p}^{p}=(1\pm\varepsilon)n\left\lVert\mathbf{x}\right\rVert_{2}^{p}\right\}\geq\frac{2}{3}

2.1.2
Random linear combinations

The next lemma shows that the
(p,2)
-norm of a matrix
\mathbf{X}
can be estimated by the
\ell_{p}
norm of
\mathbf{X}\mathbf{g}
for a Gaussian vector
\mathbf{g}
.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
. Fix a constant
p>0
. Then, for each
\delta\in(0,1)
,

\Pr_{\mathbf{g}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\mathbf{I}_{d})}\left\{\delta\lVert\mathbf{X}\mathbf{g}\rVert_{p}^{p}\lesssim\lVert\mathbf{X}\rVert_{p,2}^{p}\lesssim\frac{1}{\delta^{p}}\lVert\mathbf{X}\mathbf{g}\rVert_{p}^{p}\right\}\geq 1-\delta.

Proof.

Note that
\mathbf{e}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{X}\mathbf{g}
is distributed as a standard Gaussian with variance
\lVert\mathbf{e}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{X}\rVert_{2}^{2}
.

For the first bound, we have
\E\lvert\mathbf{e}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{X}\mathbf{g}\rvert^{p}\lesssim\lVert\mathbf{e}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{X}\rVert_{2}^{p}
, so by Markov’s inequality,
\lVert\mathbf{X}\mathbf{g}\rVert_{p}^{p}\lesssim(1/\delta)\lVert\mathbf{X}\rVert_{p}^{p}
with probability at least
1-\delta/2
.

For the second bound, note that
\lvert\mathbf{e}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{X}\mathbf{g}\rvert\gtrsim\delta\lVert\mathbf{e}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{X}\rVert_{2}
with probability at least
1-\delta/4
. Then,

\E\left[\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lVert\mathbf{e}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{X}\rVert_{2}^{p}\cdot\mathbbm{1}\left\{\lvert\mathbf{e}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{X}\mathbf{g}\rvert\lesssim\delta\lVert\mathbf{e}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{X}\rVert_{2}\right\}\right]\leq\frac{\delta}{4}\lVert\mathbf{X}\rVert_{p,2}^{p}

so by Markov’s inequality, this is at most
\lVert\mathbf{X}\rVert_{p,2}^{p}/2
with probability at least
1-\delta/2
. Note also that

\displaystyle\lVert\mathbf{X}\mathbf{g}\rVert_{p}^{p}

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lvert\mathbf{e}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{X}\mathbf{g}\rvert^{p}\cdot\mathbbm{1}\left\{\lvert\mathbf{e}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{X}\mathbf{g}\rvert\gtrsim\delta\lVert\mathbf{e}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{X}\rVert_{2}\right\}

\displaystyle\gtrsim\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta^{p}\lVert\mathbf{e}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{X}\rVert_{2}^{p}\cdot\mathbbm{1}\left\{\lvert\mathbf{e}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{X}\mathbf{g}\rvert\gtrsim\delta\lVert\mathbf{e}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{X}\rVert_{2}\right\}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta^{p}\lVert\mathbf{e}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{X}\rVert_{2}^{p}\cdot\left\lparen 1-\mathbbm{1}\left\{\lvert\mathbf{e}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{X}\mathbf{g}\rvert\lesssim\delta\lVert\mathbf{e}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{X}\rVert_{2}\right\}\right\rparen

Thus with probability at least
1-\delta/2
, we have that

\frac{1}{\delta^{p}}\lVert\mathbf{X}\mathbf{g}\rVert_{p}^{p}\geq\lVert\mathbf{X}\rVert_{p,2}^{p}-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lVert\mathbf{e}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{X}\rVert_{2}^{p}\cdot\mathbbm{1}\left\{\lvert\mathbf{e}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{X}\mathbf{g}\rvert\lesssim\delta\lVert\mathbf{e}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{X}\rVert_{2}\right\}\geq\frac{1}{2}\lVert\mathbf{X}\rVert_{p,2}^{p}.\qed

2.2
Flattening

It is known that constant factor bicriteria solutions for
\ell_{p}
subspace approximation can be computed quickly via convex relaxations
[
DTV11
]
or by combining sketching techniques with
\ell_{p}
Lewis weight sampling
[
FKW21
,
WY23a
]
. The following lemma gives a version of
[
WY23a
, Algorithm 3]
that is optimized for running time.

Lemma 2.3

(Fast constant factor approximation)
.

Let
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
,
1\leq p\leq 2
, and
k\in\mathbb{N}
. Let
\mathbf{G}\in\mathbb{R}^{t\times d}
be a sparse embedding matrix
[
NN13
,
Coh16
]
with
t=O(k\log(n/\delta))
and sparsity
s=O(\log(n/\delta))
. Let
\tilde{F}
denote the span of
O(t\log(t/\delta))
rows sampled according to the
\ell_{p}
Lewis weights of
\mathbf{A}\mathbf{G}^{\top}

[
CP15
]
. Then, with probability at least
1-\delta
, the following hold:

•

\lVert\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{P}_{\tilde{F}})\rVert_{p,2}^{p}\leq O(\mathsf{OPT}).

•

The subspace
\tilde{F}
can be computed in
\tilde{O}(\nnz(\mathbf{A})+t^{\omega})
time

Proof.

The correctness is shown in
[
WY23a
]
, so it remains to argue the running time. The sparse embedding matrix
\mathbf{G}
only requires time
\tilde{O}(\nnz(\mathbf{A})\log(1/\delta))
to apply due to its sparsity. The
\ell_{p}
Lewis weights of
\mathbf{A}\mathbf{G}^{\top}
can then be computed in time
\tilde{O}(\nnz(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{G}^{\top})+t^{\omega})=\tilde{O}(\nnz(\mathbf{A})+t^{\omega})

[
CP15
]
.
∎

By using Lemma
2.3
, we will obtain a fast algorithm for quickly
flattening
a matrix by splitting rows, which will be a crucial component of our sampling algorithm for
p<2
. Similar techniques have long been used in the literature of
\ell_{p}
subspace embeddings
[
BLM89
,
CP15
,
MMWY22
,
WY23b
]
.

Lemma 2.4

(Flattening)
.

Let
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
,
1\leq p<\infty
, and
k\in\mathbb{N}
. Let
\tilde{F}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a fixed subspace. Then, there is an
n^{\prime}\times d
matrix
\mathbf{A}^{\prime}
with
n\leq n^{\prime}\leq(3/2)n
such that
\lVert\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{P}_{F})\rVert_{p,2}^{p}=\lVert\mathbf{A}^{\prime}(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{P}_{F})\rVert_{p,2}^{p}
for every
F\in\mathcal{F}_{k}
and

\lVert\mathbf{a}_{i}^{\prime\top}(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{P}_{\tilde{F}})\rVert_{2}^{p}\leq\frac{2}{n}\lVert\mathbf{A}^{\prime}(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{P}_{\tilde{F}})\rVert_{p,2}^{p}

for every
i\in[n^{\prime}]
. Furthermore, the rows of
\mathbf{A}^{\prime}
are reweighted rows of
\mathbf{A}
.

Proof.

The proof follows, e.g.,
[
MMWY22
, Lemma 2.10]
. Note that if we replace a row
\mathbf{a}_{i}
by
l
copies of the scaled row
\mathbf{a}_{i}/l^{1/p}
, then
\lVert\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{P}_{F})\rVert_{p,2}^{p}=\lVert\mathbf{A}^{\prime}(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{P}_{F})\rVert_{p,2}^{p}
and for every row
i^{\prime}
in
\mathbf{A}^{\prime}
that is a copy of
\mathbf{A}
,
\lVert\mathbf{a}_{i^{\prime}}^{\prime\top}(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{P}_{\tilde{F}})\rVert_{2}^{p}=\lVert\mathbf{a}_{i}^{\top}(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{P}_{\tilde{F}})\rVert_{2}^{p}/l
. Now for every row
i
in
\mathbf{A}
such that
\lVert\mathbf{a}_{i}^{\top}(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{P}_{\tilde{F}})\rVert_{2}^{p}\geq 2\lVert\mathbf{A}^{\top}(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{P}_{\tilde{F}})\rVert_{p,2}^{p}/n
, replace the row
\mathbf{a}_{i}
with

l_{i}\coloneqq\left\lceil\frac{\lVert\mathbf{a}_{i}^{\top}(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{P}_{\tilde{F}})\rVert_{2}^{p}/\lVert\mathbf{A}^{\top}(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{P}_{\tilde{F}})\rVert_{p,2}^{p}}{2/n}\right\rceil

copies of
\mathbf{a}_{i}/l_{i}^{1/p}
. Note then that the number of rows we add is at most

\sum_{i=1}^{n}(l_{i}-1)\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\lVert\mathbf{a}_{i}^{\top}(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{P}_{\tilde{F}})\rVert_{2}^{p}/\lVert\mathbf{A}^{\top}(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{P}_{\tilde{F}})\rVert_{p,2}^{p}}{2/n}\leq\frac{n}{2}.

Furthermore, by construction, every row in the new matrix
\mathbf{A}^{\prime}
satisfies

\lVert\mathbf{a}_{i}^{\prime\top}(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{P}_{\tilde{F}})\rVert_{2}^{p}\leq\frac{2}{n}\lVert\mathbf{A}^{\prime}(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{P}_{\tilde{F}})\rVert_{p,2}^{p}.

∎

The advantage of flattening is that for
p<2
, it makes the
\ell_{2}
subspace approximation cost much smaller than the
\ell_{p}
subspace approximation cost. We will exploit the following result later in our results for
p<2
.

Lemma 2.5
.

Let
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
,
1\leq p\leq 2
, and
k\in\mathbb{N}
. Let
\tilde{F}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a subspace. Suppose that

\lVert\mathbf{a}^{\top}_{i}(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{P}_{\tilde{F}})\rVert_{2}^{p}\leq\frac{C}{n}\lVert\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{P}_{\tilde{F}})\rVert_{p,2}^{p}

for every
i\in[n]
. Then, we have

\lVert\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{P}_{\tilde{F}})\rVert_{F}\leq(C/n)^{1/p-1/2}\lVert\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{P}_{\tilde{F}})\rVert_{p,2}.

Proof.

We have

\displaystyle\lVert\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{P}_{\tilde{F}})\rVert_{F}^{2}

It is known that for

Let

Next, we show that ridge leverage scores upper bound the

Lemma 2.7

(Ridge leverage scores bound sensitivities)
.

Let
\lambda=\left\lVert\mathbf{A}-\mathbf{A}_{k}\right\rVert_{F}^{2}/k

Proof.

Note that

\left\lVert\mathbf{A}-\mathbf{A}_{2k}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}=\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{k+1}^{2}(\mathbf{A}-\mathbf{A}_{k})\leq\frac{1}{k}\sum_{j=1}^{k}\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{j}^{2}(\mathbf{A}-\mathbf{A}_{k})\leq\frac{\lVert\mathbf{A}-\mathbf{A}_{k}\rVert_{F}^{2}}{k}=\lambda

so

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\tau}_{i}^{\lambda}(\mathbf{A})

\displaystyle=\sup_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{[\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}](i)^{2}}{\lVert\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}\rVert_{2}^{2}+\lambda\lVert\mathbf{x}\rVert_{2}^{2}}

\displaystyle=\sup_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{[\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}](i)^{2}}{\lVert\mathbf{A}_{2k}\mathbf{x}\rVert_{2}^{2}+\lVert(\mathbf{A}-\mathbf{A}_{2k})\mathbf{x}\rVert_{2}^{2}+\lambda\lVert\mathbf{x}\rVert_{2}^{2}}

\displaystyle\geq\sup_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{[\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}](i)^{2}}{\lVert\mathbf{A}_{2k}\mathbf{x}\rVert_{2}^{2}+2\lambda\lVert\mathbf{x}\rVert_{2}^{2}}.

Now let
F\in\mathcal{F}_{k}
be any rank
k
subspace. Let
G
denote the span of the rows of
\mathbf{A}_{2k}
,
F
, and
\mathbf{a}_{i}
, which is a subspace of dimension at most

. We then set
\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{P}_{G}(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{P}_{F})\mathbf{g}
for a standard normal Gaussian vector
\mathbf{g}
. Note then that

[\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}](i)=\mathbf{a}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{P}_{G}(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{P}_{F})\mathbf{g}=\mathbf{a}_{i}^{\top}(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{P}_{F})\mathbf{g}

is distributed as a Gaussian with variance
\lVert\mathbf{a}_{i}^{\top}(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{P}_{F})\rVert_{2}^{2}

\Pr\left\{[\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}](i)^{2}\geq\lVert\mathbf{a}_{i}^{\top}(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{P}_{F})\rVert_{2}^{2}/3\right\}>\frac{1}{2}.

Note also that

\E\left[\lVert\mathbf{A}_{2k}\mathbf{x}\rVert_{2}^{2}\right]=\E\left[\lVert\mathbf{A}_{2k}\mathbf{P}_{G}(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{P}_{F})\mathbf{g}\rVert_{2}^{2}\right]=\E\left[\lVert\mathbf{A}_{2k}(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{P}_{F})\mathbf{g}\rVert_{2}^{2}\right]\leq\lVert\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{P}_{F})\rVert_{F}^{2}

\E[\lambda\lVert\mathbf{x}\rVert_{2}^{2}]=\E[\lambda\lVert\mathbf{P}_{G}(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{P}_{F})\mathbf{g}\rVert_{2}^{2}]\leq\lambda(3k+1)\leq 4\lVert\mathbf{A}-\mathbf{A}_{k}\rVert_{F}^{2}.

Then by Markov’s inequality, we have

\Pr\left\{\lVert\mathbf{A}_{2k}\mathbf{x}\rVert_{2}^{2}+2\lambda\lVert\mathbf{x}\rVert_{2}^{2}\leq 16\lVert\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{P}_{F})\rVert_{F}^{2}\right\}\geq\frac{1}{2}.

Thus with positive probability, there exists a vector
\mathbf{x}
such that

\boldsymbol{\tau}_{i}^{\lambda}(\mathbf{A})\geq\frac{[\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}](i)^{2}}{\lVert\mathbf{A}_{2k}\mathbf{x}\rVert_{2}^{2}+2\lambda\lVert\mathbf{x}\rVert_{2}^{2}}\geq\frac{1}{48}\frac{\lVert\mathbf{a}_{i}^{\top}(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{P}_{F})\rVert_{2}^{2}}{\lVert\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{P}_{F})\rVert_{F}^{2}}.

Since

as required by Lemma

\displaystyle\lVert\mathbf{S}\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{P}_{F})\rVert_{p,2}^{p}

Finally, we show that by applying Theorem

Theorem 3.7
.

Let
p<2
. Let
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
. There is an algorithm that runs in time

\mathbf{S}

\nnz(\mathbf{S})=\frac{O(k)}{\varepsilon^{4/p}}\left[(\log n)^{6/p+1}+(\log(1/\delta))^{2/p+1}\right](\log(n/\delta))^{2}(\log\log n)^{4/p}=\frac{k}{\varepsilon^{4/p}}(\log(n/\delta))^{O(1)}

Proof.

We will apply Theorem

and

4
Streaming and online coresets

We present our results on streaming and online coresets for
\ell_{p}
subspace approximation.

4.1
Online coresets

In this section, we note that our Theorems
3.5
and
3.7
give the first nearly optimal online coreset construction for
\ell_{p}
subspace approximation. In the online coreset model, the rows of the input matrix
\mathbf{A}
arrive one by one. At each row arrival, we must decide whether to include the row in the coreset or not in an
online fashion
, meaning we must irrevocably commit to keeping the row in the coreset or discard it permanently.

Online coresets typically depend on a quantity known as the
online condition number
, which is known to be necessary in many cases
[
CMP16
]
:

Definition 4.1

(Online condition number
[
CMP16
]
)
.

Let
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
. Then, the online condition number of
\mathbf{A}
is defined as

\kappa^{\mathsf{OL}}\coloneqq\lVert\mathbf{A}\rVert_{2}\max_{i=1}^{n}\lVert\mathbf{A}_{(i)}^{-}\rVert_{2},

where
\mathbf{A}_{(i)}^{-}
denotes the pseudoinverse of the
i\times d
matrix formed by the first
i
rows of
\mathbf{A}
.

Then, the following is an immediate corollary of Theorems
3.5
and
3.7
.

Corollary 4.2

(Online coresets)
.

Let
1\leq p<\infty
. Let
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
have online condition number
\kappa^{\mathsf{OL}}
. Then, there is an online coreset algorithm which constructs a diagonal map
\mathbf{S}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
satisfying Definition
1.1
with probability at least
1-\delta
, such that

\nnz(\mathbf{S})=\begin{cases}\frac{\tilde{O}(k^{p/2})}{\varepsilon^{p}}(\log(n\kappa^{\mathsf{OL}}/\delta))^{O(p^{2})}\\
\frac{\tilde{O}(k)}{\varepsilon^{4/p}}(\log(n\kappa^{\mathsf{OL}}/\delta))^{O(1)}\end{cases}

while storing at most
O(k(\log k)(\log\kappa^{\mathsf{OL}})^{2})
additional rows in an online fashion.

Proof.

The result of
[
BDM+20
, Theorem 3.1]
gives an online coreset algorithm for maintaining a
(1\pm\varepsilon)
strong coreset for
\ell_{2}
subspace approximation which stores
O(\varepsilon^{-2}k(\log k)(\log\kappa^{\mathsf{OL}})^{2})
rows. Furthermore, given such a strong coreset with
\varepsilon=O(1)
, it is shown in
[
BDM+20
, Lemma 2.11]
that one can obtain scores
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\tau}}_{i}
such that

\tilde{\boldsymbol{\tau}}_{i}\geq\boldsymbol{\tau}_{i}^{\lambda}(\mathbf{A})

for
\lambda=\left\lVert\mathbf{A}-\mathbf{A}_{k}\right\rVert_{F}^{2}/k
, and also satisfies

\sum_{i=1}^{n}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\tau}}_{i}\leq O(k\log\kappa^{\mathsf{OL}}).

The result for
p>2
then follows as an immediate corollary of Theorem
3.5
. For
p<2
, we additionally need an online constant factor approximation to flatten the matrix, which is constructed in
[
WY23a
]
by obtaining online Lewis sample due to
[
WY23b
]
. The result for
p<2
then follows as an immediate corollary of Theorem
3.7
.
∎

For integer matrices with entries bounded by
\Delta
, we may replace the dependence on the online condition number with
\Delta
, by using an analogous result of
[
BDM+20
]
for integer matrices.

Corollary 4.3

(Online coresets – integer matrices)
.

Let
1\leq p<\infty
. Let
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n\times d}
have entries bounded by
\left\lvert\mathbf{A}_{i,j}\right\rvert\leq\Delta
. Then, there is an online coreset algorithm which constructs a diagonal map
\mathbf{S}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
satisfying Definition
1.1
with probability at least
1-\delta
, such that

\nnz(\mathbf{S})=\begin{cases}\frac{\tilde{O}(k^{p/2})}{\varepsilon^{p}}(\log(n\Delta/\delta))^{O(p^{2})}\\
\frac{\tilde{O}(k)}{\varepsilon^{4/p}}(\log(n\Delta/\delta))^{O(1)}\end{cases}

while storing at most
O(k(\log\Delta)^{2})
additional rows in an online fashion.

4.2
Streaming coresets

Next, we state our corollaries for constructing streaming coresets in the row arrival model of streaming, which is slightly different from the online coreset model since we are allowed to remove rows from our coreset. In the streaming model, the resource measure is typically the space complexity, and thus the input is usually assumed to be an integer matrix as a bit complexity assumption. In this setting, we combine the classic merge-and-reduce technique
[
BDM+20
]
with the technique of
[
CWZ23
]
of first applying an online coreset to obtain a result with only
\poly(\log\log(n\Delta)
factor overhead in the coreset size.

Corollary 4.4

(Streaming coresets – integer matrices)
.

Let
1\leq p<\infty
. Let
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n\times d}
have entries bounded by
\left\lvert\mathbf{A}_{i,j}\right\rvert\leq\Delta
. Then, there is an row arrival streaming algorithm which constructs a diagonal map
\mathbf{S}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
satisfying Definition
1.1
with probability at least
1-\delta
, such that

\nnz(\mathbf{S})=\begin{cases}\frac{\tilde{O}(k^{p/2})}{\varepsilon^{p}}(\log(k/\varepsilon\delta)+\log\log(n\Delta/\delta))^{O(p^{2})}\\
\frac{\tilde{O}(k)}{\varepsilon^{4/p}}(\log(k/\varepsilon\delta)+\log(n\Delta/\delta))^{O(1)}\end{cases}

while storing at most
O(k(\log\Delta)^{2})
additional rows in an online fashion.

Proof.

We may assume without loss of generality that the stream length is at most
m=\poly(k,\varepsilon^{-1},\log(n\Delta/\delta))
by first applying Corollary
4.3
. We then apply the merge-and-reduce technique, which results in a coreset with size where the
\varepsilon
dependence is replaced by
\varepsilon^{\prime}=\varepsilon/\log m
. This results in the claimed bounds.
∎

Our main result of this section is the following theorem, which shows that root leverage score sampling yields

Theorem A.1

(Root leverage score sampling)
.

Let
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
and
\mathbf{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}

\frac{\lvert\mathbf{b}(i)\rvert^{p}}{R^{p}}\leq\begin{cases}\min\left\{1,n^{p/2-1}\boldsymbol{\tau}_{i}(\mathbf{A})^{p/2}\right\}&p>2\\
\boldsymbol{\tau}_{i}(\mathbf{A})^{p/2}&p<2\end{cases}

for every
i\in[n]
. Let
\alpha=\Theta(\varepsilon^{2})/((\log n)^{3}+\log(1/\delta))
and let

q_{i}\geq\begin{cases}\min\left\{1,n^{p/2-1}\boldsymbol{\tau}_{i}(\mathbf{A})^{p/2}/\alpha\right\}&p>2\\
\min\left\{1,\boldsymbol{\tau}_{i}(\mathbf{A})^{p/2}/\alpha\right\}&p<2\end{cases}

Let
\mathbf{S}
be the
\ell_{p}
sampling matrix (Definition
1.6
) with sampling probabilities
\{q_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
. Then, with probability at least
1-\delta
, for every
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

\left\lVert\mathbf{S}(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{b})\right\rVert_{p}^{p}=(1\pm\varepsilon)\left\lVert\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{b}\right\rVert_{p}^{p}\pm\varepsilon R^{p}

A.1
Reduction to a small number of scales

Our first task is to reduce the proof of Theorem
A.1
to showing a similar theorem when
\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}
is restricted to a certain scale, for a small number of scales. This is shown in the following lemma:

Lemma A.2
.

Let
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
and
\mathbf{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Let
1\leq p<\infty
. Let
R_{0}\geq\left\lVert\mathbf{b}\right\rVert_{p}
and
0<\varepsilon<1/2
. Suppose that

\sup_{\left\lVert\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}\right\rVert_{p}=1}\left\lvert\left\lVert\mathbf{S}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}\right\rVert_{p}^{p}-\left\lVert\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}\right\rVert_{p}^{p}\right\rvert\leq\varepsilon

and that

\sup_{\left\lVert\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}\right\rVert_{p}\leq R_{i}}\left\lvert\left\lVert\mathbf{S}(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{b})\right\rVert_{p}^{p}-\left\lVert\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{b}\right\rVert_{p}^{p}\right\rvert\leq\varepsilon R_{i}^{p}

holds for each
R_{i}=2^{i}\cdot R_{0}
,
i\in[I]
, where
I=O(\log\varepsilon^{-1})
. Then,

\left\lVert\mathbf{S}(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{b})\right\rVert_{p}^{p}=(1\pm 2\cdot 4^{p}\varepsilon)\left\lVert\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{b}\right\rVert_{p}^{p}\pm 2^{p}\varepsilon R_{0}^{p}

for every
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Proof.

First note that if
\left\lVert\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}\right\rVert_{p}\leq R_{1}
, then we immediately have

\left\lVert\mathbf{S}(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{b})\right\rVert_{p}^{p}=\left\lVert\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{b}\right\rVert_{p}^{p}\pm\varepsilon R_{1}^{p}=\left\lVert\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{b}\right\rVert_{p}^{p}\pm 2^{p}\varepsilon R_{0}^{p}.

Next, suppose that
\left\lVert\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}\right\rVert_{p}\geq R_{0}/\varepsilon
. Note that

\left\lVert\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{b}\right\rVert_{p}=\left\lVert\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}\right\rVert_{p}\pm\left\lVert\mathbf{b}\right\rVert_{p}=(1\pm\varepsilon)\left\lVert\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}\right\rVert_{p}

and similarly,

\left\lVert\mathbf{S}(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{b})\right\rVert_{p}=\left\lVert\mathbf{S}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}\right\rVert_{p}\pm\left\lVert\mathbf{S}\mathbf{b}\right\rVert_{p}=(1\pm 4\varepsilon)\left\lVert\mathbf{S}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}\right\rVert_{p}

Thus,

\left\lVert\mathbf{S}(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{b})\right\rVert_{p}=(1\pm 4\varepsilon)(1\pm\varepsilon)\left\lVert\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{b}\right\rVert_{p}=(1\pm 7\varepsilon)\left\lVert\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{b}\right\rVert_{p}.

Finally, we handle the intermediate scales between
R_{0}
and
R_{0}/\varepsilon
. Consider
\mathbf{x}
such that
R_{i}\leq\left\lVert\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}\right\rVert_{p}<2\cdot R_{i}
. Note then that

\left\lVert\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{b}\right\rVert_{p}\geq\left\lVert\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}\right\rVert_{p}-\left\lVert\mathbf{b}\right\rVert_{p}\geq\left\lVert\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}\right\rVert_{p}/2\geq R_{i}/2

so

\left\lVert\mathbf{S}(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{b})\right\rVert_{p}^{p}=\left\lVert\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{b}\right\rVert_{p}^{p}\pm\varepsilon\cdot(2R_{i})^{p}=\left\lVert\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{b}\right\rVert_{p}^{p}\pm\varepsilon\cdot(4\left\lVert\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{b}\right\rVert_{p})^{p}.

This covers all cases.
∎

such that for every
k
-dimensional subspace
F
,

\lVert\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{P}_{S}-\mathbf{P}_{S\cup F})\rVert_{p,2}^{p}\leq\varepsilon^{p}\mathsf{OPT}

where
\mathsf{OPT}=\min_{F\in\mathcal{F}_{k}}\lVert\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{P}_{F})\rVert_{p,2}^{p}
.

Proof.

The proof largely follows
[
SW18
]
combined with our improved inequalities proved earlier. We reproduce a proof for the reader’s convenience.

Lemma 6 in
[
SW18
]
shows that there is an
s
-dimensional subspace
S
such that

\lVert\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{P}_{S})\rVert_{p,2}^{p}-\lVert\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{P}_{S\cup F})\rVert_{p,2}^{p}\leq\varepsilon^{\max\{2,p\}}\mathsf{OPT}

(11)

for every
k
-dimensional subspace
F\in\mathcal{F}_{k}
. We now use the fact that for any vector
\mathbf{a}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

\lVert\mathbf{a}^{\top}(\mathbf{P}_{S}-\mathbf{P}_{S\cup F})\rVert_{2}^{2}=\lVert\mathbf{a}^{\top}(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{P}_{S})\rVert_{2}^{2}-\lVert\mathbf{a}^{\top}(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{P}_{S\cup F})\rVert_{2}^{2}

by orthogonality and Lemma
B.2
(with
\varepsilon^{\prime}=\varepsilon^{p}
) to show that

\lVert\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{P}_{S}-\mathbf{P}_{S\cup F})\rVert_{p,2}^{p}\leq\begin{cases}\varepsilon^{p}\lVert\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{P}_{S})\rVert_{p,2}^{p}+2\varepsilon^{p-2}(\lVert\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{P}_{S})\rVert_{p,2}^{p}-\lVert\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{P}_{S\cup F})\rVert_{p,2}^{p})&1\leq p<2\\
\lVert\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{P}_{S})\rVert_{p,2}^{p}-\lVert\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{P}_{S\cup F})\rVert_{p,2}^{p}&2\leq p<\infty\end{cases}

by summing up the inequalities over vectors
\mathbf{a}_{i}
for
i\in[n]
. By (
11
), we have that

\lVert\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{P}_{S}-\mathbf{P}_{S\cup F})\rVert_{p,2}^{p}\leq 3\varepsilon^{p}\mathsf{OPT}

in any case. Rescaling
\varepsilon
by constant factors yields the statement of the theorem.
∎

Finally, we combine this bound with Lemma
B.6
to conclude Theorem
B.1
.

Appendix C
Efficient constructions of nearly linear representative subspaces

In this section, we sharpen a result of
[
FKW21
]
and show that representative subspaces of dimension
\tilde{O}(k)\varepsilon^{-\Theta(p)}
can be constructed in polynomial time. This implies that strong coresets of size
\tilde{O}(k^{\max\{1,p/2\}})\poly(\varepsilon^{-1})

with an added dimension
can be constructed in time
\tilde{O}(\nnz(\mathbf{A}))+d\poly(k/\varepsilon)
. Indeed, note that the work of
[
HV20
,
WY23a
]
shows that strong coresets of size
\poly(k/\varepsilon)
can be constructed in input sparsity time via sensitivity sampling, so we may assume that
n
and
d
are at most
\poly(k/\varepsilon)
. Thus, it suffices to design a polynomial time algorithm for constructing representative subspaces. For this, we use the observation of
[
FKW21
]
that we only need an efficient bicriteria algorithm that outputs a subspace which has objective value at most
(1+\varepsilon)
times the rank
k
optimum. The work of
[
FKW21
]
uses an algorithm with bicriteria rank
\poly(k/\varepsilon)
. We will show instead how to improve this to an algorithm with bicriteria rank
\tilde{O}(k/\varepsilon)
.

We first start with a sketching result which sharpens
[
CW15
, Theorem 32]
.

Lemma C.1
.

Let
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
. Let
\mathbf{G}
be an
d\times r
random Gaussian matrix for
r=\tilde{O}((k+\log(n/\delta))/\varepsilon)
. Then,

\min_{\rank(\mathbf{X})\leq k}\lVert\mathbf{A}\mathbf{G}\mathbf{X}-\mathbf{A}\rVert_{p,2}^{p}\leq(1+\varepsilon)\min_{\rank(F)\leq k}\lVert\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{P}_{F})\rVert_{p,2}^{p}.

Proof.

Let
\mathbf{V}^{*}
be an orthonormal basis for the optimal rank
k
projection
\mathbf{P}^{*}
, so that

\mathsf{OPT}=\lVert\mathbf{A}\mathbf{V}^{*}(\mathbf{V}^{*})^{\top}-\mathbf{A}\rVert_{p,2}^{p}=\min_{\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times k}}\lVert\mathbf{X}(\mathbf{V}^{*})^{\top}-\mathbf{A}\rVert_{p,2}^{p}.

Note that the latter problem can be viewed as
n
different
\ell_{2}
linear regression problems. Then by sketching with
\mathbf{G}

[
Woo14
]
, for each
i\in[n]
, we have that with probability at least
1-\delta/n
that
\hat{\mathbf{x}}=\mathbf{e}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{G}((\mathbf{V}^{*})^{\top}\mathbf{G})^{-}
satisfies

\lVert\hat{\mathbf{x}}(\mathbf{V}^{*})^{\top}-\mathbf{e}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{A}\rVert_{2}\leq(1+\varepsilon)\min_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}}\lVert\mathbf{x}(\mathbf{V}^{*})^{\top}-\mathbf{e}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{A}\rVert_{2}.

By a union bound over the
n
rows, this is simultaneously true for all
i\in[n]
with probability at least
1-\delta
. Then by taking
p
-th powers and summing, we have

\lVert\mathbf{A}\mathbf{G}((\mathbf{V}^{*})^{\top}\mathbf{G})^{-}(\mathbf{V}^{*})^{\top}-\mathbf{A}\rVert_{p,2}^{p}\leq(1+O(\varepsilon))\min_{\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times k}}\lVert\mathbf{X}(\mathbf{V}^{*})^{\top}-\mathbf{A}\rVert_{p,2}^{p}=(1+\varepsilon)\mathsf{OPT}.\qed

The next lemma shows how Lemma
C.1
implies an efficient bicriteria algorithm of rank
\tilde{O}(k/\varepsilon)
.

Lemma C.2
.

There is a
\poly(nd)
time algorithm which, given an input matrix
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
, outputs a rank
\tilde{O}(k/\varepsilon)
subspace
\hat{F}
such that

\lVert\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{P}_{\hat{F}})\rVert_{p,2}^{p}\leq(1+\varepsilon)\min_{\rank(F)\leq k}\lVert\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{P}_{F})\rVert_{p,2}^{p}.

Proof.

By Lemma
C.1
, we have that

\min_{\rank(\mathbf{X})\leq k}\lVert\mathbf{A}\mathbf{G}\mathbf{X}-\mathbf{A}\rVert_{p,2}^{p}\leq(1+\varepsilon)\min_{\rank(F)\leq k}\lVert\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{P}_{F})\rVert_{p,2}^{p}.

Then, we can simply drop the rank
k
constraint on
\mathbf{X}
to solve for a matrix
\mathbf{Y}
of rank
\tilde{O}(k/\varepsilon)
such that
\lVert\mathbf{A}\mathbf{Y}-\mathbf{A}\rVert_{p,2}^{p}\leq(1+\varepsilon)\mathsf{OPT}
. Note that
\mathbf{Y}
can be computed efficiently by, e.g., applying Dvoretzky’s theorem to approximate the
(p,2)
-norm by a
(p,p)
-norm and then solving an instance of
\ell_{p}
regression
d
times. Finally, we can take
\hat{F}=\rowspan(\mathbf{Y})
to produce a rank
\tilde{O}(k/\varepsilon)
subspace such that

\lVert\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{P}_{\hat{F}})\rVert_{p,2}^{p}\leq(1+\varepsilon)\mathsf{OPT}.\qed

Finally the above bicriteria algorithm can be combined with the iteration algorithm idea of
[
SW18
]
, as done in
[
FKW21
]
, to prove the following theorem:

Theorem C.3
.

There is an algorithm which, given an input matrix
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
, runs in time
\poly(nd)
and outputs a rank
\tilde{O}(k/\varepsilon^{-\max\{2+p,2p\}})
subspace
S
such that

\lVert\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{P}_{F})\rVert_{p,2}^{p}=(1\pm\varepsilon)\lVert[\mathbf{A}\mathbf{P}_{S}(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{P}_{F}),\mathbf{b}_{S}]\rVert_{p,2}^{p}

for every
F\in\mathcal{F}_{k}
, where
\mathbf{b}_{S}(i)=\lVert\mathbf{a}_{i}^{\top}(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{P}_{S})\rVert_{2}
.

Proof.

To construct a linear size representative subspace in polynomial time, we will use the work of
[
FKW21
]
, which observes that
(1+\varepsilon)
approximation algorithms in the algorithm of
[
SW18
]
can be replaced by
(1+\varepsilon)
bicriteria approximation algorithms, which can in turn be implemented efficiently. The proof of this theorem now simply replaces the bicriteria algorithm of
[
FKW21
]
with our bicriteria algorithm of Lemma
C.2
and our sharpened analysis of
[
SW18
]
in Appendix
B
. Note that to find a space
S
such that

\lVert\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{P}_{S}-\mathbf{P}_{S\cup F})\rVert_{p,2}^{p}\leq\varepsilon\mathsf{OPT}

the algorithm will add a rank
\tilde{O}(k/\varepsilon)
bicriteria space for
\varepsilon^{-\max\{2/p,1\}}
iterations, so the resulting subspace has rank at most
\tilde{O}(k)\varepsilon^{-\max\{2/p+1,2\}}
. We need to apply this with
\varepsilon
replaced by
\varepsilon^{p}
, so the result bound on the rank of the representative subspace is
\tilde{O}(k)\varepsilon^{-\max\{2+p,2p\}}
.
∎
