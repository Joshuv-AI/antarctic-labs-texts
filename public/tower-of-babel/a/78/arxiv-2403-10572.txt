Discovering Invariant Neighborhood Patterns for Heterophilic Graphs
Ruihao Zhang, Zhengyu Chen, Teng Xiao, Yueyang Wang, Kun Kuang

Abstract
This paper studies the problem of distribution shifts on heterophilic graphs. Most existing graph neural network methods rely on the homophilous assumption that nodes from the same class are more likely to be linked. These methods study the degree-related or label-related distribution shifts based on the assumption of homophily. However, the assumption of homophily does not consistently apply to real-world graphs, including but not limited to social networks, biological networks, and citation networks. This discrepancy results in neighborhood pattern distribution shifts unaccounted for in previous methods. We propose a novel Invariant Neighborhood Pattern Learning (INPL) to alleviate the distribution shifts problem on heterophilic graphs. Specifically, we propose the Adaptive Neighborhood Propagation (ANP) module to capture the adaptive neighborhood information, which could alleviate the neighborhood pattern distribution shifts problem on heterophilic graphs. We propose an Invariant Heterophilic Graph Learning (IHGL) module to constrain the ANP and learn invariant graph representation on heterophilic graphs. Extensive experimental results on real-world heterophilic graphs show that INPL could achieve state-of-the-art performance for learning on large heterophilic graphs. The code is public at: https://github.com/AnnoymousCode/Invariant-Neighborhood-Pattern-Learning .

Generalization on heterophilic Graphs

Generally, these methods enhance the performance of GNNs through the following three main designs: (1) Using High-Order Neighborhoods. (2) Separating ego- and neighbor-embedding. (3) Combining Inter-layer representation.

II-B

Bias in Machine Learning

Recently, many methods have been proposed to address bias caused by distribution shifts for general machine learning problems. There are three branches of methods for the selection bias caused by distribution shifts, namely Invariant Risk Minimization, Distributionally Robust Optimization (DRO) and Reweighting.

Invariant risk minimization (IRM)
[
26
]
aims to learn an invariant predictor across multiple training environments. Recently different variants have been proposed to improve IRM. IRM
[
26
]
uses the gradient norm penalty to measure the optimality of the dummy classifier at each environment, REX
[
27
]
suggests penalizing the variance of the risks among different environments, InvRat
[
28
]
proposes a game-theoretic approach to invariant rationalization, where the method is trained to constrain the probability of the output conditional on the rationales to be the same across multiple environments. Bayesian IRM
[
29
]
proposes to fit the posterior distribution of a classifier to avoid overfitting, Sparse IRM
[
30
]
Trains a sparse neural network to avoid overfitting. MAPLE
[
31
]
reweights the training samples, then standard empirical risk minimization is trained on the weighted training data.
[
32
]
demonstrates that IRM fails catastrophically if the model is highly nonlinear and the overlap between the training domain and testing domain is small.

Distributionally robust optimization methods aim to minimize the worst-case error over an uncertainty distribution set, which is often constrained by moment or support conditions.
When the uncertainty set is properly chosen, Duchi
[
33
,
34
]
shows that DRO can improve the robustness of the model by imposing regularization.

[
35
]
proves that DRL can improve the robustness of the model by incorporating structural assumptions on distribution shifts.
Similar to IRM, DRO is shown to fail for applications on overparameterized neural networks
[
36
]
, to address this problem,

[
37
]
proposes a simple two-stage approach that first trains a standard ERM model and then trains a second model that weights the training examples that the first model misclassified.

Reweighting algorithms are popular method to address the bias caused by distribution shifts, Importance sampling is a classical sample reweighting method that estimates the test-over-training density ratio and assigns the weights to samples to match one distribution to another. However, Importance sampling assumes the prior knowledge of testing distribution is known, which is often not satisfied in real-world settings. Recently many Reweighting methods have been proposed to deal with distribution shifts where the testing distribution is unknown.
To address agnostic distribution shifts under model misspecification,
[
38
]
learns a variable decorrelation regularizer which estimates a weight for each sample such that variables are decorrelated on the weighted training data.
[
39
]
propose a sample reweighting method that reduces collinearity among input variables.

[
40
]
proposes to get rid of spurious correlations between features via learning weights for training samples.

However, these works are for bias problems with independent and identically distributed data, the properties of graph-structured data are not explicitly utilized. By contrast, our method aims to learn invariant graph representation.

II-C

Debiased Graph Neural Network

Debiased Graph Neural Networks aim to address the bias issue on graphs.
To overcome degree-related bias,
SL-DSGCN
[
41
]
mitigates the degree-related bias of GCNs by capturing both discrepancies and similarities of nodes with different degrees.
ImGAGN
[
42
]
is proposed to address the class-related bias, ImGAGN generates a set of synthetic minority nodes to balance the class distribution.
Different from these works on a single bias,
BA-GNN
[
14
]
proposes a novel Bias-Aware Graph Neural Network (BA-GNN) framework by learning node representation that is invariant across different biases and distributions for invariant prediction. Specifically, BA-GNN learns multiple biased graph partitions and selects features, neighbor, and propagation steps for nodes under multiple biased graph partitions.
EERM
[
18
]
facilitates graph neural networks to leverage invariance principles for prediction. Specifically, EERM resorts to multiple context explorers that are adversarially trained to maximize the variance of risks from multiple virtual environments, which enables the model to extrapolate from a single observed environment which is the common case for node-level prediction.

However, these works heavily rely on the assumption of homophily, which may be prohibitively failed on heterophilic graphs.
Our method differs from above methods and aims to learn invariant graph representation for heterophilic graphs.

IV
Discovering Invariant Neighborhood Patterns

We propose a novel Invariant Neighborhood Pattern Learning (INPL) framework that aims to alleviate the distribution shifts on heterophilic graphs.
1) To alleviate the neighborhood pattern distribution shifts problem on heterophilic graphs, we propose Adaptive Neighborhood Propagation, where the Invariant Propagation layer is proposed to combine both the high-order and low-order neighborhood information. Adaptive propagation is proposed to capture the adaptive neighborhood information.
2) To alleviate the distribution shifts in unknown test environments, we propose Invariant Heterophilic Graph Learning to constrain the Adaptive Neighborhood Propagation, which learns invariant graph representation on heterophilic graphs. Specifically,
we design the environment clustering module to learn multiple graph partitions, and the invariant graph learning module learns the invariant graph representation based on multiple graph partitions.

Adaptive Neighborhood Propagation

As shown in Figure

Such a method should combine different-order neighborhood information, and capture the adaptive neighborhood information. Thus, we propose an Invariant Propagation layer (IPL) to combine both the high-order and low-order neighborhood information and the Adaptive Propagation (AP) method to capture the adaptive neighborhood information. These mechanisms help to alleviate the neighborhood pattern distribution shifts problem on heterophilic graphs and the distribution shifts in unknown test environments. Adaptive Propagation.

To capture the adaptive neighborhood information, we aim to learn a discrete latent variable

s_{v}
for node
v
, which denotes the personalized optimal propagation step of node
v
.
In practice, there is no way to directly access the optimal propagation of neighborhood information.
We propose a generative network
q_{\phi}
for modeling the distribution of propagation steps conditioned on node attributes and its neighbors, i.e.,
q_{\phi}(s_{v}|x_{v},\bar{N}_{i}{(v)})
, and formulate the adaptive propagation as a variational objective, where
the goal is to find the parameters of IPL and the optimal propagation distribution, by iteratively approximating and maximizing
the log-likelihood function.

Specifically, we infer the optimal propagation distribution

and learn IPL weights

However, the optimal propagation steps

are discrete and non-differentiable which makes direct optimization difficult. Therefore, we adopt Gumbel-Softmax Sampling
[
46
,
47
]
, which is a simple yet effective way to
substitutes the original non-differentiable sample from a discrete distribution with a differentiable sample from a corresponding Gumbel-Softmax distribution.
Thus, we have the loss function as:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}({\phi},\theta)=

\displaystyle-\log p_{{\theta}}(\mathbf{y}|IPL(x_{v},\bar{N}_{i}{(v)},\theta,\hat{s}))

\displaystyle+\text{KL}(q_{\phi}(s_{v}|x_{v},\bar{N}_{i}{(v)})||p(s_{v})),

(6)

where
s_{v}
represents the personalized optimal propagation step of node
v
.

\hat{s}
is drawn from a categorical distribution with the discrete variational distribution

parameterized by
\phi
:

{linenomath}

\displaystyle\hat{s}_{k}=\frac{\exp((\log(q_{{\phi}}(s_{n}|x_{v},\bar{N}_{i}{(v)})[a_{k}])+g_{k})/\gamma_{g})}{\sum_{k^{\prime}=0}^{K}\exp((\log(q_{{\phi}}(s_{n}|x_{v},\bar{N}_{i}{(v)})[a_{k^{\prime}}])+g_{k^{\prime}})/\gamma_{g})},

where

are i.i.d samples drawn from the Gumbel (0, 1) distribution,

is the softmax

is the

and

indicates the

-th index of

, i.e., the logit corresponding the

-th layer. Clearly, when

IV-B

Invariant Heterophilic Graph Learning

The proposed Adaptive Neighborhood Propagation module could alleviate the neighborhood pattern distribution shifts problem on heterophilic graphs by capturing the adaptive neighborhood information.
However, as illustrated in Figure
2
, the testing environments are always unknown and unpredictable, where the class distributions and neighborhood pattern distribution are various. Such unknown distribution shifts would render GNNs over-optimized on the labeled training samples, which hampers their capacity for robustness and leads to a poor generalization of GNNs.

Inspired by previous invariant learning methods
[
26

In order to learn invariant graph representation across different environments, we have a commonly used assumption in previous invariant learning methods

.

This assumption shows that we could learn invariant graph representation with a proper graph neural network
\theta
across different environments.
However, the majority of available graphs do not have environmental labels. Thus, we generate multiple graph partitions as different environments by the environment clustering module and learn invariant graph representation in these different environments.

The environment clustering module takes a single graph as input and outputs multiple graph environments. The goal of the environment clustering module is to increase the similarity of nodes within the same environment while reducing the correlation between different environments.
Thus, the nodes should be clustered by the variant relation between the node and the target label. The variant information
\mathbf{h}^{(0)}_{v}
is updated with graph neural network. We also random select
K
cluster centroids
\mu_{1},\mu_{2},...,\mu_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, thus there exists
K

G^{1},G^{2},...,G^{K}\in\mathcal{E}_{tr}
at the beginning of optimization.
Here we choose K-means as the method for environmental clustering, which is one of the representative methods of unsupervised clustering.
The objective function of the environment clustering module is to minimize the sum of the distances between all nodes and their associated cluster centroids:

\displaystyle{\mathcal{L}_{env}=\min\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{K}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left\|{\mathbf{h}^{(0)}_{v}}-\mu_{j}\right\|^{2}}

(8)

For environments
\mathcal{E}_{tr}

\displaystyle{G^{v}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=argmin_{e}\left\|{\mathbf{h}^{(0)}_{v}}-\mu_{e}\right\|^{2},e\in 1,2,...,K}

(9)

For every environment

\displaystyle{\mu_{e}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\frac{\sum_{v\in G^{e}}^{N}{\mathbf{h}^{(0)}_{v}}}{N}}

IV-C

Complexity analysis

The time complexity of INPL is
\mathcal{O}(d|E|+nd^{2}L)
, where
d
is the hidden dimension (which we assume to be on the same order as the input feature dimension D),
L
is the number of layers,
n
is the number of nodes, and
|E|
is the number of edges. We require a
\mathcal{O}(d|E|)
cost for the first linear mapping of
\mathbf{A}
and a
\mathcal{O}(nd^{2})
cost to multiply by the weight matrix on hidden features, for L total layers.

V-B

Overall Performance Comparison

To evaluate the effectiveness of the proposed INPL framework, We conduct experiments on 11 heterophilic datasets. Results are shown in Table
II
.
We have the following observations:

(1) Heterophilic methods outperform graph-agnostic methods and representative general GNNs in all cases, the reason is that these general GNNs rely on the homophily assumption, such assumption does not hold on heterophilic graphs.

(2) INPL achieves state-of-the-art performance on large heterophilic graphs. This shows our method is scalable and provides superior results on datasets in a wide range of diversity.
On the one hand, the proposed ANP module captures the adaptive neighborhood information, which could alleviate the neighborhood pattern distribution shift problems on heterophilic graphs. On the other hand, the IHGL module constrains the ANP and learns invariant graph representation,
thus INPL outperforms in different biased environments and classification accuracy increases.

(3) INPL outperforms previous debiased GNNs. Previous debiased GNNs heavily rely on the homophilous assumption, which leads to poor generalization ability on heterophilic graphs. The results demonstrate the effectiveness of INPL compared with previous debiased GNNs.

In summary, our INPL achieves superior performance on heterophilic datasets, which significantly proves the effectiveness of our proposed framework and our motivation.

To further evaluate that our proposed INPL framework could alleviate neighborhood pattern distribution shifts in chameleon, we group the testing nodes of each graph according to their neighborhood pattern. Class distribution shifts
. To further evaluate that our proposed INPL framework could alleviate neighborhood pattern distribution shifts in squirrel, we group the testing nodes of each graph according to their labels. The distribution shifts between training and testing environments are various in this setting, where we evaluate methods in different environments rather than the average accuracy of all testing nodes in a single environment.
The results in class distribution shifts environments are shown in Figure

Degree distribution shifts
. For degree distribution shifts,
we organize each graph’s testing nodes into different environments based on the degree.
In this setting, the distribution shifts between training and testing environments are various, and we
evaluate methods in different environments rather than the average accuracy of all testing nodes in a single environment.
Results in Figure
7
show that INPL outperforms different baselines in all environments for chameleon, which evaluates INPL could alleviate degree-related bias.

Upon a comprehensive analysis of the results, it is clear that INPL consistently outperforms other baselines across a variety of environments. This superior performance is not confined to a specific type of environment but is rather a general trend observed across all environments with different patterns, labels, or degrees.
This indicates the robustness of INPL and its ability to adapt to different distribution shifts.
Moreover, the ability of INPL to effectively mitigate different distribution shifts on heterophilic graphs, shows that INPL can handle not only the known distribution shifts that we can explicitly test for but also the unknown shifts that are often encountered in real-world applications.

VI
Conclusion

In this paper, we focus on how to address the bias issue caused by the distribution shifts between training and testing on heterophilic graphs.
We propose a novel INPL framework that aims to learn invariant representation on heterophilic graphs. To alleviate the neighborhood pattern distribution shifts problem on heterophilic graphs, we propose Adaptive Neighborhood Propagation, where the Invariant Propagation Layer is proposed to combine both the high-order and low-order neighborhood information. Adaptive Propagation is proposed to capture the adaptive neighborhood information.
To alleviate the distribution shifts in unknown test environments, we propose Invariant Heterophilic Graph Learning, which learns invariant graph representation on heterophilic graphs.
Extensive experiments on heterophilic graphs validate the superiority of INPL and show that INPL could alleviate distribution shifts in different environments.

VII
Broader Impact

This work has the following potential positive impact on society. Firstly, it could improve our understanding of distribution shifts on heterophilic graphs and open up new directions for developing robust graph neural networks that are resilient to distributional changes. Secondly, these advancements could be applied in fields like social network analysis, recommendation systems, natural language processing, and image recognition, among others.
At the same time, this work may have some negative consequences because unethical actors might use machine learning techniques developed in the above applications to spread misinformation, deceive users, amplify prejudices, personal data, or manipulate public opinion. To mitigate these risks, transparent and interpretable machine learning models should be adopted wherever possible, and developers must adopt strict guidelines for handling user data responsibly while ensuring privacy, security, and fairness in all their algorithms and products.

VIII
Further discussion and limitations

In this paper, we conduct a comprehensive and empirical study of a novel problem, which focuses on neighborhood pattern distribution shifts problem, to the best of our knowledge, there are no studies that analyze the problem of distribution shifts of neighborhood patterns. To alleviate the neighborhood pattern distribution shifts problem on heterophilic graphs, we propose the Adaptive Neighborhood Propagation method, where the Invariant Propagation layer is proposed to combine both the high-order and low-order neighborhood information and adaptive propagation is proposed to capture the adaptive neighborhood information. However, we are still facing some challenges: 1) it is difficult to directly calculate higher-order neighborhoods on large graphs, since the model may not fit in GPU memory; 2) the proposed components of INPL probably show comparable advantages on graph data, and may be less applicable or perform worse on image or text data; 3) hyper-parameters may influence the final outcome depending on possible trade-offs resulting from experiments.
Furthermore, we hope that the empirical observations made in this work will inspire more investigation in this direction.
