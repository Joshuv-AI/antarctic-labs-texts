Bounded-Memory Strategies in Partial-Information Games
Sougata Bose, Rasmus Ibsen-Jensen, Patrick Totzke

Abstract
. We study the computational complexity of solving stochastic games with mean-payoff objectives. Instead of identifying special classes in which simple strategies are sufficient to play ε \varepsilon -optimally, or form ε \varepsilon -Nash equilibria, we consider general partial-information multiplayer games and ask what can be achieved with (and against) finite-memory strategies up to a given bound on the memory. We show 𝖭𝖯 {\mathsf{NP}} -hardness for approximating zero-sum values, already with respect to memoryless strategies and for 1-player reachability games. On the other hand, we provide upper bounds for solving games of any fixed number of players k k . We show that one can decide in polynomial space if, for a given k k -player game, ε ≥ 0 \varepsilon\geq 0 and bound b b , there exists an ε \varepsilon -Nash equilibrium in which all strategies use at most b b memory modes. For given ε > 0 \varepsilon>0 , finding an ε \varepsilon -Nash equilibrium with respect to b b -bounded strategies can be done in 𝖥𝖭𝖯 𝖭𝖯 {\mathsf{FNP}}^{{\mathsf{NP}}} . Similarly for 2-player zero-sum games, finding a b b -bounded strategy that, against all b b -bounded opponent strategies, guarantees an outcome within ε \varepsilon of a given value, can be done in 𝖥𝖭𝖯 𝖭𝖯 {\mathsf{FNP}}^{{\mathsf{NP}}} . Our constructions apply to parity objectives with minimal simplifications. Our results improve the status quo in several well-known special cases of games. In particular, for 2 2 -player zero-sum concurrent mean-payoff games, one can approximate ordinary zero-sum values (without restricting admissible strategies) in 𝖥𝖭𝖯 𝖭𝖯 {\mathsf{FNP}}^{{\mathsf{NP}}} .

1.
Introduction

We study stochastic games of infinite duration, where
k
players jointly move a pebble through a finite directed graph.
In every round, all players independently select, possibly at random, one out of finitely many actions.
The pebble is moved according to a fixed distribution for the current state and chosen action vector, the players receive an associated reward and the next round begins.
In general partial-information games, players do not observe the choices or rewards of other players nor the current state of the pebble. Instead, at the beginning of each round, they receive a signal that may or may not include that information.
This subsumes classical settings such as
perfect-information games (signals include the current state, rewards, and actions of others), turn-based games (in every round only one player’s action affects the outcome),
and non-stochastic games (all distributions are Dirac).
We focus on games with mean-payoff (aka limit-average, where
players aim to maximise their expected long-term average rewards).
We discuss in
Section

8.1
how our constructions
can be applied to parity games as well with minor simplifying adjustments.

We want to solve such games, i.e., compute (
\varepsilon
)-Nash equilibria,
zero-sum values and witnessing strategies.
However, partial-information games are very expressive
and not guaranteed to have (Nash, or
\varepsilon
-Nash) equilibria or zero-sum values
(
Solan and Ziliotto, 2016
)
.
Moreover, already for rather restricted subclasses that do admit
\varepsilon
-optimal strategies,
finding them is undecidable: this is the case
for single-player, reachability games with a unique signal, aka partially observable Markov decision processes (POMDPs)
(
Madani et al., 1999
;
Chatterjee et al., 2022
)
.
Here,
finding the best finite-memory strategy (one that is representable by a finite automaton) without providing a bound on its size up front is complete for the recursive-enumerable problems

(
Chatterjee et al., 2022
)
.
For the case of partial-information stochastic parity games, where one player has perfect-information, finding the best finite-memory strategy is EXPTIME-complete
(
Chatterjee et al., 2014
)
.
Even for perfect-information games such as the classic “Big Match”
(
Gillette, 1957
;
Blackwell and Ferguson, 1968
)
,
finite-memory strategies can be much worse than general ones
(
Hansen et al., 2023
)
.
For this reason, and motivated by applications, much work has focused on identifying special cases of games in which simple strategies are sufficient to play
\varepsilon
-optimally, or to guarantee the existence of
\varepsilon
-Nash equilibria

(
Gillette, 1957
;
Ehrenfeucht and Mycielski, 1973
;
Mertens and Neyman, 1981
;
Filar et al., 1991
;
Puterman, 1994
;
Mansour and Singh, 1999
;
Secchi and Sudderth, 2002
;
Gimbert and Zielonka, 2005
;
Hansen et al., 2011
;
Hunter et al., 2018
;
Bouyer et al., 2022
;
Bouyer et al., 2023
)
.

In this paper, we study the complexity of solving games
with respect to bounded-memory strategies
,
meaning that all players are required to play according to a finite-memory strategy
where the number of distinct memory modes is bounded a priori.
We ask what outcomes players can still guarantee, and how,
if one assumes that only strategies up to the given bound are admissible.
This approach lets us study “realistic” scenarios that require bounded representations for strategies.
Moreover, for many classes of games,
values/equilibria with respect to
b
-bounded strategies coincide with ordinary zero-sum values/equilibria.
For example, concurrent reachability games
(
Hansen et al., 2009
;
Hansen et al., 2011
;
Frederiksen and Miltersen, 2013
;
Hansen et al., 2014
)
admit
\varepsilon
-optimal strategies that are memoryless (
b=1
). Consequently, our upper bounds directly apply to such special cases.

There remain significant challenges for solving games
even with known bounds on the number of states and memory modes in admissible strategies: (1) optimal strategies/Nash-equilibria might not exist
(
Everett, 1957
)
, (2) values/outcomes of equilibria can be irrational
(
Etessami and Yannakakis, 2008
)
and can therefore at best be approximated, and (3) strategies with rational distributions may require double-exponentially small probabilities to be (
\varepsilon
-)optimal
(
Hansen et al., 2009
)
.
This last point is especially problematic since it means it requires more than polynomial time to guess/write down a good strategy with binary encoded values.

Contributions

We provide new complexity bounds for approximating
\varepsilon
-optimal strategies and
\varepsilon
-equilibria in partial-information games with bounded strategies.
We give an overview here; precise claims are delayed to
Section

3
after introducing notations.

First, we give
{\mathsf{NP}}
and
{\mathsf{coNP}}
lower bounds for approximating the value achievable by bounded strategies under very weak assumptions.
More precisely, we show that it is
\mathsf{NP}
-hard to check if in a given 1-player reachability game the value achieved by memoryless strategies exceeds a given threshold.
This holds even if the threshold is unary encoded, and even though the games we construct admit optimal memoryless strategies that do not require randomisation.
As a simple corollary, we obtain
\mathsf{coNP}
-hardness for
2
-player, zero-sum games.
An
{\mathsf{NP}}
lower bound
for checking the existence of
a memoryless winning strategy for
2
-player, partial-information, non-stochastic games
was shown by
Amoussou-Guenou et al.
(
Amoussou-Guenou et al., 2021
)
.
Our result uses stochasticity but works for any bounded-memory strategies. Moreover, the
{\mathsf{NP}}
-hardness holds already for the
1
-player case.

As a second and main contribution,
we propose a framework
that yields simple approximation algorithms
low in the polynomial hierarchy (at level
\leq 2
).
These ultimately work by guessing polynomial representations of witnessing strategies
and then approximating outcomes in the resulting Markov chains.
Inspired by
(
Frederiksen and Miltersen, 2013
)
,
we use fixed precision floating-point notations
to polynomially represent and manipulate doubly-exponentially small values in probability distributions.

The key new ingredient is an iterative state-space reduction scheme for Markov chains.
By way of encoding this in
\mathrm{FO}(\mathbb{R})
, the first-order theory of the reals
(
Basu et al., 2006
)
, we derive
(1) a
{\mathsf{PSPACE}}
algorithm for checking the existence, and computing representations of,
\varepsilon
-equilibria for
\varepsilon\geq 0
;
(2) that if suitable equilibria/strategies exist for
\varepsilon>0
, then also ones that are
polynomially representable and verifiable; (3) that the loss of precision incurred by implementing the reduction scheme approximately can be bounded.
The latter two points use a fixed-precision floating-point representation allowing us to represent doubly-exponentially small values in strategies using a polynomial number of bits.

Our results have direct consequences for solving several well-known types of
games
including partial-information parity and mean-payoff games
(
Hunter et al., 2018
)
,
stay-in-a-set games
(
Secchi and Sudderth, 2002
)
,
quitting games
(
Solan and Vieille, 2001
)

and concurrent reachability, Büchi and parity games
(
Bordais et al., 2021
;
Bouyer et al., 2021
;
Bordais et al., 2022
;
Bordais et al., 2023
)
.
In particular, we show how to approximate values/equilibria for
2
-player, zero-sum concurrent mean-payoff games (not discounted and with no assumptions on strategies - in particular, allowing infinite memory ones as is sometimes required)
(
Mertens and Neyman, 1981
;
Chatterjee et al., 2008
;
Hansen et al., 2009
;
Hansen et al., 2011
;
Oliu-Barton, 2021
)
. Our algorithm is in
{\mathsf{FNP}}^{\mathsf{NP}}
, improving on the state-of-the-art algorithms that use polynomial space based on reductions to the existential theory of the reals
(
Hansen et al., 2011
;
Etessami and Yannakakis, 2008
)
.

Notations

For a set
X
, we write
X^{k}
for its
k
-fold Cartesian product and
a[i]
for the
i
th component of a vector
a\in X^{k}
.
Let
X^{*}
and
X^{\omega}
denote
the sets of finite and infinite sequences with elements in
X
, respectively.
We write

\mathbb{D}(X)
for the set of all probability distributions over
X
.

Partial-information Games

A
k
-player game is given by finite sets of states
V
, actions
A
, signals
S
, rewards
C
, and a transition function
\Delta:V\times A^{k}\to\mathbb{D}(C^{k}\times S^{k}\times V)
that maps the current state and action vector to a probability distribution over triples describing rewards, next signals, and the next state.
The game is played in stages
i\in\{0,1,2,\dots\}
, in each of which every player receives a signal, selects an action and receives a reward.
At stage
i
, the game is in a vertex
v_{i}
.
Each player
j
is sent the signal
s_{i}[j]

and selects an action
a_{i}[j]
.
Then a triple of rewards, signals, and successor state
(c_{i+1},s_{i+1},v_{i+1})
is drawn randomly according to
\Delta(v_{i},a_{i})

and the game moves to the next stage in vertex
v_{i+1}
.
This random process goes on forever and describes
an infinite sequence

\rho=(v_{0},s_{0},a_{0},c_{0}),(v_{1},s_{1},a_{1},c_{1})\dots\in(V\times S^{k}\times A^{k}\times C^{k})^{\omega}
.
We call such an infinite sequence a
play
and let
{\mathop{Plays}\nolimits}

denote the set of all plays.

Note that games as described above are
partial-
(aka.
imperfect
) information games:
the players do not directly observe the current state, the actions selected by other players nor the rewards obtained (not even their own). All they get to see is the signals sent to them.
We call a game
perfect-information
if, at every stage
i>0
, every player receives as a signal the full action vector
a_{i-1}
, reward vector
c_{i-1}
and the current state
v_{i}
.
A game is
turn-based
(as opposed to
concurrent
) if in any stage, only one player’s action influences the next state and signals. That is, for every vertex
v\in V
there is some player
j
so that
\Delta(v,a)=\Delta(v,a^{\prime})
whenever
a[j]=a^{\prime}[j]
.

Plays and Strategies

A
strategy
is a function

\sigma:S^{*}\to\mathbb{D}(A)
,
that determines, for every sequence of signals, a probability distribution over the action set.
It is based on a set
M
of memory modes if it can be described by an
initial memory mode
m_{0}\in M
and a
pair of functions

{\sigma}_{\mathop{act}\nolimits}:M\times S\rightarrow\mathbb{D}(A)
and

{\sigma}_{\mathop{up}\nolimits}:M\times S\rightarrow\mathbb{D}(M)

that select actions and update the memory mode, respectively.
That is, for any signal sequence,

s=s_{0}s_{1}s_{2}\ldots s_{j}\in S^{*}
,

\sigma(s)={\sigma}_{\mathop{act}\nolimits}(m_{j},s_{j})
and

m_{j+1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{{\tiny{def}}}}}{{=}}{\sigma}_{\mathop{up}\nolimits}(m_{j},s_{j})
.

A strategy is called
finite-memory
if it is based on a finite memory set
M

and
memoryless
if it is based on a singleton set
M
.
A strategy is
discrete
(also
pure
) if all its choices are Dirac.
In perfect-information games, we call a strategy

stationary
if
its choices only depend on the current state (and not previous actions or rewards).

Strategy profiles, Probability measures

A
strategy profile
is a family
\sigma=(\sigma[j])_{j\leq k}
of strategies, one for each player.
Together with an initial state
v_{0}
and signal
s_{0}
,
it uniquely induces a probability measure

\mathbb{P}^{\sigma}_{v_{0},s_{0}}\in\mathbb{D}({\mathop{Plays}\nolimits})

over infinite plays
(see
(
Billingsley, 1995
)
for details). We will write
{\mathbb{E}}^{\sigma}_{v_{0},s_{0}}\left[{X}\right]
for the derived expected value of a random variable
X
and drop indices when clear from the context.

Objectives

Each player
j\leq k
has an objective, a measurable function
\mathcal{O}_{j}:{\mathop{Plays}\nolimits}\to\mathbb{R}

and selects their strategy to maximise the expectation of this objective, against any possible opponent strategies.
A game is
zero-sum
if
\sum_{j\leq k}\mathcal{O}_{j}(\rho)=0
for every
\rho\in{\mathop{Plays}\nolimits}
.

In this paper, we consider mean-payoff objectives as well as the special case of reachability objectives.
A
mean-payoff
objective
(for player
j\leq k
) assigns play

\rho=(v_{0},s_{0},a_{0},c_{0}),(v_{1},s_{1},a_{1},c_{1})\dots

the value

\underline{\mathop{MP}}(\rho)=\liminf_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=0}^{N}c_{i}[j].

The
reachability
objective for a target set
T\subseteq V
of states
assigns play

\rho
as above the value
1
if
v_{i}\in T
for some round
i\geq 0
and
0
otherwise.

Optimality criteria

The player in a 1-player game
G
plays to maximise the expectation of the given objective
\mathcal{O}
.
Here,
the
value
of the game is

{\mathtt{val}(G)}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{{\tiny{def}}}}}{{=}}\sup_{\sigma\in\Sigma}{\mathbb{E}}^{\sigma}_{v_{0},s_{0}}\left[{\mathcal{O}}\right]

and
for
\varepsilon\geq 0
, a strategy
\sigma
is called
\varepsilon
-optimal
if

{\mathtt{val}(G)}-{\mathbb{E}}^{\sigma}_{v_{0},s_{0}}\left[{\mathcal{O}}\right]\leq\varepsilon
.

For 2-player zero-sum games
G
,
let
\Sigma
and
\Pi
denote the set of strategies for players 1 and 2, respectively.
Then the
lower value
and
upper value
are defined as

{\overline{\mathtt{val}}({G})}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{{\tiny{def}}}}}{{=}}\sup_{\sigma\in\Sigma}\inf_{\pi\in\Pi}{\mathbb{E}}^{\sigma,\pi}_{v_{0},s_{0}}\left[{\mathcal{O}}\right]

and

{\underline{\mathtt{val}}({G})}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{{\tiny{def}}}}}{{=}}\inf_{\pi\in\Pi}\sup_{\sigma\in\Sigma}{\mathbb{E}}^{\sigma,\pi}_{v_{0},s_{0}}\left[{\mathcal{O}}\right]
.
By definition, it holds that
{\overline{\mathtt{val}}({G})}\leq{\underline{\mathtt{val}}({G})}
.
If the two are equal
then this quantity is called the

value
of the game, denoted by
{\mathtt{val}(G)}
.
For
\varepsilon\geq 0
, a strategy
\sigma\in\Sigma
is called
\varepsilon
-optimal

if

{\mathtt{val}(G)}-\inf_{\pi\in\Pi}{\mathbb{E}}^{\sigma,\pi}_{v_{0},s_{0}}\left[{\mathcal{O}}\right]\leq\varepsilon
(and similar for player 2 strategies
\pi
).

For general
k
-player games
G

and any
\varepsilon\geq 0
, an
\varepsilon
-Nash equilibrium
(NE) is a strategy profile
(\sigma_{j})_{j\leq k}

so that
any unilateral deviation by a player
j
increases their expected outcome by at most
\varepsilon
.
That is, for any profile
\sigma^{\prime}
where
\sigma^{\prime}[i]=\sigma[i]
for all
i\neq j
,
it holds that

{\mathbb{E}}^{\sigma^{\prime}}\left[{\mathcal{O}_{j}}\right]\leq{\mathbb{E}}^{\sigma}\left[{\mathcal{O}_{j}}\right]+\varepsilon
.

Complexity classes

Function problems
are given by a relation
R\subseteq\{0,1\}^{*}\times\{0,1\}^{*}

where for some polynomial
p
, every
(x,y)\in R
implies
{\left|y\right|}\leq p({\left|x\right|})
.
For a class
\mathcal{X}
of decision problems,

is in the corresponding function class
{\mathsf{F\mathcal{X}}}

if and only if the language
\{(x,y)\mid R(x,y)\}
is in
\mathcal{X}
,
meaning the question if
R(x,y)
is true for given

and
y

\mathcal{X}
.
This corresponds to the search problem
where for given

, or correctly

In particular, we are concerned with the classes

, at level

Floating-point Representations of Distributions

For any number

, let
\mathbb{Q}(u)

The

The floating point representation of

\langle 1^{u},0\rangle
.
The

between two non-negative

as

.

.
Let

denote the set of finite probability distributions

.
We call the primed vector the (

-bit)

of the unprimed distribution.

3.
Overview of Our Results

We consider the problem of approximating
\varepsilon
-Nash equilibria and zero-sum values
with respect to bounded-memory strategies.

Definition 3.1.

Consider some
k
-player game
with objectives
(\mathcal{O}_{j})_{j\leq k}
and a set
\mathcal{S}

of admissible strategy profiles.
Let
\varepsilon\geq 0
.

An
\varepsilon
-Nash equilibrium

with respect to

\mathcal{S}

is a strategy profile
(\sigma_{j})_{j\leq k}\in\mathcal{S}

so that
any unilateral deviation by a player
j

that yields another profile in
\mathcal{S}

can at most increase their expected outcome by
\varepsilon
.
That is, for any profile
\sigma^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}
where
\sigma^{\prime}[i]=\sigma[i]
for all
i\neq j
,
it holds that

{\mathbb{E}}^{\sigma^{\prime}}\left[{\mathcal{O}_{j}}\right]\leq{\mathbb{E}}^{\sigma}\left[{\mathcal{O}_{j}}\right]+\varepsilon
.

In the
2
-player zero-sum case, we say a strategy

\sigma
for player
1

\varepsilon
-achieves
value
v\in\mathbb{R}
with respect to
\mathcal{S}

if it guarantees expected outcome of at least
v-\varepsilon
against all admissible opponent strategies.
That is,
if
\inf_{\pi~\mid~(\sigma,\pi)\in\mathcal{S}}{\mathbb{E}}_{\sigma,\pi}\left[{\mathcal{O}_{1}}\right]\geq v-\varepsilon
.

For instance, an
\varepsilon
-Nash equilibrium with respect to finite-memory strategies, is a profile consisting of finite-memory strategies, so that
no player can, by switching to another finite-memory strategy, improve their expected payout by more than
\varepsilon
.

We are interested in approximating such equilibria and zero-sum strategies
with respect to finite memory strategies with bounded memory.
Formally, we are given a
k
-player partial-information game
G
, some
\varepsilon\in[0,1]
and a vector
{\bf b}\in\mathbb{N}^{k}
of memory bounds, one for each player
i\leq k
.
Write
\mathcal{B}_{i}({\bf b})
for the set of player
i
strategies that are based on a set
M
of size
{\left|M\right|}\leq{\bf b}[i]

and let
\mathcal{B}({{\bf b}})
be all strategy profiles wherein each player
i
uses a strategy in
\mathcal{B}_{i}({\bf b}[i])
.
For multiplayer games (
k\geq 2
) we ask if there exists an
\varepsilon
-Nash equilibrium with respect to (wrt)
\mathcal{B}({{\bf b}})
and if so, we want to compute one.
For
2
-player zero-sum games, we ask if there exists a strategy that

\varepsilon
-achieves a given value
v
with respect to (wrt)
\mathcal{B}({{\bf b}})
.

Throughout, we assume that the number
k
of players is fixed;
The inputs
\varepsilon
,
v
and rewards are given in binary
and the bounds
{\bf b}
in unary encoding
1
1

1

This assumption is natural as we want to compute representations of strategies, in particular, which
mixed action to play for any combination of game and memory state.
.
The probability distributions dictated by the transition function
\Delta

are assumed to be given in
u
-bit approximately normalised floating point notation.

Theorem 3.2.

For every fixed
k\geq 1
the following is in
\mathsf{PSPACE}
.

(1)

Given a
k
-player game, bounds
{\bf b}\in\mathbb{N}^{k}
and
\varepsilon\geq 0
,
check whether an
\varepsilon
-Nash equilibrium
exists with respect to
\mathcal{B}({{\bf b}})
.

(2)

Given a
2
-player zero-sum game, bounds
{\bf b}\in\mathbb{N}^{2}
, a number
v\in\mathbb{R}
and
\varepsilon\geq 0
, check whether there exists a player
1
strategy that
\varepsilon
-achieves value
v

with respect to
\mathcal{B}({{\bf b}})
.

Our proof is by an effective polynomial reduction of the problem to
the satisfiability problem of
\mathrm{FO}(\mathbb{R})
formula so that
the size of the constructed formula is only polynomial in the input,
has fixed alternation depth of
2
,
and solutions dictate values and witnessing strategies/equilibria.
At its core, our encoding relies on a state reduction technique for finite Markov chains, that results from fixing a strategy profile. This requires only polynomially many collapses and can therefore be “implemented” as a
small system of polynomial equations.

We show that equilibria, zero-sum values and witnessing strategies can be approximated, up to an exponential additive error, at level 2 of the polynomial hierarchy.

Theorem 3.3.

For every fixed
k\geq 1
the following are in
{\mathsf{FNP}}^{\mathsf{NP}}
.

(1)

Given a
k
-player game, bounds

and
\varepsilon\in(0,1/2)
,
find an
\varepsilon
-Nash equilibrium with respect to
\mathcal{B}({{\bf b}})

(if for some
\varepsilon^{\prime}\in[0,\varepsilon/3)
,
an
\varepsilon^{\prime}
-Nash equilibrium with respect to
\mathcal{B}({{\bf b}})
exists);

(2)

Given a
2
-player zero-sum game, bounds
b\in\mathbb{N}^{k}
,
\varepsilon\in(0,1/2)
and

5.
Mean-Payoff Values for Markov Chains

We demonstrate how to iteratively collapse Markov chains
with the aim of computing the expected mean-payoff value of a fixed initial state. The underlying idea is to simultaneously summarise the expected reward and duration (number of steps) of paths between two nodes
and adjust these summaries consistently during collapses. Once an irreducible chain is produced it is trivial to read off the mean-payoff values.

A Markov chain is a
1
-player game with only a single, unique action

for the player in every state
(the player is just there to collect the rewards and cannot influence the outcome).

{\mathop{MPR}}(\rho)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{{\tiny{def}}}}}{{=}}\liminf_{N\to\infty}\frac{\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}\mathop{r}\nolimits(s_{i},s_{i+1})}{\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}\mathop{d}\nolimits(s_{i},s_{i+1})}

8.
Applications

We discuss the implications of our results for some well-known classes of games.

8.1.
Multi-player partial-information parity games

A
parity
objective
(for player
j\leq k
) assigns a play

\rho=~(v_{0},s_{0},

a_{0},c_{0}),(v_{1},s_{1},a_{1},c_{1})\dots

the value
1
if

\limsup_{N\to\infty}c_{i}[j]
, the maximal reward seen infinitely often, is even and
0
otherwise.

In perfect-information turn-based games, this condition can be translated into a mean-payoff condition, based on the observation that the game effectively ends after a cycle is formed (see e.g.
(
Fijalkow and Monmege, 2023
, Theorem 40)
).
However, no such simple reduction can generalise even to concurrent games,
because the most significant reward may occur arbitrarily rarely.
In concurrent games, as opposed to the simpler turn-based ones,
\varepsilon
-optimal strategies might require infinite memory

(
Hansen et al., 2023
)
and mixed actions with double exponentially small probabilities
(
Hansen et al., 2009
)
.

We now argue that one can adapt our constructions to parity objectives as follows.
First, state and loop elimination (
Section

5
)
are simpler for parity since one does not need to keep track of the duration or of the exact reward but can instead just set the new reward to be the maximum of the old ones.

The only non-trivial change required is the proof of
Lemma

7.3
,
which bounds the change in values achieved by strategy profiles under small perturbations.
Specifically, our argument relies on
(
Solan, 2003
, Theorem 5)
, which only applies to mean-payoff games and not parity games.
To adapt our proof, we consider the Markov chain induced by any fixed strategy profile where each player uses finite memory.
Now almost surely, some CCS is entered and then every reward contained is seen infinitely often. Therefore a player gets overall parity reward 1 in a CCS within which their largest reward is even (and 0 otherwise).
Therefore, for each CCS, any perturbation of the probabilities in the corresponding states (i.e. state in the original game and memory vector) that have the same support will not change the outcome of that CCS.
We can therefore define an equivalent mean-payoff Markov chain
in which each CSS is collapsed to an absorbing chain with reward
1
or
0
accordingly. The claim of
Lemma

7.3
(parity) then follows from
Lemma

7.3
as stated for mean-payoff.

We conclude that

Theorems

3.2
and
3.3
hold as stated also for (multi-player, partial-information) games with parity objectives.

8.2.
Multi-player concurrent games

We consider different classes of perfect-information games
that are
concurrent
games,
meaning the motion is determined by simultaneously chosen actions
of all players, as opposed to the
turn-based
games where in each round
only the choice of one player matters.

When considering bounded strategies, it is instructive to distinguish
private
memory (accessible only by the respective player as used throughout this paper) from
public
memory, which is visible to all players.
Essentially, public memory is shared among all players (but can only be updated by its owner) (see
(
Hansen et al., 2023
, Section 5.1)
for details).

The important difference for us is that
fixing public memory strategies for all but one player results in
an ordinary MDP (a perfect-information 1-player game) for the remaining player.
On the other hand, fixing private memory strategies for all but one player,
results in a
partial-information
MDP, as the player cannot know their opponents’ memory modes.

8.2.1.
Computing
\varepsilon
-Nash Equilibria

Our technique allows us to compute general

-Nash equilibria, i.e., without restricting the set of admissible strategies, assuming that there exist equilibria with bounded public memory.
Formally, for a

For any fixed

, we get the following:
Given a

-Nash equilibrium wrt

Proof.

This follows from

Each player

Finite MDPs with mean-payoff objectives have memoryless

8.2.2.
Stay-In-a-set games

These are multi-player concurrent games where each player has a separate safety objective,
that is, wants the play to remain in a given subset of the states.

Secchi and Sudderth

(

showed that

\varepsilon
-Nash equilibria, but the players might need to remember who has already lost.
Such strategies are finite memory public strategies (with the number of memories equal to

For a fixed number of players

\varepsilon
-Nash equilibria with strategies using public memory and optimise the necessary memory bounds. That is, we can find public-memory equilibria with the least amount of memory.

Corollary 8.2.

For every

{\mathsf{FNP}}^{\mathsf{NP}}

Given a

find an
\varepsilon

Proof.

One first encodes the safety conditions as a mean-payoff condition,
so that a player receives reward

Using a subset construction, this results in a game with

In order to keep a one-to-one correspondence of equilibria it is necessary

In particular, in the original game, it is sufficient for players

Our reduction encodes this information into the states, and so it must be hidden from players (by having any state

The claim now follows by

because, if each player is playing a public and finite-memory strategy, one just needs

∎

8.2.3.
Quitting games

Quitting games
(

Solan and Vieille, 2003

are concurrent mean-payoff games
in which there is only one non-absorbing state and the players each have two actions in that state: Continue or Stop.
The play goes to an absorbing state (effectively stops) in the first round when at least one player plays their stop action.

Under mild assumptions on the payoffs
2
2

2

They ask that (i) Each player prefers to be the only quitter rather than having the game continue forever, and (ii) if a player quits, he prefers to be the only quitter.
,

(

show the existence of “cyclic”
\varepsilon

in which players’ behaviour repeat after some number of stages.
Any such equilibrium can be represented as one consisting of public finite memory strategies.
Specifically, we need to have a state of memory corresponding to each stage in the “cyclic”
\varepsilon

While they do not provide a bound directly, they prove the existence of such ‘cyclic”

Our algorithm can therefore find such an

For any fixed

Similarly, given a game and a cyclic

Approximating zero-sum values

We consider the problem of approximating ordinary (zero-sum) values for two-player concurrent mean-payoff games.
Recall that all strategies getting close to ensuring the value might require infinite memory
(
Hansen et al., 2023
)
.

Different methods (see eg.
(
Mertens and Neyman, 1981
;
Chatterjee et al., 2008
;
Hansen et al., 2011
;
Oliu-Barton, 2021
)
)
have been proposed for this.
The most well-known approach is the value iteration algorithm of

Mertens and Neyman

(
Mertens and Neyman, 1981
)
, which has later been show to take double-exponentially many iterations to
\varepsilon
-approximate the values, see
(
Hansen et al., 2011
;
Hansen et al., 2009
)
.
The current best complexity upper bound is
\mathsf{PSPACE}
:
As shown by
(
Hansen et al., 2011
;
Mertens and Neyman, 1981
)
using a double exponentially small discount factor, the corresponding discounted game to a mean-payoff game will have the same value
\pm\varepsilon
. As shown by
(
Etessami and Yannakakis, 2008
)
a discounted game can be solved in
\mathsf{PSPACE}
by reduction
3
3

3

Etessami and Yannakakis

(
Etessami and Yannakakis, 2008
)
show how to construct
\exists
\mathrm{FO}(\mathbb{R})
formulae for games with fixed discount factors, but one can create double-exponential values using repeated squaring
in the existential fragment.

to the existential fragment of
\mathrm{FO}(\mathbb{R})
.

Our technique yields the current best algorithm for this problem.

Corollary 8.5.

The following is in
{\mathsf{FNP}}^{\mathsf{NP}}
.
Given a two-player, zero-sum concurrent mean-payoff game and
\varepsilon>0
,
find the value of the game within an additive error of
\varepsilon
.

Proof.

As shown already in
(
Mertens and Neyman, 1981
)
, the value of a mean-payoff game is equal to the limit of values of the same game but with discount
d
for
d
going to
0
.

Hansen et al.

(
Hansen et al., 2011
)
show that for any discount
d
below some double exponentially small number, the value of the mean-payoff game differs by less than
\varepsilon
from that of the discounted game with discount factor
d
.
For a formal definition of discounted-payoff games, we refer to
(
Hansen et al., 2011
;
Solan and Vieille, 2001
)
.

There is a well-known reduction from discounted games to (non-discounted) mean-payoff games already present in
(

)
:
Given a

, replace each action

This results in a mean-payoff game

Such strategies are in particular public memory strategies. As is well-known, in a two-player, zero-sum game, a Nash equilibrium must be made up of two optimal strategies and an optimal strategy for each player forms an Nash equilibrium.

The claim now follows from

∎

Lemma A.2.

If
s
is recurrent then

{\mathbb{E}}_{s}\left[{{\mathop{MPR}}}\right]={\mathbb{E}}_{s}\left[{{\mathop{R}\nolimits_{s}}}\right]/{\mathbb{E}}_{s}\left[{{\mathop{D}\nolimits_{s}}}\right]
.

Proof.

Let
s
belong to some CCS
S
with
n
states.
Let
p
be the smallest non-zero probability associated to any edge between vertices in
S
. Let
R
(resp.
r
) be the most positive (resp. most negative) reward per step and
D
(resp.
d
) be the largest (resp. smallest) duration of a step.

By definition of CCS, there is always a path between any two states in
S
and thus in particular, a simple one (i.e. with no state occurring twice). This means that there is a simple path consisting of at most
n-1
edges between the states. Each edge has probability of at least
p

p^{n-1}
to be followed.
From each
s^{\prime}
consider a shortest (in number of steps) directed path
P_{s^{\prime}}
from
s^{\prime}
to
s
. We start an

whenever we visit
s
or just after we have moved along an edge not in
P_{s^{\prime}}
, where
s^{\prime}
was the last state in which we started an attempt. In particular, we start a new attempt every at most
n

s
with probability at least
p^{n-1}
. Therefore, in expectation, we need at most
p^{1-n}
many attempts. Each attempt took at most
n
steps.

This means that almost all plays visits
s
infinitely many times. Let
s_{i}
be the random variable denoting the step in which the play visits
s
the
i
th time. We view the steps inbetween
s_{i}
and

as a

and we thus have infinitely many trials.

Consider any
\varepsilon>0
.
We partition plays into two sets,
{\mathop{G}}
and
{\mathop{N}}
.
A play is in
{\mathop{G}}
, if the average reward of the trials is within
(1\pm\varepsilon)
of
{\mathbb{E}}_{s}\left[{{\mathop{R}\nolimits_{s}}}\right]
(unless
{\mathbb{E}}_{s}\left[{{\mathop{R}\nolimits_{s}}}\right]=0
, in which case, we have that the average reward of the trials is in
[-\varepsilon,\varepsilon]
for a play to be in
{\mathop{G}}
) and the average duration of the trials is within
(1\pm\varepsilon)

. The remaining plays are in
{\mathop{N}}
.

By linearity of expectation, we have that

{\mathbb{E}}_{s}\left[{{\mathop{MPR}}}\right]={\mathbb{E}}_{s}\left[{{\mathop{MPR}}\mid{\mathop{N}}}\right]\mathbb{P}_{s}\left({{\mathop{N}}}\right)+{\mathbb{E}}_{s}\left[{{\mathop{MPR}}\mid{\mathop{G}}}\right]\mathbb{P}_{s}\left({{\mathop{G}}}\right).

For any play,
{\mathop{MPR}}
is in
[\min(r/d,r/D),\max(R/d,R/D)]

{\mathbb{E}}_{s}\left[{{\mathop{MPR}}\mid{\mathop{N}}}\right]

\mathbb{P}_{s}\left({{\mathop{N}}}\right)<\varepsilon
.

By definition of
{\mathop{G}}

{\mathbb{E}}_{s}\left[{{\mathop{MPR}}\mid{\mathop{G}}}\right]\in\left[\frac{(1-\varepsilon){\mathbb{E}}_{s}\left[{{\mathop{R}\nolimits_{s}}}\right]}{(1+\varepsilon){\mathbb{E}}_{s}\left[{{\mathop{D}\nolimits_{s}}}\right]},\frac{(1+\varepsilon){\mathbb{E}}_{s}\left[{{\mathop{R}\nolimits_{s}}}\right]}{(1-\varepsilon){\mathbb{E}}_{s}\left[{{\mathop{D}\nolimits_{s}}}\right]}\right]

Conversely, if
{\mathbb{E}}_{s}\left[{{\mathop{R}\nolimits_{s}}}\right]<0
, we have that

{\mathbb{E}}_{s}\left[{{\mathop{MPR}}\mid{\mathop{G}}}\right]\in\left[\frac{(1+\varepsilon){\mathbb{E}}_{s}\left[{{\mathop{R}\nolimits_{s}}}\right]}{(1-\varepsilon){\mathbb{E}}_{s}\left[{{\mathop{D}\nolimits_{s}}}\right]},\frac{(1-\varepsilon){\mathbb{E}}_{s}\left[{{\mathop{R}\nolimits_{s}}}\right]}{(1+\varepsilon){\mathbb{E}}_{s}\left[{{\mathop{D}\nolimits_{s}}}\right]}\right]

Finally, if
{\mathbb{E}}_{s}\left[{{\mathop{R}\nolimits_{s}}}\right]=0

{\mathbb{E}}_{s}\left[{{\mathop{MPR}}\mid{\mathop{G}}}\right]\in\left[-\frac{\varepsilon}{(1-\varepsilon){\mathbb{E}}_{s}\left[{{\mathop{D}\nolimits_{s}}}\right]},\frac{\varepsilon}{(1-\varepsilon){\mathbb{E}}_{s}\left[{{\mathop{D}\nolimits_{s}}}\right]}\right]

In this section, we show how to find the outcome of an induced Markov chain for a specific player in state 1 in the first order theory over the reals. The problem, in particular, is that depending on which strategy profile we picked, the induced Markov chain could have different (end) strongly connected components and we need the formula to be the same no matter which strategy profile we picked.
