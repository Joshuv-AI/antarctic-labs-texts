Abstract
. Let Γ ⊂ ℝ d \Gamma\subset\mathbb{R}^{d} be a smooth curve containing the origin. Does every Borel subset of ℝ d \mathbb{R}^{d} of sufficiently small codimension enjoy a Sárk o ¨ \ddot{\text{o}} zy-like property with respect to Γ \Gamma , namely, contain two elements differing by a member of Γ ∖ { 0 } \Gamma\setminus\{0\} ? Kuca, Orponen, and Sahlsten [ 26 ] answer this question in the affirmative for a specific curve with nonvanishing curvature, the standard parabola ( t , t 2 ) (t,t^{2}) in ℝ 2 \mathbb{R}^{2} . In this article, we use the analytic notion of “functional type”, a generalization of curvature ubiquitous in harmonic analysis, to study containment of patterns in sets of large Hausdorff dimension. Specifically, for every curve Γ ⊂ ℝ d \Gamma\subset\mathbb{R}^{d} of finite type at the origin, we prove the existence of a dimensional threshold ε > 0 \varepsilon>0 such that every Borel subset of ℝ d \mathbb{R}^{d} of Hausdorff dimension larger than d − ε d-\varepsilon contains a pair of points of the form { x , x + γ } \{x,x+\gamma\} with γ ∈ Γ ∖ { 0 } \gamma\in\Gamma\setminus\{0\} . The threshold ε \varepsilon we obtain, though not optimal, is shown to be uniform over all curves of a given “type”. We also demonstrate that the finite type hypothesis on Γ \Gamma is necessary, provided Γ \Gamma either is parametrized by polynomials or is the graph of a smooth function. Our results therefore suggest a correspondence between sets of prescribed Hausdorff dimension and the “types” of two-point patterns that must be contained therein.

1.
Introduction

Hausdorff dimension is a notion of size universally used in geometric measure theory. The aim of this article is to establish Hausdorff dimension as an identifier of two-point “curved” patterns contained in a set, with an appropriate interpretation of curvature.

Let us start with the necessary terminology.

•

A
smooth curve
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
refers to the image of an infinitely differentiable function
\Phi\colon\mathtt{I}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
, where
\mathtt{I}\subset\mathbb{R}
is any nondegenerate compact interval. Here and throughout, we adopt the convention that derivatives at the endpoints of
\mathtt{I}
are one-sided. We also assume, from now on, that all compact intervals
\mathtt{I}
are nondegenerate, i.e. have positive length. For any smooth curve
\Gamma
, there are many functions
\Phi
and intervals
\mathtt{I}
such that
\Gamma=\Phi(\mathtt{I})
.

•

A smooth curve
\Gamma\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
will be called
unavoidable
if there exists a constant
\varepsilon>0
such that
(\Gamma\setminus\{0\})\cap(K-K)\neq\emptyset
for every Borel set
K\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
\dim_{\text{H}}K>d-\varepsilon
; if there is no such

, then
\Gamma
is
avoidable
. Here and throughout,
\dim_{\text{H}}
denotes the Hausdorff dimension; see
[
33
, Chapter 4, §4.3]
.

An unavoidable curve necessarily contains the origin. Indeed, suppose
\Gamma
is a smooth curve that does not contain the origin. Since
\Gamma
is compact, we have
\operatorname{dist}(0,\Gamma)>0
. It follows that
\Gamma\cap(K-K)=\emptyset
for any set
K
of diameter strictly less than
\operatorname{dist}(0,\Gamma)
, including Euclidean balls which not only have full Hausdorff dimension but positive Lebesgue measure. Equivalently stated, if
\Gamma
is an unavoidable curve, then sets of large Hausdorff dimension but arbitrarily small diameter would contain pairs of the form
\{x,x+\gamma\}
with
\gamma\in\Gamma
, forcing the origin to be a limit point (and thus an element) of
\Gamma
. Unavoidability is therefore a local property of a curve, reflecting its behaviour near the origin.

A classical result of Furstenberg
[
14
]
and Sárk
\ddot{\text{o}}
zy
[
39
,
40
]
shows that the difference set

of any set

of positive upper density cannot be square-free. The concept of unavoidability extends this notion to Euclidean spaces of dimension

: If
\Gamma\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
is unavoidable, then the difference set
K-K
of any “large” set

must contain a nontrivial element of
\Gamma
. Recent work of Kuca, Orponen, and Sahlsten
[
26
]
has shown that the standard parabola
\{(t,t^{2})\colon t\in[-1,1]\}
in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
is unavoidable, leading to a natural question as to which other curves enjoy this property. This article addresses this question by presenting results of two types. We show that a smooth curve is unavoidable if it is of “finite type” at the origin. We also consider two distinct subclasses of smooth curves, namely smooth graphs and polynomial curves, and show that within these classes the finite type hypothesis is necessary (as well as sufficient) for unavoidability.

To state our results, let us recall (e.g. from

1.1.
Statement of results

We assume for the entirety of the article that the ambient dimension
d\geq 2
is fixed. All constants are allowed to depend on
d
. The following are our main results.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
\Gamma\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a smooth curve of finite type at the origin. Then
\Gamma
is unavoidable.

A stronger quantitative version of Theorem
1.1
is given in Theorem
1.5
below, where the dimensional threshold is shown to be uniform across the class of curves of fixed type.

As a consequence of Theorem
1.1
, a full-dimensional set must contain every two-point pattern of finite type.

Corollary 1.2
.

Let
K\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a Borel set with
\dim_{\mathrm{H}}K=d
. Then
(\Gamma\setminus\{0\})\cap(K-K)\neq\emptyset
for every smooth curve
\Gamma\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
of finite type at the origin.

For certain classes of smooth curves, namely graphs and polynomials, the assumption of finite type at the origin is both sufficient and necessary for unavoidability.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
\Gamma\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a curve that contains the origin and is the graph of a smooth function, i.e., of the form

(1.1)

\displaystyle\Gamma=\{(t,\Psi(t))\colon t\in\mathtt{I}\}

for some compact interval
\mathtt{I}
with

and some smooth function
\Psi\colon\mathtt{I}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d-1}
with
\Psi(0)=0
. Then
\Gamma
is unavoidable if and only if
\Gamma
is of finite type at the origin.

Theorem 1.4
.

Let
\Gamma\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a polynomial curve that contains the origin, i.e.,
\Gamma=\Phi(\mathtt{I})
for some compact interval
\mathtt{I}
and some

of univariate polynomials

such that

for some

. Then the following are equivalent:

1.

\Gamma
is unavoidable.

2.

\Gamma
is of finite type at the origin.

3.

\Gamma
is not contained in any hyperplane in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

4.

There exist linearly independent polynomials
\Phi_{1},\ldots,\Phi_{d}
and a compact interval
\mathtt{I}
such that
\Gamma=\{(\Phi_{1}(t),\ldots,\Phi_{d}(t))\colon t\in\mathtt{I}\}
.

5.

If
\Phi_{1},\ldots,\Phi_{d}
and

are any choice of polynomials and a compact interval such that

are linearly independent.

We turn our attention now to a quantitative formulation of the qualitative statement in Theorem

For each

A curve of finite type at the origin may be represented as the image of many functions. The goal of this subsection is to find a parametrization that is most convenient for later usage.

2.2.1.

A recurring feature in the study of configurations is the formulation of an appropriate integral whose positivity signals the presence of the desired configuration. We describe below the integral relevant to our problem.

2.2.3.

Given a finite type curve

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}^{\ast}=\mathcal{D}^{\ast}[\vec{n}]\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\bigcup_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}\mathcal{D}_{j},\quad\text{where}\quad\mathcal{D}_{j}=\mathcal{D}_{j}[\vec{n}]\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\Big\{a+\prod_{i=1}^{d}[0,2^{-n_{i}j})\colon a\in\prod_{i=1}^{d}2^{-n_{i}j}\mathbb{Z}\Big\}.

Remark 2.5
.

The above argument relies crucially on the relationship between the curve
\Gamma
and the collection
\mathcal{D}^{*}=\mathcal{D}^{*}[\vec{\mathtt{n}}]
of dyadic boxes. We would like to explain how these boxes were chosen. For simplicity, we will consider the example of
\Gamma=\operatorname{im}\Phi
with

\displaystyle\Phi(t)=(t^{2},t^{3}+t^{4}),\quad\quad t\in[0,1],

and thus
\vec{\mathtt{n}}=(2,3)
. Roughly speaking, Propositions
2.3
and
2.2
imply that if
K
supports a measure with a spectral gap, then
K-K
intersects
\Gamma\setminus\{0\}
as desired. Proposition
2.4
provides a rectangle
\mathtt{Q}\in\mathcal{D}^{*}
such that the affine image
{\bf T}_{\mathtt{Q}}(K)
of
K
under the rescaling map for
\mathtt{Q}
supports a measure with this property. In an ideal scenario, the dimensions of our dyadic rectangles in
\mathcal{D}^{*}
would be chosen so that
\Gamma
would be invariant under the (linear part of the) inverse scaling map
{\bf T}_{\mathtt{Q}}^{-1}
. This would then allow us to pull back the desired pattern from
{\bf T}_{\mathtt{Q}}(K)
to
K
. However, the curve
\Gamma
in the present example does not enjoy any such scaling relation. Instead, it possesses an approximate scaling relation based on its leading order behaviour: The rescaled functions

\displaystyle\Phi^{j}(t)\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}(2^{2j}\Phi_{1}(2^{-j}t),2^{3j}\Phi_{2}(2^{-j}t))=(t^{2},t^{3}+2^{-j}t^{4})

yield a sequence of curves
\Gamma_{j}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\operatorname{im}\Phi^{j}
that approach the “leading order” curve

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\infty}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\{(t^{2},t^{3})\colon t\in[0,1]\}

as

, and this limit curve is invariant under the transformation
(x_{1},x_{2})\mapsto(2^{2j}x_{1},2^{3j}x_{2})
for any

. We define the rectangles in

to have dimensions

, so that (the linear parts of) their rescaling maps coincide with this transformation. Since Proposition

is in fact uniform in

contains a nonzero point

, where
\mathtt{j}
is such that
\mathtt{Q}\in\mathcal{D}_{\mathtt{j}}
. As desired, the rescalings

and

are compatible, in the sense that now

.

3.
High-dimensional graphs

The proofs of Theorems
1.3
and
1.4
require construction of counterexamples to unavoidability. For these, we will utilize the existence of high-dimensional one-parameter graphs.

Proposition 3.1
.

For every
s\in[1,d)
, there exists a Hölder-continuous function
F_{s}\colon[0,1]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d-1}
such that

(3.1)

\displaystyle\|F_{s}\|_{C^{0,\alpha}}<\infty\quad\text{for all}\quad 0<\alpha<\min\Big\{\frac{1}{s},\frac{d-s}{d-1}\Big\},

and

(3.2)

\displaystyle\dim_{\mathrm{H}}(\operatorname{graph}F_{s})=s,\quad\text{where}\quad\operatorname{graph}F_{s}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\{(t,F_{s}(t))\colon t\in[0,1]\};

here,
\|\cdot\|_{C^{0,\alpha}}
denotes the Hölder norm

\|f\|_{C^{0,\alpha}}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\sup_{t\in[0,1]}|f(t)|+\sup_{t,t^{\prime}\in[0,1]\colon t\neq t^{\prime}}\frac{|f(t)-f(t^{\prime})|}{|t-t^{\prime}|^{\alpha}}.

This classical result, originally due to Besicovitch and Ursell
[
3
]
for
d=2
, now has many variants in the literature; see for example
[
28
,
23
,
29
,
30
,
44
,
47
]
. It has been proved in its above-stated form by Kahane
[
20
]
, who shows through a random argument that such functions
F_{s}
are in fact plentiful. We briefly outline his argument, pointing the reader to the relevant sections of the text for a complete proof.

In
[
20
, Chapter 18]
and for
n,d\geq 1
, Kahane introduces an
(n,d,\gamma)
Gaussian process
\{X_{t}\colon t\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\}
with values in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that

\mathbb{E}(|X_{t}-X_{t^{\prime}}|^{2})=d|t-t^{\prime}|^{\gamma}.

Such a process is shown to exist in
[
20
, Chapter 18, §2]
when
0<\gamma\leq 2
, with an almost sure continuous version, i.e. with
t\mapsto X(t;\omega)
being a continuous function of
t
for almost every
\omega
. More precisely, the modulus of continuity
\omega_{X}
of
X(\cdot;\omega)
is shown to obey

(3.3)

\omega_{X}(h)=\sup_{|t-t^{\prime}|\leq h}|X(t)-X(t^{\prime})|=O\bigl(\sqrt{|h|^{\gamma}\log(1/|h|)}\bigr)\qquad\text{ almost surely }

on every compact subset of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. This is stated in equation (3) of

[
20
, Chapter 18]
, and follows from the content of
[
20
, Chapter 14]
. The condition (
3.3
) implies that (almost surely)

(3.4)

X(t)
is H
\ddot{\text{o}}
lder continuous with exponent
\gamma/2-\varepsilon
for every
\varepsilon\in(0,\gamma/2)
.

In
[
20
, Chapter 18, §7, Theorem 7]
, Kahane proves:

For any compact set
E\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
, the relation

\dim_{\mathrm{H}}\bigl(\operatorname{graph}X|_{E}\bigr)=\min\Bigl\{\frac{2}{\gamma}\dim_{\mathrm{H}}E,\,\dim_{\mathrm{H}}E+\Big(1-\frac{\gamma}{2}\Big)d\Bigr\}

holds almost surely
.

Here,
\operatorname{graph}X|_{E}
denotes the set
\{(t,X(t))\colon t\in E\}
. We can now obtain Proposition
3.1
from Kahane’s theorem as follows: Set
n=1
and
E=[0,1]
, and replace
d
by
(d-1)
and
X(t)
by
F_{s}(t)
to get

(3.5)

\displaystyle\dim_{\mathrm{H}}(\operatorname{graph}F_{s})=\dim_{\mathrm{H}}(\operatorname{graph}X|_{[0,1]})=\min\Bigl\{\frac{2}{\gamma},d-\frac{\gamma}{2}(d-1)\Bigr\},

If we choose

\displaystyle\gamma=\min\Big\{\frac{2}{s},\frac{2(d-s)}{d-1}\Big\}=\begin{cases}\frac{2}{s}&\text{if }1\leq s\leq d-1,\\
\frac{2(d-s)}{d-1}&\text{if }d-1<s<d,\end{cases}

then (
3.4
) implies the first conclusion of the proposition, and (
3.5
) and a bit of arithmetic confirm the second. ∎

4.
Graphs of infinite type are avoidable

The goal of this section is to prove Theorem
1.3
. We will in fact prove a slightly stronger statement (Proposition
4.1
below), namely an analogue of the theorem for curves that are not necessarily graphs, but graph-like. Additionally, we formulate a quantitative partial-avoidance result for graph-like curves of finite type (Proposition
4.2
); the proof is sketched in the Appendix.

Let us set up the relevant definition. We say that a smooth curve
\Gamma\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
is
graph-like
if there exists an invertible linear transformation
{\bf L}\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
and a smooth function
\Phi\colon\mathtt{I}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that

(i)

\mathtt{I}\subset\mathbb{R}
is a nondegenerate compact interval,

(ii)

\Phi=:(\Phi_{1},\underline{\Phi})
is of the form
\Phi_{1}(t)=t^{\mathtt{m}}\phi(t)
for some integer
\mathtt{m}\geq 1
and some smooth function
\phi\colon\mathtt{I}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
such that
\inf\{|\phi(t)|\colon t\in\mathtt{I}\}>0
,

(iii)

{\bf L}(\Gamma)=\Phi(\mathtt{I})
.

In the above, if

Proposition 4.1

4.1.
Proof of Proposition
4.1

is unavoidable. Toward proving the other direction, we assume that
\Gamma

is the identity, since unavoidability and type are both invariant under the action of invertible linear transformations. The hypothesis that
\Gamma
contains the origin, together with conditions (ii) and (iii), implies that

\displaystyle 0<\mathtt{r}<\mathtt{c}_{1}\min\bigg\{\delta,\bigg(\frac{\mathtt{c}_{0}}{\|F_{s}\|_{C^{0,\alpha}}\mathtt{C}_{\mathtt{n}}^{\alpha}}\bigg)^{\frac{1}{\mathtt{n}\alpha-\mathtt{m}}}\bigg\}^{\mathtt{m}},

•

•

•

•

\displaystyle K\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}{\bf U}(\operatorname{graph}F_{s})\cap Q_{\mathtt{r}}.

\displaystyle|\mathtt{t}|<\min\bigg\{\delta,\bigg(\frac{\mathtt{c}_{0}}{\|F_{s}\|_{C^{0,\alpha}}\mathtt{C}_{\mathtt{n}}^{\alpha}}\bigg)^{\frac{1}{\mathtt{n}\alpha-\mathtt{m}}}\bigg\}

5.
Polynomial patterns of infinite type are avoidable

In this section, we prove Theorem

5.2.
Checking conditions for (un)avoidability

As noted in Section
1
, an unavoidable smooth curve must contain the origin. Combining this observation with statements 4 and 5 in Theorem
1.4
, we get a simple criterion for checking whether a given tuple of polynomials
\Phi=(\Phi_{1},\ldots,\Phi_{d})
defines an unavoidable curve on a compact interval
\mathtt{I}
: The image of
\Phi
on
\mathtt{I}
is unavoidable if and only if
\Phi_{1},\ldots,\Phi_{d}
are linearly independent and share a common zero in
\mathtt{I}
. The following examples illustrate this with
d=3
:

•

\Gamma=\{(t^{2}-1,t^{3}+5t-6,2t^{3}-t^{2}-1)\colon t\in[0,1]\}
is unavoidable. The parametrizing polynomials are linearly independent and vanish at
t=1
.

•

\Gamma=\{(t-2,t^{2}-2t,t^{2}+t-6)\colon t\in[0,1]\}
is avoidable. The parametrizing polynomials are linearly dependent.

•

\Gamma=\{(t+1,t^{2}-1,t^{3}+2t+1)\colon t\in[0,1]\}
is avoidable. The parametrizing polynomials do not share a zero.

In general, the presence of a shared zero among polynomials can be checked by, say, using the Euclidean algorithm to compute their greatest common divisor and then using Sturm’s theorem to determine whether that divisor has a real zero.

In this section, we prove Proposition

7.3.

k_{0}<i\leq n-1
. If
x_{n}/x_{k_{0}}\leq\mathtt{a}
, then the conclusion of the lemma holds with
k=k_{0}
. Assume that
x_{n}/x_{k_{0}}\geq\mathtt{a}
. Then for
i<n
we have

\displaystyle\frac{x_{i}}{x_{n}}=\frac{x_{i}}{x_{k_{0}}}\cdot\frac{x_{k_{0}}}{x_{n}}\leq\mathtt{a}^{-n+1}\mathtt{a}^{-1}=\mathtt{a}^{-n},

and the conclusion of the lemma holds with
k=n
.
∎

Lemma 7.4
.

There exists an admissible constant
\mathtt{L}_{\mathtt{N}}
with the following property: For every function
\Phi\colon[0,1]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
in standard form that is vanishing of type
\mathtt{N}
at the origin, there exists an (inadmissible) index
\mathtt{J}\geq\mathtt{J}_{0}(\Phi)
depending on
\Phi
such that

(7.5)

\displaystyle\sup\{|\widehat{\pi}(\xi)|\colon\pi=\pi[\Phi;j,c],\,j\geq\mathtt{J},c\in(0,1]\}\leq\mathtt{L}_{\mathtt{N}}(1+|\xi|)^{-{1/\mathtt{N}}}\quad\text{for all }\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

Proof.

Fix
\pi
of the form
\pi=\pi[\Phi;j,c]
and
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\{0\}
. It follows from (
2.13
) that

(7.6)

\widehat{\pi}(\xi)=\int e(x\cdot\xi)d\pi(x)=\int_{c}^{1}e(\xi\cdot\Phi^{j}(s))ds,\quad\text{where }e(t)\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}e^{-2\pi it}.

The integral representing
\widehat{\pi}(\xi)
is a scalar oscillatory integral widely studied in harmonic analysis. Our goal is to apply the well-known method of stationary phase for oscillatory integrals (see
[
43
, Chapter VIII]
) to arrive at the desired bound (
7.5
). It is important to keep track of the implicit constants in this process to ensure uniformity in the parameters
j
and
c
; we describe the steps below.

Let us choose, in the following order, a small constant
\mathtt{a}\in(0,1]
depending on
\mathtt{K}_{\mathtt{N}}
, and a large integer
\mathtt{J}\geq\mathtt{J}_{0}
depending on
\mathtt{a}
and
\Phi
, according to the constraints

(7.7)

d\mathtt{K}_{\mathtt{N}}\mathtt{a}\leq\frac{1}{8},\qquad d\mathtt{a}^{-d}\|\Phi\|_{C^{\mathtt{N}}}2^{-\mathtt{J}}\leq\frac{1}{8}.

Here,
\|\cdot\|_{C^{n}}
refers to the standard norm on the space of
n
-times continuously differentiable functions
f\colon[0,1]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
, namely

\displaystyle\|f\|_{C^{n}}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\sum_{\ell=0}^{n}\sup_{t\in[0,1]}|f^{(\ell)}(t)|.

We assume now that
j\geq\mathtt{J}
. By Lemma
7.3
, there exists an index
k\in\{1,\ldots,d\}
depending on
\mathtt{a}
and
\xi
such that

(7.8)

\xi_{k}\neq 0,\quad\quad\frac{|\xi_{i}|}{|\xi_{k}|}\leq\mathtt{a}^{-d}\quad\text{if $1\leq i\leq k$},\quad\quad\frac{|\xi_{i}|}{|\xi_{k}|}\leq\mathtt{a}\quad\text{if $k<i\leq d$.}

Let us define a function
\varphi=\varphi_{k}
by the formula

(7.9)

\displaystyle\varphi(s)\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\frac{\xi}{\xi_{k}}\cdot\Phi^{j}(s)

\displaystyle=\xi_{k}^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{d}2^{\mathtt{n}_{i}j}\xi_{i}\Phi_{i}(2^{-j}s)\quad\text{for }s\in[c,1],

so that (
7.6
) reduces to

(7.10)

\displaystyle\widehat{\pi}(\xi)

\displaystyle=\int_{c}^{1}e(\xi_{k}\varphi(s))ds.

We claim that the
\mathtt{n}_{k}^{\text{th}}
order derivative of the phase function
\varphi
in the oscillatory integral (
7.10
) is bounded from below by an absolute positive constant, due to our choice of
\mathtt{a}
and
\mathtt{J}
in (
7.7
). To verify this, we first note that

\displaystyle\varphi^{(\mathtt{n}_{k})}(s)

\displaystyle=\xi_{k}^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{d}2^{(\mathtt{n}_{i}-\mathtt{n}_{k})j}\xi_{i}\Phi_{i}^{(\mathtt{n}_{k})}(2^{-j}s)=\mathrm{I}+\mathrm{II}+\mathrm{III},

where

\displaystyle\mathrm{I}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\Phi_{k}^{(\mathtt{n}_{k})}(2^{-j}s),\quad\mathrm{II}

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}\frac{\xi_{i}}{\xi_{k}}2^{(\mathtt{n}_{i}-\mathtt{n}_{k})j}\Phi_{i}^{(\mathtt{n}_{k})}(2^{-j}s),\quad\mathrm{III}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\sum_{i=k+1}^{d}\frac{\xi_{i}}{\xi_{k}}2^{(\mathtt{n}_{i}-\mathtt{n}_{k})j}\Phi_{i}^{(\mathtt{n}_{k})}(2^{-j}s).

Here, any empty sum is treated as zero. Noting that
2^{-j}s\in(0,2^{-\mathtt{J}_{0}}]
, we use the left inequality in (
2.10
) to bound I from below, obtaining
|\mathrm{I}|\geq\frac{1}{2}
. The strict monotonicity (
2.3
) of the exponents
\mathtt{n}_{i}
, the choice (
7.8
) of the index
k
, and the condition (
2.9
) can be used to estimate II and III from above. A combination of these properties yields

\displaystyle|\mathrm{II}|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}\mathtt{a}^{-d}2^{-j}|\Phi_{i}^{(\mathtt{n}_{k})}(2^{-j}s)|\leq d\mathtt{a}^{-d}2^{-\mathtt{J}}\|\Phi\|_{C^{\mathtt{N}}}\leq\frac{1}{8},

\displaystyle|\mathrm{III}|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=k+1}^{d}\mathtt{a}2^{(\mathtt{n}_{i}-\mathtt{n}_{k})j}\mathtt{K}_{\mathtt{N}}(2^{-j}s)^{(\mathtt{n}_{i}-\mathtt{n}_{k})}\leq d\mathtt{a}\mathtt{K}_{\mathtt{N}}\leq\frac{1}{8},

where the last step in both inequalities follows from (
7.7
). As a result, we obtain

(7.11)

|\varphi^{(\mathtt{n}_{k})}(s)|\geq|\mathrm{I}|-|\mathrm{II}|-|\mathrm{III}|\geq\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{8}-\frac{1}{8}=\frac{1}{4}\quad\text{for all }s\in[c,1].

This lower bound is critical to the application of the method of stationary phase, which proceeds via two cases.

Case 1:
First suppose that
\mathtt{n}_{k}\geq 2
. Using van der Corput’s lemma (see
[
43
, Ch. VIII, §1.2, Proposition 2]
), one can find an absolute (hence admissible) constant
c_{\mathtt{n}_{k}}\geq 1
such that

(7.12)

\displaystyle|\widehat{\pi}(\xi)|=\Big|\int_{c}^{1}e^{-2\pi i\xi_{k}\varphi(s)}ds\Big|\leq c_{\mathtt{n}_{k}}|\xi_{k}|^{-1/\mathtt{n}_{k}}.

Property (
7.8
) implies that

\displaystyle|\xi|\leq|\xi_{1}|+\cdots+|\xi_{d}|\leq(k\mathtt{a}^{-d}+(d-k)\mathtt{a})|\xi_{k}|\leq d\mathtt{a}^{-d}|\xi_{k}|.

Inserting this into (
7.12
) and considering also the trivial estimate
|\widehat{\pi}(\xi)|\leq\|\pi\|\leq 1
, we find that

(7.13)

\displaystyle|\widehat{\pi}(\xi)|\leq\min\Big\{1,c_{\mathtt{n}_{k}}\Big(\frac{\mathtt{a}^{d}}{d}|\xi|\Big)^{-1/\mathtt{n}_{k}}\Big\}

\displaystyle\leq 2c_{\mathtt{n}_{k}}\Big(\frac{\mathtt{a}^{d}}{d}\Big)^{-1/\mathtt{n}_{k}}(1+|\xi|)^{-1/\mathtt{n}_{k}}

\displaystyle\leq 2c_{\mathtt{n}_{k}}d\mathtt{a}^{-d}(1+|\xi|)^{-1/\mathtt{N}}\leq\mathtt{L}_{\mathtt{N}}(1+|\xi|)^{-1/\mathtt{N}}

with
\mathtt{L}_{\mathtt{N}}=2d\mathtt{a}^{-d}\max\{c_{2},\ldots,c_{\mathtt{N}}\}
.

Case 2:
Next suppose that
\mathtt{n}_{k}=1
. In view of (
2.3
), this means that
k=1
. Property (
7.8
) thus implies that

(7.14)

|\xi|\leq|\xi_{1}|+\cdots+|\xi_{d}|\leq(1+(d-1)\mathtt{a})|\xi_{1}|\leq d|\xi_{1}|.

By virtue of (
7.9
), (
2.9
), (
2.10
), and (
7.7
), we have

\displaystyle|\varphi^{\prime\prime}(s)|=\Bigl|\sum_{i=1}^{d}2^{(\mathtt{n}_{i}-2)j}\frac{\xi_{i}}{\xi_{1}}\Phi_{i}^{\prime\prime}(2^{-j}s)\Bigr|

\displaystyle\leq 2^{-j}\|\Phi\|_{C^{\mathtt{N}}}+\sum_{i=2}^{d}2^{(\mathtt{n}_{i}-2)j}\mathtt{a}\mathtt{K}_{\mathtt{N}}(2^{-j}s)^{\mathtt{n}_{i}-2}

(7.15)

\displaystyle\leq 2^{-\mathtt{J}}\|\Phi\|_{C^{\mathtt{N}}}+d\mathtt{a}\mathtt{K}_{\mathtt{N}}\leq 2d\mathtt{K}_{\mathtt{N}}\leq\mathtt{L}_{\mathtt{N}}

for a suitable choice of
\mathtt{L}_{\mathtt{N}}
. Combining the lower bound (
7.11
) on
|\varphi^{\prime}|
and the upper bound (
7.15
) on
|\varphi^{\prime\prime}|
with integration by parts, we obtain

\displaystyle|\widehat{\pi}(\xi)|=\Bigl|\int_{c}^{1}e(\xi_{1}\varphi(s))ds\Bigr|

\displaystyle=\Bigl|\int_{c}^{1}\frac{1}{2\pi\xi_{1}\varphi^{\prime}(s)}\cdot\frac{d}{ds}[e(\xi_{1}\varphi(s))]ds\Bigr|

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi|\xi_{1}|}\biggl|\frac{e(\xi_{1}\varphi(s))}{\varphi^{\prime}(s)}\Bigr]_{c}^{1}+\int_{c}^{1}\frac{\varphi^{\prime\prime}(s)}{(\varphi^{\prime}(s))^{2}}e(\xi_{1}\varphi(s))ds\biggr|\leq\frac{\mathtt{L}_{\mathtt{N}}}{|\xi_{1}|}

for some (larger) choice of
\mathtt{L}_{\mathtt{N}}
. We also have the trivial bound
|\widehat{\pi}(\xi)|\leq\|\pi\|\leq 1
. These estimates, together with (
7.14
), imply that

(7.16)

\displaystyle|\widehat{\pi}(\xi)|\leq\min\Bigl\{1,\frac{d\mathtt{L}_{\mathtt{N}}}{|\xi|}\Bigr\}\leq\mathtt{L}_{\mathtt{N}}(1+|\xi|)^{-1}\leq\mathtt{L}_{\mathtt{N}}(1+|\xi|)^{-{1/\mathtt{N}}},

where here we have allowed the value of
\mathtt{L}_{\mathtt{N}}
to change between the first two occurences. Combining the conclusions (
7.13
) and (
7.16
) of the two cases completes the proof of (
7.5
).
∎

We begin by defining the admissible constant
\mathtt{L}_{\mathtt{N}}

\mathtt{M}_{1}
be the admissible constant in Corollary

(appearing there as
\mathtt{L}_{\mathtt{N}}
) when applied with

(7.17)

\displaystyle\mathtt{a}=\frac{1}{2}\cdot 15^{-d}|B(0;1)|;

here,
|\cdot|
refers to Lebesgue measure. Let
\mathtt{M}_{2}
be the admissible constant in Lemma

(also appearing there as
\mathtt{L}_{\mathtt{N}}
). We set

\displaystyle\mathtt{L}_{\mathtt{N}}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\max\{\mathtt{M}_{1},\mathtt{M}_{2},2\pi|B(0;1)|+2+2\gamma_{\mathtt{N}}^{-1}\},

where

is the constant defined in (

Next we fix a function

\mathtt{N}
at the origin, and we define the inadmissible integer
\mathtt{J}(\Phi)
. For this, we can just use the integer
\mathtt{J}
from Lemma
7.4
.

Now, let
\mathtt{A},\mathtt{B},\mathtt{C}
be any choice of constants satisfying (
2.16
), and let
\mu
be any Borel probability measure
\mu
on

that obeys (
2.17

) holds. With this aim in mind, fix

with

, and fix

. Write

\displaystyle\int\mu_{\delta}\ast\pi\,d\mu=\mathbb{I}_{1}+\mathbb{I}_{2},

where

\displaystyle\mathbb{I}_{1}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\int\mu_{\mathtt{A}^{-3d}}\ast\pi\,d\mu,\quad\quad\mathbb{I}_{2}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\int(\mu_{\delta}-\mu_{\mathtt{A}^{-3d}})\ast\pi\,d\mu.

We claim that

(7.18)

\mathbb{I}_{1}\geq\frac{1}{2}\mathtt{L}_{\mathtt{N}}^{-1}\mathtt{A}^{-3d}\quad\text{and}\quad|\mathbb{I}_{2}|\leq\mathtt{L}_{\mathtt{N}}\mathtt{A}^{-4d}.

This, together with our assumption on
\mathtt{A}
in (
2.16
) and
\mathtt{L}_{\mathtt{N}}\geq 1
, would imply
(
2.18
).

We start with
\mathbb{I}_{1}
. Using the constant
\mathtt{a}
defined in (
7.17
), let

\mathcal{G}=\{x\in\operatorname{supp}\mu\colon\eqref{lower bound ball pi}\text{ holds with }x_{0}=x\}.

Setting

in Corollary

leads to the pointwise lower bound

\displaystyle\mu_{\mathtt{A}^{-3d}}\ast\pi(x)

\displaystyle\geq\mathtt{L}_{\mathtt{N}}^{-1}\mathtt{A}^{-3d}\quad\text{for every }x\in\mathcal{G},

and consequently

\displaystyle\mathbb{I}_{1}\geq\int_{\mathcal{G}}\mu_{\mathtt{A}^{-3d}}\ast\pi\,d\mu\geq\mathtt{L}_{\mathtt{N}}^{-1}\mathtt{A}^{-3d}\mu(\mathcal{G}).

. For each

, there exists by definition a ball
B_{x}
of radius

such that

\mu(B_{x})\leq\mathtt{a}r_{x}^{d}\leq\mathtt{c}|B_{x}|,\quad\text{ where }\mathtt{c}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\frac{\mathtt{a}}{|B(0;1)|}=\frac{1}{2}\cdot 15^{-d}.

The balls

\mathcal{G}^{c}
and lie within

. The Vitali covering lemma,
[
11

, yields a countable set

are pairwise disjoint. Here,

denotes a ball with the same centre as

but
m
times its radius. This leads to

\displaystyle 1-\mu(\mathcal{G})

\displaystyle=\mu([0,1]^{d}\setminus\mathcal{G})\leq\sum_{x\in X}\mu(B_{x})\leq\mathtt{c}\sum_{x\in X}|B_{x}|

\displaystyle=5^{d}\mathtt{c}\sum_{x\in X}\Big|\frac{1}{5}B_{x}\Big|=5^{d}\mathtt{c}\Bigl|\bigcup_{x\in X}\frac{1}{5}B_{x}\Bigr|\leq 5^{d}\mathtt{c}|[-1,2]^{d}|=15^{d}\mathtt{c}=\frac{1}{2}.

Thus,
\mu(\mathcal{G})\geq 1/2
, concluding the estimation of
\mathbb{I}_{1}
.

We now turn to

\displaystyle|\mathbb{I}_{2}|\leq\int|\widehat{\mu}(\xi)|^{2}|\widehat{\psi}(\delta\xi)-\widehat{\psi}({\mathtt{A}^{-3d}}\xi)||\widehat{\pi}(\xi)|d\xi.

\{|\xi|\leq\mathtt{A}\}

and

\displaystyle\int_{|\xi|\leq\mathtt{A}}|\widehat{\mu}(\xi)|^{2}|\widehat{\psi}(\delta\xi)-\widehat{\psi}({\mathtt{A}^{-3d}}\xi)||\widehat{\pi}(\xi)|d\xi

\displaystyle\leq\int_{|\xi|\leq\mathtt{A}}\pi[(\delta|\xi|)^{2}+(\mathtt{A}^{-3d}|\xi|)^{2}]d\xi

\displaystyle\leq 2\pi|B(0;\mathtt{A})|(\mathtt{A}^{-3d}\mathtt{A})^{2}\leq 2\pi|B(0;1)|{\mathtt{A}}^{-4d}.

We use the spectral gap hypothesis in (
2.17
) to control the moderate-frequency piece, namely

\displaystyle\int_{|\xi|\in[\mathtt{A},\mathtt{B}]}|\widehat{\mu}(\xi)|^{2}|\widehat{\psi}(\delta\xi)-\widehat{\psi}({\mathtt{A}^{-3d}}\xi)||\widehat{\pi}(\xi)|d\xi\leq 2\int_{|\xi|\in[\mathtt{A},\mathtt{B}]}|\widehat{\mu}(\xi)|^{2}d\xi\leq 2\mathtt{A}^{-4d};

here, we have also used that

(from (

) and (

), and our assumption on

\displaystyle\int_{|\xi|\geq\mathtt{B}}|\widehat{\mu}(\xi)|^{2}|\widehat{\psi}(\delta\xi)-\widehat{\psi}({\mathtt{A}^{-3d}}\xi)||\widehat{\pi}(\xi)|d\xi

\displaystyle\leq 2\mathtt{L}_{\mathtt{N}}\int_{|\xi|\geq\mathtt{B}}|\widehat{\mu}(\xi)|^{2}|\xi|^{-\frac{1}{\mathtt{N}}}d\xi

\displaystyle\leq 2\mathtt{L}_{\mathtt{N}}\mathtt{B}^{-\frac{1}{2\mathtt{N}}}\gamma_{\mathtt{N}}^{-1}I_{\sigma_{\mathtt{N}}}(\mu)\leq 2\mathtt{L}_{\mathtt{N}}\mathtt{B}^{-\frac{1}{2\mathtt{N}}}\gamma_{\mathtt{N}}^{-1}\mathtt{C}\leq 2\gamma_{\mathtt{N}}^{-1}\mathtt{A}^{-4d}.

Now, summing the bounds for the three pieces and recalling our choice of choice of

, it follows that

, as claimed. This completes the proof. ∎

8.
Constructing a suitable measure: Proof of Proposition
2.4

The goal of this section is to prove Proposition
2.4
, which establishes the existence of a certain measure. Before embarking on the construction of this measure, we pause to collect a few necessary tools.

8.1.
Measure-theoretic preliminaries

Let
\vec{n}=(n_{1},\ldots,n_{d})
be a vector with positive integer entries, and recall the definition of
\mathcal{D}^{\ast}=\mathcal{D}^{\ast}[\vec{n}]
given in (
2.19
). Here, we do not assume that the entries of

are distinct or ordered. The aim of this subsection is to define an anisotropic, dyadic version of “Hausdorff content” using the collection
\mathcal{D}^{*}
and establish connections between it and the standard notion of Hausdorff dimension. Although we opt to work from first principles, these connections can also be well understood using the broader framework of Hausdorff dimension in metric spaces; see
[
33
, Chapter 4]
. This perspective is described in Section
9
. The lemmas stated in the present subsection may be unsurprising to experts. However, they do not appear in standard textbooks in the form that we need, and thus we provide their proof in the Appendix.

Our version of Hausdorff content is defined as follows: Let

and

. Then

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}^{s}_{\mathcal{D}^{*}}(E)\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\inf\Big\{\sum_{Q\in\mathcal{Q}}\ell(Q)^{s}\colon\mathcal{Q}\subseteq\mathcal{D}^{*}\text{ and }E\subseteq\bigcup\mathcal{Q}\Big\},

where

is as in (

). We are particularly interested in conditions on

and

that guarantee the positivity of

\displaystyle\mathtt{N}=\mathtt{N}[\vec{n}]\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\max\{n_{1},\ldots,n_{d}\}\quad\quad\text{and}\quad\quad\mathtt{S}=\mathtt{S}[\vec{n}]\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}n_{1}+\ldots+n_{d}.

The following lemma addresses this question and establishes the basic connection between
\dim_{\mathrm{H}}E
and
\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{D}^{*}}^{s}(E)

Lemma 8.1
.

If

and

We will also need a restricted version of

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{D}_{J}^{*}}^{s}(E)\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\inf\Big\{\sum_{Q\in\mathcal{Q}}\ell(Q)^{s}\colon\mathcal{Q}\subseteq\mathcal{D}_{J}^{*}\text{ and }E\subseteq\bigcup\mathcal{Q}\Big\},

Lemma 8.2

Next, we record a version of Frostman’s lemma adapted to

Lemma 8.3
.

Our final lemma gives a connection between the Frostman condition for

and the finiteness of energy integrals. It will help us verify that the blown-up measure

Choice of constants

2.17
). The values of
\mathtt{A}
and
\mathtt{B}
will depend on
\varphi
. We define them as follows:
Let
\mathtt{A}
be chosen large enough that

(8.3)

\displaystyle\mathtt{A}^{d}\geq 4\mathtt{L}_{\mathtt{N}}^{2}\quad\quad\text{and}\quad\quad\int_{|\xi|\geq\mathtt{A}}|\widehat{\varphi}(\xi)|d\xi\leq\frac{1}{2}\mathtt{A}^{-4d},

where
\mathtt{L}_{\mathtt{N}}
is the constant appearing in (
2.16
). Let
\mathtt{B}
be chosen sufficiently large relative to
\mathtt{A},\mathtt{C}
, and
\mathtt{L}_{\mathtt{N}}
, as specified in (
2.16
). This concludes our choice of the constants
\mathtt{A},\mathtt{B},\mathtt{C}
.

We are left to define
\varepsilon
. Toward this end, we introduce another large admissible constant
\mathtt{T}
. Specifically, we take
\mathtt{T}
to be an integer satisfying

(8.4)

\displaystyle 4\pi\sqrt{d}\mathtt{B}|B(0;\mathtt{B})|2^{-\mathtt{T}}\leq\frac{1}{2}\mathtt{A}^{-4d}.

We now define

(8.5)

\displaystyle\varepsilon\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\min\Big\{\frac{\log_{2}(1+2^{-d\mathtt{N}\mathtt{T}-2})}{\mathtt{N}\mathtt{T}},\frac{1}{4\mathtt{N}^{2}}\Big\}.

8.2.2.
Locating the dyadic box
\mathtt{Q}

At this point, we fix

•

a function
\Phi\colon[0,1]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
in standard form and vanishing of type
\mathtt{N}
at the origin, and

•

a Borel set
K\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
\dim_{\mathrm{H}}K>d-\varepsilon
.

Let
\mathtt{J}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\mathtt{J}(\Phi)
as in Proposition
2.3
, and let
\vec{\mathtt{n}}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}(\mathtt{n}_{1},\ldots,\mathtt{n}_{d})
be the vector of integers associated with
\Phi
in (
2.3
). For the remainder of this section,
\vec{\mathtt{n}}
should be used whenever an object depends on a vector of integers. So, for example,
\mathcal{D}^{*}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\mathcal{D}^{*}[\vec{\mathtt{n}}]
and
\mathcal{D}_{J}^{*}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\mathcal{D}_{J}^{*}[\vec{\mathtt{n}}]
.

Our next goal is to locate a box
\mathtt{Q}\in\mathcal{D}_{\mathtt{J}}^{*}
such that
K\cap\overline{\mathtt{Q}}
will support a measure whose blow-up the obeys energy and spectral gap conditions in (
2.17
). Henceforth, we will assume that
K
is compact. A straightforward application of Frostman’s lemma for Borel sets (e.g.
[
34
, Theorem 2.7]
) shows that
K
contains a compact subset whose Hausdorff dimension strictly exceeds
d-\varepsilon
. Therefore, this assumption is permissible.

Using Lemma
8.1
and our dimension assumption on
K
, we select some

(8.6)

\displaystyle s\in(\mathtt{S}-\mathtt{N}\varepsilon,\mathtt{S}]

such that
\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{D}^{*}}^{s}(K)>0
. It follows that
\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{D}_{\mathtt{J}}^{*}}^{s}(K)>0
as well, and we also have
\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{D}_{\mathtt{J}}^{*}}^{s}(K)<\infty
trivially. We claim for each
\delta>0
there exists
Q\in\mathcal{D}_{\mathtt{J}}^{*}
such that

(8.7)

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{D}_{\mathtt{J}}^{*}}^{s}(K\cap Q)\geq(1-\delta)\ell(Q)^{s}.

To see this, note that for each
c>0
there exists
\mathcal{Q}_{c}\subseteq\mathcal{D}_{\mathtt{J}}^{*}
such that
\mathcal{Q}_{c}
covers
K
and

\displaystyle\sum_{Q\in\mathcal{Q}_{c}}\ell(Q)^{s}\leq\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{D}_{\mathtt{J}}^{*}}^{s}(K)+c.

If there were some
\delta>0
such that no
Q\in\mathcal{D}_{\mathtt{J}}^{*}
satisfied (
8.7
), then we would have

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{D}_{\mathtt{J}}^{*}}^{s}(K)\leq\sum_{Q\in\mathcal{Q}_{c}}\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{D}_{\mathtt{J}}^{*}}^{s}(K\cap Q)\leq(1-\delta)\sum_{Q\in\mathcal{Q}_{c}}\ell(Q)^{s}\leq(1-\delta)(\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{D}_{\mathtt{J}}^{*}}^{s}(K)+c),

and taking
c
sufficiently small would produce a contradiction. This proves the claim. By Lemma
8.1
, condition (
8.7
) is equivalent to the statement that

(8.8)

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{D}^{*}}^{s}(K\cap Q)\geq(1-\delta)\ell(Q)^{s}.

We now define
\mathtt{Q}
to be any
Q\in\mathcal{D}_{\mathtt{J}}^{*}
such that (
8.8
) holds with
\delta\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}2^{-\mathtt{S}\mathtt{T}-2}
.

8.2.3.
Constructing the measure
\nu

Let
\operatorname{ch}(\mathtt{Q})
denote the set of
\mathtt{T}^{\mathrm{th}}
-generation descendants of
\mathtt{Q}
in
\mathcal{D}^{*}
; that is,

\displaystyle\operatorname{ch}(\mathtt{Q})\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\{q\in\mathcal{D}^{*}\colon q\subseteq\mathtt{Q},~\ell(q)=2^{-\mathtt{T}}\ell(\mathtt{Q})\}.

For the sake of readability, we will from now on denote elements of
\operatorname{ch}(\mathtt{Q})
using the lowercase letter
q
, while the capital letter
Q
will continue to refer to generic elements of
\mathcal{D}^{*}
. We claim that

(8.9)

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{D}^{*}}^{s}(K\cap q)\geq\frac{1}{2}\ell(q)^{s}\quad\text{for every }q\in\operatorname{ch}(\mathtt{Q}).

To see this, let

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\Big\{q\in\operatorname{ch}(\mathtt{Q})\colon\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{D}^{*}}^{s}(K\cap q)\geq\frac{1}{2}\ell(q)^{s}\Big\}

and suppose for contradiction that
\mathcal{G}\subsetneq\operatorname{ch}(\mathtt{Q})
. Then, by our choice of
\mathtt{Q}
, the inequality
\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{D}^{*}}^{s}(K\cap q)\leq\ell(q)^{s}
, our choice of
s
in (
8.6
), and the definition of
\varepsilon
in (
8.5
), we have

\displaystyle 1-2^{-\mathtt{S}\mathtt{T}-2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{D}^{*}}^{s}(K\cap\mathtt{Q})}{\ell(\mathtt{Q})^{s}}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{q\in\mathcal{G}}\frac{\ell(q)^{s}}{\ell(\mathtt{Q})^{s}}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{q\in\operatorname{ch}(\mathtt{Q})\setminus\mathcal{G}}\frac{\ell(q)^{s}}{\ell(\mathtt{Q})^{s}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{q\in\operatorname{ch}(\mathtt{Q})}\frac{\ell(q)^{s}}{\ell(\mathtt{Q})^{s}}-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{q\in\operatorname{ch}(\mathtt{Q})\setminus\mathcal{G}}\frac{\ell(q)^{s}}{\ell(\mathtt{Q})^{s}}

\displaystyle\leq 2^{\mathtt{S}\mathtt{T}}2^{-\mathtt{T}s}-\frac{1}{2}\cdot 2^{-\mathtt{T}s}<2^{\varepsilon\mathtt{N}\mathtt{T}}-2^{-\mathtt{S}\mathtt{T}-1}\leq 1+2^{-\mathtt{S}\mathtt{T}-2}-2^{-\mathtt{S}\mathtt{T}-1}.

This gives a contradiction (note the strict inequality), and so the claim is proved.

Combining the lower bound (
8.9
) with Lemma
8.3
, we get for each
q\in\operatorname{ch}(\mathtt{Q})
a Borel measure
\vartheta_{q}
supported on
K\cap\overline{q}
such that

(8.10)

\displaystyle\|\vartheta_{q}\|\geq\frac{1}{2}\ell(q)^{s}\quad\quad\text{and}\quad\quad\vartheta_{q}(Q)\leq\ell(Q)^{s}\quad\text{for every }Q\in\mathcal{D}^{*}.

The measure
\nu
will be defined as a weighted sum of the measures
\vartheta_{q}
. Specifically, let
\varphi
be the bump function introduced above, and let
{\bf T}_{\mathtt{Q}}
be the rescaling map defined in (
2.22
) that takes
\mathtt{Q}
to
[0,1)^{d}
. Let

\displaystyle w(q)\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\int_{{\bf T}_{\mathtt{Q}}(q)}\varphi\quad\quad\text{and}\quad\quad\overline{\vartheta}_{q}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\frac{w(q)\ell(\mathtt{Q})^{s}}{\|\vartheta_{q}\|}\vartheta_{q}

for each
q\in\operatorname{ch}(\mathtt{Q})
. With these definitions in place, we take
\nu
to be

\displaystyle\nu\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\sum_{q\in\operatorname{ch}(\mathtt{Q})}\overline{\vartheta}_{q}.

It is clear that
\nu
is supported on
K\cap\overline{\mathtt{Q}}
and has total mass

(8.11)

\displaystyle\|\nu\|=\sum_{q\in\operatorname{ch}(\mathtt{Q})}\|\overline{\vartheta}_{q}\|=\sum_{q\in\operatorname{ch}(\mathtt{Q})}w(q)\ell(\mathtt{Q})^{s}=\ell(\mathtt{Q})^{s}\int\varphi=\ell(\mathtt{Q})^{s}>0.

Our next goal is to analyze the blow-up of
\nu
with respect to
\mathtt{Q}
. For this measure to be well defined, it must satisfy
\nu(\mathtt{Q})>0
. In view of (
8.11
), this could fail only if
\operatorname{supp}\nu
lies entirely within the boundary of
\mathtt{Q}
. We claim that

(8.12)

\displaystyle\vartheta_{q}(\partial q)=0\quad\text{for every }q\in\operatorname{ch}(\mathtt{Q});

here,
\partial E
denotes the boundary of
E
. This claim implies that
\nu(\partial\mathtt{Q})=0
, thus confirming that
\nu(\mathtt{Q})>0
, and says that for all intents and purposes the measures
\vartheta_{q}
(and
\overline{\vartheta}_{q}
) have pairwise disjoint supports. To prove (
8.12
), fix
q\in\operatorname{ch}(\mathtt{Q})
and let
J
be such that
q\in\mathcal{D}_{J}
. For each
j\geq J
, let

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}_{j}(q)\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\{Q\in\mathcal{D}_{j}\colon Q\cap q\neq\emptyset\}.

By the Frostman-type condition in (
8.10
), we have

(8.13)

\displaystyle\vartheta_{q}(\partial q)\leq\#\mathcal{Q}_{j}(q)2^{-js}.

We can estimate
\#\mathcal{Q}_{j}(q)
as follows: For each
i\in\{1,\ldots,d\}
, the boundary of
q
contains two
(d-1)
-dimensional faces with side-lengths
2^{-n_{1}J},\ldots,[2^{-n_{i}J}],\ldots,2^{-n_{d}J}
, where the term in brackets is omitted. Together, these account for all of the faces of
\partial q
. The two faces corresponding to
i
each intersect exactly

\displaystyle\frac{\prod_{k\neq i}2^{-n_{k}J}}{\prod_{k\neq i}2^{-n_{k}j}}=2^{(\mathtt{S}-n_{i})(j-J)}

boxes in
\mathcal{D}_{j}
. Hence,

(8.14)

\displaystyle\#\mathcal{Q}_{j}(q)\leq 2\sum_{i=1}^{d}2^{(\mathtt{S}-n_{i})(j-J)}\leq 2d2^{(\mathtt{S}-1)(j-J)}.

Combining (
8.13
) and (
8.14
), we get

\displaystyle\vartheta_{q}(\partial q)\leq 2d2^{-(\mathtt{S}-1)J}2^{j(\mathtt{S}-1-s)}.

By our choice of
s
in (
8.6
) and
\varepsilon
in (
8.5
) (specifically, that
\mathtt{N}\varepsilon\leq 1
), we have
\mathtt{S}-1-s<0
. Thus, sending
j\rightarrow\infty
yields (
8.12
).

8.2.4.
Verification of the energy condition

It remains to show that the blow-up
\mu\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\nu^{\mathtt{Q}}
satisfies the energy and spectral gap conditions in (
2.17
). We begin with the former, namely that
I_{\sigma_{\mathtt{N}}}(\mu)\leq\mathtt{C}
, with
\sigma_{\mathtt{N}}
and
\mathtt{C}
as defined in (
2.15
) and (
8.2
), respectively. By our choice of
s
in (
8.6
) and
\varepsilon
in (
8.5
), we have

\displaystyle s-\sigma_{\mathtt{N}}-\mathtt{S}+d\geq-\mathtt{N}\varepsilon+\frac{1}{2\mathtt{N}}\geq-\frac{1}{4\mathtt{N}}+\frac{1}{2\mathtt{N}}=\frac{1}{4\mathtt{N}}.

Therefore, by Lemma
8.4
, it suffices to show that
\mu
obeys the Frostman condition

(8.15)

\displaystyle\mu(Q)\leq 4\ell(Q)^{s}\quad\text{for every }Q\in\mathcal{D}^{*}.

For each
q\in\operatorname{ch}(\mathtt{Q})
, we use (
8.6
) and the first property in (
8.10
) to get that

(8.16)

\displaystyle w(q)\ell(\mathtt{Q})^{s}\leq\|\varphi\|_{\infty}|{\bf T}_{\mathtt{Q}}(q)|\ell(\mathtt{Q})^{s}

\displaystyle\leq 2\Big(\frac{\ell(q)}{\ell(\mathtt{Q})}\Big)^{\mathtt{S}}\ell(\mathtt{Q})^{s}

\displaystyle=2\Big(\frac{\ell(q)}{\ell(\mathtt{Q})}\Big)^{\mathtt{S}-s}\ell(q)^{s}\leq 2\ell(q)^{s}\leq 4\|\vartheta_{q}\|.

Consequently, by the second property in (
8.10
), each
\overline{\vartheta}_{q}
obeys

\displaystyle\overline{\vartheta}_{q}(Q)\leq 4\vartheta_{q}(Q)\leq 4\ell(Q)^{s}\quad\text{for every }Q\in\mathtt{\mathcal{D}}^{*}.

We claim that this property implies condition (
8.15
) with
\nu
in place of
\mu
, i.e.

(8.17)

\displaystyle\nu(Q)\leq 4\ell(Q)^{s}\quad\text{for every }Q\in\mathcal{D}^{*}.

To see this, fix
Q\in\mathcal{D}^{*}
and consider three cases:

•

If
\ell(Q)\leq 2^{-\mathtt{T}}\ell(\mathtt{Q})
, then
Q
intersects at most one box in
\operatorname{ch}(\mathtt{Q})
; thus

\displaystyle\nu(Q)\leq\max_{q\in\operatorname{ch}(\mathtt{Q})}\overline{\vartheta}_{q}(Q)\leq 4\ell(Q)^{s}.

•

If
\ell(Q)\geq\ell(\mathtt{Q})
, then (
8.11
) implies that

\displaystyle\nu(Q)\leq\|\nu\|=\ell(\mathtt{Q})^{s}\leq\ell(Q)^{s}.

•

If
2^{-\mathtt{T}}\ell(\mathtt{Q})\leq\ell(Q)\leq\ell(\mathtt{Q})
, then using the first line of (
8.16
), as well as (
8.6
), we get

\displaystyle\nu(Q)

\displaystyle\leq\#\{q\in\operatorname{ch}(\mathtt{Q})\colon q\cap Q\neq\emptyset\}\max_{q\in\operatorname{ch}(\mathtt{Q})}\|\overline{\vartheta}_{q}\|

\displaystyle=\Big(\frac{\ell(Q)}{2^{-\mathtt{T}}\ell(\mathtt{Q})}\Big)^{\mathtt{S}}\max_{q\in\operatorname{ch}(\mathtt{Q})}w(q)\ell(\mathtt{Q})^{s}

\displaystyle\leq\Big(\frac{\ell(Q)}{2^{-\mathtt{T}}\ell(\mathtt{Q})}\Big)^{\mathtt{S}}2^{-\mathtt{S}\mathtt{T}+1}\ell(\mathtt{Q})^{s}=2\Big(\frac{\ell(Q)}{\ell(\mathtt{Q})}\Big)^{\mathtt{S}-s}\ell(Q)^{s}\leq 2\ell(Q)^{s}.

Collectively, these imply (
8.17
). Now, in order to verify (
8.15
), we need one more simple fact regarding dyadic boxes, namely

\displaystyle{\bf T}_{Q}(Q^{\prime})\in\mathcal{D}^{*}\quad\text{with}\quad\ell({\bf T}_{Q}(Q^{\prime}))=\frac{\ell(Q^{\prime})}{\ell(Q)}

and (equivalently)

\displaystyle{\bf T}_{Q}^{-1}(Q^{\prime})\in\mathcal{D}^{*}\quad\text{with}\quad\ell({\bf T}_{Q}^{-1}(Q^{\prime}))=\ell(Q)\ell(Q^{\prime})\quad\quad\text{for all }Q,Q^{\prime}\in\mathcal{D}^{*}.

Combining this observation with the definition of blow-up in (
2.23
) and (
2.24
), as well as with (
8.11
) and (
8.17
), we obtain

\displaystyle\mu(Q)=\frac{\nu({\bf T}_{\mathtt{Q}}^{-1}(Q))}{\|\nu\|}\leq\ell(\mathtt{Q})^{-s}4\ell({\bf T}_{\mathtt{Q}}^{-1}(Q))^{s}=4\ell(Q)^{s}

for every
Q\in\mathcal{D}^{*}
, confirming (
8.15
).

8.2.5.
Verification of the spectral gap condition

Finally, we need to prove that
\mu
satisfies the spectral gap condition in (
2.17
). Let
\mathcal{D}_{\mathtt{T},0}
denote the set of
\mathtt{T}^{\mathrm{th}}
-generation descendants of
[0,1)^{d}
in
\mathcal{D}^{*}
; that is,

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{\mathtt{T},0}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\{Q\in\mathcal{D}_{\mathtt{T}}\colon Q\subseteq[0,1)^{d}\}=\{{\bf T}_{\mathtt{Q}}(q)\colon q\in\operatorname{ch}(\mathtt{Q})\}.

We have

(8.18)

\displaystyle\mu(Q)=\frac{\nu({\bf T}_{\mathtt{Q}}^{-1}(Q))}{\|\nu\|}=\ell(\mathtt{Q})^{-s}\|\overline{\vartheta}_{{\bf T}_{\mathtt{Q}}^{-1}(Q)}\|=w({\bf T}_{\mathtt{Q}}^{-1}(Q))=\int_{Q}\varphi\quad\text{for every }Q\in\mathcal{D}_{\mathtt{T},0}.

We will treat
\varphi
as a measure via the formula

\displaystyle\int fd\varphi\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\int f\varphi;

thus (
8.18
) becomes
\mu(Q)=\varphi(Q)
. Let
c_{Q}
denote the centre of the box
Q
. If
Q\in\mathcal{D}_{\mathtt{T},0}
, then

\displaystyle\int_{Q}e^{-2\pi ic_{Q}\cdot\xi}d\mu(x)=\int_{Q}e^{-2\pi ic_{Q}\cdot\xi}d\varphi(x)

for any
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. For fixed
\xi
, the function
x\mapsto e^{-2\pi ix\cdot\xi}
is Lipschitz with constant at most
2\pi|\xi|
. Since
|x-c_{Q}|\leq\sqrt{d}\ell(Q)
for
x\in Q
, it follows that

\displaystyle|\widehat{\mu}(\xi)-\widehat{\varphi}(\xi)|

\displaystyle=\Big|\int e^{-2\pi ix\cdot\xi}d\mu(x)-\int e^{-2\pi ix\cdot\xi}d\varphi(x)\Big|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{Q\in\mathcal{D}_{\mathtt{T},0}}\Big|\int_{Q}e^{-2\pi ix\cdot\xi}d\mu(x)-\int_{Q}e^{-2\pi ix\cdot\xi}d\varphi(x)\Big|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{Q\in\mathcal{D}_{\mathtt{T},0}}\Big(\int_{Q}|e^{-2\pi ix\cdot\xi}-e^{-2\pi ic_{Q}\cdot\xi}|d\mu(x)+\int_{Q}|e^{-2\pi ix\cdot\xi}-e^{-2\pi ic_{Q}\cdot\xi}|d\varphi(x)\Big)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{Q\in\mathcal{D}_{\mathtt{T},0}}2\pi|\xi|\sqrt{d}2^{-\mathtt{T}}(\mu(Q)+\varphi(Q))=4\pi\sqrt{d}|\xi|2^{-\mathtt{T}}.

Now, using that
\|\widehat{\mu}\|\leq 1
, as well as our assumptions on
\mathtt{A}
and
\mathtt{T}
in (
8.3
) and (
8.4
), we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{|\xi|\in[\mathtt{A},\mathtt{B}]}|\widehat{\mu}(\xi)|^{2}d\xi\leq\int_{|\xi|\in[\mathtt{A},\mathtt{B}]}|\widehat{\mu}(\xi)|d\xi

\displaystyle\leq\int_{|\xi|\in[\mathtt{A},\mathtt{B}]}|\widehat{\mu}(\xi)-\widehat{\varphi}(\xi)|d\xi+\int_{|\xi|\in[\mathtt{A},\mathtt{B}]}|\widehat{\varphi}(\xi)|d\xi

\displaystyle\leq 4\pi\sqrt{d}2^{-\mathtt{T}}\int_{|\xi|\in[\mathtt{A},\mathtt{B}]}|\xi|d\xi+\frac{1}{2}\mathtt{A}^{-4d}

\displaystyle\leq 4\pi\sqrt{d}2^{-\mathtt{T}}\mathtt{B}|B(0;\mathtt{B})|+\frac{1}{2}\mathtt{A}^{-4d}\leq\mathtt{A}^{-4d},

which completes the proof. ∎

9.
Anisotropic boxes and Hausdorff dimension in metric spaces

In the previous section, we defined the “Hausdorff content”
\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{D}^{*}}^{s}
associated to a collection
\mathcal{D}^{*}
of anisotropic dyadic boxes in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. It was used in the following way: Starting with a set
K\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
of very high Hausdorff dimension, we located a box
\mathtt{Q}\in\mathcal{D}^{*}
such that
K
had nontrivial (anisotropic) Hausdorff content within each descendant of
\mathtt{Q}
of a certain generation. This enabled us to use a version of Frostman’s lemma to construct a measure on
K\cap\overline{\mathtt{Q}}
whose blow-up satisfied both conditions in (
2.17
). In this section, we reinterpret
\mathcal{D}^{*}
and
\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{D}^{*}}^{s}
in terms of Hausdorff dimension in metric spaces; see
[
33
, Chapter 4]
. This connection was first stated in
[
26
]
, though in less generality.

Let
\vec{n}=(n_{1},\ldots,n_{d})
be a fixed vector of positive integers, and let
\rho=\rho[{\vec{n}}]
be the metric on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
given by

\displaystyle\rho(x,y)\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\max_{1\leq i\leq d}(2|x_{i}-y_{i}|)^{1/n_{i}}.

The closed
\rho
-ball of radius
r
centred at
x
takes the form

\displaystyle B_{\rho}(x;r)\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\{y\colon\rho(x,y)\leq r\}=x+\prod_{i=1}^{d}\Big[-\frac{r^{n_{i}}}{2},\frac{r^{n_{i}}}{2}\Big].

Elements of
\mathcal{D}^{*}[\vec{n}]
can therefore be viewed as
\rho
-balls. More precisely, if
Q\in\mathcal{D}^{*}
and
c_{Q}
is the centre of
Q
, then

\displaystyle\overline{Q}=c_{Q}+\prod_{i=1}^{d}\Big[-\frac{\ell(Q)^{n_{i}}}{2},\frac{\ell(Q)^{n_{i}}}{2}\Big]=B_{\rho}(c_{Q},\ell(Q)).

The
s
-dimensional Hausdorff measure
\mathscr{H}^{s}=\mathscr{H}^{s}[\rho]
is defined for the metric space
(\mathbb{R}^{d},\rho)
in the usual way, namely

\displaystyle\mathscr{H}^{s}(E)\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\lim_{\delta\searrow 0}\mathscr{H}_{\delta}^{s}(E),

where

\displaystyle\mathscr{H}_{\delta}^{s}(E)\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\inf\Big\{\sum_{U\in\mathscr{U}}\operatorname{diam}(U)^{s}\colon\mathscr{U}\text{ is a countable cover of $E$, }\sup_{U\in\mathscr{U}}\operatorname{diam}U\leq\delta\Big\}.

Of course, here
\operatorname{diam}U
refers to the
\rho
-diameter of
U
. If
U
is a
\rho
-ball, then this is
2^{1/\min_{i}n_{i}}
times its radius. The quantities
\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{D}^{*}}^{s}
and
\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{D}_{J}^{*}}^{s}
defined in the previous section can be viewed as discrete versions of
\mathscr{H}_{\infty}^{s}
and
\mathscr{H}_{2^{-J}}^{s}
, respectively.

Lemma
8.1
provided a connection between
\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{D}^{*}}^{s}
and the standard (Euclidean) Hausdorff dimension; namely, if
s
is not too large relative to
\dim_{\mathrm{H}}E
, then
\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{D}^{*}}^{s}(E)>0
. We get a more complete picture by considering Hausdorff dimension in
(\mathbb{R}^{d},\rho)
. This is again defined in the usual way:

\displaystyle\dim_{\mathrm{H}^{*}}E\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\inf\{s\colon\mathscr{H}^{s}(E)=0\}=\sup\{s\colon\mathscr{H}^{s}(E)=\infty\}.

If
\mathtt{N}
and
\mathtt{S}
are defined as in (
8.1
), then we have the relations

(9.1)

\displaystyle\mathtt{S}-(d-\dim_{\mathrm{H}}E)\mathtt{N}\leq\dim_{\mathrm{H}^{*}}E\leq\mathtt{S}

for all sets
E\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
. The proof of (
9.1
) is similar to that of Lemma
8.1
(appearing in the Appendix); in particular, the first inequality follows by establishing that
\mathscr{H}_{\infty}^{s}(E)>0
for all
s<\mathtt{S}-(d-\dim_{\mathrm{H}}E)\mathtt{N}
. It is worth noting that when
\vec{n}=(1,1,\ldots,1)
, the corresponding metric
\rho
is a multiple of the Euclidean sup norm, and the balls it defines are just Euclidean cubes. Consequently, the definition of Hausdorff dimension in

agrees with the usual one, and (

10.1.
Standardization of a function of finite type

Proof of Lemma
2.1
.

Let
\Theta\colon\mathtt{I}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a smooth function that is vanishing of type
\mathtt{N}
at the origin. This means that
\mathtt{N}
is the smallest integer with the following property:

(10.1)

\text{For every }u\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\{0\}\text{ there exists }n\in\{1,\ldots,\mathtt{N}\}\text{ such that }u\cdot\Theta^{(n)}(0)\neq 0.

Since
\Theta(0)=0
, there exists a unique
d
-tuple
\vec{\mathtt{m}}=(\mathtt{m}_{1},\ldots,\mathtt{m}_{d})
of positive integers such that

(10.2)

\displaystyle\Theta=(\Theta_{1},\ldots,\Theta_{d})\quad\text{with}\quad\Theta_{i}(t)=t^{\mathtt{m}_{i}}\theta_{i}(t)

for some smooth functions
\theta_{i}\colon\mathtt{I}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
obeying
\theta_{i}(0)\neq 0
.
We will refer to
\vec{\mathtt{m}}
as the
vanishing pattern
of
\Theta
. Our goal is to produce an invertible linear map
{\bf L}\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that the vanishing pattern
\vec{\mathtt{n}}
of
{\bf L}\circ\Theta
satisfies
\mathtt{n}_{1}<\mathtt{n}_{2}<\cdots<\mathtt{n}_{d}=\mathtt{N}
. Once we have this, we can apply a diagonal map to
{\bf L}\circ\Theta
to achieve the condition
\phi_{1}(0)=\cdots=\phi_{d}(0)=1
; such a map leaves the vanishing pattern unchanged. This would establish both requirements (
2.3
) and (
2.4
). Definition (
10.1
) ensures that the type of a function is preserved under invertible linear transformations; i.e.,
{\bf L}\circ\Theta
remains vanishing of type
\mathtt{N}
at the origin for any invertible linear transformation
{\bf L}
. In view of this, we will outline a sequence of such transformations, relabelling the transformed function
\Theta
at each stage, until we reach a function
\Theta
that obeys (
2.2
), (
2.3
), and (
2.4
). The map
{\bf L}
in the statement of Lemma
2.1
is the composition of the maps used to reach this final
\Theta
.

Our first task is to create a map
{\bf L}
such that
\mathtt{N}
is present in the vanishing pattern of
{\bf L}\circ\Theta
. From (
10.1
), we know that there exists a nonzero vector
\mathtt{u}=(\mathtt{u}_{1},\ldots,\mathtt{u}_{d})
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that

\mathtt{u}\cdot\Theta^{(n)}(0)=0\quad\text{for $1\leq n\leq\mathtt{N}-1$}\quad\quad\text{and}\quad\quad\mathtt{u}\cdot\Theta^{(\mathtt{N})}(0)\neq 0.

We may assume without loss of generality that
\mathtt{u}_{d}\neq 0
. Let
{\bf L}
be the linear map given by

{\bf L}(x)\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d-1},\mathtt{u}\cdot x)\quad\text{ for }x=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

Then
{\bf L}\circ\Theta=:(\widetilde{\Theta}_{1},\ldots,\widetilde{\Theta}_{d})
obeys

\widetilde{\Theta}_{i}(t)=\begin{cases}\Theta_{i}(t)=t^{\mathtt{m}_{i}}{\theta}_{i}(t)&\text{ if }1\leq i<d,\\
\mathtt{u}\cdot\Theta(t)=t^{\mathtt{N}}\widetilde{\theta}_{d}(t)&\text{ if }i=d\end{cases}

for some smooth function
\widetilde{\theta}_{d}
such that
\widetilde{\theta}_{d}(0)\neq 0
.
Thus,
\mathtt{N}
appears in the vanishing pattern of
{\bf L}\circ\Theta
and is necessarily its largest entry. We relabel
{\bf L}\circ\Theta
as
\Theta
and assume it takes the form (
10.2
), with
\max_{i}\mathtt{m}_{i}=\mathtt{N}
. The value of
\max_{i}\mathtt{m}_{i}
will never decrease in the sequence of transformations applied hereafter.

It remains to ensure that the entries of the vanishing pattern of
\Theta
can be made to obey the strict monotonicity in (
2.3
) after a further linear transformation
{\bf L}
. If these entries are already distinct, then the construction of
{\bf L}
is easy; we can take
{\bf L}
to be a suitable permutation map. However, in general the vanishing pattern may have repeated entries, and such coincidences have to be eliminated. With that goal in mind, we claim the following:

Let
\Theta
be a function with vanishing pattern
\vec{\mathtt{m}}
. If there exist indices
i_{0},i_{1}\in\{1,\ldots,d\}
with
i_{0}<i_{1}
such that
\mathtt{m}_{i_{0}}=\mathtt{m}_{i_{1}}
, then there exists an invertible linear map
{\bf L}
such that
{\bf L}\circ\Theta
has vanishing pattern
\vec{\mathtt{m}}+\ell{\mathtt{e}}_{i_{1}}
for some
\ell\geq 1
.

(Here,
\mathtt{e}_{1},\ldots,\mathtt{e}_{d}
denote the canonical basis vectors of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.) In other words, if the vanishing pattern of
\Theta
has a repeated value, then there exists a linear map
{\bf L}
such that the vanishing pattern of
{\bf L}\circ\Theta
is the same, except for one of the repeated entries having increased. For now, assume this claim holds. By applying the claim iteratively, we can remove any coincidences in the vanishing pattern of
\Theta
, as follows. First, set
i_{0}=1
. For every
j>1
, we check whether
\mathtt{m}_{1}=\mathtt{m}_{j}
. If not, then no action is needed. If the equality holds, we apply the claim with
i_{1}=j
(and
i_{0}=1
) to get a function whose vanishing pattern has distinct values in the first and the
j^{\text{th}}
entries; the other entries have not changed. At the end of
d-1
such checks with indices
j=2,3,\ldots,d
, we obtain a function with the property that the first entry
\mathtt{m}_{1}
of its vanishing pattern is never repeated among the later entries. This concludes the first step. Next, we repeat this process with
i_{0}=2
and
j>2
. This produces a function with a vanishing pattern in which neither of the first two entries appears among the later ones. Proceeding in this way, we reach after
d-1
steps a function whose vanishing pattern has no repeated values. Finally, we apply a permutation map to rearrange the pattern into increasing order, as suggested above. Thus, we have constructed
{\bf L}
as the composition of the linear maps behind our applications of the claim, together with the final permutation.

We are left to prove the claim. Fix the indices
i_{0}<i_{1}
with
\mathtt{m}_{i_{0}}=\mathtt{m}_{i_{1}}
, as specified there. Assuming
\Theta
takes the form (
10.2
), we define the linear map
{\bf L}
as follows:

\displaystyle{\bf L}(x)\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}y,\quad\text{where}\quad y_{i}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\begin{cases}x_{i}&\text{if }i\neq i_{1},\\
x_{i}-\frac{\theta_{i_{1}}(0)}{\theta_{i_{0}}(0)}x_{i_{0}}&\text{if }i=i_{1}.\end{cases}

The transformed function
{\bf L}\circ\Theta=:(\widetilde{\Theta}_{1},\ldots,\widetilde{\Theta}_{d})
satisfies
\widetilde{\Theta}_{i}(t)=t^{\mathtt{m}_{i}}\widetilde{\theta}_{i}(t)
for

\displaystyle\widetilde{\theta}_{i}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\begin{cases}\theta_{i}&\text{if }i\neq i_{1},\\
\theta_{i_{1}}-\frac{\theta_{i_{1}}(0)}{\theta_{i_{0}}(0)}\theta_{i_{0}}&\text{if }i=i_{1}.\end{cases}

Now, on one hand,
\widetilde{\theta}_{i_{1}}(0)=0
. On the other hand,
t^{\mathtt{m}_{i_{1}}}\widetilde{\theta}_{i_{1}}(t)=\widetilde{\Theta}_{i_{1}}(t)=\mathtt{u}\cdot\Theta(t)
for some nonzero vector
\mathtt{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and the finite type hypothesis (
10.1
) implies the existence of an integer
\mathtt{n}\in\{1,\ldots,\mathtt{N}\}
such that
\mathtt{u}\cdot\Theta(t)=t^{\mathtt{n}}\theta_{\mathtt{u}}(t)
for some smooth function
\theta_{\mathtt{u}}
with
\theta_{\mathtt{u}}(0)\neq 0
. Equating
t^{\mathtt{m}_{i_{1}}}\widetilde{\theta}_{i_{1}}(t)
and
t^{\mathtt{n}}\theta_{\mathtt{u}}(t)
and differentiating
\mathtt{n}
times at the origin shows that
\mathtt{n}>\mathtt{m}_{i_{1}}
. Setting
\ell\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\mathtt{n}-\mathtt{m}_{i_{1}}
, it follows that
{\bf L}\circ\Theta
has vanishing pattern
\vec{\mathtt{m}}+\ell{\mathtt{e}}_{i_{1}}
, and so the claim is proved.
∎

Anisotropic boxes and Hausdorff dimension

10.3.1.
Proof of Lemma
8.1

For
t\geq 0
, let
\mathscr{H}^{t}
denote the standard
t
-dimensional Hausdorff measure on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, defined by

\displaystyle\mathscr{H}^{t}(A)\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\lim_{\delta\searrow 0}\mathscr{H}_{\delta}^{t}(A),

\displaystyle\mathscr{H}_{\delta}^{t}(A)\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\inf\Big\{\sum_{U\in\mathscr{U}}\operatorname{diam}(U)^{t}\colon\mathscr{U}\text{ is a countable cover of $A$, }\sup_{U\in\mathscr{U}}\operatorname{diam}U\leq\delta\Big\}.

(10.3)

\displaystyle\dim_{\mathrm{H}}A=\inf\{t\colon\mathscr{H}^{t}(A)=0\}=\sup\{t\colon\mathscr{H}^{t}(A)=\infty\};

see
[
34

. Now, fix
E\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
s\in[0,\mathtt{S}-(d-\dim_{\mathrm{H}}E)\mathtt{N})

\mathtt{S}
and
\mathtt{N}
in (
8.1
) imply that
\mathtt{S}\leq d\mathtt{N}
. It follows that

\displaystyle s=\mathtt{S}-(d-\alpha)\mathtt{N}

for some
\alpha\in[0,\dim_{\mathrm{H}}E)
. By (
10.3
), we have
\mathscr{H}^{\alpha}(E)=\infty
, and thus there exists some
\delta\in(0,1]
such that
\mathscr{H}_{\delta}^{\alpha}(E)>0
. Let
\mathcal{Q}\subseteq\mathcal{D}^{*}
be an arbitrary cover of

. If there exists some

such that
\ell(Q_{0})^{\mathtt{N}}\geq\delta/\sqrt{d}
, then

\displaystyle\sum_{Q\in\mathcal{Q}}\ell(Q)^{s}\geq\ell(Q_{0})^{s}\geq\Big(\frac{\delta}{\sqrt{d}}\Big)^{s/\mathtt{N}}.

Suppose instead that every

\displaystyle\mathscr{C}(\mathcal{Q})\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\bigcup_{Q\in\mathcal{Q}}\mathscr{C}(Q)

\displaystyle\sum_{Q\in\mathcal{Q}}\ell(Q)^{s}=\sum_{Q\in\mathcal{Q}}\ell(Q)^{\mathtt{S}-d\mathtt{N}}\ell(Q)^{\alpha\mathtt{N}}=\sum_{C\in\mathscr{C}(\mathcal{Q})}\operatorname{diam}(C)^{\alpha}\geq\mathscr{H}_{\delta}^{\alpha}(E).

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{D}^{*}}^{s}(E)\geq\min\Big\{\Big(\frac{\delta}{\sqrt{d}}\Big)^{s/\mathtt{N}},\mathscr{H}_{\delta}^{\alpha}(E)\Big\}>0,

10.3.3.
Proof of Lemma
8.3

Our argument follows the proof of the standard version of Frostman’s lemma,
[
33
, Theorem 8.8]
, with minor adjustments. There, the idea is to create a sequence of measures
\{\vartheta^{j}\}_{j}
, with
\vartheta^{j}
obeying the required ball condition on balls of diameter at least
2^{-j}
, and then take a weak limit. The main distinction here is that the dyadic cubes used in Frostman’s proof are replaced by the anisotropic boxes in
\mathcal{D}^{*}
.

Fix a compact set
E\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
s\geq 0
. By translation, we may assume that
E
is contained in some element of
\mathcal{D}_{J}
for some
J
. For each
j\geq J
, define a measure
\vartheta_{j}^{j}
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
by specifying that

\displaystyle\vartheta_{j}^{j}|_{Q}=\begin{cases}0&\text{if }E\cap Q=\emptyset\\
\frac{\ell(Q)^{s}}{\lambda(Q)}\lambda|_{Q}&\text{if }E\cap Q\neq\emptyset\end{cases}\quad\quad\text{for each }Q\in\mathcal{D}_{j},

where
\lambda
denotes
d

\vartheta_{j}^{j},\vartheta_{j-1}^{j},\ldots,\vartheta_{j-k}^{j}
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
have been constructed, with

\displaystyle\vartheta_{j-k-1}^{j}|_{Q}=\begin{cases}\vartheta_{j-k}^{j}|_{Q}&\text{if }\vartheta_{j-k}^{j}(Q)\leq\ell(Q)^{s}\\

Let

\displaystyle j^{*}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\max\{j^{\prime}\leq j\colon E\subset Q\text{ for some }Q\in\mathcal{D}_{j^{\prime}}\},

and define

(noting that

). Let

be such that

. The following are consequences of the construction of

1.

\operatorname{supp}\vartheta^{j}\subseteq\bigcup\{Q\in\mathcal{D}_{j}\colon E\cap Q\neq\emptyset\}\subseteq Q_{j^{*}}

2.

with

\displaystyle\sup_{j\geq J}\|\vartheta^{j}\|\leq\sup_{j\geq J}\ell(Q_{j^{*}})^{s}\leq 2^{-Js},

and consequently

has a weakly convergent subsequence. Let

be the weak limit along this subsequence.

We will show that this measure satisfies the conclusions of the lemma. Statement 1 and the hypothesis that

is compact imply that
\vartheta
is supported in
E
. Fix
Q\in\mathcal{D}^{*}
. If
\ell(Q)\geq 2^{-J}
, then (
10.4
) implies that
\vartheta(Q)\leq\|\vartheta\|\leq 2^{-Js}\leq\ell(Q)^{s}
, while if
\ell(Q)\leq 2^{-J}

. Using statement 3, we select for each

the largest

such that

and

. Let

denote the collection of these elements. Then

covers both

, and distinct elements of
\mathcal{Q}_{j}
are disjoint. It follows that

\displaystyle\|\vartheta^{j}\|=\sum_{Q\in\mathcal{Q}_{j}}\vartheta^{j}(Q)=\sum_{Q\in\mathcal{Q}_{j}}\ell(Q)^{s}\geq\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{D}^{*}}^{s}(E).

Since
j
was arbitrary, we may conclude that
\|\vartheta\|\geq\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{D}^{*}}^{s}(E)
. ∎

For a fixed cube
C
, there are at most
6^{d}
cubes
C^{\prime}
such that
C\sim C^{\prime}
.

It follows from property 1 that

\displaystyle\Omega\times\Omega\setminus\Delta=\bigcup_{j=0}^{\infty}\bigcup_{\begin{subarray}{c}(C,C^{\prime})\in\mathcal{C}_{j}\times\mathcal{C}_{j}\colon\\
C\sim C^{\prime}\end{subarray}}C\times C^{\prime}.

Thus, using properties 2 and 3, together with (
10.5
) and our hypothesis on
s
, we obtain

\displaystyle I_{\sigma}(\vartheta)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(C,C^{\prime})\in\mathcal{C}_{j}\times\mathcal{C}_{j}\colon\\
C\sim C^{\prime}\end{subarray}}\iint_{C\times C^{\prime}}|x-y|^{-\sigma}d\vartheta(x)d\vartheta(y)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\sum_{C\in\mathcal{C}_{j}}\sum_{C^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}_{j}\colon C\sim C^{\prime}}2^{j\sigma}\vartheta(C)\vartheta(C^{\prime})

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}2^{j\sigma}\sum_{C\in\mathcal{C}_{j}}\vartheta(C)\#\{C^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}_{j}\colon C\sim C^{\prime}\}\max_{C^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}_{j}}\vartheta(C^{\prime})

\displaystyle\leq 6^{d}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}2^{j\sigma}\Big(\sum_{C\in\mathcal{C}_{j}}\vartheta(C)\Big)\max_{C^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}_{j}}\#\{Q\in\mathcal{D}_{j}\colon Q\cap C^{\prime}\neq\emptyset\}L2^{-js}

\displaystyle=6^{d}L\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}2^{j\sigma}\|\vartheta\|2^{j(\mathtt{S}-d)}2^{-js}\leq 6^{d}L\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}2^{j(\sigma+\mathtt{S}-d-s)}=\frac{6^{d}L}{1-2^{\sigma+\mathtt{S}-d-s}}.

Since
\vartheta
was arbitrary, we have shown that the lemma holds with

\displaystyle\mathtt{E}(t)\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\frac{6^{d}}{1-2^{-t}},

and the proof is complete. ∎
