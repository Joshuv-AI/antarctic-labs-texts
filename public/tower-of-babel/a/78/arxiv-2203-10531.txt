Approximation and interpolation of singular measures by trigonometric polynomials
Paul Catala, Mathias Hockmann, Stefan Kunis, Markus Wageringel

Abstract
Complex signed measures of finite total variation are a powerful signal model in many applications. Restricting to the d d -dimensional torus, finitely supported measures allow for exact recovery if the trigonometric moments up to some order are known. Here, we consider the approximation of general measures, e.g., supported on a curve, by trigonometric polynomials of fixed degree with respect to the Wasserstein-1 distance. We prove sharp lower bounds for their best approximation and (almost) matching upper bounds for effectively computable approximations when the trigonometric moments of the measure are known. A second class of sum of squares polynomials is shown to interpolate the characteristic function on the support of the measure and to converge to zero outside. Key words and phrases : frequency analysis, super resolution. 2010 AMS Mathematics Subject Classification : 65T40, 42A15.

Introduction

Data science in general and more specifically signal and image processing relies on mathematical methods, with the fast Fourier transform as the most prominent example.
Besides its favourable computational complexity, its success relies on the good approximation of smooth functions by trigonometric polynomials.
Mainly driven by specific applications, functions with additional properties together with their computational schemes have gained some attention: signals might for instance be sparse like in single molecule fluorescence microscopy
[
30
]
, or live on some other lower dimensional structure
like microfilaments, again in bio-imaging. Such properties are well modeled by measures, which can express the underlying structure through the geometry of their support,
e.g.
being discrete or singular continuous. This representation has in particular led to a better understanding of the sparse super-resolution problem
[
5
,
8
,
11
]
, but has also proven useful in many more applications, such as phase retrieval in X-ray crystallography, or contour reconstruction in natural images. In this work, we consider measures supported on the torus. The available data then consists in trigonometric moments of low to moderate order, and one asks for the reconstruction or approximation of the measures.

1
Preliminaries

Let
d\in\mathbb{N}
,
1\leq p\leq\infty
and let
|x-y|_{p}=\min_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\left\|x-y+k\right\|_{p}
denote the wrap-around
p
-norm on
\mathbb{T}^{d}=[0,1)^{d}
. For
d=1
these wrap-around distances coincide and we denote them by
|x-y|_{1}
to distinguish from the absolute value.
Throughout this paper, let
\mu,\nu
denote some complex Borel measures on
\mathbb{T}^{d}
with finite total variation and normalization
\mu(\mathbb{T}^{d})=\nu(\mathbb{T}^{d})=1
.
We denote the set of all such measures by
\mathcal{M}
and restrict to the real signed and non-negative case by
\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{R}}
and
\mathcal{M}_{+}
, respectively.

A function has Lipschitz-constant at most
1
if
|f(x)-f(y)|\leq|x-y|_{1}
for all
x,y\in\mathbb{T}^{d}
and we denote this by the shorthand
\Lip(f)\leq 1
.
Using the dual characterisation by Kantorovich-Rubinstein, the Wasserstein-1-distance of
\mu
and
\nu
is defined by

\displaystyle W_{1}(\nu,\mu)=\sup_{\Lip(f)\leq 1}\left|\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}f(x)\;\mathrm{d}(\nu-\mu)\left(x\right)\right|,

for any
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{M}
, and
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{M}_{+}
also admit the primal formulation

\displaystyle W_{1}(\nu,\mu)=\inf_{\pi}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2d}}|x-y|_{1}\mathrm{d}\pi(x,y)

where the infimum is taken over all couplings
\pi
with marginals
\mu
and
\nu
, respectively. We note in passing that the Wasserstein-1-distances for other
p
norms on
\mathbb{T}^{d}
are equivalent with lower and upper constant
1
and
d^{1-1/p}
, respectively.
Moreover, the Wasserstein distance defines a metric induced by the norm

\displaystyle\|\mu\|_{\Lip^{*}}=\sup_{f:\Lip(f)\leq 1,\|f\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{d}{2}}\left|\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}f(x)d\mu(x)\right|

which makes the space of Borel measures with finite total variation a Banach space.
By slight abuse of notation, we also write
W_{1}(p,\mu)
in case the measure
\nu
has density
p
, i.e.,
\mathrm{d}\nu(x)=p(x)\mathrm{d}x
.

The Fourier coefficients or trigonometric moments of
\mu
are given by

\displaystyle\hat{\mu}(k)=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\textnormal{e}^{-2\pi\mathit{i}{kx}}\mathrm{d}\mu(x),\qquad k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d},

and these are finite with
|\hat{\mu}(k)|\leq\|{\mu}\|_{\textnormal{TV}}
and
\hat{\mu}(0)=1
.
We are interested in the reconstruction of the measure given these moments for indices
k\in\{-n,\ldots,n\}^{d}
.
Besides collecting them in a vector, we also set up the finite moment matrix

\displaystyle T_{n}=\left(\hat{\mu}(k-\ell)\right)_{k,\ell\in[n]},\qquad[n]=\{0,\ldots,n\}^{d},

(1.1)

and denote its singular value decomposition by
T_{n}=U_{n}\Sigma_{n}V_{n}^{*}
,
\Sigma_{n}=\operatorname{diag}\big(\sigma_{j}^{(n)}\big)_{j}
. From the data stored in
T_{n}
, we compute trigonometric approximations
q_{n}
to the underlying measure and distinguish between pointwise convergence to the characteristic function of
\operatorname{supp}\mu
, i.e.

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}q_{n}(x)=\begin{cases}1,\quad&x\in\operatorname{supp}\mu,\\
0,\quad&\text{else,}\end{cases}

and weak convergence, i.e.

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}f(x)q_{n}(x)\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}f(x)\mathrm{d}\mu(x)

for all continuous test functions
f\colon\mathbb{T}^{d}\to\mathbb{C}
. The latter is denoted by
q_{n}\rightharpoonup\mu
as
n\to\infty
and the space of test functions can be restricted to Lipschitz continuous test functions by Portmanteau’s theorem. Moreover,
q_{n}\rightharpoonup\mu
is equivalent to
\lim_{n\to\infty}W_{1}(q_{n},\mu)=0
on the bounded set
\mathbb{T}^{d}
and we can quantify rates of weak convergence in terms of the Wasserstein distance (e.g. cf.
[
46
, Thm. 6.9]
).

Theorem 2.2
.

For any
d,n\in\mathbb{N}
and for every
\mu\in\mathcal{M}
there exists a polynomial of best approximation in the Wasserstein-1 distance. Moreover, we have

\displaystyle\sup_{\mu\in\mathcal{M}}\min_{\deg(p)\leq n}W_{1}(p,\mu)\geq\frac{1}{4(n+1)}.

Proof.

We directly have existence of a best approximation by polynomials in the Banach space of Borel measures with finite total variation (e.g. cf.
[
9
, Thm. 3.1.1]
). For the lower bound, we compute

\displaystyle\sup_{\mu\in\mathcal{M}}\inf_{\deg(p)\leq n}W_{1}(p,\mu)

\displaystyle\geq\inf_{\deg(p)\leq n}W_{1}(p,\delta_{0})

\displaystyle=\inf_{\deg(p)\leq n}\sup_{f:\Lip(f)\leq 1}\left|f(0)-\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}f(x)p(x)\mathrm{d}x\right|

\displaystyle=\inf_{\deg(p)\leq n}\sup_{\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{f: \|f\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{d}{2}}{\Lip(f)\leq 1}}\left\|f-\check{p}*f\right\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\geq\sup_{\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{f: \|f\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{d}{2}}{\Lip(f)\leq 1}}\inf_{\deg(p)\leq n}\|f-p\|_{\infty},

where
\check{p}
denotes the reflection of
p
.
It remains to find the worst case error for the best approximation of a Lipschitz function by a trigonometric polynomial. This is well-understood for
d=1
(cf.
[
1
,
14
]
) while we did not find a reference that and how
d>1
is possible as well. Therefore, we show that the idea by
[
15
]
for the case
d=1
works also for
d>1
in our situation. A main ingredient of Fishers proof is the duality relation

\displaystyle\inf_{x\in Y\subset X}\|x_{0}-x\|=\sup_{\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{\ell\in X^*}{\ell|_Y=0, \|\ell\|_{X^*}\leq 1}}|\ell(x_{0})|

for a Banach space
X
,
x_{0}\in X
, with subset
Y
and dual space
X^{*}
. The second ingredient is given by the 1-periodic Bernoulli spline of degree 1

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{1}(x)=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}}\frac{\textnormal{e}^{2\pi\mathit{i}{kx}}}{2\pi\mathit{i}k}=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{\sin(2\pi kx)}{\pi k}=\frac{1}{2}-x

(2.3)

for
0<x\leq 1
. A Lipschitz continuous and 1-periodic function
f\colon\mathbb{T}\to\mathbb{R}
with
\Lip(f)\leq 1
has a derivative
f^{\prime}
almost everywhere and this derivative satisfies
\int_{\mathbb{T}}f^{\prime}(s)=0
by the periodicity of
f
. Therefore, it follows that

\displaystyle\left(f^{\prime}*\mathcal{B}_{1}\right)(t)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{T}}f^{\prime}(s)\mathcal{B}_{1}(t-s)\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle=-\int_{0}^{t}(t-s)f^{\prime}(s)\mathrm{d}s-\int_{t}^{1}(t-s+1)f^{\prime}(s)\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle=f(t)-\int_{0}^{1}f(s)\mathrm{d}s

for
0<t,s\leq 1
. The dual space of the space of continuous periodic functions is the space of periodic finite regular Borel measures equipped with the total variation norm and the duality formulation gives

\displaystyle\sup_{\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{f:\T^d\to\R}{\|f\|_{\infty}, \Lip(f)\leq 1}}\inf_{\deg(p)\leq n}\|f-p\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle=\sup_{\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{f:\T^d\to\R}{\|f\|_{\infty}, \Lip(f)\leq 1}}\sup_{\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{\hat{\mu}(k) =0, \|k\|_{\infty}\leq n}{\TV{\mu}\leq 1}}\left|\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}f(x)\mathrm{d}\mu(x)\right|.

Our main contribution to this result is the observation how to transfer the multivariate setting back to the univariate one. It is easy to verify that
f(x)=\frac{1}{d}\sum_{\ell=1}^{d}f_{0}(x_{\ell})

for a univariate Lipschitz function
f_{0}
,
\Lip(f_{0})\leq d
,
\|f_{0}\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{d}{2}
fulfils the conditions for the Lipschitz function
f
. Additionally,
\mu^{*}=\frac{1}{d}\sum_{s=1}^{d}\mu_{s}
with
\mu_{s}=\left(\bigotimes_{\ell\neq s}\lambda(x_{\ell})\right)\otimes\mu_{0}^{*}(x_{s})
,

\displaystyle\mu_{0}^{*}(x_{s})=\frac{1}{2(n+1)}\sum_{j=0}^{2n+1}(-1)^{j}\delta_{j/(2n+2)}(x_{s})

and
\lambda
being the Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{T}
is admissible. Since this choice of
\mu_{s}
integrates
\int g\mathrm{d}\mu_{s}=0
if
g
is constant with respect to
x_{s}
(and the same holds for constant univariate functions integrated against
\mu_{0}^{*}
), we obtain

\displaystyle\sup_{\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{f:\T^d\to\R}{\|f\|_{\infty}, \Lip(f)\leq 1}}\inf_{\deg(p)\leq n}\|f-p\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\geq\sup_{\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{f_0:\T\to\R}{\|f_0\|_{\infty}\leq 1, \Lip(f_0)\leq d}}\left|\frac{1}{d^{2}}\sum_{s,\ell=1}^{d}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}f_{0}(x_{\ell})\mathrm{d}\mu_{s}(x)\right|

\displaystyle=\sup_{\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{f_0:\T\to\R}{\|f_0\|_{\infty}\leq 1, \Lip(f_0)\leq d}}\left|\frac{1}{d^{2}}\sum_{\ell=1}^{d}\int_{\mathbb{T}}f_{0}(x_{\ell})\mathrm{d}\mu_{0}^{*}(x_{\ell})\right|

\displaystyle=\sup_{\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{f_0:\T\to\R}{\|f_0\|_{\infty}\leq 1, \Lip(f_0)\leq d}}\frac{1}{d}\left|\int_{\mathbb{T}}f_{0}^{\prime}(s)\left(\int_{\mathbb{T}}\mathcal{B}_{1}(t-s)\mathrm{d}\mu_{0}^{*}(t)\right)\mathrm{d}s\right|.

We denote
\mathcal{B}_{\mu*}(s)=\int_{\mathbb{T}}\mathcal{B}_{1}(t-s)\mathrm{d}\mu_{0}^{*}(t)
and observe
\int_{\mathbb{T}}\mathcal{B}_{\mu*}(s)\mathrm{d}s=0
. Moreover,
\mu_{0}^{*}
has moments
\hat{\mu}_{0}^{*}(k)=1
for
k\in(n+1)\left(2\mathbb{Z}+1\right)
and
\hat{\mu}_{0}^{*}(k)=0
otherwise. Together with the Fourier representation (
2.3
) of
\mathcal{B}_{1}
this gives

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{\mu*}(s)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}}\frac{\textnormal{e}^{2\pi\mathit{i}{k(n+1)x}}}{2\pi\mathit{i}k(n+1)}-\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}}\frac{\textnormal{e}^{2\pi\mathit{i}{2k(n+1)x}}}{2\pi\mathit{i}2k(n+1)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n+1}\mathcal{B}_{1}((n+1)s)-\frac{1}{2n+2}\mathcal{B}_{1}((2n+2)s).

Hence, taking
f^{\prime}_{0}(s)=\pm d
depending on the sign of
\mathcal{B}_{\mu*}(s)
is possible as
\|f_{0}\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{d}{2(n+1)}<\frac{d}{2}
with this choice. Finally, we end up with

\displaystyle\sup_{\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{f:\T^d\to\R}{\|f\|_{\infty}, \Lip(f)\leq 1}}\inf_{\deg(p)\leq n}\|f-p\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\geq\int_{\mathbb{T}}\left|\mathcal{B}_{\mu*}(s)\right|\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n+1}\int_{\mathbb{T}}\left|\mathcal{B}_{1}((n+1)s)-\frac{1}{2}\mathcal{B}_{1}((2n+2)s)\right|\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n+1}\int_{\mathbb{T}}\left|\mathcal{B}_{1}(s)-\frac{1}{2}\mathcal{B}_{1}(2s)\right|\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4(n+1)}

and this was the claim.
∎

Let the Fejér kernel
F_{n}\colon\mathbb{T}\to\mathbb{R}
and by slight abuse of notation also its multivariate version be given by

\displaystyle F_{n}(x)=\sum_{k=-n}^{n}\left(1-\frac{|k|}{n+1}\right)\textnormal{e}^{2\pi\mathit{i}{kx}}=\frac{1}{n+1}\left(\frac{\sin(n+1)\pi x}{\sin\pi x}\right)^{2}

and
F_{n}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d})=F_{n}(x_{1})\cdot\ldots\cdot F_{n}(x_{d})
, respectively.
The main object of study now is the approximation

\displaystyle p_{n}(x)

\displaystyle=\left(F_{n}*\mu\right)(x)=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}F_{n}(x-y)\mathrm{d}\mu(y),

(2.4)

two examples are given in
example

2.1
.
We start by noting that the
p_{n}
can be expressed in terms of the moment matrix and preserves non-negativity and normalization.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
d,n\in\mathbb{N}
,
\mu\in\mathcal{M}
, and the moment matrix
T_{n}
in
eq.

1.1
be given, then the approximation
eq.

2.4
fulfils

\displaystyle p_{n}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{e_{n}(x)^{*}T_{n}e_{n}(x)}{(n+1)^{d}},\qquad

\displaystyle e_{n}(x)

\displaystyle=\left(\textnormal{e}^{-2\pi\mathit{i}{kx}}\right)_{k\in[n]},

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(n+1)^{d}}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sigma^{(n)}_{j}u^{(n)}_{j}(x)\overline{v^{(n)}_{j}(x)},\qquad

\displaystyle u^{(n)}_{j}(x)

\displaystyle=e_{n}(x)^{*}U_{n},\;v^{(n)}_{j}(x)=V_{n}^{*}e_{n}(x)

If
\mu\in\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{R}}
, then the moment matrix is Hermitian.
If
\mu\in\mathcal{M}_{+}
, then the moment matrix is positive semi-definite,
\|p_{n}\|_{L^{1}}=\|{\mu}\|_{\textnormal{TV}}=1
, and

\displaystyle p_{n}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(n+1)^{d}}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sigma^{(n)}_{j}\left|u^{(n)}_{j}(x)\right|^{2}.

(2.5)

Proof.

Let
q\in\mathbb{C}^{(n+1)^{d}}
, then direct computation shows

\displaystyle q^{*}T_{n}q=\sum_{k,l\in[n]}\overline{q_{k}}\left(\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\textnormal{e}^{-2\pi\mathit{i}{(k-\ell)y}}\mathrm{d}\mu(y)\right)q_{\ell}=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\left|\sum_{k\in[n]}q_{k}\textnormal{e}^{2\pi\mathit{i}{ky}}\right|^{2}\mathrm{d}\mu(y)\geq 0.

Choosing
q=e_{n}(x)
yields the second claim and by interchanging the order of integration and noting that the value of the inner integral is independent of

also

\displaystyle\|p_{n}\|_{L^{1}}=\frac{1}{(n+1)^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\left|\sum_{k\in[n]}\textnormal{e}^{2\pi\mathit{i}{k(y-x)}}\right|^{2}\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}\mu(y)=\mu(\mathbb{T}^{d})=\|{\mu}\|_{\textnormal{TV}}.

∎

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
n,d\in\mathbb{N}
, then we have

\displaystyle\frac{d}{\pi^{2}}\left(\frac{\log(n+2)}{n+1}+\frac{1}{n+3}\right)\leq\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}F_{n}(x)|x|_{1}\mathrm{d}x\leq\frac{d}{\pi^{2}}\frac{\log(n+1)+3}{n}.

Proof.

First note that

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\prod_{s=1}^{d}F_{n}(x_{s})\sum_{\ell=1}^{d}|x_{\ell}|_{1}\mathrm{d}x=\sum_{\ell=1}^{d}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\prod_{s=1}^{d}F_{n}(x_{s})|x_{\ell}|_{1}\mathrm{d}x=d\int_{\mathbb{T}}F_{n}(x)|x|_{1}\mathrm{d}x

such that it is sufficient to consider the univariate case.
With the representation
F_{n}(x)=1+2\sum_{k=1}^{n}\left(1-\frac{k}{n+1}\right)\cos(2\pi kx)
we find after elementary integration

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{T}}F_{n}(x)|x|_{1}\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=2\int_{0}^{1/2}x+2\sum_{k=1}^{n}\left(1-\frac{k}{n+1}\right)\cos(2\pi kx)x\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=2\left[\frac{1}{8}-\frac{1}{(n+1)\pi^{2}}\sum_{j=0}^{\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\rfloor}\frac{n-2j}{(2j+1)^{2}}\right]

\displaystyle=2\left[\frac{1}{\pi^{2}}\sum_{j=\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\rfloor+1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(2j+1)^{2}}+\frac{1}{(n+1)\pi^{2}}\sum_{j=0}^{\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\rfloor}\frac{1}{2j+1}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2}{\pi^{2}}\int_{\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\rfloor}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(2y+1)^{2}}\mathrm{d}y+\frac{2}{(n+1)\pi^{2}}\left(1+\int_{0}^{\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\rfloor}\frac{1}{2y+1}\mathrm{d}y\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\pi^{2}n}+\frac{1}{(n+1)\pi^{2}}\left(2+\log(n+1)\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{3+\log(n+1)}{\pi^{2}n},

since we have that
\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(2j+1)^{2}}=\frac{\pi^{2}}{8}
. The lower bound follows similarly by bounding the series from the previous calculation by integrals from below.∎

Theorem 2.5
.

Let
d,n\in\mathbb{N}
and
\mu\in\mathcal{M}
, then the measure with density
p_{n}
converges weakly to
\mu
with

\displaystyle W_{1}(p_{n},\mu)\leq\frac{d}{\pi^{2}}\frac{\log(n+1)+3}{n}\cdot\|{\mu}\|_{\textnormal{TV}},

which is sharp since
\mu\in\mathcal{M}_{+}
implies
\|{\mu}\|_{\textnormal{TV}}=1
and

\displaystyle\sup_{\mu\in\mathcal{M}}W_{1}(p_{n},\mu)\geq\frac{d}{\pi^{2}}\left(\frac{\log(n+2)}{n+1}+\frac{1}{n+3}\right).

Proof.

We compute

\displaystyle W_{1}(p_{n},\mu)

\displaystyle=\sup_{\Lip(f)\leq 1}\left|\langle F_{n}*\mu,f\rangle-\langle\mu,f\rangle\right|

\displaystyle=\sup_{\Lip(f)\leq 1}\left|\langle\mu,F_{n}*f-f\rangle\right|

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{\Lip(f)\leq 1}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}F_{n}(x)\left|f(y-x)-f(y)\right|\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}|\mu|(y)

\displaystyle\leq\|{\mu}\|_{\textnormal{TV}}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}F_{n}(x)|x|_{1}\mathrm{d}x,

note that both inequalities become equalities when choosing
\mu=\delta_{0}
and
f(x)=|x|_{1}
, and then apply
lemma

2.4
.
We note in passing that
W_{1}(F_{n},\delta_{0})=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}F_{n}(x)|x|_{1}\mathrm{d}x
.
∎

Example 2.6
.

2.5
gives a worst case lower bound and, on the other hand, the Lebesgue measure is approximated by
F_{n}*\lambda=\lambda
without any error.
We may thus ask how well a measure
\mathrm{d}\mu=w(x)\mathrm{d}x
with smooth (non-negative) density might be approximated. If we choose the analytical density
w(x)=1+\cos(2\pi x)
, then
F_{n}*w(x)-w(x)=\cos(2\pi x)/(n+1)
and, by testing with the Lipschitz function
f(x)=\cos(2\pi x)/(2\pi)
, we see that

\displaystyle W_{1}(F_{n}*w,w)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2\pi(n+1)}\int_{\mathbb{T}}\cos^{2}(2\pi x)\mathrm{d}x=\frac{1}{4\pi(n+1)}.

This effect is called saturation (e.g. cf.
[
4
]
).

In greater generality, such a lower bound holds for each measure individually and can be inferred by a nice relationship between the Wasserstein distance and a discrepancy, cf.
[
12
]
.

Theorem 2.7
.

For each individual measure
\mu\in\mathcal{M}
different from the Lebesgue measure, there is a constant
c>0
such that

\displaystyle W_{1}(p_{n},\mu)\geq\frac{c}{n+1}

holds for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

Proof.

Let
\hat{h}\in\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z}^{d})
,
\hat{h}(k)\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
,
\hat{h}(k)=\hat{h}(-k)
,
and consider the reproducing kernel Hilbert space

\displaystyle H=\{f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d}):\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}|\hat{h}(k)|^{-2}|\hat{f}(k)|^{2}<\infty\},\qquad\|f\|_{H}^{2}=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}|\hat{h}(k)|^{-2}|\hat{f}(k)|^{2}.

Given two measures
\mu,\nu
, their discrepancy (which also depends on the space
H
) is defined by

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}(\mu,\nu)=\sup_{\|f\|_{H}\leq 1}\left|\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}f~\mathrm{d}(\mu-\nu)\right|

and fulfils by the geometric-arithmetic inequality

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}(p_{n},\mu)^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\|k\|_{\infty}\leq n}|\hat{h}(k)|^{2}\left|1-\prod_{\ell=1}^{d}\left(1-\frac{|k_{\ell}|}{n+1}\right)\right|^{2}|\hat{\mu}(k)|^{2}+\sum_{\|k\|_{\infty}>n}|\hat{h}(k)|^{2}|\hat{\mu}(k)|^{2}

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{\|k\|_{\infty}\leq n}|\hat{h}(k)|^{2}\left|\frac{\|k\|_{1}}{d(n+1)}\right|^{2}|\hat{\mu}(k)|^{2}+\sum_{\|k\|_{\infty}>n}|\hat{h}(k)|^{2}|\hat{\mu}(k)|^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\|k\|_{\infty}\leq n}|\hat{h}(k)|^{2}\left|\frac{\|k\|_{1}}{d(n+1)}\right|^{2}|\hat{\mu}(k)-\hat{\lambda}(k)|^{2}+\sum_{\|k\|_{\infty}>n}|\hat{h}(k)|^{2}|\hat{\mu}_{k}-\hat{\lambda}(k)|^{2}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{d^{2}(n+1)^{2}}\|h*(\mu-\lambda)\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}^{2}

where
h(x)=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\hat{h}(k)\textnormal{e}^{2\pi\mathit{i}{kx}}
and
\lambda
denotes the Lebesgue measure with
\hat{\lambda}(0)=1
and
\hat{\lambda}(k)=0
for
k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}\setminus\{0\}
.

Our second ingredient is a Lipschitz estimate: If
f\in H
with
\|f\|_{H}\leq 1
, then

\displaystyle|f(y)-f(y+x)|^{2}

\displaystyle=\left|\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\hat{f}(k)\left(\textnormal{e}^{2\pi\mathit{i}{ky}}-\textnormal{e}^{2\pi\mathit{i}{k(y+x)}}\right)\right|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\|f\|_{H}^{2}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\left|\textnormal{e}^{2\pi\mathit{i}{ky}}-\textnormal{e}^{2\pi\mathit{i}{k(y+x)}}\right|^{2}|\hat{h}(k)|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 2\left(K(0)-K(x)\right),

where
K(x)=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}|\hat{h}(k)|^{2}\textnormal{e}^{2\pi\mathit{i}{kx}}=(h*h)(x)
denotes the so-called reproducing kernel of the space
H
.
If this kernel is
K(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d})=h^{[4]}(x_{1})\cdot\ldots\cdot h^{[4]}(x_{d})
for some univariate function
h^{[4]}\in C^{2}(\mathbb{T})
,
\left(h^{[4]}\right)^{\prime}(0)=0
, we find by a telescoping sum and direct calculation

\displaystyle K(0)-K(x)

\displaystyle=\prod_{\ell=1}^{d}h^{[4]}(0)-\prod_{\ell=1}^{d}h^{[4]}(x_{\ell})

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\ell=1}^{d}\left(h^{[4]}(0)^{\ell}\prod_{k=1}^{d-\ell}h^{[4]}(x_{k})-h^{[4]}(0)^{\ell-1}\prod_{k=1}^{d-\ell+1}h^{[4]}(x_{k})\right)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\ell=1}^{d}\|h^{[4]}\|^{d-1}_{\infty}\left[h^{[4]}(0)-h^{[4]}(x_{\ell})\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\|h^{[4]}\|^{d-1}_{\infty}\left\|\left(h^{[4]}\right)^{\prime\prime}\right\|_{\infty}|x|^{2}.

To make a specific choice, let
a\in(0,\frac{1}{8})
be some irrational number and set
h^{[2]}=\chi_{[-a,a]}*\chi_{[-a,a]}
as the convolution of the indicator function on
[-a,a]
with itself,

h^{[4]}=h^{[2]}*h^{[2]}
, and
h(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d})=h^{[2]}(x_{1})\cdot\ldots\cdot h^{[2]}(x_{d})
.
Since the space of Lipschitz test functions is at least as large as the reproducing kernel Hilbert space, we derive

\displaystyle W_{1}(p_{n},\mu)\geq\frac{1}{2}\cdot\left(\frac{\sqrt{3}}{4}\right)^{d-1}a^{1-\frac{3}{2}d}\mathcal{D}(p_{n},\mu)\geq\frac{1\cdot\left(\frac{\sqrt{3}}{4}\right)^{d-1}a^{1-\frac{3}{2}d}}{d(n+1)}\|h*(\mu-\lambda)\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}.

Since
a
is irrational, we can directly see by Parseval’s theorem that
\|h*(\mu-\lambda)\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}=0
if and only if
\mu=\lambda
. For
\mu\neq\lambda
, we obtain the statement with a positive constant
c
depending on the measure
\mu
, the constant
a
, and the spatial dimension
d
.
∎

Remark 2.8
.

The gap between upper and lower bounds can be narrowed by choosing another convolution kernel, which then however does not allow for the representation in
lemma

2.3
.
The Jackson kernel

\displaystyle J_{2m-2}(x)=\frac{3}{m(2m^{2}+1)}\frac{\sin^{4}(m\pi x)}{\sin^{4}(\pi x)},\quad m\in\mathbb{N},

has degree
n=2m-2
and satisfies

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{T}}J_{n}(x)|x|_{1}\mathrm{d}x\leq\frac{6}{m(2m^{2}+1)}\left[\int_{0}^{1/2m}m^{4}x\mathrm{d}x+\int_{1/2m}^{\infty}\frac{1}{16x^{3}}\mathrm{d}x\right]\leq\frac{3m}{2(2m^{2}+1)}\leq\frac{3}{2(n+2)}.

Analogously to
2.5
, we get

\displaystyle W_{1}(J_{n}*\mu,\mu)\leq\frac{3}{2}\frac{d\cdot\|{\mu}\|_{\textnormal{TV}}}{n+2},

(2.6)

which still is an approximate factor 6 worse than the lower bound in the univariate case. A factor 3 is due to the above estimate and a factor 2 seems to indicate that the Jackson kernel is not optimal.
Moreover, upper and lower bound differ by a factor
d
in the multivariate case which might be due to the used norms or our proof techniques.

We mention at this point that

\displaystyle W_{1}(K_{n}*\mu,\mu)\leq\|{\mu}\|_{\textnormal{TV}}W_{1}(\delta_{0},K_{n}),

and

\displaystyle W_{1}(\delta_{0},K_{n})\leq\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}|K_{n}(x)||x|_{1}\mathrm{d}x

for any kernel
K_{n}
and measure
\mu\in\mathcal{M}
with equality in the second inequality if
K_{n}
is nonnegative.

2.1
Univariate case

In one variable, the question of uniqueness of the best approximation can be equivalently characterised by the uniqueness of the best approximation in
L^{1}(\mathbb{T})
and thus allows for the following lemma.

Lemma 2.9

(Best approximation in the univariate case)
.

For
d=1
, any absolutely continuous real measure admits a unique best approximation by a polynomial of degree
n\in\mathbb{N}
with respect to the Wasserstein-1 distance.

Proof.

Let
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{R}}
and
\mathcal{B}_{1}
denote the Bernoulli spline of degree 1 from the proof of
2.2
, then we have

\displaystyle W_{1}(\nu,\mu)

\displaystyle=\sup_{f:\Lip(f)\leq 1}\left|\int_{\mathbb{T}}f(x)\left[\mathrm{d}\nu(x)-\mathrm{d}\mu(x)\right]\right|

\displaystyle=\sup_{f:\Lip(f)\leq 1}\left|\int_{\mathbb{T}}\int_{\mathbb{T}}f^{\prime}(t)\mathcal{B}_{1}(x-t)\left[\mathrm{d}\nu(x)-\mathrm{d}\mu(x)\right]\mathrm{d}t\right|

\displaystyle=\sup_{f:\Lip(f)\leq 1}\left|\int_{\mathbb{T}}f^{\prime}(t)\left[(\mathcal{B}_{1}*\nu)(t)-(\mathcal{B}_{1}*\mu)(t)\right]\mathrm{d}t\right|.

Since the integral over
f^{\prime}
is zero by the periodicity of
f
, any
c\in\mathbb{R}
yields

\displaystyle\left|\int_{\mathbb{T}}f^{\prime}(t)\left[(\mathcal{B}_{1}*\nu)(t)-(\mathcal{B}_{1}*\mu)(t)\right]\mathrm{d}t\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\int_{\mathbb{T}}f^{\prime}(t)\left[(\mathcal{B}_{1}*\nu)(t)-(\mathcal{B}_{1}*\mu)(t)-c\right]\mathrm{d}t\right|

\displaystyle\leq\inf_{c\in\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{T}}\left|(\mathcal{B}_{1}*\nu)(t)-(\mathcal{B}_{1}*\mu)(t)-c\right|\mathrm{d}t.

On the other hand, choosing
c^{*}
such that
\{t:(\mathcal{B}_{1}*\nu)(t)-(\mathcal{B}_{1}*\mu)(t)>c^{*}\}
and
\{t:(\mathcal{B}_{1}*\nu)(t)-(\mathcal{B}_{1}*\mu)(t)<c^{*}\}
have the same mass yields

\displaystyle\sup_{f:\Lip(f)\leq 1}\left|\int_{\mathbb{T}}f^{\prime}(t)\left[(\mathcal{B}_{1}*\nu)(t)-(\mathcal{B}_{1}*\mu)(t)-c^{*}\right]\mathrm{d}t\right|\geq\int_{\mathbb{T}}\left|(\mathcal{B}_{1}*\nu)(t)-(\mathcal{B}_{1}*\mu)(t)-c^{*}\right|\mathrm{d}t

by taking
f
with
f^{\prime}(t)=\pm 1
depending on the sign of the term in brackets. Because of
\int_{\mathbb{T}}(\mathcal{B}_{1}*\nu-\mathcal{B}_{1}*\mu)(t)\mathrm{d}t=0
, this gives
c^{*}=0
and thus

\displaystyle W_{1}(\nu,\mu)=\int_{\mathbb{T}}\left|(\mathcal{B}_{1}*\nu)(t)-(\mathcal{B}_{1}*\mu)(t)\right|\mathrm{d}t.

(2.7)

We proceed by computing explicitly

\displaystyle(\mathcal{B}_{1}*\mu)(t)=\frac{\mu([0,t))+\mu([0,t])}{2}-\mu([0,1))\left(t+\frac{1}{2}\right)+\int_{[0,1)}x\mathrm{d}\mu(x).

(2.8)

If
\mu
does not give mass to single points, we have that
\mathcal{B}_{1}*\mu

is continuous and hence there exists a unique best
L^{1}
-approximation
\tilde{p}
(e.g. cf.
[
9
, Thm. 3.10.9]
) which defines
p^{*}
uniquely by
\tilde{p}=\mathcal{B}_{1}*p^{*}
.
∎

Remark 2.11
.

We close by some remarks which are specific for the univariate setting:

(i)

We stress that (
2.7
) in the proof of the
lemma

2.9
allows to compute the Wasserstein distance as an
L^{1}
-distance for real signed univariate measures.
Similarly, this allows to compute the so-called star discrepancy
\|\nu([0,\cdot))\|_{\infty}
as suggested in
[
29
, eq. (2.1) and (2.2)]
. However note that (
2.8
) has some additional term such that
\nu=\frac{1}{2}\delta_{0}-\frac{1}{2}\delta_{1/2}
with
\nu(\mathbb{T})=0
gives

\displaystyle\|\nu([0,\cdot))\|_{\infty}=\frac{1}{2}\neq\frac{1}{4}=\|\mathcal{B}_{1}*\nu\|_{\infty}

and thus
[
29
, eq. (2.1) and (2.2)]
needs some adjustment.

On the real line, we indeed have
\mu((-\infty,x])=(H*\mu)(x)
for the Heaviside function

\displaystyle H(x)=\begin{cases}1,&\quad x\geq 0,\\
0,&\quad\text{else,}\end{cases}

such that the Wasserstein distance again can be computed via

\displaystyle W_{1}(\mu,\nu)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left|\mu((-\infty,x])-\nu((-\infty,x])\right|\mathrm{d}x=\|H*(\mu-\nu)\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R})},

see e.g.
[
42
, Prop. 2.17]
.

lemma

2.9
might be considered as the periodic analogue of this result.

(ii)

One can relate our work to a main result in
[
29
]
. As
lemma

2.9
reformulates the Wasserstein distance of two univariate measures in terms of the
L^{1}
-distance of their convolution with the Bernoulli spline, one can view this Bernoulli spline as a kernel of type
\beta=1
following the notation of
[
29
]
. Thus, one can take
p=1,p^{\prime}=\infty
in
[
29
, Thm. 4.1]
yielding that the Wasserstein distance between a measure
\mu
and its trigonometric approximation is bounded from above by
c/n
. The latter agrees with our
remark

2.8
which additionally gives an explicit and small constant.

(iii)

The observation that the construction of
p^{*}
for
\delta_{0}
is possible via FFT’s might lead to the idea to construct near-best approximations to any measure
\mu
by interpolating
\mathcal{B}_{1}*\mu
by some
\tilde{p}
and to obtain the polynomial
p
of near best approximation which satisfies
\tilde{p}=\mathcal{B}_{1}*p
by multiplying with the Fourier coefficients of the Bernoulli spline
\mathcal{B}_{1}
. A first problem would be that the limited knowledge of moments only allows to interpolate the partial Fourier sum
S_{n}(\mathcal{B}_{1}*\mu)
which does not converge to
\mathcal{B}_{1}*\mu
uniformly as
n\to\infty
for discrete
\mu
. Secondly, the near-best approximation
p
cannot be expected to be nonnegative for a nonnegative measure
\mu
which is another drawback compared to convolution with nonnegative kernels like the Fejér or Jackson kernel.

(iv)

Finally note that kernels
K_{n}
with stronger localization and ‘smoother’ Fourier coefficients, e.g. higher powers of the Fejér kernel, allow to improve the rate beyond
n^{-1}
if the measure has a smooth density
w
. This can be seen from partial integration

\displaystyle W_{1}(K_{n}*w,w)

\displaystyle=\sup_{\Lip(f)\leq 1}\left|\int_{\mathbb{T}}\left(K_{n}*f(y)-f(y)\right)w(y)\mathrm{d}y\right|

\displaystyle=\sup_{\psi,\Lip(\psi^{\prime})\leq 1}\left|\int_{\mathbb{T}}\left(K_{n}*\psi(y)-\psi(y)\right)w^{\prime}(y)\mathrm{d}y\right|

and the above arguments. However note that from a practical perspective, this asks for a-priori smoothness assumptions on the measure to choose a suitable kernel.

3
Interpolation

Using the singular functions of the moment matrix as in
eq.

2.5
and with
r=r(n)=\operatorname{rank}T_{n}
, we define noise- and signal-functions
p_{0,n},p_{1,n}:\mathbb{T}^{d}\to[0,1]
,

\displaystyle p_{1,n}(x)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{r}\left|u^{(n)}_{j}(x)\right|^{2},\qquad p_{0,n}(x)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=r+1}^{N}\left|u^{(n)}_{j}(x)\right|^{2},

(3.1)

respectively.
In what follows, we suppose that
V\subseteq\mathbb{T}^{d}
denotes the smallest set
containing
\operatorname{supp}\mu
that is the zero-locus of some unknown trigonometric polynomial,
i. e.
V\supseteq\operatorname{supp}\mu
is the Zariski closure of the support.
We show pointwise convergence

\displaystyle p_{1,n}(x)\xrightarrow{n\to\infty}\chi_{V}(x)=\begin{cases}1,&x\in V,\\
0,&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

(3.2)

to the characteristic function of this set. Clearly this cannot be uniform and our first result shows interpolation of the value
1
for finite
n
as well as a variational characterization.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
d,n\in\mathbb{N}
,
\mu\in\mathcal{M}
, and suppose
V(\ker T_{n})=V\subseteq\mathbb{T}^{d}
, where
V(\ker T_{n})
is the set consisting of the common roots of all the polynomials in
\ker T_{n}
. Then
p_{0,n}(x)+p_{1,n}(x)=1
for all
x\in\mathbb{T}^{d}
. In particular, we have

\displaystyle p_{1,n}(x)\begin{cases}=1,&\text{\ if $x\in V$},\\
<1,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(3.3)

If
V\subsetneq\mathbb{T}^{d}
, the variational characterization

\displaystyle p_{0,n}(x)=\max_{p}\frac{|p(x)|^{2}}{N\|p\|_{L^{2}}^{2}}

(3.4)

holds, where the maximum is subject to all trigonometric polynomials

p\in\langle\textnormal{e}^{2\pi\mathit{i}{kx}}:k\in[n]\rangle
,
p\neq 0
, such that
p(y)=0
for all
y\in V
.

Proof.

We have

p_{1,n}(x)+p_{0,n}(x)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\left|u_{j}^{(n)}(x)\right|^{2}=\frac{1}{N}e_{n}(x)^{*}U_{n}U_{n}^{*}e_{n}(x)=\frac{1}{N}e_{n}(x)^{*}e_{n}(x)=1,

(3.5)

so in particular
p_{1,n}(x)\in[0,1]
.
Since
V(\ker T_{n})=V
and
\ker T_{n}=\langle u_{j+1}^{(n)},\dots,u_{N}^{(n)}\rangle
,
it follows that the polynomials
u_{j+1}^{(n)}(x),\dots,u_{N}^{(n)}(x)
vanish on
V
,
so
p_{1,n}(x)=1
for all
x\in V
.
Conversely, if
x\in\mathbb{T}^{d}
such that
p_{1,n}(x)=1
,
we claim that
x\in V
.
Indeed, we have
1-p_{1,n}(x)=\sum_{j=r+1}^{N}\left|u_{j}^{(n)}(x)\right|^{2}=0
,
so it follows that
x
lies in the vanishing set of
u_{j+1}^{(n)}(x),\dots,u_{N}^{(n)}(x)
,
so
x\in V(\ker T_{n})=V
.

For the variational characterization,
first note that the admissible polynomials belong to
\ker T_{n}\setminus\{0\}
,
which is non-empty since
V(\ker T_{n})=V\subsetneq\mathbb{T}^{d}
.
Let
q\coloneqq U_{0}U_{0}^{*}e_{n}(x)\in\ker T_{n}
,
where
U_{0}
denotes a matrix whose columns form an orthonormal basis of
\ker T_{n}
.
For all
p\in\ker T_{n}
, we have

q^{*}p=e_{n}(x)^{*}U_{0}U_{0}^{*}p=e_{n}(x)^{*}p=p(x).

In particular, note that

\left\|q\right\|_{2}^{2}=q^{*}q=q(x)=e_{n}(x)^{*}U_{0}U_{0}^{*}e_{n}(x)=Np_{0,n}(x).

(3.6)

Therefore, by the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, it follows that

\left|p(x)\right|^{2}=\left|q^{*}p\right|^{2}\leq\left\|q\right\|_{2}^{2}\cdot\left\|p\right\|_{2}^{2}=Np_{0,n}(x)\left\|p\right\|_{2}^{2}.

Hence, we have

p_{0,n}(x)\geq\max_{p\in\ker T_{n}\setminus\{0\}}\frac{\left|p(x)\right|^{2}}{N\left\|p\right\|_{2}^{2}}\geq\frac{\left|q(x)\right|^{2}}{N\left\|q\right\|_{2}^{2}}=p_{0,n}(x),

if
q\neq 0
.
The first inequality also holds when
q=0
,
in which case the result follows due to (
3.6
).
∎

Note that (
3.3
) and (
3.5
) generalise and strengthen

[
33
, Propositions 5.2, 5.3]

from algebraic curves on the two-dimensional torus
to algebraic varieties of arbitrary dimension.

.

V(\ker T_{n})=V

3.1

Similarly, it holds for sufficiently large

3.1
Zero-dimensional situation

If the measure is given by

\displaystyle\mu=\sum_{j=1}^{r}\lambda_{j}\delta_{x_{j}}

with support
V=\operatorname{supp}\mu=\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{r}\}\subset\mathbb{T}^{d}
and complex weights
\Lambda=\operatorname{diag}(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{r})
, then the support is zero-dimensional and the moment matrix allows for the Vandermonde factorisation

\displaystyle T_{n}=A_{n}^{*}\Lambda A_{n},\qquad A_{n}=(\textnormal{e}^{2\pi\mathit{i}{kx_{j}}})_{j=1,\ldots,r;k\in[n]}\in\mathbb{C}^{r\times N},

which will be instrumental.

Theorem 3.4

(Pointwise convergence)
.

Let
\mu=\sum_{j=1}^{r}\lambda_{j}\delta_{x_{j}}
be a discrete measure
and let
x\in\mathbb{T}^{d}
such that
x\neq x_{j}
for all
1\leq j\leq r
. If
n+1>4d/\min_{j\neq\ell}|x_{j}-x_{\ell}|_{\infty}
, then

\displaystyle p_{1,n}(x)\leq\frac{1}{(n+1)^{2}}\cdot\frac{|\lambda_{\max}|}{|\lambda_{\min}|}\cdot\frac{1}{3}\sum_{j=1}^{r}\frac{1}{|x-x_{j}|_{\infty}^{2}},

in particular, this implies the pointwise convergence (
3.2
).
Moreover, if the support of
\mu
fulfils
n+1>2\sqrt{d}/\min_{j\neq\ell}|x_{j}-x_{\ell}|_{\infty}
and
\min_{j}|x-x_{j}|_{\infty}\leq\sqrt{d}/(n+1)
, then

\displaystyle p_{1,n}(x)\leq 1-\frac{3^{d-1}(2d-1)}{2^{d}d^{2+d/2}}\cdot(n+1)^{2}\cdot\min_{j}|x-x_{j}|_{\infty}^{2}.

Proof.

Comparing (
3.1
) with
lemma

2.3
and using (
2.4
) yields

\displaystyle p_{1,n}(x)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{r}\frac{\sigma_{j}}{\sigma_{\min}}\left|u^{(n)}_{j}(x)\right|^{2}=\frac{1}{\smin}\sum_{j=1}^{r}|\lambda_{j}|F_{n}(x-x_{j}).

The final estimate follows from

\displaystyle F_{n}(x-x_{j})\leq\frac{(n+1)^{d-1}}{(n+1)\sin^{2}\left(\pi|x-x_{j}|_{\infty}\right)}\leq\frac{(n+1)^{d-2}}{4|x-x_{j}|_{\infty}^{2}}

and
\sigma_{\min}\geq 0.8(n+1)^{d}|\lambda_{\min}|
, see
[
22
, Thm. 4.2]
.

Regarding the second estimate, consider the Vandermonde matrix

\displaystyle\tilde{A}_{n,x}=\begin{bmatrix}e_{n}(x_{1})&\cdots&e_{n}(x_{r})&e_{n}(x)\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{C}^{N\times(r+1)}

and note that its pseudo-inverse gives rise to the Lagrange polynomial

\displaystyle\ell_{r+1}(y)=e_{r+1}^{*}\tilde{A}_{n,x}^{\dagger}e_{n}(y).

satisfying
\ell_{r+1}(x_{j})=0
for
j=1,\ldots,r
and
\ell_{r+1}(x)=1
.
We compute

\displaystyle\|\ell_{r+1}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}|e_{r+1}^{*}\tilde{A}_{n,x}^{\dagger}e_{n}(y)\rangle|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}|\langle\tilde{A}_{n,x}^{\dagger*}e_{r+1},e_{n}(y)\rangle|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}y=\|\tilde{A}_{n,x}^{\dagger*}e_{r+1}\|_{2}^{2}\leq\sigma_{\min}(\tilde{A}_{n,x})^{-2}

and use
3.1
to bound

\displaystyle 1-p_{1,n}(x)=p_{0,n}(x)=\max_{p}\frac{|p(x)|^{2}}{N\|p\|_{L^{2}}^{2}}\geq\frac{|\ell_{r+1}(x)|^{2}}{N\|\ell_{r+1}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}}\geq\frac{\sigma_{\min}(\tilde{A}_{n,x})^{2}}{N}.

The assertion follows from known estimates on the smallest singular value for the Vandermonde matrix with pairwise clustering nodes, see
[
16
, Corollary 3.20]
.
∎

Remark 3.5
.

Actually,
3.4
shows the correct orders in
n
and
\min_{j}|x-x_{j}|_{\infty}^{2}
in the upper bound of
p_{1,n}(x)
.
First note that
1-p_{1,n}
and all its partial derivatives of order 1 vanish on
x_{1},\ldots,x_{r}
.
For fixed
x\in\mathbb{T}^{d}
, the Taylor expansion in
x_{0}=\argmin_{j}|x-x_{j}|_{\infty}
thus gives

\displaystyle 1-p_{1,n}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}(x-x_{0})^{\top}H_{x}(\xi)(x-x_{0}),\qquad H_{x}(\xi)=\left(\frac{-\partial^{2}p_{1,n}}{\partial x_{r}\partial x_{s}}\left(\xi\right)\right)_{1\leq r,s\leq d}

\displaystyle\leq\|H_{x}(\xi)\|_{\mathrm{F}}\cdot\frac{d}{2}\cdot\min_{j}|x-x_{j}|_{\infty}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 2\pi^{2}d^{2}n^{2}\min_{j}|x-x_{j}|_{\infty}^{2},

where the last inequality uses an entrywise Bernstein inequality and
\|p_{1,n}\|_{\infty}=1
.

Lemma 3.6

(Convergence of singular values)
.

Let
\mu=\sum_{j=1}^{r}\lambda_{j}\delta_{x_{j}}
be a discrete complex measure whose weights are ordered non-increasingly with respect to their absolute value.
Assume that
(n+1)\min_{j\neq\ell}|x_{j}-x_{\ell}|_{\infty}>d
, then the singular values
\sigma_{j}
of the moment matrix
T_{n}
fulfil

\displaystyle\left||\lambda_{j}|-\frac{\sigma_{j}}{N}\right|\leq\frac{1}{n+1}\cdot\frac{\left|\lambda_{1}\right|\left(1+\sqrt{\textnormal{e}}\right)r}{2\min_{j\neq\ell}|x_{j}-x_{\ell}|_{\infty}},\qquad j=1,\ldots,r.

Proof.

With the polar decomposition
\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}A_{n}^{*}=PH
,
where
P\in\mathbb{C}^{N\times r}
is unitary and
H\in\mathbb{C}^{r\times r}
is positive-definite,
we have that
\left|\lambda_{1}\right|\geq\cdots\geq\left|\lambda_{r}\right|
are the singular values
of the matrix
P\Lambda P^{*}
.
Therefore, for the singular values of
T_{n}=A_{n}^{*}\Lambda A_{n}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\max_{1\leq j\leq r}\left|\frac{\sigma_{j}}{N}-\left|\lambda_{j}\right|\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\frac{1}{N}T_{n}-P\Lambda P^{*}\right\|_{2}=\left\|H\Lambda H^{*}-\Lambda\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|H\Lambda\left(H-\mathrm{I}_{r}\right)\right\|_{2}+\left\|\left(H-\mathrm{I}_{r}\right)\Lambda\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\left|\lambda_{1}\right|\left(\left\|H\right\|_{2}+1\right)\left\|H-\mathrm{I}_{r}\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\left|\lambda_{1}\right|\left(\left\|H\right\|_{2}+1\right)\left\|(H+\mathrm{I}_{r})^{-1}\right\|_{2}\left\|H^{2}-\mathrm{I}_{r}\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\left|\lambda_{1}\right|\frac{\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\smax(A_{n})+1}{\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\smin(A_{n})+1}\left\|\frac{1}{N}A_{n}A_{n}^{*}-\mathrm{I}_{r}\right\|_{\textnormal{F}},

where the first inequality is due to
[
3
, Theorem 2.2.8]
.
Each entry of the matrix
\frac{1}{N}A_{n}A_{n}^{*}-\mathrm{I}_{r}
is a modified Dirichlet kernel and can be bounded uniformly by

\displaystyle\left\|\frac{1}{N}A_{n}^{*}A_{n}-\mathrm{I}_{r}\right\|_{\textnormal{F}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{r}\sum_{l\neq j}\left|\sum_{k\in[n]}\textnormal{e}^{2\pi\mathit{i}{k(x_{l}-x_{j})}}\right|^{2}\right)^{1/2}\leq\frac{r}{N}\cdot\frac{(n+1)^{d-1}}{2\min_{j\neq\ell}|x_{j}-x_{\ell}|_{\infty}}.

Moreover, since
(n+1)\min_{j\neq\ell}|x_{j}-x_{\ell}|_{\infty}>d
, it follows from
[
22
, Lemma 2.1]
that

\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\smax(A_{n})\leq\sqrt{\left(1+\frac{1}{d}\right)^{d}}\leq\sqrt{\textnormal{e}}.\qed

Theorem 3.7
.

Normalizing differently, we have

\frac{p_{1,n}}{\|p_{1,n}\|_{L^{1}}}\rightharpoonup\tilde{\mu}=\frac{1}{r}\sum_{j=1}^{r}\delta_{x_{j}}

as
n\to\infty
.

Proof.

First note that
\|p_{1,n}\|_{L^{1}}=r/N
.
We define
\tilde{p}_{n}=F_{n}*\tilde{\mu}
and observe that
for any continuous function
f
on
\mathbb{T}^{d}

we have

\displaystyle\mathrel{\hphantom{\leq}}\left|\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\frac{p_{1,n}(x)}{\|p_{1,n}\|_{L^{1}}}f(x)\,\mathrm{d}x-\frac{1}{r}\sum_{j=1}^{r}f(x_{j})\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left|\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\left(\frac{p_{1,n}(x)}{\|p_{1,n}\|_{L^{1}}}-\tilde{p}_{n}(x)\right)f(x)\,\mathrm{d}x\right|+\left|\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\tilde{p}_{n}(x)f(x)\,\mathrm{d}x-\frac{1}{r}\sum_{j=1}^{r}f(x_{j})\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\frac{N}{r}p_{1,n}-\tilde{p}_{n}\right\|_{L^{1}}\left\|f\right\|_{L^{\infty}}+\left|\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}f\,\mathrm{d}(F_{n}*\tilde{\mu})-\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}f\,\mathrm{d}\tilde{\mu}\right|,

so, by
2.5
,
it is enough to show that
\left\|\frac{N}{r}p_{1,n}-\tilde{p}_{n}\right\|_{L^{1}}
converges to zero for
n\to\infty
.

If
n
is sufficiently large, then by (
2.5
) we can write

\tilde{p}_{n}(x)=\frac{1}{N}e_{n}(x)^{*}\tilde{U}\tilde{\Sigma}\tilde{U}^{*}e_{n}(x)

where
\tilde{\Sigma}\in\mathbb{C}^{r\times r}
denotes the diagonal matrix
consisting of non-zero singular values
and
\tilde{U}\in\mathbb{C}^{N\times r}
denotes the corresponding singular vector matrix
of the moment matrix of
\tilde{\mu}
.

As
p_{1,n}
only depends on the signal space of the moment matrix
T_{n}
of
\mu
,
which agrees with the signal space of the moment matrix of
\tilde{\mu}
,
it follows by (
3.1
) that

p_{1,n}(x)=\frac{1}{N}e_{n}(x)^{*}\tilde{U}\tilde{U}^{*}e_{n}(x)

and thus

\left|\frac{N}{r}p_{1,n}(x)-\tilde{p}_{n}(x)\right|=\left|e_{n}(x)^{*}\tilde{U}\left(\frac{\mathrm{I}_{r}}{r}-\frac{\tilde{\Sigma}}{N}\right)\tilde{U}^{*}e_{n}(x)\right|\leq\left\|e_{n}(x)\tilde{U}\right\|_{2}^{2}\left\|\frac{1}{r}\mathrm{I}_{r}-\frac{1}{N}\tilde{\Sigma}\right\|_{2}.

Since
\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\left\|e_{n}(x)\tilde{U}\right\|_{2}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x=N\|p_{1,n}\|_{L^{1}}=r
is constant,
the result follows from
lemma

3.6
.
∎

3.2
Positive-dimensional situation

For a measure
\mu
whose support is an algebraic variety, we derive a pointwise convergence rate
p_{1,n}(x)=\mathcal{O}\left(n^{-1}\right)
outside of the variety in
3.8
and this proves (
3.2
). It is not clear whether this is already optimal, as we found
\mathcal{O}\left(n^{-2}\right)
as an approximation rate in the case of a discrete measure.

Theorem 3.8
.

Let
y\in\mathbb{T}^{d}
and
let
g\in\langle\textnormal{e}^{2\pi\mathit{i}{\left\langle k,x\right\rangle}}\mid k\in[m]\rangle
be a trigonometric polynomial of max-degree
m

such that
g(y)\neq 0
and
g
vanishes on
\operatorname{supp}\mu
.
Then

p_{1,n+m}(y)\leq 1-\frac{(n+1)^{d}}{(n+m+1)^{d}}\cdot\frac{\left|g\left(y\right)\right|^{2}}{\left(F_{n}*\left|g\right|^{2}\right)(y)}\leq\frac{\|g\|_{L^{2}}^{2}}{|g(y)|^{2}}\frac{m(4m+2)^{d}}{n+1}+\frac{dm}{\left(n+m+1\right)},

for
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
n\geq m
.

Proof.

Set
N_{n}=(n+1)^{d}
for
n\in\mathbb{N}

and define the trigonometric polynomial

p(x)=e_{n,y}(x)g(x)
of max-degree
n+m
,
where
e_{n,y}(x)\coloneqq e_{n}(x)^{*}e_{n}(y)
.
Furthermore, we define
f(x)\coloneqq\left|g(x)\right|^{2}
.
Then

\left|p(x)\right|^{2}=N_{n}F_{n}(x-y)f(x),

for all
x\in\mathbb{T}^{d}
.
On the other hand,

\left\|p\right\|_{L^{2}}^{2}=N_{n}\left(F_{n}*f\right)(y).

Thus, by (
3.4
), we obtain

1-p_{1,n+m}(y)\geq\frac{\left|p(y)\right|^{2}}{N_{n+m}\left\|p\right\|_{L^{2}}^{2}}=\frac{N_{n}}{N_{n+m}}\cdot\frac{f(y)}{\left(F_{n}*f\right)(y)}\geq\left(1-\frac{dm}{n+m+1}\right)\frac{1}{1+h_{n}},

(3.7)

where we define

h_{n}\coloneqq{\left\|F_{n}*f-f\right\|_{L^{\infty}}}/{f(y)}
,
which proves the first statement.
For the upper bound, we compute

\displaystyle\left|(F_{n}*f-f)(x)\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\sum_{k\in\{-m,\dots,m\}^{d}}\sum_{\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{s\in\{0,1\}^d}{1\leq|s|\leq d}}\frac{(-1)^{|s|}|k^{s}|}{(n+1)^{|s|}}\hat{f}_{k}\textnormal{e}^{2\pi\mathit{i}{kx}}\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\sum_{k\in\{-m,\dots,m\}^{d}}\sum_{\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{s\in\{0,1\}^d}{1\leq|s|\leq d}}\frac{(-1)^{|s|}|k^{s}|}{(n+1)^{|s|}}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\left|g(z)\right|^{2}\textnormal{e}^{2\pi\mathit{i}{k(x-z)}}\mathrm{d}z\right|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{s\in\{0,1\}^d}{1\leq|s|\leq d}}\left(\frac{m}{n+1}\right)^{|s|}(2m+1)^{d}\|g\|_{L^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\|g\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\frac{m(4m+2)^{d}}{n+1}

by using that
f=|g|^{2}
is a trigonometric polynomial of degree
m
.
Then it follows from (
3.7
) that

p_{1,n+m}(y)\leq h_{n}+\frac{dm}{\left(n+m+1\right)}\leq\frac{\|g\|_{L^{2}}^{2}}{|g(y)|^{2}}\frac{m(4m+2)^{d}}{n+1}+\frac{dm}{\left(n+m+1\right)},

since we can apply
(1+h_{n})^{-1}\geq 1-h_{n}
.
∎

4
Numerical examples

We illustrate in this section the asymptotic behaviour of
p_{n}
and
p_{1,n}
for several types of singular measures, with respect to the Wasserstein-1 distance. We compute the distance using a semidiscrete optimal transport algorithm, described below.

Our experiments focus on three examples on
\mathbb{T}^{2}
: a discrete measure
\mu_{\mathrm{d}}
supported on
15
points, with (nonnegative) random amplitudes, a uniform measure
\mu_{\mathrm{cu}}
supported on the trigonometric algebraic curve

\cos(2\pi x)\cos(2\pi y)+\cos(2\pi x)+\cos(2\pi y)=\frac{1}{4},

(4.1)

and a uniform measure
\mu_{\mathrm{ci}}
supported on the circle centered in
c_{0}=(\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2})
with radius
r_{0}=0.3
.

The moments of
\mu_{\mathrm{cu}}
are computed numerically up to machine precision using Arb
[
18
]
with a parametrization of the implicit curve (
4.1
). It follows from (
2.2
) that the trigonometric moments of the measure
\mu_{\mathrm{ci}}
are given by

\widehat{\mu_{\mathrm{ci}}}(k)=\textnormal{e}^{-2\pi\mathit{i}{kc_{0}}}J_{0}(2\pi r_{0}\left\|k\right\|_{2}).

The polynomials
p_{n}
,
J_{n}*\mu
, and
p_{1,n}
can be evaluated efficiently via the fast Fourier transform over a regular grid in
\mathbb{T}^{2}
.
For the polynomial
p_{1,n}
, the singular value decomposition of the moment matrix
T_{n}
can be computed at reduced cost by exploiting that
T_{n}
has Toeplitz structure and resorting only to matrix-vector multiplications.

To compute transport distances to the measure
\mu\in\{\mu_{\mathrm{cu}},\mu_{\mathrm{ci}}\}
, let the curve
C=\operatorname{supp}\mu\subset\mathbb{T}^{d}
denote its support with arc-length
L
.
Now let
s\in\mathbb{N}
, take a partition
C=\bigcup_{\ell=1}^{s}C_{\ell}
into path-connected curves with measure
\mu(C_{\ell})=s^{-1}
and arc-length
L_{\ell}
, and any
x_{\ell}\in C_{\ell}
, then

\displaystyle W_{1}\left(\frac{1}{s}\sum_{\ell=1}^{s}\delta_{x_{\ell}},\mu\right)

\displaystyle=\sup_{f:\Lip(f)\leq 1}\left|\sum_{\ell}\int_{C_{\ell}}\left[f(x)-f(x_{\ell})\right]\mathrm{d}\mu(x)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\ell=1}^{s}\int_{C_{\ell}}|x-x_{\ell}|_{1}\mathrm{d}\mu(x)\leq\sum_{\ell=1}^{s}\sqrt{d}L_{\ell}\mu(C_{\ell})=\frac{\sqrt{d}\cdot L}{s}.

We denote the resulting discrete measures by
\mu_{\mathrm{cu}}^{s}
and
\mu_{\mathrm{ci}}^{s}
, respectively (see Figure
4.1
). In our tests, we use
s=3000
samples, which offers a satisfactory tradeoff between computational time and accuracy for our range of degrees
n
. Indeed, the computational cost of evaluating the objective (
4.2
) or its gradient grows linearly in
s
, while for degrees up to
n=250
, sampling beyond 3000 points has no effect on the output of our algorithm for computing
W_{1}(p_{n},\mu^{s})
, see Figure
4.1
.

, that is
\tilde{\mu}^{s}=\frac{1}{s}\sum_{i=1}^{s}\delta_{x_{i}}
where
\{x_{1},\dots,x_{s}\}
is the support of
\mu
.

In the discrete case, our numerical results show that the Wasserstein distance
W_{1}(p_{n},\mu^{s})
decreases at a rate close to the worst-case bound derived in Theorem
2.5
. This is also the case for
W_{1}(p_{1,n},\tilde{\mu}^{s})
, which is coherent with the bound given in the proof of Theorem
3.7
. In the positive dimensional cases, one would need to compute the Wasserstein distances for degrees larger than
n=250
to be able to reliably estimate a rate, but this would require better optimized algorithms, in the spirit for instance of
[
23
]
, which goes beyond the scope of this paper. Still, our preliminary results seem to indicate that the rates for
F_{n}*\mu
and
J_{n}*\mu
in the positive dimensional situation are similar to the ones for discrete measures, but with better constants. For
p_{1,n}
on the other hand, although the theory does not foresee weak convergence in that case, if it were to occur, our results indicate that the rate would then be worse than in the discrete case.

5
Summary and outlook

We provided tight bounds on the pointwise approximation error as well as with respect to the Wasserstein-1 distance when approximating arbitrary measures by trigonometric polynomials.

The approximation by the convolution with the Fejér kernel is both simple and up to a logarithmic factor best possible in the worst case. In contrast and beyond the scope of this paper, a computation similar to
2.5
and
remark

2.8
shows that stronger localised trigonometric kernels seem necessary when considering the Wasserstein-2 distance.

Future work might also consider the truncation of the singular value decomposition in
section

3
if the support of the measure is only approximated by the zero set of an unknown trigonometric polynomial or the available trigonometric moments are disturbed by noise.
