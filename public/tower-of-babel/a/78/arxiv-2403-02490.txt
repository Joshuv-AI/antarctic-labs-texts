Interpolation Polynomials, Binomial Coefficients, and Symmetric Function Inequalities
Hong Chen, Siddhartha Sahi

Abstract
Interpolation polynomials were introduced by Knop–Sahi in type A A , and Okounkov in type B ​ C BC . They are inhomogeneous polynomials whose top terms are Jack and Macdonald polynomials. Thus the expansion coefficients for the product of two interpolation polynomials, known as Littlewood–Richardson coefficients, generalize the corresponding coefficients for Jack/Macdonald polynomials. Special values of interpolation polynomials, known as binomial coefficients, arise in the binomial type expansions of Jack/Macdonald polynomials and Koornwinder polynomials. We prove a number of results for interpolation polynomials and the associated coefficients. These include positivity and monotonicity results for binomial coefficients, partial positivity results for Littlewood–Richardson coefficients, and weighted sum formulas for both kinds of coefficients. As a special case of our results we obtain a new symmetric function inequality, which establishes a “duality” between Jack expansion positivity for symmetric functions, and the containment order on partitions, with respect to the shifted basis Ω λ ​ ( 𝟏 + x , τ ) \Omega_{\lambda}(\bm{1}+x;\tau) , where 𝟏 = ( 1 , … , 1 ) \bm{1}=(1,\ldots,1) and Ω λ ​ ( x , τ ) = P λ ​ ( x , τ ) / P λ ​ ( 𝟏 , τ ) \Omega_{\lambda}(x;\tau)=P_{\lambda}(x;\tau)/P_{\lambda}(\bm{1};\tau) is the normalized Jack polynomial. Our inequality can be seen as an analog of the inequalities of Cuttler–Greene–Skandera+Sra and Khare–Tao, which establish similar dualities between evaluation positivity on the positive orthant, and the dominance and weak dominance orders on partitions, with respect to the normalized Schur basis Ω λ ​ ( x ) = s λ ​ ( x ) / s λ ​ ( 𝟏 ) \Omega_{\lambda}(x)=s_{\lambda}(x)/s_{\lambda}(\bm{1}) and its shifted version Ω λ ​ ( 𝟏 + x ) \Omega_{\lambda}(\bm{1}+x) , respectively. In contrast to our result, the Jack versions of the two latter inequalities, although expected to hold, have not yet been proved.

Introduction

Symmetric functions play a central role in algebraic combinatorics and representation theory. The ring of symmetric polynomials in
n
variables admits several distinguished homogenous bases, each indexed by the set
\mathcal{P}_{n}
of partitions
\lambda
with
\leqslant n
parts, which encode a rich algebraic and combinatorial structure. Two of the most important bases are the two-parameter Macdonald polynomials
P_{\lambda}(x;q,t)
and their one-parameter specializations, the Jack polynomials
P_{\lambda}(x;\tau)
, which connect to mathematical physics, integrable systems, and special function theory. The Jack polynomials further specialize to many classic examples such as the monomial and elementary symmetric polynomials, as well as the zonal and Schur polynomials, which arise naturally in the representation theory of the symmetric and general linear groups, and their generalizations.

Macdonald and Jack polynomials admit two natural inhomogeneous analogs, known as interpolation polynomials of type
A
and type
BC
, which are characterized by certain symmetry and vanishing properties. The type
A
polynomials were introduced by Knop and Sahi in
Knop [1997]
,
Knop and Sahi [1996]
,
Sahi [1994]
,
Sahi [1996]
, while the type
BC
polynomials, which are Laurent polynomials, were introduced by Okounkov
Okounkov [1998a]
.

Interpolation polynomials play two different roles in the homogeneous theory. First, their top degree terms are Jack and Macdonald polynomials, thus their Littlewood–Richardson coefficients (structure constants) generalize the homogeneous coefficients. Second, certain special values of interpolation polynomials, known as (generalized) binomial coefficients, arise in the binomial type expansion of Jack/Macdonald polynomials and Koornwinder polynomials
Okounkov [1997]
,
Okounkov [1998a]
,
Okounkov [1998b]
,
Okounkov and Olshanski [1997a]
.

In this paper we prove several properties of interpolation polynomials and the associated coefficients. As an application we obtain a symmetric function inequality for the containment order on partitions, which can be regarded as an analog of the inequalities of Cuttler–Green–Skandera+Sra
Cuttler et al. [2011]
,
Sra [2016]
and Khare–Tao
Khare and Tao [2021]
for the dominance and weak dominance orders, respectively.
We discuss this application first since its formulation requires fewer prerequisites.

1.1
Symmetric Function Inequalities and Duality

Inequalities involving symmetric function are of broad interest. These often have the following general form: we are given a basis
(b_{\lambda}(x))_{\lambda}
of symmetric polynomials and two partial orders
\succeq_{1}
and
\succeq_{2}
on symmetric polynomials and partitions, respectively, and then the inequality asserts

b_{\lambda}\succeq_{1}b_{\mu}\text{ if and only if }\lambda\succeq_{2}\mu.

(1.1)

Definition 1
.

If (
1.1
) holds we will say that
\succeq_{1}
and
\succeq_{2}
are
dual
for the basis
(b_{\lambda}(x))_{\lambda}
.

Of course, given a basis and one of the two partial orders, one can simply
define
the other partial order so that (
1.1
) holds, but in general this will not lead to anything particularly interesting. The most significant examples are those in which both partial orders are interesting in their own right, and for which the duality seems to hold with respect to
many
different natural bases.

There are two important examples of duality, which were proved relatively recently.
In both cases the partial order on functions is defined by
evaluation positivity
, where
p(x)\succeq_{1}0
means
p(x)\geqslant 0
whenever all
x_{i}\geqslant 0
; and
p(x)\succeq_{1}q(x)
means
p(x)-q(x)\succeq_{1}0
. The partial orders on partitions in the two cases are
dominance
and
weak dominance
, also known as
majorization
and
weak majorization
. In the case of dominance,
\lambda\succeq_{2}\mu
means
\sum_{i=1}^{k}\lambda_{i}\geqslant\sum_{i=1}^{k}\mu_{i}
for all
k
, with equality for
k=n
; for weak dominance one simply omits the last equality requirement.

It turns out that evaluation positivity is dual to
both
dominance and weak dominance; the first duality holds with respect to the normalized Schur basis
\Omega_{\lambda}(x)=\frac{s_{\lambda}(x)}{s_{\lambda}(\bm{1})};\;\bm{1}=(1,1,...,1)
, while the second duality holds for the shifted basis
\Omega_{\lambda}(\bm{1}+x)
. The first result was conjectured by Cuttler–Green–Skandera
Cuttler et al. [2011]
and proved by Sra
Sra [2016]
; the second is due to Khare–Tao
Khare and Tao [2021]
.

We note that evaluation/dominance duality also holds for
several
normalized bases. The case of the monomial basis is the classical Muirhead inequality
Muirhead [1902]
, which is a far-reaching generalization of the AM–GM inequality. Moreover, as shown in
Cuttler et al. [2011]
, this duality also holds for the bases of power sums, elementary, and complete symmetric polynomials, and these results generalize many classical inequalities due to Maclaurin, Newton, Schlömilch, Gantmacher, Popoviciu and Schur.

It is natural to wonder
why
the first duality holds for so many different bases. A partial answer is that many, though not all, of these bases are special cases of Jack polynomials, and presumably those results are special cases of a duality involving Jack polynomials, or more generally for the Jacobi polynomials of Heckman–Opdam
[
McSwiggen and Novak, 2022
, Conjecture 4.7]
. We discuss this further in
Section

6
below, where we also formulate a conjectural analog of the second duality for Jack polynomials. In a companion paper
Chen et al. [2025]
we formulate a more general duality conjecture for Macdonald polynomials, and prove it in the special case of 2 variables.

We now formulate our new duality result, which is parallel to those mentioned above. Indeed, the set of partitions admits a third important partial order, the
containment
order, for which
\lambda\succeq_{2}\mu
means
\lambda_{i}\geqslant\mu_{i}
for all
i
, and it is natural to ask whether this too admits a dual notion of positivity. This turns out to be true and the condition is
expansion positivity
, wherein
p(x)\succeq_{1}0
means that
p(x)
has a positive expansion in some given basis, e.g. Schur functions. Indeed we show that Schur expansion positivity and containment are dual with respect to the basis
\Omega_{\lambda}({\bf 1}+x)
. In fact we prove a more general result involving Jack polynomials, which we now describe.

We recall that Jack polynomials
P_{\lambda}(x;\tau)
have coefficients in the field
\mathbb{Q}(\tau)
. We write
\Omega_{\lambda}(x;\tau)={\frac{P_{\lambda}(x;\tau)}{P_{\lambda}({\bf 1};\tau)}}
for the normalized Jack polynomial, and we say a symmetric polynomial is
Jack positive
if it is a combination of
\Omega_{\lambda}(x;\tau)
with coefficients in the cone
\mathbb{Q}(\tau)_{\geqslant 0}
consisting of rational functions of the form
f(\tau)/{g(\tau)}
for some polynomials
f,g
with positive coefficients.
Many classical polynomials (see
Macdonald [2015]
) arise by specializing
\tau=\tau_{0}
for some real number
\tau_{0}
. These include the monomial, elementary, and Schur functions, as well as zonal polynomials over
\mathbb{R}
and
\mathbb{H}
. We say a symmetric polynomial is
\tau_{0}
-Jack positive
if it is a combination of
\Omega_{\lambda}(x;\tau_{0})
with coefficients in
\mathbb{R}_{\geqslant 0}.

Theorem A
.

Jack positivity and containment are dual with respect to
\Omega_{\lambda}({\bf 1}+x;\tau)
. Furthermore, for each
\tau_{0}
in
[0,\infty]
,
\tau_{0}
-Jack positivity and containment are dual with respect to
\Omega_{\lambda}({\bf 1}+x;\tau_{0})
.

To the best of our knowledge, even the special cases of Theorem
A
involving monomial, elementary, Schur, and zonal polynomials are new.
Interestingly, this duality also holds for normalized power sums (see
Theorem

6.3
), although they are not special cases of Jack polynomials.

We will deduce Theorem
A
as a special case of more general results on binomial coefficients and Littlewood–Richardson coefficients for interpolation polynomials that we now describe.

1.2
Interpolation Polynomials and Binomial Coefficients

Newton’s binomial formula states that for any non-negative integer
n
(in fact, for any real number
n
and
-1<t<1
)

\displaystyle(t+1)^{n}=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\binom{n}{m}t^{m}.

(1.2)

Symmetric polynomial analogs of this binomial formula have been studied:
Bingham
Bingham [1974]
studied the expansion of the zonal polynomial
C_{\lambda}(x+\bm{1})
in
(C_{\mu}(x))
and Lascoux
Lascoux [1978]
(see also
[
Macdonald, 2015
, P.47 Example 10]
) the expansion of the Schur polynomial
s_{\lambda}(x+\bm{1})
in
(s_{\mu}(x))
, for applications in multivariate statistics and algebraic topology, respectively.
Later, in the 1990s, binomial formulas for Jack polynomials and Macdonald polynomials (and their non-symmetric counterparts) were studied by Lassalle, Kaneko, Sahi, Okounkov, Olshanski and others, in
Lassalle [1990]
,
Kaneko [1993]
,
Okounkov and Olshanski [1997b]
,
Okounkov and Olshanski [1997a]
,
Okounkov [1998b]
,
Sahi [1998]
. See also
Koornwinder [2014]
for a well-written survey.

Okounkov–Olshanski

showed for Jack polynomials that

\displaystyle\Omega_{\lambda}(x+\bm{1};\tau)=\sum_{\nu}\binom{\lambda}{\nu}_{\tau}\Omega_{\nu}(x;\tau),

(1.3)

where the
generalized binomial coefficients

\binom{\lambda}{\nu}_{\tau}
are given by evaluating
interpolation Jack polynomials
, which are
inhomogeneous
symmetric polynomials.

In this paper, we study four families of interpolation polynomials, including type
A
interpolation Jack and Macdonald polynomials due to Knop–Sahi
Sahi [1994]
,
Knop and Sahi [1996]
,
Okounkov and Olshanski [1997a]
,
Knop [1997]
,
Okounkov [1997]
,
Okounkov [1998b]
, and the type
BC
analogs due to Okounkov
Okounkov [1998a]
,
Rains [2005]
.
Denote by
{A\mathrm{J}}
and
{A\mathrm{M}}
the type
A
interpolation Jack and Macdonald polynomials, respectively, and similarly
{B\mathrm{J}},{B\mathrm{M}}
for their type
BC
analogs.
Hereafter in the paper, referred to as
the four families
of interpolation polynomials are the families
{A\mathrm{J}},{A\mathrm{M}},{B\mathrm{J}}
and
{B\mathrm{M}}
.
Each family depends on certain parameters, and the base field
\mathbb{F}
is the field of rational polynomials in the parameters.
For each family, we also define convex cones
\mathbb{F}_{\geqslant 0}
and
\mathbb{F}_{>0}
in the field
\mathbb{F}
, which we call the
cone of (strict) positivity
(see
Section

2.2
).

The four families of interpolation polynomials, denoted by
(h_{\mu}^{A\mathrm{J}})
,
(h_{\mu}^{A\mathrm{M}})
,
(h_{\mu}^{B\mathrm{J}})
and
(h_{\mu}^{B\mathrm{M}})
, can be uniformly defined by the following the interpolation condition and degree condition:

\displaystyle h_{\mu}(\overline{\lambda})=\delta_{\lambda\mu},\quad\forall\lambda\in\mathcal{P}_{n},\ |\lambda|\leqslant|\mu|,

\displaystyle\deg(h_{\mu})=|\mu|.

where
\delta_{\lambda\mu}
is the Kronecker delta function and
\overline{(\cdot)}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathcal{P}_{n}\to\mathbb{F}^{n}
is a certain “shifting” function that depends on the family in question (see

2.2
below).

It is a surprising fact, called the
extra vanishing property
, that the interpolation polynomial
h_{\mu}
vanishes at more points than required in the definition:

\displaystyle h_{\mu}(\overline{\lambda})=0\text{,\quad unless\quad}\lambda\supseteq\mu.

(1.4)

In this paper, we study the
generalized binomial coefficients
for the four families of interpolation polynomials, given by

\displaystyle b_{\lambda\mu}\coloneqq\binom{\lambda}{\mu}\coloneqq h_{\mu}(\overline{\lambda}).

(1.5)

For the family
{A\mathrm{J}}
(type
A
interpolation Jack polynomials), the corresponding binomial coefficients are precisely those in Okounkov–Olshanski’s binomial formula
Eq.

1.3
.

We prove that the binomial coefficients for the four families are monotone.

Theorem B

(Monotonicity)
.

If
\lambda\supseteq\mu
, then
\binom{\lambda}{\nu}-\binom{\mu}{\nu}
lies in
\mathbb{F}_{\geqslant 0}
.
If, in addition,
\lambda\neq\mu
and
\lambda\supseteq\nu\neq\bm{0}=(0,\dots,0)

then
\binom{\lambda}{\nu}-\binom{\mu}{\nu}
lies in
\mathbb{F}_{>0}
.

Theorem

A
is a direct corollary of
Theorem

B
and
Eq.

1.3
.
Another consequence of
Theorem

B
is the positivity of binomial coefficients; namely taking
\lambda\supseteq\mu=\nu
and using
\binom{\mu}{\mu}=1
, we see that the binomial coefficient
\binom{\lambda}{\mu}
is greater than 1, and hence, positive.
By the extra vanishing property,
\binom{\lambda}{\mu}
is 0 otherwise, and thus we conclude:

Theorem C

(Positivity)
.

The binomial coefficient
\binom{\lambda}{\mu}\in\mathbb{F}_{>0}
if and only if
\lambda\supseteq\mu
.

In the case of
{A\mathrm{J}}
and
{A\mathrm{M}}
,

The binomial coefficient admits the following formula

where

consists of saturated chains from
\lambda
to
\mu

is given in

3.8

We refer the reader to

1.3
Littlewood–Richardson Coefficients for Interpolation Polynomials

We also consider the

We prove a weighted sum formula for the structure constants.

Theorem E
.

The generalized Littlewood–Richardson coefficients admits the following formula

c_{\mu\nu}^{\lambda}=\sum_{\bm{\zeta}\in\mathfrak{C}_{\lambda\mu}}\wt_{\nu}^{\mathrm{LR}}(\bm{\zeta})\prod_{i=0}^{k-1}\binom{\bm{\zeta}_{i}}{\bm{\zeta}_{i+1}},

where the weight
\wt_{\nu}^{\mathrm{LR}}(\bm{\zeta})
is given by
Eq.

3.20

See
Theorem

3.7
for the precise statement.
In

Theorem F

(Adjacent Positivity for LR Coefficients)
.

If
\lambda\cover\mu
, then the adjacent Littlewood–Richardson coefficient

lies in
\mathbb{F}_{\geqslant 0}

\lambda\supseteq\nu\neq\bm{0}

1.4
Integrality of Interpolation Polynomials

As a further application, we address the matter of integrality, which, for Jack polynomials, means that the coefficients of
\tau
lie in
\mathbb{Z}
(see

6.23

In

Our result concerns integral adjacent binomial coefficients,

Theorem G

(Integrality and Positivity)
.

For the families
\mathcal{F}={A\mathrm{J}}
and
{B\mathrm{J}}

\lambda\cover\mu
, then the integral adjacent binomial coefficient

is a polynomial with non-negative integer coefficients in the parameter(s).
For the families
\mathcal{F}={A\mathrm{M}}
and
{B\mathrm{M}}
, if
\lambda\cover\mu
, then the integral adjacent binomial coefficient
A_{\lambda\mu}
, after a re-parametrization and up to some sign and powers, is a polynomial with non-negative integer coefficients in the new parameters.

See
Section

6.2
for the precise statements.

1.7
Related Results

For the interested reader we provide a brief discussion of some related results in the existing literature on Jack, Macdonald, and interpolation polynomials.

Jack polynomials were introduced by Jack

as a one-parameter generalization of Schur functions and of the zonal polynomials that play an important role in multivariate statistics

Along with Hall–Littlewood polynomials, they were one of the two key sources of inspiration for Macdonald’s introduction of his two-parameter family of symmetric functions

; see
Kuznetsov and Sahi [2006]
for a historical background.
These polynomials, in turn, were the impetus behind Cherednik’s discovery of the double affine Hecke algebra
Cherednik [1995a]
. There are various combinatorial formulas for Jack and Macdonald polynomials, for example,
Stanley [1989]
,
Knop and Sahi [1997]
,
Macdonald [2015]
,
Haglund et al. [2005]
,
Corteel et al. [2022a]
.
For interpolation analogs, see
Okounkov [1998a]
,
Okounkov [1998b]
,
Koornwinder [2014]
.
Non-symmetric analogs of these are studied in
Opdam [1995]
,
Cherednik [1995b]
,
Sahi [1996]
,
Knop [1997]
,
Sahi [1998]
,
Marshall [2003]
,
Haglund et al. [2008]
,
Disveld et al. [2021]
.

Interpolation polynomials arise naturally as solutions to the Capelli eigenvalue problem for invariant differential operators on a symmetric cone
Sahi [1994]
. The Capelli problem has analogs for other symmetric spaces studied in
Sahi and Zhang [2017]
,
Sahi and Salmasian [2019]
and also for symmetric superspaces
Sahi and Salmasian [2016]
,
Sahi et al. [2020]
.
The solutions of these other problems are related to interpolation polynomials defined by Okounkov, Ivanov, and Sergeev and Veselov
Ivanov [1997]
,
Okounkov [1998a]
,
Sergeev and Veselov [2005]
.
In the classical setting, the expansion of Schur functions into the power sum basis gives rise to irreducible characters of the symmetric group.
This idea is generalized to Jack and Macdonald polynomials, giving the so-called Jack and Macdonald characters in

,

2.1

For this section, we refer to
[

Throughout the paper, we will fix

All four families of interpolation polynomials are indexed by partitions of length at most

Such a

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{n}\coloneqq\left\{\,\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\dots,\lambda_{n})\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}\mathclose{}\nonscript\;\middle|\penalty\nonscript\;\mathopen{}\lambda_{1}\geqslant\lambda_{2}\geqslant\dots\geqslant\lambda_{n}\geqslant 0\,\right\}.

For

, and let
\mathcal{P}_{n}^{d}\coloneqq\left\{\,\lambda\in\mathcal{P}_{n}\mathclose{}\nonscript\;\middle|\penalty\nonscript\;\mathopen{}|\lambda|\leqslant d\,\right\}.

We write
\lambda\supseteq\mu

\lambda_{i}\geqslant\mu_{i}
for

.
This partial order is called the

or the

For

Let

\displaystyle\mathbb{F}_{\geqslant 0}\coloneqq\left\{\,\frac{f}{g}\mathclose{}\nonscript\;\middle|\penalty\nonscript\;\mathopen{}f,g\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geqslant 0}[\tau],\ g\neq 0\,\right\},

then

is a convex multiplicative cone, i.e., it is closed under addition, multiplication, and scalar multiplication by
\mathbb{Q}_{\geqslant 0}
.
When we view
\tau
as a real number instead of an indeterminate, we have

if

if and only if

Remark 1
.

Our definition of

is the same as the

in
[

, Section 1.4]

In that paper, a subcone

and

For

\displaystyle\mathbb{F}_{\geqslant 0}\coloneqq\left\{\,\frac{f}{g}\mathclose{}\nonscript\;\middle|\penalty\nonscript\;\mathopen{}f,g\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geqslant 0}[\tau,\alpha],\ g\neq 0\,\right\}.

Then for

In

2.2.3
Degree

A Laurent polynomial

can be written as

with

and nonzero for finitely many
\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
and

.
The

of

is defined by

\displaystyle\deg f\coloneqq\begin{cases}-\infty,&if $f$ is identically 0;\\
\max\left\{\,|\alpha_{1}|+\dots+|\alpha_{n}|\mathclose{}\nonscript\;\middle|\penalty\nonscript\;\mathopen{}c_{\alpha}\neq 0\,\right\},&otherwise.\end{cases}

We shall write
\Lambda^{d}
for the subspace of
\Lambda
consisting of polynomials of degree at most

when

; and at most

when
\mathcal{F}={B\mathrm{J}}
.

In this subsection, we recall some definitions and propositions of interpolation polynomials.

We begin with a proposition about symmetric interpolation.

Proposition 2.1

Fix

\displaystyle f(\overline{\lambda})=\overline{f}(\lambda),\quad\forall\lambda\in\mathcal{P}_{n}^{d}.

When

When

When
\mathcal{F}={B\mathrm{M}}

[

, Proposition 3.3]
.
When
\mathcal{F}={B\mathrm{J}}
, the proof for the case
\mathcal{F}={B\mathrm{M}}
could be easily modified for this case.
∎

Now, we can define the interpolation polynomials.

Definition 2
.

The unital

is the unique function in

that interpolates the characteristic function at

(restricted to

That is, it is the unique

\displaystyle h_{\mu}(\overline{\lambda})=\delta_{\lambda\mu},\quad\forall\lambda\in\mathcal{P}_{n},\ |\lambda|\leqslant|\mu|,

(2.7)

\displaystyle\deg h_{\mu}\leqslant\begin{cases}|\mu|,&\mathcal{F}={A\mathrm{J}},{A\mathrm{M}},{B\mathrm{M}};\\
2|\mu|,&\mathcal{F}={B\mathrm{J}}.\end{cases}

(2.8)

Remark 2
.

The degree condition above can be improved to equality.
Argue by induction on
|\mu|
.
The base case is clear since
\mathcal{P}_{n}^{0}=\{\bm{0}=(0^{n})\}
and
\Lambda^{0}
consists of constant functions.
For the inductive step, if
h_{\mu}
had a strictly smaller degree, it would lie in
\Lambda^{|\mu|-1}
and interpolate the zero function on

by definition, hence is equal to the zero function by Proposition

, a contradiction.

The normalization here is called

.

(2.9)

In

In

\displaystyle b_{\lambda\mu}^{{A\mathrm{J}}}(\tau)

\displaystyle=\lim_{\alpha\to\infty}b_{\lambda\mu}^{{B\mathrm{J}}}(\tau,\alpha),

(2.32)

\displaystyle b_{\lambda\mu}^{{A\mathrm{M}}}(q,t)

\displaystyle=\lim_{a\to\infty}b_{\lambda\mu}^{{B\mathrm{M}}}(q,t,a).

(2.33)

(4)

Limits of interpolation Macdonald polynomials as

(2.34)

(2.35)

The top degree terms of

(2.36)

\displaystyle P_{\lambda}(x;q,t)

\displaystyle=\lim_{r\to\infty}\frac{h_{\lambda}^{{\mathrm{monic}},{A\mathrm{M}}}(rx;q,t)}{r^{|\lambda|}},

(2.37)

\displaystyle P_{\lambda}(x^{2};\tau)

\displaystyle=\lim_{r\to\infty}\frac{h_{\lambda}^{{\mathrm{monic}},{B\mathrm{J}}}(rx;\tau)}{r^{2|\lambda|}},

(2.38)

\displaystyle P_{\lambda}(x;q,t)

\displaystyle=\lim_{r\to\infty}\frac{h_{\lambda}^{{\mathrm{monic}},{B\mathrm{M}}}(rx;q,t)}{r^{|\lambda|}}.

(2.39)

Proof.

Most are clear by definition.

3
Recursion and Weighted Sum Formulas

3.1
Binomial Coefficients

In 2011, the second author derived some recursion formulas and weighted sum formulas for the binomial coefficients for type
A
interpolation Jack and Macdonald polynomials, respectively, in
Sahi [2011b]
,
Sahi [2011a]
. (The treatment there also works for the non-symmetric cases.)
We now generalize the arguments and the results to type
BC
.

A key relation, the
Pieri rule
, was first observed in
[
Okounkov and Olshanski, 1997b
, Section 9]
for shifted Schur polynomials (which corresponds to our
{A\mathrm{J}}
with
\tau=1
), and in
[
Okounkov and Olshanski, 1997a
, Section 5]
for
\mathcal{F}={A\mathrm{J}}
.
Let
\varepsilon_{1}=(1,0,\dots,0)\in\mathcal{P}_{n}
.

Lemma 3.1

(Pieri Rule)
.

Fix
\mu\in\mathcal{P}_{n}
, then

\left(h_{\varepsilon_{1}}(x)-h_{\varepsilon_{1}}(\overline{\mu})\right)\cdot h_{\mu}(x)=\sum_{\nu\cover\mu}\left(h_{\varepsilon_{1}}(\overline{\nu})-h_{\varepsilon_{1}}(\overline{\mu})\right)a_{\nu\mu}h_{\nu}(x).

(3.1)

Proof.

It is clear from the definition that both sides of
Eq.

3.1
have degree (at most)
\deg h_{\varepsilon_{1}}+\deg h_{\mu}
, hence, lies in
\Lambda^{|\mu|+1}
.
By the uniqueness of interpolation (Proposition
2.1
), it suffices to check that the two sides have the same evaluations at
\overline{\lambda}
for
\lambda\in\mathcal{P}_{n}^{|\mu|+1}
, which is easily seen.
∎

Remark 5
.

Eq.

3.1
can be written as

\Bigl(\mathinner{\!\left\lVert x\right\rVert}-\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\overline{\mu}\right\rVert}\Bigr)\cdot h_{\mu}(x)=\sum_{\lambda\cover\mu}\Bigl(\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\overline{\lambda}\right\rVert}-\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\overline{\mu}\right\rVert}\Bigr)a_{\lambda\mu}h_{\lambda}(x),

(3.2)

where the “norm”
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert x\right\rVert}
is the top degree terms of
h_{\varepsilon_{1}}^{\mathrm{monic}}(x)
,
because the equation is invariant under translation and scalar multiplication of the norm
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert}
.

Fix a total order on
\mathcal{P}_{n}
that is compatible with the size function, i.e.,
|\lambda|\leqslant|\mu|
whenever
\lambda
precedes
\mu
.

Write

\displaystyle A=\left(a_{\lambda\mu}\right),\quad B=\left(b_{\lambda\mu}\right),\quad Z=\left(\,\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\overline{\mu}\right\rVert}\delta_{\lambda\mu}\right)

(3.3)

for the infinite matrices where
\lambda,\mu\in\mathcal{P}_{n}
.
Then
B
is unitriangular by
Eq.

2.7
, and hence invertible.
Denote the entry of its inverse matrix by
b_{\lambda\mu}^{\prime}
, i.e.,
B^{-1}=\left(b_{\lambda\mu}^{\prime}\right)
. We call
b_{\lambda\mu}^{\prime}
the
inverse binomial coefficients
.

Theorem 3.2

(Recursion for Binomial Coefficients)
.

(1)

The following recursion characterizes
b_{\lambda\mu}
:

\displaystyle(i)\ b_{\lambda\lambda}=1;\quad(ii)\ \Bigl(\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\overline{\lambda}\right\rVert}-\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\overline{\mu}\right\rVert}\Bigr)b_{\lambda\mu}=\sum_{\nu\cover\mu}b_{\lambda\nu}\Bigl(\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\overline{\nu}\right\rVert}-\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\overline{\mu}\right\rVert}\Bigr)a_{\nu\mu},\quad|\lambda|>|\mu|.

(3.4)

(2)

The following recursion characterizes
b_{\lambda\mu}^{\prime}
:

\displaystyle(i)\ b_{\lambda\lambda}^{\prime}=1;\quad(ii)\ \Bigl(\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\overline{\lambda}\right\rVert}-\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\overline{\mu}\right\rVert}\Bigr)b_{\lambda\mu}^{\prime}=\sum_{\nu\coveredby\lambda}a_{\lambda\nu}\Bigl(\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\overline{\nu}\right\rVert}-\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\overline{\lambda}\right\rVert}\Bigr)b_{\nu\mu}^{\prime},\quad|\lambda|>|\mu|.

(3.5)

(3)

The matrices
A,B,Z
satisfy the commutation relations:

(i)\ [Z,B]=B[Z,A];\quad(ii)\ [Z,B^{-1}]=-[Z,A]B^{-1}.

(3.6)

Proof.

We borrow the proof from
Sahi [2011a]
.

It is clear that (1)
\iff
(3.i)
\iff
(3.ii)
\iff
(2): the first and last equivalences follow by looking at the
(\lambda,\mu)
-entry of
Eq.

3.6
, while the second equivalence is a simple calculation.
(There is a typo in
Sahi [2011a]
for this part, which we fix now.)

[Z,B^{-1}]=ZB^{-1}-B^{-1}Z=-B^{-1}(ZB-BZ)B^{-1}=-B^{-1}[Z,B]B^{-1}{\mathrel{\to}^{(3.i)}}-[Z,A]B^{-1}.

Now, it suffices to prove (1):
(1.i) follows from the interpolation condition
Eq.

2.7
; for (1.ii), evaluate the Pieri rule
Eq.

3.1
at
\overline{\lambda}
;
Eq.

3.4
characterizes
b_{\lambda\mu}
by induction on
|\lambda|-|\mu|
.
∎

Theorem 3.3

(
Theorem

D
, Weighted Sum Formula for Binomial Coefficients)
.

Assume
\lambda\supseteq\mu
, and
k=|\lambda|-|\mu|
.

(1)

The binomial coefficient admits the following weighted sum formula

\displaystyle b_{\lambda\mu}=\sum_{\bm{\zeta}\in\mathfrak{C}_{\lambda\mu}}\wt(\bm{\zeta})\prod_{i=0}^{k-1}a_{\bm{\zeta}_{i}\bm{\zeta}_{i+1}},

(3.7)

where the weight
\wt(\bm{\zeta})
is defined as

\displaystyle\wt(\bm{\zeta})\coloneqq\prod_{i=0}^{k-1}\frac{\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\overline{\bm{\zeta}_{i}}\right\rVert}-\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\overline{\bm{\zeta}_{i+1}}\right\rVert}}{\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\overline{\bm{\zeta}_{0}}\right\rVert}-\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\overline{\bm{\zeta}_{i+1}}\right\rVert}}.

(3.8)

(2)

The inverse binomial coefficient admits the following weighted sum formula

\displaystyle b_{\lambda\mu}^{\prime}=\sum_{\bm{\zeta}\in\mathfrak{C}_{\lambda\mu}}\wt^{\prime}(\bm{\zeta})\prod_{i=0}^{k-1}a_{\bm{\zeta}_{i}\bm{\zeta}_{i+1}}

(3.9)

where the weight
\wt^{\prime}(\bm{\zeta})
is defined as

\displaystyle\wt^{\prime}(\bm{\zeta})\coloneqq(-1)^{k}\prod_{i=0}^{k-1}\frac{\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\overline{\bm{\zeta}_{i+1}}\right\rVert}-\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\overline{\bm{\zeta}_{i}}\right\rVert}}{\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\overline{\bm{\zeta}_{k}}\right\rVert}-\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\overline{\bm{\zeta}_{i}}\right\rVert}}.

(3.10)

Proof.

We will only prove for binomial coefficients as the other case is similar.
Let
\overline{b}_{\lambda\mu}
temporarily denote the sum in
Eq.

3.7
.
By
Theorem

3.2
, it suffices to verify that
\overline{b}_{\lambda\mu}
satisfies the recursion
Eq.

3.4
.
Clearly
\overline{b}_{\lambda\lambda}=1
since the sum involves only the single chain
\bm{\zeta}=(\lambda)
and the weight reduces to 1.
For the second part, we observe that

\wt(\bm{\zeta})=\wt(\bm{\zeta}^{\prime})\cdot\frac{\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\overline{\bm{\zeta}_{k-1}}\right\rVert}-\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\overline{\mu}\right\rVert}}{\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\overline{\lambda}\right\rVert}-\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\overline{\mu}\right\rVert}},\quad\text{where}\quad\bm{\zeta}^{\prime}=(\bm{\zeta}_{0},\bm{\zeta}_{1},\dots,\bm{\zeta}_{k-1}).

Therefore, collecting the terms in
Eq.

3.7
with
\bm{\zeta}_{k-1}=\nu
, we have

\displaystyle\overline{b}_{\lambda\mu}=\sum_{\nu\cover\mu}\left(\sum_{\bm{\zeta}^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{C}_{\lambda\nu}}\wt(\bm{\zeta}^{\prime})\prod_{i=0}^{k-2}a_{\bm{\zeta}_{i}\bm{\zeta}_{i+1}}\right)\frac{\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\overline{\nu}\right\rVert}-\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\overline{\mu}\right\rVert}}{\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\overline{\lambda}\right\rVert}-\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\overline{\mu}\right\rVert}}a_{\nu\mu}=\sum_{\nu\cover\mu}\overline{b}_{\lambda\nu}\frac{\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\overline{\nu}\right\rVert}-\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\overline{\mu}\right\rVert}}{\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\overline{\lambda}\right\rVert}-\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\overline{\mu}\right\rVert}}a_{\nu\mu}.

∎

Corollary 3.4

(Extra Vanishing Property)
.

The binomial coefficient
b_{\lambda\mu}
and the inverse binomial coefficient
b_{\lambda\mu}^{\prime}
are 0 unless
\lambda\supseteq\mu
.

Proof.

If
\lambda
does not contain
\mu
, then
\mathfrak{C}_{\lambda\mu}
is empty, hence
b_{\lambda\mu}=0
and
b_{\lambda\mu}^{\prime}=0
.
∎

We would like to point out that in the case of
{A\mathrm{J}}
, the norm
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\overline{\lambda}\right\rVert}
is simply
|\lambda|
, hence the weight
\wt(\bm{\zeta})=\frac{1}{k!}
is independent of
\bm{\zeta}
. Sahi
Sahi [2011b]
shows that
B=\exp(A)
and
b_{\lambda\mu}^{\prime}=(-1)^{|\lambda|-|\mu|}b_{\lambda\mu}
.
Such simple relations fail in other cases.

3.2
Recursion for Littlewood–Richardson Coefficients

The results in this subsection are again known in type
A
in
Sahi [2011b]
,
Sahi [2011a]
. We generalize them to type
BC
.

For any
p\in\Lambda
, one can define the
structure coefficient
(or, generalized Littlewood–Richardson coefficient)
c^{\lambda}_{\mu}(p)
by the product expansion

\displaystyle p(x)h_{\mu}(x)=\sum_{\lambda}c^{\lambda}_{\mu}(p)h_{\lambda}(x).

(3.11)

Define matrices
C=C(p)\coloneqq\left(c^{\lambda}_{\mu}(p)\right)_{\lambda,\mu}
and
D=D(p)\coloneqq\left(p(\overline{\mu})\delta_{\lambda\mu}\right)
.

Theorem 3.5
.

(1)

The following recursion characterizes
c^{\lambda}_{\mu}(p)
:

\displaystyle\begin{split}(i)&\ c^{\lambda}_{\lambda}(p)=p(\overline{\lambda});\\
(ii)&\ \text{ if }|\lambda|>|\mu|,\\
&\ \Bigl(\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\overline{\lambda}\right\rVert}-\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\overline{\mu}\right\rVert}\Bigr)c^{\lambda}_{\mu}(p)=\sum_{\zeta\cover\mu}c^{\lambda}_{\zeta}(p)\Bigl(\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\overline{\zeta}\right\rVert}-\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\overline{\mu}\right\rVert}\Bigr)a_{\zeta\mu}-\sum_{\zeta\coveredby\lambda}\Bigl(\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\overline{\lambda}\right\rVert}-\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\overline{\zeta}\right\rVert}\Bigr)a_{\lambda\zeta}c^{\zeta}_{\mu}(p).\end{split}

(3.12)

(2)

The matrices
C
and
D
satisfy:

\displaystyle(i)\ C=B^{-1}DB;\quad(ii)\ [Z,C]=[C,[Z,A]].

(3.13)

(3)

The structure coefficient admits the following formula

\displaystyle c^{\lambda}_{\mu}(p)=\sum_{\lambda\supseteq\zeta\supseteq\mu}b_{\lambda\zeta}^{\prime}b_{\zeta\mu}p(\overline{\zeta}).

(3.14)

Proof.

We again borrow the proof from
Sahi [2011a]
.

Evaluating
Eq.

3.11
at
\overline{\nu}
, we get

p(\overline{\nu})b_{\nu\mu}=\sum_{\lambda}b_{\nu\lambda}c^{\lambda}_{\mu}(p),

(3.15)

in other words,
DB=BC
, hence (2.i) holds. For (2.ii), we have

\displaystyle[Z,C]=[Z,B^{-1}DB]

\displaystyle=[Z,B^{-1}]DB+B^{-1}[Z,D]B+B^{-1}D[Z,B]

\displaystyle=-[Z,A]B^{-1}DB+B^{-1}DB[Z,A]

\displaystyle=-[Z,A]C+C[Z,A]=[C,[Z,A]].

In the second line, we use
Eq.

3.6
and the fact that
D
and
Z
are diagonal matrices.

Since
B
is unitriangular, (2.i) implies that
C
and
D
share diagonal entries, hence (1.i) holds.
Also, (1.ii) is exactly the
(\lambda,\mu)
-entry of (2.ii).
(1) characterizes
c^{\lambda}_{\mu}(p)
by induction on
|\lambda|-|\mu|
.

(3) is the
(\lambda,\mu)
-entry of (2.i).
∎

Of special interest are the LR coefficients with
p=h_{\nu}
, defined by
c_{\mu\nu}^{\lambda}\coloneqq c^{\lambda}_{\mu}(h_{\nu})
, in other words,

h_{\mu}(x)h_{\nu}(x)=\sum_{\lambda}c_{\mu\nu}^{\lambda}h_{\lambda}(x).

(3.16)

Unless otherwise stated, when we say LR coefficients, we will refer to
c_{\mu\nu}^{\lambda}
instead of
c^{\lambda}_{\mu}(p)
.
We rewrite the previous theorem in this case.

Theorem 3.6
.

(1)

The following recursions characterize
c_{\mu\nu}^{\lambda}
:

\displaystyle\begin{split}(i)&\ c_{\lambda\mu}^{\lambda}=b_{\lambda\mu}\\
(ii)&\ \Bigl(\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\overline{\lambda}\right\rVert}-\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\overline{\mu}\right\rVert}\Bigr)c_{\mu\nu}^{\lambda}=\sum_{\zeta\cover\mu}c_{\zeta\nu}^{\lambda}a_{\zeta\mu}\Bigl(\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\overline{\zeta}\right\rVert}-\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\overline{\mu}\right\rVert}\Bigr)-\sum_{\zeta\coveredby\lambda}a_{\lambda\zeta}c_{\mu\nu}^{\zeta}\Bigl(\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\overline{\lambda}\right\rVert}-\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\overline{\zeta}\right\rVert}\Bigr).\end{split}

(3.17)

(2)

The LR coefficient admits the following formula

\displaystyle c_{\mu\nu}^{\lambda}=\sum_{\lambda\supseteq\zeta\supseteq\mu,\nu}b^{\prime}_{\lambda\zeta}b_{\zeta\mu}b_{\zeta\nu}.

(3.18)

In particular, if
\lambda
does not contain
\mu
and
\nu
, then
c_{\mu\nu}^{\lambda}=0
.∎

Note that by comparing degrees, we have
c_{\mu\nu}^{\lambda}=0
if
|\lambda|>|\mu|+|\nu|
.

3.3
Weighted Sum Formula for LR coefficients

As in the case of binomial coefficients, the recursion formula for LR coefficients give rise to a weighted sum formula.
This formula is new in all cases.

Theorem 3.7

(
Theorem

E
, Weighted Sum Formula for LR Coefficients)
.

The LR coefficient admits the following weighted sum formula

c_{\mu\nu}^{\lambda}=\sum_{\bm{\zeta}\in\mathfrak{C}_{\lambda\mu}}\wt_{\nu}^{\mathrm{LR}}(\bm{\zeta})\prod_{i=0}^{k-1}a_{\bm{\zeta}_{i}\bm{\zeta}_{i+1}},

(3.19)

where
\bm{\zeta}=(\bm{\zeta}_{0},\dots,\bm{\zeta}_{k})
and the weight
\wt_{\nu}^{\mathrm{LR}}
is defined as

\wt_{\nu}^{\mathrm{LR}}(\bm{\zeta})\coloneqq\sum_{j=0}^{k}\frac{\displaystyle\prod_{0\leqslant i\leqslant k-1}\Bigl(\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\overline{\bm{\zeta}_{i}}\right\rVert}-\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\overline{\bm{\zeta}_{i+1}}\right\rVert}\Bigr)}{\displaystyle\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leqslant i\leqslant k\\
i\neq j\end{subarray}}\Bigl(\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\overline{\bm{\zeta}_{j}}\right\rVert}-\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\overline{\bm{\zeta}_{i}}\right\rVert}\Bigr)}b_{\bm{\zeta}_{j}\nu}.

(3.20)

Proof.

Temporarily denote by
\overline{c}_{\mu\nu}^{\lambda}
the sum on the RHS of
Eq.

3.19
. We will verify that they satisfy the recursion
Eq.

3.17
.

When
\lambda=\mu
, the sum is over the single chain
\bm{\zeta}=(\lambda)
, then we have
c_{\lambda\nu}^{\lambda}=b_{\lambda\nu}
.

The RHS of
Eq.

3.17
(ii), divided by
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\overline{\lambda}\right\rVert}-\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\overline{\mu}\right\rVert}
and with
c_{\mu\nu}^{\lambda}
replaced by
\overline{c}_{\mu\nu}^{\lambda}
, is

\displaystyle\mathrel{\phantom{=}}\sum_{\xi\cover\mu}\overline{c}_{\xi\nu}^{\lambda}a_{\xi\mu}\frac{\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\overline{\xi}\right\rVert}-\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\overline{\mu}\right\rVert}}{\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\overline{\lambda}\right\rVert}-\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\overline{\mu}\right\rVert}}-\sum_{\xi\coveredby\lambda}a_{\lambda\xi}\overline{c}_{\mu\nu}^{\xi}\frac{\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\overline{\lambda}\right\rVert}-\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\overline{\xi}\right\rVert}}{\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\overline{\lambda}\right\rVert}-\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\overline{\mu}\right\rVert}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\xi\cover\mu}\sum_{\bm{\zeta}^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{C}_{\lambda\xi}}\wt_{\nu}^{\mathrm{LR}}(\bm{\zeta}^{\prime})\prod_{i=0}^{k-2}a_{\bm{\zeta}_{i}\bm{\zeta}_{i+1}}a_{\xi\mu}\frac{\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\overline{\xi}\right\rVert}-\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\overline{\mu}\right\rVert}}{\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\overline{\lambda}\right\rVert}-\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\overline{\mu}\right\rVert}}

\displaystyle\mathrel{\phantom{=}}-\sum_{\xi\coveredby\lambda}\sum_{\bm{\zeta}^{\prime\prime}\in\mathfrak{C}_{\xi\mu}}\wt_{\nu}^{\mathrm{LR}}(\bm{\zeta}^{\prime\prime})\prod_{i=1}^{k-1}a_{\bm{\zeta}_{i}\bm{\zeta}_{i+1}}a_{uy}\frac{\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\overline{\lambda}\right\rVert}-\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\overline{\xi}\right\rVert}}{\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\overline{\lambda}\right\rVert}-\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\overline{\mu}\right\rVert}},

where

\bm{\zeta}^{\prime}=(\bm{\zeta}_{0}=\lambda\cover\cdots\cover\bm{\zeta}_{k-1}=\xi)\in\mathfrak{C}_{\lambda\xi},\quad\bm{\zeta}^{\prime\prime}=(\bm{\zeta}_{1}=\xi\cover\cdots\cover\bm{\zeta}_{k}=\mu)\in\mathfrak{C}_{\xi\mu}.

Extend
\bm{\zeta}^{\prime}
and
\bm{\zeta}^{\prime\prime}
to

\bm{\zeta}_{0}=\lambda\cover\cdots\cover\bm{\zeta}_{k-1}=\xi\cover\bm{\zeta}_{k}=\mu\quad\text{and}\quad\bm{\zeta}_{0}=\lambda\cover\bm{\zeta}_{1}=\xi\cover\cdots\cover\bm{\zeta}_{k}=\mu

respectively.
Then the two double sums can be viewed as summing over
\mathfrak{C}_{\lambda\mu}
.
Note that both
\displaystyle\prod_{i=0}^{k-2}a_{\bm{\zeta}_{i}\bm{\zeta}_{i+1}}\cdot a_{\xi\mu}
and
\displaystyle\prod_{i=1}^{k-1}a_{\bm{\zeta}_{i}\bm{\zeta}_{i+1}}\cdot a_{\lambda\xi}
are now written as
\displaystyle\prod_{i=0}^{k-1}a_{\bm{\zeta}_{i}\bm{\zeta}_{i+1}}
.
Hence we get

\sum_{\bm{\zeta}\in\mathfrak{C}_{\lambda\mu}}\left(\wt_{\nu}^{\mathrm{LR}}(\bm{\zeta}_{0},\dots,\bm{\zeta}_{k-1})\frac{\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\overline{\bm{\zeta}_{k-1}}\right\rVert}-\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\overline{\bm{\zeta}_{k}}\right\rVert}}{\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\overline{\bm{\zeta}_{0}}\right\rVert}-\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\overline{\bm{\zeta}_{k}}\right\rVert}}-\wt_{\nu}^{\mathrm{LR}}(\bm{\zeta}_{1},\dots,\bm{\zeta}_{k})\frac{\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\overline{\bm{\zeta}_{0}}\right\rVert}-\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\overline{\bm{\zeta}_{1}}\right\rVert}}{\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\overline{\bm{\zeta}_{0}}\right\rVert}-\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\overline{\bm{\zeta}_{k}}\right\rVert}}\right)\prod_{i=0}^{k-1}a_{\bm{\zeta}_{i}\bm{\zeta}_{i+1}}.

Hence it suffices to show

\wt_{\nu}^{\mathrm{LR}}(\bm{\zeta}_{0},\dots,\bm{\zeta}_{k})=\wt_{\nu}^{\mathrm{LR}}(\bm{\zeta}_{0},\dots,\bm{\zeta}_{k-1})\frac{\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\overline{\bm{\zeta}_{k-1}}\right\rVert}-\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\overline{\bm{\zeta}_{k}}\right\rVert}}{\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\overline{\bm{\zeta}_{0}}\right\rVert}-\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\overline{\bm{\zeta}_{k}}\right\rVert}}-\wt_{\nu}^{\mathrm{LR}}(\bm{\zeta}_{1},\dots,\bm{\zeta}_{k})\frac{\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\overline{\bm{\zeta}_{0}}\right\rVert}-\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\overline{\bm{\zeta}_{1}}\right\rVert}}{\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\overline{\bm{\zeta}_{0}}\right\rVert}-\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\overline{\bm{\zeta}_{k}}\right\rVert}},

for any chain
\bm{\zeta}=(\bm{\zeta}_{0},\dots,\bm{\zeta}_{k})\in\mathfrak{C}_{\lambda\mu}
.
The RHS is

\displaystyle\mathrel{\phantom{=}}\prod_{i=0}^{k-2}\Bigl(\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\overline{\bm{\zeta}_{i}}\right\rVert}-\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\overline{\bm{\zeta}_{i+1}}\right\rVert}\Bigr)\cdot\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\frac{b_{\bm{\zeta}_{j}\nu}}{\displaystyle\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leqslant i\leqslant k-1\\
i\neq j\end{subarray}}\Bigl(\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\overline{\bm{\zeta}_{j}}\right\rVert}-\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\overline{\bm{\zeta}_{i}}\right\rVert}\Bigr)}\cdot\frac{\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\overline{\bm{\zeta}_{k-1}}\right\rVert}-\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\overline{\bm{\zeta}_{k}}\right\rVert}}{\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\overline{\bm{\zeta}_{0}}\right\rVert}-\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\overline{\bm{\zeta}_{k}}\right\rVert}}

\displaystyle\mathrel{\phantom{=}}-\prod_{i=1}^{k-1}\Bigl(\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\overline{\bm{\zeta}_{i}}\right\rVert}-\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\overline{\bm{\zeta}_{i+1}}\right\rVert}\Bigr)\cdot\sum_{j=1}^{k}\frac{b_{\bm{\zeta}_{j}\nu}}{\displaystyle\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leqslant i\leqslant k\\
i\neq j\end{subarray}}\Bigl(\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\overline{\bm{\zeta}_{j}}\right\rVert}-\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\overline{\bm{\zeta}_{i}}\right\rVert}\Bigr)}\cdot\frac{\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\overline{\bm{\zeta}_{0}}\right\rVert}-\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\overline{\bm{\zeta}_{1}}\right\rVert}}{\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\overline{\bm{\zeta}_{0}}\right\rVert}-\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\overline{\bm{\zeta}_{k}}\right\rVert}},

which has a common factor
\displaystyle\prod_{i=0}^{k-1}\Bigl(\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\overline{\bm{\zeta}_{i}}\right\rVert}-\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\overline{\bm{\zeta}_{i+1}}\right\rVert}\Bigr)

Lemma 4.1

Fix a partition

\displaystyle\prod_{s\in\lambda}\left((\lambda_{T(s)}-a_{\lambda}^{\prime}(s)+l_{\lambda}^{\prime}(s)\tau\right)\text{\hskip 10.22217ptand\hskip 10.22217pt}\prod_{s\in\lambda}\left(q^{\lambda_{T(s)}-a_{\lambda}^{\prime}(s)}t^{l_{\lambda}^{\prime}(s)}-1\right)

Proof.

If the box

Suppose

Let

In particular,

Then the products have the following factors

\displaystyle\prod_{k=1}^{n}\prod_{i=1}^{m_{k}}\left(\lambda_{k}-(m_{n}+\dots+m_{k+1})-i+1\right)\text{\quad and\quad}\prod_{k=1}^{n}\prod_{i=1}^{m_{k}}\left(q^{\lambda_{k}-(m_{n}+\dots+m_{k+1})-i+1}-1\right)

Hence we have

\displaystyle\lambda_{k}\geqslant m_{n}+\dots+m_{k},\quad 1\leqslant k\leqslant n.

We claim that the inequalities above must all be equalities.
Proceeding by contradiction, let

Consider the first column whose first row is labeled by

However, there are at least

In other words, the two products are non-vanishing if and only if the first row of

6.1
Positivity and Inequality

6.1.1
Jack polynomials

In this subsection, we consider the monic Jack polynomials
P_{\lambda}(x;\tau)
and the binomial coefficients
\binom{\lambda}{\mu}=\binom{\lambda}{\mu}_{\tau}
of family
{A\mathrm{J}}
.
Recall that the cone of positivity
\mathbb{F}_{\geqslant 0}
is defined by
Eq.

2.3
.

Also recall that for partitions
\lambda
and
\mu
(as before, written as
n
-tuples), we say
\lambda

weakly dominates
(or,
weakly majorizes
)
\mu
, if
\sum_{i=1}^{r}\lambda_{i}\geqslant\sum_{i=1}^{r}\mu_{i}
, for
1\leqslant r\leqslant n
; if, in addition,
|\lambda|=|\mu|
, we say
\lambda

dominates
(or,
majorizes
)
\mu
.

Let
\bm{1}=(1,\dots,1)
(
n
times).
The following binomial formula is proved in
Okounkov and Olshanski [1997a]
.

\displaystyle\frac{P_{\lambda}(x+\bm{1};\tau)}{P_{\lambda}(\bm{1};\tau)}=\sum_{\mu\subseteq\lambda}\binom{\lambda}{\mu}\frac{P_{\mu}(x;\tau)}{P_{\mu}(\bm{1};\tau)}.

(6.1)

The normalization
P_{\lambda}(x;\tau)/P_{\lambda}(\bm{1};\tau)
is sometimes called
unital
, as it maps
\bm{1}
to
1
.

As a direct application of the monotonicity of binomial coefficients (
Theorem

B
), we have the following duality between the containment order on partitions and Jack positivity on symmetric functions.

Theorem 6.1

(
Theorem

A
)
.

The following statements are equivalent:

(1)

\lambda
contains
\mu
.

(2)

The difference
\displaystyle\frac{P_{\lambda}(x+\bm{1};\tau)}{P_{\lambda}(\bm{1};\tau)}-\frac{P_{\mu}(x+\bm{1};\tau)}{P_{\mu}(\bm{1};\tau)}
is
Jack positive
, namely, can be written as an
\mathbb{F}_{\geqslant 0}
-combination of Jack polynomials
P_{\nu}(x;\tau)
.

(3)

For any fixed
\tau_{0}\in[0,\infty]
, the difference
\displaystyle\frac{P_{\lambda}(x+\bm{1};\tau_{0})}{P_{\lambda}(\bm{1};\tau_{0})}-\frac{P_{\mu}(x+\bm{1};\tau_{0})}{P_{\mu}(\bm{1};\tau_{0})}
is
\tau_{0}
-
Jack positive
, namely, can be written as an
\mathbb{R}_{\geqslant 0}
-combination of Jack polynomials

.

Proof.

Note that
P_{\lambda}(\bm{1};\tau)\in\mathbb{F}_{>0}
by
[
Macdonald, 2015
, VI. (10.20)]
or
Eq.

2.10
, and
P_{\lambda}(\bm{1};\tau_{0})>0
(it suffices to check for
\tau_{0}=0
and
\infty
).

We first show that (1)
\implies
(2).
If
\lambda\supseteq\mu
, then by the binomial formula
Eq.

6.1
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{P_{\lambda}(x+\bm{1};\tau)}{P_{\lambda}(\bm{1};\tau)}-\frac{P_{\mu}(x+\bm{1};\tau)}{P_{\mu}(\bm{1};\tau)}=\sum_{\nu\subseteq\lambda}\left(\binom{\lambda}{\nu}-\binom{\mu}{\nu}\right)\frac{P_{\nu}(x;\tau)}{P_{\nu}(\bm{1};\tau)}.

By
Theorem

, the coefficient
\binom{\lambda}{\nu}-\binom{\mu}{\nu}
lies in
\mathbb{F}_{\geqslant 0}
.

(2)
\implies
(3) is clear since functions in
\mathbb{F}_{\geqslant 0}
have non-negative evaluation at
\tau_{0}\in[0,\infty]
. (When
\tau=0
and
\infty
,
\binom{\lambda}{\nu}-\binom{\mu}{\nu}
is finite.)

(3)
\implies
(1):
Assume that
\lambda
does not contain
\mu
, then

. Since

forms an

-basis for

, the difference would contain a term

-Jack positive.
∎

.

The following statements are equivalent:

(1)

\lambda

\mu

(2)

(6.2)

where

We set

In the case of

Theorem 6.3
.

\lambda

\mu

\displaystyle\frac{p_{\lambda}(x+\bm{1})}{p_{\lambda}(\bm{1})}-\frac{p_{\mu}(x+\bm{1})}{p_{\mu}(\bm{1})}

\{\,p_{\nu^{\prime}}\mathclose{}\nonscript\;|\penalty\nonscript\;\mathopen{}\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{n}\,\}

Proof.

Write
b_{\lambda}(x)\coloneqq p_{\lambda}(x)/p_{\lambda}(\bm{1})
, then
b_{\lambda}(\bm{1})=n^{\ell(\lambda)}
. (Note that
(b_{\lambda^{\prime}})_{\lambda\in\mathcal{P}_{n}}

Assume that
\lambda
contains
\mu
. We use induction on
\ell(\lambda)
.
First assume
\ell(\lambda)=1
, then by the classical binomial formulas,

\displaystyle b_{r}(x+\bm{1})=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(x_{i}+1)^{r}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{t=0}^{r}\binom{r}{t}x_{i}^{t}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{t=0}^{r}\binom{r}{t}p_{t}(x)=\sum_{t=0}^{r}\binom{r}{t}b_{t}(x).

It is well-known that the classical binomial coefficient is positive and monotone, hence if
r\geqslant s
, then
b_{r}(x+\bm{1})-b_{s}(x+\bm{1})
is power sum positive.

For the inductive step, let
L>\lambda_{1}
and

so that
(L,\lambda)
and
(M,\mu)
are partitions.
Assume that the pair
(L,\lambda)\supseteq(M,\mu)
, that is,

and
\lambda\supseteq\mu
. Then

\displaystyle\mathrel{\phantom{=}}b_{(L,\lambda)}(x+\bm{1})-b_{(M,\mu)}(x+\bm{1})

\displaystyle=b_{L}(x+\bm{1})b_{\lambda}(x+\bm{1})-b_{M}(x+\bm{1})b_{\mu}(x+\bm{1})

\displaystyle=\left(b_{L}(x+\bm{1})-b_{M}(x+\bm{1})\right)b_{\lambda}(x+\bm{1})+b_{M}(x+\bm{1})\left(b_{\lambda}(x+\bm{1})-b_{\mu}(x+\bm{1})\right),

which is power sum positive by the induction base, the induction hypothesis and the fact that power sum positive polynomials are closed under taking products.

Conversely, assume that

Letting

\displaystyle b_{\lambda}(x+\bm{1})=\prod_{k=1}^{l}b_{\lambda_{k}}(x+\bm{1})=\prod_{k=1}^{l}\sum_{\eta_{k}=0}^{\lambda_{k}}\binom{\lambda_{k}}{\eta_{k}}b_{\eta_{k}}(x)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leqslant\eta_{1}\leqslant\lambda_{1}\\[3.01389pt]
\cdots\\[3.01389pt]
0\leqslant\eta_{\ell}\leqslant\lambda_{l}\end{subarray}}\prod_{k=1}^{l}\binom{\lambda_{k}}{\eta_{k}}b_{\eta_{k}}(x)

Define

\displaystyle b_{\lambda}(x+\bm{1})=\sum_{\nu\subseteq\lambda}\left(\sum_{\eta\sim\nu}\prod_{k=1}^{l}\binom{\lambda_{k}}{\eta_{k}}\right)b_{\nu}(x),

Expansion positivity has long been an intriguing question in algebraic combinatorics and representation theory, which may indicate an underlying combinatorial or representation-theoretic structure.
For example, the product of Schur polynomials is Schur positive, by the famous Littlewood–Richardson rule.
On the one hand, the coefficients are counting Littlewood–Richardson tableaux; on the other hand, this product corresponds to the tensor product of irreducible

Other examples of expansion positivity include but are not limited to: the monomial positivity (and integrality under suitable normalization) of Jack polynomials

and Macdonald polynomials
