Spectral supersaturation: Triangles and bowties
Yongtao Li, Lihua Feng, Yuejian Peng

Abstract
A classical result of Erdős and Rademacher (1955) demonstrates a fundamental supersaturation phenomenon in extremal combinatorics: every graph on n n vertices with more than ⌊ n 2 / 4 ⌋ \lfloor n^{2}/4\rfloor edges contains at least ⌊ n / 2 ⌋ \lfloor n/2\rfloor triangles. Let λ ⁡ ( G ) \lambda(G) be the spectral radius of the adjacency matrix of a graph G G . Recently, Ning and Zhai (2023) proved that every n n -vertex graph G G with λ ⁡ ( G ) ≥ ⌊ n 2 / 4 ⌋ \lambda(G)\geq\sqrt{\lfloor n^{2}/4\rfloor} contains at least ⌊ n / 2 ⌋ − 1 \lfloor n/2\rfloor-1 triangles, unless G G is a balanced complete bipartite graph K ⌈ n 2 ⌉ , ⌊ n 2 ⌋ K_{\lceil\frac{n}{2}\rceil,\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\rfloor} . The aim of this paper is two-fold. Using a different approach which we term the supersaturation-stability method, we prove a stability variant of the Ning–Zhai result by showing that such a graph G G contains at least n − 3 n-3 triangles if no vertex lies in all triangles of G G . This bound is the best possible and it could also be viewed as a spectral analogue of a theorem of Xiao and Katona (2021), which guarantees n − 2 n-2 triangles under the assumption that e ⁡ ( G ) > ⌊ n 2 / 4 ⌋ e(G)>\lfloor n^{2}/4\rfloor and no vertex is in all triangles of G G . The second part concerns with the spectral supersaturation for the bowtie, which consists of two triangles sharing a vertex. Erdős, Füredi, Gould and Gunderson (1995) proved that every n n -vertex graph with more than ⌊ n 2 / 4 ⌋ + 1 \lfloor n^{2}/4\rfloor+1 edges contains a bowtie. The spectral supersaturation problem has not been investigated for non-color-critical substructures in graphs with given order. We give the first such result by counting bowties. Let K ⌈ n 2 ⌉ , ⌊ n 2 ⌋ + 2 K_{\lceil\frac{n}{2}\rceil,\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\rfloor}^{+2} be the graph obtained from K ⌈ n 2 ⌉ , ⌊ n 2 ⌋ K_{\lceil\frac{n}{2}\rceil,\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\rfloor} by embedding two disjoint edges in the vertex part of size ⌈ n 2 ⌉ \lceil\frac{n}{2}\rceil . We show that if n ≥ 8.8 × 10 6 n\geq 8.8\times 10^{6} and λ ⁡ ( G ) ≥ λ ⁡ ( K ⌈ n 2 ⌉ , ⌊ n 2 ⌋ + 2 ) \lambda(G)\geq\lambda(K_{\lceil\frac{n}{2}\rceil,\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\rfloor}^{+2}) , then G G has at least ⌊ n 2 ⌋ \lfloor\frac{n}{2}\rfloor bowties, and K ⌈ n 2 ⌉ , ⌊ n 2 ⌋ + 2 K_{\lceil\frac{n}{2}\rceil,\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\rfloor}^{+2} is the unique spectral extremal graph. This gives a spectral correspondence of a result of Kang, Makai and Pikhurko (2020). The method developed in our paper could be helpful in establishing the spectral results for counting other substructures, even for non-color-critical graphs.



Key words:
Supersaturation; spectral radius; triangle; bowtie.

Introduction

Extremal combinatorics is one of the essential branches of
discrete mathematics and it has experienced an impressive growth
in the last few decades.
Generally speaking, it deals with the problem of determining or estimating the maximum or minimum possible size of a combinatorial object that satisfies certain restrictions.
Such types of problems are often related to other mathematical areas, such as theoretical computer science, coding theory,
discrete geometry,
ergodic theory and harmonic analysis.
One of the most significant problems in extremal combinatorics is the famous Turán type problem,
which can be roughly formulated as follows:
given a ‘forbidden’ substructure,
what is the maximum number of edges in a graph
that does not contain the specific substructure.
The first study of the Turán type problem dates back perhaps to a classical result of Mantel,
which asserts that each graph on
n
vertices
with more than
\lfloor n^{2}/4\rfloor
edges must contain a triangle.
The bound is tight as witnessed by
a balanced complete bipartite graph
T_{n,2}
.
The Turán type problem could be viewed as
a starting point of extremal graph
theory and it has inspired extensive research in the past hundred years.
A large number of results have
been proved for a range of different substructures using a wide variety of different methods;
see, e.g.,
[
6
]
.

A result of Rademacher (unpublished, see Erdős
[
17
,
19
]
) shows that
any
n
-vertex graph with more than
\lfloor n^{2}/4\rfloor
edges has at least
\lfloor n/2\rfloor
triangles.
This bound can be achieved
by adding an edge to the larger vertex part of
T_{n,2}
.
This phenomenon is known as ‘supersaturation’ in the literature.
Subsequently,
Lovász and Simonovits
[
37
,
38
]
proved that
if
1\leq t<{n}/{2}
is an integer and
G
is an
n
-vertex graph with

e(G)\geq\lfloor{n^{2}}/{4}\rfloor+t
,
then
G
contains at least
t\lfloor{n}/{2}\rfloor

triangles.
An analogous question for other classes of graphs
has been studied extensively in extremal graph theory.
We refer the readers to
the works of Mubayi
[
41
]
, and
Pikhurko and Yilma
[
53
]

for counting color-critical graphs,
such as cliques, odd cycles, etc.
The stability results on Erdős–Rademacher’s problem
can be found in
[
57
,
35
,
5
]
.

Another interesting research direction
studies which other substructures
must appear in a graph with more than
\lfloor n^{2}/4\rfloor
edges.
Apart from the number of triangles,
a similar extremal graph problem concerns the minimum
number of edges that
are contained in a triangle.
For instance, every
n
-vertex graph with more than

\lfloor{n^{2}}/{4}\rfloor
edges has
at least
2\lfloor n/2\rfloor+1
edges contained in triangles; see, e.g.,

[
20
,
23
,
24
]
.
Other examples are results on finding many cliques
that share one or more vertices and edges,
such as,
every graph on
n

\lfloor{n^{2}}/{4}\rfloor

.
Moreover, each

,

2
Main results

We are interested in finding
the maximum number of substructures under certain spectral conditions.
These types of questions arise in a wide variety of contexts.
In this study, we will present two main results on
the spectral stability and supersaturation
by counting the number of triangles as well as bowties.

2.1
A stability of Ning–Zhai’s result

The extremal graph in Erdős–Rademacher’s theorem

2.3
Our approach, organization and nonation

Our approach.

We point out that
the original motivation is to establish Theorems
2.2

and
2.4
, since the extremal graphs in these problems
are constructed from a bipartite Turán graph
T_{n,2}

by embedding two disjoint edges.
Our proofs of Theorems
2.2

and
2.4
are quite different from those of Theorems
1.1
and
2.3
,
the latter of which employ the Perron eigenvector
in conjunction with the
2

The main idea in the proofs of our paper is based on the use of
the supersaturation-stability method
[
32
]
.
Roughly speaking, every graph with few triangles can be made bipartite
by removing a small number of edges;
see, e.g., Theorem
3.4

This seems to be of independent interest.
To prove Theorem
2.4
,
the spectral techniques due to
Cioabă, Feng, Tait and Zhang
[
12
]

will also be applied for our purpose.
As was the case in above-mentioned works
[

,
53
,
35
,
26

it is a traditional component to use the celebrated graph removal lemma
to deal with the extremal graph problems on counting substructures.
It is an advantage that all the proofs of our results make no use of the graph removal lemma,

To avoid unnecessary computations,
we did not make much effort to optimize the bound on the order of the spectral extremal graph in our arguments.
Moreover, we are optimistic that the supersaturation-stability method used in our paper
may be useful in obtaining the spectral results for counting other graphs (even for non-color-critical graphs such as the wheel graph

and the Petersen graph among others).

Notation.

for a simple undirected

and edge set

If

whose edges are those edges of

For simplicity, we denote

and we use

to denote

.
Let

denote the subgraph induced by

.
In addition, we write

for the bipartite subgraph of

whose edges have one endpoint in

and the other in

for the number of edges of

.
Let

be the set of vertices adjacent to a vertex

, and let

be the degree of

.
Moreover, we write
N_{S}(v)=N(v)\cap S

for the set of neighbors of

in

,
and we denote
d_{S}(v)=|N_{S}(v)|
for simplicity.
Let

be the number of triangles in

.

3
Preliminaries

3.1
A spectral triangle counting lemma

To begin with, we introduce the following spectral result for counting triangles.

Lemma 3.1

(See
[
8
,
12
,
50
]
)
.

Let
G
be a graph with
m
edges. Then

t(G)\geq\frac{\lambda\bigl(\lambda^{2}-m\bigr)}{3}.

The equality holds if and only if
G
is a complete bipartite graph.

This result goes back to an old theorem of Nosal
[
52
]
, which asserts that every graph
G
with
m
edges and
\lambda(G)>\sqrt{m}
contains a triangle; see
[
43
,
49
]
for related extensions.
The inequality in Lemma
3.1
can be recast as three different versions:

\lambda^{3}\leq 3t+m\lambda\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad{t\geq\frac{1}{3}\lambda(\lambda^{2}-m)}\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad m\geq\lambda^{2}-\frac{3t}{\lambda}.

Lemma
3.1
was initially studied by
Bollobás and Nikiforov
[
8
]
,
and it was recently revisited by Cioabă, Feng,
Tait and Zhang
[
12
]
as well as Ning and Zhai
[
50
]
.
Crucially, Lemma
3.1
tells us that if

\lambda(G)\geq{n}/{2}
and
t(G)\leq kn
, then
G
has at least
n^{2}/4-6k
edges.
This provides the expected number of edges
for forcing a graph to be close to bipartite.

3.2
The Moon–Moser inequality

Next, we present a result of Moon and Moser
[
40
, p. 285]
.

Theorem 3.2

(Moon–Moser)
.

Let
G
be a graph on
n
vertices with
m
edges.
Then

t(G)\geq\frac{4m}{3n}\left(m-\frac{n^{2}}{4}\right).

Two proofs of Theorem
3.2

can be found in
[
6
, p. 297]
and
[
39
, p. 443]
.
For convenience of the readers, we provide a different proof as follows.

Proof.

For each
v\in V(G)
, we denote
N_{v}=N(v)
and
N_{v}^{c}=V(G)\setminus N_{v}
. Then

\sum_{w\in N_{v}}d(w)=2e(N_{v})+e(N_{v},N_{v}^{c})=e(N_{v})+m-e(N_{v}^{c}).

Note that
3t(G)=\sum_{v\in V}e(N_{v})
.
Summing the above equalities, we get

\sum_{v\in V}\sum_{w\in N_{v}}d(w)=3t(G)+mn-\sum_{v\in V}e(N_{v}^{c}).

On the other hand,
we obtain from Cauchy–Schwarz’s inequality that

\sum_{v\in V}\sum_{w\in N_{v}}d(w)=\sum_{w\in V}d(w)^{2}\geq\frac{4m^{2}}{n}.

Combining the two inequalities,
we have

3t(G)\geq\frac{4m^{2}}{n}-mn
, as needed.
∎

Theorem
3.2

gives a lower bound on the number of triangles in a graph
with size greater than
n^{2}/4
.
As a byproduct of this result,
we see that an
n
-vertex graph with
n^{2}/4+1
edges
contains at least
n/3
triangles. Moreover,
we can obtain the following supersaturation for triangles,
which will be used in our proof of Theorem
2.4
.

Corollary 3.3
.

If
\varepsilon>0
and
G
is a graph
on
n
vertices with

e(G)\geq\frac{n^{2}}{4}+\varepsilon n^{2},

then
G
contains more than
\frac{\varepsilon}{3}n^{3}
triangles.

3.3
The supersaturation-stability

For a real number
\varepsilon>0
,
we say that a graph
G
is
\varepsilon
-far from being bipartite

if
G^{\prime}
is not bipartite
for every subgraph
G^{\prime}
of
G
with

e(G^{\prime})>e(G)-\varepsilon
.
That is to say, if
G
is
\varepsilon
-far from being bipartite,
then no matter how we delete less than
\varepsilon
edges from
G
,
the resulting graph is always non-bipartite.
Consequently, we must remove at least
\varepsilon
edges from

G
to make it bipartite.
The following theorem was proved by Balogh et al.
[
4
]
.

Theorem 3.4

(See
[
4
]
)
.

If
G
is
\varepsilon
-far from being bipartite, then

t(G)\geq\frac{n}{6}\left(m+\varepsilon-\frac{n^{2}}{4}\right).

Theorem
3.4
can imply the supersaturation on triangles
for graphs with less than
n^{2}/4
edges.
Namely, if
G
is an
n
-vertex graph with

n^{2}/4-q
edges, and
G
has less than
kn
triangles, then

G
is not
(6k+q)
-far from being bipartite.
Consequently, we can remove less than

6k+q
edges from
G
to make it bipartite, i.e.,
G
has a large bipartite subgraph with more than
n^{2}/4-2q-6k
edges.
This result enables us to avoid the use of the triangle removal lemma
so that we can get rid of the condition where the order
n
is sufficiently large.
We refer to

[
32
]
for some related applications.
As a matter of fact, finding a large bipartite subgraph
plays an important role in the proofs of
Theorems
2.2
and
2.4
.

3.4
Computations for extremal graphs

The extremal graphs in our problem are
not symmetric,
and a lot of calculations on the spectral radii of graphs are needed.
We give the following basic lemmas without proofs.

Lemma 3.5
.

Suppose that
f(x)
is a real polynomial function with the largest root

\gamma
in

(\frac{n}{2}-1,\frac{n}{2}+1)
, and
f(x)
increases monotonically in
(\frac{n}{2}-1,\frac{n}{2}+1)
.
If
x_{0}\in(\frac{n}{2}-1,\frac{n}{2}+1)
such that
f(x_{0})>0
, then
\gamma<x_{0}
;
if
f(x_{0})<0
, then
x_{0}<\gamma
.

Lemma 3.6
.

Let
f(x)
and
g(x)
be real polynomial functions with largest roots
\beta
and
\gamma
, respectively.
If
\beta,\gamma\in(\frac{n}{2}-1,\frac{n}{2}+1)
,

f(x)
and
g(x)
are increasing monotonically,
and
f(x)>g(x)
for any
x\in(\frac{n}{2}-1,\frac{n}{2}+1)
,
then
\beta<\gamma
.

The next lemma gives
a lower bound on the spectral radius of

K_{\lceil\frac{n}{2}\rceil,\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\rfloor}^{+2}

Lemma 3.7
.

(a) If

Furthermore, we get

\lambda^{2}(K_{\lceil\frac{n}{2}\rceil,\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\rfloor}^{+2})>\lfloor n^{2}/4\rfloor+4.

Proof.

(a) Let
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n})^{\top}
be the Perron eigenvector corresponding to
\lambda(K_{\frac{n}{2},\frac{n}{2}}^{+2})
.
We partition the vertex set of
K_{\frac{n}{2},\frac{n}{2}}^{+2}
as
\Pi

V(K_{\frac{n}{2},\frac{n}{2}}^{+2})=X_{1}\cup X_{2}\cup Y,

where
X_{1}=\{u_{1},u_{2},u_{3},u_{4}\}
forms a matching of size two,

X_{1}\cup X_{2}
and
Y
are partite sets of

Compared with the neighborhoods, we can see that
x_{u_{1}}=\cdots=x_{u_{4}}

all the coordinates of the vector

corresponding to the vertices of
X_{2}
are equal
(the coordinates of the vertices of

are equal).
Without loss of generality, we may assume that

for each

\begin{cases}\lambda a=a+\frac{n}{2}c,\\
\lambda b=\frac{n}{2}c,\\
\lambda c=4a+(\frac{n}{2}-4)b.\end{cases}

Thus,
\lambda(K_{\frac{n}{2},\frac{n}{2}}^{+2})
is the largest eigenvalue of

B_{\Pi}=\begin{bmatrix}1&0&\frac{n}{2}\\
0&0&\frac{n}{2}\\
4&\frac{n}{2}-4&0\end{bmatrix}.

By calculation, we know that
\lambda(K_{\frac{n}{2},\frac{n}{2}}^{+2})
is the largest root of

f(x)=\det(xI_{3}-B_{\Pi})=x^{3}-x^{2}-(n^{2}x)/4+n^{2}/4-2n.

\Pi
(into three parts)
is called an

, and

is called the

of
\Pi
.
For a connected graph with an equitable partition
\Pi
, the spectral radius of the graph equals the spectral radius of the quotient matrix
B_{\Pi}
.

(b) For odd
n
,
we partition the vertex set of
K_{\frac{n+1}{2},\frac{n-1}{2}}^{+2}

as
\Pi^{\prime}
:

V(K_{\frac{n+1}{2},\frac{n-1}{2}}^{+2})=X_{1}\cup X_{2}\cup Y,

where
X_{1}=\{u_{1},u_{2},u_{3},u_{4}\}
spans
two disjoint edges,
X_{1}\cup X_{2}
and
Y

are partite sets of
K_{\frac{n+1}{2},\frac{n-1}{2}}

satisfying

and
|Y|=\frac{n-1}{2}
. Then
a similar argument yields that

\lambda(K_{\frac{n+1}{2},\frac{n-1}{2}}^{+2})
is the largest eigenvalue of

0&0&\frac{n-1}{2}\\
4&\frac{n+1}{2}-4&0\end{bmatrix}.

Thus,
\lambda(K_{\frac{n+1}{2},\frac{n-1}{2}}^{+2})

is the largest root of

g(x)=\det(xI_{3}-B_{\Pi^{\prime}})=x^{3}-x^{2}+x/4-(n^{2}x)/4+n^{2}/4-2n+7/4.

Moreover, it is easy to check that

f(\sqrt{n^{2}/4+4})=2(-2-n+\sqrt{16+n^{2}})<0

and

g(\sqrt{(n^{2}-1)/4+4})=2(-1-n+\sqrt{15+n^{2}})<0.

By Lemma
3.5
, we get

\sqrt{\lfloor n^{2}/4\rfloor+4}<\lambda(K_{\lceil\frac{n}{2}\rceil,\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\rfloor}^{+2})
.
This completes the proof.
∎

\lfloor{n}/{2}\rfloor-2\leq s\leq\lceil{n}/{2}\rceil+2
and
t=n-s
.
If
G
has at most
n-4
triangles, then

\lambda(G)<\lambda(T_{n,2})
.

4
Proof of Theorem
2.2

We now provide the proof of Theorem
2.2
.
Assume that
G
is an
n
-vertex graph with
\lambda(G)\geq\lambda(T_{n,2})
and
\tau_{3}(G)\geq 2
.
Suppose on the contrary that

G
has at most
n-4
triangles.
In the sequel, we will deduce a contradiction.
Since
\lambda(T_{n,2})=\sqrt{\lfloor n^{2}/4\rfloor}
, we get

\lambda(G)>\frac{n-1}{2}
.
It follows from Lemma
3.1
that

e(G)\geq\lambda^{2}-\frac{3t}{\lambda}>\lambda^{2}-\frac{6t}{n-1}\geq\left\lfloor\frac{n^{2}}{4}\right\rfloor-\frac{6(n-4)}{n-1}.

Note that
e(G)
must be a positive integer. Then

e(G)\geq\left\lfloor\frac{n^{2}}{4}\right\rfloor-5.

Lemma 4.1
.

There exists a partition
V(G)=S\cup T

such that
e(S)+e(T)<12
.
Furthermore, we have
e(S,T)\geq\lfloor n^{2}/4\rfloor-16

and
n/2-4\leq|S|,|T|\leq n/2+4
.

Proof.

We claim that
G
is not
12
-far from being bipartite.
Otherwise, Theorem
3.4
implies

t(G)\geq\frac{n}{6}(e(G)+12-\frac{n^{2}}{4})>n
,
which is a contradiction with our assumption that

t(G)\leq n-4
. Therefore, the graph
G
is not

12
-far from being bipartite. In other words, there is a partition of
the vertex set of
G
as
V(G)=S\cup T
such that

e(S)+e(T)<12
. Then

e(S,T)=e(G)-e(S)-e(T)\geq e(G)-11\geq\left\lfloor\frac{n^{2}}{4}\right\rfloor-16.

By the AM-GM inequality, we get

{n}/{2}-4\leq|S|,|T|\leq{n}/{2}+4
.
∎

Lemma 4.2
.

We have that
e(S)+e(T)=2
and the two edges of
G[S]\cup G[T]
are disjoint.

Proof.

The condition
\tau_{3}(G)\geq 2
means that
G
contains at least one triangle.
Then we have
e(S)+e(T)\geq 1
.
Suppose on the contrary that
e(S)+e(T)\neq 2
.
Firstly, if
e(S)=1
or
e(T)=1
, then
all triangles in
G
can be covered by one vertex,
which contradicts with
\tau_{3}(G)\geq 2
.
Now, suppose that
e(S)+e(T)=s
, where
3\leq s\leq 11
.
Observe that each missing cross-edges between
S
and
T

is contained in at most
s
triangles. Then
we have
t(G)\geq s(\frac{n}{2}-4)-16s>n-3
for
n\geq 113
,
which is a contradiction. Thus, we must have

e(S)+e(T)=2
.
Furthermore, the two class-edges in
G[S]\cup G[T]
are
vertex-disjoint in
G
. Otherwise, there is a vertex that covers all triangles of
G
, contradicting with
\tau_{3}(G)\geq 2
.
∎

To save calculations, we give a refinement on Lemma
4.1
.

Lemma 4.3
.

We have
e(S,T)\geq\lfloor n^{2}/4\rfloor-7
and

\lfloor n/2\rfloor-2\leq|S|,|T|\leq\lceil n/2\rceil+2
.

Proof.

In contrast to the proof of Lemma
4.1
,
which heavily relied on Theorem
3.4
,
our current refinement no longer needs to invoke Theorem
3.4
.
By Lemma
4.2
, we get
e(S)+e(T)=2
, and then

e(S,T)=e(G)-e(S)-e(T)\geq\left\lfloor\frac{n^{2}}{4}\right\rfloor-7.

Thus, it follows that

\lfloor{n}/{2}\rfloor-2\leq|S|,|T|\leq\lceil{n}/{2}\rceil+2
, as desired.
∎

From Lemmas
4.2
and
4.3
,
we may assume by symmetry that either
e(S)=2
or
e(S)=e(T)=1
.
Then we get
\lambda(G)<\lambda(T_{n,2})

by using Lemmas
3.8

and
3.9
. This contradicts with the previous assumption.
So we complete the proof.
\square

5
Proof of Theorem
2.4

5.1
An overview of the proof

In this section, we shall prove Theorem
2.4
.
Our solution applies the framework set up by the authors
[
32
]
,
and we need to put much effort into the analysis of the structure
of expected extremal graph.
We always assume that
G
is a graph on
n
vertices with

\lambda(G)\geq\lambda(K_{\lceil\frac{n}{2}\rceil,\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\rfloor}^{+2})

and
G
contains at most
\lfloor n/2\rfloor
bowties.
Among such graphs, we choose
G
as the graph with the maximum spectral radius.
Our goal is to prove that
G

is exactly
the desired extremal graph
K_{\lceil\frac{n}{2}\rceil,\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\rfloor}^{+2}
.
Before starting the proof in detail, we outline the main steps as follows.

①

The first step is to obtain a bipartition of vertices of
G
.
Using a theorem of Alon and Shikhelman
[
2
]
,
we will show that
G

contains less than
10n^{2}
triangles.
Using Lemma
3.1
,
we can get
e(G)>{n^{2}}/{4}-180n
.
Applying the supersaturation-stability in Theorem
3.4
,
we obtain an approximate bipartition of
G
; see Lemma
5.2
.

②

The second step refines the structure of
the desired extremal graph.
We shall prove that
G
has few ‘bad’ vertices that have large degree in their own part and have small degree in the other part.
Moreover, we show
that
G
is nearly a complete bipartite graph whose vertex parts are constant-sized around
{n}/{2}
; see Lemmas
5.3
–
5.10
.

③

Thirdly, we show that
G
has at most

3({n}/{2}+14\sqrt{n})
triangles.
This improves the previous result in the first step.
Using Lemma
3.1
again,
we conclude that there exists a partition
V(G)=S\cup T
such that

e(S,T)\geq\lfloor n^{2}/4\rfloor-8
and
{n}/{2}-2\leq|S|,|T|\leq{n}/{2}+2
; see Lemmas
5.11
–
5.14
.

④

Finally,
we shall prove either
e(S)=2
or
e(T)=2
.
With some calculations, we determine that

G
is the expected spectral extremal graph
K_{\lceil\frac{n}{2}\rceil,\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\rfloor}^{+2}
.

5.2
The approximate structure

Recall that a set of
k
pairwise disjoint edges is
called
a matching of size
k
.
A famous theorem due to Erdős and Gallai
[
18
]

states that if
n\geq\frac{5k+3}{2}
and

G
is an
n
-vertex graph with
e(G)>k(n-k)+{k\choose 2}
,
then
G
contains a matching of size
k+1
.

Assume that
G
has less than

\lfloor{n}/{2}\rfloor
bowties.
Then we can give a bound on the number of triangles in
G
.
Indeed, for each vertex
v\in V(G)
,
the neighborhood
N(v)
does not contain
a matching of size
\sqrt{n}+1
.
Setting
k=\sqrt{n}
and applying the Erdős–Gallai theorem, we get

e(N(v))<kn=n^{3/2}
.
Using the double counting, it follows that

3t(G)=\sum_{v\in V}e(N(v))<n^{5/2}
.
Therefore,
G
contains less than
\frac{1}{3}n^{5/2}
triangles.
In order to improve this bound, we introduce a
result of Alon and Shikhelman
[
2
, Lemma 3.1]
.

Lemma 5.1

(See
[
2
]
)
.

Let
G
be a graph on
n
vertices.
If
G
is
F_{k}
-free, then

t(G)<(9k-15)(k+1)n.

In what follows, we show that
the extremal graph
G
has an approximately correct structure.
We can apply Lemma
5.1
to
show that
G
has less than
10n^{2}

triangles.
Then Lemma
3.1
implies
that
G
has more than
n^{2}/4-60n
edges.
So we can use Theorem
3.4
and obtain an optimal partition of vertices of
G
.

Lemma 5.2
.

There exists a vertex partition
V(G)=S\cup T
such that

e(S)+e(T)<120n

and

e(S,T)>\frac{n^{2}}{4}-180n.

Furthermore, we have

\frac{n}{2}-14\sqrt{n}<|S|,|T|<\frac{n}{2}+14\sqrt{n}.

Proof.

Note that
\lambda(G)\geq\lambda(K_{\lceil\frac{n}{2}\rceil,\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\rfloor}^{+2})>\frac{n}{2}
.
By Lemma
5.1
, we obtain

t(G)<10n^{2}.

Then Lemma
3.1
implies

e(G)\geq\lambda^{2}-\frac{6t}{n}>\frac{n^{2}}{4}-60n.

We claim that
G
is not
120n
-far from being bipartite.
Otherwise, if
G
is
120n
-far from being bipartite, then Theorem
3.4

implies that
G
has at least
\frac{n}{6}(\frac{n^{2}}{4}-60n+120n-\frac{n^{2}}{4})=10n^{2}
triangles, a contradiction.
Therefore,
G
is not
120n
-far from being bipartite.
Thus, there exists a partition
V(G)=S\cup T
of
the vertices of
G
such that

e(S)+e(T)<120n.

Consequently, we have

e(S,T)>e(G)-120n>\frac{n^{2}}{4}-180n.

Without loss of generality, we may assume that

1\leq|S|\leq|T|
. Suppose on the contrary that
if
|S|\leq\frac{n}{2}-14\sqrt{n}
, then by
|S|+|T|=n
, we have

|T|\geq\frac{n}{2}+14\sqrt{n}
. It follows that

e(S,T)\leq|S||T|\leq\left(\frac{n}{2}-14\sqrt{n}\right)\left(\frac{n}{2}+14\sqrt{n}\right)<\frac{n^{2}}{4}-196n,

which is a contradiction. Thus, we obtain
|S|>\frac{n}{2}-14\sqrt{n}
and

|T|<\frac{n}{2}+14\sqrt{n}
.
∎

Throughout the paper, we always assume that

V(G)=S\cup T
is a partition with a maximum cut, i.e., the bipartite subgraph
G[S,T]
has the maximum number of edges. Lemma
5.2
guarantees that there exists such a partition with
e(S,T)>\frac{1}{4}n^{2}-180n
and

\frac{n}{2}-14\sqrt{n}<|S|,|T|<\frac{n}{2}+14\sqrt{n}
.
Unlike the argument in Section
4
,
the above bipartition is not enough for our purpose.
Under this circumstance, the graph
G
has many triangles, and
applying Theorem
3.4
can only yield an
approximate vertex partition. After the bipartition is obtained,
the remaining task is to perform a more careful structural analysis.
We need to show that the extremal graph is an almost-balanced bipartite graph.

In the sequel,
we define two sets of ‘bad’ vertices of
G
. Namely,

L:=\left\{v\in V(G):d(v)\leq\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{200}\right)n\right\}.

For a vertex
v\in V(G)
, let
d_{S}(v)=|N(v)\cap S|
and
d_{T}(v)=|N(v)\cap T|
. We denote

W:=\left\{v\in S:d_{S}(v)\geq\frac{n}{150}\right\}\cup\left\{v\in T:d_{T}(v)\geq\frac{n}{150}\right\}.

In order to show that

G
is the expected extremal graph

K_{\lceil\frac{n}{2}\rceil,\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\rfloor}^{+2}
,
we need to prove that
the bad sets
L
and
W
are empty.
Next, we show that
W
and
L
are small sets.

Lemma 5.3
.

We have

|L|<\frac{n}{403}.

Proof.

Suppose that
|L|\geq\frac{n}{403}
.
Then let
L^{\prime}\subseteq L
with
|L^{\prime}|=\frac{n}{403}
.
It follows that

\displaystyle e(G\setminus L^{\prime})

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle e(G)-\sum_{v\in L^{\prime}}d(v)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\frac{n^{2}}{4}-60n-\frac{n}{403}\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{200}\right)n

\displaystyle>

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4}\left(n-\frac{n}{403}\right)^{2}+4\times 10^{-6}n^{2},

where the last inequality holds for
n\geq 8.8\times 10^{6}
.
By Corollary
3.3
, the subgraph
G\setminus L^{\prime}
contains more than

\frac{4}{3}\times 10^{-6}n^{3}
triangles, which contradicts with the fact
t(G)<10n^{2}
for every
n\geq 7.5\times 10^{6}
.
So we have
|L|<\frac{n}{403}
, as needed.
∎

In the proof of Lemma
5.3
, one can apply
Theorem
1.3
to obtain a better bound on
|L|
, for example,
|L|\leq 150
. However, Theorem
1.3
requires
n\geq 1/\delta
, where
\delta>0
is an unspecified constant. To get an exact bound on
n
, rather than on
n
being sufficiently large, we need to use Corollary
3.3
, as a slightly worse bound on
|L|
is allowed for our purpose.

Lemma 5.4
.

We have

|W|<36000.

Proof.

We denote
W_{1}=W\cap S
and
W_{2}=W\cap T
. Then

2e(S)=\sum_{u\in S}d_{S}(u)\geq\sum_{u\in W_{1}}d_{S}(u)\geq\frac{n}{150}|W_{1}|

and

2e(T)=\sum_{u\in T}d_{T}(u)\geq\sum_{u\in W_{2}}d_{T}(u)\geq\frac{n}{150}|W_{2}|.

So we obtain

e(S)+e(T)\geq(|W_{1}|+|W_{2}|)\frac{n}{300}=\frac{|W|n}{300}.

On the other hand,
Lemma
5.2
implies that

e(S)+e(T)<120n.

Then we get
\frac{|W|n}{300}<120n
,
that is,

|W|<36000
, as desired.
∎

We also need the following inclusion-exclusion principle.

Lemma 5.5
.

Let
A_{1},A_{2},\dots,A_{k}
be
k
finite sets. Then

\left|\bigcap_{i=1}^{k}A_{i}\right|\geq\sum_{i=1}^{k}|A_{i}|-(k-1)\left|\bigcup_{i=1}^{k}A_{i}\right|.

Lemma 5.6
.

We have
W\subseteq L
.

Proof.

We will show that if
u\in V(G)
and
u\notin L
, then
u\notin W
.
For notational convenience, we denote
L_{1}=L\cap S
and
L_{2}=L\cap T
. Without loss of generality,
we may assume that
u\in S
and

u\notin L_{1}.
Since
S
and
T
form a maximum cut,
we can get
d_{T}(u)\geq\frac{1}{2}d(u)
. Since
u\not\in L_{1}
, we have
d(u)>\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{200}\right)n
. So

d_{T}(u)\geq\frac{1}{2}d(u)>\left(\frac{1}{4}-\frac{1}{400}\right)n.

Recall that

|L|\leq\frac{n}{403}
,
|W|\leq 36000
and

|S|>\frac{n}{2}-14\sqrt{n}
,
we have

|S\setminus(W\cup L)|>\frac{n}{3}
.

We claim that
u
has at most one neighbor in

S\setminus(W\cup L)
.
Indeed, suppose on the contrary that

u
is adjacent to two vertices
u_{1},u_{2}
in
S\setminus(W\cup L)
.
For
i\in\{1,2\}
, since
u_{i}\not\in L
, we have
d(u_{i})>\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{200}\right)n
. At the same time, since
u_{i}\notin W
,
we have
d_{S}(u_{i})<\frac{n}{150}
. So

d_{T}(u_{i})=d(u_{i})-d_{S}(u_{i})>\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{200}-\frac{1}{150}\right)n.

By Lemma
5.5
, for each
i\in\{1,2\}
,
we have

\displaystyle|N_{T}(u)\cap N_{T}(u_{i})|

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle|N_{T}(u)|+|N_{T}(u_{i})|-|T|

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\left(\frac{1}{4}-\frac{1}{400}\right)n+\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{200}-\frac{1}{150}\right)n-\left(\frac{n}{2}+14\sqrt{n}\right)

\displaystyle>

\displaystyle\frac{n}{5},

where the last inequality holds for
n\geq 1.6\times 10^{5}
.
Let
B_{i}
be the common neighbors of
u

and
u_{i}
in
T
.
Then
|B_{i}|>n/5
for each
i\in\{1,2\}
.
Observe that for any two vertices
v_{1}\in B_{1}
and

v_{2}\in B_{2}
with
v_{1}\neq v_{2}
,
the triangles
uu_{1}v_{1}
and
uu_{2}v_{2}

form a copy of the bowtie.
In other words, we can find at least

\frac{1}{2}\frac{n}{5}(\frac{n}{5}-1)>\frac{n}{2}
copies of the bowtie, a contradiction.
Therefore
u
is adjacent to at most one vertex in
S\setminus(W\cup L)
.

Using the above claim, for
n\geq 8.7\times 10^{6}
, we have

d_{S}(u)\leq 1+|W|+|L|<1+36000+\frac{n}{403}<\frac{n}{150}.

By definition, we get
u\notin W
.
This completes the proof.
∎

Lemma 5.7
.

Both
G[S\setminus L]
and
G[T\setminus L]

contain neither three pairwise disjoint edges nor two intersecting edges.
Furthermore,
we have
e(S\setminus L)\leq 2
and

e(T\setminus L)\leq 2
. So
there exist independent sets

I_{S}\subseteq S\setminus L
and
I_{T}\subseteq T\setminus L
such that

|I_{S}|>|S|-\frac{n}{402}
and
|I_{T}|>|T|-\frac{n}{402}
.

Proof.

We proceed the proof in two cases.

Case 1.

If
e_{1},e_{2}
and
e_{3}
are pairwise disjoint edges in
G[S\setminus L]
,
then we can denote

e_{1}=\{u_{1},u_{2}\}
,
e_{2}=\{u_{3},u_{4}\}

and
e_{3}=\{u_{5},u_{6}\}
.
For
1\leq i\leq 6
,
since
u_{i}\notin L
, we get

d(u_{i})>\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{200}\right)n
.
By Lemma
5.6
,
we have
u_{i}\notin W
and

d_{S}(u_{i})<\frac{n}{150}
. Hence

d_{T}(u_{i})=d(u_{i})-d_{S}(u_{i})>\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{200}-\frac{1}{150}\right)n.

By Lemma

, we get

\displaystyle\left|\bigcap_{i=1}^{6}N_{T}(u_{i})\right|

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{6}|N_{T}(u_{i})|-5\left|\bigcup_{i=1}^{6}N_{T}(u_{i})\right|

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{200}-\frac{1}{150}\right)n\cdot 6-5\left(\frac{n}{2}+14\sqrt{n}\right)

\displaystyle>

\displaystyle\frac{n}{6},

where the last inequality follows by
n\geq 7.1\times 10^{4}
.
Observe that
each vertex of the common neighbors of

\{u_{1},u_{2},\ldots,u_{6}\}
leads to three copies of the bowtie. Consequently, there are
more than
\frac{n}{2}
copies of the bowtie in
G
,
which is a contradiction.

Case 2.

Assume that
e_{1}
and
e_{2}
are intersecting edges in
G[S\setminus L]
,
and
e_{1},e_{2}
form a copy of
K_{1,2}
.
We denote

e_{1}=\{u_{1},u_{2}\}
and
e_{2}=\{u_{1},u_{3}\}
. Similarly, we can see that
for each
i\in\{2,3\}
,
we have

\displaystyle|N_{T}(u_{1})\cap N_{T}(u_{i})|

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle|N_{T}(u_{1})|+|N_{T}(u_{i})|-|T|

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle 2\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{200}-\frac{1}{150}\right)n-\left(\frac{n}{2}+14\sqrt{n}\right)

\displaystyle>

\displaystyle\frac{n}{3},

where the last inequality holds for
n\geq 9541
.
In other words, there are more than

n/3
choices of a vertex
v_{2}\in T

such that
u_{1}u_{2}v_{2}
forms a triangle.
Similarly, we have at least
n/3
choices of a vertex

v_{3}\in T
such that
v_{3}\neq v_{2}
and

u_{1}u_{3}v_{3}
forms a triangle.
Thus, we can find
\frac{1}{2}\frac{n}{3}(\frac{n}{3}-1)
copies of the bowtie in
G
, a contradiction.

To sum up, we have proved that
both
G[S\setminus L]
and
G[T\setminus L]

have at most two non-trivial components, and each of which consists of a single edge.
Consequently, we get
e(S\setminus L)\leq 2
and
e(T\setminus L)\leq 2
.
Now, by deleting
one vertex of each edge in
G[S\setminus L]
, we can obtain
a large independent set.
So there exists an independent set

I_{S}\subseteq S\setminus L
such that

|I_{S}|\geq|S\setminus L|-2>|S|-\frac{n}{402}
by Lemma
5.3
.
The same argument gives that there is an independent set
I_{T}\subseteq T\setminus L
with

|I_{T}|>|T|-\frac{n}{402}
.
∎

In the sequel, let
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}

be a non-negative eigenvector corresponding to
\lambda(G)
.
We write
{x}_{v}
for the entry of
\mathbf{x}

corresponding to a vertex

.
By scaling, we may assume further that the vector
\mathbf{x}
has
the maximum entry equal to
1
.
Let

be a vertex of
G
with the maximum eigenvector entry and
{x}_{z}=1

\frac{n}{2}<\lambda(G)=\lambda(G){x}_{z}=\sum_{w\in N(z)}{x}_{w}\leq d(z).

By definition, we have
z\notin L
.
We may assume that
z\in S\setminus L
.

Lemma 5.8
.

We have
\sum\limits_{v\in I_{T}}{x}_{v}>\frac{n}{2}-\frac{n}{201}
.

Proof.

By Lemma

, we know that

and

|L|<\frac{n}{403}
.
From Lemma

, we have

d_{S\setminus L}(z)\leq 2
and

d_{S}(z)\leq d_{S\setminus L}(z)+|L|<\frac{n}{402}
.
Therefore, we get

\displaystyle\lambda(G)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lambda(G){x}_{z}=\sum_{v\in N_{S}(z)}{x}_{v}+\sum_{v\in N_{T}(z)}{x}_{v}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{v\in N_{S}(z)}{x}_{v}+\sum_{v\sim z,v\in I_{T}}{x}_{v}+\sum_{v\sim z,v\in T\setminus I_{T}}{x}_{v}

\displaystyle<

\displaystyle\frac{n}{402}+\sum_{v\in I_{T}}{x}_{v}+|T\setminus I_{T}|

\displaystyle<

\displaystyle\sum_{v\in I_{T}}{x}_{v}+\frac{n}{201},

where
I_{T}
is the independent set from Lemma

. Recall that
\lambda(G)\geq\lambda(K_{\lceil\frac{n}{2}\rceil,\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\rfloor}^{+2})>\frac{n}{2}
.
It follows that

\sum_{v\in I_{T}}{x}_{v}>\frac{n}{2}-\frac{n}{201}
, as required.
∎

Lemma 5.9
.

We have
L=\varnothing
.

Proof.

Suppose on the contrary that there exists a vertex
v\in L
. Then

is an independent set.

\displaystyle\lambda(G^{+})-\lambda(G)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\frac{\mathbf{x}^{\top}\left(A(G^{+})-A(G)\right)\mathbf{x}}{\mathbf{x}^{\top}\mathbf{x}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{2{x}_{v}}{\mathbf{x}^{\top}\mathbf{x}}\left(\sum_{w\in I_{T}}{x}_{w}-\sum_{u\in N_{G}(v)}{x}_{u}\right)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\frac{2{x}_{v}}{\mathbf{x}^{\top}\mathbf{x}}\left(\frac{n}{2}-\frac{n}{201}-\Bigl(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{200}\Bigr)n\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{2{x}_{v}}{\mathbf{x}^{\top}\mathbf{x}}\left(\frac{n}{200}-\frac{n}{201}\right)>0,

By Lemmas

and
e(T)\leq 2

the induced subgraph

is a complete bipartite graph.
If

and

a contradiction.

If

, then

.
By calculation, we obtain that

is the largest root of

It is easy to verify that

.
Using Lemma

,

Case 2.

Subcase 2.1.

and

If
e(S)=2
, then
G
is a subgraph of

.
Note that
G
has at most
\frac{n-1}{2}
bowties and

has exactly

\frac{n+1}{2}
bowties.
So
G
is obtained from
K_{\frac{n-1}{2},\frac{n+1}{2}}^{+2}
by deleting some edges
that destroy at least one bowtie.
Let

by deleting

It is not hard to deduce that

\lambda(K_{\frac{n-1}{2},\frac{n+1}{2}}^{+2\,\,|})

By Lemma

, we know that
\lambda(K_{\frac{n+1}{2},\frac{n-1}{2}}^{+2})

By computation, we get

f_{4}(x)-x^{3}g(x)=-({x}/{2})(4x^{2}+(2n-n^{2}+3)x+n^{2}-8n-1).

6
Concluding remarks

In this paper, we have provided a stability variant of
a spectral counting result due to Ning and Zhai.
Moreover, we have proved a spectral counting
result for the bowtie, which solves an open problem
proposed by the authors in
[
29
]
.
Next, we are going to conclude some spectral extremal graph problems concerning the number of
triangles and bowties.

An old result of Nosal
[
52
]

(see
[
43
,
49
]
)
states that every graph
G
with

m
edges and
\lambda(G)>\sqrt{m}

has a triangle.
In 2023, Ning and Zhai
[
50
]

proved further that if

\lambda(G)\geq\sqrt{m}
,
then
G
contains at least
\lfloor\frac{\sqrt{m}-1}{2}\rfloor
triangles, unless
G
is a complete bipartite graph.
This result is tight as evidenced by adding an edge to the larger vertex part of
K_{a,b}
, where
a=2(\sqrt{m}+1)
and
b=\frac{\sqrt{m}-1}{2}
.
Inspired by this result,
we conjecture that the number of triangles is close
to be doubling under the same constraint
\tau_{3}(G)\geq 2
as in Theorem
2.2
.

Conjecture 6.1
.

If
G
is an
m
-edge graph with
\lambda(G)>\sqrt{m}
, and there is no vertex contained in all triangles of
G
,
then
G
contains at least
\sqrt{m}-O(1)
triangles

f(m)=O(1)
if there are positive constants

and
m_{0}

f(m)\leq C

Remark.

The bound in Conjecture
6.1

is asymptotically tight.
Indeed, for each
b\in\mathbb{N}
, we take

a=4b+3
and
m=ab+2
, then we define
K_{a,b}^{+2}
as the graph obtained from
a complete bipartite graph

by embedding
two disjoint edges into the vertex part of size

.
We can check that

has spectral radius

, and it contains

This example shows the tightness of Conjecture

.

Theorem
2.4
gives a spectral counting result for the bowtie.
Recall in Theorem
1.3
that
for sufficiently large
n
and

, the only way to construct a graph

Our result could be regarded as a spectral version of Theorem

It is meaningful to prove a spectral version for a general

The first barrier we may encounter is to establish

Now, we denote by

by embedding

We propose the following conjecture.

Conjecture 6.2
.

If

and

Remark.

We have a premonition that,

We end this paper with a problem involving books.

Problem 6.3

For an arbitrary positive integer
