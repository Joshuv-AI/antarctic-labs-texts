Quantum metrology with imperfect measurements
Yink Loong Len, Tuvia Gefen, Alex Retzker, Jan Kołodyński

Abstract
The impact of measurement imperfections on quantum metrology protocols has not been approached in a systematic manner so far. In this work, we tackle this issue by generalising firstly the notion of quantum Fisher information to account for noisy detection, and propose tractable methods allowing for its approximate evaluation. We then show that in canonical scenarios involving N N probes with local measurements undergoing readout noise, the optimal sensitivity depends crucially on the control operations allowed to counterbalance the measurement imperfections—with global control operations, the ideal sensitivity (e.g. the Heisenberg scaling) can always be recovered in the asymptotic N N limit, while with local control operations the quantum-enhancement of sensitivity is constrained to a constant factor. We illustrate our findings with an example of NV-centre magnetometry, as well as schemes involving spin- 1 / 2 1/2 probes with bit-flip errors affecting their two-outcome measurements, for which we find the input states and control unitary operations sufficient to attain the ultimate asymptotic precision.



I
Introduction

One of the most promising quantum-enhanced technologies are the
quantum sensors

Degen et al. 2017
that by utilising quantum features of platforms such as solid-state spin systems
Awschalom et al. 2018
;
Barry et al. 2020
, atomic ensembles
Pezzè et al. 2018
and interferometers
Bongs et al. 2019
, or even gravitational-wave detectors
Tse et al. 2019
are capable of operating at unprecedented sensitivities. They all rely on the architecture in which the parameter to be sensed (e.g. a magnetic or gravitational field) perturbs a well-isolated quantum system, which after being measured allows to precisely infer the perturbation and, hence, estimate well the parameter from the measurement data. In case the sensor consists of multiple probes (atoms, photons) their inter-entanglement opens doors to beating classical limits imposed on the estimation error
Giovannetti et al. 2004
—a fact that ignited a series of breakthrough experiments
Leibfried et al. 2004
;
Mitchell et al. 2004
;
Estève et al. 2008
;
Appel et al. 2009
;
Sewell et al. 2012
;
Hosten et al. 2016
, being responsible also for the quantum-enhancement in gravitational-wave detection
Tse et al. 2019
.

These demonstrations are built upon various seminal theoretical works, in particular Refs.
Helstrom 1976
;
Holevo 1982
;
Braunstein and Caves 1994
that adopted parameter-inference problems to the quantum setting, and generalised the
Fisher information
(FI)
Kay 1993
to quantum systems. This general formalism provides tools to identify optimal probe states and measurements for any given quantum metrology task
Giovannetti et al. 2006
. Interestingly it was shown that in many multi-probe scenarios, even those that involve entangled probes, optimal readout schemes turn out to be local—each of the probes can in principle be measured independently
Giovannetti et al. 2006
.

In practice, however, engineering a measurement of a quantum system is a challenge
per se
—it relies on a scheme in which a meter component, typically light, interacts with the quantum sensor before being subsequently detected
Hammerer et al. 2010
;
Clerk et al. 2010
. This allows the state of the probes to be separately controlled, at the price of the meter component carrying intrinsic noise that cannot be completely eradicated. As a result, the implemented measurement becomes
imperfect
with the measured data being noisy due to, e.g., finite resolution of the readout signal. Such an issue naturally arises across different sensing platforms: in nitrogen-vacancy (NV) centres in diamond
Batalov et al. 2008
;
Jelezko and Wrachtrup 2006
;
Schirhagl et al. 2014
, superconducting based quantum information processors
Sete et al. 2015
;
Heinsoo et al. 2018
;
Krantz et al. 2019
;
Bergquist et al. 1986
, trapped ions
Nagourney et al. 1986
;
Sauter et al. 1986
;
Myerson et al. 2008
;
Marciniak et al. 2022
, and interferometers involving photodetection
Harris et al. 2017
;
Xu et al. 2020
. Although for special detection-noise models (e.g. Gaussian blurring) the impact on quantum metrological performance and its compensation via the so-called interaction-based readout schemes has been studied
Davis et al. 2016
;
Fröwis et al. 2016
;
Nolan et al. 2017
;
Haine 2018
and demonstrated
Linnemann et al. 2016
, a general analysis has been missing thus far.

Crucially, such a detection noise affecting the measurement cannot be generally put on the same grounds as the “standard” decoherence disturbing the (quantum) dynamics of the sensor before being measured
Maccone and Giovannetti 2011
. In the latter case, the impact on quantum metrological performance has been thoroughly investigated
Fujiwara and Imai 2008
;
Escher et al. 2011
;
Demkowicz-Dobrzański et al. 2012
and, moreover, shown under special conditions to be fully compensable by implementing methods of
quantum error correction

Dür et al. 2014
;
Arrad et al. 2014
;
Sekatski et al. 2017
;
Demkowicz-Dobrzański et al. 2017
;
Zhou et al. 2018
. This contrasts the setting of readout noise that affects the classical output (outcomes) of a measurement, whose impact cannot be inverted by employing, e.g., the methods of
error mitigation

Maciejewski et al. 2020
;
Bravyi et al. 2021
designed to recover statistical properties of the ideal readout data at the price of overhead, which cannot be simply ignored in the context of parameter estimation by increasing the sample size.

In our work, we formalize the problem of imperfect measurements in quantum metrology by firstly generalising the concept of

quantum Fisher information
(QFI)
Braunstein and Caves 1994
to the case of noisy readout. For pure probe-states, we explicitly relate the form of the resulting
imperfect QFI
to the perfect QFI, i.e. to the one applicable in presence of ideal detection. However, as we find the imperfect QFI not always to be directly computable, we discuss two general methods allowing one to tightly bound its value, as illustrated by a specific example of precision magnetometry performed with help of a NV centre
Maze et al. 2008
;
Taylor et al. 2008
, for which the measurement imperfection is naturally inbuilt in the readout procedure
Jiang et al. 2009
;
Neumann et al. 2010
. Using the
conjugate-map decomposition
formalism, we also study when the measurement imperfections can be effectively interpreted as an extra source of “standard” decoherence, in order to show that this may occur only under very strict conditions.

Secondly, we focus on the canonical metrology schemes involving multiple probes
Giovannetti et al. 2006
, in order investigate how do the measurement imperfections affect then the attainable sensitivity as a function of the probe number
N
, which in the ideal setting may scale at best quadratically with
N
—following the so-called ultimate
Heisenberg scaling
(HS)
Giovannetti et al. 2004
. Considering general local measurements undergoing detection noise, we demonstrate that the achievable precision strongly depends on the type, i.e. global vs local, of control operations one is allowed to apply on the probes before the readout is performed.

In the former case, we prove a
go-theorem
which states that there always exists a
global
control unitary such that for pure states the imperfect QFI converges to the perfect QFI with
N
, and the detection noise can then be effectively ignored in the
N\to\infty
limit. We provide a recipe how to construct the required global unitary operation, and conjecture the general form of the optimal unitary from our numerical evidence. On the contrary, when restricted to
local
control unitaries, we resort to the concept of
quantum-classical channels

Holevo 1998
that describe then not only the evolution of each probe, but also the noisy measurement each probe is eventually subject to. For this complementary scenario, we establish a
no-go theorem
which states that whenever measurements exhibit any non-trivial local detection noise, attaining the HS becomes “elusive”
Demkowicz-Dobrzański et al. 2012
—the maximal quantum-enhancement becomes restricted to a constant factor with the estimation error asymptotically following at best a classical behaviour (
\sim 1/N
), which we refer to as the
standard scaling
(SS).

In order to illustrate the applicability of both theorems,
we consider the
phase-estimation example involving
N
spin-
1/2
probes, whose binary measurements undergo bit-flip errors. On one hand, we explicitly construct the global unitary control operation, thanks to which the sensitivity quickly attains the HS with
N
, using for example the
GHZ state

Greenberger et al. 1989
. On the other, when only local control operations are allowed, we evaluate the asymptotic SS-like bound on precision analytically, and prove its saturability with
N\to\infty
by considering the probes to be prepared in a
spin-squeezed state

Wineland et al. 1992
;
Kitagawa and Ueda 1993
and measuring effectively the mean value of their total angular momentum by adequately interpreting the noisy readout data. Furthermore, we apply the above analysis in Methods to the setting of optical interferometry involving
N
-photon states and imperfect detection, which suffers from both photonic losses and dark counts.

Let us consider a
quantum metrology scenario depicted in Fig.
1

d
-dimensional qudit probe is prepared in a quantum state
\rho

\theta
that is represented by a unitary channel
\mathcal{U}_{\theta}\sim\{U_{\theta}\}

foo a
.
The probe state thus transforms onto
\rho(\theta)=\mathcal{U}_{\theta}[\rho]=U_{\theta}\rho U_{\theta}^{\dagger}
, and is subsequently rotated by a control unitary transformation

\mathcal{V}_{\vec{\phi}}\sim\{V_{\vec{\phi}}\}
specified by the vector of parameters
\vec{\phi}
. It is then subjected to a fixed projective (von Neumann) measurement
formally represented by a set of projection operators
\{\Pi_{i}\}_{i=1}^{d}
, i.e.
\Pi_{i}\Pi_{j}=\delta_{i,j}\Pi_{i}
and
\sum_{i=1}^{d}\Pi_{i}=\mathbbm{1}_{d}

\Pi_{i,\vec{\phi}}:=V_{\vec{\phi}}^{\dagger}\Pi_{i}V_{\vec{\phi}}

\mathcal{V}_{\vec{\phi}}
is to select a particular measurement basis. In an

p_{\theta,\vec{\phi}}(i)=\mathrm{Tr}\{\rho(\theta)\Pi_{i,\vec{\phi}}\}
. Repeating the procedure over many rounds, an estimate
\tilde{\theta}
can then be constructed based on all the collected data, which most accurately reproduces the true parameter value
\theta
.

In particular, it is then natural to seek
\tilde{\theta}
that minimises the
mean squared error
(MSE),
\Delta^{2}\tilde{\theta}
, while also minimising it over different measurement bases and initial states of the probe. For unbiased estimators, considering
\nu
repetitions, the MSE is generally lower limited by the

(QCRB)

;
Braunstein and Caves 1994
:

\nu\Delta^{2}\tilde{\theta}\;\geq\;\frac{1}{\mathcal{F}}\;\geq\;\frac{1}{\bar{\mathcal{F}}},

(1)

where
\mathcal{F}
is the

(QFI) that corresponds to the maximal (classical)

(FI),

, defined for a given distribution

and its derivative w.r.t. the estimated parameter,
\{\dot{p}_{\theta,\vec{\phi}}(i)\equiv\partial_{\theta}p_{\theta,\vec{\phi}}(i)\}
, i.e.

;
Braunstein and Caves 1994
:

\mathcal{F}:=\max_{\vec{\phi}}F\quad\text{with}\quad F[p_{\theta,\vec{\phi}}]:=\sum_{i}\frac{\dot{p}_{\theta,\vec{\phi}}(i)^{2}}{p_{\theta,\vec{\phi}}(i)},

(2)

that is optimised over all possible measurement bases
\vec{\phi}
.
{\bar{\mathcal{F}}}
in Eq. (
1
) is the
channel QFI
which includes a further optimisation over all possible input probe states
\rho
, i.e.
\bar{\mathcal{F}}:=\max_{\rho}\mathcal{F}=\max_{\rho,\vec{\phi}}F.

For perfect projective measurements this theory is well established—close analytical expressions for the QFI and the channel QFI exist. The QFI
for any
\rho(\theta)
reads

:

\mathcal{F}[\rho(\theta)]=\mathrm{Tr}\{\rho(\theta)L^{2}\},

(3)

where

is the symmetric-logarithmic derivative operator defined implicitly as
\partial_{\theta}\rho(\theta)=\frac{1}{2}(L\rho(\theta)+\rho(\theta)L)
, whose eigenbasis provides then the optimal measurement basis

\psi=\left|\psi\right\rangle\!\left\langle\psi\right|
. Hence, for the unitary encoding
\psi(\theta)=\mathcal{U}_{\theta}[\psi]
the channel QFI in Eq. (
1
) just reads

:

\displaystyle\bar{\mathcal{F}}[\mathcal{U}_{\theta}]=(\lambda_{\mathrm{max}}(h_{\theta})-\lambda_{\mathrm{min}}(h_{\theta}))^{2},

(4)

where
h_{\theta}=-\mathrm{i}\left(\partial_{\theta}U_{\theta}\right)U_{\theta}^{\dagger}
,
\lambda_{\mathrm{max}}(h_{\theta})
and
\lambda_{\mathrm{min}}(h_{\theta})
are the maximum and minimum eigenvalues of

, respectively, and
\bar{\mathcal{F}}
is attained by
\psi(\theta)
being an equal superposition of the corresponding two eigenvectors

;
Pang and Brun 2014
.

In practical settings, however, perfect measurements are often beyond reach. Instead, one must deal with an

\mathcal{M}

positive operators

1

The utilisation of NV centres as quantum spin probes allows for precise magnetic-field sensing with unprecedented resolution

II.2
Relations to quantum metrology with noisy encoding

Any imperfect measurement
\mathcal{M}
admits a
conjugate-map decomposition

\mathcal{M}=\Lambda^{\dagger}[\Pi]

II.3.1

We first consider multi-probe scenarios in which one is allowed to perform global unitary control operations,

III
Discussions

We have analysed
the impact of measurement imperfections on quantum metrology protocols and, in particular, the prospects of recovering the ideal quantum enhancement of sensitivity, e.g. the Heisenberg scaling (HS) of precision, despite the readout noise. The contrasting results obtained with global or local control operations can be understood by the following simple intuition.

With global control operations available, one may effectively construct a global measurement tailored to the two-dimensional subspace containing the information about any tiny changes of the parameter. Importantly, thanks to the exponential increase of the overall dimension
with the number of probes, one may then distinguish (exponentially) better and better the two states lying in this two-dimensional subspace,
within which the effective amount of readout noise diminishes, and the perfect optimal scaling prevails.
Our work, thus, motivates explicitly the use of variational approaches in identification of such global unitary control not only at the level of state preparation
Kaubruegger et al. 2019
;
Koczor et al. 2020
, but also crucially in the optimisation of local measurements
Kaubruegger et al. 2021
;
Marciniak et al. 2022
. On the other hand, it demonstrates that control operations form the key building-block in fighting measurement imperfections in quantum metrology. Although we have provided control strategies that allow to maintain the HS both in the qubit and photonic settings, these employ
N
-body interactions, while it is known that
2
-body interactions suffice in presence of Gaussian blurring arising in cold-atom experiments
Davis et al. 2016
;
Fröwis et al. 2016
;
Nolan et al. 2017
;
Haine 2018
. Thus, we believe that our results open an important route of investigating the complexity of such global control required, depending on the form of the readout noise encountered.

On the contrary, there is no exponential advantage gained when only local control operations are available. Hence, as the overall amount of noise also rises limitlessly as we increase the number of probes, the asymptotic scaling of sensitivity is constrained to be classical. Note that this conclusion is valid also in the Bayesian scenario, as by the virtue of the Bayesian CRB
van Trees 1968
also the
average
MSE is then lower-bounded by
\langle\Delta^{2}\tilde{\theta}\rangle\gtrapprox 1/\langle\bar{\mathcal{F}}^{\mathrm{\scriptstyle{(im)}}}_{N}\rangle\gtrsim 1/N
, where
\langle\dots\rangle
denotes now the averaging over some prior distribution of the parameter.

Finally, although we have primarily focussed here on phase-estimation protocols, let us emphasise once more that Thm.
1
applies to any quantum metrology scheme involving pure states and imperfect measurements. Hence, it holds also when sensing, e.g., ‘critical’ parameters at phase transitions with noisy detection
Mirkhalaf et al. 2021
. Still, generalisation to the case with mixed states (beyond product-state admixtures) remains open. This would allow us, for instance, to approach quantum thermometry protocols utilising thermalised (Gibbs) probe states with the temperature being then estimated despite coarse-graining of measurements
Hovhannisyan et al. 2021
. In such cases, one should then also characterise the (mixed) states for which the imperfect QFI is actually guaranteed to converge to the perfect QFI in the asymptotic
N
limit.

IV.1
Phase sensing with an NV centre

Within this protocol, the NV centre is firstly initialised into some superposition
state
\rho=\left|\psi\right\rangle\left\langle\psi\right|
of the
m_{s}=0
(corresponding to
\left|0\right\rangle
) and
m_{s}=1
(corresponding to
\left|1\right\rangle
) ground-state energy levels with help of a Ramsey pulse.
The NV spin is then used to sense a magnetic field of strength
B
in the
\mathrm{z}
-direction for time
t
(usually chosen to be as long as the decoherence allows for, i.e.
T_{2}^{*}
or
T_{2}

Santagati et al. 2019

For our purpose we assume the evolution time to be perfectly known (and so the gyromagnetic ratio), so that the problem of estimating the field strength
B
is effectively equivalent to estimating the relative phase
\theta
. Effectively then, the encoding channel is
\mathcal{U}_{\theta}\sim\{U_{\theta}=\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}h\theta}\}

h=\sigma_{\mathrm{z}}/2

\sigma_{\ell}
is the usual Pauli-
\ell

\ell=\mathrm{x,y,z}

In order to read out
\theta

\left|0\right\rangle\rightarrow\left|0^{\prime}\right\rangle

\left|1\right\rangle\rightarrow\left|1^{\prime}\right\rangle

\left|0^{\prime}\right\rangle
and
\left|1^{\prime}\right\rangle
correspond respectively to the
m_{s}=0
and
m_{s}=1
excited energy levels. While the optical transitions between the two
m_{s}=0
energy levels are essentially exclusive, there is a metastable singlet state to which the excited
m_{s}=1
energy state can decay non-radiatively. As a consequence, when performing now the measurement of photon emissions in such a spin-dependent fluorescence process over a designated time window, a dark signal indicates the original NV spin to be projected onto
\left|1\right\rangle
, while a bright signal corresponds to the projection onto
\left|0\right\rangle
. That is, within our general formalism,
\Pi_{1}=\left|0\right\rangle\!\left\langle 0\right|
and
\Pi_{2}=\left|1\right\rangle\!\left\langle 1\right|

\Pi_{1(2),\vec{\phi}}=\left|\pm\right\rangle\!\left\langle\pm\right|
, with
\sigma_{\mathrm{x}}\left|\pm\right\rangle=\pm\left|\pm\right\rangle
.

The bright versus dark distinction is however not perfect: the
m_{s}=1
excited state could still decay radiactively into the ground state, with the dark signal typically reducible to about 65% of the bright signal. Moreover, as the photon emissions are spontaneous and random, the same photon-number being recorded can actually come from both the dark and bright signals, albeit with different
probabilities. These for the readout of an NV-centre are modelled as two Poissonian distributions of distinct means, depending also on the number of QND repetitions

;
Schmitt et al. 2021
, often approximated by Gaussians
Neumann et al. 2010
—see Fig.
1

X=\{0,1,2,\cdots\}
, which are distributed according to the two Poissonian distributions
p(x|1)=\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda_{\left|0\right\rangle}}(\lambda_{\left|0\right\rangle})^{x}/x!
and
p(x|2)=\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda_{\left|1\right\rangle}}(\lambda_{\left|1\right\rangle})^{x}/x!
, whose means,
\lambda_{\left|0\right\rangle}
and
\lambda_{\left|1\right\rangle}

\Pi_{1,\vec{\phi}}
or
\Pi_{2,\vec{\phi}}

IV.3
Lower-bounding the FI via the moments of a probability distribution

It can be shown (see Supplement for derivation) that by including up to the first

\displaystyle F^{(K)}:=\mathrm{Tr}\{A^{-1}B\},

(26)

where
B=\boldsymbol{b}\boldsymbol{b}^{T}
with
\boldsymbol{b}=\begin{pmatrix}0,\,\dot{\mathbb{E}}[x],\,\cdots\,,\dot{\mathbb{E}}[x^{K}]\end{pmatrix}^{T}
,
and

\displaystyle A

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}1&\mathbb{E}[x]&\cdots&\mathbb{E}[x^{K}]\\
\mathbb{E}[x]&\mathbb{E}[x^{2}]&\cdots&\mathbb{E}[x^{K+1}]\\
\vdots&\,&\ddots&\,\\
\mathbb{E}[x^{K}]&\mathbb{E}[x^{K+1}]&\cdots&\mathbb{E}[x^{2K}]\end{pmatrix}

(27)

with
\mathbb{E}[x^{j}]=\sum_{x}q_{\theta,\vec{\phi}}(x)x^{j}

\dot{\mathbb{E}}[x^{j}]=\sum_{x}\dot{q}_{\vec{\phi},\theta}(x)x^{j}

IV.4
Upper-bounding the imperfect QFI given the
G
-covariance of a conjugate-map decomposition

We formalise the condition when the results of “standard” noisy metrology
Maccone and Giovannetti 2011
, in which the decoherence affects the parameter encoding, can be applied to the setting of imperfect measurements by resorting to the notion of symmetry, in particular, the
G
-covariance
Holevo 1993
;
Holevo 1996
.

Given a compact group
G
, we say that a quantum channel
\mathcal{E}
is
G
-covariant
if
Holevo 1993
;
Holevo 1996

\forall_{g\in G}:\;\mathcal{E}\circ\mathcal{V}_{g}=\mathcal{W}_{g}\circ\mathcal{E},

(28)

where
\mathcal{V}_{g}
,
\mathcal{W}_{g}
form some unitary representations of
G
.

Now, by denoting the FI in Eq. (
5
) as
F[q_{\theta,\vec{\phi}}]\equiv F[\mathcal{V}_{\vec{\phi}}[\rho(\theta)],\mathcal{M}]
to separate its dependence on the state and the POVM, we formulate the following observations.

Observation 1

(Imperfect measurement with a
G
-covariant conjugate-map decomposition)
.

Given an imperfect measurement with a conjugate-map decomposition
\mathcal{M}=\Lambda^{\dagger}[\Pi]
, and a parametrised state
\rho(\theta)
, if the following conditions are satisfied:

(a)
\Lambda
is covariant with respect to a compact group
G
.

(b) the optimal unitary that yields the imperfect QFI (
\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{opt}}
in Fig.
3
) is guaranteed to be in
G
, so that

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{\scriptstyle{(im)}}}=\max_{g\in G}\,F\!\left[\mathcal{V}_{g}[\rho(\theta)],\Lambda^{\dagger}[\Pi]\right],

(29)

then

\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{\scriptstyle{(im)}}}\leq\mathcal{F}\!\left[\Lambda[\rho(\theta)]\right]

(30)

If further
G=SU(d)
, then equality in Eq. (
30
) is assured.

Moreover, if the parameter encoding is provided in a form of a quantum channel,
\rho(\theta)=\mathcal{E}_{\theta}[\rho]
, and the optimal unitary
\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{opt}}
remains within
G
for the optimal input state, the upper bound (
30
) applies also to the corresponding imperfect channel QFI, i.e.
\bar{\mathcal{F}}^{\mathrm{\scriptstyle{(im)}}}\leq\bar{\mathcal{F}}[\Lambda\circ\mathcal{E}_{\theta}]
.

However, in case the parameter encoding satisfies the
G
-covariance property itself, we independently have that:

Observation 2

(Imperfect channel QFI for
G
-covariant parameter encodings)
.

Given an imperfect measurement with some conjugate-map decomposition
\mathcal{M}=\Lambda^{\dagger}[\Pi]
and the parameter encoding
\rho(\theta)=\mathcal{E}_{\theta}[\rho]

(a) both

\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{opt}}

\displaystyle\bar{\mathcal{F}}^{\mathrm{\scriptstyle{(im)}}}

\displaystyle=\max_{\rho}\max_{g\in G}\,F\!\left[\mathcal{V}_{g}[\mathcal{E}_{\theta}[\rho]],\Lambda^{\dagger}[\Pi]\right];

(31)

then

\displaystyle\bar{\mathcal{F}}^{\mathrm{\scriptstyle{(im)}}}

\displaystyle\leq\bar{\mathcal{F}}[\Lambda\circ\mathcal{E}_{\theta}].

(32)

IV.5
Upper-bounding the FI with the CE method for quantum-classical channel

A thorough account on the CE method is available at Refs. (
Fujiwara and Imai 2008
;
Demkowicz-Dobrzański et al. 2012
;
Kołodyński and Demkowicz-Dobrzański 2013
); here we simply highlight the general idea. In the CE method, when the probe state
\rho
undergoes an effective encoding described by a given channel
\mathcal{E}_{\theta}
, such that
\rho(\theta)=\mathcal{E}_{\theta}[\rho]
, the corresponding FI for
\theta
is bounded by considering an enlarged space with a corresponding input state
\rho_{\mathrm{ext}}
, such that
\max_{\rho}\mathcal{F}\big[\rho(\theta)\big]\leq\max_{\rho_{\mathrm{ext}}}\mathcal{F}\big[(\mathcal{E}_{\theta}\otimes\mathbbm{1})[\rho_{\mathrm{ext}}]\big]
,
where the r.h.s. can be shown to be equal to
4\min_{\tilde{\kappa}}\|\alpha_{\tilde{\kappa}}\|
, with
\tilde{\kappa}=\{\tilde{\kappa}_{i}\}
denoting all the equivalent sets of Kraus operators for
\mathcal{E}_{\theta}
, and
\alpha_{\tilde{\kappa}}:=\sum_{i}\dot{\tilde{\kappa}}_{i}^{\dagger}\dot{\tilde{\kappa}}_{i}
.

In order to apply the CE method to the canonical multi-probe metrology scheme with local control unitaries and local imperfect measurements, which has the corresponding product quantum-classical channel
\mathcal{E}(\theta,\{\vec{\phi}_{\ell}\}):=\bigotimes_{\ell=1}^{N}\Lambda_{\theta,\vec{\phi}_{\ell}}^{(\ell)}
with

\Lambda_{\theta,\vec{\phi}}=\Lambda_{\mathcal{M}}\circ\mathcal{V}_{\vec{\phi}}\circ\mathcal{U}_{\theta}

as depicted in Fig.
4
(b), we first specify the ‘canonical’ set of Kraus operators for
\Lambda_{\theta,\vec{\phi}}
,
K(\theta,\vec{\phi})=\{K_{x,j}(\theta,\vec{\phi})=\left|x\right\rangle\left\langle j\right|\sqrt{U_{\theta}^{\dagger}M_{x,\vec{\phi}}U_{\theta}}\}
, given some orthonormal basis of states
\{\left|j\right\rangle\}_{j=1}^{d}
spanning the qudit (
d
-dimensional) probe space. Importantly also, as the output classical state is diagonal in the flag basis, its QFI corresponds just to the (classical) FI of the eigenvalue distribution
Braunstein and Caves 1994
which we denote simply as
F_{N}=\mathcal{F}[\rho_{\mathrm{cl}}^{N}(\theta,\vec{\phi})]
, with the corresponding (quantum-classical) channel QFI reads
\bar{\mathcal{F}}^{\mathrm{\scriptstyle{(im,l)}}}_{N}:=\max_{\rho^{N},\vec{\phi}}F_{N}
. Hence, upon further restricting the domain of minimisation over
\tilde{\kappa_{i}}
, where we only consider Kraus operators of
\mathcal{E}(\theta,\{\vec{\phi}_{\ell}\})
with the product structure
\tilde{\kappa}_{i}(\theta,\{\vec{\phi}_{\ell}\})=\bigotimes_{\ell=1}^{N}\tilde{K}_{x_{\ell},j_{\ell}}^{(\ell)}(\theta,\vec{\phi}_{\ell})
, where
\tilde{K}(\theta,\vec{\phi}_{\ell})=\{\tilde{K}_{x_{\ell},j_{\ell}}^{(\ell)}(\theta,\vec{\phi}_{\ell})\}
is the set of Kraus operators for
\Lambda_{\theta,\vec{\phi}_{\ell}}^{(\ell)}
, it is then straightforward to arrive at

\displaystyle F_{N}\leq 4\min_{\{\tilde{K}(\theta,\vec{\phi}_{\ell})\}}\Big\|\bigoplus_{\ell=1}^{N}\alpha_{\tilde{K}(\theta,\vec{\phi}_{\ell})}^{(\ell)}+\bigoplus_{\ell\neq m}^{N}\beta_{\tilde{K}(\theta,\vec{\phi}_{\ell})}^{(\ell)}\beta_{\tilde{K}(\theta,\vec{\phi}_{m})}^{(m)}\Big\|,

(33)

where

\displaystyle\alpha_{\tilde{K}(\theta,\vec{\phi})}

\displaystyle:=\sum_{x,j}\dot{\tilde{K}}_{x,j}^{\dagger}(\theta,\vec{\phi})\dot{\tilde{K}}_{x,j}(\theta,\vec{\phi}),

(34)

\displaystyle\beta_{\tilde{K}(\theta,\vec{\phi})}

\displaystyle:=\mathrm{i}\sum_{x,j}\dot{\tilde{K}}_{x,j}^{\dagger}(\theta,\vec{\phi})\tilde{K}_{x,j}(\theta,\vec{\phi}),

(35)

with
\dot{\tilde{K}}_{x,j}:=\partial_{\theta}\tilde{K}_{x,j}
, and
||\cdots||
is the operator norm.
Finally then, we obtain our CE-bound in Eq. (
21
) directly from applying the triangle inequality of the operator norm to the r.h.s. of Eq. (
33
), which gives

\displaystyle F_{N}^{\mathrm{(CE)}}(\{\vec{\phi}_{\ell}\}):=

\displaystyle 4\min_{\{\tilde{K}(\theta,\vec{\phi}_{\ell})\}}\Big\{\sum_{\ell=1}^{N}||\alpha_{\tilde{K}(\theta,\vec{\phi}_{\ell})}||+\sum_{\ell\neq m}^{N}||\beta_{\tilde{K}(\theta,\vec{\phi}_{\ell})}||\,||\beta_{\tilde{K}(\theta,\vec{\phi}_{m})}||\Big\},

(36)

and the minimisation in both Eqs. (
33
) and (
IV.5
) is performed independently for each
\vec{\phi}_{\ell}
over all possible single-probe Kraus representations
\tilde{K}(\theta,\vec{\phi}_{\ell})
.

As we prove in the Supplement, whenever
the noisy detection channel,

\mathcal{P}\sim\{p(x|i)\}
such that
\forall_{x}:\;M_{x}=\sum_{x}p(x|i)\Pi_{i}
,
is non-trivial,
we can always find a Kraus representation such that
\beta_{\tilde{K}(\theta,\vec{\phi}_{\ell})}=0
for all
\ell
. Then, we define the asymptotic CE bound by

\displaystyle F_{N}^{\mathrm{(CE,as)}}(\{\vec{\phi}_{\ell}\}):=4\,\sum_{\ell=1}^{N}\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}\tilde{K}(\theta,\vec{\phi}_{\ell})\\
\beta_{\tilde{K}(\theta,\vec{\phi}_{\ell})}=0\end{subarray}}||\alpha_{\tilde{K}(\theta,\vec{\phi}_{\ell})}||,

(37)

which evidently satisfies
F_{N}^{\mathrm{(CE)}}(\{\vec{\phi}_{\ell}\})\leq F_{N}^{\mathrm{(CE,as)}}(\{\vec{\phi}_{\ell}\})
and
F_{N}^{\mathrm{(CE)}}\underset{n\rightarrow\infty}{\rightarrow}F_{N}^{\mathrm{(CE,as)}}
. Finally, upon optimising
F_{N}^{\mathrm{(CE,as)}}
further over all local control unitaries gives us

IV.6
Saturating
\bar{F}_{N}^{\mathrm{(CE,as)}}
with an angular momentum measurement and spin-squeezed states

To obtain the optimal asymptotic CE bound (

\Pi_{1(2),\vec{\phi}}=\left|\pm\right\rangle\!\left\langle\pm\right|
, followed by an asymmetric bit-flip channel
\mathcal{P}

\eta=\mathsf{p}+\mathsf{q}-1
and
\delta=\mathsf{p}-\mathsf{q}

\pm 1/2

x=1
or
x=2

N

\hat{O}=N\delta\mathbbm{1}/2+\eta\hat{J}_{\mathrm{x}}
that constitutes a modification of the total angular momentum
\hat{J}_{\mathrm{x}}=\sum_{\ell=1}^{N}\frac{\sigma_{\mathrm{x}}^{(\ell)}}{2}

\theta

\nu\gg 1

\displaystyle\nu\Delta^{2}\tilde{\theta}_{N}=\frac{\Delta^{2}\hat{J}_{\mathrm{x}}}{|\partial_{\theta}\langle\hat{J}_{\mathrm{x}}\rangle|^{2}}-\frac{\delta\langle\hat{J}_{\mathrm{x}}\rangle}{\eta|\partial_{\theta}\langle\hat{J}_{\mathrm{x}}\rangle|^{2}}+\frac{N}{4\eta^{2}}\frac{1-\eta^{2}-\delta^{2}}{|\partial_{\theta}\langle\hat{J}_{\mathrm{x}}\rangle|^{2}},

\displaystyle-\frac{\delta\sin\varphi\,\langle\hat{J}_{\mathrm{y}}\rangle_{\mu}}{\eta\cos^{2}\!\varphi\,\langle\hat{J}_{\mathrm{y}}\rangle_{\mu}^{2}}+\frac{N}{4\eta^{2}}\frac{1-\eta^{2}-\delta^{2}}{\cos^{2}\!\varphi\,\langle\hat{J}_{\mathrm{y}}\rangle_{\mu}^{2}},

with

\displaystyle\gamma(\vec{\phi},\psi(\theta))

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{4}\sum_{x}\frac{[\left\langle\psi_{\perp}(\theta)\right|V_{\vec{\phi}}^{\dagger}M_{x}V_{\vec{\phi}}\left|\psi(\theta)\right\rangle+\mathrm{c.c.}]^{2}}{\left\langle\psi(\theta)\right|V_{\vec{\phi}}^{\dagger}M_{x}V_{\vec{\phi}}\left|\psi(\theta)\right\rangle}.

(S.4)

Note that
4\langle\partial_{\theta}\psi_{\perp}(\theta)\left|\partial_{\theta}\psi_{\perp}(\theta)\right\rangle=\mathcal{F}[\psi(\theta)]
is nothing but the (perfect) quantum Fisher information (QFI) of
\psi(\theta)
.

The imperfect QFI is thus:

\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{\scriptstyle{(im)}}}=\left[\max_{\vec{\phi}}\gamma(\vec{\phi},\psi(\theta))\right]\mathcal{F}[\psi(\theta)].

(S.5)

Let us denote
\gamma_{\mathcal{M}}:=\max_{\vec{\phi}}\gamma(\vec{\phi},\psi(\theta))
. Clearly by an appropriate choice of
\vec{\phi}
we can map
\left|\psi(\theta)\right\rangle,\left|\psi_{\perp}(\theta)\right\rangle
to any two arbitrary orthogonal states
\left|\xi\right\rangle,\left|\xi_{\perp}\right\rangle
. Therefore, the optimization over
\vec{\phi}
is basically an optimization over any two orthogonal states
\left|\xi\right\rangle,\left|\xi_{\perp}\right\rangle
, namely:

\gamma_{\mathcal{M}}=\underset{|\xi\rangle,|\xi_{\perp}\rangle}{\max}\sum_{x}\frac{\text{Re}\{\langle\xi_{\perp}|M_{x}|\xi\rangle\}^{2}}{\langle\xi|M_{x}|\xi\rangle}.

(S.6)

Evidently,
\gamma_{\mathcal{M}}
is completely independent of the encoding of the parameter,
\psi(\theta)
,
and depends only on the imperfect measurement
\mathcal{M}\sim\{M_{x}\}_{x}
.
Clearly
\gamma_{\mathcal{M}}\geq 0
, because the FI is non-negative, and
\gamma_{\mathcal{M}}\leq 1
, because the imperfect QFI cannot be bigger than the perfect QFI. The latter can also be verified by using the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality:

\displaystyle\sum_{x}\frac{\text{Re}\{\langle\xi_{\perp}|M_{x}|\xi\rangle\}^{2}}{\langle\xi|M_{x}|\xi\rangle}\leq\sum_{x}\frac{\langle\xi_{\perp}|M_{x}|\xi_{\perp}\rangle\langle\xi|M_{x}|\xi\rangle}{\langle\xi|M_{x}|\xi\rangle}

\displaystyle=\sum_{x}\langle\xi_{\perp}|M_{x}|\xi_{\perp}\rangle=1.

(S.7)

Hence,
0\leq\gamma_{\mathcal{M}}\leq 1
, and it depends solely on the imperfect measurement
\mathcal{M}
; or, in the common cases discussed in the main text, the noisy detection channel
\mathcal{P}
that determines the effective
\mathcal{M}
, i.e., with all measurement elements then given by
M_{x}=\sum_{i}p\!\left(x|i\right)\Pi_{i}
, where
\Pi
is some fixed (perfect) projective measurement, so that the imperfect measurement is completely specified by the stochastic map,
\mathcal{P}\sim\{p(x|i)\}
, representing the readout noise. In such a situation, see also the classical interpretation below, we refer to
\gamma_{\mathcal{M}}
in Eq. (
S.6
) as
\gamma_{\mathcal{P}}
.
\blacksquare

V.2
Properties of
\gamma_{\mathcal{M}}

In this section we discuss several properties of
\gamma_{\mathcal{M}}
.

V.2.0.1
Classical interpretation of
\gamma_{\mathcal{P}}
.

For commuting
\{M_{x}\}_{x}
, namely when the imperfect measurement is specified by the noisy detection channel
\mathcal{P}\sim\{p(x|i)\}
such that
\forall_{x}:\;M_{x}=\sum_{i}p\!\left(x|i\right)\Pi_{i}
,

\gamma_{\mathcal{P}}\equiv\gamma_{\mathcal{M}}
has a classical interpretation. Note that in this case Eq. (
S.6
) reads:

\displaystyle\gamma_{\mathcal{P}}

\displaystyle=\underset{{\bf a},{\bf b}}{\max}\sum_{x}\frac{\left(\sum_{i}p\!\left(x|i\right)a_{i}b_{i}\right)^{2}}{\sum_{i}p\!\left(x|i\right)a_{i}^{2}},

(S.8)

where
{\bf a},{\bf b}
are real normalised, orthogonal vectors:
{\bf a}\cdot{\bf b}=0
,
|{\bf a}|^{2}=|{\bf b}|^{2}=1
. It is simple to see that
{\bf a},{\bf b}
can be assumed to be real vectors. In order to gain further intuition, we can define
p_{i}:=a_{i}^{2}
and a ‘derivative’
\mathrm{d}p_{i}:=a_{i}b_{i}
such that then
a_{i}=\sqrt{p_{i}}
and
b_{i}=2\mathrm{d}(\sqrt{p_{i}})
. As a result, it can be seen that the constraints of
|{\bf a}|^{2}=1
,
{\bf a}\cdot{\bf b}=0
,
|{\bf b}|^{2}=1
are equivalent to the constraints:
\sum_{i}p_{i}=1
,
\sum_{i}\mathrm{d}p_{i}=0
,
\sum_{i}\frac{\mathrm{d}p_{i}^{2}}{p_{i}}=1
. In nuce,
\left\{p_{i}\right\}_{i}
is a probability distribution,
\left\{\mathrm{d}p_{i}\right\}_{i}
is the derivative vector of the probability distribution and the constraint of
\sum_{i}\frac{\mathrm{d}p_{i}^{2}}{p_{i}}=1
is a normalization constraint: the original FI equals to
1
. In this new notation, Eq. (
S.8
) reads:

\gamma_{\mathcal{P}}=\underset{\boldsymbol{p},\boldsymbol{\mathrm{d}p}}{\max}\sum_{x}\frac{\left(\sum_{i}p\!\left(x|i\right)\mathrm{d}p_{i}\right)^{2}}{\sum_{i}p\!\left(x|i\right)p_{i}},

(S.9)

with the constraint of
\sum_{i}\frac{\mathrm{d}p_{i}^{2}}{p_{i}}=1
.
Hence,
\gamma_{\mathcal{P}}
can interpreted as the
optimal noisy classical FI
, optimised over all
\left\{p_{i}\right\}_{i},\left\{\mathrm{d}p_{i}\right\}_{i}

with the original FI of 1.

V.2.0.2
Data processing inequality
\gamma_{\mathcal{P}_{2}\circ\mathcal{P}_{1}}\leq\gamma_{\mathcal{P}_{1}}
.

Let us consider again the setting of
\gamma_{\mathcal{P}}
in Eq. (
S.8
) specified by the noisy detection channel,
\mathcal{P}
, that constitutes a stochastic map with entries
[\mathcal{P}]_{xi}=p(x|i)
and yields the imperfect measurement
\{M_{x}=\sum_{i}[\mathcal{P}]_{xi}\Pi_{i}\}_{x}
. Now, considering a composition of any two noisy detection channels
\mathcal{P}_{2}\circ\mathcal{P}_{1}
as an effective stochastic map, the corresponding imperfect measurement simply reads
\{\tilde{M}_{x}=\sum_{i}[\mathcal{P}_{2}\mathcal{P}_{1}]_{xi}\Pi_{i}\}_{x}
. We demonstrate that the resulting
\gamma
-coefficient (
S.8
) obtained via such a composition must be contractive, i.e.
\gamma_{\mathcal{P}_{2}\circ\mathcal{P}_{1}}\leq\gamma_{\mathcal{P}_{1}}
.

We prove this by first observing that Eq. (
S.9
) satisfies
\gamma_{\mathcal{P}}=\underset{\boldsymbol{p},\boldsymbol{\mathrm{d}p}}{\max}\sum_{x}\frac{\left(\sum_{i}p\!\left(x|i\right)\mathrm{d}p_{i}\right)^{2}}{\sum_{i}p\!\left(x|i\right)p_{i}}\leq\underset{\boldsymbol{p},\boldsymbol{\mathrm{d}p}}{\max}\sum_{i}\frac{\mathrm{d}p_{i}^{2}}{p_{i}}=1
, which follows from the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality
\sum_{x}\frac{\left(\sum_{i}p\!\left(x|i\right)\mathrm{d}p_{i}\right)^{2}}{\sum_{i}p\!\left(x|i\right)p_{i}}\leq\sum_{x}p\!\left(x|i\right)\left(2\mathrm{d}\sqrt{p_{i}}\right)^{2}=\sum_{i}\frac{\mathrm{d}p_{i}^{2}}{p_{i}}
. Now, if we consider Eq. (
S.9
) but for
\mathcal{P}=\mathcal{P}_{2}\circ\mathcal{P}_{1}
, we have
\gamma_{\mathcal{P}}=\underset{\boldsymbol{p},\boldsymbol{\mathrm{d}p}}{\max}\sum_{x}\frac{\left(\sum_{j,k}p_{2}\left(x|k\right)p_{1}\left(k|j\right)\mathrm{d}p_{j}\right)^{2}}{\sum_{j,k}p_{2}\left(x|k\right)p_{1}\left(k|j\right)p_{j}}
. Hence, defining
p^{\prime}_{k}=\sum_{j}p_{1}\!\left(k|j\right)p_{j}
, we similarly obtain by Cauchy-Schwarz inequality:
\gamma_{\mathcal{P}}=\underset{\boldsymbol{p},\boldsymbol{\mathrm{d}p}}{\max}\sum_{x}\frac{\left(\sum_{k}p_{2}\left(x|k\right)\mathrm{d}p^{\prime}_{k}\right)^{2}}{\sum_{k}p_{2}\left(x|k\right)p^{\prime}_{k}}\leq\underset{\boldsymbol{p},\boldsymbol{\mathrm{d}p}}{\max}\sum_{k}\frac{\left(\mathrm{d}p^{\prime}_{k}\right)^{2}}{p^{\prime}_{k}}=\gamma_{\mathcal{P}_{1}}
, which completes the proof.

V.2.0.3
Monotonically increasing with the number of probes, i.e.
\forall_{\mathcal{M}}:\;\gamma_{\mathcal{M}}\leq\gamma_{\mathcal{M}\otimes I}\leq\gamma_{\mathcal{M}\otimes\mathcal{M}}

.
Let us first prove that
\gamma_{\mathcal{M}\otimes I}\leq\gamma_{\mathcal{M}\otimes\mathcal{M}}
for any imperfect measurement
\mathcal{M}
, which follows from convexity. In general, for any two positive semidefinite operators
M_{1},M_{2}\geq 0
:

\displaystyle\frac{\left(\text{Re}\langle\xi_{\perp}|M_{1}|\xi\rangle\right)^{2}}{\langle\xi|M_{1}|\xi\rangle}+\frac{\left(\text{Re}\langle\xi_{\perp}|M_{2}|\xi\rangle\right)^{2}}{\langle\xi|M_{2}|\xi\rangle}\geq

\displaystyle\frac{\left(\text{Re}\langle\xi_{\perp}|M_{1}+M_{2}|\xi\rangle\right)^{2}}{\langle\xi|M_{1}+M_{2}|\xi\rangle},

(S.10)

which follows from the convexity of the function
\frac{x^{2}}{y}
, where we take
x_{i}=\text{Re}\langle\xi_{\perp}|M_{i}|\xi\rangle
,
y_{i}=\langle\xi|M_{i}|\xi\rangle
. This convexity implies:

\displaystyle\sum_{j,k}\frac{\left(\text{Re}\langle\xi_{\perp}|M_{j}\otimes M_{k}|\xi\rangle\right)^{2}}{\langle\xi|M_{j}\otimes M_{k}|\xi\rangle}

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{j}\frac{\left(\text{Re}\langle\xi_{\perp}|\sum_{k}M_{j}\otimes M_{k}|\xi\rangle\right)^{2}}{\langle\xi|\sum_{k}M_{j}\otimes M_{k}|\xi\rangle}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j}\frac{\left(\text{Re}\langle\xi_{\perp}|M_{j}\otimes I|\xi\rangle\right)^{2}}{\langle\xi|M_{j}\otimes I|\xi\rangle}

(S.11)

and, hence,
\gamma_{\mathcal{M}\otimes I}\leq\gamma_{\mathcal{M}\otimes\mathcal{M}}
.

Now,
\gamma_{\mathcal{M}}\leq\gamma_{\mathcal{M}\otimes I}
is assured by the fact that the maximisation over all orthogonal
\left|\xi\right\rangle
and
\left|\xi_{\perp}\right\rangle
in Eq. (
S.6
) defining 2-dimensional subspace in the support of
\mathcal{M}
, is trivially contained within the maximisation over all orthogonal
\left|\tilde{\xi}\right\rangle,\left|\tilde{\xi}_{\perp}\right\rangle
lying in the support of
\mathcal{M}\otimes I
. In particular, for any two
\left|\xi\right\rangle
and
\left|\xi_{\perp}\right\rangle
one may choose
\left|\tilde{\xi}\right\rangle=\left|\xi\right\rangle\left|\chi\right\rangle
and
\left|\tilde{\xi}_{\perp}\right\rangle=\left|\xi_{\perp}\right\rangle\left|\chi\right\rangle
with any
\left|\chi\right\rangle
, so that

\displaystyle\sum_{j}\frac{\left(\text{Re}\langle\xi_{\perp}|M_{j}|\xi\rangle\right)^{2}}{\langle\xi|M_{j}|\xi\rangle}=\sum_{j}\frac{\left(\text{Re}\langle\chi|\langle\xi_{\perp}|M_{j}\otimes I|\xi\rangle|\chi\rangle\right)^{2}}{\langle\chi|\langle\xi|M_{j}\otimes I|\xi\rangle|\chi\rangle}.

(S.12)

V.2.0.4
Sufficient condition for
\gamma_{\mathcal{M}}=1
.

We mention in the main text that a sufficient condition for a perfect QFI, i.e.
\gamma_{\mathcal{M}}=1
, is perfect distinguishability between two states: there exist two orthogonal states,
\left|\xi\right\rangle
and
\left|\xi_{\perp}\right\rangle
, such that for every
M_{x}
either
M_{x}\left|\xi\right\rangle=0
or
M_{x}\left|\xi_{\perp}\right\rangle=0
. In order to see this, observe from the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality (
S.7
) that
\gamma_{\mathcal{M}}=1
if and only if
there exist
\left|\xi\right\rangle,\left|\xi_{\perp}\right\rangle
such that
\sqrt{M_{x}}|\xi\rangle\propto\sqrt{M_{x}}|\xi_{\perp}\rangle
for every
x
. It is straightforward to see that given perfect distinguishability this condition is satisfied, with the proportionality constant being exactly zero for all
x
.

V.3
Unitary encoding with two-outcome measurement for a qubit: Optimal state and measurement

Using Bloch representation, our initial probe state is
\rho=\frac{1}{2}\big(\mathbbm{1}+\boldsymbol{r}_{0}\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma}\big)
, where the real Bloch vector has the usual constraint
|\boldsymbol{r}_{0}|^{2}=\mathrm{r}_{0\mathrm{x}}^{2}+\mathrm{r}_{0\mathrm{y}}^{2}+\mathrm{r}_{0\mathrm{z}}^{2}\leq 1
with equality for pure state. After the encoding with
U_{\theta}=\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}h\theta}
,
h=\sigma_{\mathrm{z}}/2
,
\rho
evolves to
\rho(\theta)=\frac{1}{2}\big(\mathbbm{1}+\boldsymbol{r}(\theta)\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma}\big)
, where
\mathrm{r}_{\mathrm{x}}(\theta)=\mathrm{r}_{0\mathrm{x}}\cos(\theta)+\mathrm{r}_{0\mathrm{y}}\sin(\theta),\mathrm{r}_{\mathrm{y}}(\theta)=-\mathrm{r}_{0\mathrm{x}}\sin(\theta)+\mathrm{r}_{0\mathrm{y}}\cos(\theta)
, and
\mathrm{r}_{\mathrm{z}}(\theta)=\mathrm{r}_{0\mathrm{z}}
. Moreover,
\dot{\boldsymbol{r}}(\theta)=\partial_{\theta}\boldsymbol{r}(\theta)
is perpendicular to
\boldsymbol{r}(\theta)
, with
|\dot{\boldsymbol{r}}|^{2}=\mathrm{r}_{0\mathrm{x}}^{2}+\mathrm{r}_{0\mathrm{y}}^{2}\leq 1
. The two-outcome measurement prior to the stochastic mapping are described by the operators
\Pi_{1,\vec{\phi}}=\frac{1}{2}\big(s\mathbbm{1}+\boldsymbol{m}(\vec{\phi})\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma}\big)
and
\Pi_{2,\vec{\phi}}=\frac{1}{2}\big((2-s)\mathbbm{1}-\boldsymbol{m}(\vec{\phi})\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma}\big)
, with the positive constraints
0<m\equiv|\boldsymbol{m}|\leq s^{*}\equiv\min\{s,2-s\}\leq 1
. In the main text we consider from onset projective measurements with
m=1
, but for the sake of mathematical completeness, let us for now allow any two-outcome measurement, and show later
m=1
is optimal indeed.

With
\vec{\phi}=\{m,\varphi,\vartheta\}
, we parametrize
\boldsymbol{m}(\vec{\phi})=m\big[\cos\varphi(\cos\vartheta\,\boldsymbol{b}_{1}+\sin\vartheta\,\boldsymbol{b}_{2})+\sin\varphi\,\boldsymbol{b}_{3}\big]
in the local Cartesian basis
\{\boldsymbol{b}_{1}=\boldsymbol{r}(\theta),\boldsymbol{b}_{2}=\dot{\boldsymbol{r}}/|\dot{\boldsymbol{r}}|,\boldsymbol{b}_{3}=\boldsymbol{b}_{1}\times\boldsymbol{b}_{2}\}
. The respective outcome probabilities are
p_{\theta,\vec{\phi}}(1)=\frac{1}{2}\big(s+\boldsymbol{m}\cdot\boldsymbol{r}\big)=\frac{1}{2}(s+m\cos\varphi\cos\vartheta)
and
p_{\theta,\vec{\phi}}(2)=1-p_{\theta,\vec{\phi}}(1)
, while
\dot{p}_{\theta,\vec{\phi}}(1)=-\dot{p}_{\theta,\vec{\phi}}(2)=\frac{1}{2}m\cos\varphi\sin\vartheta|\dot{\boldsymbol{r}}|
. Note that, while the
\theta
dependence are not seen explicit here, they are present still, as
\varphi
and
\vartheta
are defined with respect to
\theta
. For noisy detection channel that is specified by the stochastic mapping
\mathcal{P}\sim\{p(x|i)\}
, i.e.
\mathcal{M}\sim\{M_{x}=\sum_{i}p(x|i)\Pi_{i}\}
, we have
q_{\theta,\vec{\phi}}(x)=\sum_{i}p(x|i)p_{\theta,\vec{\phi}}(i)
, and the FI,
F=\sum_{x}f_{x}
, with

\displaystyle f_{x}

\displaystyle=\dot{q}_{\vec{\phi},\theta}(x)^{2}/q_{\theta,\vec{\phi}}(x)=\frac{\frac{1}{2}a_{x}^{2}}{b_{x}y^{2}+c_{x}y}\sin^{2}\vartheta|\dot{\boldsymbol{r}}|^{2}

(S.13)

where
y\equiv(m\cos\varphi)^{-1}\geq y^{*}\equiv 1/s^{*}\geq 1
,

a_{x}=p(x|1)-p(x|2),b_{x}=p(x|1)+(2-s)p(x|2)
, and
c_{x}=a_{x}\cos\vartheta
.

Consider now maximization of
f_{x}
over the input state and the measurement. Evidently, we should choose the input state such that
|\dot{\boldsymbol{r}}|=1
, i.e., pure state that lies in the equatorial plane of the Bloch sphere. This choice also means that
\boldsymbol{b}_{3}
is now
\boldsymbol{e}_{\mathrm{z}}
. Moreover, we should minimize the function
g_{x}(y)=b_{x}y^{2}+c_{x}y
in the denominator, subject to
y\geq y^{*}
. First then, we should choose
2-s=s^{*}
in
b_{x}
. Next, since
g_{x}
is a convex function, and the roots of
g_{x}
are 0 and
-c_{x}/b_{x}
for which
|c_{x}/b_{x}|\leq y^{*}
, we have
\min_{y\geq y^{*}}g_{x}(y)=g_{x}(y^{*})
. That is, we have
\varphi=0
and
m=s^{*}
, such that
\boldsymbol{m}
has no
\boldsymbol{b}_{3}=\boldsymbol{e}_{\mathrm{z}}
component.

To confirm that we should always choose projective measurement before the noisy detection channel whenever possible, i.e.,
m=s^{*}=1
, we put in
s^{*}=1/y^{*}
into
g_{x}(y^{*})
for an explicit convex function of
y^{*}
. One can then verify readily that the roots are now not greater than 1, and therefore, the optimal choice of
y^{*}
is 1. Finally, as all the above optimizations hold for all
f_{x}
, it follows that they apply to the total FI, and therefore

\displaystyle\bar{\mathcal{F}}^{\mathrm{(im)}}

\displaystyle=\max_{\rho}\max_{\vec{\phi}}F

\displaystyle=\max_{\vartheta}\sum_{x}\frac{\frac{1}{2}\big(p(x|1)-p(x|2)\big)^{2}\sin^{2}\vartheta}{p(x|1)+p(x|2)+\big(p(x|1)-p(x|2)\big)\cos\vartheta}.

(S.14)

Note that as Eq. (
V.3
) depends on
\vartheta
, which is an angle defined relative to
\boldsymbol{r}(\theta)
, it follows that in this case here we have a freedom to fix either the measurement or the input state and optimize over the other, as long as both are restricted to the equatorial plane. In particular, we may fix
\rho=\left|+_{\mathrm{y}}\right\rangle\left\langle+_{\mathrm{y}}\right|
, i.e.,
\boldsymbol{r}_{0}=\boldsymbol{e}_{\mathrm{y}}
, and optimize over
\{\Pi_{i,\vec{\phi}}=\left|\Pi_{i}\right\rangle\left\langle\Pi_{i}\right|\}
with
\left|\Pi_{1,2}\right\rangle=(\left|0\right\rangle\pm\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\phi}\left|1\right\rangle)/\sqrt{2}
, i.e.,
\boldsymbol{m}=\cos\phi\,\boldsymbol{e}_{\mathrm{x}}+\sin\phi\,\boldsymbol{e}_{\mathrm{y}}
. Equivalently, we may optimize over
\rho=\left|\psi\right\rangle\left\langle\psi\right|
with
\left|\psi\right\rangle=\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\phi\sigma_{\mathrm{z}}/2}\left|+_{\mathrm{y}}\right\rangle=(\left|0\right\rangle+\mathrm{i}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\phi}\left|1\right\rangle)/\sqrt{2}
, i.e.,
\boldsymbol{r}_{0}=-\sin\phi\,\boldsymbol{e}_{\mathrm{x}}+\cos\phi\,\boldsymbol{e}_{\mathrm{y}}
, with fixed
\Pi_{1,\vec{\phi}}=\left|+\right\rangle\left\langle+\right|,\Pi_{2,\vec{\phi}}=\left|-\right\rangle\left\langle-\right|
, i.e.,
\boldsymbol{m}=\boldsymbol{e}_{\mathrm{x}}
. Both give the same expression with
\vartheta=\theta+\phi-\pi/2
in Eq. (
V.3
), which turns into
Eq. (9) in the main text
.

V.4
Relations of quantum metrology with imperfect measurements to the “standard” setting of noisy parameter encoding

In this section, we discuss in detail the differences and relation between our quantum metrology with imperfect measurement model and the “standard” noisy metrology model. In the latter, the measurement is taken to be perfect, and the noise is described by a CPTP channel that acts before the measurement stage.

Fig. S1:
Different models for noisy quantum metrology.
(a) Quantum metrology with imperfect measurement
\mathcal{M}
.
\mathcal{V}_{\vec{\phi}}
is some control unitary operation for optimizing the measurement basis. (b) Equivalent picture of (a), with
\Pi
being a projective measurement which can be chosen and fixed arbitrarily, and
\Lambda
is a CPTP channel. (c) Quantum metrology with “standard” noisy parameter encoding, where the noise, described by a CPTP channel
\Lambda
, is
independent
of the choice of the measurement settings
\vec{\phi}
.

Firstly, let us denote
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}_{d})
as the set of bounded linear
operators on the Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}_{d}
with dimension
d
. Then, recall that our quantum metrology with imperfect measurement on the physical level is as depicted in Fig.
S1
(a): an encoded qudit described by the state
\rho(\theta)\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}_{d})
, undergoes a control unitary operation
\mathcal{V}_{\vec{\phi}}\sim\{V_{\vec{\phi}}\}
such that
\rho(\theta)\rightarrow\mathcal{V}_{\vec{\phi}}[\rho(\theta)]=V_{\vec{\phi}}\rho(\theta)V_{\vec{\phi}}^{\dagger}
, which is then subject to an imperfect measurement
\mathcal{M}\sim\{M_{x}\}_{x=1}^{|X|}
. Here, all the
M_{x}
and the
V_{\vec{\phi}}
are elements of
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}_{d})
as well. The probability of getting the outcome
x
is given by the Born’s rule,
q_{\theta,\vec{\phi}}(x)=\mathrm{Tr}\{M_{x}V_{\vec{\phi}}\rho(\theta)V_{\vec{\phi}}^{\dagger}\}
.

We can also think of it in a mathematically equivalent picture, as in Fig.
S1
(b),
where we describe the imperfect measurement
\mathcal{M}\sim\{M_{x}\}_{x=1}^{|X|}
rather by a noisy CPTP channel
\Lambda
that is followed by a perfect projective measurement
\Pi\sim\{\Pi_{x}=\left|x\right\rangle\!\left\langle x\right|\}_{x=1}^{|X|}
. That is, we can always find some quantum (CPTP) map
\Lambda\sim\{k_{\ell}\}_{\ell}
that allows for the
conjugate-map decomposition
of
\mathcal{M}
, i.e.:

\forall_{x=1,\cdots,|X|}:\quad M_{x}=\Lambda^{\dagger}[\Pi_{x}]=\sum_{\ell}k_{\ell}^{\dagger}\Pi_{x}k_{\ell},

(S.15)

or, rewriting the above in a compact way:

\mathcal{M}=\Lambda^{\dagger}[\Pi].

(S.16)

Note that: (i) the channel
\Lambda:\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}_{d})\rightarrow\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}_{|X|})
acts on operators of
d
dimension and outputs operators of
|X|
dimension; (ii) given
\mathcal{M}
, there are multiple
\Lambda
(and
\Pi
) satisfying Eq. (
S.16
); and (iii)
\Lambda
is defined
independently
of the control unitary
\mathcal{V}_{\vec{\phi}}
—one can consider the l.h.s. of Eq. (
S.15
) as
V_{\vec{\phi}}^{\dagger}M_{x}V_{\vec{\phi}}
instead, and define
\vec{\phi}
-dependent
\Lambda
, but doing so is neither necessary nor helpful.

One can prove that the condition (
S.15
), or (
S.16
), can always be satisfied, by considering as an example the
quantum-classical channel
that we used as well in the main text (Lemma 3 and Corollary 2)
3
3

3

Note that, in the main text, the quantum-classical channel
\Lambda_{\theta,\vec{\phi}}
is defined together with the encoding
\mathcal{E}_{\theta}
as well as the control operation, i.e.
\Lambda_{\theta,\vec{\phi}}=\Lambda\circ\mathcal{V}_{\vec{\phi}}\circ\mathcal{E}_{\theta}
, namely,

\displaystyle\Lambda\sim\Big\{\left|x\right\rangle\left\langle i\right|\sqrt{M_{x}}\Big\}_{x,i}\quad x=1,\dots,|X|;\quad i=1,\cdots,d.

(S.17)

Another example of a CPTP map that also always satisfies Eq. (
S.16
) is

\displaystyle\Lambda\sim\Big\{\sum_{x=1}^{|X|}\left|x\right\rangle\left\langle i\right|\sqrt{M_{x}}\Big\}_{i=1}^{d},

(S.18)

whose rank is now
d
, rather than

in Eq. (
S.17
).

Note that, both the orthogonal sets of basis ket

and bra

in Eqs. (
S.17
) and (
S.18
) can essentially be understood as flags, and can be chosen arbitrarily. Importantly, despite this equivalent picture in Fig.
S1
(b), it is however still not the same as the “standard” noisy metrology scheme as depicted in Fig.

(c), where the noise, described by a CPTP map
\Lambda
, acts

of the choice of the control unitary
\mathcal{V}_{\vec{\phi}}
. The crucial difference is that, in the former the control operation

the channel
\Lambda
, whereas in the latter it is applied

the channel
\Lambda
. Then, unless
\mathcal{V}_{\vec{\phi}}
commutes with
\Lambda

Let us still study more closely the two models from the perspective of parameter estimation, whereby the focus is on the QFI and channel QFI (perfect versus imperfect). Denote
F[\rho(\theta),\mathcal{M}]
as the classical Fisher information for
\theta
with the state
\rho(\theta)

\mathcal{M}\sim\{M_{x}\}_{x}
, i.e.,
F[\rho(\theta),\mathcal{M}]=\sum_{x}\frac{(\partial_{\theta}\mathrm{Tr}\{\rho(\theta)M_{x}\})^{2}}{\mathrm{Tr}\{\rho(\theta)M_{x}\}}
. Then, for the imperfect measurement model, the imperfect QFI is given by
(see Eq. (5) in the main text)
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{\scriptstyle{(im)}}}

\displaystyle=\max_{\vec{\phi}}F[\mathcal{V}_{\vec{\phi}}[\rho(\theta)],\Lambda^{\dagger}[\Pi]]

\displaystyle:=F[\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{opt}}[\rho(\theta)],\Lambda^{\dagger}[\Pi]]=F[(\Lambda\circ\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{opt}})[\rho(\theta)],\Pi].

(S.19)

Note that the optimal control unitary
\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{opt}}\sim\{V_{\mathrm{opt}}\}
generally depends on the given
\rho(\theta)
, though for simplicity of the notation we will not write out this dependence explicitly for now. It then follows that

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{\scriptstyle{(im)}}}

\displaystyle\leq\max_{W}F[(\Lambda\circ\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{opt}})[\rho(\theta)],W\Pi W^{\dagger}]

\displaystyle:=\mathcal{F}[(\Lambda\circ\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{opt}})[\rho(\theta)]],

(S.20)

where
W
is a unitary operator in
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}_{|X|})
, and so we may formally upper bound the imperfect QFI by the QFI of
(\Lambda\circ\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{opt}})[\rho(\theta)]
, which can be interpreted as a state that undergoes a “standard” noisy channel
\Lambda\circ\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{opt}}
. Moreover, given the imperfect measurement
\mathcal{M}
, one can further optimize over different possible
\Lambda
that satisfies Eq. (
S.16
), and get

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{\scriptstyle{(im)}}}

\displaystyle\leq\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}\Lambda\\
\Lambda^{\dagger}[\Pi]=\mathcal{M}\end{subarray}}\mathcal{F}[(\Lambda\circ\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{opt}})[\rho(\theta)]].

(S.21)

Despite the established formal relations Eqs. (
V.4
) and (
S.21
), note that
\Lambda\circ\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{opt}}
is defined using the knowledge about optimal control unitary. However, if we had known

\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{opt}}
, we would have already in fact obtained
\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{\scriptstyle{(im)}}}
, and there is no need for the upper bounds. In other words, the formal bounds Eqs. (
V.4
) and (
S.21
) are not that meaningful in practice.

V.4.1
Proof of the Observation 1 in Methods

Still, there is a special case worth mentioning, where we can indeed meaningfully evaluate a QFI-based upper bound without solving exactly for the
\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{opt}}
. Suppose that from the symmetry of the estimation problem we know that the optimal control operation must also carry it, so that its optimisation may be restricted to elements in some compact group
G
, i.e.,
\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{opt}}\in G
. Then, if
\Lambda
satisfying Eq. (
S.16
) is known to be
G
-covariant
, i.e.,

\forall_{g\in G}:\;\Lambda\circ\mathcal{V}_{g}=\mathcal{W}_{g}\circ\Lambda,

(S.22)

where
\mathcal{W}_{g}
is some unitary representation of
G
in
\mathcal{H}_{|X|}
— in particular, for
\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{opt}}
correspondingly to
\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{opt}}
in Eq. (
S.22
),
we have from Eq. (
V.4
) that

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{\scriptstyle{(im)}}}

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{F}[(\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{opt}}\circ\Lambda)[\rho(\theta)]]=\mathcal{F}[\Lambda[\rho(\theta)]],

(S.23)

where the equality in (
S.23
) follows from the fact that QFI is invariant under parameter-independent unitary transformation.
\blacksquare

Extension to channel QFI is straightforward. Let us denote the (perfect) parameter-encoding channel as
\mathcal{E}_{\theta}
, such that
\rho(\theta)=\mathcal{E}_{\theta}[\rho]
for the input probe state
\rho
, which we will eventually optimize over. For the imperfect measurement case, we have by definition
(see Eq. (5) in the main text)
:

\displaystyle\bar{\mathcal{F}}^{\mathrm{\scriptstyle{(im)}}}

\displaystyle=\max_{\rho}\max_{\vec{\phi}}F[(\mathcal{V}_{\vec{\phi}}\circ\mathcal{E}_{\theta})[\rho],\Lambda^{\dagger}[\Pi]]

\displaystyle:=F[(\Lambda\circ\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{opt}}\circ\mathcal{E}_{\theta})[\rho_{\mathrm{opt}}],\Pi]

\displaystyle=\sum_{x}\frac{(\mathrm{Tr}\{(\Lambda\circ\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{opt}}\circ\dot{\mathcal{E}}_{\theta})[\rho_{\mathrm{opt}}]\Pi_{x}\})^{2}}{\mathrm{Tr}\{(\Lambda\circ\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{opt}}\circ\mathcal{E}_{\theta})[\rho_{\mathrm{opt}}]\Pi_{x}\}},

(S.24)

where
\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{opt}}
and
\rho_{\mathrm{opt}}
are respectively the optimal control unitary and input state, and
\dot{\mathcal{E}}_{\theta}=\partial_{\theta}\mathcal{E}_{\theta}
is the derivative of the encoding channel w.r.t. the parameter, such that for any
\rho(\theta)=\mathcal{E}_{\theta}[\rho]
,
\partial_{\theta}\rho(\theta)=\dot{\mathcal{E}}_{\theta}[\rho]
. Similarly to Eq. (
V.4
), one then obtains

\displaystyle\bar{\mathcal{F}}^{\mathrm{\scriptstyle{(im)}}}

\displaystyle\leq\max_{\sigma}\max_{W}F[(\Lambda\circ\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{opt}}\circ\mathcal{E}_{\theta})[\sigma],W\Pi W^{\dagger}]

\displaystyle:=\bar{\mathcal{F}}[(\Lambda\circ\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{opt}}\circ\mathcal{E}_{\theta})],

(S.25)

where
\sigma
is some state in
\mathcal{H}_{d}
,
W
is a unitary operator in
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}_{{|X|}})
, and
\bar{\mathcal{F}}
is the channel QFI. Again, although Eq. (
V.4.1
) now provides a formal relation between the imperfect channel QFI and the channel QFI of a “standard” noisy encoding
(\Lambda\circ\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{opt}}\circ\mathcal{E}_{\theta})
, it requires knowledge of the optimal control unitary
\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{opt}}
(which implicitly depends on the optimal state
\rho_{\mathrm{opt}}
), and is hence in general not immediately applicable.

Still, when we know that, thanks to some symmetry of the problem, the optimisation over control
\mathcal{V}_{\vec{\phi}}
can be restricted to elements of some compact group
G
, meaningful upper bound on the imperfect channel QFI can be formulated. Firstly, following directly from the Observation 1, in case the conjugate map
\Lambda
satisfies not only Eq. (
S.16
) but also the
G
-covariant condition Eq. (
S.22
), we can immediately conclude by maximising Eq. (
S.23
) over the input probe-states that
\bar{\mathcal{F}}^{\mathrm{\scriptstyle{(im)}}}\leq\bar{\mathcal{F}}[(\Lambda\circ\mathcal{E}_{\theta})]
.

V.4.2
Proof of the Observation 2 in Methods

Secondly, suppose that the encoding channel
\mathcal{E}_{\theta}
, as well as its derivate,
\dot{\mathcal{E}}_{\theta}=\partial_{\theta}\mathcal{E}_{\theta}
, are both
G
-covariant locally around the parameter value
\theta
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\forall_{g\in G}:\;\mathcal{V}_{g}\circ\mathcal{E}_{\theta}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{E}_{\theta}\circ\mathcal{W}_{g},

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}_{g}\circ\dot{\mathcal{E}}_{\theta}

\displaystyle=\dot{\mathcal{E}}_{\theta}\circ\mathcal{W}_{g},

(S.26)

where
\mathcal{W}_{g}
is some unitary representation of
G
in
\mathcal{H}_{d}
— in particular,
\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{opt}}
correspondingly for
\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{opt}}
.
In this case, from (
V.4.1
),

\displaystyle\bar{\mathcal{F}}^{\mathrm{\scriptstyle{(im)}}}

\displaystyle\leq\bar{\mathcal{F}}[(\Lambda\circ\mathcal{E}_{\theta}\circ\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{opt}})]=\bar{\mathcal{F}}[(\Lambda\circ\mathcal{E}_{\theta})],

(S.27)

where the equality in (
S.27
) follows from the fact that channel QFI is invariant under parameter-independent unitary transformation on the input probe state. Note that it is necessary to include the local
G
-covariant condition for
\dot{\mathcal{E}}_{\theta}
here, as FI is not a function of the state alone but also its derivative, c.f. explicitly Eq. (
V.4.1
) and Eq. (
V.4.3
) below.
\blacksquare

V.4.3
Computing
\bar{\mathcal{F}}[(\Lambda\circ\mathcal{E}_{\theta})]
in Eq. (
S.27
) as an SDP via a ‘seesaw’ method

The QFI is defined as a function of a quantum state
\rho\equiv\rho(\theta)
and its derivative
\dot{\rho}\equiv\partial_{\theta}\rho(\theta)
, as follows
Braunstein and Caves 1994
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}[\rho,\dot{\rho}]:=\begin{array}[]{c}\mathrm{Tr}\!\left\{\rho L^{2}\right\}=\mathrm{Tr}\!\left\{\dot{\rho}L\right\}\\
\textnormal{s.t.}\;\dot{\rho}=\frac{1}{2}\left\{\rho,L\right\}\end{array}

.

Equivalently, it may be expressed as the maximisation of the
error propagation formula
over all the quantum observables, i.e., Hermitian operators
O=O^{\dagger}
, as
(
Escher 2012
)
:

\mathcal{F}[\rho,\dot{\rho}]=\max_{O=O^{\dagger}}\frac{\left|\left\langle\dot{O}\right\rangle\right|^{2}}{\Delta^{2}O}=\max_{O=O^{\dagger}}\frac{\left|\mathrm{Tr}\!\left\{\dot{\rho}O\right\}\right|^{2}}{\mathrm{Tr}\!\left\{\rho O^{2}\right\}-\mathrm{Tr}\!\left\{\rho O\right\}^{2}},

(S.30)

which is always maximised by
O_{\mathrm{opt}}=L-\mathrm{Tr}\!\left\{\rho L\right\}

with
L
being the SLD operator defined implicitly in Eq. (
V.4.3
).

However, the fraction in Eq. (
S.30
) can always be rewritten by introducing another maximisation, i.e.:

\frac{\left|\left\langle\dot{O}\right\rangle\right|^{2}}{\Delta^{2}O}=\max_{\alpha\in\mathbb{R}}\left\{-\alpha^{2}\Delta^{2}O+2\alpha\left|\left\langle\dot{O}\right\rangle\right|\right\},

(S.31)

with the maximum occurring at
\alpha_{\mathrm{opt}}=\frac{\left|\left\langle\dot{O}\right\rangle\right|}{\Delta^{2}O}
.
Hence, we may write again Eq. (
S.30
) as

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}[\rho,\dot{\rho}]

\displaystyle=\max_{O=O^{\dagger}}\max_{\alpha\in\mathbb{R}}\left\{-\alpha^{2}\Delta^{2}O+2\alpha\left|\left\langle\dot{O}\right\rangle\right|\right\}

(S.32)

\displaystyle=\max_{O^{\prime}=O^{\prime\dagger}}\left\{-\Delta^{2}O^{\prime}+2\left|\left\langle\dot{O}^{\prime}\right\rangle\right|\right\},

(S.33)

while noticing that the two maximisations can be recast into one by defining
O^{\prime}:=\alpha O
. Moreover, for any
O^{\prime}
above we may define a shifted operator
X:=O^{\prime}-\left\langle O^{\prime}\right\rangle
, so that substituting
O^{\prime}=X+\mathrm{Tr}\!\left\{\rho O^{\prime}\right\}

into Eq. (
S.33
), we obtain
(
Macieszczak 2013
)
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}[\rho,\dot{\rho}]

\displaystyle=\max_{X=X^{\dagger}}\left\{-\left\langle X^{2}\right\rangle+2\left|\left\langle\dot{X}\right\rangle\right|\right\}

(S.34)

\displaystyle=\max_{X=X^{\dagger}}\left\{-\left\langle X^{2}\right\rangle+2\left\langle\dot{X}\right\rangle\right\},

(S.35)

where the maximum is now performed over all Hermitian operators
X=X^{\dagger}
. We have also dropped the absolute value, as
\left\langle\dot{X}\right\rangle=\mathrm{Tr}\!\left\{\dot{\rho}X\right\}=\partial_{\theta}\mathrm{Tr}\!\left\{\rho X\right\}
is real, while the first (quadratic) term above is unaffected by the
X\to-X
transformation—and so must be the maximal value attained in Eq. (
S.35
).

Let us note that Eq. (
S.35
) constitutes a valid lower bound on the QFI for any fixed
X
, i.e.

\forall_{X=X^{\dagger},\rho,\dot{\rho}}:\;-\mathrm{Tr}\!\left\{\rho X^{2}\right\}+2\mathrm{Tr}\!\left\{\dot{\rho}X\right\}\;\leq\;\mathcal{F}[\rho,\dot{\rho}],

(S.36)

while the optimal
X_{\mathrm{opt}}
, yielding the maximum in Eq. (
S.35
), is related to the actual optimal observable
O_{\mathrm{opt}}
in Eq. (
S.30
) via

\displaystyle X_{\mathrm{opt}}

\displaystyle=O^{\prime}_{\mathrm{opt}}-\left\langle O^{\prime}_{\mathrm{opt}}\right\rangle=\alpha_{\mathrm{opt}}\left(O_{\mathrm{opt}}-\left\langle O_{\mathrm{opt}}\right\rangle\right)

(S.37)

\displaystyle=\frac{\left|\left\langle\dot{O}_{\mathrm{opt}}\right\rangle\right|}{\Delta^{2}O_{\mathrm{opt}}}\left(O_{\mathrm{opt}}-\left\langle O_{\mathrm{opt}}\right\rangle\right).

(S.38)

Hence, by substituting further
O_{\mathrm{opt}}=L-\mathrm{Tr}\!\left\{\rho L\right\}
, we can explicitly relate
X_{\mathrm{opt}}

to the SLD and the QFI, i.e.:

X_{\mathrm{opt}}=\frac{\mathcal{F}[\rho,\dot{\rho}]}{\left|\mathrm{Tr}\!\left\{\dot{\rho}L\right\}\right|}\left(L-\mathrm{Tr}\!\left\{\rho L\right\}\right).

(S.39)

Let us consider for our purposes the case of unitary parameter encoding, i.e.
\mathcal{E}_{\theta}=\mathcal{U}_{\theta}\sim\{\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\theta H}\}
so that
\dot{\rho}=\mathrm{i}[\rho,H]
, but the following analysis can be directly generalised to allow for arbitrary
\mathcal{E}_{\theta}
and
\dot{\mathcal{E}}_{\theta}
. Then, using the expression (
V.4.3
) for the QFI, we may rewrite the potentially valid—e.g. given the
G
-covariance (
S.22
) or (
S.26
)—upper bound on the imperfect QFI
\bar{\mathcal{F}}^{\mathrm{\scriptstyle{(im)}}}
in Eq. (
S.27
) for the encoding
U_{\theta}=\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\theta H}
as:

\displaystyle\bar{\mathcal{F}}[H,\Lambda]

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\bar{\mathcal{F}}[(\Lambda\circ\mathcal{U}_{\theta})]

(S.40)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\max_{\sigma\geq 0}\;\mathcal{F}\!\left[\Lambda[\sigma],\Lambda\!\left[\mathrm{i}[\sigma,H]\right]\right]

(S.41)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\max_{\sigma\geq 0}\begin{array}[]{c}\mathrm{i}\,\mathrm{Tr}\!\left\{\left[\sigma,H\right]\Lambda^{\dagger}\!\left[L\right]\right\}\\
\textnormal{s.t.}\;\Lambda\!\left[\mathrm{i}[\sigma,H]\right]=\frac{1}{2}\left\{\Lambda[\sigma],L\right\}\end{array}.

Moreover, we may now use Eq. (
S.35
) to further re-express the above channel QFI (for
\Lambda\circ\mathcal{U}_{\theta}
) as

\bar{\mathcal{F}}[H,\Lambda]=\max_{\sigma\geq 0}\max_{X=X^{\dagger}}\mathrm{Tr}\!\left\{\sigma\left(-\Lambda^{\dagger}\!\left[X^{2}\right]+2\mathrm{i}\left[H,\Lambda^{\dagger}[X]\right]\right)\right\}.

(S.45)

As a result, we can formulate a numerical
‘seesaw’ algorithm
that allows us to compute the channel QFI (
V.4.3
) by exploiting Eq. (
S.45
), as follows
(
Macieszczak 2013
)
:

1.

Select (randomly) a starting state
\sigma_{0}
and calculate the corresponding QFI
\mathcal{F}
as well as the SLD
L
it would lead to in Eq. (
V.4.3
), i.e. without performing the maximisation over the input states
\sigma
.

2.

Use the obtained
\mathcal{F}
and
L
to compute the optimal operator
X_{0}=X_{\mathrm{opt}}
according to Eq. (
S.39
), which then maximises Eq. (
S.45
) for the fixed state
\sigma_{0}
.

3.

Maximise the expression (
S.45
) over the input states with the operator
X_{0}
being now fixed, in order to determine the best state
\sigma_{1}
that then yields
the tightest lower bound (
S.36
) on QFI for
X_{0}
.

4.

Return to step 1 and use
\sigma_{1}
as the new starting state.

The above procedure is computationally efficient, as
\mathcal{F}
and
L
in step 1 are obtained solving a linear
programme, as in Eq. (
V.4.3
), while finding the optimal input state in step 3 corresponds to solving the maximal eigenvalue of a Hermitian operator defined within
(\dots)
of Eq. (
S.45
). Although the convergence of the algorithm is generally assured
(
Macieszczak 2013
)
, even if its rate is slow, at any stage it yields a valid lower bound (
S.36
) on the channel QFI (
S.45
).

V.4.4
Maximising
\bar{\mathcal{F}}[(\Lambda\circ\mathcal{E}_{\theta})]
in Eq. (
S.27
) further, over all conjugate-map decompositions of an imperfect measurement

Recall that
\Lambda
in Eq. (
S.27
), and so in Eq. (
S.45
), corresponds to some valid conjugate-map decomposition of a given imperfect measurement
\mathcal{M}
, for which it must fulfil the condition (
S.16
). As we now show, the above ‘seesaw’ formulation allows naturally to incorporate in Eq. (
S.45
) also the maximisation over all such conjugate maps, i.e. all quantum (CPTP) channels
\Lambda
satisfying
\mathcal{M}=\Lambda^{\dagger}[\Pi]
for some projective measurement
\Pi
.

Let us define the corresponding maximum as

\displaystyle\bar{\mathcal{F}}[H,\mathcal{M}]

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\max_{\Lambda\in\mathrm{CPTP}}\begin{array}[]{c}\bar{\mathcal{F}}[H,\Lambda]\\
\mathrm{s.t.}\;\Lambda^{\dagger}[\Pi]=\mathcal{M}\end{array},

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}\Lambda\in\mathrm{CPTP}\\
\sigma\geq 0\end{subarray}}\begin{array}[]{c}\mathrm{i}\,\mathrm{Tr}\!\left\{\left[\sigma,H\right]\Lambda^{\dagger}\!\left[L\right]\right\}\\
\mathrm{s.t.}\;\begin{array}[]{c}\Lambda^{\dagger}[\Pi]=\mathcal{M}\\
\Lambda\left[\mathrm{i}[\sigma,H]\right]=\frac{1}{2}\left\{\Lambda\left[\sigma\right],L\right\}\end{array}\end{array},

where have substituted for
\bar{\mathcal{F}}[H,\Lambda]
according to Eq. (
V.4.3
). Note that in the above expression any projective measurement,
\Pi
, can be used as a reference, because
\bar{\mathcal{F}}[H,\mathcal{M}]
is invariant under the transformation
\Pi\to W\Pi W^{\dagger}
,
L\to WLW^{\dagger}
and
\Lambda[\bullet]\to W\Lambda[\bullet]W^{\dagger}
, which implies
\Lambda^{\dagger}[\bullet]\to\Lambda^{\dagger}[W^{\dagger}\bullet W]
, for any unitary
W
.

Now, similarly to Eq. (
S.45
), we rewrite Eq. (
V.4.4
) as

\bar{\mathcal{F}}[H,\mathcal{M}]=\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}\Lambda\in\mathrm{CPTP}\\
\sigma\geq 0,X=X^{\dagger}\end{subarray}}\begin{array}[]{c}\mathrm{Tr}\!\left\{\sigma\left(-\Lambda^{\dagger}\!\left[X^{2}\right]+2\mathrm{i}\!\left[H,\Lambda^{\dagger}[X]\right]\right)\right\}\\
\mathrm{s.t.}\;\Lambda^{\dagger}[\Pi]=\mathcal{M}\end{array},

(S.54)

which we simplify further by denoting the action of any CPTP map
\Lambda
via its Choi-Jamiołkowski (CJ) state
(
Bengtsson and Życzkowski 2006
)
.

In particular, for any input state
\sigma
it is true that
\Lambda[\sigma]=\mathrm{Tr}_{\mathrm{B}}\!\left\{\varrho_{\Lambda}(\mathbb{I}\otimes\sigma^{T})\right\}
, where the CJ state of the map
\Lambda
is defined as
\varrho_{\Lambda}:=\Lambda\otimes\mathcal{I}\left[\left|\!\left.\mathbb{I}\right\rangle\right\rangle\!\!\left\langle\left\langle\mathbb{I}\right.\!\right|\right]
. Here, any square matrix
A
defines a bipartite state
\left|\!\left.A\right\rangle\right\rangle=\sum_{ij}A_{ij}\left|i,j\right\rangle_{\mathrm{AB}}=\sum_{ij}A_{ij}\left|i\right\rangle_{\mathrm{A}}\left|j\right\rangle_{\mathrm{B}}
, so that
\left|\!\left.\mathbb{I}\right\rangle\right\rangle=\sum_{i}\left|i,i\right\rangle_{\mathrm{AB}}
is the (unnormalised) maximally entangled state. Then, it is straightforward to prove that the CJ state for the conjugate map of
\Lambda
, i.e.
\Lambda^{\dagger}
, is nothing but

\varrho_{\Lambda^{\dagger}}=\mathbb{S}_{\mathrm{AB}}\varrho_{\Lambda}^{*}\mathbb{S}_{\mathrm{AB}}
,
where
\mathbb{S}_{\mathrm{AB}}
is the swap operator such that
\mathbb{S}_{\mathrm{AB}}\left|\psi\right\rangle_{\mathrm{A}}\left|\phi\right\rangle_{\mathrm{B}}=\left|\phi\right\rangle_{\mathrm{A}}\left|\psi\right\rangle_{\mathrm{B}}

for any states
\left|\phi\right\rangle
and
\left|\psi\right\rangle
.
Consequently, the action of
\Lambda^{\dagger}
corresponds to

\displaystyle\forall_{X=X^{\dagger}}:\;\Lambda^{\dagger}[X]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}_{\mathrm{B}}\!\left\{\varrho_{\Lambda^{\dagger}}(\mathbb{I}\otimes X^{T})\right\}

(S.55)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}_{\mathrm{B}}\!\left\{\mathbb{S}_{\mathrm{AB}}\varrho_{\Lambda}^{*}\mathbb{S}_{\mathrm{AB}}(\mathbb{I}\otimes X^{T})\mathbb{S}_{\mathrm{AB}}\mathbb{S}_{\mathrm{AB}}\right\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}_{\mathrm{A}}\!\left\{\varrho_{\Lambda}^{*}(X^{T}\otimes\mathbb{I})\right\},

(S.56)

and the TP-property of
\Lambda
, imposing
\mathrm{Tr}_{\mathrm{A}}\varrho_{\Lambda}=\mathbb{I}
, ensures consistently that
\mathrm{Tr}_{\mathrm{B}}\varrho_{\Lambda^{\dagger}}=\mathrm{Tr}_{\mathrm{B}}\!\left\{\mathbb{S}_{\mathrm{AB}}\varrho_{\Lambda}^{*}\mathbb{S}_{\mathrm{AB}}\right\}=\left(\mathrm{Tr}_{\mathrm{A}}\!\left\{\varrho_{\Lambda}\right\}\right)^{\star}=\mathbb{I}
,
so that the conjugate map is indeed always unital, i.e.
\Lambda^{\dagger}[\mathbb{I}]=\mathbb{I}
.

Finally, using Eq. (
S.56
) to replace the maximisation over quantum maps
\Lambda
in Eq. (
S.54
) by the corresponding CJ states
\varrho_{\Lambda}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\bar{\mathcal{F}}[H,\mathcal{M}]

\displaystyle=\max_{\varrho_{\Lambda}\geq 0}\max_{\sigma\geq 0}\max_{X=X^{\dagger}}\quad\begin{array}[]{l}-\mathrm{Tr}\!\left\{\sigma\,\mathrm{Tr}_{\mathrm{A}}\!\left\{\varrho_{\Lambda}^{*}\left((X^{2})^{T}\otimes\mathbb{I}\right)\right\}\right\}+2\mathrm{i}\mathrm{Tr}\!\left\{\sigma\left[H,\mathrm{Tr}_{\mathrm{A}}\!\left\{\varrho_{\Lambda}^{*}(X^{T}\otimes\mathbb{I})\right\}\right]\right\}\\
\mathrm{s.t.}\;\forall_{x}:\,\mathrm{Tr}_{\mathrm{A}}\!\left\{\varrho_{\Lambda}^{*}\left((\Pi_{x})^{T}\otimes\mathbb{I}\right)\right\}=M_{x},\;\mathrm{Tr}_{\mathrm{A}}\varrho_{\Lambda}=\mathbb{I}\end{array}

\displaystyle=\max_{\varrho_{\Lambda}\geq 0}\max_{\sigma\geq 0}\max_{X=X^{\dagger}}\quad\begin{array}[]{l}-\mathrm{Tr}\!\left\{\varrho_{\Lambda}^{*}\left((X^{2})^{T}\otimes\sigma\right)\right\}+2\mathrm{i}\mathrm{Tr}\!\left\{\varrho_{\Lambda}^{*}(X^{T}\otimes\left[\sigma,H\right])\right\}\\
\mathrm{s.t.}\;\forall_{x}:\,\mathrm{Tr}_{\mathrm{A}}\!\left\{\varrho_{\Lambda}^{*}\left((\Pi_{x})^{T}\otimes\mathbb{I}\right)\right\}=M_{x},\;\mathrm{Tr}_{\mathrm{A}}\varrho_{\Lambda}=\mathbb{I}\end{array},

which we evaluate by adding to the aforementioned ‘seesaw’ algorithm one more step in which we maximise over
\varrho_{\Lambda}\geq 0
(given the linear constraints to reproduce the elements of the imperfect measurement), while fixing the input state,
\sigma
, and the Hermitian operator,
X
.

V.4.5
Example 1: Single-qubit phase sensing with bit-flip noise

Let us first illustrate the subtleties of
G
-covariance, and limited applicability of the Observation 1 discussed in Sec.
V.4.1
for the
imperfect QFI
,
\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{\scriptstyle{(im)}}}
, by considering the unitary encoding
\mathcal{E}_{\theta}\sim\{\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\theta\sigma_{z}/2}\}
on a qubit, with imperfect measurement
\mathcal{M}\sim\{M_{1}=\mathsf{p}\left|+\right\rangle\!\left\langle+\right|+(1-\mathsf{q})\left|-\right\rangle\!\left\langle-\right|,M_{2}=\mathsf{q}\left|+\right\rangle\!\left\langle+\right|+(1-\mathsf{p})\left|-\right\rangle\!\left\langle-\right|\}
with
0\leq\mathsf{p},\mathsf{q}\leq 1
. Consider also the input probe state in the equatorial Bloch plane, such that for example the encoded state is
\rho(\theta)=\left|\psi(\theta)\right\rangle\!\left\langle\psi(\theta)\right|,\left|\psi(\theta)\right\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\left|0\right\rangle+\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\theta}\left|1\right\rangle)
.

As we have established above in Sec.
V.3
, the optimal control unitary in this case must have the structure
V_{\mathrm{opt}}=\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\varphi_{\mathrm{opt}}\sigma_{z}}
for some
\varphi_{\mathrm{opt}}
and, hence, belongs to the U(1) group with unitary representation:
\mathcal{V}_{g}\sim\{\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}g\sigma_{z}}\}
and
g\in[0,2\pi)
. Consequently, when
\Lambda
can be chosen to not only fulfil (
S.16
) but also be
phase-covariant

Holevo 1993
;
Holevo 1996
;
Smirne et al. 2016
, i.e.
\forall_{g}\,:\;[\Lambda,\mathcal{V}_{g}]=0
, the
G
-covariance condition (
S.22
) is satisfied. For the special case of symmetric mixing with
\mathsf{p}=\mathsf{q}
and any
0\leq\mathsf{p}\leq 1
, one can show that the dephasing channel fulfilling (
S.16
),
\Lambda_{\mathrm{dep}}\sim\{\sqrt{\mathsf{p}}\mathbbm{1},\sqrt{1-\mathsf{p}}\sigma_{z}\}
, is phase-covariant and, hence, the upper bound (
S.23

V.5
Hierarchy of moment-based lower bounds on the FI

Using only partial information from a full probability distribution, such as considering only up to certain finite moments, one obtains a lower bound for the FI. For the case of univariate and single-parameter estimation, we provide here a simple “physicist’s” reformulation for constructing such a lower bound, which consistently agrees with more abstract considerations
Sankaran 1964
;
Jarrett 1984
;
Stein 2021
.

We first rewrite the FI
F=\sum_{x}\dot{q}_{\vec{\phi},\theta}(x)^{2}/q_{\theta,\vec{\phi}}(x)
by making use of essentially a simple identity: any real quadratic function
g(y)=-ay^{2}+2by
with
a>0,b\in\mathbb{R}
, has its maximum given by
\max_{y}g(y)=g(b/a)=b^{2}/a
, so equivalently
F=\sum_{x}\max_{y_{x}}\{-q_{\theta,\vec{\phi}}(x)y_{x}^{2}+2\dot{q}_{\vec{\phi},\theta}(x)y_{x}\}
. Using a series
ansatz

y_{x}=\sum_{k=0}^{K}\alpha_{k}w(x)^{k}
for some chosen function
w(x)
with
K
smaller than the cardinality of the probability distribution, we obtain a lower bound
F^{(K)}
on FI after maximizing now over the finite set
\{\alpha_{k}\}_{k=0}^{K}
. By construction, we have
F^{(0)}\leq F^{(1)}\leq F^{(2)}\leq\cdots\leq F^{(K)}\leq F
, and
F^{(K)}
can be computed straightforwardly as

\displaystyle F^{(K)}=

\displaystyle-\max_{\{\alpha\}}\Bigg[\sum_{k=0}^{K}\alpha_{k}\sum_{j=0}^{K}\alpha_{j}\sum_{x}q_{\theta,\vec{\phi}}(x)w(x)^{k+j}

\displaystyle\phantom{-\max_{\{\alpha\}}\Bigg[}\;+\;2\sum_{k=0}^{K}\alpha_{k}\sum_{x}\dot{q}_{\vec{\phi},\theta}(x)w(x)^{k}\Bigg]

\displaystyle=\max_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}-\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{T}A\boldsymbol{\alpha}+2\boldsymbol{b}^{T}\boldsymbol{\alpha}=\boldsymbol{b}^{T}A^{-1}\boldsymbol{b},

(S.63)

where
\boldsymbol{\alpha}=\begin{pmatrix}\alpha_{0},\,\alpha_{1},\,\cdots\,,\alpha_{K}\end{pmatrix}^{T}
, and
A
and
\boldsymbol{b}
are as in
Eqs. (26, 27) in the main text
, with the more general replacement
\mathbb{E}[x^{j}]\rightarrow\mathbb{E}[w(x)^{j}]
and
\dot{\mathbb{E}}[x^{j}]\rightarrow\dot{\mathbb{E}}[w(x)^{j}]
. If we choose further
w(x)=x
,
F^{(K)}
becomes a lower bound on
F
that takes into account up to the
2K
-th moment of the distribution
q_{\theta,\vec{\phi}}
. For
K=0
and
K=1
, we have explicitly
F^{(0)}=0
and
F^{(1)}=\dot{\mathbb{E}}[w(x)]^{2}/(\mathbb{E}[w(x)^{2}]-\mathbb{E}[w(x)]^{2})
. For
K\geq 2
, Eq. (
V.5
) can be computed numerically and efficiently by standard matrix inversion techniques.

As the results and expressions derived in Lemma 1 are independent of the number of probes, it follows that Equations (
V.1
-
V.1
) hold still.
Applying specifically to the multi-probe scenario discussed in the main text with the structure of measurement as

, i.e. each probe is still measured independently but in an imperfect manner, Eqs. (

) and (

\displaystyle F_{N}=

\displaystyle 4\;\gamma(\vec{\Phi},\psi^{N}(\theta))\;\langle\partial_{\theta}\psi_{\perp}^{N}(\theta)\left|\partial_{\theta}\psi_{\perp}^{N}(\theta)\right\rangle,

(S.64)

with

\displaystyle\gamma(\vec{\Phi},\psi^{N}(\theta)):=

\displaystyle\qquad\frac{1}{4}\sum_{\boldsymbol{x}}\frac{[\left\langle\psi_{\perp}^{N}(\theta)\right|V_{\vec{\Phi}}^{\dagger}M_{\boldsymbol{x}}V_{\vec{\Phi}}\left|\psi^{N}(\theta)\right\rangle+\mathrm{c.c.}]^{2}}{\left\langle\psi^{N}(\theta)\right|V_{\vec{\Phi}}^{\dagger}M_{\boldsymbol{x}}V_{\vec{\Phi}}\left|\psi^{N}(\theta)\right\rangle},

(S.65)

where now
\boldsymbol{x}=(x_{1},x_{2},\cdots,x_{N})
, with
\mathcal{M}^{\otimes N}\sim\{M_{\boldsymbol{x}}\}
,

\displaystyle M_{\boldsymbol{x}}=M_{x_{1}}\otimes M_{x_{2}}\otimes\cdots\otimes M_{x_{N}},

(S.66)

where
M_{x_{\ell}}
is the noisy measurement operator for outcome
x_{\ell}
for the
\ell
-th probe.

A lower bound on
\gamma(\vec{\Phi},\psi^{N}(\theta))
, and hence on
F_{N}
, for any given choice of
V_{\vec{\Phi}}
can be constructed as follows. Define
\left|\psi_{\pm}^{N}(\theta)\right\rangle:=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\Big(\left|\psi^{N}(\theta)\right\rangle\pm\left|\psi_{\perp}^{N}(\theta)\right\rangle\Big)
, and
p_{\pm}(\boldsymbol{x}):=\left\langle\psi_{\pm}^{N}(\theta)\right|V_{\vec{\Phi}}^{\dagger}M_{\boldsymbol{x}}V_{\vec{\Phi}}\left|\psi_{\pm}^{N}(\theta)\right\rangle
, where in order not to overload the notation, we have kept the
\theta
and
\vec{\Phi}
dependence implicit. Then, the numerator of
4\gamma(\vec{\Phi},\psi^{N}(\theta))
is

\displaystyle\big(p_{+}(\boldsymbol{x})-p_{-}(\boldsymbol{x})\big)^{2},

(S.67)

while the denominator is

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\Big(p_{+}(\boldsymbol{x})+p_{-}(\boldsymbol{x})

\displaystyle+\left\langle\psi_{-}^{N}\right|V_{\vec{\Phi}}^{\dagger}M_{\boldsymbol{x}}V_{\vec{\Phi}}\left|\psi_{+}^{N}\right\rangle+\left\langle\psi_{+}^{N}\right|V_{\vec{\Phi}}^{\dagger}M_{\boldsymbol{x}}V_{\vec{\Phi}}\left|\psi_{-}^{N}\right\rangle\Big),

(S.68)

which by the Cauchy-Schwartz inequality is smaller or equal to

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\Big(\sqrt{p_{+}(\boldsymbol{x})}+\sqrt{p_{-}(\boldsymbol{x})}\Big)^{2}.

(S.69)

Hence, we have

\displaystyle\gamma(\vec{\Phi},\psi^{N}(\theta))

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\boldsymbol{x}}\Big(\sqrt{p_{+}(\boldsymbol{x})}-\sqrt{p_{-}(\boldsymbol{x})}\Big)^{2}

\displaystyle=\Big(1-\sum_{\boldsymbol{x}}\sqrt{p_{+}(\boldsymbol{x})p_{-}(\boldsymbol{x})}\Big).

(S.70)

Let us now denote:

\displaystyle V_{\vec{\Phi}}\left|\psi^{N}_{+}(\theta)\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\left|\zeta^{N}\right\rangle,

\displaystyle V_{\vec{\Phi}}\left|\psi^{N}_{-}(\theta)\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\left|\zeta^{N}_{\perp}\right\rangle,

(S.71)

and note that
p_{\pm}(\boldsymbol{x})
depends solely on our choice of
\left|\zeta^{N}\right\rangle,\left|\zeta^{N}_{\perp}\right\rangle.

To attain the lower bound in
Eq. (17) of Thm. 1
, consider a specific
choice of
\left|\zeta^{N}\right\rangle,\left|\zeta^{N}_{\perp}\right\rangle
(obtained by a suitable
V_{\vec{\Phi}}
) :

\displaystyle\left|\zeta^{N}\right\rangle=\left|\zeta\right\rangle^{\otimes N},

\displaystyle\left|\zeta^{N}_{\perp}\right\rangle=\left|\zeta_{\perp}\right\rangle^{\otimes N}

(S.72)

for some orthogonal single-qudit states
\{\left|\zeta\right\rangle,\left|\zeta_{\perp}\right\rangle\}
. Then, with
p_{+}(x):=\left\langle\zeta\right|M_{x}\left|\zeta\right\rangle,p_{-}(x):=\left\langle\zeta_{\perp}\right|M_{x}\left|\zeta_{\perp}\right\rangle
, and
c:=\sum_{x}\sqrt{p_{+}(x)p_{-}(x)}
, we have
p_{\pm}(\boldsymbol{x})=\prod_{j=1}^{N}p_{\pm}(x_{j})
, and thus

\displaystyle\sum_{\boldsymbol{x}}\sqrt{p_{+}(\boldsymbol{x})p_{-}(\boldsymbol{x})}=c^{N}.

(S.73)

Evidently, we have
0\leq c\leq 1
, and so we complete the proof of Thm 1.
\blacksquare

A few remarks are in order:
Given a specific choice of
\{\left|\zeta\right\rangle^{\otimes N},\left|\zeta_{\perp}\right\rangle^{\otimes N}\}
(equivalently
V_{\vec{\Phi}}
and
p_{\pm}(\boldsymbol{x})\}
)
the FI converges to the perfect QFI exponentially fast with an approximate prefactor (see Eqs. (
V.6
) and (
S.73
)):

\displaystyle 1-c^{N}

\displaystyle=1-\left(\sum_{x}\sqrt{p_{+}\left(x\right)p_{-}\left(x\right)}\right)^{N}

\displaystyle=1-\left(\underset{\boldsymbol{x}}{\sum}\sqrt{p_{+}\left(\boldsymbol{x}\right)p_{-}\left(\boldsymbol{x}\right)}\right).

(S.74)

Note that this prefactor is the Hellinger distance,
H\left(p_{+}\left(\boldsymbol{x}\right),p_{-}\left(\boldsymbol{x}\right)\right)
, between the distributions
p_{+}\left(\boldsymbol{x}\right)
and
p_{-}\left(\boldsymbol{x}\right)

Basu et al. 2019
, while the convergence rate
\chi
(
c\equiv\mathrm{e}^{-\chi}
) reads

\displaystyle\chi

\displaystyle=-\log\left(\sum_{x}\sqrt{p_{+}\left(x\right)p_{-}\left(x\right)}\right)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{N}\log\bigg(1-H\big(p_{+}\left(\boldsymbol{x}\right),p_{-}\left(\boldsymbol{x}\right)\big)\bigg).

(S.75)

For very close distributions it can be observed that the convergence rate is equal to the Fisher metric:
\sum_{x}\frac{\left(p_{+}\left(x\right)-p_{-}\left(x\right)\right)^{2}}{8p_{+}\left(x\right)}
.

As
N\rightarrow\infty
, generally we can use the central limit theorem to approximate
H\left(p_{+}\left(\boldsymbol{x}\right),p_{-}\left(\boldsymbol{x}\right)\right)

as the Hellinger distance between two Gaussian distributions. This would then imply a convergence rate of:
\frac{1}{4}\frac{\left(\mu_{+}-\mu_{-}\right)^{2}}{\sigma_{+}^{2}+\sigma_{-}^{2}}
, where
\mu_{\pm},\sigma_{\pm}
are the average and standard deviation of
p_{\pm}\left(x\right)

respectively, which of course depends on our choice of
\left|\zeta\right\rangle,\left|\zeta_{\perp}\right\rangle
.

We can thus apply this analysis to obtain the convergence rate for specific cases. For Poissonian channel (with coefficients
\lambda_{\left|0\right\rangle},\lambda_{\left|1\right\rangle}
), such as in NV centres, we find a convergence rate of:
\frac{1}{2}\left(\sqrt{\lambda_{\left|0\right\rangle}}-\sqrt{\lambda_{\left|1\right\rangle}}\right)^{2}
.
This convergence rate is achieved by taking
\left|\zeta^{N}\right\rangle,\left|\zeta_{\perp}^{N}\right\rangle
to be
|0\rangle^{\otimes N},|1\rangle^{\otimes N}
(similar convergence rate is obtained by taking them to be any superposition of
|0\rangle^{\otimes N},|1\rangle^{\otimes N}
).
This implies that for realistic experimental values of NV centres,
\lambda_{\left|0\right\rangle}=0.1,\lambda_{\left|1\right\rangle}=0.07
(without the use of nuclear spins as memory), the number of probes that obtains
95\%
of the perfect QFI would be
\sim 2000
.
For a binary asymmetric bit-flip channel with probabilities
\mathsf{p},\mathsf{q}
,
we find a convergence rate of:
\frac{1}{4}\frac{\left(\mathsf{p}+\mathsf{q}-1\right)^{2}}{\mathsf{p}\left(1-\mathsf{p}\right)+\mathsf{q}\left(1-\mathsf{q}\right)}
, given a similar choice of
|\zeta\rangle=|0\rangle^{\otimes N},|\zeta_{\perp}\rangle=|1\rangle^{\otimes N}
.
As a side note, we may also compute
c
directly in this case, and it can be verified that indeed
c^{N}=\left(\sqrt{\mathsf{p}(1-\mathsf{q})}+\sqrt{\mathsf{q}(1-\mathsf{p})}\right)^{N}\approx\exp\left(-\chi N\right)
with the said
\chi
.

A note on optimality: Let us justify our choice of unitary, namely the choice of
|\zeta^{N}\rangle=|0\rangle^{\otimes N},|\zeta_{\perp}^{N}\rangle=|1\rangle^{\otimes N}
. Given the approximated value of the convergence rate, Eq. (
V.6
), we claim that this choice yields the optimal convergence rate.
That is, we would like to choose
|\zeta^{N}\rangle,|\zeta_{\perp}^{N}\rangle
that maximize the Hellinger distance between
\left\{p_{+}\left(\boldsymbol{x}\right)=\langle\zeta^{N}|M_{\boldsymbol{x}}|\zeta^{N}\rangle\right\}_{\boldsymbol{x}},\left\{p_{-}\left(\boldsymbol{x}\right)=\langle\zeta_{\perp}^{N}|M_{\boldsymbol{x}}|\zeta_{\perp}^{N}\rangle\right\}_{\boldsymbol{x}}
. As before, we focus on the classical noise channel, namely commuting
M_{x}
:
M_{x}=\sum_{i}p\!\left(x|i\right)\Pi_{i}
. Observe that:

•

The Hellinger distance is convex in the probability distributions:
H\left(\lambda p_{1}+\left(1-\lambda\right)p_{2},q\right)\leq\lambda H\left(p_{1},q\right)+\left(1-\lambda\right)H\left(p_{2},q\right)
.

•

Let
\left\{|j\rangle\right\}_{j}
be the common eigenbasis of
\left\{M_{x}\right\}_{x}
, then given
|\zeta\rangle=\sqrt{\lambda}|j_{1}\rangle+\sqrt{1-\lambda}|j_{2}\rangle
:
\langle\zeta|M_{x}|\zeta\rangle=\lambda\langle j_{1}|M_{x}|j_{1}\rangle+\left(1-\lambda\right)\langle j_{2}|M_{x}|j_{2}\rangle
. In words: taking superpositions of the eigenstates leads to convex combinations of the probabilities.

The above two observations imply that the maximal Hellinger distance is achieved by taking
|\zeta^{N}\rangle,|\zeta_{\perp}^{N}\rangle
to be elements in the common eigenbasis (and not superpositions of them). Hence, we just need to find the two basis states that yield maximal Hellinger distance. For
N
qudits, we thus need to find the two states
|j_{1}\rangle,|j_{2}\rangle
with maximal Hellinger distance out of the
d
-dimensional eigenbasis, and then the optimal choice of
|\zeta^{N}\rangle,|\zeta_{\perp}^{N}\rangle
would be
|j_{1}\rangle^{\otimes N},|j_{2}\rangle^{\otimes N}
. For the case of NV centres with the local projective measurement
\{\Pi_{1}=\left|0\right\rangle\!\left\langle 0\right|,\Pi_{2}=\left|1\right\rangle\!\left\langle 1\right|\}
for each of the
N
NV centres, this immediately implies that the optimal
|\zeta^{N}\rangle,|\zeta_{\perp}^{N}\rangle
are
|0\rangle^{\otimes N},|1\rangle^{\otimes N}
.

In fact this intuition together with some numerical evidence leads us to conjecture
that for any classical noise channel,
M_{x}=\sum_{i}p\!\left(x|i\right)\Pi_{i}
that is applied independently on each of the
N
probes, the optimal
|\zeta^{N}\rangle,|\zeta_{\perp}^{N}\rangle
take the form of “cat states”:

\displaystyle\begin{split}|\zeta^{N}\rangle&=\cos\left(\theta\right)|j\rangle^{\otimes N}+\sin\left(\theta\right)|k\rangle^{\otimes N},\\
|\zeta_{\perp}^{N}\rangle&=-\sin\left(\theta\right)|j\rangle^{\otimes N}+\cos\left(\theta\right)|k\rangle^{\otimes N},\end{split}

where
\left|j\right\rangle,\left|k\right\rangle
can be found numerically for just a single probe, and
\theta
depends on
N
and should be found numerically. In particular for
N
qubits we connjecture that the optimal
|\zeta^{N}\rangle,|\zeta_{\perp}^{N}\rangle
take the form of
\cos\left(\theta\right)|0\rangle^{\otimes N}+\sin\left(\theta\right)|1\rangle^{\otimes N},-\sin\left(\theta\right)|0\rangle^{\otimes N}+\cos\left(\theta\right)|1\rangle^{\otimes N}.

Proof of Corollary 2

We say that a detection channel
\mathcal{P}
acts
non-trivially
on a given subset
\tilde{I}\subseteq I
of ‘inaccessible’ outcomes, if for any pair
i,i^{\prime}\in\tilde{I}

there exists at least one ‘observable’ outcome
x
such that the transition probabilities of
\mathcal{P}
satisfy
p(x|i)p(x|i^{\prime})>0
. Moreover, if this
\tilde{I}
contains the outcomes spanning the subspace of the encoding Hamiltonian, i.e.
h=\sum_{i,i^{\prime}\in\tilde{I}}\mathsf{h}_{i,i^{\prime}}\left|i\right\rangle\left\langle i^{\prime}\right|
with
\det\{\mathsf{h}\}\neq 0
, we say that
\mathcal{P}
acts non-trivially on the encoding subspace. However, for our purposes we consider
\mathcal{P}
that act non-trivially on
all
the outcomes in
I
, and refer to these as
non-trivial
.

Suppose the imperfect measurement
\mathcal{M}
is composed of a perfect von Neumann measurement, i.e.,
\Pi\sim\{\Pi_{i}=\left|i\right\rangle\!\left\langle i\right|\}_{i}
with
\Pi_{i}\Pi_{i^{\prime}}=\delta_{i,i^{\prime}}\Pi_{i}
, followed by a noisy detection channel
\mathcal{P}\sim\{p(x|i)\}
, such that
M_{x}=\sum_{i}p(x|i)\Pi_{i}
.
As a result, the condition (
S.92
) is equivalent to
h=\sum_{i,i^{\prime}\in I}V_{\vec{\phi}}^{\dagger}\left|i\right\rangle\sum_{x}\sqrt{p(x|i)}\left\langle i\right|A_{x}(\vec{\phi})\left|i^{\prime}\right\rangle\sqrt{p(x|i^{\prime})}\left\langle i^{\prime}\right|V_{\vec{\phi}}
, where the entries
\left\langle i\right|A_{x}(\vec{\phi})\left|i^{\prime}\right\rangle=2\mathsf{g}_{x,i;x,i^{\prime}}(\vec{\phi})
can be chosen arbitrarily (of some Hermitian matrix). Now, given that
\mathcal{P}
is
non-trivial
, so that for any pair
i,i^{\prime}\in I
there exists
x
such that
\sqrt{p(x|i)p(x|i^{\prime})}\neq 0
, we can define a Hermitian matrix
\mathsf{C}
whose
all
entries,
\mathsf{C}_{i,i^{\prime}}:=2\sum_{x}\sqrt{p(x|i)p(x|i^{\prime})}\,\mathsf{g}_{x,i;x,i^{\prime}}(\vec{\phi})
for all
i,i^{\prime}\in I
, can be freely chosen (in particular, non-zero) by varying
\mathsf{g}
. Consequently, we may again rewrite Eq. (
S.92
) as
\mathsf{h}=\mathsf{V}^{\dagger}\mathsf{C}\mathsf{V}
, where
\mathsf{V}:=\sum_{i,i^{\prime}\in I}\left\langle i\right|V_{\vec{\phi}}\left|i^{\prime}\right\rangle\left|i\right\rangle\!\left\langle i^{\prime}\right|
is an (invertible) unitary matrix. Hence, we can always satisfy the condition (
S.92
) by choosing
\mathsf{g}
such that
\mathsf{C}=\mathsf{V}\mathsf{h}\mathsf{V}^{\dagger}
. In summary, whenever the stochastic map
\mathcal{P}
representing the detection noise is non-trivial,
Eq. (
S.92
) can always be fulfilled, which by the virtue of the asymptotic CE bound forces the MSE to asymptotically follow the SS.

\blacksquare

F_{N}^{\mathrm{(CE)}}
and

First, in view of the symmetry in the problem, and as suggested by the expression of

Then, note that the calculations for
F_{N}^{\mathrm{(CE)}}(\vec{\phi})

\theta

\theta_{0}

\mathsf{u}

\theta

\tilde{K}_{x,j}(\theta,\vec{\phi})=\sum_{x^{\prime},j^{\prime}}\mathsf{u}_{x,j;x^{\prime},j^{\prime}}(\theta,\vec{\phi})K_{x^{\prime},j^{\prime}}(\theta,\vec{\phi})

\mathsf{u}=\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}(\theta-\theta_{0})\mathsf{g}}

\mathsf{g}
, such that at
\theta=\theta_{0}
eventually, the Kraus operators obey Eqs (
S.89

S.90

\min_{\tilde{K}}

\min_{\mathsf{g}}

\displaystyle F_{N}^{\mathrm{(CE)}}(\vec{\phi})

\displaystyle=4N\min_{\mathsf{g}}\{\lambda_{a}^{2}+(N-1)\lambda_{b}^{2}\}\quad\text{with}\quad\boldsymbol{A},\boldsymbol{B}\geq 0,

(S.95)

where

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{A}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\sqrt{\lambda_{a}}\mathbbm{1}_{d}&\dot{\tilde{\boldsymbol{K}}}^{\dagger}\\
\dot{\tilde{\boldsymbol{K}}}&\sqrt{\lambda_{a}}\mathbbm{1}_{d_{\mathrm{out}}}\end{bmatrix},\quad\boldsymbol{B}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\sqrt{\lambda_{b}}\mathbbm{1}_{d}&(\mathrm{i}\dot{\tilde{\boldsymbol{K}}}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{K})^{\dagger}\\
\mathrm{i}\dot{\tilde{\boldsymbol{K}}}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{K}&\sqrt{\lambda_{b}}\mathbbm{1}_{d_{\mathrm{out}}}\end{bmatrix},

(S.96)

and

imposed in Eq. (

For particular simple examples analytical answers can be obtained. In particular, for the case of

Error-propagation formula with imperfect measurement

The mean squared error of estimators obtained from measuring the mean of some observable
\hat{O}
with large number of repetitions
\nu
, is well approximated by the so-called “error-propagation formula”
Wineland et al. 1992

\displaystyle\nu\Delta^{2}\tilde{\theta}_{N}=\frac{\Delta^{2}\hat{O}}{\Big|\dfrac{\partial\langle\hat{O}\rangle}{\partial\theta}\Big|^{2}},

(S.100)

where
\Delta^{2}{O}=\langle\hat{O}^{2}\rangle-\langle\hat{O}\rangle^{2}
, with
\langle A\rangle
being the expectation value of the operator
A
over the quantum state
\rho(\theta)
, i.e.
\langle A\rangle=\mathrm{Tr}\{\rho(\theta)A\}
.

For our quantum-classical channel scenario with noisy detection channel represented by the stochastic map
\mathcal{P}\sim\{p(x|i)\}
, while we have the freedom to choose the measurement basis
\Pi_{i,\vec{\phi}}
, we need to keep in mind that the only observable and effective measurement that we have is
\{M_{x,\vec{\phi}}=\sum_{i}p(x|i)\Pi_{i,\vec{\phi}}\}
, and it is not projective in general. The observable that we measure is thus
\hat{O}=\sum_{x}f_{x}M_{x,\vec{\phi}}
for some
\{f_{x}\}
defining the observable (which can be chosen quite arbitrarily). For
N
independent quantum-classical channel, we can then construct the joint observable
\hat{O}=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\hat{O}^{(j)}=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sum_{x}f_{x}M_{x,\vec{\phi}}^{(j)}
, where
j
labels the different channels. Alternatively, we may also consider a second kind of joint observable, where instead of summing over the constituent single-particle operators, we perform
product
over them:
\hat{O}=\prod_{j=1}^{N}\hat{O}^{(j)}=\prod_{j=1}^{N}\big(\sum_{x}f_{x}M_{x,\vec{\phi}}\big)^{(j)}
.

While it maybe tempting, we cannot however simply use
\hat{O}^{2}=\Big(\sum_{j=1}^{N}\hat{O}^{(j)}\Big)^{2}
or
\hat{O}^{2}=\Big(\prod_{j=1}^{N}\hat{O}^{(j)}\Big)^{2}
to compute
\Delta^{2}\hat{O}
in Eq. (
S.100
). The reason is, Eq. (
S.100
) uses the implicit assumption that the observable
\hat{O}
is measured at its eigenbasis, and that is not the case here. The effective
\hat{O}^{2}
that we should use in Eq. (
S.100
) is one which mimics the statistics that we would get as if we are measuring the eigenbasis, i.e.,
as if

\{M_{x,\vec{\phi}}\}
are projective. That is, we have, for the first kind of observables,

\displaystyle\hat{O}^{2}=

\displaystyle\sum_{j}\Big(\sum_{x}f_{x}M_{x,\vec{\phi}}^{(j)}\Big)^{2}

\displaystyle+\sum_{j\neq k}\Big(\sum_{x}f_{x}M_{x,\vec{\phi}}^{(j)}\Big)\Big(\sum_{x}f_{x}M_{x,\vec{\phi}}^{(k)}\Big)

(S.101)

\displaystyle\longrightarrow\hat{O}^{\prime 2}=

\displaystyle\sum_{j}\sum_{x}f_{x}^{2}M_{x,\vec{\phi}}^{(j)}

\displaystyle+\sum_{j\neq k}\Big(\sum_{x}f_{x}M_{x,\vec{\phi}}^{(j)}\Big)\Big(\sum_{x}f_{x}M_{x,\vec{\phi}}^{(k)}\Big),

(S.102)

and, for the second kind of observables,

\displaystyle\hat{O}^{2}=

\displaystyle\prod_{j}\Big(\sum_{x}f_{x}M_{x,\vec{\phi}}^{(j)}\Big)^{2}

\displaystyle\longrightarrow\hat{O}^{\prime 2}=

\displaystyle\prod_{j}\Big(\sum_{x}f_{x}^{2}M_{x,\vec{\phi}}\Big)^{(j)}

and then

\displaystyle\nu\Delta^{2}\tilde{\theta}_{N}=\frac{\langle\hat{O}^{\prime 2}\rangle-\langle\hat{O}\rangle^{2}}{\Big|\dfrac{\partial\langle\hat{O}\rangle}{\partial\theta}\Big|^{2}}

(S.104)

for our quantum-classical channel.

We apply Eq. (
S.104
) to the case of
N
qubits, each of which undergoes a projective measurement
\Pi_{1,\vec{\phi}}=\left|+\right\rangle\left\langle+\right|,\Pi_{2,\vec{\phi}}=\left|-\right\rangle\left\langle-\right|
where
\sigma_{\mathrm{x}}\left|\pm\right\rangle=\pm\left|\pm\right\rangle
, and
|X|=2
. We consider a binary mixing channel
\mathcal{P}
that flips the measurement outcomes with
p(1|1)=\mathsf{p}
,
p(2|2)=\mathsf{q}
, so that the effective measurements corresponding to the observed outcomes read
M_{1,\vec{\phi}}=(1+\delta)\mathbbm{1}/2+\eta\sigma_{\mathrm{x}}/2
and
M_{2,\vec{\phi}}=(1-\delta)\mathbbm{1}/2-\eta\sigma_{\mathrm{x}}/2
with
\eta:=\mathsf{p}+\mathsf{q}-1,\delta:=\mathsf{p}-\mathsf{q}
. Then, for the first kind of observables, we have

\displaystyle\hat{O}=

\displaystyle(f_{1}-f_{2})\eta\hat{J}_{\mathrm{x}}+\frac{N}{2}[f_{1}+f_{2}+(f_{1}-f_{2})\delta],

(S.105)

\displaystyle\hat{O}^{\prime 2}=

\displaystyle(f_{1}^{2}-f_{2}^{2})\eta\hat{J}_{\mathrm{x}}+\frac{N}{2}[f_{1}^{2}+f_{2}^{2}+(f_{1}^{2}-f_{2}^{2})\delta]

\displaystyle+(\hat{J}_{\mathrm{x}}^{2}-\frac{N}{4})\eta^{2}(f_{1}-f_{2})^{2}

\displaystyle+(N-1)[f_{1}+f_{2}+(f_{1}-f_{2})\delta]

\displaystyle\phantom{+}\Big(\eta(f_{1}-f_{2})\hat{J}_{\mathrm{x}}+\frac{N}{4}[f_{1}+f_{2}+(f_{1}-f_{2})\delta]\Big),

(S.106)

where
\hat{J}_{\ell}=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\frac{\sigma_{\ell}^{(j)}}{2}
is the usual total angular momentum operator in the
\ell
-direction with
\ell=\{\mathrm{x,y,z}\}
. After some straightforward algebra, one obtains

\displaystyle\nu\Delta^{2}\tilde{\theta}_{N}=\frac{\Delta^{2}\hat{J}_{\mathrm{x}}}{|\partial_{\theta}\langle\hat{J}_{\mathrm{x}}\rangle|^{2}}-\frac{\delta\langle\hat{J}_{\mathrm{x}}\rangle}{\eta|\partial_{\theta}\langle\hat{J}_{\mathrm{x}}\rangle|^{2}}+\frac{N}{4\eta^{2}}\frac{1-\eta^{2}-\delta^{2}}{|\partial_{\theta}\langle\hat{J}_{\mathrm{x}}\rangle|^{2}}.

(S.107)

Given
U_{\theta}=\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\theta\sigma_{\mathrm{z}}/2}
to be the unitary encoding the estimated parameter
\theta
onto each probe, the state of all the probes just before the measurement reads
\rho^{N}(\theta):=\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\theta\hat{J}_{\mathrm{z}}}\rho^{N}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\theta\hat{J}_{\mathrm{z}}}
, where
\rho^{N}
is the input
N
-qubit probe state. In such as case, we have that
\partial_{\theta}\langle\hat{J}_{\mathrm{x}}\rangle=-\langle\sin\theta\hat{J}_{\mathrm{x}}+\cos\theta\hat{J}_{\mathrm{y}}\rangle_{\rho^{N}}=\mathrm{Tr}\{\rho^{N}(\sin\theta\hat{J}_{\mathrm{x}}+\cos\theta\hat{J}_{\mathrm{y}})\}
.

For the second kind of observables, let us consider for example the (imperfect) parity operator, i.e., with
f_{1}=-f_{2}=1
. Then, we have

\displaystyle\hat{O}

\displaystyle=\hat{P}\equiv\prod_{j=1}^{N}\big(M_{1,\vec{\phi}}-M_{2,\vec{\phi}}\big)^{(j)}=\prod_{j}\Big(\delta\mathbbm{1}+\eta\sigma_{\mathrm{x}}\Big)^{(j)},

(S.108)

\displaystyle\hat{O}^{\prime 2}

\displaystyle=\prod_{j=1}^{N}\big(M_{1,\vec{\phi}}+M_{2,\vec{\phi}}\big)^{(j)}=\mathbbm{1},

(S.109)

and finally

\displaystyle\nu\Delta^{2}\tilde{\theta}_{N}=\frac{1-\langle\hat{P}\rangle^{2}}{|\partial_{\theta}\langle\hat{P}\rangle|^{2}}.

(S.110)

Consider the measurement of the (imperfect) parity operator,
\hat{P}=\prod_{j=1}^{N}\big(M_{1,\vec{\phi}}^{(j)}-M_{2,\vec{\phi}}^{(j)}\big)
, with
M_{1,\vec{\phi}}=(1+\delta)\mathbbm{1}/2+\eta\sigma_{\mathrm{x}}/2
and
M_{2,\vec{\phi}}=(1-\delta)\mathbbm{1}/2-\eta\sigma_{\mathrm{x}}/2
as above. Then, by the error-propagation formula once more (see Supplement for details), we have

\displaystyle\nu\Delta^{2}\tilde{\theta}_{N}=

\displaystyle\frac{1-\langle\hat{P}\rangle^{2}}{|\partial_{\theta}\langle\hat{P}\rangle|^{2}},

(S.111)

where
\langle\hat{P}\rangle=\mathrm{Tr}\{\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\theta\hat{J}_{\mathrm{z}}}\rho^{N}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\theta\hat{J}_{\mathrm{z}}}\hat{P}\}
. Using the (rotated) GHZ input state,
\rho^{N}=\left|\psi\right\rangle\left\langle\psi\right|
,
\left|\psi\right\rangle=\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\phi\hat{J}_{\mathrm{z}}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\big(\left|0\dots 0\right\rangle+\left|1\dots 1\right\rangle\big)
, we thus get

\displaystyle\nu\Delta^{2}\tilde{\theta}_{N}=

\displaystyle\frac{1-\big(\delta^{N}+\eta^{N}\cos(N\varphi))^{2}}{N^{2}\eta^{2N}\sin^{2}(N\varphi)},

(S.112)

with
\varphi=\phi+\theta
as before. While the parity measurement with GHZ state will perform poorly for large
N
by virtue of the exponential factor
\eta^{-2N}
, it does however make a good candidate for small
N
regime where the
1/N^{2}
factor dominates. Indeed, upon optimising over
\phi
, we obtain the blue curve in
Fig. 6 of the main text
.
