Approximate Heavy Tails in Offline (Multi-Pass) Stochastic Gradient Descent
Krunoslav Lehman Pavasovic, Alain Durmus, Umut Şimşekli

Abstract
A recent line of empirical studies has demonstrated that SGD might exhibit a heavy-tailed behavior in practical settings, and the heaviness of the tails might correlate with the overall performance. In this paper, we investigate the emergence of such heavy tails. Previous works on this problem only considered, up to our knowledge, online (also called single-pass) SGD, in which the emergence of heavy tails in theoretical findings is contingent upon access to an infinite amount of data. Hence, the underlying mechanism generating the reported heavy-tailed behavior in practical settings, where the amount of training data is finite, is still not well-understood. Our contribution aims to fill this gap. In particular, we show that the stationary distribution of offline (also called multi-pass) SGD exhibits ‘approximate’ power-law tails and the approximation error is controlled by how fast the empirical distribution of the training data converges to the true underlying data distribution in the Wasserstein metric. Our main takeaway is that, as the number of data points increases, offline SGD will behave increasingly ‘power-law-like’. To achieve this result, we first prove nonasymptotic Wasserstein convergence bounds for offline SGD to online SGD as the number of data points increases, which can be interesting on their own. Finally, we illustrate our theory on various experiments conducted on synthetic data and neural networks.

Introduction

Many machine learning problems can be cast as the following population risk minimization problem:

\displaystyle\text{ minimize }x\mapsto F(x):=\mathbb{E}[f(x,Z)]\;,\quad Z\sim\mu_{z}\;,

(1)

where
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
denotes the model parameters,

Suppose we have access to an

\displaystyle X_{k+1}=X_{k}-\frac{\eta}{b}\sum_{i\in\Omega_{k+1}}\nabla f(X_{k},Z_{i})\;,

(2)

While drawing i.i.d. data samples at each iteration is possible in certain applications, in the majority of practical settings, we only have access to a finite number of data points, preventing online SGD use. More precisely, we have access to a dataset of

Despite its ubiquitous use in modern machine learning applications, the theoretical properties of offline SGD have not yet been well-established. Another line of research has investigated the theoretical links between heavy tails and the generalization performance of SGD. Under different theoretical settings and based on different assumptions,

Main problematic and contributions.
Although empirically observed, it is currently unknown how heavy-tailed behavior arises in offline SGD since the aforementioned theory

Our main contribution in this paper is to make these observations rigorous and explicit. In particular, we extend the tail estimation results for online SGD
[
9
,
10
]
to offline SGD, and show that the stationary distribution of offline SGD iterates will exhibit
‘approximate’
heavy tails. Informally, for both quadratic and a class of strongly convex losses, we show that, with high probability, there exist constants
c_{1},c_{2}
, such that for large enough

we have the following tail bound:

\displaystyle\left[\frac{c_{1}}{t^{\alpha}}-\frac{1}{t}\mathbf{W}_{1}(\mu_{z},\mu_{z}^{(n)})\>\right]\lesssim\mathbb{P}(\|X^{(n)}_{\infty}\|>t)\>\lesssim\left[\frac{c_{2}}{t^{\alpha}}+\frac{1}{t}\mathbf{W}_{1}(\mu_{z},\mu_{z}^{(n)})\right],

(6)

where
\alpha

denotes the empirical measure of the data points in the data sample

, and
\mathbf{W}_{1}
denotes the Wasserstein-1 distance (to be defined formally in the next section).

Our result indicates that the tail behavior of offline SGD is mainly driven by two terms: (i) a persistent term
(c/t^{\alpha})
that determines the power-law decay, and (ii) a vanishing term
\mathbf{W}_{1}(\mu_{z},\mu_{z}^{(n)})/t
, as we take
n\to\infty
.
In other words, compared to (
5

X_{\infty}^{(n)}
exhibits an

power-law decay behavior, with a discrepancy controlled by
\mathbf{W}_{1}(\mu_{z},\mu_{z}^{(n)})
.

Fortunately, the term
\mathbf{W}_{1}(\mu_{z},\mu_{z}^{(n)})
has been well-studied in the literature. By combining our results with Wasserstein convergence theorems for empirical measures
[
8
]
, we further present nonasymptotic tail bounds for offline SGD.
Our main takeaway is as follows: as
n
increases, offline SGD will exhibit approximate power-laws, where the approximation error vanishes with a rate given by how fast the empirical distribution of the data points
\mu_{z}^{(n)}
converges to the true data distribution
\mu_{z}
as
n\to\infty
.

To prove our tail bounds, as an intermediate step, we prove nonasymptotic Wasserstein convergence results for offline SGD in the form:

\mathbf{W}_{1}(\pi,\pi^{(n)})\lesssim\mathbf{W}_{1}(\mu_{z},\mu_{z}^{(n)})
, with high probability, recalling that
\pi
and
\pi^{(n)}
respectively denote online and offline SGD stationary distributions. These results, we believe, hold independent interest for the community. Finally, we support our theory with various experiments conducted on both synthetic quadratic optimization problems and real neural network settings.

2.2
Heavy tails in online SGD

Definition 1

A random vector

, with probability larger than
1-\epsilon_{n}-\zeta
, the following inequalities hold:

\displaystyle\Biggl[\frac{1}{2^{\alpha}}\frac{c-\epsilon}{t^{\alpha}}-\frac{\tilde{c_{1}}}{tn^{1/2}}\sqrt{\log\frac{\tilde{c_{2}}}{\zeta}}\Biggr]\leq\mathbb{P}(\|X^{(n)}_{\infty}\|>t)\>\leq\Biggl[2^{\alpha}\frac{c+\epsilon}{t^{\alpha}}+\frac{2\tilde{c_{1}}}{tn^{1/2}}\sqrt{\log\frac{\tilde{c_{2}}}{\zeta}}\Biggr]\;,

(13)

where
c
is given in (
8
).

This result shows that whenever the online SGD recursion admits heavy tails, the offline SGD recursion will exhibit ‘approximate’ heavy tails. More precisely, as can be seen in (
13
), the tails will have a global power-law behavior due to the term
t^{-\alpha}
, where
\alpha
is the tail index determined by online SGD. On the other hand, the power-law behavior is only approximate, as we have an additional term, which vanishes at a rate
\mathcal{O}(n^{-1/2})
. Hence, as
n
increases, the power-law behavior will be more prominent, which might bring an explanation for why heavy tails are still observed even when
n
is finite.

To establish Theorem
3
, as an intermediate step, we show that
\pi^{(n)}
converges to
\pi
in the Wasserstein-1 metric. The result is given in the following theorem and can be interesting on its own.

Theorem 4
.

Under the setting of Theorem
3
, for
p,q>0
with
1/p+1/q=1
,
p>d/2
,
q<\alpha
, the following holds with probability larger than
1-\epsilon_{n}
:

\displaystyle\mathbf{W}_{1}(\pi,\pi^{(n)})\leq c_{0}\frac{\eta}{1-\delta_{A}}\mathbf{W}_{p}(\mu_{\mathbf{A},\mathbf{b}},\mu_{\mathbf{A},\mathbf{b}}^{(n)})\;,

(14)

where

c_{0}=(\mathbb{E}[\|X_{0}^{(n)}\|^{q}])^{1/q}+1
with
X_{0}^{(n)}\sim\pi^{(n)}
, and
\delta_{A}=\mathbb{E}[\|\operatorname{I}_{d}-\eta\mathbf{A}_{1}\|]
.

This result shows that the stationary distribution of offline SGD will converge to the one of online SGD as
n\to\infty
, and the rate of this convergence is determined by how fast the empirical distribution of the dataset converges to the true data distribution, as measured by
\mathbf{W}_{p}(\mu_{\mathbf{A},\mathbf{b}},\mu_{\mathbf{A},\mathbf{b}}^{(n)})
. Once (
14
) is established, to obtain Theorem
3
, we use the relationship that
\mathbf{W}_{1}(\pi,\pi^{(n)})\geq\mathbf{W}_{1}(\mathscr{L}(\|X_{\infty}^{(n)}\|),\mathscr{L}(\|X_{\infty}\|))=\int_{0}^{\infty}|\mathbb{P}(\|X_{\infty}^{(n)}\|>t)-\mathbb{P}(\|X_{\infty}\|>t)|\>\mathrm{d}t
(see Lemmas
1
and
3
in the Appendix).
Finally, by exploiting the assumptions made on
\mu_{\mathbf{A},\mathbf{b}}
in Theorem
1
, we can invoke
[
8
]
(see Lemma
2
in Section
A.1
) and show that
\mathbf{W}_{p}(\mu_{\mathbf{A},\mathbf{b}},\mu_{\mathbf{A},\mathbf{b}}^{(n)})
behaves as
\mathcal{O}(n^{-1/2})
.

3.2
Strongly convex objectives

We now focus on more complex loss functions, as in Theorem
2
.
For simplicity, we consider
b=1
. The general case, while similar, would complicate the notation and, in our opinion, make our results more difficult to digest.
We start by rewriting offline SGD with respect to an empirical measure
\mu_{z}^{(n)}
:

X_{k+1}^{(n)}=X_{k}^{(n)}-\eta\nabla f(X_{k}^{(n)},\hat{Z}_{k+1}^{(n)})\;,

(15)

where
(\hat{Z}_{k}^{(n)})_{k\geq 1}
are i.i.d. random variables associated with the empirical measure defined for any
\mathsf{A}\in\mathcal{Z}
as:

\textstyle\mu_{z}^{(n)}(\mathsf{A})=n^{-1}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\updelta_{\hat{Z}_{j}^{(n)}}(\mathsf{A})\;.

(16)

We first make the following assumption.

Assumption 1
.

(a)

There exists
L>0
such that

\|\nabla f(x,z)-\nabla f(x,z^{\prime})\|\leq L(\|x\|+1)(\|z-z^{\prime}\|)
, for
any
z,z^{\prime}\in\mathsf{Z}
and
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

(b)

The data
distribution
\mu_{z}
has finite
q
-th moments with some

q\geq 1
.

Assumption
1
-
(a)
is a Lipschitz-like condition that is useful for decoupling
x
and
z
, and is commonly used in the analysis of optimization and stochastic approximations algorithms; see, e.g.,
[
4
]
.
We can now state our second main contribution, an extension of Theorem
2
to the offline setting.

Theorem 5
.

Let
(X_{k})_{k\geq 0}
and
(X^{(n)}_{k})_{k\geq 0}
be defined as in (
2
) and (
4
) with
b=1
and
n\geq 1
. Assume Assumption
1
and the conditions of Theorem
2
with
\beta>1
hold.
Further assume that
R(Z_{1})
is non-deterministic (see Theorem
2
),
(X_{k})_{k\geq 0}
is geometrically ergodic and
\epsilon_{n}\in[0,1]
is the probability that
(X^{(n)}_{k})_{k\geq 0}
is not ergodic (in the Wasserstein sense). Then, for every
\varepsilon>0
, there exist constants
c,c_{\alpha}
,
c_{\beta}
, and
t_{0}\geq 0
, such that for all
t>t_{0}
, the following inequalities hold with probability larger than
1-\epsilon_{n}
:

\displaystyle\Biggl[\frac{1}{2^{\alpha+\epsilon}}\frac{c_{\alpha}}{t^{\alpha+\epsilon}}-\frac{c}{t}\mathbf{W}_{1}(\mu_{z},\mu_{z}^{(n)})\Biggr]\leq\mathbb{P}(\|X_{\infty}^{(n)}\|

\displaystyle>t)\leq\Biggl[2^{\beta-\epsilon}\frac{c_{\beta}}{t^{\beta-\epsilon}}+\frac{2c}{t}\mathbf{W}_{1}(\mu_{z},\mu_{z}^{(n)})\Biggr],\quad\forall n,

where
c=(\mathbb{E}[\|X_{0}^{(n)}\|]+1)L\eta/(1-\delta_{R})
with
\delta_{R}=\mathbb{E}[R(Z_{1})]
, and
X_{0}^{(n)}\sim\pi^{(n)}
.

This theorem shows that the approximate power-laws will also be apparent for more general loss functions: for large enough
n
, the tails of offline SGD will be upper- and lower-bounded by two power-laws with exponents
\alpha
and
\beta
. Note that the ergodicity of
(X_{k})_{k\geq 0}
can be established by using similar tools provided in
[
6
]
. On the other hand, the assumption on
R(Z_{1})
being non-deterministic and
\mathbb{E}[R^{\beta}(Z_{1})]=1
with
\beta>1
jointly indicate that
\delta_{R}=\mathbb{E}[R(Z_{1})]<1
, which is a form of contraction on average, hence the strong convexity condition is needed.

Similarly to Theorem
3
, in order to prove this result, we first obtain a nonasymptotic Wasserstein convergence bound between
\pi^{(n)}
and
\pi
, stated as follows:

Theorem 6
.

Under the setting of Theorem
5
, the following holds with probability greater than
1-\epsilon_{n}
:

\displaystyle\mathbf{W}_{1}(\pi,\pi^{(n)})\leq c_{0}L\frac{\eta}{1-\delta_{R}}\mathbf{W}_{1}(\mu_{z},\mu_{z}^{(n)})\;,

with
c_{0}=\mathbb{E}[\|X_{0}^{(n)}\|]+1
, where
X_{0}^{(n)}\sim\pi^{(n)}
.

The convergence rate implied by Theorem
6
is implicitly related to the finite moments of the data-generating distribution
\mu_{z}
. Using the results of
[
8
]
( Lemma
2
in the Appendix), we can determine the convergence rate
r
, where with high probability it will hold that
\mathbf{W}_{1}(\pi,\pi^{(n)})=\mathcal{O}(n^{-r})
, with
r\in(0,1/2]
. Let the data have finite moments up to order
q
. Then, for example, if
q>4d
, we get

r=1/2
, i.e., the fastest rate. As another example: if
d>2
and
q=3
, we obtain a rate
r=1/d
. In other words, the moments of the data-generating distribution determine whether we can achieve a dimension-free rate or fall victim to the curse of dimensionality. Thus, if the data do not admit higher-order moments, our results show that we need a large number of data points

to be able to observe power-law tails in offline SGD.

5
Conclusion

We established a relationship between the data-generating distributions and stationary distributions of offline and online SGD. This enabled us to develop the first theoretical result illuminating the heavy-tailed behavior in offline SGD. We extended previous results encompassing both quadratic losses, as well as more sophisticated strongly convex loss functions. Through an experimental study, we validated our theoretical findings in a variety of settings.

Limitations.
There are two main limitations to our work. Firstly, our analysis focuses predominantly on the ‘upper’ tails of the distributions.
Studying the bulk of the distribution of the iterates may reveal new findings. For example, research in this direction could connect our findings to learning theory by leveraging existing generalization bounds (e.g., see Corollary 2 in
[
11
]
for a link between ‘lower’ tails and generalization error). Secondly, it would be of great interest to extend our results to the non-convex deep learning settings.
Finally, since this is a theoretical paper studying online and offline SGD, our work contains no direct potential negative societal impacts.

Lemma 1

(Explicit solution of Wasserstein distance
[
17
]
)
.

Let
\mu_{1},\mu_{2}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R})
be two probability measures on
\mathbb{R}
, and denote their cumulative distribution functions by
F_{1}(x)
and
F_{2}(x)
respectively. Then, the Wasserstein-
p
distance between
\mu_{1}
and
\mu_{2}
has an explicit formula:

\displaystyle\mathbf{W}_{p}\left(\mu_{1},\mu_{2}\right)=\left(\int_{0}^{1}\left|F_{1}^{-1}(q)-F_{2}^{-1}(q)\right|^{p}\mathrm{~d}q\right)^{1/p},

where
F_{1}^{-1}
and
F_{2}^{-1}
denote the quantile functions. In the case when
p=1
, by applying the change of variables, one can obtain the following:

\displaystyle\mathbf{W}_{1}\left(\mu_{1},\mu_{2}\right)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left|F_{1}(x)-F_{2}(x)\right|\mathrm{~d}x.

Now, for
q>0,\alpha>0
,
\gamma>0
and
\mu\in\mathcal{P}\left(\mathbb{R}^{d}\right)
, let:

\displaystyle M_{q}(\mu):=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|x|^{q}\mu(\mathrm{d}x)\;\text{ and }\;\mathcal{E}_{\alpha,\gamma}(\mu):=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathrm{e}^{\gamma|x|^{\alpha}}\mu(\mathrm{d}x).

Furthermore, consider an i.i.d. sequence
\left(X_{k}\right)_{k\geq 1}
of
\mu
-distributed random variables and, for
n\geq 1
, define the empirical measure by:

\displaystyle\mu^{(n)}:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\updelta_{X_{k}}.

We can now proceed to state the following result.

Lemma 2

(
[
8
]
)
.

Let
\mu\in\mathcal{P}\left(\mathbb{R}^{d}\right)
and let
p>0
. Assume one of the three following conditions:

(1)
\alpha>p,\gamma>0,\mathcal{E}_{\alpha,\gamma}(\mu)<\infty
,

(2)
\alpha\in(0,p),\gamma>0,\mathcal{E}_{\alpha,\gamma}(\mu)<\infty
,

(3)
q>2p,M_{q}(\mu)<\infty
.

Then for all
n\geq 1
, all
x\in(0,\infty)
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\mathbf{W}_{p}(\mu^{(n)},\mu)\geq x\right)\leq a(n,x)\mathbf{1}_{\{x\leq 1\}}+b(n,x)

where

\displaystyle a(n,x)=C\begin{cases}\exp\left(-cnx^{2}\right)&\text{ if }p>d/2\\
\exp\left(-cn(x/\log(2+1/x))^{2}\right)&\text{ if }p=d/2\\
\exp\left(-cnx^{d/p}\right)&\text{ if }p\in[1,d/2)\end{cases}

and

\displaystyle b(n,x)=C\begin{cases}\exp\left(-cnx^{\alpha/p}\right)\mathbf{1}_{\{x>1\}}&\text{ under (1), }\\
\exp\left(-c(nx)^{(\alpha-\varepsilon)/p}\right)\mathbf{1}_{\{x\leq 1\}}+\exp\left(-c(nx)^{\alpha/p}\right)\mathbf{1}_{\{x>1\}}&\forall\varepsilon\in(0,\alpha)\text{ under (2), }\\
n(nx)^{-(q-\varepsilon)/p}&\forall\varepsilon\in(0,q)\text{ under (3). }\end{cases}

The positive constants
C
and
c
depend only on
p,d
and either on
\alpha,\gamma,\mathcal{E}_{\alpha,\gamma}(\mu)
(under (1)) or on
\alpha,\gamma,\mathcal{E}_{\alpha,\gamma}(\mu),\varepsilon
(under (2)) or on
q,M_{q}(\mu),\varepsilon
(under (3)).

Appendix B
Technical Lemmas

Lemma 3
.

Let
X
and
Y
be two random vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Then, we have

\displaystyle\mathbf{W}_{1}(\mathscr{L}(X),\mathscr{L}(Y))\geq

\displaystyle\mathbf{W}_{1}(\mathscr{L}(\|X\|),\mathscr{L}(\|Y\|)).

(17)

Proof.

By the definition of the Wasserstein distance, we have that

\displaystyle\mathbf{W}_{1}(\mathscr{L}(X),\mathscr{L}(Y))=

\displaystyle\inf_{\gamma\in\Gamma(\mathscr{L}(X),\mathscr{L}(Y))}\int_{{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}\|x-y\|\mathrm{d}\gamma(x,y)

(18)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}\|x-y\|\mathrm{d}\gamma^{*}(x,y),

(19)

where
\gamma^{*}
is the coupling that attains the infimum (the proof of the existence of such coupling for any Wasserstein-
p
distance, where
p\geq 1
, can be found in, e.g.,
[
2
, Theorem 8.10.45]
). Then, by the reverse triangle inequality, we have

\displaystyle\mathbf{W}_{1}(\mathscr{L}(X),\mathscr{L}(Y))\geq

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left|\|x\|-\|y\|\right|\mathrm{d}\gamma^{*}(x,y)

(20)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}}|x-y|\mathrm{~d}T_{\#}\gamma^{*}(x,y),

(21)

where
T:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\mapsto\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}
is the map
(x,y)\mapsto(\|x\|,\|y\|)
and
T_{\#}\gamma^{*}
is the pushforward measure such that for a measurable set
B\subset\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}
, we have
T_{\#}\gamma^{*}(B)=\gamma^{*}(T^{-1}(B))
. As
T_{\#}\gamma^{*}
is a coupling between
\|X\|
and
\|Y\|
, we have that

\displaystyle\mathbf{W}_{1}(\mathscr{L}(X),\mathscr{L}(Y))\geq

\displaystyle\inf_{\gamma\in\Gamma(\mathscr{L}(\|X\|),\mathscr{L}(\|Y\|))}\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}}|x-y|\mathrm{~d}\gamma(x,y)

(22)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbf{W}_{1}(\mathscr{L}(\|X\|),\mathscr{L}(\|Y\|)).

(23)

This concludes the proof.
∎

C.1
Proof of Theorem
4

Proof.

First, note that the unique stationary distributions of
(X_{k})_{k\geq 0}
and
(X_{k}^{(n)})_{k\geq 0}
are denoted respectively by
\pi
and
\pi^{(n)}
.
Denote by
E_{n}
the event on which
(X_{k}^{(n)})_{k\geq 0}
has a stationary distribution
\pi^{(n)}
such that
\mathbb{P}(E_{n})\geq 1-\epsilon_{n}
(where
\epsilon_{n}\in[0,1]
is the probability that
(X^{(n)}_{k})_{k\geq 0}
is not ergodic, in the Wasserstein sense).
Given
E_{n}
, let
(\mathbf{A}_{k},\mathbf{A}_{k}^{(n)})_{k\geq 1}
and
(\mathbf{b}_{k},\mathbf{b}_{k}^{(n)})_{k\geq 1}
be the sequences of i.i.d. optimal couplings for
\mu_{\mathbf{A}}
and
\mu^{(n)}_{\mathbf{A}}
,
\mu_{\mathbf{b}}
and
\mu^{(n)}_{\mathbf{b}}
respectively. Therefore, by construction, for any
k\in\mathbb{N}
,
\mathbb{E}[\|\mathbf{A}_{k}-\mathbf{A}_{k}^{(n)}\|]=\mathbf{W}_{1}(\mu_{\mathbf{A}},\mu^{(n)}_{\mathbf{A}})
, and
\mathbb{E}[\|\mathbf{b}_{k}-\mathbf{b}_{k}^{(n)}\|]=\mathbf{W}_{1}(\mu_{\mathbf{b}},\mu^{(n)}_{\mathbf{b}})
. Based on these two sequences, we consider the processes
(X_{k})_{k\geq 0}
,
(X_{k}^{(n)})_{k\geq 0}
,
(Y_{k})_{k\geq 0}
defined by the recursions:

1.

X_{k+1}=(\operatorname{I}_{d}-\eta\mathbf{A}_{k+1})X_{k}+\eta\mathbf{b}_{k+1}
, where
X_{0}\sim\pi
,

2.

X_{k+1}^{(n)}=(\operatorname{I}_{d}-\eta\mathbf{A}_{k+1}^{(n)})X_{k}^{(n)}+\eta\mathbf{b}_{k+1}^{(n)}
, where
X_{0}^{(n)}\sim\pi^{(n)}
,

3.

Y_{k+1}=(\operatorname{I}_{d}-\eta\mathbf{A}_{k+1})Y_{k}+\eta\mathbf{b}_{k+1}
, where
Y_{0}=X_{0}^{(n)}
.

Note that
(X_{k})_{k\geq 0}
corresponds to the online SGD recursion (
7
), and
(X_{k}^{(n)})_{k\geq 0}
to the offline SGD recursion (
9
). In addition, since these two Markov chains are started at stationarity, we have:

\displaystyle\mathbf{W}_{1}(\pi,\pi^{(n)})=\mathbf{W}_{1}(\mathscr{L}(X_{0}),\mathscr{L}(X^{(n)}_{0}))=\mathbf{W}_{1}(\mathscr{L}(X_{k}),\mathscr{L}(X^{(n)}_{k})),\ \forall k.

With these definitions at hand and this observation, using triangle inequality, we can obtain:

\displaystyle\mathbf{W}_{1}(\pi,\pi^{(n)})

\displaystyle=\mathbf{W}_{1}(\mathscr{L}(X_{k}),\mathscr{L}(X^{(n)}_{k}))

\displaystyle\leq\mathbf{W}_{1}(\mathscr{L}(X_{k}),\mathscr{L}(Y_{k}))+\mathbf{W}_{1}(\mathscr{L}(Y_{k}),\mathscr{L}(X^{(n)}_{k})).

Now, by
[
9
, Theorem 8]
, we have that
Y_{k}
is geometrically ergodic with respect to its stationary distribution
\pi
, i.e., there exist constants
c_{\rho}>0
,
\rho\in(0,1)
such that the following inequality holds:

\displaystyle\mathbf{W}_{1}(\mathscr{L}(Y_{k}),\pi)\leq c_{\rho}\mathbf{W}_{1}(\mathscr{L}(Y_{0}),\pi)\mathrm{e}^{-\rho k}\;,\text{ for any }k\geq 0\;.

(24)

Therefore, we have:

\displaystyle\mathbf{W}_{1}(\pi,\pi^{(n)})\leq c_{\rho}\mathrm{e}^{-\rho k}\mathbf{W}_{1}(\pi,\mathscr{L}(Y_{0}))+\mathbf{W}_{1}(\mathscr{L}(Y_{k}),\mathscr{L}(X^{(n)}_{k})),

as
\mathbf{W}_{1}(\mathscr{L}(X_{k}),\mathscr{L}(Y_{k}))=\mathbf{W}_{1}(\pi,\mathscr{L}(Y_{k}))\leq c_{\rho}\mathrm{e}^{-\rho k}\mathbf{W}_{1}(\pi,\mathscr{L}(Y_{0}))
. Rearranging the above terms, we get
\mathbf{W}_{1}(\pi,\pi^{(n)})(1-c_{\rho}\mathrm{e}^{-\rho k})\leq\mathbf{W}_{1}(\mathscr{L}(Y_{k}),\mathscr{L}(X^{(n)}_{k}))
implying:

\displaystyle\mathbf{W}_{1}(\pi,\pi^{(n)})\leq(1-c_{\rho}\mathrm{e}^{-\rho k})^{-1}\mathbf{W}_{1}(\mathscr{L}(Y_{k}),\mathscr{L}(X^{(n)}_{k})).

(25)

To bound the right-hand side of (
25
), we consider the following difference by using the recursion definitions:

\displaystyle Y_{k+1}-X^{(n)}_{k+1}

\displaystyle=Y_{k}-\eta\mathbf{A}_{k+1}Y_{k}+\eta\mathbf{b}_{k+1}-X_{k}^{(n)}+\eta\mathbf{A}_{k+1}^{(n)}X_{k}^{(n)}-\eta\mathbf{b}_{k+1}^{(n)}

(26)

\displaystyle=(\operatorname{I}_{d}-\eta\mathbf{A}_{k+1})(Y_{k}-X^{(n)}_{k})-\eta(\mathbf{A}_{k+1}-\mathbf{A}^{(n)}_{k+1})X^{(n)}_{k}+\eta(\mathbf{b}_{k+1}-\mathbf{b}^{(n)}_{k+1}).

(27)

Writing out the recursion, we can obtain:

\displaystyle Y_{k+1}-X^{(n)}_{k+1}

\displaystyle=(Y_{0}-X^{(n)}_{0})\prod_{i=0}^{k}(\operatorname{I}_{d}-\eta\mathbf{A}_{i+1})

\displaystyle-\eta\sum_{i=0}^{k}\left[X^{(n)}_{i}(\mathbf{A}_{i+1}-\mathbf{A}^{(n)}_{i+1})\prod_{j=i+2}^{k+1}(\operatorname{I}_{d}-\eta\mathbf{A}_{j})\right]

\displaystyle+\eta\sum_{i=0}^{k}\left[(\mathbf{b}_{i+1}-\mathbf{b}^{(n)}_{i+1})\prod_{j=i+2}^{k+1}(\operatorname{I}_{d}-\eta\mathbf{A}_{j})\right],

where for any sequence
(a_{i})_{i\geq 0}
, we let
\prod_{i=j}^{k}a_{i}=1
when
j>k
. Now, taking the norm of both sides, using the triangle inequality, and taking expectations given
E_{n}
, we obtain:

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}[\|Y_{k+1}-X^{(n)}_{k+1}\|]&\leq\mathbb{E}[\|(Y_{0}-X^{(n)}_{0})\prod_{i=0}^{k}(\operatorname{I}_{d}-\eta\mathbf{A}_{i+1})\|]\\
&+\eta\mathbb{E}[\sum_{i=0}^{k}\|X^{(n)}_{i}(\mathbf{A}_{i+1}-\mathbf{A}^{(n)}_{i+1})\prod_{j=i+2}^{k+1}(\operatorname{I}_{d}-\eta\mathbf{A}_{j})\|]\\
&+\eta\mathbb{E}[\sum_{i=0}^{k}\|(\mathbf{b}_{i+1}-\mathbf{b}^{(n)}_{i+1})\prod_{j=i+2}^{k+1}(\operatorname{I}_{d}-\eta\mathbf{A}_{j})\|].\end{split}

(28)

We shall now analyze each of the three summands separately.
For the first term in (
28
), as we start with
Y_{0}=X^{(n)}_{0}
, it equals zero. For the second term in (
28
), we have:

\displaystyle\eta\mathbb{E}[\sum_{i=0}^{k}\|X^{(n)}_{i}(\mathbf{A}_{i+1}-\mathbf{A}^{(n)}_{i+1})\prod_{j=i+2}^{k+1}(\operatorname{I}_{d}-\eta\mathbf{A}_{j})\|]

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textnormal{(a)}}}{{\mathstrut{=}}}\eta\sum_{i=0}^{k}\mathbb{E}[\|X^{(n)}_{i}(\mathbf{A}_{i+1}-\mathbf{A}^{(n)}_{i+1})\prod_{j=i+2}^{k+1}(\operatorname{I}_{d}-\eta\mathbf{A}_{j})\|]

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textnormal{(b)}}}{{\mathstrut{\leq}}}\eta\sum_{i=0}^{k}\mathbb{E}[\|X^{(n)}_{i}(\mathbf{A}_{i+1}-\mathbf{A}^{(n)}_{i+1})\|]\mathbb{E}^{k-i}[\|\operatorname{I}_{d}-\eta\mathbf{A}_{1}\|]

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textnormal{(c)}}}{{\mathstrut{\leq}}}\eta\sum_{i=0}^{k}(\mathbb{E}[\|X^{(n)}_{i}\|^{q}])^{\frac{1}{q}}(\mathbb{E}[\|\mathbf{A}_{i+1}-\mathbf{A}^{(n)}_{i+1}\|^{p}])^{\frac{1}{p}}\mathbb{E}^{k-i}[\|\operatorname{I}_{d}-\eta\mathbf{A}_{1}\|],

where (
C.1
) follows from the linearity of expectation, (
C.1
) follows as
\mathbf{A}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{A}_{i-1}
are i.i.d. and independent from
\mathbf{A}_{i},\dots,\mathbf{A}_{k+1}
, and (
C.1
) is obtained by applying the Hölder’s inequality, where
p
and
q
are such that
1/p+1/q=1
, with
p,q\in(1,+\infty)
,
q<\alpha
and
p>d/2
(used later in the proof), where
\alpha
is the tail index.

As the chain
(X_{k}^{(n)})_{k\geq 0}
starts from its stationary distribution
\pi^{(n)}
, we have that
(\mathbb{E}[\|X^{(n)}_{i}\|^{q}])^{1/q}=(\mathbb{E}[\|X^{(n)}_{0}\|^{q}])^{1/q},\forall i
. Using the fact that
(\mathbf{A}_{i}-\mathbf{A}^{(n)}_{i})_{i=1}^{k+1}
are i.i.d. random variables (the randomness arises from the empirical measure
\mu^{(n)}_{\mathbf{A}}
),
\mathbb{E}[\|\operatorname{I}_{d}-\eta\mathbf{A}_{1}\|^{\alpha}]=1
and
\alpha>1
, we have that:

\displaystyle\delta_{A}:=\mathbb{E}[\|\operatorname{I}_{d}-\eta\mathbf{A}_{1}\|]<1.

(29)

Therefore, we can obtain the following bound:

\displaystyle\eta\mathbb{E}[\sum_{i=0}^{k}\|X^{(n)}_{i}(\mathbf{A}_{i+1}-\mathbf{A}^{(n)}_{i+1})\prod_{j=i+2}^{k+1}(\operatorname{I}_{d}-\eta\mathbf{A}_{j})\|]

\displaystyle\leq\eta(\mathbb{E}[\|X^{(n)}_{0}\|^{q}])^{\frac{1}{q}}(\mathbb{E}[\|\mathbf{A}_{1}-\mathbf{A}^{(n)}_{1}\|^{p}])^{\frac{1}{p}}\sum_{i=0}^{k}\mathbb{E}^{k-i}[\|\operatorname{I}_{d}-\eta\mathbf{A}_{1}\|]

\displaystyle\leq\eta(\mathbb{E}[\|X^{(n)}_{0}\|^{q}])^{\frac{1}{q}}(\mathbb{E}[\|\mathbf{A}_{1}-\mathbf{A}^{(n)}_{1}\|^{p}])^{\frac{1}{p}}\frac{1}{1-\delta_{A}},

where the last equality follows from (
29
) and the power series sum formula. Using the same arguments, we can bound the third term in (
28
):

\displaystyle\eta\mathbb{E}[\sum_{i=0}^{k}\|(\mathbf{b}_{i+1}-\mathbf{b}^{(n)}_{i+1})\prod_{j=i+2}^{k+1}(\operatorname{I}_{d}-\eta\mathbf{A}_{j})\|]\leq\eta\mathbb{E}[\|\mathbf{b}_{1}-\mathbf{b}^{(n)}_{1}\|]\frac{1}{1-\delta_{A}}.

Combining the above, we can obtain that:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\|Y_{k+1}-X^{(n)}_{k+1}\|]

\displaystyle\leq\eta(\mathbb{E}[\|X^{(n)}_{0}\|^{q}])^{\frac{1}{q}}(\mathbb{E}[\|\mathbf{A}_{1}-\mathbf{A}^{(n)}_{1}\|^{p}])^{\frac{1}{p}}\frac{1}{1-\delta_{A}}+\eta\mathbb{E}[\|\mathbf{b}_{1}-\mathbf{b}^{(n)}_{1}\|]\frac{1}{1-\delta_{A}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\eta}{1-\delta_{A}}\left((\mathbb{E}[\|X^{(n)}_{0}\|^{q}])^{\frac{1}{q}}(\mathbb{E}[\|\mathbf{A}_{1}-\mathbf{A}^{(n)}_{1}\|^{p}])^{\frac{1}{p}}+\mathbb{E}[\|\mathbf{b}_{1}-\mathbf{b}^{(n)}_{1}\|]\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\eta}{1-\delta_{A}}\left((\mathbb{E}[\|X^{(n)}_{0}\|^{q}])^{\frac{1}{q}}(\mathbb{E}[\|\mathbf{A}_{1}-\mathbf{A}^{(n)}_{1}\|^{p}])^{\frac{1}{p}}+(\mathbb{E}[\|\mathbf{b}_{1}-\mathbf{b}^{(n)}_{1}\|^{p}])^{\frac{1}{p}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\eta}{1-\delta_{A}}\left((\mathbb{E}[\|X^{(n)}_{0}\|^{q}])^{\frac{1}{q}}\mathbf{W}_{p}(\mu_{\mathbf{A}},\mu^{(n)}_{\mathbf{A}})+\mathbf{W}_{p}(\mu_{\mathbf{b}},\mu^{(n)}_{\mathbf{b}})\right)

(30)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\eta}{1-\delta_{A}}\left(((\mathbb{E}[\|X^{(n)}_{0}\|^{q}])^{\frac{1}{q}}+1)\mathbf{W}_{p}(\mu_{\mathbf{A},\mathbf{b}},\mu^{(n)}_{\mathbf{A},\mathbf{b}})\right),

(31)

where the penultimate inequality follows from Jensen’s inequality: it implies that
\mathbf{W}_{p}(\mu,\nu)\leq\mathbf{W}_{q}(\mu,\nu)
, for
p\leq q
(see, e.g.,
[
24
]
). The last inequality follows as
\mathbf{W}_{p}(\mu_{\mathbf{A}},\mu_{\mathbf{A}}^{(n)})\leq\mathbf{W}_{p}(\mu_{\mathbf{A},\mathbf{b}},\mu_{\mathbf{A},\mathbf{b}}^{(n)})
, and
\mathbf{W}_{p}(\mu_{\mathbf{b}},\mu^{(n)}_{\mathbf{b}})\leq\mathbf{W}_{p}(\mu_{\mathbf{A},\mathbf{b}},\mu^{(n)}_{\mathbf{A},\mathbf{b}})
. Together with (
25
), we have:

\displaystyle\mathbf{W}_{1}(\pi,\pi^{(n)})\leq((\mathbb{E}[\|X^{(n)}_{0}\|^{q}])^{\frac{1}{q}}+1)(1-c_{\rho}\mathrm{e}^{-\rho k})^{-1}\frac{\eta}{1-\delta_{A}}\mathbf{W}_{p}(\mu_{\mathbf{A},\mathbf{b}},\mu^{(n)}_{\mathbf{A},\mathbf{b}})\;.

By taking the limit as
k\rightarrow\infty
, we can conclude the proof of Theorem
4
.
∎

Now, taking the norm of both sides and using

\displaystyle\|Y_{k+1}-X_{k+1}^{(n)}\|

Now,

\displaystyle\|\operatorname{I}_{d}-\eta\int_{0}^{1}\nabla^{2}f(y-u(y-x),z)\mathrm{~d}u\|

(43)

where again

\displaystyle\|Y_{k+1}-X_{k+1}^{(n)}\|\leq R(Z_{k+1})\|Y_{k}-X_{k}^{(n)}\|+\eta L(\|X_{k}^{(n)}\|+1)\|Z_{k+1}-\hat{Z}_{k+1}^{(n)}\|.

\displaystyle\|Y_{k+1}-X_{k+1}^{(n)}\|\leq R(Z_{k+1})\|Y_{k}-X_{k}^{(n)}\|+\eta LB_{k}^{(n)}\|Z_{k+1}-\hat{Z}_{k+1}^{(n)}\|

\displaystyle\leq\|Y_{0}-X_{0}^{(n)}\|\prod_{i=1}^{k+1}R(Z_{i})+\eta L\sum_{i=0}^{k}B_{i}^{(n)}\|Z_{i+1}-\hat{Z}_{i+1}^{(n)}\|\prod_{j=i+1}^{k}R(Z_{j+1}),

where again, for any sequence

(44)

, and

are i.i.d. Furthermore, using (

\displaystyle\leq\eta L(\mathbb{E}[\|X_{0}^{(n)}\|]+1)\mathbb{E}[\|Z_{1}-\hat{Z}_{1}^{(n)}\|]\frac{1}{1-\delta_{R}}.

Finally, denoting

Appendix F
Strongly Convex Problem Example

Consider the following one-dimensional logistic regression where the regularization parameter
\lambda\sim Exp(\mu)
, i.e., it follows an Exponential distribution with mean
1/\mu
- its probability density function is as follows:

\displaystyle f(\lambda;\mu)=\begin{cases}\mu^{-1}e^{-\lambda/\mu}&\lambda\geq 0\\
0&\lambda<0\end{cases}.

As before, let
(a_{i},q_{i})_{i\geq 1}
be i.i.d. random variables in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, such that
z_{i}\equiv(a_{i},q_{i})
. In this scenario, where
a\sim N(0,\sigma^{2})
and
y\in\{0,1\}
, the loss function equals:

\displaystyle\ell(x,z)=-y\ln\left(\frac{1}{\mathrm{e}^{-ax}+1}\right)-(1-y)\ln\left(1-\frac{1}{\mathrm{e}^{-ax}+1}\right)+\frac{1}{2}\lambda\|x\|^{2}.

Now, the second derivative of the loss with respect to the parameter
x
equals
\nabla^{2}\ell(x,z)=\frac{a^{2}\mathrm{e}^{ax}}{\left(\mathrm{e}^{ax}+1\right)^{2}}+\lambda
. Note that
|\nabla^{2}\ell(x,z)|\leq a^{2}/4+\lambda,\forall x\in X
. In the SGD case, from
[
10
]
, we have
r(z)=\liminf_{\|x\|\rightarrow\infty}\sigma_{\min}\left(\operatorname{I}_{d}-\gamma\nabla^{2}\ell(x,z)\right)
and
R(z)=\sup_{x}\left\|\operatorname{I}_{d}-\gamma\nabla^{2}\ell(x,z)\right\|
. In other words, as we are in the one-dimensional setting, we have:

\displaystyle r(z)=\liminf_{|x|\rightarrow\infty}\left|1-\gamma\lambda-\gamma\frac{a^{2}\mathrm{e}^{ax}}{(\mathrm{e}^{ax}+1)^{2}}\right|

\displaystyle=|1-\gamma\lambda|,\text{ and}

\displaystyle R(z)=\sup_{x}\left|1-\gamma\lambda-\gamma\frac{a^{2}\mathrm{e}^{ax}}{(\mathrm{e}^{ax}+1)^{2}}\right|

\displaystyle=\max\left(|1-\gamma\lambda|,|1-\gamma\lambda-\gamma\frac{a^{2}}{4}|\right).

Now, we require
\mathbb{P}(r(Z_{1})>1)>0
and
\mathbb{E}[R(Z_{1})]<1
. As
\lambda\sim Exp(\mu)
, we have that
\mathbb{P}(r(Z_{1})>1)>0
. For the latter condition, we can calculate the required expectation:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}|1-\gamma\lambda|

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\frac{1}{\gamma}}(1-\gamma\lambda)\mu\mathrm{e}^{-\mu\lambda}\mathrm{~d}\lambda+\int_{\frac{1}{\gamma}}^{\infty}(\gamma\lambda-1)\mu\mathrm{e}^{-\mu\lambda}\mathrm{~d}\lambda

\displaystyle=-\frac{(\gamma-\mu)-\gamma\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{\mu}{\gamma}}}{\mu}+\frac{\gamma\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{\mu}{\gamma}}}{\mu}

\displaystyle=\frac{2\gamma\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{\mu}{\gamma}}-\gamma}{\mu}+1.

Furthermore, we have:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}|1-\gamma\frac{x^{2}}{4}-\gamma\lambda|

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\int_{0}^{1-\frac{\gamma x^{2}}{4}}(1-\gamma\frac{x^{2}}{4}-\gamma\lambda)\mu\mathrm{e}^{-\mu\lambda}\mathrm{~d}\lambda+\mathbb{E}\int_{1-\frac{\gamma x^{2}}{4}}^{\infty}(\gamma\frac{x^{2}}{4}+\gamma\lambda-1)\mu\mathrm{e}^{-\mu\lambda}\mathrm{~d}\lambda

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{2\gamma}{\mu}\mathrm{e}^{\frac{\mu}{\gamma}(1-\frac{\gamma x^{2}}{4})}+1-\frac{\gamma}{\mu}-\frac{\gamma x^{2}}{4}\right]

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\left(\frac{2\gamma}{\mu}\mathrm{e}^{\frac{\mu}{\gamma}(1-\frac{\gamma x^{2}}{4})}+1-\frac{\gamma}{\mu}-\frac{\gamma x^{2}}{4}\right)\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}x^{2}}}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^{2}}}\mathrm{~d}x

\displaystyle=\frac{2\sqrt{2}\gamma}{\mu\sqrt{2-\sigma^{2}\mu}}\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{\mu}{\gamma}}-\gamma(\frac{1}{\mu}+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{4})+1

We can see that, for example, both expressions are less than 1 in modulus for
\mu=0.1
,
\sigma^{2}=1
, and
\gamma=0.1
. It is important to highlight that the aforementioned calculations necessitate the condition
\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}-\frac{\mu}{4}>0
, or equivalently
\sigma^{2}\mu<2
. This condition can be interpreted as a stability criterion, indicating that the mean of the penalization term
\lambda
must increase as the variance of the data grows. In other words, a larger variance necessitates a higher mean value for
\lambda
.
