Sub- and Super-solution Approach to Accuracy Analysis of Portfolio Optimization Asymptotics in Multiscale Stochastic Factor Markets
Jean-Pierre Fouque, Ruimeng Hu, Ronnie Sircar

Abstract
The problem of portfolio optimization when stochastic factors drive returns and volatilities has been studied in previous works by the authors. In particular, they proposed asymptotic approximations for value functions and optimal strategies in the regime where these factors are running on both slow and fast timescales. However, the rigorous justification of the accuracy of these approximations has been limited to power utilities and a single factor. In this paper, we provide an accurate analysis for cases with general utility functions and two timescale factors by constructing sub- and super-solutions to the fully nonlinear problem so that their difference is at the desired level of accuracy. This approach will be valuable in various related stochastic control problems.



AMS subject classification
: 91G10, 93E20, 60H30, 35C20

Keywords
: portfolio optimization, utility maximization, stochastic volatility, rigorous asymptotics, sub-solution, super-solution

1
Introduction

The study of the portfolio optimization problem in continuous time has a long history dating back to
[
14
,
15
]
. Specifically, an agent aims to maximize her expected utility of the terminal wealth when the investment is divided between risky assets and a riskless asset, with particular types of utility functions. In Merton’s seminal work, the risky assets are assumed to follow the Black-Scholes model, where the expected returns and volatilities are constant. Since then, the problem has been studied extensively in various settings and levels of generality, including considering transaction costs
[
4
,
10
]
, stochastic volatility models for risky assets (see, for instance
[
9
]
, and references therein), and various trading constraints (see
[
16
]
for a survey).

In this paper, we consider the portfolio optimization problem in a stochastic environment, where both the expected return
\mu
and volatility
\sigma
of a stock price
S
are driven by two factors
Y_{t}
and
Z_{t}
evidenced in empirical study
[
8
]
:

\frac{\,\mathrm{d}S_{t}}{S_{t}}=\mu(Y_{t},Z_{t})\,\mathrm{d}t+\sigma(Y_{t},Z_{t})\,\mathrm{d}W_{t}.

(1.1)

Here
W
is a standard Brownian motion.
The two factors are characterized by the small parameters
\varepsilon
and
\delta
, the fast timescale being represented by
\varepsilon
, and the slow timescale by
1/\delta
. Along this direction, results have been developed in
[
9
]
about asymptotic expansions of the optimal trading strategy and the maximized utility when the utility function is general, as both
\varepsilon
and
\delta
tend to zero.

However, the accuracy of approximation was rigorously justified only in the cases with power utilities and one stochastic factor, when a distortion transformation of Hopf-Cole type introduced in
[
17
]
enables a reduction to a
linear
PDE problem. This is only possible in the case of multiple factors in special cases; see
[
1
]
.
After that, several attempts have been made to partially justify these expansions in more general cases,
e.g.
, in
[
5
,
12
,
6
]
; see also
[
11
]
for a comprehensive review.
The technique of sub- and super-solutions to prove the accuracy of asymptotic approximations is used in
[
2
]
for a model of power utility maximization with two nearly correlated factors corresponding to a regular perturbation problem, and in
[
3
]
for optimal investment under stochastic volatility and transaction costs.
The contribution of this paper is to rigorously justify the heuristic expansion provided in
[
9
]
. The methodology presented here can be adapted to the derivation of accuracy results in various contexts, as in
[
7
]
for instance, where fast mean-reversion is shown in data and the corresponding control problem appears as a Ricatti equation with fast mean-reverting random coefficients. Proof of accuracy of an approximate solution to this singular perturbation problem was obtained by constructing sub- and super-solutions.

We shall construct two functions close to the value function of the problem. These two functions act as lower and upper bounds, namely sub- and super- solutions of this problem. By requiring certain properties for each function and, most importantly, by constructing their difference to reach the desired order, one can show that the asymptotic approximations derived in
[
9
]
are rigorous under general utility. The requirement for the sub-solution is rather relaxed, and we shall work with a particular zeroth-order strategy. Since the super-solution acts as an upper bound of the problem, we need to ensure the property holds for all admissible strategies.

The rest of this paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
, we first introduce the Merton problem in a multiscale stochastic environment and the associated Hamilton-Jacobi-Bellman (HJB) equation, and we describe the main result of the paper, Theorem
2.1
. In Section
2.1
, we briefly explain our approach which consists in constructing sub- and super-solutions so that their difference is at the desired level. The asymptotic approximations derived in
[
9
]
are reviewed in Section
2.2
. Section
2.3
summarizes standing assumptions in this paper and some preliminary estimates which facilitate the asymptotic analysis in Section
3
. Section
3
is dedicated to the construction of the sub- and super-solutions, thus completing the proof of Theorem
2.1
. We make concluding remarks in Section
4
.

2
Merton Problem under Multiscale Stochastic Environment

We consider the utility maximization problem on the finite horizon
[0,T]
with general terminal utility
U
, where the (single) underlying asset
S_{t}
is driven by two factors: one fast mean-reverting
Y_{t}
, and one slowly varying
Z_{t}
:

\displaystyle\,\mathrm{d}S_{t}=\mu(Y_{t},Z_{t})S_{t}\,\mathrm{d}t+\sigma(Y_{t},Z_{t})S_{t}\,\mathrm{d}W_{t},

(2.1)

\displaystyle\,\mathrm{d}Y_{t}=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}b(Y_{t})\,\mathrm{d}t+\frac{1}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}a(Y_{t})\,\mathrm{d}W_{t}^{Y},

(2.2)

\displaystyle\,\mathrm{d}Z_{t}=\delta c(Z_{t})\,\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{\delta}g(Z_{t})\,\mathrm{d}W_{t}^{Z},

(2.3)

Here
W,W^{Y},W^{Z}
are standard Brownian motions on a filtered probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
that are correlated:

\,\mathrm{d}\left\langle W,W^{Y}\right\rangle_{t}=\rho_{1}\,\mathrm{d}t,\quad\,\mathrm{d}\left\langle W,W^{Z}\right\rangle_{t}=\rho_{2}\,\mathrm{d}t,\quad\,\mathrm{d}\left\langle W^{Y},W^{Z}\right\rangle_{t}=\rho_{12}\,\mathrm{d}t,

(2.4)

where
\left|\rho_{1}\right|<1
,
\left|\rho_{2}\right|<1
,
\left|\rho_{12}\right|<1
and
1+2\rho_{1}\rho_{2}\rho_{12}-\rho_{1}^{2}-\rho_{2}^{2}-\rho_{12}^{2}>0
to ensure the positive definiteness of the covariance matrix of
(W,W^{Y},W^{Z})
. The time scales of
Y_{t}
and
Z_{t}
are described by the small positive parameters
\varepsilon
and
\delta
respectively. We shall assume that the process
Y_{t}
is ergodic and has a unique invariant distribution
\Phi
which is independent of
\varepsilon
as its infinitesimal generator is of the form
\varepsilon^{-1}{\cal L}_{y}
.
Further assumptions on the model parameters
\mu,\sigma,b,a,c,g
will be list in Section
2.3
. In particular, they will imply that the system (
2.1
)-(
2.3
) has a unique strong solution.

Let
X_{t}^{\pi}
be a wealth process with
\pi_{t}
being the dollar amount invested in the underlying asset at time
t
and the remaining held in a money market earning interest at rate
r
. Under the self-financing assumption, and without loss of generality, assuming
r=0
, the wealth process follows:

\,\mathrm{d}X_{t}^{\pi}=\pi_{t}[\mu(Y_{t},Z_{t})\,\mathrm{d}t+\sigma(Y_{t},Z_{t})\,\mathrm{d}W_{t}].

(2.5)

We are interested in the utility maximization of the terminal wealth:

\sup_{\pi}\mathbb{E}[U(X_{T}^{\pi})],

for all admissible strategies
\pi
(see Definition
2.4
), and where
U(\cdot)
is a general utility function satisfying Assumption
2.3
.

Restricting the problem to Markovian strategies of the form
\pi(t,x,y,z)
, we denote by
V^{\varepsilon,\delta}(t,x,y,z)
the value function

V^{\varepsilon,\delta}(t,x,y,z):=\sup_{\pi}\mathbb{E}_{t}[U(X_{T}^{\pi})],\quad\mathbb{E}_{t}[\cdot]=\mathbb{E}[\cdot|X_{t}^{\pi}=x,Y_{t}=y,Z_{t}=z].

(2.6)

As described in
[
9
]
, the Hamilton-Jacobi-Bellman (HJB) PDE associated to this problem is,

\sup_{\pi}Q^{\pi}[V^{\varepsilon,\delta}](t,x,y,z)=0,\quad V^{\varepsilon,\delta}(T,x,y,z)=U(x),

(2.7)

where
Q^{\pi}=\partial_{t}+{\cal L}^{\pi}
and
{\cal L}^{\pi}
is the infinitesimal generator of
(X^{\pi}_{t},Y_{t},Z_{t})
for any Markovian strategy
\pi(t,x,y,z)
. Specifically,

\displaystyle Q^{\pi}

\displaystyle=\partial_{t}+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\mathcal{L}_{y}+\sqrt{\frac{\delta}{\varepsilon}}\mathcal{M}_{yz}+\delta\mathcal{L}_{z}+\frac{1}{2}\pi^{2}\sigma(y,z)^{2}\partial_{xx}

(2.8)

\displaystyle+\pi\left[\mu(y,z)\partial_{x}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}\rho_{1}a(y)\sigma(y,z)\partial_{xy}+\sqrt{\delta}\rho_{2}g(z)\sigma(y,z)\partial_{xz}\right],

(2.9)

where

\mathcal{L}_{y}=b(y)\partial_{y}+\frac{1}{2}a^{2}(y)\partial_{yy},\quad\mathcal{L}_{z}=c(z)\partial_{z}+\frac{1}{2}g^{2}(z)\partial_{zz},\quad\mathcal{M}_{yz}=\rho_{12}a(y)g(z)\partial_{yz}.

(2.10)

We stress the fact that, in general, it is not known if the PDE (
2.7
) admits a classical solution.

The formal asymptotic expansion in the regime where
(\varepsilon,\delta)
are both small, performed in
[
9
, Section 4]
,
shows
that
V^{\varepsilon,\delta}\approx v^{(0)}+\sqrt{\varepsilon}v^{(1,0)}+\sqrt{\delta}v^{(0,1)}
where
v^{(0)}
,
v^{(1,0)}
and
v^{(0,1)}
are functions of
(t,x,z)
given here in Section
2.2
.
The main result of this paper is to justify this asymptotic expansion,
i.e.
, to prove the following theorem.

Theorem 2.1
.

Under Assumptions
2.3
–
2.5
, the following accuracy estimate for
V^{\varepsilon,\delta}
defined in (
2.6
) holds:

\left|V^{\varepsilon,\delta}-\left(v^{(0)}+\sqrt{\varepsilon}v^{(1,0)}+\sqrt{\delta}v^{(0,1)}\right)\right|(t,x,y,z)\leq\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon+\delta),

(2.11)

for fixed
(t,x,y,z)\in[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{2}
and sufficiently small
(\varepsilon,\delta)
, where
v^{(0)}
,
v^{(1,0)}
and
v^{(0,1)}
will be given in Section
2.2
.

Additionally, the strategy
{\pi^{(0)}}
given by (
3.3
) is asymptotically optimal in the sense:

\left|V^{\varepsilon,\delta}(t,x,y,z)-\mathbb{E}_{t}[U(X_{T}^{\pi^{(0)}})]\right|\leq\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon+\delta).

(2.12)

2.1
Methodology

The method for proving Theorem
2.1
is to construct two functions
V^{\pm}(t,x,y,z)
as sub- and super-solutions, whose asymptotic expanded terms of the orders up to
\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{\varepsilon}+\sqrt{\delta})
coincide with
v^{(0)}+\sqrt{\varepsilon}v^{(1,0)}+\sqrt{\delta}v^{(0,1)}
. Specifically, we aim to find a function
V^{-}
for the sub-solution such that, for all
(x,y,z)
and sufficiently small
(\varepsilon,\delta)
, the following requirements are satisfied:

(
R1
)

The function value
V^{-}(T,x,y,z)
is dominated by
U(x)
;

(
R2
)

The process
V^{-}(t,X_{t}^{\pi^{(0)}},Y_{t},Z_{t})
along a zero-order strategy, denoted by
{\pi^{(0)}}
, is a submartingale.

Thus, by the definition of
V^{\varepsilon,\delta}(t,x,y,z)
,
(R1)
and
(R2)
, one can obtain:

V^{\varepsilon,\delta}(t,x,y,z)\geq\mathbb{E}_{t}[U(X_{T}^{\pi^{(0)}})]\geq\mathbb{E}_{t}[V^{-}(T,X_{T}^{\pi^{(0)}},Y_{T},Z_{T})]\geq V^{-}(t,x,y,z).

(2.13)

Then, we aim to find a function
V^{+}
for the super-solution such that, for all
(x,y,z)
and sufficiently small
(\varepsilon,\delta)
, the following requirements are satisfied:

(
R3
)

The function value
V^{+}(T,x,y,z)
dominates
U(x)
;

(
R4
)

\widehat{Q}[V^{+}](t,x,y,z):=\sup_{\pi}Q^{\pi}[V^{+}](t,x,y,z)
exists and is non-positive;

(
R5
)

The Itô integrals
\int_{0}^{t}V^{+}_{x}\pi\sigma(Y_{s},Z_{s})\,\mathrm{d}W_{s}
,
\int_{0}^{t}V^{+}_{y}\frac{1}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}a(Y_{s})\,\mathrm{d}W_{s}^{Y}
and
\int_{0}^{t}V^{+}_{z}\sqrt{\delta}g(Z_{s})\,\mathrm{d}W_{s}^{Z}
are true martingales, for any admissible
\pi
.

Then, as in the argument used for (
2.13
), one can deduce:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{t}[U(X_{T}^{\pi})]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{t}[V^{+}(T,X_{T}^{\pi},Y_{T},Z_{T})]

(2.14)

\displaystyle=V^{+}(t,x,y,z)+\mathbb{E}_{t}\left[\int_{t}^{T}Q^{\pi}[V^{+}](s,X_{s}^{\pi},Y_{s},Z_{s})\,\mathrm{d}s\right]

(2.15)

\displaystyle\quad+\mathbb{E}_{t}\left[\int_{t}^{T}V^{+}_{x}\pi\sigma(Y_{s},Z_{s})\,\mathrm{d}W_{s}\right]+\mathbb{E}_{t}\left[\int_{t}^{T}V^{+}_{y}\frac{1}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}a(Y_{s})\,\mathrm{d}W_{s}^{Y}\right]

(2.16)

\displaystyle\quad+\mathbb{E}_{t}\left[\int_{t}^{T}V^{+}_{z}\sqrt{\delta}g(Z_{s})\,\mathrm{d}W_{s}^{Z}\right]

(2.17)

\displaystyle\leq V^{+}(t,x,y,z)+\mathbb{E}_{t}\left[\int_{t}^{T}\widehat{Q}[V^{+}](s,X_{s}^{\pi},Y_{s},Z_{s})\,\mathrm{d}s\right]\leq V^{+}(t,x,y,z).

(2.18)

Taking the supremum over all admissible
\pi
on both sides of (
2.18
) gives
V^{\varepsilon,\delta}(t,x,y,z)\leq V^{+}(t,x,y,z)
.
Combining (
2.13
) and (
2.18
) gives

\displaystyle v^{(0)}+\sqrt{\varepsilon}v^{(1,0)}+\sqrt{\delta}v^{(0,1)}+o(\sqrt{\varepsilon}+\sqrt{\delta})

\displaystyle=V^{-}(t,x,y,z)\leq V^{\varepsilon,\delta}(t,x,y,z)\leq V^{+}(t,x,y,z)

(2.19)

\displaystyle=v^{(0)}+\sqrt{\varepsilon}v^{(1,0)}+\sqrt{\delta}v^{(0,1)}+o(\sqrt{\varepsilon}+\sqrt{\delta}).

(2.20)

In fact, the next order terms after
v^{(0)}+\sqrt{\varepsilon}v^{(1,0)}+\sqrt{\delta}v^{(0,1)}
in the construction of
V^{\pm}
are
\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon+\delta)
. Therefore
o(\sqrt{\varepsilon}+\sqrt{\delta})
can be replaced by
\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon+\delta)
and (
2.11
) follows.

Our choice of
V^{\pm}
takes the following form:

\displaystyle V^{\pm}

\displaystyle=v^{(0)}+\sqrt{\varepsilon}v^{(1,0)}+\sqrt{\delta}v^{(0,1)}+\varepsilon w^{(2,0)}+\varepsilon^{3/2}w^{(3,0)}+\varepsilon\sqrt{\delta}w^{(2,1)}

(2.21)

\displaystyle\quad\pm(2T-t)(\varepsilon N_{A}+\delta N_{B}+\sqrt{\varepsilon\delta}N_{C})\pm\varepsilon^{2}F\pm\varepsilon^{3/2}\sqrt{\delta}H\pm\varepsilon\delta G,

(2.22)

where
(N_{A},N_{B},N_{C})
are functions of
(t,x,z)
, and
(F,G,H)
are functions of
(t,x,y,z)
. The intuition for such form is the following: (a) functions
w^{(2,0)},w^{(3,0)},w^{(2,1)}
are added to eliminate terms of
\mathcal{O}(1),\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{\varepsilon})
and
\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{\delta})
when applying the operator
Q^{\pi}
to
V^{\pm}
; (b)
(N_{A},N_{B},N_{C})
and
(F,G,H)
helps to fulfill
(R2)
and
(R4)
; (c) the coefficient
2T-t
is for
(R1)
and
(R3)
. In Section
3
, we shall show how these functions are determined and why they can be chosen as functions of particular variables such that the requirements
(R1)
–
(R5)
are satisfied. In the rest of this section, we briefly review the existing derivations of
v^{(0)},v^{(1,0)},v^{(0,1)}
, the definition of
w^{(2,0)},w^{(3,0)},w^{(2,1)}
, the standing assumptions in this paper, and some preliminary estimates.

2.2
Multiscale asymptotic expansions

Generally, closed-form solutions are barely available for HJB equations. In our setup, we do not even know if
V^{\varepsilon,\delta}
solves (
2.7
) in the viscosity sense.
In
[
9
]
, a first-order expansion of
V^{\varepsilon,\delta}
around small
(\varepsilon,\delta)
is formally derived via singular and regular perturbation techniques. Since the formulas of these terms and the equations they satisfy play an important role in proving our main theorem, we summarize them below for readers’ convenience. For detailed derivations, we refer the readers to
[
9
, Section 4]
and
[
6
, Sections 2 and 3]
.

The combined expansion in slow and fast scales of
V^{\varepsilon,\delta}
is of the following form:

V^{\varepsilon,\delta}=v^{(0)}+\sqrt{\varepsilon}v^{(1,0)}+\sqrt{\delta}v^{(0,1)}+\varepsilon v^{(2,0)}+\delta v^{(0,2)}+\sqrt{\varepsilon\delta}v^{(1,1)}+\cdots,

(2.23)

where the superscript of
v
corresponds to the powers in
\sqrt{\varepsilon}
and
\sqrt{\delta}
and where
v^{(0,0)}
has been rewritten as
v^{(0)}
. To precisely give the equations which identify these terms, we introduce the following notations, following
[
9
]
. Denote by
\left\langle\cdot\right\rangle
the average with respect to the
\varepsilon
-independent invariant distribution
\Phi
of
Y
:
\left\langle g\right\rangle=\int g(y)\,\Phi(\mathrm{d}y)
, and by
M(t,x;\lambda)
the solution to the classical Merton PDE where
\mu
and
\sigma
are constants:

M_{t}-\frac{1}{2}\lambda^{2}\frac{M_{x}^{2}}{M_{xx}}=0,\quad M(T,x;\lambda)=U(x),\quad\lambda=\mu/\sigma\quad\mbox{(Sharpe ratio)}.

(2.24)

We define the associate risk-tolerance function

R(t,x;\lambda):=-\frac{M_{x}(t,x;\lambda)}{M_{xx}(t,x;\lambda)},

and the differential operators:

D_{k}(\lambda)=R(t,x;\lambda)^{k}\partial_{x}^{k},\quad k=1,2,\cdots;\quad\mathcal{L}_{t,x}(\lambda)=\partial_{t}+\frac{1}{2}\lambda^{2}D_{2}(\lambda)+\lambda^{2}D_{1}(\lambda).

(2.25)

We denote the “square-averaged” Sharpe ratio
\overline{\lambda}(z)=\sqrt{\left\langle\lambda^{2}(\cdot,z)\right\rangle}
, and the version of
D_{k}(\lambda)
that will be used in the sequel is
D_{k}(\overline{\lambda})=R(t,x;\overline{\lambda}(z))^{k}\partial_{x}^{k}
. We shall use
D_{k}
for brevity (omitting the argument
\overline{\lambda}
).
We also define the averaged Sharpe ratio:

\widehat{\lambda}(z)=\left\langle\lambda(\cdot,z)\right\rangle
.
Now we are ready to present the formulations of
v^{(0)}
,
v^{(1,0)}
and
v^{(0,1)}
.

Proposition 2.2
.

(
[
9
, Section 4]
and
[
6
, Sections 2 & 3]
)

(i)

The
leading order term

v^{(0)}
is defined as the classical solution to the Merton PDE

v^{(0)}_{t}-\frac{1}{2}\overline{\lambda}^{2}(z)\frac{\left(v^{(0)}_{x}\right)^{2}}{v^{(0)}_{xx}}=0,\quad v^{(0)}(T,x,z)=U(x).

(2.26)

Since it possesses a unique solution (see
[
6
, Proposition 2.2]
), we have

v^{(0)}(t,x,z)=M(t,x;\overline{\lambda}(z)).

(2.27)

(ii)

The
first order correction
in the fast variable
v^{(1,0)}
is defined as the solution to the linear PDE:

\mathcal{L}_{t,x}(\overline{\lambda}(z))v^{(1,0)}=\frac{1}{2}\rho_{1}B(z)D_{1}^{2}v^{(0)},\qquad v^{(1,0)}(T,x,z)=0,

(2.28)

where
B(z)=\left\langle\lambda(\cdot,z)a(\cdot)\partial_{y}\theta(\cdot,z)\right\rangle
, and
\theta
is a solution of the Poisson equation

\mathcal{L}_{y}\theta(y,z)=\lambda^{2}(y,z)-\overline{\lambda}^{2}(z).

(2.29)

It is explicitly given in terms of
v^{(0)}
by

v^{(1,0)}(t,x,z)=-\frac{1}{2}(T-t)\rho_{1}B(z)D_{1}^{2}v^{(0)}(t,x,z).

(2.30)

(iii)

The
first order correction
in the slow variable
v^{(0,1)}
is defined as the solution to the linear PDE:

\mathcal{L}_{t,x}(\overline{\lambda}(z))v^{(0,1)}=\rho_{2}\widehat{\lambda}(z)g(z)\frac{v^{(0)}_{x}}{v^{(0)}_{xx}}v^{(0)}_{xz},\qquad v^{(0,1)}(T,x,z)=0,

(2.31)

and
v^{(0,1)}
can be expressed in terms of
v^{(0)}
by

\displaystyle v^{(0,1)}(t,x,z)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}(T-t)\rho_{2}\widehat{\lambda}(z)g(z)D_{1}v^{(0)}_{z}(t,x,z)

(2.32)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}(T-t)^{2}\rho_{2}\widehat{\lambda}(z)\overline{\lambda}(z)\overline{\lambda}^{\prime}(z)g(z)D_{1}^{2}v^{(0)}(t,x,z).

(2.33)

(iv)

The
z
-derivatives of the leading order term
v^{(0)}
and the risk-tolerance function
R
satisfy

\displaystyle v^{(0)}_{z}(t,x,z)

\displaystyle=(T-t)\overline{\lambda}(z)\overline{\lambda}^{\prime}(z)D_{1}v^{(0)}(t,x,z),

(2.34)

\displaystyle R_{z}(t,x;\overline{\lambda}(z))

\displaystyle=(T-t)\overline{\lambda}(z)\overline{\lambda}^{\prime}(z)R^{2}R_{xx}(t,x;\overline{\lambda}(z)).

(2.35)

(v)

The term
v^{(2,0)}
solves the linear PDE:
\mathcal{L}_{y}v^{(2,0)}+\mathcal{L}_{t,x}(\lambda(y,z))v^{(0)}=0
, and so has the form

v^{(2,0)}(t,x,y,z)=-\frac{1}{2}\theta(y,z)D_{1}v^{(0)}+C_{1}(t,x,z),

(2.36)

where
\theta
solves (
2.29
).

(vi)

The term
v^{(3,0)}
solves the linear PDE:

\mathcal{L}_{y}v^{(3,0)}+\mathcal{L}_{t,x}(\lambda(y,z))v^{(1,0)}=\frac{1}{2}\rho_{1}\lambda(y,z)a(y)\partial_{y}\theta(y)D_{1}^{2}v^{(0)}

and so has the form

v^{(3,0)}(t,x,y,z)=\frac{1}{2}(T-t)\rho_{1}\theta(y,z)B(z)(\frac{1}{2}D_{2}+D_{1})D_{1}^{2}v^{(0)}+\frac{1}{2}\rho_{1}\theta_{1}(y,z)D_{1}^{2}v^{(0)}\\
+C_{2}(t,x,z),

(2.37)

where
\theta_{1}(y,z)
solves the Poisson equation

\mathcal{L}_{y}\theta_{1}(y,z)=\lambda(y,z)a(y)\partial_{y}\theta(y,z)-B(z).

(vii)

The term
v^{(2,1)}
solves the linear PDE:

\mathcal{L}_{y}v^{(2,1)}+\mathcal{L}_{t,x}(\lambda(y,z))v^{(0,1)}+\rho_{2}g(z)\lambda(y,z)D_{1}v^{(0)}_{z}=0.

With (
2.27
) and (
2.33
),
v^{(2,1)}
is given by

\displaystyle v^{(2,1)}(t,x,y,z)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}(T-t)^{2}\theta(y,z)\rho_{2}\widehat{\lambda}(z)\overline{\lambda}(z)\overline{\lambda}^{\prime}(z)g(z)(\frac{1}{2}D_{2}+D_{1})D_{1}^{2}v^{(0)}

(2.38)

\displaystyle\quad-\rho_{2}(T-t)\theta_{2}(y,z)g(z)\overline{\lambda}(z)\overline{\lambda}^{\prime}(z)D_{1}^{2}v^{(0)}+C_{3}(t,x,z),

(2.39)

where
\theta_{2}(y,z)
solves the Poisson equation

\mathcal{L}_{y}\theta_{2}(y,z)=\lambda(y,z)-\widehat{\lambda}(z).

In the sequel, when deriving the concrete formula of
V^{\pm}
, we shall choose
w^{(2,0)}
,
w^{(3,0)}
and
w^{(2,1)}
to be the corresponding terms in the expansion of
V^{\varepsilon,\delta}
with
C_{i}(t,x,z)\equiv 0
,
i=1,2,3
. That is, we choose

\displaystyle w^{(2,0)}(t,x,y,z)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\theta(y,z)D_{1}v^{(0)},

4
Conclusion

This paper provides the accuracy analysis of asymptotics for the portfolio optimization problem with general utility functions and two (fast and slow) stochastic factors. This sets up the theoretical foundation of using asymptotic expansion to derive approximations for value functions and optimal strategies in the regime where these factors are running on both slow and fast timescales. Specifically, we construct the sub- and super-solutions to the fully nonlinear problem so that their difference is at the desired level of accuracy. In the present context, the fast varying factor requires a careful treatment of the singular perturbation for a fully nonlinear equation. Moreover, the proofs presented here can be adapted to justify other derivations of accuracy in various contexts as in
[
7
]
for instance.
