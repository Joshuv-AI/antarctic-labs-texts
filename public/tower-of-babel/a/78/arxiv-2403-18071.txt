From Sontag’s to Cardano-Lyapunov Formula for Systems Not Affine in the Control: Convection-Enabled PDE Stabilization
M. C. Belhadjoudja, M. Krstić, M. Maghenem, E. Witrant

Abstract
We propose the first generalization of Sontag’s universal controller to systems not affine in the control, particularly, to PDEs with boundary actuation. We assume that the system admits a control Lyapunov function (CLF) whose derivative, rather than being affine in the control, has either a depressed cubic, quadratic, or depressed quartic dependence on the control. For each case, a continuous universal controller that vanishes at the origin and achieves global exponential stability is derived. We prove our result in the context of convection-reaction-diffusion PDEs with Dirichlet actuation. We show that if the convection has a certain structure, then the L 2 L^{2} norm of the state is a CLF. In addition to generalizing Sontag’s formula to some non-affine systems, we present the first general Lyapunov approach for boundary control of nonlinear PDEs. We illustrate our results via a numerical example.



I
Introduction and Main Result

I-A

Sontag’s formula

Artstein considers in his 1983 paper
[
1
]
the problem of smooth stabilization of control systems

\displaystyle\dot{u}=f(u,v),

(1)

where, for consistency with the notation used in PDE control,
u
denotes the state,
v
denotes the control input, and
f
a smooth vector field. One of Artstein’s results is that, if the system admits a smooth CLF, then there exists a stabilizing feedback
v(u)
with
v(0)=0
, which is smooth everywhere except possibly at the origin
u=0
. If the system possesses the ‘small control property,’ then continuity at the origin can be guaranteed.

Artstein’s proof is not constructive: the feedback is not designed. For systems affine in the control,

\displaystyle\dot{u}=f(u)+g(u)v,

(2)

where
f
and
g
are smooth, Sontag provides a feedback formula in his 1989 paper
[
2
]
. Among various pedagogical presentations of Sontag’s universal controller, the reader may consult Section 1.3 in
[
3
]
.

Extensions of Sontag’s formula are too numerous to comprehensively survey. For example, in
[
4
]
, a bounded version of Sontag’s controller is proposed under the assumption that the system admits a CLF whose derivative can be made negative by choosing the control input
v
in a bounded set. In
[
5
, equation (2.4)]
, the concept of adaptive CLF is introduced for stabilization of nonlinear systems, linear in the unknown constant parameters, and an “adaptive Sontag’s formula” is given. The extension of Sontag’s formula to systems affine in a deterministic disturbance is given in
[
6
, equation (35)]
. Sontag’s formula for stochastic systems is introduced in
[
7
, equation (5.12)]
. In
[
8
]
, Sontag’s formula is generalized to the case where the input is affected by a disturbance, to guarantee integral-input-to-state stability. Finally, in
[
9
]
, Sontag’s formula is adapted for the problem of event-based stabilization of control-affine systems.

I-B

Going beyond control-affine systems: Framework

None of the results above go beyond the control-affine case. Our first contribution here is to take the construction of a universal feedback controller beyond the control-affine case, motivated by classes of boundary-actuated PDEs. Specifically, the question we ask, a third of a century after
[
2
]
, is: Given a CLF
V
for a control system (not necessarily finite-dimensional), for which
\dot{V}
is not affine in the control
v
, can we construct a ’universal’ feedback controller
v
that vanishes at the origin, is at least ’continuous everywhere’, and stabilizes the existing solutions? In other words, can we extend the universal control concept beyond the control-affine case?

To answer the above question, we consider the class of scalar-valued PDE systems admitting a CLF
V
, whose time derivative has a polynomial structure in the control input
v
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\dot{V}(u)\leq\phi(u)+\gamma(v,u),

(3)

where
\phi
is some operator acting on the state
u
that verifies
\phi(0)=0
and such that
\phi(u)
does not depend explicitly on
v
, and
\gamma(v,u)
is a polynomial in the control input
v
, whose higher-order coefficient is constant, and whose lower-order coefficients may depend on
u
but not on
v
.

The inspiration for this work comes from
[
10
]
,
[
11
]
,
[
12
]
,
[
13
]
, and
[
14
]
. In
[
10
]
, stabilization of the inviscid Burgers’ equation is achieved by exploiting the quadratic convection
-(u^{2})_{x}
, which makes
\dot{V}
cubic in the control, with
V
being the
L^{2}
norm of the state. In
[
11
]
,
[
12
]
, and
[
13
]
, it is shown that the
L^{2}
norm of the state of the Kuramoto-Sivashinsky is a CLF, because of the convection
-(u^{2})_{x}
. Stabilization is then achieved. In
[
14
]
, the authors consider a reaction-diffusion PDE, observe that a spatially weighted
L^{2}
norm of the state is quadratic in the control, thanks to the diffusion term, and propose a stabilizing feedback, which is a root of a quadratic equation.

We consider the following structures for
\gamma
:

\displaystyle\gamma(v,u):=\pm v^{3}+\beta(u)v\quad

(depressed cubic),

(4)

\displaystyle\gamma(v,u):=-v^{2}+\beta(u)v\quad

(quadratic),

(5)

\displaystyle\gamma(v,u):=-v^{4}+\beta(u)v\quad

(depressed quartic),

(6)

where
\beta
is some continuous operator acting on
u
that verifies
\beta(0)=0
. For each structure on
\gamma(v,u)
, we construct a universal continuous feedback

\displaystyle v\left(V(u),\beta(u),\phi(u)\right)

(7)

that verifies
v(0)=0
and, under which, the regular closed-loop solutions verify

\dot{V}(u)\leq-\alpha(V(u))\,,\quad\alpha\in{\cal K}\,,

(8)

where
\alpha\in\mathcal{K}
if
\alpha
is continuous, strictly increasing and
\alpha(0)=0
. For example, one can choose
\alpha(V):=V
to enforce exponential stability. The controller (
7

\phi(u)
and
\beta(u)
, which are general/universal. The structure of the controller depends only on the structure of the actuated part of the CLF, i.e.
\gamma(v,u)
.

The universal feedback (
7
), when
\gamma(v,u)
has the cubic structure (
4
); namely, when

\dot{V}\leq\phi+\beta v\pm v^{3}\,,

(9)

is given by what we suggest to be called the
Cardano-Lyapunov formula

\displaystyle\framebox{$\displaystyle v:=\sqrt[3]{-\frac{q}{2}+\sqrt{\frac{q^{2}}{4}\pm\frac{\beta^{3}}{27}}}+\sqrt[3]{-\frac{q}{2}-\sqrt{\frac{q^{2}}{4}\pm\frac{\beta^{3}}{27}}}$}

(10)

where

\displaystyle\framebox{$\displaystyle q:=~\pm\left[|\phi(u)|+\frac{2\sqrt{3}}{9}|\beta(u)|^{\frac{3}{2}}+\alpha(V(u))\right]$}

(11)

Note that, unlike the classical control-affine case,
\dot{V}=L_{f}V+L_{g}Vv
, where one has to assume that
L_{g}V=0\Rightarrow L_{f}V<0
, in (
9
) the cubic term
\pm v^{3}
removes the need for any assumptions on
\phi
and
\beta
—namely,
V
is a CLF by virtue of (

Going beyond control-affine systems: Motivation

We reveal that the

CRD PDEs are difficult to control because of the reaction, which is the main source of instability. An example of a CRD PDE that is difficult to stabilize is

\displaystyle u_{t}=\epsilon u_{xx}-(u^{2})_{x}+\lambda u^{3}

I-D

Main result

For each case of the convection term
C(u)_{x}
, we design a feedback controller that achieves stabilization in the sense of inequality (
8
). Note that our approach is not limited to the CRD PDEs. It can be easily adapted to any control system that admits a CLF whose derivative verifies (

), under one of the structures (

), (

), or (

).

Theorem 1

Consider the control system (
12
), (
13
), (
14
), along with
V
defined in (
18
). For each of the convection terms in (
15
), (
16
), and (
17
), there exists a continuous feedback

\displaystyle v\left(V,u_{x}(0),\int_{0}^{1}\left(u(x)R(u(x))-\epsilon u_{x}(x)^{2}\right)dx\right),

(20)

with the property
v(0)=0
and guaranteeing that (
8
) holds for the closed-loop regular solutions.

\square

The rest of the paper is devoted to the proof of Theorem
1
, i.e. to the construction of the feedback (

). The universal controllers that we construct in this paper are remarkably simple in comparison with nonlinear backstepping controllers, as they are constructed using only finite number of additions, multiplications, and extraction of

roots. Hence, our result not only generalizes Sontag formula to non-affine cases, but also introduces a new method for boundary control of PDEs, distinct from PDE backstepping. We just need the PDE to possess a
helpful convective structure
.

II
Construction of the Universal Controllers

In this section, we provide a constructive proof of Theorem
1
, i.e. we design the feedback (
20
). The proof follows in two steps. First, we differentiate the Lyapunov function candidate
V
along the regular solutions of (
12
), (
13
), (
14
). We obtain, on
\dot{V}
, a structure that is not affine in the control, in the form of (
3
). Then, we consider the cases of flow convection
\pm(u^{2})_{x}
, counter-convection
u_{x}
, and Buckmaster convection
(u^{3})_{x}
. We show that the flow convection leads to a depressed cubic structure on the control input
v
, the counter-convection leads to a quadratic structure, and that the Buckmaster convection leads to a depressed quartic structure. In each case, we construct the corresponding universal controller.

II-A

Upperbounding
\dot{V}

By differentiating
V
along the regular solutions of (
12
), (
13
), (
14
), we find

\displaystyle\dot{V}=

\displaystyle~\int_{0}^{1}u(x)C(u(x))_{x}dx+\int_{0}^{1}u(x)R(u(x))dx

\displaystyle~+\epsilon\int_{0}^{1}u(x)u_{xx}(x)dx.

(21)

Using integration by parts and the boundary conditions (
13
), (
14
), we obtain

\displaystyle\dot{V}=-vC(v)-\int_{0}^{1}u_{x}(x)C(u(x))dx-\epsilon vu_{x}(0)

\displaystyle~-\epsilon\int_{0}^{1}u_{x}(x)^{2}dx+\int_{0}^{1}u(x)R(u(x))dx.

(22)

Next, we perform the change of variable
s:=u
, leading to

. Equation (

\displaystyle\dot{V}=

\displaystyle~-vC(v)+\int_{0}^{v}C(s)ds-\epsilon\int_{0}^{1}u_{x}(x)^{2}dx

\displaystyle~+\int_{0}^{1}u(x)R(u(x))dx-\epsilon vu_{x}(0).

(23)

II-B

Flow Convection

We distinguish between two cases. Either
C(u):=u^{2}
, or
C(u):=-u^{2}
. Let us start with a
C(u):=u^{2}
. Note that

\displaystyle-vC(v)+\int_{0}^{v}C(s)ds=

\displaystyle~-\frac{2}{3}v^{3}.

(24)

As a consequence, equation (
23
) becomes

\displaystyle\dot{V}=

\displaystyle~\int_{0}^{1}u(x)R(u(x))dx-\epsilon\int_{0}^{1}u_{x}(x)^{2}dx

\displaystyle~-\frac{2}{3}v^{3}-\epsilon vu_{x}(0).

(25)

The function
\dot{V}
has a depressed cubic structure on the control input
v
. The first idea to enforce (
8
) is, therefore, to set
v
as a real root of the cubic equation

\displaystyle 0=v^{3}+\frac{3\epsilon}{2}vu_{x}(0)

\displaystyle~-\frac{3}{2}\left(\alpha(V)+\int_{0}^{1}\left(u(x)R(u(x))-\epsilon u_{x}(x)^{2}\right)dx\right).

(26)

Selecting arbitrarily one real root of (
26

. This being said, we propose to consider instead the cubic equation

\displaystyle 0=

\displaystyle~v^{3}+\frac{3\epsilon}{2}u_{x}(0)v-\frac{3}{2}\bigg(\alpha(V)+\frac{\sqrt{2\epsilon^{3}}}{3}|u_{x}(0)|^{\frac{3}{2}}

\displaystyle~+\bigg|\int_{0}^{1}\left(u(x)R(u(x))-\epsilon u_{x}(x)^{2}\right)dx\bigg|\bigg).

(27)

If

\displaystyle\dot{V}\leq

\displaystyle~-\alpha(V)-\frac{\sqrt{2\epsilon^{3}}}{3}|u_{x}(0)|^{\frac{3}{2}}

\displaystyle~+\int_{0}^{1}\left(u(x)R(u(x))-\epsilon u_{x}(x)^{2}\right)dx

\displaystyle~-\bigg|\int_{0}^{1}\left(u(x)R(u(x))-\epsilon u_{x}(x)^{2}\right)dx\bigg|,

which implies (
8
). It remains to choose the real root

of (

) such that it is continuous with respect to the coefficients of (
27

) always admits a real root, which is given by Cardano formula, which is a continuous expression relating the coefficients of (
27
). This is due to the fact that the discriminant of (
27

) is
\Delta:=q^{2}/4+\beta^{3}/27
, where
\beta
and

are given by

\displaystyle\beta:=

\displaystyle~\frac{3\epsilon}{2}u_{x}(0),

(29)

\displaystyle q:=

\displaystyle~-\frac{3}{2}\bigg(\alpha(V)+\bigg|\int_{0}^{1}\left(uR(u)-\epsilon u_{x}^{2}\right)dx\bigg|\bigg)

\displaystyle~-\frac{\sqrt{2\epsilon^{3}}}{2}|u_{x}(0)|^{\frac{3}{2}}.

(30)

Note that

\displaystyle q=

\displaystyle~-\frac{3}{2}\bigg(\alpha(V)+\bigg|\int_{0}^{1}\left(uR(u)-\epsilon u_{x}^{2}\right)dx\bigg|\bigg)

\displaystyle~-\frac{2\sqrt{3}}{9}|\beta|^{\frac{3}{2}}.

(31)

A direct computation gives us

\displaystyle\Delta=

\displaystyle~\frac{1}{4}\bigg(\frac{9}{4}\bigg(\alpha(V)+\bigg|\int_{0}^{1}\left(uR(u)-\epsilon u_{x}^{2}\right)dx\bigg|\bigg)^{2}

\displaystyle~+\frac{\sqrt{3}}{3}\bigg(\alpha(V)+\bigg|\int_{0}^{1}\left(uR(u)-\epsilon u_{x}^{2}\right)dx\bigg|\bigg)

\displaystyle~|\beta|^{\frac{3}{2}}+\frac{4}{27}|\beta|^{3}\bigg)+\frac{\beta^{3}}{27}\geq\frac{1}{27}(|\beta|^{3}+\beta^{3})\geq 0.

(32)

We, therefore, propose to set the control input

as

\displaystyle v:=\sqrt[3]{-\frac{q}{2}+\sqrt{\frac{q^{2}}{4}+\frac{\beta^{3}}{27}}}+\sqrt[3]{-\frac{q}{2}-\sqrt{\frac{q^{2}}{4}+\frac{\beta^{3}}{27}}}.

(33)

This control input vanishes if the state vanishes. Indeed, if
u=0
, then
\beta=q=0
, which implies that

.

Considering now a negative convection, we obtain

\displaystyle-vC(v)+\int_{0}^{v}C(s)ds=

\displaystyle~\frac{2}{3}v^{3}.

(34)

As a consequence, equation (
23
) becomes

\displaystyle\dot{V}=

\displaystyle~\int_{0}^{1}u(x)R(u(x))dx-\epsilon\int_{0}^{1}u_{x}(x)^{2}dx

\displaystyle~+\frac{2}{3}v^{3}-\epsilon vu_{x}(0).

(35)

To enforce inequality (
8
), we consider the cubic equation

\displaystyle 0=

\displaystyle~v^{3}-\frac{3}{2}\epsilon u_{x}(0)v+\frac{3}{2}\bigg(\alpha(V)+\frac{\sqrt{2\epsilon^{3}}}{3}|u_{x}(0)|^{\frac{3}{2}}

\displaystyle~+\bigg|\int_{0}^{1}\left(u(x)R(u(x))-\epsilon u_{x}(x)^{2}\right)dx\bigg|\bigg).

(36)

The discriminant of (

) is
\Delta:=q^{2}/4+\beta^{3}/27
with
\beta
and
q
defined as

\displaystyle\beta:=

\displaystyle~-\frac{3\epsilon}{2}u_{x}(0),

(37)

\displaystyle q:=

\displaystyle~\frac{3}{2}\bigg(\alpha(V)+\bigg|\int_{0}^{1}\left(uR(u)-\epsilon u_{x}^{2}\right)dx\bigg|\bigg)

\displaystyle~+\frac{\sqrt{2\epsilon^{3}}}{2}|u_{x}(0)|^{\frac{3}{2}}.

(38)

Since

, then we propose to set the control input

as in (

II-C

Counter-convection

In this case, we have

\displaystyle-vC(v)+\int_{0}^{v}C(s)ds=

\displaystyle~-\frac{1}{2}v^{2}.

(39)

As a result, equation (
23
) becomes

\displaystyle\dot{V}=

\displaystyle~\int_{0}^{1}u(x)R(u(x))dx-\epsilon\int_{0}^{1}u_{x}(x)^{2}dx

\displaystyle~-\frac{1}{2}v^{2}-\epsilon vu_{x}(0).

(40)

The function
\dot{V}
has a quadratic structure in

. The first idea to enforce (

), is to set

as a real root of the quadratic equation

\displaystyle 0=

\displaystyle~\int_{0}^{1}\left(u(x)R(u(x))-\epsilon u_{x}(x)^{2}\right)dx

\displaystyle~-\frac{1}{2}v^{2}-\epsilon u_{x}(0)v+\alpha(V).

However, there is no guarantee that (

\displaystyle 0=

\displaystyle~\bigg|\int_{0}^{1}\left(u(x)R(u(x))-\epsilon u_{x}(x)^{2}\right)dx\bigg|

\displaystyle~-\frac{1}{2}v^{2}-\epsilon u_{x}(0)v+\alpha(V).

If

is a real root of (

), then (

) is verified. The polynomial equation (

) admits either one double real root or two distinct real roots. This is due to the fact that its discriminant is nonnegative. Indeed, the discriminant of (

\Delta:=b^{2}-4ac
, where
a
,
b
and
c
are given by

\displaystyle a:=-\frac{1}{2},\quad b:=-\epsilon u_{x}(0),

(43)

\displaystyle c:=\alpha(V)+\bigg|\int_{0}^{1}\left(uR(u)-\epsilon u_{x}^{2}\right)dx\bigg|.

(44)

Hence,

. We set then the control input

as

\displaystyle v:=b\pm\sqrt{b^{2}+2c}.

(45)

If

.

II-D

Buckmaster Convection

The convection

is characteristic of the Buckmaster equation

. We have

\displaystyle-vC(v)+\int_{0}^{v}C(s)ds=

\displaystyle~-\frac{3}{4}v^{4}.

As a consequence, equation (

) becomes

\displaystyle\dot{V}=

\displaystyle~\int_{0}^{1}u(x)R(u(x))dx-\epsilon\int_{0}^{1}u_{x}(x)^{2}dx

\displaystyle~-\frac{3}{4}v^{4}-\epsilon vu_{x}(0).

The function

\displaystyle-\epsilon vu_{x}(0)\leq

\displaystyle~\epsilon|v|\sqrt{|u_{x}(0)|}\sqrt{|u_{x}(0|}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle~\frac{1}{2}|u_{x}(0)|v^{2}+\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{2}|u_{x}(0)|.

Equation (

\displaystyle\dot{V}\leq

\displaystyle~\int_{0}^{1}u(x)R(u(x))dx-\epsilon\int_{0}^{1}u_{x}(x)^{2}dx

\displaystyle~-\frac{3}{4}v^{4}+\frac{1}{2}|u_{x}(0)|v^{2}+\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{2}|u_{x}(0)|.

The function
\dot{V}
has a biquadratic structure in

\displaystyle~\int_{0}^{1}u(x)R(u(x))dx-\epsilon\int_{0}^{1}u_{x}(x)^{2}dx

To enforce inequality (

), we consider the quadratic equation

\displaystyle 0=

\displaystyle~\bigg|\int_{0}^{1}\left(u(x)R(u(x))-\epsilon u_{x}(x)^{2}\right)dx\bigg|

\displaystyle~-\frac{3}{4}\tilde{v}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}|u_{x}(0)|\tilde{v}+\alpha(V)+\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{2}|u_{x}(0)|.

The discriminant of (

\Delta:=b^{2}-4ac
, with

,

and

\displaystyle a:=

\displaystyle~-\frac{3}{4},\quad b:=\frac{1}{2}|u_{x}(0)|,

\displaystyle c:=

\displaystyle~\bigg|\int_{0}^{1}\left(u(x)R(u(x))-\epsilon u_{x}(x)^{2}\right)dx\bigg|

\displaystyle~+\alpha(V)+\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{2}|u_{x}(0)|.

(53)

Since the discriminant is nonnegative, we set the control input

as

\displaystyle v:=\pm\sqrt{\frac{2}{3}\left(b+\sqrt{b^{2}+3c}\right)}.

If

, then

Remark 2

Well-posedness of infinite dimensional systems such as (
12
) is far from being trivial, especially when the boundary controller is non-smooth. Non-smooth (yet continuous) feedback control of PDEs is not new. It first appears in
[
10
]
, where a non-smooth boundary controller is designed to stabilize an inviscid Burgers’ equation. However, since this first paper, except for very few works such as
[
14
]
, non-smooth control of PDEs has not been studied. The principal reason for such poor exploration is that it is difficult to prove closed-loop well-posedness via the standard functional analysis techniques used for more regular boundary controllers. This being said, well-posedness of infinite-dimensional systems subject to non-smooth (yet continuous) boundary feedback controllers is a fundamental mathematical question that has not been solved yet, but that should not hold us back from exploring what kind of stability results one can obtain using less regular controllers.

IV
Conclusion

We proposed in this work the first generalization of Sontag’s formula to systems that admit a CLF whose derivative is not affine in the control input, but polynomial. Depending on the structure of the resulting polynomial, we designed continuous universal controllers that vanish at the origin and achieve stabilization. We proved our results in the context of boundary control of convection-reaction-diffusion PDEs. Finally,
our results have been illustrated on a numerical example where the PDE to control possesses a superlinear reaction term that yields finite-time blow-up phenomena. In future work, we would like to guarantee in addition to continuity boundedness of the boundary control input in the context of CRD PDEs. We would also like to see if it is possible to design universal controllers for other structures of the derivative of the CLF, and for more general convective terms, such as a combination of flow convection and counter-convection. It would be also interesting to generalize our approach to higher-order PDEs, such as the Kuramoto-Sivashinsky and the Korteweg-de Vries equations.
