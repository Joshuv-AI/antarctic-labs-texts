Quasioptical modeling of wave beams with and without mode conversion: IV. Numerical simulations of waves in dissipative media
K. Yanagihara, I. Y. Dodin, S. Kubo

Abstract
We report the first quasioptical simulations of wave beams in a hot plasma using the quasioptical code PARADE (PAraxial RAy DEscription) [Phys. Plasmas 26 , 072112 (2019)]. This code is unique in that it accounts for inhomogeneity of the dissipation-rate across the beam and mode conversion simultaneously. We show that the dissipation-rate inhomogeneity shifts beams relative to their trajectories in cold plasma and that the two electromagnetic modes are coupled via this process, an effect that was ignored in the past. We also propose a simplified approach to accounting for the dissipation-rate inhomogeneity. This approach is computationally inexpensive and simplifies analysis of actual experiments.



I
Introduction

Modeling of radiofrequency waves in fusion plasmas requires accurate calculations of their power deposition through various resonant mechanisms. For electron-cyclotron (EC)
ref:bornatici83
;
ref:erckmann94
and lower-hybrid
ref:bonoli84
waves, which have short enough wavelengths, this is commonly done using geometrical-optics ray tracing
ref:bernstein75
;
ref:friedland80
. However, this method ignores a number of important effects, including diffraction, so the deposition profiles are often predicted to be more peaked than they are in reality
ref:poli01a
.
To deal with this problem, a number of quasioptical models have been proposed
ref:poli01a
;
ref:poli01b
;
ref:poli18
;
ref:pereverzev98
;
ref:mazzucato89
;
ref:nowak93
;
ref:peeters96
;
ref:farina07
;
ref:balakin07a
;
ref:balakin07b
;
ref:balakin08a
;
ref:balakin08b
. However, most of these models
ref:poli01a
;
ref:poli01b
;
ref:poli18
;
ref:pereverzev98
;
ref:mazzucato89
;
ref:nowak93
;
ref:peeters96
;
ref:farina07
assume that wave beams maintain a particular (Gaussian) transverse profile, and they also ignore variations of the dissipation-rate within the beam cross section.
One exception to this is the model by Balakin
et al.

ref:balakin07a
;
ref:balakin07b
;
ref:balakin08a
;
ref:balakin08b
; still, this model assumes single-mode beams and thus cannot describe mode conversion, which is often important
ref:dodin17
;
ref:tsujimura15
;
ref:kubo15
. To fill the gap, a more comprehensive quasioptical theory and the corresponding code PARADE (PAraxial RAy DEscription) have been developed recently
ref:pop1
;
ref:pop2
;
ref:pop3
, and its preliminary applications have already been reported
ref:itc27
.

Here, we report the first applications of PARADE to modeling quasioptical wave beams with mode conversion in hot plasma. Our findings indicate that conventional simulations overlook important effects connected with: (i) the inhomogeneity of the dissipation-rate within the beam cross section and (ii) the O–X mode conversion. The simulations reported here are the first ones that account for these effects simultaneously. We find that the dissipation-rate inhomogeneity shifts wave beams relative to their trajectories in cold plasma. PARADE also predicts that the two plasma modes are coupled via this process, an effect that was ignored in the past. We also propose a simplified approach to accounting for the dissipation-rate inhomogeneity to speed-up the calculations in a practical fusion plasma geometry.

Our paper is organized as follows. In Sec.
II
, we briefly overview the theoretical model underlying PARADE. In Sec.
III
, we introduce the two dissipation models that we use, and we also report the coupling between the X and O modes caused by the dissipation. In Sec.
IV
, we summarize our main results.

II
Theoretical model

II.1
Basic equations

To describe the theory that underlies PARADE, let us start with the equation for the electric field
\boldsymbol{\rm E}
of a linear wave governed by a general dispersion operator
\smash{\widehat{\boldsymbol{\rm D}}}
:

\displaystyle\smash{\widehat{\boldsymbol{\rm D}}}\boldsymbol{\rm E}=0.

(1)

We assume that the field is stationary, with constant frequency
\omega
, and has an eikonal form
\boldsymbol{\rm E}=e^{-i\omega t+i\theta(\boldsymbol{\rm x})}\boldsymbol{\rm\psi}(\boldsymbol{\rm x})
. (The time dependence is henceforth omitted for brevity.) Here, the scalar function
\theta
is a rapidly varying “reference phase”,
\boldsymbol{\rm k}\doteq\nabla\theta
is the local wave vector (the symbol
\doteq
denotes definitions), and the complex vector
\boldsymbol{\rm\psi}
is a slowly varying envelope. We also introduce the following small parameters:

\displaystyle\epsilon_{\parallel}\doteq\lambda/L_{\parallel},\quad\epsilon_{\perp}\doteq\lambda/L_{\perp},\quad\epsilon_{\parallel}\sim\epsilon_{\perp}^{2}\ll 1,

(2)

where
\lambda\doteq 2\pi/k
is the wavelength,
L_{\parallel}
is the characteristic scale of the beam field along the group velocity at the beam center, and
L_{\perp}
is the minimum scale of the field in the plane transverse to the group velocity. The medium-inhomogeneity scale is assumed to be of the same order as
L_{\parallel}
or larger. Under these assumptions, Eq. (
1
) can be expressed as

\displaystyle\mathsf{D}\boldsymbol{\rm\psi}+\smash{\widehat{\boldsymbol{\rm\mathcal{L}}}}\boldsymbol{\rm\psi}=0,

(3)

where the operator
\smash{\widehat{\boldsymbol{\rm\mathcal{L}}}}=\mathcal{O}(\epsilon_{\perp})
is specified in Ref.
ref:pop1
(also see below), and the matrix
\mathsf{D}
is found from the Weyl symbol of
\smash{\widehat{\boldsymbol{\rm D}}}
, or the local dispersion matrix
\boldsymbol{\rm D}

ref:pop1
, that satisfies the ordering

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\rm D}_{H}=\mathcal{O}(1),\quad\boldsymbol{\rm D}_{A}\leq\mathcal{O}(\epsilon_{\perp}).

(4)

The indices
H
and
A
denote the Hermitian part and the anti-Hermitian part, respectively. For our purposes, it is sufficient to adopt
ref:pop1

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\rm D}_{H}(\boldsymbol{\rm x},\boldsymbol{\rm p})=\frac{c^{2}}{16\pi\omega^{2}}\,[\boldsymbol{\rm p}\boldsymbol{\rm p}-(\boldsymbol{\rm p}\cdot\boldsymbol{\rm p})\mathbb{1}]+\frac{1}{16\pi}\,\boldsymbol{\rm\varepsilon}_{H}(\boldsymbol{\rm x},\boldsymbol{\rm p}),

(5)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\rm D}_{A}(\boldsymbol{\rm x},\boldsymbol{\rm p})=\frac{1}{16\pi}\,\boldsymbol{\rm\varepsilon}_{A}(\boldsymbol{\rm x},\boldsymbol{\rm p}),

(6)

where
\mathbb{1}
is a unit matrix and
\boldsymbol{\rm\varepsilon}
is the dielectric tensor found, for example, in Ref.
book:stix
; its dependence on
\omega
is assumed but not emphasized, since
\omega
is constant. (Here,
\boldsymbol{\rm p}
denotes any given wave vector, as opposed to
\boldsymbol{\rm k}
, which is the specific wave vector determined by
\theta
; see above.)

II.2
Polarization vectors and matrices

Since
\boldsymbol{\rm D}_{H}
is assumed as the dominant part of the dispersion operator in Eq. (
3
), it is convenient to decompose the envelope
\boldsymbol{\rm\psi}
in the basis
\{\boldsymbol{\rm\eta}_{s}\}
of the orthogonal eigenvectors of
\boldsymbol{\rm D}_{H}
, i.e.,
\boldsymbol{\rm D}_{H}\boldsymbol{\rm\eta}_{s}=\Lambda_{s}\boldsymbol{\rm\eta}_{s}
. This decomposition can be written as follows:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\rm\psi}=\boldsymbol{\rm\eta}_{\rm o}a^{\rm o}+\boldsymbol{\rm\eta}_{\rm x}a^{\rm x}+\bar{\boldsymbol{\rm\eta}}\bar{a},

(7)

where
a^{\rm o}
,
a^{\rm x}
, and
\bar{a}
are complex coefficients,
\boldsymbol{\rm\eta}_{\rm o}
and
\boldsymbol{\rm\eta}_{\rm x}
are the polarization vectors of the O- and X-mode in homogeneous plasma, and
\bar{\boldsymbol{\rm\eta}}
is the third eigenvector of
\boldsymbol{\rm D}
that is orthogonal to both of them.

In general, the O and X modes are coupled, which means that both
\Lambda_{\rm o}
and
\Lambda_{\rm x}
are close to zero simultaneously and

\displaystyle a^{\rm o}=\mathcal{O}(1),\quad a^{\rm x}=\mathcal{O}(1),\quad\bar{a}=\mathcal{O}(\epsilon_{\perp}).

(8)

The small amplitude
\bar{a}
can be calculated perturbatively and does not enter quasioptical equations explicitly. Instead, we work with a two-dimensional amplitude vector

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\rm a}=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}a^{\rm o}\\
a^{\rm x}\end{array}\right)

and the
3\times 2
“polarization matrix”
\boldsymbol{\rm\Xi}
that contains the vectors
\boldsymbol{\rm\eta}_{\rm o}
and
\boldsymbol{\rm\eta}_{\rm x}
as its columns,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\rm\Xi}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\boldsymbol{\rm\eta}_{\rm o}&\boldsymbol{\rm\eta}_{\rm x}\end{array}\right).

Then,
\boldsymbol{\rm\psi}
can be expressed as follows:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\rm\psi}=\boldsymbol{\rm\Xi}\boldsymbol{\rm a}+\mathcal{O}(\epsilon_{\perp}).

(12)

Since we consider the beam dynamics in coordinates that are close to Euclidean, the dual-basis vectors can be adopted in the form
\boldsymbol{\rm\eta}^{\rm o}\approx\boldsymbol{\rm\eta}_{\rm o}
and
\boldsymbol{\rm\eta}^{\rm x}\approx\boldsymbol{\rm\eta}_{\rm x}
, and we also introduce a
2\times 3
matrix

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\rm\Xi}^{+}=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\boldsymbol{\rm\eta}^{{\rm o}*}\\
\boldsymbol{\rm\eta}^{{\rm x}*}\end{array}\right).

[For more general definitions, see Ref.
ref:pop1
.] As seen easily, this matrix satisfies
\boldsymbol{\rm\Xi}^{+}\boldsymbol{\rm\Xi}=\mathbb{1}
, and

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\rm\Lambda}\doteq\boldsymbol{\rm\Xi}^{+}\boldsymbol{\rm D}_{H}\boldsymbol{\rm\Xi}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\Lambda_{\rm o}&0\\
0&\Lambda_{\rm x}\end{array}\right).

In the single-mode case, also considered in Refs.
ref:balakin07a
;
ref:balakin07b
;
ref:balakin08a
;
ref:balakin08b
, the above equations are simplified. For example, assume that a wave consists mainly of the O mode. (The X-mode case is treated similarly.) Then,

\displaystyle a^{\rm o}=\mathcal{O}(1),\quad a^{\rm x}=\mathcal{O}(\epsilon_{\perp}),\quad\bar{a}=\mathcal{O}(\epsilon_{\perp}),

(17)

and the polarization matrix becomes
3\times 1
dimensional,
\boldsymbol{\rm\Xi}=\boldsymbol{\rm\eta}_{\rm o}
, so it is just the O-mode polarization vector. Accordingly,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\rm\psi}=\boldsymbol{\rm\Xi}a^{\rm o}+\mathcal{O}(\epsilon_{\perp}),

(18)

where the correction
\mathcal{O}(\epsilon_{\perp})
can be found perturbatively but if needed but otherwise is inessential. Similarly,
\boldsymbol{\rm\Xi}^{+}
is a row vector in this case, namely,
\boldsymbol{\rm\Xi}^{+}=\boldsymbol{\rm\eta}^{\rm o}
. Accordingly,
\boldsymbol{\rm\Xi}^{+}\boldsymbol{\rm\Xi}=1
and
\Lambda_{\rm o}\doteq\boldsymbol{\rm\Xi}^{+}\boldsymbol{\rm D}_{H}\boldsymbol{\rm\Xi}
is a scalar. This single-mode model is used in simulations reported below in Secs.
III.1
and
III.2
.

II.3
Reference ray and new coordinates

The evolution of the wave amplitude is considered relative to the a “reference ray” (RR) that is governed by Hamilton’s equations

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\rm X}}{\mathrm{d}\zeta}=\frac{1}{V_{\star}}\,\frac{\partial H_{\star}}{\partial\boldsymbol{\rm K}},\quad\frac{\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\rm K}}{\mathrm{d}\zeta}=-\frac{1}{V_{\star}}\,\frac{\partial H_{\star}}{\partial\boldsymbol{\rm X}},

(19)

where
\boldsymbol{\rm X}
and
\boldsymbol{\rm K}
are the ray coordinate and the ray wavevector,
\zeta
is the path along the ray trajectory, and
V_{\star}\doteq|\partial H_{\star}/\partial\boldsymbol{\rm K}|
is the absolute value of the group velocity. The ray Hamiltonian
H_{\star}
is

\displaystyle H_{\star}\doteq\frac{1}{2}\,(\Lambda_{\star{\rm o}}+\Lambda_{\star{\rm x}})

(20)

for a mode-converting beam and
H_{\star}\doteq\Lambda_{\star s}
for a single-mode beam. Here and further, the index
\star
denotes that the corresponding quantity is evaluated on the RR.

Next, we introduce the RR-based curvilinear coordinates
\tilde{x}^{\mu}\equiv\{\zeta,\tilde{\varrho}^{1},\tilde{\varrho}^{2}\}
, where
\tilde{\varrho}^{\sigma}
are, loosely speaking, the orthogonal coordinates on the plane transverse to the group velocity of the RR as specified in
ref:pop2
. (Here and further, the indices
\sigma
and
{\bar{\sigma}}
span from 1 to 2; other Greek indices span from 1 to 3.) The basis vectors
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\rm e}}_{\mu}
of the new coordinates (
\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\rm x}=\tilde{\boldsymbol{\rm e}}_{\mu}\mathrm{d}\tilde{x}^{\mu}
) are defined such that

\displaystyle\tilde{\boldsymbol{\rm e}}_{\star\mu}\cdot\tilde{\boldsymbol{\rm e}}_{\star\nu}=\delta_{\mu\nu},\quad\left[\partial\tilde{\boldsymbol{\rm e}}_{\sigma}(\tilde{x})/\partial\tilde{\varrho}^{\bar{\sigma}}\right]_{\star}=0.

(21)

Then,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\rm x}\approx\boldsymbol{\rm X}(\zeta)+\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\rm e}}_{\star 1}&\tilde{\boldsymbol{\rm e}}_{\star 2}\end{array}\right)\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\tilde{\varrho}^{1}\\
\tilde{\varrho}^{2}\end{array}\right).

II.4
Quasioptical equation

To simplify the field equation, we introduce the rescaled complex vector amplitude
\boldsymbol{\rm\phi}=\sqrt{V_{\star}}\boldsymbol{\rm a}
. Then, as shown in Ref.
ref:pop3
, Eq. (
3
) leads to the following parabolic equation:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{\rm\phi}}{\partial\zeta}=\frac{1}{V_{\star}}\biggl[

\displaystyle-i(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\rm\mathfrak{L}}}_{\star\sigma{\bar{\sigma}}}\tilde{\varrho}^{\sigma}\tilde{\varrho}^{{\bar{\sigma}}}+\tilde{\boldsymbol{\rm\mathfrak{M}}}_{\star\sigma}\tilde{\varrho}^{\sigma}+\boldsymbol{\rm M}_{\star}-\boldsymbol{\rm U}_{\star})\boldsymbol{\rm\phi}

\displaystyle+\frac{i}{2}\,\tilde{\boldsymbol{\rm\Phi}}^{\sigma{\bar{\sigma}}}_{\star}\partial^{2}_{\sigma{\bar{\sigma}}}\boldsymbol{\rm\phi}+\boldsymbol{\rm\Gamma}\boldsymbol{\rm\phi}

\displaystyle-(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\rm u}}^{\sigma}_{\star}+\tilde{\boldsymbol{\rm\vartheta}}_{\star}^{\sigma}{}_{{\bar{\sigma}}}\tilde{\varrho}^{{\bar{\sigma}}})\partial_{\sigma}\boldsymbol{\rm\phi}-\frac{\tilde{\boldsymbol{\rm\vartheta}}_{\star}^{\sigma}{}_{\sigma}}{2}\boldsymbol{\rm\phi}\biggr],

(25)

In the single-mode case, when
\boldsymbol{\rm\phi}
is a scalar, one similarly has
ref:pop2

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial\zeta}=\frac{1}{V_{\star}}\biggl[

\displaystyle-i(\tilde{\mathfrak{L}}_{\star\sigma{\bar{\sigma}}}\tilde{\varrho}^{\sigma}\tilde{\varrho}^{{\bar{\sigma}}}-U_{\star})\phi+\frac{i}{2}\,\tilde{\Phi}^{\sigma{\bar{\sigma}}}_{\star}\partial^{2}_{\sigma{\bar{\sigma}}}\phi

\displaystyle+\Gamma\phi-\tilde{\vartheta}_{\star}^{\sigma}{}_{{\bar{\sigma}}}\tilde{\varrho}^{{\bar{\sigma}}}\partial_{\sigma}\phi-\frac{\tilde{\vartheta}_{\star}^{\sigma}{}_{\sigma}}{2}\phi\biggr],

(26)

which is an alternative representation of the field equation assumed in Refs.
ref:balakin07a
;
ref:balakin07b
;
ref:balakin08a
;
ref:balakin08b
. Here,
\partial_{\sigma}\doteq\partial/\partial\tilde{\rho^{\sigma}}
, summation over repeating indices is assumed, and the coefficients are expressed through
\boldsymbol{\rm D}
as described in Ref.
ref:pop3
. Models for
\boldsymbol{\rm\Gamma}
are discussed in Sec.
III
in detail.

III
Dissipation in hot plasma

The matrix
\boldsymbol{\rm\Gamma}
, which represents dissipation, is given by
ref:pop3

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\rm\Gamma}=\boldsymbol{\rm\Xi}_{\star}^{+}\boldsymbol{\rm D}_{A}(\boldsymbol{\rm x},\boldsymbol{\rm k}(\boldsymbol{\rm x}))\boldsymbol{\rm\Xi}_{\star},

(27)

where
\boldsymbol{\rm D}_{A}
is given by Eq. (
6
) and
\boldsymbol{\rm\Xi}_{\star}
is the polarization matrix [Eq. (
II.2
)] evaluated on the RR. (In the single-mode case,
\boldsymbol{\rm\Xi}_{\star}
becomes a vector, and then
\Gamma
is a scalar.) In an inhomogeneous medium,
\boldsymbol{\rm\Gamma}
is inhomogeneous, which results in variation of the dissipation-rate within the beam cross section [Fig.
1
(a)]. Most quasioptical codes
ref:poli01a
;
ref:poli01b
;
ref:poli18
;
ref:pereverzev98
;
ref:mazzucato89
;
ref:nowak93
;
ref:peeters96
;
ref:farina07
ignore this fact and adopt

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\rm\Gamma}\approx\boldsymbol{\rm\Gamma}_{\star}=\boldsymbol{\rm\Xi}_{\star}^{+}\boldsymbol{\rm D}_{A\star}\boldsymbol{\rm\Xi}_{\star}

(28)

instead [Fig.
1
(b)]. This leads to incorrect predictions for actual heating rates, as discussed in Ref.
note:nf1
. In PARADE, we adopt two different models to calculate
\boldsymbol{\rm\Gamma}
more accurately, as discussed in Sec.
III.1
and Sec.
III.2
. Also note that unlike in conventional single-mode models that treat the dissipation coefficient as a scalar
ref:poli01a
;
ref:poli01b
;
ref:poli18
;
ref:pereverzev98
;
ref:mazzucato89
;
ref:nowak93
;
ref:peeters96
;
ref:farina07
;
ref:balakin07a
;
ref:balakin07b
;
ref:balakin08a
;
ref:balakin08b
;
ref:pop2
, our
\boldsymbol{\rm\Gamma}
[Eq. (
27
)] is generally nondiagonal, so it couples
\phi^{\rm o}
and
\phi^{\rm x}
in Eq. (
II.4
). This effect, which we call dissipation-driven mode conversion, is discussed in Sec.
III.3
.

III.2
Approximate dissipation matrix

Calculating
\boldsymbol{\rm\Gamma}
using Eq. (
27
) is computationally expensive and can be impractical, for example, for data analysis between discharges and for optimization of the launching geometry. Because of this, we also propose a simplified model as an alternative, which is as follows. Assuming that the beam width is smaller than the characteristic scale of
\boldsymbol{\rm\Gamma}
, the latter can be Taylor-expanded to the first order in
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\rm\varrho}}
. To do this, note that

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\rm\Gamma}=\boldsymbol{\rm\Xi}_{\star}^{+}\boldsymbol{\rm D}_{A}(\boldsymbol{\rm X}+\tilde{\boldsymbol{\rm\varrho}},\boldsymbol{\rm K}+\tilde{\boldsymbol{\rm\pi}}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\rm\varrho}}))\boldsymbol{\rm\Xi}_{\star},

(34)

where
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\rm\pi}}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\rm\varrho}})\doteq\boldsymbol{\rm k}(\boldsymbol{\rm x})-\boldsymbol{\rm K}
can be expressed as
ref:pop2

\displaystyle\tilde{\pi}_{\mu}=-\frac{1}{V_{\star}^{2}}\frac{\partial H_{\star}}{\partial\tilde{\varrho}^{\sigma}}\frac{\partial H_{\star}}{\partial\tilde{\pi}_{\mu}}\tilde{\varrho}^{\sigma}.

(35)

This leads to

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\rm\Gamma}\approx\boldsymbol{\rm\Gamma}_{\star}+\boldsymbol{\rm\mathcal{G}}_{\star\sigma}\tilde{\varrho}^{\sigma},

(36a)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\rm\mathcal{G}}_{\star\sigma}=\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{\rm\Gamma}_{\star}}{\partial\tilde{\varrho}^{\sigma}}-\frac{1}{V_{\star}^{2}}\frac{\partial H_{\star}}{\partial\tilde{\varrho}^{\sigma}}\frac{\partial H_{\star}}{\partial\tilde{\pi}_{\mu}}\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{\rm\Gamma}_{\star}}{\partial\tilde{\pi}_{\mu}}.

(36b)

Because the matrix
\boldsymbol{\rm\mathcal{G}}_{\star\sigma}
has to be calculated only on the RR rather than at each grid point, this approach significantly speeds up calculations. We call it a first-order model. (Accordingly, the homogeneous-dissipation model used in other codes can be classified as the zeroth-model.) For single-mode beams, this approximation is equivalent to that used by Balakin
et al.

ref:balakin08a
, but our model extends to mode-converting beams as well.
When the first-order term exceeds the zeroth-order term, “numerical pumping” can occur (Fig.
4
; see also Ref.
ref:balakin08a
). To prevent this spurious effect in practical simulations, we introduce a cutoff:

\displaystyle\Gamma_{s^{\prime}s}\approx\begin{cases}\Gamma_{\star s^{\prime}s}+\mathcal{G}_{\star s^{\prime}s\sigma}\tilde{\varrho}^{\sigma},&\mathcal{G}_{\star s^{\prime}s\sigma}\tilde{\varrho}^{\sigma}\geq-\Gamma_{\star s^{\prime}s},\\
0,&\mathcal{G}_{\star s^{\prime}s\sigma}\tilde{\varrho}^{\sigma}<-\Gamma_{\star s^{\prime}s}.\end{cases}

(37)

IV
Conclusions

Here, we report the first quasioptical simulations of wave beams in a hot plasma using the quasioptical code PARADE (PAraxial RAy DEscription). This code
ref:pop1
;
ref:pop2
;
ref:pop3
is unique in that it accounts for inhomogeneity of the dissipation rate across the beam and mode conversion simultaneously. We show that the dissipation-rate inhomogeneity shifts beams relative to their trajectories in cold plasma and that the two electromagnetic modes are coupled via this process, an effect that was ignored in the past. We also propose a simplified approach to accounting for the dissipation-rate inhomogeneity. This approach is computationally inexpensive and simplifies analysis of actual experiments. Our results lay the foundation for comparing PARADE simulations with experimental data, as to be reported in our next paper
note:nf1
.
