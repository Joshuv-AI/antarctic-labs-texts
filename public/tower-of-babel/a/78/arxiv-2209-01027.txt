1.
Exponential automorphisms

An
automorphism
of the field
K

is a function
\sigma:K\rightarrow K

such that

\sigma(z_{1}+z_{2})=\sigma(z_{1})+\sigma(z_{2})

and

\sigma(z_{1}z_{2})=\sigma(z_{1})+\sigma(z_{2})

for all
z_{1},z_{2}\in K
, and also

\sigma(1)=1.

An
exponential automorphism
of the field
\mathbf{C}
of complex numbers is a function
\alpha:\mathbf{C}\rightarrow\mathbf{C}

such that

(1)

\alpha(z_{1}+z_{2})=\alpha(z_{1})+\alpha(z_{2})

and

(2)

\alpha\left(e^{z}\right)=e^{\alpha(z)}

for all
z,z_{1},z_{2}\in\mathbf{C}
.
The identity function
z\mapsto z
and complex conjugation
z\mapsto\overline{z}

are examples of automorphisms and exponential automorphisms.

Let
\ln r
denote the usual real-valued logarithm of the positive real number
r
.
For every positive integer
k
, let
2^{1/k}
be the positive
k
th root of 2.
Jan Mycielski
[
1
]

asked if, for every exponential automorphism
\alpha
,
we have
\alpha\left(2^{1/k}\right)=2^{1/k}
for
k=2,3,4

and
\alpha(\ln 2)=\ln 2
.
He observed that the answers to these questions are “yes”
if
\alpha
is the identity function or if
\alpha
is complex conjugation.

Theorem 1
.

If
\alpha
is an exponential automorphism, then

\alpha\left(t\right)=t

for all
t\in\mathbf{Q}
.

Proof.

We have

\alpha(1)=\alpha(1+0)=\alpha(1)+\alpha(0)

and so

\alpha(0)=0

and

\alpha(1)=\alpha\left(e^{0}\right)=e^{\alpha(0)}=e^{0}=1.

For all positive integers
m
and
n
we have

m=m\alpha(1)=\alpha(\underbrace{1+\cdots+1}_{m\text{ summands}})=\alpha(m)=\alpha\left(\underbrace{\frac{m}{n}+\cdots+\frac{m}{n}}_{n\text{ summands}}\right)=n\alpha\left(\frac{m}{n}\right)

and so

\alpha(m)=m\qqand\alpha\left(\frac{m}{n}\right)=\frac{m}{n}.

For all
z\in\mathbf{C}
we have

0=\alpha(0)=\alpha(z+(-z))=\alpha(z)+\alpha(-z)

and so
\alpha(-z)=-\alpha(z)
. Thus,
\alpha(t)=t
for all
t\in\mathbf{Q}
.
This completes the proof.
∎

Theorem 2
.

For every exponential automorphism
\alpha

and for every positive rational number
t
there is an integer

n=n(\alpha,t)
such that

\alpha(\ln t)=\ln t+2\pi ni.

Proof.

We have

t=\alpha(t)=\alpha\left(e^{\ln t}\right)=e^{\alpha(\ln t)}

and so

\alpha(\ln t)=\ln t+2\pi ni

for some integer
n
.
This completes the proof.
∎

Thus,
\alpha(\ln 2)=\ln 2+2\pi ni
for some integer
n
.

2.
Review of logarithms

The complex logarithm
\log z
is a function from the set
\mathbf{C}^{\times}

of nonzero complex numbers
to the set of sets of complex numbers:
If
z=re^{i\theta}\in\mathbf{C}^{\times}

with
r,\theta\in\mathbf{R}
and
r>0
, then

\log z=\left\{\ln r+i\theta+2\pi ni:n\in\mathbf{Z}\right\}.

For all
n\in\mathbf{Z}
we have

e^{\ln r+i\theta+2\pi ni}=e^{\ln r}e^{i\theta}e^{2\pi ni}=re^{i\theta}=z.

Thus, we can define
e^{\log z}=z
for all
z\in\mathbf{C}^{\times}
.

For all
\theta\in\mathbf{R}
there is a unique integer
n
such that

-\pi<\theta+2\pi n\leq\pi
.
The
principal branch
of the complex logarithm is the function

\Log:\mathbf{C}^{\times}\rightarrow\mathbf{C}
defined by

\Log z=\ln r+i\theta\in\log z\qquad\text{with $-\pi<\theta\leq\pi$.}

We have

(3)

\log z=\left\{\Log z+2\pi ni:n\in\mathbf{Z}\right\}

and
\Log r=\ln r
for all
r>0
.

Let
z_{1}=r_{1}e^{\theta_{1}i}
and
z_{2}=r_{2}e^{\theta_{2}i}
, with

-\pi<\theta_{1},\theta_{2}\leq\pi
. We have

\Log z_{1}=\ln r_{1}+i\theta_{1}\qqand\Log z_{2}=\ln r_{2}+i\theta_{2}

and

-2\pi<\theta_{1}+\theta_{2}\leq 2\pi.

There is a unique integer
\delta\in\{0,1,-1\}
such that

-\pi<\theta_{1}+\theta_{2}+2\pi\delta\leq\pi.

Let
\theta_{3}=\theta_{1}+\theta_{2}+2\pi\delta
.
It follows that

z_{1}z_{2}=r_{1}r_{2}e^{(\theta_{1}+\theta_{2})i}=r_{1}r_{2}e^{(\theta_{3}-2\pi\delta)i}=r_{1}r_{2}e^{\theta_{3}i}

and so

(4)

\displaystyle\Log z_{1}z_{2}

\displaystyle=\ln r_{1}r_{2}+\theta_{3}i

\displaystyle=\ln r_{1}+\ln r_{2}+(\theta_{1}+\theta_{2}+2\pi\delta)i

\displaystyle=\ln r_{1}+\theta_{1}i+\ln r_{2}+\theta_{2}i+2\pi\delta i

\displaystyle=\Log z_{1}+\Log z_{2}+2\pi\delta i.

3.
From exponential automorphisms to automorphisms

Theorem 3
.

Every exponential automorphism
\alpha
is an automorphism of
\mathbf{C}
.

Proof.

By Theorem
1
, the restriction of the function

\alpha
to
\mathbf{Q}
is the identity.
Because
\alpha
satisfies the additivity relation (
1
),
it suffices to prove that

\alpha(z_{1}z_{2})=\alpha(z_{1})\alpha(z_{2})

for all
z_{1},z_{2}\in\mathbf{C}^{\times}
.
By
(
4
), there exists
\delta=\delta(z_{1},z_{2})\in\{0,1,-1\}

such that

\displaystyle\alpha(z_{1}z_{2})

\displaystyle=\alpha\left(e^{\Log z_{1}z_{2}}\right)

\displaystyle=e^{\alpha\left(\Log z_{1}z_{2}\right)}

\displaystyle=e^{\alpha\left(\Log z_{1}+\Log z_{2}+2\pi\delta i\right)}

\displaystyle=e^{\alpha(\Log z_{1})+\alpha(\Log z_{2})+\alpha(2\pi\delta i)}

\displaystyle=e^{\alpha(\Log z_{1})}e^{\alpha(\Log z_{2})}e^{\alpha(2\pi\delta i)}

\displaystyle=\alpha\left(e^{\Log z_{1}}\right)\alpha\left(e^{\Log z_{2}}\right)\alpha\left(e^{2\pi\delta i}\right)

\displaystyle=\alpha(z_{1})\alpha(z_{2})\alpha(1)

\displaystyle=\alpha(z_{1})\alpha(z_{2}).

This completes the proof.
∎

Theorem 4
.

Let
r
be a positive rational number and let
k
be a positive integer.
If
r^{1/k}
is the positive
k
th root of
r
, then

\alpha\left(r^{1/k}\right)=\zeta r^{1/k}

where
\zeta
is a
k
th root of unity.

Proof.

Let
t=r^{1/k}
. We have

r=\alpha(r)=\alpha\left(t^{k}\right)=\alpha(t)^{k}

and so

\alpha\left(r^{1/k}\right)=\alpha\left(t\right)=\zeta r^{1/k}

for some
k
th root of unity
\zeta
.
∎

4.
An open problem

Theorem 5
.

Let
\alpha
be an exponential automorphism. If
\alpha(x)\in\mathbf{R}
for all
x\in\mathbf{R}

or if
\alpha
is continuous, then the answer to Mycielski’s question is “yes.”

Proof.

The following argument uses classical results in algebra (cf. Yale
[
2
]
),
whose proofs are in the Appendix.

By Theorem
3
,
every exponential automorphism
\alpha
of
\mathbf{C}
is an automorphism of
\mathbf{C}
.
By Theorem
6
,
the only automorphism of
\mathbf{R}
is the identity.
Thus, if
\alpha
is an exponential automorphism
such that
\alpha(x)\in\mathbf{R}
for all
x\in\mathbf{R}
,
then
\alpha
is the identity on
\mathbf{R}
.
By Theorem
7
,
the only automorphisms of
\mathbf{C}
whose restriction to
\mathbf{R}
is the identity
are the identity and complex conjugation.
By Theorem
8
,
the only continuous automorphisms of
\mathbf{C}
are
the identity and complex conjugation. The answer to Mycielski’s
question is “yes” for both these automorphisms.
∎

It is natural to ask if the only exponential automorphisms are the identity
function and complex conjugation.
Equivalently, is every exponential automorphism real-valued on
\mathbf{R}
?
Is every exponential automorphism continuous?

Appendix A
Automorphisms of
\mathbf{R}
and
\mathbf{C}

Theorem 6
.

The only automorphism of the field
\mathbf{R}
is the identity.

Proof.

Let
\sigma
be an automorphism of
\mathbf{R}
.
We have
\sigma(1)=1
. It follows from
the proof of Theorem
1
that

\sigma(t)=t
for all
t\in\mathbf{Q}
.

If
x\in\mathbf{R}
and
x>0
, then
x=y^{2}
for some nonzero
y\in\mathbf{R}

and so
\sigma(x)=\sigma\left(y^{2}\right)=\sigma(y)^{2}>0
.
If
x,y\in\mathbf{R}
and
x<y
, then
y-x>0
and

\sigma(y)-\sigma(x)=\sigma(y-x)>0.

Thus,
x<y
implies
\sigma(x)<\sigma(y)
and the function
\sigma
is order preserving.

If
x\in\mathbf{R}
and
\sigma(x)\neq x
, then either
x<\sigma(x)
or
\sigma(x)<x
.
If
x<\sigma(x)
, then there exists
t\in\mathbf{Q}
such that

x<t<\sigma(x)

and so

\sigma(x)<\sigma(t)=t<\sigma(x)

which is absurd.
If
\sigma(x)<x
, then there exists
t\in\mathbf{Q}
such that

\sigma(x)<t<x

and so

\sigma(x)<t=\sigma(t)<\sigma(x)

which is also absurd.
Therefore,
\sigma(x)=x
for all
x\in\mathbf{R}
.
This completes the proof.
∎

Theorem 7
.

The only automorphisms
\sigma
of
\mathbf{C}
such that
\sigma(x)\in\mathbf{R}

for all
x\in\mathbf{R}
are the identity and complex conjugation.

Proof.

Let
\sigma
be an automorphism of
\mathbf{C}
.
If
\sigma(x)\in\mathbf{R}
for all
x\in\mathbf{R}
, then the restriction of
\sigma
to
\mathbf{R}
is an automorphism of
\mathbf{R}
and so
\sigma
is the identity on
\mathbf{R}
.
We have

\sigma(i)^{2}=\sigma(i^{2})=\sigma(-1)=-1
and so
\sigma(i)=i\quad\text{or}\quad\sigma(i)=-i
.
Let
z=x+yi\in\mathbf{C}
with
x,y\in\mathbf{R}
. If
\sigma(i)=i
, then

\sigma(x+yi)=\sigma(x)+\sigma(y)\sigma(i)=x+yi=z

and
\sigma
is the identity on
\mathbf{C}
.
If
\sigma(i)=-i
, then

\sigma(x+yi)=\sigma(x)+\sigma(y)\sigma(i)=x+y(-i)=x-yi=\overline{z}

and
\sigma
is complex conjugation on
\mathbf{C}
.
This completes the proof.
∎

Theorem 8
.

The only continuous automorphisms of
\mathbf{C}
are the identity
and complex conjugation.

Proof.

Let
\sigma
be a continuous automorphism of
\mathbf{C}
.
We have
\sigma(t)=t
for all
t\in\mathbf{Q}
and
\sigma(i)=\pm i
.
If
\sigma(i)=i
, then
\sigma(t_{1}+it_{2})=t_{1}+it_{2}
for all Gaussian numbers
t_{1}+it_{2}\in\mathbf{Q}(i)
.
If
\sigma(i)=-i
, then
\sigma(t_{1}+it_{2})=t_{1}-it_{2}
for all Gaussian numbers
t_{1}+it_{2}\in\mathbf{Q}(i)
.

The Gaussian numbers are dense in
\mathbf{C}
. For every complex nunber
z

there is a sequence of Gaussian numbers
(w_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}
such that

z=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}w_{n}.

If
\sigma
is a continuous automorphism of
\mathbf{C}
, then

\displaystyle\sigma(z)

\displaystyle=\sigma\left(\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}w_{n}\right)=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\sigma\left(w_{n}\right)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}w_{n}&\text{if $\sigma(i)=i$}\\
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\overline{w_{n}}&\text{if $\sigma(i)=-i$}\end{cases}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}z&\text{if $\sigma(i)=i$}\\
\overline{z}&\text{if $\sigma(i)=-i$}\end{cases}

and so
\sigma
is either the identity function or complex conjugation.
This completes the proof.
∎
