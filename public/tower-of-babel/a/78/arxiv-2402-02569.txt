On the Complexity of Finite-Sum Smooth Optimization under the Polyak–Łojasiewicz Condition
Yunyan Bai, Yuxing Liu, Luo Luo

Abstract
This paper considers the optimization problem of the form min 𝐱 ∈ ℝ d ⁡ f ⁡ ( 𝐱 ) ≜ 1 n ​ ∑ i = 1 n f i ​ ( 𝐱 ) \min_{{\bf x}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}f({\bf x})\triangleq\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}f_{i}({\bf x}) , where f ⁡ ( ⋅ ) f(\cdot) satisfies the Polyak–Łojasiewicz (PL) condition with parameter μ \mu and { f i ​ ( ⋅ ) } i = 1 n \{f_{i}(\cdot)\}_{i=1}^{n} is L L -mean-squared smooth. We show that any gradient method requires at least Ω ⁡ ( n + κ ​ n ​ log ⁡ ( 1 / ϵ ) ) \Omega(n+\kappa\sqrt{n}\log(1/\epsilon)) incremental first-order oracle (IFO) calls to find an ϵ \epsilon -suboptimal solution, where κ ≜ L / μ \kappa\triangleq L/\mu is the condition number of the problem. This result nearly matches upper bounds of IFO complexity for best-known first-order methods. We also study the problem of minimizing the PL function in the distributed setting such that the individuals f 1 ​ ( ⋅ ) , … , f n ​ ( ⋅ ) f_{1}(\cdot),\dots,f_{n}(\cdot) are located on a connected network of n n agents. We provide lower bounds of Ω ⁡ ( κ / γ ​ log ⁡ ( 1 / ϵ ) ) \Omega(\kappa/\sqrt{\gamma}\,\log(1/\epsilon)) , Ω ⁡ ( ( κ + τ ​ κ / γ ) ​ log ⁡ ( 1 / ϵ ) ) \Omega((\kappa+\tau\kappa/\sqrt{\gamma}\,)\log(1/\epsilon)) and Ω ⁡ ( n + κ ​ n ​ log ⁡ ( 1 / ϵ ) ) \Omega\big(n+\kappa\sqrt{n}\log(1/\epsilon)\big) for communication rounds, time cost and local first-order oracle calls respectively, where γ ∈ ( 0 , 1 ] \gamma\in(0,1] is the spectral gap of the mixing matrix associated with the network and τ > 0 \tau>0 is the time cost of per communication round. Furthermore, we propose a decentralized first-order method that nearly matches above lower bounds in expectation.

Introduction

We study the optimization problem of the form

\displaystyle\min_{{\bf{x}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}f({\bf{x}})\triangleq\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}f_{i}({\bf{x}}),

(1)

where
\{f_{i}(\cdot)\}_{i=1}^{n}
is
L

The complexity of finding stationary points in Problem (
1

[

However, finding the global solution is intractable for the general nonconvex smooth optimization
[

.
This paper focuses on the minimization Problem (
1

[

\displaystyle f({\bf{x}})-\inf_{{\bf{y}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}f({\bf{y}})\leq\frac{1}{2\mu}\left\|\nabla f({\bf{x}})\right\|^{2}

{\bf{x}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}

is a constant.
This inequality suggests the function value gap

is dominated by the square of gradient norm, which leads to the gradient descent (GD) method linearly converge to the global minimum without the convexity

25

The PL condition covers a lot of popular applications, such as deep neural networks

We typically solve the finite-sum optimization Problem (

[

]
, which can access the pair
\{f_{i}({\bf{x}}),\nabla f_{i}({\bf{x}})\}
for given
{\bf{x}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}

.
This class of methods can leverage the structure of the objective
[

,
15
,
51
,
64
]
to iterate with one or mini-batch individual gradient, which is more efficient than the iteration with the full-batch gradient.
IFO methods have received a lot of attention in recent years.
For example, the stochastic variance reduced gradient (SVRG) methods with negative momentum

,
3
,

,

,
1

achieve the (near) optimal IFO complexity for convex optimization;

14

For the PL condition,

proposed SVRG-type methods that find the
\epsilon
-suboptimal solution within at most

is the condition number.
Later,

,
Wang et al. 2019
,
Li et al. 2021
improved the upper bound to

by stochastic recursive gradient estimator.
Recently,
Yue et al. 2023
established a tight lower complexity bound of the full-batch gradient methods for minimizing the PL function.
However, the optimality of existing IFO methods for the finite-sum setting is still an open problem.

For large-scale optimization problems, we are interested in designing the distributed algorithms.
Specifically, we allocate individuals
f_{1}(\cdot),\dots,f_{n}(\cdot)
on
n
different agents and desire the agents solve the problem collaboratively.
We focus on the decentralized setting that agents are linked by a connected network, so that each agent can only access its own local first-order oracle (LFO) and exchange messages with its neighbours.
Besides the LFO complexity, we also require considering the communication complexity and the time complexity.
It is worth noting that the time complexity in distributed optimization does not directly corresponds to the weighted sum of the LFO complexity and the communication complexity, since some agents may skip the computation of local gradient during the iterations

,
39
]

Most of work for decentralized optimization focus on the convex case

,

,

Recently,

In this paper, we provide the nearly tight lower bounds for the finite-sum optimization problem under the PL condition.

We provide the lower bound of

2.2
The Finite-Sum Optimization

The complexity of first-order methods for solving the finite-sum optimization Problem (
1
) on single machine mainly depends on the number of access to the incremental first-order oracle (IFO), which is defined as follows
[
1
,
56
]
.

Definition 2.7
.

The incremental first-order oracle (IFO) takes the input
i\in[n]
and
{\bf{x}}
, and returns the pair
(f_{i}({\bf{x}}),\nabla f_{i}({\bf{x}}))
.

Then we formally define the IFO algorithm.

Definition 2.8
.

An IFO algorithm for given initial point
{\bf{x}}^{0}
is defined as a measurable mapping from functions
\{f_{i}(\cdot)\}_{i=1}^{n}
to an infinite sequence of point and index
pairs
\{({\bf{x}}^{t},i_{t})\}_{t=0}^{\infty}
with random variable
i_{t}\in[n]
, which satisfies

\displaystyle{\bf{x}}^{t}\in\mathrm{Lin}\big(\{{\bf{x}}^{0},\ldots,{\bf{x}}^{t-1},\nabla f_{i_{0}}({\bf{x}}^{0}),\ldots,\nabla f_{i_{t-1}}({\bf{x}}^{t-1})\}\big),

where
\mathrm{Lin}(\cdot)
denotes the linear span and
i_{t}
denotes the index of individual function chosen at the
t
-th step.

For the distributed optimization over a network of
n
agents, the
i
-th agent can only perform the computation on its local function
f_{i}(\cdot)
directly.
Hence, we describe the complexity of computational cost by the number of access to the local first-order oracle (LFO).

Definition 2.9
.

The local first-order oracle (IFO) takes the input
i\in[n]
and
{\bf{x}}
, and returns the pair
(f_{i}({\bf{x}}),\nabla f_{i}({\bf{x}}))
.

Recall that agents on network can only communicate with their neighbours, which means the agent in decentralized algorithms cannot arbitrarily establish the linear space of all local gradients.
Additionally, one iteration of the algorithm allows a mini-batch of agents to compute their local gradient in parallel.
Therefore, besides the LFO complexity, we also need to separately consider the communication complexity and the time complexity.
This motivates the following definition for decentralized first-order oracle algorithm (DFO)
[
50
]
.

Definition 2.10
.

A decentralized first-order oracle (DFO) algorithm over a network of
n
agents satisfies the following constraints:

•

Local memory:
Each agent
i
can store past values in a local memory
\mathcal{M}_{i,s}
at time
s>0
.
These values can be accessed and used at time
s
by running the algorithm on agent
i
. Additionally, for all
i\in[n]
, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{i}^{s}\subset\mathcal{M}_{{\rm comp},i}^{s}\bigcup\mathcal{M}_{{\rm comm},i}^{s},

where
\mathcal{M}_{{\rm comp},i}^{s}
and
\mathcal{M}_{{\rm comm},i}^{s}
are the values come from the computation and communication respectively.

•

Local computation:
Each agent
i
can access its local first-order oracle
\{f_{i}({\bf{x}}),\nabla f_{i}({\bf{x}})\}
for given
{\bf{x}}\in\mathcal{M}_{i,s}
at time
s
. That is, for all
i\in[n]
, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{{\rm comp},i}^{s}={\rm Lin}\big(\{{\bf{x}},\nabla f_{i}({\bf{x}}):x\in\mathcal{M}_{i}^{s-1}\}\big).

•

Local communication:
Each agent
i
can share its value to all or part of its neighbors at time
s
. That is, for all
i\in[n]
, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{{\rm comm},i}^{s}={\rm Lin}\Bigg(\bigcup_{j\in{\rm nbr}(i)}\mathcal{M}_{j}^{s-\tau}\Bigg),

where
{\rm nbr}(i)
is the set consists of the indices for the neighbours of agent
i
and
\tau<s
.

•

Output value:
Each agent
i
can specify one vector in its memory as local output of the algorithm at time
s
. That is, for all
i\in[n]
, we have

{\bf{x}}_{i}^{s}\in\mathcal{M}_{i}^{s}
.

The Lower Bound on IFO Complexity

This section provides the lower bound on IFO complexity to show the optimality (up to logarithmic factors) of existing first-order methods
[
66
,
55
,
32
]
.
Without loss of generality, we always assume the IFO algorithm iterates with the initial point
{\bf{x}}^{(0)}={\bf{0}}
in our analysis for lower bound. Otherwise, we can take the functions
\{f_{i}({\bf{x}}+{\bf{x}}^{(0)})\}_{i=1}^{n}
into consideration.

We first consider the case of
n={\mathcal{O}}(\kappa^{2})
.
We introduce the functions
\psi_{\theta}:{\mathbb{R}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
,
q_{T,t}:{\mathbb{R}}^{Tt}\to{\mathbb{R}}
and
g_{T,t}:{\mathbb{R}}^{Tt}\to{\mathbb{R}}
provided by
Yue et al. 2023
, that is

\displaystyle\psi_{\theta}(x)=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{2}x^{2},&x\leq\frac{31}{32}\theta,\\[3.00003pt]
\frac{1}{2}x^{2}-16(x-\frac{31}{32}\theta)^{2},&{\frac{31}{32}\theta<x\leq\theta,}\\[3.00003pt]
\frac{1}{2}x^{2}-\frac{1}{32}\theta^{2}+16(x-\frac{33}{32}\theta)^{2},&{\theta<x\leq\frac{33}{32}\theta,}\\[3.00003pt]
\frac{1}{2}x^{2}-\frac{1}{32}\theta^{2},&{x>\frac{33}{32}\theta,}\end{cases}

\displaystyle q_{T,t}({\bf{x}})=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=0}^{t-1}\Big(\Big(\frac{7}{8}x_{iT}-x_{iT+1}\Big)^{2}+\sum_{j=1}^{T-1}(x_{iT+j+1}-x_{iT+j})^{2}\Big),

\displaystyle\text{and}\quad g_{T,t}({\bf{x}})=q_{T,t}({\bf{b}}-{\bf{x}})+\sum_{i=1}^{Tt}\psi_{b_{i}}(b_{i}-x_{i}),

where we define
x_{0}=0
and
{\bf{b}}\in\mathbb{R}^{Tt}
with
b_{kT+\tau}=({7}/{8})^{k}
for
k\in\{0\}\cup[T-1]
and
\tau\in[T]
.
We can verify that

\displaystyle g_{T,t}^{*}\triangleq\inf_{{\bf{y}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{Tt}}g_{T,t}({\bf{y}})=0.

The following lemma shows the function
g_{T,t}
holds the zero-chain property
[
42
,
13
]
and describes its smoothness, PL parameter and optimal function value gap, which results the tight lower bound of full-batch first-order methods
[
61
, Section 4]
.

Lemma 3.1
.

The function
g_{T,t}:{\mathbb{R}}^{Tt}\to{\mathbb{R}}
holds that:

(a)

For any
{\bf{x}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{Tt}
satisfying
{\rm supp}({\bf{x}})\subseteq\left\{1,2,\cdots,k\right\}
, it holds
{\rm supp}(\nabla g_{T,t}({\bf{x}}))\subseteq\left\{1,2,\cdots,k+1\right\}
.

(b)

The function
g_{T,t}
is 37-smooth.

(c)

The function
g_{T,t}
is
1/(aT)
-PL with
a=19708
.

(d)

The function
g_{T,t}
satisfies that
g_{T,t}(\mathbf{0})-g_{T,t}^{*}\leq 3T
.

(e)

For any
\delta<0.01
,
t=2\lfloor\log_{{8}/{7}}{2}/{(3\delta)}\rfloor
and
{\bf{x}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{Tt}
satisfying
{\rm supp}({\bf{x}})\subseteq\left\{1,2,\cdots,Tt/2\right\}
, it holds that
g_{T,t}({\bf{x}})-g_{T,t}^{*}>3T\delta
.

We can establish the mean-squared smooth functions by the composition of orthogonal transform.
Compared with the study on convex and general nonconvex problem
[
14
,
65
]
, we present the following lemma by additionally considering the PL condition.

Lemma 3.2
.

Given a function
g:\mathbb{R}^{m}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
that is
\hat{L}
-smooth and
\hat{\mu}
-PL, define
f_{i}({\bf{x}})=g({\bf U}^{(i)}{\bf{x}})
with
{\bf{x}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{mn}
,
i\in[n]
and
{\bf U}^{\left(i\right)}=[{\bf{e}}_{(i-1)m+1},\cdots,{\bf{e}}_{im}]^{\top}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m\times mn}
.
Then the function set
\{f_{i}:\mathbb{R}^{mn}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\}_{i=1}^{n}
is
\hat{L}/\sqrt{n}
-mean-squared smooth,
and the function
f(\cdot)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}f_{i}(\cdot)
is
\hat{\mu}/n
-PL with
f(\mathbf{0})-\inf_{{\bf{y}}\in{\mathbb{R}}}f({\bf{y}})=g(\mathbf{0})-\inf_{{\bf{y}}\in{\mathbb{R}}}g({\bf{y}})
.

To achieve the hard instance functions with the desired smoothness and PL parameters, we also require the scaling lemma as follows.

Lemma 3.3
.

Suppose the function
g:\mathbb{R}^{m}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is
\hat{L}
-smooth,
\hat{\mu}
-PL and has lower bound
g^{*}=\inf_{{\bf{y}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}}g({\bf{y}})
,
then the function
\hat{g}({\bf{x}})=\alpha g(\beta{\bf{x}})
is
\alpha\beta^{2}\hat{L}
-smooth,
\alpha\beta^{2}\hat{\mu}
-PL and satisfies that
\hat{g}(\mathbf{0})-\hat{g}^{*}=\alpha(g(\mathbf{0})-g^{*})
for any
\alpha,\beta>0
, where
\hat{g}^{*}=\inf_{{\bf{y}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}}\hat{g}({\bf{y}})
.

Based on above lemmas

,
3.2
and
3.3

\Omega\big(\kappa\sqrt{n}\log(1/\epsilon)\big)
on IFO complexity for large

Theorem 3.4
.

For any
L,\mu,n,\Delta
and
\epsilon
with
\epsilon<0.005\Delta
and

L\geq 37a\sqrt{n}\mu
,
there exists
L
-mean-squared smooth function set
\{f_{i}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}\}_{i=1}^{n}

with

We present the proof sketch of Theorem

Specifically, we take the function
g_{T,t}:{\mathbb{R}}^{Tt}\to{\mathbb{R}}

and

Finally, the statements (a) and (e) of Lemma

We then consider the case of
n=\Omega(\kappa^{2})
.
Following the analysis by

IFO calls.

For any

Lower Bounds in Decentralized Setting

This section provides lower bounds for decentralized setting.
The main idea of our construction is splitting the function
g_{T,t}:{\mathbb{R}}^{Tt}\to{\mathbb{R}}
(defined in Section
3
) as follows

\displaystyle g_{T,t}({\bf{x}})=q_{1}({\bf{b}}-{\bf{x}})+q_{2}({\bf{b}}-{\bf{x}})+r({\bf{x}}),

where we define the functions
q_{1}:{\mathbb{R}}^{Tt}\to{\mathbb{R}}
,
q_{2}:{\mathbb{R}}^{Tt}\to{\mathbb{R}}
and
r:{\mathbb{R}}^{Tt}\to{\mathbb{R}}
as

\displaystyle\begin{split}&q_{1}({\bf{x}})=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{Tt/2}(x_{2i-1}-x_{2i})^{2},\\
&q_{2}({\bf{x}})=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=0}^{t-1}\left[\Big(\frac{7}{8}x_{iT}-x_{iT+1}\Big)^{2}+\sum_{j=iT/2+1}^{(i+1)T/2-1}(x_{2j}-x_{2j+1})^{2}\right],\\
&\text{and}\quad r({\bf{x}})=\sum_{i=1}^{Tt}\psi_{b_{i}}(b_{i}-x_{i}),\end{split}

Based on above decomposition for
g_{T,t}
, we can establish an hard instance of
n
individual functions for communication complexity and time complexity.
We let
{\mathcal{G}}=\{{\mathcal{V}},{\mathcal{E}}\}
be the graph associated to the network of the agents, where the node set
{\mathcal{V}}=\{1,\dots,n\}
corresponds to the
n
agents and the edge set
{\mathcal{E}}=\{(i,j):\text{node $i$ and node $j$ are connected}\}
describes the topology for the network of agents.
For given a subset
{\mathcal{C}}\subseteq\mathcal{V}
, we define the function
h_{i}^{\mathcal{C}}({\bf{x}}):{\mathbb{R}}^{Tt}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
as

\displaystyle h_{i}^{\mathcal{C}}({\bf{x}})=\begin{cases}\frac{r({\bf{x}})}{n}+\frac{q_{1}({\bf{b}}-{\bf{x}})}{|{\mathcal{C}}|},&{i\in{\mathcal{C}}},\\[3.99994pt]
\frac{r({\bf{x}})}{n}+\frac{q_{2}({\bf{b}}-{\bf{x}})}{|{\mathcal{C}}_{\sigma}|},&{i\in{\mathcal{C}}_{\sigma}},\\[3.99994pt]
\frac{r({\bf{x}})}{n},&{\text{otherwise}},\end{cases}

where
{\mathcal{C}}_{\sigma}=\left\{v\in\mathcal{V}:{\rm dis}({\mathcal{C}},v)\geq\sigma\right\}
and
{\rm dis}({\mathcal{C}},v)
is the distance between set
{\mathcal{C}}
and node
v
.

We can verify the function sets
\{h_{i}^{\mathcal{C}}:{\mathbb{R}}^{Tt}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}\}_{i=1}^{n}
has the following properties.

Lemma 4.1
.

We define
h(\cdot)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}h_{i}^{\mathcal{C}}(\cdot)
, then we have:

(a)

The function sets
\{h_{i}^{\mathcal{C}}:{\mathbb{R}}^{Tt}\to{\mathbb{R}}\}_{i=1}^{n}
is mean-squared smooth with parameter
33/n+\max\left\{2/|{\mathcal{C}}|,2/|{\mathcal{C}}_{\sigma}|\right\}
.

(b)

The function
h:{\mathbb{R}}^{Tt}\to{\mathbb{R}}
is
1/\left(anT\right)
-PL.

(c)

The function holds
h(\mathbf{0})-\inf_{{\bf{y}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{Tt}}h({\bf{y}})\leq 3T/n
.

Now we provide the lower bounds for communication complexity and time complexity based on the scaling on the functions
\{h_{i}^{\mathcal{C}}:{\mathbb{R}}^{Tt}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}\}_{i=1}^{n}
.

Lemma 4.2
.

We let
f_{i}({\bf{x}})=\alpha h_{i}^{\mathcal{C}}(\beta{\bf{x}})
with
{\bf{x}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{Tt}
and

communication complexity and

.

For given spectral gap
\gamma\in(0,1]
, we consider the linear graph
{\mathcal{G}}=\{{\mathcal{V}},{\mathcal{E}}\}
with the node set
{\mathcal{V}}=\{1,\dots,n\}
and the edge set
{\mathcal{E}}=\{(i,j):|i-j|=1,i\in{\mathcal{V}}~~\text{and}~~j\in{\mathcal{V}}\}
.
Combining with the function

defined in Lemma

Theorem 4.3

For any

and
\epsilon

For decentralized optimization, it is natural to integrate GD with gradient tracking

,

The following theorem shows that the communication complexity and the time complexity of DGD-GT nearly match the lower bounds shown in Corollary

Theorem 5.1
.

We suppose that Assumption
2.1
–
2.6
hold, then running Algorithm
3
(DGD-GT) with appropriate parameters setting achieves

{\mathbb{E}}[f({\bf{x}}^{\rm out})-f^{*}]\leq\epsilon
within
communication complexity of

\tilde{\mathcal{O}}\big(\kappa/\sqrt{\gamma}\log(1/\epsilon)\big)
, time complexity of
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}\big(\kappa(1+\tau/\sqrt{\gamma}\,)\log(1/\epsilon)\big)

and LFO complexity of
{\mathcal{O}}\big(n\kappa\log(1/\epsilon)\big)

in expectation.

However, the upper bound on LFO complexity of DGD-GT (Algorithm
3
) shown in Theorem
5.1
does not match the lower bound of
\Omega\big(n+\kappa\sqrt{n}\log(1/\epsilon)\big)
provided by Corollary
4.4
.
Recall that the optimal IFO methods for non-distributed setting are based on the stochastic recursive ‘gradient
[
32
,
66
,
55
]
.
We borrow this idea to construct the recursive gradient with respect to local agents, i.e., we update the local gradient estimator by

\displaystyle\begin{split}{\bf{g}}^{t+1}(i)=\begin{cases}\nabla f_{i}({\bf{x}}^{t+1}(i)),&\zeta^{t}=1,\\[4.26773pt]
{\bf{g}}^{t}(i)+\dfrac{\xi^{t}_{i}}{bq}\big(\nabla f_{i}({\bf{x}}^{t+1}(i))-\nabla f_{i}({\bf{x}}^{t}(i))\big),&\zeta^{t}=0,\end{cases}\end{split}

where we introduce random variables
\zeta^{t}\sim{\rm Bernoulli}(p)
and
[\xi^{t}_{1},\cdots,\xi^{t}_{n}]^{\top}\sim{\rm Multinomial}(b,q{\bf{1}})
with some small probabilities
p
and
q
which encourage only few of agents compute local gradients in most of iterations.
Similar to the procedure of DGD-GT (Algorithm
3
), we can also introduce steps of gradient tracking and Chebyshev acceleration to improve the communication efficiency.
Finally, we achieve
d
ecentralized
r
ecursive l
o
cal gradie
n
t d
e
scent (DRONE) method, which is formally presented in Algorithm
4
.

We analyze the complexity of DRONE by the following Lyapunov function

\displaystyle\Phi^{t}=\mathbb{E}[f({{\bar{\bf{x}}}}^{t})-f^{*}]+\alpha U^{t}+\beta V^{t}+LC^{t},

where
\alpha=2\eta/p
,
\beta=8L\rho^{2}n\eta^{2}
,

\displaystyle U^{t}=\mathbb{E}\Bigg\|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\big({\bf{g}}^{t}(i)-\nabla f_{i}({\bf{x}}^{t}(i))\big)\Bigg\|^{2},

\displaystyle V^{t}=\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}\|{\bf G}^{t}-\nabla{\bf F}({\bf X}^{t})\|^{2},

\displaystyle\text{and}~~~~C^{t}={\mathbb{E}}\|{\bf X}^{t}-\mathbf{1}{{\bar{\bf{x}}}}^{t}\|^{2}+\eta^{2}\mathbb{E}\|{\bf S}^{t}-\mathbf{1}{{\bar{\bf{s}}}}^{t}\|^{2}.

Compared with the analysis of
Luo and Ye 2022
,
Li et al. 2022a
,
Xin et al. 2022
for general nonconvex case, we additionally introduce the term of
f^{*}
into our Lyapunov function to show the desired linear convergence under the PL condition, i.e., we can show that

\displaystyle\Phi^{T}\leq(1-\mu\eta)^{T}\Phi^{0}

by taking
\eta=\Theta(1/L)
.
We present the convergence result of DRONE formally in the following theorem and corollary.

Theorem 5.2
.

We suppose that Assumption
2.1
–
2.6
hold, then running DRONE (Algorithm
4
) with parameters setting

\displaystyle\begin{split}&p\in\left[\frac{1}{n+1},\frac{1}{2}\right],~~~b\in\left[\frac{1-p}{p},n\right],~~~\eta\leq\min\left\{\frac{1}{20L},\frac{p}{2\mu}\right\},\\
&~q=\frac{1}{n},~~K=\left\lceil\frac{\sqrt{2}\,(4+\log n)}{(\sqrt{2}-1)\sqrt{\gamma}}\right\rceil~~\text{and}~~T\geq\left\lceil\frac{1}{\mu\eta}\log\frac{\Phi^{0}}{\epsilon}\right\rceil\end{split}

{\mathbb{E}}[f({\bf{x}}^{\rm out})-f^{*}]\leq\epsilon

Corollary 5.3
.

Under the setting of Theorem
5.2

\displaystyle\begin{split}&p=\frac{1}{\min\{\sqrt{n},\kappa\}+1},~~b=\left\lceil\frac{1-p}{p}\right\rceil,~~\eta=\min\left\{\frac{1}{20L},\frac{p}{2\mu}\right\},\\
&~q=\frac{1}{n},~~K=\left\lceil\frac{\sqrt{2}\,(4+\log n)}{(\sqrt{2}-1)\sqrt{\gamma}}\right\rceil~~\text{and}~~T=\left\lceil\frac{1}{\mu\eta}\log\frac{\Phi^{0}}{\epsilon}\right\rceil\end{split}

\tilde{\mathcal{O}}\big(\kappa/\sqrt{\gamma}\log(1/\epsilon)\big)
, time complexity of
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}\big(\kappa(1+\tau/\sqrt{\gamma}\,)\log(1/\epsilon)\big)

{\mathcal{O}}\big((n+\kappa\sqrt{n}\,)\log(1/\epsilon)\big)

Note that the setting of
p=1/(\min\{\sqrt{n},\kappa\}+1)
leads to
b={\mathcal{O}}(\min\{\sqrt{n},\kappa\})
,
\eta=\Theta\left({1}/{L}\right)

and

4.4

.

As a comparison, the analysis of PAGE
[
32
]
(for single machine optimization) takes
p=\Theta(1/\sqrt{n}\,)

b=\Theta(\sqrt{n}\,)

If we directly apply these parameters to DRONE, it will result LFO complexity of
{\mathcal{O}}\big((n+\kappa\sqrt{n}\,)\log(1/\epsilon)\big)

\tilde{\mathcal{O}}\big((\kappa+\sqrt{n}\,)\log(1/\epsilon)/\sqrt{\gamma}\,\big)

In the case of

Intuitively, our analysis for DRONE considers the larger stepsize

than PAGE when

7
Conclusion

We provide the lower complexity bound for smooth finite-sum optimization under the PL condition,
which implies the upper bound of IFO complexity archived by existing first-order methods
[
55
,
32
,
66
]

Appendix D
More Details for Experiments

All of our experiments are performed on PC with Intel(R) Core(TM) i7-8550U CPU@1.80GHz processor and we implement the algorithms by MPI for Python 3.9.

We formally present the details of centralized gradient descent (CGD) in Algorithm
5
. The network in CGD has sever-client architecture, which allows the server to communicate with all clients.
The convergence of CGD can be described by gradient descent step

\displaystyle\tilde{\bf{x}}^{t+1}=\tilde{\bf{x}}^{t}-\eta\nabla f(\tilde{\bf{x}}^{t}),

which can find an
\epsilon
-suboptimal solution within
{\mathcal{O}}(\kappa\log(1/\epsilon))
iterations
[
25
]
.
Therefore, it requires the LFO complexity of
{\mathcal{O}}(\kappa n\log(1/\epsilon))
.
Since the sever-client architecture does not suffer from consensus error, it has communication complexity of
{\mathcal{O}}\left(\kappa\log(1/\epsilon)\right)
and time complexity of

{\mathcal{O}}\left(\kappa\left(1+{\tau}\right)\log(1/\epsilon)\right)
.

The objective functions for linear regression and logistic regression are not strongly convex when
d>m
, while they satisfy the PL conditions. Please refer to Section 3.2 of
Karimi et al. 2016
.
