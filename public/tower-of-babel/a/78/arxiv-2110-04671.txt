1
Symmetry protected topological phases

Phase transitions in macroscopic systems have been among central topics of study in theoretical physics.
Traditional examples of phase transitions were those between a disordered phase and an ordered phase.
It is well known that such a phase transition can be characterized by identifying a suitable order parameter, which is nothing but an extensive sum of some local operators. A notable example is the ferromagnetic phase transition in the Ising model, where the order parameter is the bulk magnetization.
This is the topic of the lecture note by Michael Aizenman in the present volume.

In recent years it has become clear that certain quantum many-body systems exhibit phase transitions between two phases which have no orders.
Such phase transitions cannot be characterized by standard order parameters, and are called “topological phase transitions”
[
No
]
.
We note that here the term “topological” should not be interpreted in its strict mathematical sense.

In the present lecture we focus on mathematical aspects of the problem of symmetry protected topological phases in quantum spin systems.
The problem has its root in Haldane’s discovery (which brought him the 2016 Nobel prize in physics
[
No
]
) that the antiferromagnetic Heisenberg chain with spin 1 has “exotic” low energy properties, essentially different from
those of its spin
1/2
counterpart.
The problem has been extensively studied from experimental, numerical, theoretical, and mathematical points of view.
After the introduction by Gu and Wen
[
GW
]
of the notion of symmetry protected topological (SPT) phases, it has become clear that the model studied by Haldane belongs to a nontrivial SPT phase characterized by an index that takes value in the second group cohomology.
The problem of SPT phases has been further developed by physicists, and it has been conjectured that SPT phases in
\nu
dimensional quantum spin systems are classified by an index that takes value in the
(\nu+1)
-th group cohomology of the relevant symmetry group.

Let us be slightly more precise.
A quantum spin system is characterized by a self-adjoint operator called Hamiltonian, which governs the time evolution of the system.
In short SPT phases are equivalence classes of Hamiltonians satisfying certain conditions on the symmetry as well as low energy properties.
Let us consider the set of all Hamiltonians with some fixed symmetry, which have a unique gapped ground state in the bulk.
In two or higher dimensions, in order to exclude models with so called “topological order”, we further assume that all Hamiltonians in the set can be smoothly
deformed into
a common trivial gapped Hamiltonian without closing the gap.
We say
two such Hamiltonians are equivalent, if they can be smoothly deformed into
each other, without breaking the symmetry.
We call an equivalence class of this classification, a
symmetry protected topological (SPT) phase.
In this lecture, we consider this problem in operator algebraic framework.
In this framework, what we deal with is not Hamiltonians, but a time evolution
(strongly continuous one parameter group of automorphisms on a
C^{*}
-algebra).
The reason for considering the problem in this framework is because
it enables us to treat infinite system directly.
It is essential to treat the infinite system directly for analysis of SPT phases for us,
because the invariant of our classification can be most naturally defined on the infinite system.
The situation is quite analogous to that of Fredholm index.
Recall that
the Fredholm index of an operator
T
(with

\dim\ker T,\dim\ker T^{*}<\infty
and
T{\mathcal{H}}
closed) on a Hilbert space
{\mathcal{H}}
is defined by

\mathop{\mathrm{ind}}\nolimits T:=\dim\ker T-\dim\ker T^{*}
.
By linear algebra, this is always
0
if
{\mathcal{H}}
is of finite dimensional.
However, it is not the case if the dimension of
\dim{\mathcal{H}}
is infinite.
For example, on
{\mathcal{H}}=l^{2}({\mathbb{N}})
, the unilateral shift

\displaystyle S\left(\xi_{1},\xi_{2},\xi_{3},\ldots\right):=\left(0,\xi_{1},\xi_{2},\xi_{3},\ldots\right),\quad\left(\xi_{1},\xi_{2},\xi_{3},\ldots\right)\in l^{2}({\mathbb{N}})

has
\ker S=\{0\}
,
\ker S^{*}={\mathbb{C}}(1,0,0,0,\ldots)
and

\mathop{\mathrm{ind}}\nolimits S=0-1=-1
.
From this, we see that in order to get non-zero Fredholm index, one need to consider infinite dimensional
Hilbert spaces.
Quite analogous phenomena occurs in SPT-theory.

In this section, we introduce the SPT-phases in operator algebraic framework.

1.1
Finite dimensional quantum mechanics

In order to motivate us for the operator algebraic framework of quantum statistical mechanics,
we first recall finite dimensional quantum mechanics in this subsection.

In finite-dimensional quantum mechanics,
physical observables are represented by
elements of
\mathop{\mathrm{M}}\nolimits_{n}
, the algebra of
n\times n
-matrices.
Each positive matrix
\rho
with
\mathop{\mathrm{Tr}}\nolimits\rho=1
(called a density matrix) defines
a physical state by

\omega_{\rho}:\mathop{\mathrm{M}}\nolimits_{n}\ni A\mapsto\mathop{\mathrm{Tr}}\nolimits\left(\rho A\right)\in{\mathbb{C}}.

We call this map
\omega_{\rho}
a sate.
This corresponds to the
expectation values of each physical observables
A\in\mathop{\mathrm{M}}\nolimits_{n}
, in the
physical state
\omega_{\rho}
.
When a state
{\omega_{\rho}}
can not be written as a convex combination of two
different states, it is said to be
pure
.
A state
\omega_{\rho}
is pure if and only if
\rho
is a rank one projection.
Time evolution is given by a self-adjoint matrix
H
,
called Hamiltonian,
via a formula

\mathop{\mathrm{M}}\nolimits_{n}\ni A\mapsto\tau_{t}(A):=e^{it{H}}Ae^{-it{H}},\quad t\in{\mathbb{R}}.

Let
p
be the spectral projection of
H
corresponding to the
lowest eigenvalue.
A state
{\omega_{\rho}}(A):=\mathop{\mathrm{Tr}}\nolimits{\rho}A
on
\mathop{\mathrm{M}}\nolimits_{n}
is said to be a ground state of
H

if the support of
\rho
is under
p
.
Ground state is unique if and only if

p
is a rank one projection, i.e., the lowest eigenvalue of
H

is non-degenerated.
In this case, the unique ground state is
\omega_{p}(A):=\mathop{\mathrm{Tr}}\nolimits{p}A
,
and it is pure because
p
is rank one.
Let
G
be a finite group and suppose that there is a group action

\beta:G\to\mathop{\mathrm{Aut}}\nolimits(\mathop{\mathrm{M}}\nolimits_{n})
given by unitaries
V_{g}
,
g\in G

{\beta_{g}}(A):=\mathop{\mathrm{Ad}}\nolimits\left({V_{g}}\right)\left(A\right),\quad A\in\mathop{\mathrm{M}}\nolimits_{d},\quad g\in G.

If a Hamiltonian
H
satisfies

{\beta_{g}}({H})={H}
for all
g\in G
,
we say

H
is
\beta
-invariant.
If a
\beta
-invariant Hamiltonian
H
has a
unique ground state
\omega_{p}(A):=\mathop{\mathrm{Tr}}\nolimits{p}A
,
then this unique ground state
\omega_{p}
is
\beta
-invariant
\omega_{p}\left(\beta_{g}(A)\right)=\omega_{p}(A)
,
A\in\mathop{\mathrm{M}}\nolimits_{n}
, because the spectral projection
p
is
\beta
-invariant,
i.e.,
\beta_{g}(p)=p
.

1.2
Basic facts about
C^{*}
-dynamical systems

In order to extend the finite dimensional quantum mechanics
to infinite ones, we move to general
C^{*}
-dynamical systems.
In general
C^{*}
-dynamical systems, physical observables are
represented by elements of a
C^{*}
-algebra.
A
C^{*}
-algebra
{\mathfrak{A}}
is a Banach
*
-algebra with norm satisfying

\left\|A^{*}A\right\|=\left\|A\right\|^{2},\quad A\in{\mathfrak{A}}.

In this lecture, we always consider
C^{*}
-algebras with a unit
\mathbb{I}
, i.e., a unital
C^{*}
-algebra.
The algebra of
n\times n
matrices
\mathop{\mathrm{M}}\nolimits_{n}
is a
C^{*}
-algebra with uniform norm

\left\|A\right\|:=\sup\left\{\left\|A\xi\right\|\mid\xi\in{\mathbb{C}}^{n},\;\left\|\xi\right\|=1\right\}.

A linear functional
\omega
over a
C^{*}
-algebra
{\mathfrak{A}}
is defined to be positive if

\omega(A^{*}A)\geq 0,

for all
A\in{\mathfrak{A}}
.
In physical settings, we regard a
C^{*}
-algebra
{\mathfrak{A}}
an algebra of physical local observables and

\omega(A)
denotes the expectation value of an observable
A\in{\mathfrak{A}}
in a state
\omega
.
A positive linear functional
\omega
on
{\mathfrak{A}}
with
\left\|\omega\right\|=1
is called a state.
A state
\omega
over a
C^{*}
-algebra
{\mathfrak{A}}
is defined to be pure
if it can not be written as a convex combination of two different states.
Recall that physical state in finite quantum mechanics is a map

\omega_{\rho}:\mathop{\mathrm{M}}\nolimits_{n}\to{\mathbb{C}}
given by a density matrix.
In fact, this
\omega_{\rho}
is a positive normalized linear functional on
\mathop{\mathrm{M}}\nolimits_{n}
, namely it is a state in the above sense.
It is pure if and only if
\rho
is a one-rank projection.

A representation of a
C^{*}
-algebra
{\mathfrak{A}}
is defined to be a pair
({\mathcal{H}},\pi)
, where
{\mathcal{H}}

is a Hilbert space and
\pi
is a
*
-homomorphism of
{\mathfrak{A}}
into
{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{H}})

(the set of all bounded operators on
{\mathcal{H}}
.)
Two representations of a
C^{*}
-algebra
{\mathfrak{A}}
,
({\mathcal{H}}_{1},\pi_{1})
,
({\mathcal{H}}_{2},\pi_{2})

are unitary equivalent if
there is a unitary
u:{\mathcal{H}}_{1}\to{\mathcal{H}}_{2}
such that

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathrm{Ad}}\nolimits(u)\circ\pi_{1}(A)=\pi_{2}(A),\quad A\in{\mathfrak{A}}.

Given a state, we may associate a corresponding representation.

Theorem 1.1
.

For each state
\omega
on
{\mathfrak{A}}
, there exist a
representation
\pi_{\omega}
of
{\mathfrak{A}}
on a Hilbert space
{\mathcal{H}}_{\omega}

and a unit vector
\Omega_{\omega}\in{\mathcal{H}}_{\omega}

such that

\displaystyle\omega(A)=\left\langle\Omega_{\omega},\pi_{\omega}(A)\Omega_{\omega}\right\rangle,\quad A\in{\mathfrak{A}},\quad\text{and}\quad{\mathcal{H}}_{\omega}=\overline{\pi_{\omega}({\mathfrak{A}})\Omega_{\omega}}.

(1.1)

Here,
\overline{\cdot}
denotes the norm closure.
It is unique up to unitary equivalence.

The latter condition in (
1.1
) means that the vector
\Omega_{\omega}
is cyclic
for
\pi_{\omega}({\mathfrak{A}})
in
{\mathcal{H}}_{\omega}
.
We use the convention of mathematical physics for inner product : namely, it is linear for the right variable.
The representation
({\mathcal{H}}_{\omega},\pi_{\omega},\Omega_{\omega})
is called a
Gelfand-Naimark-Segal (GNS)-representation/GNS-triple.

An injective
*
-homomorphism of a
C^{*}
-algebra
{\mathfrak{A}}

onto itself is called a
*
-automorphism of
{\mathfrak{A}}
.
We denote by
\mathop{\mathrm{Aut}}\nolimits({\mathfrak{A}})
, the set of all
*
-automorphisms on
{\mathfrak{A}}
.
For example, for a unitary
V\in{\mathfrak{A}}
,

\mathop{\mathrm{Ad}}\nolimits(V)(A):=VAV^{*}
,
A\in{\mathfrak{A}}

defines a
*
-automorphism on
{\mathfrak{A}}
.
Such a
*
-automorphism is called an inner automorphism.
In this lecture, we occasionally write
\alpha=({\rm{inner}})
when
\alpha=\mathop{\mathrm{Ad}}\nolimits(V)

with some unitary
V\in{\mathfrak{A}}
.
A
*
-automorphism which is not inner is said to be outer.

For a given representation
({\mathcal{H}},\pi)
of a
C^{*}
-algebra

{\mathfrak{A}}
, we say an automorphism
\alpha
of
{\mathfrak{A}}

is implementable by a unitary in
({\mathcal{H}},\pi)

if there is a unitary
u
on
{\mathcal{H}}

satisfying

\mathop{\mathrm{Ad}}\nolimits(u)\left(\pi(A)\right)=\pi\circ\alpha(A),\quad A\in{\mathfrak{A}}.

If a state
\omega
is invariant under some
\alpha\in\mathop{\mathrm{Aut}}\nolimits({\mathfrak{A}})
,
then it can be implemented in the GNS-representation by a unitary.
Namely the following theorem holds.

Theorem 1.2
.

If
\omega\circ\alpha=\omega
, then there is a unitary
V_{\alpha}
on
{\mathcal{H}}_{\omega}

such that

\displaystyle V_{\alpha}\pi_{\omega}(A)V_{\alpha}^{*}=\pi_{\omega}\left(\alpha(A)\right),\quad V_{\alpha}\Omega_{\omega}=\Omega_{\omega},\quad A\in{\mathfrak{A}}.

Note from the condition that

\displaystyle V_{\alpha}\pi_{\omega}(A)\Omega_{\omega}=\pi_{\omega}\left(\alpha(A)\right)\Omega_{\omega},\quad A\in{\mathfrak{A}}.

In fact, that is how
V_{\alpha}
is defined.

In particular, let
\gamma:G\to\mathop{\mathrm{Aut}}\nolimits({\mathfrak{A}})

be a group action of
G
on a
C^{*}
-algebra
{\mathfrak{A}}
, and suppose that
a state
\omega
is invariant under
\gamma
.
Then from the Theorem, we get
unitaries
V_{g}:=V_{\gamma_{g}}
.
It is a genuine unitary representation because

\displaystyle V_{g}V_{h}\pi_{\omega}(A)\Omega_{\omega}=\pi_{\omega}\circ\gamma_{g}\gamma_{h}(A)\Omega_{\omega}=\pi_{\omega}\circ\gamma_{gh}(A)\Omega_{\omega}=V_{gh}\pi_{\omega}(A)\Omega_{\omega}.

Using the GNS representation and the Hahn-Banach theorem,
we can show that any
C^{*}
-algebra is isomorphic to a norm-closed self-adjoint algebra of bounded operators on a Hilbert space.

For a subset
{\mathcal{M}}
of operators on a Hilbert space
{\mathcal{H}}
,
let
{\mathcal{M}}^{\prime}
denote its commutant, i.e., the set of all bounded operators on
{\mathcal{H}}

commuting with every operator in
{\mathcal{M}}
.
A von Neumann algebra on a Hilbert space
{\mathcal{H}}
is a
*
-subalgebra
{\mathcal{M}}
of
{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{H}})

(the set of all bounded operators on
{\mathcal{H}}
)
such that
{\mathcal{M}}={\mathcal{M}}^{\prime\prime}
.
A center of a von Neumann algebra is defined as
{\mathcal{Z}}({\mathcal{M}}):={\mathcal{M}}\cap{\mathcal{M}}^{\prime}
.
A von Neumann algebra
{\mathcal{M}}
is called a factor if it has a trivial center.
Given a state
\omega
on a
C^{*}
-algebra,
we obtain a von Neumann algebra
\pi_{\omega}({\mathfrak{A}})^{\prime\prime}

out of the GNS representation of
\omega
.
We call this von Neumann algebra
a von Neumann algebra associated to
\omega
.

A von Neumann algebra is said to be a type
I
-factor if it is isomorphic to

{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{H}})
for some Hilbert space
{\mathcal{H}}
.
Type
I
factors will play an important role in the analysis of SPT phases.
From this point of view, the following theorems are useful.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
{\mathcal{H}}_{1}
,
{\mathcal{H}}_{2}
be Hilbert spaces
and
\tau:{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{H}}_{1})\to{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{H}}_{2})

a
*
-isomorphism.
Then there is a unitary
u:{\mathcal{H}}_{1}\to{\mathcal{H}}_{2}

such that

\tau(x)=uxu^{*}
,
x\in{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{H}}_{1})
.

Theorem 1.4
.

The following conditions are equivalent for a state
\omega
:

(1)

The representation
({\mathcal{H}}_{\omega},\pi_{\omega})

is irreducible, i.e., the only closed subspaces of
{\mathcal{H}}_{\omega}

which are invariant under the action of
\pi_{\omega}({\mathfrak{A}})

are the trivial subspaces
\{0\}
and
{\mathcal{H}}_{\omega}
.

(2)

\omega
is pure,

(3)

\pi_{\omega}({\mathfrak{A}})^{\prime\prime}={\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{H}}_{\omega})
,

(4)

\pi_{\omega}({\mathfrak{A}})^{\prime}={\mathbb{C}}\mathbb{I}
.

Another notion which plays an important role in the analysis of SPT phases is

quasi-equivalence
.
In probability theory, a probability measure
\lambda
is absolutely continuous with respect to
another probability measure
\mu
if there is a positive
h\in L^{1}(\mu)

satisfying

\int f\;d\lambda=\int f\;h\;d\mu,

for all bounded measurable functions
f
.
Furthermore, two measures are equivalent if they are absolutely continuous to each other.
Quasi-equivalence is a non-commutative version of it.

If
({\mathcal{H}},\pi)
is a representation of a
C^{*}
-algebra
{\mathfrak{A}}
,
then a state
\varphi
of
{\mathfrak{A}}
is said to be
\pi
-normal if there exists
a positive trace-class operator
\rho
(called a density operator)
on
{\mathcal{H}}
with
\mathop{\mathrm{Tr}}\nolimits(\rho)=1

such that

\displaystyle\varphi(A)=\mathop{\mathrm{Tr}}\nolimits\left(\rho\pi(A)\right),\quad\text{for all}\quad A\in{\mathfrak{A}}.

(1.2)

Two representations
\pi_{1}
and
\pi_{2}
of a
C^{*}
-algebra
{\mathfrak{A}}
are said to be
quasi-equivalent, written
\pi_{1}\sim_{q.e.}\pi_{2}

if each
\pi_{1}
-normal state is
\pi_{2}
-normal and conversely.

Theorem 1.5
.

The following conditions are equivalent for representations
\pi_{1}
and
\pi_{2}
of
{\mathfrak{A}}
:

(1)

\pi_{1}\sim_{q.e.}\pi_{2}

(2)

there exists an isomorphism
\tau:\pi_{1}({\mathfrak{A}})^{\prime\prime}\to\pi_{2}({\mathfrak{A}})^{\prime\prime}

such that
\tau\left(\pi_{1}(A)\right)=\pi_{2}(A)

for all
A\in{\mathfrak{A}}
.

Two states
\omega_{1}
and
\omega_{2}
of
{\mathfrak{A}}
are said to be quasi-equivalent if

\pi_{\omega_{1}}
and
\pi_{\omega_{2}}
are quasi-equivalent.
Although any states on a matrix algebra are quasi-equivalent,
that is not the case for infinite systems.
Indeed, the following physical interpretation can be made.
By the cyclicity of the GNS vector
\Omega_{\omega}
in the GNS-representation (
1.1
),
if
\omega_{1}
and
\omega_{2}
are quasi-equivalent,
then

\omega_{2}
can be approximated arbitrarily well in norm topology of
{\mathfrak{A}}^{*}

by states of the form

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\omega_{1}\left(A_{n}^{*}\cdot A_{n}\right),

with some
A_{n}\in{\mathfrak{A}}
.
Regarding elements in
{\mathfrak{A}}
as local observables, it may be interpreted
that
\omega_{2}
can be obtained from
\omega_{1}
via some
local perturbation.
Because the converse also holds, heuristically we may say that
two states
\omega_{1}
and
\omega_{2}
being quasi-equivalent
means that they are macroscopically the same.

Two representations
\pi_{1}
and
\pi_{2}
of a
C^{*}
-algebra
{\mathfrak{A}}

are mutually singular if
none of
\pi_{2}
-normal state is
\pi_{1}
-normal and converse.
One can regard this as an analog of mutually singular
measures (like
\delta
-function and Lebesgue measure)
in probability theory.

Recall that a von Neumann algebra is a factor if it has a trivial center.

Theorem 1.6
.

Let
({\mathcal{H}},\pi)
be a representation of a
C^{*}
-algebra
{\mathfrak{A}}
.
If
\pi({\mathfrak{A}})^{\prime\prime}
is a factor,
then any subrepresentation of
\pi

is quasi-equivalent to
\pi
.

We say that a state
\omega
is a factor state
if associated von Neumann algebra is a factor.
Physically, it can be regarded as a
homogeneous
state, in the sense that
it cannot be written as a mixture of macroscopically distinct states (like a convex combination of
all spin up state and all spin down states).
A pure state is of course homogeneous, which even forbids mixture of macroscopically same states.

If an automorphism
\alpha
on a
C^{*}
-algebra
{\mathfrak{A}}
is implementable (by a unitary operator
u
)
in a GNS-representation

({\mathcal{H}}_{\omega},\pi_{\omega})
of a state
\omega
on
{\mathfrak{A}}
,
then
\alpha
preserves the set of
\pi_{\omega}
-normal states.
Indeed, let
\varphi
be a
\pi_{\omega}
-normal state.
Then there is a density operator
\rho
on
{\mathcal{H}}_{\omega}

satisfying

\varphi(A):=\mathop{\mathrm{Tr}}\nolimits\left(\rho\pi_{\omega}(A)\right),\quad A\in{{\mathfrak{A}}}.

We then have

\displaystyle\varphi\circ\alpha(A)=\mathop{\mathrm{Tr}}\nolimits\left(\rho\pi_{\omega}\circ\alpha(A)\right)=\mathop{\mathrm{Tr}}\nolimits\left(\rho u\pi_{\omega}(A)u^{*}\right)=\mathop{\mathrm{Tr}}\nolimits\left(\left(u^{*}\rho u\right)\pi_{\omega}(A)\right),\quad A\in{{\mathfrak{A}}}.

Hence the state
\varphi\circ\alpha
is given by
a density operator
u^{*}\rho u
.
This means that
\varphi\circ\alpha
is
\omega
-normal.
From the above interpretation of macroscopic equivalence,

being implementable on the GNS representation of
\omega

means that it does not move
\omega

macroscopically
.

We also prepare tensor product of

-algebras.

.

\omega_{{\mathfrak{A}}}

,

{\mathfrak{A}}
,

{\mathfrak{B}}

such that

\displaystyle\omega_{{\mathfrak{A}}}\alpha_{{\mathfrak{A}}}\otimes\omega_{{\mathfrak{B}}}\alpha_{{\mathfrak{B}}}=\left(\omega_{{\mathfrak{A}}}\otimes\omega_{{\mathfrak{B}}}\right)\circ\mathop{\mathrm{Ad}}\nolimits V,

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathrm{Ad}}\nolimits(w)\left(\pi_{{\mathfrak{A}}}\otimes\pi_{{\mathfrak{B}}}\right)=\left(\pi_{{\mathfrak{A}}}\otimes\pi_{{\mathfrak{B}}}\right)\left(\alpha_{{\mathfrak{A}}}\otimes\alpha_{{\mathfrak{B}}}\right).

(We absorbed
\mathop{\mathrm{Ad}}\nolimits V^{*}

.)
From this, we get

\mathop{\mathrm{Ad}}\nolimits(w)\left(\mathbb{I}_{{\mathcal{H}}_{{\mathfrak{A}}}}\otimes\pi_{{\mathfrak{B}}}({\mathfrak{B}})^{\prime\prime}\right)\subset\mathbb{I}_{{\mathcal{H}}_{{\mathfrak{A}}}}\otimes\pi_{{\mathfrak{B}}}({\mathfrak{B}})^{\prime\prime},\quad\mathop{\mathrm{Ad}}\nolimits(w^{*})\left(\mathbb{I}_{{\mathcal{H}}_{{\mathfrak{A}}}}\otimes\pi_{{\mathfrak{B}}}({\mathfrak{B}})^{\prime\prime}\right)\subset\mathbb{I}_{{\mathcal{H}}_{{\mathfrak{A}}}}\otimes\pi_{{\mathfrak{B}}}({\mathfrak{B}})^{\prime\prime}.

Hence we obtain

\mathop{\mathrm{Ad}}\nolimits(w)\left(\mathbb{I}_{{\mathcal{H}}_{{\mathfrak{A}}}}\otimes\pi_{{\mathfrak{B}}}({\mathfrak{B}})^{\prime\prime}\right)=\mathbb{I}_{{\mathcal{H}}_{{\mathfrak{A}}}}\otimes\pi_{{\mathfrak{B}}}({\mathfrak{B}})^{\prime\prime}.

Now, because
\omega_{{\mathfrak{B}}}
is a pure state, we have

\pi_{{\mathfrak{B}}}({\mathfrak{B}})^{\prime\prime}={\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{H}}_{{\mathfrak{B}}})
by
Theorem
1.4
.
This means there is a
*
-automorphism
\tau
on
{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{H}}_{{\mathfrak{B}}})

such that

\mathop{\mathrm{Ad}}\nolimits(w)\left(\mathbb{I}_{{\mathcal{H}}_{{\mathfrak{A}}}}\otimes x\right)=\mathbb{I}_{{\mathcal{H}}_{{\mathfrak{A}}}}\otimes\tau(x),\quad x\in{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{H}}_{{\mathfrak{B}}}).

By Theorem
1.3
, a
*
-isomorphism
\tau:{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{H}}_{{\mathfrak{B}}})\to{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{H}}_{{\mathfrak{B}}})

is implemented by a unitary
u_{\mathfrak{B}}\in{\mathcal{H}}_{{\mathfrak{B}}}
, i.e.

\tau(x)=\mathop{\mathrm{Ad}}\nolimits(u_{\mathfrak{B}})(x),\quad x\in{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{H}}_{{\mathfrak{B}}}).

Hence from all the equations above, we obtain

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\left(\mathbb{I}_{{\mathcal{H}}_{{\mathfrak{A}}}}\otimes\pi_{{\mathfrak{B}}}\alpha_{{\mathfrak{B}}}(A)\right)=\mathop{\mathrm{Ad}}\nolimits(w)\left(\mathbb{I}_{{\mathcal{H}}_{{\mathfrak{A}}}}\otimes\pi_{{\mathfrak{B}}}(A)\right)\\
&=\mathbb{I}_{{\mathcal{H}}_{{\mathfrak{A}}}}\otimes\tau\pi_{{\mathfrak{B}}}(A)=\mathbb{I}_{{\mathcal{H}}_{{\mathfrak{A}}}}\otimes\mathop{\mathrm{Ad}}\nolimits(u_{\mathfrak{B}})\pi_{{\mathfrak{B}}}(A),\quad A\in{\mathfrak{B}}.\end{split}

This proves the Lemma.

\square

1.3
Quantum spin systems

Let
d\in{\mathbb{N}}
and
\nu\in{\mathbb{N}}
be fixed.
Physically,
\frac{d-1}{2}
denotes
the size of on-site spin (spin quantum number) and

\nu
denotes the spacial dimension.
We denote by
{\mathfrak{S}}_{{\mathbb{Z}}^{\nu}}
, the set of all finite subsets of
{\mathbb{Z}}^{\nu}
.
For each finite subset
\Lambda\in{\mathfrak{S}}_{{\mathbb{Z}}^{\nu}}
,
we associate a finite dimensional
C^{*}
-algebra

\displaystyle{\mathcal{A}}_{\Lambda}:=\bigotimes_{\Lambda}\mathop{\mathrm{M}}\nolimits_{d}.

Here,
\mathop{\mathrm{M}}\nolimits_{d}
is the algebra of
d\times d
-matrices.
If
\Lambda_{1},\Lambda_{2}\in{\mathfrak{S}}_{{\mathbb{Z}}^{\nu}}

satisfy an inclusion relation
\Lambda_{1}\subset\Lambda_{2}
,
then we may embed
{\mathcal{A}}_{\Lambda_{1}}
into
{\mathcal{A}}_{\Lambda_{2}}
as

\displaystyle\iota_{\Lambda_{1},\Lambda_{2}}:{\mathcal{A}}_{\Lambda_{1}}\to{\mathcal{A}}_{\Lambda_{2}},\quad\iota_{\Lambda_{1},\Lambda_{2}}(A):=A\otimes\mathbb{I}_{\Lambda_{2}\setminus\Lambda_{1}},\quad A\in{\mathcal{A}}_{\Lambda_{1}}.

Clearly, we have
\iota_{\Lambda_{2},\Lambda_{3}}\iota_{\Lambda_{1},\Lambda_{2}}=\iota_{\Lambda_{1},\Lambda_{3}}
if
\Lambda_{1}\subset\Lambda_{2}\subset\Lambda_{3}
.
Then the
\nu
-dimensional quantum spin system
{\mathcal{A}}_{{\mathbb{Z}}^{\nu}}
is the

C^{*}
-inductive limit of this inductive net
(\iota_{\Lambda_{1},\Lambda_{2}},{\mathcal{A}}_{\Lambda})
. Namely,

{\mathcal{A}}_{{\mathbb{Z}}^{\nu}}
is the unique
C^{*}
-algebra satisfying the following conditions.

(i)

There is a net of
*
-homomorphisms

\mu_{\Lambda}:{\mathcal{A}}_{\Lambda}\to{\mathcal{A}}_{{\mathbb{Z}}^{\nu}}
, with
\Lambda\in{\mathfrak{S}}_{{\mathbb{Z}}^{\nu}}
,
such that
\mu_{\Lambda_{1}}=\mu_{\Lambda_{2}}\circ\iota_{\Lambda_{1},\Lambda_{2}}

if

\Lambda_{1}\subset\Lambda_{2}
, and

(ii)

If
({\mathcal{B}},\{\lambda_{\Lambda}\}_{\Lambda\in{\mathfrak{S}}_{{\mathbb{Z}}^{\nu}}})
is a system,
where
{\mathcal{B}}
is a
C^{*}
-algebra,
\lambda_{\Lambda}:{\mathcal{A}}_{\Lambda}\to{\mathcal{B}}

is a
*
-homomorphism for each
\Lambda\in{\mathfrak{S}}_{{\mathbb{Z}}^{\nu}}
, and where
\lambda_{\Lambda_{1}}=\lambda_{\Lambda_{2}}\circ\iota_{\Lambda_{1},\Lambda_{2}}
for any

\Lambda_{1},\Lambda_{2}\in{\mathfrak{S}}_{{\mathbb{Z}}^{\nu}}

with
\Lambda_{1}\subset\Lambda_{2}
,
then there is one and only one
*
-homomorphism
\lambda:{\mathcal{A}}_{{\mathbb{Z}}^{\nu}}\to{\mathcal{B}}

such that
\lambda_{\Lambda}=\lambda\circ\mu_{\Lambda}
.

The subset
\cup_{\Lambda\in{\mathfrak{S}}_{{\mathbb{Z}}^{\nu}}}\mu_{\Lambda}\left({\mathcal{A}}_{\Lambda}\right)
,
which is dense in

{\mathcal{A}}_{{\mathbb{Z}}^{\nu}}
, is called a local algebra, and we denote it by
{\mathcal{A}}_{\rm loc}
.
Because
\mu_{\Lambda}
is injective, we identify

{\mathcal{A}}_{\Lambda}
and
\mu_{\Lambda}\left({\mathcal{A}}_{\Lambda}\right)

and omit the symbol
\mu_{\Lambda}

For each infinite subset
\Gamma
, we may define
{\mathcal{A}}_{\Gamma}

in exactly the same manner.
The
C^{*}
-algebra
{\mathcal{A}}_{\Gamma}
can be regarded as a
C^{*}
-subalgebra
of
{\mathcal{A}}_{{\mathbb{Z}}^{\nu}}
that we will regard it so.
We say an element
A
has a support in
\Gamma
if
it belongs to
{\mathcal{A}}_{\Gamma}
.
If an automorphism
\alpha
acts trivially on
{\mathcal{A}}_{\Gamma^{c}}

for some
\Gamma\subset{\mathbb{Z}}^{\nu}
,
we say that
\alpha
has a support in
\Gamma
.
In the analysis of SPT-phases, this support becomes important.

Corresponding to the space translation of
{\mathbb{Z}}^{\nu}
,
we may define a
{\mathbb{Z}}^{\nu}
-action
\tau:{\mathbb{Z}}^{\nu}\to\mathop{\mathrm{Aut}}\nolimits\left({\mathcal{A}}_{{\mathbb{Z}}^{\nu}}\right)

on
{\mathcal{A}}_{{\mathbb{Z}}^{\nu}}

by

\tau_{\bf y}\left(A^{(\bf x)}\right)=\left(A^{(\bf x+y)}\right)

for
A\in\mathop{\mathrm{M}}\nolimits_{d}
and
{\bf x,y}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{\nu}
.
Here
A^{(\bf x)}
indicates an element in

{\mathcal{A}}_{{\mathbb{Z}}^{\nu}}
with
A
in the
\bf x
-site of the tensor product of

{\mathcal{A}}_{{\mathbb{Z}}^{\nu}}
and the unit in any other site.
This
\tau
is called the space translation on
{\mathcal{A}}_{{\mathbb{Z}}^{\nu}}
.

In operator algebraic framework, physical models are specified with a map
called
interactions
.
An interaction
\Phi
is a map

\Phi:{\mathfrak{S}}_{{\mathbb{Z}}^{\nu}}\to{\mathcal{A}}_{\rm loc}

satisfying

\displaystyle\Phi(X)=\Phi(X)^{*}\in{\mathcal{A}}_{X}

for all
X\in{\mathfrak{S}}_{{\mathbb{Z}}^{\nu}}
.
Physically, this
\Phi(X)
indicates an interaction term between
spins inside of
X
.
An interaction
\Phi
is said to be translationally invariant,
if it satisfies the following condition

\tau_{\bf x}\left(\Phi(X)\right)=\Phi(X+{\bf x}),\quad A\in{\mathfrak{S}}_{{\mathbb{Z}}^{\nu}},\quad{\bf x}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{\nu}.

The easiest type of interaction is an
on-site interaction
, satisfying

\displaystyle\Phi(X)=0,\quad\text{if}\quad|X|\neq 1.

(1.5)

It means, the only possibly non-zero interaction terms are of the form

\Phi(\{\bf x\})
, with
{\bf x}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{\nu}
.
In this case, all interaction terms commute with each other.

Physically, we are more interested in interactions which do have non-zero
interaction terms between different sites of
{\mathbb{Z}}^{\nu}
.
For example, let

\{S_{j}\}_{j=1,2,3}
be generators of the irreducible representation of
{\mathfrak{su}(2)}
on
{\mathbb{C}}^{d}
.
Then an interaction of
{\mathcal{A}}_{{\mathbb{Z}}^{\nu}}
given by

\displaystyle\Phi(\{x,x+1\})=\sum_{j=1}^{3}\,S^{(x)}_{j}S^{(x+1)}_{j},

(1.6)

is called antiferromagnetic Heisenberg chain and has been extensively studied.

Now, given an interaction,
we would like to define a dynamics on
{\mathcal{A}}_{{\mathbb{Z}}^{\nu}}
out of it.
In order for that, we need to assume that
\Phi
is “suitably local”.
The simplest one among such good conditions is
uniformly bounded and finite range
.
An interaction is
of finite range if there exists an
m\in{\mathbb{N}}
such that

\Phi(X)=0
for
X
with a diameter larger than
m
.
It is uniformly bounded if it satisfies

\displaystyle\sup_{X\in{\mathfrak{S}}_{{\mathbb{Z}}^{\nu}}}\left\|\Phi(X)\right\|<\infty.

We can extensively relax these conditions. See
[
NSY
]
.

Given a good interaction, we may define a
C^{*}
-dynamics, i.e., strongly continuous one parameter group of
automorphisms on
{\mathcal{A}}_{{\mathbb{Z}}^{\nu}}
.
For an interaction
\Phi
and a finite set
\Lambda\subset{{\mathbb{Z}}^{\nu}}
, we define the local Hamiltonian
by

\left(H_{\Phi}\right)_{\Lambda}:=\sum_{X\subset{\Lambda}}\Phi(X).

(1.7)

For a uniformly bounded finite range interaction
\Phi
, the limit

\alpha_{t}^{\Phi}(A)=\lim_{\Lambda\to{\mathbb{Z}}^{\nu}}e^{it(H_{\Phi})_{\Lambda}}Ae^{-it(H_{\Phi})_{\Lambda}},\quad t\in{\mathbb{R}},\quad A\in{\mathcal{A}}_{{\mathbb{Z}}^{\nu}}

(1.8)

exists and defines a dynamics
\alpha^{\Phi}
on
{\mathcal{A}}_{{\mathbb{Z}}^{\nu}}
.
The reason for us to consider the dynamics
\alpha^{\Phi}

instead of Hamiltonians, is because there is no
mathematically meaningful limit of local Hamiltonians
\left(H_{\Phi}\right)_{\Lambda}

as
\Lambda\to{\mathbb{Z}}^{\nu}
in (
1.7
).
But
the limit (
1.8
) makes sense.
For the same reason, a ground state is defined in terms of the dynamics
\alpha_{\Phi}
.

Definition 1.9
.

Let
\delta_{\Phi}
be the generator of
\alpha^{\Phi}
.
A state
\omega
on
{\mathcal{A}}_{{\mathbb{Z}}^{\nu}}
is called an
\alpha^{\Phi}
-ground state
if the inequality

-i\omega\left(A^{*}{\delta_{\Phi}}\left(A\right)\right)\geq 0

holds
for any element
A
in the domain
{\mathcal{D}}({\delta_{\Phi}})
of
{\delta_{\Phi}}
.

We occasionally say ground state of
\Phi
instead of
\alpha^{\Phi}
-ground state.

Let
({\mathcal{H}}_{\varphi},\pi_{\varphi},\Omega_{\varphi})
be the GNS triple of a
\alpha^{\Phi}
-ground state
\varphi
.
Then there exists a unique positive operator
H_{\varphi,\Phi}
on
{\mathcal{H}}_{\varphi}
such that

e^{itH_{\varphi,\Phi}}\pi_{\varphi}(A)\Omega_{\varphi}=\pi_{\varphi}(\tau_{t}^{\Phi}(A))\Omega_{\varphi}
,
for all
A\in{\mathcal{A}}
and
t\in\mathbb{R}
.
We call this
H_{\varphi,\Phi}
the bulk Hamiltonian associated with
\varphi
.
Note that
\Omega_{\varphi}
is an eigenvector of
H_{\varphi,\Phi}
with eigenvalue
0
. See
[
BR2
]
for the general theory.

Let us consider the corresponding condition for a finite quantum system
\mathop{\mathrm{M}}\nolimits_{n}
with dynamics
given by a Hamiltonian
H

\displaystyle\alpha_{t}(A)=e^{itH}Ae^{-itH},\quad t\in{\mathbb{R}},\quad A\in{\mathop{\mathrm{M}}\nolimits_{n}}.

(1.9)

Let
P
be the spectral projection of
H
corresponding to the lowest eigenvalue
E_{0}
.
Recall that
a state
\omega
on
\mathop{\mathrm{M}}\nolimits_{n}
is given by a density matrix
\rho

with the formula
\omega(A)=\mathop{\mathrm{Tr}}\nolimits\rho A
.
Let
s(\omega)
be the range projection of this
\rho
.
Then one can check that
\omega

is an
\alpha
-ground state if and only if
the support
s(\omega)
of
\omega
satisfies
s(\omega)\leq P
.
Recall that this is the very definition of ground state, in finite quantum mechanics.
In fact note that the generator
\delta
of
\alpha
in (
1.9
) is

\delta(A)=i[H,A]
.
If
s(\omega)\leq P
, then we have

\displaystyle-i\omega\left(A^{*}{\delta}\left(A\right)\right)=\omega\left(A^{*}(H-E_{0})A\right)\geq 0,\quad A\in\mathop{\mathrm{M}}\nolimits_{n}.

Conversely, suppose that
-i\omega\left(A^{*}{\delta}\left(A\right)\right)\geq 0

for all
A\in\mathop{\mathrm{M}}\nolimits_{n}
. For unit eigenvectors
\xi,\eta
of
H
with
H\xi=E_{0}\xi
,
H\eta=E\eta
, for
E>E_{0}
,
set
A\in\mathop{\mathrm{M}}\nolimits_{n}
to be a matrix satisfying
A\zeta=\left\langle\eta,\zeta\right\rangle\xi
for any
\zeta\in{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
.
Substituting this
A
, we get

\displaystyle 0\leq-i\omega\left(A^{*}{\delta}\left(A\right)\right)=\left(E_{0}-E\right)\left\langle\eta,\rho\eta\right\rangle

Because
E_{0}-E<0
, this means that
\left\langle\eta,\rho\eta\right\rangle=0
for any such
\eta
.
Hence we conclude that
\rho P=\rho
, namely,
s(\omega)\leq P
.

Therefore, our definition in operator algebraic framework can be regarded
as a generalization of this physical definition of a ground state.

Note, in general, there can be many states satisfying the condition in Definition
1.9
.
Namely, the ground state does not need to be unique.
However, what we are interested in this lecture is the situation that it is unique.
Furthermore, we require the existence of gap.

Definition 1.10
.

Suppose that there is a unique
\alpha^{\Phi}
-ground state
\omega_{\Phi}
.
Then we say
\Phi
has a unique gapped ground state in the bulk if
there exists a
\gamma>0
such that

\displaystyle-i\omega_{\Phi}\left(A^{*}{\delta_{\Phi}}\left(A\right)\right)\geq\gamma\omega_{\Phi}(A^{*}A),\quad\text{for all }A\in{\mathcal{D}}({\delta_{\Phi}})\;\text{with}\;\omega_{\Phi}(A)=0.

(1.10)

Let us consider the corresponding condition for a finite system
\mathop{\mathrm{M}}\nolimits_{n}
with dynamics
(
1.9
).
Then the above condition corresponds to the situation
that “the lowest eigenvalue of
H
is non-degenerated and
the difference between the lowest eigenvalue and the second lowest eigenvalue is at least
\gamma
”.

Let
\Lambda_{N}:=[-N,N]^{\nu}\cap{\mathbb{Z}}^{\nu}
.
Let
\omega_{N,\Phi}
be a ground state of the local Hamiltonian

\left(H_{\Phi}\right)_{\Lambda_{N}}
on
{\mathcal{A}}_{\Lambda_{N}}
.
Then, any wk
*
-accumulation point of
\omega_{N,\Phi}

for
N\to\infty
is an
\alpha^{\Phi}
-ground state.
For this reason, there is always an
\alpha^{\Phi}
-ground state.
Let
\sigma\left(\left(H_{\Phi}\right)_{\Lambda_{N}}\right)

be the spectrum of
\left(H_{\Phi}\right)_{\Lambda_{N}}
,
and
E_{N}
its infimum.
Suppose that there is a
\gamma>0

such that

\sigma\left(\left(H_{\Phi}\right)_{\Lambda_{N}}\right)\setminus\{E_{N}\}\subset[E_{N}+\gamma,\infty)

holds
for any
N\in{\mathbb{N}}
.
If furthermore the
\alpha^{\Phi}
-ground state
\omega_{\Phi}
is unique,
then we have

\omega_{\Phi}={\mathrm{wk}}*-\lim_{N\to\infty}\omega_{N,\Phi}

and it is a unique gapped ground state with gap at least
\gamma
.

One remarkable property of unique gapped ground state is the exponential decay of correlation functions.

Theorem 1.11

(
[
HK
]

[
NS06
]

[
NS09
]
)
.

Let
\Phi
be a uniformly bounded finite
range interaction with a unique gapped ground state
\omega_{\Phi}
.
Then the correlation functions of
\omega_{\Phi}

decay exponentially fast : there exists
\mu>0
and a constant

C>0
such that for all
A\in{\mathcal{A}}_{X}
,

B\in{\mathcal{A}}_{Y}
, with finite
X,Y\subset{\mathbb{Z}}^{\nu}

\left|\omega_{\Phi}\left(AB\right)-\omega_{\Phi}(A)\omega_{\Phi}(B)\right|\leq C\left\|A\right\|\left\|B\right\||X|{e^{-\mu d(X,Y)}}.

This means

is “almost like a product state”.

In the theory of SPT-phases, we are interested in interactions satisfying some given symmetry.
In this lecture, we consider on-site finite group symmetry in
one and two-dimensional quantum spin systems
and reflection symmetry in one dimensional quantum systems.

On-site finite group symmetry is given as follows.
We fix a finite group
G
and a (projective) unitary representation
U
of
G

on
{\mathbb{C}}^{d}
.
Then there is a unique automorphism
\beta_{g}
satisfying

\displaystyle\beta_{g}(A)=\left(\bigotimes_{x\in\Lambda}U(g)\right)A\left(\bigotimes_{x\in\Lambda}U(g)^{*}\right),\;g\in G,\;\Lambda\in{\mathfrak{S}}_{{\mathbb{Z}}^{\nu}},\;A\in{\mathcal{A}}_{\Lambda}.

Clearly, this gives an action of
G
on
{\mathcal{A}}_{{\mathbb{Z}}^{\nu}}
, i.e.,
\beta_{g}\beta_{h}=\beta_{gh}

for
g,h\in G
.
We call this action of
G
, an on-site symmetry given by
G
and
U
.
Likewise, for each subset
\Gamma\subset{\mathbb{Z}}^{\nu}
,
we may define an action
\beta_{g}^{\Gamma}
.
We say an interaction
\Phi
is
\beta
-invariant
if
\beta_{g}(\Phi(X))=\Phi(X)

for all
X\in{\mathfrak{S}}_{{\mathbb{Z}}^{\nu}}
and
g\in G
.
For a ground state
\varphi
of a
\beta
-invariant interaction
\Phi
, one can check that

\varphi\circ\beta_{g}
is also a ground state of
\Phi
.
Therefore, if a
\beta
-invariant interaction
\Phi

has a unique ground state
\omega_{\Phi}
, the ground state is

\beta
-invariant,
\omega_{\Phi}\circ\beta_{g}=\omega_{\Phi}
.

For example, let
U
be a projective representation of

G={\mathbb{Z}}_{2}\times{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}
on
{\mathbb{C}}^{d}
, given by

\displaystyle U\left([1],[0]\right)=e^{i\pi S_{1}},\quad U\left([0],[1]\right)=e^{i\pi S_{2}},\quad U\left([1],[1]\right)=e^{i\pi S_{3}}

Here,
\{S_{j}\}_{j=1,2,3}
are generators of the irreducible representation of
{\mathfrak{su}(2)}
on
{\mathbb{C}}^{d}
.
If
d
is odd, this gives a genuine representation while
if
d
is even, it is a projective representation.
One can check that antiferromagnetic Heisenberg chain (
1.6
)
is invariant under this symmetry.

Reflection symmetry in one dimensional quantum systems is given as follows.
Let
R:{\mathbb{Z}}\to{\mathbb{Z}}
be the reflection :
R(i):=-i-1
,
i\in{\mathbb{Z}}
, and
\Theta_{R}\in\mathop{\mathrm{Aut}}\nolimits({\mathcal{A}}_{{\mathbb{Z}}})

an automorphism corresponding to
R
.
More precisely,
\Theta_{R}
is defined as follows.
By
Q^{(j)}
,
j\in{\mathbb{Z}}
, we denote the element of
{\mathcal{A}}_{{\mathbb{Z}}}

with
Q\in\mathop{\mathrm{M}}\nolimits_{d}
in the
j
-th component of the tensor product of
{\mathcal{A}}_{{\mathbb{Z}}}
and the unit in any other component.
The reflection
\Theta_{R}
is the unique
*
-automorphism on
{\mathcal{A}}_{{\mathbb{Z}}}

which satisfies

\displaystyle\Theta_{R}\left(Q^{(j)}\right)=Q^{(-j-1)},\quad\text{for all}\;Q\in\mathop{\mathrm{M}}\nolimits_{d}\;\text{and}\;j\in{\mathbb{Z}}.

An interaction
\Phi
is reflection invariant
if
\Theta_{R}(\Phi(X))=\Phi(R(X))

for all
X\in{\mathfrak{S}}_{\mathbb{Z}}
.
One can check that antiferromagnetic Heisenberg chain (
1.6
)
is invariant under this symmetry.

1.4
SPT-phases

We denote the set of all uniformly bounded finite range interactions with
unique gapped ground state by
{\mathcal{P}}_{U.G.}
.
First, we introduce an equivalence relation
\sim
in this set
{\mathcal{P}}_{U.G.}

as follows.:

Definition 1.12

(Definition of
\sim
)
.

Two interactions
\Phi_{0},\Phi_{1}\in{\mathcal{P}}_{U.G.}
are equivalent (denoted as
\Phi_{0}\sim\Phi_{1}
)
if there is a path

{\mathcal{P}}_{U.G.}

in
\Phi:[0,1]\to{\mathcal{P}}_{U.G.}

satisfying the following conditions.

(i)

The path connects given interactions, i.e.,
\Phi(0)=\Phi_{0}
and
\Phi(1)=\Phi_{1}
.

(ii)

For each
X\in{\mathfrak{S}}_{{\mathbb{Z}}^{\nu}}
,
the matrix-valued function
[0,1]\ni s\mapsto\Phi_{s}(X)\in{\mathcal{A}}_{X}
is smooth,

(iii)

The gap is uniformly bounded from below by some
\gamma>0
along the path.
Namely, for each
s\in[0,1]
, there is a
\gamma_{s}>0
satisfying (
1.10
)
for
\Phi=\Phi(s)
, and
\inf_{s\in[0,1]}\gamma_{s}>0
.

(iv)

The path of expectation values
[0,1]\ni s\mapsto\omega_{s}(A)\in{\mathbb{C}}
of sub-exponentially localized
elements

A\in{\mathcal{A}}_{{\mathbb{Z}}^{\nu}}
with respect to the ground state
\omega_{s}
is regular
(in the sense that it is differentiable and the derivative is not too large compared to
some norm of
A
)
with respect to
s\in[0,1]
.

For more precise description of the conditions on the path, see subsection
1.6
Assumption
1.21
Assumption
1.25
.
A part of these assumptions are for technical reason, namely we would like to apply
Theorem
1.26
.
Physically, this condition means that
there is no phase transition along the path
.

Among many interactions in
{\mathcal{P}}_{U.G.}
, what corresponds to SPT is
the ones
with short-range entanglement
.
Recall the definition of on-site interaction (
1.5
).
Fix some on-site interaction
\Phi_{0}
which belongs to

{\mathcal{P}}_{U.G.}
.
Such an interaction can be constructed as follows.
For each
x\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{\nu}
, fix any unit vector
\xi_{x}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{d}
.
Let
p_{x}
be an orthogonal projection on
{\mathbb{C}}^{d}
onto the subspace

{\mathbb{C}}\xi_{x}
.
Set
\Phi_{0}
as

\displaystyle\Phi_{0}\left(\{x\}\right)=\mathbb{I}-p_{x},\quad x\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{\nu},

and
\Phi_{0}(X)=0
for any other
X\in{\mathfrak{S}}_{{\mathbb{Z}}^{\nu}}
.
Then
\Phi_{0}\in{\mathcal{P}}_{U.G.}

and its unique ground state is of the form

\displaystyle\omega_{\Phi_{0}}=\bigotimes_{x\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{\nu}}\rho_{\xi_{x}},

where
\rho_{\xi_{x}}
is a vector state on
\mathop{\mathrm{M}}\nolimits_{d}

given by
\rho_{\xi_{x}}(A)=\left\langle\xi_{x},A\xi_{x}\right\rangle
.
Note that any on-site interaction can be smoothly deformed into
an on-site interaction of this form inside of
{\mathcal{P}}_{U.G.}
,
just by smoothly flattering the spectrum of each
\Phi(\{x\})
.
Furthermore, any on-site interactions of this form can be deformed
into each other by rotating
\xi_{x}
smoothly.
Therefore, the following set does not depend on the choice of
\Phi_{0}
:

\displaystyle{\mathcal{P}}_{U.G.}^{0}:=\left\{\Phi\in{\mathcal{P}}_{U.G.}\mid\Phi\sim\Phi_{0}.\right\}.

As we see in the next subsection, this set physically represents
interactions whose unique gapped ground state is
short-range entangled
.
This excludes
exotic states with “topological order” (or long-range entanglement) such as the ground states of the Kitaev toric code model
[
Ki
]
. In general, a pure state with a non-trivial super selection sector
has long-range entanglement
[
NO
]
.

Now we introduce the on-site symmetry
\beta
to the game.
We are interested in
the set

\displaystyle{\mathcal{P}}_{U.G.{{\beta}}}^{0}:=\left\{\Phi\in{\mathcal{P}}_{U.G.}^{0}\mid\beta\text{-invariant}\right\}.

(1.11)

We introduce an equivalence relation
\sim_{\beta}
on
this set
{\mathcal{P}}_{U.G.{{\beta}}}^{0}
.

Definition 1.13

(Definition of
\sim_{\beta}
)
.

Two interactions
\Phi_{0},\Phi_{1}\in{\mathcal{P}}_{U.G.{{\beta}}}^{0}
are equivalent (denoted as
\Phi_{0}\sim_{\beta}\Phi_{1}
)
if there is a path
in
\Phi:[0,1]\to{\mathcal{P}}_{U.G.{{\beta}}}^{0}

satisfying the conditions (i)-(iv) of Definition
1.12
.

The equivalence class of
{\mathcal{P}}_{U.G.{{\beta}}}^{0}

with this classification are the SPT-phases.

Definition 1.14

(Symmetry Protected Topological (SPT) phases)
.

Each equivalence class of
{{\mathcal{P}}}_{U.G.{\beta}}^{0}

with respect to
\sim_{\beta}
is called
Symmetry Protected Topological (SPT) phases.

This notion of SPT-phases was introduced by Gu and Wen in
[
GW
]
.
This is the object we would like to classify.
Analogously, we can define SPT phases
{\mathcal{P}}_{U.G.{{\Theta_{R}}}}^{0}

for the reflection symmetry
\Theta_{R}
.

1.5
Split property

The split property, studied extensively in operator algebra has turned out to be quite important
in analysis of SPT phases.
In this section we introduce the notion.

Let us consider an inclusion of two von Neumann algebras
{\mathcal{N}}\subset{\mathcal{M}}
.
We say the inclusion is

I
factor
{\mathcal{R}}

such that
{\mathcal{N}}\subset{\mathcal{R}}\subset{\mathcal{M}}

The inclusion which shows up in this lecture is the simplest

{\mathcal{N}}
itself is a type

In particular, we will use the following terminology in

Definition 1.15

-
\Gamma_{2}

(Theorem 2.30 V
[

1.6
Automorphic equivalence

For the investigation of the equivalence relation
\sim
, automorphic equivalence
on smooth path of interactions plays an important role.
Automorphic equivalence started as Hasting’s adiabatic Lemma
[
H2
]
[
HW
]
.
After seminal mathematical development in
[
BMNS
]

[
NSY
]
,
it is now a strong mathematical machinery to analyze
gapped state phases.
It basically says the following : given a smooth path of gapped local Hamiltonians,
there is a
concrete
smooth path of automorphisms
interpolating ground state spaces.
Original Hastings’s adiabatic Lemma was about finite systems.
In the framework given by
[
BMNS
]

[
NSY
]
,
the existence of gap for
finite
systems is assumed as well, and
the thermodynamic limit taken afterwords.
An infinite version, assuming the gap in the
infinite
system
was derived in
[
MO
]
.

In this subsection, we review Hasting’s original adiabatic Lemma,
and introduce its infinite dimensional version.
Let

H:[0,1]\ni s\to H(s)\in\mathop{\mathrm{M}}\nolimits_{n}

be a smooth path of self-adjoint matrices of size
n
.
In physical settings this
H(s)
is a Hamiltonian.
We consider the situation that there is a gap
between the lowest eigenvalue of
H(s)

and the rest of the spectrum, which is uniform in
s
.
What we are interested in is the lowest eigenvalue eigenspace
of the Hamiltonian i.e, the ground state space.
For each parameter
s\in[0,1]
,
let
E(s)
be the lowest eigenvalue of
H(s)

and
P(s)
be the spectral projection of
H(s)

corresponding to the eigenvalue
E(s)
.
As we saw in subsection
1.3
,
a ground state of
H(s)

is a positive linear functional on
\mathop{\mathrm{M}}\nolimits_{n}

with support under
P(s)
.
We assume that there is a constant
\gamma>0

such that

\sigma(H(s))\setminus\{E(s)\}\subset[E(s)+\gamma,\infty)

for any
s\in[0,1]
.
Here
\sigma(H(s))
indicates the spectrum of
H(s)
.
Namely, we require that there is no cross-over of the ground state energy along the path.
The Hastings adiabatic Lemma is that there is a path of unitaries
following an
explicit differential equation
, which
maps
P(0)
, to
P(s)
.

Theorem 1.20

(Hastings
[
H2
]
)
.

In the setting given above,
there is a smooth path of unitaries
U:[0,1]\to\mathop{\mathrm{M}}\nolimits_{n}

such that

\displaystyle\begin{split}&P(s)=U(s)P(0)U(s)^{*},\quad s\in[0,1],\\
&\frac{d}{ds}U(s)=iD(s)U(s),\quad U(0)=\mathbb{I},\\
&D(s):=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dtW_{\gamma}(t)e^{itH(s)}H^{\prime}(s)e^{-itH(s)}.\end{split}

Here,
W_{\gamma}
is a
L^{1}
-function on
{\mathbb{R}}

with Fourier transformation
satisfying

\displaystyle\hat{W}_{\gamma}(k)=-\frac{i}{\sqrt{2\pi}k},\quad k\notin(-\gamma,\gamma).

(1.13)

This theorem says that there is a smooth path of unitaries
which maps ground state space
P(0)
to
P(s)
.
An important point for application
is that the path of unitaries is given by quite a concrete differential equation.
For the choice of
W_{\gamma}
,
there is some freedom.
The proof goes as follows.
Let
s_{0}\in[0,1]
and
\Gamma
be a contour on
{\mathbb{C}}

having
E(s)
inside and
and
\sigma(H(s))\setminus\{E(s)\}
outside
for
s
near
s_{0}
.
Then our
P(s)
can be represented as

\displaystyle P(s):=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\Gamma}dz(z-H(s))^{-1}

near
s_{0}
.
Now we derive a differential equation satisfied by
P(s)
.
In order to do so, we first simply differentiate the above representation of

P(s)
.
We then obtain

\displaystyle\frac{d}{ds}P(s)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\Gamma}dz(z-H(s))^{-1}H^{\prime}(s)(z-H(s))^{-1}.

(1.14)

Now we make a very simple but key observation about a
path of orthogonal projetions. That is

\displaystyle P(s)P^{\prime}(s)P(s)=(1-P(s))P^{\prime}(s)(1-P(s))=0.

(1.15)

This can be obtained by differentiating
P(s)^{2}=P(s)
,

\displaystyle P(S)P^{\prime}(s)+P^{\prime}(s)P(s)=P^{\prime}(s).

Multiplying
P(s)
(resp.
1-P(s)
) from left and right,
we obtain (
1.15
).
Therefore, we can rewrite (
1.14
), picking up off-diagonal
terms:

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\frac{d}{ds}P(s)\\
&=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\Gamma}dz\left(\begin{gathered}P(s)(z-H(s))^{-1}H^{\prime}(s)(z-H(s))^{-1}\left(1-P(s)\right)\\
+(1-P(s))(z-H(s))^{-1}H^{\prime}(s)(z-H(s))^{-1}P(s)\end{gathered}\right)\\
&=P(s)H^{\prime}(s)\frac{1}{2\pi i}\left(\int_{\Gamma}dz(z-E(s))^{-1}(z-H(s))^{-1}\left(1-P(s)\right)\right)\\
&\quad+\frac{1}{2\pi i}\left(\int_{\Gamma}dz(1-P(s))(z-E(s))^{-1}(z-H(s))^{-1}\right)H^{\prime}(s)P(s)\\
&=P(s)H^{\prime}(s)(E(s)-H(s))^{-1}\left(1-P(s)\right)+(1-P(s))(E(s)-H(s))^{-1}H^{\prime}(s)P(s).\end{split}

In the last line we used the fact that

\Gamma

has
E(s)
inside and
and
\sigma(H(s))\setminus\{E(s)\}
outside.
On the other hand, using the property of the Fourier transform (
1.13
),
the last term can be written in terms of
D(s)
as

\displaystyle P(s)H^{\prime}(s)(E(s)-H(s))^{-1}\left(1-P(s)\right)+(1-P(s))(E(s)-H(s))^{-1}H^{\prime}(s)P(s)

\displaystyle=i\left[D(s),P(s)\right].

Hence
P(s)
satisfies the differential equation

\displaystyle\frac{d}{ds}P(s)=i\left[D(s),P(s)\right].

This proves the Theorem.

Now we would like to apply this Theorem to the Heisenberg dynamics given by time-dependent interactions.

Assume that

in the setting of Theorem

\omega_{s}:=\mathop{\mathrm{Tr}}\nolimits P(s)(\cdot)

It suffices to show that

From the definition of

\displaystyle 0=\dot{\omega}_{s}\circ\alpha_{s}^{-1}(A)+\omega_{s}\left(i\left[D(s),\alpha_{s}^{-1}(A)\right]\right).

The question is what is
\dot{\omega}_{s}
.
To consider it,

is a ground state of

Therefore, we have

\omega_{s}\circ\left(i[H(s),\cdot]\right)=0.

Differentiating this, we obtain

\displaystyle\dot{\omega}_{s}\left(i[H(s),A]\right)+\omega_{s}\left(i[\dot{H}(s),A]\right)=0.

If

Unfortunately,

In order to solve the issue, an operation called Hasting’s operator

Let

be a non-negative

-function
such that

\mathop{\mathrm{supp}}\nolimits\hat{w}_{\gamma}\subset(-\gamma,\gamma),\quad\text{and}\quad\int dtw_{\gamma}(t)=1.

In fact we may take this

and

(in Theorem

so that vanishing at infinity and

\frac{d}{dt}W_{\gamma}(t)=-\omega_{\gamma}(t),\quad t\in[0,\infty).

(See
[

]

Set

Because of the support condition of

,
we have

\displaystyle\omega_{s}\left(B^{*}I_{s}(A)\right)=\omega_{s}(B^{*})\omega_{s}(A),

for any

In this section, we study SPT-phases in

2.1
H^{2}(G,{\rm U}(1))
and projective representations

For SPT-phases in one dimension with on-site symmetry,
physicists conjectured that there is a
H^{2}(G,{\rm U}(1))
-valued invariant
of the classification.
In this subsection, we recall the definition of
H^{2}(G,{\rm U}(1))
and its relation to projective representations.

A
2
-cochain
C^{2}(G,{\rm U}(1))
is a map from

G^{\times 2}
to
{\rm U}(1)
.
It is said to be a
2
-cocycle if the relation

\displaystyle\sigma(gh,k)\sigma(g,h)=\sigma(g,hk)\sigma(h,k)

holds.
The set of all
2
-cocycles are denoted by
Z^{2}(G,{\rm U}(1))
.
We introduce an equivalence relation to
Z^{2}(G,{\rm U}(1))
by

\sigma^{\prime}\simeq\sigma
if there exists
c:G\mapsto{\mathbb{T}}
such that

\displaystyle\sigma^{\prime}(g,h)=\sigma(g,h)\frac{c(gh)}{c(g)c(h)}.

The equivalence classes forms a group, which is the second group cohomology

H^{2}(G,{U(1)})
.

The second group cohomology
H^{2}(G,{U(1)})

shows up naturally, when we consider projective representations.
A projective representation of
G
on a Hilbert space
{\mathcal{H}}

is a map from
G
to a unitary operators
{\mathcal{U}}({\mathcal{H}})

on
{\mathcal{H}}

satisfying

\displaystyle u(g)u(h)=\sigma(g,h)u(gh),\quad g,h\in G.

Here,
\sigma(g,h)
is a
{\rm U}(1)
-phase.
Using the associativity of
u
, we can show that
\sigma

is a
2
-cocycle.
Indeed,
we have

\displaystyle u(g)u(h)u(k)=\sigma(g,h)u(gh)u(k)=\sigma(g,h)\sigma(gh,k)u(ghk),

and

\displaystyle u(g)u(h)u(k)=\sigma(h,k)u(g)u(hk)=\sigma(h,k)\sigma(g,hk)u(ghk).

Comparing these equations, we obtain

2
-cocycle relation for
\sigma
.

Note that given a group action
G
on
{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{H}})
, for a Hilbert space
{\mathcal{H}}
,
we get some projective representation.
Let

\gamma:G\to\mathop{\mathrm{Aut}}\nolimits\left({\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{H}})\right)

be a group homomorphism.
Then
by Theorem
1.3
, there is a unitary
u_{g}
on
{\mathcal{H}}

such that

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathrm{Ad}}\nolimits(u_{g})(x)=\gamma_{g}(x),\quad x\in{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{H}}).

Note that there is some
{\rm U}(1)
-ambiguity about the choice of
u_{g}
:
for any map
c:G\to{\rm U}(1)
,
we also have

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathrm{Ad}}\nolimits(c_{g}u_{g})(x)=\gamma_{g}(x),\quad x\in{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{H}}).

(2.1)

This
u:G\to{\mathcal{U}}\left({\mathcal{H}}\right)
is a projective group representation.
Indeed, we have

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathrm{Ad}}\nolimits(u_{g}u_{h})(x)=\gamma_{g}\gamma_{h}(x)=\gamma_{gh}(x)=\mathop{\mathrm{Ad}}\nolimits(u_{gh})(x),\quad x\in{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{H}}).

This means
there is some
\sigma(g,h)\in{\rm U}(1)(1)

such that

\displaystyle u_{g}u_{h}=\sigma(g,h)u_{gh}.

Namely,
u
is a projective representation, and hence

\sigma
is a
2
-cocycle.
The
{\rm U}(1)
-ambiguity of the choice
c

(
2.1
) results in ambiguity of the
2
-cocycle

\sigma
.
If we choose
c_{g}u_{g}
instead of
u_{g}
,
then the
2
-cocycle obtained is

\displaystyle\tilde{\sigma}(g,h)=\sigma(g,h)c_{g}c_{h}\bar{c_{gh}}.

However, from this equation, we see that the resulting second cohomology class
does not depend on such a choice, namely we have

\left[\tilde{\sigma}\right]_{H^{2}(G,{\rm U}(1))}=\left[\sigma\right]_{H^{2}(G,{\rm U}(1))}.

Hence, given a group action
\gamma
on
{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{H}})
,
we obtain an associated second cohomology class without ambiguity.

2.3

Analysis of SPT-phases was initially carried out for matrix product states.
In this section, we introduce matrix product states.

In general, even constructing an example of gapped Hamiltonians is a highly non-trivial problem.

This is the case already for local Hamiltonians.
If the interaction terms

and
[
N

In this section, we explain this recipe.

Recently there was great progress in higher dimensions.
The technics from
[

]
and
[

Definition 2.2

Let

for all

Because

Theorem 2.9

(

Suppose that primitive

2.4
H^{2}(G,{\rm U}(1))
-index for MPS with on-site symmetry

Now let us consider MPS which are invariant under the on-site symmetry
\beta
given by a unitary representation
U
of a finite group

In
[

]
,
[
PTBO2
]
,
[
PWSVC
]

In this section we explain about this.

Let
\omega_{{\boldsymbol{v}}}
be a
\beta
-invariant MPS
given by a primitive
{\boldsymbol{v}}\in\mathop{\mathrm{M}}\nolimits_{k}^{\times d}

Because
\omega_{{\boldsymbol{v}}}
is
\beta_{g}
-invariant,
we can check that
d
-tuple
\tilde{\boldsymbol{v}}(g)
given by

\tilde{v}_{\mu}(g):=\sum_{\nu=1}^{d}U(g)_{\nu\mu}v_{\nu},\quad g\in G,\quad\mu=1,\ldots,d

also gives the same state.
Namely, we have

\omega_{\tilde{\boldsymbol{v}}(g)}=\omega_{{\boldsymbol{v}}}.

Then, by the fundamental theorem of MPS,
there is
c(g)\in{\mathbb{T}}
, a
k\times k

unitary matrix
u(g)
, such that

\displaystyle c(g)u(g)v_{\mu}u(g)^{*}=\tilde{v}_{\mu}(g)=\sum_{\nu=1}^{d}U(g)_{\nu\mu}v_{\nu}.

Now, let us consider
\tilde{v}_{\mu}(gh)
.
From above, we have

\displaystyle\tilde{v}_{\mu}(gh)=c(gh)u(gh)v_{\mu}u(gh)^{*}.

On the other hand, by the definition of
\tilde{v}_{\mu}(gh)
,
we have

\displaystyle\tilde{v}_{\mu}(gh)=\sum_{\nu=1}^{d}U(gh)_{\nu\mu}v_{\nu}=\sum_{\nu,\lambda=1}^{d}U(g)_{\nu\lambda}U(h)_{\lambda\mu}v_{\nu}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\lambda=1}^{d}U(h)_{\lambda\mu}\tilde{v}_{\lambda}(g)=c(g)u(g)\sum_{\lambda=1}^{d}U(h)_{\lambda\mu}v_{\lambda}u(g)^{*}

\displaystyle=c(g)c(h)u(g)u(h)v_{\mu}u(h)^{*}u(g)^{*}.

Hence we obtain

\displaystyle c(gh)u(gh)v_{\mu}u(gh)^{*}=c(g)c(h)u(g)u(h)v_{\mu}u(h)^{*}u(g)^{*},

for all
\mu=1,\ldots,d
.
Recalling (3) of Theorem
2.3
,
we then see that
c(gh)=c(g)c(h)
and

\displaystyle u(gh)xu(gh)^{*}=u(g)u(h)xu(h)^{*}u(g)^{*},

for all
x\in\mathop{\mathrm{M}}\nolimits_{k}
.
This means there is some
\sigma(g,h)\in{\rm U}(1)

such that

\displaystyle u(g)u(h)=\sigma(g,h)u(gh),\quad g,h\in G.

Hence we obtain a projective representation, and a second cohomology
[\sigma]_{H^{2}(G,{\rm U}(1))}\in H^{2}(G,{\rm U}(1))
.
Here we considered linear representation of

but anti-linear ones can be treated analogously.
This is the index obtained in

]
,
[
PTBO2
]
,
[
PWSVC
]

2.5

The derivation of

Thanks to Matsui’s theorem, we know that our unique ground state

\beta
-invariant

From Theorem
2.1

From now on, we use this structure of GNS triple repeatedly.
Furthermore, because
\omega_{\Phi}
is a unique gapped ground state
of
\beta
-invariant interaction, it is
\beta
-invariant.
Therefore, by Theorem

, there is a unitary representation

of

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathrm{Ad}}\nolimits\left(V_{g}\right)\circ\left(\rho_{L}\otimes\rho_{R}\right)=\rho_{L}\beta_{g}^{L}\otimes\rho_{R}\beta_{g}^{R},\quad V_{g}\Omega=\Omega.

If we restrict this to
{\mathcal{A}}_{R}
,
then we get

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathrm{Ad}}\nolimits\left(V_{g}\right)\left(\mathbb{I}_{{\mathcal{K}}_{L}}\otimes\rho_{R}(A)\right)=\mathbb{I}_{{\mathcal{K}}_{L}}\otimes\rho_{R}\beta_{g}^{R}(A),\quad A\in{\mathcal{A}}_{R}.

From this and irreducibility of
\rho_{R}
, we obtain a
*
-automorphism

of

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathrm{Ad}}\nolimits\left(V_{g}\right)\left(\mathbb{I}_{{\mathcal{K}}_{L}}\otimes x\right)=\mathbb{I}_{{\mathcal{K}}_{L}}\otimes\gamma_{g}(x),\quad x\in{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{K}}_{R}),

Note that

By Theorem
1.3
, there is a unitary
u_{R}(g)
on
{\mathcal{K}}_{R}

such that

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathrm{Ad}}\nolimits(u_{R}(g))(x)=\gamma_{g}(x),\quad x\in{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{K}}_{R}).

Because

is a group action

u_{R}
of

(

O4
]
)

This second cohomology class

(2.7)

From the fact that

Proposition 2.11
.

[
O4
]

If
\Phi_{1}\sim_{\beta}\Phi_{2}
, then
there are automorphisms
\alpha_{L}\in\mathop{\mathrm{Aut}}\nolimits({\mathcal{A}}_{L})
and
\alpha_{R}\in\mathop{\mathrm{Aut}}\nolimits({\mathcal{A}}_{R})
satisfying

(i)

\omega_{\Phi_{2}}
and
\omega_{\Phi_{1}}\circ\left(\alpha_{L}\otimes\alpha_{R}\right)

are quasi-equivalent,

(ii)

\alpha_{L}\circ\beta_{g,L}=\beta_{g,L}\circ\alpha_{L}
, and

\alpha_{R}\circ\beta_{g,R}=\beta_{g,R}\circ\alpha_{R}

for all

.

(i) says that we can move from

to
\omega_{\Phi_{2}}
without
changing the correlation between left-right chains, by

.
(ii) says that each
\alpha_{L}
and

do not

Hence

in terms of the group action, can not be changed by
\alpha_{L}\otimes\alpha_{R}
.
This proposition is nothing but the factorization property of the automorphic equivalence
explained in subsection
1.6
.

To proceed the proof, let

u_{R,1}
,
u_{R,2}
,
({\mathcal{K}}_{R,1},\rho_{R,1})
,
({\mathcal{K}}_{R,2},\rho_{R,2})

be the projective representations and irreducible representations given above,
corresponding to
\omega_{\Phi_{1}}
and
\omega_{\Phi_{2}}
.
They satisfy

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathrm{Ad}}\nolimits\left({u_{R,1}(g)}\right)\circ\rho_{R,1}(A)=\rho_{R,1}\circ\beta_{g,R}(A),\quad\mathop{\mathrm{Ad}}\nolimits\left({u_{R,2}(g)}\right)\circ\rho_{R,2}(A)=\rho_{R,2}\circ\beta_{g,R}(A),

Let
\pi_{1}
,

\omega_{\Phi_{1}}
and
\omega_{\Phi_{2}}

Then we have

\displaystyle\pi_{2}\sim_{\rm q.e.}\pi_{1}\circ\left(\alpha_{L}\otimes\alpha_{R}\right),

.
From this, we have

\displaystyle\rho_{R,2}\sim_{\rm q.e.}\left.\pi_{2}\right|_{{\mathcal{A}}_{R}}\sim_{\rm q.e.}\left.\pi_{1}\right|_{{\mathcal{A}}_{R}}\circ\alpha_{R}\sim_{\rm q.e.}\rho_{R,1}\circ\alpha_{R}.

*
-isomorphism

\displaystyle\rho_{R,2}=\tau\circ\rho_{R,1}\circ\alpha_{R}.

and the irreducibility of

, there exists a

Using this, we get

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathrm{Ad}}\nolimits(u_{R,2}(g))\circ\rho_{2,R}=\rho_{2,R}\circ\beta_{g,R}=\tau\circ\rho_{R,1}\circ\alpha_{R}\circ\beta_{g,R}

\alpha_{R}
and

Therefore,

\displaystyle\tau\circ\rho_{R,1}\circ\alpha_{R}\circ\beta_{g,R}=\tau\circ\rho_{R,1}\circ\beta_{g,R}\circ\alpha_{R}

\displaystyle=\mathop{\mathrm{Ad}}\nolimits\left(\mathop{\mathrm{Ad}}\nolimits_{W}(u_{R,1}(g))\right)\circ\rho_{R,2}

\displaystyle u_{R,2}(g)=c_{g}\mathop{\mathrm{Ad}}\nolimits_{W}(u_{R,1}(g))\quad g\in G.

and

2.6
Lieb-Schultz-Mattis (LSM) type theorems

The cohomology valued index
h_{\Phi,\beta}
is important as an invariant of our classification of SPT phases.
However, there is still another use of it, that is, Lieb-Schultz-Mattis (LSM) type theorem which we explain in this subsection.

It is a hard problem to decide if a given interaction has a unique gapped ground state or not.
For example, Haldane conjectured in 1983 that
Antiferromagnetic Heisenberg chain

\displaystyle\Phi_{Heisenberg}(\{x,x+1\})=\sum_{j=1}^{3}\,S^{(x)}_{j}S^{(x+1)}_{j},

does not have a unique gapped ground state if
d
is even,
while it does if
d
is odd.
The latter part of the conjecture is still an open problem.
Lieb-Schultz-Mattis
[
LSM
]
and Affleck-Lieb
[
AL
]

proved affirmatively the first part of the conjecture.
They proved the following : if interaction has

{\rm U}(1)
on-site symmetry and space translation symmetry
and the spin-size
d
is even, then it cannot have a unique gapped ground state.
They used the continuity of
{\rm U}(1)
in an ingenious way, to prove the existence of
low-energy excitation.
There has been a lot of progress after that
[
H3
]
[
Os
]
[
NS07
]
[
M2
]
, but
traditionally, the symmetry has been some continuous ones.
Recently, it was claimed by physicists that Lieb-Schultz-Mattis type theorem holds for some
discrete symmetries. It is proved for matrix product states parent Hamiltonians

[
CGW
]
,

[
WPVZ
]

[
Fu
]
.

Using our
H^{2}(G,{\rm U}(1))
-valued index, we can prove their claim.
Let
G
be a finite group and let
\beta_{g}:=\bigotimes_{{\mathbb{Z}}}\mathop{\mathrm{Ad}}\nolimits(U_{g})

with
U
a projective representation of
G

on
{\mathbb{C}}^{d}
.

Theorem 2.12

(
[
OT
]
,
[
OTT
]
)
.

If the second cohomology class
h
of
U
is non-trivial, then there is no
translation invariant interaction in
{\mathcal{P}}_{U.G.\beta}
.

Suppose that there exists an interaction
\Phi\in{\mathcal{P}}_{U.G.\beta}
which is translation invariant.
Let

h_{\Phi,\beta}^{x}\in H^{2}(G,U(1))
be an
index associated to
\left.\omega_{\Phi}\right|_{{\mathcal{A}}_{[x,\infty)\cap{\mathbb{Z}}}}

for each
x\in{\mathbb{Z}}
. (Note that we can cut the chain anywhere.)
Then we have

h_{\Phi,\beta}^{x}=h+h_{\Phi,\beta}^{x+1},\quad\text{and}\quad h_{\Phi,\beta}^{x}=h_{\Phi,\beta}^{x+1}.

Hence
h=0
.

\square

Recall that antiferromagnetic Heisenberg chain
\Phi_{Heisenberg}

has the
{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}\times{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}
-symmetry.
There, cohomology class of the projective representation
U
of
{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}\times{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}
is trivial if
d
is odd,
nontrivial if
d
is even.
Hence we have
\Phi_{Heisenberg}\notin{\mathcal{P}}_{U.G.\beta}
, if
d
is even.
This gives an alternative proof for
the first half of Haldane conjecture.
Analogously, we can prove the following, using the
H^{2}(G,{\rm U}(1))
-index.

Theorem 2.13

(
[
OTT
]
)
.

If there is
\Phi\in{\mathcal{P}}_{U.G.\beta}
, origin-reflection invariant,
then
h=2h_{1}
with some
h_{1}\in H^{2}(G,{\rm U}(1))
.

2.8
An easy derivation of

In subsection

proven for all the unique gapped ground states
.
It relied on Matsui’s Theorem, which is a corollary

]

However, if you are interested in

,

-valued index.
The point is, if
\Phi
is in a SPT phase, by definition,

\Phi\sim\Phi_{0}
, with the fixed reference on-site interaction
\Phi_{0}
.
Using the automorphic equivalence,
this means there is an automorphism
\alpha
given by local interactions
such that
\omega_{\Phi}=\omega_{\Phi_{0}}\circ\alpha
.
Recall that
\omega_{\Phi_{0}}
is a pure product state.
Therefore, there are pure states
\omega_{L}
,
\omega_{R}
on
{\mathcal{A}}_{L}
,
{\mathcal{A}}_{R}
such that

\omega_{\Phi_{0}}=\omega_{L}\otimes\omega_{R}.

On the other hand, as we saw in subsection
1.6
,

\alpha
satisfies
a factorization property

\alpha=({\rm{inner}})\circ\left(\alpha_{L}\otimes\alpha_{R}\right).

Now, because
\omega_{\Phi}
is a unique ground state of
\beta
-invariant interaction

\Phi
, it is
\beta
-invariant.
From this and above decompositions of
\omega_{\Phi_{0}}
,
\alpha
,
we get

\displaystyle\left(\omega_{L}\alpha_{L}\otimes\omega_{R}\alpha_{R}\right)\circ({\rm{inner}})=\omega_{\Phi}=\omega_{\Phi}\circ\beta_{g}=\left(\omega_{L}\alpha_{L}\beta_{g}^{L}\otimes\omega_{R}\alpha_{R}\beta_{g}^{R}\right)\circ({\rm{inner}}).

Because
\omega_{L}
,
\omega_{R}
are pure states, we may apply
Lemma
1.7
.
Let
({\mathcal{H}}_{R},\pi_{R},\Omega_{R})
be a GNS representation of
\omega_{R}
.
Because
\omega_{R}
is pure,
\pi_{R}
is irreducible.
Applying this Lemma, we obtain
a unitary

on

such that

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathrm{Ad}}\nolimits(u_{g})\circ\pi_{R}\circ\alpha_{R}(A)=\pi_{R}\circ\alpha_{R}\circ\beta_{g}^{R}(A),\quad A\in{{\mathcal{A}}_{R}}.

Note that

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathrm{Ad}}\nolimits(u_{g}u_{h})\circ\pi_{R}\circ\alpha_{R}(A)=\pi_{R}\circ\alpha_{R}\circ\beta_{g}^{R}\beta_{h}^{R}(A)

\displaystyle=\pi_{R}\circ\alpha_{R}\circ\beta_{gh}^{R}(A)=\mathop{\mathrm{Ad}}\nolimits(u_{gh})\circ\pi_{R}\circ\alpha_{R}(A),

for each

and

Because

is irreducible, this implies

Hence there is some

Namely,

In fact this is the very projective representation

]

Let

(See

As in section

This signature is Pollmann et.al.’s

Now, as before, we would like to extend it to general
\omega_{\Phi}
, for

\Theta_{R}
-invariant

Let
\Phi\in{\mathcal{P}}_{U.G.}
be a
\Theta_{R}
-invariant interaction.
By the split property and the reflection symmetry of

\displaystyle({\mathcal{H}}\otimes{\mathcal{H}},\hat{\pi}:=\left(\pi\circ\Theta_{R}|_{{\mathcal{A}}_{L}}\right)\otimes\pi,\Omega).

Let

Definition 2.16

(O ’19)

Define the index of

For simplicity, we assume that the GNS-vector

is cyclic and separating for
{\mathbb{C}}\mathbb{I}\otimes{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{H}})

By this simplification, there is an anti-liner operator

\displaystyle S\left(\mathbb{I}\otimes x\right)\Omega:=\left(\mathbb{I}\otimes x^{*}\right)\Omega,\quad x\in{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{H}}).

Then the following holds.

(
[
O6
]
)

There is an anti-unitary

Let us consider a
\Theta_{R}
-invariant MPS

{\boldsymbol{v}}

For simplicity, we still pretend that the GNS-vector

\displaystyle\Delta^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(\mathbb{I}\otimes x\right)\Omega=\left(\mathbb{I}\otimes\rho_{\boldsymbol{v}}^{\frac{1}{2}}x\rho_{\boldsymbol{v}}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\right)\Omega,\quad x\in B({\mathcal{H}}).

(2.11)

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\left(\mathbb{I}\otimes\rho_{\boldsymbol{v}}^{\frac{1}{2}}\theta S_{\mu}\theta^{*}\rho_{\boldsymbol{v}}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\right)\Omega=J\Delta^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(\mathbb{I}\otimes\rho_{\boldsymbol{v}}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\theta S_{\mu}^{*}\theta^{*}\rho_{\boldsymbol{v}}^{\frac{1}{2}}\right)\Omega\\

3.1
Derivation of
H^{3}(G,{\rm U}(1))
out of cocycle action

In one dimensional system, we obtained a
H^{2}(G,{\rm U}(1))
-valued
index out of projective representations.
According to physicists, we are supposed to find
a
H^{3}(G,{\rm U}(1))
-valued index.
How can we find such an index?

We can find a hint in literatures of operator algebra
on the classification of cocycle actions.

[
C
]
,
[
J
]
.

Let
\{\gamma_{g}\}_{g\in G}
be automorphisms on a factor
{\mathcal{M}}
.
Assume that there are unitaries
\{u(g,h)\}_{g,h\in G}\subset{\mathcal{U}}({\mathcal{M}})
such that

\displaystyle\gamma_{g}\gamma_{h}=\mathop{\mathrm{Ad}}\nolimits\left(u(g,h)\right)\gamma_{gh}.

(3.1)

This
\gamma
is called a cocycle action.
Using this relation twice, we obtain

\displaystyle\gamma_{g}\gamma_{h}\gamma_{k}=\mathop{\mathrm{Ad}}\nolimits\left(u(g,h)\right)\gamma_{gh}\gamma_{k}=\mathop{\mathrm{Ad}}\nolimits\left(u(g,h)u(gh,k)\right)\gamma_{ghk}.

Here we used the relation (
3.1
)
to
\gamma_{g}\gamma_{h}
and then to
\gamma_{gh}\gamma_{k}
.
On the other hand, we have

\displaystyle\gamma_{g}\gamma_{h}\gamma_{k}=\gamma_{g}\mathop{\mathrm{Ad}}\nolimits\left(u(h,k)\right)\gamma_{hk}=\mathop{\mathrm{Ad}}\nolimits\left(\gamma_{g}\left(u(h,k)\right)\right)\gamma_{g}\gamma_{hk}

\displaystyle=\mathop{\mathrm{Ad}}\nolimits\left(\gamma_{g}\left(u(h,k)\right)u(g,hk)\right)\gamma_{ghk}.

Here we used the relation (
3.1
)
to
\gamma_{h}\gamma_{k}
and then
\gamma_{g}\gamma_{hk}
.
By the associativity, of course above two equations should give the same value.
Hence we get

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathrm{Ad}}\nolimits\left(u(g,h)u(gh,k)\right)(a)=\mathop{\mathrm{Ad}}\nolimits\left(\gamma_{g}\left(u(h,k)\right)u(g,hk)\right)(a),\quad a\in{\mathcal{M}}.

This means

\displaystyle\left(u(g,h)u(gh,k)\right)^{*}\left(\gamma_{g}\left(u(h,k)\right)u(g,hk)\right)\in{\mathcal{M}}\cap{\mathcal{M}}^{\prime}={\mathbb{C}}\mathbb{I}.

Namely,
there exists some phase
c(g,h,k)

such that

\displaystyle u(g,h)u(gh,k)=c(g,h,k)\gamma_{g}\left(u(h,k)\right)u(g,hk),\quad g,h,k\in G.

(3.2)

Using this relation repeatedly, we can show that

c:G^{\times 3}\to{\rm U}(1)
satisfies the
3
-cocycle relation.
Indeed, for
g,h,k,f\in G
, by repeated use of (
3.2
), we get

\displaystyle u(g,h)u(gh,k)u(ghk,f)=\left[u(g,h)u(gh,k)\right]u(ghk,f)

\displaystyle=c(g,h,k)\gamma_{g}\left(u(h,k)\right)u(g,hk)u(ghk,f)

\displaystyle=c(g,h,k)\gamma_{g}\left(u(h,k)\right)\cdot\left[u(g,hk)u(ghk,f)\right]

\displaystyle=c(g,h,k)\gamma_{g}\left(u(h,k)\right)\cdot c(g,hk,f)\left(\gamma_{g}\left(u(hk,f)\right)\left(u(g,hkf)\right)\right)

\displaystyle=c(g,h,k)c(g,hk,f)\left(\gamma_{g}\left[u(h,k)u(hk,f)\right]\right)\cdot\left(u(g,hkf)\right)

\displaystyle=c(g,h,k)c(g,hk,f)c(h,k,f)\gamma_{g}\left(\gamma_{h}\left(u(k,f)\right)\left(u(h,kf)\right)\right)\cdot\left(u(g,hkf)\right)

\displaystyle=c(g,h,k)c(g,hk,f)c(h,k,f)\cdot\gamma_{g}\gamma_{h}\left(u(k,f)\right)\cdot\left[\left(\gamma_{g}\left(u(h,kf)\right)\right)u(g,hkf)\right]

\displaystyle=c(g,h,k)c(g,hk,f)c(h,k,f)\overline{c(g,h,kf)}\cdot\left\{\gamma_{g}\gamma_{h}\left(u(k,f)\right)\right)\}\cdot\left(u(g,h)u(gh,kf)\right).

Here, (and below) we apply (
3.2
) for terms in
\left[\cdot\right]
to get the succeeding equality.
Using (
3.1
) for
\gamma_{g}\gamma_{h}

and again (
3.2
), we obtain

\displaystyle(\ref {temp})

\displaystyle=c(g,h,k)c(g,hk,f)c(h,k,f)\overline{c(g,h,kf)}\left(\mathop{\mathrm{Ad}}\nolimits\left(u(g,h)\right)\gamma_{gh}\right)\left(u(k,f)\right)\left(u(g,h)u(gh,kf)\right)

\displaystyle=c(g,h,k)c(g,hk,f)c(h,k,f)\overline{c(g,h,kf)}\left(u(g,h)\right)\left[\gamma_{gh}\left(u(k,f)\right)\left(u(gh,kf)\right)\right]

\displaystyle=c(g,h,k)c(g,hk,f)c(h,k,f)\overline{c(g,h,kf)}\overline{c(gh,k,f)}\left(u(g,h)u(gh,k)u(ghk,f)\right).

Hence, we obtain

\displaystyle c(g,h,k)c(g,hk,f)c(h,k,f)\overline{c(g,h,kf)}\overline{c(gh,k,f)}=1,\quad\text{for all}\quad g,h,k,f\in G.

This means
c\in Z^{3}(G,{\mathbb{T}})
.
Hence we obtain
[c]_{H^{3}(G,{\rm U}(1))}\in H^{3}(G,{\rm U}(1))
.
Note that resulting
[c]_{H^{3}(G,{\rm U}(1))}
does not
depend on the choice of
u
.
Indeed, if
\gamma_{g}\gamma_{h}=\mathop{\mathrm{Ad}}\nolimits\left(\tilde{u}(g,h)\right)\gamma_{gh}

holds for another
\tilde{u}(g,h)\in{\mathcal{M}}
,
the difference

u(g,h)^{*}\tilde{u}(g,h)
belongs to the center

{\mathcal{M}}\cap{\mathcal{M}}^{\prime}={\mathbb{C}}\mathbb{I}
.
Hence there is some
a(g,h)\in{\rm U}(1)

such that

u(g,h)^{*}\tilde{u}(g,h)=a(g,h)
.
Let
\tilde{c}(g,h,k)\in{\rm U}(1)
be
the phase satisfying
\tilde{u}(g,h)\tilde{u}(gh,k)=\tilde{c}(g,h,k)\gamma_{g}\left(\tilde{u}(h,k)\right)\tilde{u}(g,hk)
.
Then we have

\displaystyle a(g,h)a(gh,k)\left(u(g,h)u(gh,k)\right)

(3.4)

\displaystyle=\tilde{u}(g,h)\tilde{u}(gh,k)

\displaystyle=\tilde{c}(g,h,k)\gamma_{g}\left(\tilde{u}(h,k)\right)\tilde{u}(g,hk)

\displaystyle=\tilde{c}(g,h,k)a(h,k)a(g,hk)\gamma_{g}\left(u(h,k)\right)u(g,hk)

\displaystyle=\tilde{c}(g,h,k)a(h,k)a(g,hk)\overline{c(g,h,k)}u(g,h)u(gh,k).

Hence we have
\tilde{c}(g,h,k)=c(g,h,k)\overline{a(h,k)a(g,hk)}a(g,h)a(gh,k)
.
This means the difference between
\tilde{c}
and
c
is just a coboundary, and
we get
[c]_{H^{3}(G,{\mathbb{T}})}=[\tilde{c}]_{H^{3}(G,{\mathbb{T}})}
.

The above argument tells us,
if we have automorphisms
\{\gamma_{g}\}_{g\in G}
on
{\mathcal{A}}

such that
\gamma_{g}\gamma_{h}\gamma_{gh}^{-1}
is inner,
then we get some
[c]\in H^{3}(G,{\rm U}(1))
.
This can be a hint to find a
H^{3}(G,{\rm U}(1))
-valued index.

Before closing the subsection, we would like to emphasize the importance of
considering infinite system, not finite quantum spin systems.
On a matrix algebra, any automorphism is inner.
Therefore, if we collect some
\{\gamma_{g}\}_{g\in G}
,
they are represented as

\gamma_{g}=\mathop{\mathrm{Ad}}\nolimits(u_{g})
, by some unitary matrix
u_{g}
.
Then we may take

u(g,h)
in

\displaystyle\gamma_{g}\gamma_{h}=\mathop{\mathrm{Ad}}\nolimits\left(u(g,h)\right)\gamma_{gh}.

(3.5)

as
u_{g}u_{h}u_{gh}^{*}
.
Substituting this to

\displaystyle u(g,h)u(gh,k)=c(g,h,k)\gamma_{g}\left(u(h,k)\right)u(g,hk),

we obtain
c(g,h,k)=1
.
Hence we get only a trivial element of
H^{3}(G,{\rm U}(1))
.
This can be seen as a reason that we consider
infinite

quantum spin systems.

3.2
Action of
\beta_{g}^{U}
on automorphisms

From what we saw from the subsection
3.1
,
our task can be finding automorphisms
\{\gamma_{g}\}_{g\in G}
on
{\mathcal{A}}

such that
\gamma_{g}\gamma_{h}\gamma_{gh}^{-1}
is inner.
But how can we find such a thing?
We have a genuine group action
\beta_{g}
but a priori, there is no
set of automorphisms which are “nicely obstacled”
like the ones in cocycle action.

A hint comes from former works on gapped phases.
The wisdom we can see from there is

if you cut the system, you may obtain some non-trivial index
.
In fact, that is the heart of the phenomena
called
bulk-edge correspondence
, in free Fermions.
This correspondence says that if you would like to know about the bulk(=entire system), cut the system into two
and look at the edge.
We did the same thing in section
2
for quantum spin chains as well.
We restricted our attention to the right half of the chain.
Also in
[
BDF
]
, an integer-valued index for systems with
U(1)
-symmetry
was derived by considering
U(1)
-action on half of the system.
From these works, it looks to be a good idea to consider the action of
the
G
-symmetry on the half-plane.
Recall that from the automorphic equivalence,
the ground state
\omega_{\Phi}
of
\Phi\in{\mathcal{P}}_{U.G.\beta}^{0}

is given from a product state
\omega_{\Phi_{0}}

via some automorphism
\alpha
, as
\omega_{\Phi}=\omega_{\Phi_{0}}\circ\alpha
.
This motivates us to think of an action (on the half infinite plane) of
G
on automorphisms.
More precisely we consider the following action.

As we stated above, we consider an action of
the
G
-symmetry corresponding to the upper half-plane.

\displaystyle\beta_{g}^{U}:=\mathop{\mathrm{id}}\nolimits_{{\mathcal{A}}_{H_{D}}}\otimes\bigotimes_{x\in H_{U}}\mathop{\mathrm{Ad}}\nolimits\left(U(g)\right),\quad g\in G.

on the automorphism group
\mathop{\mathrm{Aut}}\nolimits({\mathcal{A}}_{{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}})
.
The action is
given by

\displaystyle G\times\mathop{\mathrm{Aut}}\nolimits({\mathcal{A}}_{{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}})\ni(g,\alpha)\to\beta_{g}^{U}\alpha\left(\beta_{g}^{U}\right)^{-1}\in\mathop{\mathrm{Aut}}\nolimits({\mathcal{A}}_{{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}}).

An idea to allow us to derive
H^{3}(G,{\rm U}(1))
is

cut further
.
Namely, we consider the following situation.

Lemma 3.1
.

Suppose that

\displaystyle\beta_{g}^{U}\alpha\left(\beta_{g}^{U}\right)^{-1}\alpha^{-1}=({\rm{inner}})\left(\eta_{g,L}\otimes\eta_{g,R}\right).

(3.6)

Set
\gamma_{g}:=\eta_{g,R}\beta_{g}^{U,R}
.
Then we have
\gamma_{g}\gamma_{h}\gamma_{gh}^{-1}=\mathop{\mathrm{Ad}}\nolimits\left(u(g,h)\right)

with some unitary

Hence we obtain some

Set

Taking inverse of (

Because

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathrm{id}}\nolimits=\alpha\beta_{g}^{U}\alpha^{-1}\circ\alpha\beta_{h}^{U}\alpha^{-1}\circ\left(\alpha\beta_{gh}^{U}\alpha^{-1}\right)^{-1}

\displaystyle=({\rm{inner}})\circ\left(\gamma_{g,L}\gamma_{h,L}\gamma_{gh,L}^{-1}\right)\otimes\left(\gamma_{g,R}\gamma_{h,R}\gamma_{gh,R}^{-1}\right).

This means

\gamma_{g,R}\gamma_{h,R}\gamma_{gh,R}^{-1}

is inner.

\square

In fact, in a model derived out of Dijkgraaf-Witten model in TQFT,
the ground state
\omega_{\Phi}
is given by
some automorphism
\alpha
and a product state
\omega_{0}

as
\omega_{\Phi}=\omega_{0}\circ\alpha
.
This
\alpha
satisfies the property (
3.6

and above procedure allows us to recover the ”built in” third cohomology class.

.)

We note from the proof that what is happening is heuristically the following:
we are considering a genuine action
\alpha\beta_{g}^{U}\alpha^{-1}

on the whole space
{\mathcal{A}}_{{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}}
.
This automorphism
\alpha\beta_{g}^{U}\alpha^{-1}
can be

The fact that the original

is a genuine action result in the

of

is a cocycle action, i.e.,

modulo inner automorphisms

If the automorphism
\alpha
in the automorphic equivalence satisfies

-valued index.
Unfortunately, it seems a it too much to ask.

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\mathop{\mathrm{Ad}}\nolimits(W_{g})\left(\mathbb{I}_{{\mathcal{H}}_{L}}\otimes u(h,k)\right)=\mathop{\mathrm{Ad}}\nolimits(W_{g})\left(\mathbb{I}_{{\mathcal{H}}_{L}}\otimes\pi_{R}\alpha_{R}\left(v_{R}(h,k)\right)\right)\\

&=\pi_{0}\left(\mathbb{I}_{{\mathcal{A}}_{H_{L}}}\otimes\left(\alpha_{R}\gamma_{g,R}\beta_{g}^{RU}v_{R}(h,k)\right)\right),\end{split}

because
\Theta
is localized in
C_{\theta_{0}}^{c}
.
(We substituted
\eta_{g,\sigma}:=\gamma_{g,\sigma}
at the last line.)
Substituting this, we get

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\left(W_{g}\left(\mathbb{I}_{{\mathcal{H}}_{L}}\otimes u(h,k)\right)W_{g}^{*}\right)\left(\mathbb{I}_{{\mathcal{H}}_{L}}\otimes u(g,hk)\right)\\
&=\mathbb{I}_{{\mathcal{A}}_{H_{L}}}\otimes\pi_{R}\alpha_{R}\left(\gamma_{g,R}\beta_{g}^{RU}\left(v_{R}(h,k)\right)v_{R}(g,hk)\right)\\
&=\overline{c_{R}(g,h,k)}\cdot\mathbb{I}_{{\mathcal{A}}_{H_{L}}}\otimes\pi_{R}\alpha_{R}\left(v_{R}(g,h)v_{R}(gh,k)\right)\\
&=\overline{c_{R}(g,h,k)}\cdot\mathbb{I}_{{\mathcal{A}}_{H_{L}}}\otimes u(g,h)u(gh,k).\end{split}

This proves the theorem.

\square

.
Therefore, a term
\psi_{\bm{s},g}(\bm{x},\pi,j)
corresponding to
(\bm{x},\pi,j)
cancels out with that of
(\bm{x^{\prime}},{\pi^{\prime}},j^{\prime})

, if the term
\psi_{\bm{s},g}(\bm{x^{\prime}},{\pi^{\prime}},j^{\prime})
also appears in (
3.47
).
Recall that there is exactly one
(\bm{x^{\prime}},{\pi^{\prime}},j^{\prime})
such that
(\bm{x},\pi,j)\sim(\bm{x^{\prime}},{\pi^{\prime}},j^{\prime})
.
The terms
which may not disappear is that of either
\bm{x}\in\partial\Lambda_{L}^{(2)}

or

\displaystyle\bm{x}\in\Lambda_{L}^{(1)}\times\{0\},\quad\text{and}\quad j=2,\quad\text{and}\quad\pi=\mathop{\mathrm{id}}\nolimits.

(3.48)

The former terms form some unitary in
{\mathcal{D}}_{\partial\Lambda_{L}^{(2)}}
.
For the latter case,
setting
y\in\Lambda_{L}^{(1)}
so that
(y,0)=\bm{x}
,
we obtain

\displaystyle{\bm{v}}_{0,\bm{x}}^{\left(\pi\right)}=\left(y,0\right),\quad{\bm{v}}_{1,\bm{x}}^{\left(\pi\right)}=\left(y+1,0\right).

(3.49)

Hence we have

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\tilde{d}^{0}V_{+,L}^{(0)}\left(g\right)\\
&=\left(\text{unitary in }{\mathcal{D}}_{\partial\Lambda_{L}^{(2)}}\right)\cdot\sum_{\bm{s}\in G^{\times\left(\Lambda_{L+1}^{(1)}\times\{\bm{0}\}\right)}}\\
&\quad\prod_{y\in\Lambda_{L}^{(1)}}\nu\left(\begin{gathered}e,g,s\left((y,0)\right),s\left((y+1,0)\right)\end{gathered}\right)^{-1}\cdot e_{\bm{s},\bm{s}}^{\left(\Lambda_{L+1}^{(1)}\times\{\bm{0}\}\right)}\\
&=\left(\text{unitary in }{\mathcal{D}}_{\partial\Lambda_{L}^{(2)}}\right)\cdot V_{L}^{(1)}(g).\end{split}

This completes the proof of the first part of (ii).
The proof for the second part of (ii) is the same,
except for this time,
[0,L]
is replaced by
[-L,L]
.
Therefore, there is no contribution from out side of
\partial\Lambda_{L}^{(2)}
.

The proof of (iii) is basically the same.
We have

\displaystyle\begin{split}&V_{+,L}^{(1)}(g)\cdot\left(\beta_{g}^{U}\left(V_{+,L}^{(1)}(h)\right)\right)\cdot\left(V_{+,L}^{(1)}(gh)\right)^{-1}\\
&=\sum_{\bm{s}\in G^{\times\left([0,L+1]\times\{0\}\right)}}\;\;\prod_{y\in[0,L]}\\
&\quad\left(\begin{gathered}\nu\left(g,gh,s(y,0),s(y+1,0)\right)^{-1}\\
\nu\left(e,gh,s(y,0),s(y+1,0)\right)\nu\left(e,g,s(y,0),s(y+1,0)\right)^{-1}\end{gathered}\right)e_{\bm{s},\bm{s}}^{[0,L+1]\times\{0\}}\\
&=\sum_{\bm{s}\in G^{\times\left([0,L+1]\times\{\bm{0}\}\right)}}\;\;\prod_{y\in[0,L]}\left(\nu\left(e,g,gh,s(y+1,0)\right)^{-1}\nu\left(e,g,gh,s(y,0)\right)\right)e_{\bm{s},\bm{s}}^{[0,L+1]\times\{0\}}\\
&=\left(\text{unitary in }{\mathcal{D}}_{\partial\Lambda_{L}^{(1)}}\right)\sum_{s\in G}\nu\left(e,g,gh,s\right)\cdot e_{s,s}^{\{\bm{0}\}}.\end{split}

For the second equality, we used (
3.38
).
Third equality is just a simple cancellation.

(iv) can be checked directly, using (
3.38
).

\square

We can easily take the thermodynamic limit.

Lemma 3.12
.

There are automorphisms

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\sigma^{(0)}\in\mathop{\mathrm{Aut}}\nolimits\left({{\mathcal{A}}_{{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}}}\right),\quad\sigma_{+}^{(0)}\in\mathop{\mathrm{Aut}}\nolimits\left({\mathcal{A}}_{{\mathbb{Z}}\times[0,\infty)}\right),\quad\sigma_{-}^{(0)}\in\mathop{\mathrm{Aut}}\nolimits\left({\mathcal{A}}_{{\mathbb{Z}}\times(-\infty,-1]}\right)\\
&\Xi^{(0)}\in\mathop{\mathrm{Aut}}\nolimits\left({\mathcal{A}}_{{\mathbb{Z}}^{(1)}\times\{-1,0\}}\right),\quad\Xi_{+}^{(0)}\in\mathop{\mathrm{Aut}}\nolimits\left({\mathcal{A}}_{{[0,\infty)}\times\{-1,0\}}\right),\Xi_{-}^{(0)}\in\mathop{\mathrm{Aut}}\nolimits\left({\mathcal{A}}_{(-\infty,-1]\times\{-1,0\}}\right),\quad\\
&\sigma_{+}^{(1)}(g)\in\mathop{\mathrm{Aut}}\nolimits\left({\mathcal{A}}_{[0,\infty)\times\{0\}}\right),\quad\sigma_{-}^{(1)}(g)\in\mathop{\mathrm{Aut}}\nolimits\left({\mathcal{A}}_{(-\infty,-1]\times\{0\}}\right)\end{split}

such that

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\sigma^{(0)}(A)=\lim_{L\to\infty}\mathop{\mathrm{Ad}}\nolimits\left(V_{L}^{(0)}\right)(A),\quad\sigma_{+}^{(0)}(A)=\lim_{L\to\infty}\mathop{\mathrm{Ad}}\nolimits\left(V_{+,L}^{(0)}\right)(A),\\
&\sigma_{-}^{(0)}(A)=\lim_{L\to\infty}\mathop{\mathrm{Ad}}\nolimits\left(V_{-,L}^{(0)}\right)(A),\quad\Xi^{(0)}(A)=\lim_{L\to\infty}\mathop{\mathrm{Ad}}\nolimits\left(V_{\partial L}^{(0)}\right)(A),\\
&\Xi_{+}^{(0)}(A)=\lim_{L\to\infty}\mathop{\mathrm{Ad}}\nolimits\left(V_{\partial,L,+}^{(0)}\right)(A),\quad\Xi_{-}^{(0)}(A)=\lim_{L\to\infty}\mathop{\mathrm{Ad}}\nolimits\left(V_{\partial,L,-}^{(0)}\right)(A),\\
&\sigma^{(1)}(g)(A)=\lim_{L\to\infty}\mathop{\mathrm{Ad}}\nolimits\left(V_{L}^{(1)}\left(g\right)\right)(A),\quad\sigma_{+}^{(1)}\left(g\right)(A)=\lim_{L\to\infty}\mathop{\mathrm{Ad}}\nolimits\left(V_{+,L}^{(1)}\left(g\right)\right)(A),\\
&\sigma_{-}^{(1)}\left(g\right)(A)=\lim_{L\to\infty}\mathop{\mathrm{Ad}}\nolimits\left(V_{-,L}^{(1)}\left(g\right)\right)(A),\quad\Xi^{(1)}\left(g\right)(A)=\lim_{L\to\infty}\mathop{\mathrm{Ad}}\nolimits\left(V_{\partial,L}^{(1)}\left(g\right)\right)(A),\end{split}

(3.50)

for any
g\in G
,
L\in{\mathbb{N}}
, and
A\in{\mathcal{A}}_{\Lambda_{L-1}^{(2)}}
.

Furthermore, the following holds:

(i)

For each

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\sigma^{(0)}=\Xi^{(0)}\left(\sigma_{-}^{(0)}\otimes\sigma_{+}^{(0)}\right),\quad\Xi^{(0)}=({\rm{inner}})\left(\Xi_{-}^{(0)}\otimes\Xi_{+}^{(0)}\right),\\
&\sigma^{(1)}\left(g\right)=\Xi^{(1)}\left(g\right)\circ\left(\sigma_{-}^{(1)}\left(g\right)\otimes\sigma_{+}^{(1)}\left(g\right)\right).\end{split}

(3.51)

(ii)

For each
g\in G
,

\displaystyle d^{1}_{H_{U}}\sigma_{+}^{(0)}\left(g\right)=\sigma^{(1)}\left(g\right).

(3.52)

(iii)

\displaystyle\sigma_{+}^{(1)}(g)\beta_{g}^{U}\sigma_{+}^{(1)}\left(h\right)\left(\beta_{g}^{U}\right)^{-1}\left(\sigma_{+}^{(1)}(gh)\right)^{-1}=\mathop{\mathrm{Ad}}\nolimits\left(V\left(g,h\right)\right)

(3.53)

For any
M,L\in{\mathbb{N}}
with
M+1\geq L
,
V_{L}^{(m)}
is of the form

\displaystyle V_{L}^{(m)}=V_{M+1}^{(m)}\left(\text{unitary in }\quad{\mathcal{D}}_{\Lambda_{L}^{(d)}\setminus\Lambda_{M}^{(d)}}\right).

(3.54)

From this and the fact that

V_{L}^{(m)},V_{M+1}^{(m)}
belong to a common abelian subalgebra, we have

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathrm{Ad}}\nolimits\left({V_{L}^{(m)}}\right)(A)=\mathop{\mathrm{Ad}}\nolimits\left({V_{M+1}^{(m)}}\right)(A),\quad\mathop{\mathrm{Ad}}\nolimits\left({\left(V_{L}^{(m)}\right)^{*}}\right)(A)=\mathop{\mathrm{Ad}}\nolimits\left({\left(V_{M+1}^{(m)}\right)^{*}}\right)(A),

(3.55)

for all
A\in{\mathcal{A}}_{\Lambda_{M}^{(2)}}
.
Therefore, taking
L\to\infty
limit, we obtain an automorphism
satisfying (
3.50
).
The existence of other automorphisms are the same.
The properties (i), (ii), (iii) corresponds to the (i), (ii), (iii) of Lemma
3.11
.

\square

Let

\displaystyle\xi:=\frac{1}{\sqrt{|G|}}\sum_{g\in G}\delta_{g},

(3.56)

and set a state
\rho
on
{\mathcal{B}}(l^{2}(G))
such that

\displaystyle\rho(A)=\left\langle\xi,A\xi\right\rangle,\quad A\in{\mathcal{B}}(l^{2}(G)).

(3.57)

Combining Lemma
3.11
and Lemma
3.12
, we obtain the following:

Theorem 3.13
.

Let
\sigma^{(0)}
be an automorphism given by Lemma
3.12
.
Let
\omega_{0}
be an infinite tensor product state on

{\mathcal{A}}_{{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}}:=\bigotimes_{\bm{x}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}}{\mathcal{B}}(l^{2}(G))

defined by

\displaystyle\omega_{0}:=\bigotimes_{\bm{x}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}}\rho

(3.58)

Then the
H^{3}(G,{\rm U}(1))
-valued index of the model is

\left[\Psi^{3}\left(\nu\right)\right]_{H^{3}(G,{\rm U}(1))}
.

Note that all the automorphisms in Lemma
3.12
and their conjugation with respect to

\beta_{g}^{U}
mutually commute.
Using Lemma
3.12
, we obtain

\displaystyle\begin{split}&d^{0}_{H_{U}}\left(\sigma^{(0)}\right)(g)=d^{0}_{H_{U}}\left(\Xi^{(0)}\left(\sigma_{-}^{(0)}\otimes\sigma_{+}^{(0)}\right)\right)(g)\\
&=({\rm{inner}})\circ\left(d^{0}_{H_{U}\cap H_{L}}\Xi^{(0)}_{-}(g)\otimes d^{0}_{H_{U}\cap H_{R}}\Xi^{(0)}_{+}(g)\right)\circ d^{0}_{H_{U}\cap H_{R}}\sigma_{+}^{(0)}\left(g\right)\\
&=({\rm{inner}})\circ\left(d^{0}_{H_{U}\cap H_{L}}\Xi^{(0)}_{-}(g)\circ\sigma_{-}^{(1)}(g)\otimes d^{0}_{H_{U}\cap H_{R}}\Xi^{(0)}_{+}(g)\circ\sigma_{+}^{(1)}(g)\right)\end{split}

Hence

d^{0}_{H_{U}}\sigma^{(0)}
is factorized into left and right with respect to automorphisms

\displaystyle\begin{split}\gamma_{g,L}=d^{0}_{H_{U}\cap H_{L}}\Xi^{(0)}_{-}(g)\circ\sigma_{-}^{(1)}(g)\\
\gamma_{g,R}=d^{0}_{H_{U}\cap H_{R}}\Xi^{(0)}_{+}(g)\circ\sigma_{+}^{(1)}(g).\end{split}

(3.59)

For this
\gamma_{g,R}
, by a straight forward calculation, we get

\displaystyle\begin{split}&d^{1}_{H_{U}\cap H_{R}}\gamma_{R}(g,h)=d^{1}_{H_{U}\cap H_{R}}d^{0}_{H_{U}\cap H_{R}}\Xi^{(0)}_{+}(g,h)\circ d^{1}_{H_{U}}\sigma_{+}^{(1)}(g,h)=\mathop{\mathrm{Ad}}\nolimits\left(V\left(g,h\right)\right).\end{split}

(3.60)

Hence
we may set
v(g,h)=V(g,h)
in Theorem
3.10
.
Then the corresponding
3
-cocycle

c_{R}
of Theorem
3.10

is
c(g,h,k)=\Psi_{3}(\nu)(g,h,k)

from (iv) of Lemma
3.11
.
This proves the theorem.

\square
