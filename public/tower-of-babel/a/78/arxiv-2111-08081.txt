Rigidity for Monogamy-of-Entanglement Games {abroadbe,eculf019}@uottawa.ca
Anne Broadbent and Eric Culf

Abstract
In a monogamy-of-entanglement (MoE) game, two players who do not communicate try to simultaneously guess a referee’s measurement outcome on a shared quantum state they prepared. We study the prototypical example of a game where the referee measures in either the computational or Hadamard basis and informs the players of her choice. We show that this game satisfies a rigidity property similar to what is known for some nonlocal games. That is, in order to win optimally, the players’ strategy must be of a specific form, namely a convex combination of four unentangled optimal strategies generated by the Breidbart state. We extend this to show that strategies that win near-optimally must also be near an optimal state of this form. We also show rigidity for multiple copies of the game played in parallel. We give three applications: (1) We construct for the first time a weak string erasure (WSE) scheme where the security does not rely on limitations on the parties’ hardware. Instead, we add a prover, which enables security via the rigidity of this MoE game. (2) We show that the WSE scheme can be used to achieve bit commitment in a model where it is impossible classically. (3) We achieve everlasting-secure randomness expansion in the model of trusted but leaky measurement and untrusted preparation and measurements by two isolated devices, while relying only on the temporary assumption of pseudorandom functions. This achieves randomness expansion without the need for shared entanglement.

Introduction

Monogamy-of-entanglement (MoE) games provide an intuitive way to understand the strength of quantum multipartite correlations. Such games pit two cooperating players, usually named Bob and Charlie, against an honest referee, Alice. The players try, without communicating, to simultaneously guess the outcome of Alice’s measurement on a quantum state provided by the players and with which they may share entanglement freely. Interestingly, any one of the players can always correctly guess the result of any projective measurement Alice makes, by providing her with one register of a maximally entangled state, whereas two players are prohibited from
simultaneously
doing as well since tripartite correlations of the shared state are weaker.

The quintessential MoE game is the original example introduced by Tomamichel, Fehr, Kaniewski, and Wehner
[
TFKW13
]
. In this game, Alice’s space consists of a single qubit and she measures either in the computational basis or the Hadamard basis with equal probability to get a one-bit answer. As shown there, Bob and Charlie can win with probability at most
\cos^{2}{\frac{\pi}{8}}\approx 0.85
. Further, this game has an exponentially small winning probability when played in parallel, which was shown to yield applications such as a one-sided device independent quantum key distribution (DI-QKD) scheme. The TFKW game has a particularly simple optimal strategy: Bob and Charlie share no entanglement; they just send Alice a pure Breidbart state
\lvert\beta\rangle\propto\lvert 0\rangle+\lvert+\rangle
, that sits directly between the computational zero
\lvert 0\rangle
and the Hadamard zero
\lvert+\rangle
, and always guess
0
for the measurement outcome. It is straightforward to see that, due to the symmetries of Alice’s measurement bases under the action of the Pauli operators, there are at least
4
optimal unentangled strategies: the Wiesner-Breidbart states
\lvert\beta\rangle

Z\lvert\beta\rangle

. But the question remains: are these all the possible optimal strategies? Particularly, are there optimal strategies where the players use entanglement? This question is tantamount to asking about the
rigidity
of the TFKW game.

The idea of rigidity, first formally introduced by Mayers and Yao
[
MY04
]
, is that certain games can be used to “self-test” quantum states: if such a game is won with high enough probability, then the self-test property tells us that the players must hold some quantum state, up to local isometry. More general are robust self-tests, where even a near-optimal winning probability gives a guarantee that the state is near to this optimal one. Up until now, the study of rigidity has been limited to
nonlocal games
. Nonlocal games are similar to monogamy-of-entanglement games (both belong to the class of extended nonlocal games
[
JMRW16
]
), except the referee is classical while the players might be asked different questions and be expected to provide different answers. This area of study grew around the CHSH game. This game, introduced by Clauser, Horne, Shimony, and Holt
[
CHSH69
]
as a discrete-variable analogue of a Bell inequality
[
Bel64
]
, was known, even before rigidity was formalised, to self-test a maximally-entangled state on two qubits
[
Tsi93
]
. This result was later extended to be robust
[
MYS12
]
and to hold under parallel repetition
[
Col17
]
. The rigidity of the CHSH game has found many applications: for example it was used to construct a protocol for quantum delegated computation, which was then used to show the equivalence of complexity classes
\textsf{QMIP}=\textsf{MIP}^{\ast}

[
RUV13
]
. Other examples of nonlocal games include the Mermin-Peres magic square game — which can always be won and self-tests two copies of the maximally entangled state, and was used to show
\textsf{MIP}^{\ast}=\textsf{RE}

[
JNV+21
]
— and more generally linear constraint games
[
CMMN20
]

Our main contribution is to prove the first rigidity result for a monogamy-of-entanglement game:

Main Theorem

(informal)
.

The state of any optimal strategy for the TFKW game is given as a convex combination of the unentangled optimal states
\lvert\beta\rangle
,
X\lvert\beta\rangle
,
Z\lvert\beta\rangle
,
XZ\lvert\beta\rangle
. This is robust and extends to multiple rounds played in parallel.

By convex combination, we mean a superposition of tensor product states where the components on Alice’s register are the optimal Wiesner-Breidbart states and the components on Bob and Charlie’s register have orthogonal supports. That is, Bob and Charlie can simultaneously distinguish which unentangled optimal state Alice receives. Note that a similar notion of rigidity holds for some nonlocal games: for example, Mančinska, Nielsen, and Prakash
[
MNP21
]
show that the glued magic square game self-tests a convex combination of inequivalent optimal strategies. This requirement on optimal strategies of the TFKW game forces Bob and Charlie to
not
use any of their shared entanglement while playing.

For applications, it is often necessary to extend the rigidity result to be robust and to the scenario where games are played in parallel. This is because playing the game only once gives essentially no information on the winning probability of the strategy used. What Alice can do to remedy this is to get Bob and Charlie to play many games at the same time and use that information to build up statistics about how often they win. As such, she needs the result to be robust — a guarantee that the state is near-optimal if the winning probability is near-optimal — as the sampling cannot quite show that the strategy is optimal. Also, she needs the result to hold for games played in parallel, to ensure that there is nothing different and more exotic they may do using their entanglement to win multiple games optimally. We show that the rigidity of the TFKW game holds in this general case.

Application 1: Weak string erasure.

We construct for the first time a weak string erasure (WSE) scheme that is secure against adversaries with unrestricted quantum systems. WSE is a cryptographic primitive introduced by König, Wehner, and Wullschleger
[
KWW12
]
that allows the sharing of partial information between mistrustful parties, a sender Alice and a receiver Bob. In WSE, Alice receives a random bit string
x
while Bob receives a substring; Bob knows which bits of
x
he holds but is unable to determine the remainder, while Alice is unable to determine which substring Bob holds. As shown in
[
KWW12
]
, WSE implies both bit commitment and oblivious transfer from Alice to Bob. Since these are information-theoretically impossible in both the classical and quantum plain model
[
May96
,
LC97
,
BS16
]
, additional assumptions are necessary to be able to realise WSE. In
[
KWW12
]
, they use a noisy-storage model to limit the amount of storage a dishonest party can access.

In our model, we introduce a third party, a prover Charlie who is initially in full collusion with Bob, but who is isolated from Bob once Alice measures. Under the assumption of a public broadcast from Alice to Bob and Charlie, we are able to exploit the rigidity of the TFKW game to arrive at a secure scheme for WSE. This scheme requires no entanglement for the honest parties and may be run with one round of communication — in particular it can be realised as a relativistic prepare-and-measure scheme.

Application 2: Bit commitment.

Two-prover bit commitment was studied before in the classical context
[
BGKW88
]
, where it was shown that separating the
sender
into two isolated parties can be used to ensure the binding property (see also
[
CSST11
]
). In contrast, the WSE that we achieve implies, using
[
KWW12
]
, a bit commitment with two isolated
receivers
and a single sender. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first such scheme; furthermore, we show that, with classical communication only, our model reduces to the single-receiver model — where unconditionally secure bit commitment is impossible — meaning that we have identified a new qualitative advantage for quantum communication in cryptography.

Application 3: Everlasting randomness expansion.

Randomness is a precious resource for computation and cryptography. Pseudorandom generators are functions that produce large amounts of randomness from a small random seed, but the quality of this randomness is inherently based on a computational assumption,
e.g.
the existence of one-way functions. Thus, given sufficient computational power or time, an adversary can eventually break the scheme.

Quantum entanglement has long been known to provide an advantage in creating unconditionally secure randomness
[
Col06
,
AM16
]
. By verifying that two isolated parties violate a Bell inequality, a verifier is able to guarantee, due to the randomness inherent in quantum mechanics, that the players’ outputs provide intrinsic, fresh randomness. Such schemes are able to yield exponential randomness expansion
[
VV12
]
. Further, using the rigidity of the CHSH game, it is possible to guarantee that the randomness is secure against side information, and thus allow composition, providing arbitrarily large randomness expansion
[
CY14
]
.
The technical difficulty with these schemes is that they require entanglement between isolated parties, which remains difficult to generate in sufficient quantities. Based on the experimental demonstration of a loophole-free Bell inequality violation
[
HBD+15
]
, recent work has been able to achieve a randomness expansion of 24% over a period of 91 hours; however, the new randomness is only secure against classical and not quantum side information
[
SZB+21
]
.

Here, we give a protocol where entanglement between isolated parties is not required in order to expand randomness. In order to achieve this, we make use of an adapted version of the WSE protocol as described above. First, the questions Alice asks are pseudorandom rather than uniformly random; this allows Alice to start with only a small random seed. With polynomial overhead, we can extract statistically near-uniform randomness using the rigidity of the TFKW game. To do this, Alice uses many of the bits to verify that the shared state is near the state of an optimal strategy, and then extracts randomness using her knowledge of the remainder of the state. We thus require the computational assumption to hold during the interaction of the protocol, after which the output randomness becomes nearly indistinguishable from uniform, even to an unbounded adversary — this concept is called
everlasting
security and was previously studied in the context of quantum key distribution
[
SML10
]
and multi-party computation
[
Unr13
]
. Furthermore, we note that in our model, all of the measurement settings Alice uses can be leaked as she measures, without compromising the security or uniformity of the randomness.

1.1
Summary of Techniques

In this section, we summarise the techniques used to show our results. First, we mention our interpretation of MoE games, and then go through the general method we follow to prove rigidity of the TFKW game and apply it to achieve weak string erasure and everlasting randomness expansion.

Monogamy-of-entanglement games.

We give an expression of a two-answer MoE game, such as the TFKW game, in terms of a game polynomial where the variables are Bob and Charlie’s observables. In this way, we may study the strategies of a game by studying the positivity of this operator-valued polynomial. This technique expands upon one that has been used previously to study nonlocal games
[
CMMN20
]
.

Rigidity.

We present a sum-of-squares (SOS) decomposition of the game polynomial for the TFKW game. The state
\lvert\psi\rangle
of any optimal strategy is an eigenspace of the game polynomial in terms of the observables of that strategy, which provides a selection of relations for the observables. There are two types of relations that come out: one allows to exchange Bob and Charlie’s observables and the other gives that
\lvert\psi\rangle
is an eigenvector of a particular sum of observables. In particular, these imply that either of the players’ observables must commute with respect to the state, generating a
\lvert\psi\rangle
-representation of
\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{2}
. As such, we invoke the Gowers-Hatami theorem as in
[
Vid18
]
to locally dilate the players’ space isometrically and transform this into a bona fide representation. These observables are simultaneously diagonalisable, so the dilated shared space can be decomposed as a direct sum of orthogonal subspaces on which they act as scalars. Returning to the relations from the SOS decomposition using the dilated observables allows us to constrain where the shared state lives in this orthogonal sum and show that the components on Alice’s space must take the form
X^{s_{0}}Z^{s_{1}}\lvert\beta\rangle
.

We then build on this technique to show the rigidity in the robust case. Here, however, since the winning probability is assumed to be some
\varepsilon>0
smaller than optimal, the value of each of the terms in the SOS decomposition are not zero when acting on the state, but rather in
O(\varepsilon)
. Nevertheless, we can use the relations to get an approximate representation, which we dilate similarly with Gowers-Hatami. Of course, this cannot give that the state is exactly a convex combination as above, but rather that its projection onto the unwanted subspaces is small, giving that this is
O(\sqrt{\varepsilon})
close to an optimal state.

We show the exact rigidity for a parallel repetition of
n
TFKW games by extracting many optimal strategies for a single game, assuming Bob and Charlie can guess each of the answer bits for the repeated games with optimal probability. We show first that the observables related to each copy of the TFKW game must act in the same way on
\lvert\psi\rangle
by using the rigidity decomposition, and then use this as tool to show that all of the observables commute. This induces, again with Gowers-Hatami, a representation of
(\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{2})^{n}
and lets us conclude in a similar way as for the single-game case that the state must be a convex combination of tensor products of states of the form
X^{s_{0}}Z^{s_{1}}\lvert\beta\rangle
.

The most general rigidity result we prove is the robust case of the parallel repetition of TFKW games. To generalise the exact-case method, we use a technique of
[
Col17
]
to extract sufficiently many strategies for TFKW that win near-optimally. Proceeding similarly as before, we get that the state is
O(n^{3}\sqrt{\varepsilon})
away from an optimal state.

Finally, we adapt a technique of
[
RUV13
]
to be able to pass from winning statistics Alice may observe when playing TFKW games in parallel to a guarantee on the winning probability of a large subset of the games. Knowing upper bounds on the winning probability of each of the games, we can couple independent Bernoulli random variables to each game, and use Hoeffding’s inequality to show that there is but a low probability that the players win most games while the winning probability for too many of them is more than
\varepsilon
away from optimal.

Weak string erasure.

We construct a WSE scheme whose security is based upon the rigidity of the TFKW game. The receiver Bob prepares a state
\rho_{ABC}
shared between Alice, Bob, and Charlie, where Alice holds
N\in\texttt{poly}(n)
qubits. In the honest case, this has the form of an unentangled optimal strategy for the parallel-repeated TFKW game. Then, Alice verifies that the state must be near an optimal state for the TFKW game by playing the game with Bob and Charlie using
N-n
of her qubits. This check fails with exponentially small probability in
n
. On the remaining
n
qubits, however, she measures in a random Wiesner-Breidbart basis,
i.e.
either the basis
\lvert\beta\rangle,XZ\lvert\beta\rangle
or the basis
Z\lvert\beta\rangle,X\lvert\beta\rangle
. Giving Bob the information about which basis she chose for these
n
qubits, he may guess on average half of the bits and have no information about the rest. This provides security against a dishonest Bob. For security against a dishonest Alice, we note that the rigidity still gives Bob the freedom to choose the Wiesner-Breidbart state on the register he gives to Alice. It can be seen from
Fig.

1
that these states constitute a pair of mutually-unbiased bases. Therefore, if Bob chooses the state randomly, this eliminates Alice’s chance of guessing which bits he knows. The isolation requirement between Bob and Charlie is necessary to prevent an attack where they jointly share a maximally entangled state with Alice and then can always measure each bit in the correct basis. The requirement that Alice broadcast publicly which
n
bits are used to generate the output string is to prevent an attack where she asks Bob and Charlie to play the TFKW game on different bits, and uses Charlie’s replies to extract information about Bob’s prepared conjugate-coding basis.

Everlasting Randomness Expansion.

We use the rigidity of the TFKW game, as well as a computational assumption on the existence of pseudorandom generators, to construct a randomness expansion scheme that is everlasting, in the sense that the output randomness is guaranteed to be near-uniform in trace norm, as long as the computational assumption is not broken during the execution of the protocol.
As in the previous protocol, Alice interacts with a pair of adversaries, Bob and Charlie, and they all share an adversarially-prepared state
\rho_{ABC}
, where Alice holds
N\in\texttt{poly}(n)
qubits. Alice plays the TFKW game on
N-n
of the qubits to verify that the shared state is near an optimal state. However, rather than choosing the locations and questions for the TFKW game rounds uniformly at random, she chooses them by sampling the output of a pseudorandom generator, given a random seed. Bob and Charlie, who are assumed to be computationally bounded, have only a negligible probability of distinguishing this from the uniformly random case, and thus this check has only a negligibly small probability of failure. Alice measures each of the remaining
n
qubits in the basis
\lvert 0_{\circlearrowleft}\rangle,\lvert 1_{\circlearrowleft}\rangle
that diagonalises the Pauli
Y
operator. Since this basis is mutually unbiased with both of the Wiesner-Breidbart bases, the outcome is nearly uniformly random, and neither Bob nor Charlie have information on what this outcome is, as long as they stay isolated.

1.2
Further Related Work

The study of monogamy-of-entanglement games is a burgeoning field in quantum information, with several applications to cryptography. As mentioned earlier, these games were introduced in
[
TFKW13
]
, where they also introduced an important technique using overlaps of measurement operators to upper bound the winning probabilities. Johnston, Mittal, Russo, and Watrous
[
JMRW16
]
adapted the overlap technique to show that all MoE games with two questions can be won using an unentangled strategy and satisfy perfect parallel repetition, and gave a generalisation of the NPA hierarchy
[
NPA08
]
that can be used on MoE games. Broadbent and Lord
[
BL20
]
used the TFKW game to study uncloneable encryption in the quantum random oracle model. Most recently, Coladangelo, Liu, Liu, and Zhandry
[
CLLZ21
]
defined a new MoE game of a slightly different style where Alice measures in a basis of subspace coset states, and Bob and Charlie try to guess different strings, for which an upper bound on the winning probability was shown by Culf and Vidick
[
CV22
]
using overlaps. In
[
CLLZ21
]
, they use this game along with some computational assumptions to construct schemes for uncloneable decryption and copy-protection of pseudorandom functions.

The idea of using the probabilistic nature of quantum mechanics to create randomness is well-established. Colbeck
[
Col06
]
pointed out that violations of Bell inequalities can be used to generate randomness, which, as mentioned, was expanded by Vazirani and Vidick
[
VV12
]
, and then Coudron and Yuen
[
CY14
]
, to give very powerful device-independent randomness expansion protocols. Our scheme may be contrasted with the work of Brakerski, Christiano, Mahadev, Vazirani, and Vidick
[
BCM+18
]
, where they also use a short-term computational assumption to achieve everlasting randomness expansion. They use the learning with errors (LWE) assumption to construct noisy trapdoor claw-free functions, using which it is possible to verify that an untrusted quantum device approximately prepares states in the Hadamard basis and then measures in the computational basis. This protocol does not require a communication assumption or a trusted measurement, as ours does. On the other hand, it requires a specific computational assumption, far less general than existence of pseudorandom generators, and requires a full fault-tolerant quantum computer in the honest case, rather than simply preparation and measurement of single-qubit states. Less demanding models, where some aspects of the devices are trusted, have also been considered. In a semi-device-independent model, it is assumed that the dimensions of the devices’ Hilbert spaces are constrained
[
PB11
]
. Randomness expansion schemes in this model do not require entanglement, but make use of strong assumptions on the devices: a finite distribution of states and measurements, and no entanglement with another system
[
LYW+11
]
. There are also more asymmetric models, like quantum steering, where one of the devices may be completely trusted while the other is untrusted
[
BCW+12
]
.

, and
\lvert\psi_{i}\rangle\in\textsf{H}
is a pure quantum state. The set of mixed states corresponds to the set of positive operators with trace
1
, and we write this set
\mathcal{D}(\textsf{H})
. Every mixed state may be purified by appending some auxiliary register (
Lemma

A.1
). We call a state
classical
if it is diagonal in the canonical basis of
H
– it corresponds exactly to a probability distribution on
H
.

A quantum measurement is represented by a positive operator-valued measurement (POVM), which is a map

\displaystyle\begin{matrix}P:&X&\rightarrow&\mathcal{P}(\textsf{H})\\
&x&\mapsto&P_{x}\end{matrix}

(1)

where
X
is the (finite) set of possible measurement outcomes and
\sum_{x}P_{x}=\mathbb{I}_{H}
. The probability of measuring outcome
x
given a state
\rho\in\mathcal{D}(\textsf{H})
is given by Born’s rule as
\Tr(P_{x}\rho)
. Again, any measurement may be purified, by adding auxiliary registers, to a projector-valued measurement (PVM), which is a measurement where the
P_{x}
are orthogonal projectors
P_{x}P_{y}=\delta_{x,y}P_{x}
(
Lemma

A.2
). This then corresponds to a measurement in some basis followed by a deterministic classical function.

Given two registers
H
and
K
, the corresponding joint quantum system is given by the tensor product Hilbert space
\textsf{H}\otimes\textsf{K}
. If necessary, we (perhaps inconsistently) add the name of the register as a subscript onto a state/operator to distinguish which register it acts on/belongs to. A state
\lvert\psi\rangle\in\textsf{H}\otimes\textsf{K}
is
separable
if it can be written as a pure tensor of the form
\lvert\psi\rangle=\lvert v\rangle_{H}\otimes\lvert w\rangle_{K}
. Otherwise, the state is
entangled
. Nevertheless, any pure state
\lvert\psi\rangle\in\textsf{H}\otimes\textsf{K}
admits a
Schmidt decomposition

\lvert\psi\rangle=\sum_{i}\sqrt{p_{i}}\lvert i\rangle_{H}\otimes\lvert i\rangle_{K}
where the
p_{i}\geq 0
, and
\vphantom{\lvert i\rangle_{H}}\left\{\smash{\lvert i\rangle_{H}}\right\}
and
\vphantom{\lvert i\rangle_{K}}\left\{\smash{\lvert i\rangle_{K}}\right\}
are sets of orthonormal vectors. An operation is
local
if it acts as a pure tensor. The
partial trace
of a register
H
is the linear map
\Tr_{H}:\mathcal{L}(\textsf{H}\otimes\textsf{K})\rightarrow\mathcal{L}(\textsf{K})
defined on pure tensors as
\Tr_{H}(A\otimes B)=\Tr(A)B
(and extended linearly), corresponding to making a measurement on the space
H
and then forgetting the result. For a state
\rho_{HK}\in\mathcal{D}(\textsf{H}\otimes\textsf{K})
, we write the state on
H
as
\rho_{H}=\Tr_{K}(\rho_{HK})
.

The Euclidean norm
\|\lvert v\rangle\|=\sqrt{\!\vphantom{v}\left\langle\smash{v}\middle|\smash{v}\right\rangle}
gives the appropriate distance metric between pure states. For mixed states, we use the
trace distance

\displaystyle d_{\Tr}(\rho,\sigma)=\|\rho-\sigma\|_{\Tr}=\frac{1}{2}\Tr\lvert\rho-\sigma\rvert,

(2)

where the absolute value of an operator is
\lvert L\rvert=\sqrt{L^{\dagger}L}
. For other operators, we use the
operator norm

\displaystyle\|L\|=\sup\vphantom{\|L\lvert v\rangle\|\vphantom{\big|}\vphantom{v}\left\langle\smash{v}\middle|\smash{v}\right\rangle=1}\left\{{\|L\lvert v\rangle\|}\middle|{\vphantom{\big|}\vphantom{v}\left\langle\smash{v}\middle|\smash{v}\right\rangle=1}\right\}.

(3)

Important properties of these norms are given in
Appendix

A
.

An important system is the
bit

Q=\mathbb{Z}_{2}=\{0,1\}
, and its corresponding Hilbert space, the
qubit

\textsf{Q}\cong\mathbb{C}^{2}
. The basis
\vphantom{\lvert 0\rangle,\lvert 1\rangle}\left\{\smash{\lvert 0\rangle,\lvert 1\rangle}\right\}
is the
computational basis
and the basis
\vphantom{\lvert+\rangle,\lvert-\rangle}\left\{{\lvert+\rangle,\lvert-\rangle}\right\}
where
\lvert+\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\lvert 0\rangle+\lvert 1\rangle)
and
\lvert-\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\lvert 0\rangle-\lvert 1\rangle)
is the
Hadamard basis
. The
Hadamard operator
is the Hermitian unitary
H:\textsf{Q}\rightarrow\textsf{Q}
that maps the computational basis to the Hadamard basis, which is expressed in either basis as

\displaystyle H=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{bmatrix}1&1\\
1&-1\end{bmatrix}.

(4)

In the computational basis, the
Pauli operators
are

\displaystyle Z=\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\
0&-1\end{bmatrix}\qquad X=\begin{bmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\end{bmatrix}\qquad Y=\begin{bmatrix}0&-i\\
i&0\end{bmatrix}.

(5)

It is direct to check that
Z
and
X
anticommute, and that the Hadamard diagonalises
X
, so that
X=HZH
. We define the
Breidbart
operator
\be:\textsf{Q}\rightarrow\textsf{Q}
as the Hermitian unitary that diagonalises
H
, so
H=\be Z\be
and in the computational basis

\displaystyle\be=\begin{bmatrix}\cos\frac{\pi}{8}&\sin\frac{\pi}{8}\\
\sin\frac{\pi}{8}&-\cos\frac{\pi}{8}\end{bmatrix};

(6)

and the
Breidbart state

\lvert\beta\rangle=\be\lvert 0\rangle
. Important relations that follow from the definition are
H\lvert\beta\rangle=\lvert\beta\rangle
,
Z\lvert\beta\rangle=\be\lvert+\rangle
,
X\lvert\beta\rangle=\be\lvert-\rangle
, and
ZX\lvert\beta\rangle=\be\lvert 1\rangle
. Finally, we define the
conjugate-coding/Wiesner/BB84
states on
n\in\mathbb{N}
qubits for
x,\theta\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{n}
as
\lvert x^{\theta}\rangle=H^{\theta}\lvert x\rangle=H^{\theta_{1}}\lvert x_{1}\rangle\otimes\cdots\otimes H^{\theta_{n}}\lvert x_{n}\rangle\in\textsf{Q}^{\otimes n}
; and we also call the states
\be^{\otimes n}\lvert x^{\theta}\rangle
the
Wiesner-Breidbart states
.

More details are given in any of the many good resources for quantum information, such as

2.3
Probability

Any probability distribution on a finite set
X
may be represented by a function
\pi:X\rightarrow[0,1]
such that
\sum_{x\in X}\pi(x)=1
. Then, the probability of an event
S\subseteq X
is
\Pr(S)=\sum_{x\in S}\pi(x)
. For any function
f:X\rightarrow\textsf{V}
, where
V
is a
\mathbb{C}
-vector space, we write the expectation value with respect to this distribution as

\displaystyle\underset{x\leftarrow\pi}{\text{\raisebox{-3.0pt}{\scalebox{1.5}{$\mathbb{E}$}}}}f(x)=\sum_{x\in X}\pi(x)f(x).

(7)

We distinguish the uniform probability distribution
\mathbbm{u}:X\rightarrow[0,1]
, which is
\mathbbm{u}(x)=\frac{1}{|X|}
; and we write
\mathbbm{E}_{x\in X}
to mean
\mathbbm{E}_{x\leftarrow\mathbbm{u}}
. Any probability distribution
\pi
on
X
can be represented as a classical state
\mu_{\pi}=\mathbbm{E}_{x\leftarrow\pi}\vphantom{x}\left\lvert\smash{x}\middle\rangle\!\middle\langle\smash{x}\right\rvert\in\mathcal{D}(\textsf{X})
, where we write in particular the maximally mixed state as the classical state of the uniform distribution
\mu_{X}:=\mu_{\mathbbm{u}}
. For a random variable
\Gamma
, we use the same notattion
\underset{x\leftarrow\Gamma}{\text{\raisebox{-3.0pt}{\scalebox{1.5}{$\mathbb{E}$}}}}
to denote
x
sampled from the image of
\Gamma
with repsect to its distribution. If
\Gamma
has image in a vector space, we write its expectation as
\underset{x\leftarrow\Gamma}{\text{\raisebox{-3.0pt}{\scalebox{1.5}{$\mathbb{E}$}}}}x=\mathbb{E}\Gamma
.

An important bound we make use of is Hoeffding’s inequality. Let
\Gamma_{1},\ldots,\Gamma_{n}
be independent random variables with image in
[0,1]
, and write their sum
\Gamma=\Gamma_{1}+\ldots+\Gamma_{n}
. The inequality states that for any
t\geq 0
,

\displaystyle\Pr\left\lparen\Gamma-\mathbb{E}\Gamma\geq t\right\rparen\leq e^{-2\frac{t^{2}}{n}}

(8)

2.4
Exact and Approximate Representation Theory

Throughout this section, let
G
be a finite group.

A
representation
of
G
over
\mathbb{C}
is a group homomorphism
\gamma:G\rightarrow\mathcal{GL}(\textsf{V})
, where
V
is a finite-dimensional
\mathbb{C}
-vector space. Two representations
\gamma_{i}:G\rightarrow\mathcal{GL}(\textsf{V}_{i})
for
i=0,1
are
isomorphic
if there exists an invertible linear map
U:\textsf{V}_{0}\rightarrow\textsf{V}_{1}
such that
U\gamma_{0}(g)=\gamma_{1}(g)U

\forall\,g\in G
(intertwining operator). Every representation is isomorphic to a unitary representation, i.e. where
\gamma(G)\subseteq\mathcal{U}(\textsf{V})
. A representation is
irreducible
if the only subspaces invariant under the action of
G
are
V
and
0
. By Maschke’s theorem, every representation of
G
decomposes as a direct sum of irreducible representations. Let
\textsf{Irr}(G)
be a set of representatives for the isomorphism classes of the irreducible representations;
\textsf{Irr}(G)
has finitely many elements and the sum
\sum_{\gamma\in\textsf{Irr}(G)}d_{\gamma}\Tr(\gamma(g))=|G|\delta_{g,1}
, where
d_{\gamma}
is the dimension of the representation
\gamma
. The only irreducible representations of an Abelian group are
1
-dimensional. The important example we see in this paper is
\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{n}
under addition. The irreducible representations are indexed by the elements
s\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{n}
, and they take the form
\gamma_{s}(x)=(-1)^{x\cdot s}
, where
x\cdot s=x_{1}s_{1}+x_{2}s_{2}+\ldots+s_{n}x_{n}
.

This concludes our whirlwind summary of some of the representation theory of finite groups; complete explanations are available from many perspectives, such as
[
Wei03
,
Ser77
]
. We now go into rather more detail about the theory of approximate representations, which hinges on a result of Gowers and Hatami
[
GH17
]
.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
V
and
W
be Hilbert spaces and let
\lvert\psi\rangle\in\textsf{V}\otimes\textsf{W}
. For
\varepsilon\geq 0
, an
(\varepsilon,\lvert\psi\rangle)
-representation
of
G
is a map
f:G\rightarrow\mathcal{U}(\textsf{V})
such that, for every
y\in G
,

\displaystyle\underset{x\in G}{\text{\raisebox{-3.0pt}{\scalebox{1.5}{$\mathbb{E}$}}}}\|\left\lparen f(x)f(y)-f(xy)\right\rparen\lvert\psi\rangle\|^{2}\leq\varepsilon^{2}.

(9)

The following theorem characterises how close an approximate representation is to a true representation.

Theorem 2.2

(Gowers-Hatami)
.

Let
f:G\rightarrow\mathcal{U}(\textsf{V})
be a
(\varepsilon,\lvert\psi\rangle)
-representation. Then, there exists a Hilbert space
\textsf{V}^{\prime}
, an isometry
V:\textsf{V}\rightarrow\textsf{V}^{\prime}
, and a representation
g:G\rightarrow\mathcal{U}(\textsf{V}^{\prime})
such that, for any
x\in G
,

\displaystyle\|\left\lparen Vf(x)-g(x)V\right\rparen\lvert\psi\rangle\|\leq\varepsilon.

(10)

Note that the above definition and theorem have a slightly different form from how they were presented in previous work
[
NV17
]
[
Vid18
,
CMMN20
]
.

The proof given here is almost identical to the proof of
[
Vid18
]
, which uses the notion of the Fourier transform of a function acting on a group. Given a function
f:G\rightarrow\textsf{V}
for
V
a
\mathbb{C}
-vector space, the Fourier transform is the map acting on
\textsf{Irr}(G)
defined as

\hat{f}(\gamma)=\frac{1}{|G|}\sum_{g\in G}f(g)\otimes\gamma(g).

It is straightforward to check that the inverse transform is

f(g)=\sum_{\gamma\in\textsf{Irr}(G)}d_{\gamma}\Tr_{V_{\gamma}}\left\lparen(\mathbb{I}_{V}\otimes\gamma(g^{-1}))\hat{f}(\gamma)\right\rparen.

Proof of
Theorem

2.2
.

First, we construct the necessary objects. The dilated space is

\displaystyle\textsf{V}^{\prime}=\bigoplus_{\gamma\in\textsf{Irr}(G)}\textsf{V}\otimes\textsf{V}_{\gamma}\otimes\textsf{V}_{\gamma},

(11)

and the representation is taken to be

\displaystyle g(x)=\bigoplus_{\gamma\in\textsf{Irr}(G)}\mathbb{I}\otimes\mathbb{I}_{\gamma}\otimes\overline{\gamma(x)},

(12)

where the complex conjugate on
\textsf{V}_{\gamma}
is taken with respect to a fixed basis
\lvert i\rangle_{\gamma}
for
i=1,...,d_{\gamma}
. Then, we take the isometry to be

\displaystyle V\lvert v\rangle=\bigoplus_{\gamma\in\textsf{Irr}(G)}\sqrt{d_{\gamma}}\sum_{i=1}^{d_{\gamma}}\hat{f}(\gamma)(\lvert v\rangle\otimes\lvert i\rangle_{\gamma})\otimes\lvert i\rangle_{\gamma}.

(13)

This is in fact an isometry as

\displaystyle\begin{split}V^{\dagger}V&=\sum_{\gamma\in\textsf{Irr}(G)}d_{\gamma}\sum_{i=1}^{d_{\gamma}}(\mathbb{I}\otimes\langle i\rvert_{\gamma})\hat{f}(\gamma)^{\dagger}\otimes\langle i\rvert_{\gamma}\sum_{i=1}^{d_{\gamma}}\hat{f}(\gamma)(\mathbb{I}\otimes\lvert i\rangle_{\gamma})\otimes\lvert i\rangle_{\gamma}\\
&=\sum_{\gamma\in\textsf{Irr}(G)}d_{\gamma}\sum_{i=1}^{d_{\gamma}}(\mathbb{I}\otimes\langle i\rvert_{\gamma})\hat{f}(\gamma)^{\dagger}\hat{f}(\gamma)(\mathbb{I}\otimes\lvert i\rangle_{\gamma})\\
&=\underset{x,y\in G}{\text{\raisebox{-3.0pt}{\scalebox{1.5}{$\mathbb{E}$}}}}\sum_{\gamma\in\textsf{Irr}(G)}d_{\gamma}\sum_{i=1}^{d_{\gamma}}f(x)^{\dagger}f(y)\vphantom{i\gamma(x)^{\dagger}\gamma(y)i}\left\langle\smash{i}\middle\rvert\smash{\gamma(x)^{\dagger}\gamma(y)}\middle\rvert\smash{i}\right\rangle_{\gamma}\\
&=\underset{x,y\in G}{\text{\raisebox{-3.0pt}{\scalebox{1.5}{$\mathbb{E}$}}}}f(x)^{\dagger}f(y)\sum_{\gamma\in\textsf{Irr}(G)}d_{\gamma}\Tr\lparen\gamma(x^{-1}y)\rparen\\
&=\underset{x,y\in G}{\text{\raisebox{-3.0pt}{\scalebox{1.5}{$\mathbb{E}$}}}}f(x)^{\dagger}f(y)|G|\delta_{x,y}=\underset{x\in G}{\text{\raisebox{-3.0pt}{\scalebox{1.5}{$\mathbb{E}$}}}}f(x)^{\dagger}f(x)\\
&=\mathbb{I}.\end{split}

(14)

Thus, with a similarly long equation we may simplify

\displaystyle\begin{split}V^{\dagger}g(x)V&=\sum_{\gamma\in\textsf{Irr}(G)}d_{\gamma}\sum_{i,j=1}^{d_{\gamma}}(\mathbb{I}\otimes\langle i\rvert_{\gamma})\hat{f}(\gamma)^{\dagger}\hat{f}(\gamma)(\mathbb{I}\otimes\lvert j\rangle_{\gamma})\vphantom{i\overline{\gamma(x)}j}\left\langle\smash{i}\middle\rvert\smash{\overline{\gamma(x)}}\middle\rvert\smash{j}\right\rangle_{\gamma}\\
&=\underset{y,z\in G}{\text{\raisebox{-3.0pt}{\scalebox{1.5}{$\mathbb{E}$}}}}f(y)^{\dagger}f(z)\sum_{\gamma\in\textsf{Irr}(G)}d_{\gamma}\sum_{i,j=1}^{d_{\gamma}}\vphantom{i\gamma(y)^{\dagger}\gamma(z)j}\left\langle\smash{i}\middle\rvert\smash{\gamma(y)^{\dagger}\gamma(z)}\middle\rvert\smash{j}\right\rangle\!\!\!\vphantom{j\gamma(x)^{\dagger}i}\left\langle\smash{j}\middle\rvert\smash{\gamma(x)^{\dagger}}\middle\rvert\smash{i}\right\rangle_{\gamma}\\
&=\underset{y,z\in G}{\text{\raisebox{-3.0pt}{\scalebox{1.5}{$\mathbb{E}$}}}}f(y)^{\dagger}f(z)\sum_{\gamma\in\textsf{Irr}(G)}d_{\gamma}\Tr\left\lparen\gamma(y^{-1}zx^{-1})\right\rparen\\
&=\underset{y,z\in G}{\text{\raisebox{-3.0pt}{\scalebox{1.5}{$\mathbb{E}$}}}}f(y)^{\dagger}f(z)|G|\delta_{z,yx}\\
&=\underset{y\in G}{\text{\raisebox{-3.0pt}{\scalebox{1.5}{$\mathbb{E}$}}}}f(y)^{\dagger}f(yx).\end{split}

(15)

Noting that
\|\left\lparen f(x)f(y)-f(xy)\right\rparen\lvert\psi\rangle\|^{2}=2\vphantom{\psi}\left\langle\smash{\psi}\middle|\smash{\psi}\right\rangle-2\latRe\vphantom{\psi f(y)^{\dagger}f(x)^{\dagger}f(xy)\psi}\left\langle\smash{\psi}\middle\rvert\smash{f(y)^{\dagger}f(x)^{\dagger}f(xy)}\middle\rvert\smash{\psi}\right\rangle
, we can use the above and the hypothesis to get

\displaystyle\begin{split}\|(Vf(x)-g(x)V)\lvert\psi\rangle\|^{2}&=2\vphantom{\psi}\left\langle\smash{\psi}\middle|\smash{\psi}\right\rangle-2\latRe\vphantom{\psi f(x)^{\dagger}V^{\dagger}g(x)V\psi}\left\langle\smash{\psi}\middle\rvert\smash{f(x)^{\dagger}V^{\dagger}g(x)V}\middle\rvert\smash{\psi}\right\rangle\\
&=\underset{y\in G}{\text{\raisebox{-3.0pt}{\scalebox{1.5}{$\mathbb{E}$}}}}\left\lparen 2\vphantom{\psi}\left\langle\smash{\psi}\middle|\smash{\psi}\right\rangle-2\latRe\vphantom{\psi f(x)^{\dagger}f(y)^{\dagger}f(yx)\psi}\left\langle\smash{\psi}\middle\rvert\smash{f(x)^{\dagger}f(y)^{\dagger}f(yx)}\middle\rvert\smash{\psi}\right\rangle\right\rparen\\
&=\underset{y\in G}{\text{\raisebox{-3.0pt}{\scalebox{1.5}{$\mathbb{E}$}}}}\|\left\lparen f(y)f(x)-f(yx)\right\rparen\lvert\psi\rangle\|^{2}\leq\varepsilon^{2}.\end{split}

(16)

which completes the proof.
∎

Later, we naturally come across approximate representations of
\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{n}
. These representations are induced by approximate commutation relations of the generators. To show they are in fact approximate representations, we need to relate approximate commutation of the generators to approximate commutation of all the elements. First, we tackle the case that needs no extra assumptions,
G=\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{2}
.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
V
and
W
be Hilbert spaces, let
\lvert\psi\rangle\in\textsf{V}\otimes\textsf{W}
, and let
U_{0},U_{1}\in\mathcal{U}(\textsf{V})
be self-inverse such that

\displaystyle\|[U_{0},U_{1}]\lvert\psi\rangle\|\leq\delta,

(17)

for some
\delta\geq 0
. Then, the function
f:\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{2}\rightarrow\mathcal{U}(\textsf{V})
defined by
f(00)=\mathbb{I}
,
f(01)=U_{0}
,
f(10)=U_{1}
, and
f(11)=U_{0}U_{1}
is an
\lparen\tfrac{\delta}{\sqrt{2}},\lvert\psi\rangle\rparen
-representation of
\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{2}
.

Proof.

This is straightforward to check using the hypothesis and the fact that the action by a unitary does not change the Euclidean norm. For
y=00
,

\displaystyle\underset{x\in G}{\text{\raisebox{-3.0pt}{\scalebox{1.5}{$\mathbb{E}$}}}}\|\left\lparen f(x)f(00)-f(x+00)\right\rparen\lvert\psi\rangle\|^{2}=\underset{x\in G}{\text{\raisebox{-3.0pt}{\scalebox{1.5}{$\mathbb{E}$}}}}\|\left\lparen f(x)-f(x)\right\rparen\lvert\psi\rangle\|^{2}=0\leq\frac{\delta^{2}}{2}.

(18)

For
y=01
,

\displaystyle\begin{split}\underset{x\in G}{\text{\raisebox{-3.0pt}{\scalebox{1.5}{$\mathbb{E}$}}}}\|\left\lparen f(x)f(01)-f(x+01)\right\rparen\lvert\psi\rangle\|^{2}&=\frac{1}{4}\big(\|(U_{0}-U_{0})\lvert\psi\rangle\|^{2}+\|(U_{0}^{2}-\mathbb{I})\lvert\psi\rangle\|^{2}\\
&+\|(U_{1}U_{0}-U_{0}U_{1})\lvert\psi\rangle\|^{2}+\|(U_{0}U_{1}U_{0}-U_{1})\lvert\psi\rangle\|^{2}\big)\\
&\leq\frac{\delta^{2}}{2}.\end{split}

(19)

For
y=10
,

\displaystyle\begin{split}\underset{x\in G}{\text{\raisebox{-3.0pt}{\scalebox{1.5}{$\mathbb{E}$}}}}\|\left\lparen f(x)f(10)-f(x+10)\right\rparen\lvert\psi\rangle\|^{2}&=\frac{1}{4}\big(\|(U_{1}-U_{1})\lvert\psi\rangle\|^{2}+\|(U_{0}U_{1}-U_{0}U_{1})\lvert\psi\rangle\|^{2}\\
&+\|(U_{1}^{2}-\mathbb{I})\lvert\psi\rangle\|^{2}+\|(U_{0}U_{1}U_{1}-U_{0})\lvert\psi\rangle\|^{2}\big)\\
&=0\leq\frac{\delta^{2}}{2}.\end{split}

(20)

And finally, for
y=11
,

\displaystyle\begin{split}\underset{x\in G}{\text{\raisebox{-3.0pt}{\scalebox{1.5}{$\mathbb{E}$}}}}\|\left\lparen f(x)f(11)-f(x+11)\right\rparen\lvert\psi\rangle\|^{2}&=\frac{1}{4}\big(\|(U_{0}U_{1}-U_{0}U_{1})\lvert\psi\rangle\|^{2}+\|(U_{0}U_{0}U_{1}-U_{1})\lvert\psi\rangle\|^{2}\\
&+\|(U_{1}U_{0}U_{1}-U_{0})\lvert\psi\rangle\|^{2}+\|(U_{0}U_{1}U_{0}U_{1}-\mathbb{I})\lvert\psi\rangle\|^{2}\big)\\
&\leq\frac{\delta^{2}}{2}.\end{split}

(21)

∎

Extending a result of this form to
\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{n}
for
n>2
requires another condition on the unitaries, in order to be able to use the commutation with respect to
\lvert\psi\rangle
even when there are operators sitting between the state and the unitaries. To do this, we impose an additional relation, arising from our sum-of-squares decomposition, which allows to swap operators onto another register while incurring only a small error.

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
\textsf{V},\textsf{W}
be Hilbert spaces, let
\lvert\psi\rangle\in\textsf{V}\otimes\textsf{W}
, and let
U_{1},...,U_{n},V_{1},...,V_{n}\in\mathcal{U}(\textsf{V})
be a collection of self-inverse unitaries such that

\displaystyle\|[U_{i},U_{j}]\lvert\psi\rangle\|\leq\delta

(22)

\displaystyle\|U_{i}\lvert\psi\rangle-V_{i}\lvert\psi\rangle\|\leq\epsilon

(23)

\displaystyle[U_{i},V_{j}]=0

(24)

for some
\delta,\epsilon\geq 0
. Then, the map

\displaystyle\begin{matrix}f:&\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{n}&\rightarrow&\mathcal{U}(\textsf{V})\\
&x&\mapsto&U^{x},\end{matrix}

(25)

where
U^{x}:=U_{1}^{x_{1}}\cdots U_{n}^{x_{n}}
, is an
(n^{2}(3\epsilon+\delta),\lvert\psi\rangle)
-representation of
\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{n}
.

Proof.

Let
x,y\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{n}
. Then
f(x)f(y)-f(x+y)=U^{x}U^{y}-U^{x+y}
. Write
V^{x}=V_{n}^{x_{n}}\cdots V_{1}^{x_{1}}
Suppose the first nonzero term of
y
is at position
i_{0}
. Write
x^{1}=x_{1}\ldots x_{i_{0}-1}0\ldots 0
and
x^{2}=0\ldots 0x_{i_{0}+1}\ldots x_{n}
and similarly for
y
. By hypothesis, this gives via
Eq.

23

\displaystyle\begin{split}\|(U^{x}U^{y}-U^{x+y})\left\lvert\psi\right\rangle\|&=\|(U^{x^{1}}U_{i_{0}}^{x_{i_{0}}}U^{x^{2}}U_{i_{0}}U^{y^{2}}-U^{x+y})\left\lvert\psi\right\rangle\|\\
&\leq\|(U^{x^{1}}U_{i_{0}}^{x_{i_{0}}}U^{x^{2}}U_{i_{0}}V^{y^{2}}-U^{x+y})\left\lvert\psi\right\rangle\|+|y^{2}|\epsilon.\end{split}

(26)

Now, we can shift
U_{i_{0}}
up through
U^{x^{2}}
by using the commutation relations
Eqs.

22
and
24
and then replacing that term of
U^{x^{2}}
with the corresponding
V
term, and continuing recursively. This adds an error

\displaystyle\|(U^{x^{1}}U_{i_{0}}^{x_{i_{0}}}U^{x^{2}}U_{i_{0}}V^{y^{2}}-U^{x+y})\left\lvert\psi\right\rangle\|\leq\|(U^{x^{1}}U_{i_{0}}^{x_{i_{0}}+y_{i_{0}}}V^{y^{2}}V^{x^{2}}-U^{x+y})\left\lvert\psi\right\rangle\|+|x^{2}|(\epsilon+\delta).

(27)

We can then shift
V^{x^{2}}
and the first term,
i_{1}
, of
y^{2}
back:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\|(U^{x^{1}}U_{i_{0}}^{x_{i_{0}}}U^{x^{2}}U_{i_{0}}V^{y^{2}}-U^{x+y})\left\lvert\psi\right\rangle\|&\leq\|(U^{x^{1}}U_{i_{0}}^{x_{i_{0}}+y_{i_{0}}}U^{x^{2}}U^{y_{i_{1}}}V^{{y^{2}}^{2}}-U^{x+y})\left\lvert\psi\right\rangle\|+|x^{2}|(2\epsilon+\delta)+\epsilon\\
&\leq\|(U^{x^{1}}U_{i_{0}}^{x_{i_{0}}+y_{i_{0}}}U^{x^{2}}U^{y_{i_{1}}}V^{{y^{2}}^{2}}-U^{x+y})\left\lvert\psi\right\rangle\|+n(2\epsilon+\delta)\end{split}

(28)

Note that the above estimate is relatively crude. This process can be repeated another
|y^{2}|
times to get

\displaystyle\|(U^{x}U^{y}-U^{x+y})\left\lvert\psi\right\rangle\|\leq n|y|(2\epsilon+\delta)+|y^{2}|\epsilon\leq n^{2}(2\epsilon+\delta)+n\epsilon,

(29)

which gives the result.
∎

3.1
Definitions

Informally, a monogamy-of-entanglement (MoE) game is a game played by three quantum parties: a trusted referee, Alice, against two collaborating adversaries, Bob and Charlie, who may agree on a strategy but do not communicate while the game is in play. Such a game is played as follows:

(1)

The adversaries prepare a quantum state
\rho_{ABC}
shared between the three players. After this, they may no longer communicate.

(2)

Alice chooses a measurement to make on her space and provides Bob and Charlie the information about what measurement she chose.

(3)

Alice measures, and Bob and Charlie both try to guess her measurement outcome using their parts of the state.

(4)

The adversaries win the game if they simultaneously guessed Alice’s outcome correctly.

The setup for a generic MoE game is given in
Fig.

2
. Note that if there were only one adversary, they would always be able to guess Alice’s measurement (as long as it is projective) by sharing a maximally entangled state. However, this is not in general true for MoE games because there is no maximal tripartite entanglement. We can define such a game more formally as follows.

Definition 3.1
.

An

•

•

•

\displaystyle\begin{matrix}A:&\Theta\times Y&\rightarrow&\mathcal{P}(\textsf{A})\\

(30)

such that, for each
\theta

is a POVM.

The MoE game we study here is the original game of this kind introduced in

3.2
Observables, Bias, and Positivity

In this section, we assume that we are working with an MoE game that has only two answers, in which case we may identify
Y
with
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
, so
\texttt{G}=\left\lparen\Theta,\mathbb{Z}_{2},\textsf{A},\pi,A\right\rparen
.

Similarly to what is often done for nonlocal games
[
CMMN20
]
, we transform the expression for the winning probability into an expression in terms of observables rather than measurements.

Definition 3.10
.

Let
H
be a Hilbert space and let
P:\mathbb{Z}_{2}\rightarrow\mathcal{P}(\textsf{H})
be a POVM. Then, the
observable
of this POVM is
\overline{P}=P_{0}-P_{1}
.

The observable completely characterises the measurement as
P_{y}=\frac{1}{2}\left\lparen\mathbb{I}+(-1)^{y}\overline{P}\right\rparen
; and
\overline{P}
is unitary if and only if
P
is projective. For the measurements of an MoE game, we write the observables
A_{\theta}=\overline{A^{\theta}}
for simplicity, and similarly for the adversaries’ observables. It is a direct calculation to express the winning probability in terms of the observables:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathfrak{w}_{\texttt{G}}(\texttt{S})&=\frac{1}{8}\underset{\theta\leftarrow\pi}{\text{\raisebox{-3.0pt}{\scalebox{1.5}{$\mathbb{E}$}}}}\sum_{y\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}}\Tr\left[\left\lparen(\mathbb{I}_{A}+(-1)^{y}A_{\theta})\otimes(\mathbb{I}_{B}+(-1)^{y}B_{\theta})\otimes(\mathbb{I}_{C}+(-1)^{y}C_{\theta})\right\rparen\rho\right]\\
&=\frac{1}{4}\underset{\theta\leftarrow\pi}{\text{\raisebox{-3.0pt}{\scalebox{1.5}{$\mathbb{E}$}}}}\Tr\left[\left\lparen A_{\theta}\otimes(B_{\theta}\otimes\mathbb{I}_{C}+\mathbb{I}_{B}\otimes C_{\theta})+\mathbb{I}_{A}\otimes(\mathbb{I}_{BC}+B_{\theta}\otimes C_{\theta})\right\rparen\rho\right].\end{split}

(38)

As in the case of a nonlocal game, we study the bias of a strategy rather than the winning probability, since it quantifies how much better or worse a strategy does than a random but coordinated guess.

Definition 3.11
.

The
bias
of a strategy
S
for an MoE game
G
is

\displaystyle\mathfrak{b}_{\texttt{G}}(\texttt{S})=4\mathfrak{w}_{\texttt{G}}(\texttt{S})-2=\underset{\theta\leftarrow\pi}{\text{\raisebox{-3.0pt}{\scalebox{1.5}{$\mathbb{E}$}}}}\Tr\left[\left\lparen A_{\theta}\otimes(B_{\theta}\otimes\mathbb{I}_{C}+\mathbb{I}_{B}\otimes C_{\theta})-\mathbb{I}_{A}\otimes(\mathbb{I}_{BC}-B_{\theta}\otimes C_{\theta})\right\rparen\rho\right].

(39)

The optimal bias of the game is
\mathfrak{b}_{\texttt{G}}=\sup_{\texttt{S}}\mathfrak{b}_{\texttt{G}}(\texttt{S})
.

The bias lives in the range [-2,2] and the bias of a strategy is
0
if its winning probability is
\frac{1}{2}
. The optimal bias of the TFKW game is
\mathfrak{b}_{\texttt{TFKW}}=\sqrt{2}
.

To shorten expressions, we define
b_{\theta}=B_{\theta}\otimes\mathbb{I}_{C}
,
c_{\theta}=\mathbb{I}_{B}\otimes C_{\theta}
, and omit identities as much as possible, replacing them with
1
. Then, the bias is

\displaystyle\mathfrak{b}_{\texttt{G}}(\texttt{S})=\underset{\theta\leftarrow\pi}{\text{\raisebox{-3.0pt}{\scalebox{1.5}{$\mathbb{E}$}}}}\Tr\left[\left\lparen A_{\theta}\otimes(b_{\theta}+c_{\theta})-1\otimes(1-b_{\theta}c_{\theta})\right\rparen\rho\right].

(40)

For any strategy, we call the operator
\sum_{\theta\in\Theta}\pi(\theta)\left\lparen A_{\theta}\otimes(b_{\theta}+c_{\theta})-1\otimes(1-b_{\theta}c_{\theta})\right\rparen
the
game polynomial
.

For the TFKW game, the observables take the form of Pauli operators
A_{0}=Z
and
A_{1}=X
, so the game polynomial is

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left\lparen Z\otimes(b_{0}+c_{0})+X\otimes(b_{1}+c_{1})-1\otimes(1-b_{0}c_{0})-1\otimes(1-b_{1}c_{1})\right\rparen.

(41)

A simple but powerful observation is that a value
\beta\in\mathbb{R}
upper bounds the bias
\beta\geq\mathfrak{b}_{\texttt{G}}(\texttt{S})
if

\displaystyle\beta-\underset{\theta\leftarrow\pi}{\text{\raisebox{-3.0pt}{\scalebox{1.5}{$\mathbb{E}$}}}}\left\lparen A_{\theta}\otimes(b_{\theta}+c_{\theta})-1\otimes(1-b_{\theta}c_{\theta})\right\rparen\geq 0

(42)

as operators. It follows from consideration of the eigenvalues that the smallest value of
\beta
for which this holds for any valid choice of the
b_{\theta},c_{\theta}
is the optimal bias
\mathfrak{b}_{\texttt{G}}
. Conversely, checking whether
Eq.

42
holds for some fixed
\beta
with any choice of observables provides a way to show that
\beta\geq\mathfrak{b}_{\texttt{G}}
.

This provides a way to upper bound the winning probability of an MoE game using a positivity argument. In particular, we consider whether a polynomial in a certain noncommutative algebra is positive under the matrix representations of the algebra. In language closer to
[
Oza13
]
, the algebra we consider is the semi-pre-
\mathrm{C}^{\ast}
-algebra
\mathcal{L}(\textsf{A})\otimes\mathbb{C}[\mathbbm{F}^{2}_{|\Theta|}\!\times\!\mathbbm{F}^{2}_{|\Theta|}]
, where
\mathbbm{F}_{n}^{k}
is the free group with
n
generators of order
k
. The first copy of the free group corresponds to Bob’s observables, since the only relation we need impose on them is that they are self-inverse. Similarly, the second free group corresponds to Charlie’s observables, and since Bob’s observables commute with Charlie’s, this is in Cartesian product with Bob’s free group. The algebra is constructed as a matrix algebra over a semi-pre-
\mathrm{C}^{\ast}
-algebra by taking the group algebra
\mathbb{C}[\mathbbm{F}^{2}_{|\Theta|}\!\times\!\mathbbm{F}^{2}_{|\Theta|}]
and then extending the scalars to an algebra containing all of Alice’s observables. Therefore, an element
P
corresponding to the game polynomial belongs to this algebra, and a unitary representation where Bob and Charlie’s observables are in tensor product corresponds to a strategy.

As highlighted in
[
Oza13
]
, one way to approach positivity of elements in such an algebra is to use a sum-of-squares (SOS) argument. That is, if
\beta-P
, corresponding to the left hand side of
Eq.

42
, admits a decomposition as a
sum of Hermitian squares
of the form
\beta-P=\sum_{i}S_{i}^{\dagger}S_{i}
, then it must be positive under any matrix representation simply because a Hermitian square is always a positive matrix. In fact, an SOS decomposition is guaranteed to exist for
\beta=\mathfrak{b}_{\texttt{G}}+\varepsilon
for every
\varepsilon>0

[
Oza13
]
. We will make use of an SOS decomposition with
\beta=\mathfrak{b}_{\texttt{G}}
for the TFKW game.

4
Rigidity of the TFKW Game

In this section, we prove the main result of the paper, that the TFKW game satisfies a rigidity condition. In

Section

4.2

4.1

Let

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4\sqrt{2}}\left[\lparen Z\otimes b_{0}+X\otimes c_{1}-\sqrt{2}\rparen^{2}+\lparen Z\otimes c_{0}+X\otimes b_{1}-\sqrt{2}\rparen^{2}\right]+\frac{1}{4}\left[(b_{0}-c_{0})^{2}+(b_{1}-c_{1})^{2}\right].

4.3
Robust Rigidity

Now, we move on to the study of the robust rigidity, where we assume that the winning probability is in some small neighbourhood of the optimal probability. We can approach the proof in about the same way as the exact case, while keeping track of the error.

Theorem 4.2

(robust rigidity)
.

Let
\texttt{S}=\left\lparen\textsf{B},\textsf{C},B,C,\vphantom{\psi}\left\lvert\smash{\psi}\middle\rangle\!\middle\langle\smash{\psi}\right\rvert\right\rparen

and isometries

and

\displaystyle\Big\|(V\otimes W)\lvert\psi\rangle-\sum_{s\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{2}}X^{s_{0}}Z^{s_{1}}\lvert\beta\rangle\otimes\lvert\psi_{s}\rangle\Big\|\leq K\sqrt{\varepsilon},

(61)

where the

\textsf{B}^{\prime}
and
\textsf{C}^{\prime}

, and commuting observables

and

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\left\|VB_{\theta}\lvert\psi\rangle-B_{\theta}^{\prime}V\lvert\psi\rangle\right\|\leq L\sqrt{\varepsilon}\\
&\left\|WC_{\theta}\lvert\psi\rangle-C_{\theta}^{\prime}W\lvert\psi\rangle\right\|\leq L\sqrt{\varepsilon},\end{split}

(62)

\displaystyle B_{\theta}^{\prime}\lvert\psi_{s}\rangle=C_{\theta}^{\prime}\lvert\psi_{s}\rangle=(-1)^{s_{\theta}}\lvert\psi_{s}\rangle.

(63)

The proof below allows us to take

and

as the necessary constants. Note also that, as seen for the CHSH game in
[

, the order

Proof.

By hypothesis,
\mathfrak{w}_{\texttt{TFKW}}(\texttt{S})\geq\cos^{2}\tfrac{\pi}{8}-\varepsilon

, giving that
\vphantom{\psi\sqrt{2}-P\psi}\left\langle\smash{\psi}\middle\rvert\smash{\sqrt{2}-P}\middle\rvert\smash{\psi}\right\rangle\leq 4\varepsilon
, which, using the sum-of-squares decomposition, is

\displaystyle\begin{split}16\sqrt{2}\varepsilon\geq&\vphantom{\psi\lparen Z\otimes b_{0}+X\otimes c_{1}-\sqrt{2}\rparen^{2}\psi}\left\langle\smash{\psi}\middle\rvert\smash{\lparen Z\otimes b_{0}+X\otimes c_{1}-\sqrt{2}\rparen^{2}}\middle\rvert\smash{\psi}\right\rangle+\vphantom{\psi\lparen Z\otimes c_{0}+X\otimes b_{1}-\sqrt{2}\rparen^{2}\psi}\left\langle\smash{\psi}\middle\rvert\smash{\lparen Z\otimes c_{0}+X\otimes b_{1}-\sqrt{2}\rparen^{2}}\middle\rvert\smash{\psi}\right\rangle\\
&+\sqrt{2}\left[\vphantom{\psi(b_{0}-c_{0})^{2}\psi}\left\langle\smash{\psi}\middle\rvert\smash{(b_{0}-c_{0})^{2}}\middle\rvert\smash{\psi}\right\rangle+\vphantom{\psi(b_{1}-c_{1})^{2}\psi}\left\langle\smash{\psi}\middle\rvert\smash{(b_{1}-c_{1})^{2}}\middle\rvert\smash{\psi}\right\rangle\right].\end{split}

(64)

Since each of the terms is positive, we must have that

and
8\sqrt{2}\varepsilon\geq\min\left\{\vphantom{\psi\lparen Z\otimes b_{0}+X\otimes c_{1}-\sqrt{2}\rparen^{2}\psi}\left\langle\smash{\psi}\middle\rvert\smash{\lparen Z\otimes b_{0}+X\otimes c_{1}-\sqrt{2}\rparen^{2}}\middle\rvert\smash{\psi}\right\rangle,\vphantom{\psi\lparen Z\otimes c_{0}+X\otimes b_{1}-\sqrt{2}\rparen^{2}\psi}\left\langle\smash{\psi}\middle\rvert\smash{\lparen Z\otimes c_{0}+X\otimes b_{1}-\sqrt{2}\rparen^{2}}\middle\rvert\smash{\psi}\right\rangle\right\}
. This can be converted to Euclidean norm conditions by taking square roots:

\displaystyle 2(8)^{1/4}\sqrt{\varepsilon}\geq\min\left\{\left\|\lparen Z\otimes b_{0}+X\otimes c_{1}-\sqrt{2}\rparen\lvert\psi\rangle\right\|,\left\|\lparen Z\otimes c_{0}+X\otimes b_{1}-\sqrt{2}\rparen\lvert\psi\rangle\right\|\right\}

(65)

\displaystyle 4\sqrt{\varepsilon}\geq\left\|(b_{\theta}-c_{\theta})\lvert\psi\rangle\right\|.

(66)

Using
Eq.

66
in
Eq.

65
, we get

\displaystyle\left\|\lparen Z\otimes b_{0}+X\otimes b_{1}-\sqrt{2}\rparen\lvert\psi\rangle\right\|\leq\left\|\lparen Z\otimes b_{0}+X\otimes c_{1}-\sqrt{2}\rparen\lvert\psi\rangle\right\|+\left\|X\otimes(b_{1}-c_{1})\lvert\psi\rangle\right\|

and

Defining

and since

From the decomposition of Bob and Charlie’s spaces, we have that the shared space is

4.4

Similarly to the case of a single game, we begin with the parallel repetition in the exact case. That is, we assume

Theorem 4.4

(parallel-repeated exact rigidity)
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
and let
\texttt{S}=\left\lparen\textsf{B},\textsf{C},B,C,\rho=\vphantom{\psi}\left\lvert\smash{\psi}\middle\rangle\!\middle\langle\smash{\psi}\right\rvert\right\rparen

\textsf{B}^{\prime}
and
\textsf{C}^{\prime}

on both
\textsf{B}^{\prime}
and
\textsf{C}^{\prime}
are orthogonal.

, assuming that Alice measures on her

-th qubit, i.e.

.

. However, since the
i
-th winning probability is not quite optimal, showing that the
{{}^{\varphi,i}\texttt{S}}
win near-optimally proves to be an obstacle. To get past this, we adapt a technique of
[
Col17
]
for parallel repetition of CHSH games. It guarantees that there is a “good set” of strategies that win with only slightly relaxed probability, and the set is large enough to continue the proof as for the exact case.

Proof.

Define
\varepsilon_{\varphi,i}\geq 0
such that
\mathfrak{w}_{\texttt{TFKW}}({{}^{\varphi,i}\texttt{S}})=\cos^{2}{\tfrac{\pi}{8}}-\varepsilon_{\varphi,i}
. Then, we have that, for each
i
,
\varepsilon\geq\mathbbm{E}_{\begin{subarray}{c}\varphi\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{n}\\
\varphi_{i}=0\end{subarray}}\varepsilon_{\varphi,i}
We want to collect a large enough number of terms where
\varepsilon_{\varphi,i}
is not too large with respect to
\varepsilon
. To that effect, define the set of good values of
\varphi
for
i
as

\displaystyle G_{i}=\vphantom{\varphi\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{n},\varphi_{i}=0\varepsilon_{\varphi,i}\leq 5\varepsilon}\left\{{\varphi\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{n},\varphi_{i}=0}\middle|{\varepsilon_{\varphi,i}\leq 5\varepsilon}\right\}.

(111)

As in
[
Col17
]
, we claim that
|G_{i}|\geq 2^{n-2}+2^{n-3}+1
. In fact, suppose
|G_{i}|<2^{n-2}+2^{n-3}+1
. Then, there are at least
2^{n-3}
values of
\varphi
where
\varepsilon_{\varphi,i}>5\varepsilon
. This gives however that

\displaystyle\varepsilon\geq\frac{1}{2^{n-1}}\sum_{\varphi}\varepsilon_{\varphi,i}>\frac{1}{2^{n-1}}2^{n-3}(5\varepsilon)=\frac{5}{4}\varepsilon>\varepsilon,

(112)

which is a contradiction. Now, as for the case of a single game, for
\varphi\in G_{i}
, the SOS decomposition implies

\displaystyle\left\|(Z_{i}\otimes{{}^{\varphi,i\!}B_{0}}+X_{i}\otimes{{}^{\varphi,i\!}B_{1}}-\sqrt{2})\lvert\psi\rangle\right\|\leq 2\sqrt{5}(2+8^{1/4})\sqrt{\varepsilon}

(113)

\displaystyle\left\|{{}^{\varphi,i\!}B_{\theta}}\lvert\psi\rangle-{{}^{\varphi,i}C_{\theta}}\lvert\psi\rangle\right\|\leq 4\sqrt{5}\sqrt{\varepsilon}.

(114)

This gives the commutation of
{{}^{\varphi,i\!}B_{0}}
and
{{}^{\varphi,i\!}B_{1}}
with respect to
\left\lvert\psi\right\rangle
as

\displaystyle\left\|[{{}^{\varphi,i\!}B_{0}},{{}^{\varphi,i\!}B_{1}}]\lvert\psi\rangle\right\|\leq 2\sqrt{5}(2+\sqrt{2})(2+8^{1/4})\sqrt{\varepsilon}=:K_{0}\sqrt{\varepsilon}.

(115)

Now, we need commutation between operators for different values of
i
. Let
i\neq i^{\prime}\in[n]
,
\theta,\theta^{\prime}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}
and
\varphi,\varphi^{\prime}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{n}
such that
\varphi_{i}=\varphi^{\prime}_{i^{\prime}}=0
. By the pigeonhole principle, there exists a
\chi\in(G_{i}+\theta 1^{i})\cap(G_{i^{\prime}}+\theta^{\prime}1^{i^{\prime}})
, so using
Lemma

4.6
with
\delta=5\varepsilon
, there exists

such that

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\left\|({{}^{\varphi,i\!}B_{\theta}}-{{}^{\chi+\theta 1^{i},i\!}B_{\theta}})\lvert\psi\rangle\right\|\leq\sqrt{5}Q\sqrt{\varepsilon}\\
&\left\|({{}^{\varphi^{\prime},i^{\prime}\!}B_{\theta^{\prime}}}-{{}^{\chi+\theta^{\prime}1^{i^{\prime}},i^{\prime}\!}B_{\theta^{\prime}}})\lvert\psi\rangle\right\|\leq\sqrt{5}Q\sqrt{\varepsilon},\end{split}

(116)

and identically for Charlie’s observables. Thus, knowing
\left\|[{{}^{\chi+\theta 1^{i},i\!}B_{\theta}},{{}^{\chi+\theta^{\prime}1^{i^{\prime}},i^{\prime}\!}B_{\theta^{\prime}}}]\lvert\psi\rangle\right\|\leq K_{0}\sqrt{\varepsilon}
, we have

\displaystyle\begin{split}\left\|[{{}^{\varphi,i\!}B_{\theta}},{{}^{\varphi^{\prime},i^{\prime}\!}B_{\theta^{\prime}}}]\left\lvert\psi\right\rangle\right\|&\leq\left\|\lparen{{}^{\varphi,i\!}B_{\theta}}\otimes{{}^{\varphi^{\prime},i^{\prime}}C_{\theta^{\prime}}}-{{}^{\varphi^{\prime},i^{\prime}\!}B_{\theta^{\prime}}}\otimes{{}^{\varphi,i}C_{\theta}}\rparen\left\lvert\psi\right\rangle\right\|+8\sqrt{5}\sqrt{\varepsilon}\\
&\leq\left\|\lparen{{}^{\chi+\theta 1^{i},i}B_{\theta}}\otimes{{}^{\chi+\theta^{\prime}1^{i^{\prime}},i^{\prime}}C_{\theta^{\prime}}}-{{}^{\chi+\theta^{\prime}1^{i^{\prime}},i^{\prime}}B_{\theta^{\prime}}}\otimes{{}^{\chi+\theta 1^{i},i}C_{\theta}}\rparen\left\lvert\psi\right\rangle\right\|\\
&\qquad+(4\sqrt{5}Q+8\sqrt{5})\sqrt{\varepsilon}\\
&\leq(4\sqrt{5}(Q+4)+K_{0})\sqrt{\varepsilon}.\end{split}

(117)

Now, for any
i
, we may pick some

, and define

. We have

\displaystyle\|[{{}^{i\!}B_{\theta}},{{}^{i^{\prime}\!}B_{\theta^{\prime}}}]\left\lvert\psi\right\rangle\|\leq(4\sqrt{5}(Q+4)+K_{0})\sqrt{\varepsilon}.

(118)

Then, we use

2.4
with
U_{i\theta}={{}^{i\!}B_{\theta}}
and
V_{i\theta}={{}^{i}C_{\theta}}
so
\epsilon=4\sqrt{5}\sqrt{\varepsilon}
and
\delta=(4\sqrt{5}(Q+4)+K_{0})\sqrt{\varepsilon}
to generate an

-representation of
(\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{2})^{n}
, where

. The same holds in the same way for Charlie’s observables.

So, this puts us in the right place to use the Gowers-Hatami theorem again. There exist Hilbert spaces with orthogonal decompositions
\textsf{B}^{\prime}=\bigoplus_{t\in(\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{2})^{n}}\textsf{B}_{t}
and
\textsf{C}^{\prime}=\bigoplus_{t\in(\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{2})^{n}}\textsf{C}_{t}
; isometries

and

; and unitary observables
{{}^{i\!}B_{\theta}^{\prime}}=\sum_{t\in(\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{2})^{n}}(-1)^{t_{i\theta}}\mathbb{I}_{B,t}\in\mathcal{U}(\textsf{B}^{\prime})
and

such that

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\left\|(V\,{{}^{i\!}B_{\theta}}-{{}^{i\!}B^{\prime}_{\theta}}V)\lvert\psi\rangle\right\|=Ln^{2}\sqrt{\varepsilon}\\

(119)

Let
\left\lvert\psi^{\prime}\right\rangle=(V\otimes W)\left\lvert\psi\right\rangle
. We can put these observables back into the original inequalities to get

\displaystyle\left\|(Z_{i}\otimes{{}^{i\!}B^{\prime}_{0}}+X_{i}\otimes{{}^{i\!}B^{\prime}_{1}}-\sqrt{2})\lvert\psi^{\prime}\rangle\right\|\leq(2Ln^{2}+2\sqrt{5}(2+8^{1/4}))\sqrt{\varepsilon}

(120)

\displaystyle\left\|({{}^{i\!}B^{\prime}_{\theta}}-{{}^{i}C^{\prime}_{\theta}})\lvert\psi^{\prime}\rangle\right\|\leq(2Ln^{2}+4\sqrt{5})\sqrt{\varepsilon}.

(121)

Since the quantum state
\lvert\psi^{\prime}\rangle\in\bigoplus_{t,t^{\prime}\in(\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{2})^{n}}\textsf{A}_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes\textsf{A}_{n}\otimes\textsf{B}_{t}\otimes\textsf{C}_{t^{\prime}}\otimes\textsf{R}
, we can write it as
\lvert\psi^{\prime}\rangle=\sum_{t,t^{\prime}\in(\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{2})^{n}}\left\lvert v_{t,t^{\prime}}\right\rangle
. Using

\displaystyle Z_{i}\otimes{{}^{i\!}B_{0}^{\prime}}+X_{i}\otimes{{}^{i\!}B_{1}^{\prime}}-\sqrt{2}=2\sqrt{2}\sum_{t}(\vphantom{\beta_{t_{i}}}\left\lvert{\beta_{t_{i}}}\middle\rangle\!\middle\langle{\beta_{t_{i}}}\right\rvert_{i}-\mathbb{I})\otimes\mathbb{I}_{B,t}

(122)

and defining

, we have

\displaystyle\begin{split}\left\|\lvert\psi^{\prime}\rangle-\lvert v_{\beta,n}\rangle\right\|&\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\left\|\lvert v_{\beta,i}\rangle-\lvert v_{\beta,i+1}\rangle\right\|\\
&\leq\frac{1}{2\sqrt{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\left\|(Z_{i}\otimes{{}^{i\!}B^{\prime}_{0}}+X_{i}\otimes{{}^{i\!}B^{\prime}_{1}}-\sqrt{2})\lvert\psi^{\prime}\rangle\right\|\\
&\leq\tfrac{n}{\sqrt{2}}(Ln^{2}+\sqrt{5}(2+8^{1/4}))\sqrt{\varepsilon}.\end{split}

(123)

On the other hand,
Eq.

121
implies

\displaystyle(2Ln^{2}+4\sqrt{5})\sqrt{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle\geq\Big\|\sum_{t,t^{\prime}}((-1)^{t_{i\theta}}-(-1)^{t_{i\theta}^{\prime}})\lvert v_{t,t^{\prime}}\rangle\Big\|=2\Big\|\sum_{t_{i\theta}\neq t_{i\theta}^{\prime}}\lvert v_{t,t^{\prime}}\rangle\Big\|

(124)

Writing
\lvert\psi^{\prime}\rangle=\lvert v_{0}\rangle+\lvert v_{1}\rangle
where
\lvert v_{0}\rangle=\sum_{t\in(\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{2})^{n}}\lvert v_{t,t}\rangle
and
\lvert v_{1}\rangle=\sum_{t\neq t^{\prime}}\lvert v_{t,t^{\prime}}\rangle
. Then,

\displaystyle\left\|\lvert v_{1}\rangle\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{t\neq t^{\prime}}\vphantom{v_{t,t^{\prime}}}\left\langle{v_{t,t^{\prime}}}\middle|{v_{t,t^{\prime}}}\right\rangle\leq\sum_{\theta}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{t_{i\theta}\neq t^{\prime}_{i\theta}}\vphantom{v_{t,t^{\prime}}}\left\langle\smash{v_{t,t^{\prime}}}\middle|\smash{v_{t,t^{\prime}}}\right\rangle\leq 2n\left[(Ln^{2}+2\sqrt{5})\sqrt{\varepsilon}\right]^{2}.

(125)

Now, let
\lvert v_{\beta}\rangle=\sum_{t\in(\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{2})^{n}}(\vphantom{\beta_{t_{1}}}\left\lvert\smash{\beta_{t_{1}}}\middle\rangle\!\middle\langle\smash{\beta_{t_{1}}}\right\rvert\otimes\cdots\vphantom{\beta_{t_{n}}}\left\lvert\smash{\beta_{t_{n}}}\middle\rangle\!\middle\langle\smash{\beta_{t_{n}}}\right\rvert)\lvert v_{t,t}\rangle
, then
\left\|\left\lvert v_{\beta}\right\rangle-\left\lvert v_{\beta,n}\right\rangle\right\|\leq\|\left\lvert v_{1}\right\rangle\|,

so

\displaystyle\begin{split}\|\left\lvert\psi^{\prime}\right\rangle-\left\lvert v_{\beta}\right\rangle\|&\leq\|\left\lvert\psi^{\prime}\right\rangle-\left\lvert v_{\beta,n}\right\rangle\|+\|\left\lvert v_{\beta,n}\right\rangle-\left\lvert v_{\beta}\right\rangle\|\\

(126)

Now,

has the form we want, but it may not be normalised. Define
\lvert\phi\rangle=\frac{\lvert v_{\beta}\rangle}{\left\|\lvert v_{\beta}\rangle\right\|}
. We have

\displaystyle\left\|\lvert\phi\rangle-\lvert v_{\beta}\rangle\right\|=\left\|\lvert\psi^{\prime}\rangle\right\|-\left\|\lvert v_{\beta}\rangle\right\|\leq\left\|\lvert\psi^{\prime}\rangle-\lvert v_{\beta}\rangle\right\|,

(127)

giving

\displaystyle\|\left\lvert\psi^{\prime}\right\rangle-\left\lvert\phi\right\rangle\|\leq 2\|\left\lvert\psi^{\prime}\right\rangle-\left\lvert v_{\beta}\right\rangle\|\leq\sqrt{2}\left[n(Ln^{2}+\sqrt{5}(2+8^{1/4}))+2\sqrt{n}(Ln^{2}+2\sqrt{5})\right]\sqrt{\varepsilon}

(128)

∎

.

Let

, for each

K\geq 0
and isometries

and

such that

(129)

where

\displaystyle\lvert\phi\rangle=\sum_{t\in(\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{2})^{n}}X^{t_{10}}Z^{t_{11}}\lvert\beta\rangle\otimes\cdots\otimes X^{t_{n0}}Z^{t_{n1}}\lvert\beta\rangle\otimes\lvert\psi_{t}\rangle

(130)

for some vectors

4.6
Observed Statistics

In any self-testing scenario, the referee cannot actually query the winning probability of the adversaries’ strategy. To get around this, she may play many rounds of the game in parallel and use the players’ winning statistics to approximate their winning probability. The difficulty that arises, however, is that the players’ strategies need not be independent for the different rounds of the game, and therefore the information Alice receives might not be meaningful. A technique of
[
RUV13
]
allows us to get around this: first, we bound the probability of winning too many of the games if enough are too far from optimal, and then find good values of the bounding constants, depending on the application, so Alice may extract information about the state.

Lemma 4.9
.

Let
0<\varepsilon,\eta<1
and let
\delta\in\mathbb{R}
such that
\delta\leq\eta\varepsilon
. Let
S
be a strategy for
\texttt{TFKW}^{n}
. Let
E\subseteq\{0,\ldots,n\}
be the set of rounds
i
such that
\mathfrak{w}_{\texttt{TFKW}^{n}}^{i}(\texttt{S})\geq\cos^{2}\tfrac{\pi}{8}-\varepsilon
, and let
W\in\{0,\ldots,n\}
be the number of rounds the adversaries win. Then, if
|E|<(1-\eta)n
,

\displaystyle\Pr\left\lparen W\geq(\cos^{2}\tfrac{\pi}{8}-\delta)n\right\rparen\leq e^{-2n(\eta\varepsilon-\delta)^{2}}.

(131)

We can make use of this in contrapositive. That is, other than with small probability, if the adversaries win at least
(\cos^{2}\tfrac{\pi}{8}-\delta)n
games, then at least
(1-\eta)n
of the games win with near-optimal winning probability. The proof proceeds in the same way as a similar result for sequentially repeated games in
[
RUV13
]
.

Proof.

Write
w^{\ast}=\cos^{2}\tfrac{\pi}{8}
for convenience. Let
W_{i}
be the random variable that is
1
if the adversaries won round
i
and
0
if they lost. Then, we have that
W
is the random variable
W=\sum_{i=1}^{n}W_{i}
. Since
\Pr(W_{i}=1)=\mathfrak{w}_{\texttt{TFKW}^{n}}^{i}(\texttt{S})
, if
i\in E
, we know that
\Pr(W_{i}=1)\leq w^{\ast}
, and if
i\notin E
,
\Pr(W_{i}=1)\leq w^{\ast}-\varepsilon.
Let
\Gamma_{1},\ldots,\Gamma_{n},\Lambda_{1},\ldots,\Lambda_{n}
be independent Bernoulli variables such that the
\Pr(\Gamma_{i}=1)=w^{\ast}
and
\Pr(\Lambda_{i}=1)=w^{\ast}-\varepsilon
. By the above, we can couple them to the
W_{i}
so that
W_{i}\leq\Gamma_{i}
if
i\in E
and
W_{i}\leq\Lambda_{i}
is
i\notin E
. This implies directly that
W\leq\sum_{i\in E}\Gamma_{i}+\sum_{i\notin E}\Lambda_{i}
, so

\displaystyle\Pr(W\geq(w^{\ast}-\delta)n)\leq\Pr\Big\lparen\sum_{i\in E}\Gamma_{i}+\sum_{i\notin E}\Lambda_{i}\geq(w^{\ast}-\delta)n\Big\rparen.

(132)

Since
\mathbb{E}\Big\lparen\sum_{i\in E}\Gamma_{i}+\sum_{i\notin E}\Lambda_{i}\Big\rparen=|E|w^{\ast}+(n-|E|)(w^{\ast}-\varepsilon)
, Hoeffding’s inequality implies

\displaystyle Pr(W\geq(w^{\ast}-\delta)n)\leq e^{-\frac{2}{n}((n-|E|)\varepsilon-\delta n)^{2}}\leq e^{-2n(\eta\varepsilon-\delta)^{2}}

(133)

∎

A simple canonical choice of variables for large
n
is
\varepsilon=\eta=\delta=\frac{1}{n^{1/4}}
, which allows Alice to test sets of
k\sim n^{1/25}
parallel rounds, where she is able say that the state is
\sim\frac{1}{k^{1/8}}
near-optimal for those rounds with probability exponentially close to
1
in
k
.

In view of applications, we give in
Theorem

4.10
a version of
Corollary

4.8
where Alice has less information about the winning probabilities of the strategy. Rather than assuming that she knows that they win each round near-optimally, we will assume that Alice only knows with high probability that each round wins near-optimally. Then, we are able to ascertain the behaviour of the shared state in expectation. This will allow us to directly apply the result of
Lemma

4.9
to get conclusions about the rigidity of the state.

Theorem 4.10
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
and let
\texttt{S}=\left\lparen\textsf{B},\textsf{C},B,C,\rho\right\rparen
be a strategy for
\texttt{TFKW}^{n}
. Suppose that for some
\varepsilon,\eta\in[0,1]
, for each
i\in[n]
, there is a probability
1-\eta
that the
i
-th game wins with probability
\mathfrak{w}_{\texttt{TFKW}^{n}}^{i}(\texttt{S})\geq\cos^{2}\tfrac{\pi}{8}-\varepsilon
. Then, there exists a constant
K\geq 0
, Hilbert spaces
\textsf{B}^{\prime}
and
\textsf{C}^{\prime}
, and isometries
V:\textsf{B}\rightarrow\textsf{B}^{\prime}
and
W:\textsf{C}\rightarrow\textsf{C}^{\prime}
such that the expected value of the distance between quantum states

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left\|(V\otimes W)\rho(V\otimes W)^{\dagger}-\Tr_{R}(\vphantom{\phi}\left\lvert\smash{\phi}\middle\rangle\!\middle\langle\smash{\phi}\right\rvert)\right\|\leq Kn^{3}\sqrt{\varepsilon}+n\eta,

(134)

where
\lvert\phi\rangle=\sum_{t\in(\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{2})^{n}}X^{t_{10}}Z^{t_{11}}\lvert\beta\rangle\otimes\cdots\otimes X^{t_{n0}}Z^{t_{n1}}\lvert\beta\rangle\otimes\lvert\psi_{t}\rangle
for some auxiliary register
R
and
\lvert\psi_{t}\rangle\in\textsf{B}^{\prime}\otimes\textsf{C}^{\prime}\otimes\textsf{R}
with orthogonal supports on both
\textsf{B}^{\prime}
and
\textsf{C}^{\prime}
.

Proof.

For each
i\in[n]
, let
H_{i}
be the random variable indicating if
\mathfrak{w}_{\texttt{TFKW}^{n}}^{i}(\texttt{S})\geq\cos^{2}\tfrac{\pi}{8}-\varepsilon
. We have
\Pr(H_{i}=1)=1-\eta
. Let
H\subseteq[n]
be the register-valued random variable such that
i\in H
if and only if
H_{i}=1
; let
L=[n]\backslash H
be the complement. Since for any round in
H
,
\mathfrak{w}_{\texttt{TFKW}^{n}}^{i}(\texttt{S})\geq\cos^{2}\tfrac{\pi}{8}-\varepsilon
, we can apply the rigidity of
Corollary

4.8
to those rounds. Then, there exists a constant
K\geq 0
, Hilbert spaces
\textsf{B}^{\prime}
and
\textsf{C}^{\prime}
, isometries
V:\textsf{B}\rightarrow\textsf{B}^{\prime}
and
W:\textsf{C}\rightarrow\textsf{C}^{\prime}
, and a state
\lvert\phi\rangle\in\textsf{A}_{H}\otimes\textsf{A}_{L}\otimes\textsf{B}^{\prime}\otimes\textsf{C}^{\prime}\otimes\textsf{R}
of the form
\lvert\phi\rangle=\sum_{t\in(\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{2})^{|H|}}\lvert\beta_{t}\rangle_{A_{H}}\otimes\lvert\psi_{t}\rangle\in\textsf{A}\otimes\textsf{B}^{\prime}\otimes\textsf{C}^{\prime}\otimes\textsf{R}

where the supports of the
\lvert\psi_{t}\rangle\in\textsf{A}_{L}\otimes\textsf{B}^{\prime}\otimes\textsf{C}^{\prime}\otimes\textsf{R}
on both
\textsf{B}^{\prime}
and
\textsf{C}^{\prime}
are orthogonal such that

\displaystyle\left\|(V\otimes W)\rho(V\otimes W)^{\dagger}-\Tr_{R}(\vphantom{\phi}\left\lvert\smash{\phi}\middle\rangle\!\middle\langle\smash{\phi}\right\rvert)\right\|_{\Tr}\leq K|H|^{3}\sqrt{\varepsilon}\leq Kn^{3}\sqrt{\varepsilon}.

(135)

Let
\sigma=\vphantom{\beta}\left\lvert\smash{\beta}\middle\rangle\!\middle\langle\smash{\beta}\right\rvert^{\otimes L}_{A_{L}}\otimes\Tr_{A_{L}R}(\vphantom{\phi}\left\lvert\smash{\phi}\middle\rangle\!\middle\langle\smash{\phi}\right\rvert)
. Then,
\sigma
has the form we want and
\|\Tr_{R}(\vphantom{\phi}\left\lvert\smash{\phi}\middle\rangle\!\middle\langle\smash{\phi}\right\rvert)-\sigma\|\leq n-|H|
, giving that
\mathbb{E}\|\Tr_{R}(\vphantom{\phi}\left\lvert\smash{\phi}\middle\rangle\!\middle\langle\smash{\phi}\right\rvert)-\sigma\|\leq n-\mathbb{\sum}_{i}H_{i}=n\eta
. Using the triangle inequality, we get the wanted result.
∎

A
classical-quantum state
(cq) is a state

(140)

We also want to be able to estimate the uncertainty of a register given another. This is done using the

Generally, the evolution of a quantum system is given by a quantum channel, which subsumes both measurements and unitary evolution. A quantum channel is represented by a completely positive trace-preserving (CPTP) map, which is a linear map

5.2
Weak String Erasure

Weak string erasure (WSE) is a fundamental cryptographic primitive, introduced in
[
KWW12
]
. It is a simple yet powerful way to share partial information between a sender Alice and a receiver Bob. A WSE protocol provides Alice with a random string
x\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{n}
, and Bob with a string
\hat{x}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{n}
and a subset
\iota\subseteq[n]
such that
|\iota|
is on average
n/2
. The strings satisfy the property that they are equal on the positions indexed by the elements of
\iota
:
x_{\iota}=\hat{x}_{\iota}
. Security for such a scheme consists of Alice being unable to guess which substring of
x
Bob knows, while Bob is unable to guess the remaining bits of
x
,
i.e.
the substring
x_{\iota^{c}}
. WSE was used in
[
KWW12
]
to create bit commitment and oblivious transfer schemes. In their construction of a BC scheme, the roles of the sender and the receiver are preserved: the string Alice commits to is the image of her WSE output
x
by a randomness extractor, and in the reveal phase, Bob uses the part of the string he knows
x_{\iota}
to verify that Alice had in fact committed to this string. Hence, however, since bit commitment is impossible with no additional assumptions
[
BS16
]
, WSE needs some assumptions about the model to hold. Accordingly,
[
KWW12
]
used a quantum noisy-storage model to achieve it, generalising results on bounded quantum storage used to achieve oblivious transfer
[
DFSS08
]
. We formally define security for WSE in the original two-party model.

Definition 5.1

(
[
KWW12
]
)
.

A
(n,\lambda,\varepsilon)
-weak string erasure (WSE) scheme
is a protocol between two parties, Alice and Bob, that creates a state
\rho_{XAI\hat{X}B}
, where
X
,
I
, and
\hat{X}
are classical registers such that
X=\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{n}
holds string
x
,
\hat{X}=\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{n}
holds Bob’s guess of
x
, and
I=P([n])
holds
\iota
; and
A
and
B
are optional quantum registers corresponding to Alice and Bob’s remaining quantum states. The scheme must satisfy
correctness
, and
security
for both Alice and Bob:

Correctness
: If both Alice and Bob are honest then
\rho_{XI\hat{X}_{I}}=\rho_{XIX_{I}}
and
\rho_{XI}=\mu_{X}\otimes\mu_{I}
.

Security for Alice
: If Alice is honest
H^{\varepsilon}_{\mathrm{min}}(X|B)_{\rho}\geq\lambda n
.

Security for Bob
: If Bob is honest,
\rho_{AI}=\rho_{A}\otimes\mu_{I}
in the event that Alice does not abort.

We say that a protocol is a
(n,\lambda,\varepsilon)
-WSE scheme that fails with probability
p
if any one of the three conditions does not hold with probability at most
p
.

Here, we show WSE in an alternative model. Instead of resorting to limitations on the quantum devices of parties, we add an additional dishonest prover, Charlie, who colludes with the receiver.
1
1

1

Note that oblivious transfer in yet another three-party model has been considered before. In
[
YXTZ14
]
, they consider a model where an untrusted third party prepares entangled states for Alice and Bob to use. However, they make use of much stronger assumptions: the third party produces each state identically and independently, Alice and Bob need to cooperate to verify that these states are correct before running the protocol, and the third party does not collude with any of the other parties.
Instead of the storage limitation, we place restrictions on the communications: the prover is not allowed to communicate with the receiver, and the sender is required to communicate by publicly broadcasting. The former restriction helps an honest sender constrain the action of a dishonest receiver; the latter condition blocks a subtle cheating method of a dishonest sender, where she attempts to extract different information from the receiver and the prover.

Definition 5.2
.

A WSE scheme in the
three-party model
consists of a sender, Alice, a receiver, Bob, and a prover, Charlie. It satisfies the following:

•

Charlie is dishonest if and only if Bob is dishonest.

•

Alice communicates by publicly broadcasting.

•

Bob and Charlie are isolated from each other once Alice starts broadcasting.

In this model, there is an additional prover Charlie, so the state takes the form
\rho_{XAI\hat{X}BC}
, where
C
is an additional register held by Charlie. If he is dishonest, he should not be able to get more information out of the protocol than his collaborator, Bob. Thus, we require that the security for Alice from
Definition

5.1
is satisfied with respect to either Bob or Charlie’s registers. We state this formally.

Definition 5.3
.

A
(n,\lambda,\varepsilon)
-WSE scheme in the three-party model
is a protocol that produces a shared state
\rho_{XAI\hat{X}BC}
such that it is a two-party scheme for Alice and Bob, and the security for Alice is symmetric:

Two-party WSE:

\rho_{XAI\hat{X}B}
, the state with Charlie’s register traced out, satisfies
Definition

5.1
.

Symmetric security for Alice
: If Alice is honest
H^{\varepsilon}_{\mathrm{min}}(X|C)\geq\lambda n
.

For our protocol in this model, as Bob colludes with Charlie but may not communicate with him, an honest sender exploits the rigidity of the TFKW game to constrain their actions. Note that Charlie needs to remain out of the reach of Bob’s communications for as long as Bob is using his output data in order for it to stay secure. Since Alice must broadcast publicly, Bob and Charlie will receive the same TFKW game questions even if she is dishonest.

5.3
Bit Commitment from WSE

Bit commitment (BC) is a cryptographic primitive where a sender, Alice, sends an encoded bit (or more generally a bit string) to a receiver, Bob, and may choose to reveal it at a later time. Accordingly, a scheme for BC consists of a
commit
protocol and a
reveal
protocol. Ideally, the scheme should be
hiding
— Bob is unable to learn the bit until Alice chooses to reveal it — and
binding
— Alice must reveal the same bit that she originally chose. In
[
KWW12
]
, they consider a BC scheme where Alice commits to a random bit string rather then one she chooses freely. We formally define such a scheme in essentially the same way they do.

Definition 5.7
.

A
(\ell,\varepsilon)
-randomised bit string commitment (RBC) scheme
is a pair of protocols between two parties Alice and Bob: a protocol
commit
that creates a state
\rho_{YAB}
and a protocol
reveal
that creates from this a state
\rho_{YA^{\prime}\hat{Y}FB^{\prime}}
. Here,
Y=\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{\ell}
holds Alice’s committed string;
\hat{Y}=\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{\ell}
holds the string Alice reveals;
F=\mathbb{Z}_{2}
indicates whether Bob accepts (1) or rejects (0) the reveal; and
A,A^{\prime}
and
B,B^{\prime}
are additional quantum registers for Alice and Bob, respectively. The scheme must be
correct
,
\varepsilon
-hiding
, and
\varepsilon
-binding
:

Correctness
: If Alice and Bob are honest, for
\sigma_{YF}=\mu_{Y}\otimes\vphantom{1}\left\lvert\smash{1}\middle\rangle\!\middle\langle\smash{1}\right\rvert
,
\|\rho_{Y\hat{Y}F}-\sigma_{YYF}\|_{\Tr}\leq\varepsilon
.

\varepsilon
-hiding

\|\rho_{YB}-\mu_{Y}\otimes\rho_{B}\|\leq\varepsilon
.

\varepsilon

such that, applying

As for WSE, we say this scheme

In
[
KWW12
]
, they provide a way to construct an RBC scheme using a weak string erasure scheme and a linear code. The roles of the sender and the receiver from the WSE scheme are preserved. In particular, with an

-WSE scheme and an

gives a form of bit commitment in a model with two receivers. In this model, Alice is a sender who is required to broadcast, and Bob and Charlie are colluding receivers who are isolated from each other. Similarly to WSE, we only require that Bob be able to read the revealed bit string, rather than both receivers. We call this the
two-receiver model
.

Corollary 5.8
.

Let

be constants that satisfy

5.6
, and let

such that there exists an

-linear code. Then, for

-randomised bit string commitment scheme that fails with probability

.

Using the construction of
[
KWW12

, the correctness and
\varepsilon

As mentioned before, a construction of
[
BGKW88
]
provides classical bit commitment in a model with two senders who may not communicate. We observe that, in contrast, bit commitment is classically impossible in our two-receiver model. The first step of a protocol in our model consists of the preparation of an initial shared state by Bob. If only classical operations are allowed, Bob is just sampling from a probability distribution and sharing the result. In particular, he can make sure that all three parties receive the same classical information. Next, for the remainder of the protocol, Bob and Charlie may not communicate. However, since Alice must communicate by publicly broadcasting, Bob and Charlie receive exactly the same information from her, and may respond to all the same challenges. As such, classically, our model becomes equivalent to the standard two-party model. In particular, bit commitment is impossible. The difference with the two-sender model, where bit commitment does exist classically, arises due to the additional communication restriction we imposed: the receiver of
[
BGKW88
]
may share different information with each of the senders, rather than broadcasting publicly.

5.4
Everlasting Randomness Expansion

The creation of fresh, trusted, uniform randomness is an important part of many computational and cryptographic tasks. Since quantum mechanics is inherently probabilistic, it stands to reason that quantum procedures prove useful for this task. A major theoretical hurdle in achieving this is that it is difficult to characterise the behaviour of an untrusted quantum device: one needs to verify that their source of randomness is truly random and not shared by an eavesdropper. Largely, the methods to bypass this difficulty use a nonlocal game to verify entanglement between two untrusted provers. However, this requires, in particular, that the provers are able to produce entangled states, and keep them from decohering throughout the running time of the protocol. This can be an impractical requirement.

In our contribution, we remove the need for long-distance entanglement, and instead make the assumption of a trusted but leaky measurement, as well as a standard computational assumption.
The protocol consists of two steps: first, Alice samples the output of a pseudorandom generator, allowing her to increase the size of her random string; then, she uses this as the source of randomness to play the TFKW game against computationally-bounded and isolated Bob and Charlie, where the rigidity allows her to extract a string that is uniformly random and unknown to either Bob or Charlie. First, we need to formalise the model we are working in, based on the structure of an MoE game.

Definition 5.9
.

The
MoE model for randomness expansion
consists of three quantum parties: a trusted verifier Alice, who interacts with two untrusted provers, Bob and Charlie. The model satisfies the following:

•

Bob and Charlie are able to prepare a tripartite shared state but then are isolated.

•

Alice can make trusted measurements on her register, which are leaky in the sense that Bob and Charlie can learn the measurement bases.

Now, we can define randomness expanders in this model.

Definition 5.10
.

A
(s(n),\varepsilon)
-local randomness expander
is a protocol in the MoE model, where, given a uniformly random seed in
S=\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{s(n)}
, Alice, Bob, and Charlie construct a quantum state
\rho_{YSBC}
, where
Y=\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{n}
and
S
are classical registers that Alice holds and
B
and
C
are potentially quantum registers that Bob and Charlie hold, respectively, such that

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\|\rho_{YSB}-\mu_{Y}\otimes\mu_{S}\otimes\rho_{B}\|_{\Tr}\leq\varepsilon\\
&\|\rho_{YSC}-\mu_{Y}\otimes\mu_{S}\otimes\rho_{C}\|_{\Tr}\leq\varepsilon,\end{split}

(158)

if Alice does not abort during the execution. As before, we say this scheme
fails with probability
p
if these conditions do not hold with probability at most
p
.

The idea of this definition is that Alice’s output needs to be approximately uniformly random in any case, but we can also get the additional guarantee that, as long as Bob and Charlie stay isolated, they cannot hold onto side information that allows them to guess the output. However, we do not constrain their ability to guess the output if they come back together: for example, the register
BC
could be maximally entangled with Alice’s register before she makes her final measurement, without either
B
or
C
being maximally entangled on their own.

The main computational tool we will be making use of is the idea of a pseudorandom generator against computationally-bounded adversaries.

Definition 5.11
.

An algorithm

can be undertaken by first acting by some QPT unitary

Now, we can introduce pseudorandom generators as functions that take a uniformly random string to a longer string that no QPT algorithm can distinguish from uniform.

Definition 5.12

A family of functions

(159)

Now we can define a variant of the TFKW game that uses a pseudorandom rather than uniformly random question distribution.

Definition 5.13
.

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\Pr\left[Q(G_{N}(\Gamma_{1}),G_{b}(\Gamma_{2}))=1\right]=\Pr\left[U\geq(\cos^{2}\left\lparen\tfrac{\pi}{8}\right\rparen-\delta)N\right],\\

(162)

giving

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\Big\lvert\Pr\left[U\geq(\cos^{2}\left\lparen\tfrac{\pi}{8}\right\rparen-\delta)N\right]-\Pr\left[W\geq(\cos^{2}\left\lparen\tfrac{\pi}{8}\right\rparen-\delta)N\right]\Big\rvert\\

as

and

. Now, using
Lemma

4.9
as in

5.6
, if less than

of the rounds have winning probability greater than

By the above, then

(165)

So, other than with negligible failure probability, at least

of the rounds have winning probability greater than

If we select
n

As

Lemma A.1

(State purification)
.

Let
H
be a Hilbert space. For any mixed state
\rho\in\mathcal{D}(\textsf{H})
, there exists a Hilbert space
R
and a pure state
\lvert\psi\rangle\in\textsf{H}\otimes\textsf{R}
such that
\rho=\Tr_{R}(\vphantom{\psi}\left\lvert\smash{\psi}\middle\rangle\!\middle\langle\smash{\psi}\right\rvert)
.

Lemma A.2

(Measurement purification)
.

Let
H
be a Hilbert space. Any POVM
P:X\rightarrow\mathcal{P}(\textsf{H})
can be simulated by a PVM by enlarging the state space.

This lemma appeared in
[
TFKW13
]
and was used in the same way as it is here. We see from the proof below that the enlargement of the state space is an isometry.

Proof.

Consider the linear operator

\displaystyle\begin{matrix}V:&\textsf{H}&\rightarrow&\textsf{H}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{X}\\
&\lvert\psi\rangle&\mapsto&\sum_{x}\sqrt{P_{x}}\lvert\psi\rangle\otimes\lvert x\rangle.\end{matrix}

(174)

Since
\vphantom{\varphi V^{\dagger}V\psi}\left\langle\smash{\varphi}\middle\rvert\smash{V^{\dagger}V}\middle\rvert\smash{\psi}\right\rangle=\sum_{x}\vphantom{\varphi P_{x}\psi}\left\langle\smash{\varphi}\middle\rvert\smash{P_{x}}\middle\rvert\smash{\psi}\right\rangle=\vphantom{\varphi\psi}\left\langle\smash{\varphi}\middle|\smash{\psi}\right\rangle
, we have that
V
is an isometry. Fix some
x_{0}\in X
and, identifying
H
with the subspace
\textsf{H}\otimes\lvert x_{0}\rangle
of
\textsf{H}\otimes\textsf{X}
, we can extend
V
to a unitary operator
U:\textsf{H}\otimes\textsf{X}\rightarrow\textsf{H}\otimes\textsf{X}
. Define now
\tilde{P}:X\rightarrow\mathcal{P}(\textsf{H}\otimes\textsf{X})
by
\tilde{P}_{x}=U^{\dagger}(\mathbb{I}_{H}\otimes\vphantom{x}\left\lvert{x}\middle\rangle\!\middle\langle{x}\right\rvert)U
.
\tilde{P}
is a projective measurement as
\tilde{P}_{x}\tilde{P}_{y}=\vphantom{xy}\left\langle\smash{x}\middle|\smash{y}\right\rangle U^{\dagger}(\mathbb{I}_{H}\otimes\vphantom{xy}\left\lvert{x}\middle\rangle\!\middle\langle{y}\right\rvert)U=\delta_{x,y}\tilde{P}_{x}
, and
\sum_{x}\tilde{P}_{x}=U^{\dagger}(\mathbb{I}_{H}\otimes\mathbb{I}_{X})U=\mathbb{I}_{HX}
. Finally,
\tilde{P}
acts as
P
on
\textsf{H}\otimes\lvert x_{0}\rangle
as

\displaystyle(\langle\varphi\rvert\otimes\langle x_{0}\rvert)\tilde{P}_{x}(\lvert\psi\rangle\otimes\lvert x_{0}\rangle)=\sum_{y,y^{\prime}}\vphantom{\varphi\sqrt{P_{y^{\prime}}}\sqrt{P_{y}}\psi}\left\langle\smash{\varphi}\middle\rvert\smash{\sqrt{P_{y^{\prime}}}\sqrt{P_{y}}}\middle\rvert\smash{\psi}\right\rangle\vphantom{y^{\prime}x}\left\langle{y^{\prime}}\middle|{x}\right\rangle\vphantom{xy}\left\langle{x}\middle|{y}\right\rangle=\vphantom{\varphi P_{x}\psi}\left\langle\smash{\varphi}\middle\rvert\smash{P_{x}}\middle\rvert\smash{\psi}\right\rangle.

(175)

∎

Lemma A.3

(Properties of the trace distance)
.

Let
H
and
K
be Hilbert spaces.

•

d_{\Tr}:\mathcal{D}(\textsf{H})\times\mathcal{D}(\textsf{H})\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{+}
is a metric.

•

For
\rho,\sigma\in\mathcal{D}(\textsf{H})
,
\|\rho-\sigma\|_{\Tr}\leq 1
with equality iff
\rho
and
\sigma
have orthogonal supports.

•

For any quantum channel
\Phi:\mathcal{L}(\textsf{H})\rightarrow\mathcal{L}(\textsf{K})
,
\|\Phi(\rho)-\Phi(\sigma)\|_{\Tr}\leq\|\rho-\sigma\|_{\Tr}
.

•

For pure states
\lvert\psi\rangle,\lvert\phi\rangle\in\textsf{H}
,
\|\vphantom{\psi}\left\lvert\smash{\psi}\middle\rangle\!\middle\langle\smash{\psi}\right\rvert-\vphantom{\phi}\left\lvert\smash{\phi}\middle\rangle\!\middle\langle\smash{\phi}\right\rvert\|_{\Tr}\leq\|\lvert\psi\rangle-\lvert\phi\rangle\|
.

Lemma A.4

(Properties of the operator norm)
.

Let
H
and
K
be Hilbert spaces.

•

The operator norm is a norm.

•

The operator norm of a normal (e.g., Hermitian or unitary) operator is maximum of the moduli of the eigenvalues.

•

For
A\in\mathcal{L}(\textsf{H})
and
\lvert v\rangle\in\textsf{H}
,
\|A\lvert v\rangle\|\leq\|A\|\|\lvert v\rangle\|
.

•

For
A,B\in\mathcal{L}(\textsf{H})
,
\|AB\|\leq\|A\|\|B\|
.

•

For any isometry
V:\textsf{H}\rightarrow\textsf{K}
,
\|VAV^{\dagger}\|=\|A\|
.
