A Theory-Driven Self-Labeling Refinement Method for Contrastive Representation Learning
Pan Zhou, Caiming Xiong, Xiao-Tong Yuan, Steven Hoi

Abstract
For an image query, unsupervised contrastive learning labels crops of the same image as positives, and other image crops as negatives. Although intuitive, such a native label assignment strategy cannot reveal the underlying semantic similarity between a query and its positives and negatives, and impairs performance, since some negatives are semantically similar to the query or even share the same semantic class as the query. In this work, we first prove that for contrastive learning, inaccurate label assignment heavily impairs its generalization for semantic instance discrimination, while accurate labels benefit its generalization. Inspired by this theory, we propose a novel self-labeling refinement approach for contrastive learning. It improves the label quality via two complementary modules: (i) self-labeling refinery (SLR) to generate accurate labels and (ii) momentum mixup (MM) to enhance similarity between query and its positive. SLR uses a positive of a query to estimate semantic similarity between a query and its positive and negatives, and combines estimated similarity with vanilla label assignment in contrastive learning to iteratively generate more accurate and informative soft labels. We theoretically show that our SLR can exactly recover the true semantic labels of label-corrupted data, and supervises networks to achieve zero prediction error on classification tasks. MM randomly combines queries and positives to increase semantic similarity between the generated virtual queries and their positives so as to improves label accuracy. Experimental results on CIFAR10, ImageNet, VOC and COCO show the effectiveness of our method. Code will be released online.

Motivation.
But the one-hot labels in contrastive learning are indeed inaccurate and uninformative. It is because for a query, it could be semantically similar or even more similar to some negatives than its positives. Indeed, some negatives even belong to the same semantic class as the query

So the one-hot label cannot well reveal the semantic similarity between query and its positives and “negatives", and cannot guarantee the semantically similar samples to close each other, leading to performance degradation.

Contributions.
In this work, we alleviate the above label
issue, and derive some new results and alternatives for contrastive learning. Particularly, we theoretically show that inaccurate labels impair the performance of contrastive learning. Then we propose a self-labeling refinement method to obtain more accurate labels for contrastive learning. Our main contributions are highlighted below.

Our first contribution is proving that the generalization error of MoCo for instance discrimination linearly depends on the discrepancy between the estimated labels (e.g. one-hot labels) in MoCo and the true labels that really reflect semantical similarity between a query and its positives and negatives. Formally, given
n

\bm{\mathcal{D}}\!\!=

\{\bm{x}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}

Inspired by our theory, we propose a Self-lAbeliNg rEfinement (SANE) method which iteratively employs the network and data themselves to generate more accurate and informative soft labels for contrastive learning. SANE has two complementary modules: (i)
Self-Labeling Refinery (SLR)
to explicitly generate accurate labels, and (ii)
Momentum Mixup (MM)
to increase similarity between query and its positive and implicitly improve label accuracy. Given a query, SLR uses its one positive to estimate semantic similarity between the query and its keys (i.e. its positive and negatives) by computing their feature similarity, since a query and its positive come from the same image and should have close similarity on the same keys. Then SLR linearly combines the estimated similarity of a query with its vanilla one-hot label in contrastive learning to iteratively generate more accurate and informative soft labels. Our strategy is that at the early training stage, one-hot label has heavy combination weight to provide relatively accurate labels; along with more training, the estimated similarity becomes more accurate and informative, and its combination weight becomes larger as it explores useful underlying semantic information between the query and its keys that is missing in the one-hot labels. Besides, we prove that when the semantic labels in the instance discrimination task are corrupted, our SLR can exactly recover the true semantic labels of training data, and networks trained with our SLR can exactly predict the true semantic labels of test samples.

Moreover, we introduce MM for contrastive learning to further reduce the possible label noise and also increase augmentation diversity. Specifically, we randomly combines queries

and the positive key
\widetilde{\bm{x}}_{k}

Inspiration: A Generalization Analysis of MoCo

In this section, we first briefly review the MoCo
[
1
]
method popularly studied for contrastive learning, and then analyze the impact of inaccurate label assignment on its generalization ability.

Review of MoCo.
The MoCo method contains an online network
f_{\bm{w}}
and a target network

g_{\bm{\xi}}

receptively parameterized by
\bm{w}
and
\bm{\xi}
. Both
f_{\bm{w}}
and

g_{\bm{\xi}}

consists of a feature encoder and a projection head (
e.g.
3-layered MLP). Given a minibatch

at each iteration, it first randomly augments each vanilla image
\bm{c}_{i}
into two views

and optimizes the following contrastive loss:

\mathcal{L}_{\mbox{\scriptsize{n}}}(\bm{w})=-\frac{1}{s}\sum\nolimits_{i=1}^{s}\log\Big(\frac{\sigma(\bm{x}_{i},\widetilde{\bm{x}}_{i})}{\sigma(\bm{x}_{i},\widetilde{\bm{x}}_{i})+\sum_{l=1}^{b}\sigma(\bm{x}_{i},\bm{b}_{l})}\Big),

(1)

where

\sigma

(\bm{x}_{i},\widetilde{\bm{x}}_{i})\!=\!\exp\big(-\!\frac{\langle f(\bm{x}_{i}),g(\widetilde{\bm{x}}_{i})\rangle}{\tau\|f(\bm{x}_{i})\|_{2}\cdot\|g(\widetilde{\bm{x}}_{i})\|_{2}}\big)
with a temperature

\tau

. The dictionary
\bm{B}\!=\!\{\bm{b}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{b}
denotes the negative keys of current minibatch queries
\{\bm{x}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{s}
, and is often of huge size to achieve satisfactory performance, e.g. 65,536 in MoCo. In practice,
\bm{B}
in MoCo is updated by the minibatch features
\{g(\widetilde{\bm{x}}_{i})\}_{i=1}^{s}
in a first-in and first-out order. By fixing

g_{\bm{\xi}}

and updating
f_{\bm{w}}
in Eqn. (
1
), MoCo pushes the query
\bm{x}_{i}
away from its negative keys in dictionary
\bm{B}
while pulling together its positive key
\widetilde{\bm{x}}_{i}
. For

g_{\bm{\xi}}

, it is updated via exponential moving average, i.e.
\bm{\xi}\!=\!(1-

\iota

)
\bm{\xi}+

\iota

\bm{w}
with a constant

\iota

\in(0,1)
.

From Eqn. (
1
), one can observe that MoCo views each image as an individual class and uses one-hot label
\bm{y}\!\in\!{\mathbb{R}^{b+1}}
(its nonzero position is at the position of its positive key) to train
f_{\bm{w}}
.
However, as mentioned in Sec.
1
, the one-hot labels cannot reveal the semantic similarity between a query
\bm{x}_{i}
and its positive and negatives and thus impair representation learning. In the following, we theoretically analyze the effect of inaccurate labels to the generalization of MoCo for instance discrimination.

Generalization Analysis.
We focus on analyzing MoCo in the final training stage where the sample (key) distribution in the dictionary
\bm{B}
is almost fixed. This simplified setup is reasonable because (i) in the final training stage, the target network

g_{\bm{\xi}}

almost does not change due to the very small momentum updating parameter

\iota

f_{\bm{w}}

Let
\bm{\mathcal{D}}=\{(\bm{x}_{i},\widetilde{\bm{x}}_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
denote the training positive pairs in MoCo sampled from an unknown distribution
\bm{\mathcal{S}}
. Moreover, the query

has ground truth soft label

, where the

-th entry

measures the semantic similarity between

and the
t

in

and keys in

\widetilde{\bm{\mathcal{Q}}}(f_{\bm{w}})=\frac{1}{n}\sum\nolimits_{i=1}^{n}\ell(h(f_{\bm{w}}(\bm{x}_{i}),\bm{B}_{i}),\bm{y}_{i}),

(2)

where
h(f_{\bm{w}}(\bm{x}_{i}),\!\bm{B}_{i})\!=\![\sigma(\bm{x}_{i},\!\widetilde{\bm{x}}_{i}),\sigma(\bm{x}_{i},\!\bm{b}_{1}),\cdots\!,\sigma(\bm{x}_{i},\!\bm{b}_{b})]
denotes the predicted class probability, and

is cross-entropy loss.
Ideally, one should sample sufficient pairs

from the distribution
\bm{\mathcal{S}}
and use the ground truth label

of
\bm{x}_{i}

\bm{\mathcal{Q}}(f_{\bm{w}})=\mathbb{E}_{(\bm{x}_{i},\widetilde{\bm{x}}_{i})\sim\bm{\mathcal{S}}}\left[\ell(h(f_{\bm{w}}(\bm{x}_{i}),\bm{B}_{i}),\bm{y}_{i}^{*})\right].

(3)

Here we assume the ground truth label

is soft which is indeed more reasonable and stricter than the one-hot label setting especially for contrastive learning
[
25
]
. It is because soft label requires the networks to capture the semantic similarity between query and the instances in

and bring semantically similar instances together, greatly helping downstream tasks (e.g. classification) where global semantic information is needed, while one-hot label only needs networks to distinguish each instance from others and does not consider the global semantic structures in the data. As both the data distribution

3
Self-Labeling Refinement for Contrastive Learning

Our Self-lAbeliNg rEfinement (SANE) approach for contrastive learning contains (i)
Self-Labeling Refinery
(SLR for short) and (ii)
Momentum Mixup
(MM) which complementally refine noisy labels respectively from label estimation and positive pair construction. SLR uses current training model and data to estimate more accurate and informative soft labels, while MM increases similarity between virtual query and its positive, and thus improves label accuracy.

We begin by slightly modifying the instance discrimination task in MoCo. Specifically, for the query
\bm{x}_{i}
in the current minibatch
\{(\bm{x}_{i},\widetilde{\bm{x}}_{i})\}_{i=1}^{s}
, we maximize its similarity to its positive sample
\widetilde{\bm{x}}_{i}
in the key set
\bar{\bm{B}}\!=\!\{\widetilde{\bm{x}}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{s}\!\cup\!\{\bm{b}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{b}
and minimize its similarity to the remaining samples in
\bar{\bm{B}}
:

\mathcal{L}_{\mbox{\scriptsize{c}}}\big(\bm{w},\{(\bm{x}_{i},\bm{y}_{i})\}\big)=-\frac{1}{s}\sum\nolimits_{i=1}^{s}\sum\nolimits_{k=1}^{s+b}\bm{y}_{ik}\log\bigg(\frac{\sigma(\bm{x}_{i},\bar{\bm{b}}_{k})}{\sum_{l=1}^{s+b}\sigma(\bm{x}_{i},\bar{\bm{b}}_{l})}\bigg),

(4)

where
\bar{\bm{b}}_{k}
is the
k
-th sample in
\bar{\bm{B}}
, and
\bm{y}_{i}
is the one-hot label of query
\bm{x}_{i}
whose
i
-th entry
\bm{y}_{ii}
is one. In this way, the labels of current queries
\{\bm{x}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{s}
are defined on a shared set
\bar{\bm{B}}
, and can be linearly combined which is key for SLR & MM. Next, we aim to improve the quality of label
\bm{y}_{i}
in (
4
) below.

3.1
Self-Labeling Refinery

Methodology.
As analyzed in Sec.
1
and
2
, the one-hot labels in Eqn. (
4
) could not well reveal the semantic similarity between
\bm{x}_{i}
and the instance keys in the set
\bar{\bm{B}}
, and thus impairs good representation learning. To alleviate this issue, we introduce Self-Labeling Refinery (SLR) which employs network and data themselves to generate more accurate and informative labels, and improves the performance of contrastive learning. Specifically, to refine the one-hot label
\bm{y}_{i}
of query
\bm{x}_{i}
, SLR uses its positive instance
\widetilde{\bm{x}}_{i}
to estimate the underlying semantic similarity between
\bm{x}_{i}
and instances in
\bar{\bm{B}}\!=\!\{\widetilde{\bm{x}}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{s}\!\cup\!\{\bm{b}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{b}
, since
\bm{x}_{i}
and
\widetilde{\bm{x}}_{i}
come from the same image and should have close semantic similarity with instances in
\bar{\bm{B}}
. Let
\bar{\bm{b}}_{k}
be the
k
-th sample in
\bar{\bm{B}}
. Then at the
t
-th iteration, SLR first estimates the instance-class probability
\bm{p}^{t}_{i}\!\in\!{\mathbb{R}^{s+b}}
of
\bm{x}_{i}
on the set
\bar{\bm{B}}
whose
k
-th entry is defined as

\bm{p}^{t}_{ik}=\sigma^{1/\tau^{\prime}}(\widetilde{\bm{x}}_{i},\bar{\bm{b}}_{k})\big/\sum\nolimits_{l=1}^{s+b}\sigma^{1/\tau^{\prime}}(\widetilde{\bm{x}}_{i},\bar{\bm{b}}_{l}),\ \quad(\tau^{\prime}\in(0,1]).

The constant
\tau^{\prime}
sharpens
\bm{p}^{t}_{i}
and removes some possible small noise, since
smooth labels cannot well distillate their knowledge to a network
[
26
]
. Then SLR uses
\bm{p}^{t}_{i}
to approximate the semantic similarity between
\bm{x}_{i}
and the instances in
\bar{\bm{B}}
and employs it as the soft label of
\bm{x}_{i}
for contrastive learning.

However, since
\widetilde{\bm{x}}_{i}
is highly similar to itself,
\bm{p}^{t}_{ii}
could be much larger than others and conceals the similarity of other semantically similar instances in
\bar{\bm{B}}
. To alleviate this artificial effect, SLR removes
\widetilde{\bm{x}}_{i}
from the set
\bar{\bm{B}}
and re-estimates the similarity between
\bm{x}_{i}
and the remaining instances in
\bar{\bm{B}}
:

\bm{q}^{t}_{ik}\!=\!\sigma^{1/\tau^{\prime}}(\widetilde{\bm{x}}_{i},\bar{\bm{b}}_{k})\big/\!\sum\nolimits_{l=1,l\neq i}^{s+b}\!\sigma^{1/\tau^{\prime}}(\widetilde{\bm{x}}_{i},\bar{\bm{b}}_{l}),\ \ \bm{q}^{t}_{ii}=0.

Finally, SLR linearly combines the one-hot label
\bm{y}_{i}
and two label estimations, i.e.
\bm{p}_{i}
and
\bm{q}_{i}
, to obtain more accurate, robust and informative label
\bar{\bm{y}}^{t}_{i}
of
\bm{x}_{i}
at the
t
-th iteration:

\bar{\bm{y}}^{t}_{i}=(1-\alpha_{t}-\beta_{t})\bm{y}_{i}+\alpha_{t}\bm{p}^{t}_{i}+\beta_{t}\bm{q}^{t}_{i},

(5)

where
\alpha_{t}
and
\beta_{t}
are two constants. In our experiments, we set

\alpha_{t}\!=\!\mu\max_{k}\bm{p}^{t}_{ik}/z
and
\beta_{t}\!=\!\mu\max_{k}\bm{q}^{t}_{ik}/z
, where
z\!=\!1\!+\!\mu\max_{k}\bm{p}^{t}_{ik}\!+\!\mu\max_{k}\bm{q}^{t}_{ik}
, the constants
1
,
\max_{k}\bm{p}^{t}_{ik}
and
\max_{k}\bm{q}^{t}_{ik}
respectively denote the largest confidences of labels
\bm{y}_{i}
,
\bm{p}^{t}_{i}
and
\bm{q}^{t}_{i}
on a certain class. Here hyperparameter
\mu
controls the prior confidence of
\bm{p}^{t}
and
\bm{q}^{t}
. So SLR only has two parameters
\tau^{\prime}
and
\mu
to tune.

The Benefit Analysis of Label Refinery.
Now we analyze the performance of our SLR on label-corrupted data. We first describe the dataset. Let
\{\bm{c}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{K}\!\!\subset\!\mathbb{R}^{d}
be
K
vanilla samples belonging to
\bar{K}\!\leq\!K
semantic classes, and
\{(\bm{x}_{i},\bm{y}_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}\!\!\in\!{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\!\times\!{\mathbb{R}}
be the random crops of
\{\bm{c}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{K}
. Since in practice, one often cares more the semantic class prediction performance of a model which often directly reflects the performance on the downstream tasks, we assume that the labels
\{\bm{y}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
denote corrupted semantic-class labels. Accordingly, we will analyze whether SLR can refine the corrupted labels
\{\bm{y}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
and whether it helps a model learn the essential semantic-class knowledge of
\{\bm{x}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
. Finally, while allowing for multiple classes, we assume the labels are scalars and take values in
[-1,1]
interval for simplicity. We formally define our label-corrupted dataset below.

Definition 1

(
(\rho,\varepsilon,\delta)
-corrupted dataset)
.

Let
\{(\bm{x}_{i},\bm{y}_{i}^{*})\}_{i=1}^{n}
denote the pairs of crops (augmentations) and ground-truth semantic label, where crop
\bm{x}_{i}
generated from the
t
-th sample
\bm{c}_{t}
obeys
\|{\bm{x}_{i}-\bm{c}_{t}}\|_{2}\!\leq\!\varepsilon
with a constant
\varepsilon
, and
\bm{y}_{i}^{*}\!\in\!\{\gamma_{t}\}_{t=1}^{\bar{K}}
of
\bm{x}_{i}
is the label of
\bm{c}_{t}
.
Moreover, samples and the crops are normalized, i.e.
\|{\bm{c}_{i}}\|_{2}\!=\!\|{\bm{x}_{k}}\|_{2}\!=\!1(\forall i,k)
. Each
\bm{c}_{i}
has
n_{i}
crops, where
c_{l}\frac{n}{K}\!\leq\!n_{i}\!\leq\!c_{u}\frac{n}{K}
with two constants
c_{l}
and
c_{u}
.
Besides, different classes are separated with a label separation
\delta
:

|\gamma_{i}-\gamma_{k}|\geq\delta,\quad\|{\bm{c}_{i}-\bm{c}_{k}}\|_{2}\geq 2\varepsilon,\ \ (\forall i\neq k).

A
(\rho,\varepsilon,\delta)
-corrupted dataset
\{(\bm{x}_{i},\bm{y}_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
obeys the above conditions but with corrupted label
\{\bm{y}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
. Spefically, for each sample
\bm{c}_{i}
, at most
\rho n_{i}
augmentations are assigned to wrong labels in
\{\gamma_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\bar{K}}
.

Then we study a network of one hidden layer as an example to investigate the label refining performance of our SLR. The network parameterized by
\bm{W}\!\!\in\!\mathbb{R}^{k\times d}
and
\bm{v}\!\in\!{\mathbb{R}^{k}}
is defined as

\bm{x}\in{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mapsto f(\bm{W},\bm{x})=\bm{v}^{\top}\phi(\bm{W}\bm{x}),

(6)

where

\phi
is an activation function. Following
[
27
,
28
,
29
]
which analyze convergence of networks or robust learning of network, we fix
\bm{v}
to be a unit vector where half the entries are
1/\!\sqrt{k}
and other half are
-1/\!\sqrt{k}
to simplify exposition. So we only optimize over
\bm{W}
that contains most network parameters and will be shown to be sufficient for label refinery. Then given a
(\rho,\varepsilon,\delta)
-corrupted dataset
\{(\bm{x}_{i},\bm{y}_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
, at the
t
-iteration we train the network via minimizing the quadratic loss:

\mathcal{L}_{t}(\bm{W})\!=\!\frac{1}{2}\sum\nolimits_{i=1}^{n}(\bar{\bm{y}}^{t}_{i}-\!f(\bm{W}\!,\bm{x}_{i}))^{2}\!=\!\frac{1}{2}\|{\bar{\bm{y}}^{t}-\!f(\bm{W},\bm{X})}\|_{2}^{2}.

Here the label
\bar{\bm{y}}^{t}_{i}
of sample
\bm{x}_{i}
is estimated by Eqn. (
5
) in which
\bm{p}^{t}_{i}=f(\bm{W}_{t},\widetilde{\bm{x}}_{i})
denotes predicted label by using the positive
\widetilde{\bm{x}}_{i}
of
\bm{x}_{i}
, i.e.
\|\widetilde{\bm{x}}_{i}-\bm{c}_{l}\|_{2}\leq\varepsilon
if
\bm{x}_{i}
is augmented from vanilla sample
\bm{c}_{l}
. We set
\beta_{t}\!=\!0
and
\tau^{\prime}\!=\!1
for simplicity, as (i) performing nonlinear mapping on network output greatly increases analysis difficulty; (ii)
our refinery (
5
) is still provably sufficient to refine labels when
\beta_{t}\!=\!0
and
\tau^{\prime}\!=\!1
. Then we update
\bm{W}
via gradient descent algorithm with a learning rate
\eta
:

\bm{W}_{t+1}=\bm{W}_{t}-\eta\nabla\mathcal{L}_{t}(\bm{W}_{t}).

(7)

Following most works on network convergence analysis
[
27
,
28
,
29
]
, we use gradient descent and quadratic loss, since (i) gradient descent is expectation version of stochastic one and often reveals similar convergence behaviors; (ii) one can expect similar results for other losses, e.g. cross entropy, but quadratic loss gives simpler gradient computation. For analysis, we impose mild assumptions on network (
6
) and our SLR, which are widely used in network analysis
[
30
,
31
,
32
,
33
]
.

Assumption 1
.

For network (
6
), assume
\phi
and its first- and second-order derivatives obey
|\phi(0)|,|\phi^{\prime}(z)|,|\phi^{\prime\prime}(z)|\!\leq\!\Gamma
for
\forall z
and some
\Gamma\!\geq\!1
,
the entries of initialization
\bm{W}_{0}
obey i.i.d.
\mathcal{N}(0,1)
.

Assumption 2
.

Define network covariance matrix
\bm{\Sigma}(\bm{C})\!=\!(\bm{C}\bm{C}^{\top})\odot\mathbb{E}_{\bm{u}}[\phi^{\prime}(\bm{C}\bm{u})\phi^{\prime}(\bm{C}\bm{u})^{\top}]

where
\bm{C}\!=\![\bm{c}_{1}\dots\bm{c}_{K}]^{\top}
,
\bm{u}\!\sim\bm{\mathcal{N}}(\bm{0},\bm{I})
,
\odot
is the elementwise product. Let
\lambda(\bm{C})\!>\!0
be the minimum eigenvalue of
\bm{\Sigma}(\bm{C})
. For label refinery, assume
3\sqrt{n}\sum_{t=0}^{t_{0}-1}\!|\alpha_{t}-\alpha_{t+1}|\leq\psi_{1}\|{f(\bm{W}_{0},\bm{X})-\bm{y}^{*}}\|_{2}
and
3\sqrt{n}\sum_{t=0}^{t_{0}-1}\big(1-\frac{\eta\alpha^{2}}{4}\big)^{t_{0}-t}|\alpha_{t}-\alpha_{t+1}|\!\leq\!\psi_{2}\|{f(\bm{W}_{0},\bm{X})-\bm{y}^{*}\!}\|_{2}^{2}
, where
t_{0}\!=\!\!\frac{c_{1}K}{\eta n\lambda(\bm{C})}\!\log\!\big(\frac{\Gamma\sqrt{n\!\log\!K}}{(1-\alpha_{\mbox{\scriptsize{max}}})\rho}\big)
with three constants
\psi_{1}
,
\psi_{2}
and
c_{1}
. Here
\alpha_{\mbox{\scriptsize{max}}}
is defined as
\alpha_{\mbox{\scriptsize{max}}}\!=\!\max_{1\leq t\leq t_{0}}\!\alpha_{t}
.

Assumption
1
is mild, as most differential activation functions, e.g. softplus and sigmoid, satisfy it, and the Gaussian initialization is used in practice. We assume Gaussian variance to be one for notation simplicity, but our technique is applicable to any constant variance. Assumption
2
requires that the discrepancy between
\alpha_{t}
and
\alpha_{t+1}
until some iteration number
t_{0}
are bounded, which holds by setting proper
\alpha_{t}
.
For
\lambda(\bm{C})
, many works
[
30
,
31
,
32
,
33
,
29
]
empirically and theoretically show
\lambda(\bm{C})\!>\!0
. Based on these assumptions, we state our results in Theorem
2
with constants
c_{1}\!\sim\!c_{6}
.

Theorem 2
.

Assume
\{(\bm{x}_{i},\bm{y}_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
is a
(\rho,\varepsilon,\delta)
-corrupted dataset with noiseless labels
\{\bm{y}_{i}^{*}\!\}_{i=1}^{n}
. Let
\xi\!=\!\log\big(\frac{\Gamma\!\sqrt{\!n\!\log\!K}}{\rho}\!\big).

Suppose
\varepsilon
and the number
k
of hidden nodes satisfy
\varepsilon\!\leq\!c_{2}\min(\frac{\lambda(\bm{C})}{K\Gamma^{2}\xi^{6}},\frac{\rho}{\alpha_{\mbox{\scriptsize{max}}}})
,
k\!\geq\!{\frac{c_{3}K^{2}\Gamma^{10}\xi^{6}\|\bm{C}\|^{4}}{\alpha_{\mbox{\scriptsize{max}}}^{2}\lambda(\bm{C})^{4}}}.
Let
\psi^{\prime}=1+\frac{\psi_{1}}{2}+\sqrt{\psi_{2}}
.
If step size
\eta\leq\frac{K}{2c_{up}n\Gamma^{2}\left\|\bm{C}\right\|^{2}}
, with probability
1\!-\!3/K^{100}\!-\!K\exp(-100d)
, after
t\!\geq\!t_{0}\!=\!\frac{c_{4}K}{\eta n\lambda(\bm{C})}\log\big(\frac{\Gamma\sqrt{n\log K}}{(1-\alpha_{\mbox{\scriptsize{max}}})\rho}\big)
iterations, the gradient descent (
7
) satisfies:

(1) By defining
\zeta\!=\!4\rho\!+\!c_{5}\varepsilon\psi^{\prime}K\Gamma^{3}\xi\sqrt{\log K}/\lambda(\bm{C})
and
\bm{y}^{*}{}=[\bm{y}_{1}^{*},\!\cdots,\bm{y}_{n}^{*}]
, the discrepancy between the label
\bar{\bm{y}}^{t}
estimated by our SLR (
5
) and the true label
\bm{y}^{*}
of the augmentation data
\{\bm{x}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
is bounded:

\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\|{\bar{\bm{y}}^{t}-\bm{y}^{*}}\|_{2}\leq\frac{1-\alpha_{t}}{\sqrt{n}}\|{\bm{y}-\bm{y}^{*}}\|_{2}+\alpha_{t}\zeta.

where
\bar{\bm{y}}^{t}=[\bar{\bm{y}}^{t}_{1},\!\cdots,\bar{\bm{y}}^{t}_{n}]
.
Moreover, if
\rho\!\leq\!\frac{\delta}{32}
,
\varepsilon\!\leq\!c_{6}\delta\min\big(\!\frac{\lambda(\bm{C})^{2}}{{\psi^{\prime}\Gamma^{5}K^{2}}\xi^{3}},\frac{1}{\Gamma\sqrt{d}}\big)
,
1\!-\!\frac{3}{4}\delta\leq\!\alpha_{t}
, the estimated label
\bar{\bm{y}}^{t}
predicts true label
\bm{y}_{i}^{*}
of any crop
\bm{x}_{i}
:

\gamma_{k^{*}}=\bm{y}_{i}^{*}\quad\text{with}\quad k^{*}=\argmin\nolimits_{1\leq k\leq\bar{K}}|\bar{\bm{y}}^{t}_{i}-\gamma_{k}|.

(2) By using the refined label
\bar{\bm{y}}^{t}
in (
5
) to train network and letting
f(\bm{W}_{t},\bm{X})\!=\![f(\bm{W}_{t},\bm{x}_{1}),\cdots,f(\bm{W}_{t},\bm{x}_{n})]
, the error of network prediction on
\{\bm{x}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
is upper bounded

\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\|{f(\bm{W}_{t},\bm{X})-\bm{y}^{*}}\|_{2}\leq\zeta.

If assumptions on
\rho
and
\varepsilon
in (1) hold, for vanilla sample
\bm{c}_{k}
(
\forall k=\!1\cdots K
), network
f(\bm{W}_{\!t},\cdot)
predicts the true semantic label
\gamma_{k}
of its any augmentation
\bm{x}
that satisfies
\|{\bm{x}\!-\!\bm{c}_{k}}\|_{2}\!\leq\!\varepsilon
:

\gamma_{k^{*}}=\gamma_{k}\quad\text{with}\quad k^{*}=\argmin\nolimits_{1\leq i\leq\bar{K}}|f(\bm{W}_{t},\bm{x})-\gamma_{i}|.

See its
proof roadmap
and proof in Appendix
D.2
. The first result in Theorem
2
shows that after training iterations
t_{0}
, the discrepancy between the label
\bar{\bm{y}}^{t}
estimated by our label refinery (
5
), i.e. SLR, and ground truth label
\bm{y}^{*}
of cropped training data
\{\bm{x}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
is upper bounded by
\mathcal{O}\big(\|{\bm{y}-\bm{y}^{*}}\|_{2}+\zeta\big)
. Both factors
\|{\bm{y}-\bm{y}^{*}}\|_{2}
and
\rho
in the factor
\zeta
reflect the label error of the provided corrupted label
\bm{y}
. Another important factor in
\zeta
is the smallest eigenvalue
\lambda(\bm{C})
of network covariance matrix
\bm{\Sigma}(\bm{C})
in Assumption
2
. Typically, the performance of a network heavily relies on the data diversity even without label corruption. For instance, if two samples are nearly the same but have different labels, the learning of a network is difficult.
\lambda(\bm{C})
can quantify this data diversity, as one can think of
\lambda(\bm{C})
as a condition number associated with the network which measures the diversity of the vanilla samples
\{\bm{c}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
. Intuitively, if there are two similar vanilla samples,
\bm{\Sigma}(\bm{C})
is trivially rank deficient and has small minimum eigenvalue, meaning more challenges to distinguish the augmentations
\bm{x}
generated from
\bm{c}_{i}
.
Moreover, when the label corruption ratio
\rho
and the augmentation distance
\varepsilon
are small, the label
\bar{\bm{y}}^{t}_{i}
estimated by our SLR can predict the true semantic label
\bm{y}_{i}^{*}
for any crop sample
\bm{x}_{i}
, and thus can supervises a network to learn the essential semantic-class knowledges from
\{\bm{x}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
.

The second result in Theorem
2
shows that by using the refined label
\bar{\bm{y}}^{t}
in our SLR (
5
) to train network
f(\bm{W},\cdot)
, the error of network prediction on augmentations
\{\bm{x}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
can be upper bounded by
\zeta
. Similarly, the factor
\rho
and
\lambda(\bm{C})
in
\zeta
respectively reflect the initial label error and the data diversity, which both reflect the learning difficulty for a model on the augmentation data
\{(\bm{x}_{i},\bm{y}_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
. More importantly, our results also guarantee the test performance of the trained network
f(\bm{W}_{\!t},\cdot)
. Specifically, when the label corruption ratio
\rho
and sample augmentation distance
\varepsilon
are small, for any vanilla sample
\bm{c}_{k}
(
\forall k\!=\!1\cdots K
), the network
f(\bm{W}_{\!t},\cdot)
trained by our SLR can exactly predict the true semantic label
\gamma_{k}
of its any augmentation
\bm{x}
(i.e.
\|{\bm{x}\!-\!\bm{c}_{k}}\|_{2}\!\leq\!\varepsilon
). These results accord with Theorem
1
that shows the more accurate of training labels, the better generalization of the trained network. These results show the effectiveness of the refined labels by our method.

3.2
Momentum Mixup

Now we propose momentum mixup (MM) to further reduce the possible label noise in realistic data and increase the data diversity as well. Similar to vanilla mixup
[
34
]
, we construct virtual instance as

\bm{x}_{i}^{\prime}=\theta\bm{x}_{i}+(1-\theta)\widetilde{\bm{x}}_{k},\quad\bm{y}_{i}^{\prime}=\theta\bar{\bm{y}}_{i}+(1-\theta)\bar{\bm{y}}_{k},\quad\theta\sim\textsf{\footnotesize{Beta}}(\kappa,\kappa)\in[0,1],

(8)

where
\widetilde{\bm{x}}_{k}
is randomly sampled from the key set
\{\widetilde{\bm{x}}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{s}
,
\bar{\bm{y}}_{i}
denotes the refined label by Eqn. (
5
),
\textsf{\footnotesize{Beta}}(\kappa,\kappa)
is a beta distribution. Here
\bm{x}_{i}
and
\widetilde{\bm{x}}_{i}
share the same label
\bar{\bm{y}}_{i}
on the set
\bar{\bm{B}}\!=\!\{\widetilde{\bm{x}}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{s}\cup\{\bm{b}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{b}
, as they come from the same instance. We call the mixup (
8
) as “momentum mixup", since the sample
\widetilde{\bm{x}}_{k}
is fed into the momentum-updated network

g_{\bm{\xi}}

, and plays a contrastive key for instance discrimination. So MM differs from the vanilla mixup used in
[
22
,
23
]
where
\widetilde{\bm{x}}_{k}
is replaced with
\bm{x}_{k}
and both are fed into online network
f_{\bm{w}}
, and enjoys the following advantages.

Firstly, MM can improve the accuracy of the label
\bm{y}_{i}^{\prime}
compared with vanilla mixup.
For explanation, assume
\bar{\bm{y}}_{i}
in (
8
) is one-hot label. Then
\bm{x}_{i}^{\prime}
has two positive keys
\widetilde{\bm{x}}_{i}
and
\widetilde{\bm{x}}_{k}
in
\bar{\bm{B}}
decided by its label
\bm{y}_{i}^{\prime}
. Accordingly, the component
\widetilde{\bm{x}}_{k}
in
\bm{x}_{i}^{\prime}\!=\!\theta\bm{x}_{i}\!+\!(1\!-\!\theta)\widetilde{\bm{x}}_{k}
directly increases the similarity between the query
\bm{x}_{i}^{\prime}
and its positive key
\widetilde{\bm{x}}_{k}
in
\bar{\bm{B}}
. So the label weight
(1\!-\!\theta)
of label
\bm{y}_{i}^{\prime}
on the key
\widetilde{\bm{x}}_{k}
to bring
\bm{x}_{i}^{\prime}
and
\widetilde{\bm{x}}_{k}
together is relatively accurate, as
\bm{x}_{i}^{\prime}
really contains the semantic information of
\widetilde{\bm{x}}_{k}
. Meanwhile, the sum of label weights in
\bm{y}_{i}^{\prime}
on remaining instance in
\bar{\bm{B}}\backslash\widetilde{\bm{x}}_{k}
is scaled by
\theta
, which also scales the possible label noise on instances in
\bar{\bm{B}}\backslash\widetilde{\bm{x}}_{k}
smaller due to
\theta\!<\!1
. By comparison, for vanilla mixup, the label weight
(1\!-\!\theta)
of label
\bm{y}_{i}^{\prime}
on the key
\widetilde{\bm{x}}_{i}
does not improve label accuracy. It is because the positive pair
\bm{x}_{k}
and
\widetilde{\bm{x}}_{k}
are obtained via random augmentation, e.g. crop, and may not be semantically similar, meaning that the component
\bm{x}_{k}
in
\bm{x}_{i}^{\prime}
could not increase similarity with
\widetilde{\bm{x}}_{k}
.
So its label weight
(1-\!\theta)
to push
\bm{x}_{i}^{\prime}
close to the key
\widetilde{\bm{x}}_{k}
is not as accurate as the one in MM.

Another advantage of MM is that it allows us to use strong augmentation. As observed in
[
12
]
, directly using strong augmentation in contrastive learning, e.g. MoCo, leads to performance degradation, since the instance obtained by strong augmentation often heavily differs from the one with weak augmentation. As aforementioned, the component
\widetilde{\bm{x}}_{k}
in
\bm{x}_{i}^{\prime}=\theta\bm{x}_{i}+(1-\theta)\widetilde{\bm{x}}_{k}
increases the similarity between the query
\bm{x}_{i}^{\prime}
and the key
\widetilde{\bm{x}}_{k}
in
\bar{\bm{B}}
, even though
(\bm{x}_{i},\widetilde{\bm{x}}_{i})
is obtained via strong augmentation. So MM could reduce the matching difficulty between positive instances.

With all the components in place, we are ready to define our proposed
SANE model
as follows:

\mathcal{L}(\bm{w})=(1-\lambda)\mathcal{L}_{\mbox{\scriptsize{c}}}\big(\bm{w},\{(\bm{x}_{i},\bm{y}_{i})\}\big)+\lambda\mathcal{L}_{\mbox{\scriptsize{c}}}\big(\bm{w},\ \{(\bm{x}_{i}^{\prime},\bm{y}_{i}^{\prime})\}\big),

(9)

where
\mathcal{L}_{\mbox{\scriptsize{c}}}\big(\bm{w},\{(\bm{x}_{i},\bm{y}_{i})\}\big)
defined in Eqn. (
4
) denotes the vanilla contrastive loss with one-hot label
\bm{y}_{i}
,
\mathcal{L}_{\mbox{\scriptsize{c}}}\big(\bm{w},\{(\bm{x}_{i}^{\prime},\bm{y}_{i}^{\prime})\}\big)
denotes the momentum mixup loss with label
\bm{y}_{i}^{\prime}
estimated by our self-labeling refinery (
5
), and
\lambda
is a constant. Experimental results in Sec.
4
show the effectiveness of both loss terms. See algorithm details in Algorithm
1
of Appendix
B
.

Limitation Discussion.
SANE follows MoCo-alike framework and hopes to obtain a more accurate soft label of a query over its positive and negatives for instance discrimination. So one limitation of SANE is that it does not apply to BYOL-alike methods
[
6
]
that only pulls positive pair together and does not require any labels. However, momentum mixup in SANE which increases the similarity of positive pair may also benefit BYOL, which is left as our future work to thoroughly test.

5
Conclusion

In this work, we prove the benefits of accurate labels to
the generalization of contrastive learning. Inspired by this theory, we propose SANE to improve label quality in contrastive learning via self-labeling refinery and momentum mixup. The former uses the positive of a query to generate informative soft labels and combines with vanilla one-hot label to improve label quality. The latter randomly combines queries and positives to make virtual queries more similar to their corresponding positives, improving label accuracy. Experimental results testified the advantages of SANE.

D.1.1
General Model Formulation

In this section, we outline our approach to proving robustness of overparameterized neural networks. Towards this goal, we consider a general formulation where we aim to fit a general nonlinear model of the form
\bm{x}\mapsto f(\bm{w},\bm{x})
with
\bm{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
denoting the parameters of the model. For instance in the case of neural networks
\bm{w}
represents its weights. Given a data set of
n

\{(\bm{x}_{i},\bm{y}_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}\subset{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\times\mathbb{R}

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{t}(\bm{w})=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\bar{\bm{y}}^{t}_{i}-f(\bm{w},\bm{x}_{i}))^{2}.

where
\bar{\bm{y}}^{t}_{i}=(1-\alpha_{t})\bm{y}_{i}+\alpha_{t}\bm{p}^{t}=(1-\alpha_{t})\bm{y}_{i}+\alpha_{t}f(\bm{w}_{t},\bm{x}_{i})

denotes the estimated label of sample
\bm{x}_{i}
. In Assumption
2
we assume
\beta_{t}\!=\!0
and
\tau^{\prime}=1
for simplicity, since performing nonlinear mapping on network output greatly increases analysis difficulty. But we will show that even though
\beta_{t}\!=\!0
and
\tau^{\prime}=1
, our refinery (
5
) is still sufficient to refine labels. It can also be written in the more compact form

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{t}(\bm{w})=\frac{1}{2}\left\|f(\bm{w})-\bar{\bm{y}}^{t}\right\|_{\ell_{2}}^{2}\quad\text{with}\quad f(\bm{w}):=\begin{bmatrix}f(\bm{w},\bm{x}_{1})\\
f(\bm{w},\bm{x}_{2})\\
\vdots\\
f(\bm{w},\bm{x}_{n})\end{bmatrix}.

(13)

To solve this problem we run gradient descent iterations with a constant learning rate
\eta
starting from an initial point
\bm{w}_{0}
. These iterations take the form

\displaystyle\bm{w}_{t+1}=\bm{w}_{t}-\eta\nabla\mathcal{L}_{t}(\bm{w}_{t})\quad\text{with}\quad\nabla\mathcal{L}(\bm{w})=\mathcal{J}^{T}(\bm{w})\left(f(\bm{w})-\bar{\bm{y}}^{t}\right).

(14)

Here,
\mathcal{J}(\bm{w})
is the

Jacobian matrix associated with the nonlinear mapping

defined via

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}(\bm{w})=\begin{bmatrix}\frac{{\partial}f(\bm{w},\bm{x}_{1})}{{\partial}\bm{w}}~\dots~\frac{{\partial}f(\bm{w},\bm{x}_{n})}{{\partial}\bm{w}}\end{bmatrix}^{T}.

(15)

Define the
n
-dimensional residual vector and corrupted residual vector
\bm{e}
where

\bm{r}_{t}=\bm{r}_{t}(\bm{w})=\begin{bmatrix}f(\bm{x}_{1},\bm{w}_{t})-\bar{\bm{y}}^{t}_{1}&\ldots&f(\bm{x}_{n},\bm{w}_{t})-\bar{\bm{y}}^{t}_{n}\end{bmatrix}^{T}\quad\text{and}\quad\bm{e}_{t}=\bar{\bm{y}}^{t}-\bm{y}^{*}.

A key idea in our approach is that we argue that (1) in the absence of any corruption

approximately lies on the subspace
\mathcal{S}_{+}
and (2) if the labels are corrupted by a vector

approximately lies on the complement space.

Throughout,

denotes the smallest singular value of a given matrix. We first introduce helpful definitions that will be used in our proofs. Given a matrix

\sigma_{\min}(\bm{X},\bm{\mathcal{S}})=\sup_{\|{\bm{v}}\|_{2}=1,\bm{U}\bm{U}^{T}=\mathcal{P}_{\bm{\mathcal{S}}}}\|{\bm{v}^{T}\bm{U}^{T}\bm{X}}\|_{2}.

Here,

Our approach is based on the hypothesis that the nonlinear model has a Jacobian matrix with

Before proving Theorem

In the

, we prove Theorem

) by using Theorem

. We consider

to its associated cluster center, i.e.

\displaystyle\widetilde{\bm{W}}_{t+1}=\widetilde{\bm{W}}_{t}-\nabla\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}_{t}(\widetilde{\bm{W}}_{t})\quad\text{where}\quad\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}_{t}(\widetilde{\bm{W}})=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\bm{y}_{ti}-f(\widetilde{\bm{W}},\widetilde{\bm{x}}_{i}))^{2}

Theorem

shows that for neural networks, our method still can upper bound the distance

and the residuals

for refining label satisfy certain conditions.

In the

\displaystyle\bm{W}_{t+1}=\bm{W}_{t}-\eta\nabla\mathcal{L}_{t}(\bm{W}_{t})\quad\text{where}\quad\mathcal{L}_{t}(\bm{W})=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\bm{y}_{ti}-f(\bm{W},\bm{x}_{i}))^{2}.

(16)
