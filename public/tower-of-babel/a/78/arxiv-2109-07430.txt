Multiparameter estimation for qubit states with collective measurements: a case study
Yink Loong Len

Abstract
Quantum estimation involving multiple parameters remains an important problem of both theoretical and practical interest. In this work, we study the problem of simultaneous estimation of two parameters that are respectively associate with the length and direction of the Bloch vector for identically prepared qubit states that is confined to a plane, where in order to obtain the optimal estimation precision for both parameters, collective measurements on multiple qubits are necessary. Upon treating N N qubits as an ensemble of spin-1/2 systems, we show that simultaneous optimal estimation for both parameters can be attained asymptotically with a simple collective measurement strategy—First, we estimate the length parameter by measuring the populations in spaces corresponding to different total angular momentum values j j , then we estimate the direction parameter by performing a spin projection onto an optimal basis. Furthermore, we show that when the state is nearly pure, for sufficiently but not arbitrarily large N N , most information will be captured in the largest three j j -subspaces. Then, we study how the total angular-momentum measurement can be realized by observing output signatures from a Bell multiport setup, either exactly for N = 2 , 3 N=2,3 , or approximately when the qubits are nearly pure for other N N values. We also obtain numerical results that suggest that using a Bell multiport setup, one can distinguish between projection onto the j = N / 2 j=N/2 and j = N / 2 − 1 j=N/2-1 subspaces from their respective interference signatures at the output.

3
Two-parameter estimation for qubit states

In this section, we apply the general formalism described above to the estimation of the length and direction of Bloch vector for a qubit state. Then, we show how one can asymptotically achieve compatible joint estimation for the two parameters, by a measuring an observable that can be thought of as the total angular momentum squared operator, followed by a commuting, local projective measurement. Furthermore, we look deeper into the case of nearly-pure qubit estimation, and reveal a connection with the problem of superresolution imaging in the sub-Rayleigh limit.

and

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{N;\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=N\frac{1}{1-s(\varepsilon)^{2}}\Big(\frac{\partial s(\varepsilon)}{\partial\varepsilon}\Big)^{2}.

(16)

Then, as
\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\sigma_{y}\varphi/2}\sigma_{z}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\sigma_{y}\varphi/2}=\hat{\mathrm{e}}_{\boldsymbol{s}}\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma}
, from Eq. (
15
), the optimal precision
\mathcal{F}_{N;\varepsilon}
can be attained by performing the projective measurement specified by the directions
\pm\hat{\mathrm{e}}_{\boldsymbol{s}}
, i.e.,
\{\frac{\mathds{1}+\hat{\mathrm{e}}_{\boldsymbol{s}}\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma}}{2},\frac{\mathds{1}-\hat{\mathrm{e}}_{\boldsymbol{s}}\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma}}{2}\}
for each of the qubit, which has the respective probability
p_{\pm}(\varepsilon)=\frac{1\pm s(\varepsilon)}{2}
of obtaining them, and so
\varepsilon=s^{-1}(p_{+}(\varepsilon)-p_{-}(\varepsilon))
. Note that in this case the optimal measurement does not depend on the parameter
\varepsilon
itself.

An unbiased estimator (in the asymptotic
\nu\rightarrow\infty
limit)
\hat{\varepsilon}_{N}
that attains the QFI
\mathcal{F}_{N;\varepsilon}
for any
N
is the maximum-likelihood estimator (MLE)
[
52
,
53
]
. More precisely, denote
\nu_{\pm}^{(i)}
as the relative frequencies of obtaining the outcome of
\frac{\mathds{1}\pm\hat{\mathrm{e}}_{\boldsymbol{s}}\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{(i)}}{2}
for the
i
-th qubit over the
\nu
repetitions, such that
\nu_{+}^{(i)}+\nu_{-}^{(i)}=1
for all
i
. Then, our data is essentially
D=\{\nu_{+},\nu_{-}\}
with
\nu_{\pm}:=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{\nu_{\pm}^{(i)}}{N}
, and the MLE is defined by

\displaystyle\hat{\varepsilon}_{N}(D):=\arg\max_{\varepsilon}L(D|\varepsilon)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\arg\max_{\varepsilon}p_{+}(\varepsilon)^{N\nu\nu_{+}}p_{-}(\varepsilon)^{N\nu\nu_{-}}.

(17)

Note that, for data where
\nu_{+}-\nu_{-}
is non-negative (which will have overall likelihood approaching one as
\nu\rightarrow\infty
by virtue of the central limit theorem such that
\sqrt{\nu}\nu_{\pm}
converges to a Gaussian peaked at
\sqrt{\nu}p_{\pm}(\varepsilon)
), the MLE can be conveniently written as
\hat{\varepsilon}_{N}(D):=s^{-1}(\nu_{+}-\nu_{-})
, which coincides with the linear inversion estimator. Otherwise, the maximization in Eq. (
17
) needs to be carried out explicitly, and the MLE will in general have a small bias (which vanishes as
\nu\rightarrow\infty
) as compared to the linear inversion estimator.

3.2.2
Estimation of
\varphi

With
\varepsilon
being known and fixed instead, we find that

\displaystyle L_{\varphi}

\displaystyle=2s(\varepsilon)\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}J_{y}\varphi}J_{x}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}J_{y}\varphi},

(18)

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{N;\varphi}

\displaystyle=Ns(\varepsilon)^{2},

(19)

where the optimal precision
\mathcal{F}_{N;\varphi}
can be obtained with the projective measurement onto the direction specified by
\pm\hat{\mathrm{e}}_{\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}}
for each qubit, where
\hat{\mathrm{e}}_{\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}}=\cos\varphi\,\hat{\mathrm{e}}_{x}-\sin\varphi\,\hat{\mathrm{e}}_{z}
. Note that in this case the optimal measurement does depend on the parameter
\varphi
itself—a common feature in local estimation problem. In particular then, suppose we are performing measurement in the basis specified by
\pm\hat{\mathrm{e}}_{\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}}
with
\hat{\mathrm{e}}_{\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}}=\cos\alpha\,\hat{\mathrm{e}}_{x}-\sin\alpha\,\hat{\mathrm{e}}_{z}
where eventually
\alpha\rightarrow\varphi
, such that the respective probablity is given by
p_{\pm}(\varphi)=\frac{1\pm s(\varepsilon)\sin(\varphi-\alpha)}{2}
, and then
\varphi=\alpha+\sin^{-1}(\frac{p_{+}(\varphi)-p_{-}(\varphi)}{s(\varepsilon)})
. In order to attain the QFI
\mathcal{F}_{N;\varphi}
in the asymptotic limit
\nu\rightarrow\infty
for any
N
we may consider the MLE as defined by

\displaystyle\hat{\varphi}_{N}(D):=\arg\max_{\varphi}L(D|\varphi)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\arg\max_{\varphi}p_{+}(\varphi)^{N\nu\nu_{+}}p_{-}(\varphi)^{N\nu\nu_{-}},

(20)

where now
\nu_{\pm}:=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{\nu_{\pm}^{(i)}}{N}
is the sum of the relative frequencies for the projection onto
\frac{\mathds{1}\pm\hat{\mathrm{e}}_{\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}}\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{(i)}}{2}
. Similar to the discussion earlier, the MLE will coincide with the linear inversion estimator with
\hat{\varphi}_{N}(D):=\varphi+\sin^{-1}(\frac{\nu_{+}-\nu_{-}}{s(\varepsilon)})
after taking
\alpha\rightarrow\varphi
, when
\frac{\nu_{+}-\nu_{-}}{s(\varepsilon)}
is bounded between zero and one, which will be mostly the case as
\nu\rightarrow\infty
. As a remark,
\mathcal{F}_{N;\varphi}
can only be attained by the said projective measurement onto the direction
\pm\hat{\mathrm{e}}_{\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}}
with
\alpha=\varphi
for any
s(\varepsilon)
that is strictly not equal to zero or unity; for
s(\varepsilon)=1
, arbitrary values of
\alpha
would also attain
\mathcal{F}_{N;\varphi}
.

3.3
Joint-parameter estimation

First, from a quick observation where the state
\rho_{N}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
(
13
) and the two SLDs (
15
,
18
) are essentially all real in the same basis, we can immediately conclude that the weak commutativity condition holds. It then follows that the Holevo bound and the multi-parameter QFI CRB are equal, which dictate the first order convergence rate of the weighted trace of the covariance matrix as
N\rightarrow\infty
. Moreover, one can readily verify that we have
\mathrm{Tr}\{\rho_{N}(\boldsymbol{\theta})L_{\varepsilon}L_{\varphi}\}=\mathrm{Tr}\{\rho_{N}(\boldsymbol{\theta})L_{\varphi}L_{\varepsilon}\}=0
, and therefore the QFI matrix is diagonal, such that the two parameters are globally orthogonal and it is in principle possible to realize compatible joint estimation for them. That is, the optimal precisions in simultaneously estimating the two parameters in
\rho_{N}(\varepsilon,\varphi)
are given by their respective QFI as in the single-parameter scenarios; see Eq. (
16
) and Eq. (
19
).

Notice that, however,
L_{\varepsilon}L_{\varphi}\neq L_{\varphi}L_{\varepsilon}
, which means that the optimal local measurement bases for estimation of
\varepsilon
and
\varphi
actually do not commute. In fact, as
\hat{\mathrm{e}}_{\boldsymbol{s}}\cdot\hat{\mathrm{e}}_{\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}}=0
, they correspond to mutually unbiased bases
[
27
,
68
]
—acquiring optimal information about one parameter as such completely erases information about the other, and therefore we could never obtain simultaneous estimation of both parameters with optimal precision by measuring the
N
qubits separately. Therefore in order to attain still the multi-parameter QFI CRB, we must now consider collective or global measurements on the whole
N
-qubit ensemble instead.

3.3.1
Measurement with
\boldsymbol{J}^{2}
and
L_{\varphi}

We look for collective measurements that preserve information about the second parameter, say
\varphi
, when accessing information about the first parameter, say
\varepsilon
. Then, as
\varphi
serves as the angle of rotation generated by angular momentum
J_{y}
, see Eq. (
13
), it is inviting for us to consider measuring the angular momentum squared operator,
\boldsymbol{J}^{2}
, which is invariant under rotation, for estimation of
\varepsilon
. Indeed, as the eigenvalues or spectrum of the state
\rho(\boldsymbol{\theta})
, which is
\frac{1\pm s(\varepsilon)}{2}
, is related exclusively to the parameter
\varepsilon
but not the parameter
\varphi
, the estimation of
\varepsilon
can also be viewed as estimation of the spectrum of
\rho(\boldsymbol{\theta})
, where Keyl and Werner have shown that the
\boldsymbol{J}^{2}
measurement on
\rho_{N}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
produces estimates that have vanishing error as
N\rightarrow\infty

[
69
]
. This fact has also been discussed in
[
17
]
, and similar conclusions can also be observed in Refs.
[
36
,
51
]
.

In spite of the insights and conclusions from the above references, let us here provide a slightly different but perhaps the most direct and explicit account on the MSE for estimation of
\varepsilon
with the
\boldsymbol{J}^{2}
measurement, by applying the well-known error-propagation formula
[
70
]
for
\nu\rightarrow\infty
, which reads (see
A
for details of the evaluation)

\displaystyle\nu\Delta^{2}\hat{\varepsilon}_{N}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathrm{Tr}\{(\boldsymbol{J}^{2})^{2}\rho_{N}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\}-\mathrm{Tr}\{\boldsymbol{J}^{2}\rho_{N}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\}^{2}}{|\frac{\partial}{\partial\varepsilon}\mathrm{Tr}\{\boldsymbol{J}^{2}\rho_{N}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\}|^{2}}

(21)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\Bigg[\frac{(3-2N)s(\varepsilon)^{4}+2(N-3)s(\varepsilon)^{2}+3}{2(N-1)s(\varepsilon)^{2}\left(\frac{\partial s(\varepsilon)}{\partial\varepsilon}\right)^{2}}\Bigg].

Eq. (
21
) applies to most estimator
\hat{\varepsilon}_{N}
that takes in the mean of
\boldsymbol{J}^{2}
(as estimated from the data by
\langle\boldsymbol{J}^{2}\rangle(D)=\sum_{j=N/2-\lfloor N/2\rfloor}^{N/2}j(j+1)\nu_{j}
, where
\nu_{j}
is the relative frequency for the projection onto the
j
-subspace of
\boldsymbol{J}^{2}
over
\nu
repetitions) as input, and precisely estimates
\varepsilon
when the estimated mean is exactly the true mean value, i.e.,
\langle\boldsymbol{J}^{2}\rangle(D)=\Tr\{\rho_{N}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\boldsymbol{J}^{2}\}
. Then, we check that
in the large
N
limit,
\nu\Delta^{2}\hat{\varepsilon}_{N}
has the asymptotic behaviour

\displaystyle\nu\Delta^{2}\hat{\varepsilon}_{N}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\Big[\frac{N}{N-1}(1-s(\varepsilon)^{2})+\frac{3s(\varepsilon)^{2}-6+3s(\varepsilon)^{-2}}{2(N-1)}\Big]\left(\frac{\partial s(\varepsilon)}{\partial\varepsilon}\right)^{-2}

(22)

\displaystyle\sim\frac{1}{N}(1-s(\varepsilon)^{2})\left(\frac{\partial s(\varepsilon)}{\partial\varepsilon}\right)^{-2}+\Or\Big(\frac{1}{N^{2}}\Big)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{F}_{N;\varepsilon}^{-1}+\Or\Big(\frac{1}{N^{2}}\Big),

where
\Or(1/N^{2})
stands for terms that are of order
1/N^{2}
and beyond. We thus have a clear demonstration that as
N
gets ever larger,
\nu\Delta^{2}\hat{\varepsilon}_{N}\sim\frac{N}{N-1}\mathcal{F}_{N;\varepsilon}^{-1}\rightarrow\mathcal{F}_{N;\varepsilon}^{-1}
, i.e., indeed the
\boldsymbol{J}^{2}
measurement permits construction of estimator which asymptotically estimates
\varepsilon
as precise as using the optimal projective measurement when assuming the parameter
\varphi
is known (c.f. Eqs. (
15
,
16
)). Importantly, although
\nu\Delta^{2}\hat{\varepsilon}_{N}\rightarrow\mathcal{F}_{N;\varepsilon}^{-1}
only asymptotically, the estimation of
\varepsilon
is global here, in the sense that the observable
\boldsymbol{J}^{2}
is independent of the two parameters
\varepsilon
and
\varepsilon
, and so allows us to continue estimating
\varphi
without any potential lost of information as desired. That is, as
\boldsymbol{J}^{2}
commutes with
L_{\varphi}
—one can explicitly confirm with Eq. (
18
), we may still effectively measure in the
L_{\varphi}
basis, and obtain, in the
\nu\rightarrow\infty
limit for any
N
,

\displaystyle\nu\Delta^{2}\hat{\varphi}_{N}=\mathcal{F}_{N;\varphi}^{-1},

(23)

using for example the MLE
\hat{\varphi}_{N}
introduced in Eq. (
20
) in Sec.
3.2.2
.

In Fig.
2
, we illustrate the convergence of the weighted sum of two the estimation precision,
\frac{1}{2}\nu\Delta^{2}\hat{\varepsilon}_{N}+\frac{1}{2}\nu\Delta^{2}\hat{\varphi}_{N}
, with the
\boldsymbol{J}^{2}
and
L_{\varphi}
measurement strategy, to the ultimate bound
\frac{1}{2}\mathcal{F}_{N;\varepsilon}^{-1}+\frac{1}{2}\mathcal{F}_{N;\varphi}^{-1}
, for the chosen parametrization of
s(\varepsilon)=1-\varepsilon^{2}/8
.

.

3.3.2
Nearly-pure qubit estimation

Having established the general results for compatible joint estimation for
\varepsilon
and
\varphi
, let us look deeper into the scenario where the qubits are nearly pure, i.e.,
s(\varepsilon)\approx 1
. In particular, we focus on “regular” parametrization where
s(\varepsilon)
is smooth around some value
\varepsilon_{0}
with
s(\varepsilon_{0})=1
, such that we can perform the Taylor expansion in powers of
\delta\ll 1
around
\varepsilon_{0}
for some
\varepsilon=\varepsilon_{0}-\delta
. Then, while the conclusion below is generally true for any regular parametrization of
s(\varepsilon)
as well, for explicit illustration, let us consider specifically
s(\varepsilon)=1-\varepsilon^{2}/8
with
\varepsilon\ll 1
, a choice that is motivated by a case study in quantum imaging superresolution problem: As reported in Ref.
[
50
]
, estimating
\varphi
and
\varepsilon
in this parameterization could be seen as estimating the centroid and the separation between two close incoherent light sources (up to some multiplicative constant), respectively.

We shed some light as to how the
\boldsymbol{J}^{2}
measurement works in this
\varepsilon\ll 1
regime. From the theory of angular momentum, see textbooks like Refs.
[
71
,
72
]
, we know that the full
N

\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{total}}
, can be decomposed into direct sum of orthogonal subspaces specified by two numbers,
j
and
g(j)

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{total}}

\displaystyle=\bigoplus_{j=N/2-\lfloor N/2\rfloor}^{N/2}\Big(\bigoplus_{g(j)=1}^{\mu_{j}}\mathcal{H}_{j,g(j)}\Big).

(24)

Here,
j=\{N/2,N/2-1,\ldots,N/2-\lfloor N/2\rfloor\}
is nothing but the usual quantum number associated with the total angular momentum squared, while
g(j)=\{1,\ldots,\mu_{j}-1,\mu_{j}\}
specifies the
\mu_{j}
different subspaces for the same
j
value. Generally, the multiplicity factor
\mu_{j}
depends on how the total angular momentum is composed; for our case of
N
spin-1/2s, it is known that, see for example Refs.
[
73
]
and
[
74
]

,

\mu_{j}={\left(\begin{array}[]{@{}c@{}}N\\

\mathcal{P}_{j}

4
Realization of the
\boldsymbol{J}^{2}
and
L_{\varphi}
measurement

Let us now discuss the implementation of the
\boldsymbol{J}^{2}
and
L_{\varphi}
measurement. While measuring the local operator
L_{\varphi}
should be straightforward, measuring the global operator
\boldsymbol{J}^{2}
is more challenging. For the simplest scenario with
N=2
, exact implementation of such a scheme is known for bosonic system: measurement of the
\boldsymbol{J}^{2}
basis is realized using the Hong-Ou-Mandel (HOM) interference effect
[
76
,
77
]
, whereas the
L_{\varphi}
measurement is performed in its local basis, after the interference. In fact, a recent experiment demonstrating superresolution estimation of the centroid and separation between two mutually incoherent sources in the sub-Rayleigh limit
[
78
]
—a task which can be well approximated as the estimation of
\varepsilon
and
\varphi
in our model
[
50
]
—utilizes essentially the same idea.

For the sake of clarity as well as introducing the notation, below, we first review the known results of how the setup of HOM interferometer followed by projective measurements at the end works for the
N=2
case. Note that here, our study include both bosons and fermions. Then, new in this work we show how this can be generalized to the
N=3
case, before moving on to briefly discussing the more complicated scenarios with larger
N
, where the
\boldsymbol{J}^{2}
measurement is approached from a quantum circuit perspective, with ancilla qubits involved. Lastly, we look closer into the case of nearly-pure qubit estimation, where in conjunction with the discussion in Sec.
3.3.2
, non-exact but
approximate
implementations that focus on distinguishing between just the
j=N/2
and
j=N/2-1
subspaces will be considered. In what follows, for convenience of the notation, we will use
\{\textsc{h},\textsc{v}\}
to specify a basis for the qubit system, such that, e.g.,
\sigma_{z}={\left|{\textsc{h}}\right\rangle}\!{\left\langle{\textsc{h}}\right|}-{\left|{\textsc{v}}\right\rangle}\!{\left\langle{\textsc{v}}\right|}
.

\{\frac{\mathds{1}+\hat{\mathrm{e}}_{\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}}\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma}}{2},\frac{\mathds{1}-\hat{\mathrm{e}}_{\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}}\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma}}{2}\}
then completes the measurement of
L_{\varphi}
.
(b) Bell multiport
: The HOM in (a) can be seen as a special case of a Bell multiport with
N=2
, where the Hadamard transformation is now generalized to the discrete Fourier transform. For
N=3
, the measurement statistics (including coincidences) at (between) the various output ports allow us to implement the
\boldsymbol{J}^{2}
and
L_{\varphi}
measurement exactly; see Eqs. (
4.2
) and (
4.2
). For
N\geq 4
, however, Bell multiport cannot implement
\boldsymbol{J}^{2}
perfectly, as not all interference signatures for different
j
values are now distinct. Nevertheless, we suggest that the interference signatures for
j=N/2
and
j=N/2-1
will be distinct, which we can then make use of in the limit of nearly-pure qubit estimation, as most detection events in this case are contained in this two subspaces; see Sec.
3.3.2
.

A HOM interferometer, schematically depicted in Fig.
4
(a), consists of a 50:50 beam splitter (BS), with one qubit entering simultaneously from each of the input ports. The action of the BS can be summarized as a Hadamard transformation
\mathcal{U}
linking the input and output creation operators, i.e.,

\displaystyle\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}b^{\dagger}_{\footnotesize{\alpha},1}\\
b^{\dagger}_{\footnotesize{\alpha},2}\end{array}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{U}\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}a^{\dagger}_{\footnotesize{\alpha},1}\\
a^{\dagger}_{\footnotesize{\alpha},2}\end{array}\right)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}1&1\\
1&-1\end{array}\right)\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}a^{\dagger}_{\footnotesize{\alpha},1}\\
a^{\dagger}_{\footnotesize{\alpha},2}\end{array}\right),

where the operator
a^{\dagger}_{\footnotesize{\alpha},i}(b^{\dagger}_{\footnotesize{\alpha},i})
specifies the creation of a qubit with the state
\alpha=\{\textsc{h},\textsc{v}\}
at the input (output) port
i=\{1,2\}
. As usual, we have
\big[a^{\dagger}_{\footnotesize{\alpha},i},a^{\dagger}_{\footnotesize{\beta},j}\big]=\big[b^{\dagger}_{\footnotesize{\alpha},i},b^{\dagger}_{\footnotesize{\beta},j}\big]=0
for bosons, and
\big\{a^{\dagger}_{\footnotesize{\alpha},i},a^{\dagger}_{\footnotesize{\beta},j}\big\}=\big\{b^{\dagger}_{\footnotesize{\alpha},i},b^{\dagger}_{\footnotesize{\beta},j}\big\}=0
for fermions, which denies the possibility of having two fermions with same degrees of freedom at the same output. In this notation, we thus have
\rho_{N}(\varepsilon,\varphi)=\bigotimes_{i=1}^{N}\rho^{(i)}(\varepsilon,\varphi)
, with

\rho^{(i)}(\varepsilon,\varphi)=\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\sigma_{y}\varphi/2}\Big(\frac{1+s(\varepsilon)}{2}a^{\dagger}_{\footnotesize{\textsc{h}},i}{\left|{\textsc{vac}}\right\rangle}\!{\left\langle{\textsc{vac}}\right|}a_{\footnotesize{\textsc{h}},i}+\frac{1-s(\varepsilon)}{2}a^{\dagger}_{\footnotesize{\textsc{v}},i}{\left|{\textsc{vac}}\right\rangle}\!{\left\langle{\textsc{vac}}\right|}a_{\footnotesize{\textsc{v}},i}\Big)\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\sigma_{y}\varphi/2},

(45)

where
{\left|{\textsc{vac}}\right\rangle}
is the vacuum state,
a_{\footnotesize{\alpha},i}=(a^{\dagger}_{\footnotesize{\alpha},i})^{\dagger}
. One can then show that (see
B
for explicit demonstration with the bosonic case) we have the following unique signatures at the output ports:

\displaystyle j=1

\displaystyle:\quad\textrm{all two qubits exit at the same ports;}

\displaystyle j=0

\displaystyle:\quad\textrm{all two qubits exit at distinct ports,\quad\quad[bosons]}

(46)

or

\displaystyle j=1

\displaystyle:\quad\textrm{all two qubits exit at distinct ports;}

\displaystyle j=0

\displaystyle:\quad\textrm{all two qubits exit at the same ports.\quad[fermions]}

(47)

Note that these signatures are independent of the parameter
\varphi
, and hence information about it is preserved as it should. To then extract information about and estimate
\varphi
, we simply measure the projectors
\{\frac{\mathds{1}+\hat{\mathrm{e}}_{\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}}\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma}}{2},\frac{\mathds{1}-\hat{\mathrm{e}}_{\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}}\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma}}{2}\}
at the end of each of the output ports, which of course corresponds to measuring the eigenspaces of
L_{\varphi}
. Note that, number-resolving detection technique is needed here to capture all the signatures.

4.2
N=3

A natural question arises: Can we still implement the
\boldsymbol{J}^{2}
and
L_{\varphi}
measurement for the case of
N\geq 3
, using similar idea of observing different measurement statistics, including coincidence counts, at the various output ports? Here, we attempt to answer this question by considering a generalization of HOM interferometer to the
Bell multiport
setup
[
79
,
80
]
, as schematically depicted in Fig.
4
(b), and can generally be realized with combinations of beam splitters, phase shifters, and mirrors
[
81
]
. In this case, one qubit simultaneously enters from the each of the
N
input of the Bell multiport, whose action, in generalization to Eq. (
4.1
), is the discrete Fourier transform, i.e.,

\displaystyle b^{\dagger}_{\footnotesize{\alpha},k}=\sum_{\ell=1}^{N}\mathcal{U}_{k\ell}a^{\dagger}_{\footnotesize{\alpha},\ell},\quad\quad\mathcal{U}_{k\ell}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\mathrm{e}^{\frac{2\pi\mathrm{i}}{N}(k-1)(\ell-1)}.

(48)

We work out explicitly the case for
N=3
here, where the Bell multiport is also known as the Bell tritter
[
79
,
82
]
and has been realized experimentally for example in photonic systems
[
83
,
84
]
. Leaving the details to
C
, we find that the projection onto the
j
-eigenspace of
\boldsymbol{J}^{2}
has the following unique signatures at the output ports:

\displaystyle j=\frac{3}{2}

\displaystyle:\quad\textrm{all three qubits exit at the same ports \emph{or} distinct ports;}

\displaystyle j=\frac{1}{2}

\displaystyle:\quad\textrm{two qubits exit at the same ports,}

(49)

another at a different port,    [bosons]

or

\displaystyle j=\frac{3}{2}

\displaystyle:\quad\textrm{all three qubits exit at distinct ports;}

\displaystyle j=\frac{1}{2}

\displaystyle:\quad\textrm{two qubits exit at the same ports,}

(50)

another at a different port.     [fermions]

Thus, upon measuring the projectors
\{\frac{\mathds{1}+\hat{\mathrm{e}}_{\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}}\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma}}{2},\frac{\mathds{1}-\hat{\mathrm{e}}_{\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}}\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma}}{2}\}
at the outputs, all the outcome statistics indeed allow us to implement exact
\boldsymbol{J}^{2}
and
L_{\varphi}
measurements with a Bell tritter for
N=3
.

4.3
N\geq 4

For
N\geq 4
, exact realization of the measurement of
\boldsymbol{J}^{2}
basis using interference signatures of the “paths” in a passive, linear setup such as a HOM or Bell tritter, to the best knowledge of the author, is not available. For example, with the Bell multiport with
N=4
in Eq. (
48
), we do find that between the
j=2
and
j=1
eigenspaces the interference signatures are unique, but then there are no more room for another distinct signature for the
j=0
eigenspace. In fact, as computing the output probabilities for an arbitrarily
given
transformation
\mathcal{U}
on the creation operators set
\{a^{\dagger}_{\footnotesize{\alpha},\ell}\}
with
fixed

\alpha
is already hard
[
85
,
86
]
—a central identity in boson sampling
[
87
,
88
]
, finding a transformation
\mathcal{U}
that has output signatures that are different for each
j
-eigenspace, which now includes also inputs of different
\alpha
, is even harder as
N
gets larger.

Let us hence briefly switch to discuss the problem from a quantum computation perspective, where instead of just a passive setup, we allow the usage of ancillary qubits and control gates to realize the measurement of the common eigenbasis of
\boldsymbol{J}^{2}
and
L_{\varphi}
. Indeed then, upon identifying the
\boldsymbol{J}^{2}
(and
L_{\varphi}
common) eigenbasis as the Schur basis
[
89
]
—the union of bases for both irreducible representations of the globally-symmetric unitary group as well as the permutation group—the task of projection onto this common eigenbasis can be mapped onto the outcomes of a general Schur transformation circuit, for which efficient constructions have been developed
[
89
,
90
,
91
,
92
]
. In particular, the whole circuit can be implemented with
\Or(N^{3})
two-level gates (unitaries that act non-trivially on two dimensions), which can then be approximately executed with the universal and fault-tolerant set of Clifford and T gates
[
93
]
. Then, should each of these two-level gates can be approximately executed with up to at most
\Or(\gamma/N^{3})
error, the full Schur transformation circuit can be realized with an error
\gamma
using overall
\Or(N^{4}\log(N/\gamma))
universal gates, as well as
\Or(\log N)
ancilla
[
91
,
92
]
.

4.4
Nearly-pure qubit estimation: Approximate realization

Consider now the situation in Sec.
3.3.2
, i.e., nearly-pure qubit estimation. In particular, we stick with the parameterization of
s(\varepsilon)=1-\varepsilon^{2}/8,\varepsilon\ll 1
.

As we have discussed in Sec.
3.3.2
, in the limit of
\varepsilon\ll 1
and
N\varepsilon^{2}\ll 1
, the rare
j=N/2-1
detection events are the ones that contain most information about
\varepsilon
. We can see this from another angle, by writing Eq. (
13
) differently, namely, up to terms of
\Or(N\varepsilon^{2})
,

\displaystyle\rho_{N}(\varepsilon,\varphi)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}J_{y}\varphi}\Big(\frac{1+s(\varepsilon)}{2}{\left|{\textsc{h}}\right\rangle}\!{\left\langle{\textsc{h}}\right|}+\frac{1-s(\varepsilon)}{2}{\left|{\textsc{v}}\right\rangle}\!{\left\langle{\textsc{v}}\right|}\Big)^{\otimes N}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}J_{y}\varphi}

(51)

\displaystyle\approx\Big(1-\frac{N\varepsilon^{2}}{16}\Big)\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}J_{y}\varphi}{\left|{\textsc{h}\cdots\textsc{h}}\right\rangle}\!{\left\langle{\textsc{h}\cdots\textsc{h}}\right|}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}J_{y}\varphi}

\displaystyle\hphantom{=}+\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{16}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}J_{y}\varphi}\Big({\left|{\textsc{h}\cdots\textsc{h}\textsc{v}}\right\rangle}\!{\left\langle{\textsc{h}\cdots\textsc{h}\textsc{v}}\right|}+\textsf{permutations}\Big)\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}J_{y}\varphi}

\displaystyle\equiv w_{0}(\varepsilon)\tau_{0}(\varphi)+w_{1}(\varepsilon)\tau_{1}(\varphi),

where
w_{0}(\varepsilon)=1-\frac{N\varepsilon^{2}}{16},w_{1}(\varepsilon)=\frac{N\varepsilon^{2}}{16}
,
\tau_{0}(\varphi)=\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}J_{y}\varphi}{\left|{\textsc{h}\cdots\textsc{h}}\right\rangle}\!{\left\langle{\textsc{h}\cdots\textsc{h}}\right|}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}J_{y}\varphi}
, and
\tau_{1}(\varphi)=\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}J_{y}\varphi}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\tau_{1,i}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}J_{y}\varphi}/N
with
\tau_{1,i}={\left|{\textsc{v}}\right\rangle}\!{\left\langle{\textsc{v}}\right|}^{(i)}\bigotimes_{j(\neq i)}^{N}{\left|{\textsc{h}}\right\rangle}\!{\left\langle{\textsc{h}}\right|}^{(j)}
.

Ignoring differences beyond
\Or(N\varepsilon^{2})
with the exact
\rho_{N}(\varepsilon,\varphi)
, we may then consider the state as given by the last line of Eq. (
51
) instead, and suppose we send this state into the
N
-Bell multiport. Then, we verified explicitly up to
N=12
that the interference signatures for all the
\tau_{1,i}
are the same, and we denote this set of signatures by
\mathsf{S}_{1}
. Furthermore, our numerical results show that
\mathsf{S}_{1}
and the signature set for
\tau_{0}(\varphi)
,
\mathsf{S}_{0}
, are not exactly distinct, but they have an overlap, and the probability of having an interference signature from
\tau_{1,i}
inside this overlapping subset is given by
1/N
. The overall probability of observing
\mathsf{S}_{0}
is then

\displaystyle\mathsf{Pr}({\mathsf{S}_{0}})=w_{0}(\varepsilon)+w_{1}(\varepsilon)\frac{1}{N}=1-\frac{(N-1)\varepsilon^{2}}{16},

(52)

while the rest of signatures have probability

\displaystyle\mathsf{Pr}({\mathsf{S}_{1}\setminus\mathsf{S}_{0}})=w_{1}(\varepsilon)(1-\frac{1}{N})=\frac{(N-1)\varepsilon^{2}}{16}.

(53)

Over
\nu
repetitions then, should we obtain the relative frequencies
\nu_{0}
and
\nu_{1}=1-\nu_{0}
for observing signatures in
\mathsf{S}_{0}
and
\mathsf{S}_{1}\setminus\mathsf{S}_{0}
respectively, we may estimate
\varepsilon
by linear inversion estimator as defined by

\displaystyle\hat{\varepsilon}(D)=4\sqrt{\frac{\nu_{1}}{N-1}}=\sqrt{\frac{8(1+\nu_{1}-\nu_{0})}{N-1}},

(54)

which in this case is always physical, and is unbiased
for the approximate state
at last line of Eq. (
51
). As a reminder to the reader, by obtaining
\nu_{0}
and
\nu_{1}
and then estimating
\varepsilon
, we have not disturbed any information about
\varphi
which we shall extract from
L_{\varphi}
measurement, as the interference signatures are invariant under an overall and equal transformation on all the qubits. In Fig.
5
, we present a numerical simulation for the approximate estimation of different true values of
\varepsilon
with the state
\rho_{N}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
as given by Eq. (
13
) (not the approximate state) with
N=4,5,
and 6, via the linear estimator (
54
). As
N
increases, we see that the biases in the estimator grow, which is unavoidable in this case, as now the number of angular momentum modes increases, and their contributions to both the set
\mathsf{S}_{0}
and
\mathsf{S}_{0}\setminus\mathsf{S}_{1}
increases, making the linear estimator less accurate. Evidently, this effect increases as
\varepsilon
increases as well.

It turns out that the probability of getting the signature sets
\mathsf{S}_{0}
and
\mathsf{S}_{1}\setminus\mathsf{S}_{0}
are respectively equal to
p_{N/2}
and

, see Eqs. (

) and (

) versus Eqs. (
27

). Is this correspondence accidental? Evidently,
{\left|{\textsc{h}\cdots\textsc{h}}\right\rangle}={\left|{j=\frac{N}{2},m_{z}=\frac{N}{2}}\right\rangle}
, and so
\tau_{0}(\varphi)=\mathcal{P}_{\frac{N}{2}}\tau_{0}(\varphi)\mathcal{P}_{\frac{N}{2}}
, such that
\mathrm{Tr}\{\mathcal{P}_{\frac{N}{2}}\tau_{0}(\varphi)\}=1
. Meanwhile, we have
\tau_{1}(\varphi)=(\mathcal{P}_{\frac{N}{2}}+\mathcal{P}_{\frac{N}{2}-1})\tau_{1}(\varphi)(\mathcal{P}_{\frac{N}{2}}+\mathcal{P}_{\frac{N}{2}-1})
, and one can show easily that
\mathrm{Tr}\{\mathcal{P}_{\frac{N}{2}}\tau_{1}(\varphi)\}=\frac{1}{N}
. Hence, just like
\mathsf{Pr}({\mathsf{S}_{0}})
and
\mathsf{Pr}({\mathsf{S}_{1}\setminus\mathsf{S}_{0}})
, we have
p_{N/2}\approx w_{0}(\varepsilon)+w_{1}(\varepsilon)(1/N)=1-(N-1)\varepsilon^{2}/16
and
p_{N/2-1}\approx w_{1}(\varepsilon)(1-1/N)=(N-1)\varepsilon^{2}/16
, indeed. It is, therefore, inviting for us to associate the
1/N
overlap between
\mathsf{S}_{1}
and
\mathsf{S}_{0}
as originated from the
1/N
fraction of
\tau_{1}(\varphi)
in the
\mathcal{P}_{\frac{N}{2}}
subspace, and therefore identify
\mathsf{S}_{0}
as the signature set for
j=N/2
, while
\mathsf{S}_{1}\setminus\mathsf{S}_{0}
as the signature set for
j=N/2-1
.

We take note that
\mathsf{S}_{1}
has been so far not obtained from any arbitrary states in the
j=N/2-1
subspace, but only those with
m_{z}=N/2-1
via the states
\tau_{1,i}={\left|{\textsc{v}}\right\rangle}\!{\left\langle{\textsc{v}}\right|}^{(i)}\bigotimes_{j(\neq i)}^{N}{\left|{\textsc{h}}\right\rangle}\!{\left\langle{\textsc{h}}\right|}^{(j)}
,
i=1,\cdots,N
. Similarly,
\mathsf{S}_{0}
is obtained from considering just
\tau_{0}
, which is a particular state in the
j=N/2
subspace with the specific value of
m_{z}=N/2
. To further confirm our assertion that
\mathsf{S}_{0}
and
\mathsf{S}_{1}\setminus\mathsf{S}_{0}
do unambiguously distinguish between the projection onto the two

-subspaces respectively, we check explicitly the signatures for

the eigenstates,
{\left|{j,m_{z},g(j)}\right\rangle}
, in each

-eigenspaces for

and

up to

have signatures in
\mathsf{S}_{1}\setminus\mathsf{S}_{0}
. We then end this section by suggesting the following from our numerical observations:

Given
N
spin-1/2s with one spin entering simultaneously from each of

the input ports of an
N
-Bell multiport, the interference signatures for

the

Put differently, we propose that an

-Bell multiport allows us to distinguish between projection onto the

5
Discussion

Ideally, we would like to have a passive implementation of the
\boldsymbol{J}^{2}
measurement for any valid range of
\varepsilon
and
N
. Unfortunately however, we only manage to do so for
N=2,3
with Bell multiport, and at the moment be content with approximate realizations in the nearly-pure qubit regime, where projection onto the
j=N/2
and
N/2-1
subspaces is sufficient. To proceed further, instead of Bell multiport with the corresponding discrete Fourier transform then, one may perhaps consider more general complex Hadamard transform
[
94
]
. Of course, more generally, measuring
\boldsymbol{J}^{2}
and
L_{\varphi}
need not be the only scheme that achieves the multi-parameter QFI CRB; there might be some other schemes as well, with different implementation method altogether. We demonstrated one such scheme here and will leave these other possibilities open for now.

Regarding the interference signatures in
\mathsf{S}_{0}
and
\mathsf{S}_{1}
with
N
-Bell multiport in the above section, we also observe an interesting feature for the case of bosonic qubits. With
N
bosons and
N
outputs in the Bell multiport, there are in total
|\textsf{S}|:={\left(\begin{array}[]{@{}c@{}}2N-1\\
N\end{array}\right)}
different signatures possible. Then, we find that apart from some specific
N
, the total number of signatures in
\mathsf{S}_{1}
,
|\mathsf{S}_{1}|
, is exactly
|\textsf{S}|
, which again confirms that there are no room for other
j\neq N/2
values to have distinguishable signature set. Up to
N=12
as we have verified, the few exceptional
N
values for which
|\mathsf{S}_{1}|\neq|\textsf{S}|
are found to be
N=6,10,12
, with
|\mathsf{S}_{1}|/|\textsf{S}|=\frac{449}{462},\frac{90358}{92378},\frac{1321717}{1352078}
respectively. Incidentally,
N=6,10,12
are the first three elements in the sequence of numbers which are not integer power of a prime number, and it is a well known open problem in mathematical physics and quantum information whether there exists
N+1
mutually unbiased bases (MUB) for Hilbert space with dimension of such numbers
[
68
,
95
]
. Given that one of the important tools in studying MUB is the complex Hadamard matrices for which discrete Fourier transform is one of such
[
94
,
68
]
, it is then perhaps not surprising to have such a correspondence between the non-unity ratio of
|\mathsf{S}_{1}|/|\textsf{S}|
and the MUB problem. However, exactly how or why they are related would require a separate analysis, and is beyond the scope of this work.

Finally, as we have put forward in Sec.
3.1
, the qubit model we studied in this work can be seen as a particular case of quantum metrology or quantum channel estimation problem, in which we would like to estimate the phase parameter in an unitary encoding channel as well as the dephasing parameter in the dephasing channel, using the

More generally, one may consider entangled input states (such as one- or two axis-spin squeeze states) and general measurement strategies instead, and obtain better estimation precisions for the two parameters. Then, one may show that, when assuming one parameter is known and we estimate only the second parameter, their optimal attainable MSE in the large

\displaystyle\Delta^{2}\hat{\varepsilon}_{N,\mathrm{met}}

6
Conclusion

In summary, in this work, we have studied the joint estimation of the length parameter
\varepsilon
and the direction parameter
\varphi
of the Bloch vector for qubit states, a model which has relevance to various physical problems, e.g., superresolution quantum imaging. We show that these two parameters can be simultaneously estimated with optimal precisions asymptotically, using the collective measurement of the angular-momentum squared operator, followed by a local projective measurement. We also discuss how such measurement scheme can be realized using Bell multiport, either exactly for
N=2,3
, or approximately when the qubits are nearly pure for other
N
values. Finally, we propose that with a Bell multiport setup, the interference signatures in the
j=N/2
and
j=N/2-1
angular-momentum spaces will be distinct.

Appendix A
Precision of estimation for
\varepsilon
with
\boldsymbol{J}^{2}

In order to help the readers verify the expression in Eq. (
21
), we list down explicitly here the various constituent terms. With
\rho_{N}(\varepsilon,\varphi)
as in Eq. (
13
), and the notation
{\left\langle{A}\right\rangle}\equiv\mathrm{Tr}\{\rho_{N}(\varepsilon,\varphi)A\}
for the operator
A
, we have, by a simple rotation, or equivalently a re-definition of the
(x,y,z)
-directions,

\displaystyle{\left\langle{J_{x}^{2}}\right\rangle}

\displaystyle={\left\langle{J_{y}^{2}}\right\rangle}=\frac{N}{4},\qquad{\left\langle{J_{z}^{2}}\right\rangle}=\frac{N}{4}+\frac{N(N-1)}{4}s(\varepsilon)^{2},

(58)

such that

\displaystyle{\left\langle{\boldsymbol{J}^{2}}\right\rangle}

\displaystyle={\left\langle{J_{x}^{2}+J_{y}^{2}+J_{z}^{2}}\right\rangle}=\frac{3N}{4}+\frac{N(N-1)}{4}s(\varepsilon)^{2},

(59)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial\varepsilon}{\left\langle{\boldsymbol{J}^{2}}\right\rangle}

\displaystyle=\frac{N(N-1)}{2}s(\varepsilon)\frac{\partial s(\varepsilon)}{\partial\varepsilon},

(60)

and

\displaystyle{\left\langle{J_{x}^{4}}\right\rangle}

\displaystyle={\left\langle{J_{y}^{4}}\right\rangle}=\frac{N(3N-2)}{16},

\displaystyle{\left\langle{J_{x}^{2}J_{y}^{2}}\right\rangle}

\displaystyle={\left\langle{J_{y}^{2}J_{x}^{2}}\right\rangle}=\frac{N}{16}\Big(N-2(N-1)s(\varepsilon)^{2}\Big),

\displaystyle{\left\langle{J_{x}^{2}J_{z}^{2}}\right\rangle}

\displaystyle={\left\langle{J_{z}^{2}J_{x}^{2}}\right\rangle}={\left\langle{J_{z}^{2}J_{y}^{2}}\right\rangle}={\left\langle{J_{y}^{2}J_{z}^{2}}\right\rangle}=\frac{N^{2}}{16}\Big(1+(N-1)s(\varepsilon)^{2}\Big),

\displaystyle{\left\langle{J_{z}^{4}}\right\rangle}=

\displaystyle\Big(\frac{N^{4}}{16}-\frac{3N^{3}}{8}+\frac{11N^{2}}{16}-\frac{3N}{8}\Big)s(\varepsilon)^{4}

(61)

\displaystyle+\Big(\frac{3N^{3}}{8}-\frac{7N^{2}}{8}+\frac{N}{2}\Big)s(\varepsilon)^{2}-\frac{3N^{2}}{16}-\frac{N}{8},

such that

\displaystyle{\left\langle{(\boldsymbol{J}^{2})^{2}}\right\rangle}={\left\langle{J_{x}^{4}+J_{y}^{4}+J_{z}^{4}+J_{x}^{2}J_{y}^{2}+J_{y}^{2}J_{x}^{2}+J_{x}^{2}J_{z}^{2}+J_{z}^{2}J_{x}^{2}+J_{y}^{2}J_{z}^{2}+J_{z}^{2}J_{y}^{2}}\right\rangle}

\displaystyle=\Big(\frac{N^{4}}{16}-\frac{3N^{3}}{8}+\frac{11N^{2}}{16}-\frac{3N}{8}\Big)s(\varepsilon)^{4}+\frac{N(N-1)(5N-6)}{8}s(\varepsilon)^{2}-\frac{3N(5N-2)}{16}.

(62)

Finally then,

\displaystyle{\left\langle{(\boldsymbol{J}^{2})^{2}}\right\rangle}-{\left\langle{\boldsymbol{J}^{2}}\right\rangle}^{2}=\frac{N(N-1)}{8}\Big[(3-2N)s(\varepsilon)^{4}+2(N-3)s(\varepsilon)^{2}+3\Big],

(63)

and the MSE of estimating
\varepsilon
in Eq. (
21
) now follows immediately from Eq. (
60
) and Eq. (
63
).

Appendix B
Transformation of
\boldsymbol{J}^{2}
eigenstates under HOM

For
N=2
bosonic qubits, the four
\boldsymbol{J}^{2}
eigenstates at the input can be chosen as

\displaystyle{\left|{j=1,m_{z}=1}\right\rangle}

\displaystyle=a^{\dagger}_{\footnotesize{\textsc{h}},1}a^{\dagger}_{\footnotesize{\textsc{h}},2}{\left|{\textsc{vac}}\right\rangle},

\displaystyle{\left|{j=1,m_{z}=0}\right\rangle}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(a^{\dagger}_{\footnotesize{\textsc{h}},1}a^{\dagger}_{\footnotesize{\textsc{v}},2}+a^{\dagger}_{\footnotesize{\textsc{v}},1}a^{\dagger}_{\footnotesize{\textsc{h}},2}){\left|{\textsc{vac}}\right\rangle},

\displaystyle{\left|{j=1,m_{z}=-1}\right\rangle}

\displaystyle=a^{\dagger}_{\footnotesize{\textsc{v}},1}a^{\dagger}_{\footnotesize{\textsc{v}},2}{\left|{\textsc{vac}}\right\rangle},

\displaystyle{\left|{j=0,m_{z}=0}\right\rangle}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(a^{\dagger}_{\footnotesize{\textsc{h}},1}a^{\dagger}_{\footnotesize{\textsc{v}},2}-a^{\dagger}_{\footnotesize{\textsc{v}},1}a^{\dagger}_{\footnotesize{\textsc{h}},2}){\left|{\textsc{vac}}\right\rangle}.

(64)

After traversing the HOM interferometer, these states are respectively transformed as

\displaystyle{\left|{j=1,m_{z}=1}\right\rangle}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left((b^{\dagger}_{\footnotesize{\textsc{h}},1})^{2}-(b^{\dagger}_{\footnotesize{\textsc{h}},2})^{2}\right){\left|{\textsc{vac}}\right\rangle},

\displaystyle{\left|{j=1,m_{z}=0}\right\rangle}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(b^{\dagger}_{\footnotesize{\textsc{h}},1}b^{\dagger}_{\footnotesize{\textsc{v}},1}-b^{\dagger}_{\footnotesize{\textsc{h}},2}b^{\dagger}_{\footnotesize{\textsc{v}},2}){\left|{\textsc{vac}}\right\rangle},

\displaystyle{\left|{j=1,m_{z}=-1}\right\rangle}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left((b^{\dagger}_{\footnotesize{\textsc{v}},1})^{2}-(b^{\dagger}_{\footnotesize{\textsc{v}},2})^{2}\right){\left|{\textsc{vac}}\right\rangle},

\displaystyle{\left|{j=0,m_{z}=0}\right\rangle}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(b^{\dagger}_{\footnotesize{\textsc{v}},1}b^{\dagger}_{\footnotesize{\textsc{h}},2}-b^{\dagger}_{\footnotesize{\textsc{h}},1}b^{\dagger}_{\footnotesize{\textsc{v}},2}){\left|{\textsc{vac}}\right\rangle}.

(65)

From Eq. (
B
), we thus see that the projection onto the respective
j
-eigenspace of
\boldsymbol{J}^{2}
can be identified with the unique signatures as stated in Eq. (
4.1
). Similar analysis can be done for fermions to obtain Eq. (
4.1
).

Appendix C
Transformation of
\boldsymbol{J}^{2}
eigenstates under Bell tritter

For
N=3
bosonic qubits, a choice of the
j=\frac{3}{2}
-eigenstates for
\boldsymbol{J}^{2}
at the input is

\displaystyle{\left|{j=\frac{3}{2},m_{z}=\frac{3}{2}}\right\rangle}

\displaystyle=a^{\dagger}_{\footnotesize{\textsc{h}},1}a^{\dagger}_{\footnotesize{\textsc{h}},2}a^{\dagger}_{\footnotesize{\textsc{h}},3}{\left|{\textsc{vac}}\right\rangle},

\displaystyle{\left|{j=\frac{3}{2},m_{z}=\frac{1}{2}}\right\rangle}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}(a^{\dagger}_{\footnotesize{\textsc{h}},1}a^{\dagger}_{\footnotesize{\textsc{h}},2}a^{\dagger}_{\footnotesize{\textsc{v}},3}+a^{\dagger}_{\footnotesize{\textsc{h}},1}a^{\dagger}_{\footnotesize{\textsc{v}},2}a^{\dagger}_{\footnotesize{\textsc{h}},3}+a^{\dagger}_{\footnotesize{\textsc{v}},1}a^{\dagger}_{\footnotesize{\textsc{h}},2}a^{\dagger}_{\footnotesize{\textsc{h}},3}){\left|{\textsc{vac}}\right\rangle},

\displaystyle{\left|{j=\frac{3}{2},m_{z}=\frac{-1}{2}}\right\rangle}

\displaystyle={\left|{j=\frac{3}{2},m_{z}=\frac{1}{2}}\right\rangle}\Bigg|_{\textsc{h}\leftrightarrow\textsc{v}},

\displaystyle{\left|{j=\frac{3}{2},m_{z}=\frac{-3}{2}}\right\rangle}

\displaystyle={\left|{j=\frac{3}{2},m_{z}=\frac{3}{2}}\right\rangle}\Bigg|_{\textsc{h}\leftrightarrow\textsc{v}}.

(66)

We focus on the
m_{z}=\frac{3}{2}
and
m_{z}=\frac{1}{2}
kets, as the other two are equivalent to them up to interchanging
\textsc{h}\leftrightarrow\textsc{v}
, and thus will have the same interference signatures by a Bell tritter. Straightforward algebra then leads us to

\displaystyle{\left|{j=\frac{3}{2},m_{z}=\frac{3}{2}}\right\rangle}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{3\sqrt{3}}\Big((b^{\dagger}_{\footnotesize{\textsc{h}},1})^{3}+(b^{\dagger}_{\footnotesize{\textsc{h}},2})^{3}+(b^{\dagger}_{\footnotesize{\textsc{h}},3})^{3}-3b^{\dagger}_{\footnotesize{\textsc{h}},1}b^{\dagger}_{\footnotesize{\textsc{h}},2}b^{\dagger}_{\footnotesize{\textsc{h}},3}\Big){\left|{\textsc{vac}}\right\rangle},

\displaystyle{\left|{j=\frac{3}{2},m_{z}=\frac{1}{2}}\right\rangle}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{3}\Big((b^{\dagger}_{\footnotesize{\textsc{h}},1})^{2}b^{\dagger}_{\footnotesize{\textsc{v}},1}+(b^{\dagger}_{\footnotesize{\textsc{h}},2})^{2}b^{\dagger}_{\footnotesize{\textsc{v}},2}+(b^{\dagger}_{\footnotesize{\textsc{h}},3})^{2}b^{\dagger}_{\footnotesize{\textsc{v}},3}

(67)

\displaystyle-b^{\dagger}_{\footnotesize{\textsc{h}},1}b^{\dagger}_{\footnotesize{\textsc{h}},2}b^{\dagger}_{\footnotesize{\textsc{v}},3}-b^{\dagger}_{\footnotesize{\textsc{h}},1}b^{\dagger}_{\footnotesize{\textsc{v}},2}b^{\dagger}_{\footnotesize{\textsc{h}},3}-b^{\dagger}_{\footnotesize{\textsc{v}},1}b^{\dagger}_{\footnotesize{\textsc{h}},2}b^{\dagger}_{\footnotesize{\textsc{h}},3}\Big){\left|{\textsc{vac}}\right\rangle}.

We thus see that the signatures for
j=\frac{3}{2}
after traversing the Bell tritter are either all qubits exit at the same output ports, or all exit at different output ports.

For
j=\frac{1}{2}
, there are
\mu_{j}=2
subspaces. The first one for example has the eigenkets

\displaystyle{\left|{j=\frac{1}{2},m_{z}=\frac{1}{2},g(j)=1}\right\rangle}

\displaystyle=\Big(\frac{-1}{\sqrt{6}}(a^{\dagger}_{\footnotesize{\textsc{h}},1}a^{\dagger}_{\footnotesize{\textsc{v}},2}+a^{\dagger}_{\footnotesize{\textsc{v}},1}a^{\dagger}_{\footnotesize{\textsc{h}},2})a^{\dagger}_{\footnotesize{\textsc{h}},3}+\sqrt{\frac{2}{3}}a^{\dagger}_{\footnotesize{\textsc{h}},1}a^{\dagger}_{\footnotesize{\textsc{h}},2}a^{\dagger}_{\footnotesize{\textsc{v}},3}\Big){\left|{\textsc{vac}}\right\rangle},

\displaystyle{\left|{j=\frac{1}{2},m_{z}=\frac{-1}{2},g(j)=1}\right\rangle}

\displaystyle=-{\left|{j=\frac{1}{2},m_{z}=\frac{1}{2},g(j)=1}\right\rangle}\Bigg|_{\textsc{h}\leftrightarrow\textsc{v}}.

(68)

The
m_{z}=\frac{1}{2}
state, upon introducing
u=\mathrm{e}^{2\pi\mathrm{i}/3}
, is also

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{18}}\Big(u(b^{\dagger}_{\footnotesize{\textsc{h}},1})^{2}b^{\dagger}_{\footnotesize{\textsc{v}},2}+u^{*}(b^{\dagger}_{\footnotesize{\textsc{h}},1})^{2}b^{\dagger}_{\footnotesize{\textsc{v}},3}+u^{*}(b^{\dagger}_{\footnotesize{\textsc{h}},2})^{2}b^{\dagger}_{\footnotesize{\textsc{v}},1}+u(b^{\dagger}_{\footnotesize{\textsc{h}},2})^{2}b^{\dagger}_{\footnotesize{\textsc{v}},3}+u(b^{\dagger}_{\footnotesize{\textsc{h}},3})^{2}b^{\dagger}_{\footnotesize{\textsc{v}},1}

\displaystyle+u^{*}(b^{\dagger}_{\footnotesize{\textsc{h}},3})^{2}b^{\dagger}_{\footnotesize{\textsc{v}},2}-ub^{\dagger}_{\footnotesize{\textsc{h}},1}b^{\dagger}_{\footnotesize{\textsc{v}},1}b^{\dagger}_{\footnotesize{\textsc{h}},2}-u^{*}b^{\dagger}_{\footnotesize{\textsc{h}},1}b^{\dagger}_{\footnotesize{\textsc{v}},1}b^{\dagger}_{\footnotesize{\textsc{h}},3}-u^{*}b^{\dagger}_{\footnotesize{\textsc{h}},2}b^{\dagger}_{\footnotesize{\textsc{v}},2}b^{\dagger}_{\footnotesize{\textsc{h}},1}-ub^{\dagger}_{\footnotesize{\textsc{h}},2}b^{\dagger}_{\footnotesize{\textsc{v}},2}b^{\dagger}_{\footnotesize{\textsc{h}},3}

\displaystyle-ub^{\dagger}_{\footnotesize{\textsc{h}},3}b^{\dagger}_{\footnotesize{\textsc{v}},3}b^{\dagger}_{\footnotesize{\textsc{h}},1}-u^{*}b^{\dagger}_{\footnotesize{\textsc{h}},3}b^{\dagger}_{\footnotesize{\textsc{v}},3}b^{\dagger}_{\footnotesize{\textsc{h}},2}\Big){\left|{\textsc{vac}}\right\rangle},

(69)

which has the signature of two qubits exit at the same output ports, and one at another. The same conclusion holds for the
m_{z}=-\frac{1}{2}
state.

Lastly, for the eigenkets in the second
j=\frac{1}{2}
-subspace, we have

\displaystyle{\left|{j=\frac{1}{2},m_{z}=\frac{1}{2},g(j)=2}\right\rangle}

\displaystyle=\Big(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(a^{\dagger}_{\footnotesize{\textsc{h}},1}a^{\dagger}_{\footnotesize{\textsc{h}},2}-a^{\dagger}_{\footnotesize{\textsc{v}},1}a^{\dagger}_{\footnotesize{\textsc{h}},2})a^{\dagger}_{\footnotesize{\textsc{h}},3}\Big){\left|{\textsc{vac}}\right\rangle},

\displaystyle{\left|{j=\frac{1}{2},m_{z}=\frac{-1}{2},g(j)=2}\right\rangle}

\displaystyle=-{\left|{j=\frac{1}{2},m_{z}=\frac{1}{2},g(j)=2}\right\rangle}\Bigg|_{\textsc{h}\leftrightarrow\textsc{v}}.

(70)

The
m_{z}=\frac{1}{2}
state is also

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{i}}{\sqrt{18}}\Big(u(b^{\dagger}_{\footnotesize{\textsc{h}},1})^{2}b^{\dagger}_{\footnotesize{\textsc{v}},2}-u^{*}(b^{\dagger}_{\footnotesize{\textsc{h}},1})^{2}b^{\dagger}_{\footnotesize{\textsc{v}},3}-u^{*}(b^{\dagger}_{\footnotesize{\textsc{h}},2})^{2}b^{\dagger}_{\footnotesize{\textsc{v}},1}+u(b^{\dagger}_{\footnotesize{\textsc{h}},2})^{2}b^{\dagger}_{\footnotesize{\textsc{v}},3}+u(b^{\dagger}_{\footnotesize{\textsc{h}},3})^{2}b^{\dagger}_{\footnotesize{\textsc{v}},1}

\displaystyle-u^{*}(b^{\dagger}_{\footnotesize{\textsc{h}},3})^{2}b^{\dagger}_{\footnotesize{\textsc{v}},2}-ub^{\dagger}_{\footnotesize{\textsc{h}},1}b^{\dagger}_{\footnotesize{\textsc{v}},1}b^{\dagger}_{\footnotesize{\textsc{h}},2}+u^{*}b^{\dagger}_{\footnotesize{\textsc{h}},1}b^{\dagger}_{\footnotesize{\textsc{v}},1}b^{\dagger}_{\footnotesize{\textsc{h}},3}+u^{*}b^{\dagger}_{\footnotesize{\textsc{h}},2}b^{\dagger}_{\footnotesize{\textsc{v}},2}b^{\dagger}_{\footnotesize{\textsc{h}},1}-ub^{\dagger}_{\footnotesize{\textsc{h}},2}b^{\dagger}_{\footnotesize{\textsc{v}},2}b^{\dagger}_{\footnotesize{\textsc{h}},3}

\displaystyle-ub^{\dagger}_{\footnotesize{\textsc{h}},3}b^{\dagger}_{\footnotesize{\textsc{v}},3}b^{\dagger}_{\footnotesize{\textsc{h}},1}+u^{*}b^{\dagger}_{\footnotesize{\textsc{h}},3}b^{\dagger}_{\footnotesize{\textsc{v}},3}b^{\dagger}_{\footnotesize{\textsc{h}},2}\Big){\left|{\textsc{vac}}\right\rangle},

(71)

which again has the signature of two qubits exiting at the same output ports, and one at another, and the same conclusion holds for the
m_{z}=-\frac{1}{2}
state. Therefore, overall, the projections onto
j=\frac{1}{2}
subspaces all have the same interference signature, which is perfectly distinguishable from the signatures associated with the
j=\frac{3}{2}
subspace as summarized in Eq. (
4.2
). Similar analysis can be done for fermions to obtain Eq. (
4.2
).
