Error Estimators for the Small-Biot Lumped Approximation for the Conduction Dunking Problem
Kento Kaneko, Claude Le Bris, Anthony T Patera

Abstract
We consider the dunking problem: a solid body at uniform temperature T i T_{\text{i}} is placed in a environment characterized by farfield temperature T ∞ T_{\infty} and spatially uniform time-independent heat transfer coefficient. We permit heterogeneous material composition: spatially dependent density, specific heat, and thermal conductivity. Mathematically, the problem is described by a heat equation with Robin boundary conditions. The crucial parameter is the Biot number — a nondimensional heat transfer (Robin) coefficient; we consider the limit of small Biot number. We introduce first-order and second-order asymptotic approximations (in Biot number) for several quantities of interest, notably the spatial domain average temperature as a function of time; the first-order approximation is simply the standard engineering ‘lumped’ model. We then provide asymptotic error estimates for the first-order and second-order approximations for small Biot number, and also, for the first-order approximation, alternative strict bounds valid for all Biot number. Companion numerical solutions of the heat equation confirm the effectiveness of the error estimates for small Biot number. The second-order approximation and the first-order and second-order error estimates depend on several functional outputs associated to an elliptic partial differential equation; the latter is derived from Biot-sensitivity analysis of the heat equation eigenproblem in the limit of small Biot number. Most important is ϕ \phi , the only functional output required for the first-order error estimates; ϕ \phi admits a simple physical interpretation in terms of conduction length scale. We investigate the domain and property dependence of ϕ \phi : most notably, we characterize spatial domains for which the standard lumped-model error criterion — Biot number (based on volume-to-area length scale) small — is deficient.

2.2
Eigenproblem

We can associate to our initial value problem, in standard fashion, the corresponding eigenproblem: for given

\displaystyle-\boldsymbol{\nabla}\cdot(\kappa\boldsymbol{\nabla}\psi_{j})=\lambda_{j}\sigma\psi_{j}\text{ in }\Omega\,,

(14)

\displaystyle\kappa\partial_{n}\psi_{j}+B\psi_{j}=0\text{ on }\partial\Omega\,,

(15)

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\sigma\psi^{2}_{j}=1\;.

(16)

The corresponding weak form is provided in Appendix

\psi_{j}(B)
to indicate the spatial eigenfunction for any given Biot number

{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\mathscr{R}_{\text{eq}}}}=\phi{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\left[\mathscr{L}/(k_{\inf}|\partial\Omega|)\right]}}\left[1+(\text{Bi}^{\prime}_{\text{dunk}})^{-1}\right]

We now turn to the time-dependent problem. We associate to the
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\hat{T}_{\text{avg}}}}
node a heat capacitance
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{(\overline{\rho c})|\Omega|}}
which then directly yields

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{(\overline{\rho c})|\Omega|\partial_{t}\hat{T}_{\text{avg}}=-\dfrac{\hat{T}_{\text{avg}}-\hat{T}_{\infty}}{\mathscr{R}_{\text{eq}}}}}.

(66)

We now define

and nondimensionalize (

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\hat{u}^{*}_{\text{avg}}=-\dfrac{B\gamma}{1+\phi B/\gamma}\hat{u}^{*},\quad t>0

(67)

subject to

from our uniform initial condition. We can also identify the eigenproblem associated with (

\displaystyle\dfrac{B\gamma}{1+\phi B/\gamma}\hat{\psi}=\hat{\lambda}\hat{\psi},

(68)

where

4.3
Lumped Approximations

We begin with the eigenproblem in order to arrive at the lumped approximation. We directly identify from (
68
) our first-order approximation — neglecting the
\pazocal{O}(B^{2})
contributions — as
\tilde{\lambda}^{1}=B\gamma
, and our second-order approximation — including now the
\pazocal{O}(B^{2})
terms — as
\tilde{\lambda}^{2\text{P}}=\frac{B\gamma}{1+\phi B/\gamma}
. We have already presented and analyzed these approximations in Proposition
3.4
, respectively. We can now also include a physical interpretation in particular for the second-order approximation: for larger
\phi
, hence larger conduction length scale, the heat transfer coefficient resistance is relatively smaller compared to the conduction (average) resistance, and thus
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\hat{T}_{{\partial{\text{avg}}}}}}
is closer to
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{T_{\infty}}}
; thus the heat transfer rate to the environment,
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{h|\partial\Omega|(T_{\text{avg}}-T_{\infty})}}/(1+\text{Bi}^{\prime}_{\text{dunk}})
is reduced; and hence the time constant —
1/\tilde{\lambda}^{2\text{P}}
— is increased.

Turning now to the initial value problem, we can directly identify from (
67
) our first-order approximation for the domain average — neglecting the
\pazocal{O}(B^{2})
contributions — as
\tilde{u}_{\text{avg}}^{1}\equiv\exp(-B\gamma t)

\pazocal{O}(B^{2})
terms — as
\tilde{u}_{\text{avg}}^{2}\equiv\exp\left(-\frac{B\gamma t}{1+\phi B/\gamma}\right)
; note again that larger
\phi

\tilde{u}_{\Delta}^{2}\equiv\frac{\phi B}{\gamma}
(independent of time) — we use superscript

5.3
Foundations for Alternative Evaluation Strategies

As discussed in the introduction, our lumped approximation, a simple exponential, has many attractive features: transparency — explicit parameter dependence; flexibility — ready incorporation into design or optimization or system analysis; rapid evaluation — just a few standard elementary function evaluations.

However, our error estimates shall require the new quantity
\phi
: we already observe in Proposition
3.3
that the relative error estimate for the first-order lumped approximation to the first eigenvalue is given by
\text{Bi}^{\prime}_{\text{dunk}}(\equiv\phi\,\text{Bi}_{\text{dunk}})
; we shall similarly observe in the Section
6
that the error in the first-order lumped approximation to
u_{\mathrm{avg}}
is similarly given by
\text{Bi}^{\prime}_{\text{dunk}}/e
(Proposition
6.5
). We now understand that
\phi
may, in fact, be large — and hence the implicit engineering assumption (say,
\phi=1
) can not, in general, be trusted:
\phi
must be evaluated. We further know that, in general,
\phi
cannot be obtained in closed form except for a few simple geometries. We conclude that we must, explicitly or implicitly, resort to computational treatment of the partial differential equation (
129
) for
\psi^{\prime 0}
in order to evaluate
\phi
from (
130
). Do we thus lose the advantages of the lumped approximation? In the next paragraphs we show that, in fact, we do not lose these advantages.

We first note that although the set of geometries amenable to closed-form evaluation of
\phi
(Table
1
, Proposition
5.6
) is small, these geometries are of practical interest as they often arise in both engineered and natural systems, and furthermore, these geometries can serve to approximate more general shapes. We also emphasize that this set of geometries is further enriched by Proposition
5.7
, providing closed-form
\phi
expressions for finite circular cylinder, orthogonal parallelepiped, and certain triangular prisms.

However, complex geometries not amenable to simple approximations often arise in engineering applications. In practice, we do not lose the advantages of the lumped approximation: a sufficiently accurate approximation to our single elliptic partial differential equation can be very rapidly obtained by adaptive finite element methods (see Section
5.4
); furthermore, more recent software advances, in particular containers and microservices, permit cloud-based deployment to virtually any user — with no issues of licenses or installation or compilation or access. Nevertheless, the lumped approximation requires only the volume and surface area of the body, whereas our partial differential equation requires a full geometry (and material composition) description — often an inconvenience. We may thus wish to supplement direct evaluation of
\phi
with a more convenient alternative strategy in which
\phi
is replaced by an inexpensive surrogate.

As regards geometry, there are two options. In both cases, we are given the desired domain,
\Omega
, and a dictionary of
(\Omega,\phi)^{k}
pairs. In the first option, we estimate
\phi
as
\phi^{k}
for the element
k
of the dictionary for which
\text{dist}(\Omega,\Omega^{k})
(with suitable registration) is smallest. In the second option, we identify a few features which permit approximate separation of our dictionary entries for
\phi
smaller and
\phi
larger; in the offline stage we train the classifier, and then in the online stage we query the classifier for any given desired domain,
\Omega
. We are thus presented with two mathematical questions: (for the first option) How should we choose
\mathrm{dist}
, in particular to provide Lipschitz continuity for
\phi
—
\mathrm{dist}(\Omega^{i},\Omega^{j})
small implies
|\phi^{i}-\phi^{j}|
small
[
13
]
?; (for the second option) How should we choose our features to provide adequate separation between the
\phi
smaller and
\phi
larger subsets of the dictionary (or, more generally, training set)?

We cannot yet provide rigorous answers. However, we have conjectures which we will then support (in the next section) with computational evidence. A subsequent manuscript will elaborate on the material presented here and provide (and assess) specific surrogate techniques.

For the distance we propose

\displaystyle\text{dist}(\Omega^{i},\Omega^{j})\equiv C_{1}d_{\mathrm{Hausdorff}}(\Omega^{i},\Omega^{j})+C_{2}\left||\partial\Omega^{i}|-|\partial\Omega^{j}|\right|,

(85)

where

is the Hausdorff distance
[
14
]
and
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
are positive real constants.

The geometric features should, in general, increase with
\phi
. For the first feature, we propose

\displaystyle\text{Feature}_{1}

\displaystyle\equiv\text{Modified Weighted Inverse Angle (MWIA)},

(86)

\text{Feature}_{1}
is complicated and we thus restrict attention to
\Omega\equiv\Omega^{\text{2D}}\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
(or associated extruded domains
\Omega\times(0,W)
in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
) and furthermore polygonal domains
\Omega^{\text{2D}}

\text{Feature}_{1}
we consider all polygonal domains which are sufficiently close to
\Omega^{\text{2D}}

The ‘modified’ qualifier indicates that a pre-processing procedure in which small segments (compared to its neighbors) are removed to account for ‘smoothed’ sharp angles which may fool an ‘unmodified’ procedure. In short,
\text{Feature}_{1}
will be large if some good fraction of the boundary corresponds to small interior angles.

For the second feature, we propose

\displaystyle\text{Feature}_{2}

\displaystyle\equiv\text{InRadius $\gamma$}.

(87)

Examples will be provided in the next section.

(Scale-Invariance of

Appendix A

A.1
Bilinear Forms

We first define
L^{2}(\Omega)
, the space of functions
w
which are square-integrable over
\Omega
, and associated norm
\|w\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\equiv(\int_{\Omega}w^{2})^{1/2}
. We next introduce the space
H^{1}(\Omega)\equiv\{w\in L^{2}(\Omega)\,|\,|\boldsymbol{\nabla}w|\in L^{2}(\Omega)\}
, and associated norm

\displaystyle\|w\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}\equiv\int_{\Omega}w^{2}+|\boldsymbol{\nabla}w|^{2}\;;

(117)

note that this norm shall only be applied to nondimensional quantities, and hence we do not need to include any constants for dimensional homogeneity. Finally, for convenience, we define
X\equiv X(\Omega)\equiv H^{1}(\Omega)
and
\|\,\cdot\,\|_{X}\equiv\|\,\cdot\,\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}
.

We then introduce, for all
w\in X
and all
v\in X
,

\displaystyle m(w,v;\sigma)\equiv

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\sigma\,w\,v\,,

(118)

\displaystyle a_{0}(w,v;\kappa)\equiv

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\kappa\,\boldsymbol{\nabla}w\cdot\boldsymbol{\nabla}v\,,

(119)

\displaystyle a_{1}(w,v)\equiv

\displaystyle\int_{\partial\Omega}w\,v\,,

(120)

\displaystyle a(w,v;B;\kappa)\equiv

\displaystyle\,a_{0}(w,v;\kappa)+Ba_{1}(w,v)\,.

(121)

It is readily demonstrated that
m
,
a_{0}
,
a_{1}
, and
a
are (i) continuous in
X
, (ii) symmetric, and (iii) positive semi-definite. Note we shall, as convenient, suppress explicit reference to the coefficient functions
\sigma
and
\kappa
.

A.2
Initial Value Problem

We shall require
\sigma\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
and
\kappa\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
.
We then seek, for given
B\in\mathbb{R}_{0+}
,
u(\cdot,t;B)
for
t\in(0,{t_{\text{final}}}]
such that

\displaystyle m(\partial_{t}u(\cdot,t;B),v;\sigma)+a(u(\cdot,t;B),v;B;\kappa)=0,\forall v\in X\,,

(122)

subject to initial condition

\displaystyle u(\cdot,t=0;B)=1\;.

(123)

Recall that
\partial_{t}
refers to the partial derivative with respect to time.

The Robin heat equation admits a unique solution in Bochner space
C^{0}([0,{t_{\text{final}}}],L^{2}(\Omega))\cap L^{2}((0,{t_{\text{final}}}),H^{1}(\Omega))
; here
C^{0}([0,{t_{\text{final}}}])
refers to the space of continuous functions over the interval
[0,{t_{\text{final}}}]
. It follows from
u\in L^{2}((0,{t_{\text{final}}}),H^{1}(\Omega))
that
u(\cdot,t;B)\in X
for almost all
t\in[0,{t_{\text{final}}}]
; the qualification “almost all” in particular addresses the issue of short-time singularity.

A.3
Eigenproblem

There is a very large literature on the Laplacian eigenproblem for Dirichlet, Neumann, and Robin boundary conditions; see
[
17
]
for a review.

We seek
(\psi_{j}(B)\in X,\lambda_{j}(B)\in\mathbb{R}_{0+})_{j=1,2,\ldots}
such that

\displaystyle a(\psi_{j}(B),v;B;\kappa)

\displaystyle=\lambda_{j}(B)m(\psi_{j}(B),v;\sigma),\forall v\in X\;,

(124)

subject to normalization

\displaystyle m(\psi_{j}(B),\psi_{j}(B);\sigma)

\displaystyle=1\;.

(125)

Recall
\mathbb{R}_{0+}
refers to the non-negative reals.

We recall a few points from the main document: It is standard to note that the eigenvalues are real and non-negative for
B\geq 0
. We shall enumerate the eigenvalues in increasing magnitude, hence
0\leq\lambda_{1}(B)<\lambda_{2}(B)\leq\lambda_{3}(B)\leq\ldots
. It is also standard to note that the eigenfunctions are orthonormal:

\displaystyle m(\psi_{i}(B),\psi_{j}(B);\sigma)=\delta_{ij}\;,\;i,j=1,2,\dots\;;

(126)

here
\delta_{ij}
is the Kronecker-delta symbol. We can also demonstrate that
\lambda_{1}(B)
is of multiplicity unity and that
\psi_{1}(B)
is one-signed — we shall choose
\psi_{1}(B)
positive, which, with (
125
), uniquely specifies
\psi_{1}(B)
.
We shall henceforth adopt the convention that
\psi(B)
(respectively,
\lambda(B)
) without subscript shall refer to the first eigenfunction,
\psi_{1}(B)
(respectively, the first eigenvalue,
\lambda_{1}(B)
), and that furthermore
\psi^{0}(B)
(respectively,
\lambda^{0}(B)
) shall refer to
\psi(B=0)
(respectively,
\lambda(B=0))
.

A.4
Sensitivity Equation: Weak Form

We provide here the weak form for the sensitivity equation introduced in (strong form in) Proposition
3.5
. We introduce space
Z_{0}(\sigma)\equiv\{w\in X\,|\,m(w,1;\sigma)=0\}\subset H^{1}(\Omega)
; we imbue
Z_{0}(\sigma)
with the standard

inner product and associated norm. We first provide a weak form for test space

\psi^{\prime\,0}(\sigma,\kappa)\in Z_{0}(\sigma)

\displaystyle a_{0}(\psi^{\prime\,0},v;\kappa)=|\Omega|^{-1/2}L(v;\sigma)\;,\forall v\in X\;.

(127)

where linear form

\displaystyle L(v;\sigma)\equiv\gamma\int_{\Omega}\sigma\,v-\int_{\partial\Omega}v.

(128)

We also provide an equivalent weak form for test space

Find
\psi^{\prime\,0}(\sigma,\kappa)\in Z_{0}(\sigma)

\displaystyle a_{0}(\psi^{\prime\,0},v;\kappa)=-|\Omega|^{-1/2}\int_{\partial\Omega}v\;,\forall v\in Z_{0}(\sigma)\;.

In either case we may then evaluate

\displaystyle\phi(\sigma,\kappa)=a_{0}(\psi^{\prime\,0},\psi^{\prime\,0};\kappa)\;;

Note that, in addition to the constrained elliptic problems (

\psi^{\prime\,0}
as a maximization principle — useful for theoretical purposes, and a saddle problem — useful for numerical purposes.
We discuss the former in context in Appendix

A.5
Sensitivity Equation: Saddle Form

We now look for the pair
(\psi^{\prime\,0}\in X,p\in\mathbb{R})
such that

\displaystyle a_{0}(\psi^{\prime\,0},v;\kappa)+p\,m(1,v;\sigma)

\displaystyle=-|\Omega|^{-1/2}a_{1}(1,v),\;\forall v\in X

\displaystyle q\,m(1,\psi^{\prime\,0};\sigma)

\displaystyle=0\;,\forall q\in\mathbb{R}\;.

(132)
