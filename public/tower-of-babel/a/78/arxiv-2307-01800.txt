Classically efficient regimes in measurement based quantum computation performed using diagonal two qubit gates and cluster measurements
Sahar Atallah 1 , Michael Garn 1,2 , Yukuan Tao 3 , and Shashank Virmani 1

Abstract
In a recent work atallah we showed that there is a constant λ > 0 \lambda>0 such that it is possible to efficiently classically simulate a quantum system in which (i) qudits are placed on the nodes of a graph, (ii) each qudit undergoes at most D D diagonal gates, (iii) each qudit is destructively measured in the computational basis or bases unbiased to it, and (iv) each qudit is initialised within λ − D \lambda^{-D} of a diagonal state according to a particular distance measure. In this work we explicitly compute λ \lambda for any two qubit diagonal gate, thereby extending the computation of atallah beyond C ​ Z CZ gates. For any finite degree graph this allows us to describe a two parameter family of pure entangled quantum states (or three parameter family of thermal states) which have a non-trivial classically efficiently simulatable “phase” for the permitted measurements, even though other values of the parameters may enable ideal cluster state quantum computation. The main the technical tool involves considering separability in terms of “cylindrical” sets of operators. We also consider whether a different choice of set can strengthen the algorithm, and prove that they are optimal among a broad class of sets, but also show numerically that outside this class there are choices that can increase the size of the classically efficient regime.

Introduction

Since the original cluster state scheme for measurement based quantum computation
Raussendorf
was first invented, many other resource states have been proposed that can support quantum computation under local measurements of both destructive and non-destructive forms.

One approach to constructing such examples is to understand how to first transform a given entangled resource into a cluster state using local measurements, after which the cluster state can be used to support quantum computation (e.g.
mora_universal_2010
;

;
Terry
;

;

;

;

;

In this work our focus will not be on when quantum computation is possible, but instead to construct non-trivial regions of entangled quantum resource states that can be efficiently simulated classically.
Understanding when quantum systems can or cannot be efficiently simulated classically is an important problem in quantum computation, and developing new approaches to the problem will help us to pin down when quantum advantage arises and what enables it. Approaches to classical simulation often fit into broad themes or combinations of themes, e.g. they might exploit algebraic properties (e.g.
GK
;

;
Somma

;

;

;

We will primarily build upon the work of
atallah
, in which we developed a generalised separability based algorithm for efficiently simulating variations on cluster state quantum computing involving qudits placed at the nodes of a finite degree graph. The method of
atallah
uses a notion called ‘cylindrical’ separability, which is distinct from the usual definition of quantum separability. In this work we will apply it to a two parameter family of pure entangled quantum states of qubits. We will also briefly discuss how the method can straightforwardly be extended to a three parameter family of thermal/noisy states. The pure states we consider are made by initialising qubits in identical pure product states at the nodes of arbitrary finite degree
D
graphs, and then interacting them using arbitrary diagonal two qubit gates. While five parameters are needed to describe the initial qubit states and the interactions (two for the pure states, and three for a diagonal unitary up to a global phase), our measurements will be restricted to ones of the original cluster state scheme, i.e. adaptive destructive measurements of observables
\cos(\theta)X+\sin(\theta)Y
or
Z

At specific values of these parameters we have ideal cluster states on arbitrary finite degree graphs. We will explicitly compute regions of the parameter space for which the systems can be efficiently classically, in the sense that a classical algorithm can sample from a probability distribution that approximates the distribution of measurement outcomes to arbitrary fixed total variation distance in polynomial time. The regions depends upon
D
, but no other features of the graph. The performance of the simulation is inherited from the algorithm
HN
for simulating (noisy) separable quantum systems, which was leveraged by

and hence is implicit in the present work.

Some of the systems that we consider have been considered in different contexts in previous works. When the interaction is fixed to a control-

However, if we are considering classical simulatability, then prior to

I.1
Generalised Separability

Before summarising in more detail the results of this paper, we briefly summarise the notion of generalised separability that we need, and how it plays a role in classical simulation algorithms. We will say that a state
\rho
of two or more quantum particles
A,B,C,...
is separable with respect to local sets of operators
S_{A},S_{B},S_{C},....
, or more concisely
(S_{A},S_{B},S_{C},...)
-separable if we may write:

\rho=\sum_{i}p_{i}\rho^{i}_{A}\otimes\rho^{i}_{B}\otimes\rho^{i}_{C}\otimes....

(1)

where the local operators
\rho^{i}_{A},\rho^{i}_{B},\rho^{i}_{C},...
are drawn from sets of the local sets of operators
S_{A},S_{B},S_{C},...
respectively. We will refer to the
S_{K}
, for
K=A,B,C,...
, as local ‘state spaces’. The possibility that the
S_{K}
may contain operators that are not positive semi-definite is the origin of the ‘generalised’ in ‘generalised separable’. However, for brevity we will often simply use the term ‘separable’ when the sets
S_{A},S_{B},S_{C},...
are clear from the context.

If we permit the sets
S_{K}
to be anything then the definition of generalised separability is too general to be of use to us. For our present purposes the sets
S_{K}
will only be useful if (possibly with some ‘shrinking’) they are contained in the ‘normalised duals’ of the measurements permitted on each particle. By this we mean the following: suppose that we are permitted to measure each particle
A,B,C,...
destructively using sets
M_{A},M_{B},M_{C},...
of POVM operators respectively. The normalised dual of the set
M_{K}
is the set of operators

\{\sigma|\mbox{tr}\{\sigma\}=1,\mbox{tr}\{\sigma P\}\geq 0\,\,\forall P\in M_{K}\}

When the
S_{K}
are in the normalised duals of the
M_{K}
, then under appropriate technical conditions, generalised separability can lead to efficient classical simulation algorithms for quantum entangled systems, as was exploited in
atallah
, and previously scoped out in
RV
;
RVprep
;
AJRV1
;
AJRV2
. We note that much broader notions of generalised entanglement were considered in the earlier work
Barnum
, and moreover some of those notions have also been used to construct efficient classical simulation algorithms
Somma
. However, the Lie algebraic approach of
Somma
does not appear to be applicable to the systems we consider here. Nevertheless, there is still a common theme to all these works that entanglement should be considered as ‘relative’ to privileged sets of observables or measurements.

In
atallah
two forms of classical simulation were considered, a ‘fine grained’ scheme in which each particle was treated separately, and a ‘coarse grained’ scheme in which, given an appropriate lattice, the particles were grouped into blocks. We will only review the qubit ‘fine grained’ scheme here as we will not exploit the structure of graphs beyond the value of
D
, and we will only consider qubits in this work. The fine grained scheme applies to systems in which qubits are interacted using diagonal gates and measured destructively in standard cluster state measurements. At the beginning of the classical simulation, a (generalised) separable decomposition is constructed for the system that involves certain sets
S_{K}
of local operators of a particular size. To maintain a separable decomposition, however, each time that the gates are applied the local sets
S_{K}
of the interacting particles have to ‘grow’ in size by
\lambda
. The
\lambda
s needed are called ‘disentangling growth rates’ (sometimes with an added prefix such as ‘cylindrical’ to describe the type of entanglement that is being considered). This separable decomposition enables us to use the algorithm of
HN
to give an efficient classical algorithm for sampling measurement outcomes in the sense of sampling in polynomial time for a fixed total variation distance. It turns out that for the classical simulation to work, one needs the size of the local sets to not to exceed unity, and this means that at the beginning of the simulation the local sets used
S_{K}
must not exceed size
\lambda^{-D}
, where
D
is the number of gates that any given particle undergoes. If the particles are initialised in local states - including some pure states - drawn from starting sets of this size, then the quantum system can be efficiently simulated classically.

II
Overview of results and structure of paper

In
atallah
, in the context of qubits, the sets
S_{K}
were ‘cylinders’ of Bloch vectors - these will be defined in equation (
5
). Explicit values of
\lambda
were computed for
CZ
(control-
Z
) interactions.
In this work we will extend this by computing the disentangling growth rates for
all
two qubit diagonal gates. This is the main tool we will need to obtain the classically efficient ‘phases’ that we will identify.

We will also present some results on whether considering other types of ‘state spaces’ (other than the cylinders) may lead to a more wide ranging classically efficient simulation. We will show - in a specific sense to be described later - that cylindrical state spaces are optimal among a large family of state spaces, but nevertheless we discuss numerical evidence indicating that other state spaces can broaden (albeit very slightly) the classically efficient regime.

In section
III
we explain notation. In section
IV
we first point out the straightforward fact that a diagonal two-qubit unitary is equivalent to a gate of the form
V_{\varphi}:=|00\rangle\langle 00|+|01\rangle\langle 01|+|10\rangle\langle 10|+e^{i\varphi}|11\rangle\langle 11|
up to unimportant local diagonal unitaries, and then will derive an analytical form for the cylinder disentangling growth rates for all such gates.
In section
V
we apply theses rates to a two parameter (up to certain symmetries) family of pure quantum states, or a three parameter family of thermal states, deriving a classically efficient region. In section
VI
we consider alternative state spaces, showing that qubit cylinders have slower disentangling growth rates among a large family of state spaces, while providing numerical evidence that other state spaces can increase the size of the classically efficient regime.
In section
VII
we conclude.

IV
Cylinder disentangling growth rates for arbitrary diagonal two-qubit gates

Consider a general 2-qubit unitary that is diagonal in the computational basis, i.e.:

e^{i\varphi_{1}}|00\rangle\langle 00|+e^{i\varphi_{2}}|01\rangle\langle 01|+e^{i\varphi_{3}}|10\rangle\langle 10|+e^{i\varphi_{4}}|11\rangle\langle 11|

(3)

where
0\leq\varphi_{i}\leq 2\pi
. For any such diagonal gate we define the parameter
\varphi=\varphi_{4}+\varphi_{1}-\varphi_{2}-\varphi_{3}

It is not difficult to show that up to local diagonal unitaries, the gate (

V_{\varphi}=|00\rangle\langle 00|+|01\rangle\langle 01|+|10\rangle\langle 10|+e^{i\varphi}|11\rangle\langle 11|

(4)

Proof:
Multiplying the gate of equation (
3
)
by the diagonal product unitary

\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0&0\\

By setting

4

where
\varphi=\varphi_{4}+\varphi_{1}-\varphi_{2}-\varphi_{3}

Hence w.l.o.g. we may consider two qubit diagonal unitaries of this controlled-phase form, so that

\blacksquare

\sum_{i}p_{i}\left[\begin{array}[]{c}1\\

Conversely, suppose now that this operator has a

It is convenient to re-express (

\displaystyle\left(I+f_{A}X\right)\otimes\left(I+f_{B}X\right)+f_{A}f_{B}(e^{-i\varphi}-1)|00\rangle\langle 11|

\displaystyle+f_{A}f_{B}(e^{i\varphi}-1)|11\rangle\langle 00|.

\varphi\neq 0

\displaystyle\left(I+f_{A}X\right)\otimes\left(I+f_{B}X\right)+c\left(e^{-i\gamma}|00\rangle\langle 11|+e^{i\gamma}|11\rangle\langle 00|\right)

1.

f_{A}>1

2.

This means that the term

Under our assumption that

3.

The final possibility is

Then we have that equation (

K+L

We now note that

The Weyl inequalities

V
Classically efficient phases, with cluster state measurements

We now discuss how the above lemma can be used to determine an efficiently classically simulatable ‘phase’ for a family of systems with two parameters
r\in[0,1],\varphi\in[0,2\pi)
.
In particular, suppose that we have a state created in the following way:

1.

Qubits drawn from
{\rm Cyl}(r)
- i.e. states with Bloch vectors
[x,y,z]
such that
x^{2}+y^{2}\leq r^{2}
- are placed at the nodes of a graph.

2.

Each qubit undergoes at most
D
gates
V_{\varphi}
.

3.

Each qubit is then measured adaptively in the computational basis or equatorial bases.

We will apply the lemma, following the approach of
atallah
, to derive a region of
r,\varphi
for which the system can be efficiently simulated classically, in the sense of sampling to within an arbitrary fixed total variational distance in polynomial time.

We begin by considering the equation (
7
) in the symmetric case
f_{A}=f_{B}=f
. In this case the determinant simplifies to:

\left(1-f^{2}\right)\left(\left(1-f^{2}\right)^{3}+4(\cos(\varphi)-1)f^{4}\right)

As
1-f^{2}
is always positive (apart from
f=1
, which we are excluding when
\varphi\neq 0
), we have separability if and only if:

\left(1-f^{2}\right)^{3}+4(\cos(\varphi)-1)f^{4}\geq 0

In terms of the ratios
R/r
this can be written as (also multiplying through by
R^{6}/r^{6}
):

\left({R^{2}\over r^{2}}-1\right)^{3}+4(\cos(\varphi)-1){R^{2}\over r^{2}}\geq 0

(14)

Denote by
\lambda(\varphi)>1
be the minimum value of
R/r>1
satisfying this equation.
By the arguments of
atallah
, it follows that the region in
r,\varphi
space satisfied by

\{(r,\varphi)|r\leq\lambda(\varphi)^{-D}\}

(15)

can be efficiently simulated classically.
To understand equation (
14

\alpha:=4(\cos(\varphi)-1)
and
t={R^{2}\over r^{2}}-1
. We have that
\alpha<0
as we are only considering
\varphi\neq 0
, and we have that
t>0
as we need
R/r>1
. Hence we are looking for solutions to the cubic

t^{3}+\alpha t+\alpha\geq 0

(16)

in the region
t>0
. The part

is an odd function of

with a root at

, and it satisfies
t^{3}+\alpha t\rightarrow\infty
as

. As
\alpha
is negative, this then can be used to show that cubic has exactly one positive root. Let us call that root
T(\varphi)
, then we have that
\lambda(\varphi)=\sqrt{T(\varphi)+1}
. This is plotted in figure Fig.

.

Let us briefly remark on how temperature may be included into the computations. The hamiltonian of the system can be taken to be, in analogy with the cluster state case,

V_{tot}\left(\sum_{n}A_{n}\right)V^{{\dagger}}_{tot}

where

\displaystyle-{1\over 2}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\sin(\theta)\left(|0\rangle\langle 1|\otimes U_{n}(-\varphi)+|1\rangle\langle 0|\otimes U^{\dagger}_{n}(\varphi)\right)+\cos(\theta)Z_{n}

\displaystyle U_{n}(\varphi):=\bigotimes_{neighbours(n)}\left(|0\rangle\langle 0|+\exp(i\varphi)|1\rangle\langle 1|\right)

over the neighbours of particle

If there is thermal noise, then instead of initialising the qubits in a pure state we would effectively initialise them in a mixed state, with the components of the Bloch vector shrunk by a factor

\left\{(\varphi,\theta,T)\bigg|\theta\leq\sin^{-1}\left({\lambda(\varphi)^{-D}\over 1-2p_{T}}\right)\right\}

Local dephasing noise can be handled in a similar way - it simply changes the effective size of the starting cylinder that we can efficiently simulate, making it larger.

We note that while incorporating noise/temperature in this way is straightforward, we anticipate that other methods such as

VI
The possibility of alternative state spaces

The classical simulation works because the cylinders have the following properties: (i) they contain interesting input states, (ii) in order to maintain a separable decomposition under the action of the entangling gates, we can grow the cylinders to some extent without producing negative probabilities for the allowed measurements. This leads to the question of whether there are better ‘state spaces’ other than the cylinders. For instance are there different choices of state space that contain interesting inputs with greater values of
r
, but perhaps don’t grow so fast and therefore yield classical simulations for larger regions? We note that in
atallah
for lattices with a sufficiently regular structure, a relatively straightforward coarse graining scheme (grouping particles into blocks) was discussed that can be used to classically simulate larger regions inputs - while this was only explicitly shown for the
D=4
square lattice, it seems likely that the same approach would yield improvements for other graphs.

However, in this section we will make no assumptions on the structure of graph, other than the value of
D
, and so we will consider whether different choices of single particle state spaces can improve the the range of
(\varphi,\theta)
that can be efficiently simulated classically. In this context we will show two things: (a) among a reasonably sized family of state spaces, the cylinders have the slowest radial growth required to maintain separability under action of the
V_{\varphi}
, (b) in spite of this, we show numerically that the classical simulation scheme used above can, at least in the case of the
CZ
gate, be extended to an ever so slightly greater set of quantum inputs by considering other state spaces. The latter fact implies that the curves of Fig.
2
are not tight. We note that, as pointed out in
atallah
, any attempt to construct a separable decomposition will ultimately face obstacles from non-locality. For states with
(\varphi,\theta)
values that are close enough to the top right corner of Fig.
2
, we will be able to create an ideal Bell pair between two different particles by postselecting on appropriate measurements on the other particles. If this is possible for a given choice of
(\varphi,\theta)
and
D
, this rules out the possibility of a separable decomposition using
any
state space. We leave details of this discussion to another occasion
GarnPrep
, as here we will focus on the task of choosing the best state spaces that we can.

Consider two particles
A
and
B
with local state spaces
S_{A}
and
S_{B}
respectively. We assume that each of these state spaces is represented by a compact set of Bloch vectors denoted as
[x,y,z]
, but the vectors may protrude from the Bloch sphere, thereby representing normalised but not necessarily positive semi-definite operators. However, we will not permit Bloch vectors with
|z|>1
, as they must return non-negative values for computational basis measurements under the Born rule. We ask ourselves whether there are local state spaces which maintain a separable decomposition with lower radial growth than the
\lambda(\varphi)
growth needed for cylinders. A convenient way to express this problem mathematically is to re-express it in terms of local dephasing noise. Given
r\geq 0
us define the transformation:

{\mathcal{T}}_{r}(\rho):=\left({1+r\over 2}\right)\rho+\left({1-r\over 2}\right)Z\rho Z^{{\dagger}}

where
Z
is the pauli
Z
operator. When
r\in[0,1)
the transformation
\mathcal{T}_{r}
is dephasing noise, albeit parameterised in a non-standard way. For
r=1
it is the identity, and for
r>1
it is the inverse of a dephasing noise. Geometrically
\mathcal{T}_{r}
multiplies the
x,y
components of a Bloch vector by
r
, leaving the
z
components unchanged. Hence
\mathcal{T}_{r}\mathcal{T}_{s}=\mathcal{T}_{rs}
.
In
atallah

\mathcal{T}_{r}
is referred to as a ‘phasing’ operator for general
r
. Suppose that we act with a gate
\mathcal{V}
on the sets
S_{A}\otimes S_{B}
, denote the resulting set by
\mathcal{V}(S_{A}\otimes S_{B})
. We assume that
\mathcal{V}
is transformation corresponding to a diagonal unitary, and hence it commutes with local diagonal unitaries. To compute the minimal radial growth, we would like to work out the smallest (assuming it exists) value of
R\geq 0
such that:

\mathcal{V}(S_{A}\otimes S_{B})\subseteq{\rm Conv}\left(\mathcal{T}_{R}(S_{A})\otimes\mathcal{T}_{R}(S_{B})\right)

(18)

where
{\rm Conv}
denotes taking the convex hull. In general it can happen that such an
R
does not exist. However, we will assume that
S_{A},S_{B}
are such that there is a minimum value of
R
satisfying (
18
).
Under all of these assumptions, another way to pose the question is as follows. Given
S_{A},S_{B}
, what is the minimum value of
R
such that

\mathcal{T}_{1/R}\otimes\mathcal{T}_{1/R}\left(\mathcal{V}(S_{A}\otimes S_{B})\right)\subseteq{\rm Conv}\left(S_{A}\otimes S_{B}\right)\,\,\,\,?

Let us denote this optimal value of
R
by
R^{*}(S_{A},S_{B})
, the notation signifying that it depends upon what the sets
S_{A},S_{B}
are. It is clear that the value of
R^{*}
does not change if we replace
S_{A}
by
US_{A}U^{\dagger}
where
U
is an arbitrary diagonal unitary, as such a unitary commutes
with both
\mathcal{V}
and
\mathcal{T}_{1/R}
. The following observation will be useful:

Lemma 2:
Consider two pairs of compact sets
(S_{A},S_{B})
and
(T_{A},S_{B})
for which
R^{*}(S_{A},S_{B}),R^{*}(T_{A},S_{B})
both exist. Then

R^{*}({\rm Conv}(S_{A},T_{A}),S_{B})\leq\max\{R^{*}(S_{A},S_{B}),R^{*}(T_{A},S_{B})\}

Proof:
The sets are all the convex hulls of their extremal points.
{\rm Conv}(S_{A},T_{A})
cannot have new extremal points beyond those that are already extremal points of
S_{A},T_{A}
individually. But the set of separable states with respect to the pair of sets
({\rm Conv}(S_{A},T_{A}),S_{B})
contains the sets of separable states w.r.t. the pairs
(S_{A},S_{B})
and
(T_{A},S_{B})
. Hence the
R
value needed to induce separability for the sets
{\rm Conv}(S_{A},T_{A}),S_{B}
is no greater than the largest of
R^{*}(S_{A},S_{B}),R^{*}(T_{A},S_{B})
.

\blacksquare

This means that we have a kind of convex hull analogue of ‘twirling’ arguments that have proven useful in entanglement theory - given a pair of set
S_{A},S_{B}
for which
R^{*}(S_{A},S_{B})
exists, then for any diagonal unitary
U
we have that
R^{*}(S_{A},S_{B})=R^{*}(US_{A}U^{{\dagger}},S_{B})
, and hence
R^{8}({\rm Conv}(S_{A},US_{A}U^{{\dagger}}),S_{B})\leq R^{*}(S_{A},S_{B})
by the above lemma. Continuing this line of reasoning for the full group of local diagonal unitaries, we see that:

R^{*}({\rm Conv}(\mathcal{U}(S_{A})),S_{B})\leq R^{*}(S_{A},S_{B})

(19)

where
{\rm Conv}(\mathcal{U}(S_{A}))
denotes the convex hull of the sets obtained by acting on
S_{A}
with the group of local diagonal unitaries.
We may then apply exactly the same argument to
S_{B}
, and hence we find that we can never get a greater value of
R^{*}
by taking a symmetrised version of the state spaces we are considering - by which we mean a state space obtained by ‘spinning’ the state space around the
z
axis and taking the convex hulls. Moreover, in performing the symmetrisation of a state space we cannot get a smaller state space, only a larger one, and this means that the symmetrisation can only contain more inputs.

It is now only a short argument to prove the following:

Lemma 3:
Consider compact sets
(S_{A},S_{B})
such that
R^{*}(S_{A},S_{B})
exist, and the
z
values lie in
[-1,+1]
. Assume that the sets contain a Bloch vector of the form
[x,y,\pm 1]
for some
x,y\neq 0,0
.
Then the cylinders can only have a lower value of
R^{*}
, the radial growth needed to maintain separability when acted upon by a transformation
\mathcal{V}
representing a diagonal gate.

Proof:
Assume that the vector is of the form
[x,y,+1]
, as the cases involving
[x,y,-1]
will follow straightforwardly. The sets
{\rm Conv}(\mathcal{U}(S_{A}))
and
{\rm Conv}(\mathcal{U}(S_{B}))
contain the discs
\{[x,y,1]|x^{2}+y^{2}\leq r^{2}_{A}\}
and
\{[x,y,1]|x^{2}+y^{2}\leq r^{2}_{B}\}
for some value of
r_{A},r_{B}>0
. However, as
z
values do not change when undergoing
\mathcal{V}
, and as
z=1
is an extremal value of
z
, for the output to be separable w.r.t.
{\rm Conv}(\mathcal{U}(S_{A})),{\rm Conv}(\mathcal{U}(S_{B}))
, it needs to have a separable decomposition w.r.t. a radially grown version of the same discs with
z=1
. However, as the cylinders are the convex hulls of discs at
z=+1
and
z=-1
, this means that
R^{*}(S_{A},S_{B})
can be no lower than that of the cylinder.

\blacksquare

This lemma tells us why cylinders are a pretty good choice of state space - to maintain a separable decomposition it requires a lower growth rate than many other potential state spaces. This makes it easier for us to meet the requirement that the final state space should not be too large. However, while this gives reasonable motivation for the use of cylinders, as well as a useful symmetry technique, it does not rule out the possibility that (i) a state space without a Bloch vector of the form
[x,y,\pm 1]
may still have lower growth, or (ii) there may be better performing scheme involving state spaces that change in more complex ways with each gate, rather than just considering radial growth.

Indeed, we can easily find examples numerically that show that the graphs of Fig.
2
cannot be tight. We have numerically investigated an alternative scheme which very slightly outperforms the use of cylinders, as we now describe. Suppose that we wish to consider an input quantum state
|\psi\rangle
whose Bloch vector has radius
\sqrt{x^{2}+y^{2}}=r
, and the interaction is a
CZ
gate. In our approach so far, for a general lattice of degree
D
(without considering the benefits of coarse graining as was done in
atallah
), we would need to have
r\leq\lambda(\pi)^{-D}
for the classical simulation to work. The classical simulation worked by considering the input state
|\psi\rangle
as an element of a cylinder, and then growing it radially in order to maintain a separable decomposition. However, we now modify the scheme by considering a different family of state spaces. In particular consider the following state space
B(r,h)
, defined as the convex hull of the following two sets of Bloch vectors:

\{[x,y,z]|x^{2}+y^{2}\leq r^{2},z=\pm h\}

and

\{[0,0,\pm 1]\}

For an illustration see Fig.
3
. The initial state
|\psi\rangle
is contained within
B(r,\sqrt{1-r^{2}})\subset B(r,1)={\rm Cyl}(r)
. In the previous classical simulation we found that two elements of
{\rm Cyl}(r)
, after a
CZ
gate, would be separable w.r.t. a bigger cylinder
{\rm Cyl}(\lambda(\pi)r)
. However, we have found numerically for most values of
r
that when two elements of
B(r,\sqrt{1-r^{2}})
undergo a
CZ
gate, then they can be given a separable decomposition with respect to a cylinder of very slightly smaller radius. These slightly smaller cylinders then can then be grown by
\lambda(\pi)
for each subsequent gate to maintain a separable decomposition as before. However, by changing the initial state space from
{\rm Cyl}(r)
to
B(r,\sqrt{1-r^{2}})
, we make slight gains in the size of the initial region we can efficiently simulate. The numerics were performed as follows: we discretised the cylinder state space keeping 40 extremal points, and then use a Matlab linear programming function to test the smallest output cylinder radius needed to give a cylinder separable decomposition for extremal inputs from
B(r,\sqrt{1-r^{2}})
. For lattices of degree
D=3
, for example, we find that this permits classical simulations for input
r\leq 0.1153

VII
Summary and Discussion

We have computed the cylindrical disentangling growth rate for an abitrary two-qubit diagonal gate, thereby extending the computation in
atallah
. This enables us to write down classically efficiently simulatable ‘phases’ made by placing pure qubits on a lattice, interacting them with diagonal two qubit gates, and measuring them destructively using
Z
basis and
XY
plane measurements. The notion of classical simulation used is inherited from
HN
, i.e. for our purposes efficient classical simulation means ‘sampling in polynomial time from a probability distribution that approximates the actual distribution to arbitrary fixed total variation distance’. We note that aside from the issue of efficient classical simulation, the methods here also construct local hidden variable models for the regions below the curves in Fig.
2
.

Examples of non-trivial quantum systems that are known to be efficiently simulated classically are somewhat rare, and quantum entangled pure state ‘phases’ that are classically efficiently simulatable even more so. To our knowledge the pure state examples presented in this work cannot be efficiently simulated through other known methods. For instance, they are not stabilizer systems, so Gottesman-Knill does not apply
GK
, and tensor network methods (e.g.
peps
) cannot be straightforwardly applied as the states can be transformed to ideal cluster states by local transformation. The curves we obtain show that a non-trivial ‘computational transition’ happens in these systems, as one point corresponds to ideal cluster states. Obtaining more information on where this transition may lie would be an interesting problem for future work.

Critical to the arguments has been the fact that the measurements available are restricted. It would be interesting to know the extent to which this can be relaxed. The coarse graining of
atallah
implies that at least in the case of the
D=4
square lattice (and likely for other regular lattices too), coarse graining can either increase the range of inputs that can be efficiently simulated, or alternatively can be used to increase the set of measurements that can be efficiently simulated. This shows that there is scope for classically simulating more than only the cluster state measurements, at least for square lattices. Could we - at least for some points
(\varphi,\theta)
- sample from all local destructive measurements efficiently? If this is possible then it would require a different approach, because an exact separable decomposition is ruled out by the fact that all entangled multiparty pure states demonstrate non-locality
LoPop
, so either an approximate separable decomposition would be needed, some method of coarse graining, or an entirely different method altogether. However, we cannot rule out the opposite possibility either - that all points
(\varphi\neq 0,\theta)
can support non-classical computation as soon as all destructive measurements are allowed. It would be interesting to have the answers to these questions.

We have also presented some observations on how we might hunt for other state spaces that may lead to more powerful classical simulations than the use of cylinders. Through a convex hull analogue of group averaging we have seen that optimal choices inherit the
U_{z}
symmetry of the system, and we used this to argue that cylinders have the lowest radial disentangling growth rates among all sets that contain at least one point of the form
[0,0,\pm 1]
. However, we have also seen a numerical proof that in the first step of the algorithm, replacing cylinders with state spaces of the form of
B(r,\sqrt{1-r^{2}})
can increase slightly the set of
(\varphi=\pi,\theta)
that can be efficiently simulated, showing that the curves of Fig.
2
are not tight. It will be interesting to know what happens in the rest of
(\varphi,\theta)
space for various lattices.

A further natural question is how entangled are the pure systems corresponding to the curves in Fig.
2
? There is no clear cut answer to this question, as there is no unambiguous way to quantify entanglement in multiparty settings. However, we will make a few remarks. The states on the curves have fairly low local (single particle) entropy - at most
\approx 0.01
bits for certain
(\varphi,\theta)
on the
D=3
curve. However, this does not necessarily mean that they are weakly entangled. All multiparty pure states demonstrate nonlocality
LoPop
given access to all local measurements, and because we can consider translationally invariant lattices the quantum entanglement is certainly genuinely multiparty. Further, as pointed out in
gross2007measurement
, states with arbitrarily low local entropy can still support full quantum computation. In fact using similar ideas, for any arbitrarily small desired local entropy, it is possible to construct a finite (albeit large)
D
state at points of the form
(\varphi=\pi,\theta=\epsilon)
for
\epsilon
small that can support quantum computation
example
. This leads us back to a question highlighted above: is it the case that below the curves of figure Fig.
2
we have pure systems with correlations that are too weak to perform non-classical computation, or is it the case that there are strong correlations that cannot be exploited by the restricted measurements that are available? Whichever direction the eventual answer falls, the use of generalised separability has enabled us, relatively straightforwardly, to obtain classically efficient regions which are not straightforwardly identifiable using other methods.
