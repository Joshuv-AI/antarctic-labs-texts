Abstract
The paper discusses the relationships between electrical quantities, such as voltages, currents, and frequency, and geometrical ones, namely curvature and torsion. The proposed approach is based on the Frenet frame utilized in differential geometry and provides a general framework for the definition of the time derivative of electrical quantities in stationary as well as transient conditions. As a byproduct, the proposed approach unifies and generalizes the time- and phasor-domain frameworks. Other noteworthy results are a new interpretation of the link between frequency and the time derivatives of voltage and current; and a definition of the rate of change of frequency that includes the novel concept of “torsional frequency.” Several numerical examples based on balanced, unbalanced, harmonically-distorted and transient voltages illustrate the findings of the paper.

II
Introduction

II-A

Motivation

The study and simulation of circuit dynamics has traditionally been
approached using different frameworks. Stationary AC circuits are
conveniently studied using quantities such as phasors and impedances;
circuits with harmonic contents are studied using Fourier analysis or
similar frequency-domain approaches; rotating machines and power
electronic devices are often studied using Park and/or Clarke
transforms; generic transients are studied using a time-domain
analysis
[
1
]
. In this paper, we propose an approach based
on differential geometry, more specifically on the Frenet frame

[
2
]
. This approach leads to the definition of a framework
that admits, as special cases, the circuit analysis transformations
mentioned above.

Literature Review

Differential geometry finds applications in several fields of science

three-dimensional object description

robotic control along geodesic paths

The utilization of concepts of geometric algebra in circuit and power

time derivative of a multi-dimensional curve

In this work, we exploit differential geometry rather than geometric

II-C

Contributions

We apply differential geometry to define a general framework for the
definition of electrical quantities and their time derivatives. The
specific contributions of the paper are the following.

•

The derivation of the expressions of the tangent, normal and
binormal vectors of the Frenet frame in terms of the voltage (or
current) of an electrical circuit.

•

A novel interpretation of the time derivative of any order of
voltage and current in electrical circuits.

•

An expression of the
rocof
which involves the definition of
the novel concept of “torsional frequency,” which is also proposed
and defined in the paper.

•

An example that shows that analytic signals commonly utilized in
signal processing are a special case of the proposed framework in
two dimensions.

The meaning and derivation of the vectors of the Frenet frame when
applied to electric quantities such as voltage, current and frequency
are duly discussed in the paper.

III
Frenet Frame of Space Curves

Let us consider a space curve

\boldsymbol{x}:[0,+\infty)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{3}
with

\boldsymbol{x}=(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})
. Where
x_{1}=x_{1}(t)
,
x_{2}=x_{2}(t)
,

x_{3}=x_{3}(t)
, is the set of parametric equations for the
curve. Equivalently:

\boldsymbol{x}=x_{1}\,\boldsymbol{\rm e}_{\rm 1}+x_{2}\,\boldsymbol{\rm e}_{\rm 2}+x_{3}\,\boldsymbol{\rm e}_{\rm 3}\,,

(1)

where
(\boldsymbol{\rm e}_{\rm 1},\boldsymbol{\rm e}_{\rm 2},\boldsymbol{\rm e}_{\rm 3})
is an orthonormal basis. The length
s

of the curve is defined as:

s=\int_{0}^{t}\sqrt{\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}(r)\cdot\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}(r)}\,dr+s_{0}\,,

(2)

from which one obtains the expression:

s^{\prime}=\frac{ds}{dt}=\sqrt{\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}\cdot\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}}=|\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}|\,,

(3)

where

\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}=\frac{d}{dt}(x_{1}\,\boldsymbol{\rm e}_{\rm 1})+\frac{d}{dt}(x_{2}\,\boldsymbol{\rm e}_{\rm 2})+\frac{d}{dt}(x_{3}\,\boldsymbol{\rm e}_{\rm 3})\,,

(4)

and
\cdot
represents the inner product of two vectors, which in
three dimensions, for
\boldsymbol{a}=(a_{1},a_{2},a_{3})
,

\boldsymbol{b}=(b_{1},b_{2},b_{3})
, becomes:

\boldsymbol{a}\cdot\boldsymbol{b}=a_{1}b_{1}+a_{2}b_{2}+a_{3}b_{3}\,.

(5)

The length
s
is an invariant of the curve. It is relevant to
observe that, according to the chain rule, the derivative of
\boldsymbol{x}

with respect to
s
can be written as:

\dot{\boldsymbol{x}}=\frac{d\boldsymbol{x}}{ds}=\frac{d\boldsymbol{x}}{dt}\frac{dt}{ds}=\frac{\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}}{s^{\prime}}=\frac{\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}}{|\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}|}\,.

(6)

The vector
\dot{\boldsymbol{x}}
has magnitude 1 and is tangent to the curve

\boldsymbol{x}
.

The Frenet frame is defined by the tangent vector
\boldsymbol{\rm T}
, the normal
vector
\boldsymbol{\rm N}
and the binormal vector
\boldsymbol{\rm B}
, as follows:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\rm T}

\displaystyle=\dot{\boldsymbol{x}}\,,

(7)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\rm N}

\displaystyle=\frac{\ddot{\boldsymbol{x}}}{|\ddot{\boldsymbol{x}}|}\,,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\rm B}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\rm T}\times\boldsymbol{\rm N}\,,

where
\times
represents the cross product, which in three dimensions
can be written as the determinant of a matrix, as follows:

\boldsymbol{a}\times\boldsymbol{b}=\left|\begin{matrix}\boldsymbol{\rm e}_{\rm 1}&\boldsymbol{\rm e}_{\rm 2}&\boldsymbol{\rm e}_{\rm 3}\\
a_{1}&a_{2}&a_{3}\\
b_{1}&b_{2}&b_{3}\\
\end{matrix}\right|\,.

(8)

The vectors in (
7
) are orthonormal,
i.e.
\boldsymbol{\rm T}=\boldsymbol{\rm N}\times\boldsymbol{\rm B}
and
\boldsymbol{\rm N}=\boldsymbol{\rm B}\times\boldsymbol{\rm T}
, and have relevant
properties, which can be expressed as follows
[
2
]
:

\displaystyle\dot{\boldsymbol{\rm T}}

\displaystyle=\phantom{-}\kappa\boldsymbol{\rm N}\,,

(9)

\displaystyle\dot{\boldsymbol{\rm N}}

\displaystyle=-\kappa\boldsymbol{\rm T}+\tau\boldsymbol{\rm B}\,,

\displaystyle\dot{\boldsymbol{\rm B}}

\displaystyle=-\tau\boldsymbol{\rm N}\,,

where
\kappa
and
\tau
are the
curvature
and the

torsion
, respectively, which are given by:

\kappa=|\ddot{\boldsymbol{x}}|=\frac{|\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}\times\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime\prime}|}{|\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}|^{3}}\,,

(10)

and

\tau=\frac{\dot{\boldsymbol{x}}\cdot\ddot{\boldsymbol{x}}\times\dddot{\boldsymbol{x}}}{\kappa^{2}}=\frac{\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}\cdot\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime\prime}\times\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime\prime\prime}}{|\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}\times\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime\prime}|^{2}}\,.

(11)

The quantities defined above, namely
\kappa
and
\tau
, as well as
(
9
), are utilized in the following section.

IV
Electrical Quantities in the Frenet Frame

This section presents the main theoretical results of the paper. In
particular, the Frenet frame as well as of the curvature and torsion
of a space curve are expressed in terms of electrical quantities.
Then the expressions of the time derivatives of the vectors of
voltage, current as well as the frequency of these quantities are
derived based on the Frenet frame. A general expression for
higher-order derivatives is also presented at the end of the section.

IV-A

Voltage and its Time Derivative in the Frenet Frame

The starting assumption of the discussion given in this section is
that the vector of the voltage,
\boldsymbol{v}
, is the time derivative of a
space curve. From a physical point of view, this means assuming that
the vector that describes the magnetic flux, say
\boldsymbol{\varphi}
, is formally
defined as:

\boldsymbol{\varphi}=-\boldsymbol{x}\,.

(12)

Then, Faraday’s law gives:

\boldsymbol{v}=-\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}^{\prime}=\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}\,.

(13)

Then one can rewrite the expressions of the vectors
\boldsymbol{\rm T}
,
\boldsymbol{\rm N}
and
\boldsymbol{\rm B}

of the Frenet frame in terms of the vector for the voltage and its
derivatives.

Let us observe first that the derivative of the length
s
, according
to (
3
) and (
13
), becomes
[
21
]
:

s^{\prime}=|\boldsymbol{v}|=v\,,

(14)

and, then

\dot{\boldsymbol{x}}=-\dot{\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}}=-\frac{\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}^{\prime}}{s^{\prime}}=\frac{\boldsymbol{v}}{v}\,,

(15)

and:

\ddot{\boldsymbol{x}}=-\ddot{\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}}=\frac{\boldsymbol{v}^{\prime}}{v^{2}}-\frac{v^{\prime}\,\boldsymbol{v}}{v^{3}}\,,

(16)

and:

\dddot{\boldsymbol{x}}=-\dddot{\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}}=\frac{\boldsymbol{v}^{\prime\prime}}{v^{3}}-3\frac{v^{\prime}\,\boldsymbol{v}^{\prime}}{v^{4}}+3\frac{(v^{\prime})^{2}\,\boldsymbol{v}}{v^{5}}-\frac{v^{\prime\prime}\,\boldsymbol{v}}{v^{4}}\,,

(17)

where
v^{\prime}=\frac{d}{dt}(v)
and
v^{\prime\prime}=\frac{d^{2}}{dt^{2}}(v)
. It is
relevant to observe that, from the property

\dot{\boldsymbol{x}}\cdot\ddot{\boldsymbol{x}}=0
, as these vectors are orthogonal by
construction, and from (
15
) and (
16
), one obtains

[
21
]
:

\rho=\frac{v^{\prime}}{v}\,.

(18)

As it is well known, in time-frequency analysis and signal processing,
the quantity
\rho
is defined as the
instantaneous bandwidth

[
28
]
. In this work, however, we rather use the
interpretation of
\rho
given in
[
21
]
, namely, the
symmetric part of the
geometric frequency
.

work, the terms symmetric and antisymmetric do not refer to the
properties of a matrix but rather to the effect of operators.

is also relevant to note that, from a geometrical point of view,

\rho\,v=v^{\prime}
can be viewed as the “radial” component of the
velocity
\boldsymbol{v}
. In this vein,
\rho
can be defined as

radial frequency
.

On the other hand, from (

), (
13
) and
(
15

\boxed{\kappa=\frac{|\boldsymbol{v}\times\boldsymbol{v}^{\prime}|}{v^{3}}=\frac{|\boldsymbol{\omega}|}{v}=\frac{\omega}{v}}\,,

(19)

where the vector
\boldsymbol{\omega}
is defined as the antisymmetric
component of the geometric frequency, as follows

[
21
]
:

2

Note that in
[

the wedge

\boldsymbol{\omega}=\frac{\boldsymbol{v}\times\boldsymbol{v}^{\prime}}{v^{2}}\,.

(20)

From a geometrical point of view, in 3 dimensions,

\boxed{\tau=\frac{\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{v}^{\prime}\times\boldsymbol{v}^{\prime\prime}}{\omega^{2}\,v^{4}}}\,.

(21)

The vectors of the Frenet frame can be written as:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\rm T}=\frac{\boldsymbol{v}}{v}\,,\qquad\boldsymbol{\rm N}=\frac{\boldsymbol{n}}{n}\,,\qquad\boldsymbol{\rm B}=\frac{\boldsymbol{\omega}}{\omega}\,,

(22)

where
\boldsymbol{n}
is the normal vector before normalization, as follows:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{n}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{v}^{\prime}-\rho\boldsymbol{v}\,,

(23)

\displaystyle n

\displaystyle=|\boldsymbol{n}|=\sqrt{|\boldsymbol{v}^{\prime}|^{2}-(\rho v)^{2}}\,.

Note that, from the following property of the scalar triple product:

\boldsymbol{a}\cdot\boldsymbol{b}\times\boldsymbol{c}=\boldsymbol{c}\cdot\boldsymbol{a}\times\boldsymbol{b}\,,

(24)

the expression of the torsion can be rewritten as follows:

\tau=\frac{\boldsymbol{v}^{\prime\prime}\cdot\boldsymbol{v}\times\boldsymbol{v}^{\prime}}{\omega^{2}v^{4}}=\frac{\boldsymbol{v}^{\prime\prime}\cdot\boldsymbol{\omega}}{\kappa^{2}}\,,

(25)

which indicates that the torsion is null, apart from the obvious cases

\boldsymbol{v}^{\prime\prime}=\boldsymbol{0}
and
\boldsymbol{\omega}=\boldsymbol{0}
, if
\boldsymbol{v}^{\prime\prime}
is perpendicular
to
\boldsymbol{\omega}
. This happens if the voltage vector is unbalanced, as

We are now ready to present one of the main results of this paper.
Recalling that the Frenet vectors are orthonormal and, in particular,

\boldsymbol{\rm N}=\boldsymbol{\rm B}\times\boldsymbol{\rm T}
, one has:

\frac{\boldsymbol{n}}{n}=\frac{\boldsymbol{\omega}}{\omega}\times\frac{\boldsymbol{v}}{v}\,.

(26)

Noting that
n
is equal to the azimuthal speed, i.e.
n=\omega v

(see the proof in the Appendix), the expression above can be
simplified as:

\boldsymbol{n}=\boldsymbol{\omega}\times\boldsymbol{v}\,,

(27)

and, from (
23
):

\boxed{\boldsymbol{v}^{\prime}=\rho\,\boldsymbol{v}+\boldsymbol{\omega}\times\boldsymbol{v}}\,.

(28)

The previous expression is relevant as it allows the definition of a
time derivative operator. Equation (
28
) can be, in fact,
written as:

\left(\frac{d}{dt}-[\rho+\boldsymbol{\omega}\times]\right)\boldsymbol{v}=\boldsymbol{0}\,,

(29)

which leads to define the operator:

D_{t}^{\boldsymbol{v}}:=\frac{d}{dt}-[\rho+\boldsymbol{\omega}\times]\,.

(30)

In (
30
), the upper symbol
\boldsymbol{x}
in the notation

D^{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t}
indicates the vector upon which the operator is
applied, whereas the subindex
t
indicates the independent variable
with respect to which the operator is calculated, i.e. time.

Equation (
28
) and the operator (
30
) can be
interpreted as follows: the time derivative of a vector can be split
into two components, one symmetric (
\rho
) and one antisymmetric
(
\boldsymbol{\omega}\times
). This operator has been obtained without any
assumption on the time dependence of
\boldsymbol{v}
nor on its dimension.
For dimensions greater than 3, in fact, it is sufficient to substitute
the cross product with the wedge product
[
21
]
.

While it has been obtained considering the vector of the voltage, the
time derivative operator defined in (
30
) can be applied to
any vector that represents the first time derivative of a space curve.
In particular, if one considers the vector of the current

\boldsymbol{\imath}=\boldsymbol{q}^{\prime}
, being
\boldsymbol{q}
the vector of the electric charge
in a given point of a circuit
[
21
]
, then:

D_{t}^{\boldsymbol{\imath}}\,\boldsymbol{\imath}=\boldsymbol{0}\,.

(31)

For simplicity, in the remainder of this work, we focus exclusively on
the voltage. However, all examples provided in Section

V
can be equivalently applied to currents.

IV-B

Derivative of the Vector Frequency in the Frenet Frame

To obtain an expression of
\boldsymbol{\omega}^{\prime}
, we start with (
9
)
and rewrite the vectors in terms of the derivative with respect to
time, recalling that, for the chain rule:

\boldsymbol{\rm T}^{\prime}=v\dot{\boldsymbol{\rm T}}\,,\qquad\boldsymbol{\rm N}^{\prime}=v\dot{\boldsymbol{\rm N}}\,,\qquad\boldsymbol{\rm B}^{\prime}=v\dot{\boldsymbol{\rm B}}\,.

(32)

Then, one obtains:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\rm T}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\phantom{-}\omega\boldsymbol{\rm N}\,,

(33)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\rm N}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=-\omega\boldsymbol{\rm T}+\xi\boldsymbol{\rm B}\,,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\rm B}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=-\xi\boldsymbol{\rm N}\,,

where
\omega=v\kappa
from (
19
) and:

\xi=v\tau\,,

(34)

where
\xi
can be defined as the
torsional frequency
and has
the unit of s
-1
, as
\omega
and
\rho
. From (
22
)
and letting
\omega^{\prime}=\frac{d}{dt}\omega
, the third equation of
(
33
) can be rewritten as follows:

\frac{d}{dt}\frac{\boldsymbol{\omega}}{\omega}=\frac{\boldsymbol{\omega}^{\prime}}{\omega}-\frac{\boldsymbol{\omega}\omega^{\prime}}{\omega^{2}}=-\xi\frac{\boldsymbol{n}}{n}\,,

(35)

or, equivalently,

\boldsymbol{\omega}^{\prime}=\omega^{\prime}\frac{\boldsymbol{\omega}}{\omega}-\omega\xi\frac{\boldsymbol{n}}{n}\,,

(36)

or, equivalently,

\boxed{\boldsymbol{\omega}^{\prime}=\eta\,\boldsymbol{\omega}+\tau\,\boldsymbol{v}\times\boldsymbol{\omega}}\,,

(37)

where
\eta=\omega^{\prime}/\omega
and
[\tau\boldsymbol{v}\times]
are the
symmetric and antisymmetric parts, respectively, of the time
derivative of
\boldsymbol{\omega}
.

Equation (

) expresses the generalization of the

, commonly utilized in power system studies as a metric of
the severity of a transient following a contingency or a power
imbalance. For a balanced system,
\tau
is null and, thus,
(
36
) leads to:

|\boldsymbol{\omega}^{\prime}|=\omega^{\prime}\,,

(38)

which represents the conventional definition of
rocof
. However,
(
36
) shows that, when the torsion is not null, e.g., in
unbalanced cases,
\boldsymbol{\omega}^{\prime}
is a vector with richer information than the
usual understanding of the
rocof
. Section
V

illustrates (

) through numerical examples.

Finally, the time derivative of the symmetric part of the geometric
frequency defined in
[
21
]
is:

\rho^{\prime}=\frac{\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{v}^{\prime\prime}}{v^{2}}+\omega^{2}-\rho^{2}=\frac{v^{\prime\prime}}{v}-\rho^{2}\,,

(39)

which is obtained from the first equation of (
20
) and
(
18
).

IV-C

Higher-Order Time Derivatives

The vectors of the Frenet frame constitute a basis for 3-dimensional
systems, hence any vector, including any time derivatives of the
voltage, current, and frequency can be written in terms of
\boldsymbol{\rm N}
,
\boldsymbol{\rm T}

and
\boldsymbol{\rm B}
. This can be deduced from (
33
). For example,
the second time derivative of
\boldsymbol{\rm T}
becomes:

\boldsymbol{\rm T}^{\prime\prime}=\omega^{\prime}\,\boldsymbol{\rm N}+\omega\,\boldsymbol{\rm N}^{\prime}=\omega^{\prime}\,\boldsymbol{\rm N}-\omega^{2}\,\boldsymbol{\rm T}+\omega\xi\,\boldsymbol{\rm B}\,,

(40)

or, equivalently, in terms of
(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{n},\boldsymbol{\omega})
:

\boldsymbol{v}^{\prime\prime}=(\rho^{\prime}+\rho^{2}-\omega^{2})\,\boldsymbol{v}-(2\rho-\eta)\,\boldsymbol{n}-v\xi\,\boldsymbol{\omega}\,.

(41)

While the complexity of the expressions of the coefficients of
higher-order derivatives increases, the following general expression
for the
r
-th derivative holds:

\boxed{\boldsymbol{v}^{(r)}=a_{r}\,\boldsymbol{v}+b_{r}\,\boldsymbol{n}+c_{r}\,\boldsymbol{\omega}}\,.

(42)

For example, for the first two derivatives, one has:

\displaystyle a_{1}

\displaystyle=\rho\,,

\displaystyle b_{1}

\displaystyle=1\,,

\displaystyle c_{1}

\displaystyle=0\,,

\displaystyle a_{2}

\displaystyle=\rho^{\prime}+\rho^{2}-\omega^{2}\,,

\displaystyle b_{2}

\displaystyle=2\rho-\eta\,,

\displaystyle c_{2}

\displaystyle=v\xi\,.

The following remarks are relevant.

•

Expressions with the same structure as (
42
) can be
written also for
\boldsymbol{n}^{(r)}
and
\boldsymbol{\omega}^{(r)}
.

•

Since
\boldsymbol{n}=\boldsymbol{\omega}\times\boldsymbol{v}
, equation (
42
)
is, in turn, a function exclusively of
\boldsymbol{v}
and
\boldsymbol{\omega}
.

V
Examples

This section illustrates the theoretical results above through special
cases that are relevant in circuit and power system analysis. The
first two examples are aimed at illustrating the properties of
(
30
) in the special cases of DC circuits and stationary AC
circuits. The subsequent examples show the effect of imbalances and
harmonics on the various components of the frequency of three-phase
voltages, as well as on the time derivative of the frequency itself.
In all examples below, we assume that voltages are curves in three
dimensions, where the basis, unless otherwise stated, is given by the
following orthonormal vectors:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\rm e}_{\rm 1}

\displaystyle=(1,0,0)\,,

(43)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\rm e}_{\rm 2}

\displaystyle=(0,1,0)\,,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\rm e}_{\rm 3}

\displaystyle=(0,0,1)\,.

As already said in the theoretical sections, systems with dimensions
higher than 3 can be also considered by using the wedge product rather
than the cross product and the generalization of the Frenet-Serret
formulas. However, the study of voltages with dimensions higher than
three is beyond the scope of this paper.

In Sections
V-C
to
V-E
, we show state-space
three-dimensional plots of the voltage
\boldsymbol{v}
as this quantity has a
clear physical meaning and it is widely used in practice. However, it
is important to keep in mind that, in the proposed framework, the
voltage is, in effect, the time derivative of a position (flux) vector

\boldsymbol{x}=-\boldsymbol{\varphi}
. The curvature and torsion, thus, are those
of such a flux vector, not of the voltage.

In the following, we do not discuss explicitly the behavior of the
vectors
\boldsymbol{\rm T}
,
\boldsymbol{\rm N}
and
\boldsymbol{\rm B}
. However, it is relevant to
observe that the voltage vector
\boldsymbol{v}
and the antisymmetric part of
the geometric frequency
\boldsymbol{\omega}
, are the tangent and binormal
vectors, namely
\boldsymbol{\rm T}
and
\boldsymbol{\rm B}
, of the Frenet frame before
normalization. Moreover,
\boldsymbol{n}
, which is the normal vector

of the Frenet frame before normalization, has an important
role in the definition of the time derivative of the voltage,

\boldsymbol{v}^{\prime}
, as it is the antisymmetric part of such a derivative.
Thus, the discussions on the behavior of
\boldsymbol{v}
,
\boldsymbol{v}^{\prime}
and

\boldsymbol{\omega}
in the examples below are, indirectly, also discussions
of the behavior of the vectors of the Frenet frame.

V-A

DC Voltage

As a first example, we show the effect of (
30
) on DC
voltages. From the geometrical point of view, a DC voltage is
equivalent to the time derivative of a curve with one dimension,
i.e. a straight line. Using the vector notation, in DC, the voltage
is a curve that has only one component along one direction, say

\boldsymbol{\rm e}_{\rm 1}
of the basis. Then:

\boldsymbol{v}=v_{\rm dc}\,\boldsymbol{\rm e}_{\rm 1}+0\,\boldsymbol{\rm e}_{\rm 2}+0\,\boldsymbol{\rm e}_{\rm 3}\,.

(44)

It is immediate to show that, for a curve in one dimension,

\kappa=\tau=0
,
\boldsymbol{\omega}=\boldsymbol{0}
and, the operator (
30
)
is reformulated as:

D^{\boldsymbol{v}}_{t}=\frac{d}{dt}-\rho\,.

(45)

Thinking in terms of curves, this result comes with no surprise, as a
straight line cannot rotate or twist. Only the radial component of
the velocity, thus, can be nonnull. It is also relevant to note that,
according to (
30
), expression (
45
) states that
the antisymmetric component of the time derivative of DC quantities is
always null.

V-B

Stationary Single-Phase AC Voltage

Let us consider a stationary single-phase voltage with constant
angular frequency
w_{o}
and magnitude
V
. Then the voltage vector
can be written as:

\boldsymbol{v}=V\cos(w_{o}t+\alpha)\,\boldsymbol{\rm e}_{\rm 1}+V\sin(w_{o}t+\alpha)\,\boldsymbol{\rm e}_{\rm 2}+0\,\boldsymbol{\rm e}_{\rm 3}\,,

(46)

where
\alpha
is a constant phase shift.

Note that the representation of the voltage in (
46
) is
that of an
analytic signal
, that is, the first component is
the signal itself, namely
u(t)=V\cos(w_{o}t+\alpha)
, whereas the
second component is the Hilbert transform of the signal, i.e.,

\mathcal{H}[u(t)]=V\sin(w_{o}t+\alpha)

[
29
,
30
]
. It is important to note that analytic signals are defined
as complex quantities rather than vectors but, nonetheless, they are
intrinsically two dimensional quantities. The utilization of the
Frenet framework and the interpretation of the signal as a

curve
is more general and, as shown in this example, admits
analytic signals as a special case.

It is also relevant to note that, a single phase AC voltage
represents, from the geometric point of view, a plane curve. This is
also consistent with the fact that an AC signal requires two
independent quantities to be defined, e.g., magnitude and phase angle
and, hence, the coordinate basis requires two dimensions to be
complete.
Applying the definitions given in the previous section, one can easily
find that:

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \rho&=0\,,&\qquad\boldsymbol{\omega}&=w_{o}\,\boldsymbol{\rm e}_{\rm 3}\,,\qquad\xi=0\,.\end{aligned}

(47)

These results were expected for a plane curve, which can rotate
(
\kappa=w_{o}/V\neq 0
) but cannot twist (
\tau=\xi/V=0
). On
the other hand,
\rho=0
is a consequence of the fact that

V=\rm const
. Moreover, from (
30
), one obtains:

D^{\boldsymbol{v}}_{t}=\frac{d}{dt}-w_{o}\,[\boldsymbol{\rm e}_{\rm 3}\times]\,.

(48)

It is worth to further elaborate on (
48
). We note first
that, since
\rho
,
\kappa
and
\tau
are geometric invariants, same
results can be obtained using any other orthonormal basis. In
particular, if one chooses a basis that rotates at constant angular
speed
w_{o}
:

\displaystyle\hat{\boldsymbol{\rm e}}_{\rm 1}

\displaystyle=(\cos(w_{o}t),0,0)\,,

(49)

\displaystyle\hat{\boldsymbol{\rm e}}_{\rm 2}

\displaystyle=(0,\sin(w_{o}t),0)\,,

\displaystyle\hat{\boldsymbol{\rm e}}_{\rm 3}

\displaystyle=(0,0,1)\,,

then the voltage vector becomes:

\boldsymbol{v}=V\cos(\alpha)\,\hat{\boldsymbol{\rm e}}_{\rm 1}+V\sin(\alpha)\,\hat{\boldsymbol{\rm e}}_{\rm 2}=v_{1}\,\hat{\boldsymbol{\rm e}}_{\rm 1}+v_{2}\,\hat{\boldsymbol{\rm e}}_{\rm 2}\,,

with
v=|\boldsymbol{v}|=V
and:

\boldsymbol{v}^{\prime}=w_{o}\,\hat{\boldsymbol{\rm e}}_{\rm 3}\times\boldsymbol{v}=-w_{o}v_{2}\,\hat{\boldsymbol{\rm e}}_{\rm 1}+w_{o}v_{1}\,\hat{\boldsymbol{\rm e}}_{\rm 2}\,,

(50)

where we have used the identities:

-\hat{\boldsymbol{\rm e}}_{\rm 1}=\hat{\boldsymbol{\rm e}}_{\rm 3}\times\hat{\boldsymbol{\rm e}}_{\rm 2}\,,\qquad\hat{\boldsymbol{\rm e}}_{\rm 2}=\hat{\boldsymbol{\rm e}}_{\rm 3}\times\hat{\boldsymbol{\rm e}}_{\rm 1}\,.

Equation (
50
) has a striking formal similarity with the
well-known expression in phasor-domain where the derivative is given
by
\jmath\,w_{o}
, where
\jmath
is the imaginary unit. As a matter of
fact, in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, the cross (wedge) vector is isomorphic to
complex numbers. Thus, one can define the following correspondences:

\displaystyle\hat{\boldsymbol{\rm e}}_{\rm 1}

\displaystyle\Rightarrow 1\,,

(51)

\displaystyle\hat{\boldsymbol{\rm e}}_{\rm 2}

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\jmath\,,

\displaystyle w_{o}[\hat{\boldsymbol{\rm e}}_{\rm 3}\times]

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\jmath\,w_{o}\,,

which leads to rewrite the vector
\boldsymbol{v}
as the well-known phasors,
namely
\bar{v}=v_{1}+\jmath\,v_{2}
.
We note also that, in two dimensions,
|\boldsymbol{\omega}|=w_{o}
hence the
azimuthal frequency coincides with the well-known angular frequency of
circuit analysis and with the
instantaneous frequency
as
commonly defined in time-frequency analysis and signal processing
based on analytic signals
[
28
,
29
]
.

Noteworthy, this observation can be generalized for any signal
u(t)

for which the Hilbert transforms
\mathcal{H}[u(t)]
exists. With
this assumption, the analytic signal associated with
u(t)
is the
following complex quantity:

\bar{u}(t)=u(t)+j\mathcal{H}[u(t)]=u(t)+j\hat{u}(t)\,,

(52)

the instantaneous frequency of which is defined as
[
28
]
:

\phi^{\prime}(t)=\frac{\hat{u}^{\prime}(t)u(t)-u^{\prime}(t)\hat{u}(t)}{u^{2}(t)+\hat{u}^{2}(t)}\,,

(53)

where

\phi(t)={\rm arctan}\left(\frac{\hat{u}(t)}{u(t)}\right)

(54)

is the phase angle of
\bar{u}(t)
. Using the approach proposed in
this work, on the other hand, we define the vector:

\boldsymbol{u}(t)=u(t)\,\boldsymbol{\rm e}_{\rm 1}+\hat{u}(t)\,\boldsymbol{\rm e}_{\rm 2}+0\,\boldsymbol{\rm e}_{\rm 3}\,,

(55)

which leads to:

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \rho&=\frac{u^{\prime}u+\hat{u}^{\prime}\hat{u}}{u^{2}+\hat{u}^{2}}\,,&\quad\boldsymbol{\omega}&=\frac{\hat{u}^{\prime}u-u^{\prime}\hat{u}}{u^{2}+\hat{u}^{2}}\,\boldsymbol{\rm e}_{\rm 3}\,,&\quad\xi&=0\,,\end{aligned}

(56)

where the time dependency has been omitted for simplicity. Both
formulations, thus, leads to the same expression for the instantaneous
frequency, i.e.,
\phi^{\prime}=|\boldsymbol{\omega}|
. However, the proposed vector-based
approach is more general as it allows defining the quantities
\rho

and
\xi
and is not limited to two dimensions.

V-C

Three-Phase AC Voltages

In this section, we consider a three-phase AC system. A possible
approach is to utilize the same coordinates that we have considered
for the single-phase AC voltage of the previous example. This
approach is the one commonly utilized in circuit analysis. In this
section, however, we show that choosing as coordinates the voltages of
each phase leads to interesting results. Hence, we define the voltage
vector as:

\boldsymbol{v}=v_{a}\,\boldsymbol{\rm e}_{\rm 1}+v_{b}\,\boldsymbol{\rm e}_{\rm 2}+v_{c}\boldsymbol{\rm e}_{\rm 3}\,.

(57)

Let us first consider the case of voltages represented by a single
harmonic:

\displaystyle v_{a}

\displaystyle=V_{a}\,\sin(\theta_{a})\,,

(58)

\displaystyle v_{b}

\displaystyle=V_{b}\,\sin(\theta_{b})\,,

\displaystyle v_{c}

\displaystyle=V_{c}\,\sin(\theta_{c})\,.

The expressions of
\rho
,
\boldsymbol{\omega}
and
\xi
for (
58
)
are:

\rho=\frac{\sum_{i}V_{i}^{2}\theta^{\prime}_{i}\sin(2\theta_{i})+V_{i}V^{\prime}_{i}(1-\cos(2\theta_{i}))}{v^{2}}\,,

(59)

\boldsymbol{\omega}=\frac{\sum_{ijk}(r_{jk}+u_{jk})\,\boldsymbol{\rm e}_{i}}{v^{2}}\,,

(60)

\xi=\frac{v\,\sum_{i}(p_{i}\sin(\theta_{i})+q_{i}\cos(\theta_{i}))\,\omega_{i}}{\sum_{jk}(r_{jk}+u_{jk})^{2}}\,,

(61)

where
i\in\{a,b,c\}
,
ijk\in\{abc,bca,cab\}
,

jk\in\{bc,ca,ab\}
and:

\displaystyle v

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\textstyle\sum_{i}V_{i}^{2}(1-\cos(2\theta_{i}))}\,,

\displaystyle r_{jk}

\displaystyle=(V_{j}V^{\prime}_{k}-V_{k}V^{\prime}_{j})\sin(\theta_{j})\sin(\theta_{k})\,,

\displaystyle u_{jk}

\displaystyle=V_{j}V_{k}(\theta^{\prime}_{k}\sin(\theta_{j})\cos(\theta_{k})-\theta^{\prime}_{j}\sin(\theta_{k})\cos(\theta_{j}))\,,

\displaystyle p_{i}

\displaystyle=V_{i}V_{i}^{\prime\prime}+(V^{\prime}_{i})^{2}-V_{i}(\theta^{\prime}_{i})^{2}\,,

\displaystyle q_{i}

\displaystyle=V^{\prime}_{i}\theta^{\prime}_{i}-V_{i}\theta_{i}^{\prime\prime}\,.

The following remarks are relevant.

Remark 1

In general,
\rho
,
\boldsymbol{\omega}
and
\xi
depend on
the time derivatives of both the magnitudes and the phase angles of
the three-phase voltages. This result is counter-intuitive. In the
common understanding, in fact, the angular frequency is defined as the
time derivative of the sole phase angle of the voltage (see

[
31
]
). The conventional expression of the angular frequency
is obtained if
V^{\prime}_{i}=0
. This result reiterates that the common
definition of “frequency” is, in effect, a special case of the
framework proposed in this paper.

Remark 2

It is possible to have
\rho=0
and

\boldsymbol{\omega}\neq\boldsymbol{0}
(for example, the obvious case of balanced and
stationary three-phase voltages, which is illustrated below) but also

\rho\neq 0
and
\boldsymbol{\omega}=\boldsymbol{0}
(for example a balanced voltage with

V_{i}^{\prime}\neq 0
).

Remark 3

The torsional frequency
\xi
is non-null if and
only if
\boldsymbol{\omega}\neq\boldsymbol{0}
. This is consequence of the expression of the
torsion given in (
25
). The condition
\xi\neq 0
holds for

V_{i}^{\prime\prime}\neq 0
and/or
\theta_{i}^{\prime\prime}\neq 0
.

Remark 4

A question might arise on why the base
considered in Section
V-B
for the single-phase AC system is
not also utilized for the three-phase system. One can, of course,
consider each phase of the three-phase system separately, effectively
considering each phase as a single-phase system as in the previous
example. This is the common approach in three-phase circuit analysis
based on phasors. However, in this and in the following examples, the
three-phase voltages are assumed to form a three-dimensional vector.
It is relevant to note that, since
\rho
,
\kappa
and
\tau
are
geometric invariants, it does not matter which coordinates one chooses
as long as these coordinates form a complete basis for the system.

where

Positive and negative sequence voltages

From (

), one has
for the stationary positive sequence with
V_{a}=V_{b}=V_{c}=\rm const.

\theta_{bo}=-\theta_{co}=-{2\pi}/{3}

Analogously, the stationary negative sequence, namely

V_{a}=V_{b}=V_{c}=\rm const.

\theta_{bo}=-\theta_{co}={2\pi}/{3}

\rho=\xi=0\,,\quad\boldsymbol{\omega}=-\frac{w_{o}}{\sqrt{3}}(\boldsymbol{\rm e}_{\rm 1}+\boldsymbol{\rm e}_{\rm 2}+\boldsymbol{\rm e}_{\rm 3})\,.

Hence, for the stationary positive and negative sequences, the time

30

Unbalanced voltage

Unbalanced voltages are characterized by
V_{a}\neq V_{b}\neq V_{c}

and/or
\theta_{bo}\neq-\theta_{co}\neq-2\pi/3
rad. Then, assuming that
the voltage magnitudes and phase shifts are constants, from
(

)-(

\displaystyle\rho

\displaystyle=\frac{w_{o}\sum_{i}V_{i}^{2}\sin(2\theta_{i})}{v^{2}}\,,\quad

\displaystyle\xi=0\,,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\omega}

\displaystyle=\frac{w_{o}\sum_{ijk}V_{j}V_{k}\sin(\theta_{j}-\theta_{k})\boldsymbol{\rm e}_{\rm i}}{v^{2}}\,.

In this case, thus, the components of the time derivative in
(
28
) depend on the parameters of the voltage.

Zero-sequence voltage

This example allows us discussing the issue of the choice of the basis
for the voltage vector.
A zero-sequence voltage is composed of three equal AC voltages. In
this case, thus, we cannot utilize the same coordinates we have used
in the previous two examples, namely
(v_{a},v_{b},v_{c})

linearly dependent and do not form a basis. One has to proceed as
discussed in Section
V-B
for a single-phase AC voltage, for

voltages, one should thus first remove the zero-sequence from the
voltage signals. This is common practice, anyway, for the
zero-sequence to be generally treated separately or simply just
removed from the positive and negative ones in power system
measurements and protections.

) through the following

Park Transform

In this final example, we consider the time derivative of the voltage
in the Park reference frame. Let us consider a voltage vector in the

\rm dqo
coordinates:

\boldsymbol{v}=v_{\rm d}\,\boldsymbol{\rm e}_{\rm d}+v_{\rm q}\,\boldsymbol{\rm e}_{\rm q}+v_{\rm o}\,\boldsymbol{\rm e}_{\rm o}\,.

(66)

The time derivative of this vector in the “inertial” reference is
given by:

\boldsymbol{v}^{\prime}=(v^{\prime}_{\rm d}-w_{\rm dq}v_{\rm q})\,\boldsymbol{\rm e}_{\rm d}+(v^{\prime}_{\rm q}+w_{\rm dq}v_{\rm d})\,\boldsymbol{\rm e}_{\rm q}+v^{\prime}_{\rm o}\,\boldsymbol{\rm e}_{\rm o}\,,

(67)

where
w_{\rm dq}
is the angular speed of the Park reference frame and we
have assumed a choice of the
\rm dqo
-axis such that

\boldsymbol{\rm e}^{\prime}_{\rm d}=w_{\rm dq}\boldsymbol{\rm e}_{\rm q}
,
\boldsymbol{\rm e}^{\prime}_{\rm q}=-w_{\rm dq}\boldsymbol{\rm e}_{\rm d}
, and
\boldsymbol{\rm e}^{\prime}_{\rm o}=\boldsymbol{0}

[
1
]
.

The time derivative in the inertial reference in (
67
)
is composed of two parts, namely the derivative in the rotating

\rm dqo
-axis frame plus the effect of rotation:

\boldsymbol{v}^{\prime}=\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}^{\prime}+\boldsymbol{r}\times\boldsymbol{v}\,,

(68)

where:

\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}^{\prime}=v^{\prime}_{\rm d}\,\boldsymbol{\rm e}_{\rm d}+v^{\prime}_{\rm q}\,\boldsymbol{\rm e}_{\rm q}+v^{\prime}_{\rm o}\,\boldsymbol{\rm e}_{\rm o}\,,

(69)

and

\boldsymbol{r}=w_{\rm dq}\,\boldsymbol{\rm e}_{\rm o}\,.

(70)

Next, we apply (
28
) and show that the proposed approach
leads to the same results as (
67
) but with a different
structure of the components of
\boldsymbol{v}^{\prime}
with respect to
(
68
). The quantities
\rho
and
\boldsymbol{\omega}
are:

\rho=\frac{v_{\rm d}v^{\prime}_{\rm d}+v_{\rm q}v^{\prime}_{\rm q}+v_{\rm o}v^{\prime}_{\rm o}}{v^{2}}\,,

(71)

and

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\omega}=

\displaystyle\frac{v_{\rm q}v^{\prime}_{\rm o}-v_{\rm o}v^{\prime}_{\rm q}-w_{\rm dq}v_{\rm o}v_{\rm d}}{v^{2}}\,\boldsymbol{\rm e}_{\rm d}+

(72)

\displaystyle\frac{v_{\rm o}v^{\prime}_{\rm d}-v_{\rm d}v^{\prime}_{\rm o}-w_{\rm dq}v_{\rm o}v_{\rm q}}{v^{2}}\,\boldsymbol{\rm e}_{\rm q}+

\displaystyle\frac{v_{\rm d}v^{\prime}_{\rm q}-v_{\rm q}v^{\prime}_{\rm d}+w_{\rm dq}(v_{\rm d}^{2}+v_{\rm q}^{2})}{v^{2}}\,\boldsymbol{\rm e}_{\rm o}\,,

where
v^{2}=v_{\rm d}^{2}+v_{\rm q}^{2}+v_{o}^{2}
.
Although requiring some tedious algebraic manipulations, it is not
difficult to show that, in fact,
\rho\boldsymbol{v}+\boldsymbol{\omega}\times\boldsymbol{v}
is
equal to the right-hand side of (
67
). On the other
hand, it is straightforward to observe that:

\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}^{\prime}\neq\rho\boldsymbol{v}\,,\qquad\boldsymbol{r}\times\boldsymbol{v}\neq\boldsymbol{\omega}\times\boldsymbol{v}\,,

(73)

i.e.,
\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}^{\prime}
is not equal to the symmetric component of the time
derivative and the term
\boldsymbol{r}\times\boldsymbol{v}
is not equal to the
antisymmetric component of the time derivative.

A relevant case is for
v_{o}=0
, namely balanced conditions, which
leads to:

\rho=\frac{v_{\rm d}v_{\rm d}^{\prime}+v_{\rm q}v_{\rm q}^{\prime}}{v^{2}}\,,\quad\boldsymbol{\omega}=\frac{v_{\rm d}v^{\prime}_{\rm q}-v_{\rm q}v^{\prime}_{\rm d}+w_{\rm dq}v^{2}}{v^{2}}\,\boldsymbol{\rm e}_{\rm o}\,,

(74)

where
v^{2}=v_{\rm d}^{2}+v_{\rm q}^{2}
. We note that
\rho=v^{\prime}/v
, as expected,
and:

\frac{v_{\rm d}v^{\prime}_{\rm q}-v^{\prime}_{\rm d}v_{\rm q}}{v^{2}}=\frac{d}{dt}\arctan\left(\frac{v_{\rm q}}{v_{\rm d}}\right)=\Delta\omega\,,

(75)

which is the deviation of the angular frequency of
\boldsymbol{v}
with
respect to
w_{\rm dq}
. Hence the inertial- and rotating-frame time
derivatives of the voltage can be written as:

\boldsymbol{v}^{\prime}=\rho\boldsymbol{v}+(w_{\rm dq}+\Delta\omega)\,\boldsymbol{\rm e}_{\rm o}\times\boldsymbol{v}\,,

(76)

and:

\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}^{\prime}=\rho\boldsymbol{v}+\Delta\omega\,\boldsymbol{\rm e}_{\rm o}\times\boldsymbol{v}\,.

(77)

The following remarks are relevant.

Remark 5

The only case for which
\rho\boldsymbol{v}=\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}^{\prime}
and

\boldsymbol{r}\times\boldsymbol{v}=\boldsymbol{\omega}\times\boldsymbol{v}
is if the angular speed of
the Park transform is the actual frequency of
\boldsymbol{v}
. In this case,
in fact,
\Delta\omega=0
. This result is consistent with the
commonly-used Park-Concordia model of the synchronous machine

[
34
]
.

Remark 6

The Clarke transform can be viewed as a special
case of the Park transform, with
w_{\rm dq}=0
. In this case
(
68
) leads to
\boldsymbol{v}^{\prime}=\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}^{\prime}
. This result is
consistent with (
76
).

Remark 7

In stationary conditions, i.e., for

v^{\prime}_{\rm d}=v^{\prime}_{\rm q}=0
, (
76
) leads to the same expression as
(
50
), thus confirming that balanced stationary
three-phase voltages are equivalent to a single-phase phasor.

VI
Conclusions

The paper elaborates on the geometrical interpretation of electric
quantities and deduces several expressions that link the time
derivatives of the voltage, current and frequency in electrical
circuits with the Frenet frame. Among these expressions, we mention
in particular (
28
) and (
37
). Equation
(
28
) indicates that the time derivative of the voltage (and
the current) is composed of two parts, one symmetric, that depends
only on the magnitude, and one antisymmetric that depends on the
“rotation” of the quantity itself. Equation (
37
) shows
that the time derivative of the vector frequency is more complex than
the common notion of
rocof
and includes a “rotational” and a
“torsional” component. The latter is defined in this paper for the
first time. It is interesting to note that the antisymmetric
component of the
rocof
may affect the implementation and/or
performance of existing controllers. Since the proposed approach
allows separating the symmetric and antisymmetric terms, it appears as
a useful tool for the study of power system transients and the design
of controllers. More in general, we believe that the proposed
approach may find relevant applications in estimation, control and
stability analysis of power systems.

The proposed theory is certainly more complex than the current
conventional approach based on phasors. However, it shows added
values from the theoretical point of view, as follows.

•

It is a generalization of the conventional approach. The
conventional approach, in fact, appears to be a special case of the
proposed theory.

•

It is an example of interdisciplinary approach. Differential
geometry and the Frenet frame, in fact, were originally developed
for mechanical systems. Their applications, under certain
hypotheses, to electrical circuits appears as an interesting advance
which paves the way to several further developments.

An interesting byproduct of the latter point is that the proposed
theory allows “visualizing” electrical quantities. This is
important, as, in the experience of the first author, students always
struggle with the lack of visual aid when studying circuit theory.
Such a support is a given in mechanical engineering. Thus, the
ability to re-utilize well-known concepts such as curvature and torsion
also adds a didactic value to the proposed approach.

We anticipate several future work directions. Among these, we mention
the development of a geometric framework for circuit analysis; the
applications of the formulas to estimate unbalanced conditions in
three-phase circuits as well as to circuits with more than three
phases using Cartan’s extensions of the Frenet framework (see, e.g.,

[
35
]
); and the development of active controllers to
reduce the effect of harmonics and imbalances.
