Effective estimation of the dimension of a manifold from random samples
Lucien Grillet, Juan Souto

Abstract
. We give explicit theoretical and heuristical bounds for how big does a data set sampled from a reach- 1 1 submanifold M M of euclidian space need to be, to be able to estimate the dimension of M M with 90 % 90\% confidence.



1.
Introduction

The
manifold hypothesis
asserts that naturally ocurring data sets
X\subset\mathbb{R}^{s}
behave as if they had been sampled from a low-dimensional submanifold
M
. There is then a large literature on
manifold learning
, that is on understanding how properties of the underlying manifold
M
can be ”learned” from the data set
X
—see for example
[
3
,
15
,
22
,
21
,
5
,
20
,
7
]
—, but it is maybe fair to say that estimating the dimension
\dim(M)
of the manifold, that is figuring out the
intrinsic dimension
of the data set, seems to be of particular interest. This is maybe so because the dimension is one of the most basic quantities associated to a manifold, but maybe also because of the importance of understanding the applicability to the data set
X
of different dimension reduction schemes. In any case, there are very numerous estimators for the intrinsic dimension of a data set. We refer to the surveys
[
9
,
10
,
7
]
for a brief discussion of many of those estimators and to the references therein for details—see also Chapter 3 in the monograph
[
20
]
. Anyways, all these dimension estimators are based on the idea that manifolds look locally like their tangent spaces, that is like euclidean space. For example, non-linear PCA aims at finding the dimension of tangent spaces. ANOVA aims at computing the average angle between vectors in a tangent space, exploiting the fact that for euclidean space itself one can read the dimension from the expected value. The estimator (
1.1
) below, as well as closely related algorithms due to Takens
[
25
,
26
]
, Theiler
[
27
]
or Grassberger-Procaccia
[
18
]
, exploits the fact that the
correlation dimension
of the manifold is just its dimension. There are other such estimators where the correlation dimension is replaced by the
Box counting dimension
or the
Kolmogorov capacity
. This list of estimators is far from being all inclusive.

All those estimators work very well—in the sense that they yield with high probability the desired result—as long as we apply them with common sense and under suitable conditions, that is if

1)

we work at a scale at which the manifold
M
resembles euclidean space, and

2)

the data set
X
is large enough and the points therein have been obtained by uniform sampling.

However, amazingle little is known when it comes to quantifying 1) or 2). Indeed, there are classical estimates due to Grassberger
[
17
]
, Procaccia
[
23
]
, and Eckmann-Ruelle
[
13
]
giving absolute lower bounds, in terms of the dimension, for the number of measurements needed for the results to be reliable: Grassberger argues that there are absolute lower bounds at all, Procaccia argues that if the intrindic dimension is
\dim
then one might need a data set of at least
10^{\dim}
measurements, while Eckmann-Ruelle suggest a lower bound of the form
C\cdot 10^{\frac{1}{2}\dim}
for some large but undetermined
C
. One can summarize these results as asserting that, when the dimension grows, the minimal cardinality of a data set allowing to compute the dimension grows exponentially—this is indeed already the case (see
[
28
]
) when one just wants to distinguish between spheres of consecutive dimensions.

The absolute lower bounds for the data size that we just mention do not help however with the following less philosophical question:
I suspect, or hypothesize, that my data set is sampled out of a manifold with this or that properties. To what extent can I trust the intrinsic dimension estimation given by this or that estimator?
The only result we know along those lines is due to Niyogi-Smale-Weinberger
[
22
]
, at least as long as one wants to allow for variable curvature manifolds. In
[
22
]
the authors give namely an algorithm to compute the homology of a closed submanifold
M\subset\mathbb{R}^{s}
out of a set
X
sampled from
M
, and they estimated how large does the data size have to be so that their algorithm has success in at least
90\%
of the cases—observe that knowing the homology we also know the dimension of the manifold. A problem is that the needed data size is astronomical. For example, the estimate in
[
22
]
for the number of points needed to be sampled to compute with
90\%
probability of success the homology of the 4-dimensional Clifford torus
\mathbb{T}^{4}=\mathbb{S}^{1}\times\mathbb{S}^{1}\times\mathbb{S}^{1}\times\mathbb{S}^{1}
is 24.967.788 points, that is about 25 million points.

One might contend that the Niyosi-Smale-Weinberger algorithm computes something much more sophisticated than the dimension, that they are basically learning the whole manifold, and that being able to do that is an overkill if what one wants to do is to estimate its dimension. We agree with that point of view. We will consider the estimator

(1.1)

\dim_{\Corr(\varepsilon_{1},\varepsilon_{2})}(X)=\round\left(\frac{\log|PX(\varepsilon_{1})|-\log|PX(\varepsilon_{2})|}{\log(\varepsilon_{1})-\log(\varepsilon_{2})}\right)

where

(1.2)

PX(\varepsilon)=\big\{\{x,y\}\subset X\text{ with }0<|x-y|\leqslant\varepsilon\big\},

and our goal will be to estimate how large does
X\subset M
need to be for the estimator (
1.1
) to have a
90\%
success rate. It is definitively much smaller. For example, applying (
1.1
) to randomly sampled data sets in
\mathbb{T}^{4}

18.262

Comparisson with other estimators

In Section

2.
The estimator

In this section we recall what is the correlation dimension and how it can be approximated to obtain dimension estimators, but first we introduce some notation that we will use through out the paper. First, distances in eulidean space will be denoted either by
d_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(x,y)
or by
|x-y|
. If
x
and
y
are points in a Riemannian manifold
M
then
d_{M}(x,y)
is their distance with respect to the Riemannian metric. The ball in
M
of radius
R
centered at some point
x\in M
is denoted by
B^{M}(x,R)
, although sometimes, when there is no risk of confussion, we drop the subscript writing thus simply
B(x,R)
. Again, if there is no risk of confussion with the dimension, balls in the ambient euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{s}
will be denoted by blackboard bold, that is
\mathbb{B}(x,R)=B^{\mathbb{R}^{s}}(x,R)
. Finally, the intersection of balls in the ambient space
\mathbb{R}^{s}
with the manifold
M
will be denoted by

\mathbb{B}^{M}(x,R)=M\cap\mathbb{B}(x,R)

With this notation in place, we turn our attention to the correlation dimension and the estimator (
1.1
).

As we mentioned earlier, there are plenty of dimension estimators (see
[
9
,
10
]
and
[
20
, Chapter 3]
). In the language of
[
10
]
, (
1.1
) is a
fractal-based estimator
. Fractal-based because what one is aiming at, is computing a dimension which makes sense for fractal objects—in this case the correlation dimension. Recall that the
correlation integral
at scale
\varepsilon
of a Borel measure
\mu
on
\mathbb{R}^{s}
is the integral

C(\mu,\varepsilon)=\int\mu(\mathbb{B}(x,\varepsilon))\,d\mu(x)

The
upper correlation dimension
and
lower correlation dimension
are then defined as

D^{+}(\mu)=\limsup_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{\log(C(\mu,\varepsilon))}{\log(\varepsilon)}\text{ and }D^{-}(\mu)=\liminf_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{\log(C(\mu,\varepsilon))}{\log(\varepsilon)}.

When both of them agree, then one refers to

D(\mu)=D^{+}(\mu)=D^{-}(\mu)

as the
correlation dimension
of
\mu
. There are numerous situations of interest in dynamics
[
24
]
in which the correlation dimension of a measure exists. It is also trivial that it exists if
\mu
is a smooth measure whose support is a submanifold of euclidean space. Since this is the case in which we will find ourselves, we state this fact as a lemma:

Lemma 2.1
.

If
\mu
is a smooth finite measure of full support of a
d
-dimensional submanifold
M\subset\mathbb{R}^{s}
, then the correlation dimension exists and we have
D(\mu)=d
.∎

In
[
18
]
Grassberger and Procaccia note that if
\varepsilon>0
is small enough and if we have a set
X=(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
consisting of
n
points (
n
very large) sampled independently with respect to the measure
\mu
then
D(\mu)
is approximated by

(2.3)

\dim_{GP}(X,\varepsilon)=\frac{\log\left(\frac{|DX(\varepsilon)|}{n(n-1)}\right)}{\log(\varepsilon)}

where

(2.4)

DX(\varepsilon)=\{(x_{i},x_{j})\in X\times X\text{ with }i\neq j\text{ and }|x_{i}-x_{j}|\leqslant\varepsilon\}

is the set of (ordered) pairs of points in
X
within
\varepsilon
of each other. Grassberger and Procaccia propopose (
2.3
) as an estimator for the intrinsic dimension of the data set
X
—variants are discussed by Takens
[
25
,
26
]
and Theiler
[
27
]
. We will however focus here on the version from
[
20
]
, or more precisely the quantity

(2.5)

\dim_{\Corr(\varepsilon_{1},\varepsilon_{2})}(X)=\round\left(\frac{\log|DX(\varepsilon_{1})|-\log|DX(\varepsilon_{2})|}{\log\varepsilon_{1}-\log\varepsilon_{2}}\right)

Note that this expression is nothing other than (
1.1
) above.

A problem when using (
2.5
) as an estimator, or for that matter when we use (
2.3
), is that we have to pick up the appropriate scales. In practial implementations, this is often by-passed by taking many possible scales
\varepsilon_{1}<\varepsilon_{2}<\dots<\varepsilon_{k}
, computing
|DX(\varepsilon_{i})|
for each one of those scales, plotting the result in a log-log-plot and choosing what looks to us as the slope at some region where the slope looks constant. In practice, at least when tested on synthetic data, the log-log plot method is surprisingly effective, but it is very unclear how one can get out of that a formal statistical test, or how can one evaluate how much confidence can one have on the obtained number. Theorem
1.1
from the introduction gives such bounds, at least if the manifold we are working with is sufficiently regular, in the sense of having
reach
\tau(M)\geqslant 1
. We discuss a few aspects of the geometry of the reach in the next section.

3.
Some Geometry

In this section we recall a few facts about the geometry of reach-1 submanifolds
M
of euclidean space. Combining these facts with standard arguments from Riemannian geometry we give upper and lower bounds for the volume of the
\varepsilon
fat diagonal in
M\times M
.

3.1.
Reach-1 manifolds

We start recalling the definition of the
reach
of a closed subset of euclidean space:

Definition 1
.

The reach
\tau(S)
of a closed subset
S\subset\mathbb{R}^{s}
is the supremum of those
T\geqslant 0
with the property that for every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{s}
with
d_{\mathbb{R}^{s}}(x,S)\leqslant T
there is a unique point in
S
closest to
x
.

Since the reach was introduced by Federer in
[
14
]
, it has proved to be a useful notion. Indeed, it follows from the very definition that positive reach sets, that is sets
S
with
\tau(S)>0
, have some neighborhood
\mathcal{N}(S)
on which the closest point projection
\pi:\mathcal{N}(S)\to S
is well-defined—the existence of such a projection is enough to show that sets of positive reach share many regularity properties with convex sets.

Here we will be working from the very beginning with very regular objects, namely closed smooth submanifolds
M\subset\mathbb{R}^{s}
of euclidean space. In this setting the reach, or rather a lower bound for the reach, helps to quantify how distorted is the inner geometry of
M
with respect to that of the ambient euclidean space. We summarize what we will need in the following proposition:

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
M\subset\mathbb{R}^{s}
be a closed submanifold with reach
\tau(M)\geqslant 1
. Then we have:

(1)

d_{M}(x,y)\leqslant 2\arcsin\left(\frac{|x-y|}{2}\right)
for any two
x,y\in M
with
|x-y|<2
.

(2)

The set
\mathbb{B}^{M}(x,r)=M\cap\mathbb{B}(x,r)
is geodesically convex for any
x\in\mathbb{R}^{s}
and any
r<1
.

(3)

We have
\angle\big(\gamma^{\prime}(0),\gamma^{\prime}(t)\big)\leqslant d_{M}(\gamma(0),\gamma(\ell))
for every geodesic
\gamma:[0,\ell]\to M
parametrized by arc length.

(4)

The manifold
M
has sectional curvature pinched by
-2\leqslant\kappa_{M}\leqslant 1
.

See, in that order Lemma 3, Corollary 1 and Lemma 5 in
[
6
]
for the first three claims of Proposition
3.1
. See then Proposition A.1 (iii) in
[
1
]
for the final claim. In any case, we refer to
[
1
]
and
[
6
]
, and to the references therein for general facts about submanifolds
M\subset\mathbb{R}^{s}
of positive reach.

3.2.
Injectivity radius

Armed with Proposition
3.1
we can now derive a lower bound for the injectivity radius of those submanifolds
M\subset\mathbb{R}^{s}
with reach
\tau(M)\geqslant 1
. Recall that the
injectivity radius
of a geodesically complete Riemannian manifold
M
at a point
x\in M
is defined as

\inj(M,x)=\sup\{t>0\ |\ \exp_{x}:T_{x}M\to M\text{ is injective on }B^{T_{x}M}(0,t)\}

where
B^{T_{x}M}(0,t)=\{v\in T_{x}M\text{ with }|v|\leqslant r\}
and where
\exp_{x}
is the Riemannian exponential map. The
injectivity radius
of
M
itself is then defined to be

\inj(M)=\inf_{x\in M}\inj(M,x).

Recall also that the
systole

\syst(M)
of a closed manifold
M
is the length of the shortest non-trivial closed geodesic. The importance of the systole now is that, together with an upper bound
\kappa_{M}\leqslant\kappa
for the sectional curvature, the systole yields very a usable bound
[
4
, Thm. 89]
for the injectivity radius:

\inj(M)\geqslant\min\left\{\frac{\pi}{\sqrt{\kappa}},\frac{1}{2}\syst(M)\right\}.

Altogether, see
[
4
]
for basic facts and definitions from Riemannian geometry.

Suppose now that
M\subset\mathbb{R}^{s}
is a closed submanifold of euclidean space with
\tau(M)\geqslant 1
, and note the that assumption that
M
is closed implies that it is metrically complete and thus geodesically complete by the Hopf-Rinow theorem. On the other hand if
\gamma:\mathbb{S}^{1}\to M\subset\mathbb{R}^{s}
is any smooth curve and if
t
is such that
|\gamma(t)-\gamma(0)|
is maximal, then
\langle\gamma^{\prime}(0),\gamma^{\prime}(t)\rangle=0
. We thus get from (3) in Proposition
3.1
that every closed geodesic reaches at least distance
\frac{\pi}{2}
, and hence that

\syst(M)\geqslant\pi.

Now, this fact together with the upper bound for the sectional curvature from (4) in Proposition
3.1
and with the lower bound for the injectivity radius yields the following:

Corollary 3.2
.

If
M\subset\mathbb{R}^{s}
is a closed submanifold of euclidean space with reach
\tau(M)\geqslant 1
, then we have
\inj(M)\geqslant\frac{\pi}{2}
.∎

It should be noted that if
\dim(M)=1
and
\tau(M)\geqslant 1
then
\syst(M)\geqslant 2\pi
and hence
\inj(M)\geqslant\pi
. The standard circle shows that this is optimal. We do not know by how much can one improve the bound given in Corollary
3.2
in other dimensions.

3.3.
Volumes of balls

Although we will be mostly interested in the volume of sets
\mathbb{B}^{M}(x,r)=M\cap\mathbb{B}(x,r)
we start by considering the volume of actual metric balls in the manifold. First note that having bounds on the curvature, we also get bounds on the volumes of balls, at least as long as the radius remains below the injectivity radius. More precisely, if
M
is a Riemannian manifold of dimension
d\geqslant 2
and with curvature pinched in
[-2,1]
then we have

(3.6)

\vol(B^{\mathbb{S}^{d}}(r))\leqslant\vol(B^{M}(x,r))\leqslant\vol(B^{\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\mathbb{H}^{d}}(r))

for all
r<\inj_{M}(x)
(see
[
4
, Thm. 103]
and
[
4
, Thm. 107]
). Here, the sphere
\mathbb{S}^{d}
and the scaled hyperbolic space
\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\mathbb{H}^{d}
are respectively the simply connected complete
d
-dimensional manifolds of constant curvature
1
and
-2
. Having bounds for the volumes of balls we also have bounds for the ratios between volumes of balls of different radius. We can get however somewhat better results:

Proposition 3.3
.

Suppose that
M
is a
d
-dimensional (
d\geqslant 2
) Riemannian manifold with sectional curvature pinched in
[-2,1]
. We then have

\frac{R}{r}\cdot\left(\frac{\sin(R)}{\sin(r)}\right)^{d-1}\leqslant\frac{\vol(B^{M}(R,x))}{\vol(B^{M}(r,x))}\leqslant\frac{\int_{0}^{\sqrt{2}\cdot R}\sinh^{d-1}(t)\ dt}{\int_{0}^{\sqrt{2}\cdot r}\sinh^{d-1}(t)\ dt}

for any two
0<r<R<\min\{\pi,\inj(M)\}
and any
x\in M
.

Proof.

We begin with the upper bound. We get from the curvature bound
\kappa\geqslant-2
and the Bishop-Gromov comparison theorem
[
4
, p.310]
that

\frac{\vol(B^{M}(x,R))}{\vol(B^{M}(x,r))}\leqslant\frac{\vol(B^{\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\mathbb{H}^{d}}(R))}{\vol(B^{\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\mathbb{H}^{d}}(r))}=\frac{\int_{0}^{\sqrt{2}\cdot R}\sinh^{d-1}(t)\ dt}{\int_{0}^{\sqrt{2}\cdot r}\sinh^{d-1}(t)\ dt}

for all
x\in M
, as we wanted.

Let us now deal with the lower bound. Well, the fact that
M
has curvature pinched from above by
1
implies that
M
is is locally a CAT(
1
)-space—see
[
8
,
11
]
for facts about CAT(
\kappa
)-spaces and comparisson geometry. Recall now that we are working at a scale smaller than
\pi
and the injectivity radius. In particular, the CAT(
1
) property implies that geodesic triangles we encounter are thinner in our manifold than in
\mathbb{S}^{d}
. This implies in particular that, for
0<r<R<\min\{\pi,\inj(M)\}
, the radial projection

\proj:S^{M}(x,R)\to S^{M}(x,r),\ \ \proj(y)=\exp_{x}(r\cdot R^{-1}\cdot\exp_{x}^{-1}(y))

contracts distances more (expands distances less) than the corresponding map in the sphere, meaning that
\proj
is
\frac{\sin(r)}{\sin(R)}
-Lipschitz. We deduce thus that

\mathrm{Area}(S^{M}(x,r))\leqslant\left(\frac{\sin(r)}{\sin(R)}\right)^{d-1}\cdot\mathrm{Area}(S^{M}(x,R))

where
S^{M}(x,r)=\partial B^{M}(x,r)
is the distance-
r
-sphere in
M
and
\mathrm{Area}(\cdot)
stands for the
(d-1)
-dimensional volume. Anyways, if we set
T=\frac{r}{R}
then we get from the co-area formula that

\displaystyle\vol(B^{M}(x,r))

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{r}\mathrm{Area}(S^{M}(x,t))\ dt

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle s=\frac{1}{T}t}}{{=}}\int_{0}^{R}\mathrm{Area}(S^{M}(x,T\cdot s))\cdot T\ ds

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{r}{R}\cdot\int_{0}^{R}\left(\frac{\sin(Ts)}{\sin(s)}\right)^{d-1}\mathrm{Area}(S^{M}(x,s))\ ds

The function

(0,\pi)\to\mathbb{R},\ s\to\frac{\sin(Ts)}{\sin(s)}

is monotonically increasing (because
T=\frac{r}{R}\in(0,1)
). This means that

\displaystyle\vol(B^{M}(x,r))

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{r}{R}\cdot\left(\frac{\sin(r)}{\sin(R)}\right)^{d-1}\cdot\int_{0}^{R}\mathrm{Area}(S^{M}(x,s))\ ds

\displaystyle=\frac{r}{R}\cdot\left(\frac{\sin(r)}{\sin(R)}\right)^{d-1}\cdot\vol(B^{M}(x,R))

And we are done.
∎

We come now to the result we really care about:

Corollary 3.4
.

Suppose that
M\subset\mathbb{R}^{s}
is a closed
d
-dimensional (
d\geqslant 1
) submanifold with reach
\tau(M)\geqslant 1
. We then have

\vol(B^{\mathbb{S}^{d}}(r))\leqslant\vol(\mathbb{B}^{M}(x,r))\leqslant\vol\left(B^{\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\mathbb{H}^{d}}\left(2\arcsin\left(\frac{r}{2}\right)\right)\right)

and

\frac{\frac{R}{2}}{\arcsin(\frac{r}{2})}\!\left(\!\frac{\sin(R)}{\sin(2\arcsin\frac{r}{2})}\!\right)^{d-1}\!\!\!\!\leqslant\frac{\vol(\mathbb{B}^{M}(x,R))}{\vol(\mathbb{B}^{M}(x,r))}\!\leqslant\frac{\int_{0}^{\sqrt{2}\cdot 2\cdot\arcsin(\frac{R}{2})}\!\sinh^{d-1}(t)dt}{\int_{0}^{\sqrt{2}\cdot r}\sinh^{d-1}(t)dt}

for any two
0<r<R<1
and any
x\in M
.

Proof.

Let’s assume for the time being that
d\geqslant 2
. From part (1) in Proposition
3.1
we get for any
t<2
that

(3.7)

B^{M}(x,t)\subset\mathbb{B}^{M}(x,t)\subset B^{M}\left(x,2\arcsin\left(\frac{t}{2}\right)\right)

The first claim then follows directly from (
3.6
).

On the other hand, if we combine (
3.7
) with the upper bound in Proposition
3.3
we get that

\displaystyle\frac{\vol(\mathbb{B}^{M}(x,R))}{\vol(\mathbb{B}^{M}(x,r))}

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{\vol\left(B^{M}\left(x,2\arcsin\left(\frac{R}{2}\right)\right)\right)}{\vol(B^{M}(r,x))}

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{\int_{0}^{\sqrt{2}\cdot 2\arcsin\left(\frac{R}{2}\right)}\sinh^{d-1}(t)\ dt}{\int_{0}^{\sqrt{2}\cdot r}\sinh^{d-1}(t)\ dt},

and we are done with the upper bound of the second claim. The lower bound is obtained analogously and we leave the details to the reader.

So far we have been focusing on the case of dimension
d\geqslant 2
. In dimension
d=1
we actually get from (
3.7
) that

R-r\leqslant\vol(\mathbb{B}^{M}(x,R))-\vol(\mathbb{B}^{M}(x,R))\leqslant 2\arcsin\left(\frac{R-r}{2}\right)

This implies directly that

\frac{R}{r}\leqslant\frac{\vol(\mathbb{B}^{M}(x,R))}{\vol(\mathbb{B}^{M}(x,r))}\leqslant 1+\frac{2\arcsin\left(\frac{R-r}{2}\right)}{r}

for all
0<r<R<1
. The so-obtained bound for
d=1
is slightly better than the one we had claimed. We also note that this bound still works for
0<r<R<2
.
∎

3.4.
Volume of thick diagonal

Our goal here is to estimate how the volume
\vol(DM(\varepsilon))
of the
\varepsilon
-thick diagonal

DM(\varepsilon)=\{(x,y)\in M\times M\text{ with }|x-y|\leqslant\varepsilon\}

of a reach-1 submanifold
M\subset\mathbb{R}^{s}
varies when we replace
\varepsilon
by something else. Here the volume is computed as a subset of the Riemannian manifold
M\times M
, but can be expressed as an integral over
M
as follows:

(3.8)

\vol(DM(\varepsilon))=\int_{M}\vol(\mathbb{B}^{M}(x,\varepsilon))\ dx

In other words,
\vol(DM(\varepsilon))
is nothing other than the correlation integral at scale
\varepsilon
of the riemannian measure of
M
when considered as a measure on the ambient euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{s}
. We stress that the thick diagonal is defined in terms of the ambient distance in euclidean space, not in terms of the intrinsic distance of
M
. Anyways, now we prove the following:

Theorem 3.1
.

Suppose that
M\subset\mathbb{R}^{s}
is a
d
-dimensional (
d\geqslant 1
) closed submanifold with reach
\tau(M)\geqslant 1
. Then we have

\frac{\frac{\varepsilon_{1}}{2}}{\arcsin(\frac{\varepsilon_{2}}{2})}\!\left(\!\frac{\sin(\varepsilon_{1})}{\sin(2\arcsin\frac{\varepsilon_{2}}{2})}\!\right)^{d-1}\!\!\!\!\leqslant\frac{\vol(DM(\varepsilon_{1}))}{\vol(DM(\varepsilon_{2}))}\!\leqslant\frac{\int_{0}^{\sqrt{2}\cdot 2\cdot\arcsin(\frac{\varepsilon_{1}}{2})}\!\sinh^{d-1}(t)dt}{\int_{0}^{\sqrt{2}\cdot\varepsilon_{2}}\sinh^{d-1}(t)dt}

for any two
0<r<R<1
.

Proof.

From the expression (
3.8
) we get that

\min_{x\in M}\frac{\vol(\mathbb{B}^{M}(R,x))}{\vol(\mathbb{B}^{M}(r,x))}\leqslant\frac{\vol(DM(R))}{\vol(DM(r))}\leqslant\max_{x\in M}\frac{\vol(\mathbb{B}^{M}(R,x))}{\vol(\mathbb{B}^{M}(r,x))}

Now the claim follows from Corollary
3.4
.
∎

4.
Sampling the thick diagonal

In this section we will be still assuming that
M\subset\mathbb{R}^{s}
is a closed
d
-dimensional submanifold (
d\geqslant 1
) with reach
\tau(M)\geqslant 1
. Basically our goal is to bound the number of points that we have to sample from
M
to get a decent result when we use (
1.1
).

4.1.
Some probability

Suppose that we have a symmetric, say bounded, function

f:M\times M\to\mathbb{R}

We are interested in the sequence of random variables

(4.9)

X_{n}^{f}:M^{\mathbb{N}}\to\mathbb{R},\ \ X_{n}(x_{1},x_{2},\dots)\mapsto\sum_{i,j\leqslant n,\ i\neq j}f(x_{j},x_{j})

when
n
tends to
\infty
. Here we have endowed
M
with the probability measure

\Prob=\frac{1}{\vol(M)}\vol

proportional to the Riemannian measure. Accordingly,
M^{k}
and
M^{\mathbb{N}}
are all endowed with the corresponding product measure, again denoted by
\Prob
.

Being the sum of random variables, the expectation and variance of
X_{n}^{f}
are easy to get. Here they are:

\displaystyle E(X_{n}^{f})

\displaystyle=n(n-1)\cdot E(f)

\displaystyle\Var(X_{n}^{f})

\displaystyle=2\cdot n(n-1)\cdot\Var(f)+4\cdot n(n-1)(n-2)\cdot\cov(f)

where
\cov(f)
is the co-variance of
(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\mapsto f(x_{1},x_{2})
and
(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\mapsto f(x_{1},x_{3})
, or in a formula

\cov(f)=\int_{M\times M\times M}f(x,y)\cdot f(x,z)\ d\Prob(x,z,y)-E(f)^{2}.

Besides the expectation and the variance, what we will need to estimate is the quantity
\frac{\Var}{E^{2}}
for the random variables
X_{n}^{f}
. Well, this is what we get if we just use our expressions for the expectation and the variance:

(4.10)

\frac{\Var(X_{n}^{f})}{E(X_{n}^{f})^{2}}=\frac{2}{n(n-1)}\cdot\frac{\Var(f)}{E(f)^{2}}+\frac{4(n-2)}{n(n-1)}\cdot\frac{\cov(f)}{E(f)^{2}}

The reason why we will care about this last quantity is the following surely standard consequence of the Bienaymé-Chebyshev inequality:

Lemma 4.1
.

For any integrable random variable
X
in a probability space
X
we have

\Prob\left(\left|\log\left(\frac{X}{E(X)}\right)\right|>\delta\right)\leqslant\frac{1}{(1-e^{-\delta})^{2}}\cdot\frac{\Var(X)}{E(X)^{2}}

Proof.

Well, let us compute

\displaystyle\Prob\left(\left|\log\left(\frac{X}{E(X)}\right)\right|>\delta\right)

\displaystyle=\Prob\left(\frac{X}{E(X)}\notin[e^{-\delta},e^{\delta}]\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant\Prob\left(\left|\frac{X}{E(X)}-1\right|>1-e^{-\delta}\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant\Prob\left(\left|X-E(X)\right|>(1-e^{-\delta})\cdot E(X)\right)

Setting

k=\frac{(1-e^{-\delta})\cdot E(X)}{\sqrt{\Var(X)}}

in the standard Bienaymé-Chebysheff inequality

\Prob\left(|X-E(X)|\geqslant k\cdot\sqrt{\Var(X)}\right)\leqslant k^{-2}

we get

\Prob\left(\left|\log\left(\frac{X}{E(X)}\right)\right|>\delta\right)\leqslant\frac{\Var(X)}{(1-e^{-\delta})^{2}\cdot E(X)^{2}}

as we had claimed.
∎

4.2.
The function we care about

We are going to be interested in all of this in the case that
f=f_{\varepsilon}
is the characteristic function of
DM(\varepsilon)
, that is

(4.11)

f_{\varepsilon}(x,y)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}1&\text{ if }|x-y|\leqslant\varepsilon\\
0&\text{ otherwise}\end{array}\right.

This function satisfies that

E(f_{\varepsilon})=\frac{\vol(DM(\varepsilon))}{\vol(M)^{2}}

and hence we get that

E(X_{n}^{f_{\varepsilon}})=n(n-1)\cdot\frac{\vol(DM(\varepsilon))}{\vol(M)^{2}}

for all
n\geqslant 2
. Besides knowing the expectation, to be able to use Lemma
4.1
when we need to know, or at least estimate, is the quantity
\frac{\Var}{E^{2}}
. To apply (
4.10
) we need first to be able to estimate the variance and covariance of
f_{\varepsilon}
. Well, since
f_{\varepsilon}
only takes the values
0
and
1
, the variance is easily calculated:

\Var(f_{\varepsilon})=E(f_{\varepsilon})-E(f_{\varepsilon})^{2}=\frac{\vol(DM(\varepsilon))}{\vol(M)^{2}}-\left(\frac{\vol(DM(\varepsilon))}{\vol(M)^{2}}\right)^{2}

When it comes to the covariance we have

\displaystyle\cov(f_{\varepsilon})

\displaystyle=\int\left(\frac{\vol(\mathbb{B}^{M}(x,\varepsilon))}{\vol(M)}\right)^{2}d\Prob(x)-\frac{\vol(DM(\varepsilon))^{2}}{\vol(M)^{4}}

\displaystyle=\int\left(\frac{\vol(\mathbb{B}^{M}(x,\varepsilon))}{\vol(M)}\right)^{2}d\Prob(x)-\left(\int_{M}\frac{\vol(DM(\varepsilon))}{\vol(M)}d\Prob(x)\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=\Var\left(x\mapsto\frac{\vol(\mathbb{B}^{M}(x,\varepsilon))}{\vol(M)}\right)

This means that when the volume of
\mathbb{B}^{M}(x,\varepsilon)=M\cap\mathbb{B}(x,\varepsilon)
is constant then the covariance vanishes. This is for example the case for
M=\mathbb{S}^{d}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
or for the Clifford torus
M=\mathbb{T}^{d}\subset\mathbb{R}^{2d}
. However, in general we do not get anything better than the bound coming from Popoviciu’s inequality, that is

\cov(f_{\varepsilon})\leqslant\frac{\left(V^{M}_{\max}(\varepsilon)-V^{M}_{\min}(\varepsilon)\right)^{2}}{4\cdot\vol(M)^{2}}

where we have set

V_{\max}^{M}(\varepsilon)=\max_{x\in M}\vol(\mathbb{B}^{M}(x,\varepsilon))\text{ and }V_{\min}^{M}(\varepsilon)=\min_{x\in M}\vol(\mathbb{B}^{M}(x,\varepsilon))

Now, using (
4.10
), the bound for
\cov(f_{\varepsilon})
, as well as the bound
\vol(DM(\varepsilon))\geqslant\vol(M)\cdot V_{\min}(\varepsilon)
we get that

\frac{\Var(X_{n}^{f_{\varepsilon}})}{E(X_{n}^{f_{\varepsilon}})^{2}}\leqslant\frac{2}{(n-1)^{2}}\cdot\frac{\vol(M)}{V^{M}_{\min}(\varepsilon)}+\frac{1}{n-1}\cdot\left(\frac{V_{\max}^{M}(\varepsilon)}{V_{\min}^{M}(\varepsilon)}-1\right)^{2}

To get bounds that only depend on the dimension and on
\varepsilon<2
recall that from Corollary
3.4
we get that

(4.12)

\begin{split}V^{M}_{\min}(\varepsilon)&\geqslant\vol(B^{\mathbb{S}^{d}}(\varepsilon))\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\mathcal{V}(\varepsilon)\\
\frac{V_{\max}^{M}(\varepsilon)}{V_{\min}^{M}(\varepsilon)}&\leqslant\frac{\vol\left(B^{\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\mathbb{H}^{d}}\left(2\arcsin\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\right)\right)\right)}{\vol(B^{\mathbb{S}^{d}}(\varepsilon))}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\mathcal{R}(\varepsilon)\end{split}

Using these bounds we get

\frac{\Var(X_{n}^{f_{\varepsilon}})}{E(X_{n}^{f_{\varepsilon}})^{2}}\leqslant\frac{2}{(n-1)^{2}}\cdot\frac{\vol(M)}{\mathcal{V}(\varepsilon)}+\frac{1}{n-1}\cdot\left(\mathcal{R}(\varepsilon)-1\right)^{2}

We record what we have so far:

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
M\subset\mathbb{R}^{s}
be a closed submanifold with dimension
\dim(M)=d
and reach
\tau(M)\geqslant 1
, and for some
\varepsilon<2
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
consider
f_{\varepsilon}
and
X_{n}^{f_{\varepsilon}}
as in (
4.11
) and (
4.9
). Then we have

\displaystyle E(X_{n}^{f_{\varepsilon}})=

\displaystyle n(n-1)\cdot\frac{\vol(DM(\varepsilon))}{\vol(M)^{2}}

\displaystyle\frac{\Var(X_{n}^{f_{\varepsilon}})}{E(X_{n}^{f_{\varepsilon}})^{2}}\leqslant

\displaystyle\frac{2}{(n-1)^{2}}\cdot\frac{\vol(M)}{\mathcal{V}(\varepsilon)}+\frac{1}{n-1}\cdot\left(\mathcal{R}(\varepsilon)-1\right)^{2}

where
\mathcal{V}(\varepsilon)
and
\mathcal{R}(\varepsilon)
are as in (
4.12
).
∎

Before moving any further let us give explicit formulas for
\mathcal{V}(\varepsilon)
and
\mathcal{R}(\varepsilon)
:

(4.13)

\begin{split}\mathcal{V}(\varepsilon)&=\vol(\mathbb{S}^{d-1})\cdot\int_{0}^{\varepsilon}\sin^{d-1}(t)dt\\
\mathcal{R}(\varepsilon)&=\frac{2^{-\frac{d}{2}}\int_{0}^{2\sqrt{2}\arcsin\frac{\varepsilon}{2}}\sinh(t)^{d-1}dt}{\int_{0}^{\varepsilon}\sin^{d-1}(t)dt}\end{split}

Note also that the two summands in the bound for
\frac{\Var(X_{n}^{f_{\varepsilon}})}{E(X_{n}^{f_{\varepsilon}})^{2}}
in Lemma
4.2
are rather different. Assume for example that
d
is fixed. Then the weight of the second factor decreases when
\varepsilon
decreases. On the other hand the value of the first one explodes. Recall also that the second factor can be ignored if
\cov
vanishes, that is if all balls
\mathbb{B}^{M}(x,\varepsilon)
have the same volume.

4.3.
Some technical results

Recall that to estimate the dimension of
M
via (
1.1
), or equivalently via (
2.5
), what we do is to take, for two scales
\varepsilon_{1}>\varepsilon_{2}
random values of
X^{\varepsilon_{1}}_{n}=X^{f_{\varepsilon_{1}}}_{n}
and
X^{\varepsilon_{2}}_{n}=X^{f_{\varepsilon_{2}}}_{n}
, compute

\frac{\log\frac{X^{\varepsilon_{1}}_{n}}{n(n-1)}-\log\frac{X^{\varepsilon_{2}}_{n}}{n(n-1)}}{\log\varepsilon_{1}-\log\varepsilon_{2}}

and hope the that obtained value has something to do with
\dim(M)
. Well, what we get from Lemma
4.1
is an estimate of the probability that this value is far from the expectation. Indeed, since we have

\displaystyle\Prob\left(\left|\frac{\log X^{\varepsilon_{1}}_{n}-\log X^{\varepsilon_{2}}_{n}}{\log\varepsilon_{1}-\log\varepsilon_{2}}-\frac{\log\vol(DM(\varepsilon_{1}))-\log\vol(DM(\varepsilon_{2}))}{\log\varepsilon_{1}-\log\varepsilon_{2}}\right|>\rho\right)

\displaystyle\phantom{blablabla}=\Prob\left(\left|\frac{\log X^{\varepsilon_{1}}_{n}-\log X^{\varepsilon_{2}}_{n}}{\log\varepsilon_{1}-\log\varepsilon_{2}}-\frac{\log E(X^{\varepsilon_{1}}_{n})-\log E(X^{\varepsilon_{2}}_{n})}{\log\varepsilon_{1}-\log\varepsilon_{2}}\right|>\rho\right)

\displaystyle\phantom{blablabla}=\Prob\left(\left|\log\left(\frac{X^{\varepsilon_{1}}_{n}}{E(X^{\varepsilon_{1}}_{n})}\right)-\log\left(\frac{X^{\varepsilon_{2}}_{n}}{E(X^{\varepsilon_{2}}_{n})}\right)\right|>\log\left(\left(\frac{\varepsilon_{1}}{\varepsilon_{2}}\right)^{\rho}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\phantom{blablabla}\leqslant\sum_{i=1,2}\Prob\left(\left|\log\left(\frac{X^{\varepsilon_{i}}_{n}}{E(X^{\varepsilon_{i}}_{n})}\right)\right|>\log\left(\left(\frac{\varepsilon_{1}}{\varepsilon_{2}}\right)^{\frac{\rho}{2}}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\phantom{blablabla}\leqslant\frac{1}{\left(1-\left(\frac{\varepsilon_{2}}{\varepsilon_{1}}\right)^{\frac{\rho}{2}}\right)^{2}}\sum_{i=1,2}\frac{\Var(X_{n}^{\varepsilon_{i}})}{E(X_{n}^{\varepsilon_{i}})^{2}}

Plugging in the statement of Lemma
4.2
we get:

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
M\subset\mathbb{R}^{s}
be a closed submanifold with reach
\tau(M)\geqslant 1
, pick two scales
0<\varepsilon_{2}<\varepsilon_{1}
. Also, for
n\geqslant 2
set

\rho=\sum_{i=1,2}\left(\frac{2}{(n-1)^{2}}\cdot\frac{\vol(M)}{\mathcal{V}(\varepsilon_{i})}+\frac{1}{n-1}\cdot\left(\mathcal{R}(\varepsilon_{i})-1\right)^{2}\right)

Then we have

\Prob\left(\left|\frac{\log X^{\varepsilon_{1}}_{n}-\log X^{\varepsilon_{2}}_{n}}{\log\varepsilon_{1}-\log\varepsilon_{2}}-\frac{\log\left(\frac{\vol(DM(\varepsilon_{1}))}{\vol(DM(\varepsilon_{2}))}\right)}{\log\varepsilon_{1}-\log\varepsilon_{2}}\right|>\Delta\right)\leqslant\frac{\rho}{\left(1-\left(\frac{\varepsilon_{2}}{\varepsilon_{1}}\right)^{\frac{\Delta}{2}}\right)^{2}}

for any
\delta>0
. ∎

Let us get an slightly more user friendly version:

Corollary 4.3
.

With the same assumptions and notation as in Theorem
4.1
suppose that for some positive
\alpha_{i}
’s with
\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}=1
and for some
\rho>0
we have

n\geqslant 1+\frac{1}{\alpha_{i}\cdot\rho}\cdot\left(\mathcal{R}(\varepsilon_{i})-1\right)^{2}+\sqrt{\frac{2}{\alpha_{i}\cdot\rho}\cdot\frac{\vol(M)}{\mathcal{V}(\varepsilon_{i})}}

for
i=1,2
. Then we also have

P\left(\left|\frac{\log X^{\varepsilon_{1}}_{n}-\log X^{\varepsilon_{2}}_{n}}{\log\varepsilon_{1}-\log\varepsilon_{2}}-\frac{\log\left(\frac{\vol(DM(\varepsilon_{1}))}{\vol(DM(\varepsilon_{2}))}\right)}{\log\varepsilon_{1}-\log\varepsilon_{2}}\right|>\Delta\right)\leqslant\rho\cdot\left(1-\left(\frac{\varepsilon_{2}}{\varepsilon_{1}}\right)^{\frac{\Delta}{2}}\right)^{-2}

Proof.

In terms of Theorem
4.1
what we have to do is to guarantee for
i=1,2
that

\alpha_{i}\rho\geqslant\frac{2}{(n-1)^{2}}\cdot\frac{\vol(M)}{\mathcal{V}(\varepsilon_{i})}+\frac{1}{n-1}\cdot\left(\mathcal{R}(\varepsilon_{i})-1\right)^{2}

It thus suffices to ensure that
n-1
is larger than the solution
X
of the equation

5.
Heuristics

Theorem
4.1
gives us a bound for the number of points needed in a data set to be able to get from (
1.1
) at least
90\%
of the time its dimension. In concrete examples, we expect that this confidence level can be achieved with significantly less points. We will discuss this difference between theory and practice, suggesting a simpler heuristic model supported by computational examples.

Heuristic bound

We begin by discussing a heuristic model representing an ideal situation without curvature. More concretely we will be running (
1.1
) at some scales
\varepsilon_{1}
and
\varepsilon_{2}
at which we can ignore curvature effects. In effect, we will act as if all balls in
M
of radius at most
\varepsilon_{1}
were euclidean and totally geodesic.

The first observation is that the statistic (
1.1
) is computed from information extracted from the distances
|x-y|
for those (unordered) pairs

\{x,y\}\in PX(\varepsilon)=\big\{\{x,y\}\subset X\text{ with }x\neq y\text{ with }|x-y|\leqslant\varepsilon\big\}

rather than from the points themselves. This means that the performance of the algorithm should depend on
|PX(\varepsilon_{1})|
and on the dimension
d
, instead of directly on the total number
n
of points.

We think of the distance
|x-y|
for
\{x,y\}\in PX(\varepsilon_{1})
as a random variable, and from now on, we will take the point of view that we have
N=|PX(\varepsilon_{1})|
random variables
(X_{i})_{1\leqslant i\leqslant N}
given by taking the distance between pairs of points at distance at most
\varepsilon_{1}
and sampled uniformly on
M
. We then consider the variables
Y_{i}
equal to 1 if
X_{i}
is smaller than
\varepsilon_{2}
and 0 otherwise. With this notation in place, the estimator (
1.1
) becomes

\dim_{\Corr(\varepsilon_{1},\varepsilon_{2})(X)}=\round\left(\frac{\log(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}Y_{i})}{\log(\varepsilon_{2})-\log(\varepsilon_{1})}\right)

Note that since we are assuming that all the balls are euclidean, the mean value of
Y_{i}
is then
E_{d}=(\varepsilon_{2}/\varepsilon_{1})^{d}
and as the variables
Y_{i}
only take the values 0 and 1, their variance is
\sigma_{d}^{2}=E_{d}-E_{d}^{2}
.

In reality, the variables
X_{i}
, and thus the variables
Y_{i}
have no reason to be independent. Still, most of them are when the volume is large when compared to the size of the data set. So, from now on, we will put ourselves in the ideal situation that the
N
variables
Y_{i}
are independent. Independence implies that the distribution of the sample mean
Z=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i}Y_{i}
is binomial of parameters
N
and
E_{d}
, which can be approximated by a normal distribution of mean value
E_{d}
and of variance
\frac{1}{N}\sigma_{d}^{2}
using the central limit theorem. It is then known that the probability of
Z
to be in the interval
[E_{d}-1.64\cdot\sigma_{d}/\sqrt{N},E_{d}+1.64\cdot\sigma_{d}/\sqrt{N}]
is about 90%. If we set
\gap_{d}=\min\left(E_{d-0.5}-E_{d},E_{d}-E_{d+0.5}\right)
, we want to find
N
so that
1.64\cdot\sigma_{d}/\sqrt{N}=\gap_{d}
. This number gives a number of pairs sufficient to obtain the right dimension with a confidence of 90%.

Suppose for example that the manifold
M
has dimension 4. For
\varepsilon_{1}=0.54
and
\varepsilon_{2}=0.23
, the scales coming from Theorem
1.1
, we can then compute the values of
\gap_{4}
and of
\sigma_{4}
and deduce the required value of

.

\displaystyle=\min(E_{3.5}-E_{4},E_{4}-E_{4.5})\simeq 0.01143

\displaystyle\sigma_{4}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{E_{4}-E_{4}^{2}}\simeq 0.1784

\displaystyle N

\displaystyle=\left(1.64\cdot\sigma_{4}/\gap_{4}\right)^{2}\simeq 655\text{ pairs}

This is an approximation using the central limit theorem (that is, replacing the binomial distribution by the normal distribution), but more precise computation can be done working directly with the binomial distribution. Doing this, we can take

Numerical evidence

Experimental examples seem to confirm the heuristical bounds presented on Table
3
. By numerically sampling points on different manifolds, we obtain results close to what was expected. Here is the procedure we followed:

(1)

Choose a manifold
M
of known dimension
d
and consider the scales and number of pairs
N
given by Table
3
.

(2)

Uniformly and independently sample points in
M
until we obtain
N
pairs at distance
\varepsilon_{1}
. The sampling is done by repeating a program specific to the desired manifold that samples a single point randomly and uniformly on it.

(3)

Estimate the dimension using estimator (
1.1
) with scales
\varepsilon_{1}
and
\varepsilon_{2}
.

(4)

Repeat steps 2 and 3 one hundred times and count the number of success.

We ran this experiment a variety of manifolds of reach 1 and always obtained a rate of success in a range of
\pm 6\%
of the target rate. This difference between the actual rate and the target would be totally normal even in an ideal situation. Indeed, repeating an experiment 100 time with a probability of success of 90% gives a result with a standard deviation of 3 successes. Every experimental result then falls into the usual range of two times the standard deviation.

A problem with all the results we have been discussing so far is that in practice one has little clue what the reach of the underlying manifold could be. However, as we already mentioned in the introduction, one can actually derive from Table

Comparison with other estimators

In this paper we study the estimator (
1.1
), but a variety of other algorithms exist. For example, instead of doing statistics using only the distances between points, we could also use the angles between points, or other more complex features. These different approaches can be compared using the previous heuristic model. We refer to
[
9
,
10
]
and specially to
[
20
, Chapter 3]
for a review of different dimension estimators. We will compare (
1.1
) with the estimators ANOVA, local PCA, and with the implementation of (
1.1
) where one tries to avoid picking scales, reading instead the dimension from a log-log chart.

ANOVA

Diaz, Quiroz and Velasco propose in
[
12
]
a method based on the angles refered as ANOVA in the literature. Their idea is to estimate the local dimension around a point
x
by considering its
k
nearest neighbors and the
{k\choose 2}
angles at
x
formed by these points. From the variance of these
{k\choose 2}
angles, we can identify the closest
\beta_{d}
and deduce the dimension
d
, where
\beta_{d}
is defined as follows.

\beta_{d}=\frac{1}{\vol(\mathbb{S}^{d-1})^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\left(\angle(\theta,\eta)-\dfrac{\pi}{2}\right)^{2}d\theta d\eta

The global dimension can then be recovered by taking the median, the mode or the mean of the local dimensions.

To be able to compare ANOVA to the estimator (
1.1
) we will instead take the variance of all angles. More precisely, consider every ordered triple of points in which all three points are at distance at most
\varepsilon_{1}
from each other. Each such triple
(x,y,z)
determines an angle
\angle(y-x,z-x)
. We compute the variance of the so-obtained angles we can locate the closest
\beta_{d}
. We take that
d
to be the ANOVA dimension of our data set.

Putting ourselves again in the ideal situation that we are working in a scale at which curvature can be ignored and assuming (and that is a lot of assuming in this case) that the angles we find are independent of each other, we can argue as earlier in the discussion of the heuristic bound and we get that, in dimension 4, we would need at least
652
angles to achieve a confidence level of 90% with the ANOVA estimator.

Suppose now that we sample 1958 points from the 4-dimensional Clifford torus
M=\mathbb{T}^{4}
. That number was chosen so that we expect to have
516
pairs within
\varepsilon_{1}=0.54
of each other, the heuristic bound for (
1.1
) in dimension
4
. On the other hand we expect to have

\text{number of unordered triples}={1958\choose 3}\cdot\left(\frac{\vol(B^{\mathbb{R}^{4}}(\varepsilon_{1}))}{\vol(M)}\right)^{2}\simeq 91.

Each unordered pair gives 3 angles, meaning can expect to find about
273
angles. This is much less than what we estimated that would needed.

And this phenomenon gets worse when the volume of the underlying manifold grows: if the manifold has volume
64\pi^{4}
and if we have a data set with
516
pairs of points within
\varepsilon_{1}=0.54
then we expect to only have
45
triples, that is about
135
angles. The reason for this is that the number of points needed to have a given number of pairs grows with
\vol(M)^{\frac{1}{2}}
while it grows as
\vol(M)^{\frac{2}{3}}
when we fix the number of triples instead. This means that for large volumes the algorithm will only become worse as finding triples of points will become more and more difficult.

1.1
) gives significantly better results than ANOVA. However, we cannot conclude that estimator (

) has better performance in general. In particular, for manifolds of smaller volume or for different choices of scales, ANOVA could in principle give better results.

Local PCA

Principal Component Analysis aims to find the best linear space containing a given data set. According to
[
7
]
, PCA is the gold standard of dimension estimation. It works as follows. To our given data set
X=(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
we associate first the mean

\bar{x}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{n}

and then the
n\times N
matrix
A
whose rows are the vectors
u_{i}-\bar{u}
, and one computes then the singular values
s_{1}\geqslant s_{2}\geqslant\dots\geqslant s_{\min\{n,N\}}
. If
X
is contained in a linear subspace of dimension
d
then
s_{k}=0
for all
k\geqslant d+1
. Accordingly, one can declare that
X
has PCA-dimension
k
if the gap
s_{k}-s_{k+1}
is maximal. Another possibility would be to fix a threshold
\varepsilon
and declare the PCA-dimension of
X
to be the largest
k
with
s_{k}\geqslant\varepsilon
. For example, if what one wants to do is to test the hypothesis that
X
is contained in a
d
-dimensional subspace then one can check for example if
s_{d+1}
is below some threshold
\varepsilon<\frac{1}{d+2}
—the number
\frac{1}{d+2}
arises because it is the expected value for
s_{d}
if
X
is uniformly sampled out of the unit ball in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

In any case, that was PCA. The idea of
local PCA
, or
Nonlinear PCA
, is to apply PCA to certain subsets of the data set and then, for good measure, average the so obtained numbers. As we see, one does not only need to agree on what one calls PCA, but also on what subsets does one wants to subject to the PCA treatement. For example, as in
[
7
]
one can cluster the data set using single linkage clustering
1
1

1

In single linkage clustering the clusters are, for some
\varepsilon
, the connected components of the graph with vertex set
X
and where two vertices are joined by an edge if they are with
\varepsilon
of each other.
and then apply PCA to each custer. This is, in our humble and uneducated opinion, a very reasonable choice if, for data sets
X
uniformly sampled out of a submanifold
M\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
, what one wants to do is to find linear spaces (approximately) containing each connected component of
M
. On the other hand, if what one wants to do is to recover the dimension of
M
then it seems reasonable to rather apply PCA to (some of) the sets
X\cap\mathbb{B}(x,\varepsilon)
for
x\in X
and for some
\varepsilon
chosen so that
M\cap\mathbb{B}(x,\varepsilon)
is well-approximated by its tangent space.

Note now that every set consisting of
d+1
points is contained in a
d
-dimensional affine subspace of
\mathbb{R}^{s}
. This means that if we want to distinguish dimension
d
from dimension
d+1
we need at the very least to find
d+2
tuples of nearby points. Now, if we once again sample
1958
points out of the Clifford torus
M=\mathbb{T}^{4}
so that we can expect the magic number of
516
paris of points at most at distance
0.54
from each other, then we can expect to find

\begin{array}[]{c}\text{number of unordered}\\
\text{6-tuples at scale }0.54\end{array}={1958\choose 6}\cdot\left(\frac{\vol(B^{\mathbb{R}^{4}}(0.54))}{\vol(M)}\right)^{5}\simeq 0.110373....

In other words, the expectation is to not have any
6
-tuples, meaning that, if we are working at the same scales as we were implementing (
1.1
), then we have no chance of distinguishing our 4-manifold from a 5-manifold using local PCA. Note that even if we work at a scale of
2
, scale at which we are acting as if the round sphere were totally flat, then we can expect to only have 2 6-tuples

\begin{array}[]{c}\text{number of unordered}\\
\text{6-tuples at scale }2\end{array}={1958\choose 6}\cdot\left(\frac{\vol(B^{\mathbb{R}^{4}}(2))}{\vol(M)}\right)^{5}\simeq 2.043944....

As we see, even if we work at scales at which we are flat earthers, we do not have by far enough sufficiently populated clusters for local PCA to be meaningful.

log-log plots

Another method to derive the estimator (
1.1
) when the reach is unknown, is to take a lot of scales

\varepsilon_{1}<\varepsilon_{2}<\dots<\varepsilon_{k},

and to consider the graph of the piecewise linear function with corners

\big(\log(\varepsilon_{i}),\log(|PX(\varepsilon_{i})|)\big)

and try to read out some sort of meaningful slope of that function (see
[
20
, Chapter 3]
). We implemented this procedure for 1 000 points uniformly sampled from the product of two 2-dimensional rotation tori of reach 1. We obtained the graph presented on Figure

Let us conclude with two further comments on the log-log procedure:

(1)
The log-log procedure seems to allow us to estimate the dimension of
M
without any assumption on its reach. It is however difficult to define a precise algorithm for it. Consequently, we cannot really compare its performance with that of the other estimators.

(2)

Additionally, this method gives us insight on the choice of scales that could be relevant. Indeed, the scales determined in Section
4.4
have no reason to be optimal for the heuristic model. And as this model does not take the curvature nor the reach into account, it is impossible to use it to find optimal scales. When considering Figure
3
, it seems natural to choose two scales that are far apart to measure the slope as precisely as possible, but close enough so that they do no get too close to the extremities. Here, two scales that would seem relevant could be
\exp(0.5)\simeq 1.6
and
\exp(-0.5)\simeq 0.6
. In any case, the choice of scales
0.54\simeq\exp(-0.6)
and
0.23\simeq\exp(-1.5)
seem to be far from optimal. Using the estimator (
1.1
) with scales 1.6 and 0.6 on computational examples, we obtain a rate of success of 99% with only 1 000 points for the product of two tori. For the scales 0.54 and 0.23, the heuristic model gives us a number of points of 3916 for a rate of success of 90%. This rate falls to 41% with only 1 000 points on computational examples. However, these scales have been chosen after the study of the results to obtain the desired answer on a given manifold. But this does not give us a method to choose optimal scales a priori. For example, the scales 1.6 and 0.6 one give a rate of success of 45% for 100 points on the 4-sphere, while the scale 1 and 0.6 give a rate of 92%.
