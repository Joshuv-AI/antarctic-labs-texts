The sharp interface limit of a Navier–Stokes/Allen–Cahn system with constant mobility: Convergence rates by a relative energy approach
Sebastian Hensel, Yuning Liu

Abstract
. We investigate the sharp interface limit of a diffuse interface system that couples the Allen–Cahn equation with the instationary Navier–Stokes system in a bounded domain in ℝ d \mathbb{R}^{d} with d ∈ { 2 , 3 } d\in\{2,3\} . This model is used to describe a propagating front in a viscous incompressible flow with the width of the transition layer being characterized by a small parameter ε > 0 \varepsilon>0 . We show that the solutions converge to a limit two-phase fluid system with surface tension that couples the mean curvature flow and the Navier–Stokes system. The main assumptions are that the evolution of the limit system is sufficiently regular and that the associated evolving interface does not intersect the boundary of the container. For quantitatively well-prepared initial data, we even establish an optimal convergence rate. This is the first rigorous result of this kind which is valid in all physically relevant ambient dimensions. Keywords: Allen–Cahn equation, complex fluid, relative entropy, mean curvature flow, sharp interface limit. Mathematical Subject Classification : Primary: 76T99; Secondary: 76D45, 76D05, 35R35, 53E10, 35K57.



1.
Introduction

1.1.
Context

Curvature driven interface evolution is a challenging topic in PDE theory
due to the inherent emergence of topology changes. Indeed, classical
descriptions (i.e., via parametrization of the evolving interface) cease
to work once the solution approaches the first time of such a topology change.
One popular tool to describe the dynamics even after these consists of
phase-field models, where instead of a sharp interface one considers
a diffuse interface layer of finite width (which is typically small related to
a given scaling parameter
\varepsilon
). In the specific example of the evolution
of two (macroscopically) immiscible fluids in the presence of surface tension,
an associated fundamental phase-field model is the so-called
model H

coupling Navier–Stokes dynamics with the fourth-order Cahn–Hilliard dynamics.
This model was in fact introduced in
[
27
,
33
]
.

Next to obvious PDE questions concerning existence and uniqueness of solutions to
such a phase-field model, another highly relevant and natural question
is the consistency with a sharp interface model. Indeed, by formally taking the limit

\varepsilon\to 0
one hopes to identify the underlying dynamics
in terms of the sharp interface model under consideration, and therefore
to justify the transition to the phase-field model. Needless to say,
for numerical applications apart from qualitative convergence results
also quantitative results in the form of convergence rates in certain norms
are of interest.

In the context of model H (and even for more general models
allowing, e.g., a non-constant density), the formal
derivation of the associated sharp interface limit model by means
of asymptotic expansion techniques is performed in
[
2
]
.
Rigorous justifications of these arguments are scarce though. For global-in-time
convergence of solutions to model H (or a related model) to a rather weak
solution to the sharp interface limit model (i.e., a concept
of varifold solutions), one may consult
[
3
]

(or
[
10
]
, respectively). Short-time (quantitative)
convergence to strong solutions of the sharp interface
limit model is in turn shown in the recent works
[
6
]
and
[
5
]
,
where, however, only the setting of the stationary Stokes operator is treated.
At the time of this writing, the rigorous derivation of convergence rates
incorporating the full Navier–Stokes dynamics in model H remains an
important open problem in the field.

In the present work, we study instead of model H (or related models)
a phase-field model with fluid mechanical coupling in ambient dimension
d\in\{2,3\}

which is based on the second-order Allen–Cahn operator.
We further consider the scaling regime
of constant mobility, and with respect to the fluid mechanical modeling we
restrict ourselves to the case of Navier–Stokes dynamics with constant
density and constant viscosity (we refer to Subsection
1.2

for a mathematical formulation of the model). Phase-field models of such
Navier–Stokes/Allen–Cahn type were first introduced in
[
46
,
36
]
.
Formal asymptotic expansions (cf.
[
12
]
) suggest that the associated sharp-interface
limit model is given by a Navier–Stokes two-phase flow with surface tension,
where, as a consequence of the constant mobility assumption, the interface
separating the two fluid phases is not merely transported by the fluids
but is also subject to mean curvature flow (we again refer to Subsection
1.2

for a mathematical formulation of the corresponding model). In particular, the mass of each individual
fluid phase is not preserved and the resulting model may be interpreted as
a simplified model for two-phase fluid flow incorporating phase transitions and surface tension.
For global-in-time existence of weak solutions to the phase-field model,
we refer to
[
45
]
(see also
[
26
]
for longtime behavior of solutions).

In this setting, the main result of the present work rigorously justifies the formally
derived sharp interface limit model in ambient dimension
d\in\{2,3\}
.
Our convergence result holds true on the time horizon of existence
of a (sufficiently regular) strong solution of the sharp interface limit.
We even derive sharp convergence rates in strong norms under the
assumption of correspondingly well-prepared initial data.
For precise mathematical statements of these two main results, we refer
to Theorem
1
and Corollary
2
below.
We mention that a similar convergence result was recently established
in
[
1
]
(cf. also
[
37
]
)
by a completely different approach (we provide further comments on the methods later).
The results of
[
1
]
are, however, restricted to ambient dimension
d=2
,
so that our work, to the best of our knowledge, is the first to rigorously justify
the above sharp interface limit in all physically relevant dimensions. It has to be said,
though, that the authors of
[
1
]
are in addition able to treat the regime
of different viscosities. Even though we expect this to be manageable also in the
framework of our strategy (without restrictions on the ambient dimension),
this may very well lead to a doubling of the length of the present paper
(cf. the corresponding challenges in
[
23
]
). For this reason, we
restrict ourselves to the most basic setting. We finally mention
that
[
4
]
contains a preliminary convergence result
preceding ours and the one of
[
1
]
, where the authors replace
the full Navier–Stokes dynamics by the stationary Stokes operator
(and again only in ambient dimension
d=2
).

Without coupling to a fluid mechanical system, the rigorous
convergence of phase-field models to sharp interface evolutions
is of course an already extensively studied subject in the literature.
For (qualitative and/or quantitative) convergence of solutions
of the Allen–Cahn equation towards various weak or strong notions of solutions
for mean curvature flow, one may consult, e.g., the
works
[
21
,
20
,
34
,
49
,
52
,
24
,
28
]
.
For the inclusion of constant contact angles, corresponding results can
be found in
[
50
,
39
,
18
,
17
,
48
,
38
,
8
,
9
,
29
,
32
]
,
whereas (qualitative and/or quantitative) convergence of solutions
of the vectorial Allen–Cahn equation towards evolution by multiphase
mean curvature flow is the subject of
[
43
,
25
]
.
The results on the connection of the Allen–Cahn equation with
mean curvature flow have a fourth-order analogue, namely,
the convergence of solutions of the Cahn–Hilliard equation
towards evolution by Mullins–Sekerka flow as, e.g., shown
in
[
19
,
14
,
44
]
(cf. also the discussion in
[
52
]
).
For a corresponding result with disparate mobilities, we refer
to the recent work
[
41
]
. Finally, we mention
[
42
]

for a scaling limit result modeling nematic-isotropic phase
transitions in the context of Landau–De Gennes theory of liquid crystals.

In order to establish the above convergence results,
a variety of techniques and frameworks is used depending
on the precise goals (i.e., qualitative long-time convergence
towards weak solutions of the sharp interface limit model
vs. quantitative convergence towards sufficiently
smooth solutions of the sharp interface limit model
until the latter run into their first topology change).
In fact, most of the above works are either based on
notions from geometric measure theory (e.g., varifolds
and their first variations), gradient flow techniques
in the spirit of
[
51
]
(cf. also
[
52
]
),
or combining rigorous asymptotic expansions with a
stability analysis of the linearized operator associated
with the phase-field model. Especially in the context
of the previously mentioned quantitative convergence results
incorporating a fluid mechanical coupling, the latter strategy seems
to be the only one used so far to the best of our knowledge.

However, in the recent inspiring work
[
24
]
,
a new approach for the derivation of convergence rates
was introduced and implemented for the simplest
setting of the Allen–Cahn equation posed on the full space
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
This strategy is closest to the gradient flow perspective
in the sense that it first generates a distance measure
for the difference of the phase-field and sharp interface
solutions based on the phase-field energy, and then estimates
its time evolution by a Gronwall-type argument based, amongst other things,
on the dissipation structure of the phase-field model.
The method developed in
[
24
]
is therefore
reminiscent of a well-established technique to establish
(weak-strong) uniqueness of solutions in the context of a variety of classical
continuum mechanics models (e.g., incompressible and compressible Navier–Stokes flow,
or conservation laws): the so-called relative entropy method.
In fact, the work
[
24
]
draws motivation from recent
results extending the relative entropy method (and thus weak-strong uniqueness)
to problems incorporating geometric evolution. This was first implemented
for binormal curvature flow of curves in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
in
[
35
]
,
or for Navier–Stokes two-phase flow with surface tension in
[
23
]
.
Subsequent extensions are able to deal with multiphase mean curvature flow
[
22
]

(cf. also
[
30
]
), contact angle problems
[
31
,
29
]
,
or a novel notion of varifold solutions for mean curvature flow
in the spirit of ideas from De Giorgi
[
28
]
.
In view of these developments, it is not surprising that also
[
24
]

already led to several follow-up works in the context of scaling limits for phase-field models,
see
[
42
,
32
,
47
,
25
]
.

In the present work, we continue this story and extend the approach from
[
24
]

to the case of a Navier–Stokes/Allen–Cahn model with constant mobility.
We refer the reader to Section
2
and
especially Section
3
for a mathematical account
on our strategy.

1.2.
The phase-field model and its sharp interface limit

Let
\Omega\subset{\mathbb{R}^{d}}
,
d\in\{2,3\}
, be a bounded domain with orientable
and
C^{2}
-boundary. We then consider the following most basic
Navier–Stokes/Allen–Cahn problem in
\Omega

(1a)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}v_{\varepsilon}+(v_{\varepsilon}\cdot\nabla)v_{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=\Delta v_{\varepsilon}-\nabla\pi_{\varepsilon}-\nabla\cdot\big(\varepsilon\nabla\varphi_{\varepsilon}\otimes\nabla\varphi_{\varepsilon}\big)

\displaystyle\text{in }\Omega\times(0,T_{0}),

(1b)

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot v_{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\text{in }\Omega\times(0,T_{0}),

(1c)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\varphi_{\varepsilon}+(v_{\varepsilon}\cdot\nabla)\varphi_{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=\Delta\varphi_{\varepsilon}-\smash{\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}W^{\prime}(\varphi_{\varepsilon})}

\displaystyle\text{in }\Omega\times(0,T_{0}),

(1d)

\displaystyle v_{\varepsilon}(\cdot,0)

\displaystyle=v_{\varepsilon,0}

\displaystyle\text{in }\Omega,

(1e)

\displaystyle\varphi_{\varepsilon}(\cdot,0)

\displaystyle=\varphi_{\varepsilon,0}\in[-1,1]

\displaystyle\text{in }\Omega,

(1f)

\displaystyle(\varphi_{\varepsilon},v_{\varepsilon})

\displaystyle=(-1,0)

\displaystyle\text{on }\partial\Omega\times(0,T_{0}).

Here,
W
denotes a double-well potential satisfying standard assumptions
(see, e.g.,
[
4
]
). We fix
the associated surface tension constant by

\displaystyle c_{0}:=\int_{-1}^{1}\sqrt{2W(r)}\,dr.

In the sharp interface limit
\varepsilon\to 0
one expects to obtain
a simplified model for a two-phase fluid flow with phase transitions,
which in our case reads

(2a)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}v+(v\cdot\nabla)v

\displaystyle=\Delta v-\nabla\pi

\displaystyle\text{in }\big(\Omega{\times}(0,T_{*})\big)\setminus\mathcal{I},

(2b)

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot v

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\text{in }\Omega{\times}(0,T_{*}),

(2c)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\chi+(v\cdot\nabla)\chi

\displaystyle=-H_{\mathcal{I}}|\nabla\chi|

\displaystyle\text{in }\Omega{\times}(0,T_{*}),

(2d)

\displaystyle[\![v]\!]

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle\text{on }\mathcal{I},

(2e)

\displaystyle[\![2\nabla^{\mathrm{sym}}v-p\mathrm{Id}]\!]\frac{\nabla\chi}{|\nabla\chi|}

\displaystyle=c_{0}H_{\mathcal{I}}\frac{\nabla\chi}{|\nabla\chi|}

\displaystyle\text{on }\mathcal{I},

(2f)

\displaystyle v(\cdot,0)

\displaystyle=v_{0}

\displaystyle\text{in }\Omega,

(2g)

\displaystyle\chi(\cdot,0)

\displaystyle=\chi_{0}

\displaystyle\text{in }\Omega,

(2h)

\displaystyle(\chi,v)

\displaystyle=(0,0)

\displaystyle\text{on }\partial\Omega{\times}(0,T_{*}).

In the above system,
\chi
denotes a time-dependent characteristic function.
From now on, we assume that we are provided with a sufficiently regular solution
(\chi,v)
for
the sharp interface limit model (for precise assumptions, we refer to
Definition
4
below).
In particular,
\operatorname{supp}|\nabla\chi|\cap(\Omega{\times}[0,T_{*}))

shall model a sufficiently regular interface

(3)

\mathcal{I}={\bigcup_{t\in[0,T_{*})}\mathcal{I}(t){\times}\{t\}}

in space-time associated with an open and sufficiently regular
set

\Omega_{+}={\bigcup_{t\in[0,T_{*})}\Omega_{+}(t){\times}\{t\}}\subset\Omega{\times}(0,T_{*}).

The map
\chi
is assumed to be
\equiv 1
in
\Omega_{+}

as well as
\equiv 0
in
(\Omega{\times}[0,T_{*}))\setminus\overline{\Omega_{+}}
.
Denoting for all
t\in(0,T_{*})
by
\mathbf{n}_{\mathcal{I}}(\cdot,t)
the associated unit normal
along
\mathcal{I}(t)
pointing inside
\Omega_{+}(t)
, the evolution equation
for the interface then translates into (all scalar geometric
quantities are oriented with respect to the normal vector field
\mathbf{n}_{\mathcal{I}}
)

(4)

\displaystyle V_{\mathcal{I}}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{n}_{\mathcal{I}}\cdot v+H_{\mathcal{I}}

\displaystyle\text{on }\mathcal{I}.

Here,
V_{\mathcal{I}}(\cdot,t)
and
H_{\mathcal{I}}(\cdot,t)
denote the normal speed and
the scalar mean curvature of the interface
\mathcal{I}(t)
,
t\in[0,T_{*})
.
Finally, in order to avoid issues originating from contact point dynamics, we assume
throughout the rest of this work that

(5)

\displaystyle\overline{\Omega_{+}(t)}

\displaystyle\subset\Omega

\displaystyle\text{for all }t\in[0,T_{*}).

2.
Main result

The main result of the present work is concerned with
a rigorous convergence result for solutions
of the phase-field model (
1a
)–(
1f
)
(even quantitatively with sharp convergence rates when starting from well-prepared initial data)
towards strong solutions of the sharp interface limit
model (
2a
)–(
2h
).
As already mentioned in the introduction, our approach is not based
on the strategy of combining rigorous asymptotic expansions
with stability estimates for the underlying linearized operator.
In contrast, our results are facilitated by the introduction of two
error functionals which aim to encode the difference between
a solution of the phase-field model and a solution for the expected
sharp interface limit. More precisely, directly inspired by the recent
work of Fischer, Laux and Simon
[
24
]
on the (quantitative) convergence
of solutions of the Allen–Cahn equation to classical solutions
of mean curvature flow, we will work on
[0,T_{*})
with a relative energy

(6)

\displaystyle E[\varphi_{\varepsilon},v_{\varepsilon}|\chi,v]

\displaystyle:=\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{2}\big|v_{\varepsilon}{-}v\big|^{2}\,dx

\displaystyle\penalty\penalty\penalty\penalty\displaystyle+\int_{\Omega}\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\big|\nabla\varphi_{\varepsilon}\big|^{2}+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}W\big(\varphi_{\varepsilon}\big)-\xi\cdot\nabla\Big(\int_{-1}^{\varphi_{\varepsilon}}\sqrt{2W(r)}\,dr\Big)\,dx

as well as with a suitable measure for the difference in the phase indicators

(7)

\displaystyle E_{\mathrm{vol}}[\varphi_{\varepsilon}|\chi]:=\int_{\Omega}\bigg|c_{0}\chi-\int_{-1}^{\varphi_{\varepsilon}}\sqrt{2W(r)}\,dr\bigg||\vartheta|\,dx.

In the above definitions, the vector field
\xi

will be a suitable extension of the unit normal vector field
of the smoothly evolving interface
\mathcal{I}
whereas
\vartheta

will be a suitable truncation of the associated signed distance function
(for precise assumptions on
\xi
and
\vartheta
, we refer
to Subsection
3.1
and Subsection
3.3
,
respectively). Both qualitative and quantitative convergence then follows
from a Gronwall-type stability estimate for the two error
functionals (
6
) and (
7
).
The precise statement reads as follows.

Theorem 1

(Error estimates for general initial data)
.

Let
d\in\{2,3\}
, let
\Omega\subset{\mathbb{R}^{d}}

be a bounded domain with orientable and

C^{2}
-boundary
\partial\Omega
,
and consider two finite time horizons
0<T_{*}\leq T_{0}<\infty
.
Let
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
and let
(\varphi_{\varepsilon},v_{\varepsilon})
be a weak
solution of the Navier–Stokes/Allen–Cahn
system
(
1a
)–(
1f
)

with time horizon
T_{0}
and initial data
(\varphi_{\varepsilon,0},v_{\varepsilon,0})

in the sense of Definition
3
.
Finally, let
(\chi,v)
be a strong solution for the sharp interface
limit model
(
2a
)–(
2h
)

with time horizon
T_{*}
and initial data
(\chi_{0},v_{0})

in the sense of Definition
4
.

Then, for a.e.
T\in(0,T_{*})
there exists a constant

C=C(\chi,v,T)\in(0,\infty)
, a continuous vector field

\xi{=}\xi(\chi,v,T)\colon\Omega{\times}[0,T]\to{\mathbb{R}^{d}}
, and a continuous

\vartheta{=}\vartheta(\chi,v,T)\colon\Omega{\times}[0,T]\to\mathbb{R}

\displaystyle E[\varphi_{\varepsilon},v_{\varepsilon}|\chi,v](t)

\displaystyle\text{for a.e.\ }t\in[0,T],

(9)

\displaystyle E_{\mathrm{vol}}[\varphi_{\varepsilon}|\chi](t)

\displaystyle\leq e^{Ct}\big(E[\varphi_{\varepsilon},v_{\varepsilon}|\chi,v](0)+E_{\mathrm{vol}}[\varphi_{\varepsilon}|\chi](0)\big),

the above theorem leads to a sharp

Corollary 2

(Sharp convergence rates for well-prepared initial data)
.

Let the assumptions and notation of Theorem

For well-prepared initial data in the sense of

\displaystyle E[\varphi_{\varepsilon},v_{\varepsilon}|\chi,v](0)+E_{\mathrm{vol}}[\varphi_{\varepsilon}|\chi](0)\leq C(\chi_{0})\varepsilon^{2},

\displaystyle\big\|(v_{\varepsilon}{-}v)(\cdot,t)\big\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\bigg\|\bigg(c_{0}\chi(\cdot,t){-}\int_{-1}^{\varphi_{\varepsilon}(\cdot,t)}\sqrt{2W(r)}\,dr\bigg)\bigg\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}\leq Ce^{Ct}\varepsilon

3.
The relative energy functional

3.1.
Definition of the relative energy functional

In this subsection, we follow ideas first developed in
[
24
]
.
We start by defining the appropriate
BV
-pendant of
c_{0}\chi
at
the level of the phase-field model via

(14)

\displaystyle\psi_{\varepsilon}(x,t):=\int_{-1}^{\varphi_{\varepsilon}(x,t)}\sqrt{2W}(r)\,dr,\quad(x,t)\in\Omega\times(0,T_{0}).

We then introduce a “unit-normal vector field”
\mathbf{n}_{\varepsilon}\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
by means of

(15)

\displaystyle\mathbf{n}_{\varepsilon}(\cdot,t):=\begin{cases}\frac{\nabla\varphi_{\varepsilon}(\cdot,t)}{|\nabla\varphi_{\varepsilon}(\cdot,t)|}&\text{if }\nabla\varphi_{\varepsilon}(\cdot,t)\neq 0,\\
\mathrm{s}&\text{else},\end{cases}\qquad t\in(0,T_{0}),

with
\mathrm{s}\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
being a fixed but otherwise arbitrary unit vector.
Note that because of the previous two definitions we always have the relations

(16)

\displaystyle\mathbf{n}_{\varepsilon}|\nabla\varphi_{\varepsilon}|=\nabla\varphi_{\varepsilon}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathbf{n}_{\varepsilon}|\nabla\psi_{\varepsilon}|=\nabla\psi_{\varepsilon}\quad\text{throughout}\quad\Omega\times(0,T_{0}).

We now define a measure for the difference between the phase-field approximation

(\varphi_{\varepsilon},v_{\varepsilon})
and the classical solution
(\chi,v)
for the sharp interface
limit model as follows: for
t\in(0,T_{*})\subset(0,T_{0})
let

(17)

\displaystyle E[\varphi_{\varepsilon},v_{\varepsilon}|\chi,v](t)

\displaystyle:=\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{2}\big|(v_{\varepsilon}{-}v)(\cdot,t)\big|^{2}\,dx+\int_{\Omega}\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\big|\nabla\varphi_{\varepsilon}(\cdot,t)\big|^{2}+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}W\big(\varphi_{\varepsilon}(\cdot,t)\big)-(\xi\cdot\nabla\psi_{\varepsilon})(\cdot,t)\,dx.

The vector field
\xi
associated with the strong sharp interface
limit solution
(\chi,v)
is yet to be determined.
Note already that the above functional has
a form reminiscent of that of a relative entropy. Indeed,

(18)

\displaystyle E[\varphi_{\varepsilon},v_{\varepsilon}|\chi,v]=E[\varphi_{\varepsilon},v_{\varepsilon}]-\int_{\Omega}v_{\varepsilon}\cdot v+\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{2}|v|^{2}\,dx-\int_{\Omega}\xi\cdot\nabla\psi_{\varepsilon}\,dx,

where we dropped for notational convenience the dependence on the time variable.
Continuing in this fashion, a straightforward computation moreover shows

(19)

\displaystyle E[\varphi_{\varepsilon},v_{\varepsilon}|\chi,v]

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{2}|v_{\varepsilon}-v|^{2}+\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{2}\Big(\sqrt{\varepsilon}|\nabla\varphi_{\varepsilon}|-\frac{1}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}\sqrt{2W(\varphi_{\varepsilon})}\Big)^{2}+\int_{\Omega}(1-\xi\cdot\mathbf{n}_{\varepsilon})|\nabla\psi_{\varepsilon}|.

Ensuring non-negativity of
E[\varphi_{\varepsilon},v_{\varepsilon}|\chi,v]
thus motivates

|\xi|\leq 1
as an absolute minimal assumption on the vector field
\xi
.

The main goal of our approach is to derive a stability estimate on
E[\varphi_{\varepsilon},v_{\varepsilon}\,|\,\chi,v]

in terms of the initial distance
E_{0}:=E[\varphi_{\varepsilon,0},v_{\varepsilon,0}|\chi_{0},v_{0}]
by a Gronwall argument,
cf. the stability estimate (
8
) in Theorem
1
.
This in turn necessitates control on the time evolution
of the relative energy. In particular, we will need an appropriate control on the time
evolution of the vector field
\xi
. To this end, it turns out to be beneficial (mostly for clarity of
exposition and efficient organization of terms in the relative energy inequality)
to introduce a second vector field
B
. One should keep in mind the following interpretation
of the pair
(\xi,B)
: the vector field
\xi
will represent a suitable extension of the
unit normal vector field
\mathbf{n}_{\mathcal{I}}
for the smoothly evolving sharp interface
\mathcal{I}
,
whereas
B
shall denote a suitable extension of the associated
normal velocity vector of
\mathcal{I}
. More precisely, we impose the following
conditions on the pair
(\xi,B)
which will turn out to be sufficient.

For every
T\in[0,T_{*})
there are
c=c(\chi,T)\in(0,1)

and
C=C(\chi,v,T)\in(0,\infty)
such that it holds
(employing the convenient notation
\operatorname{dist}(\cdot,\mathcal{I})

representing the space-time map
\overline{\Omega}{\times}[0,T_{*})\ni(x,t)\mapsto\operatorname{dist}(x,\mathcal{I}(t))
):

•

(Regularity estimates) In terms of qualitative regularity, it has to hold

(20a)

\displaystyle\xi

\displaystyle\in C^{0,1}\big([0,T];L^{\infty}(\Omega;{\mathbb{R}^{d}})\big)\cap L^{\infty}\big([0,T];C^{1,1}_{c}(\Omega;{\mathbb{R}^{d}})\big),

(20b)

\displaystyle B

\displaystyle\in L^{\infty}\big([0,T];C^{0,1}_{c}(\overline{\Omega};{\mathbb{R}^{d}})\big),

with a bound on the corresponding highest-order
derivatives in form of

(20c)

\displaystyle\|(\partial_{t}\xi,\nabla^{2}\xi,\nabla B)\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega{\times}(0,T))}

\displaystyle\leq C.

•

(Coercivity and consistency) It holds

(20d)

\displaystyle|\xi|

\displaystyle\leq 1{-}c\min\{\operatorname{dist}^{2}(\cdot,\mathcal{I}),1\}

\displaystyle\text{a.e.\ on }\Omega{\times}[0,T]

(20e)

\displaystyle\xi

\displaystyle=\mathbf{n}_{\mathcal{I}}\text{ and }\nabla\cdot\xi=-H_{\mathcal{I}}

\displaystyle\text{on }\mathcal{I}.

•

(Approximate transport of
\xi
by
B
) We have

(20f)

\displaystyle\big|\partial_{t}\xi+(B\cdot\nabla)\xi+(\nabla B)^{\mathsf{T}}\xi\big|

\displaystyle\leq C\min\{\operatorname{dist}(\cdot,\mathcal{I}),1\}

\displaystyle\text{a.e.\ on }\Omega{\times}[0,T],

(20g)

\displaystyle\big|\xi\cdot(\partial_{t}+B\cdot\nabla)\xi\big|

\displaystyle\leq C\min\{\operatorname{dist}^{2}(\cdot,\mathcal{I}),1\}

\displaystyle\text{a.e.\ on }\Omega{\times}[0,T].

•

(Interpretation of
B
as a normal velocity) It holds

(20h)

\displaystyle\big|(B{-}v)\cdot\xi+\nabla\cdot\xi\big|

\displaystyle\leq C\min\{\operatorname{dist}(\cdot,\mathcal{I}),1\}

\displaystyle\text{a.e.\ on }\Omega{\times}[0,T],

(20i)

\displaystyle\big|\xi\cdot(\xi\cdot\nabla)B\big|

\displaystyle\leq C\min\{\operatorname{dist}(\cdot,\mathcal{I}),1\}

\displaystyle\text{a.e.\ on }\Omega{\times}[0,T].

3.2.
Coercivity properties of the relative energy functional

We collect several useful coercivity estimates for
E[\varphi_{\varepsilon},v_{\varepsilon}|\chi,v]
,
dropping in the process again for notational convenience the dependence
on the time variable. The proof of these estimates is provided
afterwards.

Lemma 5
.

Let the assumptions and notation of Subsection
3.1
be in place.
First, the relative energy
E[\varphi_{\varepsilon},v_{\varepsilon}|\chi,v]

provides control on the error in the fluid velocity by means of

(21)

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{2}|v_{\varepsilon}-v|^{2}\,dx\leq E[\varphi_{\varepsilon},v_{\varepsilon}|\chi,v].

Second, it entails a “tilt-excess” type control on the geometry in the form of

(22)

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}(1-\mathbf{n}_{\varepsilon}\cdot\xi)|\nabla\psi_{\varepsilon}|\,dx\leq E[\varphi_{\varepsilon},v_{\varepsilon}|\chi,v].

Third, one obtains control on the lack of equipartition of energy through

(23)

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{2}\Big(\sqrt{\varepsilon}|\nabla\varphi_{\varepsilon}|-\frac{1}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}\sqrt{2W(\varphi_{\varepsilon})}\Big)^{2}\,dx\leq E[\varphi_{\varepsilon},v_{\varepsilon}|\chi,v].

Fourth, for every
T\in[0,T_{*})
there exists
a constant
C=C(\chi,v,T)\in(0,\infty)
such that
for all times in
[0,T]
it holds

(24)

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}|\mathbf{n}_{\varepsilon}-\xi|^{2}|\nabla\psi_{\varepsilon}|\,dx+\int_{\Omega}\min\{\operatorname{dist}^{2}(\cdot,\mathcal{I}),1\}|\nabla\psi_{\varepsilon}|\,dx

\displaystyle\leq CE[\varphi_{\varepsilon},v_{\varepsilon}|\chi,v],

(25)

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}|\mathbf{n}_{\varepsilon}-\xi|^{2}\varepsilon|\nabla\varphi_{\varepsilon}|^{2}\,dx+\int_{\Omega}\min\{\operatorname{dist}^{2}(\cdot,\mathcal{I}),1\}\varepsilon|\nabla\varphi_{\varepsilon}|^{2}\,dx

\displaystyle\leq CE[\varphi_{\varepsilon},v_{\varepsilon}|\chi,v],

as well as finally

(26)

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\big(\min\{\operatorname{dist}(\cdot,\mathcal{I}),1\}+\sqrt{1{-}\mathbf{n}_{\varepsilon}\cdot\xi}\big)\big|\varepsilon|\nabla\varphi_{\varepsilon}|^{2}-|\nabla\psi_{\varepsilon}|\big|\,dx\leq CE[\varphi_{\varepsilon},v_{\varepsilon}|\chi,v].

Proof.

The assertions (
22
)–(
26
)
are essentially already contained
and proved in
[
24
, Subsection 2.3]
. For the sake of completeness,
we re-produce the straightforward argument here.

By (
14
) and (
15
), we can
write (
17
) as the sum of three integrals
with each featuring a non-negative integrand:

\displaystyle E[\varphi_{\varepsilon},v_{\varepsilon}|\chi,v](t)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{2}\big|(v_{\varepsilon}{-}v)(\cdot,t)\big|^{2}\,dx

\displaystyle+\int_{\Omega}|\nabla\psi_{\varepsilon}(\cdot,t)|-(\xi\cdot\nabla\psi_{\varepsilon})(\cdot,t)\,dx

\displaystyle+\int_{\Omega}\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\big|\nabla\varphi_{\varepsilon}(\cdot,t)\big|^{2}+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}W\big(\varphi_{\varepsilon}(\cdot,t)\big)-|\nabla\psi_{\varepsilon}(\cdot,t)|\,dx.

Using (
14
) and the chain rule, we can complete the square in
the last integral above, and this proves (
21
),
(
22
) and (
23
).
The estimate (
24
) follows from (
20d
)
and (
22
).

Next, adding zero and employing Young’s inequality in the form of

\displaystyle\ \varepsilon\left|\nabla\varphi_{\varepsilon}\right|^{2}

\displaystyle=\left|\nabla\psi_{\varepsilon}\right|+\sqrt{\varepsilon}\left|\nabla\varphi_{\varepsilon}\right|\left(\sqrt{\varepsilon}\left|\nabla\varphi_{\varepsilon}\right|-\frac{1}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}\sqrt{2W\left(\varphi_{\varepsilon}\right)}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\left|\nabla\psi_{\varepsilon}\right|+\frac{1}{2}\varepsilon\left|\nabla\varphi_{\varepsilon}\right|^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\left(\sqrt{\varepsilon}\left|\nabla\varphi_{\varepsilon}\right|-\frac{1}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}\sqrt{2W\left(\varphi_{\varepsilon}\right)}\right)^{2}

we infer

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}(1-\mathbf{n}_{\varepsilon}\cdot\xi)\varepsilon\left|\nabla\varphi_{\varepsilon}\right|^{2}dx

\displaystyle\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \leq 2\int_{\Omega}(1-\mathbf{n}_{\varepsilon}\cdot\xi)\left|\nabla\psi_{\varepsilon}\right|\,dx+4\int_{\Omega}\left(\sqrt{\varepsilon}\left|\nabla\varphi_{\varepsilon}\right|-\frac{1}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}\sqrt{2W\left(\varphi_{\varepsilon}\right)}\right)^{2}\,dx

(27)

\displaystyle\overset{\eqref{eq:coercivityRelEntropy2},\eqref{eq:coercivityRelEntropy3}}{\leq}CE[\varphi_{\varepsilon},v_{\varepsilon}|\chi,v],

which then together with
2(1-\mathbf{n}_{\varepsilon}\cdot\xi)\geq|\mathbf{n}_{\varepsilon}-\xi|^{2}

and (
20d
) leads to (
25
). Finally

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\min\{\operatorname{dist}(\cdot,\mathcal{I}),1\}\big|\varepsilon|\nabla\varphi_{\varepsilon}|^{2}-|\nabla\psi_{\varepsilon}|\big|\,dx

\displaystyle\overset{\eqref{eq:conditionsXiB4}}{\leq}C\int_{\Omega}\sqrt{1-\mathbf{n}_{\varepsilon}\cdot\xi}\sqrt{\varepsilon}\left|\nabla\varphi_{\varepsilon}\right|\left|\sqrt{\varepsilon}\left|\nabla\varphi_{\varepsilon}\right|-\frac{1}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}\sqrt{2W\left(\varphi_{\varepsilon}\right)}\right|\,dx.

So (
26

3.2
) and the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality.
∎

3.3.
Error in the phase indicators

The second step in the proof consists of deriving an estimate
for the error in the phase indicators

and
\chi

This in turn is done by means of a stability estimate à la

for an appropriate error functional defined for all

(28)

\displaystyle E_{\mathrm{vol}}[\varphi_{\varepsilon}|\chi](t):=\int_{\Omega}\big|\psi_{\varepsilon}(\cdot,t)-c_{0}\chi(\cdot,t)\big|\big|\vartheta(\cdot,t)\big|\,dx.

Here,
\vartheta\colon\overline{\Omega}{\times}[0,T_{*})\to[-1,1]
denotes
a (by the velocity field

transported) time-dependent weight function
satisfying the following conditions, which in turn will be sufficient to derive

.

For every
T\in[0,T_{*})
there are
c=c(\chi,T)\in(0,1)

and

such that it holds
(employing again the convenient notation
\operatorname{dist}(\cdot,\mathcal{I})

representing the space-time map
\overline{\Omega}{\times}[0,T_{*})\ni(x,t)\mapsto\operatorname{dist}(x,\mathcal{I}(t))

•

(Regularity estimates) We have

(29a)

\displaystyle\vartheta\in C^{0,1}\big([0,T];L^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega})\big)\cap L^{\infty}\big([0,T];C^{0,1}(\overline{\Omega})\big)

with a corresponding estimate for the highest-order derivatives in form of

(29b)

\displaystyle\|(\partial_{t}\vartheta,\nabla\vartheta)\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega{\times}(0,T))}

\displaystyle\leq C.

•

(Coercivity and consistency) It holds

(29c)

\displaystyle c\min\{\operatorname{dist}(\cdot,\mathcal{I}),1\}\leq|\vartheta|

\displaystyle\leq C\min\{\operatorname{dist}(\cdot,\mathcal{I}),1\}

\displaystyle\text{a.e.\ on }\Omega{\times}[0,T],

as well as for all

(29d)

\displaystyle\vartheta(\cdot,t)

\displaystyle<0

\displaystyle\text{a.e.\ in the interior of }\{\chi(\cdot,t){=}1\}\cap\Omega,

(29e)

\displaystyle\vartheta(\cdot,t)

\displaystyle>0

\displaystyle\text{a.e.\ in the interior of }\{\chi(\cdot,t){=}0\}\cap\Omega.

•

(Transport equation) Finally, it is required that

(29f)

\displaystyle\big|\partial_{t}\vartheta+(B\cdot\nabla)\vartheta\big|

\displaystyle\leq C\min\{\operatorname{dist}(\cdot,\mathcal{I}),1\}

\displaystyle\text{a.e.\ on }\Omega{\times}[0,T].

As in the case of the relative energy functional,
we conclude the discussion of this subsection
by recording useful coercivity properties of
the error functional
E_{\mathrm{vol}}[\varphi_{\varepsilon}|\chi]

(dropping yet again the dependence on the time variable).
More precisely, for every
T\in[0,T_{*})
there exists
a constant

such that
for all times in
[0,T]
it obviously holds that

(30)

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\min\{\operatorname{dist}(\cdot,\mathcal{I}),1\}|\psi_{\varepsilon}-c_{0}\chi|\,dx\leq CE_{\mathrm{vol}}[\varphi_{\varepsilon}|\chi].

More importantly, it holds for all
\lambda\in(0,1)

(31)

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}|c_{0}\chi{-}\psi_{\varepsilon}||v_{\varepsilon}{-}v|\,dx

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C}{\lambda}\big(E_{\mathrm{vol}}[\varphi_{\varepsilon}|\chi]{+}E[\varphi_{\varepsilon},v_{\varepsilon}|\chi,v]\big)+\lambda\int_{\Omega}|\nabla v_{\varepsilon}{-}\nabla v|^{2}\,dx.

Proof of the coercivity estimate (
31
).

Let
\delta=\delta(\chi,T)\in(0,\smash{\frac{1}{2}}]
be the thickness
of the tubular neighborhood
B_{\delta}(\mathcal{I}(t)):=\{x\in{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\colon\operatorname{dist}(x,\mathcal{I}(t))<\delta\}
of
\mathcal{I}(t)
. For fixed
t\in[0,T]
, we have

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega\setminus B_{\delta}(\mathcal{I}(t))}|c_{0}\chi{-}\psi_{\varepsilon}||v_{\varepsilon}{-}v|\,dx

\displaystyle\penalty\ \penalty\ \overset{\eqref{eq:coercivityBulkError1}}{\leq}\frac{1}{\delta}\int_{\Omega}\min\{\operatorname{dist}(\cdot,\mathcal{I}),1\}|c_{0}\chi{-}\psi_{\varepsilon}||v_{\varepsilon}{-}v|\,dx

\displaystyle\penalty\ \penalty\ \overset{\eqref{eq:conditionsWeakSolPhaseField2}}{\leq}\frac{C}{\delta}\int_{\Omega}\sqrt{\min\{\operatorname{dist}(\cdot,\mathcal{I}),1\}}|c_{0}\chi{-}\psi_{\varepsilon}|^{1/2}|v_{\varepsilon}{-}v|\,dx

\displaystyle\overset{\eqref{eq:coercivityBulkError1},\,\eqref{eq:coercivityRelEntropy1}}{\leq}\frac{C}{\delta}\big(E_{\mathrm{vol}}[\varphi_{\varepsilon}|\chi]{+}E[\varphi_{\varepsilon},v_{\varepsilon}|\chi,v]\big).

It remains to estimate the integral over
B_{\delta}(\mathcal{I}(t))
, and we shall use the following elementary inequality

(32)

\displaystyle\left(\int_{0}^{\tau}g(r)dr\right)^{2}\leq 2\|g\|_{L^{\infty}(0,\tau)}\int_{0}^{\tau}rg(r)dr,\quad\forall g(r)\geq 0,\tau\geq 0.

Recall that
\mathbf{n}_{\mathcal{I}}(y,t)
is the inward normal vector at

.
By the area formula and the one-dimensional Gagliardo–Nirenberg–Sobolev inequality,

\displaystyle\int_{B_{\delta}(\mathcal{I}(t))}|c_{0}\chi{-}\psi_{\varepsilon}||v_{\varepsilon}{-}v|\,dx

\displaystyle\penalty\ \leq C\int_{\mathcal{I}(t)}\int_{-\delta}^{\delta}|c_{0}\chi{-}\psi_{\varepsilon}||v_{\varepsilon}{-}v|(y{+}\mathbf{n}_{\mathcal{I}}(y,t)r)\,drd\mathcal{H}^{d-1}(y)

\displaystyle\penalty\ \leq C\int_{\mathcal{I}(t)}\sup_{|r|\leq\delta}|v_{\varepsilon}{-}v|(y{+}\mathbf{n}_{\mathcal{I}}(y,t)r)\left(\int_{-\delta}^{\delta}\left|c_{0}\chi{-}\psi_{\varepsilon}\right|(y{+}\mathbf{n}_{\mathcal{I}}(y,t)r)dr\right)d\mathcal{H}^{d-1}(y)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{def: sobolev 1d}}}{{\leq}}C\int_{\mathcal{I}(t)}\left\|(v_{\varepsilon}{-}v)(y{+}\mathbf{n}_{\mathcal{I}}r)\right\|_{L^{2}((-\delta,\delta);dr)}^{\frac{1}{2}}\left\|(v_{\varepsilon}{-}v)(y{+}\mathbf{n}_{\mathcal{I}}r)\right\|_{H^{1}((-\delta,\delta);dr)}^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\qquad\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \cdot\sqrt{\int_{-\delta}^{\delta}\left|r\|c_{0}\chi{-}\psi_{\varepsilon}\right|(y{+}\mathbf{n}_{\mathcal{I}}r)\,dr}\,d\mathcal{H}^{d-1}(y).

By Hölder’s and Young’s inequalities, the terms in the last step can be controlled
by the right hand side of (
31
).
∎

Proposition 6
.

Let the assumptions and notation of Theorem

(\xi,B)
be a pair of vector fields

as well as (

Then, the following relative energy inequality holds true

\displaystyle E[\varphi_{\varepsilon},v_{\varepsilon}|\chi,v](T)

\displaystyle\leq E[\varphi_{\varepsilon},v_{\varepsilon}|\chi,v](0)-\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla v_{\varepsilon}-\nabla v|^{2}\,dxdt

\displaystyle\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ -\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{2\varepsilon}\Big|H_{\varepsilon}+\sqrt{2W(\varphi_{\varepsilon})}(\nabla\cdot\xi)\Big|^{2}\,dxdt

\displaystyle\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ -\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{2\varepsilon}\Big|H_{\varepsilon}-\big((B-v)\cdot\xi\big)\varepsilon|\nabla\varphi_{\varepsilon}|\Big|^{2}\,dxdt
