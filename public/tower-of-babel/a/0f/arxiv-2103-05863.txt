AutoDO: Robust AutoAugment for Biased Data with Label Noise via Scalable Probabilistic Implicit Differentiation
Denis Gudovskiy 1 Luca Rigazio 2 Shun Ishizaka 3 Kazuki Kozuka 3 Sotaro Tsukizawa 3 1 Panasonic AI Lab, USA 2 AIoli Labs, USA 3 Panasonic Technology Division, Japan denis.gudovskiy@us.panasonic.com luca@aiolilabs.com {ishizaka.shun, kozuka.kazuki, tsukizawa.sotaro}@jp.panasonic.com

Abstract
AutoAugment [ 6 ] has sparked an interest in automated augmentation methods for deep learning models. These methods estimate image transformation policies for train data that improve generalization to test data. While recent papers evolved in the direction of decreasing policy search complexity, we show that those methods are not robust when applied to biased and noisy data. To overcome these limitations, we reformulate AutoAugment as a generalized automated dataset optimization (AutoDO) task that minimizes the distribution shift between test data and distorted train dataset. In our AutoDO model, we explicitly estimate a set of per-point hyperparameters to flexibly change distribution of train data. In particular, we include hyperparameters for augmentation, loss weights, and soft-labels that are jointly estimated using implicit differentiation. We develop a theoretical probabilistic interpretation of this framework using Fisher information and show that its complexity scales linearly with the dataset size. Our experiments on SVHN, CIFAR-10/100, and ImageNet classification show up to 9.3% improvement for biased datasets with label noise compared to prior methods and, importantly, up to 36.6% gain for underrepresented SVHN classes 1 1 1 Our code is available at github.com/gudovskiy/autodo .



1
Introduction

Data augmentation (DA) plays one of the key roles in improving accuracy of deep neural networks (DNNs)
[
18
]
. DA increases size and diversity of train dataset and consequently improves generalization to test data distribution. Unfortunately, DA design requires expert domain knowledge, dataset analysis, and numerous costly experiments. In real applications with biased
[
35
]
and noisy-label data
[
43
]
, the handpicking of DA becomes a challenging task.

Figure 1:
Shared-policy DA dilemma: the distribution of augmented train data (dashed blue) may not match the test data (solid red) in the latent space: ”2” is under-augmented, while ”5” is over-augmented. As a result, prior methods cannot match the test distribution and the decision of the learned classifier
f({\bm{\theta}})
is inaccurate.

The automation of DA aims to estimate data transformation models without incurring the aforementioned difficulties. The seminal AutoAugment
[
6
]
(AA) proposes a DA policy model estimated by reinforcement learning (RL). Though AA outperforms prior classification baselines, its policy search takes thousands of GPU hours. Follow-up works
[
12
,
20
,
11
]
address the search complexity problem while keeping comparable accuracy results.

Figure 2:
General setting: the validation dataset is selected to approximate the test data distribution
Q^{\mathrm{test}}_{{\bm{x}},{\bm{y}}}
. The train dataset is subject to the specified data distortions and unknown DA hyperparameters. Unlike prior DA models with shared policies for all train data points, our AutoDO model
g({\bm{\lambda}})
estimates hyperparameters
{\bm{\lambda}}_{i=1\ldots N}\in\mathbb{R}^{K\times 1}
for each of
N
train data points via implicit differentiation framework.

In this paper, we show that existing methods are not robust to train dataset distortions such as distribution bias and noisy labels. We attribute this to the policy model that
shares parameters
across all data points. We illustrate this with the toy classification task in Figure
1
. The distribution of digits ”2” and ”5” in the train dataset (solid blue) is uneven and less diverse than test distribution (solid red) in the latent space before a linear classifier. Then, the estimated augmentation policies virtually increase train data size (dashed blue line). However, such DA model
evenly
increases diversity of all digits: digit ”2” is under-augmented, while digit ”5” is over-augmented. This pushes decision boundary of the linear classifier in the wrong direction. A multi-class classifier is even less robust
[
35
]
, specifically, when it overfits to examples with noisy labels
[
43
]
.

To overcome these limitations, we propose to estimate DA hyperparameters for
each train data point
. Moreover, our model includes and jointly optimizes loss weights to capture data biases and soft-labels to address noisy labels. This reformulates the original AA shared-policy search task into a generalized automated dataset optimization (AutoDO). The objective of our AutoDO model is to match the distribution of a small clean unbiased validation dataset with a large distorted train dataset using a set of per-point hyperparameters as illustrated in Figure
2
. We optimize our model with large-scale hyperparameters using implicit differentiation
[
25
]
and analytically show that it is equivalent to maximizing the Fisher information between empirical datasets. Experiments on class-imbalanced data with noisy labels show the advantages of our approach.

Related work

DA has been widely used in computer vision from shallow convolutional neural networks (CNNs) for MNIST
[
32
]
to deep CNNs for large-scale ImageNet
[
18
]
recognition. Other applications such as segmentation
[
4
]
and object detection
[
24
]
benefit from DA as well. Recent Mixup
[
44
]
and CutMix
[
41
]
heuristically mix images to produce better regularization at the semantic level. However, all these methods require to handpick a set of hyperparameters.

Naturally, the idea to estimate DA hyperparameters emerged in AutoAugment
[
6
]
(AA) by sequentially sampling policies and using validation accuracy as a reward to update the RL controller. However, AA requires thousands of GPU hours to achieve results superior to the baseline. To speed up policy search, Ho
et al.

[
12
]
propose population-based training
[
15
]
that can be efficiently parallelized across distributed CPUs or GPUs. Unlike AA, this population-based augmentation (PBA) searches for DA policy schedules rather than individual augmentations. Recent Fast AutoAugment
[
20
]
(FAA) further extends this idea by searching for policy schedules that maximize the match between distribution of augmented train data and unaugmented validation data. Inspired by Tran
et al.

[
36
]
, Bayesian optimization is adopted by FAA to achieve density matching.

Though the methods in
[
12
,
20
]
substantially decrease the complexity of AA
[
6
]
, gradient-based optimization can be scaled up to even larger models and datasets. Furthermore, it is directly supported by differentiable DNNs and ML frameworks. Zhang
et al.

[
45
]
and Lin
et al.

[
21
]
adopt the REINFORCE gradient estimator
[
39
]
, which further decreases learning time. Authors use the same policy model of
[
6
,
20
]
, but estimate DA hyperparameters online within the bilevel gradient optimization framework. In this framework, an inner objective minimizes conventional train loss, while an outer objective minimizes adversarial
[
45
]
or validation
[
21
]
loss. Similarly, Hataya
et al.

[
11
]
and Li
et al.

[
19
]
employ the DARTS gradient estimator
[
23
]
inspired by its success in a neural architecture search.

Lastly, RandAugment
[
7
]
and UniformAugment
[
22
]
propose to dramatically decrease policy search space by a reparameterization scheme and to manually select only few DA hyperparameters. These near search-free methods are almost as effective as the computationally expensive search-based methods
[
6
,
20
]
. This observation raises questions on the
applicability and optimality
of the search-based methods. In this paper, we show that these DA methods with the same shared-policy model perform very similarly only on undistorted train datasets. At the same time, the search-based methods can be significantly more effective and robust in the case of biased datasets with label noise. However, prior search-based methods with the shared-policy model are ill-equipped to handle distorted datasets that is sketched in Figure
1
. Motivated by this, we develop our AutoDO model with the following contributions:

•

To control train dataset distribution more accurately, we introduce the AutoDO model that jointly optimizes per-point hyperparameters for DA, loss weights and soft-labels using implicit differentiation.

•

We analytically show that implicit differentiation minimizes the distribution shift between validation and train data by maximizing Fisher information. We also show a connection to DARTS
[
23
]
used in
[
11
,
19
]
.

•

Our model outperforms prior methods in image classification by up to 9.3% when the task model is trained on class-imbalanced data with label noise. More importantly, we show that it improves accuracy of underrepresented classes: by up to 36.6% for SVHN.

Theoretical background

3.1
Problem statement

Let
({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})
be an input-label pair where a given label
{\bm{y}}={\bm{1}}_{c}\in\mathbb{B}^{C\times 1}
is one-hot vector (
hard-label
) for classification task with
C
classes. A validation dataset
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{val}}=\{({\bm{x}}^{\mathrm{v}}_{i},{\bm{y}}^{\mathrm{v}}_{i})\}_{i\in{\mathbb{M}}}
of size
M
approximates test data
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{test}}
. A train dataset
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{train}}=\{({\bm{x}}_{i},{\bm{y}}_{i})\}_{i\in{\mathbb{N}}}
of size
N
may have a different from
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{val}}
distribution and noisy labels
{\bm{y}}
.

There are a vector of parameters
{\bm{\theta}}
for a
task model

f({\bm{\theta}})
and a vector
{\bm{\lambda}}
of
hyperparameters
. The latter only
implicitly
influences the train loss
\mathcal{L}({\bm{\lambda}},{\bm{\theta}})
such that the optimal parameters
{\bm{\theta}}^{\ast}
can be written as an
implicit function
of hyperparameters
{\bm{\theta}}^{\ast}({\bm{\lambda}})
. Then, the
inner objective
optimization can be expressed as

{\bm{\theta}}^{\ast}({\bm{\lambda}}):=\argmin_{{\bm{\theta}}}\mathcal{L}({\bm{\lambda}},{\bm{\theta}}),

(1)

where the train loss is the empirical risk:
\mathcal{L}({\bm{\lambda}},{\bm{\theta}})=\sum\nolimits_{i\in{\mathbb{N}}}\mathcal{L}({\bm{y}}_{i},\hat{{\bm{y}}}_{i})/N=\sum_{i\in{\mathbb{N}}}\mathcal{L}({\bm{y}}_{i},f({\bm{x}}_{i},{\bm{\theta}}({\bm{\lambda}})))/N
.

Since test distribution
Q^{\mathrm{test}}_{{\bm{x}},{\bm{y}}}
is not known, it is usually replaced by an empirical distribution
\hat{Q}^{\mathrm{val}}_{{\bm{x}},{\bm{y}}}
of the validation dataset
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{val}}

[
20
]
. Then, the optimal hyperparameters
{\bm{\lambda}}^{\ast}
minimize an
outer objective
as

{\bm{\lambda}}^{\ast}:=\argmin_{{\bm{\lambda}}}\mathcal{L}^{\ast}_{\mathrm{v}}({\bm{\lambda}})=\argmin_{{\bm{\lambda}}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{v}}({\bm{\lambda}},{\bm{\theta}}^{\ast}({\bm{\lambda}})),

(2)

where the validation dataset loss
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{v}}({\bm{\lambda}},{\bm{\theta}}^{\ast}({\bm{\lambda}}))=\sum\nolimits_{i\in{\mathbb{M}}}\mathcal{L}({\bm{y}}^{\mathrm{v}}_{i},\hat{{\bm{y}}}^{\mathrm{v}}_{i})/M=\sum_{i\in{\mathbb{M}}}\mathcal{L}({\bm{y}}^{\mathrm{v}}_{i},f({\bm{x}}^{\mathrm{v}}_{i},{\bm{\theta}}^{\ast}({\bm{\lambda}})))/M
, and it does not
explicitly
depend on
{\bm{\lambda}}
.

A solution for (
1
-
2
) problem statement can be found by
bilevel
(nested) optimization procedure
[
21
,
19
,
11
]
, where the inner objective is the conventional training to find
{\bm{\theta}}^{\ast}
and the outer objective is a hyperparameter optimization (HO) to estimate
{\bm{\lambda}}^{\ast}
. Unlike search-free methods
[
7
,
22
]
, HO can find nearly optimal
{\bm{\lambda}}^{\ast}
in automated fashion. However, the solution to bilevel optimization can be computationally costly for large-scale hyperparameters. We find a scalable solution using gradient-based HO for fully-differentiable DNN models. Specifically, our AutoDO model relies on an implicit differentiation framework.

3.2
Solution using implicit differentiation

Since differentiation of (
1
) is known
[
27
]
, we are only interested in obtaining
\partial\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{v}}/\partial{\bm{\lambda}}
in (
2
). This derivative at a point
({\bm{\lambda}},{\bm{\theta}}^{\ast}({\bm{\lambda}}))
can be found using a chain rule as

\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{v}}}{\partial{\bm{\lambda}}}=\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{v}}}{\partial{\bm{\lambda}}}+\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{v}}}{\partial{\bm{\theta}}^{\ast}({\bm{\lambda}})}\frac{\partial{\bm{\theta}}^{\ast}({\bm{\lambda}})}{\partial{\bm{\lambda}}}=\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{v}}}{\partial{\bm{\theta}}^{\ast}({\bm{\lambda}})}\frac{\partial{\bm{\theta}}^{\ast}({\bm{\lambda}})}{\partial{\bm{\lambda}}},

(3)

where the direct derivative
\partial\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{v}}/\partial{\bm{\lambda}}=0
, because
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{v}}
by definition does not explicitly depend on
{\bm{\lambda}}
. The derivative
\partial\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{v}}/\partial{\bm{\theta}}^{\ast}({\bm{\lambda}})
can be computed using conventional differentiation. However, the derivative
\partial{\bm{\theta}}^{\ast}({\bm{\lambda}})/\partial{\bm{\lambda}}
is not known because implicit function
{\bm{\theta}}^{\ast}({\bm{\lambda}})
is not defined.

Recently, Lorraine
et al.

[
25
]
proposed to use the implicit function theorem (IFT)
[
3
]
to find
\partial{\bm{\theta}}^{\ast}({\bm{\lambda}})/\partial{\bm{\lambda}}
. Let
S({\bm{\lambda}},{\bm{\theta}})=\partial\mathcal{L}({\bm{\lambda}},{\bm{\theta}})/\partial{\bm{\theta}}:{\bm{\Lambda}}\times{\bm{\Theta}}\rightarrow{\bm{\Theta}}
be a continuously differentiable function (
C^{1}
). For a fixed point
(\acute{{\bm{\lambda}}},\acute{{\bm{\theta}}})
, if
S(\acute{{\bm{\lambda}}},\acute{{\bm{\theta}}})=0
and
\det J_{{\bm{\theta}}}S(\acute{{\bm{\lambda}}},\acute{{\bm{\theta}}})\neq 0
: a) there exists an implicit function
{\bm{\theta}}=s({\bm{\lambda}})
for
\|{\bm{\lambda}}-\acute{{\bm{\lambda}}}\|\leq r_{1}
and
\|{\bm{\theta}}-\acute{{\bm{\theta}}}\|\leq r_{2}
, b) the function
{\bm{\theta}}=s({\bm{\lambda}})
is of class
C^{1}
, and its Jacobian is defined as

\partial{\bm{\theta}}({\bm{\lambda}})/\partial{\bm{\lambda}}=-\left[J_{{\bm{\theta}}}S({\bm{\lambda}},{\bm{\theta}})\right]^{-1}J_{{\bm{\lambda}}}S({\bm{\lambda}},{\bm{\theta}}).

(4)

Using the definition of
S({\bm{\lambda}},{\bm{\theta}})
and (
4
), we rewrite (
3
) as

\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{v}}}{\partial{\bm{\lambda}}}=-\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{v}}}{\partial{\bm{\theta}}}\left[\frac{\partial^{2}\mathcal{L}}{\partial{\bm{\theta}}\partial{\bm{\theta}}^{T}}\right]^{-1}\frac{\partial^{2}\mathcal{L}}{\partial{\bm{\theta}}\partial{\bm{\lambda}}^{T}}.

(5)

The difficulty in implicit differentiation (
5
) is to find inverse of Hessian
{\bm{H}}_{{\bm{\theta}}}^{-1}=\left[\partial^{2}\mathcal{L}/(\partial{\bm{\theta}}\partial{\bm{\theta}}^{T})\right]^{-1}
, which scales quadratically
({\bm{H}}_{{\bm{\theta}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{L\times L})
with the size of task model parameters
{\bm{\theta}}\in\mathbb{R}^{L\times 1}
. Fortunately, ML frameworks
[
27
]
are able to calculate Jacobian-vector products to reduce memory. Computationally,
{\bm{H}}_{{\bm{\theta}}}^{-1}
can be approximated by conjugate gradients
[
28
,
29
]
, quasi-Newton methods
[
1
]
, or Neumann series
[
17
,
25
]
. We rely on the latter approach.

Data pipeline of AutoDO model
g({\bm{\lambda}})
for joint dataset optimization: augmentation, loss reweighting and soft-labeling are accomplished by, correspondingly,
g_{A}({\bm{\lambda}}^{A})
,
g_{W}({\bm{\lambda}}^{W})
and
g_{S}({\bm{\lambda}}^{S})
using per-point hyperparameter vectors
{\bm{\lambda}}=[{\bm{\lambda}}^{A};{\bm{\lambda}}^{W};{\bm{\lambda}}^{S}]\in\mathbb{R}^{K\times 1}
.

Diagram of the proposed AutoDO for loss reweighting and soft-labeling: conventional cross-entropy loss for classification is replaced by a weighted Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence.

3.3
Implicit differentiation meets density matching

One may ask what is the meaning of implicit differentiation (
5
) in the context of the bilevel framework (
1
-
2
)? The classification loss in (
1
) typically minimizes Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence between joint train data distribution
Q_{{\bm{x}},{\bm{y}}}
with density
q({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})
and the learned model distribution
P_{{\bm{x}},{\bm{y}}}({\bm{\theta}})
with
p({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}}|{\bm{\theta}})
density. However, it can be shown that KL objective learns only a
conditional distribution

p({\bm{y}}|{\bm{x}},{\bm{\theta}})
of the train data distribution
Q_{{\bm{y}}|{\bm{x}}}

[
10
]
. In other words,
f({\bm{\theta}})
is a strictly
discriminative model
.

The KL loss (
1
) for the empirical train distribution
\hat{Q}_{{\bm{x}}}
with hard-labels
{\bm{y}}
can be simplified to

\mathcal{L}({\bm{\lambda}},{\bm{\theta}})=-\sum\nolimits_{i\in{\mathbb{N}}}\log p({\bm{y}}_{i}|{\bm{x}}_{i},{\bm{\theta}}({\bm{\lambda}}))/N.

(6)

By substituting (
6
) into each term in (
5
) and rewriting them as gradients for
i
th data point, it can be shown that
{\bm{u}}^{\mathrm{v}}_{j}({\bm{\theta}})=-\partial\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{v}}(j)/\partial{\bm{\theta}}=\nabla_{{\bm{\theta}}}\log p({\bm{y}}^{\mathrm{v}}_{j}|{\bm{x}}^{\mathrm{v}}_{j},{\bm{\theta}}({\bm{\lambda}}))
and
{\bm{u}}_{i}({\bm{\theta}})=-\partial\mathcal{L}(i)/\partial{\bm{\theta}}=\nabla_{{\bm{\theta}}}\log p({\bm{y}}_{i}|{\bm{x}}_{i},{\bm{\theta}}({\bm{\lambda}}))
are the Fisher scores
\wrt
validation and train data points
[
37
]
, respectively. Moreover, the expectation of Hessian
-{\mathbb{E}}_{\hat{Q}_{{\bm{x}}}}\left[{\bm{H}}_{{\bm{\theta}}}\right]
is equal to Fisher information metric
\mathcal{{\bm{I}}}_{{\bm{\theta}}}=\sum\nolimits_{i\in{\mathbb{N}}}{\bm{u}}_{i}({\bm{\theta}}){\bm{u}}_{i}({\bm{\theta}})^{T}/N

[
26
]
.

Then, the gradient of (
5
) for empirical datasets simply maximizes Fisher kernel between
\hat{Q}^{\mathrm{val}}_{{\bm{x}}}
and
\hat{Q}_{{\bm{x}}}
as

\nabla_{{\bm{\lambda}}}{\mathbb{E}}_{\hat{Q}^{\mathrm{val}}_{{\bm{x}}},\hat{Q}_{{\bm{x}}}}\left[\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{v}}\right]={\mathbb{E}}_{\hat{Q}^{\mathrm{val}}_{{\bm{x}}}}\left[{\bm{u}}^{\mathrm{v}}({\bm{\theta}})\right]\mathcal{{\bm{I}}}_{{\bm{\theta}}}^{-1}{\mathbb{E}}_{\hat{Q}_{{\bm{x}}}}\left[{\bm{u}}({\bm{\theta}}){\bm{u}}({\bm{\lambda}})^{T}\right],

(7)

where the detailed derivations are presented in Appendix
C
.

Therefore, the implicit differentiation in (
5
) finds the gradient (
7
) for an outer objective (
2
) that changes hyperparameters
{\bm{\lambda}}
in the direction to match density of
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{val}}
by
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{train}}
density on a Riemannian manifold with a local metric given by Fisher information
[
8
]
. If replace
\mathcal{{\bm{I}}}_{{\bm{\theta}}}
with the identity matrix and recall the IFT condition
\partial\mathcal{L}(\acute{{\bm{\lambda}}},\acute{{\bm{\theta}}})/\partial\acute{{\bm{\theta}}}=0
, (
7
) is equivalent to the DARTS gradient estimator
[
23
]
used in prior gradient-based methods
[
11
,
19
]
.

Typically, we can assume a setup in Figure
2
with a
hybrid
model where the discriminative model
f({\bm{\theta}})
is separated from an additional model
g({\bm{\lambda}})
. The latter changes the data distribution
p({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}}|{\bm{\lambda}})
. Hence,
g({\bm{\lambda}})
can be viewed as a
generative model
with the outer objective (
2
) to minimize KL divergence between
\hat{Q}^{\mathrm{val}}_{{\bm{x}},{\bm{y}}}
and
\hat{Q}_{{\bm{x}},{\bm{y}}}
distributions
[
14
]
.

4
The proposed AutoDO model

We use the theoretical framework introduced in Section
3
to
jointly optimize

\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{train}}
hyperparameters. Figure
2
shows a general data setup:
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{val}}
is selected to approximate
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{test}}
distribution, and
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{train}}
is subject to data distortions and unknown hyperparameters. In addition to a task model
f({\bm{\theta}})
, there is the proposed AutoDO model
g({\bm{\lambda}})
. The latter is parameterized by a vector
{\bm{\lambda}}
and can be applied to input or output of
f({\bm{\theta}})
. In contrast to prior models, we propose to use per-point dataset hyperparameters
{\bm{\lambda}}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{K\times 1}
that form a matrix
{\bm{\Lambda}}\in\mathbb{R}^{K\times N}
for dataset
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{train}}
of size
N
.

Figure
3
shows a diagram of our pipeline, where the AutoDO model
g({\bm{\lambda}})
is divided into application-specific sub-models:
g_{A}({\bm{\lambda}}^{A})
for augmentation,
g_{W}({\bm{\lambda}}^{W})
for loss reweighting, and
g_{S}({\bm{\lambda}}^{S})
for soft-labeling. Hence, a generic vector
{\bm{\lambda}}_{i}
is a concatenation of application-specific vectors:
{\bm{\lambda}}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{K\times 1}=[{\bm{\lambda}}_{i}^{A};{\bm{\lambda}}_{i}^{W};{\bm{\lambda}}_{i}^{S}]\in\mathbb{R}^{(A+W+S)\times 1}
.

A conventional data sampler uniformly generates train data points
{\bm{x}}_{i}
that are passed to the augmentation sub-model to perform
g_{A}({\bm{x}}_{i},{\bm{\lambda}}^{A}_{i})
in Figure
3
. This augmentation block outputs a modified train data point
{\bm{x}}^{A}_{i}
to the task model for training procedure. Simultaneously, soft-labels and loss weights are propagated to calculate a weighted Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence loss
w_{i}\mathcal{L}({\bm{y}}^{S}_{i},\hat{{\bm{y}}}_{i})
. This part is detailed in Figure
4
.

4.1
Augmentation sub-model

Our augmentation sub-model contains binary probabilities
{\bm{b}}_{i}\in\mathbb{B}^{A\times 1}
and continuous magnitudes
{\bm{m}}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{A\times 1}
that parameterize
A
operations for each
i
th data point. We argue that it is less restrictive to model each data point distribution compared to finite-length policies in
[
6
,
20
]
.

We use a multivariate Gaussian prior for magnitudes:
{\bm{m}}_{i}\sim\mathrm{rng}\frac{M}{10}\mathcal{N}\left({\bm{\mu}},{\bm{\Sigma}}_{i}\right)
, where
M
normalizes magnitudes as in
[
7
]
. The mean vector
{\bm{\mu}}\in\mathbb{R}^{A\times 1}
is usually consists of zeros or ones depending on operation. Only the covariance matrix
{\bm{\Sigma}}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{A\times A}
is parameterized by AutoDO. Although it is possible to model multivariate covariance at the expense of quadratically-growing hyperparameters, we experiment with an univariate option:
{\bm{\Sigma}}_{i}=\sigma(\textrm{diag}({\bm{\lambda}}^{A_{m}}_{i}))
, where
{\bm{\lambda}}^{A_{m}}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{A\times 1}
is the magnitude hyperparameter vector and
\sigma
is a conventional sigmoid nonlinearity. We initialize the magnitude hyperparameters to zeros, which sets the variances of
{\bm{\Sigma}}_{i}
to be in the middle of available range (
\mathrm{rng}
).

While the widely-used reparameterization trick allows to obtain gradients for magnitudes
{\bm{m}}_{i}
with continuous Gaussian distribution, it is not trivial for the binomial probability distribution of each operation:
{\bm{b}}_{i}\sim\mathrm{Bern}(\sigma({\bm{\lambda}}^{A_{b}}_{i}))\in\{0,1\}
. We use Gumbel-softmax
[
16
]
to relax backpropagation through the discrete Bernoulli distribution. Initially, we set probability of enabling the
a
th operation
b^{a}_{i}
to 25%.

Finally, we model a sequence (
a=1\ldots A
) of augmentation operations
\mathcal{O}:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathcal{X}
on the image space
\mathcal{X}
as

{\bm{x}}^{A}_{i}(a)=\begin{cases}\mathcal{O}({\bm{x}}^{A}_{i}(a-1),m^{a}_{i}),&\text{if $b^{a}_{i}=1$}\\
{\bm{x}}^{A}_{i}(a-1),&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

(8)

where
{\bm{x}}^{A}_{i}(0)={\bm{x}}_{i}
is the input to augmentation sub-model.

The number of hyperparameters in this particular sub-model
{\bm{\lambda}}^{A}_{i}=[{\bm{\lambda}}^{A_{m}}_{i};{\bm{\lambda}}^{A_{b}}_{i}]\in\mathbb{R}^{2A\times 1}
scales linearly with the number of augmentations
A
and train data
i\in{\mathbb{N}}
. In general, it is possible to jointly model augmentations not only along
A
-dimension using
{\bm{\Sigma}}_{i}
, but also along sample dimension using
\eg
Gaussian mixture model.

4.2
Reweighting and soft-labeling sub-models

Our loss reweighting sub-model multiplies
i
th train loss by a scalar:
g_{W}(\mathcal{L}_{i},\lambda^{W}_{i})=w_{i}\mathcal{L}_{i}
, where the weights are parameterized as:
w_{i}=1.44\times\mathrm{softplus}(\lambda^{W}_{i})
. Hyperparameters
\lambda^{W}_{i}
are initialized to zeros such that the weights
w_{i}
are ones at the start and their output is in range
[0:\infty)
.

Although soft-label estimation in the presence of noise has been recently studied in
[
34
,
40
]
, we approach it as part of joint optimization procedure with other types of applications. Moreover, we apply implicit differentiation rather than the alternating optimization as in
[
34
,
40
]
.

Our soft-label sub-model consists of hyperparameters
{\bm{\lambda}}^{S}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{C\times 1}
that are estimated for each label in
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{train}}
from the noise-free
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{val}}
. Similarly to
[
40
]
, we set soft-labels as follows:
{\bm{y}}^{S}_{i}=g_{S}({\bm{y}}_{i},{\bm{\lambda}}^{S}_{i})=\mathrm{softmax}({\bm{\lambda}}^{S}_{i})
. But, unlike
[
40
]
, we initialize
{\bm{\lambda}}^{S}_{i}
at epoch
e=0
in a way to output smooth-labels
[
33
]
:
{\bm{y}}^{S}_{i}(0)=(1-\alpha){\bm{y}}_{i}+\alpha/C
. Our hyperparameters at the initialization step are:
{\bm{\lambda}}^{S}_{i}(0)=({\bm{y}}_{i}-0.5)\log(1-C-C/\alpha)
, where
{\bm{y}}_{i}={\bm{1}}_{c}\in\mathbb{B}^{C\times 1}
are potentially noisy hard-labels and
\alpha
is a small constant. The
\alpha
constant is usually in the range
\alpha=[0.05-0.2]

[
33
]
.

Lastly, we replace the conventional asymmetric KL loss in (
1
) with its symmetric version. This step is necessary because we simultaneously optimize conditional distribution
p({\bm{y}}|{\bm{x}},{\bm{\theta}})
and joint data distribution
p({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}}|{\bm{\lambda}})

4.3
AutoDO optimization and complexity

Our optimization procedure is described in Alg.
1
with two additional tweaks compared to the theoretical description in Section
3
. First, we adopt widely-used stochastic optimization using mini-batches
\mathcal{B}
, which speeds up learning process at the expense of less accurate gradients. Second, the IFT in (
4
) requires to be at a minima point. Hence, we start HO only after reaching epoch
E
(line 10).

We estimate complexity of Alg.
1
in terms of forward and backward passes through a DNN. The conventional inner-objective training (lines 3-9) requires
2N
passes per epoch. The outer-objective implicit differentiation in lines 12-19 require
(5+T)N
passes per epoch, where
T
is the number of Neumann iterations. We set
T=5
using ablation study in
[
25
]
and
E=0.5\times\mathrm{epochs}
. This results in a factor of
(1+0.5(5+T)/2)=3.5\times
increase in computation (lines 3-19) compared to only conventional training (lines 3-9). In comparison, DARTS
[
23
]
in
[
11
,
19
]
, without a finite-difference approximation, has
T=0
and a factor of
(1+0.5\times 5/2)=2.25\times
increase in corresponding computation. Runtime estimates are compared in Appendix
A
.

6
Conclusions

We introduced the AutoDO model that addresses the limitations of existing policy-based AutoAugment models by incorporating the set of hyperparameters independently for each train data point. We jointly optimized these large-scale hyperparameters using an elegant low-complexity implicit differentiation framework. Our probabilistic interpretation using Fisher information matched the objective of minimizing the distribution shift between small noise-free unbiased validation dataset and large distorted train dataset.

AutoDO components such as augmentation hyperparameters, loss weights and soft-labels showed their effectiveness on SVHN, CIFAR-10/100 and ImageNet classification in ablation studies. For class-imbalanced train datasets with label noise, our AutoDO improved the average accuracy and, more importantly, aligned the precision on underrepresented classes by a significant margin compared to previous methods. Hence, our AutoDO is more robust in real applications with imperfect train datasets.

Appendix C
Theoretical background: implicit differentiation meets density matching

The gradient of expectation in (
7
) over two independent distributions
\hat{Q}^{\mathrm{val}}_{{\bm{x}}}
and
\hat{Q}_{{\bm{x}}}
is a product

\nabla_{{\bm{\lambda}}}{\mathbb{E}}_{\hat{Q}^{\mathrm{val}}_{{\bm{x}}},\hat{Q}_{{\bm{x}}}}\left[\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{v}}\right]=-{\mathbb{E}}_{\hat{Q}^{\mathrm{val}}_{{\bm{x}}}}\left[\nabla_{{\bm{\theta}}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{v}}\right]{\mathbb{E}}_{\hat{Q}_{{\bm{x}}}}\left[{\bm{H}}_{{\bm{\theta}}}^{-1}\nabla_{{\bm{\theta}}}\nabla_{{\bm{\lambda}}}^{T}\mathcal{L}\right].

The Hessian
{\bm{H}}_{{\bm{\theta}}}
itself is calculated as expectation over
\hat{Q}_{{\bm{x}}}
due to computational and stability reasons. It leads to a connection to the Fisher information metric
\mathcal{{\bm{I}}}_{{\bm{\theta}}}

[
26
]
as

{\mathbb{E}}_{\hat{Q}_{{\bm{x}}}}\left[{\bm{H}}_{{\bm{\theta}}}\right]={\mathbb{E}}_{\hat{Q}_{{\bm{x}}}}\left[\frac{\partial^{2}\mathcal{L}}{\partial{\bm{\theta}}\partial{\bm{\theta}}^{T}}\right]={\mathbb{E}}_{\hat{Q}_{{\bm{x}}}}\left[{\bm{u}}_{i}({\bm{\theta}}){\bm{u}}_{i}({\bm{\theta}})^{T}\right]=-\mathcal{{\bm{I}}}_{{\bm{\theta}}},

where
{\bm{u}}_{i}({\bm{\theta}})=-\partial\mathcal{L}(i)/\partial{\bm{\theta}}=\nabla_{{\bm{\theta}}}\log p({\bm{y}}_{i}|{\bm{x}}_{i},{\bm{\theta}}({\bm{\lambda}}))
are the Fisher scores
[
37
]
for
\log
-likelihood loss function.

The expectation
{\mathbb{E}}_{\hat{Q}_{{\bm{x}}}}\left[\nabla_{{\bm{\theta}}}\nabla_{{\bm{\lambda}}}^{T}\mathcal{L}\right]
contains a second-order derivative of the train loss
\mathcal{L}(i)=-\log p({\bm{y}}_{i}|{\bm{x}}_{i},{\bm{\theta}}({\bm{\lambda}}))=-\log p
from (6) that can be similarly simplified to

\begin{split}&{\mathbb{E}}_{\hat{Q}_{{\bm{x}}}}\left[\frac{\partial^{2}\mathcal{L}}{\partial{\bm{\theta}}\partial{\bm{\lambda}}^{T}}\right]=-{\mathbb{E}}_{\hat{Q}_{{\bm{x}}}}\left[\frac{\partial}{\partial{\bm{\theta}}}\left(\frac{\partial\log p}{\partial{\bm{\lambda}}^{T}}\right)\right]=\\
&-{\mathbb{E}}_{\hat{Q}_{{\bm{x}}}}\left[-\frac{1}{p^{2}}\frac{\partial p}{\partial{\bm{\theta}}}\frac{\partial p}{\partial{\bm{\lambda}}^{T}}+\frac{1}{p}\frac{\partial^{2}p}{\partial{\bm{\theta}}\partial{\bm{\lambda}}^{T}}\right]={\mathbb{E}}_{\hat{Q}_{{\bm{x}}}}\left[\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial{\bm{\theta}}}\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial{\bm{\lambda}}^{T}}\right],\end{split}

where the term
-{\mathbb{E}}_{\hat{Q}_{{\bm{x}}}}\left[\frac{1}{p}\frac{\partial^{2}p}{\partial{\bm{\theta}}\partial{\bm{\lambda}}^{T}}\right]=-\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial{\bm{\theta}}\partial{\bm{\lambda}}^{T}}\int_{x}pd{\bm{x}}=0
.

By substituting the above derivations, the final form of (
7
) and its practical variant with equal-probability data points can be obtained as

\begin{split}&\nabla_{{\bm{\lambda}}}{\mathbb{E}}_{\hat{Q}^{\mathrm{val}}_{{\bm{x}}},\hat{Q}_{{\bm{x}}}}\left[\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{v}}\right]={\mathbb{E}}_{\hat{Q}^{\mathrm{val}}_{{\bm{x}}}}\left[{\bm{u}}^{\mathrm{v}}({\bm{\theta}})\right]\mathcal{{\bm{I}}}_{{\bm{\theta}}}^{-1}{\mathbb{E}}_{\hat{Q}_{{\bm{x}}}}\left[{\bm{u}}({\bm{\theta}}){\bm{u}}({\bm{\lambda}})^{T}\right]\\
&=\left[\frac{1}{M}\sum_{j\in{\mathbb{M}}}{\bm{u}}^{\mathrm{v}}_{j}({\bm{\theta}})\right]\mathcal{{\bm{I}}}_{{\bm{\theta}}}^{-1}\left[\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i\in{\mathbb{N}}}{\bm{u}}_{i}({\bm{\theta}}){\bm{u}}^{T}_{i}({\bm{\lambda}})\right].\end{split}
