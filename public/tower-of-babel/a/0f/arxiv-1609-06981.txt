Fundamental energy cost for quantum measurement
Kais Abdelkhalek, Yoshifumi Nakata, David Reeb

Abstract
Measurements and feedback are essential in the control of any device operating at the quantum scale and exploiting the features of quantum physics. As the number of quantum components grows, it becomes imperative to consider the energetic expense of such elementary operations. Here, we derive energy requirements for general quantum measurement, extending previous models and obtaining stronger bounds in relevant situations, and then study two important classes of measurements in detail. One is the projective measurement, where we obtain the exact cost rather than a lower bound, and the other is the so-called inefficient measurement, in which we explicitly show that energy extraction is possible. As applications, we derive the energy-precision trade-off in quantum Zeno stabilisation schemes and the exact energy expense for quantum error correction. Our results constitute fundamental energetic limitations against which to benchmark implementations of future quantum devices as they grow in complexity.

2
Setting -our measurement model-

In this section, we explain the framework in which we study the energy cost of general quantum measurements. The framework is based on the one presented in Ref.
[
16
,
17
]
.

Unlike the naive expectation that the energy cost is simply given by the energy difference between the states before and after measurement, for counting all energy expense, it is necessary to explicitly consider the physical
implementation
of a measurement device.
The implementation should consist of two steps: the
measurement step

{\mathcal{M}}
, to store the measurement outcome in a memory for readout and feedback, and the
resetting step

{\mathcal{R}}
, to restore the measurement device to the initial setting so that the device can be used repeatedly.
Taking into account the resetting step is essential because, otherwise, it is possible to ‘borrow’ energy from the measurement device.
The total energy cost of general quantum measurements is obtained only when we consider the costs in these two steps properly.

In the following, we first overview general quantum measurement in Subsec.
2.1
. The measurement and resetting step are separately explained in Subsecs.
2.2
and
2.3
, respectively. We then explain how the total energy cost for quantum measurement should be considered in Subsec.
2.4
.

Before we get started, we here introduce our notation. We denote by
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
a set of all bounded operators on the Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
. Similarly, a set of all quantum states on
\mathcal{H}
is denoted by
\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{H})
, i.e.
\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{H})=\{\rho\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}):\rho\geq 0,\tr[\rho]=1\}
. To make it clear on which Hilbert space the operator acts on, we often use subscript, e.g.
X_{S}
is an operator on
\mathcal{H}_{S}
. We also denote the marginal states
\tr_{M}[\rho_{SM}]
and
\tr_{S}[\rho_{SM}]
of
\rho_{SM}
by
\rho_{S}
and
\rho_{M}
for short, respectively.

2.1
Overview of quantum measurement

A quantum measurement on a system
S
with Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}_{S}
is mathematically described by a
quantum instrument
, i.e. a set of completely positive maps
\{T_{k}\}_{k=1,...,K}
on
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}_{S})
satisfying
\sum_{k}T^{*}_{k}(\mathbbm{1}_{S})=\mathbbm{1}_{S}
, where
k
corresponds to the measurement outcome and
T^{*}_{k}
denotes the adjoint of
T_{k}
. The action of the map
T_{k}
on a state
\rho_{S}\in\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{H_{S}})
can always be written in terms of Kraus operators
M_{ki}
via
T_{k}(\rho_{S})=\sum_{i=1}^{I(k)}M_{ki}\rho_{S}M_{ki}^{\dagger}
, where
I(k)
is often referred to as the Kraus rank of
T_{k}
.

A measurement is called
efficient
or
pure
if
I(k)=1
for all
k
.
In addition to this, if
M_{k}
are all projection operators, then the measurement is called the
projective
measurement.
A measurement that is not efficient is called
inefficient
.
Note that a quantum instrument characterises both the probability
p_{k}=\tr[T_{k}(\rho_{S})]
to obtain outcome
k
and the corresponding post-measurement state
\rho^{\prime}_{S,k}=T_{k}(\rho_{S})/p_{k}
.

For a more comprehensive introduction of quantum measurements, we refer to Ref.
[
25
]
.

2.2
Measurement step
{\mathcal{M}}

When we are interested in the energy cost, it is important to consider all instruments incorporating all relevant systems that are involved in the measurement process.
In particular, the measurement outcome
k
has to be stored in degrees of freedom of a physical system
M
.
The register
M
is often modeled by a quantum system with Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}_{M}=\bigoplus_{k=1}^{K}\mathcal{H}_{k}
equipped with a Hamiltonian
H_{M}=\bigoplus_{k=1}^{K}H_{k}
, which naturally captures all the important properties one generally demands from a classical memory
[
26
]
.
The measurement outcome
k
is stored in a state
\rho^{\prime}_{M,k}\in\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{H}_{M})
, whose support is only on
\mathcal{H}_{k}
.
In this case, projection operators
\{Q_{k}\}_{k=1,...,K}
onto the subspaces
\mathcal{H}_{k}
, respectively, can be applied when one would like to read out the measurement outcome from the register.
Note that the set of projection operators
\{Q_{k}\}_{k=1,...,K}
satisfy
\sum_{k}Q_{k}=\mathbbm{1}_{M}
.

To take into account the register
M
, we define the
measurement step

\mathcal{M}
in the following way, which is quite common in the literature
[
16
,
17
,
26
]
.
First, a tuple
(\rho_{M},U_{SM},\{Q_{k}\})
is called an implementation of a quantum measurement, where
\rho_{M}
is the initial state of the memory register
M
, the unitary dynamics
U_{SM}
describes the interaction between the measured system
S
and the register
M
, and the projections
\{Q_{k}\}
on
M
with which the outcome
k
can be read out from the register after measurement.
To any such tuple
(\rho_{M},U_{SM},\{Q_{k}\})
, a measurement is described by the channel that takes as input an arbitrary initial state
\rho_{S}
of
S
and outputs the post-measurement state

\rho^{\prime}_{SM,k}=(\mathbbm{1}\otimes Q_{k})U_{SM}(\rho_{S}\otimes\rho_{M})U_{SM}^{\dagger}(\mathbbm{1}\otimes Q_{k})/p_{k}

(1)

on
S
and
M
with probability

p_{k}=\tr[(\mathbbm{1}\otimes Q_{k})U_{SM}(\rho_{S}\otimes\rho_{M})U_{SM}^{\dagger}]\ ,

(2)

for each
k=1,...,K
.
We then say that a tuple
(\rho_{M},U_{SM},\{Q_{k}\})
is
an implementation of a given measurement
\{T_{k}\}_{k}
if the associated measurement step outputs the correct post-measurement states on the measured system,
\tr_{M}[\rho^{\prime}_{SM,k}]=\rho^{\prime}_{S,k}=T_{k}(\rho_{S})/p_{k}
, with correct probability
p_{k}=\tr[T_{k}(\rho_{S})]
for all possible input states
\rho_{S}
.

The measurement step
\mathcal{M}
therefore outputs the state

\displaystyle\rho^{\prime}_{SM}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k}p_{k}\rho^{\prime}_{SM,k}

(3)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k}(\mathbbm{1}\otimes Q_{k})U_{SM}(\rho_{S}\otimes\rho_{M})U_{SM}^{\dagger}(\mathbbm{1}\otimes Q_{k}),

(4)

on
S
and
M
, which correctly stores the outcome
k
on
M
, since
\rho^{\prime}_{M,k}\equiv\tr_{S}[\rho^{\prime}_{SM,k}]
has by construction only support on
\mathcal{H}_{k}
.
The energy cost for the measurement step
\mathcal{M}
is defined by

\Delta E_{\mathcal{M}}:=\Delta E_{S}+\tr[H_{M}(\rho^{\prime}_{M}-\rho_{M})],

(5)

where
\Delta E_{S}=\tr[H_{S}(\rho^{\prime}_{S}-\rho_{S})]
with the system Hamiltonian
H_{S}
is a trivial energy change in the system due to the measurement.

Before we go onto the next section, we make two remarks.
First, it can be observed from Eq. (
4
) that the measurement step effectively produces a dephased state, rather than the state fully projected onto the subspace corresponding to some outcome
k
, because the values
k
are not stored in another register.
In Appendix
B
, we show that the dephasing operation can be unitarily realised without costing any additional energy, which may be of crucial importance if one would like to link the energy cost, Eq. (
5
), to the thermodynamics work.
Second, for any instrument
\{T_{k}\}_{k}
there exists an implementation
(\rho_{M},U_{SM},\{Q_{k}\})
. Conversely, any
(\rho_{M},U_{SM},\{Q_{k}\})
is an implementation of some instrument
\{T_{k}\}_{k}
.
In this sense, the above measurement model does not place any restrictions and can be considered to be fully general.

2.3
Resetting step
\mathcal{R}

After the measurement, the final state
\rho^{\prime}_{M}=\sum_{k}p_{k}\rho^{\prime}_{M,k}
of the register stores the information of the measurement outcome
k
. This information has to be erased by resetting the register to its initial state
\rho_{M}
after the measurement outcome is read out and before the register is used another time.
We call this process the
resetting step

\mathcal{R}
, which can be done with the help of a thermal bath
B
in the following way.

Let
H_{B}
be the Hamiltonian of the thermal bath
B
. The state is initially set to be thermal at inverse temperature
\beta=\frac{1}{k_{B}T}
:

\rho^{\prime}_{B}=\exp(-\beta H_{B})/Z_{B}

(6)

where
Z_{B}=\tr[\exp(-\beta H_{B})]
is the partition function.
To achieve erasure, the register unitarily interacts with the thermal bath
B
, described by
U_{MB}
, such that its state
\rho^{\prime}_{M}
after
\mathcal{M}
evolves back to the initial state.
That is, denoting
\rho^{\prime}_{MB}=\rho^{\prime}_{M}\otimes\rho^{\prime}_{B}
and
\rho^{\prime\prime}_{MB}=U_{MB}(\rho^{\prime}_{M}\otimes\rho^{\prime}_{B})U_{MB}^{\dagger}
, the states of
MB
before and after the resetting step, respectively, the following condition should be satisfied:

\tr_{B}[U_{MB}(\rho^{\prime}_{M}\otimes\rho^{\prime}_{B})U_{MB}^{\dagger}]=\rho_{M}.

(7)

This resetting process
\mathcal{R}
is also known as Landauer erasure
[
29
]
, for which energy cost is naturally defined by

\Delta E_{\mathcal{R}}:={\rm tr}[(H_{M}+H_{B})(\rho^{\prime\prime}_{MB}-\rho^{\prime}_{MB})].

(8)

Note that using additional resources, such as a thermal bath
B
, in the resetting step is inevitable since the resetting process generally needs to change the rank and the spectrum of the state, which cannot be done by unitary dynamics only on
M
. In this framework, we consider thermal states a free resource and make use of it because they can easily be obtained by weakly coupling quantum systems to thermal baths at the desired ambient temperature
T
.
Still, the energy cost of the resetting step, specifically to implement the unitary
U_{MB}
, needs to be accounted for.

We also emphasise that, in the resetting step
{\mathcal{R}}
, the measurement outcome
k
should not be made use of. The whole process in
{\mathcal{R}}
must solely rely on a thermal bath
B
at temperature
T
as for usual Landauer erasure
[
15
]
. This is because the post-measurement state
\rho^{\prime}_{S,k}
has usually been altered by feedback following
{\mathcal{M}}
.

2.4
Total energy cost for
\mathcal{M}
and
\mathcal{R}

Since the implementation of quantum measurements
\{M_{ki}\}
consist of the measurement and resetting steps, the overall energy expense is given by the sum of their costs,

E_{\rm cost}:=\Delta E_{\mathcal{M}}+\Delta E_{\mathcal{R}},

(9)

which is the main scope in this paper.

Throughout this paper, we are especially interested in the expression of
E_{\rm cost}
that is characterised only in terms of system quantities
\rho_{S}
,
H_{S}
and the measurement
\{M_{ki}\}
. This is because, although a quantum measurement
\{M_{ki}\}
has many different implementations, we are looking for the least expensive in terms of energy. Expressing
E_{\rm cost}
by the system quantities, we will obtain fundamental results that are independent of the concrete physical measurement implementation, which is in contrast to Ref.
[
14
,
27
]
.

Figure 2:
Physical implementation of a measurement.
To perform a general quantum measurement
\{M_{ki}\}
on state
\rho_{S}
, system
S
is input to a measurement device (dark box) that subjects it to a measurement step
{\mathcal{M}}
and leaves it in the final state
\rho^{\prime}_{S,k}
, conditional on the outcome
k
. Subsequent feedback on
S
is possible as different outcomes belong to orthogonal subspaces on the memory
M
. Before the device is used again, the memory
M
should be reset to a proper initial state
\rho_{M}
, using a thermal resource
\rho_{B}
. This process is called a resetting step
{\mathcal{R}}
. Hamiltonians
H_{S}
,
H_{M}
,
H_{B}
determine the total energy cost
E_{\text{cost}}=\Delta E_{\mathcal{M}}+\Delta E_{\mathcal{R}}
required to operate the device physically.

3
Energy cost of quantum measurement

We now provide our results about the energy cost of general quantum measurement.
Since it can be split into two parts, we derive the energy cost for the measurement and the resetting step in Subsecs.
3.1
and
3.2
, respectively. The total energy cost is then given in Subsec.
3.3
.

3.1
Energy cost for the measurement step
\mathcal{M}

We here show that the energy cost of implementing the measurement step
\mathcal{M}
, defined in Eq. (
5
), splits into a sum of operationally meaningful quantities as follows:

\beta\Delta E_{\mathcal{M}}=\beta\Delta E_{S}+\Delta S+\beta\Delta F_{M}+\mathcal{I}+\Delta_{Q}.

(10)

Here, using Eqs. (
1
), (
4
), and
\rho_{SM}:=\rho_{S}\otimes\rho_{M}
,
each term is given by

\displaystyle\Delta E_{S}=\tr[H_{S}(\rho^{\prime}_{S}-\rho_{S})]

(11)

\displaystyle\Delta S=S(\rho_{S})-\sum_{k}p_{k}S(\rho^{\prime}_{S,k})

(12)

\displaystyle\Delta F_{M}=F(\rho^{\prime}_{M})-F(\rho_{M})

(13)

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}=\sum_{k}p_{k}I(S:M|k)

(14)

\displaystyle\Delta_{Q}=S(\rho^{\prime}_{SM})-S(\rho_{SM}).

(15)

where
S(\rho)=-\tr[\rho\ln\rho]
is the von Neumann entropy,
F(\rho)
is the free energy given by
\tr[\rho H]-S(\rho)/\beta
, and
I(X:Y):=S(\rho_{X})+S(\rho_{Y})-S(\rho_{XY})
is the a mutual information.
It is clear that each term has an operational meaning that
the
\Delta S
is the average change of the information about the state,
the
\Delta F_{M}
is the purely thermodynamic contribution from the memory in terms of the free energy,

\mathcal{I}
is the average amount of correlations built up between
S
and
M
,
and finally
\Delta_{Q}
is the the total entropy increase during the measurement step induced by the projections
\{Q_{k}\}
.

Although Eq. (
10
) may have been implicitly obtained by extending the results in the literature
[
16
,
17
]
, our derivation is based on less assumptions and generalises the existing work (see Appendix
A
as well). Hence, we explicitly derive Eq. (
10
) below.

We first note that the post-measurement states
\rho^{\prime}_{M,k}
(and hence, also the states
\rho^{\prime}_{SM,k}
) are mutually orthogonal due to the projection operators
\{Q_{k}\}
. Denoting the Shannon entropy of the probability distribution
p_{k}
by
H(\{p_{k}\})=-\sum_{k}p_{k}\ln p_{k}
, the total entropy increase is given by

\displaystyle\Delta_{Q}

\displaystyle=S(\rho^{\prime}_{SM})-S(\rho_{SM})

\displaystyle=H(\{p_{k}\})+\sum_{k}p_{k}S(\rho_{SM,k}^{\prime})-\big(S(\rho_{S})+S(\rho_{M})\big)

\displaystyle=H(\{p_{k}\})+\sum_{k}p_{k}\left(S(\rho_{S,k}^{\prime})+S(\rho_{M,k}^{\prime})-I(S:M|k)\right)-\big(S(\rho_{S})+S(\rho_{M})\big)

\displaystyle=S(\rho^{\prime}_{M})-S(\rho_{M})+\sum_{k}p_{k}S(\rho_{S,k}^{\prime})-S(\rho_{S})-\sum_{k}p_{k}I(S:M|k)

\displaystyle=S(\rho^{\prime}_{M})-S(\rho_{M})-\Delta S-\mathcal{I},

(16)

where we used the additivity of the von Neumann entropy under tensor products, i.e.
S(\rho\otimes\sigma)=S(\rho)+S(\sigma)
for all states
\rho
and
\sigma
.

The energy cost of the measurement step is therefore given by

\displaystyle\beta\Delta E_{\mathcal{M}}

\displaystyle=\beta\Delta E_{S}+\beta\mathrm{tr}[H_{M}(\rho_{M}^{\prime}-\rho_{M})]

\displaystyle=\beta\Delta E_{S}+\beta F(\rho^{\prime}_{M})+S(\rho^{\prime}_{M})

(17)

\displaystyle\hskip 76.82243pt-(\beta F(\rho_{M})+S(\rho_{M}))

\displaystyle=\beta\Delta E_{S}+\beta\Delta F_{M}+\Delta S+\mathcal{I}+\Delta_{Q},

and we obtain Eq. (
10
).
\hfill\blacksquare

The equation (
10
) is insightful since it shows how the energy cost for the measurement step
\mathcal{M}
is split into operationally meaningful quantities. It is however sometimes nicer if the cost is given by quantities that are easy to deal with. To this end, we note that
\mathcal{I}
and
\Delta_{Q}
are non-negative:
\mathcal{I}
inherits this property from the non-negativity of the mutual information
I(S:M|k)
, whereas
\Delta_{Q}
is non-negative because the measurement step
\mathcal{M}
corresponds to a dephasing channel (see Subsec.
2.2
), which is unital
[
28
]
.
Hence Eq. (
10
) immediately implies that

\Delta E_{\mathcal{M}}\geq\Delta E_{S}\\
+k_{B}T\Big(S(\rho_{S})-\sum_{k}p_{k}S(\rho^{\prime}_{S,k})\Big)+\Delta F_{M}.

(18)

3.2
Energy cost for the resetting step
\mathcal{R}

Similar to the previous section, we here show that the cost
\Delta E_{\mathcal{R}}
of the resetting step
\mathcal{R}
can be expressed as a sum of operational quantities.
Letting
\beta
be the temperature of a thermal bath, we derive

\beta\Delta E_{\mathcal{R}}=-\beta\Delta F_{M}+\beta\Delta F_{B}+\mathcal{I}_{MB}.

(19)

Here,
\Delta F_{M}=F(\rho^{\prime}_{M})-F(\rho_{M})
as defined in the previous section, which also equals to
-(F(\rho^{\prime\prime}_{M})-F(\rho^{\prime}_{M}))
,
\Delta F_{B}:=F(\rho^{\prime\prime}_{B})-F(\rho^{\prime}_{B})
, and
\mathcal{I}_{MB}:=I(M:B)_{\rho^{\prime\prime}_{MB}}
is the mutual information built up between
M
and
B
during step
\mathcal{R}
.

The derivation of Eq. (
19
) is straightforward, but we will explicitly derive it for completeness:

\displaystyle\Delta E_{\mathcal{R}}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{tr}[H_{MB}(\rho_{MB}^{\prime\prime}-\rho^{\prime}_{MB})]

\displaystyle=\mathrm{tr}[H_{MB}(\rho_{MB}^{\prime\prime}-\rho^{\prime}_{MB})]-\frac{1}{\beta}S(\rho_{MB}^{\prime\prime})+\frac{1}{\beta}S(\rho^{\prime}_{MB})

(20)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{tr}[H_{MB}(\rho_{MB}^{\prime\prime}-\rho^{\prime}_{MB})]-\frac{1}{\beta}[S(\rho_{M})+S(\rho_{B}^{\prime\prime})-I(M:B)_{\rho^{\prime\prime}_{MB}}-S(\rho^{\prime}_{M})-S(\rho^{\prime}_{B})]

\displaystyle=F(\rho_{M})+F(\rho^{\prime\prime}_{B})-F(\rho^{\prime}_{M})-F(\rho^{\prime}_{B})+\frac{1}{\beta}\mathcal{I}_{MB}

\displaystyle=-\Delta F_{M}+\Delta F_{B}+\frac{1}{\beta}\mathcal{I}_{MB},

where we used in the second equality that unitary dynamics does not change the entropies, implying that
S(\rho_{MB}^{\prime\prime})=S(\rho^{\prime}_{MB})
.
\hfill\blacksquare

We further note that
\Delta F_{B}\geq 0
, which follows from

\displaystyle\Delta F_{B}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\beta}D(\rho^{\prime\prime}_{B}||\rho^{\prime}_{B}),

(21)

where
D
is the relative entropy, and Klein’s inequality
[
28
]
.
It is also the case that
\mathcal{I}_{MB}\geq 0
, which is a basic property of the mutual information.
Hence, it immediately follows from Eq. (
19
) that

\Delta E_{\mathcal{R}}\geq-\Delta F_{M}.

(22)

It has been shown that an optimal process, in the sense that exact equality in the inequality (
22
) holds, does in general not exist
[
29
]
. However, if the dimension of the thermal bath
B
is not restricted, one can approach the lower bound
-\Delta F_{M}
arbitrarily closely, e.g. by conducting a process that consists of multiple intermediate steps in which the memory gets temporarily thermalised
[
29
,
30
]
.
Since we are interested in the fundamental bound of the energy cost, we assume in the rest of the paper that the resetting step
\mathcal{R}
is conducted in such a way that

\Delta E_{\mathcal{R}}=-\Delta F_{M}.

(23)

We however emphasise that this assumption is only for simplification of our results, but does not restrict the validity in a more general setting. Dropping this assumption will only increase the lower bounds by additional non-negative quantities.

3.3
Total energy cost of a general quantum measurement

From Eqs. (
10
) and (
23
), we obtain the total energy cost needed to implement a quantum measurement, given by the sum of the cost of step
\mathcal{M}
and step
\mathcal{R}
,

\beta E_{\mathrm{cost}}=\beta\Delta E_{S}+\Delta S+\mathcal{I}+\Delta.

(24)

Using the relation
\Delta S+\mathcal{I}+\Delta_{Q}=S(\rho^{\prime}_{M})-S(\rho_{M})
, as shown in the derivation of Eq. (
16
), we have another expression that

\displaystyle\beta E_{\mathrm{cost}}=\beta\Delta E_{S}+S(\rho^{\prime}_{M})-S(\rho_{M}),

(25)

implying that the total energy cost is essentially determined by the entropy change in the memory.

Although we will make a direct use of Eq. (
25
) in later sections, it is important, from the viewpoint of the fundamental limit of the energy cost, to replace the left-hand side with the quantities that sorely depend on the system.
Using the non-negativity of
\mathcal{I}
and
\Delta_{Q}
, we have from Eq. (
24
) that

E_{\mathrm{cost}}\geq\Delta E_{S}+k_{B}T\big[S(\rho_{S})-\sum_{k}p_{k}S(\rho^{\prime}_{S,k})\big],

(26)

which is independent of the specific measurement implementation
(U_{SM},\rho_{M},\{Q_{k}\})
.

Note that Eq. (
25
) is consistent with applying Landauer’s principle
[
15
,
29
]
to the measurement process, as has been first done by Bennett
[
14
]
and was generalised to feedback protocols by Ref.
[
27
]
.
We however emphasise that our framework derives Eq. (
26
) in the purely quantum mechanical framework.

Our derivation is also similar to the previous one in Ref.
[
16
,
17
,
19
]
, but extends to more general case because, unlike the previous derivation, ours can be applied to inefficient measurements without assuming thermality of
\rho_{S}
. This generalization enables us to argue the energy cost of important quantum measurement in a much more concrete manner, as we will see in later sections.

See Appendix
A
for the comparison with previously known results
[
14
,
15
,
16
,
17
,
19
,
20
]
, including those not explained here, in more detail.

4
Special cases

In this section, to illustrate the strength of our results Eqs. (
26
) and (
32
), we consider two special cases of measurements:
projective measurements
in Subsec.
4.1
and
inefficient measurements
in Subsec.
4.2
.

4.1
Case 1: projective measurements

Projective measurements are the textbook examples of “standard” quantum measurements. They are described by projective measurement operators
M_{k}=P_{k}
with
P_{k}^{2}=P_{k}^{\dagger}=P_{k}
and map the initial state
\rho_{S}
of the measured system to the post-measurement state
\rho^{\prime}_{S,k}=P_{k}\rho_{S}P_{k}/p_{k}
with probability
p_{k}=\tr[P_{k}\rho_{S}]
. In particular, projective measurements belong to the class of efficient measurements due to the one-to-one correspondence between measurement operator
P_{k}
and outcome
k
.

A measurement device that implements such a projective measurement
\{P_{k}\}
on
S
is described by a tuple
(U_{SM},\rho_{M},\{Q_{k}\})
satisfying

\tr_{M}\left[(\mathbbm{1}\otimes Q_{k})U_{SM}(\rho_{S}\otimes\rho_{M})U_{SM}^{\dagger}(\mathbbm{1}\otimes Q_{k})\right]\\
=P_{k}\rho_{S}P_{k}\quad\forall\rho_{S},\forall k.

(27)

We require this equality to hold for any states
\rho_{S}
.

Due to Eq. (
25
), to obtain the energy cost
E_{\rm proj}
of projective measurements, it suffices to compute the entropy difference in the memory.
While the entropy difference is in general hard to compute, it can be simplified in the case of projective measurement.
Denoting
\tr_{S}\big[U_{SM}(\rho_{S}\otimes\rho_{M})U_{SM}^{\dagger}\big]
by
\tilde{\rho}_{M}
, the state of the memory after the measurement is given by
\rho^{\prime}_{M}=\sum_{k}Q_{k}\tilde{\rho}_{M}Q_{k}
.
Using the fact that
(U_{SM},\rho_{M},\{Q_{k}\})
is, by assumption, an implementation of the projective measurement
\{P_{k}\}
, we can show that the state
\tilde{\rho}_{M}
takes the form

\displaystyle\tilde{\rho}_{M}=\sum_{k}\tr[P_{k}\rho_{S}]\sigma_{M,k},

(28)

where the
\sigma_{M,k}=Q_{k}\sigma_{M,k}Q_{k}
are mutually orthogonal and have entropy
S(\sigma_{M,k})=S(\rho_{M})
for all
k
(see Appendix
C
).
The post-measurement state of the memory is therefore given by

\displaystyle\rho^{\prime}_{M}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k}Q_{k}\Big(\sum_{k^{\prime}}\tr[P_{k^{\prime}}\rho_{S}]\sigma_{M,k^{\prime}}\Big)Q_{k}

(29)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k}\tr[P_{k}\rho_{S}]\sigma_{M,k}

(30)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k}p_{k}\sigma_{M,k},

(31)

implying that
S(\rho^{\prime}_{M})=H(\{p_{k}\})+S(\rho_{M})
, where
H(\{p_{k}\})=-\sum_{k}p_{k}\ln p_{k}
is the Shannon entropy.
Hence, we obtain that

\beta E_{\mathrm{proj}}=\beta\Delta E_{S}+H(\{p_{k}\}).

(32)

This result is a significant improvement over Eq. (
26
) unless
\rho_{S}
was already classical, in which case both results agree.
It thus reveals that quantum coherences require much more energy for measurement than previously known.
The equation (
32
) is strong also from the viewpoint that, similar to single-shot treatments of general processes
[
20
]
, it is derived directly from quantum mechanics and goes beyond the traditional state-transformation ideas in thermodynamics
[
37
]
.
We compare Eq. (
32
) with the result obtained in Ref.
[
20
]
, which disagrees since the situations are different, in Appendix
A
.

4.2
Special case 2: inefficient measurements

Another interesting class of quantum measurment is an
inefficient
measurement.
As explained in Subsec.
2.1
, a measurement is said to be inefficient if the measurement changes the state as
\rho_{S}\mapsto\rho^{\prime}_{S}=\sum_{k,i}M_{ki}\rho_{S}M_{ki}^{\dagger}
. The index
i
ranges from
1
to the Kraus rank
I(k)
of the channel
T_{k}
.
We henceforth call the maximal Kraus rank of all elements
T_{k}
of a given quantum instrument the
inefficiency
I
of the quantum instrument
\{T_{k}\}
. Clearly, if
I=1
we recover the case of efficient measurements.

To derive the energy cost in this case, let us denote by
p_{k}=\tr\big[\sum_{i}M_{ki}\rho_{S}M_{ki}^{\dagger}\big]
the probability of receiving outcome
k
and by
\rho^{\prime}_{S,k}=\sum_{i}M_{ki}\rho_{S}M_{ki}^{\dagger}/p_{k}
the corresponding post-measurement state on
S
. Furthermore, define
r_{ki}:=\tr\big[M_{ki}\rho_{S}M_{ki}^{\dagger}\big]
. We then have

\displaystyle\sum_{k}p_{k}S(\rho_{S,k})

\displaystyle=\sum_{k}p_{k}S\Big(\sum_{i}\frac{r_{ki}}{p_{k}}\frac{M_{ki}\rho_{S}M_{ki}^{\dagger}}{r_{ki}}\Big)

(33)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k}p_{k}\left[H\Big(\Big\{\frac{r_{ki}}{p_{k}}\Big\}_{i}\Big)+\sum_{i}\frac{r_{ki}}{p_{k}}S\Big(\frac{M_{ki}\rho_{S}M_{ki}^{\dagger}}{r_{ki}}\Big)\right]

(34)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k}p_{k}\left[\ln I+\sum_{i}\frac{r_{ki}}{p_{k}}S\Big(\frac{\sqrt{\rho_{S}}M_{ki}^{\dagger}M_{ki}\sqrt{\rho_{S}}}{r_{ki}}\Big)\right]

(35)

\displaystyle\leq\ln I+S\Big(\sum_{k,i}\sqrt{\rho_{S}}M_{ki}^{\dagger}M_{ki}\sqrt{\rho_{S}}\Big)

(36)

\displaystyle=\ln I+S(\rho_{S}),

(37)

where Ineq. (
34
) and (
36
) follow from the well-known property of the von Neumann entropy, Ineq. (
35
) holds because the Shannon entropy of any probability distribution with
I
elements is bounded from above by
\ln I
and
S(LL^{\dagger})=S(L^{\dagger}L)
for any linear operator
L
.
Together with Ineq. (
26
), we obtain

\beta E_{\mathrm{cost}}\geq\beta\Delta E_{S}-\ln I.

(38)

Noting that the right-hand side of Ineq. (
38
) can be negative when
I>1
, it suggests that it may be possible to
extract
a positive amount of energy in the process of quantum measurement. This possibility was first pointed out in Ref.
[
21
]
by similarly showing in a different setting that a lower bound of the energy cost can be negative.
It was however not addressed whether the bound is tight and, if it is, how to achieve the bound.
We below provide an explicit construction of the measurement device that saturates Ineq. (
38
), showing the tightness of Ineq. (
38
) and proving for the first time that energy can be indeed extracted when the measurement is inefficient.

Figure 3:
Extracting energy from measurement.
A pure qubit
\rho_{S}=|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|_{S}
is measured in the spin-
z
basis by a device with bipartite memory
M=M_{A}M_{B}
and initial state
\rho_{M}=|0\rangle\langle 0|_{M_{A}}\otimes\mathbbm{1}_{M_{B}}/2
, in one of two different ways. Either measurement implementation yields the same outcome distribution
\{p_{k}\}
and enables feedback via
Q_{k}=|k\rangle\langle k|_{M_{A}}\otimes\mathbbm{1}_{M_{B}}
but counterintuitively, the inefficient one allows to extract useful energy
E_{\rm ext}
from the device, in contrast to previous results
[
17
,
18
]
.
(a)
The measurement is projective with
M_{k}=|k\rangle\langle k|
. Operating such a device can never yield energy,
E_{\rm ext}\leq 0
in accordance with Eq. (
32
).
(b)
The measurement is given by
M_{ki}=|i\rangle\langle k|/\sqrt{2}
and thus is inefficient. Intuitively, the device implements the same unitary interaction
U_{SM_{A}M_{B}}
as in (a), but with an additional swap of the systems
S
and
M_{B}
before the projections
\{Q_{k}\}
. This modified projective measurement always outputs a fully mixed state
\rho^{\prime}_{S,k}={\mathbbm{1}}_{S}/2
and yields energy
E_{\rm ext}\geq 0
(see Eq. (
44
)).

To better understand the mechanism to extract energy during the measurement process, we consider two measurements on a qubit
S
. The first one is an efficient measurement and hence, no energy can be extracted. The second is a slight variation of the first, but is inefficient and allows us to extract
k_{B}T\ln 2
of energy. See Figure
3
for a brief summary.

Our first example is a rank-1 projective measurement on a qubit system
S
with projection operators
\{|k\rangle\langle k|\}_{k=0,1}
and we denote by
\rho_{S}
and
\rho_{M}
the initial state of the measured system
S
and memory
M
, respectively. The final state of
S
and
M
is of the form

\rho^{\prime}_{SM}=\sum_{k=0,1}p_{k}|k\rangle\langle k|_{S}\otimes\rho^{\prime}_{M,k}\ ,

(39)

where
p_{k}=\langle k|\rho_{S}|k\rangle
and the states
\rho^{\prime}_{M,k}
have support on orthogonal subspaces.
One of the measurement devices
(\rho_{M},U_{SM},\{Q_{k}\})
implementing this projective measurement is given as follows: let a memory
M
consist of two qubits
M_{A}
and
M_{B}
with an initial state

\displaystyle\rho_{M}=|0\rangle\langle 0|_{M_{A}}\otimes\frac{\mathbbm{1}_{M_{B}}}{2}\ .

(40)

Additionally, we take projections
Q_{k}=|k\rangle\langle k|_{M_{A}}\otimes\mathbbm{1}_{M_{B}}
and the unitary interaction between system and memory

U_{SM}=\Big(|0\rangle\langle 0|_{S}\otimes|0\rangle\langle 0|_{M_{A}}\\
+|1\rangle\langle 1|_{S}\otimes|1\rangle\langle 0|_{M_{A}}+...\Big)\otimes\mathbbm{1}_{M_{B}},

(41)

where the dots indicate that any unitary extension is freely chosen.
It can be directly verified that this measurement device outputs the desired final state with
\rho^{\prime}_{M,k}=|k\rangle\langle k|_{M_{A}}\otimes\mathbbm{1}_{M_{B}}/2
.

Since this first example is a projective measurement, we have already derived in Subsec.
4.1
that the energy cost is

\beta E_{\mathrm{proj}}=\beta\Delta E_{S}+H(\{p_{k}\}),

(42)

implying that
E_{\mathrm{ext}}=\Delta E_{S}-E_{\mathrm{proj}}\leq 0
.
Hence, as explained, no energy can be extracted for any initial state of
S
.

The situation changes if we consider a slight variation, which is our second example.
Consider the situation where we are only interested in the outcome probabilities
p_{k}
of our measurement and not in the final state of
S
. We can then construct a measurement device that in addition to the previous device performs, after
U_{SM}
but before the projections
\{Q_{k}\}
, a swap operation
U_{S\leftrightarrow M_{B}}
between
S
and
M_{B}
(see Figure
3
). The unitary interaction between measured system and memory in this device is then given by
U_{S\leftrightarrow M_{B}}\circ U_{SM}
. The post-measurement state reads

\rho^{\prime}_{SM}=\frac{\mathbbm{1}_{S}}{2}\otimes\sum_{k=0,1}p_{k}|k\rangle\langle k|_{M_{A}}\otimes|k\rangle\langle k|_{M_{B}}.

(43)

Note that the measurement device correctly outputs the outcome probabilities.

In contrast to the device in the first example, it always leaves the measured system in the completely mixed state.
In this situation, We now have from Eq. (
25
) that

\beta E_{\mathrm{ext}}=\beta\Delta E_{S}-\beta E_{\mathrm{cost}}=\ln 2-H(\{p_{k}\})\geq 0,

(44)

implying that, if the measured system starts in any of the states
\{|k\rangle\langle k|_{S}\}
, this measurement device outputs
E_{\mathrm{ext}}=k_{B}T\ln 2
of useful energy.

The reason why this slight modification of the setup allows us to extract energy is that the additional swap process introduces inefficiency into the measurement: A measurement that always outputs states of the form
\rho^{\prime}_{SM}=\frac{\mathbbm{1}_{S}}{2}\otimes\rho^{\prime}_{M}
cannot have a one-to-one correspondence between measurement operator
M_{k}
and outcome
k
. Indeed, our device implements the quantum instrument
\{T_{k}(\rho_{S})=\sum_{i=1}^{2}\frac{1}{2}|i\rangle\langle k|\rho_{S}|k\rangle\langle i|\}
with inefficiency
I=2
and hence saturates our inefficiency bound given by Ineq. (
38
).

We finally comment that our energy results can be in fact considered to be statements about thermodynamic work
[
31
]
as we accounted for all energetic contributions while employing unitary actions.
It may then be surprising that, in contrast to previous findings
[
17
]
the implementation in Fig.
3
can extract
work
from the measurement device, while still respecting the Second Law of Thermodynamics (see Appendix
D
for the detailed discussion).

5
Applications

In this section, we provide two applications of our result to fundamental tasks in quantum information processing. One is quantum Zeno stabilisation and the other is quantum error correction (QEC).
We derive the fundamental energy cost for achieving these tasks.

5.1
Energy costs of quantum Zeno measurements

Quantum Zeno stabilization
[
22
]
is a paradigmatic quantum control protocol
[
23
]
.
For simplicity, we here consider a one-qubit system. The task is simply to stabilise a pure state
|0\rangle
for some time span
t
, while the system undergoes time evolution with a Hamiltonian
H_{S}
, which we assume to be
E\sigma_{X}
with the Pauli operator
\sigma_{X}
and energies
\pm E
.
Since the state
|0\rangle
is not the eigenstate of
H_{S}
, the state gradually rotates to be a different state. In the stabilization protocol, we try to avoid this effect.

In quantum Zeno stabilization protocol, this task is achieved using repeated projective measurements.
More precisely, we apply the projective measurement
\{M_{0}=|0\rangle\langle 0|,M_{1}=|1\rangle\langle 1|\}
, with
M_{0}
the projector onto the desired state at
N
regular time intervals
\delta t=t/N
over the time span
t
whilst the disturbing Hamiltonian
H_{S}
is acting.
The projective nature of the measurement makes the state indeed stabilised. This is due to the fact that the probability for the state
|0\rangle
to be transferred to
|1\rangle
by the time evolution scales quadratically in
\delta t
, i.e.
(\delta t)^{2}=(t/N)^{2}
. If projective measurements are applied
N
times, the total probability for the state to be changed is given by
(t/N)^{2}\times N=t^{2}/N
, which can be arbitrarily small if
N
is chosen to be sufficiently large.

The question we ask here is
how much energy should be invested for the quantum Zeno stabilization scheme?
On the one hand, one may naively think that the energy cost will diverge since the number
N
of projective measurements needs to be arbitrarily large for the perfect stabilization.
On the other hand, it may not be the case because the energy cost for one projective measurement also depends on
N
. The energy cost for one measurement is in general smaller for larger
N
since, in that case, the state to be measured is nearly close to
|0\rangle
, so that the measurement induces little effect on the state.
From these two intuitions, it will be clear that the overall energy cost is determined by the trade-off between
how many times the projective measurement should be repeated to achieve a required accuracy of the stabilization?
and
how much energy we should invest in each measurement?

Here, based on the result in Subsec.
4.1
, we derive the exact relation between the energy cost of the quantum Zeno stabilisation and its accuracy.

Let us denote the state on
S
after the
n
-th measurement by
\rho_{S}^{(n)}=(1-\epsilon_{n})|0\rangle\langle 0|+\epsilon_{n}|1\rangle\langle 1|
.
The probability that the process returns
|1\rangle
after
n
steps is then given by
\epsilon_{n}
.
Hence, the fidelity
[
28
]

F:=\langle 0|\rho_{S}^{(N)}|0\rangle
of the state after
N
steps is given by
1-\epsilon_{N}
.
Between the measurements, the system undergoes free time evolution according to the unitary
U=\exp(-i\delta tH_{S}/\hbar)
such that the probabilities after the
(n+1)
-th measurement change to
\epsilon_{n+1}=\epsilon_{n}\cos(E\delta t/\hbar)^{2}+(1-\epsilon_{n})\sin(E\delta t/\hbar)^{2}
. Since
\epsilon_{0}=0
by assumption, it follows that

\epsilon_{n}=\frac{1}{2}(1-\cos(2E\delta t/\hbar)^{n})=n\left(\frac{E\delta t}{\hbar}\right)^{2}+\mathcal{O}(\delta t^{4}).

(45)

We now calculate the energy cost. First, the measurement at each time step projects the state to either
|0\rangle
or
|1\rangle
, hence
\Delta E_{S}=0
.
From this fact and Eq. (
32
) in Subsec.
4.1
, the
n
-th measurement consumes energy
\beta E_{\mathrm{proj}}^{(n)}=H(\{\epsilon_{n},1-\epsilon_{n}\})
. The total energy required is then given by

\displaystyle\beta E_{\mathrm{Zeno}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=1}^{N}H(\{\epsilon_{n},1-\epsilon_{n}\})\ .

(46)

We are interested in stabilisation schemes that yield high target fidelity
F
, which can be achieved by applying the measurements in shorter and shorter time scales,
\delta t=t/N\rightarrow 0
, or in other words by applying more measurements
N\rightarrow\infty
in constant time span
t
. In this limit the higher order terms
\mathcal{O}(\delta t^{4})
of
\epsilon_{n}
will not contribute to the energy cost of the measurements, so we set
\epsilon_{n}\simeq n\left(\frac{E\delta t}{\hbar}\right)^{2}
. We then have
F\simeq 1-\frac{1}{N}\left(\frac{Et}{\hbar}\right)^{2}
and

\displaystyle\beta E_{\mathrm{Zeno}}

\displaystyle=-\sum_{n=1}^{N}\epsilon_{n}\ln\epsilon_{n}-\sum_{n=1}^{N}(1-\epsilon_{n})\ln(1-\epsilon_{n})

(47)

\displaystyle\simeq-\left(\frac{Et}{\hbar}\right)^{2}\left(\sum_{n=1}^{N}\frac{1}{N}\frac{n}{N}\ln\frac{n}{N}+\frac{N(N+1)}{2N^{2}}\ln\big[1-F\big]\right)-\sum_{n=1}^{N}\left(1-n\left(\frac{Et}{\hbar N}\right)^{2}\right)\ln\left[1-n\left(\frac{Et}{\hbar N}\right)^{2}\right]

(48)

\displaystyle\simeq\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{Et}{\hbar}\right)^{2}\ln\left[\frac{4.5}{1-F}\right],

(49)

to the leading order, where we have used that
\sum_{n=1}^{N}\frac{1}{N}\frac{n}{N}\ln\frac{n}{N}\simeq\int_{0}^{1}x\ln xdx=-1/4
and
\ln[1-n\left(\frac{Et}{\hbar N}\right)^{2}]\simeq-n(\frac{Et}{\hbar N})^{2}
in the limit
N\rightarrow\infty
.

Hence, we find that the total energy required for stabilisation grows logarithmically in
1/(1-F)
for increasing target fidelity
F
and diverges if the perfect stabilization (
F=1
) is required.
This implies that any restriction on the energy available for the stabilization scheme directly limits the achievable accuracy, which is more clear by rewriting Eq. (
49
) to be

F\simeq 1-4.5\exp\left[-\frac{2\hbar^{2}\beta}{(Et)^{2}}E_{\rm Zeno}\right].

(50)

Similar energy demands apply to Zeno schemes for dragging or holonomic computation
[
23
,
32
]
as well.

5.2
Energy cost of quantum error correction (QEC)

In any quantum information processing, the system is inevitably disturbed by noise. Hence, it is of significant importance to protect information from the noise. To this end, quantum error correction (QEC) was invented
[
6
,
7
]
, where the important information is encoded onto
logical
qubits
{\mathcal{L}}
using a QEC code
{\mathcal{C}}
, consisting of a number of
physical
qubits. The physical qubits are subject to noise, but logical qubits are designed to be free from noise due to the redundancy induced by the encoding.
The heart of QEC then consists in performing repeated measurements of the
error syndrome

s
on
{\mathcal{C}}
followed by suitable feedback operations
V_{s}
. When this error correction is performed frequently enough, reliable information processing is possible even on noisy hardware, as guaranteed by the threshold theorem
[
7
]
.

In this section, we consider the energy cost of quantum error correction.
Energetic considerations are paramount in this context since syndrome measurements with feedback must be performed many times and on many qubits in a scalable set-up, which is possible only when the energy cost for one syndrome measurement is not too high.
As the error syndrome measurement makes extensive use of projective measurements, we can exactly compute the energy cost based on our result, Eq. (
32
).

As a paradigmatic example, we examine the
5-qubit code

{\mathcal{C}}_{5}

[
33
]
, in which the state of a single logical qubit
|\psi\rangle=\alpha_{0}|0_{L}\rangle+\alpha_{1}|1_{L}\rangle
in the code space
\mathbb{C}^{2}_{\mathcal{L}}
, with
\alpha_{0},\alpha_{1}\in\mathbb{C}
and
|\alpha_{0}|^{2}+|\alpha_{1}|^{2}=1
, is encoded into the space
\mathcal{C}_{5}\equiv(\mathbb{C}^{2})^{\otimes 5}
of five physical qubits (see e.g.
[
28
]
for the concrete way of encoding).
As an error model, we consider the amplitude damping independently acting on each physical qubit, which is given by

\mathcal{N}_{\gamma}(\rho)=J_{1}\rho J_{1}^{\dagger}+J_{2}\rho J_{2}^{\dagger}

(51)

with Kraus operators
J_{1}=\sqrt{\gamma}|0\rangle\langle 1|
and
J_{2}=\sqrt{\mathbbm{1}-J_{1}^{\dagger}J_{1}}
, where
\gamma\in[0,1]
determines the noise strength.
Note that our formalism can be also applied to arbitrary noise models.
The noise on the physical qubits induces a certain error also on the logical qubit, changing the state to
\rho_{S,\gamma}=\mathcal{N}_{\gamma}^{\otimes 5}(|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|)
. The error can be however detected by the error syndrome measurements, described below, and can be corrected by the followed feed-back operation.

The syndrome measurements are done in the basis of each operator in
\{\mathcal{S}^{1},\mathcal{S}^{2},\mathcal{S}^{3},\mathcal{S}^{4}\}
, where

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}^{1}

\displaystyle=X\otimes Z\otimes Z\otimes X\otimes I

(52)

\displaystyle\quad\mathcal{S}^{2}

\displaystyle=I\otimes X\otimes Z\otimes Z\otimes X

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}^{3}

\displaystyle=X\otimes I\otimes X\otimes Z\otimes Z

(53)

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}^{4}

\displaystyle=Z\otimes X\otimes I\otimes X\otimes Z\ ,

where
X,Y,Z
denote the Pauli operators and
I
is the identity matrix. Note that all
\mathcal{S}^{j}
commute and so, they are jointly measurable.
Each syndrome measurement
\mathcal{S}^{j}
has outcomes
s^{j}\in\{-1,1\}
with probability
p^{(j)}_{s^{j}}=\tr[P^{(j)}_{s^{j}}\rho_{S,\gamma}]
, where
P^{(j)}_{s^{j}}
denotes the projector on the subspace corresponding to the eigenvalue
s^{j}
. The set of outcomes
(s^{1},s^{2},s^{3},s^{4})
is often called the
syndrome
of the error.

We here consider two implementations of the syndrome measurements. One is to use four devices, each implementing
\mathcal{S}^{j}
, which we call
separate measurement scheme
.
To introduce the other, we note that the same syndrome can be obtained by performing one measurement
\mathcal{S}
with
16
outcomes, given by projections
\{P_{s}\}_{s=0}^{15}
with
P_{s}=P_{s^{1}}^{(1)}P_{s^{2}}^{(2)}P_{s^{3}}^{(3)}P_{s^{4}}^{(4)}
. In this case, the syndrome
s:=(s^{1},s^{2},s^{3},s^{4})
is obtained with probability
p_{s}=\tr[P_{s}\rho_{S,\gamma}]
. We call this scheme
joint measurement scheme
.
Clearly, both schemes provide the same syndrome and so, there is no difference from the viewpoint of QEC. It however does not mean they both require the same amount of energy expense. Indeed, we show in the following that the joint measurement scheme costs less energy than the separable one.

In the following, we ignore the average energy change on
S
,
\Delta E_{S}
, which is just for simplicity.
We find from Eq. (
32
) that the energy cost
E_{\mathcal{C}_{5}}^{\mathrm{sep}}
of the separable measurement scheme is given by

E_{\mathcal{C}^{5}}^{\mathrm{sep}}=k_{B}T\sum_{j=1}^{4}H(\{p^{(j)}_{s^{j}}\}_{s^{j}=\pm 1})

(54)

and the cost
E_{\mathcal{C}^{5}}^{\mathrm{joint}}
of the joint measurement scheme is

E_{\mathcal{C}_{5}}^{\mathrm{joint}}=k_{B}TH(\{p_{s}\}_{s=0}^{15}).

(55)

Using the relation between the Shannon entropy and the mutual information, it is straightforward to obtain

\beta(E_{\mathcal{C}^{5}}^{\mathrm{sep}}-E_{\mathcal{C}_{5}}^{\mathrm{joint}})\\
=I(S^{1}:S^{2})+I(S^{1}S^{2}:S^{3})+I(S^{1}S^{2}S^{3}:S^{4}).

(56)

Noting that the mutual information is non-negative, we obtain
E_{\mathcal{C}^{5}}^{\mathrm{joint}}\leq E_{\mathcal{C}^{5}}^{\mathrm{sep}}
for any
\gamma
.
Further, the equality holds only when the measurement outcomes are uncorrelated, because the mutual informations in Eq. (
56
) represent the correlations between measurement outcomes.
In other words, we can conclude that the joint measurement scheme can exploit correlations in the measurement outcomes to reduce the cost of the resetting step
\mathcal{R}
, and hence it costs less than the separable measurement scheme.

Energy cost of quantum error correction.
For the 5-qubit code under amplitude damping noise, the cost
E_{{\mathcal{C}}_{5}}^{\rm joint}
given by Eq. (
55
) for the syndrome measurement increases with the noise level
\gamma

(red curve)
.
When the syndrome measurement is performed separately, the cost is given by

6
Summary and discussion

In this paper, based on the purely quantum-mechanical framework, we have derived a bound on the energy cost for general quantum measurement (Ineq. (
26
)), consisting of the cost of the measurement itself and the cost of resetting.
We then investigated two special cases of quantum measurement. One is a projective measurement, where we have derived the exact energy cost (Eq. (
32
)) rather than its lower bound.
The other is a so-called inefficient measurement, and we have shown that it is possible to extract energy in the process of measurement (Ineq. (
38
)) when we are only interested in the measurement outcome. By explicitly providing the setting and the measurement device which does extract a positive amount of energy, we prove that the energy extraction is indeed achievable (Eq. (
44
)).

These results have been applied to two fundamental protocols in quantum information processing, quantum Zeno stabilisation and QEC. Since both are using projective measurement, we obtained their exact costs. In quantum Zeno stabilisation protocol, the energy-precision trade-off relation has been derived (Eq. (
50
)), which explicitly provides the energetic limitation of stabilising quantum states by the protocol.
On the other hand, in QEC, we have especially considered the energy cost of the so-called 5-qubit code, and have shown that the energy cost depends on how to perform the syndrome measurement. More precisely, even if two different quantum measurements offer the same syndrome, and hence result in the successful error correction, the energy cost has been shown to be less when all the syndrome is measured at once (Eqs. (
54
) and (
55
)). This is because the correlation between the measurement outcomes can be made use of in the resetting process.

Our result about the projective measurement (Eq. (
32
)) also establishes a remarkable link between the viability of quantum technologies and the existence of uncertainty relations: whenever incompatible measurements have to be performed, as in quantum state tomography
[
34
]
or quantum Monte Carlo sampling
[
35
]
, entropic uncertainty relations
[
36
]
yield strictly positive bounds on the entropic term, independent of the input state. Physical energy constraints together with uncertainty relations therefore place fundamental limitations on tomographic accuracy or sample quality.

Our study paves the way for investigations into the energy costs of further elementary operations
[
37
]
in the quantum sciences or engineering. It would be particularly interesting to extend the strength of our implementation requirement to the single-shot approach
[
20
]
and quantify the arising energy fluctuations
[
21
]
.

Comparison with previous literature

In this section, we compare our framework and results about the energy cost of general quantum measurement with those in the literature. In particular, we here consider those in Sagawa/Ueda
[
16

17
]
, Jacobs
[

]
, Bennett

,

.

A.1
Comparison with Sagawa/Ueda
[
16
,
17
]

Our framework is based on the one from Sagawa and Ueda’s setting
[
16
,
17
]
, where they proved the following lower bound
E_{SU}
on the energy cost for an efficient quantum measurement
\{M_{k}\}
:

E_{\mathrm{cost}}\geq E_{SU}:=\Delta E_{S}+k_{B}T\mathcal{I},

(59)

where
\mathcal{I}=S(\rho_{S})+H(\{p_{k}\})+\sum_{k}\tr[M_{k}\rho_{S}M_{k}\ln M_{k}\rho_{S}M_{k}]
. As clarified in Erratum
[
17
]
, the derivation relies on the fact that the measurement is
efficient
. Our result given in Eq. (
26
) is hence a generalisation of their result to
any
measurements, including inefficient ones.
Our generalisation is proper since our bound given in Ineq. (
26
) reduces to Ineq. (
59
) (Ref.
[
17
]
) in the case of efficient measurements. We also note that the form of the bound given in Ref.
[
17
]
does not give a correct generalization to general inefficient measurements.

A.2
Comparison with Jacob
[
19
]

A generalisation of the lower bound in Ineq. (
59
) to general inefficient measurements was already inferred from Jacob’s work
[
19
]
.
Instead of calculating the energy expense in purely quantum mechanical set-up, the result in Ref.
[
19
]
answered the converse question:
after
some “black box” has performed the required measurement
\{M_{ki}\}
on the state
\rho_{S}
, how much energy can be extracted from a feedback protocol that makes use of the measurement result
k
and the post-measurement state
\rho^{\prime}_{S,k}
? This feedback process is required to map each post-measurement state
\rho^{\prime}_{S,k}
to the initial state
\rho_{S}
such that the overall process (measurement and feedback) is cyclic. It is found that the amount of energy that can be extracted in an optimal feedback process is given by the average free energy difference

E_{ext,Jacobs}=\sum_{k}p_{k}(F(\rho_{S})-F(\rho^{\prime}_{S,k}))=\Delta E_{S}+k_{B}T\Big[S(\rho_{S})-\sum_{k}p_{k}S(\rho^{\prime}_{S,k})\Big].

(60)

By assuming the Second Law of Thermodynamics, stating that no net amount of energy can be extracted in a cyclic process that involves a single thermal bath, the same amount of energy should be invested into the measurement device, implying that

E_{cost}\geq\Delta E_{S}+k_{B}T\Big[S(\rho_{S})-\sum_{k}p_{k}S(\rho^{\prime}_{S,k})\Big],

(61)

which is identical to our result.

This derivation however depends on two crucial assumptions. One is that the “measurement black-box” acts on a thermal state (Note that in Ref.
[
19
]
a non-thermal state
\rho_{S}
is first reversibly transformed into a thermal state in Eq. (7)). The other is the Second Law of Thermodynamics.
Because our results do not rely on these assumptions, they can be considered to be a
quantum mechanical derivation
of the Jacob’s one, or more precisely its generalisation to
any
quantum states, even those far from thermodynamics equilibrium.
Our approach has strong advantages especially when we consider the cost of quantum information processing, such as those we have done in this paper, because the state during information processing is in general not a thermal state and the validity of the laws of thermodynamics is rather unclear.
Our explicit microscopic modelling is also useful e.g. in order to display the work-extracting implementation of an inefficient measurement, which is never possible in the black-box treatment in Ref.
[
19
]
.

A.3
Comparison with Bennett
[
14
,
15
]

We also briefly compare our results with Bennetts’s
[
14
]
and other implementation-dependent bounds, such as those in Ref.
[
27
]
for feedback protocols.

According to Ref.
[
14
]
, the total energy cost for measurement and erasure is
E_{\rm cost}=\Delta E_{S}+k_{B}T(S(\rho^{\prime}_{M})-S(\rho_{M}))
, where
\rho_{M}
and
\rho^{\prime}_{M}
denote the
states of the measurement device
before and after the measurement step
{\mathcal{M}}
. We indeed rederived this statement within our framework in Eq. (
25
), but argue that this result by itself is not very useful in our context, since it is
not
stated in terms of the measurement specification
\{M_{ki}\}
(and
\rho_{S}
,
H_{S}
). For any fixed measurement
\{M_{ki}\}
, one can find
many
implementations
(\rho_{M},U_{SM},\{Q_{k}\})
yielding different values of
S(\rho^{\prime}_{M})-S(\rho_{M})
.

All of our results are given solely in terms of the measurement specification
\{M_{ki}\}
and the system quantities
\rho_{S}
,
H_{S}
. This feature allows us to address theoretical bounds on energy costs independently of the details of the implemented devices.

A.4
Comparison with Faist
et al.

[
20
]

In the context of general quantum operation, rather than thermodynamic operations, the energy costs for implementing quantum operation were given in Faist
et al.

[
20
]
with a great generality.
We below comment on the two main differences between our framework and theirs.
For simplicity, we ignore the trivial energy costs on the system
S
, i.e. setting
H_{S}=0
, as is done in Ref.
[
20
]
.

In Ref.
[
20
]
, all results are derived within the so-called single-shot scenario. The energy cost is then given by the
\epsilon
-smoothed conditional max entropy

[
A1
,
A2
]
, and later translated to the asymptotic
independently and identically distributed (i.i.d.)
setting.
This approach often considers the situation where the measurement device works correctly only on a certain subset of quantum states, which is controlled by the parameter
\epsilon
.
Due to this nature of their approach, a big difference arises, as we will describe later.
Another difference arises because they make use of the post-measurement state
\rho^{\prime}_{S}
in the resetting step, but we do not. In the situation we are mainly interested in,
\rho^{\prime}_{S}
as well as the measurement outcome
k
will be used in the later information processing, and so the post-measurement state is not available in the resetting step.
Below, we explain these differences more clearly by comparing our results with those in Ref.
[
20
]
in two most relevant cases, the zero-error single-shot case and the i.i.d. case.

In the zero-error single-shot case, the measurement device should act correctly for any initial state
\rho_{S}
, corresponding to the case where
\epsilon=0
in Ref.
[
20
]
. In this case, the energy cost is given by

E_{\rm Faist}^{0}=k_{B}T(\ln|\!|\mathcal{E}(\Pi_{S})|\!|_{\infty}+\ln\mathrm{rank}[\rho^{\prime}_{M}]),

(62)

where
\Pi_{S}
is the projector onto the support of the input state
\rho_{S}
.
This is surely different from our result, and the difference can be intuitively understood that
E_{\rm Faist}^{0}
is the
worst-case estimate
for the energy costs in the single-shot scenario.
It however does not mean
E_{\rm Faist}^{0}
is always larger than or equal to our energy cost since, in their setting, the post-measurement state
\rho^{\prime}_{S}
is made use of in the resetting step, which saves the total energy cost.

Indeed, if we consider the situation where a projective measurement in the computational basis
\{|0\rangle,|1\rangle\}
is performed on a pure state
(|0\rangle+|1\rangle)(\langle 0|+\langle 1|)/2
, then our result (Eq. (
32
)) yields the energy cost of
E_{\rm proj}=k_{B}T\log 2
, but
E_{\rm Faist}^{0}=0
according to Supplementary Note 4F, Example (III) in Ref.
[
20
]
. That is, our energy cost is greater than
E_{\rm Faist}^{0}
. On the other hand, if we perform the same measurement on the state
p_{0}|0\rangle_{S}\langle 0|+p_{1}|1\rangle_{S}\langle 1|
, our energy cost can be computed to be
k_{B}TH(\{p_{0},p_{1}\})
, which is generally less than
E_{\rm Faist}^{0}=k_{B}T\log\mathrm{rank}[\{p_{0},p_{1}\}]
.
Again, these differences are very natural because the settings in their and our analysis are rather different.

Which framework is best suited will highly depend what one would like to know.
We think that, in the applications we have considered in this paper, i.e. quantum Zeno stabilisation and QEC, our framework is suitable since we cannot use the post-measurement state for resetting.

We also compare our results with the i.i.d. results in Ref.
[
20
]
, which is a little more confusing. In this case, the initial state is assumed to be
\rho_{S}^{\otimes n}
, where
n
is often referred to as the number of
copies
, and the asymptotic limit of
n\rightarrow\infty
is taken in the end.
In this case, the energy cost in Ref.
[
20
]
is obtained after some simple calculation that

E_{\rm Faist}^{\rm iid}=S(\rho_{S})-\sum_{k}p_{k}S(\rho^{\prime}_{S,k}),

(63)

which equals to our lower bound presented in Ineq. (
26
) for general measurements (note that
H_{S}=0
), i.e.
E_{\rm Faist}^{\rm iid}\leq E_{\rm cost}
.
From the fact that our result is just a lower bound while Eq. (
63
) is the exact cost, one may naively think their result is stronger than ours. This is however not the case because, again, our setting differs from theirs.
To illustrate this point more clearly, let us consider the energy cost of projective measurements, where we have also derived the exact energy cost in Eq. (
32
).
Denoting our energy cost by
E_{\rm proj}
and theirs by
E_{\rm Faist}^{\rm iid,\mathrm{proj}}
, it also follows that
E_{\rm Faist}^{\rm iid,\mathrm{proj}}\leq E_{\rm proj}
and that the two values agree if and only if
\rho_{S}
was already diagonal with respect to the measurement basis, implying that
E_{\rm Faist}^{\rm iid,\mathrm{proj}}<E_{\rm proj}
for most initial states although both are
exact
energy costs. From this example, it is clear that they are considering radically different situations.

In this case, the main difference arises from the fact that the device constructed in Ref.
[
20
]
works correctly only on a certain subspace, known as the
typical subspace
of the initial state, rather than on the whole state space (see Supplementary Note 4A in Ref.
[
20
]
). The idea behind this is the information theoretic fact that the probability to obtain outcomes corresponding to the states outside the typical subspace, namely the states in the
atypical subspace
, becomes arbitrarily small if the number
n
of copies is sufficiently large. Hence, even if the device does not work correctly on the atypical subspace, the error can be ignored in the asymptotic limit,
n\rightarrow\infty
.
This situation is rather different from ours, and we require something
stronger
. That is, we require that the measurement device should act correctly on the
full
state space. Due to this stronger requirement, our energy cost result exceeds the one in Ref.
[
20
]
.

Whether to use the i.i.d. limit of Ref.
[
20
]
or our results again depends on the physical situation. We however argue that in many applications to quantum information processing, including those we have studied in this paper, our setting is more suitable since the state is in general not in the form of
\rho^{\otimes n}
, rather it is an entangled state
\rho_{\rm ent}^{(n)}
.

Appendix B
Energy cost of the dephasing operation in the measurement step
\mathcal{M}

In the main text, we claimed that the projections
\{Q_{k}\}
onto the different subspaces
\mathcal{H}_{k}
of the memory
M
employed during the measurement step
\mathcal{M}
is effectively seen as a dephasing operation, and that the dephasing operation can be implemented unitarily without any energy costs using an environmental system
E
. Here we prove this statement.
This result may be of independent interest
[
37
]
.
We also point out that the following result actually improves the main result of Ref.
[
18
]
: the lower bound from Ref.
[
18
]
on the energy cost of a dephasing operation is negative whenever it changes the state, whereas we will show that it cab be always exactly zero.

Let
T_{M}(\sigma_{M})=\sum_{k}Q_{k}\sigma_{M}Q_{k}
be the dephasing operation on the memory
M
. Then, for any Stinespring dilation
U_{ME}

[
A3
,
A4
]
of
T_{M}
, i.e.

T_{M}(\sigma_{M})=\tr_{E}[U_{ME}(\sigma_{M}\otimes\sigma_{E})U_{ME}^{\dagger}],\qquad\forall\sigma_{M},

(64)

where
\sigma_{E}
is a thermal state on an environment
E
with Hamiltonian
H_{E}
, we define the corresponding energy cost by

E_{\mathrm{deph}}:=\tr[H_{ME}(U_{ME}(\sigma_{M}\otimes\sigma_{E})U_{ME}^{\dagger}-\sigma_{M}\otimes\sigma_{E})].

(65)

Recalling that the Hamiltonian of the memory is given by
H_{M}=\bigoplus_{k=1}^{K}H_{k}
, where
H_{k}
is a Hamiltonian on the respective subspace
\mathcal{H}_{k}
with corresponding projection
Q_{k}
,
[Q_{k},H_{k}]=0
, which implies that the dephasing operation
T_{M}
does not change the average energy on
M
. Hence, all energy expenses of this implementation are due to energy changes in the environment
E
, i.e.

E_{\mathrm{deph}}=\tr[H_{E}(\sigma^{\prime}_{E}-\sigma_{E})].

(66)

The initial state of the environment is, by assumption, thermal, i.e.
\sigma_{E}=\exp(-\beta H_{E})/Z_{E}
with
Z_{E}
the partition function. The final state of
E
on the other hand can be characterised by using the statement in Appendix
C
, from which we obtain that there exist states
\sigma_{E,k}
with
S(\sigma_{E,k})=S(\sigma_{E})
for all
k
such that

\sigma_{E}^{\prime}=\sum_{k}\tr[Q_{k}\sigma_{M}]\sigma_{E,k},\qquad\forall\sigma_{M}.

(67)

Substituting this, and using the fact that thermal state minimizes the average energy on an entropic orbit,
\beta\tr[H_{E}(\sigma_{E,k}-\sigma_{E})]=D(\sigma_{E,k},\sigma_{E})\geq 0
for all
k
, where
D(\rho,\sigma)
is the relative entropy and is non-negative, it follows that

\displaystyle E_{\mathrm{deph}}

\displaystyle=\tr[H_{E}(\sigma^{\prime}_{E}-\sigma_{E})]=\sum_{k}\tr[Q_{k}\sigma_{M}]\tr[H_{E}(\sigma_{E,k}-\sigma_{E})]\geq 0.

(68)

That is, the energy cost of implementing the dephasing operation
T_{M}
is always non-negative.

We further show that there exist
\sigma_{E}
and
U_{ME}
such that the energy cost
E_{\mathrm{deph}}
of the corresponding dilation of
T_{M}(\sigma_{M})
is precisely zero. To this end, we first provide a characterisation of all unitaries
U_{ME}
which, given any fixed full rank state
\sigma_{E}
, satisfy Eq. (
64
), or more precisely,

\tr_{E}[U_{ME}(\sigma_{M}\otimes\sigma_{E})U_{ME}^{\dagger}]=\sum_{k}Q_{k}\sigma_{M}Q_{k},\qquad\forall\sigma_{M}.

(69)

In the end, we will describe a simple explicit construction.

Consider a pure state
\sigma_{M}=\psi=|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|
on
M
in the support of a fixed projector
Q_{k}
, i.e.
Q_{k}\psi=\psi
. From Eq. (
69
), the marginal on
M
,

\tr_{E}[U_{ME}(\psi\otimes\sigma_{E})U_{ME}^{\dagger}]=\psi,

(70)

is pure, while by Eq. (
67
), the marginal state
\sigma_{E}^{\prime}
on
E
is

\tr_{S}[U_{ME}(\psi\otimes\sigma_{E})U_{ME}^{\dagger}]=\sigma_{E,k},

(71)

where
\sigma_{E,k}
is a state which is independent of
\psi
. This implies that

U_{ME}(\psi\otimes\sigma_{E})U_{ME}^{\dagger}=\psi\otimes\sigma_{E,k}.

(72)

Let us now denote the spectral decomposition of
\sigma_{E}
by
\sigma_{E}=\sum_{j}\lambda_{j}|\phi_{j}\rangle\langle\phi_{j}|_{E}
. We note that all eigenvalues
\lambda_{j}
are strictly positive since
\sigma_{E}
is assumed to have full rank. Substituting the decomposition of
\sigma_{E}
into Eq. (
72
) we obtain

\sum_{j}\lambda_{j}U_{ME}(\psi\otimes|\phi_{j}\rangle\langle\phi_{j}|_{E})U_{ME}^{\dagger}=\psi\otimes\sigma_{E,k}.

(73)

We therefore obtain that

\tr_{E}\Big[U_{ME}(\psi\otimes|\phi_{j}\rangle\langle\phi_{j}|_{E})U_{ME}^{\dagger}\Big]=\psi,\qquad\forall j,

(74)

which further implies that the unitary
U_{ME}
that satisfies Eq. (
69
) with full rank states
\sigma_{E}
is of the form

U_{ME}\left(|\psi\rangle_{M}\otimes|\phi_{j}\rangle_{E}\right)=|\psi\rangle_{M}\otimes V_{\psi}|\phi_{j}\rangle_{E},

(75)

with a unitary
V_{\psi}
depending on
\psi\in Q_{k}
. Note however that the right-hand side of Eq (
75
) must be linear in
\psi
due to the linearity of the left-hand side. Hence,
V_{\psi}
can only depend on the label
k
of the subspace corresponding to
Q_{k}
, so we write
V_{k}=V_{\psi}
.

To characterise the unitaries
V_{k}
, we next evaluate Eq. (
69
) on a mixed initial state
\sigma_{M}
on
M
.
From Eq. (
75
), it follows that

\displaystyle U_{ME}(\sigma_{M}\otimes\sigma_{E})U_{ME}^{\dagger}=U_{ME}\Big(\sum_{ij}Q_{i}\sigma_{M}Q_{j}\otimes\sigma_{E}\Big)U_{ME}^{\dagger}=\sum_{ij}Q_{i}\sigma_{M}Q_{j}\otimes V_{i}\sigma_{E}V_{j}^{\dagger}.

(76)

which together with Eq. (
69
) leads to

\sum_{ij}\tr_{E}\big[V_{i}\sigma_{E}V_{j}^{\dagger}\big]Q_{i}\sigma_{M}Q_{j}=\sum_{k}Q_{k}\sigma_{M}Q_{k},\qquad\forall\sigma_{M}.

(77)

This holds if and only if the unitaries
V_{k}
satisfy

\tr[V_{i}\sigma_{E}V_{j}^{\dagger}]=\delta_{ij},\quad\forall i,j,

(78)

implying that the unitaries
V_{k}
must form an orthonormal unitary operator basis with respect to the modified scalar product Eq. (
78
). Such orthonormal unitary operator bases only exist if the Hilbert space dimension
d_{E}
of the environmental system
E
is sufficiently large compared to the number
K
of possible outcomes
k
, i.e.
d_{E}\geq\sqrt{K}
. For further properties of unitary operator bases we refer to Ref.
[
A4
]
.
Thus, given a full rank state
\sigma_{E}
on
E
, any unitary
U_{ME}
satisfying Eq. (
69
) is of the form Eq. (
75
) with unitaries
V_{k}
that meet the condition Eq. (
78
).

To show that there exists an implementation of the dephasing channel (
69
) with vanishing energy cost
E_{\mathrm{deph}}
, we may therefore choose the Hamiltonian of the environment
E
to be trivial,
H_{E}=0
, implying that the initially thermal state of
E
is maximally mixed for all
\beta
,
\sigma_{E}=\mathbbm{1}_{E}/d_{E}
, and that the energy cost is
E_{\mathrm{deph}}=0
by Eq. (
66
). The corresponding unitary
U_{ME}
that implements the dephasing channel is given by
U_{ME}=\sum_{k}Q_{k}\otimes V_{k}
with unitary operators
V_{k}
satisfying
\tr[V_{i}V_{j}^{\dagger}]=d_{E}\delta_{ij}
for all
i,j
, which can be easily checked that

\displaystyle\tr_{E}[U_{ME}(\sigma_{M}\otimes\sigma_{E})U_{ME}^{\dagger}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{k}Q_{k}\sigma_{M}Q_{k}.

(79)

More concretely, the unitaries
V_{k}
can for example be chosen as distinct elements from the set of unitaries

V_{l,m}=\sum_{r=0}^{d_{E}-1}\mathrm{e}^{\frac{2\pi i}{d_{E}}rm}|l+r\rangle\langle r|,\qquad l,m=0,1,...,d_{E}-1,

(80)

where the addition in
|l+r\rangle
is taken modulo
d_{E}
. These
d_{E}^{2}
operators can be understood as a discrete version of the Heisenberg-Weyl operators and indeed satisfy, as one can easily compute,

\tr[V_{l,m}V_{s,t}^{\dagger}]=d_{E}\delta_{l,s}\delta_{m,t},\qquad\forall l,m,s,t\in\{0,...,d_{E}-1\}.

(81)

Appendix C
The states after projective measurements

We here show an important statement about the states after projective measurements, which are used in Sebsec.
4.1
and Appendix
B
. More precisely, we show the following.
Let
\{P_{k}\}
be a projective measurement on a quantum system
S
. Let the Stinespring dilation of the “measurement channel”
T_{S}(\rho_{S}):=\sum_{k}P_{k}\rho_{S}P_{k}
be given by

\sum_{k}P_{k}\rho_{S}P_{k}=\tr_{E}\left[U_{SE}(\rho_{S}\otimes\rho_{E})U_{SE}^{\dagger}\right],\quad\forall\rho_{S},

(82)

where
\rho_{E}
is an initial state of a quantum system
E
and
U_{SE}
is a unitary on
S
and
E
.
Then, there exist quantum states
\sigma_{E,k}
with
S(\rho_{E})=S(\sigma_{E,k})
for all
k
such that the post-measurement state of
E
,
\rho_{E}^{\prime}=\tr_{S}\left[U_{SE}(\rho_{S}\otimes\rho_{E})U_{SE}^{\dagger}\right]
, can be written as

\rho_{E}^{\prime}=\sum_{k}\tr[P_{k}\rho_{S}]\sigma_{E,k},\quad\forall\rho_{S}.

(83)

If, additionally,
U_{SE}
and
\rho_{E}
together with projections
\{Q_{k}\}
on
E
form an implementation
(U_{SE},\rho_{E},\{Q_{k}\})
of the projective measurement
\{P_{k}\}
on
S
, and

P_{k}\rho_{S}P_{k}=\tr_{E}\left[(\mathbbm{1}\otimes Q_{k})U_{SE}(\rho_{S}\otimes\rho_{E})U_{SE}^{\dagger}(\mathbbm{1}\otimes Q_{k})\right],\quad\forall\rho_{S},\ \forall k,

(84)

then
\sigma_{E,k}=Q_{k}\sigma_{E,k}Q_{k}
for all
k
, i.e. the
\sigma_{E,k}
are mutually orthogonal.

The proof is based on the Stinespring dilation theorem, according to which we can always write the channel
T_{S}(\rho_{S})
as a unitary
U_{SA}
acting on
S
and an ancilla
A
initially in a pure state
|0\rangle\langle 0|_{A}
such as

T_{S}(\rho_{S})=\tr_{A}[U_{SA}(\rho_{S}\otimes|0\rangle\langle 0|_{A})U_{SA}^{\dagger}].

(85)

The minimal Stinespring dilation can be chosen to be any unitary extension
U_{SA}
of the operator
\sum_{k}P_{k}\otimes|k\rangle\langle 0|_{A}
, whose action is only defined on states of the form
|\psi\rangle_{S}\otimes|0\rangle_{A}
, where the ancilla Hilbert space
A
is spanned by the orthonormal basis
|k\rangle

[
28
]
. The corresponding complementary channel takes the form

\displaystyle T_{A}(\rho_{S})

\displaystyle:=\tr_{S}[U_{SA}(\rho_{S}\otimes|0\rangle\langle 0|_{A})U_{SA}^{\dagger}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{k}\tr[P_{k}\rho_{S}]|k\rangle\langle k|_{A}.

(86)

This channel is not the only possible complementary channel of
T_{S}
. Using Eq. (
82
), we find another complementary channel,

T_{E\tilde{E}}(\rho_{S})=\tr_{S}[U_{SE\tilde{E}}(\rho_{S}\otimes\psi_{E\tilde{E}})U_{SE\tilde{E}}^{\dagger}]\ ,

(87)

where
\tilde{E}
is a purifying system of
E
such that the pure state
\psi_{E\tilde{E}}
satisfies
\tr_{\tilde{E}}[\psi_{E\tilde{E}}]=\rho_{E}
and
U_{SE\tilde{E}}:=U_{SE}\otimes\mathbbm{1}_{\tilde{E}}
. The Stinespring theorem states that these two complementary channels,
T_{A}
and
T_{E\tilde{E}}
are related by an isometry
V:\mathcal{H}_{A}\rightarrow\mathcal{H}_{E}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{\tilde{E}}
. Hence, we obtain

T_{E\tilde{E}}(\rho_{S})=\sum_{k}\tr[P_{k}\rho_{S}]|\gamma_{k}\rangle\langle\gamma_{k}|_{E\tilde{E}}

(88)

with
|\gamma_{k}\rangle_{E\tilde{E}}:=V|k\rangle_{A}
forming an orthonormal basis.
Note that the complementary channel
T_{E\tilde{E}}
and the final state
\rho^{\prime}_{E}
of
E
are, by construction, linked via the partial trace,

\displaystyle\rho^{\prime}_{E}

\displaystyle=\tr_{S}\left[U_{SE}(\rho_{S}\otimes\rho_{E})U_{SE}^{\dagger}\right]=\tr_{\tilde{E}}\left[T_{E\tilde{E}}(\rho_{S})\right]\ .

(89)

Hence, the final state on
E
takes the form

\rho^{\prime}_{E}=\sum_{k}\tr[P_{k}\rho_{S}]\sigma_{E,k},\qquad\forall\rho_{S},

(90)

where we define the states
\sigma_{E,k}=\tr_{\tilde{E}}[V|k\rangle\langle k|_{A}V^{\dagger}]
, which are independent of
\rho_{S}
.

To show
S(\rho_{E})=S(\sigma_{E,k})
, let now
\rho_{S}=\psi_{k}
be a pure state supported on the subspace characterised by one
P_{k}
, i.e.
P_{k}\psi_{k}=\psi_{k}
. Then by Eq. (
90
)

\rho^{\prime}_{E}=\tr_{S}[U_{SE}(\psi_{k}\otimes\rho_{E})U_{SE}^{\dagger}]=\sigma_{E,k},

(91)

and the final state on
S
is pure,

\rho_{S}^{\prime}=\tr_{E}[U_{SE}(\psi_{k}\otimes\rho_{E})U_{SE}^{\dagger}]=\sum_{k^{\prime}}P_{k^{\prime}}\psi_{k}P_{k^{\prime}}=\psi_{k}.

(92)

Hence there are no correlations between the marginals of the final
SE
state, i.e.
U_{SE}(\psi_{k}\otimes\rho_{E})U_{SE}^{\dagger}=\psi_{k}\otimes\sigma_{E,k}
. Since unitaries do not change the spectrum, we have
S(\rho_{E})=S(\sigma_{E,k})
, which concludes the first part of the proof.

For the second part of the proof, we start with Eq. (
84
). By summing Eq. (
84
) over all
k
, we obtain Eq. (
82
), implying that all statements within the first part of the proof remain valid. We can thus take
\psi_{k}
to be a state in the support of
P_{k}
as above to find by Eq. (
91
) that

\displaystyle\sigma_{E,k}

\displaystyle=\tr_{S}\big[U_{SE}(\psi_{k}\otimes\rho_{E})U_{SE}^{\dagger}\big].

(93)

We then observe that the quantity
Q_{k}\sigma_{E,k}Q_{k}
is a positive operator with unit trace for all
k
since it follows from Eq. (
84
) that

\displaystyle\tr_{E}\big[Q_{k}\sigma_{E,k}Q_{k}\big]

\displaystyle=\tr_{SE}\big[(\mathbbm{1}\otimes Q_{k})U_{SE}(\psi_{k}\otimes\rho_{E})U_{SE}^{\dagger}(\mathbbm{1}\otimes Q_{k})\big]

\displaystyle=\tr_{S}\big[P_{k}\psi_{k}P_{k}\big]=\tr\psi_{k}=1.

(94)

Thus, we have

\displaystyle 1=\tr[\sigma_{E,k}]

\displaystyle=\tr[(Q_{k}+(\mathbbm{1}-Q_{k}))\sigma_{E,k}(Q_{k}+(\mathbbm{1}-Q_{k}))]

\displaystyle=\tr[Q_{k}\sigma_{E,k}Q_{k}]+\tr[(\mathbbm{1}-Q_{k})\sigma_{E,k}(\mathbbm{1}-Q_{k})]

\displaystyle=1+\tr[(\mathbbm{1}-Q_{k})\sigma_{E,k}(\mathbbm{1}-Q_{k})],

(95)

which implies that
(\mathbbm{1}-Q_{k})\sigma_{E,k}=\sigma_{E,k}(\mathbbm{1}-Q_{k})=0
. Thus, we obtain
\sigma_{E,k}=Q_{k}\sigma_{E,k}Q_{k}
for all
k
.

Appendix D
Work cost of quantum measurements and the Second Law of Thermodynamics

In this section we argue why our results of energy costs developed in this paper are, in fact, also statements about thermodynamic work.
More concretely, we argue that, by identifying the projective measurements with the dephasing operations and using the result in Appendix
B
, all energy costs of an implementation of a quantum measurement can be considered to stem from unitary dynamics
U
. Hence, thermodynamic work cost is given as the average energy change
[
A5
,
30
,
31
]

W=\tr[H(U\rho U^{\dagger}-\rho)]\

(96)

of a system with Hamiltonian
H
initially in the state
\rho
, which is equal to the energy cost
E_{\mathrm{cost}}
in our results.

As a consequence of this identification of our energy cost and work, the energy extraction example in Subsec.
4.2
illustrates that

extracting useful thermodynamic work from the measurement device is possible
. This may seem intriguing in the context of the Second Law of Thermodynamics: discussions on the net work gain in a whole cycle of a Szilard engine typically assume that no work can be extracted in the measurement itself and argue that all work gained in the extraction phase of the cycle is completely cancelled by the cost imposed by Landauer’s principle
[
15
]
for resetting the memory that stores the measurement outcome
[
A6
]
.

The work extraction from the measurement process however does not contradict the Second Law of Thermodynamics for the following reason. When considering the overall work gain of a Szilard engine that employs a measurement device as described in our work extraction example in Subsec.
4.2
, one needs to incorporate the cost of completing the thermodynamic cycle by restoring the initial pure state on
S
. This restoring step consumes all work gained during the measurement. Indeed, one finds from Eq. (
26
) that

\displaystyle W_{\mathrm{cost}}

\displaystyle\geq\Delta E_{S}+k_{B}T\Delta S

(97)

\displaystyle=\Delta F_{S}+k_{B}T(S(\rho^{\prime}_{S})-S(\rho_{S}))+k_{B}T\Delta S

(98)

\displaystyle=\Delta F_{S}+k_{B}T\Big(S(\rho^{\prime}_{S})-\sum_{k}p_{k}S(\rho^{\prime}_{S,k})\Big)

(99)

\displaystyle\geq\Delta F_{S},

(100)

where
\Delta F_{S}=F_{S}(\rho^{\prime}_{S})-F_{S}(\rho_{S})
is the free energy change in the system during the measurement, which corresponds to the work cost of the aforementioned restoring step of the measured system
S
.
This shows that the overall work expense in a full thermodynamic cycle that includes measurements is always non-negative and shows the validity of the Second Law in our general setting, as expected.
