Abstract
. We study the function series ∑ n = 1 ∞ ϕ 2 ​ m + 2 ​ cosch 2 ​ m + 2 ​ ( n ​ ϕ / 2 ) \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\phi^{2m+2}\text{cosch}^{2m+2}(n\phi/2) , and similar series, for integers m m and complex ϕ \phi . This hyperbolic series is linearly related to the Lambert series. The Lambert series is known to satisfy a functional equation which defines the Ramanujan polynomials. By using residue theorem (summation theorem) we find the functional equation satisfied by this hyperbolic series. The functional equation identifies a class of polynomials which can be seen as a generalization of the Ramanujan polynomials. These polynomials coincide with the asymptotic expansion of the hyperbolic series at the origin and they all vanish for ϕ = ± 2 ​ π ​ i \phi=\pm 2\pi i . We furthermore derive several identities between Harmonic numbers and ordinary and generalized Bernoulli polynomials.

1.
Introduction and main results

Given an integer number
m\geq 0
and a complex number
\phi
, we study the function series

S_{2m+2}(\phi)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\phi^{2m+2}}{\sinh^{2m+2}(n\phi/2)},

henceforth simply denoted as hyperbolic series.
The hyperbolic series defines a function in
\phi
that is analytic in any domain
not intersecting the imaginary axis, where the series diverges everywhere except that in the origin.
By using the summation theorem to write the sums on the series as a complex integral,
we find a functional relation satisfied by this series.
The functional relation separates the function into a polynomial part and into a part
that is not analytical in
\Re(\phi)=0
. The polynomial identified in this way coincides
with the asymptotic expansion of the hyperbolic series in
\phi=0
.

The functional relations are easily understood once we establish the connections between
the hyperbolic series and the Lambert series.

Definition 1.1

(Lambert series)
.

We denote with
\mathcal{L}_{q}(s)
the Lambert series of the type

(1.1)

\mathcal{L}_{q}(s)=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{k^{s}q^{k}}{1-q^{k}}\qquad s\in\mathbb{R},\,q\in\mathbb{C}

with
|q|<1
.

Proposition 1.2
.

For
m\geq 0
integer and
\Re(\phi)\neq 0
, the hyperbolic series is the linear combination
of the Lambert series
\mathcal{L}_{q}(s)
,

(1.2)

S_{2m+2}(\phi)=\frac{(2\phi)^{2m+2}}{(2m+1)!}\sum_{i=0}^{m}c_{2i+1}^{(m)}\,\mathcal{L}_{e^{-\sigma\phi}}(2i+1),

where
\sigma=
sgn
(\Re(\phi))
and
c_{i}^{(m)}
are real numbers given by:

c_{2i+1}^{(m)}=\frac{(2m+1)!}{(2i+1)!}\frac{B_{2m-2i}^{(2m+2)}(m+1)}{(2m-2i)!}

with
B_{n}^{(m)}(t)
the generalized Bernoulli polynomial.

Definition 1.3

(Generalized Bernoulli polynomials)
.

For integers
n
and
m
and real
t
, we denote with
B_{n}
the Bernoulli number, with
B_{n}(t)
the Bernoulli polynomial and with

B_{n}^{(m)}(t)
the generalized Bernoulli polynomial
[
14
, p. 145]
(see also

[
11
]
), defined respectively by the following exponential generating functions:

\displaystyle\frac{x}{e^{x}-1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{B_{n}}{n!}\,x^{n}\quad|x|<2\pi\,;\qquad\frac{x\,e^{t\,x}}{e^{x}-1}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{B_{n}(t)x^{n}}{n!}\quad|x|<2\pi\,;

\displaystyle\frac{x^{m}}{(e^{x}-1)^{m}}e^{t\,x}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{B_{n}^{(m)}(t)\,x^{n}}{n!}\quad|x|<2\pi\,.

Some functional relations for the Lambert series were given by Ramanujan
[
16
]

and later proved by several authors, see
[
5
]
and reference therein.
When
s
is a negative odd integer
s=-2m-1
with
m=0,1,2,\dots
and for any
\Re(\phi)>0
, the Lambert series
satisfies the functional relation
[
5
, Entry 21(i) Chapter 14]

(1.3)

\begin{split}\mathcal{L}_{e^{-\phi}}(-2m-1)=&(-1)^{m}\left(\frac{\phi}{2\pi}\right)^{2m}\mathcal{L}_{e^{-4\pi^{2}/\phi}}(-2m-1)+\\
&-\frac{1}{2}\left[1-(-1)^{m}\frac{\phi^{2m}}{(2\pi)^{m}}\right]\zeta(2m+1)+\frac{1}{2^{2m+2}\phi}\mathcal{R}_{2m+2}(\phi),\end{split}

where
\zeta
is the Riemann zeta and
\mathcal{R}
is the Ramanujan polynomial
[
9
]
defined as

(1.4)

\mathcal{R}_{2m+2}(\phi)=-2^{2m+1}\sum_{k=0}^{m+1}(2\pi i)^{2k}\frac{B_{2k}}{(2k)!}\frac{B_{2m+2-2k}}{(2m+2-2k)!}\phi^{2m+2-2k},

with
B_{j}
the
j
-th Bernoulli number.
Taking advantage of the identity

\coth x=1+\frac{2}{e^{2x}-1}

we can also write the Ramanujan identity (
1.3
) as an identity for hyperbolic cotangent series

\begin{split}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\coth(n\phi/2)}{n^{2m+1}}=&(-1)^{m}\left(\frac{\phi}{2\pi}\right)^{2m}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\coth(4\pi^{2}n/2\phi)}{n^{2m+1}}+\\
&+\frac{1}{2^{2m+1}\phi}\mathcal{R}_{2m+2}(\phi).\end{split}

In contrast to this series, the hyperbolic series
S_{2m+2}
is related to the Lambert
series with positive argument and, for any integer
m\geq 0
, the functional relation for
the Lambert series with
s=2m+1
becomes
[
5
, Entry 13 Chapter 14]

(1.5)

\begin{split}\mathcal{L}_{e^{-\phi}}(2m+1)=&-(-1)^{m}\left(\frac{2\pi}{\phi}\right)^{2m+2}\mathcal{L}_{e^{-4\pi^{2}/\phi}}(2m+1)-\frac{\delta_{m,0}}{2\phi}+\\
&+\frac{1}{2}\frac{B_{2m+2}}{2m+2}\left[1+\frac{(-1)^{m}(2\pi)^{2m+2}}{\phi^{2m+2}}\right].\end{split}

We can then plug relations (
1.5
) into (
1.2
) to find functional
equations relating the hyperbolic series.

Theorem 1.4
.

The series
S_{2m+2}(\phi)
for any integer
m\geq 0
and any complex
\phi
such that
\Re(\phi)\neq 0

satisfy the functional relation

(1.6)

S_{2m+2}(\phi)-\sum_{i=0}^{m}\mathcal{S}_{i}^{(m)}(\phi)S_{2i+2}\left(\frac{4\pi^{2}}{\phi}\right)=\mathcal{B}_{2m+2}(\phi)+(-1)^{m+1}\frac{4\sigma}{m+2}\phi^{2m+1},

where
\sigma=
sgn
(\Re(\phi))
,
\mathcal{S}_{i}^{(m)}
are polynomials on
\phi
of degree
2m+2
and

\mathcal{B}_{2m+2}(\phi)
is the polynomial

(1.7)

\mathcal{B}_{2m+2}(\phi)=-2^{2m+1}\sum_{k=0}^{m+1}(2\pi i)^{2k}\frac{B_{2k}}{(2k)!}\frac{B_{2m+2-2k}^{(2m+2)}(m+1)}{(2m+2-2k)!}\,\phi^{2m+2-2k}.

We do not have a general expression for the polynomials
\mathcal{S}_{i}^{(m)}
but we
give the procedure to find them.
The main difference with Lambert functional equations is that the hyperbolic
function
S_{2m+2}(\phi)
is related not just to
S_{2m+2}(4\pi^{2}/\phi)
but to all functions

S_{2i+2}(4\pi^{2}/\phi)
with
i=0,1,\dots,m
.

Similarly to functional equation (
1.3
), the (
1.6
)
defines a class of polynomials which can be seen as a generalization of Ramanujan polynomials.
Indeed the form of the polynomials (
1.7
) suggests to define the generalization of
Ramanujan polynomial as

\mathcal{R}^{(s,r)}_{2m+2}(\phi)=-2^{2m+1}\sum_{k=0}^{m+1}(2\pi i)^{2k}\frac{B_{2k}}{(2k)!}\frac{B_{2m+2-2k}^{(2s+r)}(s)}{(2m+2-2k)!}\,\phi^{2m+2-2k}.

It follows immediately from the generalized Bernoulli properties that the generalized Ramanujan polynomials
reduce to ordinary Ramanujan polynomials and to
\mathcal{B}
polynomials for the following choice of
parameters:

\mathcal{R}^{(0,1)}_{2m+2}(\phi)=\mathcal{R}_{2m+2}(\phi),\quad\mathcal{R}^{(m+1,0)}_{2m+2}(\phi)=\mathcal{B}_{2m+2}(\phi).

Notice that each term of the series
S_{2i+2}(4\pi^{2}/\phi)
and the last
term in (
1.6
) are non analytical in
\phi=0
. Then, the functional
relation (
1.6
) identifies the polynomial
\mathcal{B}_{2m+2}
as
the only analytical part of the hyperbolic series and it can be used to assign
a value to the series for imaginary
\phi
. The study of this series and its
analytical part is also motivated by a recent application in Physics.
Exact solutions for thermal states of a quantum relativistic fluid in the presence
of both acceleration and rotation are found by extracting the analytic part
of a function series that do not converge in the whole complex plane
[
4
]
.
In particular, it was shown in ref.
[
4
]
that every thermal distribution
quantity of an accelerating fluid composed by non-interacting massless scalar particles
can be given as a linear combination of the polynomials (
1.7
) derived form the
hyperbolic series discussed here.

The paper is organized as follows.
In Section
2
we consider three types of hyperbolic series, we study
their uniform convergence, and we show that they are not analytic in the imaginary axis.
In Proposition
2.6
we establish a linear relation between the hyperbolic
series and the Lambert series. In Sec.
3

we show that the linearity coefficients of proposition
2.6
are given by special values
of the generalized Bernoulli polynomials. This is done by relating the ratio of two gamma functions
to the generalized Bernoulli polynomials (Theorem
3.1
).
In Sec.
4
we take advantage of Theorem
3.1

to derive several identities involving ordinary and generalized Bernoulli
polynomials as well as Bell polynomials and Harmonic numbers. In Sec.
5
we first prove
the functional relation satisfied by Lambert series and then we prove Theorem
1.4
.
We also show that the polynomial
\mathcal{B}
corresponds
to the asymptotic expansion of
S_{2m+2}
at the origin and we analyze the zeros of the polynomials.

2.
Function series with hyperbolic cosecant

The results quoted in Section
1
can be extended to the following class of hyperbolic series.

Definition 2.1

(Hyperbolic series)
.

Given
m
and
\gamma
two integers such that
m\geq 0
and
\gamma<m+1
and given
\phi
a complex number
such that
\Re(\phi)\neq 0
, we refer to hyperbolic series as the following function series involving the
hyperbolic cosecant:

(2.1)

\displaystyle S^{(\gamma)}_{2m+2}(\phi)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\phi^{2m+2}\cosh^{\gamma}(n\,\phi)}{\sinh^{2m+2}(n\phi/2)},

(2.2)

\displaystyle S^{(\sinh,\gamma)}_{2m+2}(\phi)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\phi^{2m+2}\sinh^{\gamma}(n\,\phi)}{\sinh^{2m+2}(n\phi/2)},

(2.3)

\displaystyle S_{2m+2}(\phi)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\phi^{2m+2}}{\sinh^{2m+2}(n\phi/2)},

where
S_{2m+2}(\phi)
is the series discussed in Section
1
and it corresponds to
S^{(\gamma)}_{2m+2}(\phi)

in Eq. (
2.1
) with
\gamma=0
.

In this section we show that these series are analytic functions in
\phi
in the whole complex plane
except the imaginary axis, that they are continuous function in the real axis but that they are not real
analytic in any open set containing the zero. These properties are inherited directly from Lambert series
thanks to linearity relation (
1.2
).
Furthermore, we do not need to study all of the three type of hyperbolic function defined above.
Indeed, the series (
2.1
) and (
2.2
) are linear combination of
S_{2m+2}(\phi)
and

S^{(\sinh,1)}_{2m+2}(\phi)
.

Proposition 2.2
.

The series (
2.1
) and (
2.2
) are linear combination
of
S_{2m+2}(\phi)
and
S^{(\sinh,1)}_{2m+2}(\phi)
. Set
\gamma=2p
if
\gamma
is even,
otherwise
\gamma=2p+1
, then

\displaystyle S^{(\gamma)}_{2m+2}(\phi)

\displaystyle=\sum_{l=0}^{\gamma}\genfrac{(}{)}{0.0pt}{0}{\gamma}{l}2^{l}\,S_{2(m-l)+2}(\phi);

\displaystyle S^{(\sinh,2p)}_{2m+2}(\phi)

\displaystyle=4^{p}\sum_{l=0}^{p}\genfrac{(}{)}{0.0pt}{0}{p}{l}\,S_{2(m+l-2p)+2}(\phi);

\displaystyle S^{(\sinh,2p+1)}_{2m+2}(\phi)

\displaystyle=4^{p}\sum_{l=0}^{p}\genfrac{(}{)}{0.0pt}{0}{p}{l}\,S^{(\sinh,1)}_{2(m+l-2p)+2}(\phi).

Proof.

This is easily proved by taking advantage of hyperbolic function relations.
Indeed, using the relation
\cosh(n\phi)=2\sinh^{2}(n\phi/2)+1
and the binomial expansion we obtain

\begin{split}S^{(\gamma)}_{2m+2}(\phi)&=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\phi^{2m+2}\left(2\sinh^{2}(n\phi/2)+1\right)^{\gamma}}{\sinh^{2m+2}(n\phi/2)}=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\sum_{l=0}^{\gamma}\genfrac{(}{)}{0.0pt}{0}{\gamma}{l}\frac{\phi^{2m+2}2^{l}\sinh^{2l}(n\phi/2)}{\sinh^{2m+2}(n\phi/2)}\\
&=\sum_{l=0}^{\gamma}\genfrac{(}{)}{0.0pt}{0}{\gamma}{l}2^{l}\,S_{2(m-l)+2}(\phi).\end{split}

In the same way, taking advantage of

\sinh^{2}(n\phi)=\cosh^{2}(n\phi)-1=4\sinh^{2}(n\phi/2)+4\sinh^{4}(n\phi/2),

the series
S^{(\sinh,\gamma)}_{2m+2}(\phi)
for an even
\gamma=2p
can be written as

\begin{split}S^{(\sinh,2p)}_{2m+2}(\phi)&=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\phi^{2m+2}4^{p}\left(\sinh^{2}(n\phi/2)+\sinh^{4}(n\phi/2)\right)^{p}}{\sinh^{2m+2}(n\phi/2)}\\
&=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\sum_{l=0}^{p}\genfrac{(}{)}{0.0pt}{0}{p}{l}\frac{\phi^{2m+2}4^{p}\sinh^{4p-2l}(n\phi/2)}{\sinh^{2m+2}(n\phi/2)}\\
&=4^{p}\sum_{l=0}^{p}\genfrac{(}{)}{0.0pt}{0}{p}{l}\,S_{2(m+l-2p)+2}(\phi).\end{split}

The case with odd
\gamma=2p+1
is the same as the previous equation multiplied by

\sinh(n\phi)
, thus leading to the result written above.
∎

Hence, from now on we only consider
S_{2m+2}(\phi)
and
S^{(\sinh,1)}_{2m+2}(\phi)
.
All their properties are transported to the others hyperbolic series thanks to
these linear relations. Moving on now to find the relation with Lambert series,
we have first to write the hyperbolic series just in terms of exponential functions.

Lemma 2.3
.

For any integer
m\geq 0
and complex number
\phi
such that
\Re(\phi)\neq 0
, the hyperbolic series are represented by

(2.4)

\displaystyle S_{2m+2}(\phi)

\displaystyle=(2\phi)^{2m+2}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\genfrac{(}{)}{0.0pt}{0}{k+m}{k-m-1}\frac{e^{-\sigma k\phi}}{1-e^{-\sigma k\phi}},

(2.5)

\displaystyle S^{(\sinh,1)}_{2m+2}(\phi)

\displaystyle=(2\sigma\phi)^{2m+1}\phi\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\left[\genfrac{(}{)}{0.0pt}{0}{k+m-1}{k-m-1}+\genfrac{(}{)}{0.0pt}{0}{k+m}{k-m}\right]\frac{e^{-\sigma k\phi}}{1-e^{-\sigma k\phi}};

where
\sigma
is the sign of the real part of
\phi
.

Proof.

We start by expressing a generic term of
S_{2m+2}(\phi)
with exponential functions:

\frac{\phi^{2m+2}}{\sinh^{2m+2}(n\phi/2)}=\frac{2^{2m+2}\phi^{2m+2}}{\left(e^{n\phi/2}-e^{-n\phi/2}\right)^{2m+2}}=\frac{2^{2m+2}\phi^{2m+2}e^{-n\phi(m+1)}}{\left(1-e^{-n\phi}\right)^{2m+2}}.

When
\Re(\phi)>0
, we can use the well-known binomial property

\frac{1}{(1-z)^{\beta+1}}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\genfrac{(}{)}{0.0pt}{0}{k+\beta}{k}z^{k}\qquad|z|<1

and the generic term of the hyperbolic series is given by the series

\frac{\phi^{2m+2}}{\sinh^{2m+2}(n\phi/2)}=2^{2m+2}\phi^{2m+2}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\genfrac{(}{)}{0.0pt}{0}{k+2m+1}{k}e^{-n(k+m+1)\phi}.

We plug this inside the hyperbolic series and we invert the order of summation,
then the last sum is the geometrical series which is converging for
\Re(\phi)>0
. We find:

\begin{split}S_{2m+2}(\phi)&=2^{2m+2}\phi^{2m+2}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\genfrac{(}{)}{0.0pt}{0}{k+2m+1}{k}e^{-n(k+m+1)\phi}\\
&=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\genfrac{(}{)}{0.0pt}{0}{k+2m+1}{k}\frac{2^{2m+2}\phi^{2m+2}}{e^{(k+m+1)\phi}-1}.\end{split}

Then, we change the summation index name from
k
to
k^{\prime}=k+m+1
, so that

\begin{split}S_{2m+2}(\phi)&=\!\!\sum_{k=m+1}^{\infty}\!\!\genfrac{(}{)}{0.0pt}{0}{k+m}{k-m-1}\frac{2^{2m+2}\phi^{2m+2}}{e^{k\phi}-1}=\!\!\sum_{k=m+1}^{\infty}\!\!\genfrac{(}{)}{0.0pt}{0}{k+m}{k-m-1}\frac{(2\phi)^{2m+2}e^{-k\phi}}{1-e^{-k\phi}}.\end{split}

At last, noticing that the binomial coefficient is vanishing in
k=0,1,\dots,m
we recover Eq. (
2.4
).
The same procedure for
S^{(\sinh,1)}_{2m+2}(\phi)
leads to

S^{(\sinh,1)}_{2m+2}(\phi)=\frac{(2\phi)^{2m+2}}{2}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\left[\genfrac{(}{)}{0.0pt}{0}{k+m+1}{k-m}-\genfrac{(}{)}{0.0pt}{0}{k+m-1}{k-m-2}\right]\frac{e^{-k\phi}}{1-e^{-k\phi}}.

Replacing the first binomial factor with

\genfrac{(}{)}{0.0pt}{1}{k+m+1}{k-m}=\genfrac{(}{)}{0.0pt}{1}{k+m}{k-m-1}+\genfrac{(}{)}{0.0pt}{1}{k+m}{k-m}=\genfrac{(}{)}{0.0pt}{1}{k+m-1}{k-m-1}+\genfrac{(}{)}{0.0pt}{1}{k+m-1}{k-m-2}+\genfrac{(}{)}{0.0pt}{1}{k+m}{k-m}

reproduces Eq. (
2.5
) for
\Re(\phi)>0
.
Noticing that
S_{2m+2}(\phi)
is an even function in the exchange
\phi\to-\phi
,
while
S^{(\sinh,1)}_{2m+2}(\phi)
is odd, we obtain Eq. (
2.4
) and Eq. (
2.5
)
for
\Re(\phi)<0
.
∎

Lemma 2.4
.

The binomial
\genfrac{(}{)}{0.0pt}{1}{k+m}{k-m-1}
for integer
m\geq 0
is a odd polynomial in
k

of degree
2m+1
. And the binomial combination

\genfrac{(}{)}{0.0pt}{1}{k+m-1}{k-m-1}+\genfrac{(}{)}{0.0pt}{1}{k+m}{k-m}
for integer
m\geq 0

is an even polynomial in
k
of degree
2m
.

Proof.

The fact that they are polynomial of finite degree follows from the definition of binomial coefficient,
consider for instance the first binomial coefficient:

\begin{split}\genfrac{(}{)}{0.0pt}{0}{k+m}{k-m-1}&=\frac{(k+m)!}{(k-m-1)!(2m+1)!}\\
&=\frac{1}{(2m+1)!}\underbrace{(k+m)(k+m-1)\cdots(k+1)k(k-1)\cdots(k-m)}_{2m+1\text{ factors}}.\end{split}

Now, to show that is odd, we first send
k
to
-k
in the previous equation and we obtain

\genfrac{(}{)}{0.0pt}{0}{m-k}{-k-m-1}=\frac{1}{(2m+1)!}(m-k)(m-1-k)\cdots(1-k)(-k)(-k-1)\cdots(-k-m);

then, we gather a
-1
sign in every round bracket, obtaining an overall
(-1)^{2m+1}=-1
sign:

\genfrac{(}{)}{0.0pt}{0}{m-k}{-k-m-1}=\frac{-1}{(2m+1)!}(k-m)(k-m+1)\cdots(k-1)k(k+1)\cdots(k+m-1)(k+m);

therefore, by comparison we see that

\genfrac{(}{)}{0.0pt}{0}{k+m}{k-m-1}=-\genfrac{(}{)}{0.0pt}{0}{m-k}{-k-m-1}

meaning that
\genfrac{(}{)}{0.0pt}{1}{k+m}{k-m-1}
is odd with respect to
k
.
The others binomial coefficients reads

\begin{split}\genfrac{(}{)}{0.0pt}{0}{k+m-1}{k-m-1}+\genfrac{(}{)}{0.0pt}{0}{k+m}{k-m}&=\frac{2k}{(2m)!}\Big[\underbrace{(k+m-1)\cdots(k-m+1)}_{2m-1\text{ factors}}\Big].\end{split}

Using the same method as before we see that the polynomials inside the square bracket is odd, therefore
the combination
\genfrac{(}{)}{0.0pt}{1}{k+m-1}{k-m-1}+\genfrac{(}{)}{0.0pt}{1}{k+m}{k-m}
is an even polynomial.
∎

Proposition 2.7
.

Let
s
be any complex number, the Lambert series
\mathcal{L}_{q}(s)
as a function of
q
is
an analytic function for any
|q|<1
.

Proof.

First, we prove that the functions series
\mathcal{L}_{q}(s)
is a convergent series.
The ratio test on Lambert series

\lim_{k\to\infty}\left|\frac{(k+1)^{s}q^{k+1}}{1-q^{k+1}}\frac{1-q^{k}}{k^{s}q^{k}}\right|=|q|

shows that
\mathcal{L}_{q}(s)
is pointwise convergent for
|q|<1
and
\forall\,s\in\mathbb{C}
.
But the Lambert series can also be written as a power series
[
1
, §24.3.3]
:

\mathcal{L}_{q}(s)=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{k^{s}q^{k}}{1-q^{k}}=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\sigma_{s}(n)q^{n}\qquad|q|<1.

Therefore, the Lambert series is a convergent power series in
|q|<1
, which proves that it is analytic.
∎

Proposition 2.8
.

Choose
q=e^{-i\theta}
with
\theta
a real positive number, the Lambert series of definition
1.1

is a divergent series.

Proof.

In this case the series becomes

\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{k^{s}}{e^{ik\theta}-1}.

The root test for convergence gives

\alpha=\limsup_{k\to\infty}\left(\frac{|k^{s}|}{|e^{ik\theta}-1|}\right)^{1/k}=\limsup_{k\to\infty}\frac{k^{s/k}}{\left[2-2\cos(k\theta)\right]^{1/2k}}.

Since for any
\bar{k}>0
and small
\epsilon>0
, it exist an integer
n>\bar{k}
such that

\cos(n\theta)>1-\epsilon
, then the sup diverges; that is

\alpha=\lim_{k\to\infty}\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\frac{k^{s/k}}{\epsilon^{1/2k}}=\infty.

Therefore the series diverges.
∎

As a consequence, also the hyperbolic series are divergent series for
\phi
in the imaginary
axis (except in
\phi=0
). This also means that for any
\phi
not in the imaginary axis,
the radius of convergence of the hyperbolic series could not be greater than the distance of
\phi

from the imaginary axis. This fact prevent the hyperbolic series to be real analytic in a open
set containing
\phi=0
.

Proposition 2.9
.

The hyperbolic series (
2.1
), (
2.2
) and (
2.3
) are analytic
function for
\phi
in the complex plane except the imaginary axis. The hyperbolic series are continuous function
in the real axis.

Proof.

Since the hyperbolic series for
\Re(\phi)>0
(
\Re(\phi)<0
) are linear combination
of Lambert series which are analytic function in that domain, they are analytic function in that
region too.

To prove that in the real axis they are continuous functions, we show that they converge
uniformly in a open set containing the origin.
When we set
\phi
to zero we obtain the well known series related to the Riemann zeta function
\zeta
:

\begin{split}S^{(\gamma)}_{2m+2}(0)&=S_{2m+2}(0)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{2^{2m+2}}{n^{2m+2}}=2^{2m+2}\zeta\left(2m+2\right)\\
&=(-1)^{m}\frac{2^{2m+1}(2\pi)^{2m+2}B_{2m+2}}{(2m+2)!};\\
S^{(\sinh,\gamma)}_{2m+2}(0)&=0;\end{split}

where
B_{n}
are the Bernoulli numbers, Definition
1.3
.
The series are clearly pointwise convergent in
\phi=0
.

When
\phi\neq 0
to prove absolute convergence it suffices to use the ratio test; for
S_{2m+2}(\phi)
we have

\lim_{n\to\infty}\left|\frac{\left(e^{n\phi/2}-e^{-n\phi/2}\right)^{2m+2}}{\left(e^{(n+1)\phi/2}-e^{-(n+1)\phi/2}\right)^{2m+2}}\right|=e^{-|\phi|(m+1)}<1\quad\text{for }m>-1;

instead, for
S^{(\sinh,1)}_{2m+2}(\phi)
we have

\lim_{n\to\infty}\left|\frac{\left(e^{n\phi/2}-e^{-n\phi/2}\right)^{2m+2}}{\left(e^{(n+1)\phi/2}-e^{-(n+1)\phi/2}\right)^{2m+2}}\frac{e^{(n+1)\phi}-e^{-(n+1)\phi}}{e^{n\phi}-e^{-n\phi}}\right|=e^{-|\phi|m}

that is smaller than 1, hence absolutely convergent, for
m>1
.

For uniform convergence of
S^{(\sinh,1)}_{2m+2}(\phi)
, we notice that for all
n
and
\phi\in\mathbb{R}

the sequence functions in the series are monotonically increasing.
Therefore, by Dini’s lemma, see
[
17
, Theorem 7.13 on p. 150]

and
[
10
, Theorem 12.1 on p. 157]
, the hyperbolic function
S^{(\sinh,1)}_{2m+2}(\phi)
is uniformly
convergent in every compact set contained in
\mathbb{R}
. In particular, we showed that is uniformly convergent
in a set not containing the origin
\phi=0
.

Consider now a closed interval
I_{0}=[-a,a]
with
a>0
a real number, we use Weierstrass M-test to
prove uniform convergence of
S_{2m+2}(\phi)
on
I_{0}
. Let be
f_{n}
the sequence functions in the series
S_{2m+2}(\phi)
:

f_{n}(\phi)=\frac{\phi^{2m+2}}{\sinh^{2m+2}(n\phi/2)}.

Notice that
f_{n}
have a maximum in
\phi=0
. Then we can built the sequence
M_{n}
by

|f_{n}(\phi)|\leq|f_{n}(0)|=\frac{2^{2m+2}}{n^{2m+2}}\equiv M_{n},\quad\forall\phi\in I_{0}.

Since the sequence
M_{n}
converges, then by Weierstrass M-test the series converges absolutely and uniformly on
I_{0}
.

Because the hyperbolic functions are defined by an uniformly convergent series and every functions in the succession is

C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
, then the hyperbolic functions are continuous in
I_{0}
and because we know that they are
analytic in every other part of the real axis then they are continuous in all
\mathbb{R}
.
∎

3.
Bernoulli polynomials representation for a ratio of Gamma functions

In this section we give the values of the coefficients
c_{2i+1}^{(m)}
and
d_{2i}^{(m)}
of
Def.
2.5
which are needed to take advantage of the linearity relations between
hyperbolic series and the Lambert series. By using a power series representation of the ratio of
two gamma functions, we find that these coefficients can be given in terms of the generalized Bernoulli
polynomials. First of all, we can invert the definition of the coefficients and compute them
through the following derivatives:

(3.1)

\begin{split}c_{i}^{(m)}&=\frac{(2m+1)!}{i!}\frac{\partial^{i}}{\partial k^{i}}\genfrac{(}{)}{0.0pt}{0}{k+m}{k-m-1}\Big|_{k=0},\\
d_{i}^{(m)}&=\frac{(2m)!}{i!}\frac{\partial^{i}}{\partial k^{i}}\left[\genfrac{(}{)}{0.0pt}{0}{k+m-1}{k-m-1}+\genfrac{(}{)}{0.0pt}{0}{k+m}{k-m}\right]\Big|_{k=0}.\end{split}

The binomial coefficient can also be written as a ratio of Euler gamma functions:

\begin{split}c_{i}^{(m)}&=\frac{1}{i!}\frac{\partial^{i}}{\partial k^{i}}\frac{\Gamma(k+m+1)}{\Gamma(k-m)}\Big|_{k=0},\\
d_{i}^{(m)}&=\frac{1}{i!}\frac{\partial^{i}}{\partial k^{i}}\left[\frac{\Gamma(k+m)}{\Gamma(k-m)}+\frac{\Gamma(k+m+1)}{\Gamma(k-m+1)}\right]\Big|_{k=0}.\end{split}

Therefore, if we express the ratio of gamma functions in the previous equation as a power series of
k

we will readily obtain the coefficients.
Tricomi and Erdélyi
[
18
]
gave the asymptotic expansion for the ratio
of two gamma functions

\frac{\Gamma(z+\alpha)}{\Gamma(z+\beta)}\sim\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{\Gamma(1+\alpha-\beta)}{\Gamma(\alpha-\beta-n+1)}\frac{B_{n}^{(\alpha-\beta+1)}(\alpha)}{n!}z^{\alpha-\beta-n}\quad\text{as }z\to\infty,

valid for
\alpha,\beta
such that
\alpha-\beta\neq-1,-2,\dots
.
The asymptotic expansion becomes an exact relation when
\alpha-\beta\geq 0
is an
integer
[
11
, Sec 2.11, Eq. (12)]
. That is the case we are interested in.
We can then reformulate the representation of the ratio of two gamma functions of
[
18
]

in a form suitable for our scope.

Theorem 3.1

(Luke 1969
[
11
]
)
.

Given
z\in\mathbb{C}
and two integer
\alpha,\beta\geq 0
, then

(3.2)

\frac{\Gamma(z+\alpha)}{\Gamma(z-\beta)}=\sum_{i=0}^{\alpha+\beta}\frac{(\alpha+\beta)!}{i!}\frac{B_{\alpha+\beta-i}^{(1+\alpha+\beta)}(\alpha)}{(\alpha+\beta-i)!}z^{i}

where
\Gamma
is the Euler Gamma.

Proof.

We follow the proof of
[
18
]
which was based on Watson’s lemma.
In our case
\beta
is negative and we are not interested in the value for
z\to\infty

and
\alpha
and
\beta
are integers.
Furthermore, our case is much more simple because we know that the ratio

\Gamma(z+\alpha)/\Gamma(z-\beta)
is analytic and can be written as a power series:

(3.3)

\frac{\Gamma(z+\alpha)}{\Gamma(z-\beta)}=\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\gamma_{i}z^{i}.

Following
[
18
]
for every
\alpha
and
\beta
such that
\alpha+\beta\neq-1,-2,\dots

and for every
z
in the complex plane which does not lay in the real segment from
-\alpha
to
-\alpha-\infty
,
the ratio between gamma functions has the integral representation

\begin{split}\frac{\Gamma(z+\alpha)}{\Gamma(z-\beta)}=&\frac{\Gamma(1+\alpha+\beta)}{2\pi\,i}\int_{-\infty\cdot e^{i\delta}}^{(0^{+})}\frac{e^{z\,t}\,e^{\alpha\,t}}{(e^{t}-1)^{1+\alpha+\beta}}{\rm d}t\\
=&\frac{\Gamma(1+\alpha+\beta)}{2\pi\,i}\int_{-\infty\cdot e^{i\delta}}^{(0^{+})}\frac{e^{z\,t}}{t^{1+\alpha+\beta}}\left(\frac{1}{e^{t}-1}\right)^{1+\alpha+\beta}e^{\alpha\,t}\,{\rm d}t,\end{split}

where
-\pi/2<\delta<\pi/2
.
The coefficient
\gamma_{i}
of Eq. (
3.3
) can be found through

\gamma_{i}=\lim_{z\to 0}\frac{\gamma_{i}(z)}{i!}

where
\gamma_{i}(z)
is the derivative

\begin{split}\gamma_{i}(z)=&\frac{{\rm d}^{i}}{{\rm d}z^{i}}\frac{\Gamma(z+\alpha)}{\Gamma(z-\beta)}\\
=&\frac{\Gamma(1+\alpha+\beta)}{2\pi\,i}\int_{-\infty\cdot e^{i\delta}}^{(0^{+})}\frac{e^{z\,t}}{t^{1+\alpha+\beta-i}}\left(\frac{1}{e^{t}-1}\right)^{1+\alpha+\beta}e^{\alpha\,t}\,{\rm d}t.\end{split}

In the previous equation we recognize the generating function of the generalized
Bernoulli polynomials (Def.
1.3
), from which the derivative becomes

\gamma_{i}(z)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{\Gamma(1+\alpha+\beta)}{n!}B_{n}^{(1+\alpha+\beta)}(\alpha)\frac{1}{2\pi\,i}\int_{-\infty\cdot e^{i\delta}}^{(0^{+})}\frac{e^{z\,t}}{t^{1+\alpha+\beta-i-n}}{\rm d}t.

Then, using the integral
[
18
]

\frac{1}{2\pi\,i}\int_{-\infty\cdot e^{i\delta}}^{(0^{+})}\frac{e^{z\,t}}{t^{a+1}}{\rm d}t=\frac{z^{a}}{\Gamma(a+1)}

we obtain

\gamma_{i}(z)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{\Gamma(1+\alpha+\beta)}{\Gamma(1+\alpha+\beta-i-n)}\frac{B_{n}^{(1+\alpha+\beta)}(\alpha)}{n!}z^{\alpha+\beta-i-n}.

In the limit
z\to 0
we are just left with the term in
n=1+\alpha+\beta-i
,
therefore the coefficient of the series (
3.3
) is

\gamma_{i}=\frac{(\alpha+\beta)!}{i!}\frac{B_{\alpha+\beta-i}^{(1+\alpha+\beta)}(\alpha)}{\Gamma(1+\alpha+\beta-i)}.

At last, noticing that

1/\Gamma(1+\alpha+\beta-i)=0\quad\text{for }i>\alpha+\beta,

we have that the powers series stops at
i=\alpha+\beta
.
∎

In the same way we can prove the following proposition.

Proposition 3.2
.

Given
z\in\mathbb{C}
and an integer
m
, then

(3.4)

\frac{\Gamma(z+m+1)}{\Gamma(z-m)}=\sum_{i=0}^{m}\frac{(2m+1)!}{(2i+1)!}\frac{B_{2m-2i}^{(2m+2)}(m+1)}{(2m-2i)!}z^{2i+1}

and

(3.5)

\begin{split}\frac{\Gamma(z+m)}{\Gamma(z-m)}+\frac{\Gamma(z+m+1)}{\Gamma(z-m+1)}=&\sum_{i=0}^{m}\frac{2(2m)!}{(2i)!(2m-2i)!}B_{2m-2i}^{(2m+1)}(m)z^{2i}.\end{split}

Proof.

This are just special cases of theorem
3.1
.
We already know from Lemma
2.4
that the ratio

\Gamma(z+m+1)/\Gamma(z-m)
is a odd polynomial of degree
2m+1
.
Choosing
\alpha=m+1
and
\beta=m
in theorem
3.1
we find (
3.4
).

It also follows from lemma
2.4
that the (
3.5
)
is an even polynomial of degree
2m
.
Following the demonstration of theorem
3.1
we end up with

\begin{split}\frac{\Gamma(z+m)}{\Gamma(z-m)}+\frac{\Gamma(z+m+1)}{\Gamma(z-m+1)}=&\sum_{i=0}^{m}\frac{(2m)!}{(2i)!(2m-2i)!}\left(B_{2m-2i}^{(2m+1)}(m)\right.\\
&\left.+B_{2m-2i}^{(2m+1)}(m+1)\right)z^{2i}.\end{split}

Then from the definition of generalized Bernoulli polynomials in Def.
1.3

it clearly follows that
[
11
]

B^{(m)}_{n}(t)=(-1)^{n}B_{n}^{(m)}(m-t),

and in particular that
B_{2m-2i}^{(2m+1)}(m)=B_{2m-2i}^{(2m+1)}(m+1)
.
∎

The coefficients
c_{2k+1}^{(m)}
and
d_{2k+1}^{(m)}
immediately follow from
proposition
3.2
.

Proposition 3.3
.

The coefficient of linearity between hyperbolic series and Lambert series of Def.
2.5
are

(3.6)

\begin{split}c_{2k+1}^{(m)}&=\frac{(2m+1)!}{(2k+1)!}\frac{B_{2m-2k}^{(2m+2)}(m+1)}{(2m-2k)!}\quad k\geq 0,\\
d_{2k}^{(m)}&=\frac{2(2m)!}{(2k)!(2m-2k)!}B_{2m-2k}^{(2m+1)}(m)\quad k\geq 0.\end{split}

From the representation of the ratio of two gamma function in theorem
3.1

we can also find some special zeros of the generalized Bernoulli polynomials.

Lemma 3.4
.

For any integer
m\geq 1
the followings are zeros of the generalized Bernoulli polynomials

(3.7)

B^{(2m+1)}_{2m}\left(m\right)=0.

Proof.

We recall that from theorem
3.2
given an integer
m
we have

\begin{split}\frac{\Gamma(z+m)}{\Gamma(z-m)}+\frac{\Gamma(z+m+1)}{\Gamma(z-m+1)}=&\sum_{i=0}^{m}\frac{2(2m)!}{(2i)!(2m-2i)!}B_{2m-2i}^{(2m+1)}(m)z^{2i}.\end{split}

If we evaluate the previous expression in
z=0
for integer
m\geq 1
, the two ratios of gamma
functions are vanishing

\frac{\Gamma(m)}{\Gamma(-m)}+\frac{\Gamma(m+1)}{\Gamma(-m+1)}=2\Gamma(m)\Gamma(m+1)\frac{\sin(m\pi)}{\pi}=0

and the sum contains only one terms:

0=\frac{2(2m)!}{(2m)!}B^{(2m+1)}_{2m}\left(m\right),

which gives the zeros in (
3.7
).
∎

To find some special zeros of the polynomial
\mathcal{B}_{2m+2}(\phi)
, coming from the functional
relation (
1.6
), we need to prove the following identity, which uses the previous lemma.

Lemma 3.5
.

For every integer
m
such that
m\geq 0
, the following identity holds true

(3.8)

\sum_{k=0}^{m+1}\frac{B_{2k}\,B_{2m+2-2k}^{(2m+2)}(m+1)}{(2k)!(2m+2-2k)!}=0.

Proof.

Consider the function

(3.9)

f(x)=\left(\frac{x}{e^{x}-1}+\frac{x}{2}\right)\left(\frac{x\,e^{x/2}}{e^{x}-1}\right)^{2m+2}.

The function
f
admits an expansion in power series of
x

(3.10)

f(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}d_{k}\,x^{k}

which is converging for
|x|\leq 2\pi
.
Let us focus just on the even powers of
x
, i.e. on
d_{2k}
.
By expanding the product in (
3.9
) as

f(x)=\frac{x}{e^{x}-1}\left(\frac{x\,e^{x/2}}{e^{x}-1}\right)^{2m+2}+\frac{x}{2}\left(\frac{x\,e^{x/2}}{e^{x}-1}\right)^{2m+2},

we see that the second term is odd, so it does not contributes to the even coefficients
d_{2k}
.
The first term instead can be expanded with the generalized Bernoulli polynomials

\frac{x}{e^{x}-1}\left(\frac{x\,e^{x/2}}{e^{x}-1}\right)^{2m+2}=\left(\frac{x}{e^{x}-1}\right)^{2m+3}e^{(m+1)x}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{k!}B^{(2m+3)}_{k}(m+1)x^{k}

from which, thanks to lemma
3.4
, follows that for every integer
m\geq 0

d_{2m+2}=\frac{1}{(2m+2)!}B^{(2m+3)}_{2m+2}(m+1)=0.

The
f
function has been built such that the first factor in brackets of (
3.9
) is
the generating function of Bernoulli numbers of which we have subtracted the only odd term in
x
:

\frac{x}{e^{x}-1}+\frac{x}{2}=\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\frac{B_{2i}}{(2i)!}x^{2i};

instead, the second brackets is the generating function of Bernoulli polynomials involved
in the identity we want to prove:

\left(\frac{x\,e^{x/2}}{e^{x}-1}\right)^{2m+2}=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{B^{(2m+2)}_{2j}(m+1)}{(2j)!}x^{2j}.

Using the Cauchy product on (
3.9
) we therefore obtain the expansion

f(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\sum_{l=0}^{k}\frac{B_{2l}}{(2l)!}\frac{B^{(2m+2)}_{2k-2l}(m+1)}{(2k-2l)!}x^{2k}.

Equating this expansion with the one in (
3.10
), we obtain an expression for the
vanishing coefficient
d_{2m+2}

\sum_{l=0}^{m+1}\frac{B_{2l}}{(2l)!}\frac{B^{(2m+2)}_{2m+2-2l}(m+1)}{(2m+2-2l)!}=d_{2m+2}=0

which is the identity we wanted.
∎

That is all we need to know to study the functional relations of the hyperbolic series.
However, the theorem
3.1
and alternative way of finding the coefficients
(
3.6
) make it possible to establish several identities between the generalized
Bernoulli polynomials and other special functions. Those relation are established in the
next section, while the functional relations of the hyperbolic series are derived in Sec.
5
.

4.
Bernoulli polynomial identities

The coefficients in Proposition
2.6
are given by (
3.6
)
in terms of the generalized Bernoulli polynomials of the type

B_{n}^{2m+2}(m+1),\qquad B_{n}^{2m+1}(m).

Those defines a new class of polynomials in
m
called the reduced Bernoulli polynomials
[
8
]
.
The representation of the ratio of two gamma functions (
3.2
) and different
methods to derive the coefficients
c_{i}^{(m)}
and
d_{i}^{(m)}
of Proposition
2.6
,
allow to establish identities for ordinary Bernoulli polynomials and reduced Bernoulli polynomials.

For instance, the coefficients can also be derived either by using the Polygamma function

\psi^{(s-1)}(z)=\frac{\partial^{s}}{\partial z^{s}}\log\left(\Gamma(z)\right)

or by the Pochhammer symbol
(x)_{n}
, which is related to generalized Bernoulli polynomials by
[
11
]

(x)_{n}=\frac{\Gamma(x+n)}{\Gamma(x)}=(-1)^{n-1}B_{n-1}^{(n)}(x).

Lemma 4.1
.

The coefficients of linearity between hyperbolic series and Lambert series of Def.
2.5
are

(4.1)

\begin{split}c_{i}^{(m)}=&\frac{\Gamma(m+1)}{\Gamma(-m)(i!)}\sum_{l=0}^{i}\sum_{s=1}^{i-l}\sum_{j=1}^{l}(-1)^{j}\genfrac{(}{)}{0.0pt}{0}{i}{l}Y_{i-l,s}\left(\psi^{(0)}(m+1),\dots,\psi^{(i-l-s)}(m+1)\right)\\
&\times Y_{l,j}\left(\psi^{(0)}(-m),\dots,\psi^{(l-j)}(-m)\right),\\
d_{2k+1}^{(m)}=&\sum_{l=0}^{i}\sum_{s=1}^{i-l}\sum_{j=1}^{l}\frac{(-1)^{j}}{i!}\genfrac{(}{)}{0.0pt}{0}{i}{l}\left\{\frac{\Gamma(m)}{\Gamma(-m)}Y_{i-l,s}\left(\psi^{(0)}(m),\dots,\psi^{(i-l-s)}(m)\right)\right.\\
&\times Y_{l,j}\left(\psi^{(0)}(-m),\dots,\psi^{(l-j)}(-m)\right)\\
&+\frac{\Gamma(m+1)}{\Gamma(1-m)}Y_{i-l,s}\left(\psi^{(0)}(m+1),\dots,\psi^{(i-l-s)}(m+1)\right)\\
&\left.\times Y_{l,j}\left(\psi^{(0)}(1-m),\dots,\psi^{(l-j)}(1-m)\right)\right\}.\end{split}

where
Y_{n,k}
are the incomplete Bell Polynomials

\begin{split}Y_{n,k}(x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{n-k+1})=&\sum\frac{n!}{j_{1}!j_{2}!\cdots j_{n-k+1}!}\times\\
&\times\left(\frac{x_{1}}{1!}\right)^{j_{1}}\left(\frac{x_{2}}{2!}\right)^{j_{2}}\cdots\left(\frac{x_{n-k+1}}{(n-k+1)!}\right)^{j_{n-k+1}}.\end{split}

Instead in terms of the ordinary Bernoulli polynomials and the Stirling number
of the first kind
s(n,k)=\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{1}{n}{k}
,
the coefficients are given by

(4.2)

\begin{split}c_{i}^{(m)}=&\frac{1}{i!}\sum_{j=i-1}^{2m}\frac{2m+1}{j+1}\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{0}{2m}{j}\,(j+2-i)_{i}\,B_{j+1-i}(m),\\
d_{i}^{(m)}=&\frac{1}{i!}\sum_{j=i-1}^{2m-1}\frac{4m}{j+1}\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{0}{2m-1}{j}\,(j+2-i)_{i}\,B_{j+1-i}(m).\end{split}

Proof.

The (
4.1
) are simply obtained by evaluating the
derivatives in (
3.1
) using the Faà di Bruno formula.
Using the chain rules inside (
3.1
) the
coefficient is written as

(4.3)

\begin{split}c_{i}^{(m)}=&\frac{1}{i!}\frac{\partial^{i}}{\partial k^{i}}\frac{\Gamma(k+m+1)}{\Gamma(k-m)}\Big|_{k=0}\\
=&\frac{1}{i!}\sum_{l=0}^{i}\genfrac{(}{)}{0.0pt}{0}{i}{l}\left[\left(\frac{{\rm d}^{i-l}}{{\rm d}z^{i-l}}\Gamma(k+m+1)\right)\left(\frac{{\rm d}^{l}}{{\rm d}z^{l}}\frac{1}{\Gamma(k-m)}\right)\right]_{k=0}.\end{split}

Consider now the derivative of the Gamma function.
In terms of the Polygamma function, if we set

q(y)=e^{y},\qquad g(z)=\log\Gamma(z)

then the Faà di Bruno formula for the derivative of the Gamma function gives

\begin{split}\frac{\partial^{s}}{\partial z^{s}}\Gamma(z)=&\frac{\partial^{s}}{\partial z^{s}}e^{\log\Gamma(z)}=\frac{{\rm d}^{n}}{{\rm d}z^{n}}q(g(z))\\
=&\sum_{s=1}^{n}q^{(s)}(g(z))Y_{n,s}\left(g^{(1)}(z),g^{(2)}(z),\dots,g^{(n-s+1)}(z)\right),\end{split}

where we defined

\begin{split}q^{(s)}(y)=\frac{\partial^{s}}{\partial y^{s}}q(y)=e^{y},\quad q^{(s)}(g(z))=\Gamma(z)\quad g^{(s)}(z)=\psi^{(s-1)}(z).\end{split}

Therefore, we have

(4.4)

\frac{\partial^{s}}{\partial z^{s}}\Gamma(z)=\Gamma(z)\sum_{s=1}^{n}Y_{n,s}\left(\psi^{(0)}(z),\psi^{(1)}(z),\dots,\psi^{(n-s)}(z)\right).

Similarly, choosing
q(y)=e^{-y}
, for the derivative of the inverse Gamma function we find

(4.5)

\frac{\partial^{s}}{\partial z^{s}}\frac{1}{\Gamma(z)}=\frac{1}{\Gamma(z)}\sum_{s=1}^{n}(-1)^{s}Y_{n,s}\left(\psi^{(0)}(z),\psi^{(1)}(z),\dots,\psi^{(n-s)}(z)\right).

Plugging (
4.4
) and (
4.5
) in (
4.3
) proves the proposition for
c_{i}^{(m)}
.
The same procedure gives
d_{i}^{(m)}
.

The Eq.s (
4.2
) are obtained taking advantage of the relation between
the Pochhammer symbol and the Bernoulli polynomials:

\frac{\Gamma(x+1)}{\Gamma(x-n+1)}=(x)_{n}=\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\frac{n}{j+1}\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{0}{n-1}{j}\left(B_{j+1}(x)-B_{j+1}\right).

The coefficient (
3.1
) can then be written as

\begin{split}c_{i}^{(m)}=&\frac{1}{i!}\frac{\partial^{i}}{\partial k^{i}}\frac{\Gamma(k+m+1)}{\Gamma(k-m)}\Big|_{k=0}=\frac{1}{i!}\left(\frac{\partial^{i}}{\partial k^{i}}(k+m)_{2m+1}\right)\Big|_{k=0}\\
=&\frac{1}{i!}\sum_{j=0}^{2m}\frac{2m+1}{j+1}\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{0}{2m}{j}\left(\frac{\partial^{i}}{\partial k^{i}}B_{j+1}(k+m)\right)\Big|_{k=0}.\end{split}

Therefore, making use of

\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}x}B_{n}(x)=\begin{cases}nB_{n-1}(x)&n\geq 1\\

Functional relations for hyperbolic series

We give two different proofs of the functional relations for the hyperbolic series.
In the first one, the functional relation is derived by using the summation theorem applied to
the sum that defined the hyperbolic series. In the second one, the functional relations is
inherited by the functional relation for the Lambert series. Before doing that we prove
the functional relation of the Lambert series using the summation theorem.

Theorem 5.1

(see
[
5
]
Entry 13 Chapter 14)
.

Let
\phi
be a complex number such that
\Re(\phi)>0
and let
m\geq 0
be an integer, then
the Lambert series satisfies the functional equation

(5.1)

\begin{split}\mathcal{L}_{e^{-\phi}}(2m+1)=&-(-1)^{m}\left(\frac{2\pi}{\phi}\right)^{2m+2}\mathcal{L}_{e^{-4\pi^{2}/\phi}}(2m+1)-\frac{\delta_{m,0}}{2\phi}+\\
&+\frac{1}{2}\frac{B_{2m+2}}{2m+2}\left[1+\frac{(-1)^{m}(2\pi)^{2m+2}}{\phi^{2m+2}}\right].\end{split}

Proof.

From the definition of Lambert series we define a function
f(\phi,k)
as

\mathcal{L}_{e^{-\phi}}(2m+1)=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{k^{2m+1}e^{-\phi k}}{1-e^{-\phi k}}=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}f(\phi,k).

Notice that if we exchange
k
to
-k
in
f
we obtain the identity

(5.2)

f(\phi,-k)=f(\phi,k)+k^{2m+1}.

Then, from (
5.2
) if we sum on all positive and negative
k
we obtain

\sum_{k=-\infty,k\neq 0}^{\infty}\frac{k^{2m+1}e^{-\phi k}}{1-e^{-\phi k}}=2\mathcal{L}_{e^{-\phi}}(2m+1)+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}k^{2m+1}.

The last term is divergent and does not depend on
\phi
, we denote it with

D_{1}(2m+1)=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}k^{2m+1}.

The Lambert series is nevertheless finite and can be obtained as

\mathcal{L}_{e^{-\phi}}(2m+1)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=-\infty,k\neq 0}^{\infty}f(\phi,k)-\frac{1}{2}D_{1}(2m+1).

To adopt the summation theorem we promote
k
to a complex variable
z
.
The function
f(\phi,z)
has poles in
z=2\pi in/\phi
for
n=1,2,3,\dots
; in particular
it does not have poles on the real axis.
Consider now the meromorphic function
h

h(z)=\frac{2\pi\,i}{e^{2\pi\,i\,z}-1};

whose only poles are single poles at the integers (including the zero) where the residues are all 1.
Let
C_{N}
be a rectangular closed curve enclosing
-N,-N+1,\dots,0,1,

\dots,N
and cutting the
imaginary axis in
\pm i\epsilon
with
\epsilon<\Im(2\pi i/\phi)
(see Figure
1
),
then the residue theorem gives:

\sum_{k=-N,k\neq 0}^{N}f(\phi,k)=\oint_{C_{N}}f(\phi,z)h(z)\frac{{\rm d}z}{2\pi i}-\text{Res}\left[h(z)f(\phi,z),z=0\right].

Figure 1.
The
C_{N}
path. If you choose
\epsilon<\Im(2\pi i/\phi)
none of the pole of
f
is enclosed
by
C_{N}
.

The summation theorem
[
12
]
is obtained performing the limit for
N\to\infty
.
In this case, as seen earlier, the infinite sum
\sum_{k\neq 0}f(\phi,k)
reproduces the Lambert series:

\mathcal{L}_{e^{-\phi}}(2m+1)=\frac{1}{2}\oint_{C}f(\phi,z)h(z)\frac{{\rm d}z}{2\pi i}-\frac{1}{2}\text{Res}\left[h(z)f(\phi,z),z=0\right]-\frac{1}{2}D_{1}(2m+1)

where we denote with Res
\left[f(z),z=z_{0}\right]
the residue of

in
z_{0}
and

C
is the limit of the closed path
C_{N}
for
N\to\infty
and
\epsilon
approaching to
0^{+}
.
It is easy to verify that

-\frac{1}{2}\text{Res}\left[h(z)f(\phi,z),z=0\right]=-\frac{\delta_{m,0}}{2\phi}.

The integral on the complex path can be decomposed in the four segments of the rectangle:

\begin{split}\oint_{C}f(\phi,z)h(z)\frac{{\rm d}z}{2\pi i}=&\int_{-\infty-i0^{+}}^{+\infty-i0^{+}}f(\phi,z)h(z)\frac{{\rm d}z}{2\pi i}+\int_{+\infty+i0^{+}}^{-\infty+i0^{+}}f(\phi,z)h(z)\frac{{\rm d}z}{2\pi i}\\
&+\int_{V^{+}}f(\phi,z)h(z)\frac{{\rm d}z}{2\pi i}+\int_{V^{-}}f(\phi,z)h(z)\frac{{\rm d}z}{2\pi i},\end{split}

where
V^{+}
is the vertical line in the area where
\Re(z)>0
,
while
V^{-}
is the vertical line for
\Re(z)<0
.
Noticing that

\lim_{\Re(z)\to\infty}f(\phi,z)h(z)=0

we conclude that the integral in
V^{+}
is vanishing in the limit. Instead the integral in

V^{-}
is diverging, indeed we have

\lim_{\Re(z)\to-\infty}f(\phi,z)h(z)=\infty.

Since the Lambert series is converging and finite, we expect that at the end all the divergent parts cancel.
The integral over
V^{-}
can be re-written performing the change of variable from
z
to
-z
and
taking advantage of (
5.2
) and of the relation

h(-z)=-h(z)-2\pi i.

We find

\begin{split}\int_{V^{-}}f(\phi,z)h(z)\frac{{\rm d}z}{2\pi i}=&\lim_{\begin{subarray}{c}\epsilon\to 0\\
R\to\infty\end{subarray}}\int_{-R+i\epsilon}^{-R-i\epsilon}f(\phi,z)h(z)\frac{{\rm d}z}{2\pi i}\\
=&\int_{V^{+}}f(\phi,z)h(z)\frac{{\rm d}z}{2\pi i}+\lim_{\begin{subarray}{c}\epsilon\to 0\\
R\to\infty\end{subarray}}\int_{-R+i\epsilon}^{-R-i\epsilon}z^{2m+1}(h(z)+2\pi i)\frac{{\rm d}z}{2\pi i}.\end{split}

Since the integral in
V^{+}
is vanishing only the second term contributes. Then, we see that
the integral in
V^{-}
is diverging but does not depend on
\phi
, we indicate it as

\int_{V^{-}}f(\phi,z)h(z)\frac{{\rm d}z}{2\pi i}=D_{2}(2m+1).

The integrals on the horizontal paths are made again with residue theorem closing them in the
appropriate way. Since for
\Im(z)<0
we have

\lim_{|z|\to\infty}|f(\phi,z)h(z)|=0,

the lower horizontal line can be closed in the lower-half plane. The upper horizontal line
can be moved to the lower one performing again the change of variable from
z
to
-z

This time we find

\begin{split}\int_{+\infty+i0^{+}}^{-\infty+i0^{+}}f(\phi,z)h(z)\frac{{\rm d}z}{2\pi i}=&\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}{\rm d}z\left[f(\phi,z)+z^{2m+1}\right]+\int_{-\infty-i0^{+}}^{+\infty-i0^{+}}f(\phi,z)h(z)\frac{{\rm d}z}{2\pi i}\\
&+\int_{-\infty-i0^{+}}^{\infty-i0^{+}}\frac{{\rm d}z}{2\pi i}z^{2m+1}h(z).\end{split}

The second term is identical to the integration to the lower horizontal line.
The last term is vanishing because we can evaluate it with residue theorem closing the path in the lower
half-plane but the integrand does not have poles. Instead the first term can be decomposed in
two parts and transformed again as follows

\begin{split}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}{\rm d}z&\left[f(\phi,z)+z^{2m+1}\right]=\int_{-\infty}^{0}{\rm d}z\left[f(\phi,z)+z^{2m+1}\right]+\int_{0}^{\infty}{\rm d}z\left[f(\phi,z)+z^{2m+1}\right]\\
=&\int_{0}^{\infty}{\rm d}z\left[f(\phi,z)+z^{2m+1}-z^{2m+1}\right]+\int_{0}^{\infty}{\rm d}zf(\phi,z)+\int_{0}^{\infty}{\rm d}z\,z^{2m+1}\\
=&2\int_{0}^{\infty}{\rm d}zf(\phi,z)+D_{3}(2m+1).\end{split}

Again we have decomposed the integral into a diverging part that does not depend on
\phi
and into a finite
part which gives:

\int_{0}^{\infty}{\rm d}zf(\phi,z)=\frac{(-1)^{m}}{2}\frac{B_{2m+2}}{2m+2}\left(\frac{2\pi}{\phi}\right)^{2m+2}.

The last piece we need to evaluate is the integral over the lower horizontal line.
As said, we close the path with a semicircle in the lower half-plane and we compute it with
the residue theorem. Inside that path the function
h
does not have any pole but
f
has
infinite many poles located in
z=-2\pi in/\phi
counted by integer
n
starting from 1.
As a consequence, the result is

\begin{split}\int_{-\infty-i0^{+}}^{+\infty-i0^{+}}f(\phi,z)h(z)\frac{{\rm d}z}{2\pi i}=&\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\text{Res}\left[h(z)f(\phi,z),z=-\frac{2\pi in}{\phi}\right]\\
=&-(-1)^{m}\left(\frac{2\pi}{\phi}\right)^{2m+2}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{n^{2m+1}e^{-4\pi^{2}n/\phi}}{1-e^{-4\pi^{2}n/\phi}}\\
=&-(-1)^{m}\left(\frac{2\pi}{\phi}\right)^{2m+2}\mathcal{L}_{e^{-4\pi^{2}/\phi}}(2m+1),\end{split}

where in the last step we recognized the Lambert series. If we put all the pieces together we obtain

\begin{split}\mathcal{L}_{e^{-\phi}}(2m+1)=&-(-1)^{m}\left(\frac{2\pi}{\phi}\right)^{2m+2}\mathcal{L}_{e^{-4\pi^{2}/\phi}}(2m+1)-\frac{\delta_{m,0}}{2\phi}+\\
&+\frac{(-1)^{m}}{2}\frac{B_{2m+2}}{2m+2}\left(\frac{2\pi}{\phi}\right)^{2m+2}+D(2m+1),\end{split}

where

is the sum of all the diverging terms. Since for

all the pieces of the previous

must also be finite. Furthermore, since

does
not depend on
\phi
it can be fixed choosing a value for
\phi
and enforcing the relation. In particular
we choose to evaluate the relation as an asymptotic expansion around
\phi=0
. In this case we have
that the contribution from the Lambert series on the r.h.s of the functional relation is vanishing

while the asymptotic expansion for Lambert series in

if we change variable to

in this way the limit

Next, to perform the derivative in the residue we use the generalized Leibnitz rule:

(A.1)

\frac{{\rm d}^{2m+2}}{{\rm d}z^{2m+2}}\left[F(\phi,z)H(z)\right]=\sum_{k=0}^{2m+2}\genfrac{(}{)}{0.0pt}{0}{2m+2}{k}F^{(2m+2-k)}(\phi,z)\,H^{(k)}(z).

To derive

we recognize the generating functions of Bernoulli numbers in

H(z)=\frac{2\pi i\,z}{e^{2\pi\,i\,z}-1}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(2\pi i)^{n}\,B_{n}}{n!}z^{n}

from which it immediately follows its derivatives

H^{(k)}(z)=\sum_{n=k}^{\infty}\frac{B_{n}(2\pi i)^{n}}{(n-k)!}z^{n-k}

and, particularly, in the limit

(A.2)

H^{(k)}(0)=B_{k}(2\pi i)^{k}.

The previous expression holds even for

Now we evaluate the
s

\begin{split}F(\phi,z)&=\frac{\phi^{2m+2}z^{2m+2}}{\sinh^{2m+2}\left(z\frac{\phi}{2}\right)}=\left(\frac{\phi z}{\sinh\left(z\frac{\phi}{2}\right)}\right)^{2m+2}=\left(\frac{2z\,\phi}{e^{z\phi}-1}\right)^{2m+2}e^{z(m+1)\phi}\\

Therefore, the derivatives of

F^{(s)}(\phi,z)=2^{2m+2}\sum_{n=s}^{\infty}\frac{B_{n}^{(2m+2)}(m+1)\phi^{n}}{(n-s)!}z^{n-s}

and when

F^{(s)}(\phi,0)=2^{2m+2}B_{s}^{(2m+2)}(m+1)\phi^{s}.

Another form of the residue

Going back to Eq. (

We can look at the function

F^{(n)}(z)=\frac{{\rm d}^{n}}{{\rm d}z^{n}}q(g(z))=\sum_{s=1}^{n}q^{(s)}(g(z))Y_{n,s}\left(g^{(1)}(z),g^{(2)}(z),\dots,g^{(n-s+1)}(z)\right),

(A.4)

The derivatives immediately follow

z\to 0
