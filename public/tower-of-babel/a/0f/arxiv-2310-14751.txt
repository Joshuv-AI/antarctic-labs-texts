Efficient and Interpretable Bandit Algorithms
Subhojyoti Mukherjee, Ruihao Zhu, Branislav Kveton ∗

Abstract
Motivated by the importance of explainability in modern machine learning, we design bandit algorithms that are efficient and interpretable . A bandit algorithm is interpretable if it explores with the objective of reducing uncertainty in the unknown model parameter. To quantify the interpretability, we introduce a novel metric of model error , which compares the rate reduction of the mean reward estimates to their actual means among all the plausible actions. We propose CODE , a bandit algorithm based on a C onstrained O ptimal DE sign, that is interpretable and maximally reduces the uncertainty. The key idea in CODE is to explore among all plausible actions, determined by a statistical constraint, to achieve interpretability. We implement CODE efficiently in both multi-armed and linear bandits and derive near-optimal regret bounds by leveraging the optimality criteria of the approximate optimal design. CODE can be also viewed as removing phases in conventional phased elimination, which makes it more practical and general. We demonstrate the advantage of CODE by numerical experiments on both synthetic and real-world problems. CODE outperforms other state-of-the-art interpretable designs while matching the performance of popular but uninterpretable designs, such as upper confidence bound algorithms.

Introduction

A
multi-armed bandit (MAB)
is a class of sequential decision-making problems that have gained significant attention in various fields, including machine learning, economics, and operations research, due to their broad applicability in real-world scenarios, such as clinical trials
(
Thompson 1933
;
Agrawal and Goyal 2012
)
, online advertising
(
Li et al. 2010
;
Foster and Krishnamurthy 2021
)
, and recommender systems
(
Li et al. 2016
;
Eide and Zhou 2018
;
Bendada et al. 2020
;
Mehrotra et al. 2020
)
. In MAB, the goal is to maximize the cumulative reward by interacting with a set of actions under an initially unknown model parameter. Therefore, we have to balance exploration (learning the parameter) and exploitation (reward maximization). Two notable approaches that efficiently balance exploration and exploitation are
upper confidence bound (UCB)

(
Auer et al. 2002
;
Abbasi-Yadkori et al. 2011
)
and
Thompson sampling (TS)

(
Agrawal and Goyal 2012
;
Agrawal and Goyal 2013
)
. At a high level, UCB is a frequentist approach that uses the estimated mean reward of each action and its confidence interval to strike the right balance. TS is a Bayesian approach that maintains a posterior distribution of the model parameter, and samples actions accordingly.

Perhaps not surprisingly, apart from statistical efficiency, another important factor in modern machine learning is
interpretability

(
Carvalho et al. 2019
;
Molnar 2020
)
. Interpretability can increase human trust in machine learning models for certain critical tasks, such as medical diagnoses. Therefore, it is critical to study interpretability in MABs
(
Hüyük et al. 2022
)
. In the context of MABs, some of the most important quantities of interest are the mean reward of the optimal and near-optimal actions. For example, in clinical trials, besides the best treatments, one is also interested in the optimal and near-optimal treatment effects. Knowing these values can help to determine if one should proceed to the next phase of the trials and which treatments should be included. Moreover, as shown in
(
Garivier et al. 2018
)
(see Appendix C), when the optimal mean reward is known, one can achieve constant regret in MABs. Therefore, the near-optimal mean rewards of one MAB task can help to inform future decision-making in similar MABs (
e.g.,
when some of these actions remain available). Following these ideas, we define interpretability as learning the mean rewards of near-optimal actions accurately. To further take reward maximization into account, we say that a bandit algorithm that satisfies the following as both
efficient
and
interpretable
:

A bandit algorithm is efficient and interpretable if it takes actions to attain low regret and learn the mean rewards of near-optimal actions accurately.

In this paper, we study the design of efficient and interpretable bandit algorithms. To this end, we make the following two observations:

•

While both UCB and TS are efficient because they maximize the cumulative reward by exploring at a near-optimal rate, neither of them does a good job in learning the rewards of near-optimal actions (see the forthcoming
Section

6
);

•

On the other hand,
explore-then-commit (EtC)
algorithms
(
Langford and Zhang 2008
;
Garivier et al. 2016
;
Jin et al. 2021
)
) are interpretable. This is because they first explore to minimize model parameter uncertainty, which gives a good estimate of each action’s reward, and then commit to the best empirical action. However, they are statistically sub-optimal due to non-adaptivity and may incur high regret.

As a partial remedy, several phased elimination algorithms have been proposed
(
Soare et al. 2014
;
Fiez et al. 2019
;
Katz-Samuels et al. 2020
;
Wagenmaker et al. 2021
;
Mukherjee et al. 2022
)
. These algorithms run in phases and eliminate sub-optimal actions in between. Within each phase, they rely on an optimal design
(
Pukelsheim 2006
;
Fedorov 2010
)
to find a distribution over actions that maximally explore the unknown parameter, to learn it. As a result, they are more efficient than EtC as they are more adaptive. In addition, they are interpretable because their exploration is guided by optimal designs over plausible, or near-optimal (given the collected data), actions. This helps to minimize the uncertainty of the mean reward estimates of the plausible actions.
However, these algorithms

1.

Perform poorly empirically since no sub-optimal actions are removed in the middle of a phase, even if they are detected early within the phase; and

2.

More critically, cannot handle changing action sets (see Note 1 in Section 22.1 of
Lattimore and Szepesvári 2020
), which is often the case in contextual bandits.

We try to bridge the gap between efficiency and interpretability. Specifically, we make the following contributions:

•

We propose
CODE
, a bandit algorithm based on a
C
onstrained
O
ptimal
DE
sign, that is both efficient and interpretable. In each round,
CODE
uses an approximate version of the
D
-optimal design to explore all plausible actions, determined by a statistical constraint that excludes sub-optimal actions. This leads to a statistically sound exploration guided by the optimal design that maximally reduces the uncertainty in the model parameter. Critically,
CODE
avoids inefficient phases in phased elimination algorithms by replacing the elimination step with the constraints, and hence, can deal with time-varying action sets.

•

Motivated by optimal designs
(
Pukelsheim 2006
;
Fedorov 2010
)
, we propose the first metric for interpretability in the bandit setting. This metric estimates the reduction in model uncertainty. We quantify our metric by estimating the mean reward estimates of all the plausible actions to their actual means. The faster this reduction occurs, the better is the model estimate of the algorithm. We call it the
model error
.

•

Next we instantiate
CODE
in both multi-armed and linear bandits and show that it attains near-optimal regret. Unlike in conventional analysis, the regret of
CODE
in each round depends on the confidence intervals of both the taken and optimal actions. We leverage the optimality criteria of the approximate optimal design problem to control the confidence interval of the optimal actions.

•

Finally, we demonstrate empirically that
CODE
outperforms other state-of-the-art interpretable bandit algorithms in linear bandits, in both synthetic problems and simulations based on real-world data.

This paper is organized as follows. In
Section

2
, we introduce our notation and problem setting. In
Section

3
, we outline key ideas in our explainable and efficient exploration. In
Section

4
, we apply them to
K
-armed bandits. In
Section

5
, we address the more general setting of linear bandits with a changing action set. Finally, we show numerical experiments in
Section

6
and conclude in
Section

7
.

Problem Formulation

We start by introducing our general notation. For any
p\geq 0,
we define a
p
-norm
\|\cdot\|_{p}
.
We denote by
\mathbf{1}_{k}
the
k

1

We use

to denote the indicator function. For any positive semi-definite matrix

. A
d\times d
identity matrix is denoted by
\mathbf{I}_{d}
. We also define
[m]\coloneqq\{1,2,\ldots,m\}
.

The bandit setting is defined as follows. The learning horizon has
n

t\in[n]
. The action set is denoted by
\mathcal{A}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
. It can change with round
t
and we denote by
\mathcal{A}_{t}\subseteq\mathcal{A}
the action set in round

. A random variable
X
is
\sigma^{2}
-sub-Gaussian if
\mathbb{E}[X]=0
and satisfies
\mathbb{E}[\exp(sX)]\leq\exp(s^{2}\sigma^{2}/2)
for all
s\in\mathbb{R}
. The learner interacts with the bandit sequentially. At round

, it takes an action
A_{t}\in\mathcal{A}_{t}
and obtains a reward
X_{t}=\langle A_{t},\bm{\theta}_{*}\rangle+\eta_{t},
where
\eta_{t}
is conditionally
1
-sub-Gaussian. The model parameter
\bm{\theta}_{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is unknown to the learner but the features of the actions in
\mathcal{A}_{t}
are known. We assume that
\|\bm{\theta}_{*}\|_{2}\leq S
and
\|a\|_{2}\leq L
for any
a\in\mathcal{A}
. The cardinality of each
\mathcal{A}_{t}
is at most
K,

|\mathcal{A}_{t}|\leq K
.

The goal of the learner is to minimize pseudo-regret

\displaystyle R_{n}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\sum_{t=1}^{n}\left\langle A_{t}^{*},\bm{\theta}_{*}\right\rangle\right)-\left(\sum_{t=1}^{n}\left\langle A_{t},\bm{\theta}_{*}\right\rangle\right)\right]\,,

where
A^{*}_{t}=\mathop{\rm arg\,max}_{a\in\mathcal{A}_{t}}\langle a,\bm{\theta}_{*}\rangle
is the optimal action at round
t
.

3
Interpretable Exploration via Constrained Optimal Design

We introduce
CODE
, a bandit algorithm based on a
C
onstrained
O
ptimal
DE
sign, that is both efficient and interpretable. The key idea in
CODE
is to take the action that maximally reduces the uncertainty in the unknown model parameter
\bm{\theta}_{*}
from plausible actions. This is formulated as an optimization problem, a variant of the
D
-optimal design subject to constraint. The constraint excludes high-probability sub-optimal actions and defines the plausible action set. In round
t
,
CODE
takes a
deterministic action
that is computed by solving

\displaystyle A_{t}=

\displaystyle\mathop{\rm arg\,max}_{a\in\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}_{t}}\log\det(V_{t}+aa^{\top})

(1)

\displaystyle\text{ s.t. }\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}_{t}

\displaystyle=\{a\in\mathcal{A}_{t}:\max_{\bm{\theta}\in\mathcal{C}_{t}}\langle a,\bm{\theta}\rangle\geq\max_{a^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}_{t}}\min_{\bm{\theta}\in\mathcal{C}_{t}}\langle a^{\prime},\bm{\theta}\rangle\}\,

where
V_{t}\coloneqq\sum_{s=1}^{t-1}A_{s}A^{\top}_{s}
is the
design matrix
up to round
t
that allows computation of high-probability confidence intervals in linear and generalized linear models, and
\mathcal{C}_{t}
represents a high-probability confidence set inside which the unknown model parameter
\bm{\theta}_{*}
resides. We make the following remarks about
CODE
:

(1)

Plausible Action Set
\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}_{t}
:
To handle the possibly changing action set, we introduce a set of per-round constraints that excludes high-probability sub-optimal actions. This is achieved by first building the confidence set
\mathcal{C}_{t}
at the beginning of each round
t
. Then we compare the
upper confidence bound (UCB)
of each action
a
, given by
\max_{\bm{\theta}\in\mathcal{C}_{t}}\langle a,\bm{\theta}\rangle
, against the
lower confidence bound (LCB)
of any other action
a^{\prime}
, given by
\min_{\bm{\theta}\in\mathcal{C}_{t}}\langle a^{\prime},\bm{\theta}\rangle
. The constraint ensures that actions with lower UCBs than any LCB are excluded. That is, if
\max_{\bm{\theta}\in\mathcal{C}_{t}}\langle a,\bm{\theta}\rangle<\min_{\bm{\theta}\in\mathcal{C}_{t}}\langle a^{\prime},\bm{\theta}\rangle
for any
a^{\prime}
, the constraint can be only satisfied by not taking action
a
.

This is in a sharp contrast to classic elimination algorithms, which eliminate sub-optimal actions from a fixed action set. Our design immediately enables
CODE
to deal with changing action sets.

(2)

Interpretability:

CODE
elevates interpretability by design. Specifically, it excludes high-probability sub-optimal actions through the constraint. Then it takes the action, from the remaining plausible actions, that maximally reduces uncertainty in
\bm{\theta}_{*}
,
i.e.
, the one with highest uncertainty in its estimated mean reward. To see this, note that

\displaystyle\mathop{\rm arg\,max}_{a\in\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}_{t}}

\displaystyle\log\det(V_{t}+aa^{\top})

\displaystyle\overset{(a)}{=}\mathop{\rm arg\,max}_{a\in\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}_{t}}\log\det(\mathbf{I}_{d}+V_{t}^{-1/2}aa^{\top}V_{t}^{-1/2})

\displaystyle\overset{(b)}{=}\mathop{\rm arg\,max}_{a\in\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}_{t}}\log(1+\|a\|^{2}_{V_{t}^{-1}})\,.

(2)

Here equality (a) follows from
V_{t}
being constant at round
t
. Equality (b) follows from the fact that for any vector
b
,
\mathbf{I}_{d}+bb^{\top}
has
d-1
eigenvalues
1
and one eigenvalue
1+\|b\|_{2}^{2}
. Therefore, its determinant is
1+\|b\|_{2}^{2}
and its logarithm is
\log(1+\|b\|_{2}^{2})
. By definition, all near-optimal actions are in
\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}_{t}
with a high probability, and reducing the uncertainty in their reward estimates increases interpretability.

3.1
Explainability Metric

To measure an algorithm’s interpretability, we introduce the metric of
cumulative model uncertainty error
,

\displaystyle Q_{n}=\sum_{t=1}^{n}\max_{a\in\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}_{t}}\,(a^{\top}(\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{t}-\bm{\theta}_{*}))^{2}\,,

(3)

where
\bm{\theta}_{*}
is the true unknown model parameter and
\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{t}
is its maximum likelihood estimate from all interactions up to round
t
. We emphasize that, when evaluating an algorithm, the set
\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}_{t}
is computed w.r.t. this algorithm’s own historical data.

When the value of
Q_{n}
is small, it must be true that most
\max_{a\in\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}_{t}}(a^{\top}(\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{t}-\bm{\theta}_{*}))^{2}
are small, and therefore the mean reward estimates of the near-optimal actions in most rounds are close to the corresponding actual mean rewards. As discussed before, this facilitates interpretability, since a human policy designer could act more informatively on a model where the mean rewards of near-optimal actions are estimated more precisely. This could help to dramatically reduce regret for similar MAB problems in the future
(
Garivier et al. 2018
)
.

3.2
Related Optimal Design Works

Now we discuss some related works that connect our optimization problem in (
1
) to optimal designs. There is a growing body of literature on optimal designs for bandits
(
Pukelsheim 2006
;
Fedorov 2010
;
Lattimore and Szepesvári 2020
)
. The output of optimal designs is a distribution over actions. When the learner takes
n
actions according to this distribution, it minimizes some notion of uncertainty in the estimate of
\bm{\theta}_{*}
within a fixed budget of
n
samples. We note that the optimization in (
1
) maximizes the log determinant of the design matrix, which is similar to the
D
-optimal design. By Kiefer-Wolfowitz theorem
(
Kiefer and Wolfowitz 1960
)
, the
D
- and
G
-optimal designs have identical solutions in some problem classes.

Several bandits papers used
G
-optimal designs for regret minimization
(
Soare et al. 2014
;
Fiez et al. 2019
;
Katz-Samuels et al. 2020
;
Wagenmaker et al. 2021
;
Jamieson and Jain 2022
;
Zhu and Kveton 2022b
)
. However, they used them in phases of increasing length and eliminated sub-optimal actions at the end of each phase. The next phase communicates with the earlier one only through non-eliminated actions and neglects all earlier observations. Therefore, these algorithms are conservative and need a fixed action set
\mathcal{A}
. In contrast,
CODE
does not have phases and can be applied to changing action sets
\mathcal{A}_{t}
.
CODE
also does not compute a sampling distribution over actions at each round
t
, because it takes a plausible deterministic action that maximally reduces the uncertainty in the estimate of
\bm{\theta}_{*}
. We outperform the phased elimination algorithms empirically (
Section

6
).

Finally, optimal designs have been used in pure exploration and related settings
(
Soare et al. 2014
;
Fiez et al. 2019
;
Katz-Samuels et al. 2020
;
Fontaine et al. 2021
;
Mason et al. 2021
;
Mukherjee et al. 2022
;
Zhu and Kveton 2022a
)
. We focus on cumulative regret minimization in this work.

\displaystyle T_{t}(a)\coloneqq\sum_{s=1}^{t-1}\mathds{1}\!\left\{A_{s}=a\right\}

\displaystyle\widehat{\mu}_{t}(a)\coloneqq T_{t}^{-1}(a)\sum_{s=1}^{t-1}\mathds{1}\!\left\{A_{s}=a\right\}Y_{s}\,,

where

is the observed reward at round

. Note that the vectors in

are orthonormal. Therefore, the constrained optimization in (
1
) reduces to

\displaystyle A_{t}

\displaystyle\overset{(a)}{=}\mathop{\rm arg\,max}_{a\in\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}_{t}}\log\det(V_{t}+\mathbf{e}_{a}\mathbf{e}_{a}^{\top})

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}_{t}

\displaystyle=\{a\in[K]:\max_{\bm{\theta}\in\mathcal{C}_{t}}\langle\mathbf{e}_{a},\bm{\theta}\rangle\geq\max_{a^{\prime}\in[K]}\min_{\bm{\theta}\in\mathcal{C}_{t}}\langle\mathbf{e}_{a^{\prime}},\bm{\theta}\rangle\}\,

where the equality (a) holds from (

. It follows then that

\displaystyle A_{t}

\displaystyle\overset{(a)}{=}\mathop{\rm arg\,max}_{a\in\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}_{t}}\log(1+\|\mathbf{e}_{a}\|^{2}_{V_{t}^{-1}})\overset{(b)}{=}\mathop{\rm arg\,max}_{a\in\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}_{t}}\|\mathbf{e}_{a}\|^{2}_{V_{t}^{-1}}

\displaystyle\overset{(c)}{=}\mathop{\rm arg\,max}_{a\in\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}_{t}}\dfrac{1}{T_{t}(a)}\,.

Equality (a) holds from (
2

\mathbf{e}_{a}
. Equality (b) follows from the monotonicity of the logarithm. Equality (c) holds because

Now recall that
\mathcal{C}_{t}
in the constraint of (

-armed bandits, we have

\displaystyle\max_{\bm{\theta}\in\mathcal{C}_{t}}\langle\mathbf{e}_{a},\bm{\theta}\rangle

\displaystyle=\widehat{\mu}_{t}(a)+c_{t}(a)\,,

\displaystyle\min_{\bm{\theta}\in\mathcal{C}_{t}}\langle\mathbf{e}_{a^{\prime}},\bm{\theta}\rangle

\displaystyle=\widehat{\mu}_{t}(a^{\prime})-c_{t}(a)\,,

where
c_{t}(a)\coloneqq\sqrt{\frac{2\log(1/\delta)}{T_{t}(a)}}
and
\delta\in(0,1)
is the failure probability of the bounds. Let
U_{t}(a)\coloneqq\widehat{\mu}_{t}(a)+c_{t}(a)
and

. With the above, the constrained optimization problem in (

\displaystyle A_{t}=\mathop{\rm arg\,max}_{a\in\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}_{t}}\ \dfrac{1}{T_{t}(a)}

(4)

\displaystyle\mathrm{s.t.}\ \widetilde{\mathcal{A}}_{t}=\{a\in[K]:U_{t}(a)\geq\max_{a^{\prime}\in[K]}L_{t}(a^{\prime})\}\,.

The constraint in (

To summarize,

solves the optimization in (

) in every round

4.1
Regret Analysis

In this section, we bound the regret of
CODE
in
K
-armed bandits. We state the bound first.

Theorem 1
.

The regret of
CODE
in
K
-armed bandits is
R_{n}=O\left(\dfrac{K\log n}{\Delta_{\min}}\right)
, where
\Delta_{\min}\coloneqq\min_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\Delta(a)
.

The regret bound in
1
scales linearly with the number of actions
K
and depends on the minimum gap
\Delta_{\min}
. This is natural in
K
-armed bandits as actions do not share any information. The factor of
\log n
is standard in gap-dependent regret bounds in
K
-armed bandits
(
Auer et al. 2002
;
Lattimore and Szepesvári 2020
)
. In fact, from Theorem 16.4 of
Lattimore and Szepesvári 2020
, our regret bound matches the gap-dependent lower bound in
K
-armed bandits. This bound also matches that of
LinUCB
and
LinTS
.

Gap-independent Bound:
Note that the result of
1
is an instance-dependent bound. We can get an instance-free minimax bound by setting
\Delta_{\min}=\sqrt{K\log n/n}
. It follows then that

\displaystyle R_{n}=O\left(\sum_{a\in[K]}\Delta(a)+\dfrac{K\log n}{\Delta_{\min}}\right)=O(\sqrt{Kn\log n})

Therefore the gap-independent bound yields a regret of
O(\sqrt{Kn\log n})
.

1
:

, the

Lemma 4.1

(Concentration)

Let

be a good event, that the confidence intervals hold jointly over all rounds and actions,

\displaystyle\xi_{n}\coloneqq\bigcap_{a\in[K]}\bigcap_{t=1}^{n}\left\{\left|\widehat{\mu}_{t}(a)-\mu(a)\right|\leq c_{t}(a)\right\}.

Then
\mathbb{P}(\xi_{n})\geq 1-2Kn^{2}\delta

.

The proof of this lemma is given in

A.1

From

we know

(1-2Kn^{2}\delta)

Lemma 4.2

(Per-Round Regret)

Suppose event
\xi_{n}

\mu(a^{*})-\mu(A_{t})\leq 2c_{t}(A_{t})+2c_{t}(a^{*})

Proof.

The regret of round

\displaystyle\mu(a^{*})-\mu(A_{t})

\displaystyle=(\mu(a^{*})-L_{t}(a^{*}))

\displaystyle+(L_{t}(a^{*})-U_{t}(A_{t}))+(U_{t}(A_{t})-\mu(A_{t}))

\displaystyle\leq(\mu(a^{*})-L_{t}(a^{*}))+(U_{t}(A_{t})-\mu(A_{t})),

(5)

where the inequality follows because according to the constraints in (

is at least as large as the LCB of

. We also observe that the per-round regret depends not only on the taken action
A_{t}
, but also on the optimal action
a^{*}
.

Lemma 4.3
.

(Optimal Action Confidence Interval)

Let event
\xi_{n}
occur and
A_{t}
be the action taken by
CODE
in round
t
. Then
\mu(a^{*})-\mu(A_{t})\leq 4c_{t}(A_{t})
holds.

Proof.

Now observe from the solution of the constrained optimization in (
4
) that it takes those actions that are least taken. This implies that these actions have a higher confidence width than the actions that are taken more.
Since the action
A_{t}
taken at round
t
has the highest confidence interval width, we have that
c_{t}(A_{t})\geq c_{t}(a^{*})
. Following
Lemma

4.2
, we have
\mu_{(}a^{*})-\mu_{(}A_{t})\leq 4c_{t}(A_{t})
. The claim of the lemma follows.
∎

From
Lemma

4.3
observe that the solution in (
4
) ensures that
c_{t}(A_{t})\geq c_{t}(a^{*})
which leads to the per step regret bound of
4c_{t}(A_{t})
.
We finish the proof of
1
by calling
Lemma

4.1
,
4.2
, and
4.3
and
showing that the optimal action
a^{*}
eliminates sub-optimal actions and bounding the number of samples of each sub-optimal action
a
till horizon
n
. The full proof is given in
Section

A.1
.
\blacksquare

4.2
Explainability Metric

A
K
-armed bandit can be also used to show that
CODE
minimizes (
3
). Specifically, based on the formulation in (
4
),
CODE
takes the least taken action among plausible actions
\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}_{t}
. Since the cardinality of
\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}_{t}
is at most
K
, each plausible action must be taken at least
\lfloor(t-1)/K\rfloor
times up to round
t
. Taking the form of high-probability confidence intervals
c_{t}(a)
into account, we have that

\displaystyle Q_{n}\leq K+\sum_{t=K+1}^{n}\frac{4\log(1/\delta)}{\lfloor(t-1)/K\rfloor}=O(K\log(1/\delta)\log n)

holds with probability at least
Kn\delta
.

This setting also shows why classic algorithms, like Thompson sampling, do not minimize
Q_{n}
. Roughly speaking, the plausible actions in TS are all actions because any action can be taken in any round, albeit with a low probability. The unlikely taken actions are highly uncertain by definition and essentially drive
\max_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\,(a^{\top}(\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{t}-\bm{\theta}_{*}))^{2}
in (
3
).

5.1
Regret Analysis

In this section, we bound the regret of
CODE
in linear bandits. We state the bound first.

Theorem 2
.

The regret of
CODE
in linear bandits is given by

R_{n}=\widetilde{O}(d\sqrt{n})
, where
\widetilde{O}
hides logarithmic factors.

This rate is standard in linear bandits
(
Abbasi-Yadkori et al. 2011
;
Lattimore and Szepesvári 2020
)
, and comparable to those of
LinUCB
and
LinTS
with infinitely many actions. From Theorem 24.2 of
Lattimore and Szepesvári 2020
, our regret bound matches the gap-free lower bound of
\Omega(d\sqrt{n})
in this setting. Finally, we note that one
\sqrt{d}
in our bound could be exchanged for
\sqrt{\log K}
if the confidence ellipsoid in (
7
) was designed for finitely many actions.

Proof Sketch of
2
:

The regret bound proof follows a similar rationale as
1
with a few novel techniques. We start off by considering the good event
\xi_{n}
such that
\bm{\theta}_{*}\in\mathcal{C}_{t}
for all
t\in[n]
as follows:

\xi_{n}\coloneqq\bigcap_{t=1}^{n}\{\bm{\theta}_{*}\in\mathcal{C}_{t}\}
.
In the following lemma, we show that the good event
\xi_{n}
holds with high probability.

Lemma 5.1
.

(Confidence Ellipsoid, Theorem 2 of
Abbasi-Yadkori et al. 2011
)

Let
V_{0}=\lambda\mathbf{I}_{d},\lambda>0
. Assume that
\left\|\bm{\theta}_{*}\right\|_{2}\leq S
and
\left\|A_{t}\right\|_{2}\leq L
. Then, for any
\delta>0
, with probability at least
1-\delta
, for all
t\geq 0,\bm{\theta}_{*}
lies in the confidence set
\mathcal{C}_{t}
defined in (
7
).

The proof of this lemma is in
Abbasi-Yadkori et al. 2011
. It follows from
Lemma

5.1
that
\mathbb{P}(\xi_{n})\geq 1-\delta
.
Then we provide a key lemma that upper bounds the per-step regret.

Lemma 5.2
.

(Per-Round Regret)

Under the good event
\xi_{n}
we can show that the expected regret of round
t
is bounded from above as

\displaystyle\langle A_{t}^{*},\bm{\theta}_{*}\rangle-\langle A_{t},\bm{\theta}_{*}\rangle

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left(\langle A_{t}^{*},\bm{\theta}_{*}\rangle-\min_{\bm{\theta}\in\mathcal{C}_{t}}\langle A_{t}^{*},\bm{\theta}\rangle\right)+\left(\max_{\bm{\theta}\in\mathcal{C}_{t}}\langle A_{t},\bm{\theta}\rangle-\langle A_{t},\bm{\theta}_{*}\rangle\right).

The proof of this lemma is similar to that of
Lemma

4.2
, and is deferred to
Appendix

B
.
Following conventional linear bandits analysis, the second term on the R.H.S. of
Lemma

5.2
can be bounded as

\displaystyle\max_{\bm{\theta}\in\mathcal{C}_{t}}\langle A_{t},\bm{\theta}\rangle-\langle A_{t},\bm{\theta}_{*}\rangle\leq

\displaystyle\|A_{t}\|_{V^{-1}_{t}}\max_{\bm{\theta}\in\mathcal{C}_{t}}\|\bm{\theta}-\bm{\theta}_{*}\|_{V_{t}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\|A_{t}\|_{V_{t}^{-1}}\sqrt{\beta_{t}(\delta)},

(8)

where
\beta_{t}(\delta)
is the width of
\mathcal{C}_{t}
, the confidence region of
\bm{\theta}
.
For the first term of the R.H.S. in
Lemma

5.2
, we can similarly have

\displaystyle\langle A^{*}_{t},\bm{\theta}\rangle-\min_{\bm{\theta}\in\mathcal{C}_{t}}\langle A^{*}_{t},\bm{\theta}_{*}\rangle\leq

\displaystyle\|A_{t}^{*}\|_{V^{-1}_{t}}\max_{\bm{\theta}\in\mathcal{C}_{t}}\|\bm{\theta}-\bm{\theta}_{*}\|_{V_{t}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\|A^{*}_{t}\|_{V_{t}^{-1}}\sqrt{\beta_{t}(\delta)}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\|A_{t}\|_{V_{t}^{-1}}\sqrt{\beta_{t}(\delta)},

(9)

where the last step holds by definition of
CODE
.

Combining the above, we have that the per round regret of
CODE
under the good event
\xi_{n}
is at most
2\|A_{t}\|_{V_{t}^{-1}}\sqrt{\beta_{t}(\delta)}
. From here, one can apply the log determinant inequality in
Lemma

A.1
to give us the desired regret bound. The full proof is given in
Section

A.2
.

We remark that
CODE
builds the per-round constraint set
\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}_{t}
at every round
t
that excludes sub-optimal actions with high probability. In contrast, Theroem 22.1 of
Lattimore and Szepesvári 2020
(for phased based elimination Algorithm 12) keeps eliminating high probability sub-optimal actions from a fixed action set. Hence, their algorithm (and the proof technique) cannot handle changing action set.

7
Conclusions and Future Work

We propose algorithm
CODE
for linear bandits that is computationally efficient, statistically efficient, and interpretable. The key idea in
CODE
is to take the action that maximally reduces the uncertainty in the unknown model parameter, from potentially optimal actions. Therefore,
CODE
is comparably simple to other interpretable algorithms, such as
EtC
and
\epsilon
-greedy
.
CODE
can also be viewed as removing phases in phased elimination algorithms. It addresses many issues that plagued these designs
(
Soare et al. 2014
;
Fiez et al. 2019
)
and affected their practical performance. We analyze
CODE
in
K
-armed and linear bandits, and prove tight regret bounds. Finally, we empirically evaluate
CODE
and compare it to state-of-the-art baselines in linear bandits.
CODE
outperforms all interpretable baselines, while matching the performance of popular but uninterpretable designs like
LinUCB
.
In the future work, we want to extend this work to a more general class of reinforcement learning (RL) problems, such as regret minimization in contextual bandits and linear MDPs.

Define the confidence interval for each action

(10)

where,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\bigcup_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\bigcup_{t=1}^{n}\{\widehat{\mu}_{t}(a)\geq\mu(a)+c_{t}(a)\}\right)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\bigcup_{t=1}^{n}\{\widehat{\mu}_{t}(a^{*})\leq\mu(a^{*})-c_{t}(a^{*})\}\right)\leq Kn^{2}\delta

\displaystyle\mu(a^{*})-\mu(a)\leq 2c_{t}(a)\implies T_{t}(a)\leq\dfrac{8\log n}{\Delta^{2}(a)}

as follows:

\displaystyle R_{n}=\sum_{a=1}^{K}\Delta(a)\mathbb{E}[T_{n}(a)]

\displaystyle=\sum_{a=1}^{K}\Delta(a)\mathbb{E}[T_{n}(a)\mathbb{I}\{\xi_{n}\}]+\sum_{a=1}^{K}\Delta(a)\mathbb{E}[T_{n}(a)\mathbb{I}\{\xi^{C}_{n}\}]

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{a=1}^{K}\Delta(a)\mathbb{E}[T_{n}(a)\mathbb{I}\{\xi_{n}\}]+\sum_{a=1}^{K}\Delta(a)\mathbb{E}[\mathbb{I}\{\xi^{C}_{n}\}]

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{a=1}^{K}\Delta(a)\left(1+\frac{8\log n}{\Delta^{2}(a)}\right)+2\sum_{a=1}^{K}\Delta(a)n\delta

\displaystyle=\sum_{a=1}^{K}\Delta(a)+\sum_{a=1}^{K}\frac{8\log(1/\delta)}{\Delta(a)}\ +2\sum_{a=1}^{K}\Delta(a)n\delta

The claim of the theorem follows by setting
\delta=\frac{1}{n^{3}}
.

∎

A.2
Linear Bandit Proof

Lemma A.1
.

(Restatement of Lemma 19.4 from
Lattimore and Szepesvári 2020
)

Let
A_{1},\ldots,A_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a sequence of vectors with
\left\|A_{t}\right\|_{2}\leq L<\infty
for all
t\in[n]
,
V_{t}=\sum_{s=1}^{t-1}A_{s}A^{\top}_{s}+\lambda\mathbf{I}_{d}
with
\lambda\geq L^{2}
, then

\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{n}\left\|A_{t}\right\|_{V_{t}^{-1}}^{2}\leq 2\log\left(\frac{\operatorname{det}V_{n+1}}{\operatorname{det}V_{1}}\right)\leq 2d\log\left(\frac{\operatorname{trace}V_{1}+nL^{2}}{d\operatorname{det}\left(V_{1}\right)^{1/d}}\right)=2d\log\left(d+nL^{2}/\lambda\right).

Theorem 2
.

(Restatement)

The regret of
CODE
in the linear bandits setting is given by

\displaystyle R_{n}\leq 4(2+L)\sqrt{(1+LS)nd\log(\lambda+nL/d)}\left(\lambda^{1/2}S+\sqrt{2\log(1/\delta)+d\log(1+nL/(\lambda d))}\right)

where,
\left\|a\right\|_{2}\leq L
for any
a\in\mathcal{A}_{t}
and
\|\bm{\theta}_{*}\|_{2}\leq S
.

Proof.

Step 1 (Definition)
: Following the work of
Abbasi-Yadkori et al. 2011
we define the width of the confidence interval as

\displaystyle\beta_{t}(\delta)\coloneqq R\sqrt{d\log\left(\frac{1+tL^{2}/\lambda}{\delta}\right)}+\lambda^{1/2}S.

Then define the confidence set (ellipsoid) at round
t
as follows:

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{t}\coloneqq\left\{\bm{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\left\|\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{t}-\bm{\theta}\right\|_{V_{t}}\leq R\sqrt{d\log\left(\frac{1+tL^{2}/\lambda}{\delta}\right)}+\lambda^{1/2}S\right\}.

Finally, define the good event that
\bm{\theta}_{*}\in\mathcal{C}_{t}
for all
t\in[n]
as follows:

\displaystyle\xi_{n}=\bigcap_{t=1}^{n}\{\bm{\theta}_{*}\in\mathcal{C}_{t}\}

We can show from
Lemma

5.1
that
\mathbb{P}(\xi_{n})\geq 1-\delta
.
Finally, for convenience, we state the algorithm once more as follows:

\displaystyle A_{t}=

\displaystyle\mathop{\rm arg\,max}_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\ \log(1+\|a\|^{2}_{V_{t}^{-1}})

(14)

\displaystyle\mathrm{s.t.}\ \left[\underbrace{\langle a^{\prime},\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{t}\rangle+\sqrt{2\|a^{\prime}\|^{2}_{V^{-1}_{t}}\log(\delta^{-1})}}_{U_{t}(a^{\prime})}-\left(\underbrace{\langle a,\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{t}\rangle-\sqrt{2\|a\|^{2}_{V^{-1}_{t}}\log(\delta^{-1})}}_{L_{t}(a)}\right)\right]\geq 0\quad\forall a,a^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}\,.

where
U_{t}(a)
and
L_{t}(a)
denote the UCB and LCB of the action
a
. Let
\ell_{t}=\mathop{\rm arg\,max}_{a}L_{t}(a)
be the index of the action with the highest LCB. We also define the confidence width of action
a
as
c_{t}(a)=\sqrt{2\|a\|^{2}_{V^{-1}_{t}}\log(\delta^{-1})}
.

Step 2 (Per-step Regret):
Using
Lemma

5.2
we can show that the per step regret of round
t
is bounded as follows

\displaystyle\langle A_{t}^{*},\bm{\theta}_{*}\rangle-\langle A_{t},\bm{\theta}_{*}\rangle\leq\left(\langle A_{t}^{*},\bm{\theta}_{*}\rangle-\min_{\bm{\theta}\in\mathcal{C}_{t}}\langle A_{t}^{*},\bm{\theta}\rangle\right)+\left(\max_{\bm{\theta}\in\mathcal{C}_{t}}\langle A_{t},\bm{\theta}\rangle-\langle A_{t},\bm{\theta}_{*}\rangle\right).

(15)

Step 3 (First and Second Term of (
15
)):
The second term of (
15
) can be upper-bounded as

\displaystyle\max_{\bm{\theta}\in\mathcal{C}_{t}}\langle A_{t},\bm{\theta}\rangle-\langle A_{t},\bm{\theta}_{*}\rangle\leq

\displaystyle\|A_{t}\|_{V^{-1}_{t}}\max_{\bm{\theta}\in\mathcal{C}_{t}}\|\bm{\theta}-\bm{\theta}_{*}\|_{V_{t}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\|A_{t}\|_{V_{t}^{-1}}\sqrt{\beta_{t}(\delta)},

(16)

where
\beta_{t}(\delta)
is the width of
\mathcal{C}_{t}
, the confidence region of
\bm{\theta}
.

For the first term of (
15
), we can similarly have

\displaystyle\langle A^{*}_{t},\bm{\theta}\rangle-\min_{\bm{\theta}\in\mathcal{C}_{t}}\langle A^{*}_{t},\bm{\theta}_{*}\rangle\leq

\displaystyle\|A_{t}^{*}\|_{V^{-1}_{t}}\max_{\bm{\theta}\in\mathcal{C}_{t}}\|\bm{\theta}-\bm{\theta}_{*}\|_{V_{t}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\|A^{*}_{t}\|_{V_{t}^{-1}}\sqrt{\beta_{t}(\delta)}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\|A_{t}\|_{V_{t}^{-1}}\sqrt{\beta_{t}(\delta)},

(17)

where the last step holds by definition of
CODE
.

Step 4 (Regret Decomposition):
Combining the above and putting them back to (
15
), we have the expected regret of a round
t
is at most upper bounded by

\displaystyle r_{t}\leq 2\|a\|_{V_{t}^{-1}}\sqrt{\beta_{t}(\delta)}.

(18)

Sum of this over
t=1,\ldots,n
yields the following:

2\sum_{t=1}^{n}\|A_{t}\|_{V_{t}^{-1}}\sqrt{\beta_{t}(\delta)}.

We can apply the log determinant inequality in
Lemma

A.1
from here.
Thus, setting
\lambda>0
, with probability at least
1-\delta
, for all
n\geq 0

\displaystyle R_{n}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{n\sum_{t=1}^{n}r_{t}^{2}}\leq\sqrt{8\beta_{n}(\delta)n\sum_{t=1}^{n}\|A_{t}\|^{2}_{V_{t}^{-1}})}

\displaystyle\leq 8\sqrt{\beta_{n}(\delta)n\log\left(\operatorname{det}\left(V_{n}\right)\right)}

\displaystyle\leq 8\sqrt{nd\log(\lambda+nL/d)}\left(\lambda^{1/2}S+R\sqrt{2\log(1/\delta)+d\log(1+nL/(\lambda d))}\right)

where the last two steps follow from
Lemma

A.1
.
∎

Appendix B
Proof of
Lemma

5.2

Proof.

We can show that the expected regret of round
t
is

\displaystyle\langle A_{t}^{*},\bm{\theta}_{*}\rangle-\langle A_{t},\bm{\theta}_{*}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle A_{t}^{*},\bm{\theta}_{*}\rangle-\min_{\bm{\theta}\in\mathcal{C}_{t}}\langle A^{*}_{t},\bm{\theta}\rangle+\min_{\bm{\theta}\in\mathcal{C}_{t}}\langle A_{t}^{*},\bm{\theta}\rangle-\left(\max_{\bm{\theta}\in\mathcal{C}_{t}}\langle A_{t},\bm{\theta}\rangle-\max_{\bm{\theta}\in\mathcal{C}_{t}}\langle A_{t},\bm{\theta}\rangle+\langle A_{t},\bm{\theta}_{*}\rangle\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle A_{t}^{*},\bm{\theta}_{*}\rangle-\min_{\bm{\theta}\in\mathcal{C}_{t}}\langle A^{*}_{t},\bm{\theta}\rangle+\underbrace{\min_{\bm{\theta}\in\mathcal{C}_{t}}\langle A_{t}^{*},\bm{\theta}\rangle-\max_{\bm{\theta}\in\mathcal{C}_{t}}\langle A_{t},\bm{\theta}\rangle}_{\textbf{Constraint}}-\left(-\max_{\bm{\theta}\in\mathcal{C}_{t}}\langle A_{t},\bm{\theta}\rangle+\langle A_{t},\bm{\theta}_{*}\rangle\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\langle A_{t}^{*},\bm{\theta}_{*}\rangle-\min_{\bm{\theta}\in\mathcal{C}_{t}}\langle A_{t}^{*},\bm{\theta}\rangle-\left(-\max_{\bm{\theta}\in\mathcal{C}_{t}}\langle A_{t},\bm{\theta}\rangle+\langle A_{t},\bm{\theta}_{*}\rangle\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle A_{t}^{*},\bm{\theta}_{*}\rangle-\min_{\bm{\theta}\in\mathcal{C}_{t}}\langle A_{t}^{*},\bm{\theta}\rangle+\left(\max_{\bm{\theta}\in\mathcal{C}_{t}}\langle A_{t},\bm{\theta}\rangle-\langle A_{t},\bm{\theta}_{*}\rangle\right).

(19)

The claim of the lemma follows.
