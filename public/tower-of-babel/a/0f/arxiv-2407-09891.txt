A Close Analysis of the Subset Construction
Ivan Baburin, Ryan Cotterell

Abstract
Given a nondeterministic finite-state automaton (NFA), we aim to estimate the size of an equivalent deterministic finite-state automaton (DFA). We demonstrate that computing the state complexity of an NFA within polynomial precision is PSPACE -hard. Furthermore, we also demonstrate that it is PSPACE -hard to decide whether the classical subset construction will yield an equivalent DFA with an exponential increase in the number of states. This result implies that making any a priori estimate of the running time of the subset construction is inherently difficult. To address this, and to enable forecasting of such exponential blow-up in certain special cases, we introduce the notion of subset complexity, which provides an upper bound on the size of the DFA produced by the subset construction. We show that the subset complexity can be efficiently bounded above using the cyclicity and rank of the transition matrices of the NFA. This yields a sufficient condition for identifying NFAs that can be efficiently determinized via the subset construction.

Introduction

The equivalence in expressivity between nondeterministic finite-state automata (NFAs) and deterministic finite-state automata (DFAs) was one of the first foundational results in the theory of finite-state automata. The original proof by Rabin and Scott
[
20
]
introduced the powerset construction, a general algorithm for converting an arbitrary NFA into an equivalent DFA.
Despite its simplicity and pedagogical value, a major drawback of the powerset construction is that it yields a DFA with exactly
2^{n}
states, where
n
is the number of states in the original NFA.
In general, the equivalent DFA produced by the powerset construction is
not
minimal and can, thus, often be reduced using standard finite-state minimization techniques
[
5
]
. Moreover, in certain pathological cases, the minimal DFA is
exponentially
smaller than the DFA returned by the powerset construction—meaning the algorithm may perform an exponential amount of unnecessary computation.
A trivial example illustrates this phenomenon: consider an arbitrary
n
-state NFA with
no
final states. In this case, the minimal DFA is the 1-state automaton that accepts the empty language.
However, the exponential blow-up is unavoidable in the worst case. Specifically,
[
17
]
showed the existence of an
n
-state NFA, known as the Moore automaton,
1
1

1

See
Section

3
for further exposition.

for which the
minimal
equivalent DFA requires
exactly

2^{n}
states, i.e., the exponentially large DFA output by the powerset construction is already minimal.
Subsequent research identified other examples of NFAs for which the powerset construction yields the minimal DFA
[
4
]
.

A complete theory of determinization for finite-state automata ought to provide a principled understanding of precisely when the minimal DFA equivalent to an NFA incurs an exponential blow-up in the number of states, and when it does not. To this end, several alternative determinization algorithms have been proposed, such as the classic subset construction
[
1
]
and Brzozowski’s algorithm
[
6
]
.
In addition, it has been shown that certain properties of NFAs can be decided
without
performing explicit determinization despite the fact that many naive algorithms for those tasks construct an equivalent DFA.
For example, NFA equivalence and universality can be tested directly
[
3
]
. Although these problems are often
PSPACE
-hard to decide—universality being a prime example—modern techniques frequently outperform approaches that rely on full determinization
[
10
]
.
Nevertheless, our overall understanding remains fragmented, and a comprehensive, unifying theory of determinization continues to elude us.

In this paper, we present new results on forecasting the size of an NFA’s equivalent DFA.
Our primary focus is a novel analysis of the subset construction, a dynamic modification of the powerset construction, which only keeps accessible states.
The subset construction is the most basic and widely used determinization procedure, implemented in many automata toolkits
[
1
]
. It is also relatively well understood—the cases where it results in the minimal DFA were fully characterized by Brzozowski
[
5
]
.
However, Brzozowski’s characterization still does not tell us whether an NFA’s equivalent minimal DFA blows up exponentially.
In that context, as our first contribution, in
Section

3
, we prove that no algorithm can efficiently predict whether the minimal DFA equivalent to a given NFA will exhibit an exponential increase in the number of states.
Moreover, even approximating such a forecast in a coarse-grained manner is
PSPACE
-hard (
Proposition

1
).
More interestingly, we also show that the same holds when forecasting whether the subset construction will produce an equivalent DFA, non-minimal in general, with exponentially more states (
Proposition

2
).
As our second contribution, we turn to analyzing the size of the DFA produced by the subset construction.
Simply restricting nondeterminism does not prevent exponential blow-up when performing the subset construction: unambiguous NFAs and automata with multiple initial states (but otherwise deterministic structure) can still yield exponentially larger DFAs
[
16
]
.
Prior attempts to bound the blow-up have examined special classes such as homogeneous NFAs
[
8
]
and employed algebraic tools like monoid complexity
[
2
]
, but no general bound for subset construction has emerged.
Thus, in
Section

4
, we introduce a simple yet general measure called subset complexity, which provides an upper bound on the size of the DFA produced by the subset construction (
Theorem

4.1
), and thereby a bound on the size of the minimal DFA.
Finally, in
Section

5
, we propose an efficient estimate for computing the subset complexity in practice (
Proposition

3
).
Taken together, we hope these results contribute to a deeper theoretical understanding of the efficiency of determinizing NFAs.

Given an

Corollary 1

Determining the state complexity of a given NFA, i.e., the number of states in a minimal equivalent DFA, is

Proof

This follows immediately from

Note that the state complexity of

The question of optimality for subset construction was resolved in

A well-known special case of this result is given below.

Theorem 3.2
(Brzozowski

6

If an NFA
{{\mathcal{A}}}
is trim and co-deterministic, then the subset construction produces the minimal equivalent DFA.

Even though the subset construction will not produce the minimal DFA for most NFAs, it does have one major advantage—it produces a DFA where all states are accessible.
A more fine-grained analysis says that, if

is an NFA and

This reduces the question of whether it is possible to efficiently find an equivalent DFA to one of computing the size of the subset automaton.

While we believe the next result to be known, we were unable to find a formal presentation or reference for it in the literature, hence we state it as an observation and provide a proof sketch.

Observation 1

Given an NFA
{{\mathcal{A}}}
, determining the state complexity and the size of the subset automaton can be computed in polynomial space.
Hence, the corresponding decision problems are in
PSPACE
.

Proof
(sketch)

Consider the following two decision problems.
(i) For a given automaton
{{\mathcal{A}}}
and

, is the size of the subset automaton larger than
k
?
(ii) For a given automaton
{{\mathcal{A}}}
and
k\in{{\mathbb{N}}}
, is the state complexity of
{{\mathcal{A}}}
larger than
k
?
We claim that both of these problems are in
NPSPACE
, and, hence, due to Savitch’s theorem, also in
PSPACE

[
21
]
.

First, let
{{\mathcal{A}}}={{\left({{\Sigma}},{{Q}},{{I}},{{F}},{{\delta}}\right)}}
be an NFA.
Consider the set of characteristic vectors for the set of states
{{Q}}
in the NFA
{{\mathcal{A}}}
.
Additionally, consider a
{{|{{Q}}|}}
-bit counter which stores the number of distinct accessible states in the corresponding subset automaton.

To demonstrate that (i) is in
NPSPACE
, we enumerate the
2^{|{{Q}}|}
characteristic vectors in an arbitrary total ordering.
For each vector in the enumeration, consider the corresponding subset of the states
{{\mathcal{Q}}}\subseteq{{Q}}
.
Nondeterministically guess the path from
{{\mathcal{Q}}}_{{I}}
(defined in
Algorithm

1
) to
{{\mathcal{Q}}}
if one exists.
The nondeterministic guessing procedure has at most
2^{{{|{{Q}}|}}}
steps, where, in each step, we either nondeterministically choose a symbol

that leads us to the next state or halt.
In the case that we successfully guessed a path to
{{\mathcal{Q}}}
, we increase the
{{|{{Q}}|}}
-bit counter by
1
; otherwise, we keep it unchanged.
After repeating this for all
2^{{|{{Q}}|}}
characteristic vectors, we compare the counter value and
k
, i.e., accept or reject the input.
Thus, (i) is in
NPSPACE
and, by Savitch’s theorem, in
PSPACE
.

To demonstrate that (ii) is in
NPSPACE
, we borrow an idea suggested by Stefan Kiefer
[

]

Because DFA minimization is in

[
9
]
and, hence, due to Savitch’s theorem, in

, we are only required to keep track of polynomially many states from subset construction at once to compute the number of equivalence classes, i.e., determine the state complexity.

\blacksquare

4
Bounding the State Complexity

Due to
Proposition

1
and
Proposition

2
, no reasonable approximation to the state complexity or the size of the DFA produced by the subset construction is possible assuming
{{\textup{{PSPACE}}}}\neq{{\textup{{P}}}}
.
Thus, an alternative is to look for some easily computable lower and upper bounds and keep them as general as possible.
We focus on bounding the size of the DFA produced by the subset construction (
Algorithm

1
), which directly tells us how hard it would be to determinize the chosen NFA in practice.
Note, however, that, by the results in
Section

3
, we repeat that such bounds cannot be both (i) arbitrarily good, and (ii) easy to compute for all NFAs.

One way of achieving such a bound for the subset construction is to consider the transition monoid
{{\mathbb{S}({{\mathcal{A}}})}}
.
It is known that the size of the transition monoid directly translates into an upper bound on the subset automaton.
In the case of unary, commutative, and dense automata, this fact can be used to produce useful bounds
[
2
]
.
However, it remains unclear how to bound the size of the transition monoid for an arbitrary NFA.

Lemma 2
(Monoid bound
[
2
]
)

Given an NFA
{{\mathcal{A}}}
, the DFA produced by the subset construction
{{{{\mathcal{A}}}_{\textsc{ss}}}}
satisfies
|{{{{\mathcal{A}}}_{\textsc{ss}}}}|\leq|{{\mathbb{S}({{\mathcal{A}}})}}|
.

Another natural way to bound the size of subset automaton is to consider the range
{{\mathcal{R}\!\left(T^{({{w}})}\right)}}
for all transition matrices in

{{{\mathcal{T}}}}\coloneqq\{T^{({{w}})}\ |\ {{w}}\in{{\Sigma}}\}.

(7)

Because every state in the subset construction corresponds to a characteristic vector over
{{Q}}
, it has to be in the image of some transition matrix.
Hence, the size of their combined images is an upper bound on the size of the subset automaton.

Lemma 3
(Range bound)

Let
{{\mathcal{A}}}
be an NFA with a set of transition matrices
{{{\mathcal{T}}}}
.
Then the DFA output by the subset construction
{{{{\mathcal{A}}}_{\textsc{ss}}}}
satisfies

|{{{{\mathcal{A}}}_{\textsc{ss}}}}|\leq 1+\sum_{{{w}}\in\Sigma}\left|{{\mathcal{R}\!\left(T^{({{w}})}\right)}}\right|,

(8)

where
{{\mathcal{R}\!\left(T\right)}}
is the range of the matrix
T
over the Boolean semifield
{{\mathbb{B}}}
.

Proof

Let
{{{{\mathcal{A}}}_{\textsc{ss}}}}=({{\Sigma}},{{{{Q}}_{\textsc{ss}}}},{{I}},{{{{F}}_{\textsc{ss}}}},{{{{\delta}}_{\textsc{ss}}}})
.
Consider any
{{\mathcal{Q}}}_{J}\in{{{{Q}}_{\textsc{ss}}}}

and let
{{w}}_{1}\cdots{{w}}_{N}\in{{{{\Sigma}}^{*}}}
be a word such that
{{\mathcal{Q}}}_{J}={{\delta}}({{\mathcal{Q}}}_{I},{{w}}_{1}\cdots{{w}}_{N})

Then,
{{\mathcal{Q}}}_{J}
’s characteristic vector is given by

J=I^{\top}T^{({{w}}_{1})}T^{({{w}}_{2})}\cdots T^{({{w}}_{N})}.

(9)

Moreover, because this holds for any
J
, we have

{{{{Q}}_{\textsc{ss}}}}\setminus\{{{\mathcal{Q}}}_{{I}}\}\subseteq\bigcup_{{{w}}\in{{\Sigma}}}{{\mathcal{R}\!\left(T^{({{w}})\top}\right)}}=\bigcup_{{{w}}\in\Sigma}{{\mathcal{R}\!\left(T^{({{w}})}\right)}}.

(10)

Applying the union bound and adding
1
to account for the initial state
{{\mathcal{Q}}}_{{I}}
concludes the proof.

\blacksquare

Serendipitously, both the range and monoid bounds can be naturally combined, admitting a more fine-grained perspective on the nondeterministic blow-up, each compensating for the slackness of the other.

Definition 9

Given an NFA
{{\mathcal{A}}}={{\left({{\Sigma}},{{Q}},{{I}},{{F}},{{\delta}}\right)}}
, we define the
subset complexity

{{\left\lVert{{\mathcal{A}}}\right\rVert}}
as

{{\left\lVert{{\mathcal{A}}}\right\rVert}}=\min_{{{{\mathcal{J}}}}\subseteq{{\Sigma}}}\left(1+\sum_{{{w}}\in{{\Sigma}}\setminus{{{\mathcal{J}}}}}\left|{{\mathcal{R}\!\left(T^{({{w}})}\right)}}\right|\right)|{{\mathbb{S}({{{\mathcal{J}}}})}}|,

(11)

where
{{\mathbb{S}({{{\mathcal{J}}}})}}
denotes the submonoid generated by transitions
\{T^{({{w}})}\ |\ {{w}}\in{{{\mathcal{J}}}}\}
.

Essentially, subset complexity allows us to split
{{\mathcal{A}}}
into subautomata according to whether the symbols on the transitions are in
{{{\mathcal{J}}}}
or
\Sigma\setminus{{{\mathcal{J}}}}
, respectively.
Then, we either apply the monoid or the range bound independently.
The subset complexity is then the best possible split.

Theorem 4.1
(Subset complexity)

Let
{{\mathcal{A}}}
be an NFA and let
{{{{\mathcal{A}}}_{\textsc{ss}}}}
be the DFA output by the subset construction with
{{\mathcal{A}}}
as input.
Then, we have
|{{{{\mathcal{A}}}_{\textsc{ss}}}}|\leq{{\left\lVert{{\mathcal{A}}}\right\rVert}}
.

Proof

Let
{{{\mathcal{J}}}}\subseteq{{\Sigma}}
be arbitrary.
We first show that

|{{{{\mathcal{A}}}_{\textsc{ss}}}}|\leq\left(1+\sum_{a\in\Sigma\setminus{{{\mathcal{J}}}}}\left|{{\mathcal{R}\!\left(T^{({{w}})}\right)}}\right|\right)|{{\mathbb{S}({{{\mathcal{J}}}})}}|.

(12)

For any word
{{\boldsymbol{x}}}\in{{{{\Sigma}}^{*}}}
,
let
{{\boldsymbol{x}}}={{\boldsymbol{y}}}{{\boldsymbol{z}}}={{\boldsymbol{y}}}z_{1}\cdots z_{M}
be the unique decomposition where
z_{m}\in{{{\mathcal{J}}}}
and
M
is maximal.
Furthermore, let
{{\mathcal{Q}}}_{J}={{\delta}}({{\mathcal{Q}_{{{I}}}}},{{\boldsymbol{x}}})
.
Similar to the proof of
Lemma

3
, we consider a characteristic vector
J
for
{{\mathcal{Q}}}_{J}
.
For clarity, we will denote transition matrices corresponding to symbols
{{w}}\in{{{\mathcal{J}}}}
with
M^{({{w}})}
and transition matrices which correspond to symbols
{{w}}\notin{{{\mathcal{J}}}}
with
R^{({{w}})}
.
Then, the characteristic vector

can be written as

J=J^{\prime}M^{(z_{1})}\cdots M^{(z_{M})},

(13)

for some
J^{\prime}\in\bigcup_{{{w}}\in{{\Sigma}}\setminus{{{\mathcal{J}}}}}{{\mathcal{R}\!\left(R^{({{w}})}\right)}}
.
2
2

2

Note that

will, in general, be the product of both
M
and
R

Thus, there are at most
1+\sum_{{{w}}\in\Sigma\setminus{{{\mathcal{J}}}}}\left|{{\mathcal{R}\!\left(R^{({{w}})}\right)}}\right|

and
|{{\mathbb{S}({{{\mathcal{J}}}})}}|
choices for the matrix

.
Thus, the total number of choices for

\blacksquare

Our notion of subset complexity is a direct improvement over both monoid and range bounds, because it allows us to cherry-pick individual transition matrices according to their properties to achieve the tightest bound.

Example 2

Consider the

{{{{\mathcal{A}}}_{\textsc{moore}}}}

.

{{{{{\mathcal{A}}}^{\prime}}_{\textsc{moore}}}}

Claim

For an

{{{{{\mathcal{A}}}^{\prime}}_{\textsc{moore}}}}
, the following inequality holds
{{\left\lVert{{{{{\mathcal{A}}}^{\prime}}_{\textsc{moore}}}}\right\rVert}}\leq 3n^{2}+3n

5
A Simple Upper Bound

In this section, we discuss how to efficiently compute the estimates for the subset complexity from
Definition

9
. This requires computing the size of the range space as well as the size of the transition monoid. The size of the range space is known to be equivalent to counting maximal independent sets in bipartite graphs
[
22
]
, which is a
\#{{\textup{{P}}}}
-complete problem
[
19
]
. The monoid size is generally hard to determine.
3
3

3

One notable exception is when all the matrices are permutations, in which case we use the Schreier–Sims algorithm to compute the size in almost quadratic time
[
15
]
.

Despite these obstacles, we can still derive an efficiently computable upper bound for subset complexity. We start by considering the case where our NFA is unary: for some automaton
{{\mathcal{A}}}_{1}
let
A
be its unique transition matrix. Then the bound on
|{{\mathbb{S}({{\mathcal{A}}}_{1})}}|
relies solely on the
precedence graph

{\mathcal{G}}(A)
, i.e., a directed graph with
|{{\mathcal{A}}}|
vertices and edges defined by the transition matrix
A
.

Definition 10

Given a directed graph
\mathcal{{\mathcal{G}}}
the
cyclicity
of
{\mathcal{G}}
, denoted with
c({\mathcal{G}})
, is the least common multiple of cyclities of all maximal strongly connected components of
{\mathcal{G}}
, where the cyclicity of a strongly connected component
{{{\mathcal{C}}}}
is the greatest common divisor of all cycles in
{{{\mathcal{C}}}}
.

Lemma 4
(Section 3 in
[
2
]
)

Let
{{\mathcal{A}}}_{1}=(\{{{w}}\},{{Q}}_{1},{{\delta}}_{1},{{I}}_{1},{{F}}_{1})
be a
n
-state unary automaton with transition matrix
A
. Then

c({\mathcal{G}}(A))\leq|{{\mathbb{S}({{\mathcal{A}}}_{1})}}|\leq c({\mathcal{G}}(A))+n^{2}-2n+2.

(14)

Now we turn our attention to the range of a Boolean matrix and the methods that can be used to bound its size.

Lemma 5
(Lemma 1 in
[
7
]
)

Given an
n\times n
Boolean matrix
A
, let
{{\mathrm{rank}(A)}}
be the rank of
A
over the Galois field
{{\mathbb{F}}}_{2}
. Then the range of
A
satisfies

{{\mathrm{rank}(A)}}\leq|{{\mathcal{R}\!\left(A\right)}}|\leq 2^{{{\mathrm{rank}(A)}}^{2}/4+{{\mathcal{O}\left({{\mathrm{rank}(A)}}\right)}}}.

Remark 1

Other possibilities for bounding the range exist.
For example, one can consider the size of maximum matching or the notion of Boolean width; see
[
7
]
for in-depth discussion.

Using the simplified bounds for range on symbols
{{\Sigma}}\setminus\{{{a}}\}
and monoid size on the remaining symbol
{{a}}
we get a simple upper bound on the subset complexity for any automaton
{{\mathcal{A}}}
.

Proposition 3
(All-but-one)

Given an
n
-state NFA
{{\mathcal{A}}}={{\left({{\Sigma}},{{Q}},{{I}},{{F}},{{\delta}}\right)}}
, an arbitrary target symbol
{{a}}\in{{\Sigma}}
and any
\varepsilon>0
, the following holds

{{\left\lVert{{\mathcal{A}}}\right\rVert}}\in{{\mathcal{O}\left({{|{{\Sigma}}|}}(c({\mathcal{G}}(T^{({{a}})}))+n^{2})\max_{{{b}}\in{{\Sigma}}\setminus\{{{a}}\}}2^{{{\mathrm{rank}(T^{({{b}})})}}^{2}/(4-\varepsilon)}\right)}}.

This bound has an intuitive interpretation—if all but one of the transition matrices are of low rank, then the subset complexity will be polynomially bounded; the cyclicity is large only in very exceptional cases.
Note that cyclicity can be computed using a simple breadth-first search, while rank can be calculated using Gaussian elimination over
{{\mathbb{F}}}_{2}
.

6
Conclusion

Given an NFA, we have attempted to forecast the state complexity of the minimal equivalent DFA as well as the size of the subset automaton.
In both cases, we show that such forecasting is
PSPACE
