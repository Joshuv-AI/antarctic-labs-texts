Theoretical Analysis of Leave-one-out Cross Validation for Non-differentiable Penalties under High-dimensional Settings
Haolin Zou, ??, Arnab Auddy, ??, Kamiar Rahnama Rad, ??, Arian Maleki ??

Abstract
Despite a large and significant body of recent work focused on estimating the out-of-sample risk of regularized models in the high dimensional regime, a theoretical understanding of this problem for non-differentiable penalties such as generalized LASSO and nuclear norm is missing. In this paper we resolve this challenge. We study this problem in the proportional high dimensional regime where both the sample size n n and number of features p p are large, and n / p n/p and the signal-to-noise ratio (per observation) remain finite. We provide finite sample upper bounds on the expected squared error of leave-one-out cross-validation ( LO {\rm LO} ) in estimating the out-of-sample risk. The theoretical framework presented here provides a solid foundation for elucidating empirical findings that show the accuracy of LO {\rm LO} .

Introduction

1.1
Main goal

Estimating the out-of-sample risk (OO) to assess model performance and to set model complexity is a crucial task in statistical learning. A large body of recent work has studied the theoretical and empirical properties of cross-validation (or its variants) for estimating OO
(
Rahnama Rad et al. 2020
;
Rahnama Rad and Maleki 2020
;
Patil et al. 2021
;
Patil et al. 2022
;
Patil et al. 2023
;
Xu et al. 2021
;
Bellec 2023
;
Beirami et al. 2017
;
Auddy et al. 2023
;
Stephenson and Broderick 2020
;
Stephenson et al. 2021
;
Luo et al. 2023
)
.

In spite of these recent advances, a complete quantitative description of the risk in using leave-one-out cross-validation (LO) is missing when it comes to problems that have the following characteristics 1) non-differentiable penalties such as generalized LASSO and nuclear norm, and 2) finite signal-to-noise ratio when the dimension of the feature space grows proportionally with the number of observations. In this paper, we focus on the aforementioned characteristics for regularized generalized linear models (including logistic and Poisson regression). We use minor assumptions about the data generating process to provide finite sample upper bounds on the expected squared error of leave-one-out cross-validation in estimating the out-of-sample error. A consequence of our theory is that leave-one-out cross-validation is a consistent estimator of the out-of-sample risk.

1.2
Problem statement and related work

Consider the dataset
\mathcal{D}=\{(y_{i},\mathbf{x}_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
, where
\mathbf{x}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
and
y_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
denote the features and response of the
i^{\rm th}
data point, respectively. Observations are independent and identically distributed (iid) draws from some unknown joint distribution
q(y_{i}|\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\top}\bm{\beta}^{*})p(\mathbf{x}_{i})
, where
\bm{\beta}^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
represents the true parameter.

The class of estimates, known as regularized empirical risk minimizers (R-ERM), are based on the following optimization:

\widehat{\bm{\beta}}:=\underset{\bm{\beta}\in\bm{\Theta}}{\argmin}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ell(y_{i},\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\top}\bm{\beta})+\lambda r(\bm{\beta}),

where
\ell(y,z)
is a non-negative convex loss function between
y
and
z
, e.g., square loss
\ell(y,z)=\frac{1}{2}(y-z)^{2}
,
r(\bm{\beta})
is a non-negative convex regularizer, and
\bm{\Theta}
is a convex set to which
\bm{\beta}^{*}
belongs. By selecting particular forms for
\ell
and
r
we can cover a wide range of popular estimators within the high-dimensional setting. For instance, one may use logistic or Poisson for the loss function, and fused LASSO or nuclear norm for the regularizer.

One widely used criterion for model selection and evaluating the accuracy of
\widehat{\bm{\beta}}
is the out-of-sample prediction error (
{\rm OO}
), defined as

{\rm OO}:=\mathbb{E}[\phi(y_{0},\mathbf{x}_{0}^{\top}\widehat{\bm{\beta}})|{\cal D}],

where
\phi(y,z)
is a function measuring the difference between
y
and
z
(often, but not necessarily the same as
\ell(y,z)
), and
(y_{0},\mathbf{x}_{0})
is a sample from the same joint distribution
q(y|\mathbf{x}^{\top}\bm{\beta}^{*})p(\mathbf{x})
, independent of the training set
{\cal D}=\{(y_{i},\mathbf{x}_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
.

Several methods are proposed to estimate
{\rm OO}
such as

K
-fold cross-validation, Generalized Cross-Validation, and Bootstrap, among others. In this paper, we employ the term “risk estimate” as a generic term for referring to these techniques. Despite the abundance of theoretical and empirical results in the literature, studies specifically examining the accuracy of risk estimates in high-dimensional settings, where the number of features is either larger than or comparable to the sample size
n
, are noticeably scarce. As a result many fundamental questions regarding the performance of risk estimates have remained open.

The main objective of this paper is to prove the accuracy of the leave one out risk estimate
{\rm LO}
for a large class of R-ERMs under the high-dimensional setting.
For
1\leq i\leq n
, define

\widehat{\bm{\beta}}_{/i}:=\underset{\bm{\beta}\in\bm{\Theta}}{\argmin}\sum_{j\neq i}\ell(y_{j},\mathbf{x}_{j}^{\top}\bm{\beta})+\lambda r(\bm{\beta}).

{\rm LO}
is defined as:

{\rm LO}:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\phi(y_{i},\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\top}\widehat{\bm{\beta}}_{/i}).

The success of
{\rm LO}
in practical scenarios has encouraged numerous researchers to investigate its accuracy and develop several computationally-efficient approximations for it
(
Rahnama Rad et al. 2020
;
Rahnama Rad and Maleki 2020
;
Patil et al. 2021
;
Patil et al. 2022
;
Patil et al. 2023
;
Xu et al. 2021
;
Bellec 2023
;
Beirami et al. 2017
;
Auddy et al. 2023
;
Stephenson and Broderick 2020
;
Stephenson et al. 2021
;
Luo et al. 2023
;
Austern and Zhou 2020
)
. Notably, key references that are related to our current work are
(
Rahnama Rad et al. 2020
;
Burman 1989
)
. In their study,
(
Burman 1989
)
studied the accuracy of
{\rm LO}
in the low-dimensional setting,
p
fixed, while
n\rightarrow\infty
and
established the consistency of
{\rm LO}
. In other words, they proved that
{\rm LO}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p}}{{\to}}{\rm OO}
. In the more recent paper
(
Rahnama Rad et al. 2020
)
, the authors studied the accuracy of the
{\rm LO}
under the high-dimensional setting, similar to the setting we consider in this paper. However, it is worth noting that the conclusions drawn in
(
Rahnama Rad et al. 2020
)
are contingent upon the following two assumptions that restrict the broader applicability of their outcomes:

1.

Restriction 1: The regularizer is twice differentiable.

2.

Restriction 2: The set
\bm{\Theta}
is the same as
\mathbb{R}^{p}
.

The first assumption excludes a majority of crucial regularizers commonly employed in practical scenarios, including the
\ell_{1}
norm, nuclear norm, group-LASSO, etc. Additionally, the second assumption eliminates scenarios where parameters adhere to a specific set. Instances of such situations arise when a practitioner seeks to estimate a positive definite matrix or when the elements of
\bm{\beta}^{*}
are required to be positive, increasing, or both.

Our paper aims to eliminate Restriction 1 and Restriction 2, which constrained the applicability of the results in
(
Rahnama Rad et al. 2020
)
to numerous real-world problems, and still obtain a sharp upper bound on the error
{\rm LO}-{\rm OO}
.

The main technical novelties that have enabled us to achieve these goals are clarified in Section
2
.

2.1
Assumptions

As we discussed in Section
1.2
our goal is to prove the accuracy of LO under the high-dimensional settings. Before we state our main theorem we explain some of the assumptions we have made and discuss their validity.

A1

\bm{\Theta}\subset\mathbb{R}^{p}
is a closed convex set.

A2

Both
n
and
p
are large, while
n/p\equiv\gamma_{0}\in(0,\infty)
.

A3

\mathbf{X}=(\mathbf{x}_{1},\cdots,\mathbf{x}_{n})^{\top}

are i.i.d.

A4

r(\bm{\beta})=(1-\eta)r_{0}(\bm{\beta})+\eta\bm{\beta}^{\top}\bm{\beta}

A5

Assume that

,

2.2
Discussion of Assumptions

The goal of this section is to provide further intuition on the assumptions we introduced in the previous section.

Assumption A1 is standard in convex optimization. Assumption A2 has become one of the standard frameworks for studying high-dimensional problems
(
Obuchi and Sakata 2019
;
Mousavi et al. 2018
;
Xu et al. 2021
;
Miolane and Montanari 2021
;
Donoho et al. 2009
;
Donoho et al. 2011
;
Bu et al. 2019
;
Patil et al. 2021
;
Patil et al. 2022
;
Jalali and Maleki 2016
;
Bellec 2023
;
Celentano et al. 2023
;
Li and Wei 2022
;
Liang and Sur 2022
;
Fan 2022
;
Dudeja and Bakhshizadeh 2022
;
Dudeja et al. 2023
)
, since it has been able to prove some of the peculiar features estimators exhibit in high-dimensional settings, such as phase transitions
(
Weng et al. 2018
)
.

The Gaussianity of
\mathbf{x}_{i}
in Assumption A3 is prevalent in theoretical papers dealing with high-dimensional problems. While it is straightforward to relax this assumption to sub-Gaussianity, we focus on Gaussian distribution to keep the notations clear and simple. The scaling we have adopted here, i.e.,

p^{-1}c_{X}\leq\sigma_{\min}(\bm{\Sigma})\leq\sigma_{\max}(\bm{\Sigma})\leq p^{-1}C_{X},

is based on the following rationale. First note that

\frac{c_{X}}{p}\|\bm{\beta}^{*}\|_{2}^{2}\leq\mathbb{E}(\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\top}\bm{\beta}^{*})^{2}\leq\frac{C_{X}}{p}\|\bm{\beta}^{*}\|_{2}^{2}.

When
n,p\rightarrow\infty
with
n/p\equiv\gamma_{0}
and when the elements of
\bm{\beta}^{*}
are
O(1)
, we have
\|\bm{\beta}^{*}\|=O(\sqrt{p})
, and hence
\mathbb{E}(\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\top}\bm{\beta}^{*})^{2}=O(1)
. Therefore, under the settings of the paper we can see that the signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) of each data point, defined as
\frac{\var(\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\top}\bm{\beta}^{*})}{\var(y_{i}|\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\top}\bm{\beta}^{*})}
, remains bounded. The reader may check Appendix
B
for details. Intuitively speaking, if SNR per datapoint is very large, the estimation problem will be easy and the problem of risk estimation is not of particular interest; maximum likelihood estimators perform very well. On the other hand, if SNR per data point is very small, the accurate estimation of
\widehat{\bm{\beta}}
will not be possible unless we impose conditions on
\bm{\beta}^{*}
, such as stringent sparsity assumption. However, since we want our results to be generic in terms of
\bm{\beta}^{*}
, we do not want to impose any stringent constraint on
\bm{\beta}^{*}
.

Another way we can justify the scaling in Assumption A3 is that under this scaling, neither the loss
\sum_{j}\ell(y_{j},\mathbf{x}_{j}^{\top}\bm{\beta}^{*})
nor the regularizer
\lambda r(\bm{\beta}^{*})
dominate each other when
n,p\to\infty
. In other words, for a large class of losses
\sum_{j}\ell(y_{j},\mathbf{x}_{j}^{\top}\bm{\beta}^{*})=O_{p}(n)
and for a large class of regularizers
\lambda r(\bm{\beta}^{*})=O_{p}(p)
. Given that
n/p=\gamma_{0}
, the two terms have the same order. Effectively, this makes the optimal choice of
\lambda
(that gives the minimum out-of-sample error),
O_{p}(1)
. For more precise arguments behind this, we refer the reader to
(
Mousavi et al. 2018
;
Wang et al. 2020
;
Wang et al. 2022
)
.

Assumptions A4 guarantees the loss function to be
2\lambda\eta
-strongly convex. Researchers have noticed that in wide range of applications, adding
\eta\bm{\beta}^{\top}\bm{\beta}
in addition to the non-differentiable regularizer often improves the prediction performance
(
Hastie et al. 2017
;
Mazumder et al. 2023
;
Wang et al. 2022
;
Guo et al. 2023
)
.

Assumption A4 also posits the Lipschitz continuity of
r_{0}
. Nearly all popular non-differentiable regularizers satisfy this condition. Appendix
C.1
presents several examples including LASSO, generalized LASSO and Schatten norms with the nuclear norm as a special case.

Assumption A5 also holds for a wide range of models that are used in practice. For instance Appendix
C.2
considers standard data generation mechanisms that are used in linear regression, logistic regression and Poisson regression, and show that for all those models Assumption A5 holds.

2.3
Main theorem

The following theorem and its discussion are the main contributions of this paper:

Theorem 2.1
.

Under Assumption A1-A5, there exists a constant
C_{v}
depending only on
\{\gamma_{0},\lambda,\eta,s,C_{X},C_{Y}(\cdot)\}
such that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}({\rm LO}-{\rm OO})^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C_{v}}{n}.

Before we present the sketch of the proof, we would like to discuss this theorem and provide some intuition.

Remark 2.1
.

A simple conclusion of this theorem is that under the asymptotic setting in which
n,p\rightarrow\infty
and the ratio
n/p
remains fixed, and
p^{-1}c_{X}\leq\sigma_{\min}(\bm{\Sigma})\leq\sigma_{\max}(\bm{\Sigma})\leq p^{-1}C_{X}
, LO offers a consistent estimate of the out-of-sample prediction error in the sense that
{\rm LO}\rightarrow{\rm OO}
in probability. However, note that Theorem
2.1
offers more than the consistency, and it captures the convergence rate as well.

Remark 2.2
.

The rate
\frac{1}{n}
obtained in Theorem
2.1
is expected to be sharp. Note that the leave-one-out cross-validation takes an average of
n
estimates of the OO, namely
\phi(y_{i};\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\top}\widehat{\bm{\beta}}_{/i})
. The variance of each of these estimates is
O(1)
. Hence, if all these estimates were independent, the variance of LO would be still proportional to
O(1/n)
, which is the same as the bound we have obtained.

Remark 2.3
.

The rate
O(1/n)
has also been seen in the previous work on the analysis of LO under the low-dimensional asymptotic, where
p
is fixed, while
n\rightarrow\infty
. For instance,
(
Burman 1989
)
provided a rate estimate of the variance of
{\rm LO}
(Theorem 6.2(c) in
(
Burman 1989
)
, notations modified) and showed:

\displaystyle\var({\rm LO}-{\rm OO})=O(1/n).

Note that
\var({\rm LO}-{\rm OO})\leq\mathbb{E}({\rm LO}-{\rm OO})^{2}
.
Our result show that the rate is still
O(1/n)
in high-dimensional settings. More recently, the paper
(
Austern and Zhou 2020
)
has characterized the limiting distribution of the
k
-fold cross-validation under the low-dimensional setting. In order to obtain a non-degenerate limiting distribution for LO,
(
Austern and Zhou 2020
)
has to scale
{\rm LO}-{\rm OO}
with
\sqrt{n}
. This scaling translates to the same scaling as the one in
(
Burman 1989
)
.

Proofs

3.1
Main challenges and novel techniques

As mentioned earlier,
(
Rahnama Rad et al. 2020
)
established a result akin to Theorem
2.1
in the case where the regularizer is twice differentiable, and there is no constraint, i.e.
\bm{\Theta}=\mathbb{R}^{p}
.

In our proof, there are two new challenges: the non-smoothness of the regularizer
r
, and the existence of the convex constraint
\bm{\Theta}
. We introduce two novel elements that allowed us to significantly broaden the scope of Theorem
2.1
well beyond what was offered by
(
Rahnama Rad et al. 2020
)
: smoothing and projection.

1.

Smoothing: We start with approximating the non-smooth regularizer,
r_{0}(\bm{\beta})
with a smooth function:

r_{0}^{\alpha}(\bm{\beta})=\int_{\bm{\Theta}}r_{0}(\bm{\beta}-\mathbf{z})\alpha\phi(\alpha\mathbf{z})d\mathbf{z}

where
\phi(\mathbf{z})=(2\pi)^{-p/2}e^{-\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{z}^{\top}\mathbf{z}}
is the density of a standard Gaussian vector.
*
*

*

Note that when
\bm{\beta}
is a
p_{1}
by
p_{2}
matrix, we concatenate its rows to form a vector of dimension
p=p_{1}\times p_{2}
.
The following lemma shows one of the properties of this approximation that will be used throughout the paper.

Lemma 1
.

Suppose
\bm{\Theta}
is closed and
r\in{\cal C}^{0}(\bm{\Theta})
.

(a)

Suppose there exists a positive integer
K
such that
r(\mathbf{z})\left\lVert\mathbf{z}\right\rVert_{2}^{k}e^{-\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert\bm{\beta}-\mathbf{z}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}}

defined in (

{\cal C}^{k}(\bm{\Theta}_{0})
where

is the interior of
\bm{\Theta}

(b)

Suppose there exist constants

|r(\mathbf{x})-r(\mathbf{y})|\leq L\left\lVert\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\right\rVert_{2}^{k}.

Then as

\left\lVert r^{\alpha}-r\right\rVert_{\infty}\to 0.

The proof of this lemma is presented in Appendix

\displaystyle\widehat{\bm{\beta}}^{\alpha}

\displaystyle:=\underset{\bm{\beta}\in\bm{\Theta}}{\argmin}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\ell_{j}(\bm{\beta})+\lambda(1-\eta)r_{0}^{\alpha}(\bm{\beta})+\lambda\eta\bm{\beta}^{\top}\bm{\beta}

\displaystyle\widehat{\bm{\beta}}^{\alpha}_{/i}

\displaystyle:=\underset{\bm{\beta}\in\bm{\Theta}}{\argmin}\sum_{j\neq i}\ell_{j}(\bm{\beta})+\lambda(1-\eta)r_{0}^{\alpha}(\bm{\beta})+\lambda\eta\bm{\beta}^{\top}\bm{\beta}.

2.

Projection operator: The second challenge we have to address in proving Theorem

is the existence of the constraint set
\bm{\Theta}
. One can write the optimization problem

\displaystyle\widehat{\bm{\beta}}=\underset{\bm{\beta}}{\argmin}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ell_{i}(\bm{\beta})+\lambda r(\bm{\beta})+{\mathbf{I}}_{\bm{\Theta}}(\bm{\beta}),

{\mathbf{I}}_{\bm{\Theta}}(\bm{\beta})
is the convex indicator function of the set

\widehat{\bm{\beta}}^{\alpha}
in a different way. The following lemma provides this alternative representation:

Lemma 2
.

Suppose

•

R:\mathbb{R}^{p}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{-\infty,\infty\}

•

\mathbf{prox}_{R}(\mathbf{u}):=\argmin_{\mathbf{x}}\left\{R(\mathbf{x})+\frac{1}{2}\|\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{x}\|_{2}^{2}\right\}.

Then a solution of the following equation:

\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{prox}_{R}(\mathbf{x}-\nabla L(\mathbf{x}))

is also a minimizer of the problem

\min_{\mathbf{x}}\left\{L(\mathbf{x})+R(\mathbf{x})\right\}

and vice versa.

Proof.

See Propositon 3.1 (iii)(b) in
(
Combettes and Wajs 2005
)

\displaystyle\widehat{\bm{\beta}}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{prox}_{\mathbf{I}_{\bm{\Theta}}}(\widehat{\bm{\beta}}-\sum_{i}\dot{\ell}_{i}(\widehat{\bm{\beta}})\mathbf{x}_{i}-\lambda\nabla r(\widehat{\bm{\beta}}))

\displaystyle=\bm{\Pi}_{\bm{\Theta}}(\widehat{\bm{\beta}}-\sum_{i}\dot{\ell}_{i}(\widehat{\bm{\beta}})\mathbf{x}_{i}-\lambda\nabla r(\widehat{\bm{\beta}})),

(6)

where
\bm{\Pi}_{\bm{\Theta}}
is the metric projection operator

\bm{\Pi}_{\bm{\Theta}}(\mathbf{x}):=\underset{\mathbf{z}\in\bm{\Theta}}{\argmin}\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{z}\|_{2}.

Note that to obtain the last equality in (
6
), we have used the fact that the proximal operator of
\mathbf{I}_{\bm{\Theta}}
is the same as the metric projection onto set
\bm{\Theta}
.

Using (
6
), instead of representing
\widehat{\bm{\beta}}^{\alpha}
as the minimizer of
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ell_{i}(\bm{\beta})+\lambda r^{\alpha}(\bm{\beta})+{\mathbf{I}}_{\bm{\Theta}}(\bm{\beta})
, we represent it as the solution of the following fix point equation:

\displaystyle\widehat{\bm{\beta}}^{\alpha}=\bm{\Pi}_{\bm{\Theta}}(\widehat{\bm{\beta}}^{\alpha}-\nabla h^{\alpha}(\widehat{\bm{\beta}}^{\alpha}))

where
\bm{\Pi}_{\bm{\Theta}}(\cdot)
denotes the metric projection onto
\bm{\Theta}
. Similarly,

\widehat{\bm{\beta}}_{/i}^{\alpha}
satisfies:

\displaystyle\widehat{\bm{\beta}}_{/i}^{\alpha}=\bm{\Pi}_{\bm{\Theta}}(\widehat{\bm{\beta}}_{/i}^{\alpha}-\nabla h_{/i}^{\alpha}(\widehat{\bm{\beta}}_{/i}^{\alpha})),

The following lemma establishes some of the important properties of the projection operators that are particularly useful in our paper:

Lemma 3
.

Suppose
\bm{\Theta}
is a closed convex set in
\mathbb{R}^{p}
, and
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
. Let
\bm{\Pi}_{\bm{\Theta}}
be the metric projection onto
\bm{\Theta}
. Then there exists a matrix-valued function

with

\bm{\Pi}_{\bm{\Theta}}(\mathbf{y})-\bm{\Pi}_{\bm{\Theta}}(\mathbf{x})=\int_{0}^{1}\mathbf{J}(t)dt(\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x}).

For completeness, we include the proof of this well known result in Section

In the next section, we explain how using the surrogate estimates
\widehat{\bm{\beta}}_{/i}^{\alpha}
and
\widehat{\bm{\beta}}^{\alpha}
and representing them as the solution of, e.g.

\displaystyle\widehat{\bm{\beta}}_{/i}^{\alpha}=\bm{\Pi}_{\bm{\Theta}}(\widehat{\bm{\beta}}_{/i}^{\alpha}-\nabla h_{/i}^{\alpha}(\widehat{\bm{\beta}}_{/i}^{\alpha})),

enable us to obtain an upper bound on the difference
{\rm LO}-{\rm OO}

4
Conclusion

In this paper, our focus was on the class of regularized empirical risk minimization (R-ERM) techniques that incorporate non-differentiable regularizers. We studied the accuracy of leave-one-out cross-validation techniques within a high-dimensional setting, where both the number of observations
n
and the number of features
p
are large while the ratio
n/p
is bounded. We derived a finite-sample upper bound for the difference between the out-of-sample prediction error and its leave-one-out cross-validation estimate. Our upper bound shows that if
{\rm OO}
and
{\rm LO}
represent the out-of-sample prediction error and its leave-one-out estimate, then
\mathbb{E}({\rm LO}-{\rm OO})^{2}=O(\frac{1}{n})
.

B
Bounded Signal-to-noise Ratio

First we explain what we mean by ’bounded SNR’ in Section
2.1
, using the three examples of linear, logistic and Poisson regression (with log exponential link):

•

Linear:
y_{i}|\mathbf{x}_{i}\sim N(\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\top}\bm{\beta}^{*},\sigma^{2})

•

Logistic:
y_{i}|\mathbf{x}_{i}\sim{\rm Binomial}((1+e^{-\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\top}\bm{\beta}^{*}})^{-1})

•

Poisson:
y_{i}|\mathbf{x}_{i}\sim{\rm Poisson}(\log(1+e^{\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\top}\bm{\beta}^{*}}))

Define the signal-to-noise ratio as

{\rm SNR}:=\frac{\var(\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\top}\bm{\beta}^{*})}{\var(y_{i}|\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\top}\bm{\beta}^{*})}.

When
\|\bm{\beta}^{*}\|_{2}=O(\sqrt{p})
or each elements of of
\bm{\beta}^{*}
is
O(1)
, by Assumption A2 we have

\var(\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\top}\bm{\beta}^{*})=\bm{\beta}^{*\top}\bm{\Sigma}\bm{\beta}^{*}\leq\frac{C_{X}}{p}\|\bm{\beta}^{*}\|_{2}^{2}=O(1).

On the other hand,

\displaystyle[\var(y_{i}|\mathbf{x}_{i})]^{-1}=\begin{cases}\sigma^{-2},&\text{ Linear }\\
(e^{-\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\top}\bm{\beta}^{*}}+e^{\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\top}\bm{\beta}^{*}})^{2},&\text{ Logistic}\\
[\log(1+e^{\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\top}\bm{\beta}^{*}})]^{-1},&\text{ Poisson}\end{cases}

They are all
O_{p}(1)
when
n,p
increase. To see this, notice that

\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\top}\bm{\beta}^{*}\sim N(0,\bm{\beta}^{*\top}\bm{\Sigma}\bm{\beta}^{*})

and

\displaystyle 4\leq(e^{-\frac{1}{2}z}+e^{\frac{1}{2}z})^{2}\leq 2(e^{|z|}+1),

\displaystyle(\log(2)+z_{+})^{-1}\leq[\log(1+e^{z})]^{-1}\leq z_{+}^{-1}

where
z_{+}=z
when
z\geq 0
and
0
otherwise. Suppose
C_{1}\leq p^{-1/2}\|\bm{\beta}^{*}\|\leq C_{2}
for some constants
C_{1},C_{2}
, then
\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\top}\bm{\beta}^{*}=\Theta_{p}(1)
and so is the ratio
\frac{\var(\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\top}\bm{\beta}^{*})}{\var(y_{i}|\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\top}\bm{\beta}^{*})}
.

C
Discussion of the assumptions

C.1
On Assumption A4

In this subsection we present several commonly used regularizers in machine learning, and show that they are all Lipshitz continuous.

Example C.1

(LASSO)
.

The classic LASSO penalty is
r_{0}(\bm{\beta})=\|\bm{\beta}\|_{1}
and is clearly non-negative, convex and Lipschitz continuous.

Example C.2

(Group LASSO)
.

The group LASSO was introduced in
(
Yuan and Lin 2005
)
to achieve joint variable selection among different data groups. Assume the features are partitioned in
G
groups
J_{1},J_{2},\ldots,J_{G}
, and the penalty takes the form
r_{0}(\bm{\beta})=\sum_{j=1}^{G}(\bm{\beta}_{J_{j}}^{\top}\mathbf{K}_{j}\bm{\beta}_{J_{j}})^{1/2}
where
\bm{\beta}_{J_{j}}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{j}}
is the coefficient vector for group
j
,
\mathbf{K}_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{j}\times p_{j}}
is a positive definite matrix,
\bm{\beta}=(\bm{\beta}_{J_{1}}^{\top},\cdots,\bm{\beta}_{J_{G}}^{\top})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
is the concatenated coefficient with
p=\sum_{j=1}^{G}p_{j}
.

Clearly
r_{0}
is non-negative. Moreover it is also convex since it is a sum of convex functions
r_{0j}(\bm{\beta})=(\bm{\beta}_{J_{j}}^{\top}\mathbf{K}_{j}\bm{\beta}_{J_{j}})^{1/2}
. Finally,
r_{0}
is also
\sqrt{\sum_{k=1}^{J}\sigma_{\max}(K_{j})}
-Lipschitz with respect to
\bm{\beta}
.

Example C.3

(Generalized LASSO)
.

The generalized LASSO penalty takes the form of
r_{0}(\bm{\beta})=\|\mathbf{D}\bm{\beta}\|_{1}
and encompasses many LASSO type penalty such as LASSO (
\mathbf{D}=\mathbf{I}_{p}
) and fused LASSO (
\mathbf{D}=(d_{ij})_{(p-1)\times p}
where
d_{ij}=1
if
i=j
,
d_{ij}=-1
if
j=i+1
and
d_{ij}=0
otherwise). The nonnegativity and convexity are immediate since
r_{0}
is the
\ell_{1}
norm of
\mathbf{D}\bm{\beta}
. Moreover, it follows that
\|\mathbf{D}\bm{\beta}\|_{1}
is
\sigma_{\max}(\mathbf{D})
-Lipschitz in
\bm{\beta}
.

Example C.4

(Nuclear norm and Schatten norms)
.

When the estimand is a matrix
\mathbf{B}
with rank
d
, the nuclear norm is a popular regularizer:

r_{0}(\mathbf{B})=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\sigma_{i}(\mathbf{B})

where
\sigma_{i}(\mathbf{B})
is the
i
-th largest singular value of
\mathbf{B}
.
More generally, the Schatten norm of
\mathbf{B}
is defined as

r_{0}(\mathbf{B})=\left(\sum_{i=1}^{d}\sigma_{i}^{p}(\mathbf{B})\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}

and takes the nuclear norm as a special case when
p=1
. Since
r_{0}(\mathbf{B})
is a norm on the singular values of
\mathbf{B}
, we obtain the nonnegativity and convexity of
r_{0}
. To show the Lipschitz continuity we argue as follows. Since
r_{0}(\mathbf{B})
is the p-norm of the vector
(\sigma_{1}(\mathbf{B}),\cdots,\sigma_{d}(\mathbf{B}))
and using the triangular inequality we have

\displaystyle|r_{0}(\mathbf{x})-r_{0}(\mathbf{y})|\leq

\displaystyle~r_{0}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\begin{cases}K^{\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{2}}\left\lVert\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\right\rVert_{2}\quad&\text{if }1\leq p\leq 2\\
\left\lVert\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\right\rVert_{2}\quad&\text{if }p\geq 2\end{cases}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle~\sqrt{K}\left\lVert\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\right\rVert_{2},

where
\left\lVert\mathbf{B}\right\rVert_{2}
denotes the Frobenius norm of
\mathbf{B}
. The second line uses the relationship between p-norms: for
\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
,

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{u}\|_{p}

\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{k=1}^{K}u_{k}^{p}\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}

\displaystyle\leq\begin{cases}K^{\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{2}}\left\lVert\mathbf{u}\right\rVert_{2}\quad&\text{if }1\leq p\leq 2\\
\left\lVert\mathbf{u}\right\rVert_{2}\quad&\text{if }p\geq 2.\end{cases}

Then if we let
\mathbf{u}=(\sigma_{1}(\mathbf{B}),\cdots,\sigma_{K}(\mathbf{B}))^{\top}
we then have
\|\mathbf{u}\|_{p}=r_{0}(\mathbf{B})
and

\|\mathbf{u}\|_{2}=\left(\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sigma_{k}^{p}(\mathbf{B})\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}=\sqrt{\tr(\mathbf{B}^{\top}\mathbf{B})}=\|\mathbf{B}\|_{2}.

C.2
On Assumption A5

In this section, we show that the moment bound of
y_{i}
and polynomial growth of
\ell,\dot{\ell},\phi,\dot{\phi}
in Assumption A5 are justified for many popular data generating mechanisms. We show this for three examples: linear, logistic and Poisson regression (with log exponential link). In this subsection we assume Assumptions A2 and A3 hold, namely,
n/p\equiv\gamma_{0}>0
and
\mathbf{x}_{i}
are i.i.d.
N(0,\bm{\Sigma})
with
\sigma_{\max}(\bm{\Sigma})\leq C_{X}/p
. In addition we assume
p^{-1/2}\|\bm{\beta}^{*}\|\leq\xi
for some
\xi>0
. Finally, both
\ell
and
\phi
are set to the negative log-likelihood.

Example C.5

(Linear)
.

Suppose
y_{i}|\mathbf{x}_{i}\sim N(\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\top}\bm{\beta}^{*},\sigma^{2})
. Then
y_{i}\sim N(0,\tilde{\sigma}^{2})
where
\tilde{\sigma}^{2}=\sigma^{2}+\bm{\beta}^{*\top}\bm{\Sigma}\bm{\beta}^{*}
.
Using standard results on Gaussian moments, we have

\mathbb{E}|y|^{m}=\tilde{\sigma}^{m}\times\dfrac{2^{m/2}\Gamma\left(\frac{m+1}{2}\right)}{\sqrt{\pi}}\leq\left(\sigma^{2}+C_{X}\xi^{2}\right)^{m/2}\dfrac{2^{m/2}\Gamma\left(\frac{m+1}{2}\right)}{\sqrt{\pi}}\,\,\text{for any }m\geq 0.

For Gaussian linear model, the negative log-likelihood is known to be the
\ell_{2}
loss:

\ell(y,z)=\frac{1}{2}(y-z)^{2}\leq y^{2}+z^{2},

and
|\dot{\ell}(y,z)|=|z-y|\leq|z|+|y|
.

Example C.6

(Logistic)
.

Suppose
y_{i}|\mathbf{x}_{i}\sim{\rm Bernoulli}(p)
where
p=(1+e^{-\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\top}\bm{\beta}^{*}})^{-1}
. Then
y_{i}\in\{0,1\}
and obviously

\mathbb{E}|y|^{m}\leq 1\,\,\text{for all }m\geq 0.

The negative log-likelihood of this model is

\ell(y,z)=y\log(1-e^{-z})+(1-y)\log(1+e^{z}),\quad y\in\{0,1\},

and

|\ell(y,z)|\leq 2\log(2)+2|z|.

For its derivative,

|\dot{\ell}(y,z)|=\left\lvert\frac{e^{z}}{1+e^{z}}-y\right\rvert\leq 1+|y|.\\

Example C.7

(Poisson)
.

Suppose
y_{i}|\mathbf{x}_{i}\sim{\rm Poisson}(\mu)
with
\mu=\log(1+e^{\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\top}\bm{\beta}^{*}})
. Then
y_{i}
are non-negative.
Using the equivalence between Poisson and exponential distributions, it can be shown that
y\sim{\rm Poisson}(\mu)
implies
y\sim{\rm Sub-exp}(\mu)
. It then follows by results for sub-exponential random variables (see, e.g., Proposition 2.7.1 of
(
Vershynin 2018
)
) that

\mathbb{E}\left(|y|^{m}|\mathbf{x}_{i}\right)\leq(C_{*}\mu)^{m}m^{m}

whence taking expectation over
\mathbf{x}
it follows that for any
m\geq 0
we have

\mathbb{E}y^{m}\leq\mathbb{E}(\log(1+e^{\mathbf{x}^{\top}\bm{\beta}^{*}}))^{m}\times(C_{*}m)^{m}\leq(C_{*}m)^{m}\mathbb{E}e^{m\mathbf{x}^{\top}\bm{\beta}^{*}}\leq(C_{*}m)^{m}e^{\bm{\beta}^{*\top}\bm{\Sigma}\bm{\beta}^{*}m^{2}/2}\leq(C_{*}m)^{m}e^{m^{2}C_{X}\xi^{2}/2}.

for a numerical constant
C_{*}>0
. In the last series of inequalities we have used first the fact that
\log(1+x)\leq x
for
x>0
. Next we have used the moment generating function of the normal distribution
\mathbf{x}^{\top}\bm{\beta}^{*}\sim N(0,\bm{\beta}^{*\top}\bm{\Sigma}\bm{\beta}^{*})
and finally the inequality
\bm{\beta}^{*\top}\bm{\Sigma}\bm{\beta}\leq C_{X}\xi^{2}
in the last step.

The negative log-likelihood is

\displaystyle\ell(y,z)|

\displaystyle=\log(y!)+\log(1+e^{z})-y\log\log(1+e^{z})

\displaystyle\leq|y\log(y)|+\log(2)+|z|+|y\log(\log(2)+|z|)|

\displaystyle\leq y^{2}+\log(2)+|z|+|y(\log\log(2)+\frac{z}{\log(2)})|

\displaystyle\leq y^{2}+\log(2)+|z|+\log\log(2)|y|+\frac{1}{2\log(2)}(y^{2}+z^{2})

\displaystyle\leq C(y^{2}+z^{2}+1),

and the derivative satisfies

|\dot{\ell}(y,z)|=\left\lvert\frac{1}{1+e^{-z}}-\frac{ye^{z}}{(1+e^{z})\log(1+e^{z})}\right\rvert\leq 1+|y|.

Combining (

\displaystyle\left\lVert\widehat{\bm{\beta}}-\widehat{\bm{\beta}}^{\alpha}\right\rVert^{2}\leq\frac{2(1-\eta)}{\eta}\left\lVert r_{0}^{\alpha}-r_{0}\right\rVert_{\infty}.

From lemma

We first obtain an upper bound for the

\displaystyle\sqrt{\mathbb{E}\dot{\phi}_{0}^{4}(\bm{\beta})}\leq C_{\phi}^{2}\left(1+\left(\frac{C_{X}}{p}\|\bm{\beta}\|^{2}\right)^{s}\right)

For the second moment, notice that

\displaystyle\sqrt{\mathbb{E}\dot{\phi}_{0}^{2}(\bm{\beta})}

\displaystyle\leq[\mathbb{E}\dot{\phi}_{0}^{4}(\bm{\beta})]^{\frac{1}{4}}

\displaystyle\leq C_{\phi}\sqrt{1+\left(\frac{C_{X}}{p}\|\bm{\beta}\|^{2}\right)^{s}}

\displaystyle\leq C_{\phi}\left(1+\left(\frac{C_{X}}{p}\|\bm{\beta}\|^{2}\right)^{s/2}\right).

where the last step uses
\sqrt{a+b}\leq\sqrt{a}+\sqrt{b}
