Biorthogonal measures, polymer partition functions, and random matrices
Mattia Cafasso, Tom Claeys

Abstract
We develop the study of a particular class of biorthogonal measures, encompassing at the same time several random matrix models and partition functions of polymers. This general framework allows us to characterize the partition functions of the Log Gamma polymer and the mixed polymer in terms of explicit biorthogonal measures, as it was previously done for the homogeneous O’Connell-Yor polymer by Imamura and Sasamoto. In addition, we show that the biorthogonal measures associated to these three polymer models (Log Gamma, O’Connell-Yor, and mixed polymer) converge to random matrix eigenvalue distributions in small temperature limits. We also clarify the connection between different Fredholm determinant representations and explain how our results might be useful for asymptotic analysis and large deviation estimates.

Introduction

We study a class of biorthogonal measures on
\mathbb{R}^{N}
, characterized by kernels admitting double contour integral representations. Specific measures of this form are known to be eigenvalue distributions of random matrices, for instance in the Gaussian, Laguerre, and Jacobi Unitary Ensembles (GUE, LUE, and JUE)
[
33
,
53
]
, as well as in generalizations of these like the GUE with external source
[
22
,
23
]
, the LUE with external source
[
4
,
32
]
, sums of LUE and GUE matrices
[
27
]
, product random matrix ensembles
[
1
,
2
,
34
,
35
,
46
,
50
,
51
]
, and Muttalib-Borodin ensembles
[
13
]
. Another measure of this form characterizes the partition function of the O’Connell-Yor polymer partition function
[
41
]
. The first purpose of this paper is to study general properties of this class of biorthogonal measures. This will allow us to interpret all of the above models as instances of a unified framework, and to identify other models which fit in our scheme. More precisely, we will prove that the Log Gamma polymer
[
56
]
and the mixed polymer
[
15
]
partition functions can also be characterized in terms of explicit biorthogonal measures.
These characterizations are direct analogues of a result by Imamura and Sasamoto
[
41
]
for the homogeneous O’Connell-Yor polymer. Moreover, they can be seen as finite temperature generalizations of the connection between last passage percolation with exponential weights and the LUE
[
44
,
20
,
30
]
, and of the connection between Brownian queues and the GUE
[
8
,
38
]
(see also
[
5
]
for a different last passage percolation model). From another perspective, they are finite

variants of the connection between the Kardar-Parisi-Zhang (KPZ) equation and the Airy point process
[
3
]
.
In addition, the biorthogonal measures enable us to clarify the connection between various Fredholm determinant expressions which appeared in the literature before, and to derive a novel representation of these Fredholm determinants, which is possibly more convenient in view of asymptotic analysis and large deviation estimates.

A probability measure on
\mathbb{R}^{N}
of the form

\frac{1}{Z_{N}}\det\left(f_{m}(x_{k})\right)_{k,m=1}^{N}\det\left(g_{m}({x_{k}})\right)_{m,k=1}^{N}\prod_{k=1}^{N}{\rm d}x_{k},

(1.1)

for certain sets of functions
f_{1},\ldots,f_{N}
and
g_{1},\ldots,g_{N}
,
is called a biorthogonal ensemble
[
13
]
. Observe that there is a lot of freedom in the choices of
f_{1},\ldots,f_{N}
and
g_{1},\ldots,g_{N}
for a given biorthogonal ensemble, since one can take linear combinations of columns and rows of the matrices
\left(f_{m}(x_{k})\right)_{k,m=1}^{N}
and
\left(g_{m}(x_{k})\right)_{m,k=1}^{N}
without affecting the distribution.
Biorthogonal ensembles are special cases of determinantal point processes: for
n=1,\ldots,N
, the
n
-point correlation function

\rho_{n,N}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}):=\frac{N!}{(N-n)!Z_{N}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N-n}}\det\left(f_{m}(x_{k})\right)_{k,m=1}^{N}\det\left(g_{m}({x_{k}})\right)_{m,k=1}^{N}\prod_{k=n+1}^{N}{\rm d}x_{k}

(1.2)

can be expressed as a determinant:

\rho_{n,N}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})=\det\left(L_{N}(x_{m},x_{k})\right)_{m,k=1}^{n}.

(1.3)

The correlation kernel
L_{N}
can be taken of the form

L_{N}(x,x^{\prime})=\sum_{k=1}^{N}F_{k}(x)G_{k}(x^{\prime}),

(1.4)

where
F_{1},\ldots,F_{N}
have the same linear span as
f_{1},\ldots,f_{N}
,
G_{1},\ldots,G_{N}
have the same linear span as
g_{1},\ldots,g_{N}
, and they satisfy the biorthogonality relations

\int_{\mathbb{R}}F_{k}(x)G_{m}(x){\rm d}x=\delta_{km},\qquad k,m=1,\ldots,N.

(1.5)

Average multiplicative statistics in biorthogonal ensembles can be expressed as Fredholm series: for any bounded measurable
\sigma:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{C}
, we have

\mathbb{E}\left[\prod_{k=1}^{N}(1-\sigma(x_{k}))\right]=\sum_{k=0}^{N}\frac{(-1)^{k}}{k!}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{k}}\det\left(L_{N}(x_{j},x_{m})\right)_{j,m=1}^{k}\prod_{j=1}^{k}\sigma(x_{j}){\rm d}x_{j}.

(1.6)

The right hand side is the (truncated) Fredholm series associated to the kernel
\sigma(x)L_{N}(x,x^{\prime})
, which we will denote henceforth as
\det(1-\sigma L_{N})_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}
. The subscript
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
indicates that we are integrating over
\mathbb{R}
, however at this point, we do not interpret
\sigma L_{N}
yet as a trace-class operator acting on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
, and
we use
\det(1-\sigma L_{N})_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}
simply as a short-hand notation for the right hand side of (
1.6
).

Orthogonal polynomial ensembles

[
47
]
form an important subclass of biorthogonal ensembles, corresponding to

f_{m}(x)=x^{m-1}
and
g_{m}(x)=x^{m-1}{w(x)}
for some weight function
w
. Then we can take
F_{k}=P_{k-1}
to be the degree
k-1
normalized orthogonal polynomial on the real line with respect to the weight
w
, and
G_{k}=P_{k-1}w
. Orthogonal polynomial ensembles arise for instance as eigenvalue distributions of unitarily invariant Hermitian random matrices, see e.g.
[
28
]
.

Polynomial ensembles

[
49
]
are more general and correspond to
f_{m}(x)=x^{m-1}
with arbitrary
g_{m}
. Examples of them arise as distributions of singular values of products of random matrices and in Muttalib-Borodin ensembles
[
13
]
.

Pólya ensembles

[
37
,
43
]
are polynomial ensembles with a specific derivative structure, corresponding to
f_{m}(x)=x^{m-1}
and
g_{m}(x)=\displaystyle\frac{{\rm d}^{m-1}}{{\rm d}x^{m-1}}g(x)
for some function
g
.

In this paper, we consider a class of complex-valued measures on
\mathbb{R}^{N}
,
N\in\mathbb{N}
, of the following form

{\rm d}\mu_{N}(\vec{x})\equiv{\rm d}\mu_{N}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{N}):=\frac{1}{N!}\det\left(L_{N}(x_{m},x_{k})\right)_{m,k=1}^{N}\prod_{k=1}^{N}{\rm d}x_{k},

(1.7)

where the kernel
L_{N}
can be represented as a double contour integral of the form

L_{N}(x,x^{\prime})=\frac{1}{(2\pi\mathrm{i})^{2}}\int_{\Sigma_{N}}{\rm d}u\int_{\ell_{N}}{\rm d}v\frac{W_{N}(v)}{W_{N}(u)}\frac{{\rm e}^{-vx+ux^{\prime}}}{v-u}.

(1.8)

Here,
W_{N}
,
\ell_{N}
, and
{\Sigma_{N}}
need to satisfy the following assumptions.

Assumption 1.1
.

There exists a vertical strip
\mathcal{S}_{N}:=\{z\in\mathbb{C}:\alpha_{N}<\mathrm{Re}\,z<\beta_{N}\}
in the complex plane such that the following holds:

1.

W_{N}:\mathcal{S}_{N}\to\mathbb{C}
is analytic, not identically zero and such that
W_{N}(z)=O(|z|^{-\epsilon})
as
z\to\infty
in
\mathcal{S}_{N}
, for some
\epsilon>0
;

2.

\ell_{N}
is a vertical line in
\mathcal{S}_{N}
, oriented upwards, i.e.
\ell_{N}=c_{N}+\mathrm{i}\mathbb{R}
with
\alpha_{N}<c_{N}<\beta_{N}
;

3.

{\Sigma_{N}}
is a closed positively oriented curve in
\mathcal{S}_{N}
without self-intersections, lying at the left of
\ell_{N}
, which has
N
(not necessarily distinct) zeros of
W_{N}
in its interior, and none on its image; we denote these zeros as
a_{1},\ldots,a_{N}
.

Note that we allow
W_{N}
to have other zeros in
\mathcal{S}_{N}
than
a_{1},\ldots,a_{N}
, and that the zeros
a_{1},\ldots,a_{N}
do not need to be distinct. The expression (
1.8
) is independent of the choice of
{\Sigma_{N}}
and
\ell_{N}
, as long as the above assumptions are satisfied.
Under these assumptions, we will show that the complex-valued measures (
1.7
) are biorthogonal: they can be written in the form (
1.1
). We will use the term
biorthogonal measure
instead of
biorthogonal ensemble
to indicate that the measures are in general not probability measures. All the examples we study, nevertheless, are real-valued, even if not necessarily positive.
We also define, in analogy with (
1.2
), marginals for such measures,

\rho_{n,N}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}):=\frac{1}{(N-n)!}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N-n}}\det\Big(L_{N}(x_{m},x_{k})\Big)_{m,k=1}^{N}\prod_{k=n+1}^{N}\mathrm{d}x_{k}.

(1.9)

Note that we use the same normalization as for the correlation functions given in (
1.2
), however we avoid the term correlation functions because the measure
\mu_{N}
is not necessarily positive.

Our first result contains the essence of the biorthogonal structure of the measures (
1.7
).

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
W_{N},{\Sigma_{N}},\ell_{N}
satisfy Assumption
1.1
, and let
\mu_{N}
be defined by (
1.7
).

1.

The kernel
L_{N}
given by (
1.8
)
satisfies the properties

\int_{\mathbb{R}}L_{N}(x,t)L_{N}(t,x^{\prime}){\rm d}t=L_{N}(x,x^{\prime}),\qquad\int_{\mathbb{R}}L_{N}(x,x){\rm d}x=N,

(1.10)

and, with
\rho_{n,N}
as in (
1.9
), we have

\rho_{n,N}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})=\det\Big(L(x_{m},x_{k})\Big)_{m,k=1}^{n},\qquad n=1,\ldots,N.

(1.11)

2.

If
a_{1},\ldots,a_{N}
are distinct, we have

L_{N}(x,x^{\prime})=\sum_{m=1}^{N}{\rm e}^{a_{m}x^{\prime}}\psi_{m}({\rm e}^{x}),\quad\text{where}\quad\psi_{m}(y):=\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}W_{N}^{\prime}(a_{m})}\int_{\ell_{N}}\frac{W_{N}(v)y^{-v}}{v-a_{m}}{\rm d}v,

(1.12)

and we have the biorthogonality relations

\int_{\mathbb{R}}{\rm e}^{a_{m}x}\psi_{k}({\rm e}^{x}){\rm d}x=\delta_{mk},\qquad k,m=1,\ldots,N.

(1.13)

The measure (
1.7
) is then a biorthogonal measure, given explicitly by

{\rm d}\mu_{N}(\vec{x})=\frac{1}{N!}\det\left({\rm e}^{a_{m}x_{k}}\right)_{k,m=1}^{N}\det\left(\psi_{m}({\rm e}^{x_{k}})\right)_{m,k=1}^{N}\prod_{k=1}^{N}{\rm d}x_{k}.

(1.14)

3.

In the confluent case
a_{1}=\cdots=a_{N}=a
, the measure (
1.7
) is also biorthogonal and given explicitly by

{\rm d}\mu_{N}(\vec{x})=\frac{1}{Z_{N}}\Delta(\vec{x})\det\left(\phi_{m}({\rm e}^{x_{k}})\right)_{m,k=1}^{N}\prod_{k=1}^{N}{\rm d}x_{k},\quad\Delta(\vec{x})=\prod_{1\leq j<k\leq N}(x_{k}-x_{j}),

(1.15)

where

Z_{N}:=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\Delta(\vec{x})\det\left(\phi_{m}({\rm e}^{x_{k}})\right)_{m,k=1}^{N}\prod_{k=1}^{N}{\rm d}x_{k},

(1.16)

and

\phi_{m}(y):=\frac{y^{a}}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\int_{\ell_{N}}\frac{W_{N}(v)y^{-v}}{(v-a)^{N-m+1}}{\rm d}v.

(1.17)

Remark 1.3
.

As we will explain in detail in Sections
5
,
6
and
7
, positive biorthogonal measures of the form (
1.7
)–(
1.8
) occur in a variety of random matrix models. Three choices of
W
are particularly relevant in this paper: they are given by

\begin{array}[]{rcl}W_{N}^{{\rm LUE+}}(z;{\vec{b}},\nu)&=&\displaystyle\frac{\prod_{j=1}^{N}(z-{b_{j}})}{(z-1)^{N+\nu}},\\
\\
W_{N}^{{\rm GUE+}}(z;\vec{a},\tau)&=&\displaystyle{\rm e}^{\tau z^{2}/2}\prod_{j=1}^{N}(z-a_{j})\\
\\
W_{N}^{{\rm GLUE+}}(z;{\vec{b}},\tau)&=&\displaystyle{\frac{z^{N}{\rm e}^{\tau z^{2}/2}}{\prod_{j=1}^{N}(z-1+b_{j})}}.\end{array}

(1.18)

They are associated, respectively, to the biorthogonal ensembles given by the eigenvalues of a LUE (Laguerre Unitary Ensemble) matrix with external source, a GUE (Gaussian Unitary Ensemble) with external source, and a sum of a LUE matrix with external source and a GUE matrix. As such, the choices of
W
given above define positive-valued measures. We anticipate that these three models describe the small temperature limits of three different polymer models: Log Gamma, O’Connell-Yor and O’Connell-Yor with boundary sources. More details about these small temperature limits are given at the end of the introduction.

Remark 1.4
.

Observe that the integral expressions for
\psi_{m}
and
\phi_{m}

are independent of the choice of the vertical line
\ell_{N}
in the strip
\mathcal{S}_{N}

Recalling the definition of the direct and inverse Mellin transforms,

\mathcal{M}[g](v):=\int_{0}^{\infty}y^{v-1}g(y){\rm d}y,\qquad\mathcal{M}^{-1}[G](y):=\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\int_{\ell_{N}}{G(v)y^{-v}}{\rm d}v,

In exponential variables,

\psi_{m}({\rm e}^{x})=\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}W_{N}^{\prime}(a_{m})}\int_{\ell_{N}}\frac{W_{N}(v)}{v-a_{m}}{\rm e}^{-vx}{\rm d}v=\frac{{\rm e}^{-cx}}{2\pi W_{N}^{\prime}(a_{m})}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{W_{N}(c+\mathrm{i}t)}{c+\mathrm{i}t-a_{m}}{\rm e}^{-\mathrm{i}tx}{\rm d}t

(1.20)

for
\alpha_{N}<c<\beta_{N}
and

, and
similarly for

.

Remark 1.5
.

The confluent form of the biorthogonal measure has the structure of a complex-valued (not necessarily positive) polynomial ensemble, and more specifically of a Pólya ensemble, since
-\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\phi_{m}({\rm e}^{x})=\phi_{m+1}({\rm e}^{x})

coincide, but also in cases where some of them occur with multiplicity bigger than one.

.

In exponential variables

In the special case where

Note that the mechanism to obtain a polynomial ensemble is different here than in the confluent case, as it takes place for equi-spaced

The above biorthogonal structure of the measures (
1.7
) gives us access to determinantal representations for averages of multiplicative statistics of the form
\prod_{k=1}^{N}(1-\sigma(x_{k}))
.
The simplest is an expression in terms of an
N\times N
determinant. In addition, we have several Fredholm determinant expressions.
Specific Fredholm determinants of these types appear in random matrix theory and in the literature of polymer models and Whittaker processes, see for instance
[
14
,
15
,
18
]
.
In our next result, we will gather all these different determinantal representations for average multiplicative statistics. This will allow us in particular to identify models of a different nature, random matrix ensembles and polymer models, as instances of our class of biorthogonal measures.

We will denote henceforth
\mu_{N}[\sigma]
for the average multiplicative statistic associated to a function
\sigma
:

\mu_{N}[\sigma]:=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\prod_{k=1}^{N}(1-\sigma(x_{k})){\rm d}\mu_{N}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{N}).

(1.24)

Given a curve
\gamma
in the complex plane, we recall the definition of the Fredholm determinant of an operator via the Fredholm series associated to its kernel:

\det(1-K)_{L^{2}(\gamma)}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{k}}{k!}\int_{\gamma^{k}}\det\left(K(z_{j},z_{m})\right)_{j,m=1}^{k}\prod_{j=1}^{k}{\rm d}z_{j}.

(1.25)

The relevant curves for us will be
\gamma=\mathbb{R}
,
\gamma=(0,+\infty)
, and
\gamma={\Sigma_{N}}
.
As already mentioned, for now, we write

\det(1-K)_{L^{2}(\gamma)}
whenever (
1.25
) is convergent, and we do not require the associated operator to be trace-class. Later on, we will see that suitable conjugations

of the relevant kernels

give rise to rank

and thus trace-class operators on

(in that case the series truncates after the term

A first identity expresses

A third identity holds only for the specific choice

Next, we introduce a deformation of the biorthogonal measure (

\sigma:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{C}
, we define the measures

{\rm d}\nu_{N,t}(\vec{x}):=\frac{1}{Z_{N,t}}\det\Big(L_{N}(x_{m},x_{k})\Big)_{m,k=1}^{N}\prod_{k=1}^{N}\big(1-\sigma_{t}(x_{k})\big){\rm d}x_{k},\quad t\in\mathbb{R},\ \sigma_{t}(x):=\sigma(x+t),

(1.33)

where

Z_{N,t}:=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\det\Big(L_{N}(x_{m},x_{k})\big)_{m,k=1}^{N}\prod_{k=1}^{N}\big(1-\sigma_{t}(x_{k})\big){\rm d}x_{k},

(1.34)

so that
\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}{\rm d}\nu_{N,t}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{N})=1
, and where we assume that
\sigma,N,t
are such that
Z_{N,t}\neq 0
.

For
\sigma(x)=1_{(0,\infty)}(x)
, the deformed measure is simply the original biorthogonal measure, restricted to
(-\infty,-t)^{N}
and renormalized or, in other words, the original biorthogonal ensemble conditioned on
\max\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{N}\}\leq-t
, in case the measure is positive. For general
\sigma
, if the biorthogonal measure is positive, it can be seen as a pushed particle system
[
48
]
in which the particles
x_{1},\ldots,x_{N}
are pushed to regions where
\sigma_{t}(x)
is small; it can be constructed via a procedure of marking and conditioning of the original biorthogonal ensemble
[
26
]
.

By definition, the deformed measure (
1.33
) is also a biorthogonal measure. However, the associated kernel in general does not admit an explicit double integral form like in (
1.8
), which makes the deformed measure harder to analyze. Nevertheless, the deformed measure encodes
\mu_{N}[\sigma]
in a simple way:
as we will see, logarithmic derivatives of average multiplicative statistics
\mu_{N}[\sigma]
of the non-deformed measure can be expressed in terms of average linear statistics
of the deformed measure. Average linear statistics of determinantal measures are much easier to analyze than average multiplicative statistics, since they can be expressed in terms of the one-point functions. Similarly to the one-point function
\rho_{1,N}(x)
defined by (
1.2
) and given by (
1.3
), we define the one-point function of the deformed measure as

\kappa_{N,t}(x_{1})=\frac{N}{Z_{N,t}}(1-\sigma_{t}(x_{1}))\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N-1}}\det\left(L_{N}(x_{m},x_{k})\right)_{m,k=1}^{N}\prod_{k=2}^{N}(1-\sigma_{t}(x_{k})){\rm d}x_{k}.

(1.35)

It is then straightforward to verify that

\mathop{\text{\Large$\int_{\text{\normalsize$\scriptstyle\mathbb{R}^{N}$}}$}}\nolimits\left(\sum_{k=1}^{N}f(x_{k})\right){\rm d}\nu_{N,t}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{N})=\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(x)\kappa_{N,t}(x){\rm d}x,

(1.36)

for any bounded and measurable
f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{C}
.
Later on, it will be convenient to interpret
(
1.33
) as an absolutely continuous measure on
\mathbb{R}^{N}
with respect to the deformed measure
(1-\sigma_{t}(x)){\rm d}x
instead of the Lebesgue measure. With respect to this deformed measure, the one-point function becomes

\widetilde{\kappa}_{N,t}(x_{1})=\frac{N}{Z_{N,t}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N-1}}\det\big(L_{N}(x_{m},x_{k})\big)_{m,k=1}^{N}\prod_{k=2}^{N}(1-\sigma_{t}(x_{k})){\rm d}x_{k}.

(1.37)

We have
\widetilde{\kappa}_{N,t}(x)=\kappa_{N,t}(x)/(1-\sigma_{t}(x))
if
\sigma_{t}(x)\neq 0
, but
\widetilde{\kappa}_{t}(x)
has the advantage that it is also defined when
\sigma_{t}(x)=1
.

Theorem 1.10
.

Let
W_{N},{\Sigma_{N}},\ell_{N}
satisfy Assumption
1.1
, let
\sigma:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{C}
be bounded and
C^{1}
with bounded derivative, and define
\sigma_{t}(x):=\sigma(x+t)
. Let
{\rm d}\mu_{N}
and
{\rm d}\nu_{N,t}
be defined by (
1.7
) and (
1.33
), and
\mu_{N}[\sigma]
defined by (
1.24
).
If
\mu_{N}[\sigma_{t}]\neq 0
, we have the identity

\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}\log\mu_{N}[\sigma_{t}]=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\sigma^{\prime}(x+t)\widetilde{\kappa}_{N,t}(x){\rm d}x.

(1.38)

If
\sigma(x)=\displaystyle\frac{1}{1+{\rm e}^{-x}}
, the above identity simplifies to

\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}\log\mu_{N}[\sigma]=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{1}{1+{\rm e}^{-x-t}}\kappa_{N,t}(x){\rm d}x.

(1.39)

Remark 1.11
.

We believe that the last identity (
1.39
) will be useful to study the asymptotic behavior of average multiplicative statistics
\mu_{N}[\sigma]
as
N\to\infty
. Via (
1.39
), these asymptotics are encoded simply in the one-point function
\kappa_{N,t}(x)
of the deformed biorthogonal measure. Even if the deformed biorthogonal measure is not particularly simple, there is a variety of techniques available to study one-point functions, like Coulomb gas techniques and characterizations via equilibrium problems. For specific choices of
W_{N}
, see Sections
5
,
6
and
7
, the above result implies that the one-point function
\kappa_{N,t}
encodes tail asymptotics and large deviations of polymer partition functions.
The condition that
\sigma
is
C^{1}
is convenient for the proof of (
1.38
), but it is not a necessary condition, and one can generalize the identity to piecewise
C^{1}
-functions. For instance, for
\sigma_{t}=1_{(-t,+\infty)}
, the identity (
1.38
) continues to hold if we replace
\sigma^{\prime}
by a Dirac
\delta
-function, such that

\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}\log\mu_{N}[1_{(-t,\infty)}]=\widetilde{\kappa}_{N,t}(-t).

(1.40)

2
Biorthogonal structure of the measures

We will suppose throughout this section that
W_{N},{\Sigma_{N}},\ell_{N}
satisfy Assumption
1.1
, and that
L_{N}
is defined by (
1.8
). The goal of this section is to prove Theorem
1.2
.

To start, we focus on the situation where the zeros
a_{1},\ldots,a_{N}
of
W_{N}
inside
{\Sigma_{N}}
are all distinct. Then, we prove the following.

Proposition 2.1
.

If
a_{1},\ldots,a_{N}
are all distinct, we have

L_{N}(x,x^{\prime})=\sum_{m=1}^{N}{\rm e}^{a_{m}x^{\prime}}\psi_{m}({\rm e}^{x}),

(2.1)

with
\psi_{m}
as in (
1.20
), and we have the orthogonality relation (
1.13
). Consequently,
L_{N}
has the reproducing property

\int_{\mathbb{R}}L_{N}(x,t)L_{N}(t,x^{\prime}){\rm d}t=L_{N}(x,x^{\prime}).

(2.2)

Proof.

We can evaluate the
u
-integral in (
1.8
) using the residue theorem, and in this way, we immediately obtain (
2.1
).

Next, since

\psi_{m}({\rm e}^{x})=\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}W_{N}^{\prime}(a_{m})}\int_{\ell_{N}}\frac{W_{N}(v){\rm e}^{-xv}}{v-a_{m}}{\rm d}v,

for any vertical line
\ell_{N}
(avoiding
a_{m}
) in the strip
\mathcal{S}_{N}=\{z\in\mathbb{C}:\alpha_{N}<\mathrm{Re}\,z<\beta_{N}\}
, and since
W_{N}(z)=O(|z|^{-\epsilon})
as
z\to\infty
,
it is easy to verify that, for any
\delta>0
,

\psi_{m}({\rm e}^{x})=O\left({\rm e}^{-(\alpha_{N}+\delta)x}\right)\quad\mbox{as $x\to-\infty$},\qquad\psi_{m}({\rm e}^{x})=O\left({\rm e}^{-(\beta_{N}-\delta)x}\right)\quad\mbox{as $x\to+\infty$}.

(2.3)

The integrals
\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}{\rm e}^{a_{k}x}\psi_{m}({\rm e}^{x}){\rm d}x
,
k=1,\ldots,N
are thus well-defined, since
a_{1},\ldots,a_{N}\in\mathcal{S}_{N}
.

To obtain the biorthogonality relations, recalling the direct and inverse Mellin transform from (
1.19
), we observe that

\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}{\rm e}^{a_{k}x}\psi_{m}({\rm e}^{x}){\rm d}x=\int_{0}^{\infty}s^{a_{k}-1}\psi_{m}(s){\rm d}s=\frac{1}{W_{N}^{\prime}(a_{k})}\left(\mathcal{M}\circ\mathcal{M}^{-1}\right)\left[\frac{W_{N}(\cdot)}{\cdot-a_{m}}\right](a_{k}).

Since
W_{N}
has simple zeros at the points
a_{1},\ldots,a_{N}
, the above expression is
0
for
k\neq m
, and to
1
for
k=m
.

Finally, it is straightforward to deduce the reproducing property (
2.2
) from (
2.1
) and (
1.13
).
∎

As a consequence of (
2.1
), using the multiplicativity of the determinant, we can rewrite
(
1.7
) as

\displaystyle{\rm d}\mu_{N}(\vec{x})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N!}\det\left(\sum_{j=1}^{N}{\rm e}^{a_{j}x_{k}}\psi_{j}({\rm e}^{x_{m}})\right)_{m,k=1}^{N}\prod_{k=1}^{N}{\rm d}x_{k}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N!}\det\left({\rm e}^{a_{j}x_{k}}\right)_{k,j=1}^{N}\det\left(\psi_{j}({\rm e}^{x_{m}})\right)_{j,m=1}^{N}\prod_{k=1}^{N}{\rm d}x_{k},

(2.4)

which is (
1.14
), such that part 2 of Theorem
1.2
is proved.

Proposition 2.2
.

For arbitrary
a_{1},\ldots,a_{N}
,
L_{N}
satisfies the reproducing property (
2.2
) and we have
\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}L_{N}(x,x){\rm d}x=N
.

Proof.

The reproducing property is already proved in the case where all
a_{1},\ldots,a_{N}
are distinct.
If not all
a_{1},\ldots,a_{N}
are distinct, write

W_{N}(z)=W_{0}(z)\prod_{m=1}^{N}(z-a_{m}).

For the sake of clarity, let us temporarily make explicit the dependence of
L_{N}
on the parameters
a_{1},\ldots,a_{N}
in our notations, and write

L_{N}(x,x^{\prime};\vec{a})\equiv L_{N}(x,x^{\prime})=\frac{1}{(2\pi\mathrm{i})^{2}}\int_{\Sigma_{N}}{\rm d}u\int_{\ell_{N}}{\rm d}v\frac{W_{0}(v)\prod_{m=1}^{N}(v-a_{m})}{W_{0}(u)\prod_{m=1}^{N}(u-a_{m})}\frac{{\rm e}^{-vx+ux^{\prime}}}{v-u}.

Given
W_{0}
and
{\Sigma_{N}}
, the kernel
L_{N}
is continuous in
\vec{a}
, as long as
a_{1},\ldots,a_{N}
remain inside
{\Sigma_{N}}
. We already know that
L_{N}
has the reproducing property if
a_{1},\ldots,a_{N}
are distinct:

\int_{\mathbb{R}}L_{N}(x,t;\vec{a})L_{N}(t,x^{\prime};\vec{a}){\rm d}t=L_{N}(x,x^{\prime};\vec{a}).

(2.5)

Both sides of this identity are continuous in
\vec{a}
, thus the identity continues to hold when
a_{1},\ldots,a_{N}
are not distinct.
Similarly, by the biorthogonality relation (
1.13
) and by (
2.1
), we have that

\int_{\mathbb{R}}L_{N}(x,x;\vec{a}){\rm d}x=N

for
a_{1},\ldots,a_{N}
distinct, and by continuity this continues to hold
in general.
∎

Our next result is a determinant expression for the marginals
\rho_{n,N}
defined in (
1.9
). It follows from a standard integrate-out trick, we record the proof here for the convenience of the reader, and because it is found in many classical references for Hermitian kernels only.

Proposition 2.3
.

The marginals
\rho_{n,N}
of the measure
\mu_{N}
defined by (
1.7
), satisfy the determinantal identity

\rho_{n,N}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})=\det\Big(L_{N}(x_{j},x_{k})\Big)_{j,k=1}^{n},\quad n=1,\ldots,N.

(2.6)

Proof.

We need to prove that

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N-n}}\det\Big(L_{N}(x_{j},x_{m})\Big)_{j,m=1}^{N}\mathrm{{\rm d}}x_{n+1}\cdots{\rm d}x_{N}=(N-n)!\det\Big(L_{N}(x_{j},x_{k})\Big)_{j,k=1}^{n},

(2.7)

for
n=1,\ldots,N-1
.
Let
k\in\{2,\ldots,N\}
, and write
M:=\Big(L_{N}(x_{i},x_{j})\Big)_{i,j=1}^{k}
.
In the rest of this proof, we will write
M^{a,b}
for the
(k-1)\times(k-1)
matrix obtained from
M
by removing the
a
-th line and the
b
-th column, and similarly

M^{a_{1}a_{2},b_{1}b_{2}}
for the
(k-2)\times(k-2)
matrix obtained from
M
by removing the two corresponding rows and columns.
We now expand
\det M
with respect to the last row, to obtain

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}\det M{\rm d}x_{k}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\sum_{j=1}^{k}(-1)^{j+k}L_{N}(x_{k},x_{j})\det M^{k,j}{\rm d}x_{k}

\displaystyle=\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}L_{N}(x_{k},x_{k}){\rm d}x_{k}\right)\det M^{k,k}+\int_{\mathbb{R}}\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}(-1)^{j+k}L_{N}(x_{k},x_{j})\det M^{k,j}{\rm d}x_{k}.

Observing that the integral in the first term is equal to
N
(recall (
1.10
)) and expanding
\det M^{k,j}
once more, now with respect to the last column, we find

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}\det M{\rm d}x_{k}

\displaystyle=N\det M^{k,k}+\int_{\mathbb{R}}\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}(-1)^{j+k}L_{N}(x_{k},x_{j})\sum_{m=1}^{k-1}(-1)^{m+k-1}L_{N}(x_{m},x_{k})\det M^{km,jk}{\rm d}x_{k}

\displaystyle=N\det M^{k,k}-\sum_{j,m=1}^{k-1}(-1)^{j+m}L_{N}(x_{m},x_{j})\det M^{km,jk}

(2.8)

\displaystyle=(N-k+1)\det M^{k,k},

(2.9)

where we used the reproducing property (
1.10
) in the second line, and where the last line is verified again by row or column expansion.
This implies that

\int_{\mathbb{R}}\det\left(L_{N}(x_{i},x_{j})\right)_{i,j=1}^{k}{\rm d}x_{k}=(N-k+1)\det\Big(L_{N}(x_{i},x_{j})\Big)_{i,j=1}^{k-1},

for every
k\in\{2,\ldots,N\}
. Repeatedly applying this identity for
k=N,N-1,\ldots,n+1
, we obtain (
2.7
).
∎

Propositions
2.2
and
2.3
together imply part 1 of Theorem
1.2
.

If
a_{1}=\cdots=a_{N}=a
, we
can still evaluate the
u
-integral in the double contour integral expression for
L_{N}
using the residue theorem:

L_{N}(x,x^{\prime})=\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\int_{\ell_{N}}{\rm Res}\left(\frac{{\rm e}^{ux^{\prime}}}{W_{0}(u)(u-a)^{N}(v-u)};u=a\right)W_{N}(v){\rm e}^{-vx}{\rm d}v.

The residue takes the form

{\rm e}^{ax^{\prime}}\sum_{j=1}^{N}c_{j}(v)x^{\prime j-1}
, with
c_{j}(v)=\frac{1}{v-a}P_{N-j}\left(\frac{1}{v-a}\right)
, for some polynomial
P_{N-j}
of degree
N-j
. It follows that

L_{N}(x,x^{\prime})={\rm e}^{ax^{\prime}}\sum_{j=1}^{N}x^{\prime j-1}\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\int_{\ell_{N}}W_{N}(v)c_{j}(v){\rm e}^{-vx}{\rm d}v.

Hence,
(
1.7
) is equal to

\frac{1}{N!}\det\left({\rm e}^{ax_{k}}\sum_{j=1}^{N}x_{k}^{j-1}\int_{\ell_{N}}W_{N}(v)c_{j}(v){\rm e}^{-vx_{m}}{\rm d}v\right)_{k,m=1}^{N}\prod_{k=1}^{N}{\rm d}x_{k}\\
=\frac{1}{N!}\det\left(x_{k}^{j-1}\right)_{k,j=1}^{N}\det\left(\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\int_{\ell_{N}}c_{j}(v)W_{N}(v){\rm e}^{-vx_{m}}{\rm d}v\right)_{j,m=1}^{N}\prod_{k=1}^{N}{\rm e}^{ax_{k}}{\rm d}x_{k}.

(2.10)

In the latter expression, we can replace
\displaystyle\int_{\ell_{N}}c_{j}(v)W_{N}(v){\rm e}^{-vx_{m}}{\rm d}v
,
j=1,\ldots,N
, by any set of functions with the same span, like for instance

\displaystyle\int_{\ell_{N}}\frac{(v-a)^{j-1}W_{N}(v)}{(v-a)^{N}}{\rm e}^{-vx_{m}}{\rm d}v
,
j=1,\ldots,N
, at the price of replacing the normalization constant
N!
by a different constant
Z_{N}
.
We then obtain that

\displaystyle{\rm d}\mu_{N}(\vec{x})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N!}\det\left(x_{k}^{j-1}\right)_{k,j=1}^{N}\det\left(\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\int_{\ell_{N}}c_{j}(v)W_{N}(v){\rm e}^{-vx_{k}}{\rm d}v\right)_{j,k=1}^{N}\prod_{k=1}^{N}{\rm e}^{ax_{k}}{\rm d}x_{k}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{Z_{N}}\det\left(x_{k}^{j-1}\right)_{k,j=1}^{N}\det\left(\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\int_{\ell_{N}}\frac{(v-a)^{j-1}W_{N}(v)}{(v-a)^{N}}{\rm e}^{-vx_{k}}{\rm d}v\right)_{j,k=1}^{N}\prod_{k=1}^{N}{\rm e}^{ax_{k}}{\rm d}x_{k}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{Z_{N}}\det\left(x_{k}^{j-1}\right)_{k,j=1}^{N}\det\left(\phi_{j}(x_{k})\right)_{j,k=1}^{N}\prod_{k=1}^{N}{\rm d}x_{k}

This proves part 3 of Theorem
1.2
.

In a similar way, one can obtain explicit expressions for the biorthogonal measures in cases where some, but not all, of the zeros

are confluent.

Determinant identities

In this section, we will prove the determinant identities from Theorem

Again, throughout the section, we assume that

3.1
Matrix determinants

The finite size determinants of part 1 of Theorem
1.7
are obtained after a straightforward application of Andréief’s identity.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
\sigma:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{C}
be bounded and measurable.

1.

If
a_{1},\ldots,a_{N}
are distinct, we have

\mu_{N}[\sigma]=\det\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}(1-\sigma(x)){\rm e}^{a_{m}x}\psi_{k}({\rm e}^{x}){\rm d}x\right)_{m,k=1}^{N}.

2.

If
a_{1},\ldots,a_{N}=a
, we have

\mu_{N}[\sigma]=\frac{\displaystyle\det\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}(1-\sigma(x))x^{m-1}\phi_{k}({\rm e}^{x}){\rm d}x\right)_{m,k=1}^{N}}{\displaystyle\det\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}x^{m-1}\phi_{k}({\rm e}^{x}){\rm d}x\right)_{m,k=1}^{N}}.

Proof.

Let
a_{1},\ldots,a_{N}
be distinct. From the expression (
1.14
) for
\mu_{N}
, we have

\mu_{N}[\sigma]=\frac{1}{N!}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\det\left({\rm e}^{a_{j}x_{k}}\right)_{k,j=1}^{N}\det\left(\psi_{j}({\rm e}^{x_{m}})\right)_{j,m=1}^{N}\prod_{j=1}^{N}\left(1-\sigma(x_{j})\right){\rm d}x_{j}.

We now apply Andréief’s identity on the right hand side and obtain the result.

If
a_{1}=\cdots=a_{N}=a
,
we have

\mu_{N}[\sigma]=\frac{1}{Z_{N}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\det\left(x_{j}^{k-1}\right)_{k,j=1}^{N}\det\left(\phi_{j}({\rm e}^{x_{m}})\right)_{j,m=1}^{N}\prod_{j=1}^{N}\left(1-\sigma(x_{j})\right){\rm d}x_{j},

with

Z_{N}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\det\left(x_{j}^{k-1}\right)_{k,j=1}^{N}\det\left(\phi_{j}({\rm e}^{x_{m}})\right)_{j,m=1}^{N}\prod_{j=1}^{N}{\rm d}x_{j}.

Applying Andréief’s identity to the above two expressions, we obtain the result.
∎

3.2
Fredholm determinants

The following lemma is another classical result concerning biorthogonal ensembles. Once again, we recall the proof for convenience, and to convince the reader that it does not require positivity of the biorthogonal measure.

Proposition 3.2
.

The average multiplicative statistics defined in (
1.24
) satisfy the identity

\mu_{N}[\sigma]=1+\sum_{n=1}^{N}\frac{(-1)^{n}}{n!}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\det\Big(\sigma(x_{j})L_{N}(x_{j},x_{m})\Big)_{j,m=1}^{n}{\rm d}x_{1}\cdots{\rm d}x_{n}.

(3.1)

Proof.

We expand the left hand side of the identity as

\displaystyle\mu_{N}[\sigma]

\displaystyle=\int\prod_{j=1}^{N}\left(1-\sigma(x_{j})\right){\rm d}\mu_{N}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{N})

\displaystyle=1+\sum_{n=1}^{N}(-1)^{n}\binom{N}{n}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\prod_{j=1}^{n}\sigma(x_{j}){\rm d}\mu_{N}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{N})

\displaystyle=1+\sum_{n=1}^{N}(-1)^{n}\frac{1}{n!(N-n)!}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\prod_{j=1}^{n}\sigma(x_{j})\det\Big(L_{N}(x_{k},x_{m})\Big)_{k,m=1}^{N}{\rm d}x_{1}\cdots{\rm d}x_{N}.

Integrating out the variables
x_{n+1},\ldots,x_{N}
, using Proposition
2.3
(or the equivalent identity (
2.7
)), we obtain that the latter is equal to

\displaystyle 1+\sum_{n=1}^{N}\frac{(-1)^{n}}{n!}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\det\left(\sigma(x_{j})L_{N}(x_{j},x_{m})\right)_{j,m=1}^{n}{\rm d}x_{1}\cdots{\rm d}x_{n},

(3.2)

∎

We recognize the right hand side of the identity (
3.1
) as the truncated Fredholm series
\det(1-\sigma L_{N})_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}
, and the identity (
1.30
) is proven.

To prove the other identities in Theorem
1.7
(points 3 and 4), we need to rely on properties of Fredholm determinants. For that reason, we will now construct trace-class operators associated to suitable conjugations of the kernels we considered before, such that we can interpret the truncated Fredholm series as proper Fredholm determinants associated to (finite rank) trace-class operators.

The kernel

L_{N}(x,x^{\prime})=\sum_{m=1}^{N}{\rm e}^{a_{m}x^{\prime}}\psi_{m}({\rm e}^{x})

does not directly define an integral operator on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
, because neither
{\rm e}^{a_{m}x^{\prime}}
nor
\psi_{m}({\rm e}^{x})
are (in general) in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
. We can however define a new kernel of the form

\widetilde{L}_{N}(x,x^{\prime}):=\frac{h(x)}{h(x^{\prime})}L_{N}(x,x^{\prime}),

(3.3)

such that the Fredhom series
\det(1-\sigma L_{N})_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}
and
\det(1-\sigma\widetilde{L}_{N})_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}
are equal by definition, but the integral operator
\widetilde{L}_{N}
defined by

\widetilde{L}_{N}[f](x):=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\widetilde{L}_{N}(x,x^{\prime})f(x^{\prime}){\rm d}x^{\prime},

(3.4)

is a genuine integral operator acting on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
, of rank
N
and in particular trace-class.
A suitable choice for
h
turns out to be

h(x)=\begin{cases}{\rm e}^{(a_{\max}+\epsilon)x},&x\geq 0,\\
{\rm e}^{(a_{\min}-\epsilon)x},&x<0,\end{cases}

(3.5)

for sufficiently small
\epsilon>0
, with
a_{\min}
and
a_{\max}
as in (
1.28
). Then, the asymptotic behavior (
2.3
) for
\psi({\rm e}^{x})
as
x\to\pm\infty
implies indeed that
\widetilde{L}_{N}
is a bounded linear operator on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
.
Similarly, we define for
H_{N}^{\sigma}
from (
1.26
)
the conjugated kernel

\widetilde{H}_{N}^{\sigma}(y,y^{\prime}):={\rm e}^{-(a_{\max}+\epsilon)(y-y^{\prime})}H_{N}^{\sigma}(y,y^{\prime}),

(3.6)

and the associated integral operator

{\widetilde{H}_{N}^{\sigma}}[f](y)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\widetilde{H}_{N}^{\sigma}(y,y^{\prime})f(y^{\prime}){\rm d}y^{\prime},

acting on
L^{2}(0,\infty)
.

Proposition 3.3
.

Let
W_{N}
satisfy Assumption
1.1
and be such that

W_{N}(v)=O(|v|^{-1-\epsilon})
as
v\to\infty
in
\mathcal{S}_{N}
.
Let
\sigma:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{C}
be bounded and measurable, and such that there exists
\epsilon>0
such that

\sigma(x)=O\left({\rm e}^{(a_{\max}-a_{\min}+\epsilon)x}\right),\qquad x\to-\infty.

The operators
{\widetilde{H}_{N}^{\sigma}}
and
\sigma\widetilde{L}_{N}
are trace-class, and we have the identity

\det(1-{\widetilde{H}_{N}^{\sigma}})_{L^{2}(0,\infty)}=\det(1-\sigma\widetilde{L}_{N})_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}.

(3.7)

Proof.

Given
\Psi_{1}
and
\Psi_{2}
as in (
1.27
), define two operators

{A:L^{2}(\mathbb{R})\to L^{2}(0,\infty)\quad\text{and}\quad B:L^{2}(0,\infty)\to L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}

with kernels

A(y,x):=\frac{{\rm e}^{-(a_{\max}+\epsilon)y}}{h(x)}\Psi_{1}\left({\rm e}^{y+x}\right),\quad B(x,y^{\prime})={\rm e}^{(a_{\max}+\epsilon)y^{\prime}}h(x)\sigma(x)\Psi_{2}\left({\rm e}^{y^{\prime}+x}\right).

Observe also that
\Psi_{2}
is well-defined since
W_{N}(v)=O(|v|^{-1-\epsilon})
as
v\to\infty
in
\mathcal{S}_{N}
.
Using Assumption
1.1
and the definition (
1.27
) of
\Psi_{1}
, it is straightforward to check by deforming
\Sigma_{N}
to a sufficiently narrow curve encircling
a_{1},\ldots,a_{N}
that, for any
\epsilon>0
,

\Psi_{1}({\rm e}^{y+x})=O\left({\rm e}^{(a_{\min}-\epsilon/2)(y+x)}\right)\ \mbox{as $y+x\to-\infty$,}\quad\Psi_{1}({\rm e}^{y+x})=O\left({\rm e}^{(a_{\max}+\epsilon/2)(y+x)}\right)\ \mbox{as $y+x\to+\infty$},

such that

A(y,x)=O\left({\rm e}^{-\epsilon|x+y|/2}\right),\qquad x+y\to\pm\infty.

It is now straightforward to check that
{A}
is a Hilbert-Schmidt operator.

Similarly, for sufficiently small
\epsilon
, we can deform
\ell_{N}
to the vertical line with real part

, and hence

\Psi_{2}({\rm e}^{y+x})=O\left({\rm e}^{-(a_{\max}+\frac{3}{2}\epsilon)(y+x)}\right)\quad\mbox{as $y+x\to\pm\infty$},

such that

which implies that

As compositions of Hilbert-Schmidt operators,

\det(1-AB)_{L^{2}(0,\infty)}=\det(1-BA)_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}.

(3.8)

It is easy to see that
\widetilde{H}_{N}^{\sigma}=AB
by comparing the kernels of both operators, and we will now prove that
\sigma\widetilde{L}_{N}=BA
, which implies the result.

Using the integral representation of
\Psi_{1}
and
\Psi_{2}
from (
1.27
), we compute the kernel of the operator

acting on

as follows:

\displaystyle(BA)(x,x^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(2\pi\mathrm{i})^{2}}\sigma(x)\frac{h(x)}{h(x^{\prime})}\int_{0}^{\infty}{\rm d}y\int_{{\Sigma_{N}}}{\rm d}u\frac{{\rm e}^{(y+x^{\prime})u}}{W_{N}(u)}\int_{\ell_{N}}{\rm d}vW_{N}(v){\rm e}^{-(y+x)v}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(2\pi\mathrm{i})^{2}}\sigma(x)\frac{h(x)}{h(x^{\prime})}\int_{{\Sigma_{N}}}{\rm d}u\frac{{\rm e}^{x^{\prime}u}}{W_{N}(u)}\int_{\ell_{N}}{\rm d}vW_{N}(v){\rm e}^{-xv}\int_{0}^{\infty}{\rm d}y{\rm e}^{y(u-v)}

\displaystyle=\sigma(x)\frac{h(x)}{h(x^{\prime})}L_{N}(x,x^{\prime})=\sigma(x)\widetilde{L}_{N}(x,x^{\prime}),

such that indeed

.
∎

Remark 3.4
.

If
\sigma

\displaystyle H_{N}^{\sigma}(y,y^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}{\rm d}r\sigma(r)\int_{{\Sigma_{N}}}\frac{{\rm d}u}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\frac{1}{W_{N}(u)}{\rm e}^{(y+r)u}\int_{\ell_{N}}\frac{{\rm d}v}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}W_{N}(v){\rm e}^{-(y^{\prime}+r)v}

We constructed the Hilbert-Schmidt operators

Proposition 3.5
.

Let
W_{N}

Let

, and let
\sigma_{t}(x)=\frac{1}{1+{\rm e}^{-x-t}}

If

,
we have the identity

{\det\left(1-\widetilde{H}_{N}^{\sigma_{t}}\right)_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}=\det\left(1+K_{N,t}\right)_{L^{2}({\Sigma_{N}})}},

(3.9)

with
K_{N,t}
the integral operator acting on
L^{2}(\Sigma_{N})
with kernel
K_{N,t}
defined in (

Proof.

Using the fact that
\frac{1}{v-u^{\prime}}=\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}{\rm d}y\,{\rm e}^{-y(v-u^{\prime})}

from (

It is then straightforward to verify that

4
Logarithmic derivatives and deformed biorthogonal measures

In this section, we will prove Theorem
1.10
. The proof of this result is essentially a special case of a more general result in
[
26
]
. Besides Assumption
1.1
, we assume here that
\sigma:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{C}
is
C^{1}
, bounded, and with bounded derivative, and that
t
is such that

\mu_{N}[\sigma_{t}]=\det(1-\sigma_{t}L_{N})_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}=\det(1-\sigma_{t}\widetilde{L}_{N})_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}\neq 0,

with
\sigma_{t}(x)=\sigma(x+t)
.

For simplicity, we will first assume that
a_{1},\ldots,a_{N}
are distinct. Later, we will use a continuity argument to prove our identities in general.
By Proposition
2.1
, we know that
\widetilde{L}_{N}
can be written in the form

\widetilde{L}_{N}(x;x^{\prime})=\sum_{k=1}^{N}F_{k}(x)G_{k}(x^{\prime}),\qquad\int_{\mathbb{R}}F_{k}(x)G_{j}(x){\rm d}x=\delta_{jk},\quad\forall\,j,k=1,\ldots,N,

(4.1)

with
F_{k}(x)=h(x)\psi_{k}({\rm e}^{x})
and
G_{k}(x)={\rm e}^{a_{k}x}/h(x)
.
The asymptotic behavior (
2.3
) of
\psi_{k}
and the definition (
3.5
) imply that

F_{k}(x)
and
G_{k}(x)
decay rapidly as
x\to\pm\infty
. Consequently, the operator
\widetilde{L}_{N}
is a bounded linear operator, which is moreover finite rank and thus trace-class.
By the Jacobi identity, we have

\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}\log\mu_{N}[\sigma_{t}]=\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}\log\det(1-\sigma_{t}\widetilde{L}_{N})_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}=-\mathrm{Tr}\,\left(\left(\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}\sigma_{t}\right)\widetilde{L}_{N}(1-\sigma_{t}\widetilde{L}_{N})^{-1}\right)_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})},

(4.2)

where we observe that
(\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}\sigma_{t})\widetilde{L}_{N}(1-\sigma_{t}\widetilde{L}_{N})^{-1}=\sigma_{t}^{\prime}\widetilde{L}_{N}(1-\sigma_{t}\widetilde{L}_{N})^{-1}
is a trace-class operator if
\sigma_{t}^{\prime}
is bounded.
We thus have

\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}\log\mu_{N}[\sigma_{t}]=-\mathrm{Tr}\,\left(\sigma_{t}^{\prime}\widetilde{L}_{N}(1-\sigma_{t}\widetilde{L}_{N})^{-1}\right)_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}.

(4.3)

By definition (
3.4
) and by Proposition
2.2
, we directly verify that
\widetilde{L}_{N}^{2}=\widetilde{L}_{N}
, such that
\widetilde{L}_{N}
is a rank
N
(not necessarily Hermitian) projection operator.
We denote
\mathcal{F}_{N}
for the linear span of
F_{1},\ldots,F_{N}
and
\mathcal{G}_{N}
for the linear span of
G_{1},\ldots,G_{N}
.

Proposition 4.1
.

\widetilde{L}_{N}
is the unique projection operator on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
with range
\mathcal{F}_{N}
and kernel
\mathcal{G}_{N}^{\perp}
.

Proof.

It is straightforward to verify from (
3.3
)–(
3.4
) and (
4.1
)
that
\widetilde{L}_{N}[f]=f
for
f\in\mathcal{F}_{N}
and that
\widetilde{L}_{N}[f]=0
for
f\in\mathcal{G}_{N}^{\perp}
.
Moreover,
\mathcal{F}_{N}\oplus\mathcal{G}_{N}^{\perp}=L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
. Indeed, suppose that this is not the case; then there exists a non-zero
f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
such that
f\perp h
for every
h\in\mathcal{F}_{N}\oplus\mathcal{G}_{N}^{\perp}
.
Consequently,
f\in\mathcal{F}_{N}^{\perp}\cap\mathcal{G}_{N}
. but

\mathcal{F}_{N}^{\perp}\cap\mathcal{G}_{N}=\{0\}
,
by the orthogonality relation
\int_{\mathbb{R}}F_{j}(x)G_{k}(x){\rm d}x=\delta_{jk}
, which gives a contradiction.
Using the above properties, we obtain that
\widetilde{L}_{N}
is the unique projection with range
\mathcal{F}_{N}
and kernel
\mathcal{G}_{N}^{\perp}
.
∎

Let us now consider a kernel
M_{t}(x,x^{\prime})
of the operator
\widetilde{L}_{N}(1-\sigma_{t}\widetilde{L}_{N})^{-1}
acting on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
, such that

\widetilde{L}_{N}(1-\sigma_{t}\widetilde{L}_{N})^{-1}[f](x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}M_{t}(x,x^{\prime})f(x^{\prime}){\rm d}x^{\prime}.

Then we define an operator
\mathcal{P}_{t}
acting on
L^{2}\left(\mathbb{R},(1-\sigma_{t}(x)){\rm d}x\right)
, with respect to the deformed Lebesgue measure
(1-\sigma_{t}(x)){\rm d}x
, as follows:

\mathcal{P}_{t}[g](x)=\widetilde{L}_{N}(1-\sigma_{t}\widetilde{L}_{N})^{-1}[(1-\sigma_{t})g]=\int_{\mathbb{R}}M_{t}(x,x^{\prime})g(x^{\prime})(1-\sigma_{t}(x^{\prime})){\rm d}x^{\prime}.

(4.4)

Proposition 4.2
.

The operator

\mathcal{P}_{t}

is the unique projection operator on
L^{2}\left((1-\sigma_{t}(x)){\rm d}x\right)
with range
\mathcal{F}_{N}
and kernel
\mathcal{G}_{N}^{\perp}
.

Proof.

Note first that the orthogonal complement in
\mathcal{G}_{N}^{\perp}
here stands for orthogonality with respect to the deformed Lebesgue measure
(1-\sigma_{t}(x)){\rm d}x
.

Let us first prove that
\mathcal{F}_{N}\oplus\mathcal{G}_{N}^{\perp}=L^{2}\left((1-\sigma_{t}(x)){\rm d}x\right)
.
Similarly as before, suppose that this is not true, then there is a non-zero
g\in L^{2}\left(\mathbb{R},(1-\sigma_{t}(x)){\rm d}x\right)
in the orthogonal complement of

\mathcal{F}_{N}\oplus\mathcal{G}_{N}^{\perp}
. This means that
g\in\mathcal{F}_{N}^{\perp}\cap\mathcal{G}_{N}
.
In other words,
f=g(1-\sigma_{t})\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R},{\rm d}x)
belongs to the
L^{2}(\mathbb{R},{\rm d}x)
-orthogonal complement of
\mathcal{F}_{N}
, which has trivial intersection with
\mathcal{G}_{N}
. This gives a contradiction, and we conclude that
\mathcal{F}_{N}\oplus\mathcal{G}_{N}^{\perp}=L^{2}\left((1-\sigma_{t}(x)){\rm d}x\right)
.

Let
g\in\mathcal{F}_{N}
, such that
(1-\sigma_{t})g=(1-\sigma_{t}\widetilde{L}_{N})g
.
Then,

\mathcal{P}_{t}[g]=\widetilde{L}_{N}(1-\sigma_{t}\widetilde{L}_{N})^{-1}[(1-\sigma_{t})g]=\widetilde{L}_{N}[g]=g.

Finally, let
g\in\mathcal{G}_{N}^{\perp}
, such that
\widetilde{L}_{N}[(1-\sigma_{t})g]=0
and
(1-\sigma_{t})g=\left(1-\sigma_{t}\widetilde{L}_{N}\right)[(1-\sigma_{t})g]
.
Thus,

\mathcal{P}_{t}[g]=\widetilde{L}_{N}[(1-\sigma_{t})g]=0.

Combining the above properties, we obtain the result.
∎

Recall from (
4.4
) that the
L^{2}\left(\mathbb{R},(1-\sigma_{t}(x)){\rm d}x\right)
-operator
\mathcal{P}_{t}
has integral kernel
M_{t}(x,x^{\prime})
, i.e.

\mathcal{P}_{t}[g]=\widetilde{L}_{N}(1-\sigma_{t}\widetilde{L}_{N})^{-1}[(1-\sigma_{t})g]=\int_{\mathbb{R}}M_{t}(x,x^{\prime})g(x^{\prime})(1-\sigma_{t}(x^{\prime})){\rm d}x,

and we can take
M_{t}
of the form

M_{t}(x,x^{\prime})=\sum_{m=1}^{N}\widetilde{F}_{m}(x)\widetilde{G}_{m}(x^{\prime}),\quad\text{with}\quad\int_{\mathbb{R}}\widetilde{F}_{j}(x)\widetilde{G}_{k}(x)(1-\sigma_{t}(x)){\rm d}x=\delta_{jk},\quad j,k=1,\ldots,N,

where
\widetilde{F}_{1},\ldots,\widetilde{F}_{N}
span the range
\mathcal{F}_{N}
of
\widetilde{L}_{N}
, and
\widetilde{G}_{1},\ldots,\widetilde{G}_{N}
span
\mathcal{G}_{N}
, since
\mathcal{G}_{N}^{\perp}
(in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R},(1-\sigma_{t}(x)){\rm d}x)
) is the kernel of
\mathcal{P}_{t}
.
The biorthogonal measure

{\rm d}\widetilde{\nu}_{N,t}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{N}):=\frac{1}{N!}\det\left(M_{t}(x_{j},x_{k})\right)_{j,k=1}^{N}\prod_{k=1}^{N}(1-\sigma_{t}(x_{k})){\rm d}x_{k}

can then be rewritten as

\displaystyle{\rm d}\widetilde{\nu}_{N,t}(\vec{x})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N!}\det\left(\sum_{m=1}^{N}\widetilde{F}_{m}(x_{j})\widetilde{G}_{m}(x_{k})\right)_{j,k=1}^{N}\prod_{k=1}^{N}(1-\sigma_{t}(x_{k}){\rm d}x_{k}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N!}\det\left(\widetilde{F}_{m}(x_{j})\right)_{j,m=1}^{N}\det\left(\widetilde{G}_{m}(x_{k})\right)_{m,k=1}^{N}\prod_{k=1}^{N}(1-\sigma_{t}(x_{k})){\rm d}x_{k}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{Z_{N,t}}\det\left(F_{m}(x_{j})\right)_{j,m=1}^{N}\det\left(G_{m}(x_{k})\right)_{m,k=1}^{N}\prod_{k=1}^{N}(1-\sigma_{t}(x_{k})){\rm d}x_{k}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N!}\det\left(\sum_{m=1}^{N}F_{m}(x_{j})G_{m}(x_{k})\right)_{j,k=1}^{N}\prod_{k=1}^{N}(1-\sigma_{t}(x_{k})){\rm d}x_{k}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{Z_{N,t}}\det\left(L_{N}(x_{j},x_{k})\right)_{j,k=1}^{N}\prod_{k=1}^{N}(1-\sigma_{t}(x_{k})){\rm d}x_{k}={\rm d}\nu_{N,t}(\vec{x}).

We can now conclude from (
1.38
) that

\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}\log\mu_{N}[\sigma_{t}]=-\mathrm{Tr}\,\left(\sigma_{t}^{\prime}\widetilde{L}_{N}(1-\sigma_{t}\widetilde{L}_{N})^{-1}\right)_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\sigma_{t}^{\prime}(x)M_{t}(x,x){\rm d}x,

and recalling (
1.35
) and (
1.37
), we moreover have

M_{t}(x,x)=\widetilde{\kappa}_{N,t}(x)
, and
M_{t}(x,x)=\frac{\kappa_{N,t}(x)}{1-\sigma_{t}(x)}
if
\sigma_{t}(x)\neq 1
. The above identities imply Theorem
1.10
in the case of distinct
a_{1},\ldots,a_{N}
, and the general identities follow as before by continuity.

Log Gamma polymer partition function and LUE with external source

In the last three sections of this paper, we give an overview of concrete polymer and random matrix models which fit in our general framework, and to which the results presented in Section
1
can be applied. A concise table with a (probably non-exhaustive) list of models which can be described in terms of our general class of biorthogonal measures is given at the end, see Table
1
.

The model.

The Log Gamma polymer was introduced by Seppäläinen in
[
56
]
.
Let
n,N>1
be two positive integers.
We consider the lattice

\Big\{(j,k):j\in\{1,2,\ldots,n\},k\in\{1,2,\ldots,N\}\Big\},

and assign to each point
(j,k)
in the lattice independently a random variable
d_{j,k}\sim\Gamma^{-1}(\gamma_{j,k})
with inverse Gamma distribution,

\mathbb{P}(d_{j,k}\leq y)=\frac{1}{\Gamma(\gamma_{j,k})}\int_{0}^{y}x^{-\gamma_{j,k}-1}{\rm e}^{-1/x}{\rm d}x.

The mean of
d_{j,k}
thus exists only for
\gamma_{j,k}>1
and is then equal to
\frac{1}{\gamma_{j,k}-1}
, while the variance is finite only if
\gamma_{j,k}>2
, and then equal to
\frac{1}{(\gamma_{j,k}-1)^{2}(\gamma_{j,k}-2)}
.
The parameters
\gamma_{j,k}
take the form

\gamma_{j,k}=\alpha_{j}-a_{k}
, where

\vec{\alpha}=(\alpha_{j})_{j=1}^{n}
and
\vec{a}=(a_{k})_{k=1}^{N}
are two vectors of parameters such that
\alpha_{j}-a_{k}>0
for all
j=1,\ldots,n
and
k=1,\ldots,N
.

Figure 1:
The Log Gamma polymer lattice for
n=9
,
N=6
, with a possible up-right path. The values
\alpha_{j},a_{k}
determine the weight of the point
(j,k)
in an up-right path.

The Log Gamma polymer partition function
Z_{n,N}^{{\rm Log}\Gamma}({\vec{\alpha},\vec{a}})
is the random variable

Z_{n,N}^{{\rm Log}\Gamma}(\vec{\alpha},\vec{a}):=\sum_{\pi:(1,1)\nearrow(n,N)}\prod_{(j,k)\in\pi}d_{j,k},

(5.1)

where the sum is taken over all up-right directed lattice paths
\pi
from
(1,1)
to
(n,N)
, and the product over all lattice points in an up-right directed lattice path. See Figure
1
.

Heuristically, given the fact that the mean and variance of the random variables
d_{j,k}
are small for
\gamma_{j,k}
large, we can expect that for large values of
\gamma_{j,k}
, the weights
\prod_{(j,k)\in\pi}d_{j,k}
of different paths
\pi
will then typically be small and not show large deviations, such that many different paths will contribute to the leading order of the partition function (large entropy). For
\gamma_{j,k}
small, we expect on the other hand large fluctuations, and then we expect that the partition function will be dominated by few paths with large weights (small entropy), such that the model degenerates to a last passage percolation or corner growth model. See
[
56
,
17
]
for more details about this model.

Fredholm determinant identity.

Suppose that
n\geq N
and that there exist constants
\delta_{1},\delta_{2}\in(0,1)
such that
\delta_{1}<\min\{\delta_{2},1-\delta_{2}\}<1/2,
and such that

0\leq a_{j}<\delta_{1},\quad\alpha_{k}>\delta_{2},\quad\forall j=1,\ldots,N,\;\forall k=1,\ldots,n.

(5.2)

Under these assumptions,
Borodin, Corwin, and Remenik in
[
17
, Corollary 1.8]
proved the identity

\mathbb{E}\left[{\rm e}^{-{\rm e}^{t}Z_{n,N}^{{\rm Log}\Gamma}({\vec{\alpha}},{\vec{a}})}\right]=\det(1+K_{N,t})_{L^{2}({\Sigma_{N}})},

(5.3)

where
K_{N,t}
(now also depending on
n,\vec{\alpha},{\vec{a}}
) is as in (
1.29
), with
{\Sigma_{N}}
the circle of radius
\delta_{1}
around the origin,
\ell_{N}
the vertical line
\mathrm{Re}\,z=\delta_{2}
, and
W_{N}
given by

W_{N}(z)=W_{n,N}^{{\rm Log}\,\Gamma}(z;\vec{\alpha},\vec{a}):=\frac{\prod_{j=1}^{n}\Gamma(\alpha_{j}-z)}{\prod_{k=1}^{N}\Gamma(z-a_{k})}.

(5.4)

Assumption
1.1
is valid for this choice of
W_{N}
if
n>N
, and also for
n=N
if
\sum_{k=1}^{N}(\alpha_{j}+a_{j}-2\delta_{2})<0
. The stronger decay assumption for
W_{N}
needed in parts 3 and 4 of Theorem
1.7
is also valid if

n>N
, and if
n=N
provided that
\sum_{k=1}^{N}(\alpha_{j}+a_{j}-2\delta_{2})<-1
.
This is easily seen by using the following variant of Stirling’s approximation (see, e.g.,
[
31
, formula (5.11.9)]
),

\Gamma\left(x+\mathrm{i}y\right)\sim\sqrt{2\pi}|y|^{x-\frac{1}{2}}{\rm e}^{-\frac{\pi}{2}|y|},\qquad x,y\in\mathbb{R},\ y\to\pm\infty.

(5.5)

The homogenenous Log Gamma polymer occurs when
\alpha_{k}=\alpha>0
for
k=1,\ldots,n
and
a_{m}=0
for
m=1,\ldots,N
, and is of particular interest. Observe that this case meets the assumptions also when
n=N
and
\alpha<2
, since we can then take
\delta_{2}>\alpha/2
. The stronger decay needed in parts 3 and 4 of Theorem
1.7
is also valid for
\alpha<2
if
n=N
is sufficiently large.

Remark 5.1
.

The analogue of the kernel
K_{N,t}
in
[
17
]
was defined without squaring
2\pi\mathrm{i}
in the denominator, but this is due to an extra factor
\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}
in the definition of the scalar product on
L^{2}({\Sigma_{N}})
there.

Associated biorthogonal measures.

As anticipated in the introduction, Remark
1.9
, combining the Fredholm determinant identity (
5.3
) with Theorem
1.7
, part 4, we can relate the partition function of the Log Gamma polymer to an explicit biorthogonal measure. In the following corollary and below, given
b\in\mathbb{C}
,
[b]_{k}
will denote a
k
-dimensional vector with
b
in each entry and
\vec{e}_{m}
will denote the
N
-dimensional vector containing
1
in position
m
and
0
elsewhere. We also recall the definition of Meijer
G
-function (see for instance
[
31
]
)

G^{\,m,n}_{p,q}\left\lparen\begin{smallmatrix}a_{1},\ldots,a_{p}\\
b_{1},\ldots,b_{q}\end{smallmatrix}\middle|\,z\right\rparen:=\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\int_{L}\frac{\prod_{\ell=1}^{m}\Gamma(b_{\ell}-v)\prod_{\ell=1}^{n}\Gamma(1-a_{\ell}+v)}{\prod_{\ell=m}^{q-1}\Gamma(1-b_{\ell+1}+v)\prod_{\ell=n}^{p-1}\Gamma(a_{\ell+1}-v)}z^{v}{\rm d}v.

The choice of the contour
L
depends on the positions of the parameters
\{a_{i},\,i=1,\ldots,p\}
and
\{b_{j},\,j=1,\ldots,q\}
. For our specific cases below, we can choose it as the vertical line
\ell_{N}
.

Corollary 5.2
.

Consider two sets of parameters
\vec{\alpha}=\left(\alpha_{j}\right)_{j=1}^{n}
and
\vec{a}=\left(a_{k}\right)_{k=1}^{N}
with
n\geq N
and such that
\alpha_{j}-a_{k}>0
for all
j=1,\ldots,n
and
k=1,\ldots,N
. Suppose moreover that there exist two positive constants
\delta_{1},\delta_{2}
such that
\delta_{1}<\min\{\delta_{2},1-\delta_{2}\}<1/2
and such that equations (
5.2
) are satisfied. In the case where
n=N
we assume in addition that
\sum_{k=1}^{N}(\alpha_{j}+a_{j}-2\delta_{2})<-1
. Let
W_{N}
be as in (
5.4
),
\Sigma_{N}
the circle of radius
\delta_{1}
around the origin, and
\ell_{N}
the vertical line
\mathrm{Re}z=\delta_{2}
.
Then:

1.

\mathbb{E}\left[{\rm e}^{-{\rm e}^{t}Z_{n,N}^{{\rm Log}\Gamma}({\vec{\alpha}},{\vec{a}})}\right]=\mu_{N}[\sigma_{t}],\qquad\sigma_{t}(x)=\frac{1}{1+{\rm e}^{-x-t}},

(5.6)

where the measure
\mu_{N}
is given by equations (
1.7
)–(
1.8
).

2.

\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}\log\mathbb{E}\left[{\rm e}^{-{\rm e}^{t}Z_{n,N}^{{\rm Log}\Gamma}({\vec{\alpha}},{\vec{a}})}\right]=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\sigma_{t}(x)\kappa_{N,t}(x){\rm d}x,

(5.7)

where
\kappa_{N,t}
is the one-point function (
1.35
) of the deformed biorthogonal measure (
1.33
).

3.

In the case where the parameters
\vec{a}
are all distinct, the biorthogonal measure is given explicitly, in terms of Meijer
G
-function, by

{\rm d}\mu_{n,N}^{{\rm Log}\Gamma}(\vec{x};\vec{\alpha},\vec{a})=\frac{1}{Z_{N}}\det\left({\rm e}^{a_{m}x_{k}}\right)_{k,m=1}^{N}\det\left(G^{\,n,0}_{0,n+N}\left\lparen\begin{smallmatrix}-\\
{\vec{\alpha}};[1]_{m}+{\vec{a}}-\vec{e}_{m}\end{smallmatrix}\middle|\,{\rm e}^{-x_{k}}\right\rparen\right)_{m,k=1}^{N}\prod_{k=1}^{N}{\rm d}x_{k}.

(5.8)

In the confluent case
a_{1}=\cdots=a_{N}=0
, the biorthogonal measure is given by

{\rm d}\mu_{n,N}^{{\rm Log}\Gamma}(\vec{x};\vec{\alpha},\vec{0})=\frac{1}{Z_{N}}\Delta(\vec{x})\det\left(G^{\,n,0}_{0,n+N}\left\lparen\begin{smallmatrix}-\\
{\vec{\alpha}};[0]_{N-m+1};[1]_{m-1}\end{smallmatrix}\middle|\,{\rm e}^{-x_{k}}\right\rparen\right)_{m,k=1}^{N}\prod_{k=1}^{N}{\rm d}x_{k}.

(5.9)

Proof.

The first part is a direct consequence of the Fredholm determinant identity (
5.3
) and Theorem
1.7
, while the second part is a corollary of Theorem
1.10
. As for the third part, one can compute explicitly the functions
\psi_{m}
from (
1.12
) as

\displaystyle\psi_{m}(y)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}\left(W_{n,N}^{{\rm Log}\Gamma}\right)^{\prime}(a_{m};\vec{\alpha},\vec{a})}\int_{\ell_{N}}\frac{\prod_{j=1}^{n}\Gamma(\alpha_{j}-v)\ y^{-v}}{(v-a_{m})\prod_{j=1}^{N}\Gamma(v-a_{j})}{\rm d}v

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}\left(W_{n,N}^{{\rm Log}\Gamma}\right)^{\prime}(a_{m};\vec{\alpha},\vec{a})}\int_{\ell_{N}}\frac{\prod_{j=1}^{n}\Gamma(\alpha_{j}-v)\ y^{-v}}{\Gamma(v-a_{m}+1)\prod_{j\neq m}\Gamma(v-a_{j})}{\rm d}v,

and we recognize the right hand side as a Meijer
G
-function
[
31
]
:

\psi_{m}(y)=\frac{1}{\left(W_{n,N}^{{\rm Log}\Gamma}\right)^{\prime}(a_{m};\vec{\alpha},\vec{a})}G^{\,n,0}_{0,n+N}\left\lparen\begin{smallmatrix}-\\
{\vec{\alpha}};[1]_{N}+{\vec{a}}-\vec{e}_{m}\end{smallmatrix}\middle|\,1/y\right\rparen.

(5.10)

In the confluent case where
a_{1}=\cdots=a_{N}=0
, we have similarly that the functions
\phi_{m}
in (
1.17
) are given by

\displaystyle\phi_{m}(y)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\int_{\ell_{N}}\frac{\prod_{j=1}^{n}\Gamma(\alpha_{j}-v)}{\Gamma(v)^{N}}\frac{y^{-v}}{v^{N-m+1}}{\rm d}v

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\int_{\ell_{N}}\frac{\prod_{j=1}^{n}\Gamma(\alpha_{j}-v)}{\Gamma(v)^{m-1}\Gamma(v+1)^{N-m+1}}{y^{-v}}{\rm d}v

\displaystyle=G^{\,n,0}_{0,n+N}\left\lparen\begin{smallmatrix}-\\
{\vec{\alpha}};[0]_{N-m+1};[1]_{m-1}\end{smallmatrix}\middle|\,1/y\right\rparen.

∎

Remark 5.3
.

It is easy to see, using the definition of Meijer
G
-functions, that when
W_{N}
is given by (
5.4
), the associated functions
\{\psi_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
and
\{\phi_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
, defined by (
1.12
) and (
1.17
), are real valued:

\psi_{i}({\rm e}^{\bar{x}})=\overline{\psi_{i}({\rm e}^{x})},\quad\phi_{i}({\rm e}^{\bar{x}})=\overline{\phi_{i}({\rm e}^{x})},\quad i=1,\ldots,N.

Consequently,
d\mu_{n,N}^{{\rm Log}\Gamma}
is a signed real-valued measure.

LUE with external source.

Let
B
be a deterministic positive-definite Hermitian
N\times N
matrix with eigenvalues
b_{1},\ldots,b_{N}\in[0,1)
, and consider
the probability distribution

\frac{1}{Z_{N}^{\prime}}(\det M)^{\nu}{\rm e}^{-{\rm Tr}((I-B)M)}{\rm d}M,\qquad\nu>0,\quad{\rm d}M=\prod_{j=1}^{N}{\rm d}M_{jj}\ \prod_{1\leq j<k\leq N}{\rm d}\mathrm{Re}\,M_{jk}\,{\rm d}\mathrm{Im}\,M_{jk},

on the space of
N\times N
positive-definite Hermitian matrices. This model is known as the chiral Unitary Ensemble or LUE with external source and is connected to sample covariance matrices, see e.g.
[
4
,
29
,
32
,
33
]
.
For
B=0
, this is the classical LUE or Wishart-Laguerre ensemble.

The eigenvalues of
M
have the joint probability distribution

\frac{1}{Z_{N}}\Delta(\vec{x})\ \det\left(x_{j}^{\nu}{\rm e}^{-(1-b_{k})x_{j}}\right)_{j,k=1}^{N}\prod_{j=1}^{N}{\rm d}x_{j},\qquad x_{1},\ldots,x_{N}>0.

(5.11)

This is a determinantal point process with kernel (see
[
29
, Formula (4.2)]
or
[
33
, Formula (5.158)]
)

L_{N}^{\rm LUE+}(x,x^{\prime};\vec{b},\nu):=\frac{{\rm e}^{x^{\prime}-x}}{(2\pi\mathrm{i})^{2}}\int_{\gamma_{1}}{\rm d}u\int_{\gamma_{2}}{\rm d}v\left(\frac{u}{v}\right)^{N+\nu}\prod_{j=1}^{N}\frac{v-1+b_{j}}{u-1+b_{j}}\frac{{\rm e}^{-ux^{\prime}+vx}}{v-u},\qquad x,x^{\prime}>0,

(5.12)

where
\gamma_{1}
encircles
1-b_{1},\ldots,1-b_{N}
anti-clockwise,
\gamma_{2}
encircles
0
anti-clockwise, and they don’t intersect.
Changing
u\mapsto 1-u
,
v\mapsto 1-v
, this is equal to

L_{N}^{\rm LUE+}(x,x^{\prime};\vec{b},\nu):=\frac{1}{(2\pi\mathrm{i})^{2}}\int_{\Sigma_{N}}{\rm d}u\int_{\ell_{N}}{\rm d}v\left(\frac{u-1}{v-1}\right)^{N+\nu}\prod_{j=1}^{N}\frac{v-b_{j}}{u-b_{j}}\frac{{\rm e}^{-vx+ux^{\prime}}}{v-u},

(5.13)

where
\Sigma_{N}
is a positively oriented circle around
b_{1},\ldots,b_{N}
with radius
<1
, and
\ell_{N}
a negatively oriented circle around
1
, which can moreover be deformed (after changing the orientation) to a vertical line lying between
\Sigma_{N}
and
1
if
\nu>0
.
This kernel is of the form (
1.8
) with

W_{N}(z)\equiv W_{N}^{\rm LUE+}(z;\vec{b},\nu):=\frac{\prod_{j=1}^{N}(z-b_{j})}{(z-1)^{N+\nu}}.

(5.14)

Biorthogonal systems associated to this kernel can be constructed in terms of multiple Laguerre polynomials, see
[
10
]
.
We will now explain that (
5.13
), with
\nu=n-N
a non-negative integer, is the
zero-temperature limit
of the Log Gamma polymer biorthogonal measure.

Zero-temperature limit.

Let us consider the Log Gamma polymer with parameters

\alpha_{1}=\cdots=\alpha_{n}=T\to 0

and re-scaled
a
-parameters
a_{j}=Tb_{j},\quad j=1\ldots,N
with
0\leq b_{1},\ldots,b_{N}<1
.
We then have

\displaystyle W^{{\rm Log}\Gamma}_{n,N}(T\zeta;[T]_{n},T\vec{b})

\displaystyle=\frac{\Gamma(T(1-\zeta))^{n}}{\prod_{m=1}^{N}\Gamma(T(\zeta-b_{m}))}

\displaystyle=T^{N-n}\frac{\prod_{m=1}^{N}(\zeta-b_{m})}{(1-\zeta)^{n}}\frac{\Gamma(1+T(1-\zeta))^{n}}{\prod_{m=1}^{N}\Gamma(1+T(\zeta-b_{m}))}.

and hence

\lim_{T\to 0}\frac{W^{{\rm Log}\Gamma}_{n,N}(Tv;[T]_{n},T\vec{b})}{W^{{\rm Log}\Gamma}_{n,N}(Tu;[T]_{n},T\vec{b})}=\frac{W_{N}^{\rm LUE+}(v;\vec{b},n-N)}{W_{N}^{\rm LUE+}(u;\vec{b},n-N)},\qquad\mbox{for $v\in\ell_{N}$, $u\in\Sigma_{N}$}.

(5.15)

A more careful analysis leads us to the following.

Proposition 5.4
.

We have the point-wise limit

\lim_{T\to 0}\frac{1}{T}L_{n,N}^{{\rm Log}\Gamma}\left(\frac{x}{T},\frac{x^{\prime}}{T};[T]_{n},T\vec{b}\right)=L_{N}^{\rm LUE+}\left(x,x^{\prime};\vec{b},\nu=n-N\right),\qquad x,x^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R},

(5.16)

and there exists
C_{n,N}(\vec{b})
such that for
\epsilon,T>0
sufficiently small,

\left|\frac{1}{T}L_{n,N}^{{\rm Log}\Gamma}\left(\frac{x}{T},\frac{x^{\prime}}{T};[T]_{n},T\vec{b}\right)\right|\leq C_{n,N}(\vec{b})h(x)\tilde{h}(x^{\prime}),

(5.17)

with

h(x)={\rm e}^{-(b_{\max}+2\epsilon)x},\qquad\tilde{h}(x^{\prime})=\begin{cases}{\rm e}^{(b_{\min}-\epsilon)x^{\prime}},&x^{\prime}<0\\
{\rm e}^{(b_{\max}+\epsilon)x^{\prime}},&x^{\prime}\geq 0\end{cases}.

(5.18)

Proof.

It is straightforward to verify that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{T}L_{n,N}^{{\rm Log}\Gamma}\left(\frac{x}{T},\frac{x^{\prime}}{T};[T]_{n},T\vec{b}\right)=\frac{1}{(2\pi\mathrm{i})^{2}}\int_{\Sigma_{N}}{\rm d}s\int_{\ell_{N}}{\rm d}w\frac{W_{n,N}^{{\rm Log}\Gamma}(Tw;[T]_{n},T\vec{b})}{W_{n,N}^{{\rm Log}\Gamma}(Ts;[T]_{n},T\vec{b})}\frac{{\rm e}^{-wx+sx^{\prime}}}{w-s}

\displaystyle\quad=\frac{1}{(2\pi\mathrm{i})^{2}}\int_{\Sigma_{N}}{\rm d}s\int_{\ell_{N}}{\rm d}w\left(\frac{s-1}{w-1}\right)^{n}\frac{\Gamma(1+T(1-w))^{n}}{\Gamma(1+T(1-s))^{n}}\prod_{j=1}^{N}\frac{w-b_{j}}{s-b_{j}}\frac{\Gamma(1+T(s-b_{j}))}{\Gamma(1+T(w-b_{j}))}\frac{{\rm e}^{-wx+sx^{\prime}}}{w-s},

where
\Sigma_{N}
is a contour surrounding
b_{1},\ldots,b_{N}
and no other zeros of
W_{n,N}^{{\rm Log}\Gamma}(Tu)
, and
\ell_{N}
lies at the right of
\Sigma_{N}
and at the left of
1
, and where we used
\Gamma(z+1)=z\Gamma(z)
to pass to the second line.
Hence,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{T}L_{n,N}^{{\rm Log}\Gamma}\left(\frac{x}{T},\frac{x^{\prime}}{T};[T]_{n},T\vec{b}\right)

\displaystyle\quad=\frac{1}{(2\pi\mathrm{i})^{2}}\int_{\Sigma_{N}}{\rm d}s\int_{\ell_{N}}{\rm d}w\frac{W_{N}^{\rm LUE+}(w;\vec{b},n-N)}{W_{N}^{\rm LUE+}(s;\vec{b},n-N)}\left(\frac{\Gamma(1+T(1-w))^{n}}{\Gamma(1+T(1-s))^{n}}\prod_{j=1}^{N}\frac{\Gamma(1+T(s-b_{j}))}{\Gamma(1+T(w-b_{j}))}\right)\frac{{\rm e}^{-wx+sx^{\prime}}}{w-s}.

The integrand converges point-wise to
0
as
T\to 0
. Moreover, the part between brackets is uniformly bounded for
T
sufficiently small,
s\in\Sigma_{N}
, and
w\in\ell_{N}
, provided that we take
\ell_{N}
close to
1
. To see the uniformity in
w
, one has to use (
5.5
). We can then use Lebesgue’s dominated convergence theorem to conclude that we have the point-wise convergence (
5.16
).

To obtain the bound (
5.17
), we take
\ell_{N}=b_{\max}+2\epsilon+\mathrm{i}\mathbb{R}
, and
\Sigma_{N}
within the strip
b_{\min}-\epsilon\leq\mathrm{Re}\,s\leq b_{\max}+\epsilon
, with
\epsilon>0
sufficiently small so that
b_{\max}+2\epsilon<1
. For
T>0
sufficiently small, it then follows that

\displaystyle\left|\frac{1}{T}L_{n,N}^{{\rm Log}\Gamma}\left(\frac{x}{T},\frac{x^{\prime}}{T};[T]_{n},T\vec{b}\right)\right|

\displaystyle\leq 2\frac{h(x)\tilde{h}(x^{\prime})}{(2\pi)^{2}}\int_{\Sigma_{N}}|{\rm d}s|\int_{\ell_{N}}|{\rm d}w|\left|\frac{W_{N}^{\rm LUE+}(w;\vec{b},n-N)}{W_{N}^{\rm LUE+}(s;\vec{b},n-N)}\right|\frac{1}{|w-s|}

\displaystyle\leq C_{n,N}(\vec{b})h(x)\widetilde{h}(x^{\prime}).

The latter inequality holds only if
n>N
, since
\left|\frac{W_{N}^{\rm LUE+}(w;\vec{b},n-N)}{W_{N}^{\rm LUE+}(s;\vec{b},n-N)}\right|\frac{1}{|w-s|}
is not integrable for
n=N
. One can however refine the above estimates by taking into account the decay
of

\frac{\Gamma(1+T(1-w))^{n}}{\Gamma(1+T(1-s))^{n}}\prod_{j=1}^{N}\frac{\Gamma(1+T(s-b_{j}))}{\Gamma(1+T(w-b_{j}))}
as
w\to\infty
on
\ell_{N}
, such that the same inequality remains to hold.

∎

Corollary 5.5
.

As
T\to 0
, we have the convergence

\mathbb{E}\left[{\rm e}^{-{\rm e}^{t/T}Z_{n,N}^{{\rm Log}\Gamma}([T]_{n},T\vec{b})}\right]\longrightarrow\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{LUE+}}\left(\max\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{N}\}\leq-t;\vec{b},\nu={n-N}\right),

(5.19)

where the expectation on the left is with respect to the distribution of the Log Gamma polymer partition function, and the probability on the right is with respect to the LUE with external source (
5.11
).

Proof.

As a consequence of the previous result, we have point-wise convergence of the determinants

\det\left(\frac{\sigma_{t/T}(x_{j}/T)}{T}L_{n,N}^{{\rm Log}\Gamma}\left(\frac{x_{j}}{T},\frac{x_{k}}{T};[T]_{n},T\vec{b}\right)\right)_{j,k=1}^{N}\to\det\left(1_{(-t,+\infty)}(x)L_{N}^{\rm LUE+}\left(x_{j},x_{k};\vec{b},n-N\right)\right)_{j,k=1}^{N}

as
T\to 0
, where
\sigma_{t/T}(x/T)=\frac{1}{1+{\rm e}^{-\frac{x+t}{T}}}
. Moreover, we have the domination

\left|\det\left(\frac{\sigma_{t/T}(x_{j}/T)}{T}L_{n,N}^{{\rm Log}\Gamma}\left(\frac{x_{j}}{T},\frac{x_{k}}{T};[T]_{n},T\vec{b}\right)\right)_{j,k=1}^{N}\right|\leq C_{n,N}^{N}\prod_{j=1}^{N}h(x_{j})\tilde{h}(x_{j})\sigma_{t/T}(x_{j}/T)\ |\det O(1)|,

where
O(1)
here denotes an
N\times N
matrix with uniformly bounded entries.
Integrating over
\mathbb{R}^{N}
yields

\mu_{n,N}^{{\rm Log}\Gamma}[\sigma_{t/T}(./T)]\to\mu_{N}^{\rm LUE+}[1_{(t,+\infty)}]
, which implies the result by (
5.6
).
∎

Remark 5.6
.

The right hand side of (
5.19
) is the largest eigenvalue distribution in the LUE with external source, and it is well-known
[
44
,
20
,
30
]
that this distribution characterizes last passage percolation with exponential weights, which is the zero temperature limit of the Log Gamma polymer. The above results are thus not surprising, but reveal how the zero-temperature limit takes place on the level of the associated biorthogonal measures. Moreover, it now becomes clear that (
5.6
) is the natural finite temperature generalization of the results from
[
44
,
20
,
30
]
which express the Laplace transform of last passage percolation in terms of the largest eigenvalue of the LUE with external source: the biorthogonal measures (
5.8
) and (
5.9
) play a similar role in the Log Gamma polymer as the LUE with external source in last passage percolation.

Remark 5.7
.

Corollary
5.5
is consistent with known large
N
asymptotic results from
[
17
,
7
]
, stating that suitably rescaled Log Gamma polymer partition function has Tracy-Widom and Baik-Ben Arous-Péché fluctuations. These types of behavior are well-known to occur also in the large
N
limits of the LUE and the LUE with external source, respectively.

6
O’Connell-Yor polymer partition function and GUE with external source

The model.

The O’Connell-Yor polymer
[
54
]
, or semi-discrete directed polymer, is a semi-discrete version of the Log Gamma polymer. In order to define it, we fix a real positive number
\tau>0
, a positive integer
N>0
and real numbers
a_{1},\ldots,a_{N}
.
For any
i=1,\ldots,N
,
B_{i}
will denote a one-dimensional Brownian motion with drift
a_{i}
. In other words, for any
s\geq 0
,
B_{i}(s)\sim a_{i}s+B(s)
, where
B(s)
is a standard one-dimensional Brownian motion. Moreover, we assume that
B_{1},\ldots,B_{N}
are independent.
A semi-discrete up-right path between
(0,1)
and
(\tau,N)
consists of horizontal segments that can jump vertically between certain points
(s,k)
and
(s,k+1)
, see Figure
2
. The energy of such a path
\phi
is given by the sum of the Brownian increments between the jump points,

E(\phi)=B_{1}(s_{1})+(B_{2}(s_{2})-B_{2}(s_{1}))+\ldots+(B_{N}(\tau)-B_{N}(s_{N-1})).

The O’Connell-Yor polymer partition function is then defined as

Z_{N}^{\rm OY}(\vec{a},\tau):=\int_{0<s_{1}<\ldots<s_{N-1}<\tau}{\rm e}^{E(\phi)}{\rm d}s_{1}\cdots{\rm d}s_{N-1}.

(6.1)

N=6
, and jump points
s_{1},\ldots,s_{5}
. The weight of a path with jump at
(s_{k},k)
in the O’Connell-Yor polymer depends on the value of
a_{k}
.

Fredholm determinant identity.

Suppose that
|a_{i}|<\delta_{1}<1/2
for all
i=1,\ldots,N
, and take
\delta_{2}\in(0,1)
such that
\delta_{2}>2\delta_{1}
. Then, Borodin and Corwin
[
16
, Theorem 5.2.11]
proved the identity

\mathbb{E}\left[{\rm e}^{-{\rm e}^{t}Z_{N}^{\rm OY}({\vec{a}},\tau)}\right]=\det(1+K_{N,t})_{L^{2}({\Sigma_{N}})},

(6.2)

with

K_{N,t}
as in (
1.29
), with
{\Sigma_{N}}
the circle of radius
\delta_{1}
around the origin,
\ell_{N}
the vertical line
\mathrm{Re}\,z=\delta_{2}
, and
W_{N}
given by

W_{N}(z)=W_{N}^{\rm OY}(z;\vec{a},\tau):=\frac{{\rm e}^{\frac{\tau z^{2}}{2}}}{\prod_{k=1}^{N}\Gamma(z-a_{k})}.

(6.3)

It is easy to check that
W_{N}
satisfies Assumption
1.1
, because of the decay of
{\rm e}^{\frac{\tau z^{2}}{2}}
as
\mathrm{Im}\,z\to\pm\infty
.

Associated biorthogonal measures.

In the same fashion as for the Log Gamma polymer, we can combine the identity (
6.2
) with part 4 of Theorem
1.7
to relate the partition function of the O’Connell-Yor polymer to an explicit biorthogonal measure. In the homogeneous case, the following corollary was already established by Imamura and Sasamoto in
[
41
, Theorem 2]
.

Corollary 6.1
.

Consider a set of real parameters
\vec{a}=(a_{i})_{i=1}^{N}
with

|a_{i}|<\delta_{1}<1/2
for all
i=1,\ldots,N
, and take
\delta_{2}\in(0,1)
such that
\delta_{2}>2\delta_{1}
. Let
W_{N}
be as in (
6.3
),
{\Sigma_{N}}
the positively oriented circle of radius
\delta_{1}
around
0
, and
\ell_{N}
the vertical line with real part
\delta_{2}
.
Then:

1.

\mathbb{E}\left[{\rm e}^{-{\rm e}^{t}Z_{N}^{{\rm OY}}({\vec{a}},\tau)}\right]=\mu_{N}[\sigma_{t}],\qquad\sigma_{t}(x)=\frac{1}{1+{\rm e}^{-x-t}},

(6.4)

where the measure
\mu_{N}
is given by equations (
1.7
)–(
1.8
).

2.

\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}\log\mathbb{E}\left[{\rm e}^{-{\rm e}^{t}Z_{N}^{{\rm OY}}({\vec{a}},\tau)}\right]=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\sigma_{t}(x)\kappa_{N,t}(x){\rm d}x,

(6.5)

where
\kappa_{N,t}
is the one-point function (
1.35
) of the deformed biorthogonal measure (
1.33
).

3.

In the case where the parameters
\vec{a}
are all distinct, the biorthogonal measure
\mu_{N}
is given explicitly as

{\rm d}\mu_{N}^{{\rm OY}}(\vec{x};\vec{a},\tau)=\frac{1}{Z_{N}}\det\left({\rm e}^{a_{m}x_{k}}\right)_{k,m=1}^{N}\det\left(\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\int_{\frac{1}{2}+\mathrm{i}\mathbb{R}}\frac{{\rm e}^{\tau\frac{t^{2}}{2}}{\rm e}^{-tx_{k}}}{(t-a_{m}){\prod_{j=1}^{N}\Gamma(t-a_{j})}}{\rm d}t\right)_{m,k=1}^{N}\prod_{k=1}^{N}{\rm d}x_{k}.

In the confluent case
a_{1}=\cdots=a_{N}=a
,
it is given by

{\rm d}\mu_{N}^{{\rm OY}}(\vec{x};\vec{a},\tau)=\frac{1}{Z_{N}}\det\left(x_{k}^{m-1}\right)_{k,m=1}^{N}\det\left(\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\frac{1}{2}+\mathrm{i}\mathbb{R}}\frac{{\rm e}^{-x_{k}(t-a)}{\rm e}^{\tau\frac{t^{2}}{2}}}{\Gamma(t-a)^{N}(t-a)^{N-m+1}}{\rm d}t\right)_{m,k=1}^{N}\prod_{k=1}^{N}{\rm d}x_{k}.

Remark 6.2
.

Using the integral representations of
\{\psi_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
and
\{\phi_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
with
W_{N}
given by (
6.3
), it is easy to prove that

\psi_{i}({\rm e}^{\bar{x}})=\overline{\psi_{i}({\rm e}^{x})},\quad\phi_{i}({\rm e}^{\bar{x}})=\overline{\phi_{i}({\rm e}^{x})},\quad i=1,\ldots,N.

Consequently,
d\mu_{N}^{{\rm OY}}
is a signed real-valued measure.

GUE with external source.

The
N\times N
GUE with external source
can be defined as

\frac{1}{Z_{N}}{\rm e}^{-{\rm Tr}\,\left(\frac{M^{2}}{2\tau}-AM\right)}{\rm d}M,\quad{\rm d}M=\prod_{j=1}^{N}{\rm d}M_{jj}\ \prod_{1\leq j<k\leq N}{\rm d}\mathrm{Re}\,M_{jk}\,{\rm d}\mathrm{Im}\,M_{jk},

where
A
is a deterministic Hermitian
N\times N
matrix, and
\tau>0
.
For
A=0
and
\tau=1
, this is the classical GUE. In general, the random matrix
M
is the sum of a GUE matrix with the deterministic matrix
A
, see
[
22
,
23
,
45
,
10
]
. The eigenvalue distribution is given by

\frac{1}{Z_{N}}\Delta(\vec{x})\ \det\left({\rm e}^{-\frac{x_{j}^{2}}{2\tau}+a_{k}x_{j}}\right)_{j,k=1}^{N}\prod_{j=1}^{N}{\rm d}x_{j},\qquad x_{1},\ldots,x_{N}\in\mathbb{R},

(6.6)

if
a_{1},\ldots,a_{N}
are distinct, and is explicit in confluent cases as well.
For general
a_{1},\ldots,a_{N}
, the eigenvalue distribution is a biorthogonal ensemble with kernel
L_{N}
of the form (
1.8
), with
W_{N}
given by

W_{N}^{\rm GUE+}(z;{\vec{a}},{\tau})=\prod_{m=1}^{N}(z-a_{m})\ {\rm e}^{{\tau}z^{2}/2}.

(6.7)

see e.g.
[
33
]
. We will denote the associated kernel, defined by (
1.8
), as
L_{N}^{\rm GUE+}
.
Biorthogonal systems associated to this measure can be constructed in terms of multiple Hermite polynomials, see
[
10
]
.

T\to 0
limit.

Consider the O’Connell-Yor polymer partition function with rescaled parameters tau parameter
\tau/T^{2}
and with re-scaled
a_{m}
-parameters
Ta_{1},\ldots,Ta_{N}
.
Then, the kernel of the associated biorthogonal measure
L_{N}
is given by

L_{N}^{\rm OY}\left(x,x^{\prime};T\vec{a},\tau/T^{2}\right)=\frac{1}{(2\pi\mathrm{i})^{2}}\int_{\Sigma_{N}}{\rm d}u\int_{\ell_{N}}{\rm d}v\frac{W_{N}^{\rm OY}(v;T\vec{a},\tau/T^{2})}{W_{N}^{\rm OY}(u;T{\vec{a}},\tau/T^{2})}\frac{{\rm e}^{-vx+ux^{\prime}}}{v-u},

or equivalently by

\frac{1}{T}L_{N}^{\rm OY}(x/T,x^{\prime}/T;T\vec{a},\tau/T^{2})=\frac{1}{(2\pi\mathrm{i})^{2}}\int_{\widetilde{\Sigma}_{N}}{\rm d}s\int_{\widetilde{\ell}_{N}}{\rm d}t\frac{\widetilde{W}_{N}^{\rm OY}(t;{\vec{a}},\tau)}{\widetilde{W}_{N}^{\rm OY}(s;{\vec{a}},\tau)}\frac{{\rm e}^{-tx+sx^{\prime}}}{t-s},

with

\widetilde{W}_{N}^{\rm OY}(z;\vec{a},\tau)=\frac{{\rm e}^{\tau z^{2}/2}}{\prod_{k=1}^{N}\Gamma(T(z-a_{k}))}=\frac{T^{N}\prod_{k=1}^{N}(z-a_{k})}{\prod_{k=1}^{N}\Gamma((1+T(z-a_{k})))}{\rm e}^{\tau z^{2}/2},

and where

\tilde{\ell}_{N}:=T^{-1}\ell_{N},\quad\widetilde{\Sigma}_{N}:=T^{-1}\Sigma_{N}.

It is easy to see that

\lim_{\tau\to\infty}\frac{\widetilde{W}_{N}^{\rm OY}(v;{\vec{a}},\tau)}{\widetilde{W}_{N}^{\rm OY}(u;{\vec{a}},\tau)}=\frac{W_{N}^{\rm GUE+}(v;{\vec{a}},\tau)}{W_{N}^{\rm GUE+}(u;{\vec{a}},\tau)},

where
W_{N}^{\rm GUE+}
is defined in equation (
6.7
) above. We can now prove the following results similarly as for the Log Gamma polymer, with the simplification that the integrands can be dominated more easily thanks to the Gaussian factors.

Proposition 6.3
.

We have the point-wise limit

\lim_{T\to 0}T^{-1}L_{N}^{{\rm OY}}\left(x/T,x^{\prime}/T;T{\vec{a}},\tau/T^{2}\right)=L_{N}^{\rm GUE+}\left(x,x^{\prime};\vec{a},\tau\right),\qquad x,x^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R},

(6.8)

and there exists
C_{N}(\vec{a})
such that for
\epsilon>0
sufficiently small and
T>0
sufficiently small,

\left|T^{-1}L_{N}^{{\rm OY}}\left(x/T,x^{\prime}/T;T{\vec{a}},\tau/T^{2}\right)\right|\leq C_{N}(\vec{a})h(x)\tilde{h}(x^{\prime}),

(6.9)

with
h,\tilde{h}
as in (
5.18
).

Corollary 6.4
.

As
T\to 0
, we have the convergence

\mathbb{E}\left[{\rm e}^{-{\rm e}^{t/T}Z_{N}^{{\rm OY}}(T{\vec{a}},\tau/T^{2})}\right]\longrightarrow\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{GUE+}}\left(\max\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{N}\}\leq-t;\vec{a},\tau\right),

where the expectation on the left is with respect to the distribution of the O’Connell-Yor polymer partition function, and the probability on the right is with respect to the GUE with external source, equation (
6.6
).

Remark 6.5
.

Like for the Log Gamma polymer, this result should not come as a surprise. The zero temperature version of the O’Connell-Yor polymer is a model of Brownian queues which can be characterized by the GUE
[
8
,
38
]
. The first identity in (
6.4
) is the finite temperature analogue of these characterizations, in which the role of the GUE (with external source) is played by the biorthogonal measure
\mu_{N}
.

7
Mixed polymer partition function

The model.

The mixed polymer, or O’Connell-Yor polymer with boundary sources, is a mixture of the Log Gamma and the O’Connell-Yor polymer. It was introduced by Borodin, Corwin, Ferrari, and Vető in
[
15
]
.
Given parameters
n,N\in\mathbb{N}
,
\tau>0
,
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n}
, and
a_{1},\ldots,a_{N}
with
\alpha_{j}-a_{k}>0
for every
j=1,\ldots,n
and
k=1,\ldots,N
, the mixed polymer partition function is defined as

Z_{n,N}^{\rm Mixed}(\vec{\alpha},\vec{a},\tau)=\sum_{k=1}^{N}Z_{k,N}^{\rm Log\Gamma}(\vec{\alpha},a_{1},\ldots,a_{k})Z_{N-k}^{\rm OY}(a_{k+1},\ldots,a_{N},\tau).

(7.1)

It can be interpreted as the partition function for up-right paths consisting of a discrete (Log Gamma polymer type) path from
(1,1)
to a point
(N,k)
, concatenated with a semi-discrete (O’Connell-Yor polymer type) path from
(N,k)
to
(N+\tau,n)
, where
N,n,\tau
are given, and
k
depends on the chosen path, see Figure
3
.

Fredholm determinant identity.

As far as we know, a Laplace transform formula of the form (
5.3
) or (
6.2
) has not appeared in the literature for the general mixed polymer partition function, although there are Fredholm determinant identities for the Laplace transform that look alike
[
15
]
, but with the kernel defined through integration over an unbounded contour instead of
{\Sigma_{N}}
(recall Remark
1.9
). It is not clear whether such unbounded contour integral Fredholm determinant expressions can be related directly to our class of biorthogonal measures.
There is however another general Fredholm determinant identity obtained by Imamura and Sasamoto in
[
42
, Proposition 4.5]
:

\mathbb{E}\left[{\rm e}^{-{\rm e}^{t}Z_{N,N}^{\rm Mixed}({\vec{\alpha}},{\vec{a}},\tau)}\right]=\det(1-\sigma_{t}M)_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})},

(7.2)

where
\sigma_{t}(x)=\frac{1}{1+{\rm e}^{-x-t}}
,
M
is given by

M(x,x^{\prime})=\sum_{k=1}^{N}\Phi_{k}(x)\Psi_{k}(x^{\prime}),

(7.3)

and

\displaystyle\Phi_{k}(x)=\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\int_{{\Sigma_{N}}}{\rm d}u\frac{{\rm e}^{ux-\tau u^{2}/2}}{u-a_{k}}\prod_{j=1}^{k-1}\frac{u-\alpha_{j}}{u-a_{j}}\prod_{j=1}^{N}\frac{\Gamma(1+u-a_{j})}{\Gamma(1+\alpha_{j}-u)},

(7.4)

\displaystyle\Psi_{k}(x)=\frac{\alpha_{k}-a_{k}}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}}{\rm d}w\frac{{\rm e}^{-\mathrm{i}wx-\tau w^{2}/2}}{\alpha_{k}-\mathrm{i}w}\prod_{j=1}^{k-1}\frac{\mathrm{i}w-a_{j}}{\mathrm{i}w-\alpha_{j}}\prod_{j=1}^{N}\frac{\Gamma(1+\alpha_{j}-\mathrm{i}w)}{\Gamma(1+\mathrm{i}w-a_{j})}.

(7.5)

For the above identity to hold,
{\Sigma_{N}}
has to enclose all the points
a_{1},\ldots,a_{N}
but none of the poles of
\prod_{m=1}^{N}\Gamma(1+u-a_{m})
. Note that this is only possible when there are no
a_{k}
-values which differ by an integer. Below, we assume that this is the case. We mention however that our final results Corollary
7.3
, Proposition
7.6
, and Corollary
7.7
hold without this assumption. This can be seen by a continuity argument.

An up-right path in the mixed polymer model, corresponding to
n=N=6
,
k=3
. The values
\alpha_{j},a_{k}
determine the weight of the point
(j,k)
in the discrete part of the lattice, while the values
a_{k}
determine the weight of jump points
(s_{k},k)
in the lattice.

Associated biorthogonal measures.

We will show, starting from equation (
7.2
), that the mixed polymer partition function is also related to an explicit biorthogonal measure of the form (
1.14
). We start with the following

Lemma 7.1
.

Let
N\in\mathbb{N},\{a_{1},\ldots,a_{N}\}
and
\{\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{N}\}
two sets of real parameters such that
\alpha_{j}-a_{k}>0
for all
j,k=1,\cdots,N
. Then the two families of functions
\{\Phi_{k},\;k=1,\ldots,N\}
and
\{\Psi_{k},\;k=1,\ldots N\}
, defined in (
7.4
)–(
7.5
), are biorthogonal:

\int_{\mathbb{R}}\Phi_{k}(x)\Psi_{m}(x){\rm d}x=\delta_{km},\qquad k,m=1,\ldots,N.

Proof.

We first observe that
\Psi_{m}(x)
is a Fourier transform:

\Psi_{m}(x)=\frac{\alpha_{m}-a_{m}}{2\pi}\mathcal{F}[g_{m}]\left(\frac{x}{2\pi}\right),\quad g_{m}(w)=\frac{{\rm e}^{-\tau w^{2}/2}}{\alpha_{m}-\mathrm{i}w}\prod_{j=1}^{m-1}\frac{\mathrm{i}w-a_{j}}{\mathrm{i}w-\alpha_{j}}\prod_{j=1}^{N}\frac{\Gamma(1+\alpha_{j}-\mathrm{i}w)}{\Gamma(1+\mathrm{i}w-a_{j})},

where

\mathcal{F}[g](t):=\int_{\mathbb{R}}g(w){\rm e}^{-2\pi\mathrm{i}wt}{\rm d}t,\quad\mathcal{F}^{-1}[G](w)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}G(t){\rm e}^{2\pi\mathrm{i}wt}{\rm d}t.

It follows that

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}\Phi_{k}(x)\Psi_{m}(x){\rm d}x

\displaystyle=-\frac{\alpha_{m}-a_{m}}{4\pi^{2}\mathrm{i}}\int_{{\Sigma_{N}}}{\rm d}u\frac{1}{(u-a_{k})(u-\alpha_{k})g_{k}(-\mathrm{i}u)}\int_{\mathbb{R}}{\rm d}x{\rm e}^{ux}\mathcal{F}[g_{m}]\left(\frac{x}{2\pi}\right){\rm d}x

\displaystyle=-\frac{\alpha_{m}-a_{m}}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\int_{{\Sigma_{N}}}{\rm d}u\frac{1}{(u-a_{k})(u-\alpha_{k})g_{k}(-\mathrm{i}u)}\int_{\mathbb{R}}{\rm d}s{\rm e}^{2\pi su}\mathcal{F}[g_{m}]\left(s\right){\rm d}s

\displaystyle=-\frac{\alpha_{m}-a_{m}}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\int_{{\Sigma_{N}}}{\rm d}u\frac{1}{(u-a_{k})(u-\alpha_{k})g_{k}(-\mathrm{i}u)}\left(\mathcal{F}^{-1}\circ\mathcal{F}\right){[g_{m}]}\left(-\mathrm{i}u\right)

\displaystyle=-\frac{\alpha_{m}-a_{m}}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\int_{{\Sigma_{N}}}{\rm d}u\frac{1}{(u-a_{k})(u-\alpha_{k})}\frac{g_{m}\left(-\mathrm{i}u\right)}{g_{k}(-\mathrm{i}u)}.

If
m=k
, this is equal to
-\frac{\alpha_{m}-a_{m}}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\int_{{\Sigma_{N}}}{\rm d}u\frac{1}{(u-a_{k})(u-\alpha_{k})}=1
. If
m>k
, the integrand is analytic such that the integrand is
0
. If
m<k
, the integrand is a rational function without zeros in the exterior of
{\Sigma_{N}}
and decaying like
O(1/u^{2})
as
u\to\infty
, such that we can deform
{\Sigma_{N}}
to a large circle to see that the integral is
0
.
∎

Using the techniques from Section
3.1
, we can then conclude that the kernel
M
defined in (
7.3
) is a kernel
satisfying the reproducing property, which defines
the
N
-point biorthogonal measure

{\rm d}\widetilde{\mu}_{N}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{N}):=\frac{1}{N!}\det\left(M(x_{m},x_{k})\right)_{m,k=1}^{N}\prod_{k=1}^{N}{\rm d}x_{k},

(7.6)

which can be equivalently rewritten as

{\rm d}\widetilde{\mu}_{N}(\vec{x})=\frac{1}{N!}\det\left(\Phi_{j}(x_{m})\right)_{m,j=1}^{N}\det\left(\Psi_{j}(x_{k})\right)_{j,k=1}^{N}\prod_{k=1}^{N}{\rm d}x_{k}.

(7.7)

We will now show that the above biorthogonal measure is of the form (
1.7
).

Proposition 7.2
.

Let
N\in\mathbb{N},\{a_{1},\ldots,a_{N}\}
and
\{\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{N}\}
two sets of real parameters such that
\alpha_{j}-a_{k}>0
for all
j,k=1,\cdots,N
.
The measure (
7.7
)
is equal to (
1.7
) and (
2.1
), with
{\Sigma_{N}}
in the left half plane and enclosing
a_{1},\ldots,a_{N}
but none of the poles of
\prod_{j=1}^{N}\Gamma(1+v-a_{j})
,
\ell_{N}
a vertical line at the right of
\Sigma_{N}
, and
W_{N}
given by

W_{N}(z)\equiv W_{N}^{\rm Mixed}(z;\vec{\alpha},\vec{a},\tau):={\rm e}^{\tau z^{2}/2}\frac{\prod_{j=1}^{N}\Gamma(\alpha_{j}-z)}{\prod_{k=1}^{N}\Gamma(z-a_{k})}.

(7.8)

Proof.

To show that
\widetilde{\mu}_{N}
is equal to
\mu_{N}
with
W_{N}
given by (
7.8
), it is sufficient to prove that these measures are equal in case
a_{1},\ldots,a_{N}
are distinct, by continuity.
In that case, we can easily evaluate the functions
\Phi_{1},\ldots,\Phi_{N}
using the residue theorem, which implies that they have the same linear span as
{\rm e}^{a_{1}x},\ldots,{\rm e}^{a_{N}x}
. Similarly, we show that
\Psi_{1},\ldots,\Psi_{N}
have the same linear span as
\psi_{1}({\rm e}^{x}),\ldots,\psi_{N}({\rm e}^{x})
defined in (
1.12
): we have

\displaystyle\Psi_{k}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha_{k}-a_{k}}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}{\rm d}w{\rm e}^{-\mathrm{i}wx-\tau w^{2}/2}\frac{\prod_{j=1}^{k-1}(\mathrm{i}w-a_{j})}{\prod_{j=1}^{k}{(\mathrm{i}w-\alpha_{j})}}\prod_{j=1}^{N}\frac{\Gamma(1+\alpha_{j}-\mathrm{i}w)}{\Gamma(1+\mathrm{i}w-a_{j})}

\displaystyle=\pm\frac{\alpha_{k}-a_{k}}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}{\rm d}w{\rm e}^{-\mathrm{i}wx-\tau w^{2}/2}\frac{\prod_{j=1}^{k-1}(\mathrm{i}w-a_{j})\ \prod_{j=k+1}^{N}(\mathrm{i}w-\alpha_{j})}{\prod_{j=1}^{N}{(\mathrm{i}w-\alpha_{j})}\ }\prod_{j=1}^{N}\frac{\Gamma(\alpha_{j}-\mathrm{i}w)}{\Gamma(\mathrm{i}w-a_{j})}

\displaystyle=\pm\frac{\alpha_{k}-a_{k}}{2\pi}\int_{\ell_{N}}{\rm d}u{\rm e}^{-ux+\tau u^{2}/2}\frac{\prod_{j=1}^{k-1}(u-a_{j})\prod_{j=k+1}^{N}{(u-\alpha_{j})}}{\prod_{j=1}^{N}{(u-a_{j})}}\prod_{j=1}^{N}\frac{\Gamma(\alpha_{j}-u)}{\Gamma(u-a_{j})}

\displaystyle=\pm\frac{\alpha_{k}-a_{k}}{2\pi}\int_{\ell_{N}}{\rm d}u{\rm e}^{-ux}\frac{\prod_{j=1}^{k-1}(u-a_{j})\prod_{j=k+1}^{N}{(u-\alpha_{j})}}{\prod_{j=1}^{N}{(u-a_{j})}}W_{N}(u).

On the other hand, we already know that

\psi_{1}({\rm e}^{x}),\ldots,\psi_{N}({\rm e}^{x})
span every function of the form

\int_{\ell_{N}}{\rm d}w{\rm e}^{-ux}\frac{P_{N-1}(u)}{\prod_{j=1}^{N}{(u-a_{j})}}W_{N}(u),

with
P_{N-1}
a polynomial of degree
\leq N-1
. We conclude that
\Psi_{1}(x),\ldots,\Psi_{N}(x)
and
\psi_{1}({\rm e}^{x}),\ldots,\psi_{N}({\rm e}^{x})
have the same linear span, such that
(
7.7
) can be rewritten as

{\rm d}\widetilde{\mu}_{N}(\vec{x}):=\frac{1}{N!}\det\left({\rm e}^{a_{j}x_{m}}\right)_{m,j=1}^{N}\det\left(\psi_{j}({\rm e}^{x_{k}})\right)_{j,k=1}^{N}\prod_{k=1}^{N}{\rm d}x_{k},

which is equal to
{\rm d}\mu_{N}(\vec{x})
by (
1.14
). The result if proved.
∎

Combining the above results with Theorem
1.10
, we obtain the following characterization of the mixed polymer partition function in terms of the biorthogonal measures
\mu_{N}
and
\nu_{N,t}
.

Corollary 7.3
.

Let
N\in\mathbb{N}
, and let
\{a_{1},\ldots,a_{N}\}
,
\{\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{N}\}
two sets of real parameters such that
\alpha_{j}-a_{k}>0
for all

.
Then:

1.

(7.9)

where the measure
\mu_{N}
is given by equations (
1.7
), (
1.8
) with
W_{N}
defined by (
7.8
), with
\Sigma_{N}
a loop in the left half plane enclosing
a_{1},\ldots,a_{N}
but none of the poles of
\prod_{j=1}^{N}\Gamma(1+v-a_{j})
, and
\ell_{N}
a vertical line at the right of
\Sigma_{N}
.

2.

\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}\log\mathbb{E}\left[{\rm e}^{-{\rm e}^{t}Z_{N,N}^{{\rm Mixed}}({\vec{\alpha}},{\vec{a}},\tau)}\right]=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\sigma_{t}(x)\kappa_{N,t}(x){\rm d}x,

(7.10)

where
\kappa_{N,t}
is the one-point function (
1.35
) of the deformed biorthogonal measure (
1.33
).

3.

In the case where the parameters
\vec{a}
are all distinct,
the biorthogonal measure
\mu_{N}
is given explicitly as

{\rm d}\mu_{N}^{{\rm Mixed}}(\vec{x};\vec{\alpha},\vec{a},\tau)=\frac{1}{Z_{N}}\det\left({\rm e}^{a_{m}x_{k}}\right)_{k,m=1}^{N}\det\left(\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\int_{\frac{1}{2}+\mathrm{i}\mathbb{R}}\frac{{\rm e}^{\tau\frac{t^{2}}{2}}\prod_{j=1}^{n}\Gamma(\alpha_{j}-t)}{(t-a_{m})\prod_{k=1}^{N}\Gamma(t-a_{k})}{{\rm e}}^{-tx_{k}}{\rm d}t\right)_{m,k=1}^{N}\prod_{k=1}^{N}{\rm d}x_{k}.

In the confluent case
a_{1}=\cdots=a_{N}=a
,
it is given by

{\rm d}\mu_{N}^{{\rm Mixed}}(\vec{x};\vec{\alpha},\vec{a},\tau)=\frac{1}{Z_{N}}\det\left(x_{k}^{m-1}\right)_{k,m=1}^{N}\det\left(\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\frac{1}{2}+\mathrm{i}\mathbb{R}}\frac{{\rm e}^{\tau\frac{t^{2}}{2}}{\prod_{j=1}^{n}\Gamma(\alpha_{j}-t)}{{\rm e}}^{-(t-a)x_{k}}}{(t-a)^{N-m+1}\Gamma(t-a)^{N}}\mathrm{d}t\right)_{m,k=1}^{N}\prod_{k=1}^{N}{\rm d}x_{k}.

Remark 7.4
.

Using the integral representations of
\{\psi_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
and
\{\phi_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
with
W_{N}
given by (
7.8
), it is easy to prove that

\psi_{i}({\rm e}^{\bar{x}})=\overline{\psi_{i}({\rm e}^{x})},\quad\phi_{i}({\rm e}^{\bar{x}})=\overline{\phi_{i}({\rm e}^{x})},\quad i=1,\ldots,N.

Consequently,
d\mu_{N}^{{\rm Mixed}}
is a signed real-valued measure.

Remark 7.5
.

Corollary
7.3
contains both Corollary
5.2
with
n=N
and Corollary
6.1
as degenerate cases: the Log Gamma polymer corresponds to
\tau=0
, while the O’Connell-Yor polymer corresponds to
\alpha_{1}=\ldots=\alpha_{N}\to\infty
.
As already mentioned, the above identities are finite temperature generalizations of classical characterizations of last passage percolation and Brownian queues models in terms of the GUE and LUE with external sources. The biorthogonal measures
\mu_{N}
replace the GUE and LUE with external source in the corresponding finite temperature models. The identity (
7.9
) can also be seen as a finite
N
version of the characterization from
[
3
]
of the narrow wedge solution of the KPZ equation (or continuum directed polymer) in terms of the Airy point process. In this sense, the biorthogonal measures
\mu_{N}
play the same role in (semi-)discrete directed polymer models as the Airy point process in the continuum directed polymer model.
Other instances of these relations between stochastic models and point processes are available in the literature. In
[
11
]
, the stochastic higher spin six vertex model is related to the MacDonald measure, which specializes to the Schur measure in the case of the homogenenous six vertex model. According to the author, the determinantal structure of the O’Connel-Yor polymer
[
41
]
could be a degeneration of the one for the stochastic higher spin six vertex model. Other types of degenerations lead to characterize the Asymmetric Exclusion Process (ASEP) in terms of the discrete Laguerre ensemble, and of the discrete Hermite ensemble in a large time regime
[
19
]
. Back to the KPZ equation, the characterization of the narrow wedge solution of the KPZ equation (or continuum directed polymer) in terms of the GUE Airy point process extends to half space case initial data, upon replacing the GUE Airy point process with the GOE Airy point process, which is not determinantal but Pfaffian
[
6
,
55
]
. In the discrete case, thanks to combinatorial identities between
q
-Whittaker measures and periodic and free boundary Schur measures
[
39
]
, Pfaffian Fredholm determinants characterize the
q
-PushTASEP and the Log Gamma polymer in half space
[
40
]
.

Sum of LUE with external source with GUE.

Consider an
N\times N
matrix
M
from the LUE with external source, with distribution (
5.11
) and
\nu=0
, and consider an
N\times N
GUE matrix
H
, with distribution (
6.6
) with
A=0
. Set
Q=M+\sqrt{\tau N}H
.
The eigenvalue distribution of
Q
can be computed using
[
27
, Theorem 2.3 (b)]
, or more directly using the re-scaled variant of this identity given in
[
25
, Equation (1.6)]
. This result gives an integral expression for the correlation kernel of the eigenvalues of
Q
in terms of the eigenvalue correlation of
M
, valid for any random matrix
M
whose eigenvalue distribution is a polynomial ensemble, and thus in particular for our LUE with external source random matrix
M
.
It states that an eigenvalue correlation kernel
K_{N}(x,x^{\prime})
of
Q
is given by

K_{N}(x,x^{\prime})=\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}\tau}\int_{\mathrm{i}\mathbb{R}}{\rm d}s\int_{\mathbb{R}}{\rm d}t\;k_{N}(s,t){\rm e}^{\frac{1}{2\tau}\left((x^{\prime}-s)^{2}-(x-t)^{2}\right)},

(7.11)

where
k_{N}(s,t)
is a correlation kernel of the eigenvalue distribution of
M
which is polynomial in
s
: more precisely, it is of the form

k_{N}(s,t)=\sum_{k=1}^{N}p_{k}(s)q_{k}(t),

(7.12)

where
p_{1},\ldots,p_{N}
are polynomials of degree
N-1
, and we have the biorthogonality relations

\int_{\mathbb{R}}p_{j}(s)q_{k}(s){\rm d}s=\delta_{jk},\qquad j,k=1,\ldots,N.

In our situation, we can take

k_{N}(s,t)=\frac{1}{(2\pi\mathrm{i})^{2}}\int_{\Sigma}{\rm d}u\int_{\ell}{\rm d}v\left(\frac{v}{u}\right)^{N}\prod_{j=1}^{N}\frac{u-1+b_{j}}{v-1+b_{j}}\frac{{\rm e}^{us-vt}}{v-u},\qquad s,t\in\mathbb{R}

(7.13)

where
\Sigma
encircles
0
, and
\ell=c+\mathrm{i}\mathbb{R}
is a vertical line at the right of
\Sigma
and at the left of
1-b_{1},\ldots,1-b_{N}
. The
v
-integral is defined as a Cauchy principal value integral,
\int_{\ell}=\lim_{R\to\infty}\int_{c-\mathrm{i}R}^{c+\mathrm{i}R}{\rm d}v
.
Indeed, for
t<0
, we can compute this principal value integral by closing the integration contour
[c-\mathrm{i}R,c+\mathrm{i}R]
with a large semi-circle to the left, and it follows then from a residue computation that
k_{N}(s,t)=0
. Similarly, for
t>0
, we can close the integration contour with a large semi-circle on the right, and then we obtain that

k_{N}(s,t)={\rm e}^{s-t}L_{N}(s,t),

with
L_{N}=L_{N}^{\rm LUE+}
given by
(
5.12
) with
\nu=0
.
It is also straightforward to verify using the residue theorem that
k_{N}(s,t)
is polynomial in
s
, and more precisely of the required form (
7.12
). As for the biorthogonality relations, we refer to
[
10
]
.
We then apply (
7.11
) and find
that
K_{N}(x,x^{\prime})
is given by

\displaystyle K_{N}(x,x^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}\tau}\int_{\mathrm{i}\mathbb{R}}{\rm d}s\int_{\mathbb{R}}{\rm d}t\;k_{N}(s,t){\rm e}^{\frac{1}{2\tau}\left((x^{\prime}-s)^{2}-(x-t)^{2}\right)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(2\pi\mathrm{i})^{3}\tau}\int_{\mathrm{i}\mathbb{R}}{\rm d}s\int_{\mathbb{R}}{\rm d}t\int_{\Sigma}{\rm d}u\int_{\ell}{\rm d}v\left(\frac{v}{u}\right)^{N}\prod_{j=1}^{N}\frac{u-1+b_{j}}{v-1+b_{j}}\frac{{\rm e}^{us-vt}}{v-u}{\rm e}^{\frac{1}{2\tau}\left((x^{\prime}-s)^{2}-(x-t)^{2}\right)}.

Changing the order of integration and writing
\tilde{W}_{N}(z)=\frac{z^{N}}{\prod_{j=1}^{N}(z-1+b_{j})}
, we obtain after a straightforward computation that

\displaystyle K_{N}(x,x^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(2\pi\mathrm{i})^{3}\tau}\int_{\Sigma}{\rm d}u\int_{\ell}{\rm d}v\frac{\tilde{W}_{N}(v)}{\tilde{W}_{N}(u)}\frac{{\rm e}^{\frac{\tau}{2}v^{2}-\frac{\tau}{2}u^{2}+ux^{\prime}-vx}}{v-u}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\times\ \left(\int_{\mathrm{i}\mathbb{R}}{\rm d}s\;{\rm e}^{\frac{1}{2\tau}\left(x^{\prime}-s-\tau u\right)^{2}}\right)\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}{\rm d}t\;{\rm e}^{-\frac{1}{2\tau}\left(x-t-\tau v\right)^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(2\pi\mathrm{i})^{2}}\int_{\Sigma}{\rm d}u\int_{\ell}{\rm d}v\frac{\tilde{W}_{N}(v){\rm e}^{\frac{\tau}{2}v^{2}}}{\tilde{W}_{N}(u){\rm e}^{\frac{\tau}{2}u^{2}}}\frac{{\rm e}^{x^{\prime}u-xv}}{v-u}.

We conclude that
K_{N}(x,x^{\prime})
is again a kernel of the form (
1.8
), but now with transformed function
W
given by

W_{N}^{\rm GLUE+}(z;\vec{b},\tau):=\frac{z^{N}{\rm e}^{\tau z^{2}/2}}{\prod_{k=1}^{N}(z-1+b_{k})}.

(7.14)

We will now point out that this biorthogonal measure corresponds to the small temperature limit of the biorthogonal measure associated to the mixed polymer.

Small temperature limit for the mixed polymer.

Consider the mixed polymer partition function with re-scaled
\tau=\tau/T^{2}
,
a_{1}=\cdots=a_{N}=0
, and with re-scaled
\alpha
-parameters
\alpha_{1}=T-Tb_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{N}=1-Tb_{N}
, in the limit where
T\to 0
.
Then, the kernel of the associated biorthogonal measure
L_{N}
is given by

{L_{N}^{\rm Mixed}\left(x,x^{\prime};\left[T\right]_{N}-T\vec{b},[0]_{N},\tau/T^{2}\right)}=\frac{1}{(2\pi\mathrm{i})^{2}}\int_{\Sigma_{N}}{\rm d}u\int_{\ell_{N}}{\rm d}v\frac{W_{N}^{\rm Mixed}(v;[T]_{N}-T\vec{b},[0]_{N},\tau/T^{2})}{W_{N}^{\rm Mixed}(u;[T]_{N}-T\vec{b},[0]_{N},\tau/T^{2})}\frac{{\rm e}^{-vx+ux^{\prime}}}{v-u},

or equivalently by

\frac{1}{T}L_{N}^{\rm Mixed}\left(\frac{x}{T},\frac{x^{\prime}}{T};[T]_{N}-T\vec{b},[0]_{N},\tau/T^{2}\right)=\frac{1}{(2\pi\mathrm{i})^{2}}\int_{\widetilde{\Sigma}_{N}}{\rm d}s\int_{\widetilde{\ell}_{N}}{\rm d}t\frac{\widetilde{W}_{N}^{\rm Mixed}(t;[1]_{N}-\vec{b},[0]_{N},\tau,T)}{\widetilde{W}_{N}^{\rm Mixed}(s;[1]_{N}-\vec{b},[0]_{N},\tau,T)}\frac{{\rm e}^{-tx+sx^{\prime}}}{t-s},

with

\widetilde{W}_{N}^{\rm Mixed}(z;[1]_{N}-\vec{b},[0]_{N},\tau,T)=\frac{{\rm e}^{\tau z^{2}/2}\prod_{k=1}^{N}\Gamma((1-b_{k}-z)T)}{\Gamma(zT)^{N}}=\frac{z^{N}{\rm e}^{\tau z^{2}/2}}{\prod_{k=1}^{N}(1-b_{k}-z)}\frac{\prod_{k=1}^{N}\Gamma(1+(1-b_{k}-z)T)}{\Gamma(1+zT)^{N}},

and where

\tilde{\ell}_{N}:=\frac{1}{T}\ell_{N},\quad\widetilde{\Sigma}_{N}:=\frac{1}{T}\Sigma_{N}.

It is easy to see that

\lim_{T\to 0}\frac{\widetilde{W}_{N}^{\rm Mixed}(v;[T]_{N}-T\vec{b},[0]_{N},\tau,T)}{\widetilde{W}_{N}^{\rm Mixed}(u;[T]_{N}-T\vec{b},[0]_{N},\tau,T)}=\frac{W_{N}^{\rm GLUE+}(v;\vec{b},\tau)}{W_{N}^{\rm GLUE+}(u;\vec{b},\tau)}.

In analogy to the zero temperature results for the Log Gamma polymer and the O’Connell-Yor polymer, we obtain the following.

Proposition 7.6
.

We have the point-wise limit

\lim_{T\to 0}\frac{1}{T}L_{N}^{{\rm Mixed}}\left(\frac{x}{T},\frac{x^{\prime}}{T};[T]_{N}-T\vec{b},[0]_{N},\tau/T^{2}\right)=L_{N}^{\rm GLUE+}\left(x,x^{\prime};\vec{b},\tau\right),\qquad x,x^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R},

(7.15)

and there exists
C_{N}(\vec{b},\tau)
such that for
\epsilon>0
sufficiently small and
T>0
sufficiently small,

\left|\frac{1}{T}L_{N}^{{\rm Mixed}}\left(\frac{x}{T},\frac{x^{\prime}}{T};[T]_{N}-T\vec{b},[0]_{N},\tau/T^{2}\right)\right|\leq C_{N}(\vec{b},\tau)h(x)\tilde{h}(x^{\prime}),

(7.16)

with
h,\tilde{h}
as in (
5.18
).

Corollary 7.7
.

As
T\to\infty
, we have the convergence

\mathbb{E}\left[{\rm e}^{-{\rm e}^{t/T}Z_{N,N}^{{\rm Mixed}}\left([T]_{N}-T\vec{b},[0]_{N},\tau/T^{2}\right)}\right]\longrightarrow\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{GLUE+}}\left(\max\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{N}\}\leq-t;\vec{b},\tau\right),

where the expectation on the left is with respect to the distribution of the mixed polymer partition function, and the probability on the right is with respect to the distribution of the eigenvalues
x_{1},\ldots,x_{N}
of the random matrix
Q
constructed above as a sum of a GUE with an LUE with external source matrix.

Remark 7.8
.

To the best of our knowledge, the appearance of the sum of an LUE with external source matrix with a GUE matrix is completely new.
One can also think of the sum
Q=M+\sqrt{\tau N}H
as the matrix obtained by applying independent Brownian motions to the independent entries of
M
. The resulting process for the eigenvalues of this process is called Dyson’s Brownian motion, see e.g.
[
45
]
.
In the zero temperature limit,
\tau
thus plays the role of time in Dyson’s Brownian motion applied to the eigenvalue configuration of a random matrix from the LUE with external source. If the measures
{\rm d}\mu_{N}^{\rm Mixed}(\vec{x};\vec{\alpha},{\vec{a}},\tau)
are positive polynomial ensembles, such an interpretation in terms of Dyson’s Brownian motion remains valid for finite temperature: let
\tau^{\prime}>\tau
, and suppose that
\vec{x}
is a random configuration of points at time
\tau
; we then obtain a random configuration of points at time
\tau^{\prime}
by applying Dyson’s Brownian motion to
\vec{x}
for a time period
\tau^{\prime}-\tau
.
