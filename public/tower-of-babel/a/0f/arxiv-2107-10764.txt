Nonlinear transformation of complex amplitudes via quantum singular value transformation
Naixu Guo, Kosuke Mitarai, Keisuke Fujii

Abstract
Due to the linearity of quantum operations, it is not straightforward to implement nonlinear transformations on a quantum computer, making some practical tasks like a neural network hard to be achieved. In this work, we define a task called nonlinear transformation of complex amplitudes and provide an algorithm to achieve this task. Specifically, we construct a block-encoding of complex amplitudes from a state preparation unitary. This allows us to transform the complex amplitudes by using quantum singular value transformation. We evaluate the required overhead in terms of input dimension and precision, which reveals that the algorithm depends on the roughly square root of input dimension and achieves an exponential speedup on precision compared with previous work. We also discuss its possible applications to quantum machine learning, where complex amplitudes encoding classical or quantum data are processed by the proposed method. This paper provides a promising way to introduce the highly complex nonlinearity of the quantum states, which is essentially missing in quantum mechanics.



I
Introduction

Quantum mechanics is fundamentally linear.
Quantum operations are all unitaries in matrix representation, and the measurement is the only way in which we can produce the nonlinearity.
If deterministic nonlinear operations are allowed, quantum computers can solve NP-hard problems in polynomial time
Abrams and Lloyd 1998
.
This result implies the difficulty of nonlinear quantum operations.
However, nonlinearity plays an essential role in applications such as nonlinear differential equations and machine learning.
It is important to consider how to implement a nonlinear transformation on a quantum computer, even if it can only be applied probabilistically.

The key idea for applying probabilistic nonlinear operations is to use a post-selection, i.e., the desired transformation is applied on target subsystems if ancilla qubits are measured in a certain state.
The Harrow-Hassidim-Lloyd (HHL) algorithm
Harrow et al. 2009
, for example, used this idea to provide an exponential speedup for solving linear systems of equations compared with classical computers.

In recent years, methods for performing nonlinear operations have been developed rapidly.
Quantum signal processing
Low and Chuang 2017a
is a technique to perform desired nonlinear functions on eigenvalues of a unitary operator.
Quantum singular value transformation (QSVT)
Gilyén et al. 2019
is its generalization to perform nonlinear functions on singular values of a matrix encoded in a unitary.
Existing quantum algorithms can be reconstructed in terms of QSVT
Gilyén et al. 2019
;
Martyn et al. 2021
, which unifies the disparate problems like Hamiltonian simulation
Childs and Wiebe 2012
;
Berry et al. 2015a
;
Low and Chuang 2017a
;
Low and Chuang 2019
, linear equation solving
Harrow et al. 2009
;
Childs et al. 2017
and amplitude amplification techniques
Berry et al. 2015b
;
Yoder et al. 2014
;
Low and Chuang 2017b
, implying the importance of developing tools to introduce nonlinearity on quantum computers.

Here, we consider how to perform nonlinear transformations on the state amplitudes directly, rather than the nonlinear transformation of matrices described in the previous work
Gilyén et al. 2019
.
We call this task
nonlinear transformation of complex amplitudes
(NTCA), which is defined as follows.
Assume we have access to a state preparation oracle

\displaystyle U:\ket{0}\rightarrow\sum_{k=1}^{N}c_{k}\ket{k},

(1)

which encodes a complex vector
\vec{c}=\{c_{1},\cdots,c_{N}\}
.
We also assume having access to its adjoint and their controlled versions.
For given nonlinear functions
P,Q:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
, NTCA is a task to prepare a quantum circuit such that its output state is

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{c}}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\left(P(x_{k})+Q(y_{k})\right)\ket{k},

(2)

where
x_{k}+iy_{k}=c_{k}
and
c
is a normalization factor.

In this work, we provide an algorithm for NTCA based on QSVT.
To achieve this, we develop a method called
block-encoding of amplitudes
, where a unitary operator constructed from the state preparation oracle encodes a Hermitian matrix, whose singular values are the real or imaginary parts of amplitudes, into its subspace.
We show that if functions
P
and
Q
can be approximated by
d
-degree polynomials
P^{\prime}
and
Q^{\prime}
, NTCA can be implemented using controlled-
U
and controlled-
U^{\dagger}

\mathcal{O}\left(d\sqrt{N/\sum_{k=1}^{N}|P^{\prime}(x_{k})+Q^{\prime}(y_{k})|^{2}}\right)

times in expectation.
We also provide a discussion about how to exploit this algorithm for quantum machine learning.
More specifically, we consider how to perform a neural network on the quantum computer and discuss its possible applications.
This work opens up a way to implement nonlinear operations on a quantum computer and may contribute to the field of quantum machine learning.

This paper is organized as follows.
We define the task NTCA and summarize existing important subroutines in Section
II
.
In Section
III.1
, we introduce the
block-encoding of amplitudes
.
Combining this method with QSVT, we construct an algorithm to perform NTCA in Section
III.2
.
In Section
IV
, we provide the application of this algorithm and show that NTCA provides us a way to perform the neural network task directly on a quantum computer.

II
Preliminary

In this section, we first define a task called NTCA and summarize some subroutines from existing works. Throughout this paper,
N
denotes the dimension of data,
n=\lceil\log_{2}(N)\rceil
, and
\mathcal{I}_{s}
denotes the identity operator for
s
qubits.
A quantum state
\ket{\psi}
represented in the form
\ket{\psi}=c_{a}\ket{a}+\cdots
means that we focus on the term
c_{a}\ket{a}
and the residual term is orthogonal to the first term.

First, we formally define NTCA as follows:

Definition 1

(Nonlinear transformation of complex amplitudes (NTCA))
.

Assume we have access to a state preparation oracle
U
such that
U\ket{0}=\sum_{k=1}^{N}c_{k}\ket{k}
, where
c_{k}=x_{k}+iy_{k}
and
x_{k},y_{k}
are real numbers.
We also assume having access to its adjoint and their controlled versions.
For nonlinear functions
P,Q:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
bounded on the interval
[-1,1]
,
output a quantum state
\frac{1}{\sqrt{c^{\prime}}}\sum_{k=1}^{N}b_{k}\ket{k}
such that it is an
\varepsilon
-approximation to the
\frac{1}{\sqrt{c}}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\left(P(x_{k})+Q(y_{k})\right)\ket{k}
.
Here,
c,c^{\prime}
are the normalization coefficients, and
\varepsilon
-approximation means that, for all
k
and
x_{k},y_{k}\in[-1,1]
,

\displaystyle|b_{k}-P(x_{k})-Q(y_{k})|\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{N}.

(3)

To achieve NTCA, we utilize QSVT, which is a technique to apply nonlinear functions on singular values of a matrix.
It exploits the so-called block-encoding of a target matrix defined as follows.

Definition 2

(Block-encoding
Low and Chuang 2019
;
Chakraborty et al. 2019
)
.

Suppose that A is an n-qubit operator,
\alpha,\varepsilon\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
and
a\in\mathbb{N}
. Then we say that the
(a+n)
-qubit unitary
U
is an
(\alpha,a,\varepsilon)
-block-encoding of A if

\displaystyle\|A-\alpha(\bra{0}^{\otimes a}

\displaystyle\otimes\mathcal{I}_{n})U(\ket{0}^{\otimes a}\otimes\mathcal{I}_{n})\|\leq\varepsilon.

(4)

Using the block-encoding, QSVT can implement
P(A)
of a Hermitian matrix
A
for any polynomial
P
. More formally, we can achieve the following.

Lemma 3

(Polynomial eigenvalue transformation of arbitrary parity
(
Gilyén et al. 2019
, Theorem 56)
)
.

Suppose that
U
is an
(\alpha,a,\varepsilon)
-block-encoding of a Hermitian matrix
A
.
If
\delta\geq 0
and
P:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
is a d-degree polynomial satisfying that

\displaystyle\mathrm{for\ all}\ x\in[-1,1]:\left|P(x)\right|\leq\frac{1}{4},

(5)

then there is a quantum circuit
\tilde{U}
, which is an
(1,a+3,4d\sqrt{\varepsilon/\alpha}+N\delta)
-block-encoding of
P(A/\alpha)
, and consists of d applications of
U
and
U^{\dagger}
gates, a single application of controlled-U and
\mathcal{O}(ad)
other one- and two-qubit gates. Moreover, we can compute a description of such a circuit with a classical computer in time
\mathcal{O}(\operatorname{poly}(d,\log(1/\delta)))
.

We note that this theorem is not exactly Theorem 56 of Ref.
Gilyén et al. 2019
but a slightly generalized version which is mentioned in Ref.
Gilyén et al. 2019
.
The original theorem is a similar claim for real polynomial
P_{\Re}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
requiring two ancilla qubits instead of three in the above theorem.

III.2
Nonlinear transformation of complex amplitudes

In this subsection, we provide an algorithm to implement NTCA.
The idea is to apply Lemma
3
to the block-encodings obtained in Theorem
4
to implement desired functions.
We assume the existence of polynomial approximations of functions
P
and
Q
to utilize Theorem
3
.
Note that in principle, any continuous real-valued function defined on the interval
[-1,1]
can be approximated by a polynomial function with arbitrary precision by the Weierstrass approximation theorem
Pinkus 2000
.

Our quantum algorithm for NTCA is the following.

Theorem 5

(Nonlinear transformation of complex amplitudes)
.

If functions
P
and
Q
in Definition
1
can be approximated by
d_{p}
- and
d_{q}
-degree polynomials

Let

\gamma:=\underset{x\in[-1,1]}{\max}\{|P(x)|,|Q(x)|\}

1

\mathcal{O}\left(d\gamma\sqrt{N/\sum_{k=1}^{N}|P^{\prime}(x_{k})+Q^{\prime}(y_{k})|^{2}}\right)

\mathcal{O}\left(nd\gamma\sqrt{N/\sum_{k=1}^{N}|P^{\prime}(x_{k})+Q^{\prime}(y_{k})|^{2}}\right)

III.3
Comparison with previous work

Our work is inspired by the previous work
Mitarai et al. 2019
, based on which we focus on how to apply nonlinear functions on amplitudes of a quantum state on the quantum computer.
Ref.
Mitarai et al. 2019
also considered performing nonlinear transformation of amplitudes.
Let us compare the present work with Ref.
Mitarai et al. 2019
in the case where we wish to implement only
P:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
acting on real parts of amplitudes.
Their algorithm works by first performing quantum phase estimation
Cleve et al. 1998
of the unitary
G
defined in Eq. (
9
) to encode
x_{k}
(and
y_{k}
) into bitstrings as
\sum_{k=1}^{N}\ket{k}\ket{\bar{x}_{k}}
, where
\bar{x}_{k}
is a binary representation of
x_{k}
.
Then, using an ancilla qubit and arithmetical operations, it prepares a state in the form of

\sum_{k=1}^{N}\ket{k}\ket{\bar{x}_{k}}\left(P(x_{k})\ket{0}+\sqrt{1-P(x_{k})^{2}}\ket{1}\right),

from which we can obtain the desired state if we measure the ancilla qubit in
\ket{0}
state.
If this algorithm is applied to the same setting as Definition
1
, it would require
\mathcal{O}(N/\varepsilon)
uses of the controlled-
G
and hence the same amount of controlled-
U
in the phase estimation step.
In total, it would require

\tilde{\mathcal{O}}\left(N/\varepsilon\sqrt{N/\sum_{k=1}^{N}|P(x_{k})|^{2}}\right)

uses of controlled-
U

In contrast, the algorithm presented in Theorem

uses

\mathcal{O}\left(d\gamma\sqrt{N/\sum_{k=1}^{N}|P^{\prime}(x_{k})+Q^{\prime}(y_{k})|^{2}}\right)

of controlled-
U

implicitly depends on the precision requirement
N/\varepsilon

In many cases,

can be taken as
d=\mathcal{O}(\log(N/\varepsilon))

Gilyén et al. 2019
, and thus we achieve exponential speedup in terms of
N
and
\varepsilon
.
This improvement is important when we consider the running time of possible applications like a quantum neural network, as described in the next section.
We also need fewer ancilla qubits for NTCA.
While our quantum algorithm needs
\mathcal{O}(n)
ancilla qubits, Ref.
Mitarai et al. 2019
needs
\mathcal{O}(n+\log(N/\varepsilon))
.

IV
Application to quantum machine learning

Let us finally discuss possible applications of NTCA for machine learning tasks.
A straightforward application of the NTCA is to implement the neural network by using the complex amplitudes as its nodes as follows.
For a (classical) data vector
\vec{x}=\{x_{1},x_{2},\cdots,x_{N}\}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
, let us assume that we have a data-encoding unitary
U_{\vec{x}}
such that,

\displaystyle U_{\vec{x}}:\ket{0}\rightarrow\sum_{k=1}^{N}x_{k}\ket{k}.

(23)

This is the so-called amplitude encoding of classical data
Schuld and Petruccione 2018
.

U_{\vec{x}}
can be constructed via the use of quantum random access memory (QRAM)
Giovannetti et al. 2008
with the data structure mentioned in Ref.
Kerenidis and Prakash 2016
.
Using
U_{\vec{x}}
and an orthogonal matrix
O=\sum_{j,k}o_{jk}|j\rangle\langle k|
, we can prepare,

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{N}P\left(\sum_{j=1}^{N}o_{kj}x_{j}\right)\ket{k},

(24)

whose amplitudes correspond to the outputs of a single-layer neural network with an activation function
P
and an orthogonal weight matrix, by using
OU_{\mathrm{data}}
as
U
in Theorem
5
.
Note that the normalization factor is omitted to simplify the notation.
We will do so henceforth if the normalization factor is not needed explicitly for discussions.
We also analyze the concrete implementation of a famous activation function
P(x)=\tanh(x)
in Appendix
C
.

We can straightforwardly generalize them for a complex input vector
\ket{\psi}=\sum_{k=1}^{N}\psi_{k}\ket{k}
.
Let
U_{\psi}\ket{0}:=\ket{\psi}
.
Our NTCA algorithm can prepare a state in the form of,

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{N}F\left(\sum_{j=1}^{N}v_{kj}\psi_{j}\right)\ket{k}

(25)

F:\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{C}
is an activation function in the form of
F(\psi)=P(\mathrm{Re}(\psi))+Q(\mathrm{Im}(\psi))
, and

are elements of a (tranable) unitary

The amplitudes of this state can be regarded as the outputs of a single-layer neural network with input datum
\{\psi_{k}\}

While

23

it is notable that

can be any encoding of a classical data
\vec{x}
to quantum state

such as the ones proposed in quantum machine learning literatures

;
Havlíček et al. 2019
;
Farhi and Neven 2018

Moreover, we believe that a more important situation for NTCA is when the input state

has intrinsic quantumness.
For example, we might be able to learn properties of a certain family of Hamiltonians

The generated state has a variety of possible applications.
A straightforward application would be the classification.

Note that in this case, we do not need to output a full vector like Eq. (

Another possible application is to use the output state in Eq. (

Ng and Jordan 2002

The purpose of generative models is to create a machine that can sample from a probability distribution that is close to the distribution of data.
Since it has been shown that sampling from a certain family of quantum states is classically hard

In contrast to the previous works

We first consider how to achieve the two-layer neural network.

V
Conclusion

We have deﬁned a task called NTCA considering how to apply nonlinear functions onto the amplitudes of a quantum state.
To this end,
we introduced
block-encoding of amplitudes
,
which encodes amplitudes of a quantum state on a block element of
a unitary operator by using the state preparation oracle.
This allows us to treat complex amplitudes of a quantum state
as singular values of a matrix encoded into a unitary operator and hence to apply QSVT
for NTCA.
Since the proposed mapping from a state preparation oracle
to a block-encoding unitary is very general,
we expect that it can be applied widely when we want to process
a quantum state in such a way that cannot be achieved by a simple unitary transformation.
As a concrete example of such an application,
we proposed to use our algorithm to implement neural networks
on quantum computers, where complex amplitudes
are used as nodes for the neural networks and
nonlinear transformations, which cannot be realized by a unitary operation,
are performed by the proposed NTCA.
From scaling analysis,
our result implies that there could be
a complexity-theoretic benefit to use
a quantum computer to process and perform
a nonlinear transformation on a high dimensional input data,
while it still relies on several assumptions such as
the state preparation oracle.
It is an interesting open question whether the proposed nonlinear transformation or quantum neural network can provide an advantage in classical machine learning tasks.
We think that it is, at least, inevitable to apply quantum machine learning for quantum data
since a systematic nonlinear transformation is essentially missing in quantum systems
without the proposed method.

Appendix B
Detailed error analysis for Theorem
5

Note that
\mathcal{P}
is a
(1,4,\epsilon/(16\gamma))
-block-encoding of a matrix whose eigenvalues are
P^{\prime}(x_{k})/(4\gamma)
.
For all

, we have

\displaystyle\left|P^{\prime}(x_{k})/(4\gamma)-P^{\prime\prime}(x_{k})\right|\leq\varepsilon/(16\gamma N),

(35)

where
P^{\prime\prime}(x_{k})
denotes the exact value achieved by Lemma
3
.
By assumption, we have
|P(x_{k})-P^{\prime}(x_{k})|\leq\varepsilon/(4N)
.
Therefore,

\displaystyle|P(x_{k})-4\gamma P^{\prime\prime}(x_{k})|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle|P(x_{k})-P^{\prime}(x_{k})|+|P^{\prime}(x_{k})-4\gamma P^{\prime\prime}(x_{k})|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle|P(x_{k})-P^{\prime}(x_{k})|+4\gamma|P^{\prime}(x_{k})/(4\gamma)-P^{\prime\prime}(x_{k})|\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{2N},

(36)

where the inequalities comes from triangle inequality.
The same discussion applies for the imaginary part, i.e.,

.
Finally, for all

we have

\displaystyle|4\gamma P^{\prime\prime}(x_{k})+4\gamma Q^{\prime\prime}(y_{k})-P(x_{k})-Q(y_{k})|\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{N},

(37)

satisfying the error bound condition for NTCA.

Appendix C
Polynomial approximation of tanh

Here, we show that
P(x)=\tanh(x)
can be approximated to an precision
\varepsilon
with a degree
d=\mathcal{O}(\log(1/\varepsilon))
polynomial
P^{\prime}_{d}(x)
in the sense that
|P^{\prime}_{d}(x)-\tanh(x)|\leq\varepsilon
.
It suffices to use the simple Taylor series.
By Taylor expansion,

\displaystyle\tanh(x)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}(-1)^{n-1}\frac{2^{2n}(2^{2n}-1)B_{2n}}{(2n)!}x^{2n-1},

(38)

where
B_{2n}
is the Bernoulli number.
We take,

\displaystyle P^{\prime}_{d}(x)=\sum_{n=1}^{d}(-1)^{n-1}\frac{2^{2n}(2^{2n}-1)B_{2n}}{(2n)!}x^{2n-1}.

(39)

Then, the error can be written as,

\displaystyle|P^{\prime}_{d}(x)-\tanh(x)|=\sum_{n=d+1}^{\infty}(-1)^{n-1}\frac{2^{2n}(2^{2n}-1)B_{2n}}{(2n)!}x^{2n-1}.

(40)

As mentioned in Ref.
Leeming 1989
, we have

\displaystyle|B_{2n}|<5\sqrt{\pi n}\left(\frac{n}{\pi e}\right)^{2n}.

(41)

Also, since
\left(n/e\right)^{n}\leq n!
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{2^{2n}(2^{2n}-1)B_{2n}}{(2n)!}

\displaystyle<5\sqrt{\pi n}\left(\frac{4\frac{n}{\pi e}}{\frac{2n}{e}}\right)^{2n}

\displaystyle=5\sqrt{\pi n}\left(\frac{2}{\pi}\right)^{2n}.

(42)

Further, since
\sqrt{d+1}\leq(\pi/2)^{d+1}
for
d\geq 0
, we have

\displaystyle 5\sqrt{\pi(d+1)}\left(\frac{2}{\pi}\right)^{2d+2}\leq 5\sqrt{\pi}\left(\frac{2}{\pi}\right)^{d+1}.

(43)

Therefore, the error in Eq. (
40
) can be bounded by

\displaystyle\sum_{n=d+1}^{\infty}(-1)^{n-1}\frac{2^{2n}(2^{2n}-1)B_{2n}}{(2n)!}x^{2n-1}

\displaystyle<

\displaystyle\sum_{n=d+1}^{\infty}\frac{2^{2n}(2^{2n}-1)B_{2n}}{(2n)!}x^{2n-1}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{n=d+1}^{\infty}5\sqrt{\pi}\left(\frac{2}{\pi}\right)^{d+1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{5\sqrt{\pi}}{1-(2/\pi)}\left(\frac{2}{\pi}\right)^{d+1}.

(44)

It follows that the error bound of
\varepsilon
can be achieved by setting
d=\mathcal{O}\left(\log\left(1/\varepsilon\right)\right)
.
