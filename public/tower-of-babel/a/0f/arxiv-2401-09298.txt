Dynamical signatures of non-Markovianity in a dissipative–driven qubit
Graziano Amati

Abstract
We investigate signatures of non-Markovianity in the dynamics of a periodically-driven qubit coupled to a dissipative bosonic environment. We propagate the dynamics of the reduced density matrix of the qubit by integrating the numerically exact hierarchical equations of motion. Non-Markovian features are quantified by comparing on an equal footing the predictions from diverse and complementary approaches to quantum dissipation. In particular, we analyze the distinguishability of quantum states, the decay of the volume accessible to the qubit on the Hilbert space, the negativity of the canonical rates in the generalized Lindblad equation and the relaxation of the memory kernels in the Nakajima–Zwanzig generalized quantum master equation. We study the effects of controlled driving on the coherent dynamics of the system. We show that a suitable external field can offset the ergodic relaxation of time correlation functions, increase distinguishability over time and strengthen non-Markovian effects witnessed by the canonical dissipation channels. We furthermore observe the phenomenon of eternal non-Markovianity for sufficiently small system–bath coupling and we discuss how this can be enhanced by modulating the frequency of the external drive. The present work provides a broad theoretical analysis of quantum dissipation in the framework of open quantum dynamics and quantum information.



I
Introduction

To date, uncontrolled dissipation is a crucial issue limiting both the reliability of quantum-information protocols and the scalability of quantum-computing devices.

Babu, Tuorila, and Ala-Nissila 2021
;
Ray et al. 2023
;
Verney et al. 2019
;
Harrington, Mueller, and Murch 2022

Improving coherence lifetime is a task of the utmost importance in order to enable quantum computers to tackle relevant and challenging numerical analyses, e.g. in the framework of drug discovery,
Outeiral et al. 2021
artificial intelligence
Ahmed and Mähönen 2021
and the simulation of many-body quantum systems.
Smith et al. 2019
;
Franke et al. 2019

From a theoretical standpoint, it is in the first place controversial how to systematically define and measure dissipation in quantum mechanics.
It is in particular debated how this ubiquitous physical process relates to the occurrence of memory effects, otherwise called signatures of
non-Markovianity
(in contrast to Markovian memory-less processes).
Breuer 2012
;
Rivas, Huelga, and Plenio 2014

Memory effects in classical dynamics can be witnessed by measuring the probability of a physical system of being in a given state in the present, conditioned on its past history.
Breuer et al. 2016

This probabilistic approach is applicable provided a series of measurements is performed over time.
While measurement processes do not alter classical states, they do however affect coherence in quantum systems.
In particular, projective measurements lead to the irreversible collapse of entangled quantum states.
Schlosshauer 2005

Hence, in order to minimize external perturbations induced by measurement processes, alternative methods have been proposed to quantify dissipation and non-Markovianity in quantum mechanics.

The
trace-distance approach
to quantum non-Markovianity measures dissipation from the loss of distinguishability over time between two maximally distinguishable states of a quantum subsystem, due to the interaction with an external environment.
Breuer, Laine, and Piilo 2009
;
Breuer et al. 2016
;
Amato, Breuer, and Vacchini 2018

In particular, one can prepare the subsystem in two states maximizing an operator metric known as the trace distance.
Unitary dynamics conserve this distance, while it decreases monotonically over time in the opposite limit of strongly dissipative dynamics.
It can however occur that, even in the limit of an infinitely large dissipative bath, the trace distance increases over time.
Increasing distinguishability has been related to the occurrence of information backflow from the environment.
Breuer et al. 2016
;
Chakraborty 2018

From this perspective, it has been proposed to leverage non-Markovianity itself as a resource for quantum information and control.
Reich, Katz, and Koch 2015
;
Pineda et al. 2016
;
Mirkin, Poggi, and Wisniacki 2019

The
volume of states
accessible by the qubit provides another measure of dissipation induced by the environment.
Lorenzo, Plastina, and Paternostro 2013a

It has been discussed in the literature that a drawback of this method is its potential inability to accurately capture weak dissipation occurring across a subset of channels in the Hilbert space of the subsystem.
This issue is however resolved by analyzing the
canonical rates
, time-dependent coefficients appearing in an exact time-local master equation
in Lindblad form.
Hall et al. 2014
;
Erika Andersson and Hall 2007
;
Maldonado-Mundo et al. 2012
;
Hayden and Sorce 2022

The latter approach is capable of quantifying memory effects occurring along each quantum channel.
Specifically, a generalized Lindblad equation, uniquely expressed in canonical form, involves a set of time-dependent canonical dissipation rates, which are non-negative functions of time under Markovian dynamics.
Therefore, the negativity of each rate serves as an indicator of non-Markovian behavior within the corresponding channel.
Eternal non-Markovianity
arises whenever certain rates assume negative values at all times.
Shrikant, Srikanth, and Banerjee 2022
;
Gulácsi and Burkard 2023

The
Nakajima–Zwanzig formalism
is another rigorous, first-principled approach to quantum dissipation.
Nakajima 1958
;
Zwanzig 1960
;
Mori 1965

The method relies on expressing open quantum dynamics in terms of an exact integro–differential equation of motion.
An integral term featuring a memory kernel encapsulates complete information on the interaction between the subsystem and the environment.
The kernel vanishes in the limit of unitary dynamics, whereas it collapses to a Dirac delta function in the opposite Markovian limit.
As the timescale to relaxation of the kernel is highly sensitive to environmental dissipation and decoherence, it represents a natural measure of non-Markovianity.
Cohen and Rabani 2011
;
Ng and Rabani 2022

All aforementioned methods provide an interpretation of dissipation from different perspectives, not necessarily in agreement with each other
Chruściński, Kossakowski, and Rivas 2011a
;
Chruściński, Macchiavello, and Maniscalco 2017
;
Bhattacharya et al. 2017
;
Chanda and Bhattacharya 2016

and, as discussed in Ref.
Pollock et al. 2018a
;
Pollock et al. 2018b
, they do not recover the classical definition of Markovianity expressed by the Kolmogorov conditions.
It is still subject for debate whether a unique measure to dissipation can be defined in the first place, or whether several different approaches should be regarded as complementary to each other.

The effects of dissipation on the long-time limits of open dynamical systems can be inspected from the perspective of ergodic theory.
From a classical standpoint, environmental dissipation
leads to the ergodic relaxation of the initial distribution to its invariant thermal limit, fulfilling detailed balance.
Hawkins 2021

For quantum mechanical systems it has been conjectured, within the
eigenstate thermalization hypothesis
, that thermalization can ultimately occur if the asymptotic limit of quantum operators is consistent with the microcanonical ensemble.
Deutsch 2018
;
Luca D’Alessio and Rigol 2016

Predicting the occurrence of chaos and thermalization in quantum dynamics remains however a hard and unresolved theoretical problem.
Analyzing the long-time behavior of open quantum dynamics becomes even more challenging in presence of an external time-dependent field.
This scenario is particularly relevant from the perspective of quantum error correction (QEC), given that coupling dissipative dynamics to an external drive is a well-know and effective strategy to protect the dynamics of the system from environmental decoherence.

Jirari and Pötz 2006
;
Jirari and Pötz 2007
;
Jirari 2020
;
Rebentrost and Wilhelm 2009

In particular, loss of coherence in a system of qubits occurs naturally due to the uncontrolled coupling to an external environment.
To counterbalance the effects of environmental noise, a variety of QEC techniques has been developed in the last decades.
Lidar and Brun 2013
;
Vozhakov et al. 2022
;
Terhal 2015
;
Babu, Tuorila, and Ala-Nissila 2021

Among those, a successful family of approaches is based on
dynamical-decoupling techniques
, protecting the dynamics from environmental dissipation by means of controlled external pulses.

Bastrakova, Klenov, and Satanin 2019
;
Rebentrost and Wilhelm 2009
;
Viola, Knill, and Lloyd 1999
;
Viola, Knill, and Lloyd 2000

Inspired by dynamical-decoupling strategies, in the present work we analyze how an external time-dependent drive affects environmental dissipation in a spin–boson model externally coupled to a periodic monochromatic field.
Engelhardt, Platero, and Cao 2019
;
Magazzù et al. 2018

We propagate the open quantum dynamics of the qubit by integrating the hierarchical equations of motion (HEOM).
Tanimura 2014
;
Tanimura 2020

This allows us to obtain a numerically exact solution for the reduced density matrix (RDM) of the qubit.

A convenient strategy for reducing dissipation in open quantum dynamics is to maximize non-Markovian effects. In fact, strongly dissipative processes tend to display Markovian memory-less features.
Non-Markovian effects are instead associated to a weak-to-intermediate coupling between a quantum subsystem and an external environment (see the later discussion in
Section

In the following

IV.2
Non-Markovianity

IV.2.1
Trace distance

The trace-distance approach to quantum non-Markovianity relies on the interpretation of dissipation as loss of distinguishability between quantum states over time.
Vacchini 2012
;
Breuer et al. 2016

In this section we outline the fundamental aspects of this approach, and we apply it to the present study of dissipative–driven dynamics.
Extended discussions and reviews on the method can be found, e.g., in Refs.
Laine, Piilo, and Breuer 2010
;
Breuer et al. 2016
.

Let us consider two copies of the qubit prepared in two well-defined initial states, with RDM’s
\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{s}}^{(1)}(0)
and
\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{s}}^{(2)}(0)
and corresponding Bloch vectors
\bm{v}^{(1)}(0)
and
\bm{v}^{(2)}(0)
.
A measure of distinguishability between these states is given by the
trace distance

\mathcal{D}^{(1,2)}(t)=\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{s}}^{(1)}(t)-\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{s}}^{(2)}(t)\right\rVert,

(IV.5)

where
\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert
is the trace norm on
\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{s}}
.
Watrous 2018

Not that the trace distance of a Hermitian operator
\hat{A}\in\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{s}}
can be expressed in terms of the absolute sum of its eigenvalues
d_{i}
’s, that is
\left\lVert\hat{A}\right\rVert=\sum_{i=1,2,3}|d_{i}|
.
Breuer et al. 2016

This, in particular, implies that for a two-level subsystem the trace distance coincides with half of the Euclidean distance between the respective Bloch vectors:

\mathcal{D}^{(1,2)}(t)=\frac{1}{2}|\bm{v}^{(1)}(t)-\bm{v}^{(2)}(t)|.

(IV.6)

Equivalently, by making use of
Eq.

II.15
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}^{(1,2)}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left\{\sum_{j=x,y,z}\left[\sum_{i=x,y,z}\left(v^{(1)}_{i}-v^{(2)}_{i}\right)\mathcal{C}_{ij}(t)\right]^{2}\right\}^{1/2}.

(IV.7)

Let us note that the correlations
\mathcal{C}_{0\nu}(t)
,
\nu\in\{0,x,y,z\}
do not contribute to the trace distance, and that all the terms involved in
Eq.

IV.7
relax to zero at long times (as discussed in
Section

IV.1
).
This indicates that information on the initial state is completely dissipated to the environment at long times.
A limitation of the trace distance is that the approach cannot capture non-Markovian effects due to correlation functions
\mathcal{C}_{0\nu}(t)
generating the non-unital part of the dynamics and leading to nonzero stationary limits.
Utagi, Banerjee, and Srikanth 2023

Divisible time-dependent dynamical processes act as contractions of the trace distance, that is
\dot{\mathcal{D}}^{(1,2)}(t)\leq 0
for all
\bm{v}^{(1)}
and
\bm{v}^{(2)}
.
Ruskai 1994
;
Breuer et al. 2016

Non-divisibility can instead be interpreted as memory effects, given that time propagation on a finite time interval cannot be arbitrarily decomposed at intermediate times. The present observations led Breuer, Laine and Piilo (BLP) to introduce as a witness of non-Markovianity the integral measure

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{BLP}}

\displaystyle=\max_{\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{s}}^{(1)},\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{s}}^{(2)}}\int_{\dot{\mathcal{D}}^{(1,2)}(t)>0}\mathrm{d}t\;\dot{\mathcal{D}}^{(1,2)}(t).

(IV.8)

The maximization in
Eq.

IV.8
guarantees that
\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{BLP}}
is exclusively a function of the dynamical map
\Phi_{t}
describing the open quantum dynamics, rather than of any specific couple of initial densities.
It has been proven in Ref.
Wißmann et al. 2012
that the densities maximizing
Eq.

IV.8
are mutually orthogonal and lie on the boundary of the Hilbert space of the subsystem
\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{s}}
.
For the specific case of two-level systems, those densities are represented by antipodal vectors of unit norm on the surface of the Bloch sphere.
We can hence rewrite
Eq.

IV.8
as

\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{BLP}}=\max_{|\bm{v}|=1}\int_{\dot{\mathcal{D}}^{\bm{v}}(t)>0}\mathrm{d}t\;\dot{\mathcal{D}}^{\bm{v}}(t),

(IV.9)

where

{\mathcal{D}}^{\bm{v}}(t)=\left\{\sum_{j=x,y,z}\left[\sum_{i=x,y,z}v_{i}\mathcal{C}_{ij}(t)\right]^{2}\right\}^{1/2}.

(IV.10)

IV.2.2
Volume of accessible states

From the propagation of the Bloch vector in
Eq.

II.15
we identify the volume on the Bloch sphere

\mathcal{V}(t)=\lvert\det\mathcal{C}^{T}(t)\rvert

(IV.11)

accessible to the qubit at a given time
t
.
Lorenzo, Plastina, and Paternostro 2013b
;
Chruściński, Macchiavello, and Maniscalco 2017
;
Puthumpally-Joseph et al. 2018
;
Mangaud et al. 2018

Another insightful expression for
\mathcal{V}(t)
follows from the first-order expansion

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}(t+\delta t)

\displaystyle=\left\lvert\det\left[\mathcal{C}^{T}(t)+\delta t\dot{\mathcal{C}}^{T}(t)\right]\right\rvert+\mathcal{O}(\delta t^{2})

\displaystyle=\left\lvert\left\{\mathcal{I}_{4}+\delta t\tr_{\mathrm{s}}[\Xi(t)]\right\}\mathcal{V}(t)\right\rvert+\mathcal{O}(\delta t^{2}),

(IV.12)

where we introduced the
damping matrix

Hall et al. 2014

\Xi(t)=\dot{\mathcal{C}}^{T}(t)[\mathcal{C}^{T}(t)]^{-1}=\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\ln[\mathcal{C}^{T}(t)].

(IV.13)

Equation

IV.12
leads to the
differential equation

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\mathcal{V}(t)=\tr_{\mathrm{s}}[\Xi(t)]\mathcal{V}(t),

(IV.14)

solved by

\mathcal{V}(t)=\mathcal{V}(0)\exp\left\{\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{d}t^{\prime}\;\tr_{\mathrm{s}}[\Xi(t^{\prime})]\right\}.

(IV.15)

\Xi(t)
is also the generator of the dynamics in the Bloch representation.
In fact, from
Eq.

II.15
,

\displaystyle\dot{v}_{\mu}(t)

\displaystyle=\Xi_{\mu\nu}(t)v_{\nu}(t).

(IV.16)

Equivalently, given that

\displaystyle\dot{v}_{\mu}(t)

\displaystyle=\tr_{\mathrm{s}}\left[\Lambda_{t}[\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{s}}(t)]\hat{\sigma}_{\mu}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\tr_{\mathrm{s}}\left[\Lambda_{t}[\hat{\sigma}_{\nu}]\hat{\sigma}_{\mu}\right]v_{\nu}(t),

(IV.17)

we identify

\Xi_{\mu\nu}(t)=\frac{1}{2}\tr_{\mathrm{s}}\left[\Lambda_{t}[\hat{\sigma}_{\nu}]\hat{\sigma}_{\mu}\right].

(IV.18)

It can be proven that for completely positive open quantum dynamics the volume
\mathcal{V}(t)
decreases monotonically in time.
Wolf and Cirac 2008
;
Raja et al. 2018

This applies in particular to the BM limit discussed in
Section

II
.
Therefore, similarly to
Eq.

IV.8
, it has been proposed in Ref.
Lorenzo, Plastina, and Paternostro 2013a
as a witness of non-Markovianity the integral measure

\mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{V}}=\frac{1}{\mathcal{V}(0)}\int_{\dot{\mathcal{V}}(t)>0}\mathrm{d}t\;\dot{\mathcal{V}}(t).

(IV.19)

The time evolution of
\mathcal{V}(t)
is shown in the insets of
Figs.

3
and
3
.
We note that the timescale to relaxation of
\mathcal{V}(t)
increases for larger inverse temperature
\beta
and for smaller system–bath coupling
\eta
.
This suggests that the relaxation of the volume provides a simple and effective measure of decoherence, sensitive to the strength of dissipation induced by the bath.
However, for all considered system parameters
\mathcal{V}(t)
decreases monotonically in time, hence
\mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{V}}=0
.
This result appears in contrast with the analysis of the trace distance, which, as discussed in
Section

IV.2.1
, witnesses non-Markovian effects in all the considered systems.
A well-known issue of the volume approach is that
\mathcal{V}(t)
accounts exclusively for the average dissipation over all quantum channels, potentially leading to an oversight of weak non-Markovian effects localized on a subset of channels on the Hilbert space.
Hall et al. 2014
;
Chruściński, Macchiavello, and Maniscalco 2017
;
Mangaud, Meier, and Desouter-Lecomte 2017

We will extensively discuss this issue in the next
Section

IV.2.3
,
within the framework of the time-local master equation
Eq.

II.10
.
Despite the aforementioned limitations, the analysis
\mathcal{V}(t)
can still offer valuable insight into the invertibility of open quantum dynamics, a necessary condition to construct the time-local canonical master equation
Eq.

II.10
.
In particular, from the insets of
Fig.

3
, we observe that, in the small coupling limit, the monochromatic drive can extend invertibility to longer times compared to the non-driven system with
\Omega=0
.

IV.2.3
Canonical rates

Studying the dynamics of the canonical rates within the time-local master equation
Eq.

II.10
provides a rigorous method for quantifying non-Markovianity in open quantum systems.
In particular, it can be shown that, in the BM limit of
Eq.

II.6
, the operators
\hat{L}_{i}
’s become time-independent, and the rates
\gamma_{i}
’s converge to non-negative constants.
Lindblad 1976
;
de Vega and Alonso 2017
Beyond the BM approximation, the rates remain non-negative functions of time in case of completely positive divisible dynamics, as proven by the
Gorini–Kossakowski–Sudarshan theorem
.
Gorini, Kossakowski, and Sudarshan 1976

In light of these observations, it has been proposed to measure non-Markovian effects on each channel by studying the negativity of the respective rate.
Hall et al. 2014

This analysis provides in general deeper insight compared to the study of the volume of accessible states [
Eq.

IV.11
].
Hall et al. 2014
;
Chruściński and Maniscalco 2014

In fact, the exponent of
Eq.

IV.15
can be rewritten as
Hall et al. 2014

\tr_{\mathrm{s}}[\Xi(t)]=-2\sum_{i=1,2,3}\gamma_{i}(t).

(IV.20)

In conjunction with
Eq.

IV.15
,
Eq.

IV.20
tells us that
\mathcal{V}(t)
provides information only on the average dissipation occurring on all quantum channels.
The analysis of all rates avoids instead possible compensation effects, which can in particular impair the detection of weak non-Markovian effects.

The knowledge of the full set of STCF for a given system provides complete information required to calculate the canonical dissipation rates.
In fact, the
\gamma_{i}(t)
’s are the eigenvalues of the
decoherence matrix

\xi(t)
, with components

\displaystyle\xi_{ij}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}\tr_{\mathrm{s}}\left[\hat{\sigma}_{\lambda}\hat{\sigma}_{i}\Lambda_{t}[\hat{\sigma}_{\lambda}]\hat{\sigma}_{j}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}\tr_{\mathrm{s}}\left[\hat{\sigma}_{\lambda}\hat{\sigma}_{i}\hat{\sigma}_{\rho}\hat{\sigma}_{j}\right]\Xi_{\rho\lambda}(t),

(IV.21)

related to
\mathcal{C}(t)
via
Eq.

IV.13
.
A proof of
Eq.

IV.21
is shown in
Appendix

D
.

IV.2.4
Generalized quantum master equation

In the following we introduce a dynamical approach to measure non-Markovian effects from the analysis of the GQME.

Equation

II.6a

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\mathcal{C}(t,\tau)=\mathcal{C}(t,\tau)\mathcal{X}(t)-\int_{\tau}^{t}\mathrm{d}\tau^{\prime}\;\mathcal{C}(\tau^{\prime},\tau)\mathcal{K}(t,\tau^{\prime}).

\mathcal{X}(t)

B.17

Both approaches discussed here tend converge towards a Markovian behavior for strong coupling with the environment.
Similarly to the study carried out in

Furthermore, the complementary GQME approach can be effectively utilized to construct a Markovian approximation of the open dynamics in the strong-coupling regime, where revivals of information become negligible or vanish altogether.

V
Conclusions

In this paper we studied the open quantum dynamics of a dissipative–driven qubit, from multiple perspectives and with several approaches.
We integrated the HEOM of the model to propagate the RDM of the qubit over time across a wide range of parameters.
By decomposing the solution of the RDM onto a complete set of correlation functions, we observed that the non-unital part of the dynamics leads to the emergence of non-equilibrium stationary states, involving the same periodicity of the total Hamiltonian.

We calculated a witness of non-Markovianity by measuring the time evolution of the trace distance between two maximally distinguishable RDM’s.
This approach provides a dynamical measure of information flow between the qubit and the external environment.
By tuning the frequency of an periodic monochromatic field, we witnessed interplay between dissipation and drive, leading to pronounced resonant peaks in information backflow.
This study highlights a valuable approach for devising control approaches to alleviate loss of coherence in dissipative quantum dynamics.
da Costa Morazotti et al. 2023
;
Jones, Ladd, and Fong 2012

We noticed that for the model systems studied in this work the analysis of the volume of accessible states fails to witness weak signatures of non-Markovianity occurring on a finite subset of quantum channels.
This issue, as consistently observed in other studies,
Hall et al. 2014
;
Chruściński, Macchiavello, and Maniscalco 2017
;
Mangaud, Meier, and Desouter-Lecomte 2017
is due to the fact that the accessible volume only accounts for the average dissipation occurring over all quantum channels, and can overlook weak non-Markovian signatures.
The contradiction is resolved by decomposing the non-Hermitian part of the open quantum dynamics onto the canonical channels, and by measuring non-Markovian effects from the dissipation rates on each channel.
This numerical analysis allowed us to identify, for sufficiently small values of the system–bath coupling, stable signatures of
eternal non-Markovianity
.
Gulácsi and Burkard 2023
;
Hall et al. 2014
;
Megier et al. 2017

We observed that sufficiently strong drive can enhance non-Markovian effects compared to the non-driven dynamics.
This relevant observation could be effectively leveraged to advance quantum control strategies.

The time-nonlocal GQME is an exact equation of motion for the RDM of an open quantum system.
It provides an interpretation of dissipation in terms of dynamical memory effects, captured by fast-decaying memory kernels.
In this paper we introduced and studied a timescale to relaxation of the kernel as a measure of non-Markovian effects.
We showed that, for our model system and parameter domain, dynamical memory effects in the GQME decrease monotonically for increasing values of the system–bath coupling.
Hence, the GQME formalism can be proficiently utilized as a numerical tool to construct an effective Markovian approximation of open quantum dynamics for a strongly-dissipative bath.

Several questions tackled in the present work are worth further investigation in future research, from both theoretical and technological perspectives.

It has been recently shown how features of eternal non-Markovianity emerge in the dynamics of superconducting qubits, a leading technology in the framework of quantum computing.
Gulácsi and Burkard 2023

It could be highly insightful to apply and extend the analysis of the present work to those systems, while tackling the challenging problem of improving coherence lifetime in multi-qubit gates.
Nakamura and Ankerhold 2024

In this work we focused on the dissipative effects induced by the environment on a small quantum subsystem.
A complementary theoretical approach worth investigating involves a somehow reversed perspective, i.e. the study of how a quantum subsystem affects the dynamics of the environment in a thermal state.
This strategy could lead to meaningful insight, in particular in the development of QEC protocols relying on the design of engineered noise as a control resource.
Kapit 2017

The formalism of the time-local master equation is based on the decomposition of the open quantum dynamics onto a set of time-independent operators, associated to the canonical dissipation channels in the Hilbert space.
It could be insightful to implement a dynamical and topological analysis of these channels to identify dissipation-free subspaces in the spirit of dynamical-decoupling techniques.
Fortunato et al. 2002

Among new perspectives for future research we mention the utilization of variational tools from control theory to design quantum systems subjected to minimal dissipation and stable coherence over long time scales.
Jirari and Pötz 2006
;
Jirari 2020
;
Palao, Kosloff, and Koch 2008

Finally, it can be highly insightful to connect the present analysis to the process-tensor approach, as proposed in Ref.
Pollock et al. 2018a
;
Pollock et al. 2018b
.
The method provides a robust operational criterion for measuring non-Markovian effects by examining the divisibility of quantum processes.
This strategy has proven effective in the investigation of non-Markovian noise in quantum-information devices, and in the development of successful error correction techniques based on dynamical decoupling.
White et al. 2020
;
Berk et al. 2023

Appendix A
High-frequency limit

In this appendix we determine a factorization of the propagator in
Eq.

II.3
which allows us to straightforwardly calculate the
\Omega\to+\infty
limit of the dynamics.

Firstly let us remark that, due to the non-diagonal term
\Delta\hat{\sigma}_{x}
in the total Hamiltonian
\hat{H}(t)
,
it is not possible to remove the time ordering in the integration in
Eq.

II.3
. We can, however, factorize
\hat{U}(t)
in two terms whose
\Omega\to\infty
limit can be straightforwardly calculated.
This is accomplished by introducing the rotating frame (R), defined by the unitary transformation

\displaystyle\hat{U}_{\mathrm{R}}(t)

\displaystyle=\exp_{+}\left\{-i\hat{\sigma}_{z}\varepsilon_{\mathrm{d}}\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{d}t^{\prime}\;\cos(\Omega t^{\prime})\right\}

\displaystyle=\hat{\mathcal{I}}_{\mathrm{s}}\cos\left[\frac{\varepsilon_{\mathrm{d}}\sin(\Omega t)}{\Omega}\right]-i\hat{\sigma}_{z}\sin\left[\frac{\varepsilon_{\mathrm{d}}\sin(\Omega t)}{\Omega}\right],

(A.1)

rotating a quantum state
\ket{\psi(t)}
in phase with an external drive.
The rotated state
\ket{\psi_{\mathrm{R}}(t)}=\hat{U}_{\mathrm{R}}^{\dagger}(t)\ket{\psi(t)}
obeys the Schrödinger equation
Shirai, Mori, and Miyashita 2018

\displaystyle i\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\ket{\psi_{\mathrm{R}}(t)}

\displaystyle=\left[\hat{H}_{\mathrm{R}}(t)-(\hat{\sigma}_{z}\otimes\hat{\mathcal{I}}_{\mathrm{b}})\varepsilon_{\mathrm{d}}\cos(\Omega t)\right]\ket{\psi_{\mathrm{R}}(t)},

(A.2a)

where

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{\mathrm{R}}(t)

\displaystyle=\hat{U}_{\mathrm{R}}^{\dagger}(t)\hat{H}(t)\hat{U}_{\mathrm{R}}(t)

\displaystyle=[\varepsilon_{0}+\varepsilon_{\mathrm{d}}\cos(\Omega t)]\hat{\sigma}_{z}\otimes\hat{\mathcal{I}}_{\mathrm{b}}+\hat{\mathcal{I}}_{\mathrm{s}}\otimes\hat{H}_{\mathrm{b}}+\hat{H}_{\mathrm{sb}}

\displaystyle\quad+\Delta\Bigg[\cos\left(\frac{2\varepsilon_{\mathrm{d}}\sin(\Omega t)}{\Omega}\right)\hat{\sigma}_{x}

\displaystyle\quad-i\sin\left(\frac{2\varepsilon_{\mathrm{d}}\sin(\Omega t)}{\Omega}\right)\hat{\sigma}_{y}\Bigg]\otimes\hat{\mathcal{I}}_{\mathrm{b}}.

(A.3)

Let us introduce now the propagator

\hat{V}_{\mathrm{R}}(t)=\exp_{+}\left\{-i\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{d}t^{\prime}\;\left[\hat{H}_{\mathrm{R}}(t)-(\hat{\sigma}_{z}\otimes\hat{\mathcal{I}}_{\mathrm{b}})\varepsilon_{\mathrm{d}}\cos(\Omega t)\right]\right\},

(A.4)

solving the Schrödinger equation
Eq.

A.2a
.
Given that, for all
\ket{\psi(0)}
,

\displaystyle\ket{\psi(t)}

\displaystyle=\hat{U}(t)\ket{\psi(0)}=\hat{U}_{\mathrm{R}}(t)\ket{\psi_{\mathrm{R}}(t)}=\hat{U}_{\mathrm{R}}(t)\hat{V}_{\mathrm{R}}(t)\ket{\psi(0)},

(A.5)

it follows that

\hat{U}(t)=\hat{U}_{\mathrm{R}}(t)\hat{V}_{\mathrm{R}}(t).

(A.6)

Equation

A.6
allows us to calculate the strong-frequency limit of the driven dynamics

\displaystyle\lim_{\Omega\to+\infty}\hat{U}(t)

\displaystyle=\lim_{\Omega\to+\infty}\hat{V}_{\mathrm{R}}(t)=\lim_{\varepsilon_{\mathrm{d}}\to 0}\hat{U}(t).

(A.7)

Equation

A.7
proves the correspondence between the limits
\Omega\to+\infty
and
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{d}}\to 0
discussed throughout the paper (see e.g.
Fig.

1
).

Appendix B
GQME for dissipative–driven dynamics

B.1
Derivation

The Nakajima–Zwanzig GQME has affirmed itself as a powerful theoretical tool within the framework of open quantum systems and nonadiabatic dynamics.
Nakajima 1958
;
Zwanzig 1960
;
Brian and Sun 2021
;
Montoya-Castillo and Reichman 2016b
;
Kelly, Brackbill, and Markland 2015

Thus far, the approach has been extensively utilized to analyze and interpret non-Markovian effects in model systems described by time-independent Hamiltonians.
The formalism can be straightforwardly extended to the case of dissipative–driven dynamics, where the Hamiltonian involves an explicit dependence on time.
In the present appendix we provide a derivation of the approach in this general case.
The following derivation applies specifically to a two-level subsystem, although it can be extended with no limitations to an arbitrary number
N
of quantum levels (see the concluding remark of
Section

II
).
We follow here the derivation originally presented in Ref.
Haake 1973
, with the main difference that in our case we define projection operators in the spin basis.
The present approach is particularly well suited to tackle the analysis of the spin–spin correlation functions in a variety of models in quantum nonadiabatic dynamics.

Let us consider a time-independent projection superoperator,
\mathcal{P}=\mathcal{P}^{2}
, acting on the total Hilbert space of system and environment.
Its complementary is defined by
\mathcal{Q}=\mathbb{1}-\mathcal{P}
, where
\mathbb{1}
is the identity superoperator.

We aim to derive a non-Markovian equation of motion for the open quantum dynamics of correlation functions of the subsystem.
To do so, we start by decomposing the equation of motion of the two propagator onto the two directions of projection:

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\exp_{-}\left\{i\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\mathrm{d}t^{\prime}\;\mathcal{L}_{t^{\prime}}\right\}=i\exp_{-}\left\{i\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\mathrm{d}t^{\prime}\;\mathcal{L}_{t^{\prime}}\right\}\mathcal{P}\mathcal{L}_{t}

\displaystyle\quad+i\exp_{-}\left\{i\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\mathrm{d}t^{\prime}\;\mathcal{L}_{t^{\prime}}\right\}\mathcal{Q}\mathcal{L}_{t}.

(B.1)

We can now make use of the Dyson identity
Montoya-Castillo and Reichman 2016b

\displaystyle\exp_{-}\left\{\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\mathrm{d}t^{\prime}\;\mathcal{B}_{t^{\prime}}\right\}

\displaystyle=\exp_{-}\left\{-\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\mathrm{d}t^{\prime}\;\mathcal{A}_{t^{\prime}}\right\}-\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\mathrm{d}\tau\;\exp_{-}\left\{-\int_{t_{0}}^{\tau}\mathrm{d}t^{\prime}\;\mathcal{A}_{t^{\prime}}\right\}

\displaystyle\quad\times(\mathcal{A}_{\tau}+\mathcal{B}_{\tau})\exp_{-}\left\{\int_{\tau}^{t}\mathrm{d}t^{\prime}\;\mathcal{B}_{t^{\prime}}\right\},

(B.2)

defined for two generic superoperators
\mathcal{A}_{t}
and
\mathcal{B}_{t}
.
By replacing
\mathcal{A}_{t}=-i\mathcal{L}_{t}
and
\mathcal{B}_{t}=i\mathcal{Q}\mathcal{L}_{t}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\exp_{-}\left\{i\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\mathrm{d}t^{\prime}\;\mathcal{L}_{t^{\prime}}\right\}

\displaystyle=\exp_{-}\left\{i\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\mathrm{d}t^{\prime}\;\mathcal{Q}\mathcal{L}_{t^{\prime}}\right\}+i\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\mathrm{d}\tau\;\exp_{-}\left\{i\int_{t_{0}}^{\tau}\mathrm{d}t^{\prime}\;\mathcal{L}_{t^{\prime}}\right\}

\displaystyle\quad\times\mathcal{P}\mathcal{L}_{\tau}\exp_{-}\left\{i\int_{\tau}^{t}\mathrm{d}t^{\prime}\;\mathcal{Q}\mathcal{L}_{t^{\prime}}\right\},

(B.3)

which can be replaced onto the right-hand side of
Eq.

B.1
to find
Kelly et al. 2016

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\exp_{-}\left\{i\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\mathrm{d}t^{\prime}\;\mathcal{L}_{t^{\prime}}\right\}=i\exp_{-}\left\{i\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\mathrm{d}t^{\prime}\;\mathcal{L}_{t^{\prime}}\right\}\mathcal{P}\mathcal{L}_{t}

\displaystyle\quad+i\exp_{-}\left\{i\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\mathrm{d}t^{\prime}\;\mathcal{Q}\mathcal{L}_{t^{\prime}}\right\}\mathcal{Q}\mathcal{L}_{t}

\displaystyle\quad-\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\mathrm{d}\tau\;\exp_{-}\left\{i\int_{t_{0}}^{\tau}\mathrm{d}t\;\mathcal{L}_{t^{\prime}}\right\}

\displaystyle\quad\times\mathcal{P}\mathcal{L}_{\tau}\exp_{-}\left\{i\int_{\tau}^{t}\mathrm{d}t^{\prime}\;\mathcal{Q}\mathcal{L}_{t^{\prime}}\right\}\mathcal{Q}\mathcal{L}_{t}.

(B.4)

To construct an equation of motion for the STCF
Eq.

II.12
, we consider a Redfield-type projection superoperator
Kelly et al. 2016
spanning the full Hilbert space of the subsystem.
This is written in Liouville space
Mukamel 1999
as

\mathcal{P}=|\hat{\sigma}_{\lambda}\rrangle\llangle\hat{\rho}_{0}\hat{\sigma}_{\lambda}|.

(B.5)

After multiplying
Eq.

B.4
from left and right by
\llangle\hat{\rho}_{0}\hat{\sigma}_{\mu}|
and
|\hat{\sigma}_{\nu}\rrangle
respectively, we obtain the GQME
Eq.

IV.22
, involving the memory kernel

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}_{\mu\nu}(t,\tau)=\llangle\hat{\rho}_{0}\hat{\sigma}_{\mu}|\mathcal{L}_{\tau}\exp_{-}\left\{i\int_{\tau}^{t}\mathrm{d}t^{\prime}\;\mathcal{Q}\mathcal{L}_{t^{\prime}}\right\}\mathcal{Q}\mathcal{L}_{t}|\hat{\sigma}_{\nu}\rrangle

\displaystyle=\llangle\hat{\rho}_{0}\hat{\sigma}_{\mu}|\mathcal{L}_{\tau}\mathcal{Q}\exp_{-}\left\{i\int_{\tau}^{t}\mathrm{d}t^{\prime}\;\mathcal{Q}\mathcal{L}_{t^{\prime}}\right\}\mathcal{Q}\mathcal{L}_{t}|\hat{\sigma}_{\nu}\rrangle,

(B.6)

and drift matrix

\mathcal{X}_{\mu\nu}(t)=i\llangle\hat{\rho}_{0}\hat{\sigma}_{\mu}|\mathcal{L}_{t}|\hat{\sigma}_{\nu}\rrangle.

(B.7)

The present choice of projection operators allows for a convenient simplification in the structure of the open quantum dynamics.
In fact, the matrix elements of the complicated projected dynamics in the second term of the right-hand side of
Eq.

B.4
vanish identically:

\displaystyle i\llangle\hat{\rho}_{0}\hat{\sigma}_{\mu}|\exp_{-}\left\{i\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\mathrm{d}t^{\prime}\;\mathcal{Q}\mathcal{L}_{t^{\prime}}\right\}\mathcal{Q}\mathcal{L}_{t}|\hat{\sigma}_{\nu}\rrangle

\displaystyle=i\llangle\hat{\rho}_{0}\hat{\sigma}_{\mu}|\mathcal{Q}\exp_{-}\left\{i\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\mathrm{d}t^{\prime}\;\mathcal{L}_{t^{\prime}}\mathcal{Q}\right\}\mathcal{Q}\mathcal{L}_{t}|\hat{\sigma}_{\nu}\rrangle

\displaystyle=i\llangle\hat{\rho}_{0}\hat{\sigma}_{\mu}|\exp_{-}\left\{i\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\mathrm{d}t^{\prime}\;\mathcal{L}_{t^{\prime}}\mathcal{Q}\right\}\mathcal{Q}\mathcal{L}_{t}|\hat{\sigma}_{\nu}\rrangle

\displaystyle\quad-i\llangle\hat{\rho}_{0}\hat{\sigma}_{\mu}|\hat{\sigma}_{\lambda}\rrangle\llangle\hat{\rho}_{0}\hat{\sigma}_{\lambda}|\exp_{-}\left\{i\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\mathrm{d}t^{\prime}\;\mathcal{L}_{t^{\prime}}\mathcal{Q}\right\}\mathcal{Q}\mathcal{L}_{t}|\hat{\sigma}_{\nu}\rrangle=0,

(B.8)

where we made use of the orthogonality of the scalar product
\llangle\hat{\rho}_{0}\hat{\sigma}_{\mu}|\hat{\sigma}_{\nu}\rrangle=\tr[\hat{\rho}_{0}\hat{\sigma}_{\mu}\hat{\sigma}_{\nu}]=\delta_{\mu\nu}
.

The memory kernel in
Eq.

B.6
depends on the
projected dynamics, which is hard to simulate numerically, both exactly and with approximated quasiclassical approaches.
Amati et al. 2022

To circumvent this issue, we follow an established strategy originally introduced in Ref.
Shi and Geva 2003
.
In particular, we construct a recursive relation for the memory kernel by inserting again the Dyson identity
Eq.

B.3
onto
Eq.

B.6
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}_{\mu\nu}(t,\tau)=\llangle\hat{\rho}_{0}\hat{\sigma}_{\mu}|\mathcal{L}_{\tau}\mathcal{Q}\exp_{-}\left\{i\int_{\tau}^{t}\mathrm{d}t^{\prime}\;\mathcal{L}_{t^{\prime}}\right\}\mathcal{Q}\mathcal{L}_{t}|\hat{\sigma}_{\nu}\rrangle

\displaystyle\quad-i\int_{\tau}^{t}\mathrm{d}\tau^{\prime}\;\llangle\hat{\rho}_{0}\hat{\sigma}_{\mu}|\mathcal{L}_{\tau}\mathcal{Q}\exp_{-}\left\{i\int_{\tau}^{\tau^{\prime}}\mathrm{d}t^{\prime}\;\mathcal{L}_{t^{\prime}}\right\}|\hat{\sigma}_{\lambda}\rrangle

\displaystyle\quad\times\llangle\hat{\rho}_{0}\hat{\sigma}_{\lambda}|\mathcal{L}_{\tau^{\prime}}\exp_{-}\left\{i\int_{\tau^{\prime}}^{t}\mathrm{d}t^{\prime}\;\mathcal{Q}\mathcal{L}_{t^{\prime}}\right\}\mathcal{Q}\mathcal{L}_{t}|\hat{\sigma}_{\nu}\rrangle,

(B.9)

written in matrix form as

\mathcal{K}(t,\tau)=\mathcal{K}^{(1)}(t,\tau)+\int_{\tau}^{t}\mathrm{d}\tau^{\prime}\;\mathcal{K}^{(3)}(\tau^{\prime},\tau)\mathcal{K}(t,\tau^{\prime}),

(B.10)

where we introduced the two auxiliary kernels

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}_{\mu\nu}^{(1)}(t,\tau)

\displaystyle=\llangle\hat{\rho}_{0}\hat{\sigma}_{\mu}|\mathcal{L}_{\tau}\mathcal{Q}\exp_{-}\left\{i\int_{\tau}^{t}\mathrm{d}t^{\prime}\;\mathcal{L}_{t^{\prime}}\right\}\mathcal{Q}\mathcal{L}_{t}|\hat{\sigma}_{\nu}\rrangle,

(B.11a)

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}_{\mu\nu}^{(3)}(t,\tau)

\displaystyle=-i\llangle\hat{\rho}_{0}\hat{\sigma}_{\mu}|\mathcal{L}_{\tau}\mathcal{Q}\exp_{-}\left\{i\int_{\tau}^{t}\mathrm{d}t^{\prime}\;\mathcal{L}_{t^{\prime}}\right\}|\hat{\sigma}_{\nu}\rrangle.

(B.11b)

Equations

B.10
and
B.11
allow us to conveniently calculate the solution of kernel
Eq.

B.11
via projection-free input correlation functions.

In particular, by expanding the projectors in
Eq.

B.11
we obtain the relations

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}^{(1)}(t,\tau)

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}}{\mathrm{d}t\mathrm{d}\tau}\mathcal{C}(t,\tau)+\mathcal{X}(\tau)\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\mathcal{C}(t,\tau)

\displaystyle\quad-\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\tau}\mathcal{C}(t,\tau)\mathcal{X}(t)-\mathcal{X}(\tau)\mathcal{C}(t,\tau)\mathcal{X}(t),

(B.12a)

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}^{(3)}(t,\tau)

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\tau}\mathcal{C}(t,\tau)+\mathcal{X}(\tau)\mathcal{C}(t,\tau).

(B.12b)

By comparing
Eqs.

B.12a
and
B.12b
, we find a relation between the two auxiliary kernels

\mathcal{K}^{(1)}(t,\tau)=\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\mathcal{K}^{(3)}(t,\tau)-\mathcal{K}^{(3)}(t,\tau)\mathcal{X}(t).

(B.13)

Therefore, to obtain with projection-free input the full kernel
Eq.

B.6
it suffices to calculate directly only
\mathcal{K}^{(3)}(t,\tau)
via
Eq.

II.12
, and then to make use of the identities
Eqs.

B.13
and
B.10
.

B.2
Markovian approximation

The memory kernel
\mathcal{K}(t)
encapsulates complete information on the dynamical effects of the environment on the subsystem.
Hence, one could ask at which extent the kernel relates to the other measures of non-Markovianity discussed in the present work.
To tackle this question, we consider the Born–Markov (BM) approximation of
Eq.

IV.22
,
Breuer and Petruccione 2002
corresponding to the assumption of memory-less dynamics
\mathcal{K}(t,\tau)\propto\delta(t-\tau)
.
This leads to the time-local master equation

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}{\mathcal{C}}_{\mathrm{BM}}(t,\tau)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{BM}}(t,\tau)\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{BM}}(t,\tau),

(B.14a)

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{BM}}(t,\tau)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{X}(t)-\int_{\tau}^{t}\mathrm{d}\tau^{\prime}\;\mathcal{K}(t,\tau^{\prime}),

(B.14b)

which is solved by

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{BM}}(t,\tau)

\displaystyle=[\Phi_{\mathrm{BM}}(t,\tau)\mathcal{I}_{4}]^{T},

(B.15a)

\displaystyle\Phi_{\mathrm{BM}}(t,\tau)

\displaystyle=\exp_{+}\left\{\int_{\tau}^{t}\mathrm{d}t^{\prime}\;\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{BM}}^{T}(t^{\prime},\tau)\right\},

(B.15b)

where we noticed that the initial value
\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{BM}}(0,0)
is equal to the
4\times 4
identity matrix
\mathcal{I}_{4}
.
Note that the BM propagator
\Phi_{\mathrm{BM}}(t,\tau)
, being a time-ordered exponential, is divisible,
J Evans and P Morriss 2007
i.e.,

\Phi_{\mathrm{BM}}(t,0)=\Phi_{\mathrm{BM}}(t,\tau)\Phi_{\mathrm{BM}}(\tau,0),\hskip 8.53581pt0\leq\tau\leq t.

(B.16)

This indicates that the BM approximation of the GQME is consistent with the correspondence between Markovianity and divisibility of the dynamical map, laying at the foundation of all the approaches to quantum dissipation discussed in the present work.
Breuer et al. 2016
;
Rivas, Huelga, and Plenio 2010
;
Chruściński, Kossakowski, and Rivas 2011b
;
Chruściński and Maniscalco 2014

For a non-driven time-independent Hamiltonian,
Eq.

IV.22
simplifies to

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\mathcal{C}(t)=\mathcal{C}(t)\mathcal{X}-\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{d}\tau^{\prime}\;\mathcal{C}(\tau^{\prime})\mathcal{K}(t-\tau^{\prime}),

(B.17)

with corresponding BM approximation

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}{\mathcal{C}}_{\mathrm{BM}}(t)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{BM}}(t)\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{BM}},

(B.18a)

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{BM}}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{X}-\int_{0}^{+\infty}\mathrm{d}\tau^{\prime}\;\mathcal{K}(\tau^{\prime}),

(B.18b)

generating divisible dynamics.
One can prove that
Eq.

B.18
preserves the same long-time limits of the exact dynamics
Eq.

B.17
(see Appendix B of Ref.
Amati et al. 2022
for further details).
This supports the relevance and physical significance of the present Markovian approximation of the GQME.

Appendix C
Canonical form of the time-local master equation

Studying the dynamics of open quantum systems via the time-local master equation
Eq.

II.10
provides a rigorous and well defined strategy to witness the occurrence of non-Markovian effects.
In this appendix we present a detailed derivation of this master equation, following Refs.
Mangaud, Meier, and Desouter-Lecomte 2017
;
Hall et al. 2014
;
Erika Andersson and Hall 2007
.

First of all, we observe that the linearity of the generator
\Lambda_{t}
[
Eq.

II.6
] implies that, if the propagator
\Phi_{t}
is invertible, two sets of operators
\{\hat{A}_{\mu}(t)\}_{\mu}
and
\{\hat{B}_{\mu}(t)\}_{\mu}
exist such that

\displaystyle\Lambda_{t}\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{s}}(t)=\hat{A}_{\mu}(t)\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{s}}(t)\hat{B}_{\mu}^{\dagger}(t).

(C.1)

By expanding

\hat{A}_{\mu}(t)=\hat{\sigma}_{\nu}a_{\nu\mu}(t),\hskip 28.45274pt\hat{B}_{\mu}(t)=\hat{\sigma}_{\nu}b_{\nu\mu}(t),

(C.2)

we rewrite
Eq.

C.1
as

\displaystyle\Lambda_{t}\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{s}}(t)

\displaystyle=a_{\nu\mu}(t)\hat{\sigma}_{\nu}\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{s}}(t)b^{*}_{\lambda\mu}(t)\hat{\sigma}_{\lambda}=\xi_{\nu\lambda}(t)\hat{\sigma}_{\nu}\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{s}}(t)\hat{\sigma}_{\lambda},

(C.3)

where we introduced the
decoherence matrix

\xi(t)
, with components
\xi_{\nu\lambda}(t)=a_{\nu\mu}(t)b^{*}_{\lambda\mu}(t)
[see also
Eq.

IV.21
for an alternative expression].
Given that
\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{s}}(t)
and
\Lambda_{t}\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{s}}(t)
are Hermitian operators,
\xi(t)
is a Hermitian matrix at all times.
In fact,

\displaystyle[\Lambda_{t}\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{s}}(t)]^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=\xi_{\nu\lambda}^{*}(t)\hat{\sigma}_{\lambda}\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{s}}(t)\hat{\sigma}_{\nu}=\xi_{\lambda\nu}^{*}(t)\hat{\sigma}_{\nu}\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{s}}(t)\hat{\sigma}_{\lambda},

(C.4)

and, by comparing
Eqs.

C.3
and
C.4
, it follows that
\xi_{\nu\lambda}(t)=\xi_{\lambda\nu}^{*}(t)
.
To identify in
Eq.

C.3
the Hermitian part of the open dynamics, we expand

\displaystyle\Lambda_{t}\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{s}}(t)

\displaystyle=\xi_{00}(t)\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{s}}(t)+\xi_{i0}(t)\hat{\sigma}_{i}\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{s}}(t)+\xi_{0j}(t)\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{s}}(t)\hat{\sigma}_{j}

\displaystyle\quad+\xi_{ij}(t)\hat{\sigma}_{i}\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{s}}(t)\hat{\sigma}_{j},

(C.5)

and, by defining

\hat{h}_{\mathrm{c}}(t)=\tfrac{1}{2}\xi_{00}(t)\hat{\mathcal{I}}_{\mathrm{s}}+\xi_{i0}(t)\hat{\sigma}_{i},

(C.6)

we recast
Eq.

C.5
as

\displaystyle\Lambda_{t}\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{s}}(t)

\displaystyle=\hat{h}_{\mathrm{c}}(t)\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{s}}(t)+\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{s}}(t)\hat{h}_{\mathrm{c}}^{\dagger}(t)+\xi_{ij}(t)\hat{\sigma}_{i}\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{s}}(t)\hat{\sigma}_{j}.

(C.7)

By taking the trace of
Eq.

C.7
and making use of the trace preservation of the density matrix
(
\tr_{\mathrm{s}}[\Lambda_{t}\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{s}}(t)]=0
), we obtain

\displaystyle\hat{h}_{\mathrm{c}}(t)+\hat{h}_{\mathrm{c}}^{\dagger}(t)=-\xi_{ij}(t)\hat{\sigma}_{j}\hat{\sigma}_{i}.

(C.8)

The Hermitian operator

\hat{H}_{\mathrm{c}}(t)=\frac{i}{2}\left[\hat{h}_{\mathrm{c}}(t)-\hat{h}_{\mathrm{c}}^{\dagger}(t)\right],

(C.9)

generates the unitary part of the dynamics in
Eq.

C.7
:

\displaystyle\Lambda_{t}\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{s}}(t)

\displaystyle=-i[\hat{H}_{\mathrm{c}}(t),\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{s}}(t)]

\displaystyle\quad+\xi_{ij}(t)\left[\hat{\sigma}_{i}\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{s}}(t)\hat{\sigma}_{j}-\tfrac{1}{2}\left\{\hat{\sigma}_{j}\hat{\sigma}_{i},\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{s}}(t)\right\}\right].

(C.10)

The negativity of the eigenvalues of
\xi(t)
provides a measure of the discrepancy from the Markovian limit of the dynamics.
In particular, we can decompose
\xi(t)
in terms of its real eigenvalues
\gamma_{i}(t)
’s and corresponding orthonormal eigenvectors
\bm{u}^{(i)}(t)
’s according to

\displaystyle\xi_{ij}(t)

\displaystyle=u_{i}^{(k)}(t)\gamma_{k}(t)\left[u^{(k)}_{j}(t)\right]^{*}=\mathcal{U}_{ik}(t)\gamma_{k}(t)\mathcal{U}_{ik}^{\dagger}(t),

(C.11)

where
\mathcal{U}_{ij}(t)=u_{i}^{(j)}(t)
.
The time-dependent operators

\displaystyle\hat{L}_{i}(t)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{U}_{ij}(t)\hat{\sigma}_{j},

(C.12)

allow us to rewrite
Eq.

C.10
as

\displaystyle\Lambda_{t}\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{s}}(t)

\displaystyle=-i[\hat{H}_{\mathrm{c}}(t),\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{s}}(t)]

\displaystyle\quad+\left(\mathcal{U}^{\dagger}_{ki}(t)\xi_{ij}(t)\mathcal{U}_{jl}(t)\right)\hat{L}_{k}(t)\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{s}}(t)\hat{L}^{\dagger}_{l}(t)

\displaystyle\quad-\frac{1}{2}\left(\mathcal{U}^{\dagger}_{li}(t)\xi_{ij}(t)\mathcal{U}_{jk}(t)\right)\{\hat{L}^{\dagger}_{k}(t)\hat{L}_{l}(t),\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{s}}(t)\},

(C.13)

which corresponds to
Eq.

II.10
, given that
\left[\mathcal{U}^{\dagger}(t)\xi(t)\mathcal{U}(t)\right]_{ij}=\delta_{ij}\gamma_{j}(t)

Appendix D
Decomposition of superoperators of the subsystem

In the present appendix we include the derivation of
Eq.

IV.21
, an identity relating the canonical rates to the damping matrix
Eq.

IV.13
.
The following proof corresponds to Lemma 2.2 Ref.
Gorini, Kossakowski, and Sudarshan 1976
.

Lemma:
Let us denote by
\{\hat{\pi}_{\mu}\}_{\mu}
a complete orthonormal set (COS) of operators on
\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{s}}
.
For
\hat{O}:\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{s}}\to\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{s}}
, the family of superoperators
\{\mathcal{G}_{\mu\nu}\}_{\mu\nu}
defined by

\hat{O}\mapsto\mathcal{G}_{\mu\nu}\hat{O}=\hat{\pi}_{\mu}\hat{O}\hat{\pi}_{\nu}^{\dagger},

(D.1)

is a COS in
L(\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{s}})
, the linear space of superoperators acting on
\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{s}}
.

Proof:
First of all, we notice that

\hat{\pi}_{\mu}^{\dagger}\hat{O}\hat{\pi}_{\mu}=\hat{\mathcal{I}}_{\mathrm{s}}\tr_{\mathrm{s}}[\hat{O}].

(D.2)

In fact, being the left-hand side of
Eq.

D.2
invariant under a change of COS,
we can replace
\{\hat{\pi}_{\mu}\}_{\mu}
with
\{\hat{E}_{ab}=\ket{a}\bra{b}\}_{a,b=0,1}
, and expand

\hat{E}_{ab}^{\dagger}\hat{O}\hat{E}_{ab}=\ket{b}\braket{a|\hat{O}|a}\bra{b}=\hat{\mathcal{I}}_{\mathrm{s}}\tr[\hat{O}].

(D.3)

L(\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{s}})
is a unitary space with inner product

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}\rangle=\tr_{\mathrm{s}}[(\mathcal{A}\hat{\tau}_{\mu})^{\dagger}(\mathcal{B}\hat{\tau}_{\mu})],\hskip 14.22636pt\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}\in L(\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{s}}),

(D.4)

also independent on any specific choice of COS
\{\hat{\tau}_{\mu}\}_{\mu}
.
We can now prove that
\{\mathcal{G}_{\mu\nu}\}_{\mu\nu}
is a COS in
L(\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{s}})
.
In fact, via
Eq.

D.2
,

\displaystyle\langle{\mathcal{G}}_{\alpha\beta},{\mathcal{G}}_{\mu\nu}\rangle

\displaystyle=\tr_{\mathrm{s}}\left[({\mathcal{G}}_{\alpha\beta}\hat{\tau}_{\lambda})^{\dagger}({\mathcal{G}}_{\mu\nu}\hat{\tau}_{\lambda})\right]

\displaystyle=\tr_{\mathrm{s}}\left[\left(\hat{\pi}_{\alpha}\hat{\tau}_{\lambda}\hat{\pi}_{\beta}^{\dagger}\right)^{\dagger}\left(\hat{\pi}_{\mu}\hat{\tau}_{\lambda}\hat{\pi}_{\nu}^{\dagger}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\tr_{\mathrm{s}}\left[\hat{\pi}_{\beta}(\hat{\tau}^{\dagger}_{\lambda}\hat{\pi}_{\alpha}^{\dagger}\hat{\pi}_{\mu}\hat{\tau}_{\lambda})\hat{\pi}_{\nu}^{\dagger}\right]

\displaystyle=\tr_{\mathrm{s}}[\hat{\pi}_{\alpha}^{\dagger}\hat{\pi}_{\mu}]\tr_{\mathrm{s}}[\hat{\pi}_{\beta}\hat{\pi}_{\nu}^{\dagger}]=\delta_{\alpha\mu}\delta_{\beta\nu}.\;\;\blacksquare

The above result implies that the generator
\Lambda_{t}
can be decomposed on the basis
\{\mathcal{G}_{\mu\nu}\}_{\mu\nu}
according to

\Lambda_{t}\;\bullet=\langle\Lambda_{t},{\mathcal{G}}_{\nu\lambda}\rangle{\mathcal{G}}_{\nu\lambda}\;\bullet.

(D.5)

IV.21

D.5

\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{s}}(t)

\mathcal{G}_{\mu\nu}

\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\{\hat{\sigma}_{\mu}\}_{\mu}

C.3
