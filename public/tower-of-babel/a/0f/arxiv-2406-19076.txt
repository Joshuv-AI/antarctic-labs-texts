Refinable modeling for unbinned SMEFT analyses
Robert Schöfbeck

Abstract
We present methods to estimate systematic uncertainties in unbinned LHC data analyses, focusing on constraining Wilson coefficients in the standard model effective field theory (SMEFT). Our approach also applies to broader parametric models of non-resonant phenomena beyond the standard model (BSM). By using machine-learned surrogates of the likelihood ratio, we extend well-established procedures from binned Poisson counting experiments to the unbinned case. This framework handles various theoretical, modeling, and experimental uncertainties, laying the foundation for future unbinned analyses at the LHC. We also introduce a tree-boosting algorithm that learns precise parametrizations of systematic effects, providing a robust, flexible alternative to neural networks for modeling systematics. We demonstrate this approach with an SMEFT analysis of highly energetic top quark pair production in proton-proton collisions.

Introduction

The Large Hadron Collider (LHC) generates vast amounts of data from particle decays in high-energy interactions, offering a unique opportunity to explore fundamental physics. Recent advances in machine learning (ML) provide powerful tools not only for reconstruction and object-tagging but also for novel analysis techniques. High-dimensional unbinned analyses, where dozens of features probe a large number of model parameters, are now feasible with machine-learned surrogates optimized for hypothesis testing.

In the theoretical domain, the lack of new resonance signals has led to the adoption of the standard model effective field theory (SMEFT)

Nevertheless, most current LHC measurements are straightforward Poisson counting experiments, partially because these reduce the computational demand. Large-scale computing infrastructure has become more accessible, but the application of unbinned techniques is still hampered by the absence of a comprehensive set of tools that bring decades of experience with treating systematic effects in binned Poisson measurements on par with the unbinned case.
The available methodology for treating systematic uncertainties in unbinned simulation-based inference techniques, such as ML optimal observables, is sparse.
While optimal ML observables have a sound footing in well-developed statistical methodology,
the otherwise finely honed procedures for treating systematic uncertainties are rarely seen in this light.
The present work aims to change that situation through a comprehensive statistical interpretation of procedures for treating systematic effects in SBI.
We explain how the factorization of individual systematic effects facilitates the training of multi-variate parametrized regressors and how to address uncertainties in the normalization of processes. The most significant advantage of this approach is its stage-wise nature. Adding new processes or systematic uncertainties does not invalidate partially available training.

The conceptual cornerstone of the modeling of collider phenomena underpinning SBI relies on a hierarchical separation of processes by energy scale, starting from hypothetical UV phenomena and their SMEFT parametrization at
\Lambda_{\textrm{SMEFT}}
.
The SMEFT Wilson coefficients, our POIs, are denoted by
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
, and we aim at parameter inference through frequentist confidence intervals
[
21

.
Those parameters are, therefore, not stochastic. However, we note that there is no conceptual limitation to Bayesian SMEFT analysis.

Figure
1

Collider event simulation mirrors this staging: at the parton level, ME generators sample SMEFT ME-squared terms. Key systematic uncertainties here include unphysical effects from technically unavoidable energy scales linked to the perturbative renormalization of fixed-order predictions and the factorization of collinear and infrared radiation. Uncertainties in the parton distribution functions (PDFs) cover our lack of knowledge in the composition of the
pp
initial state.
At the scale of
\Lambda_{\textrm{QCD}}
, particle-level simulations handle parton shower (PS) effects, fragmentation, hadronization, decay, and underlying event modeling. Tuning these models introduces systematic uncertainties, and modeling generator differences as a “two-point” systematic uncertainty can be effective. At the detector level, simulations include particle interactions with detector material, digitization, trigger logic, and event reconstruction.

Our approach provides a general method to obtain ML-based parametrizations of all these effects by grouping them into classes of systematic variations addressed individually. With these procedures in place, we can build unbinned models that can be iteratively refined. Adding new effects or background contributions does not invalidate surrogates trained on the initial model.

On the technical side, we fill a gap in the methodology by developing a tree-boosting algorithm that can learn arbitrarily accurate parametrizations of systematic effects. This is done by extending tree algorithms to produce regressors that are parametric in externally provided data; in our case, the nuisance parameters (
{\boldsymbol{\nu}}
) linked to systematic effects. The resulting “Boosted Parametric Tree” (BPT) offers a robust and flexible alternative to neural networks for this purpose, with the training procedure fully grounded in unbinned model building.
Therefore, it enables a full understanding of tree-boosting within the context of unbinned hypothesis tests. The terminal nodes of the trees act as measurement bins, allowing for an analytic dependence on nuisance parameters similar to the binned case. The BPT thus learns an expressive surrogate for differential cross-section ratios (DCRs), accommodating a potentially high-dimensional set of model parameters.

3
Unbinned likelihood ratio tests

Given a data set
\mathcal{D}
, confidence level (CL) intervals for the POIs
{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}
are determined from the profiled likelihood ratio test statistic.
In this section, we relate it to quantities that a machine can learn. Splitting the data in a primary set
\mathcal{D}
and an auxiliary set
\mathcal{A}
, we have

\displaystyle q_{{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}}(\mathcal{D})

\displaystyle=-2\log\frac{\max_{{\boldsymbol{\nu}}\phantom{,{\boldsymbol{\nu}}}}L(\mathcal{D},\mathcal{A}|{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}},{\boldsymbol{\nu}})}{\max_{{\boldsymbol{\nu}},{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}}L(\mathcal{D},\mathcal{A}|{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}},{\boldsymbol{\nu}})}=-2\log\frac{L(\mathcal{D},\mathcal{A}|{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}},{{\boldsymbol{\hat{\nu}}}}_{{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}})}{L(\mathcal{D},\mathcal{A}|\boldsymbol{\hat{\theta}},\boldsymbol{\hat{\nu}})\;\;\,}.

(1)

The auxiliary data set has components to constrain systematic uncertainties, such as in the integrated luminosity or the jet energy scale calibration. The maximum-likelihood estimate (MLE) of the nuisance parameters for a given
{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}
is
\boldsymbol{\hat{\nu}}_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}
, while
(\boldsymbol{\hat{\theta}},\boldsymbol{\hat{\nu}})
represents the MLE or all model parameters simultaenously.

By design,
q_{{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}}(\mathcal{D})
is always non-negative for any
{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}
, with larger values indicating greater incompatibility between the model defined by
{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}
and the data
\mathcal{D}
. Without nuisance parameters, the Neyman-Pearson lemma states that a hypothesis test of fixed size
\alpha
based on
q_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathcal{D})
has maximum power, meaning it is most likely to correctly reject the null hypothesis when the alternative is true
[
25
]
.

Technically,
\mathcal{A}
should be an argument of
q_{{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}}(\mathcal{D})
, but we omit it in the notation, as an analytic approximation of the corresponding likelihood factor, described below, will capture all its effects. We assume that
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{D}
do not overlap and that SMEFT effects, parametrized by
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
, are negligible in
\mathcal{A}
. Under this assumption, the auxiliary data set produces a multiplicative term
L(\mathcal{A}|{\boldsymbol{\nu}})
in the likelihood,

\displaystyle L(\mathcal{D},\mathcal{A}|{\boldsymbol{\nu}},{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}})=L(\mathcal{D}|{\boldsymbol{\nu}},{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}})L(\mathcal{A}|{\boldsymbol{\nu}}).

(2)

According to Wilks’ theorem
[
26
]
, if
\mathcal{D}
is distributed under
\boldsymbol{\theta}
, then
q_{{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}}(\mathcal{D})
is asymptotically distributed as a
\chi^{2}_{N_{{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}}}
distribution, where
N_{{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}}
is the number of independent degrees of freedom in
{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}
. This asymptotic distribution is independent of the nuisance parameters, which simplifies the limit-setting procedure and is a primary reason why LHC data analyses commonly use the profiled likelihood ratio test statistic.
The confidence intervals at a confidence level of, e.g.,
1-\alpha=95\%
, are given by solving

\displaystyle q_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}=F^{-1}_{\chi^{2}_{N_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}}(1-\alpha),

(3)

where
F_{\chi^{2}_{N_{{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}}}}
is the cumulative distribution function of the
\chi^{2}_{N_{{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}}}
distribution.

To obtain confidence intervals with some POIs profiled, these parameters are treated as nuisance parameters in
q_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
, reducing
N_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}
accordingly. However, large quadratic terms in the SMEFT expansion can invalidate Wilks’ theorem
[
27
]
. In such cases, the distribution must be determined by other means, such as toy experiments, to ensure the desired confidence level.

The likelihood functions in Eq.
1
are “extended” likelihoods: a Poisson-distributed counting variable describes the random fluctuation in the total number of observed events. The remaining discriminating information is encoded in the fiducial detector-level probability density function (pdf)
p(\boldsymbol{x}|{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}},{\boldsymbol{\nu}})
, which relates to the fiducial differential cross-section as

\displaystyle{\textrm{d}}\Sigma(\boldsymbol{x}|{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}},{\boldsymbol{\nu}})=\sigma({{\boldsymbol{\theta}}},{\boldsymbol{\nu}})\,p(\boldsymbol{x}|{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}},{\boldsymbol{\nu}})\,{\textrm{d}}\boldsymbol{x}.

(4)

We denote the inclusive fiducial cross-section by
\sigma({{\boldsymbol{\theta}}},{\boldsymbol{\nu}})
. In general,
{\textrm{d}}\Sigma(\boldsymbol{x}|{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}},{\boldsymbol{\nu}})
includes contributions from multiple processes.
With the integrated luminosity
\mathcal{L}({\boldsymbol{\nu}})
, subject to systematic uncertainties whose effects we parametrize by nuisance parameters, the likelihood to observe a data set
\mathcal{D}
of size
N
can, in general, be written in terms of the differential cross-section as

\displaystyle L(\mathcal{D}|{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}},{\boldsymbol{\nu}})

\displaystyle=\textrm{P}_{\mathcal{L}({\boldsymbol{\nu}})\sigma({{\boldsymbol{\theta}}},{\boldsymbol{\nu}})}(N)\prod_{i=1}^{N}p(\boldsymbol{x}_{i}|{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}},{\boldsymbol{\nu}}){}=\textrm{P}_{\mathcal{L}({\boldsymbol{\nu}})\sigma({{\boldsymbol{\theta}}},{\boldsymbol{\nu}})}(N)\prod_{i=1}^{N}\frac{1}{\sigma({{\boldsymbol{\theta}}},{\boldsymbol{\nu}})}\frac{{\textrm{d}}\Sigma(\boldsymbol{x}_{i}|{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}},{\boldsymbol{\nu}})}{{\textrm{d}}\boldsymbol{x}},

(5)

where
\textrm{P}_{\lambda}
denotes the Poisson distribution with mean
\lambda
.
The extended log-likelihood ratio for two sets of model parameters becomes

\displaystyle\log\frac{L(\mathcal{D}|{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{1}},{{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{1}})}{L(\mathcal{D}|{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}},{{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{0}})}

\displaystyle=-\mathcal{L}({{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{1}})\sigma({\boldsymbol{\theta}_{1}},{{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{1}})+\mathcal{L}({{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{0}})\sigma({\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}},{{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{0}})+\sum_{i=1}^{N}\log\left(\frac{\mathcal{L}({{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{1}})}{\mathcal{L}({{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{0}})}\frac{{\textrm{d}}\Sigma(\boldsymbol{x}_{i}|{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{1}},{{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{1}})}{{\textrm{d}}\Sigma(\boldsymbol{x}_{i}|{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}},{{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{0}})}\right).

(6)

Each of the
K
sources of systematic uncertainty is associated with a nuisance parameter
\nu_{k}
, collectively denoted by
{\boldsymbol{\nu}}
. Systematic effects related to detector calibration, the measurement of the integrated luminosity, theoretical calculations, and more are modeled with these nuisance parameters. Measurements can constrain some of these uncertainties through the observed auxiliary data set
\mathcal{A}_{0}
, which is the specific instance of
\mathcal{A}
found in real data and, therefore, not a random quantity
[
22
]
.
We set
{\boldsymbol{\nu}}={\mathbf{0}}
to correspond to the maximum of
L(\mathcal{A}_{0}|{\boldsymbol{\nu}})
, so that by definition,

\displaystyle\max_{\boldsymbol{\nu}}L(\mathcal{A}_{0}|{\boldsymbol{\nu}})=L(\mathcal{A}_{0}|{\mathbf{0}}).

(7)

The central value
{\boldsymbol{\nu}}={\mathbf{0}}
represents the best available calibrations across all modeling aspects before considering
\mathcal{D}
. The nuisance parameters then parametrize deviations from this hypothesis. Combined with
{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}={\mathbf{0}}
, this choice defines an SM reference hypothesis with likelihood

\displaystyle L(\mathcal{D},\mathcal{A}|\textrm{SM})\equiv L(\mathcal{D}|{\mathbf{0}},{\mathbf{0}})L(\mathcal{A}|{\mathbf{0}}),

(8)

which describes the SM without any SMEFT effects and includes the best available calibrations.
We can parametrize the systematic effects to make the
\nu_{k}
as uncorrelated as possible and scale them so that the auxiliary log-likelihood ratio becomes a simple analytic expression in terms of
{\boldsymbol{\nu}}
. In the Gaussian approximation, this is achieved by diagonalizing the Hessian of the auxiliary likelihood function at
{\boldsymbol{\nu}}={\mathbf{0}}
, resulting in a penalty of the form

\displaystyle-2\log\frac{L(\mathcal{A}|{\boldsymbol{\nu}})}{L(\mathcal{A}|{\mathbf{0}})}=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\nu_{k}^{2},

(9)

though generalizations to other probability distributions are possible.

If a specific nuisance parameter is only constrained by the primary data set and not by
\mathcal{A}_{0}
, it is excluded from the penalty term in Eq.
9
and referred to as “floating”. While some uncertainties, such as those related to PDFs, are clearly interpretable in terms of SM parameters, this is not always the case. For example, uncertainties from renormalization or factorization scales address limitations in perturbative accuracy but do not guarantee statistical coverage when these scales vary in the simulation.
With this caveat in mind, we treat all systematic uncertainties heuristically in the same way.

We normalize the likelihoods in Eq.
1
by dividing both the numerator and denominator by the SM reference likelihood in Eq.
8
. This yields

\displaystyle q_{{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}}(\mathcal{D})=\min_{{\boldsymbol{\nu}}}u(\mathcal{D},\mathcal{A}|{\boldsymbol{\nu}},{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}})-\min_{{\boldsymbol{\nu}},{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}}u(\mathcal{D},\mathcal{A}|{\boldsymbol{\nu}},{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}),

(10)

where

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}u(\mathcal{D},\mathcal{A}|{\boldsymbol{\nu}},{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}})

\displaystyle=-\mathcal{L}({\boldsymbol{\nu}})\,\sigma({{\boldsymbol{\theta}}},{\boldsymbol{\nu}})+\mathcal{L}_{0}\,\sigma(\textrm{SM})+\sum_{i=1}^{N(\mathcal{D})}\log\left(\frac{\mathcal{L}({\boldsymbol{\nu}})}{\mathcal{L}_{0}}\frac{{\textrm{d}}\Sigma(\boldsymbol{x}_{i}|{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}},{\boldsymbol{\nu}})}{{\textrm{d}}\Sigma(\boldsymbol{x}_{i}|\textrm{SM})}\right)-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\nu_{k}^{2},

(11)

and
\mathcal{L}_{0}
denotes the central value of the auxiliary luminosity measurement.

The main drawback of the unbinned likelihood ratio test statistic in Eq.
11
is the need to evaluate the DCR, inclusive cross-section, and integrated luminosity as functions of the model parameters. While log-normal (multiplicative) nuisances effectively model the integrated luminosity dependence around the central value
[
28
,
29
]
, we also require estimates for the DCR and inclusive cross-section.
Event generators cannot provide these estimates parametrically in terms of model parameters, as they operate in “forward” mode through sequential stochastic processes. However, for inference, the minimization in Eq.
1
requires to evaluate the DCR for externally provided simulated or real events.

4
Simulation for inference

Although computationally intensive, robust predictions from Monte Carlo (MC) simulations are invaluable for modeling highly energetic scattering processes
[
30
]
.
Our strategy is to provide the necessary parametric estimates by breaking down the primary task of modeling the DCR into smaller, manageable machine-learning tasks. The tasks are based on simulation and capitalize on the high quality of the MC methods. As illustrated in Figure
2
, we proceed in the backward mode, from left to right, and separate the training into distinct parton-level processes that are simulated separately.
For each process, we learn parametrizations of systematic effects and POI dependencies with the help of efficiently generated “synthetic” data sets that correspond to the systematic variations in the binned approach.
Inheriting these procedures, we leverage the extensive experience from over a decade of binned LHC data analyses.

Training tasks for systematics can be divided into uncorrelated groups of nuisance parameters which then separately estimate these effects. A high granularity allows a gradual refinement of each aspect of the final model without invalidating unrelated tasks.
In the following sections, we describe how the results of ML training tasks combine into a refinable surrogate model
{\textrm{d}}\Sigma(\boldsymbol{x}|{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}},{\boldsymbol{\nu}})
that provides the needed DCR in Eq.
11
.

4.1
Hierarchical data representations and staged event simulation

Systematic effects can modify predictions at any energy scale. As shown in Fig.

4.2
Semi-analytic modeling at the parton level

At the parton level, the ME generators provide a (possibly weighted) sampling of the ME-squared SMEFT terms.
The generic parton-level DCR for a single process is

\displaystyle{\textrm{d}}\sigma_{\textrm{SMEFT}}(\boldsymbol{z}_{p}|{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}},\nu_{R},\nu_{F},{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{\textrm{PDF}})\propto\sum_{f_{1},f_{2},h}\left|\mathcal{M}_{\textrm{SMEFT}}(\boldsymbol{z}_{p},h|{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}},\mu_{R}(\nu_{R}),\mu_{F}(\nu_{F}))\right|^{2}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\times\textrm{PDF}(f_{1},x_{\textrm{Bjorken},1},\mu_{F}(\nu_{F}),{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{\textrm{PDF}})\textrm{PDF}(f_{2},x_{\textrm{Bjorken},2},\mu_{F}(\nu_{F}),{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{\textrm{PDF}}){\textrm{d}}\boldsymbol{z}_{p},

(14)

where
\mu_{R}
and
\mu_{F}
are the renormalization and factorization scales, respectively.
For single-operator insertions, the dependence on the SMEFT Wilson coefficients
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
is accurately described by a quadratic polynomial.
The flavors of the incoming partons are denoted by
f_{1/2}
and take values in
\{\mathrm{u},\overline{\mathrm{u}},\mathrm{d},\overline{\mathrm{d}},\mathrm{c},\overline{\mathrm{c}},\mathrm{s},\overline{\mathrm{s}},\mathrm{b},\overline{\mathrm{b}},\mathrm{g}\}

x_{\textrm{Bjorken},1/2}
.
The relevant latent parton-level configuration, sufficient for evaluating the parton distribution functions (PDFs)
[

]
, is then given by
\{f_{1},f_{2},x_{\textrm{Bjorken},1},x_{\textrm{Bjorken},2}\}
.
Equation
14
also sums over the initial and final-state helicity configurations, denoted by
h
. This choice removes the helicity configuration from the parton-level latent-space event representation
\boldsymbol{z}_{\textrm{p}}
and is called ‘‘helicity-ignorant’’

Helicity-aware and helicity-ignorant SMEFT predictions are compared in Ref.

]
.
in the context of reweighted predictions
[

]
. A list of four-momenta then represents the final state parton-level dynamics, and we arrive at the parton-level representation

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{z}_{\textrm{p}}=\{f_{1},f_{2},x_{\textrm{Bjorken},1},x_{\textrm{Bjorken},2},p^{\mu}_{1},p^{\mu}_{2},\ldots\}.

(15)

If the helicity configuration is kept (“helicity-aware”
[
48

) and enters the particle-level simulation,
h
is also included. The only required change in Eq.
14
in that case is to use a separate differential
{\textrm{d}}\boldsymbol{z}_{\textrm{p}}^{(h)}
for each helicity configuration
[
7
]
.

Uncertainties in the PDFs can be expressed as variations along Hessian eigen-directions of an underlying parametrization
[
47
]
, with the corresponding nuisance parameters denoted by
{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{\textrm{PDF}}
. These PDF variations represent different hypotheses about proton parton dynamics and have a clear physical interpretation. In contrast, the dependence on
\mu_{R}
and
\mu_{F}
arises from technical artifacts, namely the finite perturbative order and the separation of collinear radiation between the ME and the PDF.
Despite this, we treat scale uncertainties with standard nuisance parameters
\nu_{R}
and
\nu_{F}
, corresponding to up and down variations around the central values
\mu_{R,0}
and
\mu_{F,0}
. If values of
\pm 1
correspond to variation factors of 2, then

\displaystyle\mu_{R}(\nu_{R})=2^{\nu_{R}}\mu_{R,0}\quad\textrm{and}\quad\mu_{F}(\nu_{F})=2^{\nu_{F}}\mu_{F,0}.

(16)

We account for uncertainties in the overall normalization of the process with log-normal nuisances in Sec.
6
and will not discuss them here. However, we include a general parton-level ad-hoc reweighting function
\alpha_{\textrm{rw}}(\boldsymbol{z}_{\textrm{p}},{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{\textrm{rw}})
, useful when higher-order perturbative corrections significantly depend on the parton-level configuration and we want to adjust the parton-level distribution with an ad-hoc modification.
The total parton-level prediction is then written as

\displaystyle{\textrm{d}}\sigma(\boldsymbol{z}_{p}|{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}},{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{\textrm{p}})

\displaystyle=\alpha_{\textrm{rw}}(\boldsymbol{z}_{\textrm{p}},{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{\textrm{rw}})\,{\textrm{d}}\sigma_{\textrm{SMEFT}}(\boldsymbol{z}_{p}|{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}},\nu_{R},\nu_{F},{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{\textrm{PDF}})

(17)

An example of this type is the modeling of the transverse top quark momenta in the
\textrm{t}\bar{\textrm{t}}
process, which is well understood from higher-order pertubation theory
[
49
]
, but is not yet available from SMEFT ME generators. The nuisances
{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{\textrm{rw}}
modify parameters in the reweighting function
\alpha_{\textrm{rw}}(\boldsymbol{z}_{p},{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{\textrm{rw}})
within their uncertainties. Since such procedures are highly application-dependent, we do not elaborate further, except to note that
\alpha_{\textrm{rw}}(\boldsymbol{z}_{p},{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{\textrm{rw}})
must always be positive.
The parton-level nuisance parameters considered so far are

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{\textrm{p}}=\{\nu_{R},\nu_{F},{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{\textrm{PDF}},{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{\textrm{rw}}\}

(18)

and are associated with systematic effects that can be modeled semi-analytically, allowing for efficient computation.

We use Eq.
17
to define a parton-level pdf and the inclusive parton-level cross-section generically as

\displaystyle{\textrm{d}}\sigma(\boldsymbol{z}_{\textrm{p}}|{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}},{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{\textrm{p}})=\bar{\sigma}({{\boldsymbol{\theta}}},{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{\textrm{p}})\,p(\boldsymbol{z}_{\textrm{p}}|{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}},{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{\textrm{p}})\,{\textrm{d}}\boldsymbol{z}_{\textrm{p}}.

(19)

The bar on
\bar{\sigma}({{\boldsymbol{\theta}}},{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{\textrm{p}})
indicates that the inclusive cross-section pertains to the entire kinematic phase-space, unaffected, e.g., by the finite detector acceptance. By definition, we have

\displaystyle\int d\sigma(\boldsymbol{z}_{\textrm{p}}|{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}},{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{\textrm{p}})=\bar{\sigma}({{\boldsymbol{\theta}}},{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{\textrm{p}})\quad\textrm{and}\quad\int{\textrm{d}}\boldsymbol{z}_{\textrm{p}}\,p(\boldsymbol{z}_{\textrm{p}}|{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}},{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{\textrm{p}})=1,

(20)

which differs from the fiducial cross-section
\sigma({{\boldsymbol{\theta}}},{\boldsymbol{\nu}})
in Eq.
4
by the acceptance effects and the event selection from the subsequent modeling stages, in particular at the detector level.
With an ME generator, we obtain a possibly weighted sample of identically and independently distributed events from Eq.
17
as

\displaystyle\{w_{i},\boldsymbol{z}_{p,i}\}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textrm{i.i.d.}}}{{\sim}}\bar{\sigma}({{\boldsymbol{\theta}}},{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{\textrm{p}})\,p(\boldsymbol{z}_{\textrm{p}}|{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{\textrm{p}}).

(21)

The overall normalization of weights
w_{i}
can be set to
\sum w_{i}=\bar{\sigma}({{\boldsymbol{\theta}}},{\boldsymbol{\nu}})
.

These parton-level systematic effects enable tractable simulation, allowing an inexpensive way to modify an existing sample, such as by reweighting, to approximate model parameters beyond the nominal values. Many other effects, such as the choice of different ME generators, do not allow for tractable simulation. If we model differences between such “two point alternatives” with nuisance parameters, it becomes impossible to compute the likelihood ratio for one generator choice while sampling from another.
However, in Sec.
5
, we show how to use machine learning to create parametrizations that interpolate between two point modeling alternatives using a nuisance parameter. This approach is crucial in practice, as uncertainties, especially those in modeling nonperturbative aspects of QCD, are often of this type.

4.3
Forward-mode event generation at particle and reconstruction level

Particle-level simulation, staged as described in Sec.
4.1
, includes the PS, initial- and final-state radiation, multiple-parton interactions, color reconnection, hadronization, the underlying event, and hadron decay. The subsequent reconstruction-level simulation models the detector interaction and event reconstruction.
For each parton-level event
\{w_{i},\boldsymbol{z}_{\textrm{p},i}\}
, the particle and detector-level simulations, along with observable reconstruction, produce an event representation of the form

\displaystyle\{w_{i},\boldsymbol{x}_{i},\boldsymbol{z}_{\textrm{reco},i},\boldsymbol{z}_{\textrm{ptl},i},\boldsymbol{z}_{\textrm{p},i}\}.

(22)

Due to finite detector acceptance, some events will not pass the event selection. Poor reconstruction performance near reconstruction thresholds motivates defining a fiducial region, denoted by
\mathcal{X}
. Analysis-specific selections, including reliable object-level calibrations, background reduction, and prior knowledge of SMEFT sensitivity, are incorporated into the definition of
\mathcal{X}
. We only require that for a real event, it is possible to determine if it belongs in
\mathcal{X}
.
In particular,
\mathcal{X}
can include requirements on latent variables like
\boldsymbol{z}_{\textrm{reco}}
: online selection acceptance, thresholds on reconstructed object properties, and various data-cleaning event vetoes are part of the definition of
\mathcal{X}
, even if these variables are typically not in
\boldsymbol{x}
.

In contrast to the parton level, the particle and reconstruction-level simulation is computationally expensive, and most effects at these stages can not be simulated tractably. Event samples are, therefore, only available for a limited set of model parameters for different
{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{\textrm{ptl}}
and
{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{\textrm{reco}}
.
For each such configuration, the event sample is written as

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}({{\boldsymbol{\theta}}},{\boldsymbol{\nu}})=\{w_{i},\boldsymbol{x}_{i},\boldsymbol{z}_{\textrm{reco},i},\boldsymbol{z}_{\textrm{ptl},i},\boldsymbol{z}_{\textrm{p},i}\}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textrm{i.i.d.}}}{{\sim}}\sigma({{\boldsymbol{\theta}}},{\boldsymbol{\nu}})\,p(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{z}|{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}},{\boldsymbol{\nu}})\quad\textrm{if}\quad\{\boldsymbol{x}_{i},\boldsymbol{z}_{\textrm{reco},i}\}\in\mathcal{X},

(23)

where the total fiducial cross-section in Eq.
23
is

\displaystyle\sigma({{\boldsymbol{\theta}}},{\boldsymbol{\nu}})=\sum_{\boldsymbol{x}_{i},\boldsymbol{z}_{\textrm{reco},i}\in\mathcal{X}}w_{i}.

(24)

There is a conceptual difference between Eq.
21
and Eq.
23
. While the parton-level distribution in Eq.
21
on the r.h.s. is analytically known and used in the MC sampling, Eq.
23
should be understood in the reverse direction. The simulated sample approximates the joint pdf in the fiducial region on the r.h.s., which is unavailable otherwise.
Concretely, the formal approximation of the joint pdf is

\displaystyle\sigma({{\boldsymbol{\theta}}},{\boldsymbol{\nu}})\,p(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{z}|{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}},{\boldsymbol{\nu}})\approx\sum_{\mathcal{D}({{\boldsymbol{\theta}}},{\boldsymbol{\nu}})}w_{i}\delta(\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{x}_{i})\delta(\boldsymbol{z}-\boldsymbol{z}_{i}),

(25)

and we can interpret the event weights as

\displaystyle w_{i}=\sigma({{\boldsymbol{\theta}}},{\boldsymbol{\nu}})p(\boldsymbol{x}_{i},\boldsymbol{z}_{i}|{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}},{\boldsymbol{\nu}}).

(26)

At NLO, the generated samples are necessarily weighted at the ME stage, and the weights can partly be negative
[
50
]
. Negative weights, in principle, invalidate the interpretation in Eq.
26
, but Eq.
25
still holds, provided the large sample limit is respected.

To connect to the binned analyses, the expected yield in a given bin
\Delta\boldsymbol{x}\subset\mathcal{X}
is given by
\lambda_{\Delta\boldsymbol{x}}({{\boldsymbol{\theta}}},{\boldsymbol{\nu}})=\mathcal{L}({\boldsymbol{\nu}})\sigma_{\Delta\boldsymbol{x}}({{\boldsymbol{\theta}}},{\boldsymbol{\nu}})
with

\displaystyle\sigma_{\Delta\boldsymbol{x}}({{\boldsymbol{\theta}}},{\boldsymbol{\nu}})

\displaystyle=\int_{\Delta\boldsymbol{x}}{\textrm{d}}\boldsymbol{x}\frac{{\textrm{d}}\sigma(\boldsymbol{x}|{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}},{\boldsymbol{\nu}})}{{\textrm{d}}\boldsymbol{x}}=\sigma({{\boldsymbol{\theta}}},{\boldsymbol{\nu}})\int_{\Delta\boldsymbol{x}}{\textrm{d}}\boldsymbol{x}\,p(\boldsymbol{x}|{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}},{\boldsymbol{\nu}})

\displaystyle=\sigma({{\boldsymbol{\theta}}},{\boldsymbol{\nu}})\int_{\Delta\boldsymbol{x}}{\textrm{d}}\boldsymbol{x}\int{\textrm{d}}\boldsymbol{z}\,p(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{z}|{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}},{\boldsymbol{\nu}})\approx\sum_{\boldsymbol{x}_{i}\in\mathcal{D}({{\boldsymbol{\theta}}},{\boldsymbol{\nu}})\,\cap\,\Delta\boldsymbol{x}}w_{i},

(27)

where the sum extends over all events within
\mathcal{D}({\boldsymbol{\nu}},{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}})
that fall in the volume
\Delta\boldsymbol{x}
.
We denote this selection by
\mathcal{D}({{\boldsymbol{\theta}}},{\boldsymbol{\nu}})\cap\Delta\boldsymbol{x}
. Cross-section weighted expectation values, used to define the loss functions in Sec.
5
, are approximated as

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{O}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{z})\rangle_{\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{z}|{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}},{\boldsymbol{\nu}}}\equiv\int{\textrm{d}}\boldsymbol{x}\,{\textrm{d}}\boldsymbol{z}\,\sigma({{\boldsymbol{\theta}}},{\boldsymbol{\nu}})\,p(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{z}|{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}},{\boldsymbol{\nu}})\,\mathcal{O}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{z})\approx\sum_{\mathcal{D}({{\boldsymbol{\theta}}},{\boldsymbol{\nu}})}\,w_{i}\mathcal{O}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i},\boldsymbol{z}_{i}).

(28)

We emphasize that the per-event weights
w_{i}
in Eq.
28
are typically only known for a small set of model parameter configurations.

4.4
Synthetic data sets and tractable simulation

\boldsymbol{x}
and
\boldsymbol{z}
, modifies the event weight and, thereby, tractably changes the distributions of both the latent and observed event features. Events in the region “NA” fail acceptance, and the region “OF” depicts an overflow bin. Middle: A function
J_{{\boldsymbol{\nu}}}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{z})
provides variations of observed features, for example, related to uncertainties in the calibration of the underlying reconstructed objects. Simulated samples are computationally expensive. While it is always possible to obtain a simulation based on Eq.
23
for a specific model parameter configuration
({\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}},{{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{0}})
, it is practically important to know whether we can efficiently generate a new simulated (synthetic) data set from an existing one when
{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}\neq{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}}
or
{\boldsymbol{\nu}}\neq{{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{0}}
for some model parameters. In such cases, the simulation is called “tractable” for these parameters.

We discuss two tractable cases: likelihood-based reweighting and variations in the calibration of the reconstructed objects. Since SMEFT effects can also be modeled tractably, we address this separately. If a simulation is non-tractable for a model parameter, we must use Eq.
23
to obtain systematically varied data sets. A visualization of these approaches is shown in Fig.
3
.

4.4.1
Uncertainties in the calibration of reconstructed objects

An important type of tractable simulation addresses uncertainties in the calibration of underlying object properties, such as jet and lepton momenta or the discriminator value of a
\mathrm{b}
-tagging algorithm. Variations of
\boldsymbol{x}
from these uncertainties are obtained by recomputing it based on modified event properties, defining a function
\boldsymbol{x}_{\boldsymbol{\nu}}=J_{{\boldsymbol{\nu}}}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{z})
. This function provides adjusted values of
\boldsymbol{x}
that depend on latent object-level and event properties, so
J_{{\boldsymbol{\nu}}}
also depends on
\boldsymbol{z}
.

Evaluating
J_{\boldsymbol{\nu}}
provides information on how the observation changes as a function of the model parameters, but it does not give the likelihood or cross-section ratio as a function of the model parameters for a fixed observation, which is needed for Eq.
11
.
For simulated data at a reference parameter point
\{w_{i},\boldsymbol{x}_{i},\boldsymbol{z}_{i}\}\mathbin{\overset{\textrm{iid}}{\kern 0.0pt\sim}}p(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{z}|{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}},{{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{0}})
, applying
J_{\boldsymbol{\nu}}
instead implies

\displaystyle p(\boldsymbol{x}_{i},\boldsymbol{z}_{i}|{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}},{{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{0}})=p\left(J_{\boldsymbol{\nu}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i},\boldsymbol{z}_{i}),\boldsymbol{z}_{i}|{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}},{\boldsymbol{\nu}}\right),

(29)

meaning the joint likelihood remains unchanged with
{\boldsymbol{\nu}}
when we simultaneously modify the observation to
\boldsymbol{x}_{{\boldsymbol{\nu}},i}=J_{{\boldsymbol{\nu}}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i},\boldsymbol{z}_{i})
.
Synthetic data samples can be efficiently generated as

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}({\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}},{\boldsymbol{\nu}})=\{w_{i},\boldsymbol{x}_{{\boldsymbol{\nu}},i}=J_{{\boldsymbol{\nu}}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i},\boldsymbol{z}_{i}),\boldsymbol{z}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N_{\textrm{sim}}}\quad\textrm{for all}\quad\{w_{i},\boldsymbol{x}_{i},\boldsymbol{z}_{i}\}\in\mathcal{D}({\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}},{{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{0}}),

(30)

and are enough to learn a DCR surrogate as a function of
{\boldsymbol{\nu}}
, as discussed in Sec.
5
. We assume that calibration-type uncertainties are independent of the POIs, so
{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}}
appears on both sides of Eq.
29
.

4.4.2
Synthetic data from event-reweighting

When the change in the likelihood of observing an event as a function of a specific model parameter can be computed without resampling the pdf, we can generate reweighted synthetic data sets as

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}({{\boldsymbol{\theta}}},{\boldsymbol{\nu}})=\{w_{i}\times r(\boldsymbol{x}_{i},\boldsymbol{z}_{i}|{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}},{\boldsymbol{\nu}},{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}},{{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{0}}),\boldsymbol{x}_{i},\boldsymbol{z}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N_{\textrm{sim}}}\quad\textrm{for all}\quad\{w_{i},\boldsymbol{x}_{i},\boldsymbol{z}_{i}\}\in\mathcal{D}({\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}},{{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{0}}).

(31)

A reweighting function
r(\boldsymbol{x}_{i},\boldsymbol{z}_{i}|{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}},{\boldsymbol{\nu}},{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}},{{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{0}})
must be available for the corresponding model parameter and can depend on both observables and latent features. Important tractable cases occur at the parton level, where access to the analytic form of ME-squared terms allows computation of the joint likelihood ratio.
In addition to parton-level parametrizations of the form

\displaystyle r(\boldsymbol{x}_{i},\boldsymbol{z}_{i}|{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}},{\boldsymbol{\nu}})=\alpha_{\textrm{rw}}(\boldsymbol{z}_{i,\textrm{p}},{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{\textrm{rw}})

(32)

designed to approximate higher-order perturbative corrections as in Eq.
17
, it is useful to describe uncertainties related to other theoretical inaccuracies with nuisance parameters. For example, the predicted rates of events with high particle-level jet multiplicity (
N_{\textrm{gen-jet}}
) depend on the specifics of matching between the ME calculation and the PS
[
51
]
, often with significant uncertainties. To address this, we can introduce ad-hoc uncertainties for events with different particle-level jet multiplicities through the variation

\displaystyle r(\boldsymbol{x}_{i},\boldsymbol{z}_{i}|{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}},{\boldsymbol{\nu}})=1+\alpha_{1}^{\nu_{\textrm{gen-jet}}}\delta_{1,N_{\textrm{gen-jet}}}+\alpha_{2}^{\nu_{\textrm{gen-jet}}}\delta_{2,N_{\textrm{gen-jet}}}+\cdots

(33)

where
\alpha_{1}
,
\alpha_{2}
, etc., are constants, and
\nu_{\textrm{gen-jet}}
is the associated nuisance parameter.

A special case is reweighting functions that depend solely on
\boldsymbol{x}
. In this case, we can access the DCR as a function of the observed features without requiring any learning.
For example, consider a selection with a fixed lepton multiplicity
N_{\ell}
, where the reconstruction efficiency has a relative uncertainty
\Delta\textrm{SF}(\ell)/\textrm{SF}(\ell)
, and a corrective scale factor
\textrm{SF}(\ell)
is already included in the nominal simulation. We treat the uncertainty
\Delta\textrm{SF}(\ell)
with a nuisance parameter
\nu_{\ell}
. If
\boldsymbol{x}
includes the properties of the leptons, allowing
\Delta\textrm{SF}(\ell)
to be computed solely from
\boldsymbol{x}
, we have

\displaystyle r(\boldsymbol{x}_{i}|\nu_{\ell})=\prod_{i=1}^{N_{\ell}}\left(1+\frac{\Delta\textrm{SF}(\ell_{i})}{\textrm{SF}(\ell_{i})}\right)^{\nu_{\ell}},

(34)

which provides the DCR as a function of
\nu_{\ell}
without needing a surrogate. However, if
\boldsymbol{x}
alone is insufficient to compute the scale factors, the right-hand side of Eq.
34
represents
r(\boldsymbol{x}_{i},\boldsymbol{z}_{i}|\nu)
rather than
r(\boldsymbol{x}_{i}|\nu)
, and a surrogate is required.

4.4.3
SMEFT modeling

SMEFT effects can be modeled with synthetic data, which initially motivated the development of optimal SMEFT observables
[
13
,
14
,
15
,
16
,
17
,
18
]
. The ME in Eq.
14
can be efficiently recomputed as a function of the POIs
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
. The SMEFT dependence of the DCR is

\displaystyle r(\boldsymbol{x}_{i},\boldsymbol{z}_{i}|{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}},{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}})

(35)

\displaystyle=\frac{\sigma({{\boldsymbol{\theta}}})\;\;\;\,}{\sigma(\textrm{SM})}\frac{p(\boldsymbol{x}_{i},\boldsymbol{z}_{\textrm{reco},i},\boldsymbol{z}_{\textrm{ptl},i},\boldsymbol{z}_{\textrm{p},i}|{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}})\;\;\;\,}{p(\boldsymbol{x}_{i},\boldsymbol{z}_{\textrm{reco},i},\boldsymbol{z}_{\textrm{ptl},i},\boldsymbol{z}_{\textrm{p},i}|\textrm{SM})}=\frac{\sigma({{\boldsymbol{\theta}}})\;\;\;\,}{\sigma(\textrm{SM})}\frac{p(\boldsymbol{x}|\boldsymbol{z}_{\textrm{reco}})}{p(\boldsymbol{x}|\boldsymbol{z}_{\textrm{reco}})}\frac{p(\boldsymbol{z}_{\textrm{reco}}|\boldsymbol{z}_{\textrm{ptl}})}{p(\boldsymbol{z}_{\textrm{reco}}|\boldsymbol{z}_{\textrm{ptl}})}\frac{p(\boldsymbol{z}_{\textrm{ptl}}|\boldsymbol{z}_{\textrm{p}})}{p(\boldsymbol{z}_{\textrm{ptl}}|\boldsymbol{z}_{\textrm{p}})}\frac{p(\boldsymbol{z}_{\textrm{p}}|{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}})\;\;\;\,}{p(\boldsymbol{z}_{\textrm{p}}|\textrm{SM})}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sigma({{\boldsymbol{\theta}}})\;\;\;\,}{\sigma(\textrm{SM})}\frac{p(\boldsymbol{z}_{\textrm{p},i}|{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}})\;\;\;\,}{p(\boldsymbol{z}_{\textrm{p},i}|\textrm{SM})}=\frac{\left|\mathcal{M}(\boldsymbol{z}_{\textrm{p},i},{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}})\right|^{2}\;\;\;\,}{\left|\mathcal{M}(\boldsymbol{z}_{\textrm{p},i},\textrm{SM})\right|^{2}}=1+\theta_{m}r^{(m)}(\boldsymbol{z}_{\textrm{p},i})+\theta_{m}\theta_{n}r^{(mn)}(\boldsymbol{z}_{\textrm{p},i}),

(36)

where we have omitted the nuisance parameter dependence, as the numerator and denominator are evaluated for the same
{{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{0}}
.
The conditional probabilities in the third term, which are not tractable, cancel in the ratio with excellent accuracy. The remainder is the parton-level DCR, which is available at the level of the ME generator.
Thus,
r(\boldsymbol{x}_{i},\boldsymbol{z}_{i}|{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}},{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}})
does not depend on
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}
. By calculating the per-event polynomial coefficients
r^{(m)}(\boldsymbol{z}_{\textrm{p},i})
and
r^{(mn)}(\boldsymbol{z}_{\textrm{p},i})
with
m,n=1,\ldots,N_{{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}}
from the ME-squared terms at various
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
values, we can construct a parametrization of
r(\boldsymbol{x}_{i},\boldsymbol{z}_{i}|{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}},{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}})
valid across the entire SMEFT parameter space, making synthetic data sets
\mathcal{D}({{\boldsymbol{\theta}}},{{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{0}})
readily available. We provide further details in Appendix
A
. Note that the SM point in the denominator is not unique; Eq.
36
can be applied for any
{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}}
, allowing for simulation at EFT parameter points other than the SM.

4.5
Large sample limit and overflow bins

Learning surrogates of the likelihood ratio requires sufficient simulated data, and in the following sections, we assume the large sample limit when minimizing loss functions. For any finite simulated data set, observables with energy units often imply a threshold beyond which simulation becomes too sparse.
Since SMEFT effects can increase with energy, removing events in the tails of energetic variable distributions is generally counterproductive. Instead, for each such variable in
\boldsymbol{x}
, we can define a threshold beyond which we do not fully trust the modeling of the differential cross-section but still find acceptable uncertainties in the cumulative yield.

We accumulate all events with features above certain thresholds in several “overflow bins” (OF) and treat these bins using standard Poisson likelihoods. Suppose
x_{n}
is a feature in the vector of observables
\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}
representing the event before introducing overflow. In that case, we must address the fact that synthetic data insufficiently samples
p(\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}|x_{n}>x_{n,0},{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}},{\boldsymbol{\nu}})
.
The simplest solution is to drop all event features for
x_{n}>x_{n,0}
and represent the event only by its presence in the overflow of
x_{n}
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}=\begin{cases}\boldsymbol{x}&x_{n}<x_{n,0}\\
\textrm{OF}_{n}&\textrm{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(37)

For a number of overflow bins (
N_{\textrm{OF}}
), one for each required observable, we reduce the observation to the counting variable
N(\textrm{OF}_{n})
instead of using fully unbinned information. The number
N_{\textrm{OF}}
characterizes the measurement, while the observed number of events in the
n
-th overflow bin
N(\textrm{OF}_{n})
characterizes the observation. Explicitly,
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}=\mathcal{D}\cup\{N(\textrm{OF}_{n})\}_{n=1}^{N_{\textrm{OF}}}
.
The likelihood for the overflow bin observation follows from Eq.
5
as a Poisson factor for each overflow bin as

\displaystyle L(\mathcal{D}^{\prime}|{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}},{\boldsymbol{\nu}})=L(\mathcal{D}|{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}},{\boldsymbol{\nu}})\times\prod_{n=1}^{N_{\textrm{OF}}}\,\textrm{P}\left(N(\textrm{OF}_{n})|\lambda(\textrm{OF}_{n}|{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}},{\boldsymbol{\nu}})\right).

(38)

As assumed, the predicted yield in each overflow bin as a function of the POIs and nuisances, denoted by
\lambda(\textrm{OF}_{n}|{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}},{\boldsymbol{\nu}})
, is available from simulation with sufficient precision. This additional factor is a standard binned likelihood term and should be handled as in traditional binned SMEFT analyses. To simplify the formulas in the following sections, we assume that this factor is included in Eq.
5
and that
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{X}
implies the event is not in any overflow bin.

5
Learning from simulation

With procedures in place for obtaining simulated and synthetic data sets, we now outline how parametrizations can be learned using common loss functions, such as cross-entropy loss. We consider a general expressive function
\hat{f}(\boldsymbol{x})
, without specifying its implementation; it could be a neural network, the BPT from Sec.
5.4
, or any other trainable multivariate predictive function.

5.1
Likelihood-ratio trick and cross-entropy loss

We begin with the well-known “likelihood-ratio trick,” which underpins the learning tasks in this work: a sufficiently expressive machine trained on a classification task learns a (monotonic function of) the likelihood ratio. If the training data is normalized by the differential cross-section, the classifier learns the DCR.
This fact is at the heart of learning techniques for parametric surrogates.
Let us take two fixed hypotheses (
{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}}
,
{{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{0}}
) and (
{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{1}}
,
{{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{1}}
), obtained either by independent simulation or produced synthetically as described in Sec.
4.4
, and minimize the cross-entropy loss function

\displaystyle L_{\textrm{CE}}[\hat{f}]

\displaystyle=-\langle\log\hat{f}(\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{x}})\rangle_{\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{z}|{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}},{{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{0}}}-\langle\log(1-\hat{f}(\boldsymbol{x}))\rangle_{\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{z}|{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{1}},{{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{1}}}.

(39)

The minimum of Eq.
39
for
\hat{f}(\boldsymbol{x})
is attained where the function derivative vanishes,

\displaystyle\frac{\delta L_{\textrm{CE}}[\hat{f}]}{\delta\hat{f}(\boldsymbol{x})}=0.

(40)

Because
\hat{f}(\boldsymbol{x})
does not depend on
\boldsymbol{z}
by construction, we can formally integrate over the latent configuration using
\int{\textrm{d}}\boldsymbol{z}\,p(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{z}|{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}},{\boldsymbol{\nu}})=p(\boldsymbol{x}|{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}},{\boldsymbol{\nu}})
, separately for the summands in Eq.
39
. The solution is then expressed in terms of latent-space integrals as

\displaystyle f^{\ast}_{\textrm{CE}}(\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{x}})

\displaystyle\equiv\argmin_{\hat{f}}L_{\textrm{CE}}[\hat{f}]=\left(1+\frac{\sigma({\boldsymbol{\theta}_{1}},{{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{1}})\,\int{\textrm{d}}\boldsymbol{z}p(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{z}|{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{1}},{{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{1}})}{\sigma({\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}},{{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{0}})\,\int{\textrm{d}}\boldsymbol{z}p(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{z}|{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}},{{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{0}})}\right)^{-1}=\left(1+\frac{{\textrm{d}}\sigma(\boldsymbol{x}|{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{1}},{{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{1}})}{{\textrm{d}}\sigma(\boldsymbol{x}|{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}},{{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{0}})}\right)^{-1}.

(41)

This expression is a monotonous function of the DCR for two fixed choices of the model parameters and can be rearranged to

\displaystyle\frac{{\textrm{d}}\sigma(\boldsymbol{x}|{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{1}},{{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{1}})}{{\textrm{d}}\sigma(\boldsymbol{x}|{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}},{{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{0}})}=\frac{1}{f^{\ast}_{\textrm{CE}}(\boldsymbol{x})}-1.

(42)

Alternative loss functions and their minima are discussed in Appendix
B
.
The simulation-based approximation of Eq.
39

\displaystyle L_{\textrm{CE}}[\hat{f}]\approx-\sum_{\mathcal{D}({\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}},{{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{0}})}w_{i}\log\hat{f}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i})-\sum_{\mathcal{D}({\boldsymbol{\theta}_{1}},{{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{1}})}w_{i}\log(1-\hat{f}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i})),

(43)

and explicitly uses two different samples in the two terms.
For the reweighting-based tractable effects in Sec.

and Sec.
4.4.3

\displaystyle L_{\textrm{CE}}[\hat{f}]

\displaystyle=-\int{\textrm{d}}\boldsymbol{x}\,{\textrm{d}}\boldsymbol{z}\,\sigma({\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}},{{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{0}})\,p(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{z}|{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}},{{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{0}})\left(\log\hat{f}(\boldsymbol{x})+r(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{z}|{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{1}},{{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{1}},{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}},{{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{0}})\log(1-\hat{f}(\boldsymbol{x}))\right).

(44)

The two terms in Eq.

39

The approximation of Eq.
44
for a synthetic data set is

\displaystyle L_{\textrm{CE}}[\hat{f}]

\displaystyle\approx-\sum_{\mathcal{D}({\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}},{{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{0}})}w_{i}\left(\log\hat{f}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i})+r(\boldsymbol{x}_{i},\boldsymbol{z}_{i}|{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{1}},{{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{1}},{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}},{{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{0}})\log(1-\hat{f}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i}))\right).

(45)

The main difference to Eq.

\hat{f}(\boldsymbol{x})
is implemented as a finitely but sufficiently expressive ML algorithm, approximating the exact solution. We denote this approximation by

\displaystyle\hat{f}(\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{x}})

\displaystyle\simeq\left(1+\frac{{\textrm{d}}\sigma(\boldsymbol{x}|{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{1}},{{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{1}})}{{\textrm{d}}\sigma(\boldsymbol{x}|{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}},{{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{0}})}\right)^{-1}.

(46)

Machine-learning systematic parametrizations

To learn parametrizations, we replace

The ansatz

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{1+\exp(\hat{T}(\boldsymbol{x}|{\boldsymbol{\nu}}))}

The ML estimate of the DCR is then

The exponential function removes the necessity of ensuring that

Inserting Eq.

Next, we approximate the logarithm of the DCR with a polynomial ansatz in

To determine

5.3
Two-point alternatives

For some systematic effects, like binary generator choices, no tractable simulation exists–only two alternate simulations. In this case, the sum over
\mathcal{V}
in Eq.

\nu_{\textrm{2P}}=0

\displaystyle L[\hat{\Delta}]=

\displaystyle\langle\textrm{Soft}^{+}(\hat{\Delta}(\boldsymbol{x}))\rangle_{\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{z}|0}+\left\langle\textrm{Soft}^{+}(-\hat{\Delta}(\boldsymbol{x}))\right\rangle_{\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{z}|1}

Minimization provides an estimate

\displaystyle\hat{S}_{\textrm{2P}}(\boldsymbol{x}|\nu_{\textrm{2P}})=\exp\left(\nu_{\textrm{2P}}\hat{\Delta}(\boldsymbol{x})\right)\simeq\left(\frac{{\textrm{d}}\sigma_{1}(\boldsymbol{x})}{{\textrm{d}}\sigma_{0}(\boldsymbol{x})}\right)^{\nu_{\textrm{2P}}},

(55)

\boldsymbol{x}

When profiling the nuisance parameter
\nu_{2\textrm{P}}

As in the binned case, we generally recommend avoiding two-point alternatives and instead using a single model with meaningful and flexible parameters. Two-point alternatives, such as using alternative generators, are beneficial as cross-checks. When profiling the effects of

5.4
The Boosted Parametric Tree algorithm

The coefficient functions in Eq.
50
could be implemented using standard neural networks. However, with hundreds of nuisance parameters in a realistic analysis, it is advantageous to develop a low-maintenance, flexible algorithm to separately learn the numerous systematic dependencies. In the following, we describe a tree-boosting regressor, the “Boosted Parametric Tree” (BPT), designed for learning systematic effects. Its complete derivation is provided in Appendix
C
.

Boosted tree algorithms have a strong track record in classification and regression tasks and were recently applied to novel searches for resonant phenomena
[
52
]
. They use an additive sequence of weak learners, each generating a coarsely binned prediction based on hierarchical phase-space partitioning, which is computationally efficient.
Each tree’s terminal nodes are linked to a predictive function that varies non-linearly across the phase-space boundaries of these nodes. Here, we extend standard tree-based regression algorithms, such as those in
TMVA

[
53
]
, by introducing a more flexible terminal-node predictor that provides the DCR to arbitrary order in the expansion in
{\boldsymbol{\nu}}
. The summed prediction from these weak learners, trained iteratively through boosting, is both smooth and arbitrarily expressive.

The simplicity of the weak learner leads to relatively mild failure modes. For instance, if a tree is trained with insufficient data, it cannot extrapolate incorrectly to phase-space regions beyond the training data; it will simply predict the value of the highest populated bin it finds, respecting regularization requirements, such as a minimum number of events per terminal node.

Additionally, a tree algorithm with axis-linear node splits, like the “Classification and Regression Tree” (CART)
[
54
]
algorithm, does not interpolate stepwise features in the training data. If features take only discrete values, the algorithm will partition phase space based on selections at these discrete values. Therefore, features related to object multiplicity need no special handling. Replacing nominal training data with digitized values according to a chosen binning can serve as validation, allowing sensitivity comparisons between unbinned and binned reference results.

The trees’ terminal nodes can be associated with more complex quantities than simple class probabilities or regression values, as shown in applications learning polynomial SMEFT dependence
[
10
,
9
]
.
We exploit this flexibility for developing a boosted tree algorithm where terminal nodes of the weak learner are linked to parametrizations of systematic effects of the training data in the nodes.
With the tools from Sec.
5.2
, the BPT provides a tree-based estimate
\hat{T}(\boldsymbol{x}|{\boldsymbol{\nu}})
of the logarithm of the DCR in the polynomial expansion

\displaystyle\hat{T}(\boldsymbol{x}|{\boldsymbol{\nu}})=\nu_{A}\hat{\Delta}_{A}(\boldsymbol{x})\simeq\log\frac{{\textrm{d}}\sigma(\boldsymbol{x}|{\boldsymbol{\nu}})\;\;\;}{{\textrm{d}}\sigma(\boldsymbol{x}|\textrm{SM})}

(56)

so that
\hat{S}(\boldsymbol{x}|{\boldsymbol{\nu}})=\exp(\hat{T}(\boldsymbol{x}|{\boldsymbol{\nu}}))

The free parameters in

It fits the weak learners of an additive expansion, one at a time, to the pseudo-residuals of the preceding boosting iteration. The complete construction is discussed in Appendix

During training, each weak learner captures only part of the total parameter dependence in each terminal node, with this fraction controlled by the algorithm’s learning rate. We set a number

6
Gradually refinable modeling

With the setup for the training of parametric regressors in place, we discuss gradually refinable modeling as a flexible approach to unbinned analyses, incorporating incremental improvements while preserving existing results.
Based on procedures commonly employed in binned analyses, we discuss the factorization of POI and nuisance parameter dependencies in the unbinned case. Uncorrelated groups of systematic effect are isolated and subsequently learned independently. With the help of an additive model, summing over the various contributing processes, we can incrementally extend and refine an existing model without invalidating existing components.
In this way, gradually refinable modeling aligns with established practices for systematic uncertainty management in binned analyses, extending these strategies to unbinned data while allowing the analysis to evolve with growing data and improved modeling techniques.

6.1
The binned Poisson likelihood

We begin with the binned Poisson likelihood for several observations (
N_{\textrm{bin}}
) in disjoint phase-space regions (bins), a setup described in detail in Refs.
[
59
,
60
]
. Multiple processes, labeled by
p=1,\ldots,N_{p}
, contribute to the cross-section component
\sigma_{n,p}(\textrm{SM})
in bin
n
.
The dependence on SMEFT POIs is assumed to factorize from the systematic effects. Since SMEFT ME-squared terms are polynomial or can be truncated to polynomial form, a small set of non-zero values of
{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}
suffices to determine the coefficients in the SMEFT parametrization
\sigma_{n,p}(\theta)=R_{n,p}({{\boldsymbol{\theta}}})\sigma_{n,p}(\textrm{SM})

[
7
]
. Here,
R_{n,p}({{\boldsymbol{\theta}}})
satisfies
R_{n,p}(\textrm{SM})=1
and fully encodes the SMEFT dependence in each bin.
If
R_{n,p}({{\boldsymbol{\theta}}})=1
holds to a good approximation for all
n
, we call the process
p
a background.

The Poisson expectation of the yield in bin
n
can then be expressed as

\displaystyle\lambda_{n}({{\boldsymbol{\theta}}},{\boldsymbol{\nu}})=\mathcal{L}({\boldsymbol{\nu}})\sum_{p=1}^{N_{p}}R_{n,p}({{\boldsymbol{\theta}}})\,\exp\left({\boldsymbol{\nu}}^{\intercal}\Delta_{n,p,1}+\nu^{\intercal}\Delta_{n,p,2}{\boldsymbol{\nu}}\right)\sigma_{n,p}(\textrm{SM}),

(63)

where the exponential is a second-order interpolation
4
4

4

[
21
]
.
of the systematic effects in terms of
K

\Delta_{n,p,1}
and
K\times K
matrices
\Delta_{n,p,2}
. The nuisances
{\boldsymbol{\nu}}
are conventionally chosen to minimize their linear correlation. In the uncorrelated case, off-diagonal entries in
\Delta_{n,p,2}

[
59
]

The binned ansatz in Eq.
63

\Delta_{n,p,1/2}
are assumed to be accurately determined by individually varied simulations, with all other model parameters held fixed. Second, the model is additive.
This inconspicuous fact, combined with the factorization of systematic effects, is key to enabling gradual model refinement, an implicit feature in the binned case. Once the per-process expectations and systematic parametrizations in Eq.
63
are established, most of these values can remain unchanged even if the model is refined to include a new process or nuisance parameter. Although this computational saving is modest in the binned case, the unbinned analysis replaces the bin-by-bin constants in Eq.
63

\boldsymbol{x}
-dependent ML parametrizations. Selecting a flexible additive model minimizes the need for re-training when refining the model, offering potentially significant gains in computational efficiency.

The additivity in Eq.
63

Specifically, setting

from the penalty, allowing the process’s normalization to float during profiling.

\displaystyle\hat{R}_{p}(\boldsymbol{x}|{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}})\simeq\frac{{\textrm{d}}\sigma_{p}(\boldsymbol{x}|{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}},{\mathbf{0}})}{{\textrm{d}}\sigma_{p}(\boldsymbol{x}|\textrm{SM})\;}

\displaystyle\hat{S}_{p}(\boldsymbol{x}|{\boldsymbol{\nu}})\simeq\frac{{\textrm{d}}\sigma_{p}(\boldsymbol{x}|{\mathbf{0}},{\boldsymbol{\nu}})\,}{{\textrm{d}}\sigma_{p}(\boldsymbol{x}|\textrm{SM})\;\,}.

(68)

The validity of Eq.

The separation of particle and detector-level effects from SMEFT effects is generally accurate due to the different energy scales involved; the POIs typically do not influence low-energy detector interactions. At the parton level, we need to verify the independence of POIs from systematic effects. PDFs, for example, may depend on SMEFT POIs

Similarly, the linear and quadratic SMEFT terms may have scale uncertainties differing from the SM prediction. In this case,

From now on, we assume the factorization

\displaystyle\hat{R}_{p}(\boldsymbol{x}|{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}})\,\hat{S}_{p}(\boldsymbol{x}|{\boldsymbol{\nu}})\simeq\frac{{\textrm{d}}\sigma_{p}(\boldsymbol{x}|{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}},{\boldsymbol{\nu}})}{{\textrm{d}}\sigma_{p}(\boldsymbol{x}|\textrm{SM})\,}

\displaystyle\hat{S}_{p}(\boldsymbol{x}|{\boldsymbol{\nu}})=\prod_{k=1}^{K}\exp\left(\nu_{k}\hat{\Delta}_{p,k,1}(\boldsymbol{x})+\nu_{k}^{2}\hat{\Delta}_{p,k,2}(\boldsymbol{x})\right)

6.3
A general unbinned surrogate model

In analogy to Eq.
63
, we define a general model for the fiducial differential cross-section,

\displaystyle{\textrm{d}}\Sigma(\boldsymbol{x}|{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}},{\boldsymbol{\nu}})=\sum_{p=1}^{N_{p}}\,\hat{R}_{p}(\boldsymbol{x}|{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}})\,\alpha_{\textrm{norm},\,p}^{\nu_{\textrm{norm},\,p}}\,\hat{S}_{p}(\boldsymbol{x}|{\boldsymbol{\nu}})\,{\textrm{d}}\sigma_{p}(\boldsymbol{x}|\textrm{SM}).

(71)

Next, we create a likelihood-free ML surrogate, relying only on differential cross-section ratios. Dividing by

\displaystyle{\textrm{d}}\Sigma(\boldsymbol{x}|\textrm{SM})=\sum_{p}{\textrm{d}}\sigma_{p}(\boldsymbol{x}|\textrm{SM}).

(72)

gives per-process DCRs that we replace with surrogates.
The simplest approach divides each
{\textrm{d}}\sigma_{p}(\boldsymbol{x}|\textrm{SM})
in Eq.
71
by Eq.
72
,

\displaystyle\frac{{\textrm{d}}\Sigma(\boldsymbol{x}|{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}},{\boldsymbol{\nu}})}{{\textrm{d}}\Sigma(\boldsymbol{x}|\textrm{SM})\;}

\displaystyle=\sum_{p=1}^{N_{p}}\,\hat{R}_{p}(\boldsymbol{x}|{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}})\,\alpha_{\textrm{norm},\,p}^{\nu_{\textrm{norm},\,p}}\,\hat{S}_{p}(\boldsymbol{x}|{\boldsymbol{\nu}})\,\hat{g}_{p}(\boldsymbol{x})\quad\textrm{where}\quad\hat{g}_{p}(\boldsymbol{x})\simeq\frac{{\textrm{d}}\sigma_{p}(\boldsymbol{x}|\textrm{SM})}{\sum_{q}{\textrm{d}}\sigma_{q}(\boldsymbol{x}|\textrm{SM})}

(73)

estimates the DCR of each process relative to the SM total.

A classifier trained to distinguish process
p
from the total SM simulation can learn
\hat{g}_{p}(\boldsymbol{x})
using the likelihood ratio trick. The DCR for arbitrary denominators, as required for profiling in Eq.
1
, can then be obtained from double ratios, allowing
{\textrm{d}}\Sigma_{p}(\boldsymbol{x}|\textrm{SM})
to cancel out.

Equation
73
provides significant modeling flexibility, as the quotient of Eq.
71
and Eq.
72
can be represented in various ways using the surrogates
\hat{g}_{p}(\boldsymbol{x})
. We present two examples demonstrating how this flexibility can solve common challenges in analysis development.

6.4
Refining an existing model

New systematic effects can be gradually incorporated, as expanding the dimension of the nuisance vector
{\boldsymbol{\nu}}
in, for example, Eq.
70
does not invalidate existing surrogates
\hat{\Delta}_{p,1/2}
. Only the new components require training.

Similarly, additional background sources can be seamlessly included. With the additive structure of Eq.
71
, a new process can be added by adjusting the steps leading to Eq.
73
. For instance, if a missing background
{\textrm{d}}\sigma_{\textrm{BKG}}(\boldsymbol{x})
, with
R_{p}(\boldsymbol{x}|{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}})=1
, is identified, we can extend
{\textrm{d}}\Sigma(\boldsymbol{x}|{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}},{\boldsymbol{\nu}})
by adding a term,

\displaystyle{\textrm{d}}\Sigma^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{x}|{\boldsymbol{\nu}},{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}})={\textrm{d}}\Sigma(\boldsymbol{x}|{\boldsymbol{\nu}},{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}})+{\textrm{d}}\sigma_{\textrm{BKG}}(\boldsymbol{x}).

(74)

We can express the DCR in terms of the existing model as

\displaystyle\frac{{\textrm{d}}\Sigma^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{x}|{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}},{\boldsymbol{\nu}})}{{\textrm{d}}\Sigma^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{x}|\textrm{SM})\;}

\displaystyle=\frac{{\textrm{d}}\Sigma(\boldsymbol{x}|{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}},{\boldsymbol{\nu}})+{\textrm{d}}\sigma_{\textrm{BKG}}(\boldsymbol{x})}{{\textrm{d}}\Sigma(\boldsymbol{x}|\textrm{SM})+{\textrm{d}}\sigma_{\textrm{BKG}}(\boldsymbol{x})\;}=\frac{\frac{{\textrm{d}}\Sigma(\boldsymbol{x}|{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}},{\boldsymbol{\nu}})}{{\textrm{d}}\Sigma(\boldsymbol{x}|\textrm{SM})}+\frac{{\textrm{d}}\sigma_{\textrm{BKG}}(\boldsymbol{x})}{{\textrm{d}}\Sigma(\boldsymbol{x}|\textrm{SM})}}{1+\frac{{\textrm{d}}\sigma_{\textrm{BKG}}(\boldsymbol{x})}{{\textrm{d}}\Sigma(\boldsymbol{x}|\textrm{SM})}\;}

\displaystyle\simeq\frac{\sum_{p=1}^{N_{p}}\,\hat{R}_{p}(\boldsymbol{x}|{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}})\,\alpha_{\textrm{norm},\,p}^{\nu_{\textrm{norm},\,p}}\,\hat{S}_{p}(\boldsymbol{x}|{\boldsymbol{\nu}})\,\hat{g}_{p}(\boldsymbol{x})+\hat{g}^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{x})}{1+\hat{g}^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{x})}

(75)

where

\displaystyle\hat{g}^{\prime}_{p}(\boldsymbol{x})\simeq\frac{{\textrm{d}}\sigma_{\textrm{BKG}}(\boldsymbol{x})}{\sum_{q}{\textrm{d}}\sigma_{q}(\boldsymbol{x}|\textrm{SM})}.

(76)

The only new component is
\hat{g}^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{x})
, a classifier that gives the DCR for the new process relative to the previous total. The new process adds to both the numerator and denominator, with no change to the rest of the model. If the new background has uncertainties, we replace
{\textrm{d}}\sigma_{\textrm{BKG}}(\boldsymbol{x})
with
{\textrm{d}}\sigma_{\textrm{BKG}}(\boldsymbol{x}|{\boldsymbol{\nu}})
in Eq.
74
and repeat the derivation, yielding

\displaystyle\frac{{\textrm{d}}\Sigma^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{x}|{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}},{\boldsymbol{\nu}})}{{\textrm{d}}\Sigma^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{x}|\textrm{SM})\;}

\displaystyle\simeq\frac{\sum_{p=1}^{N_{p}}\,\hat{R}_{p}(\boldsymbol{x}|{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}})\,\alpha_{\textrm{norm},\,p}^{\nu_{\textrm{norm},\,p}}\,\hat{S}_{p}(\boldsymbol{x}|{\boldsymbol{\nu}})\,\hat{g}_{p}(\boldsymbol{x})+\hat{S}_{\textrm{BKG}}(\boldsymbol{x}|{\boldsymbol{\nu}})\hat{g}^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{x})}{1+\hat{g}^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{x})},

(77)

where
g^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{x})
from Eq.
76
remains, and we only need to learn one extra factor,
\hat{S}_{\textrm{BKG}}(\boldsymbol{x}|{\boldsymbol{\nu}})
, to model the background’s systematic effects,

\displaystyle\hat{S}_{\textrm{BKG}}(\boldsymbol{x}|{\boldsymbol{\nu}})\simeq\frac{{\textrm{d}}\sigma_{\textrm{BKG}}(\boldsymbol{x}|{\mathbf{0}},{\boldsymbol{\nu}})\,}{{\textrm{d}}\sigma_{\textrm{BKG}}(\boldsymbol{x}|\textrm{SM})\;\,},

(78)

similar to Eq.
68
. Refinable modeling thus avoids retraining existing regressors and enables incremental analysis development. Because an event sample for
{\textrm{d}}\sigma_{\textrm{BKG}}
is the only ingredient for obtaining
\hat{g}^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{x})
, it could alternatively be measured in real-data side bands, supporting the development of data-driven unbinned estimation strategies.

6.5
Refinement for a high-purity process

Now, consider a single SMEFT-dependent signal process
{\textrm{d}}\sigma_{\textrm{SMEFT}}(\boldsymbol{x}|{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}},{\boldsymbol{\nu}})
and multiple
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
-independent backgrounds,
{\textrm{d}}\sigma_{p}(\boldsymbol{x}|{\boldsymbol{\nu}})
. To form the DCR, we divide each term in the sums of both sides of Eq.
73
by
{\textrm{d}}\sigma_{\textrm{SMEFT}}(\boldsymbol{x}|\textrm{SM})
, which leaves the result unchanged but modifies the parametrization to

\displaystyle\frac{{\textrm{d}}\Sigma(\boldsymbol{x}|{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}},{\boldsymbol{\nu}})}{{\textrm{d}}\Sigma(\boldsymbol{x}|\textrm{SM})\;}

\displaystyle\simeq\frac{\alpha_{\textrm{norm}}^{\nu_{\textrm{norm}}}\;\hat{R}(\boldsymbol{x}|{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}})\;\hat{S}(\boldsymbol{x}|{\boldsymbol{\nu}})+\sum_{p=1}^{N_{p}}\,\alpha_{\textrm{norm},\,p}^{\nu_{\textrm{norm},\,p}}\,\hat{S}_{p}(\boldsymbol{x}|{\boldsymbol{\nu}})\,\hat{g}^{\prime\prime}_{p}(\boldsymbol{x})}{1+\sum_{p}\hat{g}^{\prime\prime}_{p}(\boldsymbol{x})}

(79)

where

\displaystyle g^{\prime\prime}_{p}(\boldsymbol{x})\simeq\frac{{\textrm{d}}\sigma_{p}(\boldsymbol{x}|\textrm{SM})}{{\textrm{d}}\sigma_{\textrm{SMEFT}}(\boldsymbol{x}|\textrm{SM})},

(80)

and signal quantities have no process index. The classifier
\hat{g}^{\prime\prime}(\boldsymbol{x})
is trained to distinguish each background from the SMEFT signal at the SM point. Adding a new background process only requires training one additional classifier, as expanding the sum over
p
leaves the rest of the model unaffected.

The steps in this and the previous section can be combined and repeated as modeling is refined. Equation
71
thus supports incremental refinements, similar to the binned model in Eq.
63
. Once a new effect prediction is available, it can be incorporated. Unlike in the binned case, expectations here come from separately trained surrogates rather than binned yields.

The comparisons in previous sections are one-dimensional projections. For a more general check of whether the BPT is fully expressive in the high-dimensional

84

Figure 10:
A neural-network classifier tests the surrogate
\hat{S}_{\textrm{HF}}(\boldsymbol{x}|\nu_{\textrm{HF}})

The C2ST is a non-parametric method for assessing if two samples originate from the same distribution. It trains a binary classifier on a combined dataset of the two samples, using labels to indicate sample origin. The classifier’s accuracy reveals distribution similarity; accuracy above chance suggests different distributions.

Given a specific nuisance parameter
{\boldsymbol{\nu}}
and a pair of synthetic data sets,
\mathcal{D}_{\textrm{SM}}
and
\mathcal{D}_{\nu}
with
\nu\neq 0

is accurate and fully expressive, then

\displaystyle\hat{S}(\boldsymbol{x}|\nu)=\frac{{\textrm{d}}\sigma(\boldsymbol{x}|{\boldsymbol{\nu}})\;\;\,\,}{{\textrm{d}}\sigma(\boldsymbol{x}|\textrm{SM})}

(91)

for all
\boldsymbol{x}
and
{\boldsymbol{\nu}}

to form

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{\textrm{reweighted}}=\left\{w^{\prime}_{i}=\hat{S}(\boldsymbol{x}|{\boldsymbol{\nu}})^{-1}w_{i},\boldsymbol{x}_{i}\;\;\textrm{for all}\;\;w_{i},\boldsymbol{x}_{i}\in\mathcal{D}_{\boldsymbol{\nu}}\right\},

The Asimov dataset

[
59
]

[

]
extends this to the unbinned case, enabling sampling-free exclusions within continuous parametric models. Here, we consider composite hypotheses involving two Wilson coefficients, which we denote by
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
. Under the exclusion scenario,
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
represents the null hypothesis with
N_{{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}}=2
, while other Wilson coefficients are profiled as nuisance parameters. The alternative hypothesis assumes
{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}={\mathbf{0}}
.

Wilks’ theorem states that if the data are distributed under the null hypothesis
\boldsymbol{\theta}
, the test statistic
p(q_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}|{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}},{\boldsymbol{\nu}})
asymptotically follows a central
\chi^{2}
distribution with
N_{{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}}
degrees of freedom. This distribution is independent of the true values of the nuisance parameters. Given that our POIs primarily influence the predictions linearly
[
74
]
, we assume any minor quadratic terms do not invalidate Wilks’ theorem
[
27
]
. However, in practical applications, this assumption should be verified, as shown in Ref.
[
8
]
, where good agreement was observed.
Since
q_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}
is monotonic with the p-value, it can define acceptance regions for
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
at confidence levels of 68% (
\alpha=32\%
) or 95% (
\alpha=5\%
). We anticipate excluding a hypothesis
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
at a given CL if there’s a 50% or greater probability for
q_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}
to fall outside the corresponding acceptance region when the alternate hypothesis
{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}={\mathbf{0}}
is true.
Therefore, we must solve

\displaystyle\int_{q_{{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}},\textrm{med}}}^{\infty}p(q_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}|{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}){\textrm{d}}q_{{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}}=\alpha\quad\textrm{and}\quad q_{{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}},\textrm{med}}=\textrm{Med}(q_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}|{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}={\mathbf{0}}).

(93)

The final ingredient is Wald’s theorem
[
86
]
, which implies that the distribution
\textrm{p}(q_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}|{\mathbf{0}})
asymptotically follows a non-central
\chi^{2}
distribution with
N_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}
degrees of freedom and non-centrality parameter
\Lambda
. This parameter can be computed (see Ref.
[
8
]
for details) for the unbinned likelihood ratio. As in the binned case, it corresponds to the Asimov expectation of Eq.
11
for the alternate hypothesis, multiplied by
-2
. In our notation, the result is

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\Lambda

\displaystyle=-\mathcal{L}({\boldsymbol{\nu}})\,\sigma({{\boldsymbol{\theta}}},{\boldsymbol{\nu}})+\mathcal{L}_{0}\,\sigma(\textrm{SM})+\mathcal{L}_{0}\left\langle\log\left(\mathcal{L}({\boldsymbol{\nu}})\mathcal{R}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i}|{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}},{\boldsymbol{\nu}})/\mathcal{L}_{0}\right)\right\rangle_{\textrm{SM}}-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\nu_{k}^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\boldsymbol{x}_{i},w_{i}\in\mathcal{D}_{0}\cap\mathcal{X}}w_{i}\left(-\mathcal{L}({\boldsymbol{\nu}})\mathcal{R}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i}|{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}},{\boldsymbol{\nu}})+\mathcal{L}_{0}+\mathcal{L}_{0}\log\left(\mathcal{L}({\boldsymbol{\nu}})\mathcal{R}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i}|{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}},{\boldsymbol{\nu}})/\mathcal{L}_{0}\right)\right)-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\nu_{k}^{2}

(94)

with the integrated luminosity from Eq.
81
and the model’s DCR
\mathcal{R}(\boldsymbol{x}|{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}},{\boldsymbol{\nu}})
from Eq.
82
. The sum is over all events in the nominal
\mathrm{t}\overline{\mathrm{t}}(2\ell)
sample passing the event selection. The ratio
\mathcal{R}(\boldsymbol{x}|{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}},{\boldsymbol{\nu}})
appears in both the logarithm and the “extended” term for the total fiducial cross-section. This expression provides the test statistic under the alternate hypothesis, allowing us to obtain the expected exclusion contour from the profiled likelihood test statistic. The minimization is performed with the

8
Conclusion

This paper presents a comprehensive, scalable framework for modeling the effects of systematic uncertainties in unbinned analyses of collider data. By factorizing systematic effects across parton, particle, and detector levels, we make them accessible for machine learning. With a highly granular factorization of the various dependencies, we leverage the extensive knowledge gained from binned LHC data analyses and fully capitalize on high-quality Monte Carlo simulation.
A flexible approach facilitates the progressive refinement of unbinned models, including but not restricted to applications in SMEFT. It accommodates new systematic effects or background contributions without invalidating previously trained surrogates.

A significant technical innovation introduced is the Boosted Parametric Tree (BPT), an extension of tree-boosting algorithms designed to learn accurate parametrizations of systematic dependencies. BPTs offer a robust and efficient alternative to neural networks for modeling systematic effects, providing reliable surrogate models for complex, high-dimensional parameter spaces in unbinned hypothesis testing.

Our work thus bridges a critical gap in the methodological toolbox for SMEFT analyses, searches for other non-resonant effects beyond the standard model, and similar inference problems. We demonstrate the practical application through a semi-realistic case study of top quark pair production in the dilepton channel, which underscores the effectiveness of our approach in learning and incorporating systematic effects.
Overall, the new techniques pave the way for more refined and adaptable unbinned hypothesis tests, enhancing the accuracy and reliability of SMEFT analyses. We anticipate these advancements will be instrumental in exploiting the data from future collider experiments.
Finally, we believe that publicly available refined models would be useful for future SMEFT combinations and for providing legacy LHC results.

and
L_{2}
as (standard) functions of
\hat{f}
and denote the (standard) derivative by
L^{\prime}
, it is straightforward to show that the conditions

\displaystyle-\frac{L_{2}^{\prime}}{L_{1}^{\prime}}=\frac{1}{\hat{f}}-1\qquad\textrm{and}\qquad-\frac{L_{2}^{\prime}}{L_{1}^{\prime}}=\hat{f}

(B.7)

lead to loss functions minimized by Eq.
42

and Eq.
B.4
, respectively. The loss functions discussed so far are special cases of Eq.
B.7
. To control the loss behavior away from the minimum, one can choose, therefore, an appropriate
L_{1}[\hat{f}]
or
L_{2}[\hat{f}]
and compute the other term from Eq.
B.7
.

Appendix C
Construction of the BPT algorithm

This section provides a step-by-step derivation of the BPT algorithm.
A summary of the resulting procedures is described in Sec.
5.4
.

C.1
Tree-boosting of parametric regressors

It is instructive to discuss boosting for generic non-parametric estimators based on the cross-entropy loss function
L_{\textrm{CE}}[\hat{f}]
in Eq.
39
.
After the replacement in Eq.
47
, we have

\displaystyle L[\hat{T}]

\displaystyle=\left\langle\textrm{Soft}^{+}(\hat{T}(\boldsymbol{x}))\right\rangle_{\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{z}|{\mathbf{0}}}+\left\langle\textrm{Soft}^{+}(-\hat{T}(\boldsymbol{x}))\right\rangle_{\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{z}|{\boldsymbol{\nu}}},

(C.1)

where we do not yet specify the implementation of
\hat{T}(\boldsymbol{x})
.
The loss would attain its minimum at

\displaystyle T^{\ast}(\boldsymbol{x})=\log\frac{{\textrm{d}}\sigma(\boldsymbol{x}|{\boldsymbol{\nu}})}{{\textrm{d}}\sigma(\boldsymbol{x}|{\mathbf{0}})\,},

(C.2)

but instead of obtaining this result in a single fit, we chose a number
B
of boosting iterations and corresponding learning rates
0<\eta^{(b)}<1
for
b=1,\ldots,B
. We use an additive expansion of
\hat{T}(\boldsymbol{x})
in terms of the weak learners
\hat{t}^{(b)}(\boldsymbol{x})
. To this end, we iterate the boosting relations

\displaystyle t^{(b)}{}^{\ast}(\boldsymbol{x})

\displaystyle=\argmin_{\hat{t}^{(b)}}L\left[\hat{t}^{(b)}{}(\boldsymbol{x})+\hat{T}^{(b-1)}(\boldsymbol{x})\right],

(C.3)

\displaystyle\hat{T}^{(b)}(\boldsymbol{x})

\displaystyle=\hat{T}^{(b-1)}(\boldsymbol{x})+\eta^{(b)}\hat{t}^{(b)}{}^{\ast}(\boldsymbol{x})

(C.4)

a number of
B
times, starting with the initial choice
\hat{T}^{(0)}(\boldsymbol{x})=0
.
Equation
C.3
obtains the weak learner
\hat{t}^{(b)}(\boldsymbol{x})
when the result of the preceding iteration
\hat{T}^{(b-1)}(\boldsymbol{x})
is known. Equation
C.4
updates the additive model with a fraction
\eta^{(b)}
of this weak learner’s prediction.
After
B
iterations, the boosted prediction
\hat{T}^{(B)}
can be expressed as

\displaystyle\hat{T}^{(B)}(\boldsymbol{x})

\displaystyle=\sum_{b=1}^{B}\eta^{(b)}t^{(b)}{}^{\ast}(\boldsymbol{x}).

(C.5)

An important practicality for boosting learners that are fit to synthetic data sets follows from the minimum condition in Eq.
C.3
. It implies that the minimum at iteration
b
satisfies

\displaystyle t^{(b)}{}^{\ast}(\boldsymbol{x})+\hat{T}^{(b-1)}(\boldsymbol{x})\simeq\log\frac{{\textrm{d}}\sigma(\boldsymbol{x}|{\boldsymbol{\nu}})}{{\textrm{d}}\sigma(\boldsymbol{x}|{\mathbf{0}})}=\log\frac{\sigma({\boldsymbol{\nu}})}{\sigma({\mathbf{0}})}\frac{\int{\textrm{d}}\boldsymbol{z}\,p(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{z}|{\boldsymbol{\nu}})}{\int{\textrm{d}}\boldsymbol{z}\,p(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{z}|{\mathbf{0}})}

(C.6)

which we rearrange to

\displaystyle t^{(b)}{}^{\ast}(\boldsymbol{x})\simeq\log\frac{\sigma({\boldsymbol{\nu}})}{\sigma({\mathbf{0}})}\frac{\int{\textrm{d}}\boldsymbol{z}\,p(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{z}|{\boldsymbol{\nu}})\times\,\exp\left(-\hat{T}^{(b-1)}(\boldsymbol{x})\right)}{\int{\textrm{d}}\boldsymbol{z}\,p(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{z}|{\mathbf{0}})\phantom{\times\,\exp\left(-\hat{S}^{(b-1)}(\boldsymbol{x})\right)}\;}.

(C.7)

By reading this equation as an
\boldsymbol{x}
-dependent scaling of the joint-space integration measure
{\textrm{d}}\sigma(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{z}|{\boldsymbol{\nu}})=\sigma({\boldsymbol{\nu}})\,p(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{z}|{\boldsymbol{\nu}}){\textrm{d}}\boldsymbol{x}{\textrm{d}}\boldsymbol{z}
by the reciprocal of the estimate of the preceding boosting iteration, we find that
t^{(b)}{}^{\ast}
can also be obtained if, instead of using the additive expansion, we replace
\sigma({\boldsymbol{\nu}})\,p(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{z}|{\boldsymbol{\nu}})\rightarrow\exp\left(-\hat{T}^{(b-1)}(\boldsymbol{x})\right)\,\sigma({\boldsymbol{\nu}})\,p(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{z}|{\boldsymbol{\nu}})
.
Because Eq.
C.4
provides the exponent
\hat{T}
iteratively, we only have to multiply the cross-section by
\exp(-\eta^{(b-1)}t^{(b-1)}{}^{\ast}(\boldsymbol{x}))
when moving from iteration
b-1
to iteration
b
. This way, the boosting equations read

\displaystyle\sigma({\boldsymbol{\nu}})p^{(b)}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{z}|{\boldsymbol{\nu}})

\displaystyle=\exp\left(-\eta^{(b-1)}t^{(b-1)}{}^{\ast}(\boldsymbol{x})\right)\,\sigma({\boldsymbol{\nu}})p^{(b-1)}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{z}|{\boldsymbol{\nu}})

(C.8)

\displaystyle t^{(b)}{}^{\ast}(\boldsymbol{x})

\displaystyle=\argmin_{\hat{t}^{(b)}}L\left[\hat{t}^{(b)}{}(\boldsymbol{x})\right],

(C.9)

\displaystyle\hat{T}^{(b)}(\boldsymbol{x})

\displaystyle=\hat{T}^{(b-1)}(\boldsymbol{x})+\eta^{(b)}\hat{t}^{(b)}{}^{\ast}(\boldsymbol{x}),

(C.10)

initialized by
\hat{T}^{(0)}(\boldsymbol{x})=t^{(0)}{}^{\ast}(\boldsymbol{x})=0
.
The advantage of this formulation is that Eq.
C.9
is a standard loss function minimization without the additive model appearing in the argument as in Eq.
C.3
. The update of the synthetic data set
\mathcal{D}_{{\boldsymbol{\nu}}}^{(b)}=\{w_{i}^{(b)},\boldsymbol{x}_{{\boldsymbol{\nu}},i},\boldsymbol{z}_{i}\}
, now also defined for each iteration
b
, follows from Eq.
C.8
as

\displaystyle w_{i}^{(b)}=\exp\left(-\eta^{(b-1)}t^{(b-1)}{}^{\ast}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i})\right)w^{(b-1)}_{i}.

(C.11)

This prescription can be interpreted as a recursive weighting of the differential cross-section of
\mathcal{D}_{{\boldsymbol{\nu}}}
in the second term in Eq.
C.1
towards
\mathcal{D}_{{\mathbf{0}}}
in the first term.
The boosting algorithm removes the learned approximation from the training data as the regressor learns to approximate the DCR more accurately.
It is customary to chose
\eta^{(b)}
independently of
b
, and values between
10^{-3}
and
3\cdot 10^{-1}
for this universal learning rate have proven efficient.

The sample
\mathcal{D}_{{\mathbf{0}}}
stays unchanged in the boosting procedure because we decided to write the
\boldsymbol{x}
-dependent scaling in Eq.
C.8
in the numerator. The choice of only reweighting the sample
\mathcal{D}_{\boldsymbol{\nu}}
is a critical detail. It holds the key to a boosting algorithm that works for the fully parametric regressor, including the
{\boldsymbol{\nu}}
dependence.
We can construct the loss for a parametric tree-based algorithm from the general parametric loss function in Eq.
51
, which is a sum of equally structured terms

\displaystyle L

\displaystyle=\sum_{{\boldsymbol{\nu}}\in\mathcal{V}}L_{\textrm{CE}}[\hat{T}(\boldsymbol{x}|{\boldsymbol{\nu}})]=\sum_{{\boldsymbol{\nu}}\in\mathcal{V}}\left(\left\langle\textrm{Soft}^{+}(\hat{T}(\boldsymbol{x}|{\boldsymbol{\nu}}))\right\rangle_{\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{z}|{\mathbf{0}}}+\left\langle\textrm{Soft}^{+}(-\hat{T}(\boldsymbol{x}|{\boldsymbol{\nu}}))\right\rangle_{\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{z}|{\boldsymbol{\nu}}}\right).

(C.12)

The synthetic data set in the first expectation value in each sum term is always
\mathcal{D}_{\mathbf{0}}
, irrespective of the value of
{\boldsymbol{\nu}}
. The synthetic data set in the second expectation is
\mathcal{D}_{{\boldsymbol{\nu}}}
and is different for each
\mathcal{{\boldsymbol{\nu}}}\in\mathcal{V}
.
It is this term whose synthetic data set changes during the boosting algorithm, and because there is one such set for each
{\boldsymbol{\nu}}\in\mathcal{V}
, the reweighting can be done simultaneously for each
{\boldsymbol{\nu}}
in the sum over
\mathcal{V}
in Eq.
C.12
.
Repeating the steps starting at Eq.
C.1
with a sufficiently expressive
{\boldsymbol{\nu}}
-dependent function
\hat{T}(\boldsymbol{x}|{\boldsymbol{\nu}})
, it is straightforward to show that aside from the extra
{\boldsymbol{\nu}}
-dependence in the notation nothing else changes. Concretely, we only need to modify Eq.
C.5
to notate the
{\boldsymbol{\nu}}
-dependence in the weak learner
\hat{t}(\boldsymbol{x}|{\boldsymbol{\nu}})
. The other steps follow analogously, and Eq.
C.11
generalizes to

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{{\boldsymbol{\nu}}}^{(b)}=\left\{\exp(-\eta^{(b-1)}t^{(b-1)\ast}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i}|{\boldsymbol{\nu}}))\,w^{(b-1)}_{i},\boldsymbol{x}_{i},\boldsymbol{z}_{i}\right\}\;\;\textrm{for all}\;\;\{w^{(b-1)},\boldsymbol{x}_{i},\boldsymbol{z}_{i}\}\in\mathcal{D}_{{\boldsymbol{\nu}}}^{(b-1)}\;\;\textrm{for all}\;\;{\boldsymbol{\nu}}\in\mathcal{V},

(C.13)

which is the same as Eq.
C.11
except for that it is carried out simultaneously for each
{\boldsymbol{\nu}}\in\mathcal{V}
. This completes the boosting algorithm for generic weak learners, and we can proceed with constructing the tree-based implementation.

C.2
Learning the phase-space partitioning

We construct the parametric weak learner
\hat{t}^{(b)}(\boldsymbol{x}|{\boldsymbol{\nu}})
in two steps. Because the procedure is identical at each boosting iteration, we drop the superscript
(b)
in this section in favor of readability and write
\hat{t}(\boldsymbol{x}|{\boldsymbol{\nu}})
in place of
\hat{t}^{(b)}(\boldsymbol{x}|{\boldsymbol{\nu}})
.
We first specify the non-linearity in
\boldsymbol{x}
while keeping a parametric
{\boldsymbol{\nu}}
-dependence fully general.

We decompose the phase space
\mathcal{X}
into non-overlapping regions
\Delta\boldsymbol{x}_{j}
, collectively denoted by
\mathcal{J}
. Such a phase-space partitioning satisfies

\displaystyle\mathcal{X}=\bigcup_{j\in\mathcal{J}}\Delta\boldsymbol{x}_{j}\quad\textrm{and}\quad\Delta\boldsymbol{x}_{j}\cap\Delta\boldsymbol{x}_{j^{\prime}}=\emptyset\;\;\longleftrightarrow\;\;j\neq j^{\prime}.

(C.14)

The nonlinearity in a tree ansatz can always be expressed via the index function

\displaystyle\mathbbm{1}_{j}(\boldsymbol{x})=\begin{cases}1&\textrm{if}\;\boldsymbol{x}\in\Delta\boldsymbol{x}_{j}\\
0&\textrm{otherwise}\end{cases}

(C.15)

in terms of, for now, arbitrary functions
\hat{t}_{j}({\boldsymbol{\nu}})
that have no
\boldsymbol{x}
-dependence,

\displaystyle\hat{t}(\boldsymbol{x}|{\boldsymbol{\nu}})=\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}}\mathbbm{1}_{j}(\boldsymbol{x})\,\hat{t}_{j}({\boldsymbol{\nu}}).

(C.16)

The function
\hat{t}_{j}({\boldsymbol{\nu}})
should describe the DCR in bin
j
.

Because the
\boldsymbol{x}
-dependence is only in the index function, we can insert Eq.
C.16
into Eq.
C.12
and use Eq.
43
to carry out the event sums over the synthetic data sets. The result is

\displaystyle L[\mathcal{J},\hat{t}_{j}]=\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}}L_{j}[\hat{t}_{j}]=\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}}\sum_{{\boldsymbol{\nu}}\in\mathcal{V}}\left[\sigma_{j,0}\,\textrm{Soft}^{+}(\hat{t}_{j}({\boldsymbol{\nu}}))+\sigma_{j,{\boldsymbol{\nu}}}\,\textrm{Soft}^{+}(-\hat{t}_{j}({\boldsymbol{\nu}}))\right].

(C.17)

The
\sigma_{j,0}
and
\sigma_{j,{\boldsymbol{\nu}}}
are given in terms of the training data as

\displaystyle\sigma_{j,0}=\sum_{(\boldsymbol{x}_{i},w_{i})\,\in\,\mathcal{D}_{\mathbf{0}}\cap\Delta\boldsymbol{x}_{j}}w_{i}\qquad\textrm{and}\qquad\sigma_{j,{\boldsymbol{\nu}}}=\sum_{(\boldsymbol{x}_{i},w_{i})\,\in\,\mathcal{D}_{\boldsymbol{\nu}}\cap\Delta\boldsymbol{x}_{j}}w_{i}

(C.18)

and can be understood as the synthetic predictions for the cross-section in bin
j\in\mathcal{J}
for nuisance parameters
{\mathbf{0}}
and
{\boldsymbol{\nu}}
, respectively.
We have now decomposed our problem into two related problems that each pertain to different trainable parameters: the phase space partitioning
\mathcal{J}
and, independently in each region of the partitioning, a function
\hat{t}_{j}({\boldsymbol{\nu}})
whose
{\boldsymbol{\nu}}
-dependence we still have to specify.

Before we tackle these problems, it is instructive to develop an intuition for the loss function in Eq.
C.17
.
We assume an infinitely expressive
\hat{t}_{j}({\boldsymbol{\nu}})
and functionally differentiate
Eq.
C.17
to arrive at

\displaystyle 0=\frac{\delta L_{j}}{\delta\hat{t}_{j}}=\sum_{{\boldsymbol{\nu}}\in\mathcal{V}}\left[\frac{\sigma_{j,{\mathbf{0}}}}{1+\exp\left(-\hat{t}_{j}({\boldsymbol{\nu}})\right)}-\frac{\sigma_{j,{\boldsymbol{\nu}}}}{1+\exp\left(\hat{t}_{j}({\boldsymbol{\nu}})\right)}\right].

(C.19)

This equation is satisfied exactly if

\displaystyle\hat{t}_{j}({\boldsymbol{\nu}})=\log\frac{\sigma_{j,{\boldsymbol{\nu}}}}{\sigma_{j,{\mathbf{0}}}}\quad\textrm{for all}\quad{\boldsymbol{\nu}}\in\mathcal{V},

(C.20)

which we can always fulfill for sufficiently expressive
\hat{t}_{j}({\boldsymbol{\nu}})
; a perfect representation of the
{\boldsymbol{\nu}}
-dependence in each tree node
j\in\mathcal{J}
reproduces the logarithm of the DCR for those values of
{\boldsymbol{\nu}}
whose synthetic data sets we included in the loss function.
The predictive function of a tree is finitely expressive. Hence, we will seek an approximation for Eq.
C.20
in the next section. But we can meanwhile use the result to shed light on the loss function Eq.
C.17
. Formally eliminating
\hat{t}_{j}({\boldsymbol{\nu}})
in favor of its predictions at the points
{\boldsymbol{\nu}}\in\mathcal{V}
, we express the loss solely in terms of the per-bin cross-sections
\sigma_{j,{\mathbf{0}}}
and
\sigma_{j,{\boldsymbol{\nu}}}
,

\displaystyle L[\mathcal{J}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}}\sum_{{\boldsymbol{\nu}}\in\mathcal{V}}\left[\sigma_{j,{\mathbf{0}}}\log\left(1+\frac{\sigma_{j,{\boldsymbol{\nu}}}}{\sigma_{j,{\mathbf{0}}}}\right)+\sigma_{j,{\boldsymbol{\nu}}}\log\left(1+\frac{\sigma_{j,{\mathbf{0}}}}{\sigma_{j,{\boldsymbol{\nu}}}}\right)\right].

(C.21)

This equation would provide a loss function for finding the optimal phase space partitioning if we did not need to use finitely expressive
\hat{t}_{j}({\boldsymbol{\nu}})
.
If we assume small
{\boldsymbol{\nu}}
such that a Taylor expansion of
\sigma_{j,{\boldsymbol{\nu}}}
around
{\boldsymbol{\nu}}={\mathbf{0}}
is a good approximation, we get

\displaystyle L[\mathcal{J}]

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{4}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}}\sum_{{\boldsymbol{\nu}}\in\mathcal{V}}\nu_{a}\nu_{b}I_{(ab),j}+\ldots

(C.22)

where the ellipsis comprise
\mathcal{O}(\nu^{3})
terms and
\mathcal{J}
-independent contributions. The quantity

\displaystyle I_{(ab),j}=\frac{1}{\sigma_{j,{\mathbf{0}}}}\frac{\partial\sigma_{j,{\boldsymbol{\nu}}}}{\partial\nu_{a}}\frac{\partial\sigma_{j,{\boldsymbol{\nu}}}}{\partial\nu_{a}}\Bigg|_{{\boldsymbol{\nu}}={\mathbf{0}}}

(C.23)

is the leading contribution in
L[\mathcal{J}]
and represents the Fisher information matrix of a Poisson measurement in bin
j
regarding the model parameters.
We thus show that our loss function will guide the algorithm towards finding a partitioning
\mathcal{J}
that maximizes the sum of the Fisher information over all terminal tree nodes.

C.3
Terminal node predictions

The second and final constructive step is to curtail the
{\boldsymbol{\nu}}
-dependence of
\hat{t}_{j}
for each node in the weak learner. We can choose it in analogy to the binned case as it will turn out.
We use the ansatz

\displaystyle\hat{t}_{j}({\boldsymbol{\nu}})

\displaystyle=\nu_{a}\hat{\Delta}_{a,j}+\nu_{a}\nu_{b}\hat{\Delta}_{ab,j}+\nu_{a}\nu_{b}\nu_{c}\hat{\Delta}_{abc,j}+\cdots=\nu_{A}\hat{\Delta}_{A,j}

(C.24)

with the multi-index notation as explained in Sec.
5.2
. The polynomial order and the coefficients at each polynomial order are truncated to the application’s required accuracy. We also
allow for its fine-tuning by excluding some of the terms in the polynomial for application-specific reasons.
If the node
j
is small enough that the DCR does not significantly vary with
\boldsymbol{x}
, we get for the first term

\displaystyle\hat{\Delta}_{j,a}\approx\frac{\partial}{\partial\nu_{a}}t_{j}({\boldsymbol{\nu}})\Bigg|_{{\boldsymbol{\nu}}={\mathbf{0}}}=\frac{\partial}{\partial\nu_{a}}\log\frac{{\textrm{d}}\sigma(\boldsymbol{x}|{\boldsymbol{\nu}})}{{\textrm{d}}\sigma(\boldsymbol{x}|{\mathbf{0}})}\Bigg|_{{\boldsymbol{\nu}}={\mathbf{0}}}=s_{a}+\frac{\partial}{\partial\nu_{a}}\log\sigma({\boldsymbol{\nu}})\Bigg|_{{\boldsymbol{\nu}}={\mathbf{0}}}\quad\textrm{for}\quad\boldsymbol{x}\in\Delta\boldsymbol{x}_{j}.

(C.25)

The last expression relates
\hat{\Delta}_{j,a}
to the well-known score vector
s_{a}
, a sufficient statistic for small
{\boldsymbol{\nu}}
and, therefore, an optimal observable. The log-derivative of the inclusive cross-section in the last term does not depend on the phase-space partitioning and, thus, is irrelevant to the optimization. The algorithm will, therefore, aim to reduce the expectation of the variance of the score in the training sample. This is, by definition, the negative value of the Fisher information matrix, consistent with the interpretation in the preceding section. Depending on the concrete problem and the desired accuracy, the higher-order terms in Eq.
C.25
can improve the parametrization for larger values of
{\boldsymbol{\nu}}
.

We now determine the terminal node predictions of a weak learner up to a fixed arbitrary polynomial ordering
{\boldsymbol{\nu}}
by computing
\hat{\Delta}_{A,j}
.
The parametric tree ansatz is

\displaystyle\hat{t}(\boldsymbol{x}|{\boldsymbol{\nu}})=\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}}\mathbbm{1}_{j}(\boldsymbol{x})\left(\nu_{A}\hat{\Delta}_{A,j}\right).

(C.26)

An exact solution cannot be obtained for the optimal values of
\hat{\Delta}
in the general case because the resulting equations

\displaystyle\nu_{A}\hat{\Delta}_{A,j}

\displaystyle=\log\frac{\sigma_{j,{\boldsymbol{\nu}}}}{\sigma_{j,{\mathbf{0}}}}\quad\textrm{for all}\quad{\boldsymbol{\nu}}\in\mathcal{V}

(C.27)

are overdetermined if
|\mathcal{V}|>N_{\Delta}
. We note that Eq.
C.27
has the same form as Eq.
C.20
except for the finite expressivity on the l.h.s. We are content with an approximate solution of the per-node parametrization because boosting the weak learner will iteratively reduce the shortcomings either way. Any deficiency of a concrete weak learner will be reduced in the subsequent boosting iteration.
For
|\mathcal{V}|<N_{\Delta}
, the training data cannot provide a unique estimate, and more data sets must be obtained.

The simplest approach for approximately solving Eq.
C.27
is by minimizing the mean-squared error separately for each node
j\in\mathcal{J}
,

\displaystyle L_{j,\textrm{MSE}}[\hat{\Delta}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{{\boldsymbol{\nu}}\in\mathcal{V}}\left(\nu_{A}\hat{\Delta}_{A,j}-\log\frac{\sigma_{j,{\boldsymbol{\nu}}}}{\sigma_{j,{\mathbf{0}}}}\right)^{2}.

(C.28)

It is solved by

\displaystyle\hat{\Delta}_{A,j}=\left[\;\sum_{\nu\in\mathcal{V}}{\boldsymbol{\nu}}{\boldsymbol{\nu}}^{\textrm{T}}\right]_{AB}^{-1}\left[\sum_{\;{\boldsymbol{\nu}}\in\mathcal{V}}{\boldsymbol{\nu}}\log\frac{\sigma_{j,{\boldsymbol{\nu}}}}{\sigma_{j,{\mathbf{0}}}}\right]_{B}.

(C.29)

The matrix

\displaystyle V_{AB}=\left[\sum_{\nu\in\mathcal{V}}{\boldsymbol{\nu}}{\boldsymbol{\nu}}^{\textrm{T}}\right]_{AB},

(C.30)

appearing in the approximate solution, is invertible if the base point coordinate matrix has full rank, as we have assumed in Sec.
5.2
.

It is instructive to check that the weak learner appropriately responds to training data that is perfectly, not just approximately, consistent with the polynomial ansatz. If we take constants
\delta_{A}
and consider a model that predicts
\sigma_{j,{\boldsymbol{\nu}}}=\exp(\nu_{A}\delta_{A})\sigma_{j,{\mathbf{0}}}
in a given region, we can insert into Eq.
C.29
and find
\hat{\Delta}_{A}=\delta_{A}
, confirming that the algorithm learns the exact solution if it has the chance.

To complete the construction of the weak learner, we insert the ansatz Eq.
C.26
into Eq.
C.17
and get

\displaystyle L[\mathcal{J}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}}\sum_{{\boldsymbol{\nu}}\in\mathcal{V}}\left[\sigma_{j,{\mathbf{0}}}\,\textrm{Soft}^{+}\left(\nu_{A}\hat{\Delta}_{j,A}\right)+\sigma_{j,{\boldsymbol{\nu}}}\,\textrm{Soft}^{+}\left(-\nu_{A}\hat{\Delta}_{j,A}\right)\right],

(C.31)

where
\hat{\Delta}_{A,j}
are obtained from Eq.
C.29
and
\sigma_{j,0}
and
\sigma_{j,{\boldsymbol{\nu}}}
from Eq.
C.18
.
The data samples
\mathcal{D}_{\mathbf{0}}
, used for the prediction of
\sigma_{j,{\mathbf{0}}}
in the first term, can either taken to be the same or statistically independent samples.
This loss function is amenable to standard tree algorithms, for example, the CART algorithm or the “Tree Alternate Optimization” (TAO)
[
55
,
56
,
57
,
58
]
algorithm, both providing tree structures with a hierarchical selection using the features
\boldsymbol{x}
and that satisfy the requirements in Eq.
C.14
.
These algorithms proceed by recursively splitting the training data along either axis-aligned (for CART) or linear combinations of the input features (for TAO), reducing the loss at each iteration. The maximum iteration depth and the minimum number of events in each node are hyperparameters that regularize the fit. If no more splits can be performed, the terminal selections (nodes) represent a phase space partitioning
\mathcal{J}
of the form in Eq.
C.14
and the quantities
\hat{\Delta}_{j}
can be computed from Eq.
C.29
. The tree then estimates the (log-)DCR as in Eq.
C.26
.
As a function of
\boldsymbol{x}
, the prediction of a single tree changes discontinuously if
\boldsymbol{x}
traverses a boundary between nodes, and the possibly poor approximation close to the boundaries weakens the learner. Utilizing boosting, i.e., using a sequence of trees in Eq.
C.3
and Eq.
C.4
, we recover the smooth behavior in
\boldsymbol{x}
of an arbitrarily expressive regressor. Because each tree in the boosted result in Eq.
C.5
is parametric in
{\boldsymbol{\nu}}
, so is the final parametric regression tree.

C.4
Algorithm summary

We can combine the steps in Secs.
C.1
–
C.3
to summarize the BPT algorithm. It is an iterative fit of a tree-based weak learner with the loss in Eq.
C.31
to the residuals of the preceding boosting iteration whose predictions are obtained from Eqs.
C.8
–
C.11
.
Concretely, we start with training data
\mathcal{D}_{0}
at a reference point and several synthetic data sets associated with model parameters
{\boldsymbol{\nu}}\in\mathcal{V}
. We must have enough data so
V_{AB}
has full rank. We fit a weak learner using the CART algorithm. At each iteration, the CART algorithm recursively divides the feature space by greedily selecting the dimension and cut value combination that minimizes the loss function.
Overfitting is mitigated by enforcing a maximum tree depth and a minimum number of events in each terminal node.
We construct new synthetic data from the weak learner’s prediction using Eq.
C.11
to replace
\mathcal{D}_{\boldsymbol{\nu}}
. This reweighting procedure brings the samples
\mathcal{D}_{\boldsymbol{\nu}}
closer to
\mathcal{D}_{0}
by an amount controlled by the learning rate
\eta
. We iterate the whole procedure
B
times and obtain the final result from Eq.
C.5
as

\displaystyle\hat{T}(\boldsymbol{x}|{\boldsymbol{\nu}})=\log\hat{S}(\boldsymbol{x}|{\boldsymbol{\nu}})=\sum_{b=1}^{B}\eta^{(b)}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}^{(b)}}\mathbbm{1}_{j}(\boldsymbol{x})\nu_{A}\hat{\Delta}^{(b)}_{A,j},

(C.32)

where
{\boldsymbol{\nu}}
is the model parameter we like to predict for and
\mathcal{J}^{(b)}
is the phase-space partitioning obtained from the CART algorithm. The
\hat{\Delta}^{(b)}_{A,j}
are the polynomial coefficients of the DCR parametrization in the terminal node
j
at boosting iteration
b
.
Algorithm
1
is a pseudo-code summary of these steps and defines the parametric regression tree algorithm.
It is efficiently implemented using the
Numpy
package
[
88
]
and available at
[
89
]
.

C.5
An analytic toy example

To illustrate the BPT, we consider an arbitrarily chosen one-dimensional two-parameter model

\displaystyle{\textrm{d}}\sigma(\boldsymbol{x}|\nu_{1},\nu_{2})

\displaystyle=N\exp\left(0.25\left(\nu_{1}\sin(x)+\nu_{2}\cos(0.5x)\right)^{2}\right){\textrm{d}}x

(C.33)

with support
x\in[-\pi,\pi]
. The logarithm of the cross-section is a quadratic polynomial in
{\boldsymbol{\nu}}
for all
\boldsymbol{x}
, suggesting a perfect fit with a two-parameter parametric tree at quadratic accuracy. For the training, we chose five base points
\mathcal{V}=\{(0.5,0),\,(0,0.5),\,(1,0),\,(0,1),\,(0.5,0.5)\}
that lead to a full-rank matrix
V
in Eq.
C.30
. With a nominal data set at
(\nu_{1},\nu_{2})=(0,0)
, we have six synthetic data sets, each with
5\cdot 10^{5}
events, sufficient to train the algorithm. We fit
B=100
boosting iterations and require a maximum tree depth of
4
and a minimum requirement for the number of events in each terminal node, which is 50 events. The learning rate is set to 0.2 for all boosting iterations.

In Fig.
12
, we compare the true and predicted values for the DCR for various model parameters. The model parameter configurations include the training and new synthetic data, which are absent during training. After only five iterations, the prediction begins to resemble the true DCRs. After 100 iterations, the fit is nearly perfect; dashed lines show the true DCR from the training data and are not separately visible because of the fit’s quality, including the parameter configurations not used during training. In realistic applications, the logarithms of the true DCRs will not be exactly polynomial, mandating some degree of validation of the fit quality on unseen data.

Appendix D
Additional angular observables in the
\mathrm{t}\overline{\mathrm{t}}(2\ell)
final state

We briefly describe the angular observables introduced in Ref.
[
78
]
. A measurement of these quantities is performed in Ref.
[
77
]
.
After reconstructing the top-quark momenta, the event is boosted into the
\mathrm{t}\overline{\mathrm{t}}
rest frame, and the following axes are defined. The axis
\hat{k}
points toward the positively charged top quark. The axis
\hat{r}
is orthogonal to
\hat{k}
and must lie within the beam plane, spanned by the
\hat{k}
and the momentum of the incoming parton in the
\mathrm{t}\overline{\mathrm{t}}
rest frame. The axis
\hat{n}
is orthogonal to the beam plane, and
\{\hat{r},\hat{k},\hat{n}\}
must form a right-handed orthonormal basis.
The lepton directions of flight, denoted by
\hat{\ell}^{+}
and
\hat{\ell}^{-}
, are measured in the corresponding top quark center-of-mass frame, which is reached from the
\mathrm{t}\overline{\mathrm{t}}
frame by a rotation-free Lorentz transformation. Then, the quantities
\xi_{ab}=\cos\theta_{a}^{+}\cos\theta_{b}^{-}
are defined where
\cos\theta_{a}^{+}=\hat{\ell}^{+}\cdot\hat{a}
and
\cos\theta_{b}^{-}=\hat{\ell}^{-}\cdot\hat{a}
and the axis
a
and
b
can each be one of
\{\hat{r},\hat{k},\hat{n}\}
. For
a\neq b
, sums and differences of these are considered, e.g.,
\xi_{nr}^{\pm}=\xi_{nr}\pm\xi_{rn}
and analogously for the other combinations. Two more axes
\hat{r}^{\ast}
and
\hat{k}^{\ast}
are defined by flipping the direction of
\hat{r}
and
\hat{k}
depending on the sign of the top quarks’ rapidity difference in the laboratory frame while keeping the system orthonormal. The resulting 12 independent quantities characterize the spin-density matrix of the
\mathrm{t}\overline{\mathrm{t}}(2\ell)
system. More details, including the behavior of these quantities under the discrete SM symmetries, are provided in Ref.
[
78
]
.
