Abstract
. We prove that the topological entropy of any dominant rational self-map of a projective variety defined over a complete non-Archimedean field is bounded from above by the maximum of its dynamical degrees, thereby extending a theorem of Gromov and Dinh-Sibony from the complex to the non-Archimedean setting. We proceed by proving that any regular self-map which admits a regular extension to a projective model defined over the valuation ring has necessarily zero entropy. To this end we introduce the ϵ \epsilon -reduction of a Berkovich analytic space, a notion of independent interest.

To state our main theorem, we now need to discuss the notion of dynamical degrees first introduced by Russakovskii-Shiffman

When the characteristic of

Our first result states that the upper bound (

Theorem A
.

Let

Let us briefly indicate how our proof works in arbitrary dimension. For each

Fix an ample line bundle

, and

of the line bundle
\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\pi^{*}_{i}L

In the complex setting, we may choose a smooth positive metrization on

By Wirtinger’s theorem the volume of

By a clever calculation due to Gromov, we obtain

\limsup_{n}\frac{1}{n}\log\deg_{L}(\Gamma_{n})\leq\max_{0\leq i\leq d}\{\log\lambda_{i}(f)\}

On the other hand, the minimality of complex subvarieties (or a theorem by Lelong) implies the volume

-separated orbits up to a uniform constant which implies (

Definitions of a Kähler form have been proposed in

Given any entourage

It is natural to ask whether conversely any regular self-map of a projective variety having vanishing topological entropy has good reduction.
In dimension

Conjecture 1

, and complete the proof of Theorem

in Section

.
Base change is a powerful tool in non-Archimedean analysis. We discuss the behaviour of the topological entropy under this operation in the Appendix

1.
Topological entropy

In this section, we gather some general facts on the topological entropy of continuous maps, and more generally
for partially defined continuous maps on compact sets.

1.1.
Complexity of covers

A finite cover
\mathfrak{U}=\{U_{i}\}_{i\in I}
of a non-empty set
X
is a finite family such that
\cup_{i}U_{i}=X
.
We define its complexity
N(\mathfrak{U})\in{\mathbb{N}}^{*}
as the minimum number of sets belonging to
\mathfrak{U}
that
are needed to cover
X
.
Given a second cover
\mathfrak{V}=\{V_{j}\}_{j\in J}
, we define the joint cover by
\mathfrak{U}\vee\mathfrak{V}=\{U_{i}\cap V_{j}\}_{i,j}
.
Observe that
N(\mathfrak{U}\vee\mathfrak{V})\leq N(\mathfrak{U})\times N(\mathfrak{V})
.
A cover
\mathfrak{V}
is a refinement of
\mathfrak{U}
if for all
j\in J
one can find
i=i(j)\in I
such that
V_{j}\subset U_{i}
.
When
\mathfrak{V}
refines
\mathfrak{U}
, we write
\mathfrak{V}\leq\mathfrak{U}
, and it follows that
N(\mathfrak{V})\geq N(\mathfrak{U})
.

Let
f\colon X\to X
be any map. Any cover
\mathfrak{U}
induces a cover
f^{-1}(\mathfrak{U})=\{f^{-1}(U_{i})\}_{i\in I}

whose complexity satisfies
N(f^{-1}(\mathfrak{U}))\leq N(\mathfrak{U})
with equality when
f
is surjective.
For each
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
, write
\mathfrak{U}_{n}=\mathfrak{U}\vee\cdots\vee f^{-(n-1)}(\mathfrak{U})
.
The previous observations yield

N(\mathfrak{U}_{n+m})\leq N(\mathfrak{U}_{n})\times N(f^{-n}(\mathfrak{U}_{m}))\leq N(\mathfrak{U}_{n})\times N(\mathfrak{U}_{m})

so that we can define
h(f,\mathfrak{U})=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log N(\mathfrak{U}_{n})=\liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log N(\mathfrak{U}_{n})\in{\mathbb{N}}^{*}
.
Sometimes, we write
N(f,\mathfrak{U},n)
for
N(\mathfrak{U}_{n})
to emphasis
f.

Remark 1.1
.

We shall work both with open covers (when
U_{i}
’s are all open) and with compact covers (when
U_{i}
’s are all compact). The latter situation appears naturally in
Berkovich theory where proper analytic sets admits finite covers by affinoids.

Let
\Gamma
be any non-empty family of finite covers of
X
. Then we define
the
\Gamma
-entropy of
f
by:

h(f,\Gamma):=\sup_{\mathfrak{U}\in\Gamma}h(f,\mathfrak{U})~.

For any two non-empty families
\Gamma_{1},\Gamma_{2}
of finite covers of
X
, we say that
\Gamma_{1}
is finer than
\Gamma_{2}
, if
any finite cover in
\Gamma_{2}
admits a refinement by a finite cover in
\Gamma_{1}
.

The next lemma follows immediately from the remarks in the previous section.

Lemma 1.1
.

Let
X
be a set and
f:X\to X
be an endomorphism. Let
\Gamma_{1},\Gamma_{2}
be two non-empty families of finite covers of
X
.
If
\Gamma_{1}
is finer than
\Gamma_{2}
, then
h(f,\Gamma_{1})\geq h(f,\Gamma_{2}).

1.2.
Topological entropy and the variational principle

When
X
is a topological space, denote by
\Gamma_{{\rm top}}
the family of finite open covers.
The topological entropy of
f
is then defined as

\htop(f):=\htop(f,\Gamma_{\rm top})=\sup_{\mathfrak{U}\in\Gamma_{\rm top}}h(f,\mathfrak{U})~.

Recall that a quasi-compact space is a topological space for which any open cover admits a finite subcover,
and that compact spaces are Hausdorff quasi-compact spaces. The notion of topological entropy is mainly interesting when
X
is quasi-compact and
f
is continuous.
Although
h(f,\mathfrak{U})
is finite for all
\mathfrak{U}
, it may happen that
\htop(f)
is infinite (e.g. when
f
is the full shift on an infinite set of symbols), so that in general
\htop(f)\in[0,+\infty]
.

Suppose now that
X
is compact. A Radon measure is a positive measure
\mu

on the
\sigma
-algebra of Borelian sets which is inner regular, i.e., for all Borel set
A\subset X

we have
\mu(A)=\sup\mu(K)
where the supremum is taken over all compact sets
K\subset A
.

By Riesz’ representation theorem, any positive linear functional on the space of continuous functions on
X

is represented by a unique Radon measure.

Let
\mu
be any probability measure on
X
.
The entropy of a finite partition
\xi=\{\xi_{i}\}_{i\in I}
of
X
by Borel sets is defined to be

H(\xi)=-\sum_{i\in I}\mu(\xi_{i})\log\mu(\xi_{i})
. One can check that
H(\xi\vee\zeta)\leq H(\xi)+H(\zeta)
,
so that if
f\colon X\to X
is a measure preserving map we have

H(\xi_{n+m})\leq H(\xi_{n})+H(\xi_{m})
where
\xi_{n}=\xi\vee\cdots\vee f^{-(n-1)}(\xi)

so that
h(f,\xi)=\lim\frac{1}{n}H(\xi_{n})
is well-defined.

The metric entropy of
f
with respect to
\mu
is then the quantity

h(f,\mu)=\sup_{\xi}h(f,\xi)
where the supremum is taken over all finite partitions of
X
by Borel subsets.

The topological entropy and the metric entropy are related by the so-called variational principle which states:

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
X
be a (Hausdorff) compact topological space, and let
f\colon X\to X
be any continuous map.
Let
\mathcal{M}(f)
be the set of all Radon probability measures that are
f
-invariant.
Then

\htop(f)=\sup_{\mathcal{M}(f)}h_{\mu}(f)~.

The original proof by Goodwyn
[
Gow69
]
and Goodman
[
Gom71
]
is valid in this degree of generality.
Note that the Hausdorff assumption plays a crucial role in their proofs. It is believed that the variational principle holds for normal quasi-compact spaces, see
[
Ho74
]

and the
references therein.

1.3.
Uniform structures and entropy of continuous maps

Our basic reference is
[
Ke55
, §6]
.
A uniform structure on a set
X
is a non-empty collection
\Phi
of sets
\mathcal{E}\subset X\times X
(called entourage)
containing the diagonal
\Delta=\{(x,x),\,x\in X\}
, and satisfying the conditions:

•

\mathcal{E},\mathcal{E}^{\prime}\in\Phi
implies
\mathcal{E}\cap\mathcal{E}^{\prime}\in\Phi
;

•

\mathcal{E}\in\Phi
and
\mathcal{E}\subset\mathcal{E}^{\prime}
implies
\mathcal{E}^{\prime}\in\Phi
;

•

\mathcal{E}^{-1}:=\{(y,x),\,(x,y)\in\mathcal{E}\}\in\Phi
whenever
\mathcal{E}\in\Phi
;

•

for any
\mathcal{E}\in\Phi
, there exists
\mathcal{E}^{\prime}\in\Phi
such that
\mathcal{E}\supset\mathcal{E}^{\prime}\circ\mathcal{E}^{\prime}:=\{(x,y),\,(x,z),(z,y)\in\mathcal{E}\text{ for some }z\}
.

An entourage
\mathcal{E}
satisfying
\mathcal{E}^{-1}=\mathcal{E}
is called symmetric.

Any uniform structure
\Phi
defines a topology
\Phi^{\top}
for which a set
U
is open iff for every point
x\in U
one can find an entourage
\mathcal{E}
such that

B_{\mathcal{E}}(x)=\{y\in X,\,(x,y)\in\mathcal{E}\}\subset U
.
A topological space with collection of open sets
\mathcal{T}

is uniformazible if one can find a uniform structure
\Phi
such that
\Phi^{\top}=\mathcal{T}
.
One can prove that a topological space is uniformizable iff it is completely regular, see
[
Ke55
, p.188]
.

Any compact space
X
is uniformizable; more precisely there exists a unique uniform structure
\Phi
on
X
defining its topology, see
[
Ke55
, p.198]
.
The uniform structure is given by the set of all neighborhoods of the diagonal. Note that any entourage
\mathcal{E}
contains a symmetric one of the form

\cup_{i}U_{i}\times U_{i}
where
\{U_{i}\}_{i\in I}
is a finite open cover of
X
.

Any metric space
(X,d)
is also uniformizable: take
\Phi
to be the neighborhoods of
\Delta
included in

\{(x,y),d(x,y)<\epsilon\}
for some
\epsilon>0
.

Let
X
be any compact space equipped with its canonical uniform structure. Pick
any continuous map
f\colon X\to X
. Observe that
f
is uniformly continuous, i.e.
the preimage of any entourage by
f
remains an entourage. We aim at characterizing the topological entropy of
f

in terms of entourages, following
[
Ho74
, §2]
.

Let
\mathcal{E}
be any symmetric entourage of
X
, and pick any integer
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
.

•

Two points
x,y
are said to be
(n,\mathcal{E})
-separated iff there exists an integer

k\leq n
such that
(f^{k}(x),f^{k}(y))\notin\mathcal{E}
.

•

A family of points
F\subset X
is called an
(n,\mathcal{E})
-covering set,
iff for any point
x\in X
, there exists a point
y\in F
such that
(f^{k}(x),f^{k}(y))\in\mathcal{E}
for all
k\leq n
.

For any symmetric entourage
\mathcal{E}
, and for integer
n
, we write

R(n,\mathcal{E})
for the minimum of the cardinality of an

(n,\mathcal{E})
-covering set, and
S(n,\mathcal{E})
for the maximum of the cardinality of
set of points that are pairwise
(n,\mathcal{E})
-separated.

Because
X
is assumed to be compact, these two functions have finite values.
They are also decreasing on
\mathcal{E}
with respect to the inclusion.
Let us prove that

(3)

R(n,\mathcal{E})\leq S(n,\mathcal{E})\leq R(n,\mathcal{E}^{\prime})

if
\mathcal{E}^{\prime}\circ\mathcal{E}^{\prime}\subset\mathcal{E}
. Any maximal

(n,\mathcal{E})
-separating set is also an
(n,\mathcal{E})
-covering set hence
S(n,\mathcal{E})\geq R(n,\mathcal{E})
. If
E
is an
(n,\mathcal{E})
-separating set, and
F
an
(n,\mathcal{E}^{\prime})
-covering set with

\mathcal{E}^{\prime}\circ\mathcal{E}^{\prime}\subset\mathcal{E}
, we choose
for all
x\in E
a point
\phi(x)\in F
such that

(x,\phi(x))
lies in
\mathcal{E}^{\prime}
. It is then clear that
\phi
is then
injective.

Proposition 1.3
.

For any continuous map
f\colon X\to X
on a compact space
X
, we have

\htop(f)=\sup_{\mathcal{E}}\limsup_{n}\frac{1}{n}\log R(n,\mathcal{E})=\sup_{\mathcal{E}}\limsup_{n}\frac{1}{n}\log S(n,\mathcal{E})~.

When
(X,d)
is a compact metric space, we recover the standard definition of the topological entropy
given by Bowen
[
Bo71
]
.

Proof.

Denote by
h(f)
the quantity in the right hand side.
Let us fix
\mathcal{E}
any entourage of
X
. One can then find a finite open cover

\mathfrak{U}=\{U_{i}\}
such that
U_{i}\times U_{i}\subset\mathcal{E}
for all
i
. If
E
is an
(n-1,\mathcal{E})
-separating set of maximal cardinality,
then an element of
\bigvee_{0}^{n-1}f^{-i}\mathfrak{U}
contains at most one element of
E
, hence

(4)

S(n-1,\mathcal{E})\leq N\left(\bigvee_{0}^{n-1}f^{-i}\mathfrak{U}\right).

It follows that

\htop(f)\geq h(f)
.

For the converse inequality, fix any finite open cover
\mathfrak{U}=\{U_{i}\}
.

Lemma 1.4
.

There exists an entourage
\mathcal{E}
such that for every
x\in X
, the set
B_{\mathcal{E}}(x)
is included in some
U_{i}
.

Fix any such entourage,
and let
B^{n}_{\mathcal{E}}(x):=\{y,\,(f^{i}(x),f^{i}(y))\in\mathcal{E}\text{ for all }0\leq i\leq n\}
.
Observe that for all
x\in X
and for all
0\leq i\leq n
we have
f^{i}(B^{n}_{\mathcal{E}}(x))\subseteq B_{\mathcal{E}}(f^{i}(x))
, so that
f^{i}(B^{n}_{\mathcal{E}}(x))
is contained in an element of

\mathfrak{U}
.
Let
F
be a
(n-1,\mathcal{E})
-covering set with minimal cardinality so that

X\subseteq\cup_{x\in F}B^{n-1}_{\mathcal{E}}(x)
.
Since, for every
x\in F
, there exist
j(x)_{0},\dots,j(x)_{n-1}
such that

B^{n-1}_{\mathcal{E}}(x)\subseteq U_{j(x)_{0}}\cap\dots\cap f^{-(n-1)}(U_{j(x)_{n-1}}),

it follows that

\{U_{j(x)_{0}}\cap\dots\cap f^{-(n-1)}(U_{j(x)_{n-1}}),x\in F\}
forms a cover of
X
,
and we have

(5)

N\left(\bigvee_{0}^{n-1}f^{-i}\mathfrak{U}\right)\leq R(n-1,\mathcal{E}).

The inequality

h(f)\geq\htop(f)
follows.
∎

Proof of Lemma
1.4
.

Pick any
x\in X
. We claim that there exists an entourage
\mathcal{E}(x)
such that

B_{\mathcal{E}(x)}(x)\subset U_{i(x)}
.

Pick any
U_{i}
containing
x
, set
i(x):=i
, and choose two open neighborhoods
V,W
of
x
such that

x\in V\subset\bar{V}\subset W\subset U_{i}
. Set
\mathcal{E}(x):=W^{2}\cup(X\setminus\bar{V})^{2}
.
By construction
B_{\mathcal{E}(x)}(x)\subset W\subset U_{i}
which proves the claim.

For each
x
, choose an entourage
\mathcal{E}^{\prime}(x)
such that
\mathcal{E}^{\prime}(x)\circ\mathcal{E}^{\prime}(x)\subset\mathcal{E}(x)
, and pick a finite cover

X\subset B_{\mathcal{E}^{\prime}(x_{1})}(x_{1})\cup\cdots\cup B_{\mathcal{E}^{\prime}(x_{n})}(x_{n})
.
Set
\mathcal{E}:=\cap_{j=1}^{n}\mathcal{E}^{\prime}(x_{j})
.

Choose
x\in X
. Then we may find
j\in\{1,\cdots,n\}
such that
x\in B_{\mathcal{E}^{\prime}(x_{j})}(x_{j})
.
It follows that

B_{\mathcal{E}}(x)\subset B_{\mathcal{E}^{\prime}(x_{j})}(x)\subset B_{\mathcal{E}^{\prime}(x_{j})\circ\mathcal{E}^{\prime}(x_{j})}(x_{j})\subset B_{\mathcal{E}(x_{j})}(x_{j})\subset U_{i(x_{j})}~.

This concludes the proof.
∎

1.4.
Partially continuous maps

Let
X
be any compact space equipped with its canonical uniform structure.
A partially continuous self-map of
X
is a continuous map
f\colon X\setminus I(f)\to X
where

I(f)\subseteq X
is a closed subset.

Remark 1.2
.

A typical example of partially continuous self-map is a rational self-map of a complex projective variety.
Another source of examples arise from rational maps on algebraic varieties endowed with the constructible topology, see Section
2.4
below.
Note that in the latter case
I(f)
can have non-empty interior.

We define an
admissible sequence
of
(X,f)
to be a sequence
\xi:=\{\xi_{n},n\geq 0\}
of (possibly empty) subsets of
X
such that for
n\geq 1
,
\xi_{n}\subseteq X\setminus I(f)
and
f(\xi_{n})\subseteq\xi_{n-1}
. By induction on
n
, one sees that if
\xi_{n}
is a non-empty open set for all
n
, then the
n
th iterate
f^{n}
defines a partially continuous map
with
I(f^{n}):=X\setminus\xi_{n}
.

There is a maximal choice
\xi^{\max}(f)
of admissible sequence which is defined as follows:
\xi^{\max}(f)_{0}:=X
, for
n\geq 1
,
\xi^{\max}(f)_{n}:=f|_{X\setminus I(f)}^{-1}(\xi^{\max}(f)_{n-1}).
We note that all sets
\xi^{\max}(f)_{n}
are open.

Another natural choice of admissible sequence is
\xi^{\self}(f)
given by
\xi^{\self}_{n}(f):=\cap_{j\geq 0}\xi^{\max}_{j}(f)
(note however that
\xi^{\self}_{n}(f)
may not be open).
When
I(f)=\emptyset
, we have
\xi^{\max}(f)_{n}=\xi^{\self}(f)_{n}=X
for
n\geq 0.

Remark 1.3
.

Since
X
is compact, it is a Baire space, hence
\overline{\xi^{\max}(f)_{n}}=X
for all
n
implies

\overline{\xi^{\self}(f)_{n}}=X
.

Let
\mathcal{E}
be any symmetric entourage of
X
. For
n\geq 0
and every
x\in\xi^{\max}_{n},

define
B^{n}_{\mathcal{E}}(x):=\{y\in\xi_{n}^{\max},\,(f^{i}(x),f^{i}(y))\in\mathcal{E},i=0,\dots,n\}.
It is a neighborhood of
x
in
\xi_{n}^{\max}
,
and the map
\mathcal{E}\mapsto B^{n}_{\mathcal{E}}(x)
is increasing with respect to inclusion.

•

If
y\in B^{n}_{\mathcal{E}}(x)
, then
x\in B^{n}_{\mathcal{E}}(y).

•

For any symmetric entourage
\mathcal{E}^{\prime}
such that
\mathcal{E}^{\prime}\circ\mathcal{E}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathcal{E}
, then
x,y\in B^{n}_{\mathcal{E}^{\prime}}(z)
implies
y\in B^{n}_{\mathcal{E}}(x).

Lemma 1.5
.

Let
\mathcal{E},\mathcal{E}^{\prime}
be any symmetric entourages of
X
satisfying
\mathcal{E}^{\prime}\circ\mathcal{E}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathcal{E}
.
Let
Y,Z
be subsets of
\xi_{n}^{\max}
. If
Y\subseteq\cup_{z\in Z}B^{n}_{\mathcal{E}^{\prime}}(z),
then
\overline{Y}\cap\xi_{n}^{\max}\subseteq\cup_{z\in Z}B^{n}_{\mathcal{E}}(z).

In particular, we have
\overline{Y}\cap\xi_{n}^{\max}\subseteq\cup_{y\in Y}B^{n}_{\mathcal{E}}(y).

Proof.

For every
x\in\overline{Y}\cap\xi_{n}^{\max}
, there is
y\in B^{n}_{\mathcal{E}^{\prime}}(x)\cap Y
.
Pick
z\in Z
such that
y\in B^{n}_{\mathcal{E}^{\prime}}(z)
. Then
x\in B^{n}_{\mathcal{E}}(z)
as required.
∎

1.5.
Topological entropy of a partially continuous map

We fix any partially continuous map
f
on a compact space
X
and an admissible sequence
\xi
.
We shall define the entropy
\htop(f,\xi)
.

Pick any symmetric entourage
\mathcal{E}
of
X
, and any integer
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
.

•

Two points
x,y\in\xi_{n}
are said to be
(n,\xi,\mathcal{E})
-separated iff
x\not\in B^{n}_{\mathcal{E}}(y).

•

A family of points
F\subset\xi_{n}
is called an
(n,\xi,\mathcal{E})
-covering set,
iff
\xi_{n}\subseteq\cup_{x\in F}B^{n}_{\mathcal{E}}(x).

For any symmetric entourage
\mathcal{E}
, and for integer
n
, we write

R(n,\xi,\mathcal{E})
for the minimum of the cardinality of an

(n,\xi,\mathcal{E})
-covering set, and
S(n,\xi,\mathcal{E})
for the maximum of the cardinality of
the set of points that are pairwise
(n,\xi,\mathcal{E})
-separated.

When
\xi=\xi^{\max}
, we often omit
\xi
in
R(n,\xi,\mathcal{E})
and
S(n,\xi,\mathcal{E})
.

Lemma 1.6
.

For every
n\geq 0
,
R(n,\xi,\mathcal{E})
is finite.
Moreover, we have

(6)

R(n,\xi,\mathcal{E})\leq S(n,\xi,\mathcal{E})\leq R(n,\xi,\mathcal{E}^{\prime})<\infty

if
\mathcal{E}^{\prime}\circ\mathcal{E}^{\prime}\subset\mathcal{E}
.

Proof.

We begin with proving that for any subset
U\subset X
there exists a finite subset
F_{U}\subset U
such that
U\subset\cup_{x\in F_{U}}B_{\mathcal{E}^{\prime}}(x).

Indeed since
X
is compact, there is a finite subset
F^{\prime}\subseteq X
such that
U\subseteq X=\cup_{x\in F^{\prime}}B_{\mathcal{E}^{\prime}}(x).

Set
F^{\prime\prime}:=\{x\in F^{\prime},B_{\mathcal{E}^{\prime}}(x)\cap U\neq\emptyset\}.

For every
x\in F^{\prime\prime}
, pick
y_{x}\in B_{\mathcal{E}^{\prime}}(x)\cap U.
Set
F_{U}=\{y_{x},\,x\in F^{\prime\prime}\}
. Then for every
y\in U
, there is
x\in F^{\prime\prime}
such that

y\in B_{\mathcal{E}^{\prime}}(x)
, hence
y\in B_{\mathcal{E}}(y_{x})
which concludes the proof of the claim.

Let us now prove that
R(n,\xi,\mathcal{E})
is finite by induction on
n.

Pick any symmetric entourage
\mathcal{E}^{\prime}
such that
\mathcal{E}^{\prime}\circ\mathcal{E}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathcal{E}
.

The fact that
R(0,\xi,\mathcal{E})
is finite follows from the claim applied to
U=\xi_{0}
.
Next assume that
R(n,\xi,\mathcal{E})<+\infty
. We will show that
R(n+1,\xi,\mathcal{E})<+\infty.

The induction hypothesis implies that there is a finite set
G\subseteq\xi_{n}
such that
\xi_{n}\subseteq\cup_{x\in G}B^{n}_{\mathcal{E}^{\prime}}(x).

By the previous claim for each
x\in G
, there exists a finite set
F_{x,n}\subset f^{-1}(B^{n}_{\mathcal{E}^{\prime}}(x))\cap\xi_{n+1}
such that

f^{-1}(B^{n}_{\mathcal{E}^{\prime}}(x))\cap\xi_{n+1}\subset\cup_{x\in F_{x,n}}B_{\mathcal{E}^{\prime}}(x).

Pick any point
y\in\xi_{n+1}
. Then there exists a point

x\in G
such that
f(y)\in B^{n}_{\mathcal{E}^{\prime}}(x)
, and then a point

x^{\prime}\in F_{x,n}
such that
y\in B_{\mathcal{E}^{\prime}}(x^{\prime})
. Since
F_{x,n}\subset f^{-1}(B^{n}_{\mathcal{E}^{\prime}}(x))\cap\xi_{n+1}
, we have

f(x^{\prime})\in B^{n}_{\mathcal{E}^{\prime}}(x)
hence
y\in B^{n+1}_{\mathcal{E}}(x^{\prime})
, and
R(n+1,\xi,\mathcal{E})
is less than the cardinality of

\cup_{x\in G}F_{x,n}
.

The proof of (
6
) is identical to (
3
).
∎

Note that both
S(n,\xi,\mathcal{E})
and
R(n,\xi,\mathcal{E})
are increasing on
n
and decreasing on
\mathcal{E}
with respect to the inclusion, and by (
3
),
we have

\sup_{\mathcal{E}}\limsup_{n}\frac{1}{n}\log R(n,\xi,\mathcal{E})=\sup_{\mathcal{E}}\limsup_{n}\frac{1}{n}\log S(n,\xi,\mathcal{E})~.

Definition 1.1
.

Pick any partially continuous self-map
f
on a compact space
X
. Suppose
\xi
is an admissible sequence for which
\xi_{n}\neq\emptyset
for all
n
.
Then we define the topological entropy of
(X,f,\xi)
by

\htop(f,\xi):=\sup_{\mathcal{E}}\limsup_{n}\frac{1}{n}\log R(n,\xi,\mathcal{E})=\sup_{\mathcal{E}}\limsup_{n}\frac{1}{n}\log S(n,\xi,\mathcal{E})\in[0,+\infty]~.

We also write
\htop(f):=\htop(f,\xi^{\max}),
and call it the entropy of
f
.

Remark 1.4
.

Note that when
\xi_{n}=\emptyset
for some
n
, then
R(n,\xi,\mathcal{E})=0
.
In that case, it is convenient to set
\htop(f,\xi)=-\infty
.

We now aim at comparing the entropy when the admissible sequence is modified.

Lemma 1.7
.

Let
\xi,\xi^{\prime}
be two admissible sequences for
(X,f).

Let
\mathcal{E}^{\prime},\mathcal{E}^{\prime\prime}
be symmetric entourages satisfying

Proof.

We keep the same notations as in the previous paragraph.

3.
Zero entropy maps on Noetherian and Priestley topological spaces

In this section, we prove two results. First we show that the topological entropy of any continuous self-map
f\colon X\to X
on a quasi-compact Noetherian topological space is
0
(Theorem
3.1
).
We actually estimate directly the growth of the complexity of covers of the form
\mathfrak{U}\vee\cdots\vee f^{-n}\mathfrak{U}
.

Then we discuss the notion of Priestley topological spaces which are compact totally disconnected spaces homeomorphic the
spectrum of a commutative ring endowed with its constructible topology.
We introduce the notion of Noetherian Priestley space, and prove the vanishing of the entropy of any
partially continuous self-map of such a space (Theorem
3.4
). This in turn implies Theorem
C
.

In Section
3.4.4
, we discuss an example of Noetherian Priestley spaces that will play a prominent role in the next section:
the set of all (not necessarily prime) ideals of a Noetherian ring.

3.1.
Entropy on Noetherian spaces

A topological space
X
is said to be Noetherian if any decreasing sequence of closed subsets is eventually stationnary.
Equivalently
X
is Noetherian if and only if every nonempty collection of closed subsets has an element which is minimal under inclusion.
A closed set
F\subset X
is said to be irreducible if it cannot be written as a union
F=F_{1}\cup F_{2}
of two strict closed subsets
F_{1},F_{2}\subsetneq F
.
It is a fact that any closed subset
F\subset X
can be written as a finite union
F=F_{1}\cup\cdots\cup F_{r}
of irreducible closed subsets.
A decomposition with
F_{i}\not\subset F_{j}
for
i\neq j
is unique up to permutation, and in this case the
F_{i}
’s are called the irreducible components of
F
, see
[
Ha77
, Proposition 1.5]
.

One also defines the dimension of a Noetherian topological space
X
as the supremum over all integers
d
such that there exists a chain
\emptyset\subsetneq F_{0}\subset F_{1}\subset\cdots\subset F_{d}
of distinct irreducible closed subsets of length
d+1
.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
X
be any quasi-compact Noetherian space, and let
f\colon X\to X
be any continuous map. Then for any open cover
\mathfrak{U}
of
X
, and for any
\epsilon>0
, one has

(11)

N(\mathfrak{U}_{n})\leq C(1+\epsilon)^{n}

for some
C>0
. In particular, we have
\htop(f)=0
.

Remark 3.1
.

In particular for any quasi-compact Noetherian scheme
X
, and any regular map

we have
\htop(f,\tau^{\zar})=0
which yields a special case of Theorem

.

Remark 3.2
.

It was proved by Gignac that any signed Borel measure of finite mass on a Noetherian space for which every non-empty irreducible closed subset has a generic point
(a.k.a. a Zariski space) is necessarily atomic, see
[

.
It is however not clear whether the variational principle applies on arbitrary non Hausdorff quasi-compact spaces so that Gignac’s theorem
does not immediately imply

Proof.

We need the following lemma.

Lemma 3.2
.

Pick any integer
\epsilon>0
. Then one can find positive numbers

and positive integers

such that

N(n,\mathfrak{U})\leq N(n-1,\mathfrak{U})+M_{1}\,N(n-N_{1},\mathfrak{U})+\cdots+M_{k}\,N(n-N_{k},\mathfrak{U})~,

for all

.

This lemma implies that the growth of the sequence
N(n,\mathfrak{U})
is bounded from above by the growth of the sequence
\{U_{n}\}
defined by induction

U_{n}=U_{n-1}+M_{1}\,U_{n-N_{1}}+\cdots+M_{k}\,U_{n-N_{k}}
,
U_{i}=N(i,\mathfrak{U})
for all

.
The characteristic polynomial of the linear recurrence equation is equal to

P(T)=T^{N_{k}}-T^{N_{k}-1}-M_{1}T^{N_{k}-N_{1}}-\cdots-M_{k}
.
Note that any complex root
\lambda
of

satisfies

|\lambda|=\left|1+\frac{M_{1}}{\lambda^{N_{1}-1}}+\cdots+\frac{M_{k}}{\lambda^{N_{k}-1}}\right|

so that if
|\lambda|\geq 1+\epsilon
, then we obtain

|\lambda|\leq 1+\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\frac{\epsilon}{10^{i}}<1+\epsilon
, which is a contradiction.
It follows that all complex roots of

have modulus
\leq 1+\epsilon
, and the bound (

For any closed subset

, hence we may (and shall) assume that
X
is irreducible.
Write
\mathfrak{U}=\{U_{1},\dots,U_{m}\}.

For every irreducible closed subset
Z
of
X
, there is
j(Z)\in\{1,\dots,m\}
such that
Z\cap U_{j(Z)}\neq\emptyset.

Fix an integer
N\geq 1.
Set
H(Z,N):=Z\setminus(\cap_{i=0}^{N}f^{-i}(U_{j(f^{i}(Z))})),
which is a (possibly reducible) proper closed subset of
Z.

For
n\geq N,
if
\mathfrak{V}
is a subset of
\mathfrak{U}_{n-N}
that covers
f^{N}(Z)
and
\mathfrak{V}^{\prime}
is a subset of
\mathfrak{U}_{n}
which covers
H(Z,N)
, the union of

\{U_{j(Z)}\cap\dots\cap f^{-N}U_{j(f^{N-1}(Z))}\}\vee f^{-N}(\mathfrak{V})\text{ and }\mathfrak{V}^{\prime}

is a subset of
\mathfrak{U}_{n}
which covers
Z.

We thus get

(12)

N_{Z}(n,\mathfrak{U})\leq N_{f^{N}(Z)}(n-N,\mathfrak{U})+N_{H(Z,N)}(n,\mathfrak{U})\leq N(n-N,\mathfrak{U})+N_{H(Z,N)}(n,\mathfrak{U})~,

Apply this argument first to
Z=X
and
N=1
so that

N(n,\mathfrak{U})\leq N(n-1,\mathfrak{U})+N_{H_{1}}(n,\mathfrak{U}),n\geq 1

for some proper closed subset
H_{1}
of
X
.
Denote by
M_{1}
the number of irreducible components of
H_{1}
, and pick
N_{1}\geq 1
large enough such that

M_{1}(1+\epsilon)^{(1-N_{1})}\leq\frac{\epsilon}{10}
.
Now apply (
12
) to
N=N_{1}
and to each irreducible component of
H_{1}
. We obtain

N_{H_{1}}(n,\mathfrak{U})\leq M_{1}N(n-N_{1},\mathfrak{U})+M_{1}N_{H_{2}}(n,\mathfrak{U}),n\geq N_{1}

where
H_{2}
is a proper closed subset of
H_{1}
.
We may thus proceed by induction, obtaining a sequence of integers
M_{i}
and closed subsets
H_{i+1}\subsetneq H_{i}

such that

N(n,\mathfrak{U})\leq N(n-1,\mathfrak{U})+M_{1}\,N(n-N_{1},\mathfrak{U})+\cdots+M_{i}\,N(n-N_{i},\mathfrak{U})+M_{i}\,N_{H_{i+1}}(n,\mathfrak{U}),n\geq N_{i}.

Since
X
is Noetherian, we have
H_{i}=\emptyset
for
i
large enough, which completes the proof.
∎

3.2.
Priestley spaces

We refer to
[
D19
]
for generalities on this notion.

Recall that in a poset
(X,\leq)
a down-set (resp. an up-set)
U
is a set such that
x\in U
and
y\leq x
(resp.
x\leq y
) implies
y\in U
.
Note that
U
is a down-set if and only if
X\setminus U
is an up-set.
A topological poset
(X,\tau,\leq)
is called a Priestley space if it is quasi-compact and for any
y\not\leq x

there exists a clopen down-set
U
such that
x\in U
and
y\notin U
. Any Priestley space is Hausdorff, zero-dimensional, and in fact
its set of clopen down-sets and up-sets forms a basis for its topology.

Pick any Priestley space
(X,\tau,\leq)
. The set
\tau^{u}
of all open up-sets of
X
defines a topology, and
(X,\tau^{u})
is
a spectral space
7
7

7

In other words,
X
is quasi-compact, satisfies the
T_{0}
-separation axiom, the set of its quasi-compact open subsets
is closed under finite intersections and forms a basis for its topology, and any non-empty irreducible closed subset of
X
has a generic point.
.
By a theorem of Hochster
[
Hoc69
]
, a topological space is spectral iff it is homeomorphic to the spectrum of a commutative ring (hence the name).

Conversely, let
(X,\tau)
be any spectral space. Define the order relation
x\leq y
iff
x
belongs to the closure of
\{y\}
.
The constructible (or patch) topology
\tau^{\#}
on
X
is determined by a basis of open sets given by quasi-compact open subsets of
X
and their complements.
Then
(X,\tau^{\#},\leq)
is a Priestley space.

A continuous map
f\colon X\to Y
between spectral spaces is called spectral if the preimage of any quasi-compact open subset remains quasi-compact open.
The induced map
f^{\#}\colon X^{\#}\to Y^{\#}
on the corresponding Priestley spaces is continuous and order-preserving. Any continuous and order-preserving map
f\colon X\to Y
between Priestley spaces is called a Priestley map.

The above correspondence induces an equivalence of categories between Priestley spaces and spectral spaces
[
D19
, 1.5.15]
. Unlike Noetherian spaces, there are Priestley selfmaps with positive entropy, see e.g. Remark
3.5
. In the next subsection, we present an important subclass of Priestley spaces (to be used later in the paper) for which selfmaps always have zero entropy.

3.3.
Noetherian Priestley spaces and entropy

Let
\tau^{u}
be the collection of all open up-sets.

Definition 3.1
.

We say that a Priestley space
(X,\tau,\leq)
is Noetherian when its associated spectral space
(X,\tau^{u})
is a Noetherian topological space.

Remark 3.4
.

Equivalently
(X,\tau,\leq)
is Noetherian if any decreasing sequence of closed down-sets is eventually stationnary. If
(X,\tau)
is a Noetherian topological space, then

(X,\tau,\leq)
is a Noetherian Priestley space. However a Noetherian Priestley space is not necessarily a Noetherian topological space (see §
3.4.3
below for examples). To complicate things further, there exist Noetherian spectral topological spaces that are not homeomorphic to the spectrum of a Noetherian ring, see
[
D19
, 12.4.10]
.

The following theorem generalizes
[
Xie21
, Theorem 1.12]
; see
[
Gi14
, Theorem 1.2]
for another related result.

Theorem 3.3
.

Any Radon measure
\mu
of finite mass on a Noetherian Priestley space
(X,\tau,\leq)
is atomic: there exists a countable set
F
such that

\mu=\sum_{x\in F}\mu(x)\delta_{x}
where
\sum_{F}\mu(x)<\infty
.

Proof of Theorem
3.3
.

Any Radon measure can be decomposed as a sum of an atomic measure and a measure without atom.
It is thus sufficient to prove that for any probability Radon measure
\mu
on
X
, there exists
x\in X
such that
\mu(x)>0
.

We proceed by contradiction and assume
\mu(x)=0
for all
x\in X
.
We claim that for every clopen down-set
K
of
X
with
\mu(K)>0
, there is a clopen down-set
K^{\prime}\subsetneq K
with
\mu(K^{\prime})>0.

Note that
X
is a clopen down-set, applying this claim, we get a sequence of clopen down-sets
K_{n},n\geq 0
satisfying
K_{0}=X
, for every
n\geq 0,

\mu(K_{n})>0
and

K_{n+1}\subsetneq K_{n}.
This contradicts the Noetherianity assumption.

Now we prove the claim. First assume that there is a maximal element
o
of
K.
Then
U:=K\setminus\{o\}
is open and
\mu(U)>0.

Since
\mu
is a Radon measure, there is a compact subset
E\subseteq U
such that
\mu(E)>0.

For every
x\in E
, since
x\not\geq o
, there is a clopen
K_{x}
of
X
such that
x\in K_{x}
but
o\not\in K_{x}
. After replacing
K_{x}
by
K_{x}\cap K
, we may assume that
K_{x}\subseteq K.
Then
K_{x}\subsetneq K.

Since
E
is compact, there is a finite subset
F\subseteq E
such that
E\subseteq\cup_{x\in F}K_{x}.

So there is
x\in F
, such that
\mu(K_{x})>0.

Now we may assume that for every
x\in K
, there is
y_{x}\in K
such that
y_{x}>x.

Then there is a clopen
K_{x}
of
X
such that
x\in K_{x}
but
y_{x}\not\in K_{x}
. After replacing
K_{x}
by
K_{x}\cap K
, we may assume that
K_{x}\subseteq K.
Then
K_{x}\subsetneq K.
Since
K
is compact, there is a finite subset
F\subseteq K
such that
K=\cup_{x\in F}K_{x}.

So there is
x\in K
, such that
\mu(K_{x})>0.

∎

This result yields the following estimates on entropy.

Theorem 3.4
.

Let
f\colon X\setminus I(f)\to X
be any partially continuous self-map of a Noetherian Priestley space such that
I(f)
is clopen. Then we have
\htop(f)=0
.

In particular, the topological entropy of any continuous self-map of a Noetherian Priestley space is
0
.

Let us insist on the fact that we did not assume
f
to be a Priestley map in the previous theorem.

Proof.

We first prove the statement when
f
is continuous. Since
X
is compact, the variational principle applies (Theorem
1.2
). As any ergodic measure
\mu
is supported on a periodic cycle by Theorem
3.3
, we get
\htop(f)=0
as required.

Suppose now that
f
is a partially continuous self-map. To reduce to the previous case we proceed as follows.
Consider the poset
X_{+}
obtained by adding a singleton
\{o\}
to
X
so that the inclusion
X\subset X_{+}
preserves the order and
o<x
for all
x\in X
.
The topology on
X_{+}
is the coarsest one such that the inclusion
X\subset X_{+}
is continuous and
\{o\}
is clopen.
Then
X_{+}
is still a Noetherian Priestley space, and
o
is a minimal element.

Consider the map
f_{+}\colon X_{+}\to X_{+}
defined by
f_{+}(x)=f(x)
if
x\in X\setminus I(f)
and
f_{+}(x)=o
if
x\in I(f)\cup\{o\}
.
Since both
\{o\}
and
I(f)
are clopen, the map
f_{+}
is continuous so that
\htop(f_{+})=0
by what precedes.

Pick any entourage
\mathcal{E}
of
X
. Then
\mathcal{E}_{+}=\mathcal{E}\cup(\{o\}\times\{o\})
is an entourage of
X_{+}
and
a simple observation shows
R(n,\xi^{\max}(f),\mathcal{E})\leq R(n,\xi^{\max}(f_{+}),\mathcal{E}_{+})
.
It follows that
\htop(f)\leq\htop(f_{+})=0
.
∎

3.4.
Examples of Priestley spaces

3.4.1.
Totally disconnected compact sets

Every totally disconnected compact space (i.e. any Stone space) endowed with the trivial order relation (
x\leq y
iff
x=y
) is a Priestley space.
Any totally disconnected compact subset
C
of
[0,1]
endowed with the usual total order
\leq
of the real line is also a Priestley space.
One can build more general Priestley spaces by patching together totally disconnected compact subset of the unit segment
following a given tree pattern.

Remark 3.5
.

There exists continuous maps on a Cantor set with positive entropy (e.g. the shift map on
\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{Z}}
).
Hence there exist Priestley maps of positive entropy.

3.4.2.
Partially ordered spaces

Let
(X,\leq)
be a partially ordered set.
For every
x\in X
, define
K_{x}:=\{y\in X,y\leq x\}.

Let
\tau_{\leq}
be the topology generated by the sets
K_{x},X\setminus K_{x}
for all
x\in X.

For every down-set
K\subseteq X
, we have
K=\cup_{x\in K}K_{x}
.
So every down-set of
X
is open with respect to
\tau_{\leq}.

Remark 3.6
.

For
x,y\in X
, if
y\not\leq x
, then
x\in K_{x}
and
y\not\in K_{x}
. So
(X,\tau_{\leq},\leq)
is a Priestley space if and only if
(X,\tau_{\leq})
is compact.

We say that
(X,\leq)
satisfies the descending chain condition if for every decreasing sequence

x_{1}\geq\ldots\geq x_{n}\geq x_{n+1}
in
X
,
there is
N\geq 1
such that
x_{n}=x_{N}
for every
n\geq N.

The next proposition is not essential to our discussion. We include it however for the convenience of the reader, as it clarifies the
problem on characterizing posets for which
(X,\tau_{\leq})
is a Priestley space.

Proposition 3.5
.

The following two conditions are equivalent:

(i)

(X,\tau_{\leq})
is compact;

(ii)

–

(X,\leq)
satisfies the descending chain condition;

–

there is a finite subset
F\subseteq X
such that
X=\cup_{x\in F}K_{x}
;

–

for any
x,y\in X
there exists a finite set
F(x,y)\subset X
such that
K_{x}\cap K_{y}=\cup_{z\in F(x,y)}K_{z}
.

Proof.

Assume (i). Let
x_{n}\geq x_{n+1}
be any decreasing sequence of points in
X
. Then
K_{\infty}:=\cap K_{x_{n}}
is a non-empty compact set. It is also a down-set hence it is open.
The family
X\setminus K_{x_{i}}
forms an open cover of the compact set
X\setminus K_{\infty}
. We conclude to the existence of an integer
N\geq 1
such that
X\setminus K_{\infty}=\cup_{i=1}^{N}(X\setminus K_{x_{i}}),

which implies
x_{i}=x_{N}
for all
i\geq N
. This proves
X
satisfies the descending chain condition.
Since
\{K_{x}\}_{x\in X}
forms an open cover of
X
and
X
is compact, we have
X=\cup_{x\in F}K_{x}
for some finite set
F
.
Finally
\{K_{z}\}_{z\in K_{x}\cap K_{y}}
is an open cover of
K_{x}\cap K_{y}
, and again
K_{x}\cap K_{y}=\cup_{z\in F(x,y)}K_{z}

for some finite set
F(x,y)
. This completes the proof
(i)\Rightarrow(ii)
.

Assume that (ii) holds. We only need to show that for every
x\in F
,
K_{x}
is compact.
Let
\{U_{i}\}_{i\in I}
be any open cover of
K_{x}
. We proceed by contradiction and assume that it does not admit any finite subfamily covering
K_{x}
.

For a finite subset
S
of
X
, set
K_{S}:=\cap_{s\in S}K_{s}
and
K^{S}:=\cup_{s\in S}K_{s}
.
A basis of the topology of
(X,\tau_{\leq})
is given by clopen of the form

K_{S}\setminus K^{T}
, where
S,T
are finite subsets of
X
.
We may thus assume that for each index
i
there exist finite sets
S_{i}
and
T_{i}
such that
U_{i}=K_{S_{i}}\setminus K^{T_{i}}
.

Pick any index
i_{0}\in I
, such that
x\in U_{i_{0}}=K_{S_{i_{0}}}\setminus K^{T_{i_{0}}}
. Observe that we may take
S_{i_{0}}=\{x\}
and that
x\not\in T_{i_{0}}.

Choose any point
x_{1}\in T_{i_{0}}
such that
K_{x_{1}}
is not covered by any finite subset of
\{U_{i}\cap K_{x_{1}},i\in I\}
.
By our standing assumption,
K_{x_{1}}\cap K_{x}
is a finite union of sets of the form
K_{y}
so that
x_{1}
by one of the element
y

we may suppose that
x_{1}<x
.

Proceeding by induction, we get an infinite sequence of points
x>x_{1}>\ldots>x_{n}
such that for
n\geq 0
,
K_{x_{n}}
is not covered by any finite subset of
\{U_{i}\cap K_{x_{n}},i\in I\}.
This contradicts the descending chain condition.
∎

Proposition 3.6
.

Assume that

(X,\tau_{\leq})
is compact.
Then
(X,\tau_{\leq},\leq)
is a Noetherian Priestley space.

Proof.

Because
(X,\tau_{\leq})
is compact, Remark
3.6
implies
(X,\tau_{\leq},\leq)
to be a Priestley space.
Let

K_{1}\supseteq K_{2}\supseteq\dots

be any decreasing sequence of non-empty closed down-sets in
X
. Then
K_{\infty}:=\cap_{i=1}^{\infty}K_{i}
is a closed down-set, hence is clopen.
Note that
X\setminus K_{\infty}=\cup_{i=1}^{\infty}(X\setminus K_{i})
.
Since
X\setminus K_{\infty}
is compact and
X\setminus K_{i}
are open, there is
N\geq 1
such that
X\setminus K_{\infty}=\cup_{i=1}^{N}(X\setminus K_{i}),

and the proof is complete.
∎

3.4.3.
Spectra of commutative rings with unity

Let
R
be any commutative ring with unity. Then
\spec R
endowed with the Zariski topology is a spectral space.
Recall the definition of the constructible topology from Section
2.3
:
it is the topology
\tau^{\con}
induced by the product topology when
\spec R
is viewed as a subset of
\{0,1\}^{R}

by attaching to any prime ideal
\mathfrak{A}
its characteristic function
\chi_{\mathfrak{A}}\colon R\to\{0,1\}
.
The space
(\spec R,\tau^{\con})
is compact and totally disconnected.
The triple
(\spec R,\tau^{\con},\leq)
is in fact a Priestley space where
\leq
is as follows: for
\mathfrak{A},\mathfrak{B}\in\spec R
,
\mathfrak{A}\leq\mathfrak{B}
if and only if
\mathfrak{B}\subseteq\mathfrak{A}.

Assume that
R
is Noetherian, then
\tau^{\con}
equals to
\tau_{\leq}
(as in Section
3.4.2
). By Proposition
3.6
,
the triple
(\spec R,\tau^{\con},\leq)
forms a Noetherian Priestley space. But in general (e.g., when
R=k[T]
with
k
an infinite field),
(\spec R,\tau^{\con})
is not a Noetherian topological space.

3.4.4.
The ideal space of a commutative ring with unity

The next construction has been considered in a couple of papers including
[

,

]

[

.

Let
R

Note that

The space

To prove quasi-compactness, we take a collection of closed sets

of the form

The ideal generated by

hence there exists a finite subset

Remark 3.8
.

The restriction of the Zariski topology of

When

Suppose

(13)

\{\mathfrak{A}\in\Id(R),\,f_{1},\dots,f_{m}\in\mathfrak{A},g_{1},\dots,g_{n}\not\in\mathfrak{A}\}~.

B
is given in §
5.5
. It is based on an argument of absolute Noetherian approximation, that we develop with some details.

5.1.
\varepsilon
-reduction of strictly affinoid domains

Let
A
be any strictly affinoid
k
-algebra. Recall the definition of the spectral norm
\rho(a):=\lim_{n}\|a^{n}\|^{1/n}
, and
that we set
A^{\circ}:=\{\rho\leq 1\}
.

Lemma 5.1
.

For any strictly affinoid
k
-algebra
A
and for any
1\geq\varepsilon>0
,
then

A_{\varepsilon}:=A^{\circ}/\{\rho<\varepsilon\}

is a finitely generated
k_{\varepsilon}
-algebra with unit.

Proof.

When
A=k\{T\}
is the Tate algebra, then
A_{\varepsilon}=k^{\circ}\{T\}/\mathfrak{m}_{\varepsilon}\{T\}=k_{\varepsilon}[T]
so that the result is clear.
When
A
is reduced, since
k
is algebraically closed, then it follows from
[
BGR84
, §6.4.3]
that there exists an epimorphism

\alpha\colon k\{T\}\to A
such that
\rho
is equal to the residual norm.
We get a surjective morphism
k_{\varepsilon}[T]\to A_{\varepsilon}
and
A_{\varepsilon}
is finitely generated over
k_{\varepsilon}
.
Finally, when
A
is not reduced, we conclude by observing that
A_{\varepsilon}=B_{\varepsilon}
where

B
is the quotient of
A
by its nilradical.
∎

Remark 5.1
.

The previous proof works for any stable fields in the sense of
[
BGR84
, §3.6]
.
When
\varepsilon=1
, then it is known that
\tilde{A}:=A_{1}
is finitely generated over
\tilde{k}

without any assumption on
k
. It remains open whether
A_{\varepsilon}
is finitely generated over
k_{\varepsilon}

when
k
is not necessarily stable.

Pick any multiplicative
semi-norm
x\in\mathcal{M}(A)
, and set
\mathfrak{A}_{\varepsilon}(x):=\{a\in A^{\circ},\,|a(x)|<\varepsilon\}
so that
\mathfrak{A}_{\varepsilon}(x)\cap k^{\circ}=\mathfrak{m}_{\varepsilon}
.
Since
\mathfrak{A}_{\varepsilon}(x)\supset\{\rho<\varepsilon\}
, the ideal
\mathfrak{A}_{\varepsilon}(x)
projects onto an ideal
\tilde{\mathfrak{A}}_{\varepsilon}(x)
of
A_{\varepsilon}

which satisfies
\tilde{\mathfrak{A}}_{\varepsilon}(x)\cap k_{\varepsilon}=(0)
.
We get a canonical reduction map
\red_{\varepsilon}\colon\mathcal{M}(A)\to\Id(A_{\varepsilon})
sending
x

to
\tilde{\mathfrak{A}}_{\varepsilon}(x)
.

Recall from §
3.4.4
that the Zariski topology on
\Id(A_{\varepsilon})
is generated by open sets of the form:

U:=\{\mathfrak{A}\in\Id(A_{\varepsilon}),\,a\not\in\mathfrak{A}\}~,

so that
\red_{\varepsilon}^{-1}(U)=\{x\in\mathcal{M}(A),\,|a(x)|\geq\varepsilon\}
is closed.
It follows that
\red_{\varepsilon}
is anti-continuous from the affinoid space
\mathcal{M}(A)
to
(\Id(A_{\varepsilon}),\tau^{\zar})
.

Remark 5.2
.

When
\varepsilon=1
, the image of the reduction map
\red_{1}(A)
is precisely the spectrum of
\tilde{A}
, see
[
BGR84
, 7.1.5/4]
,
[
Ber90
, §2.4]
.
The description of
\red_{\varepsilon}(A)
is unclear when
\varepsilon<1
. In general
\red_{\varepsilon}(A)
is not closed in
\Id(A_{\varepsilon})

endowed either with the Zariski or the constructible topology.

Remark 5.3
.

It is instructive to compute the
\varepsilon
-reduction of
\bar{\mathbb{D}}^{1}(0,1)=\mathcal{M}(k\{T\})
. Given any
a\in k^{\circ}
and any
r\in[0,1]
,
denote by
\zeta(a,r)
the point in
\bar{\mathbb{D}}^{1}(0,1)
defined by
|P(\zeta(a,r))|=\sup_{\bar{B}(a,r)}|P|
.

Direct computations show that
\red_{\varepsilon}(\zeta(a,r))
is the prime ideal in
k_{\varepsilon}[T]
generated by
(T-a)
if
r<\varepsilon
; whereas
for any
r\geq\varepsilon
,
\red_{\varepsilon}(\zeta(a,r))
is not a prime ideal. Indeed, one has

\red_{\varepsilon}(\zeta(a,r))=\{\sum_{i}a_{i}(T-a)^{i},\,a_{0}=0,|a_{1}|r<\varepsilon\}
.
Also, we have
\red_{\varepsilon}(\zeta(a,r))=\red_{\varepsilon}(\zeta(\alpha,\rho))
iff either
\zeta(a,r)=\zeta(\alpha,\rho)
, or

and
|a-\alpha|<\varepsilon
.

Observe that these computations imply that
\red_{\varepsilon}(\mathcal{M}(k\{T\}))
is
not
a closed subset of
\Id(k_{\varepsilon}[T])
(at least when
k
is spherically complete).

The
\varepsilon
-reduction map is functorial. For any bounded homomorphism of
k
-algebras
f^{*}\colon A\to B
inducing an analytic map

,
we have
\rho(f(a))\leq\rho(a)
hence we get a canonical map
f_{\varepsilon}\colon\Id(B_{\varepsilon})\to\Id(A_{\varepsilon})
making the following diagram commutative:

(15)

\textstyle{\mathcal{M}(B)\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{f}
\scriptstyle{\red_{\varepsilon}}
\textstyle{\mathcal{M}(A)\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\red_{\varepsilon}}
\textstyle{\Id(B_{\varepsilon})\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{f_{\varepsilon}}
\textstyle{\Id(A_{\varepsilon})}

The map

is continuous for both the Zariski and constructible topologies.

Let us finish this section by the following generalization of Lemma
4.5
.

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
\mathfrak{U}
be a finite open cover of
\mathcal{M}(A)
. Then for every sufficiently small
\varepsilon\in|k^{*}|
, there is
a finite constructible cover
\mathfrak{V}
of
(\Id(A_{\varepsilon}),\tau^{\con})
such that

\{\red_{\varepsilon}^{-1}(U),\,U\in\mathfrak{V}\}
refines
\mathfrak{U}
.

Proof.

As in the proof of Lemma
5.1
, we may suppose that
A
is reduced and choose an epimorphism
\alpha^{*}\colon k\{T\}\to A

such that the spectral norm on

is equal to the residual norm. This induces a closed embedding
\alpha\colon\mathcal{M}(A)\to\mathcal{M}(k\{T\})
,
and an epimorphism
k_{\varepsilon}[T]\to A_{\varepsilon}\to 0
of
k_{\varepsilon}
-algebras.
For each
U\in\mathfrak{U}
choose an open set

of
\mathcal{M}(k\{T\})
such that

\mathfrak{U}^{\prime}:=\{U^{\prime}\}\cup\{\mathcal{M}(k\{T\})\setminus\mathcal{M}(A)\}

of
\mathcal{M}(k\{T\})
.

) that

∎

5.2.
Primary ideals

There is a technical difficulty in globalizing the
\varepsilon
-reduction using the sets
\Id(A_{\varepsilon})
as above.
We refer to Remark
5.8
below for a discussion on this problem.
In order to properly globalize the
\varepsilon
-reduction and to develop smoothly our descent argument,
we will work instead with the set of primary ideals in
A_{\varepsilon}
.
In this section, we discuss briefly some general properties of this set.

Recall that an ideal
I
is primary if
fg\in I
and
f\notin I
implies
g^{n}\in I
for some
n\geq 1
.
Any prime ideal is primary, and the radical of a primary ideal is prime.

Definition 5.1
.

Let
R
be any commutative ring with unit. Then we let
{\rm P}(R)
be the set of all primary ideals
in
\Id(R)
and
\prim(R)
be its closure (for the constructible topology)
in
\Id(R)
.

The set of primary ideals is a countable intersection of clopen sets. As the examples below show, it is neither closed, nor open, nor dense in general.

Example 5.1
.

Let
k
be an algebraically closed field, and set
R=k[x,y]
.

Then
I=(xy)
is not a primary ideal, but
the sequence of primary ideals
(x^{n},y^{n},xy)
converges to
I

in
\Id(R)
. This shows that the set of primary ideals is not closed in general.

Take
I^{\prime}=(x)
. Then a basis for open neighbourhoods of
I^{\prime}
are those of the form
U=\{J\in\Id(R),f_{i}\in J,g_{j}\notin J\}
(where
f_{i}
divides
x
). Each such
U

contains two maximal ideals
J_{1}
and
J_{2}
for which the product
J_{1}\cdot J_{2}
is not primary
and belongs to
U
. This proves the set of primary ideals is not open in general.

Consider now
I^{\prime\prime}=(xy(x+y-1))
. Then the set
\{J\in\Id(R),xy\notin J,y(x+y-1)\notin J,x(x+y-1)\notin J,xy(x+y-1)\in J\}

is an open neighborhood of
I^{\prime\prime}
but contains no primary ideals. Indeed if
J
would be a primary ideal lying in this set then we could find
an integer
n>0
such that
x^{n},y^{n}
and
(x+y-1)^{n}
belong to
J
, a contradiction.
This shows that the set of primary ideals is not dense in general.

Let
f
be any non-nilpotent element in
R
.
Recall that
R[f^{-1}]=R[T]/(1-Tf)
is an overring of
R
so that we have two natural maps

\res\colon\Id(R[f^{-1}])\to\Id(R)
and
\ext\colon\Id(R)\to\Id^{+}(R[f^{-1}])

defined by
\res(I)=I\cap R
and
\ext(I)=I\cdot R[f^{-1}]
respectively.
Beware that if
f^{n}\in I
for some
I\in\Id(R)
then
\ext(I)=R
.

Lemma 5.3
.

The map
\res\colon\Id(R[f^{-1}])\to\Id(R)

is injective and its image consists of those
ideals
I\in\Id(R)

such that if
gf^{n}\in I
for some
g\in R
and some
n\geq 0
, then
g\in I
.

Proof.

We first prove the second statement.
Suppose
I=J\cap R
for some ideal
J
of
R[f^{-1}]
.
If
gf^{n}\in I
for some
g\in R
and some
n\geq 0
, then
g\in J

since
f
is invertible in
R[f^{-1}]
. It follows that
g\in I
.

Conversely suppose
I
is an ideal in
R
such that

gf^{n}\in I
for some
g\in R
and some
n\geq 0
implies
g\in R
.
We shall prove that
\ext(I)\cap R=I
.
It is clear that
\ext(I)\cap R\supset I
. So pick
h\in\ext(I)\cap R
and write
h=g/f^{n}
with
g\in I
.
Then
hf^{n}\in I
hence
h\in I
.

The former arguments imply
\ext\circ\res(J)=J
for any
J\in R[f^{-1}]
. This implies
\res
to be injective.
∎

Observe that
\res(I)
is a prime (resp. primary) ideal whenever
I
is prime (resp. primary).

Lemma 5.4
.

A primary ideal
I\subset R
belongs to

\res(\Id(R[f^{-1}]))
if and only if

f^{n}\notin I

for all
n\geq 0
.

Proof.

Suppose
I=J\cap R
for some
J\in\Id(R[f^{-1}])
, and
f^{n}
belongs to
I
for some
n>0
.
Then the previous lemma implies
1\in I
, a contradiction.

Conversely, suppose
I
is primary
and
f^{n}\notin I

for all
n\geq 0
. Suppose that

gf^{n}\in I
for some
g\in R
and some
n\geq 0
.
If
g\notin I
, then
f^{nm}\in I
for some
m

since
I
is primary, a contradiction. Hence
g\in I
, and
the previous lemma implies

I=J\cap R
for some
J\in\Id(R[f^{-1}])
.
∎

Proposition 5.5
.

For any commutative ring
R
with unit, and any non-nilpotent element
f\in R
,

\res\colon\prim(R[f^{-1}])\to\prim(R)
induces a homeomorphism onto its image
which is compact in
\prim(R)
.

Moreover, for any finite family of non-nilpotent elements

f_{1},\cdots,f_{m}\in R
generating the unit ideal,
the set
\{\res(\prim(R[f_{i}^{-1}])\}
forms a finite compact cover of
\prim(R)
.

Proof.

By Lemma
5.3
, the map
\res
is injective. Since it is also continuous (for the constructible topology),
the restriction of
\res
to any compact subset of
\Id(R[f^{-1}])
induces a homeomorphism onto its image.
By construction,
\prim(R[f^{-1}])
is compact in
\Id(R[f^{-1}])
, and the first statement follows.

We now prove the second statement. Suppose

f_{1},\cdots,f_{m}
generate the unit ideal, and pick any primary ideal
I
.
Suppose that
I
does not belong to the union of
\{\res(\prim(R[f_{i}^{-1}])\}
. Then, by the previous lemma, for each index
i
, we have
f_{i}^{n_{i}}\in I
for some integer
n_{i}
.
Set
n=\max n_{i}
. Then
f^{n}_{1},\cdots,f^{n}_{m}\in I
, but it is also easy to show that they generate the unit ideal.
This is a contradiction.
∎

Remark 5.4
.

The previous result is not true when
\prim(R)
is replaced by
\Id(R)
. Indeed, in general

\{\Id(R[f_{i}^{-1}])\}
does not form a cover of
\Id(R)
if
(f_{1},\cdots,f_{n})
generates the unit ideal
(take
R={\mathbb{Z}}
,
f_{1}=2
and
f_{2}=3
for instance).

Remark 5.5
.

To simplify notation, in the sequel we shall always identify
\prim(R[f^{-1}])
with its image inside
\prim(R)
.

We conclude this section with the following observations.

Lemma 5.6
.

For any strictly affinoid algebra
A
and any
\varepsilon>0
,
we have
\red_{\varepsilon}(\mathcal{M}(A))\subset{\rm P}(A_{\varepsilon})
.

Proof.

Pick any
x\in\mathcal{M}(A)
. Then
\red_{\varepsilon}(x)=\{f\in A_{\varepsilon},|f(x)|<\varepsilon\}
is a primary ideal.
Indeed suppose
fg\in\red_{\varepsilon}(x)
and
f\notin\red_{\varepsilon}(x)
. Then

|fg(x)|<\varepsilon
and
|f(x)|\geq\varepsilon
, hence
|g(x)|<1
. Hence we can find
n>0
such that

|g^{n}(x)|<\varepsilon
, and
g^{n}\in\red_{\varepsilon}(x)
.
∎

To simplify notation, write

\bar{R}_{\varepsilon}(A)
for the closure (in
\prim(A_{\varepsilon})
) of
the set
\red_{\varepsilon}(\mathcal{M}(A))
.

Proposition 5.7
.

Let
A
be any strictly affinoid algebra
A
.
For any
\varepsilon\in|k^{*}|
, and any
f\in A^{\circ}
,
we have

\bar{R}_{\varepsilon}(A\langle f^{-1}\rangle)=\bar{R}_{\varepsilon}(A)\cap\prim(A_{\varepsilon}[f^{-1}])
, and this set is a clopen subset of

\bar{R}_{\varepsilon}(A)
.

Proof.

Write
\varepsilon=|a|
for some
a\in k^{*}
.
Observe first that

\red_{\varepsilon}(\mathcal{M}(A\langle f^{-1}\rangle))=\red_{\varepsilon}(\mathcal{M}(A))\cap\prim(A_{\varepsilon}[f^{-1}])=\red_{\varepsilon}(\mathcal{M}(A))\cap\Id(A_{\varepsilon}[f^{-1}])~.

We now prove

\red_{\varepsilon}(\mathcal{M}(A))\cap\Id(A_{\varepsilon}[f^{-1}])=\{I\in\red_{\varepsilon}(\mathcal{M}(A)),af\notin I\}~.

Suppose
I=\red_{\varepsilon}(x)
for some
x\in\mathcal{M}(A)
.
Observe that

f^{n}\notin I
for all
n
, if and only if
|f(x)|=1
, and
the latter is equivalent to
|(af)(x)|=\varepsilon
.
By Lemma
5.4
, this proves

I\in\Id(\mathcal{M}(A\langle f^{-1}\rangle))
if and only if

af\notin I
as required.

Since the set of ideals
I\in\Id(\mathcal{M}(A)))
such that
af\notin I

is clopen, the closure of
\{I\in\red_{\varepsilon}(\mathcal{M}(A)),af\notin I\}
equals
\{I\in\bar{R}_{\varepsilon}(A),af\notin I\}

which implies

\bar{R}_{\varepsilon}(A\langle f^{-1}\rangle)=\{I\in\bar{R}_{\varepsilon}(A),af\notin I\}\subset\bar{R}_{\varepsilon}(A)\cap\prim(A_{\varepsilon}[f^{-1}]).

Pick
I\in\bar{R}_{\varepsilon}(A)\cap\prim(A_{\varepsilon}[f^{-1}])
. Observe that
a\notin I
. (Indeed,
for any
x\in\mathcal{M}(A)
we have
\red_{\varepsilon}(x)\cap k_{\varepsilon}=(0)
. Hence,
for any ideal
J\in\bar{R}_{\varepsilon}(A)

we have
J\cap k_{\varepsilon}=(0)
, so that
a\notin J
since
a\in k_{\varepsilon}
.)

Now the set
\{J\in\Id(A),a\notin J\}
is both open and closed hence
the closure of the set of primary ideals of
A_{\varepsilon}[f^{-1}]
not containing
a

is equal to
\prim(A_{\varepsilon}[f^{-1}])\cap\{J\in\Id(A),a\notin J\}
.
It follows that

\displaystyle\prim(A_{\varepsilon}[f^{-1}])\cap\{J\in\Id(A),a\notin J\}

\displaystyle=\overline{\{J\in{\rm P}(A_{\varepsilon}),f^{n}\notin J~\text{for all}~n,a\notin J\}}

\displaystyle\subseteq\overline{\{J\in{\rm P}(A_{\varepsilon}),af\notin J\}}

and we conclude that
af\notin I
. This proves

\bar{R}_{\varepsilon}(A)\cap\prim(A_{\varepsilon}[f^{-1}])=\bar{R}_{\varepsilon}(A\langle f^{-1}\rangle)

and concludes the proof.
∎

5.3.
Formal affinoid subdomains

Let
A
be any strictly affinoid
k
-algebra. An affinoid subdomain
V
of
\mathcal{M}(A)
is a compact subset

V\subset\mathcal{M}(A)
such that there exists a bounded morphism of affinoid
k
-algebras
\alpha\colon A\to A_{V}
satisfying
the following universal property. Given any bounded morphism of affinoid
k
-algebras
\beta\colon A\to B

such that
\beta^{*}(\mathcal{M}(B))\subset V
, then we may write
\beta=\theta\circ\alpha
for some unique bounded morphism

\theta\colon A_{V}\to B
.

For any elements
a,b\in A
generating the unit ideal, and for any positive
r,s\in|k^{*}|
, the
Weierstrass domain

\{x\in\mathcal{M}(A),\,|a(x)|\leq r\}
,
the Laurent domain

\{x\in\mathcal{M}(A),\,|a(x)|\leq r,|b(x)|\geq s\}
,
and
the rational domain

\{x\in\mathcal{M}(A),\,|a(x)|\leq r|b(x)|\}
,
are all affinoid subdomains of
\mathcal{M}(A)
. In each case, it is possible to describe the affinoid algebra of the affinoid subdomain
explicitely in terms of
A
and the data
a,b,r,s
. For instance, we have

\{x\in\mathcal{M}(A),\,|b(x)|\geq 1\}=\mathcal{M}\left(A\langle b^{-1}\rangle\right)

where
A\langle b^{-1}\rangle=A\{T\}/(Tb-1)
is endowed with the residual norm, and the ring

A\{T\}:=\{\sum_{n}a_{n}T^{n},\,a_{n}\in A,|a_{n}|\to 0\}
with the Gauss norm.

Following
[
Ber90
, §4.3]
, we say that an affinoid subdomain
V
of
\mathcal{M}(A)
is formal
when the morphism induced at the level of their (standard) reductions
\spec\tilde{A}_{V}\to\spec\tilde{A}

is an open immersion.
It follows from
[
BGR84
, 7.2.6]
that for any
f\in A^{\circ}
such that
\|f\|_{\sup}=1
, then
the Laurent domain
V:=\{|f|\geq 1\}=\{|f|=1\}
is a formal affinoid subdomain
so that
\tilde{A}_{V}=\tilde{A}[\tilde{f}^{-1}]
.
Conversely, by
[
Bo77
, Satz 1.2]
, any formal affinoid subdomain of
\mathcal{M}(A)
is a finite union of domains
of the form
\{|f_{i}|\geq 1\}
with
f_{i}\in A^{\circ}
.

Lemma 5.8
.

For any formal affinoid subdomain
V\subset\mathcal{M}(A)
and for any
\varepsilon>0
, the canonical
map
\Id((A_{V})_{\varepsilon})\to\Id(A_{\varepsilon})
induces a homeomorphism from
(\prim((A_{V})_{\varepsilon}),\tau^{\con})

onto a closed subset of
(\prim(A_{\varepsilon}),\tau^{\con})
.

Proof.

Let us first consider the case
V=\{|f|\geq 1\}
with
f\in A^{\circ}
.
By
[
BGR84
, 7.2.6/2]
the morphism
\sigma\colon A\to A_{V}
satisfies

|\sigma(a)|_{\sup}=\lim_{n\to\infty}|f^{n}a|_{\sup}\leq|a|_{\sup}
so that
we have a canonical morphism
\sigma_{\varepsilon}\colon A_{\varepsilon}\to(A_{V})_{\varepsilon}
.
Since
f
is a unit in
A_{V}^{\circ}
, we get the following commutative diagram

\textstyle{A_{\varepsilon}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\sigma_{\varepsilon}}
\scriptstyle{\phi}
\textstyle{(A_{V})_{\varepsilon}}
\textstyle{A_{\varepsilon}[f_{\varepsilon}^{-1}]\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\imath}

where
f_{\varepsilon}
denotes the class of
f
in
A_{\varepsilon}
.
Note that the lemma follows from Proposition
5.5

as soon as we can prove that
\imath
is an isomorphism.

To see that
\imath
is injective, suppose that

\imath(af_{\varepsilon}^{-n})=0
with
a\in A_{\varepsilon}
and
n\geq 0
. Then
\imath(a)=0
so that
\sigma_{\varepsilon}(a)=0
. Choose

b\in A^{\circ}
representing
a
. Since
|\sigma(b)|_{\sup}<\varepsilon
, we have
|f^{m}b|_{\sup}<\varepsilon
for some integer
m
, hence

f_{\varepsilon}^{m}a=0
in
A_{\varepsilon}
, which implies
a=0
in
A_{\varepsilon}[f_{\varepsilon}^{-1}]
, and

\imath
is injective.

Pick any
b\in A^{\circ}_{V}
.
Observe that
A[f^{-1}]
is dense (for the sup-norm) in
A_{V}=A\langle f^{-1}\rangle
, hence
we can find
a\in A
and
n\in{\mathbb{N}}^{*}
such that
|f^{n}b-\sigma(a)|_{\sup}<\varepsilon
. But this implies

\sigma(a)\in A_{V}^{\circ}
, and
b=f^{-n}\sigma(a)
modulo
\{\rho<\varepsilon\}
.
It follows that
\imath
is surjective, and the claim is proven.

Suppose now that
V
is a general formal affinoid subdomain of
\mathcal{M}(A)
. Then
V
is a finite union of affinoid subdomains

V_{i}=\{|f_{i}|\geq 1\}
with
f_{i}\in A^{\circ}
.
By what precedes we have
\prim((A_{V_{i}})_{\varepsilon})=\prim((A_{V})_{\varepsilon}[f_{i,\varepsilon}^{-1}])
.

We claim that
\prim((A_{V_{i}})_{\varepsilon})
covers
\prim((A_{V})_{\varepsilon})
.
To see this, consider the usual reduction map
\red\colon\mathcal{M}(A_{V})\to\spec(\tilde{A}_{V})
, and denote by
\tilde{f_{i}}
the image of
f_{i}
in
\tilde{A}_{V}
.
Since

Appendix A

Let
k
be any complete metrized field, and
X
a projective variety defined over

Let

be any dominant rational map.
Recall that

is defined in §

Let us prove the following easy bound (thereby proving Proposition

Proof.

Base change induces a canonical continuous map
\pi_{K/k}\colon X^{\an}_{K}\to X^{\an}_{k}

This map is surjective
since the scheme morphism
X_{K}\to X_{k}
is surjective, and any norm on a field
L
admits at least one extension
to any of its field extension

, see
[
ZS60
, §VI.6]
.
9
9

9

This fact also follows from Lemma
4.1
.
Observe now that
\Ind(f_{K})=\Ind(f)_{K}
.
We conclude the proof using Corollary
1.12
.
∎

We now prove a partial converse to this proposition.

Proposition A.2

Let

be the characteristic of

Let us consider the separable closure

Note that the algebraic extension

Recall the norm on
