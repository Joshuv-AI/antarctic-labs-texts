Electronic transport in Weyl semimetals with a uniform concentration of torsional dislocations
Daniel A. Bonilla, Enrique Muñoz

Abstract
In this article, we consider a theoretical model for a type I Weyl semimetal, under the presence of a diluted uniform concentration of torsional dislocations. By a mathematical analysis for partial wave scattering (phase-shift) for the T-matrix, we obtain the corresponding retarded and advanced Green’s functions that include the effects of multiple scattering events with the ensemble of randomly distributed dislocations. Combining this analysis with the Kubo formalism, and including vertex corrections, we calculate the electronic conductivity as a function of temperature and concentration of dislocations. We further evaluate our analytical formulas to predict the electrical conductivity of several transition metal monopnictides, i.e. TaAs, TaP, NbAs and NbP.



I
Introduction

Weyl semimetals (WSMs) constitute a remarkable example of three-dimensional, gapless materials with nontrivial topological properties. First proposed theoretically,
Wan et al. 2011
;
Fang et al. 2012
;
Ruan et al. 2016
;
Vafek and Vishwanath 2014
;
Yan and Felser 2017
;
Armitage et al. 2018
;
Burkov 2018
and, more recently, discovered experimentally on TaAs crystals
Xu et al. 2015
.

In a WSM, the band structure possesses an even number of Weyl nodes with linear dispersion, where
the conduction and valence bands touch. These nodes are monopolar sources of Berry curvature, and hence are protected from being gapped since their charge (chirality) is a topological invariant
Burkov 2018
. In the vicinity of these nodes, low energy conducting states behave as Weyl fermions, i.e. massless quasi-particles with pseudo-relativistic Dirac linear dispersion
Vafek and Vishwanath 2014
;
Yan and Felser 2017
;
Armitage et al. 2018
;
Armitage et al. 2018
;
Burkov 2018
. In Weyl fermions, conserved chirality determines the projection of spin over their momentum direction, a condition referred to as “spin-momentum locking”. While Type I WSMs are Lorentz covariant, this symmetry is violated in Type II WSMs, where the Dirac cones are strongly tilted
Vanderbilt 2018
.

The presence of Weyl nodes in the bulk spectrum determines the emergence of Fermi arcs
Xu et al. 2015
, the chiral anomaly, and the chiral magnetic effect, among other remarkable properties
Vanderbilt 2018
. Therefore, considerable attention has been paid to understand the electronic transport properties of WSMs
Hosur and Qi 2013
;
Hu et al. 2019
;
Nagaosa et al. 2020
. For instance, there are recent works on charge transport
Hosur et al. 2012
in the presence of spin-orbit coupled impurities
Liu et al. 2017
, electrochemical
Flores-Calderón and Martín-Ruiz 2021
and nonlinear transport induced by Berry curvature dipoles
Zeng et al. 2021
. Somewhat less explored are the effects of mechanical strain and deformations in WSMs. From a theoretical perspective, it has been proposed that different types of elastic strains can be modeled as gauge fields in WSMs
Cortijo et al. 2015
;
Cortijo et al. 2016
;
Arjona and Vozmediano 2018
. In previous works, we have studied the combined effects of a single torsional dislocation and an external magnetic field on the electronic
Muñoz and Soto-Garrido 2019
;
Soto-Garrido et al. 2020
and thermoelectric
Muñoz and Soto-Garrido 2019
;
Bonilla et al. 2021
transport properties of WSMs, using the Landauer ballistic formalism in combination with a mathematical analysis for the quantum mechanical scattering cross-sections
Muñoz and Soto-Garrido 2017
.

In this work, we extend our previous analysis to study the case of a diluted, uniform concentration of torsional dislocations and its effects on the electrical conductivity of type I WSMs. In contrast to our former studies
Bonilla et al. 2021
;
Muñoz and Soto-Garrido 2019
;
Soto-Garrido et al. 2020
, here we employ the Kubo linear-response formalism at finite temperatures, that therefore requires to explicitly calculate the retarded and advanced Green´s functions for the system, including the multiple scattering events due to the random distribution of dislocation defects in the form of a disorder-averaged self-energy term. For this purpose, we first analyze the phase shift arising from a single torsional dislocation, and obtain the corresponding (retarded and advanced) Green’s function in terms of the T-matrix elements by solving analytically the Lippmann-Schwinger equation. We further extend this analysis, incorporating the effect of a random distribution of such dislocations, with a concentration
n_{d}
, in the form of a disorder-averaged self-energy into the corresponding Dyson’s equation. Finally, we analyze the correction to the scattering vertex, and by including this additional contribution we calculate the electrical conductivity from the Kubo formula, as a function of temperature and concentration of dislocations. We present explicit evaluations of our analytical expressions for the electrical conductivity as a function of temperature and concentration of dislocations
n_{d}
, for several materials in the family of transition metals monopnictides, i.e. TaAs, TaP, NbAs and NbP, where the corresponding microscopic parameters, estimated by ab-initio methods, where reported in the literature
Szot et al. 2018
;
Lee et al. 2015
;
Grassano et al. 2018
.

Scattering by a single dislocation

As a continuum model for a type I WSM under the presence of a single dislocation defect, as depicted in Fig. (
1
), we consider the Hamiltonian

\hat{H}^{\xi}=\xi\hbar v_{F}\bm{\sigma}\cdot\left(\mathbf{p}+e\mathbf{A}^{\xi}\right)+\sigma_{0}V_{0}\delta(r-a)\equiv\hat{H}^{\xi}_{0}+\hat{H}^{\xi}_{1},

(1)

where

\displaystyle\hat{H}^{\xi}_{0}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\xi v_{F}\bm{\sigma}\cdot\mathbf{p},

(2)

\displaystyle\hat{H}^{\xi}_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\xi ev_{F}\left(\bm{\sigma}\cdot\bm{\hat{\phi}}\right)\frac{1}{2}B^{\xi}r\Theta(a-r)+V_{0}\delta(r-a)\sigma_{0}.

(3)

Here,
\xi=\pm
labels each of the Weyl nodes located at
\mathbf{K}_{\pm}=\pm\mathbf{b}/2
. The expression in Eq. (
2
) is the free-particle Hamiltonian, whereas the expression in Eq. (
3
) represents the interaction with the dislocation, where torsional strain is described as a pseudo-magnetic field inside the cylinder
Muñoz and Soto-Garrido 2017
;
Bonilla et al. 2021
;
Soto-Garrido et al. 2020
, as well as the lattice mismatch effect at the boundary of the dislocation, modeled as
repulsive delta barrier on its surface
Bonilla et al. 2021
.

Pictorial description of the scattering of free incident Weyl fermions coming from a left reservoir by a single cylindrical dislocation defect.

The ”free” spinor eigenfunctions for the defect-free reference system satisfy

\hat{H}_{0}^{\xi}\left|\Phi_{\mathbf{k},\lambda}\right\rangle=\mathscr{E}^{(0,\xi)}_{\lambda,\mathbf{k}}\left|\Phi_{\mathbf{k},\lambda}\right\rangle,

(4)

where the energy spectrum is given by

\mathscr{E}^{(0,\xi)}_{\lambda,\mathbf{k}}=\lambda\xi\hbar v_{F}|\mathbf{k}|,

(5)

and
\lambda=\pm 1
is the band (helicity) index. When projected onto coordinate space, these spinor eigenfunctions have the explicit form

\Phi_{\lambda,\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{r})=\sqrt{\frac{|\mathbf{k}|+\lambda k_{z}}{2|\mathbf{k}|}}\begin{pmatrix}1\\
\frac{\lambda|\mathbf{k}|-k_{z}}{k_{x}-ik_{y}}\end{pmatrix}e^{i\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{r}},

(6)

and constitute an orthonormal basis for the Hilbert space.

If we now consider the (elastic) scattering effects induced by the torsional dislocation modeled by Eq. (
3
), we need to look for the eigenvectors

\left|\Psi_{\lambda,\mathbf{k}}\right\rangle
of the total Hamiltonian in Eq. (
1
) with the same energy as in Eq. (
5
). The answer is provided by the solution to the well known Lippmann-Schwinger equation

\left|\Psi_{\mathbf{k},\lambda}\right\rangle=\left|\Phi_{\mathbf{k},\lambda}\right\rangle+\hat{G}^{\xi}_{R,0}(E)\hat{H}_{1}^{\xi}\left|\Psi_{\mathbf{k},\lambda}\right\rangle,

(7)

where the free Green’s function can be expressed in a coordinate-independent representation form via the
resolvent
,

\hat{G}^{\xi}_{R/A,0}(E)=\left[E-\hat{H}^{\xi}_{0}\pm i\eta^{+}\right]^{-1}.

(8)

Here, the index
R/A
stands for retarded and advanced, respectively. As shown in detail in the Appendix
A
, in the coordinate representation the corresponding free Green’s function is given by the explicit matrix form
\mathbf{G}^{\xi}_{R,0}\left(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r^{\prime}};k\right)=\delta(z-z^{\prime})\mathbf{G}^{\xi}_{R,0}\left(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x^{\prime}};k\right)
, where
\mathbf{r}=(\mathbf{x},z)
and

\displaystyle\mathbf{G}^{\xi}_{R,0}\left(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x^{\prime}};k\right)

\displaystyle=-\frac{\lambda\xi ik}{4\hbar v_{F}}

\displaystyle\times\begin{bmatrix}H^{(1)}_{0}\left(k|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x^{\prime}}|\right)&i\lambda e^{-i\varphi}H^{(1)}_{1}\left(k|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x^{\prime}}|\right)\\
i\lambda e^{i\varphi}H^{(1)}_{1}\left(k|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x^{\prime}}|\right)&H^{(1)}_{0}\left(k|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x^{\prime}}|\right)\end{bmatrix}.

(9)

Here,
H_{0}^{(1)}(z)
and
H_{1}^{(1)}(z)
are the Hankel functions and
\mathbf{x}=(x,y)
is the position vector on any plane perpendicular to the cylinder’s axis.

For the scattering analysis, we need the retarded resolvent for the full Hamiltonian, which is defined as the solution to the equation

\left(E+i\eta^{+}-\hat{H}^{\xi}\right)\hat{G}^{\xi}_{R}(E)=\hat{I}.

(10)

Pictorial description of the scattering event on a plane perpendicular to the cylindrical defect axis.

Combining Eq. (
10
) with Eq. (
8
), we readily obtain

\displaystyle\hat{G}^{\xi}_{R}(E)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\hat{G}^{\xi}_{R,0}(E)+\hat{G}^{\xi}_{R,0}(E)\hat{H}^{\xi}_{1}\hat{G}^{\xi}_{R}(E)

(11)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\hat{G}^{\xi}_{R,0}(E)+\hat{G}^{\xi}_{R,0}(E)\hat{T}^{\xi}(E)\hat{G}^{\xi}_{R,0}(E),

where we introduced the standard definition of the T-matrix operator
\hat{T}^{\xi}(E)
, that can be formally expressed in closed form by

\displaystyle\hat{T}^{\xi}(E)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{1}^{\xi}+\hat{H}_{1}^{\xi}\hat{G}^{\xi}_{R,0}(E)\hat{T}^{\xi}(E)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{1}^{\xi}\left(\hat{I}-\hat{G}^{\xi}_{R,0}(E)\hat{H}_{1}^{\xi}\right)^{-1}.

Using this definition, along with the property
\hat{H}_{1}^{\xi}|\Psi_{\mathbf{k},\lambda}\rangle=\hat{T}^{\xi}|\Phi_{\mathbf{k},\lambda}\rangle
,
we obtain the Lippmann-Schwinger Eq. (
7

\displaystyle\Psi_{\mathbf{k},\lambda}(\mathbf{r})=\Phi_{\mathbf{k},\lambda}(\mathbf{r})\,+

\displaystyle\int d^{3}r^{\prime}\int d^{3}r^{\prime\prime}\left\langle\mathbf{r}\middle|\hat{G}^{\xi}_{R,0}\left(E\right)\middle|\mathbf{r^{\prime}}\right\rangle

(13)

\displaystyle\times\left\langle\mathbf{r^{\prime}}\middle|\hat{T}^{\xi}(E)\middle|\mathbf{r^{\prime\prime}}\right\rangle\Phi_{\mathbf{k},\lambda}(\mathbf{r^{\prime\prime}}).

As shown in detail in Appendix
B
, by considering the asymptotic behavior of the Hankel functions,
H^{(1)}_{\nu}(x)\sim\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi x}}e^{i\left(x-\frac{\nu\pi}{2}-\frac{\pi}{4}\right)}
(for
x\rightarrow\infty.
), Eq. (
13

\Psi_{\mathbf{k}_{\parallel},\lambda}(\mathbf{x})\sim\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{pmatrix}1\\
\lambda\end{pmatrix}e^{ikx}-\frac{\lambda\xi}{2\hbar v_{F}}\sqrt{\frac{ik}{\pi}}T^{(\lambda,\xi)}_{\mathbf{k^{\prime}}_{\parallel}\mathbf{k}_{\parallel}}\begin{pmatrix}1\\

(14)

where as we explain in the Appendix, the particles have only momenta perpendicular to the defect’s axis, i.e.,
\mathbf{k}_{\parallel}=(k_{x},k_{y})
. Comparing this last result with our previous reported expression for the scattering amplitude
Soto-Garrido and Muñoz 2018

\begin{bmatrix}f_{1}(\phi)\\
f_{2}(\phi)\end{bmatrix}=\frac{e^{-\frac{i\pi}{4}}}{\sqrt{4\pi k}}\sum_{m=-\infty}^{\infty}\begin{bmatrix}e^{im\phi}\\
\lambda e^{i(m+1)\phi}\end{bmatrix}\left(e^{2i\delta_{m}}-1\right),

(15)

we identify
T^{(\lambda,\xi)}_{\mathbf{k^{\prime}}\mathbf{k}}=-2\lambda\xi\hbar v_{F}\sqrt{\pi/ik}f_{1}(\phi)
. Therefore, we arrived at an explicit analytical expression for the T-matrix elements in terms of the phase shift
\delta_{m}(k)
for each angular momentum channel

(16)

where
\phi
is the angle between
\mathbf{k}_{\parallel}
and
\mathbf{k^{\prime}}_{\parallel}

B

III
Scattering by a uniform concentration of dislocations

Figure 3:
Random distribution of torsional dislocations seen from a plane perpendicular to the cylinders axis.

Let us now consider a uniform concentration
n_{d}=N_{d}/A
(per unit transverse surface) of identical cylindrical dislocations, as depicted in FIG.
3
, represented by the density function

\rho(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{j=1}^{N_{d}}\delta(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{X}_{j}),

(17)

where
\mathbf{X}_{j}
is the position of the
j
-defect’s axis. The Fourier transform of this density function is thus given by the expression

\tilde{\rho}(\mathbf{k}_{\parallel})=\int d^{2}xe^{-i\mathbf{k}_{\parallel}\cdot\mathbf{x}}\rho(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{j=1}^{N_{d}}e^{-i\mathbf{k}_{\parallel}\cdot\mathbf{X}_{j}}.

(18)

The operator that plays the role of a scattering potential for this distribution of dislocation defects is

V(\mathbf{x})=\int d^{2}x^{\prime}\rho(\mathbf{x^{\prime}})H^{\xi}_{1}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime})=\sum_{j=1}^{N_{d}}H^{\xi}_{1}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{X}_{j}),

(19)

where
H^{\xi}_{1}
is defined in Eq. (
3
) as the contribution from a single dislocation. The matrix elements of the scattering operator Eq. (
19
) in the free spinor basis defined by Eq. (
4
) are

\displaystyle\left\langle\Phi_{\mathbf{k}_{\parallel},\lambda}\middle|V(\mathbf{x})\middle|\Phi_{\mathbf{k^{\prime}}_{\parallel},\lambda^{\prime}}\right\rangle=\left[\tilde{V}(\mathbf{k}_{\parallel}-\mathbf{k^{\prime}}_{\parallel})\right]_{\lambda\lambda^{\prime}},

(20)

where
\tilde{V}(\mathbf{k}_{\parallel})
is the Fourier transform

\displaystyle\tilde{V}(\mathbf{k}_{\parallel})

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}d^{2}xe^{-i\mathbf{k}_{\parallel}\cdot\mathbf{x}}V(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{j=1}^{N_{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}d^{2}xe^{-i\mathbf{k}_{\parallel}\cdot\mathbf{x}}H^{\xi}_{1}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{X}_{j})

\displaystyle=\tilde{H}^{\xi}_{1}(\mathbf{k}_{\parallel})\tilde{\rho}(\mathbf{k}_{\parallel}).

(21)

Then, the matrix elements of the potential in Eq. (
20
) become

\left[\tilde{V}(\mathbf{k}_{\parallel})\right]_{\lambda\lambda^{\prime}}=\left[\tilde{H}^{\xi}_{1}(\mathbf{k}_{\parallel})\right]_{\lambda\lambda^{\prime}}\tilde{\rho}(\mathbf{k}_{\parallel}).

(22)

Let us also introduce the configurational average of some quantity
f(\mathbf{X}_{j})
over the distributed dislocations as

\left\langle f\right\rangle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}d^{2}X_{j}\,\,P(\mathbf{X}_{j})f(\mathbf{X}_{j}),

(23)

where
P(\mathbf{X}_{j})
is the normalized distribution function for the defects in the sample. In particular,
for a uniform distribution we have
P(\mathbf{X}_{j})=1/A
,
where
A
is the area of the plane normal to each cylinder’s axis.
Now, the full retarded Green’s function satisfies for the potential of several dislocations
\hat{V}
given in Eq. (
19

\hat{G}^{\xi}_{R}(E)=\hat{G}^{\xi}_{R,0}(E)+\hat{G}^{\xi}_{R,0}(E)\hat{V}\hat{G}^{\xi}_{R}(E).

(24)

The configurational average, as defined in Eq. (
23

\left\langle\hat{G}^{\xi}_{R}(E)\right\rangle=\hat{G}^{\xi}_{R,0}(E)+\hat{G}^{\xi}_{R,0}(E)\Sigma_{R}^{\lambda,\xi}(E)\left\langle\hat{G}^{\xi}_{R}(E)\right\rangle.

(25)

This is the Dyson’s equation with the retarded self-energy
\Sigma_{R}^{\lambda,\xi}(E)
, that can be explicitly solved to yield

\displaystyle\left\langle G_{R}^{\lambda,\xi}(\mathbf{k}_{\parallel})\right\rangle=\frac{1}{E-\lambda\xi\hbar v_{F}|\mathbf{k}_{\parallel}|-\Sigma_{R}^{\lambda,\xi}(\mathbf{k}_{\parallel})}.

(26)

The effect of the statistical distribution of dislocations’ is entirely dictated by the function
\tilde{\rho}(\mathbf{k}_{\parallel})

n^{th}
-products of the form
\tilde{\rho}(\mathbf{k_{1}})\tilde{\rho}(\mathbf{k_{2}})\cdots\tilde{\rho}(\mathbf{k}_{n})
. The configurational average of these products are

\displaystyle\left\langle\tilde{\rho}(\mathbf{k}_{\parallel})\right\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\langle\sum_{j=1}^{N_{d}}e^{-i\mathbf{k}_{\parallel}\cdot\mathbf{X}_{j}}\right\rangle=\sum_{j=1}^{N_{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}d^{2}X_{j}\,\frac{1}{A}\,e^{-i\mathbf{k}_{\parallel}\cdot\mathbf{X}_{j}}

(27)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{N_{d}}{A}(2\pi)^{2}\delta^{(2)}(\mathbf{k}_{\parallel}),

for a single factor. For the product of two factors, we obtain

\displaystyle\left\langle\tilde{\rho}(\mathbf{k}_{\parallel}^{1})\tilde{\rho}(\mathbf{k}_{\parallel}^{2})\right\rangle=\left\langle\sum_{j=1}^{N_{d}}\sum_{l=1}^{N_{d}}e^{-i\mathbf{k}_{\parallel}^{1}\cdot\mathbf{X}_{j}-i\mathbf{k}_{\parallel}^{2}\cdot\mathbf{X}_{l}}\right\rangle

(28)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\langle\sum_{j=l}e^{-i(\mathbf{k}_{\parallel}^{1}+\mathbf{k}_{\parallel}^{2})\cdot\mathbf{X}_{j}}+\sum_{j\neq l}e^{-i\mathbf{k}_{\parallel}^{1}\cdot\mathbf{X}_{j}-i\mathbf{k}_{\parallel}^{2}\cdot\mathbf{X}_{l}}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{N_{d}}{A}(2\pi)^{2}\delta^{(2)}(\mathbf{k}_{\parallel}^{1}+\mathbf{k}_{\parallel}^{2})+\frac{N_{d}(N_{d}-1)}{A^{2}}(2\pi)^{4}\delta^{(2)}(\mathbf{k}_{\parallel}^{1})\delta^{(2)}(\mathbf{k}_{\parallel}^{2}),

and we have a similar behavior for higher order products. Now, notice that for
N_{d}\gg 1
we have
N_{d}(N_{d}-1)\approx N_{d}^{2}
,
N_{d}(N_{d}-1)(N_{d}-3)\approx N_{d}^{3}
and so on. We define the concentration of defects, i.e., the number of dislocations per unit of area perpendicular to the cylinder’s axis as
n_{d}=N_{d}/A
. As discussed in standard references
Hewson 1993
;
Mahan 2000
, for small concentrations
n_{d}\ll 1
the scaling discussed before ensures that the total Green’s function in Eq. (
25

4

T
-matrix over the random distribution of dislocations after Eq. (
23
)

\Sigma_{R}^{\lambda,\xi}(E)=\left\langle\hat{T}^{\xi}(E)\right\rangle=n_{d}T^{(\lambda,\xi)}_{\mathbf{k}_{\parallel}\mathbf{k}_{\parallel}}.

(29)

Using the expression in Eq. (

\mathbf{k}_{\parallel}=\mathbf{k^{\prime}}_{\parallel}
then

contains an infinite sum over highly oscillatory terms, that converges to zero. So no contribution comes from the real part of the self-energy; The imaginary part, on the other hand, defines the relaxation time,

III.1
Electrical Conductivity in the linear-response

Now, we consider a single Fourier mode for an external electric field in the gauge

\mathbf{E}=-\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{r},t)

(32)

where
\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{r},t)=\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{r},\omega)e^{-i\omega t}
is the vector potential. Then,
\mathbf{E}=i\omega\mathbf{A}
. In the linear response formalism, the current is given by the Kubo expression

\displaystyle j_{\alpha}(\mathbf{r},\omega)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int d^{3}r^{\prime}\,\sigma_{\alpha\beta}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r^{\prime}};\omega)E_{\beta}(\mathbf{r^{\prime}}.\omega),

(33)

Here, the conductivity tensor is given by

\sigma_{\alpha\beta}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r^{\prime}};\omega)=\frac{1}{i\omega}K_{\alpha\beta}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r^{\prime}};\omega).

(34)

The tensor
K_{\alpha\beta}
is defined, in the Kubo formalism, in terms of the retarded current-current correlator as follows

\displaystyle K_{\alpha\beta}(\mathbf{r},t;\mathbf{r^{\prime}},t^{\prime})=i\hbar^{-1}\theta(t-t^{\prime})\rm{Tr}\left\{\hat{\rho}\left[\hat{j}_{\alpha}(\mathbf{r},t),\hat{j}_{\beta}(\mathbf{r^{\prime}},t^{\prime})\right]\right\},

(35)

where
\hat{\rho}
is the statistical density matrix operator. As shown in detail in the Appendix
D
, the Fourier transform to the frequency domain of this tensor can be expressed by

\displaystyle K^{\xi}_{\alpha\beta}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r^{\prime}};\omega)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e^{2}v_{F}^{2}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{dE^{\prime}}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{dE}{2\pi}\frac{f_{0}\left(E^{\prime}\right)-f_{0}\left(E\right)}{\hbar\omega+E-E^{\prime}+i\eta^{+}}

(36)

\displaystyle\times\rm{Tr}\left[\sigma_{\alpha}\bm{\mathscr{A}}^{\xi}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r^{\prime}};E^{\prime})\sigma_{\beta}\bm{\mathscr{A}}^{\xi}(\mathbf{r^{\prime}},\mathbf{r};E)\right],

where
f_{0}(E)=\left[e^{(E-\mu)/kT}+1\right]^{-1}
is the Fermi distribution, and we introduced the (disorder-averaged) spectral function

\displaystyle\mathscr{A}^{\lambda,\xi}(k)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle i\left[\left\langle G_{R}^{\lambda,\xi}(\mathbf{k}_{\parallel})\right\rangle-\left\langle G_{A}^{\lambda,\xi}(\mathbf{k}_{\parallel})\right\rangle\right]

(37)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{2\left(\frac{\hbar}{2\tau^{(\lambda,\xi)}(k)}\right)}{\left(E-\mathscr{E}^{\lambda,\xi}_{k}\right)^{2}+\left(\frac{\hbar}{2\tau^{(\lambda,\xi)}(k)}\right)^{2}},

that clearly reduces to a Lorentzian distribution whose spectral width is defined by the inverse of the relaxation time. See Appendix
C
for the details. After some algebraic manipulations, we obtain the conductivity tensor at finite frequency and temperature

\displaystyle\text{Re}\,\sigma^{\xi}_{\alpha\beta}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r^{\prime}};\omega)=-\frac{e^{2}\hbar v_{F}^{2}}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dE\left\{\frac{f_{0}(E+\hbar\omega)-f_{0}(E)}{\hbar\omega}\right\}

\displaystyle\times\rm{Tr}\left[\sigma_{\alpha}\bm{\mathscr{A}}^{\xi}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r^{\prime}};E+\hbar\omega)\sigma_{\beta}\bm{\mathscr{A}}^{\xi}(\mathbf{r^{\prime}},\mathbf{r};E)\right].

(38)

Using the coordinates representation of the spectral function given in Appendix
C
, after Eq. (
86
), we can read off the Fourier transform to momentum space of the conductivity

\displaystyle\sigma^{\xi}_{\alpha\beta}(\mathbf{q};\omega)=-\frac{e^{2}\hbar v_{F}^{2}}{2\pi}\int\frac{d^{3}k}{(2\pi)^{3}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dE\left\{\frac{f_{0}(E+\hbar\omega)-f_{0}(E)}{\hbar\omega}\right\}

\displaystyle\quad\times\sum_{\lambda,\lambda^{\prime}}\Tr\left\{\sigma_{\alpha}\left(\sigma_{0}+\lambda\frac{\bm{\sigma\cdot}(\mathbf{k}_{\parallel}+\mathbf{q})}{|\mathbf{k}_{\parallel}+\mathbf{q}|}\right)\sigma_{\beta}\left(\sigma_{0}+\lambda^{\prime}\frac{\bm{\sigma\cdot}\mathbf{k}_{\parallel}}{|\mathbf{k}_{\parallel}|}\right)\right\}

\displaystyle\quad\times\mathscr{A}^{\lambda,\xi}(|\mathbf{k}_{\parallel}+\mathbf{q}|;E+\hbar\omega)\mathscr{A}^{\lambda^{\prime},\xi}(|\mathbf{k}_{\parallel}|;E).

(39)

We are interested in the DC conductivity, so we take the limit
\mathbf{q}\rightarrow\mathbf{0}
first and then the limit
\omega\rightarrow 0
. After a long calculation (details in the Appendix
D
), the result is

\displaystyle\sigma_{\alpha\beta}^{(\lambda,\xi)}(T)

\displaystyle=\delta_{\alpha\beta}\frac{e^{2}\hbar v_{F}^{2}}{\pi^{3}}\int_{0}^{\infty}dk\,\,\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dE\,\,\left(-\frac{\partial f_{0}(E)}{\partial E}\right)

\displaystyle\times\left\langle G_{R}^{\lambda,\xi}(\mathbf{k}_{\parallel})\right\rangle\left\langle G_{A}^{\lambda,\xi}(\mathbf{k}_{\parallel})\right\rangle\mathbf{k}_{\parallel}\cdot\mathbf{k}_{\parallel}.

(40)

V
Conclusions

In this work, we have studied the effect of a distribution of mechanical defects, i.e. torsional dislocations, over the electrical conductivity of the family of transition metals monopnictides TaAs, TaP, NbAs and NbP. Our theory is based on the mathematical analysis of the scattering phase shifts from a single defect, as stated in our previous work
Bonilla et al. 2021
;
Muñoz and Soto-Garrido 2019
;
Muñoz and Soto-Garrido 2017
;
Soto-Garrido and Muñoz 2018
;
Soto-Garrido et al. 2020
. We extended this previous analysis to develop a Green´s function formalism, in order to represent the scattering due to a finite concentration of randomly distributed defects. Within the non-crossing approximation for the self-energy, we solved explicitly for the disorder-averaged retarded Green´s function, that allows us to calculate the electrical conductivity in the Kubo linear-response formalism. We obtained general analytical expressions in terms of the parameters involved in the low-energy model representing the family of materials, and using the
ab-initio
estimations for such parameters, we provided a characterization of the conductivity as a function of temperature and concentration of defects for the transition metal monopnictides TaAs, TaP, NbAs and NbP. As a universal feature, we identified a
\sim T^{2}
temperature dependence for
T\gg\hbar v_{F}k_{F}/k_{B}
, where the pre-factor depends on material-specific microscopic parameters as well as in the concentration of dislocations
n_{d}
through the scattering relaxation time.
Our results do not involve the electron-phonon scattering effects, that will presumably contribute at higher temperatures, which is a subject of further study.

Appendix A
Calculation of the retarded free Green’s function

The free retarded Green’s function (GF) is represented in the coordinate basis as follows

\mathbf{G}^{\xi}_{R,0}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r^{\prime}};E)=\left\langle\mathbf{r}\middle|\frac{1}{E-\hat{H}^{\xi}_{0}+i\eta^{+}}\middle|\mathbf{r^{\prime}}\right\rangle.

(54)

In this basis,
\mathbf{G}^{\xi}_{R,0}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r^{\prime}};E)
satisfies the differential equation

\left(E+i\eta^{+}-\xi v_{F}\bm{\sigma}\cdot\frac{\hbar}{i}\bm{\nabla}\right)\mathbf{G}^{\xi}_{R,0}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r^{\prime}};E)=\sigma_{0}\delta^{(3)}(\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r^{\prime}}).

(55)

where
\sigma_{0}
is the
2\times 2
unit matrix. Let us introduce the scalar GF,
\mathscr{G}^{\xi}_{R,0}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r^{\prime}})
, by means of the expression

\mathbf{G}^{\xi}_{R,0}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r^{\prime}};E)=\left(E+i\eta^{+}+\xi v_{F}\bm{\sigma}\cdot\frac{\hbar}{i}\bm{\nabla}\right)\mathscr{G}^{\xi}_{R,0}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r^{\prime}}).

(56)

Bearing in mind that we are treating the elastic scattering problem, the energy of the out-state must be the same as those of the incident-free-particle state, i.e.,
E=\lambda\xi\hbar v_{F}|\mathbf{k}|
. Then, the scalar GF satisfies the Helmholtz equation

\left(\nabla^{2}+k^{2}+i\eta^{+}\right)\mathscr{G}^{\xi}_{R,0}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r^{\prime}})=\frac{1}{\hbar^{2}v_{F}^{2}}\delta^{(3)}(\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r^{\prime}}).

(57)

Due to the symmetry along the
z
-axis we can decouple it from its perpendicular plane as follows

\mathscr{G}^{\xi}_{R,0}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r^{\prime}};k)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{dq_{z}}{2\pi}e^{iq_{z}(z-z^{\prime})}\mathscr{G}^{\xi}_{R,0}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x^{\prime}};q_{z},k),

(58)

where
\mathscr{G}^{\xi}_{R,0}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x^{\prime}};q_{z},k)
is a reduced GF and
\mathbf{x}=(x,y)
is the position vector on the plane. Then, the Helmholtz equation for the reduced GF on the plane takes the form

\left(\nabla_{\parallel}^{2}-q_{z}^{2}+k^{2}+i\eta^{+}\right)\mathscr{G}^{\xi}_{R,0}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x^{\prime}};q_{z},k)=\frac{1}{\hbar^{2}v_{F}^{2}}\delta^{(2)}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x^{\prime}}),

(59)

where
\nabla_{\parallel}^{2}=\partial_{x}^{2}+\partial_{y}^{2}
. As we can be seen from the FIG.
1
, the free incident particle’s propagation is normal to the cylinder’s axis. We assume that the incident particles have negligible momentum along the
z
-axis, and by momentum conservation, they remain with negligible momentum along that direction during the transport process. Then, we can write
\mathbf{k}=(\mathbf{k}_{\parallel},0)
where
\mathbf{k}_{\parallel}=(k_{x},k_{y})
. Hence, the system is reduced to an effective two-dimensional description and we can consider the reduced GF on the plane as independent of the Fourier mode
q_{z}
. Then, from Eq.(
58
) we have
\mathscr{G}^{\xi}_{R,0}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r^{\prime}};k)=\delta(z-z^{\prime})\mathscr{G}^{\xi}_{R,0}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x^{\prime}};k)
, and we can expand the reduced GF on the plane in the traverse Fourier space

\mathscr{G}^{\xi}_{R,0}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x^{\prime}};k)=\int\frac{d^{2}q_{\parallel}}{(2\pi)^{2}}e^{i\mathbf{q}_{\parallel}\cdot(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x^{\prime}})}\tilde{\mathscr{G}}^{\xi}_{R,0}(\mathbf{q}_{\parallel}),

(60)

where
\mathbf{q}_{\parallel}=(q_{x},q_{y})
. Replacing in the Eq.(
59
), we obtain in the traverse Fourier space

\tilde{\mathscr{G}}^{\xi}_{R,0}(\mathbf{q}_{\parallel})=-\frac{1}{\hbar^{2}v_{F}^{2}}\frac{1}{q_{\parallel}^{2}-k^{2}-i\eta^{+}}.

(61)

We perform the integration in Eq.(
60
) in polar coordinates

\displaystyle\mathscr{G}^{\xi}_{R,0}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x^{\prime}};k)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\hbar^{2}v_{F}^{2}}\frac{2\pi}{(2\pi)^{2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}dq_{\parallel}\,q_{\parallel}\frac{J_{0}(q_{\parallel}R)}{q_{\parallel}^{2}-k^{2}-i\eta^{+}},

(62)

where
R=|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x^{\prime}}|
and we have used the integral representation of the Bessel functions
\int_{0}^{2\pi}e^{iz\cos\phi\pm in\phi}d\phi=2\pi i^{n}J_{n}(z)
.
In order to perform the last integration we need the result

\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{x^{\nu+1}J_{\nu}(bx)}{\left(x^{2}+a^{2}\right)^{\mu+1}}dx=\frac{a^{\nu-\mu}b^{\mu}}{2^{\mu}\Gamma(\mu+1)}K_{\nu-\mu}(ab),

(63)

together with the relation
K_{n}(z)=\frac{\pi}{2}i^{n+1}H^{(1)}_{n}\left(iz\right)
. The result is

\mathscr{G}^{\xi}_{R,0}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x^{\prime}};k)=-\frac{i}{4\hbar^{2}v_{F}^{2}}H^{(1)}_{0}\left(k|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x^{\prime}}|\right).

(64)

This form is adequate because in the asymptotic form for large
|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x^{\prime}}|
it produces outgoing cylindrical waves as it is desired for the retarded GF. Now, to obtain the final form for the free GF matrix we apply the definition in Eq.(
56
) with
E=\lambda\xi\hbar v_{F}|\mathbf{k}|
, taking into account that we have reduced to a two dimensional system on the plane
x
-
y

\displaystyle\mathbf{G}^{\xi}_{R,0}\left(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r^{\prime}};k\right)

\displaystyle=-\frac{i\lambda\xi}{4\hbar v_{F}}\delta(z-z^{\prime})

\displaystyle\quad\quad\times\left(k\sigma_{0}-i\lambda\bm{\sigma}\cdot\bm{\nabla}_{\parallel}\right)H^{(1)}_{0}\left(k|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x^{\prime}}|\right).

(65)

In plane polar coordinates

\bm{\sigma}\cdot\bm{\nabla}_{\parallel}=\left(\bm{\sigma}\cdot\mathbf{\hat{r}}\right)\frac{\partial}{\partial r}+\left(\bm{\sigma}\cdot\bm{\hat{\varphi}}\right)\frac{1}{r}\frac{\partial}{\partial\varphi}

(66)

where
r=|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x^{\prime}}|
,
\varphi
is the angle the vector
\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x^{\prime}}
makes with the
x
axis and

\bm{\sigma}\cdot\mathbf{\hat{r}}=\begin{pmatrix}0&e^{-i\varphi}\\
e^{i\varphi}&0\end{pmatrix},\quad\bm{\sigma}\cdot\bm{\hat{\varphi}}=\begin{pmatrix}0&-ie^{-i\varphi}\\
ie^{i\varphi}&0\end{pmatrix}.

(67)

The final form for the retarded Green’s function matrix in the coordinates representation is

\displaystyle\mathbf{G}^{\xi}_{R,0}\left(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r^{\prime}};k\right)

\displaystyle=-\frac{\lambda\xi ik}{4\hbar v_{F}}\delta(z-z^{\prime})

\displaystyle\times\begin{bmatrix}H^{(1)}_{0}\left(k|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x^{\prime}}|\right)&i\lambda e^{-i\varphi}H^{(1)}_{1}\left(k|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x^{\prime}}|\right)\\
i\lambda e^{i\varphi}H^{(1)}_{1}\left(k|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x^{\prime}}|\right)&H^{(1)}_{0}\left(k|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x^{\prime}}|\right)\end{bmatrix},

(68)

which produces Eq.(
9
).

for large
|\mathbf{x}|
as follows

|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x^{\prime}}|\sim r-\mathbf{\hat{n}}\cdot\mathbf{x^{\prime}}+\mathscr{O}\left((r^{\prime}/r)^{2}\right),

(76)

where
r=|\mathbf{x}|
,
r^{\prime}=|\mathbf{x^{\prime}}|
and
\mathbf{\hat{n}}
is the unit vector in the direction of
\mathbf{x}
, i.e.,
\mathbf{\hat{n}}=\mathbf{x}/r
. Noting that in this asymptotic form the direction of
\mathbf{k^{\prime}}_{\parallel}
coincides with that of
\mathbf{x}
and is practically the same of
\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x^{\prime}}
, i.e.,
\mathbf{k^{\prime}}_{\parallel}=k\mathbf{\hat{n}}
and that the angle
\phi
the vector
\mathbf{k^{\prime}}_{\parallel}
makes with the
\mathbf{k}_{\parallel}
incident momentum is approximately the same angle
\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x^{\prime}}
does, i.e.,
\phi\sim\varphi
, we have the asymptotic form for the free Green’s function in Eq.(
9
)

\mathbf{G}^{\xi}_{R,0}\left(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x^{\prime}};k\right)\sim-\frac{\lambda\xi k}{4\hbar v_{F}}\sqrt{\frac{2i}{\pi k}}\begin{bmatrix}1&\lambda e^{-i\phi}\\
\lambda e^{i\phi}&1\end{bmatrix}e^{-i\mathbf{k^{\prime}}_{\parallel}\cdot\mathbf{x^{\prime}}}\frac{e^{ikr}}{\sqrt{r}}.

(77)

Replacing the asymptotic form in Eq.(
77
) in the Eq.(
72
) we obatain Eq.(
14
)

\Psi_{\mathbf{k}_{\parallel},\lambda}(\mathbf{x})\sim\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{pmatrix}1\\
\lambda\end{pmatrix}e^{ikx}-\frac{\lambda\xi}{2\hbar v_{F}}\sqrt{\frac{ik}{\pi}}T^{(\lambda,\xi)}_{\mathbf{k^{\prime}}_{\parallel}\mathbf{k}_{\parallel}}\begin{pmatrix}1\\
\lambda e^{i\phi}\end{pmatrix}\frac{e^{ikr}}{\sqrt{r}},

(78)

where the
T
-matrix elements are

T^{(\lambda,\xi)}_{\mathbf{k^{\prime}}_{\parallel}\mathbf{k}_{\parallel}}\left(E\right)=\int d^{2}x^{\prime}\Phi^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k^{\prime}}_{\parallel},\lambda}(\mathbf{x^{\prime}})\mathbf{T}^{\xi}(\mathbf{x^{\prime}},E)\Phi_{\mathbf{k}_{\parallel},\lambda}(\mathbf{x^{\prime}}).

(79)

In order to compute the
T
-matrix elements we need the phase shifts, whose analytical expression is presented in Eq.(32) of the supplemental material of our previous work Ref.
Bonilla et al. 2021
. Here we reproduce the final result

\tan\delta_{m}(k)=\frac{\beta J_{m+1}(ka)-\varrho_{n}^{\xi}J_{m}(ka)\cdot z_{a}^{\frac{|m+1|-|m|}{2}}\frac{L_{n^{\prime}_{\rho}}^{|m+1|}\left(z_{a}\right)}{L_{n_{\rho}}^{|m|}\left(z_{a}\right)}+\tan\alpha\left[J_{m}(ka)+\beta\varrho_{n}^{\xi}J_{m+1}(ka)\cdot z_{a}^{\frac{|m+1|-|m|}{2}}\frac{L_{n^{\prime}_{\rho}}^{|m+1|}\left(z_{a}\right)}{L_{n_{\rho}}^{|m|}\left(z_{a}\right)}\right]}{\beta Y_{m+1}(ka)-\varrho_{n}^{\xi}Y_{m}(ka)\cdot z_{a}^{\frac{|m+1|-|m|}{2}}\frac{L_{n^{\prime}_{\rho}}^{|m+1|}\left(z_{a}\right)}{L_{n_{\rho}}^{|m|}\left(z_{a}\right)}+\tan\alpha\left[Y_{m}(ka)+\beta\varrho_{n}^{\xi}Y_{m+1}(ka)\cdot z_{a}^{\frac{|m+1|-|m|}{2}}\frac{L_{n^{\prime}_{\rho}}^{|m+1|}\left(z_{a}\right)}{L_{n_{\rho}}^{|m|}\left(z_{a}\right)}\right]},

(80)

where
z_{a}=|B_{\xi}|a^{2}/2\tilde{\phi}_{0}
(
a
is the cylinder’s radius).

Appendix C
The spectral function

The spectral function can be defined as follows

\displaystyle\hat{\mathscr{A}}^{\xi}(E)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle i\left[\hat{G}^{\xi}_{R}(E)-\hat{G}^{\xi}_{A}(E)\right],

(81)

in terms of the complete retarded and advanced Green’s functions. Then, the spectral function is Hermitian
\left[\hat{\mathscr{A}}^{\xi}(E)\right]^{\dagger}=\hat{\mathscr{A}}^{\xi}(E)
. Given the averaged complete retarded Green’s function in Eq.(
26
), the form of the spectral function in momentum space is

\displaystyle\mathscr{A}^{\lambda,\xi}(\mathbf{k}_{\parallel})=i\left[\left\langle G_{R}^{\lambda,\xi}(\mathbf{k}_{\parallel})\right\rangle-\left\langle G_{A}^{\lambda,\xi}(\mathbf{k}_{\parallel})\right\rangle\right]

\displaystyle=i\left[\frac{1}{E-\lambda\xi\hbar v_{F}k-\Sigma_{R}^{\lambda,\xi}(\mathbf{k}_{\parallel})}-\frac{1}{E-\lambda\xi\hbar v_{F}k-\Sigma_{A}^{\lambda,\xi}(\mathbf{k}_{\parallel})}\right].

(82)

Clearly, it takes the form of a Lorentzian distribution with compact support around the free particle’s energy

\mathscr{A}^{\lambda,\xi}(k)=\frac{2\left(\frac{\hbar}{2\tau^{(\lambda,\xi)}(k)}\right)}{\left(E-\mathscr{E}^{\lambda,\xi}_{k}\right)^{2}+\left(\frac{\hbar}{2\tau^{(\lambda,\xi)}(k)}\right)^{2}},

(83)

where
\tau^{(\lambda,\xi)}(k)
is the
relaxation time
and
\mathscr{E}^{\lambda,\xi}_{k}=\lambda\xi\hbar v_{F}k
. In the limit of low concentration of defects, i.e., large relaxation time because of Eq.(
31
), the spectral function becomes a delta distribution

\lim_{\tau\rightarrow\infty}\mathscr{A}^{\lambda,\xi}(k)=2\pi\delta\left(E-\mathscr{E}^{\lambda,\xi}_{k}\right).

(84)

Due to its behavior as a Lorentzian, the spectral function has the important property
Mahan 2000

\mathscr{A}^{\lambda,\xi}(k)\mathscr{A}^{\lambda^{\prime},\xi}(k^{\prime})=\mathscr{A}^{\lambda,\xi}(k)\mathscr{A}^{\lambda,\xi}(k)\delta_{\lambda\lambda^{\prime}}.

(85)

Representing the spectral function Eq.(
81
) in the coordinate basis using the complete set of eigenstates of the full Hamiltonian we have

\bm{\mathscr{A}}^{\xi}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r^{\prime}})=\int\frac{d^{3}k}{(2\pi)^{3}}e^{i\mathbf{k}\cdot(\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r^{\prime}})}\sum_{\lambda}\left(\sigma_{0}+\lambda\frac{\bm{\sigma\cdot}\mathbf{k}_{\parallel}}{|\mathbf{k}_{\parallel}|}\right)\mathscr{A}^{\lambda,\xi}(k).

(86)

Notice that because
\mathbf{k}_{\parallel}=(k_{x},k_{y})
, when we perform the integration the spectral function takes the form
\bm{\mathscr{A}}^{\xi}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r^{\prime}})=\delta(z-z^{\prime})\bm{\mathscr{A}}^{\xi}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x^{\prime}})
, which looks similar to the decoupled form of the GF in Eq.(
68
).

Appendix D
Linear Response Theory

The tensor
K_{\alpha\beta}
is defined using the retarded current-current correlator in Eq.(
35
). Introducing in Eq.(
35
) the complete and orthonormal basis
\left\{\left|\Psi_{\lambda,\mathbf{k}}\right\rangle\right\}
of the total Hamiltonian, such that
\hat{H}^{\xi}\left|\Psi_{\lambda,\mathbf{k}}\right\rangle=\mathscr{E}^{\lambda,\xi}_{\mathbf{k}}\left|\Psi_{\lambda,\mathbf{k}}\right\rangle
and
\hat{\rho}\left|\Psi_{\lambda,\mathbf{k}}\right\rangle=\rho\left(\mathscr{E}^{\lambda,\xi}_{\mathbf{k}}\right)\left|\Psi_{\lambda,\mathbf{k}}\right\rangle
, we obtain

\displaystyle K^{\xi}_{\alpha\beta}(\mathbf{r},t;\mathbf{r^{\prime}},t^{\prime})=i\hbar^{-1}\theta(t-t^{\prime})

\displaystyle\quad\times\int\frac{d^{3}k}{(2\pi)^{3}}\int\frac{d^{3}k^{\prime}}{(2\pi)^{3}}\sum_{\lambda,\lambda^{\prime}}\left[\rho\left(\mathscr{E}^{\lambda,\xi}_{\mathbf{k}}\right)-\rho\left(\mathscr{E}^{\lambda^{\prime},\xi}_{\mathbf{k^{\prime}}}\right)\right]

\displaystyle\quad\times\left\langle\Psi_{\lambda,\mathbf{k}}\middle|\hat{j}_{\alpha}(\mathbf{r})\middle|\Psi_{\lambda^{\prime},\mathbf{k^{\prime}}}\right\rangle\left\langle\Psi_{\lambda^{\prime},\mathbf{k^{\prime}}}\middle|\hat{j}_{\beta}(\mathbf{r^{\prime}})\middle|\Psi_{\lambda,\mathbf{k}}\right\rangle e^{\frac{i}{\hbar}\left(\mathscr{E}^{\lambda,\xi}_{\mathbf{k}}-\mathscr{E}^{\lambda^{\prime},\xi}_{\mathbf{k^{\prime}}}\right)(t-t^{\prime})}.

(87)

Using the Fourier representation of the Heaviside step function we obtain the correlator in the frequency domain

\displaystyle K^{\xi}_{\alpha\beta}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r^{\prime}};\omega)

\displaystyle=\int\frac{d^{3}k}{(2\pi)^{3}}\int\frac{d^{3}k^{\prime}}{(2\pi)^{3}}\sum_{\lambda,\lambda^{\prime}}\frac{\left[\rho\left(\mathscr{E}^{\lambda^{\prime},\xi}_{\mathbf{k^{\prime}}}\right)-\rho\left(\mathscr{E}^{\lambda,\xi}_{\mathbf{k}}\right)\right]}{\hbar\omega+\mathscr{E}^{\lambda,\xi}_{\mathbf{k}}-\mathscr{E}^{\lambda^{\prime},\xi}_{\mathbf{k^{\prime}}}+i\eta^{+}}

\displaystyle\times\left\langle\Psi_{\lambda,\mathbf{k}}\middle|\hat{j}_{\alpha}(\mathbf{r})\middle|\Psi_{\lambda^{\prime},\mathbf{k^{\prime}}}\right\rangle\left\langle\Psi_{\lambda^{\prime},\mathbf{k^{\prime}}}\middle|\hat{j}_{\beta}(\mathbf{r^{\prime}})\middle|\Psi_{\lambda,\mathbf{k}}\right\rangle.

(88)

The electric current density operator for the Weyl equation is
\mathbf{\hat{j}}^{\xi}(\mathbf{r})=-e\xi v_{F}\left|\mathbf{r}\right\rangle\bm{\sigma}\left\langle\mathbf{r}\right|
. Then

\displaystyle K^{\xi}_{\alpha\beta}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r^{\prime}};\omega)

\displaystyle\quad=e^{2}v_{F}^{2}\int\frac{d^{3}k}{(2\pi)^{3}}\int\frac{d^{3}k^{\prime}}{(2\pi)^{3}}\sum_{\lambda,\lambda^{\prime}}\frac{\left[\rho\left(\mathscr{E}^{\lambda^{\prime},\xi}_{\mathbf{k^{\prime}}}\right)-\rho\left(\mathscr{E}^{\lambda,\xi}_{\mathbf{k}}\right)\right]}{\hbar\omega+\mathscr{E}^{\lambda,\xi}_{\mathbf{k}}-\mathscr{E}^{\lambda^{\prime},\xi}_{\mathbf{k^{\prime}}}+i\eta^{+}}

\displaystyle\quad\phantom{=}\times\Tr\left[\sigma_{\alpha}\Psi_{\lambda^{\prime},\mathbf{k^{\prime}}}(\mathbf{r})\otimes\Psi_{\lambda^{\prime},\mathbf{k^{\prime}}}^{\dagger}(\mathbf{r^{\prime}})\sigma_{\beta}\Psi_{\lambda,\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{r^{\prime}})\otimes\Psi_{\lambda,\mathbf{k}}^{\dagger}(\mathbf{r})\right].

(89)

We can rewrite this last expression in terms of the spectral function and the retarded/advanced GFs as follows

\displaystyle K^{\xi}_{\alpha\beta}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r^{\prime}};\omega)=-2e^{2}v_{F}^{2}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{dE}{2\pi}f_{0}(E)

\displaystyle\quad\times\Tr\left[\sigma_{\alpha}\bm{\mathscr{A}}^{\xi}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r^{\prime}};E)\sigma_{\beta}\mathbf{G}^{\xi}_{A}(\mathbf{r^{\prime}},\mathbf{r};E-\hbar\omega)\right.

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\quad\left.+\sigma_{\alpha}\mathbf{G}^{\xi}_{R}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r^{\prime}};E+\hbar\omega)\sigma_{\beta}\bm{\mathscr{A}}^{\xi}(\mathbf{r^{\prime}},\mathbf{r};E)\right],

(90)

where the additional factor of 2 is due to the spin degeneracy and
f_{0}(E)
is the Fermi distribution. In the first term, we can shift energy variable
E\rightarrow E+\hbar\omega
, such that

\displaystyle K^{\xi}_{\alpha\beta}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r^{\prime}};\omega)=-2e^{2}v_{F}^{2}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{dE}{2\pi}

\displaystyle\quad\times\left[f_{0}(E+\hbar\omega)\Tr\sigma_{\alpha}\bm{\mathscr{A}}^{\xi}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r^{\prime}};E+\hbar\omega)\sigma_{\beta}\mathbf{G}^{\xi}_{A}(\mathbf{r^{\prime}},\mathbf{r};E)\right.

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\left.+f_{0}(E)\Tr\sigma_{\alpha}\mathbf{G}^{\xi}_{R}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r^{\prime}};E+\hbar\omega)\sigma_{\beta}\bm{\mathscr{A}}^{\xi}(\mathbf{r^{\prime}},\mathbf{r};E)\right].

(91)

We are interested in the real part of the conductivity tensor. Then

\displaystyle\text{Re}\,\sigma^{\xi}_{\alpha\beta}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r^{\prime}};\omega)=\text{Re}\,\left(\frac{1}{i\omega}K^{\xi}_{\alpha\beta}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r^{\prime}};\omega)\right)

\displaystyle\quad=-\frac{i}{2\omega}\left[K^{\xi}_{\alpha\beta}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r^{\prime}};\omega)-K^{\xi\dagger}_{\beta\alpha}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r^{\prime}};\omega)\right],

(92)

where we have used that
K^{*}_{\alpha\beta}=K^{\dagger}_{\beta\alpha}
. Then, taking the Hermitian conjugate of Eq.(
91
) and using the cyclic property of the trace we can write

\displaystyle\text{Re}\,\sigma^{\xi}_{\alpha\beta}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r^{\prime}};\omega)

\displaystyle=-\frac{e^{2}\hbar v_{F}^{2}}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dE\left\{\frac{f_{0}(E+\hbar\omega)-f_{0}(E)}{\hbar\omega}\right\}

\displaystyle\quad\times\Tr\sigma_{\alpha}\bm{\mathscr{A}}^{\xi}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r^{\prime}};E+\hbar\omega)\sigma_{\beta}\bm{\mathscr{A}}^{\xi}(\mathbf{r^{\prime}},\mathbf{r};E).

(93)

Introducing the spectral density in Eq.(
86
) in the DC conductivity expression in Eq.(
93
) we have

\displaystyle\text{Re}\,\sigma^{\xi}_{\alpha\beta}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r^{\prime}};\omega)=\int\frac{d^{3}q}{(2\pi)^{3}}e^{i\mathbf{q}\cdot(\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r^{\prime}})}\left[-\frac{e^{2}\hbar v_{F}^{2}}{2\pi}\int\frac{d^{3}k^{\prime}}{(2\pi)^{3}}\right.

\displaystyle\quad\times\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dE\,\left\{\frac{f_{0}(E+\hbar\omega)-f_{0}(E)}{\hbar\omega}\right\}

\displaystyle\quad\times\sum_{\lambda,\lambda^{\prime}}\Tr\left\{\sigma_{\alpha}\left(\sigma_{0}+\lambda\frac{\bm{\sigma\cdot}(\mathbf{k}_{\parallel}+\mathbf{q})}{|\mathbf{k}_{\parallel}+\mathbf{q}|}\right)\sigma_{\beta}\left(\sigma_{0}+\lambda^{\prime}\frac{\bm{\sigma\cdot}\mathbf{k}_{\parallel}}{|\mathbf{k}_{\parallel}|}\right)\right\}

\displaystyle\quad\left.\phantom{-\frac{e^{2}\hbar v_{F}^{2}}{2\pi}}\times\mathscr{A}^{\lambda,\xi}(|\mathbf{k}_{\parallel}+\mathbf{q}|;E+\hbar\omega)\mathscr{A}^{\lambda^{\prime},\xi}(|\mathbf{k}_{\parallel}|;E)\right].

(94)

Then, the Fourier transform to the momentum space of the conductivity is the result in Eq.(
39
). We are computing the DC conductivity, so we take the limit
\mathbf{q}\rightarrow\mathbf{0}
first and then the limit
\omega\rightarrow 0
. The result is

\displaystyle\sigma^{\xi}_{\alpha\beta}(T)=-\frac{e^{2}\hbar v_{F}^{2}}{2\pi}\int\frac{d^{3}k}{(2\pi)^{3}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dE\frac{\partial f_{0}(E)}{E}

\displaystyle\quad\times\sum_{\lambda}\Tr\left\{\sigma_{\alpha}\left(\sigma_{0}+\lambda\frac{\bm{\sigma\cdot}\mathbf{k}_{\parallel}}{|\mathbf{k}_{\parallel}|}\right)\sigma_{\beta}\left(\sigma_{0}+\lambda\frac{\bm{\sigma\cdot}\mathbf{k}_{\parallel}}{|\mathbf{k}_{\parallel}|}\right)\right\}

\displaystyle\quad\times\mathscr{A}^{\lambda,\xi}(|\mathbf{k}_{\parallel}|;E)\mathscr{A}^{\lambda,\xi}(|\mathbf{k}_{\parallel}|;E).

(95)

where we have used Eq.(
85
). Now, we perform the trace

\displaystyle\Tr\left\{\sigma_{\alpha}\left(\sigma_{0}+\lambda\frac{\bm{\sigma\cdot}\mathbf{k}_{\parallel}}{|\mathbf{k}_{\parallel}|}\right)\sigma_{\beta}\left(\sigma_{0}+\lambda\frac{\bm{\sigma\cdot}\mathbf{k}_{\parallel}}{|\mathbf{k}_{\parallel}|}\right)\right\}

\displaystyle\quad=\Tr\left\{\sigma_{\alpha}\sigma_{\beta}+\lambda\left(\sigma_{\alpha}\sigma_{\beta}\sigma_{\gamma}+\sigma_{\alpha}\sigma_{\gamma}\sigma_{\beta}\right)\frac{k_{\gamma}}{|\mathbf{k}_{\parallel}|}\right.

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\quad\left.+\lambda^{2}\sigma_{\alpha}\sigma_{\gamma}\sigma_{\beta}\sigma_{\gamma^{\prime}}\frac{k_{\gamma}k_{\gamma^{\prime}}}{|\mathbf{k}_{\parallel}|^{2}}\right\}

\displaystyle\quad=4\frac{k_{\alpha}k_{\beta}}{|\mathbf{k}_{\parallel}|^{2}},

(96)

where we have used the Pauli matrices trace technology and that
\lambda^{2}=1
. The angular integration is performed immediately as follows

\int d\Omega\,\,k_{\alpha}k_{\beta}=k^{2}\int_{0}^{\pi}d\theta\sin\theta\int_{0}^{2\pi}d\phi\,\,n_{\alpha}n_{\beta}=2\pi k^{2}\delta_{\alpha\beta},

(97)

where
n_{\alpha}
is the component of the unit vector
\mathbf{n}
along the
\alpha
-direction (on the plane) and
\mathbf{k}_{\parallel}=k\mathbf{n}
. Then, we have

\displaystyle\sigma^{\xi}_{\alpha\beta}(T)

\displaystyle=-\delta_{\alpha\beta}\frac{4e^{2}\hbar v_{F}^{2}}{(2\pi)^{3}k_{B}T}\sum_{\lambda}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dE\,\,f_{0}(E)\left[1-f_{0}(E)\right]

\displaystyle\quad\times\int_{0}^{\infty}dk\left[\left\langle G_{R}^{\lambda,\xi}(\mathbf{k}_{\parallel})\right\rangle-\left\langle G_{A}^{\lambda,\xi}(\mathbf{k}_{\parallel})\right\rangle\right]

\displaystyle\quad\times\left[\left\langle G_{R}^{\lambda,\xi}(\mathbf{k}_{\parallel})\right\rangle-\left\langle G_{A}^{\lambda,\xi}(\mathbf{k}_{\parallel})\right\rangle\right]\mathbf{k}_{\parallel}\cdot\mathbf{k}_{\parallel},

(98)

where we have used the definition of the spectral function in terms of the retarded and advanced GFs. In the limit of low concentration of defects, i.e.,
n_{d}\rightarrow 0
, we have that the unique leading contribution to the conductivity are given by the combination

\displaystyle\left\langle G_{R}^{\lambda,\xi}(\mathbf{k}_{\parallel})\right\rangle\left\langle G_{A}^{\lambda,\xi}(\mathbf{k}_{\parallel})\right\rangle=\frac{1}{\left(E-\lambda\xi\hbar v_{F}k\right)^{2}+\left(\frac{\hbar}{2\tau^{(\lambda,\xi)}(k)}\right)^{2}}

\displaystyle\quad\rightarrow\frac{2\pi\tau^{(\lambda,\xi)}(k)}{\hbar}\delta(E-\lambda\xi\hbar v_{F}k),

(99)

where we have used that the self-energy is purely imaginary and its relation with the relaxation time. The other contributions are negligible because they are not singular in
n_{d}
. For instance, in the low concentration limit, we have a contribution for two retarded GFs of the form

\displaystyle\left\langle G_{R}^{\lambda,\xi}(\mathbf{k}_{\parallel})\right\rangle\left\langle G_{R}^{\lambda,\xi}(\mathbf{k}_{\parallel})\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{\left(E-\lambda\xi\hbar v_{F}k\right)^{2}-\left(\frac{\hbar}{2\tau^{(\lambda,\xi)}(k)}\right)^{2}}{\left\{\left(E-\lambda\xi\hbar v_{F}k\right)^{2}+\left(\frac{\hbar}{2\tau^{(\lambda,\xi)}(k)}\right)^{2}\right\}^{2}}

\displaystyle+i\left(E-\lambda\xi\hbar v_{F}k\right)2\pi\delta(E-\lambda\xi\hbar v_{F}k).

(100)

The second term is zero and the first term is a function centered at
E-\lambda\xi\hbar v_{F}k
, but when integrated over
k
it gives zero. Something similar occurs with the contribution of two advanced GFs. The result is the diagonal conductivity tensor given in Eq.(
40
).
