Quantum Electrodynamics of Dicke States: Resonant One-Photon Exchange Energy and Entangled Decay Rate
U. D. Jentschura, C. M. Adhikari

Abstract
We calculate the one-photon exchange contribution to the interatomic interaction potential between electrically neutral, identical atoms, one of which is assumed to be in an excited state, by matching the scattering matrix ( S S matrix) element with the effective Hamiltonian. This approach allows us to use covariant perturbation theory, where the two possible time orderings of emission and absorption are summarized in a single Feynman amplitude. Our results encompass the full retardation correction to the one-photon exchange van-der-Waals potential. We employ the temporal gauge for the virtual photon propagator. Based on the Feynman prescription, we obtain the imaginary part of the interaction energy, which leads to an interaction-induced correction to the decay rate. Our results lead to precise formulas for the distance-dependent enhancement and suppression of the decay rates of entangled superradiant and subradiant Dicke state, as a function of the interatomic distance. We apply the result to an example calculation involving two hydrogen atoms, one of which is in the ground state, the other in an excited P P state.



I
Introduction

Normally, one assumes that
interatomic long-range interactions
(van der Waals interactions) are associated
with fluctuating dipole moments of
the two interacting atoms, which, in turn,
are due to quantum fluctuations
of the electron positions in both atoms.
In the dipole approximation,
the interaction Hamiltonian is
equal to the scalar product
of the dipole operator and the
second-quantized electric-field
operator. If both atoms are in an initial
ground state (which is generally
spherically symmetric), then both of them
undergo a virtual dipole transition
to an excited state upon emission
(atom
A
) and absorption
(atom
B
) of a virtual photon.
The process is complete upon the exchange
of a second virtual photon,
with the order of emission
(atom
B
) and absorption
(atom
A
) reversed. The final state
has both atoms in the ground state,
without any photons present
(no excitations of the photon field).
The energy of the final state
is equal to that of the initial state,
but energy conservation does not hold for
the virtual transitions.

Because each emission and absorption of
a virtual photon involves a single power
of the interaction Hamiltonian,
the exchange of two photons (two emissions
and two absorptions) is a process of
fourth-order perturbation theory.
The corresponding interaction energy
can be calculated in both time-ordered
perturbation theory (see Ref.
Craig and Thirunamachandran 1984

and Chap. 5 of Ref.
Jentschura and Adkins 2022
)
and by matching the scattering amplitude
to the effective Hamiltonian
(see Ref.
Jentschura and Debierre 2017
and Chap. 12
of Ref.
Jentschura and Adkins 2022
).
The above situation pertains
to two interacting ground-state atoms,
and the resulting expressions contain
the polarizabilities of both involved atoms.
The result (for both identical as well as
non-identical
ground-state
atoms)
smoothly interpolates between the non-retarded
short-range
1/R^{6}
so-called van der Waals limit
and the fully retarded
1/R^{7}
long-range limit
of the interaction.
The process involves the exchange
of two virtual photons
(see Ref.
Craig and Thirunamachandran 1984

and Chap. 5 of Ref.
Jentschura and Adkins 2022
).

However, if we consider two identical atoms,
one of which is in an excited state, there can be energetically
degenerate states of the two-atom system
which are connected with the initial
state of the process by the exchange
of a single, not two, virtual photons.
In the quantum-field theoretical
picture, this situation corresponds to a nonvanishing
transition matrix elements of the interaction
Hamiltonian already in the second-order perturbation
theory of the second-quantized Hamiltonian.
For example, if one of the atoms (atom
A
) is a

The final state is energetically fully degenerate

superpositions of product states of atoms

B

|(1S)_{A}(2P)_{B}\rangle

|(2P)_{A}(1S)_{B}\rangle

In the nonretarded approximation, this process

Jonsell et al. 2002
;
Jentschura and Adhikari 2017
;
Adhikari et al. 2017

Jentschura et al. 2017

{{S}}
–Matrix and Effective Hamiltonian

We consider two identical atoms

Calculation of the Effective Hamiltonian

In time-dependent quantum

The second-quantized operators in the interaction

\displaystyle\;\int_{0}^{\infty}{\mathrm{d}}t\,{\mathrm{e}}^{{\mathrm{i}}\omega t}\,\left<\psi_{e}\,\psi_{g}\left|d_{A}^{i}(t)\,d_{B}^{j}(0)\right|\psi_{g}\,\psi_{e}\right>+\int_{-\infty}^{0}{\mathrm{d}}t\,{\mathrm{e}}^{{\mathrm{i}}\omega t}\,\left<\psi_{e}\,\psi_{g}\left|d_{B}^{j}(0)\,d_{A}^{i}(t)\right|\psi_{g}\,\psi_{e}\right>

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\;\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}+\int_{-\infty}^{0}\right){\mathrm{d}}t\,{\mathrm{e}}^{{\mathrm{i}}\omega t}\,\left<\psi_{e}\left|d_{A}^{i}(t)\right|\psi_{g}\right>\,\left<\psi_{g}\left|d_{B}^{j}(0)\right|\psi_{e}\right>\,.

(21)

We have used the fact that the atoms are identical
and undergo transitions
|g\rangle\to|e\rangle

and
|e\rangle\to|g\rangle
, respectively.
We can thus add the two integration domains and
conclude that

\displaystyle X^{ij}(\omega)=

\displaystyle\;\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}{\mathrm{d}}t\,{\mathrm{e}}^{{\mathrm{i}}\omega t}\,\left<\psi_{e}\left|d_{A}^{i}(t)\right|\psi_{g}\right>\,\left<\psi_{g}\left|d_{B}^{j}(0)\right|\psi_{e}\right>

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\;\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}{\mathrm{d}}t\,{\mathrm{e}}^{{\mathrm{i}}\omega t}\,{\mathrm{e}}^{{\mathrm{i}}(E_{2}-E_{1})\,t}\,\left<\psi_{e}\left|d_{A}^{i}(0)\right|\psi_{g}\right>\,\left<\psi_{g}\left|d_{B}^{j}(0)\right|\psi_{e}\right>

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\;2\pi\delta(E_{2}-E_{1}+\omega)\,\left<\psi_{e}\left|d_{A}^{i}(0)\right|\psi_{g}\right>\,\left<\psi_{g}\left|d_{B}^{j}(0)\right|\psi_{e}\right>\,.

(22)

The two terms in Eq. (
III
)
yield equivalent contributions,
and we obtain, with the help of the results obtained previously
for the time-ordered products of the
electric-field and atomic dipole operators,

\displaystyle S^{(2)}=

\displaystyle\;\frac{1}{2}\int{\mathrm{d}}t_{1}\int{\mathrm{d}}t_{2}\,\left<0\left|{\mathcal{T}}\left[E^{i}(\vec{R}_{A},t_{1})\,E^{j}(\vec{R}_{B},t_{2})\right]\right|0\right>\,\left<\psi_{e}\,\psi_{g}\left|{\mathrm{T}}\;d_{A}^{i}(t_{1})\,d_{B}^{j}(t_{2})\right|\psi_{g}\,\psi_{e}\right>

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\;\int{\mathrm{d}}t_{1}\int{\mathrm{d}}t_{2}\,\left[-{\mathrm{i}}\,\int\frac{{\mathrm{d}}\omega}{2\pi}\,\omega^{2}\,D_{F}^{ij}(\omega,\vec{R})\,{\mathrm{e}}^{-{\mathrm{i}}\omega(t_{1}-t_{2})}\right]{\mathrm{e}}^{-{\mathrm{i}}\,(E_{1}-E_{2})\,(t_{1}-t_{2})}\,

\displaystyle\;\times\left<\psi_{e}\left|d_{A}^{i}\right|\psi_{g}\right>\,\left<\psi_{g}\left|d_{B}^{j}\right|\psi_{e}\right>

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\;-{\mathrm{i}}\int{\mathrm{d}}(t_{1}-t_{2})\int{\mathrm{d}}t_{2}\int\frac{{\mathrm{d}}\omega}{2\pi}\omega^{2}\,D_{F}^{ij}(\omega,\vec{R}){\mathrm{e}}^{-{\mathrm{i}}(\omega+E_{1}-E_{2})\,(t_{1}-t_{2})}\left<\psi_{e}\left|d_{A}^{i}\right|\psi_{g}\right>\left<\psi_{g}\left|d_{B}^{j}\right|\psi_{e}\right>

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\;-{\mathrm{i}}\,T\,(E_{2}-E_{1})^{2}\,D_{F}^{ij}(|E_{2}-E_{1}|,\vec{R})\,\left<\psi_{e}\,\psi_{g}\left|d_{A}^{i}\,d_{B}^{j}\right|\psi_{g}\,\psi_{e}\right>\,,

(23)

where
d_{A}^{i}\equiv d_{A}^{i}(0)
and
d_{Bj}\equiv d_{Bj}(0)
.
Based on the matching relation (
12
)
and remembering Eq. (

we read off the interaction potential,

\displaystyle U(\vec{r}_{A},\vec{r}_{B},\vec{R})=

\displaystyle\;(E_{2}-E_{1})^{2}\,D^{ij}(|E_{2}-E_{1}|,\vec{R})\,d_{A}^{i}\,d_{B}^{j}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\;-\omega_{0}^{2}\,\left[\alpha^{ij}+\beta^{ij}\,\left(\frac{{\mathrm{i}}}{\omega_{0}\,R}-\frac{1}{\omega_{0}^{2}\,R^{2}}\right)\right]\,\frac{{\mathrm{e}}^{{\mathrm{i}}\omega_{0}\,R}}{4\pi R}\,d_{A}^{i}\,d_{B}^{j}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\;\left[\left(\delta^{ij}-3\frac{R^{i}\,R^{j}}{R^{2}}\right)\,\left(1-{\mathrm{i}}\omega_{0}R\right)-\left(\delta^{ij}-\frac{R^{i}\,R^{j}}{R^{2}}\right)\omega_{0}^{2}\,R^{2}\right]\,\frac{{\mathrm{e}}^{{\mathrm{i}}\omega_{0}\,R}}{4\pi R^{3}}\,d_{A}^{i}\,d_{B}^{j}\,.

(24)

This result confirms
results previously obtained in
Eqs. (13), (14), (40), (41) and (42) of Ref.
Andrews and Sherborne 1987
,
Eqs. (12), (13), (14) of Ref.
Daniels et al. 2003
,
Eq. (2.23) of Ref.
Jenkins et al. 2004
,
Eqs. (4) and (5) of Ref.
Salam 2015
,
Eq. (2.1) of Ref.
Jenkins et al. 2016
,
Eqs. (3.5), (3.6) and (3.7) of Ref.
Andrews 1989
,
Eq. (14) of Ref.
Salam 2018
,
and Eq. (5) of Ref.
Jones and Bradshaw 2019
.
One important advantage of the method of derivation
employed here is that the imaginary part
follows directly from the Feynman prescription
for the propagator denominators.
In other approaches, additional considerations are
required to fix the location of the poles
of the propagators in the complex plane
(for an illustrative discussion on this
point, see Ref.
Jones and Bradshaw 2019
).

This potential can be separated into a real and an

\displaystyle{\rm Re}[U(\vec{r}_{A},\vec{r}_{B},\vec{R})]=

\displaystyle\;\left[\beta^{ij}\,\left(\cos(\omega_{0}\,R)+\omega_{0}R\,\sin(\omega_{0}\,R)\right)-\alpha^{ij}\,\omega_{0}^{2}\,R^{2}\,\cos(\omega_{0}\,R)\right]\,\frac{d_{A}^{i}\,d_{B}^{j}}{4\pi R^{3}}\,,

(25a)

\displaystyle{\rm Im}[U(\vec{r}_{A},\vec{r}_{B},\vec{R})]=

\displaystyle\;\left[\beta^{ij}\,\left(\sin(\omega_{0}\,R)-\omega_{0}R\,\cos(\omega_{0}\,R)\right)-\alpha^{ij}\,\omega_{0}^{2}\,R^{2}\,\sin(\omega_{0}\,R)\right]\,\frac{d_{A}^{i}\,d_{B}^{j}}{4\pi R^{3}}\,.

(25b)

The real part had previously been
given in Eq. (5) of Ref.
McLone and Power 1964

and in Eq. (7.2.27) on page 149 in Chapter 7
of Ref.
Craig and Thirunamachandran 1984
.

We have used the relations given in Eq. (
19
).
Using
\hat{R}=\vec{R}/R
, one has
the static limit (
\omega_{0}R\to 0
),

U(\vec{r}_{A},\vec{r}_{B},\vec{R})\to\left(\delta_{ij}-3\hat{R}_{i}\,\hat{R}_{j}\right)\frac{d_{Ai}\,d_{Bj}}{4\pi R^{3}}\,\qquad\omega_{0}\to 0\,,

(26)

which verifies the well-known expression for the
non-retarded van-der-Waals interaction
given in Eq. (
1
). SI units can be restored by
multiplication with an additional overall factor
1/\epsilon_{0}
,
and replacing the factor
\omega_{0}\,R
by the
(dimensionless) factor
\omega_{0}\,R/c
.

.

IV
Interpretation of the Imaginary Part

The imaginary part of the interatomic interaction
potential given in Eq. (
25b
) is oscillating in
sign. It describes the one-photon resonant
emission from the decaying excited state
which forms part of the entangled Dicke
states given in Eq. (
2
).
in full analogy, the imaginary part of the
one-loop self-energy of an excited bound
state in atom is naturally interpreted
in terms of the decay width of that same
excited reference state
Barbieri and Sucher 1978
;
Jentschura 2007
;
Jentschura 2008
.
The corresponding Feynman diagram is
given in Fig.
2
.
The oscillating character of the imaginary part
could be seen as a problem because
the imaginary part of the
resonance energy of a decaying state
is required to be negative
Moiseyev 1998
;
Jentschura et al. 2009
;
a positive imaginary part would correspond
to an anti-resonance and a negative decay rate.

The solution is presented
in the following. We can
anticipate that the negative decay rate
which could otherwise naively result from the interaction
energy is compensated by the natural decay rate,
to give the subradiant and superradiant states their
(nonnegative) decay rates.

One generally writes a resonance energy in terms of

{\rm Re}\,E-{\mathrm{i}}\Gamma/2
, where

\Gamma
is the width. Hence, it is useful
to define a decay rate operator

\widehat{\Gamma}(\xi)
, where

\xi=\omega_{0}\,R
is the dimensionless
argument appearing in Eq. (
25b
).
This operator is
related to the imaginary part of the
exchange potential as follows,

\displaystyle\widehat{\Gamma}(\xi)=

\displaystyle\;-2\;{\rm Im}[U(\vec{r}_{A},\vec{r}_{B},\vec{R})]\,,\qquad\xi=\omega_{0}\,R\,.

(27)

We write the result given in Eq. (
25b
)
somewhat differently as

\displaystyle\widehat{\Gamma}(\xi)=

\displaystyle\;\frac{3}{2}\frac{\xi\,\cos(\xi)+[\xi^{2}-1]\,\sin(\xi)}{\xi^{3}}\,\frac{4\alpha}{3}\,\omega_{0}^{3}\,\left(\delta^{ij}-\frac{R^{i}\,R^{j}}{R^{2}}\right)r_{A}^{i}\,r_{B}^{j}

\displaystyle\;+3\frac{\sin(\xi)-\xi\,\cos(\xi)}{\xi^{3}}\frac{4\alpha}{3}\,\omega_{0}^{3}\,\frac{R^{i}\,R^{j}}{R^{2}}\,r_{A}^{i}\,r_{B}^{j}

(28)

This expression can be conveniently
written in terms of a
transverse decay operator
\widehat{\Gamma}_{\perp}(\xi)
, which describes
transition whose polarization axis is perpendicular to the
interatomic distance vector
\vec{R}
, and a
longitudinal decay operator
\widehat{\Gamma}_{\parallel}(\xi)
, which describes
transition whose polarization axis is parallel to the
interatomic distance vector
\vec{R}
.
The result is

\widehat{\Gamma}(\xi)=\widehat{\Gamma}_{\perp}(\xi)+\widehat{\Gamma}_{\parallel}(\xi)\,,\qquad\widehat{\Gamma}_{\perp}(\xi)=f_{\perp}(\xi)\,\widehat{\Gamma}_{\perp}(0)\,,\qquad\widehat{\Gamma}_{\parallel}(\xi)=f_{\parallel}(\xi)\,\widehat{\Gamma}_{\parallel}(0)\,.

(29)

The weight functions
f_{\perp}(\xi)
and

f_{\parallel}(\xi)
and the
transverse and longitudinal decay operator
at zero distance,
\widehat{\Gamma}_{\perp}(0)
and

\widehat{\Gamma}_{\parallel}(0)
, are given as follows,

\displaystyle\widehat{\Gamma}_{\perp}(0)=

\displaystyle\;\frac{4\alpha}{3}\,\omega_{0}^{3}\,\left(\delta^{ij}-\frac{R^{i}\,R^{j}}{R^{2}}\right)r_{A}^{i}\,r_{B}^{j}\,,

(30)

\displaystyle\widehat{\Gamma}_{\parallel}(0)=

\displaystyle\;\frac{4\alpha}{3}\,\omega_{0}^{3}\,\frac{R^{i}\,R^{j}}{R^{2}}\,r_{A}^{i}\,r_{B}^{j}\,,

(31)

\displaystyle\widehat{\Gamma}(0)=

\displaystyle\;\widehat{\Gamma}_{\perp}(0)+\widehat{\Gamma}_{\parallel}(0)=\frac{4\alpha}{3}\,\omega_{0}^{3}\,\delta^{ij}\,r_{A}^{i}\,r_{B}^{j}\,,

(32)

\displaystyle f_{\perp}(\xi)=

\displaystyle\;3\frac{\xi\cos(\xi)+[\xi^{2}-1]\,\sin(\xi)}{2\xi^{3}}=1+\mathcal{O}(\xi^{2})\,,

(33)

\displaystyle f_{\parallel}(\xi)=

\displaystyle\;3\,\frac{\sin(\xi)-\xi\,\cos(\xi)}{\xi^{3}}=1+\mathcal{O}(\xi^{2})\,,

(34)

Here,
\widehat{\Gamma}(0)
is the total decay operator
at zero distance. It fulfills the relation

\langle\psi_{e}\,\psi_{g}|\widehat{\Gamma}(0)|\psi_{g}\,\psi_{e}\rangle=\Gamma(0)
, where
\Gamma(0)
(no hat symbol over the
\Gamma
)
is the natural decay width of the excited state in vacuum,
which is obtained without consideration of the
atom-atom-interaction and its consequential
modification of the decay rate.
One compares it with Eq. (3.37) of Ref.
Jentschura and Adkins 2022
.

Let us now investigate the two Dicke states,

\displaystyle|\Psi_{\pm}\rangle=

\displaystyle\;\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\,\left[|\psi_{e}\,\psi_{g}\rangle\pm|\psi_{g}\,\psi_{e}\rangle\right]\,.

(35)

The expectation value of the decay rate operator
can be written as follows,

\displaystyle\langle\Psi_{\pm}|\widehat{\Gamma}(\xi)|\Psi_{\pm}\rangle=

\displaystyle\;\pm\langle\psi_{g}\,\psi_{e}|\widehat{\Gamma}(\xi)|\psi_{e}\,\psi_{g}\rangle

(36)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\;\pm\left[f_{\perp}(\xi)\,\Gamma^{eg}_{\perp}(0)+f_{\parallel}(\xi)\,\Gamma^{eg}_{\parallel}(0)\right]\,.

(37)

Here,

\displaystyle\Gamma^{eg}_{\perp}(0)=

\displaystyle\;\langle\psi_{g}\,\psi_{e}|\widehat{\Gamma}_{\perp}(0)|\psi_{e}\,\psi_{g}\rangle=\frac{4\alpha}{3}\,\omega_{0}^{3}\,\left(\delta^{ij}-\frac{R^{i}\,R^{j}}{R^{2}}\right)\langle g|r_{A}^{i}|e\rangle\,\langle e|r_{B}^{j}|g\rangle

(38)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\;\frac{4\alpha}{3}\,\omega_{0}^{3}\,\left(\delta^{ij}-\frac{R^{i}\,R^{j}}{R^{2}}\right)\langle g|r_{A}^{i}|e\rangle\,\langle e|r_{A}^{j}|g\rangle\,,

(39)

\displaystyle\Gamma^{eg}_{\parallel}(0)=

\displaystyle\;\langle\psi_{g}\,\psi_{e}|\widehat{\Gamma}_{\parallel}(0)|\psi_{e}\,\psi_{g}\rangle=\frac{4\alpha}{3}\,\omega_{0}^{3}\,\frac{R^{i}\,R^{j}}{R^{2}}\,\langle g|r_{A}^{i}|e\rangle\,\langle e|r_{B}^{j}|g\rangle

(40)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\;\frac{4\alpha}{3}\,\omega_{0}^{3}\,\frac{R^{i}\,R^{j}}{R^{2}}\,\langle g|r_{A}^{i}|e\rangle\,\langle e|r_{A}^{j}|g\rangle\,,

(41)

\displaystyle\Gamma(0)=

\displaystyle\;\Gamma^{eg}_{\perp}(0)+\Gamma^{eg}_{\parallel}(0)=\frac{4\alpha}{3}\,\omega_{0}^{3}\,|\langle g|\vec{r}_{A}|e\rangle|^{2}\,.

(42)

We have repeatedly used the fact that the
two atoms are identical, to replace
r_{B}^{j}\to r_{A}^{j}
.
Note that
\Gamma^{eg}_{\perp}(0)
and

\Gamma^{eg}_{\parallel}(0)
can depend on the
magnetic projections,
but the natural width
\Gamma(0)
is independent
of magnetic quantum numbers
Jentschura and Adkins 2022
.

In the space spanned by the

|\psi_{g}\psi_{e}\rangle
and

|\psi_{e}\psi_{g}\rangle
, one finally obtains
the following effective Hamiltonian
for the two-atoms system,
which takes into account the one-photon exchange
and, with it, the imaginary part of the
exchange energy,

\mathbbm{H}=\left[E_{g}+E_{e}-\tfrac{{\mathrm{i}}}{2}\Gamma(0)\right]\,\left(|\psi_{g}\psi_{e}\rangle\,\langle\psi_{g}\psi_{e}|+|\psi_{e}\psi_{g}\rangle\,\langle\psi_{e}\psi_{g}|\right)\\
+\left\{|\psi_{g}\,\psi_{e}\rangle\,\left\{{\rm Re}\,U(\xi)-\frac{{\mathrm{i}}}{2}\left[f_{\perp}(\xi)\,\Gamma_{\perp}(0)+f_{\parallel}(\xi)\,\Gamma_{\parallel}(0)\right]\right\}\langle\psi_{e}\,\psi_{e}|+\mbox{h.c.}\right\}

(43)

Here,
E_{e}={\rm Re}E_{e}
is the real part of the
energy of the excited state,
and we have supplemented the term

-{\mathrm{i}}\Gamma(0)/2
in the resonance energy of the
unperturbed states.
It means that in the basis of states

|\psi_{g}\psi_{e}\rangle
,

|\psi_{e}\psi_{g}\rangle
, the Hamiltonian matrix is

\mathbbm{H}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}E_{0}&\delta E\\
\delta E&E_{0}\\
\end{array}\right)\,,\qquad E_{0}=E_{g}+E_{e}-\frac{{\mathrm{i}}}{2}\,\Gamma(0)\,,\qquad\delta E=E_{\gamma}-\frac{{\mathrm{i}}}{2}\,\Gamma_{\gamma}\,,

(44)

Here,

E_{\gamma}=\langle\psi_{g}\,\psi_{e}|{\rm Re}\,U(\xi)|\psi_{g}\,\psi_{e}\rangle\,,\qquad\Gamma_{\gamma}=\langle\psi_{g}\,\psi_{e}|\widehat{\Gamma}(\xi)|\psi_{g}\,\psi_{e}\rangle\,,

(45)

describe the real and imaginary parts of the one-photon exchange energy.
We write the vector representation of the Dicke states

|\Psi_{-}\rangle
and
|\Psi_{+}\rangle
and

E_{-}=E_{-}(\xi)
, and
E_{+}=E_{+}(\xi)
, as follows,

|\Psi_{\pm}\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\,\left(\begin{array}[]{c}1\\
\pm 1\end{array}\right)\,,\qquad E_{\pm}=E_{0}\pm\delta E\,.

(46)

The resonance energies can be written as follows,

\displaystyle E_{-}(R)=

\displaystyle\;{\rm Re}\,E_{0}+E_{\gamma}-\frac{{\mathrm{i}}}{2}\left\{\Gamma(0)-\left[f_{\perp}(\xi)\,\Gamma^{eg}_{\perp}(0)+f_{\parallel}(\xi)\,\Gamma^{eg}_{\parallel}(0)\right]\right\}\,,

(47)

\displaystyle E_{+}(R)=

\displaystyle\;{\rm Re}\,E_{0}-E_{\gamma}-\frac{{\mathrm{i}}}{2}\left\{\Gamma(0)+\left[f_{\perp}(\xi)\,\Gamma^{eg}_{\perp}(0)+f_{\parallel}(\xi)\,\Gamma^{eg}_{\parallel}(0)\right]\right\}\,.

(48)

The effective decay rates of the Dicke states are

\displaystyle\Gamma_{-}(R)=

\displaystyle\;\Gamma(0)-\left[f_{\perp}(\xi)\,\Gamma^{eg}_{\perp}(0)+f_{\parallel}(\xi)\,\Gamma^{eg}_{\parallel}(0)\right]=\mathcal{O}(\xi^{2})\,,

(49)

\displaystyle\Gamma_{+}(R)=

\displaystyle\;\Gamma(0)+\left[f_{\perp}(\xi)\,\Gamma^{eg}_{\perp}(0)+f_{\parallel}(\xi)\,\Gamma^{eg}_{\parallel}(0)\right]=2\Gamma(0)-\mathcal{O}(\xi^{2})\,,

(50)

Hence,

|\Psi_{-}\rangle
is the entangled subradiant state,
while
|\Psi_{+}\rangle
is the superradiant state.
In the short-range limit, the subradiant state

|\Psi_{-}\rangle
becomes metastable in view of the
entanglement, while the superradiant state

|\Psi_{+}\rangle
acquires twice the
natural decay width.

V
Verification of the Result

Let us include some cryptic remarks regarding
a verification of the results given in Eqs. (
49
)
and (
50
) based on an alternative method.
To this end, one considers Eq. (7.2.4) of Ref.
Craig and Thirunamachandran 1984
,
but crucially takes into account that the
entangled photon emissions from the atoms
A
and
B

happen at different positions.
Thus, one considers the matrix element
M
,

\displaystyle M=

\displaystyle\;\tfrac{1}{2}\left|\left<\psi_{g}\,\psi_{g}\left|\hat{\epsilon}\cdot\vec{r}_{A}\;{\mathrm{e}}^{{\mathrm{i}}\vec{k}\cdot\vec{R}_{A}}+\hat{\epsilon}\cdot\vec{r}_{B}\;{\mathrm{e}}^{{\mathrm{i}}\vec{k}\cdot\vec{R}_{B}}\right|\psi_{e}\,\psi_{g}\pm\psi_{g}\,\psi_{e}\right>\right|^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\;\left|\left<\psi_{g}\left|\hat{\epsilon}\cdot\vec{r}_{A}\right|\psi_{e}\right>\right|^{2}\pm\left|\left<\psi_{g}\left|\hat{\epsilon}\cdot\vec{r}_{A}\right|\psi_{e}\right>\right|^{2}\,\cos(\vec{k}\cdot\vec{R})\,.

(51)

where
\vec{R}=\vec{R}_{A}-\vec{R}_{B}
.
In the last step, we have used the indistinguishability
of the two identical atoms.
Furthermore, on resonance, one has
|\vec{k}|=\omega_{0}
.
The sum over the two photon polarizations results in

\sum_{\lambda}\epsilon_{\lambda}^{i}\,\epsilon_{\lambda}^{j}=\delta^{ij}-\frac{k^{i}\,k^{j}}{\vec{k}^{2}}\,,

(52)

After some algebra, one shows the result

\displaystyle\int{\mathrm{d}}\Omega\frac{3}{8\pi}\left(\delta^{ij}-\frac{k^{i}\,k^{j}}{\vec{k}^{2}}\right)\,\cos(\omega_{0}\hat{k}\cdot\vec{R})=\left(\delta_{ij}-\frac{R^{i}\,R^{j}}{\vec{R}^{2}}\right)\,\,f_{\perp}(\omega_{0}R)+\frac{R^{i}\,R^{j}}{\vec{R}^{2}}f_{\parallel}(\omega_{0}R)\,,

(53)

where the previously obtained weight functions

f_{\perp}(\omega_{0}R)
and

f_{\parallel}(\omega_{0}R)
[see Eqs. (
33
) and (
34
)]
naturally appear.
Based on these identities and the formalism outlined
in Chaps. 3 and 4 of Ref.
Jentschura and Adkins 2022
,
one can verify the results given in Eqs. (
49
)
and (
50
).
The first term on the right-hand side of Eq. (
53
)
gives the term
\Gamma(0)
in
Eqs. (
49
) and (
50
),
while the
second
term on the right-hand side of Eq. (
53
)
gives the second term on the right-hand side of
Eqs. (
49
) and (
50
).

Conversely, the introduction of the Feynman prescription
into the formalism outlined in Ref.
Craig and Thirunamachandran 1984
,
which was initially designed to obtain the real
part of the resonant one-photon exchange interaction only,
leads to the imaginary part obtained here.
Notes on this point are collected in the Appendix.

VI
Example of Superradiant and Subradiant States

We would like to conclude this work with a concrete example,
namely, the hydrogen atom, where the
1S
state is the ground state
and the excited state could be one of three
2P
states,
which we take in a Cartesian basis.
Furthermore, we assume that the interatomic
distance is along the
z
axis,

\vec{R}=R\,\hat{\mathrm{e}}_{z}\,.

(54)

The wave functions are well known,

\displaystyle\psi_{g}(\vec{r})=

\displaystyle\;\psi_{1S}(\vec{r})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}\,a_{0}^{3/2}}\,\exp\left(-\frac{r}{a_{0}}\right)\,,\qquad r=|\vec{r}|\,,

(55a)

\displaystyle\psi_{e,x}(\vec{r})=

\displaystyle\;\psi_{2P,x}(\vec{r})=\frac{r\,\sin\theta\,\cos\varphi}{4\sqrt{2\pi}\,a_{0}^{5/2}}\,\exp\left(-\frac{r}{2a_{0}}\right)\,,

(55b)

\displaystyle\psi_{e,y}(\vec{r})=

\displaystyle\;\psi_{2P,y}(\vec{r})=\frac{r\,\sin\theta\,\sin\varphi}{4\sqrt{2\pi}\,a_{0}^{5/2}}\,\exp\left(-\frac{r}{2a_{0}}\right)\,,

(55c)

\displaystyle\psi_{e,z}(\vec{r})=

\displaystyle\;\psi_{2P,z}(\vec{r})=\frac{r\,\cos\theta}{4\sqrt{2\pi}\,a_{0}^{5/2}}\,\exp\left(-\frac{r}{2a_{0}}\right)\,.

(55d)

The nonvanishing dipole transition matrix elements are as follows,

\langle\psi_{g}|x|\psi_{e,x}\rangle=\langle\psi_{g}|y|\psi_{e,y}\rangle=\langle\psi_{g}|z|\psi_{e,z}\rangle=\frac{2^{7}}{3^{5}}\,\sqrt{2}\,a_{0}\,,

(56)

while all other combinations vanish
(e.g.,
\langle\psi_{g}|x|\psi_{e,y}\rangle=0
).
The resonance frequency is

\omega_{0}=\frac{3}{8}\,\alpha^{2}m

(57)

and the natural decay width is
(see Ref.
Jentschura and Mohr 2002
)

\Gamma(0)=\frac{2^{8}}{3^{8}}\alpha^{5}m\,.

(58)

The
x
and
y
polarized
P
states
cannot decay via a
z
-polarized transition,
while the
z
polarized
P
states
decays exclusively via a
z
-polarized transition.
We have the results

\Gamma_{\perp}^{0x}(0)=\Gamma_{\perp}^{0y}(0)=\Gamma_{\parallel}^{0z}(0)=\Gamma(0)\,,\qquad\Gamma_{\parallel}^{0x}(0)=\Gamma_{\parallel}^{0y}(0)=0\,,\qquad\Gamma_{\perp}^{0z}(0)=0\,.

(59)

One can thus define the distance-dependent
transverse and longitudinal decay rates,

\Gamma_{\perp}(\xi)
and
\Gamma_{\parallel}(\xi)
,

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\perp}(\xi)=

\displaystyle\;\Gamma_{\perp}^{0x}(\xi)=\Gamma_{\perp}^{0y}(\xi)=f_{\perp}(\xi)\,\Gamma(0)\,,

(60)

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\parallel}(\xi)=

\displaystyle\;\Gamma^{0z}_{\parallel}(\xi)=f_{\parallel}(\xi)\,\Gamma(0)\,,\qquad\xi=\omega_{0}\,R\,.

(61)

At short range, the decay-rate admixtures expand as follows,

\displaystyle\frac{\Gamma_{\perp}(R)}{\Gamma(0)}=

\displaystyle\;1-\frac{(\omega_{0}R)^{2}}{5}+\mathcal{O}((\omega_{0}R)^{4})\,,

(62)

\displaystyle\frac{\Gamma_{\parallel}(R)}{\Gamma(0)}=

\displaystyle\;1-\frac{(\omega_{0}R)^{2}}{10}+\mathcal{O}((\omega_{0}R)^{4})\,,

(63)

The plot shows the
function

VII
Conclusions

We have considered retardation corrections to the
one-photon exchange between identical atoms.
In Sec.
II
, we have considered the
matching of the
S
matrix element and the
effective Hamiltonian.
Specifically, our calculation
has been based on the non-forward scattering matrix
element induced by an interaction potential
of the functional form
U(\vec{r}_{A},\vec{r}_{B},\vec{R})

which depends on the relative coordinates
\vec{r}_{A}

and
\vec{r}_{B}
of the two atoms,
and the interatomic distance
\vec{R}
.
For the matching to be successful, we need to
assume the initial and final states of the process
to have identical energy.
This is the case if, e.g., the initial state
is a combination of one of the atoms in the
ground state, and the other atom in an
excited
P
state. The final state has the
energies of the two states reversed,
with the possibility of different
degeneracy indices for the initial and final
states. Because the energies of the quantum states of the individual
states have been exchanged
in the initial and final states of the identical
atoms, the total energy of the
final state is equal to that of the initial state,
and the matching of the non-forward
S
matrix
element to the effective Hamiltonian can proceed.

This program is realized in Sec.
III
,
where the calculation is realized in the
temporal gauge for the virtual photon. In this gauge,
the timelike component of the photon
propagator vanishes, which implies that it is the ideal
gauge for the calculation of the retardation corrections
to the van der Waals potential given in Eq. (
1
).
The final result is given in Eq. (
III
).
The retarded potential has both a real and an
imaginary part. The imaginary part is suppressed
for the short-range interaction, as an
expansion of Eq. (
25b
) for
R\ll a_{0}/\alpha

immediately shows.
The interpretation of the imaginary part of the
resonant one-photon exchange energy is
discussed in Sec.
IV
. In the quantum-field
theoretical picture,
the imaginary part is connected with the
virtual resonant exchange photon becoming on shell,
and thus, being emitted as a real photon by the
entangled two-atom system.
It is found that a completely consistent
picture is obtained when one calculates
the Hamiltonian matrix including the
unperturbed resonance energies of the
ground and excited states, as well as the
one-photon resonant exchange energy and its
imaginary part. The modification of the
decay rate of the entangled Dicke state is consistently
obtained, and the result allows us to obtain
consistent formulas for the distance-dependent
decay rates of the superradiant and subradiant Dicke states.

A possibility for an independent verification
of the results based on Fermi’s Golden Rule is sketched
in Sec.
V
. An example calculation
involving hydrogen
1S
and
2P
states is given in
Sec.
VI
, culminating in the results
presented in Fig.
3
and
4
.
The modification of the decay rates has a
long-range,
1/R
tail. Our calculations show that the
imaginary part of the retarded one-photon exchange
as obtained by the Feynman prescription finds
a completely consistent physical interpretation in terms
of the distance-dependent modification of the
decay rate of entangled superradiant and subradiant Dicke states
of the identical two-atom system.

VIII
Appendix: Comparison with the Literature

In Chap. 7 of Ref.
Craig and Thirunamachandran 1984
, the authors
carry out a related calculation using time-ordered
perturbation theory.
Specifically, in Eq. (7.2.21) of Ref.
Craig and Thirunamachandran 1984
,
the authors obtain an expression for the retarded
van der Waals interaction which, in our notation,
reads as follows,

\displaystyle U^{\prime}(\vec{r}_{A},\vec{r}_{B},\vec{R})]=

\displaystyle\;\lim_{\gamma\to 0^{+}}\frac{1}{2\pi^{2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}{\mathrm{d}}p\,p^{4}\,\frac{\tau_{ij}(p\,R)}{\omega_{0}^{2}-p^{2}}\,{\mathrm{e}}^{-\gamma p}\,,

(80)

\displaystyle\tau_{ij}(p\,R)=

\displaystyle\;\alpha_{ij}\frac{\sin(p\,R)}{p\,R}+\beta_{ij}\left(\frac{\cos(p\,R)}{p^{2}\,R^{2}}-\frac{\sin(p\,R)}{p^{3}R^{3}}\right)\,.

(81)

For clarity, we observe
that
\omega_{0}
is denoted as
k
in Ref.
Craig and Thirunamachandran 1984
,
and that
\epsilon_{0}
is explicitly written
out in Ref.
Craig and Thirunamachandran 1984
.
In Chap. 7 of Ref.
Craig and Thirunamachandran 1984
, the authors evaluate
the integral over
p
as a principal-value integral,
using a convergent factor, and obtain the
real part of our result,

U^{\prime}(\vec{r}_{A},\vec{r}_{B},\vec{R})]={\rm Re}[U(\vec{r}_{A},\vec{r}_{B},\vec{R})]\,.

(82)

The Feynman prescription of quantum electrodynamics is
implemented by the substitution

\frac{\tau_{ij}(p\,R)}{\omega_{0}^{2}-p^{2}}\to\frac{\tau_{ij}(p\,R)}{\omega_{0}^{2}-p^{2}+{\mathrm{i}}\epsilon}=\frac{\tau_{ij}(p\,R)}{(\omega_{0}+{\mathrm{i}}\epsilon-p)\,(\omega_{0}+{\mathrm{i}}\epsilon+p)}

(83)

in Eq. (
80
). As a function of
p
, the integrand
then obtains poles in the complex
p
plane at

p=\pm\sqrt{\omega_{0}^{2}+{\mathrm{i}}\epsilon}
,
i.e., at
p=\omega_{0}+{\mathrm{i}}\epsilon
and at

p=-\omega_{0}-{\mathrm{i}}\epsilon
,
where
\epsilon
denotes an infinitesimal
positive imaginary part.
In order to consider the contribution from the
pole, one symmetrizes the integral on the
domain
-\infty<p<\infty
, and inserts the
infinitesimal imaginary part,
leading to

U^{\prime\prime}(\vec{r}_{A},\vec{r}_{B},\vec{R})]=\lim_{\epsilon\to 0^{+}}\lim_{\gamma\to 0^{+}}\frac{1}{4\pi^{2}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}{\mathrm{d}}p\,p^{4}\,\frac{\tau_{ij}(p\,R)}{\omega_{0}^{2}-p^{2}+{\mathrm{i}}\epsilon}\,{\mathrm{e}}^{-\gamma|p|}\,.

(84)

The real part of

U^{\prime\prime}(\vec{r}_{A},\vec{r}_{B},\vec{R})
can be evaluated
by principal value,
confirming the result given in Eq. (
82
).
The imaginary part is obtained by considering the
pole at
p=\omega_{0}+{\mathrm{i}}\epsilon
which is
encircled in the mathematically positive sense,

{\mathrm{i}}\,{\rm Im}U^{\prime\prime}(\vec{r}_{A},\vec{r}_{B},\vec{R})]=2\pi{\mathrm{i}}\,\mathop{\rm Res}_{p=\omega_{0}}\frac{1}{4\pi^{2}}p^{4}\,\frac{\tau_{ij}(p\,R)}{\omega_{0}^{2}-p^{2}}=-\frac{{\mathrm{i}}}{4\pi}\,\omega_{0}^{3}\,\tau_{ij}(\omega_{0}\,R)={\mathrm{i}}\,{\rm Im}U(\vec{r}_{A},\vec{r}_{B},\vec{R})]\,,

(85)

where the latter equality is obvious by inspection
of Eqs. (
81
), (
84
) and (
25b
).
We conclude that,
if the infinitesimal imaginary part of the photon
is supplemented in the treatment outlined in
Chap. 7 of Ref.
Craig and Thirunamachandran 1984
, then

the result for the
interaction potential
U
given in Eq. (
III
)
can be obtained,

U^{\prime\prime}(\vec{r}_{A},\vec{r}_{B},\vec{R})=U(\vec{r}_{A},\vec{r}_{B},\vec{R})\,.

(86)

We should also briefly discuss certain approximations
underlying our treatment and that discussed in
Chap. 7 of Ref.
Craig and Thirunamachandran 1984
.
First, we note the Born–Oppenheimer approximation,
which allows us to consider the motion of the
electrons and nuclei separately and
corresponds to the limit of infinitely heavy atomic
nuclei (vanishing electron-nucleon mass ratio).
In the same light, we neglect the recoil energy
upon photon emission, and we assume that the
wave functions of the two atoms do not overlap.
Higher-order multipoles as well as multi-photon
transitions are also neglected.
The same approximations underlie the treatments
given in Refs.
Varfolomeev 1970
;
Varfolomeev 1972
for
systems consisting of unlike atoms with close
excitation energies.
