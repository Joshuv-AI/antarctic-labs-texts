Data-driven discovery of non-Newtonian astronomy via learning non-Euclidean Hamiltonian
Oswin So, Gongjie Li, Evangelos A. Theodorou, Molei Tao

Abstract
Incorporating the Hamiltonian structure of physical dynamics into deep learning models provides a powerful way to improve the interpretability and prediction accuracy. While previous works are mostly limited to the Euclidean spaces, their extension to the Lie group manifold is needed when rotations form a key component of the dynamics, such as the higher-order physics beyond simple point-mass dynamics for N N -body celestial interactions. Moreover, the multiscale nature of these processes presents a challenge to existing methods as a long time horizon is required. By leveraging a symplectic Lie-group manifold preserving integrator, we present a method for data-driven discovery of non-Newtonian astronomy. Preliminary results show the importance of both these properties in training stability and prediction accuracy.

2
Method

Given observations of a dynamically evolving system, our goal is to learn the
physics
that governs its evolution from the data.
Denote by
(\mathbf{q}_{k,l},\mathbf{R}_{k,l},\mathbf{p}_{k,l},\mathbf{\Pi}_{k,l})_{k=1}^{K},\;l\in[L]
a dataset of snapshots of
L
continuous-time trajectories of a system with
N
interacting rigid bodies. That is,

(\mathbf{q}_{k,l},\mathbf{R}_{k,l},\mathbf{p}_{k,l},\mathbf{\Pi}_{k,l})=\big(\mathbf{q}_{l}(k\Delta t),\mathbf{R}_{l}(k\Delta t),\mathbf{p}_{l}(k\Delta t),\mathbf{\Pi}_{l}(k\Delta t)\big),

where
\mathbf{q}_{l}(t),\mathbf{R}_{l}(t),\mathbf{p}_{l}(t),\mathbf{\Pi}_{l}(t)
is a solution of some latent Hamiltonian ODE to be learned corresponding to mechanical dynamics on
T^{*}\mathsf{SE(3)}^{\otimes N}
.
\Delta t
is a (possibly large) observation timestep,
\mathbf{R}\in\mathsf{SO}(3)^{\otimes N}
is the rotational configuration of the
N
rigid bodies, and
\mathbf{\Pi}\in\mathfrak{so}(3)^{\otimes N}
denotes each’s angular momentum in their respective body frames.

Importantly, since the configuration space
\mathcal{Q}=\mathsf{SE(3)}^{\otimes N}
is not flat, the mechanical dynamics are not given by
\dot{\mathcalboondox{q}}=\partialderivative{\Ham}{\genp},\dot{\mathcalboondox{p}}=-\partialderivative{\Ham}{\genq}
for some Hamiltonian
\mathcal{H}
that depends on the generalized coordinates
\mathcalboondox{q}\in\mathcal{Q}
and generalized momentum
\mathcalboondox{p}\in T^{*}{q}\mathcal{Q}
. Instead, the equations of motion can be derived via either Lagrange multipliers
[
20
,
26
]
or a Lie group variational principle
[
20
,
27
]
, which will be

assuming a physical Hamiltonian
\mathcal{H}(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{R},\mathbf{p},\mathbf{\Pi})=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{p}_{i}^{T}\mathbf{p}_{i}/m_{i}+\sum_{i}\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{\Pi}^{T}_{i}\mathbf{J}_{i}^{-1}\mathbf{\Pi}_{i}+V(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{R})
that sums total (translation and rotational) kinetic energy and interaction potential
V
, where
m_{i},\mathbf{J}_{i}
denote the mass and inertial tensor of the
i
th body, and
F_{\mathbf{p}},F_{\mathbf{\Pi}}
are forcing terms to model nonconervative forces.

\mathbf{\Pi}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
is a vector,
\wedge
is the map from
\mathbb{R}^{3}
to
\textrm{Skew}_{3}
and
\vee
is its inverse (
[
20
]
for more details).
By learning the potential
V
, external forcing
F_{\mathbf{p}}
and torque
F_{\mathbf{\Pi}}
, we can learn the physics of the system.

2.1
Machine Learning Challenges Posed by Dynamical Astronomy

We study this setup because it helps answer scientific questions like: what physics governs the motions of celestial bodies, such as planets in a planetary system? The leading order physics is of course already well known, namely these bodies can be approximated by point masses that are interacting through a
1/r
gravitational potential. However, planets are not point masses, and their rotations matter because they shape planetary climates
[
28
,
29
]
and even feedback to their orbits
[
30
]
. This already starts to alter
V
even if one only considers classical gravity. For example, the gravitational potential
V
for interacting bodies of finite sizes should be

V(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{R})=\sum_{i<j}V_{i,j}
, where

V_{i,j}(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{R})=\int_{\mathcal{B}_{i}}\int_{\mathcal{B}_{j}}-\frac{\mathcal{G}\rho(\mathbf{x}_{i})\rho(\mathbf{x}_{j})}{\norm{\vb{q}_i + \vb{R}_i \vb{x}_i - \vb{q}_j - \vb{R}_j \vb{x}_j}}\differential{\vb{x}_i}\differential{\vb{x}_j}\;\;\;=\;\;\;\underbrace{-\frac{\mathcal{G}m_{i}m_{j}}{\norm{\vb{q}_i - \vb{q}_j}}}_{V_{i,j,\textrm{point}}}+\underbrace{O\left(\frac{1}{\norm{q_i - q_j}^{2}}\right)}_{V_{i,j,\textrm{resid}}}.

(2)

Working with the full potential is complicated since

\mathcal{B}_{i}
is not known and the integral is not analytically known.
Can we directly learn
V_{\textrm{resid}}
from time-series data?

Classical gravity (i.e. Newtonian physics) is not the only driver of planetary motion — tidal forces and general relativity (GR) matter too. The former provides a dissipation mechanism and
plays critical roles in altering planetary orbits
[
31
,
32
]
; the latter doesn’t need much explanation and has been demonstrated by, e.g., Mercury’s precessing orbit
[
33
]
. Tidal forces depend on celestial bodies’ rotations
[
34
]
and thus is a function of both
\mathbf{q},\mathbf{R}
.
GR’s effects cannot be fully characterized with classical coordinates
\mathbf{q},\mathbf{R},\mathbf{p},\mathbf{\Pi}
,
but post-Newtonian approximations based purely on these coordinates are popular
[
35
]
.
Can we learn both purely from data if we did not have theories for either?

In addition to the scientific questions, there are also significant machine learning challenges:

Multiscale dynamics.
Rigid-body correction (
V_{\textrm{resid}}
), tidal force, and
GR
correction are all much smaller forces compared to point-mass gravity. Consequently, their effects do not manifest until long time. Thus, one challenge for learning them is that the dynamical system exhibits different behaviors over
multiple timescales
. It is reasonable to require long time series data for the small effects to be learned; meanwhile, when observations are expensive to make, the observation time step
\Delta t
can be much longer than the smallest timescales. Can we still learn the physics in this case? We will leverage symplectic integrator and its mild growth of error over long time
[
26
,
7
]
to provide a positive answer.

Respecting the Lie group manifold.

However, even having a symplectic integrator is not enough because the position variable of the latent dynamics (i.e. truth) stays on
\mathsf{SE(3)}^{\otimes N}
. If the integrated solution falls off this manifold such that
\mathbf{R}^{\intercal}\mathbf{R}=I
no longer holds, it is not only incorrect but likely misleading for the learning of
V(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{R})
. Popular integrators
such as forward Euler, Runge-Kutta 4 (RK4) and Leapfrog
[
1
,
6
,
36
]
unfortunately do no maintain the manifold structure.

2.2
Learning with Lie Symplectic RNNs

Our method can be viewed as a Lie-group generalization of the seminal work of SRNN
[
6
]
,
where a good integrator that is both
symplectic
and
Lie-group preserving
is employed as a recurrent block.

Lie
\mathrm{T}_{2}
: A Symplectic Lie-Group Preserving Integrator.

To construct an integrator that achieves both properties, we borrow from
[
20
]
the idea of Lie-group and symplecticity preserving splitting, and split our Hamiltonian as

\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{H}_{\textrm{KE}}+\mathcal{H}_{\textrm{PE}}+\mathcal{H}_{\textrm{asym}}
, which contains the axial-symmetric kinetic energy, potential energy and asymmetric kinetic energy correction terms.
This
enables computing the
exact
integrators
\phi_{t}^{[\textrm{KE}]},\phi_{t}^{[\textrm{PE}]}
and
\phi_{t}^{[\textrm{asym}]}
(see
Appendix

B
for details).
We then construct a 2nd-order symplectic integrator Lie
\mathrm{T}_{2}
by applying the Strang composition scheme. To account for non-conservative forces, the corresponding non-conservative momentum update
\phi^{[\textrm{force}]}:(\mathbf{p},\mathbf{\Pi})\leftarrow F(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{R},\mathbf{p},\mathbf{\Pi})
is inserted in the middle of the composition
[
20
]
. This gives
\phi_{h}^{[\textrm{Lie $\mathrm{T}_{2}$}]}
for stepsize
h
as

\phi_{h}^{\textrm{Lie $\mathrm{T}_{2}$}}\coloneqq\phi_{h/2}^{[\textrm{KE}]}\,\circ\,\phi_{h/2}^{[\textrm{PE}]}\,\circ\,\phi_{h/2}^{[\textrm{asym}]}\,\circ\,\phi_{h}^{[\textrm{force}]}\,\circ\,\phi_{h/2}^{[\textrm{asym}]}\,\circ\phi_{h/2}^{[\textrm{PE}]}\,\circ\,\phi_{h/2}^{[\textrm{KE}]}

(3)

\mathrm{T}_{2}

A Recurrent Architecture for Nonlinear Regression.

Given the simplicity of
V_{\textrm{point}}
, we assume this is known and learn
V_{\textrm{resid}}
and
F^{\theta}

with multi-layer perceptron (MLP)
V^{\theta}_{\textrm{resid}}
and
F^{\theta}
without assuming any pairwise structure (see
Appendix

C
for discussion). We then use

\phi^{\theta,\textrm{Lie $\mathrm{T}_{2}$}}
to integrate dynamics forward, where
\theta
denotes the dependence on the networks.
However, when the temporal spacing between observations
\Delta t
is large, using a single
\phi_{\Delta t}^{\theta,\textrm{Lie $\mathrm{T}_{2}$}{}}
will result in large errors for the fast timescale dynamics.
Instead, we compose
\phi_{h}^{\theta,\textrm{Lie $\mathrm{T}_{2}$}{}}

H
times as
(\hat{\mathcalboondox{q}}_{k+1,l},\hat{\mathcalboondox{p}}_{k+1,l})=\phi_{h}^{\theta,\textrm{Lie $\mathrm{T}_{2}$}}\circ\dots\circ\phi_{h}^{\theta,\textrm{Lie $\mathrm{T}_{2}$}}(\mathcalboondox{q}_{k,l},\mathcalboondox{p}_{k,l})
, where
H=h/\Delta t\in\mathbb{Z}
determines the integration stepsize
h
.
We perform training by minimizing the following empirical loss over random minibatches of size
N_{b}

\mathcal{L}(\theta)\coloneqq\frac{1}{N_{b}\,K}\sum_{l=1}^{N_{b}}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\Big\{\norm{\genq_{k, l} - \hat{\genq}_{k, l}^\theta}^{2}_{2}+\norm{\genp_{k,l} - \hat{\genp}_{k, l}^\theta}^{2}_{2}\Big\}

(4)

Note that we do not assume access to the true derivatives
\dot{\mathcalboondox{q}}_{k,j}
and
\dot{\mathcalboondox{p}}_{k,j}
used in the loss function of some works
[
1
,
37
,
38
]
. Our training process in summarized in
Fig.

2
(see
Appendix

C
for details).

Benefit.

Learning an accurate
V^{\theta},F^{\theta}
requires accurate numerical simulation which also leads to a trainable model.
Without preservation of the manifold structure, training can lead to ‘shortcuts’ outside the manifold that seemingly match the data but completely mislead the learning.
Symplecticity also plays a vital role in controlling the long time integration error —
under reasonable conditions, a
p
th-order symplectic integrator has linear
\mathcal{O}(\Delta th^{p})
error bound, whereas a
p
th-order nonsymplectic one
has an exponential

\mathcal{O}(e^{C\Delta t}h^{p})
error bound
[
26
,
7
]
.
While these bounds do not matter for small
\Delta t
, they are significant for multiscale problems where
\Delta t
is macroscopic but
h
is microscopic.
Consequently, improving error estimates for a nonsymplectic integrator by reducing
h
makes the RNN exponentially deep — this often renders training difficult
[
39
]
and is not desirable.

4
Broader Impact

Our work presents an approach for learning multiscale, higher order physics on the Lie-group manifold in the context of non-Newtonian astronomy. This research, though directly applicable to astronomy, can also be applied to perform data-driven discovery multiscale phenomena on Lie groups in other fields.

Appendix A
Rigid Body Equations of Motion

We present two derivations from
Chen et al. 2021
. The first derivation derives a constrained Hamiltonian system via Lagrange multipliers, while the second uses a variational principle for mechanics on Lie groups. We also note that the same equations can be derived via the use of the Port-Hamiltonian framework as in
Duong and Atanasov 2021
, though they choose to express the linear momentum coordinates in the moving body frame rather than the inertial frame as is done in this work.

A.1
Constrained Hamiltonian System

Since the manifold
\mathsf{SE}(3)^{\otimes N}
is a product space of the individual manifolds
\mathsf{SE}(3)
, we consider the latter for brevity and drop indices
i
for each body. Furthermore,
\mathsf{SE}(3)
can be seen as the product space of
\mathbb{R}^{3}\times\mathsf{SO}(3)
for which the former is unconstrained, so we focus our attention on the latter.
We can view
\mathbf{R}
to be in the embedded Euclidean space
\mathbf{R}^{3\times 3}\xhookleftarrow{}\mathsf{SO}(3)
and use
\mathbf{R}\in\mathsf{SO}(3)\coloneqq h(\mathbf{R})=\{\mathbf{R}^{\intercal}\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{I}_{3\times 3}=\mathbf{0}_{3\times 3}\}
as a holonomic constraint. Using Lagrange multipliers
\mathbf{\Lambda}

[
26
]
for the constraint
h
gives us the following Lagrangian

L(\mathbf{R},\dot{\mathbf{R}})=\frac{1}{2}\Tr\left[\dot{\mathbf{R}}\mathbf{J}_{d}{}\dot{\mathbf{R}}\right]-V(\mathbf{R})-\frac{1}{2}\Tr\left[\mathbf{\Lambda}^{\intercal}(\mathbf{R}^{\intercal}\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{I}_{3\times 3})\right]

(A5)

where
\mathbf{J}_{d}{}
denotes the nonstandard moment of inertia
[
20
,
27
]

\mathbf{J}_{d}\coloneqq\int_{\mathcal{B}}\rho(\mathbf{x})\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}^{\intercal}\differential{\vb{x}},\qquad\mathbf{J}_{d}=\Tr[\mathbf{J}]\mathbf{I}_{3\times 3}-\mathbf{J},\qquad\mathbf{J}=\Tr[\mathbf{J}_{d}]\mathbf{I}_{3\times 3}-\mathbf{J}_{d},

(A6)

and

\mathbf{\Lambda}=\begin{bmatrix}\lambda_{1}&\lambda_{4}&\lambda_{6}\\
\lambda_{4}&\lambda_{2}&\lambda_{5}\\
\lambda_{6}&\lambda_{5}&\lambda_{3}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}

(A7)

is a 6-dimensional symmetric matrix of Lagrange multipliers. Performing the Legendre transform for (
A5
) gives us the conjugate rotational momentum
\mathbf{P}
as

\mathbf{P}=\partialderivative{L(\vR, \dot{\vR})}{\dot{\vR}}=\dot{\mathbf{R}}\mathbf{J}_{d}

(A8)

and corresponding Hamiltonian

H(\mathbf{R},\mathbf{P})=\frac{1}{2}\Tr\left[\mathbf{P}{}\mathbf{J}_{d}{}^{-1}\mathbf{P}{}\right]+V(\mathbf{R})+\frac{1}{2}\Tr\left[\mathbf{\Lambda}^{\intercal}(\mathbf{R}^{\intercal}\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{I}_{3\times 3})\right]

(A9)

The constraint for
\mathbf{P}
can be obtained by taking the time derivative of
h(\mathbf{R})=0
according to
Haier et al. 2006
, i.e.,
\mathbf{J}_{d}^{-1}\mathbf{P}^{\intercal}\mathbf{R}+\mathbf{R}^{\intercal}\mathbf{P}\mathbf{J}_{d}^{1}=\mathbf{0}_{3\times 3}
. Hence, our equations of motion so far looks like

\begin{cases}\dot{\mathbf{R}}=\partialderivative{H}{\vP}=\mathbf{P}\mathbf{J}_{d}^{-1}\\
\dot{\mathbf{P}}=-\partialderivative{H}{\vR}=-\partialderivative{V}{\vR}-\mathbf{R}\mathbf{\Lambda}\end{cases}

(A10)

on the manifold

\mathcal{M}\coloneqq\{(\mathbf{R},\mathbf{P})|\mathbf{R}^{\intercal}\mathbf{R}=\mathbf{I}_{3\times 3},\quad\mathbf{J}_{d}^{-1}\mathbf{P}^{\intercal}\mathbf{R}+\mathbf{R}^{\intercal}\mathbf{P}\mathbf{J}_{d}^{-1}=\mathbf{0}_{3\times 3}

(A11)

Let
\hat{\mathbf{\Omega}}=\mathbf{R}^{\intercal}\dot{\mathbf{R}}
denote the body’s angular velocity in the inertial frame, where
\wedge
denotes the cross-product operation
\hat{u}v=u\times v
. Then, it can written using
P
as

\hat{\mathbf{\Omega}}=\mathbf{R}^{\intercal}\mathbf{P}\mathbf{J}_{d}^{-1}

(A12)

Taking the time derivative of
\hat{\mathbf{\Omega}}
gives

\dot{\hat{\mathbf{\Omega}}}=\mathbf{J}_{d}^{-1}\mathbf{P}^{\intercal}\mathbf{P}\mathbf{J}_{d}^{-1}+\mathbf{R}^{\intercal}\left(-\partialderivative{V(\vR)}{\vR}-\mathbf{R}\mathbf{\Lambda}\right)\mathbf{J}_{d}^{-1}

(A13)

Since our goal is to represent the dynamics of
\mathbf{\Pi}
, we convert the above to the body frame momentum using
\mathbf{\Pi}=\mathbf{J}\mathbf{\Omega}
. Using properties of the hat map
[
20
, Appendix A.1]
gives us

\hat{\mathbf{\Pi}}=\hat{\mathbf{J}\mathbf{\Omega}}=\Tr[\mathbf{J}_{d}]\hat{\mathbf{\Omega}}-\widehat{\mathbf{J}_{d}\mathbf{\Omega}}=\hat{\mathbf{\Omega}}\mathbf{J}_{d}{}-\mathbf{J}_{d}{}\hat{\mathbf{\Omega}}^{\intercal}.

(A14)

Taking the time derivative of the above then gives

\displaystyle\dot{\hat{\mathbf{\Pi}}}

\displaystyle=\dot{\hat{\mathbf{\Omega}}}\mathbf{J}_{d}{}-\mathbf{J}_{d}{}\dot{\hat{\mathbf{\Omega}}},

(A15)

\displaystyle=\left(\mathbf{J}_{d}^{-1}\mathbf{P}^{\intercal}\mathbf{P}-\mathbf{P}^{\intercal}\mathbf{P}\mathbf{J}_{d}^{-1}\right)-\left(\mathbf{R}^{\intercal}\partialderivative{V(\vR)}{\vR}+\left(\partialderivative{V(\vR)}{\vR}\right)^{\intercal}\mathbf{R}\right)-\left(\mathbf{\Lambda}-\mathbf{\Lambda}^{\intercal}\right),

(A16)

\displaystyle=\left(\mathbf{J}_{d}^{-1}\mathbf{P}^{\intercal}\mathbf{P}-\mathbf{P}^{\intercal}\mathbf{P}\mathbf{J}_{d}^{-1}\right)-\left(\mathbf{R}^{\intercal}\partialderivative{V(\vR)}{\vR}+\left(\partialderivative{V(\vR)}{\vR}\right)^{\intercal}\mathbf{R}\right),

(A17)

where the symmetric
\mathbf{\Lambda}
vanishes on the last line.

Since
\mathbf{P}=\mathbf{R}\hat{\mathbf{\Omega}}\mathbf{J}_{d}{}
, applying properties of the hat map
[
20
, Appendix A.1]
further simplifies the above as

\displaystyle\hat{\dot{\mathbf{\Pi}}}

\displaystyle=\left(\hat{\mathbf{\Omega}}^{\intercal}\hat{\mathbf{\Omega}}\mathbf{J}_{d}{}-\mathbf{J}_{d}{}\hat{\mathbf{\Omega}}^{\intercal}\hat{\mathbf{\Omega}}\right)-\left(\mathbf{R}^{\intercal}\partialderivative{V(\vR)}{\vR}+\left(\partialderivative{V(\vR)}{\vR}\right)^{\intercal}\mathbf{R}\right),

(A18)

\displaystyle=\widehat{\mathbf{\Omega}\times\mathbf{J}_{d}{}\mathbf{\Omega}}-\left(\mathbf{R}^{\intercal}\partialderivative{V(\vR)}{\vR}+\left(\partialderivative{V(\vR)}{\vR}\right)^{\intercal}\mathbf{R}\right).

(A19)

Thus, applying the vee map and converting back to
\mathbf{J}
and
\mathbf{\Pi}
gives

\displaystyle\dot{\mathbf{\Pi}}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{\Omega}\times\mathbf{J}_{d}{}\mathbf{\Omega}-\left(\mathbf{R}^{\intercal}\partialderivative{V(\vR)}{\vR}+\left(\partialderivative{V(\vR)}{\vR}\right)^{\intercal}\mathbf{R}\right)^{\vee},

(A20)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{\Omega}\times()\Tr[\mathbf{J}]-\mathbf{J})\mathbf{\Omega}-\left(\mathbf{R}^{\intercal}\partialderivative{V(\vR)}{\vR}+\left(\partialderivative{V(\vR)}{\vR}\right)^{\intercal}\mathbf{R}\right)^{\vee},

(A21)

\displaystyle=-\mathbf{\Omega}\times\mathbf{J}\mathbf{\Omega}-\left(\mathbf{R}^{\intercal}\partialderivative{V(\vR)}{\vR}+\left(\partialderivative{V(\vR)}{\vR}\right)^{\intercal}\mathbf{R}\right)^{\vee},

(A22)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{\Pi}\times\mathbf{J}^{-1}\mathbf{\Pi}-\left(\mathbf{R}^{\intercal}\partialderivative{V(\vR)}{\vR}+\left(\partialderivative{V(\vR)}{\vR}\right)^{\intercal}\mathbf{R}\right)^{\vee}.

(A23)

Hence, the rotational equations of motion on
\mathsf{SO}(3)
are

\begin{cases}\dot{\mathbf{R}}=\mathbf{R}\widehat{\mathbf{J}^{1}\mathbf{\Pi}},\\
\dot{\mathbf{\Pi}}=\mathbf{\Pi}\times\mathbf{J}^{-1}\mathbf{\Pi}-\left(\mathbf{R}^{\intercal}\partialderivative{V(\vR)}{\vR}-\left(\partialderivative{V(\vR)}{\vR}\right)^{\intercal}\mathbf{R}\right)^{\vee}.\end{cases}

(A24)

A.2
Variational Principle for Mechanics on the Lie Group

We can also apply the Euler-Lagrange equations for Hamilton’s variational principle on a Lie group, a topic that has been well studied (e.g.,
Marsden and Ratiu 2013
,
Holm et al. 2009
)
[
20
]
, and we summarize the results for the special case of rigid bodies from the expository part of
Lee et al.
.

Denote the infinitesimally varied rotation by
\mathbf{R}_{\epsilon}=\mathbf{R}\exp(\epsilon\hat{\veta})
, with
\epsilon\in\mathbb{R}
and
\mathbf{\eta}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
, where
\exp(\cdot)
is the exponential map from
\mathfrak{so}(3)
to
\mathsf{SO}(3)
. The varied angular velocity
\hat{\mathbf{\Omega}}_{\epsilon}
is

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{\Omega}}_{\epsilon}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{R}_{\epsilon}^{\intercal}\dot{\mathbf{R}}_{\epsilon}=\exp(-\epsilon\hat{\veta})\left(\dot{\mathbf{R}}\exp(\epsilon\hat{\veta})+\mathbf{R}\exp(\epsilon\hat{\veta})\epsilon\hat{\mathbf{\eta}}\right),

(A25)

\displaystyle=\exp(-\epsilon\hat{\veta})\hat{\mathbf{\Omega}}\exp(-\epsilon\hat{\veta})+\exp(-\epsilon\hat{\veta})\hat{\dot{\mathbf{\eta}}},

(A26)

\displaystyle=\hat{\mathbf{\Omega}}+\epsilon\left(\hat{\dot{\mathbf{\eta}}}+\hat{\mathbf{\Omega}}\hat{\mathbf{\eta}}-\hat{\mathbf{\eta}}\hat{\mathbf{\Omega}}\right)+O(\epsilon^{2}).

(A27)

Consider the action

S(\mathbf{\Omega},\mathbf{R})=\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}L(\mathbf{\Omega},\mathbf{R})\differential{t}=\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}\frac{1}{2}\Tr[\hat{\mathbf{\Omega}}\mathbf{J}_{d}{}\hat{\mathbf{\Omega}}]-V(\mathbf{R})\differential{t}.

(A28)

Taking the variation
S_{\epsilon}
of the action
S
, we have

\begin{split}S_{\epsilon}(\mathbf{\Omega},\mathbf{R})&=S(\mathbf{\Omega}_{\epsilon},\mathbf{R}_{\epsilon}),\\
&=S(\mathbf{\Omega},\mathbf{R})+\epsilon\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}\bigg\{\frac{1}{2}\Tr[-\hat{\dot{\mathbf{\eta}}}\left(\mathbf{J}_{d}{}\hat{\mathbf{\Omega}}+\hat{\mathbf{\Omega}}\mathbf{J}_{d}{}\right)\\
\hfil&\quad+\hat{\mathbf{\eta}}\hat{\mathbf{\Omega}}\left(\mathbf{J}_{d}{}\hat{\mathbf{\Omega}}+\hat{\mathbf{\Omega}}\mathbf{J}_{d}{}\right)-\hat{\mathbf{\eta}}\left(\mathbf{J}_{d}{}\hat{\mathbf{\Omega}}+\hat{\mathbf{\Omega}}\mathbf{J}_{d}{}\hat{\mathbf{\Omega}}\right)\Big]+\Tr[\hat{\mathbf{\eta}}\mathbf{R}^{\intercal}\partialderivative{V(\vR)}{\vR}]\bigg\}\differential{t}\\
&\quad+O(\epsilon^{2}).\end{split}

(A29)

Using
Hamilton’s Principle
, we have
\evaluated{\dv{\epsilon}}_{\epsilon=0}S_{\epsilon}=0
, i.e.,

\frac{1}{2}\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}\Tr\left[\hat{\mathbf{\eta}}\left\{\widehat{\mathbf{J}\dot{\mathbf{\Omega}}}+\widehat{\mathbf{\Omega}\times\mathbf{J}\mathbf{\Omega}}+2\mathbf{R}^{\intercal}\partialderivative{V(\vR)}{\vR}\right\}\right]\differential{t}=0,

(A30)

for any
\mathbf{\eta}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
. Hence,
\widehat{\mathbf{J}\dot{\mathbf{\Omega}}}+\widehat{\mathbf{\Omega}\times\mathbf{J}\mathbf{\Omega}}+2\mathbf{R}^{\intercal}\partialderivative{V(\vR)}{\vR}
must be skew-symmetric, giving us

\widehat{\mathbf{J}\dot{\mathbf{\Omega}}}=-\widehat{\mathbf{\Omega}\times\mathbf{J}\mathbf{\Omega}}+\left(\partialderivative{V(\vR)}{\vR}^{\intercal}\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{R}^{\intercal}\partialderivative{V(\vR)}{\vR}\right).

(A31)

Thus, by definition of
\mathbf{\Pi}
and applying the vee map, we recover the same update

\dot{\mathbf{\Pi}}=\mathbf{\Pi}\times\mathbf{J}^{-1}\mathbf{\Pi}+\left(\partialderivative{V(\vR)}{\vR}^{\intercal}\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{R}^{\intercal}\partialderivative{V(\vR)}{\vR}\right)^{\vee}.

(A32)

Appendix B
Details of the Lie
\mathrm{T}_{2}
splitting integrator

The full Hamiltonian
H
takes the form

\mathcal{H}(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{R},\mathbf{p},\mathbf{\Pi})=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{p}_{i}^{\intercal}\mathbf{p}_{i}/m_{i}+\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{\Pi}_{i}^{\intercal}\mathbf{J}_{i}^{-1}\mathbf{\Pi}_{i}+V(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{R})

(B33)

We borrow from
Chen et al. 2021
the idea of Lie-group and symplecticity preserving splitting and split our Hamiltonian as

\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{H}_{\textrm{KE}}+\mathcal{H}_{\textrm{PE}}+\mathcal{H}_{\textrm{asym}}
, where

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{\textrm{KE}}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{p}_{i}^{\intercal}\mathbf{p}_{i}/m_{i}+\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{\Pi}_{i}^{\intercal}\mathbf{J}_{i,\text{sym}}^{-1}\mathbf{\Pi}_{i}

(B34)

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{\textrm{PE}}

\displaystyle\coloneqq V(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{R})

(B35)

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{\textrm{asym}}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{\Pi}_{i}^{\intercal}\mathbf{J}_{i,\textrm{asym}}^{-1}\mathbf{\Pi}_{i}

(B36)

where we assume that
\mathbf{J}_{i}
is axis-symmetric, i.e.,

\mathbf{J}_{i}\coloneqq\begin{bmatrix}J_{i}^{(1)}&0&0\\
0&J_{i}^{(2)}&0\\
0&0&J_{i}^{(3)}\end{bmatrix}

(B37)

and
\mathbf{J}_{i,\text{sym}}^{-1}
and
\mathbf{J}_{i,\textrm{asym}}^{-1}
denote the axial-symmetric and residual terms of
\mathbf{J}_{i}^{-1}
such that
\mathbf{J}_{i,\text{sym}}^{-1}+\mathbf{J}_{i,\textrm{asym}}^{-1}=\mathbf{J}_{i}^{-1}
, i.e.,

\mathbf{J}_{i,\text{sym}}^{-1}\coloneqq\begin{bmatrix}1/J_{i}^{(1)}&0&0\\
0&1/J_{i}^{(1)}&0\\
0&0&1/J_{i}^{(3)}\end{bmatrix},\qquad\mathbf{J}_{i,\text{asym}}^{-1}\coloneqq\begin{bmatrix}0&0&0\\
0&1/J_{i}^{(2)}-1/J_{i}^{(1)}&0\\
0&0&0\end{bmatrix}.

(B38)

Then, each of
\mathcal{H}_{\textrm{KE}}+\mathcal{H}_{\textrm{PE}}+\mathcal{H}_{\textrm{asym}}
can be integrated exactly.

Exact integration of
\bm{\mathcal{H}_{\textrm{KE}}}

For
\mathcal{H}_{\textrm{KE}}
, we have the equations of motion (using (
1a
)-(
1d
) but with
V=0
)

\displaystyle\dot{\mathbf{q}}_{i}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{p}_{i}/m_{i}

(B39a)

\displaystyle\dot{\mathbf{p}}_{i}

\displaystyle=0

(B39b)

\displaystyle\dot{\mathbf{R}}_{i}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{R}{}_{i}\widehat{\mathbf{J}_{i,\text{sym}}^{-1}\mathbf{\Pi}_{i}}

(B39c)

\displaystyle\dot{\mathbf{\Pi}}_{i}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{\Pi}{}_{i}\times\mathbf{J}_{i,\text{sym}}^{-1}\mathbf{\Pi}_{i}

(B39d)

The equation for
\dot{\mathbf{\Pi}}_{i}
(
B39d
) is the Euler equation for a free rigid body
[
20
]
. It is exactly solvable with a simple expression for axial-symmetric bodies, since in this case
\dot{\mathbf{\Pi}}
simplifies as

\dot{\mathbf{\Pi}}_{i}=\mathbf{\Pi}_{i}\times\mathbf{J}_{i,\textrm{sym}}^{-1}\mathbf{\Pi}_{i}=\begin{bmatrix}\left(1/J_{i}^{(3)}-1/J_{i}^{(1)}\right)\Pi_{i,y}\Pi_{i,z}\\
-\left(1/J_{i}^{(3)}-1/J_{i}^{(1)}\right)\Pi_{i,y}\Pi_{i,z}\\
0\end{bmatrix}

(B40)

Consequently,
\Pi_{i,z}(t)=\Pi_{i,z}(0)
, meaning that we can express the above as the linear differential equation

\displaystyle\dot{\mathbf{\Pi}}_{i}(t)

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}0&\left(1/J_{i}^{(3)}-1/J_{i}^{(1)}\right)\Pi_{i,z}(0)&0\\
-\left(1/J_{i}^{(3)}-1/J_{i}^{(1)}\right)\Pi_{i,z}(0)&0&0\\
0&0&0\end{bmatrix}\mathbf{\Pi}_{i}(0)

(B41)

\displaystyle=-\theta t\widehat{\begin{bmatrix}0\\
0\\
1\end{bmatrix}}\mathbf{\Pi}_{i}(0)

(B42)

where
\theta\coloneqq\left(1/J_{i}^{(3)}-1/J_{i}^{(1)}\right)\Pi_{i,z}(0)
and has the solution

\mathbf{\Pi}_{i}(t)=\exp\left(-\theta t\widehat{\begin{bmatrix}0\\
0\\
1\end{bmatrix}}\right)\mathbf{\Pi}_{i}(0)=R_{z}^{\intercal}(\theta t)\mathbf{\Pi}_{i}(0)

(B43)

where
R_{z}
denotes the rotation matrix around the
z
axis. Taking the above back to (
B39c
) then gives us the solution for
\mathbf{R}_{i}
as well, giving the flow
\phi_{h}^{[\textrm{KE}]}
of
\mathcal{H}_{\textrm{KE}}
as

\begin{cases}\mathbf{q}_{i}(h)=\mathbf{q}_{i}(0)+\mathbf{p}_{i}/m_{i}h,\\
\mathbf{p}_{i}(h)=\mathbf{p}_{i}(0),\\
\mathbf{R}_{i}(h)=\mathbf{R}_{i}(0)R_{\mathbf{\Pi}_{i}(0)}\left(\norm{\vPi_i(0)}h/J_{i}^{(1)}\right)R_{z}(\theta h),\\
\mathbf{\Pi}_{i}(h)=R^{\intercal}_{z}(\theta h)\mathbf{\Pi}_{i}(0).\end{cases}

(B44)

Exact integration of
\bm{\mathcal{H}_{\textrm{PE}}}

For
\mathcal{H}_{\textrm{PE}}
, the equations of motion are
[
20
]

\begin{cases}\dot{\mathbf{q}}_{i}=0,\dot{\mathbf{p}}_{i}=-\partialderivative{V}{\vq_i},\\
\dot{\mathbf{R}}_{i}=0,\dot{\mathbf{\Pi}}_{i}=-\left(\mathbf{R}_{i}^{\intercal}\partialderivative{V}{\vR_i}-\left(\partialderivative{V}{\vR_i}\right)^{\intercal}\mathbf{R}_{i}\right)^{\vee}.\end{cases}

(B45)

Since
\mathbf{q}_{i}
and
\mathbf{p}_{i}
stay constant,
\mathbf{p}_{i}
and
\mathbf{\Pi}_{i}
change at constant rates. Hence, the flow
\phi_{h}^{[\textrm{PE}]}
of
\mathcal{H}_{\textrm{PE}}
is given by

\begin{cases}\mathbf{q}_{i}(h)=\mathbf{q}_{i}(0)\\
\mathbf{p}_{i}(h)=\mathbf{p}_{i}(0)-\partialderivative{V}{\vq_i}h,\\
\mathbf{R}_{i}(h)=\mathbf{R}_{i}(0),\\
\mathbf{\Pi}_{i}(h)=\mathbf{\Pi}_{i}(0)-\left(\mathbf{R}_{i}^{\intercal}\partialderivative{V}{\vR_i}-\left(\partialderivative{V}{\vR_i}\right)^{\intercal}\mathbf{R}_{i}\right)^{\vee}h.\end{cases}

(B46)

Exact integration of
\bm{\mathcal{H}_{\textrm{asym}}}

Finally, the equations of motion of
\mathcal{H}_{\textrm{asym}}
are given by (again adapting (
1a
)-(
1d
))

\begin{cases}\dot{\mathbf{q}}_{i}=0,\dot{\mathbf{p}}_{i}=0,\\
\dot{\mathbf{R}}_{i}=\mathbf{R}_{i}\widehat{\mathbf{J}_{i,\textrm{asym}}^{-1}\mathbf{\Pi}_{i}},\dot{\mathbf{\Pi}}_{i}=\mathbf{\Pi}_{i}\times\mathbf{J}_{i,\textrm{asym}}^{-1}\mathbf{\Pi}_{i}.\end{cases}

(B47)

which can be solved to obtain the flow
\phi_{h}^{[\textrm{asym}]}
of
\mathcal{H}_{\textrm{asym}}
as

\begin{cases}\mathbf{q}_{i}(h)=\mathbf{q}_{i}(0)\\
\mathbf{p}_{i}(h)=\mathbf{p}_{i}(0),\\
\mathbf{R}_{i}(h)=R_{y}(\delta\Pi_{i}^{(2)}h)\mathbf{R}_{i}(0),\\
\mathbf{\Pi}_{i}(h)=R_{y}(-\delta\Pi_{i}^{(2)}h)\mathbf{\Pi}_{i}(0).\end{cases}

(B48)

where
\delta\coloneqq 1/J_{i}^{(2)}-1/J_{i}^{(1)}
.

Having obtained analytical solutions for each of the flows
\phi^{[\textrm{KE}]},\phi^{[\textrm{PE}]},\phi^{[\textrm{asym}]}
, we then combine them with the non-conservative momentum update from the non-conservative forcing terms
F_{\mathbf{p}_{i}}
and
F_{\mathbf{\Pi}_{i}}
with flow
\phi_{h}^{[\textrm{force}]}

\mathbf{q}_{i}(h)=\mathbf{q}_{i}(0)\\
\mathbf{p}_{i}(h)=\mathbf{p}_{i}(0)+F_{\mathbf{p}_{i}}h,\\
\mathbf{R}_{i}(h)=\mathbf{R}_{i}(0),\\
\mathbf{\Pi}_{i}(h)=\mathbf{\Pi}_{i}(0)+F_{\mathbf{\Pi}_{i}}h.

(B49)

Consequently, the full Lie
\mathrm{T}_{2}
integrator is obtained by applying the Strang composition scheme to obtain

\phi_{h}^{\textrm{Lie $\mathrm{T}_{2}$}}\coloneqq\phi_{h/2}^{[\textrm{KE}]}\circ\phi_{h/2}^{[\textrm{PE}]}\circ\phi_{h/2}^{[\textrm{asym}]}\circ\phi_{h}^{[\textrm{force}]}\circ\phi_{h/2}^{[\textrm{asym}]}\circ\phi_{h/2}^{[\textrm{PE}]}\circ\phi_{h/2}^{[\textrm{KE}]}

(B50)

Structure of
V_{\textrm{resid}}^{\theta}

Note that we
do not
assume prior knowledge on the pairwise structure of the potential function
V_{\text{resid}}
or of the forcing terms
F_{\bf{p}},F_{\bf{\Pi}}
.
More specifically, since the true Gravitational potential (
2
) only acts
pairwise
between rigid bodies, the rigid body correction potential also acts pairwise and has the structure

V_{\text{resid}}(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{R})=\sum_{i<j}V_{i,j,\text{resid}}(\mathbf{q}_{i},\mathbf{q}_{j},\mathbf{R}_{i},\mathbf{R}_{j}).

(C51)

For the forcing terms, the tidal forcing term also acts pairwise with coupled effects on
\bf{\mathbf{p}}_{i}
and
\bf{\Pi}_{i}
due the relationship between forces and torques, while the post-Newton general-relativity correction term acts on each planet individually and affects only
\bf{\mathbf{p}}_{i}
. However, in this work, we assume that none of this prior knowledge is available and aim to learn everything purely from data. Hence, we choose to learn the high dimensional forms of

The fact that we were able to obtain improvements despite the high dimensionality of the input space (
8
planets each with
(\mathbf{q}_{i},\mathbf{R}_{i})\in\mathbb{R}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}\approx\mathbb{R}^{12}

\mathbb{R}^{96}
for
V
and
\mathbb{R}^{144}
for
F_{\bf{p}},F_{\bf{\Pi}}
) is an indication of the generality of our approach. We believe that the proposed approach can be made more scalable and have better generalization if we do assume some knowledge about the structure of the forces at play and instead, e.g., learn
V_{\text{resid}}^{\theta}(\mathbf{q}_{i},\mathbf{q}_{j},\mathbf{R}_{i},\mathbf{R}_{j},\phi_{i},\phi_{j})

and similarly for the forcing terms, where

contains known or potentially learned information about physical properties about each rigid body which are needed. Here, since the ordering of
i
and
j
is not important, some type of permutation-invariant encoding such as
[
48
]
should be used to further improve generalization.

Details on Integrators used for Comparison in

To answer the question
Q2
of how important are
symplecticity
(
{\color[rgb]{1,0.3203,0.3203}\bm{\mathcal{S}}}
) and
Lie-group preservation
(
{\color[rgb]{0.2656,0.543,1}\bm{\mathcal{L}}}
) for learning, we vary the choice of integrator in our experiments. The integrators used can be broadly split into four categories:

Neither
Symplectic

{\color[rgb]{1,0.3203,0.3203}\bm{\mathcal{S}}}
nor
Lie-group preserving

{\color[rgb]{0.2656,0.543,1}\bm{\mathcal{L}}}
:
This category contains the popular explicit Euler and Runge-Kutta 4 integrators, neither of which are symplectic or Lie-group preserving. The finite-difference scheme from
Greydanus et al. 2019
,
Greydanus and Sosanya 2022
can be interpreted as an application of the explicit Euler integrator
David and Méhats 2021
. Explicit Euler is also used in the works of

[
]

. Runge-Kutta 4 is a popular fourth-order integrator due to its implementation simplicity and is used in
Finzi et al. 2020
,
Zhong et al.
,
Zhong et al. 2021b
,
Zhong et al. 2021a
,
Duong and Atanasov 2021
.

Symplectic

{\color[rgb]{1,0.3203,0.3203}\bm{\mathcal{S}}}
but not
Lie-group preserving

{\color[rgb]{0.2656,0.543,1}\bm{\mathcal{L}}}
:
This category contains the verlet integrator, which we define loosely in the current work as the integrator obtained by using classical splitting and Strang composition, but
without
performing exact integration on the manifold. In the Euclidean case where
\mathcal{H}(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{p})=\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{p}^{\intercal}\mathbf{p}/m+V(\mathbf{q})
, this takes the form

\displaystyle\mathbf{q}(h/2)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{q}(0)+\frac{\mathbf{p}(0)}{m}h,

(D52)

\displaystyle\mathbf{p}(h)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{p}(0)-\partialderivative{V}{\vq}h,

(D53)

\displaystyle\mathbf{q}(h)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{q}(h/2)+\frac{\mathbf{p}(1)}{m}h,

(D54)

and corresponds to the “leapfrog” proposed in SRNN
Chen et al. 2019
.
In our setting where the space is not Euclidean, we interpret a “naive” implementation to look like the following

\displaystyle\mathbf{q}_{i}(h/2)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{q}_{i}(0)+\frac{\mathbf{p}_{i}(0)}{m}h,

(D55a)

\displaystyle\mathbf{R}_{i}(h/2)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{R}_{i}(0)+\mathbf{R}_{i}(0)\;\widehat{\mathbf{J}_{i}^{-1}\mathbf{\Pi}_{i}(0)}h,

(D55b)

\displaystyle\mathbf{p}_{i}(h)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{p}_{i}(0)-\partialderivative{V}{\vq_i}h,

(D56a)

\displaystyle\mathbf{\Pi}_{i}(h)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{\Pi}_{i}(0)+\left(\mathbf{\Pi}_{i}(0)\times\mathbf{J}_{i}^{-1}\mathbf{\Pi}_{i}(0)-\left(\mathbf{R}_{i}^{\intercal}(0)\partialderivative{V}{\vR_i}-\left(\partialderivative{V}{\vR_i}\right)^{\intercal}\mathbf{R}_{i}(0)\right)^{\vee}\right)h,

(D56b)

\displaystyle\mathbf{q}_{i}(h)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{q}_{i}(h/2)+\frac{\mathbf{p}_{i}(h)}{m}h,

(D57a)

\displaystyle\mathbf{R}_{i}(h)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{R}_{i}(h/2)+\mathbf{R}_{i}(h)\widehat{\mathbf{J}_{i}^{-1}\mathbf{\Pi}_{i}(h)}h,

(D57b)

Not
Symplectic

{\color[rgb]{1,0.3203,0.3203}\bm{\mathcal{S}}}
but is
Lie-group preserving

{\color[rgb]{0.2656,0.543,1}\bm{\mathcal{L}}}
:
This category contains the Lie RK2 (CF2) and Lie RK4 (CF4) commutator-free Lie-group preserving integrators from
Celledoni et al.
. These integrators are Lie-group preserving but are not symplectic. Specifically, the flow of Lie RK2 is described by

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{q}_{i}(h/2)\\
\mathbf{p}_{i}(h/2)\\
\mathbf{\Pi}_{i}(h/2)\end{bmatrix}&=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{q}_{i}(0)\\
\mathbf{p}_{i}(0)\\

Appendix F
Limitations and Future Directions

One big assumption we make in our work is that the masses and inertial tensors
m_{i},\mathbf{J}_{i}
are known. This may be a restricting assumption when applying this method to learning physics for rigid bodies where this information is not available and must be learned jointly with the physics, an approach taken in many works on learning with Hamiltonian structure (e.g.,
[
51
,
16
]
). Moreover, as discussed in
Appendix

C
, our framework does not assume that prior knowledge on the structure (e.g., pairwise, independent) of the potential or forcing terms is known. If we make this assumption, then additional physical properties for each rigid body may need to be provided to fully specify the structured physics (e.g., time lag and tide constants for tidal forcing).
Extending this framework to handle the learning of per-body physical properties at the same time is left for future work.

Another direction that we have not been able to explore in this work is the robustness of our method to noise in the dataset. In this work, we use a dataset generated using the GRIT
[
20
]
simulator (see
Appendix

C
). Consequently, the dataset used is clean and not corrupted by any noise. As noted in SRNN
Chen et al. 2019
, integrating for multiple consecutive timesteps may allow the network to better discern the true noiseless trajectory when computing the loss function. Moreover, the benefit from performing this multi-step training is integrator dependent, with improvements not observed when using the naive explicit Euler integration. Given that, 1. our configuration space now lies on a Lie-group manifold, and 2. the physics of our system are mutiscale,
exploring whether those insights are applicable to the problem considered in this work is a future interesting direction that will help inform practitioners wishing to apply this methodology to real world data.
