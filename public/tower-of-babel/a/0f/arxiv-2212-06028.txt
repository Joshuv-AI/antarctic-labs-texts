Upgrading MLSI to LSI for reversible Markov chains
Justin Salez, Konstantin Tikhomirov, Pierre Youssef

Abstract
For reversible Markov chains on finite state spaces, we show that the modified log-Sobolev inequality (MLSI) can be upgraded to a log-Sobolev inequality (LSI) at the surprisingly low cost of degrading the associated constant by log ⁡ ( 1 / p ) \log(1/p) , where p p is the minimum non-zero transition probability. We illustrate this by providing the first log-Sobolev estimate for Zero-Range processes on arbitrary graphs. As another application, we determine the modified log-Sobolev constant of the Lamplighter chain on all bounded-degree graphs, and use it to provide negative answers to two open questions by Montenegro and Tetali (2006) and Hermon and Peres (2018). Our proof builds upon the ‘regularization trick’ recently introduced by the last two authors.



1
Introduction

Functional inequalities constitute a powerful set of tools for the study of the
concentration of measure
phenomenon
[
24
,
25
]
. On a conceptual level, those inequalities relate certain statistics of a measure (such as the variance and entropy) to the Dirichlet form of a Markov process that preserves the measure. Besides concentration, those inequalities are intimately connected to the rate of convergence to equilibrium of the considered Markov process. Perhaps the most popular functional inequalities are the
Poincaré Inequality
(PI) and the

log-Sobolev Inequality
(LSI), which were initially studied in the continuous setting.
On discrete spaces, a variant called the
Modified log-Sobolev Inequality
(MLSI) has been put forward and exploited due to its connection with the entropic exponential ergodicity of the underlying semi-group.
Informally, it is a standard fact (see for example
[
3
]
) that

\text{LSI}\;\Rightarrow\;\text{MLSI}\;\Rightarrow\;\text{PI},

and the aim of this paper is to investigate the reverse implications.

1.1
Setup

Throughout the paper, we consider an irreducible Markov generator
Q
on a finite state space
\mathcal{X}
, and we assume that
Q
is reversible with respect to a probability measure
\pi
.
The associated
Dirichlet form
is given, for any observables
f,g\colon\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
, by the formula

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}\left(f,g\right)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\sum_{x,y\in\mathcal{X}}\pi(x)Q(x,y)\left(f(x)-f(y)\right)\left(g(x)-g(y)\right).

The
variance
and
entropy
of a function
f\colon\mathcal{X}\to(0,\infty)
are defined as
{\mathrm{Var}}(f):={\mathbf{E}}[f^{2}]-{\mathbf{E}}^{2}[f]
and
{\mathrm{Ent}}(f):={\mathbf{E}}[f\log f]-{\mathbf{E}}[f]\log{\mathbf{E}}[f]
, where
{\mathbf{E}}[\cdot]
denotes expectation with respect to
\pi
and where ‘
\log
’ stands for the natural logarithm. The
log-Sobolev
constant
{\rm t}_{\textsc{ls}}
, the
modified log-Sobolev
constant
{\rm t}_{\textsc{mls}}
, and the
Poincaré
constant
{\rm t}_{\textsc{rel}}
are then respectively defined as the optimal constants in the functional inequalities

{\mathrm{Ent}}(f)\ \leq\ t\mathcal{E}(\sqrt{f},\sqrt{f});

\,{\mathrm{Ent}}(f)\ \leq\ t\mathcal{E}\left(f,\log f\right);

\,{\mathrm{Var}}(f)\ \leq\ t\mathcal{E}\left(f,f\right),

for all functions
f\colon\mathcal{X}\to(0,\infty)
. We refer the unfamiliar reader to the seminal papers
[
11
,
23
,
3
]
, the book
[
27
]
, or the more recent works
[
17
,
29
,
2
,
7
]
for details about those fundamental constants and their many variants.

It is classical that (
1.1
), (
1.1
), (
1.1
) are decreasing in strength, in the exact sense that

\displaystyle\frac{{\rm t}_{\textsc{rel}}}{2}\ \leq

\displaystyle{\rm t}_{\textsc{mls}}

\displaystyle\leq\ \frac{{\rm t}_{\textsc{ls}}}{4}.

(1)

The leftmost inequality is obtained by a standard perturbation argument around constant functions, while the rightmost one is a direct consequence of the inequality

\displaystyle 4\mathcal{E}\left(\sqrt{f},\sqrt{f}\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(f,\log{f}),

(2)

for all
f\colon\mathcal{X}\to(0,\infty)
, which in turns follows from the easy bound
4(\sqrt{u}-\sqrt{v})^{2}\leq(u-v)\log\frac{u}{v}
for all
u,v>0
. However, in contrast with what happens in the smooth setting of diffusions on manifolds, the functional inequality (
2
) can here
not
be reversed uniformly in
f
: indeed, as soon as
|\mathcal{X}|\geq 2
, the quantity
\mathcal{E}\left(f,\log{f}\right)
can be made arbitrarily larger than
\mathcal{E}\left(\sqrt{f},\sqrt{f}\right)
by simply increasing the value of
f
at a single point. This degeneracy is one of the many infamous consequences of the lack of a
chain rule
for Markov generators on discrete spaces
[
24
]
. It introduces a fundamental discrepancy between the inequalities (
1.1
) and (
1.1
), which results in two very different meanings for their optimal constants:
{\rm t}_{\textsc{mls}}
measures the
entropy production
along the semi-group, while
{\rm t}_{\textsc{ls}}
quantifies the much stronger
hyper-contractivity
. As a result, the ratio
{\rm t}_{\textsc{ls}}/{\rm t}_{\textsc{mls}}
can be arbitrarily large even for two-point chains (see
[
3
, Section 3]
), and any general upper bound on it will inevitably have to depend on the data
(Q,\pi)
. Understanding
how
is the task we undertake here.

To the best of our knowledge, the only general upper bound available on the ratio
{\rm t}_{\textsc{ls}}/{\rm t}_{\textsc{mls}}
is the one resulting from the chain of inequalities

\displaystyle\frac{{\rm t}_{\textsc{ls}}}{{\rm t}_{\textsc{mls}}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{2{\rm t}_{\textsc{ls}}}{{\rm t}_{\textsc{rel}}}\ \leq\ \frac{2}{1-2\pi_{\star}}\log\left(\frac{1}{\pi_{\star}}-1\right),

(3)

where
\pi_{\star}:=\min_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\pi(x)
. The first inequality follows from (
1
), and the second from a direct comparison with the Dirichlet form of the trivial chain
Q(x,y)=\pi(y)
, whose log-Sobolev constant is explicit (see, e.g.
[
11
]
). Note that this ‘extreme’ chain, which mixes in a single jump, actually saturates both inequalities in (
3
). However, a quick look at more reasonable examples should convince the reader that the ratio
{{\rm t}_{\textsc{ls}}}/{{\rm t}_{\textsc{mls}}}
is typically much smaller than
\log\left({1}/{\pi_{\star}}\right)
in practice, and this observation was the motivation behind the present paper.

1.2
Main result

Writing
E:=\left\{(x,y)\in\mathcal{X}^{2}\colon Q(x,y)>0\right\}
for the (symmetric) set of allowed transitions and
Q(x):=\sum_{y\in\mathcal{X}\setminus\{x\}}Q(x,y)
for the total jump rate at
x\in\mathcal{X}
, we define the
sparsity
parameter

\displaystyle p

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\min_{(x,y)\in E}\frac{Q(x,y)}{Q(x)\vee Q(y,x)}.

(4)

In particular, in the standard
stochastic case
where
Q(x)=1
for all
x\in\mathcal{X}
, this reduces to

\displaystyle p

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\min_{(x,y)\in E}Q(x,y).

Our main result is that the lost equivalence between (
1.1
) and (
1.1
) on discrete spaces can be restored at the surprisingly low cost
\log(1/p)
only.

Theorem 1

(Upgrading MLSI to LSI)
.

For any reversible Markov generator, we have

\displaystyle{\rm t}_{\textsc{ls}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 20\,{\rm t}_{\textsc{mls}}\log\left(\frac{1}{p}\right).

The improvement over (
3
) can be considerable, since
p
is typically much larger than
\pi_{\star}
. To appreciate this, consider the important case of simple random walk on a finite graph
G=(V_{G},E_{G})
: the classical estimate (
3
) predicts
{\rm t}_{\textsc{ls}}/{\rm t}_{\textsc{mls}}=O(\log|V_{G}|)
, whereas our result gives
{\rm t}_{\textsc{ls}}/{\rm t}_{\textsc{mls}}=O(\log d)
where
d
denotes the maximum degree. As a concrete example, when
G
is the transposition walk on the symmetric group
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
, we obtain
{\rm t}_{\textsc{ls}}/{\rm t}_{\textsc{mls}}=O(\log n)
instead of
{\rm t}_{\textsc{ls}}/{\rm t}_{\textsc{mls}}=O(n\log n)
. Incidentally, this example demonstrates that our result is sharp except for the value of the universal prefactor, which we did not try to optimize (see, e.g.
[
13
]
). On bounded-degree graphs, Theorem
1
shows that (
1.1
) and (
1.1
) are actually equivalent, a new fact with surprising consequences (see Section
1.3
below).

Beyond the theoretical interest of a universal comparison between (
1.1
) and (
1.1
), Theorem
1
can be used in practice to derive new, ready-to-use functional-analytic estimates. Obviously, this can be done in two different directions. First, we can produce lower bounds on
{\rm t}_{\textsc{mls}}
in situations where a lower bound on
{\rm t}_{\textsc{ls}}
is available. In Section
1.3
below, we illustrate this by determining the modified log-Sobolev constant of the Lamplighter chain on all bounded-degree graphs, and we use this to provide negative answers to two open questions by Montenegro and Tetali and by Hermon and Peres. Conversely, there are several concrete classes of chains for which a (
1.1
) was established by methods that do not carry over to (
1.1
). Examples include Bernoulli-Laplace models
[
5
]
, Zero-Range processes
[
21
,
5
]
, or negatively-dependent measures on the Boolean hypercube
[
18
,
8
]
. In such examples, using Theorem
1
to convert the known upper bound on
{\rm t}_{\textsc{mls}}
into a new one on
{\rm t}_{\textsc{ls}}
is of practical interest for at least three reasons:

(i)

Mixing times
: writing
f_{t}
for the density with respect to equilibrium of the Markov process at time
t
, the parameter
{\rm t}_{\textsc{mls}}
only controls the decay rate of the relative entropy
{\mathrm{Ent}}(f_{t})
, while
{\rm t}_{\textsc{ls}}
controls the much stronger uniform norm
\left\|f_{t}-1\right\|_{\infty}
(see, e.g.,
[
27
]
).

(ii)

Isoperimetry
: by specializing (
1.1
) to
\{0,1\}-
valued functions, the constant
{\rm t}_{\textsc{ls}}
captures
small-set expansion
, which has numerous applications (see, e.g.,
[
14
]
).

(iii)

Robustness
: unlike
{\rm t}_{\textsc{mls}}
, an estimate on
{\rm t}_{\textsc{ls}}
can be transferred to other chains using the classical and well-developed comparison theory for Dirichlet forms (see, e.g.,
[
12
]
).

In Section
1.4
below, we will illustrate this by providing the very first log-Sobolev estimate for the Zero-Range Process with increasing rates on arbitrary graphs.

1.3
Application to Lamplighter chains

Fix a finite graph
G=(V_{G},E_{G})
and imagine that each vertex is equipped with a lamp that can be either
off
or
on
. Now, consider a lamplighter performing a simple random walk on
G
and randomly switching the lamps off or on on his way. More formally, let us describe the state of the system by a pair
(i,\sigma)
where
i\in V
represents the position of the lamplighter and
\sigma\in\{0,1\}^{V}
indicates which lamps are on. The
Lamplighter chain
on
G
is the continuous-time Markov chain on
\mathcal{X}:=V\times\{0,1\}^{V}
whose generator acts on any
f\colon\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
as follows:

\displaystyle(Qf)(i,\sigma)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left(f(i,\sigma^{i})-f(i,\sigma)\right)+\frac{1}{2\deg(i)}\sum_{j\sim i}\left(f(j,\sigma)-f(i,\sigma)\right).

In this formula, the notation
j\sim i
means that
\{i,j\}\in E_{G}
, while
\sigma^{i}
denotes the configuration obtained from
\sigma\in\{0,1\}^{V}
by replacing
\sigma_{i}
with
1-\sigma_{i}
. The behavior of this process is extremely rich and has drawn considerable interest across various fields, including graph theory, group theory, spectral theory, discrete functional analysis and probability. We refer the reader to the book
[
26
, Chapter 19]
and the references therein for a quick introduction.

The works
[
16
,
28
,
1
,
22
,
15
]
are all devoted to the fundamental question of relating the mixing properties of the Lamplighter chain to those of the underlying graph
G
. In particular, the relaxation time, the total-variation mixing time and the uniform mixing time are now completely understood. This is also true for the log-Sobolev constant, at least on bounded-degree graphs. Specifically, the Lamplighter chain on
G
was shown in
[
1
]
to satisfy

\displaystyle{\rm t}_{\textsc{ls}}

\displaystyle\asymp_{d}

\displaystyle\frac{|V_{G}|}{\gamma(G)},

(5)

where
\gamma(G)
denotes the spectral gap of
G
and
d=\max_{i\in V_{G}}\deg(i)
the maximum degree. Here, the notation
a\asymp_{d}b
means that the ratio
a/b
is bounded from above and below by positive numbers that depend only on
d
. However, in contrast with many other mixing parameters, nothing seems to be known about the modified log-Sobolev constant of Lamplighter chains, even on simple graphs such as the
n-
cycle
\mathbb{Z}_{n}
. Since the Lamplighter chain has sparsity
p=1/(2d)
, our main result allows us to determine
{\rm t}_{\textsc{mls}}
on all bounded-degree graphs.

Corollary 1

(MLSI for the Lamplighter chain on bounded-degree graphs)
.

We have

\displaystyle{\rm t}_{\textsc{mls}}

\displaystyle\asymp_{d}

\displaystyle\frac{|V_{G}|}{\gamma(G)}.

On the discrete torus
G=\mathbb{Z}^{d}_{n}
, we thus obtain
{\rm t}_{\textsc{mls}}=\Theta(n^{d+2})
for any fixed dimension
d\geq 1
. Surprisingly, this is always much larger than the total-variation mixing time of the Lamplighter chain, which is known
[
16
,
28
]
to be
{\rm t}_{\textsc{mix}}=\Theta(n^{2})
when
d=1
,
{\rm t}_{\textsc{mix}}=\Theta(n^{2}\log^{2}n)
when
d=2
, and
{\rm t}_{\textsc{mix}}=\Theta(n^{d}\log n)
for any fixed
d\geq 3
. Thus, Lamplighter chains on discrete tori constitute a simple family of counter-examples to the following classical question, which appears as Question 8.2 in the classical monograph
[
27
]
by Montenegro and Tetali.

Question 1

(Question 8.2 in
[
27
]
)
.

Is there a universal constant
c>0
such that

{\rm t}_{\textsc{mls}}\leq c\,{\rm t}_{\textsc{mix}},

for all irreducible Markov chains?

Very recently, Hermon and Peres
[
17
, Question 7.1]
proposed the following more reasonable conjecture, in which
{\rm t}_{\textsc{mix}}
is replaced by the larger relative-entropy mixing time
{\rm t}_{\textsc{ent}}
(see
[
15
]
or
[
17
]
for the precise definition).

Question 2

(Question 7.1 in
[
17
]
)
.

Is there a universal constant
c>0
such that

{\rm t}_{\textsc{mls}}\leq c\,{\rm t}_{\textsc{ent}},

for all irreducible Markov chains?

Since the Lamplighter chain on the
n-
cycle
\mathbb{Z}_{n}
is known to satisfy
{\rm t}_{\textsc{ent}}=\Theta(n^{2}\log n)

[
15
]
, the estimate
{\rm t}_{\textsc{mls}}=\Theta(n^{3})
given by Corollary
1
again provides a negative answer to this question. Finally, we note that our example also refutes Question 7.2 in the same paper, which asks for an even stronger upper bound on
{\rm t}_{\textsc{mls}}
.

1.4
Application to Zero-Range Processes

Introduced by Spitzer
[
30
]
, the
Zero-Range Process
(
ZRP
) is a generic interacting particle system in which individual jumps occur at a rate that only depends on the current number of particles present at the source. The model is parameterized by the following ingredients:

•

two integers
m,n\geq 1
representing the number of particles and sites, respectively;

•

a symmetric stochastic matrix
G=(G_{ij})_{1\leq i,j\leq n}
specifying the geometry;

•

a function
r_{i}\colon\{1,2,\ldots\}\to(0,\infty)
encoding the kinetics at each site
i\in[n]
.

The
ZRP
with these parameters is a continuous-time Markov chain on the state space

\displaystyle\mathcal{X}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\left\{x=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}^{n}\colon\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}=m\right\},

(6)

where
x_{i}
represents the number of particles at site
i
. The action of the generator is given by

\displaystyle(Qf)(x)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\sum_{1\leq i,j\leq n}r_{i}(x_{i})G_{ij}\left(f(x+\delta_{j}-\delta_{i})-f(x)\right),

(7)

where
(\delta_{1},\ldots,\delta_{n})
denotes the canonical
n-
dimensional basis, and with the convention that
r_{i}(0)=0
for all
i\in[n]
(no jumps from empty sites). In words, a site
i
with
k\geq 1
particles expels a particle at rate
r_{i}(k)
, and the destination is chosen according to
G
. A natural choice for the latter is the transition matrix of simple random walk on a regular graph. In fact, the model is already interesting on the complete graph (the so-called
mean-field
case):

\displaystyle\forall(i,j)\in[n]^{2},\qquad G_{ij}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}.

(8)

Obtaining quantitative estimates on the convergence to equilibrium of the ZRP has been and continues to be a subject of active research, see e.g.,
[
6
,
20
]
in the mean-field case and
[
9
,
10
]
on integer lattices. A standard assumption on the rate functions
(r_{i})_{1\leq i\leq n}
is that their increments all lie in a fixed compact subset of
(0,\infty)
:

\displaystyle\delta\ \leq

\displaystyle r_{i}(k+1)-r_{i}(k)

\displaystyle\leq\ \Delta,

(9)

for some
\delta,\Delta>0
and every
i\in[n]
and
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
.
Under this
weak interaction
condition, and in the
mean-field
setting (
8
), the ZRP was shown in
[
21
]
to satisfy the dimension-free MLSI

\displaystyle{\rm t}_{\textsc{mls}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{2\Delta}{\delta^{2}}.

Note that our sparsity parameter
p
here satisfies
p\geq\frac{\delta}{\Delta mn}
, because
Q(x)\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}r_{i}(x_{i})\leq\Delta m
for all
x\in\mathcal{X}
and
Q(x,y)\geq\delta/n
for all
(x,y)\in E
.
Thus, Theorem
1
produces the following estimate which, to the best of our knowledge, is the very first LSI for the mean-field ZRP.

Corollary 2

(LSI for mean-field ZRP)
.

Under assumptions (
8
) and (
9
), we have

\displaystyle{\rm t}_{\textsc{ls}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{40\Delta}{\delta^{2}}\log\left(\frac{\Delta mn}{\delta}\right),

for any choice of the dimension parameters
n,m
.

The dependency in
n
is optimal, as can be seen by investigating the case of a single particle (
m=1
). As already mentioned, one of the advantages of
{\rm t}_{\textsc{ls}}
over
{\rm t}_{\textsc{mls}}
is its robustness under comparison of Dirichlet forms. This is particularly true in the present setting, because it was shown in
[
19
]
that replacing a general symmetric stochastic matrix
G
by its mean-field version (
8
) can not increase the Dirichlet form of the ZRP by more than a factor
1/\gamma(G)
, where
\gamma(G)
denotes the spectral gap of
G
. Consequently, our mean-field LSI estimate can be directly transferred to arbitrary geometries, yielding the following result.
To the best of our knowledge, log-Sobolev estimates for the ZRP were so far restricted to lattices
[
9
]
.

Corollary 3

(LSI for ZRP on arbitrary geometries)
.

Under Assumption (
9
), we have

\displaystyle{\rm t}_{\textsc{ls}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{40\Delta}{\gamma(G)\delta^{2}}\log\left(\frac{\Delta mn}{\delta}\right),

for any choice of the dimension parameters
n,m
and of the symmetric stochastic matrix
G
.

Remark 1

(Asymmetric geometries)
.

Interestingly, the symmetry of
G
is never actually used in
[
21
,
19
]
, so Corollary
3
generalizes to any stochastic matrix
G
as follows:

\displaystyle{\rm t}_{\textsc{ls}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{40\Delta}{\gamma(G)\delta^{2}}\log\left(\frac{\Delta m}{\delta p_{\star}}\right),

where
p_{\star}
denotes the smallest entry of the invariant probability vector of
G
, and where
\gamma(G)
denotes the spectral gap of the additive reversibilization of
G
.

Proof of the main result

The proof of Theorem
1
builds upon a simple but fruitful idea, recently introduced by the last two authors in
[
31
]
: the
regularization trick
. It consists in restricting functional inequalities to observables
f\colon\mathcal{X}\to(0,\infty)
that are
smooth
, in an appropriate sense.

2.1
The regularization trick

Fix a parameter
r\geq 1
. Following
[
31
]
, we say that a function
f\colon\mathcal{X}\to(0,\infty)
is

r-
regular
if

\displaystyle\forall(x,y)\in E,\qquad f(x)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle rf(y).

(10)

Our starting point is the elementary but new observation that, once restricted to
r-
regular functions, the basic inequality (
2
) can be reversed at the optimal cost

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}(r)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\frac{\sqrt{r}+1}{\sqrt{r}-1}\log r.

(11)

Note that
\mathcal{H}(\infty)=\infty
, in agreement with our earlier observation that the unrestricted inequality (
2
) can
not
be reversed. At the other extreme, we have
\mathcal{H}(1+)=4
, in agreement with the fact that (
2
) is an equality in the infinitely smooth setting of diffusions on manifolds.

Lemma 1

(Exploiting regularity)
.

If
f\colon\mathcal{X}\to(0,\infty)
is
r-
regular, then

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(f,\log f)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}(r)\mathcal{E}(\sqrt{f},\sqrt{f}).

Proof.

With
\mathcal{H}(1):=4
, the formula (
11
) defines a function
\mathcal{H}\colon(0,\infty)\to(0,\infty)
which increases on
[1,\infty)
and satisfies
\mathcal{H}(u)=\mathcal{H}(u^{-1})
for all
u\in(0,\infty)
. It follows that
\mathcal{H}(u)\leq\mathcal{H}(r)
for all
u\in[r^{-1},r]
. On the other hand, elementary manipulations give

\displaystyle(u-v)\log\left(\frac{u}{v}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}\left(\frac{u}{v}\right)(\sqrt{u}-\sqrt{v})^{2},

for all
u,v>0
. Taking
u=f(x)
and
v=f(y)
, and recalling our assumption (
10
), we obtain

\displaystyle(f(x)-f(y))\log\left(\frac{f(x)}{f(y)}\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}\left(r\right)(\sqrt{f(x)}-\sqrt{f(y)})^{2},

for any
(x,y)\in E
. To conclude, we multiply by
\pi(x)Q(x,y)
and sum over all
x,y\in\mathcal{X}
.
∎

Let us respectively denote by
{\rm t}_{\textsc{ls}}(r)
and
{\rm t}_{\textsc{mls}}(r)
the optimal constants in the inequalities (
1.1
) and (
1.1
), when restricted to
r-
regular functions. The above lemma readily implies

\displaystyle{\rm t}_{\textsc{ls}}(r)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}(r){\rm t}_{\textsc{mls}}(r),

(12)

which can be seen as a regularized version of Theorem
1
. To conclude, we now need to relate the constants
{\rm t}_{\textsc{mls}}(r)
and
{\rm t}_{\textsc{ls}}(r)
to their unregularized versions
{\rm t}_{\textsc{mls}}
and
{\rm t}_{\textsc{ls}}
. Of course, we trivially have

{\rm t}_{\textsc{mls}}(r)\leq{\rm t}_{\textsc{mls}}

and
{\rm t}_{\textsc{ls}}(r)\leq{\rm t}_{\textsc{ls}}
, by definition.
We will now show that those inequalities can be reversed, provided
r
is large enough. Specifically, we henceforth set

\displaystyle r

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\frac{4}{p^{2}},

(13)

and we assume that
p\leq 1/2
(if this is not the case, then irreducibility and reversibility imply that
|\mathcal{X}|\leq 2
, so that Theorem
1
can be checked by hands). Note that we then have
3\mathcal{H}(r)\leq{20}\log\frac{1}{p}
. Thus, Theorem
1
is a consequence of the following crucial result.

Theorem 2

(Regularization)
.

With
r
as in (
13
), we have

{\rm t}_{\textsc{ls}}\leq 3{\rm t}_{\textsc{ls}}(r)
and
{\rm t}_{\textsc{mls}}\leq 3{\rm t}_{\textsc{mls}}(r)
.

In other words, to establish (
1.1
) or (
1.1
) for a reversible Markov chain, it is enough to restrict attention to
r-
regular observables. Such a costless regularization is of course interesting beyond its role in the present paper. A first version of it (for
{\rm t}_{\textsc{mls}}
, and with an additional factor
\gamma=\frac{\max\pi}{\min\pi}
in the regularization parameter
r
) was recently used in
[
31
]
to obtain a sharp (
1.1
) for the
switch chain
on regular bipartite graphs and as a consequence prove a long-standing conjecture about the mixing time of that chain (see
[
32
]
and references therein). Our proof below follows the same strategy, but optimizes it so as to remove the dependency of
r
on
\gamma
. Note that this improvement is crucial for our application to the ZRP.

We write
d(\cdot,\cdot)
for the graph distance on
(\mathcal{X},E)
. Following
[
31
]
, we fix an arbitrary observable
f\colon\mathcal{X}\to(0,\infty)
and we define a new observable
f_{\star}\colon\mathcal{X}\to(0,\infty)
by

\displaystyle\forall x\in\mathcal{X},\qquad f_{\star}(x)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\max_{z\in\mathcal{X}}\,r^{-d(x,z)}f(z).

It is immediate to check that
f_{\star}
is
r-
regular and above
f
(it is in fact the smallest such function, but we will not use this property here). The following propositions show that the quantities
\mathcal{E}(\sqrt{f},\sqrt{f})
,
\mathcal{E}(f,\log f)
and
{\mathrm{Ent}}(f)
do not change much upon replacing
f
by
f_{\star}
.

Proposition 1

(Comparison of Dirichlet forms)
.

For
r
as in (
13
), we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(\sqrt{f_{\star}},\sqrt{f_{\star}})\ \leq\ \frac{4}{3}\mathcal{E}(\sqrt{f},\sqrt{f})

and

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}({f_{\star}},\log{f_{\star}})\ \leq\ \frac{4}{3}\mathcal{E}(f,\log{f}).

Proposition 2

(Comparison of entropies)
.

For
r
as in (
13
), we have

{\mathrm{Ent}}(f)\leq 2{\mathrm{Ent}}(f_{\star})
.

Those propositions clearly imply Theorem
2
, and we henceforth focus on their proofs.

2.2
Comparing Dirichlet forms (Proposition
1
)

The proof of Proposition
1
consists of three steps, of which only the last one uses the specific choice for
r
made at (
13
). We define the distance from an edge
e\in E
to
e^{\prime}\in E
in the obvious way:
d(e,e^{\prime})=\ell-1
, where
\ell\geq 1
is the minimum length of a path
(x_{0},\ldots,x_{\ell})
with
(x_{0},x_{1})=e
and
(x_{\ell-1},x_{\ell})=e^{\prime}
.
We first compare the variations of
f
and
f_{\star}
across edges.

Lemma 2
.

For each
e=(x,y)\in E
with
f_{\star}(x)\leq f_{\star}(y)
, there is
e^{\prime}=(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\in E
so that

\displaystyle r^{-d(e,e^{\prime})}f(x^{\prime})\ \leq

\displaystyle f_{\star}(x)\ \leq\ f_{\star}(y)

\displaystyle\leq\ r^{-d(e,e^{\prime})}f(y^{\prime}).

(14)

Proof.

If
f_{\star}(y)=f(y)
, then we simply choose
e^{\prime}=e
and (
14
) trivially holds. If on the contrary
f_{\star}(y)<f(y)
, then we take
e^{\prime}=(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})
where
y^{\prime}
is such that
f_{\star}(y)=r^{-d(y,y^{\prime})}f(y^{\prime})
, and where
x^{\prime}
is the penultimate vertex on a geodesic from
y
to
y^{\prime}
. We then have
d(e,e^{\prime})=d(y,y^{\prime})=1+d(y,x^{\prime})\geq d(x,x^{\prime})
. Thus, the last inequality in (
14
) holds with equality, while the definition of
f_{\star}
ensures that
f_{\star}(x)\geq r^{-d(x,x^{\prime})}f(x^{\prime})\geq r^{-d(e,e^{\prime})}f(x^{\prime})
.
∎

We now use this local comparison to estimate how the global quantities
\mathcal{E}(\sqrt{f},\sqrt{f})
and
\mathcal{E}(f,\log{f})
change upon replacing
f
with
f_{\star}
. For
(x,y)\in E
, we introduce the short-hand

\displaystyle c(x,y)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\pi(x)Q(x,y).

Lemma 3
.

We have

\mathcal{E}(\sqrt{f_{\star}},\sqrt{f_{\star}})\leq\kappa\,\mathcal{E}(\sqrt{f},\sqrt{f})
and
\mathcal{E}(f_{\star},\log f_{\star})\leq\kappa\,\mathcal{E}(f,\log f)
, where

\displaystyle\kappa

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\max_{e^{\prime}\in E}\left\{\frac{1}{c(e^{\prime})}\sum_{e\in E}c(e)r^{-d(e,e^{\prime})}\right\}.

(15)

Proof.

Let
\nabla f(e):=f(y)-f(x)
denote the discrete gradient of
f
across an edge
e=(x,y)
. To each edge
e\in E
, Lemma
2
associates a new edge
e^{\prime}\in E
such that

\displaystyle\left(\nabla\sqrt{f_{\star}}\right)_{+}(e)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle r^{-d(e,e^{\prime})}\left(\nabla\sqrt{f}\right)_{+}(e^{\prime}),

where
h_{+}=\max(h,0)
denotes the positive part of
h
. Consequently, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(\sqrt{f_{\star}},\sqrt{f_{\star}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{e\in E}c(e)\left(\nabla\sqrt{f_{\star}}\right)^{2}_{+}(e)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{e,e^{\prime}\in E}c(e)r^{-d(e,e^{\prime})}\left(\nabla\sqrt{f}\right)^{2}_{+}(e^{\prime})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\kappa\sum_{e^{\prime}\in E}c(e^{\prime})\left(\nabla\sqrt{f}\right)^{2}_{+}(e^{\prime})\ =\ \kappa\mathcal{E}(\sqrt{f},\sqrt{f}),

as desired. The second claim is obtained in exactly the same way.
∎

To obtain Proposition
1
, it finally remains to estimate the constant
\kappa
defined at (
15
).

Lemma 4
.

Choosing
r
as in (
13
) ensures that
\kappa\leq 4/3
.

Proof.

If
e,e^{\prime}\in E
satisfy
d(e,e^{\prime})=1
, then we can write
e=(x,y)
and
e^{\prime}=(y,z)
. Using reversibility and the definition of
p
at (
4
), we have

\displaystyle c(e)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\pi(x)Q(x,y)\ =\ \pi(y)Q(y,x)\ \leq\ \pi(y)Q(y)\ \leq\ p^{-1}\pi(y)Q(y,z)\ =\ p^{-1}c(e^{\prime}).

By an immediate induction, we deduce that
c(e)\leq p^{-d(e,e^{\prime})}c(e^{\prime})
for all
e,e^{\prime}\in E
. Thus,

\displaystyle\kappa

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\max_{e^{\prime}\in E}\left\{\sum_{e\in E}(rp)^{-d(e,e^{\prime})}\right\}\ \leq\ \sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\Delta^{k}(rp)^{-k},

where
\Delta
denotes the maximum degree of the graph
(\mathcal{X},E)
. But
\Delta\leq p^{-1}
, because from every vertex, the outgoing transition probabilities are at least
p
and must sum to
1
. Thus,
\kappa\leq\sum_{k}(p^{2}r)^{-k}=4/3
, thanks to our choice for
r
.
∎

2.3
Comparing entropies (Proposition
2
)

As in
[
31
]
, we use the variational characterization of entropy
[
4
]
: for any
g\colon\mathcal{X}\to(0,\infty)
,

\displaystyle{\mathrm{Ent}}(g)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\max\left\{{\mathbf{E}}[gh]\colon h\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{X}},{\mathbf{E}}[e^{h}]\leq 1\right\}.

(16)

We start with an elementary lemma, which only uses the fact that
f_{\star}\geq f
.

Lemma 5
.

We have

5{\mathrm{Ent}}(f)\leq 6{\mathrm{Ent}}(f_{\star})+(6\log 6){\mathbf{E}}[f_{\star}-f]
.

Proof.

Upon multiplying
f
(hence also
f_{\star}
) by a constant, we may assume without loss of generality that
{\mathbf{E}}[f]=1
. Then, the function
h:=\log\left(\frac{1+5f}{6}\right)
satisfies
{\mathbf{E}}[e^{h}]=1
so (
16
) yields

\displaystyle{\mathrm{Ent}}(f_{\star})

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle{\mathbf{E}}[f_{\star}h]\ =\ {\mathbf{E}}[fh]+{\mathbf{E}}[(f_{\star}-f)h].

The claim now readily follows from the pointwise bounds
h\geq\frac{5}{6}{\log f}
and
h\geq\log\frac{1}{6}
.
∎

In view of this lemma, Proposition
2
boils down to the following result.

Lemma 6
.

With
r
as in (
13
), we have
(3\log 6){\mathbf{E}}[f_{\star}-f]\leq{\mathrm{Ent}}(f)
.

Proof.

For each
x\in\mathcal{X}
, let us choose a state
T(x)\in\mathcal{X}
that achieves the maximum in the definition of
f_{\star}(x)
(breaking ties arbitrarily), i.e.

\displaystyle f_{\star}(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle r^{-d(x,T(x))}f\left(T(x)\right).

Thus,
T(x)=x
if and only if
f_{\star}(x)=f(x)
. Note that if we had
f(T(x))<f_{\star}(T(x))
, then

\displaystyle f_{\star}(x)

\displaystyle<

\displaystyle r^{-d(x,T(x))}f_{\star}\left(T(x)\right)\ =\ r^{-d(x,T(x))}r^{-d(T(x),T^{2}(x))}f\left(T^{2}(x)\right)\ \leq\ r^{-d(x,T^{2}(x))}f\left(T^{2}(x)\right),

which would contradict the maximal definition of
f_{\star}(x)
. Thus, we must in fact have
f(T(x))=f_{\star}(T(x))
, or equivalently,
T^{2}(x)=T(x)
. This shows that
T(A)=A^{c}
, where

\displaystyle A

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\{x\in\mathcal{X}\colon T(x)\neq x\}\ =\ \{x\in\mathcal{X}\colon f(x)\neq f_{\star}(x)\}.

Now, coming back to our goal, let us write

\displaystyle{\mathbf{E}}[f_{\star}-f]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{x\in A}\pi(x)(f_{\star}(x)-f(x))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{x\in A}\pi(x)f(T(x))r^{-d(x,T(x))}-\sum_{x\in A}\pi(x)f(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{y\in T(A)}\pi(y)f(y)h(y)-\sum_{x\in A}\pi(x)f(x),

where for
y\in T(A)
, we have introduced the short-hand

\displaystyle h(y)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\sum_{x\in T^{-1}(\{y\})}\frac{\pi(x)}{\pi(y)}r^{-d(x,y)}.

Recalling that
T(A)=A^{c}
, we may set
h=-1
on
A
to rewrite the previous computation as

\displaystyle{\mathbf{E}}[f_{\star}-f]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\mathbf{E}}[fh].

In light of the variational characterization of entropy (
16
) (with
3h\log 6
instead of
h
), it remains to check that
{\mathbf{E}}[6^{3h}]\leq 1
. As in the proof of Lemma
4
, our choice
r=4p^{-2}
easily ensures that
h\leq\sum_{k=1}(p^{2}r)^{-k}=1/3
, so that
6^{3h}\leq 1+15h
on
A^{c}
. Recalling the definition of
h
, we deduce that

\displaystyle{\mathbf{E}}\left[(6^{3h}-1){\bf 1}_{A^{c}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 15{\mathbf{E}}[h{\bf 1}_{A^{c}}]\ =\ 15\sum_{x\in A}\pi(x)r^{-d(x,T(x))}\ \leq\ \frac{15}{16}\pi(A),

because
r\geq 16
. On the other hand,
{\mathbf{E}}\left[(6^{3h}-1){\bf 1}_{A}\right]=(6^{-3}-1)\pi(A)\leq-\frac{15}{16}\pi(A)
.
∎
