On universal sets and sumsets
I.D. Shkredov

Abstract




Annotation.

We study the concept of universal sets from the additive–combinatorial point of view.
Among other results we obtain some applications of this type of uniformity to sets avoiding solutions to linear equations, and
get
an optimal upper bound for the covering number of general sumsets.

1
Introduction

Let
{\mathbf{G}}
be an abelian group.
Given two sets
A,B\subseteq{\mathbf{G}}
, define
the
sumset

of
A
and
B
as

A+B:=\{a+b~:~a\in{A},\,b\in{B}\}\,.

In a similar way we define the
difference sets
and the
higher sumsets
, e.g.,
2A-A
is
A+A-A
.
The study of the structure of sumsets is a fundamental problem in classical additive combinatorics
[
28
]
, and this relatively new branch of mathematics provides us with a number of excellent results on this object.
For example,
the Plünnecke–Ruzsa inequality (see, e.g.,
[
19
]
or
[
28
]
) says that for any positive integers
n
and
m

the following holds

|nA-mA|\leqslant\left(\frac{|A+A|}{|A|}\right)^{n+m}\cdot|A|\,.

(1)

Thus, bound (
1
) connects the cardinality of the original
set
A
and its higher sumsets
nA-mA
.
In this paper we do not consider cardinalities of sumsets, but rather deal with a new characteristic of a numerical set, namely universality and study how this characteristic changes from addition.
Namely, a set
A\subseteq{\mathbf{G}}
is called the
k
–universal
set if for any
x_{1},\dots,x_{k}\in{\mathbf{G}}
there exists
s\in{\mathbf{G}}
such that
x_{1}+s,\dots,x_{k}+s\in A
.
The term “universal set” was coined in
[
2
]
and
some applications to the Kakeya problem can be found in
[
12
]
.
If we denote the maximal
k
with the above property by
{\rm un}(A)
, then our first result says that

{\rm un}(A+B)\geqslant{\rm un}(A){\rm un}(B)\,.

(2)

The estimate (
2
) is optimal and it shows, in particular, that the characteristic
{\rm un}(\cdot)
grows rapidly upon addition.
A “statistical” or “quantitative” version of inequality (
2
) can be found in Section
3
and can be considered as a generalization of the Ruzsa triangle inequality
[
28
]
.
The author hopes that such characteristics are interesting in its own right and can find application in further additive–combinatorial problems.
In the proofs we make extensive use of the concept of higher sumsets/higher energies, see
[
22
]
and therefore
the main idea
of obtaining inequalities as (
2
)
is to move into higher dimensions.

Any
k
–universal set has some random properties; universality can be thought of as a combinatorial version of ordinary uniform sets (see, e.g.,
[
28
]
).
For example, we show in Section
5
that any
k
–universal subset
U
of a finite group
{\mathbf{G}}
intersects an arbitrary sumset
A+B
, where
|A|=\alpha|{\mathbf{G}}|
,
|B|=\beta|{\mathbf{G}}|
and
\alpha\gg\frac{\log(1/\beta)}{k}
, say.
In particular,
U
intersects any sufficiently long arithmetic progression or Bohr set.
Even stronger properties of universal sets can be found in Sections
4
,
5
and
7
.
On the other hand, universality is much weaker property than classical uniformity (for example,
any
set
U\subseteq{\mathbf{G}}
of size
|U|>(1-1/k)|{\mathbf{G}}|
is
k
–universal) and this gives the concept considerable flexibility. Using this flexibility, we obtain several applications.

In our first application we study sets
A\subseteq{\mathbf{G}}
,
|{\mathbf{G}}|<\infty
,
|A|=\delta|{\mathbf{G}}|
avoiding solutions to general linear equations

\alpha_{1}x_{1}+\dots+\alpha_{n}x_{n}=\beta\,,

(3)

where
n\geqslant 3
and
\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n}
are coprime to
|{\mathbf{G}}|
.
Denote by
f_{A}(x)
the balanced function of the set
A
(all required definitions can be found in Section
2
).
In the case of
n=3
, in the first part of the Kelly–Meka proof
[
10
]
it was shown that the so–called
\mathsf{E}_{k}
–norm
f_{A}
,
k=O(\log(2/\delta))
controls the number of solutions to the equation (
3
)
in the sense that if
\|f_{A}\|_{\mathsf{E}_{k}}
is small, then (
3
) has
\Omega(\delta^{3}|{\mathbf{G}}|^{2})
solutions. For larger
n
equation (
3
) was studied in
[
8
]
,
[
13
]
,
[
21
]
and in other papers.
Using the universality one can, in particular, easily show that for large
n
one needs
\mathsf{E}_{k_{*}}
–norm of
f_{A}
with much smaller
k_{*}=k_{*}(n)
to control the number of the solutions to the equation (
3
).

Let

The second area of our applications concerns the so–called covering numbers.
Let
{\mathbf{G}}
be a finite abelian group and
A\subseteq{\mathbf{G}}
be a set.
The
covering number
of
A
is defined as

{\rm cov}(A)=\min_{X\subseteq{\mathbf{G}}}\{|X|~:~A+X={\mathbf{G}}\}\,.

(6)

In other words, the covering number of
A
is the minimum number of translates

A
required to cover the group.
In such a generalized form this concept can be found in

[
4
]
(see also previous papers
[
15
]
,
[
20
]
), which contains many references to this issue. The case of an infinite group
{\mathbf{G}}
(e.g.
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
or
\mathbb{R}^{d}
) is different, and the interested reader may refer to, for example, the classic book
[
17
]
.

In this generality, the covering number
{\rm cov}(A)
can be considered as a characteristic of the randomness of
A
.
Indeed, for any set
A
one has
{\rm cov}(A)\geqslant|{\mathbf{G}}|/|A|
and the last bound is attained if, say,
{\mathbf{G}}=\mathbb{Z}/N\mathbb{Z}
,
|A|
divides
N
and
A
is an arithmetic progression
\{1,\dots,|A|\}
.
On the other hand,
using the simplest greedy algorithm, one can show (see, e.g.,
[
4
, Corollary 3.2]
) that

\frac{|{\mathbf{G}}|}{|A|}\leqslant{\rm cov}(A)\leqslant\frac{|{\mathbf{G}}|}{|A|}\left(\log|A|+1\right)<\frac{|{\mathbf{G}}|}{|A|}\log|{\mathbf{G}}|\,.

(7)

The upper bound in (
7
) is tight and attained at a random set
A
.
Thus one would expect that
{\rm cov}(A)
should be close to the lower bound in (
7
) for structured sets as arithmetic progressions and should be close to the upper bound if
A
has some random properties.

A classic result of additive combinatorics, namely the Ruzsa covering lemma (see
[
18
]
and
[
28

be a finite abelian group,

be a set,

.

3
On universal sets

In this section we discuss
properties of universal sets and obtain a generalization of Ruzsa’s triangle inequality.
Having a set
A\subseteq{\mathbf{G}}
we put

{\rm un}(A)={\rm un}(A,{\mathbf{G}})=\max\{k\geqslant 1~:~A^{k}-\Delta_{k}({\mathbf{G}})={\mathbf{G}}^{k}\}\,.

(25)

Alternatively, since
A-\Delta_{1}({\mathbf{G}})={\mathbf{G}}
, we have in view of formula (
17
) of Lemma
17
that

{\rm un}(A)=\max\{1,\max\{k\geqslant 2~:~A^{k-1}-\Delta_{k-1}(A)={\mathbf{G}}^{k-1}\}\}\,.

(26)

For example,
{\rm un}(A)=2
iff
A-A={\mathbf{G}}
.
If there is no such
k
, then we define
{\rm un}(A)=1
appealing to the first definition (
25
).
The statement
A^{k-1}-\Delta_{k-1}(A)={\mathbf{G}}^{k-1}
is equivalent to say that
A
is a
k
–universal set,
that is for any
z_{1},\dots,z_{k}\in{\mathbf{G}}
there is
w\in{\mathbf{G}}
such that
z_{1}+w,\dots,z_{k}+w\in A
.
In particular, any set is
1
–universal and that is why we always have
{\rm un}(A)\geqslant 1
.
It is easy to see, that for any
A\neq{\mathbf{G}}
one has
{\rm un}(A)<\infty
and, by convention, we put

{\rm un}({\mathbf{G}})=\infty
.
Also, if
B\subseteq A
, then
{\rm un}(B)\leqslant{\rm un}(A)
.
Finally, we have

{\rm un}(A)={\rm un}(A+x)={\rm un}(\lambda\cdot A)

for any
x\in{\mathbf{G}}
and
\lambda\in\mathbb{Z}
such that
(\lambda,|{\mathbf{G}}|)=1
.
It is easy to observe (take a random intersection
A_{X}
) that a trivial upper bound for
{\rm un}(A)
, where
|A|=\delta N
is

{\rm un}(A)\leqslant\frac{\log N}{\log(1/\delta)}

(27)

and this bound is tight in general, see Section
5
.

In paper
[
22
, inequality (29)]
it was proved, in particular, that
for any
k
–universal set
U
and an arbitrary set

one has

|U+S|\geqslant N\cdot\left(\frac{|S|}{N}\right)^{1/k}\,.

(28)

The last bound is a consequence of the formula
U^{k-1}-\Delta_{k-1}(U)={\mathbf{G}}^{k-1}
and inequality (

, namely,

|S|N^{k-1}=|S||U^{k-1}-\Delta_{k-1}(U)|\leqslant|U^{k}-\Delta_{k}(S)|\leqslant|U-S|^{k}\,.

(29)

In particular, taking any

with
|S|=1
, we obtain

|U|\geqslant N^{1-1/k}\,.

(30)

We want to study the connection between sumsets and universal sets.
Probably, the first result in this direction

Definition 13

Let

\Delta_{m_{1},\dots,m_{n};n}(B)=\{(\Delta_{m_{1}}(b_{1}),\dots,\Delta_{m_{n}}(b_{n}))~:~(b_{1},\dots,b_{n})\in B\}\subseteq{\mathbf{G}}^{m}\,.

|\Delta_{m_{1},\dots,m_{n};n}(B)|=|B|

|Q|^{2m}|B|^{2}\leqslant|Q^{m}-\Delta_{m_{1},\dots,m_{n};n}(B)|

\times\sum_{b=(b_{1},\dots,b_{n}),\,b^{\prime}=(b^{\prime}_{1},\dots,b^{\prime}_{n})\in B}(Q\circ Q)^{m_{1}}(b_{1}-b^{\prime}_{1})\dots(Q\circ Q)^{m_{n}}(b_{n}-b^{\prime}_{n})\,,

4
Universality and sets avoiding solutions to linear equations

The
first
result of this section shows that universal sets (or, more generally, sets with large
{\mathcal{U}}_{n}
) always contain solutions of all linear equations. It can be considered as a combinatorial reformulation of the first part of Kelley–Meka proof
[
10
]
.

Proposition 17

Let
n\geqslant 3
be an integer,
A\subseteq{\mathbf{G}}
be a set,
|A|=\delta N
and
A
has no solutions to the equation

\alpha_{1}x_{1}+\dots+\alpha_{n}x_{n}=\beta\,,\quad\quad x_{j}\in A,\,j\in[n]\,,

(50)

where
\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n}
are coprime to
|{\mathbf{G}}|
.
Then for
n=3
one has

{\rm un}(A)\leqslant\delta^{-1}\log(1/\delta)\,,

(51)

and for
n>3
, we obtain

{\rm un}(A)\leqslant(2\log(1/\delta))^{\frac{1}{[n/2]-1}}\,.

(52)

Finally,
one has

\overline{{\mathcal{U}}}_{m}(A)<7/8
for all
m
greater than

(3\log(1/\delta))^{\frac{1}{[n/2]-1}}\,.

(53)

P r o o f.

By Corollary (
34
) we know that for
U=\alpha_{1}\cdot A+\dots+\alpha_{n-2}\cdot A

one has
{\rm un}(U)\geqslant{\rm un}^{n-2}(A)
.
Since the set
A
has no solutions to (
50
),
it follows that

N-|A|\geqslant|U+\alpha_{n-1}\cdot A|>N(1-{\rm un}^{-1}(U)\log(1/\delta))

and in the case
n=3
the result follows.
For
n>3
let us write
n=l+l^{\prime}+2
, where
l^{\prime}\geqslant l=[n/2]-1
and define

U_{1}=\alpha_{1}\cdot A+\dots+\alpha_{l}\cdot A
,
U_{2}=\alpha_{l+3}\cdot A+\dots+\alpha_{n}\cdot A
.
Now observe that the sets
\alpha_{l+1}\cdot A+U_{1}
,
-(\alpha_{l+2}\cdot A+U_{2})

both have sizes greater than
N/2
and thus they intersect.
Thus
(
52
) follows.

Finally, assume that
\overline{{\mathcal{U}}}_{m}(A)\geqslant 7/8
, where
m
is given by (
53
).
Using inequality (
47
), combining with (
36
), we obtain

|\alpha_{l+1}\cdot A+U_{1}|\geqslant\delta^{1/m^{l}}\overline{{\mathcal{U}}}_{m^{l}}(U_{1})N\geqslant\delta^{1/m^{l}}\overline{{\mathcal{U}}}_{m}(A)^{\frac{m^{l}-1}{m^{l}-m^{l-1}}}N\geqslant\delta^{1/m^{l}}\overline{{\mathcal{U}}}_{m}(A)^{\frac{m}{m-1}}N\,.

and similar for
|\alpha_{l+2}\cdot A+U_{2}|
.
Therefore by our choice of
m
, we get

|\alpha_{l+1}\cdot A+U_{1}|>3N/4\cdot(1-m^{-l}\log(1/\delta))\geqslant N/2\,.

This completes the proof.

\hfill\Box

In the next lemma we show that the uniformity in the Kelly–Meka sense implies universality. Of course, one can relax condition (
54
) using the Hölder inequality but we do not need this step.

Lemma 18

Let
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
be a real number,
k
be a positive integer,
A\subseteq{\mathbf{G}}
be a set,
|A|=\delta N
and

\|f_{A}\|^{2l}_{\mathsf{E}_{l}}=\sum_{x}(f_{A}\circ f_{A})^{l}(x)\leqslant\varepsilon^{2l}\delta^{2l}N^{l+1}\,,\quad\quad\forall l\in[k]\,.

(54)

Then

\overline{{\mathcal{U}}}_{k}(A)>\frac{1}{1+\varepsilon^{2}}\,.

(55)

P r o o f.

We have
(A\circ A)(x)=\delta^{2}N+(f_{A}\circ f_{A})(x)
and hence

\mathsf{E}_{k}(A)=\sum_{x}(A\circ A)^{k}(x)=\delta^{2k}N^{k+1}+\sum_{l=2}^{k}\binom{k}{l}(\delta^{2}N)^{k-l}\sum_{x}(f_{A}\circ f_{A})^{l}(x)=\delta^{2k}N^{k+1}+\mathcal{E}\,.

Using our assumption (
54
),
one obtains

\mathcal{E}\leqslant\delta^{2k}N^{k+1}\sum_{l=2}^{k}\binom{k}{l}\varepsilon^{2l}<\delta^{2k}N^{k+1}((1+\varepsilon^{2})^{k}-1)\,.

Combining the last two bounds with the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality (see, e.g., (
41
)), we get

|A|^{2k}\leqslant\mathsf{E}_{k}(A)|A^{k-1}-\Delta_{k-1}(A)|<\delta^{2k}N^{k}(1+\varepsilon^{2})^{k}|A^{k}-\Delta_{k}({\mathbf{G}})|\,.

It follows that
\overline{{\mathcal{U}}}_{k}(A)>(1+\varepsilon^{2})^{-1}
.
This completes the proof.

\hfill\Box

5
On covering numbers

Let
{\mathbf{G}}
be an abelian group and
A\subseteq{\mathbf{G}}
be a set.
Further put
\Omega=\Omega(A)=A^{c}

We have

{\rm cov}(A)={\rm cov}^{+}(A)=\min_{X\subseteq{\mathbf{G}}}\{|X|~:~A+X={\mathbf{G}}\}=\min_{X\subseteq{\mathbf{G}}}\{|X|~:~\Omega_{X}=\emptyset\}\,.

(60)

In other words, for any
Y

|Y|\leqslant{\rm cov}(A)-1:=k

\Omega_{Y}\neq\emptyset
.
The last statement is equivalent to
\Omega^{k-1}-\Delta_{k-1}(\Omega)={\mathbf{G}}^{k-1}
and thus
\Omega
is a

–universal set.
In particular, we have our basic formula (
10
) and using (
27

), we obtain the second upper bound of (
7
).
Also,
formulae (
10
), (

) give us

({\rm cov}(\Omega(A))-1)^{n}+1\leqslant{\rm cov}(\Omega(nA))\,.

(61)

More generally, for any set

.

{\rm cov}(A)={\rm cov}(A;{\mathbf{G}})\leqslant{\rm cov}(A;E)\cdot{\rm cov}(E;{\mathbf{G}})\,.

(63)

\Omega(A+X)=\Omega(A)_{X}\quad\quad\mbox{ and }\quad\quad\Omega(A_{X})=\Omega(A)+X\,,

(64)

it follows that

.

.
Another consequence of (

)

) is

{\rm un}(A+X)={\rm cov}(\Omega(A)_{X})-1\quad\quad\mbox{ and }\quad\quad{\rm un}(A_{X})={\rm cov}(\Omega(A)+X)-1\,.

(65)

In a similar way, using the second formula of (

), and

Lemma 21

Let

P r o o f.

\hfill\Box

Now we are ready to obtain

Theorem 22

Let

P r o o f.

6
Some examples and the sum–product phenomenon

Now
we
give
several
examples of sets from
\mathbb{F}_{p}
(and general abelian groups) having large covering numbers
{\rm cov}^{+},{\rm cov}^{\times}
.
We study these quantities simultaneously, thus some statements in this section
can be considered as part of the sum–product phenomenon.
The first results in this direction were obtained in
[
26
]
, where we derived (see
[
26
, Proposition 5.2]
) the following simple sum–product–type result

{\rm cov}^{\times}(A-A)\leqslant{\rm cov}^{+}(A)\,.

Our first example is well–known and uses the random construction.

Example 28

Let
A\subseteq{\mathbf{G}}
or
A\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{p}
be a random set, each element of
A
is taken independently at random with a certain probability
\delta
.
Then
[
4
, Theorem 4.1]
the following holds
{\rm cov}^{+}(A)\sim\delta^{-1}\log N
and
{\rm cov}^{\times}(A)\sim\delta^{-1}\log p
.
Generally, one can take a random subset of an interval
I
in
{\mathbf{G}}
and obtain a similar bound.
Thus, sets with small sumset can have very large covering number.
Finally, using the first inequality from
(
67
)
one can see that
{\rm cov}^{+}(A+X),{\rm cov}^{\times}(AX)\geqslant(\delta|X|)^{-1}\log p
for any set
X\subseteq{\mathbf{G}}
.

Unlike the previous example, the following set is deterministic and has large covering number.

Example 29

Let
{\mathbf{G}}=\mathbb{F}_{p}
, where
p
be a prime number and
\mathcal{R}
be the set of quadratic residue in
\mathbb{F}_{p}
.
Then
{\rm cov}^{+}(\mathcal{R})
is at least
\left(\frac{1}{2}+o(1)\right)\log_{2}p
see, e.g.,
[
22
, Proposition 14]
.
The same method gives
{\rm cov}^{\times}(\mathcal{R}+s)\geqslant\left(\frac{1}{2}+o(1)\right)\log_{2}p
for any non–zero
s\in\mathbb{F}_{p}
.
In a similar way, it is easy to check that
\overline{{\mathcal{U}}}_{k}(\mathcal{R})=1-o(1)
for
k=(1-o(1))\log_{2}p
and in view of
(
27
) this estimate is optimal.

Let us generalize the previous example.

Proposition 30

Let
p
be a prime number,
A,B\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{p}
be sets,
|A|=\alpha p
,
|B|=\beta p
such that
A/B\neq\mathbb{F}_{p}
and
s\in\mathbb{F}^{*}_{p}
.
Then both

{\rm cov}^{+}(\mathbb{F}_{p}\setminus(A/B))
and
{\rm cov}^{\times}(\mathbb{F}_{p}\setminus(A/B)+s)
are
at least
\frac{\log p}{\log(1/\alpha\beta)}(1-o(1))
.

Similarly, if
A+B\neq\mathbb{F}_{p}
,
then

{\rm cov}^{+}(\mathbb{F}_{p}\setminus(A+B)^{-1})
and
{\rm cov}^{\times}(\mathbb{F}_{p}\setminus((A+B)^{-1}+s))

are both at least
\frac{\log p}{\log(1/\alpha\beta)}(1-o(1))
.

P r o o f.

We start with the first part and
let us prove
k:={\rm cov}^{+}(\Omega(A/B))\geqslant\frac{\log p}{\log(1/\alpha\beta)}(1-o(1))
, the second inequality follows similarly.
Let
f(x)
counts the number of
solutions to
x=a/b
, where
a\in A
,
b\in B
.
Using the multiplicative Fourier transform, the Parseval identity and Weil’s bound for the sums over multiplicative characters, we get for different
x_{j}
,
j\in[k]
that

\sum_{z}f(z+x_{1})\dots f(z+x_{k})=\frac{1}{(p-1)^{k}}\sum_{\chi_{1},\dots,\chi_{k}}\widehat{f}(\chi_{1})\dots\widehat{f}(\chi_{k})\sum_{z}\chi_{1}(z+x_{1})\dots\chi_{k}(z+x_{k})

>\frac{(|A||B|)^{k}}{(p-1)^{k-1}}-(k+1)\sqrt{p}(|A||B|)^{k/2}>0\,,

(86)

provided
k=\frac{\log p}{\log(1/\alpha\beta)}(1-o(1))
.
Inequality (
86
) implies that
(A/B)_{x_{1},\dots,x_{k}}\neq\emptyset
.

Now let us obtain the second part of our result and again it is suffices to estimate the quantity
k:={\rm cov}^{+}(\Omega(A+B)^{-1})
, since the second bound follows in a similar way.
Putting
f=A*B
and applying the additive Fourier transform, we obtain

\sum_{z}f((z+x_{1})^{-1})\dots f((z+x_{k})^{-1})=\frac{1}{p^{k}}\sum_{r_{1},\dots,r_{k}}\widehat{f}(r_{1})\dots\widehat{f}(r_{k})\sum_{z}e(r_{1}(z+x_{1})^{-1}+\dots+r_{k}(z+x_{k})^{-1})\,.

One can check (calculating the Wronskian, for example) that the functions
(z+x_{1})^{-1},\dots,(z+x_{k})^{-1}
are linearly independent.
After that we use the Weil bound again
as well as
the previous argument.
In the case of
{\rm cov}^{\times}(\mathbb{F}_{p}\setminus((A+B)^{-1}+s))
our functions are
f((x_{j}z-s)^{-1})
and, thus we can actually repeat our approach.
This completes the proof.

\hfill\Box

7
Concluding remarks

Universal sets also have other very strong properties. For example, let
U\subseteq{\mathbf{G}}
be a (one–dimensional)
k
–universal set.
Then by estimate (
28
) we know that
U
has the repulsion property, namely, for
an arbitrary set
S
one has

|U+S|\geqslant N\cdot\left(\frac{|S|}{N}\right)^{1/k}\,.

(89)

Now let
m
be a positive integer,
U_{1},\dots,U_{m}
be
k
–universal sets and let us consider the following multi–dimensional set
\mathcal{U}:=U_{1}\times\dots\times U_{m}\subseteq{\mathbf{G}}^{m}
.
One can check
that

\mathcal{U}^{k}-\Delta_{k}({\mathbf{G}}^{m})=(U^{k}_{1}-\Delta_{k}({\mathbf{G}}))\times\dots\times(U^{k}_{m}-\Delta_{k}({\mathbf{G}}))={\mathbf{G}}^{mk}

and thus
\mathcal{U}
is a
k
–universal set as well.
One can rewrite the last formula as

{\rm un}(U_{1}\times\dots\times U_{m},{\mathbf{G}}^{m})=\min_{j\in[m]}{\rm un}(U_{j},{\mathbf{G}})\,.

(90)

In particular, we have an analogue of inequality (
89
) for the set
\mathcal{U}
.
It is easy to see that bound (
89
)
can be generalized and refined for the sets of the form
\Delta_{m}(S)
.
Indeed,
let
k-m<k_{*}\leqslant k
be the maximal number not exceeding
k
such that
m
divides
k_{*}
.
Write
d=k_{*}/m\in\mathbb{Z}
.
Then as in (
29
), we get

|S|N^{k_{*}-1}=|S||U^{k_{*}-1}-\Delta_{k_{*}-1}(U)|\leqslant|U^{k_{*}}-\Delta_{k_{*}}(S)|\leqslant|U^{m}-\Delta_{m}(S)|^{d}\,.

Thus if
|S|=\sigma N
, then

|U^{m}-\Delta_{m}(S)|\geqslant N^{m}\sigma^{1/d}>N^{m}(1-d^{-1}\log(1/\sigma))\,.

This argument gives us the following result about universal sets and hence about the complements of sets with the large covering number, see examples of Section
6
.
In particular, it is possible to obtain an analogue of Corollary
80
for higher sumsets.

Corollary 38

Let
U\subseteq{\mathbf{G}}
be a
k
–universal set, and
S\subseteq{\mathbf{G}}
be an arbitrary set,
|S|=\sigma N
.
Then

|U^{m}-\Delta_{m}(S)|>N^{m}\left(1-\frac{m}{k-m+1}\log(1/\sigma)\right)\,.

(91)

In particular, for any
\mathcal{A}\subseteq{\mathbf{G}}^{m}
,
|\mathcal{A}|=\alpha N^{m}
one has

(\mathcal{A}+\Delta_{m}(S))\cap U^{m}\neq\emptyset\,,

provided
\alpha\geqslant\frac{m}{k-m+1}\log(1/\sigma)
.

Thanks to formulae (
13
) of Lemma
5
we have, in particular, that

\sum_{x\in U-S}|U-(S\cap(U-x))|=|U^{2}-\Delta_{2}(S)|>N^{2}\left(1-\frac{2\log(1/\sigma)}{k-1}\right)\,,

and

\sum_{x\in U-S}|U-(S\cap(U-x))|^{2}\geqslant\sum_{x\in U-S}|U^{2}-\Delta_{2}(S\cap(U-x))|=|U^{3}-\Delta_{3}(S)|>N^{3}\left(1-\frac{3\log(1/\sigma)}{k-2}\right)\,.

Thus, for any sufficiently dense set
S\subseteq{\mathbf{G}}
there are many shifts of the universal set
U
such that
U-(S\cap(U-x))
is close to the
entire
group
{\mathbf{G}}
.

It would be interesting to find further properties of universal sets.
