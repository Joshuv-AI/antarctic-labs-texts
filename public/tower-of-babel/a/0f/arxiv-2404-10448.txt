On estimation of skewed stable linear regression
Eitaro Kawamo, Hiroki Masuda

Abstract
. We study the parameter estimation method for linear regression models with possibly skewed stable distributed errors. Our estimation procedure consists of two stages: first, for the regression coefficients, the Cauchy quasi-maximum likelihood estimator (CQMLE) is considered after taking the differences to remove the skewness of noise, and we prove its asymptotic normality and tail-probability estimate; second, as for stable-distribution parameters, we consider the moment estimators based on the symmetrized and centered residuals and prove their n \sqrt{n} -consistency. To derive the n \sqrt{n} -consistency, we essentially used the tail-probability estimate of the CQMLE. The proposed estimation procedure has a very low computational load and is much less time-consuming compared with the maximum-likelihood estimator. Further, our estimator can be effectively used as an initial value for the numerical optimization of the log-likelihood.

Introduction

1.1.
Objective and background

Let
S^{0}_{\alpha}(\beta,\sigma,\delta)
be the stable distribution with the continuous parameterization, which is given by the characteristic function

(1.1)

\varphi(u;\vartheta):=\exp\left\{i\delta u-(\sigma|u|)^{\alpha}\left(1+i\beta{\rm sgn}(u)\tan\dfrac{\alpha\pi}{2}\left(|\sigma u|^{1-\alpha}-1\right)\right)\right\},

where
\vartheta:=(\alpha,\beta,\sigma,\delta)\in(0,2]\times[-1,1]\times(0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}
.
This is called Nolan’s
0
-parametrization
[
13

Note that the possibly skewed Cauchy case is then defined to be the limiting case for
\alpha\to 1
through
\lim_{\alpha\to 1}\tan(\alpha\pi/2)(|u|^{1-\alpha}-1)=(2/\pi)\log|u|
:

(1.2)

\varphi(u;1,\beta,\sigma,\delta)=\lim_{\alpha\to 1}\varphi(u;\vartheta)=\exp\left\{i\delta u-\sigma|u|\left(1+i\beta\dfrac{2}{\pi}{\rm sgn}(u)\log(\sigma|u|)\right)\right\}.

It is known that
S_{\alpha}^{0}(\beta,\sigma,\delta)
for

admits an everywhere positive smooth Lebesgue density;
we refer to
[

and
[

We denote by

(1.3)

\phi_{\alpha,\beta}(x):=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int e^{-ixu}\varphi(u;\alpha,\beta,1,0)du.

We consider the linear regression model

where

\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2},\dots\sim\text{i.i.d.}~S_{\alpha}^{0}(\beta,1,0).

Here and in what follows the dot denotes the usual inner product.

We are concerned with estimation of the unknown parameter
\theta:=(\alpha,\beta,\sigma,\mu)\in\Theta
from a sample
\{(X_{j},Y_{j})\}_{j=1}^{n}
. Throughout this paper, we use

to denote the parameter space for the full parameter vector

(1.6)

(0,2)\times(-1,1)\times(0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}^{q},

and also it is assumed that there exists a true value

We denote by

the distribution of

The stable (regression) model has various applications such as electric-price estimation through the application of stable alpha regressions

1.2.
Outline and summary

In Section
2
, we estimate the regression coefficient by the CQMLE without assuming that the errors are stable-distributed. We prove the asymptotic normality and the tail-probability estimate of the normalized CQMLE.
In Section
3
, we estimate the stable distribution parameter
(\alpha,\beta,\sigma)
by the method of moments through the residual sequence based on the CQMLE and showed the
\sqrt{n}
-consistency of
(\hat{\alpha}_{n},\hat{\beta}_{n},\hat{\sigma}_{n},\hat{\mu}_{n})
.
Importantly, it is essential in our proof that the CQMLE satisfies the polynomial-type tail-probability estimate.
As a consequence, we obtain a practical
\sqrt{n}
-consistent estimator, that can be computed in a flash as was seen in Table
1
.
In Section
4
, we extensively conduct numerical experiments, by drawing histograms and boxplots of the initial estimator when we estimated the regression coefficient by the CQMLE and, just for comparison, the other two estimators (the least-squares and the least absolute deviation estimators). Also shown are some histograms of the normalized MLE using the initial estimators as the starting value for optimization, from which it can be seen that we had no local optimum problem.

CQMLE of location parameter

Throughout this section, we temporarily forget the assumption (

To remove the skewness of

, we take the difference in the model (

)
:

(2.1)

\Delta_{j}Y=\Delta_{j}X\cdot\mu+\sigma\Delta_{j}\epsilon,

where
\Delta_{j}\zeta:=\zeta_{j}-\zeta_{j-1}

, and

Then, the random variables

We define the CQMLE
by any element

\hat{\mu}_{n}\in\mathop{\rm argmax}_{\mu\in\Theta_{\mu}}H_{n}(\mu),

where

(2.2)

H_{n}(\mu):=\sum_{j=2}^{n}\log\frac{1}{1+(\Delta_{j}Y-\Delta_{j}X\cdot\mu)^{2}}

denotes the Cauchy quasi-likelihood (CQLF); the parameter space
\Theta_{\mu}
of

Based on the random function

, we will estimate the parameter

as if the noise part “

It will turn out that, under appropriate conditions, the CQMLE

is asymptotically normal and satisfies the tail-probability estimate; the result can be regarded as a heavy-tail counterpart of the well-known fact that the Gaussian least-squares estimator can work for a broad class of finite-variance noise distributions, and seems new in the literature on linear regression with asymmetric heavy-tailed noise; the tail-probability estimate is essential in our proof of the
\sqrt{n}
-consistency of the moment estimator associated with the lower-order moments (Section
3

The use of the Cauchy likelihood for possibly non-Cauchy noise was previously mentioned by, for example,
[
3

in the context of linear processes with symmetric stable noise.

\displaystyle-\int_{0}^{1}\frac{1}{n}\partial_{\mu}^{2}H_{n}(\mu_{0}+t(\hat{\mu}_{n}-\mu_{0}))dt

\displaystyle\xrightarrow{p}\Gamma_{0},

with

being positive definite.

Proof of (
2.31
).

Observe that

(2.33)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\partial_{\mu}H_{n}(\mu_{0})

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{j=2}^{n}\partial_{\mu}\log(1+(\Delta_{j}Y-\Delta_{j}X\cdot\mu)^{2})|_{\mu=\mu_{0}}

(2.34)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=2}^{n}\frac{2}{\sqrt{n}}\frac{S_{j}}{1+S_{j}^{2}}\Delta_{j}X=:\sum_{j=2}^{n}\chi_{n,j}.

Since the distribution of
S_{j}=\sigma_{0}\Delta_{j}\epsilon
is symmetric,

(2.35)

\displaystyle E_{\theta_{0}}[\chi_{n,j}]

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{\sqrt{n}}E_{\theta_{0}}\left[\frac{S_{2}}{1+S_{2}^{2}}\right]\Delta_{j}X=0.

We also have

\displaystyle\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}E_{\theta_{0}}\left[\left(\sum_{j=2}^{n}\chi_{n,j}\right)^{\otimes 2}\right]

(2.36)

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{4}{n}E_{\theta_{0}}\left[\sum_{i=2}^{n}\sum_{j=2}^{n}\left(\frac{S_{i}}{1+S_{i}^{2}}\right)\left(\frac{S_{j}}{1+S_{j}^{2}}\right)(\Delta_{i}X)(\Delta_{j}X)^{T}\right]

(2.37)

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{4}{n}\sum_{j=2}^{n}E_{\theta_{0}}\left[\left(\frac{S_{2}}{1+S_{2}^{2}}\right)^{2}\right](\Delta_{j}X)^{\otimes 2}

(2.38)

\displaystyle{}\qquad+4E_{\theta_{0}}\left[\left(\frac{S_{2}}{1+S_{2}^{2}}\right)\left(\frac{S_{3}}{1+S_{3}^{2}}\right)\right]

(2.39)

\displaystyle{}\qquad\times\bigg\{\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=2}^{n-1}\{(\Delta_{j}X)(\Delta_{j+1}X)^{T}+(\Delta_{j+1}X)(\Delta_{j}X)^{T}\}\bigg\}

(2.40)

\displaystyle=\Sigma_{0}.

From the above discussion and
the central limit theorem for sequences of
1
-dependent random variables
[

, Theorem 11]
, we get (

).

Proof of (
2.32
).

By Taylor’s theorem,

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{n}\int_{0}^{1}\partial_{\mu}^{2}H_{n}(\mu_{0}+t(\hat{\mu}_{n}-\mu_{0}))dt

(2.41)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{n}\partial_{\mu}^{2}H_{n}(\mu_{0})-\frac{1}{n}\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}t\partial_{\mu}^{3}H_{n}(\mu_{0}+ut(\hat{\mu}_{n}-\mu_{0}))dudt(\hat{\mu}_{n}-\mu_{0}),

By the consistency of
\hat{\mu}_{n}
proved in Lemma

and since

a.s., we have

\displaystyle\left|\frac{1}{n}\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}t\partial_{\mu}^{3}H_{n}(\mu_{0}+ut(\hat{\mu}_{n}-\mu_{0}))dudt(\hat{\mu}_{n}-\mu_{0})\right|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{n}\sup_{\mu\in\Theta_{\mu}}\left|\partial_{\mu}^{3}H_{n}(\mu)\right||\hat{\mu}_{n}-\mu_{0}|

(2.43)

\displaystyle=O_{p}(1)o_{p}(1)=o_{p}(1).

It follows that

(2.44)

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{n}\int_{0}^{1}\partial_{\mu}^{2}H_{n}(\mu_{0}+t(\hat{\mu}_{n}-\mu_{0}))dt

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{n}\partial_{\mu}^{2}H_{n}(\mu_{0})+o_{p}(1).

We have

(2.45)

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{n}\partial_{\mu}^{2}H_{n}\left(\mu_{0}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\partial_{\mu}^{2}\sum_{j=2}^{n}\log(1+(\Delta_{j}Y-\Delta_{j}X\cdot\mu)^{2})\bigg|_{\mu=\mu_{0}}

(2.46)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=2}^{n}\frac{2-2S_{j}^{2}}{(1+S_{j}^{2})^{2}}(\Delta_{j}X)^{\otimes 2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=2}^{n}\left(\frac{2-2S_{j}^{2}}{(1+S_{j}^{2})^{2}}-E_{\theta_{0}}\left[\frac{2-2S_{2}^{2}}{(1+S_{2}^{2})^{2}}\right]\right)(\Delta_{j}X)^{\otimes 2}

\displaystyle{}\qquad+E_{\theta_{0}}\left[\frac{2-2S_{2}^{2}}{(1+S_{2}^{2})^{2}}\right]\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=2}^{n}(\Delta_{j}X)^{\otimes 2}.

Using the Lindeberg-Lyapunov theorem as before, the first term on the rightmost side is

.
Thus, we obtain

-\frac{1}{n}\partial_{\mu}^{2}H_{n}\left(\mu_{0}\right)\xrightarrow{p}E_{\theta_{0}}\left[\frac{2-2S_{2}^{2}}{(1+S_{2}^{2})^{2}}\right]C_{0}=\Gamma_{0},

followed by (
2.32
).
Finally,
\Gamma_{0}>0
holds since the expectation is positive by Lemma
2.5
below.

Next, we turn to the proof of (

Recall the notation

By Assumption

\displaystyle{}\qquad+\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}E_{\theta_{0}}\left[\left|\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\partial_{\mu}H_{n}(\mu_{0})\right|^{K}\right]

(2.49)

\displaystyle{}\qquad+{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}E_{\theta_{0}}\left[\left(\sup_{\mu\in\Theta_{\mu}}n^{\frac{1}{2}-\beta_{2}}|Y_{n}(\mu)-Y(\mu)|\right)^{K}\right]}<\infty.

\displaystyle\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}E_{\theta_{0}}\left[\left(\frac{1}{n}\sup_{\mu\in\Theta_{\mu}}|\partial_{\mu}^{3}H_{n}(\mu)|\right)^{K}\right]

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{n}\partial_{\mu}^{2}H_{n}(\mu_{0})

Lemma 2.5
.

Under Assumption
2.1
(
5
), we have

(2.63)

E_{\theta_{0}}\left[\frac{1-S_{2}^{2}}{(1+S_{2}^{2})^{2}}\right]>0.

Proof.

The function
\psi(x):=\frac{1-x^{2}}{(1+x^{2})^{2}}
is symmetric, is positive (resp. negative) for
|x|\leq 1
(resp.
|x|>1
), and satisfies that
\int_{\mathbb{R}}\psi(x)dx=0
(apply the change of variable
x=\tan\alpha
).
We have
\int_{1}^{\infty}\psi(x)\mathfrak{f}(x)dx\geq\mathfrak{f}(1)\int_{1}^{\infty}\psi(x)dx
and also
\int_{0}^{1}\psi(x)\mathfrak{f}(x)dx>\mathfrak{f}(1)\int_{0}^{1}\psi(x)dx
; the latter inequality is strict since

(2.64)

\int_{0}^{1}\psi(x)(\mathfrak{f}(x)-\mathfrak{f}(1))dx\geq\int_{\mathfrak{c}_{1}}^{\mathfrak{c}_{2}}\psi(x)(\mathfrak{f}(x)-\mathfrak{f}(1))dx\geq\mathfrak{b}\mathfrak{f}(1)\,\int_{\mathfrak{c}_{1}}^{\mathfrak{c}_{2}}\psi(x)dx>0.

Thus,

\displaystyle E_{\theta_{0}}\left[\psi(S_{2})\right]

\displaystyle=2\int_{0}^{\infty}\psi(x)\mathfrak{f}(x)dx>2\mathfrak{f}(1)\int_{0}^{1}\psi(x)dx+2\mathfrak{f}(1)\int_{1}^{\infty}\psi(x)dx

\displaystyle=2\mathfrak{f}(1)\int_{\mathbb{R}}\psi(x)dx=0.

∎

We will estimate the parameters by the method of moments, based on the residuals

\displaystyle\hat{\epsilon}_{S,j}:=\hat{\epsilon}_{j}-\hat{\epsilon}_{j-1},\qquad\hat{\epsilon}_{C,j}:=\hat{\epsilon}_{j}+\hat{\epsilon}_{j-1}-2\hat{\epsilon}_{j-2}

-consistency of the moment estimator.
Introduce the following notation:

(3.2)

\displaystyle\theta_{0}^{-}

\displaystyle=(\alpha_{0},\beta_{0},\sigma_{0}),\quad\theta_{0}=(\alpha_{0},\beta_{0},\sigma_{0},\mu_{0}),\quad{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}|x|^{\langle r\rangle}:={\rm sgn}(x)|x|^{r}\quad(x\in\mathbb{R})},

\displaystyle m(\theta_{0}^{-})

(

5
, p.45]
, it suffices for (
3.8
) to prove

(3.29)

\sqrt{n}\left|\frac{1}{n-2}\sum_{j=3}^{n}|\hat{\epsilon}_{C,j}|^{\langle r\rangle}-E_{\theta_{0}}[|\epsilon_{C,3}|^{\langle r\rangle}]\right|=O_{p}(1).

We note that for all
x,y\in\mathbb{R}
and
r\in(0,1)
,

\displaystyle||x|^{\langle r\rangle}-|y|^{\langle r\rangle}|

\displaystyle=||x|^{\langle r\rangle}-|y|^{\langle r\rangle}|I({\rm sgn}(x)={\rm sgn}(y))

(3.30)

\displaystyle{}\qquad+||x|^{\langle r\rangle}-|y|^{\langle r\rangle}|I({\rm sgn}(x)\neq{\rm sgn}(y))

(3.31)

\displaystyle\leq||x|^{r}-|y|^{r}|+\left\{|x|^{r}+|y|^{r}\right\}I({\rm sgn}(x)\neq{\rm sgn}(y))

(3.32)

\displaystyle\leq||x|^{r}-|y|^{r}|+\left\{|x|^{r}+|y|^{r}\right\}I(|x-y|>|y|)

(3.33)

\displaystyle\lesssim|x-y|^{r}.

It follows that

\displaystyle\sqrt{n}\Biggl|\frac{1}{n-2}\sum_{j=3}^{n}|\hat{\epsilon}_{C,j}|^{\langle r\rangle}-E_{\theta_{0}}[|\epsilon_{C,3}|^{\langle r\rangle}]\Biggr|

(3.34)

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{n}\Biggl|\frac{1}{n-2}\sum_{j=3}^{n}\left(|\epsilon_{C,j}|^{\langle r\rangle}-E_{\theta_{0}}[|\epsilon_{C,3}|^{\langle r\rangle}]\right)\Biggr|+\frac{\sqrt{n}}{n-2}\sum_{j=3}^{n}\left||\hat{\epsilon}_{C,j}|^{\langle r\rangle}-|\epsilon_{C,j}|^{\langle r\rangle}\right|

(3.35)

\displaystyle\lesssim O_{p}(1)+\frac{\sqrt{n}}{n-2}\sum_{j=3}^{n}|\hat{\epsilon}_{C,j}-\epsilon_{C,j}|^{r}=O_{p}(1),

hence (
3.29
).
∎

Now we introduce our estimator
(\hat{\alpha}_{n}^{(0)},\hat{\beta}_{n}^{(0)},\hat{\sigma}_{n}^{(0)})
of
(\alpha,\beta,\sigma)
.
From
[
9
]
,

(3.36)

E_{\theta}[|\epsilon_{S,2}|^{r}]=\sigma^{r}\,\frac{\Gamma(1-\frac{r}{\alpha})}{\Gamma(1-r)}\frac{2^{\frac{r}{\alpha}}}{\cos\frac{r\pi}{2}}.

Let
h_{r}(\alpha,\sigma):=E_{\theta_{0}}[|\epsilon_{S,2}|^{r}]
.
The moment estimator
\hat{\alpha}_{n}^{(0)}
is the solution of the following equation:

(3.37)

\frac{(\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{j=2}^{n}|\hat{\epsilon}_{S,j}|^{r})^{2}}{\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{j=2}^{n}|\hat{\epsilon}_{S,j}|^{2r}}=\frac{h_{r}(\alpha,\sigma)}{h_{2r}(\alpha,\sigma)}.

Since
\sigma
on the right hand side disappears, we can obtain
\hat{\alpha}_{n}^{(0)}
by solving this equation. Then, we can obtain
\hat{\sigma}_{n}^{(0)}
by solving

(3.38)

\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{j=2}^{n}|\hat{\epsilon}_{S,j}|^{r}=h_{r}(\hat{\alpha}_{n}^{(0)},\sigma).

Next, to obtain the moment estimator of
\beta
, we note the following expressions
[
9
]
:

(3.39)

E_{\theta}[|\epsilon_{C,3}|^{r}]=\frac{\Gamma(1-\frac{r}{\alpha})}{\Gamma(1-r)}\left|\frac{(2+2^{\alpha})\sigma^{\alpha}}{\cos\eta}\right|^{\frac{r}{\alpha}}\frac{\cos\left(\frac{r\eta}{\alpha}\right)}{\cos\left(\frac{r\pi}{2}\right)},

(3.40)

E_{\theta}[|\epsilon_{C,3}|^{\langle r\rangle}]=\frac{\Gamma(1-\frac{r}{\alpha})}{\Gamma(1-r)}\left|\frac{(2+2^{\alpha})\sigma^{\alpha}}{\cos\eta}\right|^{\frac{r}{\alpha}}\frac{\sin\left(\frac{r\eta}{\alpha}\right)}{\sin\left(\frac{r\pi}{2}\right)},

where

(3.41)

\eta:=\arctan\left(\left(\frac{2-2^{\alpha}}{2+2^{\alpha}}\right)\beta\tan\frac{\alpha\pi}{2}\right).

Taking the ratio of the above two equations together with their empirical counterparts and then substituting
\hat{\alpha}_{n}^{(0)}
into
\alpha
, we obtain the following estimating equation for
\eta
:

(3.42)

\frac{\frac{1}{n-2}\sum_{j=3}^{n}|\hat{\epsilon}_{C,j}|^{\langle r\rangle}}{\frac{1}{n-2}\sum_{j=3}^{n}|\hat{\epsilon}_{C,j}|^{r}}=\frac{\tan\frac{r\eta}{\hat{\alpha}_{n}^{(0)}}}{\tan{\frac{r\pi}{2}}}.

By solving the above equation,

(3.43)

\hat{\eta}_{n}=\frac{\hat{\alpha}_{n}^{(0)}}{r}\arctan\left(\tan\left(\frac{r\pi}{2}\right)\frac{\frac{1}{n-2}\sum_{j=3}^{n}|\hat{\epsilon}_{C,j}|^{\langle r\rangle}}{\frac{1}{n-2}\sum_{j=3}^{n}|\hat{\epsilon}_{C,j}|^{r}}\right).

From this and the definition of
\eta
,

(3.44)

\hat{\beta}_{n}^{(0)}=\left(\frac{2+2^{\hat{\alpha}_{n}^{(0)}}}{2-2^{\hat{\alpha}_{n}^{(0)}}}\right)\frac{\tan\hat{\eta}_{n}}{\tan{\frac{\hat{\alpha}_{n}\pi}{2}}}.

Let
\hat{\theta}_{n}^{(0)}:=(\hat{\alpha}_{n}^{(0)},\hat{\beta}_{n}^{(0)},\hat{\sigma}_{n}^{(0)},\hat{\mu}_{n})
with
\hat{\mu}_{n}
being the CQMLE.
Our main claim is the following.

Theorem 3.2
.

Under Assumption
2.1
,

(3.45)

\sqrt{n}\left(\hat{\theta}_{n}^{(0)}-\theta_{0}\right)=O_{p}(1).

Proof.

By Theorem
2.4
, it suffices to show the tightness
\sqrt{n}(\hat{\alpha}_{n}^{(0)}-\alpha_{0},\,\hat{\sigma}_{n}^{(0)}-\sigma_{0})
and
\sqrt{n}(\hat{\beta}_{n}^{(0)}-\beta_{0})
separately.

Let
m_{1}(\alpha,\sigma):=\left(E_{\theta}[|\epsilon_{S,2}|^{r}],\,E_{\theta}[|\epsilon_{S,2}|^{2r}]\right)
and
M(\alpha,\sigma):=\partial_{(\alpha,\sigma)}m_{1}(\alpha,\sigma)
.
By the expression (
3.36
), the determinant of
M(\alpha_{0},\sigma_{0})
is written as

(3.46)

\displaystyle|M(\alpha_{0},\sigma_{0})|

\displaystyle=\frac{\Gamma(1-\frac{r}{\alpha_{0}})\Gamma(1-\frac{2r}{\alpha_{0}})}{\Gamma(1-2r)\Gamma(1-r)}\frac{2^{\frac{3r}{\alpha_{0}}+1}\sigma_{0}^{3r+1}r^{2}}{\alpha_{0}^{2}\cos(\frac{r\pi}{2})\cos(r\pi)}\left(\psi\left(1-\frac{r}{\alpha_{0}}\right)-\psi\left(1-\frac{2r}{\alpha_{0}}\right)\right),

where
\psi
denotes the digamma function.
Since

(3.47)

\frac{\Gamma(1-\frac{r}{\alpha_{0}})\Gamma(1-\frac{2r}{\alpha_{0}})}{\Gamma(1-2r)\Gamma(1-r)}\frac{2^{\frac{3r}{\alpha}+1}\sigma^{3r+1}r^{2}}{\alpha^{2}\cos(\frac{r\pi}{2})\cos(r\pi)}\neq 0

under (
3.4
) and since
\psi(1-\frac{r}{\alpha_{0}})-\psi(1-\frac{2r}{\alpha_{0}})\neq 0
because
\psi
is strictly increasing, it follows that
M(\alpha_{0},\sigma_{0})
is non-singular.
Lemma
3.1
and Cramér’s theorem give the tightness of
\sqrt{n}(\hat{\alpha}_{n}^{(0)}-\alpha_{0},\,\hat{\sigma}_{n}^{(0)}-\sigma_{0})
.

Write
\hat{\beta}_{n}^{(0)}=H_{n}(\hat{\alpha}_{n}^{(0)})
, where

(3.48)

\displaystyle H_{n}(\alpha)

\displaystyle:=\frac{(2+2^{\alpha})\tan(\frac{\alpha}{r}\arctan(T_{n}\tan(\frac{r\pi}{2})))}{(2-2^{\alpha})\tan(\frac{\alpha\pi}{2})},

(3.49)

\displaystyle T_{n}

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{n-2}\sum_{j=3}^{n}|\hat{\epsilon}_{C,j}|^{\langle r\rangle}\bigg/{\frac{1}{n-2}\sum_{j=3}^{n}|\hat{\epsilon}_{C,j}|^{r}}.

The tightness
\sqrt{n}(\hat{\beta}_{n}^{(0)}-\beta_{0})=\sqrt{n}\{H_{n}(\hat{\alpha}_{n}^{(0)})-\beta_{0}\}=O_{p}(1)
follows from showing
\sqrt{n}\{H_{n}(\alpha_{0})-\beta_{0}\}=O_{p}(1)
and
\partial_{\alpha}H_{n}(\tilde{\alpha}_{n})=O_{p}(1)
.
The former is obvious by Cramér’s theorem and the latter follows from Lemma
3.3
below.
The proof is complete.
∎

It is possible to deduce the asymptotic normality of
\hat{\theta}_{n}^{(0)}
at rate
\sqrt{n}
by the standard theory based on the delta method (see
[
5
, Section 7]
,
[
20
, Chapter 4]
, and so on). Nevertheless, the derivation of the closed form of the asymptotic covariance matrix would be very complicated and messy, and we will not pursue it.

Lemma 3.3
.

For any
\tilde{\alpha}_{n}\xrightarrow{p}\alpha_{0}
, we have

(3.50)

\partial_{\alpha}H_{n}(\tilde{\alpha}_{n})\xrightarrow{p}\frac{\partial_{\alpha}g(T_{0},\alpha_{0})h(\alpha_{0})-g(T_{0},\alpha_{0})\partial_{\alpha}h(\alpha_{0})}{h(\alpha_{0})^{2}},

where
h(\alpha)
and
g(x,\alpha)
are defined in the proof.

Proof.

Denote the numerator and denominator in (
3.48
) by
g(x,\alpha)
and
h(\alpha)
, respectively.
It is obvious that
(x,\alpha)\mapsto g(x,\alpha)
is
C^{1}
-class.
It remains to show that
h(\alpha)\neq 0
for
\alpha\in(0,2)
and that
h(\alpha)
is
C^{1}
-class.
It is obvious that
h(\alpha)>0
; as was mentioned in the introduction, we can easily check
\lim_{\alpha\rightarrow 1}h(\alpha)=\frac{4}{\pi}\log 2

[
13
, p.189]
.
For
\alpha\neq 1
,

(3.51)

\displaystyle\partial_{\alpha}h(\alpha)

\displaystyle=-(\log 2)2^{\alpha}\tan\left(\frac{\alpha\pi}{2}\right)+\frac{\pi}{2}\frac{(2-2^{\alpha})}{\cos^{2}(\frac{\alpha\pi}{2})}

\displaystyle=\frac{\pi}{2}(2-2^{\alpha})\tan^{2}\left(\frac{\alpha\pi}{2}\right)-(2\log 2)\tan\left(\frac{\alpha\pi}{2}\right)+\frac{\pi}{2}(2-2^{\alpha})

(3.52)

\displaystyle{}\qquad+(\log 2)(2-2^{\alpha})\tan\left(\frac{\alpha\pi}{2}\right).

Again by the fact that
\lim_{\alpha\to 1}\tan(\alpha\pi/2)(|u|^{1-\alpha}-1)=(2/\pi)\log|u|
, it is straightforward to see that
\partial_{\alpha}h(\alpha)
is continuous in
\alpha\in(0,2)
.
∎

Remark 3.4
.

Let
\ell_{n}(\theta)
denote the log-likelihood function based on
\{(X_{j},Y_{j})\}_{j\leq n}
:

(3.53)

\ell_{n}(\theta)=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\log\left\{\frac{1}{\sigma}\phi_{\alpha,\beta}\left(\frac{Y_{j}-X_{j}\cdot\mu}{\sigma}\right)\right\}.

It is known that the one-step estimator
\hat{\theta}_{n}^{(1)}:=\hat{\theta}_{n}^{(0)}-{\ell_{n}^{\prime}(\hat{\theta}_{n}^{(0)})}^{-1}l_{n}^{\prime\prime}(\hat{\theta}_{n}^{(0)})
is asymptotically equivalent to the MLE when
\ell_{n}
is smooth enough and
\hat{\theta}_{n}^{(0)}
is
\sqrt{n}
-consistent
[
22
, Theorem 5.5.4]
.
However, the calculation of
\hat{\theta}_{n}^{(1)}
turned out to be very time-consuming and unstable.
The direct numerical optimization of
\ell_{n}
with reasonable initial values may result in better performance; see Table
1
in the introduction.
We remark that, in the i.i.d.-
S^{0}_{\alpha}(\beta,\sigma,\mu)
framework,
[
10
]
recently studied the fundamental properties and asymptotic behaviors of the likelihood functions and the associated MLE.

Remark 3.5

(Incorporation of intercept)
.

Suppose that none of the
q
components of
j\mapsto X_{j}
is constant and that the model is

(3.54)

Y_{j}=m+X_{j}\cdot\mu+\epsilon_{j}

with an additional unknown location parameter
m\in\mathbb{R}
. Under suitable conditions on
X_{1},X_{2},\dots
as before,
we can estimate
\theta=(\alpha,\beta,\sigma,\mu)
without reference to
m
;
the intercept
m
is not estimable by the procedure we have seen so far since taking differences eliminates
m
.
Nevertheless, once we obtain
\hat{\theta}_{n}^{(0)}=(\hat{\alpha}_{n}^{(0)},\hat{\beta}_{n}^{(0)},\hat{\sigma}_{n}^{(0)},\hat{\mu}_{n}^{(0)})
,

loosely speaking, the random variables
(\hat{\sigma}_{n}^{(0)})^{-1}(Y_{j}-m_{0}-X_{j}\cdot\hat{\mu}_{n}^{(0)})
(
j\leq n
) are approximately distributed as
S_{\hat{\alpha}_{n}^{(0)}}(\hat{\beta}_{n}^{(0)},1,0)
, where
m_{0}
denotes the true value of
m
.

According to the standard
M
-estimation theory, it is readily expected that we could estimate
m
at rate
\sqrt{n}
by any element maximizing the random function

\displaystyle m

\displaystyle\in\mathop{\rm argmax}_{m}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\log\left(\frac{1}{\hat{\sigma}_{n}^{(0)}}\phi_{\hat{\alpha}_{n}^{(0)},\hat{\beta}_{n}^{(0)}}\left(\frac{Y_{j}-m-X_{j}\cdot\hat{\mu}_{n}^{(0)}}{\hat{\sigma}_{n}^{(0)}}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\mathop{\rm argmax}_{m}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\log\phi_{\hat{\alpha}_{n}^{(0)},\hat{\beta}_{n}^{(0)}}\left(\frac{Y_{j}-m-X_{j}\cdot\hat{\mu}_{n}^{(0)}}{\hat{\sigma}_{n}^{(0)}}\right).

Numerical experiments

(4.1)

We set

We generated
