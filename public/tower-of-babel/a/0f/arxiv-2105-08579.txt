Neural-network Quantum States for Spin-1 systems: spin-basis and parameterization effects on compactness of representations
Michael Y Pei, Stephen R Clark

Abstract
Neural network quantum states (NQS) have been widely applied to spin-1/2 systems where they have proven to be highly effective. The application to systems with larger on-site dimension, such as spin-1 or bosonic systems, has been explored less and predominantly using spin-1/2 Restricted Boltzmann Machines (RBMs) with a one-hot/unary encoding. Here we propose a more direct generalisation of RBMs for spin-1 that retains the key properties of the standard spin-1/2 RBM, specifically trivial product states representations, labelling freedom for the visible variables and gauge equivalence to the tensor network formulation. To test this new approach we present variational Monte Carlo (VMC) calculations for the spin-1 antiferromagnetic Heisenberg (AFH) model and benchmark it against the one-hot/unary encoded RBM demonstrating that it achieves the same accuracy with substantially fewer variational parameters. Further to this we investigate how the hidden unit complexity of NQS depend on the local single-spin basis used. Exploiting the tensor network version of our RBM we construct an analytic NQS representation of the Affleck-Kennedy-Lieb-Tasaki (AKLT) state in the x ​ y ​ z xyz spin-1 basis using only M = 2 ​ N M=2N hidden units, compared to M ∼ O ⁡ ( N 2 ) M\sim O(N^{2}) required in the S z S^{z} basis. Additional VMC calculations provide strong evidence that the AKLT state in fact possesses an exact compact NQS representation in the x ​ y ​ z xyz basis with only M = N M=N hidden units. These insights help to further unravel how to most effectively adapt the NQS framework for more complex quantum systems.

Introduction

The strongly-interacting quantum many-body problem is crucial to our understanding of many intriguing physical phenomena, but also inherently difficult to treat numerically owing to the exponential growth of the Hilbert space with system size. A commonly used approximate strategy is the variational method where a trial state, characterised by a tractable number of variational parameters, is optimised in energy. The effectiveness of this approach is highly dependent on the ansatz having an expressive form that can be systematically improved, to minimise bias, while also allowing relevant observables to be evaluated efficiently. Tensor networks have provided several examples of such ansatzes, with matrix product states (MPS)
Verstraete et al. 2008
;
Orús 2014
displaying impressive accuracy in one-dimensional systems, along with projected entangled-pair states (PEPS)
Verstraete et al. 2008
;
Cirac and Sierra 2010
, tree tensor networks (TTN)
Shi et al. 2006
;
Murg et al. 2010
and multi-scale entanglement renormalization ansatz (MERA)
Evenbly and Vidal 2013
making two-dimensional systems accessible. Recently artificial neural networks (ANNs) have emerged as another class of highly flexible variational ansatzes with many variants such as restricted Boltzmann machines (RBM)
Carleo and Troyer 2017
, Deep Boltzmann Machines (DBM)
Gao and Duan 2017
;
Carleo et al. 2018
;
He et al. 2019
, convolutional neural networks (CNN)
Choo et al. 2019
;
Irikura and Saito 2020
;
Schmitt and Heyl 2020
;
Liang et al. 2021
;
Levine et al. 2019
and feed-forward neural networks (FFNN)
Saito and Kato 2018
;
Choo et al. 2018
;
Luo and Clark 2019
;
Adams et al. 2020
. An important advantage of ANNs is that they are highly flexible and can be applied to any number of spatial dimensions making them a powerful method for tackling the subtle physics seen in two-dimensional systems.

Although one of the simplest ANN variants RBMs have seen widespread applications including for open quantum systems
Torlai and Melko 2018
;
Vicentini et al. 2019
;
Hartmann and Carleo 2019
;
Yoshioka and Hamazaki 2019

;

Freitas et al. 2018
;
Bausch and Leditzky 2020
and more. There are several reasons for their continued use. First, their simple structure allows for efficient sampling crucial for applying variational Monte Carlo (VMC)
Gubernatis et al. 2016
;
Becca and Sorella 2017
. Second, RBMs are also a good candidate for a weakly biased ansatz, given that they are capable of exactly representing arbitrary states when their hidden unit number
M
scales exponentially with system size

Deng et al. 2017

Clark 2018
;
Chen et al. 2018
;
Collura et al. 2020

Gao and Duan 2017
, spin Jastrow states such as Laughlin states
Glasser et al. 2018
;
Kaubruegger et al. 2018
;
Clark 2018
, general stabiliser states such as the toric code
Zheng et al. 2019
;
Zhang et al. 2018
;

;
Jia et al. 2019

Despite their efficacy for spin-
\textstyle\frac{1}{2}

Progress has been made in this direction in recent work

Variational Monte Carlo and the many-body problem

2.1
Quantum many-body problem

In this work we will focus on physical systems composed of
N
localised spinful particles. Each particle is described by a vector of spin operators
\hat{\bf S}_{j}=(\hat{S}^{x}_{j},\hat{S}^{y}_{j},\hat{S}^{z}_{j})
for

\left|\,S_{j}\right\rangle
of

N
spins can then be expanded in this basis as

\left|\,\Psi\right\rangle=\sum_{\bm{S}}\Psi({\bm{S}})\left|\,\bm{S}\right\rangle,

(1)

via its complex amplitudes
\Psi({\bm{S}})
. In the spin-
\textstyle\frac{1}{2}
case we have
\hat{S}^{\alpha}_{j}=\mbox{$\textstyle\frac{1}{2}$}\hat{\sigma}^{\alpha}_{j}
(taking
\hbar=1
throughout) for
\alpha=\{x,y,z\}
defined by the Pauli matrices, and
S_{j}\in\{+\mbox{$\textstyle\frac{1}{2}$}\equiv\,\uparrow,-\mbox{$\textstyle\frac{1}{2}$}\equiv\,\downarrow\}
. In the spin-1 case we have

\displaystyle\qquad\hat{S}^{x}_{j}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left[\begin{array}[]{ccc}0&1&0\\
1&0&1\\
0&1&0\end{array}\right],\quad\hat{S}^{y}_{j}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left[\begin{array}[]{ccc}0&-{\rm i}&0\\
{\rm i}&0&-{\rm i}\\
0&{\rm i}&0\end{array}\right],\quad\hat{S}^{z}_{j}=\left[\begin{array}[]{ccc}1&0&0\\
0&0&0\\
0&0&-1\end{array}\right],

with
S_{j}\in\{+1\equiv\,\Uparrow,0,-1\equiv\,\Downarrow\}

A key challenge in many-body physics is to find the ground state
\left|\,\Psi_{0}\right\rangle
of a system governed by an interacting Hamiltonian
\hat{H}
. In the context of spin systems

will include terms that are products of the spin operators
\hat{\bf S}_{j}
over two or more spins. Given that the expectation value of any observable

\langle A\rangle_{\Psi}=\frac{\left\langle\Psi\,\right|\hat{A}\left|\,\Psi\right\rangle}{\left\langle\,\Psi\,|\,\Psi\,\right\rangle},

(3)

the variational approach reformulates the eigenvalue problem
\hat{H}\left|\,\Psi_{0}\right\rangle=E_{0}\left|\,\Psi_{0}\right\rangle
as the minimisation of the energy
E=\langle\hat{H}\rangle_{\Psi}
over the exponentially many amplitudes
\Psi({\bm{S}})
. Performing this task exactly is only feasible for small systems
N\sim O(10)

Läuchli et al. 2011
.

2.2
Variational Monte Carlo method

A way to circumvent the “curse of dimensionality” is to instead to restrict the optimisation over a specialised class of states
\left|\,\Psi_{\bm{p}}\right\rangle
, dependent on parameters
\bm{p}
whose number
n_{\rm params}
scales polynomially with
N
. The variational principle
E_{0}\leq E_{{\bm{p}}_{0}}={\rm min}_{\bm{p}}\langle\hat{H}\rangle_{\Psi_{\bm{p}}}
then provides a route to finding the best approximation
\left|\,\Psi_{{\bm{p}}_{0}}\right\rangle
within the ansatz for
\left|\,\Psi_{0}\right\rangle
. The flexibility and utility of variational ansatz are greatly enhanced if instead of computing expectation values
\langle A\rangle_{\Psi_{\bm{p}}}
exactly we evaluate them approximately using Monte Carlo sampling. This approach is called
variational Monte Carlo
(VMC) and is described in detail in App.
A
. A key feature of VMC is that only ratios of amplitudes for an ansatz
\Psi_{\bm{p}}({\bm{S}})/\Psi_{\bm{p}}({\bm{S}}^{\prime})
between different spin states
\left|\,\bm{S}\right\rangle
and
\left|\,{\bm{S}}^{\prime}\right\rangle
are needed within the algorithm
2
2

2

For this reason we will ignore the normalisation of quantum states in this work.
. To be efficient we thus require that these amplitude ratios for our ansatz can be evaluated with an effort scaling polynomially with
N
.

All numerical calculations presented in this paper employ the powerful stochastic reconfiguration method
Sorella 2001
;
Becca and Sorella 2017
for optimising the parameters
\bm{p}
. While ansatze with a larger number of parameters may describe more varied amplitude structure, in principle allowing greater accuracy, the effort to optimise them can scale as
O(n_{\rm params}^{3})
. It is therefore crucial that the functional form of any candidate ansatz
\left|\,\Psi_{\bm{p}}\right\rangle
is judicious in its use of variational parameters to avoid excessive redundancies.

3
Neural-network quantum states in spin-1/2

In this section we review NQS in terms of RBMs, discussing their form from both an energy function and tensor network perspective. In doing so we highlight key properties of NQS that are desirable for expressiveness and the ability to represent important families of states.

3.1
Restricted Boltzmann machine approach

Restricted Boltzmann machines consist of two sets of units,
N

visible
units representing the physical system, and
M

hidden
units to be marginalised out. The units are characterised by a Boltzmann-like combined probability distribution
p({\bm{v}},{\bm{h}})=\exp(-\mathcal{E}_{\bm{\lambda}})
with an effective “energy” function

\mathcal{E}_{\bm{\lambda}}({\bm{v}},{\bm{h}})=-\sum_{j=1}^{N}a_{j}v_{j}-\sum_{i=1}^{M}b_{i}h_{i}-\sum_{i=1}^{M}\sum_{j=1}^{N}w_{ij}h_{i}v_{j},

(4)

where
{\bm{\lambda}}=\{{\bm{a}},{\bm{b}},{\bm{w}}\}
is the set of
MN+M+N

N

{\bm{a}}=(a_{1},\cdots,a_{N})
,
M

{\bm{b}}=(b_{1},\cdots,b_{M})
and
M\times N
hidden-visible interaction weights
{\bm{w}}=[w_{11},\cdots,w_{1N},\cdots,w_{MN}]
. While
p({\bm{v}},{\bm{h}})
is Boltzmann once the hidden units are traced out we are left with a more complex marginal distribution for the visible units from which the NQS amplitudes are derived
Carleo and Troyer 2017

\Psi_{\bm{\lambda}}({\bm{S}}\mapsto{\bm{v}})=\sum_{{\bm{h}}}\exp\left[-\mathcal{E}_{\bm{\lambda}}({\bm{v}},{\bm{h}})\right].

(5)

4
Generalisation to spin-1 systems

A tentative definition of a spin-1 RBM can be made by simply using the three-valued visible variables
v_{j}
with a natural Ising-like physical to visible mapping

S_{j}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ccr}\Uparrow&\mapsto&+1\\
0&\mapsto&0\\
\Downarrow&\mapsto&-1\end{array}\right\}=v_{j},

(13)

in the standard linear energy function Eq. (
4
). By retaining two-valued Ising-like hidden units
h_{i}\in\{+1,-1\}
the amplitudes
\Psi_{\bm{\lambda}}({\bm{S}}\mapsto{\bm{v}})=\sum_{{\bm{h}}}\exp\left[\mathcal{E}_{\bm{\lambda}}({\bm{v}},{\bm{h}})\right]
continue to be given by Eq. (
7
), but now admitting three-valued visible variables.

This spin-1 generalisation of the RBM lacks the key properties of the spin-
\textstyle\frac{1}{2}
variant. First,
\Psi_{\bm{\lambda}}({\bm{S}}\mapsto{\bm{v}})
cannot easily describe a generic product spin state

\qquad\left(c^{(1)}_{+}\left|\,\Uparrow\right\rangle+c^{(1)}_{0}\left|\,0\right\rangle+c^{(1)}_{-}\left|\,\Downarrow\right\rangle\right)\otimes\cdots\otimes\left(c^{(N)}_{+}\left|\,\Uparrow\right\rangle+c^{(N)}_{0}\left|\,0\right\rangle+c^{(N)}_{-}\left|\,\Downarrow\right\rangle\right),

(14)

with coefficients
c^{(j)}_{\alpha}
, since the visible bias term
\exp(a_{j}v_{j})
cannot discriminate each of the local spin-1 states. This points to issues of expressiveness since we are using the same set of
MN+M+N
parameters
\{{\bm{a}},{\bm{b}},{\bm{w}}\}
to describe a bigger state space. Second, the energy function Eq. (
4
) does not possess labelling freedom for the visible units values. An arbitrary physical to visible mapping

S_{j}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ccr}\Uparrow&\mapsto&\mu\\
0&\mapsto&\nu\\
\Downarrow&\mapsto&\omega\end{array}\right\}=\tilde{v}_{j},

(15)

is generated from an Ising-like variable
v_{j}=\{+1,0,-1\}
via the quadratic transformation
\tilde{v}_{j}=\nu+\mbox{$\textstyle\frac{1}{2}$}(\mu-\omega)v_{j}+\mbox{$\textstyle\frac{1}{2}$}(\mu+\omega-2\nu)v_{j}^{2}
. Consequently transforming visible variables in this way cannot be accommodated in
\mathcal{E}_{\bm{\lambda}}({\bm{v}},{\bm{h}})
by changing only the parameters
\{{\bm{a}},{\bm{b}},{\bm{w}}\}
. One way to avoid these issues is to use “one-hot” or “unary” encoding which has been successfully applied to both spin-1
Lu et al. 2019
and bosonic systems

;
Vargas-Calderón et al. 2020
.

4.1
Unary encoding approach

Unary encoding applies the spin-
\textstyle\frac{1}{2}

5

\textstyle\frac{1}{2}

Physical states of the spin-1 system are now contained in the unary encoded subspace where only a single-excitation occurs within any three-site unit cell which facilitates the efficient projection of the representation. This naturally generalises to a

By using this mapping a spin-

\textstyle\frac{1}{2}

compared to the naive spin-1 RBM. However, there are some obvious deficiencies of this approach. First, unary encoding appears to unnecessarily enlarge the parameter count, as evidenced by the fact that it increases it even for

4.2
Defining a spin-1 RBM and tensor network

These deficiencies show that a more general RBM energy function is needed that is sensitive to the three-valued nature of the visible units through the inclusion of terms involving the square of visible variables

via the energy function

\displaystyle\quad\mathcal{E}_{\bm{\Lambda}}({\bm{v}},{\bm{h}})=\sum_{j=1}^{N}a_{j}v_{j}+\sum_{j=1}^{N}A_{j}v_{j}^{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{M}b_{i}h_{i}+\sum_{i=1}^{M}\sum_{j=1}^{N}w_{ij}h_{i}v_{j}+\sum_{i=1}^{M}\sum_{j=1}^{N}W_{ij}h_{i}v^{2}_{j},\quad

The new contributions to a spin-1 RBM are

\displaystyle\qquad\Psi_{\bm{\Lambda}}({\bm{S}}\mapsto{\bm{v}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\prod_{i=1}^{N}e^{a_{i}v_{i}+A_{i}v^{2}_{i}}\prod_{j=1}^{M}2\cosh\left(b_{j}+\sum_{i=1}^{N}w_{ij}v_{i}+\sum_{i=1}^{N}W_{ij}v^{2}_{i}\right).\qquad

while the quadratic interaction term ensures labelling freedom for the visible variable.

4.4
Change of local spin basis

For tensor network representations, such as MPS or PEPS, the complexity (internal bond dimension) of a given state’s description is rooted in its entanglement structure. As such changing the local basis of the spins used in a calculation has no effect on this complexity. Moreover, transforming a representation from one basis to another is accomplished by simply admixing the local tensors. Within VMC a change of local spin basis leaves the locality and the sparsity of the Hamiltonian essentially unchanged. However, it is pivotal to the method that the amplitudes
\Psi_{\bm{p}}({\bm{S}})
of whatever ansatz is used can be efficiently evaluated in this new basis. This is not generally true of NQS since their sampleability is intimately tied to the basis that factorises the COPY tensors they are built from.

To understand how NQS behave consider a representation of some state
\left|\,\Psi\right\rangle
, for instance of four spin-1’s sampleable in the
S^{z}
basis

\includegraphics[scale,valign]{figures/spin1_nqs_example.pdf}.

(25)

Now suppose we transform the local
S^{z}
basis
\left|\,S\right\rangle
to a new basis
\chi
via a unitary
\left|\,\chi_{S}\right\rangle=\hat{\mathcal{B}}^{\dagger}\left|\,S\right\rangle
. Formally, from a tensor network perspective we find the
\chi
basis NQS representation by sandwiching
\mathbbm{1}=\hat{\mathcal{B}}\hat{\mathcal{B}}^{\dagger}
on each physical leg and computing the
S^{z}
basis NQS representation of
(\hat{\mathcal{B}}^{\dagger})^{\otimes N}\left|\,\Psi\right\rangle
. However, currently there is no known procedure for updating exactly an NQS after the application of an arbitrary single-spin unitary, even allowing for an increased number of hidden units. While we are guaranteed that an NQS representation of
\hat{\mathcal{B}}^{\otimes N}\left|\,\Psi\right\rangle
exists, as illustrated here schematically

\includegraphics[scale,valign]{figures/spin1_nqs_basis_change.pdf},

(26)

there is no guarantee it will be efficient, even if the representation of
\left|\,\Psi\right\rangle
was originally. An example of such a catastrophic loss of NQS efficiency has been presented by Gao and Duan
Gao and Duan 2017
. They show that, so long as the polynomial hierarchy in computational complexity theory does not collapse, a two-dimensional cluster state, which has an efficient NQS representation, has no efficient NQS representation after a specific layer of translation-invariant single-spin unitaries are applied. Thus NQS complexity depends non-trivially on the local spin basis used.

Motivated by this we will consider NQS calculations in two different local spin-1 bases to examine how the complexity varies. Specifically, the standard
S^{z}
basis and the

\displaystyle\left|\,x\right\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\left|\,\Uparrow\right\rangle-\left|\,\Downarrow\right\rangle\right),\quad\left|\,y\right\rangle=\frac{\rm i}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\left|\,\Uparrow\right\rangle+\left|\,\Downarrow\right\rangle\right),\quad\left|\,z\right\rangle=-\left|\,0\right\rangle.

as a consequence of them all contributing one eigenstate to the basis. On a practical level using the

5
Revisiting the AKLT model

We now move on to benchmark our spin-1 NQS against the analytically solvable AKLT model
Affleck et al. 1987
which is a spin-1 chain governed by a bilinear-biquadratic SU(2)-isotropic Heisenberg Hamiltonian of the form

\hat{H}_{\rm AKLT}=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\left[\hat{\bf S}_{j}\cdot\hat{\bf S}_{j+1}+\beta\left(\hat{\bf S}_{j}\cdot\hat{\bf S}_{j+1}\right)^{2}\right]+\frac{2}{3},

with periodic boundary conditions
N+1\equiv 1
. It has special significance since it was the first solvable spin-1 chain model that exhibits the ‘Haldane gap’
Haldane 1983
. The AKLT state
\left|\,\Psi_{\rm AKLT}\right\rangle
is the ground state of
\hat{H}_{\rm AKLT}
at the AKLT point
\beta=\frac{1}{3}
, and has an energy of exactly zero.

Figure 6:
Valence bond solid construction of the spin-1 AKLT state
\Psi_{\rm AKLT}
from the projection
\mathbbm{P}
of pairs of spin-
\textstyle\frac{1}{2}
particles shared between neighbouring sites on the chain.

As is well-known,
\left|\,\Psi_{\rm AKLT}\right\rangle
has a special structure of correlations which are related to a valence bond solid. Specifically, each spin-1 is envisaged as being a pair of spin-
\textstyle\frac{1}{2}
particles that are correspondingly entangled in a singlet state with a partner spin-
\textstyle\frac{1}{2}
in the nearest neighbouring spin-1 on the chain. The AKLT state is then the projection
\mathbbm{P}
of the local pairs of spin-
\textstyle\frac{1}{2}
particles into the triplet subspace, as depicted in Figure
6
. This also leads to
\left|\,\Psi_{\rm AKLT}\right\rangle
possessing a very compact MPS representation with matrices

\displaystyle{\bf A}^{+}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\hat{\sigma}^{+},\quad{\bf A}^{0}=-\frac{1}{2}\hat{\sigma}^{z},\quad{\bf A}^{-}=-\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\hat{\sigma}^{-},

where
\hat{\sigma}^{\pm}=\mbox{$\textstyle\frac{1}{2}$}(\hat{\sigma}^{x}\pm{\rm i}\hat{\sigma}^{y})
, such that the (unnormalized) amplitudes of the ground state in the
\hat{S}^{z}
basis follow as

\Psi_{\rm AKLT}({\bm{S}})={\rm tr}\left({\bf A}^{S_{1}}{\bf A}^{S_{2}}\cdots{\bf A}^{S_{N}}\right).

(30)

Since the AKLT point of
\hat{H}_{\rm AKLT}
lies in the gapped Haldane phase
\left|\,\Psi_{\rm AKLT}\right\rangle
has finite-ranged magnetic correlations,

O^{zz}_{\ell}=\left\langle\psi_{\rm AKLT}\,\right|\hat{S}^{z}_{0}\hat{S}^{z}_{\ell}\left|\,\psi_{\rm AKLT}\right\rangle\sim e^{-\ell/\xi},\quad{\rm with}\quad\xi=\frac{1}{{\rm ln}(3)},

(31)

yet it also has an unbroken spin rotation symmetry which is a hallmark of a symmetry protected topological order. Specifically, the string-order parameter

O^{\rm string}=\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\lim_{\ell\rightarrow\infty}\left\langle\psi_{\rm AKLT}\,\right|\hat{S}^{z}_{0}\prod_{j=1}^{\ell-1}e^{i\pi\hat{S}^{z}_{j}}\hat{S}^{z}_{\ell}\left|\,\psi_{\rm AKLT}\right\rangle\sim-\frac{4}{9},

(32)

reveals the presence of infinite-ranged antiferromagnetic correlations. This is evident from the structure of the MPS amplitudes. Any matrix product
{\bf A}^{\pm}{\bf A}^{0}\cdots{\bf A}^{0}{\bf A}^{\pm}=0
, so any configuration containing a ferromagnetic segment, like “+ 0 0 0 +”, with any number of 0’s is not allowed. In contrast allowed configurations contain only antiferromagnetic segments, such as “– 0 + 0 0 0 – + 0”, arising from sequences like
{\bf A}^{\pm}{\bf A}^{0}\cdots{\bf A}^{0}{\bf A}^{\mp}
where every
\pm
is partnered with a
\mp
separated by with an arbitrary string of 0’s.

Despite its simple MPS representation it is surprisingly non-trivial to capture the non-commutative matrix products making up the AKLT amplitudes with an NQS. Direct conversion to an NQS from the MPS representation gives two reasons why it must contain long-ranged hidden units. First, it has been shown
Zheng et al. 2019
that any short-ranged translationally invariant NQS cast into a MPS form by mapping hidden units into virtual bonds has
\bf A
matrices that are at most rank-1. Since the matrix
{\bf A}^{0}
in the AKLT state is rank-2 it fails this condition. Second, if we divide the chain into a sequence of three contiguous parts
a,b,c
, once we make
b
larger than the longest range of any hidden unit, so no hidden unit connects to visible units in both
a
and
c
, then the NQS amplitudes factorise as

\psi_{\rm sr-NQS}({\bm{S}}_{a},{\bm{S}}_{b},{\bm{S}}_{c})=\psi_{ab}({\bm{S}}_{a},{\bm{S}}_{b})\psi_{bc}({\bm{S}}_{b},{\bm{S}}_{c}),

(33)

implying that
{\bm{S}}_{a}
and
{\bm{S}}_{c}
are uncorrelated
Chen et al. 2018
. The AKLT amplitudes
\Psi_{\rm AKLT}({\bm{S}})
do not satisfy this property since region
b
can be any length of
0
’s and there will always be non-zero amplitudes “+ 0 0 0 …0 –” and “–  0 0 0 …0 +” encoding string order correlations that are not factorisable.

It has been previously found that the AKLT state in the
S^{z}
basis requires an NQS with
M\sim O(N^{2})
long-ranged hidden units

, and this was borne out in numerical calculations for small systems. In App.

hidden units, many of which are extensive over the system. The
O(N^{2})
scaling can be readily understood as a consequence of having hidden units that each eliminate disallowed configurations, such as “
\pm
0 0 0
\pm
”, and impose the sign for allowed configurations, such as “
\pm
0 0 0
\mp
”, for all
N
separations and
N
translations over the system. This is rather less efficient than the compact spin-
\textstyle\frac{1}{2}
NQS found for Jastrow, graph and stabilizer states in Ref.
Pei and Clark 2021
. The AKLT state can be expressed with
O(N)
hidden units but at the expense of needing a 2-layer DBM network
Zheng et al. 2019
that cannot in general be exactly sampled, complicating its use numerically. However, as we saw for the AFH model numerical results the hidden unit complexity is basis dependent. Surprisingly, we will show next that an exact
M\sim O(N)
spin-1 NQS representation of the AKLT state is obtained in the
xyz

6
Conclusion

We have introduced the most natural and direct generalisation of RBM from spin-
\textstyle\frac{1}{2}
to spin-1. This necessitated including a quadratic visible bias and a quadratic visible-hidden interaction in the RBM energy function to ensure trivial product state representation, labelling freedom and gauge equivalence to the tensor network formulation. We demonstrated its use numerically for the spin-1 AFH model in both the
S^{z}
and
xyz
bases, illustrating how the choice of basis can affect the accuracy and hidden unit complexity of an NQS representation. Using our spin-1 NQS we then re-examined how to represent the AKLT state exactly. In the
S^{z}
basis it is known to require
M\sim O(N^{2})
hidden units, yet by changing to the
xyz
basis we construct an NQS with
M\sim O(N)
hidden units.

Numerical VMC calculations have indicated that by capturing the nodal structure, either implicitly within the sampling or explicitly through the inclusion of extra hidden units, the optimisation can find even more efficient constructions for the sign structure. The resulting spin-1 NQS for the AKLT state in the
xyz
basis requires
M=N
hidden units in total making it
compact
. This example raises the interesting possibility of improving the efficiency and accuracy of NQS calculations by including single-spin basis transformations to lower the hidden unit complexity.

Several important open questions remain about NQS representations. In particular it would be instructive to build representations of classes of bosonic states using multinomial RBMs. In this case a local Fock basis is typically employed, however our findings suggest that it could be useful to explore a local basis that breaks the particle number symmetry when describing condensates. Moreover, the elevation of visible units from binary to multinomial raises the question of whether also using multinomial hidden units can enhance the expressiveness of NQS. This has been explored in the context of binary visible units in Ref.
Rrapaj and Roggero 2021
in an analytical context to precisely represent certain two- and three-body interactions. The use of multinomial hidden units for numerical VMC calculations has been largely unexplored and is the subject of forthcoming work
Pei and Clark 2021
for the Bose Hubbard model in two dimensions.

Appendix A
Monte Carlo method and sampling

Any many-body quantum state can be expressed in terms of its amplitudes on a set of configurations states in a chosen basis, but exhaustively computing the amplitudes for every possible configuration in the basis is often prohibitively expensive. Monte Carlo methods circumvent this by randomly selecting a subset of these configurations, and calculating an estimate of key observables using this selection. In this case we require only that the (unnormalised) amplitudes
\Psi_{\bm{p}}({\bm{S}})
of the ansatz (defined by parameters
\bm{p}
) in a fixed basis, such as
S^{z}
, can be computed in a time with polynomial complexity in
N
. We then rewrite the expectation value as

\langle\hat{A}\rangle_{\Psi_{\bm{p}}}=\sum_{\bm{S}}P({\bm{S}})A({\bm{S}}),\quad{\rm where}\quad P({\bm{S}})=\frac{|\Psi_{\bm{p}}({\bm{S}})|^{2}}{\sum_{\bm{S}}|\Psi_{\bm{p}}({\bm{S}})|^{2}},

(40)

is the probability of the spin configuration
\bm{S}
and

A({\bm{S}})=\sum_{{\bm{S}}^{\prime}}\frac{\Psi_{\bm{p}}({\bm{S}}^{\prime})}{\Psi_{\bm{p}}({\bm{S}})}\left\langle\bm{S}\,\right|\hat{A}\left|\,{\bm{S}}^{\prime}\right\rangle,

(41)

is the local estimator of
\hat{A}
. The sum over
{\bm{S}}^{\prime}
in Eq. (
41
) is restricted to only those configurations for which the matrix element
\left\langle\bm{S}\,\right|\hat{A}\left|\,{\bm{S}}^{\prime}\right\rangle\neq 0
, and so as long as
\hat{A}
is sparse in the chosen fixed basis then
A({\bm{S}})
can be efficiently evaluated. Typical product terms comprising short-ranged Hamiltonians
\hat{H}
fulfil this requirement. Thus an unbiased estimate of
\langle\hat{A}\rangle_{\Psi_{\bm{p}}}
is made by drawing independent samples of spin configurations
\bm{S}
from the distribution
P({\bm{S}})
. A powerful way to accomplish this is Markov-chain Monte Carlo in which a sequence of spin configurations
{\bm{S}}_{0}\rightarrow{\bm{S}}_{1}\rightarrow\cdots
is generated using the Metropolis-Hastings algorithm that accepts a proposed configuration
{\bm{S}}_{\rm prop}
for the
n
th iteration with a probability
P_{n}({\bm{S}}_{\rm prop})={\rm min}[1,|\Psi_{\bm{p}}({\bm{S}}_{\rm prop})|^{2}/|\Psi_{\bm{p}}({\bm{S}}_{n-1})|^{2}]
. A subset of visited configurations in the sequence, suitably separated to ensure they are decorrelated, is then used to estimate of
\langle\hat{A}\rangle_{\Psi_{\bm{p}}}
. Crucially both
A({\bm{S}})
and Monte Carlo sampling rely only on the ratio of ansatz amplitudes, so the intractable state normalisation
\sum_{\bm{S}}|\Psi_{\bm{p}}({\bm{S}})|^{2}
is entirely avoided.

Variational Monte Carlo proceeds by evaluating the ansatz energy
E_{\bm{p}}=\langle\hat{H}\rangle_{\Psi_{\bm{p}}}
and its variance, along with their gradient vectors with respects to parameters
\bm{p}
. The parameters can then be updated by a small step along the direction of steepest descent, with this iterated until convergence to the minimum
{\bm{p}}_{0}

Gubernatis et al. 2016
. The efficiency of this minimisation process is strongly problem and ansatz dependent. In challenging cases more sophisticated approaches such as modified stochastic optimisation
Lou and Sandvik 2007
, the ‘linear method’
Nightingale and Melik-Alaverdian 2001
;
Toulouse and Umrigar 2007
and stochastic reconfiguration
Sorella 2001
;
Becca and Sorella 2017
are needed. All numerical calculations in this paper employ stochastic reconfiguration. The method involves the construction of a
n_{\rm params}\times n_{\rm params}
matrix, requiring
O(n_{\rm samp}n_{\rm params}^{2})
operations, though with a conjugate gradient method this can be brought down to
O(n_{\rm samp}n_{\rm params})

Neuscamman et al. 2012
. The parameter changes can then be calculated from a set of linear equations involving the matrix, which scales as
O(n_{\rm params}^{2})
using matrix-vector product methods. As a rule of thumb the matrix requires a number of samples
n_{\rm samp}>10n_{\rm params}
to ensure the sampled matrix is not rank-deficient
Becca and Sorella 2017
, giving an overall minimum scaling of
O(n_{\rm params}^{2})
for the algorithm, though typically it is prudent to have a large number of samples
n_{\rm samp}\gg n_{\rm params}
as the error of any sampled observable typically scales as
1/\sqrt{n_{\rm samp}}

Becca and Sorella 2017
, including the elements of the matrix.

Appendix B
Boltzmann parameterisation of coupling matrices

Every
2\times 3
coupling matrix within the spin-1 NQS tensor network can be parameterised in Boltzmann form using the generalised energy function introduced in Sec.
4.2
as

C^{(ij)}_{h_{i}v_{j}}=\exp\left(\tilde{c}_{ij}+w_{ij}h_{i}v_{j}+W_{ij}h_{i}v_{j}^{2}+\tilde{b}_{ij}h_{i}+\tilde{a_{ij}}v_{j}+\tilde{A}_{ij}v_{j}^{2}\right),

(42)

for
h_{i}\in\{+1,-1\}
and
v_{j}\in\{+1,0,-1\}
, which includes a quadratic weight
W_{ij}
and quadratic partial bias
\tilde{A}_{ij}
. These complex parameters are found from the coupling matrix elements as

\displaystyle\tilde{a}_{ij}=\frac{1}{4}\left[\log\left(C^{(ij)}_{++}\right)+\log\left(C^{(ij)}_{-+}\right)-\log\left(C^{(ij)}_{+-}\right)-\log\left(C^{(ij)}_{--}\right)\right],

\displaystyle\tilde{b}_{ij}=\frac{1}{2}\left[\log\left(C^{(ij)}_{+0}\right)-\log\left(C^{(ij)}_{-0}\right)\right],

\displaystyle\tilde{c}_{ij}=\frac{1}{2}\left[\log\left(C^{(ij)}_{+0}\right)+\log\left(C^{(ij)}_{-0}\right)\right],

\displaystyle w_{ij}=\frac{1}{4}\left[\log\left(C^{(ij)}_{++}\right)-\log\left(C^{(ij)}_{-+}\right)-\log\left(C^{(ij)}_{+-}\right)+\log\left(C^{(ij)}_{--}\right)\right],

\displaystyle W_{ij}=\frac{1}{4}\left[\log\left(C^{(ij)}_{++}\right)-\log\left(C^{(ij)}_{-+}\right)-2\log\left(C^{(ij)}_{+0}\right)+2\log\left(C^{(ij)}_{-0}\right)+\log\left(C^{(ij)}_{+-}\right)-\log\left(C^{(ij)}_{--}\right)\right],

\displaystyle\tilde{A}_{ij}=\frac{1}{4}\left[\log\left(C^{(ij)}_{++}\right)+\log\left(C^{(ij)}_{-+}\right)-2\log\left(C^{(ij)}_{+0}\right)-2\log\left(C^{(ij)}_{-0}\right)+\log\left(C^{(ij)}_{+-}\right)+\log\left(C^{(ij)}_{--}\right)\right].

In both cases the presence of zero matrix elements in
{\bf C}^{(ij)}
, which appear frequently in our ‘by hand’ constructions, return diverging parameters. However, as discussed in Ref.
Pei and Clark 2021
this can be handled by softening the zeros via

C^{(ij)}_{h_{i}v_{j}}\mapsto\max\left(C^{(ij)}_{h_{i}v_{j}},e^{-\mathcal{S}}\right),

(43)

where typically
\mathcal{S}\approx 5-10
.

{\rm i}&0&0\end{array}\right],\quad{\bf C}_{{\rm i}\Downarrow}=\left[\begin{array}[]{ccc}1&1&1\\
0&0&{\rm i}\end{array}\right],\quad{\bf C}_{+0}=\left[\begin{array}[]{ccc}1&1&1\\
0&1&0\end{array}\right],

which in turn single out
S^{z}
basis spin-1 states
\left|\,\Uparrow\right\rangle
,
\left|\,0\right\rangle
and
\left|\,\Downarrow\right\rangle
. To cancel out in any configuration substrings
+[0\cdots 0]^{\ell}+
and
-[0\cdots 0]^{\ell}-
, starting at a given site
k
with a given separation
\ell
, we construct pairs of hidden units with

\displaystyle\{{\bf I},\cdots,{\bf I},\overbrace{{\bf C}_{+{\rm i}}}^{k},{\bf C}_{+0},\cdots,{\bf C}_{+0},\overbrace{{\bf C}_{+{\rm i}}}^{k+1+\ell},{\bf I},\cdots,{\bf I}\},\quad{\rm and}

\displaystyle\{{\bf I},\cdots,{\bf I},\overbrace{{\bf C}_{-{\rm i}}}^{k},{\bf C}_{+0},\cdots,{\bf C}_{+0},\overbrace{{\bf C}_{-{\rm i}}}^{k+1+\ell},{\bf I},\cdots,{\bf I}\}.

To capture all separations
\ell=0,1,\dots,N-2
starting on any site
k=1,2,\dots,N
we require
2N(N-1)
hidden units.

2.

Apply a factor of
(-1)^{\ell}
to the amplitude of a configuration for each substring of the form
+[0\cdots 0]^{\ell}-
it contains. To implement this rule we introduce the two additional coupling matrices

{\bf C}_{2\Uparrow}=\left[\begin{array}[]{ccc}1&1&1\\
2&0&0\end{array}\right],\quad{\bf C}_{-\Downarrow}=\left[\begin{array}[]{ccr}1&1&1\\
0&0&-1\end{array}\right].

A factor
(-1)^{\ell}
is induced on any configuration containing the substring
+[0\cdots 0]^{\ell}-
, starting at a given site
k
with a given separation
\ell
, by a hidden unit with

\{{\bf I},\cdots,{\bf I},\overbrace{{\bf C}_{2\Uparrow}}^{k},{\bf C}_{+0},\cdots,{\bf C}_{+0},\overbrace{{\bf C}_{-\Downarrow}}^{k+1+\ell},{\bf I},\cdots,{\bf I}\}.

To capture all odd separations
\ell=1,3,\dots,N-2
starting on any site
k=1,2,\dots,N
requires
N\lfloor\mbox{$\textstyle\frac{1}{2}$}(N-1)\rfloor
hidden units.

3.

Zero out the single excitation configurations
+[0\cdots 0]^{N-1}
and
-[0\cdots 0]^{N-1}
and all their translates. To implement this rule we introduce another coupling matrix

{\bf C}_{-\Uparrow}=\left[\begin{array}[]{rcc}1&1&1\\
-1&0&0\end{array}\right].

Configurations
+[0\cdots 0]^{N-1}
and
-[0\cdots 0]^{N-1}
are then cancelled out by a pair of hidden units with
\{{\bf C}_{-\Uparrow},{\bf C}_{+0},\cdots,{\bf C}_{+0}\}
and
\{{\bf C}_{-\Downarrow},{\bf C}_{+0},\cdots,{\bf C}_{+0}\}
. Capturing all the translates is achieved with
2N
hidden units.

4.

For
N
odd zero out the amplitude for configuration
[0\cdots 0]^{N}
, or for
N
even double its amplitude. To implement this rule we introduce one final coupling matrix

{\bf C}_{-0}=\left[\begin{array}[]{crc}1&1&1\\
0&-1&0\end{array}\right],

and defining a single hidden unit as
\{{\bf C}_{-0},{\bf C}_{-0},\cdots,{\bf C}_{-0}\}
.

5.

Apply a factors of
2^{(\#~+{\rm{}^{\prime}s})}\times(-1)^{(\#~0{\rm{}^{\prime}s})}\times(-1)^{(\#~-{\rm{}^{\prime}s})}
to the amplitudes of all configurations. This does not require any additional hidden units. Instead we can pick any hidden unit and right multiply each of its
N
coupling matrices by
{\bf D}={\rm diag}(2,-1,-1)
. In RBM formalism this is equivalent to setting non-zero visible unit biases.

This gives a total of
M=2N^{2}+N\lfloor\mbox{$\textstyle\frac{1}{2}$}(N-1)\rfloor+1
hidden units. Notice that the origin of the
O(N^{2})
scaling arises from spatial translations, so if this symmetry is directly imposed within the NQS ansatz, as it often is numerically, then the AKLT state in the
S^{z}
basis instead has its number of unique hidden units scaling as
M=2N+\lfloor\mbox{$\textstyle\frac{1}{2}$}(N-1)\rfloor+1
.
