Abstract
. We give a new compactness theorem for any metric ultraproducts of family of metric groups. As an application we characterize simplicity of metric ultraproducts of groups and give a couple of examples of new simple groups which are metric ultraproducts of finite and infinite symmetric groups, linear groups and interval exchange transformations group.

Introduction

The ultraproduct construction is playing an important role in model theory, topology and algebra. A more general construction is that of
metric ultraproduct
, when the objects are equipped with some king of invariant metrics. This paper is about metric ultraproducts of groups, that is groups equipped with conjugacy invariant norms. The importance of metric ultraproducts to group theory became apparent recently, especially in the case of sofic groups
[
Pes08
]
. Metric ultraproducts are currently intensively studied. The main open problem is the following conjecture.

Conjecture
.

Every group is sofic, that is
every finitely generated group can be homomorphically embedded into a
metric
ultraproduct
{\mathcal{S}}^{*}_{\text{met}}
of permutation groups
{\mathcal{S}}=(S_{n},\|\cdot\|_{H})
, equipped with the normalised Hamming norm
\|\sigma\|_{H}=\frac{1}{n}\|\supp(\sigma)\|
.

A
non-sofic group
is a group that cannot be homomorphically embedded into
{\mathcal{S}}^{*}_{\text{met}}
. No example of a non-sofic group is known. However, some well known groups (e.g. Higman group) are not known to be sofic. We believe that our new compactness theorem
2.7
better explain the structure of metric ultraproducts and give a new method to study them.

Let us briefly explain the plan. Our first aim is to prove general compactness theorem
2.8
valid for all metric ultraproducts, also for families of metric groups with
unbounded norms
. That is why we work with
finitary element-subgroup

{\mathcal{G}}^{*}_{\text{met, fin}}
(Definition
1.4
), which is a normal subgroup of metric ultraproduct
{\mathcal{G}}^{*}_{\text{met, fin}}
.
We give several applications of Theorems
2.7
,
2.8
. We characterize when
{\mathcal{G}}^{*}_{\text{met, fin}}
is a simple group (Theorem
4.3
, Section
4
). Our condition for simplicity involves covering of arbitrary big balls by neighbourhoods of conjugacy classes. We also study bounded and uniform simplicity in Section
3.2.4
, and topological simplicity in Section
4.3
. We provide a couple of examples, where our characterizations give new examples of simple groups.
We also study bounded generation and perfectness of
{\mathcal{G}}^{*}_{\text{met}}
in Sections
3.2.1
,
3.2.2
and metric version of being torsion group in Section
3.2.3
. Section
4.4
is about set theoretic consideration on some well-founded trees related with metric internal coverings (Theorem
4.14
).

Let us give more details and explain the notion of metric ultraproduct. Suppose
{\mathcal{G}}=(G_{n},\|\cdot\|_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
is a family of metric groups, so each
G_{n}
is equipped with a conjugacy invariant norm
\|\cdot\|_{n}
(see Definition
1.1
). A

of

is denoted by
{\mathcal{G}}^{*}_{\text{met}}
(Definition
1.2

is again equipped with a norm and metric:

\|\cdot\|\colon{\mathcal{G}}^{*}_{\text{met}}\to[0,\infty]\text{ defined as }\left\|(g_{n})/{\mathcal{E}}\right\|=\lim_{n\to{\mathcal{U}}}\left\|g_{n}\right\|_{n}.

Elements of a finite norm in
{\mathcal{G}}^{*}_{\text{met}}
form a normal subgroup of
{\mathcal{G}}^{*}_{\text{met}}

{\mathcal{G}}^{*}_{\text{met, fin}}
. Sometimes we work under the following assumption:

. Under this assumption

Theorem
.

2.7
(bounded case)
The following conditions are equivalent.

(1)

{\mathcal{G}}^{*}_{\text{met}}=\bigcup_{m\in{\mathbb{N}}}X_{m}

(2)

For any countable infinite sequence of positive reals

there is

Which is equivalent with the clause: for

-almost all

1.
Metric ultraproducts

Let us explain some basic terms. Our definition of a metric group below follows
[
NST18
, Sec. 2.1]
(see also
[
DK18
, Section 10.4.]
).

Let
[0,\infty]
be
[0,\infty)\cup\{\infty\}
, where
\infty>r
for all
r\in[0,\infty)
. We regard
[0,\infty]
as a compact space, where the neighbourhoods of
\infty
are of the form
(c,\infty)
, for
c\in[0,\infty)
.

Definition 1.1
.

A
bi-invariant metric
on a group
G
is a metric
d\colon G\times G\to[0,\infty]
such that
d(gx,gy)=d(x,y)=d(xg,yg)
for every
g,x,y\in G
.
Every such metric comes from a
bi-invariant
(also called
conjugacy invariant
) norm
\|\cdot\|\colon G\to[0,\infty]
satisfying

(0)

\|e\|=0
,

(1)

\|gh\|\leq\|g\|+\|h\|
,

(2)

\left\|g^{-1}\right\|=\|g\|=\|hgh^{-1}\|
,

(3)

\|g\|=0
if and only if
g=e
,

for all
g,h\in G
. That is, if
\|\cdot\|
satisfies (0), (1), (2), and (3), then
d(x,y)=\left\|xy^{-1}\right\|
is a bi-invariant metric on
G
. Moreover, if
d
is a bi-invariant metric, then
\|g\|=d(g,e)
is a bi-invariant norm. A
pseudo bi-invariant norm
is a function on
G
, satisfying (0), (1) and (2), but not necessarily (3). Such a norm gives rise to a
pseudo bi-invariant metric
. A
metric group
, or a
pseudo-metric group

(G,\|\cdot\|)
is a group
G
equipped with some bi-invariant or pseudo bi-invariant norm
\|\cdot\|
.

By
{\mathcal{U}}
we always denote a non-principal ultrafilter on
{\mathbb{N}}
. Let us define standard (discrete) ultraproduct
{\mathcal{G}}^{*}
of any family of groups
{\mathcal{G}}=(G_{n},)_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
with respect to
{\mathcal{U}}
:

(1.1)

{\mathcal{G}}^{*}=\frac{\prod_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}G_{n}}{{\mathcal{E}}_{d}},\text{ where }{\mathcal{E}}_{d}=\left\{\left(g_{n}\right)_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}\in\prod_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}G_{n}:\ \{n\in{\mathbb{N}}:g_{n}=e\}\in{\mathcal{U}}\right\}.

Let us remind the notion of the limit with respect to ultrafilter. Suppose
\{s_{n}\}_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
is a bounded sequence or reals. By
\lim_{n\to{\mathcal{U}}}s_{n}
we mean the
limit of
\{s_{n}\}_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
over
{\mathcal{U}}
, that is a real number
s\in{\mathbb{R}}
which is uniquely defined by the following condition: for every
\varepsilon>0
the set
\{n\in{\mathbb{N}}:|s_{n}-s|<\varepsilon\}
belongs to
{\mathcal{U}}
. We are now ready to define metric ultraproduct.

Definition 1.2
.

Suppose
{\mathcal{G}}=(G_{n},\|\cdot\|_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
is a family of pseudo-metric groups. A
metric ultraproduct

{\mathcal{G}}^{*}_{\text{met}}
of
{\mathcal{G}}
with regard to
{\mathcal{U}}
is defined as a quotient group:

{\mathcal{G}}^{*}_{\text{met}}=\frac{\prod_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}G_{n}}{{\mathcal{E}}},

where
{\mathcal{E}}
is the subgroup of
infinitesimals

(1.2)

{\mathcal{E}}=\left\{\left(g_{n}\right)_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}\in\prod_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}G_{n}:\lim_{n\to{\mathcal{U}}}\left\|g_{n}\right\|_{n}=0\right\}.

Remark 1.3
.

(1)

{\mathcal{G}}^{*}_{\text{met}}
is equipped with a pseudo bi-invariant norm

(1.3)

\|\cdot\|\colon{\mathcal{G}}^{*}_{\text{met}}\to[0,\infty]\text{ defined as }\left\|(g_{n})/{\mathcal{E}}\right\|=\lim_{n\to{\mathcal{U}}}\left\|g_{n}\right\|_{n}.

(2)

Since obviously
{\mathcal{E}}_{d}
from (
1.1
) is a subgroup of
{\mathcal{E}}
from (
1.2
), there is a natural epimorphism
{\mathcal{G}}^{*}\to{\mathcal{G}}^{*}_{\text{met}}
.

We are interested mainly in elements of
{\mathcal{G}}^{*}_{\text{met}}
of finite norm, that is why we define below
{\mathcal{G}}^{*}_{\text{met, fin}}
.

Definition 1.4
.

A finitary subgroup

{\mathcal{G}}^{*}_{\text{met, fin}}
of
{\mathcal{G}}^{*}_{\text{met}}
is defined as

(1.4)

{\mathcal{G}}^{*}_{\text{met, fin}}=\left\{x\in{\mathcal{G}}^{*}_{\text{met}}:\|x\|<\infty\right\}.

In fact
{\mathcal{G}}^{*}_{\text{met, fin}}={\mathcal{G}}_{\text{fin}}/{{\mathcal{E}}}
, where
{\mathcal{G}}_{\text{fin}}=\left\{\left(g_{n}\right)_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}\in\prod_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}G_{n}:\sup_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}\left\|g_{n}\right\|_{n}<\infty\right\}
.

Obviously, if the family
\{\|\cdot\|_{n}\}_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
is uniformly bounded (i.e.
\sup_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}\|\cdot\|_{n}<\infty
), then
{\mathcal{G}}^{*}_{\text{met, fin}}={\mathcal{G}}^{*}_{\text{met}}
. However, in many interesting cases
\{\|\cdot\|_{n}\}_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
is not uniformly bounded, so we work mainly with
{\mathcal{G}}^{*}_{\text{met, fin}}
.

When
\left(G_{n},\|\cdot\|_{n}\right)=(G,\|\cdot\|)
, for all
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
, we use the symbol
G^{*}_{\text{met, fin}}
for
{\mathcal{G}}^{*}_{\text{met, fin}}
. We have, in this case, a standard isometric homomorphic embedding

\varphi\colon G\to G^{*}_{\text{met, fin}},\text{ given by }\varphi(g)=(g,g,g,\ldots){\mathcal{E}}.

Let us record below some well-known properties of
{\mathcal{G}}^{*}_{\text{met, fin}}
.

Lemma 1.5
.

Fix a family
{\mathcal{G}}
of metric group.

(1)

The norm (
1.3
) makes
{\mathcal{G}}^{*}_{\text{met, fin}}
a topological group, that is, multiplication
\cdot\colon{\mathcal{G}}^{*}_{\text{met, fin}}\times{\mathcal{G}}^{*}_{\text{met, fin}}\to{\mathcal{G}}^{*}_{\text{met, fin}}
is continuous with respect to
\|\cdot\|
.

(2)

({\mathcal{G}}^{*}_{\text{met, fin}},\|\cdot\|)
is a complete metric space, that is, every countable descending family of balls

,

, has a non-empty intersection

.

The first part of Lemma

follows from the conjugacy-invariance of
\|\cdot\|
. The proof of the second part of Lemma

[

, Corollary 10.64 (1)]

[

Notation

Let us introduce some group-theoretic notation. For
g,h\in G
we put
g^{h}=h^{-1}gh
and
g^{G}=\left\{g^{h}:h\in G\right\}
.
For a metric group

,

(1.5)

{\mathcal{B}}_{\varepsilon}(g,G)=g\cdot{\mathcal{B}}(\varepsilon,G)=\left\{h\in G:\left\|g^{-1}h\right\|<\varepsilon\right\},\ {\mathcal{B}}_{\leq\varepsilon}(g,G)=g\cdot{\mathcal{B}}(\leq\varepsilon,G)=\left\{h\in G:\left\|g^{-1}h\right\|\leq\varepsilon\right\}.

2.
New compactness theorem for metric ultraproducts of groups

Let us fix throughout this section a family
{\mathcal{G}}=(G_{n},\|\cdot\|_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
of metric groups. Note that
\|\cdot\|_{n}\colon G_{n}\to[0,\infty]
, so we allow
\|\cdot\|
to have
\infty
as a value.

After introducing
metrically internal sets
in Section
2.1
, we give a new compactness theorem for
{\mathcal{G}}^{*}_{\text{met, fin}}
in Section
2.2
. Then we apply Baire category theorem to
({\mathcal{G}}^{*}_{\text{met, fin}},\|\cdot\|)
in Section
3
, to get new results on metric ultraproducts.

2.1.
Metrically internal sets

Our new compactness theorem for
{\mathcal{G}}^{*}_{\text{met, fin}}
involves
metrically internal subsets
from Definition
2.1
below. This definition has roots in non-standard analysis (see e.g.
[
DK18
, Definition 10.34]
). It is more convenient to work with subsets of
{\mathcal{G}}^{*}_{\text{met}}
rather that in
{\mathcal{G}}^{*}_{\text{met, fin}}
. That is why we define metrically internal subsets as some subsets of
{\mathcal{G}}^{*}_{\text{met}}
.

Let us briefly explain the situation for discrete metric, i.e. for standard ultraproduct
{\mathcal{G}}^{*}
. An
internal subset
X
of
{\mathcal{G}}^{*}
is of the form

(2.1)

X=\frac{\prod_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}X_{n}}{{\mathcal{E}}_{d}}=\frac{X_{0}\times X_{1}\times X_{2}\times\cdots}{{\mathcal{E}}_{d}},

where each
X_{n}
is a subset of
G_{n}
. This can be naturally generalized to

{\mathcal{G}}^{*}_{\text{met}}
as in Definition
2.1
below.

Definition 2.1
.

A subset
X\subseteq{\mathcal{G}}^{*}_{\text{met}}
is called
metrically internal
if there is a collection of sets
\{X_{n}\}_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
,
X_{n}\subseteq G_{n}
such that

X=\frac{\prod_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}X_{n}}{{\mathcal{E}}}=\frac{X_{0}\times X_{1}\times X_{2}\times\cdots}{{\mathcal{E}}},

where
{\mathcal{E}}
is the infinitesimal subgroup (see Definition
1.2
).

Let us give some examples and non-examples of metrically internal sets.

Example 2.2
.

(1)

A conjugacy class
\bar{g}^{{\mathcal{G}}^{*}_{\text{met}}}
, for any
\bar{g}=(g_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}/{\mathcal{E}}\in{\mathcal{G}}^{*}_{\text{met}}
, is metrically internal, as
\bar{g}^{{\mathcal{G}}^{*}_{\text{met}}}=\frac{\prod_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}{g_{n}}^{G_{n}}}{{\mathcal{E}}}
.

(2)

The set of all commutators
\left\{\bar{g}^{-1}\bar{h}^{-1}\bar{g}\bar{h}:\bar{g},\bar{h}\in{\mathcal{G}}^{*}_{\text{met}}\right\}
and the set of all
n
-powers
\left\{\bar{g}^{n}:\bar{g}\in{\mathcal{G}}^{*}_{\text{met}}\right\}
are metrically internal (for the same reason as in (
1
)).

(3)

An open ball
{\mathcal{B}}_{\varepsilon}\left(\bar{g}\right)

and closed ball

for

(see (

)), may not be metrically internal, but there are canonical metrically internal sets in between:

{\mathcal{B}}_{\varepsilon}\left(\bar{g},{\mathcal{G}}^{*}_{\text{met}}\right)\subseteq\frac{\prod_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}{\mathcal{B}}_{\varepsilon}(g_{n},G_{n})}{{\mathcal{E}}}\subseteq\frac{\prod_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}{\mathcal{B}}_{\leq\varepsilon}(g_{n},G_{n})}{{\mathcal{E}}}\subseteq{\mathcal{B}}_{\leq\varepsilon}\left(\bar{g},{\mathcal{G}}^{*}_{\text{met}}\right).

(4)

It is not true in general that a definable subset of
{\mathcal{G}}^{*}_{\text{met}}
corresponds to definable subsets from coordinates. That is, fix
\varphi
a formula in some first order logic, then the following natural equality (

) may not be true:

(2.2)

\left\{\bar{g}\in{\mathcal{G}}^{*}_{\text{met}}:\varphi(\bar{g})\text{ holds in }{\mathcal{G}}^{*}_{\text{met}}\right\}=\frac{\prod_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}\left\{g_{n}\in G_{n}:\varphi(g_{n})\text{ holds in }G_{n}\right\}}{{\mathcal{E}}},

We give a counterexample to (
2.2
) in (
6
) below. We consider torsion elements, that is

\varphi_{m}(\bar{g})=({\bar{g}}^{m}=e)
, for
m\in{\mathbb{N}}
.

(5)

Fix a metric group
(G,\|\cdot\|)
,
\varepsilon\geq 0
and
m\in{\mathbb{N}}
. We define
elements of
\varepsilon
-order
m
as elements from
T_{m,\leq\varepsilon}(G)
where :

(2.3)

T_{m,\leq\varepsilon}(G)=\{g\in G:\|g^{m}\|\leq\varepsilon\}\text{, also define }T_{m,\varepsilon}(G)=\{g\in G:\|g^{m}\|<\varepsilon\}.

Those set may not be internal, however we have as in (

(2.4)

T_{m,\varepsilon}\left({\mathcal{G}}^{*}_{\text{met}}\right)\subseteq\frac{\prod_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}T_{m,\varepsilon}(G_{n})}{{\mathcal{E}}}\subseteq\frac{\prod_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}T_{m,\leq\varepsilon}(G_{n})}{{\mathcal{E}}}\subseteq T_{m,\leq\varepsilon}\left({\mathcal{G}}^{*}_{\text{met}}\right).

(6)

The last
\subseteq
in (

) above may be a proper subset, as we give an example where

, but
T_{n,3,\leq 0}(G_{n})=\emptyset

G_{n}=\left({\mathbb{Z}}_{2^{n}},\|\cdot\|_{2^{n}}\right),

where
\|\cdot\|_{2^{n}}\colon{\mathbb{Z}}_{2^{n}}\to[0,1]
is a natural norm, given by
\|g\|_{2^{n}}=\frac{1}{2^{n-1}}\min\{g,2^{n}-g\}
(called
Lee norm
), for
g\in\{0,1,\ldots,2^{n}-1\}
.
Consider
\varphi_{3}(g)=(3g=0)
.
Then each
G_{n}
has no elements of order 3, but
{\mathcal{G}}^{*}_{\text{met}}
has such element, e.g.
\bar{g}=\left(\left\lfloor\frac{2^{n}}{3}\right\rfloor\right)_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}/{\mathcal{E}}
has order 3.

Let us notice that metrically internal set are closed under
\cdot
and
\cup
.

Remark 2.3
.

(1)

If
Y=\frac{\prod_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}Y_{n}}{{\mathcal{E}}}
and
Z=\frac{\prod_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}Z_{n}}{{\mathcal{E}}}
are metrically internal, then so are

Y\cdot Z=\frac{\prod_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}Y_{n}\cdot Z_{n}}{{\mathcal{E}}}\text{ and }Y\cup Z=\frac{\prod_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}Y_{n}\cup Z_{n}}{{\mathcal{E}}}.

(2)

{\mathcal{G}}^{*}_{\text{met}}\setminus X
may not be metrically internal, for metrically internal
X=\frac{\prod_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}X_{n}}{{\mathcal{E}}}
(for example
{\mathcal{G}}^{*}_{\text{met}}\setminus\{e\}
is not metrically internal for compact
G
, see Example
2.5
). However, this set is contained in a canonical metrically internal subset

{\mathcal{G}}^{*}_{\text{met}}\setminus X\subseteq\frac{\prod_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}G_{n}\setminus X_{n}}{{\mathcal{E}}}
.

Lemma
2.4
below is crucial in the proof of Theorem
2.8
, which is new analogue of the classical compactness theorem for
{\mathcal{G}}^{*}_{\text{met, fin}}=\left\{x\in{\mathcal{G}}^{*}_{\text{met}}:\|x\|<\infty\right\}
.

Lemma 2.4
.

If
X\subseteq{\mathcal{G}}^{*}_{\text{met}}
is metrically internal, then
X\cap{\mathcal{G}}^{*}_{\text{met, fin}}
is closed with respect to the topology on
{\mathcal{G}}^{*}_{\text{met, fin}}
induced by
\|\cdot\|
.

Proof.

Let
X=\prod_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}X_{n}/{\mathcal{E}}
. It suffices to prove that if
a_{0},a_{1},a_{2},\ldots
is a sequence from
X\cap{\mathcal{G}}^{*}_{\text{met, fin}}
, converging to
a_{\infty}\in G^{*}_{\text{met, fin}}
, then
a_{\infty}\in X
. For each
n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{>0}
take
{k_{n}}\in{\mathbb{N}}
such that

(2.5)

\left\|a_{k_{n}}^{-1}a_{\infty}\right\|<\frac{1}{n}.

Write
a_{k_{n}}=\left(a_{k_{n},0},a_{k_{n},1},a_{k_{n},2},a_{k_{n},3},\ldots\right){\mathcal{E}}
and
a_{\infty}=\left(a_{\infty,0},a_{\infty,1},a_{\infty,2},a_{\infty,3},\ldots\right)/{\mathcal{E}}
, for some
a_{k_{n},m}\in X_{m}
,
a_{\infty,m}\in G_{m}
. For each
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
define

U_{n}=\left\{m\in{\mathbb{N}}:\left\|a_{k_{n},m}^{-1}a_{\infty,m}\right\|_{m}<\frac{1}{n}\text{ in }G_{m}\right\}.

Every
U_{n}
belongs to
{\mathcal{U}}
, by (
2.5
). Define

V_{n}=U_{0}\cap U_{1}\cap\cdots\cap U_{n}\setminus\{n\}\in{\mathcal{U}}.

Then
V_{0}\supseteq V_{1}\supseteq V_{2}\supseteq\ldots\supseteq V_{n}\in{\mathcal{U}}
and
\bigcap_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}V_{n}=\emptyset
, since
n\not\in V_{n}
. Define

(2.6)

b_{m}=\begin{cases}a_{k_{n},m}&\text{: when }m\in V_{n}\setminus V_{n+1},\\
e&\text{: when }m\not\in V_{0}\end{cases}.

Define
b_{\infty}=(b_{0},b_{1},b_{2},b_{3},\ldots)/{\mathcal{E}}
. Then
b_{m}=a_{k_{n},m}\in X_{m}
, so
b_{\infty}\in X
. Our aim it to prove that
a_{\infty}=b_{\infty}
.
It is enough to prove that
\left\|a_{k_{n}}^{-1}b_{\infty}\right\|<\frac{2}{n}
holds for every
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
(since
a_{\infty}=\lim_{n\to\infty}a_{n}
). Fix
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
and take any
m\in V_{n}
. Then
m\in V_{t}\setminus V_{t+1}
for some
t\geq n
, so
b_{m}=a_{k_{t},m}
by (
2.6
). Hence

\left\|a_{k_{n},m}^{-1}b_{m}\right\|_{m}=\left\|a_{k_{n},m}^{-1}a_{k_{t},m}\right\|_{m}\leq\left\|a_{k_{n},m}^{-1}a_{\infty,m}\right\|_{m}+\left\|a_{\infty,m}^{-1}a_{k_{t},m}\right\|_{m}<\frac{1}{n}+\frac{1}{t}\leq\frac{2}{n}.

Therefore
\left\|a_{k_{n}}^{-1}b_{\infty}\right\|=\lim_{m\to{\mathcal{U}}}\left\|a_{k_{n},m}^{-1}b_{m}\right\|_{m}<\frac{2}{n}
.
∎

For compact groups, Lemma
2.4
gives a complete description of metrically internal sets, as explained in Example below.

Example 2.5
.

Suppose
(G,\|\cdot\|)
is a compact metric group (that is
\|\cdot\|
induces a compact topology on
G
). Then
\|\cdot\|
is a bounded function and
G^{*}_{\text{met}}=G^{*}_{\text{met, fin}}
. Furthermore
G^{*}_{\text{met}}\cong G
are isomorphic as metric groups (see
[
DK18
, Ex. 10.44]
). In this case we have that

X\subseteq G^{*}_{\text{met}}=G\text{ is metrically internal }\Longleftrightarrow X\text{ is a closed subset in }G.

Indeed,
\Rightarrow
is by Lemma
2.4
,
\Leftarrow
follows from the fact that
X=\frac{X\times X\times X\times\cdots}{{\mathcal{E}}}
for any closed subset
X
of compact
G
.

2.2.
New compactness theorem

We fix throughout this section a family of metric groups
{\mathcal{G}}=(G_{n},\|\cdot\|_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
and a countable family
\{X_{m}\}_{m\in{\mathbb{N}}}
of metrically internal (Definition
2.1
) subsets of
{\mathcal{G}}^{*}_{\text{met}}
, where

(2.7)

X_{m}=\frac{X_{m,0}\times X_{m,1}\times X_{m,2}\times\cdots}{{\mathcal{E}}},\ \ X_{m,n}\subseteq G_{n}.

We give a new compactness theorem for
{\mathcal{G}}^{*}_{\text{met, fin}}
. Our context is when
{\mathcal{G}}^{*}_{\text{met, fin}}
is covered by the union of
\{X_{m}\}_{m\in{\mathbb{N}}}
. We then find some kind of finite sub-cover. Let us first recall a well known compactness theorem for standard (discrete) ultraproduct
{\mathcal{G}}^{*}
of
{\mathcal{G}}
(see (
1.1
)).

Theorem 2.6
.

The following facts are equivalent:

(1)

{\mathcal{G}}^{*}=\bigcup_{m\in{\mathbb{N}}}X_{m}

(2)

There is
N\in{\mathbb{N}}
such that
{\mathcal{G}}^{*}=X_{0}\cup\ldots\cup X_{N}
, which is equivalent with the condition: for
{\mathcal{U}}
-almost all
n\in{\mathbb{N}}

(2.8)

G_{n}=X_{0,n}\cup\ldots\cup X_{N,n}\text{ holds in }G_{n}.

Below is a generalization of Theorem
2.6
to the metric setting. We first formulate a result assuming that
\{\|\cdot\|_{n}\}_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
is uniformly bounded, that is
{\mathcal{G}}^{*}_{\text{met, fin}}={\mathcal{G}}^{*}_{\text{met}}
.

Theorem 2.7
.

Suppose
\sup\{\|\cdot\|_{n}:n\in{\mathbb{N}}\}<\infty
(then
{\mathcal{G}}^{*}_{\text{met, fin}}={\mathcal{G}}^{*}_{\text{met}}
). The following conditions are equivalent.

(1)

{\mathcal{G}}^{*}_{\text{met}}=\bigcup_{m\in{\mathbb{N}}}X_{m}

(2)

For any countable infinite sequence of positive reals
(\varepsilon_{0},\varepsilon_{1},\ldots)\subset{\mathbb{R}}_{>0}
there is
N\in{\mathbb{N}}
such that

(2.9)

{\mathcal{G}}^{*}_{\text{met}}=X_{0}{\mathcal{B}}_{\varepsilon_{0}}(e)\cup\ldots\cup X_{N}{\mathcal{B}}_{\varepsilon_{N}}(e),\text{ holds in }{\mathcal{G}}^{*}_{\text{met}},

which is equivalent with the clause: for
{\mathcal{U}}
-almost all
n\in{\mathbb{N}}

(2.10)

G_{n}=X_{0,n}{\mathcal{B}}_{\varepsilon_{0}}(e)\cup\ldots\cup X_{N,n}{\mathcal{B}}_{\varepsilon_{N}}(e),\text{ holds in }G_{n}.

We give a general version of our compactness theorem (unbounded case).

Theorem 2.8
.

The following conditions are equivalent.

(1)

{\mathcal{G}}^{*}_{\text{met, fin}}\subseteq\bigcup_{m\in{\mathbb{N}}}X_{m}

(2)

For every
t>0
and any infinite sequence of positive reals
(\varepsilon_{0},\varepsilon_{1},\ldots)\subset{\mathbb{R}}_{>0}
there is
N\in{\mathbb{N}}
such that
(\varepsilon_{0},\varepsilon_{1},\ldots,\varepsilon_{N})
has the following property (
2.11
): for
{\mathcal{U}}
-almost all
n\in{\mathbb{N}}

(2.11)

{\mathcal{B}}_{t}(e)\subseteq X_{0,n}{\mathcal{B}}_{\varepsilon_{0}}(e)\cup\ldots\cup X_{N,n}{\mathcal{B}}_{\varepsilon_{N}}(e)\text{ holds in }G_{n}.

(3)

For every
t>0
and any infinite sequence of positive reals
(\varepsilon_{0},\varepsilon_{1},\ldots)\subset{\mathbb{R}}_{>0}
there is
N\in{\mathbb{N}}
such that

(2.12)

{\mathcal{B}}_{t}(e)\subseteq X_{0}{\mathcal{B}}_{\varepsilon_{0}}(e)\cup\ldots\cup X_{N}{\mathcal{B}}_{\varepsilon_{N}}(e)\text{ holds in }{\mathcal{G}}^{*}_{\text{met, fin}}.

Proof.

(1)\Rightarrow(2)

Assume that (2) is not true. Let
t>0
and
(\varepsilon_{m})_{m\in{\mathbb{N}}}
be counterexamples. For any
N\in{\mathbb{N}}
define

(2.13)

U_{N}=\{n\in{\mathbb{N}}:(\varepsilon_{0},\varepsilon_{1},\ldots,\varepsilon_{N})\text{ fails to satisfy (\ref{eq:comp}) in }G_{n}\}\in{\mathcal{U}}.

For any
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
and
n\in U_{k}
let
g_{k,n}\in G_{n}
be such that
\|g_{k,n}\|_{n}<t
and

(2.14)

g_{k,n}\not\in X_{0,n}{\mathcal{B}}_{\varepsilon_{0}}(e)\cup\ldots\cup X_{k,n}{\mathcal{B}}_{\varepsilon_{k}}(e)\text{ in }G_{n}.

Clearly
U_{0}\supseteq U_{1}\supseteq U_{2}\supseteq\ldots
. We may assume that
\bigcap\{U_{k}:k\in{\mathbb{N}}\}=\emptyset
(just replace
U_{k}
by
U_{k}\setminus\{0,1,\ldots,k\}
, which is still in
{\mathcal{U}}
). For each
n\in U_{0}
let
k_{n}\in{\mathbb{N}}
be the greatest
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
such that
n\in U_{k}
, that is
n\in U_{k_{n}}
, which means that
(\varepsilon_{0},\varepsilon_{1},\ldots,\varepsilon_{k_{n}})
fails to satisfy (
2.11
) in
G_{n}
.

Then
\lim_{n\to{\mathcal{U}}}k_{n}=\infty
. Define

g=(g_{k_{n},n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}/{\mathcal{E}}\in{\mathcal{G}}^{*}_{\text{met}}.

Then
\|g\|\leq t
, so
g\in{\mathcal{G}}^{*}_{\text{met, fin}}
. However
g\not\in\bigcup_{m\in{\mathbb{N}}}X_{m}
, which gives the contradiction with (1). Indeed, we prove that
g\not\in X_{m}
for every
m\in{\mathbb{N}}
. (
2.13
) and (
2.14
) imply that

g_{k_{n},n}\not\in X_{m,n}{\mathcal{B}}_{\varepsilon_{m}}(e)

holds for all
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
such that
k_{n}>m
. Therefore the distance between
g
and
X_{m}
is at least
\varepsilon_{m}>0
, so
g\not\in X_{m}
.

(2)\Rightarrow(3)
is immediate by Definitions
1.2
and
1.4
.

(3)\Rightarrow(1)
Assume (1) fails. Then there is a non-trivial
g=(g_{m})_{m\in{\mathbb{N}}}/{\mathcal{E}}\in{\mathcal{G}}^{*}_{\text{met, fin}}
such that
g\not\in\bigcup_{m\in{\mathbb{N}}}X_{m}
. This means that
\left\|gy^{-1}\right\|>0
, for all
y\in X_{m}
. Since each
X_{m}
is a closed set (by Lemma
2.4
), there is
\varepsilon>0
such that
\left\|gy^{-1}\right\|>\varepsilon
for all
y\in X_{m}
,
m\in{\mathbb{N}}
. Hence define

\varepsilon_{m}=\inf\left\{\left\|gy^{-1}\right\|:y\in X_{m}\right\}>0.

Then (3) fails for
t:=\|g\|+1>0
and
(\varepsilon_{m})_{m\in{\mathbb{N}}}
. Indeed, suppose that
(\varepsilon_{0},\varepsilon_{1},\ldots,\varepsilon_{N})
has property (
2.12
) for some
N\in{\mathbb{N}}
. Then
\left\|gy^{-1}\right\|<\varepsilon_{m}
, for some
m\leq N
and
y\in X_{m}
, contradiction.
∎

There is a further generalization of Theorem
2.8
, where instead of
{\mathcal{G}}^{*}_{\text{met, fin}}
one can put any metrically internal set
Y
. That is, one can characterize the situation when
Y\cap{\mathcal{G}}^{*}_{\text{met, fin}}
is covered by a countably many metrically internal sets as a property of
{\mathcal{U}}
-almost all coordinates.

We do not need this stronger version of
2.8
, hence we only state this in Remark
2.9
below. The proof of Remark
2.9
can be easily derived from the proof of Theorem
2.8
.

Remark 2.9
.

Suppose
Y=\frac{Y_{0}\times Y_{1}\times Y_{2}\times\cdots}{{\mathcal{E}}}
is a metrically internal subset of
{\mathcal{G}}^{*}_{\text{met}}
. The following conditions are equivalent under the notation from Theorem
2.8
:

(1)

Y\cap{\mathcal{G}}^{*}_{\text{met, fin}}\subseteq\bigcup_{m\in{\mathbb{N}}}X_{m}

(2)

For every
t>0
and any infinite sequence of positive reals
(\varepsilon_{0},\varepsilon_{1},\ldots)\subset{\mathbb{R}}_{>0}
there is
N\in{\mathbb{N}}
such that
(\varepsilon_{0},\varepsilon_{1},\ldots,\varepsilon_{N})
has the following property (
2.15
) in
G_{n}
, for
{\mathcal{U}}
-almost all
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
:

(2.15)

Y_{n}\cap{\mathcal{B}}_{t}(e)\subseteq X_{0,n}{\mathcal{B}}_{\varepsilon_{0}}(e)\cup\ldots\cup X_{N,n}{\mathcal{B}}_{\varepsilon_{N}}(e)\text{ holds in }G_{n}.

3.
Corollaries of compactness theorem

We give a couple of consequences of our compactness theorems
2.7
and
2.8
, mainly in the
bounded case
, that is when:

(3.1)

\sup\{\|\cdot\|_{n}:n\in{\mathbb{N}}\}<\infty.

This condition implies that
{\mathcal{G}}^{*}_{\text{met, fin}}={\mathcal{G}}^{*}_{\text{met}}
. We write explicitly
bounded case
, when (
3.1
) is assumed.

3.1.
Square of finite subcover

Lemma
1.5
(2) asserts that
({\mathcal{G}}^{*}_{\text{met, fin}},\|\cdot\|)
is a complete metric space, so we can apply Baire category argument. We give another consequence of cover condition (1) from Theorem
2.7
.

Theorem 3.1
.

(bounded case)
If
{\mathcal{G}}^{*}_{\text{met}}=\bigcup_{m\in{\mathbb{N}}}X_{m}
, where each
X_{m}
is metrically-internal, then there is
N\in{\mathbb{N}}
such that

(3.2)

{\mathcal{G}}^{*}_{\text{met}}=\left(X_{0}\cup\ldots\cup X_{N}\right)^{2}.

Let us observe that (
3.2
) from Theorem
3.1
cannot be simplified to apparently simpler condition
{\mathcal{G}}^{*}_{\text{met}}\subseteq X_{1}\cup\ldots\cup X_{N}
, as shown in Example
5.5
.

Proof.

({\mathcal{G}}^{*}_{\text{met}},\|\cdot\|)
is a complete metric space by Lemma
1.5
(2). Each
X_{m}\subseteq{\mathcal{G}}^{*}_{\text{met}}
is closed subset by Lemma
2.4
. By Baire category theorem, some
X_{m}
has a non-empty interior, that is there is
m\in{\mathbb{N}}
,
\varepsilon>0
and
g\in X_{m}
such that

(3.3)

{\mathcal{B}}_{\varepsilon}(g)\subseteq X_{m},\text{ hence }{\mathcal{B}}_{\varepsilon}(e)\subseteq X_{m}g^{-1}.

By applying Theorem
2.7
to
(\varepsilon,\varepsilon,\varepsilon,\ldots)
, we get
N^{\prime}\in{\mathbb{N}}
such that

(3.4)

{\mathcal{G}}^{*}_{\text{met}}=X_{0}{\mathcal{B}}_{\varepsilon}(e)\cup\ldots\cup X_{N^{\prime}}{\mathcal{B}}_{\varepsilon}(e)=\left(X_{0}\cup\ldots\cup X_{N^{\prime}}\right)\cdot X_{m}\cdot g^{-1}.

Whence
{\mathcal{G}}^{*}_{\text{met}}=\left(X_{0}\cup\ldots\cup X_{N^{\prime}}\right)\cdot X_{m}
. The conclusion is true for
N=\max\{N^{\prime},m\}
.
∎

3.2.
Bounded generation, torsion, perfectness and simplicity

Standard (discrete) ultraproduct
{\mathcal{G}}^{*}
is
saturated
in model-theoretic sense. This fact has many immediate corollaries around
uniform group properties
. Let us remind that by a
commutator

\left[g,h\right]
we mean
g^{-1}h^{-1}gh
. A group
G
is
perfect
, if every element of
G
is a product of commutators. Here are well known facts about standard ultraproduct
{\mathcal{G}}^{*}
:

•

if
{\mathcal{G}}^{*}
is a perfect group, then
{\mathcal{G}}^{*}
is
uniformly perfect
(Corollary
3.3
);

•

if
{\mathcal{G}}^{*}
is a simple group, then
{\mathcal{G}}^{*}
must be
uniformly simple
(Definition
3.8
(2));

•

if
{\mathcal{G}}^{*}
is a torsion group (i.e. for every element
g\in{\mathcal{G}}^{*}
there is
N\in{\mathbb{N}}
, such that
g^{N}=e
), then
{\mathcal{G}}^{*}
is
uniformly torsion
(also called
of finite exponent
), that is there is
N\in{\mathbb{N}}
such that
g^{N}=e
, for all
g\in{\mathcal{G}}^{*}
.

We derive below generalization some of these facts to metric ultraproduct
{\mathcal{G}}^{*}_{\text{met}}
.

3.2.1.
Bounded generation

We first give a general fact on
bounded generation
.

Lemma 3.2
.

(bounded case)
Suppose
X\subseteq{\mathcal{G}}^{*}_{\text{met}}
is a metrically internal subset. If
X
generates
{\mathcal{G}}^{*}_{\text{met}}
, then
X
generates in finitely many steps, i.e. there is
N\in{\mathbb{N}}
such that

{\mathcal{G}}^{*}_{\text{met}}=\left(X\cup X^{-1}\right)^{N}.

Proof.

Define
X_{m}=\left(X\cup X^{-1}\right)^{m}
. Then
{\mathcal{G}}^{*}_{\text{met}}=\bigcup_{m\in{\mathbb{N}}}X_{m}
, so the conclusion follows from Theorem
3.1
.
∎

We now derive a couple of corollaries.

3.2.2.
Uniform perfectness

An immediate consequence of Lemma
3.2
is the following corollary.

Corollary 3.3
.

(bounded case)
Suppose
{\mathcal{G}}^{*}_{\text{met}}
is perfect, then
{\mathcal{G}}^{*}_{\text{met}}
is uniformly perfect, i.e. there is
N\in{\mathbb{N}}
such that every element of
{\mathcal{G}}^{*}_{\text{met}}
is a product of
N
commutators.

Proof.

The conclusion follows from Lemma
3.2
, as by Example
2.2
(
2
),
X=\left\{\left[\bar{g},\bar{h}\right]:\bar{g},\bar{h}\in{\mathcal{G}}^{*}_{\text{met}}\right\}
is metrically internal.
∎

Obviously if
{\mathcal{G}}^{*}
is a perfect group, then so is
{\mathcal{G}}^{*}_{\text{met}}
, as
{\mathcal{G}}^{*}_{\text{met}}
is a homomorphic image of
{\mathcal{G}}^{*}
(Remark
1.3
(2)). The converse is not true in general, there is non-perfect
{\mathcal{G}}^{*}
with perfect
{\mathcal{G}}^{*}_{\text{met}}
, see Example
5.7
. Therefore Corollary
3.3
cannot be obtained by using only classical compactness theorem
2.6
applied to
{\mathcal{G}}^{*}
.

3.2.3.
Uniform torsion

Let us now consider torsion groups.

A well known fact of standard ultraproduct
{\mathcal{G}}^{*}
is:
{\mathcal{G}}^{*}
is torsion if and only if
{\mathcal{G}}^{*}
is uniformly torsion. We conjecture that the same is true for any metric ultraproduct
{\mathcal{G}}^{*}_{\text{met}}
.

Conjecture 3.4
.

If
{\mathcal{G}}^{*}_{\text{met}}
is a torsion group, then
{\mathcal{G}}^{*}_{\text{met}}
has finite exponent (i.e. is uniformly torsion).

We are able to prove an
\varepsilon
-analogue of Conjecture
3.4
for
{\mathcal{G}}^{*}_{\text{met}}
, provided that
\varepsilon>0
.

Definition 3.5
.

Fix
\varepsilon>0
and metric group
(G,\|\cdot\|)
.

(1)

G
is called
\varepsilon
-torsion
, if for every
g\in G
there is
N\in{\mathbb{N}}
such that
\|g^{N}\|<\varepsilon
. A stronger notion than (1) is (2):

(2)

G
is called
uniformly
\varepsilon
-torsion
(or
\varepsilon
-finite exponent
) if there is
N\in{\mathbb{N}}
, such that
\|g^{N}\|<\varepsilon
for all
g\in G
. A bit weaker that (2), but still stronger that (1) is (3) below.

(3)

G
is called
almost uniformly
\varepsilon
-torsion
if there is
N\in{\mathbb{N}}
, such that for every
g\in G
there is
m\leq N
with
\|g^{m}\|<\varepsilon
.

Let us apply Theorem
2.7
together with Example
2.2
(
5
).

Corollary 3.6
.

(bounded case) Fix
\varepsilon>0
.
If
{\mathcal{G}}^{*}_{\text{met}}
is
\varepsilon
-torsion group, then
G
is almost uniformly
2\varepsilon
-torsion.

Observe that a circle group
{\mathcal{S}}=(S^{1},\cdot)
is a non-torsion group, but
\varepsilon
-torsion for every
\varepsilon>0
. Moreover
{\mathcal{S}}^{*}_{\text{met}}={\mathcal{S}}
(by Example
2.5
) is not uniformly
\varepsilon
-torsion, but almost uniformly
\varepsilon
-torsion, for every
\varepsilon>0
. Hence Corollary
3.6
cannot be improved to uniform
\varepsilon
-torsion.

Proof.

Let
X_{m}=\frac{\prod_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}T_{m,\varepsilon}(G_{n})}{{\mathcal{E}}}
, for
m\in{\mathbb{N}}_{>0}
be metrically internal sets from (
2.3
) in Example
2.2
(
5
). Then
{\mathcal{G}}^{*}_{\text{met}}=\bigcup_{m\in{\mathbb{N}}_{>0}}X_{m}
, since
{\mathcal{G}}^{*}_{\text{met}}
is
\varepsilon
-torsion. Let us apply (
2.9
) from Theorem
2.7
to
(\varepsilon,\frac{\varepsilon}{2},\frac{\varepsilon}{3},\frac{\varepsilon}{4},\ldots)
. Then there is
N\in{\mathbb{N}}
such that

{\mathcal{G}}^{*}_{\text{met}}=\bigcup_{m=1}^{N}{\mathcal{B}}_{\frac{\varepsilon}{m}}(e)X_{m}.

Take any
g\in{\mathcal{G}}^{*}_{\text{met}}
, then there is
m\leq N
and
b\in{\mathcal{B}}_{\frac{\varepsilon}{m}}(e)
,
x\in X_{m}
such that
g=bx
. Then
\|g^{m}\|=\|(bx)^{m}\|=\|b^{m}x^{m}\|\leq\|b^{m}\|+\|x^{m}\|<m\|b\|+\varepsilon<2\varepsilon
. Hence
\|g^{m}\|<2\varepsilon
.
∎

Remark 3.7
.

Many examples of metric groups we consider do satisfy the following property:

(3.5)

\|g^{n}\|\leq\|g\|,\text{ for any }g\in G,\ n\in{\mathbb{N}}.

In particular (
3.5
) holds for:

•

permutation groups with the Hamming norm
(S_{n},\|\cdot\|_{H})
(Definition
5.1
),

•

finite groups with conjugacy length
[
ST14
, Lemma 2.5]
,
[
LS01
, Theorem 1.1]
, which is a pseudo-norm:

\|g\|_{c}=\frac{\log\left(\left|g^{G}\right|\right)}{\log\left(|G|\right)},

•

linear groups with the Jordan length: see (

If a metric group

3.2.4.
Uniform and bounded simplicity

Let us consider
simplicity
and related stronger properties.

Definition 3.8
.

(1)

A group
G
is called
boundedly simple
if for any
g\in G\setminus\{e\}
there is a natural number
N
such that

(3.6)

C_{N}(g,G):=\left(g^{G}\cup g^{-1G}\right)^{\leq N}=G.

In other words
C_{N}(g,G)
is the set of all products of at most
N
conjugates of
g
and
g^{-1}
.

(2)

G
is call
uniformly simple
if there exist
N\in{\mathbb{N}}
such that
C_{N}(g,G)=G
for every
g\in G\setminus\{e\}
. We say then that
G
is
N
-uniformly simple
.

Bounded simplicity appeared in the literature in many places (see
[
GG17
, Section 1]
) and under different names, for example as
bounded normal generation

[
DT19
]
. It is a well know fact that discrete ultraproduct
{\mathcal{G}}^{*}
is simple if and only if
{\mathcal{G}}^{*}
is uniformly simple. This is not true for
{\mathcal{G}}^{*}_{\text{met}}
(see Example
5.4
). In a metric case we need to switch to bounded simplicity.

Corollary 3.9
.

(bounded case)

{\mathcal{G}}^{*}_{\text{met}}
is simple if and only if
{\mathcal{G}}^{*}_{\text{met}}
is boundedly simple.

Proof.

By Example
2.2
(
1
) every nontrivial conjugacy class
g^{{\mathcal{G}}^{*}_{\text{met}}}
is metrically internal and generates
{\mathcal{G}}^{*}_{\text{met}}
(since
{\mathcal{G}}^{*}_{\text{met}}
is simple). The conclusion follows from Lemma
3.2
.
∎

A further study of simplicity
{\mathcal{G}}^{*}_{\text{met, fin}}
, also in unbounded case (under some mild Assumption
4.1
) is contained in Section
4
.

4.
Simplicity

This section is devoted the general study of
{\mathcal{G}}^{*}_{\text{met, fin}}
. We do not longer assume that norms are uniformly bounded. We extend Corollary
3.9
and give a general criterion for simplicity of
{\mathcal{G}}^{*}_{\text{met, fin}}
, under an Assumption
4.1
below.

Let
{\mathcal{G}}=(G_{n},\|\cdot\|_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
be a family of metric groups. We need to assume the following condition (Assumption
4.1
), in order to give a smooth criterion for simplicity of metric ultraproducts of metric groups.
This assumption is clearly satisfied when
\{\|\cdot\|_{n}\}_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
are uniformly bounded; that is when
\sup\left\{\|g\|_{n}:n\in{\mathbb{N}},g\in G_{n}\right\}<\infty
. However, there are many important family of unbounded metric groups which do satisfy (
4.1
). Intuitively (
4.1
) says that conjugacy class of any
g\in\bigcup_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}G_{n}
can be determined by uniformly
\|\cdot\|
-short elements.

Assumption 4.1
.

There is a non-decreasing function
F_{\mathcal{G}}\colon{\mathbb{R}}_{>0}\to{\mathbb{R}}_{>0}
for a family of pseudo-metric groups
{\mathcal{G}}=(G_{n},\|\cdot\|_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
such that, for every
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
and any
g\in G_{n}
,

g^{G_{n}}=\left\{h^{-1}gh:h\in G_{n},\ \|h\|_{n}<F_{\mathcal{G}}\left(\left\|g\right\|_{n}\right)\right\}.

If norms are uniformly bounded, then Assumption
4.1
is true.

We use the following immediate application of Assumption
4.1
, which says that products of conjugacy classes of elements from
{\mathcal{G}}^{*}_{\text{met, fin}}
computed in a bigger group
{\mathcal{G}}^{*}_{\text{met}}
behaves well after intersecting them with
{\mathcal{G}}^{*}_{\text{met, fin}}
.

Fact 4.2
.

Under Assumption
4.1
the following holds: for any
\bar{g}\in{\mathcal{G}}^{*}_{\text{met, fin}}
and any
n\in{\mathbb{N}}

\bar{g}^{{\mathcal{G}}^{*}_{\text{met}}}=\bar{g}^{{\mathcal{G}}^{*}_{\text{met, fin}}},\ \ C_{n}\left(\bar{g},{\mathcal{G}}^{*}_{\text{met}}\right)=C_{n}\left(\bar{g},{\mathcal{G}}^{*}_{\text{met, fin}}\right)

(where
C_{n}(g,G)=\left(g^{G}\cup{g^{-1}}^{G}\right)^{\leq n}
for
n\geq 1
, and
C_{0}(g,G)=\{e\}
, according to (
3.6
)).
Therefore
C_{n}\left(\bar{g},{\mathcal{G}}^{*}_{\text{met, fin}}\right)
is a closed subset of
{\mathcal{G}}^{*}_{\text{met, fin}}
by Example
2.2
and Lemma
2.4
.

Proof.

It is enough to prove that
\bar{g}^{{\mathcal{G}}^{*}_{\text{met}}}\subseteq\bar{g}^{{\mathcal{G}}^{*}_{\text{met, fin}}}
, as the rest follows by Remark
2.3
. Take
\bar{x}\in\bar{g}^{{\mathcal{G}}^{*}_{\text{met}}}
, then
\bar{x}=(x_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}/{\mathcal{E}}=\left(g_{n}^{h_{n}}\right)_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}/{\mathcal{E}}
, where
\|h_{n}\|_{n}<F_{{\mathcal{G}}}(\|\bar{g}\|)
. Hence

\bar{h}=(h_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}\in{\mathcal{G}}^{*}_{\text{met, fin}},

so
\bar{x}\in\bar{g}^{{\mathcal{G}}^{*}_{\text{met, fin}}}
.
∎

4.3.
Metric and topological simplicity

The condition (2) from Theorem
4.3
for a single metric group
(G,\|\cdot\|)
(that is,
{\mathcal{G}}
consists only of one metric group) does not depend on the choice of ultrafilter
{\mathcal{U}}
. Therefore, it make sense to have the following definition.

Definition 4.9
.

A metric group
(G,\|\cdot\|)
is called
metrically simple
if its ultrapower
G^{*}_{\text{met, fin}}
is a simple group, i.e.
G
satisfies the condition (2) from Theorem
4.3
.

A topological
G
group is called
topologically simple
if every nontrivial normal subgroup of
G
is dense.

Proposition 4.10
.

(1)

Every simple compact metric group (e.g.
SO_{3}({\mathbb{R}})
) is metrically simple (see Definition
4.9
).

(2)

Metric simplicity implies topological simplicity, that is if
G
is metrically simple, then
G
has no closed normal proper subgroups.

Proof.

(1) If
(G,\|\cdot\|)
is a compact metric group, then
\|\cdot\|
is a bounded function on
G
and
G=G^{*}_{\text{met, fin}}
, as every sequence from
G
is
{\mathcal{E}}
-equivalent with a constant sequence. Therefore
G^{*}_{\text{met, fin}}
is simple.

(2) If
N\lhd G
is a closed normal subgroup, then every
g\in G\setminus N
gives

g^{*}=(g,g,g,\ldots){\mathcal{E}}\in G^{*}_{\text{met, fin}}\setminus N^{*}_{\text{met, fin}},

so
G^{*}_{\text{met, fin}}
is not simple, as it has
N^{*}_{\text{met, fin}}
as a proper normal subgroup.
∎

Since
({\mathcal{G}}^{*}_{\text{met, fin}},\|\cdot\|)
is a topological group (Lemma
1.5
(1)), it makes sense to ask: is there a criterion for topological simplicity of
{\mathcal{G}}^{*}_{\text{met, fin}}
? We answer this question in theorem below.

Theorem 4.11
.

The following conditions are equivalent

(1)

Finitary metric ultraproduct
{\mathcal{G}}^{*}_{\text{met, fin}}
is a topologically simple group.

(2)

For all
t>r>0
and
\varepsilon>0
there is
N\in{\mathbb{N}}
such that for all
g\in G
with
\|g\|\in(r,t]

{\mathcal{B}}_{t}(e)\subseteq C_{N}(g,G){\mathcal{B}}_{\varepsilon}(e)\text{ holds in }G.

Proof.

(1)\Rightarrow(2)
Suppose (2) fails, that is there are
t>r>0
and
\varepsilon>0
such that for all
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
there are

with

(1)

\|g_{n}\|\in(r,t]
,
\|h_{n}\|<t
,

(2)

h_{n}\not\in C_{n}(g_{n},G){\mathcal{B}}_{\varepsilon}(e)
.

Take

and

from

. Then

h^{*}\not\in\langle\langle g^{*}\rangle\rangle{\mathcal{B}}_{\varepsilon}(e)\supseteq\overline{\langle\langle g^{*}\rangle\rangle},

where

is the normal subgroup of

generated by

. So

is a proper normal closed subgroup of

.

Suppose

∎

6.

Theorem
6.1
below yields more simple ultrapowers. Intuitively it says the following: if a metric group

{\mathcal{G}}=(G_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}

of

We prove in Theorem

, as a corollary of

\mathcal{IET}
group (the group of all interval exchange transformations of
[0,1]

4.9
).

Theorem 6.1
.

Let
{\mathcal{G}}=\left(G_{n},\|\cdot\|^{\prime}_{n}\right)_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
and
{\mathcal{H}}=(H,\|\cdot\|)
be metric groups and fix a nonprincipal ultafilter
{\mathcal{U}}
. Suppose that
{\mathcal{G}}^{*}_{\text{met, fin}}
is a simple group (boundedly simple resp.). Assume that the following condition is true for
{\mathcal{H}}
:

for any
t>0
and any
\varepsilon>0
and any

with
\|h_{1}\|,\|h_{2}\|\leq t
there are
{\mathcal{U}}
-many

such that there are
g_{1},g_{2}\in G_{n}
and
\phi_{n}\colon G_{n}\rightarrow H
an isometric homomorphism (i.e.
\left\|\phi_{n}(g)\right\|=\|g\|^{\prime}_{n}
) satisfying
\left\|\phi_{n}(g_{i}){h_{i}}^{-1}\right\|<\varepsilon
, for

.

Then

is a simple group (boundedly simple resp.) with respect to
{\mathcal{U}}
.

Proof.

Let us prove
(2)
of

4.5
for
{\mathcal{H}}
. Fix a positive numbers

and an infinite sequence of positive reals
(\varepsilon_{0},\varepsilon_{1},\ldots)
.

Since
{\mathcal{G}}^{*}_{\text{met, fin}}
is a simple group, Theorem
4.3
(2) applied to
{\mathcal{G}}
and to
\left(\frac{\varepsilon_{0}}{2},\frac{\varepsilon_{1}}{2},\ldots\right)
gives
N\in{\mathbb{N}}
such that

(6.1)

I=\left\{n\in{\mathbb{N}}:\left(\frac{\varepsilon_{0}}{2},\frac{\varepsilon_{1}}{2},\ldots,\frac{\varepsilon_{N}}{2}\right)\text{ is }\left(\frac{r}{2},2t\right)\text{-big for }G_{n}\right\}\in{\mathcal{U}},

see Definition
4.6
for ’big’ notation.
Suppose
h_{1}\in H
with
\|h_{1}\|\ >r
. Take any
h_{2}\in B_{t}(e)
in
H
. We have to prove that
h_{2}\in C_{j}(h^{\prime},H)B_{\varepsilon_{j}}(e)
for some

and

and take

suitable for

.
Since

7.
\mathcal{IET}
group

Let us apply Theorem
6.1
to get interesting examples of simple metric ultraproducts.

An
interval exchange transformation
is a bijective map
f\colon[0,1]\rightarrow[0,1]
which is piecewise translation, continuous on the right with finitely many discontinuity points. Note that any such transformation is given by a pair:
a sequence of real numbers

0=a_{1}<a_{2}<\ldots<a_{n}=1
and a permutation
\sigma\in S_{n}
. Denote such transformation by
T=T(a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{n},\sigma)
. Such
T
translates
\left[a_{i},a_{i+1}\right)
onto
\left[a_{\sigma(i)},a_{\sigma(i+1)}\right)
.

The set of all interval exchange transformation with composition form a group, which we denote by
\mathcal{IET}
. A bi-invariant norm of an element
g\in\mathcal{IET}
is a Lebesgue measure of its support:

\|g\|_{\mu}=\mu(\supp(g)).

Theorem 7.1
.

Any metric ultrapower of
\mathcal{IET}
with respect to
\|\cdot\|_{\mu}
is a simple group, in fact boundedly simple (Definition
3.8
).

Proof.

Consider
{\mathcal{G}}=\left(S_{n},\frac{1}{n}\|\cdot\|_{H}\right)_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
, where
\|\cdot\|_{H}
is the Hamming norm and
{\mathcal{H}}=(\mathcal{IET},\|\cdot~\|_{\mu})
. Example
5.4
implies that
{\mathcal{G}}^{*}_{\text{met, fin}}
is a simple group (in fact
{\mathcal{G}}^{*}_{\text{met, fin}}
is boundedly simple). It is enough to show, by Theorem
6.1
, that for any
\varepsilon>0
there is a natural number
N
such that for any
n\geq N
and any
h_{1},h_{2}\in\mathcal{IET}
there are
\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2}\in S_{n}
and an isometric homomorphism
\phi\colon S_{n}\to\mathcal{IET}
such that
\left\|\phi(\sigma_{i})h_{i}^{-1}\right\|<\varepsilon
,
i=1,2
.

Let
h_{1},h_{2}\in\mathcal{IET}
where

h_{1}=T(a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{k},\sigma_{1})
, and
h_{2}=T(b_{1},b_{2},\ldots,b_{l},\sigma_{2})
.
Let
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
be such that

n>\frac{k}{\varepsilon}
,
n>\frac{l}{\varepsilon}
and

\frac{1}{n}<\min\left(\{a_{i+1}-a_{i}:i=1,2,\ldots,k\}\cup\{b_{i+1}-b_{i}:i=1,2,\ldots,l\}\right)
. Let
a^{\prime}_{i}=\frac{\lfloor na_{i}\rfloor}{n}
and
b^{\prime}_{j}=\frac{\lfloor nb_{j}\rfloor}{n}
for
1\leq i\leq k
,
1\leq j\leq l
. Define
h^{\prime}_{1}=T(a^{\prime}_{1},a^{\prime}_{2},\ldots,a^{\prime}_{k},\sigma_{1})
, and
h^{\prime}_{2}=T(b^{\prime}_{1},b^{\prime}_{2},\ldots,b^{\prime}_{l},\sigma_{2})
.
Elements
h^{\prime}_{1},h^{\prime}_{2}
are given also by a sequence
\left(\frac{1}{n},\frac{2}{n},\ldots\frac{n}{n}\right)
and permutations
\sigma^{\prime}_{1},\sigma^{\prime}_{2}\in S_{n}
:

h^{\prime}_{1}=T\left(\frac{1}{n},\frac{2}{n},\ldots\frac{n}{n},\sigma^{\prime}_{1}\right),\text{ and }h^{\prime}_{2}=T\left(\frac{1}{n},\frac{2}{n},\ldots\frac{n}{n},\sigma^{\prime}_{2}\right).

Define a monomorphism
\phi\colon S_{n}\rightarrow\mathcal{IET}
by

\phi(\delta)=T\left(\frac{1}{n},\frac{2}{n},\ldots\frac{n}{n},\delta\right)
. Thus
h^{\prime}_{i}=\phi\left(\sigma^{\prime}_{i}\right)
. We have

\left\|\phi\left(\sigma^{\prime}_{i}\right)h_{i}^{-1}\right\|_{\mu}<\max\left(\frac{k}{n},\frac{l}{n}\right)<\varepsilon.

It is easy to check that
\phi
is an isometry.
∎

8.
Metrically uniformly simple groups

Our examples of simple metric ultraproducts from sections
5
,
7
do satisfy another property, which we call
metric uniformly simplicity
in Definition
8.1
below.
Recall from Definition
3.8
that
C_{N}(g,G):=\left(g^{G}\cup g^{-1G}\right)^{\leq N}
.

Definition 8.1
.

(1)

A group with pseudo-metric
(G,\|\cdot\|)
is called

metrically uniformly simple
if for all
t>r>0
, there is
N\in{\mathbb{N}}
such that
C_{N}(g,G)\supseteq{\mathcal{B}}_{t}(e)
holds for all
g\in G
with
\|g\|\in(r,t]
.

(2)

A family of groups with pseudo-metrics
{\mathcal{G}}=(G_{n},\|\cdot\|_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
is called
metrically uniformly simple
if for all
t>r>0
, there is
N\in{\mathbb{N}}
such that
C_{N}(g,G_{i})\supseteq{\mathcal{B}}_{t}(e)
holds for all
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
and for any
g\in G_{n}
with
\|g\|\in(r,t]
.

We conjecture the following.

Conjecture 8.2
.

If a metric ultraproduct
({\mathcal{G}}^{*}_{\text{met,fin}},\|\cdot\|)
of a family of pseudo-metric group
{\mathcal{G}}
is simple, then
{\mathcal{G}}^{*}_{\text{met,fin}}
must be metrically uniformly simple (Definition
8.1
(1)).

Remark 8.3
.

Observe that metric uniformly simplicity is preserved under taking metric ultraproducts, that is metric ultraproduct of metrically uniformly simple family of groups is metrically uniformly simple.

Lemma 8.4
.

Let
{\mathcal{G}}=(G_{n},\|\cdot\|_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
be a family of metric groups. A metric ultraproduct
G^{*}_{\text{met, fin}}
is metrically uniformly simple if and only if for any
t>r>0
there is
N\in{\mathbb{N}}
such that for any
\varepsilon>0
the following holds

\text{for }{\mathcal{U}}\text{-almost all }n\in{\mathbb{N}}\ \ C_{N}(g,G_{n})\cap{\mathcal{B}}_{t}(e)\text{ is }\varepsilon\text{-dense in }{\mathcal{B}}_{t}(e)\text{ in }G_{n}.

Proof.

(\Rightarrow)
Suppose
G^{*}_{\text{met, fin}}
is metrically uniformly simple. Let
e\neq\bar{g}\in G^{*}_{\text{met, fin}}

and let
(g_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}\in\prod_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}G_{n}
be such that
\bar{g}=(g_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}{\mathcal{E}}
. Fix
0<r<\|\bar{g}\|\leq t
and let
N
be as in Definition
8.1
. We have that

\left\{n\in{\mathbb{N}}:C_{N}(g_{n},G_{n})\cap{\mathcal{B}}_{t}(e)\text{ is $\varepsilon$-dense in }{\mathcal{B}}_{t}(e)\text{ in }G_{n}\right\}\in{\mathcal{U}},

for any
\varepsilon>0
.
So we conclude that
C_{N}(\bar{g},G^{*}_{\text{met, fin}})\supseteq{\mathcal{B}}_{t}(e)
in
G^{*}_{\text{met, fin}}
.

(\Leftarrow)
For contradiction, suppose that there are
t>r>0
such that for every natural number
N
there is
\varepsilon_{N}>0
such that
U_{N}\in{\mathcal{U}}
, where
U_{N}=

\left\{n\in{\mathbb{N}}:(\exists g_{N,n}\in G_{n})\|g_{N,n}\|_{n}\in(r,t]\land C_{N}(g_{N,n},G_{n})\cap{\mathcal{B}}_{t}(e)\text{ is not $\varepsilon_{N}$-dense in }{\mathcal{B}}_{t}(e)\right\}.

For
N\in{\mathbb{N}}
and
n\in U_{N}
let
h_{N,n}\in{\mathcal{B}}_{t}(e)
such that

\inf\left\{\|h_{N,n}g^{-1}\|:g\in C_{N}(g_{N,n},G_{n})\right\}>\varepsilon_{N}.

This gives in ultraproduct
{\mathcal{G}}^{*}_{\text{met, fin}}
elements

\overline{g_{N}}=(g_{N,1},g_{N,2},\ldots){\mathcal{E}}\text{ and }\overline{h_{N}}=(h_{N,1},h_{N,2},\ldots){\mathcal{E}},

such that
\|\overline{g_{N}}\|\in(r,t]
,
\|\overline{h_{N}}\|\leq t
and

\overline{h_{N}}\not\in C_{N}\left(\overline{g_{N}},G^{*}_{\text{met, fin}}\right),

for any
N\in{\mathbb{N}}
. Hence,
G^{*}_{\text{met, fin}}
can not be metrically uniformly simple.
∎

From now we assume that all norms are bounded, that is
\|\cdot\|\leq 1
.

We will show that Conjecture
8.2
is true under some natural assumption. First, in Theorem

-property is metrically uniformly simple. Next, in Theorem

-property.

Let us introduce some notion.

Definition 8.5
.

For a group
G
and
g\in G
denote

N(g,G)=\min\{n\in{\mathbb{N}}:C_{n}(g,G)=G\}
.

Let us introduce
(\star)
-property, which is crucial in arguments below.

Definition 8.6
.

We say that a family of groups
{\mathcal{G}}
has
(\star)
-property if (1) and (2) are true:

(1)

there is a natural number
N
such that for any
G\in{\mathcal{G}}
there is
g\in G
such that
C_{N}(g,G)=G
;

(2)

for any
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
there is
l\in{\mathbb{N}}
such that for any group
G\in{\mathcal{G}}
and elements
g,h\in G
if
N(g,G),N(h,G)\geq l
, then
N(gh,G)\geq k
.

Now we prove that every family of metric groups which is metrically uniformly simple has
(\star)
-property.

Fact 8.7
.

If
{\mathcal{G}}
is a family of metric groups which is
metrically uniformly simple (see Definition
8.1
), then
{\mathcal{G}}
has
(\star)
-property.

Proof.

Let
{\mathcal{G}}=(G_{n},\|\cdot\|_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
be a metrically uniformly simple family of groups.
For a number
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
take
l\in{\mathbb{N}}
such that for any
i\in{\mathbb{N}}
and any
g\in G_{i}
with
\|g\|>\frac{1}{2k}
we have
C_{l}(g,G_{i})=G_{i}
.

So if we take any
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
and any
g,h\in G_{i}

with
N(g,G_{i}),N(h,G_{i})>l
then
\|g\|,\|h\|<\frac{1}{2k}
so
\|gh\|<\frac{1}{k}
, and so
N(gh,G_{i})>k
.
∎

Remark 8.8
.

By theorem 1.1
[
LS01
]
, a family of finite simple groups has a
(\star)
-property. We see as consequence of Theorem
9.4
, that
(\PSL_{n}({\mathbb{C}}))_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
has
(\star)
-property.

Next notion allows us to define a set of infinitesimal sequences in products of any (even in non-necessarily metric) groups.

Definition 8.9
.

For a family of groups
{\mathcal{G}}=(G_{n})_{n\in N}
and a nonprincipal ultrafilter
{\mathcal{U}}
on
N
define

Z_{{\mathcal{U}}}=\left\{(g_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}:\forall k\in{\mathbb{N}}\ \ \{n\in{\mathbb{N}}:N(g_{n},G_{n})>k\}\in{\mathcal{U}}\right\}.

Fact 8.10
.

If a family
{\mathcal{G}}=(G_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
has a
(\star)
-property, then
Z_{{\mathcal{U}}}
is a normal subgroup of
\prod_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}G_{n}
, for any non-principal ultrafilter
{\mathcal{U}}
. Moreover
\frac{\prod_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}G_{n}}{Z_{{\mathcal{U}}}}
is a non-trivial boundedly simple group (see Definition
3.8
).

Proof.

Obviously
Z_{{\mathcal{U}}}
is a normal subset of
\prod_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}G_{n}
, that is
Z_{{\mathcal{U}}}
is closed under conjugation. It is enough to prove that
Z_{{\mathcal{U}}}
is closed under multiplication. Let
g=(g_{n})_{n\in N},h=(h_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}\in Z_{{\mathcal{U}}}
.
Fix
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
.
Observe that

\{n\in{\mathbb{N}}:N(g_{n}\cdot h_{n},G_{n})>k\}\in{\mathcal{U}},

so
(g_{n}\cdot h_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}\in Z_{{\mathcal{U}}}
.
Take a natural number
l
as in definition of
(\star)
-property.
Put

U_{g}=\{n\in{\mathbb{N}}:N(g_{n},G_{n})>l\}\text{ and }U_{h}=\{n\in{\mathbb{N}}:N(h_{n},G_{n})>l\}.

Let
U=U_{g}\cap U_{h}\in{\mathcal{U}}
. Then
N(g_{n},G_{n}),N(h_{n},G_{n})\geq l
, for any
n\in U
. Thus we have that
N(g_{n}\cdot h_{n},G_{n})\geq k
, hence
(g_{n})_{n\in N}\cdot(h_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}\in Z_{{\mathcal{U}}}
.

Let us prove the moreover part. By
(1)
of
(\star)
-property
Z_{{\mathcal{U}}}\neq\prod_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}G_{n}
.
Let
(g_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}\notin Z_{{\mathcal{U}}}
.
There is a natural number
k
such that

\{n\in{\mathbb{N}}:N(g_{n},G_{n})\leq k\}\in{\mathcal{U}}
, so

\{n\in{\mathbb{N}}:C_{k}(g_{n},G_{n})=G_{n}\}\in{\mathcal{U}}.

Finally,

C_{k}\left((g_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}Z_{{\mathcal{U}}},\frac{\prod_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}G_{n}}{Z_{{\mathcal{U}}}}\right)=\frac{\prod_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}C_{k}(g_{n},G_{n})}{Z_{{\mathcal{U}}}}=\prod_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}\frac{G_{n}}{Z_{{\mathcal{U}}}},

so
\frac{\prod_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}G_{n}}{Z_{{\mathcal{U}}}}
is boundedly simple.
∎

Fact 8.11
.

Let
{\mathcal{G}}=(G_{n},\|\cdot\|_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
be a family of pseudo-metric groups having
(\star)
-property. Then
{\mathcal{E}}\leq Z_{{\mathcal{U}}}
.
Moreover if
G^{*}_{\text{met}}
is simple then
{\mathcal{E}}=Z_{{\mathcal{U}}}
(see Definition (
1.2
) for
{\mathcal{E}}
).

Proof.

Let
\bar{g}=(g_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}\in{\mathcal{E}}
and fix a natural number
k>0
.
Take
\bar{h}=(h_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}\in\Pi_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}G_{n}
with
\|\bar{h}\|=r>0
.
We have that

\left\{n\in{\mathbb{N}}:\|g_{n}\|<\frac{r}{k}\right\}\in{\mathcal{U}}

so

\left\{n\in{\mathbb{N}}:N(g_{n},G_{n})=\min\{n\in{\mathbb{N}}:C_{n}(g_{n},G_{n})=G_{n}\}>k\right\}\in{\mathcal{U}}.

Finally,
\bar{g}\in Z_{{\mathcal{U}}}
. Moreover part is clear.
∎

Any known to us simple metric ultraproduct is in fact a metric ultraproduct of family with
(\star)
-property. For example,
{\mathcal{S}}=\left(S_{n},\frac{1}{n}\|\cdot\|_{H}\right)_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
has no
(\star)
-property (as (2) fails), but its metric ultraproduct
{\mathcal{S}}^{*}_{\text{met}}
equals to metric ultraproduct of
{\mathcal{A}}=\left(A_{n},\frac{1}{n}\|\cdot\|_{H}\right)_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
which has
(\star)
-property.

We will prove below that any simple metric ultraproduct of
(\star)
-property family is metrically uniformly simple.

Theorem 8.12
.

If a family of metric groups has a
(\star)
-property, then any its simple metric ultraproduct is uniformly metrically simple.

Proof.

Let
{\mathcal{G}}=(G_{i},\|\cdot\|_{i})_{i\in{\mathbb{N}}}
be a family of metric groups with
(\star)
-property such that
G^{*}_{\text{met}}=\Pi_{i\in{\mathbb{N}}}G_{i}/{\mathcal{E}}
is simple.
Suppose that
{\mathcal{G}}^{*}_{\text{met}}
is not uniformly metrically simple.
Then, there are a number
r>0
and a sequence
(\bar{g}_{n}=(g_{n,1},g_{n,2},g_{n,k},\ldots))_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
such that
\|\bar{g}_{n}\|>r
and
C_{n}(\bar{g}_{n},{\mathcal{G}}^{*}_{\text{met}})\neq{\mathcal{G}}^{*}_{\text{met}}
for any

.
So, for any natural number
n
there is a set
U_{n}\in{\mathcal{U}}
such that
\|g_{n,i}\|>r
and
C_{n}(g_{n,i},G_{i})\neq G_{i}
for any

. We can assume that

\{1,2,\ldots,m\}\cap U_{n}=\emptyset

for any

. For any number

let
k_{i}
be maximal number such that

and let

. Define,
\bar{h}=(h_{i})_{i\in{\mathbb{N}}}
. We have that
\bar{h}\in Z_{{\mathcal{U}}}
, because for any number
n
a set
\{i:C_{n}(h_{i},G_{i})\neq G_{i}\}\supseteq U_{n}
is in ultrafilter.
A group
{\mathcal{G}}^{*}_{\text{met}}
is simple, so by Fact

we have,
{\mathcal{E}}=Z_{{\mathcal{U}}}
.
So,
\bar{h}\in{\mathcal{E}}
, which gives a contradiction with
\|h_{i}\|>r>0
for any

.
∎

Theorem 8.13
.

If a metric ultraproduct
{\mathcal{G}}^{*}_{\text{met}}
is simple and has a
(\star)
-property, then
{\mathcal{G}}^{*}_{\text{met}}
is metrically uniformly simple (Definition

).

Proof.

Fix

Since

, a normal subgroup of

.
Since

Hence

Since
\bar{g}\in{\mathcal{E}}
then for any

So a group

∎

Proposition 8.17
.

Let
{\mathcal{G}}=(G_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
be a family with

-property and
suppose that

is a maximal normal subgroup of

Then there exists

, an ultrafilter on

such that

Suppose that

For

.

{\mathcal{U}}_{0}

.

9.
Metric ultrapowers of linear groups

We give more applications of our theorems, by using a model-theoretic argument and results of Liebeck-Shalev from
[
LS01
]
.

Let us recall an important bi-invariant pseudo-norm on linear groups from
[
Nik09
,
ST14
]
. By
F
we always denote a field. Let
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
denote the finite field of order
q
.

The
Jordan length

{\ell}_{J}
of
A\in\GL_{n}(F)
is defined as:

(9.1)

{\ell}_{J}(A)=\frac{1}{n}\cdot\min_{\lambda\in F^{*}}\rk(A-\lambda I_{n}),

where
\rk(M)
is the rank of a matrix
M
and
I_{n}
is the
n\times n
identity matrix. The Jordan length is a pseudo norm on
\GL_{n}(F)

[
ST14
, p, 79]
.

We use the following deep fact, which can be derived from
[
LS01
, Lemma 4.1]
, see also
[
ST17
, Lemma 6]
.

Lemma 9.1
.

[
LS01
, Theorem 1.1]

There is a constant
C\in{\mathbb{N}}_{>0}
such that for any
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
and any finite field
{\mathbb{F}}
the following is true for
H=\SL_{n}({\mathbb{F}})
and
N\in{\mathbb{N}}
:

(9.2)

\text{ for any }A\in H\setminus Z(H)\text{, if }{\ell}_{J}(A)\cdot N\geq C\text{, then }C_{N}(A,H)=H.

The crucial point in our argument is that, we can express the conclusion (
9.2
) of Lemma
9.1
as a first order sentence of a field in the language of rings
L=\{+,\cdot,0,1\}
, for fixed
n,N\in{\mathbb{N}}
. Let us explain this in more details. There exists a sentence
\Phi_{n,N}
build from variables, symbols of 0, 1, addition
+
, multiplication
\cdot
, logical connectives
\wedge,\vee
and quantifiers
\forall,\exists
such that for an arbitrary field
F
(not necessarily finite)

\Phi_{n,N}
is true in
F
(that is
F\models\Phi_{n,N}
) if and only if (
9.2
) holds for
H=\SL_{n}(F)
.

In order to build such
\Phi_{n,N}
, we treat a matrix of dimension
n\times n
as a tuple of length
n^{2}
. Observe that

•

addition and multiplication of matrices can be expressed by a first order formula;

•

the fact that rank of a matrix
A\in\SL_{n}(F)
is greater than
k
can be expressed by a first order quantifier free
L
-formula
\phi_{n,k}
, saying that some minor of
A
of dimension
k\times k
has a non-zero determinant;

•

in particular, the condition
{\ell}_{J}(A)\cdot N\geq C
can be expressed by

\psi_{n,N}(A)=\left(\forall\lambda\neq 0\right)\phi_{n,\frac{C\cdot n}{N}}(A-\lambda I_{n});

•

for fixed
n
and
N
, the statement (
9.2
) can be expressed as:

\Phi_{n,N}=\forall A,B\ \psi_{n,N}(A)\rightarrow(\exists T_{1},T_{2},\ldots,T_{N})\ B={A^{\pm 1}}^{T_{1}}{A^{\pm 1}}^{T_{2}}\ldots{A^{\pm 1}}^{T_{N}},

where capital letters represent tuples of length of
n^{2}
, which are elements of
\SL_{n}(F)
.

Lemma 9.2
.

For all
n,N\in{\mathbb{N}}_{n>2}
,
\Phi_{n,N}
is true in
{\mathbb{C}}
, field of complex number.

Proof.

For any prime number
p
, an algebraic closure
\overline{{\mathbb{F}}_{p}}
of
{\mathbb{F}}_{p}
is a union of increasing family of finite fields. Sentence
\Phi_{n,N}
is true in any finite field by Lemma
9.1

Claim 9.3
.

\Phi_{n,N}
is true in
\overline{{\mathbb{F}}_{p}}
for any prime number
p
.

If not, there are: a prime number
p
and matrices
A,B\in\SL_{n}(\overline{{\mathbb{F}}_{p}})
such that
{\ell}_{J}(A)>\frac{C}{N}
and

B\not\in C_{N}(A,\SL_{n}(\overline{{\mathbb{F}}_{p}})).

Take
F\subset\overline{{\mathbb{F}}_{p}}
a finite field which contains all the coefficients of matrices
A
and
B
. Observe that
\Phi_{n,N}
is not true in
F
, as
{\ell}_{J}(A)
computed in
F
cannot be smaller that
{\ell}_{J}(A)
computed in
{\mathbb{F}}_{p}
, contradiction with Lemma
9.1
.

Therefore
\Phi_{n,N}
is true in
\overline{{\mathbb{F}}_{p}}
, for all prime
p\in{\mathbb{P}}
. By standard model-theoretic argument
\Phi_{n,N}
is also true in any algebraically closed field of positive characteristic, so
\Phi_{n,k}
is true in any algebraically closed field of characteristic 0, so in
{\mathbb{C}}
.
∎

Theorem 9.4
.

Let
G_{n}=(\SL_{m_{n}}({\mathbb{C}}),{\ell}_{J})
, for some

.
Any metric ultraproduct of

is a simple group. In fact, it is metrically uniformly simple. (Definition

).

Notice that
\Phi_{n,N}
is true in
{\mathbb{C}}
for any

by Lemma

. That is,
{\mathcal{G}}=(G_{n})_{n>2}
is metrically uniformly simple (see Definition

), so by Theorem

and Remark

.

10.
Direct limits

Let
(I,\leq)
be a directed set and
{\mathcal{G}}=(G_{i},\|\cdot\|_{i},f_{i,j})_{i\leq j\in I}
be a
direct system of metric groups
. By this we mean that for any
i\leq j\in I
there is an isometric homomorphism
f_{i,j}\colon G_{i}\rightarrow G_{j}
that satisfy:

(1)

f_{i,i}
is the identity of
G_{i}
,

(2)

f_{i,k}=f_{j,k}\circ f_{i,j}
for all
i\leq j\leq k
.

A
direct limit

\varinjlim{\mathcal{G}}
of the directed system
{\mathcal{G}}=(G_{i},\|\cdot\|_{i},f_{i,j})_{i\leq j\in I}
is a group defined as follows.
Its underlying set is

\coprod_{i\in I}G_{i}/\sim,

that is, the disjoint union

\coprod_{i\in I}G_{i}
of
\{G_{i}\}_{i\in I}
modulo the following equivalence relation
\sim
defined for
g\in G_{i},\ h\in G_{j}
as:

g\sim h
if and only if there is
k>i,j
such that
f_{i,k}(g)=f_{j,k}(h)
.

A group operation in
\varinjlim{\mathcal{G}}
is defined as usual: for
g\in G_{i},\ h\in G_{j}
a product is given by formula:

[g]_{\sim}\cdot[h]_{\sim}=\left[f_{i,k}(g)\cdot f_{j,k}(h)\right]_{\sim},

where
k>i,j
.

We can also define on
\varinjlim{\mathcal{G}}
a natural pseudo-norm:

\left\|[g]_{\sim}\right\|=\|g\|_{i},\text{ if }g\in G_{i}.

Theorem 10.1
.

Let
{\mathcal{G}}=(G_{i},\|\cdot\|_{i},f_{i,j})_{i\leq j\in I}
be a direct system of metric groups. Suppose that for any reals
0<r<t
there is
N\in{\mathbb{N}}
such that for any
i,j\in I
and
g\in G_{i}
,
h\in G_{j}
with
\|g\|>r,\|h\|<t

there is
k\in I
,
k>i,j
such that:

f_{j,k}(h)\in C_{N}(f_{i,k}(g),G_{k}).

Then the direct limit
G=\varinjlim{\mathcal{G}}
is metrically uniformly simple, so its metric ultrapower
G^{*}_{\text{met, fin}}
is a metrically uniformly simple group (see Definition
8.1
(1)).

Proof.

First, let us show that
G=\varinjlim{\mathcal{G}}
is metrically uniformly simple.
Take numbers
0<r<t
and a natural number
N
good for
r,t
. Let

[g]_{\sim},[h]_{\sim}\in G
be such that
\left\|[g]_{\sim}\right\|>r
and
\left\|[h]_{\sim}\right\|<t
. It is enough to show that

h_{k}\in C_{N}(g_{k},G_{k})

for some
k\in I
and
g_{k},h_{k}\in G_{k}
such that
g_{k}\sim g
and
h_{k}\sim h
.
Suppose that
g\in G_{i}
and
h\in G_{j}
.
Take
k\in I
such that
k>i,j
and let

g_{k}=f_{i,k}(g)
,
h_{k}=f_{j,k}(h)
.
The assumption gives that

f_{j,k}(h)\in C_{N}(f_{i,k}(g),G_{k})
so,

h_{k}\in C_{N}(g_{k},G_{k})
.
So, a direct limit
G=\varinjlim{\mathcal{G}}
is uniformly metrically simple, finally by Remark
8.3
its metric ultrapower
G^{*}_{\text{met, fin}}
is metrically uniformly simple too.
∎

Example 10.2
.

Let us apply Theorem
10.1
to
\mathcal{SL}=(\SL_{n}(F),{\ell}_{J})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
, where
F
be a finite field or the field of complex numbers and
{\ell}_{J}
is the Jordan length (
9.1
). A as result we obtain a metrically uniformly simple group
\varinjlim\mathcal{SL}
.

Consider a directed set
I=({\mathbb{N}}_{>0},|)
of positive natural numbers, where
|
is the dividing relation. For
n,m\in{\mathbb{N}}_{>0}
, with
n|m
, let

f_{n,m}\colon\SL_{n}(F)\rightarrow\SL_{m}(F)

be defined as follows: for a matrix
A\in F^{n\times n}
,
f_{n,m}(A)
is a matrix of dimension
m\times m
which has
\frac{m}{n}
copies of
A
along the diagonal, that is:

f_{n,m}(A)=\left[\begin{array}[]{cccc}A&0&\ldots&0\\
0&A&\ldots&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&0&\ldots&A\end{array}\right].

For any
n,m
such that
n|m
, a function
f_{n,m}
is an isometrical homomorphism of groups.
For any reals
0<r<t
take
N>C\cdot r
where constant
C
is as in Lemma
9.1
. For
g\in G_{i}
,
h\in G_{j}
with

.

By Lemma
9.1
(when
F

9.4
(when

)
we have that
f_{i,k}(g)\in C_{N}(f_{j,k}(h),G_{k})
.
Theorem
10.1
applied to
\mathcal{SL}
gives that
\varinjlim\mathcal{SL}
