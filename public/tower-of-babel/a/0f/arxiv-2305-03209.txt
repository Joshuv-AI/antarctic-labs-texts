Well-Posedness and regularity properties of 2d β \beta -plane stochastic Navier-Stokes equations in a periodic channel
Yuri Cacchiò, Amirali Hannani, Gigliola Staffilani

Abstract
We consider the 2d β \beta -plane stochastic Navier-Stokes equations in a periodic channel. We prove the well-posedness and existence of the stationary measure, as well as certain regularity estimates concerning the support of the stationary measure. The mentioned estimates are crucial for the rigorous study of the cascade phenomena in this equation Cacchiò et al 2024 . To the best of our knowledge, this is the first mathematically rigorous treatment of these equations involving both the stochastic noise and the Coriolis force.

Despite the wide range of applications, 2d SNS fails to reflect some features of
geophysical flows
. In fact, if we deal with such flows, we must include the planetary rotation (via Coriolis force) into the stochastic Navier-Stokes equations

;
Holton 2004
;
McWilliams 2011
;
Pedlosky 1992

;
Constantinou et al 2016
;
Cope 2020
;
Galperin et al 2008
;
Galperin et al 2006
;
Huang et al 2001
.

Taking into account the Coriolis force, we consider the following system of equations, which will be the object of study in the present work

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}\partial_{t}u+\left(u\cdot\nabla\right)u+fu^{\perp}&=\nu\Delta u-\alpha u-\nabla p+\varphi;\\
\nabla\cdot u&=0,\end{array}\right.

(1.1)

where
u=(u^{1},u^{2})
and
p
are unknown velocity field and pressure,
(u\cdot\nabla)
stands for the differential operator
u^{1}\partial_{x^{1}}+u^{2}\partial_{x^{2}}
with
x=(x^{1},x^{2})
,
fu^{\perp}
is the Coriolis force where
f
is the Coriolis parameter (cf. (
1.3
)) and
u^{\perp}=(-u^{2},u^{1})

,
\varphi
is the stochastic process, and damping is given by a combination of drag
\alpha>0
and viscosity
\nu>0
.

We refer the interested reader to
Salmon 1998
;
Vallis 2017
for complete derivations of this system.

In this manuscript, we use a well-known approximation of the Coriolis force, the so-called
\beta
-plane approximation
Nazarenko 2014
;
Vallis 2017
. This regime captures the dynamic effects of rotation. This approximation is done mainly by a Taylor expansion of the Coriolis parameter around a fixed latitude
\varTheta_{0}
. Consequently, for small variations in latitude, we can write

f=2\Omega\sin\varTheta\approx 2\Omega\sin{\varTheta_0}+2\Omega(\varTheta-\varTheta_{0})\cos{\varTheta_0},

(1.2)

where
\Omega
is the angular velocity of the sphere. With further approximation, we can assume that the Coriolis parameter varies as

f=f_{0}+\beta x^{2}

(1.3)

for small variation of
x^{2}
, where
f_{0}=2\Omega\sin{\varTheta_0}
,
\beta=\partial f/\partial x^{2}=(2\Omega\cos{\varTheta_0})/\mathfrak{r}
and
\mathfrak{r}
is the radius of the planet. Typical values of
\varTheta_{0}
are:
0^{\circ}
for the so-called equatorial flows,
45^{\circ}
for the so-called mid-latitude flows, and around
80^{\circ}
for the so-called high latitude flows.

Introducing the Coriolis force in the
\beta
-plane approximation breaks the symmetry along the
x^{2}
direction making the system anisotropic. Hence, we cannot consider a standard double periodic domain (we also refer to
Gallagher and Saint-Raymond 2006a
;
Gallagher and Saint-Raymond 2006b
for more discussion in this direction).
This leads us to take the most treatable, physically relevant domain: a periodic channel
(
Salmon 1998
, p 277)
.
Then, we pose the system of equations (
1.1
) on a periodic domain in
x^{1}
,
\mathbb{T}_{x^{1}}=[0,L)
torus of size
L
, and a bounded interval in
x^{2}
,
I=[a,b]
, equipped with periodic boundary conditions in
x^{1}
and no-slip boundary conditions in
x^{2}
, i.e.

\displaystyle u(t,0,x^{2})

\displaystyle=u(t,L,x^{2}),

(1.4)

\displaystyle u(t,x^{1},\partial I)

\displaystyle=0.

(1.5)

Finally, we assume that the stochastic process is spatially regular and white in time as in
Kuksin and Shirikyan 2012
, i.e.

\varphi(t,x)=\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\zeta(t,x),\ \ \ \ \zeta(t,x)=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}b_{j}\beta_{j}(t)e_{j}(x),\ \ \ \ t\geq 0

(1.6)

where
\{e_{j}\}
is a divergence-free orthonormal basis in
H
(completion of divergence-free smooth functions with proper boundary conditions in
L^{2}(\mathbb{T}\times I)
, cf. Definition
2.1
),
b_{j}
are constants such that,

\displaystyle\varepsilon

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}b_{j}^{2}<\infty,

(1.7)

\displaystyle\eta

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j}\fint_{\mathbb{T}\crossproduct I}b_{j}^{2}|\nabla\crossproduct e_{j}|^{2}<\infty,

(1.8)

and
\{\beta_{j}\}
is a sequence of independent standard Brownian motions (cf. (
2.4
)).
The constants
\varepsilon
,
\eta
are called average energy and enstrophy input per unit time per unit area, respectively.

To the best of our knowledge, there is no rigorous mathematical study concerning these equations having both the stochastic noise and the Coriolis force.

The goal of this paper is to prove the well-posedness and existence of the stationary measure of (
1.1
).
By stationary, we mean that the law of
u(\cdot)
coincides with the law of
u(\cdot+\tau)
for any
\tau\geq 0
. In addition, some regularity estimates regarding the support of the stationary measure have been proved. More explicitly, we observe that
\nu\mathbb{E}(\|\Delta u\|^{2}_{L^{2}})
is bounded, where
\mathbb{E}
denotes the expectation w.r.t the stationary measure, meaning that the stationary measure is at least supported on
H^{2}
functions. Such regularity results pave the way for studying the
cascade
phenomena in a rigorous way as it is motivated by
Cacchiò et al 2024
.

To obtain mentioned results, we use techniques from

Temam 1970
to deal with the peculiarity of the domains, and we take advantage of the ideas presented in
Kuksin and Shirikyan 2012
to deal with the noise. Still, combining ideas presented in
Kuksin and Shirikyan 2012
,
Temam 1970
is not sufficient, and we should deal with the additional difficulties arising from the Coriolis force separately. Let us stress that the above-mentioned results are not straightforward. In fact, by adding the Coriolis force, we have an additional term to control along with the particular choice of the domain. In particular, methods in
(
Kuksin and Shirikyan 2012
, p.66)
and
(
Temam 1970
, p.282)
are not directly applicable.

To be more precise, similar to
Kuksin and Shirikyan 2012
, the main idea is to split the solution into two sub-problems: the well-posedness of the stochastic Stokes equation and the well-posedness of Navier-Stokes equations with random coefficients. Since the Coriolis force does not affect the stochastic Stokes equation, we can directly apply results from
(
Kuksin and Shirikyan 2012
, Proposition 2.4.2)
with minor adjustments regarding the domain. On the other hand, the Coriolis force appears in the Navier-Stokes equations with random coefficients. Then, we prove the well-posedness of the resulting equation using ideas inspired by
(
Temam 1970
, Theorem 3.1)
. Here the main tool is the Galerkin method. We emphasize that this method is now applied to an equation with random coefficients entering through the solution of the stochastic Stokes equation. Moreover, introducing the Coriolis force leads us to estimate a new term, which, fortunately, we will show that can also be controlled with similar techniques. As mentioned above, the planetary rotation also influences the choice of our domain which is an additional difference with respect to
Kuksin and Shirikyan 2012
, forcing us to use ideas from
Temam 1970
.
Finally, because of the
\beta
-plane approximation, we have different boundary conditions compared to
Kuksin and Shirikyan 2012
;
Temam 1970
.

We would like to conclude this part of the introduction by saying that while the readers of this note will learn some generalizations of results in
Kuksin and Shirikyan 2012
;
Temam 1970
when the Coriolis force is at play, they will also have a clear summary of well-posedness results for stochastic Navier-Stokes equations in 2D.

The organization of the paper is as follows.
In Section
2
, we define a solution of (
1.1
) in the sense of Definition
2.2
. In Section
3
, we show the well-posedness result (cf. Theorem
3.5
). We prove the existence of a stationary measure for these equations (cf. Theorem
4.2
) in the last section. Furthermore, we investigate certain regularity properties of the support of this measure in Theorem
4.3
.

3
Well-Posedness

This section is devoted to the proof of the well-posedness of the initial value problem (
2.5
) in terms of Definition
2.5
.
We follow the footsteps of
Kuksin and Shirikyan 2012
. Here the idea is to study (
2.5
) by splitting it into stochastic Stokes
equation (cf. Theorem
3.1
) and Navier-Stokes equations with random coefficients (cf. Theorem
3.3
). Since the Coriolis force appears only in the Navier-Stokes equations with random coefficients, we give a detailed proof of its well-posedness using the so-called Galerkin method. But because of the Coriolis force and choice of the boundary conditions, our equations become intrinsically anisotropic, and well-posedness is not straightforward since we have an additional term to control along with the particular choice of the domain in contrast to
Temam 1970
where the proof is given for the deterministic case.
Therefore, we revise existing results and redo estimates as in what follows, overcoming additional difficulties. Finally, we obtain the existence and uniqueness of the solution to problem (
2.5
) by summing the previous results appropriately (cf. Theorem
3.5
).

Then,
we write a general solution of (

(3.1)

z(t,0,x^{2})&=z(t,L,x^{2});\\
z(t,x^{1},\partial I)&=0;\\
z(0,x)&=0.\end{array}\right.

(3.2)

and the function

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}\partial_{t}v+\nu Av+B(v+z)+F(v+z)+\alpha(v+z)&=0;\\
\nabla\cdot v&=0;\\

v(t,x^{1},\partial I)&=0;\\
v(0,x)&=u_{0}.\end{array}\right.

(3.3)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j,l=1}^{2}\int_{\mathbb{T}\times I}u^{j}(\partial_{j}v^{l})v^{l}dx=\sum_{j,l=1}^{2}\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{T}\times I}u^{j}\partial_{j}|v|^{2}dx

By (

Using (

\displaystyle+2\alpha\mathbb{E}\int_{0}^{t}\norm{u(s)}_{L^{2}}^{2}ds+2\nu\mathbb{E}\int_{0}^{t}\norm{\nabla u(s)}_{L^{2}}^{2}ds=\mathbb{E}\norm{u_0}^{2}_{L^{2}}+2\varepsilon t,

(4.1)

for
t\geq 0
, where
\mathbb{E}
denotes the expectation w.r.t the law of the process
u
.

Proof.

We use the Itô formula in Hilbert space (cf.
(
Kuksin and Shirikyan 2012
, Theorem 7.7.5)
) to the functional
\Phi(u)=\norm{u}^{2}_{L^{2}}
.
Since

\partial_{u}\Phi(u;v)=2\langle u,v\rangle,\ \ \ \partial_{u}^{2}\Phi(u;v)=2\norm{v}^{2}_{L^{2}},

with an application of Cauchy-Schwartz and the dominated convergence theorem we see that the assumptions of the Itô formula are satisfied.

Then, we have

\displaystyle\norm{u(t\wedge\tau_n)}^{2}_{L^{2}}=\norm{u_0}^{2}_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle+2\int_{0}^{t\wedge\tau_{n}}\langle u,\nu\Delta u-\alpha u\rangle+\varepsilon\ ds

(4.2)

\displaystyle+2\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}b_{j}\underbrace{\int_{0}^{t\wedge\tau_{n}}\langle u,e_{j}\rangle d\beta_{j}(s)}_{=:M(t\wedge\tau_{n})},

where we set

\tau_{n}=\inf\{t\geq 0;\ \norm{u(t)}_{L^{2}}>n\}.

The nonlinear term and pressure vanish after integration by parts as follows

\displaystyle\langle(u\cdot\nabla)u,u\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{j,l=1}^{2}\int_{\mathbb{T}\times I}u^{j}(\partial_{j}u^{l})u^{l}dx=\sum_{j=1}^{2}\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{T}\times I}u^{j}\partial_{j}|u|^{2}dx

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{T}\times I}(\nabla\cdot u)|u|^{2}dx=0

(4.3)

\displaystyle\langle\nabla p,u\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{2}\int_{\mathbb{T}\crossproduct I}u^{j}\partial_{j}pdx=-\sum_{j=1}^{2}\int_{\mathbb{T}\crossproduct I}p\partial_{j}u^{j}dx=-\langle p,\nabla\cdot u\rangle=0.

(4.4)

On the other hand
\langle u,f\hat{k}\crossproduct u\rangle=f\langle(u^{1},u^{2}),(-u^{2},u^{1})\rangle=0
by orthogonality.

Taking the mean value in (
4.2
) we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\norm{u(t\wedge\tau_n)}^{2}_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle+2\alpha\mathbb{E}\int_{0}^{t\wedge\tau_{n}}\norm{u}^{2}_{L^{2}}\ ds+2\nu\mathbb{E}\int_{0}^{t\wedge\tau_{n}}\norm{\nabla u}^{2}_{L^{2}}\ ds

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\norm{u_0}^{2}_{L^{2}}+2\varepsilon\mathbb{E}(t\wedge\tau_{n})+2\mathbb{E}\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}b_{j}M(t\wedge\tau_{n})

(4.5)

We remark that, since
\beta_{j}
is a sequence of Brownian motion, the last term on the right hand side is a martingale stochastic process. Then we can use Doob’s optional sampling Theorem
Grimmett and Stirzaker 2001
to show

\mathbb{E}\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}b_{j}M(t\wedge\tau_{n})=\mathbb{E}\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}b_{j}M(0)=0.

It follows,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\norm{u(t\wedge\tau_n)}^{2}_{L^{2}}+2\alpha\mathbb{E}\int_{0}^{t\wedge\tau_{n}}\norm{u}^{2}_{L^{2}}\ ds

\displaystyle+2\nu\mathbb{E}\int_{0}^{t\wedge\tau_{n}}\norm{\nabla u}^{2}_{L^{2}}\ ds

(4.6)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\norm{u_0}^{2}_{L^{2}}+2\varepsilon\mathbb{E}(t\wedge\tau_{n}).

Since the trajectories of
u(t)
are continuous
H-
valued functions of time, we have
\tau_{n}\to\infty
as
n\to\infty
. Passing to the limit in (
4.6
) as
n\to\infty
and using the monotone convergence theorem, we arrive at (
4.1
).
∎

\mathbf{B}

By the Bogolyubov-Krylov argument (cf.

(4.7)

with
\Gamma\in B(X)

Theorem 4.2
.

Under the hypotheses of Theorem
3.5
, the stochastic Navier-Stokes system (
2.5
) with an arbitrary

\alpha>0

Proof.

Let us denote by
u(t,x)
the solution of (

\lambda_{t}
the law of

as a random variable in
