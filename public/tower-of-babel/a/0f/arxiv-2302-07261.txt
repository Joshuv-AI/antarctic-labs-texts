Where to Diffuse, How to Diffuse, and How to Get Back: Automated Learning for Multivariate Diffusions
Raghav Singhal, Mark Goldstein, Rajesh Ranganath, Courant Institute of Mathematical Sciences, New York University, Center for Data Science, New York University

Abstract
Diffusion-based generative models (dbgms) perturb data to a target noise distribution and reverse this process to generate samples. The choice of noising process, or inference diffusion process , affects both likelihoods and sample quality. For example, extending the inference process with auxiliary variables leads to improved sample quality. While there are many such multivariate diffusions to explore, each new one requires significant model-specific analysis, hindering rapid prototyping and evaluation. In this work, we study Multivariate Diffusion Models (mdms) . For any number of auxiliary variables, we provide a recipe for maximizing a lower-bound on the mdms likelihood without requiring any model-specific analysis. We then demonstrate how to parameterize the diffusion for a specified target noise distribution; these two points together enable optimizing the inference diffusion process. Optimizing the diffusion expands easy experimentation from just a few well-known processes to an automatic search over all linear diffusions. To demonstrate these ideas, we introduce two new specific diffusions as well as learn a diffusion process on the mnist , cifar 10, and imagenet 32 datasets. We show learned mdms match or surpass bits-per-dims (bpds) relative to fixed choices of diffusions for a given dataset and model architecture.

Diffusion models usually have time-varying drift and diffusion coefficients (e.g. use of the
\beta(t)
function).
To build diffusion models
that match the model prior, we
first extend Theorem 1 from
Ma et al. 2015

to construct non-linear Itô processes with time-varying drift and diffusion coefficients with a given stationary
distribution (
Appendix

C
).
Then, to keep transitions tractable (per
Section

3.3
), we specialize this result to linear Itô diffusions.

We directly state the result for linear time-varying diffusions with stationary distributions.
The parameterization requires a skew-symmetric matrix
-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}(s)=\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}(s)^{\top}
, a positive semi-definite matrix
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{D}}(s)
, and
a function
\nabla H(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}})
such that the desired stationary distribution
q_{\infty}
is proportional to
\exp[-H(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}})]
. Linear Itô diffusions have Gaussian stationary distributions
(
Särkkä & Solin 2019
)
meaning that
\nabla H
is linear and can be expressed as
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{S}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}
for some matrix
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{S}}
.
For a matrix
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{A}}
, let
\sqrt{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{A}}}
refer to the matrix square root defined by
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{a}}=\sqrt{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{A}}}\iff\boldsymbol{\mathbf{A}}=\boldsymbol{\mathbf{a}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{a}}^{\top}
. Then,
the Itô diffusion:

\displaystyle d\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}=\underbrace{-\Big[\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}(s)+\boldsymbol{\mathbf{D}}(s)\Big]\boldsymbol{\mathbf{S}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}}_{f(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}},s)}ds+\underbrace{\sqrt{2\boldsymbol{\mathbf{D}}(s)}}_{g(s)}d\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{B}}}_{s},

(13)

has Gaussian stationary distribution
\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{0}},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{S}}^{-1})
where
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}(s),\boldsymbol{\mathbf{D}}(s)
and
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{S}}
are parameters. For a discussion of convergence to the stationary distribution,
as well as skew-symmetric and positive semi-definite parameterizations, see
appendix

C
, where we also show that existing
diffusion processes such as
vpsde
and
cld
are included
in
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}/\boldsymbol{\mathbf{D}}
parameterization. We display the
elbo
in terms of
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}/\boldsymbol{\mathbf{D}}

in
appendix

G
and an algorithm in
appendix

H
.

For score matching and noise prediction losses
and a given
q_{\phi}
,
achieving a minimizing value with respect to
s_{\theta}
does not imply that
the generative model score will match the inference score. Modeling the data also requires the marginal distribution of
q_{\phi,T}
to approximate
\pi
. When
q_{\phi}
is constant, it is important to confirm the stationary distribution is appropriately set, and the tools used here for the
elbo
can be used to satisfy this requirement for score matching and noise prediction
(
appendix

A
).

3.5
Learning the inference process

The choice of diffusion matters, and the
elbos
in
eq.

7
have no requirement
for fixed
q_{\phi}
. We therefore
learn the inference process jointly with
s_{\theta}
.
Under linear transitions (ingredient
1
), no algorithmic details
change as the diffusion changes during training. Under stationary
parameterization (ingredient
2
), we can learn without the stationary distribution going awry.
In the experiments, learning matches or surpasses
bpds
of
fixed diffusions for a given dataset and score architecture.

In

\mathcal{L}^{\text{mdsm}}

or

\mathcal{L}^{\text{mism}}
,

q_{\phi,\infty}
may be set to equal

, but
it is

for the chosen

in the

q_{\phi}
can
choose

to reduce the cross-entropy:

\displaystyle-\mathbb{E}_{q_{\phi}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}_{T}|x)}[\log\pi_{\theta}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}_{T})].

(14)

Minimizing

14
will tighten the

for any

. Next,

is featured in the remaining terms that feature
s_{\theta}

will tighten and improve the

alongside

is featured in the expectations
and the

\displaystyle\log p_{\theta}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}_{T}^{z}=x)

\displaystyle\geq=\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{q_{\phi}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}_{0}^{v}=v|x)}}\Big[(\mathcal{L}^{\text{dsm}}\text{ or }\mathcal{L}^{\text{ism}})\underbrace{-\log q_{\phi}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}_{0}^{v}=v|x)}\Big]

When it is satisfied, no looseness in the

To learn,
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{D}}

\phi

fixed at inverse covariance of
\pi_{\theta}
.

Gaussian distributions permit gradient

7
Discussion

We present an algorithm
for training multivariate diffusions with linear
time-varying inference processes with a specified stationary distribution
and any number of auxiliary variables.
This includes automating transition kernel computation
and providing a parameterization of diffusions that have a specified stationary distribution, which
facilitate working with new diffusion processes, including learning the diffusion.
The experiments show that learning matches or surpasses the best fixed diffusion performance, bypassing the need to choose a diffusion.
mdms
achieve
bpds
similar to univariate diffusions, with as many as three times more score parameters. The proposed
mdm

elbo
reports a tighter bound for the existing
cld
relative to existing probability flow-based evaluations. This work enables future directions including interactions across data coordinates and using new stationary distributions.

Appendix A
Automated Score Matching with Learned Inference

Like for the
mdm

elbo
, the methods in this work
apply to training with the
score matching loss:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\textsc{sm}}(x,\theta,\phi)=T\mathbb{E}_{t\sim U[0,T]}\mathbb{E}_{q_{\phi}({\mathbf{y}}\mid x)}\left[\lambda(t)\left\lVert s_{\theta}({\mathbf{y}}_{t},t)-\nabla_{{\mathbf{y}}_{t}}\log q_{\phi}({\mathbf{y}}_{t}\mid x)\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\right],

where
\lambda:[0,T]\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
is a weighing function. The score-matching loss is often optimized in its simplified noise prediction form:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\textsc{np}}(x,\theta,\phi)=T\mathbb{E}_{t\sim U[0,T]}\mathbb{E}_{q_{\phi}({\mathbf{y}}\mid x)}\left[\left\lVert{\epsilon}_{\theta}({\mathbf{y}}_{t},t)-{\epsilon}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\right]

where
s_{\theta}=-{\mathbf{L}}_{t}^{-\top}{\epsilon}_{\theta}
and
{\mathbf{y}}_{t}=\mu_{t}+{\mathbf{L}}_{t}{\epsilon}
and
\epsilon
is the noise used in sampling
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}_{t}
.
We describe here
how the improvements to the
elbo
studied in this work
carry over to

\mathcal{L}_{\textsc{SM}}
and

\mathcal{L}_{\textsc{NP}}
. In the following
let
q_{0}
be the data distribution,
let
p_{(\theta,\phi),0}
be the model’s distribution of the data,
and recall that the model is defined by

(s_{\theta},f_{\phi},g_{\phi})
and prior
\pi
via a continuous-time stochastic process with drift coefficient
g_{\phi}^{2}s_{\theta}-f_{\phi}
and and diffusion coefficient
g_{\phi}
.

First, minimizing

\mathcal{L}_{\textsc{SM}}
or

\mathcal{L}_{\textsc{NP}}

so that
\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}_{t}}\log q_{\phi}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}_{t})=s_{\theta}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}_{t},t)

does not alone imply that

p_{(\theta,\phi),0}
will equal
q_{0}
;
it must also be that
q_{\phi,T}\approx\pi
. Foregoing this requirement
means
\pi
will produce samples that the generative model may not be able to push onto the path
the model was trained on (formally, the score of the generative model would not equal the time-reversal of the forward score even if

s_{\theta}
equals the forward score). This condition
can be satisfied if
q_{\phi}
can be chosen with stationary distribution
\pi
. Section 3.4 describes how to accomplish this.

Next, for any fixed
q_{\phi}
,
automatic transitions from section 3.3 streamline the computation of the score matching loss, allowing for simple score computation for a wide class of diffusions beyond VP.

Finally, for a fixed
q_{\phi}
with

q_{\phi,T}\approx\pi

and a score architecture
s_{\theta}
,
minimizing

\mathcal{L}_{\textsc{SM}}
or

\mathcal{L}_{\textsc{NP}}

w.r.t
\theta
may be suboptimal.
Optimization, like for the elbo, carries over to score matching and can
close this gap; learning w.r.t. both
\theta,\phi

increases the ability to successfully minimize the loss at each
t
(section 3.5).
In other words, since the generative model is defined by
(s_{\theta},f_{\phi},g_{\phi})
, learning
q_{\phi}
means the loss trains all three components of the generative model rather than just one. In summary, score matching is automatic and can
learn over the space of linear diffusions that tend to the model prior.

Appendix B
Does my model use auxiliary variables?

In
section

3
we gave the example choice of

q(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}_{0}^{v}|x)=\mathcal{N}(0,\boldsymbol{\mathbf{I}})
coordinate-wise.
It is also a common choice to set
\pi_{\theta}=\mathcal{N}(0,\boldsymbol{\mathbf{I}})
.
Because the optimum in diffusion models
is
p_{\theta}=q
for all
t
, we see
a peculiar phenomenon under this choice:
the model has main and auxiliary dimensions independent at both endpoints
0
and
T
. Does this mean that the model does not use auxiliary variables?
We show that even when

q_{\phi}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}_{0})
and
\pi_{\theta}
have main and auxiliary variables independent,
the model can use the auxiliary variables. A sufficient condition is
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}+\boldsymbol{\mathbf{D}}
is non-diagonal.

To make this precise, we recall that we model with
p_{\theta}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}_{T}^{z}=x)
. To show the model is using auxiliary variables, we just need to show that

\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}_{T}^{z}
(main coordinate at
T
) depends on
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}_{t}^{v}
(aux. coordinate at
t
)
for
T>t
. At optimum,

p_{\theta}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}_{T}^{z},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}_{t}^{v})=q_{\phi}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}_{0}^{z},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}_{T-t}^{v})
.
Therefore it is sufficient to show that for some time
s
,

q_{\phi}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}_{s}^{v}|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}_{0}^{z})\neq q_{\phi}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}_{s}^{v})
.
Because
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}_{0}^{z}
,
is determined by
x

we need to show
that
q_{\phi}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}_{s}^{v}|x)\neq q_{\phi}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}_{s}^{v})
.
To do that,
we first derive

q(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}_{s}|x)
and then
marginalize to get

q(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}_{s}^{v}|x)

from it. Since the former
is 2D Gaussian,
the latter is available in terms
of the former’s mean and covariance.
Suppose

\mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}_{0}^{v}]=0
,

\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}=[[0,-1],[1,0]]
and
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{D}}=[[1,0],[0,1]]

and we have
s=.1
We have:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}_{s}|x]

\displaystyle=\exp\Big[-s(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}+\boldsymbol{\mathbf{D}})\Big]\begin{pmatrix}x\\
0\end{pmatrix}=\exp\Big[\begin{bmatrix}-.1&.1\\
-.1&-.1\end{bmatrix}\Big]\begin{pmatrix}x\\
0\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}0.9003x\\
-0.090x\end{pmatrix}

(18)

Regardless of the covariance
any 1D of this 2D gaussian
will have mean that is a function of
x
,
meaning that
q(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}_{s}^{v}|x)

does not equal

q(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}_{s}^{v})
(which is also a
Gaussian but with mean depending
on
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}^{\prime}s
mean rather than
x
itself.
Therefore,
even under the setup with independent endpoints,
the optimal model makes use of the intermediate auxiliary variables in its final modeling distribution
p_{\theta}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}_{T}^{z}=x)
.

Are there choices of
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{D}}

that lead to learning models that don’t make use of the extra dimensions?
As mentioned, in the inference process,
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}
is responsible for mixing information among the coordinates, and is the only source of this when
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{D}}
is diagonal. Then, if
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}=\boldsymbol{0}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{D}}
is diagonal, none of the coordinates
for a given feature
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{j}
(including

\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}_{tj}^{z},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}_{tj}^{v_{1}},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}_{tj}^{v_{K-1}}
) interact for any
t
. Then, since
p_{\theta}=q
at optimum, independence of the coodinates at all
t
in
q
imply the same in
p_{\theta}
and the model will not make use of any auxiliary variables when modeling the marginal
\log p_{\theta}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}_{T}^{z}=x)
.

Appendix C
Stationary parameterization

The non-linear time-homogeneous Itô process family is:

\displaystyle d\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}=f(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}})dt+g(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}})\boldsymbol{\mathbf{B}}_{t}.

(19)

This family can be restricted to those with stationary distributions.

Ma et al. 2015
show a complete recipe to span
the subset of this family with a desired stationary distribution.
Let
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}
be skew-symmetric (
-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}=\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}^{\top}
) and
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{D}}
is positive semi-definite. Suppose the desired stationary distribution
is
q_{\infty}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}})
.
For a matrix
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{A}}
, let
\sqrt{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{A}}}
refer to the matrix square root defined by
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{a}}=\sqrt{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{A}}}\iff\boldsymbol{\mathbf{A}}=\boldsymbol{\mathbf{a}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{a}}^{\top}
. Then,

Ma et al. 2015
show that,
setting

\boldsymbol{\mathbf{H}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}})=-\log q_{\infty}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}})
,
g(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}})=\sqrt{2\boldsymbol{\mathbf{D}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}})}
,
and

\displaystyle f(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}})=-[\boldsymbol{\mathbf{D}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}})+\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}})]\nabla\boldsymbol{\mathbf{H}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}})+\boldsymbol{\Gamma}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}),\quad\quad\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{i}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}})=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\frac{\partial}{\partial\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}_{j}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{D}}_{ij}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}})+\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}_{ij}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}})),

(20)

yields a process
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}_{t}
with stationary distribution
q_{\infty}
. We extend it to time-varying (time in-homogeneous) processes.

Theorem 2
.

q_{\infty}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}})\propto\exp[-H(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}})]
is a stationary distribution
of

\displaystyle d\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}=\Bigg(-[\boldsymbol{\mathbf{D}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}},t)+\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}},t)]\nabla\boldsymbol{\mathbf{H}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}})+\boldsymbol{\Gamma}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}},t)\Bigg)dt+\sqrt{2\boldsymbol{\mathbf{D}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}},t)}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{B}}_{t},

(21)

for

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{i}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}},t)=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\frac{\partial}{\partial\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}_{j}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{D}}_{ij}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}},t)+\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}_{ij}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}},t)).

(22)

Proof.

The Fokker Planck equation is:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}q(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}},t)=-\sum_{i}\frac{\partial}{\partial\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}_{i}}\Big[f_{i}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}},t)q(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}},t)\Big]+\sum_{i,j}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}_{i}\partial\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}_{j}}\Big[\boldsymbol{\mathbf{D}}_{ij}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}},t)q(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}},t)\Big]

(23)

A stationary distribution is one where
the Fokker-Planck right hand side is equal to
0
.
To show that the stationary characterization also holds of time-inhomogenous processes with
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{D}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}},t)
and
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}},t)
,
we take two steps, closely following
Yin & Ao 2006
;
Shi et al. 2012
;
Ma et al. 2015
,
but noting that there is no requirement for
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{D}}
to be free of
t
. First, we show that the Fokker-Plack equation can be re-written as:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}q(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}},t)=\nabla\cdot\Bigg(\Big[\boldsymbol{\mathbf{D}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}},t)+\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}},t)\Big]\Big[q(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}},t)\nabla H(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}})+\nabla q(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}},t)\Big]\Bigg)

(24)

Second, because the whole expression is set to
0
when
the inside expression equals
0

\displaystyle q(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}},t)\nabla H(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}})+\nabla q(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}},t)=0,

(25)

we just need to show that this holds when

q(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}},t)=\exp[-H(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}})]/{\mathbf{Z}}
. The second step is concluded because

\displaystyle\Big[q(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}},t)\nabla H(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}})+\nabla q(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}},t)\Big]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{{\mathbf{Z}}}\Big[\exp[-H(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}})]\nabla H(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}})+\nabla\exp[-H(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}})]\Big]=0,

where
{\mathbf{Z}}
is the normalization constant of
\exp(-H(y))
.

It only remains to show that Fokker-Plack can be re-written in divergence form with time-dependent
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{D}}
.
In the following let
Q_{ijt}
denote
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}_{ij}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}},t)
and likewise for
D_{ijt}
.
Let
\partial_{i}
denote
\frac{\partial}{\partial\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}_{i}}
and let it denote
\frac{d}{d\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}_{i}}
for scalar functions. We will use

[Ax]_{i}=\sum_{j}A_{ij}x_{j}
.

\displaystyle\partial_{t}q_{t}

\displaystyle=\nabla\cdot\Big([\boldsymbol{\mathbf{D}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}},t)+\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}},t)][q\nabla H+\nabla q]\Big)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}\partial_{i}\Big(\Big[[\boldsymbol{\mathbf{D}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}},t)+\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}},t)][q\nabla H+\nabla q]\Big]_{i}\Big)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}\partial_{i}\sum_{j}[D_{ijt}+Q_{ijt}][q\nabla H+\nabla q]_{j}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}\partial_{i}\sum_{j}[D_{ijt}+Q_{ijt}][q\partial_{j}H+\partial_{j}q]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}\partial_{i}\sum_{j}[D_{ijt}+Q_{ijt}][q\partial_{j}H]+\sum_{i}\partial_{i}\sum_{j}[D_{ijt}+Q_{ijt}][\partial_{j}q]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}\partial_{i}\sum_{j}[D_{ijt}+Q_{ijt}][q\partial_{j}H]+\sum_{i}\partial_{i}\sum_{j}D_{ijt}[\partial_{j}q]+\sum_{i}\partial_{i}\sum_{j}Q_{ijt}[\partial_{j}q]

We re-write the 2nd and 3rd term.
Holding
i
fixed and noting
q
is scalar, we get the product rule
\sum_{j}D_{ijt}(\partial_{j}q)=\sum_{j}\partial_{j}[D_{ijt}q]-q\sum_{j}\partial_{j}D_{ijt}
for each
i
, and likewise for
q
:

\displaystyle\sum_{i}\partial_{i}\sum_{j}[D_{ijt}+Q_{ijt}][q\partial_{j}H]+\sum_{i}\partial_{i}\sum_{j}D_{ijt}[\partial_{j}q]+\sum_{i}\partial_{i}\sum_{j}Q_{ijt}[\partial_{j}q]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}\partial_{i}\sum_{j}[D_{ijt}+Q_{ijt}][q\partial_{j}H]+\sum_{i}\partial_{i}\sum_{j}\partial_{j}[D_{ijt}q]-q\sum_{j}\partial_{j}D_{ijt}

\displaystyle\quad\quad+\sum_{i}\partial_{i}\sum_{j}\partial_{j}[Q_{ijt}q]-q\sum_{j}\partial_{j}Q_{ijt}

Because
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}},t)
is skew-symmetric, we have that

\sum_{i}\partial_{i}\sum_{j}\partial_{j}[Q_{ijt}q]=0
, leaving

\displaystyle\partial_{t}q_{t}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}\partial_{i}\Bigg[\sum_{j}[D_{ijt}+Q_{ijt}][q\partial_{j}H]\Bigg]+\sum_{i}\partial_{i}\Bigg[\sum_{j}\partial_{j}[D_{ijt}q]-q\sum_{j}\partial_{j}D_{ijt}-q\sum_{j}\partial_{j}Q_{ijt}\Bigg]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}\partial_{i}\Bigg[\sum_{j}[D_{ijt}+Q_{ijt}][\partial_{j}H]q\Bigg]+\sum_{i}\partial_{i}\Bigg[\sum_{j}\partial_{j}[D_{ijt}q]-q\sum_{j}\partial_{j}(D_{ijt}+Q_{ijt})\Bigg]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}\partial_{i}\Bigg[\Big(\sum_{j}[D_{ijt}+Q_{ijt}][\partial_{j}H]-\sum_{j}\partial_{j}(D_{ijt}+Q_{ijt})\Big)q\Bigg]+\sum_{i}\sum_{j}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}_{i}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}_{j}}(D_{ijt}q)

Recalling that
f_{i}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}},t)=\Big(-[D+Q]\nabla H+\Gamma\Big)_{i}
and again
that
[Ax]_{i}=\sum_{j}A_{ij}x_{j}
, we have equality with the original Fokker-Planck

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}\partial_{i}\Bigg[\Big(\sum_{j}[D_{ijt}+Q_{ijt}][\partial_{j}H]-\sum_{j}\partial_{j}(D_{ijt}+Q_{ijt})\Big)q\Bigg]+\sum_{ij}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}_{i}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}_{j}}(D_{ijt}q)

\displaystyle=-\sum_{i}\frac{\partial}{\partial\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}_{i}}\Big[f_{i}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}},t)q(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}},t)\Big]+\sum_{ij}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}_{i}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}_{j}}\Big[\boldsymbol{\mathbf{D}}_{ij}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}},t)q(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}},t)\Big]

\displaystyle=\partial_{t}q(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}},t)

∎

We have shown
\exp[-H(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}})]/\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Z}}

is a stationary distribution of the time-varying non-linear Itô process:

\displaystyle d\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}=\Bigg(-[\boldsymbol{\mathbf{D}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}},t)+\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}},t)]\nabla H(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}})+\boldsymbol{\Gamma}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}},t)\Bigg)dt+\sqrt{2\boldsymbol{\mathbf{D}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}},t)}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{B}}_{t}.

(26)

However, for some choices of
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{D}}
,
\exp[-H(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}})]/\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Z}}
is not necessarily the unique stationary
distribution. One problematic case can occur as follows. Suppose that
row
i
of
(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}+\boldsymbol{\mathbf{D}})
is all-zero; in this case,
d\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}_{i}=0
which implies that
({\mathbf{y}}_{i})_{t}=({\mathbf{y}}_{i})_{0}
for all
t>0
. Then, the initial
distribution is also a stationary distribution. To rule out such pathological
diffusions, we make the assumption that
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}+\boldsymbol{\mathbf{D}}
is full rank.
Then, for uniqueness, recall that stationary distributions are the zeros of

\displaystyle\partial_{t}q(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}},t)=\nabla\cdot\Bigg(\Big[\boldsymbol{\mathbf{D}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}},t)+\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}},t)\Big]\Big[q(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}},t)\nabla H(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}})+\nabla q(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}},t)\Big]\Bigg)

where the expression is of the form
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{A}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}
for

\boldsymbol{\mathbf{A}}=\boldsymbol{\mathbf{D}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}},t)+\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}},t)
and

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}=\Big[q(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}},t)\nabla H(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}})+\nabla q(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}},t)\Big].

Under the assumption that
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}+\boldsymbol{\mathbf{D}}
is full rank, the expression can only be zero when
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}
is zero.
To show uniqueness under the full rank assumption, one must then show that

\displaystyle\nabla q(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}},t)=-q(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}},t)\nabla H(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}).

holds only if
q(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}},t)=\exp[-H(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}})]/\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Z}}
.
Even if
\exp[-H(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}})]/\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Z}}
is the unique stationary distribution, convergence to that distribution is a question. See
Zhang & Chen 2013
for more details.

Learning
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}_{\phi},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{D}}_{\phi}
in the
mdm

elbo
helps push
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}_{T}
to the model prior
\pi_{\theta}
and avoid issues like those discussed.

C.1
Linear Processes

Next, we specialize this general family to linear Itô
processes to maintain tractable transition distributions.
A linear process is one where the drift
f(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}},t)
and diffusion

g(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}},t)
are linear functions of
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}
.
We express the drift function of a non-linear time-varying Itô process with
stationary distribution proportional to
\exp[-H(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}})]

as

\displaystyle-(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}},t)+\boldsymbol{\mathbf{D}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}},t))\nabla H(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}})+\Gamma(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}},t).

Next, linear Itô processes
have Gaussian stationary distributions
(
Särkkä & Solin 2019
)

so
H(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}})
must be quadratic and
\nabla H(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}})
is linear, and neither are constant in
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}
. Because
\nabla H(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}})
is linear,
it can be expressed as
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{S}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}
for some matrix
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{S}}
where

\boldsymbol{\mathbf{S}}
is the inverse of the covariance matrix. Because
\nabla H
is multiplied by
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{D}}
, this means that
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{D}}
must be free of
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}
. Recalling that
\Gamma
is expressed as
a sum of derivatives w.r.t
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}
of
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}+\boldsymbol{\mathbf{D}}
, this means that
\Gamma
must satisfy
\Gamma=0
.
Next, because of the stationary requirement that
g(t)=\sqrt{2\boldsymbol{\mathbf{D}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}},t)}
, we can also
conclude by the restriction on
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{D}}
that the diffusion coefficient function must be independent of the state
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}
.
Our final form
for linear time-varying processes with stationary distributions

\mathcal{N}(0,\boldsymbol{\mathbf{S}}^{-1})
is:

\displaystyle d\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}=\underbrace{-\Big[\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}(t)+\boldsymbol{\mathbf{D}}(t)\Big]\boldsymbol{\mathbf{S}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}}_{f(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}},t)}dt+\underbrace{\sqrt{2\boldsymbol{\mathbf{D}}(t)}}_{g(t)}d\boldsymbol{\mathbf{B}}_{t}

(27)

C.2
Parameterizing
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}_{\phi}

Suppose
b_{q}(s)
is a positive scalar function defined on the time domain with known integral. Suppose

\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}}_{\phi}
is any matrix. Then
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}}_{\phi}-\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}}_{\phi}^{\top}
is skew-symmetric
with
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}}_{\phi,ij}=-\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}}_{\phi,ji}
. We can set
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}_{\phi}
to

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}_{\phi}(s)=b_{q}(s)\cdot\Big[\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}}_{\phi}-\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}}_{\phi}^{\top}\Big]

(28)

This is a general parameterization of time-independent skew-symmetric matrices, which have number of degrees of freedom equal to the number of entries in one of the triangles of the matrix, excluding the diagonal.

C.3
Parameterizing
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{D}}_{\phi}

Suppose
b_{d}(s)
is a positive scalar function defined on the time domain with known integral.
Suppose
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{D}}}_{\phi}
is any matrix. Then
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{D}}}_{\phi}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{D}}}_{\phi}^{\top}

is positive semi-definite and spans all time-independent positive semi-definite matrices. We can set
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{D}}_{\phi}
to

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathbf{D}}_{\phi}(s)=b_{d}(s)\cdot\Big[\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{D}}}_{\phi}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{D}}}_{\phi}^{\top}\Big]

(29)

To show
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{D}}}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{D}}}^{\top}
spans all positive semi-definite matrices: suppose
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{M}}
is positive semi-definite. Then it is square. Then it can be eigen-decomposed into
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{M}}=\boldsymbol{\mathbf{V}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Sigma}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{V}}^{\top}
The degrees of freedom in

\boldsymbol{\mathbf{V}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Sigma}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{V}}^{\top}
are just
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{R}}=\boldsymbol{\mathbf{V}}\sqrt{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Sigma}}}

since
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{V}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Sigma}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{V}}^{\top}=\boldsymbol{\mathbf{R}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{R}}^{\top}

and the square root is taken element-wise because
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Sigma}}
is diagonal and is real because
each
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{ij}\geq 0
, which is true because
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{M}}
is positive semi-definite. Take
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{D}}=\boldsymbol{\mathbf{R}}
.

In our experiments we parameterize
{\mathbf{D}}
as a diagonal-only matrix.

C.4
Integrals

The known integral requirement comes from the integrals required in the transition kernel, and can be relaxed two possible ways:

•

numerical integration of function with unknown integral. This is expected to have low error given that the function is scalar-in scalar-out.

•

Directly parameterize the integral and use auto-grad when needing the functions not-integrated.

We stick with the known integrals. In conclusion, the underlying parameters are positive scalar functions
b_{q}(s),b_{d}(s)
defined on the time domain and with known integral, and general matrices
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}}_{\phi},\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{D}}}_{\phi}
.

Derivation of the Covariance matrix solution

Appendix E
Generic change of measure and Jensen’s for approximate marginalization

Suppose
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}=[\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}]
and we have an expression for

p(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}=[z,v])=p(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}=z,\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}=v)
. By marginalization, we can get
p(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}=z)
, and we can introduce another distribution
q
to pick a sampling distribution of our choice:

\displaystyle\begin{split}p(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}=z)&=\int_{v}p(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}=z,\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}=v)dv\\
&=\int_{v}p(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}=z|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}=v)p(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}=v)dv\\
&=\int_{v}\frac{q(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}=v|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}=z)}{q(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}=v|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}=z)}p(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}=z|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}=v)p(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}=v)dv\\
&=\mathbb{E}_{q(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}=v|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}=z)}\Big[\frac{p(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}=z,\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}=v)}{q(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}=v|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}=z)}\Big]\end{split}

(43)

We often work with these expressions in log space, and need to pull the expectation outside to use Monte Carlo. Jensen’s bound allows this:

\displaystyle\log p(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}=z)

\displaystyle=\log\mathbb{E}_{q(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}=v|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}=z)}\Big[\frac{p(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}=z,\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}=v)}{q(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}=v|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}=z)}\Big]

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{E}_{q(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}=v|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}=z)}\Big[\log\frac{p(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}=z,\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}=v)}{q(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}=v|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}=z)}\Big]

The following shows that the bound is tight when

q(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}=v|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}=z)=p(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}=v|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}=z)
:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathbb{E}_{q(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}=v|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}=z)}\Big[\log\frac{p(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}=z,\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}=v)}{q(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}=v|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}=z)}\Big]&=_{\text{assume}}\mathbb{E}_{p(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}=v|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}=z)}\Big[\log\frac{p(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}=z,\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}=v)}{p(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}=v|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}=z)}\Big]\\
&=\mathbb{E}_{p(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}=v|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}=z)}\Big[\log\Big(\frac{p(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}=z,\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}=v)}{p(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}=v,\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}=z)}\cdot p(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}=z)\Big)\Big]\\
&=\mathbb{E}_{p(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}=v|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}=z)}\Big[\log p(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}=z)\Big]\\
&=\log p(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}=z)\end{split}

(44)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\int_{x_{-i}}\int_{x_{i}}q(x)\nabla_{x_{i}}f_{i}(x)dx_{i}dx_{-i}

Valid
elbo
with truncation

The integrand in the
elbo
and its gradients is not bounded at time
0
. Therefore, following
Sohl-Dickstein et al. 2015
and
Song et al. 2021
the integrand in
eq.

7
is integrated from
[\epsilon,T]
, rather than
[0,T]
. However, that integral is not a valid lower bound on
\log p_{\theta}(x)
.
Instead, it can be viewed as a proper lower bound on the prior for a latent variable
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}_{\epsilon}
. Therefore, to provide a bound for the data, one can introduce a likelihood and substitute the prior lower bound into a standard variational bound that integrates out the latent.

To provide a valid lower bound for multivariate diffusions, we extend theorem 6 in
Song et al. 2021
from univariate to multivariate diffusions.

Theorem 3
.

For transition kernel
q_{\phi}({\mathbf{y}}_{s}\mid{\mathbf{y}}_{0})
, we can compute upper bound the model likelihood at time
0
as follows, for any
\epsilon>0

\displaystyle\log p_{\theta}(x)\geq\mathbb{E}_{q_{\phi}({\mathbf{y}}^{v}_{0}\mid x)}\mathbb{E}_{q_{\phi}({\mathbf{y}}_{\epsilon}|{\mathbf{y}}_{0})}\left[\log\frac{p_{\theta}({\mathbf{y}}_{0}\mid{\mathbf{y}}_{\epsilon})}{q_{\phi}({\mathbf{y}}_{\epsilon}\mid{\mathbf{y}}_{0})}+\mathcal{L}_{\text{mdm}}({\mathbf{y}}_{\epsilon},\epsilon)-\log q_{\phi}({\mathbf{y}}_{0}^{v}\mid x)\right],

(57)

where
\mathcal{L}_{\text{mdm}}({\mathbf{y}}_{\epsilon},\epsilon)
is defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\text{mdm}}({\mathbf{y}}_{\epsilon},\epsilon)=\mathbb{E}_{q_{\phi}({\mathbf{y}}_{>\epsilon}\mid{\mathbf{y}}_{\epsilon})}\left[\log\pi_{\theta}({\mathbf{y}}_{T})-\int_{\epsilon}^{T}\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert s_{\phi}\right\rVert_{g_{\phi}}^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert s_{\theta}-s_{\phi}\right\rVert^{2}_{g_{\phi}}+\nabla\cdot f_{\phi}\right].

Proof.

For transition kernel
q_{\phi}({\mathbf{y}}_{s}\mid{\mathbf{y}}_{0})
, we can compute upper bound the model likelihood at time
0
following an application of the variational bound

\displaystyle\log p_{\theta}(x)

\displaystyle=\log\int_{v_{0}}p_{\theta}({\mathbf{y}}_{0}=[x,v_{0}])dv_{0}

\displaystyle=\log\int_{v_{0},{\mathbf{y}}_{\epsilon}}p_{\theta}({\mathbf{y}}_{0},{\mathbf{y}}_{\epsilon})dv_{0}d{\mathbf{y}}_{\epsilon}

\displaystyle=\log\int_{v_{0},{\mathbf{y}}_{\epsilon}}q_{\phi}({\mathbf{y}}_{\epsilon}\mid{\mathbf{y}}_{0})\frac{q(v_{0}\mid x)}{q(v_{0}\mid x)}\frac{p_{\theta}({\mathbf{y}}_{0},{\mathbf{y}}_{\epsilon})}{q_{\phi}({\mathbf{y}}_{\epsilon}\mid{\mathbf{y}}_{0})}dv_{0}d{\mathbf{y}}_{\epsilon}

\displaystyle=\log\int_{v_{0},{\mathbf{y}}_{\epsilon}}q_{\phi}({\mathbf{y}}_{\epsilon}\mid{\mathbf{y}}_{0})\frac{q(v_{0}\mid x)}{q(v_{0}\mid x)}\frac{p_{\theta}({\mathbf{y}}_{0}\mid{\mathbf{y}}_{\epsilon})p_{\theta}({\mathbf{y}}_{\epsilon})}{q_{\phi}({\mathbf{y}}_{\epsilon}\mid{\mathbf{y}}_{0})}dv_{0}d{\mathbf{y}}_{\epsilon}

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{E}_{q(v_{0}\mid x)q_{\phi}({\mathbf{y}}_{\epsilon}\mid{\mathbf{y}}_{0})}\left[\log\frac{p_{\theta}({\mathbf{y}}_{0}\mid{\mathbf{y}}_{\epsilon})}{q_{\phi}({\mathbf{y}}_{\epsilon}\mid{\mathbf{y}}_{0})}-\log q_{\phi}({\mathbf{y}}_{0}^{v}\mid x)+\log p_{\theta}({\mathbf{y}}_{\epsilon})\right]

A lower bound for
\log p_{\theta}({\mathbf{y}}_{\epsilon})
can be derived in a similar manner to
eq.

7
, such that

\displaystyle\log p_{\theta}({\mathbf{y}}_{\epsilon})\geq\mathcal{L}_{\text{mdm}}({\mathbf{y}}_{\epsilon},\epsilon)=\mathbb{E}_{q_{\phi}({\mathbf{y}}_{>\epsilon}\mid{\mathbf{y}}_{\epsilon})}\left[\log\pi_{\theta}({\mathbf{y}}_{T})-\int_{\epsilon}^{T}\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert s_{\phi}\right\rVert_{g_{\phi}}^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert s_{\theta}-s_{\phi}\right\rVert^{2}_{g_{\phi}}+\nabla\cdot f_{\phi}\right].

The choice of
p_{\theta}({\mathbf{y}}_{0}\mid{\mathbf{y}}_{\epsilon})
is arbitrary, however following
Sohl-Dickstein et al. 2015
;
Song et al. 2021
we let
p_{\theta}({\mathbf{y}}_{0}\mid{\mathbf{y}}_{\epsilon})
be Gaussian with mean
\mu_{p_{\theta},\epsilon}
and covariance
\Sigma_{p_{\theta},\epsilon}
. Suppose
q_{\phi}({\mathbf{y}}_{\epsilon}\mid{\mathbf{y}}_{0})={\mathcal{N}}({\mathbf{y}}_{\epsilon}\mid{\mathbf{A}}{\mathbf{y}}_{0},\Sigma)
, then we select the following mean
\mu_{p_{\theta},\epsilon}
and covariance
\Sigma_{p_{\theta},\epsilon}
for
p_{\theta}({\mathbf{y}}_{0}\mid{\mathbf{y}}_{\epsilon})

\displaystyle\mu_{p_{\theta},\epsilon}

\displaystyle={\mathbf{A}}^{-1}\Sigma s_{\theta}({\mathbf{y}}_{\epsilon},\epsilon)+{\mathbf{A}}^{-1}{\mathbf{y}}_{\epsilon}

\displaystyle\Sigma_{p_{\theta},\epsilon}

\displaystyle={\mathbf{A}}^{-1}\Sigma{\mathbf{A}}^{-\top}

where
\mu_{p_{\theta},\epsilon},\Sigma_{p_{\theta},\epsilon}
are derived using Tweedie’s formula
(
Efron 2011
)
by setting
\mu_{\epsilon}=\mathbb{E}[{\mathbf{y}}_{0}\mid{\mathbf{y}}_{\epsilon}]
and
\Sigma_{\epsilon}=\text{Var}\left({\mathbf{y}}_{0}\mid{\mathbf{y}}_{\epsilon}\right)
.
∎

We next derive this choice as an approximation of the optimal Gaussian likelihood.

I.1
Likelihood derivation

Suppose
{\mathbf{y}}_{0}\sim q_{0}({\mathbf{y}}_{0})
and
{\mathbf{y}}_{\epsilon}\sim{\mathcal{N}}({\mathbf{y}}_{\epsilon}\mid A{\mathbf{y}}_{0},\Sigma)
. Here,
A,\Sigma
are the mean coefficient and covariance derived from the transition kernel at time
\epsilon
. We use Tweedie’s formula to get
the mean and covariance of
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}_{0}
given
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}_{\epsilon}
under
q
.
This mean and covariance feature the true
score
\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}_{\epsilon}}\log q(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}_{\epsilon})
.
We replace the score with the score model

s_{\theta}
and then set
p_{\theta}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}_{0}|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}_{\epsilon})
to have
the resulting approximate mean and covariance.
We make this choice because the optimal
p_{\theta}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}_{0}|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}_{\epsilon})

equals the true

q(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}_{0}|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}_{\epsilon})
as discussed throughout the work.

Here
{\mathbf{y}}_{0}=[{\mathbf{x}}_{0},{\mathbf{v}}_{0}]
where
{\mathbf{x}}_{0}\sim q_{\text{data}}
.

Let
\eta
be the natural parameter for the multivariate Gaussian likelihood
{\mathcal{N}}({\mathbf{y}}_{\epsilon}\mid A{\mathbf{y}}_{0},\Sigma)
. Then, Tweedie’s formula
(
Efron 2011
)
states that:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\eta\mid{\mathbf{u}}_{\epsilon}]=\nabla_{{\mathbf{y}}_{\epsilon}}l({\mathbf{y}}_{\epsilon})-\nabla_{{\mathbf{y}}_{\epsilon}}l_{0}({\mathbf{y}}_{\epsilon})

•

l({\mathbf{y}}_{\epsilon})=\log q({\mathbf{y}}_{\epsilon})

•

s_{\theta}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}_{\epsilon},\epsilon)
is taken to be the true score
\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}_{\epsilon}}\log q(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}_{\epsilon})
so that

\nabla_{{\mathbf{y}}_{\epsilon}}l({\mathbf{y}}_{\epsilon})=s_{\theta}({\mathbf{y}}_{\epsilon},\epsilon)

•

l_{0}
is the log of the base distribution defined in the exponential family parameterization.

The base distribution is a multivariate Gaussian with mean
0
and covariance
\Sigma
, therefore
\nabla_{{\mathbf{y}}_{\epsilon}}l_{0}({\mathbf{y}}_{\epsilon})=-\Sigma^{-1}{\mathbf{y}}_{\epsilon}
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\eta\mid{\mathbf{y}}_{\epsilon}]=s_{\theta}({\mathbf{y}}_{\epsilon},\epsilon)+\Sigma^{-1}{\mathbf{y}}_{\epsilon}.

However, Tweedie’s formula is not directly applicable
since our
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}_{\epsilon}
is not directly normal with mean
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}_{0}
. Instead, to derive the conditional mean of
{\mathbf{y}}_{0}
given
{\mathbf{y}}_{\epsilon}
, we use the relation
\eta=\Sigma^{-1}{\mathbf{A}}{\mathbf{y}}_{0}
and the linearity of conditional expectation to get

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[{\mathbf{y}}_{0}\mid{\mathbf{y}}_{\epsilon}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[A^{-1}\Sigma\eta|{\mathbf{y}}_{\epsilon}]

\displaystyle=A^{-1}\Sigma\mathbb{E}[\eta\mid{\mathbf{y}}_{\epsilon}]

\displaystyle=A^{-1}\Sigma\left(s_{\theta}({\mathbf{y}}_{\epsilon},\epsilon)+\Sigma^{-1}{\mathbf{y}}_{\epsilon}\right)

\displaystyle=A^{-1}\Sigma s_{\theta}({\mathbf{y}}_{\epsilon},\epsilon)+A^{-1}{\mathbf{y}}_{\epsilon}.

For the variance, we use the following relation
{\mathbf{y}}_{\epsilon}=A{\mathbf{y}}_{0}+\sqrt{\Sigma}\epsilon
, which implies that

\displaystyle{\mathbf{y}}_{0}

\displaystyle=A^{-1}{\mathbf{y}}_{\epsilon}-A^{-1}\sqrt{\Sigma}\epsilon

\displaystyle\text{Var}\left({\mathbf{y}}_{0}\mid{\mathbf{y}}_{\epsilon}\right)

\displaystyle=A^{-1}\Sigma A^{-T}.

Therefore, for the model posterior distribution
p_{\theta}({\mathbf{y}}_{0}\mid{\mathbf{y}}_{\epsilon})
we choose a Normal with mean and covariance

\displaystyle\mu_{p_{\theta},\epsilon}

\displaystyle=A^{-1}\Sigma s_{\theta}({\mathbf{y}}_{\epsilon},\epsilon)+A^{-1}{\mathbf{y}}_{\epsilon}

\displaystyle\Sigma_{p_{\theta},\epsilon}

\displaystyle=A^{-1}\Sigma A^{-T}
