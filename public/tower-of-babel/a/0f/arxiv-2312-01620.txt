The Laplace–Beltrami Operator on the Surface of the Ellipsoid
Hans VOLKMER

Abstract
The Laplace–Beltrami operator on (the surface of) a triaxial ellipsoid admits a sequence of real eigenvalues diverging to plus infinity. By introducing ellipsoidal coordinates, this eigenvalue problem for a partial differential operator is reduced to a two-parameter regular Sturm–Liouville problem involving ordinary differential operators. This two-parameter eigenvalue problem has two families of eigencurves whose intersection points determine the eigenvalues of the Laplace–Beltrami operator. Eigenvalues are approximated numerically through eigenvalues of generalized matrix eigenvalue problems. Ellipsoids close to spheres are studied employing Lamé polynomials.

Introduction

In 1839, Lamé
[
16
]
showed that the Laplace equation

\frac{\partial^{2}u}{\partial x^{2}}+\frac{\partial^{2}u}{\partial y^{2}}+\frac{\partial^{2}u}{\partial z^{2}}=0

after transformation to ellipsoidal coordinates
\alpha
,
\beta
,
\gamma

[
1
, Section 1.6]
can be solved by the method of separation of variables.
The orthogonal coordinate surfaces of ellipsoidal coordinates are ellipsoids, hyperboloids of one sheet and
hyperboloids of two sheets; all confocal.
Lamé obtained solutions of product form

u(x,y,z)=w_{1}(\alpha)w_{2}(\beta)w_{3}(\gamma),

where
w_{1}
,
w_{2}
,
w_{3}
all satisfy the Lamé differential equation

\frac{{\rm d}^{2}w}{{\rm d}t^{2}}+\bigl(h-n(n+1)k^{2}\sn^{2}(t,k)\bigr)w=0.

(1.1)

This equation contains the Jacobian elliptic function
\sn(t,k)
with modulus
k\in(0,1)

and two separation constants
h
and
n(n+1)
.
If
n=0,1,2,\dots
, then equation (
1.1
) admits Lamé polynomial solutions
[
1
, Chapter IX]
.
Lamé polynomials are nontrivial solutions of (
1.1
) that are polynomials in
\sn(t,k),\cn(t,k)
and
\dn(t,k)
.
For given
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, there are
2n+1
special values of
h
such that (
1.1
) admits a Lamé polynomial solution.
If we choose one of these special solutions for
w_{1}
,
w_{2}
,
w_{3}
, then
u
becomes a harmonic polynomial in the variables
x
,
y
,
z
also known as an ellipsoidal harmonic.
For the theory of ellipsoidal harmonics, we refer to
[
6
,
11
,
12
]
.

We also mention Mathieu’s work
[
18
]
on the vibrating elliptic membrane; see also
[
19
, Section 4.31]
and
[
22
, p. 4]
.
The eigenvalues
\lambda
are determined from the equation
-\Delta u=\lambda u
for the two-dimensional Laplace operator
\Delta
under Dirichlet boundary conditions on an ellipse.
This problem can be solved by the method of separation of variables after transformation to planar elliptic coordinates.
We obtain two Mathieu equations coupled by the eigenvalue parameter
\lambda
and an additional separation parameter.

The goal of this paper is to demonstrate that following the method used in these examples we can find all eigenvalues and eigenfunctions of
the Laplace–Beltrami operator
\Delta_{\mathcal{E}}
on the ellipsoid
\mathcal{E}
defined by

\frac{x^{2}}{a^{2}}+\frac{y^{2}}{b^{2}}+\frac{z^{2}}{c^{2}}=1,\qquad a>b>c>0.

(1.2)

Here we consider the ellipsoid
\mathcal{E}
as a two-dimensional analytic Riemannian manifold
[
14
]
.
We show that the eigenvalue equation

-\Delta_{\mathcal{E}}u=\lambda u

(1.3)

after transformation to ellipsoidal coordinates (now denoted by
s
,
t
) can be solved by the method of separation of variables.
We find eigenfunctions of product form

u=v(s)w(t).

(1.4)

The function
w
is now a solution of the differential equation

\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}\biggl(\frac{1}{q(t)}\frac{{\rm d}w}{{\rm d}t}\biggr)+q(t)\bigl(h-\lambda k^{2}\sn^{2}(t,k)\bigr)w=0,

(1.5)

where
q(t):=\bigl(a^{2}\cn^{2}(t,k)+b^{2}\sn^{2}(t,k)\bigr)^{1/2}
,
and
v
satisfies a similar equation.
We notice that equation (
1.5
) reduces to Lamé’s equation (
1.1
)
when
a=b=1
and
\lambda=n(n+1)
.
We obtain a two-parameter Sturm–Liouville eigenvalue problem
involving two ordinary differential equations
that is fully coupled. This means that the eigenvalue parameter
\lambda
and the separation parameter
h

appear in both differential equations. To such a system we
apply the general theory of two-parameter eigenvalue problems as can be found in the books

[
2
]
and
[
22
]
. Since we have only two parameters, the eigenvalues
\lambda
of
-\Delta_{\mathcal{E}}
are then determined by intersection points of corresponding eigencurves.

The eigenfunctions of the form (
1.4
) when properly normalized will give us an orthonormal basis
for the Hilbert space
L^{2}(\mathcal{E})
.
To the best knowledge of the author, this treatment of equation (
1.3
)
is new. Also the treatment of equation (
1.5
) appears to be new.

One should point out an important difference between the Laplace–Beltrami operator
\Delta_{\mathcal{S}}
on the unit sphere
\mathcal{S}
in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
and
the Laplace–Beltrami operator
\Delta_{\mathcal{E}}
on the ellipsoid
\mathcal{E}
.
If
r
,
\theta
,
\phi
denote spherical coordinates in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
and
w(\theta,\phi)
is a spherical harmonic of degree
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
(that is,
-\Delta_{\mathcal{S}}w=n(n+1)w
), then
r^{n}w(\theta,\phi)
is a solution of
\Delta u=0
.
This shows a close connection between the Laplacian in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
and
\Delta_{\mathcal{S}}
.
There is no similar relationship between the Laplacian in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
and
\Delta_{\mathcal{E}}
when we use ellipsoidal coordinates. In particular,
there is no direct relationship between ellipsoidal harmonics and eigenfunctions of
\Delta_{\mathcal{E}}
.

In a previous paper
[
23
]
, the author studied the eigenvalues of the Laplace–Beltrami operator on a spheroid, i.e., an ellipsoid of revolution.
The eigenvalues of the Laplace–Beltrami operator on a surface of revolution in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
can always be found by the method of separation of variables.
We obtain a system of two ordinary differential equation coupled by two parameters, however, in the first equation only
one parameter appears so that this (actually trivial) eigenvalue problem can be solved first.
We are then left with a classical Sturm–Liouville problem for the second equation.
Such a “weakly” coupled system is much easier to treat than the fully coupled one.
We point out that it is not possible to simply set
c=b
in this paper and obtain results from
[
23
]
.
In order to obtain results from
[
23
]
, we have to consider the limiting case
c\to b
with
a
,
b
being fixed.
The precise justification of the limiting process is a delicate one and is not considered in this paper.

We refer to paper
[
9
]
that studies the eigenvalue problem (
1.3
) when the ellipsoid
\mathcal{E}
is close to a sphere.
We also mention the related papers
[
5
]
and
[
17
]
.
For some modern applications of eigenfunction expansions for Laplace–Beltrami operators, we refer to the introduction of
[
9
]
.
We add possible applications of fractional powers of Laplace–Beltrami operators to contemporary studies involving “non-local”
partial differential equations

[
10
]
, and also the use of Laplace–Beltrami eigenfunctions expansions on a boundary
\partial\Omega
in the theory of boundary triples
for linear partial differential operators inside a domain
\Omega

[
15
, Theorem 3.2]
.
Also note the explicit use of Laplace–Beltrami eigenfunctions expansions in
[
7
, Section 1 and Theorems 2.2–2.4]
.

This paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
, we consider the algebraic and transcendental form of ellipsoidal coordinates for the ellipsoid
\mathcal{E}
.
The transcendental form has the advantage that it leads us to regular Sturm–Liouville problems
whereas the algebraic form gives us singular Sturm–Liouville problems.
In Section
3
, we transform equation (
1.3
)
to ellipsoidal coordinates
and show that it “separates”, that is, it allows for solutions of the form (
1.4
).
In Section
4
, we obtain and investigate the two-parameter Sturm–Liouville problem that
completely determines the eigenvalues and eigenfunctions of
-\Delta_{\mathcal{E}}
of even parity.
We show in Theorem
4.2
that the system of these eigenfunctions is complete in the Hilbert space of square-integrable functions on
\mathcal{E}
with even parity.
In Section
5
, we consider the corresponding two-parameter problems for the seven other parities.
The system of all eigenfunctions is then complete in the Hilbert space of all square-integrable functions on
\mathcal{E}
.

In Section
6
, we collect some known results for the Laplace–Beltrami operator on the sphere
in terms of sphero-conal coordinates that will be useful in subsequent sections.
In Section
7
, we mention that the two-parameter eigenvalue problem
can be simplified by the Prüfer transformation.
In Section
8
, we present an efficient method to compute the eigenvalue of
-\Delta_{\mathcal{E}}
numerically.
The eigencurves are approximated by the eigenvalues of finite matrix problems.
Finally, in Section
9
, we consider ellipsoids which are close to the unit sphere.
This allows us to make a connection to the results in
[
9
]
.

2
Ellipsoidal coordinates

We consider the ellipsoid
\mathcal{E}
given by (
1.2
).
On the ellipsoid
\mathcal{E}
we introduce (algebraic) ellipsoidal coordinates
\mu
,
\nu

[
8
, Section 15.1.1]
by

\displaystyle x=ak\mu^{1/2}\nu^{1/2},

(2.1)

\displaystyle y=bk(k^{\prime})^{-1}(\mu-1)^{1/2}(1-\nu)^{1/2},

(2.2)

\displaystyle z=c(k^{\prime})^{-1}\bigl(1-k^{2}\mu\bigr)^{1/2}\bigl(1-k^{2}\nu\bigr)^{1/2},

(2.3)

where
k,k^{\prime}\in(0,1)
are determined by

k^{2}=\frac{a^{2}-b^{2}}{a^{2}-c^{2}},\qquad k^{\prime 2}=1-k^{2}=\frac{b^{2}-c^{2}}{a^{2}-c^{2}}.

(2.4)

There is a one-to-one correspondence between the rectangle
0<\nu<1<\mu<k^{-2}
and

\mathcal{E}_{+}:=\{(x,y,z)\in\mathcal{E}\colon x,y,z>0\}.

There is also a one-to-one correspondence between the closed rectangle
{0\leq\nu\leq 1\leq\mu\leq k^{-2}}

and the closure
\bar{\mathcal{E}}_{+}
of
\mathcal{E}_{+}
.
Indeed, if we start at
(\mu,\nu)=(1,0)
and move along the boundary of the rectangle, then

(x,y,z)
moves along the boundary of
\mathcal{E}_{+}
starting at
(0,0,c)
. The points
(\mu,\nu)=(1,1)
,
(\mu,\nu)=\bigl(k^{-2},1\bigr)
,
(\mu,\nu)=\bigl(k^{-2},0\bigr)
correspond to

(x,y,z)=(ak,0,ck^{\prime})
,
(x,y,z)=(a,0,0)
,
(x,y,z)=(0,b,0)
, respectively.
The functions
\mu
and
\nu
can now be extended to continuous functions on
\mathcal{E}
with parity
(0,0,0)
,
that is, functions that satisfy

f(x,y,z)=f(-x,y,z)=f(x,-y,z)=f(x,y,-z)\qquad\text{for all}\quad(x,y,z)\in\mathcal{E}.

We note that these functions
\mu
and
\nu
also satisfy

\mu\nu=a^{-2}k^{-2}x^{2},\qquad\mu+\nu=1+a^{-2}k^{-2}x^{2}+b^{-2}k^{-2}(k^{\prime})^{2}y^{2}.

(2.5)

It follows that
\mu\nu
and
\mu+\nu
are analytic functions on
\mathcal{E}
. Therefore, every symmetric polynomial
in
\mu
,
\nu
is also an analytic function on
\mathcal{E}
. However,
\mu
,
\nu
are not analytic on
\mathcal{E}
.
In fact, the functions
\mu
,
\nu
are analytic at every point of
\mathcal{E}
except the four points
with
x=\pm ak
,
y=0
. This follows from the observation that we can solve (
2.5
) for
\mu
,
\nu

locally at
(x_{0},y_{0})
by analytic functions of
x
,
y
except when
\mu(x_{0},y_{0},z_{0})=\nu(x_{0},y_{0},z_{0})
.
But
\mu=\nu
only happens when
\nu=\mu=1
and this happens only at the four points
(x_{0},y_{0},z_{0})=(\pm ak,0,\pm ck^{\prime})
.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
f
be an analytic function defined on an open region
D
in the complex plane containing the interval
\bigl[0,k^{-2}\bigr]
.
Then
f(\mu)f(\nu)
is an analytic function on
\mathcal{E}
.

Proof.

We already saw that
f(\mu)f(\nu)
is analytic on
\mathcal{E}
with the possible exception of the four points

(x_{0},y_{0},z_{0})=(\pm ak,0,\pm ck^{\prime})
.
In order to show that
f(\mu)f(\nu)
is also analytic at these four points, it will be
sufficient to show analyticity at
(x_{0},y_{0},z_{0})=(ak,0,ck^{\prime})
.
The function
f
admits a power series expansion

f(\xi)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}c_{n}(\xi-1)^{n}

for
\xi
close to
1
.
Then

f(\xi)f(\eta)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}f_{n}(\xi,\eta),\qquad f_{n}(\xi,\eta):=\sum_{m=0}^{n}c_{m}c_{n-m}(\xi-1)^{m}(\eta-1)^{n-m}

(2.6)

for
\xi
and
\eta
close to
1
.
The functions
f_{n}(\xi,\eta)
are symmetric polynomials in
\xi,\eta
. Therefore, the functions

f_{n}(\mu,\nu)
are analytic in
x
,
y

for
(x,y)
close to
(x_{0},y_{0})
, where we allow complex values of
x
,
y
.
Since the expansion in (
2.6
) converges locally uniformly in a neighborhood of
(x_{0},y_{0})
, we obtain
that
f(\mu)f(\nu)
is analytic at
(x_{0},y_{0},z_{0})
.
∎

3
The Laplace–Beltrami operator

In terms of the coordinates
\mu
and
\nu
, the Laplace–Beltrami operator
\Delta_{\mathcal{E}}
on the ellipsoid
\mathcal{E}
is given by

\displaystyle\Delta_{\mathcal{E}}u

\displaystyle{}=(g_{1}g_{2})^{-1/2}\biggl(\frac{\partial}{\partial\mu}\biggl(g_{2}^{1/2}g_{1}^{-1/2}\frac{\partial u}{\partial\mu}\biggr)+\frac{\partial}{\partial\nu}\biggl(g_{1}^{1/2}g_{2}^{-1/2}\frac{\partial u}{\partial\nu}\biggr)\biggr)

\displaystyle{}=\frac{1}{\mu-\nu}\biggl(\frac{1}{F(\mu)^{1/2}}\frac{\partial}{\partial\mu}\biggl(\frac{1}{F(\mu)^{1/2}}\frac{\partial u}{\partial\mu}\biggr)+\frac{1}{(-F(\nu))^{1/2}}\frac{\partial}{\partial\nu}\biggl(\frac{1}{(-F(\nu))^{1/2}}\frac{\partial u}{\partial\nu}\biggr)\biggr).

In the eigenvalue equation
-\Delta_{\mathcal{E}}u=\lambda u
, we can separate variables as follows.
If
v(\mu)
is a solution of the differential equation

\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}\mu}\biggl(\frac{1}{F(\mu)^{1/2}}\frac{{\rm d}v}{{\rm d}\mu}\biggr)+F(\mu)^{1/2}\bigl(\lambda\mu-hk^{-2}\bigr)v=0,\qquad 1<\mu<k^{-2},

(3.1)

and
w(\nu)
is a solution of the differential equation

\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}\nu}\biggl(\frac{1}{(-F(\nu))^{1/2}}\frac{{\rm d}w}{{\rm d}\nu}\biggr)-(-F(\nu))^{1/2}\bigl(\lambda\nu-hk^{-2}\bigr)w=0,\qquad 0<\nu<1,

(3.2)

then
u(\mu,\nu)=v(\mu)w(\nu)
is a solution of
-\Delta_{\mathcal{E}}u=\lambda u
on
\mathcal{E}_{+}
. In these equations
h\in\mathbb{R}
denotes the separation parameter.

Equation (
3.1
) can be written in the form

\frac{{\rm d}^{2}v}{{\rm d}\mu^{2}}+\frac{1}{2}\biggl(\frac{1}{\mu}+\frac{1}{\mu-1}+\frac{1}{\mu-k^{-2}}-\frac{1}{\mu-d}\biggr)\frac{{\rm d}v}{{\rm d}\mu}+\bigl(\lambda\mu-hk^{-2}\bigr)F(\mu)v=0,

(3.3)

where

d:=\frac{a^{2}}{a^{2}-b^{2}}>k^{-2}.

Equation (
3.2
) can be written in exactly the same form with
\nu
replacing
\mu
and
w
replacing
v
.
Therefore, the differential equations (
3.1
) and (
3.2
) are actually the same equation but they are considered on different intervals.
It is interesting to note that equation (
3.3
) has five singularities at
0
,
1
,
k^{-2}
,
d
and
\infty
.
The first four are regular singularities
[
20
, Sections 5 and 4]
with indices
\{0,\frac{1}{2}\}
,
\{0,\frac{1}{2}\}
,
\{0,\frac{1}{2}\}
,
\{0,\frac{3}{2}\}

respectively but
\infty
is an irregular singularity unless
\lambda=0
. Equation (
3.3
) can be considered as an extension of
the Lamé equation (
1.1
) in its algebraic form
[
21
, Section 29.2.2]
. The Lamé equation has only four singularities at
0
,
1
,
k^{-2}
,
\infty
and all of them are regular. The additional singularity
d
appears in connection with the Laplace–Beltrami operator on the ellipsoid and makes the differential equation more difficult to treat.

Since the singularities
0
,
1
,
k^{-2}
are regular and each has index
0
, equation (
3.3
)
admits nontrivial Fuchs–Frobenius power series solutions in powers of
\mu
,
\mu-1
and
\mu-k^{-2}
.
Usually, these solutions are not connected by analytic continuation.
We are interested in the exceptional case that all three solutions are connected.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let the parameters
\lambda
,
h
be such that differential equation (
3.3
)
admits a nontrivial analytic solution
f(\mu)
on an open region
D
containing the interval
\bigl[0,k^{-2}\bigr]
.
Then the function
u
on
\mathcal{E}
defined by
f(\mu)f(\nu)
is an analytic function on
\mathcal{E}
with parity
(0,0,0)
and it is an eigenfunction of
-\Delta_{\mathcal{E}}

corresponding to the eigenvalue
\lambda
.

Proof.

It follows from Lemma
2.1
that
u
is analytic on
\mathcal{E}
.
By our derivation, we know that
u
solves
-\Delta_{\mathcal{E}}u=\lambda u
on
\mathcal{E}_{+}
.
Both
u
and
\Delta_{\mathcal{E}}u
have the same parity
(0,0,0)
so
-\Delta_{\mathcal{E}}u=\lambda u
holds on
\mathcal{E}
.
∎

Note that in the situation of Lemma
3.1
the region
D
can be taken as the entire complex plane with the branch cut
[d,\infty)
removed.

4
A two-parameter Sturm–Liouville problem

Our goal is to prove existence of pairs
\lambda
,
h
as indicated in Lemma
3.1
, and to show
that the corresponding
\lambda
’s comprise all eigenvalues of
-\Delta_{\mathcal{E}}
having eigenfunction with parity
(0,0,0)
.
To that purpose, we prefer to use the transcendental ellipsoidal coordinates
s
,
t
because then we arrive at
a regular two-parameter Sturm–Liouville system.
If we use the coordinates
\mu
and
\nu
, then our Sturm–Liouville problems have singular endpoints which
makes theoretical and numerical work more difficult.

We set
\mu=k^{-2}\dn^{2}(s,k^{\prime})
and
\nu=\sn^{2}(t,k)
in (
3.3
), and, after some computations, we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}s}\biggl(\frac{1}{p(s)}\frac{{\rm d}v}{{\rm d}s}\biggr)+p(s)\bigl(-h+\lambda\dn^{2}(s,k^{\prime})\bigr)v=0,

(4.1)

\displaystyle\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}\biggl(\frac{1}{q(t)}\frac{{\rm d}w}{{\rm d}t}\biggr)+q(t)\bigl(h-\lambda k^{2}\sn^{2}(t,k)\bigr)w=0,

(4.2)

where

\displaystyle p(s):=\bigl(c^{2}\cn^{2}(s,k^{\prime})+b^{2}\sn^{2}(s,k^{\prime})\bigr)^{1/2},

(4.3)

\displaystyle q(t):=\bigl(a^{2}\cn^{2}(t,k)+b^{2}\sn^{2}(t,k)\bigr)^{1/2}.

(4.4)

When we substitute
t=K+{\rm i}K^{\prime}-{\rm i}s
,
s\in\mathbb{R}
, and use the identities

k\sn(t,k)=\dn(s,k^{\prime}),\qquad k\cn(t,k)=-{\rm i}k^{\prime}\cn(s,k^{\prime}),\qquad\dn(t,k)=k^{\prime}\sn(s,k^{\prime}),

we find that
p(s)=q(t)
and equation (
4.1
) transforms to (
4.2
).
So we may say that (
4.1
) and (
4.2

The Laplace–Beltrami operator on the sphere

It is of interest to compare

It is well known that the operator

Usually, we work with a basis of spherical harmonics that are found by the method of separation variables in spherical coordinates.
We obtain spherical harmonics that are products of associated Legendre polynomials and trigonometric functions.
However, in this work, it is more convenient to
use sphero-conal coordinates. Then we obtain spherical harmonics expressed as products of Lamé polynomials known as sphero-conal harmonics.
This is explained below in more detail.

We introduce sphero-conal coordinates

[

.
If
\theta^{\prime}(t)
is not positive throughout the interval
[0,K]
then arguing as in the proof of Lemma

one can show that there is

such that
\theta^{\prime}(t_{0})=0
,
\theta^{\prime}(t)>0
for

and

\theta^{\prime}(t)<0
for

.

Theorem 7.2
.

For all
m,n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and all parities
\kappa=(\kappa_{1},\kappa_{2},\kappa_{3})
with
\kappa_{2}=1
, we have

a^{-2}\Lambda_{m,n,\kappa}<\lambda_{m,n,\kappa}(a,b,c)<c^{-2}\Lambda_{m,n,\kappa},

(7.4)

where
\Lambda_{m,n,\kappa}
is given by (
6.1
).
If
\kappa_{2}=0
, then

\lambda_{m,n,\kappa}(a,b,c)<c^{-2}\Lambda_{m,n,\hat{\kappa}},

(7.5)

where
\hat{\kappa}=(\kappa_{1},1,\kappa_{2})
.
If
\kappa_{2}=0
and
m,n\geq 1
, then

a^{-2}\Lambda_{m-1,n-1,\hat{\kappa}}<\lambda_{m,n,\kappa}(a,b,c).

(7.6)

Proof.

Since
\lambda_{m,n,\kappa}(ra,rb,rc)=r^{-2}\lambda_{m,n,\kappa}(a,b,c)
, it is sufficient to assume that
c=1

in the proof of the second inequality in (
7.4
).
Then the function
q(t)
defined by (
4.4
) satisfies
q(t)>1
.
Let
\psi(t)
be the solution of the differential equation

\frac{{\rm d}\psi}{{\rm d}t}=f(t,\psi):=\cos^{2}\psi+\bigl(h-\lambda k^{2}\sn^{2}(t,k)\bigr)\sin^{2}\psi

determined by the initial condition
\psi(0)=\frac{1}{2}(1-\kappa_{1})\pi
.
Then Lemma
7.1
gives

\theta^{\prime}(t)-f(t,\theta(t))=\theta^{\prime}(t)\bigl(1-q(t)^{-1}\bigr)>0=\psi^{\prime}(t)-f(t,\psi(t)).

We also have
\psi(0)=\theta(0)
and
\psi^{\prime}(0)<\theta^{\prime}(0)
. Now the comparison theorem
\textcolor

[
25
, ????? II.9.III]
yields
\psi(t)<\theta(t)

for
0<t\leq K
.
Using this inequality for

, we obtain from (

) that

h_{n,\kappa}(\lambda;a,b,c)<h_{n,\kappa}(\lambda,k).

Since
p(s)>1
for
0<s\leq K^{\prime}
, we can show in a similar way that

Let
\lambda_{0}=\lambda_{m,n,\kappa}(a,b,c)
. Then

H_{m,\kappa}(\lambda_{0},k)<H_{m,\kappa}(\lambda_{0};a,b,c)=h_{n,\kappa}(\lambda_{0};a,b,c)<h_{n,\kappa}(\lambda_{0},k).

completing the proof of the second inequality in (

).
The first inequality is proved in a very similar way.

and

so

.
Now (

) applied to

in place of

Inequality (

8
Approximation by matrix eigenvalue problems

The eigenvalue functions
h_{n}(\lambda)
for equation (
4.2
) can be computed using the Prüfer angle by the
SLEIGN2 code
[
3
]
. However, we present another method which approximates the eigenvalues
h_{n}(\lambda)
by
eigenvalues of finite matrices.
We transform the differential equation (
4.2
) into trigonometric form and then expand
eigenfunctions in Fourier series.
This method was used by Ince
[
13
]
in order to compute eigenvalues of the Lamé equation (
1.1
).

Following Erdélyi
[
8
, formula (4), p. 56]
, we substitute

\tau=\tfrac{1}{2}\pi-\am(t,k)

in (
4.2
) where
\am
denotes the Jacobian amplitude function
[
21
, Section 22.16 (i)]
.
We multiply (
4.2
) by
q(t)^{3}
and use

\frac{{\rm d}\tau}{{\rm d}t}=-\dn(t,k),\qquad\cn(t,k)=\sin\tau,\qquad\sn(t,k)=\cos\tau.

Then we obtain

Dw+\lambda Cw=hBw,

(8.1)

where
D
is the differential operator

Dw:=-\bigl(a^{2}\sin^{2}\tau+b^{2}\cos^{2}\tau\bigr)\bigl(1-k^{2}\cos^{2}\tau\bigr)\frac{{\rm d}^{2}w}{{\rm d}\tau^{2}}-c^{2}k^{2}\cos\tau\sin\tau\frac{{\rm d}w}{{\rm d}\tau}

and
C
,
B
are multiplication operators

\displaystyle Cw:=k^{2}\cos^{2}\tau\bigl(a^{2}\sin^{2}\tau+b^{2}\cos^{2}\tau\bigr)^{2}w,\qquad Bw:=\bigl(a^{2}\sin^{2}\tau+b^{2}\cos^{2}\tau\bigr)^{2}w.

The boundary conditions corresponding to (
4.6
) are

w^{\prime}(0)=w^{\prime}\bigl(\tfrac{1}{2}\pi\bigr)=0,\qquad^{\prime}=\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}\tau}.

We compute the matrix representation of
D
in the trigonometric basis
\cos(2n\tau)
,
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
.
Using

\displaystyle\bigl(a^{2}\sin^{2}\tau+b^{2}\cos^{2}\tau\bigr)\bigl(1-k^{2}\cos^{2}\tau\bigr)=\tfrac{1}{2}\bigl(a^{2}+b^{2}\bigr)-\tfrac{1}{8}k^{2}\bigl(a^{2}+3b^{2}\bigr)

\displaystyle\hphantom{\bigl(a^2\sin^2\tau+b^2\cos^2\tau\bigr)\bigl(1-k^2\cos^2\tau\bigr) =}{}\!+\tfrac{1}{2}\bigl(b^{2}-a^{2}-b^{2}k^{2}\bigr)\cos(2\tau)+\tfrac{1}{8}k^{2}\bigl(a^{2}-b^{2}\bigr)\cos(4\tau),

we obtain

\displaystyle D\cos(2n\tau)=n^{2}\bigl(2\bigl(a^{2}+b^{2}\bigr)-\tfrac{1}{2}k^{2}\bigl(a^{2}+3b^{2}\bigr)\bigr)\cos(2n\tau)

\displaystyle\hphantom{D\cos(2n\tau)=}{}+n^{2}\bigl(b^{2}-a^{2}-b^{2}k^{2}\bigr)(\cos((2n-2)\tau)+\cos((2n+2)\tau))

\displaystyle\hphantom{D\cos(2n\tau)=}{}+\tfrac{1}{4}n^{2}k^{2}\bigl(a^{2}-b^{2}\bigr)(\cos((2n-4)\tau)+\cos((2n+4)\tau))

\displaystyle\hphantom{D\cos(2n\tau)=}{}+\tfrac{1}{2}nc^{2}k^{2}(\cos((2n-2)\tau)-\cos((2n+2)\tau)).

(8.2)

Similarly,

\displaystyle\bigl(a^{2}\sin^{2}\tau+b^{2}\cos^{2}\tau\bigr)^{2}\cos^{2}\tau=\tfrac{1}{16}\bigl(a^{4}+2a^{2}b^{2}+5b^{4}\bigr)+\tfrac{1}{32}\bigl(15b^{4}+2a^{2}b^{2}-a^{4}\bigr)\cos(2\tau)

\displaystyle\hphantom{\bigl(a^2\sin^2\tau+b^2\cos^2\tau\bigr)^2\cos^2\tau=}{}+\tfrac{1}{16}\bigl(b^{2}-a^{2}\bigr)\bigl(a^{2}+3b^{2}\bigr)\cos(4\tau)+\tfrac{1}{32}\bigl(a^{2}-b^{2}\bigr)^{2}\cos(6\tau)

gives

\displaystyle C\cos(2n\tau)=\tfrac{1}{16}k^{2}\bigl(a^{4}+2a^{2}b^{2}+5b^{4}\bigr)\cos(2n\tau)

\displaystyle\hphantom{ C\cos(2n\tau)=}{}+\tfrac{1}{64}k^{2}\bigl(15b^{4}+2a^{2}b^{2}-a^{4}\bigr)(\cos((2n-2)\tau)+\cos((2n+2)\tau))

\displaystyle\hphantom{ C\cos(2n\tau)=}{}+\tfrac{1}{32}k^{2}\bigl(b^{2}-a^{2}\bigr)\bigl(a^{2}+3b^{2}\bigr)(\cos((2n-4)\tau)+\cos((2n+4)\tau))

\displaystyle\hphantom{ C\cos(2n\tau)=}{}+\tfrac{1}{64}k^{2}\bigl(a^{2}-b^{2}\bigr)^{2}(\cos((2n-6)\tau)+\cos((2n+6)\tau)).

(8.3)

Finally,

\displaystyle\bigl(a^{2}\sin^{2}\tau+b^{2}\cos^{2}\tau\bigr)^{2}=\tfrac{1}{8}\bigl(3a^{4}+2a^{2}b^{2}+3b^{4}\bigr)+\tfrac{1}{2}\bigl(b^{4}-a^{4}\bigr)\cos(2\tau)+\textcolor{red}{3a^{4}\bigr)}

\displaystyle\hphantom{\bigl(a^2\sin^2\tau+b^2\cos^2\tau\bigr)^2 =}{}+\tfrac{1}{8}\bigl(a^{2}-b^{2}\bigr)^{2}\cos(4\tau)

gives

\displaystyle B\cos(2n\tau)=\tfrac{1}{8}\bigl(3a^{4}+2a^{2}b^{2}+3b^{4}\bigr)\cos(2n\tau)

\displaystyle\hphantom{B\cos(2n\tau)=}{}+\tfrac{1}{4}\bigl(b^{4}-a^{4}\bigr)(\cos((2n-2)\tau)+\cos((2n+2)\tau))

\displaystyle\hphantom{B\cos(2n\tau)=}{}+\tfrac{1}{16}\bigl(a^{2}-b^{2}\bigr)^{2}(\cos((2n-4)\tau)+\cos((2n+4)\tau)).

(8.4)

For
N\in\mathbb{N}
, we define
N+1
by
N+1
matrices
D_{N}
,
C_{N}
,
B_{N}
.
The entry in the
(j+1)
-th row and
(n+1)
-th column of
D_{N}
,
C_{N}
,
B_{N}
is the coefficient of
\cos(2j\tau)
on the right-hand side of (
8.2
)–(
8.4
), respectively, where
j,n=0,\dots,N
.
For instance, we have

B_{4}=\begin{pmatrix}b_{0}&b_{1}&b_{2}&0&0\\
2b_{1}&b_{0}+b_{2}&b_{1}&b_{2}&0\\
2b_{2}&b_{1}&b_{0}&b_{1}&b_{2}\\
0&b_{2}&b_{1}&b_{0}&b_{1}\\
0&0&b_{2}&b_{1}&b_{0}\end{pmatrix},

where

b_{0}:=\tfrac{1}{8}\bigl(3a^{4}+2a^{2}b^{2}+3b^{4}\bigr),\qquad b_{1}:=\tfrac{1}{4}\bigl(b^{4}-a^{4}\bigr),\qquad b_{2}:=\tfrac{1}{16}\bigl(a^{2}-b^{2}\bigr)^{2}.

We consider the generalized matrix eigenvalue problem

D_{N}u+\lambda C_{N}u=hB_{N}u.

We denote its eigenvalues
h
by
\hat{h}_{n,N}(\lambda;a,b,c)
,
n=0,1,\dots,N
, arranged in increasing order of their
real parts. We consider
\hat{h}_{n,N}(\lambda;a,b,c)
as an approximation to
h_{n,\kappa}(\lambda;a,b,c)

with parity
\kappa=(0,0,0)
. This is confirmed by the following result.

Theorem 8.1
.

For every
a>b>c>0
,
m,n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and
\lambda>0
, we have

\lim_{N\to\infty}\hat{h}_{n,N}(\lambda;a,b,c)=h_{n,\kappa}(\lambda;a,b,c),\qquad\text{where $\kappa=(0,0,0)$}.

Proof.

We assume that
a=1
without loss of generality. We apply a general theorem
[
23
, Theorem B.1]

from operator theory in Hilbert space.
Our Hilbert space is
H=L^{2}\bigl(0,\tfrac{1}{2}\pi\bigr)
. The operator on the left-hand side of (
8.1
)
is decomposed as
D+\lambda C=A+S
,
S=S_{1}+S_{2}+S_{3}
,
where

\displaystyle Au:=-u^{\prime\prime},

\displaystyle S_{1}u:=f_{1}(\tau)u^{\prime\prime},\qquad

\displaystyle f_{1}(\tau):=1-\bigl(\sin^{2}\tau+b^{2}\cos^{2}\tau\bigr)\bigl(1-k^{2}\cos^{2}\tau\bigr),

\displaystyle S_{2}u:=f_{2}(t)u^{\prime},\qquad

\displaystyle f_{2}(\tau):=-c^{2}k^{2}\cos\tau\sin\tau,

\displaystyle S_{3}u:=f_{3}(\tau)u,\qquad

\displaystyle f_{3}(\tau):=\lambda k^{2}\cos^{2}\tau\bigl(\sin^{2}\tau+b^{2}\cos^{2}\tau\bigr)^{2}.

The domain of
A
consists of all continuously differentiable functions
u\colon\big[0,\frac{1}{2}\pi\big]\to\mathbb{C}

such that
u^{\prime}
is absolutely continuous,
u^{\prime\prime}\in H
, and
u^{\prime}(0)=u^{\prime}\bigl(\frac{1}{2}\pi\bigr)=0
.
The operator
A
is self-adjoint, positive semi-definite with compact resolvent. Its eigenfunctions are

e_{0}(\tau)=\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}},\quad e_{\ell}(\tau)=\frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi}}\cos(2\ell\tau),\qquad\ell\in\mathbb{N}.

The system
\{e_{\ell}\}_{\ell\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
forms an orthonormal basis for
H
.
The domains of
S_{1}
,
S_{2}
are taken to be
D(A)
and the domain of
S_{3}
is
H
.

In
[
23
, Theorem B.1]
, it is assumed that there are nonnegative constants
\alpha
,
\beta
with
\beta<1
such that

\displaystyle\|Su\|^{2}\leq\alpha^{2}\|u\|^{2}+\beta^{2}\|Au\|^{2}\qquad\text{for all}\quad u\in D(A),

(8.5)

\displaystyle|\langle P_{n}Su,u\rangle|\leq\alpha\langle u,u\rangle+\beta\langle Au,u\rangle\qquad\text{for all}\quad u\in D(A),

(8.6)

where
P_{n}
is the orthogonal projection onto the linear span of
\{e_{0},e_{1},\dots,e_{n}\}
,
and
\|\cdot\|
,
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
denote the norm and inner product of
H
.
The existence of these constants is shown in the following lemma.

The statement of Theorem
8.1
now follows from
[
23
, Theorems B.1 and B.2]
.
∎

Lemma 8.2
.

There are nonnegative constants
\alpha
,
\beta
with
\beta<1
such that
(
8.5
) and (
8.6
) hold.

Proof.

We show (
8.5
).
We have

\|S_{1}u\|\leq\|f_{1}\|_{\infty}\|Au\|,\qquad\|S_{3}u||\leq\|f_{3}\|_{\infty}\|u\|.

Moreover, for every
\epsilon>0
,

\displaystyle\|S_{2}u\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\pi/2}|f_{2}(\tau)|^{2}|u^{\prime}(\tau)|^{2}\,{\rm d}\tau\leq\|f_{2}\|_{\infty}^{2}\int_{0}^{\pi/2}u^{\prime}(\tau)\overline{u^{\prime}(\tau)}\,{\rm d}\tau

\displaystyle=-\|f_{2}\|_{\infty}^{2}\int_{0}^{\pi/2}u^{\prime\prime}(\tau)\overline{u(\tau)}\,{\rm d}\tau\leq\|f_{2}\|_{\infty}^{2}\bigl(\epsilon^{-2}\|u\|^{2}+\epsilon^{2}\|Au\|^{2}\bigr).

Therefore,

\|Su\|\leq\gamma\|u\|+\delta\|Au\|,\qquad\|Su\|^{2}\leq\bigl(1+\epsilon^{-1}\bigr)\gamma^{2}\|u\|^{2}+(1+\epsilon)\delta^{2}\|Au\|^{2},

where

\gamma=\|f_{3}\|_{\infty}+\epsilon^{-1}\|f_{2}\|_{\infty},\qquad\delta=\|f_{1}\|_{\infty}+\epsilon\|f_{2}\|_{\infty}.

Since
\|f_{1}\|_{\infty}<1
, this implies (
8.5
) with
\beta<1
if we choose
\epsilon>0
sufficiently small.

We show (
8.6
).
Let

u=\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}c_{\ell}e_{\ell}\in D(A).

Then

P_{n}u(\tau)=\sum_{\ell=0}^{n}c_{\ell}e_{\ell}(\tau),\qquad\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}\tau}P_{n}u(\tau)=-\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}2\ell c_{\ell}\frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi}}\sin(2\ell\tau).

Therefore,

\|(P_{n}u)^{\prime}\|^{2}=\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}4\ell^{2}|c_{\ell}|^{2}\leq\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}4\ell^{2}|c_{\ell}|^{2}=\|u^{\prime}\|^{2}.

(8.7)

Now

\displaystyle\langle P_{n}S_{1}u,u\rangle=\langle S_{1}u,P_{n}u\rangle

\displaystyle{}=\int_{0}^{\pi/2}f_{1}(\tau)u^{\prime\prime}(\tau)\overline{P_{n}u(\tau)}\,{\rm d}\tau=-\int_{0}^{\pi/2}u^{\prime}(\tau)\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}\tau}\bigl(f_{1}(\tau)\overline{P_{n}u(\tau)}\bigr)\,{\rm d}\tau

\displaystyle{}=-\int_{0}^{\pi/2}u^{\prime}(\tau)f_{1}^{\prime}(\tau)\overline{P_{n}u(\tau)}\,{\rm d}\tau-\int_{0}^{\pi/2}u^{\prime}(\tau)f_{1}(\tau)\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}\tau}\overline{P_{n}u(\tau)}\,{\rm d}\tau.

Therefore, using (
8.7
), for any
\epsilon>0
,

\displaystyle|\langle P_{n}S_{1}u,u\rangle|

\displaystyle\leq\|f_{1}^{\prime}\|_{\infty}\int_{0}^{\pi/2}|u^{\prime}(\tau)||P_{n}u(\tau)|\,{\rm d}\tau+\|f_{1}\|_{\infty}\int_{0}^{\pi/2}|u^{\prime}(\tau)|\biggl|\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}\tau}P_{n}u(\tau)\biggr|\,{\rm d}\tau

\displaystyle\leq\epsilon^{-1}\|f_{1}^{\prime}\|_{\infty}\int_{0}^{\pi/2}|u(\tau)|^{2}\,{\rm d}\tau+(\epsilon\|f_{1}^{\prime}\|_{\infty}+\|f_{1}\|_{\infty})\int_{0}^{\pi/2}|u^{\prime}(\tau)|^{2}\,{\rm d}\tau

\displaystyle=\epsilon^{-1}\|f_{1}^{\prime}\|_{\infty}\langle u,u\rangle+(\epsilon\|f_{1}^{\prime}\|_{\infty}+\|f_{1}\|_{\infty})\langle Au,u\rangle.

Moreover, we have

\displaystyle|\langle P_{n}S_{2}u,u\rangle|\leq\|f_{2}\|_{\infty}\bigl(\epsilon^{-1}\langle u,u\rangle+\epsilon\langle Au,u\rangle\bigr),

\displaystyle|\langle P_{n}S_{3}u,u\rangle|\leq\|f_{3}\|_{\infty}\langle u,u\rangle.

By adding these estimates, we obtain (
8.6
)
with
\beta<1
if we choose
\epsilon>0
sufficiently small.
∎
