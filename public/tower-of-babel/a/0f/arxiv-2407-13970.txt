Frequentist Coverage of Bayes Posteriors in Nonlinear Inverse Problems with Gaussian Priors
F.Youngsoo Baek, S.Katerina Papagiannouli

Abstract
We study asymptotic frequentist coverage and approximately Gaussian properties of Bayes posterior credible sets in nonlinear inverse problems when a Gaussian prior is placed on the parameter of the PDE. The aim is to ensure valid frequentist coverage of Bayes credible intervals when estimating continuous linear functionals of the parameter. Our results show that Bayes credible intervals have conservative coverage under certain smoothness assumptions on the parameter and a compatibility condition between the likelihood and the prior, regardless of whether an efficient limit exists or Bernstein von-Mises (BvM) theorem holds. In the latter case, our results yield a corollary with more relaxed sufficient conditions than previous works. The theory is illustrated with a PDE that arises in predicting the transport of radioactive waste from underground repositories and optimizing oil recovery from subsurface fields: an elliptic inverse problem for Darcy flow. In this case, a near- 1 / N 1/\sqrt{N} contraction rate and conservative coverage results are obtained for linear functionals that were shown not to be estimable efficiently.

Introduction

We study a general class of statistical nonlinear inverse problems arising from partial differential equations (PDEs).
Such problems occur when an unknown function
\theta\in\Theta
, defined on a spatial domain
\mathcal{Z}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, can only be observed indirectly through a nonlinear forward operator

G:\Theta\to L^{2}(\mathcal{X})
,
where
\Theta
is a Hilbert space and
L^{2}(\mathcal{X})
denotes the space of square-integrable functions on
\mathcal{X}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, where observations are collected subject to measurement noise.
Typical examples arise in medical imaging, geophysical fluid dynamics, and climate modeling, where
\theta
encodes an unknown physical parameter of the system.

In this paper, we consider nonlinear inverse problems under the statistical model

Y_{i}=G(\theta_{0})(X_{i})+\varepsilon_{i},\qquad i=1,\ldots,N,

(1.1)

where
\theta_{0}\in\Theta
is the unknown ground-truth parameter, the design points
X_{i}\in\mathcal{X}
represent sampling locations, and the noise variables
\varepsilon_{i}
are independent Gaussian random variables. The sample size is denoted by
N\geq 1
.

In the class of problems studied here, the forward operator
G
is given by the parameter-to-solution map of a PDE describing the underlying physical system. The parameter
\theta_{0}
typically enters the PDE as a coefficient function, and
G(\theta_{0})
represents the observable solution. The primary inferential task is to recover
\theta_{0}
from the data
(Y_{i},X_{i})_{i=1}^{N}
.
In many applications, however, an estimate of
\theta_{0}
is not sufficient. The modern paradigm of uncertainty quantification (UQ) requires statistically valid measures of uncertainty derived from the data.

A widely used approach to UQ in inverse problems is Bayesian inference. Influential works such as
[
38
,
8
]
advocated the use of Bayesian methods in PDE-based inverse problems, building on earlier developments
[
39
,
25
]
. In practice, posterior distributions are often explored using Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) algorithms
[
7
]
.
From a theoretical perspective, much of the literature has focused on posterior consistency and contraction rates, that is, whether the posterior concentrates around the true parameter
\theta_{0}
as
N\to\infty
, and at what rate
[
20
,
29
]
.

A more delicate question concerns the
frequentist coverage
of Bayesian procedures: whether posterior credible sets provide valid confidence intervals for the unknown parameter. Initial results in this direction were obtained for linear inverse problems
[
27
,
5
,
6
]
. More recently,
[
34
]
and
[
30
]
developed Bernstein–von Mises (BvM) type results for nonlinear inverse problems, including PDE models such as the Schrödinger equation; see also
[
35
, Sections 2–3]
.

Roughly speaking, the BvM theorem asserts that, as
N\to\infty
, the posterior distribution becomes approximately Gaussian, centered around an efficient estimator and with variance given by the inverse Fisher information. As a consequence, posterior credible sets asymptotically achieve correct frequentist coverage. However, in infinite-dimensional settings, BvM may fail
[
16
]
, so we follow
[
34
]
and
[
30
]
in studying whether these properties are met for certain functionals of
\theta
.
In particular, it is known that when estimating a functional of form
\langle\theta,\psi\rangle
for
\psi\in\Theta
and when
\psi
does not belong to the range of the adjoint of an “information operator” (to be rigorously defined below), efficient estimation at the
1/\sqrt{N}
rate may be impossible, leading to a breakdown of BvM
[
32
]
. These observations raise the following fundamental question.

Frequentist coverage problem for Bayesian posteriors.

Consider the nonlinear regression model (
1.1
).
Set a target level
\gamma
(e.g., 5%).
Can we find a Bayesian posterior credible set
I_{N}
for estimating continuous linear functionals
\langle\theta,\psi\rangle
, containing at least
(1-\gamma)
-posterior mass, such that:

1.

asymptotically correct
coverage,

\liminf_{N\to\infty}P_{\theta_{0}}^{N}\big(\langle\theta_{0},\psi\rangle\in I_{N}\big)\geq 1-\gamma;\text{ or,}

2.

asymptotically exact
coverage,

\lim_{N\to\infty}P_{\theta_{0}}^{N}\big(\langle\theta_{0},\psi\rangle\in I_{N}\big)=1-\gamma,

even in regimes where Bernstein–von Mises theorems fail?

The goal of this paper is to study this question for a broad class of nonlinear inverse problems with Gaussian priors. A key difficulty arises from the fact that BvM-based techniques are not applicable in general. In particular, for PDE-based inverse problems such as Darcy flow, the failure of BvM is closely tied to the absence of efficient estimators. Indeed, classical results
[
43
]
show that efficient estimation of a functional requires a compatibility condition between the functional and the score operator. When this condition fails, neither efficient estimation nor BvM can hold
[
32
]
.
This motivates the need for new tools that do not rely on BvM. Our main contribution is to develop a general framework for establishing frequentist coverage of Bayesian credible sets in such settings. The key technical ingredient is a novel use of
variational inequalities
, which allow us to control the interaction between the forward operator and the prior covariance without relying on spectral diagonalization like in
[
27
]
.

As a motivating example, we study the Darcy flow problem, which models subsurface flow and has been widely used in geophysical applications
[
38
]
. Specializing to
\mathbb{R}^{2}
domains, the goal is to estimate the coefficient of a second-order divergence form elliptic PDE

-\nabla\cdot(a(s,t)\nabla u(s,t))=f(s,t)\quad\text{where}\quad(s,t)\in\mathcal{X}\subset\mathbb{R}^{2},

(1.2)

subject to problem-specific boundary conditions on
u
.
Here
a
is the unknown parameter, a function with a domain
\mathcal{X}
that is referred to as the “conductivity” or “diffusion” coefficient.
We get to observe finitely discretized measurements of
u=u(a)
, contaminated with observation errors.
The description suits the observation model (
2.1
), provided the uniqueness of
u(a)
for each
a
, so that the resulting implicit mapping
G
associates each
a
to
u(a)
.
If uniqueness and regularity are not guaranteed for
a
in a linear space, one can smoothly reparametrize the problem in terms of a linear space variable
\theta
.

Our results show that Bayesian credible intervals can still achieve valid frequentist coverage, even in regimes where BvM fails. Under suitable smoothness and compatibility conditions, we prove that posterior credible sets are asymptotically conservative, and in some cases exact, while achieving near-optimal contraction rates. These results demonstrate that the failure of BvM does not necessarily compromise uncertainty quantification in nonlinear inverse problems.

1.1
Our Contribution

Our main contribution is to develop a general framework for analyzing the frequentist coverage of Bayesian posterior credible sets in nonlinear inverse problems of the form (
1.1
), under Gaussian priors on
\Theta
. We then apply this framework to show the validity of posterior credible intervals in UQ for the Darcy flow problem.

The failure of BvM in Darcy flow has a fundamental connection to the
lack of an efficient estimator
. By efficiency, we refer to the phenomenon that a statistical estimator converges to its estimand at a
1/\sqrt{N}
-rate and attains the “best possible variance,” which should be the inverse of Fisher information, based on the Cramér-Rao lower bound
(cf.
[
28
, Section 2]
and
[
42
, Chapter 8]
).
[
43
]
studied making sense of such a statement for estimating functionals of an infinite-dimensional parameter belonging to a normed linear space. A consequence of
[
43
, Theorems 3.1, 4.1]
is that the “efficient information”, whose inverse is a lower bound to the variance of all
1/\sqrt{N}
-rate estimators of a (not necessarily linear) functional
\Psi(\theta)
is non-zero if and only if a suitably defined gradient of
\Psi
belongs to the range of the adjoint of a certain
score operator
.
[
43
, Section 7]
already contained examples demonstrating not all functionals satisfy such a property.
[
32
, Theorems 1–2]
related the score operator for the likelihood defined by (
2.1
) to a derivative
\mathbb{I}_{\theta_{0}}
of
G
at
\theta_{0}
(defined in Section
2
), before using this relation to show both the lack of estimators convergent to the true functional at
1/\sqrt{N}
-rate and the failure of BvM in Darcy flow when
\psi
does not belong to the range of the adjoint of
\mathbb{I}_{\theta_{0}}
.

In this paper, we focus on inference for
linear
functionals
\Psi(\theta)=\langle\theta,\psi\rangle
, and establish conditions under which posterior credible intervals provide valid frequentist uncertainty quantification, even in regimes where Bernstein–von Mises (BvM) theorems fail.
More precisely, we show that:

1.

Under suitable regularity conditions on the forward operator
G
, the prior covariance, and the functional
\psi
, posterior credible intervals achieve
asymptotically correct
frequentist coverage.

2.

Even in the absence of efficient estimators (and hence failure of BvM), posterior distributions of
\Psi(\theta)
contract around
\Psi(\theta_{0})
at rates that depend on the smoothness of
\theta_{0}
and increasingly approach
1/\sqrt{N}
.

A key technical challenge is that classical approaches rely on spectral analysis of the Fisher information operator or diagonalization arguments, which are generally unavailable in nonlinear PDE-based inverse problems, unlike in linear inverse problems where the forward operator admits a suitable spectral decomposition
[
27
]
.
To overcome this difficulty, we introduce a novel use of
variational inequalities
, which allow us to control the interaction between the forward operator and the prior covariance without requiring explicit spectral decompositions.

Definition 1.1

(Variational inequality)
.

Let
X,Y
be Hilbert spaces and let
A:X\to Y
be an injective bounded linear operator with non-closed range.
Let
f:[0,\infty)\to[0,\infty)
be continuous and strictly increasing, with
f(0)=0
.
We say that
x\in X
satisfies a
variational inequality
with constant
b>0
and modifier
f
if, for all
u\in X
,

b\|x-u\|_{X}^{2}\leq\|x\|_{X}^{2}-\|u\|_{X}^{2}+f(\|Ax-Au\|_{Y}).

In our setting, this inequality is applied to operators of the form

\Lambda^{-1/2}\mathbb{I}_{\theta_{0}},

where
\mathbb{I}_{\theta_{0}}
denotes the linearization of the forward map
G
at
\theta_{0}
, and
\Lambda
is the inverse of the Laplace operator which encodes the prior covariance structure. The variational inequality provides indirect control over the spectrum of the operator
\Lambda^{-1/2}\mathbb{I}_{\theta_{0}}^{*}\mathbb{I}_{\theta_{0}}\Lambda^{-1/2}
, which governs the fluctuations of the posterior distribution.
We can draw on several consequences established in the literature, which we summarize in Appendix
B
.

As we show in Section
4
, previous results like
[
32
, Theorem 5]
can be used to verify this inequality when
\theta_{0}
is smooth enough. The novelty of our application is that we use an inequality in Definition
1.1
to study the frequentist coverage of Bayes posteriors, whereas previous authors used it to derive rates of convergence for estimators in deterministic inverse problems. Under reasonable conditions stated in terms of smoothness of
\theta_{0}
and
\psi
, and a local injectivity estimate on the Cameron-Martin space of the prior, we prove both an asymptotically conservative, sometimes exact, coverage of Bayes posterior quantile-based interval estimates and an upper bound on posterior convergence rate to
\theta_{0}
, closer to
1/\sqrt{N}
for a smoother truth
\theta_{0}
.

This approach replaces classical spectral arguments and yields the key estimates required to establish frequentist coverage. In particular, it allows us to treat nonlinear inverse problems where diagonalization techniques are not available. As a consequence, we show that Bayesian credible intervals provide valid frequentist confidence sets even in settings where BvM fails due to the lack of efficient estimators.

Prior Distribution

We restrict the scope of priors considered to Gaussian measures
\Pi_{0}
supported on
L^{2}(\mathcal{Z})

2

Centered Gaussian distribution on a separable Hilbert space correspond bijectively to covariance operators

The covariance operator

\mathbb{E}\langle\theta^{\prime},h\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathcal{Z})}^{2}=\langle\mathcal{C}h,h\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathcal{Z})}

Remark
.

We make two remarks on our choice of notation.
First, the Cameron-Martin space of a Gaussian measure is often referred to as the “reproducing kernel Hilbert space (RKHS)” in nonparametric statistics literature
[
17
,
19
]
.
We use a different terminology to maintain consistency with
[
2
]
.
Second, as mentioned by
[
21
]

\mathcal{C}
correspond to
L^{-2\alpha}
for some unbounded operator

, where
\alpha>0
is our desired “smoothness parameter.” They also use the notation “
(\widetilde{H}^{s})_{s\in\mathbb{R}}
” to indicate Hilbert scales generated by
L
, so that the Cameron-Martin space of the resulting prior must be written as “
\widetilde{H}^{\alpha}
.”
Such a notation suits certain covariance choices, such as spectral powers of the negative Dirichlet Laplacian,
but is not satisfactory when one wants to decouple the smoothness of
\theta_{0}
(measured in the Sobolev sense) from the desired boundary conditions.
For example, in Section
4
, we will consider the prior of
[
20
]
and
[
30
]
obtained by smoothly truncating sample paths of a centered stationary Whittle-Matérn Gaussian process to a compact subset of a bounded domain
\mathcal{Z}
.
The construction ensures that the Cameron-Martin space of the resulting prior is continuously embedded in

, to which
\theta_{0}
is assumed to belong.
However, the boundary conditions are ensured through multiplying sample paths with a smooth cutoff function, so it is awkward to write the resulting covariance operator, implicitly defined, as an
\alpha
-th spectral power of some other operator.

3
General Results for Gaussian Priors

Let
\Psi:L^{2}(\mathcal{Z})\to\mathbb{R}
be a non-zero continuous linear functional with Riesz representer
\psi\in\widetilde{H}^{q},\;q\geq 0
.
The case
q=0
is when
\psi\in L^{2}(\mathcal{Z})
.
The goal of this section is to derive results on the contraction and frequentist coverage properties of the induced posterior on the linear functional
\Psi(\theta)=\langle\psi,\theta\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathcal{Z})}
.
We say that the posterior
contracts around
\Psi(\theta_{0})
at a rate
s_{N}
if there exists
b>0
such that for any sequence
M_{N}\to\infty
, we have
P_{\theta_{0}}^{N}(\Pi_{N}(|\Psi(\theta-\theta_{0})|>M_{N}s_{N}|\mathcal{D}^{N})\geq e^{-bN\epsilon_{N}^{2}})\to 0
.
We define a Bayesian credible interval corresponding to the posterior distribution (
2.2
) as

I_{N}:=\{r:|r-\langle\psi,\overline{\theta}_{N}\rangle_{L^{2}}|\leq Q_{N,\gamma}\},

(3.1)

where
Q_{N,\gamma}
is a
(1-\gamma)
-th quantile satisfying
\Pi_{N}\circ\Psi^{-1}(I_{N}\mid\mathcal{D}^{N})=1-\gamma
and

\overline{\theta}_{N}
is the mean of the random variable
\theta
with respect to the posterior distribution (
2.2
), which makes sense as a Bochner integral.
An often small, user-chosen level
\gamma
specifies the nominal
credible level
of this interval; the hope is that
I_{N}
has asymptotically the same
coverage
as
1-\gamma
. Precisely, coverage is defined as
P_{\theta_{0}}^{N}(\theta_{0}\in I_{N})
.
We say that the coverage is
asymptotically correct
if

\liminf_{N\to\infty}P_{\theta_{0}}^{N}(\theta_{0}\in I_{N})\geq 1-\gamma,

and
asymptotically exact
if

\lim_{N\to\infty}P_{\theta_{0}}^{N}(\theta_{0}\in I_{N})=1-\gamma.

Main Results

This section contains the main result of this work on the asymptotic frequentist coverage of Bayesian credible intervals.
We make the following assumptions about the prior, operator

Remark
.

Before proceeding any further, we first define several non-stochastic quantities that will be useful in the statement of our main results.

The bounded linear operator

The proof of the following claim is postponed to the Supplement

Lemma 3.1

For every finite

This lemma sheds some light on the importance of the sequence of vectors

Theorem 3.2
.

Suppose Assumptions

Suppose, furthermore, that for

defined in (

P_{\theta_{0}}^{N}\left(\left|\frac{\Pi_{N}^{LAN}\{\Theta_{N}-ts_{N}^{-1}\overline{\psi}_{N}|\mathcal{D}^{N}\}}{\Pi_{N}^{LAN}\{\Theta_{N}|\mathcal{D}^{N}\}}-1\right|\geq e^{-cN\epsilon_{N}^{2}}\right)\to 0,

for some

Moreover, for an arbitrarily slowly growing sequence

\Pi_{N}\{\Psi(\theta-\theta_{0})\geq M_{N}s_{N}|\mathcal{D}^{N}\}=o_{P_{\theta_{0}}^{N}}(1).

(3.8)

Remark
.

[
30

is a previous BvM for PDE parameter identification problems.

[
30

was imposed to prove a uniform

Condition (

The additional cost we pay is condition (

The proof of Theorem

The theorem reduces the study of the relative rate of decay between two deterministic sequences

\theta_{0}

An interpretation of

The following results provide a way of estimating these sequences, but the sufficient conditions are quite technical.
Importantly, we now use Assumption

Theorem 3.3
.

Under Assumptions

(i)

\delta(2+q)(\frac{\beta}{2}\wedge 1)>\frac{1}{2}

(ii)

3.6

\tau_{N}\gg N^{-\frac{2\tilde{\delta}-1}{4(1-\tilde{\delta})}}

(i)

(ii)

Remark
.

In the context of PDE-based inverse problems and model (

Inspection of the proof of Theorems

Discussion

Theorem

If

and

equivalent to

on
\widetilde{H}^{1}
.
If we now additionally assume that

is linear and

[

In our notation, then,

Whether condition

is now met depends on the smoothness of
\psi
and the obtainable dependence of
\delta(s)
on

in Assumption
3.6
.

Conditions
2
and
3
provide alternative conditions under which we can alleviate the demand

The second condition, though technical, will be useful when we analyze a Gaussian prior for Darcy flow with

The third condition states that

as previously mentioned,
[
30

stipulate this exact condition, before proving BvM (Theorems 3.7–8).
Let us compare, then, how our other sufficient conditions compare with those of
[
30
]
.

[
30

are comparable with our Assumptions

,
and Assumption

is not necessary when

Furthermore, their re-scaling sequence

, given in display (10) but with a different notation “
\alpha

The remaining conditions of

On the other hand, we assumed a more relaxed upper bound (

[

4
Application: Darcy Flow Problem

This section summarizes an application of Theorems
3.2
and
3.3
to the case of identifying the unknown parameter in PDE (
1.2
).
As mentioned in the introduction,

[
32
]
showed for this problem how
\psi\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\mathcal{Z})
need not belong to
R(\mathbb{I}_{\theta_{0}}^{*})
, even in the simplest Darcy flow model where a solution to the PDE can be derived by hand, which implied the impossibility of BvM.
This is disappointing, as one is left without any knowledge about the asymptotic coverage of posterior credible intervals.
We now show that under natural smoothness conditions on
\theta_{0}
and a target linear functional, one can recover correct (albeit not exact) asymptotic coverage of Bayesian credible intervals.

4.1
Observation Model

Consider a bounded domain
\mathcal{Z}=\mathcal{X}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
and an elliptic PDE with inhomogeneous boundary condition:

\begin{cases}\nabla\cdot(a\nabla u)=f&\text{on }\mathcal{Z},\\
u=u_{D}&\text{on }\partial\mathcal{Z}.\end{cases}

(4.1)

To highlight only the relevant statistical aspect of the inverse problem, we will assume the source term
f
and the boundary condition
u_{D}
belong to
C^{\infty}(\overline{\mathcal{Z}})
.
For a fixed, unknown conductivity field
a_{0}
, we denote the corresponding PDE solution
u=u_{a_{0}}
.
Suppose
\alpha>1+d/2
and define
n=n(x)
as the outward-pointing normal vector at
x\in\partial\mathcal{Z}
, with the boundary derivative
\frac{\partial a}{\partial n}
understood in the trace sense if necessary.
If
a_{0}\in\Gamma
, defined by:

\Gamma_{\alpha,K_{min}}:=\bigg\{a\in H^{\alpha}(\mathcal{Z}):a>K_{min}\text{ on }\mathcal{Z},\;a=1\text{ on }\partial\mathcal{Z},\;\frac{\partial^{j}a}{\partial n^{j}}=0\text{ on }\partial\mathcal{Z}\text{ for }j=1,\ldots,\alpha-1\bigg\},

(4.2)

then the differential operator
u\mapsto\nabla\cdot(a\nabla u)
is strongly elliptic on
\mathcal{Z}
. We assume a fixed lower bound
K_{min}\in(0,1)
is also known.
Given
a_{0}\in H^{\alpha}(\mathcal{Z})
,
[
18
, Theorems 8.3–13]
imply that the PDE (
4.1
) has a unique strong solution
u=u_{a_{0}}
belonging to
H^{\alpha+1}(\mathcal{Z})
.
The parameter space
\Gamma
, however, is not a linear space, so we consider a bijective reparameterization of
a
into
\theta
belonging to some linear space.

Definition 4.1
.

[
33
, Definition 7]

We say
\phi
is a
link function
if it is a smooth, strictly increasing bijection from
\mathbb{R}
to
(K_{min},\infty)
,
satisfying
\phi(0)=1
and
\phi^{\prime}>0
.
We say a link function
\phi
is
regular
if
\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}|\phi^{(k)}(x)|<\infty
for every integer
k\geq 1
.

[
33
]
showed that a link function
\phi
is a smooth bijection between the linear space
H^{\alpha}_{0}(\mathcal{Z})
and
\Gamma
.
Regularity of
\phi
was used in the authors’ proof of convergence properties of a penalized M-estimator.
An easy-to-compute link function considered by
[
38
]
of the form
\phi(x)=e^{x}+K_{min}
, on the other hand, is not regular.
Whether
\phi
is regular or not, we can smoothly parameterize
a=\phi(\theta)
and define the forward map as
G(\theta)=u_{\phi\circ\theta}
,
based on the uniqueness of the solution.

The goal of the inverse problem is to estimate the reparameterization
\theta_{0}
of
a_{0}
based on noisy observations given by the model (
2.1
).
The following assumption was introduced by
[
37
]
and has been used by previous analyses of the problem from a Bayesian angle by
[
20
]
.

[
37
]
already highlighted the fact that without such a condition, estimating
\theta_{0}
becomes a very ill-conditioned problem irrespective of
\alpha
.
Readers interested in this “low-regularity” problem may refer to the more recent results
[
3
]
.

Assumption 4.1
.

\theta_{0}
is compactly supported on some
K\subsetneq\mathcal{Z}
.
There exists a partition of
\mathcal{Z}
into subsets
\mathcal{Z}_{1}
and
\mathcal{Z}_{2}
, each possibly disconnected, such that

G(\theta_{0})
satisfies

\inf_{x\in\mathcal{Z}_{1}}\|\nabla G(\theta_{0})(x)\|_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\geq k_{1},\;\inf_{x\in\mathcal{Z}_{2}}\Delta G(\theta_{0})(x)\geq k_{2}
for positive constants
k_{1},\;k_{2}
.

Note that the above assumption is implied by a positive source term
f
strictly bounded away from 0.
For us, the above assumption has a further benefit that Assumption
3.6
now becomes verifiable for Darcy flow; see the proof of Lemma D.1
[
1
]
.

4.2
Gaussian Process Prior Distribution

We briefly review the properties of a previously proposed prior construction from
[
20
, Example 25]
.
In our notation, a sequence of
N
-dependent priors
\Pi_{N}
is constructed by choosing a decaying sequence
\tau_{N}=(\sqrt{N}\epsilon_{N})^{-1},\;\epsilon_{N}=N^{-\frac{\alpha+1}{2\alpha+2+d}}
,
and a Gaussian “base measure”
\Pi_{0}
induced by the following procedures.
First, take a centered stationary Whittle–Matérn Gaussian process

We first define some notations that are standard in studying the spectra of bounded linear operators between Hilbert spaces.
For a compact linear operator

, we define the spectral projector

E_{t}x:=\sum_{n=1,2,\ldots:\sigma^{2}_{n}<t}\langle x,v_{n}\rangle_{X}~v_{n}+P(x),

For any piecewise continuous function

\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(t)~d\|E_{t}x\|^{2}_{X}:=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}f(\sigma_{n}^{2})|\langle x,v_{n}\rangle_{X}|^{2},

(5.2)

for which Lebesgue dominated convergence theorem and integration by parts hold
[
13
, Section 2.3]
.
By extension, we also define
f(K^{*}K):=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}f(\sigma_{n}^{2})\langle\cdot,v_{n}\rangle_{X}~v_{n}
and

\|f(K^{*}K)x\|_{X}=\sqrt{\int_{0}^{\|K^{*}K\|_{\rm op}^{2}}f^{2}(t)~d\|E_{t}x\|_{X}^{2}}.

(5.3)

We refer the reader to
[
12
, Chapter 2.3]
for more details on the spectral projection theory.

A
Hilbert Scales Result

Let
A
be a Hilbert space and
\Lambda^{1/2}
an adjoint, symmetric, unbounded
A

A
, and which satisfies
\|\Lambda^{1/2}x\|\geq c\|x\|^{2}
for some

and every

.

[
13
, Proposition 8.19]
collects the important properties of the Hilbert scales
(\widetilde{H}^{p})_{p\in\mathbb{R}}
generated by
\Lambda
.

Theorem A.1
.

The Hilbert scales
(\widetilde{H}^{p})_{p\in\mathbb{R}}
induced by the operator
\Lambda
has the following properties.

(i)

For
-\infty<p<q<\infty
,
\widetilde{H}^{q}
is dense and continuously embedded in
\widetilde{H}^{p}
.

(ii)

Let
p,q\in\mathbb{R}
. The operator
\Lambda^{p-q}
defined on

has a unique extension to
\widetilde{H}^{p}
that is an isomorphism from
\widetilde{H}^{p}
to
\widetilde{H}^{q}
. If

, then the extension restricted to
\widetilde{H}^{p}
in
\widetilde{H}^{q}
is self-adjoint and strictly positive. For appropriate extensions, we have
\Lambda^{p-q}=\Lambda^{p}\Lambda^{-q}
and

.

(iii)

If

\widetilde{H}^{p}=D(\Lambda^{p/2})
and
\widetilde{H}^{-p}=(\widetilde{H}^{p})^{*}
.

(iv)

If

, then we have the interpolation inequality

\|f\|_{\widetilde{H}^{q}}\leq\|f\|_{\widetilde{H}^{p}}^{\frac{r-q}{r-p}}\|f\|_{\widetilde{H}^{r}}^{\frac{q-p}{r-p}}.

Proof of Lemma

The proof is again broken down into several parts.

The basic idea is to strengthen the weak convergence of the posterior (in probability) to a Gaussian limit in Lemma

C.2.1
Controlling the Sequence Ratio

We first state a lemma that complements the previous upper bound statement in Lemma
5.3
by showing
t_{N}/s_{N}
is also bounded
from below
away from zero.

Lemma C.1
.

If
\psi\notin{\rm ker}(\mathbb{I}_{\theta_{0}})
, then
\liminf_{N\to\infty}t_{N}/s_{N}>0
.

We proceed similarly as in the proof of
[
27
, Theorem 5.4]
.
Write
s_{N}^{2}=\int_{0}^{\infty}f_{N}^{2}(\mathbb{K}_{\theta_{0},\Lambda}^{*}\mathbb{K}_{\theta_{0},\Lambda})\\
~dF_{\psi_{\lambda}}^{2}(t)
for
f_{N}(t)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+N\tau_{N}^{2}t}}
,
and write
s_{N}^{2}=A_{N}+B_{N}
with

A_{N}=\int_{\frac{1}{N\tau_{N}^{2}}}^{\infty}f_{N}(t)~dF_{\psi_{\lambda}}^{2}(t),\;B_{N}=\int_{0}^{\frac{1}{N\tau_{N}^{2}}}f_{N}^{2}(t)~dF_{\psi_{\lambda}}^{2}(t).

Similarly, write
t_{N}^{2}=\int_{0}^{\infty}g_{N}^{2}(\mathbb{K}_{\theta_{0},\Lambda}^{*}\mathbb{K}_{\theta_{0},\Lambda})~dF_{\psi_{\lambda}}^{2}(t)
for
g_{N}(t)=\frac{\sqrt{N}\tau_{N}\sqrt{t}}{\sqrt{1+N\tau_{N}^{2}t}}
,
and
t_{N}^{2}=C_{N}+D_{N}
with

C_{N}=\int_{\frac{1}{N\tau_{N}^{2}}}^{\infty}g_{N}(t)~dF_{\psi_{\lambda}}^{2}(t),\;D_{N}=\int_{0}^{\frac{1}{N\tau_{N}^{2}}}g_{N}^{2}(t)~dF_{\psi_{\lambda}}^{2}(t).

We claim that it suffices to prove
\limsup_{N\to\infty}B_{N}/A_{N}<\infty
.
This is because

\frac{t_{N}^{2}}{s_{N}^{2}}=\frac{C_{N}+D_{N}}{A_{N}+B_{N}}\geq\frac{C_{N}}{A_{N}+B_{N}}=\frac{C_{N}/A_{N}}{1+B_{N}/A_{N}}.

It is straightforward to check that for each
N
,
C_{N}/A_{N}\geq\frac{1}{2}
, so whenever
\limsup_{N\to\infty}B_{N}/A_{N}
is finite,
we have
\liminf_{N\to\infty}t_{N}^{2}/s_{N}^{2}>0
.

We now prove if
\psi\notin{\rm ker}(\mathbb{I}_{\theta_{0}})
, then
\limsup_{N\to\infty}B_{N}/A_{N}<\infty
.
It suffices to prove that
\limsup_{N\to\infty}\\
B_{N}/A_{N}<\infty
if there exists some
L\in(0,1)
such that for all large enough
N
,

A_{N}=\int_{\frac{1}{N\tau_{N}^{2}}}^{\infty}f_{N}^{2}(t)~dF_{\psi_{\lambda}}^{2}(t)\geq L\int_{0}^{\infty}f_{N}^{2}(t)~dF_{\psi_{\lambda}}^{2}(t).

Furthermore, the above display is true if there exists some
c>0
such that
A_{N}\geq cB_{N}
; for then we have
A_{N}\geq\frac{c}{c+1}(A_{N}+B_{N})
.
We now prove this sufficient condition, and to do so, we suppose otherwise:
for every
c>0
and positive integer
N_{0}
, there exists an
N\geq N_{0}
such that
A_{N}<cB_{N}
.
For this to be true on a given
N
, since both
A_{N}
and
B_{N}
are positive quantities bounded from above by 1, it is necessary that
A_{N}=0
.
Write by
(\sigma_{n}^{2},\nu_{n})
the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of
\mathbb{K}_{\theta_{0},\Lambda}^{*}\mathbb{K}_{\theta_{0},\Lambda}
.
By definition of
A_{N}
, this can only be the case if for every
n>0
, either
\sigma_{n}^{2}<\frac{1}{N\tau_{N}^{2}}
or
\langle\psi_{\lambda},\nu_{n}\rangle=0
(or both).
Extract a subsequence of positive integers
N_{k}
such that for every
N_{k}
we have
A_{N_{k}}=0
.
Then
N_{k}
diverges to
+\infty
by the contradiction hypothesis, so we conclude that for a non-trivial operator
\mathbb{K}_{\theta_{0},\Lambda}^{*}\mathbb{K}_{\theta_{0},\Lambda}
, we must have
\langle\psi_{\lambda},\nu_{n}\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathcal{Z})}=0
for every
n
such that
\sigma_{n}^{2}>0
.
Since we have assumed
\psi\notin{\rm ker}(\mathbb{I}_{\theta_{0}})
, which is if and only if
\psi_{\lambda}\notin{\rm ker}(\mathbb{K}_{\theta_{0},\Lambda}^{*}\mathbb{K}_{\theta_{0},\Lambda})
by injectivity of
\Lambda^{-1/2}

∎

C.2.2
Triangular Central Limit of the Score Term

Next, we derive a limiting law for the sequence of random variables

by the

[
36

Recalling definitions (
5.5
) and (

\{t_{N}^{-1}\epsilon_{i}\mathbb{I}_{\theta_{0}}(\overline{\psi}_{N})(X_{i})\}_{i,N}

for each

comprises i.i.d. zero-mean random variables such that

\sigma_{N,i}^{2}:=\mathbb{E}[t_{N}^{-2}\epsilon_{i}^{2}\mathbb{I}_{\theta_{0}}(\overline{\psi}_{N})(X_{i})^{2}]=\frac{1}{N}.

We now verify the Lindeberg’s condition for the application of the central limit theorem as in
[
36
, (9.23)]
.
From the definition of perturbation (
3.3
), we have
\psi_{\lambda}\in B_{\widetilde{H}^{2}}(M)
for some
M>0
.
From Assumption 4, we can choose some dense subspace
\widetilde{H}^{p}\subset L^{2}(\mathcal{Z})
over which
\Pi_{0}(\widetilde{H}^{p})=1

since the Cameron-Martin space
\widetilde{H}^{1}
of
\Pi_{0}
is compactly embedded in
\widetilde{H}^{p}
(cf.
[
38

\|\mathbb{I}_{\theta_{0}}(\overline{\psi}_{N})(x)\|_{\infty}\leq M^{\prime}

\mathbb{E}[\epsilon_{i}^{4}]=3
, for every

\displaystyle t_{N}^{-2}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}\left[\epsilon_{i}^{2}\mathbb{I}_{\theta_{0}}(\overline{\psi}_{N})(X_{i})^{2}\mathbf{1}\left(|t_{N}^{-1}\epsilon_{i}\mathbb{I}_{\theta_{0}}(\overline{\psi}_{N})(X_{i})|>r\right)\right]

The latter probability is identical for all

This concludes the verification of the conditions for the central limit, and we conclude that
t_{N}^{-1}\widehat{\Psi}_{N}
weakly converges to

Uniform Tightness Argument

We proceed to show that
s_{N}^{-1}\{\Psi(\theta-\theta_{0})-\widehat{\Psi}_{N}\}

[

Before doing so, we claim that indeed this is enough to also show that

O_{P_{\theta_{0}}^{N}}(1)

The former term involves an expectation, which we may write as

from Assumption

3.5

\mathbb{E}[e^{s_{N}^{-1}Z_{N}}\mathbf{1}_{\Theta_{N}}\mid\mathcal{D}^{N}]

First, we observe that
s_{N}\gtrsim 1/\sqrt{N}
in all three cases given in Lemma

s_{N}^{-2}\mathbb{E}[\Psi(\theta-\theta_{0})^{2}\mathbf{1}_{\Theta_{N}^{c}}\mid\mathcal{D}^{N}]\lesssim N^{2}\sqrt{\mathbb{E}[\Psi(\theta-\theta_{0})^{4}\mid\mathcal{D}^{N}]}\sqrt{\Pi_{N}(\Theta_{N}^{c}\mid\mathcal{D}^{N})}.

3.2

for a constant

\displaystyle P_{\theta_{0}}^{N}\left(N^{2}\mathbb{E}[\Psi(\theta-\theta_{0})^{4}\mid\mathcal{D}^{N}]\Pi_{N}(\Theta_{N}^{c}\mid\mathcal{D}^{N})>\frac{1}{\log^{2}N}\right)

\sup_{\theta\in\Theta_{N}}\left|\sum_{i=1}^{N}\{G(\theta)-G(\theta_{0})\}^{2}(X_{i})-N\|G(\theta)-G(\theta_{0})\|_{L^{2}(\mathcal{Z})}^{2}\right|,

we may view it as an empirical process
with random variables
\{G(\theta)-G(\theta_{0})\}^{2}(X_{i})
dominated by
\overline{\epsilon}_{N,1}^{2}
.
From the Lipschitz estimate for
G
(

),
it can be shown that the following provides an upper bound on the left hand side of (

),
due to a consequence of the same theorem (cf.
[

, Remark 3.5.5]
):

\sqrt{N}\overline{\epsilon}_{N,1}^{2}J_{N,s}\left(1,\overline{\epsilon}_{N,1}^{2}\right),

(D.7)

which, by (
D.6
), is bounded above by a constant multiple of
\sqrt{N}\overline{\epsilon}_{N,1}^{2-\frac{d}{2\zeta}},\;\zeta<\alpha-1-d
.
It can be checked then that condition (
4.5
) implies this upper bound is
o_{N}(N\epsilon_{N}^{2})
.
∎

Finally, for the third term (
D.3
):

\sup_{\theta\in\Theta_{N}^{(t)}}\left|\sum_{i=1}^{N}\{\mathbb{I}_{\theta_{0}}(\theta-\theta_{0})(X_{i})\}^{2}-N\|\mathbb{I}_{\theta_{0}}(\theta-\theta_{0})\|_{L^{2}(\mathcal{Z})}^{2}\right|,

We can again use the Lipschitz estimate for the linear operator
\mathbb{I}_{\theta_{0}}
from (
E.8
).
We deduce that by Lemma
D.3
with the case of
r=1
,
plugging in the lower bound for
s_{N}
from Lemma 5.2,
we have
|t|s_{N}^{-1}\|\overline{\psi}_{N}\|_{C^{1}}\lesssim\overline{\epsilon}_{N,1}
for every
t\in\mathbb{R}
if the following relation holds for
\tau_{N}=(\sqrt{N}\epsilon_{N})^{-1}
:

\frac{1}{2}\geq\frac{\alpha+1}{2\alpha+2+d}\left\{\frac{\alpha-1-d/2}{\alpha+1-d/2}\frac{\alpha-1-d}{\alpha-d/2}+2\left(1-\frac{(2+q)\alpha-1}{(2+q)\alpha+1}\frac{2\alpha-1-d/2}{2\alpha}\right)\right\}.

We have assumed
\alpha>3+\frac{3}{2}d
.
By plugging in this lower bound, it can be checked the above inequality always holds for
q=0
and every
d\geq 1
, and thus holds for every
q\geq 0
since the display is decreasing in
q
.
Therefore, we conclude that indeed
|t|s_{N}^{-1}\|\overline{\psi}_{N}\|_{C^{1}}\lesssim\overline{\epsilon}_{N,1}
, so that similar calculations as done for (
D.2
) can be now done, and it again then suffices to show that (
D.7
) is true.
∎

9. Showing (
3.6
).

We define a new “linearized posterior”
\Pi_{N}^{DLAN}=\Pi_{N}^{DLAN}(\cdot\mid\mathcal{D}^{N})
as

\displaystyle\Pi_{N}^{DLAN}(A\mid\mathcal{D}^{N})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\int_{A}e^{-\ell_{N}^{DLAN}(\theta-\theta_{0})}~d\Pi_{N}(\theta)}{\int e^{-\ell_{N}^{DLAN}(\theta-\theta_{0})}~d\Pi_{N}(\theta)},

\displaystyle\ell_{N}^{DLAN}(\theta-\theta_{0})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left[\epsilon_{i}\mathbb{I}_{\theta_{0}}(\theta-\theta_{0})(X_{i})-\frac{\{\mathbb{I}_{\theta_{0}}(\theta-\theta_{0})(X_{i})\}^{2}}{2}\right].

Then, given (
D.3
), we have

\left|\frac{\Pi_{N}^{LAN}\{\Theta_{N}-ts_{N}^{-1}\overline{\psi}_{N}\mid\mathcal{D}^{N}\}}{\Pi_{N}^{LAN}\{\Theta_{N}\mid\mathcal{D}^{N}\}}-1\right|\leq e^{o(N\epsilon_{N}^{2})}\left|\frac{\Pi_{N}^{DLAN}\{\Theta_{N}-ts_{N}^{-1}\overline{\psi}_{N}|\mathcal{D}^{N}\}}{\Pi_{N}^{DLAN}\{\Theta_{N}|\mathcal{D}^{N}\}}-1\right|.

Therefore, to verify (
3.6
),
it suffices to show that there exists some
c^{\prime}>0
for which

P_{\theta_{0}}^{N}\left(\left|\frac{\Pi_{N}^{DLAN}\{\Theta_{N}-ts_{N}^{-1}\overline{\psi}_{N}|\mathcal{D}^{N}\}}{\Pi_{N}^{DLAN}\{\Theta_{N}|\mathcal{D}^{N}\}}-1\right|\geq e^{-c^{\prime}N\epsilon_{N}^{2}}\right)\to 0.

Intuitively, this new distribution
\Pi_{N}^{DLAN}(d\theta|\mathcal{D}^{N})
is a Bayes posterior naturally arising from a “linearized” statistical experiment, in which we observe
N
independent observations
Y_{i}=\mathbb{I}_{\theta_{0}}(\theta_{0})(X_{i})+\epsilon_{i}
with the underlying measure space associated with a
\sigma
-algebra generated by
(X_{i},\epsilon_{i})_{i=1}^{N}
.

We first argue that we can show this posterior has the same contraction rate upper bound in
L^{2}(\mathcal{Z})
-norm as
\Pi(\cdot\mid\mathcal{D}^{N})
, i.e., with some
b^{\prime}>0
:

P_{\theta_{0}}^{N}(\Pi_{N}^{DLAN}\{\Theta_{N}\mid\mathcal{D}^{N}\}\geq e^{-b^{\prime}N\epsilon_{N}^{2}})\to 0.

(D.8)

By the Lipschitz continuity estimate on
\mathbb{I}_{\theta_{0}}:L^{2}(\mathcal{Z})\to L^{2}(\mathcal{Z})
given by (
E.7
),
we know that this operator satisfies the same local Lipschitz estimate as
G
(
E.3
) (with possibly different constants),
and we also know that
\|\mathbb{I}_{\theta_{0}}(\theta)\|_{\infty}\leq\|\theta\|_{C_{1}}
is bounded whenever
\theta\in B_{\widetilde{H}^{s}}(M)
for some suitable
s
. As remarked in, up to multiplicative constants that may be chosen uniform over
\theta\in B_{\widetilde{H}}(M)
, such estimates are essentially the same as obtained for the nonlinear operator
G
.
Applying
[
35
, Theorems 2.2.2 and 2.3.1]
to
\Pi_{N}^{DLAN}
, then, we may conclude the posterior contraction (
D.8
) is indeed true, and that the sequence
\epsilon_{N}\to 0
can be chosen exactly the same as the original posterior
\Pi_{N}(\cdot\mid\mathcal{D}^{N})
in Assumption
3.3
.

Next, using Lemma
D.3
and plugging in the lower bounds for
s_{N}/\tau_{N}
from Lemma
5.5
,
we obtain the estimate, for each
t\in\mathbb{R}
and with our choice of
\tau_{N}
,

|t|s_{N}^{-1}\|\overline{\psi}_{N}\|_{L^{2}(\mathcal{Z})}\leq|t|\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}\epsilon_{N}}\epsilon_{N}^{(2\overline{\kappa}+1)\delta(2+q)-2\overline{\kappa}},

where
\delta(2+q)=\frac{(2+q)\alpha-1}{(2+q)\alpha+1}
by Lemma
D.1
.
The obtainable estimate of
\overline{\kappa}
is different depending on the three different regimes in Lemma
5.5
; however,
it is bounded above by 1/2, and the exponent
(2\overline{\kappa}+1)\delta(2+q)-2\overline{\kappa}
is decreasing in
\overline{\kappa}\in[0,1]
when
\delta(2+q)<1
. Therefore, an upper bound follows:

|t|s_{N}^{-1}\|\overline{\psi}_{N}\|_{L^{2}(\mathcal{Z})}\lesssim\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\epsilon_{N}^{2\{\delta(2+q)-1\}}.

Now we plug in the definition of
\epsilon_{N}=N^{-\frac{\alpha+1}{2\alpha+2+d}}
.
By the conditional stability estimate (
E.9
), we see that
\|\theta-\theta_{0}\|_{L^{2}(\mathcal{Z})}\lesssim\epsilon_{N}^{\lambda}
for
\lambda<\frac{\alpha-d/2-1}{\alpha-d/2+1}
, uniformly over the set
\Theta_{N}
given in (
4.1
). The above display is
o_{N}(\epsilon_{N}^{\lambda})
for every
\alpha\geq 1+d/2
,
d\geq 1
and
q\geq 0
,
since we obtain

\frac{1}{2}>\frac{\alpha+1}{2\alpha+2+d}\left\{\frac{\alpha-d/2-1}{\alpha-d/2+1}+\frac{4}{(2+q)\alpha+1}\right\}.

Similarly, again using Lemma
D.3
,
we see that there exists
s>1+d/2
for which we can obtain
s_{N}^{-1}\|\overline{\psi}_{N}\|_{H^{s}(\mathcal{Z})}=o_{N}(1)
.
Therefore, we conclude that (
D.8
) remains true if
\Theta_{N}
is replaced with the shifted set
\Theta_{N}-ts_{N}^{-1}\overline{\psi}_{N}
for each
t
.

Combining the above estimates and (
D.8
), we conclude that for each
t\in\mathbb{R}
, there exist
c_{1},c_{2}>0
such that the event

\left|\frac{\Pi_{N}^{DLAN}\{\Theta_{N}-ts_{N}^{-1}\overline{\psi}_{N}|\mathcal{D}^{N}\}}{\Pi_{N}^{DLAN}\{\Theta_{N}|\mathcal{D}^{N}\}}-1\right|\leq\frac{e^{-c_{2}N\epsilon_{N}^{2}}}{1-e^{-c_{1}N\epsilon_{N}^{2}}}

has
P_{\theta_{0}}^{N}
-probability convergent to 0.
Hence, condition (
3.6
) is verified.

Lemma D.3
.

For every
t>0
and
0\leq r<\alpha-d
:

\displaystyle ts_{N}^{-1}\|\overline{\psi}_{N}\|_{L^{2}(\mathcal{Z})}

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle t\tau_{N}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}\tau_{N}}\right)^{\delta(2+q)}\left(\frac{s_{N}}{\tau_{N}}\right)^{\delta(2+q)-1};

\displaystyle ts_{N}^{-1}\|\overline{\psi}_{N}\|_{C^{r}(\mathcal{Z})}

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle t\tau_{N}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}\tau_{N}}\right)^{\delta(2+q)(1-\frac{p}{2})}\left(\frac{s_{N}}{\tau_{N}}\right)^{\delta(2+q)(1-\frac{p}{2})-1},\;p>\frac{r}{\alpha}+\frac{d}{2\alpha}.

For the first statement, we proceed similarly as in proving (
5.21
) and (
5.22
) in the proof of Lemma
5.7
, to conclude

\|\overline{\psi}_{N}\|_{L^{2}(\mathcal{Z})}=\|f^{2}(\mathbb{K}_{\theta_{0},\Lambda}^{*}\mathbb{K}_{\theta_{0},\Lambda})\psi_{\lambda}\|_{\widetilde{H}^{1}}\leq\|\mathbb{K}_{\theta_{0},\Lambda}f^{2}(\mathbb{K}_{\theta_{0},\Lambda}^{*}\mathbb{K}_{\theta_{0},\Lambda})\psi_{\lambda}\|_{L^{2}(\mathcal{Z})}^{\delta(2+q)}

and therefore

ts_{N}^{-1}\|\overline{\psi}_{N}\|_{L^{2}(\mathcal{Z})}\lesssim t\tau_{N}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}\tau_{N}}\right)^{\delta(2+q)}\left(\frac{s_{N}}{\tau_{N}}\right)^{\delta(2+q)-1}.

For the second statement,
since the embedding from

,
we deduce that the prior places full measure on

Since we also know that

we conclude that

\|\overline{\psi}_{N}\|_{C^{r}}\lesssim\|\overline{\psi}_{N}\|_{\widetilde{H}^{p}}=\tau_{N}^{2}\|f^{2}(\mathbb{K}_{\theta_{0},\Lambda}^{*}\mathbb{K}_{\theta_{0},\Lambda})\psi_{\lambda}\|_{\widetilde{H}^{p-1}},

for any

, and

,
with

.
From hereon, proceeding similarly as in the proof of (
5.20
), (

) and (

we obtain

ts_{N}^{-1}\|\overline{\psi}_{N}\|_{C^{r}(\mathcal{Z})}\lesssim t\tau_{N}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}\tau_{N}}\right)^{\delta(2+q)(1-\frac{p}{2})}\left(\frac{s_{N}}{\tau_{N}}\right)^{\delta(2+q)(1-\frac{p}{2})-1}.

∎

As in the main text, we consider the elliptic PDE model given by (
4.1
),
with its conductivity parameter
a
belonging to the set
\Gamma_{\alpha,K_{min}}
(
4.2
), and
known source
f
and boundary condition

belonging to
C^{\infty}(\mathcal{Z})
.
We consider a smooth reparameterization
\phi:H_{0}^{\alpha}(\mathcal{Z})\to\Gamma_{\alpha,K_{min}}
, in the sense of Definition
4.1
.
The forward map
G(\theta)=u_{\phi\circ\theta}
uniquely defines the solution with
a=\phi\circ\theta
.
Below, we summarize some known results about the PDE that we use in our proofs.

Lemma E.1
.

Let
\theta\in H_{0}^{s}(\mathcal{Z}),\;s>1+d/2
. For each
M>0
, there exists a constant

C\equiv C(s,d,\mathcal{Z},K_{\min},f,u_{D},M)>0
such that

\sup_{\|\theta\|_{H^{s}(\mathcal{Z})}\leq M}\|G(\theta)\|_{H^{s+1}(\mathcal{Z})}\leq C<\infty.

(E.1)

See
[
35
, Proposition A.5.2]
for a proof and the explicit expression of the constant.

Lemma E.2
.

For
a=\phi(\theta)\in C^{1}(\mathcal{Z})
and
a\geq K_{min}
on the domain,

\|G(\theta)\|_{\infty}\leq c(\mathcal{Z},K_{min})\|f\|_{\infty}+\|u_{D}\|_{\infty}.

(E.2)

The proof can be derived by a Feynman-Kac representation of the solution
G(\theta)
;
see, e.g., the remarks leading up to display (A.27) in
[
35
]
.

Lemma E.3
.

For
\theta_{1},\theta_{2}\in B_{H_{0}^{s}(\mathcal{Z})}(M),\;s>1+d/2
,

\|G(\theta_{1})-G(\theta_{2})\|_{L^{2}(\mathcal{Z})}\lesssim\|\theta_{1}-\theta_{2}\|_{(H_{0}^{1})^{*}}

(E.3)

with a multiplicative constant uniform in
\theta\in B_{H_{0}^{s}(\mathcal{Z})}(M)
.
Furthermore,

\|G(\theta_{1})-G(\theta_{2})\|_{L^{2}(\mathcal{Z})}\leq c(\mathcal{Z},K_{min})\|\theta_{1}-\theta_{2}\|_{C^{1}(\mathcal{Z})}.

(E.4)

We can utilize the fact that two solutions
u_{\theta}
and
u_{\theta_{0}}
, corresponding respectively to elliptic PDE coefficients
\phi(\theta)
and
\phi(\theta_{0})
,
solve an inhomogeneous PDE

\begin{cases}(L_{\theta}u_{\theta}-L_{\theta_{0}}u_{\theta_{0}})=L_{\theta}(u_{\theta}-u_{\theta_{0}})\quad\text{on }\mathcal{Z};\\
0=u_{\theta}-u_{\theta_{0}}\quad\text{on }\partial\mathcal{Z}.\end{cases}

(E.5)

The second estimate then follows using the Feynman-Kac representation as in
E.2
.
For the proof of the first claim, see
[
35
, Section 2.1.1.3]
.
∎

For linearizing
G
, arguing as in the proof of
[
35
, Theorem 3.3.2]
, we can show that the following bounded operator for any
\theta\in H^{s}(\mathcal{Z})
,
mapping
h
from
H^{s}(\mathcal{Z}),\;s>1+\frac{d}{2}
to

\mathbb{I}_{\theta}(h)=-L_{\theta}^{-1}[\nabla\cdot\{(\phi^{\prime}\circ\theta)h\nabla u_{\theta}\}],

can be continuously extended to

and satisfies a quadratic approximation in

-norm, as shown in
[
35
, Theorem 3.3.2]
:

\|R_{\theta}(h)\|_{L^{2}(\mathcal{Z})}=O(\|h\|_{\infty}^{2}).

(E.6)

The adjoint of this operator
\mathbb{I}^{*}_{\theta}:L^{2}(\mathcal{Z})\to L^{2}(\mathcal{Z})
for
\theta\in H^{s}(\mathcal{Z}),\;s>1+\frac{d}{2}
is given by

\mathbb{I}_{\theta}^{*}(g)=(\phi^{\prime}\circ\theta)\nabla u_{\theta}\cdot\nabla L_{\theta}^{-1}[g].

In particular, the choice of link function
\phi(\theta)=e^{\theta}+K_{min}
yields
\phi^{\prime}(\theta)=e^{\theta}
,
even though this link function does
not
satisfy Definition 4.1.

Lemma E.4
.

Let
\theta_{1},\theta_{2}\in B_{H_{0}^{s}}(M),\;s>1+d/2
, and
\theta_{1}=\theta_{2}
on
\partial\mathcal{Z}
.
Then

\|\mathbb{I}_{\theta_{0}}(\theta_{1}-\theta_{2})\|_{L^{2}(\mathcal{Z})}\lesssim\|\nabla\cdot(\phi^{\prime}(\theta_{0})(\theta_{1}-\theta_{2})\nabla u_{\theta_{0}})\|_{(H_{0}^{2})^{*}}\lesssim\|\theta_{1}-\theta_{2}\|_{(H_{0}^{1})^{*}}

(E.7)

with a multiplicative constant uniform in
\theta\in B_{H_{s}^{0}}(M)
.

By Sobolev embedding,
\theta_{1},\;\theta_{2}\in B_{C^{1}}(M)
and they agree on the boundary
\partial\mathcal{X}
by the assumption. We then also know that
(H^{1})^{*}
-norm and the
(H_{0}^{1})^{*}
-norm are equivalent for
\theta_{1}-\theta_{2}
.
The Lipschitz estimate then follows a similar argument as in the initial estimates from the proof of
[
33
, Theorem 9]
:
see the arguments in pp. 390–391, displays (4.10), (4.11) and the Lipschitz estimate right below it.
∎

Lemma E.5
.

In the setting of
E.4
, we also have

\|\mathbb{I}_{\theta}(g-h)\|_{\infty}\lesssim\|g-h\|_{C^{1}}.

(E.8)

Since
\phi
realizes a bijection from
C^{1}(\mathcal{Z})
to an image contained in
C^{1}(\mathcal{Z})
, and

has

The Lipschitz estimate then follows using the definition of

derived above, with a multiplicative constant depending on the norms of

which may be made uniform in

∎

Comparison of (

)
and (

We illustrate our theory by considering an example similar to the one first studied by
[
32
]
, based on a two-dimensional Darcy flow.
Our emphasis is not on a practical demonstration of implementing the MCMC algorithm; rather, it is to consider an artificial but theoretically non-trivial example with a large but finite number

, to which the BvM results of
[
30
]
and
[

]
are inapplicable.
Consider the equation

-\nabla\cdot(a(\theta_{0})\nabla u(x,y))=f(x,y).

(F.1)

Let
\theta_{0}=0
and
a_{0}=e^{\theta_{0}}=1
. The resulting equation with Dirichlet boundary condition is an inhomogeneous Poisson equation

\begin{cases}-\Delta u(x,y)=f(x,y),\;&(x,y)\in(0,1)\times(0,1);\\
u(x,y)=0,\;&(x,y)\in[0,1]\times[0,1]\setminus(0,1)\times(0,1).\end{cases}

We choose
f=-2x(1-x)-2y(1-y)
. One can check that the unique solution is
u(x,y)=-xy(1-x)(1-y)
. Consider the following bump function defining a functional of inferential interest:

\psi(x,y)=\begin{cases}\exp\left\{-\left(\frac{1}{(8x-3)(5-8x)}+\frac{1}{(5y-1)(3-5y)}\right)\right\},\;&(x,y)\in\left(\frac{3}{8},\frac{5}{8}\right)\times\left(\frac{1}{5},\frac{3}{5}\right);\\
0&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(F.2)

The fact that
\theta_{0}
, an unknown parameter, is actually zero, and that
\psi\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\mathcal{Z})

However, an integral curve argument similar to the one found in
[
32
]
shows that because
\psi\notin R(\mathbb{I}_{\theta_{0}}^{*})
, a necessary condition for BvM is
not
met.
Therefore, despite the apparent triviality of the estimation problem, previous literature does not provide a positive statement about the posterior coverage of
\langle\psi,\theta_{0}\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathcal{Z})}
.
On the other hand, since
\theta_{0},\psi\in C^{\infty}_{0}(\mathcal{Z})
and
\theta_{0}=0

(

),

We thus expect that for reasonable Gaussian prior specifications, the Bayes credible interval covers

For the numerical study, we first generated discrete observations

over a

.

.
The tuning parameter
