Polymatroids are to finite groups as matroids are to finite fields
Ed Swartz

Abstract
Given a subgroup ℋ \mathcal{H} of a product of finite groups 𝒢 = ∏ i = 1 n Γ i \mathcal{G}=\displaystyle\prod^{n}_{i=1}\Gamma_{i} and b > 1 , b>1, we define a polymatroid P ⁡ ( ℋ , b ) . P(\mathcal{H},b). If all of the Γ i \Gamma_{i} are isomorphic to ℤ / p ​ ℤ , p \mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z},~p a prime, and b = p , b=p, then P ⁡ ( ℋ , b ) P(\mathcal{H},b) is the usual matroid associated to any ℤ / p ​ ℤ \mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z} -matrix whose row space equals ℋ . \mathcal{H}. In general, there are many ways in which the relationship between P ⁡ ( ℋ , b ) P(\mathcal{H},b) and ℋ \mathcal{H} mirrors that of the relationship between a matroid and a subspace of a finite vector space. These include representability by excluded minors, the Crapo-Rota critical theorem, the existence of a concrete algebraic object representing the polymatroid dual of P ⁡ ( ℋ , b ) , P(\mathcal{H},b), analogs of Greene’s theorem and the MacWilliams identities when ℋ \mathcal{H} is a group code over a nonabelian group, and a connection to the combinatorial Laplacian of a quotient space determined by 𝒢 \mathcal{G} and ℋ . \mathcal{H}. We use the group Crapo-Rota critical theorem to demonstrate an extension to hypergraphs of the classical duality between proper colorings and nowhere-zero flows on graphs.

Introduction

It has been almost ninety years since Whitney began the theory of matroids
[
45
]
. Since then, the reach and interest in matroids has expanded so dramatically that it is no longer possible to give a reasonably sized review of the literature involved. A significant portion of that literature involves linear algebra over finite fields. One of the most common settings for matroids starts with a matrix
A
with coefficients in a finite field
{\mathbb{F}}.
Let
E
be the columns of
A.
Then
E
is the ground set for a matroid
M[A]
. The rank function,
r_{A},
of

assigns to every subset

of the columns of
A,
the dimension of the span of the columns in
S
in the column space of
A.
An alternative approach is to start with a subspace
W
of the
{\mathbb{F}}
-vector space
{\mathbb{F}}^{E}.
Then choose a matrix
A
whose row space equals
W
and set
M[W]
to be the matroid

Since matrices with the same row space have the same column dependence relations,
M[W]
is well defined. It was this point of view, applied to elements of the complex Grassmannian and its moment map, that led Gelfand, Goresky, MacPherson, and Serganova to rediscover Edmonds’ matroid polytopes
[
17
]
.

Matroid polytopes are a special case of polymatroid polytopes. The latter were introduced by Edmonds over fifty years ago as a polyhedral generalization of the matroid greedy algorithm, which itself was a generalization of the greedy algorithm for finding maximal weight spanning trees in a graph
[
13
]
. Since Postnikov’s influential paper
[
35
]
, polymatroids have also been known as ‘generalized permutahedra’. Investigations from that perspective tend to emphasize the polyhedral and algebraic geometry of Edmonds’ original polytopal presentation. We do not examine any connection between this approach and ours. However, it seems reasonable to hope, indeed expect, that the two lines of research will eventually intersect.

Our main objective is to demonstrate that the relationship between subspaces
W
of a finite vector space
{\mathbb{F}}^{E}
and their associated matroids
M[W],
is similar to a previously unrecognized relationship between polymatroids and subgroups of a product of finite groups. Given such a

let
A
be a matrix whose row space equals
W.
For
S\subseteq E
let
\pi_{S}(W)
be the projection of
W
to
{\mathbb{F}}^{S}.
Since
{\mathbb{F}}
is finite,

equals
\log_{|{\mathbb{F}}|}|\pi_{S}(W)|.
We can think of
r_{A}
as a CT-scan of
W.
Just as a CT-scan measures how much mass interferes with x-rays from a variety of projections,

records how much of
W
lies in a variety of projections.

Let us replace
{\mathbb{F}}^{E}
with a product of finite groups
\mathcal{G}=\displaystyle\prod_{x\in E}\Gamma_{x},
and replace

with a subgroup

of
\mathcal{G}.
Fix a real number

Now our figurative CT-scan of

consists of the set of logarithms base

of the cardinality of the projections of

to

This collection of data forms a polymatroid (Theorem

An algebraic characterization of subgroups of a product of two groups goes back to Goursat’s Lemma
[
19
]
. Bauer, Sen and Zvengrowski established algebraic criteria for subgroups of an arbitrary finite product of groups
[
4
]
. Our purpose here is very different.
Most of the paper is devoted to showing several ways in which

interacts with

We do this for several areas where matroids and finite fields meet. The last two are new even for matroids.

2
Matroids and polymatroids

In this section we introduce several basic matroid and polymatroid notions. Other definitions and notation for matroids, polymatroids and other subjects will appear later as they are needed.

A
polymatroid
is a pair,
P=(E,r),
where
E
is a finite ground set and
r
is a rank function
r:2^{E}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
such that

P1.

r(\emptyset)=0.
(normalization)

P2.

If
S\subseteq T,
then
r(S)\leq r(T).
(monotonicity)

P3.

For all
S,T\subseteq E,~r(S)+r(T)\geq r(S\cup T)+r(S\cap T).
(submodularity)

A cryptomorphic definition is obtained by replacing P3 with the axiom of
diminishing returns
:

P3’.

For all
S\subseteq T
and
x\in E,

r(S\cup\{x\})-r(S)\geq r(T\cup\{x\})-r(T).

Two polymatroids
P=(E,r)
and
P^{\prime}=(E^{\prime},r^{\prime})
are
isomorphic
, written
P\cong P^{\prime},
if there is a rank-preserving bijection from
E
to
E^{\prime}.

Throughout the paper
P=(E,r)
is a polymatroid,
n=|E|
, and
[n]=\{1,\dots,n\}.
Often
P
(and
E
and
r
) will be decorated in various ways in order to distinguish explicitly which polymatroid is under consideration.

A polymatroid is
subcardinal
if for all
S\subseteq E,r(S)\leq|S|.
A subcardinal polymatroid which is integer valued is a
matroid
. From here on
M
will be a matroid, frequently decorated to specify which matroid. Matroids were first defined by Whitney
[
45
]
. For a more complete early history of matroids see
[
25
]
. This includes a frequently forgotten contemporaneous equivalent definition by Nakasawa
[
29
]
,
[
30
]
,
[
31
]
. Polymatroids were first introduced by Edmonds as a polytopal generalization of the greedy algorithm for matroids
[
13
]
.

The prototypical example of a matroid starts with an
m\times n
matrix
A
with coefficients in a field
{\mathbb{F}}.
The elements of the ground set
E
are the columns of
A
in
{\mathbb{F}}^{m}.
Distinct columns of
A
are distinct elements of
E,
even if as column vectors in
{\mathbb{F}}^{m}
they are identical. The rank
r_{A}(S)
of a subset
S
of
E
is the dimension of the span of the column vectors in
S.
A matroid is
representable over
{\mathbb{F}}
if it is isomorphic to
(E,r_{A})
for some
A.
In that case
A
is a
realization
of the matroid. Usually we use
M[A]
to denote
(E,r_{A}).

An alternative, but equivalent, approach to matroid representability is through subspaces. Suppose
W
is a subspace of
{\mathbb{F}}^{n}.
If
A
and
B
are two matrices whose row spaces
R(A)
and
R(B)
are equal to
W,
then
M[A]
is isomorphic to
M[B]
. Indeed,
A
and
B
are connected through a series of elementary row operations (where inserting or removing a row of zeros is included as an elementary row operation) and row operations do not alter column independence relations. In this situation, we will use
M[W]
to stand for this isomorphism class of matroids. As with matrices, if
M
is a matroid and
M
is isomorphic to
M[W],
then
M
is representable over
{\mathbb{F}},
and
W
is a realization of
M.

Another motivating example for matroids comes from graphs. Given a graph
G,
let
E(G)
be the edge set of
G.
The rank of a subset
S
of edges of
G
is assigned the rank
r_{G}(S)
equal to the cardinality of the largest acyclic subset of edges in
S.
Then
(E(G),r_{G})
is the
cycle matroid of
G
which we denote by
M[G].
In fact,
M[G]
is equal to
M[A]
where
A
is the following adjacency matrix. First choose an orientation of the edges of
G.
The columns of
A
are indexed by edges of
G
and the rows are indexed by the vertices of
G.
The entry of
A
corresponding to an edge
x
and vertex
v
is:
1,
if
v
is the head of
x,~-1
if
v
is the tail of
x,
and
0
if
x
and
v
are not incident or
x
is a loop. Then
A
is a realization of
M[G]
over every field.

A (usually not subcardinal) polymatroid whose rank function is integer-valued is often referred to as a
discrete
or
integer
polymatroid. Some authors define polymatroids to be integer valued. Another common nomenclature is
k
-polymatroids
for discrete polymatroids such that
k\geq\max_{x\in E}r(\{x\}).
One way to produce integer polymatroids is to start with a matroid
M=(E,r)
and let
\{T_{1},\dots,T_{n}\}
be a multiset of subsets of
E.
For
S\subseteq[n]
define
r^{\prime}(S)
to be the rank in
M
of
\displaystyle\cup_{i\in S}T_{i}.
Then
P=([n],r^{\prime})
is a polymatroid. In fact, all integer polymatroids are isomorphic to such a construction

[
34
, Theorem 11.1.9]
.
In this case,
P
is constructed from

M
and
\{T_{1},\dots,T_{n}\}.
When there is no cause for confusion, the reference to
\{T_{1},\dots,T_{n}\}
is omitted.
An integer polymatroid
P
is
representable
over a field
{\mathbb{F}}
if there exists a matrix
A
with coefficients in
{\mathbb{F}}
such that
P
is constructed from
M[A].
In this case
A
(and
\{T_{1},\dots,T_{n}\}
) is a
realization
of
P.
In a fashion similar to matroids, representability of polymatroids over a field
{\mathbb{F}}
can be approached using a subspace
W
of
{\mathbb{F}}^{m},
where
m=\displaystyle\sum^{n}_{i=1}r(\{i\}),
and choosing a basis of
W
to be the rows of
A.

As for matroids, a
flat
of a polymatroid
P
is a subset
S
of
E
such that for all
x
not in
S,~r(S\cup\{x\})>r(S).
The poset of the flats of a polymatroid ordered by inclusion forms a lattice, called the
lattice of flats of
P
, with meet being intersection. In fact, any finite lattice is the lattice of flats of a polymatroid. One can even insist that the polymatroid be one constructed from
M[K_{n}],
where
K_{n}
is the complete graph on
n
vertices. See
[
36
]
for a description and history of this result. We will use
\mathcal{L}_{P}
to represent the lattice of flats of the polymatroid
P.

There is a polymatroid analog of the characteristic polynomial of a matroid. However, unless the polymatroid is discrete, it is generally not a polynomial. Despite this, we will still call it the
characteristic polynomial
of a polymatroid. It is defined completely analogously to the definition of the characteristic polynomial of a matroid.

{\chi}_{P}(t)=\displaystyle\sum_{S\subseteq E}(-1)^{|S|}t^{r(E)-r(S)}.

The characteristic polynomial of a polymatroid
P
has many properties in common with its matroid counterpart. For instance, the characteristic polynomial of
P
is the zero function if
P
has an element
x\in E
such that
r(\{x\})=0.
Such an element is called a
loop
. There is also a formula for
{\chi}_{P}
based on the Möbius function
\mu_{P}
of
\mathcal{L}_{P}
that is identical to the usual matroid definition.

Proposition 2.1

If
P
has a loop, then
{\chi}_{P}\equiv 0.
If
P
does not contain a loop, then

{\chi}_{P}(t)=\displaystyle\sum_{S\in\mathcal{L}_{P}}\mu_{\mathcal{L}_{P}}(\emptyset,S)t^{r(E)-r(S)}.

Proof:

The same proof as for matroids works here. See, for instance,
[
50
, Proposition 7.2.1]
.

\square

3
From finite groups to polymatroids

The notation in this paragraph will be used in the remainder of the paper. For all
x\in E,~\Gamma_{x}
is a finite group. In general,
\Gamma_{x}
may or may not be isomorphic to
\Gamma_{y}.
In addition,
\mathcal{G}=\displaystyle\prod_{x\in E}\Gamma_{x},
and
\mathcal{H}
is a subgroup of
\mathcal{G}.
For
S\subseteq E,~\mathcal{G}_{S}=\displaystyle\prod_{x\in S}\Gamma_{x}
and
\pi_{S}
is the projection map
\pi_{S}:\mathcal{G}\to\mathcal{G}_{S}.
We denote the image
\pi_{S}(\mathcal{H})
by
\mathcal{H}_{S}.
By convention,
\mathcal{G}_{\emptyset}
and
\mathcal{H}_{\emptyset}
are the trivial groups.

The connection between a subgroup
\mathcal{H}\leq\mathcal{G}
and polymatroids is through a rank function. Choose
b>1,b\in\mathbb{R}.
For all
S\subseteq E
define

r_{\mathcal{H},b}(S)\equiv\log_{b}|\mathcal{H}_{S}|.

(1)

Different choices of
b
lead to rank functions which differ by a constant. Indeed,

r_{\mathcal{H},b}=\frac{\ln b^{\prime}}{\ln b}r_{\mathcal{H},b^{\prime}}.

Theorem 3.1

The pair
(E,r_{\mathcal{H},b})
is a polymatroid.

Proof:

Normality and monotonicity are evident, so let us check the law of diminishing returns for
S\subseteq T\subseteq E.
If
x\in T
then, (3’) is automatically satisfied, so assume that
x\notin T.
In general, for any
S\subseteq E,

r_{\mathcal{H},b}(S\cup\{x\})-r_{\mathcal{H},b}(S)=\log_{b}\frac{|\mathcal{H}_{S\cup\{x\}}|}{|\mathcal{H}_{S}|}=\log_{b}|\ker\pi_{S}:\mathcal{H}_{S\cup\{x\}}\to\mathcal{H}_{S}|.

(2)

Let
\mathcal{K}_{S}
be
\ker\pi_{S}:\mathcal{H}_{S\cup\{x\}}\to\mathcal{H}_{S}
and
\mathcal{K}_{T}
be
\ker\pi_{T}:\mathcal{H}_{T\cup\{x\}}\to\mathcal{H}_{T}.
Since
S\subseteq T,~\pi_{S\cup\{x\}}:\mathcal{G}_{T\cup\{x\}}\to\mathcal{G}_{S\cup\{x\}}
becomes an injection
\pi_{S\cup\{x\}}:\mathcal{K}_{T}\to\mathcal{K}_{S}
when restricted to
\mathcal{K}_{T}.
Hence
|\mathcal{K}_{T}|\leq|\mathcal{K}_{S}|
as required.

\square

We will use
P(\mathcal{H},b)
to denote the polymatroid
(E,r_{\mathcal{H},b}).
Most of the time
b
is understood or not relevant, so we suppress it and use
P(\mathcal{H}).

Example 3.2

Let
E=[n]
and for all
1\leq i\leq n,~\Gamma_{i}=\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z},~p
a fixed prime, and
\mathcal{G}=(\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z})^{n}.
A subgroup
\mathcal{H}
of
\mathcal{G}
is a also a subspace of the vector space
\mathcal{G}
over
{\mathbb{F}}=\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}.
Then, with
b=p,~P(\mathcal{H})
is a matroid and is isomorphic to
M[\mathcal{H}].

In principle, the definition of
r_{\mathcal{H},b}
makes sense for any collection of sets
\{\Lambda_{x}\}
indexed by
E
and finite subset
\mathcal{H}
of
\prod_{x\in E}{\Lambda}_{x}.
It is not clear what sort of structure is needed on the
\mathcal{H}
and the
\Lambda_{x}
to guarantee that
r_{\mathcal{H},b}
is submodular. What is clear is that some structure is needed.

Example 3.3

Let
\Lambda_{1}=\Lambda_{2}=\Lambda_{3}=\{1,2\},E=\{1,2,3\},
and
L\subseteq\Lambda_{1}\times\Lambda_{2}\times\Lambda_{3}
be

L=\{(2,2,2),(2,1,2),(1,1,2),(1,1,1),(2,1,1)\}.

Then
|L_{\{1,2\}}|\cdot|L_{\{2,3\}}|=3\cdot 3<|L_{\{1,2,3\}}|\cdot|L_{\{2\}}|=5\cdot 2.
Hence
r_{L,b}
is not submodular for all
b.

4
Representability

Throughout Sections
4
,
5
and
6
we will be assuming that all of the
\Gamma_{x}
are isomorphic to a fixed finite group
\Gamma
and that
b=|\Gamma|.
Consequently we will suppress all appearances of
b
from the notation. In this section we introduce the definition of a polymatroid being representable over a finite group that is analogous to matroid representability over a field.

Definition 4.1

A polymatroid
P=(E,r)
is
representable over a finite group
\Gamma
if there exists a finite set
E^{\prime}
and subgroup
\mathcal{H}
of
\Gamma^{E^{\prime}}
such that
P
is isomorphic to
P(\mathcal{H}).
In this case,
\mathcal{H}
is a
\Gamma
-realization
of
P.

If
\Gamma=\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z},~p
a prime, then Example
3.2
shows that a polymatroid
P
is representable over the group
\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}
if and only if it is representable over the field
\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}.
In both cases,
P
must actually be a matroid. Another way that polymatroid realizability over a group mirrors matroid realizability over a field is that it is closed under deletion, contraction and hence minors.

Given
S\subseteq E
the
deletion
of
S
from
P
is the polymatroid
P\setminus S
with ground set
E-S
and rank function equal to
r
restricted to the subsets of
E-S.
If
\mathcal{H}
is a
\Gamma
-realization of
P,
then
\mathcal{H}_{E-S}
is a
\Gamma
-realization of
P\setminus S.

Another polymatroid with ground set
E-S
is
P/S,
the
contraction
of
P
by
S.
For
T\subseteq E-S,
the rank function of
P/S
is

r_{P/S}(T)=r(S\cup T)-r(S).

If
\mathcal{H}
is a realization of
P
, then
\mathcal{K}=\pi_{E-S}(\ker\pi_{S})
is a realization of
P/S.
To see this, let
T\subseteq E-S.
Then
\pi_{T}
is an isomorphism between
\mathcal{K}_{T}
and
\ker\pi_{S}:\mathcal{H}_{S\cup T}\to\mathcal{H}_{S}.
Since
\pi_{S}
is a surjection,

|\mathcal{K}_{T}|=|\ker\pi_{S}:\mathcal{H}_{S\cup T}\to\mathcal{H}_{S}|=\frac{|\mathcal{H}_{S\cup T}|}{|\mathcal{H}_{S}|}.

Minors of polymatroids are defined as they are for matroids. A
minor
of a polymatroid
P
is a polymatroid
Q
that can be obtained from
P
by a sequence of deletions and/or contractions. Let
\mathcal{C}(\Gamma)
be the isomorphism classes of polymatroids representable over
\Gamma.
The above discussion shows that
\mathcal{C}(\Gamma)
is closed under minors. Let
\mathcal{C}
be a class of polymatroids. We say
\mathcal{C}
is
minor closed
if
\mathcal{C}
is closed under isomorphism and minors. An
excluded minor
of
\mathcal{C}
is a polymatroid
P
such that
P\notin\mathcal{C},
but every deletion and contraction of
P
is in
\mathcal{C}.
Minor closed classes of polymatroids can be described by listing their excluded minors. Before discussing excluded minors for
\mathcal{C}(\Gamma)
we consider excluded minors for
matroids
representable over
\Gamma.

In this section we restrict our attention to representability of matroids over a finite group

We denote the class of matroids representable over

For instance, the polymatroid

Two distinct elements

Theorem 5.1

Proof:

One of the cornerstones of enumerative matroid theory is Crapo and Rota’s critical theorem. The classical Crapo-Rota critical theorem is usually given in a coordinate-free form. Let

(3)

Duality for polymatroids realized over a group

One of the motivations for Whitney’s original introduction of matroids
[
45
]
was to answer the question, “What is the dual of a nonplanar graph?” He offered two solutions. One, let
M[G]
be the cycle matroid of the graph
G
. Then
M[G]^{\ast}
serves as the dual of
G.
The primary justification for this point of view is that for embedded planar graphs,
M[G]^{\ast}
is isomorphic to
M[G^{\ast}],
where
G^{\ast}
is the geometric planar dual of
G.

In order to describe Whitney’s second answer, choose an orientation for
G
and let
A
be the adjacency matrix for
G,
the columns labelled by the edges and the rows by the vertices. Now
M[A]
is isomorphic to
M[G].
Then
R(A)^{\perp},
the orthogonal complement of the row space of
A,
serves as a dual for
G.
This point of view is validated by the fact that
M[R(A)^{\perp}]
is isomorphic to
M[A]^{\ast}
which itself is isomorphic to
M[G]^{\ast}.

In this section we will closely mirror these ideas in the search for a dual of
\mathcal{H}\leq\mathcal{G}.
Throughout this section
\mathcal{G}=\displaystyle\prod_{x\in E}\Gamma_{x},
where each
\Gamma_{x}
is a finite group (not necessarily equal), and
\mathcal{H}
is a subgroup of
\mathcal{G}.
As before, we fix
b>1.
In addition, we set
{\bf{a}}\in(\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0})^{E},~{\bf{a}}_{x}=\log_{b}|\Gamma_{x}|.
We will be comparing our constructions with the special situation when for all

a fixed prime. In this case,
P(\mathcal{H})
is the matroid previously denoted by
M[\mathcal{H}],
where
\mathcal{H}
is a subspace of the vector space
(\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z})^{E}.

We have already seen the analog of Whitney’s first solution. The polymatroid

is a polymatroid dual of

When

the usual matroid dual of

Our analog of Whitney’s second solution to “What is the dual of a nonplanar graph” involves the (complex) representation theory of finite groups.

We will use the following notation to describe a candidate for a representation-based dual to

For a finite set

-vector space with basis

Specifically, if

Group codes and the Tutte polynomial

In this section we give a introduction to group codes and the Tutte polynomial of a polymatroid. We also give a glimpse at how
{\mathcal{R}}(\mathcal{H})
acts as a dual code for a group code
\mathcal{H}
through the MacWilliams identity for the weight enumerator of a code. For a close look at how
{\mathcal{R}}(\mathcal{H})
gives one possible answer for Dougherty, Kim and Sol
e
’s open problem, “Is there a duality and MacWilliams formula for codes over non-Abelian group?”
[
9
, Open Question 4.3]
, which includes MacWiliams identity for the complete weight enumerator, see
[
42
]
. Throughout this section
\mathcal{G}=\Gamma^{n}
where
\Gamma
is a finite group.

A
code of length
n
consists of a finite alphabet
\mathcal{A}
and a subset
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathcal{A}^{n}.
The elements of
\mathcal{C}
are called
code words
.
When
\mathcal{A}
is a finite field
{\mathbb{F}}
and
\mathcal{C}
a subspace of the
{\mathbb{F}}
-vector space
{\mathbb{F}}^{n}
, the code is called an
{\mathbb{F}}
-linear code.

One of the most important invariants of an
{\mathbb{F}}
-linear code is its weight enumerator.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be an
{\mathbb{F}}
-linear code. Given
c\in\mathcal{C}
the
weight
of
c
is
w(c)=|\{i:c_{i}\neq 0\}|.
The
weight enumerator
of
\mathcal{C}
is

W_{\mathcal{C}}(t)=\displaystyle\sum_{c\in\mathcal{C}}t^{w(c)}.

The Crapo-Rota critical theorem makes it clear that for an
{\mathbb{F}}
-linear code
\mathcal{C},
its matroid
M[\mathcal{C}]
determines
W_{\mathcal{C}}.
This was made precise by Greene via the Tutte polynomial of
M[\mathcal{C}]

[
20
]
.

The
Tutte polynomial
of a matroid
M
is a two-variable integer polynomial invariant
T_{M}(u,v),
defined by

T_{M}(u,v)=\displaystyle\sum_{S\subseteq E}(u-1)^{r(E)-r(S)}(v-1)^{|S|-r(S)}.

The applications of the Tutte polynomial are far too numerous to cover adequately here. For a sampling of applications, history and theory of the Tutte polynomial see
[
15
]
. An alternative definition of the Tutte polynomial is through deletion/contraction and induction.

Proposition 10.1

•

If
M
is the empty matroid, then
T_{M}(u,v)=1.

•

If
x
is a coloop of
M,
then
T_{M}(u,v)=u~T_{M\setminus x}(u,v).

•

If
x
is a loop of
M,
then
T_{M}(u,v)=v~T_{M/x}(u,v).

•

If
x
is neither a loop nor a coloop, then
T_{M}(u,v)=T_{M\setminus x}(u,v)+T_{M/x}(u,v).

The proof of this proposition is to split the sum over all subsets
S
of
E
into those which do not contain
x
and those which do contain
x.
Then first sum can be written in terms of
T_{M\setminus x}(u,v)
and the latter can be written in terms of
T_{M/x}(u,v).
The exact same idea allows one to sum up the last three cases into a single equation which can be applied to any

T_{M}(u,v)=(u-1)^{r(E)-r(E-x)}T_{M\setminus x}(u,v)+(v-1)^{1-r(\{x\})}T_{M/x}(u,v).

(10)

We will use this recursive formula for the Tutte polynomial in the proof of Theorem
10.3
below.

Theorem 10.2

(Greene’s theorem)
[
20
]

Let
\mathcal{C}
be an
{\mathbb{F}}
-linear code,
q=|{\mathbb{F}}|,
and
d=\dim_{\mathbb{F}}\mathcal{C}.
Then

W_{\mathcal{C}}(t)=(1-t)^{d}~t^{n-d}~T_{M[\mathcal{C}]}\bigg(\frac{1+(q-1)t}{1-t},\frac{1}{t}\bigg).

There have been several suggested extensions of the Tutte polynomial to integer polymatroids
[
2
]
,
[
7
]
,
[

]
. For subcardinal polymatroids, the simplest way to extend the Tutte polynomial is to use exactly the same definition. Of course, if

will not be a polynomial. In addition, it is not clear how to define

when

or

Nonetheless, Greene’s theorem holds without change for group codes.

A
\Gamma
-code
of length

\Gamma
and whose set of codewords is a subgroup
\mathcal{H}
of
\mathcal{G}=\Gamma^{n}.
The

The

of a
\Gamma

is

When

and

is a subspace of the vector space

then this definition of the weight of a codeword and of the weight enumerator of the code are the same as for

Theorem 10.3

(Greene’s theorem for group codes)
Let
\Gamma
be a finite group,

and
\mathcal{H}

\Gamma
-code of length

In addition, set

to be the polymatroid

Then,

(11)

The first proof of this theorem was by the third author in
[

]
. That proof involved interpreting

or

such a proof seems highly appropriate. Here we give a deletion/contraction proof using (

Proof:

The induction starts with

where the definitions show that both sides of (

|\mathcal{H}|-1.
For notational convenience we suppress all references to
\mathcal{H}
when discussing

and

We start by determining a deletion/contraction formula for

Let

and

If

then
w(h)=w(\pi_{E-\{x\}}(h)).
Otherwise,

Since

is surjective, the cardinality of the preimage of every

is

\frac{|\mathcal{H}|}{|\mathcal{H}_{E-\{x\}}|}=|\Gamma|^{r(E)-r(E-\{x\})}=q^{(r(E)-r(E-\{x\})}.

Now,
K=\{h\in\mathcal{H}:h_{x}=1_{\Gamma}\}
represents the contraction
P/x.
For every

We conclude that

W_{\mathcal{H}}(t)=q^{r_{\mathcal{H}}(E)-r_{\mathcal{H}}(E-\{x\})}\cdot t\cdot W_{\mathcal{H}_{E-\{x\}}}(t)+(1-t)W_{\mathcal{H}/x}(t).

(12)

The induction hypothesis, some algebra, and (

) imply,

W_{\mathcal{H}}(t)=q^{r_{\mathcal{H}}(E)-r_{\mathcal{H}}(E-\{x\})}\cdot t\cdot t^{n-1-r(E-\{x\})}\cdot(1-t)^{r(E-\{x\})}T_{P(\mathcal{H})\setminus x}\bigg(\frac{1+(q-1)t}{1-t},\frac{1}{t}\bigg)

+

\bigg(1-t\bigg)\bigg(t^{n-1-(r(E)-r(\{x\})}(1-t)^{r(E)-r(\{x\})}\bigg)T_{P(\mathcal{H})/x}\bigg(\frac{1+(q-1)t}{1-t},\frac{1}{t}\bigg)

=t^{n-r(E)}(1-t)^{r(E)}\bigg[(\frac{qt}{1-t})^{r(E)-r(E-\{x\})}T_{P(\mathcal{H})\setminus x}\bigg(\frac{1+(q-1)t}{1-t},\frac{1}{t}\bigg)\bigg]

+

t^{n-r(E)}(1-t)^{r(E)}\bigg[(\frac{1-t}{t})^{1-r(\{x\})}T_{P(\mathcal{H})/x}\bigg(\frac{1+(q-1)t}{1-t},\frac{1}{t}\bigg)\bigg]

=t^{n-r(E)}(1-t)^{r(E)}T_{P(\mathcal{H})}\bigg(\frac{1+(q-1)t}{1-t},\frac{1}{t}\bigg)

\square

11
Combinatorial Laplacian

In this last section we examine the connection between
{\mathcal{R}}(\mathcal{H})
and the combinatorial Laplacian. Throughout this section
E=[n].
If a polymatroid
(E,r_{\mathcal{H}})
has no elements
x
such that
r_{\mathcal{H}}(E)>r_{\mathcal{H}}(E-x),
then
{\mathcal{R}}(\mathcal{H})
has a concrete realization as the eigenspaces of the combinatorial Laplacian of an associated quotient space described below. This will lead us to relate the eigenvalues of the combinatorial Laplacian of the top dimension of these quotient spaces to the code weight enumerator of
\mathcal{H}
viewed as a code with alphabet
\Gamma.
This result was originally proved for binary matroids by the third author, and provides another example of how
{\mathcal{R}}(\mathcal{H})
behaves like Whitney’s subspace realization of the dual of a nonplanar graph
[
49
]
. We finish the section by using the combinatorial Laplacian to establish a topological interpretation of the Crapo-Rota critical theorem for finite groups.

The combinatorial Laplacian can be defined for any (locally finite) regular CW complex. We will work with regular CW complexes all of whose
closed
cells are simplices. Since Stanley’s paper,
[
38
]
, these types of complexes are usually called
simplicial posets
in the combinatorics literature. The name is derived from the fact that in the face poset of the complex every lower interval is isomorphic to the face poset of a simplex. Other names for simplicial posets include Boolean cell complexes and semi-simplicial complexes. Independent of the name, the reader will not go wrong in thinking of them as simplicial complexes where there may be several faces with the same vertex set. A bigon, two vertices with two parallel edges, is the smallest possible example of a simplicial poset which is not a simplicial complex.

Let
X
be a finite simplicial poset. We use
C_{j}(X)
for the simplicial
j
-chains of
X
with complex coefficients. Equivalently, we denote the
j
-dimensional faces of
X
by
F_{j}(X)
and
C_{j}(X)=\mathbb{C}[F_{j}(X)].
When a specific identification
u\leftrightarrow u^{\ast}
between
C_{j}(X)
and its dual
C_{j}(X)^{\ast}
is needed, we use the one implied by declaring the preferred basis
\{w_{\sigma}:\sigma\in F_{j}(X)\}
an orthonormal basis of
C_{j}(X).
As usual,
\partial_{j}(X):C_{j}(X)\to C_{j-1}(X)
denotes the boundary map. The empty set is a face of dimension minus one, so
C_{-1}(X)=\mathbb{C}[\emptyset]
and
C_{j}=\{0\}
for all
j<-1
and
j>\dim X.
For all
j
we define

\Delta_{j}(X):C_{j}(X)\to C_{j}(X)
by

\Delta_{j}(X)=\partial^{t}_{j}(X)\circ\partial_{j}(X)+\partial_{j+1}(X)\circ\partial^{t}_{j+1}(X),

where
t
indicates the transpose.

The
\Delta_{j}(X)
are collectively known as the
combinatorial Laplacian
of
X.
The combinatorial Laplacian has several properties. Among them:

•

Each
\Delta_{j}(X)
is diagonalizable with nonnegative real eigenvalues.

•

\ker\Delta_{j}(X)\cong H^{j}(X;\mathbb{C})\cong H_{j}(X;\mathbb{C}).
(Hodge theorem)

•

It is possible to approximate the usual Laplacian on a compact Riemannian manifold by using the combinatorial Laplacian on an appropriate sequence of triangulations of the maniold
[
10
]
.

Several authors have studied situations in which the eigenvalues of the combinatorial Laplacian are integers. These include, chessboard complexes
[
16
]
, matroid independence complexes
[
24
]
, matching complexes
[
12
]
, shifted complexes
[
11
]
. Representation theory has figured in some of these results (
[
12
]
,
[
16
]
).

In order to describe the quotient spaces associated to
\mathcal{H}\leq\mathcal{G}=\displaystyle\sum^{n}_{i=1}\Gamma_{i},
we begin by viewing every

as a zero-dimensional simplicial complex with
|\Gamma_{i}|

The facets of

In particular

When

r_{\mathcal{H}}(E-\{x\})=r_{\mathcal{H}}(E).

In contrast to matroids represented over a field,
r_{\mathcal{H}}(E-\{x\})<r_{\mathcal{H}}(E)
does not imply that
r_{\mathcal{H}}(E)=r_{\mathcal{H}}(E-\{x\})+r_{\mathcal{H}}(\{x\}).
For instance, if
\alpha:\Gamma\to\Gamma
is any nontrivial homomorphism which is not an automorphism, then
\mathcal{H}=\{(\gamma,\alpha(\gamma)):\gamma\in\Gamma\}\subseteq\Gamma^{2}
has
0<r_{\mathcal{H}}(\{2\})<r_{\mathcal{H}}(\{1,2\}),
but
r_{\mathcal{H}}(\{1,2\})=r_{\mathcal{H}}(\{1\}),
so
r_{H}(\{1,2\})<r_{\mathcal{H}}(\{1\})+r_{\mathcal{H}}(\{2\}).

In order to get a more detailed understanding of the the combinatorial Laplacian on
X/\mathcal{H}
along the lines of Theorem

we first write down the eigenvalues and eigenspaces of

in terms of the irreducible representations of
\Gamma

\mathcal{G}.
Identify
\mathbb{C}[\mathcal{G}]

using their usual isomorphisms with

Proposition 11.6

Let
W

\mathcal{G}
-invariant subspace of
\mathbb{C}[\mathcal{G}]
equivalent to an irreducible representation
\rho=\rho_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes\rho_{n}
of

all of which have eigenvalue equal to
\displaystyle\sum_{x\in\mbox{triv}\,(\rho)}|\Gamma_{x}|.
Since

is the direct sum of such

, this describes all of the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of

Proof:

First consider
n=1.
As in the proof of Theorem
11.3
,
\Delta_{0}(X)
is a
|\Gamma|\times|\Gamma|
matrix all of whose entries are
1.
So it has two eigenvalues,
|\Gamma|
and
0.
The
|\Gamma|
-eigenspace is one-dimensional and has eigenvector
\displaystyle\sum_{\gamma\in\Gamma}v_{\gamma}.
The zero-eigenspace is
(n-1)
-dimensional and consists of all vectors in
C_{0}(X)
of the form

\displaystyle\sum_{\gamma\in\Gamma}a_{\gamma}v_{\gamma},~\displaystyle\sum_{\gamma\in\Gamma}a_{\gamma}=0.

From this we see that
|\Gamma|
-eigenspace is the invariant subspace of
C_{0}(X)=\mathbb{C}[\Gamma]
corresponding to the trivial representation and that the zero-eigenspace is the direct sum of all other irreducible invariant subspaces of the regular representation of
\Gamma.
For

[

, Proposition 4.9]
.

\square

, we let

Theorem 11.7

Suppose

When

for all

.

Proof:

Since
r_{\mathcal{H}}(E-x)=r_{\mathcal{H}}(E)
for all

and
F_{n-2}(X).
Now, with

Corollary 11.8

Suppose
r_{\mathcal{H}}(E-x)=r_{\mathcal{H}}(E)
for all
x\in E.
Then
\lambda
is an eigenvalue of
\Delta_{n-1}(X/\mathcal{H})
if and only if there exists
\rho\in{\mathcal{R}}(\mathcal{H})
such that
\lambda=\displaystyle\sum_{x\in\mbox{triv}\,(\rho)}|\Gamma_{x}|.
The multiplicity of
\lambda
is

\displaystyle\sum_{\rho\in{\mathcal{R}}(\mathcal{H})}\dim\rho,

where the sum is taken over all
\rho\in{\mathcal{R}}(\mathcal{H})
such that
\displaystyle\sum_{x\in\mbox{triv}\,(\rho)}|\Gamma_{x}|=\lambda.

Proof:
Since

is injective. The dimension of

\square

Remark 11.9

If
r_{\mathcal{H}}(A\cup\{x\})=r_{\mathcal{H}}(A)
for all

then similar arguments determine all the eigenvalues and eigenspaces of

only depends on
P(\mathcal{H}).

We end by using
\Delta_{n-1}(X/\mathcal{H})
to interpret the dual Crapo-Rota critical theorem for finite groups as a statement about the topology of
X/\mathcal{H}.

Theorem 11.10

The dimension of
H_{n-1}(X/\mathcal{H};\mathbb{C})
is

\displaystyle\sum_{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rho\in{\mathcal{R}}(\mathcal{H})}}{{\mbox{triv}\,\rho=\emptyset}}}\dim\rho={\chi}_{P(\mathcal{H})^{\ast}}(|\Gamma|).

(13)

Proof:

The equality in (
13
) is the dual Crapo-Rota critical theorem for finite groups with
k=1.
Here we give an independent topological proof.

The known formulas for the homology of the join of simplicial complexes show that the only nontrivial reduced homology group of
X
is
\tilde{H}_{n-1}(X).
Since
\mathbb{C}
has characteristic zero,
H_{i}(X/\mathcal{H};\mathbb{C})
equals the elements of
H_{i}(X;\mathbb{C})
left fixed by the action of
\mathcal{H}
on
H_{i}(X;\mathbb{C})

[
1
, Theorem 2.4, pg. 120]
. By the Hodge theorem,
H_{n-1}(X;\mathbb{C})
is the kernel of
\Delta_{n-1}(X).
Proposition
11.6
tells us that this kernel is the direct sum of all of the irreducible representations
\rho
in the regular representation of
\mathcal{G}
with
\mbox{triv}\,\rho=\emptyset.
By definition,
{\mathcal{R}}(\mathcal{H})
are those
\rho
left fixed by the
\mathcal{H}
-action. Hence the dimension of
H_{n-1}(X/\mathcal{H})
equals the L.H.S. of (
13
).

To see the last equality we consider the reduced Euler characteristic of
X/\mathcal{H}.
As the only nontrivial reduced homology group of
X/\mathcal{H}
is
\tilde{H}_{n-1}(X/\mathcal{H}),
the reduced Euler characteristic of
X/\mathcal{H}
is
(-1)^{n-1}\dim H_{n-1}(X/\mathcal{H};\mathbb{C}).
On the other hand, we can compute the reduced Euler characteristic by counting faces. Let
S\subseteq E.
Define
F_{S}
to be the number of faces of
X/\mathcal{H}
corresponding to cosets of the form
\mathcal{G}_{S}/\mathcal{H}_{S}.
Then

|F_{S}|=\left|\frac{\mathcal{G}_{S}}{\mathcal{H}_{S}}\right|=\frac{|\Gamma|^{|S|}}{|\mathcal{H}_{S}|}=|\Gamma|^{|S|-r_{\mathcal{H}}(S)}=|\Gamma|^{r^{\ast}_{\mathcal{H}}(E)-r^{\ast}_{\mathcal{H}}(E-S)}.

Thus the reduced Euler characteristic of
X/\mathcal{H}
is

\displaystyle\sum_{S\subseteq E}(-1)^{|S|-1}|F_{S}|=\displaystyle\sum_{S\subseteq E}(-1)^{|S|-1}|\Gamma|^{r^{\ast}_{\mathcal{H}}(E)-r^{\ast}_{\mathcal{H}}(E-S)}

=(-1)^{n-1}\displaystyle\sum_{S\subseteq E}(-1)^{|E-S|}|\Gamma|^{r^{\ast}_{\mathcal{H}}(E)-r^{\ast}_{\mathcal{H}}(E-S)}=(-1)^{n-1}{\chi}_{r^{\ast}_{\mathcal{H}}}(|\Gamma|).

\square
