Optimal light cone for macroscopic particle transport in long-range systems: A quantum speed limit approach
Tan Van Vu, Tomotaka Kuwahara, Keiji Saito

Abstract
Understanding the ultimate rate at which information propagates is a pivotal issue in nonequilibrium physics. Nevertheless, the task of elucidating the propagation speed inherent in quantum bosonic systems presents challenges due to the unbounded nature of their interactions. In this study, we tackle the problem of macroscopic particle transport in a long-range generalization of the lattice Bose-Hubbard model through the lens of the quantum speed limit. By developing a unified approach based on optimal transport theory, we rigorously prove that the minimum time required for macroscopic particle transport is always bounded by the distance between the source and target regions, while retaining its significance even in the thermodynamic limit. Furthermore, we derive an upper bound for the probability of observing a specific number of bosons inside the target region, thereby providing additional insights into the dynamics of particle transport. Our results hold true for arbitrary initial states under both long-range hopping and long-range interactions, thus resolving an open problem of particle transport in generic bosonic systems.

Results

We consider a generic model of bosons on an arbitrary
D
-dimensional lattice, wherein bosons can hop between arbitrary sites and interact with each other.
The system Hamiltonian is time dependent (i.e., an external control protocol can be applied to the system) and can be expressed in the following form:

H_{t}\coloneqq\sum_{i\neq j\in\Lambda}J_{ij}(t)\hat{b}_{i}^{\dagger}\hat{b}_{j}+\sum_{Z\subseteq\Lambda}h_{Z}(t).

(1)

Here,
\Lambda
denotes the set of all the sites in the lattice,
\hat{b}_{i}^{\dagger}
and
\hat{b}_{i}
are the bosonic creation and annihilation operators for site
i
, respectively, and
h_{Z}(t)
is an arbitrary function of
\{\hat{n}_{i}\}_{i\in Z}
, where
\hat{n}_{i}\coloneqq\hat{b}_{i}^{\dagger}\hat{b}_{i}
is the number operator.
Note that
h_{Z}(t)
does not need to be local (i.e.,
Z
can be arbitrarily large).
In other words, both
long-range hopping
and
long-range interactions
are allowed in our setup.
The hopping terms
J_{ij}(t)
are symmetric [i.e.,
J_{ij}(t)=J_{ji}(t)
] and upper bounded by a power law in the Euclidean distance, i.e.,
|J_{ij}(t)|\leq J/\|i-j\|^{\alpha}
for some positive constant
J
, where
\|\cdot\|
denotes the Euclidean norm and the power decay
\alpha
satisfies
\alpha>D
2
2

2

Note that in the
\alpha\leq D
regime, particle transport speed can be effectively infinite. To elaborate further, there exists a transport protocol that transfers a single particle over a distance
d
within time
\tau=O(\log d)
when
\alpha=D
, and
\tau=O(1)
when
\alpha<D

[
63
]
. Given our focus on scenarios involving finite bosonic transport speeds, the region where transport speed becomes infinite is excluded from our study.
.
Examples include the standard Bose-Hubbard model, specified by
J_{ij}(t)=0
for any non-neighboring sites
i
and
j
and
\sum_{Z\subseteq\Lambda}h_{Z}(t)=(U/2)\sum_{i}\hat{n}_{i}(\hat{n}_{i}-1)-\mu\sum_{i}\hat{n}_{i}
, where
U
and
\mu
are real constants.
Let
\varrho_{t}
be the system’s density matrix at time
t
.
Then, its time evolution is described by the von Neumann equation (
\hbar=1
for simplicity),

\dot{\varrho}_{t}=-i[H_{t},\varrho_{t}].

(2)

It can be easily shown that the total number of particles in this system is conserved, denoted hereafter by
\mathcal{N}
.

We introduce some notations that will be used in this study.
For an arbitrary set
X
of lattice sites,
X_{c}\coloneqq\Lambda\setminus X
is the set of sites that are not present in
X
, and
|X|
denotes its cardinality.
The distance between any two disjoint sets

and

is defined as the minimum distance between sites belonging to each of them,

d_{XY}\coloneqq\min_{i\in X,\,j\in Y}\|i-j\|.

(3)

The number operator of local bosons occupied inside region
X
is given by
\hat{n}_{X}\coloneqq\sum_{i\in X}\hat{n}_{i}
, and the corresponding concentration operator is
\overline{n}_{X}\coloneqq\hat{n}_{X}/\mathcal{N}
.
Let
\Pi_{\vec{N}}=|\vec{N}\rangle\langle\vec{N}|
be the projection onto the Mott state
|\vec{N}\rangle
(i.e.,
\hat{n}_{i}|\vec{N}\rangle=n_{i}|\vec{N}\rangle
for
\vec{N}=[n_{i}]_{i\in\Lambda}
).
Then, the projection
P_{\hat{n}_{X}\leq N_{0}}
is defined as

P_{\hat{n}_{X}\leq N_{0}}\coloneqq\sum_{\vec{N}:\matrixelement{\vec{N}}{\hat{n}_X}{\vec{N}}\leq N_{0}}\Pi_{\vec{N}}.

(4)

The expectation of an observable
A
with respect to state
\varrho
is denoted as
\expectationvalue{A}_{\varrho}\coloneqq\tr{A\varrho}
.

To fully resolve the open problem of bosonic transport, a novel mathematical framework needs to be developed. Unlike the previous approach based on adiabatic spacetime localization observables in Ref.
[
60
]
, which could only achieve a partial resolution, we here tackle the problem using a quantum speed limit approach. It is important to note that the quantum speed limit primarily focuses on determining the minimum time required for state changes, but it may not provide accurate predictions for many-body systems
[
64
]
. On the other hand, the Lieb-Robinson bound offers valuable insights into many-body systems but does not furnish information regarding the speed of transitions inside the light cone. Consequently, it seems reasonable to anticipate that unifying these two concepts could yield a novel bound that takes advantage of their respective strengths and establishes new constraints that neither concept can fully encompass on its own. To achieve this, we develop a unified speed limit based on optimal transport theory [cf. Eq. (
20
)]. This approach enables us to integrate geometric structures of the underlying dynamics into the speed limit, allowing for meaningful limits to be derived, even when dealing with large-scale systems.

A

Our first main result is the following statement, which describes a fundamental limit on the operational time required for a macroscopic transport of bosons in long-range systems.

Theorem 1
.

Consider a situation where a fraction
\mu\in(0,1]
of all bosons is transported from region
X
to a distant region
Y
.
Then, the operational time
\tau
required for this macroscopic bosonic transport is lower bounded by the distance between the two regions as

\tau\geq\kappa_{1}^{\epsilon}d_{XY}^{\min(1,\alpha-D-\epsilon)}.

(5)

Here,
0<\epsilon<\alpha-D
is an arbitrary number,
\kappa_{1}^{\epsilon}\coloneqq[J\gamma\zeta(\alpha-\alpha_{\epsilon}-D+1)]^{-1}\mu
is a constant independent of the system size,
\alpha_{\epsilon}\coloneqq\min(1,\alpha-D-\epsilon)
,
\gamma
is a
O(1)
constant [cf. Eq. (
24
)], and
\zeta
denotes the Riemann zeta function.

We briefly explain the role of the auxiliary variable
\epsilon
.
In simple terms, it is introduced to address the case of
D<\alpha\leq D+1
. For
\alpha>D+1
, we can choose
\epsilon=\alpha-D-1
; thus,
\kappa_{1}^{\epsilon}=[J\gamma\zeta(\alpha-D)]^{-1}\mu
and
\min(1,\alpha-D-\epsilon)=1
.
In the case
D<\alpha\leq D+1
, the bound (
5
) explicitly reads

\tau\geq\frac{\mu}{J\gamma\zeta(1+\epsilon)}d_{XY}^{\alpha-D-\epsilon}.

(6)

As seen,
\epsilon
can be arbitrarily small but should not be zero as the function
\zeta
can diverge.

Some remarks on Theorem
1
are in order.
(i) First, macroscopic transport here means that an average number
\mu\mathcal{N}
of bosons is transported from
X
to
Y
, where
\mu
is a
O(1)
constant.
Since bosons are indistinguishable, a fraction
\mu
of the total bosons must be effectively transported from region
X
to region
Y
if the following inequality is satisfied:

x_{Y}(\tau)\geq x_{X_{c}}(0)+\mu,

(7)

where
x_{Z}(t)
denotes the boson concentration inside region
Z
at time
t
.
Inequality (
7
) can be interpreted as follows: Even if all bosons outside of
X
are transported to
Y
, it is still insufficient to achieve the desired concentration; consequently, at least a fraction
\mu
of the bosons must be effectively transported from
X
to
Y
.
The result holds for arbitrary initial states, including both pure and mixed states, and for the entire range of the power decay
\alpha>D
.
Notably, this type of time constraint cannot be deduced by the conventional quantum speed limits
[
2
,
18
]
.

(ii) Second, bound (
5
) can be physically interpreted as follows.
For the case
\alpha>D+1
, the bound reads
\tau\geq O(d_{XY})
, implying that bosonic transport always takes time at least proportional to the distance.
Surprisingly, this implication is the same as in the case of short-range hopping
[
33
]
, indicating that long-range hopping and long-range interactions do not enhance the speed of macroscopic bosonic transport
3
3

3

Note that it may not be the case when considering the speed of information propagation.
.
On the other hand, in the case
D<\alpha\leq D+1
, the minimum operational time is bounded in terms of the power decay
\alpha
as
\tau\geq O(d_{XY}^{\alpha-D})
, indicating the effect of long-range hopping on speeding up bosonic transport.

(iii) Last, we discuss the optimality of the bound (
5
) and show that it is optimal.
The optimality here is interpreted in the sense that the power coefficient associated with the distance term in the bound is optimal.
In other words, we need only show that there exist transport protocols such that bosonic transport can be accomplished within time
\tau=O(d_{XY}^{\min(1,\alpha-D)})
.
To this end, we first consider the case of a single particle (i.e.,
\mathcal{N}=1
).
In this case, it was shown that one could construct a rapid state-transfer protocol such that the particle can be transferred between two sites of distance
d
within time
\tau=O(d)
for
\alpha>D+1
and
\tau=O(d^{\alpha-D})
for
D<\alpha\leq D+1

[

(see Theorem 11 therein). Therefore, it can be concluded that the bound (

We note that while the form of the optimal light cone is the same as that obtained in Ref.
[
63
]
, the previous study only considers noninteracting particles, which can be simplified to the problem of single free particle. In contrast, our result applies to interacting systems, where boson-boson interactions can be arbitrary and play a crucial role in facilitating bosonic transport (cf. Lemmas
5
and
7
). Next, we show a protocol that saturates the bound for
\alpha>D+1
in the general case of an extensive boson number (i.e.,
\mathcal{N}\gg 1
). We consider a one-dimensional lattice of length
L
for simplicity and assume that the initial state is a Mott state
\ket{\mca{N},0,\dots,0}
. Here,
\ket{m}\otimes\ket{n}
is denoted by
\ket{m,n}
for convenience. Initially, all bosons are concentrated at site

. We can sequentially transport bosons within

\ket{\mca{N},0}\to\ket{\mca{N}-1,1}\to\ket{1,\mca{N}-1}\to\ket{0,\mca{N}}
. The time consumed in each step is equal to
\pi/(2J)+\pi/(J\sqrt{\mathcal{N}})=O(1)
(cf. Lemmas
6
and
7
). Thus, the total time for transporting
\mathcal{N}
bosons over a distance of
L-1
is
O(L)
.

Theorem
1
concerns the minimum time required to transport an average number of bosons between two regions.
In the sequel, we focus on the probability of finding a number of bosons occupied inside the target region within a finite time.
Our second main result, which is stated below, establishes an upper bound on this probability in terms of the operational time and the distance.

Theorem 2
.

Assume that the initial boson number outside of region
X
does not exceed
N_{0}
, i.e.,
\expectationvalue{P_{\hat{n}_{X_c}\le N_0}}_{\varrho_{0}}=1
.
Then, the probability of finding bosons inside a disjoint region
Y
is upper bounded by the operational time
\tau
and the distance between the two regions as

\expectationvalue{P_{\hat{n}_{Y}\ge N_0+\Delta N_0}}_{\varrho_{\tau}}\leq\frac{\kappa_{2}^{\epsilon}\mathcal{N}}{\Delta N_{0}}\frac{\tau}{d_{XY}^{\min(1,\alpha-D-\epsilon)}}.

(8)

Here,
\mathcal{N}
is the total number of bosons,
0<\epsilon<\alpha-D
is an arbitrary number, and
\kappa_{2}^{\epsilon}\coloneqq J\gamma\zeta(\alpha-\alpha_{\epsilon}-D+1)
is a constant independent of the system size.

This result holds under a generic setting for both initial states and system dynamics, same as in Theorem
1
.
It can be interpreted as follows.
Initially, there are at most
N_{0}
bosons outside of
X
; therefore, in order to observe more than
N_{0}
bosons inside
Y
, a certain number of bosons must be transferred from
X
to
Y
.
Bound (
8
) indicates that the probability of finding at least
N_{0}+\Delta N_{0}
inside
Y
is at most linear in time
\tau
and inversely proportional to boson number
\Delta N_{0}
and distance
d_{XY}
.
While the inequality (
8
) is valid for any boson number
\Delta N_{0}
, it is particularly relevant and meaningful to discuss macroscopic transport [i.e.,
\Delta N_{0}/\mathcal{N}\eqqcolon\mu
is a

In this case, we obtain

\expectationvalue{P_{\overline{n}_{Y}\ge\xi}}_{\varrho_{\tau}}\leq\kappa_{2}^{\epsilon}\mu^{-1}\tau d_{XY}^{-\min(1,\alpha-D-\epsilon)},

(9)

where
\xi\coloneqq(N_{0}+\Delta N_{0})/\mathcal{N}\geq x_{X_{c}}(0)+\mu
.
In the case
\alpha>D+2
, a similar probability constraint has been derived for pure states in the absence of long-range interactions

]
[see Eq. (7) therein], remaining an open question for the case of
D<\alpha\leq D+2

Our result thus completely resolves this open problem of bosonic transport for generic long-range systems.

It is worth noting that, at the qualitative level, Theorem
1
can be viewed as a corollary of Theorem
2
. For arbitrary
\mu^{\prime}\in(0,\mu)

\displaystyle\expectationvalue{P_{\overline{n}_{Y}\le x_{X_c}(0)+\mu'}}_{\varrho_{\tau}}

\displaystyle=\expectationvalue{P_{\mbb{1}-\overline{n}_{Y}\ge 1-x_{X_c}(0)-\mu'}}_{\varrho_{\tau}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\expectationvalue{\mbb{1}-\overline{n}_{Y}}_{\varrho_{\tau}}}{1-x_{X_{c}}(0)-\mu^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1-x_{Y}(\tau)}{1-x_{X_{c}}(0)-\mu^{\prime}}.

(10)

Thus, the probability of finding bosons inside region

can be lower bounded as

\expectationvalue{P_{\overline{n}_{Y}\ge x_{X_c}(0)+\mu'}}_{\varrho_{\tau}}\geq\frac{x_{Y}(\tau)-x_{X_{c}}(0)-\mu^{\prime}}{1-x_{X_{c}}(0)-\mu^{\prime}}.

(11)

When
x_{Y}(\tau)\geq x_{X_{c}}(0)+\mu

can deduce a statement that is qualitatively equivalent to Theorem

\tau\geq\frac{\mu^{\prime}(\mu-\mu^{\prime})}{\mu[1-x_{X_{c}}(0)-\mu^{\prime}]}\kappa_{1}^{\epsilon}d_{XY}^{\min(1,\alpha-D-\epsilon)},

which can be optimized with

While the bound (

It is worthwhile to investigate the extent to which our results can be extended.
We demonstrate that our main results cannot hold in more general classes of quantum many-boson systems.
This implies that, in these cases, particle transport at

Our objective here is to illustrate that even by making slight modifications to the Bose-Hubbard model, we can observe an

Theorem 3

The speed of particle transport can be infinite in a one-dimensional bosonic system of size

The subprotocol that performs the state transformation

\pi/(2JL\sqrt{L})
by the above Hamiltonian with the
U\to\infty
limit.
In the second stage, the Hamiltonian reads

\displaystyle H_{2}

\displaystyle=J(\hat{b}_{k}^{\dagger}\hat{b}_{k+1}+\hat{b}_{k+1}^{\dagger}\hat{b}_{k})

\displaystyle+J\quantity[\hat b_{k+1}^\dagger(\hat{n}_{k+1}+\hat{n}_{k})\hat b_k+\hat b_{k}^\dagger(\hat{n}_{k+1}+\hat{n}_{k})\hat b_{k+1}].

(15)

As shown in Lemma
4
, the transformation
\ket{L-1,1}\to\ket{1,L-1}
can be attained within time
\pi/(2JL)
by this Hamiltonian.
In the last stage, the Hamiltonian is given by

\displaystyle H_{3}

\displaystyle=J(\hat{b}_{k}^{\dagger}\hat{b}_{k+1}+\hat{b}_{k+1}^{\dagger}\hat{b}_{k})-U\hat{n}_{k+1}^{2}+U(2L-1)\hat{n}_{k+1}

\displaystyle+J\quantity[\hat b_{k+1}^\dagger(\hat{n}_{k+1}+\hat{n}_{k})\hat b_k+\hat b_{k}^\dagger(\hat{n}_{k+1}+\hat{n}_{k})\hat b_{k+1}].

(16)

As proved in Lemma
5
, the transformation
\ket{1,L-1}\to\ket{0,L}
can be achieved within time
\pi/(2JL\sqrt{L})
by the Hamiltonian
H_{3}
in the
U\to\infty
limit.

For the protocol constructed above, the total time consumed for the bosonic transport is

\tau=\frac{\pi}{2J}\sum_{k=1}^{L-1}\quantity(\frac{1}{L}+\frac{2}{L\sqrt{L}})<\frac{\pi}{J}

(17)

for any
L\geq 3
.
As can be observed, macroscopic transport can always be accomplished within a constant of time, independent of the distance between the regions.
Therefore, the speed of macroscopic bosonic transport is not finite in bosonic systems with interaction-induced tunneling terms.

3
Conclusion

Using optimal transport theory, we elucidated fundamental limits on the speed of macroscopic particle transport in long-range bosonic systems from the perspective of the quantum speed limit.
The results hold for the entire range
\alpha>D
of power decay and arbitrary initial states under generic long-range characteristics.
Our findings completely resolve the critical open problem of the speed of macroscopic particle transport.
It is worth noting that the speed of bosonic transport may not be finite in the presence of higher-order hopping terms (i.e., terms like
\hat{b}_{i}\hat{b}_{j}\hat{b}_{k}^{\dagger}\hat{b}_{l}^{\dagger}
), such as interaction-induced tunneling terms in Eq. (
13
).
We also note that our study does not delve into the exploration of the Lieb-Robinson bound concerning the speed of information propagation. This crucial aspect remains unaddressed and is reserved for future research.

Appendix A
Methods

A.1
Unified speed limit based on optimal transport theory

The key ingredient in the proofs of our results is a novel unified speed limit.
This speed limit is derived using optimal transport theory, which plays crucial roles in various disciplines, such as statistics and machine learning
[
69
]
, computer vision
[
70
]
, linguistics
[
71
]
, molecular biology
[
72
]
, and stochastic thermodynamics
[
73
,
74
,
75
,
76
]
.
Here we describe the unified speed limit and its derivation.

First, we begin with a very general setup, which is applicable to both classical and quantum systems. We consider a time evolution of a vector state
{\bf\it x}_{t}\coloneqq[x_{1}(t),\dots,x_{N}(t)]^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{N}
over an undirected graph
\mathcal{G}(V,E)
with the vertex set
V=\{1,\dots,N\}
and edge set
E=\{(i,j)\}
.
For each vertex
i
, let
B_{i}\coloneqq\{j\,|\,(i,j)\in E\}
denote the set of neighboring vertices of
i
.
The dynamics of
{\bf\it x}_{t}
is described by the following deterministic equation:

\dot{x}_{i}(t)=\sum_{j\in B_{i}}f_{ij}(t),

(18)

where
f_{ij}(t)=-f_{ji}(t)
denotes the flow exchange between vertices
i
and
j
for
i\neq j
.
Note that the sum
\sum_{i=1}^{N}x_{i}(t)
is time-invariant.

Next, we introduce the discrete
L^{1}
-Wasserstein distance between two distributions
{\bf\it x}
and
{\bf\it y}
.
Suppose that we have a transport plan that redistributes
{\bf\it x}
to
{\bf\it y}
by sending an amount of
\pi_{ij}\geq 0
from
x_{j}
to
y_{i}
with a cost of
c_{ij}\geq 0
per unit mass for any
i
and
j
.
Here,
\pi=[\pi_{ij}]\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{N\times N}
is a joint probability distribution such that its marginal distributions reduce to
\sum_{j}\pi_{ij}=y_{i}
and
\sum_{j}\pi_{ji}=x_{i}
, defining an admissible transport plan.
The
L^{1}
-Wasserstein distance is then defined as the minimum transport cost over all feasible plans, given by

\mathcal{W}({\bf\it x},{\bf\it y})\coloneqq\min_{\pi\in\mathcal{C}({\bf\it x},{\bf\it y})}\sum_{i,j}c_{ij}\pi_{ij},

(19)

where
\mathcal{C}({\bf\it x},{\bf\it y})
denotes the set of couplings
\pi
.
The cost matrix
[c_{ij}]
can be specified arbitrarily, as long as the following two requirements are fulfilled: (i) symmetry (
c_{ij}=c_{ji}\penalty\ \forall i,j
) and (ii) the triangle inequality (
c_{ij}+c_{jk}\geq c_{ik}\forall i,j,k
).
The freedom to set up the cost matrix is one of the powerful aspects of the Wasserstein distance. This freedom enables us to include the geometric structure of the underlying dynamics, leading to the derivation of novel physical implications.

Given the setup above, by generalizing the speed limit obtained in Ref.
[
33
]
, we prove that the minimum time required to transform state
{\bf\it x}_{0}
into
{\bf\it x}_{\tau}
is always lower bounded as

\tau\geq\frac{\mathcal{W}({\bf\it x}_{0},{\bf\it x}_{\tau})}{\expectationvalue{\sum_{(i,j)\in E}c_{ij}|f_{ij}(t)|}_{\tau}},

(20)

where
\expectationvalue{z_t}_{\tau}\coloneqq\tau^{-1}\int_{0}^{\tau}\differential{t}z_{t}
is the time-average quantity of
z_{t}
.
The unified speed limit (
20
) includes all the essence to extend the use of the conventional quantum speed limits to a wide range of quantum many-body problems with various geometric structures.
The crucial step in applying this bound effectively is the thoughtful selection of the cost matrix
[c_{ij}]
. With appropriate choices, our new bound (
20
) can play roles similar to the Lieb-Robinson bound.
In the specific case when
c_{ij}=1
for any
(i,j)\in E
and
c_{ij}
represents the shortest-path distance between vertices
i
and
j
otherwise, the unified speed limit recovers the topological speed limit
[
33
]
.

The unified speed limit can be derived with the help of the Kantorovich-Rubinstein duality
[
77
]
, which expresses the Wasserstein distance in an alternative variational form as (see Lemma
8
for the proof)

\mathcal{W}({\bf\it x},{\bf\it y})=\max_{{\bf\it\phi}}{\bf\it\phi}^{\top}({\bf\it x}-{\bf\it y}),

(21)

where the maximum is over all vectors
{\bf\it\phi}=[\phi_{1},\dots,\phi_{N}]^{\top}
satisfying
|\phi_{i}-\phi_{j}|\leq c_{ij}\penalty\ \forall i,j
.
Notice that
x_{i}(\tau)=x_{i}(0)+\int_{0}^{\tau}\differential{t}\sum_{j\in B_{i}}f_{ij}(t)
.
Then, by applying the Kantorovich-Rubinstein duality, we can prove Eq. (
20
) as follows:

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}({\bf\it x}_{0},{\bf\it x}_{\tau})

\displaystyle=\max_{{\bf\it\phi}}{\bf\it\phi}^{\top}({\bf\it x}_{\tau}-{\bf\it x}_{0})

\displaystyle=\max_{{\bf\it\phi}}{\sum_{i}\phi_{i}\int_{0}^{\tau}\differential{t}\sum_{j\in B_{i}}f_{ij}(t)}

\displaystyle=\max_{{\bf\it\phi}}{\sum_{(i,j)\in E}(\phi_{i}-\phi_{j})\int_{0}^{\tau}\differential{t}f_{ij}(t)}

\displaystyle\leq\max_{{\bf\it\phi}}\sum_{(i,j)\in E}|\phi_{i}-\phi_{j}|\int_{0}^{\tau}\differential{t}|f_{ij}(t)|

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{\tau}\differential{t}\sum_{(i,j)\in E}c_{ij}|f_{ij}(t)|

\displaystyle=\tau\Big\langle{\sum_{(i,j)\in E}c_{ij}|f_{ij}(t)|}\Big\rangle_{\tau}.

(22)

In what follows, we apply the unified speed limit to prove our main results, Theorems
1
and
2
.

A.2
Proof of Theorem
1

First, we explain some useful notations that will be used later.
For any site
i
, we denote by
i[r]
the ball of radius
r\geq 0
, which contains all sites
j
such that
\|i-j\|<r
.
Using this definition, the set
\Lambda\setminus\{i\}
can be decomposed as

\Lambda\setminus\{i\}=\cup_{\ell=1}^{\infty}(i[\ell+1]\setminus i[\ell]).

(23)

In addition, we define
\gamma
as a constant such that the following inequality holds for any
r\geq 0
:

|i[r+1]\setminus i[r]|\leq\gamma r^{D-1}.

(24)

We consider a
|\Lambda|
-dimensional vector
{\bf\it x}_{t}
, where
x_{i}(t)
denotes the boson concentration occupied at site
i
[i.e.,
x_{i}(t)\coloneqq\tr{\overline{n}_i\varrho_t}
].
Evidently,
\sum_{i}x_{i}(t)=1
for any
t
.
To define the Wasserstein distance between these vectors, the cost matrix
[c_{ij}]
needs to be specified. We consider the following cost matrix:

c_{ij}=\|i-j\|^{\alpha_{\epsilon}},

(25)

where
0<\epsilon<\alpha-D
is an arbitrary number and
\alpha_{\epsilon}\coloneqq\min(1,\alpha-D-\epsilon)
is defined for convenience.
Since
\|i-j\|+\|j-k\|\geq\|i-k\|
and
a^{z}+b^{z}\geq(a+b)^{z}
for any
a,b\geq 0
and
0\leq z\leq 1
, we can verify that

c_{ij}+c_{jk}\geq c_{ik}

(26)

for any
i,j,k\in\Lambda
.
As can be observed, this distance includes spatial information of the lattice and can be the order of the system size.

We are now ready to prove Theorem
1
.
Using the relation
[\hat{b}_{i},\hat{n}_{i}]=\hat{b}_{i}
, we can show that the time evolution of
x_{i}(t)
is governed by the following equation:

\dot{x}_{i}(t)=\sum_{j(\neq i)}2J_{ij}(t)\mathcal{N}^{-1}\imaginary\quantity[\tr{\hat{b}_i^\dagger\hat{b}_j\varrho_t}]\eqqcolon\sum_{j(\neq i)}\phi_{ij}(t).

(27)

It can be easily verified that
\phi_{ij}(t)=-\phi_{ji}(t)
.
Therefore, the dynamics of
{\bf\it x}_{t}
can be mapped to Eq. (
18
), where the graph
\mathcal{G}
is determined by
V=\Lambda
and
B_{i}=\Lambda\setminus\{i\}
.
Applying the unified speed limit (
20
) yields

\tau\geq\frac{\mathcal{W}({\bf\it x}_{0},{\bf\it x}_{\tau})}{\expectationvalue{\Phi_t}_{\tau}},

(28)

where we define
\Phi_{t}\coloneqq(1/2)\sum_{i\neq j}c_{ij}|\phi_{ij}(t)|
.
Since a fraction
\mu
of bosons must be transported from
X
to
Y
[i.e., the inequality (
7
) is satisfied], we have

\displaystyle\mu\leq x_{Y}(\tau)-x_{X_{c}}(0)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in Y,j\in\Lambda}\pi_{ij}-\sum_{i\in\Lambda,j\in X_{c}}\pi_{ij}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in Y,j\in X}\pi_{ij}-\sum_{i\in Y_{c},j\in X_{c}}\pi_{ij}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i\in Y,j\in X}\pi_{ij}.

(29)

Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}({\bf\it x}_{0},{\bf\it x}_{\tau})\geq\quantity(\min_{i\in Y,j\in X}c_{ij})\sum_{i\in Y,j\in X}\pi_{ij}\geq\mu d_{XY}^{\alpha_{\epsilon}}.

(30)

On the other hand, by applying the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality,
\phi_{ij}(t)
can be upper bounded as

|\phi_{ij}(t)|\leq|J_{ij}(t)|[{x}_{i}(t)+{x}_{j}(t)].

(31)

Consequently, the velocity term
\Phi_{t}
is bounded from above as follows:

\displaystyle\Phi_{t}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i}{x}_{i}(t)\sum_{j(\neq i)}|J_{ij}(t)|\|i-j\|^{\alpha_{\epsilon}}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i}{x}_{i}(t)\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}\sum_{j\in(i[\ell+1]\setminus i[\ell])}\frac{J}{\|i-j\|^{\alpha-\alpha_{\epsilon}}}

\displaystyle\leq J\gamma\sum_{i}{x}_{i}(t)\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\ell^{\alpha-\alpha_{\epsilon}-D+1}}

\displaystyle=J\gamma\zeta(\alpha-\alpha_{\epsilon}-D+1).

(32)

Here, we use the facts that
\|i-j\|\geq\ell
for any
j\in(i[\ell+1]\setminus i[\ell])
and
|i[\ell+1]\setminus i[\ell]|\leq\gamma\ell^{D-1}
in the third line, and
\zeta(\cdot)
denotes the Riemann zeta function.
Define
\kappa_{1}^{\epsilon}\coloneqq[J\gamma\zeta(\alpha-\alpha_{\epsilon}-D+1)]^{-1}\mu
. Note that
\kappa_{1}^{\epsilon}
is independent of the system size and finite because
\alpha-\alpha_{\epsilon}-D+1\geq 1+\epsilon
.
Combining Eqs. (
28
), (
30
), and (
32
) yields

\tau\geq\kappa_{1}^{\epsilon}d_{XY}^{\alpha_{\epsilon}},

(33)

which completes the proof.

A.3
Proof of Theorem
2

The proof strategy is similar to that of Theorem
1
.
The different point is that we consider the probability distribution of boson numbers at all sites instead of the vector of average boson concentrations.
Define the probability
p_{\vec{N}}(t)\coloneqq\tr\{\Pi_{\vec{N}}\varrho_{t}\}
.
Since the total boson number is invariant, we need only consider feasible states
|\vec{N}\rangle
that satisfy
\tr\{\hat{n}_{\Lambda}\Pi_{\vec{N}}\}=\mathcal{N}
.
The time evolution of the probability distribution
{\bf\it p}_{t}=[p_{\vec{N}}(t)]^{\top}
can be derived from the von Neumann equation as

\displaystyle\dot{p}_{\vec{N}}(t)

\displaystyle=-i\tr{\Pi_{\vec{N}}[H_t,\varrho_t]}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\neq j}iJ_{ij}(t)\sqrt{n_{i}n_{j}^{\prime}}\quantity(\mel{\vec{N}}{\varrho_t}{\vec{N}'}-\mel{\vec{N}'}{\varrho_t}{\vec{N}})

\displaystyle\eqqcolon\sum_{i\neq j}\varphi_{\vec{N}\vec{N}^{\prime}}(t),

(34)

where
\vec{N}^{\prime}
is obtained from
\vec{N}
by setting
n_{i}^{\prime}=n_{i}-1
,
n_{j}^{\prime}=n_{j}+1
, and
n_{k}^{\prime}=n_{k}
for all
k\neq i,j
.
Note that
\varphi_{\vec{N}\vec{N}^{\prime}}(t)=-\varphi_{\vec{N}^{\prime}\vec{N}}(t)\in\mathbb{R}
.
Two states
\vec{N}
and
\vec{M}
are said to be neighboring if there exist
i\neq j
such that
|n_{i}-m_{i}|=|n_{j}-m_{j}|=1
and
n_{k}=m_{k}
for any
k\neq i,j
.
For such neighboring states, the transport cost is defined as
c_{\vec{N}\vec{N}^{\prime}}=\|i-j\|^{\alpha_{\epsilon}}
.
For arbitrary states
\vec{N}
and
\vec{M}
, the cost is defined as the shortest-path cost over all possible paths connecting the two states,

c_{\vec{M}\vec{N}}=\min\sum_{k=1}^{K}c_{\vec{N}_{k}\vec{N}_{k-1}},

(35)

where
\vec{N}_{0}=\vec{N}
,
\vec{N}_{K}=\vec{M}
, and
\vec{N}_{k-1}
and
\vec{N}_{k}
are neighboring states for all
1\leq k\leq K
.
Obviously,
c_{\vec{N}\vec{N}}=0
.
Using the defined shortest-path costs, we consider the following Wasserstein distance:

\mathcal{W}({\bf\it p},{\bf\it q})\coloneqq\min_{\pi\in\mathcal{C}({\bf\it p},{\bf\it q})}\sum_{\vec{M},\vec{N}}c_{\vec{M}\vec{N}}\pi_{\vec{M}\vec{N}}.

(36)

Utilizing the unified speed limit (
20
) for this Wasserstein distance, we can arrive at the following inequality:

\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{\tau}\differential{t}\sum_{\vec{N}}\sum_{i\neq j}c_{\vec{N}\vec{N}^{\prime}}|\varphi_{\vec{N}\vec{N}^{\prime}}(t)|\geq\mathcal{W}({\bf\it p}_{0},{\bf\it p}_{\tau}).

(37)

Furthermore, applying the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, we obtain

|\varphi_{\vec{N}\vec{N}^{\prime}}(t)|\leq{|J_{ij}(t)|}\quantity[n_ip_{\vec{N}}(t)+n_j'p_{\vec{N}'}(t)].

(38)

Consequently, the left-hand side of Eq. (
37
) can be upper bounded as

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\vec{N}}\sum_{i\neq j}c_{\vec{N}\vec{N}^{\prime}}|\varphi_{\vec{N}\vec{N}^{\prime}}(t)|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\vec{N}}p_{\vec{N}}(t)\sum_{i}n_{i}\sum_{j(\neq i)}\frac{J}{\|i-j\|^{\alpha-\alpha_{\epsilon}}}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\vec{N}}p_{\vec{N}}(t)\mathcal{N}J\gamma\zeta(\alpha-\alpha_{\epsilon}-D+1)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{N}J\gamma\zeta(\alpha-\alpha_{\epsilon}-D+1).

(39)

Define
\mathcal{S}_{0}=\{\vec{N}\,|\,\sum_{i\in X_{c}}n_{i}\leq N_{0}\}
and
\mathcal{S}_{\tau}=\{\vec{N}\,|\,\sum_{i\in Y}n_{i}\geq N_{0}+\Delta N_{0}\}
.
To reach state
\vec{M}\in\mathcal{S}_{\tau}
from state
\vec{N}\in\mathcal{S}_{0}
by hopping bosons between sites, an amount of
\Delta N_{0}
bosons must be transferred from sites of
X
to sites of
Y
.
Let
\vec{N}_{i_{0}}\to\vec{N}_{i_{1}}\to\dots\to\vec{N}_{i_{L}}
be a sequence of states that transfers a boson from site
i_{0}\in X
to site
i_{L}\in Y
by iteratively hopping bosons between sites
i_{l-1}
and
i_{l}
for
1\leq l\leq L
. Then, by applying the triangle inequality, we obtain that the cost of transferring one boson is lower bounded as

\sum_{l=1}^{L}c_{\vec{N}_{i_{l}}\vec{N}_{i_{l-1}}}=\sum_{l=1}^{L}\|i_{l}-i_{l-1}\|^{\alpha_{\epsilon}}\geq\|i_{L}-i_{0}\|^{\alpha_{\epsilon}}\geq d_{XY}^{\alpha_{\epsilon}}.

(40)

Therefore, we immediately obtain
c_{\vec{M}\vec{N}}\geq\Delta N_{0}d_{XY}^{\alpha_{\epsilon}}
for any
\vec{M}\in\mathcal{S}_{\tau}
and
\vec{N}\in\mathcal{S}_{0}
.
Since
\expectationvalue{P_{\hat{n}_{X_c}>N_0}}_{\varrho_{0}}=0
,
p_{\vec{N}}(0)=0
for any
\vec{N}\notin\mathcal{S}_{0}
.
Combining this with
p_{\vec{N}}(0)=\sum_{\vec{M}}\pi_{\vec{M}\vec{N}}
immediately derives
\pi_{\vec{M}\vec{N}}=0
for any
\vec{N}\notin\mathcal{S}_{0}
and
\vec{M}
.
Using these facts, the Wasserstein distance can be lower bounded as follows:

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}({\bf\it p}_{0},{\bf\it p}_{\tau})

\displaystyle=\min_{\pi}\sum_{\vec{M},\vec{N}}c_{\vec{M}\vec{N}}\pi_{\vec{M}\vec{N}}

\displaystyle\geq\min_{\pi}\sum_{\vec{M}\in\mathcal{S}_{\tau},\vec{N}\in\mathcal{S}_{0}}c_{\vec{M}\vec{N}}\pi_{\vec{M}\vec{N}}

\displaystyle\geq\Delta N_{0}d_{XY}^{\alpha_{\epsilon}}\min_{\pi}\sum_{\vec{M}\in\mathcal{S}_{\tau}}\sum_{\vec{N}\in\mathcal{S}_{0}}\pi_{\vec{M}\vec{N}}

\displaystyle=\Delta N_{0}d_{XY}^{\alpha_{\epsilon}}\min_{\pi}\sum_{\vec{M}\in\mathcal{S}_{\tau}}p_{\vec{M}}(\tau)

\displaystyle=\Delta N_{0}d_{XY}^{\alpha_{\epsilon}}\expectationvalue{P_{\hat{n}_Y\ge N_0+\Delta N_0}}_{\varrho_{\tau}}.

(41)

Here, we use the relation
\sum_{\vec{N}\in\mathcal{S}_{0}}\pi_{\vec{M}\vec{N}}=\sum_{\vec{N}}\pi_{\vec{M}\vec{N}}=p_{\vec{M}}(\tau)
in the fourth line.
Combining Eqs. (
37
), (
39
), and (
41
) yields

\expectationvalue{P_{\hat{n}_Y\ge N_0+\Delta N_0}}_{\varrho_{\tau}}\leq\frac{\mathcal{N}J\gamma\zeta(\alpha-\alpha_{\epsilon}-D+1)\tau}{\Delta N_{0}d_{XY}^{\alpha_{\epsilon}}},

(42)

which completes the proof by setting
\kappa_{2}^{\epsilon}\coloneqq J\gamma\zeta(\alpha-\alpha_{\epsilon}-D+1)
.

A.4
Useful lemmas

We formally present useful lemmas about state transformation and their proofs in the following.

Lemma 4
.

For any
M\geq 3
, the state transformation
\ket{M-1,1}\leftrightarrow\ket{1,M-1}
can be accomplished within time
t=\pi/(2JM)
by the following Hamiltonian:

H=J(\hat{b}_{1}^{\dagger}\hat{b}_{2}+\hat{b}_{2}^{\dagger}\hat{b}_{1})+J\quantity[\hat b_{2}^\dagger(\hat{n}_{2}+\hat{n}_{1})\hat b_{1}+\hat b_{1}^\dagger(\hat{n}_{2}+\hat{n}_{1})\hat b_{2}].

(43)

Proof.

Let
\ket{\psi_t}=\sum_{m=0}^{M}a_{m}(t)\ket{m,M-m}
be the expression of the time-evolved pure state.
Then, the time evolution of
a_{m}(t)
can be obtained from the Schrödinger equation
\ket{\dot\psi_t}=-iH\ket{\psi_t}
as

\displaystyle\dot{a}_{m}(t)

\displaystyle=\innerproduct{m,M-m}{\dot\psi_t}

\displaystyle=-i\matrixelement{m,M-m}{H}{\psi_t}

\displaystyle=-iJM\sqrt{m(M-m+1)}a_{m-1}(t)-iJM\sqrt{(m+1)(M-m)}a_{m+1}(t).

(44)

Defining
{\bf\it a}_{t}\coloneqq[a_{0}(t),\dots,a_{M}(t)]^{\top}
, we can rewrite Eq. (
44
) as

\dot{{\bf\it a}}_{t}=-iJMG{\bf\it a}_{t},

(45)

where
{\bf\it a}_{0}=[0,\dots,0,1,0]^{\top}
and the matrix
G=[g_{mn}]_{0\leq m,n\leq M}
is given by

g_{mn}=\begin{cases}\sqrt{m(M-m+1)}&\text{if}\penalty\ n=m-1,\\
\sqrt{(m+1)(M-m)}&\text{if}\penalty\ n=m+1,\\
0&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(46)

Since
{\bf\it a}_{t}=e^{-iJMGt}{\bf\it a}_{0}
, it needs only show that
|[e^{-i\pi G/2}]_{1(M-1)}|=|[e^{-i\pi G/2}]_{(M-1)1}|=1
.
We divide into two cases:
M=2\bar{M}-1
and
M=2\bar{M}
.
We prove the former, and the latter can be proved analogously.
Define
\tilde{G}=PGP^{-1}=[\tilde{g}_{mn}]_{0\leq m,n\leq M}
, where
P={\rm diag}(\gamma_{0},\dots,\gamma_{M})
is a diagonal matrix that will be determined latter.
We choose
\{\gamma_{m}\}
such that

\tilde{g}_{mn}=\begin{cases}M-m+1&\text{if}\penalty\ n=m-1,\\
m+1&\text{if}\penalty\ n=m+1,\\
0&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(47)

This can be attained by setting

\frac{\gamma_{m}}{\gamma_{m+1}}=\sqrt{\frac{m+1}{M-m}}.

(48)

The matrix
\tilde{G}
is nothing but the Sylvester-Kac matrix.
Since
e^{-i\pi G/2}=P^{-1}e^{-i\pi\tilde{G}/2}P
and
\gamma_{1}=\gamma_{M-1}
,
[e^{-i\pi G/2}]_{1(M-1)}=[e^{-i\pi\tilde{G}/2}]_{1(M-1)}
; thus, we need only prove that
|[e^{-i\pi\tilde{G}/2}]_{1(M-1)}|=1
.
It was shown that
\tilde{G}
has distinct eigenvalues
\{\lambda_{k}=M-2k\}_{k=0}^{M}

[
78
]
, thus being diagonalizable.
Therefore, its left eigenvectors
\{\ket{v_k^l}\}
and right eigenvectors
\{\ket{v_k^r}\}
are orthogonal. That is,
\innerproduct{v_i^l}{v_j^r}=\delta_{ij}
for any
i
and
j
. We note that
\innerproduct{v_k^r}{v_k^r}
and
\innerproduct{v_k^l}{v_k^l}
are not necessarily equal to
1
. From the representation
\tilde{G}=\sum_{k}\lambda_{k}\outerproduct{v_k^r}{v_k^l}
, we obtain

e^{-i\pi\tilde{G}/2}=\sum_{k=0}^{M}e^{-i\pi\lambda_{k}/2}\outerproduct{v_k^r}{v_k^l}=i\sum_{k=0}^{M}(-1)^{\bar{M}+k+1}\outerproduct{v_k^r}{v_k^l}.

(49)

Next, we analytically calculate the right and left eigenvectors.
For each
\lambda_{k}=M-2k
, from the relation
\tilde{G}\ket{v_k^r}=\lambda_{k}\ket{v_k^r}
, we obtain

\displaystyle v_{k1}^{r}

\displaystyle=\lambda_{k}v_{k0}^{r},

\displaystyle Mv_{k0}^{r}+2v_{k2}^{r}

\displaystyle=\lambda_{k}v_{k1}^{r},

\displaystyle(M-1)v_{k1}^{r}+3v_{k3}^{r}

\displaystyle=\lambda_{k}v_{k2}^{r},

\displaystyle\dots

\displaystyle v_{k(M-1)}^{r}

\displaystyle=\lambda_{k}v_{kM}^{r}.

(50)

We consider an auxiliary infinite system,

\displaystyle v_{k1}^{r}

\displaystyle=\lambda_{k}v_{k0}^{r},

\displaystyle Mv_{k0}^{r}+2v_{k2}^{r}

\displaystyle=\lambda_{k}v_{k1}^{r},

\displaystyle(M-1)v_{k1}^{r}+3v_{k3}^{r}

\displaystyle=\lambda_{k}v_{k2}^{r},

\displaystyle\dots

\displaystyle v_{k(M-1)}^{r}+(M+1)v_{k(M+1)}^{r}

\displaystyle=\lambda_{k}v_{kM}^{r},

\displaystyle(M+2)v_{k(M+2)}^{r}

\displaystyle=\lambda_{k}v_{k(M+1)}^{r},

\displaystyle\dots

(51)

If there exists a nontrivial solution of Eq. (
51
) for which
v_{k(M+1)}^{r}=0
, then we immediately obtain the solution for Eq. (
50
).
Multiplying all equations of Eq. (
51
) by
1,z,z^{2},\dots
and adding them, we readily obtain

\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}v_{km}^{r}(M-m)z^{m+1}+\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}v_{km}^{r}mz^{m-1}=\lambda_{k}\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}v_{km}^{r}z^{m}.

(52)

Defining the generating function
f_{k}(z)\coloneqq\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}v_{km}^{r}z^{m}
, Eq. (
52
) can be translated to the following form:

Mzf_{k}(z)-z^{2}f_{k}^{\prime}(z)+f_{k}^{\prime}(z)=\lambda_{k}f_{k}(z),

(53)

which yields the following differential equation:

f_{k}^{\prime}(z)=\frac{\lambda_{k}-Mz}{1-z^{2}}f_{k}(z)

(54)

with the initial condition
f_{k}(0)=v_{k0}^{r}
.
Solving this differential equation,
f_{k}(z)
can be explicitly obtained as

f_{k}(z)=v_{k0}^{r}(1+z)^{M-k}(1-z)^{k}.

(55)

Consequently, the components of the right eigenvectors can be calculated as

v_{km}^{r}=v_{k0}^{r}\sum_{i=m-k}^{M-k}(-1)^{m-i}{k\choose m-i}{M-k\choose i}.

(56)

We now calculate the left eigenvectors.
From the relation
\sum_{k}{v_{ki}^{r}}{v_{km}^{l}}=\delta_{im}
, we can proceed as follows:

\displaystyle z^{m}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{M}z^{i}\delta_{im}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{M}z^{i}\sum_{k=0}^{M}v_{ki}^{r}v_{km}^{l}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{M}v_{km}^{l}\sum_{i=0}^{M}v_{ki}^{r}z^{i}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{M}v_{km}^{l}v_{k0}^{r}(1+z)^{M-k}(1-z)^{k},

(57)

from which the following relation can be obtained:

\frac{z^{m}}{(1+z)^{M}}=\sum_{k=0}^{M}v_{km}^{l}v_{k0}^{r}\quantity(\frac{1-z}{1+z})^{k}.

(58)

Let
\xi=(1-z)/(1+z)
, Eq. (
58
) can be rewritten as

\sum_{k=0}^{M}v_{km}^{l}v_{k0}^{r}\xi^{k}=2^{-M}(1-\xi)^{m}(1+\xi)^{M-m}.

(59)

By comparing the coefficients of the terms
\xi^{k}
, the components of the left eigenvectors can be derived as

v_{km}^{l}=\frac{2^{-M}}{v_{k0}^{r}}\sum_{i=k-m}^{M-m}(-1)^{k-i}{m\choose k-i}{M-m\choose i}.

(60)

Inserting the expressions obtained in Eqs. (
56
) and (
60
) to Eq. (
49
), we can calculate
[e^{-i\pi\tilde{G}/2}]_{1(M-1)}
as follows:

\displaystyle[e^{-i\pi\tilde{G}/2}]_{1(M-1)}

\displaystyle=i\sum_{k=0}^{M}(-1)^{\bar{M}+k+1}\quantity[\dyad{v_k^r}{v_k^l}]_{1(M-1)}

\displaystyle=i\sum_{k=0}^{M}(-1)^{\bar{M}+k+1}v_{k1}^{r}v_{k(M-1)}^{l}

\displaystyle=2^{-M}(-1)^{\bar{M}+1}i\quantity[M+\sum_{k=0}^{M-1}\frac{(M-2k)^2}{M-k} {M-1 \choose k}]

\displaystyle=(-1)^{\bar{M}+1}i.

(61)

Here, we use the following facts to obtain the last line:

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{M-1}{M\choose k}

\displaystyle=2^{M}-1,

(62)

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{M-1}k{M\choose k}

\displaystyle=M2^{M-1}-M,

(63)

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{M-1}k(k-1){M\choose k}

\displaystyle=M(M-1)2^{M-2}-M(M-1).

(64)

Therefore,
|[e^{-i\pi\tilde{G}/2}]_{1(M-1)}|=1
, which completes the proof.
∎

Lemma 5
.

For any
M\geq 3
and
1\leq k\leq M
, the state transformation
\ket{k,M-k}\leftrightarrow\ket{k-1,M-k+1}
can be accomplished within time
t=\pi/[2JM\sqrt{k(M-k+1)}]
in the
U\to+\infty
limit by the following Hamiltonian:

\displaystyle H

\displaystyle=J(\hat{b}_{1}^{\dagger}\hat{b}_{2}+\hat{b}_{2}^{\dagger}\hat{b}_{1})+J\quantity[\hat b_{2}^\dagger(\hat{n}_{2}+\hat{n}_{1})\hat b_1+\hat b_{1}^\dagger(\hat{n}_{2}+\hat{n}_{1})\hat b_{2}]

\displaystyle-U\hat{n}_{2}^{2}+U(2M-2k+1)\hat{n}_{2}.

(65)

Proof.

Following the same approach in Lemma
4
, we can derive the following differential equation:

\dot{{\bf\it a}}_{t}=-iG{\bf\it a}_{t},

(66)

where the matrix
G=[g_{mn}]_{0\leq m,n\leq M}
is given by

g_{mn}=\begin{cases}JM\sqrt{m(M-m+1)}&\text{if}\penalty\ n=m-1,\\
JM\sqrt{(m+1)(M-m)}&\text{if}\penalty\ n=m+1,\\
U(M-m)(M+m-2k+1)&\text{if}\penalty\ n=m,\\
0&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(67)

The real symmetric matrix
G
has distinct real eigenvalues
\lambda_{0},\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{M}
, and their corresponding eigenvectors are denoted by
\{\ket{v_m^r}\}
.
In the
U\to+\infty
limit, we can easily show that

\displaystyle\lim_{U\to+\infty}\quantity[\lambda_{k-1}-U(M-k+1)(M-k)]

\displaystyle=JM\sqrt{k(M-k+1)},

\displaystyle\lim_{U\to+\infty}\quantity[\lambda_{k}-U(M-k)(M-k+1)]

\displaystyle=-JM\sqrt{k(M-k+1)},

\displaystyle\lim_{U\to+\infty}\quantity[\lambda_m-U(M-m)(M+m-2k+1)]

\displaystyle=0\penalty\ \forall m\neq k-1,k,

\displaystyle\lim_{U\to+\infty}\ket{v_{k-1}^r}

\displaystyle=[\underbrace{0,\dots,0}_{k-1},1/\sqrt{2},1/\sqrt{2},0,\dots,0]^{\top},

\displaystyle\lim_{U\to+\infty}\ket{v_k^r}

\displaystyle=[\underbrace{0,\dots,0}_{k-1},1/\sqrt{2},-1/\sqrt{2},0,\dots,0]^{\top},

\displaystyle\lim_{U\to+\infty}\ket{v_m^r}

\displaystyle=[\delta_{mi}]^{\top}\penalty\ \forall m\neq k-1,k.

Defining
P=(\ket{v_0^r},\dots,\ket{v_M^r})
and noting that
P^{\top}=P
and
P^{2}=\mathbb{1}
, we obtain
G=P\diag{\quantity(\lambda_0,\dots,\lambda_M)}P
.
Consequently, the matrix
e^{-iGt}
can be calculated in the
U\to+\infty
limit as
e^{-iGt}=P\diag{\quantity(e^{-i\lambda_0t},\dots,e^{-i\lambda_Mt})}P
.
We need only show that
|[e^{-iGt}]_{k(k-1)}|=|[e^{-iGt}]_{(k-1)k}|=1
for
t=\pi/[2JM\sqrt{k(M-k+1)}]
.
Indeed, we can prove as follows:

\displaystyle|[e^{-iGt}]_{(k-1)k}|

\displaystyle=\quantity|e^{-i\lambda_{k-1}t}-e^{-i\lambda_{k}t}|/2

\displaystyle=\quantity|1-e^{i(\lambda_{k-1}-\lambda_{k})t}|/2

\displaystyle=\quantity|1-e^{2iJM\sqrt{k(M-k+1)}t}|/2

\displaystyle=\quantity|1-e^{i\pi}|/2

\displaystyle=1.

(68)

This completes the proof.
∎

Lemma 8
.

The Wasserstein distance
\mathcal{W}({\bf\it x},{\bf\it y})\coloneqq\min_{\pi\in\mathcal{C}({\bf\it x},{\bf\it y})}\sum_{i,j}\pi_{ij}c_{ij}
can be expressed in the following form:

\mathcal{W}({\bf\it x},{\bf\it y})=\max_{{\bf\it\phi}}{\bf\it\phi}^{\top}({\bf\it x}-{\bf\it y}),

(71)

where the maximum is over all vectors
{\bf\it\phi}=[\phi_{1},\dots,\phi_{N}]^{\top}
satisfying
|\phi_{i}-\phi_{j}|\leq c_{ij}\penalty\ \forall i,j
.

Proof.

The constraint
\pi\in\mathcal{C}({\bf\it x},{\bf\it y})
on
\pi
in the definition of
\mathcal{W}({\bf\it x},{\bf\it y})
can be relaxed to
\pi\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{N\times N}
by adding a term
R(\pi)
to the variational expression such that
R(\pi)=0
for
\pi\in\mathcal{C}({\bf\it x},{\bf\it y})
and
R(\pi)=+\infty
otherwise.
Although there are infinitely many ways to define
R(\pi)
, we conveniently choose

R(\pi)=\max_{{\bf\it f},{\bf\it g}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}}\quantity[\vb*{f}^\top\vb*{x}+\vb*{g}^\top\vb*{y}-\sum_{i,j}(g_i+f_j)\pi_{ij}].

(72)

Consequently, the Wasserstein distance can be expressed as

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}({\bf\it x},{\bf\it y})

\displaystyle=\min_{\pi\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{N\times N}}\quantity[\sum_{i,j}\pi_{ij}c_{ij}+R(\pi)]

\displaystyle=\min_{\pi\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{N\times N}}\max_{{\bf\it f},{\bf\it g}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}}\quantity[\sum_{i,j}\pi_{ij}(c_{ij}-g_i-f_j)+\vb*{f}^\top\vb*{x}+\vb*{g}^\top\vb*{y}]

\displaystyle=\min_{\pi\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{N\times N}}\max_{{\bf\it f},{\bf\it g}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}}L(\pi,{\bf\it f},{\bf\it g}).

(73)

Here, we define
L(\pi,{\bf\it f},{\bf\it g})\coloneqq\sum_{i,j}\pi_{ij}(c_{ij}-g_{i}-f_{j})+{\bf\it f}^{\top}{\bf\it x}+{\bf\it g}^{\top}{\bf\it y}
.
Since
L(\pi,{\bf\it f},{\bf\it g})
is a linear function in its arguments, the max-min inequality is also an equality, according to von Neumann’s minimax theorem.
Therefore, we can proceed further from Eq. (
73
) as

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}({\bf\it x},{\bf\it y})

\displaystyle=\min_{\pi\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{N\times N}}\max_{{\bf\it f},{\bf\it g}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}}L(\pi,{\bf\it f},{\bf\it g})

\displaystyle=\max_{{\bf\it f},{\bf\it g}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}}\min_{\pi\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{N\times N}}L(\pi,{\bf\it f},{\bf\it g})

\displaystyle=\max_{{\bf\it f},{\bf\it g}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}}\quantity[\vb*{f}^\top\vb*{x}+\vb*{g}^\top\vb*{y}+\min_{\pi\in\mbb{R}_{\ge 0}^{N\times N}}\sum_{i,j}\pi_{ij}(c_{ij}-g_i-f_j)].

(74)

If there exist
i
and
j
such that
c_{ij}-g_{i}-f_{j}<0
, then
\min_{\pi\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{N\times N}}\sum_{i,j}\pi_{ij}(c_{ij}-g_{i}-f_{j})=-\infty
.
Therefore, we need only consider
{\bf\it f},{\bf\it g}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
such that
c_{ij}-g_{i}-f_{j}\geq 0
, or equivalently
f_{j}+g_{i}\leq c_{ij}\penalty\ \forall i,j
.
Therefore, Eq. (
74
) can be expressed as

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}({\bf\it x},{\bf\it y})

\displaystyle=\max_{{\bf\it f},{\bf\it g}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}:f_{j}+g_{i}\leq c_{ij}}\quantity[\vb*{f}^\top\vb*{x}+\vb*{g}^\top\vb*{y}+\min_{\pi\in\mbb{R}_{\ge 0}^{N\times N}}\sum_{i,j}\pi_{ij}(c_{ij}-g_i-f_j)].

(75)

Now, noticing that
\min_{\pi\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{N\times N}}\sum_{i,j}\pi_{ij}(c_{ij}-g_{i}-f_{j})=0
because
c_{ij}-g_{i}-f_{j}\geq 0
, we immediately obtain the following equality from Eq. (
75
):

\mathcal{W}({\bf\it x},{\bf\it y})=\max_{{\bf\it f},{\bf\it g}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}:f_{j}+g_{i}\leq c_{ij}}({\bf\it f}^{\top}{\bf\it x}+{\bf\it g}^{\top}{\bf\it y}).

(76)

For any fixed
{\bf\it g}
, the optimal
\tilde{{\bf\it f}}
that maximizes
({\bf\it f}^{\top}{\bf\it x}+{\bf\it g}^{\top}{\bf\it y})
under the constraints is uniquely determined as

\tilde{f}_{i}=\min_{j}(c_{ij}-g_{j})\penalty\ \forall i.

(77)

Since
c_{ij}\leq c_{kj}+c_{ik}\penalty\ \forall k
, we obtain

\tilde{f}_{i}\leq\min_{j}(c_{kj}-g_{j}+c_{ik})=\tilde{f}_{k}+c_{ik}.

(78)

This implies
|\tilde{f}_{i}-\tilde{f}_{k}|\leq c_{ik}
as
c_{ik}=c_{ki}
, which also means that the vector
\tilde{{\bf\it f}}
satisfies the 1-Lipschitz condition.
Moreover, since
c_{ii}=0
, we also have

\tilde{f}_{i}\leq-g_{i}\penalty\ \text{or}\penalty\ g_{i}\leq-\tilde{f}_{i}.

(79)

Combining these inequalities, we can upper bound the Wasserstein distance as

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}({\bf\it x},{\bf\it y})

\displaystyle=\max_{{\bf\it g}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}}(\tilde{{\bf\it f}}^{\top}{\bf\it x}+{\bf\it g}^{\top}{\bf\it y})

\displaystyle\leq\max_{{\bf\it g}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}}(\tilde{{\bf\it f}}^{\top}{\bf\it x}-\tilde{{\bf\it f}}^{\top}{\bf\it y})

\displaystyle\leq\max_{{\bf\it\phi}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}:|\phi_{i}-\phi_{j}|\leq c_{ij}}{\bf\it\phi}^{\top}({\bf\it x}-{\bf\it y}).

(80)

Let
\tilde{\pi}
be the optimal coupling that attains the Wasserstein distance, that is,
\mathcal{W}({\bf\it x},{\bf\it y})=\sum_{i,j}\tilde{\pi}_{ij}c_{ij}
.
Then, we can also prove that

\displaystyle\max_{{\bf\it\phi}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}:|\phi_{i}-\phi_{j}|\leq c_{ij}}{\bf\it\phi}^{\top}({\bf\it x}-{\bf\it y})

\displaystyle=\max_{{\bf\it\phi}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}:|\phi_{i}-\phi_{j}|\leq c_{ij}}\sum_{i,j}(\phi_{i}\tilde{\pi}_{ji}-\phi_{i}\tilde{\pi}_{ij}),

\displaystyle=\max_{{\bf\it\phi}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}:|\phi_{i}-\phi_{j}|\leq c_{ij}}\sum_{i,j}\tilde{\pi}_{ij}(\phi_{j}-\phi_{i}),

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i,j}\tilde{\pi}_{ij}c_{ij}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{W}({\bf\it x},{\bf\it y}).

(81)

From Eqs. (
80
) and (
81
), we immediately obtain the desired result (
71
).
∎
