Abstract
. The theory of Leavitt path algebras is intrinsically related, via graphs, to the theory of symbolic dynamics and C ∗ C^{*} -algebras where the major classification programs have been a domain of intense research in the last 50 years. In this article, we gather together current lines of research in the classification of Leavitt path algebras, questions, conjectures, and some of the results about them that have been obtained so far.



1.
Introduction

Leavitt path algebras are the discrete, purely algebraic version of graph
C^{*}
-algebras. The classification of these algebras has received a lot of attention, as they are closely related to the classification of graph
C^{*}
-algebras and those of symbolic dynamics. However, while graph
C^{*}
-algebras have been classified by means of
K
-theoretic invariants
[
47
]
, an analogous classification for Leavitt path algebras remains conjectural.

A
C^{*}
-algebra is
simple
if it has no non-trivial closed ideals. Simple graph
C^{*}
-algebras are completely classified by the Grothendieck group
K_{0}

[
79
,
78
,
80
]
.
For graphs with unital non-simple
C^{*}
-algebras, it became clear that one way to preserve enough information is to further consider the
K
-groups of the ideals and their subquotients and how they are related to each other via the long exact sequence of
K
-theory groups.
This is now called filtered
K
-theory; after the pioneering work of Rørdam
[
79
]
and Restorff
[
74
]
, it was further investigated by Eilers, Restorff, Ruiz and Sørensen
[
46
,
48
]
. In the major article
[
47
]
they show that filtered
K
-theory is a complete invariant for unital graph
C^{*}
-algebras.

However, at the level of Leavitt path algebras, it is not known whether
K_{0}
or other
K
-theoretical invariants classify simple Leavitt path algebras. It has now been over 15 years since the question on whether the Grothendieck group
K_{0}
classifies simple purely infinite Leavitt path algebras of finite graphs was posed
[
1
]
. Similarly it is already over a decade since it was conjectured that the graded Grothendieck group
K_{0}^{\operatorname{gr}}
is a complete invariant of the graded isomorphism class of
all
unital Leavitt path algebras
[
57
]
. Despite a flurry of activity, the questions and conjectures above remain unsolved; in particular no counterexamples to them have been found.

In this survey paper, we present in one place several results on classification which are scattered in the literature and further discuss various
K
-theoretical invariants which conjecturally could classify Leavitt path algebras. We look at classical
K
-theory, graded
K
-theory, filtered
K
-theory and bivariant
K
-theory.

2.
Preliminaries

Throughout
\mathbb{N}_{0}
denotes the monoid of non-negative integers and
\mathbb{N}
denotes the positive integers.

2.1.
Monoids

Let
M
be a commutative monoid, written additively, and
\Gamma
a group acting on
M
by monoid automorphisms. For
\alpha\in\Gamma
and
a\in M
, we denote the action of
\alpha
on
a
by
{}^{\alpha}a
.
A monoid homomorphism
\phi:M_{1}\rightarrow M_{2}
is a
\Gamma
-
monoid homomorphism
if it respects the action of
\Gamma
, i.e. if
\phi({}^{\alpha}a)={}^{\alpha}\phi(a)

\forall a\in M
. Recall a
pre-order
on a set
X
is a reflexive and transitive relation. We define the
algebraic
pre-order on the monoid
M
by
a\leq b
if
b=a+c
, for some
c\in M
.
Throughout we write
a\parallel b
if the elements
a
and
b
are not comparable. A monoid is
conical
if
a+b=0
implies
a=b=0
; it is
cancellative
if
a+b=a+c
implies
b=c
. A nonzero element
a\in M
is an
atom
if
a=b+c
implies that
b=0
or
c=0
. An element

a\in M
is called
minimal
if
b\leq a\Rightarrow a\leq b
. When
M
is conical and cancellative, an element
a\in M
is an atom if and only if it is minimal if and only if
0\not=b\leq a\Rightarrow a=b
. A monoid of interest in the paper, the talented monoid of a graph
E
, denoted by
T_{E}
(see Section
8.9
) is conical and cancellative and thus the equivalence just mentioned holds.

Throughout we assume that the group
\Gamma
is abelian. Indeed in our setting of graph algebras, most of the time this group is the group of integers
\mathbb{Z}
. We call an element
0\not=a\in M

periodic
if there exists
0\not=\alpha\in\Gamma
such that
{}^{\alpha}a=a
. If
a\in M
is not periodic, we call it
aperiodic
. We denote the orbit of the action of
\Gamma
on an element
a
by
O(a)
, so
O(a)=\{{}^{\alpha}a\mid\alpha\in\Gamma\}
.

We say that
\Gamma
acts
freely
on a monoid
M
if
{}^{\gamma}m=m
, where
0\not=m\in M
, implies that
\gamma
is the identity of the group, i.e., all the isotropy groups of the action are trivial.

A
\Gamma
-
order-ideal
of a monoid
M
is a subset
I
of
M
such that for any
\alpha,\beta\in\Gamma
,
{}^{\alpha}a+{}^{\beta}b\in I
if and only if

a,b\in I
. Equivalently, a
\Gamma
-order-ideal is a submonoid
I
of
M
which is closed under the action of
\Gamma
and is

hereditary
in the sense that
a\leq b
and
b\in I
imply
a\in I
. The set
\mathcal{L}(M)
of
\Gamma
-order-ideals of
M
forms a (complete) lattice. We say
M
is a
simple

\Gamma
-
monoid
if
M\neq 0
and the only
\Gamma
-order-ideals of
M
are
0
and
M
.

2.2.
Graphs

A (directed) graph
E
is a tuple
(E^{0},E^{1},r,s)
, consisting of sets
E^{0}
and
E^{1}
and maps
s,r:E^{1}\to E^{0}
. The elements of
E^{0}
and
E^{1}
are respectively the
vertices
and the
edges
of
E
. A graph
E
is
finite
or
countable
if both
E^{0}
and
E^{1}
are.

Standing assumption 2.2.1
.

All graphs are assumed countable.

If
e\in E^{1}
then
s(e)
and
r(e)
are the
source
and the
range
of
e
. We think of an edge as an arrow going from its source to its range.
We use the convention that a
path

\alpha
in
E
of
length

|\alpha|=n\geq 1
is
a sequence

(2.2.2)

\alpha=e_{1}e_{2}\cdots e_{n}

with
e_{i}\in E^{1}
such that

r(e_{i})=s(e_{i+1})
for
1\leq i\leq n-1
. Set
s(\alpha)=s(e_{1})
, and
r(\alpha)=r(e_{n})
. A vertex is viewed as a path in
E
of length
0
.
For
n\geq 2
, we define
E^{n}
to be the set of paths in
E
of length
n
and
\operatorname{Path}(E)=\bigcup_{n\geq 1}E^{n}
, the set of all paths in
E
.

We shall also have occasion to consider
infinite paths

e_{1}e_{2}\cdots
and
bi-infinite paths

\cdots e_{-1}e_{0}e_{1}\cdots
, defined in the obvious way. The
support
of a path
\alpha
is the set
\operatorname{supp}(\alpha)
of the sources and ranges of its edges.
We say that a vertex
u

connects to a vertex

v
, and write
u\geq v
, if there is a path
\alpha
with
s(\alpha)=u
and
r(\alpha)=v
. We say that
u

connects to a path

\alpha
if there exists
v\in\operatorname{supp}(\alpha)
such that
u
connects to
v
.

A
closed path based
at a vertex
v\in E^{0}
is a path
\alpha
with
|\alpha|\geq 1
such that
v=s(\alpha)=r(\alpha)
. A
cycle
is a closed path (
2.2.2
) such that
s(e_{i})\neq s(e_{j})
for every
i\neq j
. A
loop
is a cycle of length one. We say a graph is
acyclic
if it has no cycles.

Let
v\in E^{0}
and
c
the cardinal of the set
s^{-1}(v)
of edges it emits. We say that
v
is a
sink
if
c=0
, an
infinite emitter
if
c
is infinite, and
regular
if
1\leq c<\infty
. We write
\operatorname{sink}(E)
,
\operatorname{inf}(E)
and
\operatorname{reg}(E)
for the sets of sinks, infinite emitters and regular vertices. Nonregular vertices are called
singular
; put
\operatorname{sing}(E)=E^{0}\setminus\operatorname{reg}(E)
. A graph is
regular
if
E^{0}=\operatorname{reg}(E)
and
row-finite

\operatorname{inf}(E)=\emptyset
. A vertex
v\in E^{0}
is a
source
if
r^{-1}(v)=\emptyset
; we write
\operatorname{sour}(E)
for the set of all sources of
E
. A graph
E
is
essential
if it is regular and
\operatorname{sour}(E)=\emptyset
.

There is a graph move called
source removal
, consisting of removing a source
v
as well as all edges
e\in s^{-1}\{v\}
. Applying source removal a finite number of times to a finite regular graph
E
, yields an essential graph. Other moves that appear in symbolic dynamics are reviewed in Section
6
.

The (reduced)
adjacency matrix
of a graph
E
is the matrix

A_{E}\in\mathbb{Z}^{\operatorname{reg}(E)\times E^{0}}
with entries

(2.2.3)

(A_{E})(v,w)=\#\{e\in E^{1}\,\colon\,s(e)=v,\,r(e)=w\}.

Put

(2.2.4)

I\in\mathbb{Z}^{E^{0}\times\operatorname{reg}(E)},\,\,I_{v,w}=\delta_{v,w}.

Observe that
I
results from the identity matrix with the columns corresponding to the singular vertices removed.
The matrices
A^{t}_{E}
and
I
will be used to calculate the Grothendieck group
K_{0}
of the Leavitt path algebra of
E
(see
5.2.4
).
For row-finite
E
, we shall also have occasion to consider the
unreduced
adjacency matrix
A^{\prime}_{E}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{E^{0}\times E^{0}}
with entries

(A^{\prime}_{E})_{v,w}=\#\{e\in E^{1}\,\colon\,s(e)=v,\,r(e)=w\}.

Remark that
A^{\prime}_{E}=A_{E}
if and only if
E
is regular.

Remark 2.2.5
.

As defined above, the adjacency matrix
A_{E}
is a function
\operatorname{reg}(E)\times E^{0}\to\mathbb{N}_{0}
. It does not depend on any ordering of the vertices of
E
. However if we want to represent it graphically, we need to choose an order, and we do it so that the regular vertices appear first.

Remark 2.2.6
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
A\in\operatorname{\mathbb{M}}_{n}(\mathbb{N}_{0})
. Let
E(A)
be the graph with vertices
\{1,\dots,n\}
and exactly
A_{i,j}
edges with source
i
and range
j
. It is straightforward to check that, for the given order of the vertices, we have
A^{\prime}_{E(A)}=A
and that there is a graph isomorphism
E(A^{\prime}_{E})\cong E
.

\mathbb{Z}
and when we describe the graded Grothendieck group of a Leavitt path algebra.

covering graph

\overline{E}

of

\displaystyle s(e_{n})=s(e)_{n-1},\qquad\text{ and }\qquad r(e_{n})=r(e)_{n}.

\{r(e):e\in E^{1}\text{~and~}s(e)=v\}\subseteq H\Rightarrow v\in H.

(2.3.1)

||a||^{2}=||a^{*}a||\,\,(\forall\,a\in A).

For example the algebra
\mathcal{B}(H)
of bounded operators on a Hilbert space

-algebra, as is any closed
\ast
-subalgebra of

. In fact the Gelfand-Naimark theorem

*Theorem I.9.12 says that any

-algebra is (isometrically)
\ast

\ast

for some

.

Example 2.3.2
.

Let
\mathfrak{B}
be a

-algebra and

that vanish at infinity, equipped with pointwise addition, multiplication and involution, and the supremum norm. Then

3.
Leavitt path algebras

The main focus of this survey article is on the classification of certain combinatorial algebras arising from directed graphs, namely, Leavitt path algebras.

Let
\mathsf{k}
be a field and
n\geq 2
. In
[
69
]
Leavitt considered the free unital associative
\mathsf{k}
-algebra
L_{n}=L_{n}(\mathsf{k})
generated by symbols
\{x_{i},x^{*}_{i}\mid 1\leq i\leq n\}
subject to the following relations

(3.1)

x^{*}_{i}x_{j}=\delta_{ij},\text{ for all }1\leq i,j\leq n,\text{ and }\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}x^{*}_{i}=1.

The relations guarantee that the right
L_{n}
-module homomorphism

(3.2)

\displaystyle\phi:L_{n}

\displaystyle\longrightarrow L_{n}^{n}

\displaystyle a

\displaystyle\mapsto(x^{*}_{1}a,x^{*}_{2}a,\dots,x^{*}_{n}a)

has an inverse

(3.3)

\displaystyle\psi:L_{n}^{n}

\displaystyle\longrightarrow L_{n}

\displaystyle(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})

\displaystyle\mapsto x_{1}a_{1}+\dots+x_{n}a_{n},

so
L_{n}\cong L_{n}^{n}
as right
L_{n}
-modules. Leavitt showed that

n
is the smallest
m\geq 2
such that
L_{n}\cong L_{n}^{m}
, and that
L_{n}
is a simple ring. More precisely, he showed that for any nonzero
x\in L_{n}
there exist
a,b\in L_{n}
such that

(3.4)

axb=1.

Note, however, that
L_{n}
is not a division ring or even a domain; in fact we have

(3.5)

x_{2}^{*}x_{1}=0.

It turned out the relations (
3.1
) are quite ubiquitous in mathematics and thus these rings have fascinating properties and appear in many areas.
For example Cuntz’ algebra
\mathcal{O}_{n}
, introduced in
[
40
]
, is a
C^{*}
-algebra completion of
L_{n}(\mathbb{C})
. Cuntz and Krieger
[
38
]
introduced the
C^{*}
-algebra
C^{*}(E)
of a finite graph
E
, so that
\mathcal{O}_{n}=C^{*}(\mathcal{R}_{n})
is the algebra of the graph consisting of a single vertex and
n
loops, sometimes called the
rose of
n
-petals
. Pictorially,
\mathcal{R}_{n}
is the following graph

\textstyle{\bullet\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{x_{1}}
\scriptstyle{x_{2}}
\scriptstyle{x_{3}}
\scriptstyle{x_{n}}

The Cuntz-Krieger construction was later generalized, first to row-finite graphs
[
68
]
and then to all
[
44
]
.
The Leavitt path algebra
L_{\mathsf{k}}(E)
over a field
\mathsf{k}
was introduced
[
4
,
11
]
as a purely algebraic counterpart to
C^{*}(E)
. Leavitt’s algebra is recovered as
L_{\mathsf{k}}(\mathcal{R}_{n})=L_{n}(\mathsf{k})
.

We briefly recall the construction of Leavitt path algebras and refer the reader to
[
2
]
for a comprehensive treatment.

Let
E
be a graph and
\mathsf{k}
a field. The
Cohn path algebra

C_{\mathsf{k}}(E)
of
E
is the quotient of the free associative
k
-algebra generated by the set
E^{0}\cup E^{1}\cup\{e^{*}\;|\;e\in E^{1}\}
, subject to the relations:

(0)

v\cdot w=\delta_{v,w}
for
v,w\in E^{0}
;

(1)

s(e)\cdot e=e=e\cdot r(e)
for
e\in E^{1}
;

(2)

r(e)\cdot e^{*}=e^{*}=e^{*}\cdot s(e)
for
e\in E^{1}
;

(3)

e^{*}\cdot f=\delta_{e,f}r(e)
for
e,f\in E^{1}
.

The elements of the form
e^{*}
(
e\in E^{1}
) are called
ghost edges
.
Suppose an involution
\ast:\mathsf{k}\to\mathsf{k}
is given (e.g. the identity, or, if
\mathsf{k}\subset\mathbb{C}
, complex conjugation). Then
C_{\mathsf{k}}(E)
is a
*
-algebra with the involution that fixes the vertices and maps
e
to
e^{*}
for
e\in E^{1}
. Throughout for a path

\alpha=e_{1}e_{2}\cdots e_{n}
, we write
\alpha^{*}=e_{n}^{*}e_{n-1}^{*}\cdots e_{1}^{*}
.

Let
v\in\operatorname{reg}(E)
; put

(3.6)

q_{v}:=v-\sum_{v\in s^{-1}(v)}ee^{*}.

Consider the ideal
\mathcal{K}(E)=\big\langle q_{v}\;|\;v\in\operatorname{reg}(E)\big\rangle
of
C_{\mathsf{k}}(E)
.
The
Leavitt path algebra

L_{\mathsf{k}}(E)
of
E
over the field
\mathsf{k}
(see
[
2
]
*Definition 1.2.3) is the quotient algebra
C_{\mathsf{k}}(E)/\mathcal{K}(E)
. Hence there is an extension of
\mathsf{k}
-algebras

(3.7)

\displaystyle\begin{CD}0@>{}>{}>\mathcal{K}(E)@>{l}>{}>C_{\mathsf{k}}(E)@>{p}>{}>L_{\mathsf{k}}(E)@>{}>{}>0.\end{CD}

One may also consider the Cohn and Leavitt path algebras
C_{R}(E)
and
L_{R}(E)
over any unital ring
R
, defined by the same generators and relations as above. Remark that the algebra
C_{R}(E)
is unital if and only if
L_{R}(E)
is, if and only if
E^{0}
is finite, in which case
1_{E}=\sum_{v\in E^{0}}v
is the unit element.

Throughout this paper, if
\mathsf{k}
is an arbitrary field, we simply write
C(E)
instead of
C_{\mathsf{k}}(E)
and
L(E)
instead of
L_{\mathsf{k}}(E)
. We keep the subscript when we want to emphasize the ground field or the fact that the ground ring under consideration is not a field.

The algebras above are naturally equipped with a
\mathbb{Z}
-grading (the canonical grading) where the vertices have degree zero, the edges have degree
1
and the ghost edges have degree
-1
(see
[
2
]
*Section 2.1).
This graded structure plays an important role in the theory of Leavitt path algebras as well as in the current paper. We recall one fact on the graded structure of these algebras, showcasing how germane is the
\mathbb{Z}
-grading of Leavitt path algebras.

Let
E
be a row-finite graph. Write
\mathscr{L}^{\operatorname{gr}}\big(L(E)\big)
for the lattice of graded (two-sided) ideals of
L(E)
. There is a lattice isomorphism between the set
\mathcal{T}_{E}
of hereditary saturated subsets of
E^{0}
and the set
\mathscr{L}^{\operatorname{gr}}\big(L(E)\big)
(
[
2
]
*Theorem 2.5.8). The correspondence maps a hereditary saturated set of vertices to the two-sided ideal it generates:

(3.8)

\displaystyle\Phi:\mathcal{T}_{E}

\displaystyle\longrightarrow\mathscr{L}^{\operatorname{gr}}\big(L(E)\big),

\displaystyle H

\displaystyle\longmapsto\langle H\rangle.

Remark 3.9
.

There is also a structure theorem for graded ideals of
L(E)
that holds for an arbitrary, not necessarily row-finite graph
E
, see
[
2
]
*Theorem 2.5.8.

4.
Elliott’s
K
-theoretic classification of ultramatricial algebras

4.1.
The Grothendieck group

Let
A
be a ring; write
\operatorname{Idem}(A)
for the set of idempotent elements in
A
. We say that two elements
p,q\in\operatorname{Idem}(A)
are
orthogonal
, and write
p\perp q
, if
pq=qp=0
; in that case
p+q
is again an idempotent. We say that
e,f\in\operatorname{Idem}(A)
are
Murray-von Neumann equivalent
and write
e\sim f
, if there are elements
x\in eAf
,
y\in fAe
such that
xy=e
and
yx=f
. Borrowing terminology from
[
34
]
*Capítulo 7, Sección 1, we say that the ring
A
has
enough room
if given
p,q\in\operatorname{Idem}(A)
there are
p^{\prime},q^{\prime}\in\operatorname{Idem}(A)
with
p^{\prime}\sim p
,
q^{\prime}\sim q
and
p^{\prime}\perp q^{\prime}
. For example, if
R
is unital, then the non-unital ring

\operatorname{\mathbb{M}}_{\infty}(R)=\bigcup_{n\geq 1}\operatorname{\mathbb{M}}_{n}(R)

of finite matrices of arbitrary size with coefficients in
R
has enough room. If
A
has enough room then the quotient set
\operatorname{Idem}(A)/\sim
becomes a monoid under orthogonal sum: to add the classes
[p]
and
[q]
, one finds orthogonal representatives
p^{\prime}\in[p]
and
q^{\prime}\in[q]
and sets
[p]+[q]=[p^{\prime}+q^{\prime}]
. In particular, for
\operatorname{Idem}_{\infty}(R)=\operatorname{Idem}(\operatorname{\mathbb{M}}_{\infty}(R))
,
\mathcal{V}(R)=\operatorname{Idem}_{\infty}(R)/\sim
is an abelian monoid. An element
p\in\operatorname{Idem}_{\infty}R
determines an
R
-endomorphism of
R^{(\mathbb{N})}=\bigoplus_{n\geq 1}R
and its image
pR^{(\mathbb{N})}
is a finitely generated projective right
R
-module.
The assignment
p\to pR^{(\mathbb{N})}
induces an isomorphism of monoids

(4.1.1)

\mathcal{V}(R)\overset{\sim}{\longrightarrow}\mathbb{P}^{\mathrm{iso}}(R)

with the set of isomorphism classes of finitely generated projective right
R
-modules, equipped with the direct sum. The inclusion of the category of abelian groups into that of abelian monoids has a left adjoint
M\mapsto M^{+}
, called
group completion
. The
Grothendieck group
of
R
is
K_{0}(R)=\mathcal{V}(R)^{+}
. Thus
K_{0}(R)
comes equipped with a monoid homomorphism

\iota:\mathcal{V}(R)\to K_{0}(R)
that is universal in the sense that if
G
is another abelian group and
f:\mathcal{V}(R)\to G
is a monoid homomorphism, then there exists a unique group homomorphism
\bar{f}
making the following diagram commute

\textstyle{\mathcal{V}(R)\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\iota}
\scriptstyle{f}
\textstyle{K_{0}(R)\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\bar{f}}
\textstyle{G}

Consider the submonoid
K_{0}(R)_{+}=\iota(\mathcal{V}(R))
; define a pre-order between elements of
K_{0}(R)
by
x\leq y
if
y-x\in K_{0}(R)_{+}
. This pre-order is compatible with the group structure in the sense that
x\leq y\Rightarrow x+z\leq y+z
. Thus
(K_{0}(R),\leq)
is a pre-ordered group. The class
[1]
of the unit (which corresponds to that of the free module
R
of rank one under the isomorphism (
4.1.1
)) is an
order unit
of
K_{0}(R)
in the sense that for any
x\in K_{0}(R)
there exists
n\in\mathbb{N}
such that
x\leq n[1]=[I_{n}]
; here
I_{n}\in M_{n}(R)
is the identity matrix. Thus
K_{0}(R)
is a
pointed pre-ordered
abelian group.

Remark 4.1.2
.

We saw in Subsection
2.1
that any abelian monoid is canonically pre-ordered. In particular,
\mathcal{V}(R)
is pre-ordered and
[1]
is an order unit with respect to this pre-order. The canonical monoid homomorphism
\iota:\mathcal{V}(R)\to K_{0}(R)
is a morphism of pre-ordered monoids.

Remark 4.1.3
.

Let
R,S
be Morita equivalent unital rings and let
{}_{R}P_{S}
be a bimodule implementing the equivalence. Then
-\otimes_{R}P
induces an isomorphism

\mathcal{V}(R)\cong\mathbb{P}^{\mathrm{iso}}(R)\overset{\sim}{\longrightarrow}\mathbb{P}^{\mathrm{iso}}(S)\cong\mathcal{V}(S)
and therefore an isomorphism
K_{0}(R)\overset{\sim}{\longrightarrow}K_{0}(S)
. In particular,
K_{0}
is Morita invariant. Similarly, it is straightforward to see that if
A
and
B
are unital rings, then the canonical map
\mathcal{V}(A\times B)\to\mathcal{V}(A)\times\mathcal{V}(B)
is an isomorphism. Hence
K_{0}
commutes with finite sums. Let now
\{\sigma_{n}:R_{n}\to R_{n+1}\,\colon\,n\geq 1\}
be an inductive system of unital ring homomorphisms, and let
R=\varinjlim_{n}R_{n}
. It is straightfoward to show that
\mathcal{V}(R)=\varinjlim_{n}\mathcal{V}(R_{n})
. It follows from this and the universal property of group completion that
K_{0}(R)=\varinjlim_{n}K_{0}(R_{n})
; thus
K_{0}
commutes with inductive limits.

Example 4.1.4
.

Let
R
be a unital ring. There is a canonical map
\mathbb{N}_{0}\to\mathbb{P}^{\mathrm{iso}}(R)
,
n\mapsto[R^{n}]
. This map is an isomorphism, for instance, when
R
is a division ring, a local ring or a PID; in particular
K_{0}(R)=\mathbb{Z}
in all these cases. Moreover by Remark
4.1.3
, in all these cases, if
r,n_{1},\dots,n_{r}\in\mathbb{N}
, then

K_{0}(\operatorname{\mathbb{M}}_{n_{1}}(R)\oplus\dots\oplus\operatorname{\mathbb{M}}_{n_{r}}(R))=\mathbb{Z}^{r}
.

Remark 4.1.5
.

The definition of the Grothendieck group extends to not necessarily unital rings as follows. The
unitalization
of a ring
A
is
\tilde{A}=A\oplus\mathbb{Z}
equipped with the product
(a,n)(b,m)=(ab+nb+ma,nm)
. Remark that
\tilde{A}
is a unital ring and that the projection
\pi:\tilde{A}\to\mathbb{Z}
is a unital ring homomorphism, so that
K_{0}(\pi)
is defined. Set
K_{0}(A)={\rm Ker}(K_{0}(\pi))
. One shows that this definition agrees with the previous one in the unital case, and that
K_{0}
is split-exact, so that if
A
embeds as a two-sided ideal in a ring
R
and the projection
p:R\to R/A
is split by a ring homomorphism, then
K_{0}(A)\cong{\rm Ker}(K_{0}(p))
(see
[
76
]
*Chapter 1, Section 5).
It follows from this that
K_{0}
still commutes with inductive limits when considered as a functor on the category of nonunital rings and ring homomorphisms. Hence, by Example
4.1.4
, if

R
is a division ring or a PID and
n_{1},n_{2},\dots\in\mathbb{N}
is an infinite sequence, then

K_{0}\big(\bigoplus_{i=1}^{\infty}\operatorname{\mathbb{M}}_{n_{i}}(R)\big)=\mathbb{Z}^{(\mathbb{N})}.

4.2.
Ultramatricial algebras

A
\mathsf{k}
-algebra
R
is
matricial
if there exist
r,n_{1},\dots n_{r}\in\mathbb{N}
and an isomorphism of
\mathsf{k}
-algebras

R\cong\bigoplus_{i=1}^{r}\operatorname{\mathbb{M}}_{n_{i}}(\mathsf{k}).

We say that an algebra
R
is
ultramatricial
if there is a direct system
\{\sigma_{n}:R_{n}\to R_{n+1}\}
of matricial algebras and not-necessarily unital algebra homomorphisms such that
R\cong\varinjlim_{n}R_{n}
. An ultramatricial algebra is unital if and only if it can be written as an inductive limit of matricial algebras and unital homomorphisms.

Remark 4.2.1
.

Ultramatricial algebras are a particular case of
locally matricial
algebras
[
2
]
*Definition 2.6.19. An algebra
A
is locally matricial if there exists an upwards directed partially ordered set
I
and a system
\{\sigma_{i,j}:A_{i}\to A_{j}\,\colon\,i\leq j\in I\}
of matricial algebra homomorphisms such that
A=\varinjlim_{I}A_{i}
. If
A
admits such a description for some countable index set
I
, then
A
is ultramatricial. This follows from the fact that any countable upwards directed set admits a (countable) cofinal chain.

Example 4.2.2
.

Let
E
be a countable graph. Then
L(E)
is matricial if and only if
E
is finite and acyclic
[
2
]
*Theorem 2.6.17, in which case it is a direct sum of matrix algebras indexed by
\operatorname{sink}(E)
, and thus
K_{0}(L(E))=\mathbb{Z}^{(\operatorname{sink}(E))}
. If
E
is infinite acyclic then
L(E)
is ultramatricial by
[
2
]
*Proposition 2.6.20 and Remark
4.2.1
. For example, if
E
is row-finite, then the covering graph
\overline{E}
of (
2.2.7
) is acyclic. Let
E_{n}
be the graph with

E_{n}^{0}=\{v_{i}\,\colon\,-n-1\leq i\leq n\},\quad E_{n}^{1}=\{e_{i}\,\colon\,-n\leq i\leq n\}

equipped with the restriction of the source and range maps of
\overline{E}
. Then
L(\overline{E})=\varinjlim_{n}L(E_{n})
. Every vertex of
E_{n}
connects to a sink and

\operatorname{sink}(E_{n})=\{v_{i}\,\colon\,v\in\operatorname{sink}(E)\,\,-n-1\leq i\leq n\}\sqcup\{v_{n}\,\colon\,v\in\operatorname{reg}(E)\}.

Hence by Example
4.1.4
and Remark

,

K_{0}(L(E_{n}))=\mathbb{Z}^{(\bigsqcup_{i=-n-1}^{n}\operatorname{sink}(E)_{i})}\oplus\mathbb{Z}^{(\operatorname{reg}(E))}.

One checks that the map

is the inclusion on the first summand and left multiplication by
A_{E}^{t}
on the second. Thus if
E
is regular,
K_{0}(L(\overline{E}))
is the inductive limit of the system

(4.2.3)

\textstyle{\mathbb{Z}^{E^{0}}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{A_{E}^{t}}
\textstyle{\mathbb{Z}^{E^{0}}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{A_{E}^{t}}
\textstyle{\dots}

For example, setting
E=\mathcal{R}_{n}
we obtain that
K_{0}(L(\overline{\mathcal{R}_{n}}))=\mathbb{Z}[1/n]
.

Example 4.2.4
.

Let
E
be a row-finite graph and
L(E)_{0}
the degree-zero component of
L(E)
. For each
v\in E^{0}
and
i\geq 0
, let
E^{i}v
be the set of all paths of length

. The subspace of

spanned by those

with
\alpha,\beta\in E^{i}
and

is a subalgebra isomorphic to
\operatorname{\mathbb{M}}_{E^{i}v}(\mathsf{k})
. The subspace
L(E)_{0,n}\subset L(E)_{0}
generated by all

with
\alpha,\beta\in E^{i}
, with

and

is also a subalgebra and we have an isomorphism

Hence by Example

,

K_{0}(L(E)_{0,n})=\left(\bigoplus_{v\in\operatorname{sink}(E)}\mathbb{Z}^{n+1}\right)\oplus\mathbb{Z}^{(\operatorname{reg}(E))}.

At the

level, the map induced by the inclusion

is multiplication by

on

and the inclusion on the summand corresponding to sinks. In particular, if
E
is regular,

is the inductive limit of the system (

). Thus for regular

.

5.
Algebraic Kirchberg-Phillips problem

5.1.
Simplicity and pure infinite simplicity

Let
R
be a nonzero unital ring. We say that
R
is
simple
if for every nonzero element
x\in R
, there exist
n\geq 1
and
a,b\in R^{n}
such that

\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}xb_{i}=1.

For example, any division ring is simple. We say that
R
is
simple purely infinite
(SPI) if it is not a division ring and for every nonzero
x\in R
, there exist
a,b\in R
such that

axb=1.

Remark 5.1.1
.

Simplicity and pure infinite simplicity are also defined for nonunital rings, see
[
2
]
*page 68 and Definition 3.1.8.

Theorem 5.1.2

(Ara, Goodearl and Pardo
[
10
]
*Proposition 2.1 and Corollary 2.2)
.

Let
R
be an SPI unital ring. Then the subsemigroup
\mathcal{V}(R)^{*}=\mathcal{V}(R)\setminus\{0\}\subset\mathcal{V}(R)
is a group, and the monoid homomorphism
\mathcal{V}(R)\to\mathcal{V}(R)^{*}
defined as the identity on
\mathcal{V}(R)^{*}
is a group completion. In particular
K_{0}(R)=\mathcal{V}(R)^{*}
.

Remark 5.1.3
.

It follows from Theorem
5.1.2
that if
R
is SPI, then
K_{0}(R)_{+}=K_{0}(R)
. In particular, if
G
is any partially ordered abelian group, then any group homomorphism
G\to K_{0}(R)
is order-preserving.

Example 5.1.4
.

By (
3.4
), if
n\geq 2
, then the Leavitt algebra
L_{n}
is SPI. In general, the simplicity and pure infinite simplicity of a Leavitt path algebra

over a field

are characterized in terms of certain graphic conditions on
E

[
2
]
*Theorems 2.9.7 and 3.1.10. To explain them, we need some notation and vocabulary.
An edge
f
is an
exit
of a cycle
\alpha=e_{1}\cdots e_{n}
if there exists
1\leq i\leq n
such that
s(f)=s(e_{i})
and
f\neq e_{i}
.
The algebra
L(E)
is simple if and only if
E
satisfies the following two conditions
[
2
]
*Theorem 2.9.7

(1)

Every cycle of
E
has an exit.

(2)

Every vertex in
E
connects to every singular vertex (i.e., every sink or infinite emitter) and every infinite path.

By
[
2
]

5.3.
Kasparov’s bivariant
K
-theory

Let
C^{*}
be the category of separable
C^{*}
-algebras and

*
-homomorphisms. A
*
-homomorphism
H:\mathfrak{A}\to\mathfrak{B}[0,1]
is called a
homotopy
between
\operatorname{ev}_{0}\circ H
and
\operatorname{ev}_{1}\circ H

*

. Let
\mathcal{C}
be a category. A functor

(5.3.1)

is

if it sends homotopic maps to the same map.

Let
\ell^{2}
be the Hilbert space of
2
-summable sequences of complex numbers and
\mathcal{B}=\mathcal{B}(\ell^{2})
the
C^{*}
-algebra of bounded linear operators. The ideal
\mathcal{K}\vartriangleleft\mathcal{B}
of
compact operators
is the norm closure of the ideal of finite rank operators
\ell^{2}\to\ell^{2}
. For example
\epsilon_{i,j}:\ell^{2}\to\ell^{2}
,
\epsilon_{i,j}(a)_{s}=\delta_{s,i}a_{j}
is an operator of finite rank, and thus a compact operator. The algebra
\mathcal{K}
is an example of a
nuclear

C^{*}
-algebra; for any
\mathfrak{A}\in C^{*}
, there is a unique norm
||\,||
on the algebraic tensor product
\mathfrak{A}\otimes_{\mathbb{C}}\mathcal{K}
satisfying (
2.3.1
) and such that
||x\otimes a||=||x||\cdot||a||
for all
x\in\mathcal{K}
and
a\in\mathfrak{A}
. For general
\mathfrak{A},\mathfrak{B}
there may be infinitely many such norms; we write
\mathfrak{A}\overset{\sim}{\otimes}\mathfrak{B}
for the completion of
\mathfrak{A}\otimes_{\mathbb{C}}\mathfrak{B}
with respect to the smallest one. We have a
\ast
-homomorphism
\iota_{\mathfrak{A}}:\mathfrak{A}\to\mathcal{K}\overset{\sim}{\otimes}\mathfrak{A}
,
\iota_{\mathfrak{A}}(a)=\epsilon_{1,1}\otimes a
. A functor (
5.3.1
) is

if
F(\iota_{\mathfrak{A}})
is an isomorphism for every
\mathfrak{A}
.

An
extension

of
C^{*}
-algebras is a sequence of
*
-homomorphisms

(5.3.2)

0\to\mathfrak{A}\to\mathfrak{B}\to\mathfrak{C}\to 0

which is exact as a sequence of vector spaces and which admits a completely positive splitting (for a definition of completely positive see
[
42
]
*p. 6). If (
5.3.1
) takes values in a triangulated category
\mathcal{C}
, and
C\mapsto C[-n]
is the
n

\mathcal{C}
, then

is

if it sends any extension (

F(\mathfrak{C})[+1]\overset{\partial}{\longrightarrow}F(\mathfrak{A})\to F(\mathfrak{B})\to F(\mathfrak{C}).

The map
\partial
is required to depend naturally on (

) and to satisfy certain additional compatibility conditions.

There exist a triangulated category

, whose objects are those of
C^{*}
, and a functor

is universal initial with respect to the latter properties, which roughly means that if (
5.3.1
) is another homotopy invariant, stable and excisive functor, then there is a unique triangulated functor

making the following diagram commute

\textstyle{C^{*}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{k}
\scriptstyle{F}
\textstyle{KK\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\bar{F}}
\textstyle{\mathcal{C}}

We write

KK_{n}(\mathfrak{A},\mathfrak{B})=\hom_{KK}(k(\mathfrak{A}),k(\mathfrak{B})[n]),\,\,KK(\mathfrak{A},\mathfrak{B})=KK_{0}(\mathfrak{A},\mathfrak{B}).

A major feature of Kasparov’s theory is that setting the first variable equal to
\mathbb{C}
recovers topological
K
-theory; we have

(5.3.3)

In this bivariant context, the Bott periodicity theorem says that there is a natural isomorphism
k(\mathfrak{A})[+1]\cong k(\mathfrak{A})[-1]
for all
\mathfrak{A}\in C^{*}
. For more on

[

5.6.
Flow invariants and the Cuntz splice

Theorem

shows that the original argument of Theorem

does not adapt to the purely algebraic case. Rørdam’s proof of Corollary

for finite graphs uses a different argument which at least in part, translates nicely to the algebraic case.

The starting point of the argument is Theorem
5.6.1
below. To state it, we need some vocabulary.

Whenever a graph
E
has vertices
v,w
such that there is a single edge

with

and

, one can replace
E
by a graph

where

and

are identified and

has been contracted to the zero length path

and its inverse an

and

, and their inverses,

and

[
5
]

along with the related notion of strong shift equivalence. We say that two finite essential graphs

and
F
are

if one can get from one to the other via a finite sequence of splittings, amalgamations, expansions and contractions. The
trivial flow class
is the flow equivalence class of the graph with a single vertex and no edges; its elements are the graphs consisting of a single closed path. A graph
E
is
strongly connected
if for any
v,w\in E^{0}
there is a path
\alpha
with
s(\alpha)=v
and
r(\alpha)=w
.
Remark that an essential graph that is not in the trivial flow class is strongly connected if and only if it is SPI.

(Franks
[

]

Let

and

(1)

and

(2)

\mathfrak{B}\mathfrak{F}(E)\cong\mathfrak{B}\mathfrak{F}(F)
and
\det(I-A_{E})=\det(I-A_{F})
.

Here is the first application of Franks’ theorem to the classification of Leavitt path algebras.

5.7.
Bivariant algebraic

-theory

Let

be a field and let

-algebra homomorphisms. An

is an algebra homomorphism

such that

and

, for all

. We say that

are

and write

if there are a finite sequence of algebra homomorphisms

and elementary homotopies

Let

be the category of associative algebras over
\mathsf{k}
and
\mathsf{k}
-linear ring homomorphisms,
\mathcal{C}
a category, and

(5.7.1)

\mathcal{H}:{\mathrm{Alg}_{\mathsf{k}}}\to\mathcal{C}

a functor. Call
\mathcal{H}

homotopy invariant
if it sends homotopic maps to the same map. Let
\iota_{A}:A\to\operatorname{\mathbb{M}}_{\infty}(A)

is called

if
\mathcal{H}(\iota_{A})
is an isomorphism for all

. If
\mathcal{H}
is matricially stable, then
\mathcal{H}
sends

[
33
]
*Section 2.2. An

of algebras is a sequence of algebra homomorphisms

which is exact as a sequence of vector spaces. The functor (

) is

if

is triangulated and
\mathcal{H}
maps extensions to distinguished triangles. There exists a triangulated category

, whose objects are those of

, and a functor

Furthermore setting the first variable equal to the ground ring, recovers homotopy algebraic

-theory

-regular then

6.
Symbolic dynamics: conjugacy and (strong) shift equivalence

The central objects in the theory of symbolic dynamics are the shifts of finite type, also called topological Markov chains. A general reference for this section is
[
70
]
. In this article, by a
dynamical system
we understand a pair
(X,\sigma)
consisting of a Hausdorff topological space
X
and a homeomorphism
\sigma
. Two dynamical systems
(X,\sigma)
and
(Y,\tau)
are
conjugate
if there is an homeomorphism
f:X\to Y
such that
\tau\circ f=f\circ\sigma
.

Let
E
be a finite essential graph and give
E^{1}
the discrete topology. Then
(E^{1})^{\mathbb{Z}}
with the product topology is Hausdorff and compact, by Tychonov’s theorem. The subset
X_{E}\subset(E^{1})^{\mathbb{Z}}
of all bi-infinite paths in
E
is closed, and therefore a compact Hausdorff space. The map
\sigma:X_{E}\to X_{E}
,
\sigma(\alpha)_{n}=\alpha_{n+1}
that shifts a path to the left is continuous, and so the pair
(X_{E},\sigma)
is a dynamical system; it is called
an edge shift
. Although we will not define shifts of finite type, let us mention that any shift of finite type is conjugate to an edge shift.

As a consequence of the decomposition theorem for conjugacies
[
70
]
*Corollary 7.1.5 two shifts of finite type
X_{E}
and
X_{F}
are conjugate if and only if
F
can be obtained from
E
by a series of graphs moves, called
in-splittings
and
out-splittings
, and their inverses, called in and out
amalgamations
. A theorem of Williams further characterizes the existence of a conjugacy between
X_{E}
and
X_{F}
in terms of the adjacency matrices of
E
and
F
. Two square nonnegative integer matrices
A
and
B
are called
elementary shift equivalent
, and denoted by
A\sim_{ES}B
, if there are nonnegative matrices
R
and
S
such that
A=RS
and
B=SR
.
The equivalence relation
\sim_{S}
on square nonnegative integer matrices generated by elementary shift equivalence is called
strong shift equivalence
.

Theorem 6.1

(Williams
[
85
]
)
.

Let
A
and
B
be two square nonnegative integer matrices and let
E
and
F
be their associated graphs. Assume that
E
and
F
are essential. Then
A
is strongly shift equivalent to
B
if and only if
F
can be obtained from
E
by a sequence of in-splittings, out-splittings, and their inverses.

In the same article
[
85
]
Williams also introduced the weaker notion of shift equivalence, defined as follows. The square nonnegative integer matrices
A
and
B
are called
shift equivalent
if there exist
\ell\geq 1
and nonnegative matrices
R
and
S
such that

(6.2)

\displaystyle AR=RB,\,SA=BS,

(6.3)

\displaystyle RS=A^{\ell},\,SR=B^{\ell}.

Observe that for
\ell=1
, conditions (
6.2
) and (
6.3
) hold if and only if
A\sim_{ES}B
. It follows that strong shift equivalence implies shift equivalence.

The problem of deciding whether the converse is true remained open for a long time and was known as Williams’ conjecture. It was disproved by the counterexamples of Kim and Roush
[
64
,
65
]
.

The following question remains open at the time of writing.

Question 6.4
.

Is there a list of graph moves so that two graphs have shift equivalent adjacency matrices if and only if one can transform one into the other by a finite sequence of moves in the list?

An advantage of shift equivalence over strong shift equivalence is that it can be detected by a simple invariant, Krieger’s dimension group, which we will introduce next.

A nonnegative integral
n\times n
matrix
A
gives rise to a direct system of free abelian groups with
A
acting as an order-preserving group homomorphism

\mathbb{Z}^{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle A}}{{\longrightarrow}}\mathbb{Z}^{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle A}}{{\longrightarrow}}\mathbb{Z}^{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle A}}{{\longrightarrow}}\cdots

We regard
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
as a partially ordered group with positive cone
\mathbb{N}^{n}
. The direct limit of this system,
\Delta_{A}:=\varinjlim_{A}\mathbb{Z}^{n}
is a partially ordered group whose positive cone
\Delta^{+}
is the direct limit of the associated direct system of positive cones. Multiplication by
A
induces an automorphism
\delta_{A}
of partially ordered groups. The triple
(\Delta_{A},\Delta_{A}^{+},\delta_{A})
is called
Krieger’s dimension group
.

Remark 6.5
.

Krieger’s dimension group
\Delta_{A}
is in fact a module over the Laurent polynomial ring
\mathbb{Z}[x,x^{-1}]
via
x\cdot v=\delta_{A}^{-1}(v)
. Moreover the submonoid
\Delta_{A}^{+}
is closed under multiplication by
\mathbb{N}_{0}[x,x^{-1}]
. Hence we may regard the Krieger triple as a partially ordered
\mathbb{Z}[x,x^{-1}]
-module, and there is a module isomorphism

(6.6)

\Delta_{A}\cong{\rm Coker}(I-xA:\mathbb{Z}[x,x^{-1}]^{n}\to\mathbb{Z}[x,x^{-1}]^{n})

mapping
\Delta_{A}^{+}
isomorphically onto the image of
\mathbb{N}_{0}[x,x^{-1}]^{n}
.

Example 6.7
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
\mathcal{R}_{n}
the
n

\mathcal{R}_{n^{-}}
its Cuntz’ splice, and
A
and
A^{-}
their adjacency matrices. Then it is immediate from (

\Delta_{A}=\mathbb{Z}[x,x^{-1}]/\langle 1-xn\rangle=\mathbb{Z}[1/n].

It also follows from (

) and another straightforward, but longer calculation

]
*Lemma 3.3 that

\Delta_{A^{-}}\cong\mathbb{Z}[x,x^{-1}]/\langle x^{3}+(2n-1)x^{2}-(n+2)x+1\rangle.

The following theorem was proved by Krieger (
[

*Theorem 4.2; see also

(Krieger

Let

7.1.
Graded rings

Let

such that each

for all

\gamma

of

When it is clear from context

we simply say that

graded

\Gamma_{A}=\{\gamma\in\Gamma\mid A_{\gamma}\not=0\}
is called the

of

. We say that a
\Gamma
-graded ring

is

if the support of

is the trivial group

—that is,
A_{0}=A
, so
A_{\gamma}=0
for
\gamma\in\Gamma\backslash\{0\}
. Any ring admits a trivial grading by any group. If
A
is a
\Gamma
-graded ring that happens to be an algebra over a trivially graded ring

, then

is called a
graded algebra

8.6.

The notion of graded Morita theory plays an important role in this subject. It relates the theory of Leavitt path algebras to the theory of symbolic dynamics. One of the main conjectures in the theory is around the notion of graded Morita equivalence (Conjecture

Definition 8.6.1

Let

(1)

{\operatorname{{Gr}-}}A\approx_{\operatorname{gr}}{\operatorname{{Gr}-}}B

\textstyle{{\operatorname{{Gr}-}}A\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\phi^{\prime}}
\scriptstyle{U}

Recall that for any ring

The following theorem, due to Chen and Yang

and Hazrat

(Chen, Yang
[

]
)

Let

and

and

are graded Morita equivalent.

(2)

The Leavitt path algebras

and

(3)

\operatorname{\bf D}_{\operatorname{sng}}(A(E))
and
\operatorname{\bf D}_{\operatorname{sng}}(A(F))

Assembling all the results so far, we have the following conjecture:

and

be finite regular graphs. Then the following are equivalent.

8.9.
Talented monoids and the Morita equivalence conjecture for meteor graphs

For a finite essential graph
E
, the Krieger monoid
\Delta_{A_{E}^{t}}^{+}
is the positive cone of
K_{0}^{\operatorname{gr}}(L(E))
(this follows by the same argument as in

). The latter is the

;

is its group completion. Therefore by Theorem

, from a shift equivalence of finite essential graphs

and

we obtain a

-monoid isomorphism of talented monoids

. This isomorphism gives us control over elements of the monoids (such as minimal elements, atoms, etc.) and consequently on the geometry of the graphs (the number of cycles, their lengths, etc.).

We start with a definition of talented monoids via generators and relations.

Definition 8.9.1
.

For a row-finite graph

, the

is the commutative monoid generated by
\{v(i)\mid v\in E^{0},i\in\mathbb{Z}\}
subject to

(8.9.2)

v(i)=\sum_{e\in s^{-1}(v)}r(e)(i+1),

for every

and

.

The above relations define a congruence relation which also respects the action of
\mathbb{Z}
. Namely, the monoid
T_{E}
is equipped by a natural
\mathbb{Z}
-action:

{}^{n}v(i)=v(i+n)

for

. Proposition 5.7 of
[
17
]
relates this monoid to the theory of Leavitt path algebras: there is a
\mathbb{Z}
-module isomorphism

T_{E}\cong\mathcal{V}^{\operatorname{gr}}(L(E))
. In fact we have

\displaystyle T_{E}

\displaystyle\cong\,\mathcal{V}^{\operatorname{gr}}(L(E))\cong\mathcal{V}(L(\overline{E})),

\displaystyle v(i)

\displaystyle\longmapsto\big[(L(E)v\big)(i)]\longmapsto[L(\overline{E})v_{i}]

Here, as in (
2.2.7
),
\overline{E}
is the covering graph of
E
.
Thus the talented monoid
T_{E}
is conical and cancellative (
[
17
]
*Section 5). Here we use left

-modules to construct
\mathcal{V}^{\operatorname{gr}}(L(E))
so that it matches the definition of our talented monoid.

The following theorem shows how the geometry of graphs translates into properties of their talented monoids. A graph
E
is said to satisfy
Condition (L)
if every cycle in
E
has an exit. A closed path
\alpha=e_{1}\cdots e_{n}
based at a vertex

is

if
s(e_{i})=v
then

. We say that
E
satisfies
Condition (K)
if for every vertex

in the support of a closed simple path there exist at least two distinct closed simple paths based at
v
.

Theorem 8.9.3

(
[
58
]
)
.

Let
E
be a row-finite graph and

the talented monoid associated to

.

(1)

The graph

satisfies Condition (L) if and only if
\mathbb{Z}
acts freely on
T_{E}
.

(2)

The graph
E
satisfies Condition (K) if and only if
\mathbb{Z}
acts freely on any quotient of

by an
\mathbb{Z}
-order-ideal.

A

is an essential connected graph consisting of two disjoint cycles and the paths connecting these cycles. We can give a positive answer to Conjecture

for the case of meteor graphs.

Theorem 8.9.4

(Cordeiro, Gillaspy, Gonçalves, Hazrat
[

]
)
.

Let

and

be essential graphs, where

is a meteor graph. Then the following are equivalent.

(1)

The Leavitt path algebras

and

are graded Morita equivalent.

(2)

-algebras

and

(3)

\mathbb{Z}[x,x^{-1}]

.

-isomorphic.

(5)

(6)

The graphs

Sketch of the proof.

An

\mathbb{Z}[x,x^{-1}]
-module isomorphism

Since a meteor graph consists of only two cycles and paths emitting from one cycle lands exclusively into the other cycle, using in/out-splitting one can transform this graph into a “standard form” graph, without changing the

very likely holds for graphs with disjoint cycles, this is yet to be established.

H_{0}:\phi\to\phi_{1},\dots,H_{n}:\phi_{n}\to\psi
such that each

is a graded homomorphism. The following theorem is concerned with primitive graphs. A finite graph
E
is

if there is an

such that for every pair of vertices
(v,w)
of
E
there is a path
\alpha
of length
N
with
s(\alpha)=v
and
r(\alpha)=w
.

The talented monoid
T_{E}
(Section
8.9
) of a finite graph
E
is pointed by
1_{E}=\sum_{v\in E^{0}}v(0)
.

Theorem 8.10.1

(Arnone
[
21
]
*Theorem 8.1)
.

Let
E
and
F
be two finite, primitive graphs.
If there exists an isomorphism of
\mathbb{Z}
-monoids

sending

then the algebras

and

The proof of Theorem

uses graded bivariant algebraic

]
.
The hypothesis that

arising from edges are full.

8.11.
Compatibility conditions on shift equivalence that imply graded Morita equivalence

In what follows, if
X
is a finite set we write
\mathsf{k}X
for the
\mathsf{k}
-vector space with basis
X
. We will occasionally consider
\mathsf{k}X
as a
\mathsf{k}
-algebra with the product

x\cdot y=\delta_{x,y}x
. For example
\mathsf{k}E^{0}
is isomorphic to the subalgebra of
L(E)
generated by
E^{0}
.

Let
E
and
F
be finite regular graphs. If
A=A_{E}
and
B=A_{F}
are shift equivalent with lag
\ell
, then by definition there are matrices
R\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{E^{0}\times F^{0}}
and
S\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{F^{0}\times E^{0}}
satisfying the identities (
6.2
) and (
6.3
). Let
G=G(R)
be a set with

|R|_{1}=\sum_{(v,w)\in E^{0}\times F^{0}}R_{v,w}

elements, and let
s:G\to E^{0}
and
r:G\to F^{0}
be such that

R_{v,w}=\#\big(s^{-1}(v)\cap r^{-1}(w)\big).

Next consider the
\mathsf{k}
-vector space
M_{R}=\mathsf{k}G(R)
. We give
M_{R}
a
(\mathsf{k}E^{0},\mathsf{k}F^{0})
-bimodule structure as follows: for each
g\in G=G(E)
,
v\in E^{0}
and
w\in F^{0}
, we put

vg=\delta_{v,s(g)}g,\,gw=\delta_{r(g),w}g.

Similarly we define the
\mathsf{k}
-vector space
M_{S}
and make it into a
(\mathsf{k}{F^{0}},\mathsf{k}{E^{0}})
-bimodule. Let

\displaystyle s,r:G(R)\times_{F^{0}}G(S)=\{(g,h)\in G(R)\times G(S)\,\colon\,r(g)=s(h)\}\to E^{0}\sqcup F^{0}

\displaystyle s(g,h)=s(g),\,\,r(g,h)=r(h).

Then for each pair
(v_{1},v_{2})\in E^{0}\times E^{0}

\#\{(g,h)\in G(R)\times_{F^{0}}G(S)\,\colon\,s(g,h)=v_{1},\,r(g,h)=v_{2}\}=(RS)_{v_{1},v_{2}}=A^{\ell}_{v_{1},v_{2}}=\#v_{1}E^{\ell}v_{2}.

Hence from
RS=A^{\ell}
we obtain

(8.11.1)

M_{R}\otimes_{\ell{F^{0}}}M_{S}=\bigoplus_{(g,h)\in G(R)\times_{F^{0}}G(S)}\mathsf{k}\cdot(g,h)\cong\mathsf{k}E^{\ell}\cong(\mathsf{k}{E^{1}})^{\otimes_{\mathsf{k}{E^{0}}}^{\ell}}.

The isomorphism
8.11.1
translates one of the conditions in the definition of shift equivalence to bimodule terms. We may similarly translate all of them; setting
M=M_{R}
,
N=M_{S}
we obtain bimodule isomorphisms

(8.11.2)

\displaystyle M\otimes_{\mathsf{k}{F^{0}}}N\overset{\omega_{E}}{\overset{\sim}{\longrightarrow}}(\mathsf{k}{E^{1}})^{\otimes_{\mathsf{k}{E^{0}}}^{\ell}},\,\,N\otimes_{\mathsf{k}{E^{0}}}M\overset{\omega_{F}}{\overset{\sim}{\longrightarrow}}(\mathsf{k}{F^{1}})^{\otimes_{\mathsf{k}{F^{0}}}^{\ell}},

\displaystyle\mathsf{k}{E^{1}}\otimes_{\mathsf{k}{E^{0}}}M\overset{\sigma_{M}}{\overset{\sim}{\longrightarrow}}M\otimes_{\mathsf{k}{F^{0}}}\mathsf{k}{F^{1}},\,\,\mathsf{k}{F^{1}}\otimes_{\mathsf{k}{F^{0}}}N\overset{\sigma_{N}}{\overset{\sim}{\longrightarrow}}N\otimes_{\mathsf{k}{E^{0}}}\mathsf{k}{E^{1}}.

(

]
*Theorem 3.9)

Let

and

(2)

There exist bimodules

and

To state the next theorem we need more notation. Let

9.
Filtered
K
-theory, shift equivalence, and
C^{*}
-Morita equivalence

Following the early work of Rørdam
[
79
]
and Restorff
[
74
]
, it became clear that one way to preserve enough information in the presence of ideals in a
C^{*}
-algebra, is to further consider the
K
-groups of the ideals, their subquotients, and how they are related to each other via the six-term excision sequence. Over the next ten years since
[
79
,
74
]
appeared, this approach, which is now called
filtered
K
-theory
, was subsequently investigated and further developed by Eilers, Restorff, Ruiz and Sørensen
[
46
,
48
]
, who showed that the lattice of gauge invariant prime ideals and their subquotient
K
-groups can be used as an invariant. For a graph
C^{*}
-algebra
C^{*}(E)
, we denote this invariant by
\operatorname{FK}_{0,1}(C^{*}(E))
. In their major work
[
47
]
, Eilers, Restorff, Ruiz and Sørensen proved that filtered
K
-theory is a complete invariant for unital graph
C^{*}
-algebras. Thus for finite graphs
E
and
F
,

(9.1)

\operatorname{FK}_{0,1}(C^{*}(E))\cong\operatorname{FK}_{0,1}(C^{*}(F))\Longrightarrow C^{*}(E)\cong_{\text{Morita eq.}}C^{*}(F).

In
[
45
]
the four authors introduced filtered
K
-theory in the purely algebraic setting and showed that if two Leavitt path algebras over
\mathbb{C}
have isomorphic filtered algebraic
K
-theory then the associated graph
C^{*}
-algebras have isomorphic filtered
K
-theory.

(9.2)

\operatorname{FK}_{0,1}(L_{\mathbb{C}}(E))\cong\operatorname{FK}_{0,1}(L_{\mathbb{C}}(F))\Longrightarrow\operatorname{FK}_{0,1}(C^{*}(E))\cong\operatorname{FK}_{0,1}(C^{*}(F)).

In
[
18
]
it was shown that graded
K
-theory determines filtered
K
-theory up to a certain precisely defined quotient.

(9.3)

K_{0}^{\operatorname{gr}}(L_{\mathbb{C}}(E))\cong K_{0}^{\operatorname{gr}}(L_{\mathbb{C}}(F))\Longrightarrow\operatorname{\mathrm{F}\overline{\mathrm{K}}}_{0,1}(L_{\mathbb{C}}(E))\cong\operatorname{\mathrm{F}\overline{\mathrm{K}}}_{0,1}(L_{\mathbb{C}}(F)).

This shows the richness of the graded Grothendieck group as an invariant. Namely, the single group
K_{0}^{\operatorname{gr}}(L(E))
of the Leavitt path algebra
L(E)
associated to a graph
E
, with coefficients in a field
\mathsf{k}
, contains all the information about the
K_{0}
groups and the quotient groups
\overline{K}_{1}
of
K_{1}
and how they are related via the long exact sequence of
K
-theory. The important link established in
[
18
]
, was that the existence of an isomorphism at the level of this quotient filtered
K
-theory of Leavitt path algebras still implies the existence of an isomorphism at the level of the corresponding filtered
K
-theory of
C^{*}
-algebras (compare this with (
9.2
)):

(9.4)

\operatorname{\mathrm{F}\overline{\mathrm{K}}}_{0,1}(L_{\mathbb{C}}(E))\cong\operatorname{\mathrm{F}\overline{\mathrm{K}}}_{0,1}(L_{\mathbb{C}}(F))\Longrightarrow\operatorname{FK}_{0,1}(C^{*}(E))\cong\operatorname{FK}_{0,1}(C^{*}(F)).

Using these connections one can prove the following proposition.

Proposition 9.5

(
[
18
]
)
.

Let
E
and
F
be finite regular graphs. If
E
and
F
are shift equivalent, then the
C^{*}
-algebras
C^{*}(E)
and
C^{*}(F)
are Morita equivalent.

Proof.

Since
E
and
F
are shift equivalent, we have an isomorphism of Krieger’s dimension groups

(\Delta_{E},\Delta_{E}^{+},\delta_{E})\cong(\Delta_{F},\Delta_{F}^{+},\delta_{F}).

But by Corollary
8.7.1
, Krieger’s dimension group for the graph
E
coincides with the graded Grothendieck group
K_{0}^{\operatorname{gr}}(L(E))
. We thus obtain an order-preserving
\mathbb{Z}[x,x^{-1}]
-module isomorphism
K_{0}^{\operatorname{gr}}(L(E))\cong K_{0}^{\operatorname{gr}}(L(F))
. By (
9.2
) and (
9.4
), the filtered
K
-theory of the corresponding graph
C^{*}
-algebras are isomorphic. Now (
9.1
)
gives that the
C^{*}
-algebras
C^{*}(E)
and
C^{*}(F)
are Morita equivalent.
∎
