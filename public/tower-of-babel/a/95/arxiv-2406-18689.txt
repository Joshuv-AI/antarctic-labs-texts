A finiteness condition for complex continued fraction algorithms
Charlene Kalle, Fanni M. Sélley, Jörg M. Thuswaldner

Abstract
. It is desirable that a given continued fraction algorithm is simple in the sense that the possible representations can be characterized in an easy way. In this context the so-called finite range condition plays a prominent role. We show that this condition holds for complex 𝜶 {\boldsymbol{\alpha}} -Hurwitz algorithms with parameters 𝜶 ∈ ℚ 2 {\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\in\mathbb{Q}^{2} . This is equivalent to the existence of certain finite partitions related to these algorithms and lies at the root of explorations into their Diophantine properties. Our result provides a partial answer to a recent question formulated by Lukyanenko and Vandehey.

1.
Introduction

Since antiquity, many different kinds of representations of numbers by strings of symbols have been used for various purposes. The most famous one, the
decimal system
, has a particularly nice property: We can represent positive integers by forming finite strings of digits taken from the set
\{0,\ldots,9\}
in an arbitrary way. If we forbid leading zeros, each of these strings represents a positive integer uniquely.

Things are not always that easy and often we get restrictions on the digit strings. For instance, consider the
Fibonacci sequence

(F_{n})_{n\geq 0}
defined by
F_{0}=0
,
F_{1}=1
, and
F_{n}=F_{n-1}+F_{n-2}
for
n\geq 2
. Each positive integer
N\in\mathbb{N}
can be represented in the form

N=\sum_{j=0}^{L-1}\varepsilon_{j}F_{j+2}

(1)

(for some “length”
L\in\mathbb{N}
) with digits
\varepsilon_{0},\ldots,\varepsilon_{L-1}\in\{0,1\}
.
However, even if we forbid leading zeros, uniqueness is maintained only if the digit string
\varepsilon_{0}\cdots\varepsilon_{L-1}
does not contain two consecutive ones,
i.e.
, if the pattern
11
is forbidden. In this case the expansion (
1
) is called the

Zeckendorf expansion
of
N
. Expansions of this kind go back to
[
25
]
.

In the present paper, we are concerned with
continued fraction algorithms
. They allow to represent a real or complex number
z
by digits (called
partial quotients
)
a_{1},a_{2},a_{3},\ldots
taken from an infinite set (like,
e.g.
, the set of positive integers) as

z=\cfrac{1}{a_{1}+\cfrac{1}{a_{2}+\cfrac{1}{a_{3}+\ddots}}}.

(2)

The
classical continued fraction algorithm
for real numbers is a generalization of
Euclid’s algorithm
which performs division with remainder on two integers.
It can be described dynamically
through the
Gauss map

G:(0,1)\to[0,1);\quad z\mapsto\frac{1}{z}-\Big\lfloor\frac{1}{z}\Big\rfloor,

where
\lfloor x\rfloor=\max\{n\in\mathbb{Z}\,:\,n\leq x\}
denotes the integer part of a real number
x
. For
z\in\mathbb{R}
we iteratively apply
G
on
z_{0}=z-\lfloor z\rfloor
. Writing
z_{n}=G^{n}(z_{0})
and
a_{n}=\lfloor\tfrac{1}{G^{n-1}(z_{0})}\rfloor
for each
n\geq 1
we find that

z_{0}=\frac{1}{a_{1}+z_{1}}=\cfrac{1}{a_{1}+\cfrac{1}{a_{2}+z_{2}}}=\cdots=\cfrac{1}{a_{1}+\cfrac{1}{a_{2}+\cfrac{1}{\ddots+\cfrac{1}{a_{n}+z_{n}}}}},

provided that
z_{0},\ldots,z_{n-1}
do not vanish. If
z_{0}
is irrational,
z_{n}
will never vanish and the process can be performed
ad infinitum
leading to the infinite representation (
2
) of
z_{0}=z-\lfloor z\rfloor
(see
[
20
, Chapter I]
). In fact, one can show that each infinite string
a_{1}a_{2}a_{3}\cdots
of positive integers uniquely represents an irrational number
z_{0}\in(0,1)
in the form (
2
), so there are no forbidden strings of digits. The sequence of integers
(a_{i})_{i\geq 1}
is called the
continued fraction expansion

The term “continued fraction” was introduced by John Wallis in 1656 in his
arithmetica infinitorum

[

.
of
z_{0}
and (

z_{0}=[a_{1},a_{2},\ldots]
.

If we consider only the first
n

2

\cfrac{p_{n}}{q_{n}}=[a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{n}]=\cfrac{1}{a_{1}+\cfrac{1}{a_{2}+\cfrac{1}{\ddots+\cfrac{1}{a_{n}}}}},

the
convergents
of
z_{0}
. These convergents surface naturally in Diophantine approximation, since by Lagrange’s best approximation law they optimally approximate
z_{0}
in the sense that

|\frac{p_{n}}{q_{n}}-z_{0}|<|\frac{p}{q}-z_{0}|

holds for all
p,q\in\mathbb{N}
with
q\leq q_{n}
and
(p,q)\not=(p_{n},q_{n})
.

Real numbers can be represented by continued fraction expansions in many other ways. Arguably the most efficient continued fraction algorithm is the
nearest integer algorithm
introduced first by Bernhard Minnigerode
[
15
]
in 1873. For each irrational number
z\in[-\tfrac{1}{2},\tfrac{1}{2})\setminus\{0\}
this algorithm produces an expansion of the form (
2
) with
a_{n}\in\mathbb{Z}
,
|a_{n}|\geq 2
, for all
n\geq 1
. Nearest integer continued fraction expansions can be obtained dynamically in a way very similar to above. In particular, letting

F:\Big[-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}\Big)\setminus\{0\}\to\Big[-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}\Big);\quad z\mapsto\frac{1}{z}-\Big\lfloor\frac{1}{z}\Big\rfloor_{1/2},

where
\lfloor z\rfloor_{1/2}
denotes the unique integer
a
satisfying
z-a\in\left[-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}\right)
, one sets
a_{n}=\lfloor\tfrac{1}{F^{n-1}(z_{0})}\rfloor_{1/2}
for each
n\geq 1
. The efficiency of this algorithm lies in the fact that if one considers the rational approximations to
z
given by the convergents
\frac{p_{n}}{q_{n}}
of this algorithm, then typically the sequence
(\frac{p_{n}}{q_{n}})_{n\geq 1}
converges faster to
z
than the convergents of other similar algorithms. However, like for the Fibonacci expansion, we need restrictions on the possible digit strings in order to get unique expansions.

For the algorithms
G
and
F
, write
\Delta_{G}(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})
and
\Delta_{F}(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})
for the
cylinder set
of all
x
that have
a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}
as the first
n

G
and
F
, respectively. The fact that the set of forbidden strings of digits is easy to describe relates to the
finite range property
as given in
[
24

. More specifically, the collections

\begin{split}\mathcal{G}=\,&\{G^{n}(\Delta_{G}(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}))\,:\,a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}\in\mathbb{N},\,n\geq 1\},\\
\mathcal{F}=\,&\{F^{n}(\Delta_{F}(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}))\,:\,a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}\in\mathbb{N},\,n\geq 1\}\end{split}

both contain only finitely many sets up to Lebesgue null sets. In fact, we have
\mathcal{G}=\{[0,1)\}
and
\mathcal{F}=\{[-\frac{1}{2},0),(0,\frac{1}{2}]\}

\mathcal{G}
is a singleton, which reflects the fact that each string
a_{1}a_{2}\cdots\in\mathbb{N}^{\mathbb{N}}
can occur as a classical continued fraction expansion. On the contrary,
\mathcal{F}
contains two elements which entails that the nearest integer continued fraction expansions are subject to restrictions on the digit strings. An automata theoretic description of such restrictions for a wide class of continued fraction algorithms for real numbers, including Minnegerode’s algorithm, is presented in
[
22

. For general information on continued fraction algorithms see for instance the classical work of Oskar Perron
[
19

2.
The Hurwitz algorithm

At the end of the nineteenth century the two brothers Adolf and Julius Hurwitz both created continued fraction algorithms for complex numbers, see
[
10
,
11
]
. Here we consider the complex continued fraction algorithm introduced by Adolf Hurwitz
[
10
]
in 1887, which is a generalization to the complex plane of the nearest integer algorithm for real numbers. Let

\mathbb{Z}[i]=\{a+bi\,:\,a,b\in\mathbb{Z}\}

with
i=\sqrt{-1}
denote the set of
Gaussian integers
and set

U:=\big\{z\in\mathbb{C}\,:\,-\tfrac{1}{2}\leq\Re z,\Im z<\tfrac{1}{2}\big\},

where
\Re z
and
\Im z
denote the real and imaginary part of
z\in\mathbb{C}
, respectively. The
Hurwitz algorithm
is defined in total analogy to the nearest integer continued fraction algorithm. The set
U
will play the role of the interval
[-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2})
and the floor function
\lfloor\cdot\rfloor_{1/2}
is replaced by the function
\lfloor\cdot\rfloor_{U}:\mathbb{C}\to U;\,z\mapsto z-w
, where
w
is the unique Gaussian integer satisfying
z-w\in U
. The
Hurwitz map
is given by

T:U\setminus\{0\}\to U;\quad z\mapsto\frac{1}{z}-\Big\lfloor\frac{1}{z}\Big\rfloor_{U},

(3)

and the Hurwitz algorithm is defined in terms of iterates of
T
. In particular, using this map one can assign to each
z\in U
a
Hurwitz expansion
of the form (
2
). Here
a_{1},a_{2},\ldots\in\mathbb{Z}[i]\setminus\{0,\pm 1,\pm i\}
are given by
a_{n}=\lfloor\frac{1}{T^{n-1}(z)}\rfloor
provided that
T^{n}(z)
,
n\geq 0
, do not vanish. If one of these iterates vanishes, the expansion of
z
becomes finite. In any case, the Hurwitz expansion of
z\in U
represents
z
(see
e.g.

[
8
, Chapter 5]
for a detailed account on this algorithm).
Let us define
cylinder sets

\Delta(b)=\big\{z\in U\,:\,\left\lfloor\frac{1}{z}\right\rfloor_{U}=b\big\}

Proof of the main result

Before we start the proof we recall some mapping properties of circles and lines in
\mathbb{C}
. For
r\in(0,\infty)
and
m\in\mathbb{C}
write
C_{r}(m)\subseteq\mathbb{C}
for the circle with radius
r
and center
m
. A subset of
\mathbb{C}
that is either a circle or a line will be called a
generalized circle
and can be written in the form

M(a,b,c)=\big\{z\in\mathbb{C}\;:\;az\overline{z}-\overline{b}z-b\overline{z}+c=0\big\},

(5)

with
a,c\in\mathbb{R}
and
b\in\mathbb{C}
satisfying
|b|^{2}>ac
(see
e.g.

[
7
, Section 3.2]
). In particular, if
a=0
then
M(0,b,c)
is a line with (possibly infinite) slope
-\frac{\Re b}{\Im b}
, while for
a\neq 0
the set
M(a,b,c)
is a circle with radius
r=\frac{\sqrt{|b|^{2}-ac}}{|a|}
and center
m=\frac{b}{a}
,
i.e.
,

M(a,b,c)=C_{\frac{\sqrt{|b|^{2}-ac}}{|a|}}\Big(\frac{b}{a}\Big).

(6)

The representation (
5
) is particularly convenient when taking reciprocals of generalized circles. Indeed, direct calculation shows that

\frac{1}{M(a,b,c)}=M(c,\overline{b},a).

(7)

Also the effect of translation by a complex number can be seen in terms of the representation (
5
). Indeed, again by direct calculation, for each
z\in\mathbb{C}
we gain the identity

M(a,b,c)-z=M(a,b-az,az\overline{z}-\overline{b}z-b\overline{z}+c).

(8)

3.1
. Let

[

T_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}
admits a finite partition. For convenience, we will enlarge the domain of
T_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
to

by using the same formula
z\mapsto\frac{1}{z}-\lfloor\frac{1}{z}\rfloor_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}
on the whole boundary of the half-open square

5.
Perspectives

As emphasized in
[
14
, Lemma 3.7 and Remark 3.8]
, in the one-dimensional case of
\alpha
-continued fraction algorithms
(see
[
17
]
) the associated mapping
T_{\alpha}
admits a finite partition if and only if
\alpha
is either a rational or a quadratic irrational number. For the
{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
-Hurwitz algorithm
[
14
, Corollary 3.9]
gives a class of non-quadratic irrational parameters for which this property does not hold. However, it remains open if Theorem
3.1
can be extended to parameters
{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
with quadratic irrational coordinates.

As mentioned before, Theorem
3.1
paves the way for further explorations. For instance, Richard Lakein
[
12
]
determines the optimal constant
L
for which the convergents
\tfrac{p_{n}}{q_{n}}
for
z
of the Hurwitz algorithm satisfy
|z-\tfrac{p_{n}}{q_{n}}|<L|q_{n}|^{-2}
. In his proofs the existence of a finite partition for the Hurwitz algorithm plays a crucial role. Also, the explicit construction of a geometric natural extension of the Hurwitz algorithm from
[
6
]
relies on the existence of a finite partition. Initial investigations into such a construction for the parameter
{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}=(\frac{1}{3},\frac{1}{2})
are done in
[
2
]
. Based on Theorem
3.1
one can now try to obtain such results for
{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
-Hurwitz algorithms with rational parameters
{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
.

It would also be interesting to investigate shifted versions of Jeffrey Shallit’s algorithm
[
21
, Section II.3]
.
