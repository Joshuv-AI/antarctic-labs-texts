Variational Inference of Parameters in Opinion Dynamics Models
Jacopo Lenti, Fabrizio Silvestri, Gianmarco De Francisci Morales

Abstract
Despite the frequent use of agent-based models (ABMs) for studying social phenomena, parameter estimation remains a challenge, often relying on costly simulation-based heuristics. This work uses variational inference to estimate the parameters of an opinion dynamics ABM, by transforming the estimation problem into an optimization task that can be solved directly. Our proposal relies on probabilistic generative ABMs (PGABMs): we start by synthesizing a probabilistic generative model from the ABM rules. Then, we transform the inference process into an optimization problem suitable for automatic differentiation. In particular, we use the Gumbel-Softmax reparameterization for categorical agent attributes and stochastic variational inference for parameter estimation. Furthermore, we explore the trade-offs of using variational distributions with different complexity: normal distributions and normalizing flows. We validate our method on a bounded confidence model with agent roles (leaders and followers). Our approach estimates both macroscopic (bounded confidence intervals and backfire thresholds) and microscopic ( 200 200 categorical, agent-level roles) more accurately than simulation-based and MCMC methods. Consequently, our technique enables experts to tune and validate their ABMs against real-world observations, thus providing insights into human behavior in social systems via data-driven analysis.



1
Introduction

Agent-based models (ABMs) are computational frameworks that simulate the actions and interactions of autonomous agents (individuals or collective entities) within a system.
Their goal is to understand how complex phenomena and properties of a system emerge from the individual behavior of agents.
These models integrate elements from various disciplines, including game theory, complex systems, and computational sociology; and they are popular across diverse scientific domains, such as biology, ecology, economics, and the social sciences.

In ABMs, the agents possess individual attributes and act following predefined rules of behavior and interaction, and system outcomes can be validated against real-world data.
For example, in the field of opinion dynamics,
Fortunato and Castellano 2007
show that a simple voting model can reproduce the distribution of votes received by political candidates across several countries.
Slightly more complex models of the same nature have been used to analyze the negotiations around the Paris Agreement on climate change
(
Bernardo et al. 2021
)
and to study the emergence of ideological spaces in US
(
Baumann et al. 2021
)
.
However, tuning the parameters of these models is still a considerable challenge
(
Flache et al. 2017
)
.

Especially in the context of opinion dynamics, both the global (macroscopic) and the individual (microscopic) level ABM parameters are of great interest, and this is the focus of the current work.
While ABMs offer great flexibility and a clear representation of the causal mechanisms that govern the system, they pose significant challenges in the inference phase.
The obstacles come from several factors, such as the considerable computational effort required to generate data (a complete simulation of the model), and the high dimensionality of microscopic parameters, which scale with the number of agents.
Additionally, the heterogeneity of the agents and the diversity of the rules in the ABM literature are an additional hindrance in the tractability of these models
(
Platt 2020
)
.

The current practice for parameter estimation in ABMs mostly focuses on simulation-based methods.
These methods compute parameter estimates by running a large amount of computationally expensive simulations of the model with different values of the parameters, and comparing the observed and simulated traces of the system state (a process called
calibration
)
(
Fagiolo et al. 2019
;
Lux and Zwinkels 2018
)
.
Simulation-based approaches rely on summary statistics of the system state, primarily focusing on the replication of some macroscopic property, rather than comparing the microscopic behaviors of the agents.
These summary statistics cause an inevitable loss of information at a granular scale, and potentially cause identification issues since different parameterizations of the ABM can lead to indistinguishable summary statistics
(
Gallo et al. 2022
)
.
Moreover, when the parameters are high-dimensional, an exhaustive exploration of the parameter space is not feasible.
For this reason, simulation-based approaches have been mostly used to estimate low-dimensional parameters, while ignoring the microscopic parameters. Indeed, most of the research on ABM calibration focuses on (
i
) a proper estimation choice, following the comparison of the simulated and observed summary statistics
(
Grazzini and Richiardi 2015
)
, (
ii
) an effective representation of the data trajectories
(
Lamperti et al. 2018
)
, and (
iii
) an efficient exploration of the parameter space
(
Quera-Bofarull et al. 2023
)
.

A recent line of work has suggested tackling the parameter estimation problem in ABMs in a radically different way: via a likelihood-based approach
(
Monti et al. 2020
;
Monti et al. 2022
;
Lenti et al. 2024
)
.
They propose a paradigm shift, by translating the ABMs into probabilistic generative models, called Probabilistic Generative ABMs (PGABMs).
PGABMs explicitly define the latent and observed variables of the system according to the available data, and describe the conditional probabilities connecting them.
This representation allows deriving the likelihood function of the latent variables of the system from the observed data, especially by leveraging the rigorous theory of probabilistic graphical models to extrapolate the conditional independencies among the variables in play.
As a result, this approach is more principled, more accurate, and faster than simulation-based alternatives
(
Lenti et al. 2024
)
.

Nevertheless, the likelihood-based approach requires a non-trivial analytical process to derive the likelihood function.
In addition, handling categorical variables is still an open challenge: for instance, a maximum-likelihood approach in our setting would require exploring an exponentially large combination of agents’ parameter values (up
2^{200}
).
The main contribution of this work is a technique that circumvents the need to write an explicit derivation of the likelihood, by using variational inference (VI) instead. VI assumes a tractable, parametric, functional form for the approximation of the posterior distribution of the parameters to be estimated.
Then, it directly optimizes the parameters of the approximation to minimize the distance between the approximated and real posterior.
In so doing, our VI approach can address models with intractable likelihood functions (e.g., with a large number of categorical variables).

Figure

1
depicts the proposed pipeline.

VI approximates the posterior distribution of the parameters and thus represents a Bayesian approach.
Several Bayesian, simulation-based methodologies exist to calibrate ABMs: some employ Monte Carlo schemes
(
Grazzini et al. 2017
;
Ternes et al. 2022
)
, others use neural approximation techniques
(
Dyer et al. 2022
)
.
However, the computational burden of the model simulations often limits the scalability of the inference procedure.
In particular,
Grazzini et al. 2017
compare different Bayesian approaches, including Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) and Approximate Bayesian Computation (ABC), and lament the major obstacle of simulation time.
In stark contrast to the existing literature, our approach is
not
simulation-based, as it relies on the recovery of the probabilistic generative model, and the parameter estimates are the result of a direct optimization procedure.

To demonstrate the viability of our approach, we apply it to a bounded confidence model where each agent is parameterized by a categorical role
(
Weisbuch et al. 2002
)
.
When estimating these roles, the VI-based approach is
3\times
more accurate than an MCMC baseline, and almost
8\times
more accurate than a simulation-based ABC.
In addition, VI is almost an order of magnitude faster than the alternatives.

2
Preliminaries

2.1
Variational Inference

In the typical Bayesian framework, the objective is to determine the posterior distribution
p(\theta\mid y)
of the model parameters
\theta
given the observed data
y
.
Bayes’ theorem provides the relationship
p(\theta\mid y)=p(y\mid\theta)p(\theta)/p(y)
, where
p(\theta)
is the prior distribution of the model parameters which encodes our initial beliefs about
\theta
, and
p(y\mid\theta)
is the likelihood of observing the data according to the underlying probabilistic model.
However, as
p(y)=\int p(\theta,y)\,d\theta
is generally intractable, and closed-form solutions for
p(\theta\mid y)
are limited, Bayesian inference often relies on approximations.

MCMC is one of the main techniques: it uses a sampling routine for
\theta
based on the prior distribution and is guaranteed to converge to the correct posterior, asymptotically.
However, MCMC algorithms do not scale well to large datasets and often struggle to approximate multimodal posteriors effectively
(
Blei et al. 2017
)
.
Variational Inference (VI) is an alternative to MCMC algorithms for approximating a target density via an optimization step
(
Blei et al. 2017
)
.
VI approximates the target posterior
p(\theta\mid y)
by using a more tractable family of densities
q_{\lambda}(\theta)
, called the
variational distribution
, indexed by the variational parameter
\lambda
.

The optimal variational parameter
\lambda^{*}
is the one that minimizes the KL-divergence of
q_{\lambda}(\theta)
from
p(\theta\mid y)
.
In practice, the search for this parameter is done by maximizing the evidence lower bound (ELBO), denoted as
\mathcal{L}(\lambda)
, as the KL-divergence itself is intractable

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(\lambda)=\mathbbm{E}_{q}[\log p(y,\theta)-q_{\lambda}(\theta)],

(1)

which is equivalent to

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(\lambda)=\log p(y)-KL(q_{\lambda}(\theta)\;\|\;p(\theta\mid y)).

(2)

So, maximizing the ELBO is equivalent to minimizing the KL-divergence of
q_{\lambda}(\theta)
from
p(\theta\mid y)
.

Stochastic Variational Inference (SVI) is a common routine for maximizing the ELBO
(
Hoffman et al. 2013
)
.
SVI uses gradient ascent to optimize the ELBO, and estimates the gradient on subsamples of the data instead of using the entire dataset for improved speed.

Variational Inference with Normalizing Flows.

The choice of the variational distribution represents a critical step in the VI process, as it encodes the functional form of the approximation of
p(\theta\mid y)
.
The family of normal distributions is a common choice, thanks to its simplicity and prevalence in Bayesian statistics.
However, the normal family imposes strong constraints on the variational distribution, such as symmetry and unimodality.
A recent and more flexible solution is to adopt normalizing flows (NFs) as the variational distribution
(
Rezende and Mohamed 2015
)
.
NFs are transformations of simple probability distributions (e.g., normal) into richer and more complex distributions through a sequence of invertible and differentiable mappings.
The expressiveness of these transformations enables NFs to represent arbitrarily complex distributions, which we can efficiently sample from and compute the ELBO of
(
Papamakarios et al. 2021
)
.

Categorical variables.

As the ELBO is maximized via gradient ascent, the computation is tractable only for continuous, differentiable variables.
Categorical variables pose a great challenge, both because of their discrete nature and because there is no inherent ordering within their support.
The Gumbel-Softmax reparameterization offers a viable solution for these cases
(
Maddison et al. 2016
;
Jang et al. 2016
)
.
Rather than sampling a variable
z
from a categorical distribution with probabilities
(\pi_{1},\ldots,\pi_{k})
, we sample from a distribution that smoothly approximates the categorical distribution.
Mathematically, this is expressed as

\displaystyle z=\softmax\limits_{i}\left[g_{i}+\log\pi_{i}\right],

(3)

where
g_{i}
is a Gumbel noise.
A temperature
\tau
parameterizes the softmax and controls how closely it approximates the argmax (closer for
\tau\to 0
).
If
k=2
, the Gumbel-Softmax reparameterizes a Bernoulli distribution.
This technique provides a way to relax a probabilistic generative model with categorical random variables which allows for optimizing the ELBO and, consequently, estimating the target parameters.

In the realm of ABMs, differentiable ABMs have been developed for keeping the gradient of categorical variables in simulation-based inference
(
Andelfinger 2021
)
.
In this case, the categorical variables are modeled as deterministic approximations of continuous variables, equipped with the gradient of such continuous variables.
We prefer the Gumbel-Softmax for two reasons.
First, it finds broader applicability in the probabilistic machine learning community as a versatile solution for handling categorical variables.
Second, it is supported by a more robust theoretical framework for incorporating categorical variables into probabilistic generative models.

2.2
Opinion Dynamics Model

Bounded confidence model with backfire effect
.
We analyze the bounded confidence model with backfire effect, a popular opinion dynamics model that depicts the dynamics in a social network with agreement and disagreement behaviors
(
Jager and Amblard 2005
)
.
Each agent has an opinion in
[0,1]
, where 0 and 1 represent the two polar opposites on a given topic (e.g., on the left–right political spectrum).
At each time step, an agent interacts with one of its neighbors, thus emulating a social network.
If the opinions of the two interacting agents are closer than a threshold—the bounded confidence interval (
\varepsilon^{+}
)—they have a positive interaction (
s=1
) and their opinions converge by a convergence rate (
\mu^{+}
).
Conversely, if their opinions are further than a backfire threshold (
\varepsilon^{-}
), then they have a negative interaction (
s=-1
) and their opinions diverge by a divergence rate (
\mu^{-}
).
In the other cases, their opinions remain unchanged (
s=0
).

Let

be the opinion of agent
u
at time
t

e_{t}=(u,v)
are as follows

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{lcl}x^{t+1}_{v}=x^{t}_{v}+\mu^{+}\Delta x^{t}_{uv}&\text{if}&\lvert\Delta x^{t}_{uv}\rvert<\varepsilon^{+},\\
x^{t+1}_{v}=x^{t}_{v}&\text{if}&\varepsilon^{+}\leq\lvert\Delta x^{t}_{uv}\rvert<\varepsilon^{-},\\
x^{t+1}_{v}=x^{t}_{v}-\mu^{-}\Delta x^{t}_{uv}&\text{if}&\lvert\Delta x^{t}_{uv}\rvert\geq\varepsilon^{-}.\\
\end{array}\right.

After each update, the opinions are clamped to
[0,1]
,

\displaystyle x^{t+1}_{u}=\max(0,\min(x^{t+1}_{u},1)).

(7)

Different choices of
\varepsilon^{+}
and
\varepsilon^{-}
lead to different final configurations of the opinions at the end of the simulation (time step
T
), which can capture consensus, polarization, or fragmentation
(
Jager and Amblard 2005
)
.

Probabilistic Generative ABMs

Translating the ABM into its probabilistic generative counterpart is a crucial step in the inference process, as it defines the generative model we wish to approximate.
We split the variable
s

s^{+}=1

s^{-}=0

\displaystyle P(s^{+}_{j}=1\mid x_{t},e_{j},\varepsilon,r_{v})=\sigma(\rho\cdot(\varepsilon^{+}_{r_{v}}-\lvert\Delta x^{t}_{uv}\rvert))

A note on nomenclature.

In the field of ABMs, and dynamical systems in general, a
variable
depends on time while a
parameter
is independent of time.
Conversely, the nomenclature for PGMs is more concerned about randomness:
a variable can assume different values, according to either a stochastic or deterministic process.
An observed variable without parents in the graphical model is called a
parameter

It should be clear from the context when we are referring to a latent ABM parameter that we are estimating vs. an observed PGM parameter that is given.
Similarly, the same objects are treated as ABM
parameters
and PGM

4
Inference

After having derived the PGM, we need to infer the target latent variables.
We compare 4 different methods to do so:
two methods based on variational inference that represent our proposal,
an MCMC method that is a more classic take on the concept of PGABM,
and a Bayesian simulation-based method.

Variational Inference.

The first two methods are based on VI and differ only in the choice of the family for the variational distribution.
We cast the model into a probabilistic programming language (NumPyro in our case).
Since we infer a
4+N
-dimensional variable, the variational distribution
q_{\lambda}
has the same dimensions.
The two distinct VI methods use different
q_{\lambda}
, respectively a multivariate normal distribution and normalizing flows (see
Section

2.1
).

Upon sampling from
q_{\lambda}
, we need to restrict the parameters in the appropriate domains, since
q_{\lambda}
has support in
\mathbbm{R}^{4+N}
.
We compute
\hat{\varepsilon}
and
\hat{r}
from
\theta\sim q_{\lambda}
by using a sigmoid transform.

\bullet

\hat{\varepsilon}^{+}_{F}=\sigma(\theta_{1})/2

\bullet

\hat{\varepsilon}^{+}_{L}=\sigma(\theta_{2})/2

\bullet

\hat{\varepsilon}^{-}_{F}=\sigma(\theta_{3})/2+1/2

\bullet

\hat{\varepsilon}^{-}_{L}=\sigma(\theta_{4})/2+1/2

This step ensures that
\hat{\varepsilon}^{+}_{F}
and
\hat{\varepsilon}^{+}_{L}
are bounded within
[0,0.5]
and
\hat{\varepsilon}^{-}_{F}
and
\hat{\varepsilon}^{-}_{L}
within
[0.5,1]
.

The procedure for the role vector
r
is slightly more complex.
We first define
\hat{\phi}_{u}=\sigma(\theta_{u+4})
, which represents the posterior probability that
u=L
.
Instead of sampling the role with a Bernoulli extractions of
\hat{\phi}
, we use the Gumbel-Softmax relaxation, with temperature 0.1.
Consequently, we define
\tilde{r}_{u}\sim\text{Gumbel-Softmax}(\phi_{u},1-\phi_{u})
.
Finally, we can express the bounded confidence interval for the node as the expectation over its role
\tilde{\varepsilon}_{u}=\tilde{r}_{u}\cdot\hat{\varepsilon}_{L}+(1-\tilde{r}_{u})\cdot\hat{\varepsilon}_{F}
, in a way that is similar to a linear relaxation of an integer program.
Note that this expression simply represents a superposition of the beliefs of the model given the current evidence for the role of an agent, but does not require changing the model to define an intermediate chase ‘in-between’ roles.
This formulation allows determining
\tilde{\varepsilon}_{u}
based on the sampled Gumbel-Softmax probabilities and builds an interpolation that is easier to handle.

Then, we define the process used to generate the data.
Since our goal is to estimate the ELBO, we need to sample from
q_{\lambda}
and from
p(s,\theta)
, as outlined in
Equation

1
.
From the PGABM, we have closed form solutions for
P(s^{+}_{j}=1\mid\varepsilon,X,r)
and
P(s^{-}_{j}=1\mid\varepsilon,X,r)
, representing
p(s\mid\theta)
.
Hence, we can directly sample
p(s,\theta)
, via
p(\theta)
.
Thus, this formulation allows us to automatically maximize the ELBO by seeking the optimal approximation
q_{\lambda^{*}}
of the posterior distribution of
\varepsilon
and
r
.

Markov Chain Monte Carlo.

An alternative approach to estimate the latent variables of the PGM is sampling-based inference via an MCMC algorithm.
We use No-U Turn Sampler (NUTS), an adaptive Hamiltonian Monte Carlo method that is known for its high efficiency and flexibility
(
Hoffman et al. 2014
)
.
The PGM is the same as in the variational case.

Approximate Bayesian Computation.

While the previous methods are based on PGMs, Approximate Bayesian Computation (ABC) is a likelihood-free, simulation-based method
(
Csilléry et al. 2010
)
.
ABC samples a set of parameters from a prior distribution, and for each sample it runs the entire simulation of the opinion dynamics model.
It then considers summary statistics of the generated data trace to see how ‘close’ it is to the original data.
In this case, we use the number of positive interactions and the number of negative interactions per time step as summary statistics.
If the distance between the summary statistics of the observed and simulated trajectories is lower than an acceptance threshold, then the simulation is accepted; otherwise it is rejected.
Finally, it derives the posterior distribution of the target parameters by counting the number of acceptances and rejections from the different regions of the parameter space via Bayes’ rule.

Running time.

The estimation times vary considerably among the tested methods (
Figure

5
).
First, both the VI methods scale well with the length of the ABMs trajectories (
T
).
The scalability of VI arises from its optimization-based approach, which avoids the need to explicitly evaluate the entire dataset during inference.
This computational advantage becomes more evident as the dataset size grows
(
Blei et al. 2017
)
.
Second, the estimation time of SVI with NFs grows linearly with
N
.
This reflects the increased number of parameters underlying the neural networks implementing the NFs.
Conversely, the estimation time of MCMC increases linearly with
T

Overall, SVI with normal is the most efficient method: it reduces the estimation time by a factor of
56.0
compared to SVI with NFs,
36.9
compared to MCMC, and
36.5
compared to ABC.
On average, the estimation time of SVI with normal in the largest experiments (
N=400

61

The experiments having SVI with NFs with

Figure

This analysis provides new insights into how to estimate the parameters based on the available observations.

The first two panels show that an increase in the number of positive outcomes of the interactions of the followers and of the leaders leads to a lower error.
Higher values of
\lvert\varepsilon^{+}_{F}-\varepsilon^{+}_{L}\rvert

\lvert\varepsilon^{-}_{L}-\varepsilon^{-}_{F}\rvert

Such differences determine how distinguishable are the behaviors of the leaders and the followers.

The last plot of

A low variance of the opinions at the final time

In such scenarios, the probability of observing negative interactions is extremely low and the probability of having positive interactions is extremely high, which hinders discriminating between different values of

Discussion

This work explores the use of AI technology in synergy with agent-based models (ABMs).
On the one hand, ABMs are behavioral models that put the human at the center of the system, by focusing on the macroscopic phenomena arising from individual, microscopic choices.
This perspective provides a mechanistic explanation of social phenomena.
On the other hand, the AI and ML literature offers a rich toolbox to incorporate data into models.
Moreover, these technologies can assist model design by providing new insights into the features that allow learning a model.
They enable a model refinement phase that iteratively adjusts the ABM rules to better fit the specific data context.
Furthermore, model design can benefit from model selection, where different models are compared in the analysis of the same dataset to compare different hypotheses.

Until now, the applications of AI in ABMs have primarily centered on developing intelligent agents
(
Zhang et al. 2021
)
, or representing ABMs with neural meta-models
(
van der Hoog 2019
)
.
By properly estimating the parameters of a popular opinion dynamics model with variational inference, we pave the way to the application of the broad theory of probabilistic AI in the realm of inference on ABMs.
Parameter estimation in PGABMs has been tackled via expectation maximization
(
Monti et al. 2020
;
Monti et al. 2022
)
or by direct maximization of the likelihood
(
Lenti et al. 2024
)
.
While effective, these approaches may be limited in their applicability as they require an analytical derivation of the likelihood.
In this work, we offer a proof-of-concept of a general and flexible approach able to estimate high-dimensional, categorical, and continuous parameters in ABMs.

The model we study focuses on polarizing human behavior in social media.
This area is of great importance, as polarization has been identified as a major societal risk, together with misinformation.
3
3

3

https://www.weforum.org/publications/global-risks-report-2024

These phenomena are related, as misinformation spreads faster in polarized environments
(
Nikolov et al. 2021
)
.
A wide range of opinion dynamics models for these phenomena has been proposed
(
Del Vicario et al. 2016
;
Proskurnikov et al. 2015
;
Törnberg 2018
;
Azzimonti and Fernandes 2023
)
. However, they have found limited applicability in real-world scenarios due to the lack of a methodological framework to estimate and validate them.
Our methodology offers a data-driven perspective on opinion dynamics, thus facilitating the exploration of different scenarios, and the evaluation of potential policy interventions.

More generally, we emphasize the versatility of PGABMs for applications to ABMs in different fields, such as human mobility, economics, and epidemiology.
The connection between PGABMs and real-world data can foster interdisciplinary collaborations between AI specialists and domain experts, thus advancing knowledge of human behavior and its consequences.

Limitations and Future Works.

Although our study focuses only on one specific model, we underline that the latter is not only popular but also challenging because of the high dimensionality and categorical nature of the latent parameters.
Indeed, it would be unfeasible to visit all the
2^{200}
combinations of roles within a simulation-based or likelihood-based approach.
Additionally, the presented pipeline required only minimal customization in the translation from the ABM to the PGABM, its implementation in NumPyro, and the optimization of the ELBO.
Applying it to other models should be easy given its flexibility and generality.

While the model we study aims to capture wide social behavior, the scale of this study is far smaller.
Real-world social media studies often involve tens of thousands of users.
However, our experiments were constrained to
3
hours of running time to accommodate the presentation of hundreds of experiments.
In practice, computational efforts could be allocated differently on a single dataset.
Additionally, probabilistic programming languages support GPU accelerators and can be optimized for greater efficiency.
Moreover, if computational time is an issue, SVI with normal represents a great option as it reached good estimates in the most demanding experiments in only
61
seconds and scales well.

Finally, our work focuses on methodological development, so a closer inspection of the results was beyond the scope of this paper.
We acknowledge that a deeper analysis of the posterior distributions returned by the models would be an interesting research direction.
Leveraging the Bayesian nature of VI, future analyses could explore multimodal posterior distributions to address identifiability problems.

Conclusions.

We presented a novel methodology for estimating both the macroscopic and the microscopic parameters of a bounded confidence model with backfire effect with leaders and followers.
Our approach can estimate high-dimensional, numerical and categorical, global and individual parameters of ABMs with high accuracy.
To achieve this result, we rely on stochastic variational inference with normalizing flows as variational distribution, which outperformed other Bayesian baselines.
Using a normal variational distribution achieved good results at a fraction of the time.
