Formal multiple Eisenstein series and their derivations
Henrik Bachmann, Jan-Willem van Ittersum

Abstract
. We introduce the algebra of formal multiple Eisenstein series and study its derivations. This algebra is motivated by the classical multiple Eisenstein series, introduced by Gangl–Kaneko–Zagier as a hybrid of classical Eisenstein series and multiple zeta values. In depth one, we obtain formal versions of the Eisenstein series satisfying the same algebraic relations as the classical Eisenstein series. In particular, they generate an algebra whose elements we call formal quasimodular forms. We show that the algebra of formal multiple Eisenstein series is an 𝔰 ​ 𝔩 2 \mathfrak{sl}_{2} -algebra by formalizing the usual derivations for quasimodular forms and extending them naturally to the whole algebra. Additionally, we introduce some families of derivations for general quasi-shuffle algebras, providing a broader context for these derivations. Further, we prove that a quotient of this algebra is isomorphic to the algebra of formal multiple zeta values. This gives a novel and purely formal approach to classical (quasi)modular forms and builds a new link between (formal) multiple zeta values and modular forms.

2.
Formal multiple Eisenstein series

Define the set
\mathcal{A}
, whose elements we call

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}=\left\{\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{k}{d}\mid k\geq 1,\,d\geq 0\right\}.

We are interested in
\mathbb{Q}
-linear combinations of words in the letters of
\mathcal{A}
, i.e., in elements of
\mathbb{Q}\langle\mathcal{A}\rangle
. Here and in the following, we call the monic monomials in
\mathbb{Q}\langle\mathcal{A}\rangle

.
For
k_{1},\dots,k_{r}\geq 1
and
d_{1},\dots,d_{r}\geq 0

\mathbb{Q}\langle\mathcal{A}\rangle

\displaystyle\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{k_1,\dots,k_r}{d_1,\dots,d_r}:=\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{k_1}{d_1}\dots\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{k_1}{d_1}\,,

where the product on the right is the usual non-commutative product in
\mathbb{Q}\langle\mathcal{A}\rangle
. The space
\mathfrak{H}^{1}
defined in the introduction can be viewed naturally as a subspace of
\mathbb{Q}\langle\mathcal{A}\rangle
via the inclusion

(2.1)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathfrak{H}^{1}&\longrightarrow\mathbb{Q}\langle\mathcal{A}\rangle\\
z_{k_{1}}\cdots z_{k_{r}}&\longmapsto\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{k_1,\dots,k_r}{0,\dots,0}.\end{split}

We will extend the stuffle product defined on
\mathfrak{H}^{1}
to
\mathbb{Q}\langle\mathcal{A}\rangle
in the following way.

Definition 2.1
.

Define the
stuffle product

\ast
on
\mathbb{Q}\langle\mathcal{A}\rangle
as the
\mathbb{Q}
-bilinear product, which satisfies
1\ast w=w\ast 1=w
for any word

and

\displaystyle\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{k_1}{d_1}w\ast\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{k_2}{d_2}v=\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{k_1}{d_1}\left(w\ast\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{k_2}{d_2}v\right)+\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{k_2}{d_2}\left(\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{k_1}{d_1}w\ast v\right)+\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{k_1+k_2}{d_1+d_2}(w\ast v)

for any letters
\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{k_1}{d_1},\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{k_2}{d_2}\in\mathcal{A}
and words
w,v\in\mathbb{Q}\langle\mathcal{A}\rangle
.

This turns
\mathbb{Q}\langle\mathcal{A}\rangle
into a commutative
\mathbb{Q}
-algebra
(\mathbb{Q}\langle\mathcal{A}\rangle,\ast)

(\mathfrak{H}^{1},\ast)
can be viewed as a subalgebra of

via (

Most of the time, we will work with the generating series of our objects. Let
\mathcal{B}_{0}(\mathbb{Q}\langle\mathcal{A}\rangle)=\mathbb{Q}\langle\mathcal{A}\rangle
and for

(2.2)

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{r}=\mathcal{B}_{r}(\mathbb{Q}\langle\mathcal{A}\rangle):=\mathbb{Q}\langle\mathcal{A}\rangle\llbracket X_{1},Y_{1},\dots,X_{r},Y_{r}\rrbracket\quad\text{and}\quad\mathcal{B}=\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{Q}\langle\mathcal{A}\rangle):=\bigoplus_{r\geq 0}\mathcal{B}_{r}(\mathbb{Q}\langle\mathcal{A}\rangle).\qquad

For

\displaystyle\mathfrak{A}_{r}\genfrac{(}{)}{0.0pt}{}{X_1,\dots,X_r}{Y_1,\dots,Y_r}:=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k_{1},\dots,k_{r}\geq 1\\

Definition 2.2
.

We define the

as the linear map

Note that the swap

is an involution.

Definition 2.3
.

By applying a map

to the power series

one obtains a map

\rho\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{k_1,\dots,k_r}{d_1,\dots,d_r}

In particular, we can think of the swap
\sigma:\mathbb{Q}\langle\mathcal{A}\rangle\to\mathbb{Q}\langle\mathcal{A}\rangle
as the linear map defined in the above sense.

Remark 2.4

Definition 2.5
.

We define the space of

as

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}^{\!f}\coloneqq{\mathchoice{\raisebox{3.75pt}{$\displaystyle{(\mathbb{Q}\langle\mathcal{A}\rangle,\ast)}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-3.41666pt}{$\displaystyle{\mathcal{I}}$}}{\raisebox{3.75pt}{$\textstyle{(\mathbb{Q}\langle\mathcal{A}\rangle,\ast)}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-3.41666pt}{$\textstyle{\mathcal{I}}$}}{\raisebox{3.75pt}{$\scriptstyle{(\mathbb{Q}\langle\mathcal{A}\rangle,\ast)}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-2.39166pt}{$\scriptstyle{\mathcal{I}}$}}{\raisebox{3.75pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle{(\mathbb{Q}\langle\mathcal{A}\rangle,\ast)}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-1.6994pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle{\mathcal{I}}$}}}\,,

where
\mathcal{I}
is the ideal in

generated by

for all

. For

and

in

(i)

Write

\displaystyle\operatorname{wt}(w)

\displaystyle\coloneqq k_{1}+\ldots+k_{r}+d_{1}+\ldots+d_{r},

\displaystyle\operatorname{lwt}(w)

\displaystyle\coloneqq d_{1}+\ldots+d_{r},

\displaystyle\operatorname{dep}(w)

\displaystyle\coloneqq r

(ii)

Write

(iii)

(2.5)

\displaystyle\operatorname{Fil}^{\mathrm{\operatorname{lwt}}}_{d}\mathcal{G}^{\!f}

2.2.
Formal multiple zeta values

In this section, we define the space of formal multiple zeta values. Conjecturally, these satisfy exactly the same relations as multiple zeta values. The definition in this work is equivalent to the usual definition of formal multiple zeta values as formal symbols modulo the extended double shuffle relations. The difference is that here we define the space of formal multiple zeta values as a quotient of
\mathcal{G}^{\!f}
. This approach has the benefit of allowing connections to
q
-analogues of multiple zeta values and modular form on a formal level, which is not directly possible with the usual approach. This construction is motivated by the work
[
BI
]
, where the authors consider the behaviour of the
q
-series (
1.8
) as
q\rightarrow 1
.
We define the following two subsets of the alphabet
\mathcal{A}
.

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{0}\coloneqq\left\{\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{k}{0}\mid k\geq 1\right\}\,,\qquad\mathcal{A}^{1}\coloneqq\left\{\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{1}{d}\mid d\geq 0\right\}\,.

With this we define the following ideal in
(\mathbb{Q}\langle\mathcal{A}\rangle,\ast)
generated by the set
\mathcal{A}^{*}\backslash((\mathcal{A}^{1})^{*}(\mathcal{A}_{0})^{*})

(2.13)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{N}\coloneqq\left(\mathcal{A}^{*}\backslash((\mathcal{A}^{1})^{*}(\mathcal{A}_{0})^{*})\right)_{\mathbb{Q}\langle\mathcal{A}\rangle}\,,

where for an alphabet
\mathcal{L}
by
\mathcal{L}^{*}
we denote the set of words in the letters
\mathcal{L}
, i.e., the free monoid generated by the elements in
\mathcal{L}
.
The generators of
\mathfrak{N}
are exactly those elements which are
not
of the form

(2.14)

\displaystyle G^{\!f}\!\genfrac{(}{)}{0.0pt}{}{1,\dots,1,k_1,\dots,k_r}{d_1,\dots,d_s,0,\dots,0}\,,

for some
k_{1},\dots,k_{r}\geq 1,d_{1},\dots,d_{s}\geq 0
.

Definition 2.13
.

The algebra of
formal multiple zeta values
is defined by

\displaystyle\mathcal{Z}^{f}\coloneqq{\mathchoice{\raisebox{4.49501pt}{$\displaystyle{\mathcal{G}^{\!f}\,}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-3.41666pt}{$\displaystyle{\mathfrak{N}}$}}{\raisebox{4.24501pt}{$\textstyle{\mathcal{G}^{\!f}\,}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-3.41666pt}{$\textstyle{\mathfrak{N}}$}}{\raisebox{3.24501pt}{$\scriptstyle{\mathcal{G}^{\!f}\,}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-2.39166pt}{$\scriptstyle{\mathfrak{N}}$}}{\raisebox{2.4913pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle{\mathcal{G}^{\!f}\,}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-1.6994pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle{\mathfrak{N}}$}}}\,.

The justification for the name formal multiple zeta values comes from the fact that our notion is equivalent, up to the non-vanishing of
\zeta^{f}\!(1)
in our case, to the one by Racinet
[
Rac
]
(see Theorem
2.17
below), which consists of formal symbols satisfying the extended double shuffle relations. In particular, we expect
\mathcal{Z}^{f}\cong\mathcal{Z}[T]
. Note that this definition does not coincide with the definition of formal multiple zeta values in the Introduction: it is the content of Theorem
2.17
that both definitions are equivalent.

We denote the canonical projection of the space of formal multiple Eisenstein series into the space of formal multiple zeta values by

(2.15)

\displaystyle\pi:\mathcal{G}^{\!f}\longrightarrow\mathcal{Z}^{f}\,.

This projection can be seen as a formal version of the ‘projection onto the constant term’.

Proposition 2.14
.

The map
\pi_{|\operatorname{Fil}^{\mathrm{\operatorname{lwt}}}_{0}\mathcal{G}^{\!f}}:\operatorname{Fil}^{\mathrm{\operatorname{lwt}}}_{0}\mathcal{G}^{\!f}\rightarrow\mathcal{Z}^{f}
is surjective.

Proof.

All non-zero elements in
\mathcal{Z}^{f}
are linear combinations of elements of the form

(2.16)

\displaystyle f=G^{\!f}\!\Bigg(\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{1,\dots,1,}{d_1,\dots,d_s,}\underbrace{\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{1,\dots,1}{0,\dots,0}}_{j}\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{,k_1,\dots,k_r}{,0,\dots,0}\Bigg)

with
s,r\geq 0
,
j\geq 0
,
d_{s}\geq 1
and
k_{1}\geq 2
. By induction on

one can apply the usual calculation used for the stuffle regularization (
[
BI
, Proposition 4.18]
) to show that

can be written as

\displaystyle f\equiv\sum_{m=0}^{j}f_{m}\,G^{\!f}\!(1)^{m}\quad\mod\mathfrak{N}\,,

where the

are have the same shape as (
2.16
) with
j=0
. Such elements can be expressed as products
G^{\!f}\!\genfrac{(}{)}{0.0pt}{}{1,\dots,1}{d_1,\dots,d_s}G^{\!f}\!\genfrac{(}{)}{0.0pt}{}{k_1,\dots,k_r}{0,\dots,0}
modulo
\mathfrak{N}
. By the definition of
\sigma
it is easy to see that
\sigma(\mathbb{Q}\langle\mathcal{A}^{1}\rangle)=\mathbb{Q}\langle\mathcal{A}_{0}\rangle
, i.e.,
\sigma G^{\!f}\!\genfrac{(}{)}{0.0pt}{}{1,\dots,1}{d_1,\dots,d_s}\in\operatorname{Fil}^{\mathrm{\operatorname{lwt}}}_{0}\mathcal{G}^{\!f}
. Therefore, we can find a representative for the class of

in
\mathcal{Z}^{f}
which is an element in
\operatorname{Fil}^{\mathrm{\operatorname{lwt}}}_{0}\mathcal{G}^{\!f}
.
∎

Definition 2.15
.

For
k_{1},\dots,k_{r}\geq 1
we define the
formal multiple zeta value

\zeta^{f}\!(k_{1},\dots,k_{r})
by

\displaystyle\zeta^{f}\!(k_{1},\dots,k_{r})\coloneqq\pi(G^{\!f}\!(k_{1},\dots,k_{r}))\,.

By Proposition
2.14
the
\zeta^{f}\!
span the space
\mathcal{Z}^{f}
of formal multiple zeta values. Recall that the derivation
D
(see (

(2.17)

\displaystyle DG^{\!f}\!\genfrac{(}{)}{0.0pt}{}{k_1,\dots,k_r}{d_1,\dots,d_r}\coloneqq\sum_{j=1}^{r}k_{j}\,G^{\!f}\!\genfrac{(}{)}{0.0pt}{}{k_1,\dots,k_j+1,\dots,k_r}{d_1,\dots,d_j+1,\dots,d_r}\,.

Since the formal multiple zeta values can be thought of as the ‘constant term’ of formal multiple Eisenstein series, they should vanish under taking the derivative as we will see now.

Proposition 2.16
.

We have
D\mathcal{G}^{\!f}\subset\ker(\pi)
.

Proof.

By (
2.17
) we see that
D\mathcal{G}^{\!f}\subset\mathfrak{N}
since it is a linear combination of words which all contain a letter
\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{k}{d}
with
k\geq 2
and

.
∎

This proposition implies that all the relations we proved so far for elements in
\mathcal{G}^{\!f}
are also true in
\mathcal{Z}^{f}
after setting all terms involving derivatives, or, more generally, elements in
\mathfrak{N}
, to zero.
For example, as a direct consequence of Proposition
2.9
we obtain the double shuffle relations in depth two: For
k_{1},k_{2}\geq 1
we have

\displaystyle\zeta^{f}\!(k_{1})\,\zeta^{f}\!(k_{2})

\displaystyle=\zeta^{f}\!(k_{1},k_{2})+\zeta^{f}\!(k_{2},k_{1})+\zeta^{f}\!(k_{1}+k_{2})

\displaystyle=\sum_{l_{1}+l_{2}=k_{1}+k_{2}}\left(\binom{l_{1}-1}{k_{1}-1}+\binom{l_{1}-1}{k_{2}-1}\right)\zeta^{f}\!(l_{1},l_{2})+\mathbf{1}_{k_{1}+k_{2},2}\,\zeta^{f}\!(2)\,.

In particular, we obtain the relation

by taking

. And as a consequence of Corollary

\displaystyle\zeta^{f}\!(2m)=-\frac{B_{2m}}{2(2m)!}\left(-24\zeta^{f}\!(2)\right)^{m}\,.

The
\mathbb{Q}
-linear map defined on the generators by

(2.18)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\zeta^{f}\!:\mathfrak{H}^{1}&\longrightarrow\mathcal{Z}^{f}\\

is an algebra homomorphism with respect to the stuffle product
\ast

. The justification for calling

\ast

[

,

[

]
, we have
\zeta^{f}\!(1)\neq 0

Theorem 2.17
.

The formal multiple zeta values
\zeta^{f}\!
satisfy exactly the extended double shuffle relations, i.e. the kernel of the map (

w\ast v-w\shuffle v
for

and

Proof.

In this proof, we make use of the notation and results in the appendix. First observe that the element

by

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k_{1},\dots,k_{r}\geq 1\\

satisfies, by the definition of the ideal

∎

2.3.
Realizations

Let
A
be a
\mathbb{Q}
-algebra. For a subalgebra
S\subseteq\mathcal{G}^{\!f}
we will call an algebra homomorphism
\varphi:S\rightarrow A
a
realization of
S
in
A
. Notice that finding a realization of
\mathcal{G}^{\!f}
in
A
is equivalent to finding an algebra homomorphism

(2.19)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\varphi:(\mathbb{Q}\langle\mathcal{A}\rangle,\ast)&\longrightarrow A\\
w=\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{k_1,\dots,k_r}{d_1,\dots,d_r}&\longmapsto\varphi(w)\end{split}

which is
\sigma
-invariant, i.e.
\varphi(\sigma(w))=\varphi(w)
for all words
w\in\mathbb{Q}\langle\mathcal{A}\rangle
.
Since the definition of
\mathcal{G}^{\!f}
is motivated by multiple zeta values and multiple Eisenstein series, a natural question is whether there exist corresponding realizations of
\mathcal{G}^{\!f}
. More precisely, since multiple zeta values are just defined for indices
(k_{1},\dots,k_{r})\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}^{r}
and multiple Eisenstein series are defined for indices
(k_{1},\dots,k_{r})\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 2}^{r}
a natural question is whether there are
\sigma
-invariant algebra homomorphisms (
2.19
) for
A=\mathcal{Z}
(resp.
A=\mathcal{O}(\mathbb{H})
) with
\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{k_1,\dots,k_r}{0,\dots,0}\mapsto\zeta(k_{1},\dots,k_{r})
for
k_{1},\dots,k_{r}\geq 1
(resp.
\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{k_1,\dots,k_r}{0,\dots,0}\mapsto\mathbb{G}_{k_{1},\dots,k_{r}}
for
k_{1},\dots,k_{r}\geq 2
). In particular, one difficulty is that one has to extend these maps to all
\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{k_1,\ldots,k_r}{d_1,\ldots,d_r}
. The case for
A=\mathcal{Z}
was solved in
[
BI
, Theorem 4.23]
. The case for
A=\mathcal{O}(\mathbb{H})
remains open as mentioned in the Introduction. Instead in
[
BB
]
the authors introduced a ‘rational version’ of multiple Eisenstein series, which gives a realization in
A=\mathbb{Q}\llbracket q\rrbracket
such that
\varphi(\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{k}{0})=(-2\pi i)^{-k}\mathbb{G}_{k}
for
k\geq 2
and
q=e^{2\pi i\tau}
. The construction of the images of words in higher depth is inspired by the formula for the Fourier expansion of the multiple Eisenstein series.
Both constructions in
[
BI
]
and
[
BB
]
start with the family of
q
-series
g(\cdots)
defined by the first author in
[
Ba2
]
; see (
1.8
).
Notice that in the special case
r=1
and
k\geq 1
one has
g\genfrac{(}{)}{0.0pt}{}{k}{0}=\frac{1}{(k-1)!}\sum_{n>0}\sigma_{k-1}(n)q^{n}
. The key observation is that the linear map
\varphi_{g}:\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{k_1,\dots,k_r}{d_1,\dots,d_r}\mapsto g\genfrac{(}{)}{0.0pt}{}{k_1,\dots,k_r}{d_1,\dots,d_r}
is
\sigma
-invariant (
[
Ba2
]
). This map is not an algebra homomorphism with respect to the product
\ast
, but one has
\varphi_{g}(w\ast v)\equiv\varphi_{g}(w)\varphi_{g}(v)
modulo terms of lower weight. In
[
BB
]
the authors were able to correct these lower weight terms without losing the
\sigma
-invariance to obtain the following:

Theorem 2.19

(
[
BB
]
)
.

There exists an algebra homomorphism
\varphi_{G}:\mathcal{G}^{\!f}\rightarrow\mathbb{Q}\llbracket q\rrbracket
such that

\displaystyle\varphi_{G}:G^{\!f}\!(k)\longmapsto G(k)=-\frac{B_{k}}{2k!}+\frac{1}{(k-1)!}\sum_{n>0}\sigma_{k-1}(n)q^{n},\qquad(k\geq 2).

Moreover, for any
f\in\mathcal{G}^{\!f}
we have
\varphi_{G}(D(f))=q\frac{d}{dq}\varphi_{G}(f)
.

The image of
\varphi_{G}
in Theorem
2.19
for an arbitrary
G^{\!f}\!\genfrac{(}{)}{0.0pt}{}{k_1,\dots,k_r}{d_1,\dots,d_r}
is given by the combinatorial bi-multiple Eisenstein series value defined explicitly in
[
BB
, Definition 6.4]
. Conjecturally, the space of bi-multiple Eisenstein series is isomorphic to
\mathcal{G}^{\!f}
. In particular, conjecturally, the realization
\varphi_{G}
in Theorem
2.19
is injective.

To obtain a realization of
\mathcal{G}^{\!f}
in
\mathcal{Z}
the authors in
[
BI
]
consider the (regularized) limit of
q\rightarrow 1
of (
1.8
) after multiplication with
(1-q)^{k_{1}+\dots+k_{r}+d_{1}+\dots+d_{r}}
. Lower weight terms also vanish under this limit and the resulting map is
\sigma
-invariant. As a consequence, one obtains the following:

Theorem 2.20

(
[
BI
]
)
.

There exists an algebra homomorphism
\varphi_{G}:\mathcal{G}^{\!f}\rightarrow\mathcal{Z}
such that

\displaystyle\varphi_{\zeta}:G^{\!f}\!(k_{1},\dots,k_{r})\longmapsto\zeta^{\ast}(k_{1},\dots,k_{r}),\qquad(k_{1},\dots,k_{r}\geq 1).

The image of
\varphi
in Theorem
2.20
for an arbitrary
G^{\!f}\!\genfrac{(}{)}{0.0pt}{}{k_1,\dots,k_r}{d_1,\dots,d_r}
is given by the bi-multiple zeta value defined explicitly in
[
BI
, Definition 4.22]
. Note that the statement of Theorem
2.20
is also a consequence of Theorem
2.17
: If
\pi_{\mathcal{Z}}:\mathcal{Z}^{f}\rightarrow\mathcal{Z}
denotes the projection of formal multiple zeta values to (stuffle regularized) multiple zeta values, then we get a realization
\pi_{\mathcal{Z}}\circ\pi:\mathcal{G}^{\!f}\to\mathcal{Z}
, where
\pi
is given in (
2.15
). We should mention that the definition of the ideal
\mathfrak{N}
and our definition of
\mathcal{Z}^{f}
are motivated by the work
[
BI
]
for exactly this reason.

3.
Quasi-shuffle algebras and their derivations

3.1.
Quasi-shuffle products

Suppose we have a set
\mathcal{L}
, called the set of
letters
, and a commutative and associative product
\diamond
on
\mathcal{L}
. Extending this product bi-linearly to
\mathbb{Q}\mathcal{L}
gives a commutative non-unital
\mathbb{Q}
-algebra
(\mathbb{Q}\mathcal{L},\diamond)
. We will be interested in
\mathbb{Q}
-linear combinations of multiple letters of
\mathcal{L}
, i.e., in elements of
\mathbb{Q}\langle\mathcal{L}\rangle
. Here and in the following we call monic monomials in
\mathbb{Q}\langle\mathcal{L}\rangle

words
. Moreover, we call the degree of this monomial the
length
of the word.
In the special case
\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{A}
, the following definition recovers Definition
2.1
.

Definition 3.1
.

Define the
quasi-shuffle product

\ast_{\diamond}
on
\mathbb{Q}\langle\mathcal{L}\rangle
as the
\mathbb{Q}
-bilinear product which satisfies
1\ast_{\diamond}w=w\ast_{\diamond}1=w
for any word
w\in\mathbb{Q}\langle\mathcal{L}\rangle
and

(3.1)

\displaystyle aw\ast_{\diamond}bv=a(w\ast_{\diamond}bv)+b(aw\ast_{\diamond}v)+(a\diamond b)(w\ast_{\diamond}v)

for any letters
a,b\in\mathcal{L}
and words
w,v\in\mathbb{Q}\langle\mathcal{L}\rangle
.

This gives a commutative
\mathbb{Q}
-algebra
(\mathbb{Q}\langle\mathcal{L}\rangle,\ast_{\diamond})
as shown in
[
H
]
. In the special case that
a\diamond b=0
for all
a,b\in A
, we obtain the usual shuffle product
\ast_{\diamond}=\shuffle
.

All quasi-shuffle algebras over the same alphabet are isomorphic due to the results in
[
H
]
. In particular, any quasi-shuffle algebra
(\mathbb{Q}\langle\mathcal{L}\rangle,\ast_{\diamond})
is isomorphic to the shuffle algebra
(\mathbb{Q}\langle\mathcal{L}\rangle,\shuffle)
with the isomorphism given by

(3.2)

\displaystyle\log_{\diamond}:(\mathbb{Q}\langle\mathcal{L}\rangle,\ast_{\diamond})

\displaystyle\longrightarrow(\mathbb{Q}\langle\mathcal{L}\rangle,\shuffle)

(3.3)

\displaystyle a_{1}\cdots a_{r}

\displaystyle\longmapsto

(3.4)

\displaystyle\!\!\!\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq l\leq r\\
i_{1}+\dots+i_{l}=r\\
i_{1},\dots,i_{l}\geq 1\end{subarray}}\frac{(-1)^{r-l}}{i_{1}\cdots i_{l}}(a_{1}\diamond\dots\diamond a_{i_{1}})(a_{i_{1}+1}\diamond\dots\diamond a_{i_{1}+i_{2}})\cdots(a_{i_{1}+\dots+i_{l-1}+1}\diamond\cdots\diamond a_{r})\,\,.

Its inverse is given by the algebra homomorphism

\displaystyle\exp_{\diamond}:(\mathbb{Q}\langle\mathcal{L}\rangle,\shuffle)

\displaystyle\longrightarrow(\mathbb{Q}\langle\mathcal{L}\rangle,\ast_{\diamond})

\displaystyle a_{1}\cdots a_{r}

\displaystyle\longmapsto

\displaystyle\!\!\!\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq l\leq r\\
i_{1}+\dots+i_{l}=r\\
i_{1},\dots,i_{l}\geq 1\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{i_{1}!\cdots i_{l}!}(a_{1}\diamond\dots\diamond a_{i_{1}})(a_{i_{1}+1}\diamond\dots\diamond a_{i_{1}+i_{2}})\cdots(a_{i_{1}+\dots+i_{l-1}+1}\diamond\cdots\diamond a_{r})\,.

In particular, for any derivation
\Theta
on
(\mathbb{Q}\langle\mathcal{L}\rangle,\shuffle)
, the map

\Theta_{\diamond}\coloneqq\exp_{\diamond}\circ\,\Theta\circ\log_{\diamond}

is a derivation on
(\mathbb{Q}\langle\mathcal{L}\rangle,\ast_{\diamond})
.

3.2.
Derivations

In this section, we consider examples of derivations on
(\mathbb{Q}\langle\mathcal{L}\rangle,\ast_{\diamond})
. Some of these derivations were studied previously in
[
KK
]
for the shuffle algebra, but it seems that not much research is done on derivations for quasi-shuffle algebras in general. We start with a simple construction, which gives a derivation on the level of words coming from derivations on the level of letters. Let
\varphi:\mathbb{Q}\mathcal{L}\rightarrow\mathbb{Q}\mathcal{L}
be a
\mathbb{Q}
-linear map. We extend
\varphi
to a
\mathbb{Q}
-linear map
\Theta^{\varphi}:\mathbb{Q}\langle\mathcal{L}\rangle\rightarrow\mathbb{Q}\langle\mathcal{L}\rangle
by
\Theta^{\varphi}(1)=0
and for a word
w=a_{1}\cdots a_{r}
with
a_{1},\dots,a_{r}\in\mathcal{L}
by

(3.5)

\displaystyle\Theta^{\varphi}(w)=\Theta^{\varphi}(a_{1}\cdots a_{r})=\sum_{j=1}^{r}a_{1}\cdots\varphi(a_{j})\cdots a_{r}\,.

Proposition 3.2
.

The map

(3.6)

\displaystyle\operatorname{Der}(\mathbb{Q}\mathcal{L},\diamond)

\displaystyle\longrightarrow\operatorname{Der}(\mathbb{Q}\langle\mathcal{L}\rangle,\ast_{\diamond})

(3.7)

\displaystyle\varphi

\displaystyle\longmapsto\Theta^{\varphi}

is a Lie algebra homomorphism.

Proof.

First, we show
\Theta^{\varphi}
is a derivation. For this, we need to show
\Theta^{\varphi}(w\ast_{\diamond}v)=\Theta^{\varphi}(w)\ast_{\diamond}v+w\ast_{\diamond}\Theta^{\varphi}(v)
for all words
v,w\in\mathbb{Q}\langle\mathcal{L}\rangle
. From now on, we write
\Theta
for
\Theta^{\varphi}
. We proceed by induction on the sum of the length of
w
and
v
. First of all, note the result clearly holds if
w
or
v
is the empty word. In particular, the smallest length case is clear.
For the general case we get by definition that for
a,b\in\mathcal{L}
and words
v,w\in\mathbb{Q}\langle\mathcal{L}\rangle

\displaystyle\Theta(aw

\displaystyle\ast_{\diamond}bv)

\displaystyle\>=\>

\displaystyle\Theta(a(w\ast_{\diamond}bv)+b(aw\ast_{\diamond}v)+(a\diamond b)(w\ast_{\diamond}v))

\displaystyle\>=\>

\displaystyle\varphi(a)(w\ast_{\diamond}bv)+a\,\Theta(w\ast_{\diamond}bv)+\varphi(b)(aw\ast_{\diamond}v)+b\,\Theta(aw\ast_{\diamond}v)\,+

\displaystyle\qquad\Theta(a\diamond b)(w\ast_{\diamond}v)+(a\diamond b)\,\Theta(w\ast_{\diamond}v)

\displaystyle\>=\>

\displaystyle\varphi(a)(w\ast_{\diamond}bv)+a\,(\Theta(w)\ast_{\diamond}bv)+a\,(w\ast_{\diamond}\Theta(bv))+\varphi(b)(aw\ast_{\diamond}v)+b\,(\Theta(aw)\ast_{\diamond}v)\,+

\displaystyle\qquad b\,(aw\ast_{\diamond}\Theta(v))+\varphi(a\diamond b)(w\ast_{\diamond}v)+(a\diamond b)\,(\Theta(w)\ast_{\diamond}v+w\ast_{\diamond}\Theta(v)),

where we used the induction hypothesis. On the other hand, we have

\displaystyle\Theta(aw)

\displaystyle\ast_{\diamond}bv+aw\ast_{\diamond}\Theta(bv)

\displaystyle\>=\>

\displaystyle\varphi(a)w\ast_{\diamond}bv+a\Theta(w)\ast_{\diamond}bv+aw\ast_{\diamond}\varphi(b)v+aw\ast_{\diamond}b\,\Theta(v)

\displaystyle\>=\>

\displaystyle\varphi(a)(w\ast_{\diamond}bv)+b(\varphi(a)w\ast_{\diamond}v)+(\varphi(a)\diamond b)(w\ast_{\diamond}v)+a(\Theta(w)\ast_{\diamond}bv)+b(a\Theta(w)\ast_{\diamond}v)\,+

\displaystyle+(a\diamond b)(\Theta(w)\ast_{\diamond}v)+a(w\ast_{\diamond}\Theta(b)v)+\varphi(b)(aw\ast_{\diamond}v)+(a\diamond\varphi(b))(w\ast_{\diamond}v)\,+

\displaystyle+a(w\ast_{\diamond}b\,\Theta(v))+b(aw\ast_{\diamond}\Theta(v))+(a\diamond b)(w\ast_{\diamond}\Theta(v))\,.

Hence,

\Theta(aw\ast_{\diamond}bv)-\Theta(aw)\ast_{\diamond}bv+aw\ast_{\diamond}\Theta(bv)=\bigl(\varphi(a\diamond b)-(\varphi(a)\diamond b)-(a\diamond\varphi(b))\bigr)(w\ast_{\diamond}v),

which vanishes by using
\varphi
is a derivation.
Moreover, we have

\displaystyle[\Theta^{\varphi},\Theta^{\psi}](a_{1}\cdots a_{r})\>=\>

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{r}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=1\\
j\neq i\end{subarray}}^{r}a_{1}\cdots\varphi(a_{i})\cdots\psi(a_{j})\cdots a_{r}

\displaystyle-\sum_{i=1}^{r}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=1\\
j\neq i\end{subarray}}^{r}a_{1}\cdots\varphi(a_{j})\cdots\psi(a_{i})\cdots a_{r}+\sum_{j=1}^{r}a_{1}\cdots[\varphi,\psi](a_{j})\cdots a_{r}

\displaystyle=\,

\displaystyle\Theta^{[\varphi,\psi]}(a_{1}\cdots a_{r}),

which shows that (
3.6
) is a Lie algebra homomorphism.
∎

Later we will need the following generalization of Proposition
3.2
for linear maps
\varphi
which are not (necessarily) derivations with respect to
\diamond
, but satisfy the following identity

(3.8)

\displaystyle 2\varphi(a\diamond b\diamond c)=\varphi(a\diamond b)\diamond c+\varphi(b\diamond c)\diamond a+\varphi(c\diamond a)\diamond b,

which is equivalent to the condition (
3.9
) on
\gamma
below. Clearly, if
\varphi
is a derivation on
(\mathbb{Q}\mathcal{L},\diamond)
it satisfies (
3.8
), but, since
(\mathbb{Q}\mathcal{L},\diamond)
is a non-unital algebra, this identity does not imply that
\varphi
is a derivation. Note that the map
\Theta^{\varphi}
below agrees with the map in the previous proposition in case
\varphi
is a derivation.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
\varphi:\mathbb{Q}\mathcal{L}\rightarrow\mathbb{Q}\mathcal{L}
be a
\mathbb{Q}
-linear map.
Let
\gamma:\mathbb{Q}\mathcal{L}\times\mathbb{Q}\mathcal{L}\to\mathbb{Q}\mathcal{L}
be the ‘failure of
\varphi
being a derivation’, i.e.,

\displaystyle\gamma(a,b)\coloneqq\varphi(a\diamond b)-\varphi(a)\diamond b-a\diamond\varphi(b)\qquad(a,b\in\mathbb{Q}\mathcal{L}).

Assume
\gamma(a,b)=0
for
a,b\in\operatorname{im}\diamond
and

(3.9)

\displaystyle\gamma(a\diamond c,b)+\gamma(a,b\diamond c)=\gamma(a,b)\diamond c\qquad(a,b,c\in\mathbb{Q}\mathcal{L}).

Then the
\mathbb{Q}
-linear map
\Theta^{\varphi}:\mathbb{Q}\langle\mathcal{L}\rangle\rightarrow\mathbb{Q}\langle\mathcal{L}\rangle
defined by

\displaystyle\Theta^{\varphi}(a_{1}\cdots a_{r})

\displaystyle\coloneqq\sum_{j=1}^{r}a_{1}\cdots\varphi(a_{j})\cdots a_{r}-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{r-1}a_{1}\cdots a_{j-1}\,\gamma(a_{j},a_{j+1})\,a_{j+2}\cdots a_{r}

is a derivation on
(\mathbb{Q}\langle\mathcal{L}\rangle,\ast_{\diamond})
.

Proof.

Again, we want to show
\Theta^{\varphi}(w\ast_{\diamond}v)=\Theta^{\varphi}(w)\ast_{\diamond}v+w\ast_{\diamond}\Theta^{\varphi}(v)
for all words
w=w_{1}\cdots w_{r}
and
v=v_{1}\cdots v_{s}\mspace{1mu}
. Setting
W=\{w_{1},\dots,w_{r}\}
and
V=\{v_{1},\dots,v_{s}\}
we have

\displaystyle w\ast_{\diamond}v=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}u=u_{1}\cdots u_{t}\\
\max(r,s)\leq t\leq r+s\end{subarray}}\binom{u}{w,v}_{\!\!\diamond}\,u\,

for some non-negative integers
\binom{u}{w,v}_{\!\!\diamond}
and
u_{j}\in W\cup V\cup(W\diamond V)
.

First, consider some
u_{j}\in W\cup V
in the sum above, and write
u=u^{\prime}u_{j}u^{\prime\prime}
. If
\varphi
acts on
u_{j}\mspace{1mu}
, then the same contribution
u^{\prime}\,\varphi(u_{j})\,u^{\prime\prime}
appears in both
\Theta^{\varphi}(w\ast_{\diamond}v)
and
\Theta^{\varphi}(w)\ast_{\diamond}v+w\ast_{\diamond}\Theta^{\varphi}(v)
. Moreover, if both
u_{j},u_{j+1}\in W
(resp.
V
), write
u=u^{\prime}u_{j}u_{j+1}u^{\prime\prime}
. Then, if
\gamma
acts on this pair, the same contribution

-\frac{1}{2}u^{\prime}\,\gamma(u_{j},u_{j+1})\,u^{\prime\prime}

appears in both
\Theta^{\varphi}(w\ast_{\diamond}v)
and
\Theta^{\varphi}(w)\ast_{\diamond}v
(resp.
w\ast_{\diamond}\Theta^{\varphi}(v)
).

Next, consider letters
w_{m}\in W
and
v_{n}\in V
. This gives rise to words with letter
w_{m}\diamond v_{n}
as well as to words with
w_{m}
and
v_{n}
as consecutive letters, i.e.,
u=u^{\prime}(w_{m}\diamond v_{n})u^{\prime\prime}
,
u=u^{\prime}w_{m}v_{n}u^{\prime\prime}
or
u=u^{\prime}v_{n}w_{m}u^{\prime\prime}
for some words
u^{\prime},u^{\prime\prime}
.
Then, the term
\Theta^{\varphi}(w\ast_{\diamond}v)

contains

u^{\prime}(\varphi(w_{m}\diamond v_{n}))u^{\prime\prime}-\frac{1}{2}u^{\prime}\gamma(w_{m},v_{n})u^{\prime\prime}-\frac{1}{2}u^{\prime}\gamma(v_{n},w_{m})u^{\prime\prime}.

Moreover, the terms

\Theta^{\varphi}(w)\ast_{\diamond}v+w\ast_{\diamond}\Theta^{\varphi}(v)
contain

u^{\prime}(\varphi(w_{m})\diamond v_{n})u^{\prime\prime}+u^{\prime}(w_{m}\diamond\varphi(v_{n}))u^{\prime\prime}

with the same multiplicity. Note that by definition of
\gamma
these together vanish.

The last case that we have to consider is
u=u^{\prime}(w_{m}\diamond v_{n})u_{j+1}u^{\prime\prime}
. First, assume
u_{j+1}\in V\cup W
. W.l.o.g., say
u_{j+1}=w_{m+1}\in W
. Then, the term
\Theta^{\varphi}(w\ast_{\diamond}v)

contains

-\frac{1}{2}u^{\prime}(\gamma(w_{m}\diamond v_{n},w_{m+1}))u^{\prime\prime}-\frac{1}{2}u^{\prime}(\gamma(w_{m},w_{m+1}\diamond v_{n}))u^{\prime\prime}

Moreover, the term

\Theta^{\varphi}(w)\ast_{\diamond}v
contains

-\frac{1}{2}u^{\prime}(\gamma(w_{m},w_{m+1})\diamond v_{n})u^{\prime\prime}.

Now, these contributions vanish by assumption (
3.9
) on
\gamma
.
Finally, assume
u_{j+1}\in V\diamond W
with
u=u^{\prime}(w_{m}\diamond v_{n})u_{j+1}u^{\prime\prime}
. Then, the term
\Theta^{\varphi}(w\ast_{\diamond}v)

contains

-\frac{1}{2}u^{\prime}(\gamma(w_{m}\diamond v_{n},u_{j+1})u^{\prime\prime}

Note that, as both
w_{m}\diamond v_{n}
and
u_{j+1}
are in the image of
\diamond
, this contribution is zero.

Observe that we have covered all words appearing in
\Theta^{\varphi}(w\ast_{\diamond}v),\Theta^{\varphi}(w)\ast_{\diamond}v
and
w\ast_{\diamond}\Theta^{\varphi}(v)

and that each word appear with zero coefficient in

\Theta^{\varphi}(w\ast_{\diamond}v)-\Theta^{\varphi}(w)\ast_{\diamond}v-w\ast_{\diamond}\Theta^{\varphi}(v),

i.e.,
\Theta^{\varphi}
is a derivation.
∎

Proposition 3.4
.

For any
a\in\mathcal{L}
the linear maps

\displaystyle\Theta^{[a}:\mathbb{Q}\langle\mathcal{L}\rangle

\displaystyle\longrightarrow\mathbb{Q}\langle\mathcal{L}\rangle,

\displaystyle\quad\Theta^{a]}:\mathbb{Q}\langle\mathcal{L}\rangle

\displaystyle\longmapsto\mathbf{1}_{a_{1}=a}a_{2}\cdots a_{r},

.

Proof.

Can be shown easily by induction on the length of a word.
∎

Corollary 3.5
.

For any
a\in\mathcal{L}
the linear maps

\displaystyle\Theta^{[a}_{\diamond}:\mathbb{Q}\langle\mathcal{L}\rangle,

\displaystyle\longrightarrow\mathbb{Q}\langle\mathcal{L}\rangle,

\displaystyle a_{1}\cdots a_{r}

\displaystyle a_{1}\cdots a_{r}

3.4
by applying the isomorphisms

.
∎

Proposition 3.6
.

Let
a\in\mathcal{L}
and let
\varphi:\mathbb{Q}\mathcal{L}\rightarrow\mathbb{Q}\mathcal{L}
be a linear map. Then the linear map

\displaystyle\Theta^{\varphi,a}:\mathbb{Q}\langle\mathcal{L}\rangle

\displaystyle\longrightarrow\mathbb{Q}\langle\mathcal{L}\rangle,

\displaystyle a_{1}\cdots a_{r}

\displaystyle\longmapsto\sum_{j=1}^{r-1}\mathbf{1}_{a_{j}=a}\,a_{1}\cdots a_{j-1}\varphi(a_{j+1})\cdots a_{r}-\sum_{j=2}^{r}\mathbf{1}_{a_{j}=a}\,a_{1}\cdots\varphi(a_{j-1})a_{j+1}\cdots a_{r}

is a derivation with respect to the shuffle product
\shuffle
.

Proof.

Similar as in the proof of Lemma
3.3
, for words

write

\displaystyle w\shuffle v=w_{1}\cdots w_{r}\shuffle v_{1}\cdots v_{s}=\sum_{u=u_{1}\cdots u_{r+s}}\binom{u}{w,v}\,u\,

for some non-negative integers
\binom{u}{w,v}
.
Setting
W=\{w_{1},\dots,w_{r}\}
and
V=\{v_{1},\dots,v_{s}\}
we have
u_{j}\in W\cup V
. Assume we have a word

in the sum on the right with
u_{j}=a
and
u_{j}\in W
. If

, then there also exists a word

in the shuffle product
which equals

and

interchanged and

cancel out. Therefore, the only contribution to

with

comes from those

or

(similarly

∎

.

Lemma 3.7
.

Let
(\mathbb{Q}\langle\mathcal{L}\rangle,\ast_{\diamond})
be a quasi-shuffle algebra and

such that
\operatorname{im}(\diamond)\subset\mathbb{Q}\mathcal{L}\backslash S

. Then the map

is a derivation on
(\mathbb{Q}\langle\mathcal{L}\rangle,\ast_{\diamond})

Proof.

Notice that if
S=\{a\}
and
\varphi(b\diamond c)=\varphi(b)\diamond c
for all

since

.
For general

, we proceed as in the proof of Lemma

: We have

\displaystyle w\ast_{\diamond}v=w_{1}\cdots w_{r}\ast_{\diamond}v_{1}\cdots v_{s}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}u=u_{1}\cdots u_{t}\\
\max(r,s)\leq t\leq r+s\end{subarray}}\binom{u}{w,v}_{\!\!\diamond}\,u\,

for some non-negative integers
\binom{u}{w,v}_{\!\!\diamond}
and

. Suppose
u_{j}=a
for some

. Moreover, without loss of generality, assume

and again distinguish two cases. First, if

for some

and

and

∎

Recall that we defined the alphabet

4.1.
A derivation of weight
-1

Recall that any endomorphism of

(4.2)

\sigma

\displaystyle\varphi_{j}^{+}\sigma f_{r}(\underline{X},\underline{Y})

Lemma 4.2

Let

\displaystyle d(f)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}if_{i}\,a^{i-1}

Let

a derivation of weight

d^{p}\Bigl(f-\frac{d(f)}{p}\,a\Bigr)=d^{p}(f)-\frac{d^{p}(f)}{p}\,p=0.

\displaystyle d(f\,g)

\displaystyle=d\Bigl(\sum_{i,j}f_{i}\,g_{j}\,a^{i+j}\Bigr)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j}i\,f_{i}\,g_{j}\,a^{i-1}\,a^{j}+\sum_{i,j}j\,f_{i}\,g_{j}\,a^{i}\,a^{j-1}

Now, if

4.3.
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}
-algebras

An algebra
A
is called an
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}
-algebra
if there exists a Lie algebra homomorphism
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}\rightarrow\operatorname{Der}(A)
. More explicitly, there exist three derivations
D,W,\delta\in\operatorname{Der}(A)
such that
(D,W,\delta)
forms an
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}
-triple
. This means that the satisfy the commutator relations

(4.7)

\displaystyle[W,D]=2D,\quad[W,\delta]=-2\delta,\quad[\delta,D]=W\,.

As the main example of an
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}
-algebra, we now discuss the algebra of quasimodular forms. Let
\mathbb{H}
be the
upper half plane
, i.e., the set of complex numbers
\tau
for which
\mathrm{Im}\,(\tau)>0
. The group
\Gamma:=\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
acts on
\mathbb{H}
by
Möbius transformations
. Given
\gamma=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{smallmatrix}\right)\in\Gamma=\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})

\phi:\mathbb{H}\to\mathbb{C}
,

and

, we let
\gamma\tau=\frac{a\tau+b}{c\tau+d}
and define the
slash operator
in weight
k

(\phi|_{k}\gamma)(\tau):=(c\tau+d)^{-k}\phi(\gamma\tau).

A modular form of weight
k
is a holomorphic function
\phi:\mathbb{H}\to\mathbb{C}
such that
\phi|_{k}\gamma=\phi
for all
\gamma\in\Gamma
and such that
\phi
admits a holomorphic
q
-expansion around infinity
(q=e^{2\pi\mathrm{i}\tau})
, i.e.,

\phi=\sum_{n\geq 0}a_{n}\,q^{n}\qquad\text{for some }a_{n}\in\mathbb{C}.

In order to obtain
modular forms
Eisenstein considered the series

\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\omega\in\mathbb{Z}\tau+\mathbb{Z}\\
\omega\neq 0\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{\omega^{k}}\qquad(k\geq 3).

These

are non-zero modular forms of weight
k
whenever

is even—equal to
\mathbb{G}_{k}
in (
1.3
). In case

, we write
\omega=m\tau+n
and replace the sum over
\omega
by
\sum_{m}\sum_{n}\mspace{1mu}
, where

. Then, the resulting series equal
\mathbb{G}_{2}
and

(\mathbb{G}_{2}|_{2}\gamma)(\tau)=\mathbb{G}_{2}-\pi\mathrm{i}\frac{c}{c\tau+d}\,.

Hence,
\mathbb{G}_{2}
is not a modular form.

In fact, the transformation of
\mathbb{G}_{2}
leads us to the definition of a

modular form, which are functions satisfying a more general modular transformation property than the Eisenstein series and of which

is the first non-modular example.

A

As a consequence of the relations we proved in Section

\displaystyle DG^{\!f}\!(4)

Appendix A
Classical double shuffle and swap & stuffle

(by Nils Matthes)

To make this appendix self-contained, we will recall some facts on quasi-shuffle algebras which were already mentioned in the Introduction or in Section
3

A.1.
The extended double shuffle relations

A.1.1.
Product structures on words

Let
\mathcal{L}_{z}=\{z_{k}\mid k\geq 1\}
be the alphabet of letters indexed by the positive integers and let
\mathbb{Q}\langle\mathcal{L}_{z}\rangle
be the free
\mathbb{Q}
-vector space on the free monoid
\mathcal{L}_{z}^{\ast}
of words on
\mathcal{L}_{z}\mspace{1mu}
, including the empty word
1
. It can be equipped with two product structures: the first is the index shuffle product
3
3

3

The index shuffle product
\overline{\shuffle}
is the shuffle product on the alphabet
\mathcal{L}_{z}\mspace{1mu}
. One should not confuse it with the shuffle product
\shuffle
on the alphabet
\mathcal{L}_{xy}\mspace{1mu}
.
which is defined for words
w,w_{1},w_{2}\in\mathcal{L}_{z}^{\ast}
and letters

by the recursive formula

\displaystyle z_{k_{1}}w_{1}\overline{\shuffle}z_{k_{2}}w_{2}

\displaystyle=z_{k_{1}}(w_{1}\overline{\shuffle}z_{k_{2}}w_{2})+z_{k_{2}}(z_{k_{1}}w_{1}\overline{\shuffle}w_{2})

\displaystyle 1\overline{\shuffle}w

\displaystyle=w\overline{\shuffle}1=w.

The second is the stuffle product, which is defined similarly to before, but with one extra term:

\displaystyle z_{k_{1}}w_{1}\ast z_{k_{2}}w_{2}

\displaystyle=z_{k_{1}}(w_{1}\ast z_{k_{2}}w_{2})+z_{k_{2}}(z_{k_{1}}w_{1}\ast w_{2})+z_{k_{1}+k_{2}}(w_{1}\ast w_{2})

\displaystyle 1\ast w

\displaystyle=w\ast 1=w.

Each of these products turns
\mathbb{Q}\langle\mathcal{L}_{z}\rangle
into a
\mathbb{Q}
-algebra whose unit element is the empty word in both cases. Together with the deconcatenation coproduct

\displaystyle\Delta:\mathbb{Q}\langle\mathcal{L}_{z}\rangle

\displaystyle\rightarrow\mathbb{Q}\langle\mathcal{L}_{z}\rangle\otimes\mathbb{Q}\langle\mathcal{L}_{z}\rangle

\displaystyle z_{k_{1}}\ldots z_{k_{n}}

\displaystyle\mapsto\sum_{i=0}^{n}z_{k_{1}}\ldots z_{k_{i}}\otimes z_{k_{i+1}}\ldots z_{k_{n}},

(\mathbb{Q}\langle\mathcal{L}_{z}\rangle,\overline{\shuffle},\Delta)

are commutative Hopf algebras.

Let

\mathbb{Q}
-linear morphisms

-linear isomorphism

(A.1)

\displaystyle\Hom(\mathbb{Q}\langle\mathcal{L}_{z}\rangle,R)

\displaystyle\rightarrow\prod_{n\geq 0}R\llbracket X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}\rrbracket

\displaystyle\varphi

\displaystyle\mapsto(\Phi_{n})_{n\geq 0},

where
\Phi_{0}=\varphi(1)
and
\Phi_{n}(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})=\sum_{k_{1},\ldots,k_{n}\geq 1}\varphi(z_{k_{1}}\ldots z_{k_{n}})\,X_{1}^{k_{1}-1}\ldots X_{n}^{k_{n}-1}
, for

. Under this correspondence, the convolution product
\varphi\mspace{1mu}\star\mspace{1mu}\psi:=m_{R}\circ(\varphi\otimes\psi)\circ\Delta\in\Hom(\mathbb{Q}\langle\mathcal{L}_{z}\rangle,R)

\Xi_{n}(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})=\sum_{i=0}^{n}\Phi(X_{1},\ldots,X_{i})\,\Psi_{n-i}(X_{i+1},\ldots,X_{n}).

Now define an automorphism

\displaystyle\sigma:\prod_{n\geq 0}R\llbracket X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}\rrbracket

\displaystyle\rightarrow\prod_{n\geq 0}R\llbracket X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}\rrbracket

\displaystyle(\Phi_{n})_{n\geq 0}

\displaystyle\mapsto(\sigma(\Phi_{n}))_{n\geq 0},

where
\sigma(\Phi_{n})(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}):=\Phi_{n}(X_{1}+\ldots+X_{n},X_{1}+\ldots+X_{n-1},\ldots,X_{1})
. Via the isomorphism (
A.1
), it induces an automorphism
\sigma^{*}:\Hom(\mathbb{Q}\langle\mathcal{L}_{z}\rangle,R)\rightarrow\Hom(\mathbb{Q}\langle\mathcal{L}_{z}\rangle,R)
.

A.1.3.
The extended double shuffle relations

Given a
\mathbb{Q}
-linear morphism
\varphi\in\Hom(\mathbb{Q}\langle\mathcal{L}_{z}\rangle,R)
, we define a new morphism
\varphi_{\rm corr}\in\Hom(\mathbb{Q}\langle\mathcal{L}_{z}\rangle,R)
by sending a word
z_{k_{1}}\ldots z_{k_{n}}
to its coefficient in the power series

\exp\left(\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{n}}{n}\varphi(z_{n})\cdot z_{1}\right)\in R\langle\!\langle z_{1}\rangle\!\rangle.

Definition A.1
.

We say that
\varphi
satisfies the
extended double shuffle relations
, if, for all words
w_{1},w_{2}\in\mathcal{L}_{z}^{*}
, we have that:

(i)

\varphi(w_{1}\ast w_{2})=\varphi(w_{1})\,\varphi(w_{2})
;

(ii)

\sigma^{*}(\varphi_{\rm corr}\star\varphi)(w_{1}\overline{\shuffle}w_{2})=\sigma^{*}(\varphi_{\rm corr}\star\varphi)(w_{1})\,\sigma^{*}(\varphi_{\rm corr}\star\varphi)(w_{2})
.

Remark A.2
.

This definition is equivalent to Racinet’s definition (
[
Rac
]
) in the case where
\varphi(z_{1})=0
, a condition which is, in fact, part of Racinet’s definition. The key point is that, by
[
I
, Proposition 8]
,
\sigma^{*}(\varphi_{\rm corr}\star\varphi)
is a homomorphism for the shuffle product if and only if the map

(\varphi_{\rm corr}\star\varphi)\circ\pi_{\mathcal{L}_{z}}:\mathfrak{H}^{1}\rightarrow R

is a homomorphism for the shuffle product on
\mathfrak{H}^{1}=\mathbb{Q}+\mathbb{Q}\langle x,y\rangle y\mspace{1mu}
. Here
\pi_{\mathcal{L}_{z}}:\mathfrak{H}^{1}\rightarrow\mathbb{Q}\langle\mathcal{L}_{z}\rangle
is the
\mathbb{Q}
-linear isomorphism defined by mapping
x^{k_{1}-1}y\cdots x^{k_{n}-1}y
to
z_{k_{1}}\ldots z_{k_{n}}\mspace{1mu}
. Then one checks, by the usual duality of the underlying Hopf algebras, that this is also equivalent to
\varphi
having the EDS property as defined in
[
IKZ
, Definition 1]
.

A.2.
Commutative power series

In this section, we fix a
\mathbb{Q}
-algebra
R
.

A.2.1.
Preliminaries

Let

\mathcal{B}(R):=\prod_{n=0}^{\infty}R\llbracket X_{1},\ldots,X_{n},Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{n}\rrbracket

be the
R
-module of tuples of formal power series in an increasing number of variables, where
R^{\times}
denotes the group of units of
R
(cf., Equation (
2.2
)). It is a noncommutative, associative, unital
R
-algebra with the concatenation product

\displaystyle\odot:\mathcal{B}(R)\times\mathcal{B}(R)

\displaystyle\rightarrow\mathcal{B}(R)

\displaystyle((F_{n}),(G_{n}))

\displaystyle\mapsto(H_{n}),

where

H_{n}\genfrac{(}{)}{0.0pt}{}{X_1,\ldots,X_n}{Y_1,\ldots,Y_n}\coloneqq\sum_{i=0}^{n}F_{i}\genfrac{(}{)}{0.0pt}{}{X_1,\ldots,X_i}{Y_1,\ldots,Y_i}\,G_{n-i}\genfrac{(}{)}{0.0pt}{}{X_{i+1},\ldots,X_n}{Y_{i+1},\ldots,Y_n}\qquad(n\geq 0).

The unit element is the tuple
\mathbbm{1}:=(1,0,0,\ldots)
and
F\in\mathcal{B}(R)
has an inverse
F^{\odot-1}
for
\odot
if and only if
F_{0}\in R^{\times}
, in which case
F^{\odot-1}
can be defined recursively by

F^{\odot-1}_{0}=F_{0}^{-1},\qquad F^{\odot-1}_{n}=\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}(-1)^{i+1}\frac{F_{n-i}}{F_{0}}F^{\odot-1}_{i}.

Note that there is an embedding

\displaystyle\lambda:R\llbracket t\rrbracket

\displaystyle\rightarrow\mathcal{B}(R)

(A.2)

\displaystyle P(t)=\sum_{n\geq 0}a_{n}t^{n}

\displaystyle\mapsto\lambda(P):=(a_{n})_{n\geq 0}

of
R
-algebras. In other words, every power series in one variable can be viewed as an element of
\mathcal{B}(R)
.

The algebra
\mathcal{B}(R)
is equipped with an involution (the
swap
, cf., Definition
2.2
)

\displaystyle\sigma:\mathcal{B}(R)

\displaystyle\rightarrow\mathcal{B}(R)

\displaystyle(F_{n})

\displaystyle\mapsto(\sigma(F_{n})),

where

\sigma(F_{n})\genfrac{(}{)}{0.0pt}{}{X_1,\ldots,X_n}{Y_1,\ldots,Y_n}:=F_{n}\genfrac{(}{)}{0.0pt}{}{Y_1+\ldots+Y_n,Y_1+\ldots+Y_{n-1},\ldots,Y_1}{X_n,X_{n-1}-X_n,\ldots,X_1-X_2}.

The space
\mathcal{B}(R)
contains two distinguished
R
-submodules

\mathcal{M}_{X}(R):=\prod_{n=0}^{\infty}R\llbracket X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}\rrbracket,\qquad\mathcal{M}_{Y}(R):=\prod_{n=0}^{\infty}R\llbracket Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{n}\rrbracket,

which clearly are both subalgebras.

Proposition A.3
.

For all
F\in\mathcal{M}_{Y}(R)
and
G\in\mathcal{M}_{X}(R)
, we have

\sigma(F\odot G)=\sigma(G)\odot\sigma(F).

Proof.

This is a straightforward computation. For every
n\geq 0
, we have on one hand

\sigma((F\odot G)_{n})=\sum_{i=0}^{n}F_{i}(X_{n},\ldots,X_{n-i+1}-X_{n-i+2})\,G_{n-i}(Y_{1}+\ldots+Y_{n-i},\ldots,Y_{1})

and on the other hand

\displaystyle(\sigma(G)\odot\sigma(F))_{n}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{n}G_{i}(Y_{1}+\ldots+Y_{i},\ldots,Y_{1})\,F_{n-i}(X_{n},\ldots,X_{i+1}-X_{i+2})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{n}G_{n-i}(Y_{1}+\ldots+Y_{n-i},\ldots,Y_{1})\,F_{i}(X_{n},\ldots,X_{n-i+1}-X_{n-i+2}),

which are clearly equal, since
R
is commutative.
∎

A.2.2.
A bijection

Now define a map

\displaystyle\Phi:\mathcal{M}_{X}(R)^{\times}

\displaystyle\rightarrow\mathcal{B}(R)^{\times}

\displaystyle F

\displaystyle\mapsto\sigma(F)\odot c(F)^{\odot-1}\odot F,

where
c:\mathcal{M}_{X}(R)\rightarrow\prod_{n=0}^{\infty}R
is the map which in each component sends a power series to its constant term. In the other direction, define

\displaystyle\Psi:\mathcal{B}(R)^{\times}

\displaystyle\rightarrow\mathcal{M}_{X}(R)^{\times}

\displaystyle F

\displaystyle\mapsto c_{X}(F),

where
c_{X}:\mathcal{B}(R)\rightarrow\mathcal{M}_{X}(R)
is the map which in each component sets
Y_{1}=\ldots=Y_{n}=0
.

Proposition A.4
.

The maps
\Phi
and
\Psi
define mutually inverse bijections between
\mathcal{M}_{X}(R)^{\times}
and the subset of
\mathcal{B}(R)^{\times}
consisting of those elements
F
which satisfy the following two conditions:

(i)

\sigma(F)=F
;

(ii)

There exist elements
G\in\mathcal{M}_{Y}(R)^{\times}
and
H\in\mathcal{M}_{X}(R)^{\times}
such that
c(G)=\mathbbm{1}
and such that
F=G\odot H
.

Proof.

We begin by proving that
\Phi(F)
satisfies conditions (i) and (ii) above, for every
F\in\mathcal{M}_{X}(R)^{\times}
. For condition (i), this follows directly from Proposition
A.3
since
\sigma(F)\in\mathcal{M}_{Y}(R)
. For condition (ii), it is clear that
\Phi(F)=G\odot H
for
H=F\in\mathcal{M}_{X}(R)^{\times}
and
G=\sigma(F)\odot c(F)^{\odot-1}\in\mathcal{M}_{Y}(R)^{\times}
. Moreover,
c(G)=c(\sigma(F))\odot c(F)^{\odot-1}=\mathbbm{1}
, since
c
is an algebra homomorphism for
\odot
and
c(\sigma(F))=c(F)
. Hence
\Phi(F)
satisfies conditions (i) and (ii) of the proposition.

We next prove that
\Phi
and
\Psi
are mutually inverse. Indeed, we have

\Psi(\Phi(F))=c_{X}(\sigma(F)\odot c(F)^{\odot-1}\odot F)=c(\sigma(F))\odot c(F)^{-1}\odot F=F,

since
c_{X}
agrees with
c
on the subspace
\mathcal{M}_{Y}(R)\subset\mathcal{B}(R)
and is the identity on
\mathcal{M}_{X}(R)
. On the other hand, since
c(G)=\mathbbm{1}
, we have

\Phi(\Psi(G\odot H))=\Phi(H)=\sigma(H)\odot c(H)^{\odot-1}\odot H.

In order to prove that this equals
G\odot H
, since
H
is invertible, it suffices to verify that
G=\sigma(H)\odot c(H)^{\odot-1}
. Indeed, since
G\odot H
is
\sigma
-invariant, we have

G\odot H=\sigma(G\odot H)=\sigma(H)\odot\sigma(G),

by Proposition
A.3
. Applying
c_{X}
to both sides of this equality, we see that
H=c(H)\odot\sigma(G)
, and multiplying both sides with
c(H)^{\odot-1}
, then applying
\sigma
yields the desired equality
G=\sigma(H)\odot c(H)^{\odot-1}
.
∎

A.2.3.
Hopf algebra structure

We next equip
\mathcal{B}(R)
with the structure of a complete Hopf algebra over
R
.
To begin with, define a homomorphism of
R
-algebras by
(k\geq 1,d\geq 0\text{ and }(k,d)\neq(1,0))

\displaystyle\Delta_{\ast}:R[X,Y]

\displaystyle\rightarrow R[X,Y]\otimes_{R}R[X,Y]

\displaystyle X^{k-1}Y^{d}

\displaystyle\mapsto X^{k-1}Y^{d}\otimes 1+1\otimes X^{k-1}Y^{d}

\displaystyle+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k_{1},k_{2}\geq 1\\
k_{1}+k_{2}=k\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d_{1},d_{2}\geq 0\\
d_{1}+d_{2}=d\end{subarray}}\binom{d_{1}+d_{2}}{d_{1}}\,X^{k_{1}-1}Y^{d_{1}}\otimes X^{k_{2}-1}Y^{d_{2}}.

Since
\Delta_{\ast}
respects the degree of monomials, it uniquely extends to an
R
-algebra homomorphism
\widehat{\Delta}_{\ast}:R\llbracket X,Y\rrbracket\rightarrow R\llbracket X,Y\rrbracket\,\widehat{\otimes}_{R}\,R\llbracket X,Y\rrbracket
, where
\widehat{\otimes}
denotes the completed tensor product. Finally, it extends to an
R
-linear map

\widehat{\Delta}_{\ast}:\mathcal{B}(R)\rightarrow\mathcal{B}(R)\;\widehat{\otimes}\;\mathcal{B}(R)

by the requirement that
\Delta_{\ast}
is an algebra homomorphism for the concatenation product
\odot
. The triple
(\mathcal{B}(R),\odot,\widehat{\Delta}_{\ast})
is then a complete bialgebra, and one can show that it is in fact a Hopf algebra. We let
\mathcal{G}(R)\subset\mathcal{B}(R)
denote the subspace of group-like elements, i.e., elements
F=(F_{n})_{n\geq 0}
which satisfy
\widehat{\Delta}_{*}(F)=F\otimes F
and
F_{0}=1
. In fact,
\mathcal{G}(R)\subset\mathcal{B}(R)
is a subgroup for the concatenation product.

Our next goal is to state and prove a variant of Proposition
A.4
for group-like elements. First of all, the map
\Psi:\mathcal{B}(R)^{\times}\rightarrow\mathcal{M}_{X}(R)^{\times}
which sends all variables
Y_{i}=0
respects the subspace of group-like elements, hence induces a map

\Psi:\mathcal{G}(R)\rightarrow\mathcal{G}_{X}(R),

where
\mathcal{G}_{X}(R)
denotes the group-like elements of the Hopf subalgebra
\mathcal{M}_{X}(R)\subset\mathcal{B}(R)
. Hence, if in addition
F=G\odot H
with
G,H
as in Proposition
A.4
, then
H\in\mathcal{G}_{X}(R)
. We next state a refinement of this result.

Proposition A.5
.

Let
F\in\mathcal{B}(R)^{\times}
satisfy conditions (i) and (ii) of Proposition
A.4
. Then the following are equivalent:

(i)

F\in\mathcal{G}(R)
;

(ii)

H\in\mathcal{G}_{X}(R)
, and
\sigma(\lambda(F_{\rm corr})\odot c_{X}(F))\in\mathcal{G}_{Y}(R)
, where
\lambda
is defined by (
A.2.1
),

F_{\rm corr}:=\exp\left(\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{n}}{n}f_{n,0}\,t^{n}\right),

and
f_{n,0}
is the coefficient of
X^{n-1}
in
F_{1}\in R\llbracket X,Y\rrbracket
.

Proof.

We first prove that
\text{{(i)}}\Rightarrow\text{{(ii)}}
. By the above discussion, we already know that
H\in\mathcal{G}_{X}(R)\subset\mathcal{G}(R)
. Therefore, since
\mathcal{G}(R)
is a group, we also know that
G\in\mathcal{G}_{Y}(R)
. Moreover, by Proposition
A.4
, we have

G=\sigma(F)\odot c(F)^{\odot-1}=\sigma(F)\odot\lambda(F_{\rm corr})\odot\lambda(\exp(-f_{1,0}t)),

where the last equality follows from
[
HI
, Equation (32)]
. Note that
\lambda(\exp(-f_{1,0}t))\in\mathcal{G}(R)
. Hence, using Proposition
A.3
and again the fact that
\mathcal{G}(R)
is a group, we see that
\sigma(\lambda(F_{\rm corr})\odot F)\in\mathcal{G}_{Y}(R)
. Hence, also
\sigma(\lambda(F_{\rm corr})\odot c_{X}(F))\in\mathcal{G}_{Y}(R)
as was to be shown.

Conversely, if both
H=c_{X}(F)
and
\sigma(\lambda(F_{\rm corr})\odot c_{X}(F))
are group-like, then so is
G\odot H
, since

G\odot H=\sigma(c_{X}(F))\odot c(F)^{\odot-1}\odot c_{X}(F)=\sigma(\lambda(F_{\rm corr})\odot c_{X}(F))\odot\lambda(\exp(-f_{1,0}t))\odot c_{X}(F)

is a product of group-like elements, where the first equality follows from Proposition
A.4
.
∎
