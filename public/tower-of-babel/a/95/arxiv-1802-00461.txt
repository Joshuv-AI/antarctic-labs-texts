Collective power: Minimal model for thermodynamics of nonequilibrium phase transitions
Tim Herpich, Juzar Thingna, Massimiliano Esposito

Abstract
We propose a thermodynamically consistent minimal model to study synchronization which is made of driven and interacting three-state units. This system exhibits at the mean-field level two bifurcations separating three dynamical phases: a single stable fixed point, a stable limit cycle indicative of synchronization, and multiple stable fixed points. These complex emergent dynamical behaviors are understood at the level of the underlying linear Markovian dynamics in terms of metastability, i.e. the appearance of gaps in the upper real part of the spectrum of the Markov generator. Stochastic thermodynamics is used to study the dissipated work across dynamical phases as well as across scales. This dissipated work is found to be reduced by the attractive interactions between the units and to nontrivially depend on the system size. When operating as a work-to-work converter, we find that the maximum power output is achieved far-from-equilibrium in the synchronization regime and that the efficiency at maximum power is surprisingly close to the linear regime prediction. Our work shows the way towards building a thermodynamics of nonequilibrium phase transitions in conjunction to bifurcation theory.



I
Introduction

While phase transitions are quite well understood at equilibrium, nonequilibrium phase transitions still lack a systematic treatment. They are most commonly described as dynamical phenomenon within the framework of nonlinear dynamics and bifurcation theory
Strogatz 2001
;
Guckenheimer and Holmes 1983
, but their relation to thermodynamics is rarely discussed. This is largely due to the fact that a theory of nonequilibrium thermodynamics was lacking. Stochastic thermodynamics nowadays provides one for systems described by stochastic dynamics
Seifert 2012
;
den Broeck and Esposito 2015
;
Zhang et al. 2012
;
Rao and Esposito 2018
. But until recently it has been mostly explored to study noninteracting systems or systems made of few interacting particles. We will use stochastic thermodynamics to explore the physics of nonequilibrium phase transitions in large ensembles of interacting systems.

A motivation to do so which is of great practical importance is to understand how phase transitions, and more generally interactions, affect the performance of large ensembles of nano-machines.
Indeed, while these latter have been shown to make very good energy converters, the main drawback remains their low power output. A natural way out is to assemble large numbers of nano-machines, which immediately raises the question of whether certain interactions are favorable to their overall performance. Stochastic thermodynamics provides a powerful framework to do so as it has proved instrumental to analyze the performance of small energy converters operating far-from-equilibrium
Seifert 2012
;
Pekola 2015
;
Ciliberto 2017
(e.g. thermoelectric quantum dots
Esposito et al. 2009a
;
Cuetara and Esposito 2015
, photoelectric nanocells
Rutten et al. 2009
, molecular motors
Ge et al. 2012
;
Gaspard and Gerritsma 2007
;
Lau et al. 2007
;
Seifert 2011
;
Altaner et al. 2015
) and their power-efficiency trade-off
Shiraishi et al. 2016
;
Proesmans et al. 2016
;
Pietzonka and Seifert 2018
;
Lee and Park 2017
;
Polettini and Esposito 2017
. We emphasize that going beyond linear response is essential here since this is where nonequilibrium phase transitions occur. While some works have been done in this direction, most are restricted to mean-field treatments
Vroylandt et al. 2017
;
Shiraishi 2017
;
Golubeva and Imparato 2012
;
Golubeva and Imparato 2012
;
Golubeva and Imparato 2013
;
Imparato 2015
;
Sasa 2015
. An important aspect of our study will be to analyze in details the emergence of the mean-field description from the underlying stochastic dynamics.

The paradigmatic phase transition which we will consider is synchronization: coupled units with different natural frequencies exhibiting a spontaneous phase-locking to a global frequency
Pikovsky et al. 2003
. This collective phenomenon was famously described by Huygens
Huygens 1895
who experimentally observed that two pendulum clocks attached to a common support display an “odd kind of sympathy”
Huygens 1895
, that is they synchronize in anti-phase. It was later found to be ubiquitous in nature
Strogatz 2004
.
Synchronization is typically modeled by coupled phase oscillators which exhibit phase-locking when the coupling strength exceeds a critical value
Winfree 1967
. The most commonly used (noisy) Kuramoto model
Kuramoto 2003
;
Strogatz 2000
;
Acebrón et al. 2005
;
Gupta et al. 2014
is well understood for an infinite population of oscillator at the mean-field level. Some works also considered few locally coupled oscillators
Sakaguchi et al. 1987
;
Daido 1988
;
Walgraef et al. 1983
and even the dissipation resulting from their synchronization
Izumida et al. 2016
. However, little is known about large but finite populations of stochastic oscillators (see e.g. Refs.
Risler et al. 2004
;
Risler et al. 2005
). Progress in this direction was done in Refs.
Wood et al. 2006a
;
Wood et al. 2006b
;
Wood et al. 2007
by introducing a minimal stochastic model made of interacting three-state units and shown to exhibit phase synchronization. It enabled to compare the mean-field dynamics to the Monte-Carlo one.
However, since this model is made of three unidirectional stochastic transitions, it does not allow for a consistent thermodynamic description. Furthermore, the extent in which this ingredient is essential for synchronization is not clear.
These works also did not provide a detailed understanding of how a linear and irreducible Markov dynamics can give rise to a nonlinear mean-field dynamics with increasing system size. This question is particularly intriguing since the Perron-Frobenius theorem ensures that the former dynamics has a unique stationary solution (for finite state spaces)
Van Kampen 2007
while the latter can exhibit multiple and time-periodic solutions.
It is also closely related to the emergence of hydrodynamic modes or metastability
Gaspard 1996
;
Gaspard 1998
;
Gaveau and Schulman 1998
;
Biroli and Kurchan 2001
;
Esposito and Gaspard 2005a
;
Esposito and Gaspard 2005b
;
Macieszczak et al. 2016
;
Rose et al. 2016
.

In this paper we propose and analyze in great detail a thermodynamically consistent version of the interacting three-state oscillators model. This model can be seen as a toy model for interacting molecular motors
Campàs et al. 2006
, enzymes
Hill 1977
;
Hill and Chen 1981
or switches
Raymo et al. 2003
;
Simão et al. 2011
.

At the mean-field level, it displays as a function of the inverse temperature three phases separated by two nonequilibrium phase transitions: a Hopf bifurcation separating a single stable fixed point phase from a stable limit cycle one, and an infinite-period bifurcation separating the limit-cycle phase from a phase with three stable fixed points. At equilibrium only one phase transition survives which separates a phase with a single stable fixed point from one with multiple stable fixed points via a saddle-node bifurcation.
A central result is that the spectrum of the Markovian dynamics generator is shown to encode the information about the two bifurcations that are observed in the mean field. The mean-field dynamics is demonstrated to be characterized by the three eigenvalues with dominant real parts (the null one and a complex conjugated pair). At the Hopf bifurcation, a real-part gap between these eigenvalues and the remaining eigenvalues opens up, enabling the emergence of a metastable mean-field-like oscillatory dynamics over long times. As the second bifurcation is approached, this difference in real parts further increases while the imaginary parts of the dominant eigenvalues significantly drop causing the oscillations to vanish into three metastable fixed points. The fact that the real part of the most dominant complex conjugated eigenvalue pair converges to zero while the gap with respect to the real parts of all other nonzero eigenvalues becomes larger with increasing system size explains the emergence of the mean-field solutions as the perpetuation of the metastable states.

After demonstrating the consistency of stochastic thermodynamics across scales (from the microscopic manybody level to the mean field one), we analyze the dissipated work across the different dynamical regimes.
We find that as a function of increasing inverse temperatures the transition towards synchronization is of first order while the outward transition is of second order. A crucial observation is that in the thermodynamic limit, interactions can significantly decrease the dissipated work per oscillator beyond the synchronization threshold and even more so after the second transition towards multistability. Furthermore, interactions in finite assemblies of oscillators enhance this effect in the former case but reduce it in the latter, in particular when the number of oscillators is too low to sustain a long-lasting metastable solution.
Finally, we demonstrate that when operating as an energy converter, synchronization significantly enhances the power output per oscillator. Despite operating far-from-equilibrium, the efficiency at maximum power remains quite close to the linear-response prediction of
1/2
.

Overall, our thermodynamically consistent minimal model for synchronization enables us to reveal with unprecedented detail two complementary facets of a nonequilibrium phase transition: The emergence of different dynamical phases from stochastic dynamics far-from-equilibrium and their thermodynamic characterization using stochastic thermodynamics.

The plan of the paper is as follows. First, in Sec.
II
, we introduce the description of our model and perform an exact coarse-graining of the dynamics. Next, Sec.
III
analyzes the different regimes of the mean-field dynamics which motivate the spectral analysis in
IV
. In Sec.
V
we compare dynamics between the mean field with finite systems using dynamical Monte Carlo simulations. Furthermore, the thermodynamic laws are formulated in Sec.
VI
and the work dissipated by noninteracting, small and large interacting networks is compared in
VII
. Finally, the power-efficiency trade-off in the mean field is investigated in
VIII
. We conclude with a summary and an outlook to proceeding projects in Sec.
IX
.

II.1
Setup

We consider a system consisting of
N
three-state units with energies
\epsilon_{i}
(
i=1,2,3
). Under the constraint of occupying the same state, units interact globally via an interaction potential. The system is subjected to a non-conservative rotational forcing
f
and is furthermore in contact with a heat bath at inverse temperature
\beta=(k_{b}T)^{-1}
, where we set
k_{b}\equiv 1
in the following.
The schematics of the setup are depicted in Fig.
1
.

\beta
and in the presence of a nonconservative force
f
.

We denote a microstate by the multiindex
\alpha
=
(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{i},\ldots,\alpha_{N})
with
\alpha_{i}\!=\!1,2,3
. As an example, ordering the units from left to right and from top to bottom, the microstate displayed in Fig.
1
reads
\alpha
=
(2,1,3,2,3,1,2,2,3)
.
Consider a transition from microstate
\alpha^{\prime}
to
\alpha
amounting to a transition between the single unit energy states
\epsilon_{j}
to
\epsilon_{i}
. For such a transition the occupation numbers change according to
N_{i}\!\to\!N_{i}+1
and
N_{j}\!\to\!N_{j}-1
.
To determine the change in internal energy
\Delta E(\alpha,\alpha^{\prime})\!=\!\epsilon(\alpha)\!-\epsilon(\alpha^{\prime})\!+U(\alpha)\!-U(\alpha^{\prime})
, the total interaction energy
U(\alpha)
of the network is required.
Owing to the all-to-all interaction, the total interaction energy is obtained by considering a unit in state
k
and summing up the remaining number of units occupying the same state. It thus holds

\displaystyle U(\alpha)=\frac{u}{N}\sum\limits_{k=1}^{3}\;\sum\limits_{l=1}^{N_{k}\!(\alpha)\!-\!1}\!l=\frac{u}{2N}\sum\limits_{k=1}^{3}N_{k}^{2}(\alpha)+U_{0},

(1)

where
u/N
is the interaction strength,
U_{0}\!=\!-uN/2
is a constant
(\Delta U_{0}\!=\!0)
and the notation
N_{k}(\alpha)
refers to the number of units occupying the single-unit state
k
in the microstate
\alpha
.
We thus obtain for the change in internal energy

\displaystyle\Delta E(\alpha,\alpha^{\prime})\!

\displaystyle=\!\epsilon(\alpha)\!-\!\epsilon(\alpha^{\prime})\!+\!\frac{u}{2N}\!\sum\limits_{k=1}^{3}\!\left[N_{k}^{2}(\alpha)\!-\!N_{k}^{2}(\alpha^{\prime})\right]

(2a)

\displaystyle=\epsilon_{i}-\epsilon_{j}+\frac{u}{N}(N_{i}-N_{j}+1).

(2b)

II.2
Master-Equation

The dissipative dynamics of the system, with the above energetics, is described via a Markovian master equation (ME)

\displaystyle\dot{p}_{\alpha}=\sum\limits_{\alpha^{\prime}}w_{\alpha\alpha^{\prime}}\,p_{\alpha^{\prime}}\,,

(3)

where
p_{\alpha}
denotes the probability to be in the microstate
\alpha
.
The microscopic transition rates
w_{\alpha\alpha^{\prime}}
give the probability per unit time for the system to undergo a transition
\alpha^{\prime}
to
\alpha
.
With only one transition at a time, it follows that the transition rate matrix is irreducible and stochastic,
\sum_{\alpha}w_{\alpha\alpha^{\prime}}=0
. This implies the existence of a unique stationary state
Van Kampen 2007
. We take the microrates to be of Arrhenius form, that is

\displaystyle w_{\alpha\alpha^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\Gamma\;\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{\beta}{2}\left(\Delta E(\alpha,\alpha^{\prime})-\Theta(\alpha,\alpha^{\prime})f\right)}\,,

(4)

with
\Gamma
setting the timescale. The sign function
\Theta(\alpha,\alpha^{\prime})
gives preference to transitions down the bias
f
over counteracting ones. It is defined as
\Theta(\alpha,\alpha^{\prime})\!=\!1
for
\sum_{i}(\alpha_{i}\!-\!\alpha^{\prime}_{i})\text{ mod }3=\!1
and
\Theta(\alpha,\alpha^{\prime})\!=\!-1
otherwise. Furthermore, we emphasize that the rates satisfy local detailed balance

\displaystyle\ln\frac{w_{\alpha\alpha^{\prime}}}{w_{\alpha^{\prime}\alpha}}=-\beta\left(\Delta E(\alpha,\alpha^{\prime})-\Theta(\alpha,\alpha^{\prime})f\right)\,,

(5)

ensuring that the dynamics is thermodynamically consistent. In the long-time limit
t\to\infty
, the system will tend to its unique steady state,
p^{s}_{\alpha}
, which is in nonequilibrium due to the presence of the non-conservative driving
f
.

In absence of driving, microscopic detailed balance

\displaystyle w_{\alpha\alpha^{\prime}}\,p^{eq}_{\alpha^{\prime}}=w_{\alpha^{\prime}\alpha}\,p^{eq}_{\alpha}\,,

(6)

holds and along with the local detailed balance condition in Eq. (
5
) ensures that the equilibrium probability distribution is Gibbsian,
i.e.
,

\displaystyle p^{eq}_{\alpha}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{e}^{-\beta\left(E_{\alpha}-A^{eq}\right)}\,,

(7)

with the equilibrium free energy

\displaystyle A^{eq}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\beta}\,\ln\,\sum\limits_{\alpha}\mathrm{e}^{-\beta E_{\alpha}}\,.

(8)

Formulating the stochastic process as above gives rise to an exceedingly large state space growing exponentially with the number of units in the network as
3^{N}-1
. Yet, a closer inspection reveals that a coarse-graining to a mesoscopic space can be done in which the stochastic dynamics can be represented accurately. In fact, the internal energy (and hence also the microscopic transition rates)
E(\alpha)\!\equiv\!E(\bm{N})
does not depend on the topological details encoded in
\alpha
but only on the mesostate
\bm{N}\!\equiv\!(N_{1},N_{2})
. The number of microstates
\alpha
belonging to the same mesostate
\bm{N}
is given by

\displaystyle\Omega(\bm{N})\equiv\sum\limits_{\alpha\in\bm{N}}1=\binom{N}{N_{1}}\binom{N-N_{1}}{N_{2}}=\frac{N!}{\prod\limits_{i}N_{i}!}\,,

(9)

if the network is made up of
N
units. Introducing the marginalized probability
P_{\bm{N}}
to observe the mesostate
\bm{N}

\displaystyle P_{\bm{N}}

\displaystyle\equiv\sum\limits_{\alpha\in\bm{N}}\,p_{\alpha}\,,

(10)

the ME (
3
) for the full microstate dynamics can be coarse-grained as

\displaystyle\dot{P}_{\bm{N}}

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{\alpha\in\bm{N}}\sum\limits_{\bm{N}^{\prime}}\sum\limits_{\alpha^{\prime}\in\bm{N}^{\prime}}w_{\alpha\alpha^{\prime}}\,p_{\alpha^{\prime}}

(11a)

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{\bm{N}^{\prime}}\tilde{W}_{\bm{N}\bm{N}^{\prime}}\sum\limits_{\alpha\in\bm{N}}\sum\limits_{\alpha^{\prime}\in\bm{N}^{\prime}}\;p_{\alpha^{\prime}}\,\chi_{\alpha^{\prime},\alpha}

(11b)

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{\bm{N}^{\prime}}W_{\bm{N}\bm{N}^{\prime}}\;P_{\bm{N}^{\prime}}\,,

(11c)

with the marginalized mesoscopic transition rates
W_{\bm{N}\bm{N}^{\prime}}=\Omega(\bm{N},\bm{N}^{\prime})\tilde{W}_{\bm{N}\bm{N}^{\prime}}
.
We note that the coarse-graining preserves the stochastic property and the irreducibility of the transition rate matrix.

The characteristic function
\chi_{\alpha^{\prime},\alpha}
emerging in Eq. (
11b
) is a result of pulling the sums through the microscopic transition rate matrix since the information that
w_{\alpha\alpha^{\prime}}\neq 0
, only if transitions between
\alpha^{\prime}
and
\alpha
are possible, would be lost.
Consequently, the function takes the value 1 if
\alpha^{\prime}
and
\alpha
are connected, and is 0 otherwise.

To determine the constrained multiplicity factor
\Omega(\bm{N},\bm{N}^{\prime})\equiv\sum_{\alpha\in\bm{N}}\,\chi_{\alpha^{\prime},\alpha}
, we need to address the question of how many microstates
\alpha\!\in\!\bm{N}
are connected with
\alpha^{\prime}\!\in\!\bm{N}^{\prime}
.
Two macrostates are connected, if two of three occupation numbers
(N_{1},N_{2},N\!-\!N_{1}\!-\!N_{2})
of the macrostates differ by exactly one. In the microstate space, this corresponds to, if, compared entrywise, exactly one number being different in the tuples representing the two microstates. Thus we obtain for the constrained multiplicity factor

\displaystyle\hskip-2.84544pt\Omega(\bm{N},\bm{N}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=N^{\prime}_{1}\,\delta_{N^{\prime}_{1},N_{1}+1}+N^{\prime}_{2}\,\delta_{N^{\prime}_{2},N_{2}+1}+

\displaystyle+\!(N\!\!-\!\!N^{\prime}_{1}\!\!-\!\!N^{\prime}_{2})\,\delta_{N\!-(N^{\prime}_{1}+N^{\prime}_{2}),N\!-(N_{1}\!+\!N_{2})+1}\,,

(12)

which indeed does not require any microscopic information. Hence the coarse-graining of the dynamics is exact and leads to a closed ME (
11c
) represented in terms of mesoscopic states
\bm{N}
.
This coarse-graining significantly reduces the dimensionality of the state space which grows as
\left[(N+1)(N+2)\right]/2-1
, thus quadratically as
N
becomes large. Using Boltzmann’s entropy

\displaystyle S^{int}(\bm{N})=\ln\Omega(\bm{N})\,,

(13)

the multiplicity factor of the microstates can be incorporated into the macrorates in a physically meaningful way.
The mesoscopic local detailed balance relation thus reads

\displaystyle\ln\frac{W_{\bm{N}\bm{N}^{\prime}}}{W_{\bm{N}^{\prime}\bm{N}}}=-\beta\left[\Delta A(\bm{N},\bm{N}^{\prime})-\Theta(\bm{N},\bm{N}^{\prime})f\right],

(14)

with
\Delta A(\bm{N},\bm{N}^{\prime})\!=\!\Delta E(\bm{N},\bm{N}^{\prime})\!-\!\beta^{-1}\Delta S^{int}(\bm{N},\bm{N}^{\prime})
being the difference in free energy between the macrostates
\bm{N}^{\prime}
and
\bm{N}
.
The mesoscopic sign function
\Theta(\bm{N},\bm{N}^{\prime})
is defined analogously to
\Theta(\alpha,\alpha^{\prime})
. Thus,
\Theta(\bm{N},\bm{N}^{\prime})=1
for
\sum_{i}(\bm{N}_{i}\!-\bm{N}_{i}^{\prime})\text{ mod }3=\!1
and
\Theta(\bm{N},\bm{N}^{\prime})\!=\!-1
otherwise.

The mesoscopic local detailed balance relation (
14
) implies that at
t\to\infty
and for
f=0
the mesoscopic detailed balance

\displaystyle W_{\bm{N}\bm{N}^{\prime}}\,P^{eq}_{\bm{N}^{\prime}}=W_{\bm{N}^{\prime}\bm{N}}\,P^{eq}_{\bm{N}}\,,

(15)

holds and the mesoscopic equilibrium probability distribution

\displaystyle P^{eq}_{\bm{N}}=\mathrm{e}^{-\beta\left(A_{\bm{N}}-A^{eq}\right)},

(16)

is again of the Gibbs form with the equilibrium free energy

\displaystyle A^{eq}=-\frac{1}{\beta}\ln\sum\limits_{\bm{N}}\mathrm{e}^{-\beta A_{\bm{N}}}\,.

(17)

III
Mean-Field Dynamics

In order to further reduce the complexity of the state space of the mesoscopic ME (
11c
) we first operate in the mean-field (MF) limit where
N\!\to\!\infty
. In this limit, the total change in internal energy due to a transition in Eq. (
2b
) simplifies and the corresponding scaled current density
J(n_{i},n_{j})\!\equiv\!\lim_{N\to\infty}W_{\bm{N}\bm{N}^{\prime}}/N
becomes

\displaystyle J(n_{i},n_{j})=\Gamma\,\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{\beta}{2}\!\left(\epsilon_{i}-\epsilon_{j}+u(n_{i}-n_{j})-\Theta(i,j)f\right)}\,n_{j}\,,

(18)

where
n_{i}\!=\!N_{i}/N
denotes the occupation density of the single-unit state
i

and
\Theta(i,j)=1
for
(i-j)\text{ mod }3=\!1
, while
\Theta(i,j)\!=\!-1
otherwise.

The evolution equation for the mean occupation density reads

\displaystyle\langle\dot{n}_{i}\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{j\neq i}\langle J(n_{i},n_{j})\rangle-\langle J(n_{j},n_{i})\rangle.

(19)

In the MF approximation we replace any
n
-point correlation function with a product of
n
averages thus yielding

\displaystyle\dot{\overline{n}}_{i}\equiv\langle\dot{n}_{i}\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{j\neq i}J(\langle n_{i}\rangle,\langle n_{j}\rangle)-J(\langle n_{j}\rangle,\langle n_{i}\rangle),

(20)

which represents a closed nonlinear equation. The validity of this approximation can be proved
Van Kampen 2007
in the macroscopic limit
N\!\penalty\ \to\penalty\ \!\infty
. Hence the MF system can be described by a single three-state unit, where the (average) occupation density of the single-unit states is assigned to the three states of the MF unit.
We therefore identify the MF occupation density,
\overline{n}_{i}
, as the probability for any unit to occupy the single-unit state
i=1,2,3
. Its dynamics is ruled by the nonlinear MF equation

\displaystyle\dot{\overline{n}}_{i}\!

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{j}k_{ij}\,\overline{n}_{j}\,,

(21)

with the MF transition rates

\displaystyle k_{ij}=\Gamma\,\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{\beta}{2}\!\left(\epsilon_{i}-\epsilon_{j}+u(\overline{n}_{i}-\overline{n}_{j})-\Theta(i,j)f\right)}\,,

(22)

obeying local detailed balance

\displaystyle\ln\frac{k_{ij}}{k_{ij}}

\displaystyle=-\beta\left(\epsilon_{i}-\epsilon_{j}+u(\overline{n}_{i}-\overline{n}_{j})-\Theta(i,j)\,f\right).

(23)

Unit conservation erases one degree of freedom such that there are only two independent variables
n_{1}
and
n_{2}
.

We proceed by choosing a flat energy landscape,
i.e.
by setting
\epsilon_{i}\!=\!\operatorname{const}\,\forall i
. This allows us to immediately read off the symmetric point
\overline{n}_{i}^{*}=1/3
as an analytic solution to the nonlinear MF Eq. (
21
). Linearizing the Eq. around this fixed point (FP),
\dot{\overline{n}}_{i}=\sum\limits_{j}\left.\tfrac{\partial k_{ij}}{\partial\overline{n}_{j}}\right|_{\overline{n}_{j}=\overline{n}_{j}^{*}}\overline{n}_{j}
, we find for the eigenvalues of the linear stability matrix

\displaystyle\lambda_{\pm}\!=\!-\Gamma(\beta u\!+\!3)\cosh\left(\frac{\beta f}{2}\right)\!\pm\!\mathrm{i}\sqrt{3}\,\Gamma\sinh\left(\frac{\beta f}{2}\right).

(24)

For attractive interactions (
u<0
) between the units the real part of
\lambda_{\pm}
changes its sign at
\beta_{c_{1}}=-3/u
. This crossover suggests that the stable symmetric FP destabilizes and degenerates into a limit cycle (LC) corresponding to a Hopf bifurcation indicative of synchronization.

23

IV
Spectral Analysis: Metastability

A crucial aspect of our model is that it allows us to study its (thermo-)dynamic features for large but finite system sizes and in particular to monitor the convergence of the stochastic dynamics to the MF dynamics. In order to proceed, we begin by stating the formal solution to the mesoscopic ME (
11c
) that reads

\displaystyle\bm{P}(t)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{e}^{\bm{W}t}\,\bm{P}(0)

(25a)

\displaystyle=\!\sum\limits_{i,i^{*}}\!\mathrm{e}^{\lambda_{i}t}\!\underbrace{\Phi^{L}_{i}\bm{P}(0)}_{\equiv c_{i}}\Phi^{R}_{i}+c_{i^{*}}\mathrm{e}^{\lambda_{i^{*}}t}\Phi^{R}_{i^{*}},

(25b)

where
\bm{P}(0)
is the initial probability distribution,
\lambda_{i}
are the eigenvalues and
\Phi^{L}_{i}
,
\Phi^{R}_{i}
are the left- and right eigenvectors of the non-symmetric real transition rate matrix
\bm{W}
constituting an orthonormal dual basis
\Phi^{L}_{i}\Phi^{R}_{j}=\delta_{ij}
. The index
i^{*}
characterizes, if existent, the modes with eigenvalues and eigenvectors being the complex-conjugated to those labeled with
i
.
The Perron-Frobenius theorem (PFT)
Van Kampen 2007
stipulates that for this irreducible, autonomous and stochastic matrix there is a non-degenerate eigenvalue, the Perron-Frobenius eigenvalue (PFE),
\lambda_{0}=0
, which is strictly greater than the real part of any other eigenvalue,
\operatorname{Re}(\lambda_{i})<\lambda_{0}\;\forall i\neq 0
. Note that the labeling of the eigenvalues is given by the order of their real parts,

0>\operatorname{Re}(\lambda_{1})>\ldots\;
.
Consequently, Eq. (
25b
) has a unique, infinite-time solution,
\bm{P}^{s}=c_{0}\Phi^{R}_{0}
, characterized by the PFE and the associated right eigenvector
\Phi_{0}^{R}
.

Hence the stationary state of the mesoscopic system
\bm{P}^{s}
cannot exhibit stable oscillations (S phase) or multistability (NA phase). On the other hand, one expects that the transition from the mesoscopic system to the MF is smooth as the system size
N
grows. This apparent paradox is caused by the non-commutation of the infinite-time limit
t\to\infty
and the mean-field limit
N\to\infty
,
i.e.

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to\infty}\lim_{N\to\infty}\;\bm{P}(t)\neq\lim_{N\to\infty}\;\underbrace{\lim_{t\to\infty}\;\bm{P}(t)}_{\bm{P}^{s}},\quad\text{if }\;\beta\geq\beta_{c_{1}}.

(26)

The right-hand side corresponds to the symmetric stationary state of the SA phase for all temperatures, while the left-hand side is temperature dependent: For
\beta_{c_{1}}\leq\beta<\beta_{c_{2}}
the system is in a time-periodic state (S phase) and for
\beta\geq\beta_{c_{2}}
the dynamics will go to one of the non-symmetric steady states (NA phase) depending on the chosen initial condition. At
\beta<\beta_{c_{1}}
the left-hand side also corresponds to the symmetric stationary state, hence the two limits commute only at sufficiently high temperatures.

To resolve this apparent contradiction we look for clues in the spectrum of the Markov generator in the mesoscopic ME (
11c
) and establish a link between finite-size systems and MF via the notion of metastability.
Even though the stationary state is inevitably reached in the infinite-time limit, there could be long-living metastable states that display the phenomenology of the MF. The time-scales to characterize such a state are encoded in the spectrum as follows

\displaystyle\tau_{r}

\displaystyle\sim-\frac{1}{\operatorname{Re}(\lambda_{1})}

(27a)

\displaystyle\tau_{m}

\displaystyle\sim-\frac{1}{\operatorname{Re}(\lambda_{2})}

(27b)

\displaystyle\tau_{l}

\displaystyle\equiv\tau_{r}-\tau_{m}\sim\frac{1}{|\operatorname{Re}(\lambda_{1})|}\left(1-\frac{\operatorname{Re}(\lambda_{1})}{\operatorname{Re}(\lambda_{2})}\right),

(27c)

where
\tau_{r}
is the relaxation time to reach the unique steady state,
i.e.
it specifies the time-scale at which all finite-time modes have been removed from the dynamics.
\tau_{m}
is the metastable time at which all modes have decayed except for those forming the metastable state, that is the one associated with the eigenvalue
\lambda_{1}
and the stationary one characterized by the PFE
\lambda_{0}
. Here, we assume that only a pair of modes associated with a complex-conjugated non-null eigenvalue is contributing to metastability, while there could be an arbitrary number of modes forming the metastable state. This assumption will be numerically verified in the following.

Physically,
\tau_{l}
corresponds to the lifetime of that metastable state. To reconcile the stochastic dynamics with its asymptotic solution in the macroscopic limit, the MF dynamics,
\tau_{l}
is required to become increasingly larger with the system size
N
, while
\tau_{m}
remains finite since the different MF phases emerge at finite time. Using Eqs. (
27a
)–(
27c
), these prerequisites translate into conditions on the real parts of the dominant eigenvalues of the Markov generator: The real-part gap between the two first non-null eigenvalues,
\operatorname{Re}(\lambda_{1})-\operatorname{Re}(\lambda_{2})
, has to increase by
\operatorname{Re}(\lambda_{1})
converging to zero (corresponding to a diverging relaxation time
\tau_{r}
), while
\operatorname{Re}(\lambda_{2})
has to approach a finite value (assuring the emergence of the metastable phenomena at finite times). Moreover to mimic MF dynamics the metastable state has to be oscillatory (
\operatorname{Im}(\lambda_{1})\neq 0
) in the S phase and quasistationary (
\operatorname{Im}(\lambda_{1})=0
) in the NA phase.

We have so far established a connection between linear stochastic dynamics and the deterministic nonlinear MF dynamics via the study of the spectrum of the Markov generator. Indeed, the different dynamical phases and bifurcations in the MF are encoded in the spectrum and appear as metastable states for long times in the stochastic dynamics. These predictions are confirmed by our simulations. We now proceed by analyzing the bifurcations as nonequilibrium phase transitions in the thermodynamic observables. In doing so, we link deterministic bifurcation theory to stochastic thermodynamics.

VI
Thermodynamic Laws

We first introduce the basic thermodynamic state functions in this model: the microscopic internal energy and the system entropy

\displaystyle\left\langle e\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{\alpha}E(\alpha)\,p_{\alpha}

(29a)

\displaystyle\left\langle s\right\rangle

\displaystyle=-\sum\limits_{\alpha}p_{\alpha}\ln p_{\alpha}\,.

(29b)

For our setup with an autonomous driving,
f
, these functions can only change due to the time-dependence of the probability distribution. The rate of change of internal energy

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}_{t}\langle e\rangle=\sum\limits_{\alpha,\alpha^{\prime}}E(\alpha)w_{\alpha\alpha^{\prime}}\,p_{\alpha^{\prime}}=\langle\dot{q}\rangle+\langle\dot{w}\rangle,

(30)

naturally defines the microscopic first law of thermodynamics with the heat and work rate given by

\displaystyle\langle\dot{q}\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{\alpha,\alpha^{\prime}}\left[E(\alpha)-f\,\Theta(\alpha,\alpha^{\prime})\right]w_{\alpha\alpha^{\prime}}\,p_{\alpha^{\prime}}

(31)

\displaystyle\langle\dot{w}\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{\alpha,\alpha^{\prime}}f\;\Theta(\alpha,\alpha^{\prime})\;w_{\alpha\alpha^{\prime}}\,p_{\alpha^{\prime}}\,,

(32)

where the sign function
\Theta(\alpha,\alpha^{\prime})
is defined below Eq. (
4
).
The microscopic local detailed balance relation (
5
) can be expressed in terms of the heat exchange with the bath along the forward transition

\displaystyle q(\alpha,\alpha^{\prime})=-\frac{1}{\beta}\ln\frac{w_{\alpha\alpha^{\prime}}}{w_{\alpha^{\prime}\alpha}}.

(33)

The system entropy change

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}_{t}\langle s\rangle=\langle\dot{s}_{e}\rangle+\langle\dot{\sigma}\rangle

(34)

can be decomposed into the entropy flow from the bath to the system

\displaystyle\langle\dot{s}_{e}\rangle=-\sum\limits_{\alpha,\alpha^{\prime}}w_{\alpha\alpha^{\prime}}\,p_{\alpha^{\prime}}\ln\frac{w_{\alpha\alpha^{\prime}}}{w_{\alpha^{\prime}\alpha}}=\beta\langle\dot{q}\rangle,

(35)

and the non-negative entropy production (EP) rate

\displaystyle\langle\dot{\sigma}\rangle=\sum\limits_{\alpha,\alpha^{\prime}}w_{\alpha\alpha^{\prime}}\,p_{\alpha^{\prime}}\,\ln\frac{w_{\alpha\alpha^{\prime}}p_{\alpha^{\prime}}}{w_{\alpha^{\prime}\alpha}p_{\alpha}}\geq 0.

(36)

Equation (
36
) is the second law of thermodynamics and the inequality follows straightforwardly from
\ln x\leq x-1
.
The marginalization of the microscopic probability
p_{\alpha}
performed in Sec.
II
, yet being exact on the level of the dynamics, does not a priori guarantee that the thermodynamic observables defined above are invariant under this coarse-graining
Esposito 2012
. Defining
E_{\bm{N}}
to be the internal energy of the system in the macrostate
\bm{N}
, and applying the coarse-graining from Eq. (
10
) on the expression for the internal energy in Eq. (
29a
), we obtain

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}_{t}\langle e\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{\bm{N},\bm{N}^{\prime}}\,\sum\limits_{\alpha^{\prime}\in\bm{N}^{\prime}}\,\sum\limits_{\alpha\in\bm{N}}E(\alpha)\,w_{\alpha\alpha^{\prime}}\,p_{\alpha^{\prime}}

(37a)

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{\bm{N},\bm{N}^{\prime}}\tilde{W}_{\bm{N}\bm{N}^{\prime}}\,E(\bm{N})\!\sum\limits_{\alpha^{\prime}\in\bm{N}^{\prime}}p_{\alpha^{\prime}}\sum\limits_{\alpha\in\bm{N}}1

(37b)

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{\bm{N},\bm{N}^{\prime}}E(\bm{N})\,W_{\bm{N}\bm{N}^{\prime}}\,P_{\bm{N}^{\prime}}\equiv\mathrm{d}_{t}\langle E\rangle.

(37c)

Thus the coarse-graining admits a representation in the mesospace while it keeps the internal energy invariant. The heat and work fluxes can also be exactly coarse-grained as

\displaystyle\langle\dot{Q}\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{\bm{N},\bm{N}^{\prime}}\underbrace{\big(E(\bm{N})-f\,\Theta(\bm{N},\bm{N}^{\prime})\big)W_{\bm{N}\bm{N}^{\prime}}P_{\bm{N}^{\prime}}}_{Q(\bm{N},\bm{N}^{\prime})}

(38a)

\displaystyle\langle\dot{W}\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{\bm{N},\bm{N}^{\prime}}f\,\Theta(\bm{N},\bm{N}^{\prime})W_{\bm{N}\bm{N}^{\prime}}\,P_{\bm{N}^{\prime}}.

(38b)

Consequently, the first law of thermodynamics has a closed mesoscopic representation which is identical to the one from Eq. (

We note that after the coarse-graining the heat increment

\displaystyle\!\!\!Q(\bm{N},\bm{N}^{\prime})

\displaystyle\!=\!-\frac{1}{\beta}\ln\frac{W_{\bm{N}\bm{N}^{\prime}}}{W_{\bm{N}^{\prime}\bm{N}}}\!+\!\frac{1}{\beta}\,\Delta S^{int}(\bm{N},\bm{N}^{\prime})

(39a)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\beta}\ln\frac{\tilde{W}_{\bm{N}\bm{N}^{\prime}}}{\tilde{W}_{\bm{N}^{\prime}\bm{N}}},

(39b)

is no longer directly given by the local detailed balance relation like in the microspace, cf. Eq. (
33

)
Esposito 2012
.
We define the system entropy in the mesospace

\displaystyle\left\langle S\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{\bm{N}}P_{\bm{N}}\left(\Omega(\bm{N})-\ln P_{\bm{N}}\right)\,,

(40)

consisting of the non-equilibrium entropy defined by Eq. (

\displaystyle\langle\dot{S}_{e}\rangle=-\sum\limits_{\bm{N},\bm{N}^{\prime}}W_{\bm{N}\bm{N}^{\prime}}\,P_{\bm{N}^{\prime}}\ln\frac{\tilde{W}_{\bm{N}\bm{N}^{\prime}}}{\tilde{W}_{\bm{N}^{\prime}\bm{N}}}=\beta\langle\dot{Q}\rangle,

(41)

and the EP rate

\displaystyle\langle\dot{\Sigma}\rangle=\sum\limits_{\bm{N},\bm{N}^{\prime}}W_{\bm{N}\bm{N}^{\prime}}\,P_{\bm{N}^{\prime}}\,\ln\frac{W_{\bm{N}\bm{N}^{\prime}}P_{\bm{N}^{\prime}}}{W_{\bm{N}^{\prime}\bm{N}}P_{\bm{N}}}\geq 0.

(42)

The definitions in Eqs. (

\displaystyle\!\!\!\!\langle\dot{\Sigma^{s}}\rangle\!=\!\sum\limits_{\bm{N},\bm{N}^{\prime}}\!\!W_{\bm{N}\bm{N}^{\prime}}\,P^{s}_{\bm{N}^{\prime}}\,\ln\frac{\tilde{W}_{\bm{N}\bm{N}^{\prime}}}{\tilde{W}_{\bm{N}^{\prime}\bm{N}}}=-\langle\dot{S}_{e}^{s}\rangle\geq 0,

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}_{t}\,\mathcal{E}=\sum\limits_{i,j}E_{i}\,k_{ij}\,\overline{n}_{j}=\dot{\mathcal{Q}}+\dot{\mathcal{W}},

(44)

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{i,j}f\,\Theta(i,j)\,k_{ij}\,\overline{n}_{j}\,,

(45b)

\displaystyle=-\sum\limits_{i}\overline{n}_{i}\ln\overline{n}_{i},

(46)

\displaystyle\dot{\mathcal{S}}_{e}=-\sum\limits_{i,j}k_{ij}\,\overline{n}_{j}\ln\frac{k_{ij}}{k_{ji}}=\beta\dot{\mathcal{Q}},

(47)

\displaystyle\dot{\mathcal{S}_{i}}=\sum\limits_{i,j}k_{ij}\,\overline{n}_{j}\,\ln\frac{k_{ij}\overline{n}_{j}}{k_{ji}\overline{n}_{i}}\geq 0.

As the MF represents the asymptotic limit of the mesospace, it holds that all the mesoscopic averages of the intensive observables

The non-negativity of the MF EP follows from the non-positivity of the MF heat in this model.
We have thus developed three different levels (microspace, mesospace and MF) to consistently characterize the energetics of our model. For the first law, the lower levels of description are equivalent, while for the second law they only coincide in the stationary limit. The same applies asymptotically in the macroscopic limit to the thermodynamic observables defined at the MF level.

VII

To summarize, we have obtained two major results in this section.
First, though the nonequilibrium phase transitions are naturally only present in the MF-limit where the nonlinear dynamics exhibits the supercritical Hopf and the infinite-period bifurcation, we find that the metastability observed in the finite-system dynamics translates into signatures of genuine nonequilibrium phase transition. This consistently connects linear stochastic dynamics, nonlinear deterministic dynamics, and thermodynamics and furthermore demonstrates that thermodynamics of nonequilibrium phase transitions and bifurcation theory are closely related. Secondly, any finite and attractive interaction in a network reduces the dissipated work per unit. Interestingly, if operating in the synchronous phase, it is even more economic to employ interacting but

In order to construct such an energy converter with our system both a positive force

IX
Conclusion and Perspectives

We introduced and studied a thermodynamically consistent minimal model of
N
driven and globally interacting three-state units obeying linear Markovian dynamics.

The mean-field dynamics (which is exact when
N\to\infty
) exhibits two nonequilibrium phase transitions as a function of the inverse temperature, a Hopf and an infinite-period bifurcation. These separate three distinct phases consisting respectively of a stable fixed point where all units states are equiprobable, a limit cycle corresponding to synchronization of the units, a coexistence of three stable fixed points where the units states have unequal probabilities.

We demonstrated that these transitions are encoded in the spectrum of the generator of the linear Markovian dynamics. The two dominant complex-conjugated eigenvalues, beside the null one, describe the mean-field dynamics over metastable times (i.e. times located between the inverse of the real parts of the next dominant eigenvalues and the inverse of their own real part) which increase with
N
. All predictions based on the spectral analysis were confirmed employing dynamic Monte Carlo simulations.

After having established a nonequilibrium thermodynamics description of our model at different scales, we characterized the nonequilibrium phase transitions using the work dissipated by the external force driving the units. The mean-field dissipated work which reproduces very well the large
N
results undergoes a first order phase transition followed by a second order one as a function of the inverse temperature.
When comparing a single unit to a unit in an interacting network, the average dissipated work for both units is equal in the first phase, while for the interacting unit it remarkably drops in the synchronization phase and drops even further in the third phase. Interestingly, in the presence of interactions and when
N
is too low to produce a meaningful metastable mean-field dynamics, the average dissipated work in the second (resp. third) phase is lower (resp. higher) than for in the mean field (
N\to\infty
).

Finally, when operating our system in the mean-field limit as a work to work converter, we found that the synchronization phase leads to a significant boost in the power output. The efficiency at maximum power of this far-from-equilibrium machine is surprisingly close to the universal linear-regime prediction.

The model we used is minimal in that it contains the minimal ingredients to be thermodynamically consistent and at the same time give rise to a limit cycle. As most minimal stochastic models, it may find various applications (e.g. interacting molecular motors or coupled quantum dots). The methods we used are generic in that they can be used on other models.

A natural extension of this work would consist in analyzing thermodynamic fluctuations in particular close to phase transitions based on
generating
function techniques and large deviation theory.

Another one would be to explore the effects of local interactions and of the network topology on the dissipated work. While the qualitative behavior of synchronization is likely to survive
Wood et al. 2006a
;
Wood et al. 2006b
, new interesting spatiotemporal regimes may emerge
Escaff et al. 2014
.

At the fundamental level, our work shows an instance where bifurcation theory can be augmented with a thermodynamic interpretation to move towards a theory of nonequilibrium phase transitions.
In such a theory, bifurcations would arise from the nonlinearities of the mean-field dynamics which emerges from an underlying stochastic thermodynamics of interacting systems in the macroscopic limit.

From a more utilitarian perspective, our work suggest interesting avenue towards engineering interactions between assemblies of small machines to efficiently generate power, in particular in far-from-equilibrium regimes where nonequilibrium phase transitions may arise.

gives the distance of the inverse temperature to the critical inverse temperature of the Hopf bifurcation and
g=O\big(\begin{Vmatrix}z\end{Vmatrix}\!\textsuperscript{2}\big)
is a smooth function of
(z,z^{*},\Delta\beta)
.

Such a transformation is achieved by first finding the complex eigenvectors
\bm{r}
and
\bm{v}
determined by

\displaystyle\bm{J}(0)\bm{r}=\lambda(0)\bm{r},\quad\bm{J}(0)^{\top}\bm{v}=\lambda(0)^{*}\bm{v},

(59)

where the real and non-symmetric Jacobian
\bm{J}
resulting from the linearization of Eq. (
21
) is evaluated at the bifurcation point
\beta=\beta_{c_{1}}
, yielding

\displaystyle\bm{r}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{1}{2}(-1+\sqrt{3}\,\mathrm{i}),1\right)^{\top}

(60)

\displaystyle\bm{v}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{3-\sqrt{3}\,\mathrm{i}}\left(1+\sqrt{3}\,\mathrm{i},2\right)^{\top}.

(61)

If
|\Delta\beta|
is sufficiently small, the two-dimensional system from Eq. (
21
) can be written as

\displaystyle\dot{\bm{\overline{n}}}=\bm{J}(\Delta\beta)\bm{\overline{n}}+\bm{F}(\bm{\overline{n}},\Delta\beta),

(62)

where
\bm{F}(\bm{\overline{n}},\Delta\beta)
is a smooth vector function whose components have Taylor expansions in
\bm{\overline{n}}
starting with at least quadratic terms,
F_{1,2}
=
O\big(\begin{Vmatrix}\bm{\overline{n}}\end{Vmatrix}\!\textsuperscript{2}\big)
. Using Eq. (

\langle\bm{v},\bm{r}\rangle=1
,

, one can show that

\displaystyle\!\!g(z,z^{*}\!\!,\Delta\beta)\!=\!\langle\bm{v}(\Delta\beta),\bm{F}(z\,\bm{r}(\Delta\beta)\!+\!z^{*}\bm{r}^{*}(\Delta\beta),\Delta\beta)\rangle.

(63)

The function
g
can be formally written as a Taylor series in the two complex variables
z
and
z^{*}
,

\displaystyle g(z,z^{*},\Delta\beta)=\sum\limits_{k+l\geq 2}\frac{1}{k!l!}\frac{\partial^{k+l}}{\partial z^{k}\partial z^{*^{l}}}g_{kl}(\Delta\beta)\,z^{k}z^{*^{l}},

(64)

with

\displaystyle g_{kl}(\Delta\beta)\!\!=\!\!\!\left.\langle\bm{v}(\Delta\beta),\!\bm{F}(\!z\bm{r}(\Delta\beta)\!+\!z^{\!*}\bm{r}^{\!*}(\Delta\beta),\Delta\beta)\rangle\right|_{z=0}\!.

(65)

Moreover, if the function

from Eq. (

\displaystyle F(\bm{x},0)=\frac{1}{2}B(\bm{x},\bm{x})+\frac{1}{6}C(\bm{x},\bm{x},\bm{x})+O\left(\begin{Vmatrix}x\end{Vmatrix}^{4}\right),

where

\displaystyle=\langle\bm{v},B(\bm{r},\bm{r})\rangle=0

(67a)

\displaystyle g_{11}

\displaystyle=\langle\bm{v},B(\bm{r},\bm{r}^{*})\rangle=0

(67b)

\displaystyle g_{21}

\displaystyle=\langle\bm{v},C(\bm{r},\bm{r},\bm{r})\rangle.

(67c)

In coordinates, one has for these vector functions

\displaystyle B_{i}(\bm{x},\bm{y})

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{j,k=1}^{2}\left.\frac{\partial^{2}F_{i}(\bm{\xi},0)}{\partial\xi_{j}\partial\xi_{k}}\right|_{\xi=0}\!\!\!x_{j}\,y_{k},\;\;i\!=\!1,2

(68)

\displaystyle C_{i}(\bm{x},\bm{y},\bm{u})

\displaystyle=\!\!\sum\limits_{j,k,l=1}^{2}\left.\frac{\partial^{3}F_{i}(\bm{\xi},0)}{\partial\xi_{j}\partial\xi_{k}\partial\xi_{l}}\right|_{\xi=0}\!\!\!\!\!x_{j}y_{k}u_{l},\;i\!=\!1,2.

where the eigenvalue of the Jacobian is decomposed as

and

is the LC frequency,

=0.

Appendix B
Equal-probability of stationary microstates belonging to a macrostate

A special case for which also the EP and system entropy can be exactly represented by macrostate ensemble quantities is the nonequilibrium steady state reached at large times. The probabilities associated with states in the stationary regime can be calculated via the spanning tree formula. We denote the graph representing the network by
G
. A spanning tree,
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}(G)
of a graph is defined as a covering subgraph of
G
,
i.e.
all of its edges are also edges of
G
and it contains all vertices (microstates) of
G
. It is furthermore required that
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}(G)
is connected and contains no circuits. We introduce the notation
\mathcal{A}(\mathcal{T}^{\prime(\mu)}_{\alpha}(G))
referring to the
\mu
th spanning tree rooted in
\alpha
, that is a tree whose branches are pointing towards the vertex
\alpha
.
The spanning tree formula states
Schnakenberg 1976

\displaystyle p^{s}_{\alpha}\!\!=\!\frac{\sum\limits_{\mu}\mathcal{A}(\mathcal{T}^{\prime(\mu)}_{\alpha}(G))}{\sum\limits_{\alpha}\sum\limits_{\mu}\mathcal{A}(\mathcal{T}^{\prime(\mu)}_{\alpha}(G))}\!=\!\frac{\sum\limits_{\mathcal{T}^{\prime}_{\alpha}(G)}\prod\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\text{s.t. current}\\
\text{ is directed to }\alpha\end{subarray}}w_{\alpha^{\prime}\alpha^{\prime\prime}}}{\sum\limits_{\alpha}\sum\limits_{\mathcal{T}^{\prime}_{\alpha}(G)}\prod\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\text{s.t. current}\\
\text{ is directed to }\alpha\end{subarray}}w_{\alpha^{\prime}\alpha^{\prime\prime}}}\,.

(74)

As was already discussed above, the transition rates do not depend on the microstates belonging to the same pair of macrostate. Moreover, the connectivity of the network is also not a function of the microstate, since, due to the all-to-all interaction, the number of edges of any vertex in the microspace network is always
2N
, such that the number of spanning trees rooted in
\alpha
is constant for all
\alpha
inside the same macrostate. Thus, at steady state, all microstates constituting the same macrostate

\displaystyle p^{s}_{\alpha}\!

\displaystyle=\!\frac{\!\!\!\!\sum\limits_{\mathcal{T}^{\prime}_{\alpha\in\bm{N}}(G)}\prod\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\text{s.t. current}\\
\text{ is directed to }\alpha\in\bm{N}\end{subarray}}\left.w_{\alpha\alpha^{\prime}}\right|_{\begin{subarray}{c}\alpha\in\bm{N}\\
\alpha^{\prime}\in\bm{N}^{\prime}\end{subarray}}}{\sum\limits_{\alpha\in\bm{N}}\sum\limits_{\mathcal{T}^{\prime}_{\alpha(N)}(G)}\!\!\prod\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\text{s.t. current}\\
\text{ is directed to }\alpha\in\bm{N}\end{subarray}}\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\left.w_{\alpha\alpha^{\prime}}\right|_{\begin{subarray}{c}\alpha\in\bm{N}\\
\alpha^{\prime}\in\bm{N}^{\prime}\end{subarray}}}\!=\!\mathrm{const},

(75)

are equally probable and hence

\displaystyle p^{s}_{\alpha}

\displaystyle=\frac{P^{s}_{\bm{N}}}{\Omega(\bm{N})}\,,

(76)

where
\Omega(\bm{N})
is the number of microstates forming the macrostate
\bm{N}
given by a trinomial coefficient of the occupation numbers
N_{i}
determined in Eq. (
9
).

Appendix C
Stationary solution for single unit

We consider a single unit with states
i=1,2,3
whose evolution is governed by the ME

\displaystyle P_{i}=\sum\limits_{i,j}W_{ij}\,P_{j}\,,

(77)

where
P
is the (macro-)probability to find the unit in the single state
i
with the transition rates

\displaystyle W_{ij}=\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{\beta}{2}\left(\epsilon_{i}-\epsilon_{j}+\Theta(i,j)\,f\right)}\,,

(78)

with the sign function
\Theta(i,j)
as defined in Eq. (
23
) ensuring the validity of local detailed balance.
The steady-state work current reads

\displaystyle\langle\dot{W}^{s}\rangle

\displaystyle=f\,\sum\limits_{i,j}\Theta(i,j)\,W_{ij}\,P^{s}_{\char 17\relax}\,.

(79)

Using the spanning tree formula from Eq. (
74
), one obtains for the stationary probabilities

\displaystyle P_{1}^{s}

\displaystyle=\frac{a_{1}}{a_{1}+a_{2}+a_{3}},\quad P_{2}^{s}=\frac{a_{2}}{a_{1}+a_{2}+a_{3}}\,,

(80)

where

\displaystyle a_{1}

\displaystyle=W_{13}W_{12}\!+\!W_{12}W_{23}\!+\!W_{13}W_{32}

(81a)

\displaystyle a_{2}

\displaystyle=W_{23}W_{31}\!+\!W_{21}W_{13}\!+\!W_{23}W_{21}

(81b)

\displaystyle a_{3}

\displaystyle=W_{31}W_{12}\!+\!W_{32}W_{21}\!+\!W_{32}W_{31},

(81c)

For a flat energy landscape,
\epsilon_{i}
=
\operatorname{const}
, we indeed find that the symmetric stationary solution
P_{i}=1/3
is independent of
\beta
and
f
like in the MF limit. Next, the stationary work current is given by

\displaystyle\langle\dot{W}^{s}\rangle

\displaystyle=3f\,\frac{W_{13}W_{21}W_{32}-W_{31}W_{12}W_{23}}{W_{12}(W_{13}+W_{23}+W_{31})+W_{13}(W_{21}+W_{32})+(W_{21}+W_{31})(W_{13}+W_{23}+W_{31})}

(82)

that simplifies to
\langle\dot{\mathcal{W}}^{s}\rangle
=
2\,\Gamma f\sinh\left(f\beta/2\right)
(see Sec.
VII
).
