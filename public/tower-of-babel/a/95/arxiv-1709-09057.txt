A form of Schwarz’s lemma and a bound for the Kobayashi metric on convex domains Thanks: This work is supported by a scholarship from the Indian Institute of Science
Anwoy Maitra

Abstract
. We present a form of Schwarz’s lemma for holomorphic maps between convex domains D 1 D_{1} and D 2 D_{2} . This result provides a lower bound on the distance between the images of relatively compact subsets of D 1 D_{1} and the boundary of D 2 D_{2} . This is a natural improvement of an old estimate by Bernal-González that takes into account the geometry of ∂ D 1 \partial{D_{1}} . Using similar techniques, we also provide a new estimate for the Kobayashi metric on bounded convex domains.

Primary: 32F45, 32H02

1.
Introduction

In this paper, we shall prove two theorems concerning the Kobayashi geometry of convex domains in
\mathbb{C}^{n}
. In this section, we introduce
these theorems and discuss some of the motivations behind them.

Our first theorem is motivated by the following result of Bernal-González:

Result 1.1

(Bernal-González
[
1
]
)
.

Let
E
,
F
be two complex Banach spaces. Assume that
D_{1}\subseteq E
and
D_{2}\subseteq F
are convex domains and that

D_{1}
is bounded. Fix two points
a\in D_{1}
and
b\in D_{2}
and a real number
r>0
. Then there exists a real number

\sigma=\sigma(a,b,r)>0
such that, for every holomorphic map
\phi:D_{1}\to D_{2}
satisfying
\phi(a)=b
,

\mathrm{dist}\big(\phi(\{z\in D_{1}\mid\mathrm{dist}(z,D^{\mathsf{c}}_{1})>r\}),D^{\mathsf{c}}_{2}\big)\geqslant\sigma,

where

\sigma\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\mathrm{dist}(b,D^{\mathsf{c}}_{2})\exp\left(-\frac{2\mu(a)}{\min\big(r,\mathrm{dist}(a,D^{\mathsf{c}}_{1})\big)}\right),

(1.1)

and where
\mu(a)\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\sup(\{\|z-a\|\mid z\in D_{1}\})
.

While this result is stated in a setting that is very general, the dependence of the lower bound for

\mathrm{dist}\big(\phi(\{z\in D_{1}\mid\mathrm{dist}(z,D^{\mathsf{c}}_{1})>r\}),D^{\mathsf{c}}_{2}\big)
on the parameter

r
seems to be overly conservative, given that

\exp\left(-\frac{2\mu(a)}{\min(r,\mathrm{dist}(a,D^{\mathsf{c}}_{1}))}\right)

decays extremely rapidly as
r\to 0
. Consider, in contrast, the following: if
D_{1}=D_{2}=\mathbb{D}
(in this paper,

\mathbb{D}
will denote the open unit disk centred at
0\in\mathbb{C}
),
E=F=\mathbb{C}
and
a,b\in\mathbb{D}
, in the notation
of Result
1.1
, then it follows from the Schwarz–Pick lemma that for any holomorphic map

\phi:\mathbb{D}\to\mathbb{D}
such that
\phi(a)=b
, and for any
s\in(0,1)
,

\mathrm{dist}\big(\phi(s\mathbb{D}),\mathbb{D}^{\mathsf{c}}\big)\geqslant 4^{-1}(1-s)\,\mathrm{dist}(a,\mathbb{D}^{\mathsf{c}})\,\mathrm{dist}(b,\mathbb{D}^{\mathsf{c}}),

(1.2)

where
(1-s)
serves as the parameter
r
of Result
1.1
. It is natural to think that in finite dimensions,
the bound in (
1.1
) could be replaced by a power of
r
. Interestingly, it turns out that this
power can be arbitrarily large, even in dimension
1
— as we shall see through concrete examples. All of these
form the motivation for Theorem
1.2
below.
In what follows,
\|\boldsymbol{\cdot}\|
will denote the Euclidean norm and the expression
\mathrm{dist}(x,S)
(
S
being a
non-empty set) will be understood in terms of this norm. With these remarks, we can state our first result.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
D_{1}
and
D_{2}
be open convex subsets of
\mathbb{C}^{n}
and
\mathbb{C}^{m}
respectively. Assume
D_{1}
is bounded. Fix
a\in D_{1}
and
b\in D_{2}
.
Then there exist constants
\alpha\geq 1
and
C>0
— where
\alpha
depends only on
D_{1}
and
C
depends only on
D_{1}
and

a
— such that for every holomorphic map
\phi
from
D_{1}
to
D_{2}
with
\phi(a)=b
, and for every
r>0
,

\mathrm{dist}\big(\phi(\{z\in D_{1}\mid\mathrm{dist}(z,D_{1}^{\mathsf{c}})\geqslant r\}),D_{2}^{\mathsf{c}}\big)\geqslant C\,\mathrm{dist}(b,D_{2}^{\mathsf{c}})\,r^{\alpha}.

(1.3)

Moreover, when
\partial D_{1}
is
\mathcal{C}^{2}
-smooth, we can take
\alpha=1
in (
1.3
).

While the dependence on
r
of the lower bound in
(
1.3
)
is a power — which
improves upon the expression (
1.1
) — we reiterate that the exponent can
in general
be
large. In Section
2
we give an example in which any exponent
\alpha
for which the bound
(
1.3
) holds true can be no smaller than a certain large number that is determined by the
geometry of
D_{1}
. Just as discussed
in
[
1
]
, we may view Theorem
1.2
as a form of Schwarz’s lemma for convex domains.

Bernal-González’s result relies upon a well-known estimate for the Carathéodory distance. This estimate actually holds true on
any bounded domain, whereas it is possible to provide sharper estimates on bounded convex domains. This is at the heart of our
improvement of Result
1.1
. It is more convenient to work with the Kobayashi distance. The improved estimate for the
Kobayashi distance that we shall use is due to Mercer
[
9
]
—see Section
3
for details. Our use of Mercer’s
estimate is quite similar to its use recently in
[
11
,
2
]
.

Before we move on to our second result, we need to introduce two pieces of notation:
D(a,r)
will denote the open disk in
\mathbb{C}
with
centre
a
and radius
r
, and
\kappa_{D}(p,\boldsymbol{\cdot})
will denote the Kobayashi pseudo-metric of the domain
D\subseteq\mathbb{C}^{n}
at the point

p\in D
.

It is of interest in many applications to be able to estimate
\kappa_{D}(p,\boldsymbol{\cdot})
. If nothing is assumed about
D
beyond the fact that
it is a bounded convex open set, then the best result that seems to be available is the following one by Graham:

Result 1.3

(Graham
[
5
, Theorem 3]
, also see
[
6
]
)
.

Let
D\subseteq\mathbb{C}^{n}
be a bounded convex open set. Given
p\in D
and
\xi\in T_{p}^{(1,0)}D
,
we let
r
denote the supremum of the radii of the disks centred at
p
, tangent to
\xi
, and included in
D
. Then

\frac{\|\xi\|}{2r}\leqslant\kappa_{D}(p,\xi)\leqslant\frac{\|\xi\|}{r}.

(1.4)

We ought to clarify that the non-trivial bound in (
1.4
) is the lower bound. The upper bound is a consequence
of the metric-decreasing property of holomorphic mappings. The upper bound in (
1.4
) is achieved as an
equality in rare cases. For example, if we take
D=\mathbb{D}
and
p
to be any off-centre point (
p\neq 0
) then the upper
bound for
\kappa_{\mathbb{D}}(p,1)
given by Result
1.3
is
1/(1-|p|)
, whereas the actual value of

\kappa_{\mathbb{D}}(p,1)
is
1/(1-|p|^{2})
, which is less than
1/(1-|p|)
. It is an interesting puzzle to
find a better upper bound that can be stated (as is the case in Result
1.3
) in terms of the positioning of

(p,\xi)
. As the following theorem shows: an upper bound on
\kappa_{D}(p,\xi)
is available that is strictly smaller than that
provided by Result
1.3
for
(p,\xi)
that is, in a certain sense, “generic” (see the concluding sentence of
the following theorem). This theorem also shows that this more efficient bound is governed by one of two regimes,

both
of which do arise (see the examples in Section
2
).

Theorem 1.4
.

Let
D
be a bounded convex open subset of
\mathbb{C}^{n}
. Let
p\in D
and let
\xi\in T_{p}^{(1,0)}D\setminus\{0\}
. Write

\displaystyle D(\xi)

\displaystyle\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\big\{z\in\mathbb{C}\mid p+(z/\|\xi\|)\xi\in D\big\},

\displaystyle r^{\bullet}(p,\xi)

\displaystyle\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\sup(\{r>0\mid\exists\zeta\in D(\xi)\text{ such that }0\in D(\zeta,r)\subseteq D(\xi)\}).

Let

S^{\bullet}(p,\xi)\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\big\{p+(z/\|\xi\|)\xi\mid z\in D(\xi),\;0\in\overline{D(z,r^{\bullet}(p,\xi))}\text{ and }D(z,r^{\bullet}(p,\xi))\subseteq D(\xi)\big\}.

(1.5)

Also, for any
w\in(p+\mathbb{C}\,\xi)
, let
r(w,\xi)
denote the supremum of the radii of the discs centred at
w
, tangent to
\xi
, and
included in
D
. Then

(1)

S^{\bullet}(p,\xi)
is a non-empty compact convex subset of
\mathbb{C}^{n}
(indeed, of
D\cap(p+\mathbb{C}\,\xi)
) and there exists a unique
point
q(\xi)\in S^{\bullet}(p,\xi)
such that

\|q(\xi)-p\|=\mathrm{dist}(p,S^{\bullet}(p,\xi)).

Write
\beta\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=r^{\bullet}(p,\xi)-r(p,\xi)
and
\gamma\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\|q(\xi)-p\|^{2}-\beta^{2}
.

(2)

Suppose
(2r(p,\xi)+\beta)\gamma\leqslant\beta r(p,\xi)^{2}
. Then

\kappa_{D}(p,\xi)\,\leqslant\,\frac{r^{\bullet}(p,\xi)}{r^{\bullet}(p,\xi)^{2}-\|q(\xi)-p\|^{2}}\|\xi\|.

(1.6)

(3)

Suppose
(2r(p,\xi)+\beta)\gamma>\beta r(p,\xi)^{2}
. Then

\kappa_{D}(p,\xi)\,\leqslant\,\frac{1}{2r(p,\xi)}\boldsymbol{\cdot}\frac{\beta^{2}}{\|q(\xi)-p\|(\|q(\xi)-p\|-\sqrt{\gamma})}\|\xi\|.

(1.7)

The upper bounds occurring above
are strictly smaller than
\|\xi\|/r(p,\xi)
unless
q(\xi)=p
.

The above result is also a part of the effort to provide more informative bounds for
\kappa_{D}(p,\boldsymbol{\cdot})
.
There are works describing the contribution of lower order terms in
1/\mathrm{dist}(p,D^{\mathsf{c}})
to asymptotic
expressions and to bounds for
\kappa_{D}(p,\boldsymbol{\cdot})
when
D
is a bounded strongly pseudoconvex domain;
see
[
4
,
8
,
3
]
. The description of the lower order terms in
1/\mathrm{dist}(p,D^{\mathsf{c}})
is in terms of
certain geometric invariants of the (
\mathcal{C}^{2}
-smooth) manifold
\partial D
. While we merely study convex
domains
D\Subset\mathbb{C}^{n}
, we make absolutely no assumptions about the regularity of
\partial D
, whence the latter descriptions
make no sense in general. Instead, we have the estimates of Theorem
1.4
.

The estimates in Theorem
1.4
are often easy to work with (and simplify
to quite natural expressions) when a specific domain is given. Furthermore, these inequalities are sharp. Indeed, the last
sentence of Theorem
1.4
suggests that there are instances where the upper bound
provided by Result
1.3
is not sharp whereas that provided by
Theorem
1.4
is. We provide a class of examples illustrating all
these points in Section
2
.

In Section
3
we present the lemmas that will be needed to prove Theorem
1.2
.
Section
4
contains the proof of, essentially, the planar version of
Theorem
1.4
, from which a substantial part of Theorem
1.4
is derived.
Finally, Sections
5
and
6
contain, respectively, the proofs of
Theorems
1.2
and
1.4
.

2.
Examples

We first present the example alluded to in the paragraph following Theorem
1.2
.

Example 2.1
.

An example of a bounded convex domain
\Omega_{h}\Subset\mathbb{C}
and a holomorphic map
\phi:\Omega_{h}\to\mathbb{D}

where any
\alpha
for which the bound (
1.3
), with
D_{1}=\Omega_{h}
and
D_{2}=\mathbb{D}
, holds
true is large.

Consider, for an arbitrary (small)
h>0
the bounded convex region

\Omega_{h}\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=D\big(i(1-h),1\big)\cap D\big(-i(1-h),1\big).

Write
C_{1}\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\partial D\big(i(1-h),1\big)
and
C_{2}\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\partial D\big(-i(1-h),1\big)
. Then
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
intersect at
two points
c
and
-c
, where

c\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\sqrt{2h-h^{2}}.

Observe that
\Omega_{h}
has the shape of the cross-section of a lens, with vertices
\pm c\in\mathbb{R}
.
Let us construct a biholomorphism
\phi
from
\Omega_{h}
to
\mathbb{D}
. Consider the following four functions:

\displaystyle f_{1}(z)

\displaystyle\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\frac{1}{z-c}\quad\forall\,z\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\{c\},

\displaystyle f_{2}(z)

\displaystyle\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=-\left(z+\frac{1}{2c}\right)\quad\forall\,z\in\mathbb{C},

\displaystyle f_{3}(z)

\displaystyle\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=z^{\beta}\quad\forall\,z\text{ such that }\mathsf{Re}\,z>0,

\displaystyle f_{4}(z)

\displaystyle\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\frac{z-1}{z+1}\quad\forall\,z\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\{-1\},

where
f_{3}
is the holomorphic branch of the
\beta
-th power that maps
\mathbb{R}_{+}
onto
\mathbb{R}_{+}
,

\beta\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\frac{\pi}{2A}\quad\text{and}\quad A\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\arctan\left(\frac{\sqrt{2h-h^{2}}}{1-h}\right).

Here,
2A
is the magnitude of the smaller angle that the circles
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
make with one another at both
c
and
-c
, and also
(by conformality) the acute angle between the lines
L_{1}
and
L_{2}
, which the circles
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
get mapped to, respectively,
under
f_{1}
, at their point of intersection,
-\frac{1}{2c}
. The composition

\phi\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=f_{4}\circ f_{3}\circ f_{2}\circ f_{1}
makes sense on
\Omega_{h}
,
and it follows from standard facts about Möbius transformations that it is a biholomorphism from
\Omega_{h}
to
\mathbb{D}
. The
explicit expression for
\phi
is given by

\phi(z)=\frac{\left(\frac{z+c}{2c(c-z)}\right)^{\beta}-1}{\left(\frac{z+c}{2c(c-z)}\right)^{\beta}+1}.

Now, for
t>0
small, consider the point
c-t
of
\Omega_{h}
. A simple geometric argument shows that the distance
d(t)
of this point
from
\Omega_{h}^{\mathsf{c}}
is

d(t)=t\left(\frac{2c-t}{1+\sqrt{t^{2}-2ct+1}}\right).

Therefore
d(t)\approx t
as
t\to 0
. Consider the set

\Omega_{h,t}\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\{z\in\Omega_{h}\mid\mathrm{dist}(z,\Omega_{h}^{\mathsf{c}})\geqslant d(t)\}.

Note that
c-t
is a boundary point of
\Omega_{h,t}
. Therefore
\phi(c-t)\in\phi\big(\Omega_{h,t}\big)
; it, too, is
a boundary point. Also,

\phi(c-t)=\frac{\left(\frac{2c-t}{2ct}\right)^{\beta}-1}{\left(\frac{2c-t}{2ct}\right)^{\beta}+1}.

The distance of
\phi(c-t)
from
\mathbb{D}^{\mathsf{c}}
is

1-\phi(c-t)=\frac{2(2ct)^{\beta}}{(2c-t)^{\beta}+(2ct)^{\beta}}.

Therefore, denoting by
d^{\prime}(t)
the distance of
\phi(c-t)
from
\mathbb{D}^{\mathsf{c}}
, we see that

d^{\prime}(t)\approx t^{\beta}
as
t\to 0
.

Now,
\mathrm{dist}\big(\phi(\Omega_{h,t}),\mathbb{D}^{\mathsf{c}}\big)\leqslant d^{\prime}(t)
and so

\mathrm{dist}\big(\phi(\Omega_{h,t}),\mathbb{D}^{\mathsf{c}}\big)=O(t^{\beta})
as
t\to 0
. Let us
write

I\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\inf\{\alpha>0\mid\exists C(\alpha)>0\text{ such that }\mathrm{dist}\big(\phi(\Omega_{h,t}),\mathbb{D}^{\mathsf{c}}\big)\geqslant C(\alpha)d(t)^{\alpha}\text{ as $t\to 0$}\}.

Recall that
d(t)\approx t
as
t\to 0
. Clearly, then,
I\nless\beta
. I.e., no exponent smaller than
\beta

would suffice for the bound (
1.3
) in this example. Finally, observe that
\beta
can
be made arbitrarily large by taking
h
to be sufficiently small.

Example 2.2
.

A family of examples for which the bound provided by Theorem
1.4
is sharp while the
upper bound provided by Result
1.3
is
strictly
greater.

Consider
D=\mathbb{B}^{n}
, the unit Euclidean ball with centre
0\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
. Let us consider a point
p\in\mathbb{B}^{n}\setminus\{0\}
. By the fact that unitary transformations are holomorphic automorphisms of
\mathbb{B}^{n}
, it suffices to consider

(p,\xi)
of the form
\big(x,(\|\xi\|,0,\ldots,0)\big)
,
x\in\mathbb{B}^{n}
. Let us write

v\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=(\|\xi\|,0,\ldots,0)
. We leave it to the reader to verify that

\displaystyle D(v)=D\big(-x_{1},\sqrt{1-\|x^{\prime}\|^{2}}\big),

\displaystyle\;\quad r^{\bullet}(x,v)=\sqrt{1-\|x^{\prime}\|^{2}},

\displaystyle S^{\bullet}(x,v)=\{(0,x^{\prime})\},

\displaystyle\;\quad q(v)=(0,x^{\prime}),

where we write
x=(x_{1},x^{\prime})
. It is easy to see that the expression
(2r(x,v)+\beta)\gamma
reduces to
0
, whence, by
item (
2
) of Theorem
1.4
, we get
the bound (note that
\|\xi\|=\|v\|
)

\kappa_{\mathbb{B}^{n}}(x,v)\leqslant\frac{\sqrt{1-\|x^{\prime}\|^{2}}}{1-\|x\|^{2}}\|\xi\|.

(2.1)

The exact expression for
\kappa_{\mathbb{B}^{n}}(p,\xi)
— see
[
7
, Section 3.5]
, for instance — is as follows:

\kappa_{\mathbb{B}^{n}}(p,\xi)=\kappa_{\mathbb{B}^{n}}(x,v)=\left(\frac{\|v\|^{2}}{1-\|x\|^{2}}+\frac{|\langle v,x\rangle|^{2}}{(1-\|x\|^{2})^{2}}\right)^{1/2}=\frac{\big(1-(\|x\|^{2}-|x_{1}|^{2})\big)^{1/2}}{1-\|x\|^{2}}\|\xi\|.

We see that whenever
p\neq 0
and
\xi\in\mathbb{C}{p}
, the unitary transformation that maps

\xi
to
(\|\xi\|,0,\ldots,0)
(and
p
to
x
) gives
x=(c\|p\|,0,\dots,0)
, where
c
is some complex
number with
|c|=1
. Since, for any such
(p,\xi)
,
x^{\prime}=0
, it follows from (
2.1
) that
the bound for
\kappa_{\mathbb{B}^{n}}(p,\xi)
provided by Theorem
1.4
is exactly
equal to
\kappa_{\mathbb{B}^{n}}(p,\xi)
, whereas the upper bound
provided by Result
1.3
is
\|\xi\|/(1-\|p\|)
, which is strictly greater.

Example 2.3
.

An example showing that the condition appearing in Item
3
of
Theorem
1.4
holds in simple situations.

Before presenting this example, we observe that the condition appearing in
Item
2
of Theorem
1.4

holds for the family of examples discussed in Example
2.2
.
Now, consider the domain

\displaystyle D\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=

\displaystyle\Big\{z\in\mathbb{C}\mid(|z|<1)\text{ or }\Big(2^{-1}<\mathsf{Re}(z)<2\text{ and }|\mathsf{Im}(z)|<\frac{2}{\sqrt{3}}\big(1-2^{-1}\mathsf{Re}(z)\big)\,\Big)\Big\}.

This is the unit disk together with all those
z\in\mathbb{C}
such that
\mathsf{Re}(z)>1/2
and such that
z
lies in the
angle formed by the tangent lines to the unit circle at the points
(1/2,\sqrt{3}/2)
and

(1/2,-\sqrt{3}/2)
. Consider the open subset
U
of
D
given by

\left\{z=x+iy\in\mathbb{C}\mid\sqrt{3}/2<x<1,\,|y|<5/7\sqrt{3}\,\right\}\cap\mathbb{D}.

We will show that, for every
z\in U
, the condition in Item
3
of
Theorem
1.4
holds for the pair
(z,1)
(and therefore, since we are in one dimension,
for any pair

(z,\xi)
, where
\xi\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}
). By the symmetry of
D
about the real axis, it suffices to
show that the condition holds for every
z\in U
with
y\geqslant 0
. For such a
z
,
r(z,1)
equals
the distance from
z
to the tangent to the unit circle at
(1/2,\sqrt{3}/2)
,
which is
(2-x-\sqrt{3}y)/2
. Also, for such a
z
,

r^{\bullet}(z,1)=1
and
S^{\bullet}(z,1)=\{0\}
. So, necessarily,
q(1)=0
. The quantity of our interest is

(2r(z,1)+\beta)\gamma-\beta r(z,1)^{2},

which, after substituting the expressions for
\beta
and
\gamma
, is:

|q(1)-z|^{2}\big(r^{\bullet}(z,1)+r(z,1)\big)-r^{\bullet}(z,1)^{2}\big(r^{\bullet}(z,1)-r(z,1)\big).

Substituting the actual values, we get:

\displaystyle(x^{2}+y^{2})\left(1+\frac{2-x-\sqrt{3}y}{2}\right)

\displaystyle-\left(1-\frac{2-x-\sqrt{3}y}{2}\right)

\displaystyle>\frac{(3/4)(4-x-\sqrt{3}y)-x-\sqrt{3}y}{2}

\displaystyle[\text{since }x>\sqrt{3}/2]

\displaystyle=\frac{12-7x-7\sqrt{3}y}{8}>\frac{5-7\sqrt{3}y}{8}

\displaystyle[\text{since }x<1]

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\ \;>0\quad

\displaystyle[\text{since }y<\tfrac{5}{7\sqrt{3}}].

This shows that for every
z\in U
, the condition appearing
in Item
3

of Theorem
1.4
holds for the pair
(z,1)
.

3.
Preliminary lemmas

In order to prove Theorem
1.2
, one needs to efficiently estimate the Kobayashi distance on
D_{1}
. One of the most basic
estimates, which holds true on any bounded domain
\Omega
, is that, given a compact
convex
subset
K
of
\Omega
, the Kobayashi
distance
\mathsf{k}_{\Omega}
has the following upper bound:

\mathsf{k}_{\Omega}(z,w)\leqslant\frac{1}{\mathrm{dist}(K,\Omega^{\mathsf{c}})}\|z-w\|\quad\forall\,z,w\in K.

(3.1)

This is essentially the estimate that is used by Bernal-González (he uses the Carathéodory distance, for which an analogue of
(
3.1
) holds). We need a more efficient upper bound. By the nature of these estimates, this is a challenge
only close to
\partial D_{1}
. Now (
3.1
) arises from a comparison between the Kobayashi
metrics
of

\Omega
and of an appropriate Euclidean ball embedded into
\Omega
. This comparison yields the following inequality:

\kappa_{\Omega}(p,\xi)\leqslant\frac{\|\xi\|}{\mathrm{dist}(p,\Omega^{\mathsf{c}})},\quad p\in\Omega,\ \xi\in T^{(1,0)}_{p}(\Omega).

(3.2)

The above suggests that a more efficient estimate for
\mathsf{k}_{D_{1}}
could, in principle, be obtained by a comparison between the
Kobayashi metrics of
D_{1}
and of (the embedded image of) some class of planar regions that are better adapted to the shape of
\partial D_{1}
. This leads us to appeal to an idea described and used by Mercer
[
9
]
. We consider the class of regions in
\mathbb{C}
defined as
follows: for every
\alpha>1
, let
\Lambda_{\alpha}
denote the image of
\mathbb{D}
under the holomorphic mapping

f\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=z\mapsto(z+1)^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}:\{w\in\mathbb{C}\mid\mathsf{Re}\,w>-1\}\to\mathbb{C}.

For
\alpha=2
,
\Lambda_{\alpha}
is the interior of one loop of the lemniscate. The following two results are proved in
[
9
, pp.
203–204]
:

Lemma 3.1

(Mercer,
[
9
, Lemma 2.1]
)
.

Let
z_{0}\in\Lambda_{\alpha}
. Then there exists a
C>0
such that, for all
z\in\Lambda_{\alpha}
,

\mathsf{k}_{\Lambda_{\alpha}}(z_{0},z)\leqslant C+\frac{\alpha}{2}\log\left(\frac{1}{\mathrm{dist}(z,\Lambda_{\alpha}^{\mathsf{c}})}\right).

The above lemma; a result on how the domains
\Lambda_{\alpha}
relate to a given convex, planar domain; and a comparison between the
Kobayashi distances of
\Omega
(as below) and of a suitable affine embedding of
\Lambda_{\alpha}
into
\Omega
yield the result that
we need:

Lemma 3.2

(Mercer,
[
9
, Proposition 2.3]
)
.

Let
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{C}^{n}
be a bounded convex domain, and let
z_{0}\in\Omega
. Then there are constants
\alpha>1
and
C(z_{0})>0

such that, for every
z\in\Omega
,

\mathsf{k}_{\Omega}(z_{0},z)\leqslant C(z_{0})+\frac{\alpha}{2}\log\left(\frac{1}{\mathrm{dist}(z,\Omega^{\mathsf{c}})}\right).

The bound in the above lemma can be tighter if
\Omega
, in addition to the properties stated in
Lemma
3.2
, has
\mathcal{C}^{2}
-smooth boundary. In that case, one can carry out the
procedure hinted at prior to Lemma
3.2
with
\Lambda_{\alpha}
replaced by the
unit disk
\mathbb{D}
. This argument is very classical and widely known. Its first step is the analogue of
Lemma
3.1
for
\mathbb{D}
, which is just a direct calculation: fixing a
z_{0}\in\mathbb{D}
,
there exists a
C>0
such that

\mathsf{k}_{\mathbb{D}}(z_{0},z)\leqslant C+\frac{1}{2}\log\left(\frac{1}{\mathrm{dist}(z,\mathbb{D}^{\mathsf{c}})}\right)

for all
z\in\mathbb{D}
. This leads to the classical result:

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{C}^{n}
be a bounded convex domain whose boundary is
\mathcal{C}^{2}
-smooth. Let
z_{0}\in\Omega
. Then there is a constant

C(z_{0})>0
such that, for every
z\in\Omega
,

\mathsf{k}_{\Omega}(z_{0},z)\leqslant C(z_{0})+\frac{1}{2}\log\left(\frac{1}{\mathrm{dist}(z,\Omega^{\mathsf{c}})}\right).

Finally, we have the following lemma that gives a useful estimate on
\mathsf{k}_{\Omega}

from below
:

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
\Omega\varsubsetneq\mathbb{C}^{n}
be a convex domain. Then

\mathsf{k}_{\Omega}(z,w)\geqslant\frac{1}{2}\log\left(\frac{\mathrm{dist}(w,\Omega^{\mathsf{c}})}{\mathrm{dist}(z,\Omega^{\mathsf{c}})}\right)\quad\forall\,z,w\in\Omega.

Proof.

Fix
z,w\in\Omega
. Choose
q\in\partial\Omega
such that
\mathrm{dist}(z,\Omega^{\mathsf{c}})=\|z-q\|
. By the convexity of
\Omega
,
we may choose a
\mathbb{C}
-linear functional
F:\mathbb{C}^{n}\to\mathbb{C}
such that

\Omega\subseteq\{x\in\mathbb{C}^{n}\mid\mathsf{Im}(F(x-q))>0\},

i.e., such that

H\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\{x\in\mathbb{C}^{n}\mid\mathsf{Im}(F(x-q))=0\}

is a supporting hyperplane for
\Omega
at
q
. In fact, we can choose
F
such that, for every

x\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
,
|\mathsf{Im}(F(x-q))|=\mathrm{dist}(x,H)
. Consider the
\mathbb{C}
-affine function
T
on
\mathbb{C}^{n}

given by

T(x)\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=F(x-q)\quad\forall\,x\in\mathbb{C}^{n}.

Then,
T
maps
\Omega
holomorphically into the upper half plane

\mathbb{H}
. By the Kobayashi-distance-decreasing property of
T
and the formula for
the Kobayashi distance in
\mathbb{H}
,

\mathsf{k}_{\Omega}(z,w)\geqslant\mathsf{k}_{\mathbb{H}}(T(z),T(w))\geqslant\frac{1}{2}\log\left(\frac{\mathsf{Im}(T(w))}{\mathsf{Im}(T(z))}\right)=\frac{1}{2}\log\left(\frac{\mathsf{Im}(F(w-q))}{\mathsf{Im}(F(z-q))}\right).

Recall that
\mathsf{Im}(F(z-q))=\mathrm{dist}(z,H)=\mathrm{dist}(z,\Omega^{\mathsf{c}})
. Furthermore,
\mathsf{Im}(F(w-q))=\mathrm{dist}(w,H)\geqslant\mathrm{dist}(w,\Omega^{\mathsf{c}})
.
Therefore, the sequence of inequalities above gives

\mathsf{k}_{\Omega}(z,w)\geqslant\frac{1}{2}\log\left(\frac{\mathrm{dist}(w,\Omega^{\mathsf{c}})}{\mathrm{dist}(z,\Omega^{\mathsf{c}})}\right).

Since the points
z,w\in\Omega
were arbitrarily chosen, we have the conclusion desired. ∎

4.
Lemmas concerning planar convex domains

In this section we will state and prove a number of lemmas about planar convex domains, which will be used to prove our second result. We
abbreviate
\mathrm{dist}(x,\Omega^{\mathsf{c}})
to
\delta_{\Omega}(x)
in this section.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{C}
be an open convex set and let
p,\zeta\in\Omega
. Let
R(p),R(\zeta)>0
be such that
D(p,R(p))\subseteq\Omega
and
D(\zeta,R(\zeta))\subseteq\Omega
. Then, for every
t\in[0,1]
,
\Omega
includes the disk in
\mathbb{C}
with centre
(1-t)p+t\zeta
and radius
(1-t)R(p)+tR(\zeta)
.

We omit the proof because it is straightforward. The main idea behind the proof is to show that the disk described in the above lemma is
contained in the convex hull of the union of the disks
D(p,R(p))
and
D(\zeta,R(\zeta))
.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{C}
be an open convex set and let
p\in\Omega
. Suppose
\zeta\in\Omega
is such that

p\in\overline{D\big(\zeta,\delta_{\Omega}(\zeta)\big)}.

(4.1)

Then, for every
t\in[0,1)
,

p\in D\big((1-t)p+t\zeta,(1-t)\delta_{\Omega}(p)+t\delta_{\Omega}(\zeta)\big)\subseteq\Omega.

Now suppose
\delta_{\Omega}(\zeta)>\delta_{\Omega}(p)
. Let
r_{\zeta}(t)\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=(1-t)\delta_{\Omega}(p)+t\delta_{\Omega}(\zeta)\;\forall\,t\in[0,1]
, let
\alpha\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=|\zeta-p|
and let
\beta\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\delta_{\Omega}(\zeta)-\delta_{\Omega}(p)
. If we consider the mapping

\phi_{\zeta}\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=t\mapsto\frac{r_{\zeta}(t)}{r_{\zeta}(t)^{2}-t^{2}|\zeta-p|^{2}}:[0,1)\to\mathbb{R},

then

(1)

\phi_{\zeta}
is differentiable;

(2)

If
(\alpha^{2}-\beta^{2})(2\delta_{\Omega}(p)+\beta)\leqslant\beta\delta_{\Omega}(p)^{2}
then
\phi_{\zeta}
is continuously extendable to

[0,1]
and the minimum value of
\phi_{\zeta}
is

\frac{\delta_{\Omega}(\zeta)}{\delta_{\Omega}(\zeta)^{2}-|\zeta-p|^{2}};

(3)

If
(\alpha^{2}-\beta^{2})(2\delta_{\Omega}(p)+\beta)>\beta\delta_{\Omega}(p)^{2}
then
\phi_{\zeta}
attains its minimum value at

t(\zeta)\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\delta_{\Omega}(p)\frac{-(\alpha^{2}-\beta^{2})+\alpha\sqrt{\alpha^{2}-\beta^{2}}}{(\alpha^{2}-\beta^{2})\beta}\in(0,1)

and its minimum value is

\phi_{\zeta}\big(t(\zeta)\big)=\frac{1}{2\delta_{\Omega}(p)}\boldsymbol{\cdot}\frac{\beta^{2}}{\alpha\big(\alpha-\sqrt{\alpha^{2}-\beta^{2}}\big)}.

(4)

Finally, the minimum value of
\phi_{\zeta}
is less than
\frac{1}{\delta_{\Omega}(p)}
.

Proof.

In order to prove that

p\in D\big((1-t)p+t\zeta,(1-t)\delta_{\Omega}(p)+t\delta_{\Omega}(\zeta)\big)\subseteq\Omega,

we first have to prove that
|p-\big((1-t)p+t\zeta\big)|=t|\zeta-p|<(1-t)\delta_{\Omega}(p)+t\delta_{\Omega}(\zeta)
. But by the
condition (
4.1
),

t|\zeta-p|\leqslant t\delta_{\Omega}(\zeta)<(1-t)\delta_{\Omega}(p)+t\delta_{\Omega}(\zeta)\quad\forall\,t<1.

(4.2)

The inclusion statement follows from Lemma
4.1
.

Turning to
\phi_{\zeta}
, it is clear from (
4.2
) that it is well-defined and differentiable on
[0,1)
.

Suppose first that
(\alpha^{2}-\beta^{2})(2\delta_{\Omega}(p)+\beta)\leqslant\beta\delta_{\Omega}(p)^{2}
. Then note that necessarily

|\zeta-p|<\delta_{\Omega}(\zeta)
. To see this, suppose
|\zeta-p|=\delta_{\Omega}(\zeta)
. Then

\displaystyle(\alpha^{2}-\beta^{2})(2\delta_{\Omega}(p)+\beta)

\displaystyle=\big(\delta_{\Omega}(\zeta)^{2}-(\delta_{\Omega}(\zeta)-\delta_{\Omega}(p))^{2}\big)(\delta_{\Omega}(\zeta)+\delta_{\Omega}(p))

\displaystyle=2\delta_{\Omega}(\zeta)^{2}\delta_{\Omega}(p)+\delta_{\Omega}(\zeta)\delta_{\Omega}(p)^{2}-\delta_{\Omega}(p)^{3}

\displaystyle>\beta\delta_{\Omega}(p)^{2},

which is a contradiction. So
|\zeta-p|<\delta_{\Omega}(\zeta)
and this shows that the expression for
\phi_{\zeta}(t)
makes sense for
t\in[0,1]
. Moreover,
\phi_{\zeta}
is
differentiable on
[0,1]
in this case. A calculation shows that

\phi_{\zeta}^{\prime}(t)=\frac{-\beta\delta_{\Omega}(p)^{2}+(\alpha^{2}-\beta^{2})(2\delta_{\Omega}(p)t+\beta t^{2})}{\big(r_{\zeta}(t)^{2}-t^{2}\alpha^{2}\big)^{2}}.

If
\alpha\leqslant\beta
then clearly
\phi_{\zeta}^{\prime}(t)<0
for all
t\in[0,1]
. And if
\alpha>\beta
, then, for all
t\in[0,1)
,

(\alpha^{2}-\beta^{2})(2\delta_{\Omega}(p)t+\beta t^{2})<(\alpha^{2}-\beta^{2})(2\delta_{\Omega}(p)+\beta)\leqslant\beta\delta_{\Omega}(p)^{2},

whence

\phi_{\zeta}^{\prime}(t)<0\quad\forall\,t\in[0,1).

(4.3)

So, in the case under consideration, one invariably has that
\phi_{\zeta}
attains its minimum value at
1
, and the minimum value is

\frac{\delta_{\Omega}(\zeta)}{\delta_{\Omega}(\zeta)^{2}-|\zeta-p|^{2}}.

Furthermore, by (
4.3
), the minimum value above is less than
\frac{1}{\delta_{\Omega}(p)}
.

Suppose now that
(\alpha^{2}-\beta^{2})(2\delta_{\Omega}(p)+\beta)>\beta\delta_{\Omega}(p)^{2}
. Then one necessarily has
\alpha>\beta
.
Furthermore, one expects a critical point of
\phi_{\zeta}
in
(0,1)
. In this case, by calculating the critical points of
\phi_{\zeta}
, we
obtain that
\phi_{\zeta}
attains its minimum value at

t(\zeta)\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\delta_{\Omega}(p)\frac{-(\alpha^{2}-\beta^{2})+\alpha\sqrt{\alpha^{2}-\beta^{2}}}{(\alpha^{2}-\beta^{2})\beta},

which is a point of
(0,1)
, and that the minimum value of
\phi_{\zeta}
is

\phi_{\zeta}\big(t(\zeta)\big)=\frac{1}{2\delta_{\Omega}(p)}\boldsymbol{\cdot}\frac{\beta^{2}}{\alpha^{2}-\alpha\sqrt{\alpha^{2}-\beta^{2}}}.

The following calculation shows that the minimum value above is
less than

\frac{1}{\delta_{\Omega}(p)}
:

\displaystyle\frac{\beta^{2}}{\alpha^{2}-\alpha\sqrt{\alpha^{2}-\beta^{2}}}

\displaystyle<2

\displaystyle\iff

\displaystyle 4\alpha^{2}(\alpha^{2}-\beta^{2})

\displaystyle<(2\alpha^{2}-\beta^{2})^{2}

(in this case
\alpha^{2}-\beta^{2}>0
)

\displaystyle\iff

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle<\beta^{4}.

As the last inequality is true, together with what we obtained in the other case, we get (
4
).
∎

Before we state our last lemma we need to make two definitions. For
\Omega
an open
bounded
convex subset of
\mathbb{C}
and for
p\in\Omega
, we let

r^{\bullet}_{\Omega}(p)\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\sup\big(\{\,r>0\mid\exists\zeta\in\Omega\text{ such that }p\in D(\zeta,r)\subseteq\Omega\,\}\big)

(4.4)

and we let

S^{\bullet}_{\Omega}(p)\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\{\,\zeta\in\Omega\mid p\in\overline{D(\zeta,r^{\bullet}_{\Omega}(p))}\text{ and }D(\zeta,r^{\bullet}_{\Omega}(p))\subseteq\Omega\}.

(4.5)

Proposition 4.3
.

Let
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{C}
be an open bounded convex set and let
p\in\Omega
. For any
\zeta\in\Omega
such that
\delta_{\Omega}(\zeta)>\delta_{\Omega}(p)
, let
\phi_{\zeta}
denote the same function as in Lemma
4.2
. Then

(1)

S^{\bullet}_{\Omega}(p)
is a non-empty compact convex subset of
\Omega
.

(2)

There exists a unique point
\zeta\in S^{\bullet}_{\Omega}(p)
such that

|\zeta-p|=\mathrm{dist}(p,S^{\bullet}_{\Omega}(p)).

(4.6)

In the next two statements,
\zeta
is the point in
S^{\bullet}_{\Omega}(p)
introduced in (
2
).

(3)

Suppose
\big(|\zeta-p|^{2}-(r^{\bullet}_{\Omega}(p)-\delta_{\Omega}(p))^{2}\big)\big(r^{\bullet}_{\Omega}(p)+\delta_{\Omega}(p)\big)\leqslant(r^{\bullet}_{\Omega}(p)-\delta_{\Omega}(p))\delta_{\Omega}(p)^{2}
. Then

\kappa_{\Omega}(p,1)\leqslant\frac{r^{\bullet}_{\Omega}(p)}{r^{\bullet}_{\Omega}(p)^{2}-|\zeta-p|^{2}}\leqslant\frac{1}{\delta_{\Omega}(p)},

(4.7)

where the latter is an equality if and only if
\zeta=p
.

(4)

Suppose
\big(|\zeta-p|^{2}-(r^{\bullet}_{\Omega}(p)-\delta_{\Omega}(p))^{2}\big)\big(r^{\bullet}_{\Omega}(p)+\delta_{\Omega}(p)\big)>(r^{\bullet}_{\Omega}(p)-\delta_{\Omega}(p))\delta_{\Omega}(p)^{2}
. Then, with
\alpha
and
\beta

denoting the same quantities as in Lemma
4.2
,

\kappa_{\Omega}(p,1)\leqslant\frac{1}{2\delta_{\Omega}(p)}\boldsymbol{\cdot}\frac{\beta^{2}}{\alpha\big(\alpha-\sqrt{\alpha^{2}-\beta^{2}}\big)}<\frac{1}{\delta_{\Omega}(p)}.

(4.8)

Proof.

First we prove that
S^{\bullet}_{\Omega}(p)
is non-empty. Choose an increasing sequence
(r_{\nu})_{\nu\geqslant 1}
from the set occurring
in (
4.4
) such that
r_{\nu}>r^{\bullet}_{\Omega}(p)-\tfrac{1}{\nu}
. For each
\nu
, there is a
\zeta_{\nu}\in\Omega

such that

p\in D(\zeta_{\nu},r_{\nu})\subseteq\Omega.

By the boundedness of
\Omega
, there exists a
w\in\overline{\Omega}
such that (without loss of generality)
\zeta_{\nu}\to w
.
Since, for each
\nu
,
|p-\zeta_{\nu}|<r_{\nu}
, therefore, by taking the limit,
|p-w|\leqslant r^{\bullet}_{\Omega}(p)
, i.e.,
p\in\overline{D(w,r^{\bullet}_{\Omega}(p))}
. Now suppose
x\in D(w,r^{\bullet}_{\Omega}(p))
. Let
\epsilon\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=(r^{\bullet}_{\Omega}(p)-|x-w|)/2
.
Choose
\nu^{\prime}\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
such that
r^{\bullet}_{\Omega}(p)-r_{\nu^{\prime}}<\epsilon
. Then choose
\nu\geqslant\nu^{\prime}
such that
|w-\zeta_{\nu}|<\epsilon
. Then,

|x-\zeta_{\nu}|\leqslant|x-w|+|w-\zeta_{\nu}|<|x-w|+\epsilon=r^{\bullet}_{\Omega}(p)-\epsilon<r_{\nu^{\prime}}\leqslant r_{\nu}.

So,
x\in D(\zeta_{\nu},r_{\nu})
, whence
x\in\Omega
. Therefore
D(w,r^{\bullet}_{\Omega}(p))\subseteq\Omega
. So
w\in S^{\bullet}_{\Omega}(p)
, whence
S^{\bullet}_{\Omega}(p)
is non-empty. Hence it makes sense to talk of points of
S^{\bullet}_{\Omega}(p)
at least distance
from
p
. Also, note that if
w\in S^{\bullet}_{\Omega}(p)
then
\delta_{\Omega}(w)=r^{\bullet}_{\Omega}(p)
(since
\delta_{\Omega}(w)\geqslant r^{\bullet}_{\Omega}(p)
, and if strict inequality held then the maximality of
r^{\bullet}_{\Omega}(p)
would be contradicted).

Now we prove the compactness of
S^{\bullet}_{\Omega}(p)
. Since
S^{\bullet}_{\Omega}(p)
is a bounded subset of
\mathbb{C}
,
it suffices to prove that it is a closed subset of
\mathbb{C}
. So suppose
(\zeta_{\nu})_{\nu\geqslant 1}
is a sequence of points of

S^{\bullet}_{\Omega}(p)
converging to
w\in\mathbb{C}
.
That
p\in\overline{D(w,r^{\bullet}_{\Omega}(p))}
is obvious.
Now we show that
D(w,r^{\bullet}_{\Omega}(p))\subseteq\Omega
.
Suppose
x\in D(w,r^{\bullet}_{\Omega}(p))
. Let
\epsilon\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=r^{\bullet}_{\Omega}(p)-|x-w|
. Choose
\nu\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
such that
|w-\zeta_{\nu}|<\epsilon
. Then

|x-\zeta_{\nu}|\leqslant|x-w|+|w-\zeta_{\nu}|<r^{\bullet}_{\Omega}(p).

So
x\in D(\zeta_{\nu},r^{\bullet}_{\Omega}(p))
, whence
x\in\Omega
. As this is true for any
x\in D(w,r^{\bullet}_{\Omega}(p))
, the latter is
a subset of
\Omega
. So
w\in S^{\bullet}_{\Omega}(p)
and this argument shows that
S^{\bullet}_{\Omega}(p)
is a compact subset of
\Omega
.

Finally we prove that
S^{\bullet}_{\Omega}(p)
is convex. To this end, suppose
\zeta_{1},\zeta_{2}\in S^{\bullet}_{\Omega}(p)
and that
t\in[0,1]
. We
want to prove that
(1-t)\zeta_{1}+t\zeta_{2}\in S^{\bullet}_{\Omega}(p)
. In order to do this we have to prove that
D\big((1-t)\zeta_{1}+t\zeta_{2},r^{\bullet}_{\Omega}(p)\big)\subseteq\Omega
and that
p\in\overline{D\big((1-t)\zeta_{1}+t\zeta_{2},r^{\bullet}_{\Omega}(p)\big)}
.
The first inclusion follows from Lemma
4.1
. As for the second containment,

\displaystyle|p-((1-t)\zeta_{1}+t\zeta_{2})|

\displaystyle=|((1-t)p+tp)-((1-t)\zeta_{1}+t\zeta_{2})|

\displaystyle\leqslant(1-t)|p-\zeta_{1}|+t|p-\zeta_{2}|

\displaystyle\leqslant(1-t)r^{\bullet}_{\Omega}(p)+tr^{\bullet}_{\Omega}(p)=r^{\bullet}_{\Omega}(p).

This shows that
(1-t)\zeta_{1}+t\zeta_{2}\in S^{\bullet}_{\Omega}(p)
, which proves that
S^{\bullet}_{\Omega}(p)
is convex.
This proves (
1
).

We equip
\mathbb{C}
with the standard Hilbert space structure from which the Euclidean norm arises. Note that the expressions in
(
4.6
) are derived from the Euclidean norm. Since
S^{\bullet}_{\Omega}(p)
is closed and convex, it follows from
a theorem in the elementary theory of Hilbert spaces (see
[
10
, Theorem 4.10]
, for instance) that there is a unique
\zeta\in S^{\bullet}_{\Omega}(p)
such that (
4.6
) holds.

Now suppose the condition in (
3
) holds. We divide the discussion into two further sub-cases:

Sub-case (a)

r^{\bullet}_{\Omega}(p)=\delta_{\Omega}(p)
.

In this case,
p\in S^{\bullet}_{\Omega}(p)
and so
\zeta
must be
p
. Consequently, in this case,

\kappa_{\Omega}(p,1)\leqslant\frac{1}{\delta_{\Omega}(p)}=\frac{r^{\bullet}_{\Omega}(p)}{r^{\bullet}_{\Omega}(p)^{2}-|\zeta-p|^{2}},

where we have used the estimate (
3.2
) to write the first inequality.

Sub-case (b)

r^{\bullet}_{\Omega}(p)>\delta_{\Omega}(p)
.

In this case, we note that, since
\delta_{\Omega}(\zeta)=r^{\bullet}_{\Omega}(p)
and therefore
\delta_{\Omega}(\zeta)>\delta_{\Omega}(p)
, we can appeal to Lemma
4.2
. By that lemma we have, for an arbitrary
t\in[0,1)
,
p\in D\big((1-t)p+t\zeta,r_{\zeta}(t)\big)
. Now we estimate
\kappa_{\Omega}(p,1)
. For
t\in[0,1)
arbitrary, consider the holomorphic function

f_{t}\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=z\mapsto(1-t)p+t\zeta+r_{\zeta}(t)z:\mathbb{D}\to\Omega.

That
f_{t}(\mathbb{D})\subseteq\Omega
follows from Lemma
4.1
with
R(p)=\delta_{\Omega}(p)
and
R(\zeta)=\delta_{\Omega}(\zeta)
. For every
z\in\mathbb{D}
,
f^{\prime}_{t}(z)=r_{\zeta}(t)
. Write
z_{0}\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=t(p-\zeta)/r_{\zeta}(t)\in\mathbb{D}
.
Then, by the metric-decreasing property of
f_{t}
,

\kappa_{\Omega}(p,1)=\kappa_{\Omega}\Big(f_{t}(z_{0}),f^{\prime}_{t}(z_{0})\frac{1}{r_{\zeta}(t)}\Big)\leqslant\kappa_{\mathbb{D}}\Big(z_{0},\frac{1}{r_{\zeta}(t)}\Big)=\frac{r_{\zeta}(t)}{r_{\zeta}(t)^{2}-t^{2}|\zeta-p|^{2}}=\phi_{\zeta}(t).

Minimizing the right-hand side of the inequality above with respect to
t
tells us that the minimum of the function
\phi_{\zeta}
is
an upper bound for
\kappa_{\Omega}(p,1)
. Now we determine the minimum of
\phi_{\zeta}
. The condition satisfied by
\zeta
is simply
a restatement of the condition occurring in (
2
) of Lemma
4.2
. So by that
lemma, the minimum value of
\phi_{\zeta}
is

\frac{r^{\bullet}_{\Omega}(p)}{r^{\bullet}_{\Omega}(p)^{2}-|\zeta-p|^{2}}<\frac{1}{\delta_{\Omega}(p)}

and hence

\kappa_{\Omega}(p,1)\leqslant\min_{t\in[0,1]}\phi_{\zeta}(t)=\frac{r^{\bullet}_{\Omega}(p)}{r^{\bullet}_{\Omega}(p)^{2}-|\zeta-p|^{2}}<\frac{1}{\delta_{\Omega}(p)}.

Hence, the inequalities in (
4.7
) hold in either sub-case.

Obviously, if
\zeta=p
then the second inequality is an equality (because
r^{\bullet}_{\Omega}(p)=\delta_{\Omega}(p)
). Suppose, conversely,
that the second inequality is an equality, and suppose, to get a contradiction, that
\zeta\neq p
. Then it must be that

r^{\bullet}_{\Omega}(p)>\delta_{\Omega}(p)
. Because, if not, then
r^{\bullet}_{\Omega}(p)=\delta_{\Omega}(p)
, whence we have
\zeta=p
, as argued in
sub-case (a). This is a contradiction.
So
\delta_{\Omega}(\zeta)=r^{\bullet}_{\Omega}(p)>\delta_{\Omega}(p)
and therefore we can consider
\phi_{\zeta}
and appeal to
Lemma
4.2
to get that the minimum value of
\phi_{\zeta}
is

\frac{r^{\bullet}_{\Omega}(p)}{r^{\bullet}_{\Omega}(p)^{2}-|\zeta-p|^{2}}<\frac{1}{\delta_{\Omega}(p)}.

But that is a contradiction to the hypothesis, and this completes the proof of (
3
).

Now suppose that the condition in (
4
) holds. In this case

r^{\bullet}_{\Omega}(p)>\delta_{\Omega}(p)
. The reasoning is similar to what occurs above. Therefore we can again consider

\phi_{\zeta}
, appeal to Lemma
4.2
, and, reasoning as in the previous case, get that the minimum of

\phi_{\zeta}
is an upper bound for
\kappa_{\Omega}(p,1)
. But in this case, since the condition satisfied by
\zeta

is a restatement of the condition occurring in (
3
) of
Lemma
4.2
, the minimum is precisely the expression on the right-hand side of
(
4.8
). That the latter is strictly smaller than
1/\delta_{\Omega}(p)

follows from part (
4
) of Lemma
4.2
.
∎

5.
Proof of Theorem
1.2

Before we proceed with our proof, we point out that its basic idea is inspired by the proof
of Bernal-González
[
1
]
, but with one significant departure. This
departure
is the use of a refined estimate for

\mathsf{k}_{D_{1}}
as discussed in Section
3
.

Proof.

Let

D_{1}(r)\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\{z\in D_{1}\mid\mathrm{dist}(z,D_{1}^{\mathsf{c}})\geqslant r\}

for every
r>0
sufficiently small. Note that if
D_{2}=\mathbb{C}^{m}
, then the conclusion of the theorem is trivially true.
Therefore, we suppose that
D_{2}\varsubsetneq\mathbb{C}^{m}
. Then, by Lemma
3.4
,

\mathsf{k}_{D_{2}}(b,w)\geqslant\frac{1}{2}\log\left(\frac{\mathrm{dist}(b,D^{\mathsf{c}}_{2})}{\mathrm{dist}(w,D^{\mathsf{c}}_{2})}\right)\quad\forall\,w\in D_{2}.

Let
\phi
be as in the statement of Theorem
1.2
. Then, for every
z\in D_{1}(r)
,

\mathsf{k}_{D_{2}}(b,\phi(z))\geqslant\frac{1}{2}\log\left(\frac{\mathrm{dist}(b,D^{\mathsf{c}}_{2})}{\mathrm{dist}(\phi(z),D^{\mathsf{c}}_{2})}\right).

(5.1)

Let us now suppose that
\partial D_{1}
has
lower than

\mathcal{C}^{2}
regularity. In that case we have

\mathsf{k}_{D_{2}}(b,\phi(z))\leqslant\mathsf{k}_{D_{1}}(a,z)\leqslant C(a)+\frac{\alpha}{2}\log\left(\frac{1}{\mathrm{dist}(z,D^{\mathsf{c}}_{1})}\right).

(5.2)

The second inequality follows from Lemma
3.2
.
Therefore, by (
5.1
), the above inequality, and the fact that
z\in D_{1}(r)
,

\frac{1}{2}\log\left(\frac{\mathrm{dist}(b,D^{\mathsf{c}}_{2})}{\mathrm{dist}(\phi(z),D^{\mathsf{c}}_{2})}\right)\leqslant C(a)+\frac{\alpha}{2}\log\left(\frac{1}{r}\right).

After exponentiating and a couple of computations, we get

\mathrm{dist}(\phi(z),D^{\mathsf{c}}_{2})\geqslant C\mathrm{dist}(b,D^{\mathsf{c}}_{2})r^{\alpha},

(5.3)

where
C=e^{-2C(a)}
. Since
z\in D_{1}(r)
was arbitrary, the above inequality completes the proof
under the assumption that
\partial D_{1}
has
lower than
\mathcal{C}^{2}
regularity. In this case,
\alpha
, as obtained by our argument, is greater than
1
.

If
\partial D_{1}
is
\mathcal{C}^{2}
-smooth, then, by Lemma
3.3
, we may take
\alpha=1

in (
5.2
). Every subsequent step of the argument goes through, and we arrive at the
conclusion of Theorem
1.2
with
\alpha=1
. This completes the proof. ∎
