Bounds on Area Involving Lattice Size
Jenya Soprunova

Abstract
. The lattice size of a lattice polygon P P is a combinatorial invariant of P P that was recently introduced in relation to the problem of bounding the total degree and the bi-degree of the defining equation of an algebraic curve. In this paper, we establish sharp lower bounds on the area of plane convex bodies P ⊂ ℝ 2 P\subset\mathbb{R}^{2} that involve the lattice size of P P . In particular, we improve bounds given by Arnold, and Bárány and Pach. We also provide a classification of minimal lattice polygons P ⊂ ℝ 2 P\subset\mathbb{R}^{2} of fixed lattice size ls □ ⁡ ( P ) \operatorname{ls_{\square}}(P) .

Introduction

This paper is devoted to providing sharp lower bounds on the area of plane convex bodies
P
, which involve the lattice size of
P
.
This invariant was formally introduced by Schicho, and Kastryck and Cools in
[
8
,
15
]
, although it had appeared implicitly earlier in the work of Arnold
[
3
]
, Bárány and Pach
[
5
]
, Brown and Kaspzyck
[
6
]
, and Lagarias and Ziegler
[
13
]
. The lattice size was further studied in
[
1
,
2
,
10
]
, and
[
11
]
.

We next reproduce the definition of the lattice size from
[
8
]
applying it now to a plane convex body
P
.

Definition 1.1
.

The
lattice size

\operatorname{ls}_{X}(P)
of a convex body
P\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
with respect to a set
X\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
is the smallest real non-negative
l
such that

\varphi(P)
is contained in the
l
-dilate
lX
of
X
for some transformation
\varphi
, which a combination of multiplication by a unimodular matrix and a translation by an integer vector.

When
X=[0,1]\times\mathbb{R}
, the lattice size of
P
with respect to
X
coincides with the lattice width
\operatorname{w}(P)
of
P
, an important invariant in convex geometry and its applications. Two other interesting
invariants of
P
, denoted by
\operatorname{ls_{\Delta}}(P)
and
\operatorname{ls_{\square}}(P)
, arise when
X
is the standard 2-simplex
\Delta=\operatorname{conv}\{(0,0),(1,0),(0,1)\}
or the unit square
\square=[0,1]^{2}
.

It was shown in
[
10
,
11
]
that in dimension 2
a so-called reduced basis computes both
\operatorname{ls_{\square}}(P)
and
\operatorname{ls_{\Delta}}(P)
, and that in dimension 3 it computes
\operatorname{ls_{\square}}(P)
, but not necessarily
\operatorname{ls_{\Delta}}(P)
.
One can then use the generalized basis reduction algorithm, described and analyzed in
[
10
,
12
,
14
]

to find the lattice size of a lattice polygon, which, as explained in
[
10
,
11
]
, outperforms the “onion skins” algorithm of
[
8
,
15
]
. See Definition
2.4
and Theorem
2.7
for the definition of a reduced basis and for the precise formulation of the described results from
[
10
]
and
[
11
]
.

One of the questions that we address in this paper is the following: What is the smallest possible nonzero area
A(P)
of a lattice polygon
P
of fixed lattice size
\operatorname{ls_{\square}}(P)
or
\operatorname{ls_{\Delta}}(P)
?
In Theorem
4.1
we prove a sharp bound
A(P)\geq\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{ls_{\Delta}}(P)
and describe the lattice polygons on which this bound is attained. Since
\operatorname{ls_{\Delta}}(P)\geq\operatorname{ls_{\square}}(P)
it follows that
A(P)\geq\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{ls_{\square}}(P)
, and we show in Corollary
4.3
that this bound is sharp.

In the last section of the paper we classify inclusion-minimal lattice polygons
P
with fixed lattice size
\operatorname{ls_{\square}}(P)
, see Theorem
5.4
. This classification provides an alternative proof of Corollary
4.3
.
Note that a classification of inclusion-minimal lattice polygons
P
with fixed lattice width
\operatorname{w}(P)
was provided in
[
7
]
.

In both of the above bounds it is crucial that
P
is a lattice polygon, since a plane convex body
P
of fixed lattice size
\operatorname{ls_{\Delta}}(P)
or
\operatorname{ls_{\square}}(P)
may have an arbitrarily small area.
Hence, in the case of plane convex bodies, it makes sense to look for lower bounds on the area that involve one of the lattice sizes,
\operatorname{ls_{\Delta}}(P)
or
\operatorname{ls_{\square}}(P)
, together with the lattice width
\operatorname{w}(P)
of
P
.
In the case of the lattice size with respect to the unit square, such a bound was essentially proved by Fejes-Tóth and Makai in
[
9
]
, where they showed that for a plane convex body
P
one has

A(P)\geq\frac{3}{8}{\operatorname{w}(P)}^{2}
, and that this bound is attained at
\operatorname{conv}\{(0,0),\left(w,\frac{w}{2}\right),\left(\frac{w}{2},w\right)\}
, where
w=\operatorname{w}(P)
. By a simple rescaling argument we establish in Theorem
3.1
a sharp bound
A(P)\geq\frac{3}{8}\operatorname{w}(P)\operatorname{ls_{\square}}(P)
.

In our main result, Theorem
3.3
, we establish a version of the Fejes-Tóth–Makai result where we bound the area in terms of
\operatorname{ls_{\Delta}}(P)
and
\operatorname{w}(P)
.
For a plane convex body
P
we show that
A(P)\geq\frac{1}{4}\operatorname{w}(P)\operatorname{ls_{\Delta}}(P)
and describe convex bodies on which this bound is attained.

The idea of inscribing a lattice polygon inside a small multiple of the unit square had appeared in
[
3
,
5
,
6
,
13
]
, before the lattice size was introduced formally.
Both
[
3
]
and
[
5
]
are devoted to estimating the order of the number of lattice polygons of given area, up to the lattice equivalence, with
[
5
]
improving the result of
[

]

It is shown in one of the steps of the argument in
[

that for any lattice convex polygon

of nonzero area

there exists its lattice-equivalent copy inside a square of size
36A(P)
. In terms of the lattice size this means that
\operatorname{ls_{\square}}(P)\leq 36A(P)
. Hence our result in Corollary

improves Arnold’s bound from
[

]
to a sharp one replacing a constant of 36 with 2.

A similar result is proved in
[

with nonzero area

there exists a lattice-equivalent copy of
P
inside a rectangle
[0,w]\times[0,h]
with

.
In Theorem

we improve the bound of Bárány and Pach to a sharp bound

2.
Definitions

Recall that a
plane convex body

P\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
is a compact convex subset of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
with non-empty interior.
Given
(a,b)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
,
the width of
P
in the direction
(a,b)
is

\operatorname{w}_{(a,b)}(P)=\max\limits_{(x,y)\in P}(ax+by)-\min\limits_{(x,y)\in P}(ax+by).

Consider the Minkowski sum of
P
with
-P
, its reflection in the origin, and let
K:=(P+(-P))^{\ast}
be the polar dual of the sum.
Then
K
is origin-symmetric and convex and it defines a norm on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
by

\|u\|_{K}=\inf\{\lambda>0\mid u/\lambda\in K\}.

For details see, for example,
[
4
]
. We then have

\|u\|_{K}=\inf\{\lambda>0\mid u\cdot x\leq\lambda{\rm\ for\ all\ }x\in K^{\ast}\}=\max\limits_{x\in K^{\ast}}u\cdot x=\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{w}_{u}(K^{\ast})=\operatorname{w}_{u}(P).

This, in particular, implies that
u\mapsto\operatorname{w}_{u}(P)
is a convex function on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
.

Recall that a vector
u=(a,b)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
is called
primitive
if
\gcd(a,b)=1
. The
lattice width
of
P
, denoted by
\operatorname{w}(P)
, is the minimum of
\operatorname{w}_{u}(P)
over all primitive directions
u
.

A
lattice polygon
is a convex polygon all of whose vertices have integer coordinates.
An integer square matrix
A
is called
unimodular
if
\det A=\pm 1
.
Two convex bodies in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
are called
lattice-equivalent
if one of them is the image of the other under a map which is a composition
of multiplication by a unimodular matrix and a translation by an integer vector.

Let
\Delta=\operatorname{conv}\{(0,0),(1,0),(0,1)\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
be the standard 2-simplex.

Definition 2.1
.

The
lattice size

\operatorname{ls_{\Delta}}(P)
of a convex body
P\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
with respect to the standard simplex is the smallest
l\geq 0
such that
the
l
-dilate
l\Delta
contains a lattice-equivalent copy of
P
.

Let
\square=[0,1]^{2}\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
be the unit square.

Definition 2.2
.

The
lattice size

\operatorname{ls_{\square}}(P)
of a convex body
P\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
with respect to the unit square is the smallest
l\geq 0
such that
the
l
-dilate
l\square
contains a lattice-equivalent copy of
P
.

A basis

Definition 2.5
.

Let
P\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
be a plane convex body. We define
\operatorname{nls_{\Delta}}(P)
to be the smallest
l\geq 0
such that
\varphi(P)\subset l\Delta
where

is the composition of a multiplication by a matrix of the form

\begin{bmatrix}\pm 1&0\\

and a translation by an integer vector. Equivalently, if we let

\displaystyle l_{1}(P)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\max\limits_{(x,y)\in P}(x+y)-\min\limits_{(x,y)\in P}x-\min\limits_{(x,y)\in P}y,

\displaystyle l_{2}(P)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\max\limits_{(x,y)\in P}x+\max\limits_{(x,y)\in P}y-\min\limits_{(x,y)\in P}(x+y),

\displaystyle l_{3}(P)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\max\limits_{(x,y)\in P}y-\min\limits_{(x,y)\in P}x+\max\limits_{(x,y)\in P}(x-y),

\displaystyle l_{4}(P)

Let

Note that for

\operatorname{w}_{u}(AP)=\max\limits_{x\in P}u\cdot(Ax)-\min_{x\in P}u\cdot(Ax)=\max\limits_{x\in P}(A^{T}u)\cdot x-\min_{x\in P}(A^{T}u)\cdot x=\operatorname{w}_{A^{T}u}(P).

Therefore, if the rows of

\operatorname{w}_{(0,1)}(AP)=\operatorname{w}_{u_{2}}(P)
.
In the next observation we show that if a plane convex body
P
is inscribed in

and touches all of its sides, then
\operatorname{ls_{\square}}(P)=h
.

Proposition 2.9
.

Suppose that
\operatorname{w}_{(1,0)}(P)=\operatorname{w}_{(0,1)}(P)=h
. Then
\operatorname{ls_{\square}}(P)=h
and

\operatorname{w}(P)=\min\{h,\operatorname{w}_{(1,1)}(P),\operatorname{w}_{(1,-1)}(P)\}.

Proof.

Let
(a,b)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
be a primitive direction. Without loss of generality we can assume that
|a|\geq|b|
. We then have

|a|h=\operatorname{w}_{(a,0)}(P)\leq\operatorname{w}_{(a,b)}(P)+\operatorname{w}_{(0,b)}(P)=\operatorname{w}_{(a,b)}(P)+|b|h,

and so we get
\operatorname{w}_{(a,b)}(P)\geq(|a|-|b|)h
. Hence for
|a|>|b|
we conclude that
\operatorname{w}_{(a,b)}(P)\geq h
. Hence directions
(\pm 1,\pm 1)
are the only primitive directions with respect to which the width of
P

could be less than
h
. Since no two out of these four directions can be used as rows to form a unimodular matrix, the conclusion follows.

Definition 2.10

Let

3.
Lower bounds on the area of a plane convex body in terms of its width and lattice size.

Consider a convex body
P\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
. Let
w=\operatorname{w}(P)
be its width and
A(P)
be its area. It was shown in
[
9
]
that
A(P)\geq\frac{3}{8}w^{2}
and that this bound is attained only on the convex bodies that are lattice-equivalent to
\operatorname{conv}\{(0,0),\left(w,\frac{w}{2}\right),\left(\frac{w}{2},w\right)\}
. We formulate a result which is a straight-forward corollary of this bound.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
P\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
be a convex body with
h=\operatorname{ls_{\square}}(P)
and
w=\operatorname{w}(P)
. Then for the area
A(P)
of
P
we have
A(P)\geq\frac{3}{8}wh
. This bound is sharp and attained only on the convex bodies
P
that are lattice-equivalent to
\operatorname{conv}\{(0,0),\left(w,\frac{w}{2}\right),\left(\frac{w}{2},w\right)\}
.

Remark 3.2
.

It was shown in Lemma 3 of
[
5
]
that for any lattice convex polygon
P\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
there exist numbers
w,h\geq 0
with
wh\leq 4A(P)
, such that
[0,w]\times[0,h]

contains a lattice-equivalent copy of
P
. Note that Theorem
3.1
strengthens this result replacing the constant of 4 with
\frac{8}{3}
, and also extends the result to plane convex bodies.

Proof of Theorem
3.1
.

Let the standard basis be reduced with respect to
P
. Then by Theorem
2.7

\operatorname{w}_{(1,0)}(P)=w
and
\operatorname{w}_{(0,1)}(P)=h
. Let
P^{\prime}
be the image of
P
under the map
(x,y)\mapsto(x,\frac{w}{h}y)
.
We next check that the standard basis is also reduced with respect to
P^{\prime}
. Denote by
(x_{1},y_{1})
and
(x_{2},y_{2})
points in
P
that, correspondingly, minimize and maximize
x+y
over
P
. Then we have

\operatorname{w}_{(1,1)}(P)=x_{2}+y_{2}-(x_{1}+y_{1})\geq h
. Since
y_{2}-y_{1}\leq h
, this implies that
x_{2}-x_{1}\geq 0
. Hence

\operatorname{w}_{(1,1)}(P^{\prime})\geq(x_{2}-x_{1})+\frac{w}{h}(y_{2}-y_{1})\geq\frac{w}{h}(x_{2}-x_{1})+\frac{w}{h}(y_{2}-y_{1})\geq w.

Similarly, first reflecting
P
in the line
y=w/2
, we conclude that
\operatorname{w}_{(1,-1)}(P^{\prime})\geq w
, so we have checked that the standard basis remains reduced as we pass from
P
to
P^{\prime}
.
We conclude that
\operatorname{w}(P^{\prime})=w
, and hence, as shown in
[
9
]
, we have
A(P^{\prime})\geq\frac{3}{8}w^{2}
and this bound is attained at
P^{\prime}
which are lattice-equivalent to
\operatorname{conv}\{(0,0),\left(w,\frac{w}{2}\right),\left(\frac{w}{2},w\right)\}
.
This implies that
A(P)=\frac{h}{w}A(P^{\prime})\geq\frac{3}{8}wh
and this bound can be attained only if
P
is lattice-equivalent to
\operatorname{conv}\{(0,0),\left(w,\frac{h}{2}\right),\left(\frac{w}{2},h\right)\}
. Since
the standard basis is reduced with respect to
P
, if
w\geq h/2
, we have
\operatorname{w}_{(1,-1)}(P)=\frac{w+h}{2}\geq h
, which implies
w=h
. If
w<h/2
then
\operatorname{w}_{(1,-1)}=h-w/2\geq h
, which implies
w=0
. We conclude that the bound is attained exactly at
P
which are lattice-equivalent to
\operatorname{conv}\left\{(0,0),\left(w,\frac{w}{2}\right),\left(\frac{w}{2},w\right)\right\}
.
∎

Let
P
be a convex body with

,

4.
Proving

If

Theorem 4.1
.

Let

\operatorname{conv}\{(0,0),(l,0),(0,1)\}

(b)

[0,1]^{2}
for

;

(c)

T_{0}=\operatorname{conv}\{(0,0),(1,2),(2,1)\}

It follows from this theorem that for any lattice polygon

\operatorname{conv}\{(0,0),(h,0),(0,1)\}.

\operatorname{w}_{(1,0)}(P)<h
or
\operatorname{w}_{(0,1)}(P)<h
and hence we would be able to use one of
\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\
1&\pm 1\end{bmatrix}
,
\begin{bmatrix}0&1\\
1&\pm 1\end{bmatrix}
and a lattice translation to fit
P
into a smaller square, so
Q
is minimal.

If neither of the two inequalities holds, we have
\operatorname{w}_{(1,1)}(Q)\geq h
and
\operatorname{w}_{(1,-1)}(Q)\geq h
. If both of these inequalities are strict, then after we drop a vertex to get from
Q
to
P
we would have
\operatorname{w}_{(1,1)}(P)\geq h
and

\operatorname{w}_{(1,-1)}(P)\geq h
, so the standard basis would still be reduced. We would also have either
\operatorname{w}_{(1,0)}(P)=h
or
\operatorname{w}_{(0,1)}(P)=h
, so we can conclude that
\operatorname{ls_{\square}}(P)=h
and hence
Q
is not minimal.

A similar argument works if we have
\operatorname{w}_{(1,1)}(Q)=h
and
\operatorname{w}_{(1,-1)}(Q)\geq h
, or
\operatorname{w}_{(1,1)}(Q)\geq h
and
\operatorname{w}_{(1,-1)}(Q)=h
. We would need to drop a vertex that does not change the width in the direction
(1,1)
in the first case and in the direction of
(1,-1)
in the second.

If we have
\operatorname{w}_{(1,1)}(Q)=h
and
\operatorname{w}_{(1,-1)}(Q)=h
then
\max\{a,c\}+\max\{b,d\}=h
and
\min\{a,b\}+\min\{c,d\}=h
. Since we have
\min\{a,b\}\leq\max\{b,d\}
and
\min\{c,d\}\leq\max\{a,c\}
we can conclude

\min\{a,b\}=\max\{b,d\}
and
\min\{c,d\}=\max\{a,c\}
. Hence we get
a=b=c=d=h/2
, and then
Q
contains a horizontal segment of lattice length
h
, so
Q
is not minimal.

Note that
Q
cannot satisfy both inequalities since then we would get

h<\min\{a,b\}+\min\{c,d\}\leq\max\{a,c\}+\max\{b,d\}<h.

If
Q
satisfies the second inequality then the transformation
(x,y)\mapsto(h-x,y)
maps
Q
to

\operatorname{conv}\{(h-a,0),(0,h-c),(h,b),(d,h)\}\subset[0,h]^{2},

and its parameters
a^{\prime}=h-a
,
b^{\prime}=h-c
,
c^{\prime}=h-b,d^{\prime}=h-d
satisfy

\displaystyle\min\{a^{\prime},b^{\prime}\}+\min\{c^{\prime},d^{\prime}\}

\displaystyle=\min\{h-a,h-c\}+\min\{h-b,h-d\}

\displaystyle=2h-\max\{a,c\}-\max\{b,d\}>h.

∎

This classification leads to an alternative argument for Corollary
