Quantum Optimal Control: Practical Aspects and Diverse Methods
T S Mahesh, Priya Batra, M. Harshanth Ram

Abstract
Quantum controls realize the unitary or nonunitary operations employed in quantum computers, quantum simulators, quantum communications, and other quantum information devices. They implement the desired quantum dynamics with the help of electric, magnetic, or electromagnetic control fields. Quantum optimal control (QOC) deals with designing an optimal control field modulation that most precisely implements a desired quantum operation with minimum energy consumption and maximum robustness against hardware imperfections as well as external noise. Over the last two decades, numerous QOC methods have been proposed. They include asymptotic methods, direct search, gradient methods, variational methods, machine learning methods, etc. In this review, we shall introduce the basic ideas of QOC, discuss practical challenges, and then take an overview of the diverse QOC methods.



I
Introduction

The quest for control over quantum dynamics has a long history of several decades (eg.
Peirce et al. 1988
;
Kosloff et al. 1989
;
Zare 1998
;
Rabitz et al. 2000
).
Although quantum control methods have been employed in several fields from chemical kinetics to imaging, here we focus on the methods employed for quantum information related areas.
Thanks to the ongoing revolution of quantum technologies, quantum control methods have received a major impetus in recent years
Dowling and Milburn 2003
.

In this review, we mostly use examples from nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR), although the basic principles are applicable in other architectures as well.
The early methods of quantum control for NMR quantum information tasks involved bandwidth selective fields, called shaped pulses
Cavanagh et al. 1996
. Several quantum information tasks were realized using such band-width selective pulses
Dorai et al. 2000
. Although the shaped pulses are convenient to realize, they suffered from long pulse-durations as well as undesired evolutions.
The need for precise control over quantum dynamics led to the development of advanced control methods based on optimal control theory.
Classical optimal control theory uses Pontryagin’s maximum principle to design the best controls for a given task within certain constraints

Kopp 1962
;
Pontryagin 1987
;
Kirk 2004
;
Boscain et al. 2021a
;
Boscain et al. 2021b
.
Extending the control problem to the framework of quantum theory yields quantum optimal control (QOC) theory

Werschnik and Gross 2007
;
Cong 2014
;
Glaser et al. 2015
;
d’Alessandro 2021
.

This review is an attempt to provide a broad overview of various methods, without getting into finer details. While the review is surely non exhaustive, we intend to capture several strategies and convey their gist, which may benefit a reader novice in the field. The review is arranged as follows. The next section outlines the quantum control problem formulated for quantum processors. Section 3 discusses various practical aspects which must be taken into consideration while designing the quantum control. Section 4 contains the review of a diverse collection of QOC methods.
Finally, section 5 provides a summary and outlook.

II
Quantum Control Problem

II.1
The system, control fields, and environment

We consider an overall Hamiltonian of the form

\displaystyle H(t)=H^{S}+H^{\Omega}(t)+H^{E}(t).

(1)

Here,
H^{S}
is the static
system Hamiltonian
. In quantum information processing (QIP),
H^{S}
includes both self Hamiltonians of various qubits as well as their mutual interactions.
The time-dependent
control Hamiltonian

H^{\Omega}(t)
is used to realize the desired dynamics.
The remaining undesirable interactions are collected in the effective
environmental Hamiltonian

H^{E}(t)
, which may not be completely known.
We ignore this term unless we want to see its effects or design control sequences that minimize them.
The instantaneous state
\rho(t)
of the processor satisfies the Liouville–von Neumann equation

\displaystyle\dot{\rho}(t)=-i\left[{H(t)},{\rho(t)}\right],

(2)

whose solution is of the form

\displaystyle\rho(t)

\displaystyle=U(0,t)\rho(0)U^{\dagger}(0,t),~~\mbox{with unitary operator}

\displaystyle U(0,t)

\displaystyle=D\exp\left[-i\int_{t^{\prime}=0}^{t}dt^{\prime}H(t^{\prime})\right].

(3)

Here
D
is the Dyson’s time-ordering operator. Throughout the review, we have set
\hbar=1
.

To bypass the complicated integral in Eq.
3
and facilitate the numerical evaluation of the propagator, we shall discretize the entire control field
\Omega(t)
of duration
T
into
N
piecewise constant segments
\{\Omega_{1},\Omega_{2},\cdots,\Omega_{n},\cdots,\Omega_{N}\}
, collectively termed as the control sequence
\Omega
. Discretization by itself need not be an approximation, since the modern digital
signal generators can produce the discretized control sequence directly (more on this in Sec.
III.1
).
In general, each control segment
\Omega_{n}
is a multi-component array with
M
distinct channels each with one or more distinct controls (see Fig.
1
).
For example, a typical NMR spectrometer is equipped with two to five radio-frequency (RF) channels that allow simultaneous control of different nuclear spin isotopes
Levitt 2013
. In the case of NV centers, one channel controls the electronic spin and the remaining channels control nuclear spin isotopes
15
N and
13
C
Doherty et al. 2013
. Typically, each channel has several control parameters such as amplitude, frequency, and initial phase. Collectively, we shall represent the controls of segment
n\in[1,N]
in channel
m\in[1,M]
as a control vector
\vec{\omega}_{mn}
. Amplitude is the most common control parameter since it is easier to execute in theory as well as in the experiment. In the following, for the case of a single control parameter, we shall drop the vector symbol and denote it by
\omega_{mn}
(see Fig.
1
). In general, the control Hamiltonian for the
n
th segment is of the form

\displaystyle H^{\Omega_{n}}=\sum_{m=1}^{M}{\cal H}_{m}(\vec{\omega}_{mn}),

(4)

with the
m
th channel control Hamiltonian
{\cal H}_{m}(\vec{\omega}_{mn})
.

Figure 1:
An example structure of the control sequence.

The discretized Hamiltonian for the
n
th segment of duration
\tau_{n}
and the corresponding segment propagator are

\displaystyle H_{n}=H^{S}+H^{\Omega_{n}},~~U_{n}

\displaystyle=e^{-iH_{n}\tau_{n}}.

(5)

Thus the overall propagator is simply the ordered product

\displaystyle U(0,T)=U_{1:N}

\displaystyle=U_{N}U_{N-1}\cdots U_{n}\cdots U_{2}U_{1}.

(6)

If segments are all of the equal duration,
\tau_{n}=\tau=T/N
.

The control need not be entirely unitary. For example, we can insert projective measurements or controlled dephasing (twirl operations
Anwar et al. 2005
;
Bhole et al. 2016
) in between unitary segments, and realize a net nonunitary propagator. If
\rho_{n-1}
represents the state after
n-1
segments,

\displaystyle\rho_{n}=\hat{{\cal E}}_{n}\left[U_{n}\rho_{n-1}U_{n}^{\dagger}\right],

(7)

where
\hat{{\cal E}}_{n}
is the superoperator implementing the
n
th non-unitary operation. We now discuss two types of quantum control problems.

II.3
Quantum Gate Synthesis Problem

Given a target unitary operator
U_{F}
corresponding to a quantum gate, the goal here is to find a control sequence
\Omega
that maximizes the gate fidelity
Nielsen and Chuang 2002

\displaystyle F_{G}(U_{F},U_{1:N})=\left|\frac{\langle{U_{F}}|{U_{1:N}}\rangle}{\langle{U_{F}}|{U_{F}}\rangle}\right|^{2}.

(8)

Here and throughout the rest of the article we have used

\displaystyle\langle{A}|{B}\rangle=\mathrm{Tr}\left(A^{\dagger}B\right)

(9)

to denote the overlap between the operators
A
and
B
.
The control sequence so obtained will be applicable independent of the initial state.

II.4
State to State Transfer or State Preparation Problem

Here the control sequence
\Omega
needs to transfer a specific initial state
\rho_{I}
to a specific final state
\rho_{F}
. If the two states are of the same purity, we seek a unitary sequence
U_{1:N}
that prepares the state

\displaystyle\rho_{N}

\displaystyle=U_{1:N}\rho_{I}U_{1:N}^{\dagger}

(10)

as close to
\rho_{F}
as possible.
Examples include adiabatic inversion/excitation

Cavanagh et al. 1996
;
Tannús and Garwood 1997
, preparing singlet-triplet order
Manu and Kumar 2012
;
Khurana and Mahesh 2017a
, etc.

Often, the initial and the final desired states need not have the same purity. For instance, preparing pseudopure state in NMR from thermal state
Cory et al. 1997
;
Suter and Mahesh 2008
;
Bhole et al. 2016
. In such a case we seek a nonunitary sequence as described in Eq.
7
.

In state preparation, the control sequence
\Omega
is obtained by maximizing one of the following measures.

(i)

Uhlmann state-fidelity (eg.
Wu and Rabitz 2012
):

\displaystyle F_{S}(\rho_{F},\rho_{N})=\left(\mathrm{Tr}\sqrt{\sqrt{\rho_{F}}\rho_{N}\sqrt{\rho_{F}}}\right)^{2}.

(11)

(ii)

Trace fidelity (eg.
Khaneja et al. 2005
):

\displaystyle F_{T}(\rho_{F},\rho_{N})=\langle{\rho_{F}}|{\rho_{N}}\rangle.

(12)

(iii)

For traceless parts of the density matrices, it is particularly convenient to use the following measures
Fortunato et al. 2002
:

–

Correlation:

\displaystyle F_{C}(\rho_{F},\rho_{N})

\displaystyle=\frac{\langle{\rho_{F}}|{\rho_{N}}\rangle}{\sqrt{\mathrm{Tr}(\rho_{F}^{2})}\cdot\sqrt{\mathrm{Tr}(\rho_{N}^{2})}}.

–

Attenuated correlation:

\displaystyle F_{A}(\rho_{I},\rho_{F},\rho_{N})

\displaystyle=\frac{\langle{\rho_{F}}|{\rho_{N}}\rangle}{\sqrt{\mathrm{Tr}(\rho_{I}^{2})}\cdot\sqrt{\mathrm{Tr}(\rho_{N}^{2})}},

(14)

which is also sensitive to the change in the purity of
\rho_{F}
w.r.t. the initial state
\rho_{I}
, in addition to the overlap between
\rho_{N}
and
\rho_{F}
.

While it is relatively easier to generate a state-specific control sequence compared to the the general quantum gate discussed in Sec.
II.3
, the state to state control sequence generated for a particular pair of initial and final states is not applicable for other states.

III
Practical Aspects

III.1
Control field limitations

III.1.1
Distribution of a control parameter

Suppose a control segment with a nominal amplitude value
\omega_{mn}
actually has a distribution over the range

[\omega_{mn}^{\mathrm{min}},\omega_{mn}^{\mathrm{max}}]
.
We may discretize the distribution into
L
bins, and assign probability
p_{l}
for the
l
th bin (see Fig.
2
).

Figure 2:
(a) Illustrating ideal (top) and typical (bottom) spatial distribution of RF amplitude about a nominal value of
\omega_{mn}
. Middle row: the Bloch-sphere trajectories on applying Hadamard gate
H=R_{x}(\pi)R_{y}(\pi/2)
on
|{0}\rangle
with 10% weaker (left), nominal (center), and 10% stronger (right) field-components. Note that, only for the nominal component, the qubit reaches the desired state
(|{0}\rangle+|{1}\rangle)/\sqrt{2}
.
(b) The fidelity profiles of simple and robust control sequences.

It is important to have a quantitative understanding of the distribution. For example, Pravia et al.
Pravia et al. 2003
described the experimental estimation of RF distribution via Torrey oscillation.
A microscopic system, like an NV center, may sample one particular bin of the distribution, while a macroscopic object, such as an NMR spin ensemble, experiences the entire distribution
Cavanagh et al. 1996
.
To realize a control sequence
{\Omega}
that is robust against the distribution, we optimize the weighted average by defining the performance function as
Fortunato et al. 2002
;
Khaneja et al. 2005
,

\displaystyle\Phi(\Omega)=\bar{F}=\sum_{l=1}^{L}p_{l}F_{l},

(15)

where
F_{l}
is the fidelity of the
l
th bin (see Fig.
2
(b)).

III.1.2
Power restriction

The hardware limitation imposes a maximum nominal value of
\omega_{m}^{\mathrm{MAX}}
for each control parameter
\omega_{mn}
. To restrict the control sequence
{\Omega}
within the allowed range, we maximize the improved performance function

\displaystyle\Phi(\Omega)=\bar{F}-{\cal P}(\{\omega_{m}^{\mathrm{MAX}}\}),

(16)

where the penalty function
{\cal P}
is normally zero, but rapidly grows as any control parameter reaches close to or exceeds the limits
Fortunato et al. 2002
;
Fletcher 1983
.

III.1.3
Frequency bandwidth and nonlinear response

Electronic circuits producing a control field are designed for a certain maximum frequency bandwidth, beyond which the signal profiles are distorted. This limits how fast the control parameters can be changed from one segment to another. To this end, we may seek a control sequence in terms of low-bandwidth and smoothly varying basis functions, such as Slepian functions
Lucarelli 2018
;
Feng et al. 2018
. A related issue is the finite raise-time & fall-time, as well as other nonlinearities in a practical pulse. Boulant et al.
Boulant et al. 2003
used an interesting approach that involved reading the actual nonlinear pulse profile as seen by a spy detector coil. This enabled them to model the nonlinear transfer function, find its inverse, and iteratively obtain a compensated pulse.

III.2
Noise and decoherence

As mentioned in Eq.
1
, a quantum system being controlled is often prone to certain unavoidable environmental fields or interactions, leading to decoherence. The challenge here is to design a protected quantum control, that is robust against these external influences.
One often addresses this problem with a time-optimal control sequence (eg.
Möttönen et al. 2006
;
Zhang et al. 2011
;
Tibbetts et al. 2012
), that performs the desired task before noise becomes significant.
The other powerful approach is by interlacing dynamical decoupling pulses with the control sequence to realize protected quantum gates
Xu et al. 2012
;
Zhang et al. 2014
;
Viola et al. 1999
;
Ram et al. 2021
. If additional qubits are available, one can also encode information onto logical qubits in a decoherence free subspace (DFS)
Lidar et al. 1998
.
An extensive review for controlling the open quantum system has been covered by Koch
Koch 2016
.

III.3
Computational resource for optimization

The computational resource needed for generating a control sequence is an important consideration in choosing the optimization method for a given control problem. For systems with large Hilbert-space dimensions, the bottleneck is in the matrix exponentiation in Eq.
5
. Some ideas are discussed below.

III.3.1
Limiting off-diagonal operators

Certain quantum control tasks, especially nonlocal quantum gates, require long delays (evolution under system Hamiltonian
H^{S}
) in between control pulses. The delay propagator

U_{d}(\tau)

can be efficiently evaluated in the eigenbasis
\{|{s}\rangle\}
of the system Hamiltonian
H_{S}
, i.e.,

\displaystyle U_{d}(\tau)=e^{-iH^{S}\tau}=\sum_{s}e^{-is\tau}|{s}\rangle\langle{s}|.

(17)

Therefore, a due emphasis on delays in such control sequences greatly improves the efficiency of optimization
Mahesh and Suter 2006
.

III.3.2
Diagonalizing Control Hamiltonians

In certain scenarios, it is possible to find a clever choice of basis operators that helps avoiding iterative matrix exponentiation
Bhole and Mahesh 2017
;
Bhole and Jones 2018
.
The trick is to use the Trotter decomposition and rewrite Eq.
5
as

\displaystyle U_{n}=e^{-i(H^{S}+H^{\Omega_{n}})\tau}

\displaystyle\approx e^{-iH^{S}\tau/2}e^{-iH^{\Omega_{n}}\tau}e^{-iH^{S}\tau/2}

\displaystyle~~=U_{d}(\tau/2)~e^{-iH^{\Omega_{n}}\tau}~U_{d}(\tau/2).

(18)

The nontrivial central exponential is then diagonalized using a pair of fixed unitary operators
W_{1}
and
W_{2}
so that

\displaystyle e^{-iH^{\Omega_{n}}\tau}

\displaystyle=W_{1}\left(\sum_{d}e^{-id\tau_{n}}|{d}\rangle\langle{d}|\right)W_{2},

(19)

using which
U_{n}
can be efficiently computed
Bhole and Jones 2018
.

III.3.3
Use of fixed controls or bang-bang controls

While designing quantum controls for large quantum systems, often it might be sufficient to use only the maximum power pulses, also known as the bang-bang controls, separated by delays. In this case, the matrix exponentiation in Eq.
5
reduces to

\displaystyle U_{n}=\begin{cases}&U_{d}(\tau_{n})~~\mbox{if delay segment, else}\\
&\exp\left[-i\tau\left(H_{S}+\sum_{m=1}^{M}\omega_{m}^{\mathrm{max}}H_{m}\right)\right].\end{cases}

Both cases are effectively one-time calculations and accordingly, the bang-bang controls can be generated efficiently even for larger quantum systems
Bhole et al. 2016
;
Khurana and Mahesh 2017a
.

IV.1
Asymptotic Evolution Methods

The following methods assume slow variation of some parameter, and approach exact solutions in the asymptotic limit.

IV.1.1
Adiabatic pulse method

Adiabatic pulses are considered a method of choice for state transfers in the presence of highly inhomogeneous fields
Cavanagh et al. 1996
;
Tannús and Garwood 1997
;
Garwood and DelaBarre 2001
.
Consider a state transfer problem from the ground state
|{\psi_{I}}\rangle
of an initial Hamiltonian
{\cal H}_{I}
to the ground state
|{\psi_{F}}\rangle
of the final Hamiltonian
{\cal H}_{F}
. The adiabatic method involves implementing a control Hamiltonian of the form

\displaystyle H(t)=(1-\omega(t)){\cal H}_{I}+\omega(t){\cal H}_{F}.

(20)

The scalar parameter
\omega(t)
is slowly (adiabatically) varied from
\omega(0)=0
to
\omega(T)=1
, such that the system remains in an eigenstate of the instantaneous Hamiltonian
H(t)
throughout
Messiah 2014
.
As long as there is no level-crossing, this method leads to a robust state transfer from
|{\psi_{I}}\rangle
to
|{\psi_{F}}\rangle
, since the field imperfections can only affect the intermediate trajectory, but not the final state.

Figure 4:
Different stages of adiabatic inversion.

Broadband inversion forms a simple, yet an intuitive example of the adiabatic method (see Fig.
4
).
Imagine inverting a set of spin qubits of Larmor frequencies
\delta_{i}
from
|{00\cdots 0}\rangle
to
|{11\cdots 1}\rangle
using an RF field of amplitude
\omega
, frequency
\nu_{\mathrm{RF}}
, and phase
\hat{x}
.
The effective Hamiltonian in the rotating frame of the RF is of the form

\displaystyle H_{\mathrm{eff}}(t)=\sum_{i}\Delta_{i}(t)\sigma_{z}/2+\omega\sigma_{x}/2,~~\mbox{with}~~\Delta_{i}(t)=2\pi\left[\delta_{i}-\nu_{\mathrm{RF}}(t)\right].

(21)

This corresponds to a precession of the spin qubits about an effective field
\vec{B}_{i,\mathrm{eff}}\propto\Delta_{i}(t)\cos\theta_{i}(t)\hat{z}+\omega\sin\theta_{i}(t)\hat{x}
, where
\theta_{i}(t)=\tan^{-1}\left(\omega/\Delta_{i}(t)\right)
. Initially, one starts with a highly off-resonant pulse, i.e.,
|\nu_{\mathrm{RF}}(0)|\gg\delta_{i}
and
|\Delta_{i}(0)|\gg\omega
, so that
\theta_{i}(0)\sim 0
. Thus, the initial state

|{00\cdots 0}\rangle
is an eigenstate of
H_{\mathrm{eff}}(0)
, and the qubits are locked to the effective field.
Gradually the RF frequency is swept to on-resonance (
\theta=\pm\pi/2
) and slowly taken off-resonant far on the other side. This way, all the qubits continue to get locked to the effective field and finally reach the state
\theta=\pi
, which corresponds to the state
|{11\cdots 1}\rangle
. In the adiabatic limit, the inversion happens to all the spin qubits with different Larmor frequencies, even in the presence of inhomogeneous amplitude
\omega
.

Adiabatic methods are routinely being applied in several fields including spectroscopy and imaging
Tannús and Garwood 1997
;
Garwood and DelaBarre 2001
.
Adiabatic quantum algorithms, wherein the solution of a task is modeled as the ground state of a Hamiltonian, are also popular
Albash and Lidar 2018
.

IV.1.2
Geometric phase method

If the state of a quantum system undergoes a cyclic evolution and returns to the original state
Berry 1988
;
Anandan 1992
, then apart from a dynamical phase, it acquires a geometric phase, that is purely dependent on the geometric aspects of the path followed. The dynamical phase can be effectively removed using a spin-echo type of time-reversal sequence leaving behind a pure geometric phase
Jones et al. 2000
.
In the adiabatic limit, the geometric phase is called the Pancharatnam-Berry phase
Pancharatnam 1956
;
Berry 1987
.
The geometric phase has long been observed and used in NMR
Suter et al. 1987
. Ekert and coworkers
Ekert et al. 2000
had proposed geometric phase quantum gates and recognized them to be robust against certain types of control errors.
Universal quantum computing by non-abelian geometric phase gates, or holonomic gates, was proposed by Zanardi and Rasetti
Zanardi and Rasetti 1999
.
Subsequently, there have been several experimental demonstrations of geometric quantum gates, including in NMR
Jones et al. 2000
, ion qubits

Leibfried et al. 2003
, ultracold neutrons
Filipp et al. 2009
, superconducting qubits
Berger et al. 2013
, as well as NV centers
Nagata et al. 2018
.

IV.1.3
Lyapunov method

Lyapunov method was originally used for stabilizing a dynamical system into an asymptotic stable state
Wikipedia contributors 2021
;
Isidori 1995
and is particularly useful for quantum state transfer
Grivopoulos and Bamieh 2003
.
To explain the method, we shall consider a transfer from an arbitrary initial state
|{\psi_{I}}\rangle
to an eigenstate
|{\psi_{F}}\rangle
of the system Hamiltonian
H^{S}

Hou et al. 2012
;
Wang et al. 2014
.
We model the distance between the instantaneous state and the target state in terms of a positive function, called Lyapunov function
V
, which is a continuously differentiable and decreasing function, i.e.,
\dot{V}\leq 0
. There can be multiple ways to set up the Lyapunov function, one of which is

\displaystyle V(t)=\langle{P}|{\rho(t)}\rangle,~~\mbox{with}~~P=I-|{\psi_{F}}\rangle\langle{\psi_{F}}|,

(22)

and
\rho(t)=|{\psi(t)}\rangle\langle{\psi(t)}|
being the instantaneous state. As the system reaches the target state
V(t)\rightarrow 0
. Now invoking the Lyapunov constraint,

\displaystyle\dot{V}(t)

\displaystyle=\langle{P}|{\dot{\rho}(t)}\rangle=-i\sum_{m}\omega_{m}(t)\langle{P}|{\left[H^{S}+{\cal H}_{m},\rho(t)\right]}\rangle\leq 0~~~\mbox{(using Eqn. \ref{eq:vn})},

\displaystyle=\sum_{m}\omega_{m}(t)v_{m}(t)\leq 0~~~\mbox{with}~v_{m}(t)=-i\langle{\rho(t)}|{[P,{\cal H}_{m}]}\rangle,

(23)

where we have used
[P,H^{S}]=0
.
There are different ways to satisfy the above constraint. Optimal Lyapunov solutions are discussed in Ref.
Hou et al. 2012
. For instance, if the control amplitudes are constrained by
|\omega_{m}(t)|\leq\omega_{m}^{\mathrm{max}}
, then one obtains the bang-bang control form
Hou et al. 2012

\displaystyle\omega_{m}(t)=\begin{cases}-\omega_{m}^{\mathrm{max}}~&~v_{m}(t)>0,\\
\omega_{m}^{\mathrm{max}}~&~v_{m}(t)<0,\\
0~&~v_{m}(t)=0.\end{cases}

(24)

Thus the Lyapunov method provides a systematic way to generate quantum control for state transfer from an arbitrary initial state to an eigenstate of the system Hamiltonian
H^{S}
.
It is also possible to generalize the Lyapunov method for gate synthesis (eg.
Ghaeminezhad and Cong 2018
).
Wang et al
Wang et al. 2022
described a
Lyapunov based bang-bang quantum control scheme for cooling an optomechanical oscillator.
Recently, Purkayastha
Purkayastha 2022
extended the Lyapunov method for linear open quantum systems.

IV.2
Direct Search Methods

Direct search methods rely on cleverly sampling the search space starting from a random initial guess. The following methods employ a stochastic search algorithm to reach an optimal solution.

IV.2.1
Strongly Modulating Pulses (SMP)

Fortunato et al
Fortunato et al. 2002
described generating SMPs using a stochastic search with a split and search method as illustrated in Fig.
5
.
Given a target operator
U_{F}
, it starts with just one random control segment
\Omega_{1}^{(0)}
with a sufficiently long duration
\tau_{1}^{(0)}
. The Hamiltonian for the one-segment sequence is simply
H^{(0)}_{1}=H^{S}+H^{\Omega_{1}^{(0)}}
, and the corresponding propagator is
U_{1}^{(0)}=\exp(-iH^{(0)}_{1}\tau_{1}^{(0)})
.
By maximizing the performance function
\Phi
(see Eq.
16
) using a vector stochastic search, such as Nelder-Mead simplex
Nelder and Mead 1965
, we obtain
\{\tau^{(1)}_{1},\Omega_{1}^{(1)}\}
. Now we split it into two equal segments
\{\tau^{(1)}_{1}/2,\Omega_{1}^{(1)};\tau^{(1)}_{1}/2,\Omega_{1}^{(1)}\}
and use them as the starting point for second iteration. The final solution involves a control sequence
\Omega
wherein each segment has a specific duration, amplitude, frequency, as well as initial phase.

Figure 5:
Split and search method used for generating SMP.

Although stochastic search is computationally expensive, it was successfully applied in generating controls in several QIP tasks
Boulant et al. 2003
;
Weinstein et al. 2004
;
Mahesh and Suter 2006
;
Baugh et al. 2006
,
including QOC of the 12-qubit NMR system
Negrevergne et al. 2006
.

IV.2.2
Simulated annealing (SA)

SA
Černỳ 1985
is a metaheuristic algorithm that attempts to reach the global optimum of the parameter space by gradually shifting from exploration mode to exploitation mode. In the
i
th iteration of threshold-based SA, the solution
\Omega^{(i)}
with performance
\Phi(\Omega^{(i)})
is compared against a random neighborhood point
\Omega^{\prime}
with performance
\Phi(\Omega^{\prime})
. We define

\delta\Phi^{(i)}=\Phi(\Omega^{(i)})-\Phi(\Omega^{\prime})
and the threshold function (see Fig.
6
)

\displaystyle\Delta^{(i)}=\min\left[1,T^{(i)}e^{-\delta\Phi^{(i)}/T^{(i)}}\right].

(25)

Here, the temperature
T^{(i)}
is an iteration-dependent parameter lowering which causes the threshold function to approach zero.
The selection rule is

\displaystyle\Omega^{(i+1)}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\Omega^{\prime}&\mbox{if}~~\delta\Phi^{(i)}\leq\Delta^{(i)}\\
\Omega^{(i)}&\mbox{otherwise.}\end{cases}

(26)

Figure 6:
Threshold function
\Delta^{(i)}
vs temperature
T^{(i)}
and change in performance
\delta\Phi^{(i)}
.

Notice that the random solution is selected not only when it is a better solution but also when it is slightly worse than the current solution. This is the salient feature of SA that enables it to jump over local optima and reach the best neighborhood. Eventually the algorithm should promote solutions comparable to or better than the current solution.
This is achieved by gradually reducing the temperature parameter which makes the algorithm more and more exploitative. Some recent applications of SA include solving quantum circuit transformation problem
Zhou et al. 2020
,
preparing high fidelity quantum controls
Ram et al. 2021
, and optimizing quantum circuits for simultaneous dense protocol
Situ and He 2022
.

IV.2.3
Chopped random basis optimization (CRAB)

In QOC, often high fidelity solutions may lie in a low dimensional subspace of the larger dimensional search space
Lloyd and Montangero 2014
. This property is exploited in algorithms like CRAB, where the control sequence is written as a linear combination of sensibly chosen basis functions which are fewer in numbers compared to the dimensionality of the original search space.
CRAB was first developed for optimizing time dependent density matrix simulations
Doria et al. 2011
, and was later adapted for the optimization of quantum processes
Caneva et al. 2011
;
Müller et al. 2021
.
Here, the control sequence
is modeled in terms of a continuous orthogonal functional basis, such as Fourier series, Lagrange polynomials, Hermite functions, etc. For example, in terms of Fourier basis, the amplitude of the control sequence of duration
T
for channel
m
can be modeled as

\displaystyle\omega_{m}(t)

\displaystyle=\bar{\omega}_{m}+\sum_{k=1}^{K}\alpha_{km}\cos\phi_{km}(t)+\beta_{km}\sin\phi_{km}(t),

(27)

Here
\bar{\omega}_{m}
is the constant component,
\alpha_{km}
,
\beta_{km}
are the Fourier coefficients,
K
is the total number of harmonics, and
\phi_{km}=2\pi kt(1+r_{km})/T
with random numbers
r_{km}\in[-0.5,0.5]
added to improve the convergence.
Since the control sequence is not discretized, one resorts to numerical methods such as Runge-Kutta integration to calculate the propagator (Eq.
3
). The performance function is then maximized by finding the optimal parameters for the model function. For this,
CRAB relies on the direct search, such as Nelder-Mead simplex
Riaz et al. 2019
.
The CRAB algorithm has been successfully applied to control of Bose-Einstein-Condensates
Sørensen et al. 2018
, adiabatic population transfer of dressed spin states
Wu et al. 2019a
, quantum Szilard engine optimization
Sørdal and Bergli 2019
, etc.

IV.2.4
Evolutionary Methods

Evolutionary methods, such as genetic algorithm (GA), are inspired from biological evolution processes, such as reproduction, mutation, and survival of fittest, which leads to the emergence of a strong breed in an evolving population. In GA, the initial random population of genes goes through crossover, mutation, and selection based on a fitness function. There have been several implementations of GA based QOC, such as generating unitary and nonunitary QOC
Bhole et al. 2016
, for preparing singlet order in an 11-qubit register
Khurana and Mahesh 2017b
, and QOC of qutrits
Zahedinejad et al. 2014
.
Another variant of the evolutionary method, namely differential evolution (DE),
has also been used for gate control
Zahedinejad et al. 2014
and
QOC of the open quantum system
Ma et al. 2015
.

IV.3
Gradient Methods

We now look at optimization methods that systematically move towards a local optimum by evaluating the local gradients.

IV.3.1
Gradient Ascent Pulse Engineering (GRAPE)

Khaneja et al.
Khaneja et al. 2005

proposed a gradient method for iteratively generating a control sequence. Being a local search method, it starts from a random sequence
{\Omega}^{(0)}
of
N
segments, each of duration
\tau
, and the goal is to optimize the amplitudes
\omega_{mn}
. The
control Hamiltonian is

{\cal H}_{m}({\omega}_{mn})=\omega_{mn}{\cal A}_{m},

where
{\cal A}_{m}
is the control operator.
In the
i
th iteration, each segment is corrected according to

\displaystyle\omega_{mn}^{(i)}

\displaystyle=\omega_{mn}^{(i-1)}+\epsilon\tau~g_{mn}^{(i)},

(28)

where
\epsilon
is the step size and
g_{mn}^{(i)}
is the gradient (see Fig.
7
).

Figure 7:
Illustrating the gradients
g^{(i)}_{mn}
for amplitudes
\omega_{mn}
in the
i
th iteration of GRAPE algorithm
Khaneja et al. 2005
.

GRAPE is popular, thanks to the simple analytical expressions for the first-order gradients
Khaneja et al. 2005
.

\displaystyle g_{mn}^{(i)}=\begin{cases}\mbox{Im}\left\{\langle{U_{F}}|{U_{n+1:N}{\cal A}_{m}U_{1:n}}\rangle\langle{U_{1:N}}|{U_{F}}\rangle\right\}~&~\mbox{gate synthesis}\\
-i~\langle{\widetilde{\rho}_{n}}|{\left[{{\cal A}_{m}},{\rho_{n}}\right]}\rangle~&~\mbox{state transfer.}\end{cases}

(29)

Here forward propagated state
\rho_{n}=U_{1:n}\rho_{I}U_{1:n}^{\dagger}
and
backward propagated state
~\widetilde{\rho}_{n}=U_{n+1:N}^{\dagger}\rho_{F}U_{n+1:N}
.

While GRAPE has been used for quantum control in multiple architectures
Rowland and Jones 2012
, there are also numerous variants of GRAPE. Lucarelli
Lucarelli 2018
implemented
the bandwidth limited GRAPE algorithm using Slepian basis functions. Priya et al
Batra et al. 2020
reported Push-Pull GRAPE wherein the optimization is driven by the attraction of target operators as well as the repulsion from orthogonal operators.
GRAPE for open quantum systems is discussed in
Boutin et al. 2017
. GRAPE has also been adopted for optimal control of quantum measurement
Egger and Wilhelm 2014
.

The convergence rate of the GRAPE algorithm is limited by the first-order approximation made in the gradient expression of Eq.
29
. Moreover, the step-size
\epsilon
needs to be smartly chosen for a given control problem. These limitations are overcome by the second-order method, which is computationally expensive though. The quasi-Newton method namely, Broyden–Fletcher–Goldfarb–Shanno (BFGS) is one of the popular second-order algorithms.
de Fouquieres et al
De Fouquieres et al. 2011
demonstrated convergence acceleration via BFGS algorithm.

IV.3.2
Gradient optimization of analytic controls (GOAT)

While GRAPE is quite successful, the control sequences are discrete and rugged. Like CRAB discussed earlier, one may rather prefer to express the control sequence in a smooth analytical form. It also allows encoding the solution in a lower dimensional parameter space. This is one of the benefits of gradient optimization of analytic controls (GOAT)
Machnes et al. 2018
.
The model analytical function may be an educated guess or randomly chosen. For example, if one models the control sequence in terms of the superposition of
K
Gaussian pulses,

\displaystyle\omega_{m}(t)=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\exp\left[-\frac{(t-\delta_{m,k})^{2}}{\sigma_{m,k}^{2}}\right].

(30)

Here
m
th channel parameters
\alpha_{m}=\{\delta_{m,k},\sigma_{m,k}\}
are optimized to minimize the gate infidelity

f(\Omega)=1-{\langle{U_{F}}|{U_{\Omega}}\rangle}/{\langle{U_{F}}|{U_{F}}\rangle}
.
The gradients are then given by

Machnes et al. 2018

\displaystyle\partial_{\alpha}F(\Omega)=-\mathrm{Re}\left[\frac{f^{*}(\Omega)}{|f(\Omega)|}\frac{\langle{U_{F}}|{\partial_{\alpha}U_{\Omega}}\rangle}{\langle{U_{F}}|{U_{F}}\rangle}\right].

(31)

While there is no analytical solution to the above equation, one can use the equation of motion

\partial_{t}U_{\Omega}(t)=-iH(t)U_{\Omega}(t)

to obtain the coupled system of equations

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}U_{\Omega}(t)\\
\partial_{\alpha}U_{\Omega}(t)\end{array}\right)=-i\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}H&0\\
\partial_{\alpha}H&H\end{array}\right)\left(\begin{array}[]{c}U_{\Omega}(t)\\
\partial_{\alpha}U_{\Omega}(t)\end{array}\right),

which can be solved by numerical forward integration, such as the adaptive Runge-Kutta. Machnes et al
Machnes et al. 2018
demonstrated the GOAT algorithm by generating the iSWAP gate for a pair of transmon qubits coupled to a tunable bus resonator. Kirchoff et al demonstrated a time-optimal CNOT gate on a pair of transmon qubits
Kirchhoff et al. 2018
.

IV.4
Variational methods

Variational methods are widely used for constrained optimization and are less sensitive to local optima compared to gradient methods. For example, the Krotov algorithm that is based on the Lagrange multiplier exhibits a monotonic convergent behavior
Krotov 1995
. It starts with the Lagrange multiplier

\displaystyle B_{n}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}U_{n+1:N}^{\dagger}U_{F}\langle{U_{F}}|{U_{1:N}}\rangle~&~\mbox{for gate synthesis, and}\\
U_{n+1:N}^{\dagger}\left\{\rho_{F}U_{1:N}\rho_{I}+\kappa U_{1:N}\right\}~&~\mbox{for state-to-state transfer,}\end{cases}

(38)

where
\kappa
is a positive constant to ensure positivity of fidelity. In addition to the control sequence
\Omega
, the Krotov method involves a co-sequence
\widetilde{\Omega}
, both of which are initialized with the same random guess, i.e.,

\Omega^{(0)}=\widetilde{\Omega}^{(0)}
.
These sequences are propagated forward and backward respectively according to the rules
Maximov et al. 2008
;
Reich et al. 2012
;
Batra et al. 2020

\displaystyle\omega_{mn}^{(i)}

\displaystyle=(1-\delta)\widetilde{\omega}_{mn}^{(i-1)}+\frac{\delta}{\lambda_{m}}\mathrm{Im}\langle{B_{n}^{(i-1)}}|{{\cal A}_{m}U_{0:n-1}^{(i)}}\rangle

\displaystyle\widetilde{\omega}_{mn}^{(i)}

\displaystyle=(1-\eta)\omega_{mn}^{{(i)}}+\frac{\eta}{\lambda_{m}}\mathrm{Im}\langle{B_{n}^{(i)}}|{{\cal A}_{m}U_{0:n}^{(i)}}\rangle,

(39)

where
\lambda_{m}
are penalty constants and other constants
\eta,\delta\in[0,2]
.
Application of Krotov optimization for the optimal control of spin dynamics in NMR and dynamical nuclear polarization gained significant attention
Maximov et al. 2008
;
Reich et al. 2012
. Vinding et al. showed the use of Krotov in magnetic resonance imaging for design of spatial selective RF pulses
Vinding et al. 2012
. Hwang et al. showed the control of a non-Markovian open quantum system for Z gate and identity gate
Hwang and Goan 2012
. Krotov optimization has also been applied for the control of BEC in magnetic microtraps
Jäger et al. 2014
.

IV.5
Machine Learning Methods

With the ever increasing computational power, the machine learning (ML) methods are finding applications everywhere, including QOC.
Reinforcement learning (RL), for example, is popularly used in a variety of problems of finding the best strategy that maximizes a reward function
Sutton and Barto 2018
. In QOC, RL can be modeled to find the strategy that maximizes the fidelity reward. For example, Bukov et al demonstrated RL to control out-of-equilibrium systems
Bukov et al. 2018
. RL has also been applied for problems such as state control
An and Zhou 2019
, gate control
Zhang et al. 2019
;
Baum et al. 2021
, for generating controls robust against certain types of errors
Niu et al. 2019
, and for the control of multilevel dissipative quantum systems
An et al. 2021
.
QOC with supervised ML
Wu et al. 2019b
;
Zeng et al. 2020
and with convolutional neural networks trained through deep learning architecture for a quantum particle in a disordered system
Huang et al. 2022
have also been reported. Another type of ML, namely differential programming (DP), together with a neural network was used for eigenstate preparation in a variety of single and multi-qubit systems
Schäfer et al. 2020
as well as for the control of quantum thermal machines
Khait et al. 2021
. Coopmans et al. showed the transport of magnons in a spin chain by combining DP with CRAB and shortcut to adiabaticity protocols
Coopmans et al. 2022
.

IV.6
Hybrid Methods

There have been several attempts to combine different algorithms to realize hybrid algorithms which incorporate the best of the original methods.
For example, Machnes et al.
Machnes et al. 2011a
combined GRAPE and Krotov to form the hybrid algorithm and implemented it in the DYNAMO package.

While GRAPE can get stuck in a local optimum, the simulated annealing (SA) is good at jumping over it and finding a better neighborhood.
Thus the hybrid algorithm, SAGRAPE, combines the best of the two
Ram et al. 2021
.
As explained in Sec.
IV.2.2
, an important step in SA in every iteration is to scan the neighborhood points of the current solution, which is a bottleneck in the standard SAGRAPE algorithm. This is where ML can bring about a significant speedup. Priya et al
Batra et al. 2022
have reported the recommender system (RS) expedited SAGRAPE (RSSAGRAPE), wherein both SA and GRAPE routines of SAGRAPE are speeded-up by RS.

The variation principle-based Krotov algorithm can lead to monotonic increase at low fidelity but gets slow at high fidelities. The gradient-based quasi-newton BFGS can yield high fidelity solutions, but need more computational resources. To this end, Tannor et.al
Eitan et al. 2011
proposed the hybrid Krotov-BFGS (K-BFGS) algorithm.

For many QOC problems, high fidelity solutions lie in a low dimensional subspace of the larger dimensional search space
Lloyd and Montangero 2014
. This property is exploited in algorithms like CRAB where the control sequence is written as a linear combination of sensibly chosen basis functions which are fewer in number when compared to the dimensionality of the original search space.
Sorensen et.al
Sørensen et al. 2018
proposed a hybrid algorithm called gradient optimisation using parameterisation (GROUP) that incorporates both CRAB and GRAPE algorithms. Here, an analytical expression of the gradient of the cost function w.r.t the previously mentioned linear coefficients is derived and is used in place of Nelder-Mead to update the linear coefficients. The resulting algorithm can produce higher fidelity controls for the same number of functional evaluations as the CRAB algorithm.

V
Summary and outlook

To summarize, we had set out three intentions in this review. First, to introduce the basic aspects of QOC. Second, to point out various practical aspects in optimizing QOC as well as implementing the control sequence. Third, to provide a quick overview of the diverse methods used for QOC. We provided brief descriptions of various QOC algorithms sorting them into five categories, namely, asymptotic method, direct search, gradient method, variational method, and machine learning.
