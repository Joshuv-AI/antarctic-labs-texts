Bona-Smith-type systems in bounded domains with slip-wall boundary conditions: Theoretical justification and a conservative numerical scheme
Dimitrios Antonopoulos, Dimitrios Mitsotakis

Abstract
. Considered herein is a class of Boussinesq systems of Bona-Smith type that describe the propagation of long surface water waves of small amplitude in bounded two-dimensional domains with slip-wall boundary conditions and variable bottom topography. Such boundary conditions are necessary in situations involving water waves in channels, ports, and generally in basins with solid boundaries. We prove that, given appropriate initial conditions, the corresponding initial-boundary value problems have unique solutions locally in time, which is a fundamental property of deterministic mathematical modeling. Moreover, we demonstrate that the systems under consideration adhere to three basic conservation laws for water waves: mass, vorticity, and energy conservation. The theoretical analysis of these specific Boussinesq systems leads to a conservative mixed finite element formulation. Using explicit, relaxation Runge-Kutta methods for the discretization in time, we devise a fully discrete scheme for the numerical solution of initial-boundary value problems with slip-wall conditions, preserving mass, vorticity, and energy. Finally, we present a series of challenging numerical experiments to assess the applicability of the new numerical model.

The fundamental equations describing nonlinear and dispersive water waves are the inviscid and incompressible Euler equations of fluid mechanics, accompanied by appropriate boundary conditions
[

Examples of small amplitude long water waves include tsunamis, solitary waves, and undular bores. In this work, we assume irrotational flow and we concentrate our attention on Bona-Smith-type systems posed in a bounded domain

Traditionally, Galerkin discretizations applied to problems in bounded domains with slip-wall boundary conditions incorporate Nitsche’s method

In contrast, the numerical method proposed in this work operates on a formulation of the Bona-Smith systems based on the horizontal velocity potential
\phi

\mathbf{u}=\nabla\phi

Importantly, it is worth noting that the proposed numerical method can also be applied to solve the BBM-BBM system described in

The structure of this paper is as follows: In Section

We conclude this paper with numerical experiments. In Section

2.
Justification of the mathematical model

We consider the Bona-Smith system of Boussinesq equations,
[
49
]
, written in the form

(3)

\displaystyle\eta_{t}+\nabla\!\cdot\![(D+\eta)\mathbf{u}]-b\nabla\!\cdot\!\left\{D^{2}\nabla\eta_{t}\right\}=0\ ,

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}_{t}+g\nabla\eta+\tfrac{1}{2}\nabla|\mathbf{u}|^{2}-\nabla\left\{cg\nabla\!\cdot\!(D^{2}\nabla\eta)+b\nabla\!\cdot\!(D^{2}\mathbf{u}_{t})\right\}=0\ ,

where

(4)

b=\frac{3\theta^{2}-1}{6}>0,\quad c=\frac{3\theta^{2}-2}{3}>0\quad\text{and}\quad\frac{2}{3}<\theta^{2}\leq 1\ .

The limiting case where
\theta^{2}=2/3
corresponds to the so-called BBM-BBM system (or regularized shallow-water equations) which has been studied in
[
49
]
. Moreover, the Bona-Smith systems (
3
) can be used to study waves in a bounded domain
\Omega
where the boundary consists of solid walls with no friction (slip-walls). Slip-walls can be described by the boundary conditions

(5)

\nabla\eta\cdot\mathbf{n}=0\quad\text{and}\quad\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{n}=0\quad\text{on $\partial\Omega$}\ .

Note that the solutions of the Euler equations satisfy the same conditions on a slip-wall
[
50
]
, and therefore such boundary conditions are justified.

In what follows, we will study the initial-boundary value problem comprised the system (
3
), the boundary conditions (
5
), and the initial conditions:

(6)

\eta(\mathbf{x},0)=\eta_{0}(\mathbf{x})\quad\text{and}\quad\mathbf{u}(\mathbf{x},0)=\mathbf{u}_{0}(\mathbf{x})\quad\text{for $\mathbf{x}\in\Omega$}\ .

This serves as a model initial-boundary value problem for studying the propagation of long nonlinear waves of small amplitude in a bounded region.

2.1.
Energy conservation and Hamiltonian structure

As demonstrated in
[
50
]
, the solutions of the initial-boundary value problem (
3
)–(
5

(7)

\mathcal{M}(t;\eta)=\int_{\Omega}\eta~d\mathbf{x}\ ,

the vorticity

\mathcal{V}(t;\mathbf{u})=\int_{\Omega}\nabla\!\times\!\mathbf{u}~d\mathbf{x}\ ,

and the energy functional

Note that the integral in (

Also, in the case of general bottom topography, the so-called impulse functional is not preserved since the equations are not invariant under horizontal translations – a property that holds true only when the bottom is flat. Note that the term impulse is used for quadratic invariants of water waves systems, and should not be confused with the momentum conservation

Finally, we mention that the system (

then the problem (

\mathbf{w}_{t}=\mathbf{J}\mathcal{D}\mathcal{E}(\mathbf{w})\ ,

where
\mathbf{w}=(\eta,\mathbf{u})^{T}
,

denotes the variational derivative, and

is the skew-adjoint operator

\mathbf{J}=\begin{pmatrix}0&[I-b\nabla\!\cdot\!(D^{2}\nabla)]^{-1}\nabla\!\cdot\!\\
[I-b\nabla\!\cdot\!(D^{2}\nabla)]^{-1}\nabla&0\end{pmatrix}\ .

2.4.
Equivalence between velocity field and potential formulations

Problems (
3

(\eta,\mathbf{u}=\nabla\phi)
of problem (

\phi_{0}(\mathbf{x})=\phi(\mathbf{x},0)

is also a velocity potential of
\mathbf{u}
. However, fixing the constant

of the solutions of (

Additionally, the solutions to problem (

\tfrac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}\int_{\Omega}[g\eta^{2}+(D+\eta)|\nabla\phi|^{2}+cgD^{2}|\nabla\eta|^{2}]~d\mathbf{x}=0\ .

\eta_{t}+\nabla\!\cdot\!R=0,\quad\phi_{t}+Q=0\ ,

where

. Then, we multiply the first equation of (

and the second with
-\nabla\cdot R
. Equation (

\Omega
, applying Green’s theorem appropriately and simplifying.

These observations and the fact that the solution
(\eta,\phi)
of the initial-boundary value problem (

formulates the solution

of the corresponding problem (

)–(

) suggests a numerical method that does not require the application of Nitsche’s method
[

]
for approximating slip-wall boundary conditions
[

to preserve the mass, vorticity and total energy of problem (

)–(

It should be noted that the velocity potential formulation is applicable to other systems within the general class (

2.5.
Line solitary waves

Line solitary waves are traveling wave solutions that propagate in one direction, denoted by
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
, along which they vary and exhibit symmetry, while remaining constant in the transverse direction
\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{\perp}
. Here we consider only classical solitary waves that are decreasing monotonically to zero at infinity along the direction of motion. Typically,
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
represents the direction of a channel through which the solitary wave is propagating and in which it vanishes at large distances from the wave’s peak. It is established that when the bottom is flat, with depth
D=D_{0}
, the Bona-Smith systems have possess solitary waves
[
28
,
29
]
as traveling wave solutions. Specifically, contrary to the Euler equations, for any speed
c_{s}>\sqrt{gD_{0}}
, there exists a unique solitary wave traveling at that particular speed
[
35
,
34
]
. Moreover, these waves have been experimentally tested and, contrary to the solitary waves of the Euler equations
[
79
,
39
]
, are reportedly stable under both small and large perturbations for all values of speed
c_{s}>\sqrt{gD_{0}}
,
[
33
]
.

Assume that the domain
\Omega
is the whole
\mathbb{R}^{2}
or a very long channel in the direction
\boldsymbol{\alpha}=(\alpha_{x},\alpha_{y})^{T}
with
|\boldsymbol{\alpha}|=1
and that the bottom is flat with
D(\mathbf{x})=D_{0}
. Traveling wave solutions propagating along the direction of
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
with constant speed
c_{s}>\sqrt{gD_{0}}
can be expressed as functions of the form

(25)

\eta(\mathbf{x},t)=\eta(\boldsymbol{\xi}),\qquad\mathbf{u}(\mathbf{x},t)=\mathbf{u}(\boldsymbol{\xi})\ ,

where
\boldsymbol{\xi}=(\xi,\zeta)
with
\xi=\boldsymbol{\alpha}\cdot\mathbf{x}-c_{s}t
and
\zeta=\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{\perp}\cdot\mathbf{x}
. Here,
\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{\perp}=(\alpha_{y},-\alpha_{x})^{T}
.

Substituting the
ansatz
(
25
) into the Bona-Smith system (
3
), we obtain the partial differential equations

(26)

\displaystyle-c_{s}\eta_{\xi}+\nabla_{\xi\zeta}\cdot[(D_{0}+\eta)\mathbf{A}\mathbf{u}]+c_{s}bD_{0}^{2}\Delta_{\xi\zeta}\eta_{\xi}=0\ ,

\displaystyle-c_{s}\mathbf{u}_{\xi}+g\mathbf{A}\nabla_{\xi\zeta}\eta+\tfrac{1}{2}\mathbf{A}\nabla_{\xi\zeta}|\mathbf{u}|^{2}-cgD_{0}^{2}\mathbf{A}\nabla_{\xi\zeta}\Delta_{\xi\zeta}\eta+c_{s}bD_{0}^{2}\mathbf{A}\nabla_{\xi\zeta}(\nabla_{\xi\zeta}\cdot\mathbf{A}\mathbf{u}_{\xi})=0\ ,

where

\mathbf{A}=\begin{pmatrix}\alpha_{x}&\alpha_{y}\\
\alpha_{y}&-\alpha_{x}\end{pmatrix}\ ,

\nabla_{\xi\zeta}=(\partial_{\xi},\partial_{\zeta})^{T}
and
\Delta_{\xi\zeta}=\nabla_{\xi\zeta}\cdot\nabla_{\xi\zeta}
.
Multiplying the second equation of (
26
) by
\mathbf{A}
, setting
\mathbf{w}=(w,\tilde{w})^{T}=\mathbf{A}\mathbf{u}
, and using the fact
\mathbf{A}^{2}=\mathbf{I}
, then system (
26
) is simplified to

(27)

\displaystyle-c_{s}\eta_{\xi}+\nabla_{\xi\zeta}\cdot[(D_{0}+\eta)\mathbf{w}]+c_{s}bD_{0}^{2}\Delta_{\xi\zeta}\eta_{\xi}=0\ ,

\displaystyle-c_{s}\mathbf{w}_{\xi}+g\nabla_{\xi\zeta}\eta+\tfrac{1}{2}\nabla_{\xi\zeta}|\mathbf{w}|^{2}-cgD_{0}^{2}\nabla_{\xi\zeta}\Delta_{\xi\zeta}\eta+c_{s}bD_{0}^{2}\nabla_{\xi\zeta}(\nabla_{\xi\zeta}\cdot\mathbf{w}_{\xi})=0\ .

Since we are searching for line solitary waves, we assume that the solution
\mathbf{u}
is constant along the direction of
\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{\perp}
. Thus,
\partial_{\zeta}\eta=\partial_{\zeta}w=\partial_{\zeta}\tilde{w}=0
. Integrating equations (
27
) with respect to
\xi
and assuming that
\lim_{|\xi|\to\infty}w=0
, yields the differential equations

(28)

\displaystyle-c_{s}\eta+(D_{0}+\eta)w+c_{s}bD_{0}^{2}\Delta_{\xi\zeta}\eta

\displaystyle=0\ ,

\displaystyle-c_{s}w+g\eta+\tfrac{1}{2}w^{2}-cgD_{0}^{2}\Delta_{\xi\zeta}\eta+c_{s}bD_{0}^{2}w_{\xi\xi}

\displaystyle=0\ ,

\displaystyle\tilde{w}

\displaystyle=0\ .

This particular system has been analyzed in
[
28
,
29
]
and has classical, line solitary waves as traveling wave solutions. In the case where
w=B\eta
, one can find for each value of
\theta^{2}\in(1/3,1)
an explicit solution for a solitary wave
[
29
]
, namely

(29)

\eta(\boldsymbol{\xi})=A\text{sech}^{2}\left(\lambda(\xi+\xi_{0})\right),\quad w(\boldsymbol{\xi})=B\eta(\boldsymbol{\xi}),\quad\tilde{w}(\boldsymbol{\xi})=0\ ,

where

(30)

A=3\frac{g-D_{0}B^{2}}{B^{2}},\ \lambda=\tfrac{1}{2}\sqrt{\frac{2(g-D_{0}B^{2})}{bD_{0}^{2}(2g-D_{0}B^{2})}},\ c_{s}=\frac{4\sqrt{gD_{0}}\left(\theta^{2}-\frac{2}{3}\right)}{\sqrt{2\left(\theta^{2}-\frac{1}{3}\right)\left(1-\theta^{2}\right)}},\ B=\sqrt{\frac{2g}{D_{0}}\cdot\frac{1-\theta^{2}}{\theta^{2}-\frac{1}{3}}}\ .

Returning to the original variables, for

, the solitary wave formulas become

(31)

\eta(\boldsymbol{\xi})=A\text{sech}^{2}\left(\lambda(\boldsymbol{\alpha}\cdot\mathbf{x}-c_{s}t)\right),\quad\mathbf{u}(\boldsymbol{\xi})=\boldsymbol{\alpha}B\eta(\boldsymbol{\xi})\ ,

where

(32)

\displaystyle A=\frac{9D_{0}}{2}\cdot\frac{\theta^{2}-7/9}{1-\theta^{2}},

\displaystyle\lambda=\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\frac{3(\theta^{2}-7/9)}{D_{0}(\theta^{2}-2/3)(\theta^{2}-1/3)}},

\displaystyle c_{s}=\frac{4\sqrt{gD_{0}}\left(\theta^{2}-\frac{2}{3}\right)}{\sqrt{2\left(\theta^{2}-\frac{1}{3}\right)\left(1-\theta^{2}\right)}},

\displaystyle B=\sqrt{\frac{2g}{D_{0}}\cdot\frac{1-\theta^{2}}{\theta^{2}-\frac{1}{3}}}\ .

Note that these formulas correspond to a unique solitary wave for each

3

. These analytical solutions are useful for testing purposes. However, when other solitary waves are involved in applications, we resort to numerical methods for solving system (

[
71
]
. The Petviashvili method applied to the BBM-BBM system was presented in
[

]
. In this work we extend this method for the general case of the Bona-Smith systems at hand and we present it in Section
3
.

2.6.

The Bona-Smith systems (

whereas for the Euler equations, it is represented as:

(34)

\frac{c_{s}}{\sqrt{gD_{0}}}=\sqrt{\frac{\tanh(D_{0}k)}{D_{0}k}}\ .

3.
Energy-preserving numerical method

Consider the initial-boundary value problem

(35)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\eta_{t}+\nabla\!\cdot\![(D+\eta)\nabla\phi]-b\nabla\!\cdot\!\left\{D^{2}\nabla\eta_{t}\right\}=0\ ,\\
&\phi_{t}+g\eta+\tfrac{1}{2}|\nabla\phi|^{2}-cg\nabla\!\cdot\!(D^{2}\nabla\eta)-b\nabla\!\cdot\!(D^{2}\nabla\phi_{t})=0\ ,\end{aligned}\quad\text{in $\Omega$}\ ,

\displaystyle\eta(\mathbf{x},0)=\eta_{0}(\mathbf{x})\quad\text{and}\quad\phi(\mathbf{x},0)=\phi_{0}(\mathbf{x})\ ,

\displaystyle\nabla\eta\cdot\mathbf{n}=0\quad\text{and}\quad\nabla\phi\cdot\mathbf{n}=0\quad\text{on $\partial\Omega$}\ .

Let
\Omega
be a polygonal domain and
b,d>0
,
c\geq 0
constants such that the problem (
12
) has a unique smooth solution
(\eta,\mathbf{u})
. Upon obtaining an approximate solution
(\tilde{\eta},\tilde{\phi})
to (
35

(\tilde{\eta},\tilde{\mathbf{u}})=(\tilde{\eta},\nabla\tilde{\phi})
. In what follows we present a conservative numerical method for the problem (

3.1.
Energy-preserving modified Galerkin method

Assume that
\mathcal{T}_{h}
is a regular triangulation of
\Omega
with triangles
\tau
of maximum edge
h
. We define the space of Lagrange finite elements of degree
r

\mathcal{P}_{h}^{r}=\{f\in C(\Omega)~:~f|_{\tau}\in\mathbb{P}_{r}(\tau)\text{ for all }\tau\in\mathcal{T}_{h}\}\ ,

where
\mathbb{P}_{r}
denotes the space of polynomials of degree
r
. The space
\mathcal{P}^{r}_{h}
is a subset of
C(\bar{\Omega})\cap H^{1}(\Omega)

[
41
]
. Furthermore, we denote by
P^{r}
the
L^{2}
-projection of any function
f\in L^{2}(\Omega)
onto the space
\mathcal{P}^{r}_{h}
defined by

(P^{r}[f],\chi)=(f,\chi)\quad\text{ for all }\chi\in\mathcal{P}^{r}_{h}\ .

If
w,v\in H^{1}
, we define the modified discrete Laplacian operator
\Delta_{h}:H^{1}\to\mathcal{P}^{r}_{h}
such that

(\Delta_{h}[w;v],\chi)=-(w\nabla v,\nabla\chi)\qquad\text{ for all $\chi\in\mathcal{P}^{r}_{h}$}\ .

Then, we define the numerical solution of the problem (
35
) as
\tilde{\eta},\tilde{\phi}\in\mathcal{P}^{r}_{h}
such that

(36)

\displaystyle(\tilde{\eta}_{t},\chi)+(\Delta_{h}[D+\tilde{\eta};\tilde{\phi}],\chi)-b(\Delta_{h}[D^{2};\tilde{\eta}_{t}],\chi)=0

\displaystyle\text{for all $\chi\in\mathcal{P}^{r}_{h}$}\ ,

\displaystyle(\tilde{\phi}_{t},\psi)+g(\tilde{\eta},\psi)+\tfrac{1}{2}(P^{r}[|\nabla\tilde{\phi}|^{2}],\psi)-cg(\Delta_{h}[D^{2};\tilde{\eta}],\psi)-b(\Delta_{h}[D^{2};\tilde{\phi}_{t}],\psi)=0

\displaystyle\text{for all $\psi\in\mathcal{P}^{r}_{h}$}\ ,

along with the initial conditions

(37)

\tilde{\eta}(\mathbf{x},0)=P^{r}[\eta_{0}(\mathbf{x})],\quad\tilde{\phi}(\mathbf{x},0)=P^{r}[\phi_{0}(\mathbf{x})]\ .

The semidiscretization (
36
) is an energy-conservative generalization of the modified Galerkin method introduced in
[
37
]
. It also extends the method presented in
[
66
]
to one-dimensional systems with float bottom and with high-order derivatives, similar to those considered here.

We assume that the initial-value problem (
36
), (
37
) has a unique solution in the interval

for some

. Then, the corresponding solution
(\tilde{\eta},\tilde{\phi})
of the modified Galerkin semidiscretization (
36
)–(

\mathcal{M}(t;\tilde{\eta})=\int_{\Omega}\tilde{\eta}(\mathbf{x},t)~dt\ ,

and the energy

\mathcal{E}(t;\tilde{\eta},\tilde{\phi})=\tfrac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}[g\tilde{\eta}^{2}+(D+\tilde{\eta})|\nabla\tilde{\phi}|^{2}+cgD^{2}|\nabla\tilde{\eta}|^{2}]~d\mathbf{x}\ ,

in the sense

,

. Specifically, we have the following:

Proposition 3.1
.

The solution
(\tilde{\eta},\tilde{\phi})
of the initial-value problem (
36

37
) satisfies the following conservation laws for all

:

(i)

Conservation of mass:

\frac{d}{dt}\mathcal{M}(t;\tilde{\eta})=0\ .

(ii)

Conservation of energy:

\frac{d}{dt}\mathcal{E}(t;\tilde{\eta},\tilde{\phi})=0\ .

Proof.

The mass conservation follows by taking
\chi=1
in the first equation of (
36
). To prove (ii), we define

\tilde{R}=\Delta_{h}[D+\tilde{\eta};\tilde{\phi}]-b\Delta_{h}[D^{2};\tilde{\eta}_{t}]\in\mathcal{P}^{r}_{h}\quad\text{ and }\quad\tilde{Q}=g\tilde{\eta}+\tfrac{1}{2}P^{r}[|\nabla\phi|^{2}]-cg\Delta_{h}[D^{2};\tilde{\eta}]-b\Delta_{h}[D^{2};\tilde{\phi}_{t}]\in\mathcal{P}^{r}_{h}\ .

Then, the system (
36
) can be expressed as

\displaystyle(\tilde{\eta}_{t},\chi)+(\tilde{R},\chi)=0

\displaystyle\text{ for all $\chi\in\mathcal{P}^{r}_{h}$}\ ,

\displaystyle(\tilde{\phi}_{t},\psi)+(\tilde{Q},\psi)=0

\displaystyle\text{ for all $\psi\in\mathcal{P}^{r}_{h}$}\ .

By taking
\chi=\tilde{Q}
and
\psi=\tilde{R}
and subtracting the last two equations, yields

. On the other hand, we have

\displaystyle(\tilde{\eta}_{t},\tilde{Q})-(\tilde{\phi}_{t},\tilde{R})

\displaystyle=g(\tilde{\eta}_{t},\tilde{\eta})+\tfrac{1}{2}(\tilde{\eta}_{t},P^{r}[|\nabla\phi|^{2}])-cg(\tilde{\eta}_{t},\Delta_{h}[D^{2};\tilde{\eta}])-b(\tilde{\eta}_{t},\Delta_{h}[D^{2};\tilde{\phi}_{t}])

\displaystyle-(\tilde{\phi}_{t},\Delta_{h}[D+\tilde{\eta};\tilde{\phi}])+b(\tilde{\phi}_{t},\Delta_{h}[D^{2};\tilde{\eta}_{t}])

\displaystyle=g(\tilde{\eta}_{t},\tilde{\eta})+\tfrac{1}{2}(\tilde{\eta}_{t},|\nabla\phi|^{2})+cg(\nabla\tilde{\eta}_{t},D^{2}\nabla\tilde{\eta})+b(\nabla\tilde{\eta}_{t},D^{2}\nabla\tilde{\phi}_{t})

\displaystyle+(\nabla\tilde{\phi}_{t},(D+\tilde{\eta})\nabla\tilde{\phi}])-b(\nabla\tilde{\phi}_{t},D^{2}\nabla\tilde{\eta}_{t})

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}\int_{\Omega}[g\tilde{\eta}^{2}+cgD^{2}|\nabla\tilde{\eta}|^{2}+(D+\tilde{\eta})|\nabla\tilde{\phi}|^{2}~d\mathbf{x}\ .

Thus,
\frac{d}{dt}\mathcal{E}(t;\tilde{\eta},\tilde{\phi})=0
.
∎

By introducing auxiliary unknown functions
\tilde{v},\tilde{w},\tilde{z},\tilde{f}:[0,t]\to\mathcal{P}^{r}
for the
L^{2}
-projection and the various discrete Laplacians appearing in the semidiscretization (
36
), we can express system (
36
) in the following mixed formulation:

(38)

\displaystyle(\tilde{\eta}_{t},\chi)-b(\tilde{v}_{t},\chi)

\displaystyle=(\tilde{w},\chi)

\displaystyle\text{for all $\chi\in\mathcal{P}^{r}_{h}$}\ ,

\displaystyle(D^{2}\nabla\tilde{\eta}_{t},\nabla\omega)+(\tilde{v}_{t},\omega)

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\text{for all $\omega\in\mathcal{P}^{r}_{h}$}\ ,

\displaystyle(\tilde{w},\zeta)

\displaystyle=((D+\tilde{\eta})\nabla\tilde{\phi},\nabla\zeta)

\displaystyle\text{for all $\zeta\in\mathcal{P}^{r}_{h}$}\ ,

\displaystyle(\tilde{\phi}_{t},\psi)-b(\tilde{z}_{t},\psi)

\displaystyle=cg(\tilde{v},\psi)-g(\tilde{\eta},\psi)-\tfrac{1}{2}(\tilde{f},\psi)

\displaystyle\text{for all $\psi\in\mathcal{P}^{r}_{h}$}\ ,

\displaystyle(D^{2}\nabla\tilde{\phi}_{t},\nabla\xi)+(\tilde{z}_{t},\xi)

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\text{for all $\xi\in\mathcal{P}^{r}_{h}$}\ ,

\displaystyle(\tilde{f},\theta)

\displaystyle=(|\nabla\tilde{\phi}|^{2},\theta)

\displaystyle\text{for all $\theta\in\mathcal{P}^{r}_{h}$}\ ,

along with the initial conditions:

(39)

\displaystyle\tilde{\eta}(\mathbf{x},0)=P^{r}[\eta_{0}(\mathbf{x})],\qquad\tilde{v}(\mathbf{x},0)=\Delta_{h}[D^{2};\eta_{0}](\mathbf{x})\ ,

\displaystyle\tilde{\phi}(\mathbf{x},0)=P^{r}[\phi_{0}(\mathbf{x})],\qquad\tilde{z}(\mathbf{z},0)=\Delta_{h}[D^{2};\phi_{0}](\mathbf{x})\ .

This mixed formulation is convenient for implementation purposes.

It is worth noting that since the numerical velocity field
\tilde{\mathbf{u}}
is produced by the potential
\tilde{\phi}
as
\tilde{\mathbf{u}}=\nabla\tilde{\phi}
, it will satisfy

\nabla\!\times\!\tilde{\mathbf{u}}=\nabla\!\times\!\nabla\tilde{\phi}=0
for all
\mathbf{x}\in\Omega
and
t>0
. Therefore, in addition to mass and energy, the proposed numerical method guarantees the conservation of vorticity as well.

3.2.
Time discretization

For the time discretization of the autonomous system of ordinary differential equations (
38
), we can employ an explicit relaxation Runge-Kutta method
[
54
,
73
]
. As we shall see in Section
4
experimentally, the system (
38
) appears to be non-stiff, and explicit Runge-Kutta methods should be efficient. It is noteworthy that for Bona-Smith type systems containing similar regularization operators (BBM-terms), it was proven that there is no timestep restriction requirement for the standard Galerkin semidiscretization
[
37
]
. However, for the current, conservative Galerkin semidiscretization further analysis is required to study the effects of the regularization terms, and for this reason we will use sufficiently small timestep
\Delta t<\Delta x
.

Consider the initial-value problem

(40)

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\mathbf{y}(t)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{f}(t,\mathbf{y}(t)),\quad t\in[0,T],

\displaystyle\mathbf{y}(0)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{y}_{0}\ ,

where
\mathbf{y}(t)=[\mathbf{y}_{i}(t)]
is the vector of unknown functions. Let
\Delta t
be a fixed timestep such that
0<\Delta t<1
. We define
J=\{t_{0},t_{1},\dots,t_{K}\}
a uniform grid of the interval
[0,T]
such that
t_{i+1}=t_{i}+\Delta t
with
t_{0}=0
and
t_{K}=T
. An explicit Runge-Kutta method with
s
intermediate stages can be fully described by its Butcher tableau

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{c|c}c&A\\
\hline\cr&b^{\mathrm{T}}\end{array},

where
A=[a_{ij}]_{i,j=1}^{s}
is an
s\times s
lower-triangular matrix with zeros
in the main diagonal, and
b=[b_{j}]_{j=1}^{s}
and
c=[c_{j}]_{j=1}^{s}
are

s
-dimensional vectors. If
\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{n}=\{\gamma^{1},\gamma^{2},\dots,\gamma^{K}\}
is a vector containing the so-called relaxation parameters
\gamma^{n}\in(0,1]
, and
\mathbf{y}^{n}
is the approximation of
\mathbf{y}(t_{n-1}+\gamma^{n}\Delta t)
, then a step of an explicit relaxation Runge-Kutta method corresponding to the Butcher tableau (
3.2
)
can be expressed as

(43)

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathbf{y}}^{i}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{y}^{n}+\Delta t\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}a_{ij}\,\mathbf{f}(t_{n}+c_{j}\Delta t,\tilde{\mathbf{y}}^{j}),\quad i=1,2,\ldots,s,

(44)

\displaystyle\mathbf{y}^{n+1}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{y}^{n}+\gamma^{n}\Delta t\sum_{i=1}^{s}b_{i}\,\mathbf{f}(t_{n}+c_{i}\Delta t,\tilde{\mathbf{y}}^{i}).

The parameters
\gamma^{n}
are typically close to
1
and can be determined such as the energy of the system is preserved. For example, if the energy of the system (
40
) is
\mathcal{E}(t;\mathbf{y})=\mathcal{E}(0;\mathbf{y})
, then we can choose the parameter
\gamma^{n}
such that

\mathcal{E}(t_{n+1};\mathbf{y}^{n+1})=\mathcal{E}(t_{n};\mathbf{y}^{n})\ .

For simplicity, we will denote the sum in the right-hand side of (
44
) by

\mathbf{d}^{n}=\sum_{i=1}^{s}b_{i}\,\mathbf{f}(t_{n}+c_{i}\Delta t,\tilde{\mathbf{y}}^{i})\ .

In our case, we define the vector
\mathbf{y}^{n}=(\mathcal{H}^{n},\mathcal{W}^{n},\Phi^{n},\mathcal{Z}^{n})^{T}
, where
\mathcal{H}^{n},\mathcal{W}^{n},\Phi^{n},\mathcal{Z}^{n}\in\mathcal{P}^{r}_{h}
are the fully discrete approximations of
\tilde{\eta}(\cdot,t_{n})
,
\tilde{w}(\cdot,t_{n})
,
\tilde{\phi}(\cdot,t_{n})
and
\tilde{z}(\cdot,t_{n})
, respectively. Then, the updates for
\mathcal{H}^{n}
and
\Phi^{n}
of (
44
) can be expressed in the form

(45)

\begin{aligned} \mathcal{H}^{n+1}&=\mathcal{H}^{n}+\gamma^{n}\Delta td_{\eta}^{n},\\
\Phi^{n+1}&=\Phi^{n}+\gamma^{n}\Delta td_{\phi}^{n},\end{aligned}\qquad\text{for $n=0,1,2,\dots$}\ ,

where
d_{\eta}^{n}
and
d_{\phi}^{n}
are the components of the vector
\mathbf{d}^{n}
corresponding to the solution
\mathcal{H}^{n}
and
\Phi^{n}
, respectively. To find the relaxation parameter such as the energy between times
t_{n}
and
t_{n+1}
remain constant, we need to solve the equation

(46)

\mathcal{E}(t_{n+1};\mathcal{H}^{n+1},\Phi^{n+1})=\mathcal{E}(t_{n},\mathcal{H}^{n},\Phi^{n})\ .

Substituting the updates (
45
) into equation (
46
), yields the cubic equation

(47)

\mathrm{A}x^{3}+\mathrm{B}x^{2}+\Gamma x=0\ ,

where
x=\gamma^{n}\Delta t
and

(48)

\displaystyle\mathrm{A}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}d_{\eta}|\nabla d_{\phi}|^{2}~d\mathbf{x}\ ,

\displaystyle\mathrm{B}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}gd_{\eta}^{2}+cgD^{2}|\nabla d_{\eta}|^{2}+(D+\mathcal{H}^{n})|\nabla d_{\phi}|^{2}+2d_{\eta}\nabla\Phi^{n}\cdot\nabla d_{\phi}~d\mathbf{x}\ ,

\displaystyle\Gamma

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}(2g\mathcal{H}^{n}+|\nabla\Phi^{n}|^{2})d_{\eta}+2cgD^{2}\nabla\mathcal{H}^{n}\cdot\nabla d_{\eta}+2(D+\mathcal{H}^{n})\nabla\Phi^{n}\cdot\nabla d_{\phi}~d\mathbf{x}\ .

Therefore, there are three roots, namely
\gamma^{n}_{0}=0
and

(49)

\gamma^{n}_{\pm}\cdot\Delta t=\frac{-\mathrm{B}\pm\sqrt{\mathrm{B}^{2}-4\mathrm{A}\Gamma}}{2\mathrm{A}}\ .

The parameter
\gamma^{n}
depends on the current solution of the problem making the determination of its consistency challenging. For this reason, we cannot study the roots
\gamma^{n}
further. However, whenever the explicit Runge-Kutta method converges, let’s say at order
\Delta t^{p}
, it is expected that there is a root
\gamma^{n}\approx 1
such that
|\gamma^{n}-1|=O(\Delta t^{p-1})

[
54
]
. In practice, after choosing
\Delta t\ll 1
and excluding the solution
\gamma^{n}_{0}
, we solve numerically the resulting quadratic equation (
47
) using, for instance, the secant method to avoid catastrophic cancellation phenomena.

For the numerical experiments of this work, we will use the relaxation Runge-Kutta method
related to the classical four-stage, fourth-order Runge-Kutta method given by
the Butcher tableau:

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{c|c}c&A\\
\hline\cr&b^{\mathrm{T}}\end{array}~=~\begin{array}[]{c|cccc}0&0&0&0&0\\
1/2&1/2&0&0&0\\
1/2&0&1/2&0&0\\
1&0&0&1&0\\
\hline\cr&1/6&1/3&1/3&1/6\end{array}.

Note that this numerical method has been successfully applied to the one-dimensional BBM-BBM system, as demonstrated in
[
66
,
56
,
74
]
, in conjunction with conservative spatial semidiscretizations. However, this is the first time we are applying the method to two-dimensional Boussinesq systems.

3.3.
Computation of initial velocity potential

Our objective is to generate approximations to solutions of system (
3
) with given initial conditions
\eta_{0}(\mathbf{x})
and
\mathbf{u}_{0}(\mathbf{x})
in
\Omega
using the solutions of (
12
). However, system (
12
) requires an initial velocity potential
\phi_{0}
. Determining the initial velocity potential
\phi_{0}
from the initial velocity field
\mathbf{u}_{0}
presents a challenge. Initially, we establish an initial velocity potential profile
\phi(\mathbf{x},0)=\phi_{0}(\mathbf{x})
by solving the Poisson equation

\Delta\varphi_{0}=\nabla\!\cdot\!\mathbf{u}_{0}\quad\text{in $\Omega$}\ ,

with boundary condition
\nabla\varphi_{0}\cdot\mathbf{n}=0
on
\partial\Omega
. We use the letter
\varphi_{0}
instead of
\phi_{0}
for the solution of the Laplace equation because this problem has a unique solution subject to vertical translations. To demonstrate this, let’s consider two solutions
\varphi_{0}^{1}
and
\varphi_{0}^{2}
and define
\psi=\varphi_{0}^{1}-\varphi_{0}^{2}
. This function satisfies the homogeneous Laplace equation
\Delta\psi=0
in
\Omega
with boundary condition
\nabla\psi\cdot\mathbf{n}=0
on
\partial\Omega
. Multiplying the Laplace equation by
\psi
and integrating over
\Omega
, we obtain

\int_{\Omega}|\nabla\psi|^{2}~d\mathbf{x}=0\ .

Hence,
\nabla\psi=0
, which is equivalent to
\varphi_{0}^{1}=\varphi_{0}^{2}+C
for any constant
C
. Nevertheless, this doesn’t impose a restriction, as the solutions to the problem (
3
)–(
6
) are unaffected by vertical translations of the velocity potential of (
12
) since
\mathbf{u}=\nabla\phi
.

We proceed to solve for the initial potential
\varphi_{0}
numerically using the standard Galerkin method with the triangular grid
\mathcal{T}_{h}
defined in Section
3.1
for the solution of (
12
). We obtain an approximation
\tilde{\varphi}_{0}\in\mathcal{P}^{r}_{h}
. Conventionally, we select the initial velocity potential function as
\tilde{\phi}_{0}=\tilde{\varphi}_{0}-\min_{\Omega}\tilde{\varphi}_{0}
.

3.4.
Generation of solitary waves using the Petviashvili method

To generate numerical solitary waves that travel with constant speed
c_{s}
, we employ the Petviashvili method, which is a modified fixed-point iteration applied to the time-independent equations (
28
). Specifically, to apply the Petviashvili method, we write equations (
28
) in the form

(57)

\mathcal{L}\mathbf{w}=\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{w})\ ,

where
\mathbf{w}=(\eta,w,\tilde{w})
and

(58)

\mathcal{L}=\begin{pmatrix}c_{s}-bc_{s}D_{0}^{2}\Delta_{\xi\zeta}&-D_{0}&0\\
-g+cgD_{0}^{2}\Delta_{\xi\zeta}&c_{s}-bc_{s}D_{0}^{2}\partial_{\xi}^{2}&0\\
0&0&1\end{pmatrix}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{w})=\begin{pmatrix}\eta w\\
\tfrac{1}{2}w^{2}\\
0\end{pmatrix}\ .

The Galerkin finite element method for solving system (
57
)–(
58
) is the following: seek for approximation
\mathbf{w}_{h}=(\eta_{h},w_{h},\tilde{w}_{h})^{T}\in(\mathcal{P}_{h}^{r})^{3}
such that

(59)

\mathcal{L}_{h}(\mathbf{w}_{h},\boldsymbol{\chi})=(\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{w}_{h}),\boldsymbol{\chi}),\quad\text{for all}\quad\boldsymbol{\chi}\in(\mathcal{P}_{h}^{r})^{3}\ ,

where
\mathcal{L}_{h}
is the bilinear form

(60)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{h}(\mathbf{w},\boldsymbol{\chi})=

\displaystyle c_{s}(\eta,\phi)+bc_{s}D_{0}^{2}(\nabla_{\xi\zeta}\eta,\nabla_{\xi\zeta}\phi)-D_{0}(w,\phi)

\displaystyle+c_{s}(w,\chi)+bc_{s}D_{0}^{2}(w_{\xi},\chi_{\xi})-g(\eta,\chi)-cgD_{0}^{2}(\nabla_{\xi\zeta}\eta,\nabla_{\xi\zeta}\chi)+(\tilde{w},\psi)\ ,

for all
\mathbf{w}=(\eta,w,\tilde{w})^{T}\in(\mathcal{P}_{h}^{r})^{3}
and
\boldsymbol{\chi}=(\phi,\chi,\psi)^{T}\in(\mathcal{P}_{h}^{r})^{3}
. Given an initial guess
\mathbf{w}_{h}^{0}
for the solitary wave solution, the Petviashvili method for solving the nonlinear system of equations (
59
)–(
60
) is the fixed-point iteration

(61)

\mathcal{L}_{h}(\mathbf{w}_{h}^{(n+1)},\boldsymbol{\chi})=m_{n}^{p}(\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{w}_{h}^{n}),\boldsymbol{\chi}),\quad n=0,1,\dots\ ,

for all
\boldsymbol{\chi}\in(\mathcal{P}_{h}^{r})^{3}
,
p
appropriate constant, and
m_{n}
the scalar

m_{n}=\frac{\mathcal{L}_{h}(\mathbf{w}_{h}^{n},\mathbf{w}_{h}^{n})}{(\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{w}_{h}^{n}),\mathbf{w}_{h}^{n})}\ .

The initial guess
\mathbf{w}_{h}^{0}
can be taken to be the
L^{2}
-projection of the corresponding solitary wave of the Serre equations
[
65
]
for the same speed
c_{s}
.

Typically, the Petviashvili iteration will be terminated when the normalized residual is less than a prescribed tolerance
\delta
. Specifically, we will consider

|\mathcal{L}_{h}(\mathbf{w}_{h}^{n},\mathbf{w}_{h}^{n})-(\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{w}_{h}^{n}),\mathbf{w}_{h}^{n})|/\|\mathbf{w}_{h}^{n}\|_{2}<\delta\ ,

where
\delta
is the desired tolerance. In the experiments of this paper, we took
\delta=10^{-6}
, and the Petviashvili iteration with
p=2
required less than 10 iterations to converge within the prescribed tolerance.

Experimental validation

In this section, we present several numerical experiments to provide experimental justification for both the mathematical model and the numerical method. For the numerical implementation of the fully-discrete scheme, we employed the Python library Fenics

59
]

38
). Unless otherwise specified, all experiments in this study employ the Bona-Smith system with
\theta^{2}=1
. It is important to note that similar results can be obtained for any value of
\theta
in the range
[2/3,1]
. Similar results for
\theta^{2}=2/3
with non-conservative numerical method have been presented in
[
51
,
49
]
.

4.6.
Solitary wave scattering at a Y-junction

Solitary wave scattering at a junction involves the interaction of solitary waves with the geometric features of the channel, specifically at points where the channel splits or merges. In general, as a solitary wave approaches a junction, the change in channel geometry can lead to a variety of behaviors including reflection, transmission, and splitting of the wave. When the wave reaches the junction, part of the wave may transmit into the new channels while part may reflect backward. The exact behavior can depend on the wave’s amplitude, speed, and the channel’s properties,
[
67
]
.

Here, we consider a channel with a horizontal bottom that splits into two symmetric channels. The Y-junction forms an angle

This setup presents considerable challenges, as solutions to other models such as the non-dispersive shallow water equations tend to form singularities (discontinuities) at the corners of the junction. However, in our case, due to the regularization properties of the Bona-Smith system, the numerical solution remains stable in such scenarios and no spurious oscillations result using continuous finite element methods.

Conclusions

In this work, our focus was on the Bona-Smith family of Boussinesq systems with variable bottom topography in bounded domains with slip-wall boundary conditions. We established the existence and uniqueness of smooth solutions to the corresponding initial-boundary value problem. This family of systems is specifically designed to preserve three fundamental for long waves invariants: mass, vorticity, and energy. Additionally, we developed a novel numerical method that maintains these three invariants. Spatial discretization is achieved through a modified Galerkin/finite element method, applied to both the equation of free-surface and the velocity potential, and implemented using a mixed formulation. Temporal discretization relies on explicit, relaxation Runge-Kutta methods, which are tailored to preserve the energy of the system. Mass conservation is intrinsic, due to its first-order functional relation to the free-surface elevation. The conservation of vorticity is ensured through the formulation of the problem using the velocity potential.

To assess the applicability of both the mathematical and numerical models, we conducted a series of numerical experiments. These experiments indicate that the numerical solutions converge with an optimal rate in space to both the surface elevation and the velocity potential. Furthermore, we validated the numerical solutions against laboratory data in three classical scenarios: the reflection of shoaling solitary waves by a vertical wall, the interaction of a solitary wave with a vertical cylinder and the interaction of a periodic wave-train with a submerged bar. In all cases, the agreement between the numerical and experimental data was satisfactory. Finally, we studied the scattering of a solitary wave by a Y-junction. This problem is especially challenging because it involves re-entrant corners. Overall, the stability and the accuracy of the new numerical model indicate excellent performance in simulations involving long waves of small amplitude even in domains with complicated geometric and bottom features.
