Modeling of cardiac fibers as oriented liquid crystals
Nicolás A Barnafi, Axel Osses

Abstract
In this work we propose a mathematical model that describes the orientation of ventricular cardiac fibers. These fibers are commonly computed as the normalized gradient of certain harmonic potentials, so our work consisted in finding the equations that such a vector field satisfies, considering the unitary norm constraint. The resulting equations belong to the Frank-Oseen theory of nematic liquid crystals, which yield a bulk of mathematical properties to the cardiac fibers, such as the characterization of singularities. The numerical methods available in literature are computationally expensive and not sufficiently robust for the complex geometries obtained from the human heart, so we also propose a preconditioned projected gradient descent scheme that circumvents these difficulties in the tested scenarios. The resulting model further confirms recent experimental observations of liquid crystal behavior of soft tissue, and provides an accurate mathematical description of such behavior.



1
Introduction

The heart is the blood pump of the body, and it works throughout our entire lifespan. Surgical procedures involving the heart are very invasive because it resides within the rib cage, so it is fundamental to find alternative ways to diagnose, plan and treat it. The progress in computational resources has enabled the use of increasingly complex mathematical models to describe a patient-specific heart (see
[
AShortHistoryNieder2019
]
for a review), which encompasses complex mechanisms at many different scales: ion dynamics at cell membranes, electric potential propagation within the myocardium (cardiac muscle tissue), muscle contraction and then blood circulation thanks to the synchronized action of all of these elements
[
tortora2018principles
]
. One fundamental physiological property of the heart is that the myocardium is anisotropic
[
EffectOfTissuRobert1982
]
, with the anisotropy given by an ordered set of fibers that impact many of the heart’s mechanisms, such as deformation and electric conductivity. An adequate description of cardiac fibers is thus at the core of any mathematical model of the heart.

The fibers were initially computed by means of an imaging technique known as Diffusion Tensor Magnetic Resonance Imaging (DT-MRI), which is very expensive and difficult for
in-vivo
patients
[
HeartMuscleFiZhukovNone
]
. To alleviate these costs, mathematical models known as Rule Based Models (RBM) were proposed
[
FiberOrientatiStreet1969
]
. They are in good agreement with measurements of fiber orientation in the ventricles
[
ANovelRuleBaBayer2012
]
, whereas atrial RBMs are still an active area of research
[
ModelingAtrialKruege2011
,
ModelingCardiaPiersa2021
,
AnAutomatePipZheng2021
]
. This has motivated other areas of research for atrial fiber generation, mainly through data-assimilation
[
ATechniqueForRoney2019
,
PhysicsInformeRuizH2022
]
.

RBMs consist in the manipulation of a series of harmonic potentials with different boundary conditions, i.e. solutions of the Laplace equation, whose gradients are normalized and then combined with histologically observed rotations to obtain an orthonormal basis oriented along the fibers. Despite the differences of the existing approaches
[
ThermodynamicalRossi2014
,
GeneratingFibrWong2014
,
ANovelRuleBaBayer2012
,
ARuleBasedMeDoste2019
]
, they follow similar steps as shown in
[
ModelingCardiaPiersa2021
]
:

1.

Provide adequate labels for the mesh geometry: endocardium (inner wall), epicardium (external wall), base (top cut), and the apex (bottom point).

2.

Compute a distance from the endocardium to the epicardium, known as transmural distance. Its gradient yields the transmural direction.

3.

Compute a direction going form the apex to the base, orthogonal to the transmural direction, known as the apicobasal direction.

4.

Define a local coordinate system by complementing the transmural and apicobasal directions with a longitudinal (or transversal) direction.

5.

Rotate the computed reference frame to match histological observations and finally yield a fiber direction
\mathbfit{f}
, a cross-fiber direction
\mathbfit{n}
and a sheet direction
\mathbfit{s}
.

Even though these steps are clear and well-defined, there are some limitations hidden within them that are intrinsic to all RBM formulations. These are:

•

The fibers are computed as the gradients of harmonic functions. Numerically, this means that the resulting fiber field could be discontinuous or inaccurate. This is most evident in thin muscle walls such as the right ventricle and the atria.

•

There is no known system of equations that the fibers satisfy. This makes it difficult to mathematically analyse the qualitative behavior of fiber orientation.

•

The concept of unit-vector interpolation is fundamental. In practice, two different rotations of the longitudinal direction are required on the endocardium and epicardium, so that different boundary rotations, considered correct, are then interpolated within the tissue using a weight such as the transmural distance. The situation is more difficult with the biventricle scenario, because a choice has to be made regarding the combination of the fiber fields in the intra-ventricular septum (IVS). This problem was circumvented in
[
ANovelRuleBaBayer2012
]
by interpreting the orientation as a quaternion and using well-established techniques from computer vision for unit-quaternion interpolation
[
AnimatingRotatShoema1985
]
to obtain an inter-ventricular interpolation.

The third point is the one that has received most attention, whereas the first two are largely unaddressed. Our goal is to tackle these three problems together, which we do by computing a Partial Differential Equation (PDE) satisfied by the fibers. The resulting model is a particular case of a nematic liquid crystal described by the Frank-Oseen theory (see
[
LiquidCrystalsBall2017
]
for further references), given by the minimization of the
H_{0}^{1}
norm of the vector field, subject to having unitary norm throughout the domain. This is also known in the literature as harmonic maps. The connection of liquid crystals with living tissue, even though we derived it from a purely mathematical approach, has already been observed in literature
[
LiquidCrystalsHirst2017
]
, and only very recently for cardiac fibers
[
TheNematicChiAuriau2022
]
. This grants a significant validation not only of our model, but also of the RBMs in general. Beyond Frank-Oseen, there is the Ericksen model that allows for better approximation of singularities by means of an additional variable, and while both the Frank-Oseen and Ericksen theories consider only uniaxial liquid crystals, more general scenarios are better described within the Landau-De Gennes framework. See
[
LandauDeGenneMajumd2010
,
LiquidCrystalsBall2017
]
for further details on these topics.

The existence of solutions to the Frank-Oseen equations and their regularity is well-known for most cases
[
NonlinearTheorLinF1989
]
. Additionally, work has been devoted to the numerical approximation of these equations, mainly through the study of the saddle point problem arising from the first order conditions
[
ASaddlePointHuQi2009
,
ConstrainedOptAdler2016
,
BlockPreconditBeik2018
,
AugmentedLagraXiaJ2021
]
. In liquid crystal theory, the geometries considered so far are simple. This means that the existing numerical solvers have not been tested for robustness in complex geometries. In fact, we will show that they fail under such conditions, so we propose a preconditioned projected gradient descent scheme that is robust and optimal in all our tests, even in the presence of singularities.

This work is structured as follows: In Section
2
we derive our proposed PDE for an abstract vector field and highlight its main properties for our application. After motivating the use of the Frank-Oseen model for cardiac fibers, we thoroughly characterize all vector fields used in RBMs as nematic liquid crystals. We conclude this section by showing that many important physical properties can be shown analytically, such as the vector interpolation property, and the arisal of the apex singularity. In Section
we show how a non-standard Dirichlet condition from our model is implemented in practice, and then present our preconditioned projected gradient descent strategy to solve the proposed model. We show numerically that it is optimal and more robust than what has been proposed in the literature. In Section
we numerically study our approach by doing the following tests: (i) a comparison of the use of our approach as a replacement of a potential based fiber field, (ii) a numerical computation of the possible deviation of the fiber field from being a nematic liquid crystal, (iii) a convergence study where we verify the validity of the Aubin-Nitsche trick for our model, (iv) a test where we compare our approach with the standard one in a slender wall scenario, and (v) a simple contraction test where we study the impact of using our model in a mechanical simulation. In Section
we review the relevant theoretical aspects of topological defects in liquid crystals and how they are be applied to the cardiac fiber context. Finally, in Section
we conclude our work and discuss possible future directions.

2
The fiber model

In this section we derive the proposed model by looking at each of the potentials computed in an RBM model and their corresponding vector field. We conclude this section by showing some analytical properties of the model, which are fundamental for its use as an RBM alternative. We will focus only on a left ventricle geometry, as shown in Figure
1
. This geometry
\Omega
has its boundary
\Gamma\coloneqq\partial\Omega
divided into the endocardium
\Gamma_{\texttt{endo}}
, the epicardium
\Gamma_{\texttt{epi}}
and the base
\Gamma_{\texttt{base}}
such that
\overline{\Gamma}=\overline{\Gamma_{\texttt{endo}}}\cup\overline{\Gamma_{\texttt{epi}}}\cup\overline{\Gamma_{\texttt{base}}}
. We have not considered in this partition the apex point
\Gamma_{\texttt{apex}}
, defined as the bottom point of the left ventricle, which is fundamental to induce the singularity present on it.

2.1
The transmural and apicobasal vectors

The main ingredients for computing the fiber vector field are the transmural and apicobasal vectors, both computed as the normalized gradient of harmonic potentials. We represent both problems by the following abstract Poisson problem: Find
\phi
in
H^{1}(\Omega)
such that

\displaystyle-\Delta\phi

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\text{in}\,\Omega,

(1)

\displaystyle\gamma_{D}\phi

\displaystyle=g_{D}

\displaystyle\text{on}\,\Gamma_{D},

\displaystyle\gamma_{N}\phi

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\text{on}\,\Gamma_{N},

where
g_{D}
is constant in each connected component of
\Gamma_{D}
. The steps to compute the transmural and apicobasal vectors are two, given by

Step 1: Compute the potential
\phi
by solving (
1
) using adequate boundary conditions. For the transmural potential
\phi_{\texttt{trans}}
, this means taking the values 1 on the epicardium and 0 on the endocardium. The apicobasal potential
\phi_{\texttt{ab}}
instead is 0 on the apex and 1 on the base. All remaining boundary conditions are homogeneous Neumann.

Step 2: Compute the normalized gradient as
\mathbfit{d}^{\phi}\coloneqq\Pi_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}(\grad\phi)
, where
\Pi_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}
stands for the projection into
\mathbb{S}^{2}\coloneqq\{\mathbfit{y}:|\mathbfit{y}|=1\,\text{ a.e. in $\Omega$}\}
.

Remark 1
.

A very important aspect of step 2 is that the projection is not defined for
\mathbfit{y}=\mathbf{0}
. As we will discuss further ahead, one possible solution is to consider a smoothed projection such that
\Pi_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}\mathbf{0}=\mathbf{0}
. The simplest solution in practice is to approximate such projection with

\Pi_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}^{\epsilon}\coloneqq\frac{\mathbfit{y}}{\epsilon+|\mathbfit{y}|},

where
\epsilon
is a small number.

We highlight that these steps do not have a clear mathematical objective beyond the well-established practical application. To see this, we first note that (
1
) is equivalent to solving the minimization problem

\min_{\varphi\in H_{\Gamma_{D}}^{1}(\Omega)}\Psi(\grad\varphi)\coloneqq\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}|\grad\varphi|^{2}\,dx.

In step 2 the gradient is normalized, so that the energy becomes constant:

\Psi\left(\frac{\grad\varphi}{|\grad\varphi|}\right)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}\left|\frac{1}{|\grad\varphi|}\grad\varphi\right|^{2}\,dx=\frac{|\Omega|}{2}.

Still, from (
1
) we can take the gradient of the PDE, and using the vector calculus identities
\grad\dive=\dive\grad+\curl\curl
and
\curl\grad=0
we obtain that each component of
\grad\phi
is also harmonic:

\dive\grad(\grad\phi)=\grad\dive(\grad\phi)-\curl\curl\grad\phi=0.

This means that
\grad\phi
is the minimizer of the following minimization problem:

\min_{\mathbfit{y}\in\mathbfit{H}_{\Gamma^{\mathbfit{d}}}^{1}(\Omega)}\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}|\grad\mathbfit{y}|^{2}\,dx,

where
\Gamma^{\mathbfit{d}}
represents a boundary condition yet to be defined, which we characterize in Section
2.1.1
. Motivated by the previous computations, we can rewrite the previous steps in terms of
\mathbfit{d}^{\phi}
as:

Step 1’: Compute the vector field
\mathbfit{d}
that solves the following minimization problem:

\min_{\mathbfit{y}\in\mathbfit{H}_{\Gamma^{\mathbfit{d}}}^{1}(\Omega)}\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}|\grad\mathbfit{y}|^{2}\,dx.

Step 2’: Compute the normalized vector field as
\mathbfit{d}^{\phi}\coloneqq\Pi^{\epsilon}_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}(\mathbfit{d})
.

In this formulation, steps 1’ and 2’ are no longer incompatible. Instead, they can be seen as the first iteration of a projected gradient descent for the following problem:

\min_{\mathbfit{d}\in\mathbfit{H}_{\Gamma^{\mathbfit{d}}}^{1}(\Omega)\cap\mathbb{S}^{2}}\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}|\grad\mathbfit{d}|^{2}\,dx.

(2)

This can be regarded as
the
minimization principle associated to the transmural and apicobasal vector fields, which we have derived simply by completing the already established procedure for computing them. The Euler-Lagrange equations associated to problem (
2
) are given by finding a vector field
\mathbfit{d}
in
\mathbfit{H}_{\Gamma^{\mathbfit{d}}}^{1}(\Omega)
and a Lagrange multiplier
\lambda
in
L^{2}(\Omega)
such that

\displaystyle-\Delta\mathbfit{d}+2\lambda\mathbfit{d}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{0}

\displaystyle\text{in}\,\Omega,

(3)

\displaystyle\mathbfit{d}\cdot\mathbfit{d}

\displaystyle=1

\displaystyle\text{in}\,\Omega.

We note that the multiplier satisfies
\lambda=-\frac{1}{2}|\nabla\mathbfit{d}|^{2}

[
LiquidCrystalsBall2017
]
, which can be inferred from the balance equations. Interestingly, this problem coincides with the Frank-Oseen equations for nematic liquid crystals under the
full anchoring
and
one-constant
hypotheses
[
LiquidCrystalsBall2017
]
. For the forthcoming analysis, we will make the following definition:

Definition 1
.

We say that a vector field
\mathbfit{d}
behaves as a nematic liquid crystal (NLC), or simply is a NLC, if it is a minimizer of (
2
) or if it is a solution of system (
3
) with
\lambda=-\frac{1}{2}|\grad\mathbfit{d}|^{2}
. This definition is independent of the boundary conditions.

Naturally, the previous definition yields the following lemma:

Lemma 1
.

The transmural vector
\mathbfit{d}_{\texttt{trans}}\coloneqq\grad\phi_{\texttt{trans}}
and the apicobasal vector
\mathbfit{d}_{\texttt{ab}}\coloneqq\grad\phi_{\texttt{ab}}
behave as nematic liquid crystals.

In addition, we define a loaded Frank-Oseen model given by

\displaystyle-\Delta\mathbfit{d}+2\lambda\mathbfit{d}

\displaystyle=\mathbfit{g}

\displaystyle\text{in}\,\Omega,

(4)

\displaystyle\mathbfit{d}\cdot\mathbfit{d}

\displaystyle=1

\displaystyle\text{in}\,\Omega.

Definition 2
.

We say that a vector field
\mathbfit{d}
behaves as a loaded nematic liquid crystal (LNLC), or simply is a LNLC, if it is a solution of system (
4
), where
\lambda=\frac{1}{2}\left(\mathbfit{g}\cdot\mathbfit{d}-|\grad\mathbfit{d}|^{2}\right)
. This definition is independent of the boundary conditions.

Remark 2
.

If
\mathbfit{d}
is a loaded nematic liquid crystal, then it is a minimizer of the following problem:

\min_{\mathbfit{y}\in\mathbfit{H}^{1}_{\Gamma^{\mathbfit{d}}(\Omega)\cap\mathbb{S}^{2}}}\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}|\grad\mathbfit{y}|^{2}\,dx-\int_{\Omega}\mathbfit{g}\cdot\mathbfit{y}\,dx,

and replacing the loaded Lagrange multiplier gives the following equation:

-\Delta\mathbfit{d}-|\grad\mathbfit{d}|^{2}\mathbfit{d}=(\mathsfbfit I-\mathbfit{d}\otimes\mathbfit{d})\mathbfit{g}.

It holds in particular that if
\mathbfit{g}\cdot\mathbfit{d}=0
, then
\lambda=-\frac{1}{2}|\grad\mathbfit{d}|^{2}
as in the unloaded case.

We conclude the presentation of the model by highlighting one additional advantage of considering the gradient of the potential as the primary variable. Typical heart geometries have slender walls in the atria and in the right ventricle, in many cases exhibiting a width of up to one mesh element (tetrahedron or hexahedron). In such scenarios, the gradient of a first order potential is given by a constant in the element, meaning that is will be impossible to depict any rotation through the tissue. Even if the wall presents two elements, such a coarse description of the geometry can lead to a severe lack of accuracy from the point of view of the discretization. In our formulation, if one element is used to describe the tissue walls, we can impose the boundary conditions exactly, so this problem will not be present.

2.1.1
Transforming the boundary conditions

In this section we fully characterize the boundary conditions and the boundary
\Gamma^{\mathbfit{d}}
. For this, we consider separately how to transform the Dirichlet and Neumann boundary conditions from (
1
).

Neumann boundary conditions.

Neumann boundary conditions are given by

\grad\phi\cdot\mathbfit{N}=\mathbfit{d}^{\phi}\cdot\mathbfit{N}=0,

meaning that Neumann boundary conditions of the potential are translated into normal Dirichlet boundary conditions in the vector field. We write this condition, and the corresponding boundary as

\mathbfit{d}^{\phi}\cdot\mathbfit{N}=0,\hskip 11.49994pt\text{on $\Gamma^{\mathbfit{d}}_{D,\mathbfit{N}}$}.

This boundary condition must be complemented with the behavior in the tangential direction. For this, we define the tangential projection as
\Pi_{\mathbfit{\tau}}\coloneqq\mathsfbfit I-\mathbfit{N}\otimes\mathbfit{N}
, which yields the boundary condition

\Pi_{\mathbfit{\tau}}[\grad\mathbfit{d}]\mathbfit{N}=\mathbf{0}\quad\text{on $\Gamma^{\mathbfit{d}}_{D,\mathbfit{N}}$}.

Remark 3
.

One might wonder whether having a homogeneous tangential derivative is the correct choice. Indeed, we have chosen it only for simplicity.

Dirichlet boundary conditions.

In this case, we can derivate the boundary conditions to obtain a tangential representation of the gradient. To avoid introducing notation from differential geometry, we only look at one connected component of the Dirichlet boundary condition given by
\phi=g_{D}
on
\Gamma_{D}
, and define the tangential vectors as
\mathbfit{\tau}_{1}
and
\mathbfit{\tau}_{2}
. With them, we can formally compute the tangential derivative of the Dirichlet boundary condition as

\grad_{\mathbfit{\tau}}\phi=\sum_{i}(\grad\phi\cdot\mathbfit{\tau}_{i})\mathbfit{\tau}_{i}=\sum_{i}(\grad g_{D}\cdot\mathbfit{\tau}_{i})\mathbfit{\tau}_{i}=\mathbf{0},

where the last equality is true because
g_{D}
is constant. Because of this, we conclude that

\grad_{\tau}\phi=\sum_{i}(\mathbfit{d}\cdot\mathbfit{\tau}_{i})\mathbfit{\tau}_{i}=(\mathsfbfit I-\mathbfit{N}\otimes\mathbfit{N})\mathbfit{d}=0,

which combined with the unitary norm constraint yields the following definition:

\mathbfit{d}=\mathbfit{N},\hskip 11.49994pt\text{on $\Gamma^{\mathbfit{d}}_{D,\mathbfit{\tau}}$}.

The apex boundary condition.

The apicobasal function is fundamental for obtaining the apex singularity, which is done by imposing the boundary conditions
\phi=0
on
\Gamma_{\texttt{apex}}
and
\phi=1
on
\Gamma_{\texttt{base}}
. The boundary condition on the apex is not theoretically sound, as it is imposed on a single point, but other other automated approaches have proven unsuccessful in patient-specific geometries. We thus transform this condition into another singular condition for the vector problem. Before normalization, we can restrict our analysis to a small ball around the apex
\mathbfit{x}_{0}
, where the solution will be given by
\mathbfit{d}(\mathbfit{x})=\mathbfit{x}-\mathbfit{x}_{0}
.
To normalize this function, we consider a smoothed projector given by

\Pi_{\epsilon}(\mathbfit{d})(\mathbfit{x})=\begin{cases}\mathbfit{d}(\mathbfit{x})/|\mathbfit{d}(\mathbfit{x})|&\mathbfit{x}\in[B(\mathbfit{x}_{0},\epsilon)]^{c}\\
\alpha\mathbfit{d}(\mathbfit{x})&\mathbfit{x}\in B(\mathbfit{x}_{0},\epsilon)\end{cases},

with
\alpha
such that the function is continuous and
\epsilon
sufficiently small. This projector can be well-defined in the discrete setting as well, it suffices to consider
\epsilon
to be smaller than the smallest element edge on the mesh. Considering
\epsilon\to 0
gives that the limit projector is given by

\Pi_{\infty}(\mathbfit{d})(\mathbfit{x})=\begin{cases}\mathbfit{d}(\mathbfit{x})/|\mathbfit{d}(\mathbfit{x})|&\mathbfit{x}\neq\mathbfit{x}_{0}\\
\mathbf{0}&\mathbfit{x}=\mathbfit{x}_{0}\end{cases}.

This suggests that the boundary condition on apex for the vector problem should be
\mathbfit{d}=\mathbf{0}
on
\Gamma_{\texttt{apex}}.

The resulting model.

The resulting minimization problem for both the transmural and apicobasal vector fields is thus defined in the following spaces:

\mathbfit{H}_{\Gamma_{D}^{\mathbfit{d}}}^{1}(\Omega)\coloneqq\{\mathbfit{y}\in\mathbfit{H}^{1}(\Omega):\,\mathbfit{y}=\mathbfit{N}\hskip 11.49994pt\text{ on }\Gamma_{D,\mathbfit{\tau}}^{\mathbfit{d}},\,\mathbfit{y}\cdot\mathbfit{N}=0\hskip 11.49994pt\text{ on }\Gamma_{D,\mathbfit{N}}^{\mathbfit{d}}\},\text{ and }

\mathbfit{H}_{\Gamma_{D}^{\mathbfit{d}},0}^{1}(\Omega)\coloneqq\{\mathbfit{y}\in\mathbfit{H}^{1}(\Omega):\,\Pi_{\mathbfit{\tau}}\mathbfit{y}=\mathbf{0}\hskip 11.49994pt\text{ on }\Gamma_{D,\mathbfit{\tau}}^{\mathbfit{d}},\,\mathbfit{y}\cdot\mathbfit{N}=0\hskip 11.49994pt\text{ on }\Gamma_{D,\mathbfit{N}}^{\mathbfit{d}}\}.

Remark 4
.

In the test function space, we have considered the condition
\Pi_{\mathbfit{\tau}}\mathbfit{y}=\mathbf{0}
instead of
\mathbfit{y}=\mathbf{0}
. This avoids technicalities where the test functions can not be embedded into
\mathbb{S}^{2}
.

The resulting minimization problem is given as follows:

\min_{\mathbfit{d}\in\mathcal{V}}\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}|\grad d|^{2}\,dx,

(5)

where
\mathcal{V}
is defined as

\mathcal{V}\coloneqq\mathbfit{H}_{\Gamma_{D}^{\mathbfit{d}}}^{1}(\Omega)\cap\mathbb{S}^{2}.

By using it, we can compute the transmural and apicobasal vectors,
\mathbfit{d}_{\texttt{trans}}
and
\mathbfit{d}_{\texttt{ab}}
, as minimizers of the Frank-Oseen problem, which completely characterizes their behavior as nematic liquid crystals. We do this numerically in Section
.

2.1.2
The transversal vector and the fiber field

The transversal vector is given by
\mathbfit{d}=\mathbfit{d}_{\texttt{trans}}\times\mathbfit{d}_{\texttt{ab}}
. The fiber field is computed by means of the transmural distance by interpolating the angles that the fibers have with respect to the transmural direction. For this aim we consider a transmurally varying angle
\alpha(\phi_{\texttt{trans}})=\phi_{\texttt{trans}}\alpha_{\texttt{endo}}+(1-\phi_{\texttt{trans}})\alpha_{\texttt{epi}}
together with a change of basis and a rotation given by

\mathbfit{B}=[\mathbfit{d}\,\,\mathbfit{d}_{\texttt{ab}}\,\,\mathbfit{d}_{\texttt{trans}}],\hskip 11.49994pt\mathbfit{R}(\phi_{\texttt{trans}})=\begin{bmatrix}\cos\alpha(\phi_{\texttt{trans}})&-\sin\alpha(\phi_{\texttt{trans}})&0\\
\sin\alpha(\phi_{\texttt{trans}})&\cos\alpha(\phi_{\texttt{trans}})&0\\
0&0&1\end{bmatrix}.

The fiber field is ultimately defined as
\mathbfit{f}(\phi_{\texttt{trans}})\coloneqq\mathbfit{Q}(\phi_{\texttt{trans}})\mathbfit{d}
, with
\mathbfit{Q}(\phi_{\texttt{trans}})=\mathbfit{B}\mathbfit{R}(\phi_{\texttt{trans}})\mathbfit{B}^{T}
. One may naturally wonder at this point if either
\mathbfit{d}
or
\mathbfit{f}
behave as nematic liquid crystals, and the answer is that they behave as loaded nematic liquid crystals. We show this in the following two lemmas.

Lemma 2
.

Consider two orthogonal nematic liquid crystals
\mathbfit{d}_{\texttt{trans}}
and
\mathbfit{d}_{\texttt{ab}}
, with Langrange multipliers from (
3
) given by
\lambda_{\texttt{trans}}
and
\lambda_{\texttt{ab}}
respectively. Then, the transversal vector field
\mathbfit{d}
, defined as the product
\mathbfit{d}=\mathbfit{d}_{\texttt{trans}}\times\mathbfit{d}_{\texttt{ab}}
, behaves as a loaded nematic liquid crystal, where the external force is given by

\mathbfit{F}=2[\mathbfit{d}_{\texttt{trans}}]_{k,j}[\mathbfit{d}_{\texttt{ab}}]_{\ell,j}\epsilon_{k\ell i}-2[\mathbfit{d}_{\texttt{trans}}]_{m,j}[\mathbfit{d}_{\texttt{trans}}]_{n,j}[\mathbfit{d}_{\texttt{ab}}]_{m}[\mathbfit{d}_{\texttt{ab}}]_{n}.

In particular,
\mathbfit{F}\cdot\mathbfit{d}=0
.

Proof.

First note that as
\mathbfit{d}_{\texttt{trans}}
and
\mathbfit{d}_{\texttt{ab}}
are orthogonal, the following holds:

We start by computing the norm

\epsilon_{mni}\epsilon_{pqi}=\delta_{mp}\delta_{nq}-\delta_{mq}\delta_{np}

\displaystyle|\grad\mathbfit{d}|^{2}

\displaystyle=d_{i,j}d_{i,j}

\displaystyle=([\mathbfit{d}_{\texttt{trans}}]_{m,j}[\mathbfit{d}_{\texttt{ab}}]_{n}+[\mathbfit{d}_{\texttt{trans}}]_{m}[\mathbfit{d}_{\texttt{ab}}]_{n,j})\epsilon_{mni}([\mathbfit{d}_{\texttt{trans}}]_{p,j}[\mathbfit{d}_{\texttt{ab}}]_{q}+[\mathbfit{d}_{\texttt{trans}}]_{p}[\mathbfit{d}_{\texttt{ab}}]_{q,j})\epsilon_{pqi}

\displaystyle=([\mathbfit{d}_{\texttt{trans}}]_{m,j}[\mathbfit{d}_{\texttt{ab}}]_{n}+[\mathbfit{d}_{\texttt{trans}}]_{m}[\mathbfit{d}_{\texttt{ab}}]_{n,j})([\mathbfit{d}_{\texttt{trans}}]_{m,j}[\mathbfit{d}_{\texttt{ab}}]_{n}+[\mathbfit{d}_{\texttt{trans}}]_{m}[\mathbfit{d}_{\texttt{ab}}]_{n,j}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad-[\mathbfit{d}_{\texttt{trans}}]_{n,j}[\mathbfit{d}_{\texttt{ab}}]_{m}+[\mathbfit{d}_{\texttt{trans}}]_{n}[\mathbfit{d}_{\texttt{ab}}]_{m,j})

\displaystyle=[\mathbfit{d}_{\texttt{trans}}]_{m,j}[\mathbfit{d}_{\texttt{ab}}]_{n}([\mathbfit{d}_{\texttt{trans}}]_{m,j}[\mathbfit{d}_{\texttt{ab}}]_{n}+[\mathbfit{d}_{\texttt{trans}}]_{m}[\mathbfit{d}_{\texttt{ab}}]_{n,j}-[\mathbfit{d}_{\texttt{trans}}]_{n,j}[\mathbfit{d}_{\texttt{ab}}]_{m}+[\mathbfit{d}_{\texttt{trans}}]_{n}[\mathbfit{d}_{\texttt{ab}}]_{m,j})

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+[\mathbfit{d}_{\texttt{trans}}]_{m}[\mathbfit{d}_{\texttt{ab}}]_{n,j}([\mathbfit{d}_{\texttt{trans}}]_{m,j}[\mathbfit{d}_{\texttt{ab}}]_{n}+[\mathbfit{d}_{\texttt{trans}}]_{m}[\mathbfit{d}_{\texttt{ab}}]_{n,j}-[\mathbfit{d}_{\texttt{trans}}]_{n,j}[\mathbfit{d}_{\texttt{ab}}]_{m}+[\mathbfit{d}_{\texttt{trans}}]_{n}[\mathbfit{d}_{\texttt{ab}}]_{m,j})

\displaystyle=[\mathbfit{d}_{\texttt{trans}}]_{m,j}[\mathbfit{d}_{\texttt{trans}}]_{m,j}-[\mathbfit{d}_{\texttt{trans}}]_{m,j}[\mathbfit{d}_{\texttt{ab}}]_{n}[\mathbfit{d}_{\texttt{trans}}]_{n,j}[\mathbfit{d}_{\texttt{ab}}]_{m}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+[\mathbfit{d}_{\texttt{ab}}]_{n,j}[\mathbfit{d}_{\texttt{ab}}]_{n,j}-[\mathbfit{d}_{\texttt{trans}}]_{m}[\mathbfit{d}_{\texttt{ab}}]_{n,j}[\mathbfit{d}_{\texttt{trans}}]_{n}[\mathbfit{d}_{\texttt{ab}}]_{m,j}

\displaystyle=|\grad[\mathbfit{d}_{\texttt{trans}}]|^{2}+|\grad[\mathbfit{d}_{\texttt{ab}}]|^{2}-2[\mathbfit{d}_{\texttt{trans}}]_{m,j}[\mathbfit{d}_{\texttt{trans}}]_{n,j}[\mathbfit{d}_{\texttt{ab}}]_{m}[\mathbfit{d}_{\texttt{ab}}]_{n},

which implies that

-\frac{1}{2}|\grad\mathbfit{d}|^{2}=\lambda_{\texttt{trans}}+\lambda_{\texttt{ab}}+2[\mathbfit{d}_{\texttt{trans}}]_{m,j}[\mathbfit{d}_{\texttt{trans}}]_{n,j}[\mathbfit{d}_{\texttt{ab}}]_{m}[\mathbfit{d}_{\texttt{ab}}]_{n}.

We then compute the Laplacian of the vector
\mathbfit{d}
:

\displaystyle(\dive\grad\mathbfit{d})_{i}

\displaystyle=(\dive\grad\left(\mathbfit{d}_{\texttt{trans}}\times\mathbfit{d}_{\texttt{ab}}\right))_{i}

\displaystyle=(\grad\left(\mathbfit{d}_{\texttt{trans}}\times\mathbfit{d}_{\texttt{ab}}\right))_{ij,j}

\displaystyle=(\mathbfit{d}_{\texttt{trans}}\times\mathbfit{d}_{\texttt{ab}})_{i,jj}

\displaystyle=([\mathbfit{d}_{\texttt{trans}}]_{k}[\mathbfit{d}_{\texttt{ab}}]_{\ell}\epsilon_{k\ell i})_{,jj}

\displaystyle=([\mathbfit{d}_{\texttt{trans}}]_{k,jj}[\mathbfit{d}_{\texttt{ab}}]_{\ell}+2[\mathbfit{d}_{\texttt{trans}}]_{k,j}[\mathbfit{d}_{\texttt{ab}}]_{\ell,j}+[\mathbfit{d}_{\texttt{trans}}]_{k}[\mathbfit{d}_{\texttt{ab}}]_{\ell,jj})\epsilon_{k\ell i}

\displaystyle=(2(\lambda_{\texttt{trans}}+\lambda_{\texttt{ab}})[\mathbfit{d}_{\texttt{trans}}]_{k}[\mathbfit{d}_{\texttt{ab}}]_{\ell}+2[\mathbfit{d}_{\texttt{trans}}]_{k,j}[\mathbfit{d}_{\texttt{ab}}]_{\ell,j})\epsilon_{k\ell i}

\displaystyle=2(\lambda_{\texttt{trans}}+\lambda_{\texttt{ab}})[\mathbfit{d}]_{i}+2[\mathbfit{d}_{\texttt{trans}}]_{k,j}[\mathbfit{d}_{\texttt{ab}}]_{\ell,j}\epsilon_{k\ell i}

\displaystyle=2\left(-\frac{1}{2}|\grad\mathbfit{d}|^{2}\right)[\mathbfit{d}]_{i}+2[\mathbfit{d}_{\texttt{trans}}]_{k,j}[\mathbfit{d}_{\texttt{ab}}]_{\ell,j}\epsilon_{k\ell i}-2[\mathbfit{d}_{\texttt{trans}}]_{m,j}[\mathbfit{d}_{\texttt{trans}}]_{n,j}[\mathbfit{d}_{\texttt{ab}}]_{m}[\mathbfit{d}_{\texttt{ab}}]_{n}d_{i},

, where we note that
\mathbfit{F}\cdot\mathbfit{d}=0
:

\displaystyle\mathbfit{F}\cdot\mathbfit{d}

\displaystyle=f_{i}d_{i}

\displaystyle=-2[\mathbfit{d}_{\texttt{trans}}]_{m,j}[\mathbfit{d}_{\texttt{trans}}]_{n,j}[\mathbfit{d}_{\texttt{ab}}]_{m}[\mathbfit{d}_{\texttt{ab}}]_{n}+2[\mathbfit{d}_{\texttt{trans}}]_{k,j}[\mathbfit{d}_{\texttt{ab}}]_{l,j}\epsilon_{kli}[\mathbfit{d}_{\texttt{trans}}]_{p}[\mathbfit{d}_{\texttt{ab}}]_{q}\epsilon_{pqi}

\displaystyle=-2[\mathbfit{d}_{\texttt{trans}}]_{m,j}[\mathbfit{d}_{\texttt{trans}}]_{n,j}[\mathbfit{d}_{\texttt{ab}}]_{m}[\mathbfit{d}_{\texttt{ab}}]_{n}+2[\mathbfit{d}_{\texttt{trans}}]_{k,j}[\mathbfit{d}_{\texttt{ab}}]_{l,j}[\mathbfit{d}_{\texttt{trans}}]_{p}[\mathbfit{d}_{\texttt{ab}}]_{q}(\delta_{kp}\delta_{lq}-\delta_{kq}\delta_{lp})

\displaystyle=-2[\mathbfit{d}_{\texttt{trans}}]_{m,j}[\mathbfit{d}_{\texttt{trans}}]_{n,j}[\mathbfit{d}_{\texttt{ab}}]_{m}[\mathbfit{d}_{\texttt{ab}}]_{n}-2[\mathbfit{d}_{\texttt{trans}}]_{k,j}[\mathbfit{d}_{\texttt{ab}}]_{l,j}[\mathbfit{d}_{\texttt{trans}}]_{l}[\mathbfit{d}_{\texttt{ab}}]_{k}

\displaystyle=-2[\mathbfit{d}_{\texttt{trans}}]_{m,j}[\mathbfit{d}_{\texttt{trans}}]_{n,j}[\mathbfit{d}_{\texttt{ab}}]_{m}[\mathbfit{d}_{\texttt{ab}}]_{n}+2[\mathbfit{d}_{\texttt{trans}}]_{k,j}[\mathbfit{d}_{\texttt{ab}}]_{l}[\mathbfit{d}_{\texttt{trans}}]_{l,j}[\mathbfit{d}_{\texttt{ab}}]_{k}

\displaystyle=0.

The proof is concluded by setting
\lambda\coloneqq-\frac{1}{2}|\grad\mathbfit{d}|^{2}
, by noting that
|\mathbfit{d}|=1
, that
\lambda=-\frac{1}{2}|\grad\mathbfit{d}|^{2}+\mathbfit{F}\cdot\mathbfit{d}
, and that the previous computations can be written as

-\Delta\mathbfit{d}+2\lambda\mathbfit{d}=\mathbfit{F}.

∎

The final step of this section is showing that
\mathbfit{f}\coloneqq\mathbfit{Q}\mathbfit{d}
, where
\mathbfit{d}
is a loaded nematic liquid crystal such that its load
\mathbfit{F}
satisfies
\mathbfit{F}\cdot\mathbfit{d}=0
. Then,
\mathbfit{f}
is also a loaded nematic liquid crystal. We establish this result in the following Lemma, for which we require a technical hypothesis.

Lemma 3
.

Consider a nematic liquid crystal
\mathbfit{d}
with Lagrange multiplier
\lambda
and an external force of
\mathbfit{F}
such that
\mathbfit{F}\cdot\mathbfit{d}=0
. Consider also a rotation tensor
\mathbfit{Q}
such that

\left([\Delta\mathbfit{Q}]^{T}\mathbfit{Q}-\mathbfit{Q}^{T}[\Delta\mathbfit{Q}]\right):\mathbfit{d}\otimes\mathbfit{d}=0.

(6)

Then, the vector field
\mathbfit{f}\coloneqq\mathbfit{Q}\mathbfit{d}
behaves as a loaded nematic liquid crystal with Lagrange multiplier
\lambda_{f}\coloneqq-\frac{1}{2}|\grad\mathbfit{f}|^{2}
and an external force given by

\mathbfit{S}_{i}=-Q_{ik,jj}d_{k}+Q_{ik}F_{k}-2Q_{ik,j}d_{k,j}-Q_{im,j}Q_{in,j}Q_{ip}d_{m}d_{n}d_{p}-2Q_{im,j}d_{m}d_{n}d_{n,j}.

In particular, it holds that
\mathbfit{S}\cdot\mathbfit{f}=-\frac{1}{2}\left([\Delta\mathbfit{Q}]^{T}\mathbfit{Q}-\mathbfit{Q}^{T}[\Delta\mathbfit{Q}]\right):\mathbfit{d}\otimes\mathbfit{d}=0
.

Proof.

We will require the following computation:

\displaystyle 0=\Delta(\mathbfit{Q}^{T}\mathbfit{Q})_{ij}=(Q_{ki}Q_{kj})_{,ll}=Q_{ki,ll}Q_{kj}+2Q_{ki,l}Q_{kj,l}+Q_{ki}Q_{kj,ll}.

Now we compute the gradient norm:

\displaystyle|\grad\mathbfit{f}|^{2}

\displaystyle=f_{i,j}f_{i,j}

\displaystyle=(Q_{im}d_{m})_{,j}(Q_{in}d_{n})_{,j}

\displaystyle=(Q_{im,j}d_{m}+Q_{im}d_{m,j})(Q_{in,j}d_{n}+Q_{in}d_{n,j})

\displaystyle=|\grad\mathbfit{d}|^{2}+Q_{im,j}Q_{in,j}d_{m}d_{n}+Q_{im,j}Q_{in}d_{m}d_{n,j}+Q_{im}Q_{in,j}d_{m,j}d_{n}

\displaystyle=|\grad\mathbfit{d}|^{2}+Q_{im,j}Q_{in,j}d_{m}d_{n}+2Q_{im,j}Q_{in}d_{m}d_{n,j}

\displaystyle=|\grad\mathbfit{d}|^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\left(Q_{im,jj}Q_{in}+Q_{im}Q_{in,jj}\right)d_{m}d_{n}+2Q_{im,j}Q_{in}d_{m}d_{n,j},

and as before, we compute the Laplacian of
\mathbfit{f}
:

\displaystyle(\Delta\mathbfit{f})_{i}=(\mathbfit{Q}\mathbfit{d})_{i,jj}

\displaystyle=Q_{ik,jj}d_{k}+Q_{ik}d_{k,jj}+2Q_{ik,j}d_{k,j}

\displaystyle=Q_{ik,jj}d_{k}+(\mathbfit{Q}[\Delta\mathbfit{d}])_{i}+2Q_{ik,j}d_{k,j}

\displaystyle=Q_{ik,jj}d_{k}+2\lambda f_{i}-(\mathbfit{Q}\mathbfit{F})_{i}+2Q_{ik,j}d_{k,j}

\displaystyle=Q_{ik,jj}d_{k}+2\left(\lambda-Q_{im,j}Q_{in,j}d_{m}d_{n}-2Q_{im,j}Q_{in}d_{m}d_{n,j}\right)f_{i}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad-(\mathbfit{Q}\mathbfit{F})_{i}+2Q_{ik,j}d_{k,j}+(Q_{im,j}Q_{in,j}d_{m}d_{n}+2Q_{im,j}Q_{in}d_{m}d_{n,j})Q_{ip}d_{p},

where we have built the multiplier
\lambda_{f}=-\frac{1}{2}|\grad f|^{2}
, and obtained the external force

-\mathbfit{S}=Q_{ik,jj}d_{k}-(\mathbfit{Q}\mathbfit{F})_{i}+2Q_{ik,j}d_{k,j}+(Q_{im,j}Q_{in,j}d_{m}d_{n}+2Q_{im,j}Q_{in}d_{m}d_{n,j})Q_{ip}d_{p}.

Its projection onto
\mathbfit{f}
is given by

\displaystyle-\mathbfit{S}\cdot\mathbfit{f}

\displaystyle=Q_{ik,jj}d_{k}Q_{ip}d_{p}-(\mathbfit{Q}\mathbfit{F})_{i}(\mathbfit{Q}\mathbfit{d})_{i}+2Q_{ik,j}d_{k,j}Q_{ip}d_{p}+Q_{im,j}Q_{in,j}d_{m}d_{n}+2Q_{im,j}Q_{in}d_{m}d_{n,j}

\displaystyle=Q_{ik,jj}d_{k}Q_{ip}d_{p}+2Q_{ik,j}d_{k,j}Q_{ip}d_{p}+Q_{im,j}Q_{in,j}d_{m}d_{n}-2Q_{im}Q_{in,j}d_{m}d_{n,j}

\displaystyle=Q_{ik,jj}d_{k}Q_{ip}d_{p}+Q_{ik,j}Q_{ip,j}d_{k}d_{p}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(Q_{ik,jj}Q_{ip}-Q_{ik}Q_{ip,jj}\right)d_{k}d_{p}.

As in the previous case, we see that the fiber field
\mathbfit{f}
is exactly a loaded nematic liquid crystal if
\mathbfit{S}\cdot\mathbfit{f}=0
, which holds if and only if

\left([\Delta\mathbfit{Q}]^{T}\mathbfit{Q}-\mathbfit{Q}^{T}[\Delta\mathbfit{Q}]\right):\mathbfit{d}\otimes\mathbfit{d}=0,

i.e. hypothesis (
6
). This concludes the proof.
∎

This justifies the practical importance of Lemma

2.2
The Frank-Oseen model as a unit vector interpolation method

The scope of this section is to show that the Frank-Oseen equations yield the same vector rotations used in quaternion interpolation, which substitutes the role of the weight function in RBMs. We provide three examples: (i) a 1D model in which we recover the standard quaternion interpolation formula–
slerp
–analytically, (ii) a 2D case in which we show the mechanism through with the Frank-Oseen theory generates the apex singularity and (iii) a numerical example with more complex spatial interactions, where we show that interpolation velocity can behave nonlinearly according to the boundary conditions.
