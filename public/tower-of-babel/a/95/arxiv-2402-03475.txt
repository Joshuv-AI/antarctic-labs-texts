Power Savings for Counting (Twisted) Abelian Extensions of Number Fields
Brandon Alberts

Abstract
. We prove significant power savings for the error term when counting abelian extensions of number fields (as well as the twisted version of these results for nontrivial Galois modules). In some cases over ℚ \mathbb{Q} , these results reveal lower order terms following the same structure as the main term that were not previously known. Assuming the generalized Lindelöf hypothesis for Hecke L L -functions, we prove square root power savings for the error compared to the order of the main term.



1.
Introduction

The primary focus in number field counting problems has been on identifying the main term of the asymptotic growth rate of number fields extensions with given properties. Given a number field
K
with absolute Galois group
G_{K}
and a transitive subgroup
G\subseteq S_{n}
, Malle
[
Mal02
,
Mal04
]
studied the growth rate of the function

\#\textnormal{Surj}(G_{K},G;X)=\#\{\pi\in\textnormal{Surj}(G_{K},G):|\textnormal{disc}(\pi)|\leq X\},

where
\textnormal{disc}(\pi)
is the discriminant of the field fixed by
\pi^{-1}(\textnormal{Stab}_{G}(1))\leq G_{K}
and
|\cdot|
denotes the norm down to
\mathbb{Q}
. By the Galois correspondence, this is equivalent (up to a constant factor) to the function counting degree
n
extensions
L/K
with Galois group
G
and bounded discriminant. Malle predicts that this function grows asymptotic to

cX^{1/a(G)}(\log X)^{b(K,G)-1}.

as
X
tends towards infinity, where
c>0
is some positive constant,
a(G)=\min_{g\neq 1}\textnormal{ind}(g)
,
\textnormal{ind}(g)=n-\#\{\text{orbits of }g\}
, and
b(K,G)
is the number of orbits of non-identity conjugacy classes of
G
under the cyclotomic action of minimal index.

Remark 1.1
.

It is known that, for certain groups,
b(K,G)
is the incorrect invariant to place in the power of the logarithm. There does exist a modified conjecture which attempts to correct for this error due to Türkelli
[
Alb21
,
Tür15
]
, but we will not detail it in this paper. For all the groups we consider in this paper, Malle’s conjecture is known to be correct with
b(K,G)-1
as the correct power of
\log X
.

In virtually every known case of Malle’s conjecture, the methods used can do a little better than the main term and will instead prove something of the form

\#\textnormal{Surj}(G_{K},G;X)=X^{1/a(G)}P(\log X)+o(X^{1/a(G)}),

where
P
is a polynomial of degree
b(K,G)
with positive leading coefficient. This quality of error term is not always mentioned explicitly, but often readily follows from the author’s methods (most often a Tauberian theorem). We summarize the current progress towards Malle’s conjecture in Subsection
1.4
. The relevant case for us is that Malle’s conjecture is proven for all abelian groups in
[
Wri89
]
.

The goal of this paper is to prove power saving error bounds for
\#\textnormal{Surj}(G_{K},G;X)
when
G
is an abelian group, of the form
O(X^{1/a(G)-\delta})
for some
\delta>0
. Currently, only the groups
S_{3}

[
TT13
]
,
S_{3}\times A
for
A
abelian
[
Wan17
]
, and
D_{4}
have explicit power saving error bounds rigorously treated in the literature. The existence of power savings for abelian groups is proven in
[
FLN18
]
, but is not made explicit. Cohen–Diaz y Diaz–Olivier remark in
[
CDyDO06
]
that standard contour integration techniques produce an explicit power saving error bound for counting
C_{4}
-extensions, although they could not find such a result in the literature. Similar reasoning can be applied to other small abelian groups like
C_{2}
and
C_{3}
, but explicit power saving error bounds for counting abelian extensions have not been treated in general.

The power savings for
S_{3}
-,
S_{3}\times A
-, and
C_{4}
-extensions over
\mathbb{Q}
also reveal a secondary term in the asymptotic growth rate. A secondary goal of this paper is to prove some results on the existence of lower order terms for counting abelian extensions.

1.1.
Power Saving Bounds for Counting Abelian Extensions

We give a rigorous treatment of the “standard contour integration technique”. We first prove a meromorphic continuation for the generating Dirichlet series:

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
K
be a number field and
G
an abelian group. The generating Dirichlet series for
G
-extensions of
K
ordered by discriminant is meromorphic on the right halfplane
{\rm Re}(s)>0
.

Any poles with real part larger than
\frac{1}{2a(G)}
occur at
s=\frac{1}{d}
for each
d\in\textnormal{ind}(G-\{1\})
of order
at most

b_{d}(K,G)
, where

b_{d}(K,G)=\#\{\text{orbits }o\text{ of }G\text{ under }\chi\text{ such that }\textnormal{ind}(o)=d\}.

Here,
\chi:G_{K}\to\hat{Z}^{\times}
is the cyclotomic character and induces a Galois action on
G
by
x.g=g^{\chi(g)}
.

Each pole is expected to contribute a term to the asymptotic growth rate. This is usually achieved by a Tauberian theorem, which is often proven by shifting a contour integral past the poles. This process leaves behind some error term, and we define a new invariant to capture the power saving bound for the error term in
\#\textnormal{Surj}(G_{K},G;X)
.

Definition 1.3
.

Let
K
be a number field,
G\subseteq S_{n}
a transitive group, and
D_{K,G}(s)
the generating series of
G
-extensions of
K
. Define
\theta(K,G)
to be the infimum of all real numbers
\theta
for which

(1)

D_{K,G}(s)
has a meromorphic continuation to
{\rm Re}(s)>\theta
with at most finitely many poles
s_{1},s_{2},...,s_{r}
, and

(2)

The number of
G
-extensions of
K
satisfies the bound

\#\textnormal{Surj}(G_{K},G;X)-\sum_{j=1}^{r}\underset{s=s_{j}}{\rm Res}\left(D_{K,G}(s)\frac{X^{s}}{s}\right)=O(X^{\theta}),

where the implied constant may depend on
K
and
G\subseteq S_{n}
.

If
s_{1},s_{2},...,s_{r}
are ordered so that
{\rm Re}(s_{1})\geq{\rm Re}(s_{2})\geq\cdots\geq{\rm Re}(s_{r})
with decreasing order of the poles with identical real parts, we call any term
\underset{s=s_{j}}{\rm Res}\left(D_{K,G}(s)\frac{X^{s}}{s}\right)
a
lower order term
if
{\rm Re}(s_{j})<{\rm Re}(s_{1})
or
{\rm Re}(s_{j})={\rm Re}(s_{1})
with
s_{j}
having a lower order than
s_{1}
.

A residue calculation implies that

\underset{s=s_{j}}{\rm Res}\left(D_{K,G}(s)\frac{X^{s}}{s}\right)=X^{s_{j}}P_{K,G,s_{j}}(\log X),

where
P_{K,G,s_{j}}
is a polynomial of degree one less than the order of the pole at
s_{j}
. When
G
is abelian, Theorem
1.2
tells us the locations (but not the orders) of all the poles of
D_{K,G}(s)
with real part
>1/2a(G)
, so that

\#\textnormal{Surj}(G_{K},G;X)=\sum_{d\in\textnormal{ind}(G-\{1\})}X^{1/d}P_{K,G,1/d}(\log X)+O(X^{\max\{\theta(K,G),1/2a(G)\}+\epsilon})

where
\deg P_{K,G,1/d}\leq b_{d}(K,G)-1
. This gives an asymptotic expansion for
\#\textnormal{Surj}(G_{K},G;X)
that includes the possible existence of lower order terms of the same shape as the main term that was predicted by Malle.

Remark 1.4
.

We note that Definition
1.3
(a) does not imply Definition
1.3
(b). While the meromorphic continuation allows one to apply a contour shifting argument to the line
{\rm Re}(s)=\theta+\epsilon
on Perron’s formula, this does not immediately correspond to a power saving bound on the error term. See Section
6
for the Tauberian theorem we will use to produce a power saving, and specifically Subsection
6.4
for a discussion on why the largest region of meromorphic continuation is not the same as the power saving bound for the error.

We then prove upper bounds for
\theta(K,G)
. When
G
is abelian, we give an explicit upper bound in terms of subconvexity bounds for Hecke
L
-functions in Theorem
8.1
, which we discuss in the main body of the paper. This is done so that the bounds we prove for
\theta(K,G)
will improve as researchers prove new subconvexity bounds. By inputting the subconvexity bounds proven in
[
Söh97
]
, we prove the following unconditional result:

Corollary 1.5
.

Let
K
be a number field and
G
an abelian group. Then for each
D\in\textnormal{ind}(G-\{1\})
or
D=2a(G)
,

\theta(K,G)\leq\frac{1}{a(G)}-\frac{\displaystyle\frac{1}{a(G)}-\frac{1}{D}}{\displaystyle 1+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}g\in G-\{1\}\\
\textnormal{ind}(g)<D\end{subarray}}\frac{[K:\mathbb{Q}]}{3}\left(1-\frac{\textnormal{ind}(g)}{D}\right)}.

This implies that
\theta(K,G)<1/a(G)
really does give an unconditional power savings for abelian
G
, which was previously proven by Frei–Loughran–Newton
[
FLN18
, Theorem 1.7]
. The best case scenario for subconvexity bounds is the generalized Lindelöf hypothesis (which follows from GRH). We also prove the following conditional result:

Corollary 1.6
.

Let
K
be a number field and
G
an abelian group. The generalized Lindelöf hypothesis for Hecke
L
-functions implies that
\theta(K,G)\leq\frac{1}{2a(G)}
.

Thus, we expect square root savings compared to the main term predicted by Malle. It is notable that
\textnormal{ind}(g)<2a(G)
for all
g\in G
by definition, which means that the square root power savings in Corollary
1.6
reveals all of the (potential) lower order terms associated to poles at
s=1/\textnormal{ind}(g)
given in Theorem
1.2
and Definition
1.3
.

Our methods give analogous results in certain cases when we count
G
-extensions that satisfy local conditions or when we order
G
-extensions by other invariants like the product of ramified primes. We have opted to discuss only the discriminant ordering in the main body of the paper for the sake of readability, but we include a discussion on how these results generalize in Appendix
A
.

1.2.
Lower Order Terms

Definition
1.3
allows for the possibility that
P_{K,G,1/d}
vanishes, corresponding to the possibility that
s=1/d
is not a pole of the generating series. This is allowed by Theorem
1.2
, as this theorem only prescribes an upper bound for the order of the pole (and we consider a “pole of order zero” to mean a removable singularity). It is known that the pole at
s=1/a(G)
has order exactly
b_{a(G)}(K,G)=b(K,G)
, giving the main term predicted by Malle. The usual argument used in
[
Wri89
,
Woo09
,
AO21
]
to compute the order of this pole is to bound the counting below by a similar count with restricted local conditions. However, this argument no longer works for lower order terms.

We discuss some of the obstacles to proving non-vanishing of lower order terms in Section
9
. At this time, we are not able to completely overcome these obstacles. However, in certain nice cases, we are able to use ad hoc arguments to prove the existence of some lower order terms.

Theorem 1.7
.

Let
G
be an abelian group,
\ell
the smallest prime dividing
|G|
, and
D_{\mathbb{Q},G}(s)
the generating series for
G
-extensions of
\mathbb{Q}
ordered by discriminant. Define

\bar{b}_{d}(\mathbb{Q},G)=b_{d}(\mathbb{Q},G)-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}g\in\textnormal{ind}(G-\{1\})\\
\textnormal{ind}(g)<d\end{subarray}}[\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{|\langle g\rangle|}):\mathbb{Q}]^{-1}\underset{s=\frac{\textnormal{ind}(g)}{d}}{\rm ord}\left(\zeta_{\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{|\langle g\rangle|})}(s)\right)

if this quantity is nonegative, and
\bar{b}_{d}(\mathbb{Q},G)=0
otherwise.

(1)

If
d=\frac{|G|(\ell-1)}{\ell}=a(G)
is the minimal index, then
D_{\mathbb{Q},G}(s)
has a pole at
s=1/d
of order
b_{d}(\mathbb{Q},G)=\bar{b}_{d}(\mathbb{Q},G)
.

(2)

If
d\in\textnormal{ind}(G-\{1\})
is such that

\{g\in G-\{1\}:\textnormal{ind}(g)<d\}\subseteq\Phi(G)

is contained in the Frattini subgroup, then
D_{\mathbb{Q},G}(s)
has a pole at
s=1/d
of order
\bar{b}_{d}(\mathbb{Q},G)
.

(3)

If
G=C_{2}\times A
for
|A|
odd, and
d\in\textnormal{ind}(G-\{1\})
is such that

\{g\in G-\{1\}:\textnormal{ind}(g)<d\}\subseteq\Phi(G)\cup G[2]

then
D_{\mathbb{Q},G}(s)
has a pole at
s=1/d
of order
\bar{b}_{d}(\mathbb{Q},G)
.

(4)

If
G=C_{n}
is cyclic and
d=n-1
is the index of a generator, then
D_{\mathbb{Q},G}(s)
has a pole at
s=1/d
of order
\bar{b}_{d}(\mathbb{Q},G)
.

Theorem
1.7
(i) is in fact proven by Wright
[
Wri89
]
for all number fields
K
, as the pole at
s=1/a(G)
corresponds to the main term of the asymptotic growth rate. We will give a new direct proof for this case when
K=\mathbb{Q}
, in line with the other cases. Theorem
1.7
is proven by more ad hoc arguments, essentially by getting our hands dirty with alternating sums of convergent Euler products. We prove that these cases are “nice enough” that we are able to treat or avoid the obstructions described in Section
10
.

Our discussion in Section
9
together with Theorem
1.7
can be taken as evidence for the following conjecture extending Theorem
1.2
:

Conjecture 1.8
.

Let
G
be an abelian group and
D_{\mathbb{Q},G}(s)
the generating Dirichlet series for
G
-extensions of
\mathbb{Q}
ordered by discriminant. Then
D_{\mathbb{Q},G}(s)
has a meromorphic continuation to the right halfplane
{\rm Re}(s)>\frac{1}{2a(G)}
with poles at
s=\frac{1}{d}
for each
d\in\textnormal{ind}(G-\{1\})
of order
exactly

\max_{\Phi(G)\leq H\leq G}\bar{b}_{d/[G:H]}(\mathbb{Q},H),

where
\bar{b}_{d}(\mathbb{Q},H)
is defined as in Theorem
1.7
.

We remark that the index function of
H
is related to the index function of
G
by
\textnormal{ind}_{H}(h)=\frac{1}{[G:H]}\textnormal{ind}_{G}(h)
for all
h\in H\leq G
, which is where the
d/[G:H]
factor comes from. Assuming
\zeta_{\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{m})}(s)
has no rational roots of size
>1/2
, it follows the definitions that

\max_{\Phi(G)\leq H\leq G}\bar{b}_{d/[G:H]}(\mathbb{Q},H)=\max_{\Phi(G)\leq H\leq G}b_{d/[G:H]}(\mathbb{Q},H)=b_{d}(\mathbb{Q},G)

agrees with the maximum value given in Theorem
1.2
. In principle, a number of individual Dedekind zeta function
\zeta_{K}(s)
with
K
of small discriminant are known to not have nontrivial rational zeros, but this is not known in general.

If there exists a rational counter example to the Riemann Hypothesis for
\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{m})
for some
m
, it is possible for smaller
H\leq G
to determine this maximum. For an example of how this could happen, consider
C_{15}=\langle\sigma\rangle
and
d=\textnormal{ind}(\sigma^{3})=12
. We can compute

\displaystyle\bar{b}_{12}(\mathbb{Q},C_{15})

\displaystyle=\max\left\{1-\underset{s=5/6}{\rm ord}\left(\zeta_{\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{3})}(s)\right),0\right\}

\displaystyle\bar{b}_{12/[C_{15}:C_{5}]}(\mathbb{Q},C_{5})

\displaystyle=1.

If
\zeta_{\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{3})}(s)
has a zero at
s=5/6
, then
H=C_{5}
gives the primary contribution to the pole at
s=1/12
. In the proof, this corresponds to a lower term in the sieve to
G
-extensions from
G
-étale algebras giving the order of the pole. This phenomenon occurs whenever
H
contains all the elements in
G
of index
d
, but does not contain all the elements of index smaller than
d
.

Theorem
1.7
(ii,iii) can be used to deduce some cases of this conjecture. The following corollary is immediate from the structure of Theorem
1.7
and the fact that
\zeta(s)
has no nontrivial real zeros:

Corollary 1.9
.

Conjecture
1.8
is true for
G=(C_{p^{k}})^{n}
for any prime
p
or
G=C_{2}\times(C_{p^{k}})^{n}
for any prime
p>2
. Moreover, in each of these cases,

\displaystyle\max_{\Phi(G)\leq H\leq G}\bar{b}_{d/[G:H]}(\mathbb{Q},H)=\bar{b}_{d}(\mathbb{Q},G)

for each
d\in\textnormal{ind}(G-\{1\})
.

A similar question can be asked for other number fields
K
, or in the twisted setting that we describe in the next subsection. However, these settings come with more delicate obstructions involving linear combinations of special values of
L
-functions. We do not currently have the evidence to justify extending the conjecture to such cases.

In order to turn the poles in Theorem
1.7
into lower order terms, we need to compare these to the bounds for
\theta(K,G)
in the error term. Corollary
1.6
immediately implies the following:

Corollary 1.10
.

Assume GRH. Then
\#\textnormal{Surj}(G_{\mathbb{Q}},G;X)
admits a lower order term if
G
satisfies at least one of the following:

(a)

G
is not elementary abelian, i.e.
\Phi(G)\neq 1
,

(b)

G=C_{2}\times A
for a nontrivial abelian group
A
of odd order, or

(c)

G=C_{n}
for some composite
n>1
.

Moreover, if
G=(C_{p^{k}})^{n}
for some prime
p
or
G=C_{2}\times(C_{p^{k}})^{n}
for some prime
p>2
then
\#\textnormal{Surj}(G_{\mathbb{Q}},G;X)
admits an asymptotic term of size

X^{\frac{1}{\textnormal{ind}(g)}}P_{\mathbb{Q},G,1/\textnormal{ind}(g)}(\log X)

for each
g\in G-\{1\}
for a polynomial
P_{\mathbb{Q},G,1/\textnormal{ind}(g)}
of degree exactly
b_{\textnormal{ind}(g)}(\mathbb{Q},G)-1
.

Remark 1.11
.

The lower order term referred to in Corollary
1.10
(c) of order
X^{\frac{1}{n-1}}
may not be the
secondary term
specifically, while Corollary
1.10
(a,b) does refer to a specific secondary (and possibly tertiary and so forth) term. This is because
n-1
is the largest index of an element in
C_{n}
, as opposed to the “next smallest”. If
n
is not the square of a prime, there are indices
\frac{1}{n-1}<\frac{1}{\textnormal{ind}(g)}<\frac{1}{a(C_{n})}
which are potentially associated to poles of
D_{\mathbb{Q},C_{n}}(s)
by Theorem
1.2
. However, stronger arguments are needed to prove that these poles do not cancel.

Using the bounds in Corollary
1.5
instead is much more involved. Using MAGMA
[
BCP97
]
, we are able to collect same data for cyclic groups.

Data Analysis 1.12
.

We consider
\#\textnormal{Surj}(G_{\mathbb{Q}},C_{n};X)
for composite
n<20{,}000
.

(1)

Corollary
1.5
reveals a potential lower order term for
\approx 48.5\%
of composite
n<20{,}000
. That is, a lower order term is revealed if
s=1/d
is a pole of
D_{\mathbb{Q},C_{n}}(s)
for
d
the second smallest index of
C_{n}
.

(2)

Corollary
1.5
and Theorem
1.7
reveal a lower order for
\approx 39.4\%
of composite
n<20{,}000
.

We prove this directly in some uncomplicated cases, primarily to show that
\#\textnormal{Surj}(G_{\mathbb{Q}},G;X)
admits an unconditional lower order term for infinitely many abelian groups
G
.

Corollary 1.13
.

Let
M
be a positive integer which is coprime to
6
. Then
\#\textnormal{Surj}(\mathbb{Q},C_{4M};X)
and
\#\textnormal{Surj}(\mathbb{Q},C_{6M};X)
admit an unconditional secondary term of size
X^{1/3M}
and
X^{1/4M}
respectively.

We remark that these account for
\approx 11.1\%
of cyclic groups.

1.3.
Power Saving Bounds for Counting Twisted Abelian Extensions

We also prove analogous power savings in the twisted setting developed by the author in
[
Alb21
]
. The twisted counting problem can be presented in two equivalent ways: Let
K
be a number field,
G\subseteq S_{n}
a transitive subgroup, and
T\trianglelefteq G
a normal subgroup.

(1)

Given any
G
-extension
L/K
, we can consider the counting function

\#\{F/K:[F:K]=n,\ \textnormal{Gal}(\widetilde{F}/K)\cong G,\ \widetilde{F}^{T}=L^{T},\ |\textnormal{disc}(F/K)|\leq X\}.

(2)

Alternatively, given a continuous homomorphism
\pi:G_{K}\to G
we can consider the counting function

\#\textnormal{Surj}(G_{K},T,\pi;X):=\#\{f\in Z^{1}(K,T(\pi)):f*\pi:G_{K}\to G\text{ is surjective},\ |\textnormal{disc}(f*\pi)|\leq X\},

where
T(\pi)
is the group
T
with the Galois action given by
x.t=\pi(x)t\pi(x)^{-1}
and
(f*\pi)(x)=f(x)\pi(x)
.

Up to a constant multiple, these two questions are equivalent if
L
is the field fixed by
\ker\pi
. This is called a “twisted” counting problem due to the nontrivial Galois action on
T
.

Remark 1.14
.

The set
\textnormal{Surj}(G_{K},T,\pi;X)
depends on the choice of homomorphism
\pi:G_{K}\to G
, however the cardinality of this set
\#\textnormal{Surj}(G_{K},T,\pi;X)
depends only on the Galois action on
T(\pi)
. Different homomorphisms
\pi
can produce the same Galois action, however the correspondence with the first perspective implies that these different choices do not change the size of the set. For this reason, we can write the asymptotic growth invariants as depending only on
K
and
T(\pi)
.

The author introduced this counting problem to formulate a natural generalization of Malle’s conjecture to a twisted setting, which predicts that

\#\textnormal{Surj}(G_{K},T,\pi;X)\sim c(K,T(\pi))X^{1/a(T)}(\log X)^{B(K,T(\pi))-1},

where
c(K,T(\pi))>0
,
a(T)=\min_{t\in T-\{1\}}\textnormal{ind}(t)
, and
B(K,T(\pi))\geq b(K,T(\pi))
generalizes Türkelli’s modified invariant. Notice that when
T=G
this specializes to Malle’s original conjecture (with Türkelli’s modified invariant). For our purposes we will only consider the case that
T
is abelian, for which

B(K,T(\pi))=b(K,T(\pi))=\#\{\text{orbits }o\text{ of }T\text{ under }\pi*\chi^{-1}\text{ with }\textnormal{ind}(o)=a(T)\}

where
\chi:G_{K}\to\hat{\mathbb{Z}}^{\times}
is the cyclotomic character. The same asymptotic is expected to be true for

\#H^{1}(K,T(\pi);X):=\frac{1}{|B^{1}(K,T(\pi))|}\#\{f\in Z^{1}(K,T(\pi)):|\textnormal{disc}(f*\pi)|\leq X\}

as well, with a possibly different leading coefficient.

The author together with O’Dorney
[
AO21
]
proved the twisted Malle’s conjecture for any abelian
T
, giving the main asymptotic term for
\#\textnormal{Surj}(G_{K},T,\pi;X)
. This proof uses Poisson summation associated to the Galois cohomology group
H^{1}(K,T(\pi))
to express the generating Dirichlet series as a finite sum of Euler products. The expression of the generating series as a finite sum of Euler products is much like the argument used by Wright for counting abelian extensions
[
Wri89
]
, where the use of Poisson summation in this context originates in
[
FLN18
]
. We leverage this decomposition together with a deeper study of the Tate pairing to prove the following generalization of Theorem
1.2
:

Theorem 1.15
.

Let
K
be a number field,
G\subseteq S_{n}
a transitive subgroup,
T\trianglelefteq G
abelian, and
\pi:G_{K}\to G
a continuous homomorphism. The generating Dirichlet series for
f\in Z^{1}(K,T(\pi))
for which
f*\pi
is surjective ordered by discriminant is meromorphic on the right halfplane
{\rm Re}(s)>0
.

Any poles with real part larger than
\frac{1}{2a(T)}
occur at
s=\frac{1}{d}
for each
d\in\textnormal{ind}(T-\{1\})
of order
at most

b_{d}(K,T(\pi))
, where

b_{d}(K,T(\pi))=\#\{\text{orbits }o\text{ of }T\text{ under }\pi*\chi^{-1}\text{ such that }\textnormal{ind}(o)=d\}.

Here,
\chi:G_{K}\to\hat{Z}^{\times}
is the cyclotomic character, and
\pi*\chi^{-1}
induces a Galois action on
T
by
x.t=(\pi(x)t\pi(x)^{-1})^{\chi(g)^{-1}}
.

Once again, each pole is expected to contribute a term to the asymptotic growth rate. We can define the following invariant generalizing
\theta(K,G)
:

Definition 1.16
.

Let
K
be a number field,
G\subseteq S_{n}
a transitive group,
T\trianglelefteq G
,
\pi:G_{K}\to G
a continuous homomorphism, and
D_{K,T(\pi)}(s)

2.1.
Summary of Alberts–O’Dorney

The author in a joint paper with O’Dorney
[
AO21
]
gave the asymptotic main term of the counting functions

H^{1}(K,T(\pi);X)=\{f\in H^{1}(K,T(\pi)):|\textnormal{disc}(f*\pi)|\leq X\}

by giving a decomposition for the generating series

\sum_{f\in H^{1}(K,T(\pi))}|\textnormal{disc}(f*\pi)|^{-s}.

This decomposition is produced in a natural way via Poisson summation. We summarize the main points here:
H^{1}(K,T(\pi))
is (up to a finite index subgroup) a discrete subgroup of the adelic product

H^{1}(\mathbb{A}_{K},T(\pi)):=\prod_{p}{}^{\prime}H^{1}(K_{p},T(\pi))_{H^{1}_{ur}(K_{p},T(\pi))}.

By local Tate duality and the Poitou-Tate nine term exact sequence,
H^{1}(K,T(\pi))
is exactly annihilated by the image of
H^{1}(K,T(\pi)^{*})
in
H^{1}(\mathbb{A}_{K},T(\pi)^{*})
, where
T(\pi)^{*}=\textnormal{Hom}(T(\pi),\mu)
is the Tate dual. Thus, setting
w_{s}(f)=|\textnormal{disc}(f*\pi)|^{-s}
for a fixed complex number
s
with
{\rm Re}(s)>1
, Poisson summation via
[
AO21
, Theorem 2.3]
implies

\sum_{f\in H^{1}(K,T(\pi))}|\textnormal{disc}(f*\pi)|^{-s}=\frac{|H^{0}(K,T(\pi))|}{|H^{0}(K,T(\pi)^{*})|}\sum_{h\in H^{1}(K,T(\pi)^{*})}\widehat{w_{s}}(h),

Well known properties of
\textnormal{disc}(f*\pi)
imply very nice properties for
\widehat{w_{s}}
. We state these, but leave the ideas behind their proof to
[
AO21
]
:

•

w_{s}
is periodic with respect to
\prod_{p\mid\infty}H^{1}(G_{K_{p}},T(\pi))\times\prod_{p\nmid\infty}H^{1}_{ur}(K_{p},T(\pi))
, as we know the discriminant depends only on ramification. This implies
\widehat{w_{s}}
is supported on the corresponding annihilator
\prod_{p\mid\infty}H^{1}_{ur}(K_{p},T(\pi))^{\perp}
. In particular, the righthand summation is supported on the
finite
set

H^{1}_{ur^{\perp}}(K,T(\pi)^{*}):=H^{1}(K,T(\pi)^{*})\cap\prod_{p\mid\infty}H^{1}_{ur}(K_{p},T(\pi))^{\perp}.

Finiteness follows from the fact that for all places
p\nmid|T|\textnormal{disc}(\pi)\infty
it follows that
H^{1}_{ur}(K_{p},T(\pi))^{\perp}=H^{1}_{ur}(K_{p},T(\pi)^{*})
. This is the dual Selmer group to
H^{1}_{ur}(G_{\mathbb{Q}},T(\pi))
.

•

w_{s}
is multiplicative, which implies
\widehat{w_{s}}
is also multiplicative. In particular,
\widehat{w_{s}}(h)
is given by the Euler product

\widehat{w_{s}}(h)=\prod_{p}\left(\frac{1}{|H^{0}(K_{p},T(\pi))|}\sum_{f\in H^{1}(K_{p},T(\pi))}\langle f,h_{p}\rangle|\textnormal{disc}(f*\pi)|^{-s}\right),

where

is the restriction of

to the decomposition group at
p
and

is the local Tate pairing.

The asymptotic growth rate is computed in the usual way by applying a Tauberian theorem to each of the finitely many Euler products appearing in the sum, with the rightmost pole of
\widehat{w_{s}}(0)
supplying the main term. It is shown that the rightmost pole of
\widehat{w_{s}}(0)
is not cancelled out by the poles of
\widehat{w_{s}}(h)
, nor is it cancelled out by the sieve to surjective coclasses
\textnormal{Surj}(G_{K},T,\pi;X)
, by which it is concluded that the main term has a positive leading coefficient.

2.2.
Twisted Permutation Representation

As part of our work producing meromorphic continuations, we will make use of Artin
L
-functions associated to a special type of representation.

Definition 2.1
.

A representation
\rho:G\to{\rm GL}_{n}(\mathbb{C})
is called a
twisted permutation representation
if, for each
g\in G
, the matrix
\rho(g)
has exactly one nonzero entry in each row and column. These have also been called
monomial representations
in the literature.

In this case, call
\overline{\rho}:G\to{\rm GL}_{n}(\mathbb{C})
the associated permutation representation defined by letting
\overline{\rho}(g)
have a
1
in every entry that
\rho(g)
is nonzero, and a
0
in every other entry.

This is a generalization of a permutation representation, for which every row and column has exactly one nonzero entry given by
1
. If
\rho:G_{K}\to{\rm GL}_{n}(\mathbb{C})
is a permutation representation, then the Artin
L
-function
L(s,\rho)
is analytically continued by way of the decomposition

\rho=\bigoplus_{o}\textnormal{ind}_{\textnormal{Stab}(o)}^{G_{K}}(1)

where the sum is over the orbits of the permutation action induced by
\rho
on the standard basis vectors of
\mathbb{C}^{n}
. Recalling that Artin
L
-functions are invariant under induction, this implies

L(s,\rho)=\prod_{o}\zeta_{\overline{K}^{\textnormal{Stab}(o)}}(s),

which is meromorphic with a single pole at
s=1
with order given by the number of orbits. This process generalizes for twisted permutation representations:

Lemma 2.2
.

If
\rho:G\to{\rm GL}_{n}(\mathbb{C})
is a twisted permutation representation for which
\overline{\rho}
is a transitive permutation representation, then

\rho=\textnormal{ind}_{\textnormal{Stab}(1)}^{G}(\rho|_{\textnormal{Stab}(1)}|\langle\vec{e}_{1}\rangle),

where
\vec{e}_{1}
is the basis vector associated to
1\in\{1,...,n\}
acted on by
G
via
\overline{\rho}
.

This implies that the Artin
L
-fucntion associated to a twisted permutation representation
\rho:G_{K}\to{\rm GL}_{n}(\mathbb{C})
decomposes as

L(s,\rho)=\prod_{o}L(s,\rho|_{\textnormal{Stab}(i_{o})}|\langle\vec{e}_{i_{o}}\rangle),

where
i_{o}
is a choice of base point for the orbit. In particular, these are one dimensional representations over
\textnormal{Stab}(i_{o})
and therefore given by Hecke
L
-functions. Hecke
L
-functions are entire (except for the trivial character, in which case they have a simple pole at
s=1
). This immediately proves the following:

Corollary 2.3
.

Let
\rho:G_{K}\to{\rm GL}_{n}(\mathbb{C})
be a twisted permutation representation. Then
L(s,\rho)
is meromorphic with a pole at
s=1
whose order is given by the number of orbits
o\subseteq\{1,...,n\}
under the action given by
\overline{\rho}
such that each basis vector
\vec{e}_{i}
with
i\in o
is an eigenvalue of
\rho(g)
with eigenvalue
1
for all
g\in\textnormal{Stab}(i)
.

Proof of Lemma
2.2
.

Write
V
for the
G
-module induced by
\rho
. Decompose
V=\bigoplus V_{i}
as a direct sum of irreducible
\textnormal{Stab}(1)
-modules, one of which is necessarily
V_{1}=\langle\vec{e}_{1}\rangle
. It then follows that the function

\mathbb{C}[G]\otimes_{\mathbb{C}[\textnormal{Stab}(1)]}V_{1}\to V

defined by

\left(\sum_{g}a_{g}g\right)\otimes v\mapsto\sum_{g}a_{g}g.v

is an isomorphism of
G
-modules.
∎

3.
Explicit Meromorphic Continuation

Fix some
h\in H^{1}(K,T(\pi)^{*})
and a map
w_{s}:H^{1}(\mathbb{A}_{K},T(\pi))\to\mathbb{C}
given by
w_{s}(f)=|\textnormal{disc}(f*\pi)|^{-s}
for each complex number
s\in C
. Our goal is to better understand the Fourier transform

\widehat{w_{s}}(h)=\prod_{p}\left(\frac{1}{|H^{0}(K_{p},T(\pi))|}\sum_{f_{p}\in H^{1}(K_{p},T(\pi))}\langle f_{p},h|_{G_{K_{p}}}\rangle\textnormal{disc}(f_{p}*\pi|_{G_{K_{p}}})^{-s}\right),

so that we can give a stronger, more explicit meromorphic continuation than the one used in
[
AO21
]
. This is achieved by the following theorem:

Theorem 3.1
.

Fix some
h\in H^{1}(K,T(\pi)^{*})
. Then there exists a twisted permutation representation

\rho_{h}:G_{K}\to{\rm GL}(\mathbb{C}[\textnormal{Hom}((\mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z})^{*},T(\pi))])

(given in Definition
5.1
), where
(\mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z})^{*}=\textnormal{Hom}(\mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z},\mu)
is the Tate dual of
\mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z}
, such that for each place
p\nmid|T|\textnormal{disc}(\pi)\textnormal{disc}(h)\infty

\frac{1}{|H^{0}(K_{p},T(\pi))|}\sum_{f_{p}\in H^{1}(K_{p},T(\pi))}\langle f_{p},h|_{G_{K_{p}}}\rangle\textnormal{disc}(f_{p}*\pi|_{G_{K_{p}}})^{-s}=\sum_{o\subset\textnormal{Hom}((\mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z})^{*},T(\pi))}{\rm tr}(\rho_{h}|_{\mathbb{C}o}(\textnormal{Fr}_{p}))p^{-\textnormal{ind}(o)s},

where the sum is over the orbits
o
of
\textnormal{Hom}((\mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z})^{*},T(\pi))
under the Galois action. Notice that
\textnormal{ind}(1)=0
so that
p^{-\textnormal{ind}(1)s}=1
gives the constant term.

The group
(\mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z})^{*}
is a procyclic group with Galois action given by the cyclotomic character
\chi:G_{K}\to\hat{\mathbb{Z}}^{\times}
. Choosing a generator gives a noncanonical isomorphism of groups
(\mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z})^{*}\cong\hat{\mathbb{Z}}
, and therefore a noncanonical isomorphism of groups

\textnormal{Hom}((\mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z})^{*},T(\pi))\cong T.

The index of an orbit
o
is defined to be the index of the corresponding element of
T
. This is independent of the choice of generator of
(\mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z})^{*}
, as
\textnormal{ind}(t)
depends only on the subgroup
\langle t\rangle
(or in other words, it is invariant under invertible powers).

The orbits of
\textnormal{Hom}((\mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z})^{*},T(\pi))
can be considered as tame ramification types. Indeed,
I_{p}^{\rm tame}
is canonically isomorphic to the prime-to-
p
part of
(\mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z})^{*}
. By writing all but finitely many Euler factors as a sum of characters, we can give a very explicit meromorphic continuation for
\widehat{w_{s}}(h)
. These types of Euler products were studied by Moroz
[
Mor88
]
, who proved that they all have a meromorphic continuation to the region
{\rm Re}(s)>0
.

Corollary 3.2
.

Let
w_{s}:H^{1}(\mathbb{A}_{K},T(\pi))\to\mathbb{C}
be given by
w_{s}(f)=|\textnormal{disc}(f*\pi)|^{-s}
for each complex
s\in\mathbb{C}
and fix some
h\in H^{1}(K,T(\pi)^{*})
. Then
\widehat{w_{s}}(h)
has a meromorphic continuation to the region
{\rm Re}(s)>0
.

We will need specific information about the meromorphic continuation, including the locations of poles and bounds in vertical strips, in order to use a Tauberian theorem. At the same time, we will not need the full region of meromorphic continuation proven by Moroz. Due to growth in vertical strips, it is not optimal to shift the contour integral used in a Tauberian theorem all the way to
{\rm Re}(s)=\epsilon
for some arbitrarily small
\epsilon>0
. It will suffice for us to use the following, more explicit result:

Corollary 3.3
.

Let
w_{s}:H^{1}(\mathbb{A}_{K},T(\pi))\to\mathbb{C}
be given by
w_{s}(f)=|\textnormal{disc}(f*\pi)|^{-s}
for each complex
s\in\mathbb{C}
,
h\in H^{1}(K,T(\pi)^{*})
, and
\rho_{h}
the representation given by Definition
5.1
. Then

\widehat{w_{s}}(h)=B(s)\prod_{o\subseteq\textnormal{Hom}((\mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z})^{*},T(\pi))}L(\textnormal{ind}(o)s,\rho_{h}|_{\mathbb{C}o}),

where
B(s)
is an Euler product that converges absolutely in the region
{\rm Re}(s)>\frac{1}{2a(T)}
.

Proof.

One checks that

1+b_{0}x^{a}+b_{1}x^{a+1}+\cdots+b_{N}x^{a+N}=(1+b_{0}x^{a})(1+b_{1}x^{a+1})\cdots(1+b_{N}x^{a+N})(1+O(x^{2a}))

via the distributive law. This implies that

\displaystyle\prod_{p\nmid|T|\textnormal{disc}(\pi)\textnormal{disc}(h)\infty}\sum_{o\subset\textnormal{Hom}((\mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z})^{*},T(\pi))}{\rm tr}(\rho_{h}|_{\mathbb{C}o}(\textnormal{Fr}_{p}))p^{-\textnormal{ind}(o)s}

\displaystyle=\prod_{p\nmid|T|\textnormal{disc}(\pi)\textnormal{disc}(h)\infty}(1+O(p^{-2a(T)s}))\prod_{o\subset\textnormal{Hom}((\mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z})^{*},T(\pi))}\prod_{p\nmid|T|\textnormal{disc}(\pi)\infty}\left(1+{\rm tr}(\rho_{h}|_{\mathbb{C}o}(\textnormal{Fr}_{p}))p^{-\textnormal{ind}(o)s}\right),

as
a(T)=\min\textnormal{ind}(o)
over all orbits
o\neq\{1\}
. We also know that

\left(1+{\rm tr}(\rho_{h}|_{\mathbb{C}o}(\textnormal{Fr}_{p}))p^{-\textnormal{ind}(o)s}\right)=\det\left(I+\rho_{h}|_{\mathbb{C}o}(\textnormal{Fr}_{p})p^{-\textnormal{ind}(o)s}\right)(1+O(p^{-2\textnormal{ind}(o)s})),

so that we conclude

\displaystyle\prod_{p\nmid|T|\textnormal{disc}(\pi)\textnormal{disc}(h)\infty}\sum_{o\subset\textnormal{Hom}((\mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z})^{*},T(\pi))}{\rm tr}(\rho_{h}|_{\mathbb{C}o}(\textnormal{Fr}_{p}))p^{-\textnormal{ind}(o)s}

\displaystyle=\prod_{p\nmid|T|\textnormal{disc}(\pi)\textnormal{disc}(h)\infty}(1+O(p^{-2a(T)s}))\prod_{o\subset\textnormal{Hom}((\mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z})^{*},T(\pi))}\prod_{p\nmid|T|\textnormal{disc}(\pi)\infty}\det\left(I-\rho_{h}|_{\mathbb{C}o}(\textnormal{Fr}_{p})p^{-\textnormal{ind}(o)s}\right)^{-1}.

There are only finitely many primes
p\mid|T|\textnormal{disc}(\pi)\textnormal{disc}(h)\infty
. By including the Euler factors at these places for
\widehat{w_{s}}(h)
on the left and for the Artin
L
-functions on the right, we have proven that

\widehat{w_{s}}(h)=B(s)\prod_{o\subseteq\textnormal{Hom}((\mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z})^{*},T(\pi))}L(\textnormal{ind}(o)s,\rho_{h}|_{\mathbb{C}o})

where
B(s)
is an Euler product whose Euler factors are
1+O(p^{-2a(T)s})
at all but finitely many places. At the other places, the Euler factors of
B(s)
are given by

\displaystyle\left(\frac{1}{|H^{0}(K_{p},T)|}\sum_{f_{p}\in H^{1}(K_{p},T)}\langle f_{p},h|_{G_{K_{p}}}\rangle_{p}|\textnormal{disc}(f_{p}*\pi|_{G_{K_{p}}})|^{-s}\right)

\displaystyle\times\prod_{o\subset\textnormal{Hom}((\mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z})^{*},T(\pi))}\det\left(I-\rho_{h}|_{(\mathbb{C}o)^{I_{p}}}(\textnormal{Fr}_{p})p^{-\textnormal{ind}(o)s}\right).

Each Euler factor of
B(s)
is an entire function, and the fact that all but finitely many of the Euler factors are
1+O(p^{-2a(T)s})
implies that
B(s)
converges absolutely on the right halfplane
{\rm Re}(s)>\frac{1}{2a(T)}
.
∎

Definition
5.1
implies that
\rho_{h}
is a twisted permutation representation, and therefore each of
\rho_{h}|_{\mathbb{C}o}
is induced from a Hecke character. Artin
L
-functions are invariant under inducing the representation, and Hecke
L
-functions are known to satisfy the Artin conjecture. This implies the following meromorphic continuation:

Corollary 3.4
.

Let
w_{s}:H^{1}(\mathbb{A}_{K},T(\pi))\to\mathbb{C}
be given by
w_{s}(f)=|\textnormal{disc}(f*\pi)|^{-s}
for each complex
s\in\mathbb{C}
and
h\in H^{1}(K,T(\pi)^{*})
. The Dirichlet series
\widehat{w_{s}}(h)
has a meromorphic continuation to
{\rm Re}(s)>\frac{1}{2a(T)}
with poles at
s=1/n
for each
n\in\textnormal{ind}(T\setminus\{1\})
of order

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\#\left\{o\subseteq\textnormal{Hom}((\mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z})^{*},T(\pi)):\begin{subarray}{c}\displaystyle\rho_{h}|_{\mathbb{C}o}=\textnormal{ind}_{L}^{K}(1_{L})\text{ for }L\text{ the fixed field}\\
\displaystyle\text{of }\textnormal{Stab}_{G_{K}}(e)\text{ for some }e\in o\end{subarray}\right\}

\displaystyle-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}o\subseteq\textnormal{Hom}((\mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z})^{*},T(\pi))\\
\textnormal{ind}(o)<n\end{subarray}}\underset{s=1/n}{\rm ord}L(\textnormal{ind}(o)s,\rho_{h}|_{\mathbb{C}o}),

with equality if
h=0
. Here,
\underset{s=a}{\rm ord}f(s)
denotes the order of the zero of
f(s)
at
s=a
and
1_{L}
denotes the trivial representation of
G_{L}
. In particular,
\rho_{h}|_{\mathbb{C}o}=\textnormal{ind}_{L}^{K}(1_{L})
if and only if
\rho_{h}|_{\mathbb{C}o}
is a permutation representation.

We note that it is conjectured that Artin
L
-functions satisfy GRH, and therefore have no zeros with real part larger than 1/2. Under GRH, the second term in Corollary
3.4
would vanish. However, this is unproven so we need to be careful to include the possibility that other real zeros exist. In particular, it is possible for a Siegel zero to affect the order of poles to the left of
s=1/a(T)
.

Theorems
1.2
and
1.15
follow immediately from Corollaries
3.2
and Corollary
3.4
together with the sieve to surjective coclasses. The most that the sieve to surjective coclasses can do is (partially) cancel out poles, so the upper bound on the orders of the poles is invariant under the sieve.

Proof of Corollary
3.4
.

The region of meromorphicity is immediate from Corollary
3.3
. The poles all come from the Artin
L
-functions, and the order of the pole
s=1/n
in the product

\prod_{o\subseteq\textnormal{Hom}((\mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z})^{*},T(\pi))}L(\textnormal{ind}(o)s,\rho_{h}|_{\mathbb{C}o})

is precisely

\displaystyle\#\left\{o\subseteq\textnormal{Hom}((\mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z})^{*},T(\pi)):\begin{subarray}{c}\displaystyle\rho_{h}|_{\mathbb{C}o}=\textnormal{ind}_{L}^{K}(1_{L})\text{ for }L\text{ the fixed field}\\
\displaystyle\text{of }\textnormal{Stab}_{G_{K}}(e)\text{ for some }e\in o\end{subarray}\right\}

\displaystyle-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}o\subseteq\textnormal{Hom}((\mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z})^{*},T(\pi))\\
\textnormal{ind}(o)<n\end{subarray}}\underset{s=1/n}{\rm ord}L(\textnormal{ind}(o)s,\rho_{h}|_{\mathbb{C}o})

by Corollary
2.3
. The first term comes from contributions of the poles of Artin
L
-functions
L(s,\rho)
at
s=1
, and the second term comes from the possible contribution of nontrivial zeros of Artin
L
-functions.

There is also a possibility that one of the Euler factors for
B(s)
at the finitely many places
p\mid|T|\textnormal{disc}(\pi)\textnormal{disc}(h)\infty
has a zero at
s=1/n
, which may partially cancel out the pole (i.e. lowering the order of the pole). This happens if and only if

\frac{1}{|H^{0}(K_{p},T)|}\sum_{f_{p}\in H^{1}(K_{p},T)}\langle f_{p},h|_{G_{K_{p}}}\rangle_{p}|\textnormal{disc}(f_{p}*\pi|_{G_{K_{p}}})|^{-s}

has a zero at
s=1/n
, as the factor
\det(I-\rho_{h}|_{(\mathbb{C}o)^{I_{p}}}(\textnormal{Fr}_{p})p^{-\textnormal{ind}(o)s})
is known to be zero-free on the right halfplane
{\rm Re}(s)>0
. In the case that
h=0
, the factor

\frac{1}{|H^{0}(K_{p},T)|}\sum_{f_{p}\in H^{1}(K_{p},T)}\langle f_{p},0\rangle_{p}|\textnormal{disc}(f_{p}*\pi|_{G_{K_{p}}})|^{-s}=\frac{1}{|H^{0}(K_{p},T)|}\sum_{f_{p}\in H^{1}(K_{p},T)}|\textnormal{disc}(f_{p}*\pi|_{G_{K_{p}}})|^{-s}

is strictly positive, so that
B(s)
has no real zeros on the region
{\rm Re}(s)>\frac{1}{2a(T)}
to partially cancel with the pole.
∎

These results give the necessary ingredients to use a Tauberian theorem: Corollary
3.3
gives an explicit meromorphic continuation, from which bounds in vertical strips can be obtained, and Corollary
3.4
gives the location and orders of the poles that will produce the asymptotic main and lower order terms.

4.
Parametrizing the Local Tate Pairings at Tame Places

The local Tate pairings are examples of a cup product. In order to construct a single representation that unifies these pairings, we will need a generalized version of the cup product.

Definition 4.1
.

Let
T
be a Galois module,
T^{*}=\textnormal{Hom}(T,\mu)
the Tate dual, and
\langle,\rangle:T\times T^{*}\to\mu
the natural pairing between them. Let
F/K
be a finite Galois extension containing the fields of definition for
T
and
T^{*}
. The
parametrizing local pairing
is a map

[,]:\textnormal{Hom}((\mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z})^{*},T)\times(T^{*}\rtimes\textnormal{Gal}(F/K))\to\textnormal{Hom}((\mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z})^{*},\mu),

defined as follows: for each
a\in\textnormal{Hom}((\mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z})^{*},T)
and
g=(g_{0},g_{1})\in T^{*}\rtimes\textnormal{Gal}(F/K)
, the pairing is given by

[a,g](x)=\langle a(x),g_{0}\rangle

for each
x\in(\mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z})^{*}
.

The parametrizing local pairing is built to be a consistent combination of all possible local Tate pairings at the tame places. While the construction does somewhat resemble the usual method of constructing a cup product, it is not immediately obvious why this is the correct notion or how to relate it to local Tate pairings.

The local Tate pairings are made from a cup product which canonically lands in
H^{2}(K_{p},\mu)\cong\mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z}
. They are then passed through a non-canonical isomorphism
\mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z}\cong\mu
to get roots of unity. We don’t really want the choice of non-canonical isomorphism here to make a difference in our work, and this is consistent with our definition of the parametrizing local pairing. There is a canonical isomorphism between
\mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z}
and
\textnormal{Hom}((\mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z})^{*},\mu)=(\mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z})^{**}
given in the usual way:

r\mapsto(\alpha\mapsto\alpha(r)).

We will allow ourselves to take canonical isomorphisms for granted, and treat the parametrizing local pairing as landing in
\mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z}
.

The primary result of this section is that the parametrizing local pairing specializes to (part of) the local Tate pairing at
each
place
p\nmid|T|\infty
which is unramified in
F/K
.

The Chebotarev density theorem implies that every element (up to conjugation) of
\textnormal{Gal}(F/K)
is given by Frobenius of some unramified place, so it follows that these images cover
\textnormal{Hom}((\mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z})^{*},T)\times(T^{*}\rtimes\textnormal{Gal}(F/K))
. Thus,
[,]
is determined uniquely by the compositions
[\iota_{p}^{*},{\rm ev}_{p}]
at finitely many places. We already proved that the value of
[\iota_{p}^{*},{\rm ev}_{p}]
was determined by the cup product on the Hochschild–Serre spectral sequence, which is unique.
∎

5.
Constructing the Galois representation

We are now ready to construct the Galois representation
\rho_{h}
used in Theorem
3.1
.

Definition 5.1
.

Let
h\in H^{1}(K,T^{*})
, choose a representative crossed homomorphism for
h
, let
F/K
be a finite Galois extension containing the fields of definition of
T
and
T^{*}
, and let
\pi:G_{K}\to\textnormal{Gal}(F/K)
be the canonical quotient map. Define the Galois representation

\rho_{h}:G_{K}\to{\rm GL}(\mathbb{C}[\textnormal{Hom}((\mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z})^{*},T)])

by the induced action on each of the basis elements
a\in\textnormal{Hom}((\mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z})^{*},T)

\rho_{h}(g)a=[g.a,(h*\pi)(g)](g.a),

where we choose an identification of
\textnormal{Hom}((\mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z})^{*},\mu)\cong\mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z}
with
\mu
so that the coefficient is a complex number.

Firstly, we should confirm that
\rho_{h}
is really a homomorphism. The parametrizing local pairing is not quite a bilinear map, instead it satisfies a type of twisted bilinearity. We prove this in the following lemma, which we can use to check that
\rho_{h}
is a homomorphism.

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
a,b\in\textnormal{Hom}((\mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z})^{*},T)
and
f=(f_{0},f_{1}),g=(g_{0},g_{1})\in T^{*}\rtimes\textnormal{Gal}(F/K)
. Then

(i)

[ab,f]=[a,f][b,f]
, and

(ii)

[a,fg]=[a,f][f_{1}^{-1}.a,g]
.

Using the second property, we can confirm that

\displaystyle\rho_{h}(xy)a

\displaystyle=[xy.a,(h*\pi)(xy)](xy.a)

\displaystyle=[xy.a,(h*\pi)(x)(h*\pi)(y)](xy.a)

\displaystyle=[xy.a,(h*\pi)(x)][(h*\pi)(x)_{1}^{-1}.(xy.a),(h*\pi)(y)](xy.a).

The action of
G_{K}
on
\textnormal{Hom}((\mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z})^{*},T)
factors through
\textnormal{Gal}(F/K)
, which implies
(h*\pi)(x)_{1}^{-1}
acts in the same way as
x^{-1}
. Thus

\displaystyle\rho_{h}(xy)a

\displaystyle=[xy.a,(h*\pi)(x)][x^{-1}.(xy.a),(h*\pi)(y)](xy.a)

\displaystyle=[xy.a,(h*\pi)(x)][y.a,(h*\pi)(y)](xy.a)

\displaystyle=\rho_{h}(x)[y.a,(h*\pi)(y)](y.a)

\displaystyle=\rho_{h}(x)\rho_{h}(y)a.

Thus,
\rho_{h}
really is a Galois representation.

Proof of Lemma
5.2
.

We may prove the lemma directly. Let
x\in(\mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z})^{*}
. Then

\displaystyle[ab,f](x)

\displaystyle=\langle(ab)(x),f_{0}\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle a(x)b(x),f_{0}\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle a(x),f_{0}\rangle\langle b(x),f_{0}\rangle

\displaystyle=[a,f](x)[b,f](x).

For the other coordinate, we evaluate

\displaystyle[a,fg](x)

\displaystyle=\langle a(x),(fg)_{0}\rangle.

The semi-direct product structure implies that

\displaystyle((fg)_{0},(fg)_{1})

\displaystyle=(f_{0},f_{1})(g_{0},g_{1})

\displaystyle=(f_{0}(f_{1}.g_{0}),f_{1}g_{1}).

The action on
g_{0}\in T^{*}
is given by

(f_{1}.g_{0})(y)=g_{0}(f_{1}^{-1}.y)^{\chi(f_{1})}

for
\chi:G_{K}\to\hat{\mathbb{Z}}^{\times}
the cyclotomic character. Thus,

\displaystyle[a,fg](x)

\displaystyle=\langle a(x),f_{0}(f_{1}.g_{0})\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle a(x),f_{0}\rangle\langle a(x),f_{1}.g_{0}\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle a(x),f_{0}\rangle\langle f_{1}^{-1}.(a(x)),g_{0}\rangle^{\chi(f_{1})}.

However,
\textnormal{Gal}(F/K)
acts on
\textnormal{Hom}((\mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z})^{*},T)
in a similar way:

(f_{1}^{-1}.a)(x)=f_{1}^{-1}.(a(x^{\chi(f_{1})})).

Moving the cyclotomic character all the way to the inside, we compute

\displaystyle[a,fg](x)

\displaystyle=\langle a(x),f_{0}\rangle\langle(f_{1}^{-1}.a)(x),g_{0}\rangle

\displaystyle=[a,f](x)[f_{1}^{-1}.a,g](x).

∎

This is all the setup we need to prove Theorem
3.1
.

Proof of Theorem
3.1
.

Choose a representative crossed homomorphism for
h\in H^{1}(K,T^{*})
, and let
p
be a place of
K
for which
p\nmid|T|\textnormal{disc}(\pi)\textnormal{disc}(h)\infty
. In particular, if
F/K
is a finite Galois extension containing the fields of definition for
T
and
T^{*}
then
p
is unramified in
F/K
.

For each
f_{p}\in H^{1}(K_{p},T)
, Theorem
4.2
implies that

\langle f_{p},h|_{G_{K_{p}}}\rangle_{p}=[f_{p}|_{I_{p}}\circ\iota_{p},(h*\pi)(\textnormal{Fr}_{p})],

which depends only on the restriction
f_{p}|_{I_{p}}
. The same is true for the discriminant, as
p\nmid\textnormal{disc}(\pi)
implies

|\textnormal{disc}(f_{p}*\pi|_{G_{K_{p}}})|^{-s}=p^{-\textnormal{ind}(f_{p}(\tau))s}

for any generator
\tau\in I_{p}^{\rm tame}
. Noting that the size of the set of coboundaries is
|B^{1}(K_{p},T)|=\frac{|T|}{|H^{0}(K_{p},T)|}
and that the fibers of
\textnormal{res}:Z^{1}(K_{p},T)\to Z^{1}(I_{p},T)
have size
|T|
, this implies

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|H^{0}(K_{p},T)|}\sum_{f_{p}\in H^{1}(K_{p},T)}\langle f_{p},h|_{G_{K_{p}}}\rangle_{p}|\textnormal{disc}(f_{p}*\pi|_{G_{K_{p}}})|^{-s}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|T|}\sum_{f_{p}\in Z^{1}(K_{p},T)}[f_{p}|_{I_{p}}\circ\iota_{p},(h*\pi)(\textnormal{Fr}_{p})]p^{-\textnormal{ind}(f_{p}(\tau))s}

\displaystyle=\sum_{f_{p}\in\textnormal{res}_{I_{p}}(Z^{1}(K_{p},T))}[f_{p}\circ\iota_{p},(h*\pi)(\textnormal{Fr}_{p})]p^{-\textnormal{ind}(f_{p}(\tau))s}.

Notice that because
F/K
is unramified at
p
,
Z^{1}(I_{p},T)=\textnormal{Hom}(I_{p},T)
. The restriction is then

\textnormal{res}_{I_{p}}(Z^{1}(K_{p},T))=Z^{1}(I_{p},T)^{G_{K_{p}}/I_{p}}=\textnormal{Hom}(I_{p},T)^{G_{K_{p}}/I_{p}}.

We pull back along the map
\iota_{p}:(\mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z})^{*}\to I_{p}^{\rm tame}
to remove most of the dependence on
p
, showing that this sum is equal to

\sum_{a\in\textnormal{Hom}((\mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z})^{*},T)^{G_{K_{p}}/I_{p}}}[a,(h*\pi)(\textnormal{Fr}_{p})]p^{-\textnormal{ind}(a)s}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a\in\textnormal{Hom}((\mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z})^{*},T)\\
\textnormal{Fr}_{p}.a=a\end{subarray}}[a,(h*\pi)(\textnormal{Fr}_{p})]p^{-\textnormal{ind}(a)s}.

Partitioning the sum by the
G_{K}
-orbits
o\subseteq\textnormal{Hom}((\mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z})^{*},T)
, this is equal to

\sum_{o\subseteq\textnormal{Hom}((\mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z})^{*},T)}\left(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a\in o\\
\textnormal{Fr}_{p}.a=a\end{subarray}}[a,(h*\pi)(\textnormal{Fr}_{p})]\right)p^{-\textnormal{ind}(o)s}.

The action of
G_{K}
on
\textnormal{Hom}((\mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z})^{*},T)
is given by the permutation representation
\overline{\rho_{h}}
, so that
\textnormal{Fr}_{p}.a=a
if and only if
\overline{\rho_{h}}(\textnormal{Fr}_{p})a=a
. By definition, this is equivalent to
a
being an eigenvector of
\rho_{h}(\textnormal{Fr}_{p})
. The associated eigenvalue is necessarily
[a,(h*\pi)(\textnormal{Fr}_{p})]
. Thus,

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a\in o\\
\textnormal{Fr}_{p}.a=a\end{subarray}}[a,(h*\pi)(\textnormal{Fr}_{p})]=\text{the sum of the eigenvalues of }\rho_{h}|_{\mathbb{C}o}(\textnormal{Fr}_{p})={\rm tr}(\rho_{h}|_{\mathbb{C}o}(\textnormal{Fr}_{p})).

Thus, we conclude that the Euler factor is given by

\sum_{o\subseteq\textnormal{Hom}((\mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z})^{*},T)}{\rm tr}(\rho_{h}|_{\mathbb{C}o}(\textnormal{Fr}_{p}))p^{-\textnormal{ind}(o)s}.

∎

6.
A Power Saving Tauberian Theorem

We will use the following Tauberian theorem:

Theorem 6.1
.

Let
a_{n}
be a sequence of complex numbers and
\widehat{a}_{n}
a sequence of real numbers with
|a_{n}|\leq\widehat{a}_{n}
. Let
L(s)=\sum a_{n}n^{-s}
and
\widehat{L}(s)=\sum\widehat{a}_{n}n^{-s}
be the corresponding Dirichlet series with abscissa of absolute convergence
\sigma_{a}
and
\widehat{\sigma}_{a}
respectively. Suppose that

(1)

L(s)
and
\widehat{L}(s)
have a meromorphic continuation to the right halfplane
{\rm Re}(s)>\widehat{\sigma}_{a}-\delta
with at most finitely many poles, and

(2)

|L(s)|,|\widehat{L}(s)|\ll_{\epsilon}(1+|t|)^{\xi+\epsilon}
for some
\xi\geq 0
, each
\epsilon>0
, and each
s
with
{\rm Re}(s)>\widehat{\sigma}_{a}-\delta
and sufficiently large imaginary part.

Then

\displaystyle\sum_{n\leq X}a_{n}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{r}\underset{s=s_{j}}{\rm Res}\left(L(s)\frac{X^{s}}{s}\right)+O\left(X^{\widehat{\sigma}_{a}-\frac{\delta}{\xi+1}+\epsilon}\right),

where
s_{1},s_{2},...,s_{r}
is the sequence of poles of
L(s)/s
with
{\rm Re}(s_{j})>\widehat{\sigma}_{a}-\frac{\delta}{\xi+1}
.

Landau originally proved a power savings of the form
O(X^{\widehat{\sigma}_{a}-\frac{\delta}{\lfloor\xi\rfloor+2}+\epsilon})
in
[
Lan15
]
under the condition that
L(s)
is meromorphic on all of
\mathbb{C}
, where
\lfloor\xi\rfloor
is the floor of
\xi
. In a more recent treatment, Roux
[
Rou11
, Theorems 13.3 and 13.8]
relaxed Landau’s hypotheses to produce the power savings
O(X^{\widehat{\sigma}_{a}-\frac{\delta}{\lfloor\xi\rfloor+2}+\epsilon})
under the same hypotheses as Theorem
6.1
above.

The improvement from
\widehat{\sigma}_{a}-\frac{\delta}{\lfloor\xi\rfloor+2}+\epsilon
to
\widehat{\sigma}_{a}-\frac{\delta}{\xi+1}+\epsilon
is most substantial when
\xi
is small. In particular, when
\xi=0
Theorem
6.1
gives a power savings
O(X^{\widehat{\sigma}_{a}-\delta+\epsilon})
that agrees with the region of meromorphicity. This should be expected, as
\xi=0
means that the vertical bounds of
L(s)
are negligible and should not affect the asymptotic growth rate.

Our proof is very similar to Roux’s, by shifting the contour integral in Perron’s formula. Roux uses the following version of Perron’s formula:

\int_{0}^{X}\int_{0}^{x_{1}}\cdots\int_{0}^{x_{k-1}}\sum_{n<x_{k}}a_{n}dx_{k}\cdots dx_{2}dx_{1}=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{c-i\infty}^{c+i\infty}L(s)\frac{X^{s+k}}{s(s+1)\cdots(s+k)}ds,

for some
c>\widehat{\sigma}_{a}
. Roux chooses
k=\lfloor\xi\rfloor+1
and proves that most pieces of the shifted contour are of negligible size with this choice when
T
is sufficiently large. We improve this argument by dealing with all pieces of the shifted contour directly, and by choosing
T
optimally as a function of
k
. The size of the error term produced by this choice decreases as
k\to\infty
, with
O(X^{\widehat{\sigma}_{a}-\frac{\delta}{\xi+1}+\epsilon})
being the limit.

We opt to present the proof of Theorem
6.1
in its entirety, though a number of portions are the same as those appearing in
[
Rou11
]
. We do this for the sake of completeness - it will help to clarify where our improvements occur, and it will allow readers to see an English translation of the components of
[
Rou11
]
. We also give more streamlined proofs for certain components.

6.1.
The Difference Operators

Let
N(x)=N_{0}(x)=\sum_{n<x}a_{n}
and define

N_{k}(x)=\int_{0}^{x}N_{k-1}(u)du.

We make a similar definition for
\widehat{N}_{k}(x)
for the sequence
\widehat{a}_{n}
.

In order to prove Theorem
6.1
, we will shift the contour in Perron’s formula

N_{k}(x)=\int_{0}^{X}\int_{0}^{x_{1}}\cdots\int_{0}^{x_{k-1}}\sum_{n<x_{k}}a_{n}dx_{k}\cdots dx_{2}dx_{1}=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{c-i\infty}^{c+i\infty}L(s)\frac{X^{s+k}}{s(s+1)\cdots(s+k)}ds.

We need to relate this back to
N(x)
, which we will do via difference operators.

Define

\displaystyle\Delta_{y}f

\displaystyle:x\mapsto f(x+y)-f(x)

\displaystyle\Delta_{y}^{(k)}=\underbrace{\Delta_{y}\circ\cdots\circ\Delta_{y}}_{k}.

By definition, the
k^{\rm th}
derivative
N_{k}^{(k)}(x)=N(x)
exists. We will approximate this equality using the
k
-fold difference operator:

(1)

\displaystyle y^{-k}\Delta_{y}^{(k)}N_{k}(x)\approx N(x).

The following lemma is an immediate consequence of the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus:

Lemma 6.2
.

Define

\displaystyle\widetilde{\Delta}_{y}f

\displaystyle:x\mapsto\int_{x}^{x+y}f(u)du

\displaystyle\widetilde{\Delta}_{y}^{(k)}=\underbrace{\widetilde{\Delta}_{y}\circ\cdots\circ\widetilde{\Delta}_{y}}_{k}.

Then
\Delta_{y}^{(k)}f(x)=\widetilde{\Delta}_{y}^{(k)}f^{(k)}(x)
.

This gives us an alternative way to write

y^{-k}\Delta_{y}N_{k}(x)=y^{-k}\widetilde{\Delta}_{y}^{(k)}N_{0}(x)=y^{-k}\int_{x}^{x+y}\int_{x_{1}}^{x_{1}+y}\cdots\int_{x_{k-1}}^{x_{k-1}+y}N(x_{k})dx_{k}\cdots dx_{2}dx_{1}.

By making strategic choices between the difference
\Delta_{y}
and the integral difference
\widetilde{\Delta}_{y}
at various steps in the proof, we are able to better optimize the error bounds.

The approximation (
1
) is concretely accomplished via
[
Rou11
, Proposition 16.16]
and
[
Rou11
, Proposition 16.19]
. Roux’s results can be cited without modification, but we opt to provide streamlined, translated proofs for the sake of completeness.

Proposition 6.3

(
[
Rou11
, Proposition 16.16]
)
.

Suppose
a_{n}\geq 0
and
N(x)=\sum_{n<x}a_{n}
. Then

y^{-k}\Delta_{y}^{(k)}N_{k}(X-ky)\leq N(X)\leq y^{-k}\Delta_{y}^{(k)}N_{k}(X)

This implies

\displaystyle y^{-k}\Delta_{y}^{(k)}N_{k}(X)

\displaystyle=N(X)+O\left(y^{-k}\Delta_{y}^{(k)}[N_{k}(X)-N_{k}(X-ky)]\right).

in the case that
a_{n}\geq 0
.

Proof of Proposition
6.3
.

The fact that
a_{n}\geq 0
implies that
N(X)
is an increasing function. Thus,

\displaystyle\Delta_{y}^{(k)}N_{k}(X)

\displaystyle=\widetilde{\Delta}_{y}^{(k)}N(X)

\displaystyle=\int_{X}^{X+y}\int_{x_{1}}^{x_{1}+y}\cdots\int_{x_{k-1}}^{x_{k-1}+y}N(x_{k})dx_{k}\cdots dx_{2}dx_{1}

\displaystyle\geq\int_{X}^{X+y}\int_{x_{1}}^{x_{1}+y}\cdots\int_{x_{k-1}}^{x_{k-1}+y}N(X)dx_{k}\cdots dx_{2}dx_{1}

\displaystyle=y^{k}N(X),

as the bounds on the integrals force
x_{k}\geq\cdots\geq x_{1}\geq X
.

The other inequality is similar, as

\displaystyle\Delta_{y}^{(k)}N_{k}(X-ky)

\displaystyle=\widetilde{\Delta}_{y}^{(k)}N(X-ky)

\displaystyle=\int_{X-ky}^{X-(k-1)y}\int_{x_{1}}^{x_{1}+y}\cdots\int_{x_{k-1}}^{x_{k-1}+y}N(x_{k})dx_{k}\cdots dx_{2}dx_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{X-ky}^{X-(k-1)y}\int_{x_{1}}^{x_{1}+y}\cdots\int_{x_{k-1}}^{x_{k-1}+y}N(X)dx_{k}\cdots dx_{2}dx_{1}

\displaystyle=y^{k}N(X),

as the bounds for the integrals force
x_{k}\leq x_{k-1}+y\leq\cdots\leq x_{1}+(k-1)y\leq X
.
∎

We next use
\widehat{N}(X)
to give a bound for
y^{-k}\Delta_{y}^{(k)}N_{k}(X)-N(X)
when
a_{n}
is a general sequence of complex numbers. The following proposition plays an analogous role to
[
Rou11
, Proposition 16.19]
, although it is more streamlined. The proof is shorter (not requiring an induction argument), and the result is more immediately applicable to proving Theorem
6.1
.

Proposition 6.4
.

Suppose
|a_{n}|\leq\widehat{a}_{n}
and set
N(x)=\sum_{n<x}a_{n}
and
\widehat{N}(x)=\sum_{n<x}\widehat{a}_{n}
. Then

y^{-k}\Delta_{y}^{(k)}N_{k}(X)=N(X)+O\left(y^{-k}\Delta_{y}^{(k)}\widehat{N}_{k}(X)-\widehat{N}(X)\right).

Thus, the error given by Proposition
6.3
for
\widehat{N}(X)
can also be used for
N(X)
.

Proof of Proposition
6.4
.

By Lemma
6.2
,

\displaystyle y^{-k}\Delta_{y}^{(k)}N_{k}(X)

\displaystyle=y^{-k}\widetilde{\Delta}_{y}^{(k)}N(X)

\displaystyle=y^{-k}\int_{X}^{X+y}\int_{x_{1}}^{x_{1}+y}\cdots\int_{x_{k-1}}^{x_{k-1}+y}N(x_{k})dx_{k}\cdots dx_{2}dx_{1}

\displaystyle=N(X)+y^{-k}\int_{X}^{X+y}\int_{x_{1}}^{x_{1}+y}\cdots\int_{x_{k-1}}^{x_{k-1}+y}N(x_{k})-N(X)dx_{k}\cdots dx_{2}dx_{1}

\displaystyle=N(X)+O\left(y^{-k}\int_{X}^{X+y}\int_{x_{1}}^{x_{1}+y}\cdots\int_{x_{k-1}}^{x_{k-1}+y}|N(x_{k})-N(X)|dx_{k}\cdots dx_{2}dx_{1}\right).

We remark that for
x_{k}\geq X
,

\displaystyle|N(x_{k})-N(X)|

\displaystyle=\left\lvert\sum_{X\leq n<X_{k}}a_{n}\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{X\leq n<x_{k}}|a_{n}|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{X\leq n<x_{k}}\widehat{a}_{n}

\displaystyle=\widehat{N}(x_{k})-\widehat{N}(X).

Thus

\displaystyle y^{-k}\Delta_{y}^{(k)}N_{k}(X)

\displaystyle=N(X)+O\left(y^{-k}\int_{X}^{X+y}\int_{x_{1}}^{x_{1}+y}\cdots\int_{x_{k-1}}^{x_{k-1}+y}\widehat{N}(x_{k})-\widehat{N}(X)dx_{k}\cdots dx_{2}dx_{1}\right)

\displaystyle=N(X)+O\left(y^{-k}\Delta_{y}^{(k)}\widehat{N}_{k}(X)-\widehat{N}(X)\right).

∎

6.2.
Shifting the Contour Integral

We put the contour shifting argument under a single proposition:

Proposition 6.5
.

Let
a_{n}
be a sequence of complex numbers with generating Dirichlet series
L(s)=\sum a_{n}n^{-s}
with abscissa of absolute convergence
\leq\sigma_{a}
. Suppose that

(1)

L(s)
has a meromorphic continuation to the right halfplane
{\rm Re}(s)>\sigma_{a}-\delta
with at most finitely many poles, and

(2)

|L(s)|\ll_{\epsilon}(1+|t|)^{\xi+\epsilon}
for some
\xi\geq 0
, each
\epsilon>0
, and each
s
with
{\rm Re}(s)>\sigma_{a}-\delta
and sufficiently large imaginary part.

Then setting
y=X^{1-\frac{(k-\max\{\xi-1,0\})\delta}{(k+1)\xi+k-\max\{\xi-1,0\}}}
implies that for all
k
sufficiently large depending only on
\delta
,
\xi
, and
\epsilon>0

\displaystyle y^{-k}\Delta_{y}^{(k)}N_{k}(X)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{r}\underset{s=s_{j}}{\rm Res}\left(L(s)\frac{X^{s}}{s}\right)+O_{\delta,\xi,\epsilon}\left(X^{\sigma_{a}-\frac{\delta}{\xi+1}+\epsilon}\right),

where
s_{1},s_{2},...,s_{r}
is the sequence of poles of
L(s)/s
with
{\rm Re}(s_{j})>\sigma_{a}-\frac{\delta}{\xi+1}
.

This proposition applies to both
N(X)
and
\widehat{N}(X)
. The smoother counting in
N_{k}(X)
is what allows this Proposition to use only
\sigma_{a}
instead of
\widehat{\sigma}_{a}
in the error bounds.

Proof.

Consider Perron’s formula

N_{k}(X)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{c-i\infty}^{c+i\infty}L(s)\frac{X^{s+k}}{s(s+1)\cdots(s+k)}ds,

which holds for any
c>\sigma_{a}
. We will take
c=\sigma_{a}+\epsilon
(one often takes
c=\sigma_{a}+\frac{1}{\log X}
for this type of contour shifting argument, but this will be unnecessary for our purposes).

We push the countour integral to the line
{\rm Re}(s)=\sigma_{a}-\delta+\epsilon
(with
\epsilon>0
chosen small so that
L(s)
does not have any poles on this line) by taking

\int_{\sigma_{a}+\epsilon-i\infty}^{\sigma_{a}+\epsilon+i\infty}=\int_{C}+\int_{\sigma_{a}+\epsilon-i\infty}^{\sigma_{a}+\epsilon-iT}+\int_{\sigma_{a}+\epsilon-iT}^{\sigma_{a}-\delta+\epsilon-iT}+\int_{\sigma_{a}-\delta+\epsilon-iT}^{\sigma_{a}-\delta+\epsilon+iT}+\int_{\sigma_{a}-\delta+\epsilon+iT}^{\sigma_{a}+\epsilon+iT}+\int_{\sigma_{a}+\epsilon+iT}^{\sigma_{a}+\epsilon+i\infty},

where
C
is the closed contour given by a box
\sigma_{a}+\epsilon-iT
to
\sigma_{a}+\epsilon+iT
to
\sigma_{a}-\delta+\epsilon+iT
to
\sigma_{a}-\delta+\epsilon-iT
.

Set

\Phi(X)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{C}L(s)\frac{X^{s+k}}{s(s+1)\cdots(s+k)}ds.

This is differentiable as a function of
X
, with

\displaystyle\Phi^{(k)}(X)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{C}L(s)\frac{X^{s}}{s}ds

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{R}\underset{s=s_{j}}{\rm Res}\left(L(s)\frac{X^{s}}{s}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{r}\underset{s=s_{j}}{\rm Res}\left(L(s)\frac{X^{s}}{s}\right)+O\left(X^{\sigma_{a}-\frac{\delta}{\xi+1}+\epsilon}\right),

where
s_{1},s_{2},...,s_{R}
are the poles of
L(s)
with real part
>\sigma_{a}-\delta+\epsilon
, ordered by increasing real parts. The final equality follows from the fact that

\underset{s=s_{j}}{\rm Res}\left(L(s)\frac{X^{s}}{s}\right)=O\left(X^{{\rm Re}(s_{j})+\epsilon}\right).

In particular,

\displaystyle\Delta_{y}^{(k)}\Phi(X)

\displaystyle=\widetilde{\Delta}_{y}^{(k)}\Phi^{(k)}(X)

\displaystyle=y^{k}\Phi^{(k)}(X)+\widetilde{\Delta}_{y}^{(k)}\left[\Phi^{(k)}(u)-\Phi^{(k)}(X)\right]_{u=X}

\displaystyle=y^{k}\Phi^{(k)}(X)+O\left(y^{k+1}\max_{X\leq v\leq X+ky}\{\Phi^{(k+1)}(v)\}\right).

Knowing that all the terms of
\Phi^{(k)}(X)
are of the form
X^{a}(\log X)^{b}
, we can differentiate this function directly and conclude that
\Phi^{(k)}(v)\ll v^{\sigma_{a}-1}
. Given the assumption that
y<X
, this implies

\displaystyle\Delta_{y}^{(k)}\Phi(X)

\displaystyle=y^{k}\Phi^{(k)}(X)+O_{k}\left(y^{k+1}X^{\sigma_{a}-1}\right).

We now bound the remaining contour integrals. The Dirichlet series
L(s)
converges absolutely in the region
{\rm Re}(s)>\sigma_{a}
. In particular, this implies

|L(s)|=O_{\epsilon}(1)

on the line
{\rm Re}(s)=\sigma_{a}+\epsilon
. This gives a bound

\displaystyle\left\lvert\Delta_{y}^{(k)}\int_{\sigma_{a}+\epsilon+iT}^{\sigma_{a}+\epsilon+i\infty}L(s)\frac{X^{s+k}}{s(s+1)\cdots(s+k)}\ ds\right\rvert

\displaystyle\ll\int_{\sigma_{a}+\epsilon+iT}^{\sigma_{a}+\epsilon+i\infty}\frac{|\Delta_{y}^{(k)}X^{s+k}|}{|s(s+1)\cdots(s+k)|}ds

\displaystyle\ll 2^{k}(X+ky)^{\sigma_{a}+k+\epsilon}\int_{T}^{\infty}\frac{1}{t^{k+1}}\ dt

\displaystyle\ll_{k}X^{\sigma_{a}+k+\epsilon}T^{-k}.

A similar bound holds for
\int_{\sigma_{a}+\epsilon-i\infty}^{\sigma_{a}+\epsilon-iT}
and for

\displaystyle\left\lvert\Delta_{y}^{(k)}\int_{\sigma_{a}-\delta+\epsilon\pm iT}^{\sigma_{a}+\epsilon\pm iT}L(s)\frac{X^{s+k}}{s(s+1)\cdots(s+k)}\ ds\right\rvert

\displaystyle=\left\lvert\int_{\sigma_{a}-\delta+\epsilon\pm iT}^{\sigma_{a}+\epsilon\pm iT}L(s)\frac{\Delta_{y}^{(k)}X^{s+k}}{s(s+1)\cdots(s+k)}\ ds\right\rvert

\displaystyle\ll_{k}X^{\sigma_{a}+k+\epsilon}\int_{\sigma_{a}-\delta+\epsilon\pm iT}^{\sigma_{a}+\epsilon\pm iT}\frac{(1+|t|)^{\xi+\epsilon}}{|s|^{k+1}}\ ds

\displaystyle\ll_{\delta,k}X^{\sigma_{a}+k+\epsilon}T^{\xi-k-1+\epsilon}.

We combine these together as

O_{\delta,k}(X^{\sigma_{a}+k+\epsilon}T^{\max\{\xi-1,0\}-k+\epsilon}).

For the remaining contour integral, we will need to use Lemma
6.2
to switch from
\Delta_{y}
and
\widetilde{\Delta}_{y}
. We bound

\displaystyle\left\lvert\Delta_{y}^{(k)}\int_{\sigma_{a}-\delta+\epsilon-iT}^{\sigma_{a}-\delta+\epsilon+iT}L(s)\frac{X^{s+k}}{s(s+1)\cdots(s+k)}\ ds\right\rvert

\displaystyle=\left\lvert\int_{\sigma_{a}-\delta+\epsilon-iT}^{\sigma_{a}-\delta+\epsilon+iT}L(s)\frac{\Delta_{y}^{(k)}X^{s+k}}{s(s+1)\cdots(s+k)}\ ds\right\rvert

\displaystyle=\left\lvert\int_{\sigma_{a}-\delta+\epsilon-iT}^{\sigma_{a}-\delta+\epsilon+iT}L(s)\frac{\widetilde{\Delta}_{y}^{(k)}(s+k)\cdots(s+1)X^{s}}{s(s+1)\cdots(s+k)}\ ds\right\rvert

\displaystyle=\left\lvert\int_{\sigma_{a}-\delta+\epsilon-iT}^{\sigma_{a}-\delta+\epsilon+iT}L(s)\frac{\widetilde{\Delta}_{y}^{(k)}X^{s}}{s}\ ds\right\rvert

\displaystyle\ll y^{k}X^{\sigma_{a}-\delta+\epsilon}\int_{\sigma_{a}-\delta+\epsilon-iT}^{\sigma_{a}-\delta+\epsilon+iT}\frac{(1+|t|)^{\xi+\epsilon}}{|s|}\ ds

\displaystyle\ll y^{k}X^{\sigma_{a}-\delta+\epsilon}T^{\xi+\epsilon}.

Remark 6.6
.

This bound is where our proof differs from Roux’s. Roux directly bounds
\Delta_{y}^{(k)}X^{s+k}\ll_{k}X^{\sigma_{a}+k+\epsilon}
instead of switching to
\widetilde{\Delta}_{y}
, showing that this piece of the contour is

\ll_{k}X^{\sigma_{a}+k+\epsilon}(T^{\xi-k-1+\epsilon}+O(1)).

By choosing
k>\xi+1
, each term with a
T
decays in
T
. Roux can then choose
T
sufficiently large in terms of
X
so that the error is dominated by only

O(y^{k}X^{\sigma_{a}-1})+O(X^{\sigma_{a}+k+\epsilon}).

Our bound allows us to choose an optimum value for
T
, resulting in the improved power savings.

Putting these together implies that

\displaystyle y^{-k}\Delta_{y}^{(k)}N(t)

\displaystyle=\Phi^{(k)}(X)+O_{k}(yX^{\sigma_{a}-1})+O_{\delta,k}(y^{-k}X^{\sigma_{a}+k+\epsilon}T^{\max\{\xi-1,0\}-k+\epsilon})+O(X^{\sigma_{a}-\delta+\epsilon}T^{\xi+\epsilon})

We now set
T=X^{\frac{(k+1)\delta}{(k+1)\xi+k-\max\{\xi-1,0\}}}
and
y=X^{1-\frac{(k-\max\{\xi-1,0\})\delta}{(k+1)\xi+k-\max\{\xi-1,0\}}}
. The error terms are then

\displaystyle O(yX^{\sigma_{a}-1})

\displaystyle=O(X^{\sigma_{a}-\frac{(k-\max\{\xi-1,0\})\delta}{(k+1)\xi+k-\max\{\xi-1,0\}}+\epsilon})

\displaystyle O(y^{-k}X^{\sigma_{a}+k+\epsilon}T^{\max\{\xi-1,0\}-k+\epsilon})

\displaystyle=O(X^{\sigma_{a}-\frac{(k-\max\{\xi-1,0\})\delta}{(k+1)\xi+k-\max\{\xi-1,0\}}+\epsilon})

\displaystyle O(X^{\sigma_{a}-\delta+\epsilon}T^{\xi+\epsilon})

\displaystyle=O(X^{\sigma_{a}-\frac{(k-\max\{\xi-1,0\})\delta}{(k+1)\xi+k-\max\{\xi-1,0\}}+\epsilon}).

In fact, these choices of
T
and
y
are optimal when
k
is sufficiently large as the first error term is increasing in
y
, the second is decreasing in
y
and
T
for
k>\xi-1
, and the third is increasing in
T
.

It is clear that

\lim_{k\to\infty}\sigma_{a}-\frac{(k-\max\{\xi-1,0\})\delta}{(k+1)\xi+k-\max\{\xi-1,0\}}=\sigma-\frac{\delta}{\xi+1},

so there exists a sufficiently large
k
depending only on
\delta
,
\xi
, and
\epsilon>0
such that

\sigma_{a}-\frac{(k-\max\{\xi-1,0\})\delta}{(k+1)\xi+k-\max\{\xi-1,0\}}\leq\sigma-\frac{\delta}{\xi+1}+\epsilon.

Thus, there exists a
k
sufficiently large depending only on
\delta
,
\xi
, and
\epsilon>0
such that

y^{-k}\Delta_{y}^{(k)}N(X)=\Phi^{(k)}(X)+O(X^{\sigma_{a}-\frac{\delta}{\xi+1}+\epsilon}).

Given that
\Phi^{(k)}(X)
equals the sum of residues in the Proposition statement, this concludes the proof.
∎

6.4.
The region of meromorphic continuation and the power saving bounds cannot be conflated

The process of “shifting the contour” in Perron’s formula to prove a
power saving
Tauberian theorem is a delicate process. Theorem
6.1
requires not just a meromorphic continuation of the Dirichlet series, but also bounds in vertical strips. It is a common misconception to conflate the right half plane
{\rm Re}(s)>\sigma_{a}-\delta
with power savings
O(X^{\sigma_{a}-\delta+\epsilon})
for the error term.

We believe that Lee–Oh conflated these concepts in
[
LO12
]
when studying
\theta(\mathbb{Q},C_{p})
. In particular, they make the following claim:

If
g(s)
is holomorphic on
{\rm Re}(s)>\sigma
then
\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{c-i\infty}^{c+i\infty}g(s)\frac{X^{s}}{s}ds=O(X^{\sigma+\epsilon})
.

Their meromorphic continuation results prove that the generating series has finitely many poles with real part
>\frac{1}{2a(C_{p})}
, respectively
>\frac{1}{4a(C_{p})}
under GRH. The above claim would imply that
\theta(\mathbb{Q},C_{p})\leq\frac{1}{2a(C_{p})}
, respectively
\leq\frac{1}{4a(C_{p})}
under GRH.

Lee–Oh cite Delange’s Tauberian theorem for this (see
[
Del54
, Theorem III]
or
[
Ten15
, II Theorem 7.13 and 7.28]
), however this result does not apply to just any holomorphic function. It only applies to Dirichlet series which are
convergent
for
{\rm Re}(s)>\sigma
, not just holomorphic for
{\rm Re}(s)>\sigma
. Any error bound produced from a contour integral beyond the region of convergence (or for non-Dirichlet series) will necessarily incorporate the growth of
g(s)
in vertical strips.

In particular, this claim is demonstrably false for
g(s)=\zeta(s)^{2}-\frac{1}{(s-1)^{2}}-\frac{2\gamma}{s-1}
, where
\gamma
the Euler–Mascheroni constant. The rational function pieces cancel out the poles of
\zeta(s)^{2}
, making
g(s)
an entire function. Moreover,

\Delta(X)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{c-i\infty}^{c+i\infty}g(s)\frac{X^{s}}{s}ds

is equal to the error term for Dirichlet’s divisor problem

\Delta(X)=\sum_{n\leq X}\left(\sum_{d\mid n}1\right)-X\log X-(2\gamma-1)X.

The claim would suggest that
\Delta(X)=O(X^{\epsilon})
, as
g(s)
is holomorphic on the right halfplane
{\rm Re}(s)>0
. However, Hardy previously showed that
\inf\{\theta:\Delta(X)=O(X^{\theta})\}\geq\frac{1}{4}
in
[
Har16
]
, contradicting the claim.

For this reason, we believe that the bounds for
\theta(\mathbb{Q},C_{p})
claimed in
[
LO12
]
do not follow from the arguments in that paper. To the author’s knowledge, the bounds in this paper are the best known for
\theta(\mathbb{Q},C_{p})
for
p\geq 3
. Using Söhne’s subconvexity bounds, Corollary
1.5
implies

\theta(\mathbb{Q},C_{p})\leq\frac{1}{a(C_{p})}\left(\frac{p+2}{p+5}\right)=\frac{p+2}{(p-1)(p+5)},

whereas Corollary
1.6
with GRH implies

\theta(\mathbb{Q},C_{p})\leq\frac{1}{2a(C_{p})}=\frac{1}{2(p-1)}.

Unfortunately, neither of these bounds are small enough to reveal the secondary term
X^{\frac{1}{3(p-1)}}
claimed by
[
LO12
]
under GRH. The generating Dirichlet series does have a pole at
s=\frac{1}{3(s-1)}
as described in
[
LO12
]
, but, in light of
[
Har16
]
and similar results, it is not clear if the error bounds can be made small enough to reveal the corresponding asymptotic term.

The power savings claimed in
[
LO12
]
can be proven for
smoothed
counting functions, such as

\sum_{\textnormal{Gal}(K/\mathbb{Q})\cong C_{p}}\exp\left(1-\frac{|\textnormal{disc}(K/\mathbb{Q})|}{X}\right),

in which case there would be an asymptotic term of size
X^{\frac{1}{3(p-1)}}
. This is because the rapid decay of the smoothing function
\exp(1-\frac{n}{X})
overrides any polynomial growth of the generating series in vertical strips, so that the region of meromorphic continuation actually does match the smoother error bounds.

7.
Subconvexity

Vertical bounds of the form

|L(s)|\ll_{L}(1+|t|)^{\beta}

are a topic of great interest in analytic number theory. We give a brief overview of this area, based on the material in
[
IK04
]
for the Selberg class of
L
-functions. Strictly speaking, this is called the
t
-aspect
of vertical bounds, as it relates to the growth in
t
. There is also the
q
-aspect
, which asks how the implicit constant depends on the conductor
q(L)
of the
L
-function

|L(s)|\ll q(L)^{\alpha}(1+|t|)^{\beta}.

We restrict ourselves to considering only the
t
-aspect, as this is what we require to prove the main results of this paper.

Most objects that one might call an “
L
-function” (such as those functions in the Selberg class) have a function
\mu_{L}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
associated to them defined as

\mu_{L}(\sigma)=\inf\{\xi:|L(\sigma+it)|\ll_{L}(1+|t|)^{\xi}\text{ for all }|t|\text{ sufficiently large}\}.

The Phragmen-Lindelöf principle implies that
\mu_{L}
is a
convex function
. If
\sigma_{a}
is the abscissa of absolute convergence for
L(s)
, it is clear that
\mu_{L}(\sigma)=0
for
\sigma>\sigma_{a}
. If
L(s)
has a functional equation, this symmetry can be used to determine
\mu_{L}(\sigma)
for
\sigma<0
. For example, if
L(s)
belongs to the Selberg class with degree
d
, then
\mu_{L}(\sigma)=\frac{d}{2}-\sigma
for
\sigma<0
.

The behavior of
\mu_{L}(\sigma)
in the critical strip
(0,\sigma_{a})
is a subject of great interest, in part because of the role these bounds play in Tauberian theorems like Theorem
6.1
.

Proposition 7.1
.

Let
L(s)
be an
L
-function in the Selberg class of degree
d
, so that the abscissa of absolute convergence is
\sigma_{a}=1
. Then

\mu_{L}(\sigma)\leq\frac{d}{2}(1-\sigma)

in the critical strip
0\leq\sigma\leq 1
. This is called the
convexity bound
.

This proposition follows from the fact that
\mu_{L}(\sigma)
is convex, and interpolating between the regions
\sigma<0
and
\sigma>1
.

Any bound that beats the convexity bound is called a
subconvexity bound
. These are often presented as bounds specifically for
\mu_{L}(1/2)
, from which convexity of
\mu_{L}
gives a corresponding improved bound for all
\sigma
in the critical strip. Proving new subconvexity bounds is a very rich, very active area of number theory. It would be unreasonable to attempt to summarize the best known results for all
L
-functions, especially as results for individual
L
-functions are constantly being improved. We will restrict ourselves to a general, uncomplicated subconvexity bound for Hecke
L
-functions:

Theorem 7.2

(Söhne
[
Söh97
]
)
.

Let
L(s,\chi)
be a Hecke
L
-function over the number field
K
. Then
L(s,\chi)
is an
L
-function of degree
[K:\mathbb{Q}]
and

\mu_{\chi}(1/2)\leq\frac{[K:\mathbb{Q}]}{6}.

This beats the convexity bound of
\mu_{\chi}(1/2)\leq\frac{[K:\mathbb{Q}]}{4}
.

The most one could hope for is that
\mu_{L}(1/2)=0
. This is the content of the generalized Lindelöf Hypothesis:

Conjecture 7.3

(Generalized Lindelöf Hypothesis)
.

Let
L(s)
be an
L
-function in the Selberg class. Then
\mu_{L}(1/2)=0
.

Conjecture
7.3
would follow from knowing that
L(s)
satisfies GRH. In this case, convexity of
\mu_{L}
implies that

\mu_{L}(\sigma)=d\max\left\{\frac{1}{2}-\sigma,0\right\}

where
d
is the degree of
L(s)
.

In our setting, the vertical bounds for
\widehat{w_{s}}(h)
are built from the vertical bounds for several Artin
L
-functions along with the fact that
B(s)
is absolutely convergent on the region
{\rm Re}(s)\geq 1/2a(T)
.

Corollary 7.4
.

Let
w_{s}:H^{1}(\mathbb{A}_{K},T(\pi))\to\mathbb{C}
be given by
w_{s}(f)=|\textnormal{disc}(f*\pi)|^{-s}
for each complex
s\in\mathbb{C}
and
h\in H^{1}(K_{p},T(\pi)^{*})
. Let
\mu_{h}(\sigma)
be the vertical bounds associated to the Dirichlet series
\widehat{w_{s}}(h)
, that is

\mu_{h}(\sigma)=\inf\{\xi:|\widehat{w_{s}}(h)|\ll(1+|t|)^{\xi}\text{ for all }s=\sigma+it\text{ with }|t|\text{ sufficiently large}\}.

Then

\displaystyle\mu_{h}(\sigma)\leq\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}o\subseteq\textnormal{Hom}((\mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z})^{*},T(\pi))\\
o\neq\{1\}\end{subarray}}2\mu_{\rho_{h}|_{\mathbb{C}o}}(1/2)\max\{1-\textnormal{ind}(o)\sigma,0\}

for each
\sigma>\frac{1}{2a(T)}
, where
\rho_{h}
is the twisted permutation representation given in Definition
5.1
and
\mu_{\rho}
is the vertical bound function associated to the Artin
L
-function
L(s,\rho)
.

Proof.

The proof is immediate from the vertical bounds of each factor in Corollary
3.3
.
∎

Given that
\rho_{h}|_{\mathbb{C}o}
is induced from a Hecke character, Corollary
7.4
gives vertical bounds for
\widehat{w_{s}}(h)
in terms of vertical bounds for Hecke
L
-functions. Together with Theorem
6.1
, this implies that better subconvexity bounds for Hecke
L
-functions translate to better power savings for counting abelian extensions.

9.
Obstructions to the Non-vanishing of Lower Order Terms

We discuss the lower order terms in this section. This is primarily a discussion of the obstacles towards proving the lower order terms do not vanish, or equivalently proving that the poles of
D_{K,G}(s)
in Theorem
1.2
do not vanish.

There are essentially three type of obstructions.

(1)

Real zeros of
B(s)
and of the Hecke
L
-functions that appear in Corollary
3.3
can reduce the order of the poles in Corollary
3.4
. This is evident from the structure of Corollary
3.3
.

(2)

If
H_{ur^{\perp}}^{1}(K,T(\pi)^{*})\neq 1
, then there may be cancellation between the poles of
\widehat{w_{s}}(h_{1})
and the poles of
\widehat{w_{s}}(h_{2})
. This is tricky, as the residues of
\widehat{w_{s}}(h_{j})\frac{X^{s}}{s}
giving the lower order terms include special values of
L
-functions in the critical strip. The sum over
h
of the coefficients in a potential lower order term
X^{1/d}P_{K,T(\pi),1/d}(\log X)
will then become a linear combination of rational special values of an
L
-function times a convergent Euler product. Proving non-vanishing of linear combinations of
L
-functions at values in the critical strip is immensely difficult to do directly.

(3)

Cancellation between the poles during the sieve to surjective coclasses. For any
g\in T-\{1\}
which does not generate
T
, the (potential) lower order term associated to the pole
s=1/\textnormal{ind}(g)
will appear both in the term for all coclasses and in at least one other term of the sieve. This is an alternating sieve, and so becomes an alternating sieve of lower order terms that might cancel.

In principle, these obstacles are all present for the main term with the exception that the main term does not involve any special values of
L
-functions in the critical strip. Non-cancellation of the main term is proven in a round-about way by giving a nonzero lower bound. By choosing restricted local conditions at finitely many places that avoid all three obstructions, one proves a lower bound of the right order of magnitude.

The same lower bound does not work for lower order terms. For example, suppose we knew that

a_{1}X+b_{1}X^{1/2}+O(X^{1/3})\gg a_{2}X+b_{2}X^{1/2}+O(x^{1/3})

for
a_{2},b_{2}>0
. This does implies that
a_{1}\geq a_{2}>0
, but it does not necessarily say anything about
b_{1}
. Indeed,
2X\gg X+X^{1/2}+O(X^{1/3})
. When restricting local conditions at finitely many places, the leading coefficient necessarily changes which prevents us from making conclusions about the lower order terms.

It is not immediately clear to us that these obstructions can be overcome. The presence of a linear combination of special values of
L
-functions in the critical strip in particular gives us pause.

However, we can say more if we restrict to a special case. Suppose we know that
H_{ur^{\perp}}^{1}(K,T(\pi)^{*})=1
. This avoids the second obstruction entirely, and now the generating series of for
H^{1}(K,T(\pi))
is given only by

\frac{|H^{0}(K,T(\pi))|}{|H^{0}(K,T(\pi)^{*})|}\widehat{w_{s}}(0),

which is a single Euler product. Corollary
3.4
gives the precise order of the poles for
\widehat{w_{s}}(0)
in terms of the zeros of
L
-functions rather than an upper bound, also avoiding the first obstruction.

We can classify exactly when this holds for
G
having the trivial Galois action.

Lemma 9.1
.

Let
G
be an abelian group with the trivial Galois action. Then
H_{ur^{\perp}}^{1}(K,G^{*})=1
if and only if one of the following holds:

(1)

K=\mathbb{Q}
, or

(2)

G=C_{2}^{n}
and
K
has class number
1
.

In these cases, we completely avoid the first two obstructions and only need to worry about the sieve to surjective coclasses (which in this case that
G
has the trivial Galois action would be the sieve from étale algebras to field extensions). Our explicit work in the following subsection suggests that the sieve to surjective coclasses is unlikely to cancel out the poles of
\widehat{w_{s}}(0)
. These facts are what we consider to be convincing evidence for Conjecture
1.8
.

Proof of Lemma
9.1
.

This follows from the Greenberg–Wiles identity
[

]

\frac{|H^{1}_{\mathcal{L}}(K,G)|}{|H^{1}_{\mathcal{L}^{\perp}}(K,G^{*})|}=\frac{|H^{0}(K,G)|}{|H^{0}(K,G^{*})|}\prod_{p}\frac{|L_{p}|}{|H^{0}(K_{p},G)|}.

10.1.
An Inclusion-Exclusion Lemma for Sieving Euler Products

We first prove a lemma for an inclusion-exclusion sieve of convergent Euler products. This will be applied to show that the sieve to surjective coclasses does not cancel out certain poles, as long as the sieve does not involve too many special values of
L
-functions in the critical strip.

Lemma 10.1
.

Let
(P,\leq)
be a poset with Möbius function
\mu_{P}
in which all joins exist, and
f:P\times\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
a nonegative function for which
f(x,n)
is multiplicative in
n
and supported on the squarefree integers for each
x\in P
.

Let
x\in P
such that
\#\{y\leq x:\mu_{P}(y,x)\neq 0\}
is finite and suppose that

\prod_{p}\left(1+\sum_{y\leq x}f(y,p)\right)

converges. Then

\sum_{z\leq x}\mu_{P}(z,x)\prod_{p}\left(1+\sum_{y\leq z}f(y,p)\right)=\left(\sum_{z\leq x}\mu_{P}(z,x)\right)+\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}\mu(n)^{2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(y_{p})_{p\mid n}\\
\vee_{p\mid n}y_{p}=x\end{subarray}}\prod_{p\mid n}f(y_{p},p).

In particular, this is positive if and only if
x
is minimal or there exists at least one term
\prod_{p\mid n}f(y_{p},p)>0
with
\vee_{p\mid n}y_{p}=x
.

Proof.

All of the Euler products in the statement are absolutely convergent as
z\leq x
implies

\prod_{p}\left(1+\sum_{y\leq z}f(y,p)\right)\leq\prod_{p}\left(1+\sum_{y\leq x}f(x,p)\right)<\infty.

First suppose
x\in P
is minimal, that is
\#\{y\in P:y<x\}=0
. Then

\displaystyle\sum_{z\leq x}\mu_{P}(z,x)\prod_{p}\left(1+\sum_{y\leq z}f(y,p)\right)

\displaystyle=\prod_{p}(1+f(x,p))

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\mu(n)^{2}f(x,p)

\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{z\leq x}\mu_{P}(z,x)\right)+\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}\mu(n)^{2}f(x,p).

For the remainder of the proof, suppose that
x\in P
is not minimal. We distribute the Euler products to write

\displaystyle\sum_{z\leq x}\mu_{P}(z,x)\prod_{p}\left(1+\sum_{y\leq z}f(y,p)\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{z\leq x}\mu_{P}(z,x)\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(y_{p})_{p\mid n}\\
y_{p}\leq z\end{subarray}}\mu(n)^{2}\prod_{p\mid n}f(y_{p},p)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\mu(n)^{2}\sum_{z\leq x}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(y_{p})_{p\mid n}\\
y_{p}\leq z\end{subarray}}\mu_{P}(z,x)\prod_{p\mid n}f(y_{p},p)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\mu(n)^{2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(y_{p})_{p\mid n}\\
y_{p}\leq x\end{subarray}}\left(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}z\geq y_{p}\\
\text{for each }p\mid n\end{subarray}}\mu_{P}(z,x)\right)\prod_{p\mid n}f(y_{p},p)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\mu(n)^{2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(y_{p})_{p\mid n}\\
y_{p}\leq x\end{subarray}}\left(\sum_{z\geq\vee_{p\mid n}y_{p}}\mu_{P}(z,x)\right)\prod_{p\mid n}f(y_{p},p).

The Möbius function of a poset satisfies

\sum_{z\geq y}\mu_{P}(z,x)=\begin{cases}1&y=x\\
0&y\neq x.\end{cases}

Thus, the expression simplifies to

\displaystyle\sum_{z\leq x}\mu_{P}(z,x)\prod_{p}\left(\sum_{y\leq z}f(y,p)\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}\mu(n)^{2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(y_{p})_{p\mid n}\\
\vee_{p\mid n}y_{p}=x\end{subarray}}\prod_{p\mid n}f(y_{p},p)

Note that that starting index changes to a
2
because when
n=1
,
\vee_{p\mid n}y_{p}
is an empty join. Thus, the sum is over all
z
or equivalently over all finitely many
z\leq x
with
\mu_{P}(z,x)\neq 0
. As
x
is not minimal, this term cancels out.
∎

Restricted Local Conditions and Alternate Orderings

The methods of this paper are readily applicable to the more generalized question of counting abelian extensions ordered by other admissible invariants and with restricted local conditions, as was done for the main term in

A.1.
Ordering by Admissible Invariants

Suppose we fix a weight function
{\rm wt}:G\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
for which
{\rm wt}(g)=0
if and only if

[

]

[
AO21
]
give the asymptotic growth rate of the function

\#\textnormal{Surj}_{\textnormal{inv}}(G_{K},T,\pi;X)=\#\{\pi\in Z^{1}(G_{K},T(\pi)):f*\pi\text{ surjective,}\ |\textnormal{inv}(f)|\leq X\}

for any abelian

with
\pi(G_{K})T=G

We may define the power saving invariant for an arbitrary admissible ordering in analogy with Definition
1.3
(also with an analogous twisted version).

Definition A.1
.

Let
K

The discriminant has weight given by the index function. One might predict that the results of this paper are true for any invariant with a weight function, but this is not quite the case. What is true is the following:

Theorem A.2
.

Let
{\rm wt}:G\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
be a weight function which is invariant under conjugation and invertible powers, i.e.
{\rm wt}(g)={\rm wt}(hgh^{-1})
for any

{\rm wt}

The only real consideration is in Theorem
3.1

This condition is a natural one. The inertia subgroups are only well-defined up to conjugation, so by asking

to be invariant under conjugation we are really asking that the invariant is independent of the choice of embedding for the decomposition group

While it is still possible to fix some of these choices and ask a counting question, the results in this paper suggest that the corresponding generating series may not be as nice.

{\rm ram}

Checking that the lower order terms, or even the main term, do not vanish becomes significantly more involved when we restrict to a family of local conditions. Not only does the work in Section

An interesting question is whether there exist weaker Grunwald–Wang counter examples for which the main term does not vanish, but subsequent lower order terms do. In other words, are there any cases where the analogous statement to Conjecture
