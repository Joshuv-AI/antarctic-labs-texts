Abstract
. We give an upper and lower estimate for the maximal commutator length of a noncentral element of the elementary subgroup of the general linear group over a skew-field based on the maximal commutator length of an element of the multiplicative group of that skew-field.



1.
Introduction

Questions on commutator lengths are questions on specific word maps. In general case, for a group
G

a word map
is a map

G^{n}\to G\text{,}\qquad(g_{1},\ldots,g_{m})\mapsto w(g_{1},\ldots,g_{m})\text{,}

where
w
is a word of
m
variables (i.e an element of the free group on
m
generators), and where
w(g_{1},\ldots,g_{n})
is what we get if we substitute the elements
g_{1}
,
\ldots
,
g_{n}
into this word.

By definition, the
commutator length
of the element
g\in[G,G]
if not greater than
d
if and only if
g
belongs to the image of the word map given by the word
w=[x_{1},y_{1}]\ldots[x_{d},y_{d}]
of
2d
variables. In other words, if it can be expressed as a product of
d
commutators.

In recent studies of word maps, the case of
G=\mathcal{G}(K)
, where
\mathcal{G}
is a simple or semisimple algebraic group defined over the field
K
, is of particular interest, see references in
[
2
]
,
[
16
]
,
[
15
]
. Almost all the results in this direction have been proved for the cases where
K
is an algebraically closed field, or where
\mathcal{G}
is either split or anisotropic over
K
.

The surjectivity of word maps given by the word
w=[x,y]
for different types of groups has a long history, see.
[
10
]
,
[
13
]
. For a split, or more generally quasi-split, group
\mathcal{G}^{+}(K)
such surjectivity can be proved using a method called ”the Gauss decomposition with a given semi-simple part”. More precisely it can be proved for the group
\mathcal{G}^{+}(K)/Z(\mathcal{G}^{+}(K))
if the field is assumed to be sufficiently large, see
[
10
]
. Let us recall the main idea of this method, see
[
8
]
,
[
9
]
,
[
11
]
,
[
12
]
,
[
14
]
,
[
18
]
. Let
\mathcal{G}
be a quasi-split group;
T
be a fixed maximal quasi-split torus of
\mathcal{G}
;
B
be a fixed Borel subgroup containing
T
; by
B^{-}
we denote the opposite Borel subgroup. The method is based on the possibility for each noncentral conjugacy class of the group
\mathcal{G}^{+}(K)
and any element
h\in T\cap\mathcal{G}^{+}(K)
to find a representative
g
of this class with Gauss decomposition
g=vhu
, where
u,v\in\mathcal{G}^{+}(K)
are elements of unipotent radicals of subgroups
B
and
B^{-}
correspondently.

In paper
[
5
]
, Kursov study the products of commutators in general linear group over a skew-field, see also
[
6
]
. The skew-field was assumed to be finite-dimensional over its centre and not coinciding with it. Later Kursov’s result was rediscovered by Gordeev and Egorchenkova in paper
[
4
]
, see also
[
3
]
. In this paper, the corresponding analog of the Gauss decomposition with a given semi-simple part was proved.

However, this analog does not allow to fully prescribe the diagonal factor
h
: the last element on the diagonal can be prescribed only up to multiplication by an element of the derived subgroup of the skew-field multiplicative group. Hence such a decomposition does not allow to prove that any noncentral element of the elementary group is a commutator. Nevertheless, it is proved in
[
5
]
that if any element of the derived subgroup of the skew-field multiplicative group is a product of at most
c
commutators, then any noncentral element of the elementary group is also a a product of at most
c
commutators in general linear group.

In fact, if there is no restriction on the commutator lengths in the derived subgroup of the skew-field multiplicative group, then it is impossible to express an arbitrary noncentral element of the elementary group as one commutator.

It is well known that if an element
\tau\in D^{*}
of the multiplicative group of the skew-field
D
does not belong to the derived subgroup
[D^{*},D^{*}]
, then the diagonal matrix
g=\diag(1,\ldots,1,\tau)
does not belong to the elementary subgroup because it has a nontrivial Dieudonne determinant.

To a reader who is familiar with basic model theory, we left as an exercise to use the fact above and the compactness theorem to deduce the following statement: for any integers
{n\geqslant 2}
and
{d\geqslant 1}
there exists a positive integer
N
such that for any skew-field
D
(not necessarily finite-dimensional over centre) and any
\tau\in D^{*}
, if
\tau
is not a product of
N
commutators in multiplicative group, then
\diag(1,\ldots,1,\tau)
is not a product of
d
commutators in general linear group of
n\times n
matrices. In addition, if we have
\tau\in[D^{*},D^{*}]
, then the matrix
\diag(1,\ldots,1,\tau)
belong to the elementary subgroup. Therefore, if the derived subgroup of the multiplicative group contain an element of a large commutator length, then the elementary group also contain an element of a large commutator length.

In the present paper we prove an effective version of this statement. Namely we show that one can take
N=d(8n^{2}-13n+8)-2n^{2}+3n-1
(Theorem
1
). Therefore, there is a lower estimate on the maximal commutator length in the elementary group (Corollary
1
), that depend on maximal commutator length in derived subgroup of the multiplicative group. Hence there is a necessary condition for expressing an arbitrary noncentral element as a commutator (Corollary
2
).

However the main result of
[
5
]
and
[
4
]
is not optimal. As we show, for skew-fields that are finite dimensional over its centre, if any element of the derived subgroup of the multiplicative group is a product of at most
c
commutators, then any element of the elementary subgroup is a product of at most
\lceil\tfrac{c}{n}\rceil
commutators in the general linear group of
n\times n
matrices, and also a product of at most
\lceil\tfrac{c}{n-2}\rceil
commutators in the elementary group, where by
\lceil x\rceil
we denote the ceil function of
x
(Theorem
2
). In particular, there is a sufficient condition for expressing an arbitrary noncentral element as a commutator (Corollaries
3
and
4
).

2.
Basic notation

2.1.
General linear group and elementary subgroup

Let
D
be a skew-field,
D^{*}=D\setminus\{0\}
be its multiplicative group. We fix a positive integer
n\geqslant 2
. By
\mathop{\mathrm{GL}}\nolimits(n,D)
we denote the general linear group of degree
n
over the skew-field
D
. By
\diag(\varepsilon_{1},\ldots,\varepsilon_{n})
we denote the diagonal matrix with elements
\varepsilon_{1}
,
\ldots
,
\varepsilon_{n}
on the diagonal. By
h_{i,j}(\varepsilon)
, where
1\leqslant i\neq j\leqslant n
and
\varepsilon\in D^{*}
, we denote the diagonal matrix that has the element
\varepsilon
in
i
-th diagonal position, the element
\varepsilon^{-1}
in
j
-th diagonal position, and ones in all the remaining diagonal positions.

We denote by
t_{i,j}(\xi)
, where
1\leqslant i,j\leqslant n
,
i\neq j
,
\xi\in D
, the elementary transvection, i.e. the matrix that has the element
\xi
in the position
(i,j)
, and in all the other positions it coincides with the identity matrix.

Let us also introduce notation for the root subgroups
X_{i,j}=t_{i,j}(D)\leqslant\mathop{\mathrm{GL}}\nolimits(n,D)
.

It is well known that the elementary transvections satisfy the following relations.

[formula]

t_{i,j}(\xi)t_{i,j}(\zeta)=t_{i,j}(\xi+\zeta)\text{,}

[formula]

[t_{i,j}(\xi),t_{p,q}(\zeta)]=\begin{cases}t_{i,q}(\xi\zeta)\text{,}\;&\text{ if }j=p\text{,}i\neq q\text{,}\\
e\text{,}\;&\text{ if }j\neq k\text{,}i\neq q\text{,}\end{cases}

[formula]

t_{i,j}(\zeta)t_{j,i}(\xi)=h_{i,j}(\zeta)h_{i,j}(\zeta^{-1}+\xi)t_{j,i}((1+\xi\zeta)\xi)t_{i,j}((1+\zeta\xi)^{-1}\zeta)\text{,}\;\text{ if }\zeta,1+\zeta\xi\in D^{*}\text{,}

[formula]

t_{i,j}(\xi)t_{j,i}(-\xi^{-1})t_{i,j}(\xi)=t_{j,i}(-\xi^{-1})t_{i,j}(\xi)t_{j,i}(-\xi^{-1})\text{.}

The subgroup

E(n,D)=\big\langle t_{i,j}(\xi)\colon 1\leqslant i\neq j\leqslant n\text{,}\;\xi\in D\big\rangle\text{,}

generated by all the elementary transvections is called the
elementary group
. In case of a skew-field, the elementary group coincides with the kernel of the Dieudonne determinant
\det\colon\mathop{\mathrm{GL}}\nolimits(n,D)\to D^{*}/[D^{*},D^{*}]
, see, for example,
[
1
]
. In particular, we have
[\mathop{\mathrm{GL}}\nolimits(n,D),\mathop{\mathrm{GL}}\nolimits(n,D)]\leqslant E(n,D)
. In fact, except for the case, where
n=2
and
D=\mathbb{F}_{2}
, we have
E(n,D)=[\mathop{\mathrm{GL}}\nolimits(n,D),\mathop{\mathrm{GL}}\nolimits(n,D)]
.

2.2.
Subgroups of triangular and diagonal matrices

By
U
,
V\leqslant\mathop{\mathrm{GL}}\nolimits(n,D)
we denote the groups of upper and lower unitriangular matrices correspondently; i.e. triangular matrices with ones on the diagonal. Note that
U
,
V\leqslant E(n,D)
.

Set

H=\{\diag(\varepsilon_{1},\ldots,\varepsilon_{n})\colon\varepsilon_{i}\in D^{*}\}\leqslant\mathop{\mathrm{GL}}\nolimits(n,D)\text{.}

Note that the Dieudonne determinant of the matrix
h=\diag(\varepsilon_{1},\ldots,\varepsilon_{n})
is equal to the image of the product
\varepsilon_{1}\ldots\varepsilon_{n}
in the quotient group
D^{*}/[D^{*},D^{*}]
. Therefore, the element
h
belongs to the elementary subgroup if and only if the product
\varepsilon_{1}\ldots\varepsilon_{n}
belong to the derived subgroup of the group
D^{*}
.

Set
B=HU\leqslant\mathop{\mathrm{GL}}\nolimits(n,D)
, it is the group of upper triangular matrices; and
B^{-}=HV\leqslant\mathop{\mathrm{GL}}\nolimits(n,D)
is the group of lower triangular matrices.

For
\kappa\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{n-1}
, we denote by
H_{\leqslant\kappa}
the set of elements
h\in H
that can be expressed as a product of elements
h_{i,i+1}(\varepsilon)
, where
1\leqslant i\leqslant n-1
and
\varepsilon\in D^{*}
, in such a way that the number of factors with given
i
is not greater than
\kappa_{i}
. The order of factors can be arbitrary.

2.3.
Commutator lengths

Let
G
be a group. We denote by
[G,G]_{\leqslant k}
the set of elements that are products of at most
k
commutators. For an element
g\in[G,G]
, we denote by
l_{G}(g)
its commutator length, i.e. the smallest integer
k
such that
g\in[G,G]_{\leqslant k}
. If the group in question is clear from the context, then we will write just
l(g)
.

By
Z(G)
we denote the centre of the group
G
.

Set

c=\sup l_{D^{*}}(\xi)\text{,}\qquad\xi\in[D^{*},D^{*}]\text{.}

Except for the case
n=2
,
D=\mathbb{F}_{2}
, set

C=\sup l_{\mathop{\mathrm{GL}}\nolimits(n,D)}(g)\text{,}\qquad g\in E(n,D)\setminus Z(E(n,D))\text{.}

For
n\geqslant 3
, we also set

C^{\prime}=\sup l_{E(n,D)}(g)\text{,}\qquad g\in E(n,D)\setminus Z(E(n,D))\text{.}

The aim of the present paper is to estimate the numbers
C
and
C^{\prime}
for a given value of
c
.

Let us also note that if
g\in Z(E(n,D))
, then we can decompose it as a product
g=g_{1}g_{2}
, where
g_{2}
is an arbitrary noncentral commutator in
E(n,D)
(say,
g_{2}=[t_{12}(1),t_{21}(1)]
), and
g_{1}=gg_{2}^{-1}
is a noncentral element. Therefore, the commutator length of the element
g
in the group
\mathop{\mathrm{GL}}\nolimits(n,D)
(resp.
E(n,D)
) is not greater than
C+1
(resp.
C^{\prime}+1
).

3.
A lower estimate

In this section we prove the following theorem.

Theorem 1
.

Let
D
be an arbitrary skew-field. Let
\tau\in D^{*}
be such that the matrix
\diag(1,1,\ldots,1,\tau)
is a product of
d
commutators in the group
\mathop{\mathrm{GL}}\nolimits(n,D)
, where
d\geqslant 1
. Then the element
\tau
is a product of at most

d(8n^{2}-13n+8)-2n^{2}+3n-1

commutators in the group
D^{*}
.

From this theorem we obtain the following corollary.

Corollary 1
.

In our notation, if
c\geqslant 1
, then the following inequality holds true:

C\geqslant\frac{c+2n^{2}-3n+1}{8n^{2}-13n+8}\text{.}

The proof consists of several lemmas.

Let us introduce the following notation. By
e_{k}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{n-1}
we denote the row that has one in
k
-th position and zeroes in all the other positions.

We denote by
U_{k}
the subgroup of the group
U
that consists of upper unitriangular matrices with zero in the position
(k,k+1)
. Using
(2)
, it is easy to see that
U
is a semidirect product
U=U_{k}\leftthreetimes X_{k,k+1}
and that
U_{k}^{t_{k+1,k}(\theta)}\leqslant U
for any
\theta\in D
.

The group
V_{k}
is defined similarly. Then
V=V_{k}\leftthreetimes X_{k+1,k}
.

Lemma 1
.

Let
1\leqslant k\leqslant n-1
, let
\xi\in D
. Then the following inclusion holds true:

UVUt_{k+1,k}(\xi)\leqslant H_{\leqslant 2e_{k}}UVU\text{.}

Proof.

Let
u_{1},u_{2}\in U
and
v\in V
. Let us prove that
u_{1}vu_{2}t_{k+1,k}(\xi)\in H_{\leqslant 2e_{k}}UVU
.

The element
u_{2}
can be written as
u_{2}=u_{2}^{\prime}t_{k,k+1}(\zeta)
, where
u_{2}^{\prime}\in U_{k}
. Similarly, the element
v
can be written as
v=v^{\prime}t_{k+1,k}(\eta)
, where
v^{\prime}\in V_{k}
.

Case 1
:
\zeta=0
. Using
(2)
, we obtain

u_{1}vu_{2}t_{k+1,k}(\xi)=u_{1}vu_{2}^{\prime}t_{k+1,k}(\xi)=u_{1}vt_{k+1,k}(\xi)(u_{2}^{\prime})^{t_{k+1,k}(\xi)}\in UVU\text{.}

Case 2
:
\zeta\in D^{*}
, but
\zeta\xi\neq-1
. Using
(3)
, we obtain

u_{1}vu_{2}t_{k+1,k}(\xi)=u_{1}vu_{2}^{\prime}t_{k,k+1}(\zeta)t_{k+1,k}(\xi)=\\
=u_{1}vu_{2}^{\prime}h_{k,k+1}(\zeta)h_{k,k+1}(\zeta^{-1}+\xi)t_{k+1,k}((1+\xi\zeta)\xi)t_{k,k+1}((1+\zeta\xi)^{-1}\zeta)\text{.}

Further, using that the subgroup
H
normalises the subgroups
U
,
V
and
U^{\prime}
we can move the factors
h_{k,k+1}(\zeta_{k})h_{k,k+1}(\zeta_{k}^{-1}+\xi)
to the begining; after that we do the same transformation as in the first case and we obtain
u_{1}vu_{2}t_{k+1,k}(\xi)\in H_{\leqslant 2e_{k}}UVU
.

Case 3
:
\eta\zeta\neq-1
. Acting as in the previous cases, we obtain that
u_{1}vt_{k,k+1}(\zeta)\in H_{\leqslant 2e_{k}}UV
. Therefore,

u_{1}vu_{2}=u_{1}vu_{2}^{\prime}t_{k,k+1}(\zeta)=u_{1}vt_{k,k+1}(\zeta)(u_{2}^{\prime})^{t_{k,k+1}(\zeta)}\in H_{\leqslant 2e_{k}}UV(u_{2}^{\prime})^{t_{k,k+1}(\zeta)}\text{,}

where
(u_{2}^{\prime})^{t_{k,k+1}(\zeta)}\in U_{k}
. Further, applying calculation from the first case, we obtain
u_{1}vu_{2}t_{k+1,k}(\xi)\in H_{\leqslant 2e_{k}}UVU
.

Case 4
:
\zeta\xi=-1
and
\eta\zeta=-1
, i.e.
\eta=\xi
and
\zeta=-\xi^{-1}
. In this case, using
(4)
, we obtain:

u_{1}vu_{2}t_{k+1,k}(\xi)=u_{1}v^{\prime}t_{k+1,k}(\eta)u_{2}^{\prime}t_{k,k+1}(\zeta)t_{k+1,k}(\xi)=\\
=u_{1}v^{\prime}t_{k+1,k}(\xi)t_{k,k+1}(-\xi^{-1})t_{k+1,k}(\xi)(u_{2}^{\prime})^{t_{k,k+1}(-\xi^{-1})t_{k+1,k}(\xi)}=\\
=u_{1}v^{\prime}t_{k,k+1}(-\xi^{-1})t_{k+1,k}(\xi)t_{k,k+1}(-\xi^{-1})(u_{2}^{\prime})^{t_{k,k+1}(-\xi^{-1})t_{k+1,k}(\xi)}\text{.}

As in the firs case, we obtain that
u_{1}v^{\prime}t_{k,k+1}(-\xi^{-1})\in UV
. Further
(u_{2}^{\prime})^{t_{k,k+1}(-\xi^{-1})}\in U_{k}
; hence
(u_{2}^{\prime})^{t_{k,k+1}(-\xi^{-1})t_{k+1,k}(\xi)}\in U
. Therefore,
u_{1}vu_{2}t_{k+1,k}(\xi)\in UVU
.
∎

Let us forget about general linear group for now, and prove two purely group-theoretic lemmas.

Let
G
be an arbitrary group. Recall that for any element
g\in[G,G]
we denote by
l(g)
its commutator length, i.e the smallest
k
such that
g\in[G,G]_{\leqslant k}
.

Lemma 7
.

Let
a_{1}
,
\ldots
,
a_{p}
,
b_{1}
,
\ldots
,
b_{q}\in G
, where
q\geqslant 1
. Set
a=a_{1}^{-1}\ldots a_{p}^{-1}
, and
b=b_{1}^{-1}\ldots b_{q}^{-1}
. Let
w
be a word with letters
a_{1}^{-1}
,
\ldots
,
a_{p}^{-1}
,
b_{1}
,
\ldots
,
b_{q}
, where each letter occur exactly once, and the letters
a_{i}^{-1}
relatively to each other are arranged in indices ascending order. Assume that the element of the group
G
represented by the word
w
is equal to the identity element. Then if at least one of the elements
a
and
b
belongs to the derived subgroup of the group
G
, then the second element also belongs to the derived subgroup and the following inequality holds true:
l(b)\leqslant l(a)+q-1
.

Proof.

We can reduce the problem to the case where the first letter in the word
w
is
b_{1}
. In order to do this reduction we perform the corresponding cyclic permutation of letters in the word
w
, and after that we change the numeration of the elements
a_{i}
(again by cyclic permutation), so that the condition remains true. The element
a
, therefore, became replaced by a conjugate, and its commutator length remains unchanged.

So assume that the letter
b_{1}
is the first one. Consider the sequence of transformations of the word
w
that has
q-1
steps, where on
i
-th step we move the letter
b_{i+1}
to the beginning without changing the relative order of the other letters. One such step can not increase the commutator length of the element represented by the word more than by one. After applying all the transformations we obtain the word that represents the element
b^{-1}a
. Therefore,
b^{-1}a\in[G,G]_{\leqslant q-1}
, which implies the statement in question.

∎

Lemma 8
.

Let
a_{1}
,
\dots
,
a_{k}\in G
be such that
a_{1}\ldots a_{k}=e
. Then the following inequality holds true
l(a_{1}^{-1}\ldots a_{k}^{-1})\leqslant\max(0,k-2)
.

Proof.

For
k=1
the statement is obvious; so assume that
k\geqslant 2
.

It is clear that
l(a_{1}^{-1}\ldots a_{k}^{-1})=l((a_{1}^{-1}\ldots a_{k}^{-1})^{-1})=l(a_{k}\ldots a_{1})
. Further
l(a_{k}\ldots a_{1})=l(a_{1}a_{k}\ldots a_{2})
because these elements are conjugate. Finally, the word
a_{1}a_{k}\ldots a_{2}
can be obtained from the word
a_{1}\ldots a_{k}
by
k-2
transformations, where
i
-th transformation moves the letter
a_{k-i}
to the end without changing the relative order of the other letters. One such step can not increase the commutator length of the element represented by the word more than by one. Therefore,
l(a_{1}a_{k}\ldots a_{2})\leqslant k-2
.
∎

Now let us return to the general linear group over a skew-field. Let
\kappa\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{n-1}
. Set

s(\kappa)=\max(0,\kappa_{1}-2)+\sum_{i=2}^{n-1}\max(0,\kappa_{i}-1)\text{.}

Lemma 9
.

Let
\kappa\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{n-1}
, and let
\tau\in D^{*}
be such that the matrix
g=\diag(1,\ldots,1,\tau)
belongs to
H_{\leqslant\kappa}
. Then
\tau\in[D^{*},D^{*}]_{\leqslant s(\kappa)}
.

Proof.

If
\kappa_{n-1}=0
, then
\tau=1
, and the statement is trivial. Assume that
\kappa_{n-1}\neq 0
. Moreover, without loss of generality, we may assume that
\kappa_{i}\neq 0
for all
i
. Indeed, otherwise consider the largest
k
such that
\kappa_{k}=0
. It is easy to see that in this case we have

\diag(1,\ldots,1,\tau)\in H_{\leqslant(\kappa_{k+1},\ldots,\kappa_{n-1})}\leqslant GL(n-k,D)\text{,}

and we can prove the statement for this element.

So let
\kappa_{i}\neq 0
for all
i
. Let
g
be the product of the elements
h_{i,i+1}(\xi_{i,j})
,
1\leqslant j\leqslant\kappa_{i}
in some order. In addition, we assume that for a fixed
i
these elements relatively to each other are arranged in index
j
ascending order.

Set
\varepsilon_{i}=\xi_{i,1}^{-1}\ldots\xi_{i,\kappa_{i}}^{-1}\in D^{*}
. It follows from Lemma
8
and equality
g_{11}=1
that
l(\varepsilon_{1})\leqslant\max(0,\kappa_{1}-2)
.

For
2\leqslant i\leqslant n-1
it follows from Lemma
7
and equality
g_{i,i}=1
that

l(\varepsilon_{i})\leqslant l(\varepsilon_{i-1})+\max(0,\kappa_{i}-1)\text{.}

Therefore,

l(\tau)=l(\varepsilon_{n-1})=l(\varepsilon_{1})+\sum_{i=2}^{n-1}\left(l(\varepsilon_{i})-l(\varepsilon_{i-1})\right)\leqslant s(\kappa)\text{.}\vskip-25.83325pt

∎

Now we can finish the proof of Theorem
1
. Let

g=\diag(1,\ldots,1,\tau)\in[\mathop{\mathrm{GL}}\nolimits(n,D),\mathop{\mathrm{GL}}\nolimits(n,D)]_{\leqslant d}\text{.}

Using Lemmas
5
,
6
and
9
, we obtain that
\tau\in[D^{*},D^{*}]_{\leqslant s(\kappa^{d})}
; and it is easy to see that

s(\kappa^{d})=d(8n^{2}-13n+8)-2n^{2}+3n-1\text{.}

4.
An upper estimate

In this section we prove th following theorem; its proof is the modification of the proof of the main theorem in
[
4
]
.

Theorem 2
.

Let
D
be a skew-field with the centre
K
. Let
1<\dim_{K}D<\infty
(hence
K
is an infinite field). Then in our notation the following inequality holds true:

C\leqslant\left\lceil\frac{c}{n}\right\rceil\text{,}

where by
\lceil x\rceil
we denote the ceil function of
x
.

For
n\geqslant 3
the following inequality also holds true:

C^{\prime}\leqslant\left\lceil\frac{c}{n-2}\right\rceil\text{.}

In the following two lemmas we assume the condition of Theorem
2
.

Lemma 10
.

Let
c=d_{1}+\ldots+d_{n}
be a partition of the number
c
, where
d_{n}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
. Let
g\in E(n,D)\setminus Z(E(n,D))
. Then there exists
\gamma\in E(n,D)
such that
g^{\gamma}=vhu
, where
v\in V
,
u\in U
and
h=\diag(\varepsilon_{1},\ldots,\varepsilon_{n})
, where
\varepsilon_{i}\in[D^{*},D^{*}]_{\leqslant d_{i}}
for all
1\leqslant i\leqslant n
.

Proof.

Let
k
be the smallest integer such that
d_{k}\neq 0
.

The base of induction is for
k=n
, i.e.
d_{n}=c
. By Theorem 2.1. of
[
4
]
there exists an element
\gamma\in E(n,D)
such that
g^{\gamma}=vhu
, where
v\in V
,
u\in U
and
h=\diag(1,\ldots,1,\varepsilon_{n})
. Since
g\in E(n,D)
, it follows that
\varepsilon_{n}\in[D^{*},D^{*}]=[D^{*},D^{*}]_{\leqslant c}
. The base of induction is proved.

Now let us perform the induction step from
k+1
to
k
. By induction hypothesis there exists
\gamma_{1}\in E(n,D)
such that
g^{\gamma_{1}}=v_{1}h_{1}u_{1}
, where
v_{1}\in V
,
u_{1}\in U
and
h_{1}=\diag(1,\ldots,1,\widetilde{\varepsilon}_{k+1},\ldots,\widetilde{\varepsilon}_{n})
, where
\widetilde{\varepsilon}_{i}\in[D^{*},D^{*}]_{\leqslant d_{i}}
for
i\geqslant k+2
, and
\widetilde{\varepsilon}_{k+1}\in[D^{*},D^{*}]_{\leqslant d_{k}+d_{k+1}}
.

Case 1
:
u_{1}=u_{1}^{\prime}t_{k,k+1}(\zeta)
, where
u_{1}^{\prime}\in U_{k}
and
\zeta\in D^{*}
. We express the element
\widetilde{\varepsilon}_{k+1}
as a product
\widetilde{\varepsilon}_{k+1}=\varepsilon_{k+1}\theta
, where
\varepsilon_{k+1}\in[D^{*},D^{*}]_{\leqslant d_{k+1}}
and
\theta\in[D^{*},D^{*}]_{\leqslant d_{k}}
.

Since
\zeta\in D^{*}
, it follows that
\theta=1+\xi\zeta
for some
\xi\in D
. Set
\gamma=\gamma_{1}t_{k+1,k}(\xi)
. Then, applying
(3)
, it is easy to see that
g^{\gamma}=vhu
, where
v\in V
,
u\in U
and

h=\diag(1,\ldots,1,1+\zeta\xi,\varepsilon_{k+1},\widetilde{\varepsilon}_{k+2},\ldots,\widetilde{\varepsilon}_{n})\text{.}

It remains to notice that the element
1+\zeta\xi
is conjugate to the element
1+\xi\zeta=\theta
; hence it has the same commutator length.

Case 2
:
v_{1}=v_{1}^{\prime}t_{k+1,k}(\zeta)
, where
v_{1}^{\prime}\in V_{k}
and
\zeta\in D^{*}
. The proof for this case is similar to the previous one.

Case 3
:
u_{1}\in U_{k}
and
v_{1}\in V_{k}
. It is clear that if
\widetilde{\varepsilon}_{k+1}=1
, then one can take
\gamma=\gamma_{1}
. Now assume that
\widetilde{\varepsilon}_{k+1}\neq 1
. Set
\gamma_{2}=\gamma_{1}t_{k,k+1}(1)
. Then

g^{\gamma_{2}}=t_{k,k+1}(-1)v_{1}h_{1}u_{1}t_{k,k+1}(1)=v_{1}^{t_{k,k+1}(1)}h_{1}[h_{1}^{-1},t_{k,k+1}(-1)]u_{1}^{t_{k,k+1}(1)}\text{,}

where
v_{1}^{t_{k,k+1}(1)}\in V
,
u_{1}^{t_{k,k+1}(1)}\in U_{k}
; and since
\widetilde{\varepsilon}_{k+1}\neq 1
, it follows that
[h_{1}^{-1},t_{k,k+1}(-1)]=t_{k,k+1}(\zeta)
, where
\zeta\in D^{*}
. Therefore, the problem is reduced to the first case.
∎

Lemma 11
.

Let
g=vhu\in\mathop{\mathrm{GL}}\nolimits(n,D)
, where
v\in V
,
u\in U
and
h=\diag(\varepsilon_{1},\ldots,\varepsilon_{n})
, where
\varepsilon_{i}\in[D^{*},D^{*}]_{\leqslant 1}
. Then
g\in[\mathop{\mathrm{GL}}\nolimits(n,D),\mathop{\mathrm{GL}}\nolimits(n,D)]_{\leqslant 1}
.

If, in addition, the elements
\varepsilon_{1}
and
\varepsilon_{2}
are equal to one, then
g\in[E(n,D),E(n,D)]_{\leqslant 1}
.

Proof.

Let
\varepsilon_{i}=[a_{i},b_{i}]
, where
a_{i}
,
b_{i}\in D^{*}
. If
\varepsilon_{1}=\varepsilon_{2}=1
, then we may assume that
a_{2}
,
b_{1}\in K
,
a_{1}=(a_{2}\ldots a_{n})^{-1}
, and
b_{2}=(b_{1}b_{3}\ldots b_{n})^{-1}
.

Since the field
K
is infinite, it follows that replacing for all
i\geqslant 2
the elements
a_{i}
by
t_{i}a_{i}
and also replacing
a_{1}
by
(t_{2}\ldots t_{n})^{-1}a_{1}
for suitable
t_{i}\in K^{*}
we may assume that the reduced norms of the elements
a_{i}
are pairwise distinct. The elements
\varepsilon_{i}
remains the same after such a replacing. The condition
a_{1}=(a_{2}\ldots a_{n})^{-1}
is preserved if it were assumed.

Set

\displaystyle h_{1}=\diag(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})\text{,}\qquad\tau=\diag(b_{1},\ldots,b_{n})\text{,}

\displaystyle h_{2}=\tau h_{1}^{-1}\tau^{-1}=\diag(b_{1}a_{1}^{-1}b^{-1},\ldots,b_{n}a_{n}^{-1}b_{n}^{-1})\text{.}

Therefore
h=h_{1}h_{2}
. Since the reduced norms of the elements
a_{i}
, and hence of the elements
b_{i}a_{i}b_{i}^{-1}
, are pairwise distinct, it follows that the elements
h_{1}
and
h_{2}
are
D
-regular (see Definition 3.3 and Proposition 3.4 in
[
4
]
). By Proposition 4.1 in
[
4
]
there exist
u^{\prime}\in U
and
v^{\prime}\in V
such that
v=[v^{\prime},h_{1}]
and
u=[h_{2}^{-1},u^{\prime}]
.

Similarly to the proof of the main theorem in
[
4
]
, we obtain that

g=vhu=v^{\prime}h_{1}v^{\prime-1}u^{\prime}h_{2}u^{\prime-1}=v^{\prime}h_{1}v^{\prime-1}u^{\prime}\tau h_{1}^{-1}\tau^{-1}u^{\prime-1}=\\
=[v^{\prime}h_{1}v^{\prime-1},u^{\prime}\tau v^{-1}]\in[\mathop{\mathrm{GL}}\nolimits(n,D),\mathop{\mathrm{GL}}\nolimits(n,D)]_{\leqslant 1}\text{.}

Moreover, if
\varepsilon_{1}=\varepsilon_{2}=1
, then according to our assumptions
h_{1}
,
\tau\in E(n,D)
; hence
g\in[E(n,D),E(n,D)]_{\leqslant 1}
.
∎

Let us finish the proof of Theorem
2
. Let
g\in E(n,D)\setminus Z(E(n,D))
. We prove that
g\in[\mathop{\mathrm{GL}}\nolimits(n,D),\mathop{\mathrm{GL}}\nolimits(n,D)]_{\lceil\frac{c}{n}\rceil}
.

By Lemma
10
we may assume that
g=vhu
, where
v\in V
,
u\in U
and
h=\diag(\varepsilon_{1},\ldots,\varepsilon_{n})
, where
\varepsilon_{i}\in[D^{*},D^{*}]_{\leqslant\lceil\frac{c}{n}\rceil}
for all
1\leqslant i\leqslant n
. For any
i
let
\varepsilon_{i}=\varepsilon_{i}^{\prime}\varepsilon_{i}^{\prime\prime}
, where
\varepsilon_{i}^{\prime}\in[D^{*},D^{*}]_{\leqslant\lceil\frac{c}{n}\rceil-1}
and
\varepsilon_{i}^{\prime\prime}\in[D^{*},D^{*}]_{\leqslant 1}
. Set
h^{\prime}=\diag(\varepsilon_{1}^{\prime},\ldots^{\prime},\varepsilon_{n}^{\prime})
and
h^{\prime\prime}=\diag(\varepsilon_{1}^{\prime\prime},\ldots,\varepsilon_{n}^{\prime\prime})
. We also set
\widetilde{v}=v^{h^{\prime}}
.

Therefore, we have
g=vhu=h^{\prime}\widetilde{v}h^{\prime\prime}u
. By Lemma
11
we obtain that
\widetilde{v}h^{\prime\prime}u\in[\mathop{\mathrm{GL}}\nolimits(n,D),\mathop{\mathrm{GL}}\nolimits(n,D)]_{\leqslant 1}
. It is also clear that
h^{\prime}\in[\mathop{\mathrm{GL}}\nolimits(n,D),\mathop{\mathrm{GL}}\nolimits(n,D)]_{\leqslant\lceil\frac{c}{n}\rceil-1}
. Therefore,
g\in[\mathop{\mathrm{GL}}\nolimits(n,D),\mathop{\mathrm{GL}}\nolimits(n,D)]_{\lceil\frac{c}{n}\rceil}
.

Let
n\geqslant 3
. Let us prove that
g\in[E(n,D),E(n,D)]_{\lceil\frac{c}{n-2}\rceil}
.

By Lemma
10
we may assume that
g=vhu
, where
v\in V
,
u\in U
and
h=\diag(1,1,\varepsilon_{3},\ldots,\varepsilon_{n})
, where
\varepsilon_{i}\in[D^{*},D^{*}]_{\leqslant\lceil\frac{c}{n-2}\rceil}
for all
3\leqslant i\leqslant n
. Similarly to the previous calculation, for any
i
let
\varepsilon_{i}=\varepsilon_{i}^{\prime}\varepsilon_{i}^{\prime\prime}
, where
\varepsilon_{i}^{\prime}\in[D^{*},D^{*}]_{\leqslant\lceil\frac{c}{n-2}\rceil-1}
and
\varepsilon_{i}^{\prime\prime}\in[D^{*},D^{*}]_{\leqslant 1}
. Set
h^{\prime}=\diag(1,1\varepsilon_{3}^{\prime},\ldots,\varepsilon_{n}^{\prime})
and
h^{\prime\prime}=\diag(1,1,\varepsilon_{3}^{\prime\prime},\ldots,\varepsilon_{n}^{\prime\prime})
. We also set
\widetilde{v}=v^{h^{\prime}}
.

As before,
g=h^{\prime}\widetilde{v}h^{\prime\prime}u
. By Lemma
11
we have
\widetilde{v}h^{\prime\prime}u\in[E(n,D),E(n,D)]_{\leqslant 1}
. It remains to show that
h^{\prime}\in[E(n,D),E(n,D)]_{\leqslant\lceil\frac{c}{n-2}\rceil-1}
.

For simplicity we set
d=\lceil\frac{c}{n-2}\rceil-1
. Let
\varepsilon_{i}^{\prime}=[a_{i,1},b_{i,1}]\ldots[a_{i,d},b_{i,d}]
. Set

\displaystyle h_{a,k}=\diag((a_{3,k}\ldots a_{n,k})^{-1},1,a_{3,k},\ldots,a_{n,k})\text{,}

\displaystyle h_{b,k}=\diag(1,(b_{3,k}\ldots b_{n,k})^{-1},b_{3,k},\ldots,b_{n,k})\text{.}

Therefore,
h^{\prime}=[h_{a,1},h_{b,1}]\ldots[h_{a,d},h_{b,d}]\in[E(n,D),E(n,D)]_{\leqslant d}
.

5.
Expressing matrices as one commutator

Consider the following corollaries of our results concerning the question on expressing any noncentral element of the group
E(n,D)
as one commutator.

Firstly, from the Corollary
1
we obtain the following corollary.

Corollary 2
.

Let
D
be an arbitrary skew-field. Then for any noncentral element of the group
E(n,D)
to be one commutator in
\mathop{\mathrm{GL}}\nolimits(n,D)
, it is necessary that any element of the derived subgroup of the group
D^{*}
were a product of at most
6n^{2}-10n+7
commutators.

Secondly, from Theorem
2
we obtain the following corollaries.

Corollary 3
.

Let
D
be a skew-field and
K
be its centre. Let
1<\dim_{K}D<\infty
. Then for any noncentral element of the group
E(n,D)
to be one commutator in
\mathop{\mathrm{GL}}\nolimits(n,D)
, it is sufficient that any element of the derived subgroup of the group
D^{*}
were a product of at most
n
commutators.

Corollary 4
.

Let
D
be a skew-field and
K
be its centre. Let
1<\dim_{K}D<\infty
, and let
n\geqslant 3
. Then for any noncentral element of the group
E(n,D)
to be one commutator in
E(n,D)
, it is sufficient that any element of the derived subgroup of the group
D^{*}
were a product of at most
n-2
commutators.

We denote by
\mathop{\mathrm{GL}}\nolimits(D)
the stable general linear group, i.e.
\mathop{\mathrm{GL}}\nolimits(D)=\varinjlim\mathop{\mathrm{GL}}\nolimits(n,D)
, where the limit is taken with respect to the inclusions.

\mathop{\mathrm{GL}}\nolimits(n,D)\to\mathop{\mathrm{GL}}\nolimits(n+1,D)\text{,}\qquad g\mapsto\begin{pmatrix}g&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}\text{.}

The subgroup
E(D)\leqslant\mathop{\mathrm{GL}}\nolimits(D)
is defined as generated by elementary transvections. In other words,
E(D)=\varinjlim E(n,D)
.

In case where
c<\infty
, the following corollary follows directly from the previous one. However, using the proof of Theorem
2
, we can see that the condition
c<\infty
can be lifted.

Corollary 5
.

Let
D
be a skew-field and
K
be its centre. Let
1<\dim_{K}D<\infty
. Then any element of the group
E(D)
is one commutator in
E(D)
.

Proof.

Let
g\in E(D)
. Since the centre of the group
\mathop{\mathrm{GL}}\nolimits(D)
is trivial, it follows that there exists
n>2
such that
g\in E(n,D)\setminus Z(E(n,D))
.

By Theorem 2.1. in
[
4
]
, which we refer in the proof of Lemma
10
, we may assume that
g=vhu
, where
v\in V
,
u\in U
and
h=\diag(1,\ldots,1,\tau)
. Since
g\in E(n,D)
, it follows that
\tau\in[D^{*},D^{*}]
; hence
\tau\in[D^{*},D^{*}]_{\leqslant d}
for some
d
.

Increasing the number
n
, if necessary, and conjugating
g
by a permutation matrix in such a way that the previous assumption remains true, we may assume that
n\geqslant d+2
.

In this case, it follows from the proof of Lemma
10
that the element
g
is conjugate to the element
g_{1}=v_{1}h_{1}u_{1}
, where
v_{1}\in V
,
u_{1}\in U
and
h_{1}=\diag(1,1,\varepsilon_{3}\ldots,\varepsilon_{n})
, where
\varepsilon_{i}\in[D^{*},D^{*}]_{\leqslant 1}
.

Then by Lemma
11
, the element
g_{1}
, and hence the element
g
, is one commutator in
E(n,D)
.
∎
