Implicit energy regularization of neural ordinary-differential-equation control
Lucas Böttcher, Nino Antulov-Fantulin, Thomas Asikis

Abstract
Although optimal control problems of dynamical systems can be formulated within the framework of variational calculus, their solution for complex systems is often analytically and computationally intractable. In this Letter we present a versatile neural ordinary-differential-equation control (NODEC) framework with implicit energy regularization and use it to obtain neural-network-generated control signals that can steer dynamical systems towards a desired target state within a predefined amount of time. We demonstrate the ability of NODEC to learn control signals that closely resemble those found by corresponding optimal control frameworks in terms of control energy and deviation from the desired target state. Our results suggest that NODEC is capable to solve a wide range of control and optimization problems, including those that are analytically intractable.



The problem of how to optimally control complex systems has its roots in dynamical systems and optimization theory
Kalman et al. 1960
;
Hautus 1969
;
Lin 1974
;
Liu and Barabási 2016
. Mathematically, a dynamical system is said to be “controllable” if it can be steered from any initial state
{{\boldsymbol{{x}}}}_{0}
to any target state
{{{{\boldsymbol{{x}}}}^{*}}}
in finite time
T
. Controlling complex dynamical systems is relevant in many applications such as (i) development of efficient and robust near-term quantum devices
Mabuchi 2009
;
Dong and Petersen 2010
, (ii) regulatory network control
Göttgens 2015
in cellular biology, (iii) power-grid management
Schäfer et al. 2018
, (iv) design of stable financial systems
Delpini et al. 2013
, and (v) epidemic management
Choi and Shim 2021
.

Historically, an early work by Kalman in the 1960s led to the formulation of an analytical condition for the controllability of linear systems based on the rank of the controllability matrix
Kalman et al. 1960
. An equivalent condition, the so-called Popov–Belevitch–Hautus test
Hautus 1969
, characterizes controllability of a linear system via its eigenmodes. More recently, concepts from the framework of structural controllability
Lin 1974
have been used to control complex networks
Liu et al. 2011
with a minimum set of control inputs (
i.e.
, driver nodes) that can be determined by identifying all unmatched nodes in a maximum matching problem. The direct application of this framework to general network controllability problems is, however, complicated by several factors
Pasqualetti et al. 2014
. First, finding the minimum set of driver nodes for an arbitrary network is NP hard
Olshevsky 2014
. Second, the design of an appropriate control signal is not specified in
Liu et al. 2011
and its implementation may not be realizable in practice
Pasqualetti et al. 2014
. Third, in the presence of nodal self-dynamics, which were not included in the controllability framework
Liu et al. 2011
, a single time-varying input is sufficient to achieve structural controllability
Cowan et al. 2012
, challenging the findings of
Liu et al. 2011
.

The solution of general optimal control problems is based on two main approaches: (i) Pontryagin’s maximum principle
McShane 1989
(necessary condition),
which is a boundary-value problem in a Hamiltonian framework or
(ii) solving the Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman (HJB) partial-differential equation (necessary and sufficient condition)
Zhou 1990
. Since the HJB equation usually does not admit smooth solutions
Frankowska 1989
, different approximate dynamic programming methods are used
Abu-Khalaf and Lewis 2005
;
Bellman and Dreyfus 2015
.

In this Letter, we study the ability of neural ordinary-differential-equation control (NODEC)
Asikis et al. 2020
to steer different dynamical processes towards desired target states without explicitly accounting for an energy-regularization term in the corresponding loss function.
The proposed NODEC framework extends the neural ODE formalism
Chen et al. 2018
to general control problems and automatically learns control signals that steer the evolution of an underlying networked dynamical system. Using analytical and numerical arguments, we show why NODEC is able to closely resemble the control energy of optimal control through an implicit energy regularization, resulting from the interplay of neural network initizalization and an induced gradient descent.

Neural-network control.

Before outlining the basic principles of NODEC, we first provide a mathematical formulation of the control problem of networked dynamical systems. We consider a network that consists of
N
nodes whose states are represented by the state vector
{\boldsymbol{{x}}}(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
. Initially, nodes are in state
{\boldsymbol{{x}}}(0)
and steered towards a target state
{{{{\boldsymbol{{x}}}}^{*}}}
at time
T
(
i.e.
,
{\boldsymbol{{{\boldsymbol{{x}}}}}}(T)={{{{\boldsymbol{{x}}}}^{*}}}
) by means of suitable control inputs. Interactions between nodes are described by the dynamical system

\dot{{\boldsymbol{{x}}}}(t)=f({\boldsymbol{{x}}}(t),{\boldsymbol{{u}}}(t))

(1)

and subject to the constraint that the control function
{\boldsymbol{{u}}}(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
minimizes the cost function

J=\int_{0}^{T}L({\boldsymbol{{x}}}(t^{\prime}),{\boldsymbol{{u}}}(t^{\prime}))\,dt^{\prime}+C({\boldsymbol{{x}}}(T))\,.

(2)

The function
f\colon\mathbb{R}^{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{N}
in Eq. (
1
) accounts for both the interactions between nodes
1,\dots,N
and the influence of external control inputs
{\boldsymbol{{u}}}(t)
on the dynamics. Note that the number of control inputs
M
is smaller than or equal to
N
. For linear systems, we describe node-node interactions and external control inputs by
f({\boldsymbol{{x}}},{\boldsymbol{{u}}})=A{\boldsymbol{{x}}}+B{\boldsymbol{{u}}}
. The first term in Eq. (
2
) is the integrated cost over the control horizon
T
,
e.g.
, the control energy

E_{T}[{\boldsymbol{{u}}}]=\int_{0}^{T}\left\lVert{\boldsymbol{{u}}}(t^{\prime})\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\,dt^{\prime}

(3)

if
L=\left\lVert{\boldsymbol{{u}}}(t^{\prime})\right\rVert_{2}^{2}
.
C({\boldsymbol{{x}}}(T))
is the final cost (or bequest value). Most common formulations of optimal control include the control-energy term (
3
) directly in the cost function
Yan et al. 2012
;
Sun and Motter 2013
. This approach corresponds to an explicit minimization of the control energy.

In NODEC, we take a complementary approach to reach a desired target state
{{{{\boldsymbol{{x}}}}^{*}}}
in finite time
T
and proceed in two steps. First, we approximate and solve the dynamical system in terms of neural ODEs
Chen et al. 2018
. In particular, we describe the control input
{\boldsymbol{{u}}}(t)
by a neural network with weight vector
{\boldsymbol{{w}}}
such that the corresponding control-input representation is
\hat{{\boldsymbol{{u}}}}(t;{\boldsymbol{{w}}})
. Second, we use a suitable loss function
J({\boldsymbol{{x}}},{{{{\boldsymbol{{x}}}}^{*}}})
and a gradient-descent algorithm to iteratively determine the weight vector
{\boldsymbol{{w}}}
according to
Asikis et al. 2020

{\boldsymbol{{w}}}^{(n+1)}={\boldsymbol{{w}}}^{(n)}-\eta\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{{w}}}^{(n)}}J({\boldsymbol{{x}}},{{{{\boldsymbol{{x}}}}^{*}}}),

(4)

where the superscript indicates the current number of gradient-descent steps, and
\eta
is the learning rate. For the loss function
J(\cdot)
, we use the mean-squared error

J({\boldsymbol{{x}}}(T),{{{{\boldsymbol{{x}}}}^{*}}})=\frac{1}{N}\left\lVert{\boldsymbol{{x}}}(T)-{{{{\boldsymbol{{x}}}}^{*}}}\right\rVert^{2}_{2}.

(5)

In order to calculate
\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{{w}}}^{(n)}}J(\cdot)
, we use automatic differentiation methods
Baydin et al. 2018
, where the gradients “flow” through the underlying neural network, that is time-unfolded
Schäfer and Zimmermann 2006
by ODE solvers
Shampine 2018
. We will show in the following paragraphs that, even without including the energy cost (
3
) in the loss function (
5
), NODEC approximates optimal control by minimizing the control energy (
3
). All neural-network architectures, hyperparameters, and numerical solvers are reported in the Supplemental Information (SI).

Implicit energy regularization.

To provide insights into the observed implicit energy regularization of NODEC [Fig.
1
(b)], we show that a gradient descent in the neural-network weights
{\boldsymbol{{w}}}
induces a gradient descent in the control input
\hat{{\boldsymbol{{u}}}}(t;{\boldsymbol{{w}}})
.

The evolution of the state vector
{\boldsymbol{{x}}}(t)
is described by Eq. (
1
) and is a function of
\hat{{\boldsymbol{{u}}}}(t;{\boldsymbol{{w}}})
. We now expand
\hat{{\boldsymbol{{u}}}}(t;{\boldsymbol{{w}}}^{(n+1)})=\hat{{\boldsymbol{{u}}}}(t;{\boldsymbol{{w}}}^{(n)}+\Delta{\boldsymbol{{w}}}^{(n)})
with
\Delta{\boldsymbol{{w}}}^{(n)}=-\eta\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{{w}}}^{(n)}}J
for small
\Delta{\boldsymbol{{w}}}^{(n)}
while keeping
t
constant. This expansion yields

\hat{{\boldsymbol{{u}}}}(t;{\boldsymbol{{w}}}^{(n+1)})=\hat{{\boldsymbol{{u}}}}(t;{\boldsymbol{{w}}}^{(n)})+\mathcal{J}_{\hat{{\boldsymbol{{u}}}}}\Delta{\boldsymbol{{w}}}^{(n)},

(9)

where
\mathcal{J}_{\hat{{\boldsymbol{{u}}}}}
is the Jacobian of
\hat{{\boldsymbol{{u}}}}
with elements
(\mathcal{J}_{\hat{{\boldsymbol{{u}}}}})_{ij}=\partial\hat{{\boldsymbol{{u}}}}_{i}/\partial{\boldsymbol{{w}}}_{j}
. Note that we can make
\Delta{\boldsymbol{{w}}}^{(n)}
arbitrarily small by using a small learning rate
\eta
.

Since
\Delta{\boldsymbol{{w}}}^{(n)}\propto\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{{w}}}^{(n)}}J
and
\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{{w}}}^{(n)}}J=\mathcal{J}_{\hat{{\boldsymbol{{u}}}}}^{T}\nabla_{\hat{{\boldsymbol{{u}}}}}J
, we obtain

\hat{{\boldsymbol{{u}}}}(t;{\boldsymbol{{w}}}^{(n+1)})=\hat{{\boldsymbol{{u}}}}(t;{\boldsymbol{{w}}}^{(n)})-\eta\mathcal{J}_{\hat{{\boldsymbol{{u}}}}}\mathcal{J}_{\hat{{\boldsymbol{{u}}}}}^{T}\nabla_{\hat{{\boldsymbol{{u}}}}}J.

(10)

According to Eq. (
10
), a gradient descent in
{\boldsymbol{{w}}}
[Eq. (
4
)] may induce a gradient descent in
\hat{{\boldsymbol{{u}}}}
, where the square matrix
\mathcal{J}_{\hat{{\boldsymbol{{u}}}}}\mathcal{J}_{\hat{{\boldsymbol{{u}}}}}^{T}
acts as a linear transformation on
\nabla_{\hat{{\boldsymbol{{u}}}}}J
.

To better understand the implications of this result, we briefly summarize the control steps of NODEC. As described in the prior paragraphs and as illustrated in Fig.
1
(a), NODEC starts with a small initial control signal
\hat{{\boldsymbol{{u}}}}^{(0)}(t;{\boldsymbol{{w}}}^{(0)})
, then integrates the dynamical system (
1
), and performs a gradient descent in
{\boldsymbol{{w}}}
according to Eq. (
4
). The closer the final state
{\boldsymbol{{x}}}(T)
to the target state
{{{{\boldsymbol{{x}}}}^{*}}}
, the smaller the loss (
5
) and the change in
{\boldsymbol{{w}}}
[and in
\hat{{\boldsymbol{{u}}}}
due to Eq. (
10
)]. If we initialize NODEC with a sufficiently small control input and learning rate, it will produce control trajectories that follow the vector field of the dynamical system in a “go-with-the-flow” manner and slowly adapt
\hat{{\boldsymbol{{u}}}}
to reach the desired target state. Because of the induced gradient descent (
10
), the resulting control approximates OC methods that minimize the control energy [see the comparison between the final control energy of OC and NODEC in Figs.
1
(b,d)]. This way of controlling dynamical systems is markedly different from standard (optimal) control formulations
Brogan 1991
that are, for instance, based on Pontryagin’s maximum principle and require one to explicitly minimize the control energy by including
\left\lVert{\boldsymbol{{u}}}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}
in the Hamiltonian and solving the adjoint system
Brogan 1991
. NODEC thus provides a complementary approach for solving general control problems.

Concluding remarks.

We used NODEC, a control framework that is based on neural ODEs, to steer linear and non-linear networked dynamical systems into desired target states. For the considered linear dynamics, we compared NODEC with the corresponding analytical optimal-control solution and found that NODEC is not only able to drive the dynamical system into a desired target state, but also is able to approximate the optimal-control energy. We supported this observation with analytical arguments and further compared NODEC with an optimal-control method for synchronizing oscillators in different networks, again showing that NODEC is able to approximate the optimal-control energy. Neural ODE control frameworks are very versatile, complement existing control approaches, and may be useful to solve complex and analytically intractable control problems.
