On the Polyhedrality of the Chvátal-Gomory Closure
Haoran Zhu

Abstract
In this paper, we provide an equivalent condition for the Chvátal-Gomory (CG) closure of a closed convex set to be finitely-generated. Using this result, we are able to prove that, for any closed convex set that can be written as the Minkowski sum of a compact convex set and a closed convex cone, its CG closure is a rational polyhedron if and only if its recession cone is a rational polyhedral cone. As a consequence, this generalizes and unifies all the currently known results, for the case of rational polyhedron [ 24 ] and compact convex set [ 9 ] . Key words: Chvátal-Gomory closure ⋅ \cdot polyhedral ⋅ \cdot cutting-planes



1
Introduction

Cutting-plane method is one of the most fundamental techniques for solving (mixed) integer programming problems, and often times in practice, it is combined with the branch-and-bound method.
Since the early days of Integer Programming (IP), numerous types of cutting-planes have been introduced and studied in the literature, several of them have also been widely implemented into commercial solvers.
Among those cuts, Chvàtal-Gomory (CG) cut (
[
18
,
5
]
) was the first cutting-plane that has ever been proposed, and various interesting results have been obtained from both the theoretical and practical point of view (see, e.g.,
[
6
,
3
,
15
]
).

One of the theoretical questions regarding to CG cut is, what are the structural properties of the region obtained from intersecting all of those cuts? In the terminology of cutting-plane theory, this region is referred to as
Chvàtal-Gomory (CG) closure
.
Although the definition of CG cut has traditionally been defined with respect to a rational polyhedron for an Integer Linear Programming (ILP) problem, they straightforwardly generalize to the nonlinear setting and hence can be used for convex Integer Nonlinear Programming (INLP). Let
K
be a closed convex set and let
\sigma_{K}
denote the support function of
K:\sigma_{K}(c)=\sup_{x\in K}cx
. For the ease of notation, here we abbreviate the inner product
c^{T}x
as
cx
. Given
c\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
, the CG cut for
K
that is derived from
c
is defined as:

cx\leq\lfloor\sigma_{K}(c)\rfloor.

Then, the so-called CG closure of
K
is:

K^{\prime}:=\bigcap_{c\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}}\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mid cx\leq\lfloor\sigma_{K}(c)\rfloor\}.

Here the CG closure
K^{\prime}
is essentially obtained from the intersection of potentially infinitely many half-spaces, hence the polyhedrality of
K^{\prime}
is unclear.
As named in
[
1
]
, here we call
K^{\prime}
is
finitely-generated
, if there exists a finite set
F\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{n}
, such that

K^{\prime}=\bigcap_{f\in F}\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mid fx\leq\lfloor\sigma_{K}(f)\rfloor\}.

Obviously, a finitely-generated CG closure is a rational polyhedron.
Four decades ago, Schrijver
[
24
]
shows that, when
K
is a rational polyhedron,
K^{\prime}
is finitely-generated. Schrijver further asks the following question: when
K
is an irrational polytope, is
K^{\prime}
still a (rational) polytope?

As attempts to fully understand this question, a series of studies have been conducted for the polyhedrality of CG closure of various convex sets. In
[
12
]
, Dey and Vielma show that, the CG closure of a bounded full-dimensional ellipsoid, described by rational data, is a rational polytope. In
[
8
]
, Dadush, Dey and Vielma show the CG closure of a set obtained as an intersection of a strictly convex body and a rational polyhedron is a polyhedron. Along this line of work, in
[
9
]
, the same group of authors extend the same result to compact convex sets, therefore giving affirmation answer to the long-standing open problem raised by Schrijver. Almost simultaneously this problem was also proved by Dunkel and Schulz
[
14
]
independently, where they specifically prove for the case of irrational polytope, instead of a more general compact convex set. All their proofs are very much involved, a few years later Braun and Pokutta
[
4
]
give a short proof for the same result as
[
9
]
. However,
no matter how different these proofs might seem, they all share some high-level similarities. For example, they all rely heavily on the
homogeneity property
of CG closure:
F^{\prime}=K^{\prime}\cap F
for any face
F
of
K
. By inductive hypothesis that
F^{\prime}
is rational polyhedral and some additional argument, they will be able to obtain the polyhedrality of
K^{\prime}
.
As we will see later, in contrast to all these work in the literature, we are taking a completely different perspective and do not make use of the homogeneity property of CG closure. Key is here a characterization result for general cutting-plane closures from which a fundamental Theorem
1
is derived. We believe that the basic proof technique here lends itself to potentially many more classes of cutting-planes.

Now we highlight the main results of this paper in the next section.

1.1
Main Results

With respect to the CG closure of general closed convex set, we have the first main result:

Theorem 1
.

Given a closed convex set
K
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, then
K^{\prime}
is finitely-generated if and only if there exists a finite subset
F\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{n}
, such that
\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mid fx\leq\lfloor\sigma_{K}(f)\rfloor,\forall f\in F\right\}\subseteq K.

Based on this above theorem, we are able to prove the following result.

Theorem 2
.

If
K
is a Motzkin-decomposable set, then the following statements are equivalent:

1.

K^{\prime}
is a finitely-generated.

2.

K^{\prime}
is a rational polyhedron.

3.

K
has rational polyhedral recession cone.

Here a set
K\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
is called
Motzkin-decomposable
(see, e.g.,
[
17
,
20
]
), if there exist a compact convex set
C
and a closed convex cone
D
such that
K=C+D
.
By Minkowski-Weyl theorem, a polyhedron is a Motzkin-decomposable set, thus the last theorem generalizes and unifies all the currently known results for rational polyhedron and compact convex set.
Moreover, using the characterization result in
[
11
]
for the polyhedrality of integer hull, we can immediately obtain the next result as a corollary:

Corollary 1
.

If
K
is a Motzkin-decomposable set in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and contains integer points in its interior, then
K^{\prime}
is a rational polyhedron if and only if
\operatorname{conv}(K\cap\mathbb{Z}^{n})
is a polyhedron.

Here
\operatorname{conv}(K\cap\mathbb{Z}^{n})
is called the
integer hull
of
K
.
As a footnote in
[
9
]
, the authors wrote the following sentences to justify the reason why they focus on the case of a compact convex set:

“
If the convex hull of integer points in a convex set is not polyhedral, then the CG closure cannot be expected to be polyhedral. Since we do not have a good understanding of when this holds for unbounded convex set, we restrict our attention here to the CG closure of compact convex sets.
”

Therefore, our Corollary
1
directly addresses their concern.

This paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
we present some characterizations for the polyhedrality of general cutting-plane closure and some preliminary results that will be used later. In Section
3
, we verify Theorem
1
, and in Section
4
, we verify Theorem
2
and Corollary
1
.

Notations and assumptions.

For any
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and a linear subspace
L\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we denote by
\operatorname{proj}_{L}x
the orthogonal projection of
x
onto
L
, and for any
X\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n},\operatorname{proj}_{L}X:=\{\operatorname{proj}_{L}x\mid x\in X\}
. For any set
S\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n},\operatorname{lin}(S):=S\cap(-S)
denotes the
lineality space
of
S
, which is the largest linear subspace contained in
S
.

\operatorname{cone}(S):=\{\sum_{i=1}^{k}\lambda_{i}s_{i}\mid\forall k\in\mathbb{N},\lambda_{i}\geq 0,s_{i}\in S\ \forall i\in[k]\}
denotes the
conical hull
of
S
, and
(S)_{+}:=\{\lambda s\mid\forall\lambda\geq 0,s\in S\}
denotes the cone that contains all non-negative multiplication of elements in
S
.
For a linear subspace
L
, we denote by
L^{\perp}
the orthogonal complement of
L
. For a closed convex set
K,\operatorname{rec}(K):=\{r\mid k+\lambda r\in K,\forall k\in K,\lambda\geq 0\}
denotes the
recession cone
of
K
, and
\operatorname{ext}(K)
denotes the set of extreme points of
K
.

O_{\epsilon}(x^{*})=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mid\|x-x^{*}\|\leq\epsilon\}
denotes the
\epsilon
-ball centered at
x^{*}
in its ambient space. Throughout, all norm
\|\cdot\|
refers to the Euclidean norm. For a matrix
M
, we denote by
\ker(M)
the kernel of
M
.

2
Preliminary Results

The well-known Dickson’s lemma will be used in our later proof, and it also played an important role in some other relevant closure papers, see, e.g.,
[
1
,
10
,
28
]
.

Lemma 1

(Dickson’s lemma
[
13
]
)
.

For any
X\subseteq\mathbb{N}^{n}
, the partially-ordered set (poset)
(X,\leq)
has no infinite antichain.

In order theory, an
antichain (chain)
is a subset of a poset such that any two distinct elements in the subset are incomparable (comparable).

Now we define a new concept for the convergence of rays in a cone.

Definition 1
.

Given a sequence
\{\alpha^{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\alpha^{*}\neq 0\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, if there exists
\{\lambda_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}>0
such that
\lim_{i\rightarrow\infty}\lambda_{i}\alpha^{i}=\alpha^{*}
, then we say
\{\alpha^{i}\}

conically converges
to
\alpha^{*}
, or
\alpha^{i}\xrightarrow{c}\alpha^{*}
.

For the conical convergence, we have the following easy result.

Lemma 2
.

Given a sequence
\{\alpha^{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that
\alpha^{i}\xrightarrow{c}\alpha^{*}
and
\alpha^{i}\xrightarrow{c}\beta^{*}
when
i\rightarrow\infty
, then there exists
\lambda>0,
such that
\alpha^{*}=\lambda\beta^{*}.

Proof.

By assumption, we know there exists
\{\gamma_{i}\},\{\mu_{i}\}\subseteq\mathbb{R}_{+}
, such that
\gamma_{i}\alpha^{i}\rightarrow\alpha^{*},\mu_{i}\alpha^{i}\rightarrow\beta^{*}
.
Let
\beta^{i}:=\mu_{i}\alpha^{i}
. Then we have:
\beta^{i}\rightarrow\beta^{*},\frac{\gamma_{i}}{\mu_{i}}\beta^{i}\rightarrow\alpha^{*}
. Hence
\frac{\gamma_{i}}{\mu_{i}}\rightarrow\frac{\|\alpha^{*}\|}{\|\beta^{*}\|}
, and
\alpha^{*}=\frac{\|\alpha^{*}\|}{\|\beta^{*}\|}\beta^{*}
.
∎

In a recent paper
[
28
]
, the authors study the equivalent condition for a general cutting-plane closure to be polyhedral. In this section, we will follow the same notations and definitions as in
[
28
]
, and exploit the characterization results therein to derive new results for CG closure. For the completeness of this paper, we will include the proofs for those results in the Appendix.

Given a family of cutting-planes
\alpha x\leq\beta
for any
(\alpha,\beta)\in\Omega
, it is referred to as “a family of cuts given by
\Omega
”. Then the corresponding
(cutting-plane) closure
is defined as:

\mathscr{I}(\Omega):=\bigcap_{(\alpha,\beta)\in\Omega}\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mid\alpha x\leq\beta\}.

(1)

Here without loss of generality (w.l.o.g.) we can assume that
(0,\ldots,0,1)\in\Omega
, since
(0,\ldots,0,1)
corresponds to the trivial inequality
0\cdot x\leq 1
.

For the valid inequality of the closure, we have the following result.

Proposition 1

(Proposition 1
[
28
]
)
.

Given
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
containing
(0,\ldots,0,1)
, such that
\mathscr{I}(\Omega)\neq\emptyset
. Then
\alpha x\leq\beta
is a valid inequality to
\mathscr{I}(\Omega)
if and only if
(\alpha,\beta)\in\operatorname{cl}\operatorname{cone}(\Omega)
.

For any set
S
, we use
\operatorname{cl}(S)
to denote the smallest closed set containing
S
, which is also called
closure
in topology. To avoid confusion, we will only use
\operatorname{cl}(S)
to refer the topological closure.
This above proposition immediately implies the following consequence.

Corollary 2
.

Given
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
containing
(0,\ldots,0,1)
with
\mathscr{I}(\Omega)\neq\emptyset
. Then
\mathscr{I}(\Omega)
is finitely-generated if and only if there exists a finite subset
\bar{\Omega}\subseteq\Omega
such that
\operatorname{cone}(\bar{\Omega})=\operatorname{cl}\operatorname{cone}(\Omega)
.

The proofs for both Proposition
1
and Corollary
2
can be found in Appendix
C
.
From this above Corollary
2
, we know that in order to show
\mathscr{I}(\Omega)
is finitely-generated, it suffices to show
\operatorname{cl}\operatorname{cone}(\Omega)
is a polyhedral cone and can be generated by finitely many elements from
\Omega
.
The next easy lemma is helpful for characterizing
\operatorname{cl}\operatorname{cone}(\Omega)
. Here the
\oplus
denotes the
direct sum
.

Lemma 3
.

For any
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
, let
L=\operatorname{lin}(\operatorname{cl}\operatorname{cone}(\Omega))
. Then

\operatorname{cl}\operatorname{cone}(\Omega)=\operatorname{cl}\operatorname{cone}(\operatorname{proj}_{L^{\perp}}\Omega)\oplus L
, where
\operatorname{cl}\operatorname{cone}(\operatorname{proj}_{L^{\perp}}\Omega)
is a pointed, closed convex cone.

Recall that a cone is called
pointed
if its lineality space is the origin.
In order to prove such result, we will also require the following lemma.

Lemma 4

(fact 9
[
26
]
)
.

Given a non-empty closed convex cone
K
,
K\cap\operatorname{lin}(K)^{\perp}
is a pointed cone and
K=(K\cap\operatorname{lin}(K)^{\perp})\oplus\operatorname{lin}(K)
.

Proof of Lemma
3
.

By Lemma
4
, it suffices to show:
\operatorname{cl}\operatorname{cone}(\operatorname{proj}_{L^{\perp}}\Omega)=\operatorname{cl}\operatorname{cone}(\Omega)\cap L^{\perp}
.
First, we want to show
\operatorname{cl}\operatorname{cone}(\operatorname{proj}_{L^{\perp}}\Omega)\subseteq\operatorname{cl}\operatorname{cone}(\Omega)\cap L^{\perp}
. The relation
\operatorname{cl}\operatorname{cone}(\operatorname{proj}_{L^{\perp}}\Omega)\subseteq\operatorname{cl}\operatorname{cone}(L^{\perp})=L^{\perp}
is obvious. Moreover, for any
\omega\in\Omega,\ \operatorname{proj}_{L^{\perp}}\omega=\omega-r,
for some
r\in L
. Hence
\operatorname{proj}_{L^{\perp}}\Omega\subseteq\Omega+L\subseteq\operatorname{cl}\operatorname{cone}(\Omega)+\operatorname{cl}\operatorname{cone}(\Omega)=\operatorname{cl}\operatorname{cone}(\Omega)
. Therefore,
\operatorname{cl}\operatorname{cone}(\operatorname{proj}_{L^{\perp}}\Omega)\subseteq\operatorname{cl}\operatorname{cone}(\Omega)
, which completes the proof of this
\subseteq
direction.

Then, we want to show that
\operatorname{cl}\operatorname{cone}(\operatorname{proj}_{L^{\perp}}\Omega)\supseteq\operatorname{cl}\operatorname{cone}(\Omega)\cap L^{\perp}
. Arbitrarily pick
x^{*}\in\operatorname{cl}\operatorname{cone}(\Omega)\cap L^{\perp}
. If
x^{*}\in\operatorname{cone}(\Omega)
, then
x^{*}=\operatorname{proj}_{L^{\perp}}x^{*}\in\operatorname{proj}_{L^{\perp}}\operatorname{cone}(\Omega)=\operatorname{cone}(\operatorname{proj}_{L^{\perp}}\Omega)
. If
x^{i}\rightarrow x^{*}
for a sequence of
\{x^{i}\}\subseteq\operatorname{cone}(\Omega)
, then
\operatorname{proj}_{L^{\perp}}x^{i}\rightarrow x^{*}
where
\operatorname{proj}_{L^{\perp}}x^{i}\in\operatorname{proj}_{L^{\perp}}\operatorname{cone}(\Omega)=\operatorname{cone}(\operatorname{proj}_{L^{\perp}}\Omega).
Hence
x^{*}\in\operatorname{cl}\operatorname{cone}(\operatorname{proj}_{L^{\perp}}\Omega)
. This completes the proof.
∎

It is well-known that, a pointed, closed convex cone is a polyhedral cone, if and only if it has finitely many different extreme rays. For a pointed
\operatorname{cl}\operatorname{cone}(\Omega)
, its extreme rays can be exactly characterized by elements in
\Omega
, as stated by the next lemma. We include its proof in Appendix
D
.

Lemma 5

(Corollary 1, Lemma 3
[
28
]
)
.

Given
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
with
(0,\ldots,0,1)\in\Omega
and
0\notin\Omega
. If
\operatorname{cl}\operatorname{cone}(\Omega)
is pointed, then for any extreme ray
r
of
\operatorname{cl}\operatorname{cone}(\Omega)
, either
r\in(\Omega)_{+}
, or there exist different
\{r^{i}\}\subseteq\Omega
such that
r^{i}\xrightarrow{c}r
.

Henceforth, when we mention a ray
r
of a cone, we will make no distinction between
r
and its positive scalar multiplication. In other words, we say two rays
r^{1}
and
r^{2}
are different, if and only if there does not exist
\lambda>0
, such that
r^{1}=\lambda r^{2}
.

3
Chvátal-Gomory Closure of Closed Convex Set.

We will prove Theorem
1
in this section.

For a given closed convex set
K
, we denote the family of CG cuts of
K
to be:

\Omega_{\text{CG}}:=\{(0,\ldots,0,1)\}\cup\{(c,\lfloor\sigma_{K}(c)\rfloor),\forall c\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}\}.

(2)

Then by definition of CG closure, there is
K^{\prime}=\mathscr{I}(\Omega_{\text{CG}})
. For ease of notation, when it is clear from the context, we will not specify what is the corresponding closed convex set
K
of
\Omega_{\text{CG}}
.
Throughout, a CG cut
cx\leq\lfloor\sigma_{K}(c)\rfloor
will sometimes also be referred to as a vector
(c,\lfloor\sigma_{K}(c)\rfloor)
.

Before presenting the proof for the main Theorem
1
, we will need the following lemmas.

Lemma 6

(Gordan’s lemma)
.

Given a lattice
L\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{n}
and a rational polyhedral cone
C\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Then there exists a finite set of lattice points
\{g^{1},\ldots,g^{m}\}\subset C\cap L
such that every point
x\in C\cap L
is an integer conical combination of these points:
x=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\lambda_{j}g^{j},\lambda_{j}\in\mathbb{N}
for all
j\in[m].

Here the finite generator
\{g^{1},\ldots,g^{m}\}
of
C\cap L
in the above lemma is usually referred to as the
Hilbert basis
of
C
(see, e.g.,
[
7
]
). From Gordan’s lemma we obtain the next result.

Lemma 7
.

Given a rational polyhedral cone
C\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
, a sequence of integer vectors
\{v^{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\subseteq C\cap\mathbb{Z}^{n}
, and a rational vector
q^{*}\in\mathbb{Q}^{n}
. Then there must exist an infinite set
I\subseteq\mathbb{N}
and
i^{*}\in\mathbb{N}
, such that for any
i\in I,v^{i}-v^{i^{*}}\in C
, and
v^{i}q^{*}-\lfloor v^{i}q^{*}\rfloor=v^{i^{*}}q^{*}-\lfloor v^{i^{*}}q^{*}\rfloor
.

Proof.

By Lemma
6
, we know there exist
g^{1},\ldots,g^{m}\in C\cap\mathbb{Z}^{n}
, such that
x\in C\cap\mathbb{Z}^{n}
if and only if
x
can be written as the integer conical combination of these points. Therefore, for each
v^{i}
, there exists
\lambda^{i}\in\mathbb{N}^{m}
such that
v^{i}=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\lambda^{i}_{j}g^{j}
.

Folklore

An infinite poset contains either an infinite chain or an infinite antichain.

Within the infinite poset
\Lambda:=\{\lambda^{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\subseteq\mathbb{N}^{m}
ordered by component-wise order
\leq
, from the Dickson’s Lemma
1
and this above folklore,
we know there must exist an infinite index set
I^{\prime}\subseteq\mathbb{N}
, such that
\{\lambda^{i}\}_{i\in I^{\prime}}
is an infinite chain within
\mathbb{N}^{m}.

Since
q^{*}
is a rational vector, we can write it as
q^{*}:=\frac{1}{D}z
, where
z\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
, and
D
is the least common multiple of the denominators of each
q_{1},\ldots,q_{n}
. So for any
v^{i}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n},v^{i}q^{*}
can be written as
\frac{1}{D}\cdot v^{i}z
, where
c^{i}z\in\mathbb{Z}
. Therefore,
\{v^{i}q^{*}-\lfloor v^{i}q^{*}\rfloor\mid i\in I^{\prime}\}\subseteq\{0,\frac{1}{D},\ldots,\frac{D-1}{D}\}
, which is a finite set. Here
I^{\prime}
is an infinite index set, by the pigeonhole principle, there also exists another infinite index set
I\subseteq I^{\prime}
, such that
v^{i}q^{*}-\lfloor v^{i}q^{*}\rfloor=v^{j}q^{*}-\lfloor v^{j}q^{*}\rfloor
for any
i,j\in I.

So far we have obtained an infinite index set
I
, such that for any
i\in I,v^{i}=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\lambda^{i}_{j}g^{j}
,
\{\lambda^{i}\}_{i\in I}
is an infinite chain within
\mathbb{N}^{m}
, and
v^{i}q^{*}-\lfloor v^{i}q^{*}\rfloor=v^{j}q^{*}-\lfloor v^{j}q^{*}\rfloor
for any
i,j\in I
. Since
\{\lambda^{i}\}_{i\in I}
is an infinite chain within
\mathbb{N}^{m}
, then there must exist
i^{*}\in I
such that
\lambda^{i^{*}}
is the
least element
(a.k.a. minimum element) of
\{\lambda^{i}\}_{i\in I}
.
Therefore, for any
i\in I
,
v^{i}q^{*}-\lfloor v^{i}q^{*}\rfloor=v^{i^{*}}q^{*}-\lfloor v^{i^{*}}q^{*}\rfloor
and
\lambda^{i}\geq\lambda^{i^{*}}
, which implies that
v^{i}-v^{i^{*}}=\sum_{j=1}^{m}(\lambda^{i}_{j}-\lambda^{i^{*}}_{j})g^{j}\in C
.
∎

The next lemma states that, within any infinite sequence of CG cuts of
K
, there must exist a conically convergent subsequence which converges to a valid inequality of
K
.

Lemma 8
.

Given a closed convex set
K
and a sequence
\{(r^{i},\lfloor\sigma_{K}(r^{i})\rfloor)\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\subseteq\Omega_{\text{CG}}
.
Then there exists a subsequence
\{(r^{i},\lfloor\sigma_{K}(r^{i})\rfloor)\}_{i\in I}
, such that when
i\in I,i\rightarrow\infty
,
(r^{i},\lfloor\sigma_{K}(r^{i})\rfloor)\xrightarrow{c}(r^{*},r^{*}_{0})
for some valid inequality
r^{*}x\leq r^{*}_{0}
of
K
.

Proof.

Picking
\gamma_{i}:=\frac{1}{\|(r^{i},\lfloor\sigma_{K}(r^{i})\rfloor)\|}
for all
i\in\mathbb{N}
. Then
\gamma_{i}(r^{i},\lfloor\sigma_{K}(r^{i})\rfloor)\in O_{1}(0)
which is a compact set. By Bolzano-Weierstrass theorem, we can find a convergent subsequence
\{\gamma_{i}(r^{i},\lfloor\sigma_{K}(r^{i})\rfloor)\}_{i\in I}
. Denote
\gamma_{i}(r^{i},\lfloor\sigma_{K}(r^{i})\rfloor)\rightarrow(r^{*},r^{*}_{0})
.
Since each
r^{i}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
, we know there exists an infinite subsequence of
\{r^{i}\}_{i\in I}
such that
\|r^{i}\|\rightarrow\infty
. W.l.o.g. we assume
\|r^{i}\|\rightarrow\infty
when
i\rightarrow\infty
, since
\gamma_{i}r^{i}\rightarrow r^{*}
, then there is
\gamma_{i}\rightarrow 0
. Furthermore, because
\gamma_{i}\lfloor\sigma_{K}(r^{i})\rfloor\rightarrow r^{*}_{0}
and
\gamma_{i}(\sigma_{K}(r^{i})-1)<\gamma_{i}\lfloor\sigma_{K}(r^{i})\rfloor\leq\gamma_{i}\sigma_{K}(r^{i})
, we know that
\gamma_{i}\sigma_{K}(r^{i})\rightarrow r^{*}_{0}
. Now we want to argue that
r^{*}x\leq r^{*}_{0}
is valid to
K
. If not, then there exists
x^{*}\in K
such that
r^{*}x^{*}>r^{*}_{0}
. Denote
\epsilon:=r^{*}x^{*}-r^{*}_{0}>0
. Since
\gamma_{i}(r^{i},\sigma_{K}(r^{i}))\rightarrow(r^{*},r^{*}_{0})
, then there exist
N_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
and
\kappa:=\frac{\epsilon}{2(1+\|x^{*}\|)}
, such that when
i\geq N_{0}:

\|r^{*}-\gamma_{i}r^{i}\|\leq\kappa,\quad|r^{*}_{0}-\gamma_{i}\sigma_{K}(r^{i})|\leq\kappa.

Therefore, when
i\geq N_{0}
:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\gamma_{i}r^{i}x^{*}-\gamma_{i}\sigma_{K}(r^{i})&\geq r^{*}x^{*}-\kappa\|x^{*}\|-r^{*}_{0}-\kappa\\
&=\epsilon-\kappa(1+\|x^{*}\|)\\
&=\frac{1}{2}\epsilon>0.\end{split}

This gives the contradiction since
r^{i}x\leq\sigma_{K}(r^{i})
is valid to
K
.
∎

Next we present the most crucial result for establishing the proof of the main theorem.

Proposition 2
.

Given a closed convex set
K\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
and a rational polyhedron
P\subseteq K
such that
P=\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mid fx\leq\lfloor\sigma_{K}(f)\rfloor,\forall f\in F\right\}
for some finite set
F\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{n}
. If
\{(c^{i},\lfloor\sigma_{K}(c^{i})\rfloor)\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\subseteq\Omega_{\text{CG}}
is a sequence of vectors with
\sigma_{P}(c^{i})>\lfloor\sigma_{K}(c^{i})\rfloor
for any

For closed convex sets which are not Motzkin-decomposable,

The inequality

Consider the linear system
\{\omega\cdot(x,-1)\ \forall\omega\in\Omega\}

is essentially the feasible region given by this linear system which is also non-empty, we know that linear system
\{\omega\cdot(x,-1)\ \forall\omega\in\Omega\}

and the assumption that
(0,\ldots,0,1)\in\Omega

is valid to
\mathscr{I}(\Omega)
if and only if
(\alpha,\beta)\in\operatorname{cl}\operatorname{cone}(\Omega)

By definition,
\mathscr{I}(\Omega)
is finitely-generated if there exists a finite subset
\bar{\Omega}\subseteq\Omega
with
\mathscr{I}(\bar{\Omega})=\mathscr{I}(\Omega)
. It suffices to show: for any finite subset

x\in K

h=-x^{*}
we complete the proof.
∎

Let

Now we are ready to verify Lemma

.

Proof of Lemma
5
.

From Lemma
16
, we can find a supporting hyperplane
hx=0
such that
h\omega>0
for all
\omega\neq 0\in\operatorname{cl}\operatorname{cone}(\Omega)
.
Denote the normalized version of
\Omega
:
\Omega^{\prime}=\{\frac{\omega}{h\cdot\omega}\mid\omega\in\Omega\}
, which is well-defined since
0\notin\Omega
, and for all
\omega\neq 0\in\Omega
there is
h\cdot\omega>0
.

Claim 3.

\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mid hx=1\}\cap\operatorname{cl}\operatorname{cone}(\Omega)=\operatorname{cl}\operatorname{conv}(\Omega^{\prime})
.

Proof of claim.

First, we show
\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mid hx=1\}\cap\operatorname{cl}\operatorname{cone}(\Omega)\subseteq\operatorname{cl}\operatorname{conv}(\Omega^{\prime})
. Arbitrarily pick
\alpha^{*}
such that
h\alpha^{*}=1
, and there exists
\{\alpha^{i}\}\subseteq\operatorname{cone}(\Omega)
such that
\alpha^{i}\rightarrow\alpha^{*}
. Denote
\beta^{i}:=\frac{\alpha^{i}}{h\alpha^{i}}
. Since
\alpha^{i}\rightarrow\alpha^{*},h\alpha^{*}=1
, we know
h\alpha^{i}\rightarrow 1
. Hence we also have
\beta^{i}\rightarrow\alpha^{*}
, and here
\beta^{i}\in\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mid hx=1\}\cap\operatorname{cone}(\Omega)
. In the following, we show:
\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mid hx=1\}\cap\operatorname{cone}(\Omega)\subseteq\operatorname{conv}(\Omega^{\prime})
, which will imply that
\alpha^{*}\in\operatorname{cl}\operatorname{conv}(\Omega^{\prime})
since
\beta^{i}\rightarrow\alpha^{*}
and
\beta^{i}\in\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mid hx=1\}\cap\operatorname{cone}(\Omega)
. According to the arbitrariness of
\alpha^{*}\in\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mid hx=1\}\cap\operatorname{cl}\operatorname{cone}(\Omega)
, this will complete the proof of
\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mid hx=1\}\cap\operatorname{cl}\operatorname{cone}(\Omega)\subseteq\operatorname{cl}\operatorname{conv}(\Omega^{\prime})
.

Pick
\beta\in\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mid hx=1\}\cap\operatorname{cone}(\Omega),
we can write it as:
\beta=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\lambda_{i}b^{i}
for some
\lambda_{i}>0,b^{i}\in\Omega,i\in[k],k\in\mathbb{N}
. Here because
\beta\in\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mid hx=1\}
, we know
\sum_{i=1}^{k}\lambda_{i}hb^{i}=1
. Therefore, we can also write
\beta
as:

\beta=\sum_{i=1}^{k}(\lambda_{i}hb^{i})\cdot\frac{b^{i}}{hb^{i}},\text{ here }\frac{b^{i}}{hb^{i}}\in\Omega^{\prime},\ \sum_{i=1}^{k}\lambda_{i}hb^{i}=1.

We get
\beta\in\operatorname{conv}(\Omega^{\prime})

\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mid hx=1\}\cap\operatorname{cone}(\Omega)\subseteq\operatorname{conv}(\Omega^{\prime})
.

Lastly, we show the other direction
\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mid hx=1\}\cap\operatorname{cl}\operatorname{cone}(\Omega)\supseteq\operatorname{cl}\operatorname{conv}(\Omega^{\prime})
. By definition,
\Omega^{\prime}\subseteq\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mid hx=1\}

\operatorname{cl}\operatorname{conv}(\Omega^{\prime})\subseteq\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mid hx=1\}

\Omega^{\prime}\subseteq\operatorname{cone}(\Omega)
, so
\operatorname{cl}\operatorname{conv}(\Omega^{\prime})\subseteq\operatorname{cl}\operatorname{cone}(\Omega)
, and we complete the proof for this claim.

\hfill\diamond
Given an extreme ray

, w.l.o.g. we assume

.
Then
r\in\Omega
iff
r\in\Omega^{\prime}
.
From the above claim, we also know

r

for
\lambda_{i}>0,\sum_{i=1}^{k}\lambda_{i}=1

\Omega^{\prime}
, we also have

Proof of Lemma
10

The next lemma says, the rational linear subspace
V_{\pi}
defined in Definition
2
can be characterized by any linear basis of
\{1,\pi_{1},\ldots,\pi_{n}\}
over
\mathbb{Q}
. Let
e^{1},\ldots,e^{n}
denote the canonical basis of
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
.

Lemma 18
.

Let
\{1,\pi_{i}\text{ for }i\in I\}
be a linear basis of
\{1,\pi_{1},\ldots,\pi_{n}\}
over
\mathbb{Q}
, with
\pi_{j}=q_{j,0}+\sum_{i\in I}q_{j,i}\pi_{i}\ \forall j\notin I
, here
q_{j,i}\in\mathbb{Q}\ \forall i\in\{0\}\cup I,j\notin I.

Then
V_{\pi}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mid x_{j}=\sum_{i\in I}q_{j,i}x_{i}\ \forall j\notin I\}
.

Proof.

Denote
L:=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mid x_{j}=\sum_{i\in I}q_{j,i}x_{i},j\notin I\}
.
First, we show that
V_{\pi}\supseteq L
. For any
\alpha\in\mathbb{Q}^{n}
such that
\alpha^{T}\pi\in\mathbb{Q}
, since
\pi_{j}=q_{j,0}+\sum_{i\in I}q_{j,i}\pi_{i}
for any
j\notin I
, we have:

\alpha^{T}\pi=\sum_{i\in I}\alpha_{i}\pi_{i}+\sum_{j\notin I}\alpha_{j}(q_{j,0}+\sum_{i\in I}q_{j,i}\pi_{i})=\sum_{j\notin I}\alpha_{j}q_{j,0}+\sum_{i\in I}(\alpha_{i}+\sum_{j\notin I}\alpha_{j}q_{j,i})\pi_{i}\in\mathbb{Q}.

Since
\alpha\in\mathbb{Q}^{n},q_{j,i}\in\mathbb{Q}
, and
\{1,\pi_{i}\text{ for }i\in I\}
are linearly independent over
\mathbb{Q}
, hence we obtain that
\alpha_{i}+\sum_{j\notin I}\alpha_{j}q_{j,i}=0
for any
i\in I
. For any
x\in L
, by definition of
L
, we have

\alpha^{T}x=\sum_{i\in I}(\alpha_{i}+\sum_{j\notin I}\alpha_{j}q_{j,i})x_{i}
, which is simply 0. Therefore, we have shown
V_{\pi}\supseteq L
.
Next, we show
L\supseteq V_{\pi}
.
For any
j\notin I
, define
\alpha^{j}:=e^{j}-\sum_{i\in I}q_{j,i}e^{i}
.
Then easy to verify that,
(\alpha^{j})^{T}\pi\in\mathbb{Q}
. So for any
x\in V_{\pi}
and
j\notin I
, there is
(\alpha^{j})^{T}x=0
. This is simply saying, for any
j\notin I,x_{j}=\sum_{i\in I}q_{j,i}x_{i}
, which implies that
L\supseteq V_{\pi}
.
∎

Proof of Lemma
10
.

W.l.o.g. we assume that a linear basis of
\{1,\pi_{1},\ldots,\pi_{n}\}
over
\mathbb{Q}
is
\{1,\pi_{1},\ldots,\pi_{k}\}
.
Let
\pi_{j}=q_{j,0}+\sum_{i=1}^{k}q_{j,i}\pi_{i}
for any
j>k
, here
q_{j,i}\in\mathbb{Q}\ \forall i\in\{0\}\cup[k],j>k.

By Lemma
18
, we know
V_{\pi}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mid x_{j}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}q_{j,i}x_{i},j>k\}
. When
k=n
then
V_{\pi}=\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and the statement of this lemma is a special case of Weyl’s criterion. We reduce the general case to this one.

The following elements lie in
\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mid x_{j}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}q_{j,i}x_{i},j>k\}
, which is
V_{\pi}
:

\tilde{e}^{i}:=e^{i}+\sum_{j=k+1}^{n}q_{j,i}e^{j}\ \forall i\leq k,\quad\quad\tilde{\pi}=\pi-\sum_{j=k+1}^{n}q_{j,0}e^{j}.

By Weyl’s criterion,
\mathbb{Z}^{k}+(\pi_{1},\ldots,\pi_{k})\mathbb{Z}_{>N_{0}}
is dense in
\mathbb{R}^{k}
. We reformulate this for
V
via the projection to the first
k
coordinates, which is an isomorphism between
V
and
\mathbb{R}^{k}
: a dense subset of
V_{\pi}
is
\sum_{i=1}^{k}\mathbb{Z}\tilde{e}^{i}+\tilde{\pi}\mathbb{Z}_{>N_{0}}
, which is a subset of
\mathbb{Z}^{n}+\pi\mathbb{Z}_{>N_{0}}
. This completes the proof.
∎
