Abstract
. This article focuses on a class of distributionally robust optimization (DRO) problems where, unlike the growing body of the literature, the objective function is potentially nonlinear in the distribution. Existing methods to optimize nonlinear functions in probability space use the Frechet derivatives, which present both theoretical and computational challenges. Motivated by this, we propose an alternative notion for the derivative and corresponding smoothness based on Gateaux (G)-derivative for generic risk measures. These concepts are explained via three running risk measure examples of variance, entropic risk, and risk on finite support sets. We then propose a G-derivative based Frank-Wolfe (FW) algorithm for generic nonlinear optimization problems in probability spaces and establish its convergence under the proposed notion of smoothness in a completely norm-independent manner. We use the set-up of the FW algorithm to devise a methodology to compute a saddle point of the nonlinear DRO problem. Finally, we validate our theoretical results on two cases of the entropic and variance risk measures in the context of portfolio selection problems. In particular, we analyze their regularity conditions and “sufficient statistic”, compute the respective FW-oracle in various settings, and confirm the theoretical outcomes through numerical validation.



Keywords.
Gateaux derivative, norm-free-smoothness, Frank-Wolfe algorithm, saddle point

1.
Introduction

Modern-day decision problems involve uncertainty in the form of a random variable
\xi
whose behavior is modeled via a probability distribution
\mathds{P}_{o}
. A central object to formalize such decision-making problems under uncertainty is risk measures. The most popular risk measure is arguably the expected loss, yielding the classical decision-making problem of

\text{Stochastic Program:}\quad\quad\min_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\mathds{E}_{\mathds{P}_{o}}\big[\ell(x,\xi)\big],

(1)

where
\ell
is the loss function of interest, and
\mathcal{X}
being the set of feasible decisions. The paradigm of stochastic programming (SP) relies on the assumption that the distribution
\mathds{P}_{o}
is available (or at least up to its sufficient statistics), thereby the expectation can be computed for every decision
x\in\mathcal{X}
. A common practical challenge is, however, that the complete information of
\mathds{P}_{o}
may not be available. Moreover, it might also be the case that the distribution is varying over a period of time which could be difficult to characterize. These limitations call for a more conservative risk measure to ameliorate the decision performance in such situations.

An alternative framework is Robust Optimization (RO) where the decision-maker has only access to the support of uncertainty and takes the most conservative approach:

\text{Robust Optimization:}\quad\quad\min_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\max_{\xi\in\Xi}\ell(x,\xi).

(2)

For many interesting examples, the RO min-max problem admits tractable reformulations that can be solved efficiently
[
3
]
. However, a generic RO problem is known to be computationally formidable as the inner maximization over
\xi
can be NP-hard. Moreover, if the support
\Xi
of the distribution is “large”, the results of RO tend to be extremely conservative.

Distributionally Robust Optimization (DRO)

The SP and RO decision models represent two extreme cases of having full or bare minimum distributional information, respectively. In practice, however, we often have more information about the ground truth distribution than just its support. A typical example is when we have sample realizations
\{\widehat{\xi}_{i}:i=1,2,\ldots,N\}
. Looking at such settings through the lens of SP, one may construct a
nominal distribution

\widehat{\mathds{P}}
and use it as a proxy for
\mathds{P}_{o}
in the SP (
1
). A standard data-driven nominal distribution is the discrete distribution
\widehat{\mathds{P}}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{\widehat{\xi}_{i}}
. The SP decision when
\mathds{P}_{o}=\widehat{\mathds{P}}
in (
1
) often admits a poor out-of-sample performance on a different dataset, a phenomenon that is also known as the “optimizer’s curse” or “overfitting”
[
45
]
. On the other hand, the RO viewpoint in (
2
) completely disregards the statistical information of
\mathds{P}_{o}
available through the dataset
(\widehat{\xi}_{i})_{i}
, or any other form of prior information.

An attempt to bridge the SP and RO modeling frameworks gives rise to the paradigm of
Distributionally Robust Optimization
(DRO), which dates back to the Scarf’s seminal work on the ambiguity-averse newsvendor problem in 1958
[
41
]
. The “ambiguity” set
\mathcal{P}
is a family of distributions that are close in some sense to the nominal distribution
\widehat{\mathds{P}}
, potentially including the true unknown distribution
\mathds{P}_{o}
. With this in mind, the DRO problem is formulated as

\min_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\sup_{\mathds{P}\in\mathcal{P}}\mathds{E}_{\mathds{P}}[\ell(x,\xi)].

(3)

On the one hand, if the ambiguity set is
very big
, possibly including all possible distributions, the DRO problem (
3
) reduces to the RO problem (
2
). On the other hand, if the ambiguity set
\mathcal{P}
is
very small
, potentially a singleton containing the nominal distribution, then the DRO problem reduces to the SP problem (
1
). In this light, the DRO framework (
3
) provides flexibility for the decision-maker to fill the gap between SP (
1
) and RO (
2
). The ambiguity set
\mathcal{P}
in (
3
) is typically constructed either based on the moments information
[
10
,
17
,
51
]
, or a neighborhood of
\widehat{\mathds{P}}
with respect to a notion of distance over probability distributions, e.g., Prohorov
[
12
]
, Kullback-Leiber
[
20
,
11
]
, Wasserstein
[
20
,
27
,
5
,
14
,
16
]
, Sinkhorn
[
50
]
, to name but a few; see also the surveys
[
39
,
23
]
and the references therein.

Linear DRO problems:
An important feature of (
3
) is the linearity of the objective function in the distribution
\mathds{P}
, which is also shared among all the literature mentioned above. The simplicity of this linearity in the inner maximization of (
3
) is the underlying driving force to develop tractable convex reformulations and computational solutions for various combinations of ambiguity sets and cost functions
[
38
,
53
,
40
,
43
,
7
]
. This line of research effectively translates the original infinite-dimensional DRO problem (
3
) to a tractable finite-dimensional one; see also the general optimal transport framework of
[
52
]
, and the case of mean-covariance risk measure
[
34
]
in a financial context.

Nonlinear DRO (NDRO):
Our goal here is to generalize the linear setting (
3
) to

\min_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\sup_{\mathds{P}\in\mathcal{P}}F\big(x,\mathds{P}\big),

(4)

where
F:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{P}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is a generic, possibly nonlinear, function signifying a risk measure. We refer to the decision-making problem (
4
) as
Nonlinear Distributionally Robust Optimization (NDRO)
. There are several interesting NDRO examples including the variance
[
4
]
, entropy
[
46
]
,
[
8
, Example 1, p. 14]
. In this study, instead of focusing only on the outer decision
x
in (
4
), we aim to compute an approximate saddle point between the decision-maker and the nature presented by the distribution
\mathds{P}
. A motivation supporting this effort is the fact that, unlike the worst-case distribution computed for a given decision, the saddle point distributions (also called Nash equilibria) naturally retain more realistic features
[
42
]
. From a computational perspective, the existing techniques deployed in linear DRO problems cannot be directly extended for the NDRO (
4
). The objective of this work is to precisely tackle this challenge, where we seek to devise a methodology along with mild regularity conditions under which a saddle point solution exists and can be computed.

Frank-Wolfe (FW) algorithm

It is a first-order method that only uses the information of the gradient to solve a constrained optimization problem
[
19
,
13
,
9
]
. In a nutshell, each iteration of the FW algorithm optimizes the linear function given by the gradient of the objective function and then takes a step towards the optimizer of the linear problem. On the contrary, other first-order methods like projected gradient descent require a quadratic function to be minimized at each iteration. Since solving a linear problem at each iteration is easier than a quadratic one, the iteration complexity of the FW algorithm is much simpler than other first-order methods. This is crucial for optimization problems over probability distributions since the complexity of projecting onto the ambiguity set can be as challenging as solving the original NDRO problem. In comparison, optimizing a cost function that is linear in distributions admits strong duality and tractable finite-dimensional reformulations for many interesting examples as seen in linear DRO. Therefore, we seek to use the principles of the Frank-Wolfe algorithm in the context of NDRO problems and devise an iterative procedure to compute a saddle point of (
4
).

The FW algorithm for optimization problems over probability distributions has already been introduced in
[
21
]
, which uses Frechet derivatives and the associated notion of smoothness to establish convergence. We would also like to highlight the work of
[
25
]
, where the canonical gradient ascent-descent algorithm
[
28
,
18
]
for min-max problems is extended to infinite-dimensional spaces involving probability distributions using the Frechet derivatives and its smoothness. In a similar spirit, the recent work of
[
33
]
proposes a mirror-descent algorithm
[
29
]
for constrained nonlinear optimization problems over probability distributions using the Frechet derivative. However, Frechet derivatives are difficult to deal with in practice due to several prominent challenges including their (i) existence, (ii) finite representability, and (iii) norm consistency; see Section
3.3
for more details on this. Our focus in this study is to remedy this by proposing a FW algorithm based on an alternative G-derivative along with a completely norm-independent convergence analysis.

Contributions

The main contributions of this study are summarized as follows:

(i)

Norm free smoothness in probability spaces.
We propose a novel G-derivative based notion of derivative for nonlinear risk measures (Definition
3.1
), and the associated notion of smoothness that is independent of the norm structure on the ambiguity set (Definition
3.6
). Moreover, we also derive conditions on the function
F\big(x,\mathds{P}\big)
such that the risk measure
\mathds{P}\longmapsto\min_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\ F\big(x,\mathds{P}\big)
is smooth in the sense of the proposed notion (Lemma
5.5
).

(ii)

G-derivative based Frank-Wolfe algorithm.
We provide a Frank-Wolfe algorithm based on the proposed notion of derivative for optimizing nonlinear risk measures. Moreover, the classical proof techniques for the FW algorithm carry forward under the proposed notion of derivative and smoothness, resulting in apriori (Propositions
4.4
) and aposteriori (Proposition
4.6
) convergence guarantees that exist for finite-dimensional problems.

(iii)

Saddle point seeking algorithm for NDRO problems.
For the potentially infinite-dimensional min-max problem of NDRO, we propose a FW-based algorithm to compute a saddle point (Algorithm
1
), and also quantify its convergence properties (Theorem
5.6
).

(iv)

The entropic and variance risk measures.

We study the NDRO problem and our proposed algorithm for two cases of the entropic and variance risk measures in Sections
6
and
7
, respectively. An interesting difference between these two examples is that in the context of portfolio selection, the variance preserves its “sufficient statistic” throughout the FW-algorithm while the entropic risk requires the knowledge of the entire distribution. We establish the required convex regularity conditions (Lemmas
6.3
and
7.2
, respectively), and then provide a complete description of the corresponding FW-oracle (Lemmas
6.4
and
7.4
,
7.6
, respectively). Furthermore, for the minimum variance portfolio selection problem, in the special case of

and the type-
2
Wasserstein ambiguity set, we slightly extend the results of
[
4
]
by explicitly describing the saddle point of the minimum variance problem when the feasible portfolio set
\mathcal{X}
is
any
arbitrary compact set (Proposition
7.5
). To facilitate the reproducibility of the numerical results, we also provide the open source repository
[
44
]
including the respective

code.

2.
Worst-case Nonlinear Risk Measures

In this section, we focus our attention to the potentially infinite-dimensional optimization problem:

\displaystyle R^{\ast}\coloneqq\sup_{\mathds{P}\in\mathcal{P}}R\big(\mathds{P}\big),

(5)

where
R:\mathcal{P}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is a desired, possibly
nonlinear
, concave risk measure, and
\mathcal{P}
is the ambiguity set containing a family of probability distributions over
\Xi\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Our main goal is to develop a framework with appropriate mathematical notions for a
Franke-Wolfe
(FW) like algorithm to solve problem (
5
) and investigate its convergence properties. Furthermore, since the FW algorithm for (
5
) operates in an infinite-dimensional setting, we also seek to derive its tractable finite-dimensional simplification to solve specific instances of (
5
).

Definition 2.1

(Regular risk (RR) measures & sufficient statistic)
.

A risk measure
R
in (
5
) is regular if it can be described as
R\big(\mathds{P}\big)=r\big(\mathds{E}_{\mathds{P}}[L(\xi)]\big)
, for some functions
L:\Xi\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{m}
that is integrable for all
\mathds{P}\in\mathcal{P}
and
r:\mathbb{R}^{m}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
that is concave and differentiable. We refer to
\mathds{E}_{\mathds{P}}[L(\xi)]
the “sufficient statistic” as the risk value depends on the distribution
\mathds{P}
only through this vector.

A particular feature of the regular risks in Definition
2.1
is that its value is determined based on a finite-dimensional vector
\mathds{E}_{\mathds{P}}[L(\xi)]
. This is a concept close to “sufficient statistic”, which is particularly appealing when it comes to the computation of the worst-case risk in (
5
). We will return to this in Section
4
when discussing the FW algorithm in the space of probability distributions.

Example 2.2

(RR-examples)
.

Throughout this study, we discuss three particular examples of the regular risk measures to showcase the concepts and our theoretical statements:

(a)

Variance
: A popular risk measure is the variance associated with the distribution. Formally, considering
\xi
to be a
\mathds{P}
-distributed random variable for
\mathds{P}\in\mathcal{P}
, the associated variance is

V(\mathds{P})\coloneqq\mathds{E}_{\mathds{P}}\big[\|\xi-\mathds{E}_{\mathds{P}}[\xi]\|_{2}^{2}\big].

(6a)

Considering the functions
L:\Xi\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
r:\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}

\begin{cases}\begin{aligned} L(\xi)&=(\xi\xi^{\top},\,\xi)\\
r(\Sigma,\mu)&=\trace(\Sigma)-\left\lVert\mu\right\rVert_{2}^{2},\end{aligned}\end{cases}

(6b)

one can observe that the variance is indeed an RR measure, since

V(\mathds{P})\ =\ \mathds{E}_{\mathds{P}}\big[\|\xi\|_{2}^{2}\big]-\|\mathds{E}_{\mathds{P}}[\xi]\|_{2}^{2}\ =\ \trace\left(\mathds{E}_{\mathds{P}}[\xi\xi^{\top}]\right)-\left\lVert\mathds{E}_{\mathds{P}}[\xi]\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\ =\ r\Big(\mathds{E}_{\mathds{P}}[L(\xi)]\Big).

(b)

Entropic risk
: Another interesting example of a nonlinear RR measure is the entropic risk of a multivariate distribution
[
46
, Section 5]
. If
\mathds{P}\in\mathcal{P}
is a distribution with marginals
\mathds{P}_{j}
for
j=1,2,\ldots,n
, (i.e., the
j
-th component
\xi_{j}
is
\mathds{P}_{j}
distributed). The entropic risk
\mathcal{E}(\mathds{P})
associated with the distribution
\mathds{P}
is defined as

\mathcal{E}(\mathds{P})\coloneqq\sum_{j=1}^{n}{\frac{1}{\theta_{j}}\log\left(\mathds{E}_{\mathds{P}_{j}}[e^{-\theta_{j}\xi_{j}}]\right)},

(7a)

where
(\theta_{j})_{j=1}^{n}
is a collection of positive real numbers referred to as the
risk-aversion parameters
. This is indeed an RR measure, which can be seen by introducing the functions

\begin{cases}\begin{aligned} L(\xi)&=\big(e^{-\theta_{1}\xi_{1}},e^{-\theta_{2}\xi_{2}},\ldots,e^{-\theta_{n}\xi_{n}}\big)\\
r(z)&=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{1}{\theta_{j}}\log(z_{j}).\end{aligned}\end{cases}

(7b)

(c)

Finite-support
: The final example is the case of
\Xi
being a finite set:
\Xi=\{\xi_{i}:i=1,2,\ldots,N\}
. In this case, the simplex
\Delta^{N}
is the set of all probability distributions on
\Xi
, and the ambiguity set of distributions is a subset,
\mathcal{P}\subset\Delta^{N}
. It turns out that
any
arbitrary risk measure
R
can be characterized as an RR-measure in the sense of Definition
2.1
by introducing appropriate functions
r:\mathbb{R}^{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, and
L:\Xi\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{N}
. To see this, we first observe that
\Xi
being a finite set gives rise to the enumerating bijection
b:\Xi\rightarrow\{1,2,\ldots,N\}
defined by
b(\xi)\coloneqq\{i:\xi=\xi_{i}\}
; secondly, the matrix
M\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
given by
[M]_{ij}\coloneqq(\nicefrac{{j}}{{N}})^{i}
for
i,j=1,2,\ldots,N
, is invertible. Then, considering the functions

\begin{cases}\begin{aligned} L(\xi)&=\big(\left(\nicefrac{{b(\xi)}}{{N}}\right),\left(\nicefrac{{b(\xi)}}{{N}}\right)^{2},\ldots,\left(\nicefrac{{b(\xi)}}{{N}}\right)^{N}\big)^{\top}\\
r(z)&=R\big(M^{-1}z\big)\ \text{ for }\ z\in M(\mathcal{P}),\end{aligned}\end{cases}

(8)

we have
\mathds{E}_{\mathds{P}}[L(\xi)]=M\cdot\mathds{P}
(viewing
\mathds{P}\in\Delta^{N}
as an element of
\mathbb{R}^{N}
), and as such,
R\big(\mathds{P}\big)=r(\mathds{E}_{\mathds{P}}[L(\xi)])
for all
\mathds{P}\in\mathcal{P}
.

3.
Derivatives of Risk Measures

Figure 1
.
Pictorial representation of the risk surface and its directional derivative.

A well-defined notion of the gradient is a fundamental quantity in developing iterative algorithms like that of FW to solve any optimization problem. For finite-dimensional convex problems, the FW algorithm optimizes the linear functional given by the gradient of the objective function over the feasible set at each iteration. Naturally, devising a similar algorithm for (
5
) requires at least a well-defined notion of
directional derivative
or G-derivative
[
2
, (A.3), p. 152]
.

3.1.
Gateaux directional derivatives

Definition 3.1

(G-derivative)
.

Given
\mathds{P},\mathds{Q}\in\mathcal{P}
, the Gateaux (G)-derivative
{\rm d}R\big(\mathds{P};\mathds{Q}\big)
of the risk measure
R
at
\mathds{P}
in the direction
\mathds{Q}
is defined as

{\rm d}R\big(\mathds{P};\mathds{Q}\big)\coloneqq\lim_{\gamma\downarrow 0}\frac{1}{\gamma}\Big(R\big(\mathds{P}_{\gamma}\big)-R\big(\mathds{P}\big)\Big),\quad\text{where}\quad\mathds{P}_{\gamma}=\mathds{P}+\gamma(\mathds{Q}-\mathds{P})\,.

(9)

Whenever the above limit exists, we say that the function
R
is
\mathds{Q}
-directionally differentiable at
\mathds{P}
. Moreover, we say that the risk measure
R
is directionally differentiable on
\mathcal{P}
if it is
\mathds{Q}
-directionally differentiable at every
\mathds{P}\in\mathcal{P}
and for all
\mathds{Q}\in\mathcal{P}
.

We note that the G-directional derivative in Definition
3.1
does not rely on any metric underlying the space of probability distributions. This is in fact the main feature with respect to the alternative F-derivative (Frechet derivative) that will be discussed in Section
3.3
. Figure
1
visualizes this directional derivative. Next lemma provides an explicit description of Definition
3.1
for RR measures.

Lemma 3.2

(Regular G-derivatives)
.

Suppose that the risk measure is regular, i.e.,
R\big(\mathds{P}\big)=r(\mathds{E}_{\mathds{P}}[L(\xi)])
with
\nabla r
denoting the gradient of function
r
. Then, for any
\mathds{P},\mathds{Q}\in\mathcal{P}
, we have

{\rm d}R\big(\mathds{P};\mathds{Q}\big)\;=\;\mathds{E}_{\mathds{Q}-\mathds{P}}\left[\left\langle\nabla r(\mathds{E}_{\mathds{P}}[L(\xi)]),\,L(\xi)\right\rangle\right]\;=\;\left\langle\nabla r(\mathds{E}_{\mathds{P}}[L(\xi)]),\,\mathds{E}_{\mathds{Q}-\mathds{P}}[L(\xi)]\right\rangle.

(10)

Lemma
3.2
indicates that the G-derivative of an RR-measure is essentially characterized by the “sufficient statistic” vectors
\mathds{E}_{\mathds{P}}[L(\xi)]
and
\mathds{E}_{\mathds{Q}}[L(\xi)]
(cf. (
10
)). Thus, if an algorithm optimizes an RR measure using only their directional derivatives, it then requires tracking the evolution of this finite-dimensional sufficient statistic, allowing them to be implemented efficiently.

Example 3.3

(Regular G-derivatives)
.

The directional derivative of an RR measure is completely characterized in terms of only a few finite-dimensional quantities that depend on the moments of the distribution, and the functions
r
and
L
.

(a)

Variance
: With the underlying inner product
\left\langle(\Sigma,\mu),\,(\Sigma^{\prime},\mu^{\prime})\right\rangle\coloneqq\trace(\Sigma^{\top}\Sigma^{\prime})+\mu^{\top}\mu^{\prime}
, we recall from (
6
) that
\nabla r(\Sigma,\mu)=\Big(\mathbb{I}_{n},-2\mu\Big)
. Then the
\mathds{Q}
-directional derivative of the variance
V
, at
\mathds{P}
can be calculated from (
10
) as

{\rm d}V(\mathds{P};\mathds{Q})=\trace\big(\Sigma_{\mathds{Q}}-\Sigma_{\mathds{P}}\big)-2\mu_{\mathds{P}}^{\top}(\mu_{\mathds{Q}}-\mu_{\mathds{P}}).

(11)

(b)

\mathcal{E}
-measure
: With the canonical inner product
\left\langle z,\,\widehat{z}\right\rangle=z^{\top}\widehat{z}
, we recall from (
7
) that
\nabla r(z)=\big({(\theta_{1}z_{1})^{-1}},{(\theta_{2}z_{2})^{-1}},\ldots,{(\theta_{n}z_{n})^{-1}}\big)
at every
z\in\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}
. Thus, its
\mathds{Q}
-directional derivative at
\mathds{P}
is

{\rm d}\mathcal{E}(\mathds{P};\mathds{Q})=\sum_{j=1}^{n}{\frac{\mathds{E}_{\mathds{Q}_{j}-\mathds{P}_{j}}[e^{-\theta_{j}\xi_{j}}]}{\theta_{j}\mathds{E}_{\mathds{P}_{j}}[e^{-\theta_{j}\xi_{j}}]}}.

(12)

(c)

Finite support
: Recall from (
8
) that
\mathds{E}_{\mathds{P}}[L(\xi)]=M\cdot\mathds{P}
for all
\mathds{P}\in\mathcal{P}
. Then with the canonical inner product
\left\langle z,\,z^{\prime}\right\rangle=z^{\top}z^{\prime}
, we have
\nabla r(z)=(M^{-1})^{{}^{\top}}\nabla R(M^{-1}z)
for all
z\in M(\mathcal{P})
. Substituting these quantities in (
10
) and simplifying, we get

{\rm d}R\big(\mathds{P};\mathds{Q}\big)=(\nabla R\big(\mathds{P}\big))^{\top}(\mathds{Q}-\mathds{P}).

(13)

It is to be observed that the matrix
M
has no relevance in the G-derivative as one would expect since the risk measure
R
is defined independent of the matrix
M
.

The G-derivative enjoys inherent properties that will be helpful to devise computational solutions.

Proposition 3.4

(G-derivative: properties)
.

For any
\mathds{P},\mathds{Q}\in\mathcal{P}
, and
\gamma\in[0,1]
, let
\mathds{P}_{\gamma}=\mathds{P}+\gamma(\mathds{Q}-\mathds{P})
, then the directional derivative
{\rm d}R\big(\mathds{P};\cdot\big)
in Definition
3.1
satisfies

Positively homogeneous:

\displaystyle\quad{\rm d}R\big(\mathds{P};\mathds{P}_{\gamma}\big)\;=\;\gamma{\rm d}R\big(\mathds{P};\mathds{Q}\big)

(14a)

Upper bound for concave risk measures:

\displaystyle\quad R\big(\mathds{Q}\big)-R\big(\mathds{P}\big)\;\leqslant\;{\rm d}R\big(\mathds{P};\mathds{Q}\big).

(14b)

Remark 3.5

(Optimality conditions)
.

Similar to KKT conditions, F-derivative based first-order optimality conditions for both constrained and unconstrained versions of (
5
) are given in
[
24
, Section 3]
. With G-derivatives, the upper bound (
14b
) for the concave risk measure
R
immediately gives sufficient optimality conditions for (
5
). More precisely, if
\mathds{P}^{\ast}\in\argmax_{\mathds{Q}\in\mathcal{P}}\ {\rm d}R\big(\mathds{P}^{\ast};\mathds{Q}\big)
, then applying (
14b
) for any
\mathds{Q}\in\mathcal{P}
yields

R\big(\mathds{Q}\big)\;\leqslant\;R\big(\mathds{P}^{\ast}\big)+{\rm d}R\big(\mathds{P}^{\ast};\mathds{Q}\big)\;\leqslant\;R\big(\mathds{P}^{\ast}\big)+{\rm d}R\big(\mathds{P}^{\ast};\mathds{P}^{\ast}\big)\;=\;R\big(\mathds{P}^{\ast}\big),

which establishes the optimality
\mathds{P}^{\ast}\in\argmax_{\mathds{Q}\in\mathcal{P}}\ R\big(\mathds{Q}\big)
.

3.2.
Smoothness

The proposed approach to solve (
5
) builds on the Frank-Wolfe (FW) algorithm for finite-dimensional convex optimization problems
[
13
]
. It is a well-known fact that the primal sub-optimality in FW algorithm converges at a sub-linear rate O(
\nicefrac{{1}}{{k}}
) for optimization of “smooth” objective functions over compact feasible sets in finite-dimensional problems. A convex function is said to be smooth if it has Lipschitz continuous gradients with respect to some norm. The choice of norm in an infinite-dimensional setting can be problematic, as all the norms are not equivalent (unlike the finite-dimensional setting). To extend such convergence attributes for a FW like algorithm in the infinite-dimensional setting of (
5
), we first propose an appropriate notion of smoothness in terms of the directional derivatives (as in Definition
3.1
) that is also norm-independent.

Definition 3.6

(G-smoothness)
.

The risk measure
R
is
C
-smooth if there exists a constant
C\geqslant 0
such that for all
\mathds{P},\mathds{Q}\in\mathcal{P}
and
\gamma\in[0,1]
, we have the inequality

{\rm d}R\big(\mathds{P}_{\gamma};\mathds{P}\big)+{\rm d}R\big(\mathds{P};\mathds{P}_{\gamma}\big)\;\leqslant\;\gamma^{2}C\quad\text{where $\mathds{P}_{\gamma}=\mathds{P}+\gamma\big(\mathds{Q}-\mathds{P}\big)$ }.

(15)

Connection to the existing notions of smoothness

The notion of smoothness in Definition
3.6
is a generalization of the canonical smoothness condition of Lipschitz continuous gradients
[
31
, Section 2.1.5]
. Formally, a function
f:\mathcal{D}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
with a finite-dimensional domain
(\mathcal{D},\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert)
and gradients
\nabla f
is said to be
\beta
-smooth if

\left\lVert\nabla f(x)-\nabla f(y)\right\rVert_{*}\;\leqslant\;\beta\left\lVert x-y\right\rVert\quad\text{ holds for all $x,y\in\mathcal{D}$.}

(16)

It is further generalized (or relaxed) by the notion of Holder-smoothness (particularly,
1
-Holder smooth) where it is required that

\big\langle\nabla f(x)-\nabla f(y),y-x\big\rangle\;\leqslant\;\beta\left\lVert x-y\right\rVert^{2}\quad\text{holds for all $x,y\in\mathcal{D}$}.

(17)

Furthermore, if the set
\mathcal{D}
is
\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert
-bounded in addition, then the notion of Holder-smoothness (
17
) is sufficient to the requirement that there exists some constant
C\geqslant 0
such that for every
x,y\in\mathcal{D}
,
\gamma\in[0,1]
, the function
f
satisfies the inequality

\big\langle\nabla f(x)-\nabla f(x_{\gamma}),x_{\gamma}-x\big\rangle\;\leqslant\;\gamma^{2}C\quad\text{ where $\ x_{\gamma}\coloneqq x+\gamma(y-x)$}.

(18)

Expanding the left-hand side of (
18
) as
\big\langle\nabla f(x)-\nabla f(x_{\gamma}),x_{\gamma}-x\big\rangle=\big\langle\nabla f(x),x_{\gamma}-x\big\rangle+\big\langle\nabla f(x_{\gamma}),x-x_{\gamma}\big\rangle
, we see that the individual terms are simply the directional derivatives of
f
; Definition
3.6
of the proposed notion of smoothness becomes apparent at once. We also highlight that the notion of smoothness in Definition
3.6
is closely related to the notion of “curvature coefficient” used in
[
19
, (3)]
. In fact, it can be easily shown that a function has a finite curvature coefficient if it is smooth in the sense of Definition
3.6
.

Concave quadratic lower bound via smoothness

.

It is well established that in a finite-dimensional setting, smoothness conditions like (
16
) and (
17
) give rise to a global convex (resp. concave) quadratic upper bound (resp. lower bound). For the risk measure
R
specifically, a sample representation of such a quadratic lower bound is shown in Figure

f
is
\beta
-canonically smooth (as in (
16
)), then the following inequality holds:

-\frac{\beta}{2}\left\lVert y-x\right\rVert^{2}\leqslant f(y)-f(x)-\left\langle\nabla f(x),\,y-x\right\rangle\leqslant\frac{\beta}{2}\left\lVert y-x\right\rVert^{2}\quad\text{for all }x,y\in\mathcal{D}.

The notion of smoothness in Definition
3.6
imposes similar quadratic bounds, but without using any norm. This is done by enforcing “quadratic-like” bounds to hold uniformly over all directions. The following Lemma establishes the smoothness of the class of RR measures.

Lemma 3.7

(Regular G-smoothness)
.

Suppose the RR measure
R\big(\mathds{P}\big)=r(\mathds{E}_{\mathds{P}}[L(\xi)])
satisfies

(i)

bounded diameter in expectations with
d\coloneqq\sup\limits_{\mathds{P},\mathds{Q}\in\mathcal{P}}\;\left\lVert\mathds{E}_{\mathds{P}}[L(\xi)]\;-\;\mathds{E}_{\mathds{Q}}[L(\xi)]\right\rVert\;<\;+\infty
, and

(ii)

it admits a smooth
r
in the canonical sense (
16
), i.e.,

\left\lVert\nabla r(u)-\nabla r(v)\right\rVert_{*}\leqslant\beta\left\lVert u-v\right\rVert\quad\text{for all }u,v\in\{\mathds{E}_{\mathds{P}}[L(\xi)]:\mathds{P}\in\mathcal{P}\}.

Then, it is
(\beta d^{2})
-smooth in the sense of Definition
3.6
.

3.3.
Frechet derivatives

An important observation to be made is that the
\mathds{Q}
-directional derivative
{\rm d}R\big(\mathds{P};\mathds{Q}\big)
of regular-risk measures is
affine
in
\mathds{Q}
.
1
1

1

A function
f:\mathcal{S}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is said to affine if
f(x+\theta(y-x))=f(x)+\theta(f(y)-f(x))
for every
x,y\in\mathcal{S}
and
\theta\in[0,1]
.
In principle, without any further assumptions on the risk measure
R
, its
\mathds{Q}
-directional derivative need not be affine in
\mathds{Q}
. Counter examples of functions that have well defined G-derivatives in all directions but that are nonlinear in the direction exist even among functions defined on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, let alone the infinite-dimensional setting of
\mathcal{P}
. A sufficient condition for the directional derivative to be affine in
\mathds{Q}
is the existence of a stronger notion of derivative called the
Frechet-derivative
or
F-derivative
.

Definition 3.9

(Frechet-derivative)
.

The Frechet(F)-derivative of the risk measure
R
at
\mathds{P}\in\mathcal{P}
associated with a given norm
\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{\mathcal{P}}
on
\mathcal{P}
, is a function
\ell_{\mathds{P}}:\Xi\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
such that the mapping
\mathcal{P}\ni\mathds{P}^{\prime}\longmapsto\mathds{E}_{\mathds{P}^{\prime}}[\ell_{\mathds{P}}(\xi)]
is continuous w.r.t.
\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{\mathcal{P}}
and satisfies

0=\lim\limits_{\left\lVert\mathds{P}^{\prime}-\mathds{P}\right\rVert_{\mathcal{P}}\downarrow 0}\frac{R\big(\mathds{P}^{\prime}\big)-R\big(\mathds{P}\big)-\mathds{E}_{\mathds{P}^{\prime}-\mathds{P}}[\ell_{\mathds{P}}(\xi)]}{\left\lVert\mathds{P}^{\prime}-\mathds{P}\right\rVert_{\mathcal{P}}}.

(19)

Smoothness with F-derivatives

If the F-derivative
\ell_{\mathds{P}}
were to exist at every
\mathds{P}\in\mathcal{P}
, the canonical notion of smoothness (
39
) can be naturally extended into the infinite-dimensional setting. The risk measure
R
is
F-smooth
if there exists some
\beta\geqslant 0
such that its F-derivative
\ell_{\mathds{P}}
satisfies

\text{F-smoothness: }\quad\left\lVert\ell_{\mathds{P}}-\ell_{\mathds{P}^{\prime}}\right\rVert_{\mathcal{P}^{\ast}}\;\leqslant\;\beta\left\lVert\mathds{P}-\mathds{P}^{\prime}\right\rVert_{\mathcal{P}}\quad\text{for all }\mathds{P},\mathds{P}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{P},

(20)

where
\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{\mathcal{P}^{\ast}}
is the dual norm of
\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{\mathcal{P}}
. If the risk measure in (
5
) is F-smooth, most of the convergence analysis due to smoothness in finite-dimensional convex problems simply carries forward to the infinite-dimensional setting right away. This provides a natural recipe to extend the FW algorithm into the infinite-dimensional setting of probability spaces and establish their convergence under F-smoothness. F-derivative based FW-algorithms in probability spaces have already been studied in the literature
[
21
]
with a slightly more general notion of smoothness than (
20
), and for a slightly more general class of risk measures than the usual concavity assumption. Our approach differs from
[
21
]
in the fact that we only make use of G-derivatives in both the development of the FW-algorithm and also in establishing its convergence based on only G-derivative based regularity conditions, which are simpler to deal with than F-derivatives.

Comparison with G-derivatives

It is not necessary for a function to have F-derivatives even if it has affine directional derivatives in all directions, such counterexamples exist even in a finite-dimensional setting. On the contrary, if the risk measure
R
has a well-defined F-derivative
\ell_{\mathds{P}}
, then it can be shown that its
\mathds{Q}
-directional derivatives also exist in all directions
\mathds{Q}\in\mathcal{P}
. This is easily seen by considering
\mathds{P}^{\prime}=\mathds{P}+\gamma(\mathds{Q}-\mathds{P})
for
\gamma\in[0,1]
in the definition (
19
) and observing that
\mathds{E}_{\mathds{P}^{\prime}-\mathds{P}}[\ell_{\mathds{P}}(\xi)]=\gamma\mathds{E}_{\mathds{Q}-\mathds{P}}[\ell_{\mathds{P}}(\xi)]
. When
\mathds{Q}\neq\mathds{P}
, the limit
\left\lVert\mathds{P}^{\prime}-\mathds{P}\right\rVert_{\mathcal{P}}\downarrow 0
is achieved if and only if
\gamma\downarrow 0
. Then it follows from (
19
) that

0=\lim_{\gamma\downarrow 0}\;\frac{R\big(\mathds{P}_{\gamma}\big)-R\big(\mathds{P}\big)-\gamma\mathds{E}_{\mathds{Q}-\mathds{P}}[\ell_{\mathds{P}}(\xi)]}{\gamma\left\lVert\mathds{Q}-\mathds{P}\right\rVert_{\mathcal{P}}}=\frac{1}{\left\lVert\mathds{Q}-\mathds{P}\right\rVert_{\mathcal{P}}}\Big({\rm d}R\big(\mathds{P};\mathds{Q}\big)-\mathds{E}_{\mathds{Q}-\mathds{P}}[\ell_{\mathds{P}}(\xi)]\Big).

Since
\mathds{Q}\neq\mathds{P}
, we conclude that the
\mathds{Q}
-directional derivative exists and
{\rm d}R\big(\mathds{P};\mathds{Q}\big)\,=\,\mathds{E}_{\mathds{Q}-\mathds{P}}[\ell_{\mathds{P}}(\xi)]
. Moreover, this equality holds even if
\mathds{Q}=\mathds{P}
since
{\rm d}R\big(\mathds{P};\mathds{P}\big)=0=\mathds{E}_{\mathds{P}-\mathds{P}}[\ell_{\mathds{P}}(\xi)]
.

The notion of F-derivative relies heavily on the underlying metric structure on
\mathcal{P}
, whereas, the notion (Definition
3.1
) of G-derivatives is independent of it. To compare, for a G-derivative to exist along a given direction, it is only required for the limit in (
9
) to exist. However, the existence of an F-derivative requires that the limit in (
9
) is achieved uniformly over all possible directions.

Challenges with F-derivatives

Even though the notion of F-smoothness (
20
) is a natural extension of canonical smoothness (
39
) in an infinite-dimensional setting, working with F-derivatives is potentially challenging due to the following reasons:

(i)

Existence
: It is a stronger requirement that the limit in (
20
) converges uniformly (w.r.t.
\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{\mathcal{P}}
) in all directions, which often implies that an F-derivative might not even exist.

(ii)

Finite representability
: An F-derivative is a function
\ell_{\mathds{P}}:\Xi\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
which is an infinite-dimensional object, so apriori, it is not clear as to whether it can be characterized in terms of a few finite-dimensional quantities.

(iii)

Norm consistency
: Most importantly, it is often very difficult to establish the smoothness condition (
20
) of the F-derivatives in a specific norm. To elaborate further, we know that the FW algorithm in a finite-dimensional convex problem converges sub-linearly, if the feasible set is bounded and the objective function is smooth. Since all norms on finite-dimensional vector spaces are equivalent, the choices of norms for establishing the smoothness of the objective function, and the boundedness of the feasibility set are irrelevant (even though this could potentially give rise to dimensionally dependent constants). However, since no such equivalence exists between norms on an infinite-dimensional space, it becomes then necessary that the risk measure is F-smooth w.r.t. the same norm under which the ambiguity set is bounded, which is a much stronger condition to expect.

We close this discussion by providing an example wherein a risk measure has directional derivatives and is G-smooth, yet its F-derivatives do not exist. In particular, we argue that all RR measures that satisfy conditions of Lemma
3.7
are G-smooth, whereas their F-derivative does not exist if the corresponding
L
function (i.e., sufficient statistic) is discontinuous.

Example 3.10

(G-derivative vs F-derivative)
.

Suppose the support set is
\Xi=[-1,+1]
, the ambiguity set
\mathcal{P}
contains all possible distributions supported on
\Xi
and it is equipped with a Wasserstein metric. Let the regular risk be defined by the sign function
L(\xi)=\sgn(\xi)
(with the convention that
\sgn(0)=0
) and
r(z)=z
, i.e.,
R\big(\mathds{P}\big)=\mathds{E}_{\mathds{P}}[\sgn(\xi)]
.

(a)

Existence of G-smoothness:
We conclude from Lemma
3.2
that the G-derivatives of the risk measure exist and are given by
{\rm d}R\big(\mathds{P};\mathds{Q}\big)=\mathds{E}_{\mathds{Q}-\mathds{P}}[\sgn(\xi)]
. Moreover, since the support
\Xi
is bounded, condition (i) of Lemma
3.7
is satisfied with
d=2
, and since
\nabla r(z)=1
for all
z\in[-1,+1]
, condition (ii) of Lemma
3.7
is also satisfied with
\beta=0
. Consequently, the given regular risk is
0
-smooth in the sense of Definition
3.6
.

(b)

Non-existence of F-derivatives:
Since
{\rm d}R\big(\mathds{P};\mathds{Q}\big)=\mathds{E}_{\mathds{Q}-\mathds{P}}[\sgn(\xi)]
, if the F-derivative of the risk measure
R
were to exist, the mapping
\mathds{Q}\longmapsto\mathds{E}_{\mathds{Q}-\mathds{P}}[\sgn(\xi)]
must be continuous. However, for the sequence of distributions
\mathds{Q}_{n}(\xi)=\delta(\xi-\nicefrac{{1}}{{n}})
, we see that
\mathds{E}_{\mathds{Q}_{n}-\mathds{P}}[\sgn(\xi)]=1-\mathds{E}_{\mathds{P}}[\sgn(\xi)]
for all
n=1,2,,\ldots
, and they converge to the distribution
\mathds{Q}=\delta(\xi)
in the Wasserstein metric, for which we have
\mathds{E}_{\mathds{Q}-\mathds{P}}[\sgn(\xi)]=-\mathds{E}_{\mathds{P}}[\sgn(\xi)]\neq 1-\mathds{E}_{\mathds{P}}[\sgn(\xi)]=\mathds{E}_{\mathds{Q}_{n}-\mathds{P}}[\sgn(\xi)]
. Therefore, the F-derivative of the risk measure does not exist.

More sophisticated examples of risk measures and ambiguity sets can be constructed following the same underlying idea of discontinuity of function
L
. Example
3.10
highlights the relevance of different notions of derivatives and the resulting regularity. The existence of F-derivatives and associated smoothness requires the derivative object to be continuous w.r.t. more variations of distributions whereas the notion of G-derivatives and smoothness considers variations only along a line joining any pair of distributions. Luckily, since the FW algorithm operates by taking convex combinations at each iteration, we only require the latter bounds which are considerably less restrictive.

3.4.
G-derivatives in non-flat spaces

In the following, we consider a slightly general setting wherein the ambiguity set
\mathcal{P}
may not be convex, e.g., when
\mathcal{P}\subset\mathcal{N}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
is the set of
n
multivariate Gaussian distributions. In such a setting, given two distributions
\mathds{P},\mathds{Q}\in\mathcal{P}
, the line joining them
\mathds{P}_{\gamma}=\mathds{P}+\gamma(\mathds{Q}-\mathds{P})
is not contained in
\mathcal{P}
. Even though such examples cannot be directly handled in our setting, it turns out that a slight modification of the definition of the derivative allows us to take care of such scenarios. To this end, we assume that the ambiguity set
\mathcal{P}
is equipped with

(a)

Metric
:
d:\mathcal{P}\times\mathcal{P}\longrightarrow[0,+\infty[

(b)

Geodesics
: For any
\mathds{P},\mathds{Q}\in\mathcal{P}
, there exists a parametric curve
[0,1]\ni\gamma\longmapsto\mathds{P}^{d}_{\gamma}\in\mathcal{P}
such that
\mathds{P}^{d}_{0}=\mathds{P}
,
\mathds{P}^{d}_{1}=\mathds{Q}
, and
d(\mathds{P}_{\gamma}^{d},\mathds{P}^{d}_{\gamma^{\prime}})=\left\lvert\gamma-\gamma^{\prime}\right\rvert d(\mathds{P},\mathds{Q})
.

For example, if
\mathcal{P}=\mathcal{N}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, then each distribution is uniquely identified by its first and second moments
\mu
and
\Sigma
respectively. Then, for given two distributions
\mathds{P}=\mathcal{N}(\mu,\Sigma)
and
\mathds{P}^{\prime}=\mathcal{N}(\mu^{\prime},\Sigma^{\prime})
, we define the metric
d(\mathds{P},\mathds{P}^{\prime})\coloneqq\sqrt{\left\lVert\mu-\mu^{\prime}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}+\left\lVert\Sigma-\Sigma^{\prime}\right\rVert_{F}^{2}}
, and the associated geodesic is
\mathds{P}_{\gamma}^{d}=\mathcal{N}(\mu_{\gamma},\Sigma_{\gamma})
, where
(\mu_{\gamma},\Sigma_{\gamma})=(1-\gamma)(\mu,\Sigma)+\gamma(\mu^{\prime},\Sigma^{\prime})
.

G-derivatives and smoothness.
Given a geodesic structure on
\mathcal{P}
, we define the associated G-derivatives by

{\rm d}R\big(\mathds{P};\mathds{Q}\big)\coloneqq\lim_{\gamma\downarrow 0}\frac{1}{\gamma}\left(R\big(\mathds{P}_{\gamma}^{d}\big)-R\big(\mathds{P}\big)\right).

Observe that the above definition is slightly different from that of (
9
) where the convex combination
\mathds{P}_{\gamma}
of distributions
\mathds{P}
and
\mathds{Q}
is replaced with its geodesic counterpart. Consequently, we can also define G-smoothness on non-flat spaces in a geodesic sense similar to Definition
3.6
. We say that the risk measure
R
is
C
-smooth if there exists some
C\geqslant 0
such that the inequality holds

{\rm d}R\big(\mathds{P};\mathds{P}_{\gamma}^{d}\big)+{\rm d}R\big(\mathds{P}_{\gamma}^{d};\mathds{P}\big)\leqslant\gamma^{2}C,\quad\text{for any $\mathds{P},\mathds{Q}\in\mathcal{P}$ and $\gamma\in[0,1]$.}

Geodesic concavity
. We can define a notion of concavity using the geodesic structure that is slightly more general than the usual notion. We say that a risk measure
R
is geodesically concave on
\mathcal{P}
if the mapping
[0,1]\ni\gamma\longmapsto R\big(\mathds{P}_{\gamma}^{d}\big)
is concave for every
\mathds{P},\mathds{Q}\in\mathcal{P}
. It turns out that both of the results from Proposition
3.4
hold true for derivatives defined in a geodesic sense. This allows us to consider DRO problems like

\min_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\sup_{\mathds{P}\in\mathcal{N}}\mathds{E}_{\mathds{P}}[f(x,\xi)]-\lambda G(\mathds{P},\widehat{\mathds{P}}),

where
G(\mathds{P},\widehat{\mathds{P}})
is the Gelbrich distance (or any other moment-based distance). We wish to emphasize that the FW algorithm must also be adapted to work with the geodesic derivatives instead.

4.
The Frank-Wolfe Algorithm

Given the notion of G-derivative as in Definition
3.1
, the Frank-Wolfe (FW) algorithm for (
5
) is an iterative procedure that involves solving the optimization problem

\sup_{\mathds{Q}\in\mathcal{P}}\;{\rm d}R\big(\mathds{P};\mathds{Q}\big),

(21)

Figure 3
.
FW-oracle.

for a given
\mathds{P}\in\mathcal{P}
, at each iteration. The FW-problem (
21
) is linear if and only if the G-derivative
{\rm d}R\big(\mathds{P};\mathds{Q}\big)
is affine in
\mathds{Q}
, for every
\mathds{P}\in\mathcal{P}
, translating the problem into the linear DRO class in (
3
). This is indeed the case for many interesting risk measures as seen in Example
3.3
. Moreover, in all finite-dimensional optimization problems, the corresponding FW-problem (
21
) is always linear, which need not be the case for a generic infinite-dimensional optimization problem like (
5
).

Implementation of the FW algorithm only requires a well-defined notion of the G-derivative, and as seen in Lemma
3.2
, such objects can be computed by means of a few finite-dimensional quantities in several problems of interest. Moreover, even though the
FW-problem
(
21
) is infinite-dimensional in nature, it admits tractable finite-dimensional convex reformulations for many relevant applications similar to linear DROs
[
23
]
. This is a compelling reason to investigate FW methods for optimization problems over probability distributions, particularly in the case of nonlinear risk measures as in (
5
).

4.1.
FW oracle

The FW-oracle is a set-valued mapping
\mathcal{F}:\mathcal{P}\times[0,1]\rightarrow 2^{\mathcal{P}}
defined as

\mathcal{F}(\mathds{P},\gamma)\coloneqq\left\{\mathds{Q}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{P}\text{ such that }\sup_{\mathds{Q}\in\mathcal{P}}\;{\rm d}R\big(\mathds{P};\mathds{Q}\big)\leqslant\gamma\delta C+{\rm d}R\big(\mathds{P};\mathds{Q}^{\prime}\big)\right\}.

(22)

Given the current iterate
\mathds{P}\in\mathcal{P}
, the FW algorithm involves solving the FW-problem (
21
) to obtain its approximate solution
\mathds{Q}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{P}
, then the current iterate
\mathds{P}
is updated by moving it towards
\mathds{Q}^{\prime}
as shown in Figure
3
.

Additive accuracy of the oracle

The parameter
\delta>0
is an arbitrary positive number signifying the accuracy of the FW oracle, and
C
is the smoothness constant of the risk measure
R
as in Definition
3.6
. It must be observed that in an iterative scheme to solve (
5
), it is typical that the stepsize sequence (
\gamma_{k}
) is monotonically decreasing and converges to
0
. Therefore, it is also required that the FW-oracle solves the sub-problems (
21
) up to a greater precision as the iterations progress.

The Frank-Wolfe-gap

We refer to the quantity
\sup_{\mathds{Q}\in\mathcal{P}}\;{\rm d}R\big(\mathds{P};\mathds{Q}\big)
as the
Frank-Wolfe(FW)-gap
at
\mathds{P}
, and it is crucial in defining aposteriori stopping criteria for the FW-algorithm. Along with the distribution
\mathds{Q}^{\prime}
, we assume that the FW-oracle also provides access to the quantity
{\rm d}R\big(\mathds{P};\mathds{Q}^{\prime}\big)
, which is an approximate of the FW-gap at
\mathds{P}
.

Assumption 4.1

(Accessibility of FW oracle)
.

We assume that the FW oracle (
22
) is computationally available, i.e., there exists a tractable approach to find a feasible solution from the set (
22
).

To develop our algorithm for NDRO problems, we assume the accessibility of the FW oracle in Assumption
4.1
. However, given a DRO problem at hand, one needs to ensure that the corresponding FW oracle is indeed computationally available. When the directional derivatives are linear in
\mathds{Q}
, the corresponding FW problem is a linear worst-case distribution problem, akin to the linear DRO problems (
3
). The properties of the loss function
\ell(\xi)
(suppressing the decision variable
x
in (
3
)) under which the respective linear DRO enjoys a tractable reformulation have been extensively studied in the literature. Next, we provide an example of this kind.

Remark 4.2

(Tractable FW oracle)
.

For the RR measures in Definition
2.1
, the FW oracle is a lienar DRO (
3
) with the loss function
\ell(\xi)=\left\langle\nabla r(\mathds{E}_{\mathds{P}}[L(\xi)]),\,L(\xi)\right\rangle
(cf. (
10
)). If this loss can, for instance, be described as a sum of pointwise maximum of concave functions (i.e.,
\ell(\xi)=\sum_{t\leqslant T}\max_{k\leqslant K}\ell_{tk}(\xi)
), we know that the linear DRO (
3
) under the Wasserstein ambiguity set
\mathcal{P}
and the norm transportation cost
\|\cdot\|
has a tractable reformulation
[
27
, Theorem 6.1]
. In this light, a sufficient condition for the RR measure is when each element of the sufficient statistic vector
L(\xi)
constitutes a pointwise maximum of finitely many concave functions and
\nabla r\geqslant 0
.

We note that the sufficient statistics of the variance and entropic risks in Example
2.2
do not meet the piecewise concavity condition of Remark
4.2
; they are convex quadratic and exponential function, respectively, over the desired support sets. It is interesting to see that in the case of entropic risk, the positivity
\nabla r\geqslant 0
is, however, fulfilled (see Example
3.3
(b)). Besides the tractability setting of Remark
4.2
, the literature includes various other combinations of ambiguity sets and functionals of probability distributions supported on finite support
[
38
]
, the moment-based ambiguity sets
[
10
,
17
,
51
]
, and the metric-based ambiguity set
[
15
,
23
]
, to name but a few.

Lemma 4.3

(FW-one-step-bounds)
.

Consider (
5
) with a risk measure that is
C
-smooth in the sense of Definition
3.6
for some
C\geqslant 0
, and let
R^{\ast}
be its optimal value. Let
\mathcal{F}
be the corresponding FW-oracle as in (
22
) with an arbitrary accuracy parameter
\delta\geqslant 0
. For any
\mathds{P}\in\mathcal{P}
,
\gamma\in[0,1]
, and
\mathds{Q}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{F}(\mathds{P},\gamma)
, let
\mathds{P}_{\gamma}=\mathds{P}+\gamma(\mathds{Q}^{\prime}-\mathds{P})
be the one-step-ahead FW update from
\mathds{P}
with a stepsize of
\gamma
. Then we have

R^{\ast}-R\big(\mathds{P}\big)\;\leqslant\;\sup_{\mathds{Q}\in\mathcal{P}}\;{\rm d}R\big(\mathds{P};\mathds{Q}\big)\;\leqslant\;\frac{1}{\gamma}\Big(R\big(\mathds{P}_{\gamma}\big)-R\big(\mathds{P}\big)\Big)+\gamma C(1+\delta).

(23)

Rearranging the inequality (
23
), the one-step improvement in sub-optimality is seen to be

R^{\ast}-R\big(\mathds{P}_{\gamma}\big)\;\leqslant\;\gamma^{2}C(1+\delta)+(1-\gamma)\big(R^{\ast}-R\big(\mathds{P}\big)\big).

(24)

4.2.
FW convergence guarantees

The FW-algorithm seeks to solve the optimization problem (
5
) by iteratively solving the FW-problem (
21
) using a FW-oracle as in (
22
). More precisely, given a step size sequence
(\gamma_{k})_{k}\subset[0,1]
and a distribution
\mathds{P}_{0}\in\mathcal{P}
, the FW-algorithm generates a sequence of distributions
(\mathds{P}_{k})_{k}
such that

\text{FW-algorithm:}\quad\mathds{P}_{k+1}=\mathds{P}_{k}+\gamma_{k}(\mathds{Q}_{k}-\mathds{P}_{k}),\quad\text{where}\quad\mathds{Q}_{k}\in\mathcal{F}(\mathds{P}_{k},\gamma_{k}).

(25)

It must be observed that the implementation of the FW algorithm (
25
) does not depend on the choice of a norm on the ambiguity set
\mathcal{P}
. Therefore, it is desirable to have a
norm-independent
analysis of the FW algorithm. To this end, we take inspiration from
[
19
]
, which has a similar analysis for the convergence of FW-algorithm for finite-dimensional problems by working with the notion of
curvature co-efficient
instead of the canonical smoothness (
16
) used in
[
13
]
. It turns out that the notion of smoothness defined in Definition
3.6
is also amenable to a similar analysis of the FW-algorithm; with the advantage that (
15
) is more in-line as a generalized notion of smoothness from (
16
) and (
17
).

Proposition 4.4

(Apriori bounds)
.

Consider (
5
) with a risk measure that is
C
-smooth in the sense of Definition
3.6
for some
C\in[0,+\infty)
, and let
R^{\ast}
be its optimal value. For
k=1,2,\ldots,
let
(\mathds{P}_{k})_{k}
be the sequence of distributions obtained from the FW-algorithm (
25
) with a step size sequence
\gamma_{k}=\frac{2}{k+2}
and some
\mathds{P}_{0}\in\mathcal{P}
. Then

R^{\ast}-R\big(\mathds{P}_{k}\big)\;\leqslant\;\frac{4C}{k+2}(1+\delta)\quad\text{ for all }k\geqslant 1.

(26)

Regarding the FW algorithm (
25
), an important observation to be made is that the “complexity” of the distribution
\mathds{P}_{k}
may, in general, increase with each update of
FW
-iteration. To make this more clear, suppose that the distributions of
\mathds{P}_{k}
and
\mathds{Q}_{k}
are both discrete with potentially different support sets. Then, it is straightforward to see that the support of the next iteration
\mathds{P}_{k+1}
is the union of the two supports, and as such, its cardinality increases in each iteration. However, this issue can be avoided in the case of regular risk measures.

Remark 4.5

(Sufficient statistics & reduced FW update)
.

When the risk measure is regular in the sense of Definition
2.1
(i.e.,
R\big(\mathds{P}\big)=r\big(\mathds{E}_{\mathds{P}}[L(\xi)]\big)
), we recall from (
10
) that the G-derivative
{\rm d}R\big(\mathds{P};\mathds{Q}\big)
is characterized entirely in terms of the finite-dimensional sufficient statistic vector
\mathds{E}_{\mathds{P}}[L(\xi)]
. The FW algorithm (
25
) reduces to the finite-dimensional update

\displaystyle\mu_{k+1}=\mu_{k}+\gamma_{k}\big(\nu_{k}-\mu_{k}\big),\quad\text{where}\quad\nu_{k}\in\big\{\mathds{E}_{\mathds{Q}}[L(\xi)]\in\mathbb{R}^{m}~:~\mathds{Q}\in\mathcal{F}(\mathds{P},\gamma)\big\}

(27)

in which
\mathcal{F}(\mathds{P},\gamma)
is the FW-oracle (
22
), and the initial condition is
\mu_{0}=\mathds{E}_{\widehat{\mathds{P}}}[L(\xi)]
. When the FW-oracle (
22
) is computationally available (e.g., the tractable cases in Remark
4.2
), it suffices to follow the reduced finite-dimensional update rule (
27
), instead of the infinite-dimensional update (
25
). In other words, the worst-case risk (
5
) can be solved via
\sup_{\mathds{P}\in\mathcal{P}}R\big(\mathds{P}\big)=r(\mu^{\ast})
, where
\mu^{\ast}
is the convergent point of the reduced FW-algorithm in (
27
). Thus, the finite vector
\mathds{E}_{\mathds{P}}[L(\xi)]
is indeed “sufficient” for computing the worst-case risk via the FW algorithm.

We note that the sufficient statistics in Remark (
4.5
) becomes more involved when an additional decision such as
x
in (
4
) must be determined. This shall be addressed in the next section.

4.3.
FW-gap based termination and aposteriori bounds

Suppose that we know some
C^{\prime}\geqslant 0
that satisfies the smoothness condition (
15
). Then for any given
\varepsilon>0
, if the FW algorithm is run for
K\geqslant\left\lceil\frac{4C^{\prime}(1+\delta)}{\varepsilon}\right\rceil-2
iterations with the stepsize sequence
\gamma_{k}=\frac{2}{k+2}
, then the last iterate
\mathds{P}_{K}
is guaranteed to be
\varepsilon
sub-optimal in objective value. Thus, in principle, it suffices to only know some upper bound
C^{\prime}\geqslant C
for the smoothness constant. However, if finding
C
exactly is challenging, and the known upper bound
C^{\prime}
is not tight; the theoretically guaranteed number of iterations required for
\varepsilon
-sub-optimality may not be practical. In such a setting, it turns out that the optimal value of the FW-problem (
21
) called the
FW-gap
provides a good measure to define aposteriori stopping criteria. Moreover, we will also see later for NDRO problems that terminating the FW algorithm when the FW-gap is small provides worst-case performance bounds in the context of DRO problems. We follow the analysis of
[
19
,
9
]
by considering the FW algorithm under two regimes of stepsize sequence to obtain provable upper bounds on the FW-gap towards later iterations.

Since the oracle employed to solve the linear minimization sub-problems at each iteration is only accurate to some specified precision, the actual value of the FW-gap
\sup_{\mathds{Q}\in\mathcal{P}}\,{\rm d}R\big(\mathds{P};\mathds{Q}\big)
is never known exactly. However, at each iteration
k
, the FW-oracle does provide its approximate estimate
{\rm d}R\big(\mathds{P};\mathds{Q}_{k}\big)
, which satisfies the inequality
\sup_{\mathds{Q}\in\mathcal{P}}\,{\rm d}R\big(\mathds{P};\mathds{Q}\big)\leqslant\delta\gamma_{k}C+{\rm d}R\big(\mathds{P};\mathds{Q}_{k}\big)
. Thus, for a given value of
\varepsilon>0
, we terminate the FW procedure by examining the quantity
{\rm d}R\big(\mathds{P};\mathds{Q}_{k}\big)
such that the desired FW-gap inequality:
\sup_{\mathds{Q}\in\mathcal{P}}\,{\rm d}R\big(\mathds{P};\mathds{Q}\big)\leqslant\varepsilon
, is satisfied.

Proposition 4.6

(Aposteriori bounds)
.

Consider (
5
) with a risk measure that is
C
-smooth in the sense of Definition
3.6
for some
C\geqslant 0
, and let
R^{\ast}
be its optimal value. Let
K\geqslant 1
and let
(\mathds{P}_{k})_{k}
be the sequence obtained from the FW-algorithm (
25
) using a diminishing stepsize
\gamma_{k}\coloneqq\frac{2}{k+2}
for
k=0,1,\ldots,K-1
, and then a constant stepsize
\gamma_{k}=\frac{2}{K+2}
for
k=K,K+1,\ldots,2K+1
. Finally, let
g_{k}={\rm d}R\big(\mathds{P};\mathds{Q}_{k}\big)
for
k=1,2,\ldots,2K+1
be the sequence of approximate FW-gaps. There exists
{\widehat{k}}\in\{K,K+1,\ldots,2K+1\}
such that

R^{\ast}-R\big(\mathds{P}_{{\widehat{k}}}\big)\;\leqslant\;\sup_{\mathds{Q}\in\mathcal{P}}{\rm d}R\big(\mathds{P}_{\widehat{k}};\mathds{Q}\big)\;\leqslant\;\frac{2C(2+3\delta)}{K+2},

(28)

and every such
{\widehat{k}}
is recognised by verifying the inequality
g_{{\widehat{k}}}\leqslant\frac{4C}{K+2}(1+\delta)
.

Remark 4.7

(Explicit error bounds)
.

Consider the setting of Proposition
4.6
with
K=K(\varepsilon)\coloneqq\left\lceil\frac{2C}{\varepsilon}(2+3\delta)\right\rceil-2
for an
\varepsilon>0
. Then, there exists
{\widehat{k}}\in\{K(\varepsilon),K(\varepsilon)+1,\ldots,2K(\varepsilon)+1\}
such that

\sup_{\mathds{Q}\in\mathcal{P}}\ {\rm d}R\big(\mathds{P}_{\widehat{k}};\mathds{Q}\big)\leqslant\varepsilon,

and every such
{\widehat{k}}
is recognised by verifying the inequality

g_{{\widehat{k}}}\leqslant\varepsilon\frac{2+2\delta}{2+3\delta}
.

4.4.
FW stepsize selection

The last part of this section discusses two particular features of the stepsize rule (two-regimes and diminishing behavior) in Proposition
4.6
.

Two-regimes stepsize

The two-regimes for
\gamma_{k}
in Proposition
4.6
turns out to be crucial to obtain provable guarantees that the FW-gap is bounded above in the later iterations. Even though such certificates are of independent interest in their own right, having such upper bounds is also essential in the context of DRO problems. An upper bound on the FW-gap at iteration
k
ensures that the performance of the decision
x_{k}
for the worst-case distribution is not “too-bad”.

Different stepsize selection for the FW algorithm

For generic feasible sets, the Frank-Wolfe algorithm requires that the stepsize sequence
(\gamma_{k})_{k}
be diminishing. Even though the risk measure is smooth, the direction
\mathds{Q}^{\prime}_{k}
may change dis-continuously around the optimal solution if the ambiguity set
\mathcal{P}
has flat faces (like Wasserstein-
1
balls). Therefore, the Frank-Wolfe algorithm in general requires diminishing stepsize to converge, and might not converge with constant stepsize unlike gradient descent algorithms. Even with the standard diminishing stepsize of
\gamma_{k}=\nicefrac{{2}}{{k+2}}
, the canonical FW algorithm is plagued with the
zig-zag
phenomena where the iterates keep oscillating around the optimal solution. To remedy these challenges of FW in finite-dimensional convex problems, various adaptive stepsize sequences have been proposed in the literature with provably better guarantees under some additional assumptions. In the following, we describe the main ideas of these stepsize selection rules in the context of (
5
).

(a)

Demyanov-Rubinov (DR) stepsize
: A very interesting stepsize selection is due to
[
49
,
48
]

\gamma_{k}=\min\left\{\frac{g_{k}}{2C},1\right\},

(29)

which adaptively tunes the stepsize
\gamma_{k}
using the current value of the FW-gap.

(b)

Backtracking
: Suppose, it is relatively easy to evaluate the risk measure
R
, then one can employ the backtracking based DR step size due to
[
36
,
35
]
. This stepsize selection rule adaptive tunes the smoothness constant
C
locally along the line segment
(\mathds{P}_{k},\mathds{Q}^{\prime}_{k})
. This allows the backtracking step size to take larger steps than other methods. Specific way to adaptively tune the smoothness constant may vary, the specific rule in
[
36
]
can be summarised as follows. For fixed constants
\eta\in(0,1),\tau\geqslant 1
, at each iteration the stepsize rule is

\begin{cases}\begin{aligned} \gamma_{k}&=\min\left\{\frac{g_{t}}{2\tau^{t}C_{k}},1\right\}\text{ for the largest $t=1,2,\ldots$ such that }\\
R\big(\mathds{P}_{k+1}\big)&\geqslant R\big(\mathds{P}_{k}\big)+\gamma_{k}g_{k}-\gamma^{2}\tau^{t}C_{k}.\end{aligned}\end{cases}

(30)

The smoothness constant is also updated as
C_{k+1}=\eta\tau^{t}C_{k}
. Another variant
[
35
, Assumption 6.1, Algorithm 1]
of backtracking-based step size selection in the FW algorithm gives rise to linear/geometric convergence with further assumptions on the feasible set.

(c)

Exact line search
: If it is easier to optimize the risk measure
R
on the line segment
(\mathds{P}_{k},\mathds{Q}^{\prime}_{k})
, then one can also select the stepsize by exactly maximizing

\gamma_{k}=\argmax_{\gamma\in[0,1]}\quad R\big(\mathds{P}_{k}+\gamma(\mathds{Q}^{\prime}_{k}-\mathds{P}_{k})\big).

(31)

5.
Nonlinear Distributionally Robust Optimization

Let us recall that a generic DRO problem is formulated as the min-max problem

F^{\ast}\coloneqq\inf_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\sup_{\mathds{P}\in\mathcal{P}}F\big(x,\mathds{P}\big)\,,

(32)

where
\mathcal{X}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a closed convex set, denoting the set of feasible decisions, and
\mathcal{P}
denotes a given ambiguity set of distributions. A DRO problem is said to be
feasible
if
F^{\ast}<+\infty
, which happens if and only if there exists some
x\in\mathcal{X}
such that
\sup_{\mathds{P}\in\mathcal{P}}F\big(x,\mathds{P}\big)<+\infty
. Our objective is to develop a framework to solve a generic DRO problem (
32
). Particularly, with emphasis on the case when
F\big(x,\mathds{P}\big)
is nonlinear in
\mathds{P}
for every
x\in\mathcal{X}
, to which we refer to (
32
) as a
nonlinear distributionally robust optimization
(NDRO) problem.

The way the variable
x
enters the risk
F
in (
32
) may have an impact on the scalability of the proposed FW algorithm. In particular, in the case of the regular risks in Definition
2.1
, the important feature is whether the decision
x
influences the sufficient statistics of the risk (cf. Remark
4.5
). This consideration leads to two classes of regular risk measures:

\displaystyle\text{(i)}\,F(x,\mathds{P})=r\big(x,\mathds{E}_{\mathds{P}}[L(\xi)]\big)\qquad\text{and}\quad\text{(ii)}\,F(x,\mathds{P})=r\big(\mathds{E}_{\mathds{P}}[L(x,\xi)]\big).

(33)

It should be noted that class (i) in (
33
) is a special form of class (ii). For regular risk measures in class (i), the sufficient statistic
\mathds{E}_{\mathds{P}}[L(\xi)]
is
not
influenced by the decision
x
, whereas this is not the case for general regular risk measures in class (ii). This subtle difference makes a significant impact on whether the FW algorithm can benefit from the notion of sufficient statistic described in Remark
4.5
. The variance and entropic risks investigated in this article are indeed different in view of this feature.

Example 5.1

(Regular NDRO examples)
.

The analogous examples of Example
2.2
in the NDRO context are the following:

(a)

Variance
: The variance
V(x,\mathds{P})
(cf. (
6
)) associated with the distribution
\mathds{P}
and a decision
x
is

V(x,\mathds{P})\coloneqq x^{\top}\big(\Sigma_{\mathds{P}}-\mu_{\mathds{P}}\mu_{\mathds{P}}^{\top}\big)x,\quad\text{where}\quad\Sigma_{\mathds{P}}\coloneqq\mathds{E}_{\mathds{P}}[\xi\xi^{\top}]~\text{and}~\mu_{\mathds{P}}\coloneqq\mathds{E}_{\mathds{P}}[\xi].

(34)

(b)

Entropic risk measure
: The entropic risk measure
\mathcal{E}(x,\mathds{P})
(cf. (
7
)) of a multivariate distribution
\mathds{P}
and decision
x
. If
\mathds{P}
is a distribution with marginals
\mathds{P}_{j}
for
j=1,2,\ldots,n
, (i.e., the
j
-th component
\xi_{j}
is
\mathds{P}_{j}
distributed). For a given set of positive
risk-aversion parameters

(\theta_{j})_{j=1}^{n}
in
(0,+\infty)
, the associated risk is defined as

\mathcal{E}(x,\mathds{P})\coloneqq\sum_{j=1}^{n}{\frac{1}{\theta_{j}}\log\left(\mathds{E}_{\mathds{P}_{j}}[e^{-\theta_{j}x_{j}\xi_{j}}]\right)}.

(35)

It is straightforward to see that the NDRO variance risk (
34
) belongs to the class (i) in (
33
), and thus keeping its sufficient statistic (i.e., the first two moments) intact, while the NDRO entropic risk (
35
) belongs to the class (ii) in (
33
) where the vector
L(x,\xi)
depends inseparably on
x
. Looking ahead at (
64
), we see that the proposed FW Algorithm
1
applied to variance risk measure simplifies to iterations over only sufficient statistic
(\mu,\Sigma)
and
x
.

NDRO Dual problem

Associated with the DRO problem (
32
) is its dual problem:

\text{Dual problem:}\quad F_{*}\coloneqq\sup_{\mathds{P}\in\mathcal{P}}\inf_{x\in\mathcal{X}}F\big(x,\mathds{P}\big).

(36)

In general, we have
weak-duality

F_{*}\leqslant F^{\ast}
relating the optimal values of the primal problem (
32
) and its dual (
36
). If
F_{*}=F^{\ast}
specifically, we say that
strong duality
holds between (
32
) and (
36
). Moreover, suppose the DRO problem (
32
) and its dual (
36
) admit the solutions
x^{\ast}
and
\mathds{P}^{\ast}
, i.e.,

x^{\ast}\in\argmin_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\sup_{\mathds{P}\in\mathcal{P}}F\big(x,\mathds{P}\big)\quad\text{and}\quad\mathds{P}^{\ast}\in\argmax_{\mathds{P}\in\mathcal{P}}\inf_{x\in\mathcal{X}}F\big(x,\mathds{P}\big),

Then, the pair
(x^{\ast},\mathds{P}^{\ast})
is said to be a
saddle point
solution to the problems (
32
) and (
36
), which is also characterized by the condition

\max_{\mathds{P}\in\mathcal{P}}F\big(x^{\ast},\mathds{P}\big)\;=\;F\big(x^{\ast},\mathds{P}^{\ast}\big)\;=\;\min_{x\in\mathcal{X}}F\big(x,\mathds{P}^{\ast}\big).

The existence of a saddle point is sufficient for strong duality to hold, however, it is not necessary. Therefore, whenever strong duality holds, we consider the slightly relaxed notion of an
\varepsilon
-sub-optimal saddle points as a solution concept for (
32
).

Definition 5.2

(
\varepsilon
-saddle point)
.

Given
\varepsilon\geqslant 0
, a pair
\big(x_{\varepsilon},\mathds{P}_{\varepsilon}\big)\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{P}
is an
\varepsilon
-saddle point of the DRO problem (
32
), if it satisfies

-\varepsilon+\sup_{\mathds{P}\in\mathcal{P}}F\big(x_{\varepsilon},\mathds{P}\big)\;\leqslant\;F\big(x_{\varepsilon},\mathds{P}_{\varepsilon}\big)\;\leqslant\;\varepsilon+\inf_{x\in\mathcal{X}}F\big(x,\mathds{P}_{\varepsilon}\big).

(37)

It is worth noting that if
\big(x_{\varepsilon},\mathds{P}_{\varepsilon}\big)
is an
\varepsilon
-saddle point, then we have the inequalities:

\displaystyle\sup\limits_{\mathds{Q}\in\mathcal{P}}F\big(x_{\varepsilon},\mathds{Q}\big)

\displaystyle\leqslant\;2\varepsilon+\inf\limits_{y\in\mathcal{X}}F\big(y,\mathds{P}_{\varepsilon}\big)\;\leqslant\;2\varepsilon+\inf\limits_{y\in\mathcal{X}}\sup_{\mathds{Q}\in\mathcal{P}}F\big(y,\mathds{Q}\big)\

\displaystyle=\ 2\varepsilon+F^{\ast},\quad\text{and}

\displaystyle\inf\limits_{y\in\mathcal{X}}F\big(y,\mathds{P}_{\varepsilon}\big)

\displaystyle\geqslant\;-2\varepsilon+\sup_{\mathds{Q}\in\mathcal{P}}F\big(x_{\varepsilon},\mathds{Q}\big)\;\geqslant\;-2\varepsilon+\sup_{\mathds{Q}\in\mathcal{P}}\inf\limits_{y\in\mathcal{X}}F\big(y,\mathds{Q}\big)

\displaystyle=-2\varepsilon+F_{*}.

In other words, if
(x_{\varepsilon},\mathds{P}_{\varepsilon})
is an
\varepsilon
-saddle point, then both
x_{\varepsilon}
and
\mathds{P}_{\varepsilon}
are at most
2\varepsilon
-sub-optimal to the DRO problem (
32
) and its dual (
36
) respectively. Consequently, the decision
x_{\varepsilon}
is guaranteed to be at most
2\varepsilon
worse from the best decision that could have been made in a DRO framework.

Solving the minimization over
x
in the dual-problem (
36
) results in a maximization problem (potentially nonlinear) over the distributions

\sup_{\mathds{P}\in\mathcal{P}}R\big(\mathds{P}\big),\quad\text{ where }R\big(\mathds{P}\big)\coloneqq\inf\limits_{x\in\mathcal{X}}F\big(x,\mathds{P}\big).

(38)

Denoting
x(\mathds{P})\coloneqq\argmin_{x\in\mathcal{X}}F\big(x,\mathds{P}\big)
(whenever a minimizer exists), for every
\mathds{P}\in\mathcal{P}
, the proposed method to compute an
\varepsilon
-saddle point of the DRO problem generates a sequence
(x_{k},\mathds{P}_{k})_{k}
, where
x_{k}\in x(\mathds{P}_{k})
for each
k
, and the sequence of distributions
(\mathds{P}_{k})_{k}
is obtained by applying the FW algorithm to the maximization problem (
38
). If a pair
(x^{\prime},\mathds{P}^{\prime})
satisfies
\mathds{P}^{\prime}\in\argmax_{\mathds{P}\in\mathcal{P}}\;R\big(\mathds{P}\big)
and
x^{\prime}\in x(\mathds{P}^{\prime})
, then it is not guaranteed that
x^{\prime}\in\argmin_{y\in\mathcal{X}}\;\sup_{\mathds{P}\in\mathcal{P}}\;F\big(y,\mathds{P}\big)
unless
x(\mathds{P}^{\prime})
is unique. It so turns out that the regularity assumptions on
F
and
\mathcal{P}
, required for the algorithm convergence, also ensure uniqueness.

5.1.
NDRO: continuity, derivatives and smoothness

Our proposed method to solve the NDRO problem (
32
) by applying the FW algorithm to (
38
) requires that the risk measure
R
therein has well defined G-derivatives that are also smooth in the sense of Definition
3.6
. This is not guaranteed apriori. In the following, we impose some regularity assumptions on the function
F
that guarantee the required smoothness of the risk measure
R
.

Assumption 5.3

(NDRO smoothness)
.

Let
\mathds{P}_{\gamma}\coloneqq\mathds{P}+\gamma\big(\mathds{Q}-\mathds{P}\big)
as in (
9
) and denote
F_{x}(\cdot)\coloneqq F\big(x,\cdot\big)
, for every
x\in\mathcal{X}
. We assume that there exists positive constants
\alpha,C_{1},C_{2}
such that

(i)

Continuous function: The mapping
\mathcal{X}\times[0,1]\ni(x,\gamma)\longmapsto F\big(x,\mathds{P}_{\gamma}\big)
is proper, convex-concave, and continuous for all
\mathds{P},\mathds{Q}\in\mathcal{P}
.

(ii)

Continuous derivatives
: The function
F_{x}(\mathds{P})
is directionally differentiable on
\mathcal{P}
, and the G-derivative
{\rm d}F_{x}(\mathds{P};\mathds{Q})
is
C_{1}
-Lipschitz continuous in
x
uniformly over
\mathds{P},\mathds{Q}\in\mathcal{P}
, i.e.,

{\rm d}F_{x}(\mathds{P};\mathds{Q})-{\rm d}F_{y}(\mathds{P};\mathds{Q})\;\leqslant\;C_{1}\left\lVert x-y\right\rVert,\qquad\forall\,x,y\in\mathcal{X}\quad\forall\,\mathds{P},\mathds{Q}\in\mathcal{P}.

(39)

(iii)

Smoothness
: The function
F_{x}(\mathds{P})
is
C_{2}
-smooth in the sense of Definition
3.6
, uniformly over
x\in\mathcal{X}
.

(iv)

Strong convexity
: The function
F\big(x,\mathds{P}\big)
is
\alpha
-strongly convex in
x
w.r.t. the norm
\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert
, uniformly over all
\mathds{P}\in\mathcal{P}
, i.e.,

\frac{\alpha}{2}\left\lVert x-y\right\rVert^{2}\leqslant F\big(y,\mathds{P}\big)-F\big(x,\mathds{P}\big)-\left\langle\nabla_{1}F\big(x,\mathds{P}\big),\,y-x\right\rangle,\quad\forall\,x,y\in\mathcal{X},\quad\forall\,\mathds{P},\mathds{Q}\in\mathcal{P}.

(40)

Remark 5.4

(Choice of norm on
\mathcal{X}
)
.

It must be noted that the norm
\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert
considered in the strong convexity assumption (
40
) and the continuity assumption (
39
) is identical. Considering an identical norm is not restrictive since all norms on
\mathcal{X}
are equivalent. However, using such equivalence often makes the resulting constants
\alpha,C_{1}

Theorem 5.6

(NDRO solution)

Consider the Nonlinear DRO problem (

(i)

F^{\ast}\;=\;\min\limits_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\sup\limits_{\mathds{P}\in\mathcal{P}}F\big(x,\mathds{P}\big)\;=\;\sup\limits_{\mathds{P}\in\mathcal{P}}\min\limits_{x\in\mathcal{X}}F\big(x,\mathds{P}\big)\;=\;F_{*}

(43)

(ii)

\varepsilon

Approaching an NDRO problem via the FW-based approach in Algorithm

(i)

Regularity conditions of Assumption (
5.3
)
: To ensure the convergence of the FW algorithm, we must ensure that the corresponding G-derivatives exist, and satisfy regularity conditions of Assumption
5.3
, particularly (ii)
continuous derivatives
and (iii)

(ii)

Solver for
\min_{x\in\mathcal{X}}F\big(x,\mathds{P}\big)
in (

)
: Since it is a finite-dimensional convex problem, any well known first-order methods like ISTA, FISTA
[

, Nesterov’s Accelerated Gradient Descent
[

can be applied. Moreover, since the risk function

is assumed to be strongly convex in

(iii)

Feasibility of the FW oracle (
42

: The FW worst-case distribution problem must admit tractable reformulations. In fact, when the directional derivatives are linear in the distribution, the corresponding FW problem is indeed tractable for several interesting choices of risk measures and ambiguity sets, as discussed in
[

,

,

]
.

5.3.
Slower convergence without strong-convexity

In this case, we assume that the function
F
satisfies conditions (ii) and (iii) of Assumption
5.3
. However, it may not be necessarily strongly convex in
x
. For example, the variance risk measure is strongly convex if and only if the smallest eigenvalue of the matrix
\big(\Sigma_{\mathds{P}}-\mu_{\mathds{P}}\mu_{\mathds{P}}^{\top}\big)
is bounded away from
0
uniformly over
\mathds{P}\in\mathcal{P}
, which might not be the case. Even in such a setting, we desire to develop methods that compute an
\varepsilon
-saddle point of
F
using the setup of Algorithm
1
for any
\varepsilon>0
. We take inspiration from the smoothing techniques in the optimization literature
[
32
]
for smoothing a non-smooth convex function and devise similar techniques that work with a suitable strongly-convex approximation
F_{\varepsilon}
, of
F
, and still use Algorithm
1
to compute an
\varepsilon
-saddle point of
F
. To this end, we assume that the set
\mathcal{X}
is also bounded in addition to being closed and, thus, compact. Many common examples of
\mathcal{X}
like the simplex
\Delta^{n}
, satisfy the compactness assumption.

For any given an
\varepsilon>0
,
x\in\mathcal{X}
, and
\mathds{P}\in\mathcal{P}
, let
F_{\varepsilon}(x,\mathds{P})\coloneqq F\big(x,\mathds{P}\big)+\big(\nicefrac{{\varepsilon}}{{B_{x}^{2}}}\big)\left\lVert x\right\rVert^{2}
. We propose to solve the following min-max problem in place of (
32
)

\min_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\sup_{\mathds{P}\in\mathcal{P}}F_{\varepsilon}(x,\mathds{P}),

(44)

where
B_{x}\coloneqq\max_{x\in X}\left\lVert x\right\rVert
. It is apparent at once that
F_{\varepsilon}(x,\mathds{P})
is
(\nicefrac{{2\varepsilon}}{{B_{x}^{2}}})
-strongly convex in
x
, uniformly over
\mathds{P}\in\mathcal{P}
, and consequently satisfies all the conditions in Assumption
5.3
. Thus, employing Algorithm
1
with
F_{\varepsilon}
computes a pair
(x^{\prime},\mathds{P}^{\prime})
that satisfies the inequalities

-\varepsilon+\sup_{\mathds{Q}\in\mathcal{P}}F_{\varepsilon}(x^{\prime},\mathds{Q})\;\leqslant\;F_{\varepsilon}(x^{\prime},\mathds{P}^{\prime})\;\leqslant\;\varepsilon+\inf_{y\in\mathcal{X}}F_{\varepsilon}(y,\mathds{P}^{\prime}).

(45)

It turns out that such a pair
(x^{\prime},\mathds{P}^{\prime})
is an
\varepsilon
-saddle point of
F
as well. This is easily seen by observing that on the one hand, we have

\displaystyle\big(\nicefrac{{\varepsilon}}{{B_{x}^{2}}}\big)\left\lVert x^{\prime}\right\rVert^{2}+\sup\limits_{\mathds{Q}\in\mathcal{P}}F\big(x^{\prime},\mathds{Q}\big)

\displaystyle=\;\sup\limits_{\mathds{Q}\in\mathcal{P}}F_{\varepsilon}(x^{\prime},\mathds{Q})\;\leqslant\;\varepsilon+F_{\varepsilon}(x^{\prime},\mathds{P}^{\prime})\quad\text{(from \eqref{eq:approx-saddle-point-ineq})}

\displaystyle=\ \varepsilon+\big(\nicefrac{{\varepsilon}}{{B_{x}^{2}}}\big)\left\lVert x^{\prime}\right\rVert^{2}+F\big(x^{\prime},\mathds{P}^{\prime}\big).

Thus, we immediately get
\sup_{\mathds{Q}\in\mathcal{P}}\ F\big(x^{\prime},\mathds{Q}\big)\leqslant\varepsilon+F\big(x^{\prime},\mathds{P}^{\prime}\big)
. On the other hand, since the minimization over
x
is solved exactly in Algorithm
1
, we have

\displaystyle F_{\varepsilon}(x^{\prime},\mathds{P}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\;\min_{y\in\mathcal{X}}F_{\varepsilon}(y,\mathds{P}^{\prime})\;=\;\min_{y\in\mathcal{X}}F\big(y,\mathds{P}^{\prime}\big)+\big(\nicefrac{{\varepsilon}}{{B_{x}^{2}}}\big)\left\lVert y\right\rVert^{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant\ \varepsilon+\min_{y\in\mathcal{X}}F\big(y,\mathds{P}^{\prime}\big)\quad\text{since $\left\lVert y\right\rVert\leqslant B_{x}$ for all $y\in\mathcal{X}$ }.

Thus, it is apparent that the pair
(x^{\prime},\mathds{P}^{\prime})
is an
\varepsilon
-saddle point of the function
F
as well.

Corollary 5.7

(Slower convergence)
.

Consider a function
F\big(x,\mathds{P}\big)
that is not necessarily strongly convex in
x
. Then for any desired precision
\varepsilon>0
, an
\varepsilon
-saddle point of
F
can be computed by applying Algorithm
1
with
K(\varepsilon)=\left\lceil\frac{2C_{\varepsilon}}{\varepsilon}(2+3\delta)\right\rceil-2
, to the strongly convex approximate function
F^{\varepsilon}
, where
C_{\varepsilon}=C_{2}+\frac{C_{1}B_{x}^{2}}{4\varepsilon}\Big(C_{1}+\sqrt{C_{1}^{2}+\frac{8\varepsilon}{B_{x}^{2}}C_{2}}\Big)
.

Since the strong convexity parameter of
F_{\varepsilon}
itself depends on
\varepsilon
, we conclude from Lemma
5.5
that the risk measure
R_{\varepsilon}(\mathds{P})\coloneqq\min_{y\in\mathcal{X}}F_{\varepsilon}(y,\mathds{P})
, also has an
\varepsilon
dependent smoothness constant
C_{\varepsilon}=C_{2}+\frac{C_{1}B_{x}^{2}}{4\varepsilon}\Big(C_{1}+\sqrt{C_{1}^{2}+\frac{8\varepsilon}{B_{x}^{2}}C_{2}}\Big)
. Now, Algorithm
1
terminates in
O(K(\varepsilon))
iterations, where
K(\varepsilon)=O(\nicefrac{{C_{\varepsilon}}}{{\varepsilon}})
. Since
C_{\varepsilon}=O(\nicefrac{{1}}{{\varepsilon}})
, it is easily seen that for non-strongly convex functions
F
, applying Algorithm
1
to its regularized strongly-convex approximation
F_{\varepsilon}
takes
O(\nicefrac{{1}}{{\varepsilon^{2}}})
iterations to compute an
\varepsilon
-saddle point of
F
. To compare, recall that for a strongly-convex function
F
, Algorithm
1
takes
O(\nicefrac{{1}}{{\varepsilon}})
iterations to compute an
\varepsilon
-saddle point. This trade-off between speed of convergence and precision in the approximation is a typical occurrence in standard smoothing techniques as well.

6.

This section is dedicated to study the proposed methodology and its required conditions for the entropic risk (
35

To this end, let
{\xi}(t)
,
t=1,2,\ldots,T
be i.i.d. samples drawn from some unknown underlying distribution
\mathds{P}_{o}
supported on
\Xi=\mathbb{R}^{n}

\widehat{\mathds{P}}_{i}\coloneqq\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\delta(\xi_{i}(t))

\widehat{\mathds{P}}=\Pi_{i=1}^{n}\widehat{\mathds{P}}_{i}

\mathds{P},\mathds{Q}
supported on

\mathsf{WD}_{c}(\mathds{P},\mathds{Q})

\mathsf{WD}_{c}(\mathds{P},\mathds{Q})\coloneqq\begin{cases}\begin{aligned} &\sup_{\pi}&&\mathds{E}_{\pi}\left[e^{c\left\lvert u-v\right\rvert}\right]\quad\text{where $(u,v)$ are $\pi$-jointly distributed }\\
&\sbjto&&\mathds{P}(u)=\int_{v}\pi(u,v)dv\quad\text{ and }\quad\mathds{Q}(v)=\int_{u}\pi(u,v)du.\end{aligned}\end{cases}

(46)

For each

\mathcal{P}_{c}=\Pi_{j=1}^{n}\mathcal{W}_{c}((\widehat{\mathds{P}}_{j},\rho))

Let
\theta_{j}\in(0,1)

\min_{x\in\Delta^{n}}\sup_{\mathds{P}\in\mathcal{P}_{c}}\quad\mathcal{E}(x,\mathds{P})\coloneqq\sum_{j=1}^{n}{\frac{1}{\theta_{j}}\log\left(\mathds{E}_{\mathds{P}_{j}}[e^{-\theta_{j}x_{j}\xi_{j}}]\right)}.

6.1.
Regularity conditions

Recalling the entropic risk (
7

\mathds{P}\longmapsto\mathcal{E}(x,\mathds{P})

L(x,\xi)=\Big(e^{-\theta_{1}x_{1}\xi_{1}},e^{-\theta_{2}x_{2}\xi_{2}},\ldots,e^{-\theta_{n}x_{m}\xi_{n}}\Big)\quad\text{and}\quad r(z)=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{1}{\theta_{j}}\log(z_{j}).

Thus, in view of Lemma
3.2
and using the short-hand notation
\mathcal{E}_{x}(\cdot)\coloneqq\mathcal{E}(x,\cdot)

{\rm d}\mathcal{E}_{x}(\mathds{P};\mathds{Q})=\sum_{j=1}^{n}{\frac{\mathds{E}_{\mathds{Q}_{j}-\mathds{P}_{j}}[e^{-\theta_{j}x_{j}\xi_{j}}]}{\theta_{j}\mathds{E}_{\mathds{P}_{j}}[e^{-\theta_{j}x_{j}\xi_{j}}]}}\quad\text{for every }\mathds{P},\mathds{Q}\in\mathcal{P}.

(48)

It turns out that for any saddle point

Definition 6.1

(Restricted ambiguity set)

For each

\underline{\xi}_{j}\coloneqq-\rho-\frac{\log(T)}{c}+\min_{t=1,\ldots,T}\xi_{j}(t)\quad\text{ and }\quad\overline{\xi}_{j}=\max_{t=1,\ldots,T}\xi_{j}(t),

Lemma 6.2

(Restricted ambiguity set)

A pair

Lemma 6.3

\theta>0

Minimum Variance Portfolio Selection

The minimum variance portfolio selection (
34
) is one of the textbook examples of NDRO
[
26
,
37
,
4
,
34
]
. This section is dedicated to studying the NDRO regularity of this example and the different aspects of the proposed algorithm in this context. To this end, let
\widehat{\xi}_{i}
:
i=1,2,\ldots,N
be i.i.d. samples drawn from some unknown underlying distribution
\mathds{P}_{o}
, and let
\widehat{\mathds{P}}
be the nominal distribution that is uniformly distributed over the samples
(\widehat{\xi}_{i})_{i}
. Let
\mathcal{P}=\mathcal{W}_{m}(\widehat{\mathds{P}},\rho)\coloneqq\{\mathds{Q}:\mathsf{WD}_{m}(\widehat{\mathds{P}},\mathds{Q})\leqslant\rho\}
, where
\mathsf{WD}_{m}
is the
m
-th order Wasserstein distance between the distributions, induced by the transportation cost coming from a norm
\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert
on

\mathsf{WD}_{m}(\mathds{P},\mathds{Q})\coloneqq\begin{cases}\begin{aligned} &\sup_{\pi}&&\Big(\mathds{E}_{\pi}[\left\lVert u-v\right\rVert^{m}]\Big)^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{m}}}\quad\text{where $(u,v)$ are $\pi$-jointly distributed }\\
&\sbjto&&\mathds{P}(u)=\int_{v}\pi(u,v)dv\quad\text{ and }\quad\mathds{Q}(v)=\int_{u}\pi(u,v)du.\end{aligned}\end{cases}

Associated with the distribution
\mathds{P}
, let
\Sigma_{\mathds{P}}\coloneqq\mathds{E}_{\mathds{P}}[\xi\xi^{\top}]
and
\mu_{\mathds{P}}\coloneqq\mathds{E}_{\mathds{P}}[\xi]
denote its first and second moments respectively. Finally, let
\mathcal{X}\subset\Delta^{n}
denote the set of feasible actions, then we seek to investigate the min-sup problem:

\min_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\sup_{\mathds{P}\in\mathcal{W}_{m}(\widehat{\mathds{P}},\rho)}V(x,\mathds{P})\coloneqq x^{\top}\big(\Sigma_{\mathds{P}}-\mu_{\mathds{P}}\mu_{\mathds{P}}^{\top}\big)x.

(56)

We shall study in detail the notion of the directional derivative, smoothness, and the resulting FW-oracle with its tractable formulations for (
56
) under different conditions.

7.1.
Regularity conditions

We recall that the variance risk (
6
) and note that the mapping

is an RR measure in the sense of Definition

with the respective sufficient statistic

and the function

L(\xi)=\;(\xi\xi^{\top},\,\xi)\quad\text{and}\quad r\big(x,(\Sigma,\mu)\big)=\;x^{\top}\Sigma x-\big(x^{\top}\mu\big)^{2}.

Unlike the risk entropic in Section

and

Lemma 7.2

(Variance regularity conditions)
.

Consider the minimum variance portfolio optimization problem (

holds with constants

(i)

(ii)

Smoothness:

, uniformly over

.

If Assumption

7.2.
The Frank-Wolfe algorithm

The FW-Oracle

Let us consider the FW-problem arising in the NDRO problem of minimum variance portfolio selection (
56
),

\sup\limits_{\mathds{Q}\in\mathcal{W}_{m}(\widehat{\mathds{P}},\rho)}{\rm d}V_{x}(\mathds{P};\mathds{Q}).

(59)

Since the directional derivatives
{\rm d}V_{x}(\mathds{P};\mathds{Q})
are affine in
\mathds{Q}
for every pair
(x,\mathds{P})
, the corresponding FW-problem is linear. However, the existence and characterization results of the solution to the FW-problem (
59
) change depending on the interplay of (i) the Wasserstein distance type
m
, (ii) the transportation cost
\|\cdot\|
, and (iii) the support set
\Xi
(unbounded, or compact). Therefore, the specific settings for which the corresponding FW-oracle is easy to describe are discussed later in the section. To this end, we simplify (
59
), and study its dual problem which is used later in the characterization of a solution to (
59
).

It is a simple algebraic exercise to verify that

\displaystyle{\rm d}V_{x}(\mathds{P};\mathds{Q})

\displaystyle=\;\mathds{E}_{\mathds{Q}-\mathds{P}}\Big[x^{\top}\big((\xi-2\mu_{\mathds{P}})\xi^{\top}\big)x\big]

\displaystyle=\;\mathds{E}_{\mathds{Q}}\left[(\xi-\mu_{\mathds{P}})^{\top}\big(xx^{\top}\big)(\xi-\mu_{\mathds{P}})\right]\;-\;x^{\top}\big(\Sigma_{\mathds{P}}-\mu_{\mathds{P}}\mu_{\mathds{P}}^{\top}\big)x.

Since the distribution
\mathds{P}
is constant in (
59
), eliminating terms that only depend on
\mathds{P}
does not affect the set of maximizers. Thus, we have

\argmax\limits_{\mathds{Q}\in\mathcal{W}_{m}(\widehat{\mathds{P}},\rho)}{\rm d}V_{x}(\mathds{P};\mathds{Q})\ =\argmax\limits_{\mathds{Q}\in\mathcal{W}_{m}(\widehat{\mathds{P}},\rho)}\mathds{E}_{\mathds{Q}}\left[(\xi-\mu_{\mathds{P}})^{\top}\big(xx^{\top}\big)(\xi-\mu_{\mathds{P}})\right].

(60)

We now focus on a generic version of the linear worst-case distribution problem:

\sup_{\mathds{Q}\in\mathcal{W}_{m}(\widehat{\mathds{P}},\rho)}\mathds{E}_{\mathds{Q}}[(\xi-v)^{\top}\big(xx^{\top}\big)(\xi-v)],

(61)

for any given
x,v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. We known that
[
23
, Theorem 7]
(
61
) admits an equivalent dual formulation given by

\inf_{\eta\geqslant 0}\;\eta\rho^{m}+\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\begin{cases}\begin{aligned} &\sup_{q_{i}}&&(q_{i}+\xi_{i}-v)^{\top}\big(xx^{\top}\big)(q_{i}+\xi_{i}-v)-\eta\left\lVert q_{i}\right\rVert^{m}\\
&\sbjto&&q_{i}+\xi_{i}\in\Xi\quad\text{for all}\quad i=1,2,\ldots,N.\end{aligned}\end{cases}

(62)

For
\eta\geqslant 0
and
\xi\in\Xi
, let

q(\eta,\xi,v)\coloneqq\begin{cases}\begin{aligned} &\argmax_{q}&&\big((x^{\top}q)+x^{\top}(\xi-v)^{\top}\big)^{2}-\eta\left\lVert q\right\rVert^{m}\\
&\sbjto&&q+\xi\in\Xi,\end{aligned}\end{cases}

(63)

whenever an optimal solution exists. If the dual problem (
62
) admits an optimal solution
\eta_{x}
, and
q(\eta_{x},\xi_{i},v)
exists for each
i=1,2,\ldots,N
; then for any collection
q^{\prime}_{i}\in q(\eta_{x},\xi_{i},v)
,
i=1,2,\ldots,N
, the discrete distribution
\mathds{Q}_{x}(\xi)\coloneqq\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta\big(\xi-\xi_{i}-q_{i}^{\prime}\big)
is a maximizer for the linear worst-case distribution problem (
61
).

Tractable one step FW-update

We observe that in the minimum variance problem (
56
), the only information needed pertaining to a distribution
\mathds{Q}\in\mathcal{W}_{m}(\widehat{\mathds{P}},\rho)
is its first and second order moments
\mu_{\mathds{Q}}=\mathds{E}_{\mathds{Q}}[\xi]
,
\Sigma_{\mathds{Q}}=\mathds{E}_{\mathds{Q}}[\xi\xi^{\top}]
respectively. Thus, in order to solve the DRO problem (
56
) via the FW-algorithm (Algorithm
1
), it is apparent that it suffices to track the evolution of these moments rather than the entire distribution (which gets increasingly difficult with the iterations). To this end, a single FW-update (
42
), in Algorithm
1
, for the minimum-variance problem (
56
) can be summarised in terms of the finite-dimensional quantities:

\begin{cases}\begin{aligned} &\text{Solve for $x$ }&&:\quad x_{k}\in\argmin_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\;x^{\top}(\Sigma_{k}-\mu_{k}\mu_{k}^{\top})x\\
&\text{FW-oracle }&&:\quad\begin{cases}\begin{aligned} \eta_{k}&=\eta_{x_{k}}\quad\text{ and }\quad q_{i}\in q(\eta_{k},\xi_{i},\mu_{k})\quad\text{for all}\quad i=1,2,\ldots,N,\\
\mu^{\prime}_{k}&=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(\xi_{i}+q_{i})\quad\text{ and }\quad\Sigma^{\prime}_{k}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(\xi_{i}+q_{i})(\xi_{i}+q_{i})^{\top},\end{aligned}\end{cases}\\
&\text{FW-update }&&:\quad\mu_{k+1}=\mu_{k}+\gamma_{k}\big(\mu^{\prime}_{k}-\mu_{k}\big)\,,\quad\quad\Sigma_{k+1}=\Sigma_{k}+\gamma_{k}\big(\Sigma^{\prime}_{k}-\Sigma_{k}\big).\end{aligned}\end{cases}

(64)

The tractable formulation (
64
) of the FW algorithm for variance particularly highlights the discussion in Remark
4.5
and (
33
) on the sufficient statistic for variance. In particular, it must be noted that the entire information of the distribution is characterized by
(\mu,\Sigma)
, and therefore, the FW algorithm (Algorithm
1
) can be simplified as (
64
) which is an iteration over only finite-dimensional quantities
(\mu,\Sigma)
and
x
.

Proof of Lemma

Since the set

Proof of Lemma

(i): Danskin’s theorem.
Letting
\mathds{P}_{\gamma}\coloneqq\mathds{P}+\gamma\big(\mathds{Q}-\mathds{P}\big)

\begin{cases}\begin{aligned} \mathcal{X}\times[0,1]\ni(x,\gamma)\longmapsto f(x,\gamma)\ &\coloneqq\ F\big(x,\mathds{P}_{\gamma}\big)\quad\text{and }\\
[0,1]\ni\gamma\longmapsto g(\gamma)\ &\coloneqq\ \min\limits_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\;f(x,\gamma).\end{aligned}\end{cases}

It is easily seen that
g(\gamma)=R\big(\mathds{P}_{\gamma}\big)
. Moreover, for the one sided derivatives of
g
at
\gamma\in[0,1)
defined:
{\rm d}g(\gamma;1)\coloneqq\lim_{\delta\downarrow 0}\;\frac{1}{\delta}\big(g(\gamma+\delta)-g(\gamma)\big)
, we easily verify that
{\rm d}R\big(\mathds{P};\mathds{Q}\big)={\rm d}g(0;1)
. Using the short-hand notation
f_{x}(\cdot)\coloneqq f(x,\cdot)
, we also verify similarly that
{\rm d}f_{x}(0;1)={\rm d}F_{x}(\mathds{P};\mathds{Q})
, for every
x\in\mathcal{X}
. For every
\gamma\in[0,1]
, let
x_{\gamma}\coloneqq\argmin_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\ f(x,\gamma)
, we know from the Danskin’s theorem
[
2
, (A.22), p. 154]
that the one sided derivatives
{\rm d}g(\gamma;1)
at
\gamma
, are given by

{\rm d}g(\gamma;1)={\rm d}f_{x_{\gamma}}(\gamma;1)=\lim\limits_{\delta\downarrow 0}\frac{1}{\delta}\big(f(x_{\gamma},\gamma+\delta)-f(x_{\gamma},\gamma)\big).

Collecting everything, we have
{\rm d}R\big(\mathds{P};\mathds{Q}\big)={\rm d}g(0;1)={\rm d}f_{x_{0}}(0;1)={\rm d}F_{x(\mathds{P})}(\mathds{P};\mathds{Q})
.

Proof of Lemma

(ii): Smoothness.

Recall that
\nabla_{1}F(x,\mathds{P})\coloneqq\big(\nicefrac{{\partial F}}{{\partial x}}\big)(x,\mathds{P})
denotes the partial derivative of
F
w.r.t.
x
evaluated at
(x,\mathds{P})
. Since
x(\mathds{P})=\argmin_{x\in\mathcal{X}}F\big(x,\mathds{P}\big)
, the first-order optimality conditions give

\left\langle\nabla_{1}F\big(x(\mathds{P}),\mathds{P}\big),\ y-x(\mathds{P})\right\rangle\;\geqslant\;0\quad\text{ for all }y\in\mathcal{X}.

(75)

Due to
\alpha
-strong-convexity of

in

\displaystyle\frac{\alpha}{2}\left\lVert x(\mathds{P})-x(\mathds{Q})\right\rVert^{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant\ F\big(x(\mathds{Q}),\mathds{P}\big)-F\big(x(\mathds{P}),\mathds{P}\big)-\left\langle\nabla_{1}F\big(x(\mathds{P}),\mathds{P}\big),\ x(\mathds{Q})-x(\mathds{P})\right\rangle

\displaystyle\leqslant\ F\big(x(\mathds{Q}),\mathds{P}\big)-F\big(x(\mathds{P}),\mathds{P}\big)\quad\text{from \eqref{eq:first-order-optimality-x} with }y=x(\mathds{Q}).

Similarly,
\alpha
-strong-convexity of

gives us

\frac{\alpha}{2}\left\lVert x(\mathds{Q})-x(\mathds{P})\right\rVert^{2}\leqslant F\big(x(\mathds{P}),\mathds{Q}\big)-F\big(x(\mathds{Q}),\mathds{Q}\big).

Combining the two inequalities, we infer that the inequality

\displaystyle\alpha\left\lVert x(\mathds{P})-x(\mathds{Q})\right\rVert^{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant\ F\big(x(\mathds{Q}),\mathds{P}\big)-F\big(x(\mathds{P}),\mathds{P}\big)+F\big(x(\mathds{P}),\mathds{Q}\big)-F\big(x(\mathds{Q}),\mathds{Q}\big)

(76)

\displaystyle=\ F\big(x(\mathds{Q}),\mathds{P}\big)-F\big(x(\mathds{Q}),\mathds{Q}\big)+F\big(x(\mathds{P}),\mathds{Q}\big)-F\big(x(\mathds{P}),\mathds{P}\big)

\displaystyle\leqslant\ {\rm d}F_{x(\mathds{Q})}(\mathds{Q};\mathds{P})+{\rm d}F_{x(\mathds{P})}(\mathds{P};\mathds{Q})

\displaystyle\text{from \eqref{eq:concavity-linear-bound} }

\displaystyle=\ {\rm d}R\big(\mathds{Q};\mathds{P}\big)+{\rm d}R\big(\mathds{P};\mathds{Q}\big)

\displaystyle\text{from \eqref{eq:danskin}},

holds for every

. On the one hand, for
\mathds{P}_{\gamma}=\mathds{P}+\gamma(\mathds{Q}-\mathds{P})

\displaystyle\rm{\displaystyle d}R\big(\mathds{P};\mathds{P}_{\gamma}\big)\;+\;{\rm d}R\big(\mathds{P}_{\gamma};\mathds{P}\big)

\displaystyle=\;\gamma\Big({\rm d}F_{x(\mathds{P})}(\mathds{P};\mathds{Q})\;-\;{\rm d}F_{x(\mathds{P}_{\gamma})}(\mathds{P};\mathds{Q})\Big)\;+\;\Big({\rm d}F_{x(\mathds{P}_{\gamma})}(\mathds{P};\mathds{P}_{\gamma})\;+\;{\rm d}F_{x(\mathds{P}_{\gamma})}(\mathds{P}_{\gamma};\mathds{P})\Big)

\displaystyle\leqslant\;\gamma C_{1}\left\lVert x(\mathds{P}_{\gamma})-x(\mathds{P})\right\rVert\;+\;\gamma^{2}C_{2},

Similarly if

Lemma 8.2
