Abstract
. In this paper we start to investigate a new body of questions in additive combinatorics. The fundamental Cauchy–Davenport theorem gives a lower bound on the size of a sumset A + B A+B for subsets of the cyclic group ℤ p {\mathbb{Z}}_{p} of order p p ( p p prime), and this is just one example of a large family of results. Our aim in this paper is to investigate what happens if we restrict the number of elements of one set that we may use to form the sums. Here is the question we set out to answer: given two subsets, A A and B B , does B B have a subset B ′ B^{\prime} of bounded size such that A + B ′ A+B^{\prime} is large, perhaps even comparable to the size of A + B A+B ? In particular, can we get close to the lower bound of the Cauchy–Davenport theorem? Our main results show that, rather surprisingly, in many circumstances it is possible to obtain not merely an asymptotic version of the usual sumset bound, but even the exact bound itself. For example, in ℤ {\mathbb{Z}} , we show that if A A and B B have size n n then there are three elements b 1 , b 2 , b 3 ∈ B b_{1},b_{2},b_{3}\in B such that | ( A + b 1 ) ∪ ( A + b 2 ) ∪ ( A + b 3 ) | ⩾ 2 ​ n − 1 |(A+b_{1})\cup(A+b_{2})\cup(A+b_{3})|\geqslant 2n-1 . And for ℤ p {\mathbb{Z}}_{p} itself, we show the following: if A A and B B have size n n , where n ⩽ p / 3 n\leqslant p/3 , then there is a subset B ′ B^{\prime} of B B of size c c such that | A + B ′ | ⩾ 2 ​ n − 1 |A+B^{\prime}|\geqslant 2n-1 . Here c c is an absolute constant. In the inverse direction for this result, we show that if for every subset B ′ B^{\prime} of B B of size c c we have | A + B ′ | ⩽ 2 ​ n − 1 + r |A+B^{\prime}|\leqslant 2n-1+r , where r ⩽ ε ​ n r\leqslant\varepsilon n for some absolute constant ε \varepsilon , then B B is contained in an arithmetic progression of size n + r n+r . We also prove ‘unbalanced’ forms of our results, when the sizes of A A and B B may differ. As an application, we prove some considerable extensions of the Erdős-Heilbronn problem. We also present versions in the continuous setting, and give several open problems.



1.
Introduction

The aim of this paper is to introduce a new direction in the study of sumset sizes, by asking what
happens when we may only use a bounded number of terms from one of the sets. Our main interest is in
sumsets in
{\mathbb{Z}}_{p}
, where, as usual,
p
is a prime number; in fact, throughout
this paper,
p
will always stand for an arbitrary prime. We start with some
background.

Cauchy
[
11
]
was the first to study sums of subsets in
{\mathbb{Z}}_{p}
;
over one hundred years later, his result was rediscovered by
Davenport
[
12
,
13
]
. Their result asserts that if
\emptyset\neq A,B\subset{\mathbb{Z}}_{p}
and

|A|+B|\leqslant p+1
then

|A+B|\geqslant|A|+|B|-1.

(1)

Proving this for sets of integers, without any restrictions on their sizes, is entirely trivial,
and may be viewed as an analogue of the one-dimensional case of the Brunn–Minkowski inequality.

The Cauchy–Davenport theorem was followed by important
contributions concerning sums of subsets of groups, including
{\mathbb{Z}}
itself, by
Mann
[
42
,
43
]
, Kneser
[
35
]
, Vosper
[
60
,

]
, Erdős and
Heilbronn
[

]
, Freiman
[
17
,

,

]
, Plünnecke
[

,

[

]
, and others until the 1990s,
when the subject really took off (see e.g.
[
2
,

,

,

,
48
,

,
54
,
57
,
58
]
). For various discrete analogues of the Brunn–Minkowski inequality, see

[

,

,

]
and many other results;
for surveys of the inequality itself, see
[

.

One should distinguish between ‘direct’ results, giving lower bounds for subset sums, and ‘inverse’
results, that characterise the cases when the subset sum is close to its minimum (where ‘close’ may be interpreted in
several different ways). Starting with Vosper
[
60
]
and Freiman
[

,

,

,

, much research has been done on inverse results: see Nathanson

Considerable attention has also been given to ‘restricted’ sumsets, meaning sumsets in which
we do not consider the sum of every pair. The best–known example of this is
the Erdős–Heilbronn conjecture
[

, proved by

In this paper we wish to find out what happens if in (
1
) or its analogues
we are allowed to use only boundedly many elements of
B
. This question is of
interest not only in
{\mathbb{Z}}_{p}
, but also in additive groups and even in
{\mathbb{Z}}

In the rest of this section we describe our main results.

1.1.
Direct results in
\mathbb{Z}

We start in the simplest place,
{\mathbb{Z}}
itself. If we are summing just one set
A
with
itself, then of course with
a_{1}=\min A
and
a_{2}=\max A
we have that the sets
A+a_{1}
and

A+a_{2}
meet only at
a_{1}+a_{2}
, so that
|A+\{a_{1},a_{2}\}|=2|A|-1
. But with
a sumset
A+B
of two sets then it is no longer the case that for some two elements
b_{1},b_{2}
of

B
we have that
|A+\{b_{1},b_{2}\}|=|A|+|B|-1
, even when the sets are the same size.
For example, taking
n
to be a multiple of
3
, if
A
is the set
[1,2n/3]\cup[n,4n/3]
then it is easy to see that
any translate of
A
by a distance
d
, where
0\leqslant d\leqslant n
, meets
A
in at least
n/3

elements. Hence if
B
is the interval
[0,n]
then
A
and
B
have size
n+1
and for any
b_{1},b_{2}\in B
we have that

|A+\{b_{1},b_{2}\}|\leqslant|A|+|B|-n/3
.

In light of this simple example, it is very surprising that, if we move up to
three

elements of
B
, then in fact we can recover the exact lower bound in (
1
). Of course, we
will be taking
|A|\geqslant|B|
, since if
B
is much larger than
A
then the lower bound in
(
1
) is greater than
3|A|
, which is the most one could ever obtain by taking the union of
three translates of
A
. This is our first result.

Theorem 1
.

Let
A
and
B
be finite non-empty subsets of
\mathbb{Z}
with
|A|\geqslant|B|
. Then there exist elements

b_{1},b_{2},b_{3}\in B
such that

|A+\{b_{1},b_{2},b_{3}\}|\geqslant|A|+|B|-1.

Actually, if
B
is allowed to be
larger
than
A
, but only by a given ratio, then
a bounded number of elements of
B
does still suffice to obtain the bounds in (
1
).

Theorem 2
.

For every
\alpha>0
there exists
c
such that, whenever
A
and
B
are finite non-empty subsets of

\mathbb{Z}
with
|A|\geqslant\alpha|B|
, there exist elements
b_{1},b_{2},\ldots,b_{c}\in B
such that

\large|A+\{b_{1},\ldots,b_{c}\}\large|\geqslant|A|+|B|-1.

1.2.
Direct results in
\mathbb{Z}_{p}

Let us now turn to the deeper question of what happens in the cyclic group
{\mathbb{Z}}_{p}
of prime order. Can we again
recover the exact bounds, this time in the Cauchy–Davenport theorem, if we insist that only
a given number of terms of
B
may be used? The same examples as in
\mathbb{Z}
show that two
translates do not suffice. However, a bounded number of translates is enough. This is again very
surprising, and is the one of the main results of our paper. In a certain sense, it is going far
beyond results like the Erdős–Heilbronn conjecture, where we restrict the allowed sums only by
forbidding
a
to equal
b
in a sum
a+b
we use: here on the contrary we only
allow
a fixed number
of members of
B
to appear in the sums.

Theorem 3
.

There exists a universal constant
c
such that the following holds. Whenever

A
and
B
are subsets of
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
with
|A|=|B|\leqslant p/3
,
there exist
b_{1},\ldots,b_{c}\in B
such that

|A+\{b_{1},\ldots,b_{c}\}|\geqslant|A|+|B|-1.

It would be fascinating to know if the number
c
of summands can be taken to be 3, if the sizes of
A
and
B
are a
sufficiently small multiple of
p
.

More generally, we may allow the sizes of
A
and
B
to approach
p/2
, and also their sizes need
not be the same. Note that we
have
to let the number of allowed summands in
B
increase as
the sum of the sizes of
A
and

B
gets closer to
p
: this is easily seen if we choose
A
to be a random subset.
Indeed, if
A
is a random subset of
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
of size
(1/2-\beta)p
and
c
is fixed then
with high probability we have that for
any

b_{1},\ldots,b_{c}
the union

\cup_{1\leqslant i\leqslant c}(A+b_{i})
has size about
(1-(1/2+\beta)^{c}))p
, and this is smaller than

(1-3\beta)p
if
\beta
is small. In other words, for such an
A
, and for any
B
at all of size

(1/2-\beta)p
, we have that there do not exist
c
points of
B
whose sumset with
A
has size
even close to
|A|+|B|
.

Theorem 4
.

For all
\alpha,\beta>0
there exists a constant
c
such that the
following holds. Whenever
A
and
B
are subsets of
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
with

\alpha|B|\leqslant|A|\leqslant\frac{1}{\alpha}|B|
and
|A|+|B|\leqslant(1-\beta)p
, there
exist
b_{1},\ldots,b_{c}\in B
such that

|A+\{b_{1},\ldots,b_{c}\}|\geqslant|A|+|B|-1.

Interestingly, if
A
is larger than a fixed (sufficiently large) multiple
of the size of
B
then we do have that

three
translates are enough.

Theorem 5
.

For every
\beta>0
there exists
\alpha>0
such that the following holds.
Whenever
A
and
B
are non-empty subsets of

\mathbb{Z}_{p}
with
|B|\leqslant\alpha|A|
and
|A|+|B|\leqslant(1-\beta)p
,
there exist
elements
b_{1},b_{2},b_{3}\in B
such that

|A+\{b_{1},b_{2},b_{3}\}|\geqslant|A|+|B|-1.

(We mention in passing that this result actually means that, in Theorem
4
, the condition
that
|A|\leqslant\frac{1}{\alpha}|B|
may be removed.)

An important case of this theorem is when
B
is an interval, i.e. a set of the form
[x,y]=\{x,x+1,\dots,y\}\subset{\mathbb{Z}}_{p}
.
One is tempted to imagine that in this situation everything is much easier, but this is not the case,
and indeed a substantial part of our approach to the
various results in the paper consists of first proving the result when
B
is close to an interval
(which is usually a large part of the overall work) and then seeing how, if at all, the proof
can be modified for general
B
. When
B
is an interval we also recover the minimal possible number of
translates of
A
.

Theorem 6
.

Let
A
and
B
be non-empty subsets of
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
with

|B|\leqslant|A|\leqslant 2^{-20}p
and
B
an interval.
Then there exist elements
b_{1},b_{2},b_{3}\in B

such that

|A+\{b_{1},b_{2},b_{3}\}|\geqslant|A|+|B|-1.

1.3.
Inverse results in
\mathbb{Z}_{p}

We also prove inverse theorems for our results in
{\mathbb{Z}}_{p}
– where by ‘inverse’ we as usual
mean statements of the form ‘what happens if the inequality is close to being tight’. (These are also often
known as ‘stability’ results.) These show that, unless the
lower bounds hold ‘with room to spare’, our sets must be highly structured, and in fact be very close to
arithmetic progressions.

One of our main results is the following, which may be viewed
as an extension of Freiman’s
3k-4
theorem in this group where we demand that only a bounded number of
summands from
B
are used.

Theorem 7
.

For all
\alpha,\beta>0
there exist constants
c
and
\varepsilon>0
such that the following holds.
Let
A
and
B
be subsets of
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
of size at least
2
,
with
\alpha|B|\leqslant|A|\leqslant\alpha^{-1}|B|
and

|A|+|B|\leqslant(1-\beta)p
. Suppose that for any
c
elements

b_{1},\ldots,b_{c}\in B
we have

|A+\{b_{1},\ldots,b_{c}\}|\leqslant|A|+|B|-1+r

where
r\leqslant\varepsilon|B|
. Then
B
is contained in an arithmetic progression of size

|B|+r
.

This is actually the key to proving several of our
direct
results in
{\mathbb{Z}}_{p}
. In fact, our proof shows that
\varepsilon
depends only on
\beta
: it is independent of
\alpha
.

We remark
that, in the form stated, if
A\neq B
then this is not really a ‘true’ inverse result to our results
above, as it does not describe
the structure of
A
. In fact, we cannot hope to insist that
A
is contained in a short arithmetic progression, since
we may always add to
A
a small ‘sprinkling’ of faraway points without affecting the relevant properties of the
sumsets with a bounded number of points from
B
. But one
can
obtain that
A
has small symmetric difference
with some short arithmetic progression – we will say a few words about this when we come to prove
Theorem
7
.

1.4.
Abelian groups

There is an important tool in many of our results, that will
often allow us to make progress with ‘arbitrary’ sets. This may be of independent
interest.

Theorem 8
.

For all
K
and
\varepsilon>0
there is an integer
c
such that the following holds.
Let
A
and
B
be finite subsets of an abelian group. Then there are subsets
A^{*}\subset A

and
B^{*}\subset B
, with
|A^{*}|\geqslant(1-\varepsilon)|A|
and

|B^{*}|\geqslant(1-\varepsilon)|B|
, such that if we select points
b_{1},\ldots,b_{c}
uniformly at random from

B
then

{\mathbb{E}}_{b_{1},\dots,b_{c}\in B}\ |A+\{b_{1},\dots,b_{c}\}|\geqslant\min\big((1-\varepsilon)|A^{*}+B^{*}|,\ K|A|,\ K|B|\big).

One could view Theorem
8
as an approximate form of the sharp results we wish to prove in

{\mathbb{Z}}
and
{\mathbb{Z}}_{p}
.
We remark that simple examples show that
the dependence of
c
in terms of
\varepsilon
and
K
is necessary.

Amusingly, one can use a variant of Theorem
8
(namely
Theorem
8^{\prime}
below)
to deduce Roth’s theorem
[
50
]

on three-term arithmetic
progressions. This is not surprising, since (as we shall explain later) our result builds
on work of Shao
[
56
]
, which itself builds on the arithmetic regularity lemma of
Green
[
26
]
– and the
arithmetic regularity lemma has Roth’s theorem as an immediate consequence. But it is interesting that
the deduction from Theorem
8^{\prime}
is direct.

1.5.
Restricted Sums

Although our aim in this paper is not so much to give applications, we do expect that our results will
prove to be useful tools. As an example, as an application of our methods, we turn our attention to the Erdős–Heilbronn problem.
Our results turn out to yield, very easily, some considerable extensions of this. For example, we consider
‘restricted’ sums, where for each
a\in A
there are some
b\in B
that we are
not allowed to use when forming the ‘restricted sumset’ of
A
with
B
.

Theorem 9
.

For each
\beta>0
and integer
d
there is an
n_{0}
such that following holds. Let
A
be a subset of
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
with

n_{0}\leqslant|A|=n<(1-\beta)p/2
, and suppose that we form all sums
a+b
, where
a,b\in A
, except that for
each
a\in A
there is a set of
d
values in
A
that we are not allowed to take as
b
. Then the resulting ‘restricted sumset’ has
size at least
2n-1-2d
.

Note that the Erdős–Heilbronn problem corresponds to the case when
d=1
and for each
a\in A
the one
element of
A
that we cannot sum with
a
is
a
itself.

In fact, we prove an extension of this where the number of summands from
A
that we take is bounded, as in the spirit of the rest of this paper.
Indeed, this actually
helps
us: because we are already in the situation where the second summands are
from a bounded set, the additional constraint that certain pairs are not allowed turns out to be much easier to
handle than it would be in general. So it turns out that our results here will follow directly from
Theorem
7
.

1.6.
Sums in the Continuous Setting

Our results tend to have consequences in the continuous setting, about sums of compact sets in the reals or
in the circle
\mathbb{T}=\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}
.
We mention here two typical examples. In the statements we shall use
|\cdot|
to denote Lebesgue measure on the Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}
or Haar measure (normalized Lebesgue measure) on the circle
\mathbb{T}=\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}
. As we will
see, the deductions from the corresponding discrete results (namely Theorem
1
for the first and Theorems
4

and
5
for the second)
will be fairly straightforward.

Corollary 10
.

Let
A
and
B
be non-empty compact subsets of
\mathbb{R}
with
|A|\geqslant|B|
. Then there are elements

b_{1},b_{2},b_{3}\in B
such that

|A+\{b_{1},b_{2},b_{3}\}|\geqslant|A|+|B|.

Corollary 11
.

For all
\alpha,\beta>0
there exists a constant
c
such that the
following holds. Whenever
A
and
B
are non-empty compact subsets of
\mathbb{T}
with

\alpha|B|\leqslant|A|
and
|A|+|B|\leqslant 1-\beta
, there
are
b_{1},\ldots,b_{c}\in B
such that

|A+\{b_{1},\ldots,b_{c}\}|\geqslant|A|+|B|.

It is tempting to believe that
these corollaries are equivalent to the corresponding results in the discrete case, but this does not
appear to be the case: it does not seem easy to prove the reverse implications, essentially because the
points we sample (the
b_{i}
) in the continuous case may not ‘line up’ with the way we are embedding our
discrete sets into the continuous world.

Structure

1.8.
Notation

Our notation is standard. To make our paper more readable, we often omit integer-part signs when these do not affect the
argument. For example, given

x>0
, we use the notation
[x]
for
\{1,2,\ldots,\lfloor x\rfloor\}
.

Sometimes we write ‘
x\mod d
’ as shorthand for the infinite arithmetic progression

\{y\in\mathbb{Z}:y\equiv x\mod d\}
, and refer to it as a
fibre
mod
d
.
When
S
is a subset of
\mathbb{Z}
we often write
S^{x}
for the intersection of this fibre with

S
– when the value of
d
is clear. (We sometimes write
S^{x}
as
S^{x}_{d}
when we want to stress the value of
d
.)
Thus
S^{x}=S\cap\pi^{-1}(x)
, where
\pi=\pi_{d}
denotes
the natural projection from
\mathbb{Z}
to
\mathbb{Z}_{d}
. We also write
\widetilde{S}

for
\pi_{d}(S)
.

When we write a probability or an expectation over a finite set, we always assume that the elements
of the set are being sampled uniformly. Thus, for example,
for a finite set
X\subset\mathbb{Z}
we denote the expectation and probability when we sample uniformly over
all
x\in X
by respectively
\E_{x\in X}\text{ and }\Prob_{x\in X}.
Similarly, an expectation when
we sample uniformly over a product space
X_{1}\times\cdots\times X_{n}
may be written as

\E_{\begin{subarray}{c}x_{j}\in X_{j}\\
j\in\{1,\ldots,n\}\end{subarray}}
.

We also often sample uniformly over all
c
-sets of a given set
X
. In most of those cases,
we could instead sample
c
elements uniformly and independently, but the notation would tend to get
unwieldy, and this is why we use the sampling over all
c
-sets instead.

For a set
X
and integer
c\in\mathbb{N}
we as usual denote by
X^{(c)}
the family of
all subsets of
X
of size
c
, and by
X^{(\leqslant c)}
the family of
all subsets of size at most
c
. However, in several places it is very useful, when we want to
specify that a set is small, to also allow
X
to have size smaller than
c
, and in that case
we make the convention that
X^{(c)}
denotes the singleton
\{X\}
. We hope that the reader will
not mind this convention, which will allow arguments to flow without having to deal
with many (unimportant) special cases.

For more general background on sumsets, or for background on any of the results
mentioned in the next section, see the survey of Breuillard, Green and Tao
[
10
]
or the
books of Nathanson
[
46
]
or Tao and Vu
[
59
]
.

To end this Introduction, we mention two further questions of a seemingly similar flavour,
although really there is little connection with our topic.
First, given sets
A
and
B
in an abelian group, is there a large set
of disjoint translates
A+b
with
b\in B
? The answer is given by a simple
yet fundamental tool in additive combinatorics, Ruzsa’s covering lemma
[
53
]

(see also Lemma 2.14 in Tao and Vu
[
59
]
), which states that there is a set
X\subset B

such that
X+A-A
contains
B
and
|X+A|=|A||X|
, i.e. the translates of
A
through
elements of
X
are disjoint and the translates of
A-A
through
X
cover
B
. In
particular, this shows that there are at least
|B|/|A-A|
disjoint
translates of
A+b
with
b\in B
.

Second, one might also wonder about the following related question. Given sets
A

and
B
in an Abelian group, are there
small
subsets
A^{\prime}\subset A
and

B^{\prime}\subset B
such that
A+B=(A+B^{\prime})\cup(B+A^{\prime})
? A result of Ellenberg
[
15
]
answers
this question in
{\mathbb{Z}}_{p}^{n}
, showing that we can always take
|A^{\prime}|,|B^{\prime}|\leqslant c_{q}^{n}

where

Theorem 12

|A_{0}|\geqslant(1-\varepsilon)|A|\text{ and }|B_{0}|\geqslant(1-\varepsilon)|B|\text{ and }|A_{0}+B_{0}|\leqslant|A+_{\Gamma}B|+\varepsilon N.

We will need the extension of Freiman’s

3.
Sums in
{\mathbb{Z}}

We start by proving Theorem
1
that
if
A
and
B
are finite non-empty sets of
integers with
|A|\geqslant|B|
, then there exist elements

b_{1},b_{2},b_{3}\in B
such that
|A+\{b_{1},b_{2},b_{3}\}|\geqslant|A|+|B|-1.

One might imagine that a random argument cannot
be helpful in proving this, since in the case when
A
and
B
are intervals then two of our elements
must
be the
first and last elements of
B
. But, remarkably, what we will find is that if we fix those two elements and select only the third element at random, we do obtain exactly the bound we want.

Proof of Theorem
1
.

Let
B
have first element
0
and last element
m
, and write
\pi_{m}:{\mathbb{Z}}\to{\mathbb{Z}}_{m}={\mathbb{Z}}/m{\mathbb{Z}}
for the canonical projection of
{\mathbb{Z}}
onto
{\mathbb{Z}}_{m}
. Then
A+\{0,m\}=A\cup(A+m)
satisfies

\pi_{m}(A)=\pi_{m}\big(A\cup(A+m)\big)=\widetilde{A}
. Although it is easy to bound the size of this set
A\cup(A+m)
, we record it as a lemma because we shall make use of it several times later on.

Lemma 16
.

We have

\bigg|A\cup(A+m)\bigg|\geqslant|A|+|\widetilde{A}|.

Proof.

Note that
(A+m)^{x}=A^{x}+m
for every
x\in{\mathbb{Z}}
. Hence, if
x\in\widetilde{A}=\pi_{m}(A)
then

(A+m)^{x}=A^{x}+m\neq A^{x}
,
so

\bigg|\big[A\cup(A+m)\big]^{x}\bigg|=\bigg|A^{x}\cup(A+m)^{x}\bigg|\geqslant|A^{x}|+1.

Consequently,

\bigg|A\cup(A+m)\bigg|\geqslant\sum_{x\in\widetilde{A}}\bigg|[A\cup(A+m)]^{x}\bigg|\geqslant\sum_{x\in\widetilde{A}}|A^{x}|+1=|A|+|\widetilde{A}|,

completing the proof.
∎

We now turn to the contribution from the ‘random’ term. It does seem very fortunate that this meshes so well with the bound above. Again, we record it as a separate lemma for future use.

Lemma 17
.

If we choose an element
b
from
B\setminus\{m\}
, uniformly at random, then

\E_{b\in B\setminus\{m\}}\bigg|(A+b)\setminus\pi_{m}^{-1}(\widetilde{A})\bigg|\geqslant|A|\max\bigg(0,\frac{|\widetilde{B}|-|\widetilde{A}|}{|\widetilde{B}|}\bigg).

Proof.

Clearly,
\pi_{m}:B\setminus\{m\}\to\widetilde{B}
is a bijection. Also, for
a\in A
we have

\Prob_{b\in B\setminus\{m\}}\bigg(a+b\not\in\pi^{-1}_{m}(\widetilde{A})\bigg)=\Prob_{\widetilde{b}\in\widetilde{B}}\bigg(\widetilde{a}+\widetilde{b}\not\in\widetilde{A}\bigg)\geqslant\max\bigg(0,\frac{|\widetilde{B}|-|\widetilde{A}|}{|\widetilde{B}|}\bigg).

so

\E_{b\in B\setminus\{m\}}\bigg|(A+b)\setminus\pi^{-1}_{m}(\widetilde{A})\bigg|=\sum_{a\in A}\Prob_{b\in B\setminus\{m\}}\bigg(a+b\not\in\pi_{m}^{-1}(\widetilde{A})\bigg)\geqslant|A|\max\bigg(0,\frac{|\widetilde{B}|-|\widetilde{A}|}{|\widetilde{B}|}\bigg),

as claimed.
∎

To prove Theorem
1
, we combine these two lemmas. We have

\displaystyle\E_{b\in B\setminus\{m\}}\bigg|A+\{0,b,m\}\bigg|

\displaystyle\geqslant

\displaystyle|A|+|\widetilde{A}|+|A|\max\bigg(0,\frac{|\widetilde{B}|-|\widetilde{A}|}{|\widetilde{B}|}\bigg)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle|A|+|\pi_{m}(A)|+|A|\max\bigg(0,\frac{|B|-1-|\pi_{m}(A)|}{|B|-1}\bigg).

If
|\pi_{m}(A)|\geqslant|B|-1
then

\E_{b\in B\setminus\{m\}}\bigg|A+\{0,b,m\}\bigg|\geqslant|A|+|\pi_{m}(A)|\geqslant|A|+|B|-1.

On the other hand, if
|\pi_{m}(A)|\leqslant|B|-1
then

\displaystyle\E_{b\in B\setminus\{m\}}\bigg|A+\{0,b,m\}\bigg|\hskip-17.0pt

\displaystyle\geqslant|A|+|\pi_{m}(A)|+|A|\frac{|B|-1-|\pi_{m}(A)|}{|B|-1}

\displaystyle\geqslant|A|+|\pi_{m}(A)|+|B|-1-|\pi_{m}(A)|=|A|+|B|-1,

completing our proof of Theorem
1
.
\square

In fact, the proof of Theorem
1
above shows the following stronger result, which we record here. Let
A
and
B
be finite non-empty subsets of integers with
|A|\geqslant|B|
and
B
having smallest element
0
and greatest element
m
. Then we have

\max_{b_{1},b_{2},b_{3}\in B}\bigg|A+\{b_{1},b_{2},b_{3}\}\bigg|\geqslant\E_{b\in B\setminus\{m\}}\bigg|A+\{0,b,m\}\bigg|\geqslant|A|+|B|-1.

(2)

We now collect together some variants of the results above that we shall need at later points in the paper. We urge the reader to skip these (rather pedestrian) variations, returning to them only when they are actually needed in the sequel. To avoid clutter and some repetition, we leave the proofs for the Appendix.

First, we investigate when equality is attained in inequality (
2
), that is when
|A+\{b_{1},b_{2},b_{3}\}|\leqslant|A|+|B|-1
for all
b_{1},b_{2},b_{3}\in B
.

Theorem 18
.

Let
A
and
B
be finite non-empty subsets of
\mathbb{Z}
such that
|A|=|B|
, with
\min B=0
and

\max B=m
. Suppose that when we choose an element
b
of

uniformly at random we have

\E_{b\in B\setminus\{0,m\}}\bigg|A+\{0,b,m\}\bigg|\leqslant|A|+|B|-1.

(3)

Then
A
and

are arithmetic progressions with the same common difference.

In particular, suppose that when we choose any three elements

of

\bigg|A+\{b_{1},b_{2},b_{3}\}\bigg|\leqslant|A|+|B|-1.

Then
A
and
B
are arithmetic progressions with the same common difference.

Next we have a version of Theorem

.

Theorem 19
.

Let
A
and

be finite non-empty subsets of
\mathbb{Z}
, with

and

. Then

\E_{b\in B\setminus\{m\}}\bigg|A+\{0,b,m\}\bigg|\geqslant 2|A|+|\pi_{m}(A)|\frac{|B|-1-|A|}{|B|-1}.

.

Lemma 20
.

Let
A

B

A_{1}

\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \E_{b\in B\setminus\{m\}}\bigg|(A_{1}+b)\setminus\pi_{m}^{-1}(\widetilde{A})\bigg|\geqslant|A_{1}|\max\bigg(0,\frac{|\widetilde{B}|-|\widetilde{A}|}{|\widetilde{B}|}\bigg).\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \square

Lemma 21
.

Let

and

\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \E_{b_{2},b_{3}\in B\setminus\{m\}}\bigg|A+\{0,b_{2},b_{3}\}\bigg|\geqslant 2.5|A|.\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \square

To end this section, we return to
two
translates. As pointed out above, even if
|A|=|B|
, there may
not be two points in
B
whose sum with
A
gives at least
|A|+|B|-1
points. When
would
two
translates suffice?
In the first version of this paper we proved the following result.

Theorem 23
.

There is a constant
K>0
such that for any subsets
A
and
B
of
\mathbb{Z}
with
|B|=n\geqslant 2
and

|A|\geqslant Kn\log n
there exist elements
b_{1},b_{2}
in
B

such that

|A+\{b_{1},b_{2}\}|\geqslant|A|+|B|-1.

One of the referees was kind enough to point out that this result is an immediate
consequence of a theorem of Konyagin and Lev
[
36
]
about the Erdős–Heilbronn–Olson
function
f_{A}(s)=|(A+s)\setminus A|
. Writing
f_{A}(S)
for
\max\{f_{A}(s):s\in S\}
,
Konyagin and Lev proved that
there is an absolute constant
c>0
such that
f_{A}(S)\geqslant|S|

holds for all finite sets
A\subset{\mathbb{Z}}
,
S\subset{\mathbb{N}}
with
|A|>1
and
|S|<c|A|/\log|A|
.

Now, trivially,
|A+\{b_{1},b_{2}\}|=|A|+f_{A}(b_{2}-b_{1})
, so

\max_{b_{1},b_{2}\in B}|A+\{b_{1},b_{2}\}|=|A|+f_{A}(S),

where
S=\{b_{2}-b_{1}:b_{1},b_{2}\in B,b_{1}<b_{2}\}
. Since
|S|\geqslant n-1
, Theorem
23

follows.

In fact, Lev
[
40
]
and Huicochea
[
32
]
proved analogues of the Konyagin–Lev theorem
for
{\mathbb{Z}}_{p}
, which imply the analogue of Theorem
23
in
{\mathbb{Z}}_{p}
.
We are grateful to the referee for drawing our attention to these results of Konyagin, Lev and
Huicochea.

4.
Proof of Theorem
8

Our aim in this section is to prove Theorem
8
. The main
ingredient of the proof is Theorem
12
, and our strategy is to
apply it several times in succession. In fact, we shall prove Theorem
8

in the following slightly stronger form; the easy deduction of Theorem
8

is by changing the values of
\varepsilon
and
c
slightly, as required.

Theorem
8^{\prime}
.

For all
K
and
\varepsilon>0
there is an integer
c
such that the following holds.
Let
A
and
B
be finite subsets of an abelian group. Then there are subsets
A^{*}\subset A

and
B^{*}\subset B
, with
|A^{*}|\geqslant(1-\varepsilon)|A|
and

|B^{*}|\geqslant(1-\varepsilon)|B|
, such that if we select points
b_{1},\ldots,b_{c}
uniformly at random from

B^{*}
then

\hskip 51.0pt\E_{b_{1},\ldots,b_{c}\in B^{*}}|A^{*}+\{b_{1},\ldots,b_{c}\}|\geqslant\min\big((1-\varepsilon)|A^{*}+B^{*}|,\ K|A^{*}|,\ K|B^{*}|\big).\hskip 60.0pt

Note that Theorem
8^{\prime}
asserts the existence of a pair of large subsets
A^{*}
and
B^{*}
with a property that is
‘intrinsic’ to them.

Proof of Theorem
8^{\prime}
.

Fix
\varepsilon>0
and
K>0
, where we assume that
\varepsilon
is sufficiently small and

K
is sufficiently large.
Pick
s=\lfloor\frac{50K}{\varepsilon}\rfloor
. Let

\delta
be given by Theorem
12
with parameters
\frac{\varepsilon}{s},10K
.
Also pick
\alpha
sufficiently small that

(1-t)^{\frac{10K}{\delta}}\leqslant 1-\frac{10K}{2\delta}t
for
t\leqslant\alpha
. Finally pick
c\geqslant\frac{10K}{\delta}
sufficiently large that

(1-(1-\alpha)^{n})\geqslant\frac{1-\varepsilon}{1-\varepsilon/2}\geqslant\frac{1}{2}
. Let
n_{A}
and
n_{B}
be the sizes of
A
and
B
respectively.

We shall examine a process in which we repeatedly apply Theorem
12
in order to construct a decreasing sequence of
s+1
pairs of sets
(A,B)=(A_{0},B_{0}),(A_{1},B_{1}),\ldots,(A_{s},B_{s}),

satisfying
A_{i}\subset A_{i-1}\text{ and }B_{i}\subset B_{i-1}
and

|A_{i}|\geqslant(1-\varepsilon/s)^{i}n_{A}
and
|B_{i}|\geqslant(1-\varepsilon/s)^{i}n_{B}
.
Fix
i<s
and assume that the pair of sets
(A_{i},B_{i})
has already been
constructed. We shall either stop the process at step
i
or construct the pair of
sets
(A_{i+1},B_{i+1})
.

Let
A_{i}+B_{i}=C_{i}^{+}\sqcup C_{i}^{-}
be the partition into ‘popular’ and ‘unpopular’ elements given by
C_{i}^{+}=\{c\in A_{i}+B_{i}:\ |(c-A_{i})\cap B_{i}|\geqslant\alpha|B_{i}|\}
, and

C_{i}^{-}=\{c\in A_{i}+B_{i}:\ |(c-A_{i})\cap B_{i}|<\alpha|B_{i}|\}
.
Also, let the partition
A_{i}\times B_{i}=\Gamma_{i}\sqcup\Gamma_{i}^{c}
be given by
\Gamma_{i}=\{(a,b)\in A_{i}\times B_{i}:\ a+b\in C_{i}^{+}\}\subset A_{i}\times B_{i}
, and
\Gamma_{i}^{c}=\{(a,b)\in A_{i}\times B_{i}:\ a+b\in C_{i}^{-}\}\subset A_{i}\times B_{i}
,
so that
A_{i}+_{\Gamma_{i}}B_{i}=C_{i}^{+}
and
A_{i}+_{\Gamma^{c}_{i}}B_{i}=C_{i}^{-}
.
Finally, for
each
x\in A_{i}+B_{i}
set

r_{i}(x)=|(x-A_{i})\cap B_{i}|=|\{(a,b)\in A_{i}\times B_{i}:\ x=a+b\}|,

so that
\sum_{x}r_{i}(x)=|A_{i}||B_{i}|.

We stop this process ‘early’, at step
i
, if

|\Gamma_{i}|<(1-\delta)|A_{i}||B_{i}|\text{ or }|A_{i}+_{\Gamma_{i}}B_{i}|>10K\min(|A_{i}|,|B_{i}|).

Otherwise, we apply Theorem
12
with parameters

\varepsilon/s,10K
to the pair of sets
(A_{i},B_{i})
. Thus, we produce a pair of sets
(A_{i+1},B_{i+1})
, satisfying

A_{i+1}\subset A_{i}
,
B_{i+1}\subset B_{i}
,
|A_{i+1}|\geqslant(1-\varepsilon/s)|A_{i}|
,
|B_{i+1}|\geqslant(1-\varepsilon/s)|B_{i}|

and
|A_{i+1}+B_{i+1}|\leqslant|A_{i}+_{\Gamma_{i}}B_{i}|+\frac{\varepsilon}{s}\min(|A_{i}|,|B_{i}|)
.
We shall analyse separately the cases in which the process continues until the end and in
which the process stops before that.

Claim A.

Suppose the process
stops early, say at step
j<s
. Then the pair of sets
(A_{j},B_{j})
has the desired properties.

Proof.

Case 1:
Consider first the
case when
|C^{+}_{j}|=|A_{j}+_{\Gamma_{j}}B_{j}|>10K\min(|A_{j}|,|B_{j}|).
For
x\in C_{j}^{+}
,
by construction we have that
r_{j}(x)=|(x-A_{j})\cap B_{j}|\geqslant\alpha|B_{j}|.
If we choose elements
b_{i}
uniformly at random
from
B_{j}
, for
1\leqslant i\leqslant c
, then

\displaystyle\E_{b_{1},\ldots,b_{c}\in B}|A_{j}+\{b_{1},\ldots,b_{c}\})|

\displaystyle\geqslant

\displaystyle\sum_{x}\Prob_{b_{1},\ldots,b_{c}\in B}(x\in A_{j}+\{b_{1},\ldots,b_{c}\})\geqslant\sum_{x\in C^{+}_{j}}1-[1-\Prob_{b\in B}(x\in A_{j}+b)]^{c}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{x\in C^{+}_{j}}1-[1-r_{j}(x)/|B_{j}|]^{c}\geqslant\sum_{x\in C^{+}_{j}}1-(1-\alpha)^{c}

\displaystyle\geqslant

\displaystyle(1-(1-\alpha)^{c})|C^{+}_{j}|\geqslant(1-(1-\alpha)^{c})10K\min(|A_{j}|,|B_{j}|)

\displaystyle\geqslant

\displaystyle 5K\min(|A_{j}|,|B_{j}|)\geqslant K\min(|A|,|B|).

Case 2:
Consider now the case when
|\Gamma_{j}|<(1-\delta)|A_{j}||B_{j}|.
By construction,

\sum_{x\in C_{j}^{-}}r_{j}(x)\geqslant\delta|A_{j}||B_{j}|.
Moreover, for

x\in C_{j}^{-}
we have
r_{j}(x)=|(x-A_{j})\cap B_{j}|\leqslant\alpha|B_{j}|.

In particular, we deduce that

1-[1-r_{j}(x)/|B_{j}|]^{c}\geqslant 1-[1-r_{j}(x)/|B_{j}|]^{\frac{10K}{\delta}}\geqslant\frac{5K}{\delta}r_{j}(x)/|B_{j}|.

Again, if we choose elements
b_{i}
uniformly at
random from
B_{j}
, for
1\leqslant i\leqslant c
, then

\displaystyle\E_{b_{1},\ldots,b_{c}\in B}|A_{j}+\{b_{1},\ldots,b_{c}\}|

\displaystyle\geqslant\sum_{x}\Prob_{b_{1},\ldots,b_{c}\in B}(x\in A_{j}+\{b_{1},\ldots,b_{c}\})=\sum_{x\in C^{-}_{j}}1-[1-\Prob_{b\in B}(x\in A_{j}+b)]^{c}

\displaystyle=\sum_{x\in C^{-}_{j}}1-[1-r_{j}(x)/|B_{j}|]^{c}\geqslant\sum_{x\in C^{-}_{j}}\frac{5K}{\delta}r_{j}(x)/|B_{j}|

\displaystyle\geqslant\frac{5K}{\delta}\delta|A_{j}||B_{j}|/|B_{j}|\geqslant K|A|.

We conclude the pair of sets
(|A_{j}|,|B_{j}|)
has the desired properties, so Claim A is proved.
∎

We now turn to the case when the process does not stop early.

Claim B.

Suppose that the process continues until the terminal step
s
.
Then there is an index
j\leqslant s
such that the pair of sets
(A_{j},B_{j})
has the desired
properties.

Proof.

Note that
|A_{1}+B_{1}|\leqslant|A_{0}+_{\Gamma_{0}}B_{0}|+\frac{\varepsilon}{s}\min(|A_{0}|,|B_{0}|)\leqslant 11K\min(|A_{0}|,|B_{0}|).

Moreover,
11K\min(|A|,|B|)=11K\min(|A_{0}|,|B_{0}|)\geqslant|A_{1}+B_{1}|\geqslant\ldots\geqslant|A_{s}+B_{s}|\geqslant 0.
Therefore

|A_{j+1}+B_{j+1}|\geqslant|A_{j}+B_{j}|-\frac{11K}{s-1}\min(|A|,|B|)
for some index
j
,

1\leqslant j\leqslant s
. We shall
show that the pair
(A_{j},B_{j})
has the desired properties.
Indeed, by construction,

|A_{j}+_{\Gamma_{j}}B_{j}|+\frac{\varepsilon}{s}\min(|A|,|B|)\geqslant|A_{j}+_{\Gamma_{j}}B_{j}|+\frac{\varepsilon}{s}\min(|A_{j}|,|B_{j}|)\geqslant|A_{j+1}+B_{j+1}|.

It follows that

|A_{j}+_{\Gamma_{j}}B_{j}|\geqslant|A_{j}+B_{j}|-\big(\frac{11K}{s-1}+\frac{\varepsilon}{s}\big)\min(|A|,|B|)\geqslant\big(1-\frac{20K}{s}\big)|A_{j}+B_{j}|.

If we choose elements
b_{i}
uniformly at random from
B_{j}
, for
1\leqslant i\leqslant c
then

\begin{split}\E_{b_{1},\ldots,b_{c}\in B}|A_{j}+\{b_{1},\ldots,b_{c}\}|&\geqslant\sum_{x}\Prob_{b_{1},\ldots,b_{c}\in B}(x\in A_{j}+\{b_{1},\ldots,b_{c}\})\geqslant\sum_{x\in C^{+}_{j}}1-[1-\Prob_{b\in B}(x\in A_{j}+b)]^{c}\\
&=\sum_{x\in C^{+}_{j}}1-[1-r_{j}(x)/|B_{j}|]^{c}\geqslant\sum_{x\in C^{+}_{j}}1-(1-\alpha)^{c}\\
&=(1-(1-\alpha)^{c})|C^{+}_{j}|=(1-(1-\alpha)^{c})|A_{j}+_{\Gamma_{j}}B_{j}|\\
&\geqslant(1-(1-\alpha)^{c})(1-\frac{20K}{s})|A_{j}+B_{j}|\geqslant(1-\varepsilon)|A_{j}+B_{j}|.\end{split}

Thus the pair
(|A_{j}|,|B_{j}|)
has the desired properties, so Claim B is proved.
∎

This concludes the proof of Theorem
8
: whether the process stops early or does not, the pair of sets
(A_{j},B_{j})
has the desired
properties.
\square

We now digress briefly to point out that Roth’s theorem is a direct consequence of Theorem
8^{\prime}
. Recall that Roth’s theorem
[
50
]
states that

for every
\alpha>0
there is an integer
k_{0}
such that if
k\geqslant k_{0}
then every set
A\subset[k]
of size at least
\alpha k
contains
3
terms in arithmetic progression.

Proof of Roth’s Theorem.

Fix
\alpha>0
(where we may assume that
\alpha
is sufficiently small). Pick
c
as given by
Theorem
10^{\prime}
with parameters
\frac{\alpha}{4},\frac{2}{\alpha}
, and set

k_{0}=2c[\alpha(1-\alpha)]^{-1}
.

Suppose for a contradiction that there exists a set
A
that is free of three-term arithmetic
progressions, of density
\alpha
in
[1,k]
for some
k\geqslant k_{0}
. We apply Theorem
10^{\prime}

with parameters
\frac{\alpha}{4},\frac{2}{\alpha}
to construct sets
A_{1},A_{2}\subset A
of size at least
(1-\frac{\alpha}{4})|A|
with the property that if we select
a_{1},\ldots,a_{c}
uniformly at random
from
A_{2}
then

\E_{a_{1},\ldots,a_{c}\in A_{2}}\bigg|A_{1}+\{a_{1},\ldots,a_{c}\}\bigg|\geqslant\min\bigg(\big(1-\frac{\alpha}{4}\big)|A_{1}+A_{2}|,\frac{2}{\alpha}|A|\bigg).

On the one hand, note that, as the only solutions to the equation
x+y=2z
in
A
are
x=y=z\in A
, the left-hand side is bounded from above by

|A_{1}+A_{2}|-|A_{1}\cap A_{2}|+c\geqslant\E_{a_{1},\ldots,a_{c}\in A_{2}}\bigg|A_{1}+\{a_{1},\ldots,a_{c}\}\bigg|.

On the other hand, as
|A|\geqslant\alpha k
and
|A_{1}+A_{2}|\leqslant 2k
, the right-hand side is bounded from below by

(1-\frac{\alpha}{4})|A_{1}+A_{2}|\geqslant|A_{1}+A_{2}|-\frac{\alpha}{2}k\geqslant|A_{1}+A_{2}|-\frac{|A|}{2}

Combining the two inequalities above we obtain

2c\geqslant 2|A_{1}\cap A_{2}|-|A|.

Finally, as
A_{1},A_{2}\subset A
satisfy
|A|\geqslant\alpha k
and
|A_{1}|,|A_{2}|\geqslant(1-\frac{\alpha}{4})|A|
, we conclude

2c\geqslant\alpha(1-\alpha)k

which gives the desired contradiction.
\square

Theorem 25

For all

(12)

We now focus on our main aim, Theorem

For every

Lemma 32

(66)

Lemma 33
.

For each
i=1,2,3
there exists a parameter
d_{i}\in\mathbb{N}
and there exist consecutive intervals
I_{i}=[p_{i-1}+1,p_{i}]\text{ and }J_{i}=[q_{i-1},q_{i}]
,
depending only on
I
,
J
and
B
(but not on
A
), such that the sets
A_{i}=A\cap I_{i}\text{ and }B_{i}=B\cap J_{i}

satisfy

(1)

I=I_{1}\sqcup I_{2}\sqcup I_{3}\ \text{and }\ J=J_{1}\cup J_{2}\cup J_{3}
,

(2)

6^{-13}\alpha|J_{i}|\leqslant|I_{i}|\leqslant 6^{13}\alpha^{-1}|J_{i}|
,

(3)

\max\bigg(|I_{i}\Delta A_{i}|,|J_{i}\Delta B_{i}|\bigg)\leqslant 6^{13}\gamma\min(|I_{i}|,|J_{i}|)

(4)

3d_{i}\leqslant\min(|I_{i}|,|J_{i}|)\leqslant(3^{10}+1)d_{i}

(5)

d_{i}|(q_{i}-q_{i-1})
,

(6)

J_{i}\cap\{q_{i}\ \text{\rm mod }\ d_{i}\}=B_{i}\cap\{q_{i}\ \text{\rm mod }\ d_{i}\}

Proof.

Fix parameters
k_{1}=2^{10}\text{ and }k_{3}=3^{10}

and

k_{2}=6\lfloor\frac{|J|}{\min(|I|,|J|)}\rfloor-1\text{ and }d_{2}=\lfloor 6^{-1}\min(|I|,|J|)\rfloor.

(73)

Since (
64
) holds and
|I|
is a multiple of 6, we have

|J|-6^{-1}\min(|I|,|J|)\geqslant k_{2}d_{2}\geqslant\max\bigg(|J|-2\min(|I|,|J|),2^{-1}\min(|I|,|J|)\bigg).

(74)

By the Chinese remainder theorem, there exist parameters
q_{l},q_{r},d_{1},d_{3}\in\mathbb{Z}
such that

q_{1}=q_{l}+k_{1}d_{1}
,
q_{2}=q_{r}-k_{3}d_{3}
and
q_{2}-q_{1}=k_{2}d_{2}
.
For

construct parameters
q_{0,t}=q_{l}
,
q_{1,t}=q_{1}+tk_{1}k_{3}
,
q_{2,t}=q_{2}+tk_{1}k_{3}
and

,
and

,
d_{2,t}=d_{2}
and

q_{1,t}=q_{0,t}+k_{1}d_{1,t}

q_{2,t}=q_{3,t}-k_{3}d_{3,t}

Then the set

is an interval of size

|T|\geqslant\frac{|J|-2-k_{2}d_{2}}{k_{1}k_{3}}-1
.
Using our earlier inequalities, we find that

.
Therefore, for
i=1,2,3

of sizes satisfying

,

.

|I|\geqslant 4\lfloor 2^{-2}\min(|I|,|J|)\rfloor\geqslant 4
.
Therefore, for
i=1,2,3
there are disjoint consecutive intervals
I_{i}=[p_{i-1}+1,p_{i}]
of sizes

|I_{1}|=|I_{3}|=\lfloor 2^{-2}\min(|I|,|J|)\rfloor\geqslant 1
,

.
Finally, for
i=1,2,3
and
t\in T_{2}

g_{1}(i_{1},t)=q_{0,t}+i_{1}d_{1,t}\text{, }\ \ \ g_{2}(i_{2},t)=q_{1,t}+i_{2}d_{2,t}\ \ \ \text{and}\ \ \ g_{3}(i_{3},t)=q_{3,t}-i_{3}d_{3,t}.

Form the partition

(76)

It turns out that

The final lemma we shall need in the proof of Theorem

Lemma 34
.

Under the conditions of Lemma
33
, for
i=1,2,3
we have

\E_{\begin{subarray}{c}w_{j}\in\mathbb{Z}_{d_{i}}\\
j\in[s]\end{subarray}}\bigg|A_{i}+(B_{i}^{q_{i}}\cup_{j\in[s]}B_{i}^{w_{j}})\bigg|\geqslant|A_{i}|+|J_{i}|-1\ \ \ {\text{a}nd}\ \ \ \max_{\begin{subarray}{c}w_{j}\in\mathbb{Z}_{d_{i}}\\
j\in[s]\end{subarray}}\bigg|B_{i}^{q_{i}}\cup_{j\in[s]}B_{i}^{w_{j}}\bigg|\leqslant c^{\prime}.

Proof.

Fix
i
. The proof is divided into two claims, but before we begin we need a simple remark. By condition
(5)
we can write
q_{i}-q_{i-1}=k_{i}d_{i}
for some
k_{i}\in\mathbb{N}
. Moreover, by conditions
(2)
and
(4)
we have
k_{i}\leqslant 18^{13}\alpha^{-1}
.

Claim A.
We have
\bigg|A_{i}+B_{i}^{q_{i}}\bigg|\geqslant|A_{i}|+k_{i}|\widetilde{A_{i}}|

and

\pi_{d_{i}}\bigg(A_{i}+B_{i}^{q_{i}}\bigg)=q_{i}+\widetilde{A_{i}}
.

Proof.

By condition
(6)
we have
|B_{i}^{q_{i}}|=k_{i}+1
. Also, if
x\in q_{i}+\widetilde{A_{i}}
then
|A_{i}^{x-q_{i}}|\geqslant 1
.
By Theorem
13
it follows that
\bigg|(A_{i}+B_{i}^{q_{i}})^{x}\bigg|=|A_{i}^{x-q_{i}}|+|B_{i}^{q_{i}}|-1=|A_{i}^{x-q_{i}}|+k_{i}
.
Hence

\bigg|A_{i}+B_{i}^{q_{i}}\bigg|\geqslant\sum_{x\in q_{i}+\widetilde{A}}\bigg|(A_{i}+B_{i}^{q_{i}})^{x}\bigg|\geqslant\sum_{y\in\widetilde{A_{i}}}|A_{i}^{y}|+k_{i}=|A_{i}|+k_{i}|\widetilde{A_{i}}|

completing the proof of Claim A.
∎

Claim B.
For
z\in\mathbb{Z}_{d_{i}}
, we have

\E_{\begin{subarray}{c}w_{j}\in\mathbb{Z}_{d_{i}}\\
j\in[s]\end{subarray}}\bigg|(A_{i}+\cup_{j\in[s]}B_{i}^{w_{j}})^{z}\bigg|\geqslant k_{i}
.

Proof.

By condition (3) we have

\max\bigg(\sum_{w\in\mathbb{Z}_{d_{i}}}|I_{i}^{w}\setminus A_{i}^{w}|,\sum_{w\in\mathbb{Z}_{d_{i}}}|J_{i}^{w}\setminus B_{i}^{w}|\bigg)\leqslant\max\bigg(|I_{i}\Delta A_{i}|,|J_{i}\Delta B_{i}|\bigg)\leqslant 6^{13}\gamma\min(|I_{i}|,|J_{i}|),

and condition (4) implies that

\max\bigg(\sum_{w\in\mathbb{Z}_{d_{i}}}|I_{i}^{w}\setminus A_{i}^{w}|,\sum_{w\in\mathbb{Z}_{d_{i}}}|J_{i}^{w}\setminus B_{i}^{w}|\bigg)\leqslant 18^{13}\gamma d_{i}.

By condition
(4)
, if
w\in\mathbb{Z}_{d_{i}}
then

|I_{i}^{w}|\geqslant 2\text{ and }|J_{i}^{w}|\geqslant k_{i}
, so

\max\bigg(\sum_{w\in\mathbb{Z}_{d_{i}}}\max(2-|A_{i}^{w}|,0),\sum_{w\in\mathbb{Z}_{d_{i}}}\max(k_{i}-|B_{i}^{w}|,0)\bigg)\leqslant 18^{13}\gamma d_{i}.

We conclude that the sets

X=\{w\in\mathbb{Z}_{d_{i}}\text{ : }|A_{i}^{w}|\leqslant 1\}
and
Y=\{w\in\mathbb{Z}_{d_{i}}\text{ : }|B_{i}^{w}|\leqslant k_{i}-1\}

are not too large:
\max(|X|,|Y|)\leqslant 18^{13}\gamma d_{i}\leqslant 3^{-1}d_{i}
.
Thus

\Prob_{w\in\mathbb{Z}_{d_{i}}}\bigg(w\not\in Y\text{ and }z-w\not\in X\bigg)\geqslant 1/3

and

\Prob_{\begin{subarray}{c}w_{j}\in\mathbb{Z}_{d_{i}}\\
j\in[s]\end{subarray}}\bigg(\exists j\text{ : }w_{j}\not\in Y\text{ and }z-w_{j}\not\in X\bigg)\geqslant 1-2^{s}3^{-s}
.
Equivalently, the event

E=\bigg\{(w_{1},\ldots,w_{d})\in\mathbb{Z}_{d_{i}}^{s}\text{ : }\exists j\text{ : }w_{j}\not\in Y\text{ and }z-w_{j}\not\in Y\bigg\}

has measure

\Prob_{\begin{subarray}{c}w_{j}\in\mathbb{Z}_{d_{i}}\\
j\in[s]\end{subarray}}\bigg((w_{1},\ldots,w_{s})\in E\bigg)\geqslant 1-2^{s}3^{-s}.

By construction and by Theorem
13
, if
(w_{1},\ldots,w_{s})\in E\subset\mathbb{Z}_{d_{i}}^{s}

then

\bigg|(A_{i}+\cup_{j\in[s]}B_{i}^{w_{j}})^{z}\bigg|\geqslant\max_{j\in[s]}\bigg|A_{i}^{z-w_{j}}+B_{i}^{w_{j}}\bigg|\geqslant 2+k_{i}-1=k_{i}+1.

From the last two inequalities it follows that

\begin{split}\E_{\begin{subarray}{c}w_{j}\in\mathbb{Z}_{d_{i}}\\
j\in[s]\end{subarray}}\bigg|(A_{i}+\cup_{j\in[s]}B_{i}^{w_{j}})^{z}\bigg|&\geqslant\E_{\begin{subarray}{c}w_{j}\in\mathbb{Z}_{d_{i}}\\
j\in[s]\end{subarray}}\bigg(\bigg|(A_{i}+\cup_{j\in[s]}B_{i}^{w_{j}})^{z}\bigg|\text{ }\text{ }\text{ : }\text{ }\text{ }E\bigg)\Prob_{\begin{subarray}{c}w_{j}\in\mathbb{Z}_{d_{i}}\\
j\in[s]\end{subarray}}\bigg((w_{1},\ldots,w_{s})\in E\bigg)\\
&\geqslant(k_{i}+1)(1-2^{s}3^{-s})\geqslant k_{i},\end{split}

where the last inequality follows from the estimates
k_{i}\leqslant 18^{13}\alpha^{-1}
,
s\geqslant 2^{9}\log\alpha^{-1}
and
\alpha<2^{-{10}}
.
This finishes the proof of Claim B.

7.
Applications – Restricted Sums

We now turn to the applications mentioned earlier.
In a slight variant of earlier notation, given sets
A,B\subset{\mathbb{Z}}_{p}
and a set
{\mathcal{F}}\subset A\times B
, the
{\mathcal{F}}
-restricted sum of
A
and
B
is

A+_{\mathcal{F}}B=\{a+b:\ (a,b)\not\in{\mathcal{F}}\},

so that
{\mathcal{F}}
is the set of
forbidden pairs
. These general
{\mathcal{F}}
-restricted sums were introduced and studied by Lev
[
37
,
38
,
39
]
over twenty years ago. (We hope that
the reader will not be too annoyed that the subscript now refers to the pairs we do
not
take, as opposed to the earlier notation of
A+_{\Gamma}B
: both of these notations are standard, unfortunately,
and we will use a curly letter in this section to emphasise the difference.)

For
b\in B
, we call
d(b)=\big|\{a:\ (a,b)\in{\mathcal{F}}\}\big|
the
degree
of
b\in B
, and
d({\mathcal{F}})=\max_{b\in B}d(b)
the
degree
of
{\mathcal{F}}
. Note that if
{\mathcal{F}}\subset A\times A
then
d(a)
is the number of pairs
(a^{\prime},a)\in{\mathcal{F}}
, with
a
in the
second
factor of
A\times A
.

We start with two questions. Firstly, can one extend the Erdős–Heilbronn conjecture along the lines of Theorem
3
,
our extension of the Cauchy–Davenport theorem? In other words, is there a constant
c
such that if
p\geqslant 2n-3
and
A
is a subset of
{\mathbb{Z}}_{p}
of size
n
, then there is a set
B^{\prime}\in A^{(\leqslant c)}
such that
\{a+b:\ a\in A,\ b\in B^{\prime},\ a\neq b\}
has at least
2n-3
elements?

Secondly, can one extend the Erdős–Heilbronn conjecture in another direction, replacing the sum
A+A
by an
{\mathcal{F}}
-restricted sum
A+_{\mathcal{F}}A
for a suitable family
{\mathcal{F}}
? Is it true that if
0\leqslant d\leqslant n-1
,
p\geqslant 2n
and we have
A\subset{\mathbb{Z}}_{p}
of size
n
and
{\mathcal{F}}\subset A\times A
with

d({\mathcal{F}})\leqslant d
, then the

{\mathcal{F}}
-restricted sum
A+_{\mathcal{F}}A
has at least
2n-2d-1
elements? The Cauchy–Davenport theorem is the case
d=0
, and the Erdős–Heilbronn conjecture
[
16
]
, made in 1964, and proved thirty years later
by Dias da Silva and Hamidoune
[
14
]
, using tools from linear algebra and the representation
theory of the symmetric group, is the case
d=1
with the very special family

{\mathcal{F}}=\{(a,a):a\in A\}
. Subsequently Alon, Nathanson and Ruzsa
[
1
]
gave a simpler
proof of the Erdős–Heilbronn conjecture, which was based on the polynomial method. More recently, Vu and Wood
[
62
]
gave an entirely combinatorial proof under a weak additional condition.

Our next result shows that
both
extensions hold
simultaneously
, provided
p-2n
is not too small and
d=d(n)
does not grow too fast with
n
. As we shall see, the proof is a straightforward application of Theorem
7
. Note that of course this result implies Theorem
9
from the Introduction.

Theorem 35
.

Let
(d(n))_{1}^{\infty}
be a sequence of natural numbers with
d=d(n)=o(n)
, and let
\beta>0
. Then there are integers
c

and
n_{0}
such that the following holds.

Let
A\subset{\mathbb{Z}}_{p}
with
n_{0}\leqslant|A|=n\leqslant(1-\beta)p/2
, and let
{\mathcal{F}}\subset A\times A
have degree
d
. Then there is a set
B^{\prime}\in A^{(\leqslant c)}
such that
|A+_{\mathcal{F}}B^{\prime}|\geqslant 2n-1-2d
.

Proof.

Let
c\geqslant 2
and
\varepsilon>0
be the constants guaranteed by Theorem
7
for
\alpha=1
and our
\beta>0
, and then let
n_{0}
be such that if
n\geqslant n_{0}
then
cd<\min(\epsilon n,2^{-1}\beta n)
. Finally, assume
n\geqslant n_{0}
.

If there is a set
B^{\prime}\subset A
with
|B^{\prime}|=c
such that
|A+B^{\prime}|\geqslant 2n+r=2n+cd
,
then this

will do for every

, since

|A+_{\mathcal{F}}B^{\prime}|\geqslant 2n+cd-\sum_{b\in B^{\prime}}d(b)\geqslant 2n+cd-|B^{\prime}|d=2n
.
Otherwise, assume that

\max_{B^{\prime}\in B^{C}}|A+B^{\prime}|\leqslant 2n-1+cd\leqslant 2n-1+\epsilon n

Applying Theorem

to the sets

, we find that

is contained in an arithmetic progression of size

. We may assume that the common difference of this arithmetic progression is 1, and we may also assume that

for the first element of

∎

The bound
2n-1-2d
in Theorem

cannot be improved: this is the best bound not only for
|A+_{\mathcal{F}}B^{\prime}|
but also for
|A+_{\mathcal{F}}A|
, even if we demand that we have few forbidden pairs. Indeed, assuming, as we may, that

, this is shown by the set

of size only

and the set
A=\{1,\dots,n\}
. Indeed, in this case the
{\mathcal{F}}
-restricted sum
A+_{{\mathcal{F}}}A
of
A
and
A

is
\{d+2,\dots,2n-d\}
.

Let us give another application of Theorem
7
to a restricted sum. This time we shall assume that the size of
{\mathcal{F}}
is not too large. It is perhaps surprising that
|A+_{\mathcal{F}}A|
can decrease considerably as we increase the number of forbidden pairs.

Theorem 36
.

Let
k=k(n)=o(\sqrt{n})
,
k\geqslant 2
, and
0<\beta<1/2
. Let
c\geqslant 2
and
0<\varepsilon<1/2
be the constants guaranteed by Theorem
7
for
\alpha=1
and our
\beta
.
Then, for
large enough
n
, whenever
A
is a subset of
{\mathbb{Z}}_{p}
with
|A|=n<(1-\beta)p/2
and

{\mathcal{F}}\subset A\times A
satisfies
|{\mathcal{F}}|\leqslant k(k-1)
, then
for some
B^{\prime}\in A^{(\leqslant c)}
we have

|A+_{\mathcal{F}}B^{\prime}|\geqslant 2n+1-2k.

(77)

Proof.

Let
n
be large enough to guarantee
k^{2}<\varepsilon n
and
2k^{2}<\beta p
.
If there is a set
B^{\prime}\subset A
with
|B^{\prime}|=c
such that
|A+B^{\prime}|\geqslant 2n+k^{2}
,
then (
77
) follows.

Otherwise, by Theorem
7
, the set
A
is contained in an arithmetic progression of size
n+k^{2}
. Assume, as we may, that
A=\{a_{1},\dots,a_{n}\}\subset\{1,\dots,n+k^{2}\}
, with
a_{i}<a_{i+1}
. Since
2(n+k^{2})<p
, the sum
A+A
has no ‘wraparound’. Set
d_{i}=d(a_{i})
,
s=\min_{1\leqslant i\leqslant n}\big(i+d_{i}\big)
and

. Then, crudely,
s<n/2<t
,

d_{1}\geqslant s-1,\ \ \ \ \ \ d_{2}\geqslant s-2,\ \ \ \ \dots,\ \ \ \ d_{s-1}\geqslant 1,

and

d_{n}\geqslant t-1,\ \ \ d_{n-1}\geqslant t-2,\ \ \ \dots,\ \ \ d_{n-t+2}\geqslant 1.

This implies that

so

.

Finally, if
j
and

are suffices where the minima are attained then, using

for the second summand,

\displaystyle|A+_{\mathcal{F}}\{a_{j},a_{h}\}|

\displaystyle\geqslant

\displaystyle|A+\{a_{j},a_{h}\}|-d_{j}-d_{h}

\displaystyle\geqslant

\displaystyle 2n+1-j-d_{j}-(n+1-h)-d_{h}

\displaystyle\geqslant

\displaystyle 2n+1-s-t\geqslant 2n+1-2k.

This completes our proof.
∎

The bound in Theorem
36
cannot be improved. Indeed, let
A=[n]
and

Then
\big|{\mathcal{F}}\big|=k(k-1)
and
A+_{\mathcal{F}}A=[k+1,2n-k+1]
.

It is not impossible that the bounds we have imposed on

in Theorem
35
, and on

in Theorem

are unnecessary. We shall state these as conjectures in the last section.

8.
Applications – Sums of Sets of Reals

As we shall see now, our results in the discrete world have easy corollaries in the continuous world. Throughout this section we shall denote by
|\cdot|
the Lebesgue measure on the Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}
and Haar measure (normalized Lebesgue measure) on the circle
\mathbb{T}=\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}
. Our starting point is the triviality that if
A
and
B
are non-empty compact sets of reals then
|A+B|\geqslant|A|+|B|
.

The deduction of these corollaries from the corresponding theorems is straightforward. First one reduces the statement from general compact sets to finite unions of closed intervals. Then one reduces the statement to discrete sets by taking the intersection with a sufficiently fine lattice. Let us show, for example, how one can deduce Corollary
10
from Theorem
1
.

Proof of Corollary
10
.

We first reduce Corollary
10
to the case when
A
and
B
are finite unions of intervals and
|A|>|B|
.

Fix compact sets
A
and
B
in
\mathbb{R}
and construct nested sequences of sets
A_{n}
and
B_{n}
in
\mathbb{R}
, all of which are finite unions of closed intervals,
|A_{n}|\geqslant|B_{n}|
,
\cap_{n}A_{n}=A
and
\cap_{n}B_{n}=B
.

To prove

\max_{b_{1},b_{2},b_{3}\in B}|A+\{b_{1},b_{2},b_{3}\}|\geqslant|A|+|B|

given that

\max_{b_{1}^{n},b_{2}^{n},b_{3}^{n}\in B_{n}}|A_{n}+\{b_{1}^{n},b_{2}^{n},b_{3}^{n}\}|\geqslant|A_{n}|+|B_{n}|\geqslant|A|+|B|,

it suffices to show that

\max_{b_{1},b_{2},b_{3}\in B}|A+\{b_{1},b_{2},b_{3}\}|\geqslant\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\max_{b_{1}^{n},b_{2}^{n},b_{3}^{n}\in B_{n}}|A_{n}+\{b_{1}^{n},b_{2}^{n},b_{3}^{n}\}|.

For this, note that the points
b_{1}^{n},b_{2}^{n},b_{3}^{n}
are contained inside the compact set
B_{1}
. Therefore, we can restrict to convergent subsequences
b_{1}^{n_{i}}\rightarrow b_{1}^{0}
,
b_{2}^{n_{i}}\rightarrow b_{2}^{0}
and
b_{3}^{n_{i}}\rightarrow b_{3}^{0}
. As the sets
B_{i}
are compact, it follows that
b_{1}^{0},b_{2}^{0},b_{3}^{0}\in\cap_{i}B_{n_{i}}=B
. To conclude observe that the function
f(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})=|A+\{x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}\}|
is continuous and that for all
x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}
we have
|A+\{x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}\}|\geqslant|A_{n}+\{x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}\}|-3|A_{n}\Delta A|
.

Now we further reduce Corollary
10
to Theorem
1
.
Fix sets
A
and
B
in
\mathbb{R}
that are finite unions of intervals and
|A|>|B|
, and construct sets
A_{n}=A\cap n^{-1}\mathbb{Z}
and
B_{n}=B\cap n^{-1}\mathbb{Z}
.

To prove

\max_{b_{1},b_{2},b_{3}\in B}|A+\{b_{1},b_{2},b_{3}\}|\geqslant|A|+|B|

given that

\max_{b_{1}^{n},b_{2}^{n},b_{3}^{n}\in B_{n}}|A_{n}+\{b_{1}^{n},b_{2}^{n},b_{3}^{n}\}|\geqslant|A_{n}|+|B_{n}|-1,

it suffices to show that, for
n
large,
|A_{n}+\{b_{1}^{n},b_{2}^{n},b_{3}^{n}\}|=n|A+\{b_{1}^{n},b_{2}^{n},b_{3}^{n}\}|-O(1)
,
|A_{n}|=n|A|+O(1)
and
|B_{n}|=n|B|+O(1)
.
For this, recall that
A
is a finite union of intervals and for an interval
X
it is trivial that
|X|=|X\cap n^{-1}\mathbb{Z}|+O(1)
. This concludes the proof.
∎

The proof of Corollary
11
from Theorems
4
and
5

follows identical lines. Finally, we mention that there
is also a continuous version of Theorem
8
, again proved by the same method.

Corollary 37
.

For all
K
and
\varepsilon>0
there is an integer
c
such that for all

the following holds.
Let
A
and
B
be non-empty compact subsets of the torus
\mathbb{T}^{d}
. Then there are compact subsets

Open Problems and Conjectures

Perhaps the most intriguing open problem is the question of whether, in
Theorem
4
, the number
of translates can be taken to be three, if the sizes of
A
and
B
are a sufficiently small
multiple of
p
. We
believe that this should be the case.

Conjecture 1.
There exists
0<\alpha<1/2
such that whenever

A
and
B
are subsets of
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
with
|A|=|B|\leqslant\alpha p
,
there exist
b_{1},b_{2},b_{3}\in B
such that
|A+\{b_{1},b_{2},b_{3}\}|\geqslant|A|+|B|-1.

In terms of inverse results, we have not considered any questions about inverse forms of our starting theorem about
three translates in
\mathbb{Z}
, Theorem
1
. We will address this in a forthcoming paper.

It would also be interesting to know if the influence of the structure of
B
on our results is
independent of scaling. For example, we suspect that the following is the case.

Conjecture 2.
Let integers
c
and
n
and a set

be fixed. Suppose for every set

of size

there are

c
elements

such that

. Then the same holds if

is replaced by

\lambda B
for any integer
\lambda
.

Turning to restricted sums, the main question is whether the results in Section 7 hold without the perhaps
artificial restrictions on the value of

, the maximum degree of the set of forbidden pairs.

Conjecture 3.
For every
\beta>0
there
are constants
c
and
n_{0}
such that the following holds. If
A
is a subset of
{\mathbb{Z}}_{p}

tells us that this conjecture holds for

Firstly, does Conjecture 3 actually hold for

?
Secondly, does it hold for two sets,

with

, with

having

?
It is important to note that such questions are unknown even if we drop the condition that we are passing to a subset

Turning again to

, could it be that Theorem

holds

?

When

and

are subsets of

with

\displaystyle\geqslant|A|+|\widetilde{A}|+|A|\max\bigg(0,\frac{|\widetilde{B}|-|\widetilde{A}|}{|\widetilde{B}|}\bigg)

|\pi_{m}(A)|\geqslant|B|-1
then we have

\E_{b\in B\setminus\{m\}}\bigg|A+\{0,b,m\}\bigg|\geqslant|A|+|\pi_{m}(A)|\\
\geqslant|A|+|B|-1.

If we assume that
|A|\geqslant|B|-1
and
|\pi_{m}(A)|\leqslant|B|-1
then we have

\displaystyle\E_{b\in B\setminus\{m\}}\bigg|A+\{0,b,m\}\bigg|

\displaystyle\geqslant|A|+|\pi_{m}(A)|+|A|\frac{|B|-1-|\pi_{m}(A)|}{|B|-1}

\displaystyle\geqslant|A|+|\pi_{m}(A)|+|B|-1-|\pi_{m}(A)|=|A|+|B|-1.

Finally, if we assume
|A|\leqslant|B|-1
, which implies
|\pi_{m}(A)|\leqslant|B|-1
, then we obtain

\E_{b\in B\setminus\{m\}}\bigg|A+\{0,b,m\}\bigg|\geqslant|A|+|\pi_{m}(A)|+|A|\frac{|B|-1-|\pi_{m}(A)|}{|B|-1}=2|A|+|\pi_{m}(A)|\frac{|B|-1-|A|}{|B|-1}.

This concludes the proof of Theorem
19
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
22
.

We begin with a construction and continue with some preliminary estimates. Partition
A
into two parts,

A=A_{1}\sqcup A_{2}
,
such that

\widetilde{A_{2}}=\widetilde{A}\text{ and }|A_{2}|=|\widetilde{A}|\text{ and }|A_{1}|=|A|-|\widetilde{A}|.

Lemma
16
tells us that
\pi_{m}\bigg(A\cup(A+m)\bigg)=\widetilde{A}

and
\bigg|A\cup(A+m)\bigg|\geqslant|A|+|\widetilde{A}|
, and Lemma
20
gives

\E_{b\in B\setminus\{m\}}\bigg|(A_{1}+b)\setminus\pi_{m}^{-1}(\widetilde{A})\bigg|\geqslant(|A|-|\widetilde{A}|)\max\bigg(0,\frac{|\widetilde{B}|-|\widetilde{A}|}{|\widetilde{B}|}\bigg).

Noting the bijection
\pi_{m}(B\setminus\{m\})=\widetilde{B},

for
z\in\mathbb{Z}_{d}
we have

\displaystyle\Prob_{b\in B\setminus\{m\}}\bigg((A_{2}+b)\cap\pi_{m}^{-1}(z)\neq\emptyset\bigg)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Prob_{\widetilde{b}\in\widetilde{B}}\bigg(z\in\widetilde{b}+\widetilde{A_{2}}\bigg)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Prob_{\widetilde{b}\in\widetilde{B}}\bigg(z\in\widetilde{b}+\widetilde{A}\bigg)\geqslant\max\bigg(0,\frac{|\widetilde{B}|+|\widetilde{A}|-m}{|\widetilde{B}|}\bigg).

It follows that

\displaystyle\E_{b\in B\setminus\{m\}}\bigg|(A_{2}+b)\setminus\pi_{m}^{-1}(\widetilde{A})\bigg|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{z\not\in\widetilde{A}}\Prob_{b\in B\setminus\{m\}}\bigg((A_{2}+b)\cap\pi_{m}^{-1}(z)\neq\emptyset\bigg)

\displaystyle\geqslant

\displaystyle(m-|\widetilde{A}|)\max\bigg(0,\frac{|\widetilde{B}|+|\widetilde{A}|-m}{|\widetilde{B}|}\bigg).

Now, by Theorem
19
, we may assume that

\frac{(2|\pi_{m}(A)|-m)(m-(|B|-1))-1}{|B|-1}>0.

In particular, we have
2|\pi_{m}(A)|>m
, i.e.
2|\widetilde{A}|>m
.
Since
|\widetilde{B}|+1\geqslant|\widetilde{A}|
, the last two
inequalities imply

\frac{|\widetilde{B}|+|\widetilde{A}|-m}{|\widetilde{B}|}\geqslant 0.

The inequalities in this proof can be put together to imply

\E_{b\in B\setminus\{m\}}\bigg|A+\{0,b,m\}\bigg|\geqslant|A|+|B|-1+\max\bigg(0,\frac{(2|\pi_{m}(A)|-m)(m-(|B|-1))-1}{|B|-1}\bigg),

completing the proof of Theorem
22
.
∎
