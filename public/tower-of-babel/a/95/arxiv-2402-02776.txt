Abstract
. A boundary control problem for the following generalized Korteweg-de Vries-Burgers-Huxley equation: u t = ν ​ u x ​ x − μ ​ u x ​ x ​ x − α ​ u δ ​ u x + β ​ u ​ ( 1 − u δ ) ​ ( u δ − γ ) , x ∈ [ 0 , 1 ] , t > 0 , u_{t}=\nu u_{xx}-\mu u_{xxx}-\alpha u^{\delta}u_{x}+\beta u(1-u^{\delta})(u^{\delta}-\gamma),\ x\in[0,1],\ t>0, where ν , μ , α , β > 0 , \nu,\mu,\alpha,\beta>0, δ ∈ [ 1 , ∞ ) \delta\in[1,\infty) , γ ∈ ( 0 , 1 ) \gamma\in(0,1) subject to Neumann boundary conditions is considered in this work. We first establish the well-posedness of the Neumann boundary value problem by an application of monotonicity arguments, the Hartman-Stampacchia theorem, the Minty-Browder theorem, and the Crandall-Liggett theorem. The additional difficulties caused by the third order linear term is successfully handled by proving a proper version of the Minty-Browder theorem. By using suitable feedback boundary controls, we demonstrate L 2 \mathrm{L}^{2} - and H 1 \mathrm{H}^{1} -stability properties of the closed-loop system for sufficiently large ν > 0 \nu>0 . The analytical conclusions from this work are supported and validated by numerical investigations.



1.
Introduction

One of the most basic nonlinear mathematical models that illustrates the characteristics of both dispersion and dissipation is the Korteweg-de Vries-Burgers (KdVB) equation (
[
5
,
6
,
11
,
30
]
). In this work, we consider a generalized Korteweg-de Vries-Burgers-Huxley equation which has the characteristics of
dispersion, dissipation, convection and reaction effects
.

1.1.
The model

One type of partial differential equation (PDE) for a shallow water wave with unidirectional motion is the
Korteweg-de Vries (KdV) equation
(
[
7
]
),

u_{t}=-\mu u_{xxx}-\alpha uu_{x},\ \mu,\alpha>0,

which is also a common representative of
nonlinear dispersive equations
. The
viscous Burgers equation
(
[
27
]
),

u_{t}=\nu u_{xx}-\alpha uu_{x},\ \nu,\alpha>0,

a common representative of
nonlinear dissipative equations
or
convection-diffusion equation
, is used in a number of applied mathematics fields, including traffic flow, fluid mechanics, nonlinear acoustics, and gas dynamics. The case of pure dispersion and pure dissipation is quite uncommon in many real-world physical problems. The
Korteweg-de Vries-Burgers (KdVB)
equation is formed when a diffusion term is added to the KdV equation. The KdVB equations are given by (
[
5
]
)

u_{t}=\nu u_{xx}-\mu u_{xxx}-\alpha uu_{x},\ \nu,\mu,\alpha>0.

It is discovered that this equation also represents a wide range of other physical phenomena, including lattice waves, the propagation of ion-acoustic waves in cold plasma, and pressure waves in liquid-gas bubble mixtures, etc. Actually, in addition to dispersion, several of these phenomena also show dissipative effects, which makes the so-called KdVB equation a better model (
[
13
]
).

A nonlinear PDE that explains the interplay between reaction mechanisms, convection effects, and diffusion transports is the
Burgers-Huxley equation
(
[
44
]
):

u_{t}=\nu u_{xx}-\alpha uu_{x}+\beta u(1-u)(u-\gamma),\ \nu,\mu,\alpha,\beta>0,\ \gamma\in(0,1).

The
Korteweg-de Vries-Burgers-Huxley (KdVBH)
equation is obtained by adding a reaction term similar to the one found in the Burgers-Huxley equation. We examine the following generalized version of KdVBH equation in this article:

(1.1)

\displaystyle u_{t}=\nu u_{xx}-\mu u_{xxx}-\alpha u^{\delta}u_{x}+\beta u(1-u^{\delta})(u^{\delta}-\gamma),\ x\in(0,1),\ t>0,

where
\nu,\mu,\alpha,\beta>0,\ \gamma\in(0,1),\delta\in[1,\infty),
with the initial condition

(1.2)

\displaystyle u(x,0)=u_{0}(x),\ x\in[0,1].

We call
\nu>0
as the
dissipation coefficient
,
\alpha>0
as the
convection coefficient
and
\mu>0
as the
dispersion coefficient.
The equation (
1.1
) can be considered as an example of a
convection-diffusion-reaction equation of dispersive type.

Many authors have actively worked on the KdV and KdVB equations from a variety of angles (
[
8
,
10
,
28
,
37
]
, etc., and references therein). The aim of this work is to analyze a stabilization problem by using a boundary feedback control for the system (
1.1
)-(
1.2
). Similar problems for KdVB equation have been considered in the works
[
5
,
29
]
.

1.2.
The boundary control

Control problems for partial differential equations have been a focus of intense research for the past several decades. Generally speaking, there are two types of control procedures for partial differential equations: boundary control and distributed control. It is difficult for engineers to implement distributed control, which calls for actuators to be positioned at every point in the spatial domain (
[
23
]
). Boundary control is simple and inexpensive to implement; it only requires applying actuators on the edge of the spatial region.

Let us now discuss some of the boundary control problems discussed in the literature for KdVB equation. For the forced KdVB equation, the author in
[
45
]
demonstrated that if the external forcing is time-periodic with small amplitude, then the problem also admits a unique time-periodic solution with the same period, and the solution is stable. The boundary stabilization problem of KdVB equation is studied in
[
5
,
29
]
. The authors of
[
5
,
29
]
demonstrated well-posedness, global exponential stability in
\mathrm{L}^{2}
, global asymptotic stability in
\mathrm{H}^{1}
, and semi-global exponential stability in
\mathrm{L}^{2}
of KdVB equation. Nonlinear boundary stabilization for a generalized KdVB equation is examined in
[
38
,
39
,
40
]
, etc. By proposing an another class of adaptive controls, the authors in
[
15
]
established well-posedness as well as the
\mathrm{L}^{2}
-global exponential stability of the solutions of KdV and KdVB equations. By using suitable boundary controls, the authors in
[
21
,
22
]
proved that the solutions of the KdVB equation globally exist and globally exponentially tend to zero as
t\to\infty
in a subspace of
\mathrm{H}^{s}
for
s\in[0,3]
. The authors in
[
6
]
developed two approaches for the stabilization of nonlinear KdV equation with boundary time-delay feedback. For more interesting problems on the control and stabilization of KdV and KdVB equations, we refer the interested readers to
[
1

,

, etc., and references therein.

1.3.
Difficulties, approaches and novelties

The major difficulty of the work lies in establishing the well-posedness of the generalized KdV-Burgers-Huxley equation (
1.1
)-(
1.2
) with the controls (
1.4
) and (
1.3
) for
\delta=1
and
\delta=2
, respectively. As the problem (
1.1
) is of the third order dispersive type, there are limitations in using the standard Minty-Browder result. The classical result on Minty-Browder Theorem states that any monotone, hemicontinuous and strongly coercive operator
T:\mathrm{X}\to\mathrm{X}^{*}
, where
\mathrm{X}
is a reflexive Banach space, is onto, that is,
\mathrm{Range}(T)=\mathrm{X}^{*}
(
[
16
, Theorem 3.3.1]
). By properly defining hemicontinuity and coercivity in our context (see (
2.3
) and (
2.7
) below) and applying the infinite-dimensional version of the Hartman-Stampacchia theorem (
[
25
, Theorem 1.4, Chapter III]
), we show the same result for
T:\mathrm{X}\to\mathrm{Y}^{*},
that is,
\mathrm{Range}(T)=\mathrm{Y}^{*}
(Theorem
2.3
), where
\mathrm{Y}
is also a reflexive Banach space such that the embedding
\mathrm{X}\subset\mathrm{Y}
is dense (Lemma
2.1
). We hope that the abstract result obtained in Theorem
2.3
can be used to prove the well-posedness of various systems of the type (
1.1
).

Another major difficulty is the restriction of
\delta\in\{1,2\}
for any
\nu>0
. This restriction is due to the choice of our controls given in (
1.3
) and (
1.4
) and the lack of existence and uniqueness results for the other values of
\delta
(see (
2.2
) in the proof of Theorem
2.6
). For
\delta\in\{1,2\}
, we use monotonicity arguments (Lemma
2.5
), the Minty-Browder theorem (Theorem
2.3
), and the Crandall-Liggett theorem to prove the existence and uniqueness of strong solutions for a cutoff problem (see (
2.53
) below). Then, by using uniform energy estimates, we show the global solvability of the problem (
1.1
)-(
1.2
) with the controls (
1.4
) and (
1.3
) for
\delta=1
and
\delta=2
, respectively (Theorem
2.6
). The coercivity results (see (
2.4
) and (
2.4
) below) immediately help us to obtain the
\mathrm{L}^{2}
-exponential stabilization of the problem (
1.1
)-(
1.2
) under the assumption
\nu>\frac{\beta}{4}(1-\gamma)^{2}
(Theorem
3.1
). But under a restrictive assumption on
\nu
, that is,
\nu>\frac{\alpha^{2}}{2\beta}(\delta+2)^{-\frac{2(\delta+1)}{\delta+2}}
, we observe that the convective term can be handled by using the diffusion as well as reaction terms for any
\delta\in[1,\infty)
and the well-posedness can be established. For sufficiently large
\nu
(see (
3.3
) below), we are also able to obtain
\mathrm{L}^{2}
,
\mathrm{H}^{1}
and pointwise exponential stabilization results (Theorems
3.2
,
3.3
and
3.4
).

2.
Well-posedness and Stabilization

In order to formulate the problem (
1.1
)-(
1.3
) as an abstract initial value problem, we consider the Hilbert space
\mathrm{H}=\mathrm{L}^{2}(0,1)
and the operator
\mathscr{A}:\mathrm{D}(\mathscr{A})\subset\mathrm{H}\to\mathrm{H}
as

(2.1)

\displaystyle\mathscr{A}(v):=-\nu v_{xx}+\mu v_{xxx}+\frac{\alpha}{\delta+1}(v^{\delta+1})_{x}-\beta v(1-v^{\delta})(v^{\delta}-\gamma),

and the domain

(2.2)

\displaystyle\mathrm{D}(\mathscr{A}):=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{u\in\mathrm{H}^{3}(0,1):u(0)=0,\ u_{x}(1)=-g_{1}(u(1)),\ u_{xx}(1)=g_{2}(u(1))}}\right\}.

With the above notations, the problem (
1.1
)-(
1.3
) can be reformulated as

(2.3)

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\begin{aligned} \frac{du(t)}{dt}+\mathscr{A}(u)(t)&=0,\ t>0,\\
u(0)&=u_{0}.\end{aligned}}}\right.

Performing an integration by parts in (
2.1
), for each
v\in\mathrm{D}(\mathscr{A})
, we have

\displaystyle(\mathscr{A}(v),w)

\displaystyle=\nu(v_{x},w_{x})+\nu g_{1}(v(1))w(1)-\mu(v_{xx},w_{x})+\mu g_{2}(v(1))w(1)

(2.4)

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{\alpha}{\delta+1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[v^{\delta+1}(1)w(1)-(v^{\delta+1},w_{x})}}\right]-\beta(v(1-v^{\delta})(v^{\delta}-\gamma),w),

for all

w\in\mathrm{X}:=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{u\in\mathrm{H}^{2}(0,1):u(0)=0,u_{x}(1)=-g_{1}(u(1))}}\right\}.

Let us define
\mathrm{Y}:=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{u\in\mathrm{H}^{1}(0,1):u(0)=0}}\right\}
. Clearly
\mathrm{X}\subset\mathrm{Y}
and the embedding is continuous.
Let us first prove the following technical result:

Lemma 2.1
.

The space
\mathrm{X}
is reflexive and is a dense subspace of
\mathrm{Y}
.

Proof.

Remember that closed subspaces of reflexive Banach spaces are reflexive (
[
24
, Theorem 5.9]
). As
\mathrm{H}^{2}(0,1)
is a Hilbert space, in order to prove
\mathrm{X}
is reflexive, it is enough to show that
\mathrm{X}
is closed.

Claim:

\mathrm{X}
is a closed subspace of
\mathrm{H}^{2}(0,1)
.
Let
u_{n}\in\mathrm{X}
bs such that
\|u_{n}-u\|_{\mathrm{H}^{2}}\to 0
as
n\to\infty
. Clearly
u\in\mathrm{H}^{2}(0,1)
,
u_{n}(0)=0
and
u_{n,x}(1)=-g_{1}(u_{n}(1))
. We need to show that
u(0)=0
and
u_{x}(1)=-g_{1}(u_{(}1))
. The convergence
\|u_{n}-u\|_{\mathrm{H}^{2}}\to 0
implies
\|u_{n}-u\|_{\mathrm{H}^{1}}\to 0
, and since
\mathrm{H}^{1}(0,1)\hookrightarrow\mathrm{C}([0,1])
, we deuce
u_{n}(x)\to u(x)
for all
x\in[0,1]
and
u\in\mathrm{C}([0,1])
with
u(0)=0
. Moreover, it is immediate that
u_{n}(1)\to u(1)
and since
g_{1}
is continuous function, we get
g_{1}(u_{n}(1))\to g_{1}(u(1))
as
n\to\infty
. Since
\mathrm{H}^{1}(0,1)\hookrightarrow\mathrm{C}^{1}([0,1])
, one can easily deduce that
u_{n,x}(x)\to u_{x}(x)
for all
x\in[0,1]
and by using the uniqueness of limit, we deduce
u_{x}(1)=-g_{1}(u(1))
. Therefore,
u\in\mathrm{X}
and hence
\mathrm{X}
is a closed subspace of
\mathrm{H}^{2}(0,1)
.

Claim:

\mathrm{X}
is a dense subspace of
\mathrm{Y}
.

Fix any
v\in\mathrm{Y}
, that is,
v\in\mathrm{H}^{1}(0,1)
and
v(0)=0
. As a subspace of
\mathrm{H}^{1}(0,1)
, the space
\mathrm{Y}
inherits the
\mathrm{H}^{1}
-norm, but as

, the
\mathrm{Y}
-norm is equivalent to
\|v_{x}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}}
for all

. It is just a simple application of the Poincaré inequality (see Lemma

below). Since the embedding
\mathrm{H}^{2}(0,1)\subset\mathrm{H}^{1}(0,1)
is dense, there exists a sequence
\{v_{m}\}_{m\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\mathrm{H}^{2}(0,1)
such that
\|v_{m}-v\|_{\mathrm{H}^{1}}\to 0
as
m\to\infty
. Since
\mathrm{H}^{2}(0,1)\hookrightarrow\mathrm{C}^{1}([0,1])
, we choose the sequence
\{v_{m}\}_{m\in\mathbb{N}}
in such a way that
v_{m,x}(1)\to 0
as
m\to\infty
. By defining
w_{m}(x)=v_{m}(x)-v_{m}(0)

x\in[0,1]
, we find
w_{m}(0)=0
and
\|w_{m}-v\|_{\mathrm{Y}}=\|(v_{m}-v_{m}(0)-v)_{x}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}}=\|v_{m,x}-v_{x}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}}\to 0

\{v\in\mathrm{H}^{2}(0,1):v(0)=0\}
is dense in
\mathrm{Y}
.

Let us now show that
\mathrm{X}
is dense in
\mathrm{Y}
. We consider for

,
\varphi_{m}(x)=x(1-x)^{m}

\displaystyle\|\varphi_{m}\|_{\mathrm{Y}}^{2}=\|\varphi_{m,x}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}}^{2}=\frac{m}{(2m+1)(2m-1)}\to 0\ \text{ as }\ m\to\infty.

Since
m\geq 2
, it is clear that
\varphi_{m}\in\mathrm{H}^{2}(0,1)
. Let us define

\displaystyle z_{m}(x):=w_{m}(x)+(w_{m,x}(1)+g_{1}(w_{m}(1)))\varphi_{m}(1-x).

Then
z_{m}\in\mathrm{H}^{2}(0,1)
for all

. Since

for all
x\in[0,1]
. Since
g_{1}(\cdot)
is a continuous function
g_{1}(w_{m}(1))\to g_{1}(v(1))
as
m\to\infty
. Therefore
|g_{1}(w_{m}(1))|\leq K_{1}
for all
m\in\mathbb{N}
. Note that
w_{m,x}(1)=v_{m,x}(1)\to 0
as
m\to\infty
. Thus, it can be easily seen that

\displaystyle\|z_{m}-v\|_{\mathrm{Y}}

\displaystyle=\|z_{m,x}-v_{x}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}}\leq\|w_{m,x}-v_{x}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}}+(|w_{m,x}(1)|+|g_{1}(w_{m}(1))|)\|\varphi_{m,x}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}}

\displaystyle\to 0\ \text{ as }\ m\to\infty,

which completes the proof.
∎

Remark 2.2
.

Since the continuous embedding
\mathrm{X}\subset\mathrm{Y}
is dense, from
[
19
, Lemma 2.2.27]
, we infer that the embedding
\mathrm{Y}^{*}\subset\mathrm{X}^{*}
is continuous and the reflexivity of
\mathrm{X}
implies that
\mathrm{Y}^{*}
is dense in
\mathrm{X}^{*}
.

2.1.
Abstract result

For a mapping
T:\mathrm{X}\to\mathrm{Y}^{*}
, the following result is a slight modification of the results presented in
[
3
, Corollary 2.1.3.]
,
[
16
, Theorem 3.3.1]
, etc., where the mapping
T:\mathrm{X}\to\mathrm{X}^{*}
. Therefore, we are providing a proof here (Minty’s theorem).

Theorem 2.3
.

Let
\mathrm{X}
and
\mathrm{Y}
be reflexive Banach spaces such that the continuous embedding
\mathrm{X}\hookrightarrow\mathrm{Y}
is dense. Let
T:\mathrm{X}\to\mathrm{Y}^{*}
be monotone, that is,

(2.5)

\displaystyle\langle T(u)-T(v),u-v\rangle\geq 0\ \text{ for all }\ u,v\in\mathrm{X},

hemicontinuous, that is,

(2.6)

\displaystyle\lim\limits_{\lambda\to 0}\langle T(u_{1}+\lambda u_{2}),v\rangle=\langle T(u_{1}),v\rangle,\ \text{ for all }\ u_{1},u_{2}\in\mathrm{X},\ \text{ and }\ v\in\mathrm{Y},

and coercive, that is,

(2.7)

\displaystyle\lim\limits_{\|u\|_{\mathrm{Y}\to\infty}}\frac{\langle T(u),u\rangle}{\|u\|_{\mathrm{Y}}}=\infty,\ \text{ for all }\ u\in\mathrm{X},

operator. Then
T
is onto, that is,
\mathrm{Range}(T)=\mathrm{Y}^{*}
.

Proof.

Let
f\in\mathrm{Y}^{*}
. We have for all
\mathrm{X}\ni u\neq 0
,

(2.8)

\displaystyle\langle T(u)-f,u\rangle\geq\|u\|_{\mathrm{Y}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\frac{\langle T(u),u\rangle}{\|u\|_{\mathrm{Y}}}-\|f\|_{\mathrm{Y}^{*}}}}\right].

Since
T
is coercive in the sense of (
2.7
), for any
N>0
, there exists an
R>0
such that
\frac{\langle T(u),u\rangle}{\|u\|_{\mathrm{Y}}}>N
for all
\|u\|_{\mathrm{Y}}\geq R
. Therefore, for
N>\|f\|_{\mathrm{Y}^{*}}
, there exists an
R>0
such that

(2.9)

\displaystyle\langle T(u)-f,u\rangle>0\ \text{ for all }\ \|u\|_{\mathrm{Y}}\geq R.

Let us define
\mathbb{K}:=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{u\in\mathrm{X}:\|u\|_{\mathrm{Y}}\leq R}}\right\}
. Then
\mathbb{K}
is a closed and convex subset of
\mathrm{X}
. We further define
\mathbb{K}_{R}=\mathbb{K}\cap\Sigma_{R}=\Sigma_{R}
, where
\Sigma_{R}:=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{u\in\mathrm{X}:\|u\|_{\mathrm{X}}\leq R}}\right\}
. Then
\mathbb{K}_{R}
is not only a closed and convex but also a bounded subset of
\mathrm{X}
. As
\mathrm{X}
is reflexive (Lemma
2.1
), and
T
is monotone and hemicontinuous, we infer from
[
16
, Lemma 3.3.2]
that
T
is demicontinuous. The reflexivity of
\mathrm{X}
and
[
32
, Remark 5.21]
imply that the demicontinuous operator
T:\mathbb{K}_{R}\to\mathrm{Y}^{*}
is continuous on finite dimensional subspaces of
\mathbb{K}_{R},
that is, for any finite dimensional subspace
M\subset\mathrm{X}
, the restriction of
T
to
\mathbb{K}_{R}\cap M
is weakly continuous, namely,
T:\mathbb{K}_{R}\cap M\to\mathrm{Y}^{*}
is weakly continuous. Together with this fact, the monotonicity of the operator
T:\mathbb{K}_{R}\to\mathrm{Y}^{*}
and the Hartman-Stampacchia theorem (see
[
25
, Theorem 1.4, Chapter III]
) yield the existence of a
u_{R}\in\mathbb{K}_{R}
such that

(2.10)

\displaystyle\langle T(u_{R})-f,w-u_{R}\rangle\geq 0\ \text{ for all }\ w\in\mathbb{K}_{R}.

The monotonicity as well as coercivity properties of
T:\mathbb{K}\to\mathrm{Y}^{*}
imply (see (
2.5
) and (
2.7
))

\displaystyle\frac{\langle(T(u)-f)-(T(u_{*})-f),u-u_{*}\rangle}{\|u-u_{*}\|_{\mathrm{Y}}}\to\infty\ \text{ as }\ \|u\|_{\mathrm{Y}}\to\infty,\ u\in\mathbb{K},

for some
u_{*}\in\mathbb{K}_{R}
. For instance, one can choose
u_{*}=0\in\mathbb{K}_{R}
. Choose,
K>\|T(u_{*})-f\|_{\mathrm{Y}^{*}}
and
R>\|u_{*}\|_{\mathrm{Y}}
such that

\displaystyle\langle(T(u)-f)-(T(u_{*})-f),u-u_{*}\rangle\geq K\|u-u_{*}\|_{\mathrm{Y}}\ \text{ for }\ \|u\|_{\mathrm{Y}}\geq R,\ u\in\mathbb{K}.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\langle(T(u)-f),u-u_{*}\rangle

\displaystyle\geq K\|u-u_{*}\|_{\mathrm{Y}}+\langle(T(u_{*})-f),u-u_{*}\rangle

\displaystyle\geq K\|u-u_{*}\|_{\mathrm{Y}}-\|T(u_{*})-f\|_{\mathrm{Y}^{*}}\|u-u_{*}\|_{\mathrm{Y}}

(2.11)

\displaystyle\geq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(K-\|T(u_{*})-f\|_{\mathrm{Y}^{*}}}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\|u\|_{\mathrm{Y}}-\|u_{*}\|_{\mathrm{Y}}}}\right)>0\ \text{ for }\ \|u\|_{\mathrm{Y}}=R.

Now, let
u_{R}\in\mathbb{K}_{R}
be a solution of (
2.10
). Then

\displaystyle\langle(T(u_{R})-f),u_{R}-u_{*}\rangle=-\langle(T(u_{R})-f),u_{*}-u_{R}\rangle\leq 0,

and in view of (
2.1
), we have
\|u_{R}\|_{\mathrm{Y}}\neq R
. Or in other words,
\|u_{R}\|_{\mathrm{Y}}<R
. Combining the above facts and applying
[
25
, Theorem 1.7, Chapter III]
, we deduce
the existence of a
u_{0}\in\mathbb{K}
such that

(2.12)

\displaystyle\langle T(u_{0})-f,w-u_{0}\rangle\geq 0\ \text{ for all }\ w\in\mathbb{K}.

In particular, for
w=0
, we have
\langle T(u_{0})-f,u_{0}\rangle\leq 0
, which by (
2.9
) implies that

u_{0}\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{u\in\mathrm{X}:\|u\|_{\mathrm{Y}}<R}}\right\}.

Consequently taking
w=u_{0}+\lambda v
in (
2.12
) with
\|v\|_{\mathrm{Y}}=1
and
0<|\lambda|\leq R-\|u_{0}\|_{\mathrm{Y}}
, we obtain

(2.13)

\displaystyle\langle T(u_{0})-f,v\rangle=0\ \text{ for all }\ v\in\{u\in\mathrm{X}:\|u\|_{\mathrm{Y}}=1\}.

Since the embedding
\mathrm{X}\subset\mathrm{Y}
is dense, for any
v\in\mathrm{Y}
with
\|v\|_{\mathrm{Y}}=1
, we can find a sequence
\{v_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\mathrm{X}
with
\|v_{n}\|_{\mathrm{Y}}=1
such that
\|v_{n}-v\|_{\mathrm{Y}}\to 0
as
n\to\infty
(Lemma
2.1
). Therefore, from (
2.13
), we infer

\displaystyle\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}\langle T(u_{0})-f,v_{n}\rangle=0\Rightarrow\langle T(u_{0})-f,v\rangle=0\ \text{ for all }\ v\in\{u\in\mathrm{Y}:\|u\|_{\mathrm{Y}}=1\},

and hence
T(u_{0})=f\in\mathrm{Y}^{*}.

∎

2.2.
Existence and uniqueness

We use Theorem

to show the existence and uniqueness of solutions for the problem (

)-(

) with the controls (

) and (

) for

and

, respectively.
We provide a cut-off function for the function

in order to obtain monotonicity of the nonlinear map

defined in (

Let us define the modified nonlinearity

by

\displaystyle f_{\rho}(y):=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}f(y)&\ \text{ if }\ |y|\leq\rho,\\

for

Let us define the nonlinear map

\displaystyle\leq C\big[\nu(\|v_{x}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}}+|g_{1}(v(1))|)+\mu(\|v_{xx}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}}+|g_{2}(v(1))|)+\alpha(\|f_{\rho}(v)\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}}+|f_{\rho}(v(1))|)

where we have used the continuous Sobolev embedding
\mathrm{H}^{1}(0,1)\hookrightarrow\mathrm{C}([0,1])\hookrightarrow\mathrm{L}^{p}(0,1)
for all

2.2

Remark 2.4

Note that if one takes

\displaystyle\quad-\frac{\beta}{4}(1-\gamma)^{2}\|v\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle\geq\nu\|v_{x}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}}^{2}+\frac{\eta}{2}v^{2}(1)+\frac{\alpha^{2}}{2\eta(\delta+2)^{2}}v^{2(\delta+1)}(1)+\frac{\mu}{2}(g_{1}(v(1)))^{2}-\frac{\beta}{4}(1-\gamma)^{2}\|v\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle\geq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\nu-\frac{\beta}{4}(1-\gamma)^{2}}}\right)\|v\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}}^{2}\geq 0,

provided

.
For

\displaystyle\geq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\nu-\frac{\beta}{4}(1-\gamma)^{2}}}\right)\|v\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}}^{2}\geq 0,

provided
\nu\geq\frac{\beta}{4}(1-\gamma)^{2}
.
As

, the same results hold true for

The following result shows that the operator
\mathscr{B}_{\rho,\omega}:=\mathscr{A}_{\rho}+\omega\mathrm{I}:\mathrm{X}\subset\mathrm{Y}\to\mathrm{Y}^{*}
is monotone, hemicontinuous and coercive for sufficiently large

Lemma 2.5
.

Let
g_{1},g_{2}\in\mathrm{C}(\mathbb{R})
be non-decreasing functions,
f\in\mathrm{C}^{1}(\mathbb{R})
,
\nu,\mu,\alpha,\beta,\rho>0
,
\delta\in\{1,2\}
,
\gamma\in(0,1)
be constants, and
\mathscr{A}_{\rho}
be defined by (
2.2
). Then for all
\omega\geq\omega_{\rho}
, with
\omega_{\rho}
defined by

(2.21)

\displaystyle\omega_{\rho}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\alpha L_{\rho}}{\nu}+2^{2\delta-1}\beta(1+\gamma)^{2}(\delta+1)^{2}}}\right),

the nonlinear map
\mathscr{B}_{\rho,\omega}:=\mathscr{A}_{\rho}+\omega\mathrm{I}:\mathrm{X}\to\mathrm{Y}^{*}
is
monotone
in the sense that

(2.22)

\displaystyle\langle\mathscr{B}_{\rho,\omega}(v)-\mathscr{B}_{\rho,\omega}(w),v-w\rangle\geq 0\ \text{ for all }\ v,w\in\mathrm{X}.

Moreover,
\mathscr{B}_{\rho,\omega}
is
hemicontinuous
in the sense that for all
v_{1},v_{2}\in\mathrm{X}
and
w\in\mathrm{Y}

(2.23)

\displaystyle\lim\limits_{\lambda\to 0}\langle\mathscr{B}_{\rho,\omega}(v_{1}+\lambda v_{2}),w\rangle=\langle\mathscr{B}_{\rho,\omega}(v_{1}),w\rangle.

Finally,
\mathscr{B}_{\rho,\omega}
is
coercive
in the sense that

(2.24)

\displaystyle\lim\limits_{\|v\|_{\mathrm{Y}\to\infty}}\frac{\langle\mathscr{B}_{\rho,\omega}(v),v\rangle}{\|v\|_{\mathrm{Y}}}=\infty,

for all
v\in\mathrm{X}
.

Proof.

The proof is divided into the following steps:

Step 1:

Monotonicity.

Let us first prove the monotoniciy property. For all
v,w\in\mathrm{X}
, since
f_{\rho}
satisfies a monotone condition (see (
2.29
) below),
g_{1}
is non-decreasing and (
1.2

\displaystyle\langle\mathscr{A}_{\rho}(v)-\mathscr{A}_{\rho}(w),v-w\rangle

\displaystyle=\nu\|v_{x}-w_{x}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}}^{2}-\frac{\mu}{2}(g_{1}(v(1))-g_{1}(w(1)))^{2}+\frac{\mu}{2}(v_{x}(0)-w_{x}(0))^{2}-\alpha(f_{\rho}(v)-f_{\rho}(w),v_{x}-w_{x})

\displaystyle\quad-\beta(1+\gamma)(v^{\delta+1}-w^{\delta+1},v-w)+\beta\gamma\|v-w\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}}^{2}+\beta(v^{2\delta+1}-w^{2\delta+1},v-w)

\displaystyle\quad+\nu(g_{1}(v(1))-g_{1}(w(1)))(v(1)-w(1))+\mu(g_{2}(v(1))-g_{2}(w(1)))(v(1)-w(1))

\displaystyle\quad+\alpha(f_{\rho}(v(1))-f_{\rho}(w(1)))(v(1)-w(1))

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\nu}{2}\|v_{x}-w_{x}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}}^{2}-\frac{\alpha L_{\rho}}{\nu}\|v-w\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}}^{2}+\beta\gamma\|v-w\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}}^{2}

(2.25)

\displaystyle\quad-\beta(1+\gamma)(v^{\delta+1}-w^{\delta+1},v-w)+\beta(v^{2\delta+1}-w^{2\delta+1},v-w).

The estimate of the convective term can be justified in the following way: Without loss of generality, we may assume that
|u|\leq|v|
for all
u,v\in\mathrm{Y}
. Then for
|u|,|v|\leq\rho,
we have

\displaystyle|\langle f_{\rho,x}(u)-f_{\rho,x}(v),u-v\rangle|

\displaystyle=|(f_{\rho}(u(1))-f_{\rho}(v(1)))(u(1)-v(1))-\langle f_{\rho}(u)-f_{\rho}(v),u_{x}-v_{x}\rangle|

\displaystyle=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\frac{1}{\delta+1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(u^{\delta+1}(1)-v^{\delta+1}(1)}}\right)(u(1)-v(1))-\frac{1}{\delta+1}\langle u^{\delta+1}-v^{\delta+1},u_{x}-v_{x}\rangle}}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\delta+1}\|u^{\delta+1}-v^{\delta+1}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{\infty}}\|u-v\|_{\mathrm{L}^{\infty}}+\|(\theta u+(1-\theta)v)^{\delta}(u-v)\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}}\|u_{x}-v_{x}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\|(\theta u+(1-\theta)v)^{\delta}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{\infty}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\|u-v\|_{\mathrm{L}^{\infty}}^{2}+\|u-v\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}}\|u_{x}-v_{x}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}}}}\right]

where we have used Hölder’s, Agmon’s and Young’s inequalities. For
|u|,|v|>\rho,
we have

\displaystyle|\langle f_{\rho,x}(u)-f_{\rho,x}(v),u-v\rangle|

\displaystyle=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\rho}{|u(1)|}}}\right)^{\delta+1}f(u(1))-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\rho}{|v(1)|}}}\right)^{\delta+1}f(v(1))}}\right](u(1)-v(1))}}\right.

\displaystyle\quad\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left.-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left<\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\rho}{\|u\|_{\mathrm{L}^{\infty}}}}}\right)^{\delta+1}f(u)-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\rho}{\|v\|_{\mathrm{L}^{\infty}}}}}\right)^{\delta+1}f(v),u_{x}-v_{x}}}\right>}}\right|

\displaystyle=\Bigg|\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\rho}{|u(1)|}}}\right)^{\delta+1}\frac{1}{\delta+1}(u^{\delta+1}(1)-v^{\delta+1}(1))(u(1)-v(1))

\displaystyle\quad+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\rho}{|u(1)|}}}\right)^{\delta+1}-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\rho}{|v(1)|}}}\right)^{\delta+1}}}\right]\frac{1}{\delta+1}v^{\delta+1}(1)

\displaystyle\quad-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\rho}{\|u\|_{\mathrm{L}^{\infty}}}}}\right)^{\delta+1}\frac{1}{\delta+1}\langle u^{\delta+1}-v^{\delta+1},v_{x}-u_{x}\rangle

\displaystyle\quad-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\rho}{\|u\|_{\mathrm{L}^{\infty}}}}}\right)^{\delta+1}-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\rho}{\|v\|_{\mathrm{L}^{\infty}}}}}\right)^{\delta+1}}}\right]\frac{1}{\delta+1}\langle v^{\delta+1},u_{x}-v_{x}\rangle\Bigg|

\displaystyle=\Bigg|\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\rho}{|u(1)|}}}\right)^{\delta+1}(\theta u(1)+(1-\theta)v(1))^{\delta}(u(1)-v(1))^{2}

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{\rho^{\delta+1}}{|u(1)|^{\delta+1}|v(1)|^{\delta+1}}(\theta|v(1)|+(1-\theta)|u(1)|)^{\delta}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(|v(1)|-|u(1)|}}\right)(u(1)-v(1))

\displaystyle\quad-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\rho}{\|u\|_{\mathrm{L}^{\infty}}}}}\right)^{\delta+1}\langle(\theta u+(1-\theta v))^{\delta}(u-v),u_{x}-v_{x}\rangle

\displaystyle\quad-\frac{\rho^{\delta+1}}{\|u\|_{\mathrm{L}^{\infty}}^{\delta+1}\|v\|_{\mathrm{L}^{\infty}}^{\delta+1}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\theta\|v\|_{\mathrm{L}^{\infty}}+(1-\theta)\|u\|_{\mathrm{L}^{\infty}}}}\right)^{\delta}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\|v\|_{\mathrm{L}^{\infty}}-\|u\|_{\mathrm{L}^{\infty}}}}\right)\langle v^{\delta+1},u_{x}-v_{x}\rangle\Bigg|

\displaystyle\leq 2^{2\delta}\rho^{\delta}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\|u-v\|_{\mathrm{L}^{\infty}}^{2}+\|u-v\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}}\|u_{x}-v_{x}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}}+\|u-v\|_{\mathrm{L}^{\infty}}\|u_{x}-v_{x}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}}}}\right]

(2.27)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\nu}{2}\|u_{x}-v_{x}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}}^{2}+\frac{L_{\rho}}{\nu}\|u-v\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}}^{2},

where we have used Hölder’s, Agmon’s, Sobolev’s and Young’s inequalities.
Finally, for
|u|\leq\rho
and
|v|>\rho
, we deduce

\displaystyle|\langle f_{\rho,x}(u)-f_{\rho,x}(v),u-v\rangle|

\displaystyle=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[f(u(1))-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\rho}{|v(1)|}}}\right)^{\delta+1}f(v(1))}}\right](u(1)-v(1))-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left<f(u)-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\rho}{\|v\|_{\mathrm{L}^{\infty}}}}}\right)^{\delta+1}f(v),u_{x}-v_{x}}}\right>}}\right|

\displaystyle=\Bigg|\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[1-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\rho}{|v(1)|}}}\right)^{\delta+1}}}\right]f(u(1))(u(1)-v(1))+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\rho}{|v(1)|}}}\right)^{\delta+1}(f(u(1))-f(v(1)))(u(1)-v(1))

\displaystyle\quad-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[1-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\rho}{\|v\|_{\mathrm{L}^{\infty}}}}}\right)^{\delta+1}}}\right]\langle f(u),u_{x}-v_{x}\rangle-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\rho}{\|v\|_{\mathrm{L}^{\infty}}}}}\right)^{\delta+1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left<(f(u)-f(v)),u_{x}-v_{x}}}\right>\Bigg|

\displaystyle\leq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\rho}{|u(1)|}}}\right)^{\delta+1}-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\rho}{|v(1)|}}}\right)^{\delta+1}}}\right]|f(u(1))||u(1)-v(1)|

\displaystyle\quad+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\rho}{|v(1)|}}}\right)^{\delta+1}|(f(u(1))-f(v(1)))||u(1)-v(1)|

\displaystyle\quad+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\rho}{\|u\|_{\mathrm{L}^{\infty}}}}}\right)^{\delta+1}-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\rho}{\|v\|_{\mathrm{L}^{\infty}}}}}\right)^{\delta+1}}}\right]\|f(u)\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}}\|u_{x}-v_{x}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}}

\displaystyle\quad+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\rho}{\|v\|_{\mathrm{L}^{\infty}}}}}\right)^{\delta+1}\|f(u)-f(v)\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}}\|u_{x}-v_{x}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}}

Appendix A
Some Useful Inequalities

Lemma A.1

(Poincaré’s inequality)
.

For any
w\in\mathrm{C}^{1}([0,1])
, we have

(A.1)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}w^{2}(x)dx\leq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\int_{0}^{1}w_{x}^{2}(x)dx}}\right).

Proof.

For any
w\in\mathrm{C}^{1}[0,1]
, we know that

\displaystyle w(x)=\int_{0}^{x}w_{x}(\zeta)d\zeta,

so that

\displaystyle w^{2}(x)=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\int_{0}^{x}w_{x}(\zeta)d\zeta}}\right)^{2}\leq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\int_{0}^{1}|w_{x}(\zeta)|^{2}d\zeta}}\right).

Ingratiating the above inequality from
0
to
1
, we have the Poincaré inequality (
A.1
). By a density argument, the result (
A.1
) holds true for all
w\in\mathrm{H}^{1}(0,1)
.
∎

Lemma A.2

(Agmon’s inequality)
.

For any
w\in\mathrm{C}^{1}([0,1])
, the following inequality holds:

(A.2)

\displaystyle\max_{x\in[0,1]}|w(x)|\leq\sqrt{2}\|w\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}}^{1/2}\|w_{x}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}}^{1/2}.

Proof.

An application of Hölder’s inequality yields

(A.3)

\displaystyle w^{2}(x)

\displaystyle=2\int_{0}^{x}w(\zeta)w_{x}(\zeta)d\zeta\leq 2\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\int_{0}^{1}|w(\zeta)|^{2}d\zeta}}\right)^{1/2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\int_{0}^{1}|w_{x}(\zeta)|^{2}d\zeta}}\right)^{1/2},

for all
x\in[0,1]
.
Therefore, from (
A.3
), we infer

(A.4)

\displaystyle\max\limits_{x\in[0,1]}|w(x)|^{2}\leq 2\|w\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}}\|w_{x}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}},

and (
A.2
) follows. By a density argument, the result (
A.2
) holds true for all
w\in\mathrm{H}^{1}(0,1)
.
∎

Lemma A.3
.

For any
w\in\mathrm{C}^{1}([0,1])
, the following inequality holds:

(A.5)

\displaystyle\max_{x\in[0,1]}|w(x)|\leq(\delta+2)^{\frac{1}{\delta+2}}\|w\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2(\delta+1)}}^{\frac{\delta+1}{\delta+2}}\|w_{x}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}}^{\frac{1}{\delta+2}}.

Proof.

An application of Hölder’s inequality yields

\displaystyle w^{\delta+2}(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{x}\partial_{\zeta}w^{\delta+2}(\zeta)d\zeta=(\delta+2)\int_{0}^{x}w^{\delta+1}(\zeta)w_{x}(\zeta)d\zeta

(A.6)

\displaystyle\leq(\delta+2)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\int_{0}^{1}|w(\zeta)|^{2(\delta+1)}d\zeta}}\right)^{1/2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\int_{0}^{1}|w_{x}(\zeta)|^{2}d\zeta}}\right)^{1/2},

for all
x\in[0,1]
.
Therefore, from (
A
), we infer

(A.7)

\displaystyle\max\limits_{x\in[0,1]}|w(x)|^{\delta+2}\leq(\delta+2)\|w\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2(\delta+1)}}^{\delta+1}\|w_{x}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}},

and (
A.5
) follows. By a density argument, the result (
A.5
) holds true for all
w\in\mathrm{H}^{1}(0,1)
.
∎
