Localization by particle-hole symmetry breaking: a loop expansion
K. Ziegler

Abstract
Localization by a broken particle-hole symmetry in a random system of non-interacting quantum particles is studied on a d d –dimensional lattice. Our approach is based on a chiral symmetry argument and the corresponding invariant measure, where the latter is described by a Grassmann functional integral. Within a loop expansion we find for small loops diffusion in the case of particle-hole symmetry. Breaking the particle-hole symmetry results in the creation of random dimers, which suppress diffusion and lead to localization on the scale D / | μ | \sqrt{D/|\mu|} , where D D is the effective diffusion coefficient at particle-hole symmetry and μ \mu is the parameter related to particle-hole symmetry breaking.



I
Introduction

There is strong evidence that disordered systems with particle-hole (PH) symmetry can avoid Anderson localization in
any spatial dimension. Such a behavior was observed for one-dimensional systems
at the band center of a one-dimensional tight-binding model with random hopping
some time ago
Dyson 1953
;
Theodorou and Cohen 1976
;
Eggarter and Riedinger 1978
.
There is also numerical evidence for extended states at the band center in disordered two-dimensional lattice
models
Soukoulis et al. 1982
;
Inui et al. 1994
. A renewed interest in this problem appeared with the discovery
of two-dimensional Dirac-like materials, such as
graphene
Novoselov et al. 2005
;
Castro Neto et al. 2009
;
Abergel et al. 2010
.
Graphene is a semimetal with a very robust conductivity at the PH-symmetric Dirac point. On the other hand,
after breaking the PH symmetry by doping, its conductivity changes substantially; it is either enhanced for weak
disorder or reduced for strong
disorder
Chen et al. 2008
;
Elias et al. 2009
;
Chen et al. 2009
;
Bostwick et al. 2009
.
The effect of disorder on topological materials, based on Dirac-like models, has also been the subject of some recent
theoretical research
Wilson et al. 2017
;
Haim et al. 2019
;
Pixley and Wilson 2021
.
Despite of a substantial effort, there was
no conclusive confirmation of localization away from the PH-symmetric Dirac point, neither from the
theory side
Das Sarma et al. 2011
;
Peres et al. 2006
nor from the
experiment
Chen et al. 2008
;
Elias et al. 2009
;
Chen et al. 2009
;
Bostwick et al. 2009
.

While in the PH-symmetric case diffusion was identified as the dominant behavior of non-interacting Dirac particles
in a random environment, the breaking of the PH symmetry was accompanied with the creation of random
dimers
Ziegler 2013
. A preliminary work, based on a perturbative renormalization group analysis, did not
reveal localization, though
Sinner and Ziegler 2012
.
In the following we will analyze the competition of diffusing quantum particles and randomly distributed dimers on
a
d
–dimensional lattice without employing a perturbation theory.

Starting from a PH-symmetric random Hamiltonian
H
, a symmetry-breaking term is defined by a uniform shift
\mu

of the PH-symmetric Hamiltonian. The resulting Hamiltonian is still invariant under a chiral transformation.
This invariance is relevant for the analysis of spontaneous chiral-symmetry breaking and the corresponding long-scale
behavior of the model, such that we reduce our description to the corresponding invariant measure (IM).
Then the IM, written in terms of a Grassmann functional integral
Negele and Orland 2019
, is
represented by a loop expansion, which consists of graphs with 4-vertices. Only the smallest loops are
taken into account, which is known as the nonlinear sigma model approximation. This is used as the starting
point for the analysis of the spatial correlations. As mentioned above, there is diffusion in the presence of PH symmetry
with a diffusion coefficient
D
, which depends on the scattering rate
\eta
and the average Hamiltonian

\langle H\rangle
. After breaking the PH symmetry with
\mu\neq 0
, we create small loops with two sites on the
lattice which represent repulsive lattice dimers. They act as obstacles for the diffusion and suppress the latter on large
scales, which leads to an exponential decay of the particle correlation. Since the density of the dimers is proportional
to
|\mu|
, this localization effect increases with this parameter. An estimation of the decay length gives

\sqrt{D/|\mu|}
.

II
Model: Invariant measure

We briefly recapitulate the main ideas which were developed in the previous work on the loop expansion
and the IM for non-interacting particles in a random environment
Ziegler 2009
;
Ziegler 2015
.
For this purpose a random Hamiltonian matrix
H
on a lattice
\Lambda
is considered. It is assumed that
this Hamiltonian has
an internal spinor structure; its matrix elements are of the form
H_{{\bf r}j,{\bf r}^{\prime}j^{\prime}}
, where
{\bf r},{\bf r}^{\prime}\in\Lambda
and

j,j^{\prime}=1,2,...,N
is a spinor index, where the latter can also be a band index of a multiband Hamiltonian.
We further assume that there is an
N\times N
unitary matrix
U
that (i) acts only on the spinor or band index and
(ii) for which the Hamiltonian matrix obeys the relation

UH^{T}U^{\dagger}=-H\ .

(1)

This relation implies a particle-hole transformation in the following sense: Since
H
is Hermitian, we have

H^{T}=H^{*}
and the relation (
1
) implies for the eigenstate
\Psi_{E}
of
H
with the real eigenvalue
E

H^{*}U^{\dagger}\Psi_{E}=-U^{\dagger}H\Psi_{E}=-EU^{\dagger}\Psi_{E}\ .

Complex conjugation of this equation yields

H(U^{\dagger}\Psi_{E})^{*}=-E(U^{\dagger}\Psi_{E})^{*}\ ,

such that
\Psi_{-E}=(U^{\dagger}\Psi_{E})^{*}
is eigenstate of
H
with eigenvalue
-E
. Thus, there is a PH symmetry
for
E=0
. The PH symmetry
of
H
is broken by
\mu
for
H_{\pm}:=H\pm\mu\sigma_{0}
, where
\sigma_{0}
is the
N\times N
unit matrix.
In the following we will analyze the effect of a shift of
E=0
by
\mu
, using the nonlinear sigma model approach.

Following Refs.
Sinner and Ziegler 2012
;
Ziegler 2013
, we extend
H
to the random Hamiltonian matrix

{\hat{H}}=\pmatrix{H_{+}&0&0&0\cr 0&H_{-}&0&0\cr 0&0&H_{+}^{T}&0\cr 0&0&0&H_{-}^{T}\cr}\equiv\pmatrix{{\bar{H}}&0\cr 0&{\bar{H}}^{T}\cr}\ .

(2)

At the PH symmetric point (
\mu=0
) it satisfies the relation
{\hat{H}}{\hat{S}}_{0}=-{\hat{S}}_{0}{\hat{H}}
for

{\hat{S}}_{0}=\pmatrix{0&0&\varphi_{3}U&\varphi_{1}U\cr 0&0&\varphi_{2}U&\varphi_{4}U\cr\varphi^{\prime}_{3}U^{\dagger}&\varphi^{\prime}_{2}U^{\dagger}&0&0\cr\varphi^{\prime}_{1}U^{\dagger}&\varphi^{\prime}_{4}U^{\dagger}&0&0\cr}

(3)

with some general parameters
\varphi_{j}
.
For a broken PH symmetry (
\mu\neq 0
) it satisfies
{\hat{H}}{\hat{S}}=-{\hat{S}}{\hat{H}}
for

{\hat{S}}=\pmatrix{0&0&0&\varphi_{1}U\cr 0&0&\varphi_{2}U&0\cr 0&\varphi^{\prime}_{2}U^{\dagger}&0&0\cr\varphi^{\prime}_{1}U^{\dagger}&0&0&0\cr}\ .

(4)

The PH-symmetric case was studied previously, such that we can focus subsequently on the broken PH symmetry.
Then the relation
{\hat{H}}{\hat{S}}=-{\hat{S}}{\hat{H}}

implies for
{\hat{H}}
the chiral symmetry
e^{\hat{S}}{\hat{H}}e^{\hat{S}}={\hat{H}}
of the extended Hamiltonian
defined in Eq. (
2
).
The chiral symmetry reveals some interesting properties, which will be discussed next.

Some general remarks on the IM: The goal is to calculate average quantities, such as the average Green’s function or
the average product of two Green’s functions, with respect to the random matrix elements of the Hamiltonian
{\hat{H}}

Ziegler 2009
. This should be seen as an alternative to studies, where the distribution of the random
Hamiltonian and its spectrum is
considered
Liaw 2013
;
Kostadinova et al. 2018
;
Kostadinova et al. 2020
.
Average quantities are sufficient to discuss many physically motivated questions, such as transport
Ziegler 2013
;
Ziegler 2015
.
Although simpler than the analysis of the random distribution, the averaging with respect to the random Hamiltonian
is a tedious task for a large lattice
\Lambda
. A common and often successful approximation
to this problem is to perform a saddle-point integration (also known as the method of steepest
descent)
Arfken 1985
.
Then another problem occurs when there is no unique saddle-point
solution but a manifold of saddle points due to some symmetry of the Hamiltonian. A typical example is the Hamiltonian
(
2
) with the chiral symmetry. Since all the saddle points of the manifold are equally important, we must
integrate over all of them. The resulting saddle-point integral leads to the IM. This will be discussed in detail for the specific
example of
{\hat{H}}
subsequently. The saddle-point integral can be performed in this case and leads to a sum that is
characterized by loops with increasing size. There is no problem with convergence on a finite lattice, since the loops are
strictly repulsive and the size of the loops is restricted by the size of the lattice.
The loop expansion was previously developed for the PH-symmetric case in Refs.
Ziegler 2009
;
Ziegler 2015

and will be adopted to the PH-symmetry broken case in the following.

First, we construct the IM that is associated with the chiral symmetry.
For the matrix
{\hat{S}}
and the graded determinant (cf. Eq. (
34
)) we get

detg(e^{2{\hat{S}}})=\exp[Trg(\log e^{2{\hat{S}}})]=\exp[2Trg({\hat{S}})]=1\ ,

since the graded trace vanishes:
Trg({\hat{S}})=0
.
Together with the chiral symmetry of
{\hat{H}}
this implies immediately

detg({\hat{H}}+i\eta)=detg({\hat{H}}+i\eta)detg(e^{2{\hat{S}}})=detg(e^{\hat{S}}{\hat{H}}e^{\hat{S}}+i\eta e^{2{\hat{S}}})

=detg({\hat{H}}+i\eta e^{2{\hat{S}}})\ ,

(5)

where
detg({\hat{H}}+i\eta)=1
, according to the definition of the graded determinant.
Thus,
detg({\hat{H}}+i\eta e^{2{\hat{S}}})
is invariant under the chiral transformation.

From relation (
II
) we can construct the IM through substituting the general parameters

\varphi_{j}
by a spatial Grassmann field
\varphi_{{\bf r}j}
. As explained in App.
A
, this leads to the
lattice version of the IM with

J_{j}=\det({\bf 1}+\varphi^{\prime}_{j}\varphi_{j}-\varphi^{\prime}_{j}h\varphi_{j}h^{\dagger})^{-1}=\det({\bf 1}+\varphi_{j}\varphi^{\prime}_{j}-h\varphi_{j}h^{\dagger}\varphi^{\prime}_{j})\ \ \ (j=1,2),

(6)

where the relation is derived in (
38
), with

h={\bf 1}+2i\eta{\bar{G}}_{0}\ ,\ \ \ {\bar{G}}_{0}=({\bar{H}}_{0}+i\epsilon-i\eta)^{-1}\ .

(7)

J_{j}
is an invariant for a
global
chiral transformation, provided that
h
is unitary. This is the case for
\epsilon=0
:

hh^{\dagger}={\bf 1}+O(\epsilon)

Ziegler 2015
.
It is important to realize that the random Hamiltonian
{\hat{H}}
has been replaced by its average

{\bar{H}}_{0}:=\langle{\bar{H}}\rangle
in the IM.
In Refs.
Ziegler 2009
;
Ziegler 2015
this IM was associated with the correlator
K_{{\bf R}{\bf R}^{\prime}}
between the lattice
sites
{\bf R}
and
{\bf R}^{\prime}
through the relation

K_{{\bf R}{\bf R}^{\prime}}=\frac{1}{\cal N}\int_{\Lambda}J_{j}\varphi_{{\bf R}j}\varphi^{\prime}_{{\bf R}^{\prime}j}\ ,\ \ \ {\cal N}=\int_{\Lambda}J_{1}=\int_{\Lambda}J_{2}

(8)

of a two-component Grassmann field
\varphi_{{\bf r}j}
(
{\bf r}\in\Lambda
).
\int_{\Lambda}
is the functional integral
with respect to the Grassmann field on the lattice
\Lambda
.
This correlator indicates localization on the localization length
\xi
when it decays exponentially on the scale
\xi
.
It is identical for both components of the Grassmann field
j=1,2
, such that we can drop this index subsequently.
Another important feature of the Green’s function
h
is that the relation (
1
) implies for the PH-symmetric case

\mu=0
the relation

Uh^{\dagger}U^{\dagger}=h\ ,

(9)

which does not hold for
\mu\neq 0
.

The Grassmann field can be expressed by its Fourier components as

\varphi_{\bf r}=\sum_{\bf k}e^{-i{\bf k}\cdot{\bf r}}{\tilde{\varphi}}_{\bf k}={\tilde{\varphi}}_{0}+\sum_{{\bf k}\neq 0}e^{-i{\bf k}\cdot{\bf r}}{\tilde{\varphi}}_{\bf k}\equiv{\tilde{\varphi}}_{0}+\tau_{{\bf r}}\ ,

(10)

where the zero mode
{\tilde{\varphi}}_{0}
does not depend on
{\bf r}
. Then the normalization becomes

{\cal N}=\int_{\Lambda}\det\{{\bf 1}+[{\tilde{\varphi}}_{0}+\tau-h({\tilde{\varphi}}_{0}+\tau)h^{\dagger}]({\tilde{\varphi}}_{0}^{\prime}+\tau^{\prime})\}

and with
hh^{\dagger}={\bf 1}-\epsilon\bar{\Gamma}+O(\epsilon^{2})
we get

{\tilde{\varphi}}_{0}-h{\tilde{\varphi}}_{0}h^{\dagger}={\tilde{\varphi}}_{0}({\bf 1}-hh^{\dagger})=\epsilon\bar{\Gamma}{\tilde{\varphi}}_{0}+O(\epsilon^{2})\ ,

since the space-independent
{\tilde{\varphi}}_{0}
commutes with
h
.

\epsilon
can be absorbed into the Grassmann integration by rescaling
{\tilde{\varphi}}_{0}\to{\bar{\tau}}/\epsilon
.
This implies for the normalization

{\cal N}=\epsilon\int_{\Lambda}\det[{\bf 1}+(\bar{\Gamma}{\bar{\tau}}+\tau-h\tau h^{\dagger})({\tilde{\varphi}}_{0}^{\prime}+\tau^{\prime})]\ ,

(11)

where we have neglected terms of order
\epsilon
inside the integrand.
For the unnormalized correlator this rescaling argument for
{\tilde{\varphi}}_{0}
provides a constant term

{\tilde{\varphi}}_{0}{\tilde{\varphi}}^{\prime}_{0}
from the external Grassmann variable
\varphi_{\bf R}\varphi^{\prime}_{{\bf R}^{\prime}}
:

\int_{\Lambda}J\varphi_{{\bf R}}\varphi^{\prime}_{{\bf R}^{\prime}}=\int_{\Lambda}J({\tilde{\varphi}}_{0}+\tau_{{\bf R}})({\tilde{\varphi}}^{\prime}_{0}+\tau^{\prime}_{{\bf R}^{\prime}})

=\int_{\Lambda}J{\bar{\tau}}({\tilde{\varphi}}^{\prime}_{0}+\tau^{\prime}_{{\bf R}^{\prime}})+\epsilon\int_{\Lambda}J\tau_{{\bf R}}({\tilde{\varphi}}^{\prime}_{0}+\tau^{\prime}_{{\bf R}^{\prime}})\ ,

(12)

where only the first term on the right-hand side vanishes with
\epsilon\to 0
. This implies for the normalized
correlator that the second term diverges for
\epsilon\to 0
, which is a consequence of the broken
translational invariance due to the factor
\tau_{\bf R}
.

III
Loop expansion

The IM of Eq. (
6
) can be rewritten as

J=\exp\{Tr([WW^{\dagger}]_{d}\varphi\varphi^{\prime})\}\det({\bf 1}-\varphi X^{\dagger}\varphi^{\prime}-X\varphi\varphi^{\prime}-W\varphi W^{\dagger}\varphi^{\prime})

(13)

when we define
h=[h]_{d}+W
,
X=W[h]_{d}^{\dagger}
, where
[h]_{d}
and
[WW^{\dagger}]_{d}
are the spatial
diagonal parts of
h
and
WW^{\dagger}
, respectively. Here we implicitly assume the limit
\epsilon\to 0
.
Using the determinant identity
\det(A)=\exp[Tr(\log A)]
this yields for the IM after expanding the logarithm

\log J=Tr([WW^{\dagger}]_{d}\varphi\varphi^{\prime})-\sum_{l\geq 1}\frac{1}{l}Tr[(\varphi X^{\dagger}\varphi^{\prime}+X\varphi\varphi^{\prime}+W\varphi W^{\dagger}\varphi^{\prime})^{l}]\ .

(14)

This sum terminates on a finite lattice
\Lambda
for
l=|\Lambda|
due to the Grassmann field. The trace term with
the power
l
represents a sum of loops of length
l
on the lattice, such that the sum can be considered as a loop
expansion of the IM
Ziegler 2009
.
Integration with respect to the Grassmann field yields graphs with 4-vertices from the term

W\varphi W^{\dagger}\varphi^{\prime}
because the non-zero Grassmann integral requires at each site
{\bf r}
the product

\varphi_{\bf r}\varphi^{\prime}_{\bf r}
. The 4-vertex graphs reflect the equivalence of the expression (
14
)
with the random phase representation of the IM
Ziegler 2015
.

III.1
Nonlinear sigma model

As a special case of the loop expansion (
14
) only the smallest loops are considered, namely
only loops that contain at most two hopping matrices, either
X
or
W
.
This approximation is known as the nonlinear sigma model
Gell-Mann and Lévy 1960
and becomes in the present case

\log J_{NLSM}=

Tr([WW^{\dagger}]_{d}\varphi\varphi^{\prime})-Tr(W\varphi W^{\dagger}\varphi^{\prime})-Tr(\varphi X^{\dagger}\varphi^{\prime}+X\varphi\varphi^{\prime})

-Tr[(\varphi X^{\dagger}\varphi^{\prime}+X\varphi\varphi^{\prime})(\varphi X^{\dagger}\varphi^{\prime}+X\varphi\varphi^{\prime})]\ ,

where the third term vanishes due to the trace of an off-diagonal matrix
X
, such that the IM reduces to

\log J_{NLSM}=Tr([WW^{\dagger}]_{d}\varphi\varphi^{\prime})-Tr(W\varphi W^{\dagger}\varphi^{\prime})

+Tr(X^{\dagger}\varphi\varphi^{\prime}X^{\dagger}\varphi\varphi^{\prime}-X\varphi\varphi^{\prime}X\varphi\varphi^{\prime})\ .

(15)

The first two terms represent a diffusion propagator

Tr_{N}([WW^{\dagger}]_{{\bf r}{\bf r}})\delta_{{\bf r}{\bf r}^{\prime}}-Tr_{N}(W_{{\bf r}{\bf r}^{\prime}}W^{\dagger}_{{\bf r}^{\prime}{\bf r}})

=\sum_{{\bf r}^{\prime\prime}}Tr_{N}(W_{{\bf r}{\bf r}^{\prime\prime}}W^{\dagger}_{{\bf r}^{\prime\prime}{\bf r}})\delta_{{\bf r}{\bf r}^{\prime}}-Tr_{N}(W_{{\bf r}{\bf r}^{\prime}}W^{\dagger}_{{\bf r}^{\prime}{\bf r}})\ ,

(16)

where
Tr_{N}
is the trace with respect to the spinor index.
The quartic term reads

Tr(X^{\dagger}\varphi\varphi^{\prime}X^{\dagger}\varphi\varphi^{\prime}-X\varphi\varphi^{\prime}X\varphi\varphi^{\prime})=\sum_{{\bf r},{\bf r}^{\prime}}\beta_{{\bf r}{\bf r}^{\prime}}\varphi_{{\bf r}}\varphi^{\prime}_{{\bf r}}\varphi_{{\bf r}^{\prime}}\varphi^{\prime}_{{\bf r}^{\prime}}\ .

\beta
is an imaginary matrix due to

\beta_{{\bf r}{\bf r}^{\prime}}:=Tr_{N}(X^{\dagger}_{{\bf r}{\bf r}^{\prime}}X^{\dagger}_{{\bf r}^{\prime}{\bf r}}-X_{{\bf r}{\bf r}^{\prime}}X_{{\bf r}^{\prime}{\bf r}})\ .

(17)

Relation (
9
) implies that
\beta
vanishes in the presence of the PH symmetry
(i.e., for
\mu=0
). This was also observed in Refs.
Sinner and Ziegler 2012
;
Ziegler 2013
.

Next, we represent the functional integral with a quadratic form in the Grassmann field, using a coupling of the latter
to a real Gaussian field
Q_{\bf r}
. This can be achieved by exploiting the relation

m_{\bf r}{\bar{\beta}}_{{\bf r}{\bf r}^{\prime}}m_{{\bf r}^{\prime}}-(Q_{\bf r}+V_{{\bf r}{\bf r}^{\prime\prime}}m_{{\bf r}^{\prime\prime}})V^{-1}_{{\bf r}{\bf r}^{\prime}}(Q_{{\bf r}^{\prime}}+V_{{\bf r}^{\prime}{\bf r}^{\prime\prime\prime}}m_{{\bf r}^{\prime\prime\prime}})=-Q_{\bf r}V^{-1}_{{\bf r}{\bf r}^{\prime}}Q_{{\bf r}^{\prime}}-2m_{\bf r}Q_{\bf r}

(18)

with the sum convention for paired indices and
with the correlation matrix
V=i\alpha{\bf 1}+{\bar{\beta}}
and
{\bar{\beta}}=\beta/i|\mu|
. We note that

m_{\bf r}V_{{\bf r}{\bf r}^{\prime}}m_{{\bf r}^{\prime}}=m_{\bf r}{\bar{\beta}}_{{\bf r}{\bf r}^{\prime}}m_{{\bf r}^{\prime}}
for Grassmann variables
m_{\bf r}
.
Then we introduce the Gaussian integral

\langle...\rangle_{Q}:=\frac{(i|\mu|/\pi)^{|\Lambda|/2}}{\sqrt{\det V}}\int_{Q}\exp(-i|\mu|\sum_{{\bf r},{\bf r}^{\prime}}Q_{\bf r}V^{-1}_{{\bf r}{\bf r}^{\prime}}Q_{{\bf r}^{\prime}})...\prod_{{\bf r}\in\Lambda}dQ_{\bf r}\ ,

(19)

where we have set the free positive parameter
\alpha
such that
V
is non-singular and its eigenvalues have positive
real parts. The Grassmann integration can be performed because the argument of the exponential function
is a quadratic form of the Grassmann field:

K_{{\bf R}{\bf R}^{\prime}}=\frac{1}{{\cal N}}\Big\langle\int_{\Lambda}\exp\left\{{\tilde{\gamma}}_{0}\sum_{\bf r}\varphi_{{\bf r}}\varphi^{\prime}_{{\bf r}}-\sum_{{\bf r},{\bf r}^{\prime}}\gamma_{{\bf r}{\bf r}^{\prime}}\varphi_{{\bf r}}\varphi^{\prime}_{{\bf r}^{\prime}}-2i|\mu|\sum_{{\bf r}\in\Lambda}Q_{\bf r}\varphi_{{\bf r}}\varphi^{\prime}_{{\bf r}}\right\}\varphi_{\bf R}\varphi^{\prime}_{{\bf R}^{\prime}}\Big\rangle_{Q}

=\frac{1}{{\cal N}}\langle adj_{{\bf R}{\bf R}^{\prime}}({\tilde{\gamma}}_{0}-\gamma-2i|\mu|Q)\rangle_{Q}\ .

(20)

with
\gamma_{{\bf r}{\bf r}^{\prime}}=Tr_{N}(W_{{\bf r}{\bf r}^{\prime}}W^{\dagger}_{{\bf r}^{\prime}{\bf r}})
and

{\tilde{\gamma}}_{0}=\sum_{{\bf r}^{\prime}}\gamma_{{\bf r}{\bf r}^{\prime}}
.
The adjugate matrix can be expressed by the determinant as

adj_{{\bf R}{\bf R}^{\prime}}({\tilde{\gamma}}_{0}-\gamma-2i|\mu|Q)=\det({\tilde{\gamma}}_{0}-\gamma-2i|\mu|Q)({\tilde{\gamma}}_{0}-\gamma-2i|\mu|Q)^{-1}_{{\bf R}{\bf R}^{\prime}}\ ,

(21)

provided that
{\tilde{\gamma}}_{0}-\gamma+2i|\mu|Q
is not singular. The latter can always be arranged by a deformation
of the
Q_{\bf r}
path of integration in the complex plane. The deformation moves the poles of the inverse matrix
away from the real axis, which results in an exponential decay with respect to
|{\bf R}-{\bf R}^{\prime}|
.

III.2
Estimation of the localization length

To analyze the spatial behavior of the inverse matrix we use the plane wave eigenvector

\Phi_{\bf k}=(\exp[i{\bf k}\cdot{\bf r}])
of the translational invariant matrix
\gamma
with eigenvalue
{\tilde{\gamma}}_{\bf k}
:

({\tilde{\gamma}}_{0}-\gamma-2i|\mu|Q)\Phi_{\bf k}=({\tilde{\gamma}}_{0}-{\tilde{\gamma}}_{\bf k}-2i|\mu|Q)\Phi_{\bf k}

(22)

with a diagonal matrix on the right-hand side. Since we have

\Phi_{\bf k}=({\tilde{\gamma}}_{0}-\gamma-2i|\mu|Q)^{-1}({\tilde{\gamma}}_{0}-\gamma-2i|\mu|Q)\Phi_{\bf k}

=({\tilde{\gamma}}_{0}-\gamma-2i|\mu|Q)^{-1}({\tilde{\gamma}}_{0}-{\tilde{\gamma}}_{\bf k}-2i|\mu|Q)\Phi_{\bf k}\ ,

(23)

we get for
\Phi^{\prime}_{\bf k}:=({\tilde{\gamma}}_{0}-{\tilde{\gamma}}_{\bf k}-2i|\mu|Q)\Phi_{\bf k}
the equation

({\tilde{\gamma}}_{0}-\gamma-2i|\mu|Q)^{-1}\Phi^{\prime}_{\bf k}=\Phi_{\bf k}=({\tilde{\gamma}}_{0}-{\tilde{\gamma}}_{\bf k}-2i|\mu|Q)^{-1}\Phi^{\prime}_{\bf k}\ .

(24)

Then we consider the basis
\{\Phi^{\prime}_{\bf k}\}
and define the vector
\phi^{\prime}_{{\bf R}}:=\int_{\bf k}\phi_{{\bf k},{\bf R}}\Phi^{\prime}_{\bf k}
.
This gives with Eq. (
24
)

({\tilde{\gamma}}_{0}-\gamma-2i|\mu|Q)^{-1}\phi^{\prime}_{{\bf R}}=\int_{\bf k}\phi_{{\bf k},{\bf R}}({\tilde{\gamma}}_{0}-{\tilde{\gamma}}_{\bf k}-2i|\mu|Q)^{-1}\Phi^{\prime}_{\bf k}=\int_{\bf k}\phi_{{\bf k},{\bf R}}\Phi_{\bf k}\ .

(25)

Using the special local vector
\phi_{{\bf R}^{\prime}}=(\delta_{{\bf R}^{\prime}{\bf r}})
, we get

\phi_{{\bf R}^{\prime}}\cdot({\tilde{\gamma}}_{0}-\gamma-2i|\mu|Q)^{-1}\phi^{\prime}_{{\bf R}}=\int_{\bf k}\phi_{{\bf k},{\bf R}}(\phi_{{\bf R}^{\prime}}\cdot({\tilde{\gamma}}_{0}-{\tilde{\gamma}}_{\bf k}-2i|\mu|Q)^{-1}\Phi^{\prime}_{\bf k})

=\int_{\bf k}\phi_{{\bf k},{\bf R}}(\phi_{{\bf R}^{\prime}}\cdot\Phi_{\bf k})=\int_{\bf k}\phi_{{\bf k},{\bf R}}e^{i{\bf k}\cdot{\bf R}^{\prime}}\ .

(26)

Finally, we define the expansion coefficients as

\phi_{{\bf k},{\bf R}}=e^{-i{\bf k}\cdot{\bf R}}/({\tilde{\gamma}}_{0}-{\tilde{\gamma}}_{\bf k}-2i|\mu|Q_{{\bf R}})
and obtain

\phi_{{\bf R}^{\prime}}\cdot({\tilde{\gamma}}_{0}-\gamma-2i|\mu|Q)^{-1}\phi^{\prime}_{{\bf R}}=\int_{\bf k}\frac{e^{i{\bf k}\cdot({\bf R}^{\prime}-{\bf R})}}{{\tilde{\gamma}}_{0}-{\tilde{\gamma}}_{\bf k}-2i|\mu|Q_{{\bf R}}}\ ,\ \

\phi^{\prime}_{{\bf R}}=\int_{\bf k}\frac{e^{-i{\bf k}\cdot{\bf R}}}{{\tilde{\gamma}}_{0}-{\tilde{\gamma}}_{\bf k}-2i|\mu|Q_{{\bf R}}}({\tilde{\gamma}}_{0}-{\tilde{\gamma}}_{\bf k}-2i|\mu|Q)\Phi_{\bf k}\ .

(27)

The decay of this expression with respect to
|{\bf R}-{\bf R}^{\prime}|
characterizes the localization even before averaging,
i.e., for any realization of
Q_{\bf R}
.
To calculate the decay we choose the contour
\Gamma
of the
Q_{\bf R}
integration as

Q_{\bf R}=e^{i{\rm sgn}({\bar{Q}})\zeta}{\bar{Q}}+i\eta
with real

-\infty<{\bar{Q}}<\infty
,
\eta>0
and
0<\zeta<\pi/4
, as visualized in Fig.
1
.
Moreover, we assume that
{\tilde{\gamma}}_{0}-{\tilde{\gamma}}_{\bf k}=Dk^{2}
, which is the inverse diffusion propagator
with diffusion coefficient
D
.
Then the
k_{1}
integration can be carried out for the decay along the 1–direction of the lattice as

\int_{\bf k}\frac{e^{i{\bf k}\cdot({\bf R}^{\prime}-{\bf R})}}{{\tilde{\gamma}}_{0}-{\tilde{\gamma}}_{\bf k}-2i|\mu|Q_{\bf R}}=\int_{{\bf k}_{\perp}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{e^{ik_{1}(R_{1}^{\prime}-R_{1})}}{Dk_{1}^{2}+Dk_{\perp}^{2}-2i|\mu|Q_{\bf R}}dk_{1}

=\pi\int_{{\bf k}_{\perp}}\frac{e^{-|R_{1}^{\prime}-R_{1}|\sqrt{k_{\perp}^{2}-2i|\mu|(e^{i{\rm sgn}({\bar{Q}})\zeta}{\bar{Q}}+i\eta)/D}}}{D\sqrt{k_{\perp}^{2}-2i|\mu|(e^{i{\rm sgn}({\bar{Q}})\zeta}{\bar{Q}}+i\eta)/D}}\ ,

(28)

where
{\bf k}_{\perp}
is the
{\bf k}
vector perpendicular to the 1–direction. From the decay of the exponential function
in this expression we extract a localization length as

\xi=\frac{1}{Re\left(\sqrt{k_{\perp}^{2}-2i|\mu|(e^{i{\rm sgn}({\bar{Q}})\zeta}{\bar{Q}}+i\eta)/D}\right)}\leq c(\eta,|{\bar{Q}}|,\zeta)\sqrt{D/|\mu|}

(29)

where the coefficient
c(\eta,|{\bar{Q}}|,\zeta)
of the upper bound is of order 1.

III.3
Higher order terms of the loop expansion

The contribution of large loops can be analyzed for the case when we neglect the string term

W\varphi W^{\dagger}\varphi^{\prime}
inside the trace of Eq. (

On the other hand, in the presence of a string, these loops are obstacles for the string in the full IM

Reducing the loop distribution to small loops, we have found in Sect.

To understand the interaction between loops and a string, where the latter is created by

IV
Discussion and conclusion

The effect of PH-symmetry breaking is characterized by the appearance of small repulsive dimers in a lattice system that
is diffusive at the PH-symmetric point. This means that diffusion, a classical process, is disturbed by a random
distribution of small obstacles.
These obstacles are complex (i.e., they have a phase factor) due to the imaginary matrix
\beta
in Eq. (
17
).
This effect can be seen as a quantum effect, since for classical diffusion in a random environment there are only real
obstacles. The dimers are represented by a Gaussian field with a complex correlation matrix in the Grassmann
functional integral, as given in Eqs. (
19
), (
III.1
).
This reduces the original problem of the IM in Eqs. (
6
) and (
8
)
to the rather elementary case of diffusion in the presence of random obstacles.
The effect of the latter has been estimated and gives a localization length, whose upper bound is

\sqrt{D/|\mu|}
with the diffusion coefficient
D
and the PH-symmetry breaking parameter
\mu
.
This surprisingly elementary result, which depends only on the ratio of the two model parameters, reflects the
competition between diffusion and PH-symmetry breaking. The diffusion coefficient, which is determined through
the expression
{\tilde{\gamma}}_{0}-{\tilde{\gamma}}_{\bf k}=Dk^{2}
, depends on the average Hamiltonian
H_{0}
,
the scattering rate
\eta
and
\mu
according to the expressions in Eq. (
7
).
On the other hand, the localization effect due to PH-symmetry breaking does not agree with the conventional picture
of a mobility edge somewhere in the band of the random Hamiltonian and the related second
order phase transition for dimensionality
d>2

Abrahams et al. 1979
;
Wegner 1979
.
Moreover, our result is different from the self-consistent approach to Anderson localization by Vollhardt and
Wölfle
Vollhardt and Wölfle 1980
;
Wölfle and Vollhardt 2010
, who found that the diffusion coefficient
D
vanishes at
the transition to Anderson localization
but leaves a pole of the effective propagator on the real axis instead of moving it away
into the complex plane. These differences might be related to the fact that we have considered a special class of
Hamiltonians, based on the property (
1
) and the chiral symmetry. Moreover, the type of PH-symmetry breaking in
Eq. (
2
) is special. With our results we cannot rule out that there are other types of PH-symmetry breaking which
lead to the conventional Anderson transition.

The type of Hamiltonian obeying (
1
) is known in the form of the Dirac Hamiltonian, which is realized
for low-energy quasi particles in graphene. As mentioned in the Introduction, this material has been the subject of
intense experimental as well as theoretical research for a number of years. Our result of a finite localization length
in the case of a broken PH symmetry might be useful for the characterization of transport in doped graphene.
For this system
the diffusion coefficient of the two-dimensional Dirac fermions with finite momentum cut-off reads

D=\frac{(\hbar v_{F})^{2}}{\eta}\gamma_{D}

Ziegler 2013
, where the scalar
\gamma_{D}

depends on the momentum cut-off. The Fermi velocity in graphene is typically
v_{F}\approx 10^{6}
m/sec and
a typical Fermi energy
\mu
is up to
|\mu|\approx 0.5
eV. Moreover, the typical scattering rate is

\eta\approx 0.02
eV. Together with the Planck constant

\hbar\approx 6.6\cdot 10^{-16}
eVsec we get for the localization length
\xi\sim\sqrt{D/|\mu|}\approx 10^{-8}
m.
One should keep in mind that a finite system size prevents us to distinguish extended states from localized states whose
localization length is larger than the system size.
This could be important for experiments with graphene flakes
and for numerical simulations of the localization effect away from the Dirac point, especially for a small PH-symmetry
breaking parameter
\mu
. In those cases the localization effect should be observable for localization lengths smaller
than the system size.

Since
