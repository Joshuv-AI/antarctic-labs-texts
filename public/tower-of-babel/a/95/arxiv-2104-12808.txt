Limits of Short-Time Evolution of Local Hamiltonians
Ali Hamed Moosavian, Seyed Sajad Kahani, Salman Beigi

Abstract
Evolutions of local Hamiltonians in short times are expected to remain local and thus limited. In this paper, we validate this intuition by proving some limitations on short-time evolutions of local time-dependent Hamiltonians. We show that the distribution of the measurement output of short-time (at most logarithmic) evolutions of local Hamiltonians are concentrated and satisfy an isoperimetric inequality . To showcase explicit applications of our results, we study the M AX C UT problem and conclude that quantum annealing needs at least a run-time that scales logarithmically in the problem size to beat classical algorithms on M AX C UT . To establish our results, we also prove a Lieb-Robinson bound that works for time-dependent Hamiltonians which might be of independent interest.

Introduction

The Quantum Annealing (QA) algorithm
[

was first introduced as a general purpose optimization algorithm for quantum computers in

. The adiabatic theorem states that if we initialize a system in its ground state, and then evolve the Hamiltonian of the system slowly enough, as long as the spectral gap of the Hamiltonian does not close, the system remains close to the ground state of the system at all times
[
4
,
5
]
. Because of its simple conceptual structure, QA has been implemented on many different platforms and applied to a variety of different problems, see
e.g.

[

,

,

.

In this manuscript, we focus on short-time QA algorithms. This setup is motivated by the current state of the art quantum technologies (see methods of
[
21
]
). On one hand, we still lack universal quantum computers capable of simulating general QA algorithms
[
22
,
23
]
, and on the other hand the analog QA devices lack fault-tolerance. Therefore, the total time of reliably performing a continuous computation on these analog devices is bounded by their decoherence time.

By considering short-time quantum annealing, we exit the scope of the adiabatic theorem. Hence, our results are not sensitive to the Hamiltonian gap. In this context, the algorithms considered in our work are sometimes called "diabatic quantum annealing" in the literature (E.g. see
[
24
]
and references therein).

Quantum Approximate Optimization Algorithm (QAOA) is another general purpose quantum algorithm that can yield approximate solutions to many optimization problems, particularly combinatorial ones
[
25
]
. Based on QAOA’s local nature, recent developments have shown that low-depth QAOAs are unable to produce better approximate solutions to some optimization problems than the best known classical algorithms
[
26
,
27
,
28
,
29
,
30

.

In particular, Bravyi et al
[
28
]
use the
isoperimetric inequality
of
[
31
]
that puts restrictions on the output distributions of all low-depth circuits, to show that low-depth (at most logarithmic) QAOA for
MaxCut
does not outperform best classical algorithms on
Ramanujan graphs
. Also, Farhi et al
[
26
]
show that low-depth QAOA for the problems of
MaxCut
and Maximum Independent Set, does not perform well on random regular bipartite graphs.

Thinking of the correspondence between short-time QA algorithms and low-depth quantum circuits, particularly low-depth QAOAs, it is natural to ask whether the above bounds hold for QA algorithms or not. In this paper, we show that most of the aforementioned results on limitations of QAOAs still hold for short-time QA algorithms as well. Throughout this article we use short-time to represent times that scale at most logarithmically with the system size. We first show that the isoperimetric inequality of
[
31
]
remains to hold for output distributions of short-time QA computations. Then, using this inequality as a tool (besides others), we generalize the results of
[
28
]
and
[
26
]
to the QA case.

QA and QAOA can be thought of two instances of optimal control problems. In QA we look for an optimal path in the space of Hamiltonians from a starting Hamiltonian to a target one, while in QAOA we look for optimal rotation angles of some local circuits.
Applying
Pontryagin’s maximum principle
, some authors have argued that the optimal solution to the first control theory problem takes the so called
bang-bang
cond problem. However, it was shortly noted that in some common problem instances the control Hamiltonian of the system becomes
singular
, a condition that was presumed to be extremely rare, and then the optimal control protocol is no longer of the bang-bang form
[
32
,
33
,
34
]
. This means that, in general, the optimal control problem for QA does not reduce to that of the QAOA, and needs thoughtful considerations. In fact, the observations of
[
32
,
33
,
34
]
show that we cannot directly use the results on the obstructions of QAOAs, to prove the aforementioned limitations of short-time QA algorithms.

Another idea for relating results on low-depth quantum circuits (QAOAs) to QA algorithms is
trotterization
(see
[
35
]
and references therein), which approximates the unitary evolution of a local Hamiltonian with a quantum circuit. Although one can imagine this approach working in some very specific instances, in general the trotterized circuit must have an asymptotic depth that grows polynomially with the number of qubits. This prohibits the application of the aforementioned QAOA results,
[
26
,
27
,
28
,
29
,
30
]
, as they only apply to logarithmic depths.

We use
Lieb-Robinson bounds
as a main tool for proving our results
[
36
,
37
,
38
,
39
]
. To this end, we first prove a Lieb-Robinson bound that works for time-dependent Hamiltonians and is amenable to QA algorithms. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first time-dependent Lieb-Robinson bound.

As another technical tool, we use the notion of
\gamma_{2}
-norm
, also called
factorization norm
, from matrix analysis to bound the derivative of certain functions on the space of operators. This enables us to bound certain errors induced by applying the Lieb-Robinson approximation.

Here is a summary of our results and the structure of the paper. In

2
Lieb-Robinson Bound

One of our main tools in this manuscript is the Lieb-Robinson bound
[
40
]
. Roughly speaking, it states that the evolution of a local observable in the Heisenberg picture, remains almost local in short times. We use the following version of the Lieb-Robinson bound that notably works for
time-dependent Hamiltonians
as well.

To state our theorem we need a definition. Given a graph
G
and a subset of its vertices
A
, we denote
the set of vertices on the
L
-
boundary
of
A
by

\partial_{L}(A)
. That is,

\displaystyle\partial_{L}(A)=

\displaystyle\{x\in A:\,\exists y\in A^{c},\dist_{G}(x,y)\leq L\}

(1)

\displaystyle\penalty\ \cup\{x\in A^{c}:\,\exists y\in A,\dist_{G}(x,y)\leq L\},

(2)

where
A^{c}
is the subgraph consisting of vertices not in
A
and
\dist_{G}(x,y)
is the graph distance between vertices
x
and
y
.

Theorem 1

(Lieb-Robinson Bound)
.

Let
G
be a graph that may contain loops and parallel edges with maximum degree
\Delta>1
.
Let
\mathbf{H}(t)
be a time-dependent Hamiltonian defined on vertices of
G
of the form

\mathbf{H}(t)=\sum_{e}u_{e}(t)\mathbf{h}_{e},

(3)

where
\mathbf{h}_{e}
is time-independent and acts non-trivially only on the two ends of edge
e
in
G
. Let
\mathbf{U}(t)
be the unitary evolution associated with
\mathbf{H}(t)
. Suppose that
\norm{u_e(t)\vb h_{e}}\leq g
for any
e
and
0\leq t\leq T
. Throughout this paper
\norm{.}
represents the spectral norm unless it is clearly defined otherwise. Let
\mathbf{O}_{A}
be an observable acting on region
A
of the graph. For
L>1
let

\mathbf{\widetilde{H}}_{A}(t):=\sum_{e\subseteq A\cup\partial_{L}(A)}u_{e}(t)\mathbf{h}_{e},

(4)

be the Hamiltonian consisting of terms in the region
A\cup\partial_{L}(A)
, and let
\mathbf{V}_{A}(t)
be the unitary evolution associated to
\mathbf{\widetilde{H}}_{A}(t)
.
Then we have

\norm{\vb U^\dagger(T) \vb O_A\vb U(T)-\vb V^\dagger_A(T) \vb O_A\vb V_A(T)}\leq\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}\,|A|\cdot\|\mathbf{O}_{A}\|\cdot\e^{-L\big(\log L-\log T-\log(4 g(\Delta-1))\big)-\frac{1}{2}\log L}\,.

(5)

Intuitively speaking, this theorem says that, as
\mathbf{O}_{A}
is local, it remains
almost
local under a short-time evolution of a local Hamiltonian. Indeed, since
\widetilde{\mathbf{H}}_{A}(t)
is non-trivial only in the region
A\cup\partial_{L}(A)
, then
\mathbf{V}_{A}(T)
also acts non-trivially only on this region. Therefore, by this theorem as it is schematically depicted in
Fig.

1
,
\mathbf{U}^{\dagger}(T)\mathbf{O}_{A}\mathbf{U}(T)
is approximated by
\mathbf{V}^{\dagger}_{A}(T)\mathbf{O}_{A}\mathbf{V}_{A}(T)
that acts only on
A\cup\partial_{L}(A)
.

(Color online) Illustration of
1
. Here,
A
is the region on which the operator
\mathbf{O}_{A}
acts, and the larger shaded area represents
\partial_{L}(A)
.
\mathbf{V}^{\dagger}_{A}(T)\mathbf{O}_{A}\mathbf{V}_{A}(T)
acts non-trivially on
A\cup\partial_{L}(A)
and approximates
\mathbf{U}^{\dagger}(T)\mathbf{O}_{A}\mathbf{U}(T)
, the time evolution of
\mathbf{O}_{A}
in the Heisenberg picture.

Some remarks are in line regarding this theorem. First, as the proof of this theorem given in
Appendix

A
shows, the above Lieb-Robinson bound can easily be generalized for local Hamiltonians with
k
-body interactions, by replacing the term
\log(4g(\Delta-1))
on the right hand side of
Eq.

5
with
\log(2kg(\Delta-1))
. Second, this theorem in the case where
\mathbf{H}
is time-independent is proven in
[
41
,
37
,
38
]
. Third, the type of time-dependent Hamiltonians (
Equation

3
) considered by this theorem are motivated by some quantum annealing applications in mind. Nevertheless, note that in principle any time-dependent Hamiltonian can be written in this form; simply consider the expansion of each local term of the Hamiltonian in the Pauli basis.

3
Limits on the output distribution of short-time evaluations

In this section we present our main results, namely, how the output distribution of a short-time quantum annealing algorithm is constrained. Our first main result is a generalization of the well-known
Chebyshev inequality
in probability theory that is a concentration of measure inequality. Our second result is an
isoperimetric inequality
. A similar result for low-depth circuits has been proven in
[
31
]
.

In the following we assume that
\mathbf{H}(t)
is a time-dependent Hamiltonian defined on the vertices of a graph
G
as in
Eq.

3
. We assume that starting with a
product state

\displaystyle\ket{\psi_0}=\ket{s_1}\otimes\ket{s_2}\otimes\cdots\otimes\ket{s_n},

(6)

the system evolves under the Hamiltonian
\mathbf{H}(t)
. Let

\displaystyle\ket{\psi_t}=\mathbf{U}(t)\ket{\psi_0},

(7)

where as before
\mathbf{U}(t)
is the associated unitary evolution. We assume that at time
T
we make a measurement in the computational basis to a get a distribution
p(\mathbf{x})
on
\{0,1\}^{n}
.

3.1
A Chebyshev bound

Theorem 2

(Quantum Chebyshev bound)
.

Let
\mathbf{H}(t)
be a Hamiltonian on a graph with maximum degree
\Delta>1
as in
Eq.

3
with
\|u_{e}(t)\mathbf{h}_{e}\|\leq g
for any edge
e
. Let

\displaystyle T\leq\frac{\kappa_{1}}{8g\Delta^{\frac{2-\kappa_{1}}{\kappa_{1}}}\log\Delta}\log n,

(8)

where
0<\kappa_{1}<1
is an arbitrary constant. Let
p(\mathbf{x})
be the output distribution of measuring
\ket{\psi_T}=\mathbf{U}(T)\ket{\psi_0}
in the computational basis. We assume
\ket{\psi_0}
to be a product state as in
Eq.

6
. Then for any
c>0
and
\kappa_{1}/2<\kappa_{2}<1/2
we have

\displaystyle\Pr\left[\left|w_{H}(\mathbf{x})-\left(\frac{n}{2}-\frac{m}{2}\right)\right|\geq cn^{\frac{1}{2}+\kappa_{2}}\right]

(9)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{3}{2c^{2}}n^{-(2\kappa_{2}-\kappa_{1})},

where
w_{H}(\mathbf{x})
is the hamming weight of
\mathbf{x}\in\{0,1\}^{n}
, i.e. number of non-zero coordinates of
\mathbf{x}
, and
m=\sum_{i}\bra{\psi_T}\mathbf{Z}_{i}\ket{\psi_T}
with
\mathbf{Z}
being the Pauli-
z
operator.

This theorem says that, when
T
is bounded, with high probability the hamming weight of the measurement output is close to
\frac{1}{2}(n-m)
which is the average of
w_{H}(\mathbf{x})
. That is, the Hamming weight of the measurement outcome is
concentrated
.

The proof idea of this theorem is similar to that of the standard Chebyshev inequality; we need to bound the variance of
w_{H}(\mathbf{x})
. In the standard Chebyshev inequality the coordinates of
\mathbf{x}
are independent while here they are correlated. Nevertheless, since
T
is bounded, by the Lieb-Robinson bound (
1
) pairs of coordinates whose associated Hamming weights are far from each other are almost independent. This allows us to bound the variance of
w_{H}(\mathbf{x})
. A detailed proof of this theorem is given in
Appendix

B
.

As a result of this theorem, as long as the initial state is a product state, no short-time annealing computation can produce the
n
-qubit GHZ state
\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\ket{0\cdots 0}+\ket{1\cdots 1})
in sub-logarithmic times. This is because measuring this state in the computational basis gives two far apart peaks in the hamming weight
w_{H}
,
Fig.

2
. We note that this statement holds for any annealing computation even with gapless Hamiltonians. Intuitively speaking, this is a result of the local nature of the Hamiltonian in
Eq.

3
.

In this section and the following one, we present limits of short-time quantum annealing algorithms for the
MaxCut
problem. Here, inspired by
[
28
]

our strategy is to
use the
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-symmetry of the
MaxCut
Hamiltonian, and show that short-time quantum annealing algorithm cannot outperform best classical algorithms for
MaxCut
on Ramanujan graphs
[
42
]
.

We first describe the quantum annealing algorithm for
MaxCut
.

Given a graph
G=(V,E)
, the
MaxCut
problem asks for a
cut
with maximum size in
G
; meaning what is the maximum number of edges between vertices in
V_{+}
and
V_{-}
where
V_{+}\cup V_{-}=V
form a partition of vertices? Labeling vertices in
V_{+}
with
+1
and vertices in
V_{-}
with
-1
, this problem is equivalent to minimizing the energy of the following Hamiltonian:

\mathbf{C}:=-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{(i,j)\in E}{\left(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Z}_{i}\mathbf{Z}_{j}\right)},

(20)

Where
\mathbf{Z}_{i}
’s are the Pauli-
z
operators. Indeed, letting
\Cut^{*}
be the maximum cut of
G
we have

\Cut^{*}=-\min_{\ket{\phi}}\bra{\phi}\mathbf{C}\ket{\phi}.

(21)

We may use quantum annealing to find the ground state and minimum energy of
\mathbf{C}
. To this end, a common choice for the
driving Hamiltonian
is the summation of Pauli-
x
operators:

\mathbf{B}:=-\sum_{k\in V}\mathbf{X}_{k}.

(22)

Using a control parameter
u:[0,T]\to[0,1]
with
u(0)=0
and
u(T)=1
, the resulting time-dependent Hamiltonian is given by

\mathbf{H}(t):=\left(1-u(t)\right)\mathbf{B}+u(t)\mathbf{C}

(23)

At time
t=0
, we start with the ground state of
\mathbf{H}(0)=\mathbf{B}
that is equal to

\displaystyle\ket{\psi_0}=\ket{+}^{\otimes n},

(24)

where
n=|V|
is the number of vertices. In the following we show that if
T
is bounded, then

\bra{\psi_T}\mathbf{C}\ket{\psi_T},

(25)

is bounded away from the ground energy, i.e.,
-\Cut^{*}
, for a certain family of graphs to be defined.

For a graph
G=(V,E)
on
n
vertices, its

is defined by
[
43
]

h(G)=\min_{S\subset V,0<\absolutevalue{S}\leq\frac{n}{2}}\frac{\absolutevalue{\partial^E(S)}}{\absolutevalue{S}}\,,

(26)

where
\partial^{E}(S)
is the set of
edges
with one end in
S
and one end outside of
S
.
Ramanujan graphs
are certain families of graphs that have a large Cheeger constant; for a
\Delta
-regular Ramanujan graph
G
we have

h(G)\geq\frac{1}{2}\big(\Delta-2\sqrt{\Delta-1}\big).

(27)

It is known that
[
44
,
45
,
46
]
for any
\Delta\geq 3
there exists an infinite family of
\Delta
-regular
bipartite
Ramanujan graphs. We note that for such a graph, as a bipartite graph, we have

\Cut^{*}=|E|=\frac{1}{2}\Delta n.

(28)

.

Let

-regular bipartite Ramanujan graph on

, the right hand side of

Farhi et al also show how the low-depth QAOA algorithm cannot outperform classical algorithms for the maximum independent set problem on random bipartite graphs

6
Conclusions

Motivated by short-time quantum annealing, in this manuscript we proved limits on the output distribution of local time-dependent Hamiltonian evolutions.
In particular, we showed that the distribution of the measurement outcome of a local Hamiltonian evolution is concentrated and satisfies an isoperimetric inequality if the evolution time is short.
Using our framework, we generalized results about limitations of low-depth quantum circuits, to short-time QA algorithms. To this end, an essential tool that we developed, is a Lieb-Robinson bound that works for time-dependent Hamiltonians and is amenable to QA algorithms.

Our results are general in the sense that they work for the circuit model as well; they imply limitations on the output distribution of low-depth circuits with a locality structure. The point is that we can assume that the control parameters
u_{e}(t)
in
Eq.

3
have a so-called bang-bang form: they are either zero or take a fixed value, and for each
t
the values of
u_{e_{1}}(t),u_{e_{2}}(t)
for intersecting edges
e_{1},e_{2}
cannot both be non-zero. With such bang-bang control parameters, low-depth local circuits can be seen as special cases of short-time evolutions of local Hamiltonians for which our results hold.

The short-time condition is strictly essential for the bounds we have proven, and our results do not hold without this assumption. Indeed, there are several known examples where quantum annealing with
\textup{Poly}(n)
run-time can provide superpolynomial speedups. For example, see
[
48
,
49
,
50
]
.

We believe that the techniques presented here can be applied to generalize other results about the limitation of low-depth circuits too, see
e.g.

[
29
,
27
]
, for short-time QA algorithm. We leave this for future research.

Appendix C
Derivative of a matrix function

In this appendix we compute a bound on the derivative of a certain polynomial function of matrices. This bound is an important ingredient in the proof of
3
. Indeed, in the proof, applying the Lieb-Robinson bound, a desired operator is approximated by a local one. Then, a polynomial function is applied on this operator, and the blow-up of the error of the Lieb-Robinson approximation induced by this polynomial should be estimated. We note that a naive approach to estimate this error is ineffective since the degree of this polynomial is large and some of its coefficients are exponentially large. Therefore, we need a more careful analysis of this error which itself demands a more careful analysis of the derivative of that polynomial as a map on the space of matrices.

We first need to fix some notations. Given two matrices
\mathbf{A}
and
\mathbf{B}
of the same dimensions, we denote their
entry-wise product
by
\mathbf{A}\circ\mathbf{B}
. In other words,
\mathbf{A}\circ\mathbf{B}
is a matrix whose
(i,j)
-th entry equals the product of
(i,j)
-th entries of
\mathbf{A}
and
\mathbf{B}
, i.e.,
(\mathbf{A}\circ\mathbf{B})_{ij}=\mathbf{A}_{ij}\cdot\mathbf{B}_{ij}
. The matrix
\mathbf{A}\circ\mathbf{B}
is called the Schur or Hadamard product of
\mathbf{A}
and
\mathbf{B}
.

For a continuously differentiable function
f:(a,b)\to\mathbb{R}
and a sequence
\boldsymbol{\lambda}=(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{d})
of numbers in
(a,b)
we let
\mathcal{D}_{f,\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
be the
d\times d
matrix whose
(i,j)
-th entry equals

\displaystyle\big(\mathcal{D}_{f,\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\big)_{ij}=\begin{cases}\frac{f(\lambda_{i})-f(\lambda_{j})}{\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{j}}&\quad\lambda_{i}\neq\lambda_{j},\\
f^{\prime}(\lambda_{i})&\quad\lambda_{i}=\lambda_{j},\end{cases}

(62)

where
f^{\prime}(\lambda_{i})
is the derivative of
f
at
\lambda_{i}
.
For a diagonal matrix
\mathbf{\Lambda}=\text{diag}(\boldsymbol{\lambda})
with diagonal elements in
\boldsymbol{\lambda}=(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{d})
we let

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{f,\mathbf{\Lambda}}:=\mathcal{D}_{f,\boldsymbol{\lambda}}.

(63)

Also, for a self-adjoint matrix
\mathbf{A}
, that can be diagonalized as

\mathbf{A}=\mathbf{U}\mathbf{\Lambda}\mathbf{U}^{\dagger}
where
\mathbf{U}
is unitary and
\mathbf{\Lambda}=\text{diag}(\boldsymbol{\lambda})
is a diagonal matrix containing eigenvalues of
\mathbf{A}
, we let

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{f,\mathbf{A}}=\mathbf{U}\mathcal{D}_{f,\mathbf{\Lambda}}\mathbf{U}^{\dagger}.

(64)

Lemma 3
.

[
51
, pp. 124]

Let
\mathbf{A},\mathbf{E}
be two Hermitian matrices and
f
be a real continuously differentiable function on an interval containing the eigenvalues of
\mathbf{A}+t\mathbf{E}
for all

\derivative{t}f\left(\mathbf{A}+t\mathbf{E}\right)\Big|_{t=0}=\mathcal{D}_{f,\mathbf{A}}\circ\mathbf{E}\,.

(65)

By this lemma, to bound the derivative of a matrix function as above, we need to bound the norm of the Schur product as a super-operator on the space of matrices. This super-operator norm is given by the so called
\gamma_{2}
-norm.

Lemma 4
.

[
52
, pp. 79]

For a
d\times d
matrix
\mathbf{M}
let

\displaystyle\gamma_{2}(\mathbf{M}):=\inf\,\max\{\|\ket{v_i}\|^{2}:\,1\leq i\leq d\}\cup\{\|\ket{w_j}\|^{2}:\,1\leq j\leq d\},

(66)

where the infimum is taken over all vectors
\ket{v_i},\ket{w_j}
satisfying
\mathbf{M}_{ij}=\langle v_{i}\ket{w_j}

. Then for any

It is not hard to verify that

\mathbf{M}
and
\mathbf{M}^{\prime}

where

and
\ket{\tilde w_j}=\ket{w_j}\otimes\ket{w'_j}

\displaystyle\gamma_{2}(\mathbf{M}\circ\mathbf{M}^{\prime})\leq\gamma_{2}(\mathbf{M})\cdot\gamma_{2}(\mathbf{M}^{\prime}).

Then the result follows using
5

In the proof of

Lemma 6
.

For any
0\leq\delta<1
and any sequence
\boldsymbol{\lambda}=(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{d})
with
-1\leq\lambda_{i}\leq 1+\delta
we have

\gamma_{2}(\mathcal{D}_{T_{n},\boldsymbol{\lambda}})\leq(2n^{2}-1)T_{n}(1+\delta),\qquad\forall n\geq 1\,.

(74)

Proof.

We prove the lemma by induction on
n
.
For
n=1
it can be easily shown by setting
T_{1}(x)=x
.

For an even number
2n
we can write:

\displaystyle\left(\mathcal{D}_{T_{2n},\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\right)_{ij}

\displaystyle=\frac{T_{2n}(\lambda_{i})-T_{2n}(\lambda_{j})}{\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{j}}

(75)

\displaystyle=2\frac{T_{n}^{2}(\lambda_{i})-T_{n}^{2}(\lambda_{j})}{\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{j}}

(76)

\displaystyle=2\frac{\left(T_{n}(\lambda_{i})-T_{n}(\lambda_{j})\right)\left(T_{n}(\lambda_{i})+T_{n}(\lambda_{j})\right)}{\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{j}}

(77)

\displaystyle=2\left(\mathcal{D}_{T_{n},\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\right)_{ij}\left(T_{n}(\lambda_{i})+T_{n}(\lambda_{j})\right)

(78)

\displaystyle=2\left(\mathcal{D}_{T_{n},\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\right)_{ij}\left(R_{T_{n},\boldsymbol{\lambda}}+R^{\intercal}_{T_{n},\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\right)_{ij}\,,

(79)

where
\big(\mathcal{R}_{T_{n},\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\big)_{ij}=T_{n}(\lambda_{i})
, and
R^{\intercal}_{T_{n},\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
is its transpose. Now we can write:

\displaystyle\gamma_{2}(\mathcal{D}_{T_{2n},\boldsymbol{\lambda}})

\displaystyle\leq 2\gamma_{2}(\mathcal{D}_{T_{n},\boldsymbol{\lambda}})\gamma_{2}(R_{T_{n},\boldsymbol{\lambda}}+R^{\intercal}_{T_{n},\boldsymbol{\lambda}})\,,

(80)

\displaystyle\leq 2\left((2n^{2}-1)T_{n}(1+\delta)\right)\left(\gamma_{2}(R_{T_{n},\boldsymbol{\lambda}})+\gamma_{2}(R^{\intercal}_{T_{n},\boldsymbol{\lambda}})\right)\,.

Using
\left|T_{n}(x)\right|\leq T_{n}(1+\delta)
for
x\in[-1,1+\delta]
, we can find that

\gamma_{2}(R_{T_{n},\boldsymbol{\lambda}}),\gamma_{2}(R^{\intercal}_{T_{n},\boldsymbol{\lambda}})\leq T_{n}(1+\delta)\,.

(81)

As a result

\gamma_{2}(\mathcal{D}_{T_{2n},\boldsymbol{\lambda}})\leq 4(2n^{2}-1)T_{n}^{2}(1+\delta)\,.

(82)

Now recall that
T_{n}^{2}(1+\delta)=\frac{T_{2n}(1+\delta)+1}{2}
and knowing that
T_{2n}(1+\delta)\geq 1

\displaystyle\gamma_{2}(\mathcal{D}_{T_{2n},\boldsymbol{\lambda}})

\displaystyle\leq 2(2n^{2}-1)\left(T_{2n}(1+\delta)+1\right)\,,

(83)

\displaystyle\leq 4(2n^{2}-1)T_{2n}(1+\delta)\,,

\displaystyle\leq\left(2(2n)^{2}-1\right)T_{2n}(1+\delta)\,.

Similarly for an odd number
2n+1
, we can write:

\displaystyle\left(\mathcal{D}_{T_{2n+1},\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\right)_{ij}

\displaystyle=2\frac{T_{n}(\lambda_{i})T_{n+1}(\lambda_{i})-T_{n}(\lambda_{j})T_{n+1}(\lambda_{j})}{\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{j}}-1

(84)

\displaystyle=2\frac{T_{n}(\lambda_{i})T_{n+1}(\lambda_{i})-T_{n}(\lambda_{j})T_{n+1}(\lambda_{i})+T_{n}(\lambda_{j})T_{n+1}(\lambda_{i})-T_{n}(\lambda_{j})T_{n+1}(\lambda_{j})}{\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{j}}-1

(85)

\displaystyle=2\left(\left(\mathcal{D}_{T_{n},\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\right)_{ij}T_{n+1}\left(\lambda_{i}\right)+T_{n}\left(\lambda_{j}\right)\left(\mathcal{D}_{T_{n+1},\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\right)_{ij}\right)-1

(86)

\displaystyle=2\left(\left(\mathcal{D}_{T_{n},\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\right)_{ij}\left((R_{T_{n+1},\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\right)_{ij}+\left(R^{\intercal}_{T_{n},\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\right)\left(\mathcal{D}_{T_{n+1},\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\right)_{ij}\right)-1\,.

(87)

Now by bounding
\gamma_{2}(R_{T_{n},\boldsymbol{\lambda}})
and
\gamma_{2}(R_{T_{n+1},\boldsymbol{\lambda}})

\displaystyle\gamma_{2}(\mathcal{D}_{T_{2n+1},\boldsymbol{\lambda}})\leq

\displaystyle\penalty\displaystyle 2\gamma_{2}(\mathcal{D}_{T_{n},\boldsymbol{\lambda}})\gamma_{2}\left((R_{T_{n+1},\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\right)+2\gamma_{2}(\mathcal{D}_{T_{n+1},\boldsymbol{\lambda}})\gamma_{2}\left(R^{\intercal}_{T_{n},\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\right)+1\,,

(88)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\penalty\displaystyle 2(2n^{2}-1)T_{n}(1+\delta)\gamma_{2}(R_{T_{n+1},\boldsymbol{\lambda}})+

\displaystyle\penalty\displaystyle 2\left(2(n+1)^{2}-1\right)T_{n+1}(1+\delta)\gamma_{2}(R^{\intercal}_{T_{n},\boldsymbol{\lambda}})+1\,,

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\penalty\displaystyle 2(2n^{2}-1)T_{n}(1+\delta)T_{n+1}(1+\delta)+

\displaystyle\penalty\displaystyle 2\left(2(n+1)^{2}-1\right)T_{n+1}(1+\delta)T_{n}(1+\delta)+1\,,

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\penalty\displaystyle 2\left((2n+1)^{2}-1\right)T_{n}(1+\delta)T_{n+1}(1+\delta)+1\,.

Next using
2T_{n}(1+\delta)T_{n+1}(1+\delta)=T_{2n+1}(1+\delta)+1+\delta
and the fact that
T_{2n+1}(1+\delta)\geq 1+\delta
we obtain

\displaystyle\gamma_{2}(\mathcal{D}_{T_{2n+1},\boldsymbol{\lambda}})

\displaystyle\leq\left((2n+1)^{2}-1\right)\left(T_{2n+1}(1+\delta)+1+\delta\right)+1\,,

(89)

\displaystyle\leq\left(2(2n+1)^{2}-1\right)T_{2n+1}(1+\delta)\,.

∎

A general isoperimetric inequality

In this appendix we prove

Proof.
