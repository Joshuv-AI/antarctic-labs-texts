Progressive Multi-resolution Loss for Crowd Counting
Ziheng Yan, Yuankai Qi, Guorong Li, Xinyan Liu, Weigang Zhang, Qingming Huang, Ming-Hsuan Yang

Abstract
Crowd counting is usually handled in a density map regression fashion, which is supervised via a L2 loss between the predicted density map and ground truth. To effectively regulate models, various improved L2 loss functions have been proposed to find a better correspondence between predicted density and annotation positions. In this paper, we propose to predict the density map at one resolution but measure the density map at multiple resolutions. By maximizing the posterior probability in such a setting, we obtain a log-formed multi-resolution L2-difference loss, where the traditional single-resolution L2 loss is its particular case. We mathematically prove it is superior to a single-resolution L2 loss. Without bells and whistles, the proposed loss substantially improves several baselines and performs favorably compared to state-of-the-art methods on four crowd counting datasets, ShanghaiTech A & B, UCF-QNRF, and JHU-Crowd++.



1
Introduction

Crowd counting aims to count the number of humans in a crowd scene, which has drawn increasing attention in recent years due to its wide applications in the real world.
Most existing methods generate a density map from the input image and then match it to the corresponding dot annotations.
One significant challenge in crowd counting is to associate the pixels of the predicted density map to the target dot annotations in a reasonable way.

Figure 1
:
Modeling the correspondence of predicted density map and ground truth annotation:
(a) Using Gaussian kernel to convert ground truth point annotation to density map. Then pixels at the same coordinates are viewed and matched.
(b) Using Bayes’ theorem to find the ground truth annotation for a pixel in the predicted density map.
(c) Using Optimal Transport of a cost matrix of displacement distance to find the correspondence.
(d) We also view pixels at the same coordinates as matched but we propose to measure the similarity between the generated density map and the ground truth map at multiple resolutions. By maximizing its posterior probability, we derive a new loss, where the traditional single-resolution L2 loss is its particular case.

Currently, there are three main types of methods to establish the association of pixels between the predicted density map and the ground truth annotations:
(i) Gaussian kernel as shown in Fig.
1
(a). Ground truth dot annotations are converted to a density map by placing a Gaussian kernel at each annotation point.
Then, L2 distance loss is used to narrow the gap between pixels at the same locations of the predicted density map and the converted ground truth density map
lempitsky_learning_2010
;
zhang_single-image_2016
;
Adaptive_Density_Map
;
kernel-Based
.
These methods focus on designing algorithms for selecting the Gaussian kernel bandwidth to generate a high-quality ground truth density map.
(ii) Bayesian Inference (BI) as shown in Fig.
1
(b). Instead of generating density maps from point annotations, BI
ma_bayesian_2019
computes the posterior probability of a pixel location in the predicted density map according to Bayes’ theorem to infer its corresponding ground truth annotation.
(iii) Optimal Transport (OT) as shown in Fig.
1
(c). As opposed to the above indirect approaches, a direct distance measurement based on optimal transport (OT) theory
wang_distribution_2020
;
wan_generalized_2021
is proposed to find the best match between the predictions and annotations in an end-to-end way. OT no longer requires the setting of Gaussian kernel bandwidth but relies on a cost matrix that reflects the distance between pixels of the predicted density map and ground truth annotation.
Different from the above-mentioned methods, we view pixels at the same coordinates that are matched, but we propose to measure the similarity between a predicted density map and the ground truth under multiple resolutions.
By maximizing the posterior probability in such a setting, we derive a log-formed L2-difference based loss, where the traditional single-resolution loss is its particular case.
We theoretically prove that the derived multi-resolution loss is superior to a single-resolution L2 loss.
Without bells and whistles, the proposed loss brings substantial performance improvement over baselines and favorable performance against state-of-the-art methods on four crowd counting benchmark datasets, ShanghaiTech A & B, UCF-QNRF, and JHU-Crowd++.

2
Related Work

Counting based on L2 regression of density map.

Counting based on density maps is first proposed in
lempitsky_learning_2010
.
Unlike previous methods of detection and localization for counting, this method transforms the counting problem into estimating object density, and the sum of the densities in a region is viewed as the total number of objects in that region.
Density map regression is implemented by minimizing a regularized risk quadratic cost function.
To this end, a ground truth density map is used in supervised learning. Specifically, the density map is generated by placing a fixed-bandwidth Gaussian kernel at each annotated point. However, the geometry of the scene and perspective effects can lead to distortion of the generated density map
zhang_single-image_2016
.
When the density of objects is considered to be evenly distributed, the average distance between the center and its nearest neighbors gives a reasonable estimate of the geometric distortion. Under this assumption,
zhang_single-image_2016
proposes to specify the bandwidth of the Gaussian kernel for each annotated point to the average distance from the neighbors.
In
Adaptive_Density_Map
, it is suggested that specific density maps
designed manually may not be optimal for the end-to-end training process. To address this issue, a framework for generating density maps is proposed in
Adaptive_Density_Map
, which improves the quality of density maps by fusing blurred density maps generated by various Gaussian kernels.

Crowd counting based on Bayesian Inference.

Bayesian loss (BL)
ma_bayesian_2019
, no longer using low-quality density maps generated by Gaussian kernels, constructs a density contribution probability model from the point annotations.
Compared to pixel-wise L2 regression methods, L1 regression is performed point-wisely in BL between the annotated points and the contributions of the density map.
Bayesian theory plays the role of converting prior probabilities into posterior contributions in this process. Because the computation of contributions in regions far from the annotated points is not always correct, BL introduces background modeling so that the predicted density on the background can be counted as zero. In
Congestion-Aware_Bayesian
, BL is further improved on background probability modeling, allowing BL to adapt to scale variations of people.

Crowd counting based on Optimal Transport.

DM-Count
wang_distribution_2020
theoretically and empirically shows that the generalization performance can be hurt by the “ground truth” density map smoothed by Gaussian kernels from point annotations.
DM-Count views crowd counting as a distribution matching problem.
It uses Optimal Transport (OT) to find the best match between generated density map and point annotations.
The solution of the optimal transport is iteratively approximated by using the Sinkhorn algorithm
sinkhorn
with a cost matrix.
GL
wan_generalized_2021
follows the work on matching the distribution of density maps using OT with a modified cost matrix that concerns the perspective effect in crowd images. GL changes the form of OT used in counting so that the density map matches the point annotations directly without normalization of the predicted density map, which also makes L2 and BL sub-optimal solutions to the deformed OT problem.

Proposed Method

In this section, we first formulate the crowd counting task as a probability maximization problem. Then we derive the optimization loss for deep learning model by maximizing the posterior probability, during which we also show the resulting loss function is superior to a single-resolution L2 loss.

3.1
Problem formulation

Let
\hat{\bm{y}}=f(\mathbf{x})\in\mathbb{R}^{2^{L}\times 2^{L}}
represent the predicted density map, where
f
is a counting model,
\mathbf{x}
is an input image, and
2^{L}\times 2^{L}
is the resolution of the predicted density map.
Let
\bm{y}_{ori}
be the original point annotation map, of which each element is 1 or 0, denoting the occupancy of one person or object. We convert
\bm{y}_{ori}
to
\bm{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{2^{L}\times 2^{L}}
by evenly dividing it into
2^{L}\times 2^{L}
grids and the sum of the annotations in each grid is used as the new pixel value in
\bm{y}
as shown in Fig.
2
.

In addition to measuring the similarity between
\hat{\bm{y}}
and
\bm{y}
on the resolution
2^{L}\times 2^{L}
as existing methods, we propose to also measure the similarity at multiple sub-resolutions:
2^{i}\times 2^{i},i\in\{n_{0},n_{1},\cdots,n_{k}\}
, where
n_{i}
is an integer,
n_{i}<n_{i+1}
and
k<L
.
Predicted density map
\hat{\bm{y}}_{2^{i}}
and ground truth annotation map
{\bm{y}}_{2^{i}}
at resolution
2^{i}\times 2^{i}
can be obtained by evenly dividing
\hat{\bm{y}}
or
\bm{y}
into
2^{i}\times 2^{i}
grids and summing the elements in each grid to get the value for each element in the new resolution. In such a setting, training a model
f
is to maximize the posterior probability
p(\bm{y}=\bm{y}_{2^{L}},\bm{y}_{2^{n_{k}}},\cdots,\bm{y}_{2^{n_{0}}}|\mathbf{x})
on resolutions
\mathbb{N}=\{n_{0},n_{1},\cdots,n_{k},L\}
.
According to the chain rule of conditional probability, and because
\bm{y}_{2^{n_{i+1}}}
contains all the information in
\bm{y}_{2^{n_{i}}}
and
\bm{y}
contains all the information in
\bm{y}_{2^{n_{k}}}
, the posterior probability can be decomposed

\displaystyle p(\bm{y}|\mathbf{x},\mathbb{N})\overset{def}{=}

\displaystyle p(\bm{y},\bm{y}_{2^{n_{k}}},\cdots,\bm{y}_{2^{n_{0}}}|\mathbf{x})

(1)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle p(\bm{y}|\bm{y}_{2^{n_{k}}},\mathbf{x})\left[\prod\limits_{j=0}^{k-1}p(\bm{y}_{2^{n_{j+1}}}|\bm{y}_{2^{n_{j}}},\mathbf{x})\right]p(\bm{y}_{2^{n_{0}}}|\mathbf{x}).

The goal of training the model
f
is equivalent to maximizing the marginal likelihood

f^{*}=\arg\max_{f}\mathbb{E}_{D}\log p(\bm{y},\bm{y}_{2^{n_{k}}},\cdots,\bm{y}_{2^{n_{0}}}|\mathbf{x}).

(2)

In the following we prove that the above marginal likelihood is further equivalent to minimizing the loss in Section
3.5
.

3.2
From posterior probability maximization to the loss function

In existing methods
Bayesian_Model_Adaptation
;
Bayesian_Poisson
;
Low-Level_Features_and_Bayesian_Regression
, the posterior probability
of the number of objects is set to be independent, identically distributed (i.i.d.), Gaussian. Since the dimension of the
\bm{y}_{2^{n_{0}}}
is
4^{n_{0}}=2^{n_{0}}\times 2^{n_{0}}
, we additionally assumes that the pixels of
\bm{y}_{2^{n_{0}}}
are
i.i.d. Gaussian,

p(\bm{y}_{2^{n_{0}}}|\mathbf{x})\sim\mathcal{N}(\hat{\bm{y}}_{2^{n_{0}}},\sigma_{0}^{2}\mathbf{I}_{4^{n_{0}}}),

(3)

where
\sigma_{0}^{2}
is the parameter of Gaussian distribution,
\mathbf{I}_{4^{n_{0}}}
is a
4^{n_{0}}\times 4^{n_{0}}
identity matrix. When we make this assumption, the conventional single-resolution L2 loss function becomes a particular case of our posterior distribution that
\mathbb{N}=\{n_{k},L\}
and
p(\bm{y}|\bm{y}_{2^{n_{k}}},\mathbf{x})=p(\bm{y}|\bm{y}_{2^{n_{k}}})
.

We aim to learn the high-resolution density maps without destroying the lower-resolution maps as much as possible. In order to keep the low-density map unaffected, We define that the residual density map
\bm{r}_{{j_{1}},{j_{2}}}
,
\hat{\bm{r}}_{{j_{1}},{j_{2}}}
between two density maps with different resolutions
2^{j_{1}}
and
2^{j_{2}}
,

\displaystyle\bm{r}_{{j_{1}},{j_{2}}}

\displaystyle\overset{def}{=}\bm{y}_{2^{{j_{2}}}}-4^{{j_{1}}-{j_{2}}}\mathbf{U}(\bm{y}_{2^{{j_{1}}}},2^{j_{2}-j_{1}}),

(4)

\displaystyle\hat{\bm{r}}_{{j_{1}},{j_{2}}}

\displaystyle\overset{def}{=}\hat{\bm{y}}_{2^{{j_{2}}}}-4^{{j_{1}}-{j_{2}}}\mathbf{U}(\hat{\bm{y}}_{2^{j_{1}}},2^{j_{2}-j_{1}}),

(5)

where
j_{1}<j_{2}
,
\mathbf{U}(\cdot,2^{j_{2}-j_{1}})
is the replication upsampling function of scale
2^{j_{2}-j_{1}}
.
We assume that the residual maps
\bm{r}_{n_{j},n_{j+1}}
are generated from Gaussian distribution, which means

\displaystyle p(\bm{r}_{n_{j},n_{j+1}}|\mathbf{x})\sim\mathcal{N}(\hat{\bm{r}}_{n_{j},n_{j+1}},\sigma_{j+1}^{2}\mathbf{I}_{4^{n_{j+1}}}),

(6)

where
\sigma_{j+1}^{2}
is the variance parameter of the Gaussian distributions.
\bm{r}_{n_{j+1},n_{j}}
needs to satisfy the priors that
\mathbf{D}(r_{n_{j+1},n_{j}},2^{n_{j+1}-n_{j}})=\mathbf{0}_{4^{n_{j}}}
, where
\mathbf{0}_{4^{n_{j}}}
is the zero vector of length
4^{n_{j}}
,
\mathbf{D}(\cdot,2^{n_{j+1}-n_{j}})
is the average downsampling function of scale
2^{n_{j+1}-n_{j}}
. The conditional probability

p(\bm{y}_{2^{n_{j+1}}}|\bm{y}_{2^{n_{j}}},\mathbf{x})
has the following form,

\displaystyle p(\bm{y}_{2^{n_{j+1}}}|\bm{y}_{2^{n_{j}}},\mathbf{x})=p(\bm{r}_{{n_{j}},n_{j+1}}|\bm{y}_{2^{n_{j}}},\mathbf{x})

(7)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{p(\bm{r}_{{n_{j}},n_{j+1}}|,\mathbf{x})}{\int p(\bm{v}_{{n_{j+1}},n_{j}}|,\mathbf{x})\mathbb{I}(\mathbf{D}(\bm{v}_{{n_{j+1}},n_{j}})=\mathbf{0}_{4^{n_{j}}})d\bm{v}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\mathcal{N}(\hat{\bm{r}}_{n_{j},n_{j+1}},\sigma_{j+1}^{2}\mathbf{I}_{4^{n_{j+1}}})}{\mathcal{N}(\bm{0}_{4^{n_{j}}},4^{n_{j+1}-n_{j}}\sigma_{j+1}^{2}\mathbf{I}_{4^{n_{j}}})}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sqrt{\frac{4^{(n_{j}-n_{j+1})4^{n_{j}}}}{(2\pi\sigma_{j+1}^{2})^{4^{n_{j+1}-n_{j}}}}}\exp\{-\frac{1}{2\sigma_{j+1}^{2}}\|\hat{\bm{r}}_{n_{j},n_{j+1}}-\bm{r}_{n_{j},n_{j+1}}\|_{2}^{2}\},

where
\mathbb{I}(\cdot)
is the indicator function.

Combining Eq. (
1
)(
3
)(
7
), the marginal likelihood of the training data
D
equals to a linear combination of
\mathcal{L}_{diff}^{n_{j},n_{j+1}},j=0,\cdots,k-1
,and
\mathcal{L}_{2}^{n_{0}}
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{D}

\displaystyle\log p(\bm{y}|\mathbf{x},\mathbb{N})=\mathbb{E}_{D}\ \log p(\bm{y}|\bm{y}_{2^{n_{k}}},\mathbf{x})-

(8)

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}[\frac{1}{\sigma_{j+1}^{2}}\mathcal{L}_{diff}^{n_{j},n_{j+1}}+(4^{n_{j+1}}-4^{n_{j}})\log(2\pi\sigma_{j+1}^{2})

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle(n_{j+1}-n_{j})4^{n_{j}}\log 4]-\frac{1}{2}(\frac{1}{\sigma_{0}^{2}}\mathcal{L}_{2}^{n_{0}}+4^{n_{0}}\log(2\pi\sigma_{0}^{2})),

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{i}_{2}\overset{def}{=}\mathbb{E}_{D}\|\hat{\bm{y}}_{2^{i}}-\bm{y}_{2^{i}}\|_{2}^{2},

(11)

\displaystyle=4^{-n_{0}}\mathcal{L}_{2}^{n_{0}}.

Then taking it to Eq. (

\displaystyle\max\limits_{\bm{\sigma}}\mathbb{E}_{D}

\displaystyle\log p(\bm{y}|\mathbf{x},\mathbb{N})

Theorem 1

Let

\mathbb{N}^{\prime}=\{0,1,\cdots,n_{k}\}\cup\{L\}
. Then the log-likelihood function in case of
\mathbb{N}^{\prime}

\mathbb{N}

\max\limits_{\bm{\sigma}}\mathbb{E}_{D}\log p(\bm{y}|\mathbf{x},\mathbb{N})\leq\max\limits_{\bm{\sigma}}\mathbb{E}_{D}\log p(\bm{y}|\mathbf{x},\mathbb{N}^{\prime}).

\displaystyle\max\limits_{\bm{\sigma}}\mathbb{E}_{D}\log p(\bm{y}|\mathbf{x},\mathbb{N})=\mathbb{E}_{D}\ \log p(\bm{y}|\bm{y}_{2^{n}},\mathbf{x})-\dfrac{-1+2\pi}{2}4^{n}

(14)

3.4
Regularization

In the previous analysis, no additional restrictions are added to the variance
\sigma_{j}^{2}
. Therefore, we add penalty terms to regularize them to limit the range of variance,

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{reg}=\sum_{j=0}^{n}\beta_{j}\sigma_{j}^{2}.

(15)

Since this form is too complex and has too many parameters, we simplify it to one regular term
\mathcal{L}_{2}^{n}
by taking appropriate
\beta_{0},\cdots,\beta_{n}
.

In Eq. (
14
), the term
\log\mathcal{L}_{diff}^{n,n-1}
has the largest coefficient, which makes model learning mainly based on this resolution. Therefore, We want the model to learn by combining multiple resolutions.

Because the second two terms in Eq. (
14
) are constants, our loss function is obtained by re-weighting the coefficients of the rest of the equation. The process of re-weighting coefficients is equivalent to a weighted average of log-likelihood functions with different
n
. The formulaic representation is as follows,

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{n}\alpha_{k}[\sum_{j=1}^{k}(4^{j}-4^{j-1})\log\mathcal{L}_{diff}^{j-1,j}+\log\mathcal{L}_{2}^{0}]

(16)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\log\mathcal{L}_{2}^{0}+\sum_{j=1}^{n}\log\mathcal{L}_{diff}^{j-1,j},

where
\alpha_{k}
is the coefficient for the resolution of
2^{k}\times 2^{k}
and
\alpha_{0},\cdots,\alpha_{n}
are the solution of
(4^{j}-4^{j-1})\sum_{k=j}^{n}\alpha_{k}=1,\forall j\in\{1,\cdots,n\}
,
\sum_{k=0}^{n}\alpha_{k}=1
.

3.5
Progressive Multi-resolution Loss

The progressive multi-resolution loss is defined as

\mathcal{L}_{PML}=\log\mathcal{L}^{0}_{2}+\sum_{j=1}^{n}\log\mathcal{L}^{j-1,j}_{diff}.

(17)

When the regular term
\mathcal{L}_{2}^{L}
is added, the final loss function is defined by:

\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}_{PML}+\mathcal{L}_{2}^{L}.

(18)

Our proposed loss function is evaluated on four datasets of crowd people counting: ShanghaiTech A and B
zhang_single-image_2016
, UCF-QNRF
UCF-QNRF

During the training, we sequentially use the following data augmentation: Color Jitter, Rand Scaling (from 0.9 to 1.1), Random crop (to 1024x1024), Random Rotate (from 0 to 45), and Horizontal Flip (with the probability of 0.5). VGG16 is a standard pre-trained model for crowd counting, which uses only 3×3 convolution. We use VGG16 to extract the features and then use two 1×1 convolutional layers with ReLU activation functions as the prediction header.
CSRNet, designed for crowd counting with VGG16 as the backbone, employs four dilated convolution layers with 512, 256, 128, and 64 channels in the prediction head.
ResNet18 adds direct connection channels to the network, allowing information to skip a layer directly.
ConvNeXt is a pure convolution model that can compete with vision Transformers
vit

Swin
across multiple computer vision benchmarks. ConvNeXtS is a variant of ConvNeXt with 96, 192, 384, and 768 channels in the backbone.
We add the same counting head in CSRNet to ResNet18 and ConvNext to estimate the density maps.
HRNet is a backbone widely used in localization task . HRNet-W48 is its pre-trained model on ImageNet
imagenet

5
Conclusions

In this paper, we propose a simple and efficient loss function called PML for crowd counting. Our loss function, derived from the marginal likelihood, is proved to fit the posterior distribution better than L2 loss theoretically. By taking advantage of the multi-resolution density map, PML can learn to use multi-scale information to improve counting performance. We have demonstrated our loss function on four datasets with three common backbones in crowd counting with competitive results.
