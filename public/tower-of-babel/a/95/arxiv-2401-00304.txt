Tunneling estimates for two-dimensional perturbed magnetic Dirac systems
Esteban Cárdenas, Benjamín Pavez, Edgardo Stockmeyer

Abstract
. We prove tunneling estimates for two-dimensional Dirac systems which are localized in space due to the presence of a magnetic field. The Hamiltonian driving the motion admits the decomposition H = H 0 + W H=H_{0}+W , where H 0 H_{0} is a rotationally symmetric magnetic Dirac operator and W W is a position-dependent matrix-valued potential satisfying certain smoothness condition in the angular variable. A consequence of our results are upper bounds for the growth in time of the expected size of the system and its total angular momentum.

1.
Introduction

Consider a quantum system whose dynamics is driven by a self-adjoint Hamiltonian
H_{0}
on the
Hilbert space
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2},\mathbb{C}^{q})
for
q\in\{1,2\}
.
Assume that
H_{0}
is symmetric under rotations so that it can be decomposed in an orthogonal sum of operators acting on the corresponding angular momentum subspaces
i.e.

H_{0}\cong\bigoplus_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}h_{j}\,.

Let us assume further that the
h_{j}
’s have compact resolvent,
i.e.
discrete spectrum. In particular, the unperturbed system has certain localization properties in space which may or not be uniform in
j
.
We are interested in perturbing such systems and analyse the fate of certain dynamical variables that describe the size of the quantum system.

Let us consider a hermitian multiplication operator
W
,
possibly not invariant under rotations, that it is smooth in the angular variable and it is such that the operator sum

\displaystyle H=H_{0}+W\,,

is a well-defined self-adjoint operator.
According to the Schrödinger equation,
if
\varphi\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2},\mathbb{C}^{q})
is the initial state of the system at time

t=0
, then (in units where
\hbar=1
)

\displaystyle\varphi(t)=e^{-{\rm i}tH}\,\varphi\,,

is the quantum state at time
t>0
.
Let
\mathbb{P}_{I}(H)
denote the spectral projection of
H
onto a
bounded
energy interval
I\subset\mathbb{R}
. We are motivated by the study of the long time behavior of the expected radius of the system for finite energy initial data.
More precisely, the problem we are interested in consists
of understanding

\displaystyle R_{\varphi}(t):=\left\langle\varphi(t),|{\bf x}|\,\varphi(t)\right\rangle\,,\quad\varphi=\mathbb{P}_{I}(H)\varphi\,,\quad t\gg 1\,.

(1.1)

In certain cases,

apriori
knowledge on the spectrum of
H

is helpful.
For instance, when the spectrum is purely absolutely continuous one expects ballistic dynamics
i.e.

R_{\varphi}(t)\sim t
holds
[
11
]
. Another case is when the spectrum is a discrete set, where one can show that the dynamics is localized and, in particular, that
\sup_{t>0}R_{\varphi}(t)<+\infty
holds, at least for various physically relevant models.
The former cases correspond to when the spectral measure of
H

is absolutely continuous with respect to the Lebesgue measure and
a point measure, respectively.
However, generically, the spectral measure may behave
very differently from the former two cases and in such situations there is very little to say about (
1.1
).
Moreover, in view of the Weyl - von Neumann and the Wonderland Theorems (see
e.g.

[
6
]
and
[
7
,
10
]
),
the spectral quality of the system may be very unstable under perturbations.
Therefore, it is desirable to study the underlying dynamics of such systems without relying on the spectral quality of
H
.
Indeed, notice that even if all the
h_{j}
have compact resolvent,
it is unclear whether or not the same holds for

H_{0}
.
Hence, the problem on the behavior of
R_{\varphi}(t)
is non-trivial even for
W=0
.

An approach to address this problem
for the magnetic Schrödinger operator
was proposed by Hundertmark, Vugalter and two of the present authors in
[
3
]
.
In there,
H_{0}
is the Schrödinger operator on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2},\mathbb{C})
with
rotationally symmetric magnetic field

\textbf{B}({\bf x})=B(|{\bf x}|){\bf e}_{3}\qquad\textnormal{with}\qquad B(r)\sim r^{\alpha-1}\textnormal{ as }r\rightarrow\infty\ .

(1.2)

pointing orthogonal to the plane, for some
\alpha>0
.
The perturbation
W
is an electric potential,
assumed to be
decaying at infinity and to be infinitesimally

-\Delta
-bounded.
The results of the present paper cover
the situations considered in

[
3
]
analogous to the magnetic Dirac operator on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2},\mathbb{C}^{2})
.

The strategy in
[
3
]
is based upon two steps. Firstly, one shows certain tunneling estimates, describing the decay in the probability for the system to be localized in the classically forbidden region.
This allows in particular to estimate

R_{\varphi}(t)
in terms of the
expectation value
of some power of
the angular momentum operator
-i\partial_{\theta}
.
Secondly, one estimates the time behavior of the latter expectation using Heisenberg’s evolution equation and again the tunneling
estimates. It turns out that the faster
W
decays the better are the bounds on
R_{\varphi}(t)
.

Let us now turn to the model investigated in this work. Consider from now on
H_{0}
to be the Dirac operator on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2},\mathbb{C}^{2})
with
magnetic field (
1.2
).
Contrary to the non-relativistic case, a growing
electric potential
enhances the delocalization properties of a Dirac system. This can already be seen in the simple case when
H_{0}
is perturbed by a (well-behaved) rotationally symmetric electric potential
V
(
i.e.

W=V\otimes\textbf{1}_{2\times 2}
).
Let us further explain.
To this end, let

\displaystyle A(r)=\frac{1}{r}\int_{0}^{r}sB(s)\mathrm{d}s\,

(1.3)

be the corresponding magnetic vector potential.
Then, it is known
[
16
]
that the corresponding
operator
h_{j}+V
has compact resolvent if and only if

\displaystyle\mu_{*}:=\limsup_{|{\bf x}|\to\infty}|V(|{\bf x}|)/A(|{\bf x}|)|^{2}<1\,.

(1.4)

Since
A\sim|{\bf x}|^{\alpha}
the electric potential
V
is allowed to grow at infinity, but slower than
A
.
In fact, if

\mu_{*}=1

and
V>0
, the spectrum of
h_{j}+V
splits
as discrete on the positive and absolutely continuous on the negative real line
[
19
,
16
]
. Moreover, if
\mu_{\ast}>1
the spectrum of
h_{j}+V
is purely absolutely continuous
[
16
]
and behaves for large times in time average as
R_{\varphi}(t)\sim t

[
13
]
. Hence, the system has no localization property.

In this work we consider the Dirac system
H=H_{0}+W

where
W
is an hermitian
2\times 2
-matrix valued multiplication operator that is (see Condition
2
) locally in
L^{2}
, it is smooth in the angular variable, and it is allowed to grow at infinity as long as it is controlled by
A
.
We quantify the angular regularity of
W

with a parameter
\beta>0
.
In particular,
our condition on
W

is asymptotically sharp in the limit of maximal regularity.
More precisely,
for
\beta\rightarrow\infty
one recovers (
1.4
).
See Remark (
2.4.ii
)
for more details.

Our main result, Theorem
2.8
, provides the above mentioned tunneling estimates for
H
.
They state that for finite energy the system remains localized in a certain
classically allowed region.
The system has a different classically allowed region for each angular momentum channel
j\in\mathbb{Z}
,
determined by
h_{j}

and a reference energy level
E_{j}
.
We denote by

\mathfrak{C}=\big(\mathcal{C}_{j}(E_{j})\big)_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}

the collection of all these sets.
Mathematically,
it induces an

orthogonal projection

{\bm{\theta}}=\oplus_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}\theta_{j}

in the Hilbert space
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2},\mathbb{C}^{2}).

In particular, Theorem
2.8
shows exponential decay
away from the support of

{\bm{\theta}}
(see Corollary
1
).
An important consequence of Theorem
2.8
is that for any
\nu>0
one finds constants
c,C_{1},C_{2}>0
such that for any
\varphi=\mathbb{P}_{I}(H)\varphi

\displaystyle C_{1}\left\langle\varphi,|J|^{\nu/(1+\alpha)}\,\varphi\right\rangle-c\|\varphi\|^{2}\leqslant\left\langle\varphi,|{\bf x}|^{\nu}\,\varphi\right\rangle\leqslant C_{2}\left\langle\varphi,|J|^{\nu/(1+\alpha)}\,\varphi\right\rangle+c\|\varphi\|^{2}\,,

(1.5)

where
J=-i\partial_{\theta}+\frac{1}{2}\sigma_{3}

is the total angular momentum.
As for the dynamical applications, notice first that since the spectral projection commutes with time evolution, we get for all
t>0

\displaystyle R_{\varphi}(t)\leqslant C\big(\|\varphi\|^{2}+\left\langle\varphi(t),|J|^{1/(1+\alpha)}\,\varphi(t)\right\rangle\big)\,,\quad\varphi=\mathbb{P}_{I}(H)\varphi\,.

(1.6)

Equations (
1.5
) and (
1.6
) combined give a satisfactory answer to the problem when
W
is rotationally symmetric.
Indeed, in this case the time evolution operator commutes with
the angular momentum operator.
Consequently, we deduce
that
\sup_{t\geqslant 0}R_{\varphi}(t)<+\infty
holds, whenever
R_{\varphi}(0)
is finite.
If the symmetry is perturbed the situation is less clear and the problem deserves further investigation.
However, in
[
3
]
a general tendency
was shown.
Namely, if
W
is smooth and
decays at infinity,
it is proven that

R_{\varphi}(t)\lesssim t^{\epsilon}

where
the exponent
\epsilon>0
depends on the decay rate of
W
.
In particular, if
W
decays sufficiently fast,
one gets sub-ballistic dynamics
i.e.

\epsilon\in(0,1)
.
Moreover, for exponentially decaying
W
one gets that
R_{\varphi}(t)
grows at most
logarithmically
in time. These results can be replicated here using Theorem
2.8
as an essential ingredient.

Let us now describe the new elements in our analysis.
While our strategy for the proof of Theorem
2.8
follows the general lines of
[
3
]
,
there are two new major independent obstacles:

(1)

The Dirac operator
H
is not semi-bounded.
Let us recall that the heart of the proof of
[
3
]

consists of introducing a comparison operator
\widetilde{H}

that creates a spectral gap around zero of its Schrödinger counterpart. This is achieved by
adding a suitable operator that
shifts
the spectrum to the right.
Since
H
is not lower bounded,
the same naive operation fails and no spectral gap is created.
We overcome this by introducing a novel comparison operator, formally given by

\displaystyle\widetilde{H}=H+\sigma_{3}\bm{E}{\bm{\chi}}\ .

(1.7)

The operator

\sigma_{3}\bm{E}{\bm{\chi}}=\oplus_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}\,\sigma_{3}E_{j}\chi_{j}

acts a mass term that depends on the angular momentum
j\in\mathbb{Z}
under consideration.
In particular, it creates a suitable spectral gap around zero in the sense of Proposition
7.5
.
The sequence
(E_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}
corresponds to a collection of
carefully selected

reference energy levels
, prescribed in terms
of
W
and the energy interval
I
.
For each
j\in\mathbb{Z}
, the energy level
E_{j}
determines
an allowed region
\mathcal{C}_{j}(E_{j})\subset\mathbb{R}_{+}

on which each
\chi_{j}\sim\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{C}_{j}(E_{j})}

acts as a smooth localization function.
This is a novel adaptation of the comparison operators suggested for Schrödinger-like operators in
[
9
]
and may be of interest in its own right, even for constant
\bm{E}
.

(2)

The perturbation
W
is allowed to grow at infinity.
In particular,
W
may not be relatively bounded with respect to
H_{0}
.
We recover control of
the perturbation
by carefully analyzing its growth when projected onto the
classically allowed region
\mathfrak{C}
.
In particular, we prove in Theorem
5.1
that there are constants
a\in(0,1)
,
\overline{\mu}_{\ast}\in[\mu_{\ast},1)
, and
c>0
such that

\displaystyle\|W\varphi\|^{2}\leqslant a\|H_{0}\varphi\|^{2}+\overline{\mu}_{\ast}\|{\bm{\lambda\chi}}\varphi\|^{2}+c\|\varphi\|^{2}\ ,

(1.8)

for all
\varphi
on a suitable core.
The term in the middle quantifies the possible growth of
W
at infinity:
the operator
{\bm{\chi}}=\oplus_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}\chi_{j}

is the same as in (
1.7
)
and
\bm{\lambda}=\oplus_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}\lambda_{j}

is a sequence of multipliers
such that
\lambda_{j}\sim A(|j|^{1/(1+\alpha)})
for large
|j|
; see Definition
5
.
We select the energy levels such that
\overline{\mu}_{\ast}{\bm{\lambda}}^{2}<{\bm{E}}
.
In particular, if
W

decays at infinity,
then
\overline{\mu}_{\ast}=0
and we recover the usual relative operator bound inequality.
For this case, the
E_{j}
’s can be selected to be all equal to each other;
see Definition
6
.
This corresponds to the case considered in
[
3
]
with only one energy level.

Summarizing, in order to prove Theorem
2.8

we show exponential decay of
\mathbb{P}_{I}(H)

on the support of
(1-{\bm{\chi}})
.
We proceed in the spirit of
[
1
]
using an integral representation of the spectral projection.
The method works efficiently
in terms of the comparison operator
\widetilde{H}
which is a well-defined self-adjoint operator thanks to (
1.8
) and
satisfies

\mathbb{P}_{I}(\widetilde{H})=0
.

Organization of the paper.

In Section
2

we define the precise model that we study,
state our main results and discuss its consequences.
In Section
3
we collect some preliminary facts used throughout our analysis.
The analysis of the classically allowed region and the definition of the corresponding localization function
{\bm{\chi}}
is given in Section
4
.
This is then used in Section
5

to obtain bounds on
W
as in (
1.8
); see Theorem
5.1
.
In Section
6
we
define the energy levels and state
a somewhat abstract exponential decay result, Theorem
6.3
. Moreover, we show how that result implies Theorem
2.8
.
Finally, in Section
7
we show Theorem
6.3
. Most of the section is devoted to establish the main properties of the comparison operator
\widetilde{H}
. The main text of this article is followed by several appendices containing useful technical results.

2.
The model and main results

In this section we define the mathematical model that we study. We then state our main result in Theorem
2.8
and discuss its consequences.

2.1.
The model

We consider a massless Dirac system in a two-dimensional plane, under the influence of a
magnetic field pointing orthogonal to the plane

\textbf{B}({\bf x})=B({\bf x}){\bf e}_{3}
, with
{\bf e}_{3}=(0,0,1)^{\rm T}
. The Hamiltonian describing this system operates on the
Hilbert space

\mathcal{H}:=L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2},\mathbb{C}^{2})
and may be defined on
C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2},\mathbb{C}^{2})
acting as

H_{0}={\bm{\sigma}}\cdot(-\mathrm{i}\nabla-{\bf A})\,,

(2.1)

where

(\bm{\sigma},\sigma_{3})\equiv(\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2},\sigma_{3})
correspond to the standard Pauli matrices

\sigma_{1}=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}\,,\quad\sigma_{2}=\begin{pmatrix}0&-i\\
i&0\end{pmatrix}\,,\quad\sigma_{3}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&-1\end{pmatrix}\,,

and
{\bf A}=(A_{1},A_{2})^{{\rm T}}:\mathbb{R}^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2}
is a magnetic vector potential
satisfying
\partial_{1}A_{2}-\partial_{2}A_{1}=B
. If
B
is rotationally symmetric we fix the gauge to

{\bf A}({\bf x})=A(|{\bf x}|)\,\textbf{e}_{\theta}\qquad\textnormal{ and }\qquad A(r)=\frac{1}{r}\int_{0}^{r}sB(s)\mathrm{d}s\,.

(2.2)

(The vectors
\textbf{e}_{\theta}
and
\textbf{e}_{r}

are the usual orthonormal basis of
\mathbb{R}^{3}
in polar coordinates with
\textbf{e}_{r}\times\textbf{e}_{\theta}=\textbf{e}_{3}
).
Notice that in this gauge the magnetic vector potential is related to the magnetic flux
\Phi
on the centered ball
B_{r}(0)
of radius
r>0

given by

\displaystyle\Phi(r)=\int_{B_{r}(0)}\mathbf{B}\cdot{\mathrm{d}{\bf x}}\,,

(2.3)

through
\Phi(r)=2\pi rA(r)
.

In order to exploit the symmetry we identify
\mathcal{H}
with
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+})\otimes L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{1})^{2}
, where
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+})\equiv L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+},r\mathrm{d}r)
is equipped with the measure
r\mathrm{d}r
. We define
the
total angular momentum operator

J=\textbf{1}\otimes\left(-\mathrm{i}\partial_{\theta}+\frac{1}{2}\sigma_{3}\right)\quad\textnormal{on}\quad L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+})\otimes L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{1})^{2},

(2.4)

which is self-adjoint in its maximal domain. We denote by

p_{j}=(2\pi)^{-1}|e^{ij\theta}\rangle\langle e^{ij\theta}|

the projection from
L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{1})
to the space of orbital angular momentum
j\in\mathbb{Z}
.
The resolution of the identity for
J
is given by

\displaystyle J=\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}m_{j}P_{m_{j}}\,,\qquad\textnormal{where}\qquad m_{j}:=j+\frac{1}{2}

(2.5)

and where

P_{m_{j}}=\textbf{1}\otimes\begin{pmatrix}p_{j}&0\\
0&p_{j+1}\\
\end{pmatrix}\,.

(2.6)

The sequence of projections
\big(P_{m_{j}}\big)_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}
provide a decomposition
of the Hilbert space

\mathcal{H}\cong\bigoplus_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+},\mathbb{C}^{2})
in angular momentum subspaces.
Since
H_{0}
commutes with
J

[
18
, Chapter 7]
, the action of the Hamiltonian
H_{0}
is described on each angular momentum subspace as

H_{0}\cong\bigoplus_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}h_{j}\,,\qquad\mbox{where}\qquad h_{j}:=\begin{pmatrix}0&d_{j}^{*}\\
d_{j}&0\end{pmatrix}\,,

(2.7)

and

\displaystyle d_{j}:=\frac{d}{dr}-\frac{j}{r}+A(r)\,,\quad d_{j}^{*}:=-\frac{d}{dr}-\frac{(j+1)}{r}+A(r)\,.

(2.8)

Let us now state the assumptions of the magnetic field of the unperturbed problem. They are given in terms of the flux function (
2.3
) as follows.

Condition 1
.

The magnetic field
B\in L_{\rm loc}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2},\mathbb{R})
is
rotationally symmetric.
Further, there exist constants
\alpha>0
,
\Phi_{0}>0

and a function
\phi
, with
\phi(r)=o(r^{1+\alpha})
as
r\to\infty
,
such that

\displaystyle\Phi(r)\,=\,\Phi_{0}\,r^{\alpha+1}+\phi(r)\ ,\qquad r>0\ .

(2.9)

Remark 2.1
.

The operator
H_{0}
is essentially self-adjoint on

C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2},\mathbb{C}^{2})
and
we denote its closure by
H_{0}
as well. The following can be said about its spectrum (see
e.g.

[
18
]
):

(i)

If
\alpha>1
,
{\rm spec}(H_{0})
is purely discrete.

(ii)

If
\alpha=1
,
{\rm spec}(H_{0})
is a perturbation of Landau levels.

(iii)

if
0<\alpha<1
,
{\rm spec}(H_{0})
coincides with
\mathbb{R}
, is purely essential, and dense pure point.

We shall consider perturbations
W
of
H_{0}
that have general hermitian matrix structure given through multiplication by

\displaystyle W({\bf x})=\begin{pmatrix}w_{11}({\bf x})&w_{12}({\bf x})\\
w_{21}({\bf x})&w_{22}({\bf x})\end{pmatrix}\,,\qquad{\bf x}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\ .

(2.10)

Remark 2.2
.

Recall that the set
\{\mathds{1}_{2\times 2},\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2},\sigma_{3}\}
form a basis for the space of two-dimensional hermitian matrices.
Hence, the perturbation
W
may be written in the form

W({\bf x})\ =\ \,V({\bf x})\otimes{\textbf{1}}_{2\times 2}\ +\ \bm{\sigma}\cdot\textbf{{a}}({\bf x})\ +\ \sigma_{3}m({\bf x})\ ,\qquad{\bf x}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}

(2.11)

for some functions
V:\mathbb{R}^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
,
\textbf{{a}}:\mathbb{R}^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2}
and
m:\mathbb{R}^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
.
From a physical point of view,
V
corresponds to an electric potential,
a
corresponds to a magnetic vector potential and
m
corresponds to a position-dependent mass term also known as Lorentz scalar potential.

Let us now state the precise conditions
that we impose on the perturbation
W
.
To this end, let us first introduce
the matrix-valued function on
\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{Z}

\widetilde{W}(r,j):=\begin{pmatrix}\widehat{w}_{11}(r,j)&\widehat{w}_{12}(r,j-1)\\
\widehat{w}_{21}(r,j+1)&\widehat{w}_{22}(r,j)\end{pmatrix}\ ,

(2.12)

where for

w\in L^{1}_{\textnormal{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{2},\mathbb{C})

we write its
Fourier coefficients as

\widehat{w}(r,j):=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{0}^{2\pi}w(r\textbf{e}_{r})e^{-\mathrm{i}j\theta}\mathrm{d}\theta.

Essentially, the function just defined is the Fourier transform of the map
\theta\mapsto{W}(r,\theta)

and the additional
\pm 1
account for the spin angular momentum,
introduced in (
2.5
).
In particular, we note that for
\varphi,\psi\in\mathcal{H}
smooth enough there holds

\left\langle\varphi,W\psi\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}\ =\ \sum_{j,k}\,\langle\varphi_{j},\widetilde{W}(\cdot,j-k)\psi_{k}\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+},\mathbb{C}^{2})},

(2.13)

where
\varphi_{j}\equiv P_{m_{j}}\varphi
and
\psi_{k}\equiv P_{m_{k}}\psi

are regarded as vectors in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+},\mathbb{C}^{2})
.

Notation 2.3
.

Given a matrix
A\in\mathbb{C}^{2}\times\mathbb{C}^{2}
,

\|A\|_{2\times 2}
denotes
its operator norm in
\mathbb{C}^{2}
with respect to
the usual inner product
(x,y)=\sum_{i=1}^{2}\overline{x_{i}}y_{i}
.
Hence,
|(x,Ay)|\leqslant\|A\|_{2\times 2}|x||y|
.

Condition 2
.

W\in L^{2}_{\textnormal{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{2},\mathbb{C}^{2}\times\mathbb{C}^{2})

is a hermitian matrix-valued multiplication operator.
Further, there exist
\beta>0
,
\mu_{\ast}\in[0,1)

and

v\in L_{\textnormal{loc}}^{2}([0,\infty),\mathbb{R})

such that:

(a)

(Smoothness in
\theta
). For all
r>0
and
j\in\mathbb{Z}
we have

\|\widetilde{W}(r,j)\|_{2\times 2}\leqslant v(r)e^{-\beta|j|}\,.

(b)

(Control by
A
at infinity).

\displaystyle{\limsup_{r\to\infty}}\,\,\,\left|\frac{\coth^{2}(\beta/4)v^{2}(r)}{A^{2}(r)}\right|=\mu_{\ast}<1\,.

(2.14)

Remark 2.4
.

(i)

In view of Paley–Wiener Theorem, Condition
2

(a)
corresponds to uniform real-analiticity. It is reminiscent from
the works on Gaussian-like decay for eigenfunctions of the perturbed Landau Hamiltonian initiated in
[
8
]
and reinterpreted in
[
14
]
. As noted in
[
3
]
it can be improved to Gevrey regularity, in that case the constant
\coth(\beta/4)
should be replaced by one given in terms of the Gevrey scale.

(ii)

The case
\beta=\infty
is allowed
and corresponds to the limit of maximal regularity.
That is, when

W
is rotationally symmetric.
In this situation,
the condition
(b)
is sharp:
for
\mu_{*}\geqslant 1
absolutely continuous spectrum may appear,
and
no localization properties can be expected (see the discussion
in the Introduction).

In Appendix
C
(see Remark
C.2
) we show that under the stated conditions on the fields
B
and
W

\displaystyle H=H_{0}+W\,,

(2.15)

is essentially self-adjoint on
C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2},\mathbb{C}^{2})
.
We denote its closure by the same symbol and its domain by
\mathcal{D}(H)
.

Remark 2.5
.

The spectrum of
H
undergoes through several regimes under the stated conditions. In particular, if
W
is an electric potential that grows at infinity it is known that the resolvent of
H
is compact if

\limsup_{|{\bf x}|\to\infty}W^{2}/(2B)<1
.
However, if this inequality does not hold, essential spectrum may appear (see
[
12
]
). (Notice that
B\ll A^{2}
for large
r
and compare with (
2.14
).)
In particular, if
V^{2}/B\to\infty
as
r\to\infty
one can construct examples where the spectrum of
H_{0}+V
covers the whole real line with point spectrum
[
12
, Example 1]
.
We believe that if the symmetry of this system is
slightly perturbed singular continuous spectrum should appear.

2.2.
Main result

Notation 2.6
.

We often write for the inverse of the flux exponent

\sigma=\frac{1}{1+\alpha}\,.

Notation 2.7
.

Throughout this work we denote by
\mathds{1}_{I}
the indicator function on a set
I\subset\mathbb{R}

2.3.
Consequences of the main result

Consider the setting of Theorem
2.8
. In particular, throughout this section, let
I=[-E,E]
, for some
E>0
, be a fixed energy interval.
We define the following operator
on
\mathcal{H}

implementing an
orthogonal projection
onto the classically allowed region

\displaystyle{\bm{\theta}}:=\oplus_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}\theta_{j},\quad\mbox{where}\quad\theta_{j}(r):=\mathds{1}_{(C_{1}|j|^{\sigma},C_{2}\left\langle j\right\rangle^{\sigma})}(r),\quad r>0\,.

(2.20)

The following result is a rather direct consequence of Theorem
2.8
.

Corollary 1
.

There are constant
\xi_{1},\xi_{2}>0
such that

\displaystyle(1-{\bm{\theta}}){\rm exp}(\xi_{1}|{\bf x}|^{\alpha+1})\mathbb{P}_{I}(H)\,\quad\mbox{and}\quad(1-{\bm{\theta}}){\rm exp}(\xi_{2}|J|)\mathbb{P}_{I}(H)

(2.21)

are bounded operators on
\mathcal{H}
.

Proof.

For a normalized
\varphi\in\mathcal{H}
, by
Parseval’s identity, we have that, for
\xi_{1}>0
,

\begin{split}\big\|(1-{\bm{\theta}}){\rm exp}(\xi_{1}|{\bf x}|^{\alpha+1})\mathbb{P}_{I}(H)\varphi\big\|^{2}&=\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}\big\|(1-\theta_{j}(r))e^{\xi_{1}r^{\alpha+1}}P_{m_{j}}\mathbb{P}_{I}(H)\varphi\big\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+},\mathbb{C}^{2})}^{2}\\
&\leqslant\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}\big\|(1-\theta_{j}(r))e^{\xi_{1}r^{\alpha+1}}P_{m_{j}}\mathbb{P}_{I}(H)\big\|^{2}\,.\end{split}

(2.22)

Recall that
1-\theta_{j}=\mathds{1}_{[0,C_{1}|j|^{\sigma}]}+\mathds{1}_{[C_{2}\left\langle j\right\rangle^{\sigma},\infty)}
. Moreover, for
r\in[0,C_{1}|j|^{\sigma}]

we have that

e^{\xi_{1}r^{\alpha+1}}\leqslant e^{\xi_{1}C_{1}|j|}
holds. Hence, the right hand side of (
2.22
) is finite thanks to
(
2.17
) and
(
2.18
) provided
0<\xi_{1}\leqslant\min\{C_{1}^{1+\alpha}\zeta_{1},\zeta_{2}\}
. This shows that the first operator in (
2.21
) is bounded.The second case can be dealt with analogously.
For a normalized
\varphi\in\mathcal{H}
and a constant
\xi_{2}>0
, we have

\begin{split}\big\|(1-{\bm{\theta}}){\rm exp}(\xi_{2}|J|)\mathbb{P}_{I}(H)\big\|^{2}&\leqslant\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}\big\|(1-\theta_{j}(r))e^{\xi_{2}|m_{j}|}P_{m_{j}}\mathbb{P}_{I}(H)\big\|^{2}\\
&\leqslant e^{\xi_{2}}\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}\big\|(1-\theta_{j}(r))e^{\xi_{2}|j|}P_{m_{j}}\mathbb{P}_{I}(H)\big\|^{2}\,.\end{split}

(2.23)

In view of (
2.17
) we control the case when

r\in[0,C_{1}|j|^{\sigma}]

we pick
\xi_{2}\leqslant\zeta_{1}
. Moreover, for the case
r\in[C_{2}\left\langle j\right\rangle^{\sigma},\infty)
, since
|j|<\left\langle j\right\rangle\leqslant(r/C_{2})^{1+\alpha}
, it is controlled if
\xi_{2}\leqslant\zeta_{2}C_{2}^{1+\alpha}
thanks to (
2.18
). This shows the claim for the second operator in (
2.21
) and finishes the proof.
∎

The next statement is a straightforward application of the latter result.

Corollary 2
.

Let
f:(0,\infty)\to(0,\infty)
be a non-decreasing function such that, for any
\varepsilon>0
,
\sup_{r>0}f(r)e^{-\varepsilon{r^{\alpha+1}}}
is finite. Then, there are constants
c,C>0
, such that, for all
\varphi\in\mathbb{P}_{I}(H)\mathcal{H}
,

\displaystyle\left\langle\varphi,{\bm{\theta}}f(c\left\langle J\right\rangle^{\sigma}){\bm{\theta}}\varphi\right\rangle+\left\langle\varphi,K_{f}\varphi\right\rangle\leqslant\left\langle\varphi,f(|{\bf x}|)\varphi\right\rangle\leqslant\left\langle\varphi,{\bm{\theta}}f(C\left\langle J\right\rangle^{\sigma}){\bm{\theta}}\varphi\right\rangle+\left\langle\varphi,K_{f}\varphi\right\rangle\,,

where
K_{f}=(1-{\bm{\theta}})f(|{\bf x}|)\mathbb{P}_{I}(H)
is a bounded operator on
\mathcal{H}
.

Proof.

First note that for all
\varphi\in\mathbb{P}_{I}(H)\mathcal{H}
we have that
\left\langle\varphi,f(|{\bf x}|)\varphi\right\rangle=\left\langle\varphi,{\bm{\theta}}f(|{\bf x}|){\bm{\theta}}\varphi\right\rangle+\left\langle\varphi,K_{f}\varphi\right\rangle
, where
K_{f}
is by Corollary
1
a bounded operator. Moreover, using the monotonicity of
f
and the support properties of
{\bm{\theta}}
we compute, denoting
\varphi_{j}=P_{m_{j}}\varphi
,

\displaystyle\left\langle\varphi,{\bm{\theta}}f(|{\bf x}|){\bm{\theta}}\varphi\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}\left\langle\varphi_{j},\theta_{j}f(r)\theta_{j}\varphi_{j}\right\rangle\leqslant\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}\left\langle\varphi_{j},\theta_{j}f(C_{2}\left\langle j\right\rangle^{\sigma})\theta_{j}\varphi_{j}\right\rangle

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}\left\langle\varphi_{j},\theta_{j}f(C\left\langle m_{j}\right\rangle^{\sigma})\theta_{j}\varphi_{j}\right\rangle=\left\langle\varphi,{\bm{\theta}}f(C\left\langle J\right\rangle^{\sigma}){\bm{\theta}}\varphi\right\rangle\,,

for some positive constant
C
. This gives the upper bound. For the lower bound one argues analogously. This finishes the proof.
∎

A special case of the above, with applications to the questions posed in the introduction, is contained in the following result. Its proof uses only Corollary
2
and the fact that spectral projections commute with time evolution.

Corollary 3
.

For any
\nu>0
there exists

such that for all initial states

\varphi\in\mathbb{P}_{I}(H)\mathcal{H}
it holds that

\left\langle\varphi(t),|{\bf x}|^{\nu}\varphi(t)\right\rangle\leqslant c\left\langle\varphi(t),|J|^{\nu\sigma}\varphi(t)\right\rangle+C\|\varphi\|^{2}\ ,\qquad\forall t\in\mathbb{R}\ .

(2.24)

Here
\varphi(t)=e^{-iHt}\varphi
.

Remark 2.12
.

It follows that,
whenever the Hamiltonian (or, its corresponding time evolution) commutes with total angular momentum, there holds

\displaystyle\left\langle\varphi(t),|{\bf x}|^{\nu}\varphi(t)\right\rangle\leqslant C\Big(\|\varphi\|^{2}+\left\langle\varphi,|J|^{\nu\sigma}\varphi\right\rangle\Big)\ .

(2.25)

In particular, the system is dynamically localized if the initial data

. This was previously observed in
[
2
]
.

As mentioned before, Theorem

contains tunneling estimates for the Dirac operator which are analogous to those for its

[

.
In that work, it is shown – starting from these tunneling estimates – how to obtain dynamical bounds
for the angular momentum operators.
This proof can be replicated almost without changes in our setting and we shall omit it here; we refer the reader to

Corollary 4

(Dynamical bounds for
J
)
.

Let
\mu>0
,

,
\eta>0

(1)

Assume that

\left\langle\varphi(t),|J|^{\mu}\varphi(t)\right\rangle\leqslant C\Big(\left\langle\varphi,|J|^{\mu}\varphi\right\rangle+t^{\mu\theta}\|\varphi\|^{2}\Big)\ ,\qquad\forall t\geqslant 1

3.
Preliminaries

In this section, we record some preliminary facts that
we will need in the rest of this article.

3.1.
Unitary transformations and Hilbert spaces

In the introductory section,
we have already mentioned the relationship
between the rotationally symmetric Dirac operator
H_{0}
,
and its block decomposition
\oplus_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}h_{j}
.
Let us record here the unitary transformations that implement this relationship.
While we will not display them explicitly in the main body of this work,
they are relevant in the proofs of essential self-adjointess of certain operators
provided in Appendix
B
.

First, we recall that in Section
1

we have introduced
the Hilbert space of polar coordinates

L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+},\mathbb{C}^{2})
, always assumed
to be equipped with the measure
r\mathrm{d}r
.
We shall be extensively working with
the
direct sum of its copies

\widehat{\mathcal{H}}\equiv\bigoplus_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+},\mathbb{C}^{2})\ .

(3.1)

Let us now further explain the relationship
between
H_{0}

and its diagonal decomposition on the Hilbert space

\widehat{\mathcal{H}}
.
To this end, we introduce a modified Fourier transform.
Namely,
let us denote by

\mathcal{F}_{0}:L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{1})\rightarrow\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z})

the standard Fourier series.
Then, we consider
the unitary transformation

\mathcal{F}\equiv\textbf{1}\otimes\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&i\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\mathcal{F}_{0}&0\\
0&\mathcal{F}_{0}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&e^{-i\theta}\end{pmatrix}\ :\ L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{S}^{1},\mathbb{C}^{2})\,\rightarrow\,\widehat{\mathcal{H}}\ .

(3.2)

This map is usually found in the literature
in the form

(\mathcal{F}\varphi)_{j}(r)=(\widehat{\varphi}^{1}_{j}(r),i\widehat{\varphi}^{2}_{j+1}(r))

where

\varphi=(\varphi^{1},\varphi^{2})\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{S}^{1},\mathbb{C}^{2}).

Secondly, we consider the unitary transformation that implements the change of variables from cartesian to polar coordinates in the Hilbert space at hand.
Namely,

\mathcal{U}:\mathcal{H}=L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2},\mathbb{C}^{2})\longrightarrow L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{S}^{1},\mathbb{C}^{2})\ ,

(3.3)

which is defined almost everywhere by
(\mathcal{U}\varphi)(r,\theta)\equiv\varphi(r\cos\theta,r\sin\theta)

for

(r,\theta)\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{S}^{1}
.
Thus, in terms of
\mathcal{F}\mathcal{U}:\mathcal{H}\rightarrow\widehat{\mathcal{H}}
we have that

(\mathcal{F}\mathcal{U})H_{0}(\mathcal{F}\mathcal{U})^{-1}=\bigoplus_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}h_{j}\ .

(3.4)

Throughout this article, we will often losen up the notation and omit the implemented unitary transformations.
That is, unless confusion arises,
we simply write

\textstyle H_{0}=\oplus_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}h_{j}

and similarly
\varphi=(\varphi_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}
.

Notation for the direct sum of operators
.
Let
\{\mathcal{O}_{j}\}_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}

be a sequence of linear operators
acting on

L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+},\mathbb{C}^{2})
.
Unless confusion arises, we denote its direct sum using bold-face notation

\bm{\mathcal{O}}\equiv\bigoplus_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}\mathcal{O}_{j}\ ,

(3.5)

always defined in the maximal domain

\mathcal{D}(\bm{\mathcal{O}})=\bigoplus_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{O}_{j})
.
In this notation
H_{0}\cong\bm{h}
.

3.2.
Essential self-adjointness

Let us recall that

C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2},\mathbb{C}^{2})

is an operator core for both
H_{0}
and
H
.
Since we will mostly work in polar coordinates, we find it convenient to work with a space of smooth functions with additional properties near
r=0
.
Unfortunately, contrary to the three-dimensional case,
the space
C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2}\backslash\{0\},\mathbb{C}^{2})
is not a domain core for
H_{0}
.
Instead, we will work with a space of smooth functions that–together with all its derivatives–converge polinomially fast to zero, with an arbitrarily high power.

This space is introduced in the following definition,
together with a
radial version
,
and its version with an

angular momentum cut-off
.

Definition 1

(Domain cores)
.

We introduce the following spaces.

(1)

As
a subspace of
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2},\mathbb{C}^{2})
, we define

\mathscr{C}\equiv\big\{\varphi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2},\mathbb{C}^{2}):\forall\alpha\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{2},\ \forall N\in\mathbb{N},|D^{\alpha}\varphi({\bf x})|=\mathcal{O}(|{\bf x}|^{N})\textnormal{ as }{\bf x}\rightarrow 0\big\}\ .

(3.6)

(2)

As a subspace of

L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+},\mathbb{C}^{2})
, we define

\mathscr{C}_{\mathrm{r}}\equiv C_{c}\big([0,\infty),\mathbb{C}^{2}\big)\!\cap\!\{\psi\in C^{\infty}(0,\infty):\forall N\in\mathbb{N},\forall k\in\mathbb{N}_{0},\ |\psi^{(k)}(r)|=\mathcal{O}(r^{N})\textnormal{ as }r\downarrow 0\}.

(3.7)

(3)

As a subspace of
\widehat{\mathcal{H}}=\oplus_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+},\mathbb{C}^{2})
, we define

\textstyle\mathcal{C}\equiv\big\{(\varphi_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}\in\bigoplus_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}\mathscr{C}_{\mathrm{r}}:\exists N\in\mathbb{N}:\varphi_{j}=0\textnormal{ for }|j|>N\big\}.

(3.8)

We shall often abuse notation and refer to any element
\psi

of either of these spaces as a
smooth function
.

The most important results concerning these spaces are recorded in the following Proposition, which could be
of independent interest to some readers.
To the authors best knowledge, the result is new.
For a proof, see Appendix
B
.

Proposition 3.1

(Essential self-adjointness)
.

The following statements hold true .

(1)

H_{0}|_{\mathscr{C}}
and
H|_{\mathscr{C}}
are essentially self-adjoint.

(2)

h_{j}|_{\mathscr{C}_{r}}
is essentially self-adjoint for all
j\in\mathbb{Z}
.

(3)

{H}_{0}|_{\mathcal{C}}

is essentially self-adjoint.

Unless stated otherwise,
we make no distinction in notation between an essentially self-adjoint operator
A
and its self-adjoint closure
\overline{A}
.
\mathcal{D}(A)

always stands for its domain of self-adjointness.

3.3.
The Pauli operator

An essential element in our approach
is the fact that the square of
H_{0}
is given by
the Pauli operator.
That is (at least formally) the following holds true

H_{0}^{2}=(-i\nabla-{\bf A})^{2}+\sigma_{3}B\,,

(3.9)

where
\sigma_{3}
is the third Pauli matrix.
Of course, the Pauli operator is rotationally symmetric and can be decomposed into blocks
of constant angular momentum

H_{0}^{2}=\oplus_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}h_{j}^{2}
,
where the operators
h_{j}
were
first introduced in (
2.7
) (see
e.g.

[
18
]
).
In particular, these blocks
can be put in the convenient form,
which we shall be using the rest of the article.
More precisely, it holds true in the sense of quadratic forms on

\mathscr{C}_{r}

that

h_{j}^{2}=T+V_{j}\qquad\textnormal{ where }\qquad V_{j}\equiv\begin{pmatrix}V_{j}^{+}&0\\
0&V_{j}^{-}\end{pmatrix}\quad\textnormal{and}\quad T\equiv-\bigg(\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}}{\mathrm{d}^{2}r}+\frac{1}{r}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}r}\bigg)\ .

(3.10)

Here, the operator

T\geqslant 0

corresponds to the
radial
kinetic energy
, and

V_{j}^{\pm}(r):=\Big(\frac{m_{j}\pm 1/2}{r}-A(r)\Big)^{2}\pm B(r)

(3.11)

corresponds to the
effective potentials
.

It will be crucial in our techniques to study
the spatial regions in which a
classical particle could be found, if moving under the influence of the effective potentials.
More precisely, we work extensively with the following collection of sets.

Definition 2
.

Let
j\in\mathbb{Z}
.
Then,
given
E_{j}>0
,
we define the
following set
as the
classically
allowed region
(AR) of angular momentum
j

\mathcal{C}_{j}(E_{j})\equiv\left\{r>0\,:\,V_{j}(r)\leqslant E_{j}^{2}\right\}\,.

(3.12)

Similarly, we define its complement as the
classically
forbidden region
(FR)

\mathcal{C}_{j}(E_{j})^{\perp}\equiv(0,\infty)\backslash\mathcal{C}_{j}(E_{j})\,.

(3.13)

We refer to
E_{j}
as the reference energy level of angular momentum
j\in\mathbb{Z}
.
For the full classically allowed region we write

\mathfrak{C}=\Big(\mathcal{C}_{j}(E_{j})\Big)_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}\ .

(3.14)

Remark 3.2
.

Strictly speaking
the classical character of these regions is directly associated to the Hamiltonian
h_{j}^{2}
.
In this sense, one may understand
\mathcal{C}_{j}
as the energetically allowed region of the system with Hamiltonian
h_{j}
, with energies in the range
[-E_{j},E_{j}]
.

Remark 3.3
.

In the above definition, the inequality
V_{j}(r)\leqslant E_{j}^{2}
is understood in the sense of quadratic forms on
\mathbb{C}^{2}
.
Since the effective potential is a diagonal matrix,
this is equivalent to the inequalities for its components

V_{j}^{\pm}(r)\leqslant E_{j}^{2}
.

4.
Analysis of the clasically allowed regions

In this section,
we analyze the clasically allowed regions given in Definition
2
.
In particular, in view of Remark
2.11
,
we consider only
A(r)=r^{\alpha}
.
This analysis consists of two parts:

First, in Subsection
4.1
we characterize the scale in which the classically allowed regions can be found.
Namely,
if the energy levels grow at most like
E_{j}=\mu|j|^{2\alpha\sigma}

for some
\mu\in(0,1)
as
|j|\rightarrow\infty
,
we show that

\mathcal{C}_{j}(E_{j})\subset[\delta|j|^{\sigma},c|j|^{\sigma}]\qquad\textnormal{where}\qquad\sigma=\frac{1}{1+\alpha}\ ,

(4.1)

for large enough
|j|
.
Here,
\delta
and

c
are appropiately chosen positive parameters,
independent of
j\in\mathbb{Z}
.
We refer to (
4.1
) as the
localization
of the
classically allowed regions.
This is accomplished by proving lower bounds for the effective potentials
V_{j}
when localized away from
\mathcal{C}_{j}(E_{j})
(see Lemma
4.1
).

Second, in Subsection
4.2

we introduce
a smooth version of the projections

\mathds{1}_{[\delta|j|^{\sigma},c|j|^{\sigma}]}
,
which should be understood as smooth localization functions around the ARs.
More precisely,
for each
j\in\mathbb{Z}
we consider
a pair
(\chi_{j},\chi_{j}^{\perp})
of smooth functions on
(0,\infty)
that are a partition of unity, in the sense that

\chi_{j}^{2}+(\chi_{j}^{\perp})^{2}=1\ .

(4.2)

The function
\chi_{j}

equals
1
on
\mathcal{C}_{j}(E_{j})
, and decays smoothly otherwise.
Similarly,
\chi_{j}^{\perp}
is smooth and
localized in the FRs.
In particular, the pair
(\chi_{j},\chi_{j}^{\perp})

is constructed so that its derivatives can be chosen to be as small as desired.
Most importantly, the lower bounds for the effective potentials proven in Subsection
4.1
can be recast in terms
of
\chi^{\perp}_{j}
.
This is the content of Proposition
4.5
.

4.1.
Localizing the ARs

In this subsection, we localize the classically allowed regions, asymptotically as
|j|\rightarrow\infty
.
In order to do so, in the upcoming lemma,
we prove lower bounds for the effective potentials
outside of an interval of the form
[\delta|j|^{\sigma},c\left\langle j\right\rangle^{\sigma}]
.

We remind the reader that

4.2.
Partitions of unity

In this subsection, we construct two pairs of sequences of
smooth localization functions.
The first one we denote by
(\chi_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}
,
and can be though of as a sequence of localization functions around the
AR.
The second one is denoted by
(\eta_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}
,
and should be thought of as a technical enlargement of
{\bm{\chi}}
.
In particular,
given
\varepsilon_{0}>0
,
the construction can be made so that
their derivatives are no larger than
\varepsilon_{0}
.

Let us note that the localization functions can be understood
as a smooth version of
the projection operators (
i.e.
sharp cut-offs)
first
considered in the Schrödinger case
[
3
]
.
In the present case, smoothness is required since we study the square of the original Hamiltonian
H_{0}
.
This has the effect of incorporating commutator (
i.e.
derivatives) into the analysis.

The construction starts as follows.
First, given two positive constants

a<b

we let let

g_{a,b}\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}_{+},[0,1])

be a smooth function of compact support that satisfies

\displaystyle g_{a,b}(r)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}0\,,&r\in(0,a/2)\\
\mbox{ monotone}\,,&r\in[a/2,a]\\
1\,,&r\in(a,b)\\
\mbox{ monotone}\,,&r\in[b,2b)\\
0\,,&r\in[2b,\infty)\end{array}\right.\,.

In particular,
we assume that
it has been constructed in such a way so that:

(i)

its complementary function

g_{a,b}^{\perp}\equiv\big(1-g_{a,b}^{2}\big)^{1/2}

is smooth as well; and

(ii)

\|g^{\prime}_{a,b}\|_{L^{\infty}},\ \|(g^{\perp}_{a,b})^{\prime}\|_{L^{\infty}}\leqslant C_{*}/a

for some
C_{*}>2
,
which we shall refer to as the
universal constant
.

Observe that
\textnormal{supp}(g_{a,b}^{\perp})
has two connected components:
one of them is supported on
[0,a]
,
and the second one on
[b,\infty)
.

Secondly, for technical reasons,
we need to introduce another family of smooth functions as follows.
We define

h_{a,b}\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}_{+},[0,1])

to be such that

\displaystyle h_{a,b}(r)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}1\,,&r\in(0,b)\\
g_{a,b}(r)\,,&r\in[b,\infty)\end{array}\right.\,.

Let us observe that the construction can be made so that:
its associated complementary function

h_{a,b}^{\perp}
is smooth as well;
the analogous derivative bound holds true

\|h_{a,b}^{\prime}\|\leqslant C_{*}/b
;
and
\textnormal{supp}(h_{a,b})

has only one connected component.

We now define the sequences of smooth localization
functions as follows.

Definition 4
.

Let
\delta\in(0,1)
,
c\geqslant 1

and

j_{0}\geqslant 2
.
We refer
to the following two
sequences of functions
as the

,
constructed with respect to
(\delta,c,j_{0})

(i)

For all
j\in\mathbb{Z}
, we
define

as the sequence of functions on
(0,\infty)

\displaystyle\eta_{j}(r)\equiv\begin{cases}h_{\delta,c}\Big(r/\left\langle j\right\rangle^{\sigma}\Big)\,,&|j|\leqslant j_{0}\\

5.
Relative bounds for the perturbation

Under the assumptions of Condition
2

the operator

is possibly unbounded at infinity.
Hence,
one may naturally ask if the operator

is able to dominate such perturbations.
It turns out that – in the general case – this is not possible.
Heuristically, this follows from the fact that
the magnetic Hamiltonian

vanishes in phase-space points where

From this equation one may extract a path to infinity in which

diverges.

In this section

plus an additional term, localized over the classically allowed regions, that is diagonal in the angular momentum channels.
This is the content of Theorem

.

C_{\varepsilon_{\ast}}>0
such that

\displaystyle\mathds{1}_{[R_{0},\infty)}\omega^{2}\leqslant\varepsilon_{\ast}A^{2}+C_{\varepsilon_{\ast}}\,.

(5.9)

On the other hand,
since
A\in L^{2}_{\rm loc}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
we have, by (
5.2
), that
\omega
belongs to the same space.
Therefore, by Lemma
C.1

we have that, for all
\varepsilon_{\rm l}\in(0,1)
, there is a constant
C_{\varepsilon_{\rm l}}>0
such that

\displaystyle\mathds{1}_{[0,R_{0}]}\omega^{2}\leqslant\frac{\varepsilon_{\rm l}}{\mu}H_{0}^{2}+C_{\varepsilon_{\rm l}}\,.

(5.10)

Finally, we consider the decomposition

\omega^{2}=\mathds{1}_{[R_{0},\infty)}\omega^{2}+\mathds{1}_{[0,R_{0}]}\omega^{2}
and
use (
5.9
), (
5.10
)
as well as Lemma
5.3

to conclude that

\displaystyle\omega^{2}\ \leqslant\ \varepsilon_{*}A^{2}+\frac{\varepsilon_{1}}{\mu}H_{0}^{2}+C_{\varepsilon_{1}}+C_{\varepsilon_{\ast}}\ \leqslant\ \frac{(\varepsilon_{*}+\varepsilon_{1})}{\mu}H_{0}^{2}+\varepsilon_{*}\bm{\chi}\bm{\lambda}^{2}\bm{\chi}+C

for a constant
C>0
. The last statement follows since if
\limsup_{r\to\infty}|\omega(r)|=0
we may take
\varepsilon_{\ast}=0
in (
5.9
).
This finishes the proof.
∎

6.
Exponential decay and the proof of Theorem

In this section we state an abstract result

\mathbb{P}_{I}(H)

away from the the support of

or, in other words,
away from the classically forbidden regions.
This is the content of Theorem

,
and its proof is postponed to the next section.
Here, we focus on how our main result, Theorem

Without loss of generality we consider

6.1.
Exponential weights

In order to quantify exponential decay we introduce an appropriate class of operators playing the role of
exponential weights

To this end, we consider the following space of sequences of real-valued, radial functions

\mathcal{W}:=\big\{\,F=(F_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}\ \big|\ F_{j}\in W^{1,2}_{\textnormal{loc}}\big((0,\infty),\mathbb{R}\big)\ \forall j\in\mathbb{Z}\,\big\}\ .

(6.1)

To any

\mathcal{H}

which we define on their respective maximal dense domains

Both operators are self-adjoint, positive and inverses of each other.
In addition, we define the space of

Note that for bounded weights, the associated exponential operators

7.1.
Strategy of the proof

The main goal of this section is the proof of Theorem

9
]
(see also
[
3
]

\mathbb{P}_{I}(H)=\mathds{1}_{[-E,E]}(H)
by a smooth function
g(H)
of the Hamiltonian. Let

g\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R},[0,1])

be such that
g|_{[-E,E]}=1
and
\textnormal{supp}\,g\subset[-E-\varepsilon/2,E+\varepsilon/2]
, for some
\varepsilon>0

since

\|e^{\bm{F}}\mathbb{P}_{I}(H)\|=\|e^{\bm{F}}g(H)\mathbb{P}_{I}(H)\|\leqslant\|e^{\bm{F}}g(H)\|\,.

(7.1)

It is well-known (see e.g.,
[
5

) that there is an almost analytic extension of

, denoted by
\tilde{g}
, such that

g(t)=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{\triangle}(t-z)^{-1}\,\partial_{\bar{z}}\,\tilde{g}(z)\,\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}y\,,\qquad t\in\mathbb{R}.

(7.2)

Here
\partial_{\bar{z}}\tilde{g}=\frac{1}{2}(\partial_{x}+{\mathrm{i}}\partial_{y})\tilde{g}
is supported on

\triangle:=\{z=x+iy\in\mathbb{C}\,\ |\ |x|\leqslant E+\varepsilon,\ |y|\leqslant\varepsilon\}
. The almost analytical property allow us to pick
\tilde{g}
such that

|\partial_{\bar{z}}\tilde{g}(z)|=\mathcal{O}\big(|\mathrm{Im}z|\big)
as
|\mathrm{Im}z|\downarrow 0
. To simplify the notation we choose
\varepsilon=\varepsilon_{0}
, the localization error from our previous analysis.

It is useful to compare
H
to a suitable self-adjoint operator
\widetilde{H}
such that the resolvent set of the latter is contained in
\triangle
,

i.e.

\rho(\widetilde{H})\subset\triangle
.
We refer to the latter property as a
spectral gap
condition.
Indeed, in this case
g(\widetilde{H})=0

and the following equation holds

g(H)=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{\Delta}\Big(\big(H-z\big)^{-1}-\big(\widetilde{H}-z\big)^{-1}\Big)\partial_{\bar{z}}\,\widetilde{g}(z)\,\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}y\,.

(7.3)

Theorem
6.3

might then be shown by getting suitable estimates on the operator norm of the composition

e^{\bm{F}}\big[(H-z)^{-1}-(\widetilde{H}-z)^{-1}\big]
.

An essential novel ingredient in our approach is in the choice of the operator
\widetilde{H}
.
Indeed, we work with a comparison operator acting as
H
, plus an auxiliary mass-like potential.
Formally, this is defined as the sum

\displaystyle\widetilde{H}=H+\sigma_{3}\bm{E}{\bm{\chi}}

(7.4)

where
\bm{E}{\bm{\chi}}=\oplus_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}E_{j}\chi_{j}
and
\bm{E}
is chosen as in Definition
6
.
Note that
\bm{E}
is an unbounded operator in case
W
grows at infinity (and hence
\overline{\mu}_{\ast}>0
).
At least in the case when
W=0
it is is easy to check that the desired spectral gap holds.

In the next subsections we construct
\widetilde{H}
as a self-adjoint operator and provide, in Proposition
7.5
, the main bounds to
estimate
e^{\bm{F}}
times the resolvent difference.
We give the proof of Theorem
6.3

at the end of this section.

7.2.
The free comparison operator

We consider first the
free
comparison operator acting initially on
\mathcal{C}
as,

\displaystyle\widetilde{H}_{0}:=H_{0}+\sigma_{3}{\bm{E}}{\bm{\chi}}\quad\textrm{where}\quad{\bm{E}}{\bm{\chi}}\equiv\oplus_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}E_{j}\chi_{j}\ .

(7.5)

In Lemma
B.3
, we show that
\widetilde{H}_{0}
is essentially self-adjoint.
We denote its closure by the same symbol
\widetilde{H}_{0}
,
and its domain by

\mathcal{D}(\widetilde{H}_{0})
.
We characterize the latter in Proposition
7.2
below.

A short calculation yields that as quadratic forms on
\mathcal{C}

\widetilde{H}_{0}^{2}\ =\ H_{0}^{2}+\chi{\bm{E}}^{2}\chi+L({\bm{E}})\,,

(7.6)

where

L({\bm{E}})\equiv\oplus_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}E_{j}\chi_{j}^{\prime}\,\sigma_{1}\,.

Note that while the second term in (
7.6
) is quadratic in
\bm{E}
,
the third term is only linear.
Hence, it can then be regarded as a lower order term for large energies.

Lemma 7.1
.

In the sense of quadratic forms on
\mathcal{C}
, we have

\begin{split}H_{0}^{2}+{\bm{\chi}}{\bm{E}}^{2}{\bm{\chi}}\geqslant({\bm{E}}^{2}-2\varepsilon_{0})\,.\end{split}

(7.7)

Furthermore, the following statements hold true.

(1)

\mathcal{D}(\widetilde{H}_{0})\subset\mathcal{D}({\bm{E}})
and
the following estimate holds

\begin{split}\|\widetilde{H}_{0}\varphi\|\textstyle\geqslant(1-3\varepsilon_{0})^{\frac{1}{2}}\|{\bm{E}}\,\varphi\|\ ,\qquad\forall\varphi\in\mathcal{D}(\widetilde{H}_{0})\ .\end{split}

(7.8)

(2)

\mathcal{D}(\widetilde{H}_{0})\subset\mathcal{D}(H_{0})
and

\begin{split}\|\widetilde{H}_{0}\varphi\|\textstyle\geqslant(1-2\varepsilon_{0})^{\frac{1}{2}}\|H_{0}\,\varphi\|\ ,\qquad\forall\varphi\in\mathcal{D}(\widetilde{H}_{0})\ .\end{split}

(7.9)

Proof.

Let us first show (
7.7
).
We observe that
H_{0}^{2}+{\bm{E}}{\bm{\chi}}^{2}=\oplus_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}(h_{j}^{2}+E_{j}^{2}\chi^{2}_{j})
and verify that

\displaystyle\bm{h}^{2}+{\bm{\chi}}\bm{E}^{2}{\bm{\chi}}

\displaystyle\ =\ T+\bm{V}+{\bm{\chi}}\bm{E}^{2}{\bm{\chi}}

\displaystyle\ =\ T+{\bm{\chi}}\bm{V}{\bm{\chi}}+{\bm{\chi}}^{\perp}(\bm{V}-\bm{E}^{2}){\bm{\chi}}^{\perp}+\bm{E}^{2}

\displaystyle\ \geqslant\ {\bm{\chi}}\bm{h}^{2}{\bm{\chi}}+{\bm{\chi}}^{\perp}T{\bm{\chi}}^{\perp}+{\bm{\chi}}^{\perp}(\bm{V}-\bm{E}^{2}){\bm{\chi}}^{\perp}+\bm{E}^{2}-2\varepsilon_{0}\ .

(7.10)

The claim now follows by using the positivity of
T
and
h_{j}^{2}
as well as (
6.7
).
In order to show (
7.8
) on
\mathcal{C}
,
one may verify that

\pm L(\bm{E})\ \leqslant\ |{\bm{\chi}}^{\prime}|\bm{E}\ \leqslant\ \varepsilon_{0}\bm{E}\ \leqslant\ \varepsilon_{0}\bm{E}^{2}

(7.11)

where in the last inequality we used
\bm{E}\geqslant 1
.
This, combined with (
7.6
) and (
7.7
), readily implies

\widetilde{H}_{0}^{2}\ \geqslant\ (1-3\varepsilon_{0})\bm{E}^{2}\ .

(7.12)

This yields (
7.8
) on
\mathcal{C}
,
and then on
\mathcal{D}(\widetilde{H}_{0})
after a density argument.
Finally, we prove (
7.9
).
To this end, we write
using
(
7.6
), (
7.7
) and (
7.11
)

\displaystyle\widetilde{H}_{0}^{2}

\displaystyle\geqslant H_{0}^{2}+{\bm{\chi}}\bm{E}^{2}{\bm{\chi}}-\varepsilon_{0}\bm{E}^{2}

\displaystyle\geqslant(1-2\varepsilon_{0})H_{0}^{2}+2\varepsilon_{0}(H_{0}^{2}+{\bm{\chi}}\bm{E}^{2}{\bm{\chi}})-\varepsilon_{0}\bm{E}^{2}

\displaystyle\geqslant(1-2\varepsilon_{0})H_{0}^{2}+\big[2\varepsilon_{0}(\bm{E}^{2}-2\varepsilon_{0})-\varepsilon_{0}\bm{E}^{2}\big]\ .

(7.13)

Finally, we note that the term in brackets
[\cdots]
in the last displayed equation is non-negative.
This follows from the lower bound
\bm{E}\geqslant 1

and the fact that
\varepsilon_{0}\leqslant 1/4
. This finishes the proof.
∎

Next we characterize the domain of self-adjointness of the free comparison operator as follows.

Proposition 7.2
.

We have

\textstyle\mathcal{D}(\widetilde{H}_{0})=\mathcal{D}(H_{0})\cap\mathcal{D}({\bm{E}})=\big\{\,\varphi\in\mathcal{D}(H_{0})\ |\ \textstyle\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}E_{j}^{2}\,\|P_{m_{j}}\varphi\|^{2}<\infty\,\big\}\ .

(7.14)

Proof.

We prove in Lemma B.3
that
\widetilde{H}_{0}|_{\mathcal{C}}
is essentially
self-adjoint.
From Lemma
7.1

we see that

\mathcal{D}(\widetilde{H}_{0})\subset\mathcal{D}(\bm{E})\cap\mathcal{D}(H_{0})
.
Further, for
\varphi\in\mathcal{C}

one clearly has that

\displaystyle\|\widetilde{H}_{0}\varphi\|^{2}\leqslant 2\left(\|H_{0}\varphi\|^{2}+\|\bm{E}\varphi\|^{2}+\|\varphi\|^{2}\right)=2\left(\|\sqrt{H_{0}^{2}+\bm{E}^{2}}\,\varphi\|^{2}+\|\varphi\|^{2}\right)\,.

(7.15)

Applying the spectral theorem for the strongly commuting operators
H_{0}
and
\bm{E}
(see, e.g.,
[
17
, Sec. 5.5]
) it is easy to see that

\mathcal{D}(H_{0})\cap\mathcal{D}(\bm{E})
equals the domain of the closure of
{\sqrt{H_{0}^{2}+\bm{E}^{2}}\!\upharpoonright_{\mathcal{C}}}
. Hence, we conclude that

\mathcal{D}(H_{0})\cap\mathcal{D}(\bm{E})\subset\mathcal{D}(\widetilde{H}_{0})
.
This finishes the proof.
∎

7.3.
The full comparison operator

Let us now start our discussion of the perturbations of the free comparison operator.
First, we prove the following technical lemma.
It shows that under assumptions on the derivatives of the exponential weights,
the operators
\exp(\pm F)

continuously map the operator domain of
H_{0}
into itself.

Lemma 7.3
.

Let
F\in\mathcal{W}_{b}
be a bounded weight satisfying (
F2
).
Then, the following statements hold true

(i)

e^{\pm\bm{F}}\mathcal{D}(H_{0})=\mathcal{D}(H_{0})
.

(ii)

There exists

C>0
such that
for all
\varphi\in\mathcal{D}(H_{0})

\|H_{0}e^{\bm{F}}\varphi\|\leqslant C\|e^{\bm{F}}\|\|(H_{0}+i)\varphi\|\ .

(7.16)

Proof.

Let us fix
j\in\mathbb{Z}
.
Our first observation is the following inequality,
holding in the sense of quadratic forms of
\mathscr{C}_{\mathrm{r}}

|F_{j}^{\prime}|^{2}\ \leqslant\ \theta\,\chi_{j}^{\perp}\,V_{j}\,\chi_{j}^{\perp}\ \leqslant\ \theta\,(h_{j}^{2}+2\varepsilon_{0}^{2})

(7.17)

where
\theta=(1/4)(1-a)^{1/2}

is the constant from (
F2
),
and we have made use of
(
4.23
).
Since
\mathscr{C}_{\mathrm{r}}
is a domain core for both
h_{j}
and
F_{j}^{\prime}
, we conclude that

\mathcal{D}(h_{j})\subset\mathcal{D}(F_{j}^{\prime})

and (
7.17
) extends to
\mathcal{D}(h_{j})

in the form
\|F_{j}^{\prime}\varphi\|\leqslant C(\|h_{j}\varphi\|+\|\varphi\|)
, for
\varphi\in\mathcal{D}(h_{j})
.

Let us now prove
(i)
. To this end, let us fix
\psi\in\mathcal{D}(h_{j})
.
We claim that
e^{\pm F_{j}}\psi\in\mathcal{D}(h_{j}^{*})
.
To this end, we
recall the definition of
h_{j}
in
(
2.7
) and compute that
as linear operators on
\mathscr{C}_{\mathrm{r}}

[h_{j},e^{\pm F_{j}}]=e^{\pm F_{j}}\begin{pmatrix}0&\mp F_{j}^{\prime}\\
\pm F_{j}^{\prime}&0\end{pmatrix}\,=\pm\mathrm{i}e^{-F_{j}}\sigma_{2}F_{j}^{\prime}

(7.18)

where
\sigma_{2}
is the second Pauli matrix. Then, for any
\zeta\in\mathscr{C}_{\mathrm{r}}
it follows that

\left\langle e^{\pm F_{j}}\psi,h_{j}\zeta\right\rangle=\left\langle\psi,e^{\pm F_{j}}h_{j}\zeta\right\rangle=\left\langle\psi,h_{j}e^{\pm F_{j}}\zeta\pm\mathrm{i}e^{\pm F_{j}}\sigma_{2}F_{j}^{\prime}\zeta\right\rangle=\left\langle e^{\pm F_{j}}(h_{j}\mp\mathrm{i}\sigma_{2}F_{j}^{\prime})\psi,\zeta\right\rangle\ .

(7.19)

Thus, our claim now follows after we show that
e^{\pm F_{j}}(h_{j}\mp\mathrm{i}\sigma_{2}F_{j}^{\prime})\psi\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
.
This follows from the fact that
\exp(\pm F_{j})
are bounded operators,
from our observation

\mathcal{D}(h_{j})\subset\mathcal{D}(F_{j}^{\prime})
,
and the original assumption
\psi\in\mathcal{D}(h_{j})
.
Since
h_{j}
is self-adjoint, this implies that
\exp(\pm F_{j})\mathcal{D}(h_{j})\subset\mathcal{D}(h_{j})
.
Thus, the first claim of the lemma is now proven once we sum over angular momentum channels
j\in\mathbb{Z}
,
and use the fact that
\exp(\pm F)
are continuous inverses of one another.

The proof of
(ii)

now follows from the commutator identity
(
7.18
),
inequality
(
7.17
),
and an approximation argument using the fact that

\mathcal{C}

is a domain core of
H_{0}
.
Namely, we see that for
\varphi\in\mathcal{D}(H_{0})
and
\zeta\in\mathcal{C}

\left\langle\zeta,H_{0}e^{\bm{F}}\varphi\right\rangle=\left\langle e^{\bm{F}}H_{0}\zeta,\varphi\right\rangle=\left\langle(H_{0}e^{\bm{F}}-i\sigma_{2}\nabla{\bm{F}}e^{\bm{F}})\zeta,\varphi\right\rangle=\left\langle e^{\bm{F}}\zeta,(H_{0}-i\sigma_{2}\nabla\bm{F})\varphi\right\rangle\ .

(7.20)

It suffices now to use (
7.17
) over all
j\in\mathbb{Z}
,
and take the supremum over
\zeta\in\mathcal{C}
, keeping in mind that
e^{\bm{F}}
is a bounded operator.
∎

Next, we turn to the following lemma.
With it, we can control the perturbation
W
in terms of the
free comparison operator.

Lemma 7.4
.

For all
\varphi\in\mathcal{C}
the following inequality holds true

\|W\varphi\|^{2}\leqslant\Big(\frac{\widetilde{\mu}}{\mu}\Big)^{\frac{1}{2}}\|\widetilde{H}_{0}\varphi\|^{2}\ .

(7.21)

In particular,
\mathcal{D}(\widetilde{H}_{0})\subset\mathcal{D}(W)

and the inequality (
7.21

\varphi\in\mathcal{D}(\widetilde{H}_{0}).

Let us briefly postpone the proof of the above lemma,
and introduce the
full
comparison operator as follows

\widetilde{H}\equiv\widetilde{H}_{0}+W\qquad\textnormal{on}\qquad\mathcal{D}(\widetilde{H})\equiv\mathcal{D}(\widetilde{H}_{0})\ .

(7.22)

In particular, thanks to Lemma
7.4
, inequality (
P3
), and the Kato-Rellich theorem,

\widetilde{H}
is as a self-adjoint operator.
Furthermore, the space
\mathcal{C}
introduced in Definition

\widetilde{H}
.

In the next proposition, we establish fundamental estimates regarding
\widetilde{H}
, when intertwined with an exponential weight

F1
), (
F2
) and (
F3

Let us note that thanks to Lemma

conclude that
\exp(\pm F)\mathcal{D}(\widetilde{H}_{0})=\mathcal{D}(\widetilde{H}_{0})

Thus, we define the

\widetilde{H}^{F}\equiv e^{\bm{F}}\widetilde{H}e^{-\bm{F}}\qquad\textnormal{on}\qquad\mathcal{D}(\widetilde{H}^{F})\equiv\mathcal{D}(\widetilde{H}_{0})\ .

(7.23)

Let us note that the operator
\widetilde{H}^{F}

Indeed,
since
\widetilde{H}
is self-adjoint and the maps
\exp(\pm\bm{F})

(\widetilde{H}^{F})^{*}=\widetilde{H}^{-F}
.
In particular, this shows that

\overline{\widetilde{H}^{F}}=(\widetilde{H}^{F})^{**}=\widetilde{H}^{F}

Furthermore, the inequality contained in (

)
easily implies that
\mathcal{C}
is also a domain core of

Proposition 7.5
.

The following statements hold true.

(i)

For all

\psi\in\mathcal{D}(\widetilde{H})

there holds

\displaystyle\|\widetilde{H}\psi\|\,\geqslant\,\widetilde{E}\|\psi\|\ .

(7.24)

(ii)

Additionally,
assume that
F\in\mathcal{W}_{b}
satisfies (
F2
) and (
F3

Then, for all

\psi\in\mathcal{D}(\widetilde{H}^{F})

and

\begin{split}\|\big(\widetilde{H}^{F}-z\big)\psi\|\,\geqslant\,(\delta_{0}/4)\|\mathbf{E}\psi\|\ .\end{split}

(7.25)

Now, we turn to the proof of Lemma
7.4
,
and Proposition

Proof of Lemma
7.4
.

Let us recall some important notations

Namely, in Condition

a=(\widetilde{\mu}/\mu)^{\frac{1}{2}},\qquad b=(\overline{\mu}_{*}/\widetilde{\mu})^{\frac{1}{2}},\qquad\textnormal{and}\qquad\delta_{0}=\min(1-a,1-b)

Let us now prove
(

To this end, we use

(

that in the sense of quadratic forms on
\mathcal{C}

\displaystyle\widetilde{H}_{0}^{2}\ \geqslant\ H_{0}^{2}+{\bm{\chi}}\bm{E}^{2}{\bm{\chi}}-\varepsilon_{0}\bm{E}\qquad\textnormal{and}\qquad\frac{1}{a}W^{2}\ \leqslant\ aH_{0}^{2}+b{\bm{\chi}}\bm{E}^{2}{\bm{\chi}}+\frac{C_{0}}{a}\ .

Lemma A.1
