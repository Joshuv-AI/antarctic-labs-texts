On the height of some generators of Galois extensions with big Galois group
Jonathan Jenvrin

Abstract
. We study the height of generators of Galois extensions of the rationals having the alternating group 𝔄 n \mathfrak{A}_{n} as Galois group. We prove that if such generators are obtained from certain, albeit classical, constructions, their height tends to infinity as n n increases. This provides an analogue of a result by Amoroso, originally established for the symmetric group.



1.
Introduction

In this article, we let
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}
be a fixed algebraic closure of
\mathbb{Q}
.
Given
\alpha\in\overline{\mathbb{Q}}
of degree
d
, we denote by
M(\alpha)
its Mahler measure, defined as

M(\alpha)=|a|\prod_{i=1}^{d}\max\left(1,|\alpha_{i}|\right)\geq 1

where
a
is the leading coefficient of the minimal polynomial of
\alpha
over
\mathbb{Z}
, and
\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{d}
are the conjugates of
\alpha
.
The logarithmic Weil height of
\alpha
, or, for short, the height of
\alpha
is then given by

h(\alpha)=\frac{\log(M(\alpha))}{d}.

While, by Kronecker’s theorem, it is well-known that
h(\alpha)=0
if and only if
\alpha=0
or
\alpha
is a root of unity, Lehmer’s conjecture predicts the existence of a positive constant
c>0
such that

h(\alpha)\geq\frac{c}{d}

whenever
h(\alpha)
is not zero.
The conjecture has been proved for various classes of algebraic numbers, but it is still open in general.
The most notable progress toward Lehmer’s conjecture is Dobrowolski’s result
[
Dob79
, Theorem 1]
, which proves that, if
h(\alpha)\neq 0
, then

h(\alpha)\geq\frac{c}{d}\left(\frac{\log\log d}{\log d}\right)^{3}

(one can take
c=1/4
as shown in
[
Vou96
, Theorem on p. 83]
).

If, on the one hand, Dobrowolski’s theorem stands as the sole unconditional result on this problem, on the other hand, it is possible to show that specific classes of algebraic numbers satisfy even stronger variants of Lehmer’s conjecture, such as the Bogomolov property introduced by Bombieri and Zannier in
[
BZ01
]
: a set of algebraic numbers
S
satisfies the
Bogomolov property (B)
if there exists a constant
c=c(S)>0
such that for every
\alpha\in S
either
h(\alpha)=0
or
h(\alpha)\geq c
.

Property (B) holds, for instance, for abelian extensions of number fields, as shown in
[
AZ00
,
AZ10
]
and for the field of totally real algebraic numbers, as proved in
[
Sch73
, Corollary 1]
.

A set of algebraic numbers which has attracted the attention in this respect in recent years, is the following

S_{\mathrm{Gal}}=\{\alpha\in\overline{\mathbb{Q}}\mid\mathbb{Q}(\alpha)/\mathbb{Q}\text{ is Galois }\}.

While Amoroso and David proved that Lehmer’s conjecture holds for the elements of this set (see
[
AD99
, Corollary 1.7]
), later Amoroso and Masser
[
AM16
, Theorem 3.3]
showed that
S_{\mathrm{Gal}}
satisfies even the following stronger result:
for any
\epsilon>0
, there exists a positive effective constant
c(\epsilon)
such that, for every
\alpha\in S_{\mathrm{Gal}}
of degree
d
over
\mathbb{Q}
and not a root of unity, one has

h(\alpha)\geq c(\epsilon)d^{-\epsilon}.

A result this strong might prompt the question, of whether the set
S_{\mathrm{Gal}}
satisfies Property (B) or not.

A quite natural way to tackle this issue is by fixing the Galois group of
\mathbb{Q}(\alpha)/\mathbb{Q}
.
As recalled before, the answer is known to be positive when such group is abelian (see also
[
AD00
]
), while the dihedral case has been considered in
[
AZ10
, Corollary 1.3]
.

Motivated by a question posed by Smyth (see
[
APSV
, Problem 21]
), Amoroso studied certain classes of generators of Galois extensions whose Galois group is the full symmetric group
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
and proved the following:

Theorem 1.1

(
[
Amo18
, Theorems 1.1 and 1.2]
)
.

Let
\beta
be an algebraic integer of degree
n\geq 3
, let
\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{n}
be its conjugates and assume that
\mathrm{Gal}(\mathbb{Q}(\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{n})/\mathbb{Q})=\mathfrak{S}_{n}
.

Let
a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}
be integers. Then:

(i)

If
\beta
is a unit,
\alpha=\beta_{1}^{a_{1}}\ldots\beta_{n}^{a_{n}}
is a generator of
\mathbb{Q}(\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{n})/\mathbb{Q}
if and only if
a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}
are pairwise distinct. In this case
h(\alpha)
tends to infinity as
n
increases.

(ii)

\alpha^{\prime}=a_{1}\beta_{1}+\ldots+a_{n}\beta_{n}
is a generator of
\mathbb{Q}(\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{n})/\mathbb{Q}
if and only if
a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}
are pairwise distinct. In this case
h(\alpha^{\prime})
tends to infinity as
n
increases.

This result led Amoroso to propose the following conjecture:

Conjecture 1.2

(
[
Amo18
, Conjecture 1.3]
)
.

Let
\alpha\in\overline{\mathbb{Q}}
be a generator of a Galois extension of degree
d=n!
of Galois group
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
. Then
h(\alpha)\geq c(d)

with
c(d)
a function tending to infinity with
d
.

The goal of this article is to investigate whether some analogue of Theorem
1.1
holds for other groups. Our first result provides a lower bound for the height of generators of Galois extensions as in (
i
) with no assumption on the structure of the Galois group of the extension.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
n\geq 3
be an integer and let
\beta
be an algebraic integer of degree
n
, with conjugates
\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{n}
. Let
a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}\in\mathbb{Z}
, and let

\alpha=\beta_{1}^{a_{1}}\ldots\beta_{n}^{a_{n}}.

Suppose that
\mathbb{Q}(\alpha)=\mathbb{Q}(\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{n})
. Then

h(\alpha)\geq\left({\frac{1}{n}\left|\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}\right|}\right)\cdot\log\left|N_{\mathbb{Q}(\beta)/\mathbb{Q}}(\beta)\right|.

In particular, if
\beta
is not a unit and if the sequence
\left(\frac{1}{n}\left|\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}\right|\right)_{n}
tends to infinity with
n
, then
h(\alpha)
tends also to infinity with
n
.

The remainder and most significant part of this article is devoted to proving the analogue of Theorem
1.1
for extensions having Galois group equal to the alternating group
\mathfrak{A}_{n}
. We remark that this case, besides being naturally interesting to examine, can be also consider in some sense the
generic
one. For instance, it has been proved in
[
BSK20
, Theorem 2]
that, for any integer
\ell\geq 2
, a random polynomial of degree
n
with i.i.d. random integers coefficients taking values uniformly in
\{1,\ldots,\ell\}
will have Galois group containing
\mathfrak{A}_{n}
with a probability tending to
1
as
n
goes to infinity.

Our second main result
is the analogue of
[
Amo18
, Theorem 1.1]
for
\mathfrak{A}_{n}
, for generators obtained as products of conjugates.

Theorem 1.4
.

Let
\beta
be an algebraic number of degree
n\geq 5
, let
\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{n}
be its conjugates and assume that
\mathrm{Gal}(\mathbb{Q}(\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{n})/\mathbb{Q})=\mathfrak{A}_{n}
. Let
\alpha=\beta_{1}^{a_{1}}\ldots\beta_{n}^{a_{n}}
where
a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}\in\mathbb{Z}
. Then:

(1)

\alpha
is a generator of
\mathbb{Q}(\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{n})/\mathbb{Q}
if and only if there are at most two distinct indices
i,j
such that
a_{i}=a_{j}
.

(2)

If
\alpha
is a generator of
\mathbb{Q}(\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{n})/\mathbb{Q}
and
\beta
is a unit, then

h(\alpha)\geq(1+g(n))\sqrt{\frac{n}{200\pi}}\left(\frac{\log(\log(n))}{\log(n)}\right)^{3}

where
g(n)
tends to 0 as
n
tends to infinity.
In particular,
h(\alpha)
tends to infinity with
n
.

Our last main result, a counterpart to
[
Amo18
, Theorems 1.2]
for
\mathfrak{A}_{n}
, deals with generators obtained as linear combination of conjugates:

Theorem 1.5
.

Let
\beta
be an algebraic integer of degree
n\geq 5
, let
\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{n}
be its conjugates and suppose that
\mathrm{Gal}(\mathbb{Q}(\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{n})/\mathbb{Q})=\mathfrak{A}_{n}
. Consider
\alpha=a_{1}\beta_{1}+\ldots+a_{n}\beta_{n}
where
a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}\in\mathbb{Z}
. Then:

(1)

\alpha
is a generator of
\mathbb{Q}(\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{n})/\mathbb{Q}
if and only if there are at most two distinct indices
i,j
such that
a_{i}=a_{j}
.

(2)

If
\alpha
is a generator of
\mathbb{Q}(\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{n})/\mathbb{Q}
, then

h(\alpha)\geq{\frac{1}{240}}\log\left(\frac{n}{9}\right).

In particular,
h(\alpha)
tends to infinity with
n
.

We notice that our results potentially support an extension of Conjecture
1.2
to generators of Galois extensions with Galois group containing
\mathfrak{A}_{n}
.

We now describe the structure of the article and briefly outline the main ideas involved in the proofs of our results.

The proof of Theorem
1.3
, presented in Section
2
, relies on elementary group theory and basic properties of the Mahler measure. It is notably simpler than the proof of Theorem
1.4
, which suggests that the most challenging scenario arises when
\beta
is a unit.

The proofs of Theorems
1.4
and
1.5
are presented in Sections
3
and
4
, respectively.

Our proof strategy for Theorem
1.4
builds upon Amoroso’s approach in
[
Amo18
, Theorem 1.1]
.
Point (
1
) is proved in Section
3.1
and relies on an adaption of a result of Smyth (see Proposition
3.1
) and some elementary Galois theoretic arguments.

Point (
2
) is a corollary of a more precise statement, given in Proposition
3.3
, whose proof, done in Section
3.2
, is more subtle and requires some technicalities.
First, in Proposition
3.4
, we link the Mahler measures of
\alpha
and
\beta
to the values taken by the function

\frac{2}{n!}\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{A}_{n}}\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\log|\beta_{\sigma{(j)}}|\left(a_{j}-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}\right)\right|.

We then study the properties of this function and provide an upper and lower bound for it (see Proposition
3.6
) in terms of a constant
c_{n}
(estimated in Proposition
3.7
).
Point (
2
) then follows combining the above results with the explicit version of Dobrowolski’s theorem by Voutier
[
Vou96
, Theorem, p. 83]
.

As for Theorem
1.5
, the proof of point (
1
), carried out in Section
4.1
,
is the same, in additive notation, as the one of point (
1
) of Theorem
1.4
and it is even simpler than its multiplicative variant.

It is in the proof of point (
2
), done in Section
4.2
, that lies the main novelty of our strategy.
The key point here is Lemma
4.3

which describes how the Mahler measure of
\alpha
changes when one
applies
a transposition to
\alpha
, so, by hypothesis, an element not in
\mathrm{Gal}(\mathbb{Q}(\alpha)/\mathbb{Q})
and that
should not act on
\alpha
.
This is then used to give a lower bound for
M(\alpha)
(see Proposition
4.4
)
independent on the chosen transposition, but depending, amongst others, on the quantity

V(\mathfrak{a})=\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq n}(a_{j}-a_{i}).

This bound is exploitable only when all
a_{i}
’s are distinct, and in this case the quantities involved can be bounded using, among other ingredients, estimates on the derangement numbers.

However,
\alpha
could be a generator of the extension even if two of the indices
a_{i}
’s are equal, causing the quantity
V(\mathfrak{a})
to be zero and providing no useful information. To overcome this, we construct another generator given by a linear combination of the
\beta_{i}
’s with all distinct integral coefficients and having height close to that of
\alpha
. This is done using the key Lemma
4.6
. The height of such a generator can then be bounded applying the first part of the proof, and this allows to conclude.

Finally, in Section
5
, we present some applications of our results to specific families of polynomials. Additionally, we discuss whether the proof strategy of our main results can be adapted to generators of Galois extensions with Galois groups different from
\mathfrak{A}_{n}
or
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
.

2.
Proof of Theorem
1.3

For the proof of Theorem
1.3
, we will need the following elementary lemma.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
G<\mathfrak{S}_{n}
be a transitive subgroup. For
i,k\in\{1,\dots,n\}
let
G_{i,k}=\{\sigma\in G\mid\sigma(k)=i\}
.
Then
|G_{i,k}|=\frac{|G|}{n}.

Proof.

Let
i,j,k\in\{1,\dots,n\}
be fixed, and let
\sigma\in G
.
Since
G
is transitive, we can take
\tau\in G
such that
\tau(i)=j
.
We define the function

\begin{array}[]{ccccc}\phi_{\tau}&:&G_{i,k}&\to&G_{j,k}\\
&&\sigma&\mapsto&\tau\sigma\\
\end{array}.

One can check that
\phi_{\tau}
is well-defined, and it is a bijection, with inverse given by
\sigma\mapsto\tau^{-1}\sigma
. So we have
|G_{i,k}|=|G_{j,k}|
. Since

\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}|G_{\ell,k}|=|G|

we have the sought-for equality.
∎

We can now prove Theorem
1.3
.

Proof of Theorem
1.3
.

Let
G
be the Galois group of
\mathbb{Q}(\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{n})
over
\mathbb{Q}
.
Notice that, by the definition of the Mahler measure, we have

(1)

M(\alpha)\geq\left|N_{\mathbb{Q}(\alpha)/\mathbb{Q}}(\alpha)\right|.

Our goal is to show that

(2)

\left|N_{\mathbb{Q}(\alpha)/\mathbb{Q}}(\alpha)\right|^{1/|G|}=\left|N_{\mathbb{Q}(\beta)/\mathbb{Q}}(\beta)\right|^{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}}.

We have

(3)

\left|N_{\mathbb{Q}(\alpha)/\mathbb{Q}}(\alpha)\right|=\prod_{\sigma\in G}\prod_{i=1}^{n}\left|\beta_{i}^{a_{\sigma^{-1}(i)}}\right|=\prod_{i=1}^{n}\left|\beta_{i}^{\sum_{\sigma\in G}a_{\sigma^{-1}(i)}}\right|=\prod_{i=1}^{n}\left|\beta_{i}^{\sum_{\sigma\in G}a_{\sigma(i)}}\right|.

Since
G
is a transitive subgroup of
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
, for all
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
, we have

\sum_{\sigma\in G}a_{\sigma(i)}=\sum_{\sigma\in G}a_{\sigma(1)}.

Therefore

\prod_{i=1}^{n}\left|\beta_{i}^{\sum_{\sigma\in G}a_{\sigma(i)}}\right|=\prod_{i=1}^{n}\left|\beta_{i}\right|^{\sum_{\sigma\in G}a_{\sigma(1)}}=\left|N_{\mathbb{Q}(\beta)/\mathbb{Q}}(\beta)\right|^{\sum_{\sigma\in G}a_{\sigma(1)}}.

Letting
G_{i,1}=\{\sigma\in G\mid\sigma(1)=i\}
, by Lemma
2.1
we have

\sum_{\sigma\in G}a_{\sigma(1)}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\sum_{\sigma\in G_{i,1}}a_{i}\right)=\frac{|G|}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}.

Hence, from (
3
) we obtain

\left|N_{\mathbb{Q}(\alpha)/\mathbb{Q}}(\alpha)\right|=\left|N_{\mathbb{Q}(\beta)/\mathbb{Q}}(\beta)\right|^{\frac{|G|}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}}.

This proves (
2
) by taking the
|G|
-th root.
Finally, from (
1
), we have

M(\alpha)^{1/|G|}\geq\left|N_{\mathbb{Q}(\beta)/\mathbb{Q}}(\beta)\right|^{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}}.

Applying this to
1/\alpha
and using
M(\alpha)=M(1/\alpha)
, we also have

M(\alpha)^{1/|G|}\geq\left|N_{\mathbb{Q}(\beta)/\mathbb{Q}}(\beta)\right|^{-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}}.

This proves Theorem
1.3
by noticing that
|G|=[\mathbb{Q}(\alpha):\mathbb{Q}]
.

∎

3.
Proof of Theorem
1.4

The objective of this section is to establish Theorem
1.4
.
To achieve this, as mentioned earlier, we will follow the proof outlined by Amoroso in
[
Amo18
, Sections 2 and 3]
and adapt it as needed.

For enhanced readability, the proof of the distinct points in Theorem
1.4
is divided into separate subsections. Within each subsection, we introduce the necessary technical results and definitions needed in the proof of the corresponding point.

Throughout the article, if
\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{n}\in\overline{\mathbb{Q}}
is a full set of Galois conjugates, when writing
\mathfrak{A}_{n}\subseteq\mathrm{Gal}(\mathbb{Q}(\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{n})/\mathbb{Q})
, we identify
\sigma\in\mathfrak{A}_{n}
with the automorphism
\sigma(\beta_{i})=\beta_{\sigma(i)}
.

3.1.
The multiplicative case: proof of Theorem
1.4
, point (
1
)

We start, as in
[
Amo18
]
, by adapting the multiplicative version of
[
Smy86
, Lemma 1]
:

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
\beta
be an algebraic number of degree
n\geq 5
such that
\beta^{k}\notin\mathbb{Q}
for any non-zero integer
k
.
Let
\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{n}
be the conjugates of
\beta
, and suppose that
\mathfrak{A}_{n}\subset\mathrm{Gal}(\mathbb{Q}(\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{n})/\mathbb{Q})
. Then, for every
v_{1},\dots,v_{n}\in\mathbb{Z}
, not all equal, the product
\beta_{1}^{v_{1}}\dots\beta_{n}^{v_{n}}
is not a root of unity.

Proof.

Assume, by contradiction, that
\beta_{1}^{v_{1}}\dots\beta_{n}^{v_{n}}
is a root of unity. Up to multiplying all the
v_{i}
’s by the same non-zero integer, we can suppose that
\beta_{1}^{v_{1}}\dots\beta_{n}^{v_{n}}=1
.

Notice that by hypothesis,
\mathrm{Gal}(\mathbb{Q}(\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{n})/\mathbb{Q})
contains all 3-cycles. Without loss of generality, we can suppose that
\beta=\beta_{1}
and
v_{1}\neq v_{2}
.

Applying the cycle
(1\;3\;2)
to both sides of the equality
\beta_{1}^{v_{1}}\dots\beta_{n}^{v_{n}}=1
, we obtain
\beta_{1}^{v_{1}-v_{2}}\beta_{2}^{v_{2}-v_{3}}\beta_{3}^{v_{3}-v_{1}}=1
.
Applying to this last equality the cycle
(1\;i\;j)
, for
i\neq j
and
i,j\geq 4
, we obtain
\beta_{1}^{v_{1}-v_{2}}=\beta_{i}^{v_{1}-v_{2}}
for all
i\geq 4
.

Applying further the cycles
(1\;2\;k)
and
(1\;3\;\ell)
for
k,\ell\not\in\{2,3,i\}
, we finally get that for all
1\leq i\leq n
,
\beta_{1}^{v_{1}-v_{2}}=\beta_{i}^{v_{1}-v_{2}}
. So,
\beta^{v_{1}-v_{2}}\in\mathbb{Q}
as it is fixed by all elements of
\mathrm{Gal}(\mathbb{Q}(\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{n})/\mathbb{Q})
, contradicting the hypothesis on
\beta
as
v_{1}\neq v_{2}
.
∎

Now we are able to prove point
(1)
of Theorem
1.4
.

Proof of Point
(
1
)
of Theorem
1.4
.

Assume first that
\alpha
is a generator of
\mathbb{Q}(\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{n})/\mathbb{Q}
.
If
|\{a_{i}\mid 1\leq i\leq n\}|<n-1
, then either there exist indices
i<j<k
such that
a_{i}=a_{j}=a_{k}
, or there are two couples
(i,j)\neq(k,\ell)
with
i<j
and
k<\ell
such that
a_{i}=a_{j}
and
a_{k}=a_{\ell}
.

In the first case, we have that
\alpha
is fixed by the 3-cycle
(i\;j\;k)
, while in the second case
\alpha
is fixed by the double transposition
(i\;j)(k\;\ell)
.
In all cases, we find
[\mathbb{Q}(\alpha):\mathbb{Q}]<|\mathfrak{A}_{n}|
, contradicting the fact that
\alpha
is a generator of
\mathbb{Q}(\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{n})/\mathbb{Q}
.

For the converse, assuming that
|\{a_{i},1\leq i\leq n\}|\geq n-1
, we want to show that the group
\mathrm{Gal}(\mathbb{Q}(\beta_{1},...,\beta_{n})/\mathbb{Q}(\alpha))
is trivial.

Let
\sigma\in\mathrm{Gal}(\mathbb{Q}(\beta_{1},...,\beta_{n})/\mathbb{Q}(\alpha))
and let
\tau=\sigma^{-1}
. We want to show that
\tau
is the identity.
Regarding
\sigma
as an element of
\mathfrak{A}_{n}
, the equality
\sigma(\alpha)=\alpha
gives

(4)

\prod_{k=1}^{n}\beta_{k}^{a_{k}-a_{\tau(k)}}=1.

Notice now that
\beta
satisfies the hypothesis of Proposition
3.1
. Indeed, suppose that
\beta^{k}\in\mathbb{Q}
for some integer
k>0
. Then, for all
i,j
, we have
\beta_{i}^{k}=\beta_{j}^{k}
. So, for all
i,j
, there exists a
k
-th root of unity
\zeta_{i,j,k}
such that
\beta_{i}=\zeta_{i,j,k}\beta_{j}
. We have for all
i,j

(5)

\zeta_{i,j,k}=\frac{\beta_{i}}{\beta_{j}}\in\mathbb{Q}(\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{n}).

Hence, we get for all
i,j

\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{i,j,k})\subset\mathbb{Q}(\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{n}).

Since
\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{i,j,k})/\mathbb{Q}
is Galois, then
\text{Gal}(\mathbb{Q}(\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{n})/\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{i,j,k}))
is a normal subgroup of
\text{Gal}(\mathbb{Q}(\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{n})/\mathbb{Q})=\mathfrak{A}_{n}
, which is simple for
n\geq 5
. Hence, either
\mathbb{Q}(\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{n})=\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{i,j,k})
, but this cannot be the case as
\mathfrak{A}_{n}
is not abelian for
n\geq 4
, or
\mathbb{Q}=\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{i,j,k})
.

If
\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{i,j,k})=\mathbb{Q}
for all
i,j
, since
\zeta_{i,j,k}
is a root of unity, we have either
\zeta_{i,j,k}=1
or
\zeta_{i,j,k}=-1
. If
\zeta_{i,j,k}=1
for some
i\neq j
, by (
5
), we would have
\beta_{i}=\beta_{j}
, which is a contradiction. If
\zeta_{i,j,k}=-1
for all
i\neq j
, then we have by (
5
),
\beta_{1}=-\beta_{2}=-\beta_{3}
, and so
\beta_{2}=\beta_{3}
, which is again a contradiction. Finally, for all non-zero integer
k
, we have
\beta^{k}\notin\mathbb{Q}
.

Therefore, applying the conclusion of Proposition
3.1
to (
4
), there exists an integer
c
such that
a_{k}-a_{\tau(k)}=c
for all
k
.

If
\ell
if the order of
\tau
, we have,
a_{1}=a_{\tau^{\ell}(1)}=\dots=a_{1}+c\ell
. So
c=0
and
a_{\tau(k)}=a_{k}
for all
k
.

If the
a_{i}
are all distinct, then
\tau
is necessarily the identity.

If
|\{a_{i},1\leq i\leq n\}|=n-1
, we can suppose that
a_{1}=a_{2}
. We then have,
\tau(k)=k
for all
k\geq 3
and
\tau(1),\tau(2)\in\{1,2\}
.

But
\tau\in\mathfrak{A}_{n}
, so
\tau
cannot be the transposition
(1\;2)
. Hence
\tau
is the identity, concluding the proof.
∎

Remark 3.2
.

We notice that the argument from the previous proof allows us to remove the condition
\beta
unit, also for
[
Amo18
, Theorem 1.1 point (1)]
.

3.2.
Proof of Theorem
1.4
, point (
2
)

The goal of this section is to prove the following result, of which Theorem
1.4
, point (
2
) is a simple corollary.

Proposition 3.3
.

Let
\beta
be an algebraic unit of degree
n\geq 5
, let
\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{n}
be its conjugates and assume that
\mathrm{Gal}(\mathbb{Q}(\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{n})/\mathbb{Q})=\mathfrak{A}_{n}
. Let
\alpha=\beta_{1}^{a_{1}}\ldots\beta_{n}^{a_{n}}
where
a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}\in\mathbb{Z}
.

Let
y_{j}=a_{j}-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}
and set
|y|_{1}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}|y_{j}|
. Then

M(\beta)^{|y|_{1}}\geq M(\alpha)^{{2}/{n!}}\geq M(\beta)^{c_{n}|y|_{1}},

where
c_{n}
is an effective computable constant satisfying

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}{c_{n}}\sqrt{\frac{\pi n}{2}}=1.

Before proceeding with the proof of this part, we
need to recall some definitions from
[
Amo18
, Section 2]
and adapt some other technical results therein.

Let
H_{n}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mid x_{1}+\dots+x_{n}=0\}
, and for
x\in H_{n}
, we define the usual
L^{1}
-norm on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
as

|x|_{1}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}|x_{j}|.

The group
\mathfrak{A}_{n}
acts on
H_{n}
by
\sigma(x)=\left(x_{\sigma(1)},\dots,x_{\sigma(n)}\right)
.
For
x,y\in H_{n}
, we set

s_{n}(x,y)=\frac{2}{n!}\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{A}_{n}}\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}x_{\sigma(j)}y_{j}\right|.

We notice that
s_{n}
is symmetric.

The proof of Proposition
3.3
is then a combination of three results. The first, given in the following proposition, links the Mahler measure of
\alpha
to the function
s_{n}
.

Proposition 3.4
.

Let
\alpha,\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{n}
and
y=(y_{1},\ldots,y_{n})
be as in the statement of Proposition
3.3
. Let
x=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
where
x_{i}=\log|\beta_{i}|
.
Then

\log(M(\beta))=\frac{n}{2}|x|_{1}

and

\log(M(\alpha))=\frac{n(n!)}{4}s_{n}(x,y).

Proof.

Notice that

\sum_{j=1}^{n}x_{j}=\log(N_{K/\mathbb{Q}}(\beta))=0

so
x\in H_{n}
. Let
\mathcal{I}=\{j\in\{1,\ldots,n\}\mid|\beta_{j}|\geq 1\}
. Then

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{n}|x_{j}|

\displaystyle=\sum_{j\in\mathcal{I}}\log|\beta_{j}|-\sum_{j\not\in\mathcal{I}}\log|\beta_{j}|=2\sum_{j\in\mathcal{I}}\log|\beta_{j}|

\displaystyle=2\sum_{j=1}^{n}\log(\max(|\beta_{j}|,1))=2\log(M(\beta))

proving the first equality in the statement.
By applying this equality to
\alpha
, whose conjugates are
\beta_{\sigma(1)}^{a_{1}}\dots\beta_{\sigma(n)}^{a_{n}}
for
\sigma\in\mathfrak{A}_{n}
, we obtain

\displaystyle 2\log(M(\alpha))

\displaystyle=\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{A}_{n}}|\log(|\beta_{\sigma(1)}^{a_{1}}\dots\beta_{\sigma(n)}^{a_{n}}|)|=\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{A}_{n}}\left|\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{j}x_{\sigma(j)}\right|.

Thus, by recalling that
a_{j}=y_{j}+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}
and that
\sum_{j=1}^{n}x_{\sigma(j)}=0
for every
\sigma\in\mathfrak{A}_{n}
, we obtain

\displaystyle 2\log(M(\alpha))

\displaystyle=\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{A}_{n}}\left|\sum_{j=1}^{n}y_{j}x_{\sigma(j)}+\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}\right)\left(\sum_{j=1}^{n}x_{\sigma(j)}\right)\right|

\displaystyle=\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{A}_{n}}\left|\sum_{j=1}^{n}y_{j}x_{\sigma(j)}\right|=s_{n}(x,y)\cdot\frac{n(n!)}{2}.

∎

The next step in the proof is to provide an upper and lower bound for the quantity
s_{n}(x,y)
. To this aim, for
h=1,\dots,n-1
, we define the vector
z^{(n,h)}\in H_{n}
by

z_{j}^{(n,h)}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\frac{n}{2h}&\mbox{if }j\leq h\\
-\frac{n}{2(n-h)}&\mbox{if }j>h\end{array}\right.

and we set

c_{n}=\underset{0<h,k<n}{\min}s_{n}(z^{(n,h)},z^{(n,k)}).

We have the following lemma:

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
n\geq 5
and
A=\{1,\ldots,h\}
for some
1\leq h\leq n
. Let
G
be the subgroup of
\mathfrak{A}_{n}
defined as
G=\{\sigma\in\mathfrak{A}_{n}\mid\sigma(A)=A\}
. Then, for every
x\in H_{n}
, we have

|G|^{-1}\sum_{\sigma\in G}\sigma(x)=z^{(n,h)}.

Proof.

Let
x\in H_{n}
.
Using the fact that
x_{1}+\dots+x_{n}=0
, we have

\displaystyle 1=|x|_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}|x_{j}|=\frac{1}{n}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{h}x_{j}-\sum_{j=h+1}^{n}x_{j}\right)=\frac{2}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{h}x_{j}=-\frac{2}{n}\sum_{j=h+1}^{n}x_{j}.

So, in particular

(6)

\sum_{j=1}^{h}x_{j}=\frac{n}{2}=-\sum_{j=h+1}^{n}x_{j}.

We also notice that
G
restricted to
A
is a transitive subgroup of
\mathfrak{S}(A)\simeq\mathfrak{S}_{h}
. Indeed, if
h=1
there is nothing to show. If
h=2
, then
A=\{1,2\}
, and
\tau:=(1\;2)(3\;4)\in G
verifies
\tau(1)=2
. If
h\geq 3
, let us take
i,j,k\in A
, all distinct. Then
\tau:=(i\;j\;k)\in G
verifies
\tau(i)=j
. We set for
j,i\in A
,
G_{j,i}=\{\sigma\in G\mid\sigma(i)=j\}
. We denote the
i
-th component of the vector
\sum_{\sigma\in G}\sigma(x)
by

\left(\sum_{\sigma\in G}\sigma(x)\right)_{i}.

So, by Lemma
2.1
and (
6
), we get for
1\leq i\leq h

\left(\sum_{\sigma\in G}\sigma(x)\right)_{i}=\sum_{\sigma\in G}x_{\sigma(i)}=\sum_{j=1}^{h}|G_{j,i}|x_{j}=\frac{|G|}{h}\sum_{j=1}^{h}x_{j}=\frac{n}{2h}|G|.

A similar argument shows that, for
h+1\leq i\leq n
, we have

\left(\sum_{\sigma\in G}\sigma(x)\right)_{i}=\frac{|G|}{n-h}\sum_{j=h+1}^{n}x_{j}=-\frac{n}{2(n-h)}|G|,

completing the proof of the lemma.
∎

From this lemma, we have the following proposition:

Proposition 3.6
.

For every
x,y\in H_{n}
we have

c_{n}\leq\frac{s_{n}(x,y)}{|x|_{1}|y|_{1}}\leq 1.

Proof.

We first notice that the upper bound is easy to obtain. We have

(7)

s_{n}(x,y)\leq\frac{2}{n!}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{A}_{n}}\sum_{j=1}^{n}|x_{\sigma(j)}||y_{j}|=\frac{2}{n!}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}|y_{j}|\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{A}_{n}}|x_{\sigma(j)}|.

By Lemma
2.1
, since
\mathfrak{A}_{n}
is a transitive subgroup of
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
, we know that for all
1\leq i,j\leq n
we have

|\{\sigma\in\mathfrak{A}_{n}\;|\;\sigma(j)=i\}|=\frac{|\mathfrak{A}_{n}|}{n}=\frac{(n-1)!}{2}.

Therefore, replacing in (
7
) we have

\displaystyle\frac{2}{n!}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}|y_{j}|\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{A}_{n}}|x_{\sigma(j)}|

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{n!}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}|y_{j}|\frac{(n-1)!}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}|x_{i}|

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{j=1}^{n}|y_{j}|\sum_{i=1}^{n}|x_{i}|=|x|_{1}|y|_{1}

concluding the proof of the upper bound.

We are now left with the proof of the lower bound. Let
x
and
y
be two non-zero vectors in
H_{n}
and let
h
and
k
be the number of non-negative components of
x
and
y
, respectively.
The proposition reduces to

(8)

\frac{s_{n}(x,y)}{|x|_{1}|y|_{1}}\geq\frac{s_{n}(z^{(n,h)},y)}{|y|_{1}}\geq s_{n}(z^{(n,h)},z^{(n,k)}).

Let
x=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
and
y=(y_{1},\ldots,y_{n})
.
Since for real numbers
c,c^{\prime}>0
we have
s_{n}(cx,c^{\prime}y)=cc^{\prime}s_{n}(x,y)
, we can suppose that

|x|_{1}=|y|_{1}=1
.
Moreover, letting

A=\{j\in\{1,\dots,n\}\mid x_{j}\geq 0\},

since the function
\varphi_{n,y}(x)=s_{n}(x,y)
is invariant under the action of
\mathfrak{A}_{n}
, we can further suppose that
A=\{1,\dots,h\}
. Let
G
be the subgroup of
\mathfrak{A}_{n}
defined as
G=\{\sigma\in\mathfrak{A}_{n}\mid\sigma(A)=A\}
.

As in
[
Amo18
]
, the proof now concludes with a convexity argument. As the function
\varphi_{n,y}(x)=s_{n}(x,y)
is convex and recalling that
|x|_{1}=1
, we have by Lemma
3.5

s_{n}(z^{(n,h)},y)=s_{n}\left(\frac{1}{|G|}\sum_{\sigma\in G}\sigma(x),y\right)\leq\sum_{\sigma\in G}\frac{1}{|G|}s_{n}(\sigma(x),y)=\frac{s_{n}(x,y)}{|x|_{1}}.

From this, using the symmetry of
s_{n}
by inverting the roles of
x
and
y
, and recalling that
|y|_{1}=|x|_{1}=1
, we get

\frac{s_{n}(z^{(n,h)},y)}{|y|_{1}}=\frac{s_{n}(y,z^{(n,h)})}{|y|_{1}}\geq s_{n}(z^{(n,k)},z^{(n,h)})=s_{n}(z^{(n,h)},z^{(n,k)})

which concludes the proof of (
8
) and of Proposition
3.6
.
∎

The next and last step to prove Proposition
3.3
is to provide an asymptotic estimate for the quantity
c_{n}
. This is done in the following proposition.

Proposition 3.7
.

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}{c_{n}}\sqrt{\frac{\pi n}{2}}=1.

The proof of Proposition
3.7
relies on the following two lemmas. The first gives a closed formula for the quantity
s_{n}(z^{(n,h)},z^{(n,k)})
which equals the one in
[
Amo18
, Lemma 2.3]
, even though, we notice that the definition of the function
s_{n}
therein is slightly different from ours.

Lemma 3.8
.

Let
y\in H_{n}
and
h,k\in\{1,\dots,n-1\}
. Then

(9)

s_{n}(z^{(n,h)},y)=\frac{n}{2h(n-h)}\binom{n}{h}^{-1}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}S\subset\{1,\dots,n\}\\
|S|=h\end{subarray}}\left|\sum_{j\in S}y_{j}\right|

and

(10)

s_{n}(z^{(n,h)},z^{(n,k)})=\frac{n^{2}(h-\lfloor\frac{hk}{n}\rfloor)(k-\lfloor\frac{hk}{n}\rfloor)}{2hk(n-h)(n-k)}\binom{n}{h}^{-1}\binom{k}{\lfloor\frac{hk}{n}\rfloor}\binom{n-k}{h-\lfloor\frac{hk}{n}\rfloor}

where for a real number
x
,
\lfloor x\rfloor
denotes as usual the greatest integer less than or equal to
x
.

Proof.

By the definition of
s_{n}
and
z^{(n,h)}
, and recalling that, for
y=(y_{1},\ldots,y_{n})\in H_{n}
, we also have
\sigma(y)\in H_{n}
, which is equivalent to
\sum_{j=1}^{n}y_{\sigma(j)}=0
for all
\sigma\in\mathfrak{A}_{n}
, we have

\displaystyle s_{n}(z^{(n,h)},y)

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{n!}\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{A}_{n}}\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}z_{j}^{(n,h)}y_{\sigma(j)}\right|

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{n!}\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{A}_{n}}\left|\frac{1}{2h}\sum_{j=1}^{h}y_{\sigma(j)}-\frac{1}{2(n-h)}\sum_{j=h+1}^{n}y_{\sigma(j)}\right|

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{n!}\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{A}_{n}}\left|\left(\frac{1}{2h}+\frac{1}{2(n-h)}\right)\sum_{j=1}^{h}y_{\sigma(j)}\right|

\displaystyle=\frac{n}{h(n-h)}\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{A}_{n}}\left|\sum_{j=1}^{h}y_{\sigma(j)}\right|.

To conclude the proof of (
9
) we need to show that

\frac{n}{h(n-h)}\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{A}_{n}}\left|\sum_{j=1}^{h}y_{\sigma(j)}\right|=\frac{n}{2h(n-h)}\binom{n}{h}^{-1}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}S\subset\{1,\dots,n\}\\
|S|=h\end{subarray}}\left|\sum_{j\in S}y_{j}\right|.

Let
S\subset\{1,\dots,n\}
such that
|S|=h
. Let

G_{S}:=\{\sigma\in\mathfrak{A}_{n}\mid\sigma(\{1,\ldots,h\})=S\}

and

G^{\prime}_{S}:=\{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}\mid\sigma(\{1,\ldots,h\})=S\}\simeq\mathfrak{S}_{h}\times\mathfrak{S}_{n-h}.

We suppose first that
h\leq n-2
. If
\tau
is the transposition switching
n
with
n-1
, the map
\sigma\mapsto\sigma\circ\tau
is a bijection between the sets
G_{S}
and
G^{\prime}_{S}\setminus G_{S}
.
In particular

|G_{S}|=\frac{|G^{\prime}_{S}|}{2}=\frac{h!(n-h)!}{2}

providing the sought-for equality. If
h=n-1
, we use the same argument, but with
\tau=(1\;2)
this time.

The proof of (
10
) now is precisely the one done in the proof of
[
Amo18
, Lemma 2.3]
.
∎

We recall the following result which gives the asymptotic estimate for (
10
).

Lemma 3.9

(
[
Amo18
, Lemma 2.4]
)
.

Let
(n_{m})_{m}
,
(h_{m})_{m}
,
(k_{m})_{m}
be sequences of real numbers satisfying
0<h_{m},k_{m}<n_{m}
and such that

\underset{m\rightarrow+\infty}{\lim}n_{m}=+\infty,\quad\underset{m\rightarrow+\infty}{\lim}\frac{h_{m}}{n_{m}}=u,\quad\underset{m\rightarrow+\infty}{\lim}\frac{k_{m}}{n_{m}}=v

for some
u,v\in[0,1]
. Then (with the convention
1/0=+\infty
), we have

\underset{m\rightarrow+\infty}{\lim}2\sqrt{n_{m}}\cdot s_{n_{m}}\left(z^{(n_{m},h_{m})},z^{(n_{m},k_{m})}\right)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi uv(1-u)(1-v)}}.

We can now prove the estimate for
c_{n}
from Proposition
3.7
.

Proof of Proposition
3.7
.

For an integer
m\geq 1
, let
h_{m}
and
k_{m}
be the indices where the minimum is attained in the definition of
c_{m}
. Applying Lemma
3.9
to the sequences
(h_{m})_{m}
,
(k_{m})_{m}
and
(n_{m})_{m}=(m)_{m}
, we obtain

\underset{m\rightarrow+\infty}{\lim}2{\sqrt{2\pi uv(1-u)(1-v)m}}\cdot s_{m}(z^{(m,h_{m}},z^{(m,k_{m})}))=1

for some
u,v\in[0,1]
,
and using the fact that

\underset{0<u,v<1}{\max}uv(1-u)(1-v)=\frac{1}{16}

we get the asymptotic estimate (
3.7
).
∎

We have now collected all the results needed to prove Proposition
3.3
.

Proof of Proposition
3.3
.

Combining Proposition
3.4
and Proposition
3.6
we get

c_{n}\frac{2\log(M(\beta))}{n}|y|_{1}\leq\frac{1}{n}\left(\frac{4}{n!}\log(M(\alpha))\right)\leq\frac{2\log(M(\beta))}{n}|y|_{1}

or equivalently

\log(M(\beta)^{c_{n}|y|_{1}})\leq\log(M(\alpha)^{2/n!})\leq\log(M(\beta)^{|y|_{1}}).

The result follows by taking the exponential and using Proposition
3.7
.
∎

To conclude the proof of Theorem
1.4
, we need the following lemma:

Lemma 3.10
.

Let
n\geq 5
and let
y=(y_{1},\ldots,y_{n})\in H_{n}
be such that
y_{j+1}-y_{j}\geq 1
for all
j=1,\dots,n-2
.
Then

(1)

If
y_{n}-y_{n-1}\geq 1
, then

|y|_{1}\geq\frac{n-2}{4}.

(2)

If
y_{n-1}=y_{n}
, then

|y|_{1}\geq\frac{n-3}{5}.

Proof.

The proof of (
1
) is given in
[
Amo18
, Lemma on p. 1614]
.
For the proof of point (
2
), let
1\leq k\leq n-1
be such that
y_{k}\leq 0<y_{k+1}
.
Notice that
k\leq n-2
as
y_{n-1}=y_{n}
by hypothesis.

We then have,
y_{j}\leq y_{k}-(k-j)\leq-(k-j)
for
1\leq j\leq k
and
y_{j}\geq y_{k+1}+(j-k-1)\geq j-k-1
for
k+1\leq j\leq n-1
. So

\displaystyle n|y|_{1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{k}(-y_{j})+\sum_{j=k+1}^{n-1}y_{j}+|y_{n}|\geq\sum_{h=0}^{k-1}h+\sum_{h=0}^{n-k-2}h

\displaystyle=\frac{(k-1)k}{2}+\frac{(n-k-2)(n-k-1)}{2}\geq\frac{(n-1)(n-3)}{4}.

∎

We can finally conclude the proof of Theorem
1.4
.

Proof of point (
2
) of Theorem
1.4
.

If
\alpha=\beta_{1}^{a_{1}}\dots\beta_{n}^{a_{n}}
is a generator of
\rm{Gal}(\mathbb{Q}(\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{n})/\mathbb{Q})
then, by point (
1
) of Theorem
1.4
, we have

|\{a_{i}\mid 1\leq i\leq n\}|\geq n-1.

If the
a_{i}
’s are all distinct, then so are the
y_{i}
’s defined as

y_{j}=a_{j}-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}.

As all
a_{i}
’s are integers,
|y_{\ell}-y_{k}|\geq 1
for
\ell\neq k
and moreover, since for every
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
we have
|\sigma(y)|_{1}=|y|_{1}
, up to reordering the terms we can suppose that
y_{j+1}-y_{j}\geq 1
for all
1\leq j\leq n-1
.
Thus by Lemma
3.10
(
1
) we have
|y|_{1}\geq\frac{n-2}{4}
.

If two
a_{i}
’s are equal, so are the corresponding
y_{i}
’s. Reasoning as before, up to reordering the
a_{i}
’s we might assume that
y_{j+1}-y_{j}\geq 1
for
j=1,\dots,n-2
and
y_{n-1}=y_{n}
. So, by Lemma
3.10
(
2
) we have
|y|_{1}\geq\frac{n-3}{5}
.

In all cases, we have
|y|_{1}\geq\frac{n-3}{5}
, and therefore, using also Proposition
3.7
, we get the asymptotic, as
n
tends to infinity

c_{n}|y|_{1}\sim\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi n}}|y|_{1}\geq\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi n}}\left(\frac{n-3}{5}\right)\sim\sqrt{\frac{2n}{25\pi}}.

This, together with Proposition
3.3
gives

M(\alpha)^{\frac{2}{n!}}\geq M(\beta)^{(1+o(1))\sqrt{\frac{2n}{25\pi}}}

and taking logarithms we have

h(\alpha)\geq(1+o(1))\sqrt{\frac{2n}{25\pi}}\log(M(\beta)).

By the explicit version of Dobrowolski’s theorem by Voutier
[
Vou96
, Theorem, p. 83]
, we have

\log(M(\beta))\geq\frac{1}{4}\left(\frac{\log(\log(n))}{\log(n)}\right)^{3}

concluding the proof.
∎

4.
Proof of Theorem
1.5

The aim of this section is to prove Theorem
1.5
, which is the analogue of Theorem
1.4
for
additive
generators, that is generators obtained as linear combinations of conjugates.

As usual, if
\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{n}\in\overline{\mathbb{Q}}
is a full set of Galois conjugates, when writing
\mathfrak{A}_{n}\subseteq\mathrm{Gal}(\mathbb{Q}(\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{n})/\mathbb{Q})
, we identify
\sigma\in\mathfrak{A}_{n}
with the automorphism
\sigma(\beta_{i})=\beta_{\sigma(i)}
. As before, we split the proof in different parts for clarity.

4.1.
Proof of Theorem
1.5
, point (
1
)

The proof of this point is the same, in additive notation, as the one carried in Section
3.1
and it is even simpler than its multiplicative variant.
We begin with the following additive version of Proposition
3.1
.

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
\beta
be an algebraic number of degree
n\geq 5
with conjugates
\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{n}
over
\mathbb{Q}
. Suppose that
\mathfrak{A}_{n}\subset\mathrm{Gal}(\mathbb{Q}(\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{n})/\mathbb{Q})
. Then, for any integers
v_{1},\ldots,v_{n}
, not all equal, we have

\sum_{i=1}^{n}v_{i}\beta_{i}\not\in\mathbb{Q}.

Proof.

The proof is similar to that of Proposition
3.1
, and we identify the elements of
\mathrm{Gal}(\mathbb{Q}(\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{n})/\mathbb{Q})
with permutations in the usual way.

Assume that
v_{1}\neq v_{2}
and that there exists
r\in\mathbb{Q}
such that

\sum_{i=1}^{n}v_{i}\beta_{i}=r.

Applying the cycle
(1\;3\;2)
to both sides of this equality we obtain
{(v_{1}-v_{2})}\beta_{1}+{(v_{2}-v_{3})}\beta_{2}+{(v_{3}-v_{1})}\beta_{3}=1
.
Applying to this the cycle
(1\;i\;j)
, for
i\neq j
and
i,j\geq 4
, we obtain
(v_{1}-v_{2})\beta_{1}=({v_{1}-v_{2}})\beta_{i}
for all
i\geq 4
. Letting act on both sides of this equality
the cycles
(1\;2\;k)
and
(1\;3\;\ell)
for
k,\ell\not\in\{2,3,i\}
, we obtain
({v_{1}-v_{2}})\beta_{1}=({v_{1}-v_{2}})\beta_{i}
for all
1\leq i\leq n
.
So
\beta_{1}=\ldots=\beta_{n}
and
\beta\in\mathbb{Q}
, which contradicts the hypothesis that
\mathfrak{A}_{n}\subset\mathrm{Gal}(\mathbb{Q}(\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{n})/\mathbb{Q})
.
∎

We can now conclude the proof of the first part of Theorem
1.5
.

Proof of point
(1)
of Theorem
1.5
.

The proof follows exactly the one of point (
1
) of Theorem
1.4
, by using the additive notation instead of the multiplicative one.

Suppose that
\alpha=a_{1}\beta_{1}+\ldots+a_{n}\beta_{n}
is such that
\mathbb{Q}(\alpha)=\mathbb{Q}(\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{n})
. If
|\{a_{i}\mid 1\leq n\leq i\}|<n-1
then one can show that there exists a non-trivial
\sigma\in\mathrm{Gal}(\mathbb{Q}(\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{n})/\mathbb{Q})
such that
\sigma(\alpha)=\alpha
(we take
\sigma=(i\;j\;k)
if
a_{i}=a_{j}=a_{k}
or
\sigma=(i\;j)(k\;\ell)
if
a_{i}=a_{j}
and
a_{k}=a_{\ell}
).

For the converse, assume that
|\{a_{i}\mid 1\leq i\leq n\}|\geq n-1
, let
\sigma\in\mathrm{Gal}(\mathbb{Q}(\beta_{1},...,\beta_{n})/\mathbb{Q}(\alpha))
and
\tau=\sigma^{-1}
.
The equality
\sigma(\alpha)=\alpha
gives
\sum_{k=1}^{n}{(a_{k}-a_{\tau(k)})}\beta_{k}=0
.
By Proposition
4.1
, there exists an integer
c
such that
a_{k}-a_{\tau(k)}=c
for all
k
.
If
\ell
if the order of
\tau
, we have,
a_{1}=a_{1}+c\ell
, so
c=0
and
a_{\tau(k)}=a_{k}
for all
k
.
As
|\{a_{i},1\leq i\leq n\}|\geq n-1
, we have that
\tau
is either the identity or a transposition, but this last case cannot occur as
\tau\in\mathfrak{A}_{n}
.
∎

4.2.
Proof of Theorem
1.5
, point (
2
)

This section deals with the proof of the last part of Theorem
1.5
.

First, as in the multiplicative case, we notice that one can easily establish the following upper bound for the height of
\alpha
:

Proposition 4.2
.

Let
\beta
be an algebraic integer of degree
n\geq 5
, let
\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{n}
be its conjugates and suppose that
\mathrm{Gal}(\mathbb{Q}(\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{n})/\mathbb{Q})=\mathfrak{A}_{n}
. Let
a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}
be integers and suppose that

\alpha=a_{1}\beta_{1}+\ldots+a_{n}\beta_{n}
is a generator of
\mathbb{Q}(\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{n})/\mathbb{Q}
. Then

\log\left(M(\beta)\sum_{i=1}^{n}|a_{i}|\right)\geq h(\alpha).

Proof.

It suffices to observe that, for every
\sigma\in\mathfrak{A}_{n}
, one has

\displaystyle|\sigma(\alpha)|

\displaystyle=\left|\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}\beta_{\sigma(i)}\right|\leq\max_{1\leq i\leq n}|\beta_{i}|\sum_{i=1}^{n}|a_{i}|\leq M(\beta)\sum_{i=1}^{n}|a_{i}|.

Notice that, since
\beta
is an algebraic integer, we have

M(\alpha)\leq\max_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{A}_{n}}|\sigma(\alpha)|^{\frac{n!}{2}},

and taking logarithms, this allows us to conclude.
∎

The proof of the lower bound in Theorem
1.5
, point (
2
), is instead more delicate. Before starting it we fix the setting and introduce some notation.
Let
\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{n}
be algebraic integers of degree
n\geq 5
and let
a_{1},\dots,a_{n}
be integers. Let

(11)

\alpha=\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}\beta_{i}

and assume from this point onward that
\mathbb{Q}(\alpha)=\mathbb{Q}(\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{n})
and that
\mathrm{Gal}(\mathbb{Q}(\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{n})/\mathbb{Q})=\mathfrak{A}_{n}
.

We now recall some notation from
[
Amo18
, Section 4]
. For
\tau\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
, we let

\alpha_{\tau}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}\beta_{\tau(i)},

where
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
acts on
\mathbb{Q}(\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{n})
as usual by
\sigma(\beta_{i})=\beta_{\sigma(i)}
.

We observe that for
\sigma\in\mathfrak{A}_{n}
, we have
\sigma(\alpha)=\alpha_{\sigma}
. Also, for
\tau,\tau^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
, we have

(12)

\alpha_{\tau\tau^{\prime}}=(\alpha_{\tau^{\prime}})_{\tau}.

One of the key ideas in the proof of the last part of Theorem
1.5
, and one of the main novelties of our article, is the following lemma, which describes how the Mahler measure of
\alpha
changes under the action of an element that
should not act on
\alpha
. More precisely, it compares
M(\alpha)
and
M(\alpha_{\tau})
, in the case where
\tau
is a transposition, so, by hypothesis, an element extraneous to
\mathrm{Gal}(\mathbb{Q}(\alpha)/\mathbb{Q})
.

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
\tau\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
be a transposition, then

M(\alpha_{\tau})\leq 5^{n}M(\alpha)^{5}.

Proof.

For simplicity, we may suppose that
\tau=(1\;2)
.

Let
\sigma=(1\;3\;2)
and set

\gamma=\alpha-\sigma(\alpha)=\beta_{1}(a_{1}-a_{2})+\beta_{2}(a_{2}-a_{3})+\beta_{3}(a_{3}-a_{1}).

If
\sigma^{\prime}=(1\;4\;5)
and
\delta=(4\;2)(3\;5)
, then

\delta(\gamma-\sigma^{\prime}(\gamma))=\delta((\beta_{1}-\beta_{4})(a_{1}-a_{2}))=(\beta_{1}-\beta_{2})(a_{1}-a_{2})=\alpha-\alpha_{\tau}.

So we have

(13)

\alpha_{\tau}=\alpha-\delta(\alpha)+\delta\sigma(\alpha)+\delta\sigma^{\prime}(\alpha)-\delta\sigma^{\prime}\sigma(\alpha).

Notice that
\delta,\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{A}_{n}
and so is their product, hence, by the above equality,
\alpha_{\tau}
equals a linear combination of conjugates of
\alpha
. By elementary inequalities for the height and its invariance under Galois action, we obtain that
h(\alpha_{\tau})\leq 5h(\alpha)+\log{5}
. By using that
M(\alpha)=e^{nh(\alpha)}
, we have the sought result.
∎

We now use Lemma
4.3
to get a lower bound for
M(\alpha)
independent on the transposition
\tau
.
To this aim we need to introduce some more notation.

Let
T_{n}
be the set of transpositions of
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
and,
for
\tau=(i,j)\in T_{n}
, let
A_{\tau}
be the set of permutations of
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
with support
\{1,\ldots,n\}\setminus\{i,j\}
and without orbits of length 2.

For
n\geq 3
, let
\Lambda_{n}
be the set of permutations of
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
without orbits of length
1
or
2
.
Let also

\Delta=\prod_{\tau\in T_{n}}\prod_{\sigma\in A_{\tau}}|\alpha_{\sigma\tau}-\alpha_{\sigma}|

and, for a vector of integers
\mathfrak{a}=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})
, let

V(\mathfrak{a})=\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq n}(a_{j}-a_{i}).

For an algebraic integer
\beta
of degree
n
and conjugates
\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{n}
we also let

\mathrm{disc}(\beta)=\mathrm{disc}(1,\beta,\beta^{2},\dots,\beta^{n-1})=\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq n}(\beta_{i}-\beta_{j})^{2}.

We have the following result.

Proposition 4.4
.

Let
n\geq 5
, and let
\alpha=\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}\beta_{i}
be as in (
11
). Set
\beta=\beta_{1}
and
\mathfrak{a}=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})
. Then

M(\alpha)\geq 5^{-n/6}\left(2^{-|T_{n}|}|V(\mathfrak{a})||\mathrm{disc}(\beta)|^{1/2}\right)^{|\Lambda_{n-2}|/6}.

Proof.

For
\tau=(i\;j)\in T_{n}
with
1\leq i<j\leq n
and
\sigma\in A_{\tau}
, we have

\alpha_{\sigma\tau}-\alpha_{\sigma}=(a_{j}-a_{i})(\beta_{i}-\beta_{j}).

Thus,

(14)

\Delta=(|V(\mathfrak{a})||\mathrm{disc}(\beta)|^{1/2})^{|\Lambda_{n-2}|}

as for each
\tau\in T_{n}
,
|A_{\tau}|=|\Lambda_{n-2}|
.

We now want to upper bound
\Delta
. Clearly we have that

\Delta\leq\prod_{\tau\in T_{n}}\prod_{\sigma\in A_{\tau}}2\max(|\alpha_{\sigma\tau}|,1)\max(|\alpha_{\sigma}|,1).

Let
\tau
,
\tau^{\prime}\in T_{n}
, and let
\sigma\in A_{\tau}
,
\sigma^{\prime}\in A_{\tau^{\prime}}
. Then
\sigma\tau\neq\sigma^{\prime}
(since they have different supports). If
\tau=\tau^{\prime}
and
\sigma\neq\sigma^{\prime}
then
\tau\sigma\neq\tau^{\prime}\sigma^{\prime}
. If
\tau\neq\tau^{\prime}
then
\sigma\tau\neq\sigma^{\prime}\tau^{\prime}
(since
\sigma
has no orbits of length
2
), and
\sigma\neq\sigma^{\prime}
(since the supports of
\sigma
and
\sigma^{\prime}
are not the same).

Thus, from the last inequality we deduce

\Delta\leq 2^{|T_{n}||\Lambda_{n-2}|}\prod_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}}\max(|\alpha_{\sigma}|,1).

Writing
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
as the semidirect product of
\mathfrak{A}_{n}
and a transposition
\tau
, that we shall fix, from the last inequality we have

\displaystyle\Delta

\displaystyle\leq 2^{|T_{n}||\Lambda_{n-2}|}\prod_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{A}_{n}}\max(|\alpha_{\sigma}|,1)\prod_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{A}_{n}}\max(|\alpha_{\tau\sigma}|,1)

\displaystyle=2^{|T_{n}||\Lambda_{n-2}|}\prod_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{A}_{n}}\max(|\sigma(\alpha)|,1)\prod_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{A}_{n}}\max(|\sigma(\alpha_{\tau})|,1)

hence by (
12
) we get

\Delta\leq 2^{|T_{n}||\Lambda_{n-2}|}M(\alpha)M(\alpha_{\tau}).

From Lemma
4.3
and the above inequality, we have

\Delta\leq 2^{|T_{n}||\Lambda_{n-2}|}M(\alpha)M(\alpha_{\tau})\leq 2^{|T_{n}||\Lambda_{n-2}|}5^{n}M(\alpha)^{6}.

By using the equality (
14
), we obtain

\left(|V(\mathfrak{a})||\mathrm{disc}(\beta)|^{1/2}\right)^{|\Lambda_{n-2}|}\leq 2^{|T_{n}||\Lambda_{n-2}|}5^{n}M(\alpha)^{6}

which proves the result.
∎

We now proceed to give estimate for the quantities involved in the bound from Proposition
4.4
.
The following lemma is an improvement of
[
Amo18
, Lemma, p. 1615]
.

Lemma 4.5
.

For
n\geq 3
, we have

|\Lambda_{n}|\geq\frac{n!}{8}.

Proof.

Let
d_{n}
be the
n
-th derangement number, i.e., the number of permutations of
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
without fixed points. It is known that (
[
CK92
, Corollary on p. 163]
)

d_{n}=n!\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{(-1)^{k}}{k!}.

By the inclusion–exclusion principle, we have

|\Lambda_{n}|\geq d_{n}-\frac{n(n-1)}{2}d_{n-2}.

Letting

f(n)=\frac{\left(d_{n}-\frac{n(n-1)}{2}d_{n-2}\right)}{n!},

we want to show that
f(n)\geq f(4)={1}/{8}
for all
n\geq 3
.
We can check it numerically for
n=3,4,5
. Therefore we may assume
n\geq 6
.
We have

f(n)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=0}^{n-2}\frac{(-1)^{k}}{k!}+\frac{(-1)^{n-1}}{(n-1)!}+\frac{(-1)^{n}}{n!}\geq\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=0}^{n-2}\frac{(-1)^{k}}{k!}+\frac{1-n}{n!}

where, for the last inequality, a proof by exhaustion based on the parity of
n
is sufficient.
The study of alternating series gives

\sum_{k=4}^{n-2}\frac{(-1)^{k}}{k!}\geq 0

or equivalently,

\sum_{k=0}^{n-2}\frac{(-1)^{k}}{k!}\geq\sum_{k=0}^{3}\frac{(-1)^{k}}{k!}.

Additionally, we observe that the sequence
(\frac{1-n}{n!})_{n\geq 2}
is increasing. Thus for
n\geq 6
, we have

f(n)\geq\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=0}^{3}\frac{(-1)^{k}}{k!}+\frac{1-6}{6!}=\frac{23}{144}\geq\frac{1}{8}.

This concludes the proof.
∎

Proof of point
(2)
of Theorem
1.5
.

Notice that the statement is trivial for
5\leq n\leq 9
as
M(\alpha)
is always bigger or equal than 1.
So, from now on, we can assume that
n\geq 10
.

By Lemma
4.5
, since
n-2\geq 3
, we have

(15)

|\Lambda_{n-2}|\geq\frac{(n-2)!}{8}.

Also, one has

(16)

|T_{n}|=\frac{n(n-1)}{2}\text{ and }|\mathrm{disc(\beta)}|\geq 1.

Now, in order to use Proposition
4.4
to lower bound
M(\alpha)
, we are left to estimate
|V(\mathfrak{a})|
. Notice that this quantity is zero precisely when the
a_{i}
’a are not all distinct, in which case the estimate in Proposition
4.4
is trivial.
We then carry on the proof distinguishing two cases.

Case (a): all
a_{i}
’s are distinct

In this case, like in
[
Amo18
, p. 1616]
, we are going to establish a lower bound for
|V(\mathfrak{a})|\neq 0
. Since
|V(\mathfrak{a})|
is symmetric with respect to
a_{i}
, we may assume for simplicity that
a_{j+1}-a_{j}\geq 1
for
j=1,\dots,n-1
.
Then, we have

|V(\mathfrak{a})|=\prod_{i=1}^{n}\prod_{j=i+1}^{n}|a_{j}-a_{i}|\geq\prod_{i=1}^{n}\prod_{j=i+1}^{n}(j-i)=\prod_{h=1}^{n-1}h!.

By
[
Vou96
, Lemma 1, p. 84]
we have

|V(\mathfrak{a})|\geq\prod_{h=1}^{n-1}h!\geq\exp\left(\frac{n^{2}\log(n)}{2}-\frac{3n^{2}}{4}\right).

We notice that, as
n\geq 10
, we have

2^{-\frac{n(n-1)}{2}}\exp\left(\frac{n^{2}\log(n)}{2}-\frac{3n^{2}}{4}\right)\geq 1.

Therefore, using this, (
15
) and (
16
),
Proposition
4.4
gives

\displaystyle M(\alpha)^{\frac{2}{n!}}

\displaystyle\geq\left(5^{-\frac{n}{6}}\left(2^{-\frac{n(n-1)}{2}}\exp\left(\frac{n^{2}\log(n)}{2}-\frac{3n^{2}}{4}\right)\right)^{\frac{(n-2)!}{48}}\right)^{\frac{2}{n!}}

\displaystyle=5^{-\frac{1}{3(n-1)!}}2^{-\frac{1}{48}}\exp\left(\frac{n}{n-1}\left(\frac{\log(n)}{48}-\frac{1}{32}\right)\right).

Notice that, as
n\geq 10
, we have
\frac{\log(n)}{48}\geq\frac{1}{32}
and so

\exp\left(\frac{n}{n-1}\left(\frac{\log(n)}{48}-\frac{1}{32}\right)\right)\geq\exp\left(\frac{\log(n)}{48}-\frac{1}{32}\right).

Finally

\displaystyle M(\alpha)^{\frac{2}{n!}}

\displaystyle\geq 5^{-\frac{1}{9!3}}2^{-\frac{1}{48}}\exp\left(\frac{\log(n)}{48}-\frac{1}{32}\right)\geq\left(\frac{n}{9}\right)^{\frac{1}{240}}.

Case (b) : the
a_{i}
’s are not all distinct

Now we suppose that two of the
a_{i}
’s are equal, for instance
a_{1}=a_{2}
. We set

r=2\max_{1\leq i,j\leq n}(|a_{j}-a_{i}|)

and

\alpha^{\prime}=\alpha+r(\beta_{1}-\beta_{2}).

Clearly

\alpha^{\prime}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}^{\prime}\beta_{i}

where
a_{1}^{\prime}=a_{1}+r\in\mathbb{Z}
,
a_{2}^{\prime}=a_{2}-r\in\mathbb{Z}
, and
a_{i}^{\prime}=a_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}
for all
i\geq 3
.

Since
a_{1}=a_{2}
and
\alpha
is a generator of
\mathbb{Q}(\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{n})/\mathbb{Q}
, we know, by point (
1
) of Theorem
1.5
, that the
a_{i}
’s are all distinct for
3\leq i\leq n
.

The same holds for the
a_{i}^{\prime}
’s for
3\leq i\leq n
. Of course, we have
a_{1}^{\prime}\neq a_{2}^{\prime}
. Then, by the choice of
r
, we also have
a_{1}^{\prime},a_{2}^{\prime}\notin\{a_{i}^{\prime}\mid 3\leq i\leq n\}
.

Hence, again by point (
1
) of Theorem
1.5
,
\alpha^{\prime}
is a generator of
\mathbb{Q}(\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{n})/\mathbb{Q}
and the integers
a_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,a_{n}^{\prime}
are all distinct.

Thus, according to case (a) of the proof, we have

(17)

M(\alpha^{\prime})^{\frac{2}{n!}}\geq 5^{-\frac{1}{9!3}}2^{-\frac{1}{48}}n^{\frac{1}{48}}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{32}\right).

We now want to compare
M(\alpha)
and
M(\alpha^{\prime})
. We will need the following result, which plays here the role of Lemma
4.3
.

Lemma 4.6
.

Let
n\geq 5
and
i,j,k,l\in\{1,\dots,n\}
. Then

h(\alpha+2(a_{i}-a_{j})(\beta_{k}-\beta_{l}))\leq 5h(\alpha)+\log(16).

Proof.

We may suppose
i\neq j
and
k\neq l
otherwise the result is trivial. Let
\tau=(i\;j)\in T_{n}
.
Then

\alpha-\alpha_{\tau}=(a_{i}-a_{j})(\beta_{i}-\beta_{j}).

By (
13
) from Lemma
4.3
we also have

\alpha-\alpha_{\tau}=\sigma_{1}(\alpha)+\sigma_{2}(\alpha)-\sigma_{3}(\alpha)-\sigma_{4}(\alpha)

for some
\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2},\sigma_{3},\sigma_{4}\in\mathfrak{A}_{n}
.

As
n\geq 5
, we can check that there is
\sigma\in\mathfrak{A}_{n}
such that
\sigma(\beta_{i}-\beta_{j})=\beta_{k}-\beta_{l}
(if
i\neq k
and
j\neq\ell
one can simply take
\sigma=(i\;k)(j\;\ell)
, while if
i=k
and
j\neq\ell
one can take
\sigma=(j,\ell)(s,t)
for some
s\neq t
and
s,t\not\in\{k=i,j,\ell\}
).
By properties of the height, we have

\displaystyle h(\alpha+2(a_{i}-a_{j})(\beta_{k}-\beta_{l}))

\displaystyle=h(\alpha+2(\alpha-\alpha_{\tau}))

\displaystyle=h(\alpha+2(\sigma_{1}(\alpha)+\sigma_{2}(\alpha)-\sigma_{3}(\alpha)-\sigma_{4}(\alpha)))

\displaystyle\leq 5h(\alpha)+\log(16).

∎

Returning to our proof, by Lemma
4.6

\displaystyle h(\alpha^{\prime})

\displaystyle=h\left(\alpha+2\max_{1\leq i,j\leq n}(|a_{j}-a_{i}|)(\beta_{1}-\beta_{2})\right)\leq 5h(\alpha)+\log(16)

hence
M(\alpha^{\prime})\leq 16^{n}M(\alpha)^{5}
.
Using also (
17
), we get

16^{\frac{2}{(n-1)!}}M(\alpha)^{\frac{10}{n!}}\geq 5^{-\frac{1}{9!3}}2^{-\frac{1}{48}}n^{\frac{1}{48}}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{32}\right).

Finally, recalling that
n\geq 10
, we get

\displaystyle M(\alpha)^{\frac{2}{n!}}

\displaystyle\geq 16^{-\frac{2}{9!5}}5^{-\frac{2}{9!30}}2^{-\frac{1}{240}}n^{\frac{1}{240}}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{160}\right)\geq\left(\frac{n}{9}\right)^{\frac{1}{240}}.

∎

5.
Some examples and final remarks

In this section we give some application of our results to explicit families of algebraic numbers generating Galois extensions of
\mathbb{Q}
with groups
\mathfrak{A}_{n}
. We also discuss the adaptability of the proof strategy of our main results to generators of Galois extensions having group other than
\mathfrak{A}_{n}
or
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
.

5.1.
On certain Laguerre polynomials

A family of particular interest for our applications is that of generalized Laguerre polynomials, defined, for
n\geq 1
and
\alpha\in\mathbb{Q}
, as

L_{n}^{(\alpha)}(x)=\sum_{j=0}^{n}\binom{n+\alpha}{n-j}\frac{(-x)^{j}}{j!}

where

\binom{n+\alpha}{n-j}=\frac{(n+\alpha)(n-1+\alpha)\dots(j+1+\alpha)}{(n-j)!j!}.

This family, first studied by Schur, has received the attention of many authors (see for instance
[
Gow89
]
,
[
FKT12
]
,
[
BFFL13
]
,
[
Ban14
]
,
[
BB20
]
, and
[
JL22
]
for some reference on the topic).

The following result considers a special family of Laguerre polynomials having Galois group
\mathfrak{A}_{n}
:

Proposition 5.1
.

Let
n\geq 1
be an integer divisible by
4
and let
\beta_{n,1},\ldots,\beta_{n,n}
be the roots of the polynomial

L_{n}^{(-n-1)}(x)=1+x+\frac{x^{2}}{2}+\dots+\frac{x^{n}}{n!}.

Let
a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}
be integers such that
|\{a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}\}|\geq n-1
. Then:

(a)

If
\alpha_{n}=\beta_{n,1}^{a_{1}}\cdots\beta_{n,n}^{a_{n}}
and
\sum_{i=0}^{n}a_{i}\neq 0
one has that
\mathbb{Q}(\alpha_{n})/\mathbb{Q}
is a Galois extension with Galois group
\mathfrak{A}_{n}
and

h(\alpha_{n})
tends to infinity with
n
.

(b)

If
\alpha^{\prime}_{n}=a_{1}\beta_{n,1}+\ldots+a_{n}\beta_{n,n}
, then
\mathbb{Q}(\alpha^{\prime}_{n})/\mathbb{Q}
is a Galois extension with Galois group
\mathfrak{A}_{n}
and
h(\alpha^{\prime}_{n})
tends to infinity with
n
.

Proof.

Schur showed in
[
Sch30
]
that
L_{n}^{(-n-1)}(x)
is irreducible and its Galois group over
\mathbb{Q}
is
\mathfrak{A}_{n}
if
n
is divisible by
4
(while it is
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
otherwise).
Let
\beta_{n}
be one of the roots of
L_{n}^{(-n-1)}(x)
.

We start by proving part (
a
). By point (
1
) of Theorem
1.4
, we know that
\mathbb{Q}(\alpha)
is the splitting field of
L_{n}^{(-n-1)}(x)
over
\mathbb{Q}
. This proves the statement on the Galois group.
Notice also that that
N_{\mathbb{Q}(\beta)/\mathbb{Q}}(\beta)=n!
, hence, by Theorem
1.3
,

M(\alpha)^{1/[\mathbb{Q}(\alpha):\mathbb{Q}]}\geq(n!)^{\frac{1}{n}\left|\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}\right|}

and, since
\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}\neq 0

we deduce the statement on
h(\alpha_{n})
.

To prove part (
b
), we simply notice that all hypotheses of Theorem
1.5
are satisfied, and we can conclude straightforwardly.
∎

Remark 5.2
.

We remark that our Theorem
1.4
cannot be applied to any Laguerre polynomial, while
[
Amo18
, Theorems 1.1 and 1.2]
can. Indeed, to apply point (
2
) of Theorem
1.4
, we need that
\beta
is a unit. However the roots of
L_{n}^{(\alpha)}(x)
are units if and only if
\alpha=0
, and it follows from
[
FL02
]
and
[
Haj05
]

that, for all but finitely many integers
n
,
L_{n}^{(\alpha)}(x)
is irreducible over
\mathbb{Q}
and its Galois group is
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
.

5.2.
On the adaptation of the strategy to other subgroups of
\mathfrak{S}_{n}

One might question whether the proofs of Theorems
1.4
and
1.5
, as well as their counterparts for
\mathfrak{S}_{n}

in
[
Amo18
]
, could be modified to identify generators of Galois extensions with different Galois groups.

On the proof of point (
1
)

We first notice that, in all the above cited results, the necessary and sufficient conditions on the
a_{i}
’s so that
\mathbb{Q}(\alpha)/\mathbb{Q}
is a Galois extension of group
G\in\{\mathfrak{S}_{n},\mathfrak{A}_{n}\}
can be rephrased by saying that
|\{a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}\}|\geq n-k+1
where
k
is the index of
G
in
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
.
It might be tempting to inquire if a similar condition holds for other subgroups
G
of
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
of index
2<k\leq n
, but no such groups exist. Indeed, by
[
Cla84
, Chap. 2, §85. Corollary p.64]
,
\mathfrak{A}_{n}
is the only subgroup of
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
of index strictly smaller than
n
, while by
[
Per96
, Proposition 8.10]
, for
n\neq 6
all the subgroups of
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
of index
n
are of the form
\{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}\mid\sigma(i)=i\}
, hence they are not transitive.

A second observation is that our proofs also use the fact that if
G
is either
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
or
\mathfrak{A}_{n}
, then
G
contains all
k
-cycles for some integer
2\leq k\leq n
(noting that
k=2
for
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
and
k=3
for
\mathfrak{A}_{n}
). However, if a subgroup
G
of
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
contains all
k
-cycles for some
k
, then it contains the subgroup generated by all of them, which is normal in
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
(as
k
-cycles form a full conjugacy class). But, for
n\geq 5
, this implies that this subgroup is either
\mathfrak{S}_{n}

On the proof of point (
2
)

We notice that, to prove point (
2
) of Theorem
1.4
, one crucial ingredient is Proposition
3.6
.
Indeed, the remainder of the results can essentially be derived using the same methodology, irrespective of the specific subgroup of
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
under consideration, by suitably accounting for the pertinent quantities.

One point used in the proof of Proposition
3.6
is that, if
G
is either
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
or
\mathfrak{A}_{n}
then, for any
A\subseteq\{1,...,n\}
, the stabilizer
\mathrm{Stab}_{G}{(A)}=\{\sigma\in G\mid\sigma(A)=A\}
is a transitive subgroup of
\mathfrak{S}(A)\simeq\mathfrak{S}_{|A|}
.
However this property does not hold for other subgroups
G
of
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
when
n
is big enough.

Indeed, notice first that any transitive subgroup of
\mathfrak{S}_{|A|}
has a cardinality divisible by
|A|
, so
|G|
must be divisible by
\mathrm{lcm}(1,\dots,n)
. In particular
|G|\geq 2^{n-1}
.

Moreover, it is easy to check that
G
must be a
2
-transitive subgroup of
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
. Indeed, let
(x_{1},y_{1}),(x_{2},y_{2})\in\{1,\dots,n\}^{2}
be such that
x_{1}\neq y_{1}
and
x_{2}\neq y_{2}
. Then there exists
g\in G
such that
g(x_{1})=x_{2}
and
g(y_{1})=y_{2}
where

-

if
y_{1}\neq x_{2}
, we can take
g=\gamma\sigma
with
\sigma\in\mathrm{Stab}_{H}(\{y_{1}\})
such that
\sigma(x_{1})=x_{2}
and
\gamma\in\mathrm{Stab}_{H}(\{x_{2}\})
such that
\gamma(y_{1})=y_{2}
(one can treat similarly the case
x_{1}\neq y_{2}
).

-

If
y_{1}=x_{2}
and
x_{1}=y_{2}
, we can take
g\in\mathrm{Stab}_{G}(\{x_{1},x_{2}\})\setminus\{\mathrm{Id}\}
, which is not empty by assumption on
G
.

Now, by the main result in
[
Bab82
]
, either
G
contains
\mathfrak{A}_{n}
or
|G|\leq\exp(\exp(1.18\sqrt{\log n}))
for all
n\geq 5\cdot 10^{5}
, contradicting
|G|\geq 2^{n-1}
for
n
big enough.
