Soft Quantization using Entropic Regularization
Rajmadan Lakshmanan, Alois Pichler

Abstract
The quantization problem aims to find the best possible approximation of probability measures on ℝ d \mathbb{R}^{d} using finite, discrete measures. The Wasserstein distance is a typical choice to measure the quality of the approximation. This contribution investigates the properties and robustness of the entropy-regularized quantization problem, which relaxes the standard quantization problem. The proposed approximation technique naturally adopts the softmin function, which is well known for its robustness in terms of theoretical and practicability standpoints. Moreover, we use the entropy-regularized Wasserstein distance to evaluate the quality of the soft quantization problem’s approximation, and we implement a stochastic gradient approach to achieve the optimal solutions. The control parameter in our proposed method allows for the adjustment of the optimization problem’s difficulty level, providing significant advantages when dealing with exceptionally challenging problems of interest. As well, this contribution empirically illustrates the performance of the method in various expositions. Keywords: Quantization · approximation of measures · entropic regularization Classification: 94A17, 81S20, 40A25

Introduction

Over the past few decades, extensive research has been conducted on optimal quantization techniques in order to tackle numerical problems that are related to various fields such as data science, applied disciplines, and economic models.
These problems are typically centered around
uncertainties
or
probabilities
which demand robust and efficient solutions (cf.
Graf and Mauldin 1989
,
Luschgy and Pagès 2015
,
El Nmeir et al. 2022
).
In general, these problems are difficult to handle, as the random components in the problem allow uncountable many outcomes.
As a consequence to address this difficulty, the probability measures are replaced by simpler or finite measures, which facilitates numerical computations.
However, the probability measures should be ‘close’, so that the result of the computations with approximate (discrete) measures will resemble the original problem.
In a nutshell, the goal is to find the best approximation of a diffuse
measure using a discrete measure, and it is called
optimal quantization
problem.
For a comprehensive discussion of the optimal quantization problem from a mathematical standpoint, we refer to
Graf and Luschgy 2000
.

On the other hand,
entropy
is an inevitable concept to deal with uncertainties and probabilities.
In mathematics, entropy is often used as a measure of information and uncertainty. It provides a quantitative measure of the randomness or disorder in a system or a random variable.
Its applications span across information theory, statistical analysis, probability theory, and the study of complex dynamical systems (cf.
Breuer and Csiszár 2013a
,
Breuer and Csiszár 2013b
,
Pichler and Schlotter 2020
).

In order to assess the closeness of the probability measures, distances are often considered, and one of the notable instances is the Wasserstein distance.
Ostensibly, the Wasserstein distance measures the minimum, average amount of transporting cost required to transfer one probability distribution into another.
Unlike other formulations of distances and/ or divergence, which simply compares the probabilities of the distribution functions (e.g., the total variation distance and the Kullback–Leibler divergence), the Wasserstein distance incorporates the support of the underlying distributions.
This increases the understanding of the relationships between different probability measures in a geometrically trustworthy manner.

In our research work, we focus on entropy adjusted quantization methods.
More precisely, we consider an entropy regularized version of the Wasserstein problem to quantify the quality of the approximation, and we adapt the stochastic gradient approach to obtain the optimal quantizers.

Some key features of our methodology include the following:

(i)

This regularization approach stabilizes and simplifies the standard quantization problem by introducing penalty terms or constraints that discourage overly complex or overfit models, promoting better generalizations and robustness in the solutions.

(ii)

The influence of entropy is controlled using a parameter
\lambda
, which also facilitates us to reach the genuine optimal quantizers.

(iii)

Generally, parameter tuning comes with certain limitations. However, our method builds upon the framework of the well-established softmin function, which allows us to exercise parameter control without encountering any restrictions.

(iv)

For larger regularization parameter
\lambda
, the optimal measure accumulates all its mass at the center of the measure.

Related works and contributions.

As mentioned above, optimal quantization is a well-researched topic in the field of information theory and signal processing. There are several methods that have been developed for optimal quantization problem. Here are some remarkable methods of optimal quantization:

–

Lloyd-Max Algorithm: the Lloyd-Max algorithm, also known as the Lloyd’s algorithm or the
k
-means algorithm, is a popular iterative algorithm for computing optimal vector quantizers. It iteratively adjusts the centroids of the quantization levels to minimize the quantization error (cf.
Scheunders 1996
).

–

Tree-Structured Vector Quantization (TSVQ): TSVQ is a hierarchical quantization method that uses a tree structure to partition the input space into regions. It recursively applies vector quantization at each level of the tree until the desired number of quantization levels is achieved (cf.
Wei and Levoy 2000
).

–

Expectation-maximization (EM) algorithm: the EM algorithm is a general-purpose optimization algorithm that can be used for optimal quantization. It is an iterative algorithm that estimates the parameters of a statistical model to maximize the likelihood of the observed data (cf.
Heskes 2001
).

–

Stochastic Optimization Methods: Stochastic optimization methods, such as simulated annealing, genetic algorithms, and particle swarm optimization, can be used to find optimal quantization strategies by exploring the search space and iteratively improving the quantization performance (cf.
Pagès et al. 2004
).

–

Greedy vector quantization (GVQ): the greedy algorithm tries to solve this problem iteratively, by adding one code word at every step until the desired number of code words is reached, and each time selecting the code word that minimizes the error. GVQ is known to provide suboptimal quantization compared to other non-greedy methods like the Lloyd-Max and Linde-Buzo-Gray algorithms. However, it has been shown to perform well when the data has a strong correlation structure. Notably, it utilizes Wasserstein distance to measure the error of approximation (cf.
Luschgy and Pagès 2015
).

These methods provide efficient and practical solutions for finding optimal quantization schemes with different trade-offs between complexity and performance. The choice of method depends on the problem of interset and the requirements of the application. However, most of these methods depend on strict constraints which makes the solutions overly complex or overfit models.
Our method mitigates this issue by promoting better generalizations and robustness in the solutions.

In the optimal transport community, entropy regularized version of optimal transport problem (also known as entropy regularized Wasserstein problem) is initial proposed by
Cuturi 2013
.
This entropy version of Wasserstein problem promotes fast computations using Sinkhorn’s algorithm.
As an avenue for constructive research, this study has presented a multitude of results aimed at gaining a comprehensive understanding of the subtleties involved in enhancing the computational performance of entropy optimal transport (cf.
Ramdas et al. 2017
,
Neumayer and Steidl 2021
,
[
1
]
,
Lakshmanan et al. 2023
).
These findings serve as a valuable foundation for further exploration in the field of optimal transport, providing insights into both the intricacies of the topic and potential avenues for improvement.

In contrast, we present a new, innovative approach that concentrates on the optimal quantization problem based on entropy, and its robust properties, which is a distinct contribution from standard entropy regularized optimal transport problems.

One of the principal consequences of our research substantiates the behavior of convergence of quantizers at the center of the measure.
The relationship between the center of measure and entropy regularized quantization problem has not been exposed yet.
The following plain solution is obtained by intensifying the entropy term in the regularization of the quantization problem.

Theorem 1.1
.

There exist a real valued
\lambda_{0}>0
such that the best approximation of entropy regularized optimal quantization problem is given by the Dirac-measure

P=\delta_{a}

for every
\lambda>\lambda_{0}
, where
a
is the center of the measure
P
with respect to the distance
d
.

The enthralling interpretation of our master problem facilitates us to understand the transition from a complex hard optimization solution to the simple solution in Theorem
1.1
.
Moreover, along with the theoretical discussion, we provide an algorithm and numerical exemplification, which empirically demonstrate the robustness of the method. The forthcoming sections elucidate the robustness and asymptotic properties of the methods in detail.

Outline of the paper.

5
provides numerical examples, and empirically substantiates the theoretical proofs.
Finally, Section
6
summarize our study.

Preliminaries

In what follows,
(\mathcal{X},d)
is a Polish space.
The
\sigma
-algebra generated by the Borel sets induced by the distance
d
is
\mathcal{F}
, the set of all probability measures on
\mathcal{X}
is
\mathscr{P}(\mathcal{X})
.

2.1
Distances and divergences of measures

The standard quantization problem employs the Wasserstein distance to measure the quality of the approximation, which was initially studied by Monge and Kantorovich (cf.
Monge 1781
,
Kantorovich 1942
).
One of the remarkable properties of this distance is, it metrizes the weak* topology of measures.

Definition 2.1

(Wasserstein distance)
.

Let
P
and
{\tilde{P}}
be probability measures on
(\mathcal{X},d)
.
The Wasserstein distance of order
r\geq 1
of
P
and
{\tilde{P}}\in\mathscr{P}(\mathcal{X})
is

d_{r}(P,{\tilde{P}})\coloneqq\inf\left(\iint_{\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}}d(\xi,\tilde{\xi})^{r}\,\pi(\mathrm{d}\xi,\mathrm{d}\tilde{\xi})\right)^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{r}}},

(2.1)

where the infimum is among all measures
\pi\in\mathscr{P}(\mathcal{X}^{2})
with marginals
P
and
{\tilde{P}}
, that is

\displaystyle\pi(A\times\mathcal{X})

\displaystyle=P(A)\text{ and }

(2.2)

\displaystyle\pi(\mathcal{X}\times B)

\displaystyle={\tilde{P}}(B)

(2.3)

for all sets
A
and
B\in\mathcal{F}
.
The measures

\pi_{1}(\cdot)\coloneqq\pi(\cdot\times\mathcal{X})\ \text{ and }\ \pi_{2}(\cdot)\coloneqq\pi(\mathcal{X}\times\cdot)

on
\mathcal{X}
are called the
marginal measures
of the bivariate measure
\pi
.

We may refer to the excellent monographs
[
21
,
17
]
for a comprehensive discussion of the Wasserstein distance.

Remark 2.2

(Flexibility)
.

In the subsequent discussion, our problem of interset is to approximate the measure
P
, which is a continuous, a discrete or mixed measure on
\mathcal{X}=\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
The measure
{\tilde{P}}
is used to approximate the measure
P
, which is a discrete measure. The definition of the Wasserstein distance flexibly comprises all the cases, namely continuous, semi-discrete, and discrete measures.

In contrast to the standard methodology, we investigate the quantization problem by utilizing an entropy version of the Wasserstein distance. The standard Wasserstein problem is regularized by adding the Kullback–Leibler divergence, which is also known as relative entropy.

Definition 2.3

(Kullback–Leibler divergence)
.

Let
P
and
Q\in\mathscr{P}(\mathcal{X})
be probability measures.
Denote by
Z\in L^{1}(P)
the Radon–Nikodým derivative,
\mathrm{d}Q=Z\,\mathrm{d}P
, if
Q
is absolutely continuous with respect to
P
(
Q\ll P
).
The
Kullback–Leibler divergence
is

D(Q\|P)\coloneqq\begin{cases}\E_{P}Z\log Z=\E_{Q}\log Z&\text{if }Q\ll P\text{ and }\mathrm{d}Q=Z\,\mathrm{d}P,\\
+\infty&\text{else},\end{cases}

(2.4)

where
\E_{P}
(
\E_{Q}
, resp.) is the expectation with respect to the measure
P
(
Q
, resp.).

By Gibb’s inequality, the Kullback–Leibler divergence satisfies
D(Q\|P)\geq 0
(non-negativity).
However,
D
is not a distance metric, as it does not satisfy the symmetry, and the triangle inequality properties.

We would like to emphasize the following distinctness to the Wasserstein distance (cf. Remark
2.2
): for the Kullback–Leibler divergence to be finite (
D(Q\|P)<\infty
), we necessarily have

\supp Q\subset\supp P,

where the support of the measure is (cf.
Rüschendorf 2014
)

\supp P\coloneqq\bigcap\left\{A\in\mathcal{F}\colon A\text{ is closed and }P(A)=1\right\}.

If
P
is a continuous measure on
\mathcal{X}=\mathbb{R}^{d}
, then so is
Q
. If
P
is a finite measure, then the support points of
P
contain the support points of
Q
.

2.2
The smooth minimum

In what follows we present the smooth minimum in its general form, which includes discrete and continuous measures. Numerical computations in the following section rely on results on its discrete version. Therefore, we also address the special properties of its discrete version in detail.

Definition 2.4

(Smooth minimum)
.

Let
\lambda>0
and
Y
be a random variable.
The
smooth minimum
, or
smooth minimum with respect to
{\tilde{P}}
, is

\displaystyle\mathop{\min\nolimits_{{\tilde{P}};\,\lambda}}(Y)

\displaystyle\coloneqq-\lambda\log\E_{\tilde{P}}e^{-Y/\lambda}

(2.5)

\displaystyle=-\lambda\log\int_{\mathcal{X}}e^{-Y(\eta)/\lambda}\,{\tilde{P}}(\mathrm{d}\eta),

(2.6)

provided that the expectation (integral) of
e^{-Y/\lambda}
is finite, and
\mathop{\min\nolimits_{{\tilde{P}};\,\lambda}}(Y)\coloneqq-\infty
, if it is not finite.
For
\lambda=0
, we set

\mathop{\min\nolimits_{{\tilde{P}};\,\lambda=0}}(Y)\coloneqq\essinf Y.

(2.7)

For a
\sigma
-algebra
\mathcal{G}\subset\mathcal{F}
and
\lambda>0
measurable with respect to
\mathcal{G}
, the
conditional smooth minimum
is

\mathop{\min\nolimits_{{\tilde{P}};\lambda}}(Y|\,\mathcal{G})\coloneqq-\lambda\log\E_{\tilde{P}}\left(\left.e^{-Y/\lambda}\right|\,\mathcal{G}\right).

The following lemma relates the smooth minimum with the essential infimum (cf. (
2.7
)), that is, colloquially, the ‘minimum’ of a random variable. As well, the result justifies the term
smooth minimum
.

Lemma 2.5
.

For
\lambda>0
it holds that

\mathop{\min\nolimits_{{\tilde{P}};\lambda}}(Y)\leq\E_{\tilde{P}}Y

(2.8)

and

\essinf Y\leq\mathop{\min\nolimits_{{\tilde{P}};\lambda}}(Y)\xrightarrow[\lambda\to 0]{}\essinf Y.

(2.9)

Proof.

The inequality (
2.8
) follows from Jensen’s inequality, applied to the convex function
x\mapsto\exp(-x/\lambda)
.

Next, the first inequality in the second display (
2.9
) follows from
\essinf Y\leq Y
and the fact that all operations in (
2.6
) are monotonic.
Finally, let
a>\essinf Y
. By Markov’s inequality, we have

\E_{\tilde{P}}e^{-Y/\lambda}\geq e^{-a/\lambda}\,{\tilde{P}}\bigl(e^{-Y/\lambda}\geq e^{-a/\lambda}\bigr)=e^{-a/\lambda}\,{\tilde{P}}(Y\leq a),

(2.10)

which is a variant of Chernoff’s bound. From inequality (
2.10
), it follows that

\mathop{\min\nolimits_{{\tilde{P}};\lambda}}(Y)=-\lambda\log\E_{\tilde{P}}e^{-Y/\lambda}\leq-\lambda\log\bigl(e^{-a/\lambda}\,{\tilde{P}}(Y\leq a)\bigr)=a+\lambda\log\frac{1}{{\tilde{P}}(Y\leq a)}.

(2.11)

When
\lambda>0
and
\lambda\to 0
, we have that

\mathop{\min\nolimits_{{\tilde{P}};\lambda}}(Y)\leq a,

where
a
is an arbitrary number with
a>\essinf Y
. This completes proof.
∎

Remark 2.6

(Nesting property)
.

The main properties of the smooth minimum include translation equivariance,

\mathop{\min\nolimits_{{\tilde{P}};\lambda}}(Y+c)=\mathop{\min\nolimits_{{\tilde{P}};\lambda}}(Y)+c,\qquad c\in\mathbb{R},

and positive homogeneity,

\mathop{\min\nolimits_{{\tilde{P}};\gamma\cdot\lambda}}(\gamma\cdot Y)=\gamma\cdot\mathop{\min\nolimits_{{\tilde{P}};\lambda}}(Y),\qquad\gamma>0.

As a consequence of the tower property of the expectation, we have the nesting property

\mathop{\min\nolimits_{{\tilde{P}};\lambda}}\Bigl(\mathop{\min\nolimits_{{\tilde{P}};\lambda}}(Y|\,\mathcal{G})\Bigr)=\mathop{\min\nolimits_{{\tilde{P}};\lambda}}(Y),

provided that
\mathcal{G}
is a sub-
\sigma
-algebra of
\mathcal{F}
.

2.3
Softmin function

The smooth minimum is related to the softmin function via its derivatives.
In what follows, we express variants of its derivatives, which are involved later.

Definition 2.7

(Softmin function)
.

For
\lambda>0
and a random variable
Y
with finite smooth minimum, the
softmin function
is the random variable

\sigma_{\lambda}(Y)\coloneqq\exp\left(-{Y-\mathop{\min\nolimits_{{\tilde{P}};\lambda}}(Y)\over\lambda}\right)=\frac{e^{-Y/\lambda}}{\E_{\tilde{P}}e^{-Y/\lambda}},

(2.12)

where the latter equality is obvious with the definition of the smooth minimum in (
2.6
).
The function
\sigma_{\lambda}(Y)
is also called the
Gibbs density
.

The derivative with respect to the probability measure

The definition of the smooth minimum in (
2.6
) does not require the measure
{\tilde{P}}
to be a probability measure.
Based on
{{\partial}\over{\partial}t}\log(a+t\cdot h)={h\over a}
(at
t=0
) for the natural logarithm, the directional derivative of the smooth minimum in direction of the measure
Q
is

\displaystyle\frac{1}{t}\bigl(\mathop{\min\nolimits_{{\tilde{P}}+t\cdot Q;\lambda}}(Y)-\mathop{\min\nolimits_{{\tilde{P}};\lambda}}(Y)\bigr)

\displaystyle=-{\lambda\over t}\left(\log\int_{\mathcal{X}}e^{-Y/\lambda}\,\mathrm{d}\bigl({\tilde{P}}+t\cdot Q\bigr)-\log\int_{\mathcal{X}}e^{-Y/\lambda}\,\mathrm{d}{\tilde{P}}\right)

(2.13)

\displaystyle\xrightarrow[t\to 0]{}-\lambda\cdot{\int_{\mathcal{X}}e^{-Y/\lambda}\,\mathrm{d}Q\over\int_{\mathcal{X}}e^{-Y/\lambda}\,\mathrm{d}{\tilde{P}}}

(2.14)

\displaystyle=-\lambda\cdot\int_{\mathcal{X}}\sigma_{\lambda}(Y)\,\mathrm{d}Q.

(2.15)

Note, that
-\lambda\,\sigma_{\lambda}
is (up to the constant
-\lambda
) a Radon–Nikodým density in (
2.15
). The Gibbs density
\sigma_{\lambda}(Y)
thus is proportional to the directional derivative of the smooth minimum with respect to the underlying measure
{\tilde{P}}
.

The derivative with respect to the random variable

In what follows we shall need the derivative of the smooth minimum with respect to its argument as well. With a similar reasoning as above, this is accomplished by

\displaystyle\frac{1}{t}\bigl(\mathop{\min\nolimits_{{\tilde{P}};\lambda}}(Y+t\cdot Z)-\mathop{\min\nolimits_{{\tilde{P}};\lambda}}(Y)\bigr)

\displaystyle=-{\lambda\over t}\left(\log\int_{\mathcal{X}}e^{-(Y+t\cdot Z)/\lambda}\,\mathrm{d}{\tilde{P}}-\log\int_{\mathcal{X}}e^{-Y/\lambda}\,\mathrm{d}{\tilde{P}}\right)

(2.16)

\displaystyle=-{\lambda\over t}\left(\log\int_{\mathcal{X}}e^{-Y/\lambda}\Bigl(1-{t\over\lambda}Z+\mathscr{O}(t^{2})\Bigr)\,\mathrm{d}{\tilde{P}}-\log\int_{\mathcal{X}}e^{-Y/\lambda}\,\mathrm{d}{\tilde{P}}\right)

(2.17)

\displaystyle\xrightarrow[t\to 0]{}{\int_{\mathcal{X}}Z\cdot e^{-Y/\lambda}\,\mathrm{d}{\tilde{P}}\over\int_{\mathcal{X}}e^{-Y/\lambda}\,\mathrm{d}{\tilde{P}}}

(2.18)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{X}}Z\cdot\sigma_{\lambda}(Y)\,\mathrm{d}{\tilde{P}},

(2.19)

which involves the softmin function
\sigma_{\lambda}(\cdot)
as well.

3
Regularized quantization

This section introduces the entropy regularized optimal quantization problem along with its properties, and recalls the standard optimal quantization problem first.

The standard quantization measures the quality of the approximation by the Wasserstein distance and considers the problem (cf.
Graf and Luschgy 2000
)

\inf_{\pi\colon\begin{subarray}{c}\pi_{1}=P,\,\\
\pi_{2}\in\mathscr{P}_{m}(\mathcal{X})\end{subarray}}\iint_{\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}}d(\xi,\tilde{\xi})\,\pi(\mathrm{d}\xi,\mathrm{d}\tilde{\xi}),

(3.1)

where

\mathscr{P}_{m}(\mathcal{X})\coloneqq\left\{{\tilde{P}}_{m}\in\mathscr{P}(\mathcal{X})\colon{\tilde{P}}_{m}=\sum_{j=1}^{m}{\tilde{p}}_{j}\,\delta_{y_{j}}\right\}

(3.2)

is the set of measures on
\mathcal{X}
supported by not more than
m
(
m\in\mathbb{N}
) points.

Soft quantization (or quantization, regularized with Kullback–Leibler divergence), instead of (
3.1
) involves the regularized Wasserstein distance.
The soft quantization problem is regularized with the Kullback–Leibler divergence, it is

\inf\big\{\E_{\pi}d^{r}+\lambda\cdot D(\pi\|\,P\times{\tilde{P}}_{m})\colon\pi_{1}=P\text{ and }\pi_{2}={\tilde{P}}_{m}\in\mathscr{P}_{m}(\mathcal{X})\big\},

(3.3)

where
\lambda>0
and
\E_{\pi}d^{r}=\iint_{\mathcal{X}^{2}}d(\xi,\tilde{\xi})^{r}\,\pi(\mathrm{d}\xi,\mathrm{d}\tilde{\xi})
.
The optimal measure
{\tilde{P}}_{m}\in\mathscr{P}_{m}(\mathcal{X})
solving (
3.3
) depends on the regularization parameter
\lambda
.

In the following discussion, we initially investigate the regularized approximation, which also demonstrates existence of the optimal approximation.

3.1
Approximation with inflexible marginal measures

The following proposition addresses the optimal approximation problem, regularized with Kullback–Leibler divergence and fixed marginals.
To this end, dissect the infimum in the soft quantization problem (
3.3
) as

\inf_{{\tilde{P}}_{m}\in\mathscr{P}_{m}(\mathcal{X})}\inf_{\pi\colon\begin{subarray}{c}\pi_{1}=P,\\
\pi_{2}={\tilde{P}}_{m}\end{subarray}}\E_{\pi}d^{r}+\lambda\cdot D(\pi\|\,P\times{\tilde{P}}_{m}),

(3.4)

where the marginals
P
and
{\tilde{P}}_{m}
are fixed in the inner infimum.

The following Proposition
3.1
addresses this problem with fixed bivariate distribution, which is the inner infimum in (
3.4
).
Then, Proposition
3.6
reveals that the optimal marginals coincide in this case.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
P
be a probability measure and
\lambda>0
.
The inner optimization problem in (
3.4
) relative to the fixed bivariate distribution
P\times{\tilde{P}}
is given by the explicit formula

\displaystyle\inf_{\pi\colon\pi_{1}=P}\E_{\pi}d^{r}+\lambda\cdot D(\pi\|P\times{\tilde{P}})

\displaystyle=-\lambda\int_{\mathcal{X}}\log\int_{\mathcal{X}}e^{-d(\xi,\tilde{\xi})^{r}/\lambda}\,{\tilde{P}}(\mathrm{d}\tilde{\xi})\,P(\mathrm{d}\xi)

(3.5)

\displaystyle=\E_{\xi\sim P}\Bigl(\mathop{\min\nolimits_{\tilde{\xi}\sim{\tilde{P}};\lambda}}d(\xi,\tilde{\xi})^{r}\Bigr).

(3.6)

Further, the infimum in (
3.5
) is attained.

Remark 3.2
.

The notation in (
3.6
) ((
3.8
) below, resp.) is chosen to reflect the explicit expression (
3.5
):
while the soft minimum
\mathop{\min\nolimits_{{\tilde{P}};\lambda}}
is with respect to the measure
{\tilde{P}}
, which is associated with the variable
\tilde{\xi}
, the expectation
\E_{P}
is with respect to
P
, its associated variable is
\xi

(that is, the variable
\xi
in (
3.6
) is associated with
P
, the variable
\tilde{\xi}
with
{\tilde{P}}
).

Remark 3.3
.

The result (
3.6
) extends

\displaystyle\inf_{\pi\colon\pi_{1}=P}\E_{\pi}d^{r}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{X}}\min_{\xi\in\supp{\tilde{P}}}d(\xi,\tilde{\xi})^{r}\,P(\mathrm{d}\xi)

(3.7)

\displaystyle=\E_{P}\Bigl(\min_{\tilde{\xi}\in\supp{\tilde{P}}}d(\xi,\tilde{\xi})^{r}\Bigr),

(3.8)

which is the formula without regularization (i.e.,
\lambda=0
, cf.
Pflug and Pichler 2014
).
Note that the preceding display explicitly involves the support
\supp{\tilde{P}}
, while (
3.5
) only involves the expectation (via the smooth minimum) with respect to the measure
{\tilde{P}}
.

Proof of Proposition
3.1
.

It follows from the definition of the Kullback–Leibler divergence in (
2.4
) that it is enough to consider measures
\pi
, which are absolutely continuous with respect to the product measure,
\pi\ll P\times{\tilde{P}}
; otherwise, the objective is not finite.
Hence, there is a Radon–Nikodým density
{\tilde{Z}}
such that, with Fubini’s theorem,

\pi(A\times B)=\int_{A}\int_{B}{\tilde{Z}}(\xi,\eta)\,{\tilde{P}}(\mathrm{d}\eta)P(\mathrm{d}\xi).

For the marginal constraint
\pi(A\times\mathcal{X})=P(A)
to be satisfied (cf. (
2.2
)), we have that

\int_{A}\int_{\mathcal{X}}{\tilde{Z}}(\xi,\eta){\tilde{P}}(\mathrm{d}\eta)\,P(\mathrm{d}\xi)=\pi(A\times\mathcal{X})=P(A)=\int_{A}1\,P(\mathrm{d}\xi)

for every measurable set
A
. It follows that

\int_{\mathcal{X}}{\tilde{Z}}(\xi,\eta)\,{\tilde{P}}(\mathrm{d}\eta)=1\qquad P(\mathrm{d}\xi)\text{ almost everywhere}.

We conclude that every density of the form

{\tilde{Z}}(\xi,\eta)=\frac{Z(\xi,\eta)}{\int_{\mathcal{X}}Z(\xi,\eta^{\prime})\,{\tilde{P}}(\mathrm{d}\eta^{\prime})}

(3.9)

satisfies the constraint (
2.2
), irrespective of
Z
and conversely, every
Z
– via
{\tilde{Z}}
in (
3.9
) – defines a bivariate measure
\pi
satisfying the constraints (
2.2
).
We set
\Phi(\xi,\eta)\coloneqq\log Z(\xi,\eta)
(with the convention that
\log 0=-\infty
and
\exp(-\infty)=0
, resp.) and consider

{\tilde{Z}}(\xi,\eta)={e^{\Phi(\xi,\eta)}\over\int_{\mathcal{X}}e^{\Phi(\xi,\eta^{\prime})}\,{\tilde{P}}(\mathrm{d}\eta^{\prime})}.

With that, the divergence is

\displaystyle D(\pi\|P\times{\tilde{P}})=

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{X}}\int_{\mathcal{X}}{e^{\Phi(\xi,\eta)}\over\int_{\mathcal{X}}e^{\Phi(\xi,\eta^{\prime})}\,{\tilde{P}}(\mathrm{d}\eta^{\prime})}\log{e^{\Phi(\xi,\eta)}\over\int_{\mathcal{X}}e^{\Phi(\xi,\eta^{\prime})}\,{\tilde{P}}(\mathrm{d}\eta^{\prime})}{\tilde{P}}(\mathrm{d}\eta)P(\mathrm{d}\xi)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{X}}\int_{\mathcal{X}}{e^{\Phi(\xi,\eta)}\over\int_{\mathcal{X}}e^{\Phi(\xi,\eta^{\prime})}\,{\tilde{P}}(\mathrm{d}\eta^{\prime})}\Phi(\xi,\eta)\,{\tilde{P}}(\mathrm{d}\eta)P(\mathrm{d}\xi)

\displaystyle\qquad-\int_{\mathcal{X}}\frac{e^{\Phi(\xi,\eta)}}{\int_{\mathcal{X}}e^{\Phi(\xi,\eta^{\prime})}\,{\tilde{P}}(\mathrm{d}\eta^{\prime})}\log\int_{\mathcal{X}}e^{\Phi(\xi,\eta^{\prime})}\,{\tilde{P}}(\mathrm{d}\eta^{\prime}){\tilde{P}}(\mathrm{d}\eta)P(\mathrm{d}\xi)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{X}}\int_{\mathcal{X}}\frac{e^{\Phi(\xi,\eta)}}{\int_{\mathcal{X}}e^{\Phi(\xi,\eta^{\prime})}\,{\tilde{P}}(\mathrm{d}\eta^{\prime})}\Phi(\xi,\eta)-\log\int_{\mathcal{X}}e^{\Phi(\xi,\eta^{\prime})}\,{\tilde{P}}(\mathrm{d}\eta^{\prime})P(\mathrm{d}\xi).

For the other term in the objective (
3.3
), we have

\E_{\pi}d^{r}=\int_{\mathcal{X}}\int_{\mathcal{X}}\frac{e^{\Phi(\xi,\eta)}}{\int_{\mathcal{X}}e^{\Phi(\xi,\eta^{\prime})}\,{\tilde{P}}(\mathrm{d}\eta^{\prime})}d(\xi,\eta)^{r}\,{\tilde{P}}(\mathrm{d}\eta)P(\mathrm{d}\xi).

Combining the last expressions obtained, the objective in (
3.5
) is

\displaystyle\E_{\pi}d^{r}+\lambda\,D(\pi\|P\times{\tilde{P}})

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{X}}\int_{\mathcal{X}}{e^{\Phi(\xi,\eta)}\over\int_{\mathcal{X}}e^{\Phi(\xi,\eta^{\prime})}\,{\tilde{P}}(\mathrm{d}\eta^{\prime})}\Bigl(d(\xi,\eta)^{r}+\lambda\,\Phi(\xi,\eta)\Bigr)\,{\tilde{P}}(\mathrm{d}\eta)P(\mathrm{d}\xi)

(3.10)

\displaystyle\qquad-\lambda\int_{\mathcal{X}}\log\int_{\mathcal{X}}e^{\Phi(\xi,\eta^{\prime})}\,{\tilde{P}}(\mathrm{d}\eta^{\prime})P(\mathrm{d}\xi)

(3.11)

For
\xi
fixed (
\xi
is simply suppressed in the following two
displays to abbreviate the notation), consider the function

f(\Phi)\coloneqq\int_{\mathcal{X}}{e^{\Phi(\eta)}\over\int_{\mathcal{X}}e^{\Phi(\eta^{\prime})}\,{\tilde{P}}(\mathrm{d}\eta^{\prime})}\bigl(d(\eta)^{r}+\lambda\,\Phi(\eta)\bigr)\,{\tilde{P}}(\mathrm{d}\eta)-\lambda\log\int_{\mathcal{X}}e^{\Phi(\eta^{\prime})}\,{\tilde{P}}(\mathrm{d}\eta^{\prime}).

The directional derivative in direction
h
of this function is

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to 0}\frac{1}{t}\bigl(f(\Phi+t\,h)-f(\Phi)\bigr)=

(3.12)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{X}}\frac{e^{\Phi(\eta)}}{\int_{\mathcal{X}}e^{\Phi(\eta^{\prime})}\,{\tilde{P}}(\mathrm{d}\eta^{\prime})}\Bigl(d(\eta)^{r}+\lambda\,\Phi(\eta)-\lambda\Bigr)h(\eta)\,{\tilde{P}}(\mathrm{d}\eta)

(3.13)

\displaystyle\qquad-\int_{\mathcal{X}}\frac{e^{\Phi(\eta)}\int_{\mathcal{X}}e^{\Phi(\eta^{\prime})}h(\eta^{\prime})\,{\tilde{P}}(\mathrm{d}\eta^{\prime})}{\bigl(\int_{\mathcal{X}}e^{\Phi(\eta^{\prime})}\,{\tilde{P}}(\mathrm{d}\eta^{\prime})\bigr)^{2}}\Bigl(d(\eta)^{r}+\lambda\,\Phi(\eta)\Bigr)\,{\tilde{P}}(\mathrm{d}\eta)

(3.14)

\displaystyle\qquad+\lambda\int_{\mathcal{X}}\frac{e^{\Phi(\eta)}h(\eta)}{\int_{\mathcal{X}}e^{\Phi(\eta^{\prime})}\,{\tilde{P}}(\mathrm{d}\eta^{\prime})}{\tilde{P}}(\mathrm{d}\eta)

(3.15)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{X}}\frac{e^{\Phi(\eta)}}{\int_{\mathcal{X}}e^{\Phi(\eta^{\prime})}\,{\tilde{P}}(\mathrm{d}\eta^{\prime})}\Bigl(d(\eta)^{r}+\lambda\,\Phi(\eta)\Bigr)h(\eta)\,{\tilde{P}}(\mathrm{d}\eta)

(3.16)

\displaystyle\qquad-\int_{\mathcal{X}}\frac{e^{\Phi(\eta)}\int_{\mathcal{X}}e^{\Phi(\eta^{\prime})}h(\eta^{\prime})\,{\tilde{P}}(\mathrm{d}\eta^{\prime})}{\left(\int_{\mathcal{X}}e^{\Phi(\eta^{\prime})}\,{\tilde{P}}(\mathrm{d}\eta^{\prime})\right)^{2}}\Bigl(d(\eta)^{r}+\lambda\,\Phi(\eta)\Bigr)\,{\tilde{P}}(\mathrm{d}\eta).

(3.17)

By (
3.16
) and (
3.17
), the derivative vanishes for every function
h
, if
d(\eta)^{r}+\lambda\,\Phi(\eta)=0
.
As
\xi
was arbitrary, the general minimum is attained for
\Phi(\xi,\eta)=-d(\xi,\eta)^{r}/\lambda
.
With that, the first expression in (
3.10
) vanishes, and we conclude that

\displaystyle\inf_{\pi}\E_{\pi}d^{r}+\lambda\,D(\pi\|P\times{\tilde{P}})

\displaystyle=-\lambda\int_{\mathcal{X}}\log\int_{\mathcal{X}}e^{-d(\xi,\eta)^{r}/\lambda}\,{\tilde{P}}(\mathrm{d}\eta)P(\mathrm{d}\xi)

\displaystyle=\E_{P}\left(\mathop{\min\nolimits_{{\tilde{P}};\lambda}}d(\xi,\tilde{\xi})^{r}\right).

Finally, notice that the variable
Z(\xi,\eta)=e^{\Phi(\xi,\eta)}
is completely arbitrary for the problem (
3.5
) involving the Wasserstein distance and the Kullback–Leibler divergence.
As outlined above, for every measure
\pi
with finite divergence
D(\pi\|P\times{\tilde{P}})
, there is a density
Z
as considered above.
With that, the assertion of Proposition

follows.
∎

Remark 3.4
.

The preceding proposition considers probability measures
\pi
with marginal
\pi_{1}=P
. Its first marginal distribution is (trivially) absolutely continuous with respect to
P
,
\pi_{1}\ll P
, as
\pi_{1}=P
.

The second marginal
\pi_{2}
, however, is not specified.
But for
\pi
to be feasible in (
3.5
), its Kullback–Leibler divergence with respect to
P\times{\tilde{P}}
is finite.
There is hence a (non-negative) Radon–Nikodým density
Z
so that

\pi_{2}(B)=\pi(\mathcal{X}\times B)=\iint_{\mathcal{X}\times B}Z(\xi,\eta)~P(\mathrm{d}\xi){\tilde{P}}(\mathrm{d}\eta).

It follows from Fubini’s theorem that

\pi_{2}(B)=\int_{B}\int_{\mathcal{X}}Z(\xi,\eta)\,P(\mathrm{d}\xi){\tilde{P}}(\mathrm{d}\eta)=\int_{B}Z(\eta)\,{\tilde{P}}(\mathrm{d}\eta),

where
Z(\eta)\coloneqq\int_{\mathcal{X}}Z(\xi,\eta)\,P(\mathrm{d}\xi)
.
The second marginal thus is absolutely continuous with respect to
{\tilde{P}}
,
\pi_{2}\ll{\tilde{P}}
.

Proposition
3.1
characterizes the
objective
of the quantization problem.
Its proof, implicitly, reveals the marginal of the best approximation as well. The following lemma spells out the density of the marginal of the optimal measure with respect to
{\tilde{P}}
explicitly.

Lemma 3.5

(Characterization of the best approximating measure)
.

The best approximating marginal probability measure minimizing (
3.5
) has density

Z(\tilde{\xi})=\E_{P}\sigma_{\lambda}\left(d(\xi,\tilde{\xi})^{r}\right)=\int_{\mathcal{X}}\sigma_{\lambda}\left(d(\xi,\tilde{\xi})^{r}\right)\,P(\mathrm{d}\xi),

where
\sigma_{\lambda}(\cdot)
is the softmin function (cf. Definition
2.7
).

Proof.

Recall from the proof or Proposition
3.1
the density

{\tilde{Z}}(\xi,\tilde{\xi})={e^{-d(\xi,\tilde{\xi})^{r}/\lambda}\over\E_{\tilde{P}}e^{-d(\xi,\tilde{\xi})^{r}/\lambda}}

of the optimal measure
\pi
relative to
P\times{\tilde{P}}
. From that we derive that

\pi_{2}(B)=\pi(\mathcal{X}\times B)=\int_{B}\int_{\mathcal{X}}{e^{-d(\xi,\tilde{\xi})^{r}/\lambda}\over\E_{\tilde{P}}e^{-d(\xi,\tilde{\xi})^{r}/\lambda}}\,P(\mathrm{d}\xi){\tilde{P}}(\mathrm{d}\tilde{\xi})

so that

Z(\tilde{\xi})=\int_{\mathcal{X}}{e^{-d(\xi,\tilde{\xi})^{r}/\lambda}\over\E_{\tilde{P}}e^{-d(\xi,\tilde{\xi})^{r}/\lambda}}P(\mathrm{d}\xi)=\E_{P}\sigma_{\lambda}\bigl(d(\xi,\tilde{\xi})^{r}\bigr)

is the density with respect to
{\tilde{P}}
, that is
\mathrm{d}\pi_{2}=Z\,\mathrm{d}{\tilde{P}}
(i.e.,
\pi_{2}(\mathrm{d}\tilde{\xi})=Z(\tilde{\xi})\,{\tilde{P}}(\mathrm{d}\tilde{\xi})
).
∎

3.2
Approximation with flexible marginal measure

The following proposition reveals that the best approximation of a bivariate measure in terms of a product of independent measures is given by the product of its marginals.
With that it follows that the objectives in (
3.4
) and (
3.5
) coincide for
{\tilde{P}}=\pi_{2}
.

Proposition 3.6
.

Let
P
be measure and
\pi
be a bivariate measure with marginal
\pi_{1}=P
and
\pi_{2}
. Then it holds that

D(\pi\|\,P\times\pi_{2})\leq D(\pi\|\,P\times{\tilde{P}}),

(3.18)

where
{\tilde{P}}
is an arbitrary measure.

Proof.

Define the Radon–Nikodým density
Z(\eta)\coloneqq{\pi_{2}(\mathrm{d}\eta)\over{\tilde{P}}(\mathrm{d}\eta)}
and observe that the extension
Z(\xi,\eta)\coloneqq Z(\eta)
to
\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}
is the density
Z={\mathrm{d}P\times\pi_{2}\over\mathrm{d}P\times{\tilde{P}}}
.
It follows with (
2.4
) that

\displaystyle 0\leq D(\pi_{2}\|\,{\tilde{P}})

\displaystyle=\E_{\pi_{2}}\log{\mathrm{d}\pi_{2}\over\mathrm{d}{\tilde{P}}}

(3.19)

\displaystyle=\E_{\pi}\log{\mathrm{d}\,P\times\pi_{2}\over\mathrm{d}\,P\times{\tilde{P}}}

(3.20)

\displaystyle=\E_{\pi}\bigl(\log{\mathrm{d}\,\pi\over\mathrm{d}\,P\times{\tilde{P}}}-\log{\mathrm{d}\,\pi\over\mathrm{d}\,P\times\pi_{2}}\bigr)

(3.21)

\displaystyle=D(\pi\|\,P\times{\tilde{P}})-D(\pi\|\,P\times\pi_{2}),

(3.22)

which is the assertion.
In case the measures are not absolutely continuous, the assertion (
3.19
) is trivial.
∎

Suppose now that
\pi
is a solution of the master problem (
3.5
) with some
{\tilde{P}}
.
It follows from the preceding proposition that the objective (
3.5
) improves when replacing the initial
{\tilde{P}}
by the marginal of the optimal solution,
{\tilde{P}}=\pi_{2}
.

3.3
The relation of soft quantization and entropy

The soft quantization problem (
3.5
) involves the Kullback–Leibler divergence and
not
the entropy.
The major advantage of the formulation presented above is that it works for discrete, continuous or mixed measures, while entropy usually needs to be defined separately for discrete and continuous measures.

For a discrete measure with
P(x)\coloneqq P(\{x\})
and
{\tilde{P}}(y)\coloneqq{\tilde{P}}(\{y\})
, the Kullback–Leibler divergence (
2.4
) is

\displaystyle D({\tilde{P}}\|\,P)

\displaystyle=H({\tilde{P}},P)-H({\tilde{P}})

(3.23)

\displaystyle=\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}{\tilde{P}}(x)\log{{\tilde{P}}(x)\over P(x)},

(3.24)

where

H({\tilde{P}},P)\coloneqq-\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}{\tilde{P}}(x)\cdot\log P(x)

is the
cross-entropy
of the measures
{\tilde{P}}
and
P
, and

H({\tilde{P}})\coloneqq H({\tilde{P}},{\tilde{P}})=-\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}{\tilde{P}}(x)\log{\tilde{P}}(x)

(3.25)

the
entropy
of
{\tilde{P}}
.

For a measure
\pi
with marginals
P
and
{\tilde{P}}
, the cross-entropy is

\displaystyle H(\pi,P\times{\tilde{P}})

\displaystyle=-\sum_{x,y}\pi(x,y)\log\bigl(P(x)\cdot{\tilde{P}}(y)\bigr)

(3.26)

\displaystyle=-\sum_{x,y}\pi(x,y)\log P(x)-\sum_{x,y}\pi(x,y)\log{\tilde{P}}(y)

(3.27)

\displaystyle=-\sum_{x}P(x)\log P(x)-\sum_{y}{\tilde{P}}(y)\log{\tilde{P}}(y),

(3.28)

where we have used the marginals (
2.2
).
Note, that (
3.28
) does not depend on
\pi
, and hence
H(\pi,P\times{\tilde{P}})
does not depend on
\pi
.

With (
3.23
), the quantization problem (
3.5
) thus rewrites equivalently as

\min_{\pi\colon\pi_{2}\in P}\iint_{\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}}d^{r}\,\mathrm{d}\pi-\lambda\cdot H(\pi)

(3.29)

by involving the entropy only.
For this reason, we shall call the master problem (
3.5
) also the
entropy regularized problem
.

4
Soft tessellation

The quantization problem (
3.4
) consists in finding a good (in the best case the optimal) approximation of a general probability measure
P
on
\mathcal{X}
by a simple, and discrete measure
{\tilde{P}}_{m}=\sum_{j=1}^{m}{\tilde{p}}_{j}\,\delta_{y_{j}}
.
The problem thus consists in finding good weights
{\tilde{p}}_{1},\dots,{\tilde{p}}_{m}
, as well as good locations
y_{1},\dots,y_{m}
.
Quantization employs the Wasserstein distance to measure the quality of the approximation;
soft quantization involves the regularized Wasserstein distance, instead (as in (
3.5
)):

\inf_{{\tilde{P}}_{m}\in\mathscr{P}_{m}(\mathcal{X})}\ \inf_{\pi\colon\begin{subarray}{c}\pi_{1}=P,\\
\pi_{2}={\tilde{P}}_{m}\end{subarray}}\E_{\pi}d^{r}+\lambda\cdot D(\pi\|\,P\times{\tilde{P}}_{m}),

where the measures on
\mathcal{X}
supported by not more than
m
points are (cf. (
3.2
))

\mathscr{P}_{m}(\mathcal{X})=\left\{{\tilde{P}}_{m}\in\mathscr{P}(\mathcal{X})\colon{\tilde{P}}_{m}=\sum_{j=1}^{m}{\tilde{p}}_{j}\,\delta_{y_{j}}\right\}.

We separate the problem of finding the best weights and locations.
The following Section
4.1
addresses the problem of finding the optimal weights
{\tilde{p}}
, the subsequent Section
4.2
then the problem of finding the optimal locations
y_{1},\dots,y_{m}
.
As well, we shall elaborate the numerical advantages of
soft
quantization below.

4.1
Optimal weights

Proposition
3.1
above is formulated for general probability measures
P
and
{\tilde{P}}
. The desired measure in quantization is a simple and discrete measure.
To this end recall that measures, which are feasible for (
3.5
), have marginals
\pi_{2}
with
\pi_{2}\ll{\tilde{P}}
by Remark
3.4
. It follows that the support of the marginal is smaller than the support of
{\tilde{P}}
, that is

\supp\pi_{2}\subset\supp{\tilde{P}}.

For a simple measure
{\tilde{P}}=\sum_{j=1}^{m}{\tilde{p}}_{j}\,\delta_{y_{j}}
with
{\tilde{p}}_{j}>0
, it follows in particular that
\supp\pi_{2}\subset\{y_{1},\dots,y_{m}\}
. We consider the measure
{\tilde{P}}
and the support
\{y_{1},\dots,y_{m}\}
fixed in this subsection.

To unfold the result of Proposition
3.1
for discrete measures we recall the smooth minimum and the softmin function for the discrete (empirical or uniform) measure
{\tilde{P}}=\sum_{j=1}^{m}{\tilde{p}}_{j}\,\delta_{y_{j}}
.
For this measure, the smooth minimum (
2.6
) explicitly is

\mathop{\min\nolimits_{\lambda;{\tilde{P}}}}(y_{1},\dots,y_{m})=-\lambda\log\Bigl({\tilde{p}}_{1}\,e^{-y_{1}/\lambda}+\dots+{\tilde{p}}_{m}\,e^{-y_{m}/\lambda}\Bigr).

For
\lambda=1
and uniform weights
{\tilde{p}}_{1}=\dots={\tilde{p}}_{m}=\frac{1}{m}
, this quantity is occasionally referred to as

LogSumExp
.
The softmin function (or Gibbs density (
2.12
)) is

\sigma_{\lambda}(y_{1},\dots,y_{m})=\left({e^{-y_{j}/\lambda}\over{\tilde{p}}_{1}\,e^{-y_{1}/\lambda}+\dots+{\tilde{p}}_{m}\,e^{-y_{m}/\lambda)}}\right)_{j=1}^{m}.

It follows from Lemma
3.5
that the best approximating measure is

Q=\sum_{j=1}^{m}q_{j}\,{\tilde{p}}_{j}\,\delta_{y_{j}}
, where the vector
q
of optimal weights, relative to
{\tilde{P}}
, is given explicitly by

q=\int_{\mathcal{X}}\sigma_{\lambda}\bigl(d(\xi,y_{1})^{r},\dots,d(\xi,y_{m})^{r}\bigr)\,P(\mathrm{d}\xi)=\E_{P}\sigma_{\lambda}\bigl(d(\xi,y_{1})^{r},\dots,d(\xi,y_{m})^{r}\bigr),

(4.1)

which involves computing expectations.

Soft tessellation

For
\lambda=0
, the softmin function
\sigma_{\lambda}
is

{\tilde{p}}_{j}\cdot\sigma_{\lambda=0}\bigl(d(\xi,y_{1})^{r},\dots,d(\xi,y_{m})^{r}\bigr)_{j}=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }d(\xi,y_{j})^{r}=\min\bigl(d(\xi,y_{1})^{r},\dots,d(\xi,y_{m})^{r}\bigr),\\
0&\text{else.}\end{cases}

That is, the mapping
j\mapsto{\tilde{p}}_{j}\cdot\sigma_{\lambda}(\dots)_{j}
can serve for classification, i.e., tessellation:
the point
\xi
is associated to
y_{j}
, if
\sigma_{\lambda}(\dots)_{j}\neq 0
and the corresponding region is known as Voronoi diagram.

For
\lambda>0
, the softmin
{\tilde{p}}_{j}\cdot\sigma_{\lambda}(\dots)_{j}
is not a strict indicator, but can be interpreted as probability instead. That is,

{\tilde{p}}_{j}\cdot\sigma_{\lambda}\Bigl(d(\xi,y_{1})^{r},\dots,d(\xi,y_{m})^{r}\Bigr)_{j}

is the probability of allocating
\xi\in\mathcal{X}
to the quantizer
y_{j}
.

4.2
Optimal locations

As a result of Proposition
3.1
, the objective in (
3.6
) is an expectation.
To identify the optimal support points
y_{1},\dots,y_{m}
, it is central to minimize

\min_{{\tilde{P}}=\sum_{j=1}^{m}{\tilde{p}}_{j}\delta_{y_{j}}}\E_{\xi\sim P}\Bigl(\mathop{\min\nolimits_{\lambda;y\sim{\tilde{P}}}}d(\xi,y)^{r}\Bigr).

(4.2)

This is a stochastic, non-linear and non-convex optimization problem.

f(y_{1},\dots,y_{m})\coloneqq\E f(y_{1},\dots,y_{m};\xi)=\E\Bigl(\mathop{\min\nolimits_{\lambda;{\tilde{P}}}}_{j=1,\dots,m}d(\xi,y_{i})^{r}\Bigr),

(4.3)

where the function
f(y_{1},\dots,y_{m};\xi)\coloneqq\mathop{\min\nolimits_{\lambda;{\tilde{P}}}}\{d(\xi,y_{i})^{r}\colon j=1,\dots,m\}
is non-linear and non-convex;
the optimal quantization problem constitutes an unconstrained, stochastic, non-convex and non-linear optimization problem.
The gradient of the objective is built of the components

{{\partial}\over{\partial}y_{j}}f(y_{1},\dots,y_{m})={{\tilde{p}}_{j}\cdot\exp\bigl(-d(\xi,y_{j})^{r}/\lambda\bigr)\over\sum_{j^{\prime}=1}^{m}{\tilde{p}}_{j^{\prime}}\cdot\exp\bigl(-d(\xi,y_{j^{\prime}})^{r}/\lambda\bigr)}\cdot\left.\nabla_{y}\,d(\xi,y)^{r}\right|_{y=y_{j}},

(4.4)

that is,

\nabla f={\tilde{p}}\boldsymbol{\cdot}\sigma_{\lambda}\bigl(d(\xi,y_{1})^{r},\dots,d(\xi,y_{m})^{r}\bigr)\boldsymbol{\cdot}r\,d(\xi,y)^{r-1}\boldsymbol{\cdot}\nabla_{y}\,d(\xi,y),

(4.5)

where ‘
\boldsymbol{\cdot}
’ denotes the Hadamard (element-wise) product and
{\tilde{p}}
,
d(\xi,y)^{r-1}
are the vectors with entries
{\tilde{p}}_{j}
,
d(\xi,y_{j})^{r-1}
,
j=1,\dots,m
.

4.3
Quantization with large regularization parameters

The entropy in (
3.25
) is minimal for the Dirac measure
P=\delta_{x}
(where
x
is any point in
\mathcal{X}
): in this case,
H(\delta_{x})=1\cdot\log 1=0
, while
H({\tilde{P}})>0
for any other measure.
For larger values of
\lambda

This is indeed the case, as Theorem
1.1
(above) states.
We give its proof below, after formally defining the center of the measure.

Definition 4.2

(Center of the measure)
.

Let
P
be a probability measure on
\mathcal{X}
and
d
be a distance on
\mathcal{X}
.
The point
a\in\mathcal{X}
is a
center of the measure
P
with respect to the distance
d
, if

a\in\argmin_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\E d(x,\xi)^{r},

provided that
\E d(x_{0},\xi)^{r}<\infty
for some (and thus any)
x_{0}\in\mathcal{X}
and
r\geq 1
.

In what follows, we demonstrate that the regularized quantization problem (
3.29
) links the optimal quantization problem and the center of the measure.

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

The problems (
3.29
) and (
3.5
) are equivalent by Proposition
3.1
.
Now assume that
y_{i}=y_{j}
for all
i
,
j\leq m
, then
d(y_{i},\xi)=d(y_{j},\xi)
for
\xi\in\Xi
, and it follows that

\mathop{\min\nolimits_{\lambda}}\bigl(d(y_{1},\xi)^{r},\dots,d(y_{m},\xi)^{r}\bigr)=d(y_{i},\xi)^{r},\qquad i=1,\dots,m.

The minimum of the optimization problem thus is attained at
y_{i}=a
, for each
i=1,\dots,m
, where
a
is the center of the measure
P
with respect to the distance
d
.
It follows that
y_{1}=\dots=y_{m}=a
is a local minimum and a stationary point, satisfying the first order conditions

\nabla f(y_{1},\dots,y_{m})=0

for the function
f
given in (
4.3
).
Note as well that

\sigma_{\lambda}\bigl(d(\xi,y_{1})^{r},\dots,d(\xi,y_{n})^{r}\bigr)_{i}=\frac{\exp\Bigl(-d(\xi,y_{i})^{r}/\lambda\Bigr)}{\sum_{j=1}^{n}{\tilde{p}}_{j}\exp\Bigl(-d(\xi,y_{j})^{r}/\lambda\Bigr)}=1,

the softmin function does not depend on
\lambda
at the stationary point
y_{1}=\dots=y_{m}=a
.

Recall from (
4.4
) that

\nabla\E\Bigl(\mathop{\min\nolimits_{\lambda;{\tilde{P}}}}_{j=1,\dots,n}d(\xi,y_{j})^{r}\Bigr)=\E\sigma_{\lambda}\bigl(d(y_{1},\xi)^{r},\dots,d(y_{n},\xi)^{r}\bigr)\boldsymbol{\cdot}\nabla d(\xi,y_{i})^{r}.

By the product rule, the Hessian matrix is

\nabla^{2}\E\left(\mathop{\min\nolimits_{\lambda;{\tilde{P}}}}_{j=1,\dots,n}d(\xi,y_{j})^{r}\right)=\E\begin{pmatrix}\begin{array}[]{l}\nabla\sigma_{\lambda}\bigl(d(y_{1},\xi)^{r},\dots,d(y_{n},\xi)^{r}\bigr)\boldsymbol{\cdot}\bigl(\nabla d(\xi,y_{i})^{r}\bigr)^{2}\\
\quad+\sigma_{\lambda}\bigl(d(y_{1},\xi)^{r},\dots,d(y_{n},\xi)^{r}\bigr)\boldsymbol{\cdot}\nabla^{2}d(\xi,y_{i})^{r}\end{array}\end{pmatrix}.

(4.6)

Note that the second expression is positive definite, as the Hessian
\nabla^{2}d(\xi,y_{i})^{r}
of the convex function is positive definite and
\nabla\mathop{\min\nolimits_{\lambda;{\tilde{P}}}}_{j=1,\dots,n}(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})=\sigma_{\lambda}(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\geq 0
.
Further, the Hessian of the smooth minimum (see also the appendix) is

\nabla\sigma_{\lambda}=\nabla^{2}\mathop{\min\nolimits_{\lambda}}_{j=1,\dots,n}=-\frac{1}{\lambda}\,\Sigma,

where the matrix
\Sigma
is

\Sigma\coloneqq\diag\bigl(\sigma_{1},\dots,\sigma_{n}\bigr)-\sigma\sigma^{\top}.

This matrix
\Sigma
is positive definite (as
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sigma_{i}=1
) and
0\leq\Sigma\leq 1
in Loewner order (indeed,
\Sigma
is the covariance matrix of the multinomial distribution).
It follows that the first term in (
4.6
) is
\mathcal{O}(1)
, while the second is
\mathcal{O}\bigl(\frac{1}{\lambda}\bigr)
, so that (
4.6
) is positive definite for
\lambda
sufficiently small.
That is, the extremal point
y_{i}=a
is a minimum for all
\lambda
.
In particular, there exists
\lambda_{0}>0
such that (
4.6
) is positive definite for every
\lambda>\lambda_{0}
and hence the result.
∎

Appendix A
Hessian of the softmin

The empirical measure
\frac{1}{\lambda}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{x_{i}}
is a probability measure.
Form Jensen’s inequality, it follows that
\mathop{\min\nolimits_{\lambda}}(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\leq\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}\eqqcolon\overline{x}_{n}
.
The smooth minimum thus involves a cumulant generating function for which we derive that

\displaystyle\mathop{\min\nolimits_{\lambda}}(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}{(-1)^{j-1}\over\lambda^{j-1}\cdot j!}\kappa_{j}

(A.1)

\displaystyle=\overline{x}_{n}-{1\over 2\lambda}s_{n}^{2}+\frac{1}{6\lambda^{2}}\kappa_{3}+\mathcal{O}(\lambda^{-3}),

(A.2)

where
\kappa_{j}
is the
j
-th cumulant with respect
to the empirical measure. Specifically,

\kappa_{1}=\overline{x}_{n}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i},\qquad\kappa_{2}=s_{n}^{2}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(x_{i}-\overline{x}_{n})^{2},\qquad\kappa_{3}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(x_{i}-\overline{x}_{n})^{3},

where
\overline{x}_{n}
is the ‘sample mean’ and
s_{n}^{2}
the ‘sample variance’, the following cumulants (
\kappa_{4}
, etc.) are more involved, though.
The Taylor series expansion
\log(1+x)=x-\frac{1}{2}x^{2}+\mathcal{O}(x^{3})
and

\displaystyle-\lambda\log\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}e^{-x_{i}/\lambda}

\displaystyle=\overline{x}_{n}-\lambda\log\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}e^{-(x_{i}-\overline{x}_{n})/\lambda}

\displaystyle=\overline{x}_{n}-\lambda\log\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{n}\bigl(1-\frac{x_{i}-\overline{x}_{n}}{\lambda}+\frac{1}{2}\bigl(\frac{x_{i}-x_{n}}{\lambda}\bigr)^{2}-\frac{1}{6}\bigl(\frac{x_{i}-\overline{x}_{n}}{\lambda^{3}}\bigr)+\mathcal{O}\bigl(\frac{1}{\lambda^{4}}\bigr)\bigr)

\displaystyle=\overline{x}_{n}-\lambda\log\bigl(1+\frac{1}{2\lambda^{2}}s_{n}^{2}-\frac{1}{6\lambda^{3}}\kappa_{3}+\mathcal{O}(\lambda^{-4})\bigr)

\displaystyle=\overline{x}_{n}-\lambda\bigl(\frac{1}{2\lambda^{2}}s_{n}^{2}-\frac{1}{6\lambda^{3}}\kappa_{3}+\mathcal{O}(\lambda^{-4})\bigr)

\displaystyle=\overline{x}_{n}-\frac{1}{2\lambda}s_{n}^{2}+\frac{1}{6\lambda^{2}}\kappa_{3}+\mathcal{O}(\lambda^{3}).

Note as well that the softmin function is the gradient of the smooth
minimum,

\sigma_{\lambda}(x_{1},\dots,x_{m})_{i}=\frac{{\partial}}{{\partial}x_{i}}\mathop{\min\nolimits_{\lambda}}(x_{1},\dots,x_{n}).

The softmin function is frequently used in classification in a maximum likelihood framework.
It holds that

\displaystyle{{\partial}^{2}\over{\partial}x_{i}{\partial}x_{j}}\mathop{\min\nolimits_{\lambda}}(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})

\displaystyle={{\partial}\over{\partial}x_{j}}\frac{\exp(-\lambda\,x_{i})}{\sum_{k=1}^{m}\exp(-\lambda\,x_{k})}

\displaystyle=+\lambda\frac{\exp(-\lambda\,x_{i}-\lambda\,x_{j})}{\left(\sum_{k=1}^{m}\exp(-\lambda\,x_{k})\right)^{2}}

\displaystyle=\lambda\,\sigma_{i}\sigma_{j}

for
i\neq j
and

\displaystyle{{\partial}^{2}\over{\partial}x_{i}^{2}}\mathop{\min\nolimits_{\lambda}}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})

\displaystyle={{\partial}\over{\partial}x_{i}}\frac{\exp(-\lambda\,x_{i})}{\sum_{j=1}^{m}\exp(-\lambda\,x_{j})}

\displaystyle=\frac{-\lambda\exp(-\lambda\,x_{i})\left(\sum_{j=1}^{m}\exp(-\lambda\,x_{j})\right)+\lambda\exp(-\lambda\,x_{i}-\lambda\,x_{i})}{\left(\sum_{j=1}^{m}\exp(-\lambda\,x_{j})\right)}

\displaystyle=-\lambda\,\sigma_{i}+\lambda\sigma_{i}\sigma_{i},

that is,

\nabla^{2}\mathop{\min\nolimits_{\lambda}}(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})=\lambda\Bigl(\sigma\sigma^{\top}-\diag\sigma\Bigr)=-\lambda\cdot\left(\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}\sigma_{1}&0&\ddots\\
0&\ddots&0\\
\ddots&0&\sigma_{n}\end{array}\right)-\sigma\cdot\sigma^{\top}\right).
