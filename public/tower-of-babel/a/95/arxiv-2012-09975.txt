Abstract
. A systematic theory of structural limits for finite models has been developed by Nešetřil and Ossona de Mendez. It is based on the insight that the collection of finite structures can be embedded, via a map they call the Stone pairing , in a space of measures, where the desired limits can be computed. We show that a closely related but finer grained space of (finitely additive) measures arises—via Stone-Priestley duality and the notion of types from model theory—by enriching the expressive power of first-order logic with certain “probabilistic operators”. We provide a sound and complete calculus for this extended logic and expose the functorial nature of this construction. The consequences are two-fold. On the one hand, we identify the logical gist of the theory of structural limits. On the other hand, our construction shows that the duality theoretic variant of the Stone pairing captures the adding of a layer of quantifiers, thus making a strong link to recent work on semiring quantifiers in logic on words. In the process, we identify the model theoretic notion of types as the unifying concept behind this link. These results contribute to bridging the strands of logic in computer science which focus on semantics and on more algorithmic and complexity related areas, respectively.

Introduction

While topology plays an important role, via Stone duality, in many parts of semantics, topological methods in more algorithmic and complexity oriented areas of theoretical computer science are not so common. One of the few examples,
the one we want to consider here, is the study of limits of finite relational structures.
We will focus on the
structural limits
introduced by Nešetřil and Ossona de Mendez
[
32
,
35
]
. These provide a common generalisation of various notions of limits of finite structures studied in probability theory, random graphs, structural graph theory, and finite model theory.
The basic construction in this work is the so-called
Stone pairing
.
Given a relational signature
\sigma
and a first-order formula
\varphi
in the signature
\sigma
with free variables
v_{1},\dots,v_{n}
, define

\left<\varphi,A\right>\coloneqq\frac{|\{\overline{a}\in A^{n}\mid A\models\varphi(\overline{a})\}|}{|A|^{n}}\qquad\parbox{130.0002pt}{\centering\emph{(the probability that a random assignment in $A$ satisfies $\varphi$)}.\@add@centering}

(1)

Nešetřil and Ossona de Mendez view the map
A\mapsto\left<\mkern 1.5mu\text{-}\mkern 1.5mu,A\right>
as an embedding of the finite
\sigma
-structures into the space of probability measures over the Stone space dual to the Lindenbaum–Tarski algebra of all first-order formulas in the signature
\sigma
. This space is complete and thus provides the desired limit objects for all sequences of finite structures which embed as Cauchy sequences.

Another example of topological methods in an algorithmically oriented area of computer science is the use of profinite monoids in automata theory. In this setting, profinite monoids are the subject of the extensive theory, based on theorems by Eilenberg and Reiterman, and used, among others, to settle decidability questions
[
36
]
.
In
[
7
]
, it was shown that this theory may be understood as an application of Stone duality, thus making a bridge between semantics and more algorithmically oriented work.
Bridging this semantics-versus-algorithmics gap in theoretical computer science has since gained quite some momentum, notably with the recent strand of research by Abramsky, Dawar and co-workers
[
2
,
3
]
.
In this spirit, a natural question is whether the structural limits of Nešetřil and Ossona de Mendez also can be understood semantically, and in particular whether the topological component may be seen as an application of Stone duality.

More precisely, recent work on understanding quantifiers in the setting of logic on finite words
[
10
]
has shown that adding a layer of certain quantifiers (such as classical and modular quantifiers) corresponds dually to a space-of-measures construction. The measures involved are not classical but only finitely additive and they take values in finite semirings rather than in the unit interval. Nevertheless, this appearance of
measures as duals of quantifiers
begs the further question whether the spaces of measures in the theory of structural limits may be obtained via Stone duality from a semantic addition of certain quantifiers to classical first-order logic.

The purpose of this paper is to address this question. Our main result is that the Stone pairing of Nešetřil and Ossona de Mendez is related by a retraction to a Stone space of finitely additive measures, which is dual to the Lindenbaum–Tarski algebra of a logic fragment obtained from first-order logic by adding one layer of probabilistic quantifiers, and which arises in exactly the same way as the spaces of semiring-valued measures in logic on words. That is, the Stone pairing, although originating from other considerations, may be seen as arising by duality from a semantic construction.

A foreseeable hurdle is that spaces of measures are not zero-dimensional and hence outside the scope of Stone duality. In fact, this issue stems from non-zero dimensionality of the unit interval
[0,1]
, in which the classical measures are valued. This is well-known to cause problems
e.g.
in attempts to combine non-determinism and probability in domain theory
[
22
]
. However, in the structural limits of Nešetřil and Ossona de Mendez, at the base, one only needs to talk about finite models equipped with normal distributions and thus only the finite intervals
{\mathbf{\Gamma}}_{n}\coloneqq\{0,\frac{1}{n},\frac{2}{n},\dots,1\}
are involved. A careful duality theoretic analysis identifies a codirected diagram (
i.e.
, an inverse limit system) based on these intervals compatible with the Stone pairing. The resulting inverse limit, which we denote
{\mathbf{\Gamma}}
, is a Priestley space. It comes equipped with an algebra-like structure, which allows us to reformulate many aspects of the theory of structural limits in terms of
{\mathbf{\Gamma}}
-valued measures as opposed to
[0,1]
-valued measures.

Some interesting features of
{\mathbf{\Gamma}}
, dictated by the nature of the Stone pairing and the ensuing codirected diagram, are that:

•

{\mathbf{\Gamma}}
is based on a version of
[0,1]
in which the rationals are doubled;

•

comes with section-retraction maps

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathbf{\Gamma}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces[0,1]}

Let us stress that in the present paper we deal with

Our contribution

We show that the ambient space of measures for the structural limits of Nešetřil and Ossona de Mendez can be obtained via
“adding a layer of quantifiers”
in a suitable enrichment of first-order logic. The conceptual framework for seeing this is that of
types
from classical model theory.
More precisely, we will see that a variant of the Stone pairing is a map into a space of measures with values in a Priestley space
{\mathbf{\Gamma}}
. Further, we show that this map is in fact the embedding of the finite structures into the space of (
0
-)types of an extension of first-order logic, which we axiomatise.
On the other hand,
{\mathbf{\Gamma}}
-valued measures and
[0,1]
-valued measures are tightly related by a retraction-section pair which allows the transfer of properties.
These results identify the logical gist of the theory of structural limits and provide a new interesting connection between logic on words and the theory of structural limits in finite model theory.

Stone-Priestley duality

In this paper, we will need Stone duality for bounded distributive lattices in the order-topological form due to Priestley
[
37

A

but

.

Next, we recall the construction of the Priestley space of a distributive lattice

2.2.
Stone duality and logic: type spaces

The
theory of types
is an important tool for first-order logic. We briefly recall the concept as it is closely related to, and provides the link between, two otherwise unrelated occurrences of topological methods in theoretical computer science.

Consider a first-order signature
\sigma
and a first-order theory
T
in this signature. Assume that we have a countably infinite sequence of distinct variables
(v_{1},v_{2},v_{3},\dots)
. Then, for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
, let
\mathrm{Fm}_{n}
denote the set of first-order formulas whose free variables are among
\overline{v}=(v_{1},\dots,v_{n})
, and let
\mathrm{Mod}_{n}(T)
denote the class of all pairs
(A,\alpha)
where
A
is a model of
T
and
\alpha\colon\{v_{1},\dots,v_{n}\}\to A
is an interpretation of
\overline{v}
in
A
.

Then the satisfaction relation,
(A,\alpha)\models\varphi(\overline{v})
, is a binary relation from
\mathrm{Mod}_{n}(T)
to
\mathrm{Fm}_{n}
. It induces the equivalence relations of elementary equivalence and logical equivalence on these sets, respectively:

\begin{array}[]{cccl}(A,\alpha)\equiv(B,\beta)&\kern 5.0pt\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{\Longleftrightarrow}}&\forall\varphi(\overline{v})\in\mathrm{Fm}_{n},&(A,\alpha)\models\varphi(\overline{v})\text{ iff }(B,\beta)\models\varphi(\overline{v}),\\
\varphi(\overline{v})\approx\psi(\overline{v})&\kern 5.0pt\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{\Longleftrightarrow}}&\forall(A,\alpha)\in\mathrm{Mod}_{n}(T),&(A,\alpha)\models\varphi(\overline{v})\text{ iff }(A,\alpha)\models\psi(\overline{v}).\end{array}

The quotient
\mathrm{FO}_{n}(T)\coloneqq\mathrm{Fm}_{n}/{\approx}
carries a natural Boolean algebra structure and is known as the
n
-th Lindenbaum–Tarski algebra
of
T
. Further, denoting by
[(A,\alpha)]
the
\equiv
-equivalence class of
(A,\alpha)
,
\Typ_{n}(T)\coloneqq\mathrm{Mod}_{n}(T)/{\equiv}
is naturally endowed with a topology, generated by the sets
\{[(A,\alpha)]\mid(A,\alpha)\models\varphi(\overline{v})\}
for
\varphi(\overline{v})\in\mathrm{Fm}_{n}
, and is known as the
space of
n
-types
of
T
. Furthermore, the Lindenbaum–Tarski algebra is often defined as the quotient of
\mathrm{Fm}_{n}
with respect to provable equivalence, where
\varphi(\overline{v})
and
\psi(\overline{v})
are provably equivalent (modulo
T
) if
T\vdash\varphi(\overline{v})\leftrightarrow\psi(\overline{v})
. By Gödel’s completeness theorem,
\mathrm{FO}_{n}(T)
is isomorphic to the Lindenbaum–Tarski algebra or, equivalently,
\Typ_{n}(T)
is the Stone dual of
\mathrm{Fm}_{n}
modulo provable equivalence. See
e.g.

[
18
, §6.3]
.

The Boolean algebra
\mathrm{FO}(T)
of
all
first-order formulas modulo logical equivalence over
T
is the directed colimit of the
\mathrm{FO}_{n}(T)
for
n\in\mathbb{N}
while its dual space,
\Typ(T)
, is the codirected limit of the
\Typ_{n}(T)
for
n\in\mathbb{N}
and consists of (equivalence classes of) models equipped with interpretations of the full set of variables.

Now, consider the set
\mathrm{Fin}(T)
consisting of isomorphism classes of finite
T
-models. Because two finite models are elementarily equivalent precisely when they are isomorphic,
\mathrm{Fin}(T)
can be identified with a subset of the space
\Typ_{0}(T)
of
0
-types of
T
.
If we want to study finite models, there are two equivalent approaches:
e.g.
at the level of sentences, we can either consider the theory
T_{\text{fin}}
of finite
T
-models, or the topological closure of
\mathrm{Fin}(T)
in the space
\Typ_{0}(T)
. This closure yields a space, which should tell us about finite
T
-structures. Indeed, it is equal to
\Typ_{0}(T_{\text{fin}})
, the space of pseudofinite
T
-structures. For an application of this, see
[
16
]
.
Below, we will see an application in finite model theory of the case
T=\emptyset
(in this case we write
\mathrm{FO}(\sigma)
and
\Typ(\sigma)
instead of
\mathrm{FO}(\emptyset)
and
\Typ(\emptyset)

In light of the theory of types as exposed above, the Stone pairing of Nešetřil and Ossona de Mendez (equation (
1

\Typ(\sigma)
, which set-theoretically are finitely additive functions

0

3.
The space
{\mathbf{\Gamma}}

Nešetřil and Ossona de Mendez observed in
[

]
that the Stone pairing

\varphi

\left<\varphi,A\right>+\left<\psi,A\right>=\left<\varphi\vee\psi,A\right>+\left<\varphi\wedge\psi,A\right>

and also
\left<\mathbf{f},A\right>=0
and
\left<\mathbf{t},A\right>=1
, where
\mathbf{f}
and
\mathbf{t}
denote false and true, respectively. By Stone duality, we can identify
\mathrm{FO}(\sigma)
with the Boolean algebra of clopen subsets of the space of types
\Typ(\sigma)

\Typ(\sigma)
form the Boolean algebra of measurable subsets, we view
\Typ(\sigma)
as a measurable space. Then, assigning to the second argument of the Stone pairing yields an embedding of
\mathrm{Fin}(\sigma)
, the set of isomorphism classes of finite

\Meac(\Typ(\sigma))
of finitely additive measures:

\mathrm{Fin}(\sigma)\to\Meac(\Typ(\sigma)),\qquad A\mapsto\left<\mkern 1.5mu\text{-}\mkern 1.5mu,A\right>.

The main obstacle in being able to use duality theory to investigate the logical nature of the space
\Meac(\Typ(\sigma))
is the fact that this space is not zero-dimensional, and thus not amenable to the usual duality theoretic methods. This is a direct consequence of the fact that

The construction of

The map

4.

The aim of this section is to replace

Let

\displaystyle\mu(h(a))\sim\mu(h(a\wedge b))

\displaystyle=\mu(h(a))\sim\mu(h(a)\wedge h(b))

\displaystyle\leq\mu(h(a)\vee h(b))-\mu(h(b))

\displaystyle=\mu(h(a\vee b))-\mu(h(b)),

where the middle inequality holds because
\mu
is a measure. The inequality

\mu(h(a))-\mu(h(a\wedge b))\geq\mu(h(a\vee b))\sim\mu(h(b))

is proved similarly. Thus,
\mu\cdot h\colon D\to{\mathbf{\Gamma}}
is a measure.

The mapping
\Mea(h)\colon\Mea(E)\to\Mea(D)
,
\mu\mapsto\mu\cdot h
, is clearly monotone. With respect to continuity, recall that the topology of
\Mea(D)
is generated by the sets of the form
\llbracket a<q\rrbracket=\{\nu\in\Mea(D)\mid\nu(a)<q^{\circ}\}
and
\llbracket a\geq q\rrbracket=\{\nu\in\Mea(D)\mid\nu(a)\geq q^{\circ}\}
,
with
a\in D
and
q\in\mathbb{Q}\cap[0,1]
. We have

\Mea(h)^{-1}\left(\llbracket a<q\rrbracket\right)=\left\{\mu\in\Mea(E)\mid\mu(h(a))<q^{\circ}\right\}=\llbracket h(a)<q\rrbracket

which is open in
\Mea(E)
. Similarly,
\Mea(h)^{-1}(\llbracket a\geq q\rrbracket)=\llbracket h(a)\geq q\rrbracket
, showing that
\Mea(h)
is continuous. Hence, in view of Proposition
7
,
\Mea(h)
is a morphism of Priestley spaces.
It is not difficult to see that identities and compositions are preserved, therefore
\Mea\colon\mathbf{DLat}\to\mathbf{Pries}
is a contravariant functor.

Remark 9
.

We work with the contravariant functor
\Mea\colon\mathbf{DLat}\to\mathbf{Pries}
because
\Mea
is concretely defined on the lattice side. However, by Priestley duality,
\mathbf{DLat}
is dually equivalent to
\mathbf{Pries}

\mathbf{Pries}\to\mathbf{Pries}

Next, we establish a property of the functor

Recall the maps
\gamma\colon{\mathbf{\Gamma}}\to[0,1]
and
\iota\colon[0,1]\to{\mathbf{\Gamma}}

Lemma 11
.

Let
D
be a distributive lattice. The following statements hold:

(1)

for every
\mu\in\Mea(D)
,
\gamma\cdot\mu\in\Meac(D)
;

(2)

for every
m\in\Meac(D)
,
\iota\cdot m\in\Mea(D)
.

Proof 4.5
.

(1)
The only non-trivial condition to verify is finite additivity. As pointed out after Lemma
3
, the map
\gamma
preserves both minus operations on
{\mathbf{\Gamma}}
. Hence, for all
a,b\in D
, the inequalities

\mu(a)\sim\mu(a\wedge b)\leq\mu(a\vee b)-\mu(b)
and

\mu(a)-\mu(a\wedge b)\geq\mu(a\vee b)\sim\mu(b)
imply that

\gamma\cdot\mu(a)-\gamma\cdot\mu(a\wedge b)=\gamma\cdot\mu(a\vee b)-\gamma\cdot\mu(b)
.

(2)
The first two conditions in Definition
4
are immediate. The third condition follows from the fact that
\iota(r-s)=\iota(r)-\iota(s)
whenever
s\leq r
in
[0,1]
, and
x\sim y\leq x-y
for every
(x,y)\in\mathrm{\mathrm{dom}(-)}
.

In view of the previous lemma, there are well-defined functions

\gamma^{\#}\colon\Mea(D)\to\Meac(D),\ \mu\mapsto\gamma\cdot\mu\kern 5.0pt\text{ and }\kern 5.0pt\iota^{\#}\colon\Meac(D)\to\Mea(D),\ m\mapsto\iota\cdot m.

Lemma 12
.

\gamma^{\#}\colon\Mea(D)\to\Meac(D)
is a continuous and monotone map.

Proof 4.6
.

The topology of
\Meac(D)
is generated by the sets of the form
\{m\in\Meac(D)\mid m(a)\in O\}
, for
a\in D
and

an open subset of

. In turn,

(\gamma^{\#})^{-1}\{m\in\Meac(D)\mid m(a)\in O\}=\{\mu\in\Mea(D)\mid\mu(a)\in\gamma^{-1}(O)\}

is open in
\Mea(D)
because

is continuous by Lemma

. This shows that
\gamma^{\#}\colon\Mea(D)\to\Meac(D)
is continuous.
Monotonicity is immediate.

Note that
\gamma^{\#}\colon\Mea(D)\to\Meac(D)
is surjective, since it admits
\iota^{\#}
as a (set-theoretic) section. It follows that
\Meac(D)
is a compact ordered space:

.

For each distributive lattice
D
,
\Meac(D)
is a compact ordered space.

Proof 4.7
.

The surjection
\gamma^{\#}\colon\Mea(D)\to\Meac(D)
is continuous (Lemma
12
). Since
\Mea(D)
is compact by Proposition
7
, so is
\Meac(D)
.
The order of
\Meac(D)
is clearly closed in the product topology, thus
\Meac(D)
is a compact ordered space.

Finally, we see that the set-theoretic retraction of
\Mea(D)
onto
\Meac(D)
lifts to the topological setting, provided we restrict to the down-set topologies.
If
(X,\leq)
is a partially ordered topological space, write
X^{\downarrow}
for the space with the same underlying set as
X
and whose topology consists of the open down-sets of

.

Theorem 14
.

The maps
\gamma^{\#}\colon\Mea(D)^{\downarrow}\to\Meac(D)^{\downarrow}
and
\iota^{\#}\colon\Meac(D)^{\downarrow}\to\Mea(D)^{\downarrow}
are a retraction-section pair of topological spaces.

.

It suffices to show that
\gamma^{\#}
and
\iota^{\#}
are continuous. It is not difficult to see, using Lemma

, that

, note that the topology of

is generated by the sets of the form

, for

We have

\displaystyle(\iota^{\#})^{-1}\llbracket a<q\rrbracket

which is an open set in

5.
The
{\mathbf{\Gamma}}
-valued Stone pairing and limits of finite structures

In the work of Nešetřil and Ossona de Mendez, the Stone pairing
\left<\mkern 1.5mu\text{-}\mkern 1.5mu,A\right>
is
[0,1]
-valued,
i.e.
an element of
\Meac(\mathrm{FO}(\sigma))
.
In this section, we show that basically the same construction for the recognisers arising from the application of a layer of semiring quantifiers in logic on words (
cf.
Section
2.3
) provides an embedding of finite
\sigma
-structures into the space of
{\mathbf{\Gamma}}
-valued measures. It turns out that this embedding is a
{\mathbf{\Gamma}}
-valued version of the Stone pairing. Hereafter we make a notational difference, writing
\left<\mkern 1.5mu\text{-}\mkern 1.5mu,\mkern 1.5mu\text{-}\mkern 1.5mu\right>_{\mathrm{I}}
for the (classical)
[0,1]
-valued Stone pairing.

The main ingredient of the construction are the
{\mathbf{\Gamma}}
-valued finitely supported functions.
To start with, we point out that the partial operation
-
on
{\mathbf{\Gamma}}
uniquely determines—by adjointness—a partial “plus” operation on
{\mathbf{\Gamma}}
.
Define

+\colon\mathrm{\mathrm{dom}(+)}\to{\mathbf{\Gamma}},{\quad\text{where}\quad}\mathrm{\mathrm{dom}(+)}=\left\{(x,y)\mid x\leq 1^{\circ}-y\right\},

by the following rules (whenever the expressions make sense):

r^{\circ}+s^{\circ}=(r+s)^{\circ},\ \ r^{-}+s^{\circ}=(r+s)^{-},\ \ r^{\circ}+s^{-}=(r+s)^{-},{\kern 5.0pt\text{and }\kern 5.0pt}r^{-}+s^{-}=(r+s)^{-}.

Then, for every
x\in{\mathbf{\Gamma}}
, the function
(\mkern 1.5mu\text{-}\mkern 1.5mu)+x
sending
y
to
y+x
is left adjoint to the function
(\mkern 1.5mu\text{-}\mkern 1.5mu)-x
sending
y
to
y-x
. This is the content of the next lemma (where, for all
x,y\in{\mathbf{\Gamma}}
, we let
[x,y]\coloneqq\{z\in{\mathbf{\Gamma}}\mid x\leq z\leq y\}
); for a proof, see the end of Appendix
B.4
.

Lemma 15
.

For every
x\in{\mathbf{\Gamma}}
, the monotone map
(\mkern 1.5mu\text{-}\mkern 1.5mu)+x\colon[0^{\circ},1^{\circ}-x]\to[x,1^{\circ}]
is left adjoint to
(\mkern 1.5mu\text{-}\mkern 1.5mu)-x\colon[x,1^{\circ}]\to[0^{\circ},1^{\circ}-x]
.

This plus operation allows us to define the notion of finitely supported
{\mathbf{\Gamma}}
-valued function:

{defi}

For any set
X
,
{\mathcal{F}}(X)
is the set of all functions
f\colon X\to{\mathbf{\Gamma}}
such that

(1)

the set
\mathrm{supp}(f)\coloneqq\{x\in X\mid f(x)\not=0^{\circ}\}
is finite, and

(2)

f(x_{1})+\cdots+f(x_{n})
is defined and equal to
1^{\circ}
, where
\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\}=\mathrm{supp}(f)
.

To improve readability, if the sum
y_{1}+\cdots+y_{m}
exists in
{\mathbf{\Gamma}}
, we denote it
\sum_{i=1}^{m}{y_{i}}
. Finitely supported functions in the above sense always determine measures over the power-set algebra:

Lemma 16
.

Let
X
be any set. There is a well-defined mapping
\int\colon{\mathcal{F}}(X)\to\Mea(\raisebox{2.03998pt}{\Large$\wp$}(X))
, assigning to every
f\in{\mathcal{F}}(X)
the measure

\textstyle\int f\colon M\mapsto\int_{M}f\coloneqq\sum_{x\in M\cap\mathrm{supp}(f)}{f(x)}.

Proof 5.1
.

We wish to prove that
\int f
is a measure for every
f\in{\mathcal{F}}(X)
. First, observe that
\int_{\emptyset}f=0^{\circ}
and
\int_{X}f=1^{\circ}
by definition. Moreover,
M\mapsto\int_{M}f
is monotone because
x\leq x+y
for all
x,y\in{\mathbf{\Gamma}}
for which
x+y
is defined.

It remains to show that, for any
M,N\subseteq X
,

\displaystyle\textstyle\int_{M}f\sim\int_{M\cap N}f\,\leq\,\int_{M\cup N}f-\int_{N}f{\quad\text{and}\quad}\int_{M}f-\int_{M\cap N}f\,\geq\,\int_{M\cup N}f\sim\int_{N}f.

(6)

To simplify these expressions, set
m\coloneqq\int_{M\setminus N}f
,
n\coloneqq\int_{N\setminus M}f
, and
i\coloneqq\int_{M\cap N}f
. Then, we see that

\textstyle\int_{M}f=m+i,\quad\int_{N}f=n+i{\kern 5.0pt\text{and}\kern 5.0pt}\int_{M\cup N}=m+n+i.

Consequently, the inequalities in (
6
) can be rewritten, respectively, as

(m+i)\sim i\,\leq\,(m+n+i)-(n+i){\quad\text{and}\quad}(m+i)-i\,\geq\,(m+n+i)\sim(n+i).

By Lemma
15
, these are equivalent, respectively, to

((m+i)\sim i)+(n+i)\,\leq\,m+n+i{\quad\text{and}\quad}((m+n+i)\sim(n+i))+i\leq m+i.

Next, observe that, for all
x,y\in{\mathbf{\Gamma}}
for which
x+y
is defined,
(x+y)\sim y
is always of
-
-type or equal to
0^{\circ}
, and so
(x+y)\sim y=\iota_{-}(\gamma(x))
(where the map
\iota_{-}\colon[0,1]\to{\mathbf{\Gamma}}
is defined as in Remark
5
). Therefore,
((m+i)\sim i)+n+i=\iota_{-}(\gamma(m))+n+i\leq m+n+i
and
((m+n+i)\sim(n+i))+i=\iota_{-}(\gamma(m))+i\leq m+i
.

5.1.
The
{\mathbf{\Gamma}}
-valued Stone pairing and logic on words

Fix a countably infinite sequence of distinct variables
(v_{1},v_{2},v_{3},\dots)
. Recall that
\mathrm{FO}_{n}(\sigma)
is the Lindenbaum–Tarski algebra of first-order formulas with free variables among
\{v_{1},\dots,v_{n}\}
. The dual space of
\mathrm{FO}_{n}(\sigma)
is the space of
n
-types
\Typ_{n}(\sigma)
. Its points are the elementary equivalence classes of pairs
(A,\alpha)
, where
A
is a
\sigma
-structure and
\alpha\colon\{v_{1},\dots,v_{n}\}\to A
is an interpretation of the variables. Also, recall that
\mathrm{Fin}(\sigma)
denotes the set of isomorphism classes of finite
\sigma
-structures.

We would like to define a function
\mathrm{Fin}(\sigma)\to{\mathcal{F}}(\Typ_{n}(\sigma))
which, intuitively, associates with a finite
\sigma
-structure
A
the (normalised) characteristic function of the finite set

\left\{(A,\alpha)\mid\alpha\colon\{v_{1},\dots,v_{n}\}\to A\right\},

which assigns value
\left(\frac{1}{|A|^{n}}\right)^{\circ}
to
(B,\beta)
if
A=B
, and
0
otherwise. However, we need to slightly modify this definition to take into account the fact that elements of
\Typ_{n}(\sigma)
are elementary equivalence classes of pairs
(B,\beta)
, and such an equivalence class may contain several pairs whose first component is
A
.

Define the map
\mathrm{Fin}(\sigma)\to{\mathcal{F}}(\Typ_{n}(\sigma))
which sends the isomorphism class of a finite
\sigma
-structure
A
to the finitely supported function
f_{n}^{A}
whose value at an equivalence class
E\in\Typ_{n}(\sigma)
is

f_{n}^{A}(E)\coloneqq\sum_{(A,\alpha)\in E}\left(\frac{1}{|A|^{n}}\right)^{\circ}\quad\parbox{215.00032pt}{\centering\emph{(add $\left(\frac{1}{|A|^{n}}\right)^{\circ}$ for every interpretation $\alpha$ of the free variables s.t.\ $(A,\alpha)$ is in the equivalence class)}.\@add@centering}

It is not difficult to see that this definition does not depend on the choice of a representative in the isomorphism class of
A
.

By Lemma
16
, we get a measure
\int f_{n}^{A}\colon\raisebox{2.03998pt}{\Large$\wp$}(\Typ_{n}(\sigma))\to{\mathbf{\Gamma}}
. Now, for each
\varphi\in\mathrm{FO}_{n}(\sigma)
, let
\llbracket\varphi\rrbracket_{n}\subseteq\Typ_{n}(\sigma)
be the set of (equivalence classes of)
\sigma
-structures with interpretations satisfying
\varphi
. By Stone duality we obtain an embedding
\llbracket\mkern 1.5mu\text{-}\mkern 1.5mu\rrbracket_{n}\colon\mathrm{FO}_{n}(\sigma)\hookrightarrow\raisebox{2.03998pt}{\Large$\wp$}(\Typ_{n}(\sigma))
. Restricting
\int f_{n}^{A}
to
\mathrm{FO}_{n}(\sigma)
, we get a measure

\textstyle\mu_{n}^{A}\colon\mathrm{FO}_{n}(\sigma)\to{\mathbf{\Gamma}},\quad\varphi\mapsto\int_{\llbracket\varphi\rrbracket_{n}}f_{n}^{A}.

Summing up, we have the composite map

\textstyle\mathrm{Fin}(\sigma)\to\Mea(\raisebox{2.03998pt}{\Large$\wp$}(\Typ_{n}(\sigma)))\to\Mea(\mathrm{FO}_{n}(\sigma)),\quad A\mapsto\int f_{n}^{A}\mapsto\mu_{n}^{A}.

(7)

Essentially the same construction is featured in logic on words,
cf.
equation (
2
):

•

The set of finite
\sigma
-structures
\mathrm{Fin}(\sigma)
corresponds to the set of finite words
A^{*}
.

•

The collection
\Typ_{n}(\sigma)
of (equivalence classes of)
\sigma
-structures with interpretations corresponds to
(A\times 2)^{*}
or, interchangeably,
\beta(A\times 2)^{*}
(in the case of one free variable).

•

The fragment
\mathrm{FO}_{n}(\sigma)
of first-order logic corresponds to the Boolean algebra of languages, defined by formulas with a free variable, dual to the Boolean space
X
appearing in (
2
).

•

The first map in the composite (
7

\sigma

5.2.
Limits in the spaces of measures

By Theorem

6.
The logic of measures

In view of Proposition
7

\Mea(D)

{\mathbf{\Gamma}}
-valued measures on

\PFG(D)

\PFG(D)
can be represented as the Lindenbaum–Tarski algebra for a propositional logic
P\mkern-1.0mu\mathcal{L}_{D}
obtained from
D
by adding probabilistic quantifiers.

The set of propositional variables of the logic
P\mkern-1.0mu\mathcal{L}_{D}
consists of the symbols
\mathbb{P}_{\geq p}\,a
, for every
a\in D
and
p\in\mathbb{Q}\cap[0,1]
. The logical connectives that we shall consider are
\mathbf{t},\mathbf{f},\wedge,\vee
, and the top and bottom elements of
D
are denoted
\mathbf{t}^{*}
and
\mathbf{f}^{*}
, respectively
5
5

5

To avoid cumbersome notation, we use the same symbols for infima in
D
and conjunctions in
P\mkern-1.0mu\mathcal{L}_{D}
, namely
\wedge
, and for suprema in
D
and disjunctions in
P\mkern-1.0mu\mathcal{L}_{D}
, namely
\vee
. It will be clear from the context to which concept we are referring.
.
The logic
P\mkern-1.0mu\mathcal{L}_{D}
is specified by the following rules, for all
p,q,r\in\mathbb{Q}\cap[0,1]
and
a,b\in D
(along with the usual ones for the connectives
\mathbf{t},\mathbf{f},\wedge,\vee
):

(L1)

\mathbb{P}_{\geq q}\,a\vdash\mathbb{P}_{\geq p}\,a
whenever
p\leq q

(L2)

\mathbb{P}_{\geq q}\,a\vdash\mathbb{P}_{\geq q}\,b
whenever
a\leq b

(L3)

\mathbf{t}\vdash\mathbb{P}_{\geq 0}\,\mathbf{f}^{*}
,
\mathbf{t}\vdash\mathbb{P}_{\geq q}\,\mathbf{t}^{*}
, and
\mathbb{P}_{\geq p}\,\mathbf{f}^{*}\vdash\mathbf{f}
whenever
p>0

(L4)

\mathbb{P}_{\geq p}\,a\,\wedge\,\mathbb{P}_{\geq q}\,b\vdash\mathbb{P}_{\geq p+q-r}\,(a\vee b)\,\vee\,\mathbb{P}_{\geq r}\,(a\wedge b)
whenever
0\leq p+q-r\leq 1

(L5)

\mathbb{P}_{\geq p+q-r}\,(a\vee b)\,\wedge\,\mathbb{P}_{\geq r}\,(a\wedge b)\vdash\mathbb{P}_{\geq p}\,a\,\vee\,\mathbb{P}_{\geq q}\,b
whenever
0\leq p+q-r\leq 1

The logic
P\mkern-1.0mu\mathcal{L}_{D}
can be interpreted in the space of measures
\Mea(D)
by setting, for every
\mu\in\Mea(D)
,

\mu\models\mathbb{P}_{\geq p}\,a\ \Leftrightarrow\ \mu(a)\geq p^{\circ}.

(9)

This satisfaction relation extends in the obvious way to the closure of the set of propositional variables under finite conjunctions and finite disjunctions. For any two formulas
\varphi,\psi
in the signature of
P\mkern-1.0mu\mathcal{L}_{D}
, we write
\varphi\models\psi
provided that

\forall\mu\in\Mea(D),\ \ \mu\models\varphi\text{ implies }\mu\models\psi.

The interpretation of
P\mkern-1.0mu\mathcal{L}_{D}
in
\Mea(D)
given in (
9
) is sound:

Lemma 21

(Soundness)
.

If
\varphi\vdash\psi
is any rule in
(L1)
–
(L5)
, then
\varphi\models\psi
.

Proof 6.1
.

(L1)
–
(L3)
are easily verified, and we leave the details to the reader. We spell out the case of
(L4)
; the proof of
(L5)
is the same, mutatis mutandis.

Fix arbitrary
a,b\in D
and
p,q,r\in\mathbb{Q}\cap[0,1]
such that
0\leq p+q-r\leq 1
. Let
\mu\in\Mea(D)
be any measure satisfying
\mu\models\mathbb{P}_{\geq p}\,a\,\wedge\,\mathbb{P}_{\geq q}\,b
, i.e.
\mu(a)\geq p^{\circ}
and
\mu(b)\geq q^{\circ}
. We must prove that
\mu\models\mathbb{P}_{\geq p+q-r}\,(a\vee b)\,\vee\,\mathbb{P}_{\geq r}\,(a\wedge b)
, that is either
\mu(a\vee b)\geq(p+q-r)^{\circ}
or
\mu(a\wedge b)\geq r^{\circ}
. Using the fact that
\mu
is a measure, we get

p^{\circ}\sim\mu(a\wedge b)\leq\mu(a)\sim\mu(a\wedge b)\leq\mu(a\vee b)-\mu(b)\leq\mu(a\vee b)-q^{\circ}.

If
\mu

\mu(a\wedge b)\geq r^{\circ}
, i.e.
\mu(a\wedge b)\leq r^{-}

p^{\circ}\sim r^{-}\leq\mu(a\vee b)-q^{\circ}

and therefore

(p+q-r)^{\circ}=(p^{\circ}\sim r^{-})+q^{\circ}\leq(\mu(a\vee b)-q^{\circ})+q^{\circ}\leq\mu(a\vee b).

\PFG(D)

.

.

(1) Recall that, for every

If

(2) Fix

By Lemma

is lower continuous and order reversing. Since the interval

is closed in

(cf.

). By lower continuity of

in

Adding Boolean complements.

By Theorem

Specialising to

7.
Related work

Our logic with probabilistic quantifiers (
cf.
Theorem
25
) is related to other axiomatisations of logics arising from space-of-measures constructions which have appeared in the literature. At the time of submitting this paper the authors were not aware of the almost identical axiomatisation of the dual of the probabilistic powerspace construction in the setting of stably compact spaces, due to Moshier and Jung
[
30
]
. Their work is heavily inspired by the axiomatisation given by Heckmann
[
17
]
in terms of a certain geometric propositional logic. The work of Heckmann was later streamlined by Vickers
[
40
]
. For another infinitary duality theoretic approach to logics with probabilistic quantifiers see
e.g.

[
28
,
5
]
, where an axiomatisation based on the signature of
\sigma
-complete Boolean algebras is provided.

Logics with probabilistic quantifiers have been studied also in the setting of finite model theory. In fact, Friedman’s probabilistic quantifiers (cf.
[
38
,
39
]
), which express that the probability is
>0
, are the main ingredient in the proof of one of the main results in the theory of structural limits, see
[
34
, Theorem 3.2 and Corollary 4.3]
. Furthermore, Kontinen
[
27
]
, Keisler and Lotfallah
[
24
]
, and Knyazev
[
25
]
showed that probabilistic quantifiers behave reasonably well with respect to zero-one laws.

Probabilistic quantifiers akin to ours were extensively studied already in the 1970s, see Keisler’s survey
[
23
]
. The main difference, compared to our approach, is that infinitary conjunctions or rules need to be added in order to obtain completeness, and therefore the compactness theorem is lost. Our logic, on the other hand, does not allow for nesting of probabilistic quantifiers, is finitary and satisfies the compactness theorem. The latter follows at once from duality theory and the fact that the space of
{\mathbf{\Gamma}}
-valued measures dual to
\mathcal{PL}^{*}_{D}
is topologically compact. More recently, Zhou
[
41
]
showed that a propositional version of Keisler logic can be made finitary; however, this logic still fails to satisfy the compactness theorem.

8.
Conclusion

Types are points of the dual space of a logic (identified with its Lindenbaum–Tarski algebra). In classical first-order logic,
0
-types are just the models modulo elementary equivalence. But when there are not “enough” models, as in finite model theory, the spaces of types provide completions of the sets of models.

In
[
10
]
, it was shown that for logic on words and various quantifiers we have that, given a Boolean algebra of formulas with a free variable, the space of types of the Boolean algebra generated by the formulas obtained by quantification is given by a space-of-measures construction. Here we have shown that a suitable enrichment of first-order logic gives rise to a space of measures
\Mea(\mathrm{FO}(\sigma))
closely related to the space
\Meac(\mathrm{FO}(\sigma))
used in the theory of structural limits. Indeed, Theorem
18
tells us that the ensuing Stone pairings interdetermine each other. Further, the Stone pairing for
\Mea(\mathrm{FO}(\sigma))
is just the embedding of the finite models in the completion/compactification provided by the space of
0
-types of the enriched logic (Theorem
25
).

These results identify the logical gist of the theory of structural limits, and provide a new and interesting connection between logic on words and the theory of structural limits in finite model theory. But we also expect that it may prove a useful tool in its own right.

For instance, for structural limits, it is an open problem to characterise the closure
\overline{\mathrm{Fin}(\sigma)}
of the image of the
[0,1]
-valued Stone pairing
[
33
]
. The same question translates equivalently to the
{\mathbf{\Gamma}}
-valued setting (Theorem
20
), native to logic and where we can use duality.
The closure
\overline{\mathrm{Fin}(\sigma)}
, seen as a subspace of
\Mea(\mathrm{FO}(\sigma))
, is also a Priestley space and this embedding dually corresponds to a quotient of the Lindenbaum–Tarski algebra of the enriched logic.
Understanding this quotient is crucial in order to characterise
\overline{\mathrm{Fin}(\sigma)}
. One would expect that this is the subspace
\Mea(\mathrm{FO}(T_{\text{fin}}))
of
\Mea(\mathrm{FO}(\sigma))
given by the quotient
\mathrm{FO}(\sigma)\twoheadrightarrow\mathrm{FO}(T_{\text{fin}})
onto the theory of pseudofinite structures. The purpose of such a characterisation would be to understand the points of the closure as “generalised models”.

Another subject that we would like to investigate is that of zero-one laws. The zero-one law for first-order logic states that the sequence of measures for which the
n
th measure, on a sentence
\psi
, yields the proportion of
n
-element structures satisfying
\psi
, converges to a
\{0,1\}
-valued measure. Over
{\mathbf{\Gamma}}
this will no longer be true as
1
is split into its “limiting” and “achieved” personae.
Yet, we expect the above sequence to converge also in this setting and, by Theorem
18
, it will converge to a
\{0^{\circ},1^{-},1^{\circ}\}
-valued measure.
Understanding this finer grained measure may yield useful information about the zero-one law.

Finally, it would be interesting to investigate whether the limits for schema mappings introduced by Kolaitis
et al.

[
26
]
may be seen also as a type-theoretic construction. Also, we would want to explore the connections with other semantically inspired approaches to finite model theory, such as those recently put forward by Abramsky, Dawar
et al.

[
2
,
3
]
.

[

We present an algebraic approach to extended Priestley duality which is based on the theory of canonical extensions. Its main feature is that both the space

and its dual lattice
A
embed (order-theoretically) into a complete lattice

, the

of

. This allows us to capture Priestley duality in purely algebraic terms, and is an incredibly powerful approach for dealing with additional operations on lattices. For example, if
A
comes equipped with a binary operation

then, by studying the relationship between the graph of
h
and the points of
X
in the product
A^{\delta}\times A^{\delta}\times A^{\delta}
, we can identify two partial binary operations on
X
that fully capture
h
. An instance of this situation is studied in this section. Our main inspiration comes from
[
12
,
13
]
and also
[
6
]
.

Every element of

. The set of all open elements of

is denoted

. In fact, there is a bijection between open elements of
A^{\delta}
and ideals of

and

. The latter restricts to a bijection between the set

of completely meet-irreducible elements of
A^{\delta}
and prime ideals of
A
. Recall that
p\in A^{\delta}
is
completely meet-irreducible
if, for all
S\subseteq C
,
\bigwedge S\leq p
implies
a\leq p
for some
a\in S
. The set
\mathcal{F}(A^{\delta})
of
closed elements
of
A^{\delta}
, and the set
\mathcal{J}^{\infty}(A^{\delta})
of
completely join-irreducible elements
of
A^{\delta}
are defined order-dually.

Note that
\mathcal{M}^{\infty}(A^{\delta})
does not include the top element of
A^{\delta}
. However, it is sometimes useful to add it, and so we will denote by
\mathcal{M}^{\infty}_{1}(A^{\delta})
the set
\mathcal{M}^{\infty}(A^{\delta})\cup\{1\}
and, similarly,
\mathcal{J}^{\infty}_{0}(A^{\delta})
denotes the set
\mathcal{J}^{\infty}(A^{\delta})\cup\{0\}
.

For a distributive lattice

, every element of

is a meet of completely meet-irreducible elements and a join of completely join-irreducible elements. Moreover, if we equip
\mathcal{J}^{\infty}(A^{\delta})
and
\mathcal{M}^{\infty}(A^{\delta})
with the partial orders induced by

, we have an order-isomorphism

\kappa\colon\mathcal{J}^{\infty}(A^{\delta})\to\mathcal{M}^{\infty}(A^{\delta}),\ \ j\mapsto\bigvee{\{a\in A\mid j\not\leq a\}}

satisfying, for every

,

and

u\leq\kappa(j)\iff j\nleq u{\quad\text{and}\quad}\kappa^{-1}(m)\leq u\iff u\nleq m.

This corresponds to the fact that the complement of a prime filter is a prime ideal, and vice versa. For more details, we refer the reader to
[
9
]
.

A.2.
Priestley duality

Thanks to the correspondence between prime filters (resp. prime ideals) of a distributive lattice
A
and completely join-irreducible (resp. completely meet-irreducible) elements of
A^{\delta}
, we can retrieve the Priestley space
X=(X,\leq,\tau)
dual to
A
by endowing either the set
\mathcal{M}^{\infty}(A^{\delta})
, or the set
\mathcal{J}^{\infty}(A^{\delta})
, with an order and topology. Both choices result in the same space, but we opt for the former as it provides a minor technical advantage in our applications. The order on
\mathcal{M}^{\infty}(A^{\delta})
is inherited from
A^{\delta}
and the subbase of the topology is formed by the sets
\{m\mid a\nleq m\}
and their complements, for every
a\in A
.

Remark 26
.

We warn the reader that, with the definition of dual Priestley space given in Section
2.1
, the Priestley space
X
just defined is, in fact, dual to the lattice
A^{\mathrm{op}}
and not
A
. Just observe that, for
m,n\in\mathcal{M}^{\infty}(A^{\delta})
,

m\leq n\kern 5.0pt\iff\kern 5.0ptI_{m}\subseteq I_{n}\kern 5.0pt\iff\kern 5.0ptF_{\kappa^{-1}(m)}\supseteq F_{\kappa^{-1}(n)},

where
I_{m}\coloneqq\{a\in A\mid a\leq m\}
is the prime ideal corresponding to
m\in\mathcal{M}^{\infty}(A^{\delta})
and
F_{j}\coloneqq\{a\in A\mid j\leq a\}
is the prime filter corresponding to
j\in\mathcal{J}^{\infty}(A^{\delta})
. When working with canonical extensions, it is convenient to order prime filters by
reverse
inclusion, so that both
X
and its dual lattice
A
order-theoretically embed into
A^{\delta}
. However, we opted for the inclusion order in the main text because it gives the usual pointwise order between measures. To apply the general theory to
{\mathbf{\Gamma}}
, in Appendix
B
we will take order-duals on the lattice side.

To also capture the duality between lattice homomorphisms and Priestley morphisms, we recall how maps between lattices extend to maps between their canonical extensions. For a monotone map
f\colon A\to B
between distributive lattice, its
sigma extension

f^{\sigma}\colon A^{\delta}\to B^{\delta}
is defined by

\displaystyle f^{\sigma}(k)

\displaystyle=\bigwedge\{f(a)\mid a\in A{\kern 5.0pt\text{and}\kern 5.0pt}k\leq a\}\quad\text{for all }k\in\mathcal{F}(A^{\delta})

\displaystyle f^{\sigma}(u)

\displaystyle=\bigvee\{f^{\sigma}(k)\mid k\in\mathcal{F}(A^{\delta}){\kern 5.0pt\text{and}\kern 5.0pt}k\leq u\}\quad\text{for all }u\in A^{\delta}

and, dually, its
pi extension

f^{\pi}\colon A^{\delta}\to B^{\delta}
is defined by

\displaystyle f^{\pi}(i)

\displaystyle=\bigvee\{f(a)\mid a\in A{\kern 5.0pt\text{and}\kern 5.0pt}a\leq i\}\quad\text{for all }i\in\mathcal{I}(A^{\delta})

\displaystyle f^{\pi}(u)

\displaystyle=\bigwedge\{f^{\pi}(i)\mid i\in\mathcal{I}(A^{\delta}){\kern 5.0pt\text{and}\kern 5.0pt}u\leq i\}\quad\text{for all }u\in A^{\delta}.

The two extensions are closely related as they agree on open and closed elements. Moreover, if
f
preserves binary joins then
f^{\sigma}
preserves all non-empty joins and, similarly, if
f
preserves binary meets then
f^{\pi}
preserves all non-empty meets.

Whenever
f^{\sigma}=f^{\pi}
, we say that
f
is
smooth
and denote the unique extension by
f^{\delta}
. This happens, for example, when
f
preserves finite (possibly empty) joins or finite meets.
Recall that a monotone map
f
between complete lattices preserves all joins if and only if it has a right adjoint
f^{\#}
and, dually, it preserves all meets precisely when it has a left adjoint
f^{\flat}
. Moreover, if
h\colon A\to B
is a lattice homomorphism, the right adjoint
h^{\delta}{}^{\#}
of
h^{\delta}\colon A^{\delta}\to B^{\delta}
preserves completely meet-irreducible elements and, dually, the left adjoint
h^{\delta}{}^{\flat}
preserves completely join-irreducible elements. We can compute the continuous monotone map dual to a homomorphism
h\colon A\to B
as the restriction of
h^{\delta}{}^{\#}\colon B^{\delta}\to A^{\delta}
to the completely meet-irreducible elements. Note that, if we defined dual Priestley spaces as based on
\mathcal{J}^{\infty}(A^{\delta})
, we would have to take the restriction of
h^{\delta}{}^{\flat}\colon B^{\delta}\to A^{\delta}
to completely join-irreducible elements.

A.3.
Duality theory for operators

In this section we outline some facts about the duality theory of normal double quasioperators
A^{n}\to A
based on
[
12
,
13
]
. Double quasioperators are double operators where arguments can have different monotonicity types. In order to avoid the notational difficulties that arise when presenting the theory for different monotonicity types, we assume that we have a three-sorted binary operator that is monotone in both coordinates.

{defi}

Let
A,B
and

be distributive lattices.
We say that a map

•

is a double operator,

•

i.e.

h(a,1)=1=h(1,b)
for every

and
b\in B
, and

•

h(0,0)=0
.

Remark 27
.

Everything true of three-sorted operators can also be applied to the dual scenario as well, that is, to the double operators
g\colon A\times B\to C
such that

and

. In this case, we simply treat

that is antitone in the second coordinate. Here, only the coordinates that are antitone have to be considered dually.

Throughout this section we fix a three-sorted operator

A

and

Y
and

is a complete operator (

We will also make use of the map

Lemma 28

For every

Proof A.1

For

Appendix B

is isomorphic to its canonical extension because it is finite and hence complete. The image of the order-embedding
{\mathbf{\Gamma}}_{n}\hookrightarrow L_{n}
coincides precisely with the set
\mathcal{M}^{\infty}(L_{n})
of (completely) meet-irreducible elements of
L_{n}
.

Next, we define two binary operators, with the first
\oplus\colon L_{n}\times L_{n}\to L_{n}
given by

\top\oplus u=u\oplus\top=\top\quad\forall u\in L_{n},{\quad\text{and}\quad}\frac{a}{n}\oplus\frac{b}{n}=\begin{cases}\top&\text{ if }a+b>n\\
\frac{a+b}{n}&\text{ otherwise}\end{cases}

and the second
\ominus\colon L_{n}\times L_{n}\to L_{n}
by

\displaystyle\top\ominus 0

\displaystyle=\top

\displaystyle\top\ominus\frac{b}{n}

\displaystyle=\frac{n-b+1}{n}\quad\text{ (for $b\geq 1$)}

\displaystyle\frac{a}{n}\ominus\frac{b}{n}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}0&\text{ if }b>a\\
\frac{a-b}{n}&\text{ otherwise}\end{cases}

\displaystyle u\ominus\top

\displaystyle=0\quad\forall u\in L_{n}.

In fact, these two operators are adjoint to each other: for all
u,v,w\in L_{n}
,
u\ominus v\leq w
if, and only if,
u\leq v\oplus w
. Since
L_{n}
is isomorphic to its canonical extension,
\oplus^{\pi}=\oplus^{\sigma}=\oplus
and
\ominus^{\pi}=\ominus^{\sigma}=\ominus
.

B.1.
Dualising
\oplus
on
L_{n}

Observe that
\oplus
is a three-sorted operator of type
L_{n}\times L_{n}\to L_{n}
. Because adjoints are uniquely determined, the left adjoint
f^{\flat}_{1}(u,\mkern 1.5mu\text{-}\mkern 1.5mu)
to
f(u,\mkern 1.5mu\text{-}\mkern 1.5mu)=u\oplus(\mkern 1.5mu\text{-}\mkern 1.5mu)
is given by
f^{\flat}_{1}(u,v)=v\ominus u
. Then, by Lemma
29
, the corresponding partial operation

\displaystyle z-x=\kappa(f^{\flat}_{1}(x,\kappa^{-1}z)),{\quad\text{for}\quad}x\leq z,

is well defined. The order-isomorphism
\kappa\colon\mathcal{J}^{\infty}(L_{n})\to\mathcal{M}^{\infty}(L_{n})
is given by
\top\mapsto 1
and
\frac{a}{n}\mapsto\frac{a-1}{n}
, for
a>0
. Consequently, it is not difficult to see that
-\colon\mathrm{dom}(-)\to{\mathbf{\Gamma}}_{n}
is precisely the partial subtraction on
{\mathbf{\Gamma}}_{n}
.

B.2.
Dualising
\ominus
on
L_{n}

The assignment
f(u,v)=v\ominus u
is a three-sorted operator of type
L_{n}\times L_{n}^{\mathrm{op}}\to L_{n}^{\mathrm{op}}
. Just observe that
u\ominus\top=0=0\ominus u
and
\top\ominus 0=\top
. Using Lemma
29
, we see that the right adjoint
f^{\#}_{1}(u,\mkern 1.5mu\text{-}\mkern 1.5mu)
to
f(u,\mkern 1.5mu\text{-}\mkern 1.5mu)
restricts to
\mathcal{M}^{\infty}(L_{n})^{\mathrm{op}}\times\mathcal{J}^{\infty}(L_{n}^{\mathrm{op}})\to\mathcal{J}^{\infty}_{0}(L_{n}^{\mathrm{op}})
, which is simply a monotone map
\mathcal{M}^{\infty}(L_{n})\times\mathcal{M}^{\infty}(L_{n})\to L_{n}
. Because right adjoints are unique,
f^{\#}_{1}(u,v)=u\oplus v
. Hence, the dual of
\ominus
on
{\mathbf{\Gamma}}_{n}
is the partial plus
x+z
, defined whenever
x\leq 1-z
.

In this specific case, the formula in Lemma
34
to recover
\ominus
from the partial plus looks as follows:

\displaystyle f(u,v)=\bigvee\{j\in\mathcal{J}^{\infty}(L_{n})

\displaystyle\mid\exists x\in\mathcal{M}^{\infty}(L_{n})\text{ s.t. }u\leq x,\ (x,\kappa(j))\in\mathrm{dom}(+),

\displaystyle\quad\text{ and }\kappa^{-1}(x+\kappa(j))\leq v\}.

B.3.
Duals to floor and ceiling functions.

Let
\varepsilon\colon{\mathbf{\Gamma}}_{n}\hookrightarrow{\mathbf{\Gamma}}_{nm}
be the obvious inclusion. Floor and ceiling functions are right and left adjoint, respectively, to
\varepsilon
. Just observe that, for all
\frac{a}{n}\in{\mathbf{\Gamma}}_{n}
and
\frac{b}{nm}\in{\mathbf{\Gamma}}_{nm}
, we have

\varepsilon\left(\tfrac{a}{n}\right)\leq\tfrac{b}{nm}\kern 5.0pt\iff\kern 5.0pt\tfrac{a}{n}\leq\lfloor\tfrac{b}{nm}\rfloor{\quad\text{and}\quad}\lceil\tfrac{b}{nm}\rceil\leq\tfrac{a}{n}\kern 5.0pt\iff\kern 5.0pt\tfrac{b}{nm}\leq\varepsilon\left(\tfrac{a}{n}\right).

Therefore, the floor function uniquely extends to the right adjoint of the lattice embedding
L_{n}\hookrightarrow L_{nm}
. On the other hand, the ceiling function naturally extends to the left adjoint of the lattice embedding
L^{\bot}_{n}\hookrightarrow L^{\bot}_{nm}
, with

L^{\bot}_{n}=\left(\bot<0<\frac{1}{n}<\dots<\frac{n-1}{n}<1\right)

and
L^{\bot}_{nm}
defined similarly. Therefore, as a matter of convenience, we prefer to view ceiling functions as functions of type
\mathcal{J}^{\infty}(L^{\bot}_{nm})\to\mathcal{J}^{\infty}(L^{\bot}_{n})
(
cf.
Section
A.2
). To summarise, the duals of floor functions are the lattice embeddings

i_{n,nm}\colon L_{n}\hookrightarrow L_{nm},

and the duals of ceiling functions are the lattice embeddings

i^{\prime}_{n,nm}\colon L_{n}\xrightarrow{\penalty\ t\penalty\ }L^{\bot}_{n}\hookrightarrow L^{\bot}_{nm}\xrightarrow{\penalty\ t^{-1}\penalty\ }L_{nm},

where
t\colon L_{n}\to L^{\bot}_{n}
is the renaming
\top\mapsto 1
,
0\mapsto\bot
and
\frac{a}{n}\mapsto\frac{a-1}{n}
. The duals of floor and ceiling functions can also be computed in terms of plain Priestley duality,
cf.
Section
2.1
.

Observe that
i_{n,m}\colon L_{n}\hookrightarrow L_{nm}
preserves
\oplus
but not
\ominus
, because

i_{n,nm}(\top\ominus\tfrac{b}{n})=i_{n,nm}(\tfrac{n-b+1}{n})=\tfrac{n-b+1}{n}

whereas

i_{n,nm}(\top)\ominus i_{n,nm}(\tfrac{b}{n})=\tfrac{nm-b+1}{nm},

for
1\leq b\leq n
. A similar argument shows that
i^{\prime}_{n,nm}
does not preserve
\oplus
nor
\ominus
.

The previous discussion justifies why we take the floor functions
\lfloor\mkern 1.5mu\text{-}\mkern 1.5mu\rfloor\colon{\mathbf{\Gamma}}_{nm}\twoheadrightarrow{\mathbf{\Gamma}}_{n}
as the comparison maps in the codirected diagram that defines
{\mathbf{\Gamma}}
and, moreover, why we equip the finite spaces
{\mathbf{\Gamma}}_{n}
with partial minus, rather than partial plus.

B.4.
The partial operations on
{\mathbf{\Gamma}}

Saying that
{\mathbf{\Gamma}}
is the codirected limit of the system of floor functions
\lfloor\mkern 1.5mu\text{-}\mkern 1.5mu\rfloor\colon{\mathbf{\Gamma}}_{nm}\to{\mathbf{\Gamma}}_{n}
is equivalent to saying that
{\mathbf{\Gamma}}
is the Priestley space dual to the directed colimit of the lattice embeddings
L_{n}\hookrightarrow L_{nm}
.
It is readily seen that this colimit can be identified with the lattice
\mathbf{L}=(\mathbb{Q}\cap[0,1])\cup\{\top\}
, where
\top
is the top element. Its canonical extension has the following properties:

Lemma 35
.

The following statements hold:

(1)

\mathbf{L}^{\delta}
can be identified with the lattice having underlying set

\{0^{\circ},0^{+}\}\cup\{r^{-},r^{+}\mid r\in(0,1)\setminus\mathbb{Q}\}\cup\{q^{-},q^{\circ},q^{+}\mid q\in(0,1)\cap\mathbb{Q}\}\cup\{1^{-},1^{\circ},\top\}

and the linear order defined by the following conditions:

(a)
r^{-}\prec r^{+}
for all
r\in(0,1)\setminus\mathbb{Q}

(b)
q^{-}\prec q^{\circ}\prec q^{+}
for all
q\in(0,1)\cap\mathbb{Q}

(c)
0^{\circ}\prec 0^{+}
and
1^{-}\prec 1^{\circ}\prec\top

(d)
x^{+}<y^{-}
if
x<y
in
[0,1]

where
\prec
is the covering relation, i.e.
\alpha\prec\beta
if
\alpha<\beta
and, whenever
\alpha\leq\gamma\leq\beta
, either
\alpha=\gamma
or
\beta=\gamma
.

(2)

\mathcal{M}^{\infty}(\mathbf{L}^{\delta})=\mathbf{L}^{\delta}\setminus(\{\top\}\cup\{r^{+}\mid r\in[0,1)\})
and
\mathcal{J}^{\infty}(\mathbf{L}^{\delta})=\mathbf{L}^{\delta}\setminus(\{0^{\circ}\}\cup\{r^{-}\mid r\in(0,1]\})
.

(3)

The order-isomorphism
\kappa\colon\mathcal{J}^{\infty}(\mathbf{L}^{\delta})\to\mathcal{M}^{\infty}(\mathbf{L}^{\delta})
is given by

\top\mapsto 1^{\circ}\qquad\qquad x^{+}\mapsto\begin{cases}x^{\circ}&\text{ if }x\in\mathbb{Q}\\
x^{-}&\text{ otherwise}\end{cases}\qquad\qquad q^{\circ}\mapsto q^{-}.

Proof B.1
.

For item 1, it is easy to see that
\mathbf{L}^{\delta}
is a complete lattice and that the embedding
\mathbf{L}\hookrightarrow\mathbf{L}^{\delta}
, defined as
a\mapsto a^{\circ}
and
\top\mapsto\top
, satisfies the density and compactness conditions.
Items 2 and 3 are easily verified and left to the reader.

Because the lattice embeddings
L_{n}\hookrightarrow L_{nm}
preserve the operators
\oplus\colon L_{n}\times L_{n}\to L_{n}
, also
\mathbf{L}
comes equipped with an operator
\oplus\colon\mathbf{L}\times\mathbf{L}\to\mathbf{L}
obtained as the colimit of the corresponding operators on the finite sublattices
L_{n}
. This can be expressed as follows:

\top\oplus u=u\oplus\top=\top,\quad\forall u\in\mathbf{L},\quad{\quad\text{and}\quad}\quad\frac{a}{n}\oplus\frac{b}{m}=\begin{cases}\top&\text{ if }am+bn>nm\\
\frac{am+bn}{nm}&\text{ otherwise.}\end{cases}

This time, though,
\oplus
does not have a left adjoint because
\top\ominus\frac{a}{n}
cannot be defined on
\mathbf{L}
. This mirrors the fact that the inclusion maps
L_{n}\hookrightarrow L_{mn}
do not preserve
\ominus
defined on finite lattices. Following the recipe of Section
A.4
, we compute the partial operation
-\colon\mathrm{dom}(-)\to{\mathbf{\Gamma}}
from the left adjoint
a\mathbin{\oplus^{\pi\mkern 2.0mu\flat}}(\mkern 1.5mu\text{-}\mkern 1.5mu)
of
a\oplus^{\pi}(\mkern 1.5mu\text{-}\mkern 1.5mu)
. The pi extension
\oplus^{\pi}
is commutative because so is
\oplus
. Therefore, the following lemma provides us with a full description of
\oplus^{\pi}\colon\mathbf{L}^{\delta}\to\mathbf{L}^{\delta}
.

Lemma 36
.

Suppose
x,y\in[0,1]
. Then

x+y\leq 1\kern 5.0pt\Rightarrow\hskip-30.00005pt\begin{split}&\penalty\ x^{\circ}\oplus^{\pi}y^{\circ}\kern 5.0pt=\kern 5.0pt(x+y)^{\circ}\\
&\begin{cases}x^{\circ}\oplus^{\pi}y^{-}\\
x^{-}\oplus^{\pi}y^{-}\end{cases}\kern 5.0pt=\kern 5.0pt(x+y)^{-}\end{split}\quad\text{and}\quad x+y<1\ \Rightarrow\begin{split}&\begin{cases}x^{+}\oplus^{\pi}y^{+}\\
x^{+}\oplus^{\pi}y^{\circ}\\
x^{+}\oplus^{\pi}y^{-}\end{cases}\kern 5.0pt=\kern 5.0pt(x+y)^{+},\end{split}

whenever the expressions make sense. In all the remaining cases, we get
\top
.

Proof B.2
.

Suppose
x+y\leq 1
. It is not difficult to see that the set of open elements of
\mathbf{L}^{\delta}
is
\mathcal{I}(\mathbf{L}^{\delta})=\mathcal{M}^{\infty}_{1}(\mathbf{L}^{\delta})=\mathcal{M}^{\infty}(\mathbf{L}^{\delta})\cup\{\top\}
. Just observe that every proper ideal in a linearly ordered lattice is prime. Hence, by item 2 in Lemma
35
,

\mathcal{I}(\mathbf{L}^{\delta})=\{0^{\circ}\}\cup\{r^{-}\mid r\in(0,1)\setminus\mathbb{Q}\}\cup\{q^{-},q^{\circ}\mid q\in(0,1)\cap\mathbb{Q}\}\cup\{1^{-},1^{\circ},\top\}.

By the definition of pi extensions on open elements (cf. Section
A.2
), we get

x^{\circ}\oplus^{\pi}y^{\circ}=\bigvee{\{a\oplus b\mid a,b\in\mathbf{L},\ a\leq x^{\circ}\text{ and }b\leq y^{\circ}\}}=(x+y)^{\circ}.

The same equation also tells us the results of
x^{\circ}\oplus^{\pi}y^{-}
and
x^{-}\oplus^{\pi}y^{-}
, since all the involved elements are open:

\displaystyle x^{\circ}\oplus^{\pi}y^{-}

\displaystyle=\bigvee{\{a\oplus b\mid a,b\in\mathbf{L},\ a\leq x^{\circ}\text{ and }b\leq y^{-}\}}

\displaystyle=\bigvee{\{x\oplus b\mid b\in\mathbb{Q}\cap[0,1],\ b<y\}}=(x+y)^{-},

and

x^{-}\oplus^{\pi}y^{-}=\bigvee{\{a\oplus b\mid a,b\in\mathbb{Q}\cap[0,1],\ a<x\text{ and }b<y\}}=(x+y)^{-}.

Suppose now that
x+y<1
. The definition of pi extensions on possibly non-open elements, along with the cases covered in the first part of the proof, yield

\displaystyle x^{+}\oplus^{\pi}y^{+}

\displaystyle=\bigwedge{\{w\oplus^{\pi}z\mid w,z\in\mathcal{I}(\mathbf{L}^{\delta}),\ x^{+}\leq w\text{ and }y^{+}\leq z\}}

\displaystyle=\bigwedge{\{w^{-}\oplus^{\pi}z^{-}\mid w,z\in(0,1],\ x<w\text{ and }y<z\}}

\displaystyle=\bigwedge{\{(w+z)^{-}\mid w,z\in(0,1],\ x<w\text{ and }y<z\}}=(x+y)^{+}.

All the remaining cases are computed similarly.

Since the assignment
a\mapsto a\oplus 0
is equal to the identity, its dual
i\colon{\mathbf{\Gamma}}\to{\mathbf{\Gamma}}
is also the identity. Therefore,
\mathrm{dom}(-)
is equal to
\{(z,x)\mid x\leq z\}
. Moreover, it follows from Lemma
35
, along with Lemma
37
below, that the partial operation
z-x=\kappa(x\mathbin{\oplus^{\pi\mkern 2.0mu\flat}}\kappa^{-1}z)
is exactly the one described in Section
3.2
.

Lemma 37
.

Let
u,v\in\mathbf{L}^{\delta}
. If
u\leq v
, then
u\mathbin{\oplus^{\pi\mkern 2.0mu\flat}}v=0^{\circ}
. Otherwise, for
x,y\in[0,1]
,

\begin{array}[]{|c|c|c|c|}\hline\cr\mathbin{\oplus^{\pi\mkern 2.0mu\flat}}&y^{\circ}&y^{-}&y^{+}\\
\hline\cr x^{\circ}&(x-y)^{\circ}&(x-y)^{+}&(x-y)^{-}\\
\hline\cr x^{-}&(x-y)^{-}&(x-y)^{-}&(x-y)^{-}\\
\hline\cr x^{+}&(x-y)^{+}&(x-y)^{+}&(x-y)^{-}\\
\hline\cr\end{array}

whenever the expressions make sense (read as:
(\text{row})\mathbin{\oplus^{\pi\mkern 2.0mu\flat}}(\text{colon})
). Finally,

\top\mathbin{\oplus^{\pi\mkern 2.0mu\flat}}x^{\circ}=\top\mathbin{\oplus^{\pi\mkern 2.0mu\flat}}x^{-}=(1-x)^{+}\ \text{ and }\ \top\mathbin{\oplus^{\pi\mkern 2.0mu\flat}}x^{+}=(1-x)^{-}.

Proof B.3
.

Follows by a routine computation using the usual description of left adjoints in terms of infima, i.e.
u\mathbin{\oplus^{\pi\mkern 2.0mu\flat}}v=\bigwedge{\{w\in\mathbf{L}^{\delta}\mid u\leq v\oplus^{\pi}w\}}
for every
u,v\in\mathbf{L}^{\delta}
.

Lastly, we compute the expression for the second partial operation
\sim\colon\mathrm{dom}(\sim)\to{\mathbf{\Gamma}}
. However, instead of computing the sigma extension of
\oplus
and its (partial) right adjoint, as in Section
A.5
, we use the formula from Lemma
34
. Because
i\colon{\mathbf{\Gamma}}\to{\mathbf{\Gamma}}
is the identity mapping,
\mbox{$\mathrm{dom}$($\sim$)}=\{(z,x)\mid z\nleq x\}=\{(z,x)\mid x<z\}
. Furthermore, by Lemma
34
, for every
(z,x)\in\mathrm{dom}(\sim)
we have

z\sim x=\bigvee\{z-x^{\prime}\mid x^{\prime}\in{\mathbf{\Gamma}}\text{ s.t. }x<x^{\prime}\leq z\}.

Now, since
x<z
, there are two possible cases. Either
x=q^{-}<q^{\circ}=z
for some rational
q\in(0,1]
, in which case the unique
x^{\prime}
such that
x<x^{\prime}\leq z
is
q^{\circ}
, or
x
is not an immediate predecessor of
z
. In the latter case, by a similar argument, for every
x<x^{\prime}\leq z
there is a rational
q\in(0,1]
such that
x<q^{\circ}\leq x^{\prime}
. Thus, because
-
is antitone in the second coordinate, we obtain exactly the same expression as used in Section
3.2
to define
\sim
:

z\sim x=\bigvee\{z-x^{\prime}\mid q\in(0,1]\cap\mathbb{Q}\text{ s.t. }x<q^{\circ}\leq z\}.

The only difference is that
z\sim x
, as described in Section
3.2
, is also defined for
z=x
.

To conclude, we offer a proof of Lemma
15
, which relies on the theory exposed above.

Proof B.4

(Proof of Lemma
15
)
.

Let
f
be the pi extension
\oplus^{\pi}
of
\oplus
. Then,

f(x,y)\leq z\iff\kappa^{-1}z\nleq f(x,y)\iff f^{\flat}_{1}(x,\kappa^{-1}z)\nleq y\iff y\leq z-x

for every
y\in{\mathbf{\Gamma}}
and
(z,x)\in\mathrm{dom}(-)
. By Lemma
36
, the restriction of
f
to

(x^{\circ},y^{\circ}),(x^{\circ},y^{-}),(x^{-},y^{\circ}),\text{ and }(x^{-},y^{-})

where
x+y\leq 1
(whenever the expressions make sense), is a well defined partial operation on
{\mathbf{\Gamma}}
. Observe that this domain of definition is precisely
\{(x,y)\mid x\leq 1^{\circ}-y\}
because
x\leq 1^{\circ}-y
iff
f(x,y)\leq 1^{\circ}
iff
f(x,y)\neq\top
.
