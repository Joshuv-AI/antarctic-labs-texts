Continued Fractions and Hardy Sums
Alessandro Lägeler

Abstract
. The classical Dedekind sums s ⁡ ( d , c ) s(d,c) can be represented as sums over the partial quotients of the continued fraction expansion of the rational d c \frac{d}{c} . Hardy sums, the analog integer-valued sums arising in the transformation of the logarithms of θ \theta -functions under a subgroup of the modular group, have been shown to satisfy many properties which mirror the properties of the classical Dedekind sums. The representation as sums of partial quotients has, however, been missing so far. We define non-classical continued fractions and prove that Hardy sums can be expressed as a sums of partial quotients of these continued fractions. As an application, we prove that the graph of the Hardy sums is dense in 𝐑 × 𝐙 \mathbf{R}\times\mathbf{Z} .



1.
Introduction

The Dedekind sums

(1.1)

s(d,c)=\frac{1}{4c}\sum_{k=1}^{|c|-1}\cot\frac{\pi k}{c}\cot\frac{\pi kd}{c},\;\;\;(d,c)=1,

are ubiquitous objects in mathematics appearing in diverse fields such as physics, geometry, and topology. They are completely determined by the reciprocity law

(1.2)

s(d,c)+s(c,d)=\frac{c^{2}+d^{2}+1}{cd}-\frac{1}{4}

together with the fact that
s(0,1)=0
and
s(d+c,c)=s(d,c)
.

Definition (
1.1
) of the Dedekind sums as cotangent-sums is not the only representation of these finite sums. For instance, another formula due to Hickerson
[
5
]
expresses
s(d,c)
in terms of the continued fraction expansion of the rational
\frac{d}{c}
, namely

(1.3)

s(d,c)=-\frac{1}{4}+\frac{1}{12}\left(\frac{a+d}{c}-\sum_{k=1}^{2n+1}(-1)^{k}a_{k}\right),\;\;\;0<a<c,\;ad\equiv 1\pmod{c},

where the
a_{i}\geq 0
are the partial quotients of the continued fraction expansion of

\frac{d}{c}=[0;a_{1},...,a_{2n+1}]=\frac{1}{a_{1}+\frac{1}{\cdots\;+\frac{1}{a_{2n+1}}}}.

The expression (
1.3
) was used by Hickerson to prove that the set
\{(d/c,\;s(d,c)):(d,c)=1\}
is dense in the plane
\mathbf{R}\times\mathbf{R}
, thus proving a conjecture of Rademacher
[
12
]
.

Dedekind sums first arose in the context of the transformation behavior of
\log\eta
, the logarithm of the Dedekind
\eta
-function
\eta(z)=e^{\frac{\pi i}{12}z}\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}(1-e^{2\pi inz})
, under the modular group
\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbf{Z})
.
1
1

1

Whenever we talk about the complex logarithm, we shall mean its canonical branch, i.e. with argument in
(-\pi,\pi]
.
It transforms like a weight
\frac{1}{2}
modular form with a multiplier system. For
A={\tiny\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{pmatrix}}\in\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbf{Z})
with
c>0

\log\eta(z)

(1.4)

\log\eta(A.z)-\log\eta(z)=\frac{1}{2}\log\left(\frac{cz+d}{i}\right)+\frac{\pi i}{12}\left(\frac{a+d}{c}-12\;s(d,c)\right).

Giving another proof of the reciprocity law (
1.2
), Hardy
[
4
]
looked at the contour integration of trigonometric functions over a large circle. Changing trigonometric functions in the integrand led him to other finite sums, some of which exhibit reciprocity laws. In this note, we will consider two of these ”Hardy sums,” namely

(1.5)

S(d,c)=\sum_{k=1}^{c-1}(-1)^{k+1+\left\lfloor\frac{dk}{c}\right\rfloor}\;\;\;\mathrm{for}\;c>0,\;(d,c)=1,

and

(1.6)

S_{4}(d,c)=\sum_{k=1}^{c-1}(-1)^{\left\lfloor\frac{dk}{c}\right\rfloor}\;\;\;\mathrm{for}\;c>0,\;(d,c)=1.

Both sums
S(d,c)
and
S_{4}(d,c)
in (
1.5
) and (
1.6
) are integer-valued and it is easy to see that
S(-d,c)=-S(d,c)
and
S(d+2c,c)=S(d,c)
and that the same identities also hold for
S_{4}(d,c)
.

The Hardy sums in (
1.5
) and (
1.6
) are particularly interesting, as they arise analogously to the Dedekind sums as correction factors in the transformation law of the logarithms of
\theta
-functions under subgroups of
\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbf{Z})
. These
\theta
-functions are also weight
\frac{1}{2}
forms, but with different multiplier systems.

More precisely, let

(1.7)

\theta(z)=\sum_{n\in\mathbf{Z}}e^{\pi in^{2}z}=\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}(1-e(nz))\left(1+e\left(\left(n-1/2\right)z\right)\right)^{2}

be the classical
\theta
-function, where, as usual,
e(z)=e^{2\pi iz}
. Moreover, define

(1.8)

\theta_{4}(z)=\sum_{n\in\mathbf{Z}}(-1)^{n}e^{\pi in^{2}z}=\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}(1-e(nz))\left(1-e\left(\left(n-1/2\right)z\right)\right)^{2}.

The functions
\theta(z)
and
\theta_{4}(z)
exhibit modular transformations for the subgroups

(1.9)

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\Gamma_{\theta}=\left\{{\tiny\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{pmatrix}}\in\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbf{Z}):a\equiv d,\;b\equiv c\pmod{2}\right\},\;\mathrm{resp.}\\
&\Gamma^{0}(2)=\left\{{\tiny\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{pmatrix}}\in\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbf{Z}):b\equiv 0\pmod{2}\right\}\end{split}

instead of the full modular group
\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbf{Z})
. Berndt
[
1
]
proved that

(1.10)

\log\theta(A.z)-\log\theta(z)=\frac{1}{2}\log\left(\frac{cz+d}{i}\right)+\frac{\pi i}{4}S(d,c)\;\mathrm{for}\;A={\tiny\begin{pmatrix}*&*\\
c&d\end{pmatrix}}\in\Gamma_{\theta},\;c>0,

and

(1.11)

\log\theta_{4}(A.z)-\log\theta_{4}(z)=\frac{1}{2}\log\left(\frac{cz+d}{i}\right)-\frac{\pi i}{4}S_{4}(d,c)\;\mathrm{for}\;A={\tiny\begin{pmatrix}*&*\\
c&d\end{pmatrix}}\in\Gamma^{0}(2),\;c>0.

Rademacher
[
11
]
gave the correction factors of the transformation of
\log\theta

in terms of non-trivial linear combinations of Dedekind sums (see also Sitaramachandrarao’s elementary approach

The Hardy sums

c,d
both odd or both even, but the value of the sum will then be equal to zero. Hence, we always implicitly assume that
c+d
is odd when talking about the sum

. Similarly, will always implicitly assume that
d
is odd in
S_{4}(d,c)

{\tiny\begin{pmatrix}*&*\\
c&d\end{pmatrix}}
lies in
\Gamma^{0}(2)
.

Meyer
[
8
]
proved that the sets
\{(d/c,\;S(d,c)):(d,c)=1,\;c+d\;\mathrm{odd}\}
and
\{(d/c,\;S_{4}(d,c)):(c,d)=1,\;d\;\mathrm{odd}\}
are dense in
\mathbf{R}\times\mathbf{Z}
, relying on non-trivial results of Goldberg
[
3
]
and Duke, Friedlander, and Iwaniec
[
2
]
. The goal of this paper is to give another proof of Meyer’s result along the lines of Hickerson’s proof for the classical Dedekind sums. To this end, we first write the Hardy sums in terms of the partial quotients of a continued fraction expansion.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
d
be an integer and
c>0
such that
(d,c)=1
.

(1)

For
c+d
odd, let
\frac{d}{c}=\llbracket 2c_{0};2c_{1},...,2c_{n}\rrbracket=2c_{0}-\frac{1}{2c_{1}-\frac{1}{\cdots-\frac{1}{2c_{n}}}}
with
c_{0}\in\mathbf{Z}
and
c_{1},...,c_{n}
non-zero integers be the negative continued fraction of
\frac{d}{c}
. The Hardy sum
S(d,c)
takes the form

(1.12)

S(d,c)=-\sum_{k=1}^{n}\mathrm{sign}(c_{k}).

(2)

For
d
odd, let
\frac{d}{c}=[2a_{0};a_{1},2a_{2},a_{3},...,2a_{n-1},a_{n}]
with
a_{0}\in\mathbf{Z}
and
a_{1},...,a_{n}
non-zero integers such that
|a_{k}|>1
for
k=1,3,...,n-2
. The Hardy sum
S_{4}(d,c)
takes the form

(1.13)

S_{4}(d,c)=(a_{1}+a_{3}+...+a_{n})+\sum_{k=1}^{n}(-1)^{k}\mathrm{sign}(a_{k}).

Remark:
The continued fraction expansion
\llbracket 2c_{0};2c_{1},...,2c_{n}\rrbracket
is unique; the expansion
[2a_{0};a_{1},2a_{2},a_{3},...,a_{n}]
from the second part of Theorem
1.1
, however, is not. The fact that
\frac{d}{c}
has a continued fraction expansions as claimed in Theorem
1.1
will be shown in section
2
.

Analogously to Hickerson’s proof for the density of
\{(d/c,\;s(d,c)):(d,c)=1\}
in the plane
\mathbf{R}\times\mathbf{R}
, Theorem
1.1
will be used to prove both the density of
(d/c,\;S(d,c))
and
(d/c,\;S_{4}(d,c))
in
\mathbf{R}\times\mathbf{Z}
, hence giving a new proof of Meyer’s result.

Theorem 1.2

(Meyer)
.

The sets
\{(d/c,\;S(d,c)):(d,c)=1,\;c>0,\;d+c\;\mathrm{odd}\}
and
\{(d/c,\;S_{4}(d,c)):(d,c)=1,\;c>0,\;d\;\mathrm{odd}\}
are dense in
\mathbf{R}\times\mathbf{Z}
.

Note that
S(d,c)+S_{4}(d,c)
makes sense if we restrict the coprime integers
d,c
to be such that
d
is odd and
c
is even (hence, the matrix
{\tiny\begin{pmatrix}*&*\\
c&d\end{pmatrix}}\in\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbf{Z})
lies both in
\Gamma_{\theta}
and
\Gamma^{0}(2)
). The continued fraction expansion of
\frac{d}{c}
then can be written as

\frac{d}{c}=\llbracket 2c_{0},2c_{1},...,2c_{n}\rrbracket=[2c_{0};-2c_{1},2c_{2},...,-2c_{n}],\;\;n\geq 1\;\mathrm{odd},

with both continued fraction expansions satisfying the assumptions of both parts of Theorem
1.1
. We thus get the following easy corollary.

Corollary 1.3
.

Let
c>0
be an even integer and
d
an odd integer, coprime to
c
. Let
c_{0},c_{1},...,c_{n}
be integers with
c_{1},...,c_{n}
non-zero and
n\geq 1
odd such that
\frac{d}{c}=\llbracket 2c_{0},2c_{1},...,2c_{n}\rrbracket
. We have

(1.14)

S(d,c)+S_{4}(d,c)=2\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k=1,\\
k\;\mathrm{odd}\end{subarray}}^{c-1}(-1)^{\left\lfloor\frac{dk}{c}\right\rfloor}=-2\cdot(c_{1}+c_{3}+...+c_{n}).

As an application of (
1.14
), we can prove the following density theorem, which appears to be new.

Theorem 1.4
.

The set
\{(d/c,\;S(d,c)+S_{4}(d,c)):(d,c)=1,\;c>0\;\mathrm{even},\;d\;\mathrm{odd}\}
is dense in
\mathbf{R}\times 2\mathbf{Z}
.

Hickerson proved the representation (
1.3
) of the Dedekind sums
s(d,c)
using the reciprocity law (
1.2
). Given (
1.4
), the reciprocity law can easily be proved by comparing the transformations of
\log\eta
under
A={\tiny\begin{pmatrix}*&*\\
c&d\end{pmatrix}}
and
AS={\tiny\begin{pmatrix}*&*\\
d&-c\end{pmatrix}}
, where

1&0\end{pmatrix}}
is the inversion (see, for instance, Grosswald and Rademacher’s monograph
[
12
]
).

Since
{\tiny\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}}\in\Gamma_{\theta}
, there is a reciprocity law similar to (
1.2
) for

as well, namely

(1.15)

S(d,c)+S(c,d)=\mathrm{sign}(cd),\;(c,d)=1,\;c+d\;\mathrm{odd},

where we set
S(d,-c)=-S(d,c)
(i.e. we allow negative

).

However, since
{\tiny\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}}\notin\Gamma^{0}(2)
, we should not expect a reciprocity law for
S_{4}(d,c)

[
9
]
, but expressing the right hand side of the reciprocity law by

can be given by using Theorem

, namely

(1.16)

S_{4}(d,c)+S_{4}(c,d)=2a_{0}+2a_{1}+...+2a_{n-1}+a_{n}-1,

where

are odd, coprime integers and
\frac{c}{d}=[2a_{0};2a_{1},...,2a_{n-1},a_{n}]
with

1.16

The first part of Theorem
1.1
can be proved by elementary means using the reciprocity formula (

). As there is at the moment no reciprocity formula for

, an elementary proof of the second part of Theorem

Instead, we shall use a different approach which starts with the well-known formula

\log\eta(A.z)-\log\eta(z)=\frac{\pi i}{12}\int_{z}^{A.z}E_{2}(w)\;dw,\;A\in\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbf{Z}),

where

is the Eisenstein series of weight

under

Since the methods we will use to prove our results are similar for

\log\theta_{4}

2.
Continued Fractions for Subgroups of the Modular Group

In this section, we prove the facts about continued fractions that we will use later in our proofs.

A continued fraction is defined to be

(2.1)

[a_{0};a_{1},...,a_{n}]=a_{0}+\frac{1}{a_{1}+\frac{1}{a_{2}+\frac{1}{a_{3}+\frac{1}{\cdots+\frac{1}{a_{n}}}}}}

with
n\geq 0
,
a_{0}\in\mathbf{Z}
and
a_{1},...,a_{n}
a sequence of non-zero integers. The integers
a_{1},...,a_{n}
are called the partial quotients of the continued fraction. Contrary to convention, we do
not
require
a_{k}\geq 1
for
k\geq 1
. Even though it is likely that the continued fractions we consider have already been studied before, the literature on continued fractions is too vast to tell whether this is indeed the case. We thus include all the details of the proof for the convenience of the reader. See
[
6
]
,
[
10
]
for some references on classical continued fractions.

Let
\frac{a}{c}
be a rational number with
(a,c)=1
and let
b,d\in\mathbf{Z}
be such that
ad-bc=1
. Continued fraction expansions (c.f.e.) of rational numbers are given by the representation of the matrix
{\tiny\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{pmatrix}}
in the modular group
\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbf{Z})
in terms of its generators
T={\tiny\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}}
and
S={\tiny\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}}
or
T
and
V=TST={\tiny\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
1&1\end{pmatrix}}
.

Namely, let us write
{\tiny\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{pmatrix}}=T^{a_{0}}V^{a_{1}}T^{a_{2}}\cdots V^{a_{n}}\in\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbf{Z})
with
a_{i}\in\mathbf{Z}
for
i=0,...,n
. We can then read off the c.f.e. of
\frac{a}{c}
by acting on the cusp
\infty
by Möbius transformations:

\frac{a}{c}={\tiny\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{pmatrix}}.\infty=T^{a_{0}}V^{a_{1}}T^{a_{2}}\cdots V^{a_{n}}.\infty=a_{0}+\frac{1}{a_{1}+\frac{1}{a_{2}+\frac{1}{...+\frac{1}{a_{n}}}}}=[a_{0};a_{1},...,a_{n}].

Note that since
T.\infty=\infty
, we may write
{\tiny\begin{pmatrix}a&*\\
c&*\end{pmatrix}}
for the matrix in (
2.2
), i.e. the c.f.e. of
\frac{a}{c}
is independent of multiplication of
{\tiny\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{pmatrix}}
by
T
on the right.

We hence showed that every matrix representation in
\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbf{Z})
by the generators
T,V
gives rise to a continued fraction
[a_{0};a_{1},...,a_{n}]
with
n\geq 0
odd and vice versa.
2
2

2

In fact, any continued fraction of the form
\frac{a}{c}=[a_{0};a_{1},...,a_{2n^{\prime}}]
can be written as
[a_{0};a_{1},...,a_{2n^{\prime}}-1,1]
if
a_{2n^{\prime}}\neq 1
or as
[a_{0};a_{1},...,a_{2n^{\prime}-1}+1]
if
a_{2n^{\prime}}=1
, so
any
c.f.e. gives a representation of
{\tiny\begin{pmatrix}a&*\\
c&*\end{pmatrix}}
in terms of
T,V
, which ends with a power of
V
(independently of the parity of its length).

We may argue similarly for the representation
{\tiny\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{pmatrix}}=\pm T^{c_{0}}ST^{c_{1}}\cdots T^{c_{m}}S\in\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbf{Z})
with
c_{0},c_{1},...,c_{m}\in\mathbf{Z}
in terms of the generators
T
and
S
. The negative continued fraction of
\frac{a}{c}
can again be obtained by acting on the cusp
\infty
:

(2.2)

\frac{a}{c}={\tiny\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{pmatrix}}.\infty=\pm T^{c_{0}}ST^{c_{1}}\cdots T^{c_{m}}S.\infty=c_{0}-\frac{1}{c_{1}-\frac{1}{c_{2}-\frac{1}{...-\frac{1}{c_{m}}}}}=\llbracket c_{0};c_{1},...,c_{m}\rrbracket.

Both the representation of a matrix in the modular group in terms of its generators and the c.f.e. of a rational number arise from Euclidean division. The algorithm to find the coefficients of a (positive) c.f.e. goes as follows: Let
(d,c)=1
(to not confuse the resulting rationals in the iterations of the algorithm with the partial quotients, we shall write
\frac{d}{c}
for the rational number instead of
\frac{a}{c}
now). Pick
a_{0}\in\mathbf{Z}
so that
\frac{d_{0}}{c_{0}}=\frac{d}{c}-a_{0}\in[0,1)
with
(d_{0},c_{0})=1
. If
d_{0}=0
, we are done. Otherwise, we have
\frac{c_{0}}{d_{0}}>1
. Pick
a_{1}\geq 1
an integer such that
\frac{d_{1}}{c_{1}}=\frac{d_{0}}{c_{0}}-a_{1}\in[0,1)
. Continue this process until one of the fractions
\frac{d_{k}}{c_{k}}
is equal to zero. This algorithm can also be used to find fast rational approximations of irrational numbers.

The rationals
\frac{d_{i}}{c_{i}}
in the algorithm are positive and the partial quotients
a_{i}
are the integer parts of
\frac{c_{i}}{d_{i}}
. As such the partial quotients are positive integers. We will see shortly that this will no longer be the case in the algorithms in sections
2.1
and
2.2
. This stems from the fact that the subgroups
\Gamma_{\theta}
and
\Gamma^{0}(2)
defined in (
1.9
) are generated by
T^{2}
and
S
resp.
T^{2}
and
V
, which will force some or all of the partial quotients to be even integers. The iterations
\frac{d_{i}}{c_{i}}
cannot be taken to be positive then, but only in
(-1,1)
. Thus, we need to allow negative partial quotients.

However, the c.f.e. of
\Gamma_{\theta}
- and
\Gamma^{0}(2)
-type approximate irrational numbers as their
\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbf{Z})
-counterparts do.

2.1.
\Gamma_{\theta}
-type c.f.e.

Let
c_{1},c_{2},...
be a sequence of non-zero integers and
c_{0}\in\mathbf{Z}
. We denote the
k
-th partial fraction of the sequence by
\frac{p_{k}}{q_{k}}=\llbracket 2c_{0};2c_{1},...,2c_{k}\rrbracket
with
(p_{k},q_{k})=1
. As in (
2.2
),
p_{k}
and
q_{k}
may be viewed as the entries of a matrix by

\frac{p_{k}}{q_{k}}=(T^{2c_{0}}ST^{2c_{1}}ST^{2c_{2}}S\cdots T^{2c_{k}}S).\infty={\tiny\begin{pmatrix}p_{k}&*\\
q_{k}&*\end{pmatrix}}.\infty.

Since
\Gamma_{\theta}
is generated by
T^{2}
and
S
, the matrix
{\tiny\begin{pmatrix}p_{k}&*\\
q_{k}&*\end{pmatrix}}=T^{2c_{0}}ST^{2c_{1}}ST^{2c_{2}}S\cdots T^{2c_{k}}S
lies in
\Gamma_{\theta}
. For this reason we shall call a c.f.e. of the form
\llbracket 2c_{0};2c_{1},...,2c_{k}\rrbracket
a
\Gamma_{\theta}
-type c.f.e. For the c.f.e. of
\Gamma_{\theta}
-type, we shall usually write
\llbracket 2c_{1},2c_{2},...,2c_{n}\rrbracket=\llbracket 0;2c_{1},2c_{2},...,2c_{n}\rrbracket
in the case
c_{0}=0
, dropping the semicolon.

To find the other two entries of
{\tiny\begin{pmatrix}p_{k}&*\\
q_{k}&*\end{pmatrix}}
, we let the matrix act on the point
0
:

{\tiny\begin{pmatrix}p_{k}&*\\
q_{k}&*\end{pmatrix}}.0=(T^{2c_{0}}ST^{2c_{1}}S\cdots T^{2c_{k-1}}ST^{2c_{k}}S).0=(ST^{2c_{1}}S\cdots T^{2c_{k-1}}S).\infty=\frac{p_{k-1}}{q_{k-1}}.

Hence,
{\tiny\begin{pmatrix}p_{k}&p_{k-1}\\
q_{k}&q_{k-1}\end{pmatrix}}=T^{2c_{0}}ST^{2c_{1}}S\cdots T^{2c_{k}}S\in\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbf{Z})
, which implies
p_{k}q_{k-1}-p_{k-1}q_{k}=1
.

The partial fractions
\frac{p_{k}}{q_{k}}
will be useful in finding a sequence of partial quotients
c_{i}
of a
\Gamma_{\theta}
-type c.f.e. leading to an approximation of real numbers in the same way as the classical continued fractions. We start by proving two lemmas, relating the resulting sequence of partial fractions to the sequence of partial quotients. These are analogs for the classical results for the
\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbf{Z})
-type continued fractions.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
c_{1},c_{2},...
be a sequence of non-zero integers,
k\geq 1
, and
\frac{p_{k}}{q_{k}}=\llbracket 2c_{1},...,2c_{k}\rrbracket
be the
k
-th partial fraction. The partial fractions
\frac{p_{k}}{q_{k}}
satisfy
p_{k}=2c_{k}p_{k-1}-p_{k-2}
and
q_{k}=2c_{k}q_{k-1}-q_{k-2}
for all
k\geq 3
.

Proof.

By induction. The first three partial fractions are
\frac{p_{1}}{q_{1}}=\frac{-1}{2c_{1}}
,
\frac{p_{2}}{q_{2}}=\frac{-2c_{2}}{4c_{1}c_{2}-1}
, and
\frac{p_{3}}{q_{3}}=\frac{1-4c_{2}c_{3}}{8c_{1}c_{2}c_{3}-2c_{1}-2c_{3}}
. For
k=3
, we directly verify the claim.

Suppose the claim for
k-1\geq 3
. Let
\frac{p_{k}^{\prime}}{q_{k}^{\prime}}=\llbracket 2c_{2},2c_{3},...,2c_{k}\rrbracket
. We observe that
\frac{p_{k}}{q_{k}}=-\frac{1}{2c_{1}-\frac{1}{p_{k}^{\prime}/q_{k}^{\prime}}}=\frac{-p_{k}^{\prime}}{2c_{1}p_{k}^{\prime}-q_{k}^{\prime}}
and hence
p_{k}=-p_{k}^{\prime}
and
q_{k}=2c_{1}p_{k}^{\prime}-q_{k}^{\prime}
. By the induction hypothesis, we see that

\frac{p_{k}}{q_{k}}=-\frac{1}{2c_{1}-\frac{q_{k}^{\prime}}{p_{k}^{\prime}}}=-\frac{1}{2c_{1}-\frac{2c_{k}q_{k-1}^{\prime}-q_{k-2}^{\prime}}{2c_{k}p_{k-1}^{\prime}-p_{k-2}}}=\frac{p_{k-2}^{\prime}-2c_{k}p_{k-1}^{\prime}}{2c_{1}c_{k}p_{k-1}^{\prime}-2c_{1}p_{k-2}^{\prime}-2c_{k}q_{k-1}^{\prime}+q_{k-2}^{\prime}}.

Hence

\displaystyle p_{k}=p_{k-2}^{\prime}-2c_{k}p_{k-1}^{\prime}=2c_{k}p_{k-1}-p_{k-2}\;\mathrm{and}

\displaystyle q_{k}=2c_{k}(2c_{1}p_{k-1}^{\prime}-q_{k-1}^{\prime})-(2c_{1}p_{k-2}^{\prime}-q_{k-2}^{\prime})=2c_{k}q_{k-1}-q_{k-2}.

∎

With Lemma
2.1
it is then easy to show the following useful fact about the
k
-th partial fractions.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
c_{1},c_{2},...
be any sequence of non-zero integers,
k\geq 1
, and
\frac{p_{k}}{q_{k}}=\llbracket 2c_{1},...,2c_{k}\rrbracket
be the
k
-th partial fraction. Then
|p_{k-1}|<|p_{k}|
and
|q_{k-1}|<|q_{k}|
for all
k\geq 1
. In particular,
|p_{k}|
,
|q_{k}|\to+\infty
as
k\to+\infty
.

Proof.

By induction. The proof is essentially the same for
p_{k}
and
q_{k}
, so we will only present the one for
q_{k}
. We have
q_{1}=2c_{1}
and
q_{2}=4c_{1}c_{2}-1
. Suppose
|q_{1}|\geq|q_{2}|
. Then we would have

(2.3)

2|c_{1}|=|q_{1}|\geq|q_{2}|\geq 4|c_{1}|-1,

but there is no non-zero integer
c_{1}
satisfying (
2.3
).

Assume the statement for
k-1\geq 2
, i.e.
|q_{k-1}|>|q_{k-2}|
. Then by Lemma
2.1
, we have

|q_{k}|\geq 2|c_{k}||q_{k-1}|-|q_{k-2}|>|q_{k-1}|,

as
|c_{k}|\geq 1
.
∎

With Lemma
2.2
we are set to prove the approximation of an irrational number by a
\Gamma_{\theta}
-type c.f.e. and its proof also gives the algorithm by which such an approximation can be computed.

Proposition 2.3
.

Let
x\in(-1,1)
be irrational. There is a sequence
c_{1},c_{2},...
of non-zero integers such that
\llbracket 2c_{1},2c_{2},...,2c_{k}\rrbracket\to x
as
k\to+\infty
.

Proof.

We construct the sequence
c_{1},c_{2},...
as follows. Since
|x|<1
, we have
\frac{1}{|x|}>1
. Pick
c_{1}\in\mathbf{Z}
non-zero such that
x_{1}=2c_{1}-\frac{1}{x}\in(-1,1)
or, equivalently,
x=\frac{1}{2c_{1}-x_{1}}=\llbracket 2c_{1},x_{1}^{-1}\rrbracket
. Then pick
c_{2}\in\mathbf{Z}
non-zero such that
x_{2}=2c_{2}-\frac{1}{x_{1}}\in(-1,1)
, which again is possible as
\frac{1}{|x_{1}|}>1
. Equivalently, we may also write
x=\llbracket 2c_{1},2c_{2},x_{2}^{-1}\rrbracket
. Continue this process to get a (unique) finite sequence
c_{1},...,c_{k}\in\mathbf{Z}-\{0\}
and
x_{1},...,x_{k}\in(-1,1)
with
x=\frac{1}{2c_{1}-\frac{1}{2c_{2}-\frac{1}{\cdots-x_{k}}}}=\llbracket 2c_{1},...,2c_{k},x_{k}^{-1}\rrbracket
.

Now observe that

\displaystyle|\llbracket 2c_{1},...,2c_{k},x_{k}^{-1}\rrbracket-\llbracket 2c_{1},...,2c_{k}\rrbracket|

\displaystyle=\left|\frac{\frac{1}{x_{k}}p_{k}-p_{k-1}}{\frac{1}{x_{k}}q_{k}-q_{k-1}}-\frac{p_{k}}{q_{k}}\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\frac{p_{k}q_{k-1}-p_{k-1}q_{k}}{q_{k}(\frac{1}{x_{k}}q_{k}-q_{k-1})}\right|

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|q_{k}||\frac{1}{x_{k}}q_{k}-q_{k-1}|}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{|q_{k}|}\to 0,\;\mathrm{as}\;k\to+\infty,

where we used Lemma
2.2
and that
{\tiny\begin{pmatrix}p_{k}&p_{k-1}\\
q_{k}&q_{k-1}\end{pmatrix}}
is a unimodular matrix.
∎

Note that Proposition
2.3
gives a
sequence
of integers, which approximates
x
arbitrarily close. If we were given a
\varepsilon>0
and just wanted to find a c.f.e.
\llbracket 2c_{1},...,2c_{n}\rrbracket
in
(x-\varepsilon,x+\varepsilon)
, we could have picked a rational
\frac{a}{c}\in(x-\varepsilon,x+\varepsilon)
with
c>0
,
(a,c)=1
and
a+c
odd. Finding
b,d\in\mathbf{Z}
such that
ad-bc=1
yields a matrix
A={\tiny\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{pmatrix}}\in\Gamma_{\theta}
. Writing the matrix
A
in terms of the generators
T^{2},S
gives
\llbracket 2c_{1},...,2c_{n}\rrbracket
. The downside of this argument is that we would still not know how to construct a c.f.e. with the desired properties, while the proof of Proposition
2.3
gives an explicit algorithm. Moreover, the algorithm shows that c.f.e. of
\Gamma_{\theta}
-type are unique (this can be proved rigorously through induction on the length of a c.f.e.).

2.2.
\Gamma^{0}(2)
-type c.f.e.

We now turn from the negative continued fractions to the positive continued fraction as in (
2.1
). Again, if
a,c\in\mathbf{Z}
with
(a,c)=1
and
a
an odd integer, we may find
d,b\in\mathbf{Z}
such that
ad-2bc=1
and
{\tiny\begin{pmatrix}a&2b\\
c&d\end{pmatrix}}\in\Gamma^{0}(2)
. Writing
{\tiny\begin{pmatrix}a&2b\\
c&d\end{pmatrix}}=T^{2a_{0}}V^{a_{1}}\cdots T^{2a_{n-1}}V^{a_{n}}
in terms of the generators
T^{2}
and
V
of
\Gamma^{0}(2)
gives rise to a c.f.e.:

\frac{a}{c}={\tiny\begin{pmatrix}a&2b\\
c&d\end{pmatrix}}.\infty=T^{2a_{0}}V^{a_{1}}\cdots T^{2a_{n-1}}V^{a_{n}}.\infty=[2a_{0};a_{1},...,2a_{n-1},a_{n}].

We call a c.f.e. of this kind a
\Gamma^{0}(2)
-type c.f.e.

In the remainder of this section, we present an algorithm for finding the
\Gamma^{0}(2)
-type c.f.e. of a rational number. First, we prove a technical lemma, relating the length of the continued fraction to the parity of the numerator of the rational it is representing. This will make the description of the algorithm cleaner.

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
a_{0}\in\mathbf{Z}
and
a_{1},...,a_{n}
be non-zero integers such that
a_{2k}
is even for all integers
k=0,1,...,\left\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\right\rfloor
. Pick
c>0
and
d\in\mathbf{Z}
with
(c,d)=1
such that
\frac{d}{c}=[a_{0};a_{1},...,a_{n}]
. Then
d\equiv n\pmod{2}
.

Proof.

By induction. The case
n=0
is seen from
a_{0}\equiv 0\pmod{2}
and for
n=1
, we have
\frac{d}{c}=a_{0}-\frac{1}{a_{1}}=\frac{a_{0}a_{1}-1}{a_{1}}
, and it is hence clear that
d
is odd.

Suppose the claim holds for
n-2\geq 0
and write
\frac{d^{\prime}}{c^{\prime}}=[a_{2},...,a_{n}]
. Then

[a_{0};a_{1},...,a_{n}]=a_{0}+\frac{1}{a_{1}+\frac{c^{\prime}}{d^{\prime}}}=\frac{a_{0}(a_{1}+c^{\prime})+d^{\prime}}{a_{1}+c^{\prime}}=\frac{d}{c},

hence, as
a_{0}
is even,
d\equiv d^{\prime}\pmod{2}
.
∎

Proposition 2.5
.

Let
c>0
and
d
an odd integer such that
(c,d)=1
. There exists a c.f.e.
\frac{d}{c}=[2a_{0};a_{1},2a_{2},...,a_{n}]
of odd length
n\geq 1
with
a_{0}\in\mathbf{Z}
,
a_{1},...,a_{n}\in\mathbf{Z}-\{0\}
and
|a_{1}|
,
|a_{3}|
,
...
,
|a_{n-2}|>1
.

Proof.

The algorithm is similar to the one given in the proof of Proposition
2.3
. First, pick
a_{0}\in\mathbf{Z}
such that
x_{0}=\frac{d}{c}-2a_{0}\in[-1,1]
. It is not possible that
x_{0}=0
, as then
\frac{d}{c}=[2a_{0}]
would be such that
d\equiv 0\pmod{2}
, which we excluded.

Pick
a_{1}\notin\{-1,0,1\}
such that
x_{1}=\frac{1}{x_{0}}-a_{1}\in(-1,1)
. If
x_{1}=0
, we are done. If no such
a_{1}
exists, then
x_{0}=\pm 1
. Thus, we may pick
a_{1}=x_{0}
and we are done.

Continue this process to obtain
x_{2},x_{3},...
with
x_{2n}\in[-1,1]
and
x_{2n+1}\in(-1,1)
until
x_{2n+1}=0
for an
n\geq 0
. Note that it is not possible that
x_{2n}=0
, since by Lemma
2.4
this contradicts the assumption that
d
is odd.

That the process stops can be seen in the following way. Write
x_{1}=\frac{d_{1}}{c_{1}}
with
c_{1}>0
and
-c_{1}<d_{1}<c_{1}
coprime to
c_{1}
. Then
x_{2}=\frac{c_{1}-2a_{2}d_{1}}{d_{1}}=\frac{d_{2}}{c_{2}}
with
c_{2}>0
and
-c_{2}\leq d_{2}\leq c_{2}
coprime to
c_{2}
. Since
c_{2}
divides
d_{1}
and
|d_{1}|<c_{1}
, we have
c_{2}<c_{1}
. In fact, the value of the denominator of
x_{1},x_{2},...
strictly decreases with each step. Hence, the process must come to an end.
∎

Contrary to the
\Gamma_{\theta}
-type c.f.e., the continued fraction in Proposition
2.5
is
not
unique. Take, say,
\frac{1}{x_{0}}=\frac{7}{3}
in the proof of Proposition
2.5
. We may either pick
a_{1}=2
or
a_{1}=3
to get
x_{1}=\frac{1}{x_{0}}-a_{1}\in(-1,1)
. Indeed, the continued fractions
\frac{3}{7}=[0;2,2,1]=[0;3,-2,2]
both are of the form as claimed in Proposition
2.5
.

Uniqueness can be recovered if we require that the partial quotients
a_{1}
,
2a_{2}
,
...
,
a_{n-2}
,
2a_{n-1}
are even. In that case,
\frac{d}{c}=[2a_{0};a_{1},2a_{2},a_{3},...,2a_{n-1},a_{n}]
the denominator
c>0
will then have the same parity as
a_{n}
. In particular, if
c
is even, the
\Gamma^{0}(2)
-type c.f.e. of
\frac{d}{c}
can be transformed into a
\Gamma_{\theta}
-type c.f.e., namely

[2a_{0};a_{1},2a_{2},...,2a_{n-1},a_{n}]=\llbracket 2a_{0};-a_{1},2a_{2},...,-2a_{n-1},a_{n}\rrbracket.

Proposition
2.3
is hence also applicable for continued fractions of
\Gamma^{0}(2)
-type.

3.
Hardy Sums and (Quasi-)Modular Forms

In this section, we show the analog of (
1.17
) for
\log\theta
and
\log\theta_{4}
and prove Theorem
1.1
.

3.1.
Representation of Dedekind Sums as Sums of Partial Quotients.

Let

(3.1)

E_{2}(z)=1-24\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\sigma_{1}(n)e(nz)

be the classical Eisenstein series of weight
2
, where
\sigma_{1}(n)=\sum_{d|n}d
. It is well-known that
E_{2}
transforms under the action of the modular group by

(3.2)

E_{2}(z)|_{2}{\tiny\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{pmatrix}}=(cz+d)^{-2}E_{2}\left(\frac{az+b}{cz+d}\right)=E_{2}(z)+\frac{6}{\pi i}\frac{c}{cz+d},\;{\tiny\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{pmatrix}}\in\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbf{Z}).

As mentioned in the introduction,
\log\eta(A.z)-\log\eta(z)
for
A\in\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbf{Z})
is up to a constant equal to the cycle integral of
E_{2}
; see (
1.17
). This follows from the classical fact that the logarithmic derivative of
\eta(z)
is equal to
\frac{\pi i}{12}E_{2}(z)
and that
\mathrm{H}^{1}(\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbf{Z}),\mathbf{C})=\{0\}
. More precisely, define

(3.3)

\nu(A)=\log\eta(A.z)-\log\eta(z)-\frac{\pi i}{12}\int_{z}^{A.z}E_{2}(w)dw,\;A\in\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbf{Z}),

which does not depend on the choice of
z
. For
A,B\in\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbf{Z})
, it is easy to see that
\nu(AB)=\nu(A)+\nu(B)
.

Hence,
\nu:\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbf{Z})\to(\mathbf{C},+)
is a group-homomorphism. But since the generators
S
and
TS
of
\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbf{Z})
are torsion-elements, there are no non-zero group-homomorphisms
\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbf{Z})\to(\mathbf{C},+)
. This proves that
\nu=0
, i.e. the formula (
1.17
).

Equation (
1.17
) can be used together with the transformation (
1.4
) for
\log\eta(z)
to prove Hickerson’s formula (
1.3
) for the classical Dedekind sums by splitting up the integral of
\displaystyle\int_{z}^{A.z}E_{2}(w)\;dw
into paths from
z_{i}
to
Tz_{i}
or
Vz_{i}
.

In Hickerson’s formula (
1.3
) we crucially use that the partial quotients of the c.f.e. are positive. But as we will later work with c.f.e. of
\Gamma_{\theta}
- resp.
\Gamma^{0}(2)
-type, we shall not require the partial quotients to be positive. In the following proposition, we express the cycle integral
\displaystyle\int_{z}^{A.z}E_{2}(w)\;dw
more generally in terms of a word in
T
and
V
. The proposition is well-known (see
[
7
,
15
]
). In Appendix
A
, we give a proof based on an argument we learned from an unpublished note of W. Duke which does not use the reciprocity law.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
n\geq 1
be an odd integer and
a_{0},...,a_{n}
be integers. Let
A_{k}={\tiny\begin{pmatrix}a_{k}&(-1)^{k+1}\\
(-1)^{k}&0\end{pmatrix}}
for
k=0,...,n
and write
{\tiny\begin{pmatrix}*&*\\
c_{k}&d_{k}\end{pmatrix}}=A_{0}A_{1}\cdots A_{k}
. For
A={\tiny\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{pmatrix}}=A_{0}A_{1}\cdots A_{n}
, we have

(3.4)

\displaystyle\int_{z}^{A.z}E_{2}(w)dw=\frac{6}{\pi i}\log\left(\frac{cz+d}{\mathrm{sign}(c)i}\right)+\sum_{k=0}^{n}(-1)^{k}a_{k}+3\sum_{k=1}^{n}\mathrm{sign}(c_{k}d_{k}).

Note that every matrix
A
of the form
A=T^{a_{0}}V^{a_{1}}\cdots T^{a_{n-1}}V^{a_{n}}
may be written as

A=T^{a_{0}}SS^{-1}V^{a_{1}}T^{a_{2}}\cdots T^{a_{n-1}}SS^{-1}V^{a_{n}}={\tiny\begin{pmatrix}a_{0}&-1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}}{\tiny\begin{pmatrix}a_{1}&1\\
-1&0\end{pmatrix}}\cdots{\tiny\begin{pmatrix}a_{n-1}&-1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}}{\tiny\begin{pmatrix}a_{n}&1\\
-1&0\end{pmatrix}},

i.e. in the form of Proposition
3.1
. If the integers
a_{1},...,a_{n}
are all positive, we have
c_{k}>0
and
\mathrm{sign}(d_{k})=(-1)^{k+1}
for
k=1,...,n
in the notation of Proposition
3.1
. Thus,
3\sum_{k=1}^{n}\mathrm{sign}(c_{k}d_{k})=3
. Hence, if we assume that all partial quotients of the c.f.e. of
\frac{a}{c}
are positive, we get

\int_{z}^{A.z}E_{2}(w)dw=\frac{6}{\pi i}\log\left(\frac{cz+d}{i}\right)+\sum_{k=0}^{n}(-1)^{k}a_{k}+3.

Comparing this equation to (
1.4
) gives Hickerson’s formula (
1.3
).

If the integers
a_{1},...,a_{n}
in Proposition
3.1
are sometimes negative (as they might be if they were partial quotients of a
\Gamma_{\theta}
- resp.
\Gamma^{0}(2)
-type c.f.e.), the expression
3\sum_{k=1}^{n}\mathrm{sign}(c_{k}d_{k})
in (
3.4
) does not necessarily evaluate to
3
anymore. The next proposition is a variant of Proposition
3.1
, which expresses the right hand side of (
3.4
) only in terms of the integers
a_{1},...,a_{n}
– given some additional conditions on
a_{1},...,a_{n}
, but not requiring them to be positive. In particular, these additional conditions are satisfied in the case where
a_{1},...,a_{n}
are partial quotients of a
\Gamma_{\theta}
- resp.
\Gamma^{0}(2)
-type c.f.e.

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
n\geq 1
be an odd integer and
a_{0},...,a_{n}
be integers such that
|a_{2}|,...,|a_{n}|
are at least
2
and
a_{1}\neq 0
. Let
A_{k}={\tiny\begin{pmatrix}a_{k}&(-1)^{k+1}\\
(-1)^{k}&0\end{pmatrix}}
for
k=0,...,n
and write
{\tiny\begin{pmatrix}*&*\\
c_{k}&d_{k}\end{pmatrix}}=A_{0}A_{1}\cdots A_{k}
. For
A={\tiny\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{pmatrix}}=A_{0}A_{1}\cdots A_{n}
, we have

\displaystyle\int_{z}^{A.z}E_{2}(w)dw=\frac{6}{\pi i}\log\left(\frac{cz+d}{\mathrm{sign}(c)i}\right)+\sum_{k=0}^{n}(-1)^{k}a_{k}-3\sum_{k=1}^{n}(-1)^{k}\mathrm{sign}(a_{k}).

Proof.

We show by induction that
0<|d_{k}|\leq|c_{k}|
,
\mathrm{sign}(c_{k})=\mathrm{sign}(a_{1}\cdots a_{k})
and
\mathrm{sign}(d_{k})=(-1)^{k+1}\mathrm{sign}(a_{1}\cdots a_{k-1})
for all
k=1,...,n
, which together with Proposition
3.1
suffices to prove the claim.

Suppose
n=1
. Then
A_{0}A_{1}={\tiny\begin{pmatrix}*&*\\
a_{1}&1\end{pmatrix}}
, and we see that
|d_{1}|=1\leq|a_{1}|=|c_{1}|
,
\mathrm{sign}(c_{1})=\mathrm{sign}(a_{1})
and
\mathrm{sign}(d_{1})=1
.

Suppose the claim holds for
k-1<n
. Then

{\tiny\begin{pmatrix}*&*\\
c_{k-1}&d_{k-1}\end{pmatrix}}{\tiny\begin{pmatrix}a_{k}&(-1)^{k+1}\\
(-1)^{k}&0\end{pmatrix}}={\tiny\begin{pmatrix}*&*\\
c_{k-1}a_{k}+(-1)^{k}d_{k}&(-1)^{k+1}c_{k-1}\end{pmatrix}}={\tiny\begin{pmatrix}*&*\\
c_{k}&d_{k}\end{pmatrix}},

which shows that
\mathrm{sign}(d_{k})=(-1)^{k+1}\mathrm{sign}(c_{k-1})=(-1)^{k+1}\mathrm{sign}(a_{1}\cdots a_{k-1})
. Also, observing that
c_{k}=\mathrm{sign}(a_{k})\mathrm{sign}(c_{k-1})(|a_{k}c_{k-1}|\pm d_{k-1})
gives that
\mathrm{sign}(c_{k})=\mathrm{sign}(a_{1}\cdots a_{k})
, since

|a_{k}c_{k-1}|\pm d_{k-1}\geq 2|c_{k-1}|-|d_{k-1}|\geq|c_{k-1}|=|d_{k}|>0.

∎

3.2.
Representation of Hardy Sums as Sums of Partial Quotients.

The argument in section
3.1
for
\log\eta(z)
applies to
\log\theta(z)
and
\log\theta_{4}(z)
as well. Let
L(z)=2E_{2}(z)-E_{2}\left(\frac{z}{2}\right)
, which is a modular form of weight
2
for
\Gamma^{0}(2)
. It is easy to see that it is invariant under the
|_{2}
-operator for
\Gamma^{0}(2)
using
{\tiny\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
2c&d\end{pmatrix}}.\frac{z}{2}=\frac{1}{2}{\tiny\begin{pmatrix}a&2b\\
c&d\end{pmatrix}}.z
. The modular form
L(z)
is the only modular form of weight
2
for
\Gamma^{0}(2)
up to scalar multiplication. Its Fourier-expansion is

(3.5)

L(z)=1+24\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\sigma_{1}^{\mathrm{odd}}(n)e^{\pi inz},

where
\sigma_{1}^{\mathrm{odd}}(n)=\sum_{d|n,\;d\;\mathrm{odd}}d
.

The space of modular forms of weight
2
for
\Gamma_{\theta}
is also one-dimensional and its basis element is given by

R(z)=L(z+1)=1+24\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}(-1)^{n}\sigma_{1}^{\mathrm{odd}}(n)e^{\pi inz}.

It follows from the Jacobi Triple Product Identity that

(3.6)

\theta(z)=e\left(\frac{1}{24}\int_{\infty}^{z}(E_{2}(w)-R(w))dw\right)\;\mathrm{and}\;\theta_{4}(z)=e\left(\frac{1}{24}\int_{\infty}^{z}(E_{2}(w)-L(w))dw\right).

Note that due to the branch cut of the complex logarithm we cannot simply take the logarithm on both sides of (
3.6
) to write the logarithm of
\theta
-functions as integrals over quasi-modular forms. However, we can take the logarithmic derivative. For example, the logarithmic derivative
\frac{\theta_{4}^{\prime}(z)}{\theta_{4}(z)}
is equal to
\frac{\pi i}{12}\left(E_{2}(z)-L(z)\right)
.

4.
Density of Hardy Sums

In this section, we prove Meyer’s Theorem
1.2
that the sets
\{(d/c,\;S(d,c)):c+d\;\mathrm{odd},c>0,\;(d,c)=1\}
and
\{(d/c,\;S_{4}(d,c)):c>0,\;d\;\mathrm{odd},\;(d,c)=1\}
are dense in
\mathbf{R}\times\mathbf{Z}
, as well as the new density Theorem
1.4
.

The arguments to prove the claim are quite short, but differ between
S(d,c)
and
S_{4}(d,c)
. We will present them in separate subsections.

4.1.
Proof of the Density of the Hardy Sums
S(d,c)

Let
(x,m)\in\mathbf{R}\times\mathbf{Z}
and
\varepsilon>0
. We need to show that there are coprime integers
d,c
with
c>0
and
d+c
odd such that

\left|x-\frac{d}{c}\right|<\varepsilon\;\mathrm{and}\;S(d,c)=m.

Since
S(d+2c,c)=S(d,c)
, we may assume w.l.o.g. that
x\in(-1,1)
.

By Proposition
2.3
, we may find non-zero integers
c_{1},c_{2},...,c_{n}
such that
\frac{p_{n}}{q_{n}}=\llbracket 2c_{1},...,2c_{n}\rrbracket
lies in
(x-\frac{\varepsilon}{2},x+\frac{\varepsilon}{2})
. Pick
M\in\mathbf{Z}
such that
M+m=S(p_{n},q_{n})
. If
M=0
, there is nothing more to show. Thus assume that
M\neq 0
.

Now construct the following rational in
(x-\varepsilon,x+\varepsilon)
: Pick
c_{n+1}\geq 1
big enough such that

\left|\llbracket 2c_{1},...,2c_{n},\mathrm{sign}(M)2c_{n+1}\rrbracket-\frac{p_{n}}{q_{n}}\right|\leq\frac{1}{|q_{n}|(2|c_{n+1}||q_{n}|-|q_{n-1}|)}<\frac{\varepsilon}{2|M|},

where we made use of Lemma
2.2
. Denote
\llbracket 2c_{1},...,2c_{n},\mathrm{sign}(M)2c_{n+1},\rrbracket
by
\frac{p_{n+1}}{q_{n+1}}
. Then pick
c_{n+2}\geq 1
such that

\left|\llbracket 2c_{1},...,2c_{n},\mathrm{sign}(M)2c_{n+1},\mathrm{sign}(M)2c_{n+2}\rrbracket-\frac{p_{n+1}}{q_{n+1}}\right|<\frac{\varepsilon}{2|M|},\;\mathrm{etc.}

Continuing this process
|M|
-times, we get a rational

\frac{p_{n+|M|}}{q_{n+|M|}}=\llbracket 2c_{1},...,2c_{n},2c_{n+1}^{\prime},...,2c_{n+|M|}^{\prime}\rrbracket,

where
c_{k}^{\prime}=\mathrm{sign}(M)c_{k}
for
k=n+1,...,n+|M|
. In particular,
\mathrm{sign}(c_{k}^{\prime})=\mathrm{sign}(M)
.

The rational
\frac{p_{n+|M|}}{q_{n+|M|}}
lies in
(x-\varepsilon,x+\varepsilon)
, as

\left|x-\frac{p_{n+|M|}}{q_{n+|M|}}\right|\leq\left|x-\frac{p_{n}}{q_{n}}\right|+\sum_{k=1}^{|M|}\left|\frac{p_{n+k-1}}{q_{n+k-1}}-\frac{p_{n+k}}{q_{n+k}}\right|<\varepsilon

by construction. Moreover, it follows from Theorem
1.1
that

\displaystyle S(p_{n+|M|},q_{n+|M|})

\displaystyle=-\sum_{k=1}^{n}\mathrm{sign}(c_{k})-\sum_{l=1}^{|M|}\mathrm{sign}(c_{n+l}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=m+M-\mathrm{sign}(M)|M|

\displaystyle=m.

4.2.
Proof of the Density of the Hardy Sums
S_{4}(d,c)
and of
S_{4}(d,c)+S(d,c)

To prove Theorem
1.4
, we will prove a stronger result than
\{(d/c,S(d,c)+S_{4}(d,c)):d\;\mathrm{odd},\;c>0\;\mathrm{even}\}
being dense in
\mathbf{R}\times 2\mathbf{Z}
, namely that

\{(d/c,\;S(d,c)+S_{4}(d,c),\;S_{4}(d,c)):d\;\mathrm{odd},\;c>0\;\mathrm{even}\}\subset\mathbf{R}\times 2\mathbf{Z}\times(2\mathbf{Z}+1)

is a dense subset.

For
S(d,c)+S_{4}(d,c)
to be well-defined, we need
\frac{d}{c}
with
c>0
and
(d,c)=1
to be such that
{\tiny\begin{pmatrix}*&*\\
c&d\end{pmatrix}}\in\Gamma_{\theta}\cap\Gamma^{0}(2)
. In other words,

\frac{d}{c}=\llbracket 2c_{0};2c_{1},...,2c_{n}\rrbracket=[2c_{0};-2c_{1},2c_{2},-2c_{3},...,-2c_{n}]

should be such that
d
is odd and
c+d

2.4
, this can only happen if

In this case, the Hardy sums are

\displaystyle S(d,c)=-\sum_{k=1}^{n}\mathrm{sign}(c_{k})\;\mathrm{and}\;

Lemma 4.1
.

Let

\frac{d}{c}=\llbracket 2c_{1},...,2c_{n}\rrbracket

Immediately from Theorem

Proposition 4.2

The set

Appendix A
Proof of Proposition
3.1

Let
n\geq 1
be an odd integer and
a_{0},...,a_{n}
be integers. Let
A_{k}={\tiny\begin{pmatrix}a_{k}&(-1)^{k+1}\\
(-1)^{k}&0\end{pmatrix}}
for
k=0,...,n
and write
{\tiny\begin{pmatrix}*&*\\
c_{k}&d_{k}\end{pmatrix}}=A_{0}A_{1}\cdots A_{k}
. Moreover, set
A={\tiny\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{pmatrix}}=A_{0}A_{1}\cdots A_{n}
.

We want to prove that

\displaystyle\int_{z}^{A.z}E_{2}(w)dw=\frac{6}{\pi i}\log\left(\frac{cz+d}{\mathrm{sign}(c)i}\right)+\sum_{k=0}^{n}(-1)^{k}a_{k}+3\sum_{k=1}^{n}\mathrm{sign}(c_{k}d_{k}).

We begin by rewriting the integral as

(A.1)

\int_{z}^{A.z}E_{2}(w)dw=\frac{6}{\pi i}\int_{i}^{z}\frac{c}{cw+d}dw+\int_{i}^{A.i}E_{2}(w)dw

and note that
A_{k}.i=\frac{a_{k}i-(-1)^{k}}{(-1)^{k}i}=i+(-1)^{k}a_{k}
. In particular, it follows from the Fourier expansion of
E_{2}
in (
3.1
) that

\displaystyle\int_{i}^{A_{k}.i}E_{2}(w)\;dw=\displaystyle\int_{i}^{i+(-1)^{k}a_{k}}E_{2}(w)\;dw=(-1)^{k}a_{k}.

Now we split the line integral from
z
to
A.z
into an integral over
n+1
paths (this is possible as
E_{2}(w)
is a holomorphic function):

\displaystyle\int_{i}^{A.i}E_{2}(w)\;dw

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\int_{A_{0}\cdots A_{k-1}.i}^{A_{0}\cdots A_{k}.i}E_{2}(w)\;dw

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\int_{i}^{A_{k}.i}E_{2}(w)|_{2}(A_{0}\cdots A_{k-1})\;dw

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\int_{i}^{A_{k}.i}E_{2}(w)\;dw+\frac{6}{\pi i}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\int_{i}^{A_{k}.i}\frac{c_{k-1}}{c_{k-1}w+d_{k-1}}\;dw

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{n}(-1)^{k}a_{k}+\frac{6}{\pi i}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\int_{i}^{A_{k}.i}\frac{c_{k-1}}{c_{k-1}w+d_{k-1}}\;dw.

As a primitive of
\frac{c_{k-1}}{c_{k-1}w+d_{k-1}}=\frac{-c_{k-1}}{-c_{k-1}w-d_{k-1}}
, we choose
\log\left(\frac{c_{k-1}w+d_{k-1}}{\mathrm{sign}(c_{k-1})i}\right)
. Since

{\tiny\begin{pmatrix}*&*\\
c_{k-1}&d_{k-1}\end{pmatrix}}A_{k}={\tiny\begin{pmatrix}*&*\\
c_{k-1}a_{k}+(-1)^{k}d_{k-1}&(-1)^{k+1}c_{k-1}\end{pmatrix}}={\tiny\begin{pmatrix}*&*\\
c_{k}&d_{k}\end{pmatrix}},

we have
c_{k-1}A_{k}.i+d_{k-1}=\frac{c_{k}i+d_{k}}{(-1)^{k}i}
. Thus,

(A.2)

\sum_{k=1}^{n}\int_{i}^{A_{k}.i}\frac{c_{k-1}}{c_{k-1}w+d_{k-1}}dw=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\left(\log\left(\frac{c_{k}i+d_{k}}{\mathrm{sign}(d_{k})}\right)-\log\left(\frac{c_{k-1}i+d_{k-1}}{\mathrm{sign}(c_{k-1})i}\right)\right).

We may reorder the sum on the right hand side of (
A.2
) to obtain

\displaystyle\frac{6}{\pi i}\log\left(\frac{c_{n}i+d_{n}}{\mathrm{sign}(d_{n})}\right)-\frac{6}{\pi i}\log\left(\frac{i}{i}\right)+\frac{6}{\pi i}\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\left(\log\left(\frac{c_{k}i+d_{k}}{\mathrm{sign}(c_{k})i}\right)-\log\left(\frac{c_{k}i+d_{k}}{\mathrm{sign}(d_{k})}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{6}{\pi i}\log\left(\frac{ci+d}{\mathrm{sign}(d)}\right)+3\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\mathrm{sign}(c_{k}d_{k}).

Hence, with (
A.1
), we arrive at

\displaystyle\int_{z}^{A.z}E_{2}(w)dw

\displaystyle=\frac{6}{\pi i}\log\left(\frac{cz+d}{\mathrm{sign}(c)i}\right)-\frac{6}{\pi i}\log\left(\frac{ci+d}{\mathrm{sign}(c)i}\right)+\sum_{k=0}^{n}(-1)^{k}a_{k}

\displaystyle+\frac{6}{\pi i}\log\left(\frac{ci+d}{\mathrm{sign}(d)}\right)+3\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\mathrm{sign}(c_{k}d_{k})

\displaystyle=\frac{6}{\pi i}\log\left(\frac{cz+d}{\mathrm{sign}(c)i}\right)+\sum_{k=0}^{n}(-1)^{k}a_{k}+3\sum_{k=1}^{n}\mathrm{sign}(c_{k}d_{k}).
