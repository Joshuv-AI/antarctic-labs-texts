Beyond the Spell: A Dynamic Logic Analysis of Misdirection
Benjamin Icard, Raul Fervari

Abstract
Misdirection can be defined as the intentional action of causing some misrepresentation in an agent, or in a group of agents. Such misrepresentations may result from verbal actions, as in linguistic deception, or from visual actions, as in visual misdirection. Examples of visual misdirection abound (e.g. in nature, in the military), with magic tricks providing a vivid illustration. So far, various types of verbal misdirection have been investigated from a formal perspective (e.g. lying, bluffing) but little attention has been paid to the particular case of visual misdirection. In this paper, we introduce a dynamic epistemic logic to represent not only verbal misdirection on agents’ beliefs but also visual misdirection on agents’ observations. We illustrate the dynamics of the logic by modelling a classic magic trick known as the French Drop. We also provide a sound and complete axiom system for the logic, and discuss the expressivity and scope of the setting.



keywords
Observations, Beliefs, Misdirection, Dynamic Epistemic Logic, Verbal, Visual, Simulation, Dissimulation, Surprise

1
Introduction

Human misdirection refers to some agent’s intentional action to cause misrepresentations in addressees, using “
deflection of attention for the purpose of disguise
”
Sharpe 1988
. Conceptually, misrepresentations are inaccurate representations of the world that may rely on different
modalities
, either purely verbal, visual, etc.
Bell and Whaley 1991
;
Kuhn et al. 2014
. When the modality is verbal, as in announcements, misrepresentations usually consist in inaccurate beliefs (with the notion of belief in the sense of
Von Wright 1951
), that can be associated to observations, as in so-called “
epistemic seeing
”
Dretske 1970
;
Dretske 1979
. When the modality is non-verbal, as in visual observation, misrepresentations consist in inaccurate observations that can involve inaccurate beliefs (i.e., inaccurate
epistemic seeing
) but not necessarily, as in so-called “
non-epistemic seeing
”
Dretske 1970
;
Dretske 1979
. To the best of our knowledge, no formal treatment has been given to visual misdirection and resulting inaccurate observations, in contrast to verbal misdirection. This paper proposes to use dynamic epistemic logic to analyze both verbal and visual misdirection, their similarities and differences, and resulting misrepresentations. For clarity’s sake, we call “strategies of misdirection” the matching of specific actions to specific misrepresentations.

In the conceptual and logical literature, misdirection has been mainly studied through linguistic lenses, i.e. as verbal deception. Since lying is the prototypical instance of verbal deception
Carson 2010
;
Mahon 2015
, most analyses have been focused on
lies
, as in e.g.
Sakama et al. 2010
;
van Ditmarsch
et al. 2012
, — although borderline strategies have also been formalized, such as
bluffing

Sakama 2015
;
van Ditmarsch 2014
,
half-truths

Sakama and
Caminada 2010
and
omission of information

Sakama and
Caminada 2010
;
Sakama et al. 2014
. But, in the course of those investigations, visual strategies of misdirection have been left aside, – even though the concept of visual observation has caught some attention
Charrier et al. 2016
;
Herzig et al. 2015
.

Visual misdirection is pervasive in humans
Bell and Whaley 1991
;
Forbes 2011
as well as in nature
Quicke 2017
;
Ruxton et al. 2019
.
A fundamental distinction concerning misdirection is between
dissimulation
and
simulation
. In
Bell and Whaley 1991
, dissimulation is defined as the action of hiding facts to deceive addressees, whereas simulation consists in deceiving them by showing some unreal facts as if they were really happening. Important to notice is that, in nature, camouflage and faking embody this broader distinction between
dissimulation
and
simulation
in misdirection.
In
camouflage
, one individual aims to blend into its environment to remain undetected from predators, while
faking
consists of simulating a false or counterfactual reality to distract their attention.
Dissimulation and simulation actually occur in all living entities. In humans, those strategies have been practiced since ancient times by the military, e.g.,
simulation
with the Trojan horse
Whaley 1969
. But, actually, stage magicians have also played a central role, even in the military domain
Maskelyne 1949
by crafting visual illusions to trick enemies, based on
simulation
(with fake tanks) or on
dissimulation
(with light effects)
Fisher 2011
.

This paper seeks to enhance the understanding of misdirection through a logical analysis of its verbal and visual manifestations. Rather than proposing a novel theory of misdirection, we formalize existing conceptual distinctions, such as the difference between non-epistemic and epistemic seeing
Dretske 1970
;
Dretske 1979
, as well as the contrast between visual simulation and dissimulation
Bell and Whaley 1991
. We examine how these distinctions from established literature interact from a logical perspective, clarifying their respective roles in the mechanisms of misdirection themselves.
In that respect, we model a classical magic trick, called the French Drop (see, e.g.
(
Bell 2003
;
Kuhn et al. 2014
)
for mentions), in which a performer pretends to move a coin from her left hand to her right hand, by simulating a false transfer while also dissimulating the coin at the same time. Logical investigations on magic tricks have already been conducted, notably by
Smith et al. 2016
which used
propositional dynamic logic
to analyze the sense of impossibility resulting from magical effects. We will also tackle those cognitive aspects, by modelling the surprise caused by the French Drop trick, but our main focus is to characterize the deception involved in misdirection, verbal and visual, not only in magic tricks but also in other contexts.

We present a dynamic logic of misdirection, called herein
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{DLM}}
, which incorporates verbal and visual actions, that can be truthful or untruthful. Technically,
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{DLM}}
is a specialized version of standard
dynamic epistemic logic

Baltag et al. 1998
;
Van Ditmarsch et al. 2007
whose novelty is the presence of atoms for expressing the agents’ visual observations of their surroundings, a classical doxastic modality for mirroring their beliefs, and a dynamic operator for updating beliefs and observations by performing verbal announcements and visual actions, respectively. The idea of expressing visual observations with atomic propositions is borrowed from the logic of (in)attentive agents of
Belardinelli and Bolander 2023
(see also
Bolander et al. 2016
). This approach relies on specialized atoms to model the particular facts an agent is paying attention to.
In our case, atomic observations of a fact
p
does not entail to pay attention to
p
since, the way we interpret atomic observations of
p
, it does not entail the agent to hold any representation, in particular beliefs, of
p
.
Building upon this idea, we introduce
atoms for observation
syntactically, but we distinguish atoms for
observing a fact
from atoms for
observing the negation of a fact
, a distinction that cannot be made in
Belardinelli and Bolander 2023
. Still, as in their approach, we consider that not observing some fact does not guarantee observing its negation, for which we need to impose some properties of the models. In addition, we consider special versions of action models in our dynamic modalities: on the one hand, action models contain
postconditions
, facilitating the update of a fact that is being (not) observed (as in
van Ditmarsch and Kooi 2008
;
van Ditmarsch et al. 2012
); on the other hand, we define some particular action models, in which one designated agent (as e.g. a magician) performs an act in order to misdirect the rest of them (e.g. spectators).

Even though
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{DLM}}
is a simple variant of dynamic epistemic logic, our goal with this logic is to capture some classical forms of misdirection in a natural way.
Precisely, our framework enables us to model, step by step, in a dynamic fashion, how both the beliefs and visual perception of agents change due to the execution of an action. Using this logic, we perform a formal analysis of misdirection in the French Drop trick, putting particular emphasis on visual simulation. Moreover, we discuss formally other related concepts in misdirection, such as dissimulation, stronger notions of belief and observation, and surprise as a cognitive effect.

Outline of the paper.

\boldsymbol{\mathsf{DLM}}
.

\boldsymbol{\mathsf{DLM}}

\boldsymbol{\mathsf{DLM}}^{(+,-)}

\boldsymbol{\mathsf{DLM}}
by modelling the dynamics of the French Drop trick. In section

2
Setting the Stage for Misdirection

We start out by introducing key distinctions on agents’ perspectives regarding beliefs and observations. We present extant logical frameworks for formalizing misdirection, using the classical distinction between “dissimulation” versus “simulation” as a conceptual background. Finally, we insist on the logical features needed to capture verbal and visual misdirection.

2.1
Believing and observing

For simplicity’s sake, we distinguish two perspectives for agents: the operator’s action with information versus the addressee’s reaction to the information received. Concerning modalities, we focus on the distinction between verbal versus visual actions, amongst other possibilities (auditory, kinesthetic, olfactory, etc.). As an example of verbal actions involving the operator and addresse’s perspectives, one can think of a politician (the operator) telling an anecdote in front of potential electors (the addressees) to make them believe that he or she is the best candidate for an election. For an example with visual actions, now, one can think of a stage magician (the operator) showing a trick to spectators (the addressees) to make them observe some unreal event, such as rabbit appearing or vanishing. In verbal actions, the operator is more usually called the “speaker” while the addressee is the “hearer”. In visual actions, no specific term exist but the operator may be called “the performer”, while the addressees are the “spectators”, “audience”, or “public”.

Let us start by drawing distinctions concerning verbal and visual attitudes before considering the actions affecting those attitudes. Agents can entertain three possible, yet non exclusive, attitudes with respect to their surroundings:

\blacktriangleright

Observing with believing
, called herein
“epistemic observation”
and corresponding to the notion of
“epistemic seeing”
from
Dretske 1970
;
Dretske 1979
.
Some objects, e.g., an apple or a parrot, are visible in front of agents who actually see them
as
an apple,
as
a parrot, respectively. They are perceived as specific entities and not only as shapes.

Another example is a detective observing certain pieces of evidence and interpreting them not as scattered objects and marks but as a crime scene where a struggle or a crime occurred.

This can apply to facts or events as well: an agent epistemically sees a car moving forward in the street if she does not see a simple unidentified object in motion, but she sees it
as
a car moving forward in the street. In those cases, observations imply agents to form explicit beliefs about their observations, as a result of conscious recognition of objects, facts or events.

\blacktriangleright

Believing without observing
, called herein
“simple belief”
:
now, agents believe that certain events have been occurring somewhere beyond their sight, but they cannot observe those events directly. For example, an agent may believe that a car has crashed on the street based on verbal information from a direct observer, but she has not directly observed the event herself.

\blacktriangleright

Observing without believing
, called herein
“atomic observation”
and corresponding to the notion of
“simple seeing”
from
Dretske 1970
;
Dretske 1979
, and to
“non-epistemic seeing”
in
Smith 2001
;
Demircioglu 2017
.
This can be understood as a causal process. For example, when an entity — such as an apple or a parrot, is within a field of vision and light reaches the eyes, the object is perceived without necessarily being recognized
as
an apple, or
as
a parrot. Another example involves a contemplative individual looking through her window. She observes the street in an atomic (i.e., non-epistemic) manner if she does not form any explicit doxastic representation of the events unfolding on the street (e.g., a parrot flying, a car moving forward) because she remains completely absorbed in her thoughts.

To sum up, while believing necessarily involves adopting a stance on the world’s configuration, observing does not necessarily require adopting such a stance, as noted by
Dretske 1970
;
Dretske 1979
. When observation is
epistemic
, the agent adopts a doxastic stance toward her environment since she perceives her surroundings while forming conscious beliefs about what she is observing, as a result of conscious recognition. By contrast, when observation is
atomic
, and thus
non-epistemic
, no doxastic stance is adopted by the agent since now she sees her environment without entertaining any belief about this observation, as it is the case when she does not pay attention to what is happening in her immediate surroundings.

Let us focus now on verbal and visual actions. On the one hand, verbal modalities express through actions of
telling
facts to addressees. On the other hand, visual modalities express through actions of
showing
facts to them. In both cases, successful actions always lead to epistemic effects in addressees, with subtle differences. Actions of showing lead addressees not only to observe new facts or events but, necessarily, to observe them epistemically. Indeed, showing cannot result in simple seeing since, by the action of showing, the attention of the addressee is drawn to the facts observed and thus, to form explicit beliefs about those facts. By contrast, actions of telling result either on simple believing when the addressee cannot observe her surroundings at the same time, or on epistemic observation when the addressee can observe her surroundings at the same time. In both situations, the doxastic state of the addressee consists in new beliefs either increasing or decreasing her uncertainty about the world. Operators also separate into two categories regarding their intentions to addressees:
genuine
versus
misleading
. Intentions are genuine when the operators’ goal is to inform addressees, while their intentions are misleading when they are deceptive and plan to mislead addressees. This is the case of misdirection. From the perspective of actions, misdirection can be verbal and rely on actions of telling, or visual and rely on actions of showing. Verbal misdirection consists in telling something false to addressees while visual misdirection consists in showing a false reality to them.

2.2
Two main strategies of misdirection

Building on
Bell and Whaley 1991
, we propose to formalize two key strategies of misdirection in this paper:
dissimulation of the truth
and
simulation of the false
. Our goal is to capture these strategies in their most general form, distinguishing only between their visual and verbal expressions. In order to be exhaustive, this section also reviews extant work on various subcategories of dissimulation and simulation, illustrating the wide range of forms that our formal approach aims to cover.

2.2.1
Misdirection as Dissimulation of the Truth

Dissimulation consists in hiding the truth to addressees to prevent them from making an accurate representation of the world. For e.g.
Bok 1983
;
Fallis 2020
, hiding, or concealing information, consists in
masking
true information, — aiming addressees not to form accurate beliefs on the world. Important to notice is that dissimulation in this sense (i.e., hiding or concealing), differs from dissimulation as
withholding information
, or omission. The work in
Carson 2010
insists on the fact that withholding information does not involve any effective action to deceive, contrary to hiding information which requires an effective action of concealment in that respect.

The effect of successful dissimulation is always
epistemic
: the addressee fails to reach accurate belief and/or accurate epistemic observation on her surroundings. In the verbal case, successful dissimulation leads addressees
not
to reach true beliefs, and can also lead them
not
to reach accurate epistemic observation in case speakers use verbal omission to avoid attracting the visual attention of addressees to some specific fact or event. In the visual case now, successful dissimulation necessarily leads addresses to fail to epistemically observe some true reality. This is the case in the French Drop trick, where dissimulation is visual and relies on
masking
. As part of the trick, the magician conceals the coin by
palming
it into her left hand. In doing so, she puts a physical obstacle, namely her left hand, between the eyes of the public and the coin, thus concealing it from their sight. This makes spectators fail to epistemically observe that the coin is (still) in the magician’s left hand. We argue that in the French Drop, this dissimulation through palming occurs simultaneously with the action of simulating a fake pass, which enhances the magical effect by diverting the audience’s attention.

Verbal dissimulation has been formalized from two different perspectives. The first perspective has focused on expressing the
epistemic preconditions
of dissimulation
Sakama 2015
;
van Ditmarsch
et al. 2012
;
van Ditmarsch 2014
. By default, the precondition for dissimulation is the existence of a mismatch between the level of uncertainty the speaker has about the world, and the level of uncertainty he or she wants to convey to addressees. A variant on dissimulation is bluffing
Sakama 2015
;
van Ditmarsch 2014
: now, speakers are completely uncertain about the truth or falsity of the utterances they make. But, as a precondition, the strongest form of uncertainty consists in full ignorance: in this case, speakers do not know, or at least believe to be true, the content they are uttering. Epistemic logicians have modelled different forms of ignorance but not from the sole perspective of deception
van der Hoek and
Lomuscio 2004
;
Fan et al. 2015
;
Kubyshkina and
Petrolo 2021
.

The second perspective for modeling verbal dissimulation is more concerned with the
epistemic goals
of the deceivers. Following
Chisholm and
Feehan 1977
,
Sakama and
Caminada 2010
characterizes deception by
commission
in which deceivers perform some action to hide the truth, is distinguished from deception by
omission
in which deceivers do not perform any action to this end. Omission can be either
complete
or only
partial
. When it is complete, deceivers intend to hide the whole truth to addressees and thus, to prevent the formation of
any
belief in them. In this regard,
Sakama and
Caminada 2010
;
Sakama et al. 2014
have formalized the epistemic goals of complete omission under the name of
“withholding information”
. This strategy should be contrasted with the utterance of
“half-truths”
in which dissimulation is only partial. Following
Égré and Icard 2018
;
Égré 2021
, half-truths are vague utterances whose status is deliberately uncertain between truth and falsity, and that speakers may use to make addressees draw mistaken interpretations and conclusions. The epistemic goal of such half-truths is to keep addressees in the dark, as it is modeled in e.g.
Sakama et al. 2014
.

By contrast with verbal dissimulation, visual dissimulation has not been logically investigated so far, though the notions of observation and perception have caught logical attention in e.g.
Herzig et al. 2015
;
Charrier et al. 2016
;
Belardinelli and
Bolander 2023
. In visual dissimulation, one attempts to escape the gaze of others based on using various means, detailed in the taxonomy of deception proposed by
Bell and Whaley 1991
. One of those means, called
“masking”
, consists in preventing any potential observation by putting an obstacle between oneself (or the object to hide) and the observer in order to remain invisible (or the object). Another mean classically used for visual dissimulation is
“camouflage”
, or
“crypsis”
, in which the deceiver blends into its surroundings by adequately matching its colours and/or shapes. To the best of our knowledge, none of these distinctions has been formalized in the literature.

2.2.2
Misdirection as Simulation of the False

In contrast with dissimulation, simulation consists in presenting false information to addressees, either by using available false information or by fabricating false information, as in
faking
or
fabrication

(
Fallis 2009
)
. The goal of simulation is to entertain addressees with inaccurate representations of the world.

As for dissimulation, the effect of successful simulation is always
epistemic
too, consisting in wrong beliefs and/or wrong epistemic observations for addressees. In the verbal case, successful simulation leads addressees to false beliefs, but can also lead them to false epistemic observation in case addressees also see the physical manifestation
of the simulated action.
In the visual case now, successful simulation converts atomic observation into false epistemic observations for the addressees. This is the case in the French Drop trick: before the magician performs any visual action, the spectators observe the stage atomically since they do not necessarily pay attention to any specific object or fact of the scene. They just expect the magic trick to begin. Then, the magician enters the scene and the trick starts: she dissimulates the coin by palming into her left hand and then simulates the false action of moving the coin from left to right hand. By the magician performing those visual actions, the spectators’ atomic observations become epistemic observations. At the end of the effect, indeed, the audience believe and epistemically observe that the coin has moved to the magician’s right hand when this is not the case.

Extant formal literature on verbal simulation has given particular attention to the act of
lying
, as a prototypical instance of verbal simulation in which speakers utter propositional contents they believe to be false with the intention to deceive addressees. Intention (to deceive) has been formalized in static epistemic logic with classical Kripke models
Sakama et al. 2010
;
Sakama et al. 2014
, while their assertoric and deceptive dimensions have been expressed in dynamic epistemic logic
Liu 2009
;
van Ditmarsch
et al. 2012
;
van Ditmarsch 2014
;
Agotnes et al. 2016
. Using announcement operators to express intentionally false utterances, as in
Liu 2009
,
van Ditmarsch
et al. 2012
;
van Ditmarsch 2014
logically distinguish
“subjective lies”
(i.e. believed to be false utterances that happen to be true in reality) from more
“objective lies”
(i.e. believed to be false utterances that also turn out to be really false). In
Agotnes et al. 2016
, more exotic cases of lying are formalized, such as Moorean lies, i.e. utterances that are initially false but become true once they are announced. Apart from lies, verbal simulation can also consist in making addressees perform
wrong inferences
based on the speakers’ utterances. In particular,
Sakama et al. 2010
formalizes
“deductive lies”
in which speakers want addressees to infer inaccurate conclusions from their utterances, distinguishing them from
“abductive lies”
in which speakers expect them to jump directly to false conclusions, without any deduction
(
Sakama and Inoue 2016
)
.

Here again, by contrast with verbal simulation, visual simulation has not been logically investigated to the best of our knowledge. As mentioned, only the notions of observation and perception underlying visual simulation have caught logical attention recently
Herzig et al. 2015
;
Charrier et al. 2016
;
Belardinelli and
Bolander 2023
.
In turn, our interest is to model these two concepts in general via dynamic actions changing the perception of an agent, incorporating also the notion of observation into the picture as a novelty.

2.3
Modelling verbal and visual misdirection

2.3.1
Misdirection in the French Drop Trick

Misdirection has been analyzed extensively by stage magic practitioners (see e.g.
(
Maskelyne and Devant 1911
;
Fitzkee 1975
)
). When magicians aim to master a new trick, they often break it down into two key components: the
method
used to perform the trick, and the
effect
that results from the method (for a detailed discussion on the stages involved in producing magical effects, see
Sharpe 1932
). Crucially, the method refers to the
action
, or
combination of actions
, employed by magicians to lead spectators into a deceptive state, known as the
effect
(see e.g.
Kuhn et al. 2016
).

In magic tricks, spectators are deceived because they are disoriented by the magician’s method. They expect the magician’s action to result in some state of affairs but, as a result of the magician’s action, they observe that another state of affairs obtain which contradicts their expectations. At the heart of the magician’s deceitful effect is a mismatch between the spectators’ expectations and the result they actually observe. Spectators expect the magician to do some particular action but in fact, the magician has not played this action but another one resulting in deception and surprise.

Here, we use the French Drop Trick (see
(
Bell 2003
;
Kuhn et al. 2014
)
) to formalize and illustrate visual actions of misdirection. Accordingly, states of deception in the French Drop concern the method and, thus, the effect. In this trick, a magician makes the audience wrongly observe and believe that a coin has moved from her left to right hand by making a false passing of the coin from one hand to the other. Actually, the coin has remained in the magician’s left hand all along, being concealed with palming, so the public’s observation is mistaken. At the end, the magician finally reveals the stratagem to the audience.

2.3.2
Casting the magic spell
logically

We aim to define actions of misdirection of two distinct types. The first type is verbal: it consists, for an an operator, to induce a false belief in an addressee by making a linguistic utterance. The second type is visual and consists for the operator to trigger a wrong observation
(i.e., observing something that differs from reality)
in the addressee by making gestures. Those two types act differently on addressees. In verbal misdirection, the operator’s announcement acts at the level of the addressees’ beliefs to make them believe the false, or at least to prevent them from believing the truth. In visual misdirection, the operator’s gestures act at the level of the addressees’ observations and beliefs, to make them see something that is not the case, as it happens in visual illusions for instance.

In this paper, the logic
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{DLM}}
will contain a static operator for expressing agents’ beliefs as well as special atomic formulas for expressing their (non-epistemic) observations. But
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{DLM}}
will also contain a dynamic operator to capture the agents’ informational actions. Semantically, we define specific action models for verbal actions, or actions of telling, and specific action models for visual actions, or actions of showing. In both cases, actions are either genuine or misleading.

Our setting turns out to be powerful enough to distinguish between simulation and dissimulation, in the verbal and visual cases. We argue that it can also express other notions such as epistemic observation and stronger notions of belief and observation. Our logic shows that visual actions convert atomic observation in epistemic observation but also that that simulation (of the false) implies dissimulation (of the truth). Finally, we use
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{DLM}}
as a tool to put more emphasis on the specificity of misdirection in magic tricks compared to other forms: the state of surprise caused by the deception involved. That way, we aim to capture the essence of misdirection in magic tricks but also to pave the way for using
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{DLM}}
to analyze other forms of misdirection in the future.

3
A Dynamic Logic of Misdirection

We present the syntax and semantics of our
Dynamic Logic of Misdirection
(
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{DLM}}
). We introduce a class of models called herein
observational epistemic models
. These models contain two kinds of information: about beliefs, which conceptually result from verbal communication and visual perception; and information about the facts that are observable for the agents, i.e., specifically visual perception. While beliefs will be represented in the language via a standard doxastic modality
Hintikka 1962
, observations will be modelled by special atomic symbols, similarly to the way
Belardinelli and Bolander 2023
models attention.
Unlike other approaches like
Herzig et al. 2015
;
Charrier et al. 2016
, our framework will allow us to model dynamic aspects of beliefs and visual misdirection. We also discuss the appropriateness of certain classes of models and present a standard sound and complete axiomatization for the logic.

3.1
Syntax and Semantics

The language presented herein is an adaptation of the Dynamic Epistemic Logic (DEL) for (in)attentive agents presented in
Belardinelli and Bolander 2023
, based on generalizing
Baltag et al. 1998
.
Throughout the text, we let
\mathsf{Prop}
be a countable set of propositional symbols,
\mathsf{Agt}
a finite set of agent symbols, and
\mathsf{Obs}=\{\textsf{o}_{a}{p},\textsf{o}_{a}{\neg p}\mid a\in\mathsf{Agt},\ p\in\mathsf{Prop}\}
a set of observation symbols. We read
\textsf{o}_{a}{p}
(respectively
\textsf{o}_{a}{\neg p}
) as
“agent
a
observes that
p
(respectively
\neg p
) is the case”
. Notice that in
Belardinelli and Bolander 2023
, some similar extra symbols are used to state that an agent is paying attention to a certain atomic proposition
p
. But here, the conceptual difference is that an extra atomic symbol
\textsf{o}_{a}{p}
is added to stand for non-epistemic observation, i.e., for the fact that an agent
a
only visually perceives that some fact
p
is happening, possibly without paying attention to it. We start out by formally introducing the models we use to interpret our logic.

Definition 1
.

A
relational (Kripke) model
is a tuple
\mathcal{M}=\langle W,\{R_{a}\}_{a\in\mathsf{Agt}},V\rangle
where:

\blacktriangleright

W
is a non-empty set of
possible worlds
;

\blacktriangleright

R_{a}\subseteq W\times W
;

\blacktriangleright

V:\mathsf{Prop}\cup\mathsf{Obs}\rightarrow 2^{W}
is a
valuation function
.

Let
w\in W
, we call the pair
(\mathcal{M},w)
a
pointed model
, with parentheses usually dropped.

Definition 2

We define the class of

At this point, an important distinction to make concerns the models used for (in)attentive agents in

Belardinelli and Bolander 2023

is attentive to symbol

(with

), are no longer indicating “attentiveness”, but they indicate that agent
a
is
observing
that a certain fact

holds, without necessarily paying attention to it. As already mentioned, the agent observes fact

;
Dretske 1979
;

;

\textsf{o}_{a}{p}
to indicate that the agent observes that

is the case, and
\textsf{o}_{a}{\neg p}
to indicate that the agent observes that
p
is not the case. As one can notice,
\textsf{o}_{a}{p}
being false is not the same as
\textsf{o}_{a}{\neg p}
being true since they can both be false but they cannot both be true at the same time. Finally, notice that each relation
R_{a}
is Euclidean, transitive and serial, i.e., has the usual (basic) properties of belief
Von Wright 1951
;
Hintikka 1962
.

The actions of misdirection that some agent(s) perform will be represented via standard tools of Dynamic Epistemic Logic known as
action models

Baltag et al. 1998
.
Recall that action models are objects describing semantically the effects intended by an agent over an epistemic situation (i.e., over a model). They can be used as parameters in formulas of the language, in particular, inside a dynamic modality. In a nutshell, dynamic modalities perform updates in the current model, in this case depending on the semantic description given by an action model. When interpreting the dynamic modality, action models tell us how to update the original model, raising a new epistemic situation.
In Definitions
3
and
4
, action models and the language are defined as usual, by mutual recursion, and rely on a lower step of the inductive definition.

Definition 3
.

An

\mathbf{A}=\langle E,\{\rightarrow_{a}\}_{a\in\mathsf{Agt}},{\rm Phys.~Rev.~E},\mathsf{post}\rangle

\blacktriangleright

\blacktriangleright

\rightarrow_{a}\subseteq E\times E

\blacktriangleright

{\rm Phys.~Rev.~E}:E\rightarrow\boldsymbol{\mathsf{DLM}}

\blacktriangleright

–

for all

\textsf{o}_{a}{p}\in D

\textsf{o}_{a}{\neg p}\in D

Let

, with parentheses usually dropped.

Notice that in Definition

, as in

Postconditions are also required to update the models consistently, and respecting the properties of an observational model (see Lemma

). In this regard, we only allow updates of the truth value of a finite set of symbols (either propositional or atomic observations) with
\top
or
\bot
, and for the rest,

acts as the identity function. Moreover, we ask that if an observation
\textsf{o}_{a}{p}
is updated, so is its counterpart
\textsf{o}_{a}{\neg p}
, and the update is defined accordingly. In the next definition, we make precise the syntax of our dynamic logic of misdirection.

Definition 4
.

The set of well-formed formulas of the language
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{DLM}}

\varphi,\psi::=p\mid\textsf{o}_{a}{p}\mid\textsf{o}_{a}{\neg p}\mid\neg\varphi\mid\varphi\wedge\psi\mid\mathcal{B}_{a}\varphi\mid[\mathbf{A},e]\varphi,

p\in\mathsf{Prop}
;

;

\bot:=p\wedge\neg p

\hat{\mathcal{B}}_{a}\varphi:=\neg\mathcal{B}_{a}\neg\varphi

The set of literals is defined as
\mathsf{Lit}=\{\ell\mid\ell\in\mathsf{Prop}\mbox{ or }\ell=\neg p\mbox{ with }p\in\mathsf{Prop}\}

The modalities introduced in the BNF above should be read as follows. Formula

meaning ‘

\textsf{o}_{a}{p}
indicates that

states that
agent

observes that

\mathbf{A},e

Now, we define how an action model affects an observational epistemic model, — potentially modifying the doxastic states and observations of a set of agents. In general, these actions are acts of communication between agents which can consist in verbal announcements or visual gesture, and can be either genuine or deceptive. As in usual settings, such communication can be public, private or semi-private, but might involve both verbal and visual information herein.
Moreover, notice that while in
Belardinelli and Bolander 2023
, actions of communication are
truthful
, we consider both truthful and untruthful actions here, as we want to address different forms of communication. In addition, our action models are enriched with

, which stand as descriptions of how certain facts change after an action, following e.g.
van Ditmarsch and Kooi 2008
. In our case, postconditions are used to express changes in the facts that an agent is observing.

Definition 5
.

Let
\mathcal{M}=\langle W,\{R_{a}\}_{a\in\mathsf{Agt}},V\rangle
be a relational model, and
let
\mathbf{A}=\langle E,\{\rightarrow_{a}\}_{a\in\mathsf{Agt}},{\rm Phys.~Rev.~E},\mathsf{post}\rangle

\mathcal{M}\otimes\mathbf{A}=\langle W^{\prime},\{R^{\prime}_{a}\}_{a\in\mathsf{Agt}},V^{\prime}\rangle

\blacktriangleright

W^{\prime}=\{(w,e)\in W\times E\mid\mathcal{M},w\models{\rm Phys.~Rev.~E}(e)\}
;

\blacktriangleright

(w,e)R^{\prime}_{a}(v,f)
if and only if

and

, for all

;

\blacktriangleright

V^{\prime}(p)=\{(w,e)\mid\mathcal{M},w\models\mathsf{post}(e)(p)\}
.

With all these definitions at hand, we can now define the semantics of
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{DLM}}

Definition 6
.

Let
\mathcal{M}=\langle W,\{R_{a}\}_{a\in\mathsf{Agt}},V\rangle
be a model, and let

. The satisfaction relation
\models
between
\mathcal{M},w
and formulas

is defined as follows:

\begin{array}[]{l@{\quad}c@{\quad}l}\mathcal{M},w\models p&\Leftrightarrow&w\in V(p)\ \quad(p\in\mathsf{Prop}\cup\mathsf{Obs})\\
\mathcal{M},w\models\neg\varphi&\Leftrightarrow&\mathcal{M},w\not\models\varphi\\
\mathcal{M},w\models\varphi\wedge\psi&\Leftrightarrow&\mathcal{M},w\models\varphi\mbox{ and }\mathcal{M},w\models\psi\\
\mathcal{M},w\models\mathcal{B}_{a}\varphi&\Leftrightarrow&\mbox{for all $v\in W$ such that }wR_{a}v,~\mathcal{M},v\models\varphi\\
\mathcal{M},w\models[\mathbf{A},e]\varphi&\Leftrightarrow&\mathcal{M},w\models{\rm Phys.~Rev.~E}(e)\mbox{ implies }(\mathcal{M}\otimes\mathbf{A}),(w,e)\models\varphi.\end{array}

A formula
\varphi
is satisfiable if there exists a pointed model
\mathcal{M},w
such that
\mathcal{M},w\models\varphi
, and it is valid if
\mathcal{M},w\models\varphi
, for all pointed models
\mathcal{M},w
.

Remark 7
.

The dual modality
\langle\mathbf{A},e\rangle
of
[\mathbf{A},e]
is interpreted as:

\mathcal{M},w\models\langle\mathbf{A},e\rangle\varphi\ \Leftrightarrow\ \mathcal{M},w\models{\rm Phys.~Rev.~E}(e)\mbox{ and }(\mathcal{M}\otimes\mathbf{A}),(w,e)\models\varphi.

Let us make some observations concerning the execution of an action in our setting. Here we are interested in particular
“informational actions”
, in the sense that some agent
a
performs an informative act which leads to update the beliefs and observations of the rest of the agents.
If agent
a
executes the action
e
, this action does not change her own factual beliefs and visual observation of the environment. However, other agents’ beliefs and visual observations might change, but not necessarily though. This means that the agent can execute an action with some informational purpose but she cannot guarantee that this action will modify the other agents’ beliefs and/or visual observations. Consider the following example: a magician performs an action to misdirect the attention of a crowd of spectators. The magician knows that actual facts do not change during the trick, but what she does not know, and cannot guarantee, is whether the spectators will believe that actual facts
did
change. In other words, the magician cannot be sure that her misdirection will succeed. Accordingly, she cannot guarantee that her own
high-order
beliefs, i.e., her beliefs about other agents’ own beliefs and observations, will be maintained after her action.
In formula, for an informational action model
(\mathbf{A},e)
, we have that
\mathcal{M},w\models\mathcal{B}_{a}\varphi
does not imply
\mathcal{M},w\models[\mathbf{A},e]\mathcal{B}_{a}\varphi
.

Regarding the modelling of magic tricks, the approach of
Smith et al. 2016
using Propositional Dynamic Logic (PDL) differs from the DEL approach of
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{DLM}}
is terms of focus of interest. While both rely on modelling epistemic notions, such as beliefs and expectations, to express states of deceptions, the goal of
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{DLM}}
is to better understand the nature of misdirection. In particular, we are interested in visual misdirection, so the logic encodes notions such as atomic observation, as a non-epistemic attitude, and visual action of observation, now as an epistemic attitude, in addition to classical verbal announcements. By contrast,
Smith et al. 2016
puts more emphasis on the constructional aspects of conjuring magic.

We start out by introducing the axiom system

Notice that in the presentation of the axiomatic system, we use axiom schemes instead of plain axioms, i.e., we use meta-variables for formulas instead of fixed propositional symbols
(see e.g.
van Dalen 2012
for details on the use of axiom schemes). Still, for simplicity’s sake, we refer to axiom schemes just as

in what follows.

As expected, axioms for
\mathcal{B}_{a}
are classical for doxastic modalities. Axioms
4_{\mathcal{B}}
and
5_{\mathcal{B}}
make sense for beliefs obtained by verbal means or any kind of sensory activity other than observations: it makes sense that one believes what one actually believes but also believes that she disbelieves something when it is the case (i.e., they are the standard axioms of introspection). Axiom
\mathsf{D}_{\mathcal{B}}
accounts for seriality, and means that what is believed by an agent must be believable for the agent. Concerning observation, we do not have axioms at the epistemic level, since our approach considers them to be factual information. In our framework, an agent can observe that
p
, that is
\textsf{o}_{a}{p}
, without
p
being true, as it happens to be the case in common illusions or hallucinations.
However, as expected, an agent cannot observe
p
and
\neg p
at the same time. What we need to establish is the inconsistency of observing a fact and its negation, given by axiom
\mathsf{Obs}
. Notice that this axiom is consistent with our basic requirement imposed on valuations in observational epistemic models (see Definition
2
).

The proof of soundness and completeness follows standard arguments of modal logic (see, e.g.,
Chellas 1980
;
Blackburn
et al. 2001
;
Blackburn et al. 2006
). Soundness is straightforward, except for the axiom
\mathsf{Obs}
which is novel. Notice that if a pointed observational epistemic model
\mathcal{M},w
is such that
\mathcal{M},w\models\textsf{o}_{a}{p}
, it is necessarily the case that
\mathcal{M},w\not\models\textsf{o}_{a}{\neg p}
, because
w\in V(\textsf{o}_{a}{p})
implies
w\notin V(\textsf{o}_{a}{\neg p})
, by the second condition in Definition
2
. Thus, the axiom
\textsf{o}_{a}{p}\rightarrow\neg\textsf{o}_{a}{\neg p}
is also valid.

Following
(
Blackburn
et al. 2001
, Proposition 4.12)
, given an arbitrary consistent set of formulas
\Gamma
, completeness is obtained by building a model (known as the ‘canonical model’) satisfying all the formulas in
\Gamma
. Associated notions like derivability (via
\mathsf{OL}
), theoremhood, maximal consistent sets (written MCS) and consistency/inconsistency are defined in the usual way.

Definition 9

(Canonical Model)
.

The

\mathcal{M}^{c}=\langle W^{c},\{R^{c}_{a}\}_{a\in\mathsf{Agt}},V^{c}\rangle

\blacktriangleright

\blacktriangleright

\blacktriangleright

(Truth Lemma)
.

Theorem 13
.

The axiom system
\mathsf{OL}

Proof.

Suppose

Now it is time to consider the full language
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{DLM}}
, i.e., to handle dynamic modalities as well. In order to axiomatize
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{DLM}}
, it is only needed to show that every formula

We start out by introducing a list of valid formulas, usually called

Lemma 15
.

The reduction axioms from Definition

are valid.

Before going further, let us discuss the properties of observational epistemic models in presence of an update via dynamic modalities. The next lemma states that the class of all observational epistemic models (i.e., those that are Euclidean, transitive and serial) is not closed under the execution of actions. However, if we give up the seriality condition, the system is complete with respect to the obtained class.

Lemma 16
.

Let
\mathcal{M},w
be a pointed epistemic observational model, and let
\mathbf{A},e
be an action model. Then,

1.

(\mathcal{M},w)
Euclidean, transitive and serial does not imply that
((\mathcal{M}\otimes\mathbf{A}),(w,e))
is.

2.

(\mathcal{M},w)
Euclidean and transitive implies that
((\mathcal{M}\otimes\mathbf{A}),(w,e))
is also Euclidean and transitive.

Thus, as mentioned, an alternative to obtain completeness consists in relaxing the conditions imposed on the models, concerning the seriality condition in particular.

Definition 17
.

The axiom system
\mathsf{DOL}
is defined as
\mathsf{OL}
minus the axiom
\mathsf{D}_{\mathcal{B}}
, and extended with the reduction axioms from Definition

\mathsf{RE}

\vdash\varphi(p/\psi)\leftrightarrow\varphi(p/\psi^{\prime})\vdash\psi\leftrightarrow\psi^{\prime}

where
\varphi(p/\psi)
is the result of replacing every occurrence of

\varphi
by
\psi
.

In order to eliminate all the occurrences of a dynamic modality in a formula (via reduction axioms), some form of replacement of equivalent formulas is needed. In this way, it is possible to apply the equivalences from Definition

as a rewriting system, pushing dynamic modalities inside of the formula and finally eliminating them. This is accomplished by the rule
\mathsf{RE}
.

Theorem 18
.

The system
\mathsf{DOL}
is sound and strongly complete with respect to the class of relational models that are Euclidean and transitive.

Proof.

First, thanks to Lemma

(using the rule

) equivalent to one of

∎

Based on the technical issues described above (i.e., the loss of seriality after updating a model), we obtain some undesired conceptual behaviour. If some agent
a
believes that
\varphi
in a serial model (i.e., in which the current state has at least a successor via
a
), naturally it is not the case that the agent also believes
\neg\varphi
. This is due to the fact that some successor of the current point exists, and since all successors must
satisfy
\varphi
, it cannot be the case that they also satisfy
\neg\varphi
. However, after updating a model via an action that does not preserve seriality (i.e., that removes all successors of the current state), the agent believes both
\varphi
and
\neg\varphi
, as not having successors trivializes what the agent believes. This issue is known in epistemic logic as the
omniscience
phenomenon, and has been extensively discussed in the literature (see e.g.,
Vardi 1986
;
Rasmussen 2015
). For this reason, we will not discuss this limitation any further here and choose to proceed with our framework despite it.

4
Misdirection via Action Types

We introduce a simple fragment of
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{DLM}}
that is enough to characterize the intended actions of misdirection, verbal and visual. Based on the notion of
action types
from
Liu and Wang 2013
, this fragment allows only four kinds of
action types
in dynamic modalities, that are either verbal or visual, and either truthful or untruthful in both cases.
2
2

2

More colloquial terms for truthful and untruthful
visual
actions would be “genuine” and “bogus” visual actions.

Remark 19
.

Both observational epistemic models and action models are labelled directed graphs, with nodes representing states, or worlds, in the former and atomic actions in the latter. To model the distinction graphically, we use circles to represent worlds of observational epistemic models, and squares to represent atomic actions of action models (as in Figures
2
and
3
). We use double lines to point out the actual world, both in the observational epistemic models and in action models.

In observational epistemic models, when an atomic symbol
p
(from
\mathsf{Prop}\cup\mathsf{Obs}
) appears inside a circle representing a world
w
, it indicates that
p
holds at
w
. If some atomic symbol
p
is not inside of a world
v
, this means that such a symbol does not hold at
v
.

In action models, while preconditions are explicitly displayed, postconditions are expressed as the conjuntion of the atomic symbols that are actually updated, meaning that the symbols become true if they appear in the affirmative form, and become false if they appear in the negated form. For the rest of the symbols that do not appear explicitly, the postcondition acts as the identity function.

4.2
Misdirection in the French Drop trick

With the machinery presented above, we can now formalize the French Drop trick as an illustration of the dynamic logic of misdirection
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{DLM}}
. Informally, the French Drop trick can be decomposed into three distinct steps:

1.

First, there is the
opening
of the trick: the magician shows a coin to the public, shakes her hands and states that the coin is in one of her two hands, right or left.

2.

Second, there is the
method
used by the magician, namely the actions she performs in front of the audience, and how those actions result in misdirection. On the one hand, there is an action of
simulation
through a fake passing of the coin. The magician seems to move the coin to her right hand but in fact the coin stays in her left hand. On the other hand, at the same time, there is an action of
dissimulation
through a genuine grabbing. The magician conceals the coin in her left hand by palming it. In the current section, we only model the action of simulation that takes place during the French Drop since, as we will show in subsection
5.3.2

3.

Finally, there is an
effect
produced by actions of simulation (fake passing) and dissimulation (genuine palming). The magician opens her right hand, and thus shows to the public that the coin is not in there, to the great surprise of the crowd.

Formally, we get:

\begin{array}[]{lll}\mathcal{M},w&\models&l\wedge\textsf{o}_{a}{l}\wedge\textsf{o}_{a}{\neg r}\wedge\neg\textsf{o}_{a}{r}\wedge\neg\textsf{o}_{b}{r}\\
&&\wedge\ \langle\mathsf{show}^{-}_{a}(r\wedge\neg l)\rangle(\textsf{o}_{a}{l}\wedge\textsf{o}_{a}{\neg r}\wedge\textsf{o}_{b}{r}\wedge\textsf{o}_{b}{\neg l}\\
&&\phantom{\langle\mathsf{show}^{-}_{a}(r\wedge\neg l)\rangle}\ \wedge\ \langle\mathsf{show}^{+}_{a}(l\wedge\neg r)\rangle(\textsf{o}_{b}{l}\wedge\textsf{o}_{b}{\neg r})).\end{array}

Each line in the formula above corresponds to one of the moments in which an action has been performed, and to their corresponding effects. In the first line, as described in the initial state, only the magician has a correct picture of the actual situation.
Based on this initial state, as shown in the second line, the action of misdirection by simulation is executed, making
b
(wrongly) observe that the coin is in the magician’s right hand (
\textsf{o}_{b}{r}
) and no longer in her left hand (
\textsf{o}_{b}{\neg l}
). Magician’s beliefs and observations remain unchanged, as expected. When the trick is revealed, as shown in the third line, both the magician and spectators both have an adequate picture of the coin being in the magician’s left hand (
l
) and not in the magician’s right hand (
\neg r
). Their factual observations are correct: in the actual state (i.e.
((w,e),e)
), we have that
\textsf{o}_{a}{l}
and
\textsf{o}_{a}{\neg r}
hold for the magician;
\textsf{o}_{b}{l}
and
\textsf{o}_{b}{\neg r}
hold for the spectators. Their beliefs are correct too:
\mathcal{B}_{a}l
and
\mathcal{B}_{a}\neg r
for the magician;
\mathcal{B}_{b}l
and
\mathcal{B}_{b}\neg r
for the spectators. In addition to that, the magician and spectators have conscious beliefs of their own observations: it is the case that
\mathcal{B}_{a}(\textsf{o}_{a}{l})
and
\mathcal{B}_{a}(\textsf{o}_{a}{\neg r})
, and also that
\mathcal{B}_{b}(\textsf{o}_{b}{l})
and
\mathcal{B}_{b}(\textsf{o}_{b}{\neg r})
.

It is worth to notice that every action performed by the magician (in this case,
\mathsf{show}^{-}_{a}(r\wedge\neg l)
and
\mathsf{show}^{+}_{a}(l\wedge\neg r)
) are executed only whenever their corresponding preconditions hold at their respective current situation, i.e., we use a diamond-like modality. This means that the magician
intentionally
performs these actions, in particular, she intentionally misdirects the spectators with
\mathsf{show}^{-}_{a}(r\wedge\neg l)
. By contrast, if we use a box-like modality (i.e.,
[\mathsf{show}^{-}_{a}(r\wedge\neg l)]
), the formula will also hold in case the precondition fails to be true; meaning that in case of misdirection, either the magician is not actually misdirecting the spectators, or that she unintentionally misdirects them. We focus herein on intentional actions, as discussed previously.

5
On the generality of
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{DLM}}

This section shows that
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{DLM}}
can be used to characterize more specific and general concepts, based on combining atomic observations, belief modalities and dynamic actions. We first define a stronger notion of belief, by reinforcing the basic beliefs of
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{DLM}}
with dynamic actions, and a stronger notion of observation, by using atomic observations to define epistemic observations. We then discuss how principles involving beliefs and observations interact, and why some of them should not be stated as axioms. Afterwards, we proceed to characterize the difference between misdirection as simulation (which involves an action) and misdirection as dissimulation (which involves the lack of an action). We finally attempt to capture the effect of surprise involved in the French Drop trick.

5.1
Stronger observations and beliefs

The way logic
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{DLM}}
is interpreted in Definition
6
, the notion of observation, written
o
, is atomic and non-epistemic. However, using a modal logic framework gives us the possibility to define a notion of epistemic observation, written
\mathcal{O}
, based on this initial concept of atomic (i.e., non-epistemic) observation

.

Definition 23
.

We define epistemic observation, written
\mathcal{O}
, as follows:

\mathcal{O}_{a}p\ :=\ \textsf{o}_{a}{p}\wedge\mathcal{B}_{a}(\textsf{o}_{a}{p}).

According to this definition, agent
a

that
p
when agent
a

atomically observes
that
p
, i.e.
\textsf{o}_{a}{p}
, but also believes that she is atomically observing that
p
, i.e.
\mathcal{B}_{a}(\textsf{o}_{a}{p})
. That being done, we define a notion of epistemic observation which is
stronger
in the sense that it associates epistemic observations to dynamic actions.

Definition 24
.

We define strong epistemic observation, written
\mathcal{O}^{\mathsf{S}}
, as follows:

\mathcal{O}^{\mathsf{S}}_{a}p\ :=\ \mathcal{O}_{b}p\wedge\bigvee_{a\in\mathsf{Agt}\setminus\{b\}}\langle\mathsf{tell}^{+}_{a}(p)\rangle\mathcal{B}_{b}p\vee\langle\mathsf{show}^{+}_{a}(p)\rangle\mathcal{B}_{b}p

Strong epistemic observation should be understood as epistemic observations that turn out to be supported by verbal and/or visual actions at the same time.

In contrast with our initial notion of belief
\mathcal{B}
, another notion we can define is
strong belief
. As it is, the modality
\mathcal{B}
defines belief as being weak doxastic attitude in which the agent may lack any justification for supporting her beliefs. On the contrary, we define strong beliefs as being weak beliefs supported by genuine actions, either verbal or visual, as in the following definition.

Definition 25
.

We define strong beliefs, written
\mathcal{B}^{\mathsf{S}}
, in the following way:

\mathcal{B}^{\mathsf{S}}_{b}\varphi\ :=\ \mathcal{B}_{b}\varphi\wedge\bigvee_{a\in\mathsf{Agt}\setminus\{b\}}\langle\mathsf{tell}^{+}_{a}(\varphi)\rangle\mathcal{B}_{b}\varphi\vee\langle\mathsf{show}^{+}_{a}(\varphi)\rangle\mathcal{B}_{b}\varphi

This definition means that agent
b
believes that
\varphi
in a strong sense in case
b
‘weakly’ believes that
\varphi
and this belief is robust under any truthful action with
\varphi

\langle\mathsf{tell}^{+}_{a}(\varphi)\rangle
) or visual (
\langle\mathsf{show}^{+}_{a}(\varphi)\rangle
). In other words, agent

holds a strong belief that

because
b
’s belief is supported by agent
a
’s action.

5.2
Interaction between beliefs and observations

Let us discuss some principles linking observations and beliefs, that are either satisfiable, valid or unreasonable in our setting. The first principle, hereafter separated into (1a) and (1b), states that if an agent
a
observes a certain fact as true (respectively, as false),
a
believes her observations to be true (respectively, false):

(1a)
\textsf{o}_{a}{p}\rightarrow\mathcal{B}_{a}\textsf{o}_{a}{p}

(1b)
\textsf{o}_{a}{\neg p}\rightarrow\mathcal{B}_{a}\textsf{o}_{a}{\neg p}

Principles (1a)-(1b) are
satisfiable
but not valid in our setting. As indicated before, we aim to keep a distinction between beliefs and observations since observation consists in sensory visual perception that does not necessarily involve conscious representation
(for instance, you may see an entity in the water without seeing it
as
a fish in the water).
So, if we accept principles (1a) and (1b) as universally valid, observations and belief conflate into the concept of “epistemic observation” discussed in Definition
23
. Naturally, these statements are satisfiable: it can be the case that an agent observes a fact
p
(respectively,
\neg p
) and that she believes she is observing
p
(respectively,
\neg p
).

The following principle, now divided into (2a) and (2b), states that an agent cannot have wrong beliefs about its observations:

(2a)
\mathcal{B}_{a}\textsf{o}_{a}{p}\rightarrow\textsf{o}_{a}{p}

(2b)
\mathcal{B}_{a}\textsf{o}_{a}{\neg p}\rightarrow\textsf{o}_{a}{\neg p}

Principles (2a)-(2b) are
satisfiable
since there are states in which an agent has correct beliefs about her observations. That being said, they are not valid in our setting since, otherwise, an agent would never be misdirected, contrary to our purposes. To conclude, consider the following principle, separated into (3a) and (3b):

(3a)
(\textsf{o}_{a}{p}\wedge\mathcal{B}_{a}\textsf{o}_{a}{p})\rightarrow[\mathsf{show}_{a}^{+}(p)](\textsf{o}_{b}{p}\wedge\mathcal{B}_{b}\textsf{o}_{b}{p})

(3b)
(\textsf{o}_{a}{\neg p}\wedge\mathcal{B}_{a}\textsf{o}_{a}{\neg p})\rightarrow[\mathsf{show}_{a}^{-}(p)](\textsf{o}_{b}{p}\wedge\mathcal{B}_{b}\textsf{o}_{b}{p})

Principles (3a)-(3b) are now
valid
in our framework. Instead of relating beliefs and observations statically, they make use of the dynamic actions in order to guarantee that an observation is believable. Formula (3a) states that whenever agent
a
epistemically observes that
p
, then, if
a
truthfully shows that
p
to some addressee
b
, then
b
will also come to epistemically observe that
p
too. Formula (3b) states the same success but with simulation that
p
: whenever agent
a
epistemically observes that
\neg p
, then, if
a
now untruthfully shows that
p
to some addressee
b
, then
b
will come to epistemically observe that
p
. These principles reflect
properly the spirit of the
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{DLM}}
framework.

5.3.2
Misdirection as dissimulation

So far, we have used

5.3.3
Simulation with dissimulation

Simulation of the false is a notion conceptually distinct from dissimulation of the truth (see subsection

\boldsymbol{\mathsf{DLM}}
to make this difference explicit by distinguishing simulation (

Intuitively, an agent who fakes some false reality

5.4
Magic misdirection as a source of surprise

We have seen that logic
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{DLM}}
is strong enough to express various notions characterizing misdirection in general and in magic tricks, more specifically. But in fact,
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{DLM}}
is also strong enough to express the sense of surprise raised by magic effects on spectators.

In the French Drop trick, in holds that
\langle\mathsf{show}^{-}_{a}(r\wedge\neg l)\rangle(\textsf{o}_{a}{l}\wedge\textsf{o}_{a}{\neg r}\wedge\textsf{o}_{b}{\neg l}\wedge\textsf{o}_{b}{r})
, at an intermediary step. So, after a visual action of simulation
\mathsf{show}^{-}_{a}(r\wedge\neg l)
, spectators observe that
\neg l
and
r
:
\textsf{o}_{b}{\neg l}\wedge\textsf{o}_{b}{r}
. Immediately after this effect, the magician unveils the mystery of the trick with visual action, i.e., it holds that
\langle\mathsf{show}^{+}_{a}(l\wedge\neg r)\rangle(\textsf{o}_{b}{l}\wedge\textsf{o}_{b}{\neg r})
. In words, after the execution of
\mathsf{show}^{+}_{a}(l\wedge\neg r)
, the spectators observe the true facts
l
and
\neg r
:
\textsf{o}_{b}{l}\wedge\textsf{o}_{b}{\neg r}
. As one can see, those facts are completely opposite to the facts they observed just before.

Following
(
Ortony and
Partridge 1987
;
Meyer et al. 1997
;
Lorini and
Castelfranchi 2006
)
, an agent is surprised by an incoming event when she recognizes an inconsistency, that is to say a
discrepancy
or
mismatch
, between her expectations about the world and the actual state she observes. Quite intuitively, the higher the gap between the agent’s expectations and reality, the stronger the surprise. In that respect,
(
Lorini and
Castelfranchi 2007
)
distinguishes a first-order kind of surprise called “
mismatch-based surprise
” from a second-order kind of surprise called “
astonishment
”, or “
surprise in recognition
”.

Mismatch-based surprise results from a conflict between the agent’s scrutinized representation and perceived facts or events. The agent is surprised because she has some anticipatory representation, but she cannot make incoming events fit with this representation. The strength of the surprise effect depends on the agents’ expectations, more precisely
beliefs
about future events. Surprise occurs if an unforeseen event, in the sense of
not believed
to happen, ultimately occurs. But a stronger surprise occurs if an event that was
believed not
to happen ultimately happens. In the
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{DLM}}
framework, for a given fact
p
, this can be expressed by the difference between agent
a
not believing that
p
when
p
happens, i.e.
\neg\mathcal{B}_{a}p\wedge p
, versus agent
a
believing that
\neg p
when
p
happens:
\mathcal{B}_{a}\neg p\wedge p
. Since the agent’s disbelief that
p
is stronger in the second case, surprise will also be stronger.

Astonishment, or surprise in recognition, results from the
recognition
of the absolute implausibility of a perceived fact compared to expectations. In this case, surprise is rooted in incongruity: an event not conceived as a relevant possibility actually takes place and leaves agents completely astonished. In
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{DLM}}
, agent
a
will be astonished that a fact
p
is true in case
p
is true but agent
a
actually neither believes it to be true nor believes it to be false:
p\wedge\neg\mathcal{B}_{a}p\wedge\neg\mathcal{B}_{a}\neg p
. In other words, astonishment results from the fact that when
p
occurs,
p
is an epistemic blindspot for agent
a
.

In the French Drop trick, the surprise the public experiences is of the mismatch-based kind. When action
\mathsf{show}^{-}_{a}(r\wedge\neg l)
leads spectators to atomic (i.e., non-epistemic) observations
\textsf{o}_{b}{\neg l}
and
\textsf{o}_{b}{r}
, it also leads them to epistemically observe the same facts. The reason is that
\mathsf{show}^{-}_{a}(r\wedge\neg l)
results in
\mathcal{B}_{a}(\textsf{o}_{b}{\neg l})
and
\mathcal{B}_{a}(\textsf{o}_{b}{r})
, so by definition of epistemic observation (see subsection
above), we have:
\mathcal{O}_{b}(\neg l)
and
\mathcal{O}_{b}(r)
. At the end of the trick, the magician dispels her secret with visual action
\mathsf{show}^{+}_{a}(l\wedge\neg r)
now leading the spectators to atomically observe
l
and
\neg r
:
\textsf{o}_{b}{l}
and
\textsf{o}_{b}{\neg r}
. Here again, spectators come to epistemically observe the same facts since action
\mathsf{show}^{+}_{a}(l\wedge\neg r)
results in
\mathcal{B}_{a}(\textsf{o}_{b}{l})
and
\mathcal{B}_{a}(\textsf{o}_{b}{\neg r})
, so by definition of epistemic observation, we have:
\mathcal{O}_{b}(l)
and
\mathcal{O}_{b}(\neg r)
. At the end of the trick, the spectators’ epistemic observations
\mathcal{O}_{b}(l)
and
\mathcal{O}_{b}(\neg r)
are completely opposite to their epistemic observations
\mathcal{O}_{b}(\neg l)
and
\mathcal{O}_{b}(r)
one moment earlier. This results in mismatched-based surprise.

Notice that, according to
(
Lorini and
Castelfranchi 2007
)
, astonishment would have replaced mismatched-based surprise if, when revealing the trick to the audience, the magician had shown, for instance, a banknote instead of a coin, like a rabbit straight out of her hat.

6
Conclusion

This article aims to conduct a logical analysis of misdirection, guided by the conceptual distinction between
non-epistemic
and
epistemic seeing

Dretske 1970
;
Dretske 1979
, as well as the classical contrast between
simulation
and
dissimulation

Bell and Whaley 1991
.
We introduced a dynamic logic of misdirection named
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{DLM}}
, building upon standard dynamic epistemic logic
Van Ditmarsch et al. 2007
, with the goal of formalizing both verbal and visual misdirection. The static part of the logic relies on atomic formulas for representing the agents’ visual observation of their environments, and on classical doxastic modality for representing their beliefs. For the dynamic part of the logic, we focus on a fragment named
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{DLM}}^{(+,-)}
, in which action types
\mathsf{tell}
and
\mathsf{show}
are defined for expressing verbal actions of announcing information, as well as visual actions of showing information. Those actions can be either truthful (indicated by label
+
) or untruthful (indicated by label
-
), making it possible to represent actions of verbal and visual misdirection. We used
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{DLM}}
to model a magic trick called the “French Drop” which illustrates actions of visual simulation (
fake passing
) and of visual dissimulation (
genuine palming
). The framework proved to be powerful enough to express the various notions involved in misdirection but also more complex notions, such as stronger belief attitudes, epistemic observations and the logical interactions between simulation and dissimulation. Interestingly, the setting also succeeded at capturing the specific sense of surprise resulting from magical effects.

As in extant proposals like
van Ditmarsch
et al. 2012
;
van Ditmarsch 2014
, our logic can express
truthful
as well as
untruthful
announcements, such as lies and other weaker forms of dishonesty, such as bluffing. But in addition to what is done in those frameworks,
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{DLM}}
also mirrors the truthful/untruthful distinction in case of
visual
actions. Indeed, the logic can express actions in which agents make addressees
genuinely
observe facts they also observe, but the logic can also express
bogus
actions in which they make other observe facts they do not observe themselves. One particular aspect of
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{DLM}}
is that observations are expressed statically and dynamically, contrary to extant proposals such as
Herzig et al. 2015
;
Charrier et al. 2016
in which observations are encoded only statically. Along the paper, we use the
French Drop trick
as the common thread for illustrating our presentation.

We argue that our framework sets the basis for a broader formal theory of misdirection. Epistemic and modal logics in general are suitable to model other forms of misdirection, such as withholding information or conspiration between a set of agents. For instance, we can represent collective acts of misdirection (i.e., a magician having accomplices) by generalizing informational actions with respect to more than one agent. So, information that must not be disclosed to the public can be handled via
private announcements
. On the other hand, it would be interesting to investigate different conditions on the modality of observation. In this work, we only ask for the underlying relation to be serial, in correspondence with the
\mathsf{D}
axiom. In fact, we discussed how to encode other notions in the logic, such as the general distinction between
simulation
and
dissimulation
,
epistemic observation
,
strong belief
and
surprise
, in which the seriality constraint may fall short. Therefore, it could be argued that other constraints might be imposed, as discussed in
Bonnay and Égré 2007
;
Bonnay and Égré 2009
concerning non-transitive knowledge used for modeling perceptual indistinguishability. Moreover, the classes of models with serial relations are not closed under dynamic updates, which can also stand as an issue. Thus, we would like to explore other alternatives in the future, as well as preservation properties on the classes of models.

Another interesting notion we should integrate is the
intentional
aspect of misdirection, i.e., by formalizing which kinds of intention are involved in deception. Here we assumed misdirection to be always intentional and do not investigate further on this aspect. But recent literature covers the intentional dimension from different perspectives, for instance the approach in
Sakama 2021
uses Epistemic Causal Logic to that end. It would be interesting to investigate whether such a setting could accommodate our dynamic logic analysis. Other approaches exist, like
Bonnet et al. 2021
(using STIT logic-based framework) and
Ward et al. 2023
(using causal games) formalizing intentional aspects of misdirection, that we should also take into account for comparison.

Finally, our approach based on dynamic epistemic logic allows us to use tools from
epistemic planning

Bolander et al. 2020
. As a framework combining standard AI planning with dynamic epistemic logic, epistemic planning could help model strategies of misdirection more accurately. Epistemic planning extends classical planning with the ability to take informed decisions, i.e., to look for plans that are based on the knowledge of the agents. Particularly interesting is the possibility of dealing with agents’ (partial) observations.
It would be interesting to study the aforementioned task, but where the initial state is an observational epistemic model, and where the action models and the goal(s) are defined over
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{DLM}}
, or more specifically from
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{DLM}}^{(+,-)}
. By doing so, we would be able to determine, given some state and a set of actions, if there is an observability-based plan, which leads from the initial state to a (potentially bogus) goal. Another logical tool for reasoning about strategies of misdirection is the family of logics of
knowing how

Wang 2015
;
Wang 2018
, specially those connected to epistemic planning as
Liu and Wang 2013
. Therein, an agent
knows how to achieve a certain goal
if there exists a sequence of epistemic actions, i.e. an epistemic plan, that transforms the actual model into a model satisfying such a goal. Again, it would be possible to incorporate the action types of
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{DLM}}
, or variations of it, into this framework. All these lines of investigation have been briefly discussed, and deserve further exploration in the future.
