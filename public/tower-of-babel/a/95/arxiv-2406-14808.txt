On the estimation rate of Bayesian PINN for inverse problems
Yi Sun, Debarghya Mukherjee, Yves Atchadé

Abstract
Solving partial differential equations (PDEs) and their inverse problems using Physics-informed neural networks (PINNs) is a rapidly growing approach in the physics and machine learning community. Although several architectures exist for PINNs that work remarkably in practice, our theoretical understanding of their performances is somewhat limited. In this work, we study the behavior of a Bayesian PINN estimator of the solution of a PDE from n n independent noisy measurement of the solution. We focus on a class of equations that are linear in their parameters (with unknown coefficients θ ⋆ \theta_{\star} ). We show that when the partial differential equation admits a classical solution (say u ⋆ u_{\star} ), differentiable to order β \beta , the mean square error of the Bayesian posterior mean is at least of order n − 2 β / ( 2 β + d ) n^{-2\beta/(2\beta+d)} . Furthermore, we establish a convergence rate of the linear coefficients of θ ⋆ \theta_{\star} depending on the order of the underlying differential operator. Last but not least, our theoretical results are validated through extensive simulations.



keywords

partial differential equations, inverse problems, physics-informed neural network, nonparametric regression, Bayesian deep learning, posterior contraction

Introduction

We consider a class of inverse problems that consists in estimating the parameters of a partial differential equation from noisy measurements of the solution. Specifically, let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}
be a bounded open domain with a smooth boundary equipped with a probability measure
\nu
. For
j\geq 1
, let
L^{2}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{j},\nu)
denote the
L^{2}
-space of
\mathbb{R}^{j}
-valued functions on
\Omega
, i.e.

L^{2}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{j},\nu)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\left\{f:\Omega\mapsto{\mathbb{R}}^{j}:\int_{\Omega}\|f(x)\|_{2}^{2}\ \nu(\mathrm{d}x)<\infty\right\}\,.

We consider a differential equation with parameter
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
that aims to find a smooth function
u\in L^{2}(\Omega,\mathbb{R},\nu)
satisfying

\mathcal{H}_{0}u+\theta^{\textsf{T}}\mathcal{H}_{1}u=f,\;\mbox{ with initial/boundary condition }\;\;\mathcal{B}u={\bf 0},

(1.1)

for some known function
f\in L^{2}(\Omega,\mathbb{R},\nu)
, and a pair of differential operators
\mathcal{H}_{0}:\;L^{2}(\Omega,\mathbb{R},\nu)\to L^{2}(\Omega,\mathbb{R},\nu)
and
\mathcal{H}_{1}:\;L^{2}(\Omega,\mathbb{R},\nu)\to L^{2}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{d},\nu)
. The operator
\mathcal{H}_{0}
is typically (although not necessarily) the ”time” partial derivative, and the operator
\mathcal{B}:\;L^{2}(\Omega,\mathbb{R},\nu)\to L^{2}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}},\nu)
imposes the boundary/initial conditions. A large class of differential equations (linear and nonlinear) that are linear in their parameters can be written in this form. A classical example of such an inverse problem arises from the heat equation as elaborated below:

Example 1
(Heat equation).

Given
L>0,T>0
, and parameter
\theta\in\mathbb{R}
, consider the heat equation
u_{t}-\theta u_{xx}=0
on
(0,L)
, with time domain
(0,T)
, initial condition
u(0,\cdot)=g(\cdot)
, and boundary condition
u(\cdot,0)=u(\cdot,L)=0
. Here
u_{t}
(resp.
u_{xx}
) denotes the partial derivative of
u
with respect to
t
(resp. the second order partial derivative of
u
with respect to
x
). With
\Omega=(0,T)\times(0,L)
, this equation can be written as
\mathcal{H}_{0}u-\theta\mathcal{H}_{1}u=f
, where
\mathcal{H}_{0}u=u_{t}
,
\mathcal{H}_{1}u=u_{xx}
, and
f=0
. The boundary/initial condition operator is given by

\mathcal{B}u=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}u_{\{t=0\}}-g\\
u_{\{x=0\}}\\
u_{\{x=L\}}\end{array}\right).

where
u_{\{t=0\}}
is the map
u_{\{t=0\}}(x)=u(0,x)
, with similar definition for
u_{\{x=0\}}
and
u_{\{x=L\}}
. Here
d=1
,
d_{1}=3
.

Let
(\theta_{\star},u_{\star})
be a tuple that satisfies the PDE (
1.1
).
We assume to observe some noisy measurements of
u_{\star}
as noted below:

H 1
.

We have
n
i.i.d. random locations and observations
({\bf s}_{i},Y_{i})\in\Omega\times\mathbb{R}
, where

Y_{i}\;|\;{\bf s}_{i}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle ind.}}{{\sim}}\mathbf{N}\left(u_{\star}({\bf s}_{i}),\sigma^{2}\right),

where
u_{\star}:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
is the unique solution of the pde (
1.1
) with
\theta=\theta_{\star}
, for some unknown vector
\theta_{\star}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Here,
\textbf{N}(m,v^{2})
denotes the univariate Gaussian distribution with mean
m
and variance
v^{2}
. The variance parameter
\sigma^{2}
is assumed to be known. Throughout we write
\mathbb{P}
for the joint distribution of
({\bf s}_{1},Y_{1}),\ldots,({\bf s}_{n},Y_{n})
, and
\mathbb{P}_{n}
for their corresponding empirical measure.

Our goal is to estimate
(\theta_{\star},u_{\star})
using the noisy measurements. Over the last few years, physics-informed neural network (PINN) has taken the numerical pde literature by storm (
Raissi et al. (2019)
;
Yu et al. (2018)
;
Sirignano and Spiliopoulos (2018)
;
Sun et al. (2020)
, and
Cuomo et al. (2022)
for an extensive review of the literature). In the setting of H
1
, the approach consists of estimating
u_{\star}
by regression while explicitly using the information that the true function
u_{\star}
is the solution of a PDE. In this paper, as typically done in the PINN literature, we resort to a sieve-based approach for estimating
u_{\star}
, i.e., we consider a sequence of increasingly complex parametric models. Hence let
\mathcal{F}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\{u_{W},\;W\in\mathbb{R}^{q}\}
be a function class, where
u_{W}:\;\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
is a function with parameter
W\in\mathbb{R}^{q}
. Due to their superior empirical performances,
u_{W}
is typically taken as a deep neural network, and we follow that practice, although our results can be applied more broadly. Given the data
\mathcal{D}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\{({\bf s}_{i},Y_{i}),\;1\leq i\leq n\}
, PINN in its frequentist formulation estimates jointly
\theta
and
W
by minimizing the loss function

\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(Y_{i}-u_{W}({\bf s}_{i})\right)^{2}+\frac{\lambda}{2}\left\{\alpha_{1}|f-\mathcal{H}_{0}u_{W}-\theta^{\textsf{T}}\mathcal{H}_{1}u_{W}|_{2}^{2}+\alpha_{2}|\mathcal{B}u_{W}|_{2}^{2}\right\},

(1.2)

for some regularization parameters
\lambda\geq 0
, and
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}\geq 0
, where
|\cdot|_{2}
denotes the
L^{2}
-norm on the appropriate function space. In this work, we approach the problem from a Bayesian perspective. Hence, starting from a standard Gaussian prior for
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and a sparsity inducing prior density
\Pi_{0}
for
W\in\mathbb{R}^{q}
(see Section
1.5
below for our choice of
\Pi_{0}
), we define the informative PINN prior distribution for
(\theta,W)
as the probability measure on
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{q}
with density proportional to

(\theta,W)\mapsto\Pi_{0}(W){\sf exp}\left(-\frac{\lambda}{2}\left\{\alpha_{1}|f-\mathcal{H}_{0}u_{W}-\theta^{\textsf{T}}\mathcal{H}_{1}u_{W}|_{2}^{2}+\alpha_{2}|\mathcal{B}u_{W}|_{2}^{2}\right\}-\frac{1}{2}\|\theta\|_{2}^{2}\right).

(1.3)

For appropriately large choices of
\lambda
, any realization
(\theta,W)
from the PINN prior distribution (
1.3
) produces
(\theta,u_{W})
that is biased toward solving the pde (
1.1
). Rigorous general results (not specific to PINN) of this flavor can be found, for instance, in
Hwang (1980)
. When
\theta
is known, realizations from the conditional distribution of
W
given
\theta
in (
1.3
) produce
u_{W}
that approximately solve the pde (
1.1
) for the given
\theta
. This corresponds to the initial PINN methodology of
Raissi et al. (2019)
.

For some convenience in the analysis, we will use the prior (
1.3
) with
\alpha_{1}=1
, and
\alpha_{2}=0
. Since the parameter
\theta
does not appear in the boundary condition, the case
\alpha_{2}>0
(although useful in practice) does not induce any fundamentally new behavior, and at the expense of more involved notations, our analysis can be easily modified to handle
\alpha_{2}>0
. Given the observed data
\mathcal{D}
, the data generating model postulated in (H
1
), and the PINN prior distribution (
1.3
), we thus consider the posterior distribution

\Pi(\mathrm{d}\theta,\mathrm{d}W|\mathcal{D})\propto\\
{\sf exp}\left(-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(Y_{i}-u_{W}({\bf s}_{i})\right)^{2}-\frac{\lambda}{2}|f-\mathcal{H}_{0}u_{W}-\theta^{\textsf{T}}\mathcal{H}_{1}u_{W}|_{2}^{2}-\frac{\|\theta\|_{2}^{2}}{2}\right)\Pi_{0}(\mathrm{d}W)\mathrm{d}\theta.

(1.4)

1.1
Main contributions

We study the behavior of the posterior distribution (
1.4
) as
n\to\infty
(and
q\to\infty
). We consider the case where the operator
\mathcal{H}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}(\mathcal{H}_{0},\mathcal{H}_{1})
is a differential operator that involves derivatives up to order
\tau
, for some
\tau>0
. Under some additional regularity conditions, we establish in Theorem
11
that when the true pde solution
u_{\star}
is a classical solution that possesses derivatives up to order
\beta\geq\tau
, the mean square error of the posterior mean of
\theta
under (
1.4
) satisfies (up to log terms that we ignore),

\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\vartheta\Pi^{(\theta)}(\mathrm{d}\vartheta|\mathcal{D})-\theta_{\star}\right\|_{2}^{2}\right]\lesssim n^{-2(\beta-\tau)/(m+2\beta)},

(1.5)

where
\Pi^{(\theta)}
is the
\theta
-marginal of (
1.4
). In fact we obtain this rate by showing that the 2-Wasserstein distance between
\Pi^{(\theta)}
(the posterior distribution of
\theta
) and the Gaussian distribution
\mathbf{N}(\theta_{\star},\lambda^{-1}\Sigma_{\star})
converges to zero at the rate given above (see Section
2
for the definition of
\Sigma_{\star}
). Our simulation results suggest that, in general, this convergence does not hold in the total variation metric. We also show in Theorem
7
that the pde solution
u_{\star}
is estimated at a rate at least as fast as the nonparametric minimax optimal rate:

\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{\mathbb{R}^{q}}\;|u_{W}-u_{\star}|_{2}^{2}\Pi^{(W)}(\mathrm{d}W|\mathcal{D})\right]\;\lesssim n^{-2\beta/(m+2\beta)},

where
\Pi^{(W)}
denotes the
W
-marginal of (
1.4
). We conjecture that the PINN posterior distribution actually converges faster than the nonparametric minimax rate. Indeed, in a limiting case of an infinitely strong PINN prior, we construct an estimator which, although computationally intractable in general, achieves the parametric rate
n^{-1/2}
(Theorem
8
).

1.2
Related work

Despite their popularity, the theoretical aspects of PINN-related methods remain under-studied.
Most of the existing theoretical literature on PINN deals with the forward problem of estimating
u
for a given
\theta
, when the
L^{2}
-norm
|\cdot|_{2}
is replaced by a Monte Carlo approximation (
Mishra and Molinaro (2022)
;
Shin et al. (2023)
;
Lu et al. (2022)
). The objective in this line of work is to derive the mean square error of the PINN solution as a function of the Monte Carlo sample size. More related to our work,
Mishra and Molinaro (2021)
considers a PINN inverse problem where the parameter of interest appears in the boundary condition. They work mainly in a noiseless (or small-magnitude noise) data regime. Furthermore, they assume a continuous space observation model, which is substantially different from the framework considered here.

Inverse problems as related to differential and partial differential equations have a long history that predates the current PINN literature (
Ramsay et al. (2007)
;
Xun et al. (2013)
;
McGoff et al. (2015)
). In particular, we note the close similarity between the PINN methodology and the approach by
Xun et al. (2013)
. The estimation rate in pde-driven inverse problems has been extensively studied in statistics in recent years (
Spokoiny (2020)
;
Giordano and Kekkonen (2020)
;
Nickl et al. (2020)
;
Monard et al. (2021)
;
de Hoop et al. (2023)
). However, none of these results are directly applicable to our setting or PINN more generally, since in this literature, the forward map is typically assumed known and is not parametrized as in PINN.

A Bayesian version of PINN was proposed and studied in (
Yang et al. (2021)
) from an empirical viewpoint, where the authors noted a certain robustness of Bayesian PINN to measurement noise. However, no theoretical analysis is proposed.

A related version of the inverse problem considered in this work assumes that
\theta
is high-dimensional and sparse (
Both et al. (2021)
;
Chen et al. (2020)
). The goal is then to estimate
\theta
under a sparsity assumption. These physics discovery problems can be viewed as high-dimensional versions of the problem investigated here. Thus, our work serves as a stepping-stone toward a theoretical understanding of high-dimensional physics discovery inverse problems.

1.4
The function class
\mathcal{F}=\{u_{W}\}

Although our results apply widely, we follow the PINN literature and focus on the case where the function class
\mathcal{F}
is constructed using neural networks. Let
\xi>0
be the depth of the network. Let
(p_{\xi},\ldots,p_{0})
be a sequence of integers representing the sizes of the layers of the network, with
p_{0}=m
, and
p_{\xi}=1
. For
1\leq i\leq\xi
, let
\mathsf{A}_{i}:\;\mathbb{R}^{p_{i}}\to\mathbb{R}^{p_{i}}

\Psi_{B,b}^{(i)}({\bf z})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\mathsf{A}_{i}(B{\bf z}+b),\;\;\;{\bf z}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{i-1}}.

(1.6)

We consider functions
u_{W}
of the form

u_{W}({\bf x})=\Psi_{W_{\xi},w_{\xi}}^{(\xi)}\circ\cdots\circ\Psi_{W_{1},w_{1}}^{(1)}({\bf x}),\;\;\;{\bf x}\in\mathbb{R}^{m},

(1.7)

with parameter

, where
W_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{i}\times p_{i-1}}
, and

, and where

is the composition of

with

. For convenience, and by vectorization we view

as an element of
\mathbb{R}^{q}
, where

q\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\sum_{i=1}^{\xi}p_{i}(1+p_{i-1}).

We assume that the activation functions
\mathsf{a}_{\ell}
are of class
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}
, which implies that
u_{W}
is of class
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}
. The activation function
\mathsf{a}_{\xi}
in the last layer is typically taken as the identity function.

1.5
The prior distribution
\Pi_{0}

We use sparsity to control the complexity of the function class
\{u_{W},\;W\in\mathbb{R}^{q}\}
. We use a version of the spike-and-slab prior distribution taken from
Atchade and Bhattacharyya (2019)
. We set
\mathcal{S}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\{0,1\}^{q}
. To construct the prior
\Pi_{0}
, we first define a pair of random vectors
(\Lambda,W)\in\mathcal{S}\times\mathbb{R}^{q}
as follows. Let
\Lambda_{j}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle i.i.d}}{{\sim}}\textbf{Ber}((1+q^{\mathsf{u}+1})^{-1})
,
1\leq j\leq q
, for some sparsity parameter
\mathsf{u}\geq 1
, where
\mathbf{Ber}(\alpha)
denotes the Bernoulli distribution with parameter
\alpha\in(0,1)
. Given
\Lambda\in\mathcal{S}
, the components of
W
are independent with joint density on
\mathbb{R}^{q}
given by

W\mapsto\prod_{j:\;\Lambda_{j}=1}\;\sqrt{\frac{1}{2\pi}}e^{-\frac{1}{2}W^{2}_{j}}\prod_{j:\;\Lambda_{j}=0}\sqrt{\frac{\rho_{0}}{2\pi}}e^{-\frac{\rho_{0}}{2}W_{j}^{2}},

for some given parameter
\rho_{0}>1
. We denote
\bar{\Pi}_{0}
the joint distribution of
(\Lambda,W)
, and we let
\Pi_{0}
(our prior on
\mathbb{R}^{q}
) be the distribution of
W\odot\Lambda
, where for
a,b\in\mathbb{R}^{q}
,
a\odot b
denotes the component-wise product of
a
and
b
. By construction, for any measurable function
f:\mathbb{R}^{q}\to\mathbb{R}
, we have

\Pi_{0}(f)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{q}}f(v)\Pi_{0}(\mathrm{d}v)=\int_{\mathcal{S}\times\mathbb{R}^{q}}f(\Lambda\odot W)\bar{\Pi}_{0}(\mathrm{d}\Lambda,\mathrm{d}W).

\lambda_{\textsf{min}}(A)
(resp.

Let

\left\langle f_{1},f_{2}\right\rangle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\int_{\Omega}f_{1}({\bf x})^{\textsf{T}}f_{2}({\bf x})\nu(\mathrm{d}{\bf x}),\;\;\mbox{ and norm }\;\;|f|_{2}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\sqrt{\left\langle f,f\right\rangle}.

For

, we set

.

We use multi-index derivatives: given

, where

. Given

where

. We set

(resp.

).

to denote absolute constants and

to denote a generic absolute constant that depends on
C_{0},C_{1},{\it etc.}
. The actual value of
C
may change from one appearance to the next. Similarly,

denote problem-dependent constants (that do not depend on the sample size

or the model size

). Specifically, these constants typically depend on the noise parameter
\sigma^{2}
, the dimension

, and the true parameter
\theta_{\star}
. We will also use
c
to denote a generic constant that depends on

, and that we do not track. The actual value of

2
Estimation rate of
u_{\star}

We maintain throughout the basic assumption that the operator
\mathcal{H}=(\mathcal{H}_{0},\mathcal{H}_{1})
is a differential operator of order
\tau>0
, in the sense that for all
\beta>\tau
, and for
u,v\in\mathcal{C}^{\beta}(\Omega)
,

\left|\mathcal{H}u-\mathcal{H}v\right|_{2}\leq C_{0}\max_{{\bf k}:\;|{\bf k}|\leq\tau}\;\left|D^{\bf k}u-D^{\bf k}v\right|_{\infty},

(2.1)

for some absolute constant
C_{0}
.

Because the pde equation is linear in
\theta
, and the log-prior is quadratic in
\theta
, it is straightforward to integrate out
\theta
from the posterior distribution (
1.4
). We first introduce some appropriate notations to do this. For
W\in\mathbb{R}^{q}
, we define
\bar{f}_{W}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}f-\mathcal{H}_{0}u_{W}
. Let
\Phi_{W}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
be the vector with
j
-th component given by

(\Phi_{W})_{j}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\left\langle\bar{f}_{W},(\mathcal{H}_{1}u_{W})_{j}\right\rangle,

and where
(\mathcal{H}_{1}u_{W})_{j}
denotes the
j
-component of
\mathcal{H}_{1}u_{W}
. We define
\Phi_{\star}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
similarly, replacing
u_{W}
by
u_{\star}
. Let
\Sigma_{W}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
given by

(\Sigma_{W})_{j,k}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\left\langle(\mathcal{H}_{1}u_{W})_{j},(\mathcal{H}_{1}u_{W})_{k}\right\rangle\;+\;\frac{1}{\lambda}\textbf{1}_{\{j=k\}},\;\;1\leq j,k\leq d.

We define
\Sigma_{\star}
similarly by replacing
u_{W}
by
u_{\star}
. Given
u\in L^{2}(\Omega,\mathbb{R},\nu)
we define

{\cal J}(u)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\min_{\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\;\left[|f-\mathcal{H}_{0}u-\theta^{\textsf{T}}\mathcal{H}_{1}u|_{2}^{2}+\frac{1}{\lambda}\|\theta\|_{2}^{2}\right].

(2.2)

It is easy to check that
{\cal J}(u_{W})=|\bar{f}_{W}|_{2}^{2}-\Phi_{W}^{\textsf{T}}\Sigma_{W}^{-1}\Phi_{W}
, and we can integrate out
\theta
from the posterior distribution (
1.4
) to obtain the marginal distribution of
W
given by

\Pi(W|\mathcal{D})\propto\frac{\Pi_{0}(W)}{\sqrt{{\sf det}(\Sigma_{W})}}{\sf exp}\left(-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(Y_{i}-u_{W}({\bf s}_{i}))^{2}-\frac{\lambda}{2}{\cal J}(u_{W})\right).

(2.3)

At times, when the distinction is needed we will write
\Pi^{(W)}
to denote (
2.3
).
We make the following basic assumption on the function class
\mathcal{F}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\{u_{W},\;W\in\mathbb{R}^{q}\}
.

H 2
.

1.

For all
W_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{q}
, and
r>0
, we can find
L_{W_{0},r}\geq 1
such that if
W_{1},W_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{q}
satisfy
\max(\|W_{1}-W_{0}\|_{2},\|W_{2}-W_{0}\|_{2})\leq r
, then it holds

\left|u_{W_{1}}-u_{W_{2}}\right|_{\infty}\leq L_{W_{0},r}\;\|W_{1}-W_{2}\|_{2}.

2.

For all
W\in\mathbb{R}^{q}
,
u_{W}
has derivatives to the order
\tau
, and there exists
\kappa>0
and a constant
c_{1}
such that for all
W_{1},W_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{q}
, such that
|u_{W_{1}}-u_{W_{2}}|_{\infty}\leq\varepsilon
, we have

\max_{{\bf k}:\;|{\bf k}|\leq\tau}\;\left|D^{\bf k}u_{W_{1}}-D^{\bf k}u_{W_{2}}\right|_{\infty}\leq c_{1}\varepsilon^{\kappa}.

Remark 2
.

The local Lipschitz condition imposed in H
2
-(1) is known to hold for most deep neural net architectures with Lipschitz activation functions (see e.g. Proposition 6 of
Taheri et al. (2021)
). Specifically, if
u_{W}
is a deep neural network as constructed in Section

with 1-Lipschitz activation functions, then with

satisfying

, Proposition 6 of
Taheri et al. (2021)
and some easy calculations yield

with

L_{W_{0},r}=\left(\sup_{{\bf x}\in\Omega}\|{\bf x}\|_{2}\right)\;\sqrt{\xi}\;\left(1+\frac{(\|W_{0}\|_{2}+r)^{2}}{\xi}\right)^{\xi},

(2.4)

where

is the depth of the DL function.

Remark 3
.

Since we aim to solve pdes, it is natural to require the function

to be smooth. H

-(2) further requires a Holder-type assumption on the differential operator

over the function class

. This assumption is less common in the literature, and we check its validity in Theorem

.

Let
\epsilon:\;{\mathbb{N}}\to\mathbb{R}
be a nonincreasing function such that for all

\min\left\{|u_{W}-u_{\star}|_{\infty},\;W\in\mathbb{R}^{q}:\;\|W\|_{0}\leq s\right\}\leq\epsilon(s).

(2.5)

\epsilon(s)
defines the approximation skills of the function class

at the sparsity level

at which the approximation error

matches the statistical error:

\epsilon(s)\leq\sigma\sqrt{\frac{\mathsf{u}s\log(q)}{n}}.

(2.6)

We make the following assumption.

H 3
.

There exists
\epsilon:\;{\mathbb{N}}\to\mathbb{R}
such that (
2.5
) holds. Given

and

, let

be the smallest integer satisfying (

\epsilon_{0}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\epsilon(s_{0})

Remark 4
.

We recall that we aim to estimate
u_{\star}
by the sieve method, i.e., we approximate
u_{\star}
by a collection of functions
\mathcal{F}\equiv\mathcal{F}_{n}
, where the
size
of the function class typically grows with the sample size
n
. The assumption that
\epsilon_{0}\to 0
as

in H

highlights the relationship between the sample size
n
and the model size: given
n
, we assume that the model
\mathcal{F}
is chosen such that its approximation error at some sparsity level
s
can match (or be smaller than) the statistical error at sparsity level
s
.

There has been a flurry of research activities in recent years to derive precise estimates on
\epsilon(s)
for various DL function classes, using various norms (
Yarotsky (2017)
;
Schmidt-Hieber (2020)
;
Lu et al. (2021)
;
Belomestny et al. (2022)
). For example for piecewise polynomial activation functions, and

as defined in Section
1.4
, it is known (see e.g.
Yarotsky and Zhevnerchuk (2020)
;
Lu et al. (2021)
) that at the sparsity level

, and depth

,
\mathcal{F}
can approximate a function

with precision
(1/s)^{\beta/m}
.
This yields

\epsilon(s)\lesssim(1/s)^{\beta/m}.

In that case, solving for
s_{0}
in (

) yields

s_{0}\sim\left(\frac{n}{\log(q)}\right)^{\frac{m}{m+2\beta}},\;\mbox{ and }\;\;\epsilon_{0}\sim\left(\frac{\log(q)}{n}\right)^{\frac{\beta}{m+2\beta}}.

In the high-dimensional setting considered here where
q
is typically much larger than

, (
2.8
) is typically satisfied.

Ultimately, we need some assumptions on the stability of the pde operator
\mathcal{H}
as presented below:

H 4
.

With

-(2), there exists an absolute constant

Given

,

To highlight this point, we note that if a function

(2.11)

(2.12)

2.1
Minimax nonparametric rate

Bounding the metric entropy of the function class
\mathcal{F}_{s}
in a way that leverages the pde information
{\cal J}(u_{W})\leq Cn\epsilon_{0}^{2}/(\lambda\sigma^{2})
has proved challenging. However, we note that
\mathcal{F}_{s}\subseteq\{u_{W}\in\mathcal{F}:\;\|W\|_{0}\leq s,\;\|W\|_{\infty}\leq\tau_{s}\}
, and the metric entropy of this latter set is straightforward to bound using H
2
-(1). See also (
Schmidt-Hieber (2020)
;
Lu et al. (2021)
). Indeed, for all
W,W^{\prime}
in the ball
\{W\in\mathbb{R}^{q}:\;\|W\|_{0}\leq s,\;\|W\|_{\infty}\leq\tau_{s}\}
,
\max(\|W\|_{2},\|W^{\prime}\|_{2})\leq s^{1/2}\tau_{s}
. Hence by H
2
-(1),
|u_{W}-u_{W^{\prime}}|_{\infty}\leq Ls^{1/2}\|W-W^{\prime}\|_{\infty}
, where
L=L_{{\bf 0},s^{1/2}\tau_{s}}
. The size of all the
(\varepsilon^{\prime}=\varepsilon/Ls^{1/2})
-cover of all the
\tau_{s}
-balls of all the
s
-sparse subspaces of
\mathbb{R}^{q}
in the infinity-norm is bounded from above by

{q\choose s}\left(1+\frac{2r_{s}}{\varepsilon^{\prime}}\right)^{s}.

It follows that

\log\mathcal{N}(\varepsilon,\mathcal{F}_{s},\|\cdot\|_{\infty})\leq s\log(q)+s\log\left(1+\frac{2s^{1/2}\tau_{s}L}{\varepsilon}\right),

and H
5
holds with

V_{1}(s)=s,\;\;\mbox{ and }\;\;V_{2}(s)=qr_{s}\left(1+2s^{1/2}L\right)\leq c\sqrt{\xi}q\left(1+s^{2}\log(q)\right)^{\xi+1/2},

for some constant
c
that depends only on
\mathsf{u}
, and
\sup_{x\in\Omega}\|{\bf x}\|_{2}
, where
\xi
denotes the depth of the neural network class. Hence, with
s=Cs_{0}
,

V_{1}(s)=s=Cs_{0},\;\mbox{ and }\;\;\log(\sqrt{n}V_{2}(s))\lesssim\log(n)+\xi\log(q),

where
\xi
is the depth of the neural network. Furthermore, we have seen in Remark
4
that if
u_{\star}
is a classical solution and
u_{\star}\in\mathcal{C}^{\beta}(\Omega)
, then for piecewise polynomial activation at depth
\xi=O(\log(p))
, we have

s_{0}\sim\left(\frac{n}{\log(q)}\right)^{\frac{m}{m+2\beta}}.

As a result, up to log terms that we ignore, we obtain the estimation rate

\textsf{r}\lesssim\sqrt{\frac{s_{0}}{n}}\lesssim\left(\frac{1}{n}\right)^{\frac{\beta}{m+2\beta}}.

Hence, Bayesian PINN estimates
u_{\star}
at least at the nonparametric minimax rate
n^{-\beta/(m+2\beta)}
, that is, at the optimal rate achievable when
u_{\star}
is known only to be
\beta
-smooth (
Stone (1982)
) without any PDE constraint. We note that our metric entropy calculations of the function class
\mathcal{F}_{s}
are rather crude, as they ignore the inductive bias induced by the PINN prior. Thus, the PINN posterior distributions likely enjoy a faster convergence rate than the abovementioned nonparametric minimax rate. To understand this, consider the limiting case when
\lambda=+\infty
, i.e., the prior put all the mass on
\{f\in L^{2}(\Omega,\mathbb{R},\nu):\;{\cal J}(u)=0\}
, that is, the set of functions that are solutions of the pde equation
\mathcal{H}_{0}u+\theta^{\textsf{T}}\mathcal{H}_{1}u=f
for some
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. In the subsequent section, we show that estimating
u_{\star}
at a parametric rate (up to a log factor) is possible in this limiting case.

2.2
Estimation at the parametric rate

We assume that
\theta_{\star}
belongs to a compact set
\Theta\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and for each
\theta\in\Theta
the pde equation (
1.1
) (including the initial/boundary condition) admits a unique solution
u_{\theta}\in\mathcal{C}^{\beta}(\Omega)
for some given and fixed
\beta
. Furthermore we assume that the map
\Gamma:\;\Theta\to\mathcal{C}^{\beta}(\Omega)
that maps
\theta\to u_{\theta}
is Lipschitz. Specifically,

H 6
.

There exists a constant
L>0
such that:

|\Gamma(\theta_{1})-\Gamma(\theta_{2})|_{\infty}\leq L\|\theta_{1}-\theta_{2}\|_{2}\ \ \forall\ \ \theta_{1},\theta_{2}\in\Theta\,.

Let
\Gamma(\Theta)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\{\Gamma(\theta),\;\theta\in\Theta\}
. Under the data generating process assumed in H
1
, we consider the constrained empirical risk minimization estimator

\hat{u}={\arg\min}_{u\in\Gamma(\Theta)}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(Y_{i}-u({\bf s}_{i})\right)^{2}\,.

(2.14)

The following theorem is proved in Section
A.7
.

Theorem 8
.

Assume H
1
, and H
6
. Then any solution
\hat{u}
of (
2.14
) satisfies

|\hat{u}-u_{\star}|_{2}^{2}=O_{p}\left(\frac{d}{n}\log{\left(\frac{n}{d}\right)}\right)\,.

It is immediate from the above theorem that
|\hat{u}-u_{\star}|_{2}=O_{p}(\sqrt{\frac{d}{n}})
(up to a log factor) which is the standard parametric rate for estimating a
d
dimensional parameter. The estimator
\hat{u}
is typically not computable since it requires solving the pde for each
\theta\in\Theta
. But the rate in Theorem
8
, in light of Theorem
6
, suggests that the PINN posterior distribution may actually have a convergence rate faster than the nonparametric minimax rate. More research is needed on this issue.

Regarding Assumption
6
, one may replace the
L_{2}
norm in the assumption by any
L_{p}
norm without hurting the rate, but to translate the complexity of
\Theta
to
\Gamma(\Theta)
, we believe that some sort of smoothness assumption is necessary. Whether one can do entirely without Assumption
6
is an intriguing question that we leave for future research.

2.3
Checking Assumption H
2
-(2)

We end this section with a result showing that sufficiently smooth functions typically satisfy H
2
-(2). The proof is given in Section
A.6
. We recall that
\Omega
is a bounded open subset of
\mathbb{R}^{m}
, and we write
\mathbf{B}(x,\alpha)
to denote the Euclidean ball of
\mathbb{R}^{m}
centered at
x
and with radius
\alpha
. We define
\textsf{int}_{\alpha}(\Omega)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\{x\in\Omega:\;\mathbf{B}(x,\alpha)\subset\Omega\}
. We recall also that
\mathcal{C}^{\beta}(\Omega,b)
denotes the ball with radius
b
in the Holder space
\mathcal{C}^{\beta}(\Omega)
.

Theorem 9
.

Given
\varepsilon>0
,
\beta>1
, integer
\tau\in[0,\beta)
, and
M>0
, define

\bar{\alpha}=\left(\frac{\tau\varepsilon\lfloor\beta\rfloor!}{2M(\beta-\tau)}\right)^{1/\beta}.

For all
u,\tilde{u}\in\mathcal{C}^{\beta}(\Omega,M)
such that
\sup_{x\in\Omega}\;|u(x)-\tilde{u}(x)|_{\infty}\leq\varepsilon
, and for all multi-index
{\bf k}=(k_{1},\ldots,k_{m})
, with
|{\bf k}|\leq\tau
, we have

\sup_{x\in\mathsf{int}_{\bar{\alpha}}(\Omega)}\;\left|D^{\bf k}u(x)-D^{\bf k}\tilde{u}(x)\right|_{\infty}\leq CM^{\frac{\tau}{\beta}}\varepsilon^{\frac{\beta-\tau}{\beta}},

for some constant
C
that depends only on
\tau
and
\beta
.

Remark 10
.

We note that
\bar{\alpha}\downarrow 0
as
M\uparrow\infty
. Hence if
u,\tilde{u}\in\mathcal{C}^{\beta}(\bar{\Omega},M_{0})
for some well-chosen
\bar{\Omega}\supset\Omega
, and
\sup_{x\in\bar{\Omega}}\;|u(x)-\tilde{u}(x)|_{\infty}\leq\varepsilon
then by taking
M\geq M_{0}
large enough such that
\Omega\subset\mathsf{int}_{\bar{\alpha}}(\bar{\Omega})
, we get

\sup_{x\in\Omega}\;\left|D^{\bf k}u(x)-D^{\bf k}\tilde{u}(x)\right|_{\infty}\leq CM^{\frac{\tau}{\beta}}\varepsilon^{\frac{\beta-\tau}{\beta}}.

3
A Bernstein-von-Mises theorem for the
\theta
-marginal

Given
\mu\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and
\Lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
symmetric and positive definite, we write
\textbf{N}(\mu,\Lambda)(\cdot)
as the probability measure of the Gaussian distribution
\textbf{N}(\mu,\Lambda)
. Given
W\in\mathbb{R}^{q}
, let

\hat{\theta}_{W}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\Sigma_{W}^{-1}\Phi_{W}.

(3.1)

It is then easy to see from (
1.4
) that the conditional distribution of
\theta
given
W
is
\mathbf{N}(\hat{\theta}_{W},\Sigma_{W}^{-1}/\lambda)
. For clarity’s sake we will write
\Pi^{(\theta)}
(resp.
\Pi^{(W)}
) to denote the marginal distribution of
\theta
(resp.
W
) under (
1.4
). We have

\Pi^{(\theta)}(\cdot|\mathcal{D})=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{q}}\left[\mathbf{N}\left(\hat{\theta}_{W},\frac{1}{\lambda}\Sigma_{W}^{-1}\right)(\cdot)\right]\Pi^{(W)}(\mathrm{d}W|\mathcal{D}).

We set

\Pi^{(\theta)}_{\star}(\cdot)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\mathbf{N}\left(\theta_{\star},\frac{1}{\lambda}\Sigma_{\star}^{-1}\right)(\cdot).

(3.2)

We investigate the proximity between
\Pi^{(\theta)}
and
\Pi_{\star}^{(\theta)}
for
n
large, using the 2-Wasserstein metric that we denote
\mathsf{W}_{2}
. The following result is established in Section
A.5
.

Theorem 11
.

Assume H
1
-H
5
and the notations of Theorem
7
. Then, for all
n
large enough, we have

\mathbb{E}\left[\mathsf{W}_{2}^{2}(\Pi^{(\theta)},\Pi^{(\theta)}_{\star})\right]\leq c\left(\textsf{r}^{2\kappa}+\lambda e^{-n\epsilon_{0}^{2}/(2\sigma^{2})}+\lambda e^{-V_{1}\log(V_{2}\sqrt{n})/C}\right),

for some constant
c
. In particular,

\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}u\Pi^{(\theta)}(\mathrm{d}u|\mathcal{D})-\theta_{\star}\right|^{2}\right]\leq c\left(\textsf{r}^{2\kappa}+\lambda e^{-n\epsilon_{0}^{2}/(2\sigma^{2})}+\lambda e^{-V_{1}\log(V_{2}\sqrt{n})/C}\right).

For DL functions with sufficiently smooth piecewise polynomial activation functions, and for
u_{\star}\in\mathcal{C}^{\beta}(\Omega)
, we have seen above that
\textsf{r}\lesssim n^{-\beta/(m+2\beta)}
, and by Theorem
9
,
\kappa=(\beta-\tau)/\beta
. With these we conclude from Theorem
11
that the estimation rate of
\theta_{\star}
afforded by B-PINN is at least
n^{-2(\beta-\tau)/(m+2\beta)}
. This estimation rate corresponds to the optimal plug-in rate. Recall that our pde equation is linear in
\theta
. Therefore, the bottleneck in estimating
\theta
is the difficulty of estimating
u
and its derivatives up to order
\tau
. As shown above, for a function
u\in\mathcal{C}^{\beta}(\Omega)
, PINN estimates these derivatives at the nonparametric minimax rate
n^{-2(\beta-\tau)/(m+2\beta)}
. Hence, the obtained estimation rate for
\theta_{\star}
. As conjectured above, PINN likely estimates
u_{\star}
at a rate faster than the nonparametric minimax rate. If true, this would translate to a faster estimation rate for
\theta_{\star}
. More research is needed on this point.

4
Numerical illustration

We illustrate the results above using the one-dimensional heat equation of Example
1
. Extensive illustrations of PINN and Bayesian PINN can be found in the literature (
Raissi et al. (2019)
;
Yang et al. (2021)
;
Cuomo et al. (2022)
). We focus here on comparing PINN and an approach that does not directly exploit the PDE structure of the problem. We consider the heat equation with
L=\pi
, and
T=1
. Hence
\Omega=(0,1)\times(0,\pi)
. We use the boundary condition
u(t,0)=u(t,\pi)=0
, and the initial condition
u(0,x)=\sin(x)
. Given
\theta
the heat equation has a unique solution
u_{\theta}(t,x)=\sin(x){\sf exp}(-\theta t)
. We set the true value of
\theta
to
\theta_{\star}=0.5
.
The observed data
\mathcal{D}
is generated as follows:
n
sensor locations
\{{\bf s}_{i}=(t_{i},x_{i}),\;1\leq i\leq n\}
are evenly distributed in
\Omega
, yielding measurements

Y_{i}=u_{\theta_{\star}}({\bf s}_{i})+\sigma\epsilon_{i},\mbox{ where }\;\epsilon_{i}\sim\mathbf{N}(0,1),

for some noise parameter
\sigma
that we control. For the function class
\mathcal{F}=\{u_{W}\}
,
u_{W}:\;\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
is taken to be a fully connected neural network with depth
4
, width
64
, and
tanh
activation function. The resulting posterior distribution is

\Pi(\mathrm{d}\theta,\mathrm{d}W|\mathcal{D})\propto{\sf exp}\left(-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(Y_{i}-u_{W}({\bf s}_{i})\right)^{2}-\frac{\lambda}{2}\ell_{0}(\theta,W)-\frac{1}{2}\|\theta\|_{2}^{2}\right)\Pi_{0}(\mathrm{d}W)\mathrm{d}\theta,

where

\ell_{0}(\theta,W)=|(u_{W})_{t}-\theta(u_{W})_{xx}|_{2}^{2}+|u_{W}(\cdot,0)|_{2}^{2}+|u_{W}(\cdot,\pi)|_{2}^{2}+|u_{W}(0,\cdot)-\sin(\cdot)|_{2}^{2},

(4.1)

where
|\cdot|_{2}
denotes the function space
L^{2}
-norm. Observe that
\lambda=0
in the above posterior corresponds to the estimation of
W
without the physics-informed prior (non-PINN estimation). Before presenting the numerical results, we describe briefly our MCMC sampling method.

4.1
Approximation and MCMC sampling

In general, the
L^{2}
-norm in the loss (
4.1
) is intractable and is typically replaced by a Monte Carlo or a numerical quadrature approximation. Here we use Monte Carlo by drawing
N=10,000
interior points
({\bf s}_{1}^{(i)},\ldots,{\bf s}^{(i)}_{N})
uniformly in
\Omega
to estimate the
L^{2}
norm on
\Omega
. For the
L^{2}
norm along the boundaries we draw two sets of
B=128
points
({\bf s}_{1}^{(b_{i})},\ldots,{\bf s}^{(b_{i})}_{B})
for
i=1,2
uniformly on the time and space boundaries respectively. Hence, we consider the approximate posterior distribution

\widehat{\Pi}(\theta,W|\mathcal{D})\propto\Pi_{0}(W)\\
{\sf exp}\left(-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(y_{i}-u_{W}({\bf s}_{i})\right)^{2}-\frac{\lambda}{2}\widehat{\ell}_{0}(\theta,W)-\frac{\rho}{2}\|\theta\|_{2}^{2}\right).

(4.2)

where the loss (
4.1
) is replaced by

\widehat{\ell}_{0}(\theta,W)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\left((u_{W})_{t}({\bf s}_{k}^{(i)})-\theta\times(u_{W})_{xx}({\bf s}_{k}^{(i)})\right)^{2}\\
+\frac{1}{B}\sum_{k=1}^{B}\left(u_{W}({\bf s}^{(b_{1})}_{k},0)\right)^{2}+\frac{1}{B}\sum_{k=1}^{B}\left(u_{W}({\bf s}^{(b_{1})}_{k},\pi)\right)^{2}+\frac{1}{B}\sum_{k=1}^{B}\left(u_{W}(0,{\bf s}_{k}^{(b_{2})})-\sin({\bf s}_{k}^{(b_{2})})\right)^{2}.

(4.3)

Given
W
, and as seen in Section
3
, the posterior conditional distribution of
\theta
given
W
is

\theta|W,\mathcal{D}\sim\mathbf{N}\left(\Sigma_{W}^{-1}\Phi_{W},\frac{1}{\lambda}\Sigma_{W}^{-1}\right)

(4.4)

where in the particular case of this example

\Sigma_{W}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}(u_{W})_{xx}(\mathbf{s}_{k}^{(i)})^{2}+\frac{\rho}{\lambda},\;\;\mbox{ and }\;\;\Phi_{W}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}(u_{W})_{t}(\mathbf{s}_{k}^{(i)})\times(u_{W})_{xx}(\mathbf{s}_{k}^{(i)})

We sample from (
4.2
) using the approximate asynchronous sampler of
Atchade and Wang (2023)
. The algorithm is a data-augmentation Metropolis-with-Gibbs sampler where the update of the sparsity support
\Lambda
given
\theta,W
uses asynchronous sampling, and the update of
W
given
\theta,\Lambda
is a sparse stochastic gradient Langevin dynamics. Then
\theta

W,\Lambda

\mathbf{N}\left(\Sigma_{W_{\Lambda}}^{-1}\Phi_{W_{\Lambda}},\frac{1}{\lambda}\Sigma_{W_{\Lambda}}^{-1}\right)
using (
4.4
) where
W_{\Lambda}
is the component-wise product of
W
and
\Lambda
(a sparse neural network weight). A
Pytorch
implementation is available from the GitHub page
https://github.com/xliu-522/SA-cSGLD
.

Throughout the experiment, we use
\theta^{*}=0.5
,
\lambda=n
and
\rho=1
. For each instance of the posterior distribution (
4.2
) under consideration, we run the aforementioned MCMC sampler until convergence and keep running another
200,000
iterations. We then record every 20th sample, resulting in a total of
K=10,000
samples, denoted by
\{\hat{\theta}^{(k)}\}_{k=1}^{K}
. The mean and standard deviations of the marginal posterior distribution of
\theta
are then approximated respectively by

\mu_{\hat{\theta}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\hat{\theta}^{(k)},\;\mbox{ and }\;\;\sigma_{\hat{\theta}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\sqrt{\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\left(\hat{\theta}^{(k)}-\mu_{\hat{\theta}}\right)^{2}}.

In this section, assuming that the MCMC sampler has converged, we take the distribution of the samples
\{\hat{\theta}^{(k)}\}_{k=1}^{K}
to be
\Pi^{(\theta)}
and we denote its normal approximation by
\tilde{\Pi}^{(\theta)}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\mathbf{N}(\mu_{\hat{\theta}},\sigma^{2}_{\hat{\theta}})
.
Since the exact solution
u_{\star}(t,x)=\sin(x){\sf exp}(-0.5t)
is known, we can compute the variance of the limiting Gaussian distribution in Bernstein-von Mises theorem (Theorem
11

\Sigma_{*}=\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{\pi}\left((u_{\star})_{xx}(t,x)\right)^{2}dxdt+\frac{1}{n}=\frac{\pi(1-\frac{1}{e})}{2}+\frac{1}{n}\approx 0.993+\frac{1}{n}

Hence the limiting distribution in Theorem
11
denoted
\Pi_{\star}^{(\theta)}
is

\Pi_{\star}^{(\theta)}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\mathbf{N}(\theta_{\star},\frac{1}{n}\Sigma_{\star}^{-1}).

(4.5)

4.3
Posterior contraction Behavior

From the previous section and Table
1
, we observed that the
W_{2}
distance between
\Pi^{(\theta)}
and the limiting distribution
\Pi_{\star}^{(\theta)}
given in (
4.5
) decays significantly as the sample size
n
increases, which is consistent with the conclusion of Theorem
11
. To further study this contraction behavior, we plot the histogram of
\Pi^{(\theta)}
(using samples from the MCMC sampler), its Gaussian approximation
\tilde{\Pi}^{(\theta)}=\textbf{N}(\mu_{\hat{\theta}},\sigma^{2}_{\hat{\theta}})
where
\mu_{\hat{\theta}}
and
\sigma_{\hat{\theta}}
are computed from the MCMC samples, and the limiting distribution
\Pi_{\star}^{(\theta)}
, for various sample sizes (
n=500
,
1000
,
5000
,
10000
) at a noise level of 10% (
\sigma\approx 0.025
). The perfect match between
\Pi^{(\theta)}
and
\tilde{\Pi}^{(\theta)}
in Figure
3
suggests that the posterior distribution of
\theta
is approximately Gaussian. However the persistent discrepancy between
\Pi^{(\theta)}
and
\Pi_{\star}^{(\theta)}
suggests that the
W_{2}
convergence to zero between
\Pi^{(\theta)}
and
\Pi_{\star}^{(\theta)}
(as established in Theorem
11
and illustrated above), likely do not hold in total variation. For two probability measures
P,Q
with densities
p,q
respectively, their total variation distance is
\text{TVD}(P,Q)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\frac{1}{2}\int|p(x)-q(x)|\,dx
. To further illustrate this point we compute in Table
2
a lower and an upper on the total variation distance between
\tilde{\Pi}^{(\theta)}
and
\Pi_{\star}^{(\theta)}
. We compute these bounds by noting (see
Devroye et al. (2023)
) that for any two probability measures
P,Q
with densities
p,q
respectively we have

H^{2}(P,Q)\leq\text{TVD}(P,Q)\leq\min\left(1,\sqrt{KL(P\lVert Q)/2},\sqrt{KL(Q\lVert P)/2}\right),

(4.6)

where in the univariate Gaussian case where
P\sim\mathbf{N}(\mu_{1},\sigma_{1}^{2})
with density
p(x)
and
Q\sim\mathbf{N}(\mu_{2},\sigma_{2}^{2})
with density
q(x)
, the Hellinger distance
H(P,Q)
is (see e.g.
Pardo (2006)
)

H^{2}(P,Q)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\frac{1}{2}\int\left(\sqrt{p(x)}-\sqrt{q(x)}\right)^{2}\,dx=1-\sqrt{\frac{2\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}}{\sigma_{1}^{2}+\sigma_{2}^{2}}}{\sf exp}\left(-\frac{(\mu_{1}-\mu_{2})^{2}}{4(\sigma_{1}^{2}+\sigma_{2}^{2})}\right),

and the
KL
-divergence is (see e.g.
Rasmussen and Williams (2005)
)

D_{KL}(P\|Q)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\int p(x)\log\frac{p(x)}{q(x)}\,dx=\log\left(\frac{\sigma_{2}}{\sigma_{1}}\right)+\frac{\sigma_{1}^{2}+(\mu_{1}-\mu_{2})^{2}}{2\sigma_{2}^{2}}-\frac{1}{2}.

Using these formulas, the third and fourth column of Table
2
shows the left-hand side and right-hand side of (
4.6
) respectively. From this table, we see, for instance, that at
10\%
noise level, the
W_{2}
distance decreases with the sample size, whereas the TVD lower and upper bounds do not. This lack of TV convergence is because the posterior mean does not converge to
\theta_{\star}
fast enough. Indeed, given two univariate normal distributions
\textbf{N}(\mu_{1,n},\frac{1}{n})
and
\textbf{N}(\mu_{2,n},\frac{1}{n})
, as
n\to\infty
, their
W_{2}
distance converges to
0
as soon as
|\mu_{1,n}-\mu_{2,n}|\to 0
, whereas their Hellinger distance (using the formula above) converges to zero if and only if
|\mu_{1,n}-\mu_{2,n}|
converges to zero faster than
1/\sqrt{n}
.

Proof of Theorem
7

We define

\Delta_{W}(y,{\bf s})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\left(y-u_{W}({\bf s})\right)^{2},\;\;\Delta_{\star}(y,{\bf s})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\left(y-u_{\star}({\bf s})\right)^{2},\;\;(y,{\bf s})\in\mathbb{R}\times\Omega.

We recall that
\mathbb{P}_{n}(f)=n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}f(Y_{i},{\bf s}_{i})
. It follows from (
2.3
) that for any set
C\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{q}
, we have

\Pi(C|\mathcal{D})=\frac{\int_{C}{\sf exp}\left(-\frac{n}{2\sigma^{2}}\mathbb{P}_{n}(\Delta_{W}-\Delta_{\star})+\mathcal{R}_{W}\right)\Pi_{0}(\mathrm{d}W)}{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{q}}{\sf exp}\left(-\frac{n}{2\sigma^{2}}\mathbb{P}_{n}(\Delta_{W}-\Delta_{\star})+\mathcal{R}_{W}\right)\Pi_{0}(\mathrm{d}W)},

(A.1)

where

\mathcal{R}_{W}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\mathcal{R}_{W}^{(1)}+\mathcal{R}_{W}^{(2)},\;\;\mbox{ with }\;\;\mathcal{R}_{W}^{(1)}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}-\frac{1}{2}\left(\log{\sf det}(\Sigma_{W})-\log{\sf det}(\Sigma_{\star})\right),\;\;\\
\;\;\mbox{ and }\;\;\mathcal{R}_{W}^{(2)}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}-\frac{\lambda}{2}{\cal J}(u_{W}).

We break the proof into three parts. First, in Section
A.1
, we give a lower bound on the normalizing constant of the posterior distribution as given in (
A.1
). Then we show in Section
A.2
that the prior distribution
\Pi_{0}
has a good inductive bias, as it puts a high probability on
W
that is sparse. The third part of the proof in Section
A.3
establishes some deviation bounds for the empirical process of the log-likelihood ratio. Then, we put all the pieces together in Section
A.4
.

A.1
Lower bound on the normalizing constant

Lemma 12
.

Assume H
1
-H
4
. Let
\epsilon_{0},s_{0},W_{0}
be as in H
3
. Then for all
n
large enough, with probability at least
1-2e^{-n\epsilon_{0}^{2}/(2\sigma^{2})}
, we have

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{q}}{\sf exp}\left(-\frac{n}{2\sigma^{2}}\mathbb{P}_{n}(\Delta_{W}-\Delta_{\star})+\mathcal{R}_{W}\right)\Pi_{0}(\mathrm{d}W)\geq C\times{\sf exp}\left(-\frac{Cn\epsilon_{0}^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}\right),

for some absolute constant
C
.

Proof.

By definition of
W_{0}
,
|u_{W_{0}}-u_{\star}|_{\infty}\leq\epsilon_{0}
. Let
L\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}L_{W_{0},1}\geq 1
be as in H
2
. Let
\Lambda_{0}\in\{0,1\}^{q}
denote the sparsity support of
W_{0}
, and let

\eta\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\frac{\epsilon_{0}}{Ls_{0}^{1/2}},\;\;\mbox{ and }\;\;\mathcal{V}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\{W\in\mathbb{R}^{q}:\;\mathsf{supp}(W)=\Lambda_{0},\;\|W-W_{0}\|_{\infty}\leq\eta\}.

(A.2)

Since
\eta s_{0}^{1/2}\leq 1
, for all
W\in\mathcal{V}
, we have

\|W-W_{0}\|_{2}\leq s_{0}^{1/2}\|W-W_{0}\|_{\infty}\leq s_{0}^{1/2}\eta\leq 1.

(A.3)

Using (
A.3
), and the Lipschitz assumption imposed in H
2
, we deduce that for all
W\in\mathcal{V}
,

|u_{W}-u_{W_{0}}|_{\infty}\leq L\|W-W_{0}\|_{2}\leq Ls_{0}^{1/2}\eta\leq\epsilon_{0}.

(A.4)

From the above display, and appealing to (
2.1
) and H
2
-(3) we further deduce that for all
W\in\mathcal{V}
,

|\mathcal{H}u_{W}-\mathcal{H}u_{W_{0}}|_{2}\leq C_{0}\max_{{\bf k}:\;|{\bf k}|\leq\tau}\;\left|D^{\bf k}u_{W}-D^{\bf k}u_{W_{0}}\right|_{\infty}\leq C_{0}c_{1}\epsilon_{0}^{\kappa}.

(A.5)

In view of H
4
, we also deduce that for all
W\in\mathcal{V}
,

|u_{W}-u_{\star}|_{\infty}\leq 2\epsilon_{0},\;\;\mbox{ and }\;\;|\mathcal{H}u_{W}-\mathcal{H}u_{\star}|_{2}\leq 2^{\kappa}C_{4}\epsilon_{0}^{\kappa}.

(A.6)

Step 1

First we show that with probability at least
1-2e^{-n\epsilon_{0}^{2}/(2\sigma^{2})}
, it holds

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{q}}{\sf exp}\left(-\frac{n}{2\sigma^{2}}\mathbb{P}_{n}(\Delta_{W}-\Delta_{\star})+\mathcal{R}_{W}\right)\Pi_{0}(\mathrm{d}W)\geq\frac{1}{2}e^{-4n\epsilon_{0}^{2}/\sigma^{2}}\int_{\mathcal{V}}e^{\mathcal{R}_{W}}\Pi_{0}(\mathrm{d}W).

(A.7)

To establish this, we recall that with
\xi_{i}=(Y_{i}-u_{\star}({\bf s}_{i}))/\sigma
, we have

-\frac{n}{2\sigma^{2}}\mathbb{P}_{n}(\Delta_{W}-\Delta_{\star})=-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(u_{W}({\bf s}_{i})-u_{\star}({\bf s}_{i}))^{2}+\frac{1}{\sigma}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\xi_{i}(u_{W}({\bf s}_{i})-u_{\star}({\bf s}_{i})).

Using (
A.6
), we note that for
W\in\mathcal{V}
,

\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(u_{W}({\bf s}_{i})-u_{\star}({\bf s}_{i}))^{2}\leq A,

where

A\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\frac{4n\epsilon_{0}^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}.

By Gaussian tail bounds, for all
W\in\mathcal{V}
,

\mathbb{P}\left(\frac{1}{\sigma}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\xi_{i}(u_{W}({\bf s}_{i})-u_{\star}({\bf s}_{i}))>\frac{A}{2}\;|{\bf s}_{1:n}\right)\leq{\sf exp}\left(-\frac{A^{2}}{8A}\right)\leq e^{-A/8}.

Therefore, with
\mathcal{E}_{W}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\{\mathcal{D}:\;\frac{1}{\sigma}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\xi_{i}(u_{W}({\bf s}_{i})-u_{\star}({\bf s}_{i}))\leq A/2\}
,

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{q}}{\sf exp}\left(-\frac{n}{2\sigma^{2}}\mathbb{P}_{n}(\Delta_{W}-\Delta_{\star})+\mathcal{R}_{W}\right)\Pi_{0}(\mathrm{d}W)\geq e^{-A}\int_{\mathcal{V}}\textbf{1}_{\mathcal{E}_{W}}(\mathcal{D})e^{\mathcal{R}_{W}}\Pi_{0}(\mathrm{d}W),

and with
\mathcal{E}_{W}^{c}
denoting the complement of
\mathcal{E}_{W}
, we obtain

\mathbb{P}\left[\int_{\mathbb{R}^{q}}{\sf exp}\left(-\frac{n}{2\sigma^{2}}\mathbb{P}_{n}(\Delta_{W}-\Delta_{\star})+\mathcal{R}_{W}\right)\Pi_{0}(\mathrm{d}W)<\frac{e^{-A}}{2}\int_{\mathcal{V}}e^{\mathcal{R}_{W}}\Pi_{0}(\mathrm{d}W)\;|{\bf s}_{1:n}\right]\\
\leq\mathbb{P}\left[\int_{\mathcal{V}}\textbf{1}_{\mathcal{E}_{W}}(\mathcal{D})e^{\mathcal{R}_{W}}\Pi_{0}(\mathrm{d}W)<\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathcal{V}}e^{\mathcal{R}_{W}}\Pi_{0}(\mathrm{d}W)\;|{\bf x}_{1:n}\right]\\
=\mathbb{P}\left[\int_{\mathcal{V}}\textbf{1}_{\mathcal{E}_{W}^{c}}(\mathcal{D})e^{\mathcal{R}_{W}}\Pi_{0}(\mathrm{d}W)>\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathcal{V}}e^{\mathcal{R}_{W}}\Pi_{0}(\mathrm{d}W)\;|\;{\bf x}_{1:n}\right]\\
\leq\frac{2}{\int_{\mathcal{V}}e^{\mathcal{R}_{W}}\Pi_{0}(\mathrm{d}W)}\int_{\mathcal{V}}\mathbb{P}\left(\frac{1}{\sigma}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\xi_{i}(u_{W}({\bf s}_{i})-u_{\star}({\bf s}_{i}))>\frac{A}{2}\;|\;{\bf s}_{1:n}\right)e^{\mathcal{R}_{W}}\Pi_{0}(\mathrm{d}W)\\
\leq 2e^{-A/8},

which is (
A.7
).

Step 2

We now show that

\int_{\mathcal{V}}e^{\mathcal{R}_{W}}\Pi_{0}(\mathrm{d}W)\geq c\times{\sf exp}\left(-C\frac{n\epsilon_{0}^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}\right).

(A.8)

Using the definition of
\Sigma_{W}
, we first note that for all
W_{1},W_{2}

\displaystyle\|\Sigma_{W_{1}}-\Sigma_{W_{2}}\|_{\textsf{op}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sqrt{2}\left(|\mathcal{H}u_{W_{1}}|_{2}+|\mathcal{H}u_{W_{2}}|_{2}\right)\;|\mathcal{H}u_{W_{1}}-\mathcal{H}u_{W_{2}}|_{2}

(A.9)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sqrt{2}\left(2|\mathcal{H}u_{W_{1}}|_{2}+|\mathcal{H}u_{W_{2}}-\mathcal{H}u_{W_{1}}|_{2}\right)|\mathcal{H}u_{W_{1}}-\mathcal{H}u_{W_{2}}|_{2}.

We combine this with (
A.5
) to obtain that for all
W\in\mathcal{V}
,

\|\Sigma_{W}-\Sigma_{W_{0}}\|_{\textsf{op}}\leq 2C_{0}c_{1}\left(2|\mathcal{H}u_{W_{0}}|_{2}+C_{0}c_{1}\epsilon_{0}^{\kappa}\right)\epsilon_{0}^{\kappa}\leq\frac{C_{2}}{2},

for all
n
large enough, since
\epsilon_{0}\to 0
, as
n\to\infty
. Therefore, Weyl’s inequality, and
\lambda_{\textsf{min}}(\Sigma_{W_{0}})\geq C_{2}
imply that for all
W\in\mathcal{V}
, and
n
large enough

\lambda_{\textsf{min}}(\Sigma_{W})\geq\frac{C_{2}}{2}.

As a result of the last display, we can use a first order Taylor expansion of the
\log{\sf det}
to conclude that for all
W\in\mathcal{V}
,

|\mathcal{R}_{W}^{(1)}|=\left|\frac{1}{2}\left(\log{\sf det}(\Sigma_{W})-\log{\sf det}(\Sigma_{\star})\right)\right|\leq\frac{2d^{1/2}}{C_{2}}\left\|\Sigma_{W}-\Sigma_{\star}\right\|_{\textsf{F}}\leq\frac{2d}{C_{2}}\|\Sigma_{W}-\Sigma_{\star}\|_{\textsf{op}}\\
\leq\frac{4d}{C_{2}}\left(2|\mathcal{H}u_{\star}|_{2}+|\mathcal{H}u_{W}-\mathcal{H}u_{\star}|_{2}\right)|\mathcal{H}u_{W}-\mathcal{H}u_{\star}|_{2}\\
\leq 4\frac{2^{\kappa}C_{4}d}{C_{2}}\left(2|\mathcal{H}u_{\star}|_{2}+2^{\kappa}C_{4}\epsilon_{0}^{\kappa}\right)\epsilon_{0}^{\kappa}\leq 1,

again, for all
n
large enough. For
W\in\mathcal{V}
, let
J_{W}
be the

\{(\mathcal{H}u_{W})_{i},\;1\leq i\leq d\}
in

. Note that, since
\lambda_{\textsf{min}}(\Sigma_{W})\geq C_{2}/2
, that sub-space is isomorphic to
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and by expressing the calculation in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, it easily follows that

\left|\Phi_{W}^{\textsf{T}}\Sigma_{W}^{-1}\Phi_{W}-|J_{W}\bar{f}_{W}|_{2}^{2}\right|\leq\frac{2}{\lambda C_{2}},

where we recall that
\bar{f}_{W}=f-\mathcal{H}_{0}u_{W}
. As a result, for
W\in\mathcal{V}
,

\left|\Phi_{W}^{\textsf{T}}\Sigma_{W}^{-1}\Phi_{W}-|\bar{f}_{W}|_{2}^{2}\right|\leq\frac{2}{\lambda C_{2}}+\left||J_{W}\bar{f}_{W}|_{2}^{2}-|\bar{f}_{W}|_{2}^{2}\right|=\frac{2}{\lambda C_{2}}+\left|J_{W}\bar{f}_{W}-\bar{f}_{W}\right|_{2}^{2},

where the equality uses the fact that for all

|u|_{2}^{2}=|J_{W}u|_{2}^{2}+|(J_{W}-\mathbbm{1})u|_{2}^{2}
. By the definition of the projector as closest element, and since
\bar{f}_{W}=f-\mathcal{H}_{0}u_{W}=(\mathcal{H}_{0}u_{\star}-\mathcal{H}_{0}u_{W})+\theta_{\star}^{\textsf{T}}\mathcal{H}u_{\star}

\left|J_{W}\bar{f}_{W}-\bar{f}_{W}\right|_{2}^{2}\leq\left|\theta_{\star}^{\textsf{T}}(\mathcal{H}u_{W})-\bar{f}_{W}\right|_{2}^{2}\leq 2\left|\mathcal{H}_{0}u_{\star}-\mathcal{H}_{0}u_{W}\right|_{2}^{2}+2\left|\theta_{\star}^{\textsf{T}}(\mathcal{H}u_{W})-\theta_{\star}^{\textsf{T}}(\mathcal{H}u_{\star})\right|_{2}^{2}\\
\leq C\left(1+\|\theta_{\star}\|_{2}^{2}\right)\epsilon_{0}^{2\kappa},

for some absolute constant

that depends only on

and

. We conclude that for

A.3

The last ingredient of the proof is a concentration inequality for the empirical process of the log-likelihood ratio that we derive next. The proof is based on Theorem 5.11 of

\mathbb{E}[|X|^{m}]\leq\frac{1}{2}m!K^{m-2}R^{2}\ \ \forall\ \ m=2,3,\dots

It is immediate that if a random variable

n=\mathcal{N}({\epsilon}/L,\Theta,L_{2})
. Now consider the images corresponding to these
\theta
’s, meaning,
\{\gamma_{1},\dots,\gamma_{n}\}
where
\gamma_{i}=\Gamma(\theta_{i})
. Then we have:

\sup_{\theta\in\Theta}\min_{1\leq j\leq n}\|\Gamma(\theta_{j})-\Gamma(\theta)\|_{\infty}\leq L\sup_{\theta\in\Theta}\min_{1\leq j\leq n}\|\theta_{j}-\theta\|_{2}\leq L({\epsilon}/L)={\epsilon}\,.

Hence, the proof of the lemma is complete with
c=3L
.

Proof of Theorem
8
.

We use the standard rate theorem (e.g., see 3.2.5 of
Vaart and Wellner (1997)
) to establish the convergence rate of
|\hat{u}-u_{\star}|_{2}^{2}
. Define, for simplicity,
M(u)=\mathbb{E}[(Y-u({\bf s}))^{2}]
and
M_{n}(u)=(1/n)\sum_{i}(Y_{i}-u({\bf s}_{i}))^{2}
. Therefore, from (
2.14
) we have
\hat{u}
is the minimizer of
M_{n}(u)
over
\Gamma(\Theta)
. Furthermore, we have:

M(u)-M(u_{\star})=|u-u_{\star}|_{2}^{2}\,.

To establish the rate of convergence, we need to find the modulus of continuity function
\phi_{n}(\delta)
that satisfies:

\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{|u-u_{\star}|_{2}\leq\delta}\left|\left(M_{n}(u)-M(u)\right)-\left(M_{n}(u_{\star})-M(u_{\star})\right)\right|\right]\leq\frac{\phi_{n}(\delta)}{\sqrt{n}}\,.

Let
\xi_{i}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}(Y_{i}-u_{\star}({\bf s}_{i}))/\sigma
. Some simple algebra yields:

\displaystyle\left|\left(M_{n}(u)-M(u)\right)-\left(M_{n}(u_{\star})-M(u_{\star})\right)\right|

\displaystyle=\left|(\mathbb{P}_{n}-\mathbb{P})(u({\bf s})-u_{\star}({\bf s}))^{2}+\sigma(\mathbb{P}_{n}-\mathbb{P})\xi(u({\bf s})-u_{\star}({\bf s}))\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left|(\mathbb{P}_{n}-\mathbb{P})(u({\bf s})-u_{\star}({\bf s}))^{2}\right|+\sigma\left|(\mathbb{P}_{n}-\mathbb{P})\xi(u({\bf s})-u_{\star}({\bf s}))\right|

Therefore, by symmetrization we have (using
(\eta_{1},\dots,\eta_{n})
i.i.d Rademacher random variables):

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{|u-u_{\star}|_{2}\leq\delta}\left|\left(M_{n}(u)-M(u)\right)-\left(M_{n}(u_{\star})-M(u_{\star})\right)\right|\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{|u-u_{\star}|_{2}\leq\delta}\left|(\mathbb{P}_{n}-\mathbb{P})(u({\bf s})-u_{\star}({\bf s}))^{2}\right|\right]+\sigma\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{|u-u_{\star}|_{2}\leq\delta}\left|(\mathbb{P}_{n}-\mathbb{P})\xi(u({\bf s})-u_{\star}({\bf s}))\right|\right]

\displaystyle\leq 2\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{|u-u_{\star}|_{2}\leq\delta}\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i}\eta_{i}(u({\bf s}_{i})-u_{\star}({\bf s}_{i}))^{2}\right|\right]+\sigma\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{|u-u_{\star}|_{2}\leq\delta}\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i}\xi_{i}(u({\bf s}_{i})-u_{\star}({\bf s}_{i}))\right|\right]

\displaystyle\leq 2B\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{|u-u_{\star}|_{2}\leq\delta}\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i}\eta_{i}(u({\bf s}_{i})-u_{\star}({\bf s}_{i}))\right|\right]+\sigma\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{|u-u_{\star}|_{2}\leq\delta}\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i}\xi_{i}(u({\bf s}_{i})-u_{\star}({\bf s}_{i}))\right|\right]

\displaystyle\leq C_{1}\left(\delta\sqrt{\frac{d}{n}\log{\left(\frac{C_{2}}{\delta}\right)}}+\frac{d}{n}\log{\left(\frac{C_{2}}{\delta}\right)}\right)

Here, in the second last inequality, we use Leduox-Talagrand contraction inequality. The last line follows Dudley’s chaining bound and some standard calculations (e.g., proof of Theorem 8.7 of
Sen (2018)
). Therefore, a valid choice of
\phi_{n}(\delta)
is:

\phi_{n}(\delta)=C_{1}\left(\delta\sqrt{d\log{\left(\frac{C_{2}}{\delta}\right)}}+\frac{d}{\sqrt{n}}\log{\left(\frac{C_{2}}{\delta}\right)}\right)\,.

Therefore, we conclude that
\|\hat{u}-u_{\star}\|_{2}=O_{p}(\delta_{n})
where
\delta_{n}
satisfies:

\sqrt{n}\delta_{n}^{2}\geq\phi_{n}(\delta_{n})\,.

Some simple algebra yields that a valid choice for
\delta_{n}
is
\sqrt{(d/n)\log{(n/d)}}
, which completes the proof.
