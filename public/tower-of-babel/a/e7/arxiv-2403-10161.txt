Let

Lemma 2.6
.

Let
R
be a
*
-ring. Then

(1)

for any
x\in Z^{*}(R)
and positive integer
k
,
N(x)\subseteq N(x^{k})
.

(2)

For any
x\in Z^{*}(R)
and positive integer
k
,
N(x^{k})\subseteq N(x^{k+1})
.

(3)

If
x
is a non-zero nilpotent element then
N(x)=N(x^{k})=Z^{*}(R)
for any positive integer
k
.

(4)

If
x
is a potent element (i.e.,
x^{m}=x~\text{for some positive integer m})
then
N(x)=N(x^{k})
for any positive integer
k
.

Proof.

(1)

Let
y
be adjacent to
x
in
\Gamma^{\prime}(R)
. Then
x^{n}y^{*}=0
or
y^{n}x^{*}
for some positive integer
n
. Therefore
(x^{k})^{n}y^{*}=0
or
y^{n}(x^{k})^{*}=0
. Hence

y
is adjacent to
x^{k}
. Thus
N(x)\subseteq N(x^{k})
for any positive integer
k
.

(2)

Let
y\in N(x^{k})
. Then there exists positive integer
i
such that
(x^{k+1})^{i}y^{*}=x^{i}(x^{k})^{i}y^{*}=0
or
0=y^{i}(x^{k})^{*}=y^{i}(x^{k+1})^{*}
. Therefore
y\in N(x^{k+1})
. Hence
N(x^{k})\subseteq N(x^{k+1})
.

(3)

If
x
is a non-zero nilpotent element then there exists positive integer
s
such that
x^{s}=0
. Thus
x^{s}y^{*}=0
and
(x^{k})^{s}y^{*}=0
. Therefore all vertices
y
are adjacent to
x^{k}
, for any positive integer
k
. Hence
N(x)=N(x^{k})=Z^{*}(R)
for any positive integer
k
.

(4)

Let
k
be any positive integer and
x^{m}=x
for some positive integer
m
.
If
k\leq m
, then by (2),
N(x^{k})\subseteq N(x^{m})=N(x)\subseteq N(x^{k})
. Thus
N(x)=N(x^{k})
.

Suppose
m<k
. Note that
x^{2m}=x^{m}x^{m}=x^{2}
. Therefore
N(x)\subseteq N(x^{2m})=N(x^{2})\subseteq N(x^{m})=N(x)
. Hence
N(x^{2m})=N(x)
. Inductively, we can show that
N(x^{k})=N(x)
for any positive integer
k
.

Let

Theorem 2.17

Let

Then by Caley-Hamilton theorem,

Therefore

then

, otherwise

is not a nilpotent element.
If

is a nilpotent element then all vertices in

are adjacent to

. Suppose

is not a nilpotent element and

is any non-nilpotent element, then
A
and
B
are adjacent if and only if there exists positive integer

. Thus

if and only if

. Hence

if and only if they are adjacent in
\Gamma_{1}
. A nilpotent element in

is adjacent to all other elements in
\Gamma^{\prime}(R)
. Thus
\Gamma^{\prime}(R)
is a
K_{2}
-join of
\Gamma_{1}
and
K_{|N|}
.
∎

3.
Connectedness and Completeness of
\Gamma^{\prime}(R)

In this section, we give a characterizations for completeness and connectedness of
\Gamma^{\prime}(R)
.
In the following lemma, we identify nilpotent elements and projections in
R
from
\Gamma^{\prime}(R)
.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
R
be a
*
-ring with unity and
a\in Z^{*}(R)
be an element that is adjacent to all other elements of
Z^{*}(R)
, then
a
is a nilpotent element or a projection. Further if
a
is a projection then it is orthogonal to all other non-trivial projections.

Proof.

Suppose
a\in Z^{*}(R)
and
a
and
x
are adjacent for any
x\in Z^{*}(R)
. If
a
is a nilpotent element then we are done. Assume
a
is not a nilpotent element. We will show
a=a^{*}=a^{2}
. If
a\neq a^{*}
then
a^{2}=a(a^{*})^{*}=0
. Therefore
a
is nilpotent, a contradiction. Hence
a=a^{*}
. Now, we will show
a^{2}=a
. If
a^{2}\neq a
and
a^{2}\neq 0
then
a^{3}=a(a^{2})^{*}=aa^{2}=0
. Therefore
a
is nilpotent, a contradiction. Hence
a^{2}=a
. Thus
a
is a projection.

Let
e
be a projection that is adjacent to all vertices in
\Gamma^{\prime}(R)
. Note that for any other nontrivial projection
f
in
R
,
ef=ef^{*}=0=fe
, because
e
is adjacent to
f
. Thus
e
is orthogonal to
f
.
∎

An ideal
I
of a
*
-ring
R
is a
*
-ideal if
a^{*}\in I
whenever
a\in I
(see
[
4
]
).

The following theorem gives a characterization for
\Gamma^{{}^{\prime}}(R)
to be a complete graph.

Theorem 3.2
.

Let
R
be an abelian
*
-ring with unity. Then
\Gamma^{{}^{\prime}}(R)
is complete if and only if either
R\simeq\mathbb{Z}_{2}\oplus\mathbb{Z}_{2}
or
Z^{*}(R)
is a set of nilpotent elements in
R
. Further, if
R
is a commutative ring and
\Gamma^{\prime}(R)=K_{n}
with
n>2
then
Z(R)
is a
*
-ideal of
R
.

Proof.

Let
R
be an abelian
*
-ring with unity.
Suppose
\Gamma^{{}^{\prime}}(R)
is complete. Let
a\in Z^{*}(R)
, then by Lemma
3.1
,
a
is a central projection or nilpotent. Suppose
a
is a central projection, then
R=aR\oplus(1-a)R
. For any
x\neq 0
in
aR
we have
x(1-a)=0
. Hence
x\in Z^{*}(R)
. If
x\neq a
then
x=xa=0
, a contradiction. Therefore
aR=\left\{0,a\right\}\simeq\mathbb{Z}_{2}
. Similarly
(1-e)R\simeq\mathbb{Z}_{2}
. Hence
R\simeq\mathbb{Z}_{2}\oplus\mathbb{Z}_{2}.

If

Z^{*}(R)
does not contain any projection, then
Z(R)
is a set of all nilpotent elements in
R
.

Conversely, if
R\simeq\mathbb{Z}_{2}\times\mathbb{Z}_{2}
then
\Gamma^{\prime}(R)=K_{2}
. Also if
Z(R)
is the set of all nilpotent elements in
R
then by Theorem
2.8
,
\Gamma^{\prime}(R)=K_{n}
, where
n=|Z(R)|-1
.
Assume that
R
is a commutative ring and
\Gamma^{\prime}(R)=K_{n}
with
n>2
.
Z(R)=\sqrt{0}
is a nilradical of
R
and hence ideal of
R
. By Lemma
2.7
, it is closed under involution
*
. Hence
Z(R)
is a
*
-ideal of
R
.
∎

Next, we give a necessary and sufficient condition on a
*
-ring
R
such that
\Gamma^{\prime}(R)
is a star graph. In connection with that, we need the following lemma.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
R
be a
*
-ring with unity such that
\Gamma^{{}^{\prime}}(R)
is a star graph with center
e
. If
e^{2}=0
, then
e^{*}=e
and
R
does not contain a non-trivial idempotent element.

Proof.

Let
\Gamma^{{}^{\prime}}(R)
be a star graph with center
e
. Assume that
e^{2}=0
.
Since
e
is a center of a star graph
\Gamma^{{}^{\prime}}(R)
, it is adjacent to all vertices in
Z(R)\setminus\{e,0\}
.
Note that
e\neq 1+e^{*}
. If
e=1+e^{*}
then
0=e^{2}=(1+e^{*})^{2}
this implies
1+2e^{*}+(e^{*})^{2}=0
, which gives
2e^{*}=-1
. Hence
2e=-1
. Therefore
e=(-1)(-e)=-2ee=-2e^{2}=0
a contradiction. Thus we get
e-e^{*}\neq 1
.

We will show that
e=e^{*}
. On the contrary, assume that
e\neq e^{*}
.
Since
e
is adjacent to all vertices in
\Gamma^{\prime}(R)
, for any
a\in Z(R)\setminus\{e,0\}
, there exists a positive integer
n
such that
a^{n}e^{*}=0
or
ea^{*}=0
. That is there is a positive integer
n
such that
a^{n}e^{*}=0
. Then
a^{n}(e^{*}e)^{*}=a^{n}e^{*}e=0
. Hence,
a
is adjacent to
e^{*}e
. But
e
is the vertex in
\Gamma^{\prime}(R)
which is adjacent to all other vertices. Therefore either
e^{*}e=0
or
e^{*}e=e
. If
e^{*}e=0
then
e=0
, otherwise
e
is adjacent to
e
, which is a contradiction. Therefore
e^{*}e=e
. This gives
a^{n}e^{*}e=a^{n}e=0
. Therefore
a^{n}e=0
, that is
a^{n}(e^{*})^{*}=0
, which gives
e^{*}
is adjacent to
a
. Thus
e^{*}
is the center of
\Gamma^{\prime}(R)
and hence
e^{*}=e
.

Now,
x
be any non-trivial idempotent element in
R
. Then
x(1-x^{*})^{*}=x(1-x)=x-x^{2}=0
.

We prove that
x=1-x^{*}
. If
x\neq 1-x^{*}
then
x
and
1-x^{*}
are adjacent. Since
\Gamma^{{}^{\prime}}(R)
is a star graph with center
e
, we have either
x=e
or
1-x^{*}=e
. If
x=e
, we get
0=e^{2}=x^{2}=x
. If
1-x^{*}=e
then
(1-x^{*})^{*}=e^{*}=e
that is
1-x=e
. Then
0=e^{2}=(1-x)^{2}=1-2x+x^{2}=1-2x+x=1-x
gives
x=1
. Hence in either case, we have
x=0
or
x=1
, a contradiction to
x\notin\{0,1\}
. Thus
x=1-x^{*}
.

Now, if
x=1-x^{*}
then
xx^{*}=(1-x^{*})x^{*}=x^{*}-{x^{*}}^{2}=(x-x^{2})^{*}=0
.
Since
e
and
x
are adjacent,
x^{n}e^{*}=0
for some positive integer
n
. Then
x^{n}(x+e)^{*}=x^{n}x^{*}+x^{n}e^{*}=x^{n-1}xx^{*}+x^{n}e=0
. Hence in either case
x+e
and
x
are adjacent.
Therefore the vertices
e,x,x+e
are distinct and form a triangle in
\Gamma^{{}^{\prime}}(R)
a contradiction to the fact
\Gamma^{{}^{\prime}}(R)
is a star graph. Thus
R
does not contain a non-trivial idempotent.
∎

In the following theorem, we give a characterization for
\Gamma^{\prime}(R)
to be a star graph.

Theorem 3.4
.

Let
R
be an abelian, Artinian
*
-ring with unity such that the involution
*
is proper and
\Gamma^{{}^{\prime}}(R)
contains at least three vertices. Then
\Gamma^{{}^{\prime}}(R)
is a star graph if and only if
R=\mathbb{Z}_{2}\oplus D
, where
D
is an integral domain with involution
*
.

Proof.

Suppose
\Gamma^{{}^{\prime}}(R)
is a star graph with center
e
. By Lemma
3.1
,
e
is a central projection or
e
is a nilpotent element. Suppose
e
is a central projection, then
R=eR\oplus(1-e)R
. If
a\in eR
is a non-zero element, then it is adjacent to
1-e
. But
\Gamma^{{}^{\prime}}(R)
is a star graph with center
e
, hence the vertex
1-e
is adjacent to
e
only. Therefore
a=e
and hence
eR=\mathbb{Z}_{2}
. This gives
R=\mathbb{Z}_{2}\oplus(1-e)R
.

Let
b\in(1-e)R
and
xb=0
for some non-zero
x\in(1-e)R
. Then
x,b^{*}\neq e
. If
x\neq b^{*}
, then
x(b^{*})^{*}=xb=0
. Therefore
x
and
b^{*}
are adjacent, a contradiction to
\Gamma^{{}^{\prime}}(R)
is a star graph. Therefore
x=b^{*}
i.e.
b=x^{*}
. This gives
xb=0
which is
xx^{*}=0
, hence we get
x=0
, because
*
is a proper involution. Hence
(1-e)R
does not contain non-zero zero-divisors. Thus
(1-e)R
is an integral domain.

Now, we prove that the case
e^{2}=0
does not arise. Suppose
e^{2}=0
. Then by Lemma
3.3
,
R
does not contain any non-trivial idempotent and
e^{*}=e
. First, we prove that every vertex of
\Gamma^{{}^{\prime}}(R)
is a nilpotent element. Let
a\in V(\Gamma^{{}^{\prime}}(R))\backslash\{e\}
. On the contrary, assume that
a
is not a nilpotent element.
Then
a^{n}=ra^{m}
(as
R
is Artinian), for some
r\in R
and some positive integers
m>n
. This gives
(1-ra^{m-n})a^{n}=0
and
1-ra^{m-n}\neq 0
(otherwise
a
is a unit). Therefore
a^{n}
and
(1-ra^{m-n})
are vertices in
\Gamma^{\prime}(R)
and hence adjacent to
e
. If
a^{n}=e
then
a^{2n}=e^{2}=0
and
a
is nilpotent element, a contradiction. If
(1-ra^{m-n})=e
or
1-ra^{m-n}=a^{n}
then
1=2ra^{m-n}+ra^{m-n}ra^{m-n}
or
1=ra^{m-n}+a^{n}
and hence
a
is a unit, a contradiction. Therefore
\left\{1-ra^{m-n},(a^{n})^{*},e\right\}
forms a triangle in
\Gamma^{\prime}(R)
, this contradicts the fact that
\Gamma^{\prime}(R)
is a star graph. Thus
a
must be nilpotent element.
Therefore
Z^{*}(R)
is a set of all nilpotent elements in
\Gamma^{\prime}(R)
. Hence
\Gamma^{\prime}(R)
is a complete graph. Since
\Gamma^{\prime}(R)
is a star graph,
\Gamma^{\prime}(R)=K_{2}
has only two vertices, a contradiction. Thus
e^{2}=0
is not possible.

Conversely, if
R=\mathbb{Z}_{2}\times D
and
D
is the domain then clearly
\Gamma^{\prime}(R)
is a star graph with center
(1,0)
.
∎

For a ring
R
, the following proposition gives characterization for the connectedness of
\Gamma^{\prime}(R\times R)
.

Proposition 3.5
.

Let
R
be a commutative ring and
A=R\times R
with
(x,y)^{*}=(y,x)
as an involution. Then
\Gamma^{{}^{\prime}}(A)
is connected if and only if
R
is not an integral domain.

Proof.

Let
R
be a commutative ring and
A=R\times R
. We prove that
\Gamma^{{}^{\prime}}(A)
is disconnected if and only if
R
is an integral domain.

Suppose
R
is an integral domain. Let
x,y\in R\setminus 0
. Therefore for any positive integer
n
,
(x,0)^{n}(0,y)^{*}=(x^{n},0)(y,0)=(x^{n}y,0)\neq(0,0)
and
(0,y)^{n}(x,0)^{*}=(0,y^{n})(0,x)=(0,y^{n}x)\neq(0,0)
. Also,
(x,0)^{n}(y,0)^{*}=(x^{n},0)(0,y)=(0,0)
for any positive integer
n
. Therefore
(R\setminus\{0\})\times\{0\}
and
\{0\}\times(R\setminus\{0\})
are components of the graph
\Gamma^{\prime}(A)
. Hence
\Gamma^{{}^{\prime}}(A)
is disconnected.

Conversely, suppose that
\Gamma^{{}^{\prime}}(A)
is disconnected. If
R
is not an integral domain. Then there exists non-zero elements
x,y
in
R
such that
xy=0
. Let
(a,b)
and
(c,d)
be any two vertices in
\Gamma^{\prime}(A)
. Therefore there exists
(u,v)
and
(s,t)
in
R\times R
such that
(a,b)\leftrightarrow(u,v)
and
(s,t)\leftrightarrow(c,d)
are edges. Now
u\neq 0
or
v\neq 0
and
s\neq 0
or
t\neq 0
.
If
u\neq 0
and
t\neq 0
then
(a,b)\leftrightarrow(u,0)\leftrightarrow(x,0)\leftrightarrow(0,y)\leftrightarrow(0,t)\leftrightarrow(c,d)
is a path. If
u\neq 0
and
s\neq 0
then
(a,b)\leftrightarrow(u,0)\leftrightarrow(s,0)\leftrightarrow(c,d)
is a path. Similarly, in other cases, we can find a path between
(a,b)
and
(c,d)
. Hence
\Gamma^{\prime}(A)
is connected, a contradiction. Hence
R
must be an integral domain.
∎

3.5
, it is clear that
