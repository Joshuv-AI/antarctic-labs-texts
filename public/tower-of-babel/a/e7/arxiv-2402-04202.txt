A note on Transfer maps and the Morley product in NIP theories
Kyle Gannon

Abstract
. In an important (yet unpublished) research note, Ben Yaacov describes how to turn a global Keisler measures into a type over a monster model of the randomization. This transfer methods allow one to turn questions involving measures into those involving types (in continuous logic). Assuming that T T is NIP, we show that the Morley product commutes with the transfer map for finitely satisfiable measures. We characterize when the Morley product commutes with the restriction map for pairs of global finitely satisfiable types in the randomization. We end by making some brief observations about the Ellis semigroup in this context.



1.
Introduction & Preliminaries

1.1.
Introduction

While many of the properties of types have generalizations to the context of measures (e.g., invariance, definability, finite satisfiability), the
machine that is model theory
interacts with these objects differently. The central difference is that
you can’t realize a measure
, in a strong sense. Measures are not realized in some elementary extension the way types can be realized. Granted, a lot can be accomplished in this setting, but some powerful classical tools are missing. One striking difference is the lack of arguments using indiscernible sequences. This is because, in a literal sense, there are none.

The power of the randomization
[
20
,
6
]
is that it provides a framework in which one can
realize a measure
, if you are willing to change your underlying structure to a closely related one, and also learn a smidgen of continuous logic. Instead of working with measures directly, one now works with
random variables
(or
random elements
) taking values in our original structure. This perspective restores access to a lot of model theoretic machinery, including important portions of neostability
[
7
,
3
,
6
,
2
,
17
,
21
]
. More importantly, this perspective is practical and we can prove new results about measures (this is often the case with particular kinds of convergence problems – See
[
19
, Lemma 2.10]
via
[
3
]
, or our forth coming preprint with Chernikov and Krupiński).

The Morley product is a fundamental construction in model theory, for both types and measures
[
19
]
. When it exists, this product glues measures together in a generic/non-forking way. This product plays an important role in combinatorial results
[
13
,
23
]
, convolution algebras and dynamical systems (
[
10
,
11
]
), as well as certain kinds of probability spaces (forth coming with Hanson). Understanding precisely how the Morley product interacts with the transfer maps gives us access to more tools to better analyze these constructions and contexts.

The results here are primarily about the connections between measures and random types over NIP structures. We will assume that our underlying theory
T
is both countable and NIP throughout. For some technical reasons, we focus here on the case of finitely satisfiable measures instead of the more general setting of invariant measures. Even though we make this general assumption, we try our best to specify precisely where we use the NIP and finite satisfiability assumptions. We do the following:

(1)

Basic Observations:
We verify some of the claims in Ben Yaacov’s note
[
4
]
. We also make some basic observations.

(2)

Going up:
Assuming NIP, we prove that the Morley product commutes with transfer map.

(3)

Going down:
We observe that the restriction map does not commute with the Morley product. We provide a condition on pairs of finitely satisfiable types which ensures commutativity.

(4)

Over a group:
The transfer map is an injective embedding of topological semigroups from the convolution algebra of Keisler measures to types over the randomization (under the
Newelski product
). If our group is definably amenable, then the minimal left ideal in the space of finitely satisfiable types over the randomization is a single point. We had hoped that the transfer map would provide an isomorphism on the level of minimal left ideals in the general NIP setting. However, we show that if our underlying group is not definably amenable, then the minimal left ideal in the Ellis semigroup of random finitely satisfiable types is somehow always more complicated than the minimal left ideal in the convolution algebra.

Proposition 4.10
.

Let

and

We prove by contraposition. Suppose that
p
is not stochastically independent over

. Then there exists some non-trivial
[A]\in\Sigma_{\mathbfcal{C}^{\prime}}(a)\cap\Sigma_{\mathbfcal{C}^{\prime}}(b)

\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{U})
-formula
\varphi_{1}(x,e_{1})
and
\varphi_{2}(x,e_{2})
such that

[A]=[\{t\in\Omega_{\mathbfcal{C}^{\prime}}:\mathcal{N}^{\prime}\models\varphi_{1}(a(t),f_{e_{1}})],

[A]=[\{t\in\Omega_{\mathbfcal{C}^{\prime}}:\mathcal{N}^{\prime}\models\varphi_{2}(b(t),f_{e_{2}})].

5.
Ellis semigroup of randomization

In this last section, we make some comments concerning the Ellis semigroup of the randomization. We show that the maps
s_{-}
is an injective imbedding from the convolution algebra of Keisler measures to semigroup of types over the randomization (under the Newelski product). We then show that if our group is definably amenable, then any minimal left ideal of the Ellis semigroup of finitely satisfiable types is a single point. Finally, we observe that if
G
is not definably amenable, then the minimal left ideal in
(S_{x}^{\mathrm{fs}}(\mathbfcal{C},G^{\Omega}),*)
is more complicated than the minimal left ideals of
(\mathfrak{M}^{\mathrm{fs}}_{x}(\mathcal{G},G),*)
. We recall the standard set up:

Definition 5.1
.

Let
T
be a first order theory expanding a group. Let
G\models T
and
\mathcal{G}
be a monster model such that
G\prec\mathcal{G}
. Again,
G^{\Omega}\prec\mathcal{G}^{\Omega}\prec\mathbfcal{C}
. We recall the construction of the
Newelski product
: Given
p,q\in S_{x}^{\mathrm{fs}}(\mathbfcal{C},G^{\Omega})
, any
\mathcal{L}
-formula
\varphi(x,\bar{z})
, and
b\in\mathbfcal{C}^{z}
, we have that

\mathbb{E}[\varphi(x,b))]^{p*q}=\mathbb{E}[\varphi(x\cdot y,b))]^{p\otimes q}.

We claim that if
p,q\in S_{x}^{\dagger}(\mathbfcal{C},G^{\Omega})
, then
p*q\in S_{x}^{\mathrm{fs}}(\mathbfcal{C},G^{\Omega})
.

We now quickly recall the definition of definable convolution.

Definition 5.2

(T is NIP)
.

Fix
\mu,\nu\in\mathfrak{M}_{x}^{\mathrm{fs}}(\mathcal{G},G)
. The convolution product of
\mu
and
\nu
is the unique measure
\mu*\nu
in
\mathfrak{M}_{x}^{\mathrm{fs}}(\mathcal{G},G)
such that for any
\mathcal{L}
-formula
\varphi(x,\bar{z})
and parameter
e\in\mathcal{G}^{z}
, we let

(\mu*\nu)(\varphi(x,e))=(\mu_{x}\otimes\nu_{y})(\varphi(x\cdot y,e)).

We recall that if
\mu,\nu\in\mathfrak{M}_{x}^{\mathrm{fs}}(\mathcal{G},G^{\Omega})
, then
\mu*\nu\in\mathfrak{M}_{x}^{\mathrm{fs}}(\mathbfcal{C},G^{\Omega})
.

Proposition 5.3

(T NIP)
.

The map
s_{-}:(\mathfrak{M}_{x}^{\mathrm{fs}}(\mathcal{U},G),*)\to(S_{x}^{\mathrm{fs}}(\mathbfcal{C},G^{\Omega}),*)
is an injective embedding of semigroups.

Proof.

The map
s_{-}
is clearly injective and continuous by Proposition
2.5
. We show our map is a homomorphism. Fix an
\mathcal{L}
-formula
\varphi(x,\bar{z})
. Let
\varphi^{\prime}(x;y,\bar{z})=\varphi(x\cdot y,\bar{z})
and fix
b\in\mathbfcal{C}^{z}
. Now notice that

\displaystyle(\mathbb{E}[\varphi(x,b)])^{s_{\mu*\nu}}

\displaystyle=\int_{S_{y}(M)}F_{\mu*\nu}^{\varphi}d\nu_{\operatorname{tp}(b/\mathbfcal{C})}

\displaystyle=\int_{S_{y}(M)}F_{\mu\otimes\nu}^{\varphi^{\prime}}d\nu_{\operatorname{tp}(b/\mathbfcal{C})}

\displaystyle=(\mathbb{E}[\varphi(x\cdot y,b)])^{s_{\mu\otimes\nu}}

\displaystyle\overset{(*)}{=}(\mathbb{E}[\varphi(x\cdot y,b)])^{s_{\mu}\otimes s_{\nu}}

\displaystyle=(\mathbb{E}[\varphi(x,b)])^{s_{\mu}*s_{\nu}},

where Equation
(*)
holds from Theorem
3.3
. By quantifier elimination, the statement holds.
∎

Our first proposition shows that if
G
is NIP definably amenable, then any minimal left ideal of
S_{x}^{\mathrm{fs}}(\mathbfcal{C},G)
is a single point. This observation is an extension of
[
8
, Proposition 4.18]
– if an NIP group
G
is definably amenable, then
G^{\Omega}
is extremely definably amenable. The general idea of the argument is similar and our proof relies on this work these modified to the appropriate setting. We also remark that this statement in known outside of the NIP case (i.e., if
G
is definably amenabable, then
G^{\Omega}
is extremely definably amenable
[
9
, Theorem 6.3]
).

Lemma 5.4
.

Suppose that
h\in(M^{\Omega}_{0})^{y}
and
b\in\mathbfcal{C}^{z}
. Let
\mathcal{A}
be a partition for
h
. Then for every
r\in\mathrm{supp}(\nu_{\operatorname{tp}(h,b/\mathbfcal{C})})
, if
(a,c)\models r(y,z)
then there exists some
A\in\mathcal{A}
such that
a=h|_{A}
.

Proof.

By Fact
2.1
,
\nu_{\operatorname{tp}(h/\mathbfcal{C})}=\sum_{A\in\mathcal{A}}\mathbb{P}_{0}(A)\delta_{h|_{A}}
. Let
\theta(y):=\bigvee_{A\in\mathcal{A}}(y=h|_{A})
. Notice that

\displaystyle 1

\displaystyle=\sum_{A\in\mathcal{A}}\mathbb{P}(\theta(y))

\displaystyle=\nu_{\operatorname{tp}(h/\mathbfcal{C})}(\theta(y))

\displaystyle=(\pi_{y})_{*}(\nu_{\operatorname{tp}(h,b/\mathbfcal{C})})(\theta(y))

\displaystyle=\nu_{\operatorname{tp}(h,b/\mathbfcal{C})}(\theta(y)\wedge z=z).

Since the set
\theta(y)\wedge z=z
has measure 1, it follows that every type in the support of
\nu_{\operatorname{tp}(h,b/\mathbfcal{C})}
contains the formula
\theta(y)\wedge x=x
. This implies the intended result.
∎

Proposition 5.5

(T NIP)
.

Suppose that
G
is definably amenable. Fix
\mu\in\mathfrak{M}_{x}^{\mathrm{fs}}(\mathcal{G},G)
such that
\mu
is left-
G
-invariant (these exist by
[
12
, Theorem 3.17]
). Then for any
p\in S_{x}^{\mathrm{fs}}(\mathbfcal{C},G^{\Omega})
we have that

p*s_{\mu}=s_{\mu}.

Thus
\{s_{\mu}\}
is a minimal left ideal. As consequence
s_{\mu}|_{G^{\Omega}}
is left-
G^{\Omega}
-invariant which implies
G^{\Omega}
is extremely definably amenable.

Proof.

Fix an
\mathcal{L}
-formula
\varphi(x,z)
and an element
b\in\mathbfcal{C}^{z}
. Choose
(h_{i})_{i\in I}
such that
h_{i}\in M_{0}^{\Omega}
and
\lim_{i\in I}\operatorname{tp}(h_{i}/\mathbfcal{C})=p
. Let
\varphi^{+}(x;y,z):=\varphi(y\cdot x,z)

\displaystyle(\mathbb{E}[\varphi(x,b)])^{p*s_{\mu}}

\displaystyle=\lim_{i\in I}(\mathbb{E}[\varphi(h_{i}\cdot x,b)])^{s_{\mu}}

\displaystyle=\lim_{i\in I}\int_{S_{yz}(M)}F_{\mu}^{\varphi^{+}}d\nu_{\operatorname{tp}(h_{i},b/\mathbfcal{C})}

\displaystyle\overset{(*)}{=}\lim_{i\in I}\int_{S_{yz}(M)}\left(F_{\mu}^{\varphi}\circ\pi_{z}\right)d\nu_{\operatorname{tp}(h_{i},b/\mathbfcal{C})}

\displaystyle=\lim_{i\in I}\int_{S_{z}(M)}F_{\mu}^{\varphi}d\left((\pi_{z})_{*}(\nu_{\operatorname{tp}(h_{i},b/\mathbfcal{C})})\right)

\displaystyle=\lim_{i\in I}\int_{S_{z}(M)}F_{\mu}^{\varphi}d\nu_{\operatorname{tp}(b/\mathbfcal{C})}

\displaystyle=\int_{S_{z}(M)}F_{\mu}^{\varphi}d\nu_{\operatorname{tp}(b/\mathbfcal{C})}

\displaystyle=(\mathbb{E}[\varphi(x,b)])^{s_{\mu}}

We justify Equation
(*)
.
Notice that if
r\in\mathrm{supp}(\nu_{\operatorname{tp}(h_{i},b/\mathbfcal{C})})
and
(a,c)\models r
then
a\in G
by Lemma
5.4
. Notice that since
\mu
is left-
G
-invariant, we have

F_{\mu}^{\varphi^{+}}(r)=\mu(\varphi(a\cdot x,b))=\mu(\varphi(x,b))=\left(F_{\mu}^{\varphi}\circ\pi_{z}\right)(r).\qed

Proposition 5.6

(T NIP)
.

Suppose that
G
is not definably amenable. Then no minimal left ideal of
S_{x}^{\mathrm{fs}}(\mathbfcal{C},G^{\Omega})
is not a singleton. In particular,
G^{\Omega}
is not extremely amenable.

Proof.

Suppose there exists some
q\in S_{x}^{\mathrm{fs}}(\mathbfcal{C},G^{\Omega})
such that for any
p\in S_{x}^{\mathrm{fs}}(\mathbfcal{C},G^{\Omega})
,
p*q=q
. We claim that this implies
\nu_{q}|_{G}
is
G
-left-invariant and so
G
is definably amenable, a contradiction.
∎

Our final proposition demonstrates that if
G
is not definably amenable, then the minimal left ideal of
S^{\mathrm{fs}}(\mathbfcal{C},G^{\Omega})
is not
\{s_{\mu}:\mu\in I\}
where
I
is a minimal left ideal in
(\mathfrak{M}_{x}^{\mathrm{fs}}(\mathcal{G},G),*)
. To be honest, I thought it was going to work out nicely and
\{s_{\mu}:\mu\in I\}
would be a minimal ideal. As they say,
you win some, you lose some.

Proposition 5.7

We recall from

11

I

\mu\in I

\mu

and
t\in(0,1)

\mu\neq t\lambda_{1}+(1-t)\lambda_{2}.

G
