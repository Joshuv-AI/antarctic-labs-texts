Introduction

Quantum state tomography — learning a
d
-dimensional quantum state from
n
copies — is a ubiquitous task in quantum information science.
It is the quantum analogue of the classical task of learning a
d
-outcome probability distribution from
n
samples.

In more detail, the goal is to design an algorithm that, given
\rho^{\otimes n}
for some (generally mixed) quantum state
\rho\in\mathbbm{C}^{d\times d}
, outputs (the classical description of) an estimate
2
2

2

Throughout this paper we use
boldface
to denote random variables.

\widehat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}
that is “
\epsilon
-close” to
\rho
with high probability.
The main challenge is to minimize the sample (copy) complexity
n
as a function of
d
and
\epsilon
(and sometimes other parameters, such as
r=\rank\rho
).
We will also be concerned with the practical issue of designing algorithms that make only single-copy (as opposed to collective) measurements.

An important aspect in specifying the quantum tomography task is the meaning of “
\epsilon
-close”; i.e., what the
loss
function is for judging the algorithm’s estimate.
There are many natural ways for measuring the divergence of two quantum states — even more than for two classical probability distributions — and the precise measure chosen can make a great deal of difference both to the necessary sample complexity, as well as to the utility of the final estimate for future applications.

The main goal of this paper is to show a new tomography algorithm that achieves the most stringent notion of accuracy,
(Bures)
\chi^{2}
-divergence
, while having essentially the same sample complexity as previously known algorithms using
infidelity
as a loss function.
We then given an application, to the
quantum mutual information testing
problem, which crucially relies on our ability to achieve efficient state tomography with respect to
\chi^{2}
-divergence.

1.1
Different quantum divergences, and prior work

Let us start by recalling five important notions of “distance” between two
classical

p,q

\text{($\ell_{2}^{2}$-distance)}\lesssim\text{(total variation distance)}^{2}\lesssim\text{Hellinger-squared ($\mathrm{H}^{2}$)}\lesssim\text{KL divergence}\lesssim\text{$\chi^{2}$-divergence}.

(1)

(Here the “

The first of these,

The remainder are the “

We remark that the first three quantities are bounded in

It is extremely easy to show (see

-divergence.
This also follows from a short calculation of the expected

1.2
Our results

A major question left open by the preceding results is whether quantum state tomography with
\widetilde{O}(1/\epsilon)
dependence is possible for a notion of accuracy more stringent than that of infidelity, such as quantum relative entropy or
\chi^{2}
-divergence.
Although efficient learning with respect to these more stringent measures is known to be possible in the classical case, we are not aware of any previous provable results along these lines in the quantum case.
Indeed, these divergences seem fundamentally more difficult to handle, not being bounded in
[0,1]
, and prior works seemed to suggest that negative results might hold for them.

Prior authors have considered tomography with respect to these stronger error notions.
For example, Ferrie and Blume-Kohout
[
17
]
investigated qubit tomography with respect to quantum relative entropy, and Ref.
[
34
]
uses
\chi^{2}
hypothesis testing to study tomography of (Choi states of) quantum channels.
A further motivation comes from the work of Blume-Kohout and Hayden
[
11
]
, who showed that the quantum relative entropy is singled out as the unique loss function for quantum tomography once certain plausible and general desiderata of an estimator are specified.

Our main motivation, which we return to in
Section

4
, is a property test for zero quantum mutual information.
For this application, our argument
requires
us to do quantum state tomography with respect to Bures
\chi^{2}
-divergence, as only then can we use the quantum “
\chi^{2}
-vs.-
\mathrm{H}^{2}
identity tester” from Ref.
[
7
]
.

For these two stronger error notions, we essentially show that
the strongest upper bounds that one could possibly hope for indeed hold
.
Our main theorem is the following:

1.6
.

Suppose there exists a tomography algorithm
\mathcal{A}
that obtains expected Frobenius-squared error at most
f(d,r)/n
when given
n
copies of a quantum state
\rho\in\mathbbm{C}^{d\times d}
of rank at most
r
.
Then it may be transformed into a tomography algorithm
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}
that, given
\epsilon
,
r
, and

n=\widetilde{O}\bigl(\sqrt{rd}\cdot f(d,r)/\epsilon\bigr)\text{ copies }\quad\text{(respectively, }n=\widetilde{O}(r\cdot f(d,r)/\epsilon)\text{ copies)},

(3)

of
\rho
, outputs (with probability at least
.99
) the classical description of a state
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}
having

\mathrm{D}_{\chi^{2}}(\rho\mathrel{}\mathclose{}\|\mathopen{}\mathrel{}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\rho}})\leq\epsilon\text{ Bures $\chi^{2}$-divergence accuracy}\quad\text{(respectively, }\mathrm{S}(\rho\mathrel{}\mathclose{}\|\mathopen{}\mathrel{}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\rho}})\leq\epsilon\text{ relative entropy accuracy).}

(4)

Moreover, if
\mathcal{A}
uses single-copy measurements, then
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}
does as well, with
O(\log 1/\epsilon)
rounds of adaptivity.

By plugging in
Theorems

1.3

1.7
.

There is a state tomography algorithm using collective measurements on

-divergence accuracy
\epsilon

1.8
.

There is a state tomography algorithm using collective measurements on

\epsilon
.

1.9
.

There is a state tomography algorithm using single-copy measurements and

n=\widetilde{O}(r^{1.5}d^{1.5}/\epsilon)\leq\widetilde{O}(d^{3}/\epsilon)

\epsilon

1.10

There is a state tomography algorithm using single-copy measurements and

Finally, in
Section

4

\chi^{2}
-divergence tomography algorithm to the task of

.
In this problem, the tester gets access to
n
copies of a

\rho

where
|A|=|B|=d
.
The task is to accept (with probability at least

) if the mutual information

) if

We show:

1.14
.

Testing for zero quantum mutual information can be done with
n=\widetilde{O}(1/\epsilon)\cdot(d^{2}+rd^{1.5}+r^{.5}d^{1.75})
samples when
\rho_{A},\rho_{B}
have rank at most
r\leq d
.

1.15
.

The above bound is no worse than
\widetilde{O}(d^{2.5}/\epsilon)
, and is
\widetilde{O}(d^{2}/\epsilon)
whenever
r\leq\sqrt{d}
.
One should also recall the total dimension of

is

.

1.16
.

Harrow and Montanaro
[
23
]
have considered a related “product tester” problem in the special case where the input is a pure state
|\psi\rangle
.
Whenever the maximum overlap
\langle\psi|\rho|\psi\rangle
with any product state

is

, the test passes with probability
1-\Theta(\epsilon)
using only two copies of
|\psi\rangle
.
By itself however, this bound does not test quantum mutual information in the above sense, even for the rank-1 case.

1.17
.

An important feature of our result is its (near-)linear scaling in
1/\epsilon
.
This is despite the fact that estimating mutual information to
\pm\epsilon
accuracy requires
\Omega(1/\epsilon^{2})

Our proof of
Theorem

1.14
has two steps.
First, we learn an estimate
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}_{A}\otimes\widehat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}_{B}
of the marginals
\rho_{A}\otimes\rho_{B}
that has small

-divergence.
Then second we use the “
\chi^{2}
-vs.-infidelity” state certification algorithm from
[
7
]
to test whether the unknown state
\rho

\widehat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}_{A}\otimes\widehat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}_{B}
.
The second step requires us to relate infidelity to relative entropy (and hence mutual information); but more crucial is that in the first step, we
must
be able to do state tomography with Bures

-divergence as the loss measure.
Thus we have an example where
\chi^{2}
-tomography is not just done for its own sake, but is necessary for a subsequent application.

Incidentally, we also show that the same two-step process works well for the problem of testing zero
classical
mutual information given samples from a probability distribution

[d]\times[d]

1.18
.

Testing for zero classical mutual information can be done with
n=O((d/\epsilon)\cdot\log(d/\eps))
samples.

This actually improves on the best known previous algorithm, due to Bhattacharyya–Gayen–Price–Vinodchandran
[

, by a factor of

.

2.1
.

If
\rho\in\mathbbm{C}^{d\times d}
is a matrix and
S\subseteq[d]
, we will write
\rho[S]\in\mathbbm{C}^{S\times S}
for the submatrix formed by the rows and columns from
S
.
If
S=[s]=\{1,2,\dots,s\}
, we will write simply
\rho[s]
. We use similar notation when
p\in\mathbbm{R}^{d}
is a vector.

2.2
.

As we frequently deal with

error or Frobenius-squared error in this work, we often use the “triangle inequality”
(a-c)^{2}\leq 2(a-b)^{2}+2(b-c)^{2}
without additional comment.

2.1
Classical distances and divergences

Throughout this section, let
p=(p_{1},\dots,p_{d})
and
q=(q_{1},\dots,q_{d})
denote probability distributions on
[d]
.
We also use the conventions
0/0=0
,
x/0=\infty
for
x>0
, and trust the reader to interpret other such expressions appropriately (using continuity).

We now recall some distances between probability distributions.

2.3
.

For
f:(0,\infty)\to\mathbbm{R}
strictly convex at
1
with
f(1)=0
, the associated
f
-divergence
is

\mathrm{d}_{f}(p\mathrel{}\mathclose{}\|\mathopen{}\mathrel{}q)=\E_{\boldsymbol{j}\sim q}[f(p_{\boldsymbol{j}}/q_{\boldsymbol{j}})].

(5)

2.4
.

All
f
-divergences satisfy the data processing inequality; see e.g.
[
50
, Thm. 4.2]
.

2.5
.

For
\alpha\in[0,\infty]
, the associated
Rényi divergence
is defined by

\mathrm{d}^{\textnormal{R\'{e}n}}_{\mathrm{\alpha}}(p\mathrel{}\mathclose{}\|\mathopen{}\mathrel{}q)=\frac{1}{\alpha-1}\ln\sum_{i=1}^{d}p_{i}^{\alpha}q_{i}^{1-\alpha}.

(6)

We will use a few particular cases:

2.6
.

The
total variation distance
, a metric, is the
f
-divergence with
f(x)=\tfrac{1}{2}\absolutevalue{x-1}
:

\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{TV}}(p,q)=\tfrac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\absolutevalue{p_i - q_i}.

(7)

2.7
.

The
Hellinger distance

\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{H}}(p,q)
, a metric, is the square-root of the
f
-divergence with
f(x)=(\sqrt{x}-1)^{2}
:

\mathrm{d}^{2}_{\mathrm{H}}(p,q)=\sum_{i=1}^{d}(\sqrt{p_{i}}-\sqrt{q_{i}})^{2}.

(8)

It is also essentially a Rényi divergence.
More precisely, the
Bhattacharyya coefficient
between
p
and
q
is

\mathrm{BC}(p,q)=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\sqrt{p_{i}}\sqrt{q_{i}}=\exp(-\tfrac 12 \cdot\dren{1/2}{p}{q}),

(9)

and we have
\mathrm{d}^{2}_{\mathrm{H}}(p,q)=2(1-\mathrm{BC}(p,q))
.
(Note the useful tensorization identity,
\mathrm{BC}(p_{1}\otimes p_{2},q_{1}\otimes q_{2})=\mathrm{BC}(p_{1},q_{1})\cdot\mathrm{BC}(p_{2},q_{2})
.)

2.8
.

The
KL divergence
(or
relative entropy
) is both an
f
-divergence (with
f(x)=x\ln x
or
f(x)=x\ln x-(x-1)
) and a Rényi divergence (with
\alpha=1
):

\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{KL}}(p\mathrel{}\mathclose{}\|\mathopen{}\mathrel{}q)=\sum_{i=1}^{d}p_{i}\ln(p_i/q_i).

(10)

Also, if
p
is a “bipartite” probability distribution on finite outcome set
A\times B
, and
p_{A}
,
p_{B}
denote its marginals, we may define the
mutual information

I(A:B)_{p}=\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{KL}}(p\mathrel{}\mathclose{}\|\mathopen{}\mathrel{}p_{A}\times p_{B}).

(11)

2.9
.

The
\chi^{2}
-divergence
is the
f
-divergence with
f(x)=(x-1)^{2}
:

\mathrm{d}_{\chi^{2}}(p\mathrel{}\mathclose{}\|\mathopen{}\mathrel{}q)=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\frac{(p_{i}-q_{i})^{2}}{q_{i}}=\left\lparen\sum_{i=1}^{d}\frac{p_{i}^{2}}{q_{i}}\right\rparen-1.

(12)

We will sometimes use the first formula even when
p
and/or
q
do not sum to
1
.

2.10
.

The
max-relative entropy
(or
worst-case regret
) is defined to be

\mathrm{d}^{\textnormal{R\'{e}n}}_{\mathrm{\infty}}(p\mathrel{}\mathclose{}\|\mathopen{}\mathrel{}q)=\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in[d]\\
p_{i}\neq 0\end{subarray}}\{\ln(p_i/q_i)\}.

(13)

The following chain of inequalities is well known (see, e.g.,
[
20
]
):

2.11
.

\displaystyle\tfrac{1}{2}\mathrm{d}^{2}_{\mathrm{H}}(p,q)\leq\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{TV}}(p,q)\leq\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{H}}(p,q)\leq\sqrt{\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{KL}}(p\mathrel{}\mathclose{}\|\mathopen{}\mathrel{}q)}\leq\sqrt{\mathrm{d}_{\chi^{2}}(p\mathrel{}\mathclose{}\|\mathopen{}\mathrel{}q)}.

Some of the inequalities in the above can be slightly sharpened; e.g., one also has
\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{TV}}(p,q)\leq\sqrt{\tfrac{1}{2}\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{KL}}(p\mathrel{}\mathclose{}\|\mathopen{}\mathrel{}q)}
, usually called
Pinsker’s inequality
.
Perhaps less well known is the following “reverse” form of Pinsker’s inequality:

\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{KL}}(p\mathrel{}\mathclose{}\|\mathopen{}\mathrel{}q)\leq O\bigl(\mathrm{d}^{\textnormal{R\'{e}n}}_{\mathrm{\infty}}(p\mathrel{}\mathclose{}\|\mathopen{}\mathrel{}q)\bigr)\cdot\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{TV}}(p,q).

(14)

Moreover, it is possible to strengthen the above by putting Hellinger-squared in place of total variation distance.
These facts were proven in
[
39
]
; for the convenience of the reader, we provide a streamlined proof of the following:

2.12
.

For
p
,
q
probability distributions on
[d]
we have

\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{KL}}(p\mathrel{}\mathclose{}\|\mathopen{}\mathrel{}q)\leq(2+\mathrm{d}^{\textnormal{R\'{e}n}}_{\mathrm{\infty}}(p\mathrel{}\mathclose{}\|\mathopen{}\mathrel{}q))\cdot\mathrm{d}^{2}_{\mathrm{H}}(p,q).

(15)

Proof.

Let us write
r_{i}=p_{i}/q_{i}
.
Defining

f(r)=r\ln r-(r-1),\qquad g(r)=(\sqrt{r}-1)^{2},

(16)

the elementary
Lemma

2.13
proven below shows that

\forall r\geq 0,\qquad f(r)\leq h(r)g(r),\qquad\text{where }h(r)=2+\max\{\ln r,0\}.

(17)

It follows that

\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{KL}}(p\mathrel{}\mathclose{}\|\mathopen{}\mathrel{}q)=\E_{\boldsymbol{i}\sim q}[f(r_{\boldsymbol{i}})]\leq\E_{\boldsymbol{i}\sim q}[h(r_{\boldsymbol{i}})g(r_{\boldsymbol{i}})]\leq\max_{i\in[d]}\{h(r_{i})\}\cdot\E_{\boldsymbol{i}\sim q}[g(r_{\boldsymbol{i}})]=(2+\mathrm{d}^{\textnormal{R\'{e}n}}_{\mathrm{\infty}}(p\mathrel{}\mathclose{}\|\mathopen{}\mathrel{}q))\cdot\mathrm{d}^{2}_{\mathrm{H}}(p,q).\qed

(18)

2.13
.

Equation

17
holds.

Proof.

Consider
a(r)\coloneqq h(r)g(r)-f(r)
.
This function is continuous and piecewise differentiable on
r\geq 0
with an exceptional point at
r=1
.
We will first show that
a(r)
is nonnegative and increasing on
r\geq 1
.
Clearly
a(1)=0
, so we only need to show that
a^{\prime}(r)\geq 0
. For
r\geq 1
, by the integral definition of the logarithm and the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality we have

\ln r=\int_{1}^{r}x^{-1}\mathrm{d}x\leq\left\lparen\int_{1}^{r}\mathrm{d}x\right\rparen^{1/2}\left\lparen\int_{1}^{r}x^{-2}\mathrm{d}x\right\rparen^{1/2}=\sqrt{r}-\frac{1}{\sqrt{r}}\,.

(19)

Calculating the derivative of
a(r)
and using the above inequality for the logarithm, we have that

a^{\prime}(r)=3-\frac{4}{\sqrt{r}}+\frac{1}{r}-\frac{\ln r}{\sqrt{r}}\geq 3-\frac{4}{\sqrt{r}}+\frac{1}{r}-\frac{\sqrt{r}-\frac{1}{\sqrt{r}}}{\sqrt{r}}=\frac{2\left(\sqrt{r}-1\right)^{2}}{r}\geq 0.

(20)

For the case
0\leq r\leq 1
, we change variables to
s=1/r
and define
b(s)\coloneqq h(1/s)g(1/s)-f(1/s)
for
s\geq 1
.
We have
b(1)=0
, and using again the logarithm inequality we find

b^{\prime}(s)=\frac{2\sqrt{s}-\ln s-2}{s^{2}}\geq\frac{\sqrt{s}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{s}}-2}{s^{2}}=\frac{\left(\sqrt{s}-1\right)^{2}}{s^{5/2}}\geq 0.\qed

(21)

We remark that
Equation

17
can be strengthened to
h(r)=2+\ln\bigl((2+r)/3\bigr)
, but as this does not change the scaling of any of our results, we will not use this stronger inequality or present our (annoyingly complicated) proof.

Finally, we mention the
\ell_{2}^{2}
-distance between probability distributions,
\|p-q\|_{2}^{2}=\sum_{i}(p_{i}-q_{i})^{2}
.
Though it does not have an operational meaning, the simplicity of computing it makes it a useful tool when analyzing other distances.
For example, the
\chi^{2}
-divergence is a kind of “weighted” version of
\ell_{2}^{2}
-distance, in which the error term
(p_{i}-q_{i})^{2}
is weighted by
1/q_{i}
.
We record here basic facts about estimation with respect to these distance measures.

2.14
.

Let
p
be a distribution on
[d]
, and suppose
\boldsymbol{q}
is the empirical estimator formed from
m
samples.
Then for any
S\subseteq[d]
we have

\E\bigl[\|p[S]-\boldsymbol{q}[S]\|_{2}^{2}\bigr]\leq\|p[S]\|_{1}/m.

(22)

In particular,
\E\bigl[\|p-\boldsymbol{q}\|_{2}^{2}\bigr]\leq 1/m
.

Proof.

Note that
\boldsymbol{q}_{i}
is distributed as
\textrm{Binomial}(m,p_{i})/m
, hence
\E[(p_{i}-\boldsymbol{q}_{i})^{2}]=\Var[\boldsymbol{q}_{i}]=p_{i}(1-p_{i})/m\leq p_{i}/m
.
Summing over
i\in S
completes the proof.
∎

We will also need the following variant with high-probability guarantees:

2.15
.

In the setting of
Proposition

2.14
, we have the following guarantees, where we introduce the notation
m_{\delta}=m/(c\ln(1/\delta))
for
c
some large universal constant:

(a)

\|p-\boldsymbol{q}\|_{2}^{2}\leq 1/m_{\delta}
except with probability at most
\delta
;

(b)

if
\|p[S]\|_{1}\geq 1/m_{\delta}
then
\|\boldsymbol{q}[S]\|_{1}
is within a
1.01
-factor of
\|p[S]\|_{1}
except with probability at most
\delta
;

(c)

if
\|p[S]\|_{1}\leq 1/m_{\delta}
then
\|\boldsymbol{q}[S]\|_{1}\leq 1.01/m_{\delta}
except with probability at most
\delta
.

Proof.

Items

(b)
and
(c)
follow from standard Chernoff bounds.
As for
Item

(a)
, it follows from the known high-probability bound for empirically learning a distribution with respect to
\ell_{2}^{2}
-error; see, e.g.,
[
35
]
.
We remark that it is important to use this latter result, as opposed to the generic “median-of-
O(\log(1/\delta))
-estimates” method; if we used the latter, it would be unclear how to simultaneously achieve
Items

(b)
and
(c)

∎

2.16
.

Fix a subset
S\subseteq[d]
of cardinality
s
.
Given
m
samples from an unknown distribution
p
on
[d]
, let
\boldsymbol{q}
be the estimator formed by using the add-one estimator on elements from
S
, and the empirical estimator on the remaining elements.
(Note that
\boldsymbol{q}
is itself a probability distribution.)
Then

\E[\mathrm{d}_{\chi^{2}}(p[S]\mathrel{}\mathclose{}\|\mathopen{}\mathrel{}\boldsymbol{q}[S])]\leq\frac{s}{m+s}+\left\lparen\frac{(s-1)^{2}}{(m+1)(m+s)}-\frac{1}{m+s}\right\rparen\|p[S]\|_{1}\leq s/m+(s/m)^{2}\leq 2s/m,

(23)

the last inequality assuming
m\geq s
.
In case
S=[d]
, the sharpest upper bound above equals
\frac{d-1}{m+1}\leq d/m
.

Moreover, still assuming
m\geq s
and using the notation
m_{\delta}
from
Proposition

2.15
, if
p_{i}\geq 1/m_{\delta/s}
for all
i\in S
, then except with probability at most
\delta
we have that
\boldsymbol{q}_{i}
is within a
4
-factor of
p_{i}
simultaneously for all
i\in S
.

Proof.

For
i\in S
we have that
\boldsymbol{q}_{i}
is distributed as
\frac{\boldsymbol{B}+1}{m+s}
, where
\boldsymbol{B}\sim\textrm{Binomial}(m,p_{i})
. It is elementary to show that the resulting contribution to
\mathrm{d}_{\chi^{2}}(p[S]\mathrel{}\mathclose{}\|\mathopen{}\mathrel{}\boldsymbol{q}[S])
, namely
\frac{(p_{i}-\boldsymbol{q}_{i})^{2}}{\boldsymbol{q}_{i}}
, has expectation equal to

\frac{1}{m+s}+\left\lparen\frac{(s-1)^{2}}{(m+1)(m+s)}-\frac{1}{m+s}-\frac{m+s}{m+1}(1-p_{i})^{m+1}\right\rparen p_{i}.

(24)

Dropping the term above involving
(1-p_{i})^{m+1}
, and then summing over
i\in S
, yields
Equation

23
.

As for the “moreover” statement, a Chernoff bound tells us that
\boldsymbol{B}
is within a
2
-factor of
mp_{i}
except with probability at most
\delta/s
, using
mp_{i}\geq c\log(s/\delta)
. When this occurs,
\boldsymbol{q}_{i}
is at least
p_{i}/4
(using
m\geq s
) and at most
\frac{2mp_{i}+1}{m}\leq 3p_{i}
(using
c\geq 1
), so the proof is complete by a union bound over
i\in S
.
∎

2.2
Quantum distances and divergences

The analogous theory of distances and divergences between quantum states is quite rich
[
44
,
26
]
, as there are multiple quantum generalizations of both
f
-divergences and Rényi divergences.
To distinguish between the quantum and classical cases, we use an upper-case
D
for quantum divergences and a lower-case
d
for classical divergences.

Throughout this section, let
\rho,\sigma\in\mathbbm{C}^{d\times d}
be (mixed) quantum states.

2.17
.

Given an
f
-divergence
\mathrm{d}_{f}({\cdot}\mathrel{}\mathclose{}\|\mathopen{}\mathrel{}{\cdot})
, the associated
measured (aka minimal) quantum
f
-divergence

[
26
]
is

\mathrm{D}_{f}(\rho\mathrel{}\mathclose{}\|\mathopen{}\mathrel{}\sigma)=\sup_{\text{POVMs }(E_{i})_{i=1}^{m}}\{\mathrm{d}_{f}(q_{\rho}\mathrel{}\mathclose{}\|\mathopen{}\mathrel{}q_{\sigma})\},\quad\text{where }q_{\tau}=(\tr(\tau E_1),\dots,\tr(\tau E_m)).

(25)

2.18
.

All measured
f
-divergences satisfy the (quantum) data processing inequality.
This fact follows from the definition and a reduction to the classical case.

2.19
.

For
\alpha\in[0,\infty]
, the associated
conventional quantum Rényi divergence

[
36
]
is defined by

\mathrm{D}^{\textnormal{R\'{e}n}}_{\mathrm{\alpha}}(\rho\mathrel{}\mathclose{}\|\mathopen{}\mathrel{}\sigma)=\frac{1}{\alpha-1}\ln\tr\left\lparen\rho^{\alpha}\sigma^{1-\alpha}\right\rparen.

(26)

Let us also describe a further relationship between classical and quantum Rényi entropies.
To do so let us introduce the following notation:

2.20
.

Given the spectral decompositions

\rho=\sum_{i=1}^{d}p_{i}\outerproduct{\varphi_i}{\varphi_i},\qquad\sigma=\sum_{i=1}^{d}q_{i}\outerproduct{\psi_i}{\psi_i},

(27)

we define two probability distributions
P^{\rho\sigma},Q^{\rho\sigma}
on
[d]\times[d]
, as follows:

P^{\rho\sigma}_{ij}=\absolutevalue{\braket{\varphi_i}{\psi_j}}^{2}p_{i},\qquad Q^{\rho\sigma}_{ij}=\absolutevalue{\braket{\varphi_i}{\psi_j}}^{2}q_{j}.

(28)

We now give a simple calculation that allows us to compute a quantum Rényi divergence from an associated classical probability distribution.
This calculation has appeared in the literature as early as
[
30
, Thm. 2.2]
; see
[
6
, Prop. 1]
for an explicit statement.
For convenience, we repeat the calculation here.

2.21
.

\displaystyle\mathrm{D}^{\textnormal{R\'{e}n}}_{\mathrm{\alpha}}(\rho\mathrel{}\mathclose{}\|\mathopen{}\mathrel{}\sigma)=\mathrm{d}^{\textnormal{R\'{e}n}}_{\mathrm{\alpha}}(P^{\rho\sigma}\mathrel{}\mathclose{}\|\mathopen{}\mathrel{}Q^{\rho\sigma}).

Proof.

One calculates:

\displaystyle\mathrm{D}^{\textnormal{R\'{e}n}}_{\mathrm{\alpha}}(\rho\mathrel{}\mathclose{}\|\mathopen{}\mathrel{}\sigma)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\alpha-1}\ln\tr\left\lparen\left\lparen\sum_{i\vphantom{j}=1}^{d}p_{i}^{\alpha}\outerproduct{\varphi_i}{\varphi_i}\right\rparen\left\lparen\sum_{j=1}^{d}q_{j}^{1-\alpha}\outerproduct{\psi_j}{\psi_j}\right\rparen\right\rparen

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\alpha-1}\ln\left\lparen\sum_{i,j=1}^{d}p^{\alpha}\absolutevalue{\braket{\varphi_i}{\psi_j}}^{2}q^{1-\alpha}\right\rparen=\frac{1}{\alpha-1}\ln\sum_{i,j=1}^{d}(P^{\rho\sigma}_{ij})^{\alpha}(Q^{\rho\sigma}_{ij})^{1-\alpha}=\mathrm{d}^{\textnormal{R\'{e}n}}_{\mathrm{\alpha}}(P^{\rho\sigma}\mathrel{}\mathclose{}\|\mathopen{}\mathrel{}Q^{\rho\sigma}).\qed

We now define some particular quantum distances/divergences:

2.22
.

The
trace distance
, a metric, is the measured
f
-divergence associated to total variation distance
[
25
]
:

\mathrm{D}_{\mathrm{tr}}(\rho,\sigma)=\tfrac{1}{2}\|\rho-\sigma\|_{1}.

(29)

2.23
.

The
Bures distance

\mathrm{D}_{\mathrm{B}}(\rho,\sigma)
, a metric, is the square-root of the measured
f
-divergence associated to Hellinger-squared
[
19
]
.
It has the formula

\mathrm{D}^{2}_{\mathrm{B}}(\rho,\sigma)=2(1-\mathrm{F}(\rho,\sigma)),

(30)

where
\mathrm{F}(\rho,\sigma)=\|\sqrt{\rho}\sqrt{\sigma}\|_{1}
is the
fidelity
between
\rho
and
\sigma
(in the “square root” convention). The
infidelity
between
\rho
and
\sigma
is simply
1-\mathrm{F}(\rho,\sigma)=\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{D}^{2}_{\mathrm{B}}(\rho,\sigma)
.

There is a close analogy between the quantum fidelity and the classical Bhattacharrya coefficient, and indeed the analogue of
Equation

9
holds if one uses the “sandwiched Rényi entropy”
[
28
,
48
]
.
Using instead the conventional Rényi entropy yields a slightly different notion:

2.24
.

The
quantum Hellinger affinity
is defined by

\mathrm{A}(\rho,\sigma)=\tr(\sqrt{\rho} \sqrt{\sigma})=\exp(-\tfrac 12 \cdot\Dren{1/2}{\rho}{\sigma}),

(31)

and the
quantum Hellinger distance

\mathrm{D}_{\mathrm{H}}(\rho,\sigma)
, a metric, is defined by

\mathrm{D}^{2}_{\mathrm{H}}(\rho,\sigma)=2(1-\mathrm{A}(\rho,\sigma))=\tr((\sqrt{\rho} - \sqrt{\sigma})^2)=\|\sqrt{\rho}-\sqrt{\sigma}\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}=\mathrm{D}^{2}_{\mathrm{H}}(\rho,\sigma)=\mathrm{d}^{2}_{\mathrm{H}}(P^{\rho\sigma},Q^{\rho\sigma}),

(32)

the last equality using
Proposition

2.21
.
(Note also the useful tensorization identity,
\mathrm{A}(\rho_{1}\otimes\rho_{2},\sigma_{1}\otimes\sigma_{2})=\mathrm{A}(\rho_{1},\sigma_{1})\cdot\mathrm{A}(\rho_{2},\sigma_{2})
.)

Fortunately, the preceding two distances differ by only a small constant factor:

2.25
.

\displaystyle\mathrm{D}^{2}_{\mathrm{B}}(\rho,\sigma)\leq\mathrm{D}^{2}_{\mathrm{H}}(\rho,\sigma)\leq 2\mathrm{D}^{2}_{\mathrm{B}}(\rho,\sigma).

The left inequality in
2.25
is from
\mathrm{A}(\rho,\sigma)\leq\mathrm{F}(\rho,\sigma)
; the right inequality follows from
[
6
, Eq. (32)]
.

2.26
.

The
Bures
\chi^{2}
-divergence
of
\rho
from
\sigma
is the measured
f
-divergence associated to classical
\chi^{2}
-divergence
[
12
,
42
]
.
It can also be given the following formula when
\sigma=\diag(q_{1},\dots,q_{d})
is diagonal of full rank (and this suffices to define it for general full-rank
\sigma
, since it is unitarily invariant):

\mathrm{D}_{\chi^{2}}(\rho\mathrel{}\mathclose{}\|\mathopen{}\mathrel{}\sigma)=\sum_{i,j=1}^{d}\frac{2}{q_{i}+q_{j}}\absolutevalue{\tau_{ij}}^{2},\quad\text{where }\tau=\rho-\sigma.

(33)

We will use this formula even when
q_{1},\dots,q_{d}\geq 0
do not sum to
1
.

Similar to the connection between
\ell_{2}^{2}
-distance and
\chi^{2}
-divergence in the classical case, the Bures
\chi^{2}
-divergence can be seen as a kind of “weighted” version of the Frobenius-squared distance, in which the error term
\absolutevalue{\tau_{ij}}^{2}
is weighted by
\frac{2}{q_{i}+q_{j}}=\Theta(\frac{1}{\max\{q_{i},q_{j}\}})
.

Indeed, we will frequently consider applying
Equation

33
when the
q_{i}
’s form (or approximately form) a nondecreasing sequence, meaning that (we expect)
q_{i}\leq q_{j}
. In this case it is reasonable to use
q_{i}+q_{j}\geq q_{j}
, which motivates the following simple bound:

2.27
.

In the notation from
Definition

2.26
, we define

\widehat{\mathrm{D}}_{\chi^{2}}(\rho\mathrel{}\mathclose{}\|\mathopen{}\mathrel{}\sigma)=\sum_{i,j=1}^{d}\frac{2}{q_{\max(i,j)}}\absolutevalue{\tau_{ij}}^{2}\geq\mathrm{D}_{\chi^{2}}(\rho\mathrel{}\mathclose{}\|\mathopen{}\mathrel{}\sigma);

(34)

and, for
L=[d^{\prime}]
(for
d^{\prime}\leq d
) we define

\mathrm{\widehat{D}}_{\chi^{2}}^{-L}(\rho\mathrel{}\mathclose{}\|\mathopen{}\mathrel{}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\rho}})=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i,j:\\
\max(i,j)\not\in L\end{subarray}}\frac{2}{q_{\max(i,j)}}\absolutevalue{\tau_{ij}}^{2}=\sum_{k\not\in L}\frac{2}{q_{k}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i,j:\\
\max(i,j)=k\end{subarray}}\absolutevalue{\tau_{ij}}^{2}.

(35)

Note that

\mathrm{D}_{\chi^{2}}(\rho\mathrel{}\mathclose{}\|\mathopen{}\mathrel{}\sigma)\leq\mathrm{D}_{\chi^{2}}(\rho[L]\mathrel{}\mathclose{}\|\mathopen{}\mathrel{}\sigma[L])+\mathrm{\widehat{D}}_{\chi^{2}}^{-L}(\rho\mathrel{}\mathclose{}\|\mathopen{}\mathrel{}\sigma).

(36)

2.28
.

The
quantum relative entropy

[
46
]

is defined by

\mathrm{S}(\rho\mathrel{}\mathclose{}\|\mathopen{}\mathrel{}\sigma)=\tr\left\lparen\rho(\ln\rho-\ln\sigma)\right\rparen=\mathrm{D}^{\textnormal{R\'{e}n}}_{\mathrm{1}}(\rho\mathrel{}\mathclose{}\|\mathopen{}\mathrel{}\sigma)=\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{KL}}(P^{\rho\sigma}\mathrel{}\mathclose{}\|\mathopen{}\mathrel{}Q^{\rho\sigma}),

(37)

the last equality using
Proposition

2.21
.
Also, if
\rho
is a “bipartite” quantum state on
A\otimes B
, where
A\cong B\cong\mathbbm{C}^{d}
, and if
\rho_{A}
,
\rho_{B}
denote its marginals (obtained by tracing out the
B
,
A
components, respectively), the
quantum mutual information
of
\rho
is defined to be

I(A:B)_{\rho}=\mathrm{S}(\rho\mathrel{}\mathclose{}\|\mathopen{}\mathrel{}\rho_{A}\otimes\rho_{B}).

(38)

2.29
.

The conventional quantum
\infty
-Rényi divergence (discussed in, e.g.,
[
3
]
) is, by
Proposition

2.21
,

\mathrm{D}^{\textnormal{R\'{e}n}}_{\mathrm{\infty}}(\rho\mathrel{}\mathclose{}\|\mathopen{}\mathrel{}\sigma)=\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}i,j\in[d]\\
\innerproduct{\varphi_i}{\psi_j}\neq 0\end{subarray}}\{\ln(p_i/q_j)\}\leq\ln(\|\rho\| \|\sigma^{-1}\|)\leq\ln\|\sigma^{-1}\|.

(39)

2.30
.

This quantity is
not
the same as the “quantum max-relative entropy” defined in
[
16
]
; it
would
be if one replaced the conventional Rényi entropy with its sandwiched form.

Relating some of these divergences is the following chain of inequalities:

2.31
.

\displaystyle\tfrac{1}{2}\mathrm{D}^{2}_{\mathrm{H}}(\rho,\sigma)\leq\mathrm{D}_{\mathrm{tr}}(\rho,\sigma)\leq\mathrm{D}_{\mathrm{B}}(\rho,\sigma)\leq\sqrt{\mathrm{S}(\rho\mathrel{}\mathclose{}\|\mathopen{}\mathrel{}\sigma)},\ \sqrt{\mathrm{D}_{\chi^{2}}(\rho\mathrel{}\mathclose{}\|\mathopen{}\mathrel{}\sigma)}.

The first inequality above is from from
[
6
, Thm. 2]
. The second follows from the classical case
[
19
]
. The third also follows from the classical case and the observation that the “measured” quantum relative entropy is at most
\mathrm{S}(\rho\mathrel{}\mathclose{}\|\mathopen{}\mathrel{}\sigma)
(see, e.g.
[
9
, App. A]
).
The fourth also follows from the classical case, using that Bures
\chi^{2}
is the measured form of classical
\chi^{2}

[
12
,
42
]
.
As with
Proposition

2.11
, some of these inequalities can be sharpened slightly; for example we have the quantum Pinsker inequality
\mathrm{D}_{\mathrm{tr}}(\rho,\sigma)\leq\sqrt{\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{S}(\rho\mathrel{}\mathclose{}\|\mathopen{}\mathrel{}\sigma)}
.

2.3
Quantum Tomography with Quantum Relative Entropy Loss

One of our main results follows easily from the above discussion of divergences.
The idea is to improve on certain “reverse quantum Pinsker” results which have been studied previously; see, e.g.,
[
5
]
for a quantum generalization of the reverse-Pinsker
Equation

14
.
We will use the following strengthened version with quantum Hellinger-squared in place of trace distance:

2.32
.

For
\rho,\sigma\in\mathbbm{C}^{d\times d}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{S}(\rho\mathrel{}\mathclose{}\|\mathopen{}\mathrel{}\sigma)\leq(2+\mathrm{D}^{\textnormal{R\'{e}n}}_{\mathrm{\infty}}(\rho\mathrel{}\mathclose{}\|\mathopen{}\mathrel{}\sigma))\cdot\mathrm{D}^{2}_{\mathrm{H}}(\rho,\sigma).

Proof.

This is immediate from
\mathrm{S}(\rho\mathrel{}\mathclose{}\|\mathopen{}\mathrel{}\sigma)=\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{KL}}(P^{\rho\sigma}\mathrel{}\mathclose{}\|\mathopen{}\mathrel{}Q^{\rho\sigma})
,
Proposition

2.12
, and
2.29
.
∎

Despite following directly from known results (up to constant factors), the above theorem does not seem to have appeared previously in the literature.
Our next result shows that this can be used to automatically upgrade any quantum tomography algorithm with an infidelity guarantee to one with a relative entropy guarantee, at the expense of only a log factor (cf. our main
Theorem

1.6
upgrading Frobenius-squared-tomography to
\chi^{2}
-tomography).

2.33
.

We write
\Delta_{\epsilon}
for the completely depolarizing channel, which for
0\leq\epsilon\leq 1
acts on
\rho\in\mathbbm{C}^{d\times d}
as
\Delta_{\epsilon}(\rho)=(1-\epsilon)\rho+\epsilon(\mathbbm{1}/d)
(with
\mathbbm{1}
denoting the identity matrix).

2.34
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be a state tomography algorithm that, given
n
copies of
\rho\in\mathbbm{C}^{d\times d}
and parameter
\epsilon
, outputs an estimate
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}
achieving infidelity
\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{D}^{2}_{\mathrm{B}}(\rho,\widehat{\boldsymbol{\rho}})\leq\epsilon\leq 1/2
.
Then letting
\boldsymbol{\rho}^{\prime}=\Delta_{2\epsilon}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\rho}})
, we have
\mathrm{S}(\rho\mathrel{}\mathclose{}\|\mathopen{}\mathrel{}\boldsymbol{\rho}^{\prime})\leq 16\epsilon\cdot(2+\ln(d/2\eps))
.

Applying this theorem with the previously known result of Haah–Harrow–Ji–Wu–Yu,
Theorem

1.4
, we immediately conclude
Corollary

1.8
, that there is a state tomography algorithm with respect to quantum relative entropy that has copy complexity
n=O(rd/\epsilon)\cdot\log^{2}(d/\epsilon)
(using collective measurements).

Theorem

2.34
is immediate from the following (together with the fact that Hellinger-squared is upper bounded by
4
times infidelity (
2.25
)):

2.35
.

Suppose
\rho,\sigma\in\mathbbm{C}^{d\times d}
are quantum states with
\mathrm{D}^{2}_{\mathrm{H}}(\rho,\sigma)\leq\epsilon
. Then for
\sigma^{\prime}=\Delta_{\epsilon/2}(\sigma)
we have
\mathrm{S}(\rho\mathrel{}\mathclose{}\|\mathopen{}\mathrel{}\sigma^{\prime})\leq 4\epsilon\cdot(2+\ln(2d/\eps))
.

Proof.

Since
\Delta_{\epsilon/2}(\sigma)
has smallest eigenvalue at least
\epsilon/2d
, we have
\mathrm{D}^{\textnormal{R\'{e}n}}_{\mathrm{\infty}}(\rho\mathrel{}\mathclose{}\|\mathopen{}\mathrel{}\sigma^{\prime})\leq\ln(2d/\eps)
and hence from
Theorem

2.32
it suffices to show
\mathrm{D}^{2}_{\mathrm{H}}(\rho,\sigma^{\prime})\leq 4\epsilon
. In turn, since
\mathrm{D}_{\mathrm{H}}({\cdot},{\cdot})
is a metric, by
Remark

2.2
it suffices to prove
\mathrm{D}^{2}_{\mathrm{H}}(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime})\leq\epsilon
.
But using
Proposition

2.31
, we indeed have

\mathrm{D}^{2}_{\mathrm{H}}(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime})\leq\|\sigma-\sigma^{\prime}\|_{1}=(\epsilon/2)\|\sigma-\mathbbm{1}/d\|_{1}\leq(\epsilon/2)(\|\sigma\|_{1}+\|\mathbbm{1}/d\|_{1})=\epsilon.\qed

(40)

We give a guide to this section:

•

Section

It serves as a small warmup for our main algorithm.

•

-tomography.
This section shows how to give several useful black-box “upgrades” to any Frobenius-squared estimator.

•

In
Section

-tomography algorithm “except for very small eigenvalues”.

•

The most involved

follows; it fills in all the technical details for the preceding sketch.

•

Section

3.5

Section

•

Finally, for the convenience of the reader,

3.1
Qubit tomography with single-copy measurements

As mentioned in
Section

1.1
, it has long been known that one can learn a single qubit state
\rho
to infidelity
\epsilon
using
single-copy
measurements on
O(1/\epsilon)
copies of
\rho
, combined with one “round” of adaptivity.
In this section we give a short proof of the same result but with a stronger conclusion:
\epsilon
accuracy with respect to Bures
\chi^{2}
-divergence.

We first repeat
Proposition

2.16
in the simpler context of
d=2
, and at the same time achieving a concentration bound:

3.1
.

There is a simple classical estimation algorithm that, given
n=O(\log(1/\delta)/\epsilon)
samples from an unknown probability distribution
p=(p_{0},p_{1})
on
\{0,1\}
, outputs an estimate
\widehat{\boldsymbol{p}}
satisfying
\mathrm{d}_{\chi^{2}}(p\mathrel{}\mathclose{}\|\mathopen{}\mathrel{}\widehat{\boldsymbol{p}})\leq\epsilon
except with probability at most
\delta
.

Proof.

As shown in
Proposition

2.16
, if
\widehat{\boldsymbol{q}}
is the “add-one estimator” formed from
m\geq 4/\epsilon
samples, then
\E[\mathrm{d}_{\chi^{2}}(p\mathrel{}\mathclose{}\|\mathopen{}\mathrel{}\widehat{\boldsymbol{q}})]\leq\frac{1}{m+1}\leq\epsilon/4
.
By Markov’s inequality, the estimator is “good”, meaning
\mathrm{d}_{\chi^{2}}(p\mathrel{}\mathclose{}\|\mathopen{}\mathrel{}\widehat{\boldsymbol{q}})\leq\epsilon
, except with probability at most
1/4
.
If we now use
n=O(\log(1/\delta)/\epsilon)
samples to produce
O(1/\delta)
independent such estimators, a Chernoff bound tells us that, except with probability at most
\delta
, at least a
2/3
fraction of them are “good”.
If we now associate each of our estimates
\widehat{\boldsymbol{q}}=(\widehat{\boldsymbol{q}}_{0},\widehat{\boldsymbol{q}}_{1})
with the point
\widehat{\boldsymbol{q}}_{1}
in the interval
[0,1]
, we see that all of the “good” points appear consecutively.
(That is, “reading left-to-right”, the
\widehat{\boldsymbol{q}}_{1}
values consist of some “bad” points, followed by some “good” points, followed by some “bad” points.)
The reason for this is that
\mathrm{d}_{\chi^{2}}(p\mathrel{}\mathclose{}\|\mathopen{}\mathrel{}\widehat{\boldsymbol{q}})
is a monotonic function of
|p_{1}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{q}}_{1}|
.
Thus if the algorithm now selects the median
\widehat{\boldsymbol{q}}_{1}
point (and its associated estimate
\widehat{\boldsymbol{q}}=(\widehat{\boldsymbol{q}}_{0},\widehat{\boldsymbol{q}}_{1})
), this will be among the
2/3
-fraction “good” points except with probability at most
\delta
.∎

3.2
.

There is an efficient quantum state tomography algorithm that uses
n=O(\log(1/\delta)/\epsilon)
copies of an unknown qubit state
\rho\in\mathbbm{C}^{2\times 2}
and outputs an estimate
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}
satisfying
\mathrm{D}_{\chi^{2}}(\rho\mathrel{}\mathclose{}\|\mathopen{}\mathrel{}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\rho}})\leq\epsilon
except with probability at most
\delta
.
Moreover, the algorithm is simple to implement in the following sense: The first
n/4
copies of
\rho
are separately measured in the Pauli
X
basis, the next
n/4
in the Pauli
Y
basis, the next
n/4
in the Pauli
Z
basis, and the final
n/4
in a fixed basis determined by the first
3n/4
measurement outcomes.

Proof.

The first phase of the algorithm (using
3n/4
copies)
can employ any standard single-copy quantum state tomography routine; the specific one we describe in
Section

3.6
has the stated Pauli format, and (using
Proposition

2.15
) will return a PSD matrix
\boldsymbol{\rho}^{\prime}
(not necessarily a state) satisfying

\|\rho-\boldsymbol{\rho}^{\prime}\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}\leq\epsilon/4

(41)

except with probability at most
\delta/2
.
Next, the algorithm employs a change of basis so as to make
\boldsymbol{\rho}^{\prime}
diagonal.
It suffices to estimate
\rho
in this new basis.
Since Frobenius-distance is unitarily invariant, in the new basis
Equation

41
implies

|\rho_{01}|^{2}=|\rho_{10}|^{2}\leq\epsilon/8.

(42)

As for the diagonal entries
p=(\rho_{00},\rho_{11})
of
\rho
, the algorithm measures its remaining
n/4
copies of
\rho
in the diagonal basis and employs
Lemma

3.1
.
For
n=O(\log(1/\delta)/\epsilon)
, this produces an estimate
\widehat{\boldsymbol{p}}
satisfying
\mathrm{d}_{\chi^{2}}(p\mathrel{}\mathclose{}\|\mathopen{}\mathrel{}\widehat{\boldsymbol{p}})\leq\epsilon/2
except with probability at most
\delta/2
.
The final estimate of
\rho
(in the new basis) will be
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}=\diag(\widehat{\boldsymbol{p}})
.

Except with probability at most
\delta/2+\delta/2=\delta
, both components of the preceding algorithm produce good estimates. Then using
Equation

33
we may decompose
\mathrm{D}_{\chi^{2}}(\rho\mathrel{}\mathclose{}\|\mathopen{}\mathrel{}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\rho}})
into the on-diagonal contribution, which is

\mathrm{d}_{\chi^{2}}(p\mathrel{}\mathclose{}\|\mathopen{}\mathrel{}\widehat{\boldsymbol{p}})\leq\epsilon/2
, and the off-diagonal contribution, which is
2|\rho_{01}|^{2}+2|\rho_{10}|^{2}\leq\epsilon/2
(by
Equation

42
.
This completes the proof.
∎

3.2
Upgrading Frobenius-squared tomography algorithms

3.3
.

A function
f
mapping quantum states to numbers at least
1
will be called a
rate function
.

3.4
.

We say a quantum state estimation algorithm
\mathcal{A}
has
Frobenius-squared rate
f
if the following holds:
Whenever
\mathcal{A}
is given
m\in\mathbbm{N}^{+}
as well as
\rho^{\otimes m}
for some quantum state
\rho\in\mathbbm{C}^{d\times d}
, it outputs a matrix
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}\in\mathbbm{C}^{d\times d}
(not necessarily a state) satisfying
\E[\|\rho-\widehat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}]\leq f(\rho)/m
.

Theorems

1.3
and
1.1
may be restated as follows:

3.5
.

There is an estimation algorithm with Frobenius-squared rate
O(d)
on
d
-dimensional states.

3.6
.

There is an estimation algorithm using single-copy measurements with Frobenius-squared rate
O(rd)
on
d
-dimensional states of rank at most
r
.

Finally,
Proposition

3.25
gives a simple single-copy measurement algorithm that has Frobenius-squared rate
O(d^{2})
(matching matching
Theorem

3.6
in the high-rank case).

We will now successively describe several black-box “upgrades” one may make to a Frobenius-squared estimation algorithm.

All of these will have the feature that they preserve the single-copy measurement property.

Our ultimate goal will be to upgrade to closeness guarantees with respect to much stronger distance measures, with minimal loss in rate.
To illustrate the idea, we start with a very simple upgrade (that most natural algorithms are unlikely to need):

3.7
.

A Frobenius-squared estimation algorithm may be transformed to one that always outputs Hermitian estimates, with no loss in rate.

Proof.

Given algorithm
\mathcal{A}
, let
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}
be the algorithm that on input
\rho
runs
\mathcal{A}
, producing
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}
, and then outputs
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}_{H}\coloneqq(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}+\widehat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}^{\dagger})/2
, so that
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}_{A}\coloneqq(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}^{\dagger})/2
and
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}=\widehat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}_{H}+\widehat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}_{A}
.
The Hermitian matrices are a real vector space, so by picking a (Hilbert–Schmidt) orthogonal basis (for example, the generalized Pauli matrices), is it easy to verify that for Hermitian
\rho
, we always have

\|\widehat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}-\rho\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}=\|\widehat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}_{H}-\rho\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}+\|\widehat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}_{A}\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}\geq\|\widehat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}_{H}-\rho\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}
.
The claim then follows by taking expectations.
∎

The next upgrade is not a change in algorithm, but rather in terminology.

3.8
.

Say that an estimation algorithm with Frobenius-squared rate
f

returns diagonal estimates
if, when run on
\rho\in\mathbbm{C}^{d\times d}
, it returns a unitary
\boldsymbol{U}
and a (real) diagonal matrix
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}=\diag(\boldsymbol{q})
with
\boldsymbol{q}_{1}\leq\boldsymbol{q}_{2}\leq\cdots\leq\boldsymbol{q}_{d}
such that
\E[\|\boldsymbol{U}\rho\boldsymbol{U}^{\dagger}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}]\leq f(\rho)
.

Given such an algorithm, we can get an Frobenius-squared estimator with rate
f
for
\rho
just by returning
\boldsymbol{U}^{\dagger}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}\boldsymbol{U}
.
But we will prefer the interpretation that the algorithm is allowed to “revise”
\rho
to state
\boldsymbol{U}\rho\boldsymbol{U}^{\dagger}
(with
\boldsymbol{U}
of its choosing), and then try to estimate this new state.

3.9
.

A Frobenius-squared estimation algorithm may be transformed to one that returns diagonal estimates, with no loss in rate.

Proof.

First we transform the algorithm to output Hermitian estimates, using
Proposition

3.7
.
Then, given output
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}
,
the algorithm simply chooses a unitary
\boldsymbol{U}
such that
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}=\boldsymbol{U}\diag(\boldsymbol{q})\boldsymbol{U}^{\dagger}
with
\boldsymbol{q}_{1}\leq\cdots\leq\boldsymbol{q}_{d}
, and returns the unitary
\boldsymbol{U}
along with diagonal estimate
\diag(\boldsymbol{q})
. The proof is complete because Frobenius-squared distance is unitarily invariant.
∎

3.10
.

With only constant-factor rate loss, a Frobenius-squared estimation algorithm may be transformed to one that outputs diagonal estimates
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}=\diag(\boldsymbol{q})
that are genuine quantum states, meaning that
\boldsymbol{q}
is a probability vector.

Proof.

First we apply
Proposition

3.9
, obtaining algorithm
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}
with diagonal estimates and rate
f
.
Now our transformed algorithm, when given
m
copies of
\rho
, will start by running
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}
on the first
m/2
copies (we may assume
m
is even), yielding a diagonal estimate
\boldsymbol{\rho}^{\prime}
(and, to be formal, a unitary
\boldsymbol{U}
which should be used to conjugate the remaining copies of
\rho
). Say that
\|\boldsymbol{\rho}^{\prime}-\rho\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}=\boldsymbol{\eta}
, and recall that
\E[\boldsymbol{\eta}]\leq 2f(\rho)/m
. The next step of the algorithm is to use single-copy standard basis measurements with the remaining
m/2
copies of
\rho
to make a new estimate
\boldsymbol{q}
of the diagonal of
\rho
.
Applying
Proposition

2.14
, the empirical estimator
\boldsymbol{q}
is a genuine probability distribution, and the algorithm will finally output
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}=\diag(\boldsymbol{q})
. (Actually, since
\boldsymbol{q}
might not have nondecreasing entries, we should finally “revise” by a permutation matrix.)
The Frobenius-squared error of
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}
is its off-diagonal Frobenius-squared error plus its diagonal Frobenius-squared error; the former is at most
\boldsymbol{\eta}
and the latter is, in expectation, at most
2/m
by
Proposition

2.14
. Since
\E[\boldsymbol{\eta}]\leq 2f(\rho)/m
, the total expected Frobenius-squared error is at most
2f(\rho)/m+2/m=O(f(\rho))/m
, as needed.
∎

We will also need a high-probability version of the preceding result, with some extra properties. The reader should recall the
m_{\delta}
notation from
Proposition

2.15
.

3.11
.

The algorithm from
Proposition

3.10
may be modified so that, given
0<\delta<1/2
, its output satisfies each of the following statements except with probability at most
\delta
(for any fixed
i\in[d]
):

•

\|\rho-\widehat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}\leq f/m_{\delta}
;

•

if
\rho_{ii}\geq 1/m_{\delta}
then
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}_{ii}
is within a
1.01
-factor of
\rho_{ii}
;

•

if
\rho_{ii}\leq 1/m_{\delta}
then
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}_{ii}\leq 1.01/m_{\delta}
.

Proof.

The first statement may be obtained in a black-box way using the “median trick”, which upgrades estimation-in-expectation to estimation-with-confidence-
(1-\delta)
at the expense of only an
O(\log(1/\delta))
sample complexity factor. This trick may be applied whenever the loss measure is a metric (as Frobenius distance is); see, e.g.,
[
22
, Prop. 2.4]
for details. It is sufficient to prove this statement with the
O(\cdot)
, because we may then remove it by raising
c
in the
m_{\delta}
notation. (Similarly, we may tolerate achieving
2\delta
failure probabilities, rather than
\delta
.)

To get the other two conclusions, we need to re-estimate the diagonal of
\rho
, just as we did in
Proposition

3.10
.
For this we use
Proposition

2.15
. As in
Proposition

3.10
, this re-estimation contributes some new on-diagonal Frobenius-squared distance, but only at most
1/m_{\delta}\leq f/m_{\delta}
; thus the proposition’s first statement remains okay.
The remaining statements follow from
Proposition

2.15
by taking its “
S
” to be
\{i\}
.
∎

Now we come to a most important reduction: being able to estimate
subnormalized states
.
Let us define terms, and make the simplifying assumption that rate functions for proper states only depend on dimension and rank, and that they are nondecreasing functions of these parameters.
We also assume for simplicity that our subnormalized states arise just from submatrices, but they could just as well arise from any given projector
\Pi
.

3.12
.

We say a
subnormalized
state estimation algorithm
\mathcal{A}
has Frobenius-squared rate
f(d,r,\tau)
if the following holds:
Whenever
\mathcal{A}
is given a subset
S\subseteq[d]
, as well as
\rho^{\otimes m}
for some quantum state
\rho\in\mathbbm{C}^{d\times d}
of rank at most
r
, it outputs an estimate
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}[S]\in\mathbbm{C}^{S\times S}
such that
\E[\|\rho[S]-\widehat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}[S]\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}]\leq f(d,r,\tau)/m
, where
\tau
denotes
\tr\rho[S]
.

3.13
.

In the above definition, we may also include the condition of “returning diagonal estimates” as in
Definition

3.8
, with the returned unitary
\boldsymbol{U}
being in
\mathbbm{C}^{S\times S}
.
Moreover, for linguistic simplicity we will henceforth assume that “diagonal estimates” are also required to have nonnegative (diagonal) entries.

3.14
.

Our subnormalized state estimation algorithms will actually achieve improved rate
f(d^{\prime},r^{\prime},\tau)
, where
d^{\prime}=|S|\leq d
and
r^{\prime}=\rank\rho[S]\leq r
, but we will not try to squeeze anything out of this, for simplicity.

3.15
.

A state estimation algorithm
\mathcal{A}
with Frobenius-squared rate
f(d,r)
may be transformed to a subnormalized state estimation algorithm
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}
, returning diagonal estimates, and having Frobenius-squared rate
f(d,r,\tau)=O(\tau\cdot f(d,r))
.

Proof.

We first apply
Proposition

3.10
so that
\mathcal{A}
may be assumed to output diagonal, genuine quantum states.
This only changes bounds by constant factors on
m
, to which the statement of this proposition is anyway insensitive.

Given
S\subseteq[d]
and
\rho^{\otimes m}
, let us write
\tau=\tr\rho[S]
and also introduce the quantum state
\rho_{\mid S}=\rho[S]/\tau
(when
\tau>0
).
The first step of the new algorithm
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}
is to measure each copy of
\rho
using the two-outcome PVM
(\mathbbm{1}_{S},\mathbbm{1}_{[d]\setminus S})
.
It retains all copies that have outcome
S
and discards the rest.
In this way,
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}
obtains
(\rho_{\mid S})^{\otimes\boldsymbol{m}^{\prime}}
, where
\boldsymbol{m}^{\prime}\sim\textrm{Binomial}(m,\tau)
.
If
\boldsymbol{m}^{\prime}=0
then the algorithm will return the
0
matrix.
Otherwise, if
\boldsymbol{m}^{\prime}\neq 0
the algorithm applies
\mathcal{A}
to
\rho_{\mid S}
and obtains an estimate
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}_{\mid S}
with expected Frobenius-squared error at most
f(d^{\prime},r^{\prime})/{\boldsymbol{m}^{\prime}}\leq f(d,r)/{\boldsymbol{m}^{\prime}}
, where
d^{\prime}=|S|
,
r^{\prime}=\rank\rho[S]
.
The final estimate that
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}
produces for
\rho[S]
will be
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}[S]\coloneqq(\boldsymbol{m}^{\prime}/m)\widehat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}_{\mid S}
; indeed, we can use this expression even in the
\boldsymbol{m}^{\prime}=0
case.
We now have

\E\bigl[\|\rho[S]-\widehat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}[S]\|^{2}_{\mathrm{F}}\bigr]=\E\bigl[\|\tau\rho_{\mid S}-(\boldsymbol{m}^{\prime}/m)\widehat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}_{\mid S}\|^{2}_{\mathrm{F}}\bigr]=\tau^{2}\E\bigl[\|\rho_{\mid S}-(\boldsymbol{m}^{\prime}/(\tau m))\widehat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}_{\mid S}\|^{2}_{\mathrm{F}}\bigr].

(43)

We write

\rho_{\mid S}-(\boldsymbol{m}^{\prime}/(\tau m))\widehat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}_{\mid S}=\boldsymbol{\Delta}+\boldsymbol{R},\quad\boldsymbol{\Delta}\coloneqq\rho_{\mid S}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}_{\mid S},\quad\boldsymbol{R}\coloneqq(1-\boldsymbol{m}^{\prime}/(\tau m))\widehat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}_{\mid S},

(44)

and use

\E\bigl[\|\boldsymbol{\Delta}+\boldsymbol{R}\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}\bigr]\leq 2\E\bigl[\|\boldsymbol{\Delta}\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}\bigr]+2\E\bigl[\|\boldsymbol{R}\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}\bigr].

(45)

By assumption on
\mathcal{A}
, for
m^{\prime}>0
we have

\E\bigl[\|\boldsymbol{\Delta}\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}\mid\boldsymbol{m}^{\prime}=m^{\prime}\bigr]\leq f(d,r)/m^{\prime}\leq 2f(d,r)/(m^{\prime}+1),

(46)

and this is also true even for
m^{\prime}=0
(recall we always assume
f\geq 1
).
Using the elementary fact
\E\bigl[\frac{1}{\mathrm{Bin}(m,\tau)+1}\bigr]=\frac{1-(1-\tau)^{m+1}}{\tau(m+1)}\leq 1/(\tau m)
, we conclude

\E\bigl[\|\boldsymbol{\Delta}\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}\bigr]\leq 2f(d^{\prime},r^{\prime})/(\tau m).

(47)

As for
\E\bigl[\|\boldsymbol{R}\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}\bigr]
, let us first observe that conditioned on any
\boldsymbol{m}^{\prime}=m
(including
m=0
), we have
\|\widehat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}_{\mid S}\|_{\mathrm{F}}\leq 1
with certainty, simply because
\mathcal{A}
always outputs a genuine quantum state.
Thus

\E\bigl[\|\boldsymbol{R}\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}\bigr]\leq\E\bigl[(1-\boldsymbol{m}^{\prime}/(\tau m))^{2}\bigr]=(1-\tau)/(\tau m)\leq 1/(\tau m).

(48)

Combining all of the above (and using
f\geq 1
again), we conclude

\E\bigl[\|\rho[S]-\widehat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}[S]\|^{2}_{\mathrm{F}}\bigr]\leq\tau^{2}\cdot O(f(d,r)/(\tau m))
, as needed.
∎

Finally, we use
Proposition

3.11
to obtain some high-probability guarantees:

.

A state estimation algorithm having Frobenius-squared rate
f(d,r)

Given parameters

,
\delta

\widehat{\boldsymbol{\tau}}
and a (diagonal) estimate

and recalling the notation
m_{\delta}=m/(c\ln(1/\delta))

is some universal constant), we have the following:

(i)

if
\tau\leq 1/m_{\delta}

(ii)

if
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\tau}}\leq 1.1/m_{\delta}

;

(iii)

the quantities
\tau

\widehat{\boldsymbol{\tau}}
, and

(iv)

\|\rho[S]-\widehat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}[S]\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}\leq\tau\cdot f(d,r)/m_{\delta}

simultaneously for all

\widehat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}_{ii}

, except with probability at most

simultaneously for all
i\in S

\widehat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}_{ii}\leq 1.1\theta

Since the definition of

’s value to compensate). In particular, for notational simplicity we assume that we get
2m

m

The algorithm begins by using the first

is
\boldsymbol{m}^{\prime}/m

(assuming

Similarly, the

The conclusion in

(ii)
then holds except with probability at most

We now describe how the remainder of the algorithm proceeds, when

.
Note that since it only remains to prove

(iii)
,
(iv)
,
(v)
and
(vi)

.
The algorithm proceeds similarly to

3.3
The plan for learning in
\chi^{2}
: refining diagonal estimates on submatrices

Suppose we have come up with a diagonal estimate
\sigma_{1}
of
\rho\in\mathbbm{C}^{d\times d}
having some Frobenius-squared distance
\eta_{1}=\|\rho-\sigma_{1}\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}
.
(Here we will have “revised” some original
\rho
by the unitary that makes
\sigma_{1}
diagonal; this revision will be taken into account in all future uses of
\rho

Suppose we now choose some
d_{2}\leq d_{1}\coloneqq d
, define
\rho_{2}
to be the top-left
d_{2}\times d_{2}
submatrix of
\rho
, and apply
Proposition

3.16
to it. The idea is that we hope to improve the top-left part of our estimate
\sigma_{1}
.

Recall that
Proposition

3.16
affords us a diagonal estimate
\sigma_{2}\in\mathbbm{C}^{d_{2}\times d_{2}}
; let us understand a little more carefully what this means.
The algorithm will give us a unitary
U_{2}\in\mathbbm{C}^{d_{2}\times d_{2}}
such that
\|U_{2}\rho_{2}U_{2}^{\dagger}-\sigma_{2}\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}=\eta_{2}
for some small value
\eta_{2}
.
The idea now is to “revise” both
\rho_{1}\coloneqq\rho
and
\sigma_{1}
by the unitary
U_{2}\oplus\mathbbm{1}
, where here
\mathbbm{1}
has dimension
d_{1}-d_{2}
.
By design, the revised version of
\rho_{2}
will have Frobenius-squared distance
\eta_{2}
from
\sigma_{2}
.
Moreover, after revision, the fact that
\|\rho_{1}-\sigma_{1}\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}=\eta_{1}
is unchanged (since Frobenius distance is unitarily invariant).
On the other hand, although
\sigma_{1}
was previously diagonal, it no longer will be after revision.
But it’s easy to see that it will remain diagonal
except
on its top-left
d_{2}\times d_{2}
block, which we are intending to replace by
\sigma_{2}
anyway.
In particular, the
off-diagonal

d_{2}\times(d_{1}-d_{2})
and

\sigma_{1}
remain zero.

Let us summarize.
We will first obtain a diagonal estimate
\sigma_{1}
of
\rho_{1}
with some error
\eta_{1}
.
Then after choosing some
d_{2}\in[d_{1}]
, we will obtain a further diagonal estimate
\sigma_{2}
of the top-left
d_{2}\times d_{2}
block of
\rho

\eta_{2}

We might then take as final estimate

the diagonal matrix formed by replacing the top-left
d_{2}\times d_{2}
block of
\sigma_{1}
by
\sigma_{2}
.

Naturally, this plan can be iterated (meaning we can try to improve the estimate’s top-left

block for some

) but let us pause here to discuss error.
If we’re interested in the Frobenius-squared error of our current estimate

, we can’t say more than that it is bounded by
\eta_{1}+\eta_{2}
.
Here we’re decomposing the error into the contribution from the top-left
d_{2}\times d_{2}
block (which is
\eta_{2}
) plus the contribution from the remaining
\boldsymbol{\lrcorner}
-shaped region (consisting of the bottom-right
(d_{1}-d_{2})\times(d_{1}-d_{2})

We will just bound this second error contribution by the whole Frobenius-squared distance of
\sigma_{1}
from
\rho_{1}
, which is
\eta_{1}
.

It would seem that this scheme of refining our estimate for the top-left block hasn’t helped, since it took us from Frobenius-squared error
\eta_{1}
to Frobenius-squared error (at most)

.
But the idea is that our new estimate

may have improved

-divergence

Recall the formula for

-divergence,
Equation

33

might not precisely be a state, meaning of trace
1
).
Recall also that our diagonal estimates

will also have nondecreasing entries, meaning

\max(i,j)>d_{2}\end{subarray}}|\rho_{ij}-\widehat{\rho}_{ij}|^{2}\\

3.25

Proof.

It suffices to achieve Frobenius-squared error
d/m

Assume for simplicity

d

Then there is a simple way
[

\mathcal{P}
of the edges of the complete graph on

d-1

Fix a particular matching
M\in\mathcal{P}

(X^{\pm}_{ij})_{\{i,j\}\in M},\quad\text{where}\quad X^{\pm}_{ij}=\frac{1}{2}(\ket{i}\!\bra{i}+\ket{j}\!\bra{j})\pm\frac{1}{2}(\ket{i}\!\bra{j}+\ket{j}\!\bra{i}).

(100)

When we measure
\rho
with this POVM, we obtain outcome
(\{i,j\},\pm)
with probability

p_{ij}\pm r_{ij},\quad p_{ij}\coloneqq\avg\{\rho_{ii},\rho_{jj}\},\quad r_{ij}\coloneqq\real\rho_{ij}.

(101)

If we similarly define a POVM
(Y_{ij}^{\pm})

\mathrm{i}=\sqrt{-1}

Equation

100

p_{ij}\pm s_{ij}

s_{ij}\coloneqq\imaginary\rho_{ij}

We focus on analyzing
Equation

101
, as the imaginary-part analysis will be identical.

Suppose we now measure this POVM
m
times and form the random variables
\widehat{\boldsymbol{r}}_{ij}
(for
\{i,j\}\in M
), where

\widehat{\boldsymbol{r}}_{ij}=\frac{\boldsymbol{f}^{+}_{ij}-\boldsymbol{f}^{-}_{ij}}{2},\quad\text{with}\quad\boldsymbol{f}^{\pm}_{ij}=\text{fraction of outcomes that are $(\{i,j\},\pm)$}.

(102)

Then
\E[\widehat{\boldsymbol{r}}_{ij}]=r_{ij}
, and

\E[(r_{ij}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{r}}_{ij})^{2}]=\Var[\widehat{\boldsymbol{r}}_{ij}]\leq\frac{1}{2}\Var[\boldsymbol{f}^{+}_{ij}]+\frac{1}{2}\Var[\boldsymbol{f}^{-}_{ij}]\leq\frac{p_{ij}+r_{ij}}{2m}+\frac{p_{ij}-r_{ij}}{2m}=p_{ij}/m.

(103)

Repeating the analysis for the imaginary parts, we use
2m
copies of
\rho
to get estimates for all
\rho_{ij}
,
\{i,j\}\in M
, achieving total expected squared-error

\sum_{\{i,j\}\in M}2p_{ij}/m=\sum_{i\in[d]}\rho_{ii}/m=1/m.

(104)

Repeating this for all
M\in\mathcal{P}
uses
O(dm)
copies of
\rho
and gives estimates for all off-diagonal elements of
\rho
, with total expected squared-error
(d-1)/m
.
Finally, we can use standard basis measurements to estimate the diagonal elements of
\rho
, using
Proposition

2.14
:
m
more copies of
\rho
suffice to achieve total expected squared-error
1/m
.
∎

4
Testing zero mutual information

We now move on to showing the main application of our
\chi^{2}
tomography algorithm: testing zero quantum mutual information.
We will explain below in
Section

4.3
how our
\chi^{2}
tomography algorithm is crucial to achieving this result.
But first, we introduce and analyze a variant of the quantum mutual information that features in our analysis.

4.1
Mutual information versus its Hellinger variant

The goal of this subsection is to prove the below theorem, showing that the standard quantum mutual information is not much larger than the “Hellinger mutual information”:

4.1
.

Let
\rho=\rho_{AB}
be a bipartite quantum state on
A\otimes B
, where
A\cong B\cong\mathbbm{C}^{d}
.
Writing
\mathrm{D}^{2}_{\mathrm{H}}(\rho,\rho_{A}\otimes\rho_{B})=\eta
, it holds that
I(A:B)_{\rho}\leq\eta\cdot O(\log(d/\eta))
.

We also observe that by restricting
\rho_{AB}
to be diagonal, we immediately obtain the analogous theorem concerning classical mutual information.
We remark that proving this classical version directly is no easier than proving the quantum version.

4.2
.

Let
p=p_{AB}
be bipartite classical state on
A\times B
, where
|A|=|B|=d
.
Writing
\mathrm{d}^{2}_{\mathrm{H}}(p,p_{A}\times p_{B})=\eta
, it holds that
I(A:B)_{p}\leq\eta\cdot O(\log(d/\eta))
.

We first state a bound on the continuity of mutual information in terms of the trace distance and the subsystem dimension.
A bound of the following form can be proven a number of ways, for example by appealing to the Petz–Fannes–Audenaert
[
4
]
and Alicki–Fannes
[
2
]
inequalities; see
[
18
, Appendix F]
.
The bound we use is an immediate corollary of
[
40
, Prop. 1]
which gives small explicit constants.

4.3
Continuity of quantum mutual information.

For two density operator
\rho
,
\sigma
on
A\otimes B
with
A\cong B\cong\mathbb{C}^{d}
and
\tfrac{1}{2}\|\rho-\sigma\|_{1}=\epsilon
, we have

|I(A:B)_{\rho}-I(A:B)_{\sigma}|\leq 2\epsilon\log\frac{4d}{\epsilon}\,.

(105)

Proof.

The bound from
[
40
, Prop. 1]
for the quantum conditional mutual information applies immediately with a trivial conditioning system and with the bound
\epsilon\leq 1
to get the constant 4.
∎

We also must bound how much
\mathrm{D}^{2}_{\mathrm{H}}(\rho,\rho_{A}\otimes\rho_{B})
changes relative to a depolarization of
\rho
.

4.4
.

Given a density operator
\rho
on
A\otimes B
with
A\cong B\cong\mathbb{C}^{d}
and the depolarization
\sigma=\Delta_{\epsilon}(\sigma)=(1-\epsilon)\rho+\frac{\epsilon}{d^{2}}\mathbbm{1}
, the squared Hellinger distance obeys

\mathrm{D}^{2}_{\mathrm{H}}(\sigma,\sigma_{A}\otimes\sigma_{B})\leq C\sqrt{\epsilon}+\mathrm{D}^{2}_{\mathrm{H}}(\rho,\rho_{A}\otimes\rho_{B})\,,

(106)

where the constant can be chosen as
C=4+4\sqrt{2}
.

Proof.

Since
\mathrm{D}_{\mathrm{H}}(\rho,\sigma)
is a metric, we have

\mathrm{D}_{\mathrm{H}}(\sigma,\sigma_{A}\otimes\sigma_{B})\leq\mathrm{D}_{\mathrm{H}}(\sigma,\rho)+\mathrm{D}_{\mathrm{H}}(\rho,\rho_{A}\otimes\rho_{B})+\mathrm{D}_{\mathrm{H}}(\rho_{A}\otimes\rho_{B},\sigma_{A}\otimes\sigma_{B})\,.

(107)

By
Proposition

2.31
we have
\mathrm{D}_{\mathrm{H}}(\rho,\sigma)\leq\sqrt{2\mathrm{D}_{\mathrm{tr}}(\rho,\sigma)}
and we have
\mathrm{D}_{\mathrm{tr}}(\rho,\sigma)\leq\epsilon
by the triangle inequality.
By the triangle inequality and quantum data processing inequality (monotonicity of trace distance under CPTP maps), we also have
\mathrm{D}_{\mathrm{tr}}(\rho_{A}\otimes\rho_{B},\sigma_{A}\otimes\sigma_{B})\leq\mathrm{D}_{\mathrm{tr}}(\rho_{A},\sigma_{A})+\mathrm{D}_{\mathrm{tr}}(\rho_{B},\sigma_{B})\leq 2\epsilon
.
Plugging this in above and rearranging, we have

\mathrm{D}_{\mathrm{H}}(\sigma,\sigma_{A}\otimes\sigma_{B})-\mathrm{D}_{\mathrm{H}}(\rho,\rho_{A}\otimes\rho_{B})\leq(2+\sqrt{2})\sqrt{\epsilon}\,.

(108)

For any two states
\rho,\sigma
,
\mathrm{D}_{\mathrm{H}}(\rho,\sigma)\leq\sqrt{2}
, so we can multiply both sides by
\mathrm{D}_{\mathrm{H}}(\sigma,\sigma_{A}\otimes\sigma_{B})+\mathrm{D}_{\mathrm{H}}(\rho,\rho_{A}\otimes\rho_{B})\leq 2\sqrt{2}
and the bound follows.
∎

Now we can prove
Theorem

4.1
.

Proof of
Theorem

4.1
.

We begin by smoothing the state
\rho
with a depolarizing channel to obtain

\sigma=(1-\epsilon)\rho+\frac{\epsilon}{d^{2}}\mathbbm{1}\,.

(109)

By the triangle inequality, we have
\tfrac{1}{2}\|\rho-\sigma\|_{1}=\tfrac{\epsilon}{2}\|\rho-\mathbbm{1}/d^{2}\|_{1}\leq\epsilon
.
The change in the mutual information by passing from
\rho\to\sigma
is bounded using
Lemma

4.3
,

I(A:B)_{\rho}\leq I(A:B)_{\sigma}+2\epsilon\log\frac{4d}{\epsilon}\,.

(110)

Note that
I(A:B)_{\rho}\geq I(A:B)_{\sigma}
by the quantum data processing inequality for relative entropy (monotonicity of mutual information under local CPTP maps).

By
Theorem

2.32
, we have

I(A:B)_{\sigma}\leq(2+\mathrm{D}^{\textnormal{R\'{e}n}}_{\mathrm{\infty}}(\sigma\mathrel{}\mathclose{}\|\mathopen{}\mathrel{}\sigma_{A}\otimes\sigma_{B}))\cdot\mathrm{D}^{2}_{\mathrm{H}}(\sigma,\sigma_{A}\otimes\sigma_{B})\,.

(111)

Since
\sigma-\frac{\epsilon}{d^{2}}\mathbbm{1}\geq 0
, the positivity of the partial trace map shows that the reduced states
\sigma_{A}
and
\sigma_{B}
satisfy

\sigma_{A}\geq\frac{\epsilon}{d}\mathbbm{1}\,,\text{ and}\quad\sigma_{B}\geq\frac{\epsilon}{d}\mathbbm{1}\,.

(112)

Therefore the Rényi entropy term is bounded using
2.29
as

\mathrm{D}^{\textnormal{R\'{e}n}}_{\mathrm{\infty}}(\sigma\mathrel{}\mathclose{}\|\mathopen{}\mathrel{}\sigma_{A}\otimes\sigma_{B}))\leq\log\|\sigma_{A}^{-1}\otimes\sigma_{B}^{-1}\|\leq\log\frac{d^{2}}{\epsilon^{2}}\,.

(113)

Using
Lemma

4.4
, the
\mathrm{D}^{2}_{\mathrm{H}}(\sigma,\sigma_{A}\otimes\sigma_{B})
term is bounded by

\mathrm{D}^{2}_{\mathrm{H}}(\sigma,\sigma_{A}\otimes\sigma_{B})\leq C\sqrt{\epsilon}+\mathrm{D}^{2}_{\mathrm{H}}(\rho,\rho_{A}\otimes\rho_{B})=C\sqrt{\epsilon}+\eta\,.

(114)

Putting this all together, we have that

I(A:B)_{\rho}\leq\Bigl(2+\log\frac{d^{2}}{\epsilon^{2}}\Bigr)\cdot\bigl(C\sqrt{\epsilon}+\eta\bigr)+2\epsilon\log\frac{4d}{\epsilon}\leq(\eta+\sqrt{\epsilon})\,O\bigl(\log(d/\eps)\bigr)\,.

(115)

Choosing
\epsilon=O(\eta^{2})
completes the proof.
∎

4.2
Testing zero classical mutual information

Before moving to the trickier quantum case, we warm up by showing
an efficient tester for classical mutual information, establishing
Theorem

1.18
.
First we give a short proof of the following (which appears explicitly as
[
1
, Lem. 7]
):

4.5
.

Given
n=O(d/\epsilon)
samples from two distributions
q,q^{\prime}
on
[d]
, one can output a hypothesis
\widehat{\boldsymbol{q}}\times\widehat{\boldsymbol{q}}^{\prime}
that (with probability at least
.99
) satisfies
\mathrm{d}_{\chi^{2}}(q\times q^{\prime}\mathrel{}\mathclose{}\|\mathopen{}\mathrel{}\widehat{\boldsymbol{q}}\times\widehat{\boldsymbol{q}}^{\prime})\leq\epsilon
.

Proof.

It suffices to separately learn each of
q,q^{\prime}
to
\chi^{2}
-accuracy
\epsilon/3
and high confidence (which can be done applying
Proposition

2.16
and Markov’s inequality), and then apply the below
Proposition

4.6
.
∎

4.6
.

Let
\mathrm{d}_{\chi^{2}}(p\mathrel{}\mathclose{}\|\mathopen{}\mathrel{}q)=\epsilon_{1}
,
\mathrm{d}_{\chi^{2}}(p^{\prime}\mathrel{}\mathclose{}\|\mathopen{}\mathrel{}q^{\prime})=\epsilon_{2}
.
Then we have the near-additivity formula

\mathrm{d}_{\chi^{2}}(p\otimes p^{\prime}\mathrel{}\mathclose{}\|\mathopen{}\mathrel{}q\otimes q^{\prime})=(1+\epsilon_{1})(1+\epsilon_{2})-1=\epsilon_{1}+\epsilon_{2}+\epsilon_{1}\epsilon_{2}.

(116)

Proof.

This follows essentially immediate from the second formula in
Definition

2.9
.
∎

Now to prove
Theorem

1.18
, suppose we are given access to a bipartite probability distribution
p=p_{AB}
on
[d]\times[d]
and we are promised that either
I(A:B)_{p}=0
or
I(A:B)_{p}\geq\epsilon
.
In the former case we have

I(A:B)_{p}=0\quad\implies\quad p=p_{A}\times p_{B}\quad\implies\quad\mathrm{d}_{\chi^{2}}(p\mathrel{}\mathclose{}\|\mathopen{}\mathrel{}\widehat{\boldsymbol{p}}_{A}\times\widehat{\boldsymbol{p}}_{B})\leq c\epsilon/\log(d/\eps)

(117)

with high probability if we estimate the two marginals using
O((d/\epsilon)\cdot\log(d/\eps))
samples as in
Lemma

4.5
.
(Here
c>0
may be any small constant.)
On the other hand, in case
I(A:B)_{p}\geq\epsilon
, we get
\mathrm{d}^{2}_{\mathrm{H}}(p,p_{A}\times p_{B})\geq\Omega(\epsilon/\log(d/\eps))
from
Corollary

4.2
; ensuring that the constant
c
in
Equation

117
is small enough, this implies

\mathrm{d}^{2}_{\mathrm{H}}(p,\widehat{\boldsymbol{p}}_{A}\times\widehat{\boldsymbol{p}}_{B})\geq\Omega(\epsilon/\log(d/\eps))

(118)

also.
Now again ensuring
c
is small enough, our classical mutual information tester
Theorem

1.18
follows by using the below “
\chi^{2}
-vs.-
\mathrm{H}^{2}
identity tester” of Daskalakis–Kamath–Wright with the “known” distribution being
\widehat{\boldsymbol{p}}_{A}\times\widehat{\boldsymbol{p}}_{B}
. This distinguishes our two cases (with high probability) using
O(\sqrt{d\times d}/\epsilon^{\prime})
samples,
\epsilon^{\prime}=\Theta(\epsilon/\log(d/\eps))
; in other words, with
O((d/\epsilon)\log(d/\eps))
samples.

4.7
.

(
[
15
, Thm. 1]
.)
For any “known” distribution
q
on
[d]
, there is a testing algorithm with the following guarantee:
Given
0<\epsilon\leq 1/2
, as well as
n=O(\sqrt{d}/\epsilon)
samples from an unknown distribution
p
on
[d]
, if
\mathrm{d}_{\chi^{2}}(p\mathrel{}\mathclose{}\|\mathopen{}\mathrel{}q)\leq\epsilon/2
then the test accepts with probability at least
.99
, and if
\mathrm{d}^{2}_{\mathrm{H}}(p,q)\geq\epsilon
then the test rejects with probability at least
.99
.

4.3
Testing zero quantum mutual information

To prove
Theorem

1.14
, we now endeavor to repeat the result from the previous setting in the quantum case.
Naturally, it is crucially important that we are able to do quantum state tomography with respect to Bures
\chi^{2}
-divergence.
This lets use the following quantum
“
\chi^{2}
-vs.-
\mathrm{H}^{2}
identity tester” from
[
7
]
in place of
Theorem

4.7
.

4.8
.

(
[
7
, Thm. 1]
.)
For any “known” quantum state
\sigma\in\mathbbm{C}^{d\times d}
, there is a testing algorithm with the following guarantee:
Given
0<\epsilon\leq 1/2
, as well as
n=O(d/\epsilon)
copies of an unknown state
\rho\in\mathbbm{C}^{d\times d}
, if
\mathrm{D}_{\chi^{2}}(\rho\mathrel{}\mathclose{}\|\mathopen{}\mathrel{}\sigma)\leq.49\epsilon
then the test accepts with probability at least
.99
, and if
\mathrm{D}^{2}_{\mathrm{H}}(\rho,\sigma)\geq 2\epsilon
then the test rejects with probability at least
.99
.

We can relate quantum mutual information and its Hellinger version using
Theorem

4.1
in place of
Corollary

4.2
.
It would seem then that we could establish our quantum zero mutual information tester
Theorem

1.14
in exactly the same way we did its classical analogue, using
\widetilde{O}(d^{2}/\epsilon)
copies of a bipartite
d\times d
-dimensional state
\rho
.
Unfortunately, there is a missing piece: a quantum analogue of
Lemma

4.5
with
O(d^{2}/\epsilon)
copy complexity.
We prove a slightly worse variant, which leads to our main testing
Theorem

1.14
:

4.9
.

There is a tomography algorithm that, given parameter
0<\epsilon\leq 1/2
and

n=\max\{\widetilde{O}(rd^{1.5}/\epsilon),\;\widetilde{O}(r^{.5}d^{1.75}/\epsilon)\}

(119)

copies of unknown
d
-dimensional quantum states
\xi,\rho
of rank at most
r
, outputs diagonal states
\boldsymbol{\sigma},\boldsymbol{\tau}
such that (with probability at least
.9
),

\mathrm{D}_{\chi^{2}}(\xi\otimes\rho\mathrel{}\mathclose{}\|\mathopen{}\mathrel{}\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{\prime}\otimes\boldsymbol{\tau}^{\prime})\leq\epsilon.

(120)

Proof.

The strategy is to apply our central estimation algorithm in the form of
Corollary

3.22
to both
\xi,\rho
(with the Frobenius-learner from
Theorem

1.3
with
f(d,r)=O(d)
).
We use the parameter choices

\eta=\epsilon,\qquad\tilde{\epsilon}=\epsilon\cdot\min\{1/d^{.5},r^{.5}/d^{.75}\}.

(121)

This leads to the claimed copy complexity from
Equation

119
, and yields (with probability at least
.9
) estimates
\boldsymbol{\sigma},\boldsymbol{\tau}
satisfying

\displaystyle\mathrm{D}^{\textnormal{off}}_{\chi^{2}}(\xi\mathrel{}\mathclose{}\|\mathopen{}\mathrel{}\boldsymbol{\sigma}),\ \mathrm{D}^{\textnormal{off}}_{\chi^{2}}(\rho\mathrel{}\mathclose{}\|\mathopen{}\mathrel{}\boldsymbol{\tau})

\displaystyle\leq\widetilde{O}(\epsilon\cdot(1/d^{.5}+\min\{1/r,r^{.5}/d^{.75}\}))=\widetilde{O}(\epsilon/\sqrt{d}),

(122)

\displaystyle\mathrm{D}^{\textnormal{on}}_{\chi^{2}}(\xi\mathrel{}\mathclose{}\|\mathopen{}\mathrel{}\boldsymbol{\sigma}),\ \mathrm{D}^{\textnormal{off}}_{\chi^{2}}(\rho\mathrel{}\mathclose{}\|\mathopen{}\mathrel{}\boldsymbol{\tau})

\displaystyle\leq\widetilde{O}(\epsilon),

(123)

with
\boldsymbol{\sigma},\boldsymbol{\tau}
having minimum eigenvalue at least
\epsilon/d
.
We claim that for any fixed outcomes
\boldsymbol{\sigma}=\sigma^{\prime}
and
\boldsymbol{\tau}^{\prime}=\tau^{\prime}
satisfying the above, it holds that

\mathrm{D}_{\chi^{2}}(\xi\otimes\rho\mathrel{}\mathclose{}\|\mathopen{}\mathrel{}\sigma^{\prime}\otimes\tau^{\prime})\leq\widetilde{O}(\epsilon).

(124)

This is sufficient to complete the proof.

To establish
Equation

124
, let us write
\sigma^{\prime}=\diag(s^{\prime})
and
\tau^{\prime}=\diag(t^{\prime})
, with

s^{\prime}_{a}\geq\epsilon/d,\quad t^{\prime}_{j}\geq\epsilon/d\qquad\text{for all $a,j\in[d]$.}

(125)

We will break up
\mathrm{D}_{\chi^{2}}(\xi\otimes\rho\mathrel{}\mathclose{}\|\mathopen{}\mathrel{}\sigma^{\prime}\otimes\tau^{\prime})
into three parts: on-on-diagonal, on-off-diagonal, and off-off-diagonal:

\underbrace{\sum_{a,i=1}^{d}\tfrac{1}{s^{\prime}_{a}t^{\prime}_{i}}(\xi_{aa}\rho_{ii}-s^{\prime}_{a}t^{\prime}_{i})^{2}}_{\textrm{(ON-ON)}}+\underbrace{\left\lparen\sum_{a=1}^{d}\sum_{i\neq j}\tfrac{2}{s^{\prime}_{a}t^{\prime}_{i}+s^{\prime}_{a}t^{\prime}_{j}}\absolutevalue{\xi_{aa}\rho_{ij}}^{2}+\sum_{a\neq b}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\tfrac{2}{s^{\prime}_{a}t^{\prime}_{i}+s^{\prime}_{b}t^{\prime}_{i}}\absolutevalue{\xi_{ab}\rho_{ii}}^{2}\right\rparen}_{\textrm{(ON-OFF)}}+\underbrace{\sum_{a\neq b}\sum_{i\neq j}\tfrac{2}{s^{\prime}_{a}t^{\prime}_{i}+s^{\prime}_{b}t^{\prime}_{j}}\absolutevalue{\xi_{ab}\rho_{ij}}^{2}}_{\textrm{(OFF-OFF)}}.

(126)

First, using
Proposition

4.6
,

\textrm{(ON-ON)}=\mathrm{d}_{\chi^{2}}(\diag(\xi)\otimes\diag(\rho)\mathrel{}\mathclose{}\|\mathopen{}\mathrel{}s^{\prime}\otimes t^{\prime})=(1+\mathrm{d}_{\chi^{2}}(\diag(\xi)\mathrel{}\mathclose{}\|\mathopen{}\mathrel{}s^{\prime}))(1+\mathrm{d}_{\chi^{2}}(\diag(\rho)\mathrel{}\mathclose{}\|\mathopen{}\mathrel{}t^{\prime}))-1.

(127)

But
\mathrm{d}_{\chi^{2}}(\diag(\xi)\mathrel{}\mathclose{}\|\mathopen{}\mathrel{}s^{\prime})\leq\mathrm{D}^{\textnormal{on}}_{\chi^{2}}(\xi\mathrel{}\mathclose{}\|\mathopen{}\mathrel{}\sigma^{\prime})\leq\widetilde{O}(\epsilon)
by
Equation

122
, and similarly for
\mathrm{d}_{\chi^{2}}(\diag(\rho)\mathrel{}\mathclose{}\|\mathopen{}\mathrel{}t^{\prime})
, so we conclude from
Equation

127
that
\textrm{(ON-ON)}\leq(1+\widetilde{O}(\epsilon))(1+\widetilde{O}(\epsilon))-1=\widetilde{O}(\epsilon)
, as needed for
Equation

124
.

Moving to (ON-OFF), the first term in it factorizes to

\left\lparen\sum_{a=1}^{d}\tfrac{1}{s^{\prime}_{a}}\xi_{aa}^{2}\right\rparen\left\lparen\sum_{i\neq j}\tfrac{2}{t^{\prime}_{i}+t^{\prime}_{j}}\absolutevalue{\rho_{ij}}^{2}\right\rparen

(128)

The first factor above is precisely

1+\mathrm{d}_{\chi^{2}}(\diag(\xi)\mathrel{}\mathclose{}\|\mathopen{}\mathrel{}s^{\prime})\leq 1+\mathrm{D}^{\textnormal{on}}_{\chi^{2}}(\xi\mathrel{}\mathclose{}\|\mathopen{}\mathrel{}\sigma^{\prime})\leq 1+\widetilde{O}(\epsilon)\leq O(1).

(129)

The second factor in
Equation

128
is

\sum_{i\neq j}\tfrac{2}{t^{\prime}_{i}+t^{\prime}_{j}}\absolutevalue{\rho_{ij}}^{2}=\mathrm{D}^{\textnormal{off}}_{\chi^{2}}(\rho\mathrel{}\mathclose{}\|\mathopen{}\mathrel{}\tau^{\prime})\leq\mathrm{D}_{\chi^{2}}(\rho\mathrel{}\mathclose{}\|\mathopen{}\mathrel{}\tau){t^{\prime}}\leq\widetilde{O}(\epsilon).

(130)

Thus
Equation

128
, and indeed both terms in (ON-OFF), can be bounded by
\widetilde{O}(\epsilon)
, as needed for
Equation

124
.

It remains to bound (OFF-OFF) by
\widetilde{O}(\epsilon)
.
By the AM-GM inequality,

\frac{s^{\prime}_{a}t^{\prime}_{i}+s^{\prime}_{b}t^{\prime}_{j}}{2}\geq\sqrt{s^{\prime}_{a}t^{\prime}_{i}s^{\prime}_{b}t^{\prime}_{j}}=\sqrt{s^{\prime}_{a}s^{\prime}_{b}}\sqrt{t^{\prime}_{i}t^{\prime}_{j}}.

(131)

Of course there is no reverse AM-GM inequality, but we at least have

\sqrt{xy}\geq\min(\sqrt{x/y},\sqrt{y/x})\cdot\frac{x+y}{2}\qquad\forall x,y>0.

(132)

When
(x,y)
is
(s^{\prime}_{a},s^{\prime}_{b})
or
(t^{\prime}_{i},t^{\prime}_{j})
, we have
\min(\sqrt{x/y},\sqrt{y/x})\geq\sqrt{\epsilon/d}
(from
Equation

125
), and hence

\frac{s^{\prime}_{a}t^{\prime}_{i}+s^{\prime}_{b}t^{\prime}_{j}}{2}\geq(\epsilon/d)\cdot\frac{s^{\prime}_{a}+s^{\prime}_{b}}{2}\cdot\frac{t^{\prime}_{i}+t^{\prime}_{j}}{2}

(133)

Putting this into the definition of (OFF-OFF) yields

\textrm{(OFF-OFF)}\leq(d/\epsilon)\cdot\sum_{a\neq b}\sum_{i\neq j}\tfrac{2}{s^{\prime}_{a}+s^{\prime}_{b}}\cdot\tfrac{2}{t^{\prime}_{i}+t^{\prime}_{j}}\cdot\absolutevalue{\xi_{ab} \rho_{ij}}^{2}=(d/\epsilon)\left\lparen\sum_{a\neq b}\tfrac{2}{s^{\prime}_{i}+s^{\prime}_{j}}\absolutevalue{\xi_{ab}}^{2}\right\rparen\left\lparen\sum_{i\neq j}\tfrac{2}{t^{\prime}_{i}+t^{\prime}_{j}}\absolutevalue{\rho_{ij}}^{2}\right\rparen.

(134)

The last factor here is bounded by
\mathrm{D}^{\textnormal{off}}_{\chi^{2}}(\rho\mathrel{}\mathclose{}\|\mathopen{}\mathrel{}\tau^{\prime})
in
Equation

130
, and the similar factor with
s^{\prime}
and
\xi
is similarly bounded.
Hence using
Equation

122
, we indeed get

\textrm{(OFF-OFF)}\leq O(d/\epsilon)\cdot\widetilde{O}(\epsilon/\sqrt{d})\cdot\widetilde{O}(\epsilon/\sqrt{d})=\widetilde{O}(\epsilon),

(135)

completing the proof.
∎

5
Open Problems

One obvious and by now longstanding open question related to our work is learning in infidelity to precision
\epsilon
with
O(rd/\epsilon)
samples, without any logarithms.
This would settle the sample complexity of tomography with infidelity loss up to constant factors.
In light of our work, perhaps we could even ask for more:
Given our result that learning in quantum relative entropy is possible with
\widetilde{O}(rd/\epsilon)
samples, might a similar no-logarithm bound hold here as well?

Our algorithm uses only single-copy measurements, but even these are challenging on present-day quantum computers.
A stronger assumption on measurements is to restrict to product measurements, meaning that all POVM elements factorize into tensor products over subsystems.
We believe this measurement model will require strictly greater sample complexity for learning in
\chi^{2}
-divergence and for quantum mutual information testing than the single-copy case analyzed here.

Regarding quantum mutual information testing, note that in the classical case we could learn product states to
\chi^{2}
-divergence well enough that the entire testing complexity was dominated by the
\chi^{2}
-vs.-Hellinger identity tester.
Unfortunately, in the quantum case we couldn’t quite match this.
Might it be possible to reduce the complexity of testing zero quantum mutual information down to to
\widetilde{O}(d^{2}/\epsilon)
?

For learning in
\chi^{2}
-divergence, it would be interesting to show that
\widetilde{\Omega}(\sqrt{r}d^{1.5}/\epsilon)
is the right lower bound; currently, we have nothing better than the infidelity-tomography lower bound of
\widetilde{\Omega}(rd/\epsilon)
.
As explained in
Remark

3.17
, though, it seems like reducing the upper bound could be difficult even for the case
r=1
.

Although the
Bures

\chi^{2}
-divergence is usually the largest of the “big four” quantities considered in this paper, there are other quantum generalizations of
\chi^{2}
-divergence in the literature that are larger still than Bures
\chi^{2}
-divergence (see, e.g.,
[
37
,
43
]
).
An example is the so-called “standard” quantum
\chi^{2}
-divergence, in which the the arithmetic mean reciprocal-prefactor in
Equation

33
is replaced by a geometric mean.
Similarly, there are also multiple generalizations of the quantum relative entropy besides the “Umegaki” quantum relative entropy
\mathrm{S}({\cdot}\mathrel{}\mathclose{}\|\mathopen{}\mathrel{}{\cdot})
studied herein.
As explained above, the main reason for us to consider learning with respect to
Bures

\chi^{2}
-divergence (as opposed to other metrics) is that it seems necessary for some applications; for example, our quantum mutual information testing problem.
It is an interesting open question to study state tomography with respect to other generalizations of relative entropy and
\chi^{2}
-divergence, and in particular to decide if this is possible while still having
\widetilde{O}(1/\epsilon)
scaling.

More generally, a very interesting direction is to investigate for which quantum learning and testing tasks we can get away with
\widetilde{O}(1/\epsilon)
samples, and for which we require (say)
\widetilde{\Omega}(1/\epsilon^{2})
samples.
