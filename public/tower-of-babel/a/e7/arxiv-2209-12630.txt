Lattice regularizations of θ \theta vacua: Anomalies and qubit models Preprint: IQuS@UW-21-031, INT-PUB-22-027
Mendel Nguyen, Hersh Singh

Abstract
Anomalies are a powerful way to gain insight into possible lattice regularizations of a quantum field theory. In this work, we argue that the continuum anomaly for a given symmetry can be matched by a manifestly-symmetric, local, lattice regularization in the same spacetime dimensionality only if (i) the symmetry action is offsite, or (ii) if the continuum anomaly is reproduced exactly on the lattice. We consider lattice regularizations of a class of prototype models of QCD: the (1+1)-dimensional asymptotically-free Grassmannian nonlinear sigma models ( nl σ \sigma m s) with a θ \theta term. Using the Grassmannian nl σ \sigma m s as a case study, we provide examples of lattice regularizations in which both possibilities are realized. For possibility (i), we argue that Grassmannian nl σ \sigma m s can be obtained from SU ​ ( N ) \text{SU}(N) antiferromagnets with a well-defined continuum limit, reproducing both the infrared physics of θ \theta vacua and the ultraviolet physics of asymptotic freedom. These results enable the application of new classical algorithms to lattice Monte Carlo studies of these quantum field theories, and provide a viable realization suited for their quantum simulation. On the other hand, we show that, perhaps surprisingly, the conventional lattice regularization of θ \theta vacua due to Berg and Lüscher reproduces the anomaly exactly on the lattice, providing a realization of the second possibility.

If the symmetry is offsite, then it cannot be gauged in the usual manner, and therefore the constraint is satisfied. On the other hand, if the symmetry is onsite,

II
Obstructions to lattice regularizations from anomalies

One of the aims of this work is to emphasize how obstructions to regulating a theory on the lattice arising from anomalies guide us in constructing new lattice regularizations.

In this work, we always refer to “anomaly” in the sense of an
’t Hooft anomaly
: a symmetry
G
is said to be ’t Hooft anomalous if it cannot be consistently gauged.
We emphasize that
G
is a genuine symmetry of the theory, and that the presence of an ’t Hooft anomaly does not indicate an inconsistency of the theory.
We shall also confine our attention to
internal
symmetries.

In many cases of interest, the
G
anomaly has a bit more structure.
If
G
is generated by two subgroups
G_{1},G_{2}
such that there is no obstruction to gauging
G_{1}
or
G_{2}
individually,
but gauging
G_{1}
breaks
G_{2}
, or vice versa, then we say there is a
mixed
anomaly between
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
.
In other words,
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
cannot be gauged simultaneously.
In all the cases we consider in this work, we always have a mixed anomaly, although the arguments apply more generally.

From the point of view of lattice regularizations, the presence of an ’t Hooft anomaly shows up as an obstruction to constructing certain type of lattice regularizations:
an anomaly in
G
for a
D
-dimensional continuum theory implies that we cannot have a
D
-dimensional
G
-symmetric lattice regularization with
G
both
manifest
and
onsite
.

By a “manifest” symmetry, we mean an exact symmetry of the theory which is also an invariance of the action.
In contrast, a nonmanifest symmetry is one where the action is noninvariant, but all correlation functions are invariant.
We emphasize that in both cases, we are referring to an exact symmetry of the theory.

By an “onsite” symmetry, we mean that the symmetry group acts on the full Hilbert space as a tensor product of representations on the local Hilbert spaces.
(In bosonic field theories, the full Hilbert space of a lattice model naturally admits a factorization as a tensor product of Hilbert spaces at each lattice site, which we call the
local
Hilbert space.)
We shall also say that a non-onsite symmetry is “offsite.”

If the symmetry
G
were manifest and onsite, then we could trivially gauge them both on the lattice by introducing appropriate link variables, and this would violate the anomaly.
Therefore, a local
G
-symmetric
D
-dimensional lattice regulator for a theory with a
G
anomaly must

(i)

realize
G
offsite, or

(ii)

realize
G
onsite but nonmanifestly.

Another widely-appreciated possibility is that the
D
-dimensional theory arises as the boundary theory of a
(D+1)
-dimensional lattice model in an
spt
phase
Wen 2013
;
Wang and Wen 2022
;
Kaplan 1992
.
In such cases, it is possible to obtain a lattice regulator with an exact onsite symmetry, albeit at the cost of introducing an extra dimension.
Yet another possibility is to consider lattice regulators in which
G
is not a symmetry microscopically but arises at large distances.
In this work, we only consider symmetric lattice regulators in
D
spacetime dimensions, in which case we have only the two possibilities listed above.

As mentioned in the introduction, while possibility (i) is well-known, especially in the context of
lsmoh
theorem in condensed-matter literature
Jian et al. 2018
;
Cho et al. 2017
, possibility (ii) is often missed.
Indeed, the anomaly implies that there must somehow be an obstruction to gauging
G
, while if
G
acts onsite, there seems to be no obstruction to turning on a background gauge field for it. The only possibility, therefore, seems to be that the dependence on the background gauge field cannot be gauge invariant, which is to say that the lattice regulator must

(ii*)

explicitly exhibit the anomaly
.

Indeed, this will be the case in the examples discussed in
Section

IV
. It is also the case in the modified Villain models
Sulejmanpasic and Gattringer 2019
;
Gorantla et al. 2021
;
Anosova et al. 2022a
;
Anosova et al. 2022b
.
We should also note that it may be possible for a lattice regulator with offsite symmetry to also explicitly exhibit an anomaly, such as Ginsparg–Wilson fermions
Lüscher 1998
;
Wang and Wen 2022
.

In this work, we provide examples for both scenarios (i) and (ii), using the
(1+1)
-dimensional
\text{Gr}_{k}(N)

nl
\sigma
m
as a case study.

Continuum limit and asymptotic freedom

An elegant prescription for taking the continuum-limit is provided by the

;
Chandrasekharan and Wiese 1997
;
Chandrasekharan et al. 2002

In this approach, a

There are two ingredients for this to work. The first one is that the
(d+1)
-dimensional model should be in a massless phase, and the second is dimensional reduction.

Let us assume that the two-dimensional model is in broken phase with massless Goldstone modes. (We will discuss the conditions for this in more detail below.)
We take the extent in the extra spatial dimension to be
L_{Y}
.
The low-energy continuum action (without the
\theta

\displaystyle S_{0}=\frac{1}{2g^{2}}\int_{0}^{L_{Y}}dy\int dx\,dt\,\Tr\bm{[}(\partial_{x}P)^{2}+(\partial_{t}P)^{2}+(\partial_{y}P)^{2}\bm{]}

(16)

where

When

\xi
is infinite (Goldstone modes are massless).
Now, if

is reduced and made finite, a new length scale
\xi_{\mathrm{SR}}(L_{Y})

which we may call the

.
For scales smaller than
\xi_{\mathrm{SR}}(L_{Y})

\xi_{\mathrm{SR}}(L_{Y})
, the system is disordered by the fluctuations of the massive Goldstone modes and the symmetry is restored.

For
L_{Y}

\xi_{\mathrm{SR}}(L_{Y})
must be finite. This is because, if it were infinite, then for any finite
L_{Y}
,
the system would be ordered along the
y

But this would imply that the (1+1)-dimensional system is ordered and has a broken continuous symmetry,
which is impossible due to the Coleman–Mermin–Wagner theorem.
In other words, as
L_{Y}
is reduced from being infinite to finite, the Goldstone modes pick up a mass
\sim\xi_{\mathrm{SR}}^{-1}
, which determines the scale at which the system disorders.

Let us assume that we can make
L_{Y}
small enough such that
\xi_{\mathrm{SR}}(L_{Y})\gg L_{Y}
.
Again, the physics is effectively frozen in the

\displaystyle S_{0}

\displaystyle=\frac{L_{Y}}{2g^{2}}\int dx\,dt\,\Tr(\partial_{\mu}P)^{2}

We see that the (1+1)-dimensional theory has an effective coupling
g_{\text{eff}}^{2}=g^{2}/L_{Y}
.
Now, we can use knowledge of the (1+1)-dimensional theory.
Asymptotic freedom in the (1+1)-dimensional Grassmannian models implies that the system develops an exponentially large length scale
\xi_{\mathrm{SR}}\propto e^{L_{Y}/(\beta_{0}g^{2})}
, where
\beta_{0}
is the leading

In the above argument, we assumed that

by starting from

In principle, it possible that this is not valid for some regime of

and dimensional reduction does not occur.
However, an independent argument can be given by considering

shows that

is finite (in lattice units) and grows exponentially in

We note that the above discussion is valid for any

\xi_{\mathrm{SR}}

In general, we have the following hierarchy of length scales

\displaystyle\xi(\theta)\gtrsim\xi_{\mathrm{SR}}\gg L_{Y}\gg a

(17)

Indeed, for the

Choice of representations.

We emphasize this approach strongly depends the representations at each site.
While in principle, for a given

the representations

This is because for the D-theory prescription to work, we need the two-dimensional (

IV
Lattice formulations of the Grassmannian models with Anomalies

In this section, we discuss lattice regularizations of the
\text{Gr}_{k}(N)
models in which both
C
and
\text{PSU}(N)
act onsite. As mentioned above, this can only be compatible with the anomaly of the continuum theory if the anomaly is present on the lattice.

In the continuum, the charge conjugation symmetry at
\theta=\pi
is not manifest in the sense that the action is not invariant but is shifted by
2\pi iQ
. It is thanks to the integer quantization of
Q
that the integrand
\exp(-S)
of the path integral is preserved by
C
.

The statement of the mixed anomaly between the
\text{PSU}(N)
symmetry and
C
is that when background gauge fields for the former are present, the partition function is no longer invariant under the latter
Gaiotto et al. 2017
;
Komargodski et al. 2019
;
Komargodski et al. 2018
;
Cordova et al. 2020
;
Dunne et al. 2018
. At a technical level, this is due to the fact that the presence of such background fields replaces the topological charge quantization in units of
1
, which as we just said was necessary for
C
invariance at
\theta=\pi
, by quantization in units of
1/N
. This anomaly can therefore be regarded as a consequence of the more basic fact that in the presence of background fields for the
\text{PSU}(N)
symmetry, the period of the
\theta
dependence should become
2\pi N
so that the partition function fails to be
2\pi
periodic in
\theta
once the
\text{PSU}(N)
symmetry is gauged. We may describe this more basic situation as a mixed anomaly between
\text{PSU}(N)
symmetry and
2\pi
periodicity in
\theta
.

All this is equally true in the lattice models we shall describe below.

IV.1
Berg–Lüscher
\theta

We begin with a discretization on a two-dimensional triangular Euclidean spacetime lattice based on the continuum action (
2

\displaystyle S\bm{[}P\bm{]}=-\frac{1}{g^{2}}\tsum_{\langle xy\rangle}\Tr P_{x}P_{y}-i\theta\tsum_{p}q_{p},

(18)

where the first sum runs over all nearest-neighbor bonds

on the triangular lattice, the second sum runs over all positively oriented triangular plaquettes
p

are Hermitian

projection matrices such that

is the topological charge density to be defined below.
The choice of a triangular lattice is motivated by the definition of the topological charge density.
At

\text{Gr}_{k}(N)

For

There is a symmetry of the action under

For

\displaystyle\exp(2\pi iq_{p})=\frac{\Tr P_{x}P_{y}P_{z}}{\lvert\Tr P_{x}P_{y}P_{z}\rvert}.

The branch ambiguity implied by this equation is fixed by requiring that

The total topological charge

Notice that
\text{C}\colon q_{p}\mapsto-q_{p}

We shall generalize this definition of the topological charge for
\text{Gr}_{k}(N)

Our strategy is to background-gauge the

\theta

\displaystyle P_{x}\mapsto v_{x}P_{x}v_{x}{}^{\!{\dagger}},\ V_{xy}\mapsto v_{x}V_{xy}v_{y}{}^{\!{\dagger}}.

This can be achieved by modifying the kinetic part of the action in

18

\displaystyle\tilde{S}_{0}\bm{[}P,V\bm{]}\equiv-\beta\tsum_{\langle xy\rangle}\Tr P_{x}V_{xy}P_{y}V_{yx}.

and the topological charge density in

19

\displaystyle\exp(2\pi i\hat{q}_{p})\equiv\frac{\Tr P_{x}V_{xy}P_{y}V_{yz}P_{z}V_{zx}}{\lvert\Tr P_{x}V_{xy}P_{y}V_{yz}P_{z}V_{zx}\rvert}.

However, we are gauging not

\displaystyle V_{\ell}\mapsto V_{\ell}\exp(2\pi in_{\ell}/N)

\displaystyle\exp(2\pi i\hat{q}_{p})\mapsto\exp(2\pi i\hat{q}_{p})\tprod_{\ell\in p}\exp(2\pi in_{\ell}/N).

\det U_{\mu}(x)\to\exp(a\Tr A_{\mu}(x))
(
a
is the lattice spacing). For
\text{CP}(N-1)
, this reduces to the definition used in
Seiberg 1984
.
The lattice action has a gauge symmetry under

\displaystyle\phi_{x}\mapsto\phi_{x}u_{x},\ U_{xy}\mapsto u_{x}{}^{\!{\dagger}}U_{xy}u_{y},

(37)

where
u_{x}\in\text{U}(k)
. It also has a global symmetry under

\displaystyle\phi_{x}\mapsto v\phi_{x},\ U_{\ell}\mapsto U_{\ell},

(38)

for
v\in\text{SU}(N)
. But for
v
in the center of
\text{SU}(N)
, i.e.,
v=\exp(2\pi in/N)\mathbf{1}_{N}
, this transformation is equivalent to a gauge transformation with
u_{x}=\exp(2\pi in/N)\mathbf{1}_{k}
, so as before the true symmetry group is
\text{PSU}(N)
. Finally, we have charge conjugation, which acts as

\displaystyle\text{C}\colon\phi_{x}\mapsto\phi_{x}{}^{\!*},\ U_{\ell}\mapsto U_{\ell}{}^{\!*}.

(39)

In particular, we have
\text{C}\colon q_{p}\mapsto-q_{p}
so
C
invariance is manifest at
\theta=0
but not at
\theta=\pi
, as before.

The demonstration of the anomaly here more or less follows the same lines as the above. We introduce an external gauge field for the
\text{PSU}(N)
symmetry via a pair consisting of a link-field
V_{\ell}\in\text{SU}(N)
and a plaquette-field
b_{p}\in\mathbb{Z}_{N}

with the gauge transformation rule

\displaystyle V_{xy}

\displaystyle\mapsto v_{x}V_{xy}v_{y}{}^{\!{\dagger}}\exp(2\pi in_{xy}/N),

\displaystyle b_{p}

\displaystyle\mapsto b_{p}+\tsum_{\ell\in p}n_{\ell},

(40)

where
v_{x}\in\text{SU}(N)
and
n_{\ell}\in\mathbb{Z}_{N}
.
These background fields may be coupled to the dynamical fields in a gauge-invariant fashion as follows. We replace the kinetic term by

\displaystyle\tsum_{\langle xy\rangle}\Tr\phi_{x}{}^{\!{\dagger}}V_{xy}\phi_{y}U_{yx},

(41)

which is invariant under the transformation (
40
) of the background fields combined with the following transformation of the dynamical fields:

\displaystyle\phi_{x}\mapsto v_{x}\phi_{x},\ U_{\ell}\mapsto U_{\ell}\exp(2\pi in_{\ell}/N).

(42)

The nontrivial transformation rule for
U_{\ell}
then forces us to replace the topological charge density by

\displaystyle\exp(2\pi i\tilde{q}_{p})\equiv\exp(-2\pi ikb_{p}/N)\tprod_{\ell\in p}\det U_{\ell}.

(43)

Taking the product of the last equation over all plaquettes, we get

\displaystyle\exp(2\pi i\tilde{Q})=\exp(-2\pi ikB/N),

(44)

from which the same anomaly (
33
) follows.

V
Conclusion

Anomalies have long been a powerful way to probe nonperturbative aspects of
qft
s
.
For lattice regularizations, they imply constraints on the way anomalous symmetries may be realized.
Indeed, a
qft
with an anomalous symmetry
G
is such that
G
cannot be gauged; the same must be true for any symmetric lattice regulator thereof.

In this work, we have explored the connection between discrete anomalies and lattice regularizations for a class of
nl
\sigma
m
s
with target space given by the Grassmannian manifold
\text{Gr}_{k}(N)
, which includes the well-known
\text{CP}(N-1)
models as the case with
k=1
.
These models have a mixed anomaly between

\text{PSU}(N)
and
C
symmetries.

Dunne et al. 2018
;
Gaiotto et al. 2017
;
Komargodski et al. 2019
;
Komargodski et al. 2018
;
Cordova et al. 2020
.

The question we have tried to address in this work is: What do these anomalies imply for constructing a symmetric lattice regulator?
Generally, it is thought that an anomaly in a symmetry implies that on the lattice the symmetry cannot be realized onsite, because then one could always gauge the symmetry by introducing appropriate link variables. However, this is incorrect – there is also the possibility that the anomaly is
explicitly
reproduced on the lattice.
This can be surprising, since it is often remarked that “there are no anomalies on the lattice.”
Indeed, one of the most well-known early constructions of the
\theta
term in the (1+1)-dimensional
\text{CP}(N-1)

nl
\sigma
m
due to Berg and Lüscher
Berg and Lüscher 1981
, already exhibits this mixed anomaly between
\text{PSU}(N)
and
C
, as we have demonstrated in this work.

We also noted that in some cases, strictly speaking, there is no anomaly but a more subtle scenario referred to as a “global inconsistency”
Kikuchi and Tanizaki 2017
;
Gaiotto et al. 2017
;
Tanizaki and Kikuchi 2017
. In such cases, we argued that the usual no-go theorem gets modified and presents a new kind of obstruction,
which prevents us from finding lattice regulators with manifest onsite symmetry for the two points of global inconsistency that can be continuously connected to each other.

Of course, it is possible that the anomalous symmetry is realized offsite, in which case it is not possible to gauge it in the usual way. This is another way that an anomaly may be reflected in a lattice regularization.
We demonstrated this possibility by constructing a new regularization for the
\text{Gr}_{k}(N)

nl
\sigma
m
with a
\theta
term, using the D-theory formulation. This regularization uses
\text{SU}(N)
spins as fundamental degrees of freedom with local antiferromagnetic interactions on a two-dimensional spatial
L_{X}\times L_{Y}
lattice. In the large-representation limit, the low-energy limit can be shown to be that of (1+1)–dimensional
\text{Gr}_{k}(N)

nl
\sigma
m
for a fixed
L_{Y}
. Extending on recent work for the
\text{O}(3)

nl
\sigma
m

Caspar and Singh 2022
, we argued that a similar construction allows us to obtain the obtain continuum limit of the
\text{Gr}_{k}(N)

nl
\sigma
m
at
arbitrary
non-zero
\theta
, by keeping
\gamma L_{Y}
fixed as we take the
L_{Y}\to\infty
limit.

For the
\text{CP}(N-1)
models, this provided the first completely sign-problem free lattice regularization of the
\theta
vacua
Beard et al. 2006
;
Caspar and Singh 2022
.
In the case of
\text{SU}(N)
antiferromagnets in the
(k,p)
representation with
k\neq 1
, efficient sign-problem free algorithms are not known. Even though a direct numerical confirmation of our regularization is currently limited due to this, it is interesting to note that the sign-problem in
\text{SU}(N)

(k,p)
antiferromagnets in general is of a totally different character than the conventional Berg–Lüscher
\theta

After all,
(k,p)

antiferromagnets have a sign problem even at
\theta=0

by adding staggered couplings does not make it more severe. This suggests that if an efficient algorithm for
\text{SU}(N)
antiferromagnets with

\text{Gr}_{k}(N)

nl
\sigma
m
.

The discussion of anomalies and lattice regularizations presented in this work is especially relevant in the context of four-dimensional Yang–Mills theories.
Indeed, at
\theta=\pi
, pure
\text{SU}(N)
Yang–Mills theory exhibits a mixed anomaly involving time-reversal and center symmetry

There already exist proposals for qubit regularizations for Yang–Mills as quantum link models

Brower et al. 2004

There seems to be a parallel discussion to be had in these cases. We leave this for a future publication.

Appendix A
Parametrizations of the Grassmannian manifold

The Grassmannian
\text{Gr}_{k}(N)
is the manifold parametrizing
k
-dimensional subspaces of a
N
-dimensional complex vector space.
Operationally, it can be defined as follows.
On an
N
-dimensional complex vector space, we pick a reference
k
-dimensional hyperplane
K_{0}
.
Let the hyperplane
K_{0}
be represented by
k
-orthonormal complex vectors
K_{0}=\bm{[}\vec{z}_{1},\dotsc,\vec{z}_{k}\bm{]}
.
We may then denote the
k
-dimensional plane with the projector matrix

\displaystyle P_{0}=|\vec{z}_{1}\rangle\langle\vec{z}_{1}|+\cdots+|\vec{z}_{k}\rangle\langle\vec{z}_{k}|.

(45)

Now, we may get any other
k
-dimensional hyperplane by performing an arbitrary
\text{U}(N)
rotation,

\displaystyle P_{0}\mapsto P=UP_{0}U^{\dagger}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}|Uz_{i}\rangle\langle Uz_{i}|.

(46)

However, note that any unitary performed in the subspace
K^{\perp}_{0}
orthogonal to
K_{0}
will not yield a new hyperplane.
That’s
\text{U}(N-k)
worth of unitaries which leave
P_{0}
invariant.
Morever, any unitary in the subspace
K_{0}
will only change our choice of reference vectors
\vec{z}_{1},\dotsc,\vec{z}_{k}
, but will not change the hyperplane they define. That’s another
\text{U}(k)
worth of unitaries.
We say that
\text{U}(k)\times\text{U}(N-k)
is the isotopy group (or the little group) of the reference vector
P_{0}
.
The elements of
\text{U}(N)
that actually generate a new hyperplane are

\displaystyle\frac{\text{U}(N)}{\text{U}(k)\times\text{U}(N-k)},

(47)

which is the precisely Grassmanian manifold
\text{Gr}_{k}(N)
.

We can easily see from
Eqs.

45
and
46
that
P
satisfies
P^{2}=P=P^{\dagger}
, and
\Tr P=k
.
Therefore, the Grasmannian manifold can also be defined as the set of
N\times N
complex matrices such that

\displaystyle\text{Gr}_{k}(N)=\left\{P\in M_{N}({\mathbb{C}}):P^{2}=P,\Tr P=k\right\}.

(48)

We note that Read and Sachdev
Read and Sachdev 1989a
use the parametrization in terms of
Q
variables, where
P=\frac{1}{2}(1+Q)
, such that
Q
is a Hermitian
N\times N
matrix with
Q^{2}=1
and
\Tr Q=N-2k
.

We can also represent the
k
vectors
\vec{z}_{i}
as the columns of a
N\times k
complex matrix
\phi
matrix such that
\phi^{\dagger}\phi=\mathbf{1}_{k}
, which allows us to rewrite
Eq.

45
as
P=\phi\phi^{\dagger}
. Note that if the
\text{Gr}_{k}(N)
field theory is written in terms of the
\phi
(or
\vec{z}_{i}
) fields, we must add a
\text{U}(k)
gauge field to account for the redundancy in the choice of reference vectors
\vec{z}_{i}
:

\displaystyle Z=\int\mathcal{D}P\,e^{-S\bm{[}P\bm{]}}=\int\mathcal{D}\phi\,\mathcal{D}\phi^{\dagger}\,\mathcal{D}A\,\,e^{-S^{\prime}\bm{[}\phi,\phi^{\dagger},A\bm{]}}.

(49)

It may also be useful to point out that the conventional formulation of the
\text{Gr}_{1}(2)=\text{CP}(1)
or the
O(3)
model in terms a real unit vector
\vec{n}
(as in
Eq.

7
) is related to the projector formalism by

\displaystyle P=\tfrac{1}{2}(\mathbf{1}_{2}+\vec{n}\cdot\vec{\sigma}),\quad\text{or}\quad\vec{n}=\Tr(P\vec{\sigma}).

(50)

Note that charge conjugation
P\mapsto P^{*}
sends

\displaystyle(n_{1},n_{2},n_{3})\mapsto(n_{1},-n_{2},n_{3}),

(51)

which differs from the given in
Eq.

4

(\vec{n}\mapsto-\vec{n})
by an additional
\pi
-rotation about the second axis.

Appendix B
Some properties of the Berg–Lüscher lattice
\theta
term

In this section, we discuss some properties of the Berg–Lüscher lattice
\theta
term and provide its generalization for
\text{Gr}_{k}(N)
fields.

It will be convenient to use the redundant parametrization of
\text{Gr}_{k}(N)
by
N\times k
complex matrices
\phi

\phi^{\dagger}\phi=\mathbf{1}_{k}
.
In this notation, the Berg–Lüscher

\text{CP}(N-1)=\text{Gr}_{1}(N)

\displaystyle\exp(2\pi iq_{p})=\tprod_{\langle xy\rangle\in p}\frac{(\phi_{x},\phi_{y})}{\lvert(\phi_{x},\phi_{y})\rvert},

(52)

where in this section we use the notation
(.\,,.)
for the Hermitian scalar product of vectors. We see that
\exp(2\pi iq_{p})
takes the form of a product of link variables in
\text{U}(1)
around the boundary of
p
. Thus, in taking the product of this equation over all positively oriented plaquettes, the link variables cancel in pairs, so that

\displaystyle\exp(2\pi iQ)=\tprod_{p}\exp(2\pi iq_{p})=1.

(53)

Hence, the total topological charge
Q
is an integer.

We can now easily address an issue which was elided in
Section

IV
. There, background
\text{SU}(N)
link variables
V_{\ell}
were introduced, and the topological charge
q_{p}
got replaced by
\hat{q}_{p}
which depends on the
V_{\ell}
as in (
22

\hat{q}_{p}
(which was not explained in the main text) is that
\hat{Q}=\sum_{p}\hat{q}_{p}
be integer-quantized. We can now clearly see that this is so, as we can rewrite (

) in the new notation as

\displaystyle\exp(2\pi i\hat{q}_{p})=\tprod_{\langle xy\rangle\in p}\frac{(\phi_{x},V_{xy}\phi_{y})}{\lvert(\phi_{x},V_{xy}\phi_{y})\rvert}.

(54)

Let us now find the generalization of (
52
) for the
\text{Gr}_{k}(N)
model. We first note that it is possible to realize

as a submanifold of the complex projective space

via the Plücker embedding, which is given by

\displaystyle\text{Gr}_{k}(N)

\displaystyle\to P(\wedge^{k}\mathbb{C}^{N})

\displaystyle\bm{[}z_{1},\ldots,z_{k}\bm{]}

\displaystyle\mapsto\bm{[}z_{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge z_{k}\bm{]}

(55)

Here

is a set of orthonormal column vectors in
{\mathbb{C}}^{N}

\bm{[}z_{1},\ldots,z_{k}\bm{]}
denotes the
k

{\mathbb{C}}^{N}
generated by these vectors, and
\bm{[}z_{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge z_{k}\bm{]}
denotes the
1
-dimensional subspace in
\wedge^{k}{\mathbb{C}}^{N}
generated by
z_{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge z_{k}

This embedding is helpful here because it allows us to define the topological charge of a
\text{Gr}_{k}(N)

P(\wedge^{k}{\mathbb{C}}^{N})

To see the validity of this claim, consider a

\Phi=z_{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge z_{k}

(z_{i},z_{j})=\delta_{ij}

\text{Gr}_{k}(N)

of
\Phi

\displaystyle 2\pi Q

\displaystyle=\int d\big(\Phi,d\Phi\big)

\displaystyle=\int d\big(z_{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge z_{k},d\{z_{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge z_{k}\}\big)

\displaystyle=\int d\tsum_{j=1}^{k}\big(z_{1}\wedge\ldots\wedge z_{k},z_{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge dz_{j}\wedge\ldots\wedge z_{k}\big)

\displaystyle=\int d\tsum_{j=1}^{k}\big(z_{j},dz_{j})

\displaystyle=\int d\Tr\phi^{\dagger}d\phi.

(56)

In the fourth line, we have used the formula for the scalar product in
\wedge^{k}{\mathbb{C}}^{N}
induced by the standard scalar product in
{\mathbb{C}}^{N}

\displaystyle(z_{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge z_{k},w_{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge w_{k})=\det_{ij}(z_{i},w_{j}).

(57)

The last line is just the topological charge of

\text{Gr}_{k}(N)

Back to the lattice, we see with the help of formula (

\Phi_{x}

\text{Gr}_{k}(N)
fields
\phi_{x}
as

\displaystyle\exp(2\pi iq_{p})=\tprod_{\langle xy\rangle\in p}\frac{\det\phi_{x}{}^{\!{\dagger}}\phi_{y}}{\lvert\det\phi_{x}{}^{\!{\dagger}}\phi_{y}\rvert}.

(58)

For completeness, we note that it is possible to give an expression in terms of the projector-valued field
P_{x}

\displaystyle\exp(2\pi iq_{p})=\frac{\Tr\wedge^{k}P_{x}\cdot\wedge^{k}P_{y}\cdot\wedge^{k}P_{z}}{\lvert\Tr\wedge^{k}P_{x}\cdot\wedge^{k}P_{y}\cdot\wedge^{k}P_{z}\rvert},

(59)

where

is the operator on

on
{\mathbb{C}}^{N}
.

Appendix C
Continuum presentation of the
\text{Gr}_{k}(N)
model anomalies

This appendix reviews the continuum presentation of the mixed anomaly in the two-dimensional
\text{Gr}_{k}(N)
model between
\text{PSU}(N)
symmetry and charge conjugation
C

Dunne et al. 2018
, which the lattice presentation in the main text mirrors closely. The analysis is very similar to that of the two-dimensional
\text{CP}(N-1)
model
Gaiotto et al. 2017
;
Komargodski et al. 2019
;
Komargodski et al. 2018
;
Cordova et al. 2020
.

We shall work with the continuum action

\displaystyle S=\int\lvert d\phi-\phi a\rvert^{2}+\frac{\theta}{2\pi}\int\Tr da.

(60)

To detect the mixed anomaly, we shall need to activate a background gauge field for the
\text{PSU}(N)
symmetry, which can be regarded as a pair of fields consisting of a
\text{U}(N)
gauge field
A
and a
\text{U}(1)
2-form gauge field
B
that satisfy the constraint
NB=\Tr dA

Kapustin and Seiberg 2014
. (In our conventions,
B
is pure imaginary.) This pair transforms under a generalized gauge transformation of the form

\displaystyle A\mapsto gAg^{-1}+gdg^{-1}+\lambda\mathbf{1}_{N},\ B\mapsto B+d\lambda

(61)

where
g
is a
\text{U}(N)
-valued function and
\lambda
is a
\text{U}(1)
1-form gauge field.
To couple these background fields to the dynamical fields in a gauge invariant fashion, we replace the action by

\displaystyle\tilde{S}=\int\lvert d\phi+A\phi-\phi a\rvert^{2}+\frac{\theta}{2\pi}\int\Tr(da-B\mathbf{1}_{k}).

(62)

This is invariant under the transformation (
61
) of the background fields provided we also make the following transformation on the dynamical fields:

\displaystyle\phi\mapsto g\phi,\ a\mapsto a+\lambda\mathbf{1}_{k}.

(63)

It is now straightforward to find that the partition function in the presence of the background fields satisfies

\displaystyle\tilde{Z}\bm{[}A,B,\theta+2\pi\bm{]}=\tilde{Z}\bm{[}A,B,\theta\bm{]}\exp(k\tint B),

(64)

which indicates a mixed anomaly between the
\text{PSU}(N)
symmetry and the
2\pi
-periodicity in
\theta
. An important dynamical consequence of this result is that the theory cannot be trivially gapped for all values of
\theta\in\bm{[}0,2\pi\bm{]}
.

To go further, we consider the charge conjugation symmetry at
\theta=0,\pi
. Charge conjugation acts by complex conjugating all fields:

\displaystyle\text{C}\colon\phi\mapsto\phi^{*},\ a\mapsto a^{*},\ A\mapsto A^{*},\ B\mapsto B^{*}.

(65)

But in the action, complex conjugating the fields is equivalent to reversing the sign of
\theta
; i.e.,

\displaystyle\tilde{S}\bm{[}\phi^{*},a^{*},A^{*},B^{*},\theta\bm{]}=\tilde{S}\bm{[}\phi,a,A,B,-\theta\bm{]}

(66)

Thus, at
\theta=0
, charge conjugation leaves the partition function invariant, while at
\theta=\pi
, it is equivalent to shifting
\theta
by
-2\pi
, which by (
64
) causes the partition function to pick up the phase factor
\exp(-k\int B)
:

\displaystyle\text{C}\colon\begin{cases}\tilde{Z}\bm{[}A,B,\theta=0\bm{]}\;\mapsto\tilde{Z}\bm{[}A,B,\theta=0\bm{]},\\
\tilde{Z}\bm{[}A,B,\theta=\pi\bm{]}\,\mapsto\tilde{Z}\bm{[}A,B,\theta=\pi\bm{]}\exp(-k\tint B).\end{cases}

(67)

From this, we see that at
\theta=0
, there is no anomaly, while at
\theta=\pi
, there may or may not be an anomaly. The reason we cannot yet conclude that there is an anomaly at
\theta=\pi
is that we have not yet considered the most general procedure of gauging
\text{PSU}(N)
. Said differently, we are free to add to the action any gauge-invariant local “counterterm” depending only on the background fields. To have a genuine anomaly, it must be impossible to choose a counterterm that restores the symmetry.

Suffice it to say, the only candidate for such a counterterm is of the form
p\int B
with
p\in\mathbb{Z}_{N}
. If we therefore take for the partition function

\displaystyle\tilde{Z}^{\prime}\equiv\tilde{Z}\exp(p\tint B),

(68)

then we shall have

\displaystyle\text{C}\colon\begin{cases}\tilde{Z}^{\prime}\bm{[}A,B,0\bm{]}\;\mapsto\,\tilde{Z}^{\prime}\bm{[}A,B,0\bm{]}\exp\{-2p\tint B\},\\
\tilde{Z}^{\prime}\bm{[}A,B,\pi\bm{]}\,\mapsto\,\tilde{Z}^{\prime}\bm{[}A,B,\pi\bm{]}\exp\{-(2p+k)\int B\}.\end{cases}

(69)

Now, if there were a choice of
p
such that
C
invariance held at
both

\theta=0
and
\theta=\pi
, that is, if there were an integer
p
such that
2p=0
mod
N
and
2p+k=0
mod
N
, then we would have
k=0
mod
N
, which is a contradiction (
1\leq k\leq N-1
). In fact, with a slightly more detailed argument
Dunne et al. 2018
, we find the following cases:

•

If
N
is even and
k
is odd, there is no
p
that can restore
C
invariance at
\theta=\pi
, so we have a genuine anomaly. To see this, note that if there were such a
p
with
2p+k=0
mod
N
, then since
N
is even, we would have
2p+k
even. But this would then imply that
2p
was odd, since
k
is odd – contradiction.

•

Otherwise, it is possible to choose
p
such that
C
invariance is restored at
\theta=\pi
. Indeed,

–

for
N
even and
k
even, choose
p=N-\frac{1}{2}k
,

–

for
N
odd and
k
even, choose
p=N-\frac{1}{2}k
,

–

for
N
odd and
k
odd, choose
p=\frac{1}{2}(N-k)
.

But as we have already seen, we cannot repair
C
invariance at
\theta=\pi
without spoiling it at
\theta=0
. In these cases, therefore, while we do not have a genuine anomaly, we do have a
global inconsistency

;
Gaiotto et al. 2017
;
Tanizaki and Kikuchi 2017
.

From this, we can make the following inferences about the dynamics:
In the cases with a genuine anomaly, the theory cannot be trivially gapped at
\theta=\pi
.
In the cases with global inconsistency, there must exist some value of
\theta\in\bm{[}0,\pi\bm{]}
at which the theory is not trivially gapped.

, and in particular the result
\theta=\pi p
was found. Our goal here is to show that by staggering the couplings, one should be able to achieve arbitrary values of
\theta
.
This derivation generalizes a similar derivation for the
\text{CP}(N-1)
model in appendix B of Ref.
Nguyen et al. 2022
.

Coherent states

Introduce
N\times k
harmonic oscillators

\displaystyle[a^{\alpha i},a^{\dagger}_{j\beta}]=\delta_{\beta}^{\alpha}\delta_{j}^{i},

(71)

where
\alpha,\beta=1,\ldots,N
,
i,j=1,\ldots,k
,
to represent the
\text{SU}(N)
spin operators by

\displaystyle S_{\alpha}^{\beta}=a^{\dagger}_{i\alpha}a^{\beta i}.

(72)

The
(k,p)
representation of
\text{SU}(N)
is given by the subspace on which

\displaystyle N_{i}^{j}\equiv a^{\dagger}_{i\alpha}a^{\alpha j}=p\delta_{i}^{j}.

(73)

For any
N\times k
complex matrix
\phi=(\phi^{\alpha i})
, the operator

\displaystyle\Phi

\displaystyle\equiv(k!)^{-\frac{1}{2}}a^{\dagger}_{i_{1}\alpha_{1}}\cdots a^{\dagger}_{i_{k}\alpha_{k}}\epsilon^{i_{1}\cdots i_{k}}\phi^{\alpha_{1}1}\cdots\phi^{\alpha_{k}k}

\displaystyle=(k!)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\det(a^{\dagger}\phi).

(74)

satisfies
[N_{i}^{j},\Phi]=\delta_{i}^{j}
, so that
\Phi|0\rangle
belongs to
(k,1)
. More generally, we have

\displaystyle[N_{i}^{j},\Phi^{p}]=p\delta_{i}^{j}\Phi^{p},

(75)

so that
\Phi^{p}|0\rangle
is in the representation
(k,p)
.

We define coherent states for the representation
(k,p)
by

\displaystyle|\phi\rangle\equiv(p!)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\Phi^{p}|0\rangle,

(76)

where
\phi
is required to satisfy
\phi^{\dagger}\phi=\mathbf{1}_{k}
.
It can be verified that these satisfy the following properties:

\displaystyle\langle\phi|\phi^{\prime}\rangle

\displaystyle=\det(\phi^{\dagger}\phi^{\prime})^{p},

(77)

\displaystyle\langle\phi|S_{\alpha}^{\beta}|\phi\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{p}{k}(\phi\phi^{\dagger})^{\beta}{}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}_{\alpha}

(78)

\displaystyle\tint d\Omega_{\phi}|\phi\rangle\langle\phi|

\displaystyle=\mathsf{I}.

(79)

Let us now compute the coherent state path integral representation for a single spin
(k,p)
with Hamiltonian
H=J^{\alpha}_{\beta}S_{\alpha}^{\beta}
. The transition amplitude for infinitesimal imaginary time interval
\delta\tau
in the coherent state basis is given by

\displaystyle\langle\phi(\tau)|(\mathsf{I}-\delta\tau H)|\phi(\tau-\delta\tau)\rangle

\displaystyle\quad=\langle\phi(\tau)|\phi(\tau-\delta\tau)\rangle-\delta\tau\langle\phi(\tau)|H|\phi(\tau-\delta\tau)\rangle.

(80)

The second term simply gives
\frac{p}{k}J^{\alpha}_{\beta}(\phi\phi^{\dagger})^{\beta}{}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}_{\alpha}
, while the first term gives

\displaystyle\langle\phi(\tau)|\phi(\tau-\delta\tau)\rangle

\displaystyle=\det\bm{[}\phi(\tau)^{\dagger}\phi(\tau-\delta\tau)\bm{]}^{p}

\displaystyle\approx\det\bm{[}\mathbf{1}-\delta\tau\,\phi(\tau)^{\dagger}\partial_{\tau}\phi(\tau)\bm{]}^{p}

\displaystyle\approx\bm{[}1-\delta\tau\Tr\phi(\tau)^{\dagger}\partial_{\tau}\phi(\tau)\bm{]}^{p}

\displaystyle\approx 1-p\delta\tau\Tr\phi(\tau)^{\dagger}\partial_{\tau}\phi(\tau).

(81)

We thus find the coherent state path integral
\Tr e^{-\beta H}=\int e^{-S}\mathcal{D}\phi\mathcal{D}\phi^{\dagger}
where

\displaystyle L=p\Tr(\phi^{\dagger}\partial_{\tau}\phi)+\frac{p}{k}J^{\alpha}_{\beta}(\phi\phi^{\dagger})^{\beta}{}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}_{\alpha}.

(82)

We will also need the coherent states for the conjugate representation
(\overline{k,p})
. Thus, we introduce an additional set of
N\times k
harmonic oscillators,

\displaystyle[\bar{a}^{{\dagger}i\alpha},\bar{a}_{\beta j}]=\delta^{\alpha}_{\beta}\delta^{i}_{j},

(83)

in terms of which
\text{SU}(N)
spin operators may be given by

\displaystyle\bar{S}^{\alpha}_{\beta}=-\bar{a}^{{\dagger}i\alpha}\bar{a}_{\beta i}.

(84)

The corresponding coherent states
|\bar{\chi}\rangle
are defined analogously to (
76
)
and they satisfy the properties

\displaystyle\langle\bar{\chi}|\bar{\chi}^{\prime}\rangle

\displaystyle=\det(\bar{\chi}^{\dagger}\bar{\chi}^{\prime})^{p},

(85)

\displaystyle\langle\bar{\chi}|\bar{S}^{\alpha}_{\beta}|\bar{\chi}\rangle

\displaystyle=-\frac{p}{k}(\chi\chi^{\dagger})^{\alpha}{}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}_{\beta},

(86)

\displaystyle\tint d\Omega_{\chi}|\bar{\chi}\rangle\langle\bar{\chi}|

\displaystyle=\mathsf{I}\ \ \text{on $(\overline{k,p})$}.

(87)

The coherent state path integral for the single spin Hamiltonian
H=J_{\alpha}^{\beta}\bar{S}^{\alpha}_{\beta}
is obtained in the same way as before, and one finds the Euclidean Lagrangian

\displaystyle L=-p\Tr(\chi^{\dagger}\partial_{\tau}\chi)-\frac{p}{k}J_{\alpha}^{\beta}(\chi\chi^{\dagger})^{\alpha}{}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}_{\beta}.

(88)

Note that this is simply the negative of (
82
).

Large distance effective field theory

Returning now to the spin chain (
70
), we obtain via the coherent state path integral the Euclidean Lagrangian

\displaystyle L

\displaystyle=\tsum_{n}\Tr(p\phi_{n}{}^{\!{\dagger}}\partial_{\tau}\phi_{n}-p\chi_{n}{}^{\!{\dagger}}\partial_{\tau}\chi_{n})

\displaystyle\;\;\;\;-\frac{p^{2}}{k^{2}}J_{-}\tsum_{n}\lvert\phi_{n}{}^{\!{\dagger}}\chi_{n}\rvert^{2}-\frac{p^{2}}{k^{2}}J_{+}\tsum_{n}\lvert\chi_{n}{}^{\!{\dagger}}\phi_{n+1}\rvert^{2}

(89)

We note that for
J_{+}=J_{-}
this Lagrangian is invariant under the offsite transformation

\displaystyle\phi_{n}\mapsto\chi_{n}{}^{\!*},\ \chi_{n}\mapsto\phi_{n+1}{}^{\!*},

(90)

which we shall later interpret as charge conjugation.
To obtain the large-distance effective field theory, we expand about a slowly varying Néel configuration by writing

\displaystyle\phi_{n}

\displaystyle=\phi(x),

(91)

\displaystyle\chi_{n}

\displaystyle=\bigg[1+\frac{1}{k}\lvert\epsilon(x)\rvert^{2}\bigg]^{\frac{1}{2}}\phi(x)+\epsilon(x).

(92)

Here,
\phi(x)
is taken to be a slowly varying background,
\epsilon(x)
a rapidly varying fluctuation, and we have introduced the continuum coordinate
x=an
,
a
being the lattice spacing.
Inserting this into the Lagrangian, and keeping only terms at most second order in
\epsilon
and
\partial
, we find

\displaystyle a\mathcal{L}

\displaystyle=\frac{p^{2}}{k^{2}}a^{2}J_{+}\lvert D_{x}\phi\rvert^{2}+\frac{p^{2}}{k^{2}}(J_{-}+J_{+})\lvert\epsilon\rvert^{2}

\displaystyle\;\;\;\;-\Tr\bigg[\epsilon^{\dagger}\bigg(\frac{p^{2}}{k^{2}}aJ_{+}D_{x}\phi+pD_{\tau}\phi\bigg)\bigg]

\displaystyle\;\;\;\;-\Tr\bigg[\bigg(\frac{p^{2}}{k^{2}}aJ_{+}D_{x}\phi^{\dagger}-pD_{\tau}\phi^{\dagger}\bigg)\epsilon\bigg],

(93)

where
D_{\mu}\phi\equiv\partial_{\mu}\phi-\phi(\phi^{\dagger}\partial_{\mu}\phi)
.
Integrating out the fluctuation field
\epsilon
yields

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}

\displaystyle=a\frac{p^{2}}{k^{2}}\frac{J_{-}J_{+}}{J_{-}+J_{+}}\lvert D_{x}\phi\rvert^{2}+\frac{1}{a}\frac{k^{2}}{J_{-}+J_{+}}\lvert D_{\tau}\phi\rvert^{2}

\displaystyle\;\;\;\;+p\frac{J_{+}}{J_{-}+J_{+}}\epsilon^{\mu\nu}\Tr(D_{\mu}\phi^{\dagger}D_{\nu}\phi).

(94)

This is the continuum Lagrangian of the
\text{Gr}_{k}(N)
model with coupling given by

\displaystyle\frac{1}{g^{2}}=p\frac{(J_{-}J_{+})^{\frac{1}{2}}}{J_{-}+J_{+}}=\tfrac{1}{2}p\bm{[}(1-\gamma)(1+\gamma)\bm{]}^{\frac{1}{2}}

(95)

with a
\theta
parameter given by

\displaystyle\theta=2\pi p\frac{J_{+}}{J_{-}+J_{+}}=\pi p(1+\gamma),

(96)

where we have introduced the staggering parameter
\gamma
by
J_{+}/J_{-}=(1+\gamma)/(1-\gamma)
.
For
\gamma=0
, we recover the result
\theta=\pi p
obtained in Ref.
Read and Sachdev 1989a
. Thus the presence of the lattice symmetry (
90
) exactly coincides with the presence of charge conjugation symmetry in the effective field theory.
