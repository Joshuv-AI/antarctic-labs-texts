Some perspectives on (non)local phase transitions and minimal surfaces
Serena Dipierro, Enrico Valdinoci

Abstract
. We present here some classical and modern results about phase transitions and minimal surfaces, which are quite intertwined topics. We start from scratch, revisiting the theory of phase transitions as put forth by Lev Landau. Then, we relate the short-range phase transitions to the classical minimal surfaces, whose basic regularity theory is presented, also in connection with a celebrated conjecture by Ennio De Giorgi. With this, we explore the recently developed subject of long-range phase transitions and relate its genuinely nonlocal regime to the analysis of fractional minimal surfaces.

Introduction

In this paper we deal with phase coexistence models, in connection with
the geometric problems of minimal interfaces. We will analyze the classical case,
in which the limit interface is regulated by surface tension, as well as the long-range case, which,

2.
Landau theory of phase transitions

The description of coexisting phases in a given material is a very complex topic,
combining different perspectives from statistical physics, thermodynamics, material sciences
and mathematical analysis.
Also, the setting may vary according to the specific case under consideration, given the
different underlying physical structures involved: for instance,
while in our everyday life we are mostly exposed to the changes of state
related to melting, freezing, vaporization and condensation,
other important phase transitions, such as the one from a conducting to
a superconducting state, are the outcome of brand new properties of the solid state, such as electron coupling.

Though it is virtually impossible to provide here an exhaustive account of phase transition
theories, we can recall some general facts which can be helpful to develop an intuition of the problem under consideration.

A common treat in the study of phase transitions is to describe a given system
in terms of relevant physical quantities, such as temperature
T
,
pressure
P
, volume
V
, entropy
S
, magnetic moment
M
, etc.
The energy of a system is thus an outline of its
‘‘ability to perform some tasks’’.
In this performance, however,
the system
typically ‘‘wastes’’
some energy in the form of heat.
The ‘‘useful’’ energy, that is the energy that is available to do work, thus consists in the difference between
the full internal energy of the system minus the energy that is ‘‘unavailable to perform work’’
since it gets lost through heat.
Being ‘‘free to do the work’’, such energy is often called ‘‘free energy’’, though
2
2

2

Our presentation of free energy is certainly quite inaccurate. More precisely, there are typically two versions of this quantity, depending on the physical variables that describe the state of the system.
On the one hand, when one models the system by using as main independent variables the temperature
T
and the volume
V
, one obtains the Helmholtz free energy
F=F(V,T)
, whose infinitesimal increment is defined by

dF=-S\,dT-P\,dV.

On the other hand, if the system is described in terms of the independent variables pressure
P
and temperature
T
, one obtains the Gibbs free energy
G=G(P,T)
, whose infinitesimal increment is given by

dG=-S\,dT+V\,dP.

So, some care is needed when dealing with free energy, and the precise dependence on the relevant physical values cannot be, in general, omitted.
Nonetheless, in our discussion here, we are considering the free energy in dependence of temperature only, therefore the two approaches, namely the one based on the Helmholtz free energy and that based on the Gibbs free energy, are essentially equivalent. In particular, from the infinitesimal increments, we see that when pressure and volumes are kept constant and only temperature varies, then

S=-\frac{\partial F}{\partial T}=-\frac{\partial G}{\partial T},

that is, one reconstructs the entropy from the variation in temperature of the free energies (essentially, in an equivalent way with respect to the choice of free energy).
We should also stress that the interpretation of free energy as the part of internal energy which is keen to perform work is also rather simplistic. Indeed, looking at the increments defining the free energies, one sees that they comprise the available energy to do mechanical work (related to change of pressure and volumes) but also a term which is entropy-dependent and temperature-related of the form
-S\,dT
, which is not directly related to mechanical work. The term free is likely to be related to the fact that the increments of the free energies do not present terms of the form
T\,dS
, which, in reversible processes, would correspond to heat increments
dQ
, via the Second Law of Thermodynamics (that is, very roughly speaking, the contributions of heat are removed from the free energy).

the name is under an intense debate.

Hence, the minimizers (or, more generally, the stable critical points) of this energy correspond to observable states
of our system. Concretely, the system may present significantly different features, or “phases”,
such as being in a solid or fluid state, or having a magnetic momentum, or presenting superfluid or superconductor properties,
and the appearance of these phases may be seen as an outcome of energy minimization.

The arising of different phases may be the outcome of a critical physical parameter involved in the free
energy, such as temperature: in practice,
a ‘‘disordered phase’’ typically corresponds to a high temperature, while an ‘‘exceptionally ordered phase’’
arises at low temperatures. This is the case, for instance,
for magnetization, since magnetic materials have no permanent magnetic moment above their

The Curie Temperature is named after Pierre Curie, who in 1895 related some magnetic properties to change in temperature.

so that the magnetic materials show a permanent magnetization oriented in a certain direction, see e.g.

Specifically, when the temperature

T_{c}

in the absence of external sources a magnetic system lies in a zero field state,
which happens to be a minimal configuration for the free energy corresponding to its temperature.
When the temperature is decreased below such critical temperature
T_{c}
,
the system will go through a state in which the magnetization is still zero,
but this corresponds only to a critical point,
not a minimum of the free energy, making this equilibrium configuration totally unstable.
For this reason, below the critical temperature
T_{c}

small perturbations from the environment will inevitably
induce the system to reorganize its microscopic structure to possibly preserve a zero average but creating
regions with a nontrivial magnetic momentum.
The formation of these magnetic
domains will produce a supplementary interfacial energy, which, in some sense, favors domain segregation
with a phase separation which is “as small as possible” (in a sense which will be clarified below
in Section
2.3
).

It is also interesting to remark that such a phase separation also produces a symmetry breaking:
the free energy is symmetric (since it weighs equally, say, the magnetizations
oriented towards the North pole and the ones oriented towards the South pole),
nonetheless the magnetization configuration reached by the system during the cooling
is somewhat accidental, as a result of small environmental perturbations, making
the final state reached by the system not necessarily symmetric.

2.1.
The second-order theory of phase transitions

To account for the phenomena of phase transition and phase coexistence, one can consider an
“order parameter”
\eta
which describes, in some sense, how every point of the system is “organized”
with respect to a given notion of phase. The name of this parameter is possibly inspired by its statistical mechanics
interpretation, relating different phases to the degree of “order”, or “disorder”, of a system.
In specific situations, the order parameter can be either a scalar or a vector:
for instance,
in a liquid-gas phase transition the order parameter corresponds to the difference of the densities
between the two phases, which is a scalar, while in
superfluidity and superconductivity it is a complex-valued wave function
(or, equivalently, a two-dimensional vector), and for ferromagnetic
momenta it is in general a three-dimensional vector. Phase transitions also occur
in cosmology, since as the universe expanded and cooled,
a number of symmetry-breaking phase transitions occurred, and the description of these phenomena often
relies on an order parameter which is a tensor.
Here we only consider the case in which the order parameter
\eta
is a scalar function.

2.2.
The first-order theory of phase transitions

In many physical situations, however,
the change of the state of a substance at its critical temperature
is related to a latent heat which is supplied to or extracted from the medium
without changing its temperature. These types of phase transitions correspond to a discontinuity

In these situations, the observed

the transition temperature.

It is instructive to remark that a simple modification of the above

2.3.
Interfacial energy of phase transitions

The description of phase models so far focused only on the favorable configurations of the free energy
which support one phase over the other, but we have not discussed how two different coexisting phases
are separated, that is what the geometry of the domains corresponding to each phase is.

For this, we start by observing that the coexistence of two phases occurs when they both attain the minimal value
of the free energy: this is precisely the case of first-order phase transitions at the critical temperature
and of second-order phase transitions at the critical temperature or below it,
see the discussions in Sections
2.1
and
2.2
, as well as Figures
1
,

4
and
7
.

In principle, when two minima of the free energy occur at the same level,
the two phases are equally favorable from an energetic point of view, hence any configuration
in which any point of the state lies in any of the two phases is “as good as any other”.
This however is in contradiction with common experience, since in many phenomena
the change of phase between different regions of the space occurs in very specific regions, see e.g.
Figure
10
.

Figure 10.
Moving domain walls in a grain of silicon steel caused by an increasing external magnetic field. White areas are domains with magnetization directed up, dark areas are domains with magnetization directed down
(image from Wikipedia,
by Zureks, Chris Vardon,
CC BY-SA 3.0).

In practice, the model of “pure phases” is possibly “too abrupt”, since small “fluctuations” produce values of the state
parameter which are not precisely equal to either of the two phases.
To model these fluctuations, one can consider the order parameter
\eta
as a function of the continuous spatial
coordinates and assume that the fluctuations are the byproduct of the mutual interaction between regions of spaces
corresponding to a different state parameter. Though a full understanding of these fluctuations is a very
delicate problem in statistical physics, a crude, but perhaps efficient, model is to assume that the
“effective” energy of the system comes from the free energy, plus an interaction term between different phases.
The introduction of this interaction term dates back at least to Van der Waals, who considered
molecular interactions as averaged over long ranges. One of the additional benefits of such an interaction term
is, roughly speaking, to penalize the unnecessary changes of phase and somewhat favor, among all the configurations which minimize the free energy, the ones which present a “minimal interface” between regions with different phases.

The precise notion of minimal interface certainly depends on the additional interaction term that one takes
into account, thus we describe now some specific choices of interest. Considering the spatial domain the whole of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, one can take into account an interaction energy of the form

(2.25)

\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}}(\eta(x)-\eta(y))^{2}\,{\mathcal{K}}(x,y)\,dx\,dy.

The quadratic power above is somewhat arbitrary
7
7

7

Ideally, one may want to determine a precise interaction kernel from general first principles.
However, due to the complexity of natural phenomena, in many concrete situations, the precise determination of an interaction kernel may be based on phenomenological considerations, interpretation of experimental data, or even, more frequently than one may think at a first, on the opportunity of finding useful computational simplifications.
As an example of “convenient choice” of an interaction kernel, we recall the fact that, in the
development of his new theory of gases based on statistical physics
[
78
]
,
James Clerk Maxwell introduced an interaction kernel
based on the inverse fifth power of the molecular distance.
Actually, it seems that the model was possibly taking into account
the general case of the
\kappa

[
18

“it has been argued that
Maxwell admitted that this choice of

[came] less from the
physical consequences of the choice than from the attractiveness of the possibility of explicit integration”.

if one exchanges the roles of

and

if
{\mathcal{K}}

=\,&\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left[\int_{B_{\varrho}}\Big(\nabla\eta(x)\cdot z+O\big(\sigma(x)\,|z|^{2}\big)\Big)^{2}\,K(|z|)\,dz\right]\,dx\\

3.
The Allen-Cahn equation

Critical points of the energy functional (
1.1
) give rise to the equation

(3.1)

\Delta u(x)=W^{\prime}(u(x))

for
x\in\Omega
. The model case in which the potential takes the form in (
1.3
)
reduces to

(3.2)

-\Delta u=u-u^{3},

which is known as the Allen-Cahn equation. This equation indeed produces the stationary states
of an evolution equation presented in
[
5
]
with the specific goal of
describing the phase separation in multi-component alloy systems. Moreover, solutions of the Allen-Cahn equation
are also stationary states of the so-called Cahn-Hilliard equation

(3.3)

\partial_{t}u=\Delta\left(u^{3}-u-\Delta u\right),

which was introduced in
[
33
]

to represent
the process of spontaneous phase separation in a binary fluid.

Figure 11.
Evolution of random initial data under the Cahn-Hilliard equation
(image from Wikipedia, by Yonatan Oren, public domain).

The success of equation (
3.3
) in
describing spontaneous phase separation with
a tendency of similar phases to cluster together
is indeed quite perceptible, see Figure
11
.

The above equations can also be modified to account for a heterogeneous material.
For instance, the potential in (
3.1
) can be modulated by a spatially dependent function
Q=Q(x)
,
ranging in
[\underline{Q},\overline{Q}]
, for some
\overline{Q}\geqslant\underline{Q}>0
. In this setting,
equation (
3.1
) can be generalized to

(3.4)

\Delta u(x)=Q(x)\,W^{\prime}(u(x))

and further generalization are also possible (e.g. by considering more general spatially dependent
double-well potentials or spatially dependent elliptic operators, say in divergence form
to have a consistent link with the arguments related to energy functionals in Section
2
).

An interesting feature of the Allen-Cahn equation, as well as of the equations closely related to it,
is to be strongly cross-disciplinary. And this not only because its deep relation with phase coexistence
models makes it a standard tool of investigation for physicists, chemists, biologists and engineers too,
but also because it presents an immediate link with the theory of dynamical systems,
in which the space dimension
n
is taken to be
1
, and the role space is replaced by time.
To clarify this point, one can consider equation (
3.1
) when
n=1

and replace the name of the variable
x
by
t
(to physically represent time), thus obtaining

(3.5)

\ddot{u}(t)=W^{\prime}(u(t)).

For instance, when, for all
u\in[-1,1]
, one has
W(u):=1-\cos(\pi u)
, equation (
3.5
)
reduces to
\ddot{u}(t)=\pi\sin(\pi u(t))
, which is the equation of the simple pendulum.
A similar procedure applied to (
3.4
) produces the equation
\ddot{u}(t)=\pi Q(t)\sin(\pi u(t))
, which is the equation of a pendulum in a variable in time gravitational field.

This type of relations inspired a series of works in which methodologies typical of dynamical systems
(such as the so-called Aubry-Mather theory
[
77
,
8
]
,
also related to classical works on geodesics
[
81
]
) are employed to
study phenomena strictly related to elliptic partial differential equations
and the Allen-Cahn equation, see e.g.
[
82
,
9
,
4
,
42
,
17
,
73
,
86
]
and the references therein.

For example, the theory of dynamical systems often focuses on the determination of orbits
with given “rotation numbers”: for instance, especially in periodic settings, it is common to seek trajectories
of ordinary differential
equations exhibiting
a linear growth, up to bounded oscillations, that is such that

(3.6)

|u(t)-\rho t|\leqslant K\qquad{\mbox{for all }}t\in\mathbb{R},

for some
\rho\in\mathbb{R}
and
K\geqslant 0
.

From a geometric viewpoint, one can restate (
3.6
) by saying that the graph of
u
,
that is
{\mathcal{G}}_{u}:=\big\{(t,u(t)),

t\in\mathbb{R}\big\}
, is trapped in a slab perpendicular to
\omega:=\frac{(-\rho,1)}{\sqrt{1+\rho^{2}}}

and of width
2M
, with
M:=\frac{K}{\sqrt{1+\rho^{2}}}
,
namely

(3.7)

{\mathcal{G}}_{u}\subseteq\big\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}{\mbox{ s.t. }}|x\cdot\omega|\leqslant M\big\}.

With respect to this matter, a natural counterpart for phase transitions would be to construct
examples in which the interface

{\mathcal{I}}_{u,\vartheta}:=\big\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}{\mbox{ s.t. }}|u(x)|<\vartheta\big\}

is trapped in a slab (or equivalently, trapped within two hyperplanes).
This is indeed the content of the following result (which is a particular case of Theorem 8.1
in
[

]
):

.

Let

,
\vartheta\in(0,1)
and assume that

for all

Then, there exists a constant

, depending only on

,

and

, such that, given any direction

, we can construct a solution

)
such that

The reader can appreciate the similarity between (

)
and (

). For completeness, we also mention that
the solution constructed in Theorem

enjoys several extra features,
such as it is a local minimizer of the associated energy functional,
it is periodic when

is rationally dependent, its interface fulfills a suitable non-self-intersecting property, etc.

4.
The limit interface and the theory of
\Gamma
-convergence

4.1.
Singular perturbations and
\Gamma
-convergence theory

We now come back to the perturbation problem induced by the energy functional in (
2.29
).
Compared with (
3.1
), this setting produces a “rescaled version” of the Allen-Cahn equation of the form

(4.1)

\varepsilon^{2}\Delta u(x)=W^{\prime}(u(x)).

This can be seen as a “singular perturbation” problem, since
the main term in the equation would disappear when
\varepsilon=0

and therefore a delicate analysis is required to capture the essential features of the problem
for small values of the parameter
\varepsilon
.

As a matter of fact,
this kind of singular perturbation analysis is one of the main fillip to the development
of the notion of
\Gamma
-convergence, see
[
40
]
.
To appreciate this theory, let us consider a functional of the form
{\mathcal{F}}_{\varepsilon}

and let us try to discuss a convenient meaning for a suitable convergence of
{\mathcal{F}}_{\varepsilon}

to some
{\mathcal{F}}
as
\varepsilon\searrow 0
. Notice that a pointwise convergence could be out of reach,
because a singular perturbation problem may drastically change the structure of the limit functional
as well as its natural domain of definition, therefore a “new” notion of convergence is called for.
In particular, to make this notion practical and serviceable, it is desirable to keep the notion of local energy minimizers
in the limit: namely,

(4.2)

\begin{split}&{\mbox{if\penalty\ $u_{\varepsilon}$ is a local minimizer for\penalty\ ${\mathcal{F}}_{\varepsilon}$}}\\
&{\mbox{and\penalty\ $u_{\varepsilon}\to u$ in some topology\penalty\ $X$ as\penalty\ $\varepsilon\searrow 0$,}}\\
&{\mbox{this functional notion of convergence should entail}}\\
&{\mbox{that\penalty\ $u$ is a local minimizer for\penalty\ ${\mathcal{F}}$.}}\end{split}

To this extent, the limit functional
{\mathcal{F}}

may be considered as an ‘‘effective energy’’ and the choice
8
8

8

For simplicity, we will always implicitly assume that
the topology of
X
is induced by a metric space, so that compactness and sequential compactness are the same.

of the topology
X

can be possibly made “loose enough” to ensure compactness of the minimizers beforehand
(choosing a “too strong” topology
X
produces the pitfall that minimizers
may not converge!).

It is also desirable that

(4.3)

the limit functional
{\mathcal{F}}
is lower semicontinuous

in order to develop a solid existence theory for its minimizers.

With these remarks in mind, it is not too difficult to “guess” what an “appropriate” notion
of functional convergence should be.
To this end, we distinguish between
the lower limit and the upper limit.
For the lower limit we take inspiration from the classical Fatou’s Lemma
(after all, it is sensible that a good functional convergence turns out to be compatible with the classical scenarios)
in which one considers the very special case of
u_{\varepsilon}
being a sequence of nonnegative measurable functions converging pointwise to
u
,
takes
{\mathcal{F}}_{\varepsilon}(v):={\mathcal{F}}(v):=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}v(x)\,dx
and writes that

\liminf_{\varepsilon\searrow 0}{\mathcal{F}}_{\varepsilon}(u_{\varepsilon})=\liminf_{\varepsilon\searrow 0}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}u_{\varepsilon}(x)\,dx\geqslant\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}u(x)\,dx={\mathcal{F}}(u).

Hence, a natural requirement for a general notion of functional convergence is that

(4.4)

{\mbox{whenever\penalty\ $u_{\varepsilon}\to u$ in\penalty\ $X$,}}\quad\liminf_{\varepsilon\searrow 0}{\mathcal{F}}_{\varepsilon}(u_{\varepsilon})\geqslant{\mathcal{F}}(u).

Let us now consider an upper limit condition in the light of (
4.2
).
Namely, let us consider a minimizer
u_{\varepsilon}^{\star}
for
{\mathcal{F}}_{\varepsilon}

(say, under suitable boundary or external conditions, or mass prescriptions, or so): then, it holds that,
for every competitor
u_{\varepsilon}
for
u_{\varepsilon}^{\star}
,

(4.5)

{\mathcal{F}}_{\varepsilon}(u_{\varepsilon})\geqslant{\mathcal{F}}_{\varepsilon}(u_{\varepsilon}^{\star})

and the recipe in (
4.2
) would suggest to obtain the same structural inequality as
\varepsilon\searrow 0
.
To wit, for every competitor
u
for
u^{\star}
, we aim at showing that

(4.6)

{\mathcal{F}}(u)\geqslant{\mathcal{F}}(u^{\star}).

For this, if
u_{\varepsilon}
is any sequence converging to
u
in
X
, we know from (
4.4
)
and (
4.5
) that

\limsup_{\varepsilon\searrow 0}{\mathcal{F}}_{\varepsilon}(u_{\varepsilon})\geqslant\liminf_{\varepsilon\searrow 0}{\mathcal{F}}_{\varepsilon}(u_{\varepsilon})\geqslant\liminf_{\varepsilon\searrow 0}{\mathcal{F}}_{\varepsilon}(u_{\varepsilon}^{\star})\geqslant{\mathcal{F}}(u^{\star}).

Therefore, to obtain (
4.6
), it suffices to find one, possibly very special sequence
u_{\varepsilon}

converging to
u
in
X
for which

(4.7)

{\mathcal{F}}(u)\geqslant\limsup_{\varepsilon\searrow 0}{\mathcal{F}}_{\varepsilon}(u_{\varepsilon}).

This special sequence making the job is sometimes called “recovery sequence”, the name coming from the
observation that if
u
is as in (
4.7
), by (
4.4
) one in fact has that

{\mathcal{F}}(u)=\lim_{\varepsilon\searrow 0}{\mathcal{F}}_{\varepsilon}(u_{\varepsilon}).

Thus, a natural upper limit condition to complement (
4.4
) consists in requiring that

(4.8)

\begin{split}&{\mbox{there exists a sequence\penalty\ $u_{\varepsilon}\to u$ as\penalty\ $\varepsilon\searrow 0$ in\penalty\ $X$ such that}}\\
&\limsup_{\varepsilon\searrow 0}{\mathcal{F}}_{\varepsilon}(u_{\varepsilon})\leqslant{\mathcal{F}}(u).\end{split}

Conditions (
4.4
) and (
4.8
) are often accompanied by
a compactness assumption under a bounded energy requirement, such that

(4.9)

\begin{split}&{\mbox{if }}\sup_{\varepsilon\in(0,1)}{\mathcal{F}}_{\varepsilon}(u_{\varepsilon})<+\infty,\\
&{\mbox{then there exists a subsequence\penalty\ $u_{\varepsilon^{\prime}}$ converging in\penalty\ $X$ as\penalty\ $\varepsilon^{\prime}\searrow 0$.}}\end{split}

When conditions (
4.4
), (
4.8
) and (
4.9
) are met, then we say that
{\mathcal{F}}_{\varepsilon}

\Gamma
-converges to
{\mathcal{F}}
.

We also observe that conditions (
4.4
) and (
4.8
) entail the lower semicontinuity property mentioned in (
4.3
),
because if

in

we can take a subsequence

such that

\liminf_{k\to+\infty}{\mathcal{F}}(w_{k})=\lim_{j\to+\infty}{\mathcal{F}}(w_{k_{j}}).

Also, for any given
j
,
we can find a recovery sequence
w_{j,m}
that converges to
w_{k_{j}}
in
X
as
m\to+\infty
and
such that

\lim_{m\to+\infty}{\mathcal{F}}_{\varepsilon_{m}}(w_{j,m})={\mathcal{F}}(w_{k_{j}}).

Hence, given
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
, we pick
j_{\ell}\in\mathbb{N}
such that if
j\geqslant j_{\ell}
then
\|w_{k_{j}}-w\|_{X}\leqslant\frac{1}{\ell}

and then we pick
m_{\ell}\in\mathbb{N}
such that if

then

and

\big|{\mathcal{F}}_{\varepsilon_{m}}(w_{j_{\ell},m})-{\mathcal{F}}(w_{k_{j_{\ell}}})\big|\leqslant\frac{1}{\ell}.

This construction gives that
\|w_{j_{\ell},m_{\ell}}-w\|_{X}\leqslant\frac{2}{\ell}
and consequently

in

as

\displaystyle{\mathcal{F}}(w)

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle\liminf_{\ell\to+\infty}{\mathcal{F}}_{\varepsilon_{m_{\ell}}}(w_{j_{\ell},m_{\ell}})

\displaystyle\liminf_{\ell\to+\infty}\left({\mathcal{F}}(w_{k_{j_{\ell}}})+\frac{1}{\ell}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\liminf_{k\to+\infty}{\mathcal{F}}(w_{k}),

which proves the lower semicontinuity property mentioned in (

See
[

]

5.
Minimal surfaces and one-dimensional symmetry

The results of Theorems
4.1
and
4.2
, linking the Allen-Cahn equation
to the theory of minimal surfaces (i.e., in our notation, of hypersurfaces which are boundary of sets
and minimize the perimeter functional under compactly supported perturbations in a given domain)
strongly suggest that the understanding of the locally minimal solutions of the Allen-Cahn equation
and that of perimeter minimizers are deeply related.
In general, solutions of semilinear equations, e.g. equations of the form
\Delta u=f(u)
for some

,
possess the remarkable property that their Laplacian is constant along level sets, thus again suggesting
a strong relation with geometric objects, such as hypersurfaces with constant mean curvature.

5.1.
Classical minimal surfaces

To further appreciate the links between analytic and geometric results,
we briefly review some classical aspects of the theory of minimal surfaces.
First of all, the first and second variations of the perimeter functional can be explicitly
computed in terms of the mean curvature

of the second fundamental form.
More precisely (see e.g.

&\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad-\beta\frac{\sqrt{(r^{\beta}-1)^{2}+\varepsilon}-r^{\beta}+1+\varepsilon}{\sqrt{(r^{\beta}-1)^{2}+\varepsilon}}\Bigg|\end{split}

and (
5.14
) plainly follows when
r\in\left[\frac{1}{2},2\right]
.
Also, if
r\in\left(0,\frac{1}{2}\right)
, we have that

\frac{\sqrt{(r^{\beta}-1)^{2}+\varepsilon}}{1-r^{\beta}}=\sqrt{1+\frac{\varepsilon}{(1-r^{\beta})^{2}}}\in[1-C\varepsilon,1+C\varepsilon].

Accordingly,

\displaystyle\sqrt{(r^{\beta}-1)^{2}+\varepsilon}+r^{\beta}+1

\displaystyle\in

\displaystyle\big[(1-C\varepsilon)(1-r^{\beta})+r^{\beta}+1,\,(1+C\varepsilon)(1-r^{\beta})+r^{\beta}+1\big]

\displaystyle\subseteq

\displaystyle[2-C\varepsilon,2+C\varepsilon]

and

\displaystyle\frac{\sqrt{(r^{\beta}-1)^{2}+\varepsilon}-r^{\beta}+1+\varepsilon}{\sqrt{(r^{\beta}-1)^{2}+\varepsilon}}-1=\frac{1-r^{\beta}+\varepsilon}{\sqrt{(r^{\beta}-1)^{2}+\varepsilon}}

\displaystyle\qquad\in\left[\frac{(1-C\varepsilon)(1-r^{\beta}+\varepsilon)}{1-r^{\beta}},\,\frac{(1+C\varepsilon)(1-r^{\beta}+\varepsilon)}{1-r^{\beta}}\right]\subseteq\left[1-C\varepsilon,1+C\varepsilon\right],

where, as customary,
C
has been renamed line after line.

Hence, we deduce from (
5.15
) that

|\nabla\varphi_{\varepsilon}(x)|\leqslant\frac{r^{\alpha-1}}{2}\left(\big|2(\alpha+\beta)-2\big|+C\varepsilon\right)\leqslant\left(\big|\alpha+\beta-1\big|+C\varepsilon\right)r^{\alpha-1},

which proves (
5.14
) in this case.

Finally, if
r>2
,

\frac{\sqrt{(r^{\beta}-1)^{2}+\varepsilon}}{r^{\beta}-1}=\sqrt{1+\frac{\varepsilon}{(r^{\beta}-1)^{2}}}\in[1-C\varepsilon,1+C\varepsilon].

In this case, we have that

\displaystyle\sqrt{(r^{\beta}-1)^{2}+\varepsilon}+r^{\beta}+1

\displaystyle\in

\displaystyle\big[(1-C\varepsilon)(r^{\beta}-1)+r^{\beta}+1,\,(1+C\varepsilon)(r^{\beta}-1)+r^{\beta}+1\big]

\displaystyle\subseteq

\displaystyle[(2-C\varepsilon)r^{\beta},(2+C\varepsilon)r^{\beta}]

and

\displaystyle\frac{\sqrt{(r^{\beta}-1)^{2}+\varepsilon}-r^{\beta}+1+\varepsilon}{\sqrt{(r^{\beta}-1)^{2}+\varepsilon}}=1-\frac{r^{\beta}-1-\varepsilon}{\sqrt{(r^{\beta}-1)^{2}+\varepsilon}}\in[-C\varepsilon,C\varepsilon],

yielding that

|\nabla\varphi_{\varepsilon}(x)|\leqslant\frac{r^{\alpha-1}}{2}\left(\big|2(\alpha+\beta)r^{\beta}\big|+C\varepsilon\right)\leqslant{r^{\alpha+\beta-1}}\left(\big|(\alpha+\beta)\big|+C\varepsilon\right).

Combining these pieces of information and (
5.15
)
we obtain that (
5.14
) holds true in this case as well.

Now, exploiting (
5.10
), (
5.12
)
and (
5.14
),
and possibly renaming
C
in dependence also on
E
, we see that

\displaystyle\left|\,\int_{\partial E}c^{2}(x)\,|\nabla_{T}\zeta_{\varepsilon}(x)|^{2}\,d{\mathcal{H}}^{n-1}_{x}-\int_{\partial E}c^{2}(x)\,|\nabla_{T}\varphi_{\varepsilon}(x)|^{2}\,d{\mathcal{H}}^{n-1}_{x}\right|

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle C\left(\int_{(\partial E)\cap\big(B_{2\varepsilon}\cup(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{1/\varepsilon})\big)}c^{2}(x)\,|\nabla\varphi_{\varepsilon}(x)|^{2}\,d{\mathcal{H}}^{n-1}_{x}+\int_{\partial E}c^{2}(x)\,|\nabla\tau_{\varepsilon}(x)|^{2}\,|\varphi_{\varepsilon}(x)|^{2}\,d{\mathcal{H}}^{n-1}_{x}\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle C\Bigg[\int_{(\partial E)\cap\big(B_{2\varepsilon}\cup(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{1/\varepsilon})\big)}\max\{|x|^{2\alpha+2\beta-4},|x|^{2\alpha-4}\}\,d{\mathcal{H}}^{n-1}_{x}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\int_{\partial E\cap B_{2\varepsilon}}\varepsilon^{-2}|x|^{2\alpha-2}\,d{\mathcal{H}}^{n-1}_{x}+\int_{\partial E\setminus B_{1/\varepsilon}}\varepsilon^{2}|x|^{2\alpha+2\beta-2}\,d{\mathcal{H}}^{n-1}_{x}\Bigg]

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle C\big(\varepsilon^{2\alpha+n-5}+\varepsilon^{5-2\alpha-2\beta-n}\big),

which is infinitesimal as long as condition (
5.13
) is fulfilled.
This, together with (
5.9
)
and (
5.11
), yields that

\begin{split}&\int_{(\partial E)\cap B_{1}}2c^{2}(x)\,|x|^{2\alpha-2}\,d{\mathcal{H}}^{n-1}_{x}+\int_{(\partial E)\setminus B_{1}}2c^{2}(x)\,|x|^{2\alpha+2\beta-2}\,d{\mathcal{H}}^{n-1}_{x}\\
\leqslant\;&\liminf_{\varepsilon\searrow 0}\int_{\partial E}\frac{2c^{2}(x)\,\zeta^{2}_{\varepsilon}(x)}{|x|^{2}}\,d{\mathcal{H}}^{n-1}_{x}\\
\leqslant\;&\liminf_{\varepsilon\searrow 0}\int_{\partial E}c^{2}(x)\,|\nabla_{T}\zeta_{\varepsilon}(x)|^{2}\,d{\mathcal{H}}^{n-1}_{x}\\
=\;&\liminf_{\varepsilon\searrow 0}\int_{\partial E}c^{2}(x)\,|\nabla_{T}\varphi_{\varepsilon}(x)|^{2}\,d{\mathcal{H}}^{n-1}_{x}\\
\leqslant\;&\liminf_{\varepsilon\searrow 0}\int_{\partial E}c^{2}(x)\,|\nabla\varphi_{\varepsilon}(x)|^{2}\,d{\mathcal{H}}^{n-1}_{x}.\end{split}

Owing to (
5.12
), (
5.13
) and (
5.14
), we can also
pass the limit inside the integral sign in the latter term, using the Dominated Convergence Theorem, and, recalling (
5.15
), we thus find that

(5.16)

\begin{split}&\int_{(\partial E)\cap B_{1}}2c^{2}(x)\,|x|^{2\alpha-2}\,d{\mathcal{H}}^{n-1}_{x}+\int_{(\partial E)\setminus B_{1}}2c^{2}(x)\,|x|^{2\alpha+2\beta-2}\,d{\mathcal{H}}^{n-1}_{x}\\
\leqslant\,&\int_{\partial E}c^{2}(x)\,\lim_{\varepsilon\searrow 0}|\nabla\varphi_{\varepsilon}(x)|^{2}\,d{\mathcal{H}}^{n-1}_{x}\\
=\,&\int_{(\partial E)\cap B_{1}}\alpha^{2}c^{2}(x)\,|x|^{2\alpha-2}\,d{\mathcal{H}}^{n-1}_{x}+\int_{(\partial E)\setminus B_{1}}(\alpha+\beta)^{2}c^{2}(x)\,|x|^{2\alpha+2\beta-2}\,d{\mathcal{H}}^{n-1}_{x},\end{split}

that is

(5.17)

\int_{(\partial E)\cap B_{1}}\kappa_{1}\,c^{2}(x)\,|x|^{2\alpha-2}\,d{\mathcal{H}}^{n-1}_{x}+\int_{(\partial E)\setminus B_{1}}\kappa_{2}\,c^{2}(x)\,|x|^{2\alpha+2\beta-2}\,d{\mathcal{H}}^{n-1}_{x}\leqslant 0,

where
\kappa_{1}:=2-\alpha^{2}

and
\kappa_{2}:=2-(\alpha+\beta)^{2}
.

Our goal is now to exploit the dimension assumption
n\leqslant 7
in (
5.6
)
in order to fulfill (
5.13
) and also make
\kappa_{1}
and
\kappa_{2}
strictly positive.
We stress that this would complete the proof of (
5.6
),
since we would deduce from (
5.17
) that
c
vanishes identically, giving that
\partial E
is a hyperplane.

Thus, it remains to check that it is possible to choose our free parameters
\alpha
and
\beta
such that

(5.18)

\varsigma:=\alpha+\beta<\frac{5-n}{2}<\alpha,\qquad|\varsigma|<\sqrt{2}\qquad{\mbox{and}}\qquad|\alpha|<\sqrt{2}.

Notice that if
3\leqslant n\leqslant 7
, then in particular
-\sqrt{2}<\frac{5-n}{2}<\sqrt{2}
, and therefore
it is possible to choose
\alpha
and
\beta
in such a way that the conditions in (
5.18
) are satisfied.
Namely, if
3\leqslant n\leqslant 7
we can choose

\alpha:=\frac{5-n}{4}+\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}\qquad{\mbox{and}}\qquad\beta:=-\sqrt{2},

obtaining (
5.6
) in this case.

We remark that when
n=2
the statement in (
5.6
)
is clearly satisfied, since to rule out the possibility that a minimal cone is nontrivial
in the plane it is enough to take a straight line which has less perimeter.
This completes the proof of (
5.6
).
∎

Now we provide a proof of (
5.7
).

Proof of (
5.7
).

We start by rewriting (
5.2
)
in coordinates as

(5.19)

\partial_{T,j}\phi=\partial_{j}\phi-(\nabla\phi\cdot\nu)\nu_{j},\qquad{\mbox{ for }}\;j\in\{1,\dots,n\}.

We stress that
\partial_{T,j}
plays the role of a “tangential derivative”, since
\tau_{j}:=e_{j}-\nu_{j}\,\nu

is a tangent vector to
\partial E
(because
\tau_{j}\cdot\nu=e_{j}\cdot\nu-\nu_{j}=0
) and
\nabla\phi\cdot\tau_{j}=\partial_{T,j}\phi
.
Note also that
\nabla_{T}\phi
plays the role of a tangent vector, since

(5.20)

\nu\cdot\nabla_{T}\phi=\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}\nu_{\ell}\,\partial_{T,\ell}\phi=\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}\nu_{\ell}\Big(\partial_{\ell}\phi-(\nabla\phi\cdot\nu)\nu_{\ell}\Big)=\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}\nu_{\ell}\partial_{\ell}\phi-(\nabla\phi\cdot\nu)=0.

Furthermore, we extend
\nu
to a neighborhood of
\partial E
,
say by a normal extension according to which,
for all
x\in\partial E
and
t\in\mathbb{R}
with
|t|
close enough,

(5.21)

\nu(x+t\nu(x))=\nu(x).

In this way, for each
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
,

(5.22)

\begin{split}&0=\left.\frac{d}{dt}\nu_{i}(x)\right|_{t=0}\left.=\frac{d}{dt}\nu_{i}(x+t\nu(x))\right|_{t=0}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\partial_{j}\nu_{i}(x)\,\nu_{j}(x).\end{split}

Hence, since
c
is the norm of the second fundamental form
\nabla_{T}\nu
, we have that

(5.23)

c^{2}=\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}(\partial_{T,j}\nu_{i})^{2}.

At this stage, it is also convenient to observe that the Laplace-Beltrami operator
can be reconstructed from the tangential derivatives through the formula

(5.24)

\Delta_{T}f=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\partial_{T,i}\partial_{T,i}f.

Not to interrupt the flow of the argument, we defer the proof of (
5.24
)
to Appendix
A
.

Moreover, recalling (
5.8
),

\displaystyle\int_{\partial E}\Big(\Delta_{T}f^{2}(x)-2|\nabla_{T}f(x)|^{2}-2f(x)\Delta_{T}f(x)\Big)\,g(x)\,d{\mathcal{H}}^{n-1}_{x}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\int_{\partial E}\nabla_{T}f^{2}(x)\cdot\nabla_{T}g(x)\,d{\mathcal{H}}^{n-1}_{x}-2\int_{\partial E}|\nabla_{T}f(x)|^{2}\,g(x)\,d{\mathcal{H}}^{n-1}_{x}

\displaystyle\qquad+2\int_{\partial E}\nabla_{T}f(x)\cdot\nabla_{T}(f(x)g(x))\,d{\mathcal{H}}^{n-1}_{x}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0,

that is

\frac{\Delta_{T}f^{2}}{2}=|\nabla_{T}f|^{2}+f\Delta_{T}f.

From these observations, we arrive at

(5.25)

\begin{split}&\frac{\Delta_{T}c^{2}}{2}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\Delta_{T}(\partial_{T,j}\nu_{i})^{2}=\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\Big[|\nabla_{T}\partial_{T,j}\nu_{i}|^{2}+\partial_{T,j}\nu_{i}\Delta_{T}(\partial_{T,j}\nu_{i})\Big]\\
&\qquad\qquad\qquad=\sum_{i,j,m=1}^{n}(\partial_{T,m}\partial_{T,j}\nu_{i})^{2}+\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\partial_{T,j}\nu_{i}\Delta_{T}(\partial_{T,j}\nu_{i}).\end{split}

We now recall two useful commutator identities for tangential derivatives, namely, for each
i
,
j\in\{1,\dots,n\}
,

(5.26)

\partial_{T,i}\nu_{j}=\partial_{T,j}\nu_{i}

and

(5.27)

\partial_{T,i}\partial_{T,j}\phi-\partial_{T,j}\partial_{T,i}\phi=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\Big[\nu_{i}\partial_{T,j}\nu_{k}-\nu_{j}\partial_{T,i}\nu_{k}\Big]\partial_{T,k}\phi.

The proofs of (
5.26
) and (
5.27
) are deferred to Appendix
A
.

It is also useful to observe that,
for all
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
,

(5.28)

\sum_{k=1}^{n}\nu_{k}\partial_{T,i}\nu_{k}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\partial_{T,i}\nu_{k}^{2}=\frac{1}{2}\partial_{T,i}|\nu|^{2}=\frac{1}{2}\partial_{T,i}1=0.

Moreover, we point out that

H=\operatorname{div}_{T}\nu,

where
\operatorname{div}_{T}
denotes the tangential divergence of a field, see formula (
A.3
).

Hence, using (
5.24
),
(
5.26
), (
5.27
) and (
5.28
),
for each
j\in\{1,\dots,n\}
we have that

(5.29)

\begin{split}&\Delta_{T}\nu_{j}+c^{2}\nu_{j}-\partial_{T,j}H=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\partial_{T,i}\partial_{T,i}\nu_{j}+c^{2}\nu_{j}-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\partial_{T,j}\partial_{T,i}\nu_{i}\\
&\qquad=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\partial_{T,i}\partial_{T,j}\nu_{i}+c^{2}\nu_{j}-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\partial_{T,j}\partial_{T,i}\nu_{i}\\
&\qquad=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\partial_{T,j}\partial_{T,i}\nu_{i}+\sum_{i,k=1}^{n}\Big[\nu_{i}\partial_{T,j}\nu_{k}-\nu_{j}\partial_{T,i}\nu_{k}\Big]\partial_{T,k}\nu_{i}+c^{2}\nu_{j}-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\partial_{T,j}\partial_{T,i}\nu_{i}\\
&\qquad=\sum_{i,k=1}^{n}\nu_{i}\partial_{T,j}\nu_{k}\,\partial_{T,k}\nu_{i}\\
&\qquad=0.\end{split}

Now, we consider another useful commutator identity, claiming that, for each
k\in\{1,\dots,n\}
, if
H=0
along
\partial E
then

(5.30)

\partial_{T,k}(\Delta_{T}\phi)-\Delta_{T}(\partial_{T,k}\phi)=2\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\Big[\nu_{k}(\partial_{T,i}\nu_{j})(\partial_{T,i}\partial_{T,j}\phi)+(\partial_{T,k}\nu_{j})(\partial_{T,j}\nu_{i})(\partial_{T,i}\phi)\Big].

The proof of (
5.30
) is deferred to Appendix
A
.

In the light of (
5.26
), (
5.28
),
(
5.29
) and (
5.30
) we infer that, if
H=0
along
\partial E
,

(5.31)

\begin{split}&\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}(\partial_{T,i}\nu_{j})\Delta_{T}(\partial_{T,i}\nu_{j})\\
=\;&\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}(\partial_{T,i}\nu_{j})\partial_{T,i}(\Delta_{T}\nu_{j})\\
&\quad-2\sum_{i,j,h,m=1}^{n}(\partial_{T,i}\nu_{j})\Big[\nu_{i}(\partial_{T,h}\nu_{m})(\partial_{T,h}\partial_{T,m}\nu_{j})+(\partial_{T,i}\nu_{m})(\partial_{T,m}\nu_{h})(\partial_{T,h}\nu_{j})\Big]\\
=\;&-\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}(\partial_{T,i}\nu_{j})\partial_{T,i}(c^{2}\nu_{j})\\
&\quad-2\sum_{i,j,h,m=1}^{n}(\partial_{T,j}\nu_{i})\Big[\nu_{i}(\partial_{T,h}\nu_{m})(\partial_{T,h}\partial_{T,m}\nu_{j})+(\partial_{T,i}\nu_{m})(\partial_{T,m}\nu_{h})(\partial_{T,h}\nu_{j})\Big]\\
=\;&-\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}(\partial_{T,i}\nu_{j})\partial_{T,i}(c^{2}\nu_{j})-2\sum_{i,j,h,m=1}^{n}(\partial_{T,j}\nu_{i})(\partial_{T,i}\nu_{m})(\partial_{T,m}\nu_{h})(\partial_{T,h}\nu_{j})\\
=\;&-c^{4}-\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\partial_{T,i}c^{2}\,\nu_{j}(\partial_{T,i}\nu_{j})-2\sum_{i,j,h,m=1}^{n}(\partial_{T,j}\nu_{i})(\partial_{T,i}\nu_{m})(\partial_{T,m}\nu_{h})(\partial_{T,h}\nu_{j})\\
=\;&-c^{4}-2\sum_{i,j,h,m=1}^{n}(\partial_{T,j}\nu_{i})(\partial_{T,i}\nu_{m})(\partial_{T,m}\nu_{h})(\partial_{T,h}\nu_{j}).\end{split}

Furthermore, by (
5.27
) we know that

\displaystyle\partial_{T,j}\partial_{T,h}\nu_{m}=\partial_{T,h}\partial_{T,j}\nu_{m}-\sum_{k=1}^{n}\Big[\nu_{h}\partial_{T,j}\nu_{k}-\nu_{j}\partial_{T,h}\nu_{k}\Big]\partial_{T,k}\nu_{m}

and

\displaystyle\partial_{T,m}\partial_{T,i}\nu_{j}=\partial_{T,i}\partial_{T,m}\nu_{j}-\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}\Big[\nu_{i}\partial_{T,m}\nu_{\ell}-\nu_{m}\partial_{T,i}\nu_{\ell}\Big]\partial_{T,\ell}\nu_{j}.

Thus, by (
5.20
),

\displaystyle\sum_{h=1}^{n}\nu_{h}\,\partial_{T,j}\partial_{T,h}\nu_{m}=-\sum_{h,k=1}^{n}\nu_{h}^{2}\,\partial_{T,j}\nu_{k}\,\partial_{T,k}\nu_{m}=-\sum_{k=1}^{n}\partial_{T,j}\nu_{k}\,\partial_{T,k}\nu_{m}

and

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\nu_{i}\,\partial_{T,m}\partial_{T,i}\nu_{j}=-\sum_{i,\ell=1}^{n}\nu_{i}^{2}\,\partial_{T,m}\nu_{\ell}\,\partial_{T,\ell}\nu_{j}=-\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}\partial_{T,m}\nu_{\ell}\,\partial_{T,\ell}\nu_{j}.

Taking the product of these two identities, we conclude that

\displaystyle\sum_{i,h=1}^{n}\nu_{i}\nu_{h}(\partial_{T,j}\partial_{T,h}\nu_{m})(\partial_{T,m}\partial_{T,i}\nu_{j})=\sum_{k,\ell=1}^{n}(\partial_{T,m}\nu_{\ell})(\partial_{T,\ell}\nu_{j})(\partial_{T,j}\nu_{k})(\partial_{T,k}\nu_{m}).

Therefore, in view of (
5.31
), if
H=0
along
\partial E
,

\displaystyle\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}(\partial_{T,i}\nu_{j})\Delta_{T}(\partial_{T,i}\nu_{j})=-c^{4}-2\sum_{i,j,h,m=1}^{n}\nu_{i}\nu_{h}(\partial_{T,j}\partial_{T,h}\nu_{m})(\partial_{T,m}\partial_{T,i}\nu_{j}).

From this and (
5.25
) we infer that, if
H=0
along
\partial E
,

(5.32)

\frac{\Delta_{T}c^{2}}{2}=\sum_{i,j,m=1}^{n}(\partial_{T,m}\partial_{T,j}\nu_{i})^{2}-c^{4}-2\sum_{i,j,h,m=1}^{n}\nu_{i}\nu_{h}(\partial_{T,j}\partial_{T,h}\nu_{m})(\partial_{T,m}\partial_{T,i}\nu_{j}).

Now, to complete the proof of (
5.7
),
we suppose that
H=0
along
\partial E
, we pick a regular point
x_{0}\in\partial E
and we assume, up to a rotation, that

(5.33)

\nu(x_{0})=e_{n}.

Notice that (
5.19
) evaluated at
x_{0}
gives that

(5.34)

\partial_{T,n}\phi(x_{0})=\partial_{n}\phi(x_{0})-(\nabla\phi(x_{0})\cdot e_{n})=0.

As a result, by (
5.19
), (
5.21
), (
5.22
), (
5.26
) and (
5.27
),

(5.35)

\begin{split}&\partial_{T,i}\partial_{T,n}\nu_{n}(x_{0})\\
=\,&\partial_{T,n}\partial_{T,i}\nu_{n}(x_{0})+\sum_{k=1}^{n}\Big[\nu_{i}(x_{0})\partial_{T,n}\nu_{k}(x_{0})-\nu_{n}(x_{0})\partial_{T,i}\nu_{k}(x_{0})\Big]\partial_{T,k}\nu_{n}(x_{0})\\
=\,&0+\sum_{k=1}^{n}\Big[0-\partial_{T,i}\nu_{k}(x_{0})\Big]\partial_{T,k}\nu_{n}(x_{0})\\
=\,&-\sum_{k=1}^{n}\partial_{T,i}\nu_{k}(x_{0})\,\partial_{T,k}\nu_{n}(x_{0})\\
=\,&-\sum_{k=1}^{n}\Big(\partial_{i}\nu_{k}(x_{0})-(\nabla\nu_{k}(x_{0})\cdot\nu(x_{0}))\nu_{i}(x_{0})\Big)\Big(\partial_{k}\nu_{n}(x_{0})-(\nabla\nu_{n}(x_{0})\cdot\nu(x_{0}))\nu_{k}(x_{0})\Big)\\
=\,&-\sum_{k=1}^{n}\partial_{i}\nu_{k}(x_{0})\,\partial_{k}\nu_{n}(x_{0})\\
=\,&-\sum_{k=1}^{n}\partial_{i}\nu_{k}(x_{0})\,\partial_{n}\nu_{k}(x_{0})\\
=\,&-\left.\frac{1}{2}\partial_{i}\partial_{n}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{n}\nu_{k}^{2}(x)\right)\right|_{x=x_{0}}+\sum_{k=1}^{n}\nu_{k}(x_{0})\partial_{n}\partial_{i}\nu_{k}(x_{0})\\
=\,&0+\partial_{n}\partial_{i}\nu_{n}(x_{0})\\
=\,&\partial_{n}\partial_{n}\nu_{i}(x_{0})\\
=\,&\lim_{t\to 0}\frac{\nu_{i}(x_{0}+t\nu(x_{0}))+\nu_{i}(x_{0}-t\nu(x_{0}))-2\nu_{i}(x_{0})}{t^{2}}\\
=\,&0.\end{split}

Also, owing to (
5.32
),

(5.36)

\frac{\Delta_{T}c^{2}(x_{0})}{2}=\sum_{i,j,m=1}^{n}(\partial_{T,m}\partial_{T,j}\nu_{i}(x_{0}))^{2}-c^{4}(x_{0})-2\sum_{j,m=1}^{n}(\partial_{T,j}\partial_{T,n}\nu_{m}(x_{0}))(\partial_{T,m}\partial_{T,n}\nu_{j}(x_{0})).

By (
5.34
) and (
5.35
), we know that

\partial_{T,n}\partial_{T,n}\nu_{j}(x_{0})=0=\partial_{T,m}\partial_{T,n}\nu_{n}(x_{0})

and therefore, exploiting (
5.26
),

(5.37)

\sum_{j,m=1}^{n}(\partial_{T,j}\partial_{T,n}\nu_{m}(x_{0}))(\partial_{T,m}\partial_{T,n}\nu_{j}(x_{0}))=\sum_{j,m=1}^{n-1}(\partial_{T,j}\partial_{T,m}\nu_{n}(x_{0}))(\partial_{T,m}\partial_{T,j}\nu_{n}(x_{0})).

Besides, we infer from (
5.27
) that, for all
j
,
m\in\{1,\dots,n-1\}
,

\partial_{T,j}\partial_{T,m}\phi(x_{0})=\partial_{T,m}\partial_{T,j}\phi(x_{0}),

since in this case
\nu_{j}(x_{0})=\nu_{m}(x_{0})=0
.

On this account, we can write (
5.37
) in the form

(5.38)

\sum_{j,m=1}^{n}(\partial_{T,j}\partial_{T,n}\nu_{m}(x_{0}))(\partial_{T,m}\partial_{T,n}\nu_{j}(x_{0}))=\sum_{j,m=1}^{n-1}(\partial_{T,m}\partial_{T,j}\nu_{n}(x_{0}))^{2}.

Moreover, using again (
5.34
),

\displaystyle\sum_{i,j,m=1}^{n}(\partial_{T,m}\partial_{T,j}\nu_{i}(x_{0}))^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i,j,m=1}^{n-1}(\partial_{T,m}\partial_{T,j}\nu_{i}(x_{0}))^{2}+\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}(\partial_{T,n}\partial_{T,j}\nu_{i}(x_{0}))^{2}

\displaystyle\qquad+\sum_{i,m=1}^{n}(\partial_{T,m}\partial_{T,n}\nu_{i}(x_{0}))^{2}+\sum_{j,m=1}^{n}(\partial_{T,m}\partial_{T,j}\nu_{n}(x_{0}))^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i,j,m=1}^{n-1}(\partial_{T,m}\partial_{T,j}\nu_{i}(x_{0}))^{2}+0+\sum_{i,m=1}^{n}(\partial_{T,m}\partial_{T,n}\nu_{i}(x_{0}))^{2}+\sum_{j,m=1}^{n}(\partial_{T,m}\partial_{T,j}\nu_{n}(x_{0}))^{2}.

Accordingly, by means of (
5.26
),

\displaystyle\sum_{i,j,m=1}^{n}(\partial_{T,m}\partial_{T,j}\nu_{i}(x_{0}))^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i,j,m=1}^{n-1}(\partial_{T,m}\partial_{T,j}\nu_{i}(x_{0}))^{2}+\sum_{i,m=1}^{n}(\partial_{T,m}\partial_{T,i}\nu_{n}(x_{0}))^{2}+\sum_{j,m=1}^{n}(\partial_{T,m}\partial_{T,j}\nu_{n}(x_{0}))^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i,j,m=1}^{n-1}(\partial_{T,m}\partial_{T,j}\nu_{i}(x_{0}))^{2}+2\sum_{j,m=1}^{n}(\partial_{T,m}\partial_{T,j}\nu_{n}(x_{0}))^{2}.

For this reason, recalling (
5.34
) and (
5.35
),

\displaystyle\sum_{i,j,m=1}^{n}(\partial_{T,m}\partial_{T,j}\nu_{i}(x_{0}))^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i,j,m=1}^{n-1}(\partial_{T,m}\partial_{T,j}\nu_{i}(x_{0}))^{2}+2\sum_{j,m=1}^{n-1}(\partial_{T,m}\partial_{T,j}\nu_{n}(x_{0}))^{2}.

Combining this, (
5.36
) and (
5.38
), we arrive at

(5.39)

\begin{split}&\frac{\Delta_{T}c^{2}(x_{0})}{2}+c^{4}(x_{0})\\
&\qquad=\sum_{i,j,m=1}^{n}(\partial_{T,m}\partial_{T,j}\nu_{i}(x_{0}))^{2}-2\sum_{j,m=1}^{n}(\partial_{T,j}\partial_{T,n}\nu_{m}(x_{0}))(\partial_{T,m}\partial_{T,n}\nu_{j}(x_{0}))\\
&\qquad=\sum_{i,j,m=1}^{n-1}(\partial_{T,m}\partial_{T,j}\nu_{i}(x_{0}))^{2}+2\sum_{j,m=1}^{n-1}(\partial_{T,m}\partial_{T,j}\nu_{n}(x_{0}))^{2}-2\sum_{j,m=1}^{n-1}(\partial_{T,m}\partial_{T,j}\nu_{n}(x_{0}))^{2}\\
&\qquad=\sum_{i,j,m=1}^{n-1}(\partial_{T,m}\partial_{T,j}\nu_{i}(x_{0}))^{2}.\end{split}

Recalling that our goal is to prove (
5.7
), it is now
useful to give a closer look to the quantity
|\nabla_{T}c|^{2}
. For this, by (
5.23
),

(5.40)

\begin{split}&c^{2}|\nabla_{T}c|^{2}=|c\nabla_{T}c|^{2}=\left|\frac{\nabla_{T}c^{2}}{2}\right|^{2}=\frac{1}{4}\sum_{m=1}^{n}(\partial_{T,m}c^{2})^{2}\\
&\quad=\frac{1}{4}\sum_{m=1}^{n}\left(\partial_{T,m}\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}(\partial_{T,j}\nu_{i})^{2}\right)^{2}=\sum_{m=1}^{n}\left(\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}(\partial_{T,j}\nu_{i})(\partial_{T,m}\partial_{T,j}\nu_{i})\right)^{2}\\
&\quad=\sum_{i,j,h,k,m=1}^{n}(\partial_{T,j}\nu_{i})(\partial_{T,k}\nu_{h})(\partial_{T,m}\partial_{T,j}\nu_{i})(\partial_{T,m}\partial_{T,k}\nu_{h})\\
&\quad=\sum_{i,j,h,k,m=1}^{n-1}(\partial_{T,j}\nu_{i})(\partial_{T,k}\nu_{h})(\partial_{T,m}\partial_{T,j}\nu_{i})(\partial_{T,m}\partial_{T,k}\nu_{h}).\end{split}

We stress that the last step in this calculation
ows to (
5.26
), (
5.34
) and (
5.35
).

From (
5.39
) and (
5.40
) we arrive at

(5.41)

\begin{split}\Upsilon\,:=\,&\frac{\Delta_{T}c^{2}}{2}+c^{4}-|\nabla_{T}c|^{2}\\
=\,&\sum_{i,j,m=1}^{n-1}(\partial_{T,m}\partial_{T,j}\nu_{i})^{2}-\frac{1}{c^{2}}\sum_{i,j,h,k,m=1}^{n-1}(\partial_{T,j}\nu_{i})(\partial_{T,k}\nu_{h})(\partial_{T,m}\partial_{T,j}\nu_{i})(\partial_{T,m}\partial_{T,k}\nu_{h})\\
=\,&\frac{1}{c^{2}}\sum_{i,j,m=1}^{n-1}\left[c^{2}(\partial_{T,m}\partial_{T,j}\nu_{i})^{2}-\sum_{h,k=1}^{n-1}(\partial_{T,j}\nu_{i})(\partial_{T,k}\nu_{h})(\partial_{T,m}\partial_{T,j}\nu_{i})(\partial_{T,m}\partial_{T,k}\nu_{h})\right]\\
=\,&\frac{1}{c^{2}}\sum_{i,j,h,k,m=1}^{n-1}\Big[(\partial_{T,k}\nu_{h})^{2}(\partial_{T,m}\partial_{T,j}\nu_{i})^{2}-(\partial_{T,j}\nu_{i})(\partial_{T,k}\nu_{h})(\partial_{T,m}\partial_{T,j}\nu_{i})(\partial_{T,m}\partial_{T,k}\nu_{h})\Big],\end{split}

where all the quantities are computed at the point
x_{0}
as above
(and, from now on, in the calculations needed to establish (
5.7
),
the fact that the quantities involved are computed at
x_{0}
will be omitted, to ease the notation).

To (slightly) simplify the notation in (
5.41
), one can observe that, for tensors
a_{kh}
and
b_{mji}
,

\displaystyle\sum_{i,j,h,k,m=1}^{n-1}\Big(a_{kh}b_{mji}-a_{ji}b_{mkh}\Big)^{2}=\sum_{i,j,h,k,m=1}^{n-1}\Big(a_{kh}^{2}b_{mji}^{2}+a_{ji}^{2}b_{mkh}^{2}-2a_{kh}a_{ji}b_{mji}b_{mkh}\Big)

\displaystyle\qquad=\sum_{i,j,h,k,m=1}^{n-1}\Big(a_{kh}^{2}b_{mji}^{2}-a_{kh}a_{ji}b_{mji}b_{mkh}\Big)+\sum_{i,j,h,k,m=1}^{n-1}\Big(a_{ji}^{2}b_{mkh}^{2}-a_{kh}a_{ji}b_{mji}b_{mkh}\Big)

\displaystyle\qquad=2\sum_{i,j,h,k,m=1}^{n-1}\Big(a_{kh}^{2}b_{mji}^{2}-a_{ji}a_{kh}b_{mji}b_{mkh}\Big).

Using this observation with
a_{kh}:=\partial_{T,k}\nu_{h}
and
b_{mji}:=\partial_{T,m}\partial_{T,j}\nu_{i}
, we write (
5.41
) in the form

(5.42)

\Upsilon=\frac{1}{2c^{2}}\sum_{i,j,h,k,m=1}^{n-1}\Big((\partial_{T,k}\nu_{h})(\partial_{T,m}\partial_{T,j}\nu_{i})-(\partial_{T,j}\nu_{i})(\partial_{T,m}\partial_{T,k}\nu_{h})\Big)^{2}.

Now we observe that rotations fixing
e_{n}

preserve the normalization condition (
5.33
). Accordingly, we may suppose without loss of generality that
x_{0}=|x_{0}|\,e_{n-1}
. Additionally, to complete the proof of (
5.7
) we are now exploiting the cone structure of
E
and we obtain that, using the signed distance function
d
, for all
t>0
and
x\in\partial E
we have that
d(tx)=0
and therefore

0=\left.\frac{d}{dt}d(tx)\right|_{t=1}=\nabla d(x)\cdot x=\nu(x)\cdot x.

This observation and (
5.20
) give that, for each
x\in\partial E
and
i\in\{1,\dots,n-1\}
,

0=\partial_{T,i}(\nu(x)\cdot x)=\partial_{T,i}\nu(x)\cdot x+\nu(x)\cdot(\partial_{T,i}x)=\partial_{T,i}\nu(x)\cdot x.

Evaluating this at the point
x_{0}
, we deduce that
0=|x_{0}|\,\partial_{T,i}\nu(x_{0})\cdot e_{n-1}
, whence, by (
5.26
),

(5.43)

\partial_{T,n-1}\nu_{i}(x_{0})=\partial_{T,i}\nu_{n-1}(x_{0})=0.

Consequently, by (
5.34
) and (
5.42
),

(5.44)

\begin{split}\Upsilon\,&=\frac{1}{2c^{2}}\Bigg[\sum_{{1\leqslant i,j,h,m\leqslant n-1}\atop{1\leqslant k\leqslant n-2}}\Big((\partial_{T,k}\nu_{h})(\partial_{T,m}\partial_{T,j}\nu_{i})-(\partial_{T,j}\nu_{i})(\partial_{T,m}\partial_{T,k}\nu_{h})\Big)^{2}\\
&\qquad\qquad+\sum_{{1\leqslant i,j,h,m\leqslant n-1}}(\partial_{T,j}\nu_{i})^{2}(\partial_{T,m}\partial_{T,n-1}\nu_{h})^{2}\Bigg]\\
&=\frac{1}{2c^{2}}\Bigg[\sum_{{1\leqslant i,h,m\leqslant n-1}\atop{1\leqslant j,k\leqslant n-2}}\Big((\partial_{T,k}\nu_{h})(\partial_{T,m}\partial_{T,j}\nu_{i})-(\partial_{T,j}\nu_{i})(\partial_{T,m}\partial_{T,k}\nu_{h})\Big)^{2}\\
&\qquad\qquad+\sum_{{1\leqslant i,h,m\leqslant n-1}\atop{1\leqslant k\leqslant n-2}}(\partial_{T,k}\nu_{h})^{2}(\partial_{T,m}\partial_{T,n-1}\nu_{i})^{2}+c^{2}\sum_{{1\leqslant h,m\leqslant n-1}}(\partial_{T,m}\partial_{T,n-1}\nu_{h})^{2}\Bigg]\\
&=\frac{1}{2c^{2}}\Bigg[\sum_{{1\leqslant i,m\leqslant n-1}\atop{1\leqslant j,h,k\leqslant n-2}}\Big((\partial_{T,k}\nu_{h})(\partial_{T,m}\partial_{T,j}\nu_{i})-(\partial_{T,j}\nu_{i})(\partial_{T,m}\partial_{T,k}\nu_{h})\Big)^{2}\\
&\qquad\qquad+\sum_{{1\leqslant i,m\leqslant n-1}\atop{1\leqslant j,k\leqslant n-2}}(\partial_{T,j}\nu_{i})^{2}(\partial_{T,m}\partial_{T,k}\nu_{n-1})^{2}+2c^{2}\sum_{{1\leqslant h,m\leqslant n-1}}(\partial_{T,m}\partial_{T,n-1}\nu_{h})^{2}\Bigg]\\
&=\frac{1}{2c^{2}}\Bigg[\sum_{{1\leqslant m\leqslant n-1}\atop{1\leqslant i,j,h,k\leqslant n-2}}\Big((\partial_{T,k}\nu_{h})(\partial_{T,m}\partial_{T,j}\nu_{i})-(\partial_{T,j}\nu_{i})(\partial_{T,m}\partial_{T,k}\nu_{h})\Big)^{2}\\
&\qquad\qquad+\sum_{{1\leqslant m\leqslant n-1}\atop{1\leqslant j,h,k\leqslant n-2}}(\partial_{T,k}\nu_{h})^{2}(\partial_{T,m}\partial_{T,j}\nu_{n-1})^{2}+3c^{2}\sum_{{1\leqslant h,m\leqslant n-1}}(\partial_{T,m}\partial_{T,n-1}\nu_{h})^{2}\Bigg]\\
&=\frac{1}{2c^{2}}\Bigg[\sum_{{1\leqslant m\leqslant n-1}\atop{1\leqslant i,j,h,k\leqslant n-2}}\Big((\partial_{T,k}\nu_{h})(\partial_{T,m}\partial_{T,j}\nu_{i})-(\partial_{T,j}\nu_{i})(\partial_{T,m}\partial_{T,k}\nu_{h})\Big)^{2}\\
&\qquad\qquad+4c^{2}\sum_{{1\leqslant h,m\leqslant n-1}}(\partial_{T,m}\partial_{T,n-1}\nu_{h})^{2}\Bigg]\\
&\geqslant 2\sum_{{1\leqslant h,m\leqslant n-1}}(\partial_{T,m}\partial_{T,n-1}\nu_{h})^{2}.\end{split}

Moreover, by (
5.27
), (
5.33
)
and (
5.43
), at the point
x_{0}
we have that

(5.45)

\partial_{T,i}\partial_{T,n-1}\phi-\partial_{T,n-1}\partial_{T,i}\phi=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\Big[\nu_{i}\partial_{T,n-1}\nu_{k}-\nu_{n-1}\partial_{T,i}\nu_{k}\Big]\partial_{T,k}\phi=0.

Similarly, at the point
x_{0}
we have that

(5.46)

\partial_{T,n-1}\phi=\partial_{n-1}\phi-(\nabla\phi\cdot\nu)\nu_{n-1}=\partial_{n-1}\phi.

Furthermore, since
E
is a cone we have that
\nu(tx_{0})=\nu(x_{0})
for all
t>0

and therefore
\partial_{T,m}\nu_{h}
is a homogeneous function of degree
-1
.
This yields that

\partial_{T,m}\nu_{h}(x_{0})=-\nabla\partial_{T,m}\nu_{h}(x_{0})\cdot x_{0}=-|x_{0}|\,\partial_{n-1}\partial_{T,m}\nu_{h}(x_{0}).

Hence, by (
5.45
) and (
5.46
),

\partial_{T,m}\partial_{T,n-1}\nu_{h}(x_{0})=\partial_{T,n-1}\partial_{T,m}\nu_{h}(x_{0})=\partial_{n-1}\partial_{T,m}\nu_{h}(x_{0})=-\frac{\partial_{T,m}\nu_{h}(x_{0})}{|x_{0}|}.

Combining this information with (
5.44
) we conclude that

\Upsilon\geqslant 2\sum_{{1\leqslant h,m\leqslant n-1}}\frac{\big(\partial_{T,m}\nu_{h}(x_{0})\big)^{2}}{|x_{0}|^{2}}=\frac{2c^{2}}{|x_{0}|^{2}}.

The proof of (
5.7
) is thereby complete.

5.2.
Bernstein’s problem and a conjecture by E. De Giorgi

We also recall that the regularity of minimal surfaces is strictly linked to the so-called
Bernstein’s problem (named after S. Bernstein who first solved the case in dimension 3,
see
[
16
]
).
Bernstein’s problem asks whether or not a minimal graph in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
(i.e., a minimal
surface which possesses a global graphical structure of the form
x_{n}=u(x^{\prime})
with
x^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{n-1}
) is necessarily affine.
The answer to this problem is affirmative in
dimension
n\leqslant 8

(due to the works of
[
64
,
39
,
38
,
6
,
96
]
), and negative when
n\geqslant 9
(see
[
19
]
).

As a matter of fact, the connection between Bernstein’s problem and the regularity
of minimal surfaces in (
5.4
) is clearly showcased in
[
39
,
38
]
by showing that

(5.47)

\begin{split}&{\mbox{if all minimal cones in\penalty\ $\mathbb{R}^{n-1}$ are halfplanes,}}\\
&{\mbox{then Bernstein's problem has an affirmative answer in\penalty\ $\mathbb{R}^{n}$.}}\end{split}

The link between Bernstein’s problem and the limit interfaces of phase transition models
(as described by the
\Gamma
-convergence theory in Theorem
4.1
)
was possibly an inspiring motivation for Ennio De Giorgi to state one of his most famous conjectures
[
41
]
.

The gist of this conjecture could be as follows: given that, at a large scale,
the level sets of “good” solutions of the Allen-Cahn equation approach perimeter minimizing surfaces (as made precise by the
\Gamma
-convergence theory in Theorem
4.1

and by the geometric convergence of Theorem
4.2
)
and given that minimal graphs reduce to hyperplanes in dimension
n\leqslant 8
(according to
Bernstein’s problem), would it be possible that level sets of “good” global solutions
of the Allen-Cahn equation are already hyperplanes? Since level sets corresponding to different values of the solution cannot intersect, this would say that all the level sets are in fact parallel hyperplanes and therefore the solution only depends on the distance to one of these hyperplanes
(in particular, the solution would be a function depending only on one Euclidean variable).

In all this heuristic discussion, we have been vague about what a “good” solution precisely is:
in a sense, besides boundedness and regularity assumptions, in view of Theorems
4.1
and
4.2
a natural hypothesis would be to require that the solution is a local minimizer; furthermore, to fall within the range of application of
Bernstein’s problem, it would be desirable to know that the limit minimal surface has a graphical structure and for this some monotonicity assumption on the solution could be helpful (since, at least locally, it would entail a graphical structure of the level set via Implicit Function Theorem).

It would be however desirable to keep the number of assumptions to the minimum and possibly to confine them to assumptions of ‘‘geometric’’ type: in this spirit, one may be tempted to remove the minimality assumption (which is instead of ‘‘variational’’ and ‘‘energetic’’ type)
and focus mainly on a monotonicity assumption
(roughly speaking, after all, maybe monotonicity is already an indication of some ‘‘weak’’ form of minimality
11
11

11

A more precise link between monotonicity
and this weak notion of minimality, and more precisely stability, will be given in (
5.49
).
since it avoids oscillations that increase energy).

These arguments (and likely many others of much deeper nature) have probably inspired De Giorgi for the precise formulation of this conjecture, which goes as follows:

Conjecture 5.1
.

Let
n\leqslant 8
and
u\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\cap L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
be a global solution of the Allen-Cahn equation

-\Delta u=u-u^{3}

such that

\partial_{n}u(x)>0\quad{\mbox{ for every }}\,x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

Is it true that
u
is one-dimensional, i.e. that there exist
u_{0}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
and
\omega\in\partial B_{1}
such that
u(x)=u_{0}(\omega\cdot x)
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
?

Conjecture
5.1
has been proven for
n\in\{2,3\}

but it is still open for
n\in\{4,\dots,8\}
, see
[
66
,
15
,
7
,
2
]
. For
n\geqslant 9
,
an example of global, bounded and monotone solution of the Allen-Cahn equation which is not one-dimensional has been constructed in
[
44
]
, thus confirming that the dimensional restriction in Conjecture
5.1
cannot be removed.

In dimension
n\in\{4,\dots,8\}
Conjecture
5.1
is known to hold under an additional assumption on the “profiles of the solution at infinity”. Namely, since
u

in Conjecture
5.1
is bounded and monotone in the direction of
e_{n}
, one can define, for all
x^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{n-1}
,

\overline{u}(x^{\prime}):=\lim_{x_{n}\to+\infty}u(x^{\prime},x_{n})\qquad{\mbox{and}}\qquad\underline{u}(x^{\prime}):=\lim_{x_{n}\to-\infty}u(x^{\prime},x_{n}).

In this setting, it has been proved in
[
91
]
that Conjecture
5.1

holds true under the additional assumption

(5.48)

\overline{u}(x^{\prime})=-\underline{u}(x^{\prime})=1\quad{\mbox{ for every }}\,x^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{n-1}.

For further results establishing Conjecture
5.1
under suitable assumptions
on the limit profiles see
[
56
,
58
,
59
]
. See also
[
57
]

for an overview of Conjecture
5.1
and of related problems.

We also mention that a related problem, posed by theoretical physicist Gary William Gibbons
consisted in determining whether a bounded global solution of the Allen-Cahn equation
is necessarily one dimensional under the uniform limit assumption

\lim_{T\to+\infty}\sup_{x_{n}\geqslant T}|u(x^{\prime},x_{n})-1|=0\qquad{\mbox{and}}\qquad\lim_{T\to+\infty}\sup_{x_{n}\leqslant-T}|u(x^{\prime},x_{n})+1|=0.

Note that this condition is stronger than (
5.48
).
The answer to Gibbons’ problem is known to be positive for every dimension
n
, see
[
10
,
14
,
61
]
.

It is now worth coming back to the relation between monotonicity and some weak form of minimality which was raised before the statement of Conjecture
5.1
.
A precise notion of this is given by the observation that monotonicity implies stability:
namely, if
u
is a solution of

\Delta u=W^{\prime}(u)

such that
\partial_{n}u>0
in some domain
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
, then, for all
\phi\in C^{\infty}_{0}(\Omega)
, we have that

(5.49)

\int_{\Omega}\Big(|\nabla\phi(x)|^{2}+W^{\prime\prime}(u(x))\,\phi^{2}(x)\Big)\,dx\geqslant 0.

Indeed, under the monotonicity assumption it is fair to define
\psi:=\frac{\phi^{2}}{\partial_{n}u}

and infer that

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\Big(|\nabla\phi(x)|^{2}+W^{\prime\prime}(u(x))\,\phi^{2}(x)\Big)\,dx=\int_{\Omega}\left(|\nabla\phi(x)|^{2}+\partial_{n}\Big(W^{\prime}(u(x))\Big)\,\frac{\phi^{2}(x)}{\partial_{n}u(x)}\right)\,dx

\displaystyle\qquad=\int_{\Omega}\left(|\nabla\phi(x)|^{2}+\partial_{n}\Big(\Delta u(x)\Big)\,\psi(x)\right)\,dx

\displaystyle\qquad=\int_{\Omega}\Big(\big|\nabla\big(\sqrt{\psi(x)}\,\sqrt{\partial_{n}u(x)}\big)\big|^{2}-\nabla\partial_{n}u(x)\cdot\nabla\psi(x)\Big)\,dx

\displaystyle\qquad=\int_{\Omega}\left(\left|\frac{\sqrt{\partial_{n}u(x)}\,\nabla\psi(x)}{2\sqrt{\psi(x)}}+\frac{\sqrt{\psi(x)}\,\nabla\partial_{n}u(x)}{2\sqrt{\partial_{n}u(x)}}\right|^{2}-\nabla\partial_{n}u(x)\cdot\nabla\psi(x)\right)\,dx

\displaystyle\qquad=\int_{\Omega}\left(\frac{{\partial_{n}u(x)}\,|\nabla\psi(x)|^{2}}{4{\psi(x)}}+\frac{{\psi(x)}\,|\nabla\partial_{n}u(x)|^{2}}{4{\partial_{n}u(x)}}-\frac{1}{2}\nabla\partial_{n}u(x)\cdot\nabla\psi(x)\right)\,dx

\displaystyle\qquad=\int_{\Omega}\left|\frac{\sqrt{\partial_{n}u(x)}\,\nabla\psi(x)}{2\sqrt{\psi(x)}}-\frac{\sqrt{\psi(x)}\,\nabla\partial_{n}u(x)}{2\sqrt{\partial_{n}u(x)}}\right|^{2}\,dx\geqslant 0,

which is (
5.49
).

We also note that (
5.49
) states that the second derivative of the corresponding
energy functional (say, (
4.10
) with
\varepsilon:=1
) is nonnegative. In particular,
minimizers satisfy the stability inequality in (
5.49
).

6.
Long-range interactions and the nonlocal Allen-Cahn equation

In view of the discussion on page
2.34
, it is of interest to consider minimizers,
and more generally critical points,
of the long-range energy functional

\frac{1}{4}\iint_{{\mathcal{Q}}(\Omega)}\frac{(u(x)-u(y))^{2}}{|x-y|^{n+\alpha}}\,dx\,dy+\int_{\Omega}W(u(x))\,dx,

where
{\mathcal{Q}}(\Omega)

has been defined in (
2.31
), for a given
\alpha\in(0,2)
,
and this not only in view of natural generalizations of the classical setting to more complicated ones,
but also due to the structural formulation of the phase coexistence problem which is intrinsically
long-range (being the short-range case a very handy and important simplification).

The corresponding critical points in this setting are solutions of
the fractional counterpart of the Allen-Cahn equation given by

(-\Delta)^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}u(x)+W^{\prime}(u(x))=0

for
x\in\Omega
(to be compared with the classical case in (
3.1
)).

The case of heterogeneous materials (to be compared with (
3.4
))
can also be taken into account, via the more general equation

(-\Delta)^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}u(x)+Q(x)\,W^{\prime}(u(x))=0,

for
Q

ranging in
[\underline{Q},\overline{Q}]
, for some
\overline{Q}\geqslant\underline{Q}>0
.

Here above, we are using the fractional Laplacian operator, defined (up to a normalizing constant that we omit) as

(-\Delta)^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}u(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\frac{u(x)-u(y)}{|x-y|^{n+\alpha}}\,dx\,dy.

Notice that the above integral is singular, hence, if needed, one has to consider it in the Cauchy principal value sense,
to allow for cancellations (see e.g.
[
94
,
1
]

and the references therein for the basics on the fractional Laplacian).

Solutions of the fractional heterogeneous Allen-Cahn equation in a periodic medium
whose interface is trapped within two hyperplanes have been constructed in
[
36
]

(this can be considered as a nonlocal counterpart of Theorem
3.1
).
See also
[
53
]
for a general discussion about the
fractional Allen-Cahn equation.

7.
The nonlocal limit interface and the theory of nonlocal
\Gamma
-convergence

The long-range interaction energies present a richer
\Gamma
-convergence theory than their
classical counterpart. First of all, the short-range functional in (
1.1
) needs to be replaced by its fractional analog
in (
1.4
), but also the fractional counterpart of the
rescaled functional in (
4.10
) requires some care in determining the appropriate penalization parameters.
Moreover, the result obtained in this case deeply depends on the fractional exponent
\alpha
:
as we will now clarify, for
\alpha\in(0,1)
the
\Gamma
-limit is related to nonlocal minimal surfaces and
for
\alpha\in[1,2)
to classical ones. This is especially interesting since it suggests that, while
for small values of the fractional exponent the phase transition problem always maintains a clearly distinctive long-range feature,
for large values of the fractional exponent, at a large scale, the phase transition problem
tends to resemble a local one (the threshold between “small” and “large” fractional parameters being given by
the specific value
\alpha=1
).

As for the “appropriate” rescaled functional, one can try to get inspired by (
4.1
)
and look for a perturbative fractional Allen-Cahn equation of the form

\varepsilon^{\alpha}(-\Delta)^{\alpha}u(x)+W^{\prime}(u(x))=0,

for a small parameter
\varepsilon
. This would correspond to an energy functional (up to normalization constants that we disregard) of the type

(7.1)

\varepsilon^{\alpha}\iint_{{\mathcal{Q}}(\Omega)}\frac{(u(x)-u(y))^{2}}{|x-y|^{n+\alpha}}\,dx\,dy+\int_{\Omega}W(u(x))\,dx.

But to develop a solid theory of
\Gamma
-convergence one would like to have an energy functional that has the tendency to remain
bounded as the perturbative parameter vanishes (and note that this would have been a point to raise
even in the classical case, when shifting from the equation in (
4.1
)
to the energy functional in (
4.10
)): for this, one exploits the freedom of multiplying the energy by a scalar,
which does not change the critical points, and then chooses appropriately this gauge to have a control of the energy
as the perturbative parameter vanishes.
That is, without affecting the minimization problem, we replace (
7.1
) with the functional

(7.2)

\varsigma_{\varepsilon}\left[\varepsilon^{\alpha}\iint_{{\mathcal{Q}}(\Omega)}\frac{(u(x)-u(y))^{2}}{|x-y|^{n+\alpha}}\,dx\,dy+\int_{\Omega}W(u(x))\,dx\right]

As a model transition for this calculation, one can suppose that

and take, for instance,

for a given smooth, odd function

We note that, on the one hand,

On the other hand,

\varepsilon^{\alpha-n}\iint_{{\mathcal{Q}}(\Omega)}\frac{(u_{\varepsilon}(x)-u_{\varepsilon}(y))^{2}}{|x-y|^{n+\alpha}}\,dx\,dy\simeq\begin{cases}\varepsilon^{\alpha-n}&{\mbox{ if }}\alpha\in(0,1),\\
\varepsilon^{1-n}|\ln\varepsilon|&{\mbox{ if }}\alpha=1,\\
\varepsilon^{1-n}&{\mbox{ if }}\alpha\in(1,2).\end{cases}

We defer the proof of (

)
to Appendix

As a result,

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{\alpha}\iint_{{\mathcal{Q}}(\Omega)}\frac{(u(x)-u(y))^{2}}{|x-y|^{n+\alpha}}\,dx\,dy+\int_{\Omega}W(u(x))\,dx=\begin{cases}\varepsilon^{\alpha}&{\mbox{ if }}\alpha\in(0,1),\\
\varepsilon|\ln\varepsilon|&{\mbox{ if }}\alpha=1,\\
\varepsilon&{\mbox{ if }}\alpha\in(1,2).\end{cases}

&\qquad\qquad{\mbox{with }}\;\varsigma_{\varepsilon}:=\begin{cases}\varepsilon^{-\alpha}&{\mbox{ if }}\alpha\in(0,1),\\

8.
Nonlocal minimal surfaces and one-dimensional symmetry

8.1.
Nonlocal minimal surfaces

Given the strong connection between long-range phase transitions in the “genuinely nonlocal” range
\alpha\in(0,1)
and the nonlocal minimal surfaces (as showcased in Theorems
7.1

and
7.2
), it is desirable to understand better the regularity and flatness properties of
the minimizers of the
\alpha
-perimeter. While the classical situation is fully understood,
in light of (
5.4
)
and (
5.5
), the nonlocal counterpart of this regularity theory is broadly open. To the best of our knowledge, no example of singular nonlocal minimal surface is known,
and an interior regularity theory for
\alpha
-perimeter minimizers
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
has been established

(8.1)

when
n=2
, for all
\alpha\in(0,1)
,

(8.2)

when
n\leqslant 7
, for all
\alpha\in(\alpha_{0},1)
, for a suitable
\alpha_{0}\in(0,1)
,

(8.3)

when the set has a graphical structure, for all
\alpha\in(0,1)
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

Indeed, (
8.1
) has been established in Corollary 1 of
[
88
]

(with smooth regularity coming from Theorem 1.1 in
[
12
]
),
(
8.2
) in Theorem 3 of
[
32
]
, and (
8.3
) in Theorem 1.1 of
[
26
]
.

Notice in particular that (
8.2
) carries the classical minimal surfaces regularity theory in (
5.4
)
over to the nonlocal setting, provided that the fractional exponent is “large enough”.

As for the nonlocal version of Bernstein’s problem, the classical result by De Giorgi in (
5.47
)
has a full counterpart for nonlocal minimal graphs, as proved in
Theorem 1.2 of
[
63
]
(see also
[
60
]
for a different proof and
for related results): therefore one can infer from (
8.1
)
and (
8.2
)
that

(8.4)

\begin{split}&{\mbox{Bernstein's problem for $\alpha$-minimal graphs has}}\\
&{\mbox{an affirmative answer in\penalty\ $\mathbb{R}^{n}$
when\penalty\ $n\leqslant 3$ for all\penalty\ $\alpha\in(0,1)$,}}\\
&{\mbox{as well as
when\penalty\ $n\leqslant 8$ for all\penalty\ $\alpha\in(\alpha_{0},1)$, for a suitable\penalty\ $\alpha_{0}\in(0,1)$}}.\end{split}

Once again, this transfers into the nonlocal world the classical affirmative answer to Bernstein’s problem
discussed on page
5.2
, provided that the fractional exponent is “large enough”
(no nonlocal counterexamples to the Bernstein’s problem being available so far).

It is open, and very interesting, to understand whether the results in (
8.1
)
and (
8.2
) can be carried over to higher dimensions.
In such a generality, an interesting feature is that, even without smooth regularity results,
one can obtain a perimeter estimate for
\alpha
-minimal surfaces, and, even more generally,
for
\alpha
-stable ones. As a matter of fact, as proved in Theorem 1.1
of
[
35
]
, for any dimension
n
and any fractional exponent
\alpha\in(0,1)
,
given any
\alpha
-stable set in the unit ball
B_{1}
, its classical perimeter in
B_{1/2}

is bounded by a constant depending only on
n
and
\alpha
.
This is interesting not only because it provides a regularity result
(and note that even for
\alpha
-minimizers, the perimeter bound is sharper than an
\alpha
-perimeter
bound which can follow by comparison with a given competitor), but also because
the interior bound does not depend on the shape of the set outside
B_{1}
.
In particular, the given
\alpha
-stable set in the unit ball
B_{1}
may have arbitrarily many “fingers” reaching out towards infinity, but their contribution to the perimeter in
B_{1/2}

is bounded universally, independently on the number of these fingers: that is, most of the fingers have
the tendency to either merge or get extinguished before getting to the center, to avoid the formation
of extra perimeter, see Figure
15
. Notice that this is a purely nonlocal phenomenon,
since in the classical case, an arbitrarily large number of parallel hyperplanes would describe
the case of a stable zero mean curvature set with arbitrarily large perimeter in
B_{1/2}
.

For flatness results about
\alpha
-stable case, see also
[

To understand model cases for the regularity theory of nonlocal minimal surfaces,
to start with one can also focus specifically on cones possessing some special symmetry:
in particular, inspired by Lawson’s construction

, we may be willing to
fix our attention to cones of the form

for some

This case already reveals a very intriguing property, specific of the nonlocal setting. As a matter of fact,
as proved in Theorems 3 and 4 of

for which the cone in (

(and this is already in contrast with the classical case, in which some dimensional restriction is needed).
On the other hand, there exists
\alpha_{\star}\in(0,1)
such that,

(8.6)

for
\alpha\in(0,\alpha_{\star})

these cones are unstable when

and stable when

.

That is, stability for this type
of cones occurs already in dimension

when the fractional exponent is “small enough”
(this has to be compared instead with the different
classical scenario in (

)).

The result in (

must exhibit one of these remarkable situations:

•

either the stable cones in (

are minimizers (which would affirm that the restriction in (

•

or the stable cones in (

are not minimizers (which would provide an example of stable cone which is not a minimizer,

Therefore, the result in (

beautiful is happening in the nonlocal world (though we do not understand what is really happening!).

Though we do not go into details here, we mention that the boundary regularity
for
\alpha
-minimal surfaces is also completely different from the classical case,
since in the nonlocal world these surfaces have the possibility of producing boundary jumps
(and they do so generically), according to a phenomenon that we christened
with the name of “stickiness”: see

,
21
,
50
,

47
,

for further details. See also
[

for further comparisons between classical and nonlocal minimal surfaces.

The regularity of nonlocal minimal surfaces has also a solid link with the fractional counterpart

and

could be, in principle,
different from that in Conjecture

for
\alpha
-minimal graphs, which, in its full generality, is still under investigation

) and (

is that it is known to hold true:

,

,

]
,

•

Additionally, under the additional limit assumption (

\operatorname{div}_{T}F:=\operatorname{div}F-\nabla(F\cdot\nu)\cdot\nu,

see e.g.
[
54
]
.
We observe that

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\partial_{T,i}fe_{i})\cdot\nu=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Big(\partial_{i}f-(\nabla f\cdot\nu)\nu_{i}\Big)e_{i}\cdot\nu

\displaystyle\qquad=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Big(\partial_{i}f-(\nabla f\cdot\nu)\nu_{i}\Big)\nu_{i}=(\nabla f\cdot\nu)-(\nabla f\cdot\nu)\sum_{i=1}^{n}\nu_{i}^{2}=0

and, as a result, by the Tangential Divergence Theorem (see e.g.
[
54
]
) applied
to the vector field
F:=\partial_{T,i}fe_{i}
, we have that, for all
g\in C^{1}(\partial E)
,

(A.4)

\begin{split}&\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{\partial E}\operatorname{div}_{T}\big(\partial_{T,i}f(x)\,e_{i}\big)\,g(x)\,d{\mathcal{H}}^{n-1}_{x}\\
=\;&\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{\partial E}(\partial_{T,i}f(x)\,e_{i}\big)\cdot\Big(H(x)\,\nu(x)\,g(x)-\nabla_{T}g(x)\Big)\,d{\mathcal{H}}^{n-1}_{x}\\
=\;&-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{\partial E}(\partial_{T,i}f(x)\,e_{i}\big)\cdot\nabla_{T}g(x)\,d{\mathcal{H}}^{n-1}_{x}\\
=\;&-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{\partial E}\partial_{T,i}f(x)\,\partial_{T,i}g(x)\,d{\mathcal{H}}^{n-1}_{x}.\end{split}

Moreover, recalling (
5.19
), in view of (
5.22
),

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\operatorname{div}_{T}(\partial_{T,i}fe_{i})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Big[\operatorname{div}(\partial_{T,i}fe_{i})-\nabla\big((\partial_{T,i}fe_{i})\cdot\nu\big)\cdot\nu\Big]

\displaystyle\qquad=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Big[\partial_{i}(\partial_{T,i}f)-\nabla\big(\partial_{T,i}f\nu_{i}\big)\cdot\nu\Big]

\displaystyle\qquad=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Big[\partial_{T,i}(\partial_{T,i}f)+\big(\nabla(\partial_{T,i}f)\cdot\nu\big)\nu_{i}-\nabla\big(\partial_{T,i}f\nu_{i}\big)\cdot\nu\Big]

\displaystyle\qquad=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\partial_{T,i}\partial_{T,i}f+\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\Big[\partial_{j}(\partial_{T,i}f)\nu_{j}\nu_{i}-\partial_{j}\big(\partial_{T,i}f\nu_{i}\big)\nu_{j}\Big]

\displaystyle\qquad=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\partial_{T,i}\partial_{T,i}f-\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\partial_{T,i}f\,\partial_{j}\nu_{i}\,\nu_{j}

\displaystyle\qquad=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\partial_{T,i}\partial_{T,i}f

and consequently, by (
A.4
),

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{\partial E}\partial_{T,i}\partial_{T,i}f(x)\,g(x)\,d{\mathcal{H}}^{n-1}_{x}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{\partial E}\partial_{T,i}f(x)\,\partial_{T,i}g(x)\,d{\mathcal{H}}^{n-1}_{x}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\int_{\partial E}\nabla_{T}f(x)\cdot\nabla_{T}g(x)\,d{\mathcal{H}}^{n-1}_{x}.

Owing to (
5.8
), the proof of (
5.24
) is thereby complete.
∎

Proof of (

) and (

which demonstrates (

).

Now, to prove (
5.27
), we use (
5.19
) and (
5.26
)
to see that

\displaystyle\partial_{T,i}\partial_{T,j}\phi

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\partial_{T,i}\Big(\partial_{j}\phi-(\nabla\phi\cdot\nu)\nu_{j}\Big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\partial_{T,i}\partial_{j}\phi-\sum_{k=1}^{n}\Big[(\partial_{T,i}\partial_{k}\phi)\nu_{k}\nu_{j}+\partial_{k}\phi\Big(\partial_{T,i}\nu_{k}\nu_{j}+\nu_{k}\partial_{T,i}\nu_{j}\Big)\Big]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\partial_{ij}^{2}\phi-\sum_{k=1}^{n}\Big[\partial^{2}_{jk}\phi\,\nu_{k}\nu_{i}+(\partial_{T,i}\partial_{k}\phi)\nu_{k}\nu_{j}+\partial_{k}\phi\Big(\partial_{T,i}\nu_{k}\nu_{j}+\nu_{k}\partial_{T,i}\nu_{j}\Big)\Big]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\partial_{ij}^{2}\phi-\sum_{k=1}^{n}\Big[\partial^{2}_{jk}\phi\,\nu_{k}\nu_{i}+\partial_{ik}^{2}\phi\,\nu_{k}\nu_{j}+\partial_{k}\phi\Big(\partial_{T,i}\nu_{k}\nu_{j}+\nu_{k}\partial_{T,i}\nu_{j}\Big)\Big]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\sum_{k=1}^{n}\partial_{k}\phi\Big(\partial_{T,i}\nu_{k}\nu_{j}\Big)+{\mathcal{S}},

where
{\mathcal{S}}
denotes a quantity
which is symmetric in the indexes
i
and
j
.

As a result,

(A.5)

\partial_{T,i}\partial_{T,j}\phi-\partial_{T,j}\partial_{T,i}\phi=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\Big[\partial_{T,j}\nu_{k}\nu_{i}-\partial_{T,i}\nu_{k}\nu_{j}\Big]\partial_{k}\phi.

\begin{split}&\sum_{k=1}^{n}\Big[\partial_{T,j}\nu_{k}\nu_{i}-\partial_{T,i}\nu_{k}\nu_{j}\Big]\big(\partial_{T,k}\phi-\partial_{k}\phi\big)=-\sum_{k=1}^{n}\Big[\partial_{T,j}\nu_{k}\nu_{i}-\partial_{T,i}\nu_{k}\nu_{j}\Big](\nabla\phi\cdot\nu)\nu_{k}=0,\end{split}

owing to (
5.19
) and (
5.28
).

By inserting this information into (
A.5
), we arrive at (
5.27
), as desired.
