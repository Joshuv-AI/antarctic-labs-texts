Abstract.
The exploration of shape metamorphism, surface reconstruction, and image interpolation raises fundamental inquiries concerning the
C^{1}
and higher-order regularity of
\infty
-harmonic potentials — a specialized category of
\infty
-harmonic functions. Additionally, it prompts questions regarding their corresponding approximations using
p
-harmonic potentials. It is worth noting that establishing
C^{1}
and higher-order regularity for
\infty
-harmonic functions remains a central concern within the realm of
\infty
-Laplace equations and
L^{\infty}
-variational problems.

In this study, we investigate the regularity properties from
p
-harmonic potentials
u_{p}
to
\infty
-harmonic potentials
u
within arbitrary convex rings
\Omega=\Omega_{0}\backslash\overline{\Omega}_{1}
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Here
\Omega_{0}
is a bounded convex domain in
\mathbb{R}^{n}

and
\overline{\Omega}_{1}\subset\Omega_{0}
is a compact convex set. Our main results can be summarized as follows:

•

We establish interior
C^{1}
regularity for
\infty
-harmonic potentials, providing their
C^{1}
-approximation by
p
-harmonic potentials; it answers an open problem by Lindgren and Lindqvist
[
43
,
44
]
. We also prove the existence of streamlines.

•

For any real value of
\alpha
, we show that
|Du|^{\alpha}
belongs to
W^{1,2}_{\mathop{\mathrm{}}{\,loc\,}}(\Omega)
. Furthermore, we prove weak convergence of
D|Du_{p}|^{\alpha}
to
D|Du|^{\alpha}
in
L^{2}_{{\mathop{\mathrm{}}{\,loc\,}}}(\Omega)
as
p\to\infty
, along with
D|Du|^{\alpha}\cdot\frac{Du}{|Du|}=0
almost everywhere.

•

In the degenerate case where
\overline{\Omega}_{1}
reduces to a single point, we establish the following equivalence

\Omega_{0}=B(x_{0},r)
for some
r>0

\Leftrightarrow

u\in C^{2}(\Omega)

\Leftrightarrow

u
is concave.

•

We demonstrate that the distributional second-order derivatives
\mathcal{D}^{2}u
are Radon measures with suitable upper bounds. Convergence of
D^{2}u_{p}
to
\mathcal{D}^{2}u
weakly in a measure-theoretic sense is also established.

These results extend some known findings
[
39
,
43
,
44
,
56
,
58
]
in two dimensions. Moreover, in planar convex rings, we prove that

\infty
-harmonic potentials are twice differentiable almost everywhere, providing optimal results in this context. The second-order derivatives contribute to the absolutely continuous part of
\mathcal{D}^{2}u
, enabling

u(x)=\frac{1}{2}\left(\max_{\overline{B(x,\epsilon)}}u+\min_{\overline{B(x,\epsilon)}}u\right)+o(\epsilon^{2})\hskip 9.24994pt\mbox{ for almost all $x\in\Omega$ as $\epsilon\to 0$}.

Introduction

The
\infty
-Laplace equation, denoted as

\Delta_{\infty}v:=D^{2}vDv\cdot Dv=0\quad\mbox{in a domain $U\subset{{{\mathbb{R}}}^{n}}$ with $n\geq 2$,}

is a nonlinear, highly degenerate second-order elliptic equation, particularly, one that is not in divergence form.
In this context, we will be working with its viscosity solutions as defined by Crandall-Ishii-Lions in their work
[
20
]
.
These solutions are commonly referred to as
\infty
-harmonic functions in the domain
U
, and you can find the specific definitions in Section
2
of this text.

The concept of
\infty
-harmonic functions originated with Aronsson in the 1960s
[
2
,
3
,
4
]
,
as he was investigating the Euler-Lagrange equation for the absolute minimization of the

L^{\infty}
-functional defined as

F(v,U)=\sup_{U}|Dv|^{2}
.
A function
v\in W^{1,\infty}_{\mathop{\mathrm{}}{\,loc\,}}(U)
is considered as an absolute minimizer if the following condition holds:

F(v,V)\leq F(w,V)\ \mbox{whenever $V\Subset U$ and $w\in C^{0}(\overline{V})\cap W^{1,\infty}(V)$ with $w|_{\partial V}=v|_{\partial V}$}.

The existence of absolute minimizers is discussed in Aronsson’s work
[
6
]
.
Jensen’s research
[
36
]
provided a crucial link between
\infty
-harmonic functions and absolute minimizers. He also established their uniqueness. For alternative approaches to prove uniqueness, you can refer to the works of
Barles-Busca
[
11
]
, Crandall et al
[
19
]
, Peres et al
[
53
]

and Armstrong-Smart
[
8
]
.

The
\infty
-Laplace equation is not only a crucial topic in mathematics but also finds applications in a wide range of fields, including shape metamorphism, surface reconstruction, image processing, computer vision, tug-of-war games, Lipschitz learning, and more. For specific references, you can consult works such as
[
18
,
21
,
22
,
24
,
26
,
50
,
53
]
.

In shape metamorphism and surface reconstruction, the task of finding suitable reconstruction functions is of utmost importance and has diverse applications across scientific disciplines. According to the “equal importance criteria” introduced by Cong-Parvin
[
23
,
24
]
, these reconstruction functions are solutions to the Dirichlet problem:

\Delta_{\infty}u=0

in
\Omega
;

u=0
on
\partial\Omega_{0}
and
u=1
on
\partial\overline{\Omega}_{1}
.

(1.1)

Here,
\Omega=\Omega_{0}\setminus\overline{\Omega}_{1}
, with
\Omega_{0}
being a bounded domain in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and
\Omega_{1}
being a subdomain with its closure
\overline{\Omega}_{1}
residing within
\Omega_{0}
. In some cases,
\overline{\Omega}_{1}
may reduce to a compact connected subset of
\Omega_{0}
without an interior. Such a set
\Omega
is commonly referred to as a ring domain.
Additionally, in the context of the “interpolation algorithm via propagation” devised by Casas-Torres
[
26
]
for image processing, interpolation functions are also required to solve the Dirichlet problem (
1.1
). For more precise details, please refer to Section
1.3
of the relevant literature.

Jensen’s work
[
36
]
provides a significant insight into problem (
1.1
). According to Jensen, this problem has a unique viscosity solution, commonly referred to as the
\infty
-harmonic potential in
\Omega
. Throughout this paper, we will denote this unique solution as
u
.
Notably,
u
serves as an
\infty
-harmonic function within the domain
\Omega
and adheres to the Dirichlet boundary values
u=0
on
\partial\overline{\Omega}_{0}
and
u=1
on
\partial\overline{\Omega}_{1}
. Since the Dirichlet boundary values are Lipschitz continuous, it follows that
u
is in
C^{0,1}(\overline{\Omega})
.

In the context of meeting the regularity requirements for reconstruction functions in shape metamorphism and surface reconstruction, as well as the regularity needed for interpolation functions in the interpolation algorithm via propagation, several questions arise regarding the regularity of
\infty
-harmonic potentials. These questions, detailed in Section
1.3
for further motivation, can be summarized as follows:

Question 1.1.
Can the
\infty
-harmonic potential
u
in
\Omega
be shown to have
C^{1}
regularity, meaning that
u\in C^{1}(\Omega)
?

Question 1.2.

Does the length of the (partial) derivative of
u
, denoted as
|Du|
, exhibit some Sobolev regularity? Specifically, does
|Du|
have zero partial derivative along the direction of
Du
within
\Omega
, as suggested by the equation?

Question 1.3.
Is it valid to assert that
u
is twice differentiable almost everywhere? Furthermore, what regularity can be attributed to the distributional second-order derivatives of
u
?

These questions delve into the regularity properties of
\infty
-harmonic potentials and their derivatives, which are crucial in various applications. Further insights into their motivations can be found in Section
1.3
.

The study of the regularity of
\infty
-harmonic potentials, especially considering Questions 1.1-1.3, has inherent significance. It not only addresses important issues but also provides insights into the potential
C^{1}
and higher-order regularity of
\infty
-harmonic functions, which is a central topic in this field. In particular,
one of the long-standing conjectures is as follows:

Conjecture:
\infty
-harmonic functions are continuously differentiable, i.e., they are
C^{1}
.

(1.2)

Notably, thanks to the
\infty
-harmonic function
x^{4/3}-y^{4/3}
discovered by Aronsson
[
6
]
, it is natural to inquire whether
\infty
-harmonic functions exhibit
C^{1,1/3}
regularity and Sobolev
W^{2,{\gamma}}
regularity with
{\gamma}<3/2
. Several significant contributions in the literature have addressed these questions, including works by Crandall-Evans
[
16
]
, Savin
[
56
]
, Evans-Savin
[
30
]
, Evans-Smart
[
31
,
32
]
, Koch wtih two of the authors
[
39
]
, and Dong and the authors
[
27
]
, among others.

Specifically, in two dimensions, Savin
[
56
]
proved that
\infty
-harmonic functions are
C^{1}
, thus confirming the conjecture; see also
[
60
]
for a simpification of the original proof via capacity. Evans-Savin
[
30
]
demonstrated that
\infty
-harmonic functions have
C^{1,{\gamma}}
regularity for some
0<{\gamma}<1/3
. Furthermore, in
[
39
]
and
[
27
]
, it was shown that, for any
\alpha>0
,
|Dv|^{\alpha}
belongs to
W^{1,2}_{\text{loc}}
,
D|Dv|^{\alpha}\cdot Dv=0
almost everywhere, and additionally,
-\det D[|Dv|^{\alpha}Dv]
is a nonnegative Radon measure.

In dimensions equal to or greater than three, Evans-Smart
[
31
,
32
]
established that
\infty
-harmonic functions are everywhere differentiable. These contributions collectively advance our understanding of the regularity properties of
\infty
-harmonic functions and their derivatives.

The established regularity results for
\infty
-harmonic functions naturally extend to
\infty
-harmonic potentials. Additionally, in the case of two-dimensional space, when
\overline{\Omega}_{1}
reduces to a single point within
\Omega_{0}
, further results have been obtained:
By Savin-Wang-Yu
[
58
, Corollary 1.2]
, it has been shown that
u
belongs to
C^{2}(\Omega)
if and only if
\Omega
is of the form
\Omega=B(x_{0},r)
for some
r>0
.
Also in the work of Lindgren-Lindqvist
[
43
, Theorem 4]
, it was demonstrated that
u
does not belong to
C^{1,1}_{\text{loc}}(\Omega)
if
\Omega_{0}
is not a disk.
By Brustad
[
10
]
, the
\infty
-harmonic potential in
[-1,1]^{2}\setminus\{0\}
is not everywhere twice differentiable.
These findings provide valuable insights into the regularity of
\infty
-harmonic potentials in specific scenarios.

On the other hand, an interesting aspect of
\infty
-harmonic functions is their approximation in
C^{0,\gamma}
and weakly in
W^{1,q}_{\text{loc}}
by
p
-harmonic functions that share the same Dirichlet boundary value. This approximation property has been explored by Bhattacharya-DiBenedetto-Manfredi
[
12
]
, as well as in the works of
[
37
,
46
]
.

A function
v\in W^{1,p}(U)
is termed
p
-harmonic if it serves as a weak solution to the
p
-Laplace equation:

\Delta_{p}v:={\rm div}(|Dv|^{p-2}Dv)=0\quad\mbox{in $U$.}

In the quest for achieving
C^{1}
-regularity or higher-order regularity for
\infty
-harmonic functions (and, in particular, addressing Conjecture (
1.2
)), a natural idea is to establish
C^{1}
-regularity or higher-order regularity for
p
-harmonic functions uniformly across all large values of
p
. This would effectively approximate
\infty
-harmonic functions with
p
-harmonic functions. However, this endeavor presents significant challenges. While
p
-harmonic functions have been shown to possess
C^{1,\alpha}
regularity and some higher-order regularity, these results are heavily dependent on the specific value of
p
and are not uniform for all large values of
p
. (References:
[
33
,
35
,
47
,
48
,
59
]
, among others).

Moreover, in two-dimensional space (
n=2
), although Savin
[
56
]
has demonstrated that
\infty
-harmonic functions are
C^{1}
, the problem of approximating
\infty
-harmonic functions
v
in
C^{1}
by
p
-harmonic functions
v_{p}
with the same Dirichlet boundary value, or equivalently, showing that
v_{p}
has
C^{1}
-regularity uniformly across all large values of
p
, remains a challenging open question. The most recent developments, as presented in
[
44
,
27
]
, aided by
[
39
]
, have shown that for any
\alpha>0
,
|Dv_{p}|^{\alpha}
converges to
|Dv|^{\alpha}
in
L^{2}_{\text{loc}}
, and
\det D[|Dv_{p}|^{\alpha}Dv_{p}]
weakly converges to
\det D[|Dv|^{\alpha}Dv]
in the sense of measures. These findings represent significant progress toward understanding the regularity of
\infty
-harmonic functions and their approximation by
p
-harmonic functions.

To initiate our exploration, we seek to gain insights into the
C^{1}
and higher-order approximation questions for a specific class of
\infty
-harmonic functions, such as the
\infty
-harmonic potential
u
within
\Omega
. For this purpose, let us consider
2<p<\infty
and introduce
u_{p}
as a
p
-harmonic potential within
\Omega
.
u_{p}
is defined as follows:

u_{p}
is a unique
p
-harmonic function in
\Omega
with Dirichlet boundary values
u_{p}=i
on
\partial\Omega_{i}
. In other words, it is the unique weak solution to the Dirichlet problem:

\Delta_{p}u=0

in
\Omega
;

u_{p}=1
on
\partial\Omega_{1}
and
u_{p}=0
on
\partial\Omega_{0}
.

(1.3)

Based on the findings of
[
12
]
, we know that
u_{p}
converges to
u
in
C^{0,\gamma}(\overline{\Omega})
with
\gamma\in(0,1)
.

In the context of the previous discussions and the motivation provided, we can now address the following inquiries related to the approximation by
p
-harmonic functions.

Question 1.1A.
Is it accurate to assert that
u_{p}
converges to
u
in
C^{1}(\Omega)
? Equivalently, does
u_{p}
belong to
C^{1}(\Omega)
uniformly for all values of
p
?

Question 1.2A.
Does
|Du_{p}|
exhibit Sobolev regularity that is uniform across different values of
p
? Alternatively, does
|Du_{p}|
converge to
|Du|
within a certain Sobolev space?

Question 1.3A.
Can we establish that
u_{p}
converges to
u
in specific function spaces with second-order regularity? Moreover, what kind of regularity does the second-order derivative
D^{2}u_{p}
possess, and is this regularity uniform across a range of values for
p>2
?

These questions aim to explore the regularity properties of
p
-harmonic functions as they approximate
\infty
-harmonic functions, providing insights into the convergence and regularity behavior in the context of different values of
p
.

In the two-dimensional space, i.e.
n=2
, some progress has been made toward addressing Questions 1.2A and 1.3A as in
[
44
,
27
]
.
Furthermore, in the specific case where both
\Omega_{0}
and

are convex, Lindgren-Lindqvist
[
43
,
44
]
have made significant strides. They have demonstrated that
|Du_{p}|
converges to
|Du|
locally uniformly. However, the question of whether
Du_{p}
converges to
Du
locally and uniformly remains an open question, referred to as Question 1A. These developments represent substantial advancements in our understanding of the regularity and convergence behavior of

-harmonic functions with respect to
\infty
-harmonic functions in two dimensions.

1.1
Main results: regularity and approximation in convex rings

The primary objective of this paper is to offer partial answers to Questions 1.1-1.3 by addressing, in part, Questions 1.1A-1.3A within the context of arbitrary convex ring domains in dimensions
n\geq 2
.

We say

\Omega
is a convex ring in
{{{\mathbb{R}}}^{n}}
with
n\geq 2
if

\Omega=\Omega_{0}\setminus\overline{\Omega}_{1}
is a ring domain, and both of
\Omega_{0}
and
\overline{\Omega}_{1}
are convex. Here
\overline{\Omega}_{1}
is a compact subset of
\Omega_{0}
.
Denote by
u
the
\infty
-harmonic potential in
\Omega
, and for
p\in(2,\infty)
, by
u_{p}
the
p
-harmonic potential in
\Omega
.
We extend
u
and
u_{p}
continuously to whole
\Omega_{0}
by setting
u=u_{p}=1
in
\overline{\Omega}_{1}
.

Our initial result is a comprehensive answer to Question 1.1 and Question 1A.

Theorem 1.1
.

We have
u\in C^{1}(\Omega)
and
|Du|\neq 0
in
\Omega
.
Moreover
u_{p}\to u
in
C^{1}(\Omega)
as
p\to\infty
.

Consequently, for each
t\in(0,1)
the level set
\{x\in\Omega|u(x)=t\}
is a
C^{1}
-regular

(n-1)
-dimensional surface.

In dimension
n=2
,
u\in C^{1}(\Omega)
can be attributed to the remarkable result on the
C^{1}
regularity of
\infty
-harmonic functions established by Savin
[
56
]
.
Notably, Theorem
1.1
provides a significant outcome, demonstrating that
Du_{p}\to Du
uniformly in each compact subset of
\Omega
. This result effectively resolves the previously mentioned open question raised by Lindgren-Lindqvist
[
43
,
44
]
. It’s worth noting that one should not expect the uniform convergence of

Du_{p}\to Du
accross entire domain
\Omega
, as witted by the example in
[
44
]
: In
\Omega=B(0,1)\setminus\{0\}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
,
the
\infty
-harmonic potential
u(x)=1-|x|
and the
p
-harmonic potential

u_{p}(x)=1-|x|^{\frac{p-2}{p-1}}
with
p>2
. Obviously, the convergence
Du_{p}\to Du

is not uniform in
\Omega
.

Theorem
1.1
allows us to establish the following existence of trajectory (streamline).

Corollary 1.2
.

Given a point
x\in\Omega
,
there is a curve
{\gamma}_{x}\in C^{0,1}([0,T_{x}];\overline{\Omega})\cap C^{1}([0,T_{x});\Omega)
for some
T_{x}\in(0,\infty)
as a trajectory (streamline) of
u
starting from
x
and terminating at
\partial\Omega_{1}
, that is,

\displaystyle\frac{d{\gamma}_{x}(t)}{dt}=Du({\gamma}_{x}(t))\quad\forall t\in[0,T_{x})\quad\mbox{and }\ \quad{\gamma}_{x}(0)=x,\quad{\gamma}_{x}(T_{x})\in\partial\Omega_{1}.

(1.4)

Moreover, the function
u\circ{\gamma}_{x}
is convex and its speed
|Du\circ{\gamma}_{x}|
is nondecreasing.

In two-dimensional space, Lindgren-Lindqvist’s work, as presented in
[
43
,
44
]
, not only established the existence of streamlines, as demonstrated in Corollary
1.2
, but also demonstrated their uniqueness. However, in higher dimensions
n\geq 3
, the question of the uniqueness of
{\gamma}_{x}
remains unclear and requires further investigation.

Next, we give partial answers to Question 1.2 and Question 1.2A as below.
Here and below, by abuse of notation, we use
|Du|^{\alpha}
with
\alpha=0
to denote
\ln|Du|
.

Theorem 1.3
.

For any
\alpha\in{\mathbb{R}}
, one has
|Du|^{\alpha}\in W^{1,2}_{\mathop{\mathrm{}}{\,loc\,}}(\Omega)
,

D|Du|^{\alpha}\cdot\frac{Du}{|Du|}=0
almost everywhere in
\Omega
,
and
|Du_{p}|^{\alpha}\to|Du|^{\alpha}
weakly in
W^{1,2}_{\mathop{\mathrm{}}{\,loc\,}}(\Omega)
as
p\to\infty
.

In dimension
n=2
, Theorem
1.3
is essentially
a consequence of
[
39
,
44
]
. Indeed,
when
\alpha>0
, it is a direct consequence of

[
39
,
44
]
. The case where
\alpha\leq 0
then
follows from Theorem
1.1
as
|Du|\neq 0
.

Finally, we concentrate on Question 1.3 and Question 1.3A.
We begin with the following equivalence in the degenerate case.

Theorem 1.4
.

Suppose that
\Omega_{1}=\{x_{0}\}
for some
x_{0}\in\Omega_{0}
.
The following are equivalent:

(i)

\Omega=B(x_{0},r)\setminus\{x_{0}\}
for some
r>0
.

(ii)

u\in C^{2}(\Omega)
.

(iii)

u
is concave.

In dimension
n=2
, (i)
\Leftrightarrow
(ii) was already proved by
Savin-Wang-Yu
[
58
, Corollary 1.2]
, where they do not require
the convexity of
\Omega_{0}
.
In dimension
n\geq 3
, it is unclear to us whether the convexity assumption of
\Omega_{0}

in Theorem
1.4
can be removed.

In a general convex ring domain
\Omega
, we establish the following regularity for distributional second order derivatives in a
measure sense, which
contributes to Question 1.3 and Question 1.3A. The distributional derivative

\mathcal{D}w=(\mathcal{D}_{i}u)_{i=1}^{n}
in the sense of distribution of a function
w\in L^{1}_{\mathop{\mathrm{}}{\,loc\,}}(\Omega)
is defined via integration by parts, that is,

\int_{\Omega}\mathcal{D}_{i}w(x)\phi(x)\,dx=-\int_{\Omega}w(x)\phi_{x_{i}}(x)\,dx\quad\forall\phi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\Omega).

Theorem 1.5
.

(i)
The distributional derivatives
\mathcal{D}^{2}u
are Radon measures satisfying

\int_{\Omega}\langle\mathcal{D}^{2}uDu,Du\rangle\xi\,dx=0\quad\forall\xi\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\Omega).

Moreover,
Du\in BV_{\mathop{\mathrm{}}{\,loc\,}}(\Omega)
, that is, the distributional derivatives
\mathcal{D}(Du)
are Radon measures in
\Omega
, and
\mathcal{D}(Du)=\mathcal{D}^{2}u
.

(ii)
D^{2}u_{p}\in L^{1}_{\mathop{\mathrm{}}{\,loc\,}}(\Omega)
uniformly in
p\in[4,\infty)

and
D^{2}u_{p}\to\mathcal{D}^{2}u
weakly in the sense of measure, and

Du_{p}\to Du
weakly in
BV_{\mathop{\mathrm{}}{\,loc\,}}(\Omega)
.

Additionally, when
n=2
, we are able to prove the following almost everywhere twice differentiability of
u
, which also contributes to
Question 1.3 and Question 1.3A.

Theorem 1.6
.

In dimension
n=2
,
u
is twice differentiable and

Du
are differentiable almost everywhere in
\Omega
.
The absolutely continuous part of the measure
\mathcal{D}^{2}u
with respect to Lebesgue measure
dx
is given by
D^{2}u\,dx
.

Consequently, for almost all
x\in\Omega
one has

-D^{2}uDu\cdot Du=0
,

\displaystyle u(x)=\frac{1}{2}\left[u\left(x+hDu(x)\right)+u\left(x-hDu(x)\right)\right]+o(|h|^{2})\quad\mbox{as $h\to 0$,}

(1.5)

and

\displaystyle u(x)=\frac{1}{2}\left(\max_{\overline{B(x,\epsilon)}}u+\min_{\overline{B(x,\epsilon)}}u\right)+o(\epsilon^{2})\quad\mbox{
as $\epsilon\to 0$.}

(1.6)

The above almost everywhere twice differentiability of
u
is optimal; indeed,
by Brustad
[
10
]
, the
\infty
-harmonic potential in
[-1,1]^{2}\setminus\{0\}
is not everywhere twice differentiable.

The concept of an
\infty
-harmonic function in a given domain
U\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
is closely related to the satisfaction of equation (
1.6
). This equivalence is detailed in the work of Parviainen-Manfredi-Rossi
[
52
]
.
However, it’s important to note that, in general, an
\infty
-harmonic function

does not necessarily satisfy equation (
1.6
) pointwise. This point is discussed in
[
52
]
as well.

The significance of Theorem
1.6
lies in its assertion that
\infty
-harmonic potentials within planar convex rings satisfy equation (
1.6
) almost everywhere. This result is notable because equation (
1.6
) was initially utilized by Oberman
[
50
]
for constructing numerical approximations of
\infty
-harmonic functions. Additionally, it has roots in the Dynamic Programming Principle as described in
[
9
,
53
]
. This highlights the practical utility and theoretical foundations of equation (
1.6
) in various mathematical and computational contexts.

Key ideas to prove main results

We are ready to sketch the ideas to our main results.
The quasi-concavity of
u_{p}
(that is convexity of super level sets of

[

play a crucial role. Since
u_{p}\to u
in
C^{0}(\Omega)
,
the quasi-concavity of

see Section

1.3
Equal importance criteria, interpolation by propagation,
\infty
-harmonic potential and approximation by
p
-harmonic potentials

The need to reconstruct intermediate shapes that gradually transition from a given source shape to a desired target shape is a fundamental requirement in a variety of scientific fields. This process forms a critical component in the study of shape metamorphism, surface reconstruction, image interpolation, and other related areas. Relevant references include
[
18
,
21
,
22
,
23
,
24
,
51
,
57
]
, among others.

To be more specific, consider two distinct compact
(n-1)
-dimensional surfaces, denoted as
S_{0}
and
S_{1}
.
The objective is to construct a family
\{S(t)\}_{s\in[0,1]}
of
(n-1)
-dimensional surfaces so that
S(0)=S_{0}
,
S(1)=S_{1}
, and
S(t)
maintains continuity for
t\in[0,1]
.
Additionally, for each
t\in(0,1)
,
S(t)
is expected to exhibit a certain level of smoothness, and the entire family
\{S_{t}\}_{t\in[0,1]}
is intended to span the metamorphism region

\overline{\Omega}=(\overline{\Omega}_{0}\setminus\Omega_{1})\cup(\overline{\Omega}_{1}\setminus\Omega_{0})
, where
\Omega_{i}
is the domain enclosed by
S_{i}
for
i=0,1
. This process is crucial for a wide range of applications, facilitating the smooth transition between different shapes while ensuring continuity and smoothness in the intermediate shapes.

The goal is to represent the desired intermediate surfaces using a reconstruction and interpolation function

u:\overline{\Omega}\to{\mathbb{R}}
satisfying
\{x\in\overline{\Omega}|u(x)=i\}=S_{i}
for
i=0,1
and hence
the Dirichlet boundary condition

u=1
on
S_{1}=\partial\Omega_{1}
and
u=0
on
S_{0}=\partial\Omega_{0}
,

(1.13)

where it is assumed that,

S_{0}\cap S_{1}=\emptyset
and
\Omega\neq\emptyset
, that is, either
\overline{\Omega}_{0}\subset\Omega_{1}
or
\overline{\Omega}_{1}\subset\Omega_{0}
.

(1.14)

Without loss of generality, we can assume that
\overline{\Omega}_{1}\subset\Omega_{0}
.

For any reconstruction or interpolation function
u
that meets the criteria of
u\in C^{1}(\Omega)\cap C^{0}(\overline{\Omega})
and
|Du|\neq 0
in
\Omega
, the implicit function theorem allows us to construct a collection of level surfaces:

\left\{\{x\in\overline{\Omega}|u(x)=t\}\right\}_{t\in[0,1]}.

These level surfaces provide a means to select intermediate surfaces or interpolation surfaces between the images
S_{0},S_{1}
.
One may also view

\mathcal{S}=\{(x,z)\in\overline{\Omega}\times[0,1]|u(x)=z,z\in[0,1]\}=\cup_{t\in[0,1]}S(t)\times\{t\}

as a reconstructed
n
-dimensional surface
from cross-sectional
(n-1)
-dimensional surfaces
S_{0}
and
S_{1}
, that is,

\mathcal{S}\cap{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\times\{i\}=S_{i}
for
i=0,1
. This approach allows for the smooth construction of intermediate surfaces based on the given boundary conditions and the choice of the interpolation function.

The generation of suitable reconstruction functions and the creation of intermediate shapes that fulfill various requirements are fundamental challenges in diverse scientific fields. The literature, as found in references such as
[
22
,
21
,
24
,
50
]
, has introduced several critical constraint principles for the reconstruction process. Correspondingly, various numerical methods have been developed and widely applied.

It’s important to note that different sets of constraints can lead to the study of solutions to different partial differential equations. This demonstrates the versatility and adaptability of reconstruction techniques to address the specific needs of different scientific domains.

In the absence of any information about the reconstruction process,
we expect that
every point in
\Omega
is equally important and contributes similarly to
the reconstruction process, and that any other assumption means that we know something about reconstruction functions. This natural constraint is now known as
equal importance criteria
, which was originally introduced by
[
18
,
22
,
24
]
in the study of shape metamorphism and surface reconstruction.
It was further formulated therein by requiring that the change of the reconstruction function
u
in the gradient-magnitude along direction is
0
, that is,

\displaystyle D|Du|\cdot\frac{Du}{|Du|}=0\quad\mbox{in $\Omega$}.

(1.15)

This implies, along each trajectory of the gradient of
u
, the magnitude of the gradient is a constant;
in another words, the height of
u
decrease linearly from 1 to 0,
and the level surfaces of
u

are equally distributed along the gradient.
Together with Dirichlet boundary condition, the
equal importance criteria
leads to the problem:

Find a function
u:\overline{\Omega}\to{\mathbb{R}}
to solve (
1.15
) with Dirichlet boundary condition (
1.13
).

(1.16)

We expect that a solution
u
to (
1.16
) should enjoy a nice regularity that
u\in C^{0}(\overline{\Omega})\cap C^{1}(\Omega)
,
Du\neq 0
and
|Du|
has partial derivative
0
along the direction
Du
.

Moreover, to find a suitable interpolation function as required by image process,
a remarkable
interpolation algorithm via propagation
was introduced by Casas and Torres
[
26
]
; see also Caselles-Morel-Sbert
[
22
]
. Suppose that
u
is the corresponding interpolation function obtained by this algorithm.
If

is
C^{2}
(even twice differentiable) at
x
, the propagation here requires
that

\displaystyle u(x)=\frac{1}{2}\left[u(x+hDu(x))-u(x-hDu(x))\right]+o(h^{2}).

(1.17)

By Taylor expansion, letting

h\to 0
one gets

\displaystyle\Delta_{\infty}u(x):=D^{2}u(x)Du(x)\cdot Du(x)=0.

(1.18)

This enables us to consider the problem:

Find a function
u:\overline{\Omega}\to{\mathbb{R}}
to solve (
1.18
) in
\Omega
with Dirichlet boundary condition (
1.13
).

(1.19)

Note the above algorithm does not yield a
C^{2}
interpolation function
necessarily, even which
is generally desirable in numerical analysis.
One may ask whether interpolation functions or solutions to (
1.19
) are twice differentiable almost everywhere so that (
1.17
) and (
1.18

Therefore, the problem (

The regularity required by reconstruction functions in the problem (

Our finding in Section

Since Jensen

2
Basic properties of
\infty
-harmonic functions

In this section we recall the definition of
\infty
-harmonic functions and also several useful properties. Let
U
be an arbitrary bounded domain of
{{{\mathbb{R}}}^{n}}
.

Definition 2.1
.

A function
v\in C^{0}(U)
is a viscosity subsolution to

\displaystyle-\Delta_{\infty}w=0\quad{\rm in}\quad U

(2.1)

if
at any
x_{0}\in U
, for any
\phi\in C^{2}(U)
satisfying

v(x_{0})-\phi(x_{0})\geq v(x)-\phi(x)\quad\forall x\in B(x_{0},r)\Subset U\ \mbox{for some $r>0$},

one has

-\Delta_{\infty}\phi(x_{0})\leq 0.

A function
v\in C^{0}(U)
is a viscosity supersolution to
(
2.1
) if

-v
is a viscosity supersolution of (
2.1
).

A function
v\in C^{0}(U)
is
a viscosity solution to (
2.1
) if it is both a viscosity subsolution and a viscosity
supersolution to (
2.1
).

Viscosity solutions to (
2.1
) are called
\infty
-harmonic functions in
U
.

The following property is well-known; see for example Crandall
[
15
]
.

Theorem 2.2
.

If
v\in C^{0,1}(\overline{U})
is
\infty
-harmonic in
U
,
then
{\mathop{\mathrm{}}{\,Lip}}(v,U)={\mathop{\mathrm{}}{\,Lip}}(v,\partial U)
.

Jensen
[
36
]
established a comparison principle.

Theorem 2.3
.

If
v_{1}\in C^{0,1}(\overline{U})
is viscosity subsolution to (
2.1
) in
U
and
v_{2}\in C^{0,1}(\overline{U})
is viscosity supersolution to (
2.1
) in
U
,
then

\max_{\overline{U}}[v_{1}-v_{2}]=\max_{\partial U}[v_{1}-v_{2}].

The comparison with cones was introduced by
Crandall-Evans-Gariepy

[
17
]
.

Definition 2.4
.

A function
v\in C^{0}(U)
enjoys comparison with cones
from above in
U
if for any
V\Subset U
,
x_{0}\in U
, and

b\geq 0
, it holds that

v(x)\leq v(x_{0})+b|x-x_{0}|\quad\forall x\in V

whenever

v(x)\leq v(x_{0})+b|x-x_{0}|\quad\forall x\in\partial(V\backslash\{x_{0}\}).

A function
v\in C^{0}(U)
enjoys comparison with cones from below in
U
if

-v
enjoys comparison with cones from
above in
U
.

A function
v\in C^{0}(U)

enjoys comparison with cones in
U
if it enjoys both comparison with cones from below and from above.

A crucial fact for
\infty
-harmonic functions is that they can be
characterised through comparison with cones; see Crandall-Evans-Gariepy

[
17
]
.

Theorem 2.5
.

Let
v\in C^{0}(U)
.
Then
v
is a subsolution to (
2.1
) in
U
if and only if
v
satisfies comparison with cones from above in
U
.

Consequently,
v
is
\infty
-harmonic if and only if
v
satisfies comparison with cones.

For
x\in U
and
0<r<{\mathop{\mathrm{}}{\,dist\,}}(x,\partial U)
, define the slope functions

S^{+}_{r}(v,x):=\max_{y\in\partial(B(x,r)\cap U)\backslash\{x\}}\frac{v(y)-v(x)}{r}

and

S^{-}_{r}(v,x):=\max_{y\in\partial(B(x,r)\cap U)\backslash\{x\}}\frac{v(x)-v(y)}{r}.

Denote by
{\mathop{\mathrm{}}{\,Lip}}\,v(x)
the pointwise Lipshictz constant at
x
, that is,

{\mathop{\mathrm{}}{\,Lip}}\,v(x):=\limsup_{r\to 0}\sup_{|x-y|\leq r}\frac{|u(y)-u(x)|}{r}.

Crandall-Evans-Gariepy

[
17
]
obtained the monotonicity of
the slope functions.

Lemma 2.6
.

Let
v
be upper semi-continuous in
U
and enjoy comparison with cones from above.
Then for any
x\in U
,
S^{+}_{r}(v,x)
is nondecreasing in
r\in(0,{\mathop{\mathrm{}}{\,dist\,}}(x,\partial U))
.
Moreover,

\lim_{r\to 0}S^{+}_{r}(v,x)={\mathop{\mathrm{}}{\,Lip}}\,v(x)\quad\mbox{for all $x\in U$.}

As a consequence of (
2.5
),
the following strong maximum principle can be found in
Crandall
[
15
, Section 4]
.

Corollary 2.7
.

Let
v
be an
\infty
-harmonic
function in a domain
U
.
If
v(x)=\max_{\overline{B(x,r)}}v
for some ball

, then

is a constant on

.

Consequently, if

attains its maximum at any interior point of

then it must be a constant.

Note that

-harmonic functions satisfies a similar strong maximum principle; see Lindqvist
[

]
.

Evans-Smart
[

,
32
]
proved the
everywhere differentiability of

\infty
-harmonic functions.

Theorem 2.8
.

If

is an
\infty
-harmonic function in a domain

, then

is everywhere differentiable in

{\mathop{\mathrm{}}{\,Lip}}\,v(x)=|Dv(x)|
for all

.

In this paper, unless other specify, we always assume that

\Omega_{0}
is bounded convex domain and
\overline{\Omega}_{1}

.
We always denote by

-harmonic potential in

Naturally, one extends

to the whole domain

by setting

{{{\mathbb{R}}}^{n}}\setminus\Omega_{0}
.

For each

Naturally, one extends

to the whole domain
{{{\mathbb{R}}}^{n}}

\mbox{$u_{p}=1$ in $\overline{\Omega}_{1}$ and $u_{p}=0$ in ${{{\mathbb{R}}}^{n}}\setminus\Omega_{0}$}.

Let us review some result of

due to Lewis
[

.
A function

is called quasi-concave in
{{{\mathbb{R}}}^{n}}
if for each

the super level set
\{x\in{{{\mathbb{R}}}^{n}}:f(x)>t\}
is convex whenever it is not empty set, or equivalently,

f(\lambda x+(1-\lambda)y)\geq\min\{f(x),f(y)\}\quad\forall\lambda\in[0,1],x,y\in{{{\mathbb{R}}}^{n}}.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
p\in[2,\infty)
. It holds that
u_{p}\in C^{\infty}(\Omega)
with
Du_{p}\neq 0
in
\Omega
,

u_{p}
is quasi-concave in
{{{\mathbb{R}}}^{n}}
, and

-\Delta u_{p}>0
in
\Omega
.

Consequently, we have the following.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
p\in[2,\infty)
. It holds that

u_{p}
is a viscosity subsolution to (

) in
\Omega
,
and hence
u_{p}
enjoys the comparison with
cones from above in
\Omega
. Moreover,

in

.

Proof.

Using

-\Delta_{p}u_{p}=0
and
\Delta u_{p}<0
in
\Omega
for all

, we have

-\Delta_{\infty}u_{p}=\frac{1}{p-2}\Delta u_{p}|Du_{p}|^{2}\leq 0\quad{\rm in}\quad\Omega.

This then guarantees that
u_{p}
enjoys the comparison with
cones from above. By the comparison principle, we have

in
\Omega.

∎

Observe that

in
\Omega
via the strong maximum principle, and therefore,

\Omega_{0}=\{x\in\overline{\Omega}_{0}|u_{p}(x)>0\}.

For each

, we write

and the level set

Note that

.
As a consequence of Lemma

, one has the following.

.

For each

.
If

.

The following was well-known; see
[

]
,
[

]
and
[

.

Lemma 3.4
.

As

, one has

in

and

weakly in

for any

and

.

Moreover, it follows by the strong maximum principle that

for

, and hence

\Omega_{0}=\{x\in\Omega_{0}|u(x)>0\}
and
\partial\Omega_{0}=\{x\in\overline{\Omega}_{0}|u(x)=0\}
.

For each

, we write
the super level set

\Omega_{t}:=\{x\in\Omega_{0}:u(x)>t\},

and the level set

S_{t}:=\{x\in\Omega_{0}:u(x)=t\}.

Via the fact that

in

as

and the strong maximum principle, we obtain the following property; see also

[
43
,
44
]
.
For reader’s convenience, we give details of its proof.

Lemma 3.5
.

For each
t\in[0,1)
,

\Omega_{t}
is convex, and moreover,
its boundary
\partial\Omega_{t}=S_{t}
. If
t\in(0,1)
, then

.

Proof.

Let
t\in(0,1)
.
By the continuity of
u
,
\Omega_{t}
is a non-empty domain (connected open subset of
\Omega_{0}
).
Since
u_{p}\to u
in
C^{0}(\overline{\Omega}_{0})
, the convexity of
the super level set of

yields the convexity of
\Omega_{t}
.
By

and

in
\Omega
,
we know that

\partial\Omega_{t}\subset S_{t}
and
\Omega_{t}\subset\overline{\Omega}_{t}\subset\Omega_{s}

Below we show that
S_{t}\subset\partial\Omega_{t}

by contradiction. Assume that

.
Write
E_{t}:=\{x\in\Omega_{0},u(x)\geq t\}
.
Noting that
\overline{\Omega}_{t}=\Omega_{t}\cup\partial\Omega_{t}
, we have

E_{t}\setminus\overline{\Omega}_{t}=S_{t}\setminus\partial\Omega_{t}\neq\emptyset.

Moreover, the convexity of
\Omega_{t}
yields the convexity of

\overline{\Omega}_{t}
. Similarly, the convexity of
\Omega_{t}
and continuity of

further lead
to the convexity of

.
Thus
(E_{t})^{\circ}\setminus\overline{\Omega}_{t}\neq\emptyset
.
Indeed, take any

By the convexity of

, there is unique

such that

. Using a continuity of

we can find

such that for any

That is, the open set

\{\lambda x_{0}+(1-\lambda)z|\lambda\in(\lambda_{t},1),z\in\overline{\Omega}_{t}\}\subset E_{t}\setminus\overline{\Omega}_{t}.

Therefore in the domain

,

reaches its infimum

at some interior point.
However, since

is

-harmonic in

on

. This leads to a contradiction.
∎

4
A lower bound of the length of gradients

For an
\infty
-harmonic potential
u
in a convex ring
\Omega
, given any
x\in\Omega
,
denote by
N_{x}
the collection of all unit vector
\nu\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
so that

\nu\cdot(y-x)\leq 0\quad\forall y\in\Omega_{u(x)}.

Since
\Omega_{u(x)}
is convex, one has
N_{x}\neq\emptyset
.
In other words,

N_{x}
is the collection of outer normal directions
\nu
of all supporting hyperplane of
\Omega_{u(x)}
at
x
, that is,
\Omega_{u(x)}
lies in the side

\{y\in{{{\mathbb{R}}}^{n}}|(y-x)\cdot\nu<0\}
of the hyperplane
P_{\nu}=\{y\in{{{\mathbb{R}}}^{n}}|(y-x)\cdot\nu=0\}
.

We have the following lower bound of the partial derivative of
u
along
any direction in
N_{x}
.
Note that, given any unit vector
\nu
, it follows from the convexity
of
\Omega_{0}
that
there
is an unique
r_{x,\nu}\in(0,\infty)
such that

x+r_{x,\nu}\nu\in\partial\Omega_{0}
, that is,
u(x+r_{x,\nu}\nu)=0
.

(4.1)

If
\nu\in N_{x}
, one then has

\displaystyle{\mathop{\mathrm{}}{\,dist\,}}(x,\partial\Omega_{0})\leq r_{x,\nu}\leq{\mathop{\mathrm{}}{\,diam\,}}(\Omega_{0}).

(4.2)

Lemma 4.1
.

For any
x\in\Omega
, it holds

\displaystyle-Du(x)\cdot\nu\geq\frac{u(x)-u(x+r\nu_{x})}{r}>0\quad\forall\nu\in N_{x},\ r\in(0,r_{x,\nu}].

(4.3)

Moreover,

\displaystyle N_{x}=\left\{\nu_{x}:=-\frac{Du(x)}{|Du(x)|}\right\}.

(4.4)

Consequently,

\displaystyle|Du(x)|\geq\frac{u(x)-u\left(x+r\nu_{x}\right)}{r}\quad\forall r\in(0,r_{x,\nu_{x}}].

(4.5)

In particular,

\displaystyle|Du(x)|\geq\frac{u(x)}{r_{x,\nu_{x}}}.

(4.6)

Proof.

Proof of (
4.3
).

Assume that
x\in\Omega
. Given any
r\in(0,r_{x,\nu}]
, as mentioned above we have
z=x+r\nu\in\overline{\Omega}_{0}\setminus\Omega_{u(x)}
.
Then it follows from the convexity of
\Omega_{u(x)}
that
\overline{B(z,r)}\cap\overline{\Omega_{u(x)}}=\{x\}
and

B(z,r)\cap\Omega_{u(x)}=\emptyset
.
Therefore

\displaystyle\max_{\overline{B(z,r)\cap\Omega_{0}}}u=u(x),

and then

u(y)\leq u(z)+u(x)-u(z)=u(z)+\frac{u(x)-u(z)}{r}|y-z|\quad\forall y\in\partial B(z,r)\cap\overline{\Omega}_{0}.

Since
u(x)\geq u(z)
, we get

\displaystyle u(y)=0\leq u(z)+\frac{u(x)-u(z)}{r}|y-z|,\quad\forall y\in\partial\Omega_{0}.

Noting
\partial(B(z,r)\cap\Omega_{0})\subset(\partial B(z,r)\setminus\overline{\Omega}_{0})\cup\partial\Omega_{0}
, and
by applying comparison with cones from above we obtain

\displaystyle u(y)\leq u(z)+\frac{u(x)-u(z)}{r}|y-z|,\quad\forall y\in B(z,r)\cap\Omega_{0}.

Recalling
z=x+r\nu
, then by setting
y=x+t\nu

for
t\in(0,r)
one has

\displaystyle u(x+t\nu)

\displaystyle\leq u(z)+\frac{u(x)-u(z)}{r}|x+t\nu-z|

\displaystyle=u(z)+(r-t)\frac{u(x)-u(z)}{r}=u(x)-t\frac{u(x)-u(z)}{r},

that is,

\frac{u(x)-u(x+t\nu)}{t}\geq\frac{u(x)-u(x+r\nu)}{r}.

By letting
t\to 0
, it holds that

|Du(x)|\geq-Du(x)\cdot\nu\geq\frac{u(x)-u(x+r\nu)}{r}>0,

that is, (
4.3
) holds.

Proof of (
4.4
).

In order to prove
\nu_{x}\in N_{x}
, it suffices to show that

Du(x)\cdot(y-x)\geq 0
for all
y\in\Omega_{u(x)}
.
For any
y\in\Omega_{u(x)}
, via the differentiability of
u
in
\Omega
we have that

Du(x)\cdot\frac{y-x}{|y-x|}=\lim_{t\to 0^{+}}\frac{u\left(x+t\frac{y-x}{|y-x|}\right)-u(x)}{t}.

It follows by the convexity of
\Omega_{u(x)}
that

x+t\frac{y-x}{|y-x|}=(1-\frac{t}{|y-x|})x+\frac{t}{|y-x|}y\in\Omega_{u(x)}\quad\forall 0<t<|y-x|.

Thus

u(x+t\frac{y-x}{|y-x|})>u(x)\quad\mbox{and then
$Du(x)\cdot(y-x)\geq 0$.}

Next, we show that
N_{x}=\{\nu_{x}\}
by contradiction.
Assume that there exists another

\nu\in N_{x}
with
\nu\neq\nu_{x}
.
We claim that there is a unit vector
\eta
such that

\eta\cdot\nu_{x}<0
while
\eta\cdot\nu>0
.
Indeed,
\nu
and
\nu_{x}
span a 2-dimension plane
P
.
If
\nu\cdot\nu_{x}<0
we take
\eta=\nu
.
If
\nu\cdot\nu_{x}\geq 0
, denote by
\theta
the angle between
\nu
and
\nu_{x}
,
we let
\eta
be the unit vector obtained by rotating

\nu
on the other side of
\nu_{x}
with the angle
\frac{\pi}{2}-\frac{\theta}{2}
and then

\eta\cdot\nu=\cos(\frac{\pi}{2}-\frac{\theta}{2})>0
.
The angle between
\eta
and
\nu_{x}
is
\frac{\pi}{2}+\frac{\theta}{2}
while

\eta\cdot\nu_{x}=\cos(\frac{\pi}{2}+\frac{\theta}{2})<0
. This gives the claim.

Via the claim above, we conclude that

0<|Du(x)|(-\nu_{x})\cdot\eta=Du(x)\cdot\eta=\lim_{t\to 0^{+}}\frac{u(x+t\eta)-u(x)}{t},

which implies that

u(x+t\eta)>u(x)
for all small
t>0
.
Therefore for such
t>0
, one has
x+t\eta\in\Omega_{u(x)}
,
which together with the definition of
\nu
yields that

t\eta\cdot\nu=(x+t\eta-x)\cdot\nu\leq 0.

This is a contradiction to our choice of
\eta
which yields
\eta\cdot\nu>0
.
∎

Given any
x\in\Omega
,
denote by
N^{p}_{x}
the collection of all unit vector
\nu
so that

\nu\cdot(y-x)\leq 0\quad\forall y\in\Omega^{p}_{u_{p}(x)}.

Since
\Omega_{u_{p}(x)}^{p}
is convex, then
N^{p}_{x}\neq\emptyset
.
Given any
\nu\in N^{p}_{x}
, recall
r_{x,\nu}
as given in (
4.1
).
Then

x+r_{x,\nu}\nu\in\partial\Omega_{0}
, that is,
u_{p}(x+r_{x,\nu}\nu)=0
,
and moreover,
r_{x,\nu}
also satisfies (
4.2
).
As
u_{p}
enjoys comparison with cones from above (see Section
3
), the following
follows from an argument similar to the one used to prove Lemma
4.1
. We omit the details of the proof.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
p\in(2,\infty)
. For any
x\in\Omega
, it holds

\displaystyle-Du_{p}(x)\cdot\nu\geq\frac{u_{p}(x)-u_{p}(x+r\nu)}{r}>0\quad\forall\nu\in N^{p}_{x}\quad r\in(0,r_{x,\nu}].

(4.7)

Moreover,

\displaystyle N^{p}_{x}=\left\{\nu^{p}_{x}:=-\frac{Du_{p}(x)}{|Du_{p}(x)|}\right\}.

(4.8)

Consequently,

\displaystyle|Du_{p}(x)|\geq\frac{u_{p}(x)-u_{p}\left(x+r\nu\right)}{r}\quad\forall\nu\in N_{x}^{p}\quad r\in(0,r_{x,\nu}].

(4.9)

In particular,

\displaystyle|Du_{p}(x)|\geq\frac{u_{p}(x)}{r_{x,\nu_{x}^{p}}}.

(4.10)

Remark 4.3
.

Thanks to (
4.6
) and (
4.10
), one concludes

|Du(x)|\geq\frac{u(x)}{{\rm diam}(\Omega_{0})}\quad\mbox{and}\quad|Du_{p}(x)|\geq\frac{u_{p}(x)}{{\rm diam}(\Omega_{0})}\quad\forall x\in\Omega.

This lower bound of
|Du|
and
|Du_{p}|
was given by
Lindgren-Lindqvist
[
44
]
.

In this section, we finally give the following uniform lower bound and upper bound of
|Du_{p}|
.

Lemma 4.4
.

Given any ball
B(z,2r)\Subset\Omega
we have

\displaystyle\min_{\overline{B(z,r)}}|Du_{p}|\geq\frac{1}{{\rm diam}(\Omega_{0})}\min\limits_{\overline{B(z,r)}}u_{p}\quad{\rm and}\quad\max_{\overline{B(z,r)}}|Du_{p}|\leq\frac{1}{r}.

(4.11)

Moreover, there exists a fixed constant

such that

\displaystyle\min_{\overline{B(z,r)}}|Du_{p}|\geq\frac{1}{2{\rm diam}(\Omega_{0})}\min\limits_{\overline{B(z,r)}}u>0\quad\forall p>p_{z,r}.

(4.12)

Proof.

Assume that
B(z,2r)\Subset\Omega
.
Applying the inequality (

) in Lemma

we obtain

\displaystyle\min_{\overline{B(z,r)}}|Du_{p}|\geq\frac{1}{{\rm diam}(\Omega_{0})}\min\limits_{\overline{B(z,r)}}u_{p}\quad\forall p>2.

Observe that

in

and

on

.
Via

This proves (

).

On the other hand, since

enjoys the comparison with cones from above (see Section

), by Lemma

we have

|Du_{p}(x)|={\mathop{\mathrm{}}{\,Lip}}\,u_{p}(x)\leq S^{+}_{r}(u_{p},x)=\max_{|y-x|=r}\frac{u_{p}(y)-u_{p}(x)}{r}\quad\forall x\in B(z,r).

Thanks to

∎

\mu\cdot(y-x)\leq 0
as desired.

Proof of (ii)
.
Since
x_{j}\to x\in\Omega
, via Lemma
4.4

we know that
\{|Du_{p_{j}}(x_{j})|\}_{j}
is bounded uniformly
p_{j}>2
.

We first show
\limsup_{j\to\infty}|Du_{p_{j}}(x_{j})|\leq|Du(x)|
. Indeed,
since
x_{j}\to x
as
j\to\infty
,
for all sufficiently large
j
so that
\frac{1}{2}{\rm dist}(x,\partial\Omega)\leq{\rm dist}(x_{j},\partial\Omega)
.
For such
j
, given any
0<r<\frac{1}{2}{\rm dist}(x,\partial\Omega)
,
by Lemma
2.6
and Lemma
3.2
, we have

|Du_{p_{j}}(x_{j})|\leq S^{+}_{r}(u_{p_{j}},x_{j})
.
Choose
y_{j,r}\in\partial B(x_{j},r)
so that

S^{+}_{r}(u_{p_{j}},x_{j})=\max_{|y-x_{j}|=r}\frac{u_{p_{j}}(y)-u_{p_{j}}(x_{j})}{r}=\frac{u_{p_{j}}(y_{j,r})-u_{p_{j}}(x_{j})}{r}.

We then have

|Du_{p_{j}}(x_{j})|\leq\frac{u_{p_{j}}(y_{j,r})-u_{p_{j}}(x_{j})}{r}.

Noting that

r-|x_{j}-x|=|y_{j,r}-x_{j}|-|x_{j}-x|\leq|y_{j,r}-x|\leq|y_{j,r}-x_{j}|+|x_{j}-x|=r+|x_{j}-x|,

one has
\lim_{j\to\infty}|y_{j,r}-x|=r
, and hence,
all limits of
y_{j,r}
belong to
\partial B(x,r)
.
Since
u_{p_{j}}\to u
uniformly in
\overline{\Omega}
, we get

\displaystyle\limsup_{j\to\infty}|Du_{p_{j}}(x_{j})|

\displaystyle=\limsup_{j\to\infty}\frac{u_{p_{j}}(y_{j,r})-u_{p_{j}}(x_{j})}{r}\leq\sup_{y\in\partial B(x,r)}\frac{u(y)-u(x)}{r}=S^{+}_{r}(u,x).

Therefore, by Lemma
2.6
and Theorem
2.8
, one has

\limsup_{j\to\infty}|Du_{p_{j}}(x_{j})|\leq\lim_{r\to 0}S^{+}_{r}(u,x)=|Du(x)|.

On the other hand,
by (
4.9
) in Lemma
4.2
we have

|Du_{p_{j}}(x_{j})|\geq\frac{u_{p_{j}}(x_{j})-u_{p_{j}}\left(x_{j}+t\nu_{x_{j}}^{p_{j}}\right)}{t}\quad\forall j\geq 1,\quad\forall t<\frac{1}{4}{\rm dist}(x_{j},\partial\Omega_{0}).

Since
u_{p_{j}}(x_{j})\to u(x)
and
\nu_{x_{j}}^{p_{j}}\to\nu_{x}
due to (i) in this lemma,
letting
j\to\infty
we get

\liminf_{j\to\infty}|Du_{p_{j}}(x_{j})|\geq\frac{u(x)-u\left(x+t\nu_{x}\right)}{t},\quad\forall 0<t<\frac{1}{4}{\rm dist}(x,\partial\Omega_{0}).

Using the differentiability of
u
in
\Omega
, by sending
t\to 0
we conclude

\liminf_{j\to\infty}|Du_{p_{j}}(x_{j})|\geq-Du(x)\cdot\nu_{x}=|Du(x)|.

Hence we finish this proof.
∎

Let us now proof Theorem
1.1
.

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

The
C^{1}
regularity of

follows from Lemma

.
Also, the locally uniform convergence of

.

6
Two fundamental inequalities under quasi-concavity

We establish a fundamental algebraic and geometric structural inequalities involving
\Delta_{\infty}v
for smooth
quasi-concave functions
v

via the geometry of the contour surfaces.

Lemma 6.1
.

Let
n\geq 2
and let
v:{{{\mathbb{R}}}^{n}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
be a quasi-concave function.
If
v\in C^{\infty}(U)
for some domain
U\subset{{{\mathbb{R}}}^{n}}
, then

\displaystyle 2[|D^{2}vDv|^{2}-\Delta v\Delta_{\infty}v]\geq|Dv|^{2}[|D^{2}v|^{2}-(\Delta v)^{2}]\quad{\rm in}\quad U.

(6.1)

This further allows us to get the following fundamental divergence inequality
for quasi-concave functions.

Lemma 6.2
.

Let
n\geq 2
and let
v:{{{\mathbb{R}}}^{n}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
be a quasi-concave function.
If
v\in C^{\infty}(U)
for some domain
U\subset{{{\mathbb{R}}}^{n}}
, then

\displaystyle{\rm div}\left(|Dv|^{-2}(\Delta vDv-D^{2}vDv)\right)\geq 0\quad{\rm in}\quad U\backslash\{Dv\neq 0\}.

Remark 6.3
.

In dimension
n=2
,
for any function
v\in C^{\infty}(U)
it was shown in
[
39
]
that

2[|D^{2}vDv|^{2}-\Delta v\Delta_{\infty}v]=|Dv|^{2}[|D^{2}v|^{2}-(\Delta v)^{2}]\quad{\rm in}\quad U

and

{\rm div}\left(|Dv|^{-2}(\Delta vDv-D^{2}vDv)\right)=0\quad{\rm in}\quad U\backslash\{Dv\neq 0\}.

So the main ingredient for Lemma
6.1
and Lemma
6.2
is in dimension
n\geq 3
.

We need the following lemma to prove Lemma
6.1
. We denote

e_{n}=(0,...,0,1)
.

Lemma 6.4
.

Let
n\geq 3
and let
v:{{{\mathbb{R}}}^{n}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
be a quasi-concave function.
Assume that
v\in C^{2}(B(0,r))
,
v(0)=0
and
Dv(0)=e_{n}
.
Then

\displaystyle(w_{x_{i}x_{j}})^{2}-w_{x_{i}x_{i}}w_{x_{j}x_{j}}\leq 0\quad\forall 1\leq i<j\leq n-1.

(6.2)

The inequality (
6.2
) essentially says that the sectional curvature is non-positive at
0
.
This is well-known in differential geometry.
For reader’s convenience, we give the details via an analysis argument.

Proof.

First, note that the quasi-concavity of
v
guarantees

v(y)\leq 0
for all
y\in B(0,s)
with
y_{n}=0

(6.3)

for some
0<s<r
.
Indeed, since
v\in C^{2}(B(0,r))
and
Dv(0)=e_{n}
, by the implicit theorem we know that
the equation
v(y)=0
determines a
(n-1)
-dimension surface
S
in a ball
B(0,s)

for some
0<s<r
. The boundary of

\{y\in B(0,s)|v(y)>0\}
is
(S\cap B(0,s))\cup(\partial B(0,s)\cap\{v>0\})
.
Since
Dv(0)=e_{n}
, the tangential plane of
S
at
0
is given by the hyperplane

P=\{y\in{{{\mathbb{R}}}^{n}}|y_{n}=0\}
. The convexity of

\{y\in{{{\mathbb{R}}}^{n}}|v(y)>0\}
implies that the convexity of
\{y\in B(0,s)|v(y)>0\}
.
Thus

\{y\in B(x,s)|v(y)>0\}
lies in above
P
, that is,
if
v(y)>0
, then
y_{n}>0
.
Therefore, (
6.3
) holds as desired.

Applying (
6.3
), we claim that

\displaystyle D^{2}v(0)\xi\cdot\xi\leq 0\quad\forall\xi\in{{{\mathbb{R}}}^{n}}\quad{\rm with}\quad\xi_{n}=0,

(6.4)

that is, the second fundamental form of
S
at point
0
is non-positive definite.
Indeed, given any
\xi\in\partial B(0,1)
,
by Taylor’s expansion and
Dv(0)=e_{n}
one has

v(t\xi)=v(0)+t\xi_{n}+\frac{t^{2}}{2}D^{2}v(0)\xi\cdot\xi+o(t^{2}).

If
\xi_{n}=0
,
by (
6.3
) one then has
v(t\xi)\leq v(0)=0
and hence

D^{2}v(0)\xi\cdot\xi=\lim_{t\to 0}\frac{2}{t^{2}}[v(t\xi)-v(0)]\leq 0.

This gives the claim (
6.4
).

Next, at point
0
we apply (
6.4
) to get

\displaystyle(w_{x_{i}x_{j}})^{2}-w_{x_{i}x_{i}}w_{x_{j}x_{j}}\leq 0\quad\forall 1\leq i<j\leq n-1.

Indeed, given any pair
(i,j)
with
1\leq i<j\leq n-1
, write

Q:=\begin{bmatrix}w_{x_{i}x_{i}}&\ w_{x_{i}x_{j}}\\
w_{x_{j}x_{i}}&\ w_{x_{j}x_{j}}\end{bmatrix}.

Applying (
6.4
) with
\xi=ae_{i}+be_{j}
for all

a,b\in{\mathbb{R}}
we obtain

(a,b)Q(a,b)^{T}=a^{2}w_{x_{i}x_{i}}+b^{2}w_{x_{j}x_{j}}+2abw_{x_{i}x_{j}}=\xi^{T}D^{2}w(0)\xi\leq 0.

Clearly, this quadratic form is negative semi-definite,
and hence its eigenvalues of
Q
are non-positive. Thus

\det Q=w_{x_{i}x_{i}}w_{x_{j}x_{j}}-(w_{x_{i}x_{j}})^{2}\geq 0,

which gives (
6.2
).
∎

We are ready to prove Lemma
6.1
.

Proof of Lemma
6.1
.

By Remark
6.3
, we only need to consider the dimension
n\geq 3
.
Assume that
v:{{{\mathbb{R}}}^{n}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
is a quasi-concave function and
v\in C^{\infty}(U)
.
Fix any
x\in U
. Up to considering
v(z+x)-v(x)
with
z+x\in U
we may assume that

x=0\in U
and
v(0)=0
.
If
Dv(0)=0
, then
(
6.1
) holds trivially.
So we assume
Dv(0)\neq 0
below.
Up to considering
v/|Dv(0)|
, we may assume that
|Dv(0)|=1
.
Below we consider the following two cases:
Dv(0)=e_{n}
, and
Dv(0)\neq e_{n}
.

Case
Dv(0)=e_{n}
.
At the point
0
, by

Dv=e_{n}
, a direct calculation gives

\Delta_{\infty}v=v_{x_{n}x_{n}},\quad|D^{2}vDv|^{2}=\sum_{1\leq i\leq n}(v_{x_{i}x_{n}})^{2}.

We then write (
6.1
) as

2\sum_{1\leq i\leq n}(v_{x_{i}x_{n}})^{2}-2\Delta vv_{x_{n}x_{n}}\geq|D^{2}v|^{2}-(\Delta v)^{2},

that is,

\displaystyle|D^{2}v|^{2}\leq(\Delta v)^{2}-2\Delta vv_{x_{n}x_{n}}+2\sum_{1\leq i\leq n}(v_{x_{i}x_{n}})^{2}.

(6.5)

To prove (
6.5
), we write

\displaystyle|D^{2}v|^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(v_{x_{i}x_{i}}\right)^{2}+2\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq n}\left(v_{x_{i}x_{j}}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\left(v_{x_{i}x_{i}}\right)^{2}+(v_{x_{n}x_{n}})^{2}+2\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq n-1}\left(v_{x_{i}x_{j}}\right)^{2}+2\sum_{1\leq i\leq n-1}(v_{x_{i}x_{n}})^{2}.

Note that

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\left(v_{x_{i}x_{i}}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}v_{x_{i}x_{i}}\right)^{2}-2\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq n-1}v_{x_{i}x_{i}}v_{x_{j}x_{j}}

\displaystyle=(\Delta v-v_{x_{n}x_{n}})^{2}-2\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq n-1}v_{x_{i}x_{i}}v_{x_{j}x_{j}}

\displaystyle=(\Delta v)^{2}-2\Delta vv_{x_{n}x_{n}}+(v_{x_{n}x_{n}})^{2}-2\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq n-1}v_{x_{i}x_{i}}v_{x_{j}x_{j}}

and

2\sum_{1\leq i\leq n-1}(v_{x_{i}x_{n}})^{2}=2\sum_{1\leq i\leq n}(v_{x_{i}x_{n}})^{2}-2(v_{x_{n}x_{n}})^{2}.

We therefore obtain

\displaystyle|D^{2}v|^{2}

\displaystyle=(\Delta v)^{2}-2\Delta vv_{x_{n}x_{n}}+2(v_{x_{n}x_{n}})^{2}

\displaystyle\quad\quad+2\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq n-1}[(v_{x_{i}x_{j}})^{2}-v_{x_{i}x_{i}}v_{x_{j}x_{j}}]+2\sum_{1\leq i\leq n}(v_{x_{i}x_{n}})^{2}-2(v_{x_{n}x_{n}})^{2}

\displaystyle=(\Delta v)^{2}-2\Delta vv_{x_{n}x_{n}}+2\sum_{1\leq i\leq n}(v_{x_{i}x_{n}})^{2}+2\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq n-1}[(v_{x_{i}x_{j}})^{2}-v_{x_{i}x_{i}}v_{x_{j}x_{j}}].

Since Lemma
6.4
tells us that

\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq n-1}[(v_{x_{i}x_{j}})^{2}-v_{x_{i}x_{i}}v_{x_{j}x_{j}}]\leq 0,

we get (
6.5
) as desired.

Case
Dv(0)\neq e_{n}
.
Denote by
O
the orthogonal matrix so that

Dv(0)=Oe_{n}
.
Define

w(y):=v(Oy)\quad\forall y\in U.

At point
0
, we have
Dw=O^{T}Dv=e_{n}
. Applying (
6.5
) to
w
, we get

2[|D^{2}wDw|^{2}-\Delta w\Delta_{\infty}w]\geq[|D^{2}w|^{2}-(\Delta w)^{2}].

This would imply (
6.1
) for
v
at
0
once we have

|D^{2}wDw|^{2}=|D^{2}vDv|^{2}
,
|D^{2}w|^{2}=|D^{2}v|^{2}
,
\Delta w=\Delta v
and

\Delta_{\infty}w=\Delta v_{\infty}
at 0.

We check this as below.
At point
0
,
we have

D^{2}w=O^{T}D^{2}vO
and
D^{2}wDw=O^{T}D^{2}v(OO^{T})Dv=O^{T}D^{2}vDv.

Thus
|D^{2}w|^{2}=|D^{2}v|^{2}
and
|D^{2}wDw|^{2}=|D^{2}vDv|^{2}.
By
OO^{T}=I_{n}
, we also have

\displaystyle\Delta_{\infty}w

\displaystyle=(Dw)^{T}D^{2}wDw=(Dv)^{T}(OO^{T})D^{2}vDv=(Dv)^{T}D^{2}vDv=\Delta_{\infty}v.

Moreover, the cyclic property of trace implies that

\displaystyle\Delta w

\displaystyle={\rm tr}(D^{2}w)={\rm tr}(O^{T}D^{2}vO)={\rm tr}(OO^{T}D^{2}v)={\rm tr}(D^{2}v)=\Delta v.

The proof is complete.
∎

Finally we apply Lemma
6.1
to prove Lemma
6.2
.

Proof of Lemma
6.2
.

At any point where
Dv\neq 0
, a direct calculation gives

\displaystyle{\rm div}(|Dv|^{-2}\Delta vDv)=-2|Dv|^{-4}\Delta_{\infty}v\Delta v+|Dv|^{-2}(\Delta v)^{2}+|Dv|^{-2}D\Delta v\cdot Dv

and

\displaystyle{\rm div}(|Dv|^{-2}D^{2}vDv)=-2|Dv|^{-4}|D^{2}vDv|^{2}+|Dv|^{-2}D\Delta v\cdot Dv+|Dv|^{-2}|D^{2}v|^{2}.

Combining them and using Lemma
6.1

one then obtains

\displaystyle{\rm div}\left(|Dv|^{-2}\Delta vDv-|Dv|^{-2}D^{2}vDv\right)

\displaystyle=2|Dv|^{-4}[|D^{2}vDv|^{2}-\Delta v\Delta_{\infty}v]+|Dv|^{-2}[(\Delta v)^{2}-|D^{2}v|^{2}]\geq 0

as desired.
The proof is complete.
∎

.

If

Proof.

We only consider the case
\alpha<0

For any
B(z,r)\Subset\Omega
we recall from Lemma
7.1

\displaystyle\int_{B(z,r/2)}|D|Du_{p}|^{\alpha}|^{2}\,dx

\displaystyle\leq C(1+\alpha^{2})r^{-2}\int_{B(z,r)}|Du_{p}|^{2\alpha},

where

is a universal constant.
By (
4.11
) and (
4.12
) in Lemma
4.4
,
we have
|Du_{p}|^{\alpha}\in W^{1,2}(B(z,r/2))
uniformly

.
Using the weak compactness

(see
[

),
there exists a function

\displaystyle D|Du_{p}|^{\alpha}\to Dg_{\alpha}\ {\rm weakly\ in}\ L^{2}(B(z,r))\ {\rm and}\ |Du_{p}|^{\alpha}\to g_{\alpha}\ {\rm in}\ L^{2}(B(z,r))\mbox{ as $p\to\infty$.}

Since Theorem
1.1

.

Therefore

\displaystyle=C(1+\alpha^{2})r^{-2}\int_{B(z,r)}|Du|^{2\alpha}\,dx

as desired.
∎

As a direct consequence of Lemma

along

Lemma 7.4
.

For any
\alpha\in{\mathbb{R}}
, we have

almost everywhere in
\Omega
.

Proof.

By
Du_{p}\neq 0
in
\Omega
, we
note that

For any

\int_{\Omega}D\ln|Du_{p}|\cdot Du_{p}\phi\,dx=-\frac{1}{p-2}\int_{\Omega}\Delta u_{p}\phi\,dx=\frac{1}{p-2}\int_{\Omega}Du_{p}\cdot D\phi\,dx.

Since

in

1.1

weakly in

Thus

=0 almost everywhere in
\Omega
.

Recall that

u\in C^{1}(\Omega)
and
Du\neq 0

\Omega

For any
\alpha\in{\mathbb{R}}\setminus\{0\}

Therefore

∎

Now we can finish the proof of Theorem

.

Proof of Theorem
1.3
.

It follows from Lemma

and Lemma

.

Proof of Theorem

In this section, we assume that

\infty
-harmonic potential in

To prove Theorem

Lemma 8.1
.

For any

Moreover, we have

Thanks to the convexity of

\|Du\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega_{0})}=\sup_{x\in\Omega_{0}}{\mathop{\mathrm{}}{\,Lip}}\,u(x)={\mathop{\mathrm{}}{\,Lip}}(u,\Omega_{0}):=\sup_{x\neq y,x,y\in\Omega_{0}}\frac{u(x)-u(y)}{|x-y|}.

From
\Omega=\Omega_{0}\setminus\{x_{0}\}

{\mathop{\mathrm{}}{\,Lip}}(u,\Omega_{0})={\mathop{\mathrm{}}{\,Lip}}(u,\Omega)

Since

{\mathop{\mathrm{}}{\,Lip}}(u,\Omega)={\mathop{\mathrm{}}{\,Lip}}(u,\partial\Omega)=\sup_{y\in\partial\Omega_{0}}\frac{1}{|x_{0}-y|}=\frac{1}{{\mathop{\mathrm{}}{\,dist\,}}(x_{0},\partial\Omega_{0})}.

Therefore by Theorem
2.8
,

|Du(x)|={\mathop{\mathrm{}}{\,Lip}}\,u(x)\leq\|Du\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega_{0})}=\frac{1}{{\mathop{\mathrm{}}{\,dist\,}}(x_{0},\partial\Omega_{0})}\quad\forall x\in\Omega.

Below we show that

\liminf_{x\to x_{0}}|Du(x)|\geq\|Du\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega_{0})}
.
To this end,
using Theorem 1.1 in
[
58
]
we have

\lim_{x\to x_{0}}\frac{|u(x)-u(x_{0})+\|Du\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega_{0})}|x-x_{0}||}{|x-x_{0}|}=0.

Given any
\epsilon>0
, there is

such that

\displaystyle\frac{u(x)-u(x_{0})}{|x-x_{0}|}\geq\|Du\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega_{0})}-\epsilon\quad\forall x\in B(x_{0},r_{\epsilon})\backslash\{x_{0}\}\Subset\Omega_{0}.

(8.1)

For each

4.1
we get

|Du(x)|\geq\frac{u(x)-u(x+t\nu_{x})}{t}\quad\forall 0<t<r_{x,\nu_{x}},

where
\nu_{x}=-Du(x)/|Du(x)|
and
u(x+r_{x,\nu_{x}}\nu_{x})=0
.
Observe that, given any

,
via (

) we obtain

\displaystyle\liminf_{x\to x_{0}}|Du(x)|

\displaystyle\geq\liminf_{x\to x_{0}}\frac{u(x)-u(x+t\nu_{x})}{t}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{u(x_{0})-\sup_{|z|=1}u(x_{0}+tz)}{t}

\displaystyle\geq\|Du\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega_{0})}-\epsilon.

By the arbitrariness of

, we have
\liminf_{x\to x_{0}}|Du(x)|\geq\|Du\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega_{0})}
as desired.
∎

We are ready to prove Theorem

.

.

\displaystyle=\limsup_{r\to 0}\sup_{y\in B(z,r)}\frac{u_{x_{i}}(y)-u_{x_{i}}(z)}{r}

Step 1.

Given any

[

there exists a unique solution
{\gamma}^{p}_{x}\in C^{1}([0,T_{x}^{p}),\Omega)
for some

to the problem

\displaystyle\frac{d{\gamma}^{p}_{x}(t)}{dt}

\displaystyle=Du_{p}({\gamma}^{p}_{x}(t))\quad\forall t\in[0,T_{x}^{p});\ {\gamma}^{p}_{x}(0)=x,

Moreover, we compute that

Since

\mbox{$\Delta_{p}u_{p}=|Du_{p}|^{p-2}(\Delta u_{p}+(p-2)\Delta^{N}_{\infty}u_{p})=0$, $Du_{p}\neq 0$
and $-\Delta u_{p}\geq 0$ in $\Omega$},

\displaystyle=-\frac{2}{p-2}|Du_{p}({\gamma}^{p}_{x}(t))|^{2}\Delta u_{p}({\gamma}^{p}_{x}(t))\geq 0,

is convex on

]
. For the readers of convenience, we provide the proof in this Appendix.

Lemma B.1
.

For all
B(z,r)\Subset\Omega
, we have

\displaystyle\max_{\overline{B(z,r)}}|Du_{p}|=\max_{\partial B(z,r)}|Du_{p}|,\quad\min_{\overline{B(z,r)}}|Du_{p}|=\min_{\partial B(z,r)}|Du_{p}|

(B.1)

and

\displaystyle\max_{\overline{B(z,r)}}\frac{\partial u_{p}}{\partial x_{i}}=\max_{\partial B(z,r)}\frac{\partial u_{p}}{\partial x_{i}},\quad\min_{\overline{B(z,r)}}\frac{\partial u_{p}}{\partial x_{i}}=\min_{\partial B(z,r)}\frac{\partial u_{p}}{\partial x_{i}}\quad\mbox{ for $i=1,2$}.

(B.2)

Proof.

Recall that
u_{p}\in C^{\infty}(\Omega)
and
Du_{p}\neq 0
in
\Omega
. Using equation

-(p-2)\Delta^{N}_{\infty}u_{p}=\Delta u_{p}
and the identity in Remark
6.3
one has

{\rm div}(BDw)=0\quad{\rm in}\quad\Omega,

where

w=\ln|Du_{p}|,\quad B=(p-2)\frac{Du_{p}\otimes Du_{p}}{|Du_{p}|^{2}}+I_{2}.

Here
\otimes
stands for tensor product and
I_{2}
is identity matrix.
Since
Du_{p}\neq 0
in
\Omega
, then (
B.1
) follows from the maximum principle.

To prove (
B.2
), for each
1\leq j\leq 2
we differentiate equation (
1.20
) to get

\left(|Du_{p}|^{p-2}\frac{\partial u_{p}}{\partial x_{i}}\right)_{x_{i}x_{j}}=0\quad{\rm in}\quad\Omega.

This further leads to

{\rm div}\left(A\frac{\partial u_{p}}{\partial x_{j}}\right)=0\quad{\rm in}\quad\Omega,

where

A=|Du_{p}|^{p-2}\left[(p-2)\frac{Du_{p}\otimes Du_{p}}{|Du_{p}|^{2}}+I_{2}\right].

By
Du_{p}\neq 0
in
\Omega
, thus (
B.2
) also holds via the maximum principle.
∎

Denote by
u
be the
\infty
-harmonic potential in
\Omega
.
Recall that
Du_{p}\to Du
is locally uniform on
\Omega
in Theorem
1.1
, we have the following monotonicity property for
u
.

Lemma B.2
.

For all
B(z,r)\Subset\Omega
, we have

\displaystyle\max_{\overline{B(z,r)}}|Du|=\max_{\partial B(z,r)}|Du|,\quad\min_{\overline{B(z,r)}}|Du|=\min_{\partial B(z,r)}|Du|

(B.3)

and

\displaystyle\max_{\overline{B(z,r)}}u_{x_{i}}=\max_{\partial B(z,r)}u_{x_{i}},\quad\min_{\overline{B(z,r)}}u_{x_{i}}=\min_{\partial B(z,r)}u_{x_{i}}\quad\mbox{ for $i=1,2$}.

(B.4)

Proof.

We only prove
\max_{\overline{B(z,r)}}|Du|=\max_{\partial B(z,r)}|Du|
; the others can be proved in a similar way.
Assume that is not correct. Then one can find a point
x\in B(z,r)
such that

|Du(x)|>\max_{\overline{B(z,r)}}|Du|
. Since
|Du_{p}|\to|Du|
in
C^{0}(\overline{B(z,r)})
,
for all sufficiently large
p
, we have

|Du_{p}(x)|>\max_{\overline{B(z,r)}}|Du_{p}|
, which is a contradiction.
∎

Due to Lebesgue in
[
40
]
, the monotonicity allows us to get the following.

Lemma B.3
.

For all
B(z,4r)\Subset\Omega
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{{\rm osc}_{B(z,r)}|Du|^{2}}{r}\leq C\left(\mathop{}\mkern-3.0mu\mathchoice{\hbox to0.0pt{$\displaystyle\vbox{\hbox to5.83331pt{\hss$\textstyle{-}$\hss}}$\hss}}{\hbox to0.0pt{$\textstyle\vbox{\hbox to5.83331pt{\hss$\scriptstyle{-}$\hss}}$\hss}}{\hbox to0.0pt{$\scriptstyle\vbox{\hbox to5.1215pt{\hss$\scriptscriptstyle{-}$\hss}}$\hss}}{\hbox to0.0pt{$\scriptscriptstyle\vbox{\hbox to4.82639pt{\hss$\scriptscriptstyle{-}$\hss}}$\hss}}\mkern-3.0mu\int_{B_{2r}(z)}|D|Du|^{2}|^{2}\,dx\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}

(B.5)

and

\displaystyle\frac{{\rm osc}_{B(z,r)}u_{x_{i}}}{r}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\|\mathcal{D}[u_{x_{i}}]\|(B(z,2r))}{r^{2}}\quad\mbox{ for $i=1,2$}.

(B.6)

Proof.

Let
B(z,4r)\Subset\Omega
and
0<\epsilon<\frac{1}{4}r
.
We set
g^{\epsilon}=|Du|^{2}\ast\eta^{\epsilon}
where

\eta^{\epsilon}
is standard mollifier.
Using polar coordinates in the plane, we have

\displaystyle{\rm osc}_{\partial B(z,\rho)}g^{\epsilon}\leq\int^{2\pi}_{0}\frac{\partial g^{\epsilon}}{\partial\theta}(\rho,\theta)\,d\theta.

Observing

\displaystyle|Dg^{\epsilon}|^{2}=\rho^{-2}\left(\frac{\partial g^{\epsilon}}{\partial\theta}\right)^{2}+\left(\frac{\partial g^{\epsilon}}{\partial\rho}\right)^{2}\geq\rho^{-2}\left(\frac{\partial g^{\epsilon}}{\partial\theta}\right)^{2},

we have

\displaystyle\int^{2r}_{r}{\rm osc}_{\partial B(z,\rho)}g^{\epsilon}d\rho\leq\int^{2\pi}_{0}\int^{2r}_{r}\rho^{-1}\frac{\partial g^{\epsilon}}{\partial\theta}(\rho,\theta)\rho\,d\rho d\theta\leq\int_{B(z,2r)}|Dg^{\epsilon}|\,dx.

Since
|Du|\in C^{0}_{{\mathop{\mathrm{}}{\,loc\,}}}(\Omega)\cap W^{1,2}_{{\mathop{\mathrm{}}{\,loc\,}}}(\Omega)
,
by
[
28
, Theorem 6, Appendix C.4]
we have

g^{\epsilon}\to|Du|^{2}
in
C^{0}(B(z,2r))
and
Dg^{\epsilon}\to D|Du|^{2}
in

L^{2}(B(z,2r))
as
\epsilon\to 0
.
Sending

\epsilon\to 0
, one gets

\displaystyle\int^{2r}_{r}{\rm osc}_{\partial B(z,\rho)}|Du|^{2}\,d\rho\leq\int_{B(z,2r)}|D|Du|^{2}|\,dx.

Since (
B.3
) gives

\mathop{\mathrm{}}{\,osc\,}_{B(z,r)}|Du|^{2}\leq\mathop{\mathrm{}}{\,osc\,}_{\overline{B(z,\rho)}}|Du|^{2}=\mathop{\mathrm{}}{\,osc\,}_{\partial B(z,\rho)}|Du|^{2},\quad\forall\rho\in[0,2r],

we have

\frac{\mathop{\mathrm{}}{\,osc\,}_{B(z,r)}|Du|^{2}}{r}\leq\frac{1}{r^{2}}\int^{2r}_{r}{\rm osc}_{\partial B(z,\rho)}|Du|^{2}\,d\rho\leq\frac{1}{r}\int_{B(z,2r)}|D|Du|^{2}|\,dx.

Applying the Hölder inequality, we obtain (
B.5
) as desired.

Similarly, for

1\leq i\leq 2
, write

u^{\epsilon}_{x_{i}}=u_{x_{i}}\ast\eta^{\epsilon}
where

\eta^{\epsilon}
is standard mollifier. By an argument similar to above, one has

\displaystyle\int^{2r}_{r}{\rm osc}_{\partial B(z,\rho)}u^{\epsilon}_{x_{i}}\,d\rho\leq\int_{B(z,2r)}|Du^{\epsilon}_{x_{i}}|\,dx

By
u_{x_{i}}\in BV_{{\mathop{\mathrm{}}{\,loc\,}}}(\Omega)
and
u^{\epsilon}_{x_{i}}=u_{x_{i}}\ast\eta^{\epsilon}
, one has

\limsup_{\epsilon\to 0}\int_{B(z,2r)}|Du^{\epsilon}_{x_{i}}|\,dx\leq\|\mathcal{D}[u_{x_{i}}]\|(B(z,2r)).

Using
u_{x_{i}}^{\epsilon}\to u_{x_{i}}
in
C^{0}(\Omega)
again,

\int^{2r}_{r}{\rm osc}_{\partial B(z,\rho)}u_{x_{i}}\,d\rho\leq\|\mathcal{D}[u_{x_{i}}]\|(B(z,2r)).

Since (
B.4
) yields

\frac{\mathop{\mathrm{}}{\,osc\,}_{B(z,r)}u_{x_{i}}}{r}\leq\frac{1}{r^{2}}\int^{2r}_{r}{\rm osc}_{\partial B(z,\rho)}u_{x_{i}}\,d\rho,

we obtain (
B.6
) as desired.
∎
