Nonlinear recombinations and generalized random transpositions
Pietro Caputo, Daniel Parisi

Abstract
. We study a nonlinear recombination model from population genetics as a combinatorial version of the Kac-Boltzmann equation from kinetic theory. Following Kac’s approach, the nonlinear model is approximated by a mean field linear evolution with a large number of particles. In our setting, the latter takes the form of a generalized random transposition dynamics. Our main results establish a uniform in time propagation of chaos with quantitative bounds, and a tight entropy production estimate for the generalized random transpositions, which holds uniformly in the number of particles. As a byproduct of our analysis we obtain sharp estimates on the speed of convergence to stationarity for the nonlinear equation, both in terms of relative entropy and total variation norm.

1.
Introduction

Recombinations are one of the principal components in the analysis of stochastic genetic algorithms
[
28
,
35
]
.
Nonlinear recombinations provide a simple combinatorial setup for quadratic evolutions described by a Boltzmann-like equation
[
38
]
. A particle is represented by a finite string of characters from some finite alphabet and the binary collision mechanism is
given by a recombination, that is the transposition of a random portion of the two colliding strings. The model belongs to the family of symmetric quadratic systems introduced in
[
39
]
; see also
[
7
]

for the more general framework of reversible quadratic systems.

Following the strategy introduced by Mark Kac in his seminal 1956 paper
[
25
]
, one can approximate the nonlinear evolution of one particle by a linear mean field type Markov process involving a large number of particles. Roughly speaking, if one has a good control of this approximation, together with a good control of the linear particle system, then the difficulties due to the nonlinearity in the original process can be overcome.

In the context of Boltzmann’s equation and its closely related kinetic models this line of research has witnessed important progress in recent years
[
8
,
33
,
34
,
11
]
, see also
[
10
,
17
,
27
]
for related results for mean field diffusions of McKean-Vlasov type.

The combinatorial setup considered in the present paper appears to be less explored; see however
[
37
,
40
,
3
]
for the analysis of Boltzmann-like equations with discrete velocities. One advantage of the combinatorial setting is that thanks to the discrete setup one can avoid a number of technical assumptions, such as regularity and moments constraints, on the various distributions considered. Moreover, and perhaps more importantly, in contrast with the well studied case of the Kac-Boltzmann equation
[
8
,
33
,
18
,
5
]
, in our setup it is possible to obtain tight entropy production estimates for the particle system which hold uniformly in the number of particles. This provides a class of models for which the renowned Kac program can be completed in a strong sense.

In the setting of nonlinear recombinations the linear particle system takes the form of a generalized random transposition dynamics. This yields a natural generalization of the mean field exchange dynamics that are commonly studied in the probabilistic literature such as the Bernoulli-Laplace or the random transposition model
[
15
]
. The purpose of this paper is twofold.
On one hand we establish
uniform in time propagation of chaos
. On the other hand we prove tight estimates on the
entropy production
of the linear system which hold
uniformly in the number of particles
.
As a corollary we obtain quantitative control on the convergence to stationarity for the nonlinear model in terms of relative entropy. In particular, this extends some results previously obtained in
[
38
,
7
]
by direct analysis of the entropy in the nonlinear recombination model.
We now proceed with a detailed description of the model and of our main results.

1.1.
The nonlinear equation

Let
\Omega=\prod_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}
be the set of
n
-vectors
\sigma=(\sigma_{1},\dots,\sigma_{n})
where
\sigma_{i}\in X_{i}
, and the
X_{i}
are given finite sets. We interpret
\sigma
as a particle. Thus, a particle is a string of
n
characters each taken from a finite alphabet.
A basic example is obtained by taking
\Omega=\{0,1\}^{n}
. Without loss of generality we will assume that each
X_{i}
has the form
X_{i}:=\{0,1,2,\dots,q_{i}\}
, for some
q_{i}\in{\mathbb{N}}
. Given a subset
A\subset[n],

[n]=\{1,\dots,n\}
, and
\sigma\in\Omega,

\sigma_{A}
denotes the
A-
component of
\sigma,
that is the string
(\sigma_{i},i\in A).
If
(\sigma,\eta)\in\Omega\times\Omega
is a pair of particles, the recombination at
A
consists in exchanging the
A
-component of
\sigma
with the
A
-component of
\eta.
This defines the map

(1.1)

\displaystyle(\sigma,\eta)\mapsto(\eta_{A}\sigma_{A^{c}},\sigma_{A}\eta_{A^{c}}),

where
\eta_{A}\sigma_{A^{c}}
denotes the element of
\Omega
with entries
\eta_{i}
for
i\in A
and
\sigma_{i}
for
i\in A^{c}=[n]\setminus A.

Let
\mathcal{P}(\Omega)
denote the set of probability measures on
\Omega
.
If the original pair
(\sigma,\eta)
is obtained by sampling independently from
p\in\mathcal{P}(\Omega)
, then the new particle
\eta_{A}\sigma_{A^{c}}
is distributed according to
p_{A}\otimes p_{A^{c}}
, the product of the two marginals of
p
on the
A
and
A^{c}
components respectively. By choosing the set
A\subset[n]
according to some distribution
\nu
, one obtains the quadratic collision kernel

(1.2)

\displaystyle p\mapsto Q(p)=\sum_{A\subset[n]}\nu(A)\,p_{A}\otimes p_{A^{c}}.

The nonlinear evolution is defined by the dynamical system
\dot{p}_{t}=Q(p_{t})-p_{t}
, that is

(1.3)

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\,p_{t}=\sum_{A\subset[n]}\nu(A)\left(p_{t,A}\otimes p_{t,A^{c}}-p_{t}\right)

with the initial condition
p_{0}=p\in\mathcal{P}(\Omega)
. Here
p_{t}\in\mathcal{P}(\Omega)
is the distribution of the particle at time
t
,
p_{t,A}
denotes its marginal on
A
and
\nu
is a given probability measure over the subsets of
[n]
.
The study of this model starts with the pioneering work of Geiringer
[
21
]
; see also
[
39
,
38
,
1
,
29
]
for more recent accounts. It is well known that the Cauchy problem associated to (
1.3
) has a unique solution for every initial distribution
p\in\mathcal{P}(\Omega)
; see e.g.
[
1
]
. Moreover, it is not difficult to see that the evolution preserves the single site marginals, that is
p_{t,i}=p_{i}
for all
t\;\geqslant\;0
and for all
i\in[n]
. We say that the recombination measure
\nu
is
separating
if for any
i,j\in[n]
there is a positive probability that the random set
A
with distribution
\nu
separates
i
and
j
. Equivalently, if
r(\nu)<1
where

(1.4)

r(\nu):=\max_{i<j\in[n]}\,\nu\left(\{i,j\}\subset A\text{ or }\{i,j\}\subset A^{c}\right)

denotes the maximum over
i<j
of the probability that
A
does not separate
i,j
.
It is a classical fact that, under the assumption that
\nu
is separating, the system converges to the stationary state given by the product of the marginals of
the initial state
p
; namely, if
\pi_{i}=p_{i}
denotes the marginal of
p
at site
i
, then

(1.5)

\pi=\otimes_{i=1}^{n}\,\pi_{i}

is the equilibrium distribution and one has the convergence
p_{t}\to\pi
,
t\to\infty
,
which can be interpreted as the effect of repeated fragmentations of the initial state; see e.g.
[
38
]
. Some of our results will hold for arbitrary separating
\nu
.
In some other cases we consider a slightly stronger assumption on
\nu
.
Two examples to which all our results apply are the following distributions
\nu
, which are commonly considered in the genetic recombination literature. We refer to
[
38
,
7
]
for more examples.

1)

Uniform crossover
:
\nu(A)=\frac{1}{2^{n}}
, for all
A\subset[n]
;

2)

One-point crossover
:

\nu(A)=\tfrac{1}{n+1}\sum_{i=0}^{n}\mathbf{1}_{A=J_{i}}
, where
J_{0}=\emptyset
,
J_{i}=\{1,\dots,i\}
,
i\;\geqslant\;1
.

The quantitavive analysis of the convergence to equilibrium
p_{t}\to\pi
,
t\to\infty
has been initiated in
[
39
,
38
]
, where a “mixing time” bound was obtained for the discrete time version of the model. The decay to equilibrium in relative entropy for the continuous time model was studied in
[
7
]
. These results were obtained by direct analysis of the nonlinear problem. In this paper we shall follow an entirely different approach, inspired by Kac’s program from kinetic theory. As a byproduct of our analysis, we shall obtain an alternative proof of the known results mentioned above.

1.2.
The particle system

Suppose there are
N
“particles”, described by variables
\eta(j)\in\Omega
,
j=1,\dots,N
. That is, each particle is a single string from
\Omega
and
\eta_{i}(j)
denotes the content of the
j
-th particle at site
i\in[n]
. We may picture
\eta\in\Omega^{N}
as a
N\times n
matrix such that each row is a particle with
n
entries, and for each
i\in[n]
, the
i
-th column
\eta_{i}
represents the content of site
i
for different particles.

Notice that
N
and
n
play two very different roles here. The number
N
of particles will eventually be taken to
+\infty
to recover the non linear mean field limit, in accordance with the general Kac program. The number
n
should be thought as a fixed, possibly large quantity describing the size of a single particle space.

The Markov process is given by the following random pair-exchange process.
Pairs of particles
\{j,l\}
,
1\;\leqslant\;j<l\;\leqslant\;N
are chosen independently according to a Poisson clock process with rate
1/N
. When the pair
\{j,l\}
“rings”, then
a set
A\subset[n]
is chosen with probability
\nu(A)
and the recombination

(1.6)

\displaystyle(\eta(l),\eta(j))\mapsto(\eta_{A}(j)\eta_{A^{c}}(l),\eta_{A}(l)\eta_{A^{c}}(j))

is performed, that is the
A
-content is exchanged between particle
j
and particle
l
. For all
j,l\in[N],A\subset[n],
for all
\eta\in\Omega^{N}
, we write
\eta^{j,l,A}
for the new configuration
\eta^{\prime}\in\Omega
defined by

(1.7)

\displaystyle\eta^{\prime}(k)=\eta(k),\;\forall k\neq j,l;\qquad\eta^{\prime}(l)=\eta_{A}(j)\eta_{A^{c}}(l),\qquad\eta^{\prime}(j)=\eta_{A}(l)\eta_{A^{c}}(j).

With this notation the pair configuration in the right hand side of (
1.6
) is
(\eta^{j,l,A}(l),\eta^{j,l,A}(j))
.
Define also
f^{j,l,A}(\eta)=f(\eta^{j,l,A})
for all
f:\Omega^{N}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
. Then, the infinitesimal generator of the continuous time Markov process is given by

(1.8)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{N}f=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{1\;\leqslant\;j<l\;\leqslant\;N}\sum_{A\subset[n]}\nu(A)\left(f^{j,l,A}-f\right),\qquad\;f:\Omega^{N}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}.

Any product measure
\mu_{N}
on
\Omega^{N}
of the form
\mu_{N}=\mu^{\otimes N},
where
\mu
is itself a product measure
\mu=\mu_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes\mu_{n}
on
\Omega=\prod_{i=1}^{n}X_{i},
defines a reversible measure for the generator
\mathcal{L}_{N}
. Indeed, for such a measure one has the symmetry

\mu_{N}(\eta^{j,l,A})=\mu_{N}(\eta)
for all
\eta\in\Omega^{N}
and therefore
\mathcal{L}_{N}
is self-adjoint in
L^{2}(\mu_{N})
.
The process is not irreducible in the state space
\Omega^{N}
since the content at a site for one particle is always exchanged with the content at the same site for another particle, and thus the number of particles with a given element
x\in X_{i}
at a given site
i\in[n]
is constant in time. To obtain an irreducible process one must fix the densities
\rho_{i,x},

i\in[n],x\in X_{i}
defined by

(1.9)

\displaystyle\rho_{i,x}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\mathbf{1}(\eta_{i}(j)=x).

We call
\rho=(\rho_{i,x})
the corresponding vector. Given a density vector
\rho
, consider the space

(1.10)

\displaystyle\Omega_{\rho}:=\Big\{(\eta(1),\dots,\eta(N))\in\Omega^{N}\!\!:\,\;\textstyle{\rho_{i,x}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\mathbf{1}(\eta_{i}(j)=x)},\;\,\forall i\in[n],\ x\in X_{i}\Big\}.

The set
\Omega_{\rho}
is well defined and non-empty for every vector
\rho=\rho_{N}
such that
\rho_{i,x}\in[0,1]
,
\sum_{x\in X_{i}}\rho_{i,x}=1
for all
i\in[n]
, and such that
N\rho_{i,x}
is an integer for all
i,x
. When this holds we say that
\rho_{N}
is an
admissible sequence
.
Under suitable assumptions on the recombination measure
\nu
, see Definition
1.7
, for any given admissible
\rho_{N}
, the Markov process with state space
\Omega_{\rho_{N}}
and generator
\mathcal{L}_{N}
is
irreducible and converges to the uniform distribution on
\Omega_{\rho_{N}}
. We will be interested in quantitative statements about this convergence.

We often use the following procedure to construct admissible sequences. Fix a given
\pi=(\pi_{i,x})
satisfying

\pi_{i,x}\in[0,1]
for all
i,x
and
\sum_{x\in X_{i}}\pi_{i,x}=1
for all
i\in[n]
. Then we call
\rho^{\pi}=\rho^{\pi}(\pi)
the density defined by

(1.11)

\displaystyle\rho^{\pi}_{i,x}:=\tfrac{1}{N}\,{\left\lfloor N\pi_{i,x}\right\rfloor}\,,\qquad i\in[n],\;x\in\{1,\dots,q_{i}\},

and we set
\rho^{\pi}_{i,0}=1-\sum_{x=1}^{q_{i}}\rho^{\pi}_{i,x}
.
We remark that
\rho^{\pi}=\rho^{\pi}_{N}
is an admissible sequence satisfying

\rho^{\pi}_{i,x}=\pi_{i,x}+O\left(\tfrac{1}{N}\right)

for all
i,x
.
Fixing the probability vector
\pi=(\pi_{i,x})
is equivalent to fixing the stationary measure (
1.5
) of the nonlinear evolution, and thus we use the same symbol for them.
From now on it is assumed that the densities
\pi_{i}
, and thus the corresponding product measure
\pi
as in (
1.5
), are fixed.
Without loss of generality we restrict to the case where
\pi_{i,x}\in(0,1)
for all
i\in[n],x\in X_{i}
since for each
i
we can otherwise discard those letters
x\in X_{i}
such that
\pi_{i,x}
is zero, and thus consider a new configuration space such that
\pi
is everywhere positive.

1.3.
Chaos and the propagation of chaos

The chaos property is commonly defined as follows.
A measure
\mu_{N}\in\mathcal{P}\left(\Omega^{N}\right)
is
symmetric
if it is invariant under any permutation of the
N
particles. We write
P_{k}\mu_{N}\in\mathcal{P}(\Omega^{k})
for the corresponding
k
-particle marginal.

Definition 1.1

(Kac’s chaos or “Boltzmann property”)
.

A sequence
\mu_{N}\in\mathcal{P}\left(\Omega^{N}\right)
of symmetric probabilities on
\Omega^{N}
is
\mu-
chaotic, for a given
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\Omega)
, if for any
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
one has the weak convergence

P_{k}\mu_{N}\longrightarrow\mu^{\otimes k}\,,\qquad N\to\infty.

A key step in implementing Kac’s program is to construct a correspondence between probability measures on
\Omega
and probability measures on
\Omega^{N}
. In our setting this can be formulated as follows.

Definition 1.2

(Canonical tensor product)
.

Given a probability measure
p\in\mathcal{P}(\Omega)
and an admissible sequence of density vectors
\rho_{N}
, we let

\gamma(p,\rho_{N}):=p^{\otimes N}\left(\,\cdot\,|\,\Omega_{\rho_{N}}\right)

be the tensor product of
p
conditioned on
\Omega_{\rho_{N}}
. When
\rho_{N}
is given by
\rho^{\pi}
as in (
1.11
), where the
\pi_{i,x}=p(\sigma_{i}=x)
are the marginals of
p
, we use the notation
\gamma_{N}(p):=\gamma(p,\rho^{\pi})
, and call it the canonical tensor product.

To avoid degeneracies we sometimes assume the following property.

Definition 1.3

(Irreducibility)
.

A probability measure
p\in\mathcal{P}(\Omega)
is called irreducible if for any
i\in[n]
, any
x\in\{1,\dots,q_{i}\}
, there exists
\chi\in\Omega
such that
p(\sigma_{i}=x,\sigma_{j}=\chi_{j}\;\forall j\neq i)>0
and
p(\sigma_{i}=0,\sigma_{j}=\chi_{j}\;\forall j\neq i)>0
.

If
p
is irreducible and the sequence
\rho_{N}
is sufficiently close to the marginals of
p
, then the local central limit theorem guarantees that the
k
-particle marginals of the symmetric measures
\gamma(p,\rho_{N})
converge to the product
p^{\otimes k}
as
N\to\infty
, that is
\gamma(p,\rho_{N})
is
p
-chaotic, see Theorem
2.6
for a precise statement. In particular, for the canonical tensor product
\gamma_{N}(p)
, we will see that

(1.12)

\displaystyle\|P_{k}\gamma_{N}(p)-p^{\otimes k}\|_{\rm TV}\;\leqslant\;\frac{C_{0}\,k}{N},

for some constant
C_{0}=C_{0}(p)
, where
\|\cdot\|_{\rm TV}
denotes the total variation distance.
Clearly, our reference product measure
\pi\in\mathcal{P}(\Omega)
is irreducible. In fact,
\gamma(\pi,\rho_{N})
is the
uniform probability measure on

\Omega_{\rho_{N}}
, for any admissible sequence
\rho_{N}
. In particular, it follows that its
k
-particle marginals converge to
\pi^{\otimes k}
as
N\to\infty
. One can also show that
\gamma(p,\rho_{N})
is
entropically
p
-chaotic
in the sense defined in
[
8
]
, namely that on top of the convergence of marginals one also has

(1.13)

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}\,H_{N}(\gamma(p,\rho_{N})\,|\,\gamma(\pi,\rho_{N}))=H(p\,|\,\pi),

where
H(\cdot\,|\,\cdot)
,
H_{N}(\cdot\,|\,\cdot)
denote respectively the relative entropy for probability measures on
\Omega
and on
\Omega^{N}
, see Proposition
2.8
below. Let us recall the following standard definition.

Definition 1.4

(Propagation of chaos)
.

Let
\mu_{N,t}=\mu_{N}e^{t\mathcal{L}_{N}}
,
t\;\geqslant\;0
, denote the evolution of an initial symmetric distribution
\mu_{N}\in\mathcal{P}(\Omega^{N})
under the Markov process generated by
\mathcal{L}_{N}
. Suppose that
\mu_{N}
is
p
-chaotic for some
p\in\mathcal{P}(\Omega)
and let
p_{t}
denote the evolution of the initial datum
p
under the nonlinear process (
1.3
). If
\mu_{N,t}
is
p_{t}
-chaotic for all fixed
t\;\geqslant\;0
, then we say that propagation of chaos holds. If the weak convergence
P_{k}\mu_{N,t}\longrightarrow p_{t}^{\otimes k}
,
N\to\infty
, holds uniformly in
t\;\geqslant\;0
we say that propagation of chaos holds uniformly in time.

An adaptation of well known arguments, see e.g.
[
25
,
41
]
, shows that the propagation of chaos (at fixed times) holds in our setting. In fact, the proof of this does not require the assumption that
\nu
is separating.

1.4.
Uniform in time propagation of chaos

Our first main result concerns the validity of propagation of chaos uniformly in time, with quantitative bounds on the convergence as
N\to\infty
.

Theorem 1.5
.

Assume that
\nu
is separating. The propagation of chaos holds uniformly in time, that is for any
p\in\mathcal{P}(\Omega)
, if
\mu_{N}
is
p-
chaotic, then for all fixed
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
, as
N\to\infty
,

P_{k}\mu_{N,t}\longrightarrow p_{t}^{\otimes k},\qquad\;\text{uniformly in $t\;\geqslant\;0$},

where
p_{t}
is the solution to the nonlinear equation (
1.3
) with initial datum
p_{0}=p
.
Moreover, if
\mu_{N}
is the canonical tensor product
\mu_{N}=\gamma_{N}(p)
, and
p\in\mathcal{P}(\Omega)
is irreducible, then

(1.14)

\displaystyle\|P_{k}\mu_{N,t}-p_{t}^{\otimes k}\|_{\rm TV}\;\leqslant\;\frac{C}{\sqrt{N}},

for some constant
C=C(k,p)>0
independent of
t,N
.

Remark 1.6
.

Concerning the dependency on
N
it may be that the optimal decay in (
1.14
) is
O(1/N)
rather than
O(1/\sqrt{N})
. This seems natural in light of our estimate (
1.12
) at time zero.
Moreover, that would be in agreement with the recent results in
[
27
]
, where the
O(1/N)
bound is obtained for a class of interacting diffusion processes at fixed times. The value of the constant
C
in (
1.14
) can be in principle obtained from our more detailed results in Theorem
3.1
below. However, we have not tried to optimize the dependency of
C
on
k,p
.

There are by now several results for kinetic models and for mean field diffusions establishing uniform in time propagation of chaos, see
[
13
,
10
,
17
,
11
]
.
However, the adaptation to our setting of the different techniques used in these works does not seem to be straightforward. The proof of Theorem
1.5
is based on some new contractive estimates for the nonlinear model that allow us to implement the main strategy developed in the groundbreaking work of Mischler and Mouhot
[
33
]
, see Section
3
.

1.5.
Entropy production for generalized random transpositions

Our second main result is about quantitative estimates on the decay to equilibrium for the particle system introduced above. The key feature is that these estimates hold
uniformly in the number of particles

N
. We shall actually derive such estimates in the context of the
generalized random transposition process
defined as follows.

Let
\mathcal{S}_{N,n}=S_{N}^{n}
denote the
n
-fold product of the symmetric group
S_{N}
of the permutations of
[N]=\{1,\dots,N\}
. Then
\eta\in\mathcal{S}_{N,n}
is a matrix
\eta_{i}(j)
,
i\in[n]
,
j\in[N]
, where each
\eta(j)=(\eta_{1}(j),\dots,\eta_{n}(j))\in[N]^{n}
is seen as a particle, and each
\eta_{i}=(\eta_{i}(1),\dots,\eta_{i}(N))\in S_{N}
is a permutation of
[N]
. Note that
\mathcal{S}_{N,n}=\Omega_{\rho}
where
\Omega_{\rho}
is defined in (
1.10
) when we take the extreme case
q_{i}\equiv N-1
,
\rho_{i,x}\equiv 1/N
for all
i=1,\dots,n
.

The generalized random transposition (GRT) process is defined as the process generated by the operator
\mathcal{L}_{N}
in this setup, namely the GRT process is the continuous time Markov process with state space
\mathcal{S}_{N,n}
described as follows: every pair of particles
\{\eta(l),\eta(j)\}
collides with rate
1/N
independently, and when a collision occurs, a new set
A
is sampled according to
\nu
and the
A
-content of
\eta(l),\eta(j)
is exchanged.

This setting is convenient for proving functional inequalities since by restricting to classes of functions with suitable symmetries we then recover all possible cases of processes on
\Omega_{\rho_{N}}
with generator
\mathcal{L}_{N}
, for all admissible
\rho_{N}
, see Remark
1.9
below for more details. As an example, consider the case
q_{i}\equiv 1
and suppose that
N(\rho_{N})_{i,1}=N_{i}
for some positive integers
N_{i}
,
i=1,\dots,n
. Here the process generated by
\mathcal{L}_{N}
can be seen as the GRT process restricted to functions
f:\mathcal{S}_{N,n}\mapsto{\mathbb{R}}
such that, for each
i
,
f
only depends on
(\eta_{i}(1),\dots,\eta_{i}(N))
through the unordered set
\{\eta_{i}(1),\dots,\eta_{i}(N_{i})\}
.
This can be seen as a generalized Bernoulli-Laplace process
[
14
]
.
If no confusion arises we continue to write
\mathcal{L}_{N}
for the generator of the GRT.

We remark that when
n=1
, GRT is just the usual random transposition process
[
15
]
, and that when
\nu
gives positive weight only to
A\subset[n]
such that
|A|=1
, it describes
n
independent random transposition processes. However, in the general case, the recombination measure
\nu
dynamically couples the permutations and the GRT becomes a nontrivial generalization of the standard random transpositions.

In order to guarantee the irreducibility of the GRT process, we make the following assumption on the recombination measure
\nu
, which is easily seen to be stronger than the separation assumption
r(\nu)<1
; see also Remark
1.10
.

Definition 1.7
.

We say that
\nu
is strictly separating if for all
i\in[n]
there exists
A\subset[n]
such that
i\in A
and such that both
A
and
A\setminus\{i\}
have positive
\nu
-probability.

Note that the uniform crossover and the one-point crossover are both strictly separating.
Let
\pi_{N}
denote the uniform distribution on
\mathcal{S}_{N,n}
. The GRT process is reversible with respect to
\pi_{N}
and if the measure
\nu
is strictly separating, then it is also irreducible, and any initial distribution converges to
\pi_{N}
as
t\to\infty
. To quantify this statement we consider the Dirichlet form of the GRT, defined by

(1.15)

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{N,n}(f,g)=\frac{1}{2N}\sum_{1\;\leqslant\;j<l\;\leqslant\;N}\sum_{A\subset[n]}\nu(A)\sum_{\eta\in\mathcal{S}_{N,n}}\pi_{N}(\eta)\left(f(\eta^{j,l,A})-f(\eta)\right)\left(g(\eta^{j,l,A})-g(\eta)\right),

where
f,g:\mathcal{S}_{N,n}\mapsto{\mathbb{R}}
.
The
entropy production rate
is measured by the constant

(1.16)

\displaystyle\alpha(N,n)=\inf_{f>0}\frac{\mathcal{E}_{N,n}(f,\log f)}{{\rm Ent}(f)},

where the infimum is over
f:\mathcal{S}_{N,n}\mapsto{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
such that
{\rm Ent}(f)\neq 0
and

{\rm Ent}(f)=\pi_{N}(f\log f)-\pi_{N}(f)\log\pi_{N}(f)

is the entropy of
f
w.r.t.
\pi_{N}
.
Equivalently,
\alpha(N,n)
is the best constant

\alpha
such that the inequality

(1.17)

\displaystyle{\rm Ent}(e^{t\mathcal{L}_{N}}f)\;\leqslant\;e^{-\alpha t}{\rm Ent}(f)

holds for all functions
f>0
; see e.g.
[
14
,
4
]
. Note that when
\pi_{N}(f)=1
then, for all
t\;\geqslant\;0
,
{\rm Ent}(e^{t\mathcal{L}_{N}}f)
coincides with the relative entropy
H_{N}(\mu_{N,t}\,|\,\pi_{N})

where
\mu_{N,t}=(e^{t\mathcal{L}_{N}}f)\pi_{N}
.

We also consider the entropy production rate restricted to the set of symmetric functions defined as follows. Let
{\mathbb{S}}
denote the set of
f:\Omega^{N}\mapsto{\mathbb{R}}
such that

f(\eta)=\frac{1}{N!}\sum_{\tau\in S_{N}}f(\tau\circ\eta),

where the sum runs over all permutations
\tau\in S_{N}
and
\tau\circ\eta
denotes the configuration with particles exchanged according to
\tau
, that is
\tau\circ\eta(j)=\eta(\tau(j))
.
From the point of view of Kac’s program
[
25
]
,
{\mathbb{S}}
is the relevant space of observables in the particle system. We call
\alpha_{{\mathbb{S}}}(N,n)
the constant defined as in (
1.16
), with the infimum restricted to positive functions
f\in{\mathbb{S}}
.

Our main results for the GRT process are the following estimates independent of
N
.

Theorem 1.8
.

Fix
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
and assume that
\nu
is strictly separating. Then there exists
\alpha(\nu)>0
such that for any
N\in{\mathbb{N}}
,
N\;\geqslant\;2
,

(1.18)

\alpha(N,n)\;\geqslant\;\alpha(\nu).

Moreover, if
\nu
is the one-point crossover, then

(1.19)

\alpha(N,n)\;\geqslant\;\frac{1}{4(n+1)}\,,

and if
\nu
is the uniform crossover, then

(1.20)

\alpha(N,n)\;\geqslant\;\frac{1}{4n}\,,\qquad\alpha_{\mathbb{S}}(N,n)\;\geqslant\;\frac{1}{2(n+2)}\,.

Remark 1.9
.

Consider the
entropy production rate
\alpha(\Omega_{\rho_{N}})
for the process on
\Omega_{\rho_{N}}
associated to any admissible density sequence
\rho_{N}
. The uniform distribution on
\Omega_{\rho_{N}}
is the image of the probability
\pi_{N}
(the uniform distribution on
\mathcal{S}_{N,n}
) under a simple symmetrization procedure, and the quantity
\alpha(\Omega_{\rho_{N}})
can be defined as in
(
1.16
) by restricting to invariant classes of functions with suitable symmetries; see e.g.
[
2
, Section 4.2.3]
for a version of this simple projection argument.
The estimates of Theorem
1.8
then immediately provide the lower bound

(1.21)

\alpha(\Omega_{\rho_{N}})\;\geqslant\;\alpha(N,n)\;\geqslant\;\alpha(\nu)\,,

for any admissible density sequence
\rho_{N}
.

Remark 1.10
.

The statement in Theorem
1.8
implies exponentially fast convergence to stationarity for the GRT under the strict separation assumption, see (
1.17
). In particular, it implies irreducibility. If the recombination measure
\nu
is only assumed to be separating, then the GRT process may fail to be irreducible. Therefore, some assumption such as the strict separation defined above is necessary for the statement in Theorem
1.8
. For an example of non irreducible process with separating
\nu
consider
N=2,n=4
and suppose
\nu(A)=\frac{1}{6}
for all
A\subset[4]
with
|A|=2
. Clearly,
\nu
is separating, but if we consider the initial configuration
\eta
with
\eta(1)=0000,\eta(2)=1111
, then the number of
1
’s in each particle remains even at all times.

The proof of Theorem
1.8
will be based on some new functional inequalities for permutations which imply a modified logarithmic Sobolev inequality for the GRT. We refer to Section
4
.

Concerning upper bounds on the constant
\alpha(N,n)
we establish an estimate valid for arbitrary
\nu
, which essentially shows that
\alpha(N,n)
cannot be larger than
4/n
for
n
large, provided
N
is taken large enough, possibly depending on
n
.

Proposition 1.11
.

For any
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
, any distribution
\nu
on
[n]
,

(1.22)

\limsup_{N\to\infty}\alpha(N,n)\;\leqslant\;\frac{4}{n}+O\left(\frac{1}{n^{2}}\right).

In this sense, the bounds in (
1.19
) and (
1.20
) can be considered to be optimal.

1.6.
Kac’s program completed

One of the main motivations behind Kac’s program is the derivation of quantitative bounds on the speed of convergence to equilibrium for the nonlinear equation. In our setting, as a corollary of our analysis we obtain the following relative entropy estimates. We refer to
[
43
,
8
]
for a discussion of related entropy decay estimates in the context of kinetic models. In particular, in our setup, one can say that Cercignani’s conjecture holds true. See also
[
19
]
for related results in a discrete setting under positive curvature assumptions.

Theorem 1.12
.

Assume that
\nu
is strictly separating. For any
p\in\mathcal{P}(\Omega)
, let
p_{t}
denote the solution of (
1.3
) with
p_{0}=p
and let
\pi=\otimes_{i=1}^{n}p_{i}
denote the associated equilibrium. Then for all
t\;\geqslant\;0
,

(1.23)

\displaystyle H(p_{t}\,|\,\pi)\;\leqslant\;e^{-\alpha(\nu)t}H(p\,|\,\pi),

where
\alpha(\nu)>0
is the constant in Theorem
1.8
. In particular,
\alpha(\nu)\;\geqslant\;1/4(n+1)
for one-point crossover, and
\alpha(\nu)\;\geqslant\;1/2(n+2)
for uniform crossover.

It is interesting to note that the constant
\alpha(\nu)
does not depend on the initial datum
p
in any way. We point out that, in the case of the one-point crossover and uniform crossover, the above estimates were already obtained in
[
7
]
by direct analysis of the entropy production functional of the nonlinear equation, with a slightly better constant actually:
\alpha(\nu)\;\geqslant\;1/(n+1)
in both cases. Moreover,
[
7
]
also shows that the
1/n
decay of the constant
\alpha(\nu)
in these cases is optimal up to a constant independent of
n
. Besides extending the bounds of
[
7
]
to all strictly separating distribution
\nu
, an interesting feature of Theorem
1.12
is that its proof takes a completely different route. Namely, it is based on the implementation in our setting of Kac’s original idea. More precisely, (
1.23
) is derived from the uniform control on entropy production provided by Theorem
1.8
, see also Remark
1.9
, together with the approximation, as
N\to\infty
, of both
H(p\,|\,\pi)
and
H(p_{t}\,|\,\pi)
in terms of the corresponding entropies for the
N
-particle system.

We also obtain the following general bounds on the convergence to equilibrium for the nonlinear chain. Recall the definition (
1.4
) of the constant
r(\nu)\in(0,1)
.

Theorem 1.13
.

Assume that
\nu
is separating. For any
p\in\mathcal{P}(\Omega)
, let
p_{t}
denote the solution of (
1.3
) with
p_{0}=p
and let
\pi=\otimes_{i=1}^{n}p_{i}
denote the associated equilibrium. Then for all
t\;\geqslant\;0
,

(1.24)

\displaystyle H(p_{t}\,|\,\pi)\;\leqslant\;\tfrac{1}{2}\,n(n-1)\,H(p\,|\,\pi)\,e^{-D(\nu)\,t},

where
D(\nu):=1-r(\nu)
.
Moreover, for the total variation distance we have

(1.25)

\displaystyle\|p_{t}-\pi\|_{\rm TV}\;\leqslant\;\tfrac{1}{2}\,n(n-1)\,\|p-\pi\|_{\rm TV}\,e^{-D(\nu)\,t}.

We note that an estimate similar to (
1.25
) was obtained in
[
38
]
for a discrete time version of the nonlinear process. To prove Theorem
1.13
we use a coupling argument similar to that of
[
38
]
, together with an explicit construction of the continuous time solution
p_{t}
in terms of all possible collision histories, which goes back to the pioneering works of Wild
[
44
]
and McKean
[
30
,
31
]
, see also
[
9
]
. It is interesting to note that in the case of uniform crossover one has
r(\nu)=1/2
and thus (
1.24
) provides an exponential decay which is much faster, as
n
becomes large, than the one provided by (
1.23
). Moreover, as mentioned, the
1/n
rate is known to be optimal up to a constant independent of
n
for the estimate (
1.23
). This mismatch can be explained by observing that, because of the possibly large prefactor, (
1.25
) only provides information about the large time behavior while
(
1.23
) expresses a contraction property of the relative entropy at all times, and that some particular initial distributions
p
may have a slow start in the relative entropy decay; see Lemma
4.5
below for a concrete example.

Finally, we remark that the rate of exponential decay
D(\nu)=1-r(\nu)
in Theorem
1.13
is optimal, in the sense that
t^{-1}\log\|p_{t}-\pi\|_{\rm TV}
, as
t\to\infty
, cannot be smaller than
-D(\nu)
, see Remark
3.4
. We refer to
[
16
]
for a related result on the optimal rate of decay in the context of Kac model.

2.
Local Central Limit Theorem and Chaos

A probability measure
p\in\mathcal{P}(\Omega)
induces a probability
\mu
on
X:=\{0,1\}^{K}
where
K=\sum_{i=1}^{n}q_{i}
, via the map

(2.1)

\displaystyle\sigma\in\Omega\mapsto\xi_{i,x}=\mathbf{1}(\sigma_{i}=x)\,,\qquad i=1,\dots,n\,;\;x\in\{1,\dots,q_{i}\}.

That is,
\mu
is the push forward of
p
by the above map.
Note that we did not include the indicator variable
\mathbf{1}(\sigma_{i}=0)
since this is uniquely determined as the indicator of the event
\xi_{i,x}=0
for all
x\in\{1,\dots,q_{i}\}
. When
q_{i}=1
for all
i
, then
\Omega=\{0,1\}^{n}
can be identified with
X
,
\sigma
with
\xi
, and
\mu
with
p
.

2.1.
Central limit theorem

The next results are concerned with the behavior of the sum of independent copies
\xi(1),\dots,\xi(N)
of a random variable
\xi
with values in
X
and distribution
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(X)
:

S_{N}=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\xi(j).

Thus,
S_{N}
is a random vector in
\{0,\dots,N\}^{K}
. We use the notation

\langle t,s\rangle=\sum_{i,x}t_{i,x}s_{i,x}
if
t
and
s
are indexed by
i=1,\dots,n
and
x=1,\dots,q_{i}
.
We call
V_{1}
the covariance matrix of
\mu
,

V_{1}(i,x;i^{\prime},x^{\prime})=\mu(\xi_{i,x}\xi_{i^{\prime},x^{\prime}})-\mu(\xi_{i,x})\mu(\xi_{i^{\prime},x^{\prime}}),\qquad i=1,\dots,n\,,\;x=1,\dots,q_{i}

Thus
V_{1}
is a symmetric nonnegative definite
K\times K
matrix. If
\det V_{1}\neq 0
we say that
\mu
is
nondegenerate
. The central limit theorem asserts that if
\mu
is nondegenerate, then as
N\to\infty
one has the weak convergence

(2.2)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\;V_{1}^{-1/2}\left(S_{N}-\mu^{\otimes N}(S_{N})\right)\;\longrightarrow\;N(0,\mathbf{1}_{K})\,,

where
\mu^{\otimes N}(S_{N})\in[0,N]^{K}
is the mean of the vector
S_{N}
under the product measure
\mu^{\otimes N}
, and
\mathbf{1}_{K}
denotes the
K\times K
identity matrix, so that
N(0,\mathbf{1}_{K})
is the standard normal in
K
dimensions. Note that when
\mu
is induced by a measure
p\in\mathcal{P}(\Omega)
as described in (
2.1
), then
\mu^{\otimes N}(S_{N})_{i,x}=N\pi_{i,x}
for all
i,x
, where
\pi_{i,x}
are the marginals of
p
.

The statement (
2.2
) clearly requires that
\mu
is nondegenerate. However, one can obtain similar statements in the case of degenerate measures, provided one reduces to the nondegenerate modes by eliminating the degenerate ones. More precisely, one can take the eigenvectors of
V_{1}
with nonzero eigenvalues as the new variables. A simple example is obtained if e.g.
q_{i}\equiv 1
and
\mu
gives probability
1/2
to all
1
’s and probability
1/2
to all
0
’s. Here one simply removes all variables but one.

2.2.
Local central limit theorem

We will need a local version of the central limit theorem. For this we assume the following stronger notion of nondegeneracy, which we refer to as irreducibility.

Definition 2.1
.

A measure
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(X)
is called irreducible if for all
i=1,\dots,n
, for all
x\in\{0,\dots,q_{i}\}
, there exists
\xi\in X
such that
\mu(\xi)
and
\mu(\xi^{(i,x)})
are both positive, where
\xi^{(i,x)}
denotes the vector
\xi
with the
(i,x)
-th coordinate flipped, that is
\xi^{(i,x)}_{j,y}=\xi_{j,y}
for all
(j,y)\neq(i,x)
, and
\xi^{(i,x)}_{i,x}=1-\xi_{i,x}
.

It is immediate to check that if
p\in\mathcal{P}(\Omega)
is irreducible in the sense of Definition
1.3
then the measure
\mu
induced on
X
by
p
as in (
2.1
) is irreducible in the sense of Definition
2.1
.

Proposition 2.2
.

Suppose
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(X)
is irreducible. Then there exists a finite constant
C=C(\mu)
such that for all
N\in{\mathbb{N}}
,

(2.3)

\displaystyle\max_{M_{N}}\;\left|\;\mu^{\otimes N}\left(S_{N}=M_{N}\right)\;-\;\frac{e^{-\frac{1}{2}\,\langle z_{N},z_{N}\rangle}}{(2\pi N)^{K/2}\sqrt{\det V_{1}}}\;\right|\;\leqslant\;\frac{C}{N^{(K+1)/2}},

where

z_{N}:=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\;V_{1}^{-1/2}\left(M_{N}-\mu^{\otimes N}(S_{N})\right),

and the maximum is over all possible values
M_{N}\in\{0,\dots,N\}^{K}
.

Noting that
\Omega_{\rho_{N}}=\{S_{N}=M_{N}\}
with
M_{N}=N\rho_{N}
, and that in this case

\langle z_{N},z_{N}\rangle=N\langle\rho_{N}-\pi,V_{1}^{-1}(\rho_{N}-\pi)\rangle,

the following is an immediate corollary of Proposition
2.2
.

Corollary 2.3
.

Suppose
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(X)
is irreducible, and let
\rho_{N}
be an admissible sequence
such that

(2.4)

\displaystyle\langle\rho_{N}-\pi,\rho_{N}-\pi\rangle=O(1/N),

where
\pi=\mu(\xi)
is the vector of the expected values of
\mu
.
Then there exists a constant
c=c(\mu)>0
such that for
N
sufficiently large

(2.5)

\displaystyle\mu^{\otimes N}\left(\Omega_{\rho_{N}}\right)\;\geqslant\;\frac{c}{N^{K/2}}.

In particular,

(2.6)

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}\,\log\mu^{\otimes N}\left(\Omega_{\rho_{N}}\right)=0.

We note that the condition (
2.4
) corresponds to “normal” fluctuations
\langle z_{N},z_{N}\rangle=O(1)
, and that the corollary applies, in particular, to the canonical sequence
\rho_{N}=\rho^{\pi}
defined in (
1.11
), since
\langle\rho^{\pi}-\pi,\rho^{\pi}-\pi\rangle=O(1/N^{2})
in that case.

The proof of Proposition
2.2
will be given in the appendix. Here we pause for some remarks on the assumptions we made, and then discuss the main applications to chaos.

Lemma 2.4
.

If
\mu
is irreducible then it is nondegenerate. The converse does not hold.

Proof.

If
\mu
is degenerate, then for any fixed
(i,x)
, the variable
\xi_{i,x}
can be written
\mu
-a.s. as a nontrivial linear combination of the other variables
\xi_{j,y}
,
(j,y)\neq(i,x)
. In particular, the value of
\xi_{i,x}
is
\mu
-a.s. determined by the other variables. But this is not possible if
\mu
is irreducible since by assumption there is always at least one value of all the other variables for which both values
\xi_{i,x}=0,1
happen with positive
\mu
probability. This proves the first assertion. To violate the converse, consider the following example:
n=3
,
q_{i}\equiv 1
, so that
X=\{0,1\}^{3}
and suppose that
\mu
gives probability
1/4
to the following four configurations
101,110,011,000
, and probability 0 to the four remaining configurations. Then one checks that
V_{1}=\frac{1}{4}\mathbf{1}_{3}
. In particular,
\mu
is nondegenerate. However,
\mu
is not irreducible since the condition in Definition
2.1
is violated at
i=1
. ∎

Let us remark that some irreducibility assumption is necessary for the local CLT statement in Proposition
2.2
. Consider the same counterexample from the proof of Lemma
2.4
. In this case one checks easily that if the first component of
S_{N}
is even, then the sum of the remaining two components must be even as well. This shows that the event
S_{N}=M_{N}
has probability zero for many admissible sequences such that Corollary
2.3
would predict
\mu^{\otimes N}\left(S_{N}=M_{N}\right)>0
. Thus, Proposition
2.2
does not hold for all nondegenerate
\mu
. The next lemma elucidates the role of the irreducibility assumption.

Lemma 2.5
.

Suppose
\mu
is irreducible.
Then there exists a constant
c=c(\mu)>0
such that the characteristic function
\psi(t)=\mu(e^{i\langle t,\xi\rangle})
,
t\in{\mathbb{R}}^{K}
, satisfies

(2.7)

\displaystyle|\psi(t)|\;\leqslant\;e^{-c\langle t,t\rangle}\,,\qquad\text{for all\;\;}t\in[-\pi,\pi]^{K}.

Proof.

We write

(2.8)

\displaystyle|\psi(t)|^{2}=\left|\mu\left[e^{i\langle t,\xi\rangle}\right]\right|^{2}

\displaystyle=\mu\left[\cos\langle t,\xi\rangle\right]^{2}+\mu\left[\sin\langle t,\xi\rangle\right]^{2}=\sum_{\xi,\xi^{\prime}\in X}\mu(\xi)\mu(\xi^{\prime})\cos\langle t,\xi-\xi^{\prime}\rangle,

where the last equation uses the identity
\cos(\alpha-\beta)=\sin(\alpha)\sin(\beta)+\cos(\alpha)\cos(\beta).

If
|\theta|\;\leqslant\;\pi
, then
\cos(\theta)\;\leqslant\;1-2\theta^{2}/\pi^{2}
, and therefore,

(2.9)

\cos\langle t,\xi-\xi^{\prime}\rangle\;\leqslant\;\begin{cases}1-\frac{2\langle t,\xi-\xi^{\prime}\rangle^{2}}{\pi^{2}}&\text{if}\ |\xi-\xi^{\prime}|_{1}=1\\
1&\text{if}\ |\xi-\xi^{\prime}|_{1}\neq 1\end{cases}

where
|\xi-\xi^{\prime}|_{1}=\sum_{i,x}|\xi_{i,x}-\xi_{i,x}^{\prime}|
. Since
|\xi-\xi^{\prime}|_{1}=1
iff
\xi^{\prime}=\xi^{(i,x)}
for some
i,x
,

(2.10)

\displaystyle|\psi(t)|^{2}

\displaystyle\;\leqslant\;1-\frac{2}{\pi^{2}}\sum_{i,x}\sum_{\xi\in X}\mu(\xi)\mu(\xi^{(i,x)})\,t_{i,x}^{2}\;\leqslant\;1-2c\langle t,t\rangle,

where

c:=\frac{1}{\pi^{2}}\;\inf_{i,x}\sum_{\xi\in X}\mu(\xi)\mu(\xi^{(i,x)}).

The irreducibility of
\mu
is equivalent to
c>0
.
Using
x\;\leqslant\;e^{\frac{1}{2}(x^{2}-1)}
,
x\in[0,1]
, with
x=|\psi(t)|
, we conclude

(2.11)

\displaystyle|\psi(t)|\;\leqslant\;e^{-c\,\langle t,t\rangle}.

∎

We turn to the applications to Kac chaos and entropic chaos.

2.3.
Kac chaos

Recall the definition of
\gamma(p,\rho_{N})
and of the canonical tensor product
\gamma_{N}(p)
in
Definition
1.2
.

Theorem 2.6
.

Suppose
p\in\mathcal{P}(\Omega)
is irreducible and let
\rho_{N}
be an admissible sequence
such that

(2.12)

\displaystyle\langle\rho_{N}-\pi,\rho_{N}-\pi\rangle=O(1/N).

Then for all
k=1,\dots,N
,

(2.13)

\displaystyle\|P_{k}\gamma(p,\rho_{N})-p^{\otimes k}\|_{\rm TV}\;\leqslant\;\frac{C\,k}{\sqrt{N}},

for some constant
C=C(p)
.
Moreover, when
\rho_{N}=\rho^{\pi}
, the canonical tensor product
\gamma_{N}(p)
satisfies
the stronger estimate

(2.14)

\displaystyle\|P_{k}\gamma_{N}(p)-p^{\otimes k}\|_{\rm TV}\;\leqslant\;\frac{C\,k}{N}.

Proof.

We prove (
2.13
) first, and then show how to obtain (
2.14
).
Let
\hat{\xi}_{i,x}(j)=\xi_{i,x}(j)-(\rho_{N})_{i,x}
.
We use the shorthand notation
\gamma_{N}=\gamma(p,\rho_{N})
and
\mu_{N}=p^{\otimes N}
.
For any
f:\Omega^{N}\mapsto{\mathbb{R}}
we have

(2.15)

\displaystyle\gamma_{N}(f)-\mu_{N}(f)=\frac{\mu_{N}(f(\mathbf{1}_{\Omega_{\rho_{N}}}-\mu_{N}(\Omega_{\rho_{N}}))}{\mu_{N}(\Omega_{\rho_{N}})},

Since
\Omega_{\rho_{N}}=\{S_{N}=N\rho_{N}\}
, using the Fourier transform we write

(2.16)

\displaystyle\mu_{N}(\Omega_{\rho_{N}})=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{K}}\int_{[-\pi,\pi]^{K}}dt\,\mu_{N}\left(e^{i\langle t,\hat{S}_{N}\rangle}\right),

where
\hat{S}_{N}=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\hat{\xi}(j)
.
Set
V_{N}:=NV_{1}
. The product structure of
\mu_{N}
and the change of variables
s=V_{N}^{1/2}t=\sqrt{N}V_{1}^{1/2}t\,
imply

(2.17)

\displaystyle\mu_{N}(\Omega_{\rho_{N}})=\frac{1}{B_{N}(2\pi)^{K}}\int_{Q_{N,K}}ds\,\mu_{N}\left(e^{i\langle V_{N}^{-1/2}s,\hat{\xi}(1)\rangle}\right)^{N},

where
Q_{N,K}=V_{N}^{1/2}[-\pi,\pi]^{K}
and
B_{N}=\sqrt{\det V_{N}}=N^{K/2}\sqrt{\det V_{1}}
.

In the same way, for any
f=f(\xi(1),\dots,\xi(k))
, we have

(2.18)

\displaystyle\mu_{N}(f\,\mathbf{1}_{\Omega_{\rho_{N}}})=\frac{1}{B_{N}(2\pi)^{K}}\int_{Q_{N,K}}ds\,\mu_{N}\left(e^{i\langle V_{N}^{-1/2}s,\hat{\xi}(1)\rangle}\right)^{N-k}\mu_{N}\left(f\,e^{i\langle V_{N}^{-1/2}s,\hat{S}_{k}\rangle}\right).

In conclusion, we have

(2.19)

\displaystyle\gamma_{N}(f)-\mu_{N}(f)=\frac{\int_{Q_{N,K}}ds\,\psi_{N}(s)^{N-k}\mu_{N}\left(f;\,e^{i\langle V_{N}^{-1/2}s,\hat{S}_{k}\rangle}\right)}{\int_{Q_{N,K}}ds\,\psi_{N}(s)^{N}\,},

where

\psi_{N}(s)=\mu_{N}\left(e^{i\langle V_{N}^{-1/2}s,\hat{\xi}(1)\rangle}\right),

and we use the notation
\mu_{N}(f;g)=\mu_{N}(fg)-\mu_{N}(f)\mu_{N}(g)
for the covariance of
f,g
. From Corollary
2.3
we known that (
2.17
) is at least
cN^{-K/2}
, and thus the denominator in (
2.19
) is at least some constant
c^{\prime}>0
.
Therefore, it suffices to show that the numerator is bounded by

(2.20)

\displaystyle\int_{Q_{N,K}}ds\,|\psi_{N}(s)|^{N-k}\left|\mu_{N}\left(f;\,e^{i\langle V_{N}^{-1/2}s,\hat{S}_{k}\rangle}\right)\right|\;\leqslant\;C\,|f|_{\infty}\,\frac{k}{\sqrt{N}}.

From Lemma
2.5
we know that
|\psi_{N}(s)|\;\leqslant\;e^{-a\langle s,s\rangle/N}
for some constant
a=a(p)>0
. Notice that we can assume without loss of generality that
k\;\leqslant\;N/2
, since otherwise the result (
2.13
) is trivial. Thus
|\psi_{N}(s)|^{N-k}\;\leqslant\;e^{-a\langle s,s\rangle/2}
and it is sufficient to show that

(2.21)

\displaystyle\left|\mu_{N}\left(f;\,e^{i\langle V_{N}^{-1/2}s,\hat{S}_{k}\rangle}\right)\right|\;\leqslant\;C\,|f|_{\infty}\,\frac{k}{\sqrt{N}}\,\langle s,s\rangle,

for all
s\in Q_{N,K}
. Recalling that
|e^{i\theta}-1|\;\leqslant\;|\theta|
,
\theta\in{\mathbb{R}}
, and using Schwarz’ inequality,

(2.22)

\displaystyle|\mu_{N}\left(f;\,e^{i\langle V_{N}^{-1/2}s,\hat{S}_{k}\rangle}\right)|\;\leqslant\;\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\,|f|_{\infty}\,\mu_{N}\left(\langle V_{1}^{-1/2}s,\hat{S}_{k}\rangle^{2}\right).

Now we observe that

\displaystyle\mu_{N}\left(\langle V_{1}^{-1/2}s,\hat{S}_{k}\rangle^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=k^{2}\langle V_{1}^{-1/2}s,\pi-\rho_{N}\rangle^{2}+k\langle s,s\rangle

(2.23)

\displaystyle\;\leqslant\;k^{2}\langle s,V_{1}^{-1}s\rangle\langle\pi-\rho_{N},\pi-\rho_{N}\rangle+k\langle s,s\rangle\;\leqslant\;Ck\langle s,s\rangle,

where we use
\mu_{N}(\hat{S}_{k})=k\,(\pi-\rho_{N})
, the independence of the
\hat{\xi}(j)
, and
\langle\pi-\rho_{N},\pi-\rho_{N}\rangle\;\leqslant\;C/k
which follows from the assumption
\langle\pi-\rho_{N},\pi-\rho_{N}\rangle=O(1/N)
.
This proves (
2.13
).

To prove (
2.14
), note that it is sufficient to prove (
2.21
) with
\sqrt{N}
replaced by
N
in the right hand side. For this, we are going to use the fact that
\langle\pi-\rho_{N},\pi-\rho_{N}\rangle=O(1/N^{2})
when
\rho_{N}=\rho^{\pi}
, see (
1.11
).
Let us first consider the function
\tilde{f}=f-g
, where

(2.24)

\displaystyle g(\xi(1),\dots,\xi(k))=\frac{1}{k}\langle V_{1}^{-1}v,\hat{S}_{k}\rangle,\qquad v=\mu_{N}(f;\hat{S}_{k}).

The function
g
can be seen as a linear approximation of
f
. Notice that

(2.25)

\displaystyle\mu_{N}\left(f-g\,\,;\,\langle V_{N}^{-1/2}s,\hat{S}_{k}\rangle\right)=0.

Indeed, by independence
\mu_{N}((\hat{S}_{k})_{i,x};(\hat{S}_{k})_{j,y})=kV_{1}(i,x;j,y)
, and therefore for all
s
,

(2.26)

\displaystyle\mu_{N}\left(g\,;\,\langle V_{N}^{-1/2}s,\hat{S}_{k}\rangle\right)=\langle v,V_{N}^{-1/2}s\rangle=\mu_{N}\left(f\,;\,\langle V_{N}^{-1/2}s,\hat{S}_{k}\rangle\right).

Recalling that
|e^{i\theta}-1-i\theta|=|R(\theta)|\;\leqslant\;\frac{1}{2}\theta^{2}
,
\theta\in{\mathbb{R}}
, from (
2.25
) we have

(2.27)

\displaystyle\left|\mu_{N}\left(f-g\,;\,e^{i\langle V_{N}^{-1/2}s,\hat{S}_{k}\rangle}\right)\right|^{2}\;\leqslant\;\frac{1}{N^{2}}\,\left(\var(f)+\var(g)\right)\mu_{N}\left(\langle V_{1}^{-1/2}s,\hat{S}_{k}\rangle^{4}\right)\,,

where we use the inequality
|\mu_{N}(f-g;R)|^{2}\;\leqslant\;2\left(\var(f)+\var(g)\right)\mu_{N}(R^{2})
, and we use
\var
for the variance w.r.t.
\mu_{N}
. Next, observe that
\var(f)\;\leqslant\;|f|_{\infty}^{2}
and

\var(g)=\frac{1}{k}\langle V^{-1}v,v\rangle\;\leqslant\;C\var(f)\;\leqslant\;C|f|_{\infty}^{2}.

We are going to show that

(2.28)

\displaystyle\mu_{N}\left(\langle V_{1}^{-1/2}s,\hat{S}_{k}\rangle^{4}\right)\;\leqslant\;Ck^{2}\langle s,s\rangle^{2}\,.

Suppose for a moment that (
2.28
) holds. Then we conclude that

(2.29)

\displaystyle\left|\mu_{N}\left(f-g\,;\,e^{i\langle V_{N}^{-1/2}s,\hat{S}_{k}\rangle}\right)\right|\;\leqslant\;C\,|f|_{\infty}\,\frac{k}{N}\,\langle s,s\rangle.

This proves
|\gamma_{N}(\tilde{f})-\mu_{N}(\tilde{f})|\;\leqslant\;C\,|f|_{\infty}\,k/N
.
However, noting that
\gamma_{N}(g)=0
, we have

(2.30)

\displaystyle|\gamma_{N}(f)-\mu_{N}(f)|\;\leqslant\;|\gamma_{N}(\tilde{f})-\mu_{N}(\tilde{f})|+|\mu_{N}(g)|.

The desired conclusion
|\gamma_{N}(f)-\mu_{N}(f)|\;\leqslant\;C\,|f|_{\infty}\,k/N
then follows from

(2.31)

\displaystyle|\mu_{N}(g)|\;\leqslant\;|\langle V_{1}^{-1}v,\pi-\rho^{\pi}\rangle|\;\leqslant\;\langle V_{1}^{-1}v,V_{1}^{-1}v\rangle^{1/2}\langle\pi-\rho^{\pi},\pi-\rho^{\pi}\rangle^{1/2}\;\leqslant\;\frac{C|f|_{\infty}k}{N},

where we use
\langle\pi-\rho^{\pi},\pi-\rho^{\pi}\rangle=O(1/N^{2})
, and
\langle V_{1}^{-1}v,V_{1}^{-1}v\rangle\;\leqslant\;Ck|f|_{\infty}
.

Thus, it remains to prove (
2.28
). Notice that it is sufficient to prove

(2.32)

\displaystyle\mu_{N}\left(\langle\hat{S}_{k},\hat{S}_{k}\rangle^{2}\right)\;\leqslant\;Ck^{2}\,.

We write
\tilde{\xi}=\hat{\xi}-\mu(\hat{\xi})
and
\tilde{S}_{k}=\hat{S}_{k}-\mu_{N}(\hat{S}_{k})
for the corresponding sums.
Then one checks that

\displaystyle\langle\hat{S}_{k},\hat{S}_{k}\rangle^{2}\;\leqslant\;\langle\tilde{S}_{k},\tilde{S}_{k}\rangle^{2}+Ck\langle\tilde{S}_{k},\tilde{S}_{k}\rangle^{3/2}\langle\pi-\rho^{\pi},\pi-\rho^{\pi}\rangle^{1/2}+Ck^{2}\langle\tilde{S}_{k},\tilde{S}_{k}\rangle\langle\pi-\rho^{\pi},\pi-\rho^{\pi}\rangle

(2.33)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad+Ck^{3}\langle\tilde{S}_{k},\tilde{S}_{k}\rangle^{1/2}\langle\pi-\rho^{\pi},\pi-\rho^{\pi}\rangle^{3/2}+Ck^{4}\langle\pi-\rho^{\pi},\pi-\rho^{\pi}\rangle^{2}\,.

Since
\langle\pi-\rho^{\pi},\pi-\rho^{\pi}\rangle=O(1/N^{2})
we can restrict to prove (
2.32
) for
\tilde{S}_{k}
instead of
\hat{S}_{k}
. Since
\tilde{\xi}
are centered the estimate follows easily by expanding
\langle\tilde{S}_{k},\tilde{S}_{k}\rangle^{2}
and observing that the dominant terms are of the form
\langle\tilde{\xi}(i),\tilde{\xi}(j)\rangle^{2}
, and their contribution is of order
k^{2}
. ∎

We notice that the trick of replacing
f
by
f-g
in the proof of Theorem
2.6
allowed us to obtain the decay rate
O(1/N)
instead of
O(1/\sqrt{N})
. This idea was used in
[
6
]
for the proof of a related “equivalence of ensembles” result.

Corollary 2.7
.

For any irreducible
p\in\mathcal{P}(\Omega)
, if
\eta\in\Omega^{N}
is distributed according to the canonical tensor product
\gamma_{N}(p)
, letting
\lambda_{\eta}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\delta_{\eta(j)}
denote the corresponding empirical measure,

(2.34)

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\Big[\|p-\lambda_{\eta}\|_{\rm TV}\Big]\;\leqslant\;\frac{C}{\sqrt{N}},

for some constant
C=C(p)
. Moreover, the same estimate holds with
\sqrt{N}
replaced by
N^{1/4}
if
\eta
is distributed according to
\gamma(p,\rho_{N})
, for any admissible sequence
\rho_{N}
satisfying (
2.4
).

Proof.

We write

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left[\|p-\lambda_{\eta}\|_{\rm TV}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\sigma\in\Omega}{\mathbb{E}}\left[|\lambda_{\eta}(\sigma)-p(\sigma)|\right]

(2.35)

\displaystyle\;\leqslant\;\frac{1}{2}\left(\sum_{\sigma\in\Omega}p(\sigma){\mathbb{E}}\left[(h_{\eta}(\sigma)-1)^{2}\right]\right)^{1/2}\,,

where

h_{\eta}(\sigma)=\frac{\lambda_{\eta}(\sigma)}{p(\sigma)}
and we have used Schwarz’ inequality for the product measure
p\times{\mathbb{E}}
.
Now,

(2.36)

\displaystyle\sum_{\sigma}p(\sigma){\mathbb{E}}\left[(h_{\eta}(\sigma)-1)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=-1+\frac{1}{N}\,{\mathbb{E}}\left[\frac{1}{p(\eta(1))}\right]+\frac{N(N-1)}{N^{2}}\,{\mathbb{E}}\left[\frac{\mathbf{1}_{\eta(1)=\eta(2)}}{p(\eta(1))}\right],

where we use

{\mathbb{E}}\left[\frac{1}{p(\eta(1))}\right]=\sum_{\sigma}\frac{{\mathbb{P}}(\eta(1)=\sigma)}{p(\sigma)}\,,\quad{\mathbb{E}}\left[\frac{\mathbf{1}_{\eta(1)=\eta(2)}}{p(\eta(1))}\right]=\sum_{\sigma}\frac{{\mathbb{P}}(\eta(1)=\eta(2)=\sigma)}{p(\sigma)}\,.

Since

{\mathbb{E}}\left[1/p(\eta(1))\right]\;\leqslant\;1/p_{*}
,
where
p_{*}=\min_{\sigma:\,p(\sigma)>0}p(\sigma)
we see that the second term in (
2.36
) is bounded by
1/(p_{*}N)
.
Next, consider the function

f(\eta(1),\eta(2))=\mathbf{1}_{\eta(1)=\eta(2)}/p(\eta(1))
. Note that
p^{\otimes 2}(f)=1
. Then, Theorem
2.6
implies

\left|-1+\sum_{\sigma}\frac{{\mathbb{P}}(\eta(1)=\eta(2)=\sigma)}{p(\sigma)}\right|\;\leqslant\;\frac{C}{p_{*}N}.

Since
N(N-1)/N^{2}=1-1/N
, we have shown that (
2.36
) is bounded by
C/N

for some new constant
C
depending only on
p
. Together with (
2.3
) this concludes the proof of (
2.34
). Finally, if instead
\eta
is distributed according to
\gamma(p,\rho_{N})
, for an arbitrary admissible sequence
\rho_{N}
satisfying (
2.4
) we may repeat all the steps above and use the first part of Theorem
2.6
to conclude that (
2.36
) this time is bounded by
C/\sqrt{N}
, which implies the claimed bound with
N^{1/4}
in place of
\sqrt{N}
.
∎

2.4.
Entropic chaos and Fisher chaos

The next result shows how to use the local CLT to obtain convergence of the relative entropy of tensor products. Following
[
8
]
we refer to this as entropic chaos.

Proposition 2.8
.

Suppose
p\in\mathcal{P}(\Omega)
is irreducible and let
\rho_{N}
be an admissible sequence
such that

(2.37)

\displaystyle\langle\rho_{N}-\pi,\rho_{N}-\pi\rangle=O(1/N).

Then

(2.38)

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}\,H_{N}(\gamma(p,\rho_{N})\,|\,\gamma(\pi,\rho_{N}))=H(p\,|\,\pi).

Proof.

We write

(2.39)

\displaystyle H_{N}(\gamma(p,\rho_{N})\,|\,\gamma(\pi,\rho_{N}))=\gamma(p,\rho_{N})\left[\log\left(\frac{p^{\otimes N}}{\pi^{\otimes N}}\right)\right]+\log\left(\frac{\pi^{\otimes N}(\Omega_{\rho_{N}})}{p^{\otimes N}(\Omega_{\rho_{N}})}\right).

From Corollary
2.3
we obtain

(2.40)

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}\,\log\left(\frac{\pi^{\otimes N}(\Omega_{\rho_{N}})}{p^{\otimes N}(\Omega_{\rho_{N}})}\right)=0.

By symmetry, the first term in (
2.39
) equals

N\,P_{1}\gamma(p,\rho_{N})\left[\log\left(\frac{p}{\pi}\right)\right].

Therefore the result follows from Theorem
2.6
.
∎

Another consequence of the local CLT is the following upper semi-continuity property, see
[
8
]
for a similar statement in the kinetic setting.

Proposition 2.9
.

For each
N,
let
\mu^{(N)}
be a symmetric probability on
\Omega_{\rho_{N}}
and let
\mu_{k}
be a probability on
\Omega^{k}
such that

P_{k}\mu^{(N)}\longrightarrow\mu_{k}

weakly for some integer
k
. Then, for any admissible sequence satisfying (
2.4
),

(2.41)

\displaystyle\frac{H(\mu_{k}\,|\,\pi^{\otimes k})}{k}\;\leqslant\;\liminf_{N\to\infty}\frac{H_{N}(\mu^{(N)}|\gamma(\pi,\rho_{N}))}{N}.

Proof.

From Shearer inequality one has

(2.42)

\displaystyle\sum_{A\in\mathcal{A}}H\left(P_{A}\mu^{(N)}|P_{A}\pi^{\otimes N}\right)\;\leqslant\;n_{+}(\mathcal{A})H\left(\mu^{(N)}\,|\,\pi^{\otimes N}\right),

where
\mathcal{A}
is any family of sets covering
[N]=\{1,\dots,N\}
,
n_{+}(\mathcal{A})=\max_{j}\#\{A\in\mathcal{A}:\,A\ni j\}
, and
P_{A}\nu
denotes the marginal on the variables
\{\eta(j),\,j\in A\}
of a probability
\nu\in\mathcal{P}(\Omega^{N})
, see Lemma
4.1
below for more details.
If we take
\mathcal{A}=\{A\subset[N]:\,|A|=k\}
, then
n_{+}(\mathcal{A})=\binom{N-1}{k-1}
. Moreover, by symmetry

P_{A}\mu^{(N)}=P_{k}\mu^{(N)}
for all
A\in\mathcal{A}
. Since
\binom{N}{k}/\binom{N-1}{k-1}=N/k
, this proves

(2.43)

\displaystyle H\left(P_{k}\mu^{(N)}\,|\,\pi^{\otimes k}\right)\;\leqslant\;\frac{k}{N}\,H\left(\mu^{(N)}\,|\,\pi^{\otimes N}\right).

See also Lemma 3.9 in
[
32
]
, where (
2.43
) was derived with
\frac{k}{N}
replaced by
\frac{k}{N}(1+O(\frac{k}{N}))
in the right hand side.
On the other hand,

(2.44)

\displaystyle H_{N}\left(\mu^{(N)}\,|\,\pi^{\otimes N}\right)=H_{N}(\mu^{(N)}\,|\,\gamma(\pi,\rho_{N}))-\log\left(\pi^{\otimes N}(\Omega_{\rho_{N}})\right).

The left hand side of (
2.43
) converges by assumption to
H\left(\mu_{k}\,|\,\pi^{\otimes k}\right)
. The desired conclusion then follows from Corollary
2.3
.
∎

Remark 2.10
.

Both Proposition
2.8
and Proposition
2.9
hold with
\pi
replaced by any irreducible
p^{\prime}\in\mathcal{P}(\Omega)
with the same marginals as
\pi
.

One can also establish the following analogue of the Fisher chaos property discussed in
[
24
]
.
Observe that if
\mu_{N,t}=\mu_{N}e^{t\mathcal{L}_{N}}
, for some
\mu_{N}\in\mathcal{P}(\Omega_{\rho_{N}})
, then

(2.45)

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\,H\left(\mu_{N,t}|\gamma(\pi,\rho_{N})\right)_{|_{t=0^{+}}}=-D_{N}(f_{N})\,,

where
f_{N}:=\mu_{N}/\gamma(\pi,\rho_{N})
, and

(2.46)

\displaystyle D_{N}(f_{N})=\frac{1}{2N}\sum_{j<l}\sum_{A}\nu(A)\,\gamma(\pi,\rho_{N})\left[(f_{N}^{j,l,A}-f_{N})\log\frac{f_{N}^{j,l,A}}{f_{N}}\right]\,.

Here we use the notation
f_{N}^{j,l,A}(\eta)=f_{N}(\eta^{j,l,A})
, where
\eta^{j,l,A}
is defined in (
1.7
).
On the other hand, for the nonlinear equation (
1.3
) one has

(2.47)

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\,H\left(p_{t}\,|\,\pi\right)_{|_{t=0^{+}}}=-D_{\pi}(f)\,,

where
f:=p/\pi
, and

(2.48)

\displaystyle D_{\pi}(f)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{A}\nu(A)\,\pi\left[(f^{A}f^{A^{c}}-f)\log\frac{f^{A}f^{A^{c}}}{f}\right]\,,

where
f^{A}(\sigma)=p_{A}(\sigma)/\pi_{A}(\sigma)
is the density of the marginal of
p
on
A
with respect to
\pi
.

Proposition 2.11
.

Under the same assumptions of Proposition
2.8
,

(2.49)

\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{D_{N}(f_{N})}{N}=D_{\pi}(f),

where
f_{N}:=\gamma(p,\rho_{N})/\gamma(\pi,\rho_{N})
, and
f:=p/\pi
.

Proof.

Since

(2.50)

\displaystyle D_{N}(f_{N})=-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j<l}\sum_{A}\nu(A)\,\gamma(\pi,\rho_{N})\left[(f_{N}^{j,l,A}-f_{N})\log{f_{N}}\right]\,.

and

(2.51)

\displaystyle D_{\pi}(f)=-2\sum_{A}\nu(A)\,\pi\left[(f^{A}f^{A^{c}}-f)\log{f}\right]\,,

it suffices to show that for every
j,l\in[N]
and
A\subset[n]
one has

(2.52)

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to+\infty}

\displaystyle\sum_{\eta\in\Omega_{\rho_{N}}}\gamma(\pi,\rho_{N})(\eta)\left(f_{N}(\eta^{j,l,A})-f_{N}(\eta)\right)\log\left({f_{N}(\eta)}\right)

(2.53)

\displaystyle\qquad=2\sum_{\sigma\in\Omega}(p_{A}(\sigma_{A})p_{A^{c}}(\sigma_{A^{c}})-p(\sigma))\log\left(\frac{p(\sigma)}{\pi(\sigma)}\right).

Note that

(2.54)

\displaystyle\sum_{\eta\in\Omega_{\rho_{N}}}\pi_{N}(\eta)\left(f_{N}(\eta_{j,l,A})-f_{N}(\eta)\right)\log\left({f_{N}(\eta)}\right)

(2.55)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\eta\in\Omega_{\rho_{N}}}\frac{\prod_{\ell\neq j,l}p(\eta(l))}{p^{\otimes N}\left(\Omega_{\rho_{N}}\right)}\left(p(\eta^{j,l,A}(j))p(\eta^{j,l,A}(l))-p(\eta(l))p(\eta(j))\right)\log\prod_{r=1}^{N}\frac{p(\eta(r))}{\pi(\eta(r))}

(2.56)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\eta(l),\eta(j)}\frac{p^{\otimes(N-2)}\left(\Omega^{\eta(j),\eta(l)}_{\rho_{N}}\right)}{p^{\otimes N}\left(\Omega_{\rho_{N}}\right)}\left(p(\eta^{j,l,A}(j))p(\eta^{j,l,A}(l))-p(\eta(l))p(\eta(j))\right)\log\frac{p(\eta(l))p(\eta(j))}{\pi(\eta(l))\pi(\eta(j))},

where
\Omega^{\eta(j),\eta(l)}_{\rho_{N}}
is the event

(2.57)

\displaystyle\sum_{\ell\neq j,l}\mathbf{1}(\eta_{i}(\ell)=x)=(N-2)(\widetilde{\rho_{N}})_{i,x}\,,\quad\forall i,x

and, for any fixed
\eta(j),\eta(l)
,
\widetilde{\rho_{N}}
denotes the density

(\widetilde{\rho_{N}})_{i,x}=\frac{N}{N-2}\,(\rho_{N})_{i,x}-\frac{1}{N-2}\left(\mathbf{1}(\eta_{i}(j)=x)+\mathbf{1}(\eta_{i}(l)=x)\right)\,.

Therefore, it is sufficient to show that

(2.58)

\displaystyle\frac{p^{\otimes(N-2)}\left(\Omega^{\eta(j),\eta(l)}_{\rho_{N}}\right)}{p^{\otimes N}\left(\Omega_{\rho_{N}}\right)}\;\to\;1,

for all
i,j\in[N]
, for all fixed values of
\eta(j),\eta(l)\in\Omega
.
We note that both
\rho_{N},\widetilde{\rho_{N}}
satisfy condition (
2.4
). Moreover, writing

\displaystyle z_{N}=\sqrt{N}\;V_{1}^{-1/2}\left(\rho_{N}-\pi\right),\quad\widetilde{z}_{N}=\sqrt{N}\;V_{1}^{-1/2}\left(\widetilde{\rho}_{N}-\pi\right),

we see that

\langle z_{N},z_{N}\rangle-\langle\widetilde{z}_{N},\widetilde{z}_{N}\rangle\to 0\,,\quad N\to\infty,

for all fixed values of
\eta(j),\eta(l)
. The desired claim (
2.58
) then follows from Proposition
2.2
.
∎

3.
Uniform in time propagation of chaos

The main goal in this section is to prove Theorem
1.5
.
We first introduce some notation. Let
\lambda_{\eta}\in\mathcal{P}(\Omega)
be the empirical measure
\lambda_{\eta}:=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{\eta(i)}
, where
\eta:=\left(\eta(1),\dots,\eta(N)\right)\in\Omega^{N}
.
We consider the Wasserstein distance on
\mathcal{P}(\Omega)
associated to the Hamming distance on
\Omega
:

(3.1)

\displaystyle W(p,q):=\inf_{\Gamma\in\Pi(p,q)}\sum_{\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}\in\Omega}\Gamma(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime})\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{1}_{\sigma_{i}\neq\sigma^{\prime}_{i}},

where
p,q\in\mathcal{P}(\Omega)
, and
\Pi(p,q)
denotes the set of all couplings
\Gamma
of
p
and
q
.
We remark that the total variation distance
\|p-q\|_{\rm TV}
is defined as above with
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{1}_{\sigma_{i}\neq\sigma^{\prime}_{i}}
replaced by
\mathbf{1}_{\sigma\neq\sigma^{\prime}}
and thus one has

(3.2)

\displaystyle\|p-q\|_{\rm TV}\;\leqslant\;W(p,q)\;\leqslant\;n\,\|p-q\|_{\rm TV}.

In contrast with the total variation distance, the distance
W
has a convenient monotonicity along the evolution, see Lemma
3.8
and Remark
3.9
below. Recall the definition of the non-separation probability
r(\nu)
from (
1.4
).

Theorem 3.1
.

Assume that
\mu_{N}\in\mathcal{P}(\Omega^{N})
is
p-
chaotic and the measure
\nu
is separating. Then for all
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
and any function of the form
\phi_{k}:=\phi^{1}\otimes\dots\otimes\phi^{k}:\Omega^{k}\mapsto{\mathbb{R}}
, where
\phi^{i}:\Omega\mapsto{\mathbb{R}}
, and such that
\|\phi_{k}\|_{\infty}\;\leqslant\;1
, the following inequality holds

(3.3)

\displaystyle|P_{k}\mu_{N,t}(\phi_{k})-p_{t}^{\otimes k}(\phi_{k})|

\displaystyle\;\leqslant\;\frac{2k(k-1)}{N}+\frac{4k^{2}n^{5}}{D(\nu)\,N}(1-e^{-D(\nu)\,t})+2k\,\mu_{N}\left[W\left(p,\lambda_{\chi_{N}}\right)\right],

where
\chi_{N}\in\Omega^{N}
has distribution
\mu_{N}
, and

D(\nu):=1-r(\nu)
.

The proof of Theorem
3.1
follows the steps of a general approach, the so called “abstract theorem”, see
[
23
,
33
,
34
]
, see also
[
11
]
for a review.
In our discrete setting many aspects of this approach take a simpler form, and no extra assumption on the initial distribution is needed besides the
p
-chaoticity for some
p\in\mathcal{P}(\Omega)
. This general approach requires however several model-specific inputs. Our main original contribution here consists in establishing
the key estimates stated in Theorem
3.2
and Theorem
3.5
below, where we prove new contraction inequalities for both the non linear and the linearized evolutions associated to (
1.3
).
Before proving Theorem
3.1
, let us show that it implies Theorem
1.5
.

3.1.
Proof of Theorem
1.5

It is well known that

\mu_{N}\left[W\left(p,\lambda_{\chi_{N}}\right)\right]\to 0

if and only if
\mu_{N}
is
p
-chaotic; see e.g. Lemma 3.34 in
[
11
]
. This, combined with the estimate from Theorem
3.1
, proves the uniform in time propagation of chaos asserted in Theorem
1.5
. To prove the quantitative statement (
1.14

) shows that

\nu(|F|>1)\;\leqslant\;\nu(|F|\;\geqslant\;1)\;\leqslant\;\tfrac{1}{2}\,n(n-1)\sum_{i=1}^{k}\left(\frac{r(\nu)}{2}\right)^{d_{i}(\gamma)}=\tfrac{1}{2}\,n(n-1)\,\,\omega(\gamma),

where we use the fact that the event

coincides with
\cup_{i=1}^{k}\mathcal{A}_{i}
from the proof of (
3.36
).
This ends the proof of (
3.68
), which completes the proof of (
3.49
).
∎

Monotonicity of
W
along the nonlinear evolution

Before proving Theorem
3.1
, we
show that the distance

appearing in that statement is monotone along the semigroup. We refer to
[
42
]
for a similar argument in the case of kinetic models.
It will be convenient to work with a more symmetric version of (
3.14
).
Since
p\circ q=\sum_{A}\bar{\nu}(A)(p\circ q)^{A}
we may rewrite (
3.14
) as

(3.80)

\displaystyle C_{\gamma}(p)=\sum_{\vec{A}\in\mathcal{V}_{n}^{k-1}}\nu(\vec{A})\hat{C}^{\vec{A}}_{\gamma}(p)

where
\nu(\vec{A})
is defined as in (
3.15
), and
\hat{C}^{\vec{A}}_{\gamma}(p)
represents the symmetric convolutions associated to the sampled sets
(A_{1},\dots,A_{k-1})
. In other words,

\hat{C}^{\vec{A}}_{\gamma}(p)
is defined recursively
by the following relations.
If
\gamma\in\Gamma(k)
then, decomposing
\gamma
into the left and right subtrees
\gamma_{l},\gamma_{r}
as in (
3.10
), one has

(3.81)

\displaystyle\hat{C}^{\vec{A}}_{\gamma}(p)=(\hat{C}^{\vec{A}_{l}}_{\gamma_{l}}(p)\circ\hat{C}^{\vec{A}_{r}}_{\gamma_{r}}(p))^{A_{1}}

where
A_{1}
is the set attached to the root, and
\vec{A}_{l}:=\left(A_{2},\dots,A_{j}\right)
,
\vec{A}_{r}:=\left(A_{j+1},\dots,A_{k-1}\right)
are the sets associated to the left and right subtrees respectively.
For example, in the case where
C_{\gamma}(p)=((p\circ p)\circ p)\circ p
as in Figure
3.1
, one has

(3.82)

\displaystyle C_{\gamma}(p)=\sum_{A_{1},A_{2},A_{3}\subset[n]}\bar{\nu}(A_{1})\bar{\nu}(A_{2})\bar{\nu}(A_{3})\hat{C}^{A_{1},A_{2},A_{3}}_{\gamma}(p)

where

\hat{C}^{A_{1},A_{2},A_{3}}_{\gamma}(p)=(((p\,\circ\,p)^{A_{3}}\circ\,p)^{A_{2}})\circ\,p)^{A_{1}}
.
Therefore, as in (
3.19
) one obtains the decomposition

(3.83)

\displaystyle p_{t}=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\beta_{t}(k)\sum_{\gamma\in\Gamma(k)}\alpha_{k}(\gamma)\sum_{\vec{A}\in\mathcal{V}_{n}^{k-1}}\nu(\vec{A})\,\hat{C}^{\vec{A}}_{\gamma}(p).

Lemma 3.8
.

For any
p,q\in\mathcal{P}(\Omega)
, any
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
, any
\gamma\in\Gamma(k)
, and any
\vec{A}\in\mathcal{V}_{n}^{k-1}
, one has

(3.84)

\displaystyle W\left(\hat{C}^{\vec{A}}_{\gamma}(p),\hat{C}^{\vec{A}}_{\gamma}(q)\right)\;\leqslant\;W\left(p,q\right).

In particular,
W\left(S_{t}(p),S_{t}(q)\right)\;\leqslant\;W\left(p,q\right)
for any
t\;\geqslant\;0
.

Proof.

First we show

(3.85)

\displaystyle W\left((p_{1}\circ p_{2}),(q_{1}\circ q_{2})\right)\;\leqslant\;\tfrac{1}{2}\,W\left(p_{1},q_{1}\right)+\tfrac{1}{2}\,W\left(p_{2},q_{2}\right)

for all
p_{1},p_{2},q_{1},q_{2}\in\mathcal{P}(\Omega).

For a fixed
A\subset[n]
and configurations
x_{1},x_{2}\in\Omega
we define

\displaystyle\Pi_{x_{1},x_{2},A}(z):=\tfrac{1}{2}\left({\bf 1}(z_{A}=x_{1,A},z_{A^{c}}=x_{2,A^{c}})+{\bf 1}(z_{A}=x_{2,A},z_{A^{c}}=x_{1,A^{c}})\right),\ \ z\in\Omega.

Now we choose
\{X_{1},Y_{1}\},\{X_{2},Y_{2}\}
random variables such that

•

{\mathbb{E}}\left[d\left(X_{1},Y_{1}\right)\right]=W(p_{1},q_{1}),{\mathbb{E}}\left[d\left(X_{2},Y_{2}\right)\right]=W(p_{2},q_{2}).

•

X_{i}
is distributed with
p_{i}
and
Y_{i}
is distributed with
q_{i},i=1,2.

•

\{X_{1},Y_{1}\}
and
\{X_{2},Y_{2}\}
are independent.

Note that

(3.86)

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left[\Pi_{X_{1},X_{2},A}\right]=\left(p_{1}\circ p_{2}\right)^{A}.

Now, given
x_{1},x_{2},y_{1},y_{2}\in\Omega
and
A\subset[n],
we consider the following probability measure

\displaystyle\pi(z_{1},z_{2}):=

\displaystyle\tfrac{1}{2}\big({\bf 1}(z_{1,A}=x_{1,A},z_{1,A^{c}}=x_{2,A^{c}},z_{2,A}=y_{1,A},z_{2,A^{c}}=y_{2,A^{c}})

\displaystyle+{\bf 1}(z_{1,A}=x_{2,A},z_{1,A^{c}}=x_{1,A^{c}},z_{2,A}=y_{2,A},z_{2,A^{c}}=y_{1,A^{c}})\big).

Note that this is a coupling of
\Pi_{x_{1},x_{2},A}
and
\Pi_{y_{1},y_{2},A}.
Therefeore

\displaystyle W\left(\Pi_{x_{1},x_{2},A},\Pi_{y_{1},y_{2},A}\right)

\displaystyle\;\leqslant\;\sum_{z_{1},z_{2}}\pi(z_{1},z_{2})d(z_{1},z_{2})

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{2}\left(d(x_{1,A}x_{2,A^{c}},y_{1,A}y_{2,A^{c}})+d(x_{2,A}x_{1,A^{c}},y_{2,A}y_{1,A^{c}})\right)

(3.87)

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{2}\left(d(x_{1},y_{1})+d(x_{2},y_{2})\right).

Moreover, by convexity

(3.88)

\displaystyle W\left((p_{1}\circ p_{2}),(q_{1}\circ q_{2})\right)\;\leqslant\;\sum_{A\subset[n]}\nu(A)W\left((p_{1}\circ p_{2})^{A},(q_{1}\circ q_{2})^{A}\right)

and, for each
A\subset[n]
,

(3.89)

\displaystyle W\left((p_{1}\circ p_{2})^{A},(q_{1}\circ q_{2})^{A}\right)

\displaystyle\;\leqslant\;{\mathbb{E}}\left[W\left(\Pi_{X_{1},X_{2},A},\Pi_{Y_{1},Y_{2},A}\right)\right]=\tfrac{1}{2}\,W(p_{1},q_{1})+\tfrac{1}{2}\,W(p_{2},q_{2}).

Combining the last two inequalities we obtain (
3.85
).

We now prove (
3.84
) by induction over
k\;\geqslant\;1
. Clearly,
k=1
is trivial. The case
k=2
follows by (
3.89
). Suppose that (
3.84
) holds for any
j\;\leqslant\;k-1
,
\gamma\in\Gamma(j)
,
\vec{A}\in\mathcal{V}_{n}^{j-1}
and let
A_{1}
be the set attached to the root of
\gamma
.
If
\gamma\in\Gamma(k)
then we decompose
\gamma
into the left and right subtrees
\gamma_{l},\gamma_{r}
as in (
3.10
), so that

\displaystyle\hat{C}^{\vec{A}}_{\gamma}(p)=(\hat{C}^{\vec{A}_{l}}_{\gamma_{l}}(p)\circ\hat{C}^{\vec{A}_{r}}_{\gamma_{r}}(p))^{A_{1}}

\displaystyle\hat{C}^{\vec{A}}_{\gamma}(q)=(\hat{C}^{\vec{A}_{l}}_{\gamma_{l}}(q)\circ\hat{C}^{\vec{A}_{r}}_{\gamma_{r}}(q))^{A_{1}}.

where
\vec{A}_{l}:=\left(A_{2},\dots,A_{j}\right)
and
\vec{A}_{r}:=\left(A_{j+1},\dots,A_{k-1}\right),
and
j
is the number of leaves of
\gamma_{l}.

Then, using (
3.89
) again and the inductive hypothesis we have

\displaystyle W(\hat{C}^{\vec{A}}_{\gamma}(p),\hat{C}^{\vec{A}}_{\gamma}(q))

\displaystyle=W((\hat{C}^{\vec{A}_{l}}_{\gamma_{l}}(p)\circ\hat{C}^{\vec{A}_{r}}_{\gamma_{r}}(p))^{A_{1}},(\hat{C}^{\vec{A}_{l}}_{\gamma_{l}}(q)\circ\hat{C}^{\vec{A}_{r}}_{\gamma_{r}}(q))^{A_{1}})

\displaystyle\;\leqslant\;\tfrac{1}{2}\,W(\hat{C}^{\vec{A}_{l}}_{\gamma_{l}}(p),\hat{C}^{\vec{A}_{l}}_{\gamma_{l}}(q))+\tfrac{1}{2}\,W(\hat{C}^{\vec{A}_{r}}_{\gamma_{r}}(p),\hat{C}^{\vec{A}_{r}}_{\gamma_{r}}(q))\;\leqslant\;W(p,q).

This proves (
3.84
).

By (
3.80
) and convexity,

(3.90)

\displaystyle W(C_{\gamma}(p),C_{\gamma}(q))\;\leqslant\;W(p,q).

Finally, using the expression (
3.83
) for
S_{t}(p)=p_{t}
, again by convexity we obtain

W(S_{t}(p),S_{t}(q))\;\leqslant\;W(p,q),

for all
t\;\geqslant\;0
.
∎

Remark 3.9
.

We note that (

) shows in particular that

(3.91)

\displaystyle W\left(p\circ p,q\circ q\right)\;\leqslant\;W\left(p,q\right).

This monotonicity is not satisfied by the total variation distance. For example let us consider
\Omega=\{0,1\}^{2},

p:={\bf 1}_{(1,1)},q:=\frac{1}{2}({\bf 1}_{(1,1)}+{\bf 1}_{(0,0)})
and suppose

is the uniform crossover. Then one can check that

3.6.
Proof of Theorem
3.1

For
k\in{\mathbb{N}},
the moment measures
F_{t}^{k,N}\in\mathcal{P}(\Omega^{k})
and
\bar{F}_{t}^{k,N}\in\mathcal{P}(\Omega^{k})
are defined by

(3.92)

\displaystyle F_{t}^{k,N}=\sum_{\eta\in\Omega^{N}}\mu_{N,t}(\eta)(\lambda_{\eta})^{\otimes k},\qquad\bar{F}_{t}^{k,N}=\sum_{\eta\in\Omega^{N}}\mu_{N}(\eta)(S_{t}\lambda_{\eta})^{\otimes k}.

By the triangular inequality we have

(3.93)

\displaystyle|P_{k}\mu_{N,t}(\phi_{k})-p_{t}^{\otimes k}(\phi_{k})|\;\leqslant\;\mathcal{T}_{1}+\mathcal{T}_{2}+\mathcal{T}_{3}\,,

where

(3.94)

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{1}:=|P_{k}\mu_{N,t}(\phi_{k})-F_{t}^{k,N}(\phi_{k})|\,,\quad\mathcal{T}_{2}:=|F_{t}^{k,N}(\phi_{k})-\bar{F}_{t}^{k,N}(\phi_{k})|\,,\quad\mathcal{T}_{3}:=|\bar{F}_{t}^{k,N}(\phi_{k})-p_{t}^{\otimes k}(\phi_{k})|

We are going to estimate each term separately.

The term
\mathcal{T}_{1}
can be estimated by using a simple combinatorial argument, and one obtains

(3.95)

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{1}\;\leqslant\;\frac{2k(k-1)}{N}\|\phi_{k}\|_{\infty}.

Since the proof is identical to that in
[
11
, Lemma 3.11]
we omit the details.

The last term
\mathcal{T}_{3}
is estimated using the initial chaos.
Note that

(3.96)

\displaystyle p_{t}^{\otimes k}\left(\phi_{k}\right)-\bar{F}_{t}^{k,N}\left(\phi_{k}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\eta\in\Omega^{N}}\mu_{N}(\eta)\left(\prod_{i=1}^{k}\langle S_{t}(p),\phi^{i}\rangle\,-\,\prod_{i=1}^{k}\langle S_{t}(\lambda_{\eta}),\phi^{i}\rangle\right).

Using
\prod_{i=1}^{k}a_{i}-\prod_{i=1}^{k}b_{i}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}(\prod_{1\;\leqslant\;j<i}a_{j})(a_{i}-b_{i})(\prod_{i<j\;\leqslant\;k}b_{j})
,
we obtain

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{3}

\displaystyle\;\leqslant\;\sum_{\eta\in\Omega^{N}}\mu_{N}(\eta)\sum_{i=1}^{k}\Bigg(\prod_{1\;\leqslant\;j<i}|\langle S_{t}(p),\phi^{j}\rangle|\Bigg)\left|\langle S_{t}(p)-S_{t}(\lambda_{\eta}),\phi^{i}\rangle\right|\Bigg(\prod_{i<j\;\leqslant\;k}|\langle S_{t}(\lambda_{\eta}),\phi^{j}\rangle|\Bigg)

\displaystyle\;\leqslant\;2k\|\phi_{k}\|_{\infty}\,\mu_{N}\left[W\left(S_{t}(p),S_{t}(\lambda_{\chi^{N}})\right)\right]\;\leqslant\;2k\|\phi_{k}\|_{\infty}\,\mu_{N}\left[W\left(p,\lambda_{\chi^{N}}\right)\right],

where
\chi^{N}
has distribution
\mu_{N}
, and we have used

(3.97)

\displaystyle|\langle\mu-\mu^{\prime},f\rangle|\;\leqslant\;2\|f\|_{\infty}\|\mu-\mu^{\prime}\|_{\rm TV}\;\leqslant\;2\|f\|_{\infty}W(\mu,\mu^{\prime}),

for any
\mu,\mu^{\prime}\in\mathcal{P}(\Omega)
,
f:\Omega\mapsto{\mathbb{R}}
, and the monotonicity from Lemma
3.8
.

The estimate of the second term
\mathcal{T}_{2}
is more delicate. Here we use the contraction proved in Theorem
3.2
and Theorem
3.5
.
Define, for
s\in[0,t]
,

\Psi^{k}_{s}=\sum_{\eta\in\Omega^{N}}\mu_{N,t-s}(\eta)(S_{s}(\lambda_{\eta}))^{\otimes k}.

Then
\Psi^{k}_{s}\in\mathcal{P}(\Omega^{k})
for all

, and

Therefore,

(3.98)

\displaystyle\bar{F}_{t}^{k,N}(\phi_{k})-F_{t}^{k,N}(\phi_{k})=\int_{0}^{t}\partial_{s}\left[\Psi^{k}_{s}(\phi_{k})\right]\,ds\,.

Now,

(3.99)

\displaystyle\partial_{s}\left[\Psi^{k}_{s}(\phi_{k})\right]=\sum_{\eta\in\Omega^{N}}(\partial_{s}\mu_{N,t-s}(\eta))(S_{s}(\lambda_{\eta}))^{\otimes k}(\phi_{k})+\sum_{\eta\in\Omega^{N}}\mu_{N,t-s}(\eta)\partial_{s}(S_{s}(\lambda_{\eta}))^{\otimes k}(\phi_{k})

\displaystyle=-\sum_{\eta\in\Omega^{N}}\mu_{N,t-s}(\eta)\mathcal{L}_{N}\prod_{\ell=1}^{k}\langle S_{s}(\lambda_{\eta}),\phi^{\ell}\rangle+\sum_{\eta\in\Omega^{N}}\mu_{N,t-s}(\eta)\sum_{i=1}^{k}\langle Q(S_{s}(\lambda_{\eta})),\phi^{i}\rangle\prod_{\ell\neq i}\langle S_{s}(\lambda_{\eta},\phi^{\ell}\rangle.

Next, we show that

(3.100)

\displaystyle Q(S_{s}(\lambda_{\eta}))=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i<j}\sum_{A}\nu(A)\bar{S}_{s}(\lambda_{\eta})(\lambda_{\eta^{i,j,A}}-\lambda_{\eta}).

Consider the linearized equation (
3.47

and

one has that the solution

satisfies

, that is

(3.101)

\displaystyle\bar{S}_{t}(q)(Q(q))=Q\left(S_{t}(q)\right)\,,\qquad t\;\geqslant\;0,

for all

.
To see this note that for all

\displaystyle\sum_{\sigma\in\Omega}S_{t}(q)(\sigma)=1\,,\qquad\sum_{\sigma\in\Omega}Q(S_{t}(q))(\sigma)=0\,,

and therefore

\displaystyle\partial_{t}Q\left(S_{t}(q)\right)(\sigma^{\prime})=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}A\subset[n]\\
\sigma\in\Omega\end{subarray}}\nu(A)\bigg(Q(S_{t}(q))(\sigma_{A^{c}}\sigma^{\prime}_{A})S_{t}(q)(\sigma_{A}\sigma^{\prime}_{A^{c}})

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad+S_{t}(q)(\sigma_{A^{c}}\sigma^{\prime}_{A})Q(S_{t}(q))(\sigma_{A}\sigma^{\prime}_{A^{c}})-Q(S_{t}(q))(\sigma^{\prime})S_{t}(q)(\sigma)\bigg)

\displaystyle\qquad=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}A\subset[n]\\

This proves (

). If

for some

, then

\displaystyle Q(\lambda_{\eta})=\sum_{A}\nu(A)\left((\lambda_{\eta})_{A}\otimes(\lambda_{\eta})_{A^{c}}-\lambda_{\eta}\right).

,

(3.103)

\displaystyle(\lambda_{\eta})_{A}\otimes(\lambda_{\eta})_{A^{c}}-\lambda_{\eta}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2N^{2}}\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}(\mathbf{1}_{\eta^{i,j,A}(i)}+\mathbf{1}_{\eta^{i,j,A}(j)}-\mathbf{1}_{\eta(i)}-\mathbf{1}_{\eta(j)})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i<j}(\lambda_{\eta^{i,j,A}}-\lambda_{\eta}).

It follows that

(3.105)

\displaystyle Q(\lambda_{\eta})=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i<j}\sum_{A}\nu(A)(\lambda_{\eta^{i,j,A}}-\lambda_{\eta}).

Thus, using (

) and the linearity of
\bar{S}_{s}(\lambda_{\eta})(\cdot)

(

On the other hand,

(3.106)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{N}\prod_{\ell=1}^{k}\langle S_{s}(\lambda_{\eta}),\phi^{\ell}\rangle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i<j}\sum_{A}\nu(A)\left(\prod_{\ell=1}^{k}\langle S_{s}(\lambda_{\eta^{i,j,A}}),\phi^{\ell}\rangle-\prod_{\ell=1}^{k}\langle S_{s}(\lambda_{\eta}),\phi^{\ell}\rangle\right).

Suppose we can show that

\displaystyle\quad-\sum_{u=1}^{k}\langle\bar{S}_{s}(\lambda_{\eta})(\lambda_{\eta^{i,j,A}}-\lambda_{\eta}),\phi^{u}\rangle\prod_{\ell\neq u}\langle S_{s}(\lambda_{\eta},\phi^{\ell}\rangle\Big|\;\leqslant\;K_{s},

for some function

\displaystyle\prod_{\ell=1}^{k}a_{\ell}-\prod_{\ell=1}^{k}b_{\ell}-\sum_{u=1}^{k}c_{u}\prod_{j\neq u}b_{j}=\sum_{u=1}^{k}\left[(\prod_{1\;\leqslant\;j<u}a_{j})(a_{u}-b_{u})-(\prod_{1\;\leqslant\;j<u}b_{j})c_{u}\right]\prod_{j>u}b_{j}

\displaystyle\qquad=\sum_{u=1}^{k}(a_{u}-b_{u}-c_{u})\prod_{j\neq u}b_{j}+\sum_{u=1}^{k}\left[(\prod_{1\;\leqslant\;j<u}a_{j})-(\prod_{1\;\leqslant\;j<u}b_{j})\right](a_{u}-b_{u})\prod_{j>u}b_{j}

\displaystyle\|\lambda_{\eta^{i,j,A}}-\lambda_{\eta}\|_{\rm TV}\;\leqslant\;\frac{2}{N},

\displaystyle|a_{u}-b_{u}-c_{u}|\;\leqslant\;n^{5}\,\|\phi^{u}\|_{\infty}e^{-D(\nu)\,s}\frac{4}{N^{2}}.

4.
Relative entropy decay

The goal of this section is to prove Theorem
1.8
, Proposition
1.11
, and Theorem
1.12
. We start by setting up a convenient notation and by gathering some preliminary ingredients of the proof. To simplify our notation we write
\mu=\pi_{N}
for the uniform distribution over
\mathcal{S}_{N,n}
. For any
A\subset[n]
,
f:\mathcal{S}_{N,n}\mapsto{\mathbb{R}}
, we are going to use the notation

\mu_{A}f=\mu(f\,|\,\eta_{A^{c}}),

where
\mu(\cdot\,|\,\eta_{A^{c}})
denotes the conditional expectation given the variables

\eta_{A^{c}}:=\{\eta_{i}(j),\;i\in A^{c},\;j=1,\dots,N\}.

The function
\mu_{A}f
thus depends on
\eta
only through the variabes
\eta_{A^{c}}
. When
A=[n]
we have the global expectation
\mu_{[n]}f=\mu(f)
. With this notation,
\mu_{A}
is the orthogonal projection, in
L^{2}(\mathcal{S}_{N,n},\mu)
, onto the space of functions that do not depend on the
A
-component of
\eta\in\mathcal{S}_{N,n}
. Notice the relations
\mu(f)=\mu(\mu_{A}f)
, and
\mu_{B}(f)=\mu_{B}(\mu_{A}f)
, for all
A\subset B\subset[n]
.
We also use the notation
{\rm Ent}_{A}(f)
for the entropy of
f:\mathcal{S}_{N,n}\mapsto{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
with respect to
\mu_{A}
, that is

(4.1)

{\rm Ent}_{A}(f)=\mu_{A}\left[f\log(f/\mu_{A}f)\right]\,.

Thus
{\rm Ent}_{A}(f)
is a function that depends on
\eta
through the variables
\eta_{A^{c}}
only. Its expectation satisfies

(4.2)

\mu\left[{\rm Ent}_{A}(f)\right]={\rm Ent}(f)-{\rm Ent}[\mu_{A}f]\,,

where
{\rm Ent}(f)={\rm Ent}_{[n]}(f)=\mu\left[f\log(f/\mu(f))\right]
.
In our setting the well known Shearer inequality can be formulated as follows.

Lemma 4.1
.

For any distribution
\nu
on subsets of
[n]
, for any
f:\mathcal{S}_{N,n}\mapsto{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
,

(4.3)

\sum_{A\subset[n]}\nu(A)\,\mu\left[{\rm Ent}_{A}(f)\right]\;\geqslant\;\gamma(\nu)\,{\rm Ent}(f)\,,

where
\gamma(\nu)=\min_{i\in[n]}\sum_{A:\,A\ni i}\nu(A)
.

Proof.

Reasoning as in
[
7
, Proposition 4.3]
, the estimate is a consequence of the product structure of
\mu
and the weighted version of the classical Shearer inequality for Shannon entropy. We refer e.g. to
[
12
]
for a proof of the latter.
∎

When
A=\{i\}
, a single site, we write
\mu_{i}
for
\mu_{\{i\}}
and
{\rm Ent}_{i}
for
{\rm Ent}_{\{i\}}
. An immediate consequence of Lemma

is the following well known tensorization property:

(4.4)

{\rm Ent}(f)\;\leqslant\;\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mu\left[{\rm Ent}_{i}(f)\right].

4.1.
The case
n=1

A key ingredient of our proof is the control of the base case
n=1
. Here the problem reduces to standard random transpositions and one can use a well known bound that was first derived in
[
22
,
20
]
. In our setting it can be summarized as follows, see
[
20
, Theorem 1]
or
[
22
, Corollary 3.1]
for a proof. Note that for
n=1
we have
\mathcal{S}_{N,n}=S_{N}
.

Lemma 4.2
.

for all
N\;\geqslant\;2
, for all
g:S_{N}\mapsto{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
, for any
i=1,\dots,n
,

\displaystyle\sum_{\tau\in S_{N}}

\displaystyle g(\tau)\log(g(\tau)/\bar{g})\;\leqslant\;\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j<\ell}\sum_{\tau\in S_{N}}\left(g(\tau^{j,\ell})-g(\tau)\right)\log\frac{g(\tau^{j,\ell})}{g(\tau)},

where
\bar{g}=\frac{1}{N!}\sum_{\tau\in S_{N}}g(\tau)
, and
\tau^{j,\ell}
denotes
\tau
composed with the transposition at
\{j,\ell\}
.

The proof of Theorem
1.8
is based on Lemma
4.1
, Lemma
4.2
and the following analysis of the entropy associated to partial random permutations of the particle configuration, which is the main novelty in this section.

4.2.
Permutation entropies

For any
A\subset[n]
,
g:\mathcal{S}_{N,n}\mapsto{\mathbb{R}}
, define
P_{A}g:\mathcal{S}_{N,n}\mapsto{\mathbb{R}}
, as

(4.5)

\displaystyle P_{A}g(\eta)=\frac{1}{N!}\sum_{\tau\in S_{N}}g((\tau\eta)_{A}\,\eta_{A^{c}}),

where
\tau\eta:=\tau\circ\eta
denotes the element of
\mathcal{S}_{N,n}
obtained from
\eta
by permuting the particle labels according to
\tau
:

(\tau\eta)(j)=\eta(\tau(j)).

The linear operator
P_{A}
is the orthogonal projection, in
L^{2}(\mathcal{S}_{N,n},\mu)
, onto the space of functions that are symmetric w.r.t. permutations restricted to the subset
A
. When
A=\{i\}
we write
P_{\{i\}}=P_{i}
, and note that
P_{i}g=\mu(g\,|\,\eta_{\{i\}^{c}})=\mu_{i}g
for all
i
. Note also that
P_{A},P_{B}
do not commute for general
A,B\subset[n]
, but if
A\cap B=\emptyset
then
P_{A}P_{B}=P_{B}P_{A}
. Moreover, one easily checks that if
A\subset B\subset[n]
, then

(4.6)

\displaystyle P_{A}P_{B}=P_{B\setminus A}P_{A}=P_{A}P_{B\setminus A}=P_{B}P_{A},\qquad\;A\subset B.

Also, observe that the orthogonal projection
\mu_{A}
defined above satisfies

(4.7)

\displaystyle\mu_{A}=\prod_{i\in A}P_{i}.

Notice that
P_{[n]}g=g
iff
g\in{\mathbb{S}}
, and that in this case

(4.8)

\displaystyle P_{A}g=P_{A^{c}}g=P_{A}P_{A^{c}}g=P_{A^{c}}P_{A}g\,,\qquad g\in{\mathbb{S}}.

For any fixed
f\;\geqslant\;0
, and
A\subset[n]
, define

(4.9)

\displaystyle\phi(A;f)=\mu\left[f\log(f/P_{A}f)\right].

The function
\phi
is a suitable conditional entropy of
f
on
A
. Namely,
\phi(A;f)
is the expected value with respect to
\mu
of the entropy

P_{A}\left[f\log(f/P_{A}f)\right].

In particular,
\phi(A;f)\;\geqslant\;0
.
We call
\phi(A;f)
the
permutation entropy
of
f
on
A
.
When there is no risk of confusion we write simply
\phi(A)
for
\phi(A;f)
. In general, one has

Lemma 4.3
.

Fix a function
f\;\geqslant\;0
. For any
i\in[n]
,
\phi(\{i\})=\mu\left[{\rm Ent}_{i}(f)\right]
,
and for all
A\subset[n]
:

(4.10)

\phi(A)=\mu\left[{\rm Ent}_{A}(f)\right]-\mu\left[{\rm Ent}_{A}(P_{A}f)\right]\,.

Moreover, for all
A\subset[n]

(4.11)

\phi(A)\;\leqslant\;2\mathcal{E}_{A}(f,\log f),

where

(4.12)

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{A}(f,\log f)=\frac{1}{2}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j<\ell}\mu\left[\left(f^{j,\ell,A}-f\right)\log\frac{f^{j,\ell,A}}{f}\right].

Proof.

If
A=\{i\}
, then
P_{A}f=\mu_{i}f
and therefore
\phi(\{i\})=\mu\left[{\rm Ent}_{i}(f)\right]
. In general, for any
A
,

\displaystyle\mu\left[{\rm Ent}_{A}(f)\right]

\displaystyle=\mu\left[f\log(f/\mu_{A}f)\right]

\displaystyle=\mu\left[f\log(f/P_{A}f)\right]+\mu\left[f\log(P_{A}f/\mu_{A}f)\right].

The second term above satisfies

\mu\left[f\log(P_{A}f/\mu_{A}f)\right]=\mu\left[P_{A}f\log(P_{A}f/\mu_{A}P_{A}f)\right]=\mu\left[{\rm Ent}_{A}(P_{A}f)\right]\,,

where we have used that
\mu_{B}P_{A}=\mu_{B}
for any
A\subset B\subset[n]
since
P_{A}
is an orthogonal projection. This proves (
4.10
).

To prove (
4.11
), observe that for any
A\subset[n]
,
f\;\geqslant\;0
, any fixed
\eta\in\mathcal{S}_{N,n}
, taking
g(\tau):=f((\tau\circ\eta)_{A}\eta_{A^{c}})
, one has
\bar{g}=P_{A}f
, and therefore by Lemma
4.2
,

\displaystyle P_{A}\left[f\log(f/P_{A}f)\right](\eta)=\frac{1}{N!}\sum_{\tau\in S_{N}}g(\tau)\log(g(\tau)/\bar{g})

\displaystyle\;\leqslant\;\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j<\ell}\frac{1}{N!}\sum_{\tau\in S_{N}}\left(f((\tau^{j,\ell}\eta)_{A}\eta_{A^{c}})-f((\tau\eta)_{A}\eta_{A^{c}})\right)\log\frac{f((\tau^{j,\ell}\eta)_{A}\eta_{A^{c}})}{f((\tau\eta)_{A}\eta_{A^{c}})}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j<\ell}P_{A}\left[\left(f^{j,\ell,A}-f\right)\log\frac{f^{j,\ell,A}}{f}\right](\eta).

Taking the expectation and using
\mu P_{A}=\mu
one obtains (
4.11
).
∎

Next, we compare the permutation entropy
\phi(A)
with the usual entropy
\mu\left[{\rm Ent}_{A}(f)\right]
.
The previous lemma in particular shows that
\phi(A)\;\leqslant\;\mu\left[{\rm Ent}_{A}(f)\right]
.
The next lemma allows us to give a bound in the opposite direction. Notice that such a bound needs some care since if e.g.
f\in{\mathbb{S}}
is not a constant then
\phi([n];f)=0
while
\mu\left[{\rm Ent}_{[n]}(f)\right]={\rm Ent}(f)\neq 0
.

Lemma 4.4
.

Fix
A\subset[n]
such that
0\;\leqslant\;|A|\;\leqslant\;n-1
, and
V\subset A^{c}
.
Then,

(4.13)

\phi(A)+\sum_{i\in V}\phi(A\cup\{i\})\;\geqslant\;\mu\left[{\rm Ent}_{V}f\right].

Proof.

Write
V=\{x_{1},\dots,x_{k}\}\subset[n]
,
k=|V|
, and define
A_{0}=A
, and

A_{i}=A\cup\{x_{i}\}
,
i=1,\dots,k
. In general
P_{A_{i}}
and
P_{A_{j}}
do not commute for
i,j=1,\dots,k
, but using (
4.6
) one has
P_{A_{0}}P_{A_{i}}=P_{A_{0}}P_{x_{i}}=P_{A}\mu_{x_{i}}
and for any
\ell=1,\dots,k
,

P^{\ell}:=P_{A_{0}}P_{A_{1}}\cdots P_{A_{\ell}}=P_{A}\prod_{i=1}^{\ell}\mu_{x_{i}}=\left(\prod_{i=1}^{\ell}\mu_{x_{i}}\right)P_{A}=\prod_{i=0}^{\ell}P_{A_{i}}\,,

where the last identity holds regardless of the order of the multiplications.
In other words, the operators
P_{A_{i}}
commute thanks to the presence of
P_{A_{0}}
. In particular,
P^{k}=P_{A}\mu_{V}=\mu_{V}P_{A}
.

Consider the entropy

(4.14)

\displaystyle\mu\left[f\log\frac{f}{P^{k}f}\right]=\mu\left[f\log\frac{f}{\mu_{V}f}\right]+\mu\left[f\log\frac{\mu_{V}f}{P_{A}\mu_{V}f}\right].

The first term is
\mu\left[{\rm Ent}_{V}f\right]
. The second term satisfies

(4.15)

\displaystyle\mu\left[f\log\frac{\mu_{V}f}{P_{A}\mu_{V}f}\right]=\mu\left[\mu_{V}f\log\frac{\mu_{V}f}{\mu_{V}P_{A}f}\right]=\phi(A;\mu_{V}f)\;\geqslant\;0.

On the other hand,

\log\frac{f}{P^{k}f}=\log\frac{f}{P_{A_{0}}f}+\log\frac{P_{A_{0}}f}{P_{A_{0}}P_{A_{1}}f}+\cdots+\log\frac{P_{A_{0}}P_{A_{1}}\cdots P_{A_{k-1}}f}{P_{A_{0}}P_{A_{1}}\cdots P_{A_{k}}f},

and therefore

(4.16)

\displaystyle\mu\left[f\log\frac{f}{P^{k}f}\right]=\phi(A;f)+\sum_{\ell=0}^{k-1}\mu\left[f\log\frac{P^{\ell}f}{P^{\ell+1}f}\right].

Next, we show that

(4.17)

\mu\left[f\log\frac{P^{\ell}f}{P^{\ell+1}f}\right]\;\leqslant\;\phi\left(A_{\ell+1};f\right),

for all
\ell=0,\dots,k-1
. Combined with (
4.14
)-(
4.16
), this implies
the desired conclusion:

(4.18)

\phi(A)+\sum_{i\in V}\phi(A\cup\{i\})\;\geqslant\;\mu\left[{\rm Ent}_{V}f\right]+\phi(A;\mu_{V}f)\;\geqslant\;\mu\left[{\rm Ent}_{V}f\right].

It remains to prove (
4.17
).
We first observe that

(4.19)

\mu\left[f\log\frac{P^{\ell}f}{P^{\ell+1}f}\right]=\mu\left[P^{\ell}f\log\frac{P^{\ell}f}{P^{\ell+1}f}\right]=\phi\left(A_{\ell+1};P^{\ell}f\right).

The well known variational principle for the relative entropy implies that for any
B\subset[n]

P_{B}\left(f\log\frac{f}{P_{B}f}\right)\;\geqslant\;P_{B}(fg)\,,

for any function
g
such that
P_{B}(e^{g})=1
. Choosing
g=\log\frac{P^{\ell}f}{P_{B}P^{\ell}f}
shows that

P_{B}\left(f\log\frac{f}{P_{B}f}\right)\;\geqslant\;P_{B}\left(f\log\frac{P^{\ell}f}{P_{B}P^{\ell}f}\right)\,.

Taking the expectation one finds

\phi(B;f)\;\geqslant\;\mu\left[f\log\frac{P^{\ell}f}{P_{B}P^{\ell}f}\right]\,.

If
P_{B},P^{\ell}
commute, then

\mu\left[f\log\frac{P^{\ell}f}{P_{B}P^{\ell}f}\right]=\mu\left[f\log\frac{P^{\ell}f}{P^{\ell}P_{B}f}\right]=\mu\left[P^{\ell}f\log\frac{P^{\ell}f}{P^{\ell}P_{B}f}\right]=\phi(B;P^{\ell}f).

Therefore,

\phi(B;f)\;\geqslant\;\phi(B;P^{\ell}f),

whenever
P_{B},P^{\ell}
commute. Taking
B=A_{\ell+1}
, and using the fact that
P_{A_{\ell+1}}
and
P^{\ell}
commute, one obtains

\phi\left(A_{\ell+1};P^{\ell}f\right)\;\leqslant\;\phi\left(A_{\ell+1};f\right)
. Together with (
4.19
), this implies (
4.17
).
∎

4.3.
Proof of Theorem
1.8

From the strict separation assumption, it follows that for some
\delta(\nu)>0
,

\mathcal{E}_{N,n}(f,\log f)\;\geqslant\;\frac{\delta(\nu)}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\mathcal{E}_{A_{i}}(f,\log f)+\mathcal{E}_{A_{i}\setminus\{i\}}(f,\log f)\right),

where, for every
i
,
A_{i}\subset[n]
is such that
A_{i}\ni i
and
\min\{\nu(A_{i}),\nu(A_{i}\setminus\{i\})\}\;\geqslant\;\delta(\nu)
.
Therefore, from Lemma
4.3
,

(4.20)

\mathcal{E}_{N,n}(f,\log f)\;\geqslant\;\frac{\delta(\nu)}{2n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\phi(A_{i})+\phi(A_{i}\setminus\{i\})\right).

Lemma
4.4
then implies

(4.21)

\mathcal{E}_{N,n}(f,\log f)\;\geqslant\;\frac{\delta(\nu)}{2n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mu\left[{\rm Ent}_{i}f\right].

Using (
4.4
) it follows that
\alpha(N,n)\;\geqslant\;\delta(\nu)/{2n}
. This proves the bound (
1.18
) with
\alpha(\nu)=\delta(\nu)/{2n}
.

To prove the lower bound for one-point crossover, notice that the above argument can be repeated but this time we can directly estimate

\mathcal{E}_{N,n}(f,\log f)\;\geqslant\;\frac{1}{4}\frac{1}{n+1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\phi(J_{i})+\phi(J_{i-1}))\;\geqslant\;\frac{1}{4(n+1)}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mu\left[{\rm Ent}_{i}f\right],

where
J_{0}=\emptyset
, and
J_{i}=\{1,\dots,i\}
,
i\;\geqslant\;1
.
The lower bound
\alpha(N,n)\;\geqslant\;1/4(n+1)
thus follows again by the tensorization (
4.4
).

Next, we prove the lower bound for the case of uniform crossover
\nu(A)=2^{-n}

for all
A\subset[n]
. From Lemma
4.3

\mathcal{E}_{N,n}(f,\log f)=2^{-n}\sum_{A}\mathcal{E}_{A}(f,\log f)\;\geqslant\;2^{-n-1}\sum_{A}\phi(A).

By Lemma
4.4
,

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{A}\phi(A)\mathbf{1}(i\in A)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{A:\,|A|\;\leqslant\;n-1}\phi(A\cup\{i\})\mathbf{1}(i\notin A)

\displaystyle\;\geqslant\;\sum_{A:\,|A|\;\leqslant\;n-1}\left(\mu\left[{\rm Ent}_{A^{c}}f\right]-\phi(A)\right).

Therefore,

(4.22)

\displaystyle\sum_{A}\phi(A)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{A}\phi(A)(\mathbf{1}(i\notin A)+\mathbf{1}(i\in A))

(4.23)

\displaystyle\;\geqslant\;\frac{1}{n}\sum_{A}|A^{c}|\phi(A)+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{A:\,|A|\;\leqslant\;n-1}\left(\mu\left[{\rm Ent}_{A^{c}}f\right]-\phi(A)\right)

(4.24)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{A:\,|A|\;\leqslant\;n-1}(|A^{c}|-1)\phi(A)+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{A}\mu\left[{\rm Ent}_{A^{c}}f\right].

In particular,

2^{-n-1}\sum_{A}\phi(A)\;\geqslant\;\frac{2^{-n}}{2n}\sum_{A}\mu\left[{\rm Ent}_{A^{c}}f\right].

From Lemma
4.1
it follows that

2^{-n-1}\sum_{A}\phi(A)\;\geqslant\;\frac{1}{4n}{\rm Ent}f.

This proves the desired bound
\alpha(N,n)\;\geqslant\;\frac{1}{4n}
.

Finally, in the case of symmetric functions one has

\phi(A)=\phi(A^{c})
for all
A\subset[n]
, see (
4.8
). Therefore, the previous computation now shows that

\sum_{A}\phi(A)\;\geqslant\;\frac{2}{n}\sum_{A}\mu\left[{\rm Ent}_{A^{c}}f\right]-\frac{2}{n}\sum_{A}\phi(A).

Rearranging terms yields the bound
\alpha_{\mathbb{S}}(N,n)\;\geqslant\;\frac{1}{2(n+2)}
. This concludes the proof of Theorem
1.8
.

Appendix A
Proof of Proposition
2.2

In order to prove Proposition
2.2
we adapt to our multivariate setting some classical estimates, see e.g.
[
26
,
36
]
. For the sake of clarity we give a self-contained proof. Recall the notation from Section
2
. In particular,
|t|=\sqrt{\langle t,t\rangle}
denotes the vector norm of
t\in{\mathbb{R}}^{K}
, and the random variable
\xi=(\xi_{i,x})
with distribution
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(X)
takes values in
X=\{0,1\}^{K}
, and has covariance matrix
V_{1}
. The next lemma only requires the nondegeneracy of
\mu
, that is
\det(V_{1})\neq 0
. The proof of Proposition
2.2
however requires the irreducibility of
\mu
in order to apply Lemma
2.5
.

Lemma A.1
.

Let
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(X)
be nondegenerate. For any
t\in{\mathbb{R}}^{K}
, define

\phi_{N}(t):=\mu\left(e^{i\langle V_{N}^{-1/2}t\,,\,\bar{\xi}\rangle}\right)^{N},

where
\bar{\xi}_{i,x}:=\xi_{i,x}-\mu\left[\xi_{i,x}\right],

V_{N}=NV_{1}
,
and
L_{N}:=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\,\mu\left[\big|V_{1}^{-1/2}\bar{\xi}\big|^{3}\right].
Then

(A.1)

\displaystyle\left|\phi_{N}(t)-e^{-\frac{1}{2}\langle t,t\rangle}\right|\;\leqslant\;16L_{N}|t|^{3}e^{-\frac{1}{3}\langle t,t\rangle}\,,\qquad|t|\;\leqslant\;\frac{1}{4L_{N}}.

Proof.

We split the proof into two. First, let us suppose that
\frac{1}{4L_{N}}\;\geqslant\;|t|\;\geqslant\;\frac{1}{2}L_{N}^{-\frac{1}{3}}.
If one has

(A.2)

\displaystyle|\phi_{N}(t)|^{2}\;\leqslant\;e^{-\frac{2}{3}\langle t,t\rangle}

then

(A.3)

\displaystyle\left|\phi_{N}(t)-e^{-\frac{1}{2}\langle t,t\rangle}\right|\;\leqslant\;|\phi_{N}(t)|+e^{-\frac{1}{2}\langle t,t\rangle}\;\leqslant\;2e^{-\frac{1}{3}\langle t,t\rangle}\;\leqslant\;16L_{N}|t|^{3}e^{-\frac{1}{3}\langle t,t\rangle}.

We now show (
A.2
).
Let
\phi(t):=\mu\left[e^{i\langle t,\bar{\xi}\rangle}\right].
Define
\Psi:=\Psi_{1}-\Psi_{2},
where
\Psi_{1}
and
\Psi_{2}
are independent and indentically distributed as
\bar{\xi}
, so that the characteristic function of
\Psi
is
|\phi(t)|^{2},
and its covariance matrix is
2V_{1}.
Next we use

(A.4)

\displaystyle R(x)=e^{ix}-\left(1+ix-\frac{x^{2}}{2}\right),\qquad|R(x)|\;\leqslant\;\min\left\{|x|^{2},\frac{|x|^{3}}{6}\right\},

for all
x\in{\mathbb{R}}.
By substituting
x=\langle t,\Psi\rangle
in (
A.4
) and taking the expectation,

(A.5)

\displaystyle|\phi(t)|^{2}

\displaystyle=1-\frac{1}{2}\mu\left[\left(\langle t,\Psi\rangle\right)^{2}\right]+\mu\left[R\left(\langle t,\Psi\rangle\right)\right]

(A.6)

\displaystyle\;\leqslant\;1-\langle t,V_{1}t\rangle+\frac{1}{6}\mu\left[\left|\langle t,\Psi\rangle\right|^{3}\right].

Then, by using the fact that
(1+x)^{N}\;\leqslant\;e^{Nx}
for all
x,
that
V_{N}^{-1/2}
is self-adjoint, and the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality,

(A.7)

\displaystyle|\phi_{N}(t)|^{2}

For all

Proof of Proposition
2.2
.

Set

. Using the Fourier transform as in the proof of Theorem
2.6

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{K}}e^{-i\langle s,z_{N}\rangle-\frac{\langle s,s\rangle}{2}}ds=(2\pi)^{\frac{K}{2}}e^{-\frac{\langle z_{N},z_{N}\rangle}{2}},

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{B_{N}(2\pi)^{K}}\left|\int_{Q_{N,K}}e^{-i\langle s,z_{N}\rangle}\phi_{N}(s)ds-\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{K}}e^{-i\langle s,z_{N}\rangle-\frac{1}{2}\langle s,s\rangle}ds\right|
