Quantum Backreaction effect in Optical Solitons
Sang-Shin Baak, Friedrich König

Abstract
Optical solitons classically are stationary solutions of the nonlinear Schrödinger equation. We perform a quantum field theoretic treatment by quantising a linearised fluctuation field around the classical soliton solution which can be seen as providing a background spacetime for the field. The linearised fluctuation modifies the soliton background, which is often neglected, reminiscent of the nondepleted-pump approximation. Going beyond this approximation and by using a number-conserving Bogoljubov approach, we find unstable modes that grow as the soliton propagates. Eventually, these unstable modes induce a considerable (backreaction) effect in the soliton. We calculate the backreaction in the classical field fully analytically in the leading second order. The result is a quadratic local decrease of the soliton photon number in propagation due to the backreaction effect of the unstable mode. Provided the initial pulse is close to the classical soliton solution, the unstable mode contributions always become dominant. We also consider practical scenarios for observing this quantum-induced soliton distortion, in the spectral domain. The backreaction, which we expect to be present in bright and dark, discrete and continuous solitons and other nonlinear pulses plays an important role for future optical analogue gravity experiments, for soliton lasers, and optical communications.

Therefore, we also need to describe the impact of a quantum field onto a background spacetime.

In this setting, we first need to set the background, which evolves according to its own dynamics. Next, we plant an “object” (field) onto the background, which will interact with the background and modify its dynamical evolution. This modification is what we call the backreaction. Therefore, the backreaction is dependent on the choice of both the background and the object field. For example, in cosmology the object might be the inhomogeneous matter of our universe such as galaxies, dark matter, dark energy, etc. The backreaction then refers to the difference between the observed universe and the Freedmann-Lemaître-Robertson-Walker universe

In another example, semi-classical gravity, the background is chosen to be the spacetime evolving with classical dynamics whose source includes the classical matter distribution and objects are the quantum fields residing in it. In this case, the expectation value of the energy-momentum tensor, the source term in the semi-classical Einstein equation, is regarded as a backreaction.
The investigation of the backreaction of quantum fields in gravity is interesting, as it can serve as a next-order approximation to quantum gravity with observable effects.

Although significant progress has been made on the theory, the observation of QFTCS phenomena or the backreaction in real gravity has been elusive because of the large scale or the weakness of their signature. In his paper in 1981, Unruh predicted that Hawking radiation or the evaporation of black holes can be investigated in the laboratory in fluids using sound waves

The key insight is that the Hawking effect is kinematic and therefore reproducible in non-gravitational systems.
The linear perturbations of many physical systems can be shown to reside on an effective spacetime named analogue spacetime.
Typical analogue spacetimes investigated experimentally are of black holes or cosmic expansion.
Arguably, the most celebrated result to date is the observation of the Hawking effect in various systems in the lab

Although the background dynamics generally are different compared to gravity, the backreaction in analogue gravity allows us to investigate spacetime dynamics beyond the limited simulations on classical computers and establishes novel quantum-classical systems.

One of the most promising physical systems in analogue gravity is the fiber-optical system.

The relation to the standard linear soliton perturbation theory is presented.
While the linear order does not conserve the photon number, the field correction restores number-conservation in our working order and at the same time avoids rather involved coupled equations of motion.
A typical problem of the full NCBE is that it is hard to get to a practical solution, because it results in a complicated set of coupled equations.

II
Linearisation and the number-conserving expansion of the soliton field

The quantum dynamics of optical solitons is described by the quantized NLS (QNLS) equation.
This equation is integrable and the exact solution can be obtained following a Bethe ansatz
[
41
]
.
It was shown that for large photon numbers, the soliton classical NLS solution acts as the mean field in leading order
[
42
]
.
By quantizing a perturbation of this mean field, the interesting interplay between the classical and quantized NLS equations can be studied.
In this approach, the object is the linearized quantum fluctuation of the field.
The transformation of the linear fluctuation through the interaction with the soliton mean field has been studied theoretically and experimentally and many interesting phenomena have been established such as soliton squeezing and entanglement
[
43
,
44
,

,

,

This approach, however, is inadequate for describing the backreaction, because the background evolution is not affected by the quantum fluctuation. It is reminiscent of the
non-depleted pump
approximation in parametric down-conversion
[
53
,
54
]

To describe the backreaction effect, we make use of the number-conserving approach.
In this approach, we go beyond the linear expansion order by introducing a quadratic order correction to the classical field to recover photon number conservation in our working order. The classical field correction also is the backreaction.
In what follows, we first briefly review the QNLS equation and the linearisation approach. Starting with the classical Lagrangian for the NLS equation, we canonically quantise the field and derive the QNLS equation. We identify the equation of motion for both fields, the soliton
E_{\mathrm{s}}
and the linear quantum fluctuation
\Delta E
.
We identify the expansion parameter and expand to quadratic order. Then we obtain the classical field correction
E_{\mathrm{c}}
and derive an evolution equation for this field.

II.1
Linearisation around the soliton solution to the quantum nonlinear Schrödinger equation

The classical electric field in a nonlinear optical fiber satisfies the nonlinear Schrödinger equation
[
55
]
.
The Lagrangian for the NLS equation is
[
56
]

\mathcal{L}=\frac{i}{2}(E^{*}\partial_{t}E-\partial_{t}E^{*}E)-\frac{1}{2}|\partial_{x}E|^{2}+\frac{1}{2}|E|^{4},

(1)

where
E
describes the slowly varying envelope field amplitude and we employ soliton units
[
55
]
.
The fundamental soliton solution is of the form

E_{\mathrm{s}}(t,x)=\sech x\,\mathrm{e}^{it/2}.

(2)

The solution famously has a stationary field envelope as it propagates along the fiber.

In order to quantise the equation of motion, we follow the canonical quantisation procedure.
From the Lagrangian (
1
), we calculate the conjugate momenta of the electric field,

\Pi_{E}=iE^{*}.

(3)

The QNLS equation is obtained by promoting the electric field to a quantum field.
Hence, we impose the equal-time commutation relations

\displaystyle\frac{1}{i}[\hat{E}(t,x),\hat{\Pi}_{E}(t,x^{\prime})]=[\hat{E}(t,x),\hat{E}^{\dagger}(t,x^{\prime})]

\displaystyle=\delta(x-x^{\prime}),

(4)

\displaystyle[\hat{E}(t,x),\hat{E}(t,x^{\prime})]=[\hat{E}^{\dagger}(t,x),\hat{E}^{\dagger}(t,x^{\prime})]

\displaystyle=0.

(5)

The corresponding Hamiltonian for the QNLS equation is
[
56
]

\hat{H}=\frac{1}{2}\int\mathrm{d}x[\partial_{x}\hat{E}^{\dagger}\partial_{x}\hat{E}-\hat{E}^{\dagger}\hat{E}^{\dagger}\hat{E}\hat{E}],

(6)

where normal ordering is adopted and
\hbar=1
for simplicity.
Finally, the equation of motion of the quantized envelope
\hat{E}
is

\left(i\partial_{t}+\frac{\partial_{x}^{2}}{2}+\hat{E}^{\dagger}\hat{E}\right)\hat{E}=0.

(7)

Analogous to the NLS equation, Eq. (
7
) is called the quantum NLS (QNLS) equation.
The exact energy eigenvalues and eigenfunctions for this equation can be obtained following a Bethe ansatz.
The exact solutions, however, obtained with this ansatz do not easily relate to the fundamental soliton solution under consideration here.
It is known that the quantum dynamics of the NLS soliton can be described by the Hartree approximation provided the photon number is large
[
42
,
41
]
.
This routinely used technique is a linearisation around the soliton and facilitates our investigation of the the QNLS equation.

We now briefly introduce this linearisation approach.
The procedure starts with writing the quantized envelope field
\hat{E}
as the sum of a fundamental soliton
E_{\mathrm{s}}
and a small quantum fluctuation
\Delta\hat{E}
:

\hat{E}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle!}}{{=}}E_{\mathrm{s}}+\epsilon\Delta\hat{E}.

(8)

where
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle!}}{{=}}
indicates that the expansion is only valid in the linearisation approach.
\epsilon
is a small number for specifying perturbation. It will be clarified later.
Because the classical field commutes, the commutation relations (
4
) and (
5
) become

[\Delta\hat{E}(t,x),\Delta\hat{E}^{\dagger}(t,x^{\prime})]=\delta(x-x^{\prime}),\qquad[\Delta\hat{E}(t,x),\Delta\hat{E}(t,x^{\prime})]=0.

(9)

Next we recall that the soliton is the solution of the NLS equation, i.e.

\left(i\partial_{t}+\frac{\partial_{x}^{2}}{2}+|E_{\mathrm{s}}|^{2}\right)E_{\mathrm{s}}=0,

(10)

which forms our zeroth order description. This equation does not contain a
\Delta\hat{E}
contribution, i.e. the background soliton is unaffected by the quantum fluctuation.
Substituting the ansatz (
8
) into the QNLS equation (
7
) and using Eq. (
10
), the resulting equation is at least linear order in
\Delta\hat{E}
.
Provided
|\Delta E|^{2}\ll|E_{\mathrm{s}}|^{2}
, we may neglect higher orders and arrive at the so-called Bogoliubov-de Gennes (BdG) equation

\displaystyle\left(i\partial_{t}+\frac{\partial_{x}^{2}}{2}+2|{E}_{\mathrm{s}}|^{2}\right)\Delta\hat{E}+{E}_{\mathrm{s}}^{2}\Delta\hat{E}^{\dagger}=0.

(11)

The equation with the linear approximation above describe the time evolution of the linear quantum fluctuation.
The linearisation approach has been successfully used for the quantum noise evolution or soliton squeezing
[
49
,
57
,
58
,
59
]
, but it
does not describe effects beyond the linear order
[
60
,
61
]
.
The backreaction in analogue gravity, in which we are interested, is a quantity beyond linear order; and so we need a theory going beyond linear order.
The framework we use for this purpose is the number-conserving expansion, which we explain next.

II.2
Beyond linearisation: number-conserving expansion

Before detailing the number-conserving expansion, we clarify the importance of photon-number conservation in the QNLS system.
The QNLS Hamiltonian (
6
) has a global
U(1)
-symmetry, leading to the following continuity equation for the QNLS field
\hat{E}
,

\partial_{t}\rho+\partial_{x}J=0,

(12)

where
\hat{\rho}
corresponds to the photon-number density and
\hat{J}
to the photon current:

\displaystyle\rho

\displaystyle=\langle\hat{\rho}\rangle=\langle\hat{E}^{\dagger}\hat{E}\rangle,

(13)

\displaystyle J

\displaystyle=\langle\hat{J}\rangle=\frac{1}{2i}\langle\hat{E}^{\dagger}\partial_{x}\hat{E}-\partial_{x}\hat{E}^{\dagger}\hat{E}\rangle.

(14)

where
\langle\cdot\rangle
is the quantum expectation value.
Consequently, the total photon number is conserved in time.
In addition, it can be used as an expansion parameter in the linearisation approach as follows.
Let us recall that
\int|E_{s}|^{2}\mathrm{d}x=2=\mathcal{O}(1)
. Thus, all terms in the NLS equation (
10
) are of order one.
Moreover, all the terms in the BdG equation (
11
) are of the same order as
\Delta\hat{E}
.
Also recall that if the photon number is large, the exact solution goes to the fundamental soliton solution.
Therefore, the natural choice of ratio between classical soliton field and its fluctuation is

\frac{\langle\Delta\hat{E}^{\dagger}\Delta\hat{E}\rangle}{\langle\hat{E}^{\dagger}\hat{E}\rangle}\sim\frac{1}{N}=:\epsilon^{2}\ll 1

(15)

where
N
is the total photon number of the system.
In other words, the inverse square root of the total photon number is the expansion parameter
\epsilon
in the linearisation approach.
Therefore, it is important to maintain the number-conservation in the working order to have a well-defined expansion of the field
[
36
,
62
]
.

A number-conserving expansion is a popular way to obtain a field expansion beyond linearisation.
The number conservation can be maintained in each order by inclusion of an additional annihilation operator to keep the
U(1)
-symmetry in the expansion
[
33
,
35
,
34
,
62
,
38
,
39
,
40
,
36
,
18
]
.
This often leads to complicated, coupled equations, which are hard to solve.
To avoid these complications, we modify the approach by limiting the number-conservation to our working order (
\sim\epsilon^{2}
).

In our approach, we want to obtain the classical field correction describing the backreaction effect.
Recall the classical electric field is regarded as the quantum expectation value of the quantum electric field
[
63
]
.
In the linearisation approach, this field is the classical soliton
E_{s}
. In the number-conserving approach we additionally introduce a 2nd order correction term
E_{c}
, a quantum correction to the classical field, which recovers number conservation.
It follows, that the field expansion may not lie around the soliton field
E_{\mathrm{s}}
, but needs a small correction.
We use the conventional linearisation approach, breaking the
U(1)
-symmetry, and apply a correction term to the classical field which recovers the number-conservation at the desired order (
\sim\epsilon^{2}
)
[
28
]
:

\langle\hat{E}\rangle=E_{\mathrm{s}}+\epsilon^{2}E_{\mathrm{c}}.

(16)

The number-conserving expansion for the quantized envelope field is therefore

\hat{E}=E_{\mathrm{s}}+\epsilon\Delta\hat{E}+\epsilon^{2}E_{\mathrm{c}}

(17)

The equation of motion can be obtained by expanding the QNLS equation and collecting terms in orders of
\epsilon
, respectively.
Since the correction term is of order
\epsilon^{2}
, it does not affect the leading-order and linear-order dynamics.
In other words, the leading-order expansion gives the NLS equation (
10
), and therefore the soliton is still the leading-order solution of the classical field.
The linear fluctuation
\Delta\hat{E}
satisfies the BdG equation (
19
).
The quadratic order term,
E_{\mathrm{c}}
, which corresponds to the correction of the classical electric field, satisfies an inhomogeneous equation with
\Delta\hat{E}
-dependent source terms. It has a BdG-like homogeneous part:

\displaystyle\left(i\partial_{t}+\frac{\partial_{x}^{2}}{2}+2|E_{\mathrm{s}}|^{2}\right)E_{\mathrm{c}}+E_{\mathrm{s}}^{2}E_{\mathrm{c}}^{*}=-2\langle\Delta\hat{E}^{\dagger}\Delta\hat{E}\rangle E_{\mathrm{s}}-\langle\Delta\hat{E}^{2}\rangle E_{\mathrm{s}}^{*}.

(18)

Note that the right hand side of the equation contains the expectation value of the quantum fluctuation in the background soliton.
Therefore,
E_{\mathrm{c}}
is the leading order backreaction effect and we call it the quantum backreaction (QBR) equation.

III
The Bogoliubov-de Gennes equation

In the previous section we derived a set of equations for describing the backreaction for a classical soliton background. In this section, we solve the Bogoliubov-de Gennes equation (
11
)
such that we expand the field in its normal modes. We also calculate vacuum expectation values relevant in section
IV
.

We start by considering the mathematical properties of the BdG Hamiltonian, which produces the Bogoliubov-de Gennes equation (
11
), because it allows us to understand the structure of eigenvalues and eigenvectors of the BdG solutions.
In Sec.
III.1
below, we summarize its nontrivial structure, which lies beyond Hermitian quantum mechanics.
Then, we derive the mode structure of the BdG Hamiltonian in Sec.
III.2
, including zero modes and generalized eigenmodes. We show that these modes can be used for constructing the complete solution of the BdG equation. Finally, in Sec.
III.3
, we quantise the BdG field solution and calculate the expectation value of photon number density and anomalous photon number density relevant for section
IV
.

III.1
Mathematical properties of the Bogoliubov-de Gennes Hamiltonian

With
\Delta\hat{E}(t,x)=\hat{\psi}(t,x)\mathrm{e}^{it/2}
and
\tilde{E}_{\mathrm{s}}:=\sech x
the BdG equation becomes:

\displaystyle\left(i\partial_{t}+\frac{\partial_{x}^{2}}{2}+2\tilde{E}_{\mathrm{s}}^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\right)\hat{\psi}+\tilde{E}_{\mathrm{s}}^{2}\hat{\psi}^{\dagger}=0.

(19)

We are interested in the mode solutions of (
19
). Thus, for simplicity we revert
\psi
back to a classical field.
Let us define the Nambu spinor
|\Phi\rangle:=(\psi,\psi^{*})^{\intercal}
in order to rewrite the BdG equation as a Schrödinger equation:

(i\partial_{t}-\boldsymbol{H}_{\mathrm{BdG}})|\Phi\rangle=0,

(20)

where

\boldsymbol{H}_{\mathrm{BdG}}:=\left(-\frac{\partial_{x}^{2}}{2}-2\tilde{E}_{s}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\right)\sigma_{3}-i\sigma_{2}\tilde{E}_{s}^{2}

(21)

is the BdG Hamiltonian.
The matrices
\sigma_{i}
,
i\in\{1,2,3\}
denote the Pauli matrices.
With a linear Hermitian automorphism operator
\eta
for a space of operators
\boldsymbol{O}
on the Hilbert space, one can define the
\eta
-pseudo-Hermitian adjoint

\boldsymbol{O}^{\sharp}:=\eta^{-1}\boldsymbol{O}^{\dagger}\eta.

(22)

Hence, if
\boldsymbol{O}
is identical to the pseudo-Hermitian adjoint of the operator,
\boldsymbol{O}^{\sharp}=\boldsymbol{O}
, it is called
\eta
-pseudo-Hermitian
[
64
,
65
,
66
,
67
]
.
From Eq. (
21
), one can easily show that the BdG Hamiltonian
\boldsymbol{H}_{\mathrm{BdG}}
is
\sigma_{3}
-pseudo-Hermitian,

\boldsymbol{H}_{\mathrm{BdG}}^{\sharp}=\sigma_{3}^{-1}\boldsymbol{H}_{\mathrm{BdG}}^{\dagger}\sigma_{3}=\boldsymbol{H}_{\mathrm{BdG}}.

(23)

Since
\sigma_{3}^{-1}=\sigma_{3}^{\dagger}=\sigma_{3}
, the
\sigma_{3}
-pseudo-adjoint is not only a similarity transform, but also a unitary transform.
The
\sigma_{3}
-pseudo-Hermiticity of the Hamiltonian leads to a conserved inner product
[
68
,
69
,
70
,
71
]

\langle\Phi|\Phi\rangle_{\sigma_{3}}:=\langle\Phi|\sigma_{3}|\Phi\rangle.

(24)

Another property of the
\boldsymbol{H}_{\mathrm{BdG}}
is the so-called particle-hole symmetry

\sigma_{1}\boldsymbol{H}_{\mathrm{BdG}}\sigma_{1}=-\boldsymbol{H}_{\mathrm{BdG}}^{*}.

(25)

It can be used to find a negative frequency (or negative norm) partner mode of a positive frequency mode of Eq. (
20
)
1
1

1

In fact,
\boldsymbol{H}_{\mathrm{BdG}}^{*}=\boldsymbol{H}_{\mathrm{BdG}}
here, but we use the complex conjugate for to conform to literature
[

]
.
.
To show this, let
|\Phi^{(\omega)}\rangle
be the normalised positive norm right eigenmode of the BdG Hamiltonian satisfying

\boldsymbol{H}_{\mathrm{BdG}}|\Phi^{(\omega)}\rangle=\omega|\Phi^{(\omega)}\rangle

(26)

and

\langle\Phi^{(\omega)}|\Phi^{(\omega)}\rangle_{\sigma_{3}}=1.

(27)

It is known that
{\rm Spec}(\boldsymbol{H_{\mathrm{BdG}}})\subset\mathbb{R}
and therefore,
\omega^{*}=\omega
.
Using Eqs. (
25
) and (
26
), one can show that

\boldsymbol{H}_{\mathrm{BdG}}\sigma_{1}|\Phi^{(\omega)}\rangle^{*}=-\omega\sigma_{1}|\Phi^{(\omega)}\rangle^{*}.

(28)

Therefore,
|\bar{\Phi}^{(\omega)}\rangle:=\sigma_{1}|\Phi^{(\omega)}\rangle^{*}
is a right eigenvector with eigenvalue
-\omega
.
Moreover, the spinor has negative norm as

\langle\Phi^{(\omega)}|\sigma_{1}\sigma_{3}\sigma_{1}|\Phi^{(\omega)}\rangle=-\langle\Phi^{(\omega)}|\sigma_{3}|\Phi^{(\omega)}\rangle=-1.

(29)

Positive and negative norm states appear in pairs, giving the property (
25
) its name, particle-hole symmetry.
Like for Hermitian operators, the eigenstates for differing eigenvalues are orthogonal.
Using a complete set of eigenstates, the field can be expanded in the form

\displaystyle|\Phi\rangle

\displaystyle=\SumInt|\Phi^{(\omega)}\rangle\langle\Phi^{(\omega)}|\Phi\rangle_{\sigma_{3}},

where
\SumInt
is the symbolic expression for summing and integrating over all possible modes.
Because we now have a formal expansion for the spinor field

III.2
Mode Structure for the Bogoliubov-de Gennes Hamiltonian

We obtain the eigenmodes of the BdG Hamiltonian by the inverse scattering transform method
[
73
]
.
With
|\Phi^{(\omega)}(t,x)\rangle:=|\Phi_{k}(x)\rangle\mathrm{e}^{-i\omega t}
, Eq. (
20
) becomes the mode equation

(\omega-\boldsymbol{H}_{\mathrm{BdG}})|\Phi_{k}\rangle=0,

(38)

with explicit positive frequency modes

|\Phi_{k}\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}(k^{2}+1)}\begin{pmatrix}(\tanh{x}+ik)^{2}\\
-\sech^{2}\!\!x\end{pmatrix}\mathrm{e}^{-ikx},

(39)

where the eigenmodes satisfy

\boldsymbol{H}_{\mathrm{BdG}}|\Phi_{k}\rangle=\omega_{k}|\Phi_{k}\rangle

(40)

and the eigenfrequencies are

\omega_{k}=\frac{1+k^{2}}{2}.

(41)

Using the particle-hole symmetry, we find the eigenmodes with a negative eigenvalue by using Eq. (
28
),

|\bar{\Phi}_{k}\rangle:=\sigma_{1}|\Phi_{k}\rangle^{*}.

(42)

The negative frequency partner modes have the same frequencies as in relation (
41
), but of opposite sign.
Overall, the states satisfy the orthonormality relations:

\langle\Phi_{k}|\Phi_{k^{\prime}}\rangle_{\sigma_{3}}=-\langle\bar{\Phi}_{k}|\bar{\Phi}_{k^{\prime}}\rangle_{\sigma_{3}}=\delta(k-k^{\prime}),\qquad\langle\Phi_{k}|\bar{\Phi}_{k^{\prime}}\rangle_{\sigma_{3}}=0.

(43)

The modes are best labelled by
k
, as each real
k
indicates one mode pair.

The eigenmodes we have found at this point have either positive or negative, i.e. nonzero continuous real eigenvalues. These, however, do not form a complete basis of the solution space
[
73
]
. One reason for this is that the inverse scattering method does only describe modes that are asymptotic plane waves.In fact, we need four more modes to complete the basis, because the Hamiltonian (
21
) is a complex second order differential operator. Two eigenmodes can be found using the eigenvalue zero, which do not correspond to plane waves. We call them zero modes:

\displaystyle|\Phi_{(1)}^{\mathrm{zero}}\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\sech x\begin{pmatrix}1\\
-1\end{pmatrix},

(44)

\displaystyle|\Phi_{(2)}^{\mathrm{zero}}\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\tanh{x}\,\,\sech x\begin{pmatrix}1\\
1\end{pmatrix}.

(45)

Now we are looking for the two further modes to complete the space. Note that the zero modes have zero norm. For the continuous modes, the particle-hole symmetry allowed us to find negative norm partner modes. The zero modes are their own partner modes in the particle-hole symmetry. Therefore, we need to find the two further modes by other means.
It is known that each zero mode is accompanied by a ‘generalised eigenmode’
[
74
]
. These generalised eigenmodes are not eigenstates of the BdG Hamiltonian but can be used to find further linearly independent solutions of the BdG equation.

\displaystyle|\Phi_{(1)}^{\mathrm{ge}}\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(x\tanh{x}-1)\sech x\begin{pmatrix}1\\
1\end{pmatrix},

(46)

\displaystyle|\Phi_{(2)}^{\mathrm{ge}}\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}x\sech x\begin{pmatrix}1\\
-1\end{pmatrix}.

(47)

These modes satisfy

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{H}_{\mathrm{BdG}}|\Phi_{(i)}^{\mathrm{ge}}\rangle=|\Phi_{(i)}^{\mathrm{zero}}\rangle,\quad i=1,2.

(48)

The generalised eigenmodes have zero norm, too. The existence of these modes is a consequence of the nondiagonalizability of the BdG Hamiltonian
[
71
,
75
]
.
The zero modes and generalised eigenmodes both have zero norm.
Moreover, they are orthogonal to the continuous modes.
The nonzero inner products are

\displaystyle\langle\Phi_{(i)}^{\mathrm{zero}}|\Phi_{(j)}^{\mathrm{ge}}\rangle_{\sigma_{3}}

\displaystyle=\langle\Phi_{(i)}^{\mathrm{ge}}|\Phi_{(j)}^{\mathrm{zero}}\rangle_{\sigma_{3}}=(-1)^{i}\delta_{ij}.

(49)

From this, we construct further two solutions of the Bogoliubov equation Eq. (
20
):

|\Phi_{(i)}^{\mathrm{col}}\rangle=-it|\Phi_{(i)}^{\mathrm{zero}}\rangle+|\Phi_{(i)}^{\mathrm{ge}}\rangle.

(50)

Since these are independent solutions of the BdG equation, they can be used in the field expansion. Combined with the continuous modes and the zero modes, the field can be expressed as

\displaystyle|\Phi\rangle

\displaystyle=\int\mathrm{d}k\big(a_{k}\mathrm{e}^{-i\omega_{k}t}|\Phi_{k}\rangle+a_{k}^{*}\mathrm{e}^{i\omega_{k}t}|\bar{\Phi}_{k}\rangle\big)+\sum_{i=1,2}\big(b_{i}|\Phi_{(i)}^{\mathrm{zero}}\rangle+c_{i}|\Phi_{(i)}^{\mathrm{col}}\rangle\big)

(51)

where
a_{k},a_{k}^{*},b_{i},c_{i}\in\mathbb{C}
. The coefficient of each negative eigenvalue mode,
a_{k}^{*}
, is chosen to satisfy the particle-hole symmetry.
Importantly, the norm of this field is conserved:

\partial_{t}\langle\Phi|\Phi\rangle_{\sigma_{3}}=0.

(52)

In addition, any initial field at
t=0
can be expressed using the expansion by virtue of the completeness relation

\displaystyle\mathds{1}_{2}

\displaystyle\delta(x-x^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\int\mathrm{d}k\big(|\Phi_{k}(x)\rangle\langle\Phi_{k}(x^{\prime})|\sigma_{3}+|\bar{\Phi}_{k}(x)\rangle\langle\bar{\Phi}_{k}(x^{\prime})|\sigma_{3}\big)+\sum_{i}|\Phi_{(i)}^{\mathrm{zero}}(x)\rangle\langle\Phi_{(i)}^{\mathrm{col}}(x^{\prime})|\sigma_{3}+|\Phi_{(i)}^{\mathrm{col}}(x)\rangle\langle\Phi_{(i)}^{\mathrm{zero}}(x^{\prime})|\sigma_{3},

(53)

where
\mathds{1}_{2}
is the
2\times 2
identity matrix.

Finally, we mention a relation between the discrete modes and the linear perturbations of the soliton parameters, which can be found in
[
49
]
.
The NLS soliton, written in soliton parameters, writes:

E_{\mathrm{s}}=n_{0}\mathrm{e}^{i(n_{0}^{2}-p_{0}^{2})t/2}\sech[n_{0}(x-x_{0})-n_{0}p_{0}t]\mathrm{e}^{ip_{0}(x-x_{0})}\mathrm{e}^{i\theta_{0}}.

(54)

where
n_{0}
is the soliton photon number,
x_{0}
the soliton position,
\theta_{0}
the soliton phase, and
p_{0}
the soliton momentum.
Each of the four discrete modes is related to a variation of a soliton parameter in the following way:

\displaystyle v_{n}

\displaystyle=\partial_{n_{0}}E_{\mathrm{s}}=-\boldsymbol{P}_{1}|\Phi^{\mathrm{col}}_{(1)}\rangle,

(55)

\displaystyle v_{\theta}

\displaystyle=\partial_{\theta_{0}}E_{\mathrm{s}}=i\boldsymbol{P}_{1}|\Phi_{(1)}^{\mathrm{zero}}\rangle,

(56)

\displaystyle v_{p}

\displaystyle=\partial_{p_{0}}E_{\mathrm{s}}=i\boldsymbol{P}_{1}|\Phi^{\mathrm{col}}_{(2)}\rangle,

(57)

\displaystyle v_{x}

\displaystyle=\partial_{x_{0}}E_{\mathrm{s}}=\boldsymbol{P}_{1}|\Phi_{(2)}^{\mathrm{zero}}\rangle,

(58)

where
\boldsymbol{P}_{1}=(\mathds{1}_{2}+\sigma_{3})/2
is the projection operator to the first row.
This identification shows that the modes that are related with the soliton parameter perturbations or, equivalently, the collective modes are the discrete modes, which are necessary to complete the basis expansion of the field. We observe that the perturbations corresponding to the zero modes,
\theta_{0}
and
x_{0}
, are conserving the energy, whereas perturbations of the collective modes,
n_{0}
and
p_{0}
, are conjugate to them and incrementally change the soliton energy.
In Sec.
III.3
, we now present the quantization scheme and define the vacuum configuration.
Unlike the continuous modes, the discrete modes are not of the plane wave form, even far from the soliton.
Therefore, discrete modes cannot be interpreted as a quasi-particles.

III.3
Quantisation of the field operator

We proceed now to quantise the field expansion, Eq. (
51
). The expansion of the quantum field is obtained by replacing the classical mode amplitudes with mode operators that satisfy the commutation relations

[\hat{a}_{k},\hat{a}_{k^{\prime}}^{\dagger}]=\delta(k-k^{\prime})

(59)

and with

n:=-c_{1}\quad\theta:=-i\,b_{1}\quad p:=-i\,c_{2}\quad x:=b_{2},

(60)

[\hat{\theta},\hat{n}]=[\hat{x},\hat{p}]=i.

(61)

All other pairs of operators commute.
Now, the quantised Bogoliubov-field spinor
|\hat{\Phi}\rangle:=(\hat{\psi},\hat{\psi}^{\dagger})^{\intercal}
can be written in the form,

\displaystyle|\hat{\Phi}\rangle

\displaystyle=\int\mathrm{d}k\big(\hat{a}_{k}\mathrm{e}^{-i\omega_{k}t}|\Phi_{k}\rangle+\hat{a}_{k}^{\dagger}\mathrm{e}^{i\omega t}|\bar{\Phi}_{k}\rangle\big)+i\hat{\theta}|\Phi_{(1)}^{\mathrm{zero}}\rangle+\hat{x}|{\Phi}_{(2)}^{\mathrm{zero}}\rangle-\hat{n}|\Phi_{(1)}^{\mathrm{col}}\rangle+i\hat{p}|{\Phi}_{(2)}^{\mathrm{col}}\rangle\big).

(62)

Before moving on, it is useful to rewrite the expansion in terms of creation and annihilation operators also for the discrete modes.
Recall that the expectation value of the quantum field is zero, implicit in Eq. (
16
),

\langle 0|\hat{\psi}|0\rangle=0,

(63)

where the vacuum state
|0\rangle
is defined at
t=0
and we work in the Heisenberg picture. Hence, expectation values are more easily calculated if also the discrete modes in the expansion of
\hat{\psi}
are represented by annihilation and creation operators.
We define these operators using the soliton parameter operators:

\hat{\alpha}_{(1)}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\hat{\theta}+i\hat{n}),\qquad\hat{\alpha}_{(2)}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\hat{x}+i\hat{p}).

(64)

which satisfy the discrete bosonic commutation relation
[\hat{\alpha}_{(i)},\hat{\alpha}^{\dagger}_{(j)}]=\delta_{ij}
and commute with all the creation/annihilation operators of the continuous modes.
In contrast to
\hat{a}_{k}
and
\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{k}
in Eq. (
62
), these operators do not represent the creation of quasi-particles, i.e. asymptotic plane wave solutions. Although the excitations do not come as particle-antiparticle pairs, we can consistently define the vacuum state with:

\hat{\alpha}_{(i)}|0\rangle=0,

(65)

which is required by Eq. (
63
).
In this representation, the field expansion writes:

\displaystyle|\hat{\Phi}\rangle

\displaystyle=\int\mathrm{d}k\big(\hat{a}_{k}\mathrm{e}^{-i\omega_{k}t}|\Phi_{k}\rangle+\hat{a}_{k}^{\dagger}\mathrm{e}^{i\omega_{k}t}|\bar{\Phi}_{k}\rangle\big)+\sum_{i\in\{1,2\}}\big(\hat{\alpha}_{(i)}|\Phi_{(i)}\rangle+\hat{\alpha}_{(i)}^{\dagger}|\bar{\Phi}_{(i)}\rangle\big),

(66)

where

\displaystyle|\Phi_{(1)}\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{i}{\sqrt{2}}\big(|\Phi_{(1)}^{\mathrm{zero}}\rangle+|\Phi_{(1)}^{\mathrm{col}}\rangle\big),

(67)

\displaystyle|\bar{\Phi}_{(1)}\rangle

\displaystyle=\sigma_{1}|{\Phi}_{(1)}\rangle^{*}=-\frac{i}{\sqrt{2}}\big(|\Phi_{(1)}^{\mathrm{zero}}\rangle-|\Phi^{\mathrm{col}}_{(1)}\rangle\big),

(68)

\displaystyle|\Phi_{(2)}\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\big(|\Phi_{(2)}^{\mathrm{zero}}\rangle+|\Phi^{\mathrm{col}}_{(2)}\rangle\big),

(69)

\displaystyle|\bar{\Phi}_{(2)}\rangle

\displaystyle=\sigma_{1}|{\Phi}_{(2)}\rangle^{*}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\big(|\Phi_{(2)}^{\mathrm{zero}}\rangle-|\Phi^{\mathrm{col}}_{(2)}\rangle\big).

(70)

The discrete modes introduced here have a nonzero norm.
For zero modes, we cannot use the property of particle-hole symmetry, Eq. (
28
), to find the partner mode
[
28
]
. But combinations of these states satisfy Eq. (
28
) and restore the partnered relation analogous to the continuous mode.
At
t=0
, these modes form an orthonormal set similar to the continuous modes (Eq. (
43
)),

\langle\Phi_{(i)}|\Phi_{(j)}\rangle_{\sigma_{3}}=-\langle\bar{\Phi}_{(i)}|\bar{\Phi}_{(j)}\rangle_{\sigma_{3}}=\delta_{i,j},\qquad\langle\Phi_{(i)}|\bar{\Phi}_{(j)}\rangle_{\sigma_{3}}=0.

(71)

Recall that
|\hat{\Phi}\rangle=(\hat{\psi},\hat{\psi}^{\dagger})^{\intercal}
and
(u_{k},v_{k})^{\intercal}=|\Phi_{k}(x)\rangle\mathrm{e}^{-i\omega t}
.
The quantised Bogoliubov expansion (
66
) in terms of mode functions is

\displaystyle\hat{\psi}(t,x)

\displaystyle=\int\mathrm{d}k\big(\hat{a}_{k}u_{k}(t,x)+\hat{a}_{k}^{\dagger}v_{k}^{*}(t,x)\big)+\sum_{i\in\{1,2\}}\big(\hat{\alpha}_{(i)}u_{(i)}(t,x)+\hat{\alpha}_{(i)}^{\dagger}v_{(i)}^{*}(t,x)\big),

(72)

with a similar expression for
\hat{\psi}^{\dagger}
. We now have a suitable set of modes to calculate expectation values. The amplitudes of the modes, the mode functions
(u_{k},v_{k})^{\intercal}
, can now be calculated.[
|\Phi^{(\omega)}(t,x)\rangle:=(u_{k},v_{k})^{\intercal}:=|\Phi_{k}(x)\rangle\mathrm{e}^{-i\omega t}
] The discrete modes are defined in analogy to the continuous modes,
|\Phi_{(i)}\rangle:=(u_{(i)},v_{(i)})^{\intercal}
.
We derive the explicit form of the mode function and calculate the density
\langle\hat{\psi}^{\dagger}(t,x)\hat{\psi}(t,x)\rangle
and anomalous density
\langle\hat{\psi}^{2}(t,x)\rangle
, as these will appear in the backreaction field
E_{c}
.
In what follows, we separately treat the contributions arising from the discrete and continuous modes, respectively.
Total densities are simply a sum of these contributions, because discrete and continuous modes commute and there is no mixture of discrete and continuous mode contribution in the vacuum expectation value of the second-order correlations.
We start with the continuous mode contribution.
The mode functions can be obtained from Eq. (
39
):

\displaystyle u_{k}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}(k^{2}+1)}(\tanh{x}+ik)^{2}\mathrm{e}^{-ikx}\mathrm{e}^{-i\omega_{k}t},

(73)

\displaystyle v_{k}^{*}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}(k^{2}+1)}\sech^{2}\!\!x\,\mathrm{e}^{ikx}\mathrm{e}^{i\omega_{k}t}.

(74)

Hence, using the subscript
\mathrm{con}
for the continuous mode contribution, we calculate:

\displaystyle\langle\hat{\psi}^{\dagger}\hat{\psi}\rangle_{\mathrm{con}}

\displaystyle=\int\mathrm{d}k\,|v_{k}|^{2}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\sech^{4}\!\!x\int\mathrm{d}k\frac{1}{(k^{2}+1)^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}\sech^{4}\!\!x,

(75)

\displaystyle\langle\hat{\psi}^{2}\rangle_{\mathrm{con}}

\displaystyle=\int\mathrm{d}ku_{k}v_{k}^{*}=-\frac{1}{2\pi}\sech^{2}\!\!x\int\mathrm{d}k\frac{(\tanh x+ik)^{2}}{(k^{2}+1)^{2}}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{4}\sech^{4}\!\!x.

(76)

Note that these are real functions and thus
\langle\hat{\psi}^{\dagger 2}\rangle_{\mathrm{con}}=\langle\hat{\psi}^{2}\rangle_{\mathrm{con}}
and
\langle\hat{\psi}\hat{\psi}^{\dagger}\rangle_{\mathrm{con}}=\langle\hat{\psi}^{\dagger}\hat{\psi}\rangle_{\mathrm{con}}
.
In a similar manner, the discrete modes can be obtained from (
67
) and (
69
) and the explicit solutions given in Sec.
III.2
:

\displaystyle u_{(1)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}(ix\tanh{x}+t)\sech{x},

(77)

\displaystyle v_{(1)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\big(i(x\tanh{x}-2)-t\big)\sech{x},

(78)

\displaystyle u_{(2)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\big((1-it)\tanh{x}+x\big)\sech{x},

(79)

\displaystyle v_{(2)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}(\big(1-it)\tanh{x}-x\big)\sech{x}.

(80)

Finally, using the subscript
\mathrm{dis}
for the discrete mode contribution, we obtain:

\displaystyle\langle\hat{\psi}^{\dagger}\hat{\psi}\rangle_{\mathrm{dis}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}|v_{(i)}|^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}\big[\big((1+x^{2})\tanh^{2}\!\!{x}-6x\tanh{x}+x^{2}+4\big)+t^{2}(2-\sech^{2}\!\!{x})\big]\sech^{2}\!\!x,

(81)

\displaystyle\langle\hat{\psi}^{2}\rangle_{\mathrm{dis}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}u_{(i)}v_{(i)}^{*}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}\big[\big((1+x^{2})\tanh^{2}\!\!{x}-2x\tanh{x}-x^{2}\big)+2it-t^{2}\sech^{2}\!\!x\big]\sech^{2}\!\!x.

(82)

Here,
\langle\hat{\psi}\hat{\psi}^{\dagger}\rangle_{\mathrm{dis}}=\langle\hat{\psi}^{\dagger}\hat{\psi}\rangle_{\mathrm{dis}}
and
\langle\hat{\psi}^{\dagger 2}\rangle_{\mathrm{dis}}=\langle\hat{\psi}^{2}\rangle_{\mathrm{dis}}^{*}
.
Overall the second-order correlations of the continuous modes lack time dependence, whereas the discrete mode correlations increase quadratically in time. In the next section we show how this quadratic behaviour is also found in the correction field as a result of number conservation.

IV
The classical field correction from the backreaction

In the previous section, we solved the Bogoliubov-de Gennes equation and calculated the relevant expectation values.
In this section, we solve the equation of motion for the correction field
E_{\mathrm{c}}
, which describes a backreaction.
Let us write
E_{c}(t,x)=e^{it/2}\tilde{E}_{c}(t,x)
and define the spinor
|E_{c}\rangle=(\tilde{E}_{c},\tilde{E}_{c}^{*})^{\intercal}
, which, because of Eq. (
18
), satisfies

(i\partial_{t}-\boldsymbol{H}_{\mathrm{BdG}})|E_{c}\rangle=|{\rm source}\rangle,

(83)

where

|{\rm source}\rangle=\begin{pmatrix}-\big(2\langle\hat{\psi}^{\dagger}\hat{\psi}\rangle+\langle\hat{\psi}^{2}\rangle\big)\tilde{E}_{\mathrm{s}}\\
\big(2\langle\hat{\psi}^{\dagger}\hat{\psi}\rangle+\langle\hat{\psi}^{\dagger 2}\rangle\big)\tilde{E}_{\mathrm{s}}\end{pmatrix}.

(84)

Note that, apart from the inhomogeneous source term, the equation is the same as the BdG equation (compare also the left-hand sides of Eqns. (
18
) and (
19
)).
Consequently, the classical field correction can be written in the form

\displaystyle E_{\mathrm{c}}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{e}^{it/2}\Bigg[\int\mathrm{d}k\big(b_{k}u_{k}+b_{k}^{*}v_{k}^{*}\big)+\sum_{i}\big(c_{(i)}u_{(i)}+c_{(i)}^{*}v_{(i)}^{*}\big)\Bigg]+E_{\mathrm{c}}^{(p)}.

(85)

where superscript
{(p)}
is used to denote a particular solution.

b_{k}
and
c_{(i)}
are complex numbers determined by the initial condition.
The solution of Eq. (
83
) starts with a particular solution.
The source term (
84
) only contains the density and anomalous density whose continuous and discrete mode contributions we already calculated in section
III.3
.
Since the operator
(i\partial_{t}-\boldsymbol{H}_{\mathrm{BdG}})
is a linear operator, we can split the particular solution into the contributions from the continuous and the discrete mode densities, respectively:

E_{\mathrm{c}}^{(p)}=E_{\mathrm{c},\mathrm{con}}^{(p)}+E_{\mathrm{c},\mathrm{dis}}^{(p)}.

(86)

We start with the continuous contribution. A detailed calculation of what is summarized below is given in App.
A
.
Using Eqns. (
75
) and (
76
), the part of the source (
84
) originating from the continuous mode densities is

|{\rm source}\rangle_{\mathrm{con}}=-\frac{1}{4}\sech^{5}x\begin{pmatrix}1\\
-1\end{pmatrix}.

(87)

A particular solution for the continuous contribution is (
132
):

E_{\mathrm{c},\mathrm{con}}^{(p)}(t,x)=(f_{0,\mathrm{con}}(x)+itg_{1,\mathrm{con}}(x))\mathrm{e}^{it/2},

(88)

where

\displaystyle g_{1,\mathrm{con}}(x)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{9}\sech{x},

(89)

\displaystyle f_{0,\mathrm{con}}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{36}(3\sech^{2}\!\!x-10+4x\tanh{x})\sech{x}.

(90)

For the discrete mode source term we also insert Eq. (
81
) and Eq. (
82
) into Eq. (
84
),

|{\rm source}\rangle_{\mathrm{dis}}=S_{R}(t,x)\begin{pmatrix}1\\
-1\end{pmatrix}+iS_{I}(t,x)\begin{pmatrix}1\\
1\end{pmatrix}.

(91)

where

\displaystyle S_{R}(x)

\displaystyle:=-\frac{1}{4}\big(8+x^{2}-14x\tanh{x}+3(x^{2}+1)\tanh^{2}\!\!x\big)\sech^{3}\!\!x-\frac{1}{4}t^{2}(1+3\tanh^{2}\!\!x)\sech^{3}\!\!{x},

(92)

\displaystyle S_{I}(x)

\displaystyle:=-\frac{1}{2}t\sech^{3}x.

(93)

The particular solution that vanishes in the asymptotic region
|x|\to\infty
is (
165
)

E_{\mathrm{c},\mathrm{dis}}^{(p)}=\big(f_{2,\mathrm{dis}}(x)t^{2}+f_{0,\mathrm{dis}}(x)\big)\mathrm{e}^{it/2}.

(94)

where

\displaystyle f_{2,\mathrm{dis}}(x)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{4}\sech^{3}x,

(95)

\displaystyle f_{0,\mathrm{dis}}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{4}{15}\Big(x\sinh{x}+\cosh{x}\big(\ln{(\sech{x})}-\ln 2\big)\Big)-\frac{1}{4}(1+x^{2})\sech^{3}\!\!{x}

\displaystyle\quad-\frac{1}{60}\big(41+48\ln 2+48\ln(\sech{x})\big)\sech{x}

\displaystyle\quad-\frac{2}{15}\Big(6\,\text{Li}_{2}\big(-e^{-2x}\big)+x\big(6x+6\ln(\sech{x})+5-6\ln{2}\big)\Big)\tanh{x}\sech{x}

(96)

where
\mathrm{Li}_{s}(z)
is a polylogarithm (Jonquière’s function).
Because the inhomogeneity of Eq. (
83
) is well behaved and vanishes asymptotically, physical solutions of this equation are described in the form of Eq. (
85
) .

We further point out a
t^{2}
contribution to
E_{\mathrm{dis}}^{(p)}
, which is not present in the Bogoliubov field solutions derived at the end of the previous section.
Therefore, this quadratic dependence is a characteristic of the backreaction effect via the discrete mode contribution
E_{\mathrm{dis}}^{(p)}
. It is independent of the initial condition and, hence, in the following we will be able to identify quadratic terms caused by the backreaction.
In other words, the quadratic decay of the soliton photon number investigated in
[
32
]
is in fact the backreaction effect from the discrete mode.

We now have derived an analytic form of the general solution of the backreaction field
E_{c}
.

V
Exploration of Results

Here, we will explore the resultant correction field solutions further.
Since we know the mode expansion of the Bogoliubov field and the correction field, it is possible to calculate physical quantities.
As an example, we calculate the photon number density and photon number of the Bogoliubov and correction fields, respectively.
We recover the units in order to assess the size of these fields.
The units can be recovered by putting dimensional constants in each dimensionless parameters.
For the soliton,

E_{\mathrm{s}}=\sqrt{P_{0}}\sech(\tau/T_{0})\mathrm{e}^{i\zeta/2L_{D}}.

(97)

where
P_{0}
is the soliton peak power,
T_{0}
is the temporal width and
L_{D}
is the dispersion length

L_{D}=\frac{T_{0}^{2}}{|k_{2}|}.

(98)

where
k_{2}
is the group velocity dispersion parameter at the soliton carrier frequency
[
55
]
.
In our number-conserving approach, the expansion parameter was identified as
\epsilon=1/\sqrt{n}_{0}
.
Hence, physical dimensions are recovered by identifying
t\equiv\zeta/L_{D}
and
x\equiv\tau/T_{0}
.
Also, this relates to the total soliton photon number by

2P_{0}T_{0}=n_{0}\hbar\bar{\omega}_{\mathrm{s}}

(99)

where
\bar{\omega}_{\mathrm{s}}
is the average frequency of the soliton.

V.1
Photon number density

Since we have expanded the field in orders related to the photon number, the photon number density is a useful physical quantity to explore.
In this section, we explore the expectation value of the photon number density of each of the fields in (
17
), focusing on long propagation distances
(\zeta\gg L_{d})
.
We also consider the total photon number of each field separately to monitor the number conservation, treating continuous and discrete mode contributions separately.
We start with the photon number density expectation value, which can be written with Eq. (
17
) in the form:

\rho:=\langle\hat{\rho}\rangle=|E_{\mathrm{s}}|^{2}+\epsilon^{2}\langle\hat{\psi}^{\dagger}\hat{\psi}\rangle+2\epsilon^{2}\Re[E_{\mathrm{s}}^{*}E_{\mathrm{c}}]+\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{3}):=\rho_{\mathrm{s}}+\rho_{\Delta}+\rho_{\mathrm{c}}+\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{3}),

(100)

where
\rho_{\mathrm{s}}
is the photon number density from the soliton background,
\rho_{\Delta}
is the Bogoliubov field contribution to the photon number density, and
\rho_{\mathrm{c}}
is the photon number density from the backreaction effect. Note that this is a density in time, because the QNLS describes the evolution in space.
Also note that both
\rho_{\Delta}
and
\rho_{\mathrm{c}}
are of the same order (
\epsilon^{2}
).
For the Bogoliubov field, the photon number density from continuous and discrete modes, respectively, were obtained earlier as Eq. (
75
) and Eq. (
81

From these, the photon number density
\rho_{\Delta}^{\mathrm{con}}
for continuous and
\rho_{\Delta,\mathrm{dis}}
for discrete modes can be obtained in physical dimensions of inverse time:

\displaystyle\rho_{\Delta,\mathrm{con}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2T_{0}}\langle\hat{\psi}^{\dagger}\hat{\psi}\rangle_{\mathrm{con}}=\frac{1}{8T_{0}}\sech^{4}(\tau/T_{0}),

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2T_{0}}\langle\hat{\psi}^{\dagger}\hat{\psi}\rangle_{\mathrm{dis}}=\frac{1}{8T_{0}}\Big[5+2(\tau/T_{0})^{2}-6(\tau/T_{0})\tanh{(\tau/T_{0})}-\big(1+(\tau/T_{0})^{2}\big)\sech^{2}\!\!{(\tau/T_{0})}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\quad+(\zeta/L_{D})^{2}\big(2-\sech^{2}\!\!{(\tau/T_{0})}\big)\Big]\sech^{2}(\tau/T_{0}).

(102)

For long propagation distances, the quadratic
\zeta
-dependent term of the discrete mode dominates,

(103)

As the soliton propagates, the Bogoliubov field photon number density increases .
In comparison we consider the photon number density of the backreaction field, or equivalently, the classical field correction.
We will also treat the continuous- and discrete mode contributions,
\rho_{\mathrm{c},\mathrm{con}}
and
\rho_{\mathrm{c},\mathrm{dis}}

\displaystyle\rho_{\mathrm{c},\mathrm{con}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2T_{0}}2\Re[E_{\mathrm{s}}^{*}E_{\mathrm{c},\mathrm{con}}]=\frac{1}{36T_{0}}\big(3\sech^{2}(\tau/T_{0})-10+4(\tau/T_{0})\tanh{(\tau/T_{0})}\big)\sech^{2}{(\tau/T_{0})}.

(104)

\displaystyle\rho_{\mathrm{c},\mathrm{dis}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2T_{0}}2\Re[E_{\mathrm{s}}^{*}E_{\mathrm{c},\mathrm{dis}}]=\frac{4}{15T_{0}}\Big((\tau/T_{0})\sinh{(\tau/T_{0})}+\cosh{(\tau/T_{0})}\big(\ln{(\sech{(\tau/T_{0})})}-\ln 2\big)\Big)\sech\!{(\tau/T_{0})}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\quad-\frac{1}{4T_{0}}\big(1+(\tau/T_{0})^{2}\big)\sech^{4}\!{(\tau/T_{0})}-\frac{1}{60T_{0}}\big(41+48\ln 2+48\ln(\sech{(\tau/T_{0})})\big)\sech^{2}\!{(\tau/T_{0})}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\quad-\frac{2}{15T_{0}}\Big((\tau/T_{0})\big(6(\tau/T_{0})+6\ln(\sech{(\tau/T_{0})})+5-6\ln{2}\big)\Big)\tanh{(\tau/T_{0})}\sech^{2}{(\tau/T_{0})}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\quad-\frac{4}{5T_{0}}\text{Li}_{2}\big(-e^{-2(\tau/T_{0})}\big)\tanh{(\tau/T_{0})}\sech^{2}{(\tau/T_{0})}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\quad-\frac{1}{4T_{0}}(\zeta/L_{D})^{2}\sech^{4}(\tau/T_{0})

(105)

Eventually, the quadratic increasing term of
\zeta
in discrete mode contribution dominates the density:

\rho_{\mathrm{c}}:=\rho_{\mathrm{c},\mathrm{con}}+\rho_{\mathrm{c},\mathrm{dis}}\xrightarrow{\zeta\gg L_{D}}-\frac{1}{4T_{0}}(\zeta/L_{D})^{2}\sech^{4}(\tau/T_{0}).

(106)

The minus sign means that the photon number density of the backreaction reduces the total photon number density, i.e. it compensates the increase in the Bogoliubov field.
Since both photon number densities we calculated here are in the same order of the expansion parameter
(\epsilon^{2})

Since both of these belong to the soliton, we call the sum of them,

VI
Conclusion

We presented a complete nonrelativistic quantum field theoretic description of the fibre-optical soliton, the linear quantum fluctuation, and a backreaction effect in quadratic order.
Starting from the Lagrangian we developed a field expansion in order of the photon number that preserves the photon number. We quantised the field and analytically derived a complete set of modes, which consists of continuous and discrete modes. The discrete modes were shown to exhibit instabilities that characterise the field for propagation distances larger than tens of dispersion lengths. The discrete modes do not describe quasi-particle creation and annihilation, but instead are related to collective perturbations of the soliton parameters. Our result links in with the decay of solitons shown in
[
32
]
, which is hence due to unstable discrete modes.
We show that because of the dominant behavior of the discrete modes, the photon density distortion develops a characteristic ‘crater’ shape. Moreover, we suggest a measurement of the frequency distribution of the electric correction field as a signature of a backreaction effect.

The analogue backreaction research is one of the ways to approach quantum gravity with fluid
[
29
]
.
The backreaction in leading order is a classical correction to the standard soliton solution.
This was calculated analytically for arbitrary initial conditions. As a result, the soliton temporally broadens and its peak value decreases quadratically in time. The deviations from the soliton take on a characteristic ‘crater’ shape. It is straightforward to extend this analysis to an external dynamical source term acting on the soliton.
