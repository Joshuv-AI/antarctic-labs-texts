Image Restoration: Structured Low Rank Matrix Framework for Piecewise Smooth Functions and Beyond
Jian-Feng Cai, Jae Kyu Choi, Jingyang Li, Ke Wei

Abstract
Recently, mapping a signal/image into a low rank Hankel/Toeplitz matrix has become an emerging alternative to the traditional sparse regularization, due to its ability to alleviate the basis mismatch between the true support in the continuous domain and the discrete grid. In this paper, we introduce a novel structured low rank matrix framework to restore piecewise smooth functions. Inspired by the total generalized variation to use sparse higher order derivatives, we derive that the Fourier samples of higher order derivatives satisfy an annihilation relation, resulting in a low rank multi-fold Hankel matrix. We further observe that the SVD of a low rank Hankel matrix corresponds to a tight wavelet frame system which can represent the image with sparse coefficients. Based on this observation, we also propose a wavelet frame analysis approach based continuous domain regularization model for the piecewise smooth image restoration. Finally, numerical results on image restoration tasks are presented as a proof-of-concept study to demonstrate that the proposed approach is compared favorably against several popular discrete regularization approaches and structured low rank matrix approaches.

Introduction

Image restoration, including image denoising, deblurring, inpainting, computed tomography, etc., is one of the central problems in imaging science. It aims at recovering an image of high-quality from a given measurement which is degraded during the process of imaging, acquisition, and communication. The image restoration problem is typically modeled as the following linear inverse problem:

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{f}}={\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{A}}}}\boldsymbol{u}+{\boldsymbol{\zeta}},

(1.1)

where
{\boldsymbol{f}}
is the degraded measurement or the observed image,
{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}
is a certain additive noise, and
{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{A}}}}
is some linear operator which takes different forms for different image restoration problems.

Since the operator
{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{A}}}}
is in general ill-conditioned or non-invertible, it is in general to use a regularization on the images to be restored. Most widely used examples include the variational approaches including total variation (TV)
[
61
]
and its nonlocal variants
[
68
]
, the inf-convolution
[
20
]
, the total generalized variation (TGV)
[
6
,
7
]
, and the combined first and second order total variation
[
1
,
55
]
. Apart from the variational approaches, the applied harmonic analysis approaches including curvelets
[
17
]
, Gabor frames
[
42
]
, shearlets
[
45
]
, complex tight framelets
[
38
]
, and wavelet frames
[
8
,
9
,
11
,
21
]
are also widely used in the literature. The common concept of these methods is to use a sparse regularization on discrete images under a discrete linear transformation to regularize smooth image components while preserving image singularities such as edges, ridges, and corners. However, in many applications, the true singularities lie in the continuous domain, and the discretization error will lead to the
basis mismatch

[
23
,
52
,
67
]
between the true singularities and the discrete image grid. Such a basis mismatch would destroy the sparse structure of the image, and thus can degrade the restoration quality
[
67
]
.

Recently, continuous domain regularization is emerging as a powerful alternative to the discrete domain sparse regularization
[
2
,
19
,
22
,
49
]
. By such an “off-the-grid” approach, we can exploit the sparsity prior in continuous domain, which enables us to alleviate the basis mismatch due to the discretization
[
49
]
. To the best of our knowledge, this off-the-grid regularization stems from the Prony’s method
[
58
]
which corresponds a superposition of a few sinusoids to a structured low rank matrix for the Dirac stream retrieval. Hence, we can adopt the so-called
structured low rank matrix (SLRM) approach

[
22
,
53
,
66
]
for the restoration of spectrally sparse signal whose Fourier transform is a Dirac stream
[
14
,
15
,
16
]
. Apart from the spectrally sparse signal restoration, the SLRM can also be used to restore Fourier samples of a one dimensional piecewise constant signal
[
3
,
63
]
as in this case the Fourier samples of a derivative becomes the superposition of a few sinusoids. However, even though the SLRM can be easily applied to the case of isolated singularities
[
18
,
65
]
, the extension to the multi dimensional (piecewise smooth) image restoration is not straightforward
[
52
]
. Since the image singularities such as the edges and ridges in general form a continuous curve in a two dimensional domain, it is in general challenging to construct a structured low rank matrix from the Fourier samples.

In this paper, we introduce a new structured low rank matrix framework for the piecewise smooth image restoration. Our framework is inspired by the
k
th order total generalized variation (
\mathrm{TGV}^{k}
)
[
6
,
7
]
, which is known to be effective in restoring the piecewise polynomial image with sharp edges
[
36
]
. Specifically, following the SLRM framework for the piecewise constant image
[
52
]
, we assume that the image singularities (including both jumps and hidden jumps) are located in the zero level set of a band-limited periodic function (called the
annihilating polynomial
). Then we can derive that the gradient can be decomposed into another vector field and the residual, and the Fourier samples of the residual and the symmetric gradient of this vector field can be annihilated by the convolution with the Fourier coefficients of the annihilating polynomial (called the
annihilating filter
). From these annihilation relations, we deduce that the multi-fold Hankel matrices generated from the Fourier samples of a piecewise smooth image are low rank, which in turn enables a balance between the derivatives of order
1,\ldots,k
via a combined rank minimization of multi-fold Hankel matrices.

As a by-product of the proposed structured low rank matrix framework, we further introduce a wavelet frame based sparse regularization model for the piecewise image restoration via the continuous domain regularization. Briefly speaking, if we can associate a signal/image with a low rank Hankel matrix, its right singular vectors form tight frame filter banks under which the canonical coefficients have a group sparsity according to the index of filters
[
10
]
. Then motivated by
[
35
]
, we assume that the right singular vectors are estimated from a pre-restoration process, and we propose a
sparse regularization
via the wavelet frame analysis approach (e.g.
[
13
]
) as an image restoration model via the continuous domain regularization. Notice that there are several wavelet frame based approaches for the piecewise smooth image restoration
[
12
,
24
,
41
]
in the literature. However, while these existing approaches focus on the sparse approximation of a discrete image, our approach comes from the relaxation of the structured low rank matrices generated by the Fourier samples for the continuous domain regularization.

1.1
SLRM for piecewise smooth image restoration

Our SLRM framework for the piecewise smooth function is mostly related to the recent extension of the SLRM framework to the two dimensional functions in
[
49
,
50
,
51
,
52
,
54
]
. Briefly speaking, if the singularity curves of a piecewise constant/holomorphic function, i.e. the supports of the first order (real/complex) derivatives of a target image, lie in the zero level set of an annihilating polynomial, the Fourier transform of the derivatives can be annihilated by the convolution with the annihilating filter. This annihilation relation in turn corresponds the Fourier samples of derivatives to the structured low rank matrices. Based on this framework, the SLRM framework for the piecewise constant image restoration is proposed and studied in
[
50
,
51
,
52
,
53
]
, together with a restoration guarantee
[
49
]
.

This annihilation relation of the gradient can be easily extended to the higher order derivatives whenever the jump discontinuities of the corresponding derivatives are located in the zero level set of a trigonometric polynomial
[
50
]
. For instance, we can derive an annihilation relation for the piecewise linear function by considering the Fourier transform of the second order derivatives. Based on this idea, the authors in
[
39
]
proposed a so-called generalized structured low rank (GSLR) approach for the piecewise smooth image restoration. Inspired by the
2
-fold inf-convolution
[
20
]
, the GSLR approach restores a superposition of a piecewise constant layer and a piecewise linear layer whose first and second order derivatives correspond to low rank Hankel matrices in the frequency domain respectively. By balancing the first order and second order derivatives, the GSLR approach has demonstrated significant improvements in the piecewise smooth image restoration tasks over the existing approaches.

Note that it is not difficult to extend the previous GSLR framework to generic piecewise smooth functions. More precisely, by considering the annihilation relation of higher order derivatives, we can extend the GSLR to the so-called
k
-fold inf-convolution (
k\geq 3
) for functions with higher regularity. However, in a piecewise smooth function, there could exist singularities on which the derivatives have jump discontinuities. In this case, the annihilating polynomial describing the image singularities cannot annihilate the
k
th order derivatives
[
50
, Proposition 4]
. Since we then need to consider the power of the annihilating polynomial, or equivalently, increase the size of the filter to guarantee the annihilation relation for higher order derivatives, the GSLR framework can degrade the low rank (multi-fold) Hankel matrix structure of the higher order derivatives.

Unlike the GSLR framework in
[
39
]
based on the inf-convolution, the proposed SLRM framework is based on the total generalized variation framework
[
7
]
. Briefly speaking, we decompose the gradient into the residual whose Fourier samples correspond to the structured low rank matrix and a vector field whose Fourier samples of the symmetric gradient correspond to the structured low rank matrix. In other words, since our proposed SLRM framework uses the Fourier samples of the successive first order derivatives, it does not require the powers of the annihilating polynomial describing the image singularities, which enables us to obtain better low rank Hankel matrix structures corresponding to the piecewise smooth functions. In addition, since the TGV takes the inf-convolution as a special case, it can also be verified that the proposed SLRM framework is more general than the GSLR framework for the piecewise smooth functions.

2
Structured low rank matrix framework for piecewise smooth functions

In this section, we introduce our structured low rank matrix framework for piecewise smooth functions. For simplicity, we consider the piecewise linear function throughout this paper. Note, however, it is not difficult to extend the proposed framework into the general piecewise smooth functions.

2.1
SLRM framework for one dimensional signals

We first establish the structured low rank matrix framework of the following one dimensional piecewise linear model

\displaystyle u(x)=\sum_{j=1}^{K-1}\left(\alpha_{j}x+\beta_{j}\right)1_{[x_{j},x_{j+1})}(x),

(2.1)

from its Fourier sample

\displaystyle\widehat{u}(k)={\mathscr{F}}(u)(k)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}u(x)e^{-2\pi ikx}\mathrm{d}x,~~~~~k\in\left\{-\left\lfloor N/2\right\rfloor,\ldots,\left\lfloor(N-1)/2\right\rfloor\right\},~~~N\in{\mathbb{N}}.

In
eq.

2.1
,
\alpha_{j},\beta_{j}\in{\mathbb{C}}
,
1_{[x_{j},x_{j+1})}
denotes the characteristic function on the interval
[x_{j},x_{j+1})
:
1_{[x_{j},x_{j+1})}(x)=1
if
x\in[x_{j},x_{j+1})
, and
0
otherwise, and
-1/2<x_{1}<x_{2}<\cdots<x_{K}<1/2
are the location of singularities, i.e. by the singularities we mean they include both the jumps and the hidden jumps (jumps of derivatives).

In the sense of distribution, the derivative
u^{\prime}
satisfies

\displaystyle u^{\prime}(x)=\sum_{j=1}^{K-1}\alpha_{j}1_{[x_{j},x_{j+1})}(x)+\sum_{j=1}^{K}\left[{\mathscr{T}}_{j}(u)(x_{j})\delta(x-x_{j})\right]

(2.2)

where
{\mathscr{T}}_{j}(u)(x_{j})=\left(\alpha_{j}-\alpha_{j-1}\right)x_{j}+\left(\beta_{j}-\beta_{j-1}\right)
with
\alpha_{0}=\alpha_{K}=\beta_{0}=\beta_{K}=0
. Letting

\displaystyle p(x)=\sum_{j=1}^{K-1}\alpha_{j}1_{[x_{j},x_{j+1})}(x),

(2.3)

we have

\displaystyle u^{\prime}(x)-p(x)=\sum_{j=1}^{K}{\mathscr{T}}_{j}(u)(x_{j})\delta(x-x_{j}).

Hence, the Fourier transform of
u^{\prime}-p
is a linear combination of complex sinusoids:

\displaystyle{\mathscr{F}}(u^{\prime}-p)(\xi)=\sum_{j=1}^{K}{\mathscr{T}}_{j}(u)(x_{j})e^{-2\pi ix_{j}\xi},~~~~~\xi\in{\mathbb{R}}.

(2.4)

In addition, since the derivative of

is also a Dirac stream:

\displaystyle p^{\prime}(x)=\sum_{j=1}^{K}\left(\alpha_{j}-\alpha_{j-1}\right)\delta(x-x_{j}),

(2.5)

its Fourier transform is expressed as

\displaystyle{\mathscr{F}}(p^{\prime})(\xi)=\sum_{j=1}^{K}\left(\alpha_{j}-\alpha_{j-1}\right)e^{-2\pi ix_{j}\xi},~~~~~\xi\in{\mathbb{R}}.

(2.6)

i.e. a linear combination of complex sinusoids.

We introduce the following trigonometric polynomial

\displaystyle\varphi(x)=\prod_{j=1}^{K}\left(e^{-2\pi ix}-e^{-2\pi ix_{j}}\right):=\sum_{k=0}^{K}{\boldsymbol{a}}(k)e^{-2\pi ikx}.

(2.7)

Since

\varphi\left(u^{\prime}-p\right)=0
and
\varphi p^{\prime}=0
in the sense of distribution. In the frequency domain, since we have

\displaystyle\widehat{\varphi}(\xi)={\mathscr{F}}(\varphi)(\xi)=\sum_{k=0}^{K}{\boldsymbol{a}}(k)\delta(\xi+k),

it follows that

\displaystyle\left({\mathscr{F}}(u^{\prime}-p)\ast\widehat{\varphi}\right)(\xi)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{K}{\mathscr{F}}(u^{\prime}-p)(\xi+k){\boldsymbol{a}}(k)=0,

(2.8)

\displaystyle\left({\mathscr{F}}(p^{\prime})\ast\widehat{\varphi}\right)(\xi)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{K}{\mathscr{F}}(p^{\prime})(\xi+k){\boldsymbol{a}}(k)=0,

(2.9)

for
\xi\in{\mathbb{R}}
. Therefore, the Fourier transforms of both

and
p^{\prime}
are annihilated by the convolution under the Fourier coefficients of
\varphi

In many practical cases, we consider the contiguous Fourier samples (i.e. the samples on a regular grid
\left[N\right]:=\left\{-\lfloor N/2\rfloor,\ldots,\lfloor(N-1)/2\rfloor\right\}
), so
eqs.

2.8
and
2.9
become the following systems of linear equations

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{K}{\mathscr{F}}(u^{\prime}-p)(l+k){\boldsymbol{a}}(k)

\displaystyle=0,

(2.10)

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{K}{\mathscr{F}}(p^{\prime})(l+k){\boldsymbol{a}}(k)

\displaystyle=0.

(2.11)

In the matrix-vector multiplication form,
eqs.

2.10
and
2.11
lead to

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{H}}}}\left({\mathscr{F}}\left(u^{\prime}-p\right)\big|_{[N]}\right){\boldsymbol{a}}={\mathbf{0}}~~~\text{and}~~~{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{H}}}}\left({\mathscr{F}}\left(p^{\prime}\right)\big|_{[N]}\right){\boldsymbol{a}}={\mathbf{0}},

which shows that two

Hankel matrices
{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{H}}}}\left({\mathscr{F}}\left(u^{\prime}-p\right)\big|_{[N]}\right)
and
{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{H}}}}\left({\mathscr{F}}\left(p^{\prime}\right)\big|_{[N]}\right)
have nontrivial nullspaces. In particular, for

\displaystyle e^{-2\pi imx}\varphi(x)=\sum_{k=m}^{K+m}{\boldsymbol{a}}(k-m)e^{-2\pi ikx},~~~~~m=0,\ldots,M-K

is also an annihilating polynomial, which shows that, if

, we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{rank}\left({\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{H}}}}\left({\mathscr{F}}\left(u^{\prime}-p\right)\big|_{[N]}\right)\right)\leq K~~~\text{and}~~~\mathrm{rank}\left({\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{H}}}}\left({\mathscr{F}}\left(p^{\prime}\right)\big|_{[N]}\right)\right)\leq K.

Therefore, both
{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{H}}}}\left({\mathscr{F}}\left(u^{\prime}-p\right)\big|_{[N]}\right)\in{\mathbb{C}}^{(N-M+1)\times M}
and
{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{H}}}}\left({\mathscr{F}}\left(p^{\prime}\right)\big|_{[N]}\right)\in{\mathbb{C}}^{(N-M+1)\times M}
are

which shows that it is possible to convert the piecewise regularity in the continuous domain into the low rank Hankel matrices corresponding to the discrete Fourier samples.

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{W}}}}_{1}^{*}{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{W}}}}_{1}\left({\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{D}}}}\boldsymbol{v}-\boldsymbol{q}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{l=1}^{M_{2}}{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{S}}}}_{\overline{{\boldsymbol{a}}}_{1l}}\left({\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{S}}}}_{{\boldsymbol{a}}_{1l}(-\cdot)}\left({\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{D}}}}\boldsymbol{v}-\boldsymbol{q}\right)\right)={\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{D}}}}\boldsymbol{v}-\boldsymbol{q},

(3.14)

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{W}}}}_{2}^{*}{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{W}}}}_{2}\left({\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{E}}}}\boldsymbol{q}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{l=1}^{M_{2}}{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{S}}}}_{\overline{{\boldsymbol{a}}}_{2l}}\left({\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{S}}}}_{{\boldsymbol{a}}_{2l}(-\cdot)}\left({\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{E}}}}\boldsymbol{q}\right)\right)={\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{E}}}}\boldsymbol{q},

(3.15)

and for
{\boldsymbol{k}}\in{\mathbb{O}}:{\mathbb{K}}
, we have

\displaystyle\left({\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{S}}}}_{{\boldsymbol{a}}_{1l}}\left({\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{D}}}}\boldsymbol{v}-\boldsymbol{q}\right)\right)({\boldsymbol{k}})

\displaystyle={\mathbf{0}},~~~~~l=r_{1}+1,\ldots,M_{2}

(3.16)

\displaystyle\left({\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{S}}}}_{{\boldsymbol{a}}_{2l}}\left({\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{E}}}}\boldsymbol{q}\right)\right)({\boldsymbol{k}})

\displaystyle={\mathbf{0}},~~~~~l=r_{2}+1,\ldots,M_{2},

(3.17)

where the discrete convolution is performed on each component of
{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{D}}}}\boldsymbol{v}-\boldsymbol{q}
and
{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{E}}}}\boldsymbol{q}
, respectively. Consequently, if
{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{H}}}}\left({\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{D}}}}\boldsymbol{v}-\boldsymbol{q}\right)
(and
{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{H}}}}\left({\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{E}}}}\boldsymbol{q}\right)
, respectively) is of low rank, then its right singular vectors construct a tight frame under which
{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{D}}}}\boldsymbol{v}-\boldsymbol{q}
(and
{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{E}}}}\boldsymbol{q}
, respectively) is sparsely represented.

In words,
theorem

3.7
tells us that if the SVDs of multi-fold Hankel matrices are known as oracles, we can explicitly construct tight frames under which
{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{D}}}}\boldsymbol{v}-\boldsymbol{q}
and
{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{E}}}}\boldsymbol{q}
are sparsely represented, and the sparsity of canonical coefficients can be grouped according to the filters. Hence, motivated by the idea in
[
35
]
, assume that
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{v}}\in{\mathcal{V}}
and
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{q}}\in{\mathcal{V}}_{1}
are the a-priori estimations of
\boldsymbol{v}
and
\boldsymbol{q}
with the SVDs

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{H}}}}\left({\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{D}}}}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{v}}-\widetilde{\boldsymbol{q}}\right)=\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{X}}}_{1}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{1}\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{Y}}}_{1}^{*}~~\text{and}~~{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{H}}}}\left({\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{E}}}}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{q}}\right)=\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{X}}}_{2}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{2}\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{Y}}}_{2}^{*}.

Then we define the tight frame transforms
{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{W}}}}_{1}
and
{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{W}}}}_{2}
in
eq.

3.11
via

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{a}}_{1l}=M_{2}^{-1/2}\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{Y}}}_{1}^{(:,l)}~~~\text{and}~~~{\boldsymbol{a}}_{2l}=M_{2}^{-1/2}\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{Y}}}_{2}^{(:,l)},~~~~l=1,\ldots,M_{2}.

Since
eqs.

3.16
and
3.17
are then approximately true under these
{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{W}}}}_{1}
and
{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{W}}}}_{2}
, we remove the group sparsity pattern in the canonical coefficients for the better sparse approximation instead. This leads us to solve

\displaystyle\min_{\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{q}}\frac{1}{2}\left\|{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{A}}}}\boldsymbol{v}-{\boldsymbol{f}}\right\|_{2}^{2}+\left\|\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{1}\cdot{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{W}}}}_{1}\left({\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{D}}}}\boldsymbol{v}-\boldsymbol{q}\right)\right\|_{1}+\left\|\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{2}\cdot{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{W}}}}_{2}\left({\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{E}}}}\boldsymbol{q}\right)\right\|_{1},

(3.18)

where the
\ell_{1}
norms take the form of

\displaystyle\left\|\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{1}\cdot{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{W}}}}_{1}\left({\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{D}}}}\boldsymbol{v}-\boldsymbol{q}\right)\right\|_{1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{l=1}^{M_{2}}\gamma_{1l}\left\|{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{S}}}}_{{\boldsymbol{a}}_{1l}(-\cdot)}\left({\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{D}}}}\boldsymbol{v}-\boldsymbol{q}\right)\right\|_{1}

\displaystyle\left\|\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{2}\cdot{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{W}}}}_{2}\left({\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{E}}}}\boldsymbol{q}\right)\right\|_{1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{l=1}^{M_{2}}\gamma_{2l}\left\|{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{S}}}}_{{\boldsymbol{a}}_{2l}(-\cdot)}\left({\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{E}}}}\boldsymbol{q}\right)\right\|_{1}

to reflect the different weights according to the index of filters. Finally, we reflect the singular values of the pre-restored Hankel matrices in the regularization parameters as

\displaystyle\gamma_{kl}=\frac{\nu_{k}}{\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{k}^{(l,l)}+\varepsilon}~~~~~k=1,2~~\text{and}~~l=1,\ldots,M_{2}

with some
\nu_{k}>0
and a small
\varepsilon>0
to avoid the division by zero. Hence, we relax the sparsity of tight frame coefficients over the entire range of
{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{W}}}}_{k}
’s (not necessarily in groups as in
eqs.

3.16
and
3.17
), we expect to achieve more flexibility, thereby leading to the improvements in restoration performance.

Since the wavelet frame based relaxation model
eq.

3.18
is inspired by the
eq.

3.2
via the SVDs of
{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{H}}}}\left({\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{D}}}}\boldsymbol{v}-\boldsymbol{q}\right)
and
{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{H}}}}\left({\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{E}}}}\boldsymbol{q}\right)
, we can say that our relaxation model
eq.

3.18
restores piecewise smooth functions by decomposing
{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{D}}}}\boldsymbol{v}
into
{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{D}}}}\boldsymbol{v}-\boldsymbol{q}
and
\boldsymbol{q}
, thereby balancing the low rank Hankel matrices of the Fourier samples of the first order derivatives and that of the second order derivatives. In addition, we further note that, following
[
10
]
, it is also possible to consider the following data driven tight frame model

\displaystyle\begin{split}&~~~\min_{\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{q},\left\{{\boldsymbol{c}}_{j},{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{W}}}}_{j}\right\}_{j=1}^{2}}\frac{1}{2}\left\|{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{A}}}}\boldsymbol{v}-{\boldsymbol{f}}\right\|_{2}^{2}+\frac{\mu_{1}}{2}\left\|{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{W}}}}_{1}\left({\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{D}}}}\boldsymbol{v}-\boldsymbol{q}\right)-{\boldsymbol{c}}_{1}\right\|_{2}^{2}+\left\|\boldsymbol{\gamma}\cdot{\boldsymbol{c}}_{1}\right\|_{0}\\
&\hskip 150.00023pt+\frac{\mu_{2}}{2}\left\|{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{W}}}}_{2}\left({\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{E}}}}\boldsymbol{q}\right)-{\boldsymbol{c}}_{2}\right\|_{2}^{2}+\left\|\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{2}\cdot{\boldsymbol{c}}_{2}\right\|_{0}\\
&~~~~~\text{subject to}~~{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{W}}}}_{1}^{*}{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{W}}}}_{1}={\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{W}}}}_{2}^{*}{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{W}}}}_{2}={\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{I}}}},\end{split}

(3.19)

with the
\ell_{0}
norm
\|\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{k}\cdot{\boldsymbol{c}}_{k}\|_{0}
(
k=1,2
) encoding the number of nonzero entries in
{\boldsymbol{c}}_{k}
’s, to learn tight frames and restore the Fourier sample
\boldsymbol{v}
simultaneously. Even though it is not clear at this point whether the adaptive tight frame system will lead to better restoration results or not, throughout this paper, we only consider the model
eq.

3.18
rather than the data driven tight frame model
eq.

3.19
, and the reasons are as follows. First of all, given that
{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{W}}}}_{k}
’s are properly estimated, it may not be necessary to further learn them with additional computational costs. Second, since
eq.

3.18
is convex whereas
eq.

3.19
is nonconvex, we can easily expect better behavior and theoretical support for the numerical algorithms. Most importantly, from the viewpoint of wavelet frame based image restoration, the model
eq.

3.18
is an
analysis approach

[
13
]
while the data driven tight frame model
eq.

3.19
can be classified into the
balanced approach

[
9
,
21
]
. (See
A
for the brief descriptions on the wavelet frame based models.) It is well known that the analysis approach reflects the structure of a target image better than other approaches (e.g.
[
11
]
). Since we derive the wavelet frame based approach from the structured low rank matrix framework, it can be expected that the convex relaxation model
eq.

3.18
will reflect our SLRM frameworks for the piecewise smooth functions better than the data driven tight frame model
eq.

3.19
.

Conclusion and future directions

In this paper, we have introduced a new structured low rank matrix framework for the piecewise smooth image restoration. Following the previous structured low rank matrix framework for the piecewise constant image
[
52
]
, we assume that the image singularities lie in the zero level set of a band-limited periodic function. Inspired by the total generalized variation
[
7
]
, we derive the annihilation relation of the Fourier samples of the gradient and the (symmetric) gradient tensor, which in turn leads to the low rank multi-fold Hankel matrices to balance between the first order derivatives and the second order derivatives. In addition, as a by-product of the proposed structured low rank matrix framework, we further introduce a wavelet frame based sparse regularization model for the piecewise image restoration via the continuous domain regularization, from the SVDs of low rank multi-fold Hankel matrices. Finally, the numerical experiments show that the proposed wavelet frame based model based on the proposed SLRM framework outperforms both the conventional on-the-grid approaches and the existing the SLRM framework as well as the existing rank minimization approaches for the piecewise smooth image restoration.

For the future work, we plan to generalize the frameworks in
[
52
]
to the piecewise smooth image restoration. More precisely, we need to develop sampling guarantees for the unique restoration of a continuous domain piecewise smooth image from its uniform low pass Fourier samples, based on the proposed annihilation relations. Unfortunately, it is not clear for us at this moment under what conditions we can restore the singularity set from uniform Fourier samples as the annihilation relations involve a blind deconvolution problem due to the additional unknown vector field
p
in
theorem

2.1
. Nevertheless, this is definitely a future direction we would like to work on. We are also interested in proving the restoration guarantee of
eq.

3.2
(or its convex nuclear norm relaxation) given that
{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{A}}}}={\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{R}}}}_{{\mathbb{M}}}
, i.e. the sample grid
{\mathbb{M}}
is drawn from the image grid
{\mathbb{O}}
uniformly at random, which generalizes the theoretical frameworks in
[
49
]
to the piecewise smooth image restoration.

Finally, to broaden the scope of applications, it is also likely to apply the idea in this paper to the various image restoration tasks such as the blind deconvolution (especially that in the frequency domain which is related to the retinex
[
46
]
) and the sparse angle CT. In fact, it is not clear at this moment how to extend this approach to the sparse angle CT restoration, as the measurement can be viewed as the Fourier samples on the discrete radial grid. Nevertheless, this is also an important future work for the broader scope of applications. Finally, observing the successive structure in our SLRM framework, we are also interested in developing a deep learning framework, motivated by the recent works on the deep learning techniques combined with the structured low rank matrix approaches
[
40
,
43
,
56
,
57
]
.

Appendix A
Preliminaries on tight wavelet frames

Provided here is a brief introduction on tight wavelet frames and the wavelet frame based image restoration. Interested readers may consult
[
9
,
26
,
27
,
60
,
62
]
for detailed surveys on tight wavelet frames, and
[
11
]
for detailed reviews on the wavelet frame based image restoration. For the sake of simplicity, we only discuss the real valued wavelet tight frame systems, but note that it is not difficult to extend the idea to the complex case.

Let
{\mathscr{H}}
be a Hilbert space equipped with an inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
. A (Bessel) sequence
\{\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{n}:n\in{\mathbb{Z}}\}\subseteq{\mathscr{H}}
is called a tight frame on
{\mathscr{H}}
if

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{u}\|^{2}=\sum_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}|\langle\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{n}\rangle|^{2}~~~~~\text{for all}~~~\boldsymbol{u}\in{\mathscr{H}}.

(A.1)

Given
\{\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{n}:n\in{\mathbb{Z}}\}\subseteq{\mathscr{H}}
, we define the analysis operator
{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{W}}}}:{\mathscr{H}}\to\ell_{2}({\mathbb{Z}})
as

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{u}\in{\mathscr{H}}\mapsto{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{W}}}}\boldsymbol{u}=\{\langle\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{n}\rangle:n\in{\mathbb{Z}}\}\in\ell_{2}({\mathbb{Z}}).

The synthesis operator
{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{W}}}}^{T}:\ell_{2}({\mathbb{Z}})\to{\mathscr{H}}
is defined as the adjoint of
{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{W}}}}
:

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{c}}\in\ell_{2}({\mathbb{Z}})\mapsto{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{W}}}}^{T}{\boldsymbol{c}}=\sum_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}{\boldsymbol{c}}(n)\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{n}\in{\mathscr{H}}.

Then
\{\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{n}:n\in{\mathbb{Z}}\}
is a tight frame on
{\mathscr{H}}
if and only if
{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{W}}}}^{T}{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{W}}}}={\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{I}}}}
. It follows that, for a given tight frame
\{\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{n}:n\in{\mathbb{Z}}\}
, we have the following canonical expression:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{u}=\sum_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}\langle\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{n}\rangle\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{n},

with
{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{W}}}}\boldsymbol{u}=\{\langle\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{n}\rangle:n\in{\mathbb{Z}}\}
being called the canonical tight frame coefficients. Hence, the tight frames are extensions of orthonormal bases to the redundant systems. In fact, a tight frame is an orthonormal basis if and only if
\|\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{n}\|=1
for all
n\in{\mathbb{Z}}
.

One of the most widely used class of tight frames is the discrete wavelet frame generated by a set of finitely supported filters
\{{\boldsymbol{a}}_{1},\cdots,{\boldsymbol{a}}_{m}\}
. Throughout this paper, we only discuss the two dimensional undecimated wavelet frames on
\ell_{2}({\mathbb{Z}}^{2})
, but note that it is not difficult to extend to
\ell_{2}({\mathbb{Z}}^{d})
with
d\geq 3
. For
{\boldsymbol{a}}\in\ell_{1}({\mathbb{Z}}^{2})
, define a convolution operator
{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{S}}}}_{{\boldsymbol{a}}}:\ell_{2}({\mathbb{Z}}^{2})\to\ell_{2}({\mathbb{Z}}^{2})
by

\displaystyle({\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{S}}}}_{{\boldsymbol{a}}}\boldsymbol{u})({\boldsymbol{k}})=({\boldsymbol{a}}\ast\boldsymbol{u})({\boldsymbol{k}})=\sum_{{\boldsymbol{l}}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}}{\boldsymbol{a}}({\boldsymbol{k}}-{\boldsymbol{l}})\boldsymbol{u}({\boldsymbol{l}})~~~~~\text{for}~~~\boldsymbol{u}\in\ell_{2}({\mathbb{Z}}^{2}).

(A.2)

Given a set of finitely supported filters
\{{\boldsymbol{a}}_{1},\cdots,{\boldsymbol{a}}_{m}\}
, define the analysis operator
{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{W}}}}
and the synthesis operator
{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{W}}}}^{T}
by

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{W}}}}

\displaystyle=\left[{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{S}}}}_{{\boldsymbol{a}}_{1}(-\cdot)}^{T},{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{S}}}}_{{\boldsymbol{a}}_{2}(-\cdot)}^{T},\cdots,{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{S}}}}_{{\boldsymbol{a}}_{m}(-\cdot)}^{T}\right]^{T},

(A.3)

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{W}}}}^{T}

\displaystyle=\big[{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{S}}}}_{{\boldsymbol{a}}_{1}},{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{S}}}}_{{\boldsymbol{a}}_{2}},\cdots,{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{S}}}}_{{\boldsymbol{a}}_{m}}\big],

(A.4)

respectively. Then, the direct computation can show that the rows of
{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{W}}}}
form a tight frame on
\ell_{2}({\mathbb{Z}}^{2})
(i.e.
{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{W}}}}^{T}{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{W}}}}={\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{I}}}}
) if and only if the filters
\{{\boldsymbol{a}}_{1},\cdots,{\boldsymbol{a}}_{m}\}
satisfy

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{m}\sum_{{\boldsymbol{l}}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}}{\boldsymbol{a}}_{j}({\boldsymbol{k}}+{\boldsymbol{l}}){\boldsymbol{a}}_{j}({\boldsymbol{l}})=\boldsymbol{\delta}({\boldsymbol{k}})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}1&\text{if}~{\boldsymbol{k}}={\mathbf{0}},\\
0&\text{if}~{\boldsymbol{k}}\neq{\mathbf{0}},\end{array}\right.

called the
unitary extension principle
(UEP) condition
[
37
]
. Finally, for a two dimensional discrete image on the finite grid,
{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{S}}}}_{{\boldsymbol{a}}}
in
eq.

A.2
with a finitely supported
{\boldsymbol{a}}
denotes the discrete convolution under the periodic boundary condition throughout this paper.

For the image restoration, we assume that there exists a tight wavelet frame
{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{W}}}}
defined as
eq.

A.3
with filters
\left\{{\boldsymbol{a}}_{1},\cdots,{\boldsymbol{a}}_{m}\right\}
such that
\boldsymbol{u}
is sparse under
{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{W}}}}
. This leads us to solve

\displaystyle\min_{{\boldsymbol{c}}}\frac{1}{2}\|{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{A}}}}{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{W}}}}^{T}{\boldsymbol{c}}-{\boldsymbol{f}}\|_{2}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\|({\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{I}}}}-{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{W}}}}{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{W}}}}^{T}){\boldsymbol{c}}\|_{2}^{2}+\|\boldsymbol{\gamma}\cdot{\boldsymbol{c}}\|_{1}

(A.7)

and restore
\boldsymbol{u}={\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{W}}}}^{T}{\boldsymbol{c}}
. In
eq.

A.7
, the first term is a data fidelity term, the third
\ell_{1}
norm is defined as

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{\gamma}\cdot{\boldsymbol{c}}\|_{1}=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\gamma_{j}\left\|{\boldsymbol{c}}_{j}\right\|_{1}

(A.8)

with the parameters
\boldsymbol{\gamma}=\left[\gamma_{1},\ldots,\gamma_{m}\right]^{T}
and
\gamma_{j}>0
, to promote the sparsity of
{\boldsymbol{c}}
. The second term penalizes the distance between
{\boldsymbol{c}}
and its projection onto
{\mathscr{R}}({\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{W}}}})
. Since we obtain the image via
\boldsymbol{u}={\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{W}}}}^{T}{\boldsymbol{c}}
, the second term in fact forces the sparse coefficient
{\boldsymbol{c}}
close to the canonical coefficient of the restored image. Since the magnitude/decay of the canonical coefficient reflects the regularity of the image under some mild conditions on the tight frame system
{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{W}}}}

[
4
]
, we can see that the second and third terms promote a balance between the sparsity of coefficient and the regularity of image.

We can further impose the flexibility on
eq.

A.7
by introducing the weight on the second term:

\displaystyle\min_{{\boldsymbol{c}}}\frac{1}{2}\|{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{A}}}}{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{W}}}}^{T}{\boldsymbol{c}}-{\boldsymbol{f}}\|_{2}^{2}+\frac{\mu}{2}\|({\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{I}}}}-{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{W}}}}{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{W}}}}^{T}){\boldsymbol{c}}\|_{2}^{2}+\|\boldsymbol{\gamma}\cdot{\boldsymbol{c}}\|_{1},

(A.9)

called the balanced approach
[
9
,
21
]
. If
\mu=0
, then
eq.

A.9
becomes the synthesis approach
[
25
,
29
,
31
]

\displaystyle\min_{{\boldsymbol{c}}}\frac{1}{2}\|{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{A}}}}{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{W}}}}^{T}{\boldsymbol{c}}-{\boldsymbol{f}}\|_{2}^{2}+\|\boldsymbol{\gamma}\cdot{\boldsymbol{c}}\|_{1},

(A.10)

as we aim to find the sparsest coefficient that synthesizes the image. On the other hand, if
\mu=\infty
, then it must be
({\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{I}}}}-{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{W}}}}{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{W}}}}^{T}){\boldsymbol{c}}={\mathbf{0}}
. Since we then have
{\boldsymbol{c}}\in{\mathscr{R}}({\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{W}}}})
, it follows that
{\boldsymbol{c}}={\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{W}}}}\boldsymbol{u}
for some
\boldsymbol{u}
. Hence,
eq.

A.7
becomes the analysis approach
[
13
]

\displaystyle\min_{\boldsymbol{u}}\frac{1}{2}\|{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{A}}}}\boldsymbol{u}-{\boldsymbol{f}}\|_{2}^{2}+\|\boldsymbol{\gamma}\cdot{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{W}}}}\boldsymbol{u}\|_{1}.

(A.11)

Since the analysis approach promotes the sparse canonical coefficient, it emphasizes the regularity of the restored image.

These three approaches are equivalent if and only if
{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{W}}}}^{T}{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{W}}}}={\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{W}}}}{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{W}}}}^{T}={\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{I}}}}
, i.e.
{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{W}}}}
forms an orthonormal basis. However, since we consider the redundant system, each approach will lead to the different restoration results. Notice that the coefficient vector
{\boldsymbol{c}}
obtained from the synthesis approach
eq.

A.10
is much sparser than the canonical coefficient
{\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{W}}}}\boldsymbol{u}
obtained from the analysis approach
eq.

A.11
. It is empirically observed that the synthesis approach
eq.

A.10
tends to yield some artifacts in the restored image. In contrast, the analysis approach
eq.

A.11
usually have less artifacts as it promotes the regularity of the image along the singularities. Theoretically, it has been proved in
[
11
]
that, under a suitable choice of parameters, the analysis approach
eq.

A.11
can be seen as sophisticated discretization of a variational model including the Rudin-Osher-Fatemi model
[
61
]
, which enables a geometric interpretation on
eq.

A.11
.

Appendix B
Proof of
theorem

2.1

Let
u(\boldsymbol{x})
be defined as in
eq.

2.12
where the singularity curves
\Gamma=\bigcup_{j=1}^{J}\partial\Omega_{j}
satisfy
eq.

2.14
. In the sense of distribution, the gradient of
u
satisfies

\displaystyle\nabla u(\boldsymbol{x})=\left(\partial_{1}u(\boldsymbol{x}),\partial_{2}u(\boldsymbol{x})\right)=\sum_{j=1}^{J}\left(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{j}1_{\Omega_{j}}(\boldsymbol{x})+\left(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{j}^{T}\boldsymbol{x}+\beta_{j}\right)\boldsymbol{n}_{j}(\boldsymbol{x})\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\sigma}(\boldsymbol{x})\big|_{\partial\Omega_{j}}\right)

(B.1)

where
\boldsymbol{n}_{j}=\left(n_{j1},n_{j2}\right)
is the outward normal vector on
\partial\Omega_{j}
, and
\boldsymbol{\sigma}
is the surface measure. We define

\displaystyle p(\boldsymbol{x})=\left(p_{1}(\boldsymbol{x}),p_{2}(\boldsymbol{x})\right)=\sum_{j=1}^{J}\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{j}1_{\Omega_{j}}(\boldsymbol{x})

and

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\nu}(\boldsymbol{x})=\sum_{j=1}^{J}\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\nu}_{j}(\boldsymbol{x})=\sum_{j=1}^{J}\left(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{j}^{T}\boldsymbol{x}+\beta_{j}\boldsymbol{n}_{j}(\boldsymbol{x})\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\sigma}(\boldsymbol{x})\big|_{\partial\Omega_{j}}\right).

Then it is obvious that we have

\displaystyle\nabla u-p=\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\nu}=\sum_{j=1}^{J}\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\nu}_{j}.

(B.2)

Since
|\boldsymbol{n}_{j}|=1
almost everywhere on
\partial\Omega_{j}
,
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{j}^{T}\boldsymbol{x}+\beta_{j}
is continuous on a compact set
\partial\Omega_{j}
,
\nabla u-p
in
eq.

B.2
defines a finite Radon vector measure on
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
supported on
\Gamma
. In addition, since
eq.

B.1
as well as
eq.

B.2
holds in the sense of tempered distribution, we can compute
{\mathscr{F}}(\nabla u-p)({\boldsymbol{\xi}})
as a Fourier transform of a measure (e.g.
[
32
]
). Namely, we have

\displaystyle{\mathscr{F}}(\nabla u-p)({\boldsymbol{\xi}})=\sum_{j=1}^{J}\int_{\partial\Omega_{j}}e^{-2\pi i{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\cdot\boldsymbol{x}}\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\nu}_{j}(\boldsymbol{x}).

(B.3)

Let
\varphi\in{\mathscr{S}}
, where
{\mathscr{S}}
is the space of Schwartz functions (e.g.
[
32
]
). Then the direct computation shows that
\left({\mathscr{F}}(\nabla u-p)\ast\widehat{\varphi}\right)({\boldsymbol{\xi}})
satisfies

\displaystyle\begin{split}\left({\mathscr{F}}(\nabla u-p)\ast\widehat{\varphi}\right)({\boldsymbol{\xi}})=\sum_{j=1}^{J}\int_{\partial\Omega_{j}}e^{-2\pi i{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\cdot\boldsymbol{x}}\varphi(\boldsymbol{x})\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\nu}_{j}(\boldsymbol{x}).\end{split}

(B.4)

From
eq.

B.4
, it is easy to see that if
\Gamma
lies in the zero level set of
\varphi
, then
{\mathscr{F}}(\nabla u-p)\ast\widehat{\varphi}=0
. In particular, if
\varphi
is defined as
eq.

2.14
, then

\displaystyle\widehat{\varphi}({\boldsymbol{\xi}})=\sum_{{\boldsymbol{k}}\in{\mathbb{K}}}{\boldsymbol{a}}({\boldsymbol{k}})\delta({\boldsymbol{\xi}}+{\boldsymbol{k}}),

which leads to

\displaystyle\left({\mathscr{F}}(\nabla u-p)\ast\widehat{\varphi}\right)({\boldsymbol{\xi}})=\sum_{{\boldsymbol{k}}\in{\mathbb{K}}}{\mathscr{F}}(\nabla u-p)({\boldsymbol{\xi}}+{\boldsymbol{k}}){\boldsymbol{a}}({\boldsymbol{k}})=0.

This proves
eq.

2.15
.

To complete the proof, we further note that in the sense of distribution,
\nabla_{s}p
satisfies

\displaystyle\nabla_{s}p=-\sum_{j=1}^{J}\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\alpha_{j1}n_{j1}&\displaystyle{\frac{1}{2}\left(\alpha_{j1}n_{j2}+\alpha_{j2}n_{j1}\right)}\\
\displaystyle{\frac{1}{2}\left(\alpha_{j1}n_{j2}+\alpha_{j2}n_{j1}\right)}&\alpha_{j2}n_{j2}\end{array}\right]\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\sigma}\big|_{\partial\Omega_{j}}:=\sum_{j=1}^{J}\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{j}.

Following the similar arguments, it is not difficult to verify that
\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{j}
in
appendix

B
defines a finite Radon tensor measure on
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
supported on
\Gamma
. which means that
{\mathscr{F}}(\nabla_{s}p)
is defined as

\displaystyle{\mathscr{F}}(\nabla_{s}p)({\boldsymbol{\xi}})=\sum_{j=1}^{J}\int_{\partial\Omega_{j}}e^{-2\pi i{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\cdot\boldsymbol{x}}\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{j}(\boldsymbol{x}).

(B.7)

Therefore, for
\varphi\in{\mathscr{S}}
