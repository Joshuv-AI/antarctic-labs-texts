Probabilistic Timed Automata with One Clock and Initialised Clock-Dependent Probabilities
Jeremy Sproston

Abstract
. Clock-dependent probabilistic timed automata extend classical timed automata with discrete probabilistic choice, where the probabilities are allowed to depend on the exact values of the clocks. Previous work has shown that the quantitative reachability problem for clock-dependent probabilistic timed automata with at least three clocks is undecidable. In this paper, we consider the subclass of clock-dependent probabilistic timed automata that have one clock , that have clock dependencies described by affine functions, and that satisfy an initialisation condition requiring that, at some point between taking edges with non-trivial clock dependencies, the clock must have an integer value. We present an approach for solving in polynomial time quantitative and qualitative reachability problems of such one-clock initialised clock-dependent probabilistic timed automata. Our results are obtained by a transformation to interval Markov decision processes.

Introduction

The diffusion of complex systems with timing requirements
that operate in unpredictable environments
has led to interest in formal modelling and verification techniques for
timed and probabilistic systems.
Model checking
[
CGP01
,
BK08
]
is an example of a formal verification technique,
and comprises the automatic verification of a system model against formally-specified properties.
A well-established modelling formalism for timed systems is
timed automata

[
AD94
]
.
A timed automaton consists of a finite graph equipped with
a set of real-valued variables called
clocks
,
which increase at the same rate as real time and which can be used
to constrain the relative time of events.
To model probabilistic systems formally,
frameworks such as Markov chains or Markov decision processes are used typically.
Model-checking algorithms for these formalisms have been presented in the literature:
for overviews of these techniques see, for example,
[
BFL+18
]
for timed automata,
and
[
BK08
,
FKNP11
]
for Markov chains and Markov decision processes.
Furthermore, timed automata and Markov decision processes
have been combined to obtain
the formalism of
probabilistic timed automata

[
GJ95
,
KNSS02
,
NPS13
]
,
which can be viewed as timed automata with probabilities associated with their edges
(or, equivalently, as Markov decision processes equipped with clocks and their associated constraints).

For the modelling of certain systems,
it may be advantageous to model the fact that the probability of some events,
in particular those concerning the environment in which the system is operating,
vary as time passes.
For example, in automotive and aeronautic contexts,
the probability of certain reactions of human operators may depend on factors such as fatigue,
which can increase over time
(see, for example,
[
FWHT16
]
);
an increase in the amount of time that an unmanned aerial vehicle spends performing a search and rescue operation
in a hazardous zone may increase the probability that the vehicle incurs damage from the environment;
an increase in the time elapsed before a metro train arrives at a station
can result in an increase in the number of passengers on the station’s platform,
which can in turn increase the probability of the doors failing to shut at the station,
due to overcrowding of the train
(see
[
BBH+19
]
).
A natural way of representing such a dependency of probability of events on time
is using a continuous function:
for example,
for the case in which a task can be completed between
1
and
3
time units in the future,
we could represent the successful completion of the task by probability
\frac{x+1}{4}
,
where the clock variable
x

(measuring the amount of time elapsed)
ranges over the interval
[1,3]
.
The standard probabilistic timed automaton formalism cannot express such a continuous relationship between
probabilities and time,
being limited to step functions
(where the intervals along which the function is constant must have rational-numbered endpoints).
This limitation led to the development of an extension of probabilistic timed automata
called
clock-dependent probabilistic timed automata

[
Spr21
]
,
in which the probabilities of crossing edges can depend on clock values according to piecewise constant functions.
Figure
1
gives an example of such a clock-dependent probabilistic timed automaton,
using the standard conventions for the graphical representation of (probabilistic) timed automata
(the model has one clock denoted by
x
, and black boxes denote probabilistic choices over outgoing edges).
In location
\mathrm{W}
, the system is
working
on a task,
which is completed after between
1
and
3
units of time.
When the task is completed, it is either
successful
(edge to location
\mathrm{S}
),

fails
(edge to location
\mathrm{F}
)
or leads to system
termination
(edge to location
\mathrm{T}
).
For the case in which the task completion fails,
between
4
and
5
time units after work on the task started
the system may either restart the task from the beginning (edge to location
\mathrm{W}
, resetting
x
to
0
),
or terminate (edge to location
\mathrm{T}
).
The edges corresponding to probabilistic choices are labelled with
expressions
over the clock
x
,
which describe how the probability of those edges changes in accordance with changes in the value of
x
.
For example, the longer the time spent in location
\mathrm{W}
,
the higher the value of
x
when location
\mathrm{W}
is left,
and the higher the probability of making a transition to location
\mathrm{S}
,
which corresponds to the successful completion of the task.

Previous work on clock-dependent probabilistic timed automata showed that a basic

quantitative
reachability problem,
regarding whether there is a scheduler of nondeterministic choice such that the probability of reaching a set of target locations
exceeds some probability threshold,
is undecidable,
but that an approach based on the region graph
(which is a finite-state abstraction used extensively for timed automata)
can be employed to approximate optimal
reachability probabilities
[
Spr21
]
.
The undecidability result relied on the presence of at least three clocks:
in this paper, following similar precedents in the context of (non-probabilistic and probabilistic) variants of timed automata
(for example,
[
LMS04
,
BLM08
,
JLS08
,
BBB+14
,
BBG14
,
ABK+16
]
),
we restrict our attention to clock-dependent probabilistic timed automata with a
single
clock variable.
As in
[
Spr21
]
, we consider the case in which the dependencies of transition probabilities
on the value of the clock
are described by affine functions.
Furthermore, we assume that,
between any two edges with a non-constant dependence on the clock,
the clock must have a natural-numbered value,
either through being reset to
0
or by increasing as time passes.
We call this condition
initialisation
,
following the precedents of
[
ABK+16
]
and
[
HKPV98
]
,
in which similar conditions are used to obtain decidability results for stochastic timed systems with one clock,
and hybrid automata, respectively;
intuitively, the value of the clock is “reinitialised”
(either explicitly, through a reset to
0
, or implicitly, through the passage of time)
to a known, natural value between
non-constant dependencies of probability on the value of the clock.
Note that the clock-dependent probabilistic timed automaton of Figure
1

satisfies this assumption
(although clock
x
is not reset on the edge to location
\mathrm{F}
, it must take values
3
and
4
,
i.e., at least one natural-numbered value,
before location
\mathrm{F}
can be left).
We show that, for such clock-dependent probabilistic timed automata,
quantitative reachability problems can be solved in polynomial time.
Similarly, we can also solve in polynomial time
qualitative
reachability problems,
which ask whether there exists a scheduler of nondeterminism
such that a set of target locations can be reached with probability
1
(or
0
),
or whether all schedulers of nondeterminism result in the target locations being reached with probability
1
(or
0
).

These results rely on the construction of an
interval Markov decision process
from
the one-clock clock-dependent probabilistic timed automaton.
Interval Markov decision processes have been well-studied in the verification context
(for example, in
[
PLSS13
,
HHK14
,
HM18
]
),
and also in other contexts, such as planning
[
GLD00
]
and control
[
NE05
,
WK08
]
.
They comprise a finite state space where transitions between states are achieved in the following manner:
for each state, there is a nondeterministic choice between a set of actions,
where each action is associated with a decoration of the set of edges from the state with
intervals
in
[0,1]
;
then a nondeterministic choice as to the exact probabilities associated with each outgoing edge is chosen from
the intervals associated with the action chosen in the first step;
finally, a probabilistic choice is made over the edges according to the probabilities chosen in the second step,
thus determining the next state.
In contrast to the standard formulation of interval Markov decision processes,
we allow edges corresponding to probabilistic choices to be labelled not only with closed intervals,
but also with open and half-open intervals.
While (half-)open intervals have been considered previously in the context of interval Markov chains in
[
CK15
,
Spr18
]
,
we are unaware of any work considering them in the context of interval Markov decision processes.
Open intervals in the constructed interval Markov decision process
provide a natural way of representing strict constraints on clocks
in the one-clock clock-dependent probabilistic timed automaton.

We proceed by giving some preliminary concepts in Section
2
:
this includes a reduction from interval Markov decision processes
to interval Markov chains
[
JL91
,
KU02
,
SVA06
]

with the standard Markov decision process-based semantics,
which may be of independent interest.
The reduction takes open and half-open intervals into account;
while
[
CK15
]
has shown that open interval Markov chains can be reduced to closed Markov chains for the purposes
of quantitative properties,
[
Spr18
]
shows that the open/closed distinction is critical for the evaluation of qualitative properties.
In Section
3
,
we present the definition of one-clock clock-dependent probabilistic timed automata,
and describe the transformation to interval Markov decision processes in Section
4
.
This paper extends the conference version
[
Spr20
]

with full proofs of the results.

2.
Interval Markov Decision Processes

In this section, we focus on interval Markov decision processes,
a formalism that we will subsequently use to construct exact finite-state abstractions
from one-clock clock-dependent probabilistic timed automata.
First we present a number of preliminary concepts,
before proceeding to define interval Markov chains and interval Markov decision processes.
At the end of the section, we present a novel result,
namely a translation from interval Markov decision processes
to interval Markov chains,
and prove its correctness with respect to quantitative and qualitative reachability problems.

2.1.
Preliminaries.

We use
\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
to denote the set of non-negative real numbers,

\mathbb{Q}
to denote the set of rational numbers, and

\mathbb{N}
to denote the set of natural numbers.
A (discrete) probability
distribution
over a countable set
Q
is
a function
{\mu:Q\rightarrow[0,1]}
such that
\sum_{q\in Q}\mu(q)=1
.
Let
\mathsf{Dist}(Q)
be the set of distributions over
Q
.
For a (possibly uncountable) set
Q
and a function
\mu:Q\rightarrow[0,1]
,
we define
\mathsf{support}(\mu)=\{{q\in Q\mid\mu(q)>0}\}
.
Then, for an uncountable set
Q
, we define
\mathsf{Dist}(Q)
to be the set of
functions
{\mu:Q\rightarrow[0,1]}
such that
\mathsf{support}(\mu)
is a
countable set and
\mu
restricted to
\mathsf{support}(\mu)
is a distribution.
Given a binary function
f:Q\times Q\rightarrow[0,1]
and element
q\in Q
,
we denote by
f(q,\cdot):Q\rightarrow[0,1]
the unary function
such that
f(q,\cdot)(q^{\prime})=f(q,q^{\prime})
for each
q^{\prime}\in Q
.

A
Markov chain

(MC)
\mathcal{C}
is a pair
(S,\mathbf{P})

where
S
is a set of
states

and
\mathbf{P}:S\times S\rightarrow[0,1]
is a
transition probability function
,
such that
\mathbf{P}(s,\cdot)\in\mathsf{Dist}(S)
for each state
s\in S
.
A
path
of MC
\mathcal{C}
is a sequence
s_{0}s_{1}\cdots
of states
such that
\mathbf{P}(s_{i},s_{i+1})>0
for all
i\geq 0
.
Given a path
\mathbf{r}=s_{0}s_{1}\cdots
and
i\geq 0
,
we let
\mathbf{r}(i)=s_{i}
be the
(i+1)
-th state along
\mathbf{r}
.
The set of paths of
\mathcal{C}
starting in state
s\in S
is denoted by
\mathit{Paths}^{\mathcal{C}}(s)
.
In the standard manner (see, for example,
[
BK08
,
FKNP11
]
),
given a state
s\in S
,
we can define a probability measure
\mathrm{Pr}^{\mathcal{C}}_{s}
over
\mathit{Paths}^{\mathcal{C}}(s)
.

A
Markov decision process
(MDP)

\mathcal{M}=(S,A,\Delta)
comprises
a set
S
of
states
, a set
A
of
actions
,
and a
probabilistic transition function

\Delta:S\times A\rightarrow\mathsf{Dist}(S)\cup\{{\bot}\}
.
The symbol
\bot
is used to represent the unavailability of an action in a state,
i.e.,
\Delta(s,a)=\bot
signifies that
action
a\in A
is not available in state
s\in S
.
For each state
s\in S
,
let
A({s})=\{{a\in A\mid\Delta(s,a)\neq\bot}\}
,
and assume that
A({s})\neq\emptyset
,
i.e., there is at least one available action in each state.
Transitions from state to state of an MDP are performed in two
steps: if the current state is
s
, the first step concerns a
nondeterministic selection of an action
a\in A({s})
;
the second step comprises a probabilistic choice, made according to the distribution
\Delta(s,a)
,
as to which state to make the transition
(that is, a transition to a state
s^{\prime}\in S
is made with probability
\Delta(s,a)(s^{\prime})
).
In general, the sets of states and actions
can be uncountable.
We say that an MDP is
finite
if
S
and
A
are finite sets.

A(n infinite) path of an MDP
\mathcal{M}
is a sequence
s_{0}a_{0}s_{1}a_{1}\cdots

such that
a_{i}\in A({s_{i}})
and
\Delta(s_{i},a_{i})(s_{i+1})>0

for all
i\geq 0
.
Given an infinite path
\mathbf{r}=s_{0}a_{0}s_{1}a_{1}\cdots
and
i\geq 0
,
we let
\mathbf{r}(i)=s_{i}
be the
(i+1)
-th state along
\mathbf{r}
.
Let
\mathit{Paths}^{\mathcal{M}}
be the set of infinite paths of
\mathcal{M}
.
A finite path is a sequence
r=s_{0}a_{0}s_{1}a_{1}\cdots a_{n-1}s_{n}

such that
a_{i}\in A({s_{i}})
and
\Delta(s_{i},a_{i})(s_{i+1})>0

for all
0\leq i<n
.
Let
\mathit{last}(r)=s_{n}
denote the final state of
r
. For
a\in A({s_{n}})
and
s\in S

such that
\Delta(s_{n},a)(s)>0
,
we use
ras
to denote the finite path

s_{0}a_{0}s_{1}a_{1}\cdots a_{n-1}s_{n}as

Let
\mathit{Paths}^{\mathcal{M}}_{*}
be the set of finite paths of the MDP
\mathcal{M}
.
Let
\mathit{Paths}^{\mathcal{M}}(s)
and
\mathit{Paths}^{\mathcal{M}}_{*}(s)

be the sets of infinite paths and finite paths, respectively,
of
\mathcal{M}
starting in state
s\in S
.

A

is a function

\sigma:\mathit{Paths}^{\mathcal{M}}_{*}\rightarrow\bigcup_{s\in S}\mathsf{Dist}(A({s}))

such that
\sigma(r)\in\mathsf{Dist}(A({\mathit{last}(r)}))

.

Let

Given a set

,
we define
\Diamond T=\{\mathbf{r}\in\mathit{Paths}^{\mathcal{M}}\mid\exists i\in\mathbb{N}\,.\,\mathbf{r}(i)\in T\}

.
We define the

as

\mathbb{P}^{\mathrm{max}}_{{\mathcal{M}},{s}}(\Diamond{T})=\sup_{\sigma\in\Sigma^{\mathcal{M}}}~\mathrm{Pr}^{\sigma}_{s}(\Diamond T)
.

from

is defined as

\mathbb{P}^{\mathrm{min}}_{{\mathcal{M}},{s}}(\Diamond{T})=\inf_{\sigma\in\Sigma^{\mathcal{M}}}~\mathrm{Pr}^{\sigma}_{s}(\Diamond T)
.
The

,

,

\unrhd\in\{\geq,>\}

.

\forall 0

\mathrm{Pr}^{\sigma}_{s}(\Diamond T)=0

\sigma\in\Sigma^{\mathcal{M}}
;
(
\exists 0
)

\sigma\in\Sigma^{\mathcal{M}}

such that
\mathrm{Pr}^{\sigma}_{s}(\Diamond T)=0
;
(
\exists 1
)
decide whether there exists
\sigma\in\Sigma^{\mathcal{M}}

such that
\mathrm{Pr}^{\sigma}_{s}(\Diamond T)=1
;
(
\forall 1

2.2.
Interval Markov Chains.

We let
\mathcal{I}
denote the set of (open, half-open or closed) intervals
that are subsets of
[0,1]

and that have rational-numbered endpoints.
Given an interval
I\in\mathcal{I}
,
we let
\mathsf{left}({I})
(respectively,
\mathsf{right}({I})
) be the left (respectively, right) endpoint of
I
.

An
interval distribution
over a finite set
Q
is a function
\mathfrak{d}:Q\rightarrow\mathcal{I}

such that:

(1):

\sum_{q\in Q}\mathsf{left}({\mathfrak{d}(q)})\leq 1\leq\sum_{q\in Q}\mathsf{right}({\mathfrak{d}(q)})
,

(2a):

\sum_{q\in Q}\mathsf{left}({\mathfrak{d}(q)})=1
implies that

\mathfrak{d}(q)
is left-closed for all
q\in Q
,
and

(2b):

\sum_{q\in Q}\mathsf{right}({\mathfrak{d}(q)})=1
implies that

\mathfrak{d}(q)
is right-closed for all
q\in Q
.

We define
\mathfrak{Dist}(Q)
as the set of interval distributions over
Q
.
An
assignment for interval distribution
\mathfrak{d}

is a distribution
\alpha\in\mathsf{Dist}(Q)
such that

\alpha(q)\in\mathfrak{d}(q)
for each
q\in Q
.
Note that conditions (1), (2a) and (2b) in the definition of interval distributions
guarantee that there exists at least one assignment for each interval distribution.
Let
\mathfrak{G}({\mathfrak{d}})
be the set of assignments for
\mathfrak{d}
.

An (open)
interval Markov chain
(IMC)
\mathfrak{C}
is a pair
(S,\mathfrak{P})
,
where
S
is a finite set of
states
,
and
\mathfrak{P}:S\times S\rightarrow\mathcal{I}

is a
interval-based transition function

such that
\mathfrak{P}(s,\cdot)
is an interval distribution for each
s\in S

(formally,
\mathfrak{P}(s,\cdot)\in\mathfrak{Dist}(S)
).
An IMC makes a transition from a state
s\in S
in two steps:
first an assignment
\alpha
is chosen from the set
\mathfrak{G}({\mathfrak{P}(s,\cdot)})
of assignments for
\mathfrak{P}(s,\cdot)
,
then a probabilistic choice over target states is made according to
\alpha
.
Let
\mathfrak{G}({\mathfrak{P}})=\bigcup_{s\in S}\mathfrak{G}({\mathfrak{P}(s,\cdot)})

be the set of all assignments with respect to
\mathfrak{P}
.
The semantics of an IMC corresponds to an MDP that has the same state space as the IMC
and the action set
\mathfrak{G}({\mathfrak{P}})
,
and where selecting assignment
\alpha\in\mathfrak{G}({\mathfrak{P}(s,\cdot)})
from state
s

means that the target state of the transition is chosen probabilistically according to
\alpha
.
Formally, the semantics of an IMC
\mathfrak{C}=(S,\mathfrak{P})

is the MDP
{[\hskip-1.42271pt[\mathfrak{C}]\hskip-1.42271pt]}=(S,\mathfrak{G}({\mathfrak{P}}),\Delta)
,
where
(1)
\Delta(s,\alpha)=\alpha
for all
s\in S

and
\alpha\in\mathfrak{G}({\mathfrak{P}(s,\cdot)})
,
and (2)
\Delta(s,\alpha)=\bot
for all
s\in S

and
\alpha\in\mathfrak{G}({\mathfrak{P}})\setminus\mathfrak{G}({\mathfrak{P}(s,\cdot)})
.
In previous literature (for example,
[
SVA06
,
CSH08
,
CHK13
]
),
this semantics is called the “IMDP semantics”.

Computing
\mathbb{P}^{\mathrm{max}}_{{{[\hskip-1.42271pt[\mathfrak{C}]\hskip-1.42271pt]}},{s}}(\Diamond{T})

and
\mathbb{P}^{\mathrm{min}}_{{{[\hskip-1.42271pt[\mathfrak{C}]\hskip-1.42271pt]}},{s}}(\Diamond{T})

can be done for an IMC
\mathfrak{C}
simply by transforming the IMC by closing all of its (half-)open intervals,
then employing a standard maximum/minimum reachability probability computation on the new, “closed” IMC
(for example, the algorithms of
[
SVA06
,
CHK13
]
):
the correctness of this approach is shown in
[
CK15
]
.
Algorithms for qualitative problems of IMCs (with open, half-open and closed intervals)
are given in
[
Spr18
]
.
All of the aforementioned algorithms run in polynomial time in the size of the IMC,
which is obtained as the sum over all states
s,s^{\prime}\in S

2.3.
Interval Markov Decision Processes.

An (open)

In contrast to IMCs,

(1) an action

then (2) an assignment
\alpha

and finally (3) a probabilistic choice over target states to make the transition to is performed

\alpha

Formally, the semantics of an IMDP

where
A=\mathfrak{A}\times\mathsf{Dist}(S)

and where
(1)
\Delta(s,(\mathfrak{a},\alpha))=\alpha

for all
s\in S

\mathfrak{a}\in\mathfrak{A}({s})

and
\alpha\in\mathfrak{G}({\mathfrak{D}(s,\mathfrak{a})})

and
(2)
\Delta(s,(\mathfrak{a},\alpha))=\bot

for all
s\in S

for which either (a)
\mathfrak{a}\not\in\mathfrak{A}({s})

or (b)
\mathfrak{a}\in\mathfrak{A}({s})
and
\alpha\not\in\mathfrak{G}({\mathfrak{D}(s,\mathfrak{a})})
.
Note that

3.
Clock-Dependent Probabilistic Timed Automata with One Clock

In this section, we recall the formalism of clock-dependent probabilistic timed automata.
The definition of clock-dependent probabilistic timed automata of
[
Spr21
]

features an arbitrary number of clock variables.
In contrast,
we consider models with only
one
clock variable.
This clock variable will be denoted
x
for the remainder of the paper.

A
clock valuation
is a value
v\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
,
interpreted as the current value of clock
x
.
Following the usual notational conventions for modelling formalisms based on timed automata,
we use the powerset notation
2^{\{{x}\}}

to refer to the set
\{{\{{x}\},\emptyset}\}
,
which we will use in the sequel to indicate whether the clock is reset to
0

(denoted by
\{{x}\}
)
or retains its current value (denoted by
\emptyset
).

The set
\mathit{\Psi}
of
clock constraints
over
x
is defined
as the set of conjunctions over atomic formulae of the
form
x\sim c
,
where
\sim\in\{<,\leq,\geq,>\}
and
c\in\mathbb{N}
.
A clock valuation
v
satisfies a clock constraint
\psi
,
denoted by
v\models\psi
,
if
\psi
resolves to
true
when substituting each occurrence of clock
x
with
v
.

For a set
Q
,
a
distribution template

\wp:\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\rightarrow\mathsf{Dist}(Q)

gives a distribution over
Q
for each clock valuation.
In the following, we use notation
{\wp}[{v}]
, rather than
\wp(v)
,
to denote the distribution corresponding to distribution template
\wp
and clock valuation
v
.
Let
\mathsf{Temp}(Q)
be the set of distribution templates over
Q
.

A
one-clock clock-dependent probabilistic timed automaton
(1c-cdPTA)

\mathcal{P}=(L,\mathit{inv},\mathit{prob})

comprises the following components:

•

a finite set

L
of
locations
;

•

a function
\mathit{inv}:L\rightarrow\mathit{\Psi}
associating an
invariant condition
with each location;

•

a set
\mathit{prob}\subseteq L\times\mathit{\Psi}\times\mathsf{Temp}(2^{\{{x}\}}\times L)

of
probabilistic edges
.

A probabilistic edge
(l,g,\wp)\in\mathit{prob}
comprises:
(1) a source location
l
;
(2) a clock constraint
g
, called a
guard
; and
(3) a distribution template
\wp

with respect to pairs of the form
(X,l^{\prime})\in 2^{\{{x}\}}\times L

(i.e., pairs consisting of a first element indicating whether
x
should be reset to
0
or not,
and a second element corresponding to a target location
l^{\prime}
).
We refer to pairs
(X,l^{\prime})\in 2^{\{{x}\}}\times L
as
outcomes
.

The behaviour of a 1c-cdPTA takes a similar form to
that of a standard (one-clock) probabilistic timed automaton
[
GJ95
,
KNSS02
,
JLS08
]
.
A
state
of a 1c-cdPTA is a pair comprising a location and a clock valuation
satisfying the location’s invariant condition,
i.e.,
(l,v)\in L\times\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
such that
v\models\mathit{inv}(l)
.
In any state
(l,v)
, a certain amount of time
t\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
elapses,
then a probabilistic edge is traversed.
The choice of
t
is nondeterministic.
It is required that the invariant
\mathit{inv}(l)
remains satisfied continuously while time passes.
The resulting state after the elapse of time is
(l,v{+}t)
.
A probabilistic edge
(l^{\prime},g,\wp)\in\mathit{prob}
can then be chosen
from state
(l,v{+}t)

if
l=l^{\prime}
and it is
enabled
,
i.e., the clock constraint
g
is satisfied by
v{+}t
.
The choice of which enabled probabilistic edge to take is nondeterministic.
Once a probabilistic edge
(l^{\prime},g,\wp)
is chosen,
a successor location, and whether to reset the clock to
0
,
is chosen at random,
according to the distribution
{\wp}[{v{+}t}]
.
For example, in the case of the 1c-cdPTA of Figure
1
,
from state
(\mathrm{W},0)

(i.e., the location is
\mathrm{W}
and the value of clock
x
is equal to
0
),
a time delay
t\in(1,3)
elapses,
increasing the value of
x
to
t
,
before the probabilistic edge leaving
\mathrm{W}
is traversed.
Then the resulting state will be
(\mathrm{S},t)
with probability
\frac{3t-3}{8}
,

(\mathrm{T},t)
with probability
\frac{11t-3}{16}
,
and
(\mathrm{F},t)
with probability
\frac{11t-3}{16}
.

We make the following assumptions on 1c-cdPTAs,
in order to simplify the definition of their semantics.
Firstly, we consider 1c-cdPTAs featuring invariant conditions
that prevent the clock from exceeding some upper bound
and impose no lower bound:
formally, for each location
l\in L
,
we have that
\mathit{inv}(l)

is a constraint
x\leq c
for some
c\in\mathbb{N}
,
or a constraint
x<c
for some
c\in\mathbb{N}\setminus\{{0}\}
.
Secondly, we restrict our attention to 1c-cdPTAs for which it is always possible to take a probabilistic edge,
either immediately or after letting time elapse.
Formally, for each location
l\in L
,
if
\mathit{inv}(l)=(x\leq c)
then (viewing
c
as a clock valuation)
c\models g

for some
(l,g,\wp)\in\mathit{prob}
;
instead, if
\mathit{inv}(l)=(x<c)
then
c-\varepsilon\models g

for all
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
and
(l,g,\wp)\in\mathit{prob}
.
Thirdly, we assume that all possible target states of probabilistic edges satisfy their invariants.
Observe that,
given the first assumption, this may not be the case only when the clock is
not
reset.
Formally, for all probabilistic edges
(l,g,\wp)\in\mathit{prob}
,
for all clock valuations
v\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
such that
v\models g
,
and for all
l^{\prime}\in L
,
we have that
{\wp}[{v}](\emptyset,l^{\prime})>0
implies

v\models\mathit{inv}(l^{\prime})

(recall that the clock is not reset in the case of outcomes for which
\emptyset
is the first component).
Note that we relax some of these assumptions when depicting 1c-cdPTAs graphically
(for example, the 1c-cdPTA of Figure
1

can be modified so that it satisfies these assumptions by adding invariant conditions
and self-looping probabilistic edges to locations
\mathrm{S}
and
\mathrm{T}
).

The semantics of the 1c-cdPTA

\mathcal{P}=(L,\mathit{inv},\mathit{prob})

is the MDP

{[\hskip-1.42271pt[\mathcal{P}]\hskip-1.42271pt]}=(S,A,\Delta)

where:

•

S=\{(l,v)\in L\times\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\mid v\models\mathit{inv}(l)\}
;

•

A=\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\times\mathit{prob}
;

•

for
(l,v)\in S
,

\tilde{v}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
and
(l,g,\wp)\in\mathit{prob}
such that
(1)
\tilde{v}\geq v
, (2)
\tilde{v}\models g

and (3)
w\models\mathit{inv}(l)
for all
v\leq w\leq\tilde{v}
,
then we let
\Delta((l,v),(\tilde{v},(l,g,\wp)))
be the distribution
such that, for
(l^{\prime},v^{\prime})\in S
:

\Delta((l,v),(\tilde{v},(l,g,\wp)))(l^{\prime},v^{\prime})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}{\wp}[{\tilde{v}}](\{{x}\},l^{\prime})+{\wp}[{\tilde{v}}](\emptyset,l^{\prime})&\mbox{if }v^{\prime}=\tilde{v}=0\\
{\wp}[{\tilde{v}}](\emptyset,l^{\prime})&\mbox{if }v^{\prime}=\tilde{v}>0\\
{\wp}[{\tilde{v}}](\{{x}\},l^{\prime})&\mbox{if }v^{\prime}=0\mbox{ and }\tilde{v}>0\\
0&\mbox{otherwise.}\end{array}\right.

Note that the summation in the first case of the definition of
\Delta

is performed because the valuation
v^{\prime}=0
may be obtained from
\tilde{v}=0

either by resetting the clock to
0
(summand
{\wp}[{\tilde{v}}](\{{x}\},l^{\prime})
)
or by not resetting the clock to
0
(summand
{\wp}[{\tilde{v}}](\emptyset,l^{\prime})
).

Let
F\subseteq L
be a set of locations,
and let
T_{F}=\{{(l,v)\in S\mid l\in F}\}

be the set of states of
{[\hskip-1.42271pt[\mathcal{P}]\hskip-1.42271pt]}

that have their location component in
F
.
Then the maximum value of reaching
F
from state
(l,v)\in S

corresponds to
\mathbb{P}^{\mathrm{max}}_{{{[\hskip-1.42271pt[\mathcal{P}]\hskip-1.42271pt]}},{(l,v)}}(\Diamond{T_{F}})
.
Similarly, the minimum value of reaching
F
from state
(l,v)\in S

corresponds to
\mathbb{P}^{\mathrm{min}}_{{{[\hskip-1.42271pt[\mathcal{P}]\hskip-1.42271pt]}},{(l,v)}}(\Diamond{T_{F}})
.
As in Section
2
, we can define a number of quantitative and qualitative reachability problems on 1c-cdPTA,
where the initial state is
(l,0)
for a particular
l\in L
.
The maximal reachability problem for
\mathcal{P}
,
F\subseteq L
,

l\in L
,
\unrhd\in\{\geq,>\}
and
\lambda\in[0,1]

is to decide whether
\mathbb{P}^{\mathrm{max}}_{{{[\hskip-1.42271pt[\mathcal{P}]\hskip-1.42271pt]}},{(l,0)}}(\Diamond{T_{F}})\unrhd\lambda
;
similarly, the minimal reachability problem for
\mathcal{P}
,
F\subseteq L
,

l\in L
,
\unlhd\in\{\leq,<\}
and
\lambda\in[0,1]

is to decide whether
\mathbb{P}^{\mathrm{min}}_{{{[\hskip-1.42271pt[\mathcal{P}]\hskip-1.42271pt]}},{(l,0)}}(\Diamond{T_{F}})\unlhd\lambda
.
Furthermore, we can define analogues of the qualitative problems
featured in Section
2
:
(
\forall 0
)
decide whether

\mathrm{Pr}^{\sigma}_{(l,0)}(\Diamond T_{F})=0
for all
\sigma\in\Sigma^{{[\hskip-1.42271pt[\mathcal{P}]\hskip-1.42271pt]}}
;
(
\exists 0
)
decide whether there exists
\sigma\in\Sigma^{{[\hskip-1.42271pt[\mathcal{P}]\hskip-1.42271pt]}}

such that
\mathrm{Pr}^{\sigma}_{(l,0)}(\Diamond T_{F})=0
;
(
\exists 1
)
decide whether there exists
\sigma\in\Sigma^{{[\hskip-1.42271pt[\mathcal{P}]\hskip-1.42271pt]}}

such that
\mathrm{Pr}^{\sigma}_{(l,0)}(\Diamond T_{F})=1
;
(
\forall 1
)
decide whether
\mathrm{Pr}^{\sigma}_{(l,0)}(\Diamond T_{F})=1
for all
\sigma\in\Sigma^{{[\hskip-1.42271pt[\mathcal{P}]\hskip-1.42271pt]}}
.

3.1.
Affine Clock Dependencies.

In this paper, we consider distribution templates
that are defined in terms
of sets of affine functions in the following way.
Given probabilistic edge
p=(l,g,\wp)\in\mathit{prob}
,
let
I^{p}
be the set of clock valuations in which
p
is enabled,
i.e.,
I^{p}=\{{v\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\mid v\models g\wedge\mathit{inv}(l)}\}
.
Note that
I^{p}\subseteq\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
corresponds to an interval
with natural-numbered endpoints.
Let
\overline{I^{p}}
be the closure of
I^{p}
.
We say that
p
is
affine
if,
for each
e\in 2^{\{{x}\}}\times L
,
there exists a pair
(c^{p}_{e},d^{p}_{e})\in\mathbb{Q}^{2}
of rational constants,
such that

{\wp}[{v}](e)=c^{p}_{e}+d^{p}_{e}\cdot v

for all
v\in\overline{I^{p}}
.
Note that, by the definition of distribution templates,
for all
v\in\overline{I^{p}}
,
we have
c^{p}_{e}+d^{p}_{e}\cdot v\geq 0

for each
e\in 2^{\{{x}\}}\times L
, and

\sum_{e\in 2^{\{{x}\}}\times L}(c^{p}_{e}+d^{p}_{e}\cdot v)=1
.
A 1c-cdPTA is affine if all of its probabilistic edges are affine.
Henceforth we assume that the 1c-cdPTAs we consider are affine.
An affine probabilistic edge
p
is
constant
if, for each
e\in 2^{\{{x}\}}\times L
,
we have
d^{p}_{e}=0
,
i.e.,
{\wp}[{v}](e)=c^{p}_{e}
for some
c^{p}_{e}\in\mathbb{Q}
,
for all
v\in\overline{I^{p}}
.
The following technical fact will be useful in subsequent sections:
for a probabilistic edge
p\in\mathit{prob}
,
outcome
e\in 2^{\{{x}\}}\times L

and open interval
I\subseteq I^{p}
,
if
d^{p}_{e}\neq 0
,
then
{\wp}[{v}](e)>0
for all
v\in I

(because the existence of
v_{=0}\in I
such that

{\wp}[{v_{=0}}](e)=0
,
together with
d^{p}_{e}\neq 0
and the fact that
I
is open,
would mean that there exists
v^{\prime}\in I
such that

{\wp}[{v^{\prime}}](e)<0
,
which contradicts the definition of distribution templates).

3.2.
Initialisation.

In this paper, we also introduce a specific requirement for 1c-cdPTAs
that allows us to analyse faithfully 1c-cdPTA using IMDPs
in Section
4
.
A
symbolic path fragment
is a sequence

(l_{0},g_{0},\wp_{0})(X_{0},l_{1})(l_{1},g_{1},\wp_{1})(X_{1},l_{2})\cdots(l_{n},g_{n},\wp_{n})\in{(\mathit{prob}\times(2^{\{{x}\}}\times L))}^{+}\times\mathit{prob}

of probabilistic edges and outcomes
such that

{\wp_{i}}[{v}](X_{i},l_{i+1})>0
for all
v\in I^{(l_{i},g_{i},\wp_{i})}{}

for all
i<n
.
In this paper, we consider 1c-cdPTAs for which each symbolic path fragment
that begins and ends with non-constant probabilistic edges
requires that the clock takes a natural numbered value
at some point along the path fragment,
either from being reset or from passing through guards that have at most one (natural numbered) value in common.
Formally, a 1c-cdPTA is
initialised
if,
for any symbolic path fragment

4.1.
\mathbb{B}
-minimal Schedulers.

The following technical lemma specifies that any scheduler of the 1c-cdPTA
can be made “more deterministic” in the following way:
for each interval
\tilde{B}\in\mathcal{I}_{\mathbb{B}}
and probabilistic edge
p\in\mathit{prob}
,
if, after executing a certain finite path,
a scheduler chooses (assigns positive probability to) multiple actions

(\tilde{v}_{1},p),\cdots,(\tilde{v}_{n},p)

that share the same probabilistic edge
p

and for which
\tilde{v}_{i}\in\tilde{B}

for all
1\leq i\leq n
,
then we can construct another scheduler for which the aforementioned actions are replaced by
a
single
action
(\tilde{v},p)
such that
\tilde{v}\in\tilde{B}
.
Formally, we say that a scheduler
\sigma\in\Sigma^{{[\hskip-1.42271pt[\mathcal{P}]\hskip-1.42271pt]}}
of
{[\hskip-1.42271pt[\mathcal{P}]\hskip-1.42271pt]}

is
\mathbb{B}
-minimal
if,
for all finite paths
r\in\mathit{Paths}^{{[\hskip-1.42271pt[\mathcal{P}]\hskip-1.42271pt]}}_{*}
,
for all probabilistic edges
p\in\mathit{prob}
,
and for all pairs of actions

(\tilde{v}_{1},p_{1}),(\tilde{v}_{2},p_{2})\in\mathsf{support}(\sigma(r))
,
either
p_{1}\neq p_{2}
or
\tilde{v}_{1}
and
\tilde{v}_{2}

belong to distinct intervals in
\mathcal{I}_{\mathbb{B}}
,
i.e., the intervals
\tilde{B}_{1},\tilde{B}_{2}\in\mathcal{I}_{\mathbb{B}}

for which
\tilde{v}_{1}\in\tilde{B}_{1}

and
\tilde{v}_{2}\in\tilde{B}_{2}

are such that
\tilde{B}_{1}\neq\tilde{B}_{2}
.
Let
\Sigma^{{[\hskip-1.42271pt[\mathcal{P}]\hskip-1.42271pt]}}_{\mathbb{B}}
be
the set of schedulers of
\Sigma^{{[\hskip-1.42271pt[\mathcal{P}]\hskip-1.42271pt]}}
that are
\mathbb{B}
-minimal.
The lemma allows us to consider only
\mathbb{B}
-minimal schedulers in the sequel,
permitting us to obtain a close correspondence between the schedulers of
{[\hskip-1.42271pt[\mathcal{P}]\hskip-1.42271pt]}

and the schedulers of the IMDP that we describe how to construct in the next subsection.

Lemma 4
.

Let
(l,v)\in S_{\mathcal{P}}
and
F\subseteq L
.
Then, for each
\sigma\in\Sigma^{{[\hskip-1.42271pt[\mathcal{P}]\hskip-1.42271pt]}}
,
there exists
\pi\in\Sigma^{{[\hskip-1.42271pt[\mathcal{P}]\hskip-1.42271pt]}}_{\mathbb{B}}

such that

\mathrm{Pr}^{\sigma}_{(l,v)}(\Diamond T_{F})=\mathrm{Pr}^{\pi}_{(l,v)}(\Diamond T_{F})
.

Before presenting the proof of Lemma
4
,
we introduce the general approach for the construction
of the scheduler
\pi\in\Sigma^{{[\hskip-1.42271pt[\mathcal{P}]\hskip-1.42271pt]}}_{\mathbb{B}}

from
\sigma\in\Sigma^{{[\hskip-1.42271pt[\mathcal{P}]\hskip-1.42271pt]}}
.
Each finite path of
\pi
will be associated with
a number of finite paths of
\sigma
;
then a choice of
\pi
after a finite path
r

is based on a weighted average of the corresponding choices
made by
\sigma
after the finite paths associated with
r
.
The association between finite paths of
\sigma
and finite paths of
\pi
is defined
on the basis of the locations that are visited,
the intervals from
\mathcal{I}_{\mathbb{B}}
that the value of the clock passes through,
and the probabilistic edges that are taken
along those finite paths.
To define the choice of transition of
\pi
after a finite path
r
,
we consider each pair
(\tilde{B},p)\in\mathcal{I}_{\mathbb{B}}\times\mathit{prob}
,
and, if
\sigma
assigns positive probability to at least one transition
(\tilde{v},p)

such that
\tilde{v}\in\tilde{B}
,
we define a unique transition
(\tilde{v}^{\prime},p)

that is assigned positive probability by
\pi

and for which
\tilde{v}^{\prime}\in\tilde{B}
.
There are two principal cases for the transitions assigned positive probability by
\pi
:
if
\tilde{B}
is a closed interval,
then there is only one possible choice for
\tilde{v}^{\prime}

(i.e., if
\tilde{B}=[b,b]
then
\tilde{v}^{\prime}=b
);
instead, if
\tilde{B}
is an open interval,
the choice of clock valuation
\tilde{v}^{\prime}\in\tilde{B}

used in the definition of the
\pi
is substantially more complicated.
While the scheduler
\pi
chooses transitions
for which the value of the clock remains within
\tilde{B}
,
the scheduler proceeds between three phases,
where the number of transitions in the first and third phases is arbitrary,
and where the second phase consists of at most one transition.
The first phase consists of transitions derived from constant probabilistic edges,
where the value of the clock is kept “low”
(equal to the minimum of the value of the clock at the end of the corresponding finite paths of
\sigma
).
The second phase consists of at most one transition derived from a non-constant probabilistic edge,
where the value of the clock is chosen
as a weighted average of the corresponding choices
made by
\sigma
,
so as to replicate exactly the probability of taking the same
probabilistic edge by
\sigma
.
The third phase consists of transitions derived from constant probabilistic edges,
and the value of the clock can be chosen in an arbitrary manner.
Note that the assumption of initialisation guarantees that we cannot have
more than one non-constant probabilistic edge between points at which the clock is equal to a value from
\mathbb{B}
,
and hence at most one non-constant probabilistic edge can be taken while the value of the clock
remains continuously in the open interval
\tilde{B}
.

{exa}

Consider the 1c-cdPTA of Figure

Hence we let

Now consider the subcase in which

is an open interval,
and all probabilistic edges in the sequence

are constant, but

is non-constant,

is similar to that of the first phase,

of the choice of clock valuations made by

Formally, let:

\displaystyle\tilde{v}^{\mathrm{*}}_{{\rho},({\tilde{B}},{p})}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\sum_{r^{\prime}\in\Lambda^{\sigma}({\rho})}\mathrm{Pr}^{\sigma}_{*,(l,v)}(r^{\prime})\cdot\sum_{\tilde{v}\in\Xi_{{\rho},({\tilde{B}},{p})}}\sigma(r^{\prime})(\tilde{v},p)\cdot\tilde{v}}{\sum_{r^{\prime}\in\Lambda^{\sigma}({\rho})}\mathrm{Pr}^{\sigma}_{*,(l,v)}(r^{\prime})\cdot\sum_{\tilde{v}\in\Xi_{{\rho},({\tilde{B}},{p})}}\sigma(r^{\prime})(\tilde{v},p)}\;.

We will see later in this proof that the use of the weighted average

Finally, we consider the subcase in which
\tilde{B}
is an open interval,

is non-constant,

\mathit{last}(r)

\tilde{B}\in\mathcal{I}_{\mathbb{B}}

\tilde{B}\geq B_{i}
,

\tilde{B}\in\mathcal{I}_{\mathbb{B}}

We now show that

\displaystyle\sum_{(\tilde{B},p)\in\Upsilon}\pi(r)(\tilde{v}^{\mathrm{*}}_{{\rho},({\tilde{B}},{p})},p)

\displaystyle\sum_{(\tilde{B},p)\in\Upsilon}\left(\frac{\sum_{r^{\prime}\in\Lambda^{\sigma}({\rho})}\mathrm{Pr}^{\sigma}_{*,(l,v)}(r^{\prime})\cdot\sum_{\tilde{v}\in\Xi_{{\rho},({\tilde{B}},{p})}}\sigma(r^{\prime})(\tilde{v},p)}{\mathrm{Pr}^{\sigma}_{*,(l,v)}(\Lambda^{\sigma}({\rho}))}\right)

\displaystyle 1\;.

We now proceed to show that

\mathrm{Pr}^{\sigma}_{(l,v)}(\Diamond T_{F})=\mathrm{Pr}^{\pi}_{(l,v)}(\Diamond T_{F})

\displaystyle=

4.2.
IMDP Construction.

We now present the idea of the IMDP construction.
The states of the IMDP fall into two categories:
(1) pairs comprising a location and an interval from
\mathcal{I}_{\mathbb{B}}
,
with the intuition that the state
(l,B)\in L\times\mathcal{I}_{\mathbb{B}}
of the IMDP
represents all states
(l,v)
of

such that

;
(2) triples comprising an interval from
\mathcal{I}_{\mathbb{B}}
,
a probabilistic edge and a bit that specifies whether the state refers to the left- or right-endpoint of the interval.
A single transition of the semantics of the 1c-cdPTA,
which we recall represents the elapse of time (therefore increasing the value of the clock)
followed by the traversal of a probabilistic edge,
is represented by a sequence of
two
transitions in the IMDP.
The first IMDP transition in the sequence represents the choice of
(i) the probabilistic edge,
(ii) the interval in
\mathcal{I}_{\mathbb{B}}
which contains the valuation of the clock
after letting time elapse and
immediately before the probabilistic edge is traversed,
and (iii) in the case in which the aforementioned interval is open,
an endpoint of the interval chosen in (ii).
The second IMDP transition in the sequence represents the probabilistic choice made
according to the chosen probabilistic edge, interval and endpoint chosen in the first transition of the sequence.

\displaystyle\{{(l,B)\in L\times\mathcal{I}_{\mathbb{B}}\mid B\models\mathit{inv}(l)}\}

\mathfrak{D}_{\mathfrak{M}[{\mathcal{P}}]}((\tilde{B},(l,g,\wp),\mathsf{ep}),\tau)(s)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}[\lambda^{(\tilde{B},(l,g,\wp),\mathsf{ep})}_{s},\lambda^{(\tilde{B},(l,g,\wp),\mathsf{ep})}_{s}]&\mbox{if }s\in S_{\mathfrak{M}[{\mathcal{P}}]}^{\mathsf{reg}}\\
\,\![0,0]&\mbox{otherwise.}\end{array}\right.

We recall that
\mathfrak{D}_{\mathfrak{M}[{\mathcal{P}}]}(s,\mathfrak{a})=\bot

for

and
\mathfrak{a}\in\mathfrak{A}_{\mathfrak{M}[{\mathcal{P}}]}\setminus\mathfrak{A}_{\mathfrak{M}[{\mathcal{P}}]}({s})

4.3.
Correctness of the IMDP construction.

Next, we establish the correctness of the construction of
\mathfrak{M}[{\mathcal{P}}]
,
i.e., that
\mathfrak{M}[{\mathcal{P}}]
can be used for solving quantitative and qualitative problems of the 1c-cdPTA
\mathcal{P}
.
The proof relies on showing that a transition of the semantic MDP
{[\hskip-1.42271pt[\mathcal{P}]\hskip-1.42271pt]}
of
\mathcal{P}

can be mimicked by a sequence of
two
transitions
of the semantic MDP
{[\hskip-1.42271pt[\mathfrak{M}[{\mathcal{P}}]]\hskip-1.42271pt]}
of
\mathfrak{M}[{\mathcal{P}}]
,
and vice versa.
Let
{[\hskip-1.42271pt[\mathcal{P}]\hskip-1.42271pt]}=(S_{\mathcal{P}},A_{\mathcal{P}},\Delta_{\mathcal{P}})

be the semantic MDP of
\mathcal{P}
.
Given state
(l,v)\in S_{\mathcal{P}}
,
we let
\mathsf{reg}({l,v})=(l,B)\in S_{\mathfrak{M}[{\mathcal{P}}]}^{\mathsf{reg}}

be the unique region such that

.
In the following, we let

{[\hskip-1.42271pt[\mathfrak{M}[{\mathcal{P}}]]\hskip-1.42271pt]}=(S_{\mathfrak{M}[{\mathcal{P}}]},A_{\mathfrak{M}[{\mathcal{P}}]},\Delta_{\mathfrak{M}[{\mathcal{P}}]})

be the semantic MDP of
\mathfrak{M}[{\mathcal{P}}]
.

We now show that,
for any scheduler of (the semantics of) the 1c-cdPTA
\mathcal{P}
,
there exists a scheduler of (the semantics of) the IMDP
\mathfrak{M}[{\mathcal{P}}]

such that the schedulers assign the same probability
to reaching a certain set of locations
from a given location with the value of the clock equal to
0
.
Let
\mathfrak{T}_{F}=\{{(l,B)\in S_{\mathfrak{M}[{\mathcal{P}}]}^{\mathsf{reg}}\mid l\in F}\}

be the set of regions with location component in
F
.

Let
l\in L

Proof 4.2

(Proof of Lemma
5
.)
.

Let
l\in L
,

F\subseteq L

and
\pi\in\Sigma^{{[\hskip-1.42271pt[\mathcal{P}]\hskip-1.42271pt]}}_{\mathbb{B}}
.
We proceed by describing the construction of scheduler
\hat{\pi}\in\Sigma^{{[\hskip-1.42271pt[\mathfrak{M}[{\mathcal{P}}]]\hskip-1.42271pt]}}
,
then show that
\mathrm{Pr}^{\pi}_{(l,0)}(\Diamond T_{F})=\mathrm{Pr}^{\hat{\pi}}_{\mathsf{reg}({l,0})}(\Diamond\mathfrak{T}_{F})
.

Before describing the construction of
\hat{\pi}\in\Sigma^{{[\hskip-1.42271pt[\mathfrak{M}[{\mathcal{P}}]]\hskip-1.42271pt]}}
,
we first simplify the notation for transitions along paths of
{[\hskip-1.42271pt[\mathfrak{M}[{\mathcal{P}}]]\hskip-1.42271pt]}

to remove redundant elements,
in the following way:
a sequence

(l,B)((\tilde{B},p),\alpha)(\tilde{B},p,\mathsf{ep})(\tau,\beta)(l^{\prime},B^{\prime})

of two transitions between regions
will be simplified to

(l,B)((\tilde{B},p),\alpha)(\mathsf{ep})(l^{\prime},B^{\prime})

(note that, for the endpoint indicator
(\tilde{B},p,\mathsf{ep})
,
the interval
\tilde{B}
and the probabilistic edge
p

have been featured in the previous position in the sequence,
and that, from
(\tilde{B},p,\mathsf{ep})
,
only one action is available, namely
(\tau,\beta)
,
and hence we can omit it from the sequence).

Recall that, from the proof of Lemma
4
,
each finite path
r\in\mathit{Paths}^{\pi}_{*}
of
\pi
corresponds to a
\mathbb{B}
-path.
Furthermore, for a given
\mathbb{B}
-path, we can identify a set of finite paths of
{[\hskip-1.42271pt[\mathfrak{M}[{\mathcal{P}}]]\hskip-1.42271pt]}

that agree with the
\mathbb{B}
-path in terms of locations, intervals, and probabilistic edges.
Formally, for a
\mathbb{B}
-path
\rho
which equals

(l_{0},B_{0})(\tilde{B}_{0},p_{0})(l_{1},B_{1})(\tilde{B}_{1},p_{1})\cdots(\tilde{B}_{n-1},p_{n-1})(l_{n},B_{n})

and a finite path
r
of
\mathfrak{M}[{\mathcal{P}}]
, where
r
equals

(l_{0}^{\prime},B_{0}^{\prime})((\tilde{B}_{0}^{\prime},p_{0}^{\prime}),\alpha_{0})(\mathsf{ep}_{0})(l_{1}^{\prime},B_{1}^{\prime})((\tilde{B}_{1}^{\prime},p_{1}^{\prime}),\alpha_{1})(\mathsf{ep}_{1})\linebreak\cdots\linebreak((\tilde{B}_{m-1}^{\prime},p_{m-1}^{\prime}),\alpha_{m-1})\linebreak(\mathsf{ep}_{m-1})\linebreak(l_{m}^{\prime},B_{m}^{\prime}),

we say that

corresponds to

\rho

if (1)

,
(2)

,

,
and (3)
\tilde{B}_{i}=\tilde{B}_{i}^{\prime}
and

for all

.
Given a scheduler
\hat{\pi}\in\Sigma^{{[\hskip-1.42271pt[\mathfrak{M}[{\mathcal{P}}]]\hskip-1.42271pt]}}

and a
\mathbb{B}

\rho
,
we let
\Lambda^{\hat{\pi}}({\rho})\subseteq\mathit{Paths}^{\hat{\pi}}_{*}

be the set of finite paths of
\hat{\pi}
that correspond to

.

Recall that
\mathfrak{M}[{\mathcal{P}}]
alternates between regions (elements of
S_{\mathfrak{M}[{\mathcal{P}}]}^{\mathsf{reg}}
)
and endpoint indicators (elements of
S_{\mathfrak{M}[{\mathcal{P}}]}^{\mathsf{end}}
).
We partition
\mathit{Paths}^{{[\hskip-1.42271pt[\mathfrak{M}[{\mathcal{P}}]]\hskip-1.42271pt]}}_{*}(\mathsf{reg}({l,0}))

into the set
\mathit{Paths}^{{[\hskip-1.42271pt[\mathfrak{M}[{\mathcal{P}}]]\hskip-1.42271pt]}}_{*,{S_{\mathfrak{M}[{\mathcal{P}}]}^{\mathsf{reg}}}}(\mathsf{reg}({l,0}))

of finite paths of the form

(l_{0},B_{0})((\tilde{B}_{0},p_{0}),\alpha_{0})(\mathsf{ep}_{0})(l_{1},B_{1})((\tilde{B}_{1},p_{1}),\alpha_{1})(\mathsf{ep}_{1})\cdots\linebreak((\tilde{B}_{n-1},p_{n-1}),\alpha_{n-1})\linebreak(\mathsf{ep}_{n-1})\linebreak(l_{n},B_{n})

that end with a region,
and the set
\mathit{Paths}^{{[\hskip-1.42271pt[\mathfrak{M}[{\mathcal{P}}]]\hskip-1.42271pt]}}_{*,{S_{\mathfrak{M}[{\mathcal{P}}]}^{\mathsf{end}}}}(\mathsf{reg}({l,0}))

of finite paths of the form

(l_{0},B_{0})((\tilde{B}_{0},p_{0}),\alpha_{0})(\mathsf{ep}_{0})(l_{1},B_{1})((\tilde{B}_{1},p_{1}),\alpha_{1})(\mathsf{ep}_{1})\cdots((\tilde{B}_{m},p_{m}),\alpha_{m})(\mathsf{ep}_{m})

The construction of
\hat{\pi}
is based on the following principles:
for any two finite paths that correspond to the same

-path,
the choice made by
\hat{\pi}
after those finite paths will be the same;
furthermore, for any action of
\mathfrak{M}[{\mathcal{P}}]
of the form
(\tilde{B},p)
,
the scheduler
\hat{\pi}
assigns positive probability to at most one action of
{[\hskip-1.42271pt[\mathfrak{M}[{\mathcal{P}}]]\hskip-1.42271pt]}

of the form

,
where
\alpha
is used to represent the actual clock valuation chosen by
\pi

within the interval
\tilde{B}
.

We now describe the formal construction of
\hat{\pi}
.
As elsewhere in this paper, we proceed by induction on the length of paths.
Let
r\in\mathit{Paths}^{{[\hskip-1.42271pt[\mathfrak{M}[{\mathcal{P}}]]\hskip-1.42271pt]}}_{*,{S_{\mathfrak{M}[{\mathcal{P}}]}^{\mathsf{reg}}}}(\mathsf{reg}({l,0}))

be a finite path of
\mathfrak{M}[{\mathcal{P}}]
that ends in a region,
and assume that we have already defined the choices of
\hat{\pi}
along all of the prefixes of
r
.
Let
\rho
be the unique
\mathbb{B}
-path to which
r
corresponds
(it is obtained by simply removing the components denoted by
\alpha
and
\mathsf{ep})
.
If
\Lambda^{\pi}({\rho})=\emptyset
,
i.e., there is no path of
\pi
that corresponds to
\rho
,
then the choice of
\hat{\pi}
after
r
can be made in an arbitrary manner.
Otherwise,
letting
r^{\prime}\in\mathit{Paths}^{\pi}_{*}
be the unique path of
\pi
that corresponds to
\rho
,
we aim to mimic each choice of
(\tilde{v},p)\in\mathsf{support}(\pi(r^{\prime}))

in the construction of
\hat{\pi}(r)
.
Consider
(\tilde{v},p)\in\mathsf{support}(\pi(r^{\prime}))
.
Let
\tilde{B}\in\mathcal{I}_{\mathbb{B}}
be the unique interval
for which

By definition,

Then we let
\hat{\pi}(r)((\tilde{B},p),\alpha)=\pi(r^{\prime})(\tilde{v},p)

where

let
\alpha(\tilde{B},p,\mathsf{le})=\frac{\mathsf{re}(\tilde{B})-\tilde{v}}{\mathsf{re}(\tilde{B})-\mathsf{le}(\tilde{B})}

and let
\alpha(\tilde{B},p,\mathsf{re})=1-\alpha(\tilde{B},p,\mathsf{le})=\frac{\tilde{v}-\mathsf{le}(\tilde{B})}{\mathsf{re}(\tilde{B})-\mathsf{le}(\tilde{B})}

That is,
\alpha
represents the position of the clock valuation
\tilde{v}

within the interval
\tilde{B}
.
The fact that this definition of

is adequate for our purposes is due to the fact that

as we will see later in the proof.
This completes the construction of
\hat{\pi}
,
because only the action
\tau
is available in the final states of finite paths in

\mathit{Paths}^{{[\hskip-1.42271pt[\mathfrak{M}[{\mathcal{P}}]]\hskip-1.42271pt]}}_{*,{S_{\mathfrak{M}[{\mathcal{P}}]}^{\mathsf{end}}}}(\mathsf{reg}({l,0}))

and because

can be defined in an arbitrary manner for paths not starting in

Next we show that

\mathrm{Pr}^{\pi}_{(l,0)}(\Diamond T_{F})=\mathrm{Pr}^{\hat{\pi}}_{\mathsf{reg}({l,0})}(\Diamond\mathfrak{T}_{F})
.
Our approach is to show that

\mathrm{Pr}^{\pi}_{(l,0)}(\Lambda^{\pi}({\rho}))=\mathrm{Pr}^{\hat{\pi}}_{\mathsf{reg}({l,0})}(\Lambda^{\hat{\pi}}({\rho}))

for all
\mathbb{B}
-paths
\rho

(this is sufficient to show

\mathrm{Pr}^{\pi}_{(l,0)}(\Diamond T_{F})=\mathrm{Pr}^{\hat{\pi}}_{\mathsf{reg}({l,0})}(\Diamond\mathfrak{T}_{F})

because the path in
\Lambda^{\pi}({\rho})
,
if it exists,
reaches a location in
F

if and only if all paths in
\Lambda^{\hat{\pi}}({\rho})
reach a location in
F
).
We proceed by induction on the length of
\mathbb{B}
-paths.
Consider the
\mathbb{B}
-path
\rho(\tilde{B},p)(l^{\prime},B^{\prime})
,
and assume that it has already been established that

\mathrm{Pr}^{\pi}_{(l,0)}(\Lambda^{\pi}({\rho}))=\mathrm{Pr}^{\hat{\pi}}_{\mathsf{reg}({l,0})}(\Lambda^{\hat{\pi}}({\rho}))
.
Our aim is now to show that

\mathrm{Pr}^{\pi}_{(l,0)}(\Lambda^{\pi}({\rho(\tilde{B},p)(l^{\prime},B^{\prime})}))=\mathrm{Pr}^{\hat{\pi}}_{\mathsf{reg}({l,0})}(\Lambda^{\hat{\pi}}({\rho(\tilde{B},p)(l^{\prime},B^{\prime})}))

As in the construction of
\hat{\pi}
,
also in the following we denote
\Lambda^{\pi}({\rho})
by

.
Given that
\pi

\mathbb{B}
-minimal scheduler,
there is at most
one clock valuation
\tilde{v}\in\tilde{B}

(\tilde{v},p)\in\mathsf{support}(\sigma(r^{\prime}))

For the case in which

\mathrm{Pr}^{\hat{\pi}}_{\mathsf{reg}({l,0})}(\Lambda^{\hat{\pi}}({\rho(\tilde{B},p)(l^{\prime},B^{\prime})}))=0

In the remainder of this proof,
we consider the case in which there exists
\tilde{v}\in\tilde{B}
such that

(\tilde{v},p)\in\mathsf{support}(\pi(r^{\prime}))

As in the proof of Lemma

we let

We also concentrate on the case in which

is open,

is non-constant,
and

,
which corresponds to the outcome
(\emptyset,l^{\prime})

(i.e., the clock is not reset),
with the other cases being similar.
We write
c
and

rather than

c^{p}_{(\emptyset,l^{\prime})}
and

, respectively.
Let
\alpha\in\mathsf{Dist}(S_{\mathfrak{M}[{\mathcal{P}}]}^{\mathsf{end}})
be the unique assignment such that

((\tilde{B},p),\alpha)\in\mathsf{support}(\hat{\pi}(r))

for all

(such an assignment will be unique
from the construction of
\hat{\pi}
).
Then we write

rather than

for

First we observe the following (where the second step is obtained from

\tilde{v}-\mathsf{le}(\tilde{B})=\alpha(\mathsf{re})({\mathsf{re}(\tilde{B})-\mathsf{le}(\tilde{B})})

and the fourth and fifth steps also use the fact that

\displaystyle c+d\cdot\tilde{v}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle c+d\cdot(\mathsf{le}(\tilde{B})+\tilde{v}-\mathsf{le}(\tilde{B}))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle c+d\cdot(\mathsf{le}(\tilde{B})+\alpha(\mathsf{re})(\mathsf{re}(\tilde{B})-\mathsf{le}(\tilde{B})))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle c+d\cdot(\mathsf{le}(\tilde{B})+\alpha(\mathsf{re})\cdot\mathsf{re}(\tilde{B})-\alpha(\mathsf{re})\cdot\mathsf{le}(\tilde{B}))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle c+d\cdot(\mathsf{le}(\tilde{B})+\alpha(\mathsf{re})\cdot\mathsf{re}(\tilde{B})-\mathsf{le}(\tilde{B})+\alpha(\mathsf{le})\cdot\mathsf{le}(\tilde{B}))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\alpha(\mathsf{le})+\alpha(\mathsf{re}))\cdot c+d\cdot\alpha(\mathsf{re})\cdot\mathsf{re}(\tilde{B})+d\cdot\alpha(\mathsf{le})\cdot\mathsf{le}(\tilde{B})

\displaystyle\alpha(\mathsf{le})(c+d\cdot\mathsf{le}(\tilde{B}))+\alpha(\mathsf{re})(c+d\cdot\mathsf{re}(\tilde{B}))\;.

The fact that
c+d\cdot\tilde{v}=\alpha(\mathsf{le})(c+d\cdot\mathsf{le}(\tilde{B}))+\alpha(\mathsf{re})(c+d\cdot\mathsf{re}(\tilde{B}))

is then used in the following derivation
(where

\begin{array}[]{rcl}&&\mathrm{Pr}^{\pi}_{(l,0)}(\Lambda^{\pi}({\rho(\tilde{B},p)(l^{\prime},B^{\prime})}))\\
&=&\mathrm{Pr}^{\pi}_{(l,0)}(\Lambda^{\pi}({\rho}))\cdot\pi(r^{\prime})(\tilde{v},p)\cdot\Delta_{\mathcal{P}}(\mathit{last}(r^{\prime}),(\tilde{v},p))(l^{\prime},B^{\prime})\\
&=&\mathrm{Pr}^{\pi}_{(l,0)}(\Lambda^{\pi}({\rho}))\cdot\pi(r^{\prime})(\tilde{v},p)\cdot{\wp}[{\tilde{v}}](\emptyset,l^{\prime})\\
&=&\mathrm{Pr}^{\pi}_{(l,0)}(\Lambda^{\pi}({\rho}))\cdot\pi(r^{\prime})(\tilde{v},p)\cdot(c+d\cdot\tilde{v})\\
&=&\mathrm{Pr}^{\pi}_{(l,0)}(\Lambda^{\pi}({\rho}))\cdot\pi(r^{\prime})(\tilde{v},p)\cdot\left(\alpha(\mathsf{le})(c+d\cdot\mathsf{le}(\tilde{B}))+\alpha(\mathsf{re})(c+d\cdot\mathsf{re}(\tilde{B}))\right)\\
&=&\mathrm{Pr}^{\pi}_{(l,0)}(\Lambda^{\pi}({\rho}))\cdot\pi(r^{\prime})(\tilde{v},p)\cdot\left(\alpha(\mathsf{le})\cdot{\wp}[{\mathsf{le}(\tilde{B})}](\emptyset,l^{\prime})+\alpha(\mathsf{re})\cdot{\wp}[{\mathsf{re}(\tilde{B})}](\emptyset,l^{\prime})\right)\;.\end{array}

Note that the combination of the definition of
\alpha

and the fact that clock dependencies are affine allows us to obtain the last three steps above.
For the subsequent steps, we require the following notation.
Recall that, for any given

the interval distribution

Hence, there is only one action associated with

in

which we denote as

where
\alpha^{\tilde{B},p,\mathsf{ep}}
is the assignment such that

\alpha^{\tilde{B},p,\mathsf{ep}}(s)=\lambda^{(\tilde{B},p,\mathsf{ep})}_{s}

for each
s\in S_{\mathfrak{M}[{\mathcal{P}}]}^{\mathsf{reg}}
.
Recall that we have assumed

\mathrm{Pr}^{\pi}_{(l,0)}(\Lambda^{\pi}({\rho}))=\mathrm{Pr}^{\hat{\pi}}_{\mathsf{reg}({l,0})}(\Lambda^{\hat{\pi}}({\rho}))
,
and, from the construction of
\hat{\pi}
,
have
\hat{\pi}(r)((\tilde{B},p),\alpha)=\pi(r^{\prime})(\tilde{v},p)

for all finite paths
r\in\Lambda^{\hat{\pi}}({\rho})
.
In the following, we let
\hat{r}\in\Lambda^{\hat{\pi}}({\rho})

be an arbitrary finite path from
\Lambda^{\hat{\pi}}({\rho})
.
Then by construction:

\displaystyle\mathrm{Pr}^{\pi}_{(l,0)}(\Lambda^{\pi}({\rho}))\cdot\pi(r^{\prime})(\tilde{v},p)\cdot\left(\alpha(\mathsf{le})\cdot{\wp}[{\mathsf{le}(\tilde{B})}](\emptyset,l^{\prime})+\alpha(\mathsf{re})\cdot{\wp}[{\mathsf{re}(\tilde{B})}](\emptyset,l^{\prime})\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathrm{Pr}^{\hat{\pi}}_{\mathsf{reg}({l,0})}(\Lambda^{\hat{\pi}}({\rho}))\cdot\hat{\pi}(\hat{r})((\tilde{B},p),\alpha)

\displaystyle\times\left(\alpha(\mathsf{le})\cdot\Delta_{\mathfrak{M}[{\mathcal{P}}]}((\tilde{B},p,\mathsf{le}),(\tau,\alpha^{\tilde{B},p,\mathsf{le}}))(l^{\prime},B^{\prime})\right.

\displaystyle\left.~~~+\alpha(\mathsf{re})\cdot\Delta_{\mathfrak{M}[{\mathcal{P}}]}((\tilde{B},p,\mathsf{re}),(\tau,\alpha^{\tilde{B},p,\mathsf{re}}))(l^{\prime},B^{\prime})\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathrm{Pr}^{\hat{\pi}}_{\mathsf{reg}({l,0})}(\Lambda^{\hat{\pi}}({\rho}))\cdot\hat{\pi}(\hat{r})((\tilde{B},p),\alpha)\cdot\alpha(\mathsf{le})\cdot\Delta_{\mathfrak{M}[{\mathcal{P}}]}((\tilde{B},p,\mathsf{le}),(\tau,\alpha^{\tilde{B},p,\mathsf{le}}))(l^{\prime},B^{\prime})

\displaystyle+\mathrm{Pr}^{\hat{\pi}}_{\mathsf{reg}({l,0})}(\Lambda^{\hat{\pi}}({\rho}))\cdot\hat{\pi}(\hat{r})((\tilde{B},p),\alpha)\cdot\alpha(\mathsf{re})\cdot\Delta_{\mathfrak{M}[{\mathcal{P}}]}((\tilde{B},p,\mathsf{re}),(\tau,\alpha^{\tilde{B},p,\mathsf{re}}))(l^{\prime},B^{\prime})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{r\in\Lambda^{\hat{\pi}}({\rho})}\sum_{\mathsf{ep}\in\{{\mathsf{le},\mathsf{re}}\}}\mathrm{Pr}^{\hat{\pi}}_{\mathsf{reg}({l,0})}(r)\cdot\hat{\pi}(r)((\tilde{B},p),\alpha)\cdot\alpha(\mathsf{ep})\hskip 113.81102pt

\displaystyle~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~\times\Delta_{\mathfrak{M}[{\mathcal{P}}]}((\tilde{B},p,\mathsf{ep}),(\tau,\alpha^{\tilde{B},p,\mathsf{ep}}))(l^{\prime},B^{\prime})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathrm{Pr}^{\hat{\pi}}_{\mathsf{reg}({l,0})}(\Lambda^{\hat{\pi}}({\rho(\tilde{B},p)(l^{\prime},B^{\prime})}))\;.

This completes showing that

\mathrm{Pr}^{\pi}_{(l,0)}(\Lambda^{\pi}({\rho(\tilde{B},p)(l^{\prime},B^{\prime})}))=\mathrm{Pr}^{\hat{\pi}}_{\mathsf{reg}({l,0})}(\Lambda^{\hat{\pi}}({\rho(\tilde{B},p)(l^{\prime},B^{\prime})}))

when
B^{\prime}=\tilde{B}
.
The case for
B^{\prime}=[0,0]
follows similarly.

The following lemma considers the converse direction,
namely that (starting from a given location with the clock equal to

)
any scheduler of
{[\hskip-1.42271pt[\mathfrak{M}[{\mathcal{P}}]]\hskip-1.42271pt]}

\mathbb{B}
-minimal scheduler of
{[\hskip-1.42271pt[\mathcal{P}]\hskip-1.42271pt]}

such that the schedulers assign the same probability of reaching a certain set of locations.

Lemma 6
.

Let
l\in L
be a location
and let
F\subseteq L
be a set of locations.
Given a scheduler
\hat{\pi}\in\Sigma^{{[\hskip-1.42271pt[\mathfrak{M}[{\mathcal{P}}]]\hskip-1.42271pt]}}
,
there exists a
\mathbb{B}
-minimal scheduler
\pi\in\Sigma^{{[\hskip-1.42271pt[\mathcal{P}]\hskip-1.42271pt]}}_{\mathbb{B}}
,
such that
\mathrm{Pr}^{\pi}_{(l,0)}(\Diamond T_{F})=\mathrm{Pr}^{\hat{\pi}}_{\mathsf{reg}({l,0})}(\Diamond\mathfrak{T}_{F})
.

Proof 4.3
.

Let
l\in L
,

F\subseteq L

and
\hat{\pi}\in\Sigma^{{[\hskip-1.42271pt[\mathfrak{M}[{\mathcal{P}}]]\hskip-1.42271pt]}}
.
We describe the construction of scheduler
\pi\in\Sigma^{{[\hskip-1.42271pt[\mathcal{P}]\hskip-1.42271pt]}}_{\mathbb{B}}
,
and then show that

\mathrm{Pr}^{\pi}_{(l,0)}(\Diamond T_{F})=\mathrm{Pr}^{\hat{\pi}}_{\mathsf{reg}({l,0})}(\Diamond\mathfrak{T}_{F})
.

Before presenting the details, we sketch the overall approach of the proof.
The proof proceeds in a similar manner to that of Lemma
4
:
here, as for the proof of Lemma
4
,
our aim is to obtain a (
\mathbb{B}
-minimal) scheduler
\pi
of
{[\hskip-1.42271pt[\mathcal{P}]\hskip-1.42271pt]}
,
i.e., a scheduler for which there is a one-to-one relationship between its finite paths and
(a subset of)
\mathbb{B}
-paths.
The principles underlying the construction of
\pi
are the same as those underlying
the analogous construction of Lemma
4
:
for a finite path

of
{[\hskip-1.42271pt[\mathcal{P}]\hskip-1.42271pt]}
,
in order to define
\pi(r)

we consider extensions of the unique
\mathbb{B}
-path
\rho
that corresponds to

for each of those extensions that correspond to a set of finite paths of
\hat{\pi}
,
we define a choice made by the distribution
\pi(r)

that mimics the final transition of the aforementioned set of finite paths of
\hat{\pi}
.
The probabilities of the distribution
\pi(r)

are obtained as weighted averages of the choices of
\hat{\pi}
.
Showing that

\mathrm{Pr}^{\pi}_{(l,0)}(\Diamond T_{F})=\mathrm{Pr}^{\hat{\pi}}_{\mathsf{reg}({l,0})}(\Diamond\mathfrak{T}_{F})

will be done in a similar manner to the analogous part of the proof of Lemma
5
.

We first describe the construction of
\pi\in\Sigma^{{[\hskip-1.42271pt[\mathcal{P}]\hskip-1.42271pt]}}_{\mathbb{B}}
.
Consider the finite path

r=(l_{0},v_{0})(\tilde{v}_{0},p_{0})(l_{1},v_{1})(\tilde{v}_{1},p_{1})\cdots(\tilde{v}_{n-1},p_{n-1})(l_{n},v_{n})

of
{[\hskip-1.42271pt[\mathcal{P}]\hskip-1.42271pt]}
.
Let

\rho=(l_{0},B_{0})(\tilde{B}_{0},p_{0})(l_{1},B_{1})(\tilde{B}_{1},p_{1})\cdots(\tilde{B}_{n-1},p_{n-1})(l_{n},B_{n})

be the unique
\mathbb{B}
-path such that

corresponds to
\rho
.
Similarly, recall that

is the set of finite paths of
\hat{\pi}

that correspond to

(where the notion of correspondence of finite paths of

to a

Consider the extension of

,

\tilde{B}\in\mathcal{I}_{\mathbb{B}}
,

\tilde{B}\geq B_{n}

We recall an assumption that we can make without loss of generality,
as already described in the proof of Lemma
2

given

we assume that there exists at most one

\alpha\in\mathfrak{G}({\mathfrak{D}_{\mathfrak{M}[{\mathcal{P}}]}(\mathit{last}(r^{\prime}),(\tilde{B},p))})

((\tilde{B},p),\alpha)\in\mathsf{support}(\hat{\pi}(r^{\prime}))

(this follows from the fact that the set of assignments

\mathfrak{G}({\mathfrak{D}_{\mathfrak{M}[{\mathcal{P}}]}(\mathit{last}(r^{\prime}),(\tilde{B},p))})

If such
\alpha\in\mathfrak{G}({\mathfrak{D}_{\mathfrak{M}[{\mathcal{P}}]}(\mathit{last}(r^{\prime}),(\tilde{B},p))})

exists,
we denote it by
\alpha^{r^{\prime}}_{({\tilde{B}},{p})}

As in the proof of Lemma
4
,
to define
\pi(r)
,
for each pair
(\tilde{B},p)
used to extend

,
we identify a clock valuation
\tilde{v}^{\mathrm{*}}_{{\rho},({\tilde{B}},{p})}
,
and then let:

\displaystyle\pi(r)(\tilde{v}^{\mathrm{*}}_{{\rho},({\tilde{B}},{p})},p)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\sum_{r^{\prime}\in\Lambda^{\hat{\pi}}({\rho})}\mathrm{Pr}^{\hat{\pi}}_{*,\mathsf{reg}({l,0})}(r^{\prime})\cdot\hat{\pi}(r^{\prime})((\tilde{B},p),\alpha^{r^{\prime}}_{({\tilde{B}},{p})})}{\mathrm{Pr}^{\hat{\pi}}_{*,\mathsf{reg}({l,0})}(\Lambda^{\hat{\pi}}({\rho}))}\;,

where
\mathrm{Pr}^{\hat{\pi}}_{*,\mathsf{reg}({l,0})}(\Lambda^{\hat{\pi}}({\rho}))=\sum_{r^{\prime}\in\Lambda^{\hat{\pi}}({\rho})}\mathrm{Pr}^{\hat{\pi}}_{*,\mathsf{reg}({l,0})}(r^{\prime})
,
and where
\hat{\pi}(r^{\prime})((\tilde{B},p),\alpha^{r^{\prime}}_{({\tilde{B}},{p})})=0

if there does not exist any
\alpha\in\mathfrak{G}({\mathfrak{D}_{\mathfrak{M}[{\mathcal{P}}]}(\mathit{last}(r^{\prime}),(\tilde{B},p))})

such that

((\tilde{B},p),\alpha)\in\mathsf{support}(\hat{\pi}(r^{\prime}))
.

For the case in which
\tilde{B}
is a closed interval,
i.e.,
\tilde{B}=[b,b]
for

,
we let
\tilde{v}^{\mathrm{*}}_{{\rho},({\tilde{B}},{p})}=b
.

We now consider the case in which
\tilde{B}
is an open interval.
The approach we take is analogous to that taken in the proof of Lemma
4
:
intuitively, if

is a constant probabilistic edge,
and no non-constant probabilistic edge has been taken while the clock value

\tilde{B}
,
then the scheduler
\pi
maintains the clock as having a value that is

\tilde{B}

if

\tilde{B}

\hat{\pi}

is a constant probabilistic edge,

been taken previously while the clock value

\tilde{B}

then the scheduler

can choose the value of the clock from
\tilde{B}

in an arbitrary manner.
As in the proof of Lemma
4
,
the fact that we need to consider only these three cases relies on the assumption of initialisation.
The construction is more complicated than that of the proof of Lemma
4
,
because the clock valuations must be chosen by

\hat{\pi}

(instead, for Lemma

As in the proof of Lemma

,
let

(with

We consider the following function

\tilde{B}
.

,
in the sense that the greater the probability of

\tilde{B}
.

First we consider the subcase in which all probabilistic edges in

While the clock must be set to some value in

We now identify such values.
We first define the set

(2) feature only

We now define formally

For a finite path

Recall that we have assumed w.l.o.g. that,

\displaystyle\tilde{v}^{\mathrm{*}}_{{\rho},({\tilde{B}},{p})}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\sum_{r^{\prime}\in\Lambda^{\hat{\pi}}({\rho})}\mathrm{Pr}^{\hat{\pi}}_{*,(\mathsf{reg}({l,0}))}(r^{\prime})\cdot\hat{\pi}(r^{\prime})((\tilde{B},p),\alpha^{r^{\prime}}_{({\tilde{B}},{p})})\cdot h_{\tilde{B}}(\alpha^{r^{\prime}}_{({\tilde{B}},{p})})}{\sum_{r^{\prime}\in\Lambda^{\hat{\pi}}({\rho})}\mathrm{Pr}^{\hat{\pi}}_{*,(\mathsf{reg}({l,0}))}(r^{\prime})\cdot\hat{\pi}(r^{\prime})((\tilde{B},p),\alpha^{r^{\prime}}_{({\tilde{B}},{p})})}\;.

is an open interval,

\displaystyle\sum_{(\tilde{B},p)\in\mathfrak{A}_{\mathfrak{M}[{\mathcal{P}}]}({l_{n},B_{n}})}\pi(r)(\tilde{v}^{\mathrm{*}}_{{\rho},({\tilde{B}},{p})},p)

The next step of the proof is to show that

As in previous proofs,

\mathbb{B}

Proceeding by induction on the length of
\mathbb{B}
-paths,
consider the
\mathbb{B}
-path

,

\mathrm{Pr}^{\pi}_{*,(l,0)}(\Lambda^{\pi}({\rho}))=\mathrm{Pr}^{\hat{\pi}}_{*,\mathsf{reg}({l,0})}(\Lambda^{\hat{\pi}}({\rho}))
.
Our aim is to show that

\mathrm{Pr}^{\pi}_{*,(l,0)}(\Lambda^{\pi}({\rho(\tilde{B},p)(l^{\prime},B^{\prime})}))=\mathrm{Pr}^{\hat{\pi}}_{*,\mathsf{reg}({l,0})}(\Lambda^{\hat{\pi}}({\rho(\tilde{B},p)(l^{\prime},B^{\prime})}))\;.

is open,

is non-constant

Our approach is slightly different from that used in the proof of Lemma

Let
r\in\Lambda^{\hat{\pi}}({\rho})
.
Now consider the finite paths in

\hat{\pi}

(where

\displaystyle\sum_{\mathsf{ep}\in\{{\mathsf{le},\mathsf{re}}\}}\mathrm{Pr}^{\hat{\pi}}_{*,\mathsf{reg}({l,0})}(r((\tilde{B},p),\alpha^{r}_{({\tilde{B}},{p})})(\mathsf{ep})(l^{\prime},B^{\prime}))

5.
Conclusion

We have presented a method for the transformation of a class of 1c-cdPTAs to IMDPs
such that there is a precise relationship between the schedulers of the 1c-cdPTA and the IMDP,
allowing us to use established polynomial-time algorithms for IMDPs to decide
quantitative and qualitative reachability problems on the 1c-cdPTA.
Overall, the results establish that such problems are in PTIME.
The techniques rely on the initialisation requirement,
which ensures that optimal choices for non-constant probabilistic edges
correspond to the left or right endpoints of intervals
that are derived from the syntactic description of the 1c-cdPTA.
The initialisation requirement restricts dependencies between non-constant probabilistic edges:
while this necessarily restricts the expressiveness of the formalism,
the resulting model nevertheless retains the expressive power to represent basic
situations in which the probability of certain events depends on the exact amount of time elapsed,
such as those described in the introduction.

The IMDP construction can be simplified in a number of cases:
for example, in the case in which at most two outcomes
e_{1},e_{2}

of every probabilistic edge
p
are non-constant,
i.e., for which
d^{p}_{e_{1}}\neq 0
and
d^{p}_{e_{2}}\neq 0
,
endpoint indicators are unnecessary;
instead, when a probabilistic edge is taken from an open interval
\tilde{B}
,
each of
e_{1}
and
e_{2}
are associated with (non-singleton) intervals
(other outcomes are associated with singleton intervals),
and the choice of probability to assign between the two intervals
represents the choice of clock valuation in
\tilde{B}
.
This construction is also polynomial in the size of the 1c-cdPTA.

Future work could consider
time-bounded reachability problems for 1c-cdPTAs,
or lifting one of the two restrictions that
we have applied on the original clock-dependent probabilistic timed automata formalism
as presented in
[
Spr21
]
,
namely initialisation and the restriction to one clock.
We discuss these restrictions in turn.
Consider first the case of initialisation.
Observe that initialisation enforces that the choice of clock value
at the point at which a non-constant probabilistic edge is taken
is independent of the choice of clock value at the point
at which any other non-constant probabilistic edge is taken.
For example, for the 1c-cdPTA of Figure

the clock values chosen when leaving location
\mathrm{W}

and (in the case in which the outcome to location
\mathrm{F}
is taken)
when leaving location
\mathrm{F}

are independent from each other,
because they belong to different intervals of
\mathcal{I}_{\mathbb{B}}

Instead, in the example of Figure

[

]
),
in which initialisation does not hold,
the choice of clock value when leaving location
\mathrm{B}
(
\mathrm{C}

depends on (i.e., cannot be less than)
the clock value when leaving location
\mathrm{A}
(
\mathrm{B}
, respectively).
The characteristic of independence of the choice of clock values
for different non-constant probabilistic edges
permits the reduction from 1c-cdPTAs to IMDPs presented in this paper:
the IMDP construction encodes the choice of clock value of the 1c-cdPTA
by the chosen assignment of an interval distribution
(i.e., the interval distributions with intervals
(0,1)

If the choice of clock values for different non-constant probabilistic edges are
not

as is the case when the 1c-cdPTA is not initialised,
then we would require some mechanism in the IMDP to enforce such dependencies
between the choices of assignment in different IMDP states,
which does not exist in the classical IMDP formalism.
Solutions to the qualitative problem for non-initialised 1c-cdPTAs
could potentially utilise connections with parametric MDPs
[
HHZ11

WJPK21
]

in which dependence between parameters on probabilities of transitions
from different states is an inherent part of the formalism.

Future work could explore the applications of a subclass of this formalism,
for example with multiple clocks but only one clock used for clock dependencies,
or a more strict definition of initialisation than that described above:
such subclasses may yield models that have more significance in practice than 1c-cdPTAs
while offering the possibility of exact rather than approximate analysis.

Finally, we also discuss lifting the restriction to affine clock dependencies
in the case of initialised 1c-cdPTAs.
We recall that the IMDP construction presented in this paper
depends on the fact that the distributions corresponding to
the endpoints of intervals in
\mathcal{I}_{\mathbb{B}}
are “extremal”,
in the sense that, for a given interval, non-constant probabilistic edge and outcome,
the maximum or minimum probability assigned to that outcome according to the
probabilistic edge’s distribution template will be assigned at one of the endpoints of the interval.
This property allows any scheduler of the 1c-cdPTA to be mimicked by a IMDP scheduler
by using probabilistic edges available from regions to obtain linear combinations
of the distributions corresponding to an interval’s endpoints.
In the case of non-affine clock dependencies, this property does not necessarily hold,
i.e., the maximum or minimum probability assigned to an outcome
may fall
within
an interval,
rather than at one of its endpoints.
Hence, dealing with non-affine clock dependencies requires non-trivial developments
beyond those featured in this paper,
or requires extra assumptions in order to handle the greater expressiveness of the clock dependencies,
for example restrictions on the non-affine functions used
(for example, so that their maximum or minimum
is always at the endpoint of any interval in
\mathcal{I}_{\mathbb{B}}
)
or, for any probabilistic edge, on the number of outcomes that can have non-affine clock dependencies.
