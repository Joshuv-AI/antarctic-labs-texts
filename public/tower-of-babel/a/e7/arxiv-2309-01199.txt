DKWS: A Distributed System for Keyword Search on Massive Graphs (Complete Version)
Jiaxin Jiang, Byron Choi, Xin Huang, Jianliang Xu, Sourav S Bhowmick

Abstract
Due to the unstructuredness and the lack of schemas of graphs, such as knowledge graphs, social networks, and RDF graphs, keyword search for querying such graphs has been proposed. As graphs have become voluminous, large-scale distributed processing has attracted much interest from the database research community. While there have been several distributed systems, distributed querying techniques for keyword search are still limited. This paper proposes a novel distributed keyword search system called 𝖣𝖪𝖶𝖲 \mathsf{DKWS} . First, we present a monotonic property with keyword search algorithms that guarantees correct parallelization. Second, we present a keyword search algorithm as monotonic backward and forward search phases. Moreover, we propose new tight bounds for pruning nodes being searched. Third, we propose a notify-push paradigm and 𝖯𝖨𝖭𝖤 \mathsf{PINE} programming model of 𝖣𝖪𝖶𝖲 \mathsf{DKWS} . The notify-push paradigm allows asynchronously exchanging the upper bounds of matches across the workers and the coordinator in 𝖣𝖪𝖶𝖲 \mathsf{DKWS} . The 𝖯𝖨𝖭𝖤 \mathsf{PINE} programming model naturally fits keyword search algorithms, as they have distinguished phases, to allow preemptive searches to mitigate staleness in a distributed system. Finally, we investigate the performance and effectiveness of 𝖣𝖪𝖶𝖲 \mathsf{DKWS} through experiments using real-world datasets. We find that 𝖣𝖪𝖶𝖲 \mathsf{DKWS} is up to two orders of magnitude faster than related techniques, and its communication costs are 7.6 7.6 times smaller than those of other techniques.

Backward and forward keyword search

In this section, we discuss the monotonic property of keyword search (
\mathsf{kws}
) algorithms, which is crucial for its correct parallelization
[
14
]
. Specifically, backward and forward keyword search (
\mathsf{bfkws}
) consists of two phases, namely, backward keyword search (
\mathsf{bkws}
) and forward keyword search (
\mathsf{fkws}
). Intuitively,
\mathsf{bkws}
starts from the vertices that contain the query keywords and performs a backward search to identify potential vertices that might serve as the roots of a match.
\mathsf{fkws}
initiates its search from these identified roots and proceeds forward. The objective of
\mathsf{fkws}
is to discover any missing keywords within the subtrees that consider these vertices as roots. We prove that both
\mathsf{bkws}
and
\mathsf{fkws}
have the monotonic property (detailed at the end of Sec.
III-B
and
III-C
, respectively). The monotonic property of a few popular keyword search algorithms, such as
[
17
,
35
,
10
]
, can be analyzed similarly, which is omitted.

III-A

Monotonic algorithms for keyword search

This subsection presents how the keyword search algorithm has the monotonic property. More specifically, the monotonic property is defined with a
partial order
of
match variables
from a
finite domain
. Intuitively, the shortest distance between the root
u
and a query keyword
q\in Q
, denoted as
\mathsf{dist}(u,q)
(
i.e.,

\mathsf{dist}(u,q)=\min\{\mathsf{dist}(u,v)|q\in L(v)\}
), is of a finite domain. When the monotonic property holds, its value decreases or remains unchanged during query processing and converges to the
exact
shortest distance after query processing ends. Before providing further details, we present the structure of the match variable,
\mathsf{mat}_{u}
, which maintains the substree rooted at
u
.

Definition III.1
(Match
\mathsf{mat}_{u}
)

For a given graph
G

(Q,\tau,k)
, a match
\mathsf{mat}_{u}
with its root at
u

\forall q\in Q
, if
\mathsf{dist}(u,q)\leq\tau
,
\mathsf{mat}_{u}[q]
is set to
\mathsf{dist}(u,q)
. Otherwise,
\mathsf{mat}_{u}[q]
is set to
\mathsf{null}
.

Complete matches and partial matches.

\mathsf{mat}_{u}[q]
is initialized to
{\mathsf{null}}
. Throughout the search process, certain keywords for all
u
may be discovered within
\mathsf{mat}_{u}
and
\mathsf{mat}_{u}[q]
is set to
\mathsf{dist}(u,q)
, while others may remain
\mathsf{null}
. Formally, a match
\mathsf{mat}_{u}
is referred to as a
partial match
if and only if
\exists q\in Q
,
\mathsf{mat}_{u}[q]
is
{\mathsf{null}}
(
i.e.,
some keyword is not matched). Otherwise,
\mathsf{mat}_{u}
is a

.

Definition III.2
(Monotonic
\mathsf{kws}
algorithm)

Given a graph
G=(V,E)
, where each node

is associated with
\mathsf{mat}_{u}
. A monotonic keyword search algorithm
\mathsf{kws}
satisfies the following conditions:

1.

\mathsf{mat}_{u}
of all vertices are in a finite domain; and

2.

there exists a partial order
\preceq

\mathsf{mat}_{u}

\mathsf{kws}

We next illustrate the details of the monotonic property of
\mathsf{kws}
in relation to a finite domain and a partial order on the matches.

(1) Finite domain of
\mathsf{kws}
. To illustrate a finite domain of match variables, we encode
{\mathsf{null}}

\sum_{e_{i}\in E}w(e_{i})

\mathsf{mat}_{u}[q]
.
\mathsf{mat}_{u}[q]\in\{\sum_{e_{i}\in E^{\prime}}w(e_{i})|E^{\prime}\subseteq E\}\cup\{+\infty\}

(2) Partial order of
\mathsf{kws}
. We propose the partial order
\preceq

\mathsf{mat}_{u}
which is defined as follows. Suppose

\mathsf{kws}

k

. Therefore, the number of supersteps is bounded.

(ii) Correctness.
Given that
\mathsf{kws}
is correct for query
Q
, at the superstep
R=1
,
\mathsf{kws}
returns a set of correct local matches with roots in each fragment

. Matches with roots on portal nodes are passed to their copies in other fragments (if any) at the end of each superstep. At the superstep

, each node

contains its local match
\mathsf{mat}_{u}
from the superstep

and the matches
\mathsf{mat}^{\prime}_{u}
which are rooted at its copies in other fragments. Therefore,
\mathsf{kws}
can compute the correct match for the current superstep for each node. The correctness of the final matches is thus established by induction on the supersteps.

Monotonic backward search (

We next present the major steps of backward search for keyword search (

\mathsf{bkws}

Answer

The answer to the query is a set of the top-

Maintenance of answer.

\mathcal{A}

1.

is removed and

is inserted into
\mathcal{A}

(Step 1) Initialization.
Consider a set of query keywords

(Step 2) Backward expansion.

(Step 3) Match discovery.
It discovers a complete match rooted at

III-C

Monotonic forward search (
\mathsf{fkws}
)

The main purpose of
\mathsf{fkws}
is to retrieve the missing keywords of the partial matches via forward expansion.
\mathsf{fkws}
is also widely used in existing keyword search algorithms,
e.g.,

[
17
,
51
,
5
,
38
,
23
]
. Due to a potentially large number of partial matches, forward expansion for the vertices
\bar{\mathcal{V}}
could be costly. Existing studies can be space-consuming
[
17
]
or do not guarantee exact matches
[
23
]
. We describe the forward expansion and propose
new bounds
for pruning in
\mathsf{fkws}
.

Forward expansion.
Consider a partial match
\mathsf{mat}_{u}
. Suppose a query keyword
q\in Q
is missing in
\mathsf{mat}_{u}
.
\mathsf{fkws}
forward expands from
u
by using Dijkstra’s algorithm to retrieve the nearest node that contains
q
.

Pruning in
\mathsf{fkws}
.
Some forward expansions do not lead to complete matches and can be pruned as shown in Prop.
III.2
.

Proposition III.2

Consider the forward expansion for vertex
u
. Suppose the next vertex to be expanded by Dijkstra’s algorithm is
v
, the forward expansion is terminated when any of the following conditions holds.

(i)

q\in L(v)
,
i.e.,
the keyword
q
is found;

(ii)

\mathsf{dist}(u,v)>\tau
, the vertex containing keyword
q
is farther than
\tau
from
u
or does not exist (
i.e.,

\mathsf{dist}(u,q)>\tau
); or

(iii)

\mathsf{scr}(u)+\mathsf{dist}(u,v)>S\Rightarrow\mathsf{scr}(u)+\mathsf{dist}(u,q)>S
.

As indicated by Condition
(ii)
, if
\mathsf{dist}(u,q)
has been indexed, early termination can be determined if
\mathsf{dist}(u,q)>\tau
. Furthermore, Condition
(iii)
posits that the current top
k
-th match score, denoted as
S
, serves as an upper bound. If
\mathsf{dist}(u,q)
is indexed, we can employ a tightly estimated upper bound of
S
to facilitate decisions on early termination. Thus, we engage state-of-the-art indexing techniques — PageRank-based All-distances Sketches (
\mathsf{PADS}
) and PageRank-based Keyword Distance Sketches (
\mathsf{KPADS}
)
[
20
]
. Specifically,
\mathsf{PADS}(u)
is a sketch for
u
, which indexes the shortest distance between
u
and the sketch’s centers (some vertices in the graph). Given that
\mathsf{PADS}(v_{i})
and
\mathsf{PADS}(v_{j})
may share common centers where
q\in L(v_{i})\cap L(v_{j})
, these shared centers can be merged. In the process of merging, only the smallest distance is retained.
\mathsf{KPADS}(q)
sketch is constructed through such merges and is used to index the shortest distance between the keyword
q
and the centers. These sketches assist in estimating both the upper and lower bounds of the shortest distance between
u
and
q
, where
u
belongs to
\bar{\mathcal{V}}
and
q
is a missing keyword in
\mathsf{mat}_{u}
.

Indexing.

\mathsf{PADS}{}
and
\mathsf{KPADS}{}
have been shown to be both space- and time-efficient in practice with theoretical guarantees on the accuracy of the shortest distance which can be readily distributed.
However, we remark that
[
20
]
considered undirected graphs. To support
directed graphs
, we make a modification to
\mathsf{PADS}
as follows. The sketch of a node
u
is two sets of vertices and their corresponding shortest distances from (resp. to)
u
, denoted by
\mathsf{PADS}^{\mathsf{out}}(u)=\{(w,d)\}
(resp.
\mathsf{PADS}^{\mathsf{in}}(u)=\{(w,d)\}
), where
w\in V
and
d=\mathsf{dist}(u,w)
(resp.
d=\mathsf{dist}(w,u)
). Similarly, the sketch of a keyword
q
is denoted by
\mathsf{KPADS}^{\mathsf{out}}(q)=\{(w,d)\}
(resp.
\mathsf{KPADS}^{\mathsf{in}}(q)=\{(w,d)\}
), where
w\in V
and
d=\mathsf{dist}(q,w)
(resp.
d=\mathsf{dist}(w,q)
). For brevity, we leave the construction pseudo-code of
\mathsf{PADS}
and
\mathsf{KPADS}
in
[
18
]
.

Since
\mathsf{PADS}
yields estimated bounds,
\mathsf{fkws}
needs to handle both approximate and exact matches. Specifically,
\mathsf{fkws}
computes the
upper
bound of the score for any

,
\mathsf{scr}(u)
, by estimating the shortest distance between
u
and the missing keywords,
i.e.,

\Sigma\mathsf{dist}(u,q_{i})+\Sigma\mathsf{mat}_{u}[q_{j}]
, where
q_{i},q_{j}\in Q
,
q_{i}
is missing from
\mathsf{mat}_{u}
whereas
q_{j}
has been found in
\mathsf{mat}_{u}
. If the upper bound is smaller than
S
,
\mathsf{mat}_{u}
is inserted into
\mathcal{A}
and
S
is refined accordingly. To avoid ambiguity, we denote the
\mathcal{A}
that may consist of exact matches and approximate matches by
\hat{\mathcal{A}}
. The approximate matches in
\hat{\mathcal{A}}
are further refined during forward expansion.
\hat{\mathcal{A}}
is eventually refined to yield
\mathcal{A}
.

Next, we present the upper and lower bounds of
\mathsf{dist}(u,q)
for the termination of forward expansion from

. These bounds can be applied to other keyword search semantics as they involve numerous distance computations, such as
[

,

(1) Upper bound of the shortest distance.
Given a shortest distance query

, the upper bound is computed by

and
\mathsf{KPADS}^{\mathsf{in}}(q)
as follows:

\mathsf{dist}(u,q)\leq\mathsf{dist}(u,w)+\mathsf{dist}(w,q),

(1)

where
(w,\mathsf{dist}(u,w))\in\mathsf{PADS}^{\mathsf{out}}(u),(w,\mathsf{dist}(w,q))\in\mathsf{KPADS}^{\mathsf{in}}(q)
, and

is a common center in
\mathsf{PADS}^{\mathsf{out}}(u)
and
\mathsf{KPADS}^{\mathsf{in}}(q)
.

Distributed keyword search (

We illustrate
\mathsf{PIE}

[

with a keyword search algorithm, denoted as
\mathsf{kws}
. (a)
\mathsf{PEval}

of
\mathsf{kws}

The partial evaluation (

\mathsf{mat}_{u}
for all

P_{i}

The incremental evaluation (
\mathsf{IncEval}{}

Q

F_{i}
of graph
G

Q(F_{i})

M_{i}

from the previous superstep to maximize efficiency. After every execution of

updates its state by considering

and

\mathsf{Assemble}
starts its computation when

is empty for any worker
P_{i}
.
\mathsf{Assemble}
accepts
Q(F_{i}\oplus M_{i})
as inputs. It consolidates for all

, to compute the final answer

(Fig.

The coordinator

to

\mathsf{bkws}

\mathsf{DKWS}
differs from previous studies in two major ways: (1)

is the first to introduce a

to estimate the performance improvement of executing either

Message buffers.
Each worker
P_{i}
maintains two message buffers
\mathbb{B}_{i}^{b}
and
\mathbb{B}_{i}^{f}
to keep track of backward and forward messages from other workers. The more messages are accumulated in
\mathbb{B}_{i}^{b}
(resp.
\mathbb{B}_{i}^{f}

should start
\mathsf{bkws}
(resp.
\mathsf{fkws}
) computation, and vice versa.

Expansion distance.
Denote
\mathsf{mat}_{u}^{b,\mathsf{in}}
as a message generated by
\mathsf{bkws}
and maintained in
\mathbb{B}_{i}^{b}
. If
\mathsf{mat}^{b}_{u}[q]
is larger than
\mathsf{mat}_{u}^{b,\mathsf{in}}[q]
,
\mathsf{DKWS}
needs to backward expand starting from
u
. We call
d_{q}^{b}=\min\{S-\mathsf{mat}_{u}^{b,\mathsf{in}}[q],\tau\}

backward expansion distance starting from
u
for query keyword
q
. Without prior statistics, if
d_{q}^{b}
is larger, the backward expansion is more costly and the messages are less likely to finally yield one of the top-
k
matches. Worker
P_{i}
may stop expanding
\mathsf{mat}^{b}_{u}[q]
by postponing the execution of
\mathsf{bkws}
, but start
\mathsf{fkws}
to prune some unyielding messages. Similarly, we define
d_{q}^{f}=\min\{\mathsf{f}_{u}^{\mathsf{in}}[q],\tau\}
, the
forward expansion distance
.

Staleness indicators.
Inspired by the complexities of
\mathsf{bkws}
and
\mathsf{fkws}
, we propose the staleness indicators of the accumulated backward messages and forward messages for each worker

, denoted by
{\textsf{SI}}_{i}^{b}
and
{\textsf{SI}}_{i}^{f}
.
{\textsf{SI}}_{i}^{b}
and
{\textsf{SI}}_{i}^{f}
are formally defined below:

\footnotesize{\textsf{SI}}_{i}^{b}=\begin{cases}+\infty,&\text{if $\mathbb{B}_{i}^{b}$ is empty}\\
\frac{{\sum}_{u\in F_{i}.O}{\sum}_{q\in Q}d^{b}_{q}}{|\mathbb{B}^{b}_{i}|},&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

(3)

\footnotesize{\textsf{SI}}_{i}^{f}=\begin{cases}+\infty,&\text{if $\mathbb{B}_{i}^{f}$ is empty}\\
\frac{{\sum}_{u\in F_{i}.I}{\sum}_{q\in Q}^{|Q|}d^{f}_{q}}{|\mathbb{B}^{f}_{i}|},&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

(4)

where
d^{b}_{j}
(resp.
d^{f}_{j}
) is the average backward (resp. forward) searching distance for query keywords and
|\mathbb{B}^{b}_{i}|
(resp.
|\mathbb{B}^{f}_{i}|
) is the size of backward (resp. forward) messages buffer.

If
{\textsf{SI}}_{i}^{b}<{\textsf{SI}}_{i}^{f}
, worker

conducts
\mathsf{bkws}
. Otherwise, worker
P_{i}
conducts
\mathsf{fkws}
.
\mathsf{PINE}

at the odd supersteps.

VI
Related work

Keyword search semantics.
Recently, keyword search has attracted a lot of interest from both industry and research communities. Bhalotia et al.
[
5
]
proposed keyword search on relational databases. He et al.
[
17
]
proposed an index, called
\mathsf{Blinks}
to reduce the search time. Kargar et al.
[
24
]
proposed distance restrictions on the keyword nodes,
i.e.,
the distance between each pair of keyword nodes is smaller than
\tau
. Shi et al.
[
38
]
proposed hub labelings to solve Group Steiner Trees (GST). Kargar et al.
[
25
]
proposed an approximate algorithm to retrieve the GST on weighted graphs. These studies optimize a specific keyword search semantic. Jiang et al.
[
19
]
proposed a generic index for keyword search semantics running on a standalone machine.

Distributed systems.
Several distributed systems have been proposed for graphs. Popular graph systems include
\mathsf{Pregel}

[
31
]
,
\mathsf{Giraph}

[
4
]
,
\mathsf{GraphX}

[
45
]
,
\mathsf{GraphLab}

[

]
,
\mathsf{PowerGrapah}

[
16
]
,
\mathsf{Giraph}
++
[

]
,
\mathsf{Blogel}

[
46
]
,
\mathsf{GPS}

[
36
]
,
\mathsf{GRAPE}

[

,
8
]
, and
\mathsf{AAP}

[
12
]
.
\mathsf{Pregel}

[
31
]
and
\mathsf{Giraph}

[
4
]
are implemented with the vertex-centric programming model. A superstep executes a user-defined function at each vertex in parallel.
\mathsf{GraphX}

[
45
]
is a component built on top of
\mathsf{Spark}
for graphs which exposes a set of operators (
e.g.,
subgraph, joinVertices, and aggregateMessages) as well as an optimized variant of the

[

[

]
,

[

are implemented with the block-centric programming model.
\mathsf{AAP}

[

proposes an adaptive asynchronous parallel model for graph computations on
[

. These systems are general-purpose. Keyword search algorithms have not been exploited. For instance,
\mathsf{DKWS}
can also be beneficial to existing systems. By integrating
\mathsf{PINE}
, the systems could make the query evaluation more fine-grained. By integrating the notify-push paradigm,
\mathsf{DKWS}
allows each worker to broadcast local information to their peer workers which can alleviate the straggler problem.

Distributed
\mathsf{kws}
algorithms.
Lu et al.
[
35
]
proposed a scalable algorithm for keyword search in MapReduce. However, the false matches were pruned at the last superstep, which may cause large messages. Yuan et al.
[
51
]
proposed a search strategy based on a compressed signature to avoid the exhaustive flooding search.
[
51
]
sent all the local candidate matches to the coordinator at runtime which may require large messages and extra synchronization cost.
\mathsf{DKWS}
differs from the above in the following aspects: (a) each worker computes the top-
k
matches locally.
\mathsf{DKWS}
sends the local matches to the coordinator when all of the workers terminate rather than sends massive local candidates matches; and (b)
\mathsf{DKWS}
exchanges the local upper bounds which prune some traversals early.

VII
Conclusions and future works

In this paper, we propose a distributed keyword search system called
\mathsf{DKWS}
. We derive new bounds for pruning some keyword searches that tackle the performance challenges of a general distributed system. We show that
\mathsf{bfkws}
, which can be used to express query algorithms for popular keyword semantics, has a monotonic property that ensures the correct parallelization. We propose a notify-push paradigm allows asynchronously exchanging the upper bounds across the workers and the coordinators. We also propose a programming model
\mathsf{PINE}
for
\mathsf{DKWS}
which fits keyword search algorithms as they have distinguished
n
phases, to allow preemptive searches to mitigate staleness in a distributed system. We verify that
\mathsf{DKWS}
significantly reduces the runtimes of distributed top-
k
keyword searches.

In the future, we plan to implement
\mathsf{PINE}
into the latest codebase of
\mathsf{GRAPE}
. Moreover, we will extend
\mathsf{DKWS}
to support
approximate
analysis for some keyword search semantics
[
24
,
25
,
38
]
, some community detection algorithms
[
27
]
and some dense subgraph detection algorithms
[
21
,
29
]
.

Backtrack graph for refinement propagation

Refinement propagation is potentially costly when messages are large. If we conduct the refinement propagation from the portal nodes in
F.O
, one by one, the time complexity is bounded by

Backtrack pointer.
To reduce duplicate forward traversals,

maintains a backtrack pointer

for each visited vertex

in the forward expansion of

Backtrack graph.
Given a query keyword

The time complexity on fragment
