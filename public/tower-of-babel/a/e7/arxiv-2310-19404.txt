The Morse Index of Quartic Minimal Hypersurfaces
Gavin Ball, Jesse Madnick, Uwe Semmelmann

Abstract
. The homogeneous minimal hypersurfaces in S n S^{n} have g ∈ { 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 , 6 } g\in\{1,2,3,4,6\} distinct (constant) principal curvatures. While the Morse index and nullity have been calculated for all such hypersurfaces having g = 1 , 2 , 3 g=1,2,3 , it has remained an open problem to compute these quantities for any of those with g = 4 g=4 or 6 6 . In this paper, we calculate the Morse index and nullity of two homogeneous minimal hypersurfaces in S n S^{n} with g = 4 g=4 . Moreover, we observe that their Laplace spectra contain irrational eigenvalues that are not expressible in radicals.



1.
Introduction

1.1.
Background and results

A minimal hypersurface
M^{n-1}\subset S^{n}
is a critical point of the area functional: for every variation
(M_{t})
of
M
, the function
A(t)=\mathrm{Area}(M_{t})
satisfies
A^{\prime}(0)=0
. The
Morse index
and
nullity
of
M
, denoted
\mathrm{Ind}(M)
and
\mathrm{Nul}(M)
, are the number of infinitesimal variations of
M
that satisfy
A^{\prime\prime}(0)<0
and
A^{\prime\prime}(0)=0
, respectively. Simons
[
11
]
observed that
\mathrm{Ind}(M)\geq 1
and
\mathrm{Nul}(M)\geq n
, with equality holding in either case if and only if
M
is a totally geodesic
S^{n-1}
.

Given a minimal hypersurface
M\subset S^{n}
with unit normal vector field
\nu
, along with a variation
\iota_{t}\colon M_{t}\to S^{n}
having variation vector field
\left.\frac{d}{dt}\right|_{t=0}\iota_{t}=f\nu
with
f\in C^{\infty}(M)
, the second variation formula states that

A^{\prime\prime}(0)=\int_{M}f\,\mathcal{J}_{M}(f)\,\mathrm{vol}_{M}

where
\mathcal{J}_{M}(f)=-(\Delta_{M}+|\mathrm{I\!I}|^{2}+(n-1))f
is the
Jacobi operator
of
M
. Here,
\Delta_{M}
is the (analyst’s) Laplacian of
M
, and
\mathrm{I\!I}
is the second fundamental form of
M
. As a strongly elliptic operator,
\mathcal{J}_{M}
may be diagonalized: its spectrum is an increasing sequence of real numbers
\mu_{1}<\mu_{2}<\cdots<\mu_{s}=0<\mu_{s+1}<\cdots\to\infty
of finite multiplicities
\ell_{1},\ell_{2},\ldots
. From this point of view, the Morse index and nullity are given by

\displaystyle\mathrm{Ind}(M)

\displaystyle=\ell_{1}+\cdots+\ell_{s-1}

\displaystyle\mathrm{Nul}(M)

\displaystyle=\ell_{s}

The Morse index in particular is a fundamental invariant of minimal hypersurfaces, and is the subject of various conjectures. Unfortunately, computing
\mathrm{Ind}(M)
and
\mathrm{Nul}(M)
is often impossible in actual practice. As such, there are presently very few minimal hypersurfaces whose Morse index and nullity are known explicitly. The ones that we do know help guide conjectures, placing bounds on what is reasonable to expect. For example, it is known that every non-equatorial minimal hypersurface satisfies
\mathrm{Ind}(M)\geq n+2
, and it is conjectured that equality holds precisely for the Clifford hypersurfaces
S^{p}(\sqrt{\frac{p}{n-1}})\times S^{q}(\sqrt{\frac{q}{n-1}})
with
p+q=n-1
: see, e.g.,
[
16
,
10
]
.

In view of this difficulty, it is natural to restrict attention to special classes of minimal hypersurfaces. Perhaps the most natural class consists of the
homogeneous
ones, those given as orbits of Lie groups. In that case,
M
has
g\in\{1,2,3,4,6\}
distinct principal curvatures, the cone
\mathrm{C}(M)=\{rx\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}\colon r>0,x\in M\}
is an algebraic variety of degree
g

[
8
,
9
]
, and the Jacobi operator reduces to

\mathcal{J}_{M}=-(\Delta_{M}+g(n-1)),

Thus, for these hypersurfaces, the spectrum of
\mathcal{J}_{M}
is a shift of that of
\Delta_{M}
. However, in general, computing the spectrum of
\Delta_{M}
is itself highly non-trivial. Indeed, whereas the spectrum of a Riemannian homogeneous space
\mathrm{G}/\mathrm{H}
can in principle be computed when the metric is normal, the situation for non-normal metrics is considerably more difficult. (The metrics induced on homogeneous minimal hypersurfaces of spheres are, in general, not normal.)

The homogeneous minimal hypersurfaces in
S^{n}
were classified by Hsiang and Lawson
[

, falling into
5
infinite families and
9

1.2.
Method

We now provide a brief outline of our method for computing the Morse index and nullity of a homogeneous minimal hypersurface
M^{n-1}
in
S^{n}
. It is a refinement and extension of a technique used by Solomon
[
12
,
13
]
, who applied it to the case of cubic (
g=3
) minimal isoparametric hypersurfaces. Roughly speaking, the idea is to decompose
L^{2}(M)
into pieces, and then compute the Laplace spectrum on each piece.

To be more precise, let
M^{n-1}=\mathrm{G}/\mathrm{H}
be an abstract homogeneous Riemannian manifold, and fix a positive constant
K>0
. The following four-step process yields an algorithm for calculating the Laplace eigenvalues
\lambda
of
M
(and their multiplicities) satisfying
\lambda\geq-K
.

(1)

Decompose
L^{2}(M)=\bigoplus_{\rho}W_{\rho}
into a direct sum of isotypical summands
W_{\rho}
. Since the Laplacian is
\mathrm{G}
-invariant, the restrictions
\left.\Delta\right|_{W_{\rho}}\colon W_{\rho}\to W_{\rho}
are well-defined.

(2)

For each summand
W_{\rho}
, find an explicit “generating basis”
\mathcal{B}_{\rho}
. (The finite set of functions
\mathcal{B}_{\rho}
is typically a basis of a particular subspace of
W_{\rho}
, not of
W_{\rho}
itself.)

(3)

For each
F\in\mathcal{B}_{\rho}
, express
\Delta F
as a linear combination of functions in
\mathcal{B}_{\rho}
. Because
\mathcal{B}_{\rho}
generates
W_{\rho}
in a suitable sense, this is sufficient to compute the spectrum of
\left.\Delta\right|_{W_{\rho}}
.

(4)

Apply the eigenvalue bound of Theorem
2.3
.

In
\lx@sectionsign
2.1
, we explain these steps in greater detail. Note that the presence of an embedding
M^{n-1}\to S^{n}
is not strictly necessary, but we find that it is quite helpful in constructing the “generating basis”
\mathcal{B}_{\rho}
(Step 2).

1.3.
Outlook

It is plausible that an application of the above method could yield the Morse index and nullity of the three families of quartic minimal hypersurfaces in
S^{2k-1}
,
S^{4k-1}
, and
S^{8k-1}
. On the other hand, we expect that the situation for the two sporadic quartic hypersurfaces of dimensions
18
and
30
, as well as that for the two sextic hypersurfaces, is likely to be computationally challenging.

More generally, we expect that this technique may be applied to compute the spectrum of other non-normal Riemannian homogeneous spaces.

Conventions:

•

The Laplacian
\Delta:=\Delta_{M}
of a Riemannian manifold
M
has non-positive spectrum.

•

Via the Theorem of Highest Weight, we occasionally identify irreducible
\mathrm{G}
-representations with their highest weights.

•

We sum over repeated indices unless otherwise indicated.

•

Various computations in this work were performed with the aid of a computer algebra system (viz.,
Maple
).

Preliminaries

In
\lx@sectionsign
2.1
, we explain in detail our method for computing the Morse index and nullity of homogeneous minimal hypersurfaces in
S^{n}
. It is a refinement and extension of a technique used by Solomon
[
12
,
13
]
, who applied it to the cubic case (
g=3
). Then, in
\lx@sectionsign
2.2
, we state and prove the eigenvalue bound (Theorem
2.3
) required for the last step in the process. Finally, in
\lx@sectionsign

, which will enable us to efficiently compute geometric quantities in concrete examples. We will employ basic concepts from the representation theory of Lie groups and Lie algebras; see the Appendix for details.

2.1.
The method

Let
M=\mathrm{G}/\mathrm{H}
be a homogeneous space with
\mathrm{G}
compact and
\mathrm{H}\leq\mathrm{G}
closed, and let
g_{M}
be a
\mathrm{G}
-invariant metric on
M
. The
\mathrm{G}
-action on
M=\mathrm{G}/\mathrm{H}
given by left-multiplication induces a
\mathrm{G}
-action on
L^{2}(M;\mathbb{C})
via the formula
(g^{\sharp}f)(x):=f(g^{-1}x)
, where
f\in L^{2}(M;\mathbb{C})
and
x\in M
. The
\mathrm{G}
-module
L^{2}(M;\mathbb{C})
then decomposes into
\mathrm{G}
-submodules in the following way:

Proposition 2.1

(Frobenius Reciprocity)
.

Let
\widehat{\mathrm{G}}
denote the set of equivalence classes of finite-dimensional, irreducible complex
\mathrm{G}
-modules. There exists a
\mathrm{G}
-module isomorphism

L^{2}(M;\mathbb{C})\cong\bigoplus_{\rho\in\widehat{\mathrm{G}}}V_{\rho}\otimes\mathrm{Hom}_{\mathrm{H}}(V_{\rho},\mathbb{C})=\bigoplus_{\rho\in\widehat{\mathrm{G}}}V_{\rho}^{\oplus m(\rho)}

where the
multiplicity of
V_{\rho}
is

m(\rho)=\dim_{\mathbb{C}}\mathrm{Hom}_{\mathrm{H}}(V_{\rho},\mathbb{C})=\dim_{\mathbb{C}}\{v\in V_{\rho}\colon hv=v,\ \forall h\in\mathrm{H}\}

We refer to each
V_{\rho}^{\oplus m(\rho)}=V_{\rho}\oplus\cdots\oplus V_{\rho}
as an
isotypical summand
. The method now proceeds as follows.

Step 1.
Compute
m(\rho)
. In general, this can be non-trivial.

Step 2a.
Find explicit
\mathrm{G}
-equivariant functions
\mathbf{z}_{i}\colon M\to V_{\rho_{i}}
. Here, the
\mathrm{G}
-modules
V_{\rho_{1}},\ldots,V_{\rho_{\ell}}
need not be distinct. The functions
\mathbf{z}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{z}_{\ell}
should be sufficiently numerous so as to “generate”
L^{2}(M;\mathbb{C})
in a sense to be described in Step 2b.

Step 2b.
For each irreducible representation
V_{\rho}
, its highest weight subspace
L_{\rho}\subset V_{\rho}
has complex dimension
1
. After choosing a non-zero highest weight vector, we may identify
L_{\rho}
with
\mathbb{C}
. Having done this, let
\pi_{\rho}\colon V_{\rho}\to\mathbb{C}
denote the corresponding projection map onto
L_{\rho}\simeq\mathbb{C}
, and consider the
highest weight functions

\displaystyle\zeta_{i}:=\pi_{\rho_{i}}\circ\mathbf{z}_{i}\colon M\to\mathbb{C}.

We say that
\zeta_{i}
has
weight

\rho_{i}
. Crucial properties of such functions are:

Proposition 2.2

(
[
12
]
)
.

(1)

The set of highest weight functions with weight
\rho
is a
\mathbb{C}
-vector space of complex dimension
m(\rho)
.

(2)

If
\zeta_{1},\ldots,\zeta_{\ell}
are highest weight functions of weights
\rho_{1},\ldots,\rho_{\ell}
, respectively, then
\zeta_{1}^{k_{1}}\cdots\zeta_{\ell}^{k_{\ell}}
is a highest weight function of weight
k_{1}\rho_{1}+\cdots+k_{\ell}\rho_{\ell}
.

(3)

If
\zeta
is a highest weight function of weight
\rho
, then
\mathrm{span}_{\mathbb{C}}\{g^{\sharp}\zeta\colon g\in\mathrm{G}\}\cong V_{\rho}
.

(4)

If
\zeta_{1}
,
\zeta_{2}
are highest weight functions of weights
\rho_{1}
and
\rho_{2}
, then
\nabla\zeta_{1}\cdot\nabla\zeta_{2}
and
\Delta\zeta_{i}
are highest weight functions of weights
\rho_{1}+\rho_{2}
and
\rho_{i}
, respectively.

For each isotypical summand
V_{\rho}^{\oplus m(\rho)}
, construct a basis
\mathcal{B}_{\rho}
of the set of highest weight functions of weight
\rho
. In practice, this amounts to finding a finite set
\mathcal{B}_{\rho}
of monomials
\zeta_{1}^{k_{1}}\cdots\zeta_{\ell}^{k_{\ell}}
that satisfies the following properties:

(1)

Each
F\in\mathcal{B}_{\rho}
has weight
\rho
.

(2)

\mathcal{B}_{\rho}
is linearly independent in the
\mathbb{C}
-vector space
L^{2}(M;\mathbb{C})
.

(3)

\dim_{\mathbb{C}}(\mathrm{span}_{\mathbb{C}}(\mathcal{B}_{\rho}))=m(\rho)
.

Having done this, Proposition
2.2
(c) now implies that

(2.1)

V_{\rho}^{\oplus m(\rho)}\cong\mathrm{span}_{\mathbb{C}}\{g^{\sharp}\mathcal{B}_{\rho}\colon g\in\mathrm{G}\}.

Step 3.
For each isotypical summand
V_{\rho}^{\oplus m(\rho)}
, compute the matrix representation of the Laplacian
\Delta|_{V_{\rho}^{\oplus m(\rho)}}\colon V_{\rho}^{\oplus m(\rho)}\to V_{\rho}^{\oplus m(\rho)}
. To do this, note that Proposition
2.2
(d) implies
\Delta(\operatorname{span}(\mathcal{B}_{\rho}))\subset\operatorname{span}(\mathcal{B}_{\rho})
. Therefore, in view of (
2.1
) and the
\mathrm{G}
-invariance
\Delta(g^{\sharp}F)=\Delta F
, it suffices to compute
\Delta
on the
m(\rho)
-dimensional subspace
\mathrm{span}_{\mathbb{C}}(\mathcal{B}_{\rho})\subset\mathrm{span}_{\mathbb{C}}\{g^{\sharp}\mathcal{B}_{\rho}\colon g\in\mathrm{G}\}
.
Computing
\Delta F
for each
F\in\mathcal{B}_{\rho}
, and expressing the result as a linear combination of functions in
\mathcal{B}_{\rho}
, yields a matrix representation of
\Delta
on the subspace
\mathrm{span}_{\mathbb{C}}(\mathcal{B}_{\rho})
.

Now, by the Laplacian product rule, calculating
\Delta F=\Delta(\zeta_{1}^{k_{1}}\cdots\zeta_{\ell}^{k_{\ell}})
requires the calculation of the Laplacians
\Delta\zeta_{i}
and the pairwise dot products
\nabla\zeta_{i}\cdot\nabla\zeta_{j}
. The difficulty of this computation depends on the complexity of
M
and of the functions
\zeta_{i}
, which can in general have lengthy polynomial expressions.

Step 4.
For each isotypical summand
V_{\rho}^{\oplus m(\rho)}
of
L^{2}(M;\mathbb{C})
, the result of Step 3 allows us to extract the eigenvalues of
\Delta|_{V_{\rho}^{\oplus m(\rho)}}
. Each such eigenvalue has multiplicity
\dim_{\mathbb{C}}(V_{\rho})
. So, to compute the Morse index of
M^{n-1}\subset S^{n}
, we require an exhaustive list of those summands housing
-\Delta
eigenvalues that are strictly less than
g(n-1)
.

Now, Theorem
2.3
provides a lower bound on the
-\Delta
eigenvalues on a given
V_{\rho}^{\oplus m(\rho)}
. Therefore, if an isotypical summand
V_{\rho}^{\oplus m(\rho)}
contributes to the Morse index, then its corresponding lower bound cannot exceed
g(n-1)
. This criterion yields a finite list of candidate summands
V_{\rho}^{\oplus m(\rho)}
, thereby reducing the calculation of the index to a straightforward linear algebra computation.

2.2.
Eigenvalue bounds

Let
N=\mathrm{G}/\mathrm{H}
be a homogeneous space with
\mathrm{G}
compact. Let
g_{1}
and
g_{2}
be two
\mathrm{G}
-invariant metrics on
N
and suppose
V
is a finite-dimensional subspace of
L^{2}(N)
invariant under both Laplacians
\Delta_{g_{1}},\Delta_{g_{2}}.

Let
r_{\mathrm{min}}
and
r_{\mathrm{max}}
denote the minimum and maximum eigenvalues of
g^{-1}_{1}
with respect to
g^{-1}_{2},
and let
\mu_{\mathrm{min}}
and
\mu_{\mathrm{max}}
denote the minimum and maximum eigenvalues of
-\Delta_{g_{2}}
on
V.

Theorem 2.3
.

Let
\lambda
be an eigenvalue of
-\Delta_{g_{1}}
on
V
. We have the following bounds:

r_{\mathrm{min}}\mu_{\mathrm{min}}\leq\lambda\leq r_{\mathrm{max}}\mu_{\mathrm{max}}

Proof.

The minimum and maximum eigenvalues of
-\Delta_{g_{2}}
are characterized in terms of the Rayleigh quotient by

\displaystyle\mu_{\mathrm{min}}

\displaystyle=\min_{f\in V}\frac{(-\Delta f,f)}{(f,f)}=\min_{f\in V}\frac{\int_{N}g_{2}(df,df)\,\mathrm{vol}_{g_{2}}}{\int_{N}f^{2}\,\mathrm{vol}_{g_{2}}},

\displaystyle\mu_{\mathrm{max}}

\displaystyle=\max_{f\in V}\frac{(-\Delta f,f)}{(f,f)}=\max_{f\in V}\frac{\int_{N}g_{2}(df,df)\,\mathrm{vol}_{g_{2}}}{\int_{N}f^{2}\,\mathrm{vol}_{g_{2}}}.

Let
f
be a function on
N
. We have

r_{\mathrm{min}}g_{2}(df,df)\leq g_{1}(df,df)\leq r_{\mathrm{max}}g_{2}(df,df).

We may integrate this inequality on
M
to obtain

\displaystyle r_{\mathrm{min}}\mu_{\mathrm{min}}\leq

\displaystyle r_{\mathrm{min}}\frac{\int_{N}g_{2}(df,df)\,\mathrm{vol}_{g_{2}}}{\int_{N}f^{2}\,\mathrm{vol}_{g_{2}}}\leq\frac{\int_{N}g_{1}(df,df)\,\mathrm{vol}_{g_{2}}}{\int_{N}f^{2}\,\mathrm{vol}_{g_{2}}}

\displaystyle\leq r_{\mathrm{max}}\frac{\int_{N}g_{2}(df,df)\,\mathrm{vol}_{g_{2}}}{\int_{N}f^{2}\,\mathrm{vol}_{g_{2}}}\leq r_{\mathrm{max}}\mu_{\mathrm{max}}.

for any function
f\in V.
The volume forms
\mathrm{vol}_{g_{1}}
and
\mathrm{vol}_{g_{2}}
differ by a positive contant, so we have

\frac{\int_{N}g_{1}(df,df)\,\mathrm{vol}_{g_{2}}}{\int_{N}f^{2}\,\mathrm{vol}_{g_{2}}}=\frac{\int_{N}g_{1}(df,df)\,\mathrm{vol}_{g_{1}}}{\int_{N}f^{2}\,\mathrm{vol}_{g_{1}}}.

To complete the proof, we note that any eigenvalue
\lambda
of
-\Delta_{g_{1}}
must lie in the set

\left\{\left.\frac{\int_{N}g_{1}(df,df)\,\mathrm{vol}_{g_{1}}}{\int_{N}f^{2}\,\mathrm{vol}_{g_{1}}}\right\rvert f\in V\right\}.\qed

Remark 2.4
.

In applications of this result,
g_{2}
will be a normal homogeneous metric on
N
, and
V
will be an isotypical component of
L^{2}(N)
corresponding to some irreducible
\mathrm{G}
-representation
W
. The Laplacian of the normal metric on this isotypical component is equal to
-\mu(W)\mathrm{Id},
where
\mu(W)
is the eigenvalue of the Casimir operator of
\mathrm{G}
on
W
. In particular, we have
\mu_{\mathrm{min}}=\mu(W)=\mu_{\mathrm{max}}.

2.3.
Moving frames

2.3.1.
The ambient sphere

We will be interested in hypersurfaces in the unit
n
-sphere
S^{n}=\{X\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}\colon|X|^{2}=1\}
. Note that
\mathrm{SO}(n+1)
acts transitively on
S^{n}
with stabilizer
\mathrm{SO}(n)
. Let
\mathbf{g}\colon\mathrm{SO}(n+1)\to\mathrm{M}_{n+1}(\mathbb{R})
denote the obvious embedding into the set of
n\times n
matrices, and write

\mathbf{g}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{x}&\mathbf{e}_{1}&\cdots&\mathbf{e}_{n}\end{bmatrix}.

We view the columns
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{e}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{e}_{n}\colon\mathrm{SO}(n+1)\to S^{n}
as vector-valued functions, and the map
\mathbf{x}\colon\mathrm{SO}(n+1)\to S^{n}
as a principal
\mathrm{SO}(n)
-bundle. In fact,
\mathrm{SO}(n+1)\cong F_{\mathrm{SO}(n)}S^{n}
can be identified with the oriented orthonormal frame bundle of
S^{n}
.

For computations, let
\eta\in\Omega^{1}(\mathrm{SO}(n+1);\mathfrak{so}(n+1))
denote the Maurer-Cartan form, so that
\eta=\mathbf{g}^{-1}d\mathbf{g}
. As a matrix-valued
1
-form, we write

\eta=\begin{bmatrix}0&-\alpha_{i}\\
\alpha_{i}&\beta_{ij}\end{bmatrix}

where
\beta_{ij}=-\beta_{ji}
. Geometrically, the round metric
g_{\mathrm{rd}}
on
S^{n}
pulls back to

\mathbf{x}^{*}g_{\mathrm{rd}}=\alpha_{1}^{2}+\cdots+\alpha_{n}^{2},

and its Levi-Civita connection form is the matrix-valued
1
-form

\Lambda^{2}(\mathbb{C}^{10})\cong V_{1,2}\oplus V_{0,2}
, we deduce the result.
∎

Next, consider the quadratic function

\displaystyle q\colon\mathfrak{so}(5;\mathbb{C})\cong\Lambda^{2}(\mathbb{C}^{5})

\displaystyle\to\mathbb{C}^{5}

\displaystyle q(X)

\displaystyle:=\ast(X\wedge X).

Note that
|q(X)|^{2}=0
if and only if
X
is a decomposable
2
-form. Explicitly, for an element
x_{i}Y_{i}\in\mathfrak{so}(5;\mathbb{C})\cong\Lambda^{2}(\mathbb{C}^{5})
, we have

(3.6)

q(x_{i}Y_{i})=\begin{bmatrix}2\,x_{{1}}x_{{2}}-{x_{{7}}}^{2}-{x_{{8}}}^{2}+{x_{{9}}}^{2}+{x_{{10}}}^{2}\\
\sqrt{2}x_{{5}}x_{{7}}-\sqrt{2}x_{{5}}x_{{9}}+\sqrt{2}x_{{6}}x_{{8}}-\sqrt{2}x_{{6}}x_{{10}}-2\,x_{{2}}x_{{4}}\\
-\sqrt{2}x_{{5}}x_{{8}}-\sqrt{2}x_{{5}}x_{{10}}+\sqrt{2}x_{{6}}x_{{7}}+\sqrt{2}x_{{6}}x_{{9}}+2\,x_{{2}}x_{{3}}\\
-\sqrt{2}x_{{3}}x_{{7}}+\sqrt{2}x_{{3}}x_{{9}}+\sqrt{2}x_{{4}}x_{{8}}+\sqrt{2}x_{{4}}x_{{10}}-2\,x_{{6}}x_{{1}}\\

Finally, define an quadratic map

\displaystyle s\colon\mathfrak{so}(5;\mathbb{C})

\displaystyle\to\mathrm{Sym}^{2}_{0}(\mathbb{C}^{5})

\displaystyle s(X)

\displaystyle=\textstyle X^{2}-\frac{1}{5}\mathrm{tr}(X^{2})\,\mathrm{Id}_{5}

where
\mathrm{Id}_{5}
is the
5\times 5
identity matrix.

Summary:
We now have five natural
\mathrm{SO}(5)
-equivariant functions on

:

(1)

The complexified position function
\mathbf{x}\colon M\to V_{0,2}
.

(2)

The complexified normal vector
\mathbf{n}\colon M\to V_{0,2}
.

(3)

The wedge product
\mathbf{x}\wedge\mathbf{n}\colon M\to V_{1,2}
.

(4)

.

(5)

The quadratic function

.

Remark 3.3
.

It turns out that the first four of these yield eigenfunctions because

\displaystyle\Delta\mathbf{x}

\displaystyle=-8\,\mathbf{x}

\displaystyle\Delta(\mathbf{x}\wedge\mathbf{n})

\displaystyle=-32\,(\mathbf{x}\wedge\mathbf{n})

\displaystyle\Delta\mathbf{n}

\displaystyle=-24\,\mathbf{n}

\displaystyle\Delta q(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=-16\,q(\mathbf{x}).

This was observed in
[

,

]
, and can also be verified using the structure equations (
2.2
). By contrast, using (
2.2
), one can compute

\Delta\!\begin{bmatrix}s(\mathbf{x})\\

-14&-46\end{pmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}s(\mathbf{x})\\

Upon diagonalizing this matrix, one finds that the components of

are eigenfunctions with eigenvalue

.

3.4.
Decomposition of isotypical summands (Step 2b)

Recalling the maps
\pi_{k,\ell}\colon V_{k,\ell}\to\mathbb{C}
in (
3.4
), we define the following five functions
M\to\mathbb{C}
:

\displaystyle\kappa

\displaystyle:=\pi_{1,0}\circ q(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle\zeta

\displaystyle:=\pi_{0,2}\circ\mathbf{x}

\displaystyle\rho

\displaystyle:=\pi_{1,2}\circ(\mathbf{x}\wedge\mathbf{n})

\displaystyle\sigma

\displaystyle:=\pi_{2,0}\circ s(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle\nu

\displaystyle:=\pi_{0,2}\circ\mathbf{n}.

Explicitly, from the expressions in (
3.4
), we have:

(3.7)

\displaystyle\zeta(x_{i}Y_{i})

\displaystyle=x_{9}+ix_{10},

\displaystyle\kappa(x_{i}Y_{i})

\displaystyle=\sqrt{2}x_{{5}}x_{{7}}-\sqrt{2}x_{{5}}x_{{9}}+\sqrt{2}x_{{6}}x_{{8}}-\sqrt{2}x_{{6}}x_{{10}}-2x_{{2}}x_{{4}}

\displaystyle+i\!\left(-\sqrt{2}x_{{5}}x_{{8}}-\sqrt{2}x_{{5}}x_{{10}}+\sqrt{2}x_{{6}}x_{{7}}+\sqrt{2}x_{{6}}x_{{9}}+2x_{{2}}x_{{3}}\right)\!,

\displaystyle\sigma(x_{i}Y_{i})

\displaystyle=-x_{3}^{2}+x_{4}^{2}-2x_{7}x_{9}-2x_{8}x_{10}+i\!\left(-2x_{3}x_{4}-2x_{7}x_{10}+2x_{8}x_{9}\right)\!,

\displaystyle\nu(x_{i}Y_{i})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}\left(\frac{\partial F}{\partial x_{9}}+i\frac{\partial F}{\partial x_{10}}\right)\!.

\displaystyle\rho(x_{i}Y_{i})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}(x_{9}+ix_{10})\left(\frac{\partial F}{\partial x_{3}}+i\frac{\partial F}{\partial x_{4}}\right)-\frac{1}{4}\left(\frac{\partial F}{\partial x_{9}}+i\frac{\partial F}{\partial x_{10}}\right)(x_{3}+ix_{4}).

Now, for
u,v,x,y,z\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
, we define

U_{u,v,x,y,z}:=\mathrm{span}_{\mathbb{C}}\!\left\{g^{\#}(\zeta^{u}\kappa^{v}\nu^{x}\rho^{y}\sigma^{z})\colon g\in\mathrm{SO}(5)\right\}\subset L^{2}(M;\mathbb{C}),

where
g^{\#}
denotes the
\mathrm{SO}(5)
-action on
L^{2}(M;\mathbb{C})
discussed in
\lx@sectionsign
2.1
. By Proposition
2.2
(c), we have an isomorphism of
\mathrm{SO}(5)
-modules

U_{u,v,x,y,z}\cong V_{v+y+2z,\,2(u+x+y)}.

We will now prove that, for example,

\displaystyle V_{2,0}^{\oplus m(2,0)}

\displaystyle\cong U_{0,2,0,0,0}+U_{0,0,0,0,1}=\mathrm{span}_{\mathbb{C}}\{g^{\sharp}(\kappa^{2}),\,g^{\sharp}(\sigma)\colon g\in\mathrm{SO}(5)\}

\displaystyle V_{1,2}^{\oplus m(1,2)}

\displaystyle\cong U_{0,0,0,1,0}+U_{1,1,0,0,0}+U_{0,1,1,0,0}=\mathrm{span}_{\mathbb{C}}\{g^{\sharp}(\rho),\,g^{\sharp}(\zeta\kappa),\,g^{\sharp}(\kappa\nu)\colon g\in\mathrm{SO}(5)\}

More generally:

Proposition 3.4
.

Define

W(k,\ell):=\mathrm{span}\left\{U_{u,v,x,y,z}\colon u,v,x,y,z\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0},\ y\in\{0,1\},\ v+y+2z=k,\ u+x+y=\ell\right\}\!.

Then

V_{k,2\ell}^{\oplus m(k,2\ell)}\cong W(k,\ell).

Consequently,

L^{2}(M;\mathbb{C})=\bigoplus_{k,\ell\geq 0}W(k,\ell).

Proposition
3.4
is a consequence of the following two lemmas.

Lemma 3.5
.

Fix
k
and let

\displaystyle f_{i}(t)

\displaystyle=(\sin t)^{k-2i}(\cos t)^{2i},

\displaystyle g_{i}(t)

\displaystyle=(\sin t)^{k-1-2i}(\cos t)^{2i+1}.

Then the set of functions
\{f_{0},\ldots,f_{\lfloor k/2\rfloor}\}\cup\{g_{0},\ldots,g_{\lfloor(k-1)/2\rfloor}\}
is linearly independent.

Proof.

If
k
is even, each
f_{i}
is even and each
g_{i}
is odd, and if
k
is odd, each
f_{i}
is odd and each
g_{i}
is even, so it suffices to prove that the sets
\{f_{i}(t)\}
and
\{g_{i}(t)\}
are individually linearly independent. For this, note that

\displaystyle f_{i}+f_{i+1}

\displaystyle=(\sin t)^{k-2i}(\cos t)^{2i}+(\sin t)^{k-2i-2}(\cos t)^{2i+2}=(\sin t)^{k-2i-2}(\cos t)^{2i},

\displaystyle g_{i}+g_{i+1}

\displaystyle=(\sin t)^{k-1-2i}(\cos t)^{2i+1}+(\sin t)^{k-3-2i}(\cos t)^{2i+3}=(\sin t)^{k-2i-3}(\cos t)^{2i+1},

so that

\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{p}\binom{p}{i}f_{i}(t)

\displaystyle=(\sin t)^{k-2p}

\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{p}\binom{p}{i}g_{i}(t)

\displaystyle=(\sin t)^{k-1-2p}(\cos t).

Since the sets
\{(\sin t)^{k-2p}\colon 0\leq p\leq\lfloor k/2\rfloor\}
and
\{(\sin t)^{k-1-2p}(\cos t)\colon 0\leq p\leq\lfloor(k-1)/2\rfloor\}
are linearly independent, it follows that
\{f_{i}(t)\}
and
\{g_{i}(t)\}
are, too.
∎

Define the following subsets of
W(k,\ell)
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}_{k,\ell}

\displaystyle:=\left\{\kappa^{k-2i}\sigma^{i}\zeta^{\ell-j}\nu^{j}\colon 0\leq i\leq\lfloor k/2\rfloor,\,0\leq j\leq\ell\right\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}_{k,\ell}

\displaystyle:=\left\{\rho\kappa^{k-1-2i}\sigma^{i}\zeta^{\ell-1-j}\nu^{j}\colon 0\leq i\leq\lfloor(k-1)/2\rfloor,\,0\leq j\leq\ell-1\right\}.

Lemma 3.6
.

The set
\mathcal{U}_{k,\ell}\cup\mathcal{V}_{k,\ell}
is linearly independent. Moreover,

\dim(\mathrm{span}\{\mathcal{U}_{k,\ell}\cup\mathcal{V}_{k,\ell}\})=m(k,2\ell).

Proof.

We will argue by induction, reasoning as follows. Let
T(k,l)
be the statement

“
\mathcal{U}_{k,l}\cup\mathcal{V}_{k,l}
is linearly independent.”

The base case
T(0,0)
is trivial (note that
\mathcal{V}_{0,0}
is empty). We will first prove
T(k,\ell-1)\implies T(k,\ell)
for
\ell>0
. Then we will prove both
T(2r,0)\implies T(2r+1,0)
and
T(2r,0)\implies T(2r+2,0)
for
r>0
.

Before beginning, note that the 1-parameter family of points

p_{t}=2^{-1/4}\left(\cos t\,Y_{3}-\sin t\,Y_{5}\right)+\tfrac{1}{2}(4-2\sqrt{2})^{1/2}Y_{7}\in\mathfrak{so}(5)\simeq\mathbb{R}^{10}

satisfy
F(p_{t})=0
and hence are all elements of
M.
Moreover,

\displaystyle\zeta(p_{t})

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle\nu(p_{t})

\displaystyle=-2^{-1/2}(4-2\sqrt{2})^{1/2},

\displaystyle\kappa(p_{t})

\displaystyle=-2^{-3/4}(4-2\sqrt{2})^{1/2}\sin t,

\displaystyle\sigma(p_{t})

\displaystyle=-\sqrt{2}\cos^{2}(t)

\displaystyle\rho(p_{t})

\displaystyle=2^{-3/4}(4-2\sqrt{2})^{1/2}\cos t.

First, fix
\ell>0
and suppose that we have a linear relation in
\mathcal{U}_{k,\ell}\cup\mathcal{V}_{k,\ell}
written as

(3.8)

\sum_{i}\sum_{j}a_{ij}\kappa^{k-2i}\sigma^{i}\zeta^{\ell-j}\nu^{j}+\sum_{i}\sum_{j}b_{ij}\rho\kappa^{k-1-2i}\sigma^{i}\zeta^{\ell-1-j}\nu^{j}=0.

Evaluating at
p_{t}
and absorbing constants into
a_{ij},b_{ij}
, we get

\sum_{i}a^{\prime}_{i\ell}\left(\sin t\right)^{k-2i}\left(\cos t\right)^{2i}+\sum_{i}b^{\prime}_{i,\ell-1}\left(\sin t\right)^{k-1-2i}\left(\cos t\right)^{2i+1}=0,

where the
a^{\prime}_{i\ell}
and
b^{\prime}_{i,\ell-1}
are obtained from
a_{i\ell}
and
b_{i,\ell-1}
by rescaling by some non-zero constant (which will depend on
i
,
k
,
\ell
). Now, by Lemma
3.5
, the set of functions

\{\left(\sin t\right)^{k-2i}\left(\cos t\right)^{2i}\mid 0\leq i\leq\lfloor k/2\rfloor\}\cup\{\left(\sin t\right)^{k-1-2i}\left(\cos t\right)^{2i+1}\mid 0\leq i\leq\lfloor(k-1)/2\rfloor\}

is linearly independent. Therefore,
a^{\prime}_{i\ell}
and
b^{\prime}_{i,\ell-1}
must vanish for all
i
, which implies
a_{i\ell}
and
b_{i,\ell-1}
vanish for all
i
, too. Consequently, the left-hand side of (
3.8
) is of the form
\zeta F
, where
F\in\mathrm{span}\{\mathcal{U}_{k,\ell-1}\cup\mathcal{V}_{k,\ell-1}\}
. Since
\zeta
is not the zero function, we obtain
F=0
. Therefore, linear independence of
\mathcal{U}_{k,\ell-1}\cup\mathcal{V}_{k,\ell-1}
implies linear independence of
\mathcal{U}_{k,\ell}\cup\mathcal{V}_{k,\ell}
.

Next, we show that
\mathcal{U}_{k,0}\cup\mathcal{V}_{k,0}
is linearly independent. In fact,
\mathcal{V}_{k,0}=\varnothing,
so we need to show
\mathcal{U}_{k,0}
is linearly independent. In the case where
k=2r+1
is odd, any linear relation in
\mathcal{U}_{2r+1,0}
is of the form
\kappa F=0
, where
F\in\mathrm{span}\{\mathcal{U}_{2r,0}\}
. Thus, linear independence of
\mathcal{U}_{2r,0}
implies that of
\mathcal{U}_{2r+1,0}
. In the case where
k=2r
is even, consider a linear relation in
\mathcal{U}_{2r,0}
, which we may write as

\sum_{i=0}^{r}a_{i}\kappa^{2r-2i}\sigma^{i}=0.

Evaluating at the point
p_{0}
, we have
a_{r}=0,
so the linear relation is divisible by
\kappa^{2}
. Thus, linear independence of
\mathcal{U}_{2r-2,0}
implies linear independence of
\mathcal{U}_{2r,0}
.

Finally, we establish the last claim. For this, we note that the number of elements in
\mathcal{U}_{k,\ell}\cup\mathcal{V}_{k,\ell}
is

\displaystyle\textstyle\left(\left\lfloor\frac{k}{2}\right\rfloor+1\right)(\ell+1)+\left(\left\lfloor\frac{k-1}{2}\right\rfloor+1\right)\ell

\displaystyle\textstyle=\ell\left(\left\lfloor\frac{k}{2}\right\rfloor+\left\lfloor\frac{k-1}{2}\right\rfloor+2\right)+\left\lfloor\frac{k}{2}\right\rfloor+1

\displaystyle\textstyle=\ell(k+1)+\left\lfloor\frac{k}{2}\right\rfloor+1

\displaystyle=m(k,2\ell).

∎

Lemma
3.6
implies that

W(k,\ell)=\mathrm{span}\{g^{\sharp}(\mathcal{U}_{k,\ell}\cup\mathcal{V}_{k,\ell})\colon g\in\mathrm{SO}(5)\}\cong V_{k,2\ell}^{\oplus m(k,2\ell)},

thereby establishing Proposition
3.4
.

3.5.
Computing
\Delta_{M}
on isotypical summands (Step 3)

For fixed
k,\ell\geq 0
, define functions

\displaystyle F_{i}

\displaystyle=\kappa^{k-2i}\sigma^{i},\ \ \ 0\leq i\leq\lfloor k/2\rfloor,

\displaystyle G_{j}

\displaystyle=\zeta^{\ell-j}\nu^{j},\ \ \ \ 0\leq j\leq\ell.

Note that, although it is not made clear in the notation,
F_{i}
and
G_{j}
depend on
k
and
\ell
respectively.
By definition of
\mathcal{U}_{k,\ell}
and
\mathcal{V}_{k+1,\ell+1},

\displaystyle\{F_{i}G_{j}\colon 0\leq i\leq\lfloor k/2\rfloor,0\leq j\leq\ell\}\text{ is a basis of }\mathrm{span}(\mathcal{U}_{k,\ell}),\text{ and}

\displaystyle\{\rho F_{i}G_{j}\colon 0\leq i\leq\lfloor(k-1)/2\rfloor,0\leq j\leq\ell-1\}\text{ is a basis of }\mathrm{span}(\mathcal{V}_{k+1,\ell+1}).

The purpose of this section is to compute the Laplacians
\Delta(F_{i}G_{j})
and
\Delta(\rho F_{i}G_{j})
. Recalling our convention that the Laplacian is negative semi-definite, the product rule gives

(3.9)

\displaystyle\Delta(F_{i}G_{j})

\displaystyle=F_{i}\Delta G_{j}+2\nabla F_{i}\cdot\nabla G_{j}+G_{j}\Delta F_{i}

(3.10)

\displaystyle\Delta(\rho F_{i}G_{j})

\displaystyle=\rho\Delta(F_{i}G_{j})+2\nabla\rho\cdot\nabla(F_{i}G_{j})+F_{i}G_{j}\Delta\rho.

Thus, repeatedly applying the product rule, we see that we require the pairwise dot products of the gradients
\nabla\zeta
,
\nabla\kappa
, etc., as well as the Laplacians
\Delta\zeta
,
\Delta\kappa
, etc. These are given by:

Proposition 3.7
.

We have

\displaystyle\nabla\zeta\cdot\nabla\zeta

\displaystyle=-\zeta^{2}-\nu^{2}

\displaystyle\nabla\kappa\cdot\nabla\kappa

\displaystyle=-8\kappa^{2}+4\sigma

\displaystyle\nabla\zeta\cdot\nabla\nu

\displaystyle=2\zeta^{2}-6\zeta\nu

\displaystyle\nabla\kappa\cdot\nabla\sigma

\displaystyle=-4\kappa^{3}

\displaystyle\nabla\nu\cdot\nabla\nu

\displaystyle=3\zeta^{2}-4\zeta\nu-9\nu^{2}

\displaystyle\nabla\sigma\cdot\nabla\sigma

\displaystyle=-4\kappa^{4}+8\kappa^{2}\sigma-8\sigma^{2}

and

\displaystyle\nabla\zeta\cdot\nabla\rho

\displaystyle=-4\zeta\rho,

\displaystyle\nabla\zeta\cdot\nabla\kappa

\displaystyle=-2\zeta\kappa-2\nu\kappa

\displaystyle\nabla\nu\cdot\nabla\rho

\displaystyle=-12\nu\rho

\displaystyle\nabla\zeta\cdot\nabla\sigma

\displaystyle=-2\zeta\sigma-2\nu\kappa^{2}+2\nu\sigma

\displaystyle\nabla\kappa\cdot\nabla\rho

\displaystyle=-8\kappa\rho

\displaystyle\nabla\nu\cdot\nabla\kappa

\displaystyle=-2\zeta\kappa-6\nu\kappa

\displaystyle\nabla\sigma\cdot\nabla\rho

\displaystyle=-8\sigma\rho

\displaystyle\nabla\nu\cdot\nabla\sigma

\displaystyle=-6\zeta\kappa^{2}+2\zeta\sigma-6\nu\sigma

and

\displaystyle\Delta\zeta

\displaystyle=-8\zeta

\displaystyle\Delta\kappa

\displaystyle=-16\kappa

\displaystyle\Delta\rho

\displaystyle=-32\rho

\displaystyle\Delta\nu

\displaystyle=-24\nu

\displaystyle\Delta\sigma

\displaystyle=-4\kappa^{2}-16\sigma.

Proof.

Suppose
\mu_{1},\mu_{2}\in\left\{\zeta,\nu,\rho,\kappa,\sigma\right\}
have weights
w_{1},w_{2}
respectively. We know by Proposition
2.2
(d) that
\nabla\mu_{1}\cdot\nabla\mu_{2}
is a highest weight function of weight
w_{1}+w_{2}
. We have a basis for the space of highest weight functions for a given weight, so
\nabla\mu_{1}\cdot\nabla\mu_{2}
can be expressed uniquely as a linear combination of elements of this basis. On the other hand, using (
2.2
), the explicit formulas of (
3.7
), and the technique described in
\lx@sectionsign
2.3.3

\nabla\mu_{1}\cdot\nabla\mu_{2}
in terms of the components of an adapted frame
\left(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{e}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{e}_{9}\right).
By evaluating this expression on a sufficiently general circle of points in
M,
we may determine the coefficients of the basis expansion of
\nabla\mu_{1}\cdot\nabla\mu_{2}.
The calculation of the Laplacians works similarly.

We illustrate our technique on
\nabla\zeta\cdot\nabla\kappa.
Let
\mathbf{f}\in\psi_{\pi/8}\left(\mathrm{SO}(5)\right)
be the frame given in (

), and let
A\in\mathrm{SO}(5)
be the matrix

A=\left[\begin{array}[]{ccccc}\frac{1}{2}\,\sqrt{2}&0&0&0&\frac{1}{2}\,\sqrt{2}\\
\vskip 6.0pt plus 2.0pt minus 2.0pt\cr 0&\frac{1}{2}&0&-\frac{1}{2}\,\sqrt{3}&0\\
\vskip 6.0pt plus 2.0pt minus 2.0pt\cr 0&0&1&0&0\\
\vskip 6.0pt plus 2.0pt minus 2.0pt\cr 0&\frac{1}{2}\,\sqrt{3}&0&\frac{1}{2}&0\\
\vskip 6.0pt plus 2.0pt minus 2.0pt\cr-\frac{1}{2}\,\sqrt{2}&0&0&0&\frac{1}{2}\,\sqrt{2}\end{array}\right].

Then the set of elements
\mathrm{Ad}(\exp(tY_{3})A)\mathbf{f}\in\mathrm{SO}(10),

gives a circle of points in
M
together with an adapted frame at each point. On this circle we have

(3.11)

\displaystyle\zeta

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{4}\left(-i\sqrt{3}\cos\!\left(t\right)+\sin\!\left(t\right)\right)\sin\!\left(\tfrac{1}{8}\pi\right)-\tfrac{i\sqrt{6}}{4}\cos\!\left(\tfrac{1}{8}\pi\right),

\displaystyle\nu

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{4}\left(-i\sqrt{3}\cos\!\left(t\right)+\sin\!\left(t\right)\right)\sin\!\left(\tfrac{3}{8}\pi\right)+\tfrac{i\sqrt{6}}{4}\sin\!\left(\tfrac{1}{8}\pi\right),

\displaystyle\rho

\displaystyle=-\tfrac{1}{8}\sqrt{3}\,\sin\!\left(t\right)\cos\!\left(t\right)+\tfrac{1}{8}i\cos^{2}\!\left(t\right)-\tfrac{1}{2}i,

\displaystyle\sigma

\displaystyle=-\tfrac{1}{4}\cos^{2}\!\left(t\right)+\tfrac{3}{8}\,\sqrt{2}+\tfrac{1}{4},

and we may compute

(3.12)

Comparing (

The other formulas are calculated in a similar fashion.
∎

Proposition 3.8
.

We have

\displaystyle\Delta(F_{i}G_{j})

\displaystyle=-4i(i-1)F_{i-2}G_{j}-12ijF_{i-1}G_{j-1}-4i(2k+3-6i)F_{i-1}G_{j}-4i(\ell-j)F_{i-1}G_{j+1}

\displaystyle+3j(j-1)F_{i}G_{j-2}+4j(\ell-k+3i-2j+1)F_{i}G_{j-1}-4(\ell-j)(k-3i)F_{i}G_{j+1}

\displaystyle-(\ell-j)(\ell-j-1)F_{i}G_{j+2}+4(k-2i)(k-2i-1)F_{i+1}G_{j}

\displaystyle+(-40i^{2}+8ij+32ik+4i\ell+2j^{2}-8kj-10\ell j-8k^{2}-4k\ell-\ell^{2}+8i-8j-8k-7\ell)F_{i}G_{j}

and

\displaystyle\Delta(\rho F_{i}G_{j})

\displaystyle=\rho\Delta(F_{i}G_{j})-(32+16k-16i+8\ell+16j)\rho F_{i}G_{j}.

Proof.

Using Proposition
3.7
, we calculate

(3.13)

\displaystyle\Delta F_{i}

\displaystyle=-4i(i-1)F_{i-2}-4i\left(2k+3-6i\right)F_{i-1}

\displaystyle\ \ \ \ +\left(-40i^{2}+32ik+8i-8k^{2}-8k\right)F_{i}+4(k-2i)(k-2i-1)F_{i+1}

\displaystyle\Delta G_{j}

\displaystyle=3j(j-1)G_{j-2}+4j\left(\ell-2j+1\right)G_{j-1}

\displaystyle+\left(2j^{2}-10j\ell-8j-\ell^{2}-7\ell\right)G_{j}-(\ell-j)(\ell-j-1)G_{j+2}

\displaystyle\nabla F_{i}

\displaystyle=i\kappa^{k-2i}\sigma^{i-1}\nabla\sigma+(k-2i)\kappa^{k-2i-1}\sigma^{i}\nabla\kappa

\displaystyle=j\zeta^{\ell-j}\nu^{j-1}\nabla\nu+(\ell-j)\zeta^{\ell-j-1}\nu^{j}\nabla\zeta

Taking their dot product, and applying Proposition

(3.15)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{\lfloor p/2\rfloor}(2i+1)=\left(\lfloor p/2\rfloor+1\right)^{2},

\displaystyle\dim E^{-}_{p}

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{2}(p+1)(p+2)-\dim E^{+}_{p}=\tfrac{1}{2}(p+1)(p+2)-\left(\lfloor p/2\rfloor+1\right)^{2}.

Since the contraction
\mathrm{Sym}^{p}(\mathbb{C}^{3})\to\mathrm{Sym}^{p-2}(\mathbb{C}^{3})
is surjective with kernel
\mathcal{H}_{p}
, there is a decomposition
\mathrm{Sym}^{p}(\mathbb{C}^{3})\cong\mathcal{H}_{p}\oplus\mathrm{Sym}^{p-2}(\mathbb{C}^{3})
, and the action of
\varepsilon
preserves this splitting. Consequently, the dimensions of the eigenspaces of the
\varepsilon
-action on
\mathcal{H}_{p}
are
\dim E^{\pm}_{p}-\dim E^{\pm}_{p-2}
, and

\displaystyle\dim E^{+}_{p}-\dim E^{+}_{p-2}

\displaystyle=2\lfloor p/2\rfloor+1,

\displaystyle\dim E^{-}_{p}-\dim E^{-}_{p-2}

\displaystyle=2p-2\lfloor p/2\rfloor.

Therefore, if
p
and
q
are both even or both odd,
m(p,q)=p+1,
while if only one of
p
and
q
is even,
m(p,q)=p.

∎

Appendix: Representation theory

Let
\mathrm{G}
be a compact, connected Lie group with Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
and let
(V,\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle)
be a
hermitian vector space with a unitary
\mathrm{G}
-representation, i.e. a group homomorphism
\varphi:\mathrm{G}\rightarrow\mathrm{Aut}(V)
.
Any representation
V
of a compact Lie group
\mathrm{G}
is the direct sum of irreducible summands. Combining isomorphic summands we obtain
the decomposition of
V
into
isotypical components
, i.e. sums of irreducible summands all isomorphic to a fixed irreducible
\mathrm{G}
-representation.
The
multiplicity
of an irreducible
\mathrm{G}
-representation
W
in
V
is then the number of summands in the isotypical component defined by
W
.
It is given by the dimension of
\mathrm{Hom}_{\mathrm{G}}(V,W)
, the space of
\mathrm{G}
-equivariant homomorphisms from
V
to
W
.

We recall that any representation
(V,\varphi)
of a Lie group
\mathrm{G}
defines a representation of its Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
,
i.e. we have a Lie algebra homomorphism
\varphi_{*}:\mathfrak{g}\rightarrow\mathrm{End}(V)
, where
\varphi_{*}
is the differential of
the representation
\varphi
. This is a 1-1-correspondence in case of simply connected Lie groups.
A representation
(V,\varphi)
of a Lie group
\mathrm{G}
is also called a
\mathrm{G}
-module
. Then
\mathrm{G}
-invariant
subspaces of
V
are
\mathrm{G}
-submodules and
\mathrm{G}
-equivariant homomorphisms are
\mathrm{G}
-module morphisms.

We fix a maximal torus
T\subset\mathrm{G}
with Lie algebra
\mathfrak{t}\subset\mathfrak{g}
.
Then the restriction to the maximal torus defines a representation of
T
. Since
T
is abelian it decomposes into a sum of
1
-dimensional
complex representations, the simultaneous eigenspaces of the
T
action on
V
. The
weight vectors
of the

\mathrm{G}
-module
V
are the simultaneous eigenvectors of this restricted
T
-representation.
The
weights
of
V
are the corresponding eigenvalues considered as
1
-forms on the Lie algebra
\mathfrak{t}
.
They span a lattice in the Lie algebra
\mathfrak{t}^{*}
.
More explicitly, a vector
v\in V
is a weight vector of weight
\omega
if and only if
\varphi_{*}(X)v=2\pi i\omega(X)v

for all
X\in\mathfrak{t}
. A
weight space
is the subspace of weight vectors for a fixed weight. These are
exactly the isotypical components of the
T
-representation on
V
.

As an example we have the
roots
of the Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
, which are the non-zero weights of the
adjoint representation of
\mathrm{G}
on the complexification
\mathfrak{g}^{\mathbb{C}}
of its Lie algebra. Fixing a basis of simple roots distinguishes a Weyl chamber and defines a partial
ordering on the weight lattice. For the classical groups, in particular for
\mathrm{SO}(n)
, we will use the basis of
simple roots as given in Bourbaki
[
2
]
. In the latter case, identifying
\mathfrak{t}
with
\mathbb{R}^{[\frac{n}{2}]}
, we obtain
the lexicographic order on
\mathbb{R}^{[\frac{n}{2}]}
.

By the
Theorem of the highest weight
any irreducible unitary
\mathrm{G}
-module has a
highest weight
,
characterizing the
\mathrm{G}
-module up to equivalence, i.e. up to
\mathrm{G}
-module isomorphisms. The corresponding weight space is
1
-dimensional, i.e.
the corresponding highest weight vector is unique up to scalar multiples. In this sense irreducible unitary
\mathrm{G}
-module are
parametrized by their highest weights and we will write
V=V_{\rho}
if the
V
has highest weight
\rho
.
The set of all equivalence classes of irreducible, finite-dimensional unitary
\mathrm{G}
-modules is denoted as
\hat{\mathrm{G}}

and, identifying irreducible unitary
G
-module with their highest weight, we will also write
\rho\in\hat{\mathrm{G}}
.

The
fundamental weights

\omega_{1},\ldots,\omega_{n}
are defined as a basis of
\mathfrak{t}
dual to the set of coroots associated to the simple roots.
That is, the fundamental weights are defined by the condition:

2\,\frac{\langle\omega_{i},\alpha_{j}\rangle}{\langle\alpha_{j},\alpha_{j}\rangle}\,=\,\delta_{ij},

where
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n}
are the simple roots. The fundamental representations, i.e. the corresponding
\mathrm{G}
-modules of highest
weights
\omega_{i},i=1,\ldots,n
, generate the representation ring of
\mathrm{G}
.

Finally, we mention the following facts about weights that we will need in this work:

(1)

Every
\mathrm{G}
-module has an orthogonal basis of weight vectors.

(2)

The span of the
\mathrm{G}
-orbit of a highest weight vector is irreducible.

(3)

The tensor product
V_{\rho}\otimes V_{\mu}
contains
V_{\rho+\mu}
as
\mathrm{G}
-submodule of multiplicity one.
The corresponding highest weight vector is exactly the tensor product of the two highest weight vectors of the two factors.
