Abstract
. We study the rational solutions of the Abel equation x ′ = A ⁡ ( t ) ​ x 3 + B ⁡ ( t ) ​ x 2 x^{\prime}=A(t)x^{3}+B(t)x^{2} where A , B ∈ ℂ ⁡ [ t ] A,B\in\mathbb{C}[t] . 1.
Introduction

The Abel differential equation

(1.1)

x^{\prime}=A(t)x^{3}+B(t)x^{2},

where
A,B
are polynomials or trigonometric polynomials, has been extensively studied for its own intrinsic interest
[
10
]
and
for its relationship with some real-world phenomena (see e.g.
[
2
]
and references therein), but also as a toy model
for studying important problems in the qualitative theory of differential equations.

One of these problems is the Smale-Pugh problem
[
21
]
of bounding the number of limit cycles (closed solutions isolated in the set of closed solutions),
which can be seen as a special case of Hilbert’s 16th problem. Lins-Neto
[
14
]

proved that there is no upper bound on the number of limit cycles of (
1.1
)
for
A,B
polynomials or trigonometric polynomials.

Another important problem is Poincaré Centre-Focus Problem which, in this setting,
asks when the solutions of (
1.1
) in a neighbourhood of the solution
x(t)\equiv 0

are all closed. This problem for (
1.1
) was proposed by Briskin, Françoise, and
Yondim
[
4
,
5
]
. A special case when this holds
is when the composition condition holds, as will be detailed later.
This condition seems to play a key role in the problem, as shown in
[
6
,
19
]
.

A natural problem in this context is to study polynomial or trigonometric polynomial
solutions (depending on whether
A,B
are polynomials or trigonometric polynomials).
This problem has been widely investigated. For instance, Gine et al.
[
12
]
showed
that the generalized Abel equation

x^{\prime}=A_{0}(t)+A_{1}(t)x+\ldots+A_{n}(t)x^{n}

has at most
n
polynomial solutions when
A_{i}(t)
are polynomials. For other related results, see for instance
[
7
,
11
,
15
,
18
,
22
]

and references therein.

In the present work, we consider (
1.1
) with polynomial coefficients
and examine the number of rational solutions. This problem has
been analysed for rational limit cycles, i.e., rational solutions
x(t)

defined for all
t\in[0,1]
, closed (
x(0)=x(1)
), and such that
there is no continuum of closed solutions.

In precise terms, the problem is the following. Fixed
A,B\in\mathbb{C}[t]
, and
t_{0}
such
that
A(t_{0})\neq 0
, what is the maximum number of rational
solutions
x(t)=q(t)/p(t)
of (
1.1
),
p,q\in\mathbb{C}[t]
,
p\not\in\mathbb{C}
, defined in a neighbourhood
of
t_{0}
? It can be proven that this number does not depend on the choice of
t_{0}
such that
A(t_{0})\neq 0
(see the comment after Proposition
2.1
), so that the local definition of the problem can be extended globally in a natural way.

The main result obtained is Theorem
2.12
. It
states that if
n:=\deg(A)
is even or
\deg(B)>(n-1)/2
then (
1.1
) has at most two rational solutions, and
if
n
is odd then
\binom{n}{(n+1)/2}+1
is an upper bound for the number of rational solutions. This upper bound is not sharp, as we shall show in the final section.

The problem of studying the rational solutions of (
1.1
) is equivalent to considering the existence of invariant curves of degree one in
x
, i.e., curves of the form
p(t)x+q(t)=0
. Note that
x=0
is also an invariant curve. Using Darboux’s theory of integrability, we study when there
exist
\alpha_{0},\ldots,\alpha_{r}\in\mathbb{C}
and invariant curves
p_{i}(t)x+q_{i}(t)=0
such that
the function

(1.2)

f(t,x):=x^{\alpha_{0}}\prod_{i=1}^{r}(1+p_{i}(t)x)^{\alpha_{i}}

is a first integral of (
1.1
).

Gine and Santallusia
[
13
]
characterized the Abel equations with a Darboux first integral of the form (
1.2
)
when
r=2
and
r=3
. Here, we shall be interested in the relation between
r
and
n
.

Our main result in this setting, Theorem
3.3
, states that if the number
of rational solutions,
r
, is greater than or equal to
(n+1)/2
then the equation admits a Darboux first integral of the form (
1.2
).

In the final
section, we shall look into the low degree cases and provide a computational
method to obtain the maximum number of rational solutions for a fixed degree of
A
. We show that if
n
is even or
\deg(B)>(n-1)/2
then
the upper bound of two rational solutions is sharp. If
n=1,3
, the upper bound of
\binom{n}{(n+1)/2}+1

rational solutions is also sharp, but for
n=5
we obtain a lower upper bound.
Moreover, for
n=1,3,5,7
, we obtain examples of

(n-1)/2
rational solutions for (
1.1
) without first integrals of the form (
1.2
), so
the bound provided by Theorem
3.3
could be sharp.

2.
Algebraic invariant curves of degree one in
x

Consider the Abel equation (
1.1
), and denote the associated vector field by

\mathcal{X}=\frac{\partial}{\partial t}+g\frac{\partial}{\partial x}.

Let
f\in\mathbb{C}[t,x]
. The curve
f(t,x)=0
is an algebraic invariant curve of (
1.1
) if there exists
K\in\mathbb{C}[t,x]
such that

\left(\mathcal{X}f\right)(t,x)=\left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial t}+g\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}\right)(t,x)=K(t,x)f(t,x).

The polynomial
K(t,x)
is called the cofactor of
f(t,x)
. Observe that
f_{0}(t,x):=x=0
is always an algebraic invariant curve of (
1.1
) with cofactor
K_{0}(t,x)=A(t)x^{2}+B(t)x
.

If
f(t,x)=0
is invariant and
x(t)
is a solution of (
1.1
) then, for any
t_{0}
in the domain of the solution,

f(t,x(t))=f(t_{0},x(t_{0}))\exp\left(\int_{t_{0}}^{t}K(s,x(s))ds\right).

Therefore, if
f(t_{0},x(t_{0}))=0
, then
f(t,x(t))=0
for all
t
. Consequently
f(t,x)=0
consists of trajectories of solutions of the equation.

Assume that (
1.1
) has an invariant algebraic curve of degree one in
x
, i.e., a curve of the form
q(t)+p(t)x=0
where
p(t)
and
q(t)
are complex polynomials.

Henceforth, we shall refer to such invariant curves simply as invariant curves of (
1.1
),
and assume
q(t)\not\equiv 0
, with
p,q
coprime. As
x(t)\equiv 0
is a solution of (
1.1
),
then for any invariant curve either
q(t)\neq 0
for every
t\in\mathbb{R}
or
q(t)\equiv 0
. Moreover,
in
[
17
]
it is proved that
q(t)
is constant. We include the proof for completeness.

Proposition 2.1

(
[
17
]
, Lemma 2)
.

The curve
q(t)+p(t)x=0
is an invariant curve of equation (
1.1
) if and only if
q(t)
is a constant
c\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}
and

(2.3)

p(t)p^{\prime}(t)-c\,p(t)B(t)+c^{2}A(t)=0.

Proof.

Take
f(t,x)=q(t)+p(t)x=0
to be an invariant curve of the differential equation (
1.1
), i.e., there exists a polynomial
K(t,x)
such that

\mathcal{X}f=f_{t}+f_{x}g=fK.

Taking into account the degree in
x
on both sides of the above equality, one obtains that
K(t,x)=K_{0}(t)+K_{1}(t)x+K_{2}(t)x^{2}
, where
K_{i}\in\mathbb{C}[t]
,
i=0,1,2
. Then

q^{\prime}(t)+p^{\prime}(t)x+p(t)(A(t)x^{3}+B(t)x^{2})=

=(q(t)+p(t)x)(K_{0}(t)+K_{1}(t)x+K_{2}(t)x^{2}).

Equating the coefficients of the same powers of
x
, one obtains the following:

\displaystyle\begin{split}K_{2}(t)&=A(t),\\
p(t)B(t)&=p(t)K_{1}(t)+K_{2}(t)q(t),\\
p^{\prime}(t)&=K_{1}(t)q(t),\\
q^{\prime}(t)&=q(t)K_{0}(t).\end{split}

From the last of these equalities, one has that either
q(t)\equiv 0
or
K_{0}(t)\equiv 0
. As we are assuming that
q(t)\not\equiv 0
, then
K_{0}(t)\equiv 0
. Consequently,
q(t)=c\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}
.

From the other equalities, one has

p(t)B(t)=p(t)\frac{p^{\prime}(t)}{c}+cA(t)

or equivalently

p(t)p^{\prime}(t)-cp(t)B(t)+c^{2}A(t)=0.

∎

An immediate consequence of (
2.3
) is that
p(t)
divides
A(t)
. Thus an invariant curve of (
1.1
) has the form
c+p(t)x=0
, with
c\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}
and
p\in\mathbb{C}[t]
a divisor of
A(t)
. In particular, if
A(t_{0})\neq 0
then
p(t_{0})\neq 0
.
This is the reason why the number of rational solutions of (
1.1
) does not depend on
t_{0}
, and this consequently opens the possibility of studying the problem locally.

When
p(t)\equiv K
is a non-null constant, the Abel equation becomes the separated variable equation
x^{\prime}=B(t)x^{2}(K/c+x)
whose unique rational solutions are
0
and
-c/K
. Hence, we shall only consider the case
\deg(p)\geq 1
.

Without loss of generality, we assume that
c=1
. Then (
2.3
) becomes

(2.4)

p(t)p^{\prime}(t)-p(t)B(t)+A(t)=0.

As
p(t)
divides
A(t)
, if
1+p(t)x=0
is an invariant curve of (
1.1
) then there must exist
r\in\mathbb{C}[t]
such that
A(t)=p(t)r(t)
, and then (
2.4
) transforms into

(2.5)

B(t)=p^{\prime}(t)+r(t).

The following result establishes a relation between
\deg(B)
and the degree of an invariant curve of (
1.1
).

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
A(t)=a_{n}t^{n}+a_{n-1}t^{n-1}+\ldots+a_{0}
with
a_{n}\neq 0
, and
p(t)=p_{m}t^{m}+p_{m-1}t^{m-1}+\ldots+p_{0}
with
p_{m}\neq 0
, such that
1+p(t)x=0
is an invariant curve of (
1.1
). Then

(i)

\deg(B)\leq\max(\deg(p)-1,n-\deg(p))
;

(ii)

\deg(p)=(n+1)/2
if and only if
\deg(B)\leq(n-1)/2
. In this case,
\deg(A/p)=(n-1)/2
and

\displaystyle\deg(B)<(n-1)/2,

\displaystyle\quad\text{ if }{mp_{m}^{2}+a_{n}=0},

\displaystyle\deg(B)=(n-1)/2,

\displaystyle\quad\text{ if }{mp_{m}^{2}+a_{n}\neq 0}.

Proof.

(i) Since
\deg(p^{\prime})=\deg(p)-1
and
\deg(A/p)=n-\deg(p)
, the claim follows directly from (
2.5
).

(ii)
\deg(p)\neq(n+1)/2
is equivalent to
\deg(p^{\prime})>(n-1)/2
or
(n-\deg(p))>(n-1)/2
, which is also equivalent to
\deg(B)>(n-1)/2
by (
2.5
).

If
\deg(p)=(n+1)/2
, then
\deg(A/p)=n-\deg(p)=(n-1)/2
. Hence
\deg(B)\leq(n-1)/2
because of (
2.5
), and
\deg(B)=(n-1)/2
if and only if the coefficients of the leading terms of
p^{\prime}(t)
and
A(t)/p(t)
are different.
∎

Let
1+p_{1}(t)x=0
and
1+p_{2}(t)x=0
be two different invariant curves of (
1.1
), and write
q(t):=\gcd(q_{1}(t),q_{2}(t))
. Then

p_{1}(t)=s_{1}(t)q(t)\quad\text{and}\quad p_{2}(t)=s_{2}(t)q(t),

where
s_{1},s_{2}\in\mathbb{C}[t]
have
\gcd(s_{1},s_{2})=1
.
Since
p_{1}(t)
and
p_{2}(t)
divide
A(t)
, there exists
s\in\mathbb{C}[t]
such that

A(t)=q(t)s_{1}(t)s_{2}(t)s(t).

The next step is to study the relation between
s_{1}(t)
and
s_{2}(t)
, and the expression and properties of
s(t)
.

Lemma 2.3
.

Assume that
1+p_{1}(t)x=0
and
1+p_{2}(t)x=0
are two invariant curves of (
1.1
). Then
p_{1}(t)
and
p_{2}(t)
are not relatively prime, i.e.,
\gcd(p_{1},p_{2})\neq 1
.

Proof.

Assume that
\operatorname{gcd}(p_{1}(t),p_{2}(t))=1
. Then
p_{1}(t)
and
p_{2}(t)
are relatively prime factors of
A(t)
. So
A(t)=p_{1}(t)p_{2}(t)s(t)
, and by (
2.5
) one has that

p^{\prime}_{1}(t)+p_{2}(t)s(t)=p^{\prime}_{2}(t)+p_{1}(t)s(t).

Therefore for some constant
C

p_{1}(t)-p_{2}(t)=C\exp\left(\int s(t)\,dt\right),

in contradiction with
p_{1}(t)-p_{2}(t)
being a polynomial. ∎

Note that, with the above notation, what the lemma says is that the monic polynomial
q(t)=\gcd(p_{1}(t),p_{2}(t)
is never constant.

The following result sets up the relation between any two invariant curves of (
1.1
)
and the induced decomposition of
A(t)
.

Proposition 2.4
.

Assume that
1+p_{1}(t)x=0
and
1+p_{2}(t)x=0
are two invariant curves of (
1.1
). With the above notation, if
\prod_{i=1}^{l}q_{i}(t)^{\delta_{i}}
is the decomposition of
q(t)
into irreducible monic polynomials then there exist
C\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}
and non-negative integers
\gamma_{i},\ i=1,\ldots,l,
such that

(2.6)

s_{1}(t)=s_{2}(t)+C\prod_{i=1}^{l}q_{i}(t)^{\gamma_{i}}

and

(2.7)

s(t)=\operatorname{gcd}(q(t),q^{\prime}(t))\,\sum_{i=1}^{l}(\delta_{i}+\gamma_{i})\,q^{\prime}_{i}(t)\prod_{j\neq i}q_{j}(t),

where, when
l=1
, we have defined
\prod_{j\neq i}q_{j}(t)=1
.

Proof.

Since
p_{i}(t)=q(t)s_{i}(t)
,
i=1,2
,
\frac{A(t)}{p_{1}(t)}=s_{2}(t)s(t)
and
\frac{A(t)}{p_{2}(t)}=s_{1}(t)s(t)
, it follows from (
2.5
) that

(q(t)^{\prime}s_{1}(t)+q(t)s^{\prime}_{1}(t))+s_{2}(t)s(t)=(q^{\prime}(t)s_{2}(t)+q(t)s^{\prime}_{2}(t))+s_{1}(t)s(t),

and consequently

(2.8)

q(t)(s^{\prime}_{1}(t)-s^{\prime}_{2}(t))=(s(t)-q^{\prime}(t))(s_{1}(t)-s_{2}(t)).

In particular,
s_{1}(t)-s_{2}(t)
divides
q(t)(s^{\prime}_{1}(t)-s^{\prime}_{2}(t))
.

One can write
s_{1}(t)-s_{2}(t)=\tilde{q}(t)h(t)
where

\tilde{q}(t)=\prod_{i=1}^{l}q_{i}(t)^{\gamma_{i}},\quad\gamma_{i}\text{ non-negative integers},

and none of the irreducible factors of
h(t)
divides
q(t)
. From (
2.8
) one has that
h(t)
divides
s_{1}^{\prime}(t)-s_{2}^{\prime}(t)
.

Since
s^{\prime}_{1}(t)-s^{\prime}_{2}(t)=\tilde{q}^{\prime}(t)h(t)+\tilde{q}(t)h^{\prime}(t)
,
h(t)
divides
\tilde{q}(t)h^{\prime}(t)
. Now, as none of the irreducible factors of
h(t)
divide
q(t)
and
\tilde{q}(t)
is a product of some (possibly repeated) factors of
q(t)
, the conclusion is that
h(t)
divides
h^{\prime}(t)
, which is only possible if
h(t)=C\in\mathbb{C}
. Then

s_{1}(t)=s_{2}(t)+C\prod_{i=1}^{l}q_{i}(t)^{\gamma_{i}}

for some non-negative integers
\gamma_{i},\,i=1,\ldots,l
. Furthermore,
C\neq 0
because
p_{1}(t)\neq p_{2}(t)
by hypothesis.

Substituting in (
2.8
) and solving for
s(t)
, one obtains that

\displaystyle s(t)

\displaystyle=q^{\prime}(t)+\prod_{i=1}^{l}q_{i}(t)^{\delta_{i}}\frac{C\sum_{i=1}^{l}\gamma_{i}\,q^{\prime}_{i}(t)\,q_{i}(t)^{\gamma_{i}-1}\prod_{j\neq i}q_{j}(t)^{\gamma_{j}}}{C\prod_{i=1}^{l}q_{i}(t)^{\gamma_{i}}}

\displaystyle=q^{\prime}(t)+\prod_{i=1}^{l}q_{i}(t)^{\delta_{i}-1}\sum_{i=1}^{l}\gamma_{i}\,q^{\prime}_{i}(t)\prod_{j\neq i}q_{j}(t)

\displaystyle=q^{\prime}(t)+\operatorname{gcd}(q(t),q^{\prime}(t))\sum_{i=1}^{l}\gamma_{i}\,q^{\prime}_{i}(t)\prod_{j\neq i}q_{j}(t).

Finally, taking into account that

\displaystyle q^{\prime}(t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{l}\delta_{i}\,q^{\prime}_{i}(t)\,q_{i}(t)^{\delta_{i}-1}\prod_{j\neq i}q_{j}(t)^{\delta_{j}}

\displaystyle=\operatorname{gcd}(q(t),q^{\prime}(t))\sum_{i=1}^{l}\delta_{i}\,q^{\prime}_{i}(t)\prod_{j\neq i}q_{j}(t),

one can conclude that

s(t)=\operatorname{gcd}(q(t),q^{\prime}(t))\,\sum_{i=1}^{l}(\delta_{i}+\gamma_{i})\,q^{\prime}_{i}(t)\prod_{j\neq i}q_{j}(t).

∎

The converse of Proposition
2.4
also holds. Therefore, it parametrizes
all cases of equation (
1.1
) having at least two invariant curves.

Proposition 2.5
.

Given
q(t),s_{2}(t)\in\mathbb{C}[t]
,
q(t)\not\in\mathbb{C}
,
C\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}
, and non-negative integers,
\gamma_{i},\ i=1,\ldots,l,
,
let
q_{1}(t),\ldots,q_{l}(t)
be the irreducible factors of
q(t)
. Define
s_{1}(t)
and
s(t)
by (
2.6
) and (
2.7
), respectively. Set

A(t)=q(t)s_{1}(t)s_{2}(t)s(t),\quad B(t)=(q(t)s_{1}(t))^{\prime}+s_{2}(t)s(t),

p_{1}(t)=q(t)s_{1}(t),\quad p_{2}(t)=q(t)s_{2}(t).

Then
1+p_{1}(t)x=0
and
1+p_{2}(t)x=0
are two invariant curves of (
1.1
).

Proof.

The polynomial
p_{1}(t)
satisfies (
2.4
) by definition. Now, note that (
2.6
) and (
2.7
) imply (
2.8
), from which one has that
p_{2}(t)
satisfies (
2.4
).
∎

Remark 2.6
.

In particular, considering
q(t)=q_{1}(t)=t
,
s_{2}(t)=1-t^{n-1}
,
C=1
,
and
\gamma_{1}=n-1
, one obtains that for every
n
there exist
A(t),B(t)
with
\deg(A)=n
such that (
1.1
) has at least two invariant curves.

Once the expression of
s(t)
is known, it is possible to establish relations between the degrees of the polynomials
p_{1}(t)
and
p_{2}(t)
that define the invariant curves, and, as a consequence, to provide upper bounds for the number of invariant curves.

Proposition 2.7
.

Let
n=\deg(A)
. Under the hypotheses in Proposition
2.4
, the following statements hold:

(i)

\deg(s)=\deg(q)-1
,

(ii)

n+1=\deg(p_{1})+\deg(p_{2})
,

(iii)

\deg(p_{1})=\deg(p_{2})
if and only if
\deg(p_{1})=(n+1)/2
.

Proof.

(i) By (
2.7
),
\deg(s)\leq\deg(q)-1
. Now, noting that in the expression of
s(t)
both
\delta_{i}
and
\gamma_{i}
are
non-negative integers, the equality is obtained.

(ii) As
A(t)=q(t)s_{1}(t)s_{2}(t)s(t)
,
p_{1}(t)=q(t)s_{1}(t)
, and
p_{2}(t)=q(t)s_{2}(t)
, it follows that

\begin{split}n=\deg(A)=&\deg(q)+(\deg(p_{1})-\deg(q))+\\
&+(\deg(p_{2})-\deg(q))+\deg(s)\\
=&\deg(p_{1})+\deg(p_{2})-\deg(q)+\deg(s)\\
=&\deg(p_{1})+\deg(p_{2})-1,\end{split}

from which (ii) stands.

(iii) This is a direct consequence of (ii).
∎

Corollary 2.8
.

If equation (
1.1
) has three invariant curves, then they are all of degree
(n+1)/2.

Proof.

This is a direct consequece of (ii) and (iii) of Proposition
2.7
.
∎

Next we study the number of invariant curves whose polynomial coefficients of
x
are proportional.

Proposition 2.9
.

Let
K\in\mathbb{C}
,
K\neq 0,1
. The curves
1+p(t)x=0
and
1+Kp(t)x=0
are invariant curves of (
1.1
) if and only if

A(t)=Kp^{\prime}(t)p(t),\quad B(t)=(K+1)p^{\prime}(t).

Proof.

By (
2.5
), the curve
1+p(t)x=0
is invariant if and
only if there exists
r\in\mathbb{C}[t]
such that
A(t)=p(t)r(t)
,
B(t)=p^{\prime}(t)+r(t)
.

If
1+Kp(t)x=0
is invariant then there exists
\overline{r}\in\mathbb{C}[t]
such that

A(t)=Kp(t)\bar{r}(t)=p(t)r(t),\quad B(t)=Kp^{\prime}(t)+\bar{r}(t)=p^{\prime}(t)+r(t).

Hence
r(t)=Kp^{\prime}(t)
, and therefore

A(t)=Kp(t)p^{\prime}(t),\quad B(t)=(1+K)p^{\prime}(t).

Conversely, if
A(t)=Kp^{\prime}(t)p(t)
and
B(t)=(K+1)p^{\prime}(t)
then, taking
r=Kp^{\prime}(t)
, one has

A(t)=p(t)r(t)
,
B(t)=p^{\prime}(t)+r(t)
. Consequently
1+p(t)x=0
is invariant. With a similar argument,
one has that
1+Kp(t)=0
is also invariant.
∎

It is said that (
1.1

Remark 2.10

The following result is the main one of the section. It provides an upper bound to the number of invariant curves and sufficent conditions for (

Theorem 2.12
.

Consider the differential equation (

.

(ii)

By Corollary

(i) If

(ii) If

If

must divide

3.
Darboux integrability and invariant curves

Using Darboux’s theory of integrability , we study the maximum number of invariant curves that (
1.1
) can have without forcing the existence of a Darboux first integral.

We say that
f(t,x)
, smooth enough and not identically constant, is a first integral of (
1.1
) if
\mathcal{X}f=0
, or, equivalently, if
f(t,x(t))
is constant, when
x(t)
is a solution of the equation.

We say that a first integral
f
of (
1.1
) is a Darboux first integral of (
1.1
) if
f(t,x)=\prod_{i=1}^{r}f_{i}(t,x)^{\alpha_{i}}
, where
f_{i}(t,x)=0
are invariant curves of the equation and
\alpha_{i}\in\mathbb{C}
.

First, we present the general Darboux result which links the existence of a Darboux first integral with the linear dependence of the cofactors of the invariant curves. We have adapted its statement to the present setting.

Theorem 3.1

(Darboux’s Theorem,
[
8
]
)
.

Let
f_{0}(t,x)=0,\dots,f_{r}(t,x)=0
be invariant curves of (
1.1
) with cofactors
K_{0}(t,x),\dots,K_{r}(t,x)
, respectively. If there exist
\alpha_{0},\dots,\alpha_{r}\in\mathbb{C}
such that
\sum_{i=0}^{r}\alpha_{i}K_{i}(t,x)=0
then
f(t,x)=\prod_{i=0}^{r}f_{i}(t,x)^{\alpha_{i}}
is a first integral of (
1.1
).

The following result is a direct application of Theorem
3.1
when the invariant curves of equation (
1.1
) are of degree one in
x
. See
[
13
]
for a more general version.

Proposition 3.2
.

Assume that (
1.1
) has the invariant curves
1+p_{i}(t)x=0
,
i=1,\dots,r
. Let
\alpha_{i}\in\mathbb{C}
,
i=1,\dots,r
, and
\alpha_{0}=-\sum_{i}^{r}\alpha_{i}
. Then
f(t,x)=x^{\alpha_{0}}\prod_{i=1}^{r}(1+p_{i}(t)x)^{\alpha_{i}}
is a first integral of (
1.1
) if and only if

(3.9)

\sum_{i=1}^{r}\alpha_{i}\frac{A(t)}{p_{i}(t)}=0.

Proof.

If
1+p_{i}(t)x=0
is an invariant curve of (
1.1
) then its cofactor is
K_{i}(t,x)=A(t)x^{2}+p_{i}^{\prime}(t)x
. Recall that
p_{i}(t)
divides
A(t)
by (
2.3
) and that
f_{0}(t,x)=x=0
is always an invariant curve with cofactor
K_{0}(t,x)=A(t)x^{2}+B(t)x
.

Now assume the existence of
\alpha_{i}
,
i=1,\dots,r
such that (
3.9
) holds.
Let
\alpha_{0}=-\sum_{i=1}^{r}\alpha_{i}
. By Proposition 3.1, if
\sum_{i=0}^{r}\alpha_{i}K_{i}(t,x)=0
then (
1.1
) is Darboux integrable. Thus

\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{r}\alpha_{i}K_{i}(t,x)

\displaystyle=\alpha_{0}K_{0}(t,x)+\sum_{i=1}^{r}\alpha_{i}K_{i}(t,x)

\displaystyle=\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{r}\alpha_{i}\Big)(A(t)x^{2}+B(t)x)+\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{r}\alpha_{i}\Big)(A(t)x^{2}+p_{i}^{\prime}(t)x)

\displaystyle=\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{r}\alpha_{i}(p_{i}^{\prime}(t)-B(t))\Big)x,

but, as
p_{i}^{\prime}(t)-B(t)=A(t)/p_{i}(t)
because of (
2.3
), one then has

\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{r}\alpha_{i}(p_{i}^{\prime}(t)-B(t))\Big)x=-\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{r}\alpha_{i}\frac{A(t)}{p_{i}(t)}\Big)x=0.

Conversely, if
f(t,x)=x^{\alpha_{0}}\prod_{i=1}^{r}(c_{i}+p_{i}(t)x)^{\alpha_{i}}
is a first integral then, from

(\mathcal{X}f)(t,x)=\left(\alpha_{0}A(t)x^{2}+\alpha_{0}B(t)x+\sum_{i=1}^{r}\alpha_{i}(A(t)x^{2}+p^{\prime}_{i}(t)x)\right)f(t,x)=0,

(
3.9
) follows.
∎

Now we can state the main integrability result, which provides, in terms of
\deg(A)
, the maximum number of invariant curves that equation (
1.1
) can have without having a Darboux first integral. Note that, as seen in the previous result, the cofactors are linearly dependent if and only if there exist
\alpha_{0},\ldots,\alpha_{r}\in\mathbb{C}
such that

\sum_{i=0}^{r}\alpha_{i}\frac{A(t)}{p_{i}(t)}=0.

Theorem 3.3
.

Let
n\geq 3
. If (
1.1
) has more than
(n+1)/2
invariant curves then (
1.1
) has a Darboux first integral.

Proof.

Assume that
1+p_{1}(t)x=0,\dots,1+p_{l}(t)x=0
are invariant curves, with
l>(n+1)/2
. By Corollary
2.7
,
\deg(p_{i})=(n+1)/2
for
i=1,\dots,l
.

Since
\deg(r_{i})=\deg(A/p_{i})=n-(n+1)/2=(n-1)/2
,
A/p_{i}\in\mathbb{C}_{(n-1)/2}[t]
for
i=1,\cdots,l
, and
l>(n+1)/2=\dim(\mathbb{C}_{(n-1)/2}[t])
, there must exist
\alpha_{i}\in\mathbb{C},\ i=1,\ldots,l,
such that

\sum_{i=1}^{l}\alpha_{i}\frac{A(t)}{p_{i}(t)}=0.

By Proposition
3.2
, equation (
1.1
) has a Darboux first integral.
∎

In the simplest case,

Proposition 4.1

Consider now

(2)
