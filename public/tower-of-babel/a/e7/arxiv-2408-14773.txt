1.
Introduction

Let
\mathbb{D}:=\{z\in\mathbb{C}:|z|<1\}
be the open unit disk in
\mathbb{C}
and
\mathcal{H}(\mathbb{D},\Omega)
denote the class of analytic function mapping unit disk
\mathbb{D}
into a domain
\Omega
. Let
\mathcal{A}
denote the class of analytic functions in
\mathbb{D}
normalized by
f(0)=0=f^{\prime}(0)-1
. Let
\mathcal{S}
be the subclass of
\mathcal{S}
consisting of univalent functions. For two analytic functions
f
and
g
in
\mathbb{D}
, the function
f
is said to be subordinate to
g
, written as
f\prec g
, if there is an analytic function map
\omega:\mathbb{D}\to\mathbb{D}
with
\omega(0)=0
satisfying
f(z)=g(\omega(z))
. In particular, if the function
g
is univalent in
\mathbb{D}
, then
f
is subordinate to
g
is equivalent to
f(0)=g(0)
and
f(\mathbb{D})\subset g(\mathbb{D})
.

Let
\varphi
be the analytic function with positive real part in
\mathbb{D}
that maps the unit disk
\mathbb{D}
onto regions starlike with respect to
1
, symmetric with respect to the real axis and normalized by the conditions
\varphi(0)=1
and
\varphi^{\prime}(0)>0
. For such functions, Ma and Minda (see
[
32
]
) introduced the following classes:

\displaystyle\mathcal{ST}[\varphi]:=\bigg\{f\in\mathcal{A}:\frac{zf^{\prime}(z)}{g(z)}\prec\varphi(z)\bigg\}

and

\displaystyle\mathcal{CV}[\varphi]:=\bigg\{f\in\mathcal{A}:1+\frac{zf^{\prime\prime}(z)}{f^{\prime}(z)}\prec\varphi(z)\bigg\}.

(a)

On taking
\varphi(z)=(1+Az)/(1+Bz)
,
(-1\leq B<A\leq 1)
, the class
\mathcal{ST}[\varphi]
reduces to the familiar class consisting of Janowski starlike functions (see
[
23
]
), denoted by
\mathcal{ST}[A,B]
.

(b)

The special case
A=1-2\alpha
and
B=-1
, that is,
\varphi(z)=(1+(1-2\alpha)z)/(1-z)
,
0\leq\alpha<1
yields the classes
\mathcal{ST}[\alpha]
and
\mathcal{ST}[\alpha]
of starlike and convex functions of order
\alpha
, respectively. In particular,
\alpha=0
, that is,
A=1
and
B=-1
leads to the usual classes
\mathcal{ST}
and
\mathcal{CV}
of starlike and convex functions, respectively.

(c)

For
A=1
and
B=(1-M)/M
,
M>1/2
, we obtain the class
\mathcal{ST}[{M}]
, introduced by Janowski (see
[
22
]
).

(d)

In
1939
, Robertson (see
[
36
]
) introduced a very well-known class
\mathcal{ST}^{(\beta)}:=\mathcal{ST}[-\beta,\beta]
,
0<\beta\leq 1
.

(e)

Also,
\mathcal{ST}_{(\beta)}:=\mathcal{ST}[\beta,0]
leads to a class which was introduced by MacGregor in
[
33
]
.

In
1914
, Bohr (see
[
13
]
) discovered that if a power series of analytic function
f(z)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_{n}z^{n}
converges in the unit disk
\mathbb{D}
with
|f(z)|<1
for
z\in\mathbb{D}
, then
M_{f}(r):=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}|a_{n}z^{n}|\leq 1
for
|z|=r\leq 1/6
. Weiner, Riesz and Schur independently proved that the Bohr’s result holds in the disk
\mathbb{D}_{\frac{1}{3}}:=\{z\in\mathbb{C}:|z|\leq\frac{1}{3}\}
and the radius
1/3
is best possible. For the class
\mathcal{B}:=\mathcal{H}(\mathbb{D},\mathbb{D})
, the radius
1/3
is known as the Bohr radius and the inequality
M_{f}(r)\leq 1
is commonly known as the Bohr inequality. In recent years, there has been a growing interest determining the Bohr radius for various classes of functions in one and several complex variables (see
[
7
,
25
,
26
,
27
]
), class of harmonic mappings (see
[
6
,
5
]
), class of quasiconformal mappings (see
[
28
,
31
,
4
]
).

Using the Euclidean distance
d
, the Bohr inequality for a function of the form
f(z)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_{n}z^{n}
is written as

\displaystyle d\left(\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}|a_{n}z^{n}|,|a_{0}|\right)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}|a_{n}z^{n}|\leq 1-|a_{0}|=1-|f(0)|=d(f(0),\partial\mathbb{D}),

where
\partial\mathbb{D}
is the boundary of the disc
\mathbb{D}
.

For any domain
\Omega
and all functions
f\in\mathcal{H}(\mathbb{D},\Omega)
, the Bohr radius is the largest radius
R_{\mathcal{H}}>0
such that

\displaystyle d\left(\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}|a_{n}z^{n}|,|f(0)|\right)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}|a_{n}z^{n}|\leq 1-|f(0)|=d(f(0),\partial\mathbb{D}),

for
|z|=r\leq R_{\mathcal{H}}
.

Before we continue the discussion, we fix some notations here. Throughout the discussion, we assume that
\mathcal{B}=\{f\in H_{\infty}:||f||_{\infty}\leq 1\}
and for
m\in\mathbb{N}=\{1,2,\ldots\}
, we define

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{m}=\{\omega\in\mathcal{B}:\omega(0)=\cdots=\omega^{(m-1)}(0)=0\;\mbox{and}\;\omega^{(m)}(0)\neq 0\}.

By using the Schwarz function in place of the initial power series coefficients, many authors established Bohr-type inequalities for bounded analytic functions in the unit disk (see
[
30
,
20
,
9
]
). In this paper, we continue the research by determining the sharp Bohr radius using Schwarz functions in the Bohr inequalities for some classes of analytic functions on the unit disk
\mathbb{D}
. Our main results improve various existing findings in the literature, as discussed in the subsequent sections.

2.1.
Refined Bohr inequalities for Janowski starlike functions

The Bohr radius for the Janowski starlike functions is obtained recently in
[
10
, Theorem 1]
and it is shown that the radius is sharp. In this section, we continue the study to establish a refined version of the Bohr inequality
[
10

aiming to show the corresponding radius is sharp. To achieve our goal, Bessel function of the first kind has a pivotal role. Before, we state our main result, we recall here modified Bessel function of the first kind.

2.2.
Modified Bessel function of the first kind

Bessel functions, first defined by the mathematician Daniel Bernoulli and then generalized by Friedrich Bessel, are canonical solutions
y(x)
of Bessel’s differential equation

\displaystyle\displaystyle x^{2}{\frac{d^{2}y}{dx^{2}}}+x{\frac{dy}{dx}}+\left(x^{2}-\alpha^{2}\right)y=0

for an arbitrary complex number

\alpha
, which represents the order of the Bessel function. Although,
\alpha
and
-\alpha
produce the same differential equation, it is conventional to define different Bessel functions for these two values in such a way that the Bessel functions are mostly smooth functions of

\alpha
. Bessel’s equation arises when finding separable solutions to Laplace’s equation and the Helmholtz equation in cylindrical or spherical coordinates. Bessel functions are therefore especially important for many problems of wave propagation and static potentials. In solving problems in cylindrical coordinate systems, one obtains Bessel functions of integer order
\alpha=n
; in spherical problems, one obtains half-integer orders
\alpha=n+\frac{1}{2}
. For example: Electromagnetic waves in a cylindrical waveguide,
Pressure amplitudes of inviscid rotational flows,
Heat conduction in a cylindrical object,
Modes of vibration of a thin circular or annular acoustic membrane (such as a drumhead or other membranophone) or thicker plates such as sheet metal (see Kirchhoff–Love plate theory, Mindlin–Reissner plate theory),
Diffusion problems on a lattice,
Solutions to the radial Schrödinger equation (in spherical and cylindrical coordinates) for a free particle
Position space representation of the Feynman propagator in quantum field theory.

Bessel functions of the first kind, denoted as
\mathcal{J}_{\alpha}(x)
, are solutions of Bessel’s differential equation. For integer or positive
\alpha
, Bessel functions of the first kind are finite at the origin
(x=0)
; while for negative non-integer
\alpha
, Bessel functions of the first kind diverge as
x
approaches zero. It is possible to define the function by
\alpha
times a Maclaurin series (note that
\alpha
need not be an integer, and non-integer powers are not permitted in a Taylor series), which can be found by applying the Frobenius method to Bessel’s equation:

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{\alpha}(x)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{n}}{n!\Gamma(n+\alpha+1)}\left(\frac{x}{2}\right)^{2n+\alpha},

where
\Gamma(n)
is the gamma function, a shifted generalization of the factorial function to non-integer values. The Bessel function of the first kind is an entire function if
\alpha
is an integer, otherwise it is a multi-valued function with singularity at zero.

The Bessel functions can be expressed in terms of the generalized hypergeometric series as

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{\alpha}(x)=\frac{\left(\frac{x}{2}\right)^{\alpha}}{\Gamma(\alpha+1)}{}_{0}F_{1}\left(\alpha+1;\;-\frac{x^{2}}{4}\right).

Another definition of the Bessel function, for integer values of
n
, is possible using an integral representation:

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{\alpha}(x)=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\pi}\cos\left(n\theta-x\sin\theta\right)d\theta=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}e^{i(n\theta-x\sin\theta)}d\theta,

which is also called Hansen-Bessel formula.

In particular, if
\alpha=0
, then it is easy to see that

(2.4)

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{0}(x)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n!\Gamma(n+1)}\left(\frac{x}{2}\right)^{2n}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{4(n!)^{2}}x^{2n}.

Further, putting
x=2ar
, it follows from (
2.4
) that

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{0}(2ar)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(a^{2}r^{2})^{n}}{(n!)^{2}}.

\displaystyle\leq\frac{|h^{\prime}(0)|r}{1+(\nu+\mu)+\nu\mu}+\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}\left(\frac{|h^{\prime}(0)|\displaystyle\prod_{k=0}^{n-2}\frac{|(B-A)+Bk|}{k+1}}{1+(\nu+\mu)n+\nu\mu n^{2}}\right)r^{n}

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{\Phi(r)}{|h^{\prime}(0)|}\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}\left(\frac{\displaystyle|h^{\prime}(0)|\prod_{k=0}^{n-2}\frac{|(B-A)+Bk|}{k+1}}{1+(\nu+\mu)n+\nu\mu n^{2}}\right)^{2}r^{2n}

Since the function

, using (

) and (

\displaystyle l_{(-A,-B)}(r)\leq|H\left(re^{i\theta}\right)|\leq l_{(A,B)}(r),\;0<r\leq 1.

\displaystyle d(h(0),\partial h(\mathbb{D}))=\inf_{z\in\partial f(\mathbb{D})}|h(\xi)-h(0)|\geq|h^{\prime}(0)|l_{(-A,-B)}(1).

Thus we have

(4.5)

\displaystyle|h^{\prime}(0)|\leq\frac{d(h(0),\partial h(\mathbb{D}))}{l_{(-A,-B)}(1)}.

Using (
4.3
) and (

), we obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}|a_{n}|\omega(z)|^{n}+\frac{\Phi(|\omega(z)|)}{|h^{\prime}(0)|}\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}|a_{n}|^{2}\omega(z)|^{2n}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{d(h(0),\partial h(\mathbb{D}))}{l_{(-A,-B)}(1)}\left(\frac{r}{1+(\nu+\mu)+\nu\mu}+\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}\left(\frac{\displaystyle\prod_{k=0}^{n-2}\frac{|(B-A)+Bk|}{k+1}}{1+(\nu+\mu)n+\nu\mu n^{2}}\right)r^{n}\right)

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{d(h(0),\partial h(\mathbb{D}))}{l_{(-A,-B)}(1)}\Phi(r)\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}\left(\frac{\displaystyle\prod_{k=0}^{n-2}\frac{|(B-A)+Bk|}{k+1}}{1+(\nu+\mu)n+\nu\mu n^{2}}\right)^{2}r^{2n}

for

, where the radius

is the smallest positive root of the equation (

).

is sharp, we consider the function

). Moreover,

. This yields that

\displaystyle f(z)=\frac{z}{1+(\nu+\mu)+\nu\mu}+\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}\left(\frac{\displaystyle\prod_{k=0}^{n-2}\frac{|(B-A)+Bk|}{k+1}}{1+(\nu+\mu)n+\nu\mu n^{2}}\right)z^{n}.

, we see that

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}|a_{n}|\omega(z)|^{n}+\Phi(|\omega(z)|)\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}|a_{n}|^{2}\omega(z)|^{2n}

\displaystyle=|h^{\prime}(0)|\left(\frac{r}{1+(\nu+\mu)+\nu\mu}+\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}\left(\frac{\displaystyle\prod_{k=0}^{n-2}\frac{|(B-A)+Bk|}{k+1}}{1+(\nu+\mu)n+\nu\mu n^{2}}\right)r^{n}\right)

\displaystyle\quad+|h^{\prime}(0)|{\Phi(r)}\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}\left(\frac{\displaystyle\prod_{k=0}^{n-2}\frac{|(B-A)+Bk|}{k+1}}{1+(\nu+\mu)n+\nu\mu n^{2}}\right)^{2}r^{2n}

\displaystyle=|h^{\prime}(0)|l_{(-A,-B)}(1)

\displaystyle=d(h(0),\partial h(\mathbb{D})).

Thus, the radius

is sharp.
∎

5.
The Bohr inequality for typically real functions
\mathcal{TR}

The class of typically real functions was introduced by Rogosinski (see
[
37
]
