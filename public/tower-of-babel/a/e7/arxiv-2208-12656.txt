Ramanujan’s q q -continued fractions
Gaurav Bhatnagar

Abstract
. Ramanujan’s q q -continued fractions are a central part of Ramanujan’s development of basic hypergeometric series. They appear in Chapter 16 of Part III and Chapter 32 of Part V of Ramanujan’s Notebooks edited by Berndt, and in Volume I of Andrews and Berndt’s Ramanujan’s Lost Notebook . In these references the continued fractions as presented in the order in which they appear in Ramanujan’s original notebooks. We summarize the work of several authors on this topic and re-organize Ramanujan’s q q -continued fractions.

Primary 33D15; Secondary 30B70

1.
Introduction

Ramanujan considered the
q
-analogue of the well-known Fibonacci continued fraction

\frac{1}{1}\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{}{+}\frac{1}{1}\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{}{+}\frac{1}{1}\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{}{+}\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{}{\cdots}

given by

\frac{1}{1}\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{}{+}\frac{q}{1}\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{}{+}\frac{q^{2}}{1}\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{}{+}\frac{q^{3}}{1}\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{}{+}\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{}{\cdots},

(1.1a)

and showed that for
|q|<1
, this continued fraction can be written as a ratio of two very similar sums:

\frac{\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{q^{k^{2}+k}}{{(1-q)(1-q^{2})\cdots(1-q^{k})}}}{\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{q^{k^{2}}}{(1-q)(1-q^{2})\cdots(1-q^{k})}}.

(1.1b)

The continued fraction (
1.1a
) is called the Rogers–Ramanujan continued fraction. In view of the Rogers–Ramanujan identities, this ratio can be expressed as a ratio of infinite products

\frac{(1-q)(1-q^{6})(1-q^{11})\cdots}{(1-q^{2})(1-q^{7})(1-q^{12})\cdots}\times\frac{(1-q^{4})(1-q^{9})(1-q^{14})\cdots}{(1-q^{3})(1-q^{8})(1-q^{13})\cdots}.

(1.1c)

It is clear that Ramanujan considered his work on this continued fraction as one of the highlights of his work.
Consider the last two entries of Chapter 16 of Ramanujan’s second notebook
[
47
]
. Entry 38 concerns the continued fraction above and the Rogers–Ramanujan identities. Entry 39 computes special cases (see Berndt
[
12
, p. 77–86]
). As a corollary, Ramanujan obtains

\displaystyle\frac{1}{1}\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{}{+}\frac{e^{-2\pi}}{1}\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{}{+}\frac{e^{-4\pi}}{1}\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{}{+}\frac{e^{-6\pi}}{1}\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{}{+}\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{}{\cdots}

\displaystyle=\Bigg(\sqrt{\frac{5+\sqrt{5}}{2}}-\frac{\sqrt{5}+1}{2}\Bigg)e^{2\pi/5},

(1.2a)

and

\displaystyle\frac{1}{1}\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{}{-}\frac{e^{-\pi}}{1}\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{}{+}\frac{e^{-2\pi}}{1}\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{}{-}\frac{e^{-3\pi}}{1}\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{}{+}\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{}{\cdots}

\displaystyle=\Bigg(\sqrt{\frac{5-\sqrt{5}}{2}}-\frac{\sqrt{5}-1}{2}\Bigg)e^{\pi/5}.

(1.2b)

Askey
[
10
]
suggested that Ramanujan’s
q
-extension
(
1.1a
) was the primary motivation for Ramanujan to discover the Rogers–Ramanujan identities, and indeed for Ramanujan’s development of basic hypergeometric series. In support of this hypothesis, we note that
Ramanujan ended his development of basic hypergeometric series with
(
1.2a
) and (
1.2b
). Thus
q
-continued fractions may be quite central to Ramanujan’s thought process.

Ramanujan himself included these two continued fractions in his first letter
[
15
, p. 29]
to Hardy in 1913; this is additional evidence that these were results Ramanujan liked and thought likely to impress Hardy.

And impress him, they did. About these, and another formula related to this continued fraction, Hardy
[
31
, p. 9]
wrote

(they) defeated me completely; I had never seen anything in the least like them before. A single look at them is enough to show that they could only be written by a mathematician of the highest class.

The objective of this article is to summarize the work of many mathematicians, whose work has made it easier to understand and organize Ramanujan’s continued fractions. The three formulas (
1.1a
)–(
1.1c
) serve as a model for this organization.

As it turns out, there are only five general results. In all of these a ratio of two series is expressed as a continued fraction. The remaining continued fractions are special cases of these. These general results are given in §§
2
,
3
and
6
. In some cases, the same ratio of two series is
expressed as two or three continued fractions; thus, they are transformation formulas too. We note some special cases in §
4
. There are some special cases where the ratio reduces to a single series, and many where they
reduce to a ratio of infinite products (see §
5
). When this happens, such as in the case of the Rogers–Ramanujan
continued fraction, Ramanujan computes special values.
In this article, we list all of Ramanujan’s
q
-continued fractions.
( Formulas for the computation of special values have not been considered here.)

Apart from this organization, we are interested in the ideas involved in the proofs. A variety of clever ideas are required to prove Ramanujan’s
q
-continued fractions; we present a sample,
giving details when possible, and references otherwise.
We conclude in §
7
with some remarks arising from our study, and pointers to recent work in this area.

Notation

The
q
-rising factorial
is defined as
{\left({a};q\right)_{0}}:=1
,

{\left({a};q\right)_{n}}:=(1-a)(1-aq)\cdots(1-aq^{n-1})\text{ for }n=1,2,\dots;

and, for
|q|<1
,

{\left({a};q\right)_{\infty}}:=\prod_{k=0}^{\infty}(1-aq^{k}).

In addition, we use the short-hand notation

\displaystyle{\left({a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{r}};q\right)_{k}}

\displaystyle:={\left({a_{1}};q\right)_{k}}{\left({a_{2}};q\right)_{k}}\cdots{\left({a_{r}};q\right)_{k}}.

We will require the
q
-binomial coefficient, defined as

\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n}{k}_{q}:=\frac{{\left({q};q\right)_{n}}}{{\left({q};q\right)_{k}}{\left({q};q\right)_{n-k}}},

where
n\geq k
are non-negative integers. When
n<k
, we take
\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n}{k}_{q}=0.

Proof of (
2.2c
).

We present J. Cigler’s proof (sent by email, Sept. 2012),
which illustrates an idea of Askey
[
10
]
regarding a discovery approach to the Rogers–Ramanujan identities beginning with (
1.1a
) (see also
[
18
]
).

Let
F(b,\lambda)
denote the continued fraction (
2.2c
). Then

F(b,\lambda)=\frac{1}{1-b+(b+\lambda q)F(b,\lambda q)}.

To change this non-linear equation into a linear one, we substitute

F(b,\lambda)=\frac{g(b,\lambda q)}{g(b,\lambda)},

to obtain Ramanujan’s recurrence relation given in
Entry L.I.6.3.1(iv):

g(b,\lambda)=(1-b)g(b,\lambda q)+(b+\lambda q)g(b,\lambda q^{2}).

Now assume that

g(b,\lambda)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_{n}\lambda^{n},

substitute in Ramanujan’s recurrence relation, and compare coefficients of
\lambda^{n}
, to obtain

a_{n}=(1-b)q^{n}a_{n}+bq^{2n}a_{n}+q^{2n-1}a_{n-1},

or

a_{n}=\frac{q^{2n-1}}{(1-q^{n})(1+bq^{n})}a_{n-1}.

Iterating this we obtain

a_{n}=\frac{q^{n^{2}}}{{\left({q,-bq};q\right)_{n}}}a_{0}.

Now taking
a_{0}=1
, we obtain the expression (
2.1
) for
g(b,\lambda)
and a (formal) proof of
(
2.2c
).
∎

Proof of III.16.16.

Note that the sum
\mu_{n}(s)
is finite; the index

goes from
0
to
\lfloor\frac{n-s+1}{2}\rfloor
.
We will show

\frac{\mu_{n}(s)}{\mu_{n}(s+1)}=1+\frac{\lambda q^{s+1}}{{\cfrac{\mu_{n}(s+1)}{\mu_{n}(s+2)}}},

(2.3)

for

Formula (
2

in turn. Observe that the iteration stops when

\mu_{n}(n)=1=\mu_{n}(n+1).

To prove (

\displaystyle\frac{\mu_{n}(s)}{\mu_{n}(s+1)}

\displaystyle=1+\frac{\mu_{n}(s)-\mu_{n}(s+1)}{\mu_{n}(s+1)}

\displaystyle=1+\frac{1}{\mu_{n}(s+1)}

\displaystyle\hskip 9.95863pt\times\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{q^{k^{2}+sk}\lambda^{k}{\left({q};q\right)_{n-k-s}}}{{\left({q};q\right)_{k}}{\left({q};q\right)_{n-2k-s+1}}}\left[(1-q^{n-k-s+1})-(1-q^{n-2k-s+1})q^{k}\right]

\displaystyle=1+\frac{1}{\mu_{n}(s+1)}\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{q^{k^{2}+sk}\lambda^{k}}{{\left({q};q\right)_{k-1}}}\frac{{\left({q};q\right)_{n-k-s}}}{{\left({q};q\right)_{n-2k-s+1}}}

\displaystyle=1+\frac{\lambda q^{s+1}}{\mu_{n}(s+1)}\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{q^{k^{2}+(s+2)k)}\lambda^{k}}{{\left({q};q\right)_{k}}}\frac{{\left({q};q\right)_{n-k-s-1}}}{{\left({q};q\right)_{n-2k-s-1}}},

on shifting the sum. From this (

∎

4.
Transformations of continued fractions

In §§
2
and
3
, we saw that a ratio of two series can be written in multiple ways as continued fractions.
In this section we note two transformation formulas recorded by Ramanujan, obtained by setting the same special case of two of these continued fractions equal to one another. In addition, there is one transformation which follows by taking the odd part of a continued fraction.

L.I.6.5.1

Let
k\geq 0
,
\alpha=(1+\sqrt{1+4k})/2
, and
\beta=(-1+\sqrt{1+4k})/2
. Then for
|q|<1
,

The proofs of Ramanujan’s

-continued fractions

The first proofs of many of Ramanujan’s general

As we have seen, an eclectic collection of techniques is used to prove Ramanujan’s

-continued fractions.
As Berndt
[
14

wrote in a similar context:

Methods for proving these continued fraction formulas are varied and at times ad hoc. Ramanujan evidently had a systematic procedure for proving these continued fraction formulas, but we don’t know what it is.

Nevertheless, when it comes to
q
-continued fractions, there are several interesting systematic approaches. As we have seen, all of Ramanujan’s general continued fractions (in §§
2
,
3

6

[
46
]
derived all of these general continued fractions formally by finding three-term recurrence relations from contiguous relations for
{}_{2}\phi_{1}
series.

There is, however, a gap in this formal approach to continued fractions. One does not know that the continued fraction converges; and, even if it does, whether it converges to the ratio of series in question. Jacobsen
[
37
]
has clarified such issues (see
[
42
]
for a comprehensive exposition).

Another way convergence has been addressed is by associating the continued fraction with orthogonal
polynomials, see Ismail and Stanton
[
36
]
. However, we require the parameters to be real (rather
than complex) numbers in this approach. Continued fractions are closely related to the study of orthogonal
polynomials (see, for example, Askey and Ismail
[
11
]
). Al–Salam and Ismail
[
2
]
have
studied orthogonal polynomials related to Ramanujan’s continued fractions, see also
[
20
,
35
]
.
A third approach to show convergence is to use a theorem of Pincherle

[
38
, p. 164]
, see, for examples, Masson
[
43

and Gupta and Masson

In addition to Ramanathan
