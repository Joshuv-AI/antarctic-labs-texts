Quadratic equations in the lamplighter group Thanks: The authors express their gratitude to the anonymous reviewer whose critique improved the quality of this article.
Alexander Ushakov and Chloe Weiers

Abstract
. In this paper we study the complexity of solving quadratic equations in the lamplighter group. We give a complete classification of cases (depending on genus and other characteristics of a given equation) when the problem is 𝐍𝐏 {\mathbf{NP}} -complete or polynomial-time decidable. We notice that the conjugacy problem can be solved in linear time. Finally, we prove that the problem belongs to the class 𝐗𝐏 {\mathbf{XP}} . Keywords. Diophantine problem, quadratic equations, spherical equations, conjugacy problem, metabelian groups, lamplighter group, wreath product, complexity, NP-completeness. 2010 Mathematics Subject Classification. 20F16, 20F10, 68W30.



1.
Introduction

Let
F=F(Z)
denote the free group on countably many generators

Z=\{z_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\infty}
. For a group
G
, an

equation over
G
with variables in
Z
is an equality of the form
W=1
, where
W\in F*G
. If
W=z_{i_{1}}g_{1}\cdots z_{i_{k}}g_{k}
, with
z_{i_{j}}\in Z
and
g_{j}\in G
, then we refer to
\{z_{i_{1}},\ldots,z_{i_{k}}\}
as the set of
variables
and to
\{g_{1},\ldots,g_{k}\}
as the set of
constants
(or
coefficients
) of
W
. We occasionally write
W(z_{1},\ldots,z_{k})
or
W(z_{1},\ldots,z_{k};g_{1},\ldots,g_{k})
to indicate that the variables in
W
are precisely
z_{1},\ldots,z_{k}
and (in the latter case) the constants are precisely
g_{1},\ldots,g_{k}
.

A
solution
to an equation
W(z_{1},\ldots,z_{k})=1
over
G
is a homomorphism

\varphi\colon F*G\rightarrow G

such that
\varphi|_{G}=1_{G}
and
W\in\ker\varphi
. If
\varphi
is a solution of
W=1
and
g_{i}=\varphi(z_{i})
, then we often say that
g_{1},\ldots,g_{k}
is a solution of
W=1
. We also write
W(g_{1},\ldots,g_{k})
or
W(\bar{g})
to denote the image of
W(z_{1},\ldots,z_{k})
under the homomorphism
F*G\rightarrow G
which maps
z_{i}\mapsto g_{i}
(and restricts to the identity on
G
). Note that while some authors allow for solutions in some overgroup
H
(with an embedding
G\hookrightarrow H
), in this article we only consider solutions in
G
.

In this paper we assume that
G
comes equipped with a fixed generating set
X

and elements of
G
are given as products of elements of
X
and their inverses.
This naturally defines the length (or size) of the equation
W=1
as
the length of its left-hand side
W
.

Definition 1.1
.

An equation
W=1
is called
quadratic
if each variable appears exactly twice (as either
z_{i}
or
z_{i}^{-1}
).

The
Diophantine problem
(
{\mathbf{DP}}
) in a group
G
for a class of equations
C

is an algorithmic question to decide whether a given equation
W=1

in
C
has a solution. In this paper we study the class of quadratic equations
over the standard lamplighter group
L_{2}
.

1.1.
Classification of quadratic equations

We say that equations
W=1
and
V=1
are
equivalent

if there is an automorphism
\phi\in\Aut(F\ast G)
such that

\phi
is the identity on
G
and
\phi(W)=V
. It is a well
known consequence of the classification of compact surfaces
that any quadratic equation over
G
is equivalent, via an
automorphism
\phi
computable in time
O(|W|^{2})
, to an equation
in exactly one of the following three
standard forms
(see

[
6
,
9
]
):

(1)

\displaystyle\prod_{j=1}^{k}z_{j}^{-1}c_{j}z_{j}

\displaystyle=1

\displaystyle k\geq 1,

(2)

\displaystyle\prod_{i=1}^{g}[x_{i},y_{i}]\prod_{j=1}^{k}z_{j}^{-1}c_{j}z_{j}

\displaystyle=1

\displaystyle g\geq 1,k\geq 0,

(3)

\displaystyle\prod_{i=1}^{g}x_{i}^{2}\prod_{j=1}^{k}z_{j}^{-1}c_{j}z_{j}

\displaystyle=1

\displaystyle g\geq 1,k\geq 0.

The number
g
is the
genus
of the equation, and both
g
and
k

(the number of constants) are invariants.
The standard forms are called, respectively,

spherical
,
orientable of genus
g
, and

non-orientable of genus
g
.

1.2.
Previous results

The Diophantine problem for
quadratic equations naturally generalizes fundamental (Dehn) problems
of group theory such as the word and conjugacy problems.
Furthermore, there is a deep relationship between quadratic equations
and compact surfaces, which makes quadratic equations an interesting
object of study.

Study of quadratic equations originated with Malcev,
who in
[
20
]
considered some particular
quadratic equations over free groups,
now often referred to as
Malcev’s equations
.
This line of research for free groups was continued:
solution sets were studied in
[
9
]
,

{\mathbf{NP}}
-completeness was proved in

[
7
,
14
]
.
Various classes of infinite groups were analyzed:
hyperbolic groups were studied in

[
10
,
15
]
,
the first Grigorchuk group was studied in

[
17
]
and
[
1
]
,
free metabelian groups were studied in

[
18
,
19
]
,
metabelian Baumslag–Solitar groups were studied in

[
21
]
.
The Diophantine problem for quadratic equations over the
lamplighter group
L_{2}

was recently shown to be decidable by Kharlampovich, Lopez,
and Miasnikov
(see
[
13
]
).

1.3.
History of the study of
L_{2}

The group
{L_{2}}
was first studied under the name of the lamplighter group by Kaimanovich and Vershik
[
12
]
, though it has been an object of study for much longer as a simple example of a wreath product (see
[
4
]
for a compact history). The standard lamplighter group
L_{2}
has many different realizations, including as a dynamical system, an infinite direct sum, and notably as a self-similar group generated by a
2
-state automaton (see Grigorchuk and Żuk
[
11
]
). Furthermore, Skipper and Steinberg
[
26
]
realized general lamplighter groups
A\wr{\mathbb{Z}}
, where
A
is finite, as automaton groups. The construction and mechanics of the lamplighter group extend naturally beyond
{L_{2}}
to the generalized lamplighter group
L_{n}
; dead-end elements of generalized lamplighter groups were described by Cleary and Taback in
[
5
]
, and Bartholdi and Šunik showed that generalized lamplighter groups are self-similar
[
2
]
.

Complexity of solving some fundamental problems has also been a topic of study in lamplighter groups. The conjugacy search problem was explored by Sale for general wreath products in
[
23
]
and in lamplighter groups specifically in
[
24
]
. Siehler explored the conjugacy problem and described conditions for membership in the derived subgroup of
L_{n}
in
[
25
]
. In this paper we provide a different but equivalent description of the derived subgroup of
L_{2}
, which is naturally more compact due to the simplicity of
{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}
.

1.4.
Model of computation and representation of basic objects

We use a random-access machine as a model of computation.
Integers are given in unary form unless stated otherwise.
Elements of
L_{2}
are given as words, i.e., sequences of letters
of the group alphabet
\{a^{\pm 1},t^{\pm 1}\}
for
L_{2}
.

2.
Preliminaries: the lamplighter group

Define a set

{\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{\mathbb{Z}}}=\left\{\,f\colon{\mathbb{Z}}\to{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}\;\middle|\;|\supp(f)|<\infty\,\right\}

and a binary operation
+
on
{\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{\mathbb{Z}}}
which for
f,g\in{\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{\mathbb{Z}}}
produces
f+g\in{\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{\mathbb{Z}}}
defined by

(f+g)(x)=f(x)+g(x)\ \mbox{ for }\ x\in{\mathbb{Z}}.

For a nontrivial
f
it will be convenient to define

m(f)=\min_{x\in\supp(f)}x\ \ \mbox{ and }\ \ M(f)=\max_{x\in\supp(f)}x.

For
f\in{\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{\mathbb{Z}}}
and
b\in{\mathbb{Z}}
, define
f^{b}\in{\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{\mathbb{Z}}}
by

f^{b}(x)=f(x+b)\ \mbox{ for }\ x\in{\mathbb{Z}},

which is a right
{\mathbb{Z}}
-action on
{\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{\mathbb{Z}}}
because
f^{0}=f
and

f^{(b_{1}+b_{2})}(x)=f(x+b_{1}+b_{2})=(f^{b_{1}})^{b_{2}}(x)
.
Hence we can consider a semidirect product
{\mathbb{Z}}\ltimes{\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{\mathbb{Z}}}

equipped with the operation

(\delta_{1},f_{1})(\delta_{2},f_{2})=(\delta_{1}+\delta_{2},f_{1}^{\delta_{2}}+f_{2}).

The group
{\mathbb{Z}}\ltimes{\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{\mathbb{Z}}}
is called a
restricted wreath product
of
{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}
and
{\mathbb{Z}}
and is denoted by
{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}\wr{\mathbb{Z}}
. The group
{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}\wr{\mathbb{Z}}
is also known as the
lamplighter group
,
as it can be viewed as an infinite set of lamps (each lamp indexed by an element of
{\mathbb{Z}}
), with each lamp either on or off, and a lamplighter positioned at some lamp.
Given some element
(\delta,f)\in{L_{2}}
,
f\in{\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{\mathbb{Z}}}
represents the configuration of illuminated lamps and
\delta\in{\mathbb{Z}}
represents the position of the lamplighter. Similarly, we can define
{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}\wr{\mathbb{Z}}_{n}
as
{\mathbb{Z}}_{n}\ltimes{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}^{{\mathbb{Z}}_{n}}
. Henceforth, we denote
{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}\wr{\mathbb{Z}}
as
L_{2}
, the generalized lamplighter group
{\mathbb{Z}}_{n}\wr{\mathbb{Z}}
as
L_{n}
, and
{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}\wr{\mathbb{Z}}_{n}
by
L_{2}^{n}
.

2.1.
Mechanics of
{L_{2}}

There is a natural (abelian group) isomorphism between
{\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{\mathbb{Z}}}
and the ring

{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}[x^{\pm}]
of
Laurent polynomials

with coefficients in
{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}
that maps

f\in{\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{\mathbb{Z}}}
to
\sum_{i=-\infty}^{\infty}f(i)x^{i}
.
Hence, the group
L_{2}
can be viewed
as
{\mathbb{Z}}\ltimes{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}[x^{\pm}]
with the
{\mathbb{Z}}
-action on
{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}[x^{\pm}]

defined by

f^{\delta}=f\cdot x^{-\delta}.

Similarly, we can view

{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}^{{\mathbb{Z}}_{n}}
as the quotient ring
{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}[x^{\pm}]/{\left\langle x^{n}-1\right\rangle}

and
L_{2}^{n}
as
{\mathbb{Z}}_{n}\ltimes({\mathbb{Z}}_{2}[x^{\pm}]/{\left\langle x^{n}-1\right\rangle})
. Throughout this section we use notation for

{\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{\mathbb{Z}}}
and
{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}[x^{\pm}]
interchangeably
(respectively,
{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}^{{\mathbb{Z}}_{n}}
and
{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}[x^{\pm}]/{\left\langle x^{n}-1\right\rangle}
), slightly abusing the notation.

For
b\geq 0
define a (group) homomorphism
\pi_{b}\colon{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}[x^{\pm}]\to{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}[x^{\pm}]/{\left\langle x^{b}-1\right\rangle}
by

[\pi_{b}(f)](x)=\sum_{y\equiv_{b}x}f(y)\mbox{ for }x\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{b}.

For
{\overline{b}}=(b_{1},\ldots,b_{k})\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{k}
, define
a linear function
\lambda_{{\overline{b}}}\colon({\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{\mathbb{Z}}})^{k}\to{\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{\mathbb{Z}}}

by

(f_{1},\ldots,f_{k})\ \ \stackrel{{\scriptstyle\lambda_{{\overline{b}}}}}{{\mapsto}}\ \ (1-x^{b_{1}})\cdot f_{1}+\cdots+(1-x^{b_{k}})\cdot f_{k},

b=\gcd(b_{1},\ldots,b_{k})
,
and a projection homomorphism

\pi_{\overline{b}}=\pi_{b}\colon{\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{\mathbb{Z}}}\to{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}^{{\mathbb{Z}}_{b}}
.
Throughout the paper we use the conventions that
\gcd(0,\dots,0)=0
and
{\mathbb{Z}}_{0}={\mathbb{Z}}
.

Lemma 2.1
.

If
b_{1},b_{2}>0

and
b=\gcd(b_{1},b_{2})
, then
\gcd(x^{b_{1}}-1,x^{b_{2}}-1)=x^{b}-1
.

Proof.

The statement follows from the Euclidean lemma.
Indeed, if
b_{2}=b_{1}+r
, then

(x^{b_{2}}-1)-x^{r}(x^{b_{1}}-1)=x^{r}-1,

and, hence,

\gcd(x^{b_{1}}-1,x^{b_{2}}-1)=\gcd(x^{b_{1}}-1,x^{r}-1)
.
That allows us to define a Euclidean-like process that terminates with
x^{b}-1
.
∎

Corollary 2.2
.

\im(\lambda_{{\overline{b}}})=\ker(\pi_{{\overline{b}}})
.

Proof.

A ring of Laurent polynomials over a field, such as
{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}[x^{\pm}]
,
is a principal ideal domain.
By definition of
\lambda_{{\overline{b}}}
,
\im(\lambda_{{\overline{b}}})
is the ideal generated
by
x^{b_{1}}-1,\dots,x^{b_{k}}-1
in
{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}[x^{\pm}]
.
It follows from Lemma

that
\im(\lambda_{{\overline{b}}})={\left\langle x^{b}-1\right\rangle}
.
Clearly,
\ker(\pi_{{\overline{b}}})=\ker(\pi_{b})
is the same ideal
{\left\langle x^{b}-1\right\rangle}
.
∎

Proposition 2.3
.

“

is the least
k\in{\mathbb{N}}\cup\{\infty\}
such that every
g\in[G,G]
can be expressed
as a product of at most
k
commutators.

Lemma 4.1
.

The following hold:

(a)

[{L_{2}},{L_{2}}]=\ker(\sigma_{a})\cap\ker(\sigma_{t})
.

(b)

The commutator width of
{L_{2}}
is one.

Proof.

It is easy to check that every
product of commutators belongs to
\ker(\sigma_{a})\cap\ker(\sigma_{t})

and, hence,
[{L_{2}},{L_{2}}]\subseteq\ker(\sigma_{a})\cap\ker(\sigma_{t})
.
Conversely, if
(\delta,f)\in\ker(\sigma_{a})\cap\ker(\sigma_{t})
, then

\delta=0
and

\sum_{i=-\infty}^{\infty}f(i)=0
.
Define
g\colon{\mathbb{Z}}\to{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}
by

g(j)=\sum_{i\leq j}f(i)\ \mbox{ for }\ j\in{\mathbb{Z}}.

Clearly,
g
satisfies
f(j)=g(j)-g(j-1)
for every
j

and has finite support
because
\sum_{i=-\infty}^{\infty}f(i)=0
.
Hence,

(0,f)=(0,g)(1,0)(0,g)^{-1}(1,0)^{-1},

i.e.,
(0,f)
is a commutator.
Thus,
[{L_{2}},{L_{2}}]\supseteq\ker(\sigma_{a})\cap\ker(\sigma_{t})
.

Finally, notice that the above argument shows that every element
in
[{L_{2}},{L_{2}}]
is a single commutator, which establishes (b).
∎

Proposition 4.2
.

The Diophantine problem for orientable quadratic equations over
{L_{2}}

is decidable in linear time.

Proof.

Indeed, for an orientable equation (
2
) of genus
g\geq 1
we have

(
2
) has a solution

\displaystyle\ \ \stackrel{{\scriptstyle\ref{le:commutator-width}(b)}}{{\Leftrightarrow}}\ \ \prod_{j=1}^{k}z_{j}^{-1}c_{j}z_{j}\in[{L_{2}},{L_{2}}]\mbox{ for some }z_{1},\dots,z_{k}

\displaystyle\ \ \stackrel{{\scriptstyle\ref{le:commutator-width}(a)}}{{\Leftrightarrow}}\ \ \sigma_{a}{\left(\prod_{j=1}^{k}z_{j}^{-1}c_{j}z_{j}\right)}=0\ \wedge\ \sigma_{t}{\left(\prod_{j=1}^{k}z_{j}^{-1}c_{j}z_{j}\right)}=0

\displaystyle\ \ \stackrel{{\scriptstyle\phantom{\ref{le:commutator-width}(a)}}}{{\Leftrightarrow}}\ \ \sigma_{a}(c_{1}\cdots c_{k})=0\ \wedge\ \sigma_{t}(c_{1}\cdots c_{k})=0,

where the last equivalence can be seen from the fact that
\sigma_{a}
and
\sigma_{t}

are homomorphisms into abelian groups in which
z_{j}^{-1}
and
z_{j}
cancel out.
Finally, the condition

\sigma_{a}(c_{1}\cdots c_{k})=0\ \wedge\ \sigma_{t}(c_{1}\cdots c_{k})=0

can be checked in linear time.
∎

5.
Non-orientable equations

In this section we analyze non-orientable equations in the lamplighter group
L_{2}
.
We say that
g\in G
is a
square
if
g=h^{2}
for some
h\in G
.

Lemma 5.1
.

(\delta,f)\in L_{2}
is a square if and only if

\delta
is even and
\pi_{\delta/2}(f)=0
.

Proof.

“
\Leftarrow
”
If
\delta=2b
is even and
\pi_{b}(f)=0
, then we have

\displaystyle\pi_{b}(f)=0

\displaystyle\ \Rightarrow\ f\in\ker(\pi_{b})=\im(\lambda_{b})

(Corollary
2.2
)

\displaystyle\ \Rightarrow\ f=(1-x^{b})\cdot g

(for some
g\in{\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{\mathbb{Z}}}
)

\displaystyle\ \Rightarrow\ f=(1+x^{b})\cdot g=(x^{-b}+1)\cdot(x^{b}\cdot g)

(characteristic
2
).

Hence,
(2b,f)=(b,x^{b}\cdot g)^{2}
is a square.

“
\Rightarrow
”
Suppose that
(\delta,f)=(b,g)^{2}
is a square.
Then
(\delta,f)=(2b,(x^{-b}+1)g))=(2b,g^{b}+g))
.
Then
\delta=2b
and
f=g+g^{b}
.
Clearly,
\pi_{b}(g^{b})=\pi_{b}(g)
and, hence,

\pi_{b}(g^{b}+g)=0
.
Hence, the result.
∎

Let
V
be the verbal subgroup of
L_{2}
generated by all squares, i.e.,

V={\left\langle g^{2}\mid g\in L_{2}\right\rangle}
.
It is easy to see that
V
is a normal subgroup of
L_{2}
containing
the derived subgroup
[L_{2},L_{2}]
, because every commutator can be expressed
as a product of squares as follows:

[g,h]=g^{-1}h^{-1}gh=g^{-2}(gh^{-1})^{2}h^{2}

for every
g,h
.
In particular,
L_{2}/V
is abelian.

Lemma 5.2
.

L_{2}/V\simeq{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}\times{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}
.

Proof.

Clearly,
\sigma_{t}(g)
is even and
\sigma_{a}(g)=0

for every square
g=h^{2}\in L_{2}
;
this also applies to any product of squares.
Using the contrapositive form of that observation,
the elements
a,t,at
do not belong to
V
. Similarly we verify that
the elements
1,a,t,at
are distinct in
L_{2}/V
.
The quotient group
L_{2}/V
is an abelian group in which every nontrivial element
has order
2
. Since
L_{2}
is two-generated, its abelian quotient

L_{2}/V
cannot contain more than four elements. Therefore,

L_{2}/V\simeq{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}\times{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}
.
∎

Lemma
5.2
gives a linear time algorithm
to decide if a word
w=w(a,t)
defines an element of
V

(
w\in V
if and only if
w=1
in
L_{2}/V
).
By definition, the
verbal width
of
V
is
the least
k\in{\mathbb{N}}\cup\{\infty\}
such that if
g\in V
,
then
g
is a product of at most
k
squares.

Proposition 5.3
.

The verbal width of the subgroup
V
is
2
.

Proof.

By Lemma
5.2
,
atat^{3}\in V

(its image is trivial in
L_{2}/V
)
and, by Lemma
5.1
,
atat^{3}
is not a square.
Hence, the verbal width of
V
is greater than
1
.

Now, suppose that
(\delta,f)\in V
.
Then
\delta=2(1+k)
for some
k\in{\mathbb{Z}}
. Define

g\colon{\mathbb{Z}}\to{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}
by

g(j)=\sum_{i=-\infty}^{j}f(i)\ \ \mbox{ for }x\in{\mathbb{Z}}.

It is easy to check that

(\delta,f)=(1,g)^{2}+(k,0)^{2}.

Hence, every
(\delta,f)\in V
can be expressed as a product of two squares
and the verbal width of
V
is
2
.
∎

Proposition
5.3
implies
that there are exactly two cases for non-orientable equations
over the lamplighter group: genus one and genus two
(same as genus more than one).
The latter case is easy because
V
is normal.
Consider a non-orientable equation of genus two

(8)

x^{2}y^{2}\prod z_{i}^{-1}c_{i}z_{i}=1.

Proposition 5.4
.

The Diophantine problem for equations
(
8
)
is decidable in linear time.

Proof.

By Proposition
5.3
,
(
8
) has a solution
if and only if

\prod z_{i}^{-1}c_{i}z_{i}\in V

for some
z_{1},\dots,z_{k}\in L_{2}
, which is true if and only if

\sigma_{t}(c_{1}\cdots c_{k})
is even and
\sigma_{a}(c_{1}\cdots c_{k})=0
.
The obtained condition can be checked in linear time.
∎

Consider a non-orientable equation of genus one

(9)

x^{2}\prod z_{i}^{-1}c_{i}z_{i}=1.

Clearly, if the equation has a solution, then

2\sigma_{t}(x)=-\sum\sigma_{t}(c_{i})
, i.e.,
\sigma_{t}(x)

is uniquely defined by the equation.

Lemma 5.5
.

If
c_{1}\cdots c_{k}=(0,f)
, then

\underbrace{x^{2}\prod z_{i}^{-1}c_{i}z_{i}=1}_{W}\mbox{ has a solution }\ \Leftrightarrow\ \underbrace{\prod z_{i}^{-1}c_{i}z_{i}=1}_{W^{\prime}}\mbox{ has a solution.}

Proof.

Name the genus one equation
W
and the spherical equation
W^{\prime}
.
If
W^{\prime}
has a solution, then the same assignment for
z_{1},\dots,z_{k}

and
x=1
gives a solution for
W
.
Conversely, if
W
has a solution with
x=(\delta,f)
, then

\delta=0
.
Notice that
(0,f)^{2}=1
in
L_{2}

for every
f\colon{\mathbb{Z}}\to{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}
. Hence, we may assume that
f=0

and,
W^{\prime}
has a solution.
∎

Proposition 5.6
.

The Diophantine problem
for equations (
9
) is
{\mathbf{NP}}
-hard.

Proof.

For an instance
W^{\prime}:\prod_{i=1}^{k}z_{i}^{-1}c_{i}z_{i}=1
of
{\mathbf{DP}}_{SPH_{0}}
, construct an equation
W:x^{2}\prod_{i=1}^{k}z_{i}^{-1}c_{i}z_{i}=1
of type
(
9
).
By definition of
{\mathbf{DP}}_{SPH_{0}}
,
c_{1},\dots,c_{k}\in Y
and, hence,
the assumption of Lemma
5.5
holds,
which implies that
W
has a solution if and only if
W^{\prime}
has a solution.
This establishes a polynomial-time reduction from
{\mathbf{DP}}_{SPH_{0}}
,
which is
{\mathbf{NP}}
-hard by Corollary
3.2
,
to the Diophantine problem for equations (
9
).
∎

Theorem 5.7
.

The Diophantine problem for equations (
9
)
belongs to
{\mathbf{NP}}
.

Proof.

Consider an equation
W=1
of type (
9
)
with constants
c_{i}=(\delta_{c_{i}},f_{c_{i}})
,
that has a solution

x=(\delta_{x},f_{x}),z_{1}=(\delta_{1},f_{1}),\dots,z_{k}=(\delta_{k},f_{k})
.
First, notice that

\delta_{x}=\sigma_{t}(x)=-\tfrac{1}{2}(\delta_{c_{1}}+\dots+\delta_{c_{k}})\in{\mathbb{Z}}.

If
\delta_{x}=0
, then, by Lemma
5.5
,
the equation has a solution if and only if the spherical
equation obtained by dropping
x^{2}
has a solution.
By Theorem
3.5
, spherical equations have
{\mathbf{NP}}
-certificates.

If
\delta_{x}\neq 0
, then
\delta_{c_{i}}\neq 0
for some
i
, and

0<\gcd(\delta_{x},\delta_{c_{1}},\dots,\delta_{c_{k}})=d\leq|W|.

The second component of the equation
W=1
(as an element of
{\mathbb{Z}}\ltimes{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}^{\mathbb{Z}}
) is of the form

(10)

(1+x^{-\delta_{x}})x^{-\Delta_{0}}f_{x}+\sum_{i=1}^{k}x^{-\Delta_{i}}\left[(1-x^{-\delta_{c_{i}}})f_{i}+x^{-\delta_{i}}f_{c_{i}}\right]=0,

where
\Delta_{i}=\delta_{c_{i+1}}+\dots+\delta_{c_{k}}
for
i=0,\dots,k
,
and variables

f_{x},f_{1},\dots,f_{k}\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}^{\mathbb{Z}}
and

\delta_{1},\dots,\delta_{k}\in{\mathbb{Z}}
. Notice that

(1+x^{-\delta_{x}})x^{-\Delta_{0}}f_{x}=(1-x^{-\delta_{x}})x^{-\Delta_{0}}f_{x}

because
{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}
has characteristic
2
, so (
10
) becomes

(11)

\left((1-x^{-\delta_{x}})x^{-\Delta_{0}}f_{x}+\sum_{i=1}^{k}(1-x^{-\delta_{c_{i}}})x^{-\Delta_{i}}f_{i}\right)+\sum_{i=1}^{k}x^{-\delta_{i}-\Delta_{i}}f_{c_{i}}=0.

By Proposition
2.3
,
(
11
) has a solution if and only if
the following equation:

(12)

x^{\delta_{1}^{\prime}}f_{c_{1}}+\dots+x^{\delta_{k}^{\prime}}f_{c_{k}}=0

has a solution in
{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}^{{\mathbb{Z}}_{d}}
with
\delta_{1}^{\prime},\dots,\delta_{k}^{\prime}\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{d}
. A solution of (
12
) establishes an
{\mathbf{NP}}
-certificate
in this case.
∎

Corollary 5.8
.

There is an algorithm that for a given non-orientable
equation (
9
), decides
if it has a solution or not in time
O(|W|^{k})
.

Proof.

We can reuse parts of the proof
of Theorem
5.7
.
If
\delta_{x}=0
, then the problem reduces to
a spherical equation that,
by Corollary
3.7
,
can be solved in time
O(|W|^{k})
.

If
\delta_{x}\neq 0
, then
the problem reduces to the equation (
12
)
with unknowns
\delta_{1},\dots,\delta_{k}\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{d}
,
which can be solved
as in the second part
of the proof of Corollary
3.7

(the case when
\delta\neq 0
),
by enumerating all tuples
\delta_{1},\dots,\delta_{k-1}\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{d}

and testing if the tuple
(\delta_{1}^{\prime},\dots,\delta_{k-1}^{\prime},0)
satisfies
(
12
). This can be done in
O(|W|^{k})
time.
∎

6.
Parametric complexity

In this section, we summarize some of our previous results
and state that the Diophantine problem for quadratic equations
(of all three types (
1
), (
2
),
and (
3
)) over
L_{2}

can be solved in polynomial time for any fixed bound on the number
of variables
k
, i.e., the Diophantine
problem belongs to the complexity class
{\mathbf{XP}}

(“slicewise” polynomial time).

Corollary 6.1
.

There is an algorithm that solves the Diophantine problem
for quadratic equations
W=1
of type
(
1
),
(
2
),
(
3
)
over the lamplighter group
L_{2}
in time
O(|W|^{k})
.

Proof.

Follows from
Corollary
3.7
,
Proposition
4.2
,
and
Corollary
5.8
.
∎

Corollary 6.2
.

Fix
K\in{\mathbb{N}}
. The Diophantine problem for the class of
quadratic equations with
k\leq K
is decidable in polynomial time.
The Diophantine problem for quadratic equations over the lamplighter
group
L_{2}
belongs to
{\mathbf{XP}}
.
