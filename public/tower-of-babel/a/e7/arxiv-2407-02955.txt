Conjugation groups and structure groups of quandles
Victoria Lebed

Abstract
. Quandles are certain algebraic structures showing up in different mathematical contexts. A group G G with the conjugation operation forms a quandle, Conj ⁡ ( G ) \operatorname{Conj}(G) . In the opposite direction, one can construct a group As ⁡ ( Q ) \operatorname{As}(Q) starting from any quandle Q Q . These groups are useful in practice, but hard to compute. We explore the group As ⁡ ( Conj ⁡ ( G ) ) \operatorname{As}(\operatorname{Conj}(G)) for so-called C ¯ \overline{C} -groups G G . These are groups admitting a presentation with only conjugation and power relations. Symmetric groups S n S_{n} are typical examples. We show that for C ¯ \overline{C} -groups, As ⁡ ( Conj ⁡ ( G ) ) \operatorname{As}(\operatorname{Conj}(G)) injects into G × ℤ m G\times\mathbb{Z}^{m} , where m m is the number of conjugacy classes of G G . From this we deduce information about the torsion, center, and derived group of As ⁡ ( Conj ⁡ ( G ) ) \operatorname{As}(\operatorname{Conj}(G)) . As an application, we compute the second quandle homology group of Conj ⁡ ( S n ) \operatorname{Conj}(S_{n}) for all n n , and unveil rich torsion therein.

1.
Introduction

A
quandle
is a set
Q
endowed with an idempotent binary operation
\mathrel{\triangleleft}
for which all right translations are quandle automorphisms. In other words, it satisfies the following axioms for all
a,b,c\in Q
:

1.

idempotence:
a\mathrel{\triangleleft}a=a
;

2.

bijectivity of the right translations
R_{c}\colon Q\to Q,\ a\mapsto a\mathrel{\triangleleft}c
;

3.

self-distributivity:
(a\mathrel{\triangleleft}b)\mathrel{\triangleleft}c=(a\mathrel{\triangleleft}c)\mathrel{\triangleleft}(b\mathrel{\triangleleft}c)
.

Quandles and generalisations thereof have remarkably wide ranging applications: construction of invariants of knots and knotted surfaces (the three quandle axioms encoding the three Reidemeister moves), classification of Hopf algebras, construction and study of set-theoretic solutions to the Yang–Baxter equation, integration of Leibniz algebras (non-symmetric generalisations of Lie algebras). See the surveys
[
AG03
,
Kin07
,
Car12
,
EN15
,
Kam16
,
Leb18
]
for more detail. Recently, the relevance of quandles in theoretical physics was brought to light as well
[
Fri24
,
Doi24
]
.

Quandles can be constructed starting from mathematical structures of very different nature. The two fundamental examples are:

1.

Alexander quandles

\operatorname{Al}(M)
, i.e.,
\mathbb{Z}[t^{\pm 1}]
-modules
M
with

a\mathrel{\triangleleft}b=ta+(1-t)b,

2.

conjugation quandles

\operatorname{Conj}(G)
, i.e., groups
G
with the conjugation operation

a\mathrel{\triangleleft}b=b^{-1}ab.

The
structure group
(also called the
associated
,
enveloping
, or
adjoint group
) of a quandle
(Q,\mathrel{\triangleleft})
is defined by the following quadratic presentation:

(1)

\operatorname{As}(Q)\ =\ \langle\ e_{a},\,a\in Q\,|\,e_{a}e_{b}=e_{b}e_{a\mathrel{\triangleleft}b},\,a,b\in Q\ \rangle.

It can be considered from different viewpoints.

First, it is a deformation of a free abelian group, and inherits nice properties thereof. In particular, for finite
Q
,
\operatorname{As}(Q)
is virtually free abelian: carefully chosen powers of its generators
e_{a}^{d_{a}}
form a central free abelian subgroup with a
Coxeter-like finite quotient

\operatorname{\overline{As}}(Q)

[
LV18
]
. This gives a rich source of nicely behaving groups.

Second, the structure group construction yields a functor from the category of quandles to that of groups, which is left adjoint to the conjugation quandle functor.

Third,
\operatorname{As}(Q)
is an invariant of the quandle
Q
, which opens quandle theory to group-theoretic tools. In particular, a sufficient understanding of
\operatorname{As}(Q)
allows one to compute the second
quandle homology
group
H_{2}(Q)
, which is another quandle invariant crucial for applications and in general difficult to compute (see the above referenced surveys for detail). This can be done via Eisermann’s formula
[
Eis14
]

(2)

H_{2}(Q)\ \cong\ \bigoplus_{\operatorname{\mathcal{O}}\in\operatorname{Orb}(Q)}\left(\operatorname{Stab}_{\operatorname{As}(Q)}(a_{\operatorname{\mathcal{O}}})\cap\operatorname{Ker}(\varepsilon)\right)_{Ab}.

Here
\operatorname{Orb}(Q)
is the set of the
orbits
of
Q
with respect to the action of the
inner group

\operatorname{Inn}(Q)
, which is the group of permutations of
Q
generated by the right translations
R_{c}
;
a_{\operatorname{\mathcal{O}}}\in Q
is any representative of the the orbit
\operatorname{\mathcal{O}}
;
\operatorname{As}(Q)
acts on
Q
via
a\cdot e_{b}=a\mathrel{\triangleleft}b
; and
\varepsilon
is the
degree map
, which is the group morphism defined by

(3)

\displaystyle\varepsilon\colon\operatorname{As}(Q)

\displaystyle\to\mathbb{Z},

\displaystyle e_{a}

\displaystyle\mapsto 1.

The free part of
H_{2}(Q)
is known to be
\mathbb{Z}^{c(Q)(c(Q)-1)}

[
EG03
,
LN03
]
. Here
c(Q)
is the number of the
\operatorname{Inn}(Q)
-orbits of
Q
; for
Q=\operatorname{Conj}(G)
, this is precisely the number of conjugacy classes of
G
. It is the torsion part that is used in applications, and it can be highly non-trivial.

Structure groups are very difficult to compute in practice. We know of several notable examples only: an explicit description of the groups
\operatorname{As}(\operatorname{Al}(M))
for connected (with respect to the
\operatorname{Inn}(\operatorname{Al}(M))
action) Alexander quandles
[
Cla10
]
, resulting in a computation of
H_{2}
for some of them
[
GIV16
]
; a classification of all quandles with free abelian structure groups, together with partial results on the structure groups and homology of abelian quandles (having commuting right translations)
[
LM21
]
; and full or partial results for certain more involved classes of quandles in
[
Nos17
]
. Note that in all these cases,
H_{2}
does have torsion in general.

The original aim of this work was to compute the structure group and the second homology group of the conjugation quandle of a symmetric group
S_{n}
. The tools we developed turned out to apply to a much wider class of groups, which we call
\overline{C}
-groups
. These are groups admitting a presentation with only conjugation relations (
b^{-1}ab=c
) and power relations (
a^{k}=1,\ k\geq 2
). Without loosing generality, we allow only one power relation per conjugacy class. If only conjugation relations appear, we talk about
conjugation groups
, or briefly
C
-groups
. Among
\overline{C}
-groups one finds free and free abelian groups; Artin and Coxeter groups, and various generalisations thereof (virtual, welded, homotopy, etc.)
[
BPT23
,
Dar24
]
; cactus and twin groups, and various generalisations thereof
[
NNS24
,
Mos23
,
CN24
]
; Thompson’s group
F

[
Cho23
,
Szy24
]
; knot groups; structure groups and their finite quotients; and many more. Indeed, the commutation relation, for instance, can be interpreted as
b^{-1}ab=a
, the braid relation
aba=bab
as
b^{-1}ab=c
and
a^{-1}ca=b
(
c
being an extra generator), etc.

C
-groups appeared in
[
Kul93
,
Kul94
]
, where it was shown that, for any
n\geq 2
, being a
C
-group is the same as being the fundamental group of the complement of an
n
-dimensional compact orientable manifold without boundary in
\mathbb{S}^{n+2}
. We borrow this term, even if it has an alternative usage in group theory. Note that these groups independently appeared in
[
GH95
]
in a different context and under a different name, that of
LOG-groups
(LOG = labelled oriented graph).

There are two easy ways to extract some partial information from an element of the structure group

A(G)\ :=\ \operatorname{As}(\operatorname{Conj}(G)).

The first one is to abelianise. Relation (
1
) becomes then
e_{a}=e_{a\mathrel{\triangleleft}b}
, thus all generators from the same orbit become undistinguishable. Hence a map

\operatorname{Ab}\colon A(G)\twoheadrightarrow\mathbb{Z}^{\mathfrak{C}(G)},

where
\mathfrak{C}(G)
is the set of
conjugacy classes
of
G
. This map works for all quandles. The second one is proper to conjugation quandles: one can return to the original group
G
via the group morphism defined by

\displaystyle\pi\colon A(G)

\displaystyle\twoheadrightarrow G,

\displaystyle e_{a}

\displaystyle\mapsto a.

It turns out that for a
\overline{C}
-group
G
, the combination of these two simple maps suffices to understand the entire group
A(G)
:

Theorem A
.

For a
\overline{C}
-group
G
, the following map is injective:

\pi\times\operatorname{Ab}\colon A(G)\to G\times\mathbb{Z}^{\mathfrak{C}(G)}.

The image of this map is described in terms of a pullback in Theorem
3.2
.

As a result, various properties of the group
A(G)
can be deduced from those of the
\overline{C}
-group
G
, as long as one understands the conjugacy classes of
G
reasonably well. Here are only some useful corollaries. To announce some of them, we will need to fix a
\overline{C}
-type presentation
\Pi
of
G
. These properties will be made explicit for our running example
G=S_{n}
, with its classical presentation.

1.

The kernel of the map
\pi
is a subgroup of
\mathbb{Z}^{\mathfrak{C}(G)}
, hence free abelian. A more refined analysis (Proposition
5.3
) yields an isomorphism

K(G)\ :=\ \operatorname{Ker}(\pi)\ \cong\ \mathbb{Z}^{\mathfrak{C}^{\prime}(G)},

where
\mathfrak{C}^{\prime}(G)
is the set of conjugacy classes of
G
containing no infinite-order generators from
\Pi
. Thus
A(G)
is a central extension of
G
. We study the properties of the associated group
2
-cocycle from
H^{2}(G,\mathbb{Z}^{\mathfrak{C}^{\prime}(G)})
(Propositions
5.1
and
5.2
). In particular, this yields a short exact sequence

0\ \to\ \mathbb{Z}^{P(n)}\ \to\ A(S_{n})\ \overset{\pi}{\to}\ S_{n}\ \to\ 0,

P(n)
being the
partition number
of
n
, which is the number of conjugacy classes of
S_{n}
.

2.

The map
\pi
restricts to an isomorphism of the derived groups:

A(G)^{\prime}\ \cong\ G^{\prime}.

3.

The torsion of
A(G)
is
\operatorname{Ker}(\operatorname{Ab})\cap\pi^{-1}(\operatorname{Tor}(G))
, which is isomorphic to a subgroup of
\operatorname{Tor}(G)
. For a
finite

\overline{C}
-group
G
,
\operatorname{Tor}(G)=G
, hence

\operatorname{Tor}(A(G))\ =\ \operatorname{Ker}(\operatorname{Ab})\ =\ A(G)^{\prime}\ \cong\ G^{\prime}.

For
S_{n}
, one obtains the alternating group:

\operatorname{Tor}(A(S_{n}))\ =\ A(S_{n})^{\prime}\ \cong\ S_{n}^{\prime}\ =\ A_{n}.

In particular, contrary to
C
-groups,
S_{n}
is not a subgroup of
A(S_{n})
.

4.

The center of
A(G)
is

Z(A(G))\ =\ \pi^{-1}(Z(G)).

For a centerless group, this is a subgroup of
\mathbb{Z}^{\mathfrak{C}(G)}
, hence free abelian. In particular, for
n>2
, one gets

Z(A(S_{n}))\ =\ K(S_{n})\ \cong\ \mathbb{Z}^{P(n)}.

5.

An element
g\in A(G)
acts trivially on
a\in G
if and only if
\pi(g)
and
a
commute in
G
. Thus,

\operatorname{Stab}_{A(G)}(a)\ =\ \pi^{-1}(\operatorname{Stab}_{G}(a)).

We will develop this idea further in Proposition
6.1
. Since commutation in
S_{n}
is well understood, we will use this to compute in Theorem
6.2
the torsion of
H_{2}(\operatorname{Conj}(S_{n}))
, which turns out to be very rich.

While the above subgroup interpretation of
A(G)
is instrumental in studying its properties, an alternative direct product type description is better adapted for other purposes. To get to that, we first recall a somewhat surprising characterisation of
C
-groups and certain
\overline{C}
-groups:

Theorem B
.

1.

[
DRS23
]
A group
G
is a
C
-group if and only if
G\cong\operatorname{As}(Q)
for some quandle
Q
.

2.

[
AHT22
]
A finite centerless group
G
is a
\overline{C}
-group if and only if
G\cong\operatorname{\overline{As}}(Q)
for some finite quandle
Q
.

We give short proofs of these results with our methods.

Note that the isomorphism
G\cong\operatorname{As}(Q)
or
\operatorname{\overline{As}}(Q)
yields a new presentation of
G
with
C
-type relations for
all
instead of
some
pairs of generators only.

In
[
SV24
]
the theorem was extended to
pq-generated groups

G
, which allow conjugation and generalised power relations (
a^{k}=b^{m},\ k,m\in\mathbb{Z}
) in their presentation, and to appropriate quotients of the structure group
\operatorname{As}(\operatorname{Conj}(G))
. Those quotients were studied in detail there.

A quandle
Q
satisfying the theorem is constructed explicitly, starting from a
\overline{C}
-type presentation
\Pi
of
G
. Indeed, one takes the union
\Gamma^{G}
of
G

\Gamma
of
\Pi
. This is reminiscent of dual braid monoids
[

,
Bes03
]
, generated by all “positive” conjugates of the classical generators of braid groups. The quandle

is a particular case of
Dehn quandles
from
[

]
. When

is Thompson’s group

, we can reformulate
[

to say that Thompson’s quandle, described by generators and relations in
[

, is isomorphic to our
\Gamma^{F}
.

For our favourite groups
S_{n}
with their classical presentation,
\Gamma^{S_{n}}
is the set

of all transpositions from

, endowed with the conjugation operation. We thus recover the following isomorphism from

]

Our next result shows that
A(G)
is a trivial extension of the structure group
G^{\mathcal{D}}:=\operatorname{As}(\Gamma^{G})
of the much smaller quandle

:

Theorem C
.

For any
\overline{C}
-group

, one has a group isomorphism

A(G)\ \cong\ G^{\mathcal{D}}\times\mathbb{Z}^{\mathfrak{C}_{\operatorname{ng}}(G)},

where
\mathfrak{C}_{\operatorname{ng}}(G)

.

We propose several alternative descriptions of

First, it is an intermediate object between

G^{\mathcal{A}}
obtained from a presentation

Theorem D

For a group
G

\overline{C}
-type presentation

of group surjections, where the first map imposes the element

to be central whenever

; and the second map collapses all these central elements, presenting

(4)

0\ \to\ \mathbb{Z}^{\mathfrak{C}_{\operatorname{fg}}(G)}\ \to\ G^{\mathcal{D}}\ \to\ G\ \to\ 0.

Here

is the set of all conjugacy classes of

In particular,

•

for a
C
-group

, the three groups above coincide, and we recover the isomorphism

from
[

]
, where it was conjectured to characterise

-groups;

•

for Coxeter groups, these surjections were studied in
[

]
(with the notations
W=G,\ A_{W}=G^{\mathcal{A}},\ Q_{W}=\Gamma^{G}
), generalising the

[

]
.

Second, we inject

into

in Theorem

, and interpret this as a pullback in Proposition

.

Finally, when all generators of

are from the same conjugacy class, Proposition

presents

as a semi-direct product

G^{\mathcal{D}}\ \cong\ G^{\prime}\rtimes\mathbb{Z}.

For

, one has

S_{n}^{\mathcal{A}}\cong B_{n}\ \twoheadrightarrow\ S_{n}^{\mathcal{D}}\cong\raisebox{1.99997pt}{$B_{n}\times\langle t\rangle$}\left/\raisebox{-1.99997pt}{$\sigma_{1}^{2}=t$}\right.\ \twoheadrightarrow\ S_{n}\cong\raisebox{1.99997pt}{$B_{n}$}\left/\raisebox{-1.99997pt}{$\sigma_{1}^{2}=1$}\right.,

and Theorem

Further,

is a central extension of

S_{n}^{\mathcal{D}}\ \cong\ S_{n}\underset{\varphi}{\times}\mathbb{Z},

for the

\displaystyle\varphi\colon\hskip 28.45274ptS_{n}^{2}

\displaystyle\mapsto(-\operatorname{lg}(\alpha\beta)+\operatorname{lg}(\alpha)+\operatorname{lg}(\beta))/2,

where

\sigma_{1}
.

2.
Structure groups as subgroups

The aim of this section is to prove Theorem
A
, that is, to present the structure group
A(G)
as a subgroup of the much more manageable direct product
G\times\mathbb{Z}^{\mathfrak{C}(G)}
.

Fix a group
G
with a
\overline{C}
-type presentation
\Pi
. Denote its set of generators by
\Gamma
. We will need several notations, relevant for all subsequent sections.

First, let us split the set of the conjugacy classes of
G
as

\mathfrak{C}(G)=\mathfrak{C}_{\operatorname{g}}(G)\sqcup\mathfrak{C}_{\operatorname{ng}}(G),\hskip 42.67912pt\mathfrak{C}_{\operatorname{g}}(G)=\mathfrak{C}_{\operatorname{fg}}(G)\sqcup\mathfrak{C}_{\operatorname{ig}}(G),

where the classes from
\mathfrak{C}_{\operatorname{g}}(G)
contain some
g
enerator
a\in\Gamma
, while those from
\mathfrak{C}_{\operatorname{ng}}(G)
do
n
ot; and in a class
\operatorname{\mathcal{O}}\in\mathfrak{C}_{\operatorname{fg}}(G)
all elements are of
f
inite order, denoted by
k(\operatorname{\mathcal{O}})
, while in
\mathfrak{C}_{\operatorname{ig}}(G)
everyone is of
i
nfinite order. Recall that we allow only one power relation per conjugacy class, so
k(\operatorname{\mathcal{O}})
is exactly the exponent in the only power relation
a^{k}=1
with
a\in\operatorname{\mathcal{O}}
.

From the presentation
\Pi
, one directly deduces the abelianisation of
G
:

(5)

\operatorname{Ab}(G)\ \cong\ \mathbb{Z}^{\mathfrak{C}_{\operatorname{ig}}(G)}\oplus G_{\operatorname{f}},\hskip 28.45274pt\text{ where }\ G_{\operatorname{f}}:=\ \bigoplus_{\operatorname{\mathcal{O}}\in\mathfrak{C}_{\operatorname{fg}}(G)}\mathbb{Z}_{k(\operatorname{\mathcal{O}})}.

Further notations are needed to make the abelianisation map for the structure group,
\operatorname{Ab}\colon A(G)\twoheadrightarrow\mathbb{Z}^{\mathfrak{C}(G)}
, more explicit. Denote by
c_{\operatorname{\mathcal{O}}}
the generator of
\mathbb{Z}^{\mathfrak{C}(G)}
corresponding to
\operatorname{\mathcal{O}}\in\mathfrak{C}(G)
; thus
\operatorname{Ab}(e_{a})=c_{\operatorname{\mathcal{O}}}
for
a\in\operatorname{\mathcal{O}}
. Also, denote by
\varepsilon_{\operatorname{\mathcal{O}}}\colon A(G)\twoheadrightarrow\mathbb{Z}
the composition of
\operatorname{Ab}
with the projection onto the corresponding factor of
\mathbb{Z}^{\mathfrak{C}(G)}
; thus
\varepsilon_{\operatorname{\mathcal{O}}}(e_{a})=1
if
a\in\operatorname{\mathcal{O}}
, and
0
if
a\notin\operatorname{\mathcal{O}}
. Observe that
\operatorname{Ab}=\bigoplus_{\operatorname{\mathcal{O}}\in\mathfrak{C}(G)}\varepsilon_{\operatorname{\mathcal{O}}}
, and the degree map is just the sum
\varepsilon=\sum_{\operatorname{\mathcal{O}}\in\mathfrak{C}(G)}\varepsilon_{\operatorname{\mathcal{O}}}
.

The group
A(G)
has a very big center. This will be discussed in more detail later; now we will need only central elements of the form
e_{a}^{k}
. To study those, let us recall a very general lemma, rediscovered by a remarkable number of authors:

Lemma 2.1
.

If a power
a^{k}
is central in a (not necessarily
\overline{C}
-) group
G
, then the power
e_{a}^{k}
is central in
A(G)
, and one has
e_{a}^{k}=e_{b}^{k}
for any
b
from the same conjugacy class. Taking at most one such power per conjugacy class, one generates a central free abelian subgroup of
A(G)
.

Note that such central powers exist in the structure group of any finite quandle
Q
, and are used to construct the finite quotients
\operatorname{\overline{As}}(Q)
.

Proof.

The centrality statement follows from the computation

e_{c}\mathrel{\triangleleft}(e_{a}^{k})=e_{c\mathrel{\triangleleft}(a^{k})}=a_{c}

for all
c\in G
. Here and below the symbol
\mathrel{\triangleleft}
in a group always stands for the conjugation operation. This implies

e_{a}^{k}=e_{a}^{k}\mathrel{\triangleleft}e_{c}=(e_{a}\mathrel{\triangleleft}e_{c})^{k}=e_{a\mathrel{\triangleleft}c}^{k},

hence
e_{a}^{k}=e_{b}^{k}
for any
b
from the same conjugacy class as
a
. Finally, the freeness follows from the freeness of the image in the abelianisation, since

\operatorname{Ab}(e_{a}^{k})=kc_{\operatorname{\mathcal{O}}}\in\bigoplus_{\operatorname{\mathcal{O}}\in\mathfrak{C}(G)}\mathbb{Z}c_{\operatorname{\mathcal{O}}}=\mathbb{Z}^{\mathfrak{C}(G)}.\qed

For a
\overline{C}
-group
G
, to any
\operatorname{\mathcal{O}}\in\mathfrak{C}_{\operatorname{fg}}
one then associates the central element

(6)

t_{\operatorname{\mathcal{O}}}=e_{a}^{k(\operatorname{\mathcal{O}})},\ a\in\operatorname{\mathcal{O}},

which is independent of
a\in\operatorname{\mathcal{O}}
. Indeed, the element
a^{k(\operatorname{\mathcal{O}})}=1
is central in
G
.

Example 2.2
.

To help the reader better digest these notations, we write them out explicitly for the symmetric group
S_{n}
with its classical presentation, enhanced by extra generators to get to the
\overline{C}
-type:

(7)

S_{n}=\Biggl\langle\begin{array}[]{ll|l}&&\sigma_{i}\mathrel{\triangleleft}\sigma_{j}=\sigma_{i},\ i<j-1,\\
\sigma_{i},&1\leq i<n,&\sigma_{i}\mathrel{\triangleleft}\sigma_{i+1}=\sigma_{i,i+1},\\
\sigma_{i,i+1},&1\leq i<n-1,&\sigma_{i,i+1}\mathrel{\triangleleft}\sigma_{i}=\sigma_{i+1},\\
&&\sigma_{i}^{2}=1\end{array}\Biggr\rangle.

The set of conjugacy classes
\mathfrak{C}(S_{n})
is in bijection with the set of partitions of
n
, which encode the cycle decomposition of permutations. All generators fall into the class
\operatorname{\mathcal{O}}_{2,1,\ldots,1}
, with
k(\operatorname{\mathcal{O}}_{2,1,\ldots,1})=2
. The central element
t_{\operatorname{\mathcal{O}}_{2,1,\ldots,1}}
can be written as
e_{\sigma_{i}}^{2}
for any
i
. Thus
\#\mathfrak{C}(S_{n})=P(n)
(the partition number of
n
),
\mathfrak{C}_{\operatorname{ig}}(S_{n})
is empty,
\#\mathfrak{C}_{\operatorname{fg}}(S_{n})=1
, and
(S_{n})_{\operatorname{f}}=\mathbb{Z}_{2}
.

Example 2.3
.

For the closely related braid groups
B_{n}
, a
C
-type presentation is obtained by removing the power relations
\sigma_{i}^{2}=1
from the above presentation for
S_{n}
. The set
\mathfrak{C}(B_{n})
is now infinite,
\mathfrak{C}_{\operatorname{fg}}(B_{n})
is empty, and
\#\mathfrak{C}_{\operatorname{ig}}(B_{n})=1
.

Proof of Theorem
A
.

Take an element
g\in A(G)
satisfying
\pi(g)=1_{G}
and
\varepsilon_{\operatorname{\mathcal{O}}}(g)=0
for all
\operatorname{\mathcal{O}}\in\mathfrak{C}(G)
. We need to explain why
g
is trivial in
A(G)
. Our argument is based on rewriting.

Fix a representative
a_{\operatorname{\mathcal{O}}}\in\operatorname{\mathcal{O}}
for any
\operatorname{\mathcal{O}}\in\mathfrak{C}(G)
; if
\operatorname{\mathcal{O}}\in\mathfrak{C}_{\operatorname{g}}(G)
, we require
a_{\operatorname{\mathcal{O}}}\in\Gamma
. Any other

Remark 2.4
.

We actually proved a stronger (but less elegant) group inclusion

A(G)\ \hookrightarrow\ G\times\mathbb{Z}^{\mathfrak{C}_{\operatorname{fg}}(G)}\times\mathbb{Z}^{\mathfrak{C}_{\operatorname{ng}}(G)},

obtained from the one in the theorem by ignoring the
\mathbb{Z}^{\mathfrak{C}_{\operatorname{ig}}(G)}
part of
\mathbb{Z}^{\mathfrak{C}(G)}
.

The theorem allows us to reduce many computations in
A(G)
to
G
, especially those related to commutativity. The most straightforward example is

Corollary 2.5
.

Let
G
be a
\overline{C}
-group. Then elements
f
and
g
commute in
A(G)
if and only if
\pi(f)
and
\pi(g)
commute in
G
.

Proof.

Denoting the commutator as

[f,g]=fgf^{-1}g^{-1},

one has

(\pi\times\operatorname{Ab})([f,g])\ =\ ([\pi(f),\pi(g)],0)\in G\times\mathbb{Z}^{\mathfrak{C}(G)},

so, by the injectivity of
\pi\times\operatorname{Ab}
, we get
[f,g]=1_{A(G)}\ \Leftrightarrow\ [\pi(f),\pi(g)]=1_{G}
.
∎

Similarly, one proves

Corollary 2.6
.

Let
G
be a
\overline{C}
-group. Then the center of
A(G)
can be computed as

Z(A(G))\ =\ \pi^{-1}(Z(G)).

In particular, for a centerless group, one has
Z(A(G))=\operatorname{Ker}(\pi)
.

Corollary 2.7
.

Let
G
be a
\overline{C}
-group. The map
\pi
restricts to an isomorphism of the derived subgroups:

A(G)^{\prime}\ \cong\ G^{\prime}.

Proof.

Recall that the derived group is generated by the commutators. Since
\pi([f,g])=[\pi(f),\pi(g)]
, the map
\pi
restricts to a map
A(G)^{\prime}\to G^{\prime}
. Since
\operatorname{Ab}([f,g])=0
, the theorem guarantees that this restriction is injective. Since
\pi([e_{a},e_{b}])=[a,b]
for all
a,b\in G
, it is also surjective.
∎

Corollary 2.8
.

Let
G
be a
\overline{C}
-group. The torsion of
A(G)
is

\operatorname{Tor}(A(G))\ =\ \operatorname{Ker}(\operatorname{Ab})\cap\pi^{-1}(\operatorname{Tor}(G)),

which is isomorphic to a subgroup of
\operatorname{Tor}(G)
.

Proof.

By the injectivity of
\pi\times\operatorname{Ab}
,
g
is a torsion element in
A(G)
if and only if
\pi(g)
is a torsion element in
G
, and
\operatorname{Ab}(g)
is a torsion element in a free abelian group, which means
\operatorname{Ab}(g)=0
. The injectivity of
\pi\times\operatorname{Ab}
is then used once more to show the injectivity of the restriction of
\pi
to
\operatorname{Ker}(\operatorname{Ab})\cap\pi^{-1}(\operatorname{Tor}(G))\to\operatorname{Tor}(G)
.
∎

This is particularly useful in the two extreme cases:

1.

If
G
is finite (e.g.,
G=S_{n}
), then
\operatorname{Tor}(G)=G
, hence

\operatorname{Tor}(A(G))\ =\ \operatorname{Ker}(\operatorname{Ab})\ =\ A(G)^{\prime}\ \cong\ G^{\prime}.

2.

If, on the contrary,
G
has no torsion (e.g.,
G=B_{n}
), then neither has
A(G)
:

\displaystyle\operatorname{Tor}(A(G))\

\displaystyle=\ \operatorname{Ker}(\operatorname{Ab})\cap\pi^{-1}(\operatorname{Tor}(G))\ =\ \operatorname{Ker}(\operatorname{Ab})\cap\operatorname{Ker}(\pi)

\displaystyle=\ \operatorname{Ker}(\pi\times\operatorname{Ab})\ =\ \{1_{A(G)}\}.

Example 2.9
.

Our favourite
\overline{C}
-group
S_{n}
is finite and centerless for
n>2
. The above corollaries then yield:

\operatorname{Tor}(A(S_{n}))\ =\ \operatorname{Ker}(\operatorname{Ab})\ =\ A(S_{n})^{\prime}\ \cong\ S_{n}^{\prime}\ =\ A_{n},

Z(A(S_{n}))=\operatorname{Ker}(\pi).

Here
A_{n}
is the alternating group.

Example 2.10
.

Our favourite
C
-group
B_{n}
has no torsion, hence neither has
A(B_{n})
. The center of
B_{n}
is cyclic,
Z(B_{n})=\langle\Delta_{n}^{2}\rangle
for

, thus

is generated by
\operatorname{Ker}(\pi)
and

. This yields an isomorphism

.

3.
Structure groups as pullbacks

Let us now describe the image of the group inclusion
\pi\times\operatorname{Ab}\colon A(G)\hookrightarrow G\times\mathbb{Z}^{\mathfrak{C}(G)}
for a
\overline{C}
-group
G
.

Lemma 3.1
.

There exists a unique group morphism
\operatorname{\overline{\pi}}
completing the following commutative square:

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces A(G)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathbb{Z}^{\mathfrak{C}(G)}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces G}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathbb{Z}^{\mathfrak{C}_{\operatorname{ig}}(G)}\bigoplus G_{\operatorname{f}}=\operatorname{Ab}(G)}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\operatorname{Ab}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\pi}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\operatorname{\overline{\pi}}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\operatorname{Ab}}

(where we use the computation (

)).

Proof.

Given a generator
c_{\operatorname{\mathcal{O}}}
of
\mathbb{Z}^{\mathfrak{C}(G)}
, take any of its

-preimages
\widehat{c_{\operatorname{\mathcal{O}}}}\in A(G)
, and declare
\operatorname{\overline{\pi}}(c_{\operatorname{\mathcal{O}}})
to be
\operatorname{Ab}(\pi(\widehat{c_{\operatorname{\mathcal{O}}}}))
. Different choices of the lift
\widehat{c_{\operatorname{\mathcal{O}}}}
differ by commutator factors

, with

. But these are sent by
\pi
to commutators in

ghg^{-1}h^{-1}\overset{\pi}{\mapsto}\pi(g)\pi(h)\pi(g)^{-1}\pi(h)^{-1}\overset{\operatorname{Ab}}{\mapsto}0.

Thus our
\operatorname{\overline{\pi}}(c_{\operatorname{\mathcal{O}}})
does not depend on the choice of
\widehat{c_{\operatorname{\mathcal{O}}}}
. Since the target group is abelian, we get a well defined group morphism

. It is surjective since the

’s with

are sent to the corresponding generators of
\operatorname{Ab}(G)
. The square obtained is commutative by the construction of
\operatorname{\overline{\pi}}
, and this completion of the square is unique since the upper map
\operatorname{Ab}
is surjective.
∎

Theorem 3.2
.

For a
\overline{C}
-group
G
, the structure group

is the pullback

A(G)\ \cong\ G\underset{\operatorname{Ab},\operatorname{Ab}(G),\operatorname{\overline{\pi}}}{\times}\mathbb{Z}^{\mathfrak{C}(G)}.

The above pullback will be denoted as
G\underset{\operatorname{Ab}(G)}{\times}\mathbb{Z}^{\mathfrak{C}(G)}
for brevity.

Example 3.3
.

The pullback looks far less scary in our favourite example

. Here
\mathbb{Z}^{\mathfrak{C}(S_{n})}\cong\mathbb{Z}^{P(n)}
,
\operatorname{Ab}(S_{n})\cong\mathbb{Z}_{2}
, and the maps
\operatorname{Ab}\colon S_{n}\to\mathbb{Z}_{2}
(resp.,
\operatorname{\overline{\pi}}\colon\mathbb{Z}^{P(n)}\to\mathbb{Z}_{2}
) are the signature maps: they send even permutations (resp., generators corresponding to even partitions) to

and the remaining ones to

. Thus

is really close to being a direct product:

A(S_{n})\ \cong\ S_{n}\underset{\mathbb{Z}_{2}}{\times}\mathbb{Z}^{P(n)}.

Example 3.4
.

In the case of braid groups, the group
\mathbb{Z}^{\mathfrak{C}(B_{n})}
is infinitely generated;
\operatorname{Ab}(B_{n})\cong\mathbb{Z}
; the map
\operatorname{Ab}\colon B_{n}\to\mathbb{Z}
computes the length

of a word written in the standard (equivalently, extended standard, in the sense of (

sends the generator

to

.
Hence

A(B_{n})\ \cong\ B_{n}\underset{\mathbb{Z}}{\times}\mathbb{Z}^{\mathfrak{C}(B_{n})}.

Proof of Theorem
3.2
.

Recall the definition of the pullback:

G\underset{\operatorname{Ab}(G)}{\times}\mathbb{Z}^{\mathfrak{C}(G)}=\{\ (a,x)\ |\ a\in G,\ x\in\mathbb{Z}^{\mathfrak{C}(G)},\ \operatorname{Ab}(a)=\operatorname{\overline{\pi}}(x).\ \}

Theorem

guarantee that the injection

actually takes values in

. We need to show the surjectivity, that is, given

Assume first that

, that is, its components indexed by

are trivial. Write

a_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}\cdots a_{s}^{\alpha_{s}}
, with
a_{i}\in\Gamma,\ \alpha_{i}=\pm 1
. Put

. It is almost what we need:

,

, and

(10)

p_{\operatorname{\mathcal{O}}}(\operatorname{Ab}(g^{\prime}))=p_{\operatorname{\mathcal{O}}}(\operatorname{Ab}(a))=p_{\operatorname{\mathcal{O}}}(\operatorname{\overline{\pi}}(x))=p_{\operatorname{\mathcal{O}}}(x)

for

is more subtle, since in this case

In the general case, consider

and

the above argument yields a

satisfying

and

. But then

∎

Remark 3.5
.

The lemma and the theorem above are in fact a particular case of Goursat’s lemma, valid for any subdirect product of groups.

Proof.

5.
Structure group
A(G)
as an extension of
G

Let us now focus on the map
\pi
connecting the structure group
A(G)
to the original group
G
, which we suppose to be arbitrary (not necessarily of
\overline{C}
-type) for the moment. The difference between the two groups is measured by the central (hence abelian) subgroup
K(G):=\operatorname{Ker}(\pi)
of
A(G)
, which is hard to compute in general.

The map
\pi
has an obvious
set-theoretic section

\displaystyle s\colon G

\displaystyle\to A(G),

\displaystyle a

\displaystyle\mapsto e_{a}.

It is not a group morphism:
e_{a}e_{b}
and
e_{ab}
always have different degrees. It is classical to study the
“morphicity defect”
of such a section, which is a map

\displaystyle\varphi\colon G\times G

\displaystyle\to K(G),

\displaystyle(a,b)

\displaystyle\mapsto e_{ab}^{-1}e_{a}e_{b}.

Such maps govern extensions of
G
by
K(G)
. This is written as

A(G)\ \cong\ G\underset{\varphi}{\times}K(G).

Proposition 5.1
.

The above map
\varphi
is a symmetric equivariant group
2
-cocycle. That is, it satisfies the following properties for all
a,b,c\in G
:

(13)

\displaystyle\varphi(b,c)-\varphi(ab,c)+\varphi(a,bc)-\varphi(a,b)=0,

(14)

\displaystyle\varphi(a,1)=e_{1},

(15)

\displaystyle\varphi(a\mathrel{\triangleleft}c,b\mathrel{\triangleleft}c)=\varphi(a,b),

(16)

\displaystyle\varphi(a,b)=\varphi(b,a).

Thus in principle the knowledge of the second group cohomology of
G
could shed light on the structure group
A(G)
.

Proof.

The proof of the
2
-cocycle condition (
13
) is classical:

\displaystyle\varphi(a,bc)+\varphi(b,c)

\displaystyle=e_{a(bc)}^{-1}e_{a}e_{bc}e_{bc}^{-1}e_{b}e_{c}=e_{abc}^{-1}e_{a}e_{b}e_{c},

\displaystyle\varphi(ab,c)+\varphi(a,b)

\displaystyle=e_{(ab)c}^{-1}e_{ab}e_{c}e_{ab}^{-1}e_{a}e_{b}=e_{abc}^{-1}e_{ab}e_{ab}^{-1}e_{a}e_{b}e_{c}=e_{abc}^{-1}e_{a}e_{b}e_{c},

where we used the centrality of
e_{ab}^{-1}e_{a}e_{b}=\varphi(a,b)
. Note that we alternate between the multiplicative notation in the whole group
A(G)
, and the additive notation in the abelian subgroup
K(G)
.

The normalisation condition (
14
) is straightforward.

The equivariance condition (
15
) follows from

\displaystyle\varphi(a\mathrel{\triangleleft}c,b\mathrel{\triangleleft}c)

\displaystyle=e_{(a\mathrel{\triangleleft}c)(b\mathrel{\triangleleft}c)}^{-1}e_{a\mathrel{\triangleleft}c}e_{b\mathrel{\triangleleft}c}=e_{(ab)\mathrel{\triangleleft}c}^{-1}e_{a\mathrel{\triangleleft}c}e_{b\mathrel{\triangleleft}c}=(e_{ab}^{-1}\mathrel{\triangleleft}e_{c})(e_{a}\mathrel{\triangleleft}e_{c})(e_{b}\mathrel{\triangleleft}e_{c})

\displaystyle=(e_{ab}^{-1}e_{a}e_{b})\mathrel{\triangleleft}e_{c}=e_{ab}^{-1}e_{a}e_{b},

since the central element
e_{ab}^{-1}e_{a}e_{b}
commutes with
e_{c}
.

The symmetry condition (
16
) follows from

\displaystyle\varphi(b,a)

\displaystyle=e_{ba}^{-1}e_{b}e_{a}=e_{a(b\mathrel{\triangleleft}a)}^{-1}e_{a}(e_{b}\mathrel{\triangleleft}e_{a})=e_{(a\mathrel{\triangleleft}a)(b\mathrel{\triangleleft}a)}^{-1}e_{a\mathrel{\triangleleft}a}e_{b\mathrel{\triangleleft}a}

\displaystyle=\varphi(a\mathrel{\triangleleft}a,b\mathrel{\triangleleft}a)=\varphi(a,b).

We used the equivariance condition (
15
).
∎

It is remarkable that, in some sense, the
2
-cocycle
\varphi
cannot be too trivial:

Proposition 5.2
.

The image of
\varphi
generates the whole abelian group
K(G)
.

Proof.

Take a word
w
in the letters
e_{a},\ a\in G
, representing some
g\in K(G)
. Using the relation

\varphi(a,b)e_{ab}=e_{a}e_{b},

one can replace two neighbouring letters by one, at the cost of introducing a central factor
\varphi(a,b)^{\pm 1}
. Repeating this procedure, one gets one letter
e_{d}^{\pm 1}
times some
\varphi
factors. Since
\pi(g)=1
and
\pi(\varphi(a,b))=1
, one gets
d^{\pm 1}=\pi(e_{d}^{\pm 1})=1
, hence
d=1
. But
e_{1}=\varphi(1,1)
. In total, we have presented
g
as a product of the values of
\varphi
and their inverses.
∎

Let us now return to
\overline{C}
-groups. Our understanding of the structure groups
A(G)
in this case is sufficient to determine
K(G)
completely.

Proposition 5.3
.

A
\overline{C}
-group

fits into the exact sequence

0\ \to\ \mathbb{Z}^{\mathfrak{C}_{\operatorname{ng}}(G)}\times\mathbb{Z}^{\mathfrak{C}_{\operatorname{fg}}(G)}\ \overset{\iota}{\to}\ A(G)\ \overset{\pi}{\to}\ G\ \to\ 0,

where
\iota
is the map constructed in Proposition
4.1
.

Hence
K(G)
is the free abelian group
\mathbb{Z}^{\mathfrak{C}_{\operatorname{ng}}(G)}\times\mathbb{Z}^{\mathfrak{C}_{\operatorname{fg}}(G)}
.

Proof.

Proposition
4.1
guarantees that
\iota
is injective, and its image lies in
\operatorname{Ker}(\pi)
. It remains to explain why
\operatorname{Im}(\iota)
is the whole
\operatorname{Ker}(\pi)
. By Theorem
4.10
,

splits as
G^{\mathcal{D}}\times\mathbb{Z}^{\mathfrak{C}_{\operatorname{ng}}(G)}
, where
\mathbb{Z}^{\mathfrak{C}_{\operatorname{ng}}(G)}
is the subgroup generated by the

’s with

. But this is precisely
\iota(\mathbb{Z}^{\mathfrak{C}_{\operatorname{ng}}(G)})

\ G^{\mathcal{D}}\twoheadrightarrow\ G
as described in Theorem

.
∎

6.
Homology

In this section, we will explain how our understanding of the group
A(G)
can be used, via Eisermann’s formula, to compute the second homology group of the conjugation quandle
\operatorname{Conj}(G)
of a
\overline{C}
-group
G
. As an illustration, we provide an explicit computation of
H_{2}(\operatorname{Conj}(S_{n}))
.

Proposition 6.1
.

Let
G
be a
\overline{C}
-group,
\operatorname{\mathcal{O}}
its conjugacy class,
a_{\operatorname{\mathcal{O}}}\in\operatorname{\mathcal{O}}
any representative of this class, and
\operatorname{\mathcal{O}}_{1}=\{1_{G}\}
the class of
1_{G}
. The corresponding component of formula (
2
) then splits as follows:

(17)

\left(\operatorname{Stab}_{A(G)}(a_{\operatorname{\mathcal{O}}})\cap\operatorname{Ker}(\varepsilon)\right)_{Ab}\ \cong\left(\operatorname{Stab}_{G^{\mathcal{D}}}(a_{\operatorname{\mathcal{O}}})\right)_{Ab}\ \times\ \mathbb{Z}^{\mathfrak{C}_{\operatorname{ng}}(G)\setminus\{\operatorname{\mathcal{O}}_{1}\}}.

Further, the map
\pi\colon G^{\mathcal{D}}\twoheadrightarrow G
induces the short exact sequence

0\ \to\ \mathbb{Z}^{\mathfrak{C}_{\operatorname{fg}}(G)}\ \to\ \left(\operatorname{Stab}_{G^{\mathcal{D}}}(a_{\operatorname{\mathcal{O}}})\right)_{Ab}\ \to\ \left(\operatorname{Stab}_{G}(a_{\operatorname{\mathcal{O}}})\right)_{Ab}\ \to\ 0.

Thus, to determine
H_{2}(\operatorname{Conj}(G))
, one can compute stabilisers in the initial group
G
, lift them to
G^{\mathcal{D}}
via the set-theoretic section
a\mapsto e_{a}
, and establish all relations among these lifts and the free part
\mathbb{Z}^{\mathfrak{C}_{\operatorname{fg}}(G)}
in the abelian group
\left(\operatorname{Stab}_{G^{\mathcal{D}}}(a_{\operatorname{\mathcal{O}}})\right)_{Ab}
.

Proof.

Recall first that both
G(A)
and
G^{\mathcal{D}}
act on
G
on the right via
a\cdot e_{b}=a\mathrel{\triangleleft}b
, while
G
acts by conjugation:
a\cdot b=a\mathrel{\triangleleft}b=b^{-1}ab
. In particular, for
g\in A(G)
or
G^{\mathcal{D}}
, we have

(18)

a\cdot g\ =\ a\cdot\pi(g)\ =\ a\mathrel{\triangleleft}\pi(g).

As a result, the group inclusion
G^{\mathcal{D}}\hookrightarrow A(G)
from (
12
) and the map
\pi\colon A(G)\to G
intertwine these actions on
G
.

Now, in the splitting
A(G)\cong G^{\mathcal{D}}\times\mathbb{Z}^{\mathfrak{C}_{\operatorname{ng}}(G)}
from Theorem
C
, the part
\mathbb{Z}^{\mathfrak{C}_{\operatorname{ng}}(G)}
lies in
\operatorname{Ker}(\pi)
, hence acts trivially on
G
. Consequently,

\operatorname{Stab}_{A(G)}(a_{\operatorname{\mathcal{O}}})\ \cong\ \operatorname{Stab}_{G^{\mathcal{D}}}(a_{\operatorname{\mathcal{O}}})\times\mathbb{Z}^{\mathfrak{C}_{\operatorname{ng}}(G)}.

Next,
\mathbb{Z}^{\mathfrak{C}_{\operatorname{ng}}(G)}
splits as
\mathbb{Z}^{\mathfrak{C}_{\operatorname{ng}}(G)\setminus\{\operatorname{\mathcal{O}}_{1}\}}\times\mathbb{Z}^{\{\operatorname{\mathcal{O}}_{1}\}}
, and the last factor is generated by the central element
e_{1}
. Since
\varepsilon(e_{1})=1
, one can mod
e_{1}
out and get the isomorphism

\operatorname{Stab}_{\operatorname{As}(Q)}(a_{\operatorname{\mathcal{O}}})\cap\operatorname{Ker}(\varepsilon)\ \cong\ \operatorname{Stab}_{G^{\mathcal{D}}}(a_{\operatorname{\mathcal{O}}})\ \times\ \mathbb{Z}^{\mathfrak{C}_{\operatorname{ng}}(G)\setminus\{\operatorname{\mathcal{O}}_{1}\}}.

The second factor is central, hence survives in the abelianisation, yielding (
17
).

We now focus on the interesting factor
\left(\operatorname{Stab}_{G^{\mathcal{D}}}(a_{\operatorname{\mathcal{O}}})\right)_{Ab}
. According to (
18
), an element
g\in G^{\mathcal{D}}
acts trivially on
a\in G
if and only if
\pi(g)
and
a
commute in
G
. In particular,
\operatorname{Ker}(\pi)
acts trivially on any
a
. Thus the short exact sequence (
4
) from Theorem
D
induces the short exact sequence

0\ \to\ \mathbb{Z}^{\mathfrak{C}_{\operatorname{fg}}(G)}\ \to\ \operatorname{Stab}_{G^{\mathcal{D}}}(a_{\operatorname{\mathcal{O}}})\ \to\ \operatorname{Stab}_{G}(a_{\operatorname{\mathcal{O}}})\ \to\ 0.

Since the group
\mathbb{Z}^{\mathfrak{C}_{\operatorname{fg}}(G)}
is abelian, one goes to the abelianisation and deduces the exact sequence

\mathbb{Z}^{\mathfrak{C}_{\operatorname{fg}}(G)}\ \to\ \left(\operatorname{Stab}_{G^{\mathcal{D}}}(a_{\operatorname{\mathcal{O}}})\right)_{Ab}\ \to\ \left(\operatorname{Stab}_{G}(a_{\operatorname{\mathcal{O}}})\right)_{Ab}\ \to\ 0.

Finally, the leftmost map is injective, since when composed with the map

\left(\operatorname{Stab}_{G^{\mathcal{D}}}(a_{\operatorname{\mathcal{O}}})\right)_{Ab}\ \to\ (G^{\mathcal{D}})_{Ab}\ \cong\ \mathbb{Z}^{\mathfrak{C}_{\operatorname{fg}}(G)}\times\mathbb{Z}^{\mathfrak{C}_{\operatorname{ig}}(G)}

induced by the inclusion
\operatorname{Stab}_{G^{\mathcal{D}}}(a_{\operatorname{\mathcal{O}}})\hookrightarrow G^{\mathcal{D}}
it yields multiplication by
k(\operatorname{\mathcal{O}})
on the factor corresponding to
\operatorname{\mathcal{O}}\in\mathfrak{C}_{\operatorname{fg}}(G)
, which is injective.
∎

Finally, we apply these techniques to our favourite example
G=S_{n}
. Some notations are needed to announce our result. For a partition
\lambda
of
n
(written as
\lambda\vdash n
), we call its
support
the set
\operatorname{Supp}(\lambda)
of sizes of its parts, and
repetition support
the set
\operatorname{RSupp}(\lambda)
of part sizes repeating in
\lambda
at least twice. Put

\operatorname{R}(\lambda)\ =\ \begin{cases}\#\operatorname{RSupp}(\lambda)-1&\text{ if }\operatorname{RSupp}(\lambda)\text{ contains an odd element},\\
\#\operatorname{RSupp}(\lambda)&\text{ otherwise}.\end{cases}

Given an even
u\in\mathbb{N}
, let
s(n,u)
be the number of partitions
\lambda\vdash n
satisfying the following conditions:

•

there is no odd
v\in\operatorname{RSupp}(\lambda)
;

•

u\in\operatorname{Supp}(\lambda)
;

•

a power of
2
dividing
u
necessarily divides all other even
u^{\prime}\in\operatorname{Supp}(\lambda)
;

•

u
is minimal among even numbers satisfying the two preceding properties.

Recall also that
P(n)
denotes the partition number of
n
.

Theorem 6.2
.

The second homology group of the conjugation quandle of the symmetric group can be described as follows:

\displaystyle H_{2}(\operatorname{Conj}(S_{n}))\ \cong\

\displaystyle\mathbb{Z}^{P(n)(P(n)-1)}\times\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{\sum_{\lambda\vdash n}\operatorname{R}(\lambda)}

\displaystyle\times\prod_{\text{odd }u\leq n}\mathbb{Z}_{u}^{P(n-u)}\times\prod_{\text{even }u\leq n}\left(\mathbb{Z}_{u}^{P(n-u)-s(n,u)}\times\mathbb{Z}_{\frac{u}{2}}^{s(n,u)}\right).

Note the presence of torsion which grows very fast with
n
. In particular, adding a part
1
to any partition
\lambda\vdash n
, one sees that
H_{2}(\operatorname{Conj}(S_{n}))
is a subgroup of
H_{2}(\operatorname{Conj}(S_{n+1}))
for any
n
. We get a much more interesting picture than the group homology case, where
H_{2}(S_{n})
is simply
0
for
n<4
and
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
for
n\geq 4
.

For small
n
, our formulas yield

\displaystyle H_{2}(\operatorname{Conj}(S_{3}))\ \cong\

\displaystyle\mathbb{Z}^{6}\times\mathbb{Z}_{3},

\displaystyle H_{2}(\operatorname{Conj}(S_{4}))\ \cong\

\displaystyle\mathbb{Z}^{20}\times\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{3}\times\mathbb{Z}_{3},

\displaystyle H_{2}(\operatorname{Conj}(S_{5}))\ \cong\

\displaystyle\mathbb{Z}^{42}\times\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{3}\times\mathbb{Z}_{3}^{2}\times\mathbb{Z}_{5},

\displaystyle H_{2}(\operatorname{Conj}(S_{6}))\ \cong\

\displaystyle\mathbb{Z}^{110}\times\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{4}\times\mathbb{Z}_{3}^{4}\times\mathbb{Z}_{4}^{2}\times\mathbb{Z}_{5},

\displaystyle H_{2}(\operatorname{Conj}(S_{7}))\ \cong\

\displaystyle\mathbb{Z}^{210}\times\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{7}\times\mathbb{Z}_{3}^{6}\times\mathbb{Z}_{4}^{2}\times\mathbb{Z}_{5}^{2}\times\mathbb{Z}_{7}.

Proof.

Take the conjugacy class
\operatorname{\mathcal{O}}_{\lambda}
of
S_{n}
indexed by a partition
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{k})\vdash n
, and its representative
a_{\lambda}=c_{1}\cdots c_{k}
written as a product of cycles
c_{i}
of length
\lambda_{i}
with disjoint support. The proposition above yields the isomorphism,

\left(\operatorname{Stab}_{A(S_{n})}(a_{\lambda})\cap\operatorname{Ker}(\varepsilon)\right)_{Ab}\ \cong\left(\operatorname{Stab}_{S_{n}^{\mathcal{D}}}(a_{\lambda})\right)_{Ab}\ \times\ \mathbb{Z}^{P(n)-2},

and the short exact sequence

(19)

0\ \to\ \mathbb{Z}\ \overset{\iota}{\to}\ \left(\operatorname{Stab}_{S_{n}^{\mathcal{D}}}(a_{\lambda})\right)_{Ab}\ \overset{\widetilde{\pi}}{\to}\ \left(\operatorname{Stab}_{S_{n}}(a_{\lambda})\right)_{Ab}\ \to\ 0.

The subgroup
\operatorname{Stab}_{S_{n}}(a_{\lambda})
of
S_{n}
is generated by the cycles
c_{i}
, and, for each
v\in\operatorname{RSupp}(\lambda)
appearing in
\lambda

k_{v}\geq 2
times, by a subgroup isomorphic to
S_{k_{v}}
, which permutes the supports of the
v
-cycles. In the abelianisation, the cycle
c_{i}
with
\lambda_{i}\notin\operatorname{RSupp}(\lambda)
yields a factor
\mathbb{Z}_{\lambda_{i}}
, and the
\mathbb{Z}_{v}^{k_{v}}\rtimes S_{k_{v}}
component of
\operatorname{Stab}_{S_{n}}(a_{\lambda})
corresponding to a
v\in\operatorname{RSupp}(\lambda)
yields
\mathbb{Z}_{v}\times\mathbb{Z}_{2}
. Hence

(20)

\left(\operatorname{Stab}_{S_{n}}(a_{\lambda})\right)_{Ab}\ \cong\ \prod_{u\in\operatorname{Supp}(\lambda)}\mathbb{Z}_{u}\times\prod_{v\in\operatorname{RSupp}(\lambda)}\mathbb{Z}_{2}.

For each
u\in\operatorname{Supp}(\lambda)
,
u\neq 1
, take a generator
c_{i}=c_{i;1}\cdots c_{i;u-1}
of the corresponding component
\mathbb{Z}_{u}
written as a product of
u-1
transpositions, and put
e_{u}:=e_{c_{i;1}}\cdots e_{c_{i;u-1}}
(we abusively use the same notations in a group and in its abelianisation). Finally, for
v\in\operatorname{RSupp}(\lambda)
, denote by
d_{v}
any element of
S_{n}
permuting two cycles of length
v
in
a_{\lambda}
. It generates the copy of
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
in
\mathbb{Z}_{v}\times\mathbb{Z}_{2}
. It can be written as a composition of
v
transpositions with disjoint support,
d_{v}=d_{v;1}\cdots d_{v;v}
. Let
f_{v}
be its lift
e_{d_{v;1}}\cdots e_{d_{v;v}}
in
S_{n}^{\mathcal{D}}
. By (
19
), the abelian group
\left(\operatorname{Stab}_{S_{n}^{\mathcal{D}}}(a_{\lambda})\right)_{Ab}
is generated by the
e_{u}
, the
f_{v}
, and a generator
t=e_{\tau}^{2}
of
\operatorname{Im}(\iota)
, independent of the choice of transposition
\tau
. We need to determine all relations between these generators. The most obvious ones concern the powers of these generators:

(21)

\displaystyle e_{u}^{u}

\displaystyle=t^{\frac{u(u-1)}{2}},

\displaystyle u\in\operatorname{Supp}(\lambda),u\neq 1

(22)

\displaystyle f_{v}^{2}

\displaystyle=t^{v},

\displaystyle v\in\operatorname{RSupp}(\lambda).

Indeed, we chose (the minimal) exponents such that these powers of the generators
e_{u}
and
f_{v}
lie in
\operatorname{Ker}(\widetilde{\pi})
and thus coincide with some powers of
t
, which are determined by computing degrees. Further, consider a relation

(23)

\prod_{u\in\operatorname{Supp}(\lambda)}e_{u}^{\alpha_{u}}\prod_{v\in\operatorname{RSupp}(\lambda)}f_{v}^{\beta_{v}}\ =\ t^{\gamma},\hskip 28.45274pt\alpha_{u},\beta_{v},\gamma\in\mathbb{Z},

in
\left(\operatorname{Stab}_{S_{n}^{\mathcal{D}}}(a_{\lambda})\right)_{Ab}
. Applying
\widetilde{\pi}
and comparing the image in (
20
), one sees that each
\alpha_{u}
should be a multiple of
u
, and each
\beta_{v}
a multiple of
2
. Thus relations (
21
)-(
22
) can be used to turn (
23
) into an equality between two powers of
t
, which can only be trivial since
t
generates a copy of
\mathbb{Z}
.

Let us now try to simplify relations (
21
)-(
22
) by variable substitution.

•

For odd
u
, put
E_{u}:=e_{u}t^{-\frac{(u-1)}{2}}
. Relation (
21
) then becomes
E_{u}^{u}=1
.

•

For even
u
, put
E_{u}:=e_{u}t^{-\frac{(u-2)}{2}}
. Relation (
21
) then becomes
E_{u}^{u}=t^{\frac{u}{2}}
.

•

For odd
v
, put
F_{v}:=f_{v}t^{-\frac{(v-1)}{2}}
. Relation (
22
) then becomes
F_{v}^{2}=t
.

•

For even
v
, put
F_{v}:=f_{v}t^{-\frac{v}{2}}
. Relation (
22
) then becomes
F_{v}^{2}=1
.

Case 1:

There exists an odd
v_{0}\in\operatorname{RSupp}(\lambda)
. Put
\tau:=F_{v_{0}}
. The variable
t=\tau^{2}
can then be omitted. For even
u
, put
\overline{E}_{u}:=E_{u}\tau^{-1}
. Relation (
21
) then becomes
\overline{E}_{u}^{u}=1
. Also, for odd
v\neq v_{0}
, put
\overline{F}_{v}:=F_{v}\tau^{-1}
. Relation (
22
) then becomes
\overline{F}_{v}^{2}=1
. All relations are thus transformed into the triviality of certain powers of generators. Put together, they yield

\left(\operatorname{Stab}_{S_{n}^{\mathcal{D}}}(a_{\lambda})\right)_{Ab}\ \cong\ \left(\prod_{u\in\operatorname{Supp}(\lambda)}\mathbb{Z}_{u}\right)\times\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{\#\operatorname{RSupp}(\lambda)-1}\times\mathbb{Z}.

Case 2:

There is no odd
v\in\operatorname{RSupp}(\lambda)
. Then one has a factor
\mathbb{Z}_{u}
for each odd
u\in\operatorname{Supp}(\lambda)
, and a factor
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
for each (necessarily even)
v\in\operatorname{RSupp}(\lambda)
. The remaining part
G_{1}
has as generators the
E_{u}
for even
u\in\operatorname{Supp}(\lambda)
, together with the generator
t
; they are subject to the relations
E_{u}^{u}=t^{\frac{u}{2}}
only. Denote the values of
u
concerned as
2h_{1},\ldots,2h_{s},\ h_{i}\in\mathbb{N}
, arranged so that
h_{1}
is the minimal among the
h_{i}
having the minimal
v_{2}(h_{i})
(where
v_{2}(h_{i})
is the maximal power of
2
dividing
h_{i}
). It will be denoted by
m(\lambda):=h_{1}
. Let us show that the abelian group
G_{1}
isomorphic to

G_{2}\ :=\ \mathbb{Z}_{h_{1}}\times\mathbb{Z}_{2h_{2}}\times\cdots\times\mathbb{Z}_{2h_{s}}\times\mathbb{Z}.

Our defining relations for
G_{1}
can be arranged into the following matrix:

M_{1}=\left[\begin{array}[]{ccccccccc}2h_{1}&&&&&&&&-h_{1}\\
&2h_{2}&&&&&&&-h_{2}\\
&&&&\cdots&&&&\cdots\\
&&&&&&2h_{s}&&-h_{s}\end{array}\right].

For
G_{2}
, one gets

M_{2}=\left[\begin{array}[]{ccccccccc}h_{1}&&&&&&&&0\\
&2h_{2}&&&&&&&0\\
&&&&\cdots&&&&0\\
&&&&&&2h_{s}&&0\end{array}\right].

By the existence and uniqueness of the Smith normal form, our two abelian groups are isomorphic if and only if, for all
i
, the matrices above share the same greatest common divisors (gcd) of the determinants of all their
i\times i
minors. For
M_{1}
, such a determinant is a product of factors
2h_{q}
for distinct
q
, possibly times a factor
h_{r}
. So the gcd of such determinants for a fixed
i
is
2^{i-1}
times the gcd of the products
h_{q_{1}}\cdots h_{q_{i}}
for all
1\leq q_{1}<\cdots<q_{i}\leq n
. For
M_{2}
, such a determinant is a product of factors
2h_{q}
with distinct
q>1
, possibly times the factor
h_{1}
. For any prime
p>2
, their gcd will contain the same power of
p
that the gcd for
M_{1}
. For
p=2
, one of the products containing the minimal power of
2
for both matrices is
h_{1}*2h_{2}*\cdots*2h_{i}
. Hence the two gcd coincide. Summarising, one gets

(24)

\left(\operatorname{Stab}_{S_{n}^{\mathcal{D}}}(a_{\lambda})\right)_{Ab}\ \cong\ \mathbb{Z}_{\frac{m(\lambda)}{2}}\times\left(\prod\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}u\in\operatorname{Supp}(\lambda)\\
u\neq m(\lambda)\end{subarray}}\mathbb{Z}_{u}\right)\times\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{\#\operatorname{RSupp}(\lambda)}\times\mathbb{Z},

where the first term is omitted if
\lambda
contains no even parts.

By (
2
), the terms we have just computed need to be summed for all
\lambda\vdash n
.

•

The free part of each of these
P(n)
terms is
\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{Z}^{P(n)-2}\cong\mathbb{Z}^{P(n)-1}
. Hence the free part of the sum is
\mathbb{Z}^{P(n)(P(n)-1)}
, as predicted by
[
EG03
,
LN03
]
.

•

Summing the
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
factors corresponding to the generators
F_{v}
, one gets

\prod_{\lambda\vdash n}\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{\operatorname{R}(\lambda)}\cong\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{\sum_{\lambda\vdash n}\operatorname{R}(\lambda)}.

•

Each odd
u\in\operatorname{Supp}(\lambda)
brings a factor
\mathbb{Z}_{u}
. Since there are
P(n-u)
partitions
\lambda\vdash n
having
u\in\operatorname{Supp}(\lambda)
, the overall contribution to
H_{2}
is
\mathbb{Z}_{u}^{P(n-u)}
.

•

The most delicate case is that of even
u\in\operatorname{Supp}(\lambda)
, since they bring either a factor
\mathbb{Z}_{u}
, or, when
\lambda
has no repeating odd parts and
u=m(\lambda)
, a factor
\mathbb{Z}_{\frac{u}{2}}
. The sum of these factors is

\mathbb{Z}_{u}^{P(n-u)-s(n,u)}\times\mathbb{Z}_{\frac{u}{2}}^{s(n,u)}.

The sum of these four parts yields the announced result.
∎

Remark 6.3
.

The proof easily adapts to a computation of
H_{2}(T_{n})
for the
1
-orbit quandle
T_{n}
of all transpositions in
S_{n}
, endowed with the conjugation operation. Since
\operatorname{As}(T_{n})=S_{n}^{\mathcal{D}}
, one gets

H_{2}(T_{n})\ \cong\ \left(\operatorname{Stab}_{S_{n}^{\mathcal{D}}}(a_{(2,1,\ldots,1)})\cap\operatorname{Ker}(\varepsilon)\right)_{Ab}.

This is generated by elements
t
,
e_{2}
, and, if

,
f_{1}
, subject to relations
e_{2}^{2}=f_{1}^{2}=t
. Since
\varepsilon(e_{2})=\varepsilon(f_{1})=1
, intersection with
\operatorname{Ker}(\varepsilon)
is zero for
n\leq 3
, and is generated by the element
e_{2}f_{1}^{-1}
of order
2
otherwise. One obtains

H_{2}(T_{n})\ \cong\ \begin{cases}0,&n\leq 3,\\
\mathbb{Z}_{2},&n\geq 4,\end{cases}

which recovers a computation from
[
GIV16
]
.

Remark 6.4
.

Note that in all the cases from the proof, the torsion of
\operatorname{Stab}_{S_{n}^{\mathcal{D}}}(a_{\lambda})
differs from its finite quotient
\operatorname{Stab}_{S_{n}}(a_{\lambda})
, which behaves more regularly, by at most one factor (one factor
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
disappears, or one factor
\mathbb{Z}_{2h}
becomes
\mathbb{Z}_{h}
). This suggests that an alternative organisation of terms in the most difficult case (
24
) could probably lead to a more compact final formula, which we were unable to find.
