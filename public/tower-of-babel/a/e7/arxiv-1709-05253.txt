Canonical Models and the Complexity of Modal Team Logic
Martin Lück

Abstract
. We study modal team logic MTL, the team-semantical extension of modal logic ML closed under Boolean negation. Its fragments, such as modal dependence, independence, and inclusion logic, are well-understood. However, due to the unrestricted Boolean negation, the satisfiability problem of full MTL has been notoriously resistant to a complexity theoretical classification. In our approach, we introduce the notion of canonical models into the team-semantical setting. By construction of such a model, we reduce the satisfiability problem of MTL to simple model checking. Afterwards, we show that this approach is optimal in the sense that MTL-formulas can efficiently enforce canonicity. Furthermore, to capture these results in terms of complexity, we introduce a non-elementary complexity class, TOWER(poly), and prove that it contains satisfiability and validity of MTL as complete problems. We also prove that the fragments of MTL with bounded modal depth are complete for the levels of the elementary hierarchy (with polynomially many alternations). The respective hardness results hold for both strict or lax semantics of the modal operators and the splitting disjunction, and also over the class of reflexive and transitive frames.

Introduction

It is well-known that non-linear quantifier dependencies, such as
w
depending only on
z
in the sentence
\forall x\,\exists y\,\forall z\,\exists w\,\varphi
, cannot be expressed in first-order logic.
To overcome this restriction, logics of incomplete information such as
independence-friendly logic

[
HS89
]
have been studied.
Later, Hodges
[
Hod97

introduced
team semantics
to provide these logics with a compositional interpretation.
The fundamental idea is to not consider single assignments to free variables, but instead whole sets of assignments, called
teams

In this vein, Väänänen
[
Vää07

expressed non-linear quantifier dependencies by the
dependence atom

{=\!\!(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n},y)}

y
in the team functionally depend on those of
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}
.
Logics with numerous other non-classical atoms such as
independence

\perp

[
GV13
]
,
inclusion

\subseteq
and
exclusion

\mid

[
Gal12
]
have been studied since, and manifold connections to scientific areas such as statistics, database theory, physics, cryptography and social choice theory have emerged (see also Abramsky et al.
[
AKVV16
]
).

Team semantics have also been adapted to a range of propositional
[
YV16
,
HKLV16
]
, modal
[
Vää08
]
, and temporal logics
[
KMV15
,
KMVZ18
]
.
Besides
propositional dependence logic

\mathsf{PDL}\xspace

[
YV16
]
and
modal dependence logic

\mathsf{MDL}\xspace

[
Vää08
]
, also propositional and modal logics of independence and inclusion have been studied
[
KMSV17
,
HKVV15
,
HS15
,
Han17
]
.
Unlike in the first-order setting, the atoms such as the dependence atom range over flat formulas.
For example, the instance
{=\!\!(p_{1},\ldots,p_{n},\Diamond\mathsf{unsafe})}
of a modal dependence atom
may specify that the reachability of an unsafe state is a function of
p_{1}\cdots p_{n}
, but instead of exhibiting the explicit function, the atom only stipulates its existence.

Most team logics lack the Boolean negation, and adding it as a connective
{\sim}
usually increases both the expressive power and the complexity tremendously.
The respective extensions of propositional and modal logic are called
propositional team logic

\mathsf{PTL}\xspace

[

,

,
HKVV18
]
and

\mathsf{MTL}\xspace

[

,

]
.
With

, these logics can express all the non-classical atoms mentioned above, and in fact are expressively complete for their respective class of models
[
KMSV15
,
YV17
]
.
For these reasons, they are both interesting and natural logics.

The expressive power of
\mathsf{MTL}\xspace
is well-understood
[

, and a complete axiomatization was presented by the author
[

]

Yet the complexity of the satisfiability problem has been an open question

,

,

In the same paper, however, it was shown that no elementary upper bound for full

can be established by the same approach, whereas
the best known lower bound is

Contribution.

We show that
\mathsf{MTL}\xspace
is complete for a non-elementary class we call
\mathrm{TOWER}\xspace(\mathrm{poly})
, which contains the problems decidable in a runtime that is a tower of nested exponentials of polynomial height.
Likewise, we show that the fragments
\mathsf{MTL}\xspace_{k}
of bounded modal depth

are complete for classes we call
\mathrm{ATIME}\xspace\text{-}\mathrm{ALT}\xspace(\exp_{k+1},\mathrm{poly})
and which corresponds to
(k+1)
-fold exponential runtime and polynomially many alternations.
These results fill a long-standing gap in the active field of propositional and modal team logics
(see Table
1
).

In our approach, we consider so-called
canonical models
.
Loosely speaking, a canonical model satisfies every satisfiable formula in some of its submodels, and such models have been long known for, e.g.
\xspace
, many systems of modal logic
[
BRV01
]
.
In Section
4
, we adapt this notion for modal logics with team semantics, and prove that such models exist for
\mathsf{MTL}\xspace
.
This enables us to reduce the satisfiability problem to simple model checking, albeit on models that are of non-elementary size with respect to
{|\penalty\Phi\penalty|}+k
, where
\Phi
are the available propositional variables and
k
is a bound on the modal depth.

Nonetheless, this approach is essentially optimal:
In Section
5
and
6
, we show that
\mathsf{MTL}\xspace
can, in a certain sense,
efficiently enforce
canonical models, that is, with formulas that are of size polynomial in
{|\penalty\Phi\penalty|}+k
.
In this vein, we then obtain the matching complexity lower bounds in Section
7
and
8
, where we encode computations of non-elementary length in such large models.

Finally, in Section
9
we extend the preliminary version of this paper
[
Lüc18b
]
and consider restrictions of
\mathsf{MTL}\xspace
to specific frame classes, and to so-called
strict
team-semantical connectives.

{defiC}
[
[

]

Proposition 1
.

Every problem that is

.

Clearly,

.
For the lower bound, let

Define the set

Consider the algorithm that first checks if the input

As a consequence, the function

Corollary 2
.

\mathrm{TOWER}\xspace(\mathrm{poly})

-reductions.

Proof 2.2

Suppose

Modal team logic

We fix a countably infinite set
\mathcal{PS}
of propositional symbols.

Modal team logic

\mathsf{MTL}\xspace
, introduced by Müller
[
Mül14
]
, extends classical modal logic
\mathsf{ML}\xspace
.
Formulas of classical
\mathsf{ML}\xspace
are built following the grammar

\displaystyle\alpha

\displaystyle\vcentcolon\vcentcolon=\neg\alpha\mid\alpha\land\alpha\mid\alpha\lor\alpha\mid\Box\alpha\mid\Diamond\alpha\mid p\mid\top\text{,}

where

and
\top
is constant truth.

\mathsf{MTL}\xspace
extends
\mathsf{ML}\xspace
by the grammar

\displaystyle\varphi

\displaystyle\vcentcolon\vcentcolon={\sim}\varphi\mid\varphi\land\varphi\mid\varphi\lor\varphi\mid\Box\varphi\mid\Diamond\varphi\mid\alpha\text{,}

where
\alpha
denotes an
\mathsf{ML}\xspace
-formula.

The set of propositional variables occurring in a formula
\varphi\in\mathsf{MTL}\xspace
is
\mathsf{Prop}(\varphi)
.
We use the common abbreviations
\bot\vcentcolon=\neg\top
,
\alpha\rightarrow\beta\vcentcolon=\neg\alpha\lor\beta
and
\alpha\leftrightarrow\beta\vcentcolon=(\alpha\land\beta)\lor(\neg\alpha\land\neg\beta)
.
For easier distinction, we have classical
\mathsf{ML}\xspace
-formulas denoted by

and reserve
\varphi,\psi,\vartheta,\ldots
for general
\mathsf{MTL}\xspace
-formulas.

The

\displaystyle\mathsf{md}(p)

\displaystyle\vcentcolon=\mathsf{md}(\top)

\displaystyle\vcentcolon=0

\displaystyle\mathsf{md}({\sim}\varphi)

\displaystyle\vcentcolon=\mathsf{md}(\neg\varphi)

\displaystyle\vcentcolon=\mathsf{md}(\varphi)

\displaystyle\mathsf{md}(\varphi\land\psi)

\displaystyle\vcentcolon=\mathsf{md}(\varphi\lor\psi)

\displaystyle\vcentcolon=\mathrm{max}\{\mathsf{md}(\varphi),\mathsf{md}(\psi)\}

\displaystyle\mathsf{md}(\Diamond\varphi)

\displaystyle\vcentcolon=\mathsf{md}(\Box\varphi)

\displaystyle\vcentcolon=\mathsf{md}(\varphi)+1

\mathsf{ML}\xspace_{k}
and
\mathsf{MTL}\xspace_{k}
are the fragments of
\mathsf{ML}\xspace
and

with modal depth

If the propositions are restricted to a fixed set

Let

be finite.
A

(over

or

Occasionally, by slight abuse of notation, we use the inverse mapping

By contrast,
\mathsf{MTL}\xspace
is evaluated on pairs

An

Intuitively,

In the literature on team semantics, the empty team is usually excluded in the above definition, since most

\top\lor\varphi

In fact,
\top\lor\varphi
is a tautology if and only if

\varphi
is satisfied by some non-empty team iff

\mathsf{MTL}\xspace_{0}

The usual interpretations of the latter, i.e.

For this reason, we treat

and

Note that the connectives

Using

The notation

To express that at least one element of a team satisfies

\mathsf{MTL}\xspace
can express the
(extended) dependence atom

{=\!\!(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n-1},\alpha_{n})}
of (extended) modal dependence logic
[
Vää08
,
EHM+13
]
, which states that the truth value of
\alpha_{n}
is a function of the truth values of
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n-1}
, where
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n}\in\mathsf{ML}\xspace
.
It is definable in
\mathsf{MTL}\xspace
as

{\sim}\left[\top\lor{\sim}\left(\bigwedge_{i=1}^{n-1}{=\!\!(\alpha_{i})}\rightarrowtriangle{=\!\!(\alpha_{n})}\right)\right]
,
where
{=\!\!(\alpha)}\vcentcolon=\alpha\ovee\neg\alpha
is the
constancy atom
, stating that the truth value of
\alpha\in\mathsf{ML}\xspace
is constant throughout the team.

The well-known
bisimulation
relation
\rightleftharpoons^{\Phi}_{k}
fundamentally characterizes the expressive power of modal logic
[
BRV01
]
and plays a key role in our results.

{defi}

Let
\Phi\subseteq\mathcal{PS}
and
k\geq 0
.
For
i\in\{1,2\}
, let
(\mathcal{K}_{i},w_{i})
be a pointed structure, where
\mathcal{K}_{i}=(W_{i},R_{i},V_{i})
.
Then

(\mathcal{K}_{1},w_{1})
and
(\mathcal{K}_{2},w_{2})
are
(\Phi,k)
-bisimilar
, in symbols
(\mathcal{K}_{1},w_{1})\rightleftharpoons^{\Phi}_{k}(\mathcal{K}_{2},w_{2})
, if

•

\forall p\in\Phi\colon w_{1}\in V_{1}(p)\Leftrightarrow w_{2}\in V_{2}(p)
,

•

and if
k>0
,

–

\forall v_{1}\in R_{1}w_{1}\colon\exists v_{2}\in R_{2}w_{2}\colon(\mathcal{K}_{1},v_{1})\rightleftharpoons^{\Phi}_{k-1}(\mathcal{K}_{2},v_{2})
(
forward
condition),

–

\forall v_{2}\in R_{2}w_{2}\colon\exists v_{1}\in R_{1}w_{1}\colon(\mathcal{K}_{1},v_{1})\rightleftharpoons^{\Phi}_{k-1}(\mathcal{K}_{2},v_{2})
(
backward
condition).

So-called
characteristic formulas
or
Hintikka formulas
capture the essence of the bisimulation relation in the following sense:

{propC}

[
[
GO07
, Theorem 32]
]
Let
\Phi\subseteq\mathcal{PS}
be finite,
k\geq 0
, and let
(\mathcal{K},w)
be a pointed structure.
Then there is a formula
\zeta\in\mathsf{ML}\xspace^{\Phi}_{k}
such that for all pointed structures
(\mathcal{K}^{\prime},w^{\prime})
we have
(\mathcal{K}^{\prime},w^{\prime})\vDash\zeta
if and only if
(\mathcal{K},w)\rightleftharpoons^{\Phi}_{k}(\mathcal{K}^{\prime},w^{\prime})
.

The notion of bisimulation was lifted to team semantics by Hella et al.
[
HLSV14
,
KMSV17
,
KMSV15
]
:

{defi}

Let
\Phi\subseteq\mathcal{PS}
and
k\geq 0
.
For
i\in\{1,2\}
, let
(\mathcal{K}_{i},T_{i})
be a structure with team.
Then
(\mathcal{K}_{1},T_{1})
and
(\mathcal{K}_{2},T_{2})
are
(\Phi,k)
-team-bisimilar
, written
(\mathcal{K}_{1},T_{1})\rightleftharpoons^{\Phi}_{k}(\mathcal{K}_{2},T_{2})
, if

•

\forall w_{1}\in T_{1}\colon\exists w_{2}\in T_{2}\colon(\mathcal{K}_{1},w_{1})\rightleftharpoons^{\Phi}_{k}(\mathcal{K}_{2},w_{2})
,

•

\forall w_{2}\in T_{2}\colon\exists w_{1}\in T_{1}\colon(\mathcal{K}_{1},w_{1})\rightleftharpoons^{\Phi}_{k}(\mathcal{K}_{2},w_{2})
.

If no confusion can arise, we will also refer to teams
T_{1},T_{2}
that are
(\Phi,k)
-team-bisimilar simply as
(\Phi,k)
-bisimilar.
Throughout the paper, we will make use of the following characterizations of bisimilarity.

Proposition 3
.

Let
\Phi\subseteq\mathcal{PS}
be finite, and
k\geq 0
.
For
i\in\{1,2\}
, let
(\mathcal{K}_{i},w_{i})
be a pointed structure, where
\mathcal{K}_{i}=(W_{i},R_{i},V_{i})
.
The following statements are equivalent:

(1)

\forall\alpha\in\mathsf{ML}\xspace^{\Phi}_{k}\colon(\mathcal{K}_{1},w_{1})\vDash\alpha\Leftrightarrow(\mathcal{K}_{2},w_{2})\vDash\alpha
,

(2)

(\mathcal{K}_{1},w_{1})\rightleftharpoons^{\Phi}_{k}(\mathcal{K}_{2},w_{2})
,

(3)

(\mathcal{K}_{1},\{w_{1}\})\rightleftharpoons^{\Phi}_{k}(\mathcal{K}_{2},\{w_{2}\})
,

and if
k>0
,

(4)

(\mathcal{K}_{1},w_{1})\rightleftharpoons^{\Phi}_{0}(\mathcal{K}_{2},w_{2})
and
(\mathcal{K}_{1},R_{1}w_{1})\rightleftharpoons^{\Phi}_{k-1}(\mathcal{K}_{2},R_{2}w_{2})
.

Proof 3.1
.

(1)
\Leftrightarrow
(2) is a standard result (
[
GO07
, Theorem 32]
).
(2)
\Leftrightarrow
(3) follows from Definition
3
.
For
k>0
, we show that (2) + (3) implies (4).
Clearly,
(\mathcal{K}_{1},w_{1})\rightleftharpoons^{\Phi}_{0}(\mathcal{K}_{2},w_{2})
follows from (2).
Due to Hella et al.
[
HLSV14
, Lemma 3.3]
, (3) implies
(\mathcal{K}_{1},R_{1}w_{1})\rightleftharpoons^{\Phi}_{k-1}(\mathcal{K}_{2},R_{2}w_{2})
.

Finally, we show (4)
\Rightarrow
(2).
Suppose
(\mathcal{K}_{1},w_{1})\rightleftharpoons^{\Phi}_{0}(\mathcal{K}_{2},w_{2})
and
(\mathcal{K}_{1},R_{1}w_{1})\rightleftharpoons^{\Phi}_{k-1}(\mathcal{K}_{2},R_{2}w_{2})
.
Then to show
(\mathcal{K}_{1},w_{1})\rightleftharpoons^{\Phi}_{k}(\mathcal{K}_{2},w_{2})
, it is sufficient to prove the
forward
and
backward
conditions of Definition
3
.
Suppose
v_{1}\in R_{1}w_{1}
.
Since
(\mathcal{K}_{1},R_{1}w_{1})\rightleftharpoons^{\Phi}_{k-1}(\mathcal{K}_{2},R_{2}w_{2})
, by Definition
3
there exists
v_{2}\in R_{2}w_{2}
such that
(\mathcal{K}_{1},v_{1})\rightleftharpoons^{\Phi}_{k-1}(\mathcal{K}_{2},v_{2})
, proving the
forward
condition.
The
backward
condition is symmetric.

As a consequence, the
forward
and
backward
condition from Definition
3
can be equivalently stated in terms of team-bisimilarity of the respective image teams.
A similar characterization exists for team-bisimilarity:

Proposition 4
.

Let
\Phi\subseteq\mathcal{PS}
be finite, and
k\geq 0
.
Let
(\mathcal{K}_{i},T_{i})
be a structure with team for
i\in\{1,2\}
.
Then the following statements are equivalent:

(1)

\forall\alpha\in\mathsf{ML}\xspace^{\Phi}_{k}\colon(\mathcal{K}_{1},T_{1})\vDash\alpha\Leftrightarrow(\mathcal{K}_{2},T_{2})\vDash\alpha
,

(2)

\forall\varphi\in\mathsf{MTL}\xspace^{\Phi}_{k}\colon(\mathcal{K}_{1},T_{1})\vDash\varphi\Leftrightarrow(\mathcal{K}_{2},T_{2})\vDash\varphi
,

(3)

(\mathcal{K}_{1},T_{1})\rightleftharpoons^{\Phi}_{k}(\mathcal{K}_{2},T_{2})
.

Proof 3.2
.

The above statements are all true if
T_{1}=T_{2}=\emptyset
, and they are all false if exactly one of the teams is empty, since a team
T
satisfies the
\mathsf{ML}\xspace
-formula
\bot
precisely if
T=\emptyset
.
For this reason, we can assume that both
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
are non-empty.

By Kontinen et al.
[
KMSV15
, Proposition 3.10]
, for non-empty
T_{1},T_{2}
there exists an
\mathsf{MTL}\xspace^{\Phi}_{k}
-formula
\varphi
that is true in
(\mathcal{K}_{1},T_{1})
, but holds in
(\mathcal{K}_{2},T_{2})
if and only if
(\mathcal{K}_{1},T_{1})\rightleftharpoons^{\Phi}_{k}(\mathcal{K}_{2},T_{2})
.
This immediately proves (2)
\Rightarrow
(3).
The direction (3)
\Rightarrow
(2) is due to Kontinen et al.
[
KMSV15
, Proposition 2.8]
as well.

Finally, (1)
\Leftrightarrow
(2) follows from the fact that
\mathsf{ML}\xspace^{\Phi}_{k}\subseteq\mathsf{MTL}\xspace^{\Phi}_{k}
, and that conversely every
\mathsf{MTL}\xspace^{\Phi}_{k}
-formula is equivalent to a formula of the form

\bigovee_{i=1}^{n}\Big(\alpha_{i}\land\bigwedge_{j=1}^{m_{i}}\mathsf{E}\beta_{i,j}\Big)\text{,}

where
\{\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n},\beta_{1,1},\ldots,\beta_{n,m_{n}}\}\subseteq\mathsf{ML}\xspace^{\Phi}_{k}
(see
[
Lüc18a
, Theorem 5.2]
or
[
KMSV15
, p. 11]
).

Note that the analog of condition
4
in Proposition
3
for team bisimulation is not equivalent: It is possible that
(\mathcal{K}_{1},T_{1})\rightleftharpoons^{\Phi}_{0}(\mathcal{K}_{2},T_{2})
and

(\mathcal{K}_{1},R_{1}T_{1})\rightleftharpoons^{\Phi}_{k-1}(\mathcal{K}_{2},R_{2}T_{2})
, but
(\mathcal{K}_{1},T_{1})\not\rightleftharpoons^{\Phi}_{k}(\mathcal{K}_{2},T_{2})
.

4.
Types and canonical models

Many modal logics admit a
“universal”
model, also called
canonical model
.
The defining property of a canonical model is that it simultaneously witnesses all satisfiable (sets of) formulas in some of its points.
These models are a popular tool for proving the completeness of manifold systems of modal logics; for the explicit construction of such a model for
\mathsf{ML}\xspace
, consult, e.g.
\xspace
, Blackburn et al.
[
BRV01
, Section 4.2]
.

Unfortunately, any canonical model for
\mathsf{ML}\xspace
is necessarily infinite, and consequently impractical for complexity theoretic considerations.
Instead, we use so-called
(\Phi,k)
-canonical models
for finite
\Phi\subseteq\mathcal{PS}
and
k\in\mathbb{N}\xspace
; as the name suggests they are canonical for the fragment
\mathsf{ML}\xspace^{\Phi}_{k}
.
While these models are finite, by Proposition
3
their size is at least the number of equivalence classes of
\rightleftharpoons^{\Phi}_{k}
.
We call the equivalence classes of
\rightleftharpoons^{\Phi}_{k}

types
.

A first issue arises since types are then proper classes, and in team semantics, we need to speak about
sets
of types.
For this reason, we begin this section by defining types on proper set-theoretic grounds, by indentifying the type of a point with the set of formulas that are true in it, which is a standard approach in first-order model theory.

4.1.
Types

{defi}

A set
\tau\subseteq\mathsf{ML}\xspace^{\Phi}_{k}
is a
(\Phi,k)
-type
if it is satisfiable and for all
\alpha\in\mathsf{ML}\xspace^{\Phi}_{k}
contains either
\alpha
or
\neg\alpha
.
The
(\Phi,k)
-type of a pointed structure
(\mathcal{K},w)
is

\llbracket{}\mathcal{K},w\rrbracket{}^{\Phi}_{k}\vcentcolon=\big\{\alpha\in\mathsf{ML}\xspace^{\Phi}_{k}\mid(\mathcal{K},w)\vDash\alpha\big\}.

The set of all
(\Phi,k)
-types is
\Delta^{\Phi}_{k}
.
Given a team
T
in
\mathcal{K}
, the types in
T
are

\llbracket{}\mathcal{K},T\rrbracket{}^{\Phi}_{k}\vcentcolon=\big\{\llbracket{}\mathcal{K},w\rrbracket{}^{\Phi}_{k}\mid w\in T\big\}.

The following assertions ascertain that the above definition of types properly reflects the bisimulation relation.

Proposition 5
.

Let
\Phi\subseteq\mathcal{PS}
and
k\geq 0
.
Then

(1)

The unique
(\Phi,k)
-type satisfied by
(\mathcal{K},w)
is
\llbracket{}\mathcal{K},w\rrbracket{}^{\Phi}_{k}
.

(2)

(\mathcal{K},w)\rightleftharpoons^{\Phi}_{k}(\mathcal{K}^{\prime},w^{\prime})
if and only if
\llbracket{}\mathcal{K},w\rrbracket{}^{\Phi}_{k}=\llbracket{}\mathcal{K}^{\prime},w^{\prime}\rrbracket{}^{\Phi}_{k}
.

(3)

(\mathcal{K},T)\rightleftharpoons^{\Phi}_{k}(\mathcal{K}^{\prime},T^{\prime})
if and only if
\llbracket{}\mathcal{K},T\rrbracket{}^{\Phi}_{k}=\llbracket{}\mathcal{K}^{\prime},T^{\prime}\rrbracket{}^{\Phi}_{k}
.

Proof 4.1
.

Property (1) is straightforward: two distinct types
\tau,\tau^{\prime}
satisfied by
(\mathcal{K},w)
differ in some
\alpha\in\mathsf{ML}\xspace^{\Phi}_{k}
.
But then
(\mathcal{K},w)\vDash\alpha,\neg\alpha
, contradiction.
Property (2) immediately follows from Proposition
3
.
For (3), first consider
“
\Rightarrow
”
.
Due to symmetry, we only show that
(\mathcal{K},T)\rightleftharpoons^{\Phi}_{k}(\mathcal{K}^{\prime},T^{\prime})
implies
\llbracket{}\mathcal{K},T\rrbracket{}^{\Phi}_{k}\subseteq\llbracket{}\mathcal{K}^{\prime},T^{\prime}\rrbracket{}^{\Phi}_{k}
.
Hence suppose
\tau\in\llbracket{}\mathcal{K},T\rrbracket{}^{\Phi}_{k}
.
Then there exists
w\in T
of type
\llbracket{}\mathcal{K},w\rrbracket{}^{\Phi}_{k}=\tau
.
By Definition
3
, there is
w^{\prime}\in T^{\prime}
with
(\mathcal{K},w)\rightleftharpoons^{\Phi}_{k}(\mathcal{K}^{\prime},w^{\prime})
.
Then
\llbracket{}\mathcal{K}^{\prime},w^{\prime}\rrbracket{}^{\Phi}_{k}=\tau\in\llbracket{}\mathcal{K}^{\prime},T^{\prime}\rrbracket{}^{\Phi}_{k}
by property (2).
The direction
“
\Leftarrow
”
of (3) is shown analogously.

It is unsurprising that the type of a point
w
is determined solely by the propositions in
w
and the types in the image
Rw
.
In other words, all pointed structures of type
\tau
satisfy the same propositions in their roots, viz.
\tau\cap\Phi
, and have the same types contained in their image teams.
Regarding the latter, we define

\mathcal{R}\tau\vcentcolon=\big\{\tau^{\prime}\in\Delta^{\Phi}_{k}\mid\{\alpha\mid\Box\alpha\in\tau\}\subseteq\tau^{\prime}\big\}
, given a
(\Phi,k+1)
-type
\tau
.
Intuitively,
\mathcal{R}\tau
is the set of
(\Phi,k)
-types that occur in the image team of a world of type
\tau
.

The following proposition shows that types are indeed uniquely determined by the above constituents:

Proposition 6
.

Let
\Phi\subseteq\mathcal{PS}
be finite and
k\geq 0
.

(1)

\llbracket{}w\rrbracket{}^{\Phi}_{k}\cap\Phi=V^{-1}(w)\cap\Phi
and
\llbracket{}Rw\rrbracket{}^{\Phi}_{k}=\mathcal{R}\llbracket{}w\rrbracket{}^{\Phi}_{k+1}
, for all pointed structures
(W,R,V,w)
.

(2)

The mapping
h\colon\tau\mapsto\tau\cap\Phi
is a bijection from
\Delta^{\Phi}_{0}
to
\mathfrak{P}(\Phi)
.

(3)

The mapping
h\colon\tau\mapsto(\tau\cap\Phi,\mathcal{R}\tau)
is a bijection from
\Delta^{\Phi}_{k+1}
to
\mathfrak{P}(\Phi)\times\mathfrak{P}(\Delta^{\Phi}_{k})
.

Proof 4.2
.

See the appendix.

Lemma 7
.

Let
(W,R,V,w)
be a pointed structure.

(1)

If
\tau\in\Delta^{\Phi}_{0}
, then
\llbracket{}w\rrbracket{}^{\Phi}_{0}=\tau
if and only if
V^{-1}(w)=\tau\cap\Phi
.

(2)

If
\tau\in\Delta^{\Phi}_{k+1}
, then
\llbracket{}w\rrbracket{}^{\Phi}_{k+1}=\tau
if and only if
V^{-1}(w)=\tau\cap\Phi
and
\llbracket{}Rw\rrbracket{}^{\Phi}_{k}=\mathcal{R}\tau
.

Proof 4.3
.

The direction
“
\Rightarrow
”
of 1. and 2. follows directly from Proposition
6
.
Moreover, we prove
“
\Leftarrow
”
only for statement 2., as the proof is analogous for 1.

Suppose that there are
\tau,\tau^{\prime}\in\Delta^{\Phi}_{k+1}
such that
V^{-1}(w)=\tau\cap\Phi
and
\llbracket{}Rw\rrbracket{}^{\Phi}_{k}=\mathcal{R}\tau
, but
\llbracket{}w\rrbracket{}^{\Phi}_{k+1}=\tau^{\prime}
.
Then, by
“
\Rightarrow
”
, we have
V^{-1}(w)=\tau^{\prime}\cap\Phi
and
\llbracket{}Rw\rrbracket{}^{\Phi}_{k}=\mathcal{R}\tau^{\prime}
as well.
In other words,
\tau\cap\Phi=\tau^{\prime}\cap\Phi
and
\mathcal{R}\tau=\mathcal{R}\tau^{\prime}
.
However, since the mapping
h\colon\tau\mapsto(\tau\cap\Phi,\mathcal{R}\tau)
is bijective according to Proposition
6
, we have
\tau=\tau^{\prime}=\llbracket{}w\rrbracket{}^{\Phi}_{k+1}
.

We are now ready to state the formal definition of canonicity by the notion of types:

{defi}

A structure with team
(\mathcal{K},T)
is
(\Phi,k)
-canonical
if
\llbracket{}\mathcal{K},T\rrbracket{}^{\Phi}_{k}=\Delta^{\Phi}_{k}
.

In the following, we often omit
\Phi
and
\mathcal{K}
and instead write
\llbracket{}w\rrbracket{}_{k}
and
\llbracket{}T\rrbracket{}_{k}
, respectively, and simply say that
T
is
(\Phi,k)
-canonical if
\mathcal{K}
is clear.

4.2.
Canonical models in team semantics

It is a standard result that for every
\Phi
and
k\geq 0
there exists a
(\Phi,k)
-canonical model
[
BRV01
]
, or in other words, that the logic
\mathsf{ML}\xspace^{\Phi}_{k}
admits canonical models.

We will show that, given a
(\Phi,k)
-canonical model
\mathcal{K}
, every satisfiable
\mathsf{MTL}\xspace^{\Phi}_{k}
-formula can be satisfied in some team of
\mathcal{K}
as well, despite
\mathsf{MTL}\xspace
being significantly more expressive than
\mathsf{ML}\xspace

[
KMSV15
]
.
In other words, the canonical models for
\mathsf{MTL}\xspace^{\Phi}_{k}
and
\mathsf{ML}\xspace^{\Phi}_{k}
coincide:

Theorem 8
.

Let
(\mathcal{K},T)
be
(\Phi,k)
-canonical and
\varphi\in\mathsf{MTL}\xspace^{\Phi}_{k}
.
Then
\varphi
is satisfiable if and only if
(\mathcal{K},T^{\prime})\vDash\varphi
for some
T^{\prime}\subseteq T
.

Proof 4.4
.

Assume
(\mathcal{K},T)
and
\varphi
are as above.
As the direction from right to left is trivial, suppose that
\varphi

is satisfiable, i.e.
\xspace
, has a model
(\hat{\mathcal{K}},\hat{T})
.
As a team in
\mathcal{K}
that satisfies
\varphi
, we define

T^{\prime}\vcentcolon=\Set{w\in T}{\llbracket{}\mathcal{K},w\rrbracket{}^{\Phi}_{k}\in\llbracket{}\hat{\mathcal{K}},\hat{T}\rrbracket{}^{\Phi}_{k}}\text{.}

By Proposition
4
and
5
, it suffices to prove
\llbracket{}\hat{\mathcal{K}},\hat{T}\rrbracket{}^{\Phi}_{k}=\llbracket{}\mathcal{K},T^{\prime}\rrbracket{}^{\Phi}_{k}
.
Moreover, the direction
“
\supseteq
”
is clear by definition.
As
T
is
(\Phi,k)
-canonical, for every
\tau\in\llbracket{}\hat{\mathcal{K}},\hat{T}\rrbracket{}^{\Phi}_{k}
there exists a world
w\in T
of type
\tau
.
Consequently,
\llbracket{}\hat{\mathcal{K}},\hat{T}\rrbracket{}^{\Phi}_{k}\subseteq\llbracket{}\mathcal{K},T^{\prime}\rrbracket{}^{\Phi}_{k}
.

How large is a
(\Phi,k)
-canonical model at least?
The number of types is captured by the function
\exp^{*}_{k}
, defined by

\exp^{*}_{0}(n)\vcentcolon=n\qquad\qquad\exp^{*}_{k+1}(n)\vcentcolon=n\cdot 2^{\exp^{*}_{k}(n)}\text{.}

Proposition 9
.

{|\penalty\Delta^{\Phi}_{k}\penalty|}=\exp^{*}_{k}\big(2^{|\penalty\Phi\penalty|}\big)
for all
k\geq 0
and finite
\Phi\subseteq\mathcal{PS}
.

Proof 4.5
.

By induction on
k
.
For the base case
k=0
, this follows from Proposition
6
, as there is a bijection between
\Delta^{\Phi}_{0}
and
\mathfrak{P}(\Phi)
and
\exp^{*}_{0}\big(2^{|\penalty\Phi\penalty|}\big)=2^{|\penalty\Phi\penalty|}={|\penalty\Delta^{\Phi}_{0}\penalty|}
.

We proceed with the inductive step, i.e.
\xspace
,
k+1
.
First note that by induction hypothesis

\exp^{*}_{k+1}\big(2^{|\penalty\Phi\penalty|}\big)=2^{|\penalty\Phi\penalty|}\cdot 2^{\exp^{*}_{k}(2^{|\penalty\Phi\penalty|})}={|\penalty\mathfrak{P}(\Phi)\times\mathfrak{P}(\Delta^{\Phi}_{k})\penalty|}\text{.}

Again, there exists a bijection from
\Delta^{\Phi}_{k+1}
to
\mathfrak{P}(\Phi)\times\mathfrak{P}(\Delta^{\Phi}_{k})
by Proposition
6
.

Next, we present an algorithm that solves the satisfiability and validity problems of
\mathsf{MTL}\xspace_{k}
by computing a canonical model.
Let us first explicate this construction in a lemma.

Lemma 10
.

There is an algorithm that, given
\Phi\subseteq\mathcal{PS}
and
k\geq 0
, computes a
(\Phi,k)
-canonical model in time polynomial in
{|\penalty\Delta^{\Phi}_{k}\penalty|}
.

Proof 4.6
.

The idea is to construct sets
L_{0}\cup L_{1}\cup\cdots\cup L_{k}
of worlds in stage-wise manner such that
L_{i}
is
(\Phi,i)
-canonical.
For
L_{0}
, we simply add a world
w
for each
\Phi^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{P}(\Phi)
such that
V^{-1}(w)=\Phi^{\prime}
.
For
i>0
, we iterate over all
L^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{P}(L_{i-1})
and
\Phi^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{P}(\Phi)
and insert a new world
w
into
L_{i}
such that
L^{\prime}
is the image of
w
and such that again
V^{-1}(w)=\Phi^{\prime}
.
An inductive argument based on Proposition
4
and
6
shows that
L_{i}
is
(\Phi,i)
-canonical for all
i\in\{0,\ldots,k\}
.
As
k\leq{|\penalty\Delta^{\Phi}_{k}\penalty|}
, and each
L_{i}
is constructed in time polynomial in
{|\penalty\Delta^{\Phi}_{i}\penalty|}\leq{|\penalty\Delta^{\Phi}_{k}\penalty|}
, the overall runtime is polynomial in
{|\penalty\Delta^{\Phi}_{k}\penalty|}
.

With the help of a small lemma, we conclude the upper bound for the satisfiability and validity problem of
\mathsf{MTL}\xspace
and its fragments.

Lemma 11
.

For every polynomial
p
there is a polynomial
q
such that

p(\exp^{*}_{k}(n))\leq\exp_{k}(q((k+1)\cdot n))

for all
k\geq 0
and
n\geq 1
.

Proof 4.7
.

See the appendix.

Theorem 12
.

\mathsf{SAT}(\mathsf{MTL}\xspace_{k})
and
\mathsf{VAL}(\mathsf{MTL}\xspace_{k})
are in
\mathrm{ATIME}\xspace\text{-}\mathrm{ALT}\xspace(\exp_{k+1},\mathrm{poly})
.

Proof 4.8
.

Consider the following algorithm.
Let
\varphi\in\mathsf{MTL}\xspace_{k}
be the input,
n\vcentcolon={|\penalty\varphi\penalty|}
, and
\Phi\vcentcolon=\mathsf{Prop}(\varphi)
.
Construct deterministically, as in Lemma
10
, a
(\Phi,k)
-canonical structure
\mathcal{K}=(W,R,V)
in time
p({|\penalty\Delta^{\Phi}_{k}\penalty|})
for a polynomial
p
.

By a result of Müller
[
Mül14
]
, the model checking problem of
\mathsf{MTL}\xspace
is solvable by an alternating Turing machine that has runtime polynomial in
{|\penalty\varphi\penalty|}+{|\penalty\mathcal{K}\penalty|}
, and alternations polynomial in
{|\penalty\varphi\penalty|}
.
We call this algorithm as a subroutine:
by Theorem
8
,
\varphi
is satisfiable (resp. valid) if and only if
for at least one subteam (resp. all subteams)
T\subseteq W
we have
(\mathcal{K},T)\vDash\varphi
.
Equivalently, this is the case if and only if
(\mathcal{K},W)
satisfies
\top\lor\varphi
(resp.
{\sim}(\top\lor{\sim}\varphi)
).

Let us turn to the overall runtime.

\mathcal{K}
is constructed in time polynomial in
{|\penalty\Delta^{\Phi}_{k}\penalty|}=\exp^{*}_{k}(2^{|\penalty\Phi\penalty|})\leq\exp^{*}_{k+1}({|\penalty\Phi\penalty|})\leq\exp^{*}_{k+1}(n)
.
The subsequent model checking runs in time polynomial in
{|\penalty\mathcal{K}\penalty|}+n
, and hence polynomial in
\exp^{*}_{k+1}(n)
as well.
By Lemma
11
, we obtain a total runtime of
\exp_{k+1}(q((k+2)\cdot n))
for a polynomial
q
.

The upper bound for
\mathsf{MTL}\xspace
is proved identically, since
k\vcentcolon=\mathsf{md}(\varphi)
is polynomial in
{|\penalty\varphi\penalty|}
.

Corollary 13
.

\mathsf{SAT}(\mathsf{MTL}\xspace)
and
\mathsf{VAL}(\mathsf{MTL}\xspace)
are in
\mathrm{TOWER}\xspace(\mathrm{poly})
.

The usual definition of a canonical model is a structure that has all (infinite) maximal consistent subsets of a certain class of modal formulas as worlds (see virtually any textbook on modal logic, e.g.
\xspace
,
[
BRV01
]
).
This indeed results in a finite number of worlds in the case of, say,
\mathsf{ML}\xspace^{\Phi}_{k}
(cf.
[
Cre83
,
CH96
]
).
Truly finitary constructions of canonical models can be traced back to Fine
[
Fin75
]
, whose work has been extended towards various other modal systems (e.g.
\xspace
, by Moss
[
Mos07
]
).
Furthermore, Cresswell and Hughes
[
CH96
]
used
mini canonical models
, models that are
“canonical”
only with respect to all subformulas of a fixed
\mathsf{ML}\xspace
-formula, which allows them to be finite models with finite sets of formulas as worlds.

All these approaches have in common that they still are non-constructive and intended for completeness proofs.
Even computing a
“mini canonical model”
would not be guaranteed to be feasible enough for
\mathsf{MTL}\xspace
: This would require an explicit translation of a given input
\mathsf{MTL}\xspace^{\Phi}_{k}
-formula to a Boolean combination of
\mathsf{ML}\xspace^{\Phi}_{k}
-formulas first (see the proof of Proposition
4
), and it is open whether there is an elementary translation for every fixed
k
(cf.
[
Lüc18a
]
).

In this light, our approach yields a purely constructive definition of a canonical model (in Lemma
10
), which can easily be plugged into the algorithms used for the above results, and has optimal runtime up to a polynomial.

5.
Scopes and Subteam Quantifiers

Kontinen et al.
[
KMSV15
]
proved that
\mathsf{MTL}\xspace
is expressively complete up to bisimulation: it can define every property of teams that is
(\Phi,k)
-bisimulation invariant, that is closed under
\rightleftharpoons^{\Phi}_{k}
, for some finite
\Phi
and
k
.
Two team properties that fall into this category are in fact
(\Phi,k)
-bisimilarity itself—in the sense that all worlds in a team have the same
(\Phi,k)
-type—as well as
(\Phi,k)
-canonicity.
Consequently, these properties are definable by
\mathsf{MTL}\xspace^{\Phi}_{k}
-formulas.
However, by a simple counting argument, formulas defining arbitrary team properties require non-elementary size w. r. t.
\xspace
\Phi
and
k
.

In this section, we consider a special class of structures, and on these, define
k
-bisimilarity by a formula
\chi_{k}
of polynomial size in
\Phi
and
k
.
(From now on, we always assume some finite
\Phi\subseteq\mathcal{PS}
and omit it in the notation, i.e.
\xspace
, we write
k
-canonicity
,
k
-bisimilarity
,
\rightleftharpoons_{k}
, and so on.)
Afterwards, in Section
6
we devise a formula
\mathsf{canon}_{k}
of polynomial size that expresses
k
-canonicity.

5.1.
Scopes

It is natural to implement
k
-bisimilarity by mutual recursion in the spirit of Proposition
3
: the
(k+1)
-bisimilarity of two points
w,v
is expressed in terms of
k
-team-bisimilarity of
Rw
and
Rv
, and
conversely, to verify
k
-team-bisimilarity of
Rw
and
Rv
, we proceed analogously to the
forward
and
backward
conditions of Definition
3
and reduce the problem to checking
k
-bisimilarity of pairs of points in
Rw
and
Rv
.

\mathsf{MTL}\xspace
-formulas define team properties, but we want to express a
relation
between teams such as
Rw
and
Rv
.
For this reason, we consider the
“marked union”
of
Rw
and
Rv
as a single team using the following tool.
Formally, if
\alpha\in\mathsf{ML}\xspace
, then the
“conditioned”

T_{\alpha}\vcentcolon=\Set{w\in T}{w\vDash\alpha}\text{.}

In the literature,
T_{\alpha}
is also written
T\upharpoonright\alpha

[
Gal15
,
Gal16
,
Gal18
]

The corresponding
“decoding”
operator

\alpha\hookrightarrow\varphi\vcentcolon=\neg\alpha\lor(\alpha\land\varphi)

was introduced by Galliani
[
Gal15
,
Gal16
,
Gal18
]
as well:
\alpha\hookrightarrow\varphi
is true in
T
if and only if
T_{\alpha}\vDash\varphi
.

Now, instead of defining an
n
-ary relation on teams, a formula
\varphi
can define a unary relation—a team property—parameterized by formulas
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n}\in\mathsf{ML}\xspace
.
We emphasize this by writing
\varphi(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n})
.

It will be useful if the
“markers”
of the constituent teams are invariant under traversing edges in the structure.
In that case,
we call these formulas

:

{defi}

\mathcal{K}=(W,R,V)
be a Kripke structure.

is called a

)
if

implies
w\vDash\alpha\Leftrightarrow v\vDash\alpha
.
Two scopes
\alpha,\beta
are called

\mathcal{K}
)
if

and
W_{\beta}
are disjoint.

To avoid interference, we always assume that scopes are formulas in
\mathsf{ML}\xspace^{\mathcal{PS}\setminus\Phi}_{0}
, i.e.
\xspace

\Phi

5.2.
Subteam quantifiers

We refer to the following abbreviations as
subteam quantifiers
, where
\alpha\in\mathsf{ML}\xspace
:

\displaystyle\exists^{\subseteq}_{\alpha}\;\varphi

\displaystyle\vcentcolon=\alpha\lor\varphi

\displaystyle\forall^{\subseteq}_{\alpha}\;\varphi

\displaystyle\vcentcolon={\sim}\exists^{\subseteq}_{\alpha}{\sim}\varphi

\displaystyle\exists^{1}_{\alpha}\;\varphi

\displaystyle\vcentcolon=\exists^{\subseteq}_{\alpha}\left[\mathsf{E}\alpha\land\forall^{\subseteq}_{\alpha}(\mathsf{E}\alpha\rightarrowtriangle\varphi)\right]

\displaystyle\forall^{1}_{\alpha}\;\varphi

\displaystyle\vcentcolon={\sim}\exists^{1}_{\alpha}{\sim}\varphi

Intuitively, they quantify over subteams
S\subseteq T_{\alpha}
or worlds
w\in T_{\alpha}
such that
T^{\alpha}_{S}
resp.
T^{\alpha}_{w}
satisfies
\varphi
.

Proposition 15
.

The subteam quantifiers have the following semantics:

\displaystyle\qquad T\;\vDash\;\;

\displaystyle\exists^{\subseteq}_{\alpha}\varphi

\displaystyle\;\Leftrightarrow\;\exists S\subseteq T_{\alpha}

\displaystyle\colon T^{\alpha}_{S}\vDash\varphi

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad T\;\vDash\;\;

\displaystyle\exists^{1}_{\alpha}\varphi

\displaystyle\;\Leftrightarrow\;\exists w\in T_{\alpha}

\displaystyle\colon T^{\alpha}_{w}\vDash\varphi

\displaystyle\qquad T\;\vDash\;\;

\displaystyle\forall^{\subseteq}_{\alpha}\varphi

\displaystyle\;\Leftrightarrow\;\forall S\subseteq T_{\alpha}

\displaystyle\colon T^{\alpha}_{S}\vDash\varphi

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad T\;\vDash\;\;

\displaystyle\forall^{1}_{\alpha}\varphi

\displaystyle\;\Leftrightarrow\;\forall w\in T_{\alpha}

\displaystyle\colon T^{\alpha}_{w}\vDash\varphi

Proof 5.2
.

We prove the existential cases, as the other ones work dually.

Let us first consider the
“
\Rightarrow
”
direction for
\exists^{\subseteq}_{\alpha}
.
Accordingly, suppose
T\vDash\exists^{\subseteq}_{\alpha}\,\varphi
, i.e.
\xspace
,
T\vDash\alpha\lor\varphi
.
Then there exist
S\subseteq T
and
U\subseteq T_{\alpha}
such that
S\vDash\varphi
and
T=S\cup U
.
Since
U\cap T_{\neg\alpha}=\emptyset
, it holds
T_{\neg\alpha}\subseteq S
.
Moreover,
S=(S\cap T_{\alpha})\cup(S\cap T_{\neg\alpha})=((S\cap T_{\alpha})\cap T_{\alpha})\cup T_{\neg\alpha}=T^{\alpha}_{S\cap T_{\alpha}}
.
Consequently,
T^{\alpha}_{S\cap T_{\alpha}}\vDash\varphi
for some set
S\cap T_{\alpha}\subseteq T_{\alpha}
.

For
“
\Leftarrow
”
, suppose
T^{\alpha}_{S}\vDash\varphi
for some
S\subseteq T_{\alpha}
.
Then
T^{\alpha}_{S}
and
T\setminus T^{\alpha}_{S}
form a division of
T
.
Since
T\setminus T^{\alpha}_{S}=T\setminus\left(T_{\neg\alpha}\cup(T_{\alpha}\cap S)\right)\subseteq T\setminus T_{\neg\alpha}=T_{\alpha}
, it holds
T\setminus T^{\alpha}_{S}\vDash\alpha
.
As a consequence,
T\vDash\alpha\lor\varphi
.

We proceed with
\exists^{1}_{\alpha}
.
For
“
\Rightarrow
”
, suppose that
T\vDash\exists^{1}_{\alpha}\varphi
.
Then there exists
S\subseteq T_{\alpha}
such that
T^{\alpha}_{S}\vDash\mathsf{E}\alpha\land\forall^{\subseteq}_{\alpha}(\mathsf{E}\alpha\rightarrowtriangle\varphi)
.
Since
T^{\alpha}_{S}\vDash\mathsf{E}\alpha
, there exists
w\in{(T^{\alpha}_{S})}_{\alpha}
.
As
\forall^{\subseteq}_{\alpha}
now applies to
{(T^{\alpha}_{S})}^{\alpha}_{\{w\}}=T^{\alpha}_{w}
as well, it follows
T^{\alpha}_{w}\vDash\mathsf{E}\alpha\rightarrowtriangle\varphi
, and consequently
T^{\alpha}_{w}\vDash\varphi
.

Suppose for
“
\Leftarrow
”
that
T^{\alpha}_{w}\vDash\varphi
for some
w\in T_{\alpha}
.
Let
S\subseteq T_{\alpha}
be arbitrary.
If
w\notin S
, then
{(T^{\alpha}_{w})}^{\alpha}_{S}=T^{\alpha}_{\emptyset}\nvDash\mathsf{E}\alpha
, and if
w\in S
, then
{(T^{\alpha}_{w})}^{\alpha}_{S}=T^{\alpha}_{w}\vDash\varphi
.
Therefore, for any
S\subseteq T_{\alpha}
it holds
{(T^{\alpha}_{w})}^{\alpha}_{S}\vDash(\mathsf{E}\alpha\rightarrowtriangle\varphi)
, so
T^{\alpha}_{w}\vDash\forall^{\subseteq}_{\alpha}(\mathsf{E}\alpha\rightarrowtriangle\varphi)
.
Since also
T^{\alpha}_{w}\vDash\mathsf{E}\alpha
, it follows
T\vDash\exists^{\subseteq}_{\alpha}\left[\mathsf{E}\alpha\land\forall^{\subseteq}_{\alpha}(\mathsf{E}\alpha\rightarrowtriangle\varphi)\right]
.

5.3.
Implementing bisimulation

With scopes and subteam quantifiers at our hands, we have all ingredients to implement

-bisimulation.

\displaystyle\chi_{0}(\alpha,\beta)

\displaystyle\vcentcolon=(\alpha\lor\beta)\hookrightarrow\bigwedge_{p\in\Phi}{=\!\!(p)}

\displaystyle\chi_{k+1}(\alpha,\beta)

\displaystyle\vcentcolon=\chi_{0}(\alpha,\beta)\land\Box\chi^{*}_{k}(\alpha,\beta)

\displaystyle\chi^{*}_{k}(\alpha,\beta)

\displaystyle\vcentcolon=(\neg\alpha\land\neg\beta)\ovee\Big(\mathsf{E}\alpha\land\mathsf{E}\beta\land{\sim}\big[(\alpha\ovee\beta)\lor(\mathsf{E}\alpha\land\mathsf{E}\beta\land{\sim}\exists^{1}_{\alpha}\exists^{1}_{\beta}\chi_{k}(\alpha,\beta))\big]\Big)

\chi^{*}_{k}(\alpha,\beta)\vcentcolon=\forall^{1}_{\alpha}\exists^{1}_{\beta}\chi_{k}(\alpha,\beta)\land\forall^{1}_{\beta}\exists^{1}_{\alpha}\chi_{k}(\alpha,\beta)
.
The more complicated formula shown above however avoids the exponential size that would come with two recursive calls.

as no proposition
p\in\Phi
occurs in
\alpha
or
\beta
.
The induction step is split into two parts.

“
\chi_{k}\Rightarrow\chi^{*}_{k}
”
:
Let
T
be a team and
\alpha,\beta
disjoint scopes.
Observe that
\chi^{*}_{k}
is always true if
T_{\alpha}
and
T_{\beta}
are both empty (then
\llbracket{}T_{\alpha}\rrbracket{}_{k}=\llbracket{}T_{\beta}\rrbracket{}_{k}
), and that it is always false if exactly one of them is empty (then
\llbracket{}T_{\alpha}\rrbracket{}_{k}\neq\llbracket{}T_{\beta}\rrbracket{}_{k}
).
Therefore, let
T_{\alpha}\neq\emptyset
and
T_{\beta}\neq\emptyset
.
Then
\chi^{*}_{k}(\alpha,\beta)
boils down to
{\sim}((\alpha\ovee\beta)\lor\mathsf{E}\alpha\land\mathsf{E}\beta\land{\sim}\exists^{1}_{\alpha}\exists^{1}_{\beta}\chi_{k}(\alpha,\beta))
,
which we prove equivalent to
\llbracket{}T_{\alpha}\rrbracket{}_{k}=\llbracket{}T_{\beta}\rrbracket{}_{k}
.

The first direction is proved by contradiction.
Suppose
\llbracket{}T_{\alpha}\rrbracket{}_{k}=\llbracket{}T_{\beta}\rrbracket{}_{k}
but
T\vDash(\alpha\ovee\beta)\lor\mathsf{E}\alpha\land\mathsf{E}\beta\land{\sim}\exists^{1}_{\alpha}\exists^{1}_{\beta}\chi_{k}(\alpha,\beta)
.
The disjunction is witnessed by some division
T=S\cup U
, where w.l.o.g.
\xspace
S\subseteq T_{\alpha}
satisfies
\alpha\ovee\beta
,
(if
S\subseteq T_{\beta}
, the proof is symmetric), and
U\vDash\mathsf{E}\alpha\land\mathsf{E}\beta\land{\sim}\exists^{1}_{\alpha}\exists^{1}_{\beta}\chi_{k}(\alpha,\beta)
.
Since
T_{\alpha}\cap T_{\beta}=\emptyset
, then
T_{\beta}\subseteq U
, and clearly
T_{\beta}\subseteq U_{\beta}
.
By the formula, some
w\in U_{\alpha}
exists.
By assumption that
\llbracket{}T_{\alpha}\rrbracket{}_{k}=\llbracket{}T_{\beta}\rrbracket{}_{k}
,
U_{\beta}
must contain a world
v
of type
\llbracket{}w\rrbracket{}_{k}
as well.
But then
U^{\alpha,\beta}_{w,v}\vDash\chi_{k}(\alpha,\beta)
by induction hypothesis, contradiction to
U\vDash{\sim}\exists^{1}_{\alpha}\exists^{1}_{\beta}\chi_{k}(\alpha,\beta)
.

For the other direction, suppose
\llbracket{}T_{\alpha}\rrbracket{}_{k}\neq\llbracket{}T_{\beta}\rrbracket{}_{k}
.
W.l.o.g.
\xspace
there exists
w\in T_{\alpha}
such that
\llbracket{}w\rrbracket{}_{k}\notin\llbracket{}T_{\beta}\rrbracket{}_{k}
.
(For
w\in T_{\beta}
, the proof is again symmetric.)
Consider
S\vcentcolon=T_{\alpha}\setminus\{w\}
and
U\vcentcolon=T^{\alpha}_{w}
as a division of
T
.
Then
S\vDash\alpha\ovee\beta
and
U\vDash\mathsf{E}\alpha\land\mathsf{E}\beta
.
It remains to show
U\vDash{\sim}\exists^{1}_{\alpha}\exists^{1}_{\beta}\chi_{k}(\alpha,\beta)
.
However, this is easy to see:

U\vDash\exists^{1}_{\alpha}\exists^{1}_{\beta}\chi_{k}(\alpha,\beta)
if and only if
U\vDash\exists^{1}_{\beta}\chi_{k}(\alpha,\beta)
, but
T_{\beta}
and hence
U_{\beta}
contains no world of type
\llbracket{}w\rrbracket{}_{k}
, so by induction hypothesis
U
cannot satisfy
\exists^{1}_{\beta}\chi_{k}(\alpha,\beta)
.

“
\chi^{*}_{k}\Rightarrow\chi_{k+1}
”
:
We follow Definition
3
and Proposition
3
.

\displaystyle T^{\alpha,\beta}_{w,v}\vDash\chi_{k+1}(\alpha,\beta)

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\;

\displaystyle T^{\alpha,\beta}_{w,v}\vDash\chi_{0}(\alpha,\beta)\land\Box\chi^{*}_{k}(\alpha,\beta)

(Definition of
\chi_{k+1}
)

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\;

\displaystyle w\rightleftharpoons_{0}v\text{ and }T^{\alpha,\beta}_{w,v}\vDash\Box\chi^{*}_{k}(\alpha,\beta)

(Induction hypothesis)

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\;

\displaystyle w\rightleftharpoons_{0}v\text{ and }RT^{\alpha,\beta}_{Rw,Rv}\vDash\chi^{*}_{k}(\alpha,\beta)

(Proposition
14
)

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\;

\displaystyle w\rightleftharpoons_{0}v\text{ and }Rw\rightleftharpoons_{k}Rv

(Induction hypothesis)

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\;

\displaystyle w\rightleftharpoons_{k+1}v\text{.}

(Proposition
3
)

It is routine to check that the formulas are constructible in logarithmic space from
\alpha
,
\beta
,
\Phi
and
k
, and that
\mathsf{md}(\chi_{k})=\mathsf{md}(\chi^{*}_{k})=k
.

Let us stress that
\chi_{k}
relies on disjoint scopes to be present in the structure, and it is open whether the property
{|\penalty\llbracket{}T\rrbracket{}_{k}\penalty|}\leq 1
is polynomially definable without these.
Incidentally, the related property
{|\penalty\llbracket{}Rw\rrbracket{}_{k}\penalty|}\leq 1
of points
w
was recently studied by Hella and Vilander
[
HV16
]
, and was proven to be expressible in
\mathsf{ML}\xspace
, but only by formulas of non-elementary size.
However, they proved that it is definable in exponential size in
2-dimensional modal logic

\mathsf{ML}\xspace^{2}
(for an introduction to
\mathsf{ML}\xspace^{2}
, see Marx and Venema
[
MV97
]
).
Roughly speaking,
\mathsf{ML}\xspace^{2}
is evaluated by traversing over
pairs
of points independently.
The relationship between
\mathsf{ML}\xspace^{2}
and
\mathsf{MTL}\xspace
is unclear: Arguably, pairs of points are a special case of teams.
But on the other hand, the modalities in
\mathsf{MTL}\xspace
do not act on the points in a team independently, as in
\mathsf{ML}\xspace^{2}
, but instead always proceed to a successor team
“synchronously”
.
As a consequence, it is also open whether
\mathsf{MTL}\xspace
can define one of the above properties by a formula of elementary size.

6.
Enforcing a canonical model

In this section, we approach the canonical models of
\mathsf{MTL}\xspace
from a lower bound perspective.
Here, we devise an
\mathsf{MTL}\xspace_{k}
-formula that is satisfiable but permits
only

k
-canonical models.

For
k=0
, that is propositional team logic, Hannula et al.
[
HKVV15
]
defined the
\mathsf{PTL}\xspace
-formula

\displaystyle\mathsf{max}(X)\vcentcolon={\sim}\bigvee_{\mathclap{x\in X}}{=\!\!(x)}

and proved that
T\vDash\mathsf{max}(\Phi)
if and only if
T
is
0
-canonical, i.e.
\xspace
, contains all Boolean assignment over
\Phi
.
We generalize this for all
k
, i.e.
\xspace
, construct a satisfiable formula
\mathsf{canon}_{k}
that has only
k
-canonical models.

6.2.
Enforcing canonicity

In the rest of the section, we illustrate how a
k
-staircase can be enforced in
\mathsf{MTL}\xspace
inductively.

For
\Phi=\emptyset
, the inductive step—obtaining
(k+1)
-canonicity from
k
-canonicity—is done by the formula
\forall^{\subseteq}_{\alpha}\,\exists^{1}_{\beta}\,\Box\chi^{*}_{k}(\alpha,\beta)
.
The idea is that this formula states that for every
subteam

T^{\prime}\subseteq T_{\alpha}
there exists a
point

w\in T_{\beta}
such that
\llbracket{}RT^{\prime}\rrbracket{}_{k}=\llbracket{}Rw\rrbracket{}_{k}
.
Intuitively, every possible set of types is captured as the image of some point in
T_{\beta}
.
As a consequence, if
T_{\alpha}
is
k
-canonical with offset
1
, then
T_{\beta}
will be
(k+1)
-canonical.

Note that the simpler formula
\Box^{k}\mathsf{max}(\Phi)
expresses
0
-canonicity of
R^{k}T
, but not
0
-canonicity of
T
with offset
k
(consider, e.g.
\xspace
, a singleton
T
).
Instead, we use the formula

\displaystyle\mathsf{max}_{i}\vcentcolon=\;

\displaystyle\top\lor(\Diamond^{i}\top\land{\sim}\bigvee_{\mathclap{p\in\Phi}}(\Diamond^{i}p\ovee\Diamond^{i}\neg p))\text{.}

It states not only that
R^{i}T
is
0
-canonical, but also that
R^{i}w
contains exactly one propositional assignment for each
w\in T
, which together yields
0
-canonicity with offset
i
.

Lemma 19
.

T\vDash\mathsf{max}_{i}
iff
T
is
0
-canonical with offset
i
.

Proof 6.2
.

By the distributive law
\varphi\lor(\psi_{1}\ovee\psi_{2})\equiv(\varphi\lor\psi_{1})\ovee(\varphi\lor\psi_{2})
, the duality
{\sim}(\psi_{1}\ovee\psi_{2})\equiv{\sim}\psi_{1}\land{\sim}\psi_{2}
, and the definition

\mathsf{E}\psi={\sim}\neg\psi
,

{\sim}\bigvee_{\mathclap{p\in\Phi}}(\Diamond^{i}p\ovee\Diamond^{i}\neg p)\equiv{\sim}\bigovee_{P\subseteq\Phi}\Big(\bigvee_{p\in P}\Diamond^{i}p\lor\bigvee_{\mathclap{p\in\Phi\setminus P}}\Diamond^{i}\neg p\Big)\equiv\bigwedge_{P\subseteq\Phi}\!\!\mathsf{E}\Big(\bigwedge_{p\in P}\Box^{i}\neg p\land\bigwedge_{\mathclap{p\in\Phi\setminus P}}\Box^{i}p\Big)\text{.}

The rightmost formula now states that for all types
\tau\in\Delta_{0}
(each represented by a subset of
\Phi
, cf. Proposition
6
), there exists a world
w\in T
such that
\llbracket{}R^{i}w\rrbracket{}^{\Phi}_{0}\subseteq\{\tau\}
.
Likewise,
T\vDash\Diamond^{i}\top
iff
R^{i}w\neq\emptyset
for every
w\in T
.

Based on this,
k
-canonicity with offset
i
is now recursively defined as
\rho^{i}_{k}
:

\displaystyle\rho^{i}_{0}(\beta)\,

\displaystyle\vcentcolon=\;\beta\hookrightarrow\mathsf{max}_{i}

\displaystyle\rho^{i}_{k+1}(\alpha,\beta)

\displaystyle\,\vcentcolon=\;\forall^{\subseteq}_{\alpha}\,\exists^{\subseteq}_{\beta}\left(\rho^{i}_{0}(\beta)\land\Box^{i}\forall^{1}_{\beta}\;\Box\chi^{*}_{k}(\alpha,\beta)\right)

\displaystyle\mathsf{canon}_{k}\,

\displaystyle\vcentcolon=\;\rho^{k}_{0}(\mathfrak{s}_{0})\land\bigwedge_{m=1}^{k}\rho^{k-m}_{m}(\mathfrak{s}_{m-1},\mathfrak{s}_{m})

Theorem 20
.

Let
k\geq 0
and
\mathcal{K}
be a structure with disjoint scopes
\mathfrak{s}_{0},\ldots,\mathfrak{s}_{k}
.
Then
(\mathcal{K},T)\vDash\mathsf{canon}_{k}
if and only if
(\mathcal{K},T)
is a
k
-staircase.
Moreover,
\mathsf{canon}_{k}
is an
\mathsf{MTL}\xspace_{k}
-formula constructible in space
{\mathcal{O}(\log({|\penalty\Phi\penalty|}+k))}
.

Proof 6.3
.

Similar to Theorem
16
, the construction of the above formula in logspace is straightforward.
We proceed with the correctness of the formula.
Suppose that
\mathfrak{s}_{0},\ldots,\mathfrak{s}_{k}
are disjoint scopes in
\mathcal{K}
.
We show the following by induction on
0\leq i\leq k
:
Assuming that
T_{\alpha}
is
k
-canonical with offset
i+1
, it holds that
T_{\beta}
is
(k+1)
-canonical with offset
i
if and only if
T\vDash\rho^{i}_{k+1}(\alpha,\beta)
.
With the induction basis done in Lemma
19
, the inductive step is proved by the following equivalence:

\displaystyle T_{\beta}\text{ is }(k+1)\text{-canonical with offset }i

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\;

\displaystyle\forall\tau\in\Delta_{k+1}\colon\exists w\in T_{\beta}\colon\llbracket{}R^{i}w\rrbracket{}_{k+1}=\{\tau\}

Using the inverse of the bijection
h\colon\tau\mapsto(\tau\cap\Phi,\mathcal{R}\tau)
from Proposition
6
, we can equivalently quantify over
\mathfrak{P}(\Delta_{k})
and
\mathfrak{P}(\Phi)
:

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\;

\displaystyle\forall\Delta^{\prime}\subseteq\Delta_{k}\colon\forall\Phi^{\prime}\subseteq\Phi\colon\exists w\in T_{\beta}\colon\llbracket{}R^{i}w\rrbracket{}_{k+1}=\{h^{-1}(\Phi^{\prime},\Delta^{\prime})\}

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\;

\displaystyle\forall\Delta^{\prime}\subseteq\Delta_{k}\colon\forall\Phi^{\prime}\subseteq\Phi\colon\exists w\in T_{\beta}\colon R^{i}w\neq\emptyset\text{ and }\forall v\in R^{i}w\colon\llbracket{}v\rrbracket{}_{k+1}=h^{-1}(\Phi^{\prime},\Delta^{\prime})

By Lemma
7
,
V^{-1}(v)=\Phi^{\prime}\text{ and }\llbracket{}Rv\rrbracket{}_{k}=\Delta^{\prime}
is equivalent to
\llbracket{}v\rrbracket{}_{k+1}=h^{-1}(\Phi^{\prime},\Delta^{\prime})
:

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\;

\displaystyle\forall\Delta^{\prime}\subseteq\Delta_{k}\colon\forall\Phi^{\prime}\subseteq\Phi\colon\exists w\in T_{\beta}\colon R^{i}w\neq\emptyset

\displaystyle\qquad\text{and }\forall v\in R^{i}w\colon V^{-1}(v)=\Phi^{\prime}\text{ and }\llbracket{}Rv\rrbracket{}_{k}=\Delta^{\prime}

Again by Proposition
6
,
h\colon\tau\mapsto\tau\cap\Phi
is a bijection from
\Delta_{0}
to
\mathfrak{P}(\Phi)
:

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\;

\displaystyle\forall\Delta^{\prime}\subseteq\Delta_{k}\colon\forall\tau_{0}\in\Delta_{0}\colon\exists w\in T_{\beta}\colon R^{i}w\neq\emptyset

\displaystyle\qquad\text{and }\forall v\in R^{i}w\colon V^{-1}(v)=\tau_{0}\cap\Phi\text{ and }\llbracket{}Rv\rrbracket{}_{k}=\Delta^{\prime}

Once more by Lemma
7
:

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\;

\displaystyle\forall\Delta^{\prime}\subseteq\Delta_{k}\colon\forall\tau_{0}\in\Delta_{0}\colon\exists w\in T_{\beta}\colon R^{i}w\neq\emptyset

\displaystyle\qquad\text{and }\forall v\in R^{i}w\colon\llbracket{}v\rrbracket{}_{0}=\tau_{0}\text{ and }\llbracket{}Rv\rrbracket{}_{k}=\Delta^{\prime}

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\;

\displaystyle\forall\Delta^{\prime}\subseteq\Delta_{k}\colon\forall\tau_{0}\in\Delta_{0}\colon\exists w\in T_{\beta}\colon\llbracket{}R^{i}w\rrbracket{}_{0}=\{\tau_{0}\}\text{ and }\forall v\in R^{i}w\colon\llbracket{}Rv\rrbracket{}_{k}=\Delta^{\prime}

Since
T_{\alpha}
is assumed
k
-canonical with offset
i+1
, for every
\tau^{\prime}\in\Delta_{k}
there exists
u\in T_{\alpha}
such that
\llbracket{}R^{i+1}u\rrbracket{}_{k}=\{\tau^{\prime}\}
.
Accordingly, for every
set

\Delta^{\prime}\subseteq\Delta_{k}
there exists
S\subseteq T_{\alpha}
such that
\llbracket{}R^{i+1}S\rrbracket{}_{k}=\Delta^{\prime}
:

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\;

\displaystyle\forall S\subseteq T_{\alpha}\colon\forall\tau_{0}\in\Delta_{0}\colon\exists w\in T_{\beta}\colon\llbracket{}R^{i}w\rrbracket{}_{0}=\{\tau_{0}\}\text{ and }\forall v\in R^{i}w\colon\llbracket{}Rv\rrbracket{}_{k}=\llbracket{}R^{i+1}S\rrbracket{}_{k}

8.1.

The idea of the reduction is now to capture the alternating computation of

9.
Hardness under strict semantics and on restricted frame classes

9.1.
Lax and strict semantics

In this section, we further generalize the hardness result of the previous section.

Team-semantical connectives can be evaluated either in so-called
standard
or
lax semantics
, or alternatively in
strict semantics
.
In Section
3
, we defined
\mathsf{MTL}\xspace
with lax semantics.
In strict semantics, the connectives
\lor
and
\Diamond
are replaced by their counterparts
\lor_{s}
and
\Diamond_{s}
:

\displaystyle(\mathcal{K},T)\vDash\psi\lor_{s}\theta

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\;\exists S,U\subseteq T\text{ such that }T=S\cup U\text{, }S\cap U=\emptyset\text{, }(\mathcal{K},S)\vDash\psi\text{, and }(\mathcal{K},U)\vDash\theta\text{,}

\displaystyle(\mathcal{K},T)\vDash\Diamond_{s}\psi

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\;(\mathcal{K},S)\vDash\psi\text{ for some strict successor team }S\text{ of }T\text{,}

where a
strict successor team
of
T
is a successor team
S\subseteq RT
for which there exists a surjective
f\colon T\to S
satisfying
f(w)\in Rw
for all
w\in T
.
Intuitively, in the lax disjunction the teams of the splitting may overlap, while in the strict disjunction they are disjoint.
Likewise, a lax successor team may contain multiple successor of any
w\in T
, while in a strict successor team we pick exactly one successor for each
w\in T
.

An
\mathsf{MTL}\xspace
-formula
\varphi
is
downward closed
if
(\mathcal{K},T)\vDash\varphi
implies
(\mathcal{K},S)\vDash\varphi
for all
S\subseteq T
.
For example, every
\mathsf{ML}\xspace
-formula is downward closed, as is the constancy atom
{=\!\!(\alpha)}=\alpha\ovee\neg\alpha
or generally any monotone Boolean combination of
\mathsf{ML}\xspace
-formulas.
On such formulas, strict and lax semantics are equivalent:

Proposition 42
.

Let
\varphi,\psi\in\mathsf{MTL}\xspace
such that
\varphi
is downward closed.
Then
\varphi\lor\psi\equiv\varphi\lor_{s}\psi
and
\Diamond\varphi\equiv\Diamond_{s}\varphi
.

Proof 9.1
.

Clearly
\varphi\lor_{s}\psi
entails
\varphi\lor\psi
and
\Diamond_{s}\varphi
entails
\Diamond\varphi
.
If conversely
T\vDash\varphi\lor\psi
via subteams
S,U\subseteq T
such that
S\cup U=T
,
S\vDash\varphi
and
U\vDash\psi
, then we instead split
T
into the subteams
U
and
T\setminus U
.
Since
T\setminus U\subseteq S
and
\varphi
is downward closed, this proves
T\vDash\varphi\lor_{s}\psi
.

Likewise, suppose
T\vDash\Diamond\varphi
via some successor team
S
of
T
.
Assuming the axiom of choice, there is some function
f\colon T\to S
such that
f(w)\in Rw
for each
w\in T
.
The team
\{f(w)\mid w\in T\}\subseteq S
is now a strict successor team of
T
and satisfies
\varphi
due to downward closure.

Due to Proposition
42
, the distinction between strict and lax semantics was traditionally unnecessary for many team logics such as the original
dependence logic

[
Vää07
,
Vää08
]
, as it has only downward closed formulas.
The distinction between strict and lax semantics was first made in the context of first-order team logic by Galliani
[
Gal12
]
.
It has some interesting consequences, for instance first-order inclusion logic in strict semantics is as expressive as existential second-order logic
[
GHK13
]
(see also Hannula and Kontinen
[
HK15
]
).

With modal team logic, strict semantics was studied, e.g.
\xspace
, by Hella et al.
[
HS15
,
HKMV15
,
HKMV17
]
.
In the works that explicitly study strict semantics, the underlying (first-order or modal) team logic was enriched by not downward closed constructs such as the
inclusion atom

\subseteq
or
exclusion atom

\mid
, or the
independence atom

\perp
.

In this article, where we consider team-wide negation
{\sim}
as part of the logic, the distinction between strict and lax semantics becomes apparent already for simple formulas such as
\mathsf{E}\top\lor\mathsf{E}\top\not\equiv\mathsf{E}\top\lor_{s}\mathsf{E}\top
, where the former defines non-emptiness, but the latter means that the team contains at least two points.

We prove that our hardness results also hold in strict semantics.
Let the logics
\mathsf{MTL}\xspace(\lor_{s},\Box)
and
\mathsf{MTL}\xspace_{k}(\lor_{s},\Box)
be defined like
\mathsf{MTL}\xspace
and
\mathsf{MTL}\xspace_{k}
, but with
\lor_{s}
instead of
\lor
and without
\Diamond
and
\Diamond_{s}
(i.e.
\xspace
, only using the modality
\Box
).

Theorem 43

\mathsf{SAT}(\mathcal{L})
and

if

9.2.
Restricted frame classes

A natural restriction in the context of modal logic is to focus on a specific subclass of Kripke frames, which is useful for instance for modeling belief or temporal systems.
(For an introduction to frame classes, consider, e.g.
\xspace
, Fitting
[
Fit07
]
.)
Let
F=(W,R)
denote a frame.
Prominent frame classes include

\mathsf{K}
:

all frames,

\mathsf{D}
:

serial frames (
w\in W\Rightarrow Rw\neq\emptyset
),

\mathsf{T}
:

reflexive frames (
w\in W\Rightarrow w\in Rw
),

\mathsf{K4}
:

transitive frames (
u\in Rv,v\in Rw,w\in W\Rightarrow u\in Rw
),

\mathsf{D4}
:

serial and transitive frames,

\mathsf{S4}
:

reflexive and transitive frames.

In this section, we consider these classes from a complexity theoretic perspective, and show that the lower bounds of
\mathsf{MTL}\xspace
hold when restricted to these classes.
Given a frame class
\mathcal{F}
and a fragment
\mathcal{L}
of
\mathsf{MTL}\xspace
, let
\mathsf{SAT}(\mathcal{L},\mathcal{F})
denote the set of all
\mathcal{L}
-formulas that are satisfied in a model
(W,R,V,T)
where
(W,R)
is a frame in
\mathcal{F}
.
Define
\mathsf{VAL}(\mathcal{L},\mathcal{F})
analogously.

We prove the team-semantical analog of Ladner’s theorem, which states that classical modal satisfiability and validity are
\mathrm{PSPACE}\xspace
-hard problem for any frame class between
\mathsf{S4}
and
\mathsf{K}

[
Lad77
, Theorem 3.1]
.
Note that this includes all the frame classes stated above.

Theorem 44
.

Let
\mathcal{F}
be a frame class such that
\mathsf{S4}\subseteq\mathcal{F}\subseteq\mathsf{K}
.
Then
\mathsf{SAT}(\mathsf{MTL}\xspace,\mathcal{F})
and
\mathsf{VAL}(\mathsf{MTL}\xspace,\mathcal{F})
are hard for
\mathrm{TOWER}\xspace(\mathrm{poly})
,
and
\mathsf{SAT}(\mathsf{MTL}\xspace_{k},\mathcal{F})
and
\mathsf{VAL}(\mathsf{MTL}\xspace_{k},\mathcal{F})
are hard for
\mathrm{ATIME}\xspace\text{-}\mathrm{ALT}\xspace(\exp_{k+1},\mathrm{poly})
, for
k\geq 0
.

Proof 9.3
.

We give the proof for

Let

The idea is to introduce new propositions

i

Given a

On the side of formulas, the reduction is

The non-modal connectives are ignored, i.e.

For the modalities, let

10.
Conclusion

Theorem
31
settles the complexity of
\mathsf{MTL}\xspace
and proves that its satisfiability and validity problems are complete for the non-elementary complexity class
\mathrm{TOWER}\xspace(\mathrm{poly})
.
Moreover, the fragments
\mathsf{MTL}\xspace_{k}
are proved complete for
\mathrm{ATIME}\xspace\text{-}\mathrm{ALT}\xspace(\exp_{k+1},\mathrm{poly})
, the levels of the elementary hierarchy with polynomially many alternations.

In our approach, we developed a notion of (
k
-)canonical models for modal logics with team semantics. We showed that such models exist for
\mathsf{MTL}\xspace
and
\mathsf{MTL}\xspace_{k}
, and that logspace-computable
\mathsf{MTL}\xspace_{k}
-formulas exist that are satisfiable, but only have
k
-canonical models.

Our lower bounds carry over to two-variable first-order team logic
\mathsf{FO}\xspace^{2}({\sim})
and its fragment
\mathsf{FO}\xspace^{2}_{k}({\sim})
of bounded quantifier rank
k
as well
[
Lüc18c
]
.
While the former is
\mathrm{TOWER}\xspace(\mathrm{poly})
-complete, the latter is
\mathrm{ATIME}\xspace\text{-}\mathrm{ALT}\xspace(\exp_{k+1},\mathrm{poly})
-hard.
However, no matching upper bound for the satisfiability problem of
\mathsf{FO}\xspace^{2}_{k}({\sim})
exists.

In the final section, we considered variants of the satisfiability problem for
\mathsf{MTL}\xspace
.
We showed that it is as hard as the original problem when
\mathsf{MTL}\xspace
is interpreted in strict semantics, and in fact for
\Diamond
-free formulas with
\lor
being interpreted either lax or strict.
Also, any restriction of the satisfiability problem to a frame class that includes at least the reflexive-transitive frames is as hard as the original problem.

In future research, it could be useful to further generalize the concept of canonical models to other logics with team semantics.
Do logics such as
\mathsf{FO}\xspace^{2}_{k}({\sim})
permit a canonical model in the spirit of
k
-canonical models for
\mathsf{MTL}\xspace_{k}
, and does this yield a tight upper bound on the complexity of their satisfiability problem?
How do
\mathsf{MTL}\xspace_{k}
and
\mathsf{FO}\xspace^{2}_{k}({\sim})
differ in terms of succinctness?

Other obvious open questions are the upper bounds for Theorem
43
and
44
, and also the combination of the above aspects, e.g.
\xspace
, does the lower bound still hold in strict semantics on reflexive-transitive frames?
To solve these issues, the model theory of modal team logic has to be refined.
For example, what is the analog of Proposition
4
for strict semantics?

.

\varphi

W.l.o.g.
\xspace
\alpha
occurs in
\varphi
.
If
\varphi=\alpha

\mathrm{Sub}(\varphi,\alpha,\beta)=\beta

.
However, this easily follows from

\mathsf{ML}\xspace
-formulas.

Otherwise,
\alpha

We distinguish the following cases.

•

\varphi=\neg\gamma

\displaystyle T\vDash\neg\mathrm{Sub}(\gamma,\alpha,\beta)

\displaystyle\forall w\in T\colon\{w\}\vDash\neg\mathrm{Sub}(\gamma,\alpha,\beta)

\displaystyle T\vDash\mathrm{Sub}(\psi,\alpha,\beta)\lor\mathrm{Sub}(\theta,\alpha,\beta)

\displaystyle\exists S\subseteq RT,T\subseteq R^{-1}S\colon S\vDash\psi

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\;

\displaystyle T\vDash\varphi
