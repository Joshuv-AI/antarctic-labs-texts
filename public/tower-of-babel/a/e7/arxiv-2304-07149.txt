Linearization of holomorphic Lipschitz functions
Richard Aron, Verónica Dimant, Luis C. García-Lirola, Manuel Maestre

Abstract
. Let X X and Y Y be complex Banach spaces with B X B_{X} denoting the open unit ball of X . X. This paper studies various aspects of the holomorphic Lipschitz space ℋ ​ L 0 ​ ( B X , Y ) \mathcal{H}L_{0}(B_{X},Y) , endowed with the Lipschitz norm. 1.
Introduction

Linearizing non-linear functions is a typical procedure in infinite dimensional analysis. Originating nearly 70 years ago with Grothendieck
[
31
]
(and his research about linearization of bilinear mappings through the projective tensor product), the practice of identifying spaces of continuous
non-linear
functions with spaces of continuous
linear
mappings defined on Banach spaces has proved to be a useful technique.
Accordingly, the study of geometric and topological properties of these
linearizing Banach spaces
has increasingly attracted interest.

Lipschitz functions (defined on pointed metric spaces) and holomorphic bounded functions (defined on the open unit ball of a Banach space) are really different both as sets and as function spaces. However, when looking at their linearization processes several similarities emerge. The purpose of this article is to study, in light of these resemblances, the new set of functions consisting of the intersection of the previous sets. Lipschitz holomorphic functions defined on the open unit ball of a Banach space taking the value 0 at 0 will be our focus of attention. In the exploration of this set we take advantage of a result of Ng
[
39
]
concerning the existence of preduals and all the background about related linearization processes.

We begin with a brief review of important terms and concepts. General references for Lipschitz functions include
[
30
]
and
[
44
]
and a standard reference for holomorphic functions on finite or infinite dimensional domains is
[
38
]
. The linearization process for bounded holomorphic functions is originally developed in
[
36
]
. A review about linearization procedures both for Lipschitz functions and for bounded holomorphic functions appeared in the recent survey
[
26
]
while a general approach to linearizing non-linear sets of functions was settled in
[
17
]
.

Notation

X,Y
will stand for complex Banach spaces. We denote by

(respectively

.

Recall that

2.
The space of holomorphic Lipschitz functions and its predual

In the case that the metric space
M
is
B_{X}
, the open unit ball of a complex Banach space
X
, and
Y
is another complex Banach space,

\Lip_{0}(B_{X},Y)
is the space of Lipschitz functions
f\colon B_{X}\to Y
with
f(0)=0

and:

L(f)=\sup\left\{\frac{\|f(x)-f(y)\|}{\|x-y\|}:\ x\neq y\in B_{X}\right\}.

It is well known that
L(\cdot)
defines a norm on
\Lip_{0}(B_{X},Y)
and
(\Lip_{0}(B_{X},Y),L(\cdot))
is a Banach space. Indeed,
\Lip_{0}(B_{X},Y)
is isometrically isomorphic to the space of operators
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{F}(B_{X}),Y)
, where
\mathcal{F}(B_{X})
denotes the
Lipschitz-free space
over
B_{X}
(see e.g.
[
28
,
44
]
).

Next,
\mathcal{H}^{\infty}(B_{X},Y)
stands for the space of bounded holomorphic functions from
B_{X}
to
Y
, which is a Banach space when endowed with the supremum norm. Analogous to the Lipschitz case above, we have that
\mathcal{H}^{\infty}(B_{X},Y)
is isometrically isomorphic to
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{G}^{\infty}(B_{X}),Y)
, where
\mathcal{G}^{\infty}(B_{X})
is Mujica’s canonical predual of
\mathcal{H}^{\infty}(B_{X})

[
36
]
(we will review the space
\mathcal{G}^{\infty}(B_{X})
later in this section).

The parallel behavior of these Lipschitz and
\mathcal{H}^{\infty}
spaces was the authors’ motivation to introduce and study the following space and its canonical predual:

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}L_{0}(B_{X},Y)

\displaystyle=\Lip_{0}(B_{X},Y)\cap\mathcal{H}^{\infty}(B_{X},Y).

We will also denote
\mathcal{H}L_{0}(B_{X})=\mathcal{H}L_{0}(B_{X},\mathbb{C})
. Sometimes we will deal with holomorphic Lipschitz functions without assuming
f(0)=0
, and then we use the notation
\mathcal{H}L(B_{X},Y)
and
\mathcal{H}L(B_{X})
.

Since both normed spaces
\mathcal{H}^{\infty}(B_{X},Y)
and
\Lip_{0}(B_{X},Y)
are complete (with their respective norms) and each
f\in\mathcal{H}L_{0}(B_{X},Y)
satisfies
\|f\|_{\infty}\leq L(f)
we easily derive that
\mathcal{H}L_{0}(B_{X},Y)
is a Banach space with norm
L(\cdot)
.

Given
f\in\mathcal{H}^{\infty}(B_{X},Y)
such that
df\in\mathcal{H}^{\infty}(B_{X},\mathcal{L}(X,Y))
and
f(0)=0
, by the Mean Value Theorem, we have that
\|f(x)-f(y)\|\leq\|df\|\|x-y\|
for any
x,y\in B_{X}.

Then,
f\in\Lip_{0}(B_{X},Y)
and
L(f)\leq\|df\|
. Conversely, if
f\in\mathcal{H}L_{0}(B_{X},Y)
we know that
df\in\mathcal{H}(B_{X},\mathcal{L}(X,Y))
. Also, for
x,y\in B_{X}
,

\|df(x)(y)\|=\lim_{h\to 0}\left\|\frac{f(x+hy)-f(x)}{h}\right\|\leq L(f).

This means that
df
belongs to
\mathcal{H}^{\infty}(B_{X},\mathcal{L}(X,Y))
and
\|df\|\leq L(f)
.

This shows that there is another useful representation of our primary space of interest.

Proposition 2.1
.

\mathcal{H}L_{0}(B_{X},Y)=\{f\in\mathcal{H}^{\infty}(B_{X},Y):\ df\in\mathcal{H}^{\infty}(B_{X},\mathcal{L}(X,Y));\ f(0)=0\}
. Moreover, for every
f\in\mathcal{H}L_{0}(B_{X},Y),\ L(f)=\left\|{df}\right\|;
that is,
L(f)=\sup_{x\in B_{X}}\left\|{df(x)}\right\|.

Remark 2.2
.

If
f\colon B_{X}\to Y
is a holomorphic function and
x\in B_{X}
then
f(x+h)=\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}P_{m}(x)(h)
for
h
in a suitable neighborhood of 0, where
P_{m}
is an
m
-homogeneous polynomial. Recall that
df(x)(h)=P_{1}(x)(h)
for every
h\in X
.

Note that
P|_{B_{X}}\in\mathcal{H}L_{0}(B_{X},Y)
for every
P\in\mathcal{P}(X,Y)
such that
P(0)=0
, a fact that will be useful later.

When
Y=\mathbb{C},
we can define a mapping

\displaystyle\Phi\colon\mathcal{H}L_{0}(B_{X})

\displaystyle\to\mathcal{H}^{\infty}(B_{X},X^{*})

\displaystyle f

\displaystyle\mapsto df

In general,
\Phi
is an isometry
into

\mathcal{H}^{\infty}(B_{X},X^{*}),
although if

, then

is

is not surjective for

. Indeed, given

and

is symmetric (i.e.

for every

). Note that

is linear only when

is a 2-homogeneous polynomial.
Hence, a non-symmetric element of
\mathcal{L}(X,X^{*})
(which always exists whenever the dimension of

is strictly bigger than one) cannot be in the range of
\Phi
.

\mathcal{H}L_{0}(\mathbb{D})=\{f\in\mathcal{H}^{\infty}(\mathbb{D}):\ f(0)=0\ {\rm and\ }f^{\prime}\in\mathcal{H}^{\infty}(\mathbb{D})\}.

For the case of
\mathcal{H}L(B_{X},Y)
we consider the norm
\|f\|_{\mathcal{H}L}=\max\{\|f(0)\|,L(f)\}
. The fact that this is a norm and that
(\mathcal{H}L(B_{X},Y),\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{H}L})

Note that
\|f\|_{\infty}\leq 2\|f\|_{\mathcal{H}L}
for any

\mathcal{H}L_{0}(B_{X},Y)

Proposition 2.3
.

Let
X,Y
be complex Banach spaces. Then
\mathcal{H}L(B_{X},Y)
is isometric to a
1
-complemented subspace of
\mathcal{H}L_{0}(B_{X\oplus_{1}\mathbb{C}},Y)
.

Proof.

Consider
\Phi\colon\mathcal{H}L(B_{X},Y)\to\mathcal{H}L_{0}(B_{X\oplus_{1}\mathbb{C}},Y)
given by
\Phi f(x,\lambda)=f(x)+(\lambda-1)f(0)
. It is easy to check that
\Phi f
is Lipschitz with
L(\Phi f)\leq\left\|{f}\right\|_{\mathcal{H}L}
for every
f\in\mathcal{H}L(B_{X},Y)
. Note that

L(\Phi f)\geq\sup\left\{\frac{\left\|{\Phi f(x,0)-\Phi f(y,0)}\right\|}{\left\|{x-y}\right\|}:x\neq y\in B_{X}\right\}=L(f)

and also

L(\Phi f)\geq\frac{\left\|{\Phi f(0,1)-\Phi f(0,0)}\right\|}{\left\|{(0,1)-(0,0)}\right\|_{1}}=\left\|{f(0)}\right\|,

so we actually have
L(\Phi f)=\left\|{f}\right\|_{\mathcal{H}L}
. Thus
\Phi
is an into isometry.

Now consider
T\colon\mathcal{H}L_{0}(B_{X\oplus_{1}\mathbb{C}},Y)\to\mathcal{H}L(B_{X},Y)
given by
Tg(x)=g(x,0)+g(0,1)
. One can easily check that
\left\|{T}\right\|\leq 1
and
T\circ\Phi=I_{\mathcal{H}L(B_{X},Y)}
. Therefore
P=\Phi\circ T
is a norm-one projection from
\mathcal{H}L_{0}(B_{X\oplus_{1}\mathbb{C}},Y)
onto
\Phi(\mathcal{H}L(B_{X},Y))
.

Note that there are plenty of examples of non-Lipschitz functions in
\mathcal{H}^{\infty}(\mathbb{D})
. For instance, given a sequence
(b_{n})\subset\mathbb{C}\setminus\{1\}
with
|b_{n}|=1
and
b_{n}\to 1
, define
f\colon\{b_{n}\}\cup\{1\}\to\mathbb{C}
by
f(1)=0
and
f(b_{n})=\sqrt{|b_{n}-1|}
. Then the Rudin-Carleson theorem provides an extension of
f
which lies in the disc algebra
\mathcal{A}(\mathbb{D})
(that is, the space of uniformly continuous functions in
\mathcal{H}^{\infty}(\mathbb{D})
) and has the same supremum norm, but it is not Lipschitz.

Our next goal is to show that
\mathcal{H}L_{0}(B_{X})
is indeed much smaller than both
\mathcal{H}^{\infty}(B_{X})
and
\Lip_{0}(B_{X})
. More precisely, we will prove the following result, where we denote
\mathcal{H}^{\infty}_{0}(B_{X})=\{f\in\mathcal{H}^{\infty}(B_{X}):\ f(0)=0\}
.

Theorem 2.4
.

Let
X
be a non-null complex Banach space. Then

(1)

\ell_{\infty}
is isomorphic to a subspace of
\mathcal{H}^{\infty}_{0}(B_{X})\setminus\mathcal{H}L_{0}(B_{X})\cup\{0\}
.

(2)

\ell_{\infty}
is isomorphic to a subspace of
\Lip_{0}(B_{X})\setminus\mathcal{H}L_{0}(B_{X})\cup\{0\}
.

We will provide a different proof of Theorem
2.4
.
(a)
in the Appendix. Indeed, there we build an isomorphism into its image
F\colon\ell_{\infty}\longrightarrow\mathcal{H}^{\infty}(B_{X})
such that, additionally, its restriction to
c_{0}
satisfies that
F|_{c_{0}}\colon c_{0}\longrightarrow\mathcal{A}(B_{X})
.

Proof.

(a)
For the case
X=\mathbb{C}
, it has been observed to the authors that a classical result is that given
(z_{j})
an interpolating sequence on
\mathbb{D}
there exists an topological isomorphism
S\colon\ell_{\infty}\longrightarrow\mathcal{H}^{\infty}(\mathbb{D})
such that
S(c)(z_{j})=c_{j}
for every
j
and every
c=(c_{n})\in\ell_{\infty}
(see e.g.
[
27
, Theorem VII.2.1 and applications, p. 285]
where it is made for the upper half plane), we can also get that
S(c)(0)=0
. Hence, if
\mathbb{N}
is partitioned into infinitely many infinite sequences
n_{ik}
with
i,k\in\mathbb{N}
and for
c\in\ell_{\infty}
it is defined
x_{c}\in\ell_{\infty}
,
x_{c}(n_{ik})=(-1)^{i}c_{k}
, and

Y=\{S(x_{c}):c\in\ell_{\infty}\}
, then
Y
is an subspace of
\mathcal{H}^{\infty}(\mathbb{D})
isomorphic to
\ell_{\infty}
.
And if
c\neq 0
, then
c_{k}\neq 0
for some
k
, and
S(x_{c})
takes values
\pm c_{k}
along a sequence tending to 1. So it cannot be uniformly continuous, hence it is not Lipschitz.

Now, to get the general case,
we fix
x_{0}\in S_{X}
and consider
x^{\ast}\in X^{\ast}
such that
x^{\ast}(x_{0})=1=\|x^{\ast}\|
. We define

\Psi\colon\mathcal{H}^{\infty}(\mathbb{D})\longrightarrow\mathcal{H}^{\infty}(B_{X})

by
\Psi(f)=f\circ x^{\ast}
. Clearly
\Psi
is a well-defined linear mapping and since
x^{\ast}(B_{X})=\mathbb{D}
we have that
\Psi
is an isometry onto its image.
Moreover, considering its restriction we are going to have

\Psi\colon\mathcal{H}L(\mathbb{D})\longrightarrow\mathcal{H}L(B_{X}),

that again is an isometry, now with the Lipschitz norms. Indeed,
if
f\in\mathcal{H}L(\mathbb{D})
then

L(\Psi(f))=L(f\circ x^{*})\leq L(f)L(x^{*})=L(f).

But
if
\lambda,\mu\in\mathbb{D}
, then

\displaystyle|f(\lambda)-f(\mu)|=

\displaystyle|f\circ x^{\ast}(\lambda x_{0})-f\circ x^{\ast}(\mu x_{0})|=|\Psi(f)(\lambda x_{0})-\Psi(f)(\mu x_{0})|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle L(\Psi(f))\|\lambda x_{0}-\mu x_{0}\|=L(\Psi(f))|\lambda-\mu|,

and we get
L(f)\leq L(\Psi(f))
.
Finally, due to the injectivity of
\Psi
(a direct proof is also elementary) we have that

\Psi(\mathcal{H}^{\infty}_{0}(\mathbb{D})\setminus\mathcal{H}L(\mathbb{D}))\subset\mathcal{H}^{\infty}_{0}(B_{X})\setminus\mathcal{H}L(B_{X}).

Now the claim follows.

X=\mathbb{C}
. Let
l\colon\mathbb{R}\to[0,1]
be a
C^{\infty}
function such that
l
is strictly increasing on
(1/2,1)
,
0<l(x)<1
if
1/2<x<1
,
l(x)=0
for
x\leq 1/2
,
l(x)=1
for
x\geq 1
,
l^{(k)}(1/2)=0
if
k\geq 0
and
l^{(k)}(1)=0
if
k\geq 1
.
Define
f\colon\mathbb{C}\to[0,1]
as
f(z)=l(|z|)
. Considered it as being defined on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
,
f
is
C^{\infty}
and
df\colon\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
is a continuous function. Hence, by the Mean Value Theorem,
f\in\Lip_{0}(\mathbb{D})
.
Now we define
T\colon\mathcal{H}L_{0}(\mathbb{D})\to\Lip_{0}(\mathbb{D})
as
T(g)=f\cdot g
. We claim that
T
is an isomorphism onto its image. Indeed,
given
g\in\mathcal{H}L_{0}(\mathbb{D})
and
z,u\in\mathbb{D}
,

|f(z)g(z)-f(u)g(u)|\leq|f(z)-f(u)||g(z)|+|f(u)||g(z)-g(u)|\leq 2L(f)L(g)|z-u|.

Thus
T
is a continuous linear mapping with
\|T\|\leq 2L(f)
. Now we check that
T
is bounded below. As
f(x,y)=l(\sqrt{x^{2}+y^{2}})
we have that
df(x,y)=0
if
z=x+iy
satisfies
|z|\geq 1
. By continuity on a compact set, given
\varepsilon>0
there exists
0<r<1
such that if
|z|\geq r
, then both
\|df(x,y)\|<\varepsilon
and
f(z)>1-\varepsilon
. Thus, for
g\in\mathcal{H}L_{0}(\mathbb{D})
,

L(fg)=\|d(fg)\|_{\mathbb{D}}=\|gdf+fg^{\prime}\|_{\mathbb{D}}\geq\|fg^{\prime}\|_{\mathbb{D}\setminus r\mathbb{D}}-\|g\|_{\mathbb{D}}\|df\|_{\mathbb{D}\setminus r\mathbb{D}}\geq\|fg^{\prime}\|_{\mathbb{D}\setminus r\mathbb{D}}-L(g)\varepsilon.

But, by the maximum modulus theorem

\|fg^{\prime}\|_{\mathbb{D}\setminus r\mathbb{D}}\geq(1-\varepsilon)\|g^{\prime}\|_{\mathbb{D}\setminus r\mathbb{D}}=(1-\varepsilon)\|g^{\prime}\|_{\mathbb{D}}=(1-\varepsilon)L(g).

and we get
L(fg)\geq(1-2\varepsilon)L(g)
, for every
\varepsilon>0
. As a consequence

L(Tg)=L(fg)\geq L(g),

and
T
is bounded below. Moreover,
T(g)=f\cdot g
is never holomorphic on
\mathbb{D}
for any
g\in\mathcal{H}L_{0}(\mathbb{D})\setminus\{0\}
, and

T(\mathcal{H}L_{0})(\mathbb{D})
is isomorphic to
\mathcal{H}L_{0}(\mathbb{D})
which in turn is isometric to
\mathcal{H}^{\infty}(\mathbb{D})
that has a subspace isomorphic to
\ell_{\infty}
.

The general case is a straightforward consequence of the above argument in the following natural way. Let
X
a non-null complex Banach space and take
x^{\ast}\in S_{X^{\ast}}
.
Defining
R\colon\Lip_{0}(\mathbb{D})\to\Lip_{0}(B_{X})
by
R(h)=h\circ x^{\ast}
, we are going to have that
R
is an isometry into. Hence,
R\circ T\colon\mathcal{H}L_{0}(\mathbb{D})\to\Lip_{0}(B_{X})
is an isomorphism into its image and we get that
\ell_{\infty}
is isomorphic to a subspace of

\mathcal{H}L_{0}(B_{X})
. But if
g\in\mathcal{H}L_{0}(\mathbb{D})\setminus\{0\}
, then
R\circ T(g)=(f\cdot g)x^{\ast}
is not a Gateaux holomorphic function since its restriction to
\{zx\,:z\in\mathbb{D}\}
is not holomorphic. We conclude that
\ell_{\infty}\setminus\{0\}\subset\Lip_{0}(B_{X})\setminus\mathcal{H}L_{0}(B_{X})
.
∎

In this rest of the section, we will focus the attention on the canonical predual of space
\mathcal{H}L_{0}(B_{X})
and show that it shares many properties with the canonical preduals of
\mathcal{H}^{\infty}(B_{X})
and
\Lip_{0}(B_{X})
.

Let us denote by
\tau_{0}
the compact-open topology on
\mathcal{H}L_{0}(B_{X})
. An easy argument using Montel’s theorem
[
21
, Th. 15.50]
and Remark
2.2
shows that
\overline{B}_{\mathcal{H}L_{0}(B_{X})}
is
\tau_{0}
-compact. In fact, on this ball, convergence in the topology
\tau_{0}
coincides with pointwise convergence. Thus, the Dixmier-Ng theorem
[
39
]
says that
\mathcal{H}L_{0}(B_{X})
is a dual space with predual given by

\mathcal{G}_{0}(B_{X}):=\{\varphi\in\mathcal{H}L_{0}(B_{X})^{*}:\ \varphi|_{\overline{B}_{\mathcal{H}L_{0}(B_{X})}}\textrm{ is }\tau_{0}-\textrm{continuous}\}.

For
x\in B_{X}
and
f\in\mathcal{H}L_{0}(B_{X})
, denote
\delta(x)(f)=f(x)
. Clearly
\delta(x)\colon\mathcal{H}L_{0}(B_{X})\to\mathbb{C}
is linear and continuous meaning that
\delta(x)\in\mathcal{H}L_{0}(B_{X})^{*}
. Also,
\delta(x)|_{\overline{B}_{\mathcal{H}L_{0}(B)}}
is
\tau_{0}
-continuous so
\delta(x)\in\mathcal{G}_{0}(B_{X})
.

Proposition 2.5
.

Let
X
be a complex Banach space.

(1)

The mapping

\displaystyle\delta\colon B_{X}

\displaystyle\to\mathcal{G}_{0}(B_{X})

\displaystyle x

\displaystyle\mapsto\delta(x)

is holomorphic and
\left\|{\delta(x)-\delta(y)}\right\|=\left\|{x-y}\right\|
for every
x,y\in B_{X}
. In particular,
\delta\in\mathcal{H}L_{0}(B_{X},\mathcal{G}_{0}(B_{X}))
with
L(\delta)=1
.

(2)

\mathcal{G}_{0}(B_{X})=\overline{\operatorname{span}}\{\delta(x):\ x\in B_{X}\}
.

(3)

For any complex Banach space
Y
and any
f\in\mathcal{H}L_{0}(B_{X},Y)
, there is a unique operator
T_{f}\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{G}_{0}(B_{X}),Y)
such that the following diagram commutes:

\textstyle{B_{X}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{f}
\scriptstyle{\delta}
\textstyle{Y}
\textstyle{\mathcal{G}_{0}(B_{X})\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{T_{f}}

The map
f\mapsto T_{f}
defines an isometric isomorphism from
\mathcal{H}L_{0}(B_{X},Y)
onto
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{G}_{0}(B_{X}),Y)
. These properties characterize
\mathcal{G}_{0}(B_{X})
uniquely up to an isometric isomorphism.

(4)

A bounded net
(f_{\alpha})\subset\mathcal{H}L_{0}(B_{X})
is weak-star convergent to a function
f\in\mathcal{H}L_{0}(B_{X})
if and only if
f_{\alpha}(x)\to f(x)
for every
x\in B_{X}
.

Proof.

(a)
The map
\delta
is weakly holomorphic since for any
f\in\mathcal{G}_{0}(B_{X})^{*}=\mathcal{H}L_{0}(B_{X})
we have that
f\circ\delta=f
is holomorphic. Thus,
\delta
is holomorphic (see
[
38
, Th. 8.12]
). Also, given
x,y\in B_{X}
, we have

\left\|{\delta(x)-\delta(y)}\right\|=\sup_{f\in B_{\mathcal{H}L_{0}(B_{X})}}|\langle f,\delta(x)-\delta(y)\rangle|=\sup_{f\in B_{\mathcal{H}L_{0}(B_{X})}}|f(x)-f(y)|\leq\left\|{x-y}\right\|,

and equality holds since we may take
f=x^{*}|_{B_{X}}
where
\left\|{x^{*}}\right\|=1
and
x^{*}(x-y)=\left\|{x-y}\right\|
.

(b)
Just observe that for every
f\in\mathcal{H}L_{0}(B_{X})=\mathcal{G}_{0}(B_{X})^{*}
we have that
f=0
whenever
f|_{\{\delta(x):x\in B_{X}\}}=0
.

(c)
First, note that an interpolation argument shows that the set
\{\delta(x):x\in B_{X}\setminus\{0\}\}
is linearly independent in
\mathcal{G}_{0}(B_{X})
. Indeed, assume that
\sum_{j=1}^{n}\lambda_{j}\delta(x_{j})=0
for different points
x_{j}\in B_{X}\setminus\{0\}
and
\lambda_{j}\in\mathbb{C}
. Let
x_{0}=0
and
\lambda_{0}=0
. Take
x_{ij}^{*}\in S_{X^{*}}
with
x_{ij}^{*}(x_{i}-x_{j})=\left\|{x_{i}-x_{j}}\right\|
and define
f(x)=\sum_{j=0}^{n}\overline{\lambda_{j}}\prod_{i\neq j}\frac{x_{ij}^{*}(x_{i}-x)}{\left\|{x_{i}-x_{j}}\right\|}
. Then
f\in\mathcal{H}L_{0}(B_{X})
and
0=\langle f,\sum_{j=1}^{n}\lambda_{j}\delta(x_{j})\rangle=\sum_{j=1}^{n}|\lambda_{j}|^{2}
.

Now, given
f\in\mathcal{H}L_{0}(B_{X},Y)
, we define
T_{f}(\delta(x)):=f(x)
for every
x\in B_{X}
(this is the only possibility to get a commutative diagram) and extend it linearly to
\operatorname{span}\{\delta(x):x\in B_{X}\}
. Note that, given
u=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\lambda_{j}\delta(x_{j})
,

\displaystyle\left\|{T_{f}u}\right\|

\displaystyle=\left\|{\sum_{j=1}^{n}\lambda_{j}f(x_{j})}\right\|=\sup_{y^{*}\in B_{Y^{*}}}\left|\sum_{j=1}^{n}\lambda_{j}(y^{*}\circ f)(x_{j})\right|=\sup_{y^{*}\in B_{Y^{*}}}\left|\langle u,y^{*}\circ f\rangle\right|

\displaystyle\leq\sup\{L(y^{*}\circ f):y^{*}\in B_{Y}\}\left\|{u}\right\|=L(f)\left\|{u}\right\|.

Thus,
T_{f}
extends uniquely to an operator
T_{f}\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{G}_{0}(B),Y)
with
\left\|{T_{f}}\right\|\leq L(f)
. Since
L(\delta)=1
and
f=T_{f}\circ\delta
, indeed we get that
\left\|{T_{f}}\right\|=L(f)
.

Moreover, the map
f\mapsto T_{f}
is onto since, given any
T\in L(\mathcal{G}_{0}(B_{X}),Y)
, we have that
f:=T\circ\delta
is a holomorphic Lipschitz map with
f(0)=0
and
T=T_{f}
.

The uniqueness of
\mathcal{G}_{0}(B_{X})
follows from the diagram property and the fact that
\left\|{T_{f}}\right\|=L(f)
.

(d)
The ball
\overline{B}_{\mathcal{H}L_{0}(B_{X})}
is
\tau_{0}
-compact and the weak-star topology is coarser than
\tau_{0}
, so they coincide on
\overline{B}_{\mathcal{H}L_{0}(B_{X})}
.
∎

Proposition 2.6
.

For every complex Banach space
X
we have that
X
is isometric to a 1-complemented subspace of
\mathcal{G}_{0}(B_{X})
.

Proof.

In the particular case of
f=Id\colon B_{X}\to X
, differentiating the diagram in Proposition
2.5
and using that
d(Id)(x)=Id
for all
x\in B_{X}
, we obtain another commutative diagram where all the arrows are linear:

\textstyle{X\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{Id}
\scriptstyle{d\delta(0)}
\textstyle{X}
\textstyle{\mathcal{G}_{0}(B_{X})\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{T_{Id}}

Moreover,
d\delta(0)
is an isometry. Indeed, given
x\in X
and
f\in\mathcal{H}L_{0}(B_{X})
we have

\langle f,d\delta(0)(x)\rangle=\lim_{t\to 0}\langle f,\frac{\delta(tx)-\delta(0)}{t}\rangle=\lim_{t\to 0}\frac{f(tx)-f(0)}{t}=df(0)(x)

and so

\left\|{d\delta(0)(x)}\right\|=\sup\{|df(0)(x)|:f\in B_{\mathcal{H}L_{0}(B_{X})}\}\leq\left\|{x}\right\|.

The other inequality is clear due to the commutative diagram:

\|x\|=\|T_{Id}\circ d\delta(0)(x)\|\leq\|d\delta(0)(x)\|.

Finally, let
P=d\delta(0)\circ T_{Id}
. Then, using that
T_{Id}\circ d\delta(0)=Id
, we have

P^{2}=d\delta(0)\circ T_{Id}\circ d\delta(0)\circ T_{Id}=d\delta(0)\circ T_{Id}=P,

so
P
is a norm-one projection from
\mathcal{G}_{0}(B_{X})
onto
d\delta(0)(X)
.
∎

Note that this result also holds for
\mathcal{G}^{\infty}(B_{X})

[
36
]
but not in general for
\mathcal{F}(B_{X})
. In
[
30
]
it is proved that this is true for
X
separable although for nonseparable
X
it could even occur that
\mathcal{F}(B_{X})
does not contain a subspace isomorphic to
X
. Another useful property of Lipschitz-free spaces is the fact that they contain a complemented copy of
\ell_{1}

[
19
]
, the same holds for
\mathcal{G}_{0}(B_{X})
.

Proposition 2.7
.

Let
X
be a complex Banach space. Then there is a complemented subspace of
\mathcal{G}_{0}(B_{X})
isomorphic to
\ell_{1}
.

Proof.

\ell_{\infty}
is isomorphic to a subspace of
\mathcal{H}^{\infty}(\mathbb{D})
. Since
\mathcal{H}^{\infty}(\mathbb{D})
is isometric to
\mathcal{H}L_{0}(\mathbb{D})
, which is a complemented subspace in
\mathcal{H}L_{0}(B_{X})
, the same holds for
\mathcal{H}L_{0}(B_{X})
. It is a classical result (see
[
13
, Th. 4]
) that this implies its predual
\mathcal{G}_{0}(B_{X})
contains a complemented copy of
\ell_{1}
.
∎

Next, we want to describe the closed unit ball of
\mathcal{G}_{0}(B_{X})
. For that, we introduce some more notation.
We denote by
\conv
the convex hull of a set and by
\Gamma
the absolute convex hull of a set.
As usual in the Lipschitz world, for every
x,y\in B_{X}
with
x\neq y
,
m_{x,y}
stands for the
elementary molecule

\frac{\delta(x)-\delta(y)}{\|x-y\|}
. Also, for every
x\in B_{X}
,
y\in X
and
f\in\mathcal{H}L_{0}(B_{X})
, we denote
e_{x,y}(f):=df(x)(y)
. Then
e_{x,y}\in\mathcal{G}_{0}(B_{X})
with
\left\|{e_{x,y}}\right\|=\left\|{y}\right\|
. Indeed, it is clear that

\left\|{e_{x,y}}\right\|=\sup\{|df(x)(y)|:f\in B_{\mathcal{H}L_{0}(B_{X})}\}\leq\sup\{\left\|{df(x)}\right\|:f\in B_{\mathcal{H}L_{0}(B_{X})}\}\left\|{y}\right\|\leq\left\|{y}\right\|.

Conversely, take
x^{*}\in X^{*}
with
x^{*}(y)=\left\|{y}\right\|
and
\left\|{x^{*}}\right\|=1
. Then
x^{*}|_{B_{X}}\in\mathcal{H}L_{0}(B_{X})
and
e_{x,y}(x^{*}|_{B_{X}})=x^{*}(y)=\left\|{y}\right\|
. This shows that
e_{x,y}
belongs to
\mathcal{H}L_{0}(B_{X})^{*}
and the equality of norms. Finally, by a simple application of a Cauchy’s integral formula we derive that the restriction of
e_{x,y}
to
\overline{B}_{\mathcal{H}L_{0}(B_{X})}
is
\tau_{0}
-continuous and so it belongs to
\mathcal{G}_{0}(B_{X})
.

Proposition 2.8
.

Let
X
be a complex Banach space. Then,

\overline{B}_{\mathcal{G}_{0}(B_{X})}=\overline{\Gamma}\{m_{x,y}:\ x,y\in B_{X},\ x\neq y\}=\overline{\conv}\{e_{x,y}:\ x\in B_{X},y\in S_{X}\}

Proof.

By Proposition
2.5
, we have that
\left\|{m_{x,y}}\right\|=1
for every
x,y\in B_{X}
with
x\neq y
. Also,

L(f)=\sup\{|\langle f,m_{x,y}\rangle|:x,y\in B_{X},x\neq y\}\,\text{for all }f\in\mathcal{H}L_{0}(B_{X}).

Thus,
\{m_{x,y}:x,y\in B_{X},x\neq y\}
is
1
-norming for
\mathcal{H}L_{0}(B_{X})
. Equivalently,
\overline{B}_{\mathcal{G}_{0}(B_{X})}=\overline{\Gamma}\{m_{x,y}:\ x,y\in B_{X},\ x\neq y\}
. Analogously, we have that

L(f)=\left\|{df}\right\|=\sup\{\left\|{df(x)}\right\|:x\in B_{X}\}=\sup\{|\langle f,e_{x,y}\rangle|:x\in B_{X},y\in S_{X}\}

and so
\overline{B}_{\mathcal{G}_{0}(B_{X})}=\overline{\Gamma\{e_{x,y}:x\in B_{X},y\in S_{X}\}}
. But
e_{x,\lambda y_{1}+\eta y_{2}}=\lambda e_{x,y_{1}}+\eta e_{x,y_{2}}
for every
\lambda,\eta\in\mathbb{C}
so actually
\overline{B}_{\mathcal{G}_{0}(B_{X})}=\overline{\conv}\{e_{x,y}:x\in B_{X},y\in S_{X}\}
.
∎

As a consequence, the density characters of
X
and
\mathcal{G}_{0}(B_{X})
coincide. In particular
X
is separable if and only if
\mathcal{G}_{0}(B_{X})
is separable.

We will now relate
\mathcal{G}_{0}(B_{X})
with the Lipschitz-free space
\mathcal{F}(B_{X})
and Mujica’s predual
\mathcal{G}^{\infty}(B_{X})
of
\mathcal{H}^{\infty}(B_{X})
. Note that each element of
\mathcal{F}(B_{X})
can be seen also as an element of
\mathcal{G}_{0}(B_{X})
, but maybe with a different behavior. For instance, consider
z\in B_{X}\setminus\{0\}
and
\mu
given by
\langle\mu,f\rangle=\int_{C(0,1)}f(\lambda z)d\lambda
for
f\in\Lip_{0}(B_{X})
. Then
\mu\neq 0
in
\mathcal{F}(B_{X})
but
\langle\mu,f\rangle=0
for all
f\in\mathcal{H}L_{0}(B_{X})
, so
\mu=0
when considered as an element of
\mathcal{G}_{0}(B_{X})
. The next proposition formalizes this situation. We say that an operator
T\colon X\to Y
is a
quotient operator
if
T
is surjective and
\left\|{y}\right\|=\inf\{\left\|{x}\right\|:Tx=y\}
for every
y\in Y
; this implies that
X/\ker T
is isometrically isomorphic to
Y
.

Proposition 2.9
.

Let
X
be a complex Banach space.

(1)

The operator

\displaystyle\pi\colon\mathcal{F}(B_{X})

\displaystyle\to\mathcal{G}_{0}(B_{X})

\displaystyle\delta(x)

\displaystyle\mapsto\delta(x)

is a quotient operator with kernel
\mathcal{H}L_{0}(B_{X})_{\bot}=\{\mu\in\mathcal{F}(B_{X}):\langle f,\mu\rangle=0\,\forall f\in\mathcal{H}L_{0}(B_{X})\}
. Thus
\mathcal{G}_{0}(B_{X})\equiv\mathcal{F}(B_{X})/\mathcal{H}L_{0}(B_{X})_{\bot}
isometrically.

(2)

The operator

\displaystyle\Psi\colon\mathcal{G}^{\infty}(B_{X})\widehat{\otimes}_{\pi}X

\displaystyle\to\mathcal{G}_{0}(B_{X})

\displaystyle\delta(x)\otimes y

\displaystyle\mapsto e_{x,y}

is a quotient map with
\left\|{\Psi}\right\|=1
. In addition, the operator
\Psi
is injective if and only if
X=\mathbb{C}
.

Proof.

(a)
First note that the existence of such an operator
\pi
follows from the linearization property of Lipschitz-free spaces applied to the 1-Lipschitz map
B_{X}\to\mathcal{G}_{0}(B_{X})
given by
x\mapsto\delta(x)
. Also,
\pi^{*}\colon\mathcal{H}L_{0}(B_{X})\to\Lip_{0}(B_{X})
is just the inclusion map since

\pi^{*}f(x)=\langle\pi^{*}f,\delta(x)\rangle=\langle f,\pi(\delta(x))\rangle=\langle f,\delta(x)\rangle=f(x)\quad\forall f\in\mathcal{H}L_{0}(B_{X}),\forall x\in B_{X}.

Thus,
\pi^{*}
is an isometry into. It is a standard fact that this implies that
\pi
is a quotient operator. Moreover,
\ker\pi=\pi^{*}(\mathcal{H}L_{0}(B_{X}))_{\bot}=\mathcal{H}L_{0}(B_{X})_{\bot}
.

(b)
Consider the into isometry

\displaystyle\Phi\colon\mathcal{H}L_{0}(B_{X})

\displaystyle\to\mathcal{H}^{\infty}(B_{X},X^{*})

\displaystyle f

\displaystyle\mapsto df

defined after Proposition
2.1
. Recall that
\mathcal{G}^{\infty}(B_{X})\widehat{\otimes}_{\pi}X
is a predual of
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{G}^{\infty}(B_{X}),X^{*})\simeq\mathcal{H}^{\infty}(B_{X},X^{*})
(see e.g.
[
43
]
). Thus, if we restrict
\Phi^{*}
to this predual we obtain
\Psi=\Phi^{*}|_{\mathcal{G}^{\infty}(B_{X})\widehat{\otimes}_{\pi}X}
, note that
\Psi(\delta(x)\otimes y)=e_{x,y}\in\mathcal{G}_{0}(B_{X})
for all
x,y
and so
\Psi(\mathcal{G}^{\infty}(B_{X})\widehat{\otimes}_{\pi}X)\subset\mathcal{G}_{0}(B_{X})
. Then
\left\|{\Psi}\right\|=1
and
\Psi
is a quotient operator since
\Psi^{*}=\Phi
is an into isometry. In the case
X=\mathbb{C}
, we have indeed that
\Phi\colon\mathcal{H}L_{0}(\mathbb{D})\to\mathcal{H}^{\infty}(\mathbb{D})
is an onto isometry, and thus
\Psi
is also an isometry from
\mathcal{G}^{\infty}(\mathbb{D})
onto
\mathcal{G}_{0}(\mathbb{D})
. However,
\Psi
is not injective for
X\neq\mathbb{C}
since
\Phi
is not surjective.
∎

Thus,
\mathcal{G}_{0}(\mathbb{D})
is isometric to
\mathcal{G}^{\infty}(\mathbb{D})
(which is the unique predual of
\mathcal{H}^{\infty}(\mathbb{D})

[
3
]
). We have some immediate consequences.

Corollary 2.10
.

A function
f
is an extreme point of
\overline{B}_{\mathcal{H}L_{0}(\mathbb{D})}
if and only if

is an extreme point of
\overline{B}_{\mathcal{H}^{\infty}(\mathbb{D})}
.

Corollary 2.11
.

A function
f\in\mathcal{H}L_{0}(\mathbb{D})
attains its norm as a functional on
\mathcal{G}_{0}(\mathbb{D})
if and only if
f^{\prime}\in\mathcal{H}^{\infty}(\mathbb{D})
attains its norm as a functional on
\mathcal{G}^{\infty}(\mathbb{D})
.

Let us state one more consequence of Proposition
2.9
.

Corollary 2.12

(x_{n}),(y_{n})\subset B_{X}

\varphi

(2)

There are sequences
(x_{n})\subset B_{X}
,
(y_{n})\subset S_{X}
and
(a_{n})\subset\ell_{1}

\varphi=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}a_{n}e_{x_{n},y_{n}}.

\left\|{\varphi}\right\|=\inf\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}|a_{n}|

\varphi

Given

, Proposition
2.9

[

) that there are points

Item

∎

3.
Approximation properties on
\mathcal{G}_{0}(B_{X})

Following Mujica’s ideas
[
36
]
we devote this section to study the metric approximation property (MAP) and the approximation property (AP) for
\mathcal{G}_{0}(B_{X})
whenever
X
has the same property. Beginning with the MAP, we prove the following result about approximation of elements in the closed unit ball of the dual space. We first introduce the notation:

•

\mathcal{P}_{0}(X,Y)
: The vector space of polynomials
P\colon X\to Y
such that
P(0)=0
endowed with the norm
\|dP\|=L(P|_{B_{X}})
.

•

\mathcal{P}_{f,0}(X,Y)
: The subspace of
\mathcal{P}_{0}(X,Y)
consisting of finite type polynomials.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
X
and
Y
be complex Banach spaces. Then

(1)

\overline{B}_{\mathcal{H}L_{0}(B_{X},Y)}=\overline{B_{\mathcal{P}_{0}(X,Y)}}^{\tau_{0}}
.

(2)

If
X
has the MAP then
\overline{B}_{\mathcal{H}L_{0}(B_{X},Y)}=\overline{B_{\mathcal{P}_{f,0}(X,Y)}}^{\tau_{0}}
.

Proof.

(a)
If
f\in\overline{B}_{\mathcal{H}L_{0}(B_{X},Y)}
then
f\in\mathcal{H}^{\infty}(B_{X},Y)
and
f(0)=0
. Consider the Taylor series expansion of
f
at 0:
f(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}P^{k}f(0)(x)
. As in
[
36
]
, for each
m\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
, we denote

S_{m}f(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{m}P^{k}f(0)(x)\qquad\textrm{and}\qquad\sigma_{m}f(x)=\frac{1}{m+1}\sum_{k=0}^{m}S_{k}f(x).

Since
df=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}dP^{k}f(0)\in\mathcal{H}^{\infty}(B_{X},\mathcal{L}(X,Y))
it follows from
[
36
, Prop. 5.2]
that
\sigma_{m}f(x)\to f(x)
for all
x\in B_{X}
and

\|d\sigma_{m}f\|=\|\sigma_{m}(df)\|\leq\|df\|\leq 1.

This implies that
f\in\overline{B_{\mathcal{P}_{0}(X,Y)}}^{\tau_{0}}
.

For the reverse inclusion, let
f\in\mathcal{H}L_{0}(B_{X},Y)
and
(P_{\alpha})\subset B_{\mathcal{P}_{0}(X,Y)}
such that
P_{\alpha}(x)\to f(x)
for all
x\in B_{X}
. Then
L(f)\leq 1
and so
f\in\overline{B}_{\mathcal{H}L_{0}(B_{X},Y)}
.

(b)
If
X
has the MAP there is a net of finite rank operators
(T_{\alpha})\subset\mathcal{L}(X,X)
such that
T_{\alpha}(x)\to x
for all
x\in X
and
\|T_{\alpha}\|\leq 1
for every
\alpha
. Given
P\in B_{\mathcal{P}_{0}(X,Y)}
we have that
P\circ T_{\alpha}
belongs to
B_{\mathcal{P}_{f,0}(X,Y)}
(since
L(P\circ T_{\alpha}|_{B_{X}})<1
) and
P(T_{\alpha}x)\to P(x)
for every
x
. This means that
P\in\overline{B_{\mathcal{P}_{f,0}(X,Y)}}^{\tau_{0}}
. Finally, an appeal to
(a)
yields the result.
∎

Theorem 3.2
.

X
has the MAP if and only if
\mathcal{G}_{0}(B_{X})
has the MAP.

Proof.

X
being isometric to a 1-complemented subspace of
\mathcal{G}_{0}(B_{X})
it is clear that
X
has the MAP
when
\mathcal{G}_{0}(B_{X})
has it.

Now, suppose that
X
has the MAP and consider the mapping
\delta\in\overline{B}_{\mathcal{H}L_{0}(B_{X},\mathcal{G}_{0}(B_{X}))}
. By Proposition
3.1
there exist a net
(P_{\alpha})\subset B_{\mathcal{P}_{f,0}(X,\mathcal{G}_{0}(B_{X}))}
such that
P_{\alpha}(x)\to\delta(x)
for all
x\in B_{X}
. Applying a linearization as in Proposition
2.5
we obtain finite rank linear mappings
T_{P_{\alpha}}
with norm bounded by 1, such that the following diagram commutes:

\textstyle{B_{X}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{P_{\alpha}}
\scriptstyle{\delta}
\textstyle{\mathcal{G}_{0}(B_{X})}
\textstyle{\mathcal{G}_{0}(B_{X})\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{T_{P_{\alpha}}}

Note that
T_{P_{\alpha}}(\delta(x))=P_{\alpha}(x)\to\delta(x)=Id(\delta(x))
. Then, we have that

on

is bounded the same holds for the closure. Hence,
\mathcal{G}_{0}(B_{X})

∎

Note that our arguments cannot be adapted to the case in which

has the BAP since the approximations of the identity could send the unit ball

to a bigger ball (and, hence, we cannot control the Lipschitz norm of

).

Question 1
.

Does
\mathcal{G}_{0}(B_{X})
have the BAP whenever
X
has the BAP?

The same question for
\mathcal{G}^{\infty}(B_{X})
was posed by Mujica in
[
36
]
. As far we know, this question is still open.

In contrast to this unknown case about the BAP, the analogous statement for the AP (Approximation Property -without bounds-) was successfully solved by Mujica
[
36
]
for
\mathcal{G}^{\infty}(B_{X})
. We now turn to this goal for our space
\mathcal{G}_{0}(B_{X})
, following Mujica’s scheme but somewhat simplifying the arguments.

Note that in the results about the MAP we used several times that a bounded net of linear operators converges uniformly on compact sets if and only if it converges pointwise on a dense set. For the AP we cannot make use of this kind of argument so our first step will be to describe a locally convex topology
\tau_{\gamma}
such that the following topological isomorphism holds:

(2)

(\mathcal{H}L_{0}(B_{X},Y),\tau_{\gamma})\cong(\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{G}_{0}(B_{X}),Y),\tau_{0}).

Remark 3.3
.

Note that for a topology
\tau_{\gamma}
satisfying (
2
), if
(f_{\alpha})
is a bounded net in
\mathcal{H}L_{0}(B_{X},Y)
which converges pointwise to
f\in\mathcal{H}L_{0}(B_{X},Y)
then
f_{\alpha}\overset{\tau_{\gamma}}{\to}f
. Indeed, linearizating we obtain a bounded net
(T_{f_{\alpha}})\subset\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{G}_{0}(B_{X}),Y)
which converges pointwise to

. Then,
T_{f_{\alpha}}\overset{\tau_{0}}{\to}T_{f}
implying that
f_{\alpha}\overset{\tau_{\gamma}}{\to}f
.

As a consequence, we derive from Proposition
3.1

(a)
the following identity:

(3)

\overline{B}_{\mathcal{H}L_{0}(B_{X},Y)}=\overline{B_{\mathcal{P}_{0}(X,Y)}}^{\tau_{\gamma}}.

In order to work with the
\tau_{0}
-topology in
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{G}_{0}(B_{X}),Y)
it would be good to have a useful description of the compact sets of the space
\mathcal{G}_{0}(B_{X})
. For that, we appeal to the following variation of the classical Grothendieck description of compact sets (which can be proved, for instance, by slightly modifying the proof of
[

):

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
X
be a Banach space and
V\subset S_{X}
such that
\overline{B}_{X}=\overline{\Gamma}(V)
. For each compact set

there exist sequences
(\alpha_{j})\in c_{0}
(with
\alpha_{j}>0
for all

) and
(v_{j})\subset V
such that

.

A direct consequence of this lemma, along with Proposition
2.8
is the following:

Corollary 3.5
.

Let
K\subset\mathcal{G}_{0}(B_{X})
be a compact set. Then there exist sequences
(\alpha_{j})\in c_{0}
and
(x_{j},y_{j})\subset B_{X}\times B_{X}
(with
\alpha_{j}>0
and
x_{j}\not=y_{j}
for all
j
) such that

.

Now we can introduce, as in
[
36
, Th. 4.8]
, a topology
\tau_{\gamma}
satisfying (
2
).

Theorem 3.6
.

Let
\tau_{\gamma}
be the locally convex topology on
\mathcal{H}L_{0}(B_{X},Y)
generated by the seminorms

p(f)=\sup_{j}\alpha_{j}\frac{\|f(x_{j})-f(y_{j})\|}{\|x_{j}-y_{j}\|}

where
(\alpha_{j})\in c_{0}
,
(x_{j},y_{j})\subset B_{X}\times B_{X}
and
\alpha_{j}>0
,
x_{j}\not=y_{j}
for all
j
. Then, the mapping

\displaystyle(\mathcal{H}L_{0}(B_{X},Y),\tau_{\gamma})

\displaystyle\to

\displaystyle(\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{G}_{0}(B_{X}),Y),\tau_{0})

\displaystyle f

\displaystyle\mapsto

\displaystyle T_{f}

is a topological isomorphism.

Proof.

If
K\subset\mathcal{G}_{0}(B_{X})
is a compact set, by the previous corollary there are sequences
(\alpha_{j})\in c_{0}
,
(x_{j},y_{j})\subset B_{X}\times B_{X}
with
\alpha_{j}>0
,
x_{j}\not=y_{j}
for all
j
, such that
K\subset\overline{\Gamma}(\{\alpha_{j}m_{x_{j}y_{j}}\})
. Then, for all

\sup_{u\in K}\|T_{f}u\|\leq\sup_{j}\|T_{f}(\alpha_{j}m_{x_{j}y_{j}})\|=\sup_{j}\alpha_{j}\frac{\|f(x_{j})-f(y_{j})\|}{\|x_{j}-y_{j}\|},

showing that the mapping

is
\tau_{\gamma}-\tau_{0}
continuous.

To prove the continuity of the inverse mapping note that for a seminorm
p
of
\tau_{\gamma}
, the associated sequence
\alpha_{j}m_{x_{j}y_{j}}
converges to 0 in
\mathcal{G}_{0}(B_{X})
. Thus, the set
K=\{\alpha_{j}m_{x_{j}y_{j}}\}\cup\{0\}
is a compact set in
\mathcal{G}_{0}(B_{X})
and

p(f)=\sup_{j}\|T_{f}(\alpha_{j}m_{x_{j}y_{j}})\|=\sup_{u\in K}\|T_{f}u\|
.
∎

Let us state separately the corresponding result for Lipschitz-free spaces, that we will not use but it might be of independent interest.

Theorem 3.7

(1)

(2)

generated by the seminorms

(\alpha_{j})\in c_{0}
,
(x_{j},y_{j})\subset M\times M
and
\alpha_{j}>0

\displaystyle(\mathcal{\Lip}_{0}(M,Y),\tau_{\gamma})

\displaystyle\to

\displaystyle(\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{F}(M),Y),\tau_{0})

\displaystyle f

\displaystyle\mapsto

\displaystyle T_{f}

is a topological isomorphism.

Now we examine the relationship between the topologies
\tau_{\gamma}
and
\tau_{0}
in
\mathcal{H}L_{0}(B_{X},Y)
.

Proposition 3.8
.

Let
X
and
Y
be complex Banach spaces. Then,
\tau_{\gamma}
is finer than
\tau_{0}
in
\mathcal{H}L_{0}(B_{X},Y)
, and these topologies are equivalent in
\mathcal{P}(^{m}X,Y)
for each
m\in\mathbb{N}
.

Proof.

If
K\subset B_{X}
is a compact set, then
\delta(K)\subset\mathcal{G}_{0}(B_{X})
is compact. By Corollary
3.5
, there exist sequences
(\alpha_{j})\in c_{0}
and
(x_{j},y_{j})\subset B_{X}\times B_{X}
(with
\alpha_{j}>0
and
x_{j}\not=y_{j}
for all
j
) such that
\delta(K)\subset\overline{\Gamma}(\{\alpha_{j}m_{x_{j}y_{j}}\})
. Hence, for all
f\in\mathcal{H}L_{0}(B_{X},Y)
,

\sup_{x\in K}\|f(x)\|\leq\sup_{j}\alpha_{j}\frac{\|f(x_{j})-f(y_{j})\|}{\|x_{j}-y_{j}\|},

proving the first assertion.

For the second statement, take a seminorm
p
that generates
\tau_{\gamma}
:
p(f)=\sup_{j}\alpha_{j}\frac{\|f(x_{j})-f(y_{j})\|}{\|x_{j}-y_{j}\|}
, with
(\alpha_{j})\in c_{0}
,
(x_{j},y_{j})\subset B_{X}\times B_{X}
,
\alpha_{j}>0
and
x_{j}\not=y_{j}
for all
j
. For a homogeneous polynomial
P\in\mathcal{P}(^{m}X,Y)
we have:

\displaystyle p(P)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sup_{j}\alpha_{j}\frac{\|P(x_{j})-P(y_{j})\|}{\|x_{j}-y_{j}\|}=\sup_{j}\frac{\|P(\alpha_{j}^{1/m}x_{j})-P(\alpha_{j}^{1/m}y_{j})\|}{\|x_{j}-y_{j}\|}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sup_{j}\frac{\left\|\sum_{k=1}^{m}\binom{m}{k}\widecheck{P}\left((\alpha_{j}^{1/m}(x_{j}-y_{j}))^{k},(\alpha_{j}^{1/m}y_{j})^{m-k}\right)\right\|}{\|x_{j}-y_{j}\|}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sup_{j}\left\|\sum_{k=1}^{m}\binom{m}{k}\widecheck{P}\left(\left(\frac{\alpha_{j}^{1/m}(x_{j}-y_{j})}{\|x_{j}-y_{j}\|^{1/k}}\right)^{k},(\alpha_{j}^{1/m}y_{j})^{m-k}\right)\right\|.

Note that there exist compact sets
K_{1}
and
K_{2}
in
X
such that
\left\{\alpha_{j}^{1/m}\frac{(x_{j}-y_{j})}{\|x_{j}-y_{j}\|^{1/k}}\right\}\subset K_{1}
and
\left\{\alpha_{j}^{1/m}y_{j}\right\}\subset K_{2}
(since both sequences go to 0). Then,

p(P)\leq\sum_{k=1}^{m}\binom{m}{k}\sup_{a\in K_{1},b\in K_{2}}\|\widecheck{P}(a^{k},b^{m-k})\|.

Using the polarization formula, for each
k\in\{1,\dots,m\}
,

\widecheck{P}(a^{k},b^{m-k})=\frac{1}{2^{m}m!}\sum_{\varepsilon_{i}=\pm 1}\varepsilon_{1}\cdots\varepsilon_{m}P\left(\left(\sum_{i=1}^{k}\varepsilon_{i}\right)a+\left(\sum_{i=k+1}^{m}\varepsilon_{i}\right)b\right).

Taking into account that the following set is compact

C(K_{1},K_{2})=\left\{\left(\sum_{i=1}^{k}\varepsilon_{i}\right)a+\left(\sum_{i=k+1}^{m}\varepsilon_{i}\right)b:\ a\in K_{1},b\in K_{2},k\in\{1,\dots,m\},\varepsilon_{i}=\pm 1\right\},

and that

\sup_{a\in K_{1},b\in K_{2}}\|\widecheck{P}(a^{k},b^{m-k})\|\leq\frac{1}{m!}\sup_{u\in C(K_{1},K_{2})}\|P(u)\|

we derive the intended inequality:

p(P)\leq\frac{2^{m}-1}{m!}\sup_{u\in C(K_{1},K_{2})}\|P(u)\|.

∎

.

It is enough to consider

(for any

[

4.
Relation between
\mathcal{G}_{0}(B_{X})
and
\mathcal{G}_{0}(B_{Y})
when
X\subset Y

Recall that, given metric spaces
M,N
with
0\in M\subset N
, the (real) Lipschitz-free space
\mathcal{F}(M)
canonically identifies with a subspace of
\mathcal{F}(N)
. This follows from the McShane extension theorem asserting that for every
f\in\Lip_{0}(M,\mathbb{R})
there is
\tilde{f}\in\Lip_{0}(N,\mathbb{R})
with
\tilde{f}|_{M}=f
and
L(f)=L(\tilde{f})
, see e.g.
[
44
, Th. 1.33]
. Note in passing that in the complex-valued case all extensions can have a larger Lipschitz constant.
This is why our next goal is to analyze the corresponding relation between
\mathcal{G}_{0}(B_{X})
and
\mathcal{G}_{0}(B_{Y})
when
X\subset Y
. Then
B_{X}\subset B_{Y}
and the restriction mapping has norm one:

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}L_{0}(B_{Y})

\displaystyle\to\mathcal{H}L_{0}(B_{X})

\displaystyle f

\displaystyle\mapsto f|_{B_{X}}.

Then, the following mapping also has norm one:

\displaystyle\rho:\mathcal{G}_{0}(B_{X})

\displaystyle\to\mathcal{G}_{0}(B_{Y})

\displaystyle\varphi

\displaystyle\mapsto\widehat{\varphi},

where
\widehat{\varphi}(f)=\varphi(f|_{B_{X}})
.

Whenever
\rho
is an isometry,
we write
\mathcal{G}_{0}(B_{X})\subset\mathcal{G}_{0}(B_{Y})
. Then, by the Hahn-Banach theorem, every element of
\mathcal{H}L_{0}(B_{X})
would have a norm preserving extension to
\mathcal{H}L_{0}(B_{Y})
. Since there exist polynomials which cannot be extended to a larger space it is not always true that
\mathcal{G}_{0}(B_{X})\subset\mathcal{G}_{0}(B_{Y})
. Moreover, the previous argument can be clearly reversed, so:
\mathcal{G}_{0}(B_{X})\subset\mathcal{G}_{0}(B_{Y})
if and only if every
f\in\mathcal{H}L_{0}(B_{X})
has a norm preserving extension to
\mathcal{H}L_{0}(B_{Y})
.

We study some cases where this norm preserving extension occurs. All are cases where we have an extension morphism. The simplest occurs when
X
is 1-complemented in
Y
. Here, the complementation also spreads to
\mathcal{G}_{0}(B_{X})
.

Proposition 4.1
.

If
X
is 1-complemented in
Y
then
\rho
is an isometry and
\mathcal{G}_{0}(B_{X})
is a 1-complemented subspace of
\mathcal{G}_{0}(B_{Y})
.

Proof.

Let

For an

This provides a way to extend bounded holomorphic functions

Recall that

(x_{\alpha})
and

and

in

A generalization of this procedure (which, however, uses the AB extension in its definition) is when there exists an isometric extension morphism
s\colon X^{*}\to Y^{*}
. This happens, for instance, when
X
is an M-ideal in
Y
. More generally, if

then the existence of an isometric extension morphism

is equivalent to

being 1-complemented in

. Actually, the existence of an isometric extension morphism

is equivalent to
X
being 1-locally complemented in
Y
(see the definition in the next section and the comment before Corollary

).

Note that
s(x^{*})(x)=x^{*}(x)
for all
x\in X
,

and that
\|s(x^{*})\|=\|x^{*}\|
. This extension transfers to
\mathcal{H}^{\infty}(B_{X})
in the following way:

\displaystyle\overline{s}:\mathcal{H}^{\infty}(B_{X})

\displaystyle\to\mathcal{H}^{\infty}(B_{Y})

\displaystyle f

\displaystyle\mapsto\widetilde{f}\circ s^{*}\circ i_{Y},

where

is the canonical inclusion.

The mapping
\overline{s}
is an isometric extension from
\mathcal{H}^{\infty}(B_{X})
to
\mathcal{H}^{\infty}(B_{X^{**}})
. Again, to work in
\mathcal{H}L_{0}(B_{X})
we require a symmetrically regular hypothesis.

Proposition 4.6
.

If
X
is symmetrically regular,
X\subset Y
and there is an isometric extension morphism

then

\displaystyle\overline{s}:\mathcal{H}L_{0}(B_{X})

\displaystyle\to\mathcal{H}L_{0}(B_{Y})

\displaystyle f

\displaystyle\mapsto\widetilde{f}\circ s^{*}\circ i_{Y}

is an isometric extension.

Proof.

For any
P\in\mathcal{P}(^{m}X)
we have that
\overline{s}(P)\in\mathcal{P}(^{m}Y)
and

. Now, for

,

\displaystyle d(\overline{s}(P))(y)(z)

\displaystyle=m\widecheck{(\overline{s}(P))}(y,\dots,y,z)=m\widecheck{\widetilde{P}}\left(s^{*}(i_{Y}(y)),\dots,s^{*}(i_{Y}(y)),s^{*}(i_{Y}(z))\right)

\displaystyle=d\widetilde{P}(s^{*}(i_{Y}(y)))(s^{*}(i_{Y}(z)))=(i_{Y}^{*}\circ s^{**}\circ d\widetilde{P}\circ s^{*}\circ i_{Y})(y)(z).

This says that
d(\overline{s}(P))=i_{Y}^{*}\circ s^{**}\circ d\widetilde{P}\circ s^{*}\circ i_{Y}
for every polynomial

. Then, the same equality holds for every

Since

is symmetrically regular, by Proposition

we obtain that

. Also, note that for

, we have

. This implies that
d\widetilde{f}(s^{*}(i_{Y}(x))=i_{X^{*}}(df(x))
. Therefore,

d(\overline{s}(f))(x)=i_{Y}^{*}\circ s^{**}\left(i_{X^{*}}(df(x))\right)=s(df(x)).

\displaystyle\|d(\overline{s}(f))\|

\displaystyle\geq\sup_{x\in B_{X}}\|d(\overline{s}(f))(x)\|=\sup_{x\in B_{X}}\|s(df(x))\|

\displaystyle=\sup_{x\in B_{X}}\|df(x)\|=\|df\|,

which concludes the proof.
∎

If

4.1.
Dual isometric spaces

It is known that there exist non isomorphic Banach spaces with isomorphic duals. Attending to that, Díaz and Dineen
[
22
]
posed the following question: if
X
and
Y
are Banach spaces such that
X^{*}
and
Y^{*}
are isomorphic, under which conditions is it true that
\mathcal{P}(^{n}X)
and
\mathcal{P}(^{n}Y)
are isomorphic for every
n\geq 1
? That is, if
X^{*}
and
Y^{*}
are isomorphic (i. e. the spaces of 1-homogeneous polynomials are isomorphic) does it imply that the spaces of
n
-homogeneous polynomials are isomorphic for every
n
? They also gave a partial answer to this question. Later, a relaxation of the conditions was obtained by Cabello-Sánchez, Castillo and García
[
15
, Th. 1]
and Lassalle and Zalduendo
[
34
, Th. 4]
independently, proving that the answer is affirmative whenever
X
and
Y
are symmetrically regular. We present here a version of this result for holomorphic Lipschitz functions on the ball. Since we need to remain inside the ball when changing the space we have to restrict ourselves to the case of isometric isomorphisms.

Proposition 4.8
.

If
X
and
Y
are symmetrically regular Banach spaces such that
X^{*}
and
Y^{*}
are isometrically isomorphic then
\mathcal{H}L_{0}(B_{X})
and
\mathcal{H}L_{0}(B_{Y})
are isometrically isomorphic as well.

Proof.

Let us denote by
s\colon X^{*}\to Y^{*}
the isometric isomorphism and
consider the mapping
\overline{s}:\mathcal{H}L_{0}(B_{X})\to\mathcal{H}L_{0}(B_{Y})
as in Proposition
4.6
. By the proof of that proposition we derive that
\overline{s}
is continuous and
\|\overline{s}\|\leq 1
. Since
Y
is symmetrically regular, we can use the same procedure for the mapping
\overline{s^{-1}}:\mathcal{H}L_{0}(B_{Y})\to\mathcal{H}L_{0}(B_{X})
leading to
\|\overline{s^{-1}}\|\leq 1
. Finally, appealing to
[
34
, Cor. 3]
we obtain that
\overline{s^{-1}}\circ\overline{s}(P)=P
for every homogeneous polynomial
P
on
X
and, hence,
\overline{s^{-1}}\circ\overline{s}(f)=f
for every

. Indeed, if

is the Taylor series expansion of a given

, then
\tilde{f}(z)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\tilde{P}^{k}(z)
for every

. Thus

\overline{s}(f)(y)=\tilde{f}(s^{*}(i_{Y}(y)))=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\tilde{P}^{k}(s^{*}(y))=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\overline{s}(P^{k})(y),

for every
y\in Y
. From here

\displaystyle\overline{s^{-1}}(\overline{s}(f))(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\widetilde{\overline{s}(f)}\big((s^{-1})^{*}(i_{X}(x))\big)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\widetilde{\overline{s}(P^{k})}\big((s^{-1})^{*}(i_{X}(x)\big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\overline{s^{-1}}(\overline{s}(P^{k}))(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}P^{k}(x)=f(x),

for every

. Analogously one can check that

for every

.
∎

In the previous proposition we can change the hypothesis of
X
and
Y
being symmetrically regular by
X
or
Y
being regular. Indeed, it is proved in
[
34
, Rmk. 2]
(see also
[
15

) that if
X^{*}
and
Y^{*}
are isomorphic and

Y
.

In this section, we are interested in the relationship between

We begin with a result about a special approximation behavior in the case that the bidual space has the MAP.

Proposition 5.1
.

Let
X,Y
be Banach spaces such that
X^{**}
has the MAP. For each

with

with

satisfying

for all

.

Proof.

By Proposition

it is enough to consider

with

. If
X^{**}
has the MAP we can appeal to
[

, Cor. 1]
to obtain a net of finite rank mappings

with

and

for all

. Now we define
Q_{\alpha}=P\circ t_{\alpha}
, which clearly belongs to
\mathcal{P}_{f,0}(X,Y)
. Note that, for any

,

Then,

. Since

is a finite rank mapping, we have that

. Hence,
\widetilde{Q}_{\alpha}=\widetilde{P}\circ t_{\alpha}^{**}=P\circ t_{\alpha}^{**}
. As a consequence,
\widetilde{Q}_{\alpha}(x^{**})=P(t_{\alpha}^{**}(x^{**}))\to P(x^{**})
for all

.

∎

For a symmetrically regular space

\displaystyle\Theta:B_{X^{**}}

\displaystyle\to\mathcal{G}_{0}(B_{X})^{**}=\mathcal{H}L_{0}(B_{X})^{*}

\displaystyle x^{**}

\displaystyle\mapsto[f\in\mathcal{H}L_{0}(B_{X})\mapsto\widetilde{f}(x^{**})].

.

If

is symmetrically regular then

belongs to
\mathcal{H}L_{0}(B_{X^{**}},\mathcal{G}_{0}(B_{X})^{**})
with

Proof.

If

is symmetrically regular, by Proposition

, the AB extension is an isometry from
\mathcal{H}L_{0}(B_{X})
into
\mathcal{H}L_{0}(B_{X^{**}})

is well defined.
For any

, we have
\Theta(\cdot)(f)=\widetilde{f}
, meaning that

is weak-star holomorphic and thus, it is holomorphic. Also,
\Theta(0)=0
and for any

, once again by the symmetric regularity of

\|\Theta(x^{**})-\Theta(y^{**})\|=\sup_{f\in B_{\mathcal{H}L_{0}(B_{X})}}\|\widetilde{f}(x^{**})-\widetilde{f}(y^{**})\|\leq\|x^{**}-y^{**}\|.

This means that
\Theta\in\mathcal{H}L_{0}(B_{X^{**}},\mathcal{G}_{0}(B_{X})^{**})

\|\Theta(x^{**})-\Theta(y^{**})\|\geq\sup_{x^{*}\in B_{X^{*}}}|x^{**}(x^{*})-y^{**}(x^{*})|=\|x^{**}-y^{**}\|.

Therefore,

.
∎
