Integrated Local Energy Decay for Damped Magnetic Wave Equations on Stationary Space-Times
Collin Kofroth

Abstract
. We establish local energy decay for damped magnetic wave equations on stationary, asymptotically flat space-times subject to the geometric control condition. More specifically, we allow for the addition of time-independent magnetic and scalar potentials, which negatively affect energy coercivity and may add in unwieldy spectral effects. By asserting the non-existence of eigenvalues in the lower half-plane and resonances on the real line, we are able to apply spectral theory from the work of Metcalfe, Sterbenz, and Tataru and combine with a generalization of prior work by the present author to extend the latter work and establish local energy decay, under one additional symmetry hypothesis. Namely, we assume that either the imaginary part of the magnetic potentials are uniformly small or, more interestingly and novelly, that the damping term is the dominant principal term in the skew-adjoint part of the damped wave operator within the region where the metric perturbation from that of Minkowski space is permitted to be large. We also obtain an energy dichotomy if we do not prohibit non-zero real resonances. In order to make the structure of the argument more cohesive, we contextualize the present work within requisite existing theory.

Background and Problem Set-Up

In this work, we will establish (integrated) local energy decay for a broad class of damped wave equations on stationary, asymptotically flat space-times, including those which possess magnetic and scalar potential terms. This generalizes the result of

The particular local energy estimate of interest in this work, integrated local energy decay, is a powerful quantitative statement given in the form of an inequality which may be qualitatively interpreted as expressing that the energy of a wave must disperse quickly enough to be time-integrable within any compact region of space.

In order to define the relevant energy inequalities explicitly, we will first define the local energy norms

\displaystyle\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{LE}=\sup_{j\geq 0}\left\lVert\langle x\rangle^{-1/2}u\right\rVert_{L^{2}L^{2}\big(\mathbb{R}_{+}\times A_{j}\big)},\qquad\qquad\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{LE^{1}}=\left\lVert\partial u\right\rVert_{LE}+\left\lVert\langle x\rangle^{-1}u\right\rVert_{LE}.

The predual norm to the
LE
norm is the
LE^{*}
norm, which is defined as

\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{LE^{*}}=\sum\limits_{j=0}^{\infty}\left\lVert\langle x\rangle^{1/2}f\right\rVert_{L^{2}L^{2}\big(\mathbb{R}_{+}\times A_{j}\big)}.

If we wish for the time interval to be e.g.
[0,T]
in the above norms, then we will use the notation e.g.
\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{LE[0,T]}
.
A subscript of
c
on any of these spaces denotes compact spatial support, whereas a subscript of
0
denotes the closure of
C_{c}^{\infty}
in the relevant norm.

Analogous to the local energy spaces, we define the spatial local energy spaces
\mathcal{LE},\mathcal{LE}^{1},\mathcal{LE}^{*}
when the time variable is fixed (and there is no time derivative nor integral involved in the norms, either).
We will also require spaces which allow us to track dependence on a spectral parameter
\omega
, namely

\displaystyle\mathcal{LE}_{\omega}^{1}=\mathcal{LE}^{1}\cap|\omega|^{-1}\mathcal{LE},\qquad\qquad\dot{H}^{1}_{\omega}=\dot{H}^{1}\cap|\omega|^{-1}L^{2}.

These spaces are equipped with the norms

\displaystyle\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{\mathcal{LE}_{\omega}^{1}}=\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{\mathcal{LE}^{1}}+|\omega|\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{\mathcal{LE}},\qquad\qquad\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{\dot{H}_{\omega}^{1}}=\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{\dot{H}^{1}}+|\omega|\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{L^{2}},

respectively. They will become relevant in this work when we introduce the resolvent formalism in Section
3
.
Now, we will define local energy decay.

Definition 1.6
.

We say that (integrated) local energy decay holds for an asymptotically flat damped wave operator if

(1.1)

\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{LE^{1}[0,T]}+\left\lVert\partial u\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}L^{2}[0,T]}\lesssim\left\lVert\partial u(0)\right\rVert_{L^{2}}+\left\lVert Pu\right\rVert_{LE^{*}+L^{1}L^{2}[0,T]},

with the implicit constant being independent of
T
.

The next estimate is a weaker variant of local energy decay which does not see complex eigenvalues nor non-zero real resonances (to be defined in Section
3
).

Definition 1.7
.

We say that two-point local energy decay holds for an asymptotically flat damped wave operator if

(1.2)

\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{LE^{1}[0,T]}+\left\lVert\partial u\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}L^{2}[0,T]}\lesssim\left\lVert\partial u(0)\right\rVert_{L^{2}}+\left\lVert\partial u(T)\right\rVert_{L^{2}}+\left\lVert Pu\right\rVert_{LE^{*}+L^{1}L^{2}[0,T]},

with the implicit constant being independent of
T
.

The last estimate is a standard uniform energy bound, which embodies the behavior that one observes when the energy of the system is non-increasing.

Definition 1.8
.

We say that uniform energy bounds hold for an asymptotically flat damped wave operator if

(1.3)

\left\lVert\partial u\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}L^{2}[0,T]}\lesssim\left\lVert\partial u(0)\right\rVert_{L^{2}}+\left\lVert Pu\right\rVert_{L^{1}L^{2}[0,T]},

with the implicit constant being independent of
T
.

Notice that the uniform energy bounds provide a link between the two-point local energy decay estimate and the local energy decay estimate. In order to discuss the uniform estimate further, let
Pu=f
, and assume that
P
is stationary, asymptotically flat, and of symmetric wave type. Consider the sesquilinear map
\boldsymbol{E}
on the energy space
\mathcal{E}:=\dot{H}^{1}\times L^{2}
defined by

\boldsymbol{E}[\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v}](t)=\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}P_{0}u\bar{v}-g^{00}\partial_{t}u\overline{\partial_{t}v}\,dx,\qquad P_{0}=P\big|_{D_{t}=0},\qquad\boldsymbol{w}=(w,\partial_{t}w)\in\mathcal{E}.

This induces a quadratic energy functional, i.e. an energy form

E[\boldsymbol{u}](t):=\boldsymbol{E}[\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{u}](t).

When
\partial_{t}
is a uniformly time-like vector field (which we will assume throughout),
P_{0}
is guaranteed to be elliptic (in the principal sense).
Direct integration by parts gives that

\frac{d}{dt}E[\boldsymbol{u}](t)=2\operatorname{Re}\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\partial_{t}u\bar{f}\,dx-2\operatorname{Re}\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}a|\partial_{t}u|^{2}\,dx.

If
a
is real-valued and non-negative, then it follows that

E[\boldsymbol{u}](t)\lesssim E[\boldsymbol{u}](0)+\int\limits_{0}^{T}\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}|f\partial_{t}u|\,dxdt,\qquad 0\leq t\leq T.

If
A,V\equiv 0
, then
P_{0}
is
uniformly
elliptic (in the sense of the
full symbol
), which implies that the energy is coercive, i.e.

E[\boldsymbol{u}](t)\approx\left\lVert\partial u(t)\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}.

Energy coercivity applied to the above now provides the uniform energy bound (
1.3
).

When
A
and
V
are non-zero, one is not guaranteed energy coercivity (and thus not guaranteed uniform energy bounds), even if
a
is non-negative. However, one does get an
almost
-coercive energy statement from the ellipticity of
P_{0}
and the asymptotic flatness assumption:

(1.4)

\left\lVert\partial u(t)\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}\lesssim E[\boldsymbol{u}](t)+\left\lVert u(t)\right\rVert_{L^{2}_{c}}^{2}.

When
P
is not of symmetric wave type, then one redefines the energy by replacing
P_{0}
with its symmetric part (see Section
2.3
); the estimate (
1.4
) still holds for the energy associated to
P_{0}
by ellipticity regardless of the symmetry. The uniform bound (
1.3
) still need not hold when
P_{0}
is replaced by its symmetric part. Thus, it is not straightforward to transition from (
1.2
) to (
1.1
) when
A,V\not\equiv 0
, even for well-signed dampings; this is true even in the symmetric wave type case.

1.3.
Past Results

In
[
14
]
, the authors proved (amongst other results) that local energy decay for stationary
AF

,

[
14
]
, the authors also did not necessarily possess a coercive energy; they proved local energy decay by establishing (
1.2
) using local energy estimates in different frequency regimes, then they utilized resolvent estimates.

The work
[
2
]
utilized dissipative Mourre commutator methods to establish that if the space-time is stationary and asymptotically

(i.e.

and

. This condition requires that all trapped null bicharacteristic rays intersect where

(see Definition

[
2
]
only required this for trapped geodesics due to the product manifold structure. Geometric control dates back to
[
24
]

[
10
]

[
2
]

[
10
]

In

]
, uniform energy bounds held as a result of the conditions on the damping and lack of potentials, which made it sufficient to prove the two-point bound in order to establish local energy decay. In order to establish the former estimate,
[
10
]
followed the strategy set forth in
[
14
]
:

(1)

Establish local energy estimates that imply local energy decay for Schwartz functions, whose corresponding function space we denote as
\mathcal{S}
, which are cutoff to high, medium, and low frequency regimes.

(2)

Utilize a time frequency partition of unity to prove the estimate

\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{LE^{1}}\lesssim\left\lVert Pu\right\rVert_{LE^{*}},\qquad u\in\mathcal{S}.

(3)

Apply an extension procedure to add back in the energy at times
0
and
T
.

We will generalize the work of

Let

2.1.
High Frequency Analysis

We will recall the relevant framework and results from

needed to prove the high frequency estimate. When deviation occurs, we will proceed carefully and explicitly. The need for the
\varepsilon
-damping dominant and

Throughout this section, we will assume that

The high frequency analysis is rooted in the behavior of the bicharacteristic flow generated by the principal symbol of
P
. First, we make a minor simplification. Since the constant time slices are assumed uniformly space-like, it follows that
g^{00}\lesssim-1
. Dividing through by
-g^{00}
preserves the assumptions on the operator coefficients (see e.g.
[
17
]
); hence, we may assume that
g^{00}=-1
.

After these modifications, the principal symbol of
P
is the smooth function

p(x,\tau,\xi)=-(\tau^{2}-2\tau g^{0j}(x)\xi_{j}-g^{ij}(x)\xi_{i}\xi_{j}),\qquad(t,x,\tau,\xi)\in T^{*}\mathbb{R}^{4}\setminus o.

Notice that since
P
is stationary,
p
is independent of
t
. This symbol generates a bicharacteristic flow on
\mathbb{R}\times T^{*}\mathbb{R}^{4}
given by
\varphi_{s}(w)=\left(t_{s}(w),x_{s}(w),\tau_{s}(w),\xi_{s}(w)\right)

which solves

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rlrl}\dot{t}_{s}&=\partial_{\tau}p(\varphi_{s}(w)),&\qquad\dot{\tau}_{s}&=-\partial_{t}p(\varphi_{s}(w)),\\
\dot{x}_{s}&=\nabla_{\xi}p(\varphi_{s}(w)),&\qquad\dot{\xi}_{s}&=-\nabla_{x}p(\varphi_{s}(w)),\end{array}\right.

with initial data
w\in T^{*}\mathbb{R}^{4}.
Since
g
is smooth and asymptotically flat, and
\partial_{t}
is uniformly time-like, we have a unique, smooth, globally-defined flow with smooth dependence on the data. We will have particular interest in
null
bicharacteristics, i.e. those with initial data lying in the zero set of
p
, denoted
\operatorname{Char}(P)
. We remark that there is no distinguishing between
\pm p
on
\operatorname{Char}(P)
, hence the minus sign in front of
p
is somewhat inconsequential on it.

Using the flow
\varphi_{s}
, we define the
forward
and
backward trapped
and
non-trapped
sets with respect to
\varphi_{s}
, respectively, as

\displaystyle\Gamma_{tr}

\displaystyle=\left\{w\in T^{*}\mathbb{R}^{4}\setminus o:\sup_{s\geq 0}|x_{s}(w)|<\infty\right\}\cap\operatorname{Char}(P),

\displaystyle\Lambda_{tr}

\displaystyle=\left\{w\in T^{*}\mathbb{R}^{4}\setminus o:\sup_{s\geq 0}|x_{-s}(w)|<\infty\right\}\cap\operatorname{Char}(P),

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\infty}

\displaystyle=\left\{w\in T^{*}\mathbb{R}^{4}\setminus o:|x_{s}(w)|\rightarrow\infty\operatorname{as}s\rightarrow\infty\right\}\cap\operatorname{Char}(P),

\displaystyle\Lambda_{\infty}

\displaystyle=\left\{w\in T^{*}\mathbb{R}^{4}\setminus o:|x_{-s}(w)|\rightarrow\infty\operatorname{as}s\rightarrow\infty\right\}\cap\operatorname{Char}(P).

The
trapped
and
non-trapped
sets are defined as

\Omega^{p}_{tr}=\Gamma_{tr}\cap\Lambda_{tr}\mbox{\quad and\quad}\Omega^{p}_{\infty}=\Gamma_{\infty}\cap\Lambda_{\infty},

respectively. The flow is said to be
non-trapping
if
\Omega^{p}_{tr}{\,=\,}\emptyset
. Otherwise, the flow is said to possess trapping.
Now, we may precisely state the geometric control condition.

Definition 2.1
.

We say that the geometric control condition holds if

(2.1)

\displaystyle(\forall w\in\Omega^{p}_{tr})(\exists s\in\mathbb{R})\ \ a(x_{s}(w))>0.

In order to leverage the sign of the damping,
[
10
]
utilized a scaling argument which we will also exploit. Namely, if
u
solves
Pu=f
, we consider

\tilde{u}(t,x):=u(\gamma t,\gamma x),\qquad\gamma>0.

For this discussion, a tilde over a function will denote dilation by
\gamma
in each coordinate, as done to define
\tilde{u}
. If we call

\tilde{P}=(D_{\alpha}+\gamma\tilde{A}_{\alpha})\tilde{g}^{\alpha\beta}(D_{\beta}+\gamma\tilde{A}_{\beta})+i\gamma\tilde{a}D_{t}+\gamma^{2}\tilde{V},

then

\tilde{P}(\gamma^{-2}\tilde{u})=\tilde{f}\qquad\text{ if and only if }\qquad Pu=f.

The benefit of this scaling is that we obtain an arbitrarily large constant in front of the damping function. However, we underscore that such a large constant is also inherited by the magnetic potential.

Analogous Hamiltonian systems and trapped/non-trapped sets exist for the principal symbol
\tilde{p}
of
\tilde{P}
, which amounts to simply dilating the coordinates of
g
. If we assume that geometric control holds for the flow generated by
p
, then it is proven in
[
10
]
that it holds for the flow generated by
\tilde{p}
. Since the proof is straightforward, we will omit it here and only record the result, which is Proposition 2.6 in
[
10
]
.

Proposition 2.2
.

Assume that the geometric control condition (
2.1
) holds. Then, for any
\gamma>0
, (
2.1
) holds for the flow generated by
\tilde{p}
, with
a
replaced by
\tilde{a}
.

Since the lower-order terms
A
and
V
are not at the principal level, they do not affect
p
nor
\tilde{p}
. Henceforth, we will fix a large
\gamma>0
and study the problem from the scaled perspective while reverting back to our original notation (e.g. no tildes). It is readily seen that it is equivalent to prove Theorem
1.10
for the scaled problem, where we now have a large constant in front of the damping term.

The proof of the version of Theorem
1.10
present in
[
10
]
(i.e.
A,V\equiv 0,\ \operatorname{supp}a\subset\{|x|\leq R_{0}\}
) is a positive commutator argument. At the symbolic level, this requires the construction of an escape function and a lower-order correction term. Let
p
and
s_{skew}
represent the principal symbols of the self and skew-adjoint parts of

, respectively. Namely,

\displaystyle{p}(x,\tau,\xi)

\displaystyle=-(\tau^{2}-2\tau{g}^{0j}(x)\xi_{j}-{g}^{ij}(x)\xi_{i}\xi_{j})

\displaystyle{s}_{skew}(x,\tau,\xi)

\displaystyle=i\gamma\left(\operatorname{Im}A_{\alpha}(x)(g^{\alpha 0}(x)\tau+g^{\alpha k}(x)\xi_{k})+\tau\operatorname{Re}a(x)\right).

The multiplication by
\gamma
in

will prove advantageous for a bootstrapping argument, which is precisely why we implement the

-scaling. However, the imaginary part of

has interaction with the damping and also features multiplication by

\varepsilon
-damping dominant and

The precise escape function construction is as follows.

Lemma 2.3
.

For all
\lambda>1
, there exist symbols
q_{j}\in S^{j}(T^{*}\mathbb{R}^{3})
and
m\in S^{0}(T^{*}\mathbb{R}^{3})
, all supported in
|\xi|\geq\lambda
, so that

H_{{p}}q-2i{s}_{skew}q+{p}m\gtrsim\chi_{|\xi|>\lambda}\left\langle x\right\rangle^{-2}\left(\tau^{2}+|\xi|^{2}\right),

where
q=\tau q_{0}+q_{1}
.

Here,
H_{p}
is the Hamiltonian vector field induced by
p
, and
S^{m}(T^{*}\mathbb{R}^{3})
denotes the standard Kohn-Nirenberg symbol class of order
m
.
To each symbol
b\in S^{m}(T^{*}\mathbb{R}^{3})
, we have the associated
Weyl quantization
of
b
, denoted
b^{\operatorname{w}}
, which is a pseudodifferential operator of order
m
defined by the action

b^{\operatorname{w}}(x,D)u(x)=(2\pi)^{-3}\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}e^{i(x-y)\cdot\xi}\ b\left(\frac{x+y}{2},\xi\right)u(y)\,dyd\xi,\qquad u\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{3}).

We will use
\Psi^{m}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
to denote the space of pseudodifferential operators on
\mathbb{R}^{3}
of order
m
, and write

\Psi^{-\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{3}):=\bigcap\limits_{m\in\mathbb{R}}\Psi^{m}(\mathbb{R}^{3})

for the space of smoothing operators on
\mathbb{R}^{3}.

Lemma
2.3
was proven in
[
10
]
in the special case of

and
\operatorname{supp}a\subseteq\{|x|\leq R_{0}\}
, leading to a simplified

. As in the aforementioned work, we will work with the half-wave decomposition; it is proven in
[
10

that the null bicharacterstics are equivalent through a reparameterization argument, and this fact continues to hold here without any change (lower-order terms do not affect

and hence will not affect its induced bicharacteristic flow). That is, we factor

as

p(\tau,x,\xi)=-(\tau-b^{+}(x,\xi))(\tau-b^{-}(x,\xi)),

where

b^{\pm}(x,\xi)={g^{0j}(x)\xi_{j}\pm\sqrt{[g^{0j}(x)\xi_{j}]^{2}+g^{ij}(x)\xi_{i}\xi_{j}}}.

Using that
\partial_{t}
is uniformly time-like, it is readily seen that

are both positively homogeneous of degree 1 in
\xi
, and

b^{+}(x,\xi)>0>b^{-}(x,\xi)

whenever
\xi\neq 0
. The Hamiltonians

also generate bicharacteristic flows

\varphi_{s}^{\pm}(w){\,=\,}\left(t_{s}^{\pm}(w),x_{s}^{\pm}(w),\tau_{s}^{\pm}(w),\xi_{s}^{\pm}(w)\right)

on
\mathbb{R}\times T^{*}\mathbb{R}^{4}

which solve the Hamiltonian systems

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rlrl}\dot{t}_{s}^{\pm}&=1,&\dot{\tau}_{s}^{\pm}&=0,\\
\dot{x}_{s}^{\pm}&=-\nabla_{\xi}b^{\pm}(\varphi_{s}^{\pm}(w)),&\dot{\xi}_{s}^{\pm}&=\nabla_{x}b^{\pm}(\varphi_{s}^{\pm}(w)),\end{array}\right.

with initial data
w\in T^{*}\mathbb{R}^{4}.

Observe that the
(t,\tau)
and
(x,\xi)
systems are decoupled, allowing us to project onto the
(x,\xi)
components of the flow without losing information. Notice that, after we project, we are no longer looking at null bicharacteristics but, rather, bicharacteristics with initial data having non-zero
\xi
component.

Now, we may define the forward and backward (denoted by the
\pm
notation) trapped and non-trapped sets for the half-wave flows as

\displaystyle\Gamma_{tr}^{\pm}

\displaystyle=\left\{w\in T^{*}\mathbb{R}^{3}\setminus o:\sup_{s\geq 0}|x^{\pm}_{s}(w)|<\infty\right\},

\displaystyle\Lambda_{tr}^{\pm}

\displaystyle=\left\{w\in T^{*}\mathbb{R}^{3}\setminus o:\sup_{s\geq 0}|x^{\pm}_{-s}(w)|<\infty\right\},

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\infty}^{\pm}

\displaystyle=\left\{w\in T^{*}\mathbb{R}^{3}\setminus o:|x^{\pm}_{s}(w)|\rightarrow\infty\ \operatorname{as}\ s\rightarrow\infty\right\},

\displaystyle\Lambda_{\infty}^{\pm}

\displaystyle=\left\{w\in T^{*}\mathbb{R}^{3}\setminus o:|x^{\pm}_{-s}(w)|\rightarrow\infty\ \operatorname{as}\ s\rightarrow\infty\right\}.

The trapped and non-trapped sets are

\displaystyle{\Omega}_{tr}^{\pm}=\Gamma_{tr}^{\pm}\cap\Lambda_{tr}^{\pm}

\displaystyle\mbox{\quad and\quad}{\Omega}_{\infty}^{\pm}=\Gamma_{\infty}^{\pm}\cap\Lambda_{\infty}^{\pm},

\displaystyle{\Omega}_{tr}={\Omega}_{tr}^{+}\cup{\Omega}_{tr}^{-}

\displaystyle\mbox{\quad and\quad}{\Omega}_{\infty}={\Omega}_{\infty}^{+}\cup{\Omega}_{\infty}^{-}.

As a consequence of the factoring, we have the identities

{\Omega}_{tr}=\Pi_{x,\xi}(\Omega^{p}_{tr})\mbox{\quad and\quad}{\Omega}_{\infty}=\Pi_{x,\xi}(\Omega^{p}_{\infty}),

where
\Pi_{x,\xi}(t,x,\tau,\xi)=(x,\xi).
Additionally, we may re-state geometric control in terms of the factored flow. If
w\in\Omega_{tr}
, then it is either trapped with respect the flow generated by
p^{+}
or
p^{-}
. If it is trapped with respect to
p^{+},
then there is a time so that
w
is flowed along a
p^{+}
-bicharacteristic ray to a point where the damping is positive, and similarly if it is trapped with respect to
p^{-}
.

Since
A
and
V
do not occur at the principal level for
P
, they will not affect the individual components of the escape function construction. Hence, the construction in
[
10
]
applies directly. The methodology in
[
10
]
, motivated by a combination of
[
2
]
,
[
14
]
, and
[
13
]
, is performed in the following steps:

(1)

On the characteristic set.
We will refer to
\{|x|\leq R_{0}\}
as the
interior
and
\{|x|>R_{0}\}
as the
exterior
.

(a)

Interior, semi-bounded null bicharacteristics.
Here, one considers semi-bounded null bicharacteristics with initial data living in the interior region. Working with semi-bounded trajectories is favorable since they include both trajectories that are trapped and those which escape slowly. Additionally, geometric control extends to such trajectories. This is where geometric control is used.

(b)

The remainder of the interior region.
In this region, all of the trajectories escape both forward and backward in time. This region is more classical, but care must be taken both to avoid the trapping and incorporate the half-wave structure.

(c)

The exterior region.
As a consequence of asymptotic flatness, there are no trapped trajectories in this region. Here, one appeals to geometrically-adapted flat wave theory. The multiplier also allows for the absorption of an error term which arises in the prior region.

(2)

On the elliptic set.
Here, one requires a lower-order symbol which provides no contribution on the characteristic set and provides positivity off of it. This essentially follows from the minimization of an appropriate quadratic in the dual time variable
\tau
.

Now, we cite the specific results from
[

\displaystyle\left|\left\langle{R}_{1}v_{>>\lambda},v_{>>\lambda}\right\rangle\right|+\left\lVert v_{>>\lambda}\right\rVert_{H^{1/2}_{t,x}}^{2}\lesssim\lambda^{-1}\left\lVert v\right\rVert_{LE^{1}}^{2}.

One shows that

\left|\left\langle{R}_{0}v,v\right\rangle\right|\lesssim C(\lambda)\left\lVert v\right\rVert_{L^{2}L^{2}}^{2}

in a similar manner, except that one cannot leverage frequency localization and may incur an implicit constant which depends on
\lambda
. Such a term will appear on the upper bound side of (
2.8
), so having such an implicit constant is permissible.

Summarizing, we have shown that the right-hand side of (
2.9
) is bounded below by a multiple of

(2.10)

\displaystyle\left\lVert\partial v_{>\lambda}\right\rVert_{LE_{<2R_{0}}}^{2}-C(\lambda)\left\lVert v\right\rVert_{L^{2}L^{2}}^{2}-\lambda^{-1}\left\lVert v\right\rVert_{LE^{1}}^{2}.

Next, we consider the left-hand side of (
2.9
). By the Cauchy-Schwarz and Plancherel’s theorem,

\displaystyle\left\langle Pv,\left(q^{\operatorname{w}}-\frac{i}{2}m^{\operatorname{w}}\right)v\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\left\langle Pv,\left(q^{\operatorname{w}}-\frac{i}{2}m^{\operatorname{w}}\right)v_{>>\lambda}\right\rangle+\left\langle{S}v,v\right\rangle,\qquad\qquad{S}\in\Psi^{-\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{3})

\displaystyle\lesssim C(\lambda)\left(\left\lVert Pv\right\rVert_{LE^{*}_{c}}\left\lVert v\right\rVert_{LE^{1}}+\left\lVert v\right\rVert_{L^{2}L^{2}}^{2}\right).

By once again applying frequency splitting, the remaining inner products on the left-hand side of (
2.9
) are of the form

(\gamma+\gamma^{2})\left(\left\langle\widetilde{R}_{0}v,v\right\rangle+\left\langle\widetilde{R}_{1}v_{>>\lambda},v_{>>\lambda}\right\rangle\right),\qquad\widetilde{R}_{j}\in\Psi^{j}(\mathbb{R}^{3}).

We have discussed how to bound both of these terms; namely,

\left|\left\langle\widetilde{R}_{0}v,v\right\rangle\right|+\left|\left\langle\widetilde{R}_{1}v_{>>\lambda},v_{>>\lambda}\right\rangle\right|\lesssim C(\lambda)\left\lVert v\right\rVert_{L^{2}L^{2}}^{2}+\lambda^{-1}\left\lVert v\right\rVert_{LE^{1}}^{2}.

Factoring in the scalar coefficients of these inner products, we have demonstrated that the left-hand side of (
2.9
) is bounded above by a multiple of

(2.11)

\displaystyle C(\lambda,\gamma)\left(\left\lVert Pv\right\rVert_{LE^{*}_{c}}\left\lVert v\right\rVert_{LE^{1}}+\left\lVert v\right\rVert_{L^{2}L^{2}}^{2}\right)+\gamma^{2}\lambda^{-1}\left\lVert v\right\rVert_{LE^{1}}^{2}

Combining (
2.10
)-(
2.11
) in application to (
2.9
) and completing the
LE^{1}
norm on the lower-bound side provides (
2.8
).
∎

2.2.
Remaining Frequency Analyses and Two-Point Local Energy Decay

In order to establish Theorem
1.11
, we require similar estimates in the low and medium frequency regimes. The damping does not play a meaningful role in either regime, as it may be treated as a lower-order perturbation term. Like in
[
10
]
, the relevant estimates from
[
14
]
carry through. We will briefly summarize why this is the case, in lieu of full proofs.

At low frequencies, the obstruction to local energy decay arises when
P
has a
resonance
at frequency zero (see Section
3
for a precise definition and further discussion on spectral obstructions to local energy decay). A quantitative condition on the existence of corresponding zero resonant states is the following
zero non-resonance condition
.

Definition 2.7
.

P
is said to satisfy a zero non-resonance condition if there exists some
K_{0}
, independent of
t
, such that

(2.12)

\displaystyle\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{\dot{H}^{1}}\leq K_{0}\left\lVert P_{0}u\right\rVert_{\dot{H}^{-1}}\qquad\forall u\in\dot{H}^{1}.

The elliptic operator

P_{0}=P\big|_{D_{t}=0}=(D_{j}+A_{j})g^{jk}(D_{k}+A_{k})+A_{0}g^{0j}(D_{j}+A_{j})+(D_{j}+A_{j})g^{j0}A_{0}+(A_{0})^{2}g^{00}+V

represents
P
at time frequency zero, and
we underscore that the damping does not appear. Hence, the damping has no bearing on whether or not the zero non-resonance condition holds. For example, if
P
is stationary and asymptotically flat with
\operatorname{Im}A\equiv 0
,
V>0,
and
A_{0}=0
, then
P
satisfies the zero non-resonance condition. This follows from Lemma 6.2iii in
[
14
]
, which also features a more general condition. The relevant low frequency estimate is the following, and the corresponding theorem in
[
14
]
is Theorem 6.1.

Theorem 2.8
.

Let
P
be an asymptotically flat damped wave operator which satisfies the zero non-resonance condition, and suppose that
\partial_{t}
is uniformly time-like. Then, the bound

(2.13)

\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{LE^{1}}\lesssim\left\lVert\partial_{t}u\right\rVert_{LE^{1}_{c}}+\left\lVert Pu\right\rVert_{LE^{*}}

for all
u\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{4})
.

The proof of Theorem
2.8
leverages weighted elliptic estimates for the flat Laplacian
\Delta
in order to get similar estimates for
AF
perturbations. Once again, the damping does not play a meaningful role. At frequency zero, it provides no contribution, and near frequency zero, it is absorbed by the error term in (
2.13
); the damping arises when estimating
P_{0}u
by
Pu
within a compact spatial set.

At medium frequencies, we require a weighted estimate which implies local energy decay for solutions supported at any range of time frequencies bounded away from both zero and infinity. This is rooted in Carleman estimates. The Carleman weights which we need are constructed in e.g.
[
1
]
,
[
9
]
. The main medium frequency estimate is the following, and the corresponding theorem in
[
14
]
is Theorem 5.4. We remark that the theorem does not imply an absence of embedded eigenvalues/resonances on the real line.

Theorem 2.9
.

Let
P
be an asymptotically flat damped wave operator, and suppose that
\partial_{t}
is uniformly time-like. Then, for any
\delta>0
, there exists a bounded, non-decreasing radial weight
\varphi=\varphi(\ln(1+r))
so that for all
u\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{4})
, we have the bound

(2.14)

\left\lVert(1+\varphi_{+}^{\prime\prime})^{1/2}e^{\varphi}\left(\nabla u,\left\langle r\right\rangle^{-1}(1+\varphi^{\prime})u\right)\right\rVert_{LE}+\left\lVert(1+\varphi^{\prime})^{1/2}e^{\varphi}\partial_{t}u\right\rVert_{LE}\\
\lesssim\left\lVert e^{\varphi}Pu\right\rVert_{LE^{*}}+\delta\left(\left\lVert(1+\varphi^{\prime})^{1/2}e^{\varphi}u\right\rVert_{LE}+\left\lVert\left\langle r\right\rangle^{-1}(1+\varphi_{+}^{\prime\prime})^{1/2}(1+\varphi^{\prime})e^{\varphi}\partial_{t}u\right\rVert_{LE}\right),

with the implicit constant independent of
\delta
.

The proof of this theorem utilizes two intermediate Carleman estimates within two different regions of space, which may be combined using a cutoff argument in order to prove Theorem
2.9
.

(1)

Within a large compact set:
The damping term is well-signed and readily absorbable as a perturbation due to the conditions on the weight
\varphi,
which will be convex.

(2)

Outside of a large compact set:
Here, the damping is a small
AF
perturbation, so the proof in
[
14
]
follows through without any modification. Within this region, the authors of
[
14
]
bend the weight to be constant near infinity in order to apply exterior wave estimates. This leads to breaking this case into three sub-regions: one where the Carleman weight is convex, a transition region where the conditions break in order to bend the weight to be constant near infinity, and a region near infinity where the weight is constant.

The proofs of the Carleman estimates in the above regions are based on positive commutator arguments utilizing the self- and skew-adjoint parts of the conjugated operator
P_{\varphi}=e^{\varphi}Pe^{-\varphi}.

The high, low, and medium frequency estimates are the key ingredients needed to establish Theorem
1.11
. As in
[
14
]
,
[
10
]
, it sufficient to remove the Cauchy data at times
0
and
T
in order to prove Theorem
1.11
; we will elaborate on this momentarily. This makes it significantly easier to perform frequency localization. The pertinent result in
[
14
]
is Theorem 7.1.

Theorem 2.10
.

Let
P
be a stationary, asymptotically flat damped wave operator satisfying the geometric control condition (
2.1
), and suppose that
\partial_{t}
is uniformly time-like. Then, the estimate

(2.15)

\displaystyle\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{LE^{1}}\lesssim\left\lVert Pu\right\rVert_{LE^{*}}

holds for all
u\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{4})
.

Proof.

We will utilize a time-frequency partition of unity. Let
0<\tau_{0}\ll 1
and
\tau_{1}\gg 1
, which will be chosen with more precision shortly. Then, we can write

\displaystyle u

\displaystyle=\chi_{|\tau|<\tau_{0}}(D_{t})u+\chi_{\tau_{0}<|\tau|<\tau_{1}}(D_{t})u+\chi_{|\tau|>\tau_{1}}(D_{t})u=:Q_{1}u+Q_{2}u+Q_{3}u.

Since
P
is stationary, it commutes with each
Q_{j},
and so it suffices to show that

(2.16)

\left\lVert Q_{j}u\right\rVert_{LE^{1}}\lesssim\left\lVert Pu\right\rVert_{LE^{*}},\qquad j=1,2,3.

First, we apply Theorem
2.13
to
Q_{1}u
and appeal to Plancherel’s theorem in order to obtain that

\displaystyle\left\lVert Q_{1}u\right\rVert_{LE^{1}}\lesssim\left\lVert\partial_{t}(Q_{1}u)\right\rVert_{LE^{1}_{c}}+\left\lVert P(Q_{1}u)\right\rVert_{LE^{*}}\lesssim\tau_{0}\left\lVert Q_{1}u\right\rVert_{LE^{1}_{c}}+\left\lVert Pu\right\rVert_{LE^{*}}.

If
\tau_{0}
is sufficiently small, then we may absorb the error term on the upper-hand side into lower-bound side, which provides (
2.16
) for
j=1.

We proceed similarly with
Q_{2}u
via Theorem
2.9
:

\displaystyle\left\lVert(1+\varphi_{+}^{\prime\prime})^{1/2}e^{\varphi}\left(\nabla Q_{2}u,\left\langle r\right\rangle^{-1}(1+\varphi^{\prime})Q_{2}u\right)\right\rVert_{LE}+\left\lVert(1+\varphi^{\prime})^{1/2}e^{\varphi}\partial_{t}Q_{2}u\right\rVert_{LE}

\displaystyle\lesssim\left\lVert e^{\varphi}P(Q_{2}u)\right\rVert_{LE^{*}}+\delta\left(\left\lVert(1+\varphi^{\prime})^{1/2}e^{\varphi}Q_{2}u\right\rVert_{LE}+\left\lVert\left\langle r\right\rangle^{-1}(1+\varphi_{+}^{\prime\prime})^{1/2}(1+\varphi^{\prime})e^{\varphi}\partial_{t}Q_{2}u\right\rVert_{LE}\right)

\displaystyle\lesssim\left\lVert e^{\varphi}Pu\right\rVert_{LE^{*}}+\frac{\delta}{\tau_{0}}\left\lVert(1+\varphi^{\prime})^{1/2}e^{\varphi}\partial_{t}Q_{2}u\right\rVert_{LE}+\delta\tau_{1}\left\lVert\left\langle r\right\rangle^{-1}(1+\varphi_{+}^{\prime\prime})^{1/2}(1+\varphi^{\prime})e^{\varphi}Q_{2}u\right\rVert_{LE}.

By choosing
\delta
sufficiently small, last two terms absorb into the left-hand side. Since
\varphi
is bounded and
\varphi^{\prime}\geq 0
, we obtain (
2.16
) for
j=2.

Finally, we apply Theorem
1.10
to
Q_{3}u(t-T/2)
:

\left\lVert Q_{3}u\right\rVert_{LE^{1}[-T/2,T/2]}\lesssim\left\lVert\partial(Q_{3}u)(-T/2)\right\rVert_{L^{2}}+\left\lVert\left\langle x\right\rangle^{-2}u\right\rVert_{LE[-T/2,T/2]}+\left\lVert P(Q_{3}u)\right\rVert_{LE^{*}[-T/2,T/2]}.

Taking the limit as
T\rightarrow\infty
and then applying Plancherel’s theorem give that

\left\lVert Q_{3}u\right\rVert_{LE^{1}}\lesssim\tau_{1}^{-1}\left\lVert Q_{3}u\right\rVert_{LE^{1}}+\left\lVert Pu\right\rVert_{LE^{*}}.

If
\tau_{1}
is large enough, then the error term on the right absorbs into the left, giving (
2.16
) for
j=3.

∎

We underscore how important it is that
\delta
may be chosen arbitrarily in Theorem
2.9
: It allowed for compatibility with the high and low frequency estimates regardless of how high or low the frequency thresholds became, respectively (so long as they were away from zero and infinity).

As in Section 7 of
[
14
]
, one proves that Theorem
2.10
implies Theorem
1.11
by fixing
u
and constructing a function
v
which matches the Cauchy data of
u
at times
0
and
T
(and satisfies an appropriate bound) which allows one to apply (
2.15
) to
u-v.
This construction is performing using a partition of unity on the support of
u[0]
,
u[T]
, and
Pu.
In particular, one splits into an interior region
\{|x|<4R_{0}\}
and an exterior region
\{|x|>2R_{0}\}
. The damping is non-problematic in the interior and is small in the exterior (as with the other lower-order terms). The latter fact is important since a time reversal argument is used, which makes the damping a driving force.

In the interior, one utilizes a unit time interval partition of unity
\{\chi_{j}\}
and analyzes the equations

Pv_{j}=\chi_{j}f,
matching the data at times
0
and
T
with the first and last of elements in the partition, respectively. One then generates the desired approximate of
u
via
\sum\chi_{j}v_{j}.
In the exterior region, one chooses an appropriate small
AF
perturbation of
\Box
which matches
P
in the exterior. If one considers the same differential equation (same data and forcing) but replaces
P
by the perturbation, one obtains good bounds via local energy decay. By truncating the solution appropriately to
|x|>R_{0}
and
t<T,
one obtains the desired approximate in the exterior.

2.3.
An Energy Dichotomy

Here, we apply Theorem
1.11
in order to prove the energy dichotomy present in Theorem
1.12
. Very little deviation is needed from the strategy given in
[
14
]
, although we must take advantage of the damping in the damping-dominant case. In particular, we must use the damping to absorb time derivative error terms for
|x|\leq 2R_{0}
, outside of which we may use asymptotic flatness (for space derivative terms, one can use Young’s inequality for products, at the expense of shrinking
\varepsilon
). In the
\varepsilon
-almost symmetric case, such interior errors are automatically small.

Proof.

Split
P
and
P_{0}
into self- and skew-adjoint parts

P=P^{s}+P^{a},\qquad P_{0}=P_{0}^{s}+P_{0}^{a},

respectively, and define the energy of the symmetric part of
P
as

{E}^{s}[\boldsymbol{u}](t)=\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}P_{0}^{s}u\bar{u}-g^{00}|\partial_{t}u|^{2}\,dx.

Then,

E^{s}[\boldsymbol{u}](t)=E^{s}[\boldsymbol{u}](0)+2\operatorname{Re}\int\limits_{0}^{t}\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\partial_{s}u\overline{P^{s}u}\,dxds.

Applying this to

E[\boldsymbol{u}](t)=\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}P_{0}u\bar{u}-g^{00}|\partial_{t}u|^{2}\,dx

and utilizing the symmetry assumptions yield that

(2.17)

\displaystyle E[\boldsymbol{u}](t)

\displaystyle=E^{s}[\boldsymbol{u}](0)+2\operatorname{Re}\int\limits_{0}^{t}\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\partial_{s}u\overline{P^{s}u}\,dxds+\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}P_{0}^{a}u\bar{u}\,dx

\displaystyle=E[\boldsymbol{u}](0)-\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}P_{0}^{a}u(0)\overline{u(0)}\,dx+2\operatorname{Re}\int\limits_{0}^{t}\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\partial_{s}u\overline{f}\,dxds-2\operatorname{Re}\int\limits_{0}^{t}\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\partial_{s}u\overline{P^{a}u}\,dxds+\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}P_{0}^{a}u\bar{u}\,dx

\displaystyle=E[\boldsymbol{u}](0)+2\operatorname{Re}\int\limits_{0}^{t}\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\partial_{s}u\overline{f}\,dxds-2\operatorname{Re}\int\limits_{0}^{t}\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\partial_{s}u\overline{P^{a}u}\,dxds+\int\limits_{0}^{t}\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\partial_{s}(P_{0}^{a}u\bar{u})\,dx\,ds

\displaystyle=E[\boldsymbol{u}](0)+2\operatorname{Re}\int\limits_{0}^{t}\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\partial_{s}u\overline{f}\,dxds-2\operatorname{Re}\int\limits_{0}^{t}\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\partial_{s}u\overline{P^{a}u}\,dxds-2i\operatorname{Im}\int\limits_{0}^{t}\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\partial_{s}u\overline{P_{0}^{a}u}\,dx\,ds

\displaystyle\leq E[\boldsymbol{u}](0)+2\operatorname{Re}\int\limits_{0}^{t}\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\partial_{s}u\overline{f}\,dxds-C(\varepsilon,\textbf{c})\int\limits_{0}^{t}\int\limits_{B_{2R_{0}}(0)}\operatorname{Re}a|\partial_{s}u|^{2}\,dxds+D(\varepsilon,\textbf{c})\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{LE^{1}}^{2}

where we have used the assumed symmetry assumptions in the last line, shrinking
\varepsilon
if necessary. In the above,
C(\varepsilon,\textbf{c})>0
, and
D(\varepsilon,\textbf{c})=\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon)
in the almost-symmetric case (and
C(\varepsilon,\textbf{c})=2
), whereas

D(\varepsilon,\textbf{c})=\mathcal{O}(\textbf{c})
in the damping-dominant case.

Dropping the damping term from the above and pairing the result with (
1.4
) gives that

\displaystyle\left\lVert\partial u\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}L^{2}[0,T]}^{2}\lesssim\left\lVert\partial u(0)\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}+\left\lVert\partial^{\leq 1}u(T)\right\rVert_{L^{2}_{c}}^{2}+D(\varepsilon,\textbf{c})\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{LE^{1}[0,T]}^{2}+\int\limits_{0}^{T}\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}|\partial_{t}u||f|\,dxdt,

where
\partial^{\leq 1}u:=\sum\limits_{|\alpha|\leq 1}\partial^{\alpha}u.

The Schwarz inequality and Hölder’s inequality imply that

(2.18)

\displaystyle\left\lVert\partial u\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}L^{2}[0,T]}\lesssim\left\lVert\partial u(0)\right\rVert_{L^{2}}+\left\lVert\partial^{\leq 1}u(T)\right\rVert_{L^{2}_{c}}+(\delta+D(\varepsilon,\textbf{c}))\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{LE^{1}[0,T]}+\delta^{-1}\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{LE^{*}+L^{1}L^{2}[0,T]},

where
\delta>0
is arbitrary.
Now, we will make use of the two-point local energy estimate (
1.2
).
By combining (
2.18
) with (
1.2
) and choosing
\delta
sufficiently small (and shrinking
\varepsilon
if necessary in the almost-symmetric case), we obtain that

\left\lVert\partial u(T)\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}\lesssim\left\lVert\partial u(0)\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}+\left\lVert\partial^{\leq 1}u(T)\right\rVert_{L^{2}_{c}}^{2}+\left\lVert f\right\rVert^{2}_{LE^{*}+L^{1}L^{2}[0,\infty)}.

On the other hand, (
1.2
) directly gives that

\left\lVert\partial^{\leq 1}u(t)\right\rVert_{L^{2}L^{2}_{c}}^{2}\lesssim\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{LE^{1}_{c}[0,T]}^{2}\lesssim\left\lVert\partial u(0)\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}+\left\lVert\partial u(T)\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}+\left\lVert f\right\rVert^{2}_{LE^{*}+L^{1}L^{2}[0,\infty)},

and so

(2.19)

\displaystyle\left\lVert\partial u\right\rVert_{L^{2}L^{2}[0,T]}^{2}\lesssim\left\lVert\partial u(T)\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}+(T+1)\left(\left\lVert\partial u(0)\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}+\left\lVert f\right\rVert^{2}_{LE^{*}+L^{1}L^{2}[0,\infty)}\right).

Notice that if we call
E(T)=\left\lVert\partial u\right\rVert_{L^{2}L^{2}[0,T]}^{2},
then (
2.19
) gives that

(2.20)

\displaystyle E^{\prime}(T)=\left\lVert\partial u(T)\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}\geq\alpha E(T)-(T+1)\left(\left\lVert\partial u(0)\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}+\left\lVert f\right\rVert^{2}_{LE^{*}+L^{1}L^{2}[0,\infty)}\right),

where
\alpha>0
is a constant.

We will consider two cases.
First, we assume that

E(T)<2\alpha^{-1}(T+1)\left(\left\lVert\partial u(0)\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}+\left\lVert f\right\rVert^{2}_{LE^{*}+L^{1}L^{2}[0,\infty)}\right)

for all
T>0
. In such a case, we have that, in particular,

T^{-1}\left\lVert\partial u\right\rVert_{L^{2}L^{2}[0,T]}^{2}\lesssim\left\lVert\partial u(0)\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}+\left\lVert f\right\rVert^{2}_{LE^{*}+L^{1}L^{2}[0,\infty)}.

By the mean-value theorem for integrals, there exists a sequence
(T_{j})
so that
T_{j}\rightarrow\infty
as
j\rightarrow\infty
, and

\left\lVert\partial u(T_{j})\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}\lesssim\left\lVert\partial u(0)\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}+\left\lVert f\right\rVert^{2}_{LE^{*}+L^{1}L^{2}[0,\infty)}.

We may use (
1.2
) and let
T_{j}\rightarrow\infty
to conclude that local energy decay holds.

Next, we consider if

(2.21)

\displaystyle E(T^{\prime})\geq 2\alpha^{-1}(T^{\prime}+1)\left(\left\lVert\partial u(0)\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}+\left\lVert f\right\rVert^{2}_{LE^{*}+L^{1}L^{2}[0,\infty)}\right)

for some
T^{\prime}>0
. Then,
E(T)
must bound (
2.21
) from above for all
T\geq T^{\prime}
, since (
2.20
) implies that
E(T)
is increasing for all
T>T^{\prime}.

By applying an integrating factor argument to the differential inequality (
2.20
) and using (
2.21
), we get that

E(T)\gtrsim_{T^{\prime}}e^{\alpha T}\left(\left\lVert\partial u(0)\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}+\left\lVert f\right\rVert^{2}_{LE^{*}+L^{1}L^{2}[0,\infty)}\right),\qquad T\geq T^{\prime},\qquad T^{\prime}\gg 1.

Combining this with (
2.19
) gives the exponential growth for large enough
T
.
∎

3.
Resolvent Theory and Local Energy Decay

In this section, we will introduce the spectral theory required to prove Theorem
1.14
. This follows from the resolvent formalism introduced in
[
14
]
and the corresponding scattering theory. We will summarize the relevant parts of their work here and provide details that were either omitted or are instructive to repeat. In particular, the results necessary to prove Theorem
1.14
are based on the same frequency estimates present in both
[
14
]
and here, so our work requires little deviation. Throughout this section, we assume that
P
is stationary.

Consider
Pu=0.
One arrives at the
stationary problem
by studying “mode solutions” of the form
u(t,x)=u_{\omega}(x)e^{i\omega t}
, where
\omega\in\mathbb{C}
(equivalently, one replaces
D_{t}
by
\omega
). Plugging such
u
into the given homogeneous equation generates the
stationary
equation

P_{\omega}u_{\omega}=0,\qquad\operatorname{where}\qquad P_{\omega}=\Delta_{g,A}+W(x,D_{x})+\omega B(x,D_{x})+g^{00}\omega^{2},

and

\displaystyle\Delta_{g,A}

\displaystyle=(D_{j}+A_{j})g^{jk}(D_{k}+A_{k}),

\displaystyle W(x,D_{x})

\displaystyle=A_{0}g^{0j}(D_{j}+A_{j})+(D_{j}+A_{j})g^{j0}A_{0}+(A_{0})^{2}g^{00}+V,

\displaystyle B(x,D_{x})

\displaystyle=g^{0j}(D_{j}+A_{j})+(D_{j}+A_{j})g^{j0}+2A_{0}g^{00}+ia.

The
resolvent

R_{\omega}
is defined as the inverse of
P_{\omega}
when such an inverse exists. More explicitly, if we consider the homogeneous Cauchy problem

Pu=0,\qquad u(0)=0,\qquad-g^{00}\partial_{t}u(0)=f,

then we may formally define
R_{\omega}
via the Fourier-Laplace transform of
u
, i.e.

R_{\omega}f=\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}e^{-i\omega t}u(t)\,dt=:\mathcal{F}_{t\rightarrow\omega}(\mathbbm{1}_{[0,\infty)}(t)u),\qquad\omega\in\mathbb{C}.

One may check via formal integration by parts that both definitions of
R_{\omega}
are consistent. In this section, we will take
f
to be in either
L^{2}
or
\mathcal{L}\mathcal{E}^{*}
, and it will be clear from context which is the case. It remains to make rigorous sense of
R_{\omega}
as a well-defined bounded operator.

From the global energy bounds and Gronwall’s inequality, it follows that
u
satisfies the crude estimate

(3.1)

\left\lVert\partial u(t)\right\rVert_{L^{2}}\lesssim e^{ct}\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{L^{2}},\qquad c\geq 0.

Using (
3.1
) and the Minkowski integral inequality, we obtain that

(3.2)

\displaystyle\left\lVert R_{\omega}f\right\rVert_{\dot{H}^{1}}\leq\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}e^{\operatorname{Im}\omega t}\left\lVert\nabla u(t,\cdot)\right\rVert_{L^{2}}\,dt\lesssim\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}e^{\operatorname{Im}\omega t}e^{ct}\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{L^{2}}\,dt\lesssim|\operatorname{Im}\omega+c|^{-1}\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{L^{2}},\qquad\operatorname{Im}\omega+c<0.

Meanwhile, integrating by parts once provides that
\omega R_{\omega}f=-i\mathcal{F}_{t\rightarrow\omega}(\mathbbm{1}_{[0,\infty)}\partial_{t}u).
Taking the
L^{2}
norm of the above and performing the same work as in (
3.2
) yields an identical upper bound. Combining these estimates together gives the inequality

(3.3)

\displaystyle\left\lVert R_{\omega}f\right\rVert_{\dot{H}_{\omega}^{1}}\lesssim|\operatorname{Im}\omega+c|^{-1}\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{L^{2}},\qquad\operatorname{Im}\omega+c<0.

Hence, we may validly define the resolvent as a bounded operator from
L^{2}
to
\dot{H}_{\omega}^{1}
, provided that
\omega
is in the range given in (
3.3
). Notice that if the uniform energy bound (
1.3
) holds, then the resolvent is holomorphic in the lower half-plane
\mathcal{H}:=\{\omega\in\mathbb{C}:\operatorname{Im}\omega<0\}
and satisfies the bound

(3.4)

\displaystyle\left\lVert R_{\omega}\right\rVert_{L^{2}\rightarrow\dot{H}^{1}_{\omega}}\lesssim|\operatorname{Im}\omega|^{-1},\qquad\omega\in\mathcal{H}.

If the uniform energy bound does not hold, then one is only guaranteed meromorphic continuation to
\mathcal{H}.
Due to this tie-in with uniform energy bounds, we will refer to (
3.4
) as the
uniform energy resolvent bound
. There is also an analogous resolvent bound to local energy decay, which we state as a theorem and will not prove here; see the proof of Theorem 2.3 in
[
14
]
for more. It is largely a consequence of Plancherel’s theorem, along with utilization of facts which we will discuss after the statement of the theorem. The damping plays no meaningful role here.

Theorem 3.1
.

Local energy decay holds for a stationary damped wave operator
P
if and only if
R_{\omega}
satisfies the local energy resolvent bound

(3.5)

\displaystyle\left\lVert R_{\omega}\right\rVert_{\mathcal{L}\mathcal{E}^{*}\rightarrow\mathcal{L}\mathcal{E}^{1}_{\omega}}\lesssim 1,\qquad\omega\in\mathcal{H}.

We observe that if the uniform energy resolvent bound holds, then the local energy resolvent bound holds for
\operatorname{Im}\omega\lesssim-1
, since

(3.6)

\displaystyle\left\lVert R_{\omega}f\right\rVert_{\mathcal{LE}_{\omega}^{1}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\left\lVert R_{\omega}f\right\rVert_{\dot{H}^{1}}+|\omega|\left\lVert R_{\omega}f\right\rVert_{L^{2}}=\left\lVert R_{\omega}f\right\rVert_{\dot{H}_{\omega}^{1}}\lesssim|\operatorname{Im}\omega|^{-1}\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{L^{2}}\lesssim\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{\mathcal{LE}^{*}}.

On the other hand (
3.5
) implies (
3.4
); Fredholm theory implies that
R_{\omega}
is bounded and holomorphic from
L^{2}
to
\dot{H}^{1}_{\omega}
, and the precise operator norm bound can by obtained by splitting
f\in L^{2}
as

f=\chi_{<|\operatorname{Im}\omega|^{-1}}f+\chi_{>|\operatorname{Im}\omega|^{-1}}f,

then using (
3.5
) and (
3.3
). Hence, the uniform energy and local energy resolvent bounds have close relation.
We previously alluded to two spectral obstructions to local energy decay (and hence local energy resolvent bounds), which we outline more precisely now.

Elements in the kernel of
P_{\omega}
living in
L^{2}
correspond to finite rank poles
\omega
of
R_{\omega}
. Given that
P_{\omega}
is an elliptic operator, such eigenfunctions live in
H^{s}
for all
s\in\mathbb{R}
, yet the corresponding mode solution to
Pu=0
must possess exponential growth in time since
\omega\in\mathcal{H}
. In particular, the initial energy is finite, yet both terms on the lower-bound side of the local energy decay estimate (
1.1
) are unbounded as
T\rightarrow\infty
. This behavior violates both uniform energy bounds and local energy decay. Since the eigenvalues have negative imaginary part, we will call these
negative eigenfunctions
.

Definition 3.2
.

A negative eigenfunction for
P
is a non-zero
u_{\omega}\in L^{2}
such that
P_{\omega}u_{\omega}=0
, with
\omega\in\mathcal{H}.

The corresponding eigenvalues are isolated and contained within a relatively compact set in
\mathcal{H}
, which follows due to (
3.3
) and the high frequency estimate (
1.5
).

Next, we notice that the local energy resolvent bound must hold uniformly up to the real line in order to obtain local energy decay, per Theorem
3.1
. To that end, we have another potential obstruction - a failure to obtain the continuous extension of
R_{\omega}
to
\mathbb{R}
. First, we consider when one takes the limit as the spectral parameter approaches a non-zero real value.

Definition 3.3
.

An embedded resonant state for
P
is a non-zero
u_{\omega}\in\mathcal{L}\mathcal{E}^{1}_{\omega}
, with
\omega\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
being the corresponding embedded resonance, satisfying the outgoing/Sommerfeld radiation condition

(3.7)

\left\lVert\left\langle x\right\rangle^{-1/2}(\partial_{r}+i\omega)u_{\omega}\right\rVert_{L^{2}(A_{j})}\rightarrow 0\qquad\operatorname{as}j\rightarrow\infty

such that

P_{\omega}u_{\omega}=0.

The outgoing radiation condition as stated here is a variant of the standard one, adapted to the dyadic structure of our spaces. It acts as a boundary condition at infinity to ensure unique solutions to the above problem for a fixed
\omega
. Such states are subtle obstructions, since the corresponding mode solution does not generally have finite energy (as
u_{\omega}
is only guaranteed to live in
\mathcal{L}\mathcal{E}^{1}_{\omega}
).
However, as explained in
[
14
]
, one may perform a truncation procedure and utilize normalized/Regge-Wheeler-type coordinates (see
[
26
]
) to produce functions
\chi_{>1}(t-r)e^{i\omega t}u_{\omega}
whose energy exhibits a growth rate of
t^{1/2}
yet whose image under
P
lives in
L^{1}L^{2}.

Hence, they represent an explicit obstruction to uniform and local energy bounds. The non-existence of embedded resonant states is equivalent to (
3.5
) near the punctured real line and to a limiting absorption principle. In our context, the corresponding result is as follows.

Theorem 3.4
.

Let
P
be a stationary, asymptotically flat damped wave operator which satisfies the geometric control condition and is either weakly
\varepsilon
-damping dominant for some
\varepsilon>0
or
\varepsilon
-weakly magnetic with
\varepsilon\ll_{R_{0},M_{0}}1
. Additionally, assume that
\partial_{t}
is uniformly time-like.
For any
\omega_{0}\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
, the following are equivalent:

(1)

\omega_{0}
is not a resonance.

(2)

The bound

\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{\mathcal{L}\mathcal{E}^{1}_{\omega_{0}}}\lesssim\left\lVert P_{\omega_{0}}u\right\rVert_{\mathcal{L}\mathcal{E}^{*}}

holds for all
u\in\mathcal{L}\mathcal{E}^{1}_{\omega_{0}}
satisfying the outgoing radiation condition (
3.7
).

(3)

The local energy resolvent bound (
3.5
) holds uniformly for
\omega\in\mathcal{H}
near
\omega_{0}
, and the limit

R_{\omega_{0}}f=\lim_{\mathcal{H}\ni\omega\rightarrow\omega_{0}}R_{\omega}f,\qquad f\in\mathcal{L}\mathcal{E}^{*}

converges strongly on compact sets and satisfies the outgoing radiation condition (
3.7
).

We sketch the proof, as it is similar to that given in
[
14
]
for their version of the result (which is Proposition 2.5 in their work); only minor alterations are necessary to account for the damping and the lack of the non-trapping hypothesis. The result is perturbative of the work in
[
10
]
, just as the version in
[
14
]
was perturbative of the case where
P=\Box_{g}
.

Proof.

First, consider when
A,V\equiv 0
and
a\equiv 0
for
|x|>2R_{0}.
In this scenario, we are in the setting of
[
10
]
and obtain full local energy decay. By Theorem
3.1
, we have the local energy resolvent bound (
3.5
), in which case all three statements in Theorem
3.4
hold.

In general, let
\widetilde{P}
denote the principal part of
P
, and consider
\widetilde{Q}=\widetilde{P}+\chi_{<R_{0}}iaD_{t}
. Note that our starting case applies to
\widetilde{Q}
, hence it satisfies local energy decay. Let
\widetilde{R}_{\omega}
denote the resolvent of
\widetilde{Q}_{\omega}
, which is holomorphic in
\mathcal{H}
and continuous up to the real line. We seek a solution to
P_{\omega}u=f
of the form
u=\widetilde{R}_{\omega}g
for some
g
(that is,
u
is in the range of the resolvent and hence outgoing). If
Q_{\omega}=P_{\omega}-\widetilde{Q}_{\omega}
, then

P_{\omega}u=f\qquad\text{ if and only if }\qquad(I+Q_{\omega}\widetilde{R}_{\omega})g=f.

The family of operators
Q_{\omega}\widetilde{R}_{\omega}
is compact from
\mathcal{L}\mathcal{E}^{*}
to
\mathcal{L}\mathcal{E}^{*}
,
holomorphic in the lower-half plane, and continuous up to the real line.
Further,
I+Q_{\omega}\widetilde{R}_{\omega}
is invertible for
-\operatorname{Im}\omega\gg 1
by a Neumann series argument; to see this, note that
Q_{\omega}
is first-order, then use a similar estimate to (
3.6
). Thus,
I+Q_{\omega}\widetilde{R}_{\omega}
is a family of zero-index Fredholm operators. The conclusion of the theorem follows from the analytic Fredholm theorem and its consequences.
∎

Next, we consider when the spectral parameter approaches 0. In this case, we must replace the outgoing radiation condition (which is not meaningful when
\omega=0
) with a new condition which still limits the asymptotics of the functions.

Definition 3.5
.

A zero resonant state for
P
is a non-zero
u\in\mathcal{L}\mathcal{E}_{0}
such that
P_{0}u=0
. If, in addition,
u\in L^{2},
then we call
u
a zero eigenfunction.

Such resonant states are annihilated by
P
while having finite energy. However, they also possess an unbounded
LE^{1}
norm as
T\rightarrow\infty
, which violates local energy decay, as well as two-point local energy decay. As shown in
[
14
]
(see Proposition 2.10 in their work), one also has an analogous characterization of a resonance at zero to that of the non-zero real resonances in Theorem
3.4
. We record their result without proof, for reasons which immediately follow the statement of the theorem.

Theorem 3.6
.

Let
P
be an asymptotically flat damped wave operator satisfying the geometric control condition and
\partial_{t}
be uniformly time-like. Then, the following are equivalent:

(1)

0
is not a resonance.

(2)

The zero resolvent bound

(3.8)

\displaystyle\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{\mathcal{L}\mathcal{E}^{1}}\lesssim\left\lVert P_{0}u\right\rVert_{\mathcal{L}\mathcal{E}^{*}}

holds for all
u\in\mathcal{L}\mathcal{E}^{1}_{0}
.

(3)

The zero non-resonance condition (
2.12
) holds.

(4)

The local energy resolvent bound (
3.5
) holds uniformly for
\omega\in\mathcal{H}
near
0
, and the limit

R_{0}f=\lim_{\mathcal{H}\ni\omega\rightarrow 0}R_{\omega}f,\qquad f\in\mathcal{L}\mathcal{E}^{*}

converges strongly on compact sets.

(5)

The stationary local energy decay estimate

\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{LE^{1}[0,T]}+\left\lVert\partial u\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}L^{2}[0,T]}\lesssim\left\lVert\partial u(0)\right\rVert_{L^{2}}+\left\lVert\partial u(T)\right\rVert_{L^{2}}+\left\lVert\partial_{t}u\right\rVert_{LE[0,T]}+\left\lVert Pu\right\rVert_{LE^{*}+L^{1}L^{2}[0,T]},

with the implicit constant being independent of
T
.

The damping does not arise in
P_{0}
and simply acts as an absorbable lower-order term when transitioning back to
P
, so the added context of this paper has absolutely no effect on the work present in
[
14
]
. In particular, we recover all of the same low frequency work, which is what is utilized for this theorem. Again, we refer the reader to the aforementioned work for more.

Theorem
2.8
gives the implication (3)
\implies(5)
, whereas repeating the proof of Theorem
2.8
on the resolvent side produces (3)
\implies(4).
Next, we remark that the bound present in (
3.8
) is more
directly
related to zero not constituting a resonance than the zero non-resonance condition (
2.12
). However, their equivalence can be seen from the fact that
P_{0}
is an elliptic
AF
perturbation of the Laplacian.

Now that we have outlined the obstructions to local energy decay, we proceed with a proof of Theorem
1.14
. By Theorem
3.1
, it suffices to establish (
3.5
). Once again, we may follow
[
14
]
, as the proof only requires the resolvent theory and frequency estimates.

Proof of Theorem
1.14
.

Since there are no negative eigenfunctions, the resolvent is bounded and holomorphic in
\mathcal{H}
, which implies that

\left\lVert R_{\omega}f\right\rVert_{\dot{H}^{1}_{\omega}}\leq C(\operatorname{Im}\omega)\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{\mathcal{L}\mathcal{E}^{*}},\qquad\omega\in\mathcal{H},

for some constant
C(\operatorname{Im}\omega)
depending on
\operatorname{Im}\omega
which is bounded away from the real line. This is not a uniform bound and a priori may become unbounded as
\operatorname{Im}\omega\rightarrow 0
, but it
does
holds up to any neighborhood of
\mathbb{R}
. Similar to the work in (
3.6
), this implies the local energy resolvent bound (
3.5
) away from any neighborhood of the real line. It remains to establish (
3.5
) within a strip in the lower half-plane sufficiently close to
\mathbb{R}
. We break this strip into three exhaustive cases.

Case I:
|\omega|\gg 1

This is in the high frequency regime, motivating us to use the high frequency estimate (
1.5
), which is applicable by hypothesis. Let
u
solve
P_{\omega}u=f,
and call
v=e^{i\omega t}u.
Then,
v
solves
Pv=g
, where
g=e^{i\omega t}f.
We will apply the high frequency estimate to the interval
[-T,0]
. More precisely, if we call
\tilde{v}(t,x)=v(t-T,x)
, then this solves
P\tilde{v}=\tilde{g},
where
\tilde{g}(t,x)=g(t-T,x).
Applying Theorem
1.10
to
\tilde{v}
provides that

\left\lVert\tilde{v}\right\rVert_{LE^{1}[0,T]}+\left\lVert\partial\tilde{v}\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}L^{2}[0,T]}\lesssim\left\lVert\partial\tilde{v}(0)\right\rVert_{L^{2}}+\left\lVert\left\langle x\right\rangle^{-2}\tilde{v}\right\rVert_{LE[0,T]}+\left\lVert\tilde{g}\right\rVert_{LE^{*}+L^{1}L^{2}[0,T]}.

We immediately calculate that

\displaystyle\left\lVert\tilde{v}\right\rVert_{LE^{1}[0,T]}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{e^{2T\operatorname{Im}\omega}-1}{2\operatorname{Im}\omega}\right)^{1/2}\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{\mathcal{LE}^{1}_{\omega}}

\displaystyle\left\lVert\partial\tilde{v}\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}L^{2}[0,T]}

\displaystyle=\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{\dot{H}^{1}_{\omega}}

\displaystyle\left\lVert\partial\tilde{v}(0)\right\rVert_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle=e^{T\operatorname{Im}\omega}\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{\dot{H}^{1}_{\omega}}

\displaystyle\left\lVert\left\langle x\right\rangle^{-2}\tilde{v}\right\rVert_{LE[0,T]}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{e^{2T\operatorname{Im}\omega}-1}{2\operatorname{Im}\omega}\right)^{1/2}\left\lVert\left\langle x\right\rangle^{-2}u\right\rVert_{\mathcal{LE}}

\displaystyle\left\lVert\tilde{g}\right\rVert_{LE^{*}+L^{1}L^{2}[0,T]}

\displaystyle=\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{\left(\frac{\exp{(2T\imag\omega)}-1}{2\operatorname{Im}\omega}\right)^{-1/2}\mathcal{LE^{*}}+\left(\frac{\exp{(T\imag\omega)}-1}{\operatorname{Im}\omega}\right)^{-1}{L^{2}}}.

Plugging these calculations into the high frequency bound for
\tilde{v}
and taking the limit as
T\rightarrow\infty
yields

\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{\mathcal{LE}^{1}_{\omega}}+|\operatorname{Im}\omega|^{1/2}\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{\dot{H}^{1}_{\omega}}\lesssim\left\lVert\left\langle x\right\rangle^{-2}u\right\rVert_{\mathcal{LE}}+\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{\mathcal{LE}^{*}+|\operatorname{Im}\omega|^{1/2}L^{2}}.

For sufficiently large
\omega
, the first term on the right absorbs into the first term on the left, which implies (
3.5
).

Case II:
|\omega|\ll 1

Since zero is not a resonance, Theorem
3.6
implies that (
3.5
) holds for all
\omega
sufficiently close to 0.

Case III:
|\omega|\approx 1

Since there are no real resonances, Theorem
3.4
implies that (
3.5
) holds for all
\omega
in this region.

Hence, (
3.5
) holds.
∎
