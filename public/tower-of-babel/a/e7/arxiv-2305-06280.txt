Maximum likelihood thresholds of generic linear concentration models
Daniel Irving Bernstein, Steven J. Gortler, Louis Theran

Abstract
The maximum likelihood threshold of a statistical model is the minimum number of datapoints required to fit the model via maximum likelihood estimation. In this paper we determine the maximum likelihood thresholds of generic linear concentration models. This turns out to be the number one would expect from a naive dimension count, which is surprising and nontrivial to prove given that the maximum likelihood threshold is a semi-algebraic concept. We also describe geometrically how a linear concentration model can fail to exhibit this generic behavior and briefly discuss connections to rigidity theory.

Theorem 2.1

Let

(1)

(2)

there exists a matrix

such that

, and

\Omega\in\mathcal{S}^{n}_{+}\cap L

\tr(\Omega S)=\Omega S=0

Proof.

(1)\implies(2)
:
Let

Since the objective function is strictly convex,

Since

(2)\implies(3)

From

Since

and

are PSD, the above implies that the column span
of
K^{-1}
lies in the kernel of
\Omega
. Since

.

(3)\implies(1)

Suppose (3) holds and let

We will show that for any

f(K+\lambda\Omega)\rightarrow\infty\quad{\rm as}\quad\lambda\rightarrow\lambda_{\Omega}.

(B)

It will then follow that

The restriction of

If

3
Generic linear subspaces

In this section we derive the maximum likelihood threshold of a generic linear subspace
L\subseteq\mathcal{S}^{n}
.
A note on our use of geometric and topological language is in order.
Let
V
be a finite-dimensional vector space.
Throughout this paper,
V
will either be
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, equipped with the standard inner product,
or
\mathcal{S}^{n}
, equipped with the trace-norm inner product, i.e. where the inner product of matrices
A
and
B
is defined to be
\tr(AB)
.

The
Zariski topology
on
V
is the topology whose closed sets are varieties.
We use the convention that varieties are the vanishing loci of systems of polynomials.
In particular, they need not be irreducible.
The
Euclidean topology
on
V
is the more familiar topology derived from the metric associated to the inner product on
V
.
The topology induced on the projective space
\mathbb{P}(V)
by the Zariski/Euclidean and the usual quotient map will be referred to by the same name.

The Zariski/Euclidean topology on any subset
X
of
V
or
\mathbb{P}(V)
is the subspace topology on
X
induced by the Zariski/Euclidean topology on
V
or
\mathbb{P}(V)
.
Topological statements that do not explicitly specify a topology should be interpreted in the Euclidean topology.
We will always explicitly say so when we are using the Zariski topology.
For any subset
X
of
V
or
\mathbb{P}(V)
, the Zariski topology is coarser than the Euclidean topology.

Recall that the
interior
of a subset
X
of any topological space is the union of all open sets contained in
X
.
Now let
X\subseteq V
.
The
affine hull
of
X
is the intersection of all affine subspaces (i.e. translations of linear subspaces) containing
X
.
The affine hull of
X
comes equipped with the subspace topology induced by the Euclidean topology on
V
.
The
relative interior
of
X
is the interior of
X
in this subspace topology.
Every convex set has non-empty relative interior.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
X
be an irreducible semi-algebraic set. We say that a statement is

true on

,
or holds at

of

if it is true on a nonempty Zariski-open subset of

Definition

requires

to be irreducible so that

.
The following lemma give a sufficient condition for a semi-algebraic subset of a semi-algebraic
set to be contained in a Zariski-closed subset.

Lemma 3.2

Let

If for all

of

is contained in a Zariski-closed proper

Proof.

First assume

As

[

]
.
Our assumptions imply that

is nowhere dense in

;
the same therefore holds for each

.
Since each

is a nowhere-dense smooth submanifold of

each

Thus

is of lower dimension

Meanwhile, by the results in

Let

Suppose for each

Lemma 3.6

Let

Let

Assume

Proof.

By assumption,

Let

(which is
\geq 2

Let
\Omega
be such a matrix of rank

Let

be the unique face of

Therefore

(Q^{T}\Omega Q)(Q^{T}SQ)=Q^{T}0Q=0.

Since
Q^{T}\Omega Q
and
Q^{T}SQ
are both symmetric, the map

(\Omega,S)\mapsto(Q^{T}\Omega Q,Q^{T}SQ)

on
\mathcal{S}^{n}
restricts to a linear automorphism of
B(n,d)
.
Since
\Omega
and
S
are symmetric, their column spans are orthogonal complements.
Thus if
D
is any diagonal matrix, the map

(\Omega,S)\mapsto(D\Omega D,DSD)

on
\mathcal{S}^{n}
also restricts to a linear automorphism of
B(n,d)
.

Now let
(\Omega,S)\in X(i,j)
.
Since the column-span of
\Omega
is the kernel of
S
and vice versa,
the spectral theorem provides a linear automorphism of
B(n,d)
sending
(\Omega,S)
to

\left(\begin{pmatrix}\Omega^{\prime}&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\
0&S^{\prime}\end{pmatrix}\right)

where
\Omega^{\prime}
and
S^{\prime}
are diagonal and non-singular. By permuting rows and columns and
then scaling symmetrically by multiplying on both sides by an appropriate diagonal
matrix, we find a morphism
\varphi_{\Omega,S}
of
B(n,d)
that sends
(\Omega,S)
the unique point in
X(i,j)

of the form

\left(\begin{pmatrix}A&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\
0&B\end{pmatrix}\right)

where
A
and
B
are diagonal with
\pm 1
diagonal entries in descending order.
Given two points
(\Omega_{1},S_{1})
and
(\Omega_{2},S_{2})
in
B(n,d)
,
we have a linear automorphism
\varphi_{\Omega_{2},S_{2}}^{-1}\circ\varphi_{\Omega_{1},S_{1}}
sending

(\Omega_{1},S_{1})
to
(\Omega_{2},S_{2})
.
Thus the linear automorphism group of
B(n,d)
acts transitively on each
X(i,j)
.
∎

Because
B(n,d)
is smooth, we can analyze it using
basic tools from differential topology which we now introduce.
For a more background, see e.g.
[
15
]
.
Given a smooth manifold
X
and a point
p\in X
,
the tangent space of
X
at
p
will be denoted
T_{p}X
.
Let
Y,Z
be smooth submanifolds of a smooth manifold
X
.
Given
p\in Y\cap Z
, one says that
Y
and
Z

intersect transversally at
p
in
X

if
T_{p}Y+T_{p}Z=T_{p}X
.
If this holds for every
p\in Y\cap Z
then one simply says that
Y
and
Z

intersect transversally in
X
.
When
X
is a vector space, we will identify
T_{p}X
with
X
for all
p\in X
.
In this case, if
Y
and
Z
are Euclidean-open subsets of vector subspaces of
X
,
then
Y
and
Z
intersect transversally if and only if they intersect transversally at a single
p\in Y\cap Z
.
For our purposes, the key utility of transversality is the following lemma.

Lemma 3.11

(
[
15
, p. 30, inter alia]
)
.

Let
X
be a smooth manifold with smooth submanifolds
Y
and
Z

If
Y
and
Z
intersect transversally, then
Y\cap Z
is a smooth submanifold of
X
. Moreover, at any
p\in Y\cap Z
, we have

T_{p}(Y\cap Z)=T_{p}Y\cap T_{p}Z
.

We now define some specialized notation that we will need throughout the rest of this section.
Let
1\leq d\leq n
be integers and fix a linear subspace
L\subseteq\mathcal{S}^{n}
.
Assume that
L
contains a matrix
\Omega_{0}
of rank
n-d

and let

be a matrix of rank

such that

.
The set

consists of all

with the same kernel as

.
In particular, it does not depend on our choice of

Moreover, the Zariski closure of

at any point is

.
Lemma
3.12
below is well-known but we prove it anyway for the sake of clarity.
It implies that the dimension of

is

(n-d)^{2}-\binom{n-d}{2}=\binom{n-d+1}{2}.

Lemma 3.12
.

Let

be a linear subspace of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
of dimension

.
The set

of

The dimension of

What follows is a key technical novelty in this paper:
we explore whether
Z(\Omega)
and
L
intersect transversally.
In Lemma
3.13

we show that this property allows us,
starting at some point
(\Omega_{0},S_{0})\in B(n,d)
,
to perturb
S_{0}
in any direction, and to counter this with an appropriate change to

\Omega_{0}
so that the new pair remains in
B(n,d)
. This is akin to the situation in rigidity theory where,
starting with an infinitesimally rigid framework that has an equilibrium stress,
one can perturb the framework and then appropriately track this
with an updated equilibrium stress. (See
[
11
, Lemma 5.17]
.)
This connection is further developed in Remark
3.22
.
On the other hand, once we have reduced the problem to one of transversality,
it is easy to show (Lemma
3.14
) that this must happen generically once the dimension of
L
is
sufficiently high.

The next lemma show how
transversality leads to this ability
to perturb-and-track within a neighborhood of
B(n,d)
.

Lemma 3.13
.

Let
d,n
be integers such that
1\leq d\leq n
.
Let
L\in\gr(m,\mathcal{S}^{n})
satisfy

1.

L
contains a matrix
\Omega_{0}
of rank
n-d
, and

2.

L
intersects
Z(\Omega_{0})
transversally in
\mathcal{S}^{n}
.

Let
S_{0}\in V(n,d)
satisfy
\Omega_{0}S_{0}=0
.
Then every neighborhood
W
of
S_{0}
in
V(n,d)
satisfies the property that

\tau_{n,d}(W)
contains a neighborhood of
S_{0}
in
V(n,d)
.

Proof.

The assumed transversality implies that we can express
\mathcal{S}^{n}
as the following direct sum

\mathcal{S}^{n}=L\oplus\overline{Z}(\Omega_{0}).

Thus every point in

\mathcal{S}^{n}\times\mathcal{S}^{n}

can be written as

(x,0)+(z,0)+(0,y)

for some
x\in L
,
z\in\overline{Z}(\Omega_{0})
and
y\in\mathcal{S}^{n}
.
Note that

(x,0)+(0,y)\in L\times\mathcal{S}^{n}\qquad{\rm and}\qquad(z,0)\in\overline{Z}(\Omega_{0})\times\{0\}.

Meanwhile,
\overline{Z}(\Omega_{0})\times\{0\}
is the tangent space to
\overline{Z}(\Omega_{0})\times\{S_{0}\}
at
(\Omega_{0},S_{0})
.
Together, this implies that
L\times\mathcal{S}^{n}
intersects
Z(\Omega_{0})\times\{S_{0}\}

transversally in
\mathcal{S}^{n}\times\mathcal{S}^{n}
at
(\Omega_{0},S_{0})
.

By assumption, we have

S_{0}\in V(n,d)
and
\Omega_{0}S_{0}=0
.
So
(\Omega_{0},S_{0})\in B(n,d)
and

Z(\Omega_{0})\times\{S_{0}\}
is a smooth submanifold of
B(n,d)
.
The above implies that
L\times\mathcal{S}^{n}
intersects
B(n,d)

transversally in
\mathcal{S}^{n}\times\mathcal{S}^{n}
.
Lemma
3.11
then implies that
(L\times\mathcal{S}^{n})\cap B(n,d)
is a smooth manifold.

Now let
p\in T_{(\Omega_{0},S_{0})}B(n,d)
.
Viewing
p
as an element of
\mathcal{S}^{n}\times\mathcal{S}^{n}
,
transversality of the intersection of
L\times\mathcal{S}^{n}
and
Z(\Omega_{0})\times\{S_{0}\}
in
\mathcal{S}^{n}\times\mathcal{S}^{n}

implies that there exist
q\in L\times\mathcal{S}^{n}
and
r\in\overline{Z}(\Omega_{0})\times\{0\}
such that
p=q+r
.
Since
Z(\Omega_{0})\times\{S_{0}\}
is a smooth submanifold of
B(n,d)
,

r
lies in
T_{(\Omega_{0},S_{0})}B(n,d)
.
Since
p
does too by assumption,
q
must also lie in
T_{(\Omega_{0},S_{0})}B(n,d)
.
Since
q\in L\times\mathcal{S}^{n}
and

q\in T_{(\Omega_{0},S_{0})}B(n,d)
, then from
Lemma
3.11
,

q\in T_{(\Omega_{0},S_{0})}[(L\times\mathcal{S}^{n})\cap B(n,d)]
.
We have therefore just shown that

(L\times\mathcal{S}^{n})\cap B(n,d)
and
Z(\Omega_{0})\times\{S_{0}\}

intersect transversally as submanifolds of
B(n,d)
at

(\Omega_{0},S_{0})
.

Next, consider the (surjective)
differential of
\tau_{n,d}
at the point
(\Omega_{0},S_{0})\in B(n,d)
:

d_{(\Omega_{0},S_{0})}\tau_{n,d}:T_{(\Omega_{0},S_{0})}B(n,d)\rightarrow T_{S_{0}}V(n,d).

The space
T_{(\Omega_{0},S_{0})}Z(\Omega_{0})\times\{S_{0}\}
is the kernel
of
d_{(\Omega_{0},S_{0})}\tau_{n,d}
.
Meanwhile we have shown that

T_{(\Omega_{0},S_{0})}B(n,d)=T_{(\Omega_{0},S_{0})}((L\times\mathcal{S}^{n})\cap B(n,d))\oplus T_{(\Omega_{0},S_{0})}(Z(\Omega_{0})\times\{S_{0}\})

Thus
the restriction of
\tau_{n,d}
to
(L\times\mathcal{S}^{n})\cap B(n,d)
is a submersion onto
V(n,d)
at
(\Omega_{0},S_{0})

(which is also a smooth manifold).
The image of an open set under a submersion is locally open
[
15
, Page 20]
.
Thus, if
W
is any neighborhood of
(\Omega_{0},S_{0})
in
(L\times\mathcal{S}^{n})\cap B(n,d)
,
then
\tau_{n,d}(W)
contains a neighborhood of
S_{0}
in
V(n,d)
.
∎

The next lemma says that generically,
we will see a transverse intersection.

Lemma 3.14
.

Let
d,m,n
be integers satisfying
1\leq d\leq n
and
m\geq dn-\binom{d}{2}+1
.
Let
E\subseteq\gr(m,\mathcal{S}^{n})
consist of all linear spaces
L
satisfying

1.

L
contains a PSD matrix of rank
n-d
, and

2.

Z(\Omega)
and
L
intersect non-transversally for all PSD
\Omega\in L
of rank
n-d
.

Then
E
lies inside a Zariski-closed proper subset of
\gr(m,\mathcal{S}^{n})
.

Proof.

Let
L\in E
and
\Omega\in L\cap\mathcal{S}^{n}_{+}(n-d)
. Define
k:=\dim(L\cap\overline{Z}(\Omega))
and observe that
k\geq 1
since

\dim(L)+\dim(Z(\Omega))\geq dn-\binom{d}{2}+1+\binom{n-d+1}{2}=\binom{n+1}{2}+1.

Thus we have a basis
\Omega=\Omega_{1},\dots,\Omega_{k}
of
L\cap\overline{Z}(\Omega)
.
Let
\Omega_{k+1},\dots,\Omega_{m}
be such that
\Omega_{1},\dots,\Omega_{m}
is a basis of
L
.
Define
l
to be the expected dimension of the intersection of
L
and
\overline{Z}(\Omega)
, i.e.

l:=\dim(L)+\dim(Z(\Omega))-\binom{n+1}{2}.

By assumption,
L
intersects
Z(\Omega)
non-transversely.
Then
k\geq l+1
and for almost all
\Omega_{l+1}^{\prime},\dots,\Omega_{k}^{\prime}

the intersection of the span of

\Omega_{1},\dots,\Omega_{l},\Omega_{l+1}^{\prime},\dots,\Omega_{k}^{\prime},\Omega_{k+1},\dots,\Omega_{m}

with

is transverse and non-empty, and thus not in
E
.
In particular, choose
\Omega_{l+1}^{\prime},\dots,\Omega_{k}^{\prime}
by perturbing each of
\Omega_{l+1},\dots,\Omega_{k}
an arbitrarily small amount off of

.
Meanwhile,
E
is semi-algebraic by
[
3
, Theorem 1.22]
.
Thus Lemma
3.4
then implies that
E
lies in a Zariski-closed proper subset of
\gr(m,\mathcal{S}^{n})
.
∎

.

Lemma 3.17
.

Let

m\leq nd-\binom{d}{2}

be the set of

s such that

\Lambda(K)
a linear space of dimension

The following lemma characterizes the normal space of
V(n,d)
at
S
in its embedding in
\mathcal{S}^{n}
.

Lemma 3.18
.

Let
S\in V(n,d)
and let
\Omega\in\mathcal{S}^{n}
.
Then
\Omega S=0
if and only if
\tr(\Omega X)=0
for all
X\in T_{S}V(n,d)
.

Proof.

Let
W
denote the smooth submanifold of
\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
consisting of
all

n\times n
matrices of rank
d
.
Given
M\in W
, the tangent space
T_{M}W
consists of all matrices that map the kernel of
M
into its image
[
16
, Example 14.16]
.
Since
S
is symmetric, its kernel is the orthogonal complement of its span.
Therefore if
A\in T_{S}W
and
x,y\in\ker(S)
, the following holds

0=y^{T}Ax=tr(y^{T}Ax)=tr(Axy^{T}).

(C)

The orthogonal complement of a subspace
L
of
\mathcal{S}^{n}
will be denoted by
L^{\perp_{s}}
,
and that of a subspace
L
of
\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
will be denoted
L^{\perp_{n}}
.
We now claim that (
C
) implies the following

(T_{S}W)^{\perp_{n}}=\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{(n-d)^{2}}\alpha_{i}x_{i}y_{i}^{T}:x_{i},y_{i}\in\ker(S)\ {\rm for\ all\ }i\ {\rm and\ }\alpha_{i}\in\mathbb{R}\right\}.

(D)

Indeed, the inclusion
\supseteq
is clear.
To see the
\subseteq
inclusion,
first note that
(T_{S}W)^{\perp_{n}}
has dimension
(n-d)^{2}
.
It now suffices to exhibit
(n-d)^{2}
linearly independent matrices of the form
xy^{T}
for
x,y\in\ker(S)
.
Let
x_{1},\dots,x_{n-d}
be a basis of
\ker(S)
.
The set of matrices of the form
x_{i}x_{j}
for
1\leq i\leq j\leq n
is linearly independent
which can be seen by changing our basis of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
so that
x_{i}
is the
i^{\rm th}
standard unit vector.

Viewing
\mathcal{S}^{n}
as a subspace of
\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
, we claim the following:

(T_{S}V(n,d))^{\perp_{s}}=(T_{S}W)^{\perp_{n}}\cap\mathcal{S}^{n}.

(E)

The inclusion
(T_{S}V(n,d))^{\perp_{s}}\supseteq(T_{S}W)^{\perp_{n}}\cap\mathcal{S}^{n}
is clear.

To get the reverse inclusion, it now suffices to show that

\dim(T_{S}W^{\perp_{n}}\cap\mathcal{S}^{n})\geq\dim((T_{S}V(n,d))^{\perp_{s}})=\binom{n-d+1}{2}.

Let
x_{1},\dots,x_{n-d}
be a basis of
\ker(S)
and consider the following set
\mathcal{B}
of
\binom{n-d+1}{2}
matrices

\mathcal{B}:=\{x_{i}x_{j}^{T}+x_{j}x_{i}^{T}:1\leq i\leq j\leq n\}.

By change of basis we can without loss of generality assume that
x_{i}
is the
i^{\rm th}
standard basis vector.
If
i\neq j
then
x_{i}x_{j}^{T}+x_{j}x_{i}^{T}
is the matrix where every entry is zero aside from
(i,j)
and
(j,i)
which are both
1
.
If
i=j
, then
x_{i}x_{j}^{T}+x_{j}x_{i}^{T}
is the matrix that is all zeros aside from a
2
at the
(i,i)
entry.
Thus
\mathcal{B}
is linearly independent and (
D
) implies
that
\mathcal{B}\subset(T_{S}W)^{\perp_{n}}\cap\mathcal{S}^{n}
.

In light of (
E
), the lemma follows if we prove the
the following:

\{\Omega\in\mathcal{S}^{n}:\Omega S=0\}=(T_{S}W)^{\perp_{n}}\cap\mathcal{S}^{n}

Suppose

\Omega\in(T_{S}W)^{\perp_{n}}\cap\mathcal{S}^{n}
and define
k:=\rank(\Omega)
.
Then
\Omega
has a spectral decomposition
\Omega=U\Lambda U^{T}
where
U
is an
n\times k
matrix with orthonormal columns
and
\Lambda
is a nonsingular diagonal
k\times k
matrix.
From (
D
) it follows that
\Omega S=0
.

Now suppose
\Omega\in\mathcal{S}^{n}
and
\Omega S=0
.
As before, we have a spectral decomposition
\Omega=U\Lambda U^{T}
where
U
has linearly independent columns so
U^{T}S=0
.
Thus the columns of
U
are in the kernel of
S
.
From its spectral decomposition, we see that

\Omega
can be written as a sum of outer products
of vectors in the kernel of
S
as in (
D
).
This implies that
\Omega\in(T_{S}W)^{\perp}
.
∎

Lemma 3.19
.

Lemma 3.20
.

Let

be a linear space.
Suppose that there exists an

such that there is no nonzero

Putting it all together

Theorem 3.23
.

Let

is the minimum

Proof.

For any

, Theorem

implies that the existence of a solution to the optimization problem (

, there exists a Zariski-closed proper subset

Now suppose instead that

implies existence of a Zariski-closed proper subset

Remark 3.24

We can now close the circle of ideas that started with Definition

4
Generic completion rank

Theorem
2.1
is a generalization of certain results about
Gaussian graphical models
,
which are linear concentration models of a particular type.
We will now define such models and discuss how Theorem
2.1
fits into what is known about them.
Each graph
G
on vertex set
\{1,\dots,n\}
with
e
edges
defines a linear subspace
L_{G}
of
\mathcal{S}^{n}
as follows

L_{G}:=\{A\in\mathcal{S}^{n}:A_{ij}=0\ \text{for all non-edges}\ ij\ {\rm of}\ G\}.

The dimension
m
of
L_{G}
is
e+n
.
The corresponding Gaussian model, denoted
\mathcal{M}_{G}
, is called the
Gaussian graphical model
associated to
G
.
To our knowledge, such models were first studied by Dempster in
[
12
]
,
where they proved equivalence of the first two conditions of Theorem
2.1
for Gaussian graphical models.
Uhler found an easily computable upper bound on the maximum likelihood threshold of a given Gaussian graphical model
[
22
]
.
Blekherman and Sinn called this upper bound the
generic completion rank
in
[
7
]
and provided the first known examples where it failed to be sharp.
We now extend the notion of generic completion rank to arbitrary linear covariance models and show that it still gives an upper bound on the maximum likelihood threshold.

Definition 4.1

(
[
7
, Definition 1.1]
)
.

The
generic completion rank
of a linear subspace
L\subseteq\mathcal{S}^{n}
, denoted
\gcr(L)
,
is the minimum
d
such that
\pi_{L}(V(n,d))
is full-dimensional in
L
.

When
L=L_{G}
for some graph
G
, Definition
4.1
agrees the definition of generic completion rank given in
[
7
]
. Below, we prove Theorem
4.3
, which says that generic completion rank upper-bounds the maximum likelihood thresholds, as it does for graphs. Before proving that, we need the following lemma which can be seen as the relaxation of Theorem
2.1
to allow for non-PSD
\Omega
.
It is applicable to generic completion ranks, as opposed to maximum likelihood thresholds.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
L\subseteq\mathcal{S}^{n}
be a linear subspace.
Then
\pi_{L}(V(n,d))
is full-dimensional in
L
if and only if for every regular point
S\in V(n,d)

of the restriction of
\pi_{L}
to
V(n,d)
, if
\Omega\in L
and
\Omega S=0

then
\Omega=0
.

Proof.

First assume that
\pi_{L}(V(n,d))
is full dimensional in
L
and that
S
is a regular point of the restriction of
\pi_{L}
to
V(n,d)
.
Then Sard’s Theorem
[
15
]
implies that
\pi_{L}(T_{S}V(n,d))=L
.
Let
\Omega\in L
satisfy
\Omega S=0
,
let
Y\in L
and let
X\in T_{S}V(n,d)
satisfy
\pi_{L}(X)=Y
.
Lemma
3.18
implies that
\tr(\Omega X)=0
.
Since orthogonal projection in an inner product space is equivariant with respect to the inner product,
this implies that
\tr(\pi_{L}(\Omega)\pi_{L}(X))=0
.
Since
\Omega\in L
and
\pi_{L}(X)=Y
, this gives
\tr(\Omega Y)=0
.
We have thus proven that
\Omega\in L^{\perp}
.
Since
\Omega\in L
as well,
\Omega=0
.

Now assume that for every regular point
S
of the restriction of
\pi_{L}
to
V(n,d)
,
if
\Omega\in L
and
\Omega S=0
then
\Omega=0
.
Let
S
be a regular point of this restriction.
Lemma
3.18
implies that
L\cap(T_{S}V(n,d))^{\perp}=0

and thus
L^{\perp}+T_{S}V(n,d)=\mathcal{S}^{n}
.
Therefore
\pi_{L}(T_{S}V(n,d))=L
.
Then the submersion theorem
[
15
]
implies that

\pi_{L}(V(n,d))
is full-dimensional in
L
.
∎

Theorem 4.3
.

Let
L\subseteq\mathcal{S}^{n}
be a linear subspace.
Then
\gcr(L)
is the minimum

such that for all generic PSD

of rank

,
there does not exist an

.

The first statement follows from Lemma

,
taking the non-generic locus to be the singular points of

.
The second statement now follows from part 3 of Theorem

.
∎

Corollary 4.4
.

The maximum likelihood threshold of a generic linear subspace

is equal to its generic completion rank.

Proof.

Given Theorem
4.3
,
Theorem
3.23
implies that the generic completion rank of a generic

The first connections therein were established by Gross and Sullivant in

The ideas explored in this paper are inspired by such connections and we discuss some of them here.

Let

The Gram matrix of

Let

The pair

Let

row sum vanishes whenever

symmetric matrix

with vanishing row sums and

for every non-edge

of

There is an invertible linear map from our

equilibrium stress matrices.

and

are non-adjacent in

is.

.
This space has dimension

For

to
