A note on the prediction error of principal component regression in high dimensions
Laura Hucker, Martin Wahl

Abstract
We analyze the prediction error of principal component regression (PCR) and prove high probability bounds for the corresponding squared risk conditional on the design. Our first main result shows that PCR performs comparably to the oracle method obtained by replacing empirical principal components by their population counterparts, provided that an effective rank condition holds. On the other hand, if the latter condition is violated, then empirical eigenvalues start to have a significant upward bias, resulting in a self-induced regularization of PCR. Our approach relies on the behavior of empirical eigenvalues, empirical eigenvectors and the excess risk of principal component analysis in high-dimensional regimes.



1
Introduction

Let
(\mathcal{H},\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle)
be a separable Hilbert space and let
(Y,X)
be a pair of random variables satisfying the regression equation

Y=\langle f,X\rangle+\epsilon,

(1.1)

where
f\in\mathcal{H}
,
X
is a random variable with values in
\mathcal{H}
such that
\mathbb{E}X=0
and
\mathbb{E}\|X\|^{2}<\infty
, and
\epsilon
is a real-valued random variable such that
\mathbb{E}(\epsilon\,|\,X)=0
and
\mathbb{E}(\epsilon^{2}\,|\,X)=\sigma^{2}
. We suppose that we observe
n
independent copies
(Y_{1},X_{1}),\dots,(Y_{n},X_{n})
of
(Y,X)
and consider the problem of estimating
f
.

Allowing for general Hilbert spaces
\mathcal{H}
, the statistical model (
1.1
) covers several regression problems. It includes a special case of the functional linear model, in which the responses are scalars and the covariates are curves. This model has been extensively studied in the literature (see, e.g., the monographs by Horváth and Kokoszka
[
12
]
and Hsing and Eubank
[
13
]
). It also includes kernel-based learning problems. For instance, nonparametric regression with random design can be written in the form (
1.1
), provided that the regression function is contained in a reproducing kernel Hilbert space. This connection between statistical learning theory and the theory of linear inverse problems has been developed e.g. in De Vito et al.
[
25
]
.

In this paper, we focus on estimating
f
by principal component regression (PCR). PCR is a widely known estimation procedure, in which principal component analysis (PCA) is applied in a first step to reduce the high dimensionality of the data. Then, in a second step, an estimator of
f
is obtained by regressing the responses on the leading empirical principal components.

Our main goal is to show that the behavior of the prediction error of PCR crucially depends on an effective rank condition. On the one hand, if this condition is satisfied, then PCR performs comparably to the oracle estimator obtained by replacing empirical principal components by their population counterparts. On the other hand, if this condition is violated, then the eigenvalues of the empirical covariance operator start to have a significant upward bias, resulting in a regularization effect of the PCR estimator compared to the corresponding oracle estimator.

A general account on PCR is given in the monograph by Jolliffe
[
15
]
. For a study of the prediction error of PCR with focus on minimax optimal rates of convergence under standard model assumptions, see Hall and Horowitz
[
11
]
and Brunel, Mas, and Roche
[
7
]
for the functional data context and Lu and Pereverzev
[
17
]
and Blanchard and Mücke
[
4
]
for the statistical learning context. Note that the latter study PCR (resp. spectral cut-off) within a larger class of spectral regularization methods. In an overparametrized regime, the
n
th PCR estimator corresponds to the minimum-norm least squares estimator, which can predict well although it interpolates the data, see, e.g., Bartlett et al.
[
1
]
, Tsigler and Bartlett
[
22
]
and Mei, Misiakiewicz, and Montanari
[
18
]
. One explanation of this so-called Benign overfitting phenomenon is given by the fact that the overparametrized component results in a self-induced regularization (see, e.g., Bartlett, Montanari, and Rakhlin
[
2
]
).

In settings where the number of observations is comparable to the dimension, it has been shown that high-dimensional phenomena occur in PCA, ranging from eigenvalue upward bias to eigenvector delocalization. While these phenomena are now well understood in the spiked covariance model (see, e.g., Nadler
[
19
]
, Paul
[
20
]
, Benaych-Georges and Nadakuditi
[
3
]
and Bloemendal et al.
[
5
]
), the results obtained in this paper rely on nonasymptotic extensions to general eigenvalue settings (see, e.g., Reiß and Wahl
[
21
]
, Jirak and Wahl
[
14
]
and Bartlett et al.
[
1
]
).

1.1
Basic notation

Let
\|\cdot\|
denote the norm on
\mathcal{H}
. Given a bounded (resp. Hilbert-Schmidt) operator
A
on
\mathcal{H}
, we denote the operator norm (resp. the Hilbert-Schmidt norm) of
A
by
\|A\|_{\infty}
(resp.
\|A\|_{2}
). Given a trace class operator
A
on
\mathcal{H}
, we denote the trace of
A
by
\tr(A)
. For
g\in\mathcal{H}
, the empirical and the
L^{2}(\mathbb{P}^{X})
norm are defined by
\|g\|_{n}^{2}=n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\langle g,X_{i}\rangle^{2}
and
\|g\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{P}^{X})}^{2}=\mathbb{E}\langle g,X\rangle^{2}
, respectively. For
g,h\in\mathcal{H}
, we denote by
g\otimes h
the rank-one operator on
\mathcal{H}
defined by
(g\otimes h)x=\langle h,x\rangle g
,
x\in\mathcal{H}
. We write
\mathbf{Y}=(Y_{1},\dots,Y_{n})^{T}
and
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}=(\epsilon_{1},\dots,\epsilon_{n})^{T}
. Throughout the paper,
c\in(0,1)
and
C>1
denote constants that may change from line to line (by a numerical value). If no further dependencies are mentioned, then these constants are absolute. For a real-valued random variable
Z
and
\alpha>0
, we define the norm
\|Z\|_{\psi_{\alpha}}=\sup_{p\geqslant 1}p^{-1/\alpha}\mathbb{E}^{1/p}|Z|^{p}
. For
\alpha\geqslant 1
, these norms are equivalent to the Orlicz norms.

2
Main results

In this section, we present our main bounds for the prediction error of principal component regression. All results are proved in Section
4
below.

2.1
Principal component analysis

The covariance operator of
X
is denoted by
\Sigma
. Since
X
is assumed to be centered and strongly square-integrable (meaning that
\mathbb{E}X=0
and
\mathbb{E}\|X\|^{2}<\infty
), the covariance operator
\Sigma
can be defined by
\Sigma=\mathbb{E}(X\otimes X)
, where expectation is taken in the Hilbert space of all Hilbert-Schmidt operators on
\mathcal{H}
. It is well-known that under the above assumptions, the covariance operator
\Sigma
is a positive, self-adjoint trace class operator (see, e.g.,
[
13
, Theorem 7.2.5]
). By the spectral theorem, there exists a sequence
\lambda_{1}\geqslant\lambda_{2}\geqslant\dots>0
of positive eigenvalues (which is either finite or converges to zero) together with an orthonormal system of eigenvectors
u_{1},u_{2},\dots
such that
\Sigma
has the spectral decomposition

\Sigma=\sum_{j\geqslant 1}\lambda_{j}P_{j},\qquad P_{j}=u_{j}\otimes u_{j}.

Without loss of generality we shall assume that the eigenvectors
u_{1},u_{2},\dots
form an orthonormal basis of
\mathcal{H}
such that
\sum_{j\geqslant 1}P_{j}=I
. Moreover, let

\hat{\Sigma}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}\otimes X_{i}

be the empirical covariance operator. Again, there exists a sequence
\hat{\lambda}_{1}\geqslant\hat{\lambda}_{2}\geqslant\dots\geqslant 0
of eigenvalues together with an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors
\hat{u}_{1},\hat{u}_{2},\dots
such that we can write

\hat{\Sigma}=\sum_{j\geqslant 1}\hat{\lambda}_{j}\hat{P}_{j},\qquad\hat{P}_{j}=\hat{u}_{j}\otimes\hat{u}_{j}.

For
d\geqslant 1
, we write

P_{\leqslant d}=\sum_{j\leqslant d}P_{j},\quad P_{>d}=\sum_{k>d}P_{k},\qquad\hat{P}_{\leqslant d}=\sum_{j\leqslant d}\hat{P}_{j},\quad\hat{P}_{>d}=\sum_{k>d}\hat{P}_{k}

for the orthogonal projections onto the linear subspace spanned by the first
d
eigenvectors of
\Sigma
(resp.
\hat{\Sigma}
) and onto its orthogonal complement. Similarly, we use the abbreviations
\Sigma_{>d}
,
\tr_{>d}(\Sigma)
and
\tr_{>d}(\Sigma^{2})
for
\sum_{k>d}\lambda_{k}P_{k}
,
\sum_{k>d}\lambda_{k}
and
\sum_{k>d}\lambda_{k}^{2}
, respectively. Introducing

{\mathcal{P}}_{d}=\{P:{\mathcal{H}}\to{\mathcal{H}}\,|\,P\text{ is orthogonal projection of rank }d\},

the reconstruction error of
P\in{\mathcal{P}}_{d}
is defined by

R(P)=\mathbb{E}\|X-PX\|^{2}.

The idea behind PCA is that
P_{\leqslant d}
satisfies

P_{\leqslant d}\in\argmin_{P\in{\mathcal{P}}_{d}}\limits R(P)\quad\text{and}\quad R(P_{\leqslant d})=\tr_{>d}(\Sigma).

Similarly, the empirical reconstruction error of
P\in{\mathcal{P}}_{d}
is defined by

R_{n}(P)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|X_{i}-PX_{i}\|^{2},

and we have

\hat{P}_{\leqslant d}\in\argmin_{P\in{\mathcal{P}}_{d}}\limits R_{n}(P).

The excess risk of the PCA projector
\hat{P}_{\leqslant d}
is defined by

{\mathcal{E}}^{PCA}_{d}=R(\hat{P}_{\leqslant d})-\min_{P\in{\mathcal{P}}_{d}}R(P)=R(\hat{P}_{\leqslant d})-R(P_{\leqslant d}).

It has been shown in
[
21
, Lemma 2.6]
that, for every
\mu\in\R
, the excess risk of PCA can be represented as

\displaystyle{\mathcal{E}}^{PCA}_{d}={\mathcal{E}}^{PCA}_{\leqslant d}(\mu)+{\mathcal{E}}^{PCA}_{>d}(\mu)

with

{\mathcal{E}}^{PCA}_{\leqslant d}(\mu)=\sum_{j\leqslant d}(\lambda_{j}-\mu)\|P_{j}\hat{P}_{>d}\|_{2}^{2},\quad{\mathcal{E}}^{PCA}_{>d}(\mu)=\sum_{k>d}(\mu-\lambda_{k})\|P_{k}\hat{P}_{\leqslant d}\|_{2}^{2}.

The excess risk is a natural measure of accuracy of the PCA projector
\hat{P}_{\leqslant d}
for many tasks like reconstruction and prediction (see, e.g.,
[
21
]
).

2.2
Principal component regression

Let
\hat{U}_{d}=\operatorname{span}(\hat{u}_{1},\dots,\hat{u}_{d})
be the linear subspace spanned by the first
d
eigenvectors of
\hat{\Sigma}
. Then the PCR estimator in dimension
d
is defined by

\hat{f}_{d}^{PCR}\in\argmin_{g\in\hat{U}_{d}}\limits\|\mathbf{Y}-S_{n}g\|_{n}^{2}

with sampling operator
S_{n}:\mathcal{H}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n},h\mapsto(\langle h,X_{i}\rangle)_{i=1}^{n}
.
The PCR estimator can be defined explicitly by using the singular value decomposition of
S_{n}
. In fact, writing

n^{-1/2}S_{n}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\hat{\lambda}_{j}^{1/2}\hat{v}_{j}\otimes\hat{u}_{j}

with orthonormal basis
\hat{v}_{1},\dots,\hat{v}_{n}
of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with respect to the Euclidean inner product on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, also denoted by
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
in what follows (use that
n^{-1}S_{n}^{*}S_{n}=\hat{\Sigma}
with adjoint operator
S_{n}^{*}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathcal{H},v\mapsto\sum_{i=1}^{n}v_{i}X_{i}
), we have

\hat{f}_{d}^{PCR}=n^{-1/2}\sum_{j\leqslant d}\hat{\lambda}_{j}^{-1/2}\langle\mathbf{Y},\hat{v}_{j}\rangle\hat{u}_{j},

(2.1)

provided that
\hat{\lambda}_{d}>0
. Our main goal is to analyze the squared prediction error of PCR given by

\|\hat{f}_{d}^{PCR}-f\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{P}^{X})}^{2}=\langle\hat{f}_{d}^{PCR}-f,\Sigma(\hat{f}_{d}^{PCR}-f)\rangle.

(2.2)

The following bias-variance decomposition of the mean squared prediction error of PCR conditional on the design is the starting point for our results.

Lemma 1
.

If
\hat{\lambda}_{d}>0
, then we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big(\|\hat{f}_{d}^{PCR}-f\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{P}^{X})}^{2}\,\big|\,X_{1},\dots,X_{n}\big)=B_{d}^{2}(f)+V_{d}

with

\displaystyle B_{d}^{2}(f)=\|\hat{P}_{>d}f\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{P}^{X})}^{2}\quad\text{and}\quad V_{d}=\frac{\sigma^{2}}{n}\sum_{j\leqslant d}\hat{\lambda}_{j}^{-1}\|\hat{u}_{j}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{P}^{X})}^{2}.

2.3
The oracle estimator

Letting
U_{d}=\operatorname{span}(u_{1},\dots,u_{d})
, we define the oracle PCR estimator in dimension
d
by

\hat{f}_{d}^{oracle}\in\argmin_{g\in U_{d}}\limits\|\mathbf{Y}-S_{n}g\|_{n}^{2}.

The oracle PCR estimator has an analogous decomposition as in (
2.1
), with empirical eigenvalues and eigenvectors replaced by those corresponding to the projected observations
P_{\leqslant d}X_{i}
, cf. (
4.2
) below.

Assumption 1
.

Suppose that
X
is sub-Gaussian, meaning that there is a constant
L
such that

\forall g\in\mathcal{H},\quad\|\langle g,X\rangle\|_{\psi_{2}}\leqslant L\mathbb{E}^{1/2}\langle g,X\rangle^{2}.

The oracle PCR estimator satisfies the following standard risk bound, see, e.g.,
[
10
, Chapter 11]
for similar results for the linear least squares estimator in random design or
[
9
]
.

Proposition 1
.

Grant Assumption
1
. Then there are constants
c,C>0
depending only on
L
such that

\forall d\leqslant cn,\quad\mathbb{E}(\|\hat{f}_{d}^{oracle}-f\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{P}^{X})}^{2}\,|\,X_{1},\dots,X_{n})\leqslant C\Big(\sum_{k>d}\lambda_{k}\langle f,u_{k}\rangle^{2}+\frac{\sigma^{2}d}{n}\Big)

with probability at least
1-Ce^{-cn}
.

Remark 1
.

Proposition
1
only states an upper bound, but the proof also provides a corresponding lower bound.

The oracle bias term
\sum_{k>d}\lambda_{k}\langle f,u_{k}\rangle^{2}
is affected by the fact that
U_{d}
does not necessarily have good approximation properties for
f
. In the extreme case, it is equal to
\lambda_{d+1}\|f\|^{2}
.

Corollary 1
.

Grant the assumptions of Proposition
1
.
Then we have

\forall d\leqslant cn,\quad\mathbb{E}(\|\hat{f}_{d}^{oracle}-f\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{P}^{X})}^{2}\,|\,X_{1},\dots,X_{n})\leqslant C\Big(\lambda_{d+1}\|f\|^{2}+\frac{\sigma^{2}d}{n}\Big)

with probability at least
1-Ce^{-cn}
, where
c,C
are the constants in Proposition
1
.

Corollary
1
can be improved under additional source conditions (see, e.g.,
[
17
,
4
]
). Yet, in order to keep this note short, the worst-case scenario in Corollary
1
will serve as a benchmark later on.

2.4
Main result in the classical regime

Our first main result shows that if
d\leqslant cn
and
\tr_{>d}(\Sigma)/\lambda_{d+1}\leqslant cn
, then PCR performs comparably to the oracle estimator from the previous section.

Theorem 1
.

Grant Assumption
1
. Then there are constants
c,C>0
depending only on
L
such that the following holds. If
d\geqslant 1
satisfies

\displaystyle\max\Big(d,\frac{\tr_{>d}(\Sigma)}{\lambda_{d+1}}\Big)\leqslant cn,

(2.3)

then we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big(\|\hat{f}_{d}^{PCR}-f\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{P}^{X})}^{2}\,\big|\,X_{1},\dots,X_{n}\big)

\displaystyle\leqslant C\Big(\lambda_{d+1}\|f\|^{2}+\frac{\sigma^{2}d}{n}\Big)

with probability at least
1-Ce^{-cn}
.

Let us briefly illustrate Theorem
1
for two standard eigenvalue decays from kernel-based learning and functional data analysis.

Example 1

(Exponential decay)
.

Suppose that there are constants
\alpha>0
and
C_{ev}\geqslant 1
such that

\forall j\geqslant 1,\qquad C_{ev}^{-1}e^{-\alpha j}\leqslant\lambda_{j}\leqslant C_{ev}e^{-\alpha j}.

Moreover, suppose that Assumption
1
holds. Then, by Theorem
1
, there exist constants
c,C>0
depending only on
L
,
\alpha
,
C_{ev}
,
\|f\|^{2}
and
\sigma^{2}
such that

\forall d\leqslant cn,\quad\mathbb{E}\big(\|\hat{f}_{d}^{PCR}-f\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{P}^{X})}^{2}\,\big|\,X_{1},\dots,X_{n}\big)\leqslant C\Big(e^{-\alpha d}+\frac{d}{n}\Big)

with probability at least
1-Ce^{-cn}
. Choosing
d
of size
\log(n)/\alpha
, we get the minimax optimal rate
\log(n)/n
(see, e.g.,
[
8
]
).

Example 2

(Polynomial decay)
.

Suppose that there are constants
\alpha>1
and
C_{ev}\geqslant 1
such that

\forall j\geqslant 1,\qquad C_{ev}^{-1}j^{-\alpha}\leqslant\lambda_{j}\leqslant C_{ev}j^{-\alpha}.

Moreover, suppose that Assumption
1
holds. Then, by Theorem
1
, there exist constants
c,C>0
depending only on
L
,
\alpha
,
C_{ev}
,
\|f\|^{2}
and
\sigma^{2}
such that

\displaystyle\forall d\leqslant cn,\quad\mathbb{E}\big(\|\hat{f}_{d}^{PCR}-f\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{P}^{X})}^{2}\,\big|\,X_{1},\dots,X_{n}\big)\leqslant C\Big(d^{-\alpha}+\frac{d}{n}\Big)

with probability at least
1-Ce^{-cn}
. Choosing
d
of size
n^{1/(\alpha+1)}
, we get the minimax optimal rate
n^{-\alpha/(\alpha+1)}
(see, e.g.,
[
11
,
8
]
or
[
4
]
).

2.5
Main result in the high-dimensional regime

In this section, we show that if
d\leqslant n
satisfies
\tr_{>d}(\Sigma)/\lambda_{d+1}\geqslant Bn
for an appropriate constant
B>1
depending only on
L
, then high-dimensional phenomena of PCA result in a regularization effect of the PCR estimator. For our second main result, we introduce the Karhunen-Loève coefficients given by
\eta_{j}=\lambda_{j}^{-1/2}\langle X,u_{j}\rangle
,
j\geqslant 1
.

Theorem 2
.

Grant Assumption
1
and suppose that the Karhunen-Loève coefficients
\eta_{1},\eta_{2},\dots
are independent. Then there are constants
B>1
and
c,C>0
depending only on
L
such that the following holds. Let

\displaystyle j^{*}=\min\Big\{j\geqslant 0\ \Big|\ \frac{\tr_{>j}(\Sigma)}{\lambda_{j+1}}\geqslant Bn\Big\}.

If
j^{*}\leqslant cn
, then, for all
j^{*}\leqslant d\leqslant n
and
1\leqslant t\leqslant n
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big(\|\hat{f}_{d}^{PCR}-f\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{P}^{X})}^{2}\,\big|\,X_{1},\dots,X_{n}\big)

\displaystyle\leqslant C\bigg(\frac{\tr_{>j^{*}}(\Sigma)}{n}\|f\|^{2}+t^{2}\frac{\sigma^{2}j^{*}}{n}+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{\tr_{>j^{*}}(\Sigma)}\sum_{j=j^{*}+1}^{d}\tr(\hat{P}_{j}\Sigma_{>j^{*}})\bigg)

with probability at least
1-Ce^{-ct}
. Moreover, for
d=n
, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{j=j^{*}+1}^{n}\tr(\hat{P}_{j}\Sigma_{>j^{*}})\leqslant Cn\frac{\tr_{>j^{*}}(\Sigma^{2})}{\tr_{>j^{*}}(\Sigma)}

with probability at least
1-Ce^{-cn}
.

Let us illustrate Theorem
2
in two examples. Note that polynomial or exponential decay are both covered by the classical regime in Theorem
1
.

Example 3

(The spiked covariance model)
.

Grant Assumption
1
. Suppose that
\mathcal{H}=\mathbb{R}^{p}
, that the Karhunen-Loève coefficients
\eta_{1},\eta_{2},\dots,\eta_{p}
are independent and that

\displaystyle\lambda_{1}\geqslant\dots\geqslant\lambda_{r}>\lambda_{r+1}=\dots=\lambda_{p}.

We assume that
r\leqslant cn
and
p\geqslant Cn
. Applying the first part of Theorem
2
, there are constants
c,C>0
depending only on
L
and
B
such that the following holds. If
j^{*}=r
,
then, for all
r\leqslant d\leqslant n
and all
1\leqslant t\leqslant n
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big(\|\hat{f}_{d}^{PCR}-f\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{P}^{X})}^{2}\,\big|\,X_{1},\dots,X_{n}\big)\leqslant C\Big(\frac{\tr_{>r}(\Sigma)}{n}\|f\|^{2}+t^{2}\frac{\sigma^{2}r}{n}+\frac{\sigma^{2}(d-r)}{p}\Big)

with probability at least
1-Ce^{-ct}
. To see this, note that

\displaystyle\sum_{j=r+1}^{d}\tr(\hat{P}_{j}\Sigma_{>r})=\lambda_{r+1}\sum_{j=r+1}^{d}\tr(\hat{P}_{j}P_{>r})\leqslant\lambda_{r+1}(d-r).

Hence, in this special case, the variance of the PCR estimator is piecewise linear in
d
and has a change point at
d=r+1
, where the slope decreases from
\sigma^{2}/n
to
\sigma^{2}/p
. This phenomenon is explained by the eigenvalue upward bias presented in Corollary
4
.

Example 4

(Benign overfitting phenomenon)
.

Grant the assumptions of Theorem
2
. Suppose that the Karhunen-Loève coefficients
\eta_{1},\eta_{2},\dots
are independent and that
d=n
. Then Corollary
4
implies that
\hat{\lambda}_{n}>0
with high probability, meaning that
\hat{f}_{n}^{PCR}
coincides with the minimum-norm least squares estimator

\displaystyle\hat{f}_{n}^{PCR}=\argmin_{f\in\mathcal{H}:S_{n}f=\mathbf{Y}}\limits\|f\|,

which interpolates the data:

\displaystyle\hat{f}_{n}^{PCR}=S_{n}^{*}(S_{n}S_{n}^{*})^{-1}\mathbf{Y}\quad\text{such that}\quad S_{n}\hat{f}_{n}^{PCR}=\mathbf{Y}.

By Theorem
2
, we get, for all
1\leqslant t\leqslant n
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big(\|\hat{f}_{n}^{PCR}-f\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{P}^{X})}^{2}\,\big|\,X_{1},\dots,X_{n}\big)

\displaystyle\leqslant C\bigg(\frac{\tr_{>j^{*}}(\Sigma)}{n}\|f\|^{2}+t^{2}\frac{\sigma^{2}j^{*}}{n}+\sigma^{2}n\frac{\tr_{>j^{*}}(\Sigma^{2})}{(\tr_{>j^{*}}(\Sigma))^{2}}\bigg)

with probability at least
1-Ce^{-ct}
. Note that we get the same variance term as in Theorem 1 in
[
1
]
and a similar bias term as in
[
22
]
, provided that the worst-case scenario from Corollary
1
is considered. Note that we prove these results by different arguments based on perturbation bounds for the empirical covariance operator. This provides an alternative explanation of the self-induced regularization property of
\hat{f}_{n}^{PCR}
from
[
2
]
, namely through high-dimensional phenomena such as the eigenvalue upward bias and the eigenvector delocalization.

3
Main tools

In this section, we state perturbation bounds for empirical eigenvalues, empirical eigenvectors and the excess risk of PCA, developed in
[
21
,
9
,
1
]
. These results form the basis of our analysis of the bias and the variance term in Lemma
1
.

3.1
Bounds for empirical eigenvalues

Lemma 2
.

Grant Assumption
1
. Then there are constants
c_{1},c_{2}\in(0,1)
depending only on
L
such that, for all
y>0
and all
j\geqslant 1
satisfying

\frac{1}{y\wedge 1}\sum_{k>j}\frac{\lambda_{k}}{\lambda_{j}-\lambda_{k}+y\lambda_{j}}\leqslant c_{1}n,

we have

\mathbb{P}\big(\hat{\lambda}_{j}-\lambda_{j}>y\lambda_{j}\big)\leqslant e^{1-c_{2}n(y\wedge y^{2})}.

Moreover, for all
y>0
and
j\geqslant 1
satisfying

\frac{1}{y\wedge 1}\sum_{l<j}\frac{\lambda_{l}}{\lambda_{l}-\lambda_{j}+y\lambda_{j}}\leqslant c_{1}n,

we have

\mathbb{P}\big(\hat{\lambda}_{j}-\lambda_{j}<-y\lambda_{j}\big)\leqslant e^{1-c_{2}n(y\wedge y^{2})}.

Proof.

See Theorem 2.15 in
[
21
]
.
∎

Corollary 2
.

If Assumption
1
holds, then we have

\forall j\leqslant c_{1}n/4,\quad\mathbb{P}(\hat{\lambda}_{j}\geqslant\lambda_{j}/2)\geqslant 1-e^{1-c_{2}n/4},

where
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
are the constants in Lemma
2
.

Proof.

Corollary
2
follows from the second claim in Lemma
2
applied with
y=1/2
.
∎

Corollary 3
.

Grant Assumption
1
. Let
j\geqslant 1
be such that

\displaystyle\frac{\operatorname{tr}_{>j-1}(\Sigma)}{\lambda_{j}}\geqslant c_{1}n.

Then, with probability at least
1-e^{1-c_{2}n}
, we have

\displaystyle\hat{\lambda}_{j}\leqslant\frac{2}{c_{1}}\frac{\operatorname{tr}_{>j-1}(\Sigma)}{n},

where
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
are the constants in Lemma
2
.

Proof.

For
y\geqslant 1
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{y\wedge 1}\sum_{k>j}\frac{\lambda_{k}}{\lambda_{j}-\lambda_{k}+y\lambda_{j}}\leqslant\frac{1}{y}\frac{\operatorname{tr}_{>j-1}(\Sigma)}{\lambda_{j}}.

Hence, the first assumption in Lemma
2
is satisfied for

\displaystyle y=\frac{1}{c_{1}n}\frac{\operatorname{tr}_{>j-1}(\Sigma)}{\lambda_{j}}\geqslant 1.

We conclude that

\displaystyle\hat{\lambda}_{j}\leqslant\lambda_{j}+\frac{1}{c_{1}}\frac{\operatorname{tr}_{>j-1}(\Sigma)}{n}\leqslant\frac{2}{c_{1}}\frac{\operatorname{tr}_{>j-1}(\Sigma)}{n}

Lemma 3

Grant Assumption
1

\eta_{1}
,

\displaystyle\hat{\lambda}_{n}\geqslant\frac{1}{2}\frac{\operatorname{tr}_{>n}(\Sigma)}{n}

Set

\hat{\Sigma}=n^{-1}S_{n}^{*}S_{n}

n

K_{n}=n^{-1}S_{n}S_{n}^{*}
and that

\displaystyle\hat{\lambda}_{n}=\lambda_{n}(\hat{\Sigma})=\lambda_{n}(K_{n})\geqslant\lambda_{n}(K_{n}^{\prime}),

where
\lambda_{n}(\cdot)
denotes the

\displaystyle\hat{\lambda}_{n}\geqslant\frac{\operatorname{tr}_{>n}(\Sigma)}{n}-\|K_{n}^{\prime}-\mathbb{E}K_{n}^{\prime}\|_{\infty}.

Note that
(K_{n}^{\prime})_{ii^{\prime}}=n^{-1}\sum_{j>n}\lambda_{j}\eta_{ji}\eta_{ji^{\prime}}

\displaystyle\|K_{n}^{\prime}-\mathbb{E}K_{n}^{\prime}\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leqslant C\frac{\sqrt{\sum_{k>n}\lambda_{k}^{2}}}{\sqrt{n}}+C\lambda_{n+1}

\displaystyle\leqslant C\sqrt{\lambda_{n+1}}\frac{\sqrt{\operatorname{tr}_{>n}(\Sigma)}}{\sqrt{n}}+C\lambda_{n+1}\leqslant\frac{\operatorname{tr}_{>n}(\Sigma)}{4n}+(C^{2}+C)\lambda_{n+1}

1-e^{1-c_{2}n}
. For more details, see the proof of Lemma 4 in
[
1
]

The claim now follows, provided that

.
∎

\sup_{j\geqslant 1}\|\eta_{ji}\|_{\psi_{2}}\leqslant L
for all

. This implies by centering (see, e.g., Remark 5.18 in

is a sequence of real-valued centered random variables such that

with probability at least

.

\displaystyle\tr(\hat{\Sigma}\Sigma_{>j^{*}})\leqslant(1+2C)\tr_{>j^{*}}(\Sigma^{2})

(4.9)

with probability at least
1-e^{-n}
. Combining (

Lemma 8

Appendix A
Concentration inequalities

The following lemma provides two concentration inequalities for (squared) sums of sub-exponential random variables, adapted for our purposes.

Lemma 9
.

Let
(a_{j})
be a sequence of nonnegative real numbers such that
\|a\|_{1}=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}a_{j}<\infty
. Let
(X_{ij})
be a sequence of real-valued centered random variables with
j\geqslant 1
and
1\leqslant i\leqslant n
such that
(X_{ij})_{i=1}^{n}
are i.i.d. for each
j\geqslant 1
and such that
\sup_{j\geqslant 1}\|X_{1j}\|_{\psi_{1}}=L<\infty
. Then there exists a constant
C
depending only on
L
such that, for every
t\geqslant 1
, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{j\geqslant 1}a_{j}\Big(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{ij}\Big)^{2}\leqslant C\|a\|_{1}\frac{t^{2}}{n}

and

\displaystyle\sum_{j\geqslant 1}a_{j}\Big(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{ij}\Big)\leqslant C\|a\|_{1}\Big(\frac{\sqrt{t}}{\sqrt{n}}+\frac{t}{n}\Big)

each with probability at least
1-e^{-t}
.

Proof.

For
p\geqslant 1
, Minkowski’s inequality yields

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}^{1/p}\bigg(\sum_{j\geqslant 1}a_{j}\Big(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{ij}\Big)^{2}\bigg)^{p}\leqslant\sum_{j\geqslant 1}a_{j}\mathbb{E}^{1/p}\Big(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{ij}\Big)^{2p}.

(A.1)

Bernstein’s inequality (see, e.g., Theorem 2.8.1 in
[
24
]
) combined with Theorem 2.3 in
[
6
]
yields

\displaystyle\forall p\geqslant 1,\qquad\mathbb{E}^{1/p}\Big(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{ij}\Big)^{2p}

\displaystyle\leqslant C(p!\,n^{-p}+(2p)!\,n^{-2p})^{1/p}

(A.2)

\displaystyle\leqslant C\Big(\sqrt{\frac{p}{n}}\vee\frac{p}{n}\Big)^{2}\leqslant C\frac{p^{2}}{n}

with a constant
C
depending only on
L
.
Inserting this into (
A.1
), we get

\displaystyle\forall p\geqslant 1,\qquad\mathbb{E}^{1/p}\bigg(\sum_{j\geqslant 1}a_{j}\Big(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{ij}\Big)^{2}\bigg)^{p}\leqslant Cp^{2}\frac{\|a\|_{1}}{n},

meaning that

\displaystyle\bigg\|\sum_{j\geqslant 1}a_{j}\Big(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{ij}\Big)^{2}\bigg\|_{\psi_{1/2}}\leqslant C\frac{\|a\|_{1}}{n}.

(A.3)

Now, for a nonnegative random variable
Y
with
\|Y\|_{\psi_{1/2}}<\infty
, we have
\|Y^{1/2}\|_{\psi_{1}}^{2}\leqslant\|Y\|_{\psi_{1/2}}
, as can be seen from the definition of the
\psi_{\alpha}
norms in Section
1.1
and the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality. In particular, we have
\mathbb{P}(Y\geqslant C\|Y\|_{\psi_{1/2}}t^{2})\leqslant e^{-t}
for every
t\geqslant 1
and an absolute constant
C>0
. Combining this with (
A.3
), the first claim follows.

For the second claim, Minkowski’s inequality and (
A.2
) yield

\displaystyle\forall p\geqslant 1,\qquad\mathbb{E}\bigg(\sum_{j\geqslant 1}a_{j}\Big(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{ij}\Big)\bigg)^{2p}\leqslant C^{p}\|a\|_{1}^{2p}(p!\,n^{-p}+(2p)!\,n^{-2p}).

We obtain by the second part of Theorem 2.3 in
[
6
]
that

\displaystyle\sum_{j\geqslant 1}a_{j}\Big(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{ij}\Big)

\displaystyle\leqslant 3\sqrt{C}\|a\|_{1}\Big(\sqrt{\Big(\frac{1}{n}+\frac{1}{n^{2}}\Big)t}+\frac{t}{n}\Big)

\displaystyle\leqslant 3\sqrt{2C}\|a\|_{1}\Big(\frac{\sqrt{t}}{\sqrt{n}}+\frac{t}{n}\Big)

with probability at least
1-e^{-t}
.
∎
