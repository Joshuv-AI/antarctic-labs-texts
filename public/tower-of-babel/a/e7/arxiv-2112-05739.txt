Heat equations and wavelets on Mumford curves
Patrick Erik Bradley

Abstract
A general class of heat operators over non-archimedean local fields acting on L 2 L_{2} -function spaces on affinoid domains in the local field are developed. L 2 L_{2} -spaces and integral operators invariant under the action of a non-archimedean Schottky group are constructed in order to have nice function spaces and operators on Mumford curves. General wavelets are constructed which, together with functions coming from certain graph Laplacian eigenvectors, diagonalise these operators. The corresponding Cauchy problems for the heat equations with these operators are solved in the affirmative, and properties of wavelet eigenvalues and the spectral gaps of the operators on Mumford curves are studied.



1
Introduction

Heat equations in different flavours are of great interest from various different viewpoints. In an Archimedean setting, they are extensively treated in the literature.
Ever since the forthcoming of the Taibleson-Vladimirov operator, a pseudodifferential operator on a non-archimedean local field
[
14
,
17
]
, there has been active research on heat equations on such a field, in particular the field of
p
-adic numbers, which studies this operator or generalisations of it.
Whereas in the classical case, heat equations are also extensively studied on manifolds, there is according to
[
4
]
no comparable theory of pseudodifferential operators over
p
-adic manifolds, not to say over manifolds defined over a non-archimedean local field. A construction of a certain pseudodifferential operator on a certain
p
-adic manifold was undertaken in
[
2
]
, however the generalised diffusion obtained cannot be considered a heat equation. These operators are invariant under the action of certain finite groups. A study of how
p
-adic pseudodifferential operators transform under group actions on the Bruhat-Tits tree was undertaken in
[
1
]
.
A different approach was pursued in
[
19
]
, where
p
-adic integral operators on closed open (clopen) subsets of
\mathds{Q}_{p}
were constructed which are direct analogues of graph Laplacians. In fact, they can be seen as
p
-adic forms of graph Laplacians by the dictionary developed in
[
3
]
.

A ‘nice’ theory of
p
-adic heat equations is one in which the heat operator is diagonalisable by the
p
-adic Fourier transform
[
18
]
. More general operators can be diagonalised using wavelets on ultrametric spaces
[
9
]
.
Kozyrev’s well-known
p
-adic wavelets were found to diagonalise the Taibleson-Vladimirov operator
[
10
]
.
However, the graph-based heat operators are not entirely diagonalisable by Kozyrev wavelets, one also needs the eigenvectors of the graph Laplacian
[
19
]
. The work of W. Zúñiga-Galindo contains many classes of
p
-adic heat equations, for which the Cauchy problem is solved in the affirmative. This includes those with graph-based operators, like also those in
[
15
]
.

The non-archimedean counterpart of Riemann surfaces are the
Mumford curves
which are projective algebraic curves
defined over non-archimedean fields allowing a Schottky uniformisation
[
11
]
. Locally, they are holed discs inside the base field. That means that if the base field is a non-archimedean local field, then the Haar measure allows integration of functions defined on these local pieces. The question is, how to glue together local operators on overlaps in a meaningful way.
The stable reduction theorem
[
5
]
states that there is a model over a finite extension of the base field such that the special fibre, aka the reduction curve over the residue field, is a singular projective curve whose irreducible components are all rational curves, and the singularities are ordinary double points.
Consequently, the intersection graph of the reduction curve of a Mumford curve has first Betti number equal to the genus of the curve.
The rigid analytic theory of Mumford curves
[
8
]
allows to construct the intersection graph with the help of a covering of the curve by holed discs. This fact, together with the rigid analytic proof of the stable reduction theorem from
[
7
]
gives insight into how to obtain an integral operator on Mumford curves. Namely, these are
graph-based integral operators which generalise both the Taibleson-Vladimirov operator as well as Zúñiga-Galindo’s operators on the set of
K
-rational points of a Mumford curve by taking disjoint covers refining a covering with holed discs, and where each such disjoint piece corresponds to a vertex in a reduction graph of the curve. This is the first contribution of the present article.

The integral operator constructed here has a kernel which is doubly invariant under the Schottky group by summing up twists of an operator defined on the fundamental domain of the action of the Schottky group.
In order to obtain a meaningful space of
L_{2}
-functions on a
Mumford curve, a weighted integral is defined in order to take care of the derivative of each Möbius transformation involved through the integral transformation rule.
A decomposition of the space of of invariant
L_{2}
functions on the universal covering space of a Mumford curve is obtained through first defining a very general class of wavelets which extends Kozyrev’s wavelets to such which are supported on sets which need not be discs.
The
L_{2}
-functions on the fundamental domain (a holed disc) then decompose into eigenfunctions of our graph-based operators which consist of such wavelets and of functions coming from eigenvectors of a graph Laplacian.

The next step is then to construct from these wavelets such which are invariant under the Schottky group and then prove an analoguous structure Theorem for the invariant
L_{2}
-spaces.
After proving that the operator is self-adjoint and bounded, the next result is
the statement that the Cauchy problem for the heat equation for this operator and the invariant operator has a solution which is unique.
The proof follows the methods from
[
19
]
.
Finally, some statements about the wavelet eigenvalues of the heat operators on Mumford curves are proven. In particular, the spectral gap of the new operators
can be arbitrarily small in families of Mumford curves with isomorphic stable reduction graphs. An example is given, where the spectral gap is a wavelet eigenvalue, but
it is an open question, whether the spectral gap can also be a graph Laplacian eigenvalue.

This article is subdivided into six numbered sections, the present one being the introduction.
The following Section 2 fixes some notation. This is followed
in Section 3 by a brief introduction to the rigid analytic theory of Mumford curves
and affinoid domains as far as is needed for the remaining parts.
Section 4 generalises the dictionary developed in
[
3
]

to the more general situation of this article and now contains a correspondence between matrix eigenvalues and operator eigenvalues.
Section 5 develops the wavelets and integral operators
on clopen pieces of the local field and proves the structure theorem for the
L_{2}
-spaces and the Cauchy problem for the heat equation.
Section 6 makes everything from the previous section invariant under the action of the Schottky group and thus obtains a positive answer to the Cauchy problem for the heat equation on Mumford curves.
It concludes with a study of wavelet eigenvalues for Mumford curves in general and Tate elliptic curves in particular.

3
Mumford curves and their reductions

This section is an introduction into the theory of rigid analytic geometry and Mumford curves. Readers familiar with these topics can simply browse through this section in order to pick up necessary notation and viewpoints for the later parts of this article.

3.1
Mumford curves

Mumford curves were first constructed in
[
11
]
as a successful attempt to generalise Tate’s analytic uniformisation of
p
-adic elliptic curves, which can be found in
[
13
]
.
Through this construction, Mumford revealed a one-to-one correspondence between conjugacy classes of Schottky groups in
\PGL_{2}(K)
and a certain class of projective algebraic curves defined over
K
.

In order to present more details of this correspondence, we will give a brief summary of
[
8
, Ch. I, III, IV, V]
, adapted to the setting of a non-archimedean local field.
The slightly over two pages in
[
7
, Ch. 5.4]

also contain a brief overview over Mumford curves.

A Möbius transformation
\gamma\in\PGL_{2}(K)
is called

hyperbolic
, if

\left\lvert\Tr(A_{\gamma})\right\rvert_{K}^{2}>1

where
A_{\gamma}\in\SL_{2}(K)
is the representative of
\gamma
as a special linear matrix.

A discrete subgroup of
\PGL_{2}(K)
which is freely generated by
g\geq 1
hyperbolic transformations is called a
Schottky group
.

A
limit point
of a subgroup
\Gamma
of
\PGL_{2}(K)
is a point
x\in\mathds{P}^{1}(K)
such that there exists a point
x_{0}\in\mathds{P}^{1}
and a sequence
\gamma_{n}\in\Gamma
such that

\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}\gamma_{n}(x_{0})=x

The set of limit points is denoted as
\mathscr{L}(\Gamma)
,
and
\Omega=\mathds{P}^{1}(K)\setminus\mathscr{L}(\Gamma)
is the set of
regular points
of
\Gamma
.
A Schottky group
\Gamma
has the property

\Omega\neq\emptyset

This is an open subset of
\mathds{P}^{1}(K)
.

The
Mumford curve
associated with a Schottky group
\Gamma

with
g\geq 1
generators
is the quotient space

X=\Omega/\Gamma

It is a projective algebraic curve over
K
of genus
g

[
8
, Ch. III]
.

A
rational affinoid domain
is a holed disc in
\mathds{P}^{1}
.
A Mumford curve has a finite covering
\mathcal{U}

by rational affinoid domains
[
8
, Ch. V]
.
In this covering, two overlapping patches
U\in\mathcal{U}
are glued with another along a boundary component (which is a circle).

3.2
Affinoid domains in
K
and their reductions

The
standard Tate algebra

T_{n}
is defined as

T_{n}=K\langle z_{1},\dots,z_{n}\rangle

is the subring of the ring
K[[z_{1},\dots,z_{n}]]
of all formal power series consisting of those power series

, where

The

\left\|f\right\|=\max\left\lvert c_{\alpha}\right\rvert_{K}

One further has

T_{n}^{o}={\left\{f\in T_{n}\colon\left\|f\right\|\leq 1\right\}},\quad T_{n}^{oo}={\left\{f\in T_{n}\colon\left\|f\right\|<1\right\}}

and one has the canonical isomorphism

\bar{T}_{n}=T_{n}^{o}/T_{n}^{oo}\cong k[z_{1},\dots,z_{n}]

There are spaces attached to these objects, namely

X=\Sp(T_{n})={\left\{(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\in(K^{alg})^{n}\colon\max\limits_{i=1}^{n}\left\lvert x_{i}\right\rvert_{K^{alg}}\leq 1\right\}}

and

\Max(\bar{T}_{n})=\mathds{A}^{n}(k^{alg})

there is a natural surjective map

\Red_{X}\colon\Sp(T_{n})\to\Max(\bar{T}_{n})

called

.

An

over

is a

-algebra for which there exists a

-algebra homomorphism

such that

is a finitely generated

-module.
By

7
, Thm. 3.2.1]
, every affinoid algebra

is of the form

for some ideal
I
in the Tate algebra
T_{m}
, and the Gauss norm of
T_{m}
induces a norm on
A
for which it is a Banach algebra over
K
.
One defines the
affinoid space

X=\Sp(A)={\left\{\text{maximal ideals in $A$}\right\}}

In general, if
m\in\Sp(A)
, then the field

is a finite extension of
K
.

We are interested in spaces of the form

X(K)={\left\{m\in X\colon A/m\cong K\right\}}

of
K
-rational points
of

.

A disc

3.3
Mumford curves as rigid analytic spaces

Mumford curves are obtained by glueing finitely many holed discs in such a way that one obtains a rational affinoid covering
\mathcal{U}

which is
pure
in the sense that for all
U,V\in\mathcal{U}
it holds true that

1.

If
U\cap V\neq\emptyset
, then there exists an affine subset
Z\subset\overline{U}
such that

U\cap V=\Red_{U}^{-1}(Z)

2.

If
U\cap V\neq\emptyset
, then the induced map between reductions

\overline{U\cap V}\to\overline{U}

is injective.

Example 3.6
.

This is taken from
[
7
, Ex. 4.8.5]
.
Let
U_{0}={\left\{\left\lvert x\right\rvert_{K}\leq\left\lvert\rho_{0}\right\rvert_{K}\right\}}
and

U_{1}={\left\{\left\lvert\rho_{2}\right\rvert_{K}\leq\left\lvert x\right\rvert_{K}\leq 1\right\}}

with
\rho_{0},\rho_{1}\in K^{\times}
such that

\left\lvert\rho_{1}\right\rvert_{K}\leq\left\lvert\rho_{0}\right\rvert_{K}\leq 1
.
The covering
\mathcal{U}={\left\{U_{0},U_{1}\right\}}
of the unit disc is pure if and only if
\left\lvert\rho_{0}\right\rvert_{K}=\left\lvert\rho_{1}\right\rvert_{K}
.

Given a pure affinoid covering
\mathcal{U}

of a Mumford curve
X
, one obtaines the reduction of
X
with respect to
\mathcal{U}
by glueing the reductions of the patches
U\in\mathcal{U}
along the reductions of their intersections,
and obtains a singular projective curve
\overline{X_{\mathcal{U}}}
over
k
whose irreducible components are all rational curves, and whose singular points are ordinary double points.

Definition 3.7
.

The reduction graph of a Mumford curve
X
with respect to a pure affinoid covering
\mathcal{U}
is the intersection graph of
Y=\overline{X_{\mathcal{U}}}
, i.e. the vertices correspond to the irreducible components of
Y
, and the edges to the double points.

It is a well-known fact that the reduction graph of
a Mumford curve of genus
g\geq 0
has first Betti number equal to
g
.
Its universal covering tree can be embedded as a subtree into
the Bruhat-Tits tree of
K
by
taking a maximal subtree
T
of
G_{\mathcal{U}}
and embedding it into
\mathcal{T}_{K}
. By replacing in
T
all edges with geodetic paths as in Section
3.2
, one obtains a verticial covering of a fundamental domain
\mathscr{F}
for the action of
the fundamental group
\Gamma
of the graph, and by taking
\Gamma
-orbits, a verticial covering of
X(K)
.
The corresponding quotient graph will be denoted as

G_{\mathcal{U}}
where this time
\mathcal{U}
is the unique verticial covering of
X(K)
.

It is a well-known fact that the group
\Gamma
is a free group of rank
g
, and that the construction above yields a faithful representation

\Gamma\to\PGL_{2}(K)

as a discrete subgroup generated by
g
hyperbolic (aka loxodromic) Möbius transformations. More will be said in the Section about heat equations on Mumford curves.

4
A
\pi
-adic dictionary

The aim of this section is to generalise the dictionary of

[
3
, §2]
to the setting of this article.

Let
Z\subset K
be a compact measurable set, and
\mathcal{U}
a
finite covering of
Z
by disjoint sets of finite measure.
The space of continuous complex-valued functions on
Z
will be denoted as
C(Z,\mathds{C}
or simply as
C(Z)
. The space of bounded linear operators on a Banach space
F
is denoted as
\mathcal{B}(F)
.

Let
n=\left\lvert\mathcal{U}\right\rvert
, and let
n\times n
-matrices be indexed by
\mathcal{U}
. We call these
\mathcal{U}
-matrices.
If
A=(A_{UV})
is a
\mathcal{U}
-matrix, then
we define

\left\|A\right\|_{\mathcal{U}}=\sqrt{\sum\limits_{U,V\in\mathcal{U}}\left\lvert A_{UV}\right\rvert^{2}\mu(U)\mu(V)}

this defines a norm on the algebra of
\mathcal{U}
-matrices, called
\mathcal{U}
-norm.

Lemma 4.1

(Dictionary)
.

There is an injective isometric homomorphism between algebras

\displaystyle b\colon\mathds{C}^{n\times n}

\displaystyle\to\mathcal{B}(C(Z,\mathds{C}))

\displaystyle A=(A_{UV})

\displaystyle\mapsto A(x,y)=\sum\limits_{U,V\in\mathcal{U}}A_{UV}\Omega(x\in U)\Omega(y\in V)

where the first space has the
\mathcal{U}
-norm, and the second space the norm

\left\|\mathcal{A}\right\|:=\sqrt{\int_{Z}\int_{Z}\left\lvert A(x,y)\right\rvert^{2}\,dy\,dx}

where
\mathcal{A}\in\mathcal{B}(C(Z))
has kernel
A(x,y)
.

Proof.

First observe that
b(A)
is indeed a bounded linear operator:

\displaystyle\left\|b(A)u\right\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\int_{Z}\left\lvert\int_{Z}A(x,y)u(y)\,dy\right\rvert^{2}\,dx

\displaystyle\leq\sum\limits_{U,V\in\mathcal{U}}\left\lvert A_{UV}\right\rvert^{2}\int_{Z}\Omega(x\in U)\int_{V}\left\lvert u(y)\right\rvert^{2}\,dy\,dx

\displaystyle\leq\sum\limits_{U,V\in\mathcal{U}}\left\lvert A_{UV}\right\rvert^{2}\mu(U)\mu(V)\left\|u\right\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\left\|A\right\|_{\mathcal{U}}^{2}\left\|u\right\|_{2}^{2}

Now, linearity and multiplicativity of
b
are straightforward calculations. In the case of mutliplication, one may consult the corresponding part in the proof of
[
3
, Prop. 2.2]
.

Isometry follows from the observation that

\left\|A\right\|_{\mathcal{U}}^{2}=\int_{Z}\int_{Z}\left\lvert A(x,y)\right\rvert^{2}\,dy\,dx=\left\|\mathcal{A}\right\|^{2}

Thus the assertion is proven.
∎

A
\mathcal{U}
-vector is an
n
-tupel with entries in
\mathcal{C}

by
\mathcal{U}
. If

is an

-vector, then

e(x)=\sum\limits_{V\in\mathcal{U}}\mu(V)^{-1}e_{V}\,\Omega(x\in V)

There is a map

b\colon\mathds{C}^{n}\to C(Z),\;e=(e_{v})\mapsto e(x)

We are convinced that the same letter
b
as in Lemma
4.1

will not cause confusion.

Now, there is a product defining an action of

\mathcal{B}(C(Z))
on
C(Z)
. Namely,

u\mapsto\mathcal{A}u(x)=\int_{Z}A(x,y)u(y)\,dy

We will write this in the usual way as
\mathcal{A}u
.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
A=(A_{UV})
be a
\mathcal{U}
-matrix, and
e=(e_{V})
a
\mathcal{U}
-vector. Then

b(A)b(e)=b(Ae)

Proof.

We have

Ae=c=(c_{U})

with

c_{U}=\sum\limits_{V\in\mathcal{U}}A_{UV}e_{V}

Hence,
b(c)
is the function

c(x)=\sum\limits_{U\in\mathcal{U}}\sum\limits_{V\in\mathcal{U}}A_{UV}e_{V}\Omega(x\in U)

On the other hand,
b(A)b(e)
is the function

\displaystyle\int_{Z}A(x,y)e(y)\,dy

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{U,V\in\mathcal{U}}A_{UV}\mu(V)^{-1}e_{V}\int_{V}\Omega(y\in V)\,dy\,\Omega(x\in U)

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{U,V\in\mathcal{U}}A_{UV}e_{V}\Omega(x\in U)=b(Ae)

This proves the assertion.
∎

Corollary 4.3
.

If
e=(e_{V})
is an eigenvector of
\mathcal{U}
-matrix
A
with eigenvalue
\lambda\in\mathds{C}
, then

b(e)
is an eigenfunction of
b(A)
for the same eigenvalue.

Proof.

We have

b(A)b(e)=b(Ae)=b(\lambda e)=\lambda b(e)

where the first equality is due to Lemma
4.2
.
∎

5
Heat equations associated with affinoid coverings

In the first subsection, we generalise the Kozyrev wavelets
[
10
]

not only to
K
, but to have supports which need not be balls.
These turn out to be eigenfunctions of integral operators
constructed with the help of coverings of a compact clopen,
and symmetric graph adjacency matrices.
In the second subsection, we show that these integral operators generate Feller semigroups, and
solve the Cauchy problem for the corresponding heat equation
on compact clopen sets.

5.1
K
-Wavelets

Let
Z
be a measurable subset of
K
, and let

\Cov(Z)
be a Grothendieck topology on
X

consisting of coverings of
Z
by mutually disjoint subsets.
The elements of
\Cov(Z)
are called admissible
coverings of
Z
.
\Cov(Z)
is a partially order set with refinement
\leq
as the partial order, where we denote

\mathcal{V}\leq\mathcal{U}

to mean that
\mathcal{V}
is a refinement of
\mathcal{U}
.
A finite chain in
\Cov(Z)
is a sequence

\mathcal{U}_{n}<\dots<\mathcal{U}_{i}<\mathcal{U}_{i-1}<\dots<\mathcal{U}_{0}

of coverings of
Z
. An infinite chain in
\Cov(Z)
may or may not be bounded from above or below.
A chain is
maximal
, if it cannot be made longer by inserting or appending new coverings to it.

We say that a refinement
\mathcal{V}<\mathcal{U}
is
strong
, if

\mathcal{U}\cap\mathcal{V}=\emptyset

A
strong maximal chain
is a chain in which each refinement is strong, and which cannot be strongly refined.

In the following, we fix a lift

\tau\colon k\to O_{K}

of the residue field
k
into a set of representatives modulo the uniformiser
\pi
.

In the following, we assume that

Z
is compact open, and
our Grothendieck topology consists of coverings of
Z
by rational affinoid sets (i.e. holed discs).

Definition 5.1
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a strong maximal chain of coverings in
\Cov(Z)
.
A
\mathcal{C}
-wavelet
on
Z
is a family of functions

\psi_{j,U}^{\mathcal{C}}=\mu(U)^{\frac{1}{2}}\chi_{K}(p^{\frac{d-1}{e}}\tau(j)x)\Omega(x\in U)

with
U\in\mathcal{U}\in\mathcal{C}
,
j\in k
,
and
p^{-\frac{d}{e}}
the radius of the smallest hole in
U
.

Example 5.2
.

Let
Z=\mathds{Q}_{p}
, and
\mathcal{C}
the strong maximal chain of coverings in which each covering is given by the translates of
a fixed ball in
\mathds{Z}_{p}
. Then the
\mathcal{C}
-wavelets on
\mathds{Q}_{p}
are the usual Kozyrev wavelets, if the
p
-adic lift
\tau
from
\mathds{F}_{p}
takes each element to its smallest natural counterpart.

Lemma 5.3
.

Let
\psi_{j,U}^{\mathcal{C}}
be a
\mathcal{C}
-wavelet.
Then

\int_{Z}\psi_{j,U}^{\mathcal{C}}(x)\,dx=0

Proof.

This follows from the fact that

\int_{U}\chi_{K}(ax)\,dx=0

for
\left\lvert a\right\rvert_{K}
sufficiently large.
∎

Lemma 5.4
.

Let
\psi_{j,W}^{\mathcal{C}}
be a
\mathcal{C}
-wavelet.
Then

\int_{W}\left\lvert x-y\right\rvert_{K}^{\alpha}\psi_{j,W}^{\mathcal{C}}(y)\,dy=0

for
x\in Z
.

Proof.

If for
x\in Z
fixed,
\left\lvert x-y\right\rvert_{K}
is constant for
y\in W
, then
the assertion follows from Lemma
5.3
.
In the alternative case,
we have

\int_{W}\left\lvert x-y\right\rvert_{K}^{\alpha}\psi_{j,W}^{\mathcal{C}}(y)\,dy=\sum\limits_{\nu\in\mathds{Z}}p^{\nu}\int_{W\cap S_{\nu}}\psi_{j,W}^{\mathcal{C}}(y)\,dy=0

where
S_{\nu}
is the sphere centered in Zero with radius

.
The integral vanishes again by an argument similar to that in the proof of Lemma
5.3
.
∎

Definition 5.5
.

Let
\mathcal{U}\in\Cov(Z)
, and let

A=(A_{UV})

be a complex-valued matrix indexed by
U,V\in\mathcal{U}
, called a

\mathcal{U}
-matrix
. The

Zúñiga operator
associated with
A
and
\mathcal{U}
is
the
\pi
-adic Laplace operator
\mathcal{D}_{A,\mathcal{U}}^{\alpha}

on

given as

\mathcal{D}_{A,\mathcal{U}}^{\alpha}f(x)=\int_{Z}A_{\mathcal{U}}^{\alpha}(x,y)(f(y)-f(x))\,dy

associated with the kernel

A^{\alpha}_{\mathcal{U}}(x,y)=\sum\limits_{U,V\in\mathcal{U}}A_{UV}\left\lvert x-y\right\rvert_{K}^{\alpha}\Omega(x\in U)\Omega(y\in V)

with
\alpha\in\mathds{C}
.

For
Z=K
and
A=\mathds{1}
having all entries equal to

, this operator distinguishes only notationally form the well-known Vladimirov-Taibleson operator.

We assume in the following that

\mathcal{U}\in\Cov(Z)
is a fixed covering consisting of
intersections of spheres of equal radius. Such a covering is called
verticial
. And we assume that
A_{UU}=0
.

Thus, we define the
degree
of
U\in\mathcal{U}\in\mathcal{C}
as

\deg^{Z}_{A,\alpha}(U)=\sum\limits_{V\in\mathcal{U}}A_{UV}\int_{V}\left\lvert x-y\right\rvert^{\alpha}\,dy

with
x\in U
.
Notice that this does not depend on
x\in U
:

Lemma 5.6
.

Let
\mathcal{U}
be a verticial covering of
Z
, and

U\neq V\in\mathcal{U}
. Then

\left\lvert x-y\right\rvert_{K}=\text{const.}

on
U\times V
.

Proof.

In
U,V
we have equations

\left\lvert x-u\right\rvert_{K}=r,\quad\left\lvert y-v\right\rvert_{K}=s

with
u\in U
,
v\in V
, respectively, then we have

r,s<\left\lvert u-v\right\rvert_{K}

So, we have

\left\lvert x-y\right\rvert_{K}=\left\lvert x-u+u-y\right\rvert_{K}=\left\lvert u-y\right\rvert_{K}=\left\lvert u-v+v-y\right\rvert_{K}=\left\lvert u-v\right\rvert_{K}

which is constant.
∎

Let

C_{UV}^{\alpha}=\left\lvert x-y\right\rvert_{K}^{\alpha}

for
x,y\in U\times V
, if
U\neq V
, and

C_{UU}=0

for
U,V\in\mathcal{U}
, and
\mathcal{U}
a verticial covering of
Z
.

The following Lemma and Corollary are in line with

[
19
, Lem. 10.1]
.

Lemma 5.7
.

Assume that
A=(A_{UV})
is a matrix with non-negative real entries and

A_{UU}=0
, and that
\alpha\in\mathds{R}
.
Then it holds true that

\left\|\mathcal{D}_{A,\mathcal{U}}^{\alpha}f\right\|_{2}\leq 2\sqrt{\sum\limits_{U\in\mathcal{U}}\deg_{A,\alpha}^{Z}(U)^{2}\mu(U)}\cdot\left\|f\right\|_{2}

for
f\in\mathcal{D}(Z)
.

Proof.

We have

\left\|\mathcal{D}_{A,\mathcal{U}}^{\alpha}f\right\|_{2}\leq\left\|\int_{Z}A_{\mathcal{U}}^{\alpha}(x,y)f(y)\,dy\right\|_{2}+\left\|\int_{Z}A_{\mathcal{U}}^{\alpha}(x,y)\,dy\,f(x)\right\|_{2}=:I_{1}+I_{2}

and

\displaystyle I_{1}^{2}

\displaystyle=\int_{Z}\left\lvert\int_{Z}A_{\mathcal{U}}^{\alpha}(x,y)f(y)\,dy\right\rvert^{2}\,dx

\displaystyle\leq\int_{Z}\left(\int_{Z}\left\lvert A_{\mathcal{U}}^{\alpha}(x,y)\right\rvert\left\lvert f(y)\right\rvert\,dy\right)^{2}\,dx

\displaystyle=\int_{Z}\left(\sum\limits_{U,V\in\mathcal{U}}A_{UV}\int_{V}\left\lvert x-y\right\rvert_{K}^{\alpha}\left\lvert f(y)\right\rvert\,dy\Omega(x\in U)\right)^{2}\,dx

\displaystyle\leq\int_{Z}\left(\left\|f\right\|_{2}\sum\limits_{U,V\in\mathcal{U}}A_{UV}C_{UV}^{\alpha}\mu(V)\Omega(x\in U)\right)^{2}\,dx

\displaystyle=\left\|f\right\|_{2}^{2}\sum\limits_{U\in\mathcal{U}}\deg_{A,\alpha}^{Z}(U)^{2}\int_{Z}\Omega(x\in U)\,dx

\displaystyle=\left\|f\right\|_{2}^{2}\sum\limits_{U\in\mathcal{U}}\deg_{A,\alpha}^{Z}(U)^{2}\mu(U)

as well as

\displaystyle I_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\int_{Z}\left\lvert f(x)\right\rvert^{2}\left(\int_{Z}A_{\mathcal{U}}^{\alpha}(x,y)\,dy\right)^{2}\,dx

\displaystyle=\int_{Z}\left\lvert f(x)\right\rvert^{2}\left(\sum\limits_{U\in\mathcal{U}}\deg_{A,\alpha}^{Z}(U)\Omega(x\in U)\right)^{2}\,dx

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{U\in\mathcal{U}}\deg_{A,\alpha}^{Z}(U)^{2}\int_{Z}\left\lvert f(x)\right\rvert^{2}\Omega(x\in U)\,dx

\displaystyle\leq\left\|f\right\|_{2}^{2}\sum\limits_{U\in\mathcal{U}}\deg_{A,\alpha}^{Z}(U)^{2}\mu(U)

From this, the assertion follows.
∎

Corollary 5.8
.

Under the assumptions of Lemma
5.7
,
it holds true that
\mathcal{D}_{A,\mathcal{U}}^{\alpha}
is a bounded linear operator on

Proof.

According to Lemma
5.7

\mathcal{D}_{A,\mathcal{U}}^{\alpha}
is bounded on the space of test functions. As the Schwartz space
\mathcal{D}(Z)
is dense in

\mathcal{D}_{A,\mathcal{U}}^{\alpha}
is bounded on

.
∎

Lemma 5.9
.

If
A
is symmetric with
A_{UU}=0
, and
\alpha\in\mathds{R}
, then
\mathcal{D}_{A,\mathcal{U}}^{\alpha}
is self-adjoint.

A simple calculation shows that

From this, it follows that
\mathcal{D}_{A,\mathcal{U}}^{\alpha}
, whose kernel is

A_{\mathcal{U}}^{\alpha}(x,y)-\sum\limits_{U,V\in\mathcal{U}}\deg_{A,\alpha}^{Z}(U)\mu(U)\delta(x-y)\Omega(x\in U)\Omega(x\in V)

5.2
Cauchy problem for the heat equation on
Z

Let
Z\subset K
be a compact clopen set, and
\mathcal{U}
a verticial covering of
Z
by rational affinoids.

Following

[
6
, Ch. 4.2]
, we consider the Banach space

C(Z)
of continuous real-valued functions on

w.r.t. the
L_{\infty}
-norm
\left\|\cdot\right\|_{\infty}
.

An operator is said to be
positive
, if it maps non-negative functions to non-negative functions.

A semigroup
T(t)
on
C(Z)
is
positive
,
if for each
t\geq 0
the operator
T(t)
is positive.

An operator on
C(Z)
is said to satisfy the
positive maximum principle
, if for all

f\in\dom(A)
,
x_{0}\in\Omega
, we have

\sup\limits_{x\in\Omega}f(x)=f(x_{0})\geq 0\quad\Rightarrow\quad Af(x_{0})\leq 0

Theorem 5.16

(Yosida-Hille)
.

The closure
\bar{\mathcal{A}}
of a linear operator
\mathcal{A}
on
C(Z)

with doubly invariant kernel
is single-valued and generates a strongly continuous positive, contraction semigroup (aka
Feller semigroup
)
T(t)
on
C(Z)
, if and only if

1.

\dom(\mathcal{A})
is dense in
C(Z)
.

2.

\mathcal{A}
satisfies the positive maximum principle.

3.

The resolvent set
\Res(\lambda-\mathcal{A})
is dense in
C(Z)
for some
\lambda>0
.

Proof.

Cf.
[
6
, Thm. 4.2.2]
.
∎

We take the following definition from
[
19
]
in our situation:

Definition 5.17
.

A family of bounded linear operators
P_{t}\colon C(Z)\to C(Z)
is called a
Feller semigroup
,
if

1.

P_{s+t}=P_{s}P_{t}
,
P_{0}=I
(semigroup).

2.

for any

6.1
Invariant wavelets over

Let

That is why we define a weighted integral as follows:
Let

\Gamma

\displaystyle\langle f_{\Gamma},g_{\Gamma}\rangle_{s}

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{\gamma\in\Gamma}\sum\limits_{U\in\mathcal{U}}m_{\gamma,U}^{-s}\int_{\gamma U}f_{U}(\gamma^{-1}x)\overline{g_{U}(\gamma^{-1}y)}\,dx

Definition 6.1

We have the space

$\left\|f\right\|_{s}<\infty$}\right\}}

The same space is also defined as the space

.

Let

be the vector subspace of

\Gamma
-invariant continuous functions.

Lemma 6.2
.

There are only finitely many

\left\lvert\delta^{\prime}z\right\rvert

Proof.

Observe that

The map

Lemma 6.3
.

The vectorspace

Proof.

Let

be a Cauchy sequence in

As for functions

So, let

be the limit of

Lemma 6.4

. Hence

is a closed linear operator.

We again go through the steps in the proof of

.

The conditions 1. and 2. of Theorem

are straightforward. Condition 3. means that there exists

In the proof of Theorem

, we have seen that

is convergent for

.
Hence,

This means that

Now, rewrite equation (

It follows that the closed operator

∎

We assume now that
\mathcal{U}

Further, assume that

\mathcal{U}(K)

The operator

Proof.

This follows in the same manner as in the end of Section

, by observing that

\textstyle{0\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{c(G_{\mathcal{U}},\mathds{C})\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{A(G_{\mathcal{U}},\mathds{C})\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}

\textstyle{\mathds{C}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}

Remark 6.14
.

The exact sequence (
6.3
) appears in the context of Mumford curves in

[
16
]
, where Theta functions for discontinuous groups are studied.

The decomposition of
L^{2}(\Omega)^{\Gamma}
as in

6.13
together with
Theorem
6.7
applied to an adjacency matrix
A
of the reduction graph
G_{\mathcal{U}}
of a Mumford curve

shows that the non-zero spectrum of
\mathcal{D}_{A,\Gamma}^{\alpha}

consists of the negative eigenvalues of the Laplacian associated with
A

and the quantities

-\deg_{A,\Gamma}^{\alpha}(U)

for

. We will now prove that the spectral gap of
\mathcal{D}_{A,\Gamma}^{\alpha}
can become arbitrarily small if

varies over all Mumford curves having the same combinatorial reduction graph.

Assumption 1
.

In order to eliminate the dependence of the spectrum
on the volume of the fundamental domain
\mathscr{F}
, we assume that for a given Mumford curve
X
, it is a holed disc inside
O_{K}
with maximal possible volume under such fundamental domains.

Lemma 6.15
.

Let
X=\Omega/\Gamma
be a Mumford curve of genus
g\geq 1

with fixed reduction graph
G
which is assumed to be simple.
Let
\alpha\in\mathds{R}
, and
s>1
. Let
A=(A_{UV})
be the combinatorial adjacency matrix of
G
.
Consider the sequence

p^{\frac{\beta_{\gamma,V}}{e}}=\max\limits_{V}\left\lvert\gamma^{\prime}\right\rvert_{K},\quad\gamma\in\Gamma

in its natural ordering, and let
\lambda_{V}\geq 1
be the smallest gap within the sequence
\beta_{\gamma,V}\in\mathds{Z}
. Then,
it holds true that

\deg_{A,\Gamma}^{\alpha}(U)\leq\deg_{G}(U)\max\limits_{A_{UV}\neq 0}{\left\{\mu(V)d(U,V)^{\alpha}\right\}}C(s,\lambda_{V})

with

C(s,\lambda_{V})=\left(\frac{1}{1-p^{-\frac{\lambda_{V}}{e}}}+\frac{p^{-\frac{\lambda_{V}(s-1)}{e}}}{1-p^{-\frac{\lambda_{V}(s-1)}{e}}}\right)

for
U\in\mathcal{U}
.

Proof.

We will give an upper bound for
\deg_{A,\Gamma}^{\alpha}(U)

for
U\in\mathcal{U}
as follows, where
d_{K}(U,V)
denotes the
\pi
-adic distance between the sets
U
and
V
:

\displaystyle\deg_{A,\Gamma}^{\alpha}(U)

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{V\in\mathcal{U}}A_{UV}\sum\limits_{\gamma\in\Gamma}\int_{\gamma V,s}\left\lvert x-\gamma^{-1}y\right\rvert_{K}^{\alpha}\,dy

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{V\in\mathcal{U}}A_{UV}\sum\limits_{\gamma\in\Gamma}m_{\gamma,V}^{-s}\int_{V}\left\lvert x-y\right\rvert_{K}^{\alpha}\left\lvert\gamma^{\prime}y\right\rvert_{K}\,dy

\displaystyle\leq\sum\limits_{V\in\mathcal{U}}A_{UV}\mu(V)d_{K}(U,V)^{\alpha}\sum\limits_{\gamma\in\Gamma}\frac{\max\limits_{V}\left\lvert\gamma^{\prime}\right\rvert_{K}}{\max{\left\{1,\max\limits_{V}\left\lvert\gamma^{\prime}\right\rvert_{K}\right\}}^{s}}

Now, in

S_{V}(s)=\sum\limits_{\gamma\in\Gamma}\frac{\max\limits_{V}\left\lvert\gamma^{\prime}\right\rvert_{K}}{\max{\left\{1,\max\limits_{V}\left\lvert\gamma^{\prime}\right\rvert_{K}\right\}}^{s}}

each numerator is a power of
p^{f}
, and the denominator is a power of
p^{sf}
, so each summand can be written like this:

\begin{cases}p^{-\frac{\nu_{V,n}}{e}},&m_{\gamma,V}=1\\
p^{-\frac{\nu_{V,n}(s-1)}{e}},&m_{\gamma,V}>1\end{cases}

and
\nu_{V,n}
is an incresasing sequence of natural numbers
with
\nu_{V,0}=0
.
Hence,

S_{V}(s)=\sum\limits_{n\geq 0}p^{-\frac{\nu_{V,n}}{e}}+\sum\limits_{n>0}p^{-\frac{\nu_{V,n}(s-1)}{e}}

If
\lambda_{V}
is the minimal gap in the sequence

\nu_{V,n}=\beta_{\gamma,V}
for suitable
\gamma\in\Gamma
, then we obtain

\displaystyle S_{V}(s)

\displaystyle\leq\sum\limits_{n\geq 0}p^{-\frac{n\lambda_{V}}{e}}+\sum\limits_{n>0}p^{-\frac{n\lambda_{V}(s-1)}{e}}=C(s,\lambda_{V})

Hence, we obtain

\displaystyle\deg_{A,\Gamma}^{\alpha}(U)\leq\deg_{G}(U)\max\limits_{A_{UV}\neq 0}{\left\{\mu(V)d(U,V)^{\alpha}\right\}}C(s,\lambda_{V})

as asserted.
∎

A graph is called

if every vertex not attached to a loop-edge
is attached to at least three edges.
By the stable reduction Theorem
[
5
]
, among the possible intersection graphs of the special fibre of a given projective algebraic curve, the stable intersection graph is unique.

Let

,

, and let

.

is any curve from

for some

and a verticial covering
\mathcal{U}
of

.

Proof.

By Lemma
6.15
, there is an upper bound of

\deg_{A,\Gamma}^{\alpha}
which is a multiple of

\Lambda_{X}(U)=\max\limits_{A_{UV}\neq 0}{\left\{\mu(V)d(U,V)^{\alpha}\right\}}

6.4
Degree eigenvalues of canonical heat operators on Tate curves

Here, we will exhibit an explicit calculation of

\deg_{A,q}^{\alpha}(U)=\deg_{A,\Gamma}^{\alpha}(U)

under Assumption
1
for Tate curves.
Let

X=K^{\times}/\langle q\rangle
with
\left\lvert q\right\rvert=p^{-\frac{n+1}{e}}

be a Tate curve. Let
\mathcal{U}
be the covering of the fundamental domain, which is an annulus, as follows:

\mathcal{U}={\left\{U_{0},\dots,U_{n}\right\}}

U_{i}={\left\{\left\lvert x\right\rvert_{K}=p^{-\frac{i}{e}}\right\}},\quad i\equiv 0,\dots,n+1

Theorem 6.17
.

It holds true that

\deg_{A,q}^{\alpha}(U_{i})=C_{\alpha}(i)\cdot S_{q}(s)

with

C_{\alpha}(i)=\begin{cases}\left(p^{-i\alpha}p^{-(i+1)f)}+p^{-(i-1)\alpha}p^{-(i-2)f}\right)\left(1-p^{-f}\right),&i=1,\dots,n-1\\
\left(1+p^{-(n-1)\alpha}p^{-(n-2)f}\right)\left(1-p^{-f}\right),&i=n\\
\left(1+p^{-(n-1)f}\right)\left(1-p^{-f}\right),&i=0\end{cases}

and

S_{q}(s)=\frac{1}{1-\left\lvert q\right\rvert}+\frac{\left\lvert q\right\rvert^{s-1}}{1-\left\lvert q\right\rvert^{s-1}}

where
\alpha\in\mathds{R}
and
s>1
.

By Theorem
6.7
, this gives an explicit formula for the eigenvalues corresponding to the wavelet eigenfunctions of
\mathcal{D}_{A,\Gamma}^{\alpha}
, which are the negatives of those degrees.

Proof.

We have, where natural indices are read modulo
n+1
,

\displaystyle\deg_{A,q}^{\alpha}(U_{i})

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{n\in\mathds{Z}}\int_{q^{n}U_{i+1},s}\left\lvert x-q^{-n}y\right\rvert_{K}^{\alpha}\,dy+\int_{q^{n}U_{i-1},s}\left\lvert x-q^{-n}y\right\rvert_{K}^{\alpha}\,dy

\displaystyle=\left(\mu(U_{i+1})d(U_{i},U_{i+1})^{\alpha}+\mu(U_{i-1})d(U_{i},U_{i-1})^{\alpha}\right)\sum\limits_{n\in\mathds{Z}}m_{q^{n}}^{-s}\left\lvert q\right\rvert^{n}_{K}

with

m_{q^{n}}=\max{\left\{1,\left\lvert q\right\rvert^{n}\right\}}

The factor before the sum equals

C_{\alpha}(i)

and the sum equals

\sum\limits_{n\geq 0}\left\lvert q\right\rvert^{n}+\sum\limits_{n>0}\left\lvert q\right\rvert^{n(s-1)}=S_{q}(s)

as asserted.
∎
