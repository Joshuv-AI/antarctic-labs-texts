Abstract
. We give a new proof of a recent resolution [ 18 ] by Michelen and Sahasrabudhe of a conjecture of Shepp and Vanderbei [ 19 ] that the moduli of roots of Gaussian Kac polynomials of degree n n , centered at 1 1 and rescaled by n 2 n^{2} , should form a Poisson point process. We use this new approach to verify a conjecture from [ 18 ] that the Poisson statistics are in fact universal.



1.
Introduction

Let
\xi_{0},\ldots,\xi_{n}
be i.i.d.
random variables and consider the
Kac
polynomial

(1.1)

f(z):=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\xi_{k}z^{k}.

This paper concerns the typical behavior
of the random zero set
Z(f):=\{z\in{\mathbb{C}}:f(z)=0\}
.
It is well known that if

\mathbb{E}\log(1+|\xi_{0}|)<\infty
then the roots of

f
concentrate uniformly around the unit circle

\mathbb{S}^{1}:=\{|z|=1\}
as the degree
n
tends to infinity

[
11
]
,
[
20
]

(see also
[
12
]
for a more modern perspective).
Finer results on this convergence are also known:
typically, most roots lie inside an annulus of width

O(n^{-1})
around the unit circle (see

[
19
]
for
Gaussian coefficients and

[
13
]

for more general choices of coefficients). Microscopic correlation
functions for the Gaussian case
(also, for multivariate systems of polynomials)
appear in
[
4
]
;
see
[
8
,
21
]
for a universality result in the case of non-Gaussian
coefficients.

In view of the above, the following question becomes
quite natural:

What is the typical distance of the
set of roots to the unit circle?
Indeed, this question
was already suggested by Shepp and Vanderbei

[
19
, Section 6]
, who conjectured that
for Gaussian coefficients,
the set
\{n^{2}(1-|z|)\,:\,z\in Z(f)\}
(identified with its counting measure) converges
to a Poisson point process as
n\to\infty
;
their conjecture would imply in particular that
the closest root is typically at distance of order
n^{-2}

from the unit circle. This conjecture was recently confirmed by Michelen and Sahasrabudhe

[
18
]
, using a reduction to a point process determined by the
polynomial
on the unit circle
and a
high-order Kac–Rice formula.

Theorem 1.1

(
[
18
, Theorem 1]
)
.

Assume that
f
is given by (
1.1
)
with
\xi_{i}
i.i.d.,
real-valued Gaussian of mean zero.
Then

\{n^{2}(1-|z|)\,:\,z\in Z(f)\}

converges in distribution
(with respect to the vague topology), as
n\to\infty
,
to a
homogeneous Poisson point process on
\mathbb{R}

of intensity
1/12
.
In particular, for all
x>0
,

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(n^{2}\,{\text{dist}}\left(\mathbb{S}^{1},Z(f)\right)\geqslant x\right)=e^{-x/6}.

It is natural to guess that the phenomenon described in
Theorem

1.1
is in fact universal in the sense that the
theorem
holds true for
a wide class of coefficients distribution,
and not just for Gaussians.
In this regard, it is natural (and also suggested in
[
18
]
)
to conjecture that

Theorem

1.1
holds for
random
Littlewood
polynomials, that is,
when the
\xi_{i}
’s are i.i.d.
chosen uniformly at random from the set
\{\pm 1\}
.

The goal of this paper
is multifold. We will give a new proof
of
Theorem

1.1
,
based on ideas appearing in
our recent works
[
22
]
,
[
7
]
.
In this proof, and in contrast with

[
18
]
,
we do not use the Kac–Rice formula,
a powerful tool which is hard to apply outside the case of
Gaussian coefficients (or, with considerably more effort,
coefficients whose law possesses a smooth density).
Instead, we work directly
with local linear approximations of
f

(see Section
1.1
for more details). Besides
the intrinsic interest in a different proof, the main
advantage of our approach is that it is more
suitable
toward the study of
universality problem, for which our main result
confirms the prediction. Namely, we show

Theorem 1.2
.

Theorem

1.1
continues to hold as long as the
\xi_{i}
are
i.i.d. copies of a non-degenerate, sub-Gaussian, real-valued, zero-mean
random variable.

In particular, our result
extends to random Littlewood polynomials.

Remark 1.3
.

By sub-Gaussian we mean that there exists a constant
c>0
so that

\mathbb{P}(|\xi_{0}|>t)\leq 2\exp(-ct^{2})

for all
t>0
.
A close investigation of the current method would enable us to allow
|\xi_{i}|
to have bounded
k
-th moment for some large constant
k
, however we will not elaborate on this. As it has no bearing on the conclusion, in the rest of the paper we may and will assume that the
\xi_{i}
’s are normalized to have unit variance.

Remark 1.4
.

Our methods allow one to consider other related
point processes for statistics near the unit circle, see
Section
8
.

1.1.
Heuristics and proof method

We first explain briefly the Poisson heuristic
behind the Gaussian case, which is hinted at
in
[
19
, Section 6]
. The computations of

[
19
]
and an asymptotic analysis show that
the
expected
number of roots at distance at most

x/n^{2}
from the unit
circle, denoted
N_{x}(n)
, is asymptotic to
x/6
.
(The expression in

[
13
, Theorem 2]
, based on
the Kac–Rice formula, gives that immediately, and in fact the proof
there can be adapted to cover that asymptotic.)
While computing higher moments
of this quantity via the Kac–Rice formula may be feasible,
the computation quickly becomes cumbersome, with a major
obstacle being the need to deal with
short-range correlations and their cancellations.
Assuming however that such short-range correlations do not affect
the higher order
moments, one notes that for macroscopically separated points

(z_{1},\ldots,z_{k})
on the unit circle,
the joint density of the random variables
(f(z_{i}),f^{\prime}(z_{i}))_{i=1}^{k}

nearly factorizes; accepting this factorization, one obtains from
the Kac–Rice formula
that the expectation of the
k
th moment of
N_{x}(n)
converges to the
corresponding one for a Poisson random variable of parameter
x/6
.
From this, the route to a Poisson heuristic is short. In fact, once
spatial separation between close roots is proved, the Poisson
heuristic is standard.

There are two obstacles for making the heuristic precise, even in the Gaussian case. First, one
needs to get rid of short range correlations. This is achieved by
noting, as was done in
[
16
,
18
]
, that in a tubular neighborhood of
z\in\mathbb{S}^{1}

of diameter
o(1/n)
, the pair
({\operatorname{Re}}f(z),{\operatorname{Im}}f(z))

can be approximated by linear curves, and the existence in this
neighborhood
of a root of distance
x/n^{2}
from the
circle can be decided in terms of the 4-vector

W(z):=

({\operatorname{Re}}f(z),{\operatorname{Im}}f(z),

{\operatorname{Re}}f^{\prime}(z),{\operatorname{Im}}f^{\prime}(z))
, with the result that except for exceptional
values of this vector, only one root
can possibly exist in the neighborhood.
The second obstacle involves long range correlations: while
conditioning on a fixed number of values

W(z_{i})_{i\geq 2}
for macroscopically separated
z_{i}
has only a small effect on
W(z_{1})
, by analyticity
the values
\{W(z)\}_{z\in\mathbb{S}^{1}:|z-z_{1}|>1/2}
already determine

W(z_{1})
and in particular the existence of a root close to
z_{1}
.
This is a serious obstacle in applying methods of Poisson convergence
based on second moment methods, e.g.
[
2
]
.

1.2.
Structure of the paper

We can now explain our approach to

Theorem

1.2
and contrast it with the approach to the Gaussian
case introduced in
[
18
]
.
There are essentially three major
steps.

(1)

As explained above,
in the first step we show via (
2.9
) and

Proposition

2.3
that the statistics of

n^{2}(1-|z|),z\in Z(f)
near the unit circle can be deduced
from the behavior of
(f(z_{\alpha}),f^{\prime}(z_{\alpha}))

on the unit circle,
for
z_{\alpha}=e^{i\theta_{\alpha}}
belonging to a net
of cardinality
n^{2}/\log^{K_{0}}n
(for some large constant
K_{0}
), and that one needs only consider
good events, denoted
\mathcal{A}_{\alpha}
,
where the linear approximation at
z_{\alpha}

is precise enough. Towards the universality result (and in particular to allow discrete distributions for
\xi_{0}
), for reasons described below
we remove from consideration certain “bad points”
z_{\alpha}

possessing bad arithmetic properties, leaving only
under consideration
smooth points

z_{\alpha}
, see

Definition

2.1
and
2.2
. (The latter
removal of bad points is not needed in the Gaussian case.)
Further, we show (see
2.4
and
Lemma

7.1
) that one needs only consider
z_{\alpha}
’s that are separated
by distance at least
n^{-1+\varepsilon}
for some small
\varepsilon>0
.

(2)

From Step
1
, for points
z_{\alpha}
satisfying the good event,
the location of the root close to
z_{\alpha}
can be written as

Z_{\alpha}=n^{2}\rho_{\alpha}=n^{2}\rho_{\alpha}(f(z_{\alpha}),f^{\prime}(z_{\alpha}))
, see
(
2.6
) and (
2.8
).
Then, the
k
th moment of
N_{x}(n)
can be determined in terms of the
joint distribution of
(Z_{\alpha_{j}})_{j=1}^{k}
for well-separated, smooth

z_{\alpha_{i}}
. In the Gaussian case, these are almost independent (see
the computation in Section
3.2
for a quantitative estimate), and the moments
factor, see
Lemma

3.5
, yielding
Theorem

1.2
in the Gaussian case. No notion of smoothness of the
z_{\alpha}
’s is needed for that computation.

(3)

The key tool for obtaining universality is a result imported from
[
7
]
giving a quantitative local CLT for the joint distribution of
W(z_{1}),\dots,W(z_{k})
for any fixed number of points
z_{1},\dots,z_{k}\in\mathbb{S}^{1}
that are both smooth and spread
(see
Theorem

3.9
).
Combined with small ball
estimates, also borrowed from
[
7
]

(see
Theorem

3.7
),
this leads to a comparison of probabilities between the Gaussian and general
cases, culminating with
3.12
.

We note in passing that the local CLT borrowed
from
[
7
]
, arguably the technically
most challenging component used in our
proof, is in turn a significant generalization
of a result from
[
9
]
to the multivariate case.

1.3.
Background

We compare our result with existing
literature. Konyagin and Schlag
[
15
,
16
]

showed that, for random Kac polynomials as
general as in Theorem
1.2
,
with high probability the complex roots of
f(z)

cannot be within distance
o(1/n^{2})
of the unit circle.
This is consistent with our theorem,
but the method of
[
16
]
,
on the other hand, cannot be used to
study asymptotic statistics. More relevant to our universal statistics,
[
21
, Theorem 5.7]
of Tao and Vu, or
[
8
, Theorem 2.3]
of Do et
al. established universality for the correlation functions
of complex roots of
f(z)
within balls of radius
\Theta(1/n)
near the unit circle; it seems that
the techniques and results there cannot be applied to our problem because the region under current consideration is at a finer scale and too close to the circle.

We already mentioned that
Theorem

1.1
, the
Gaussian case of
Theorem

1.2
, was already proved in
[
18
]
,
using a method based on the higher
Kac–Rice formula, which seems ill-suited
to computations in the non-Gaussian case. It does not seem
straightforward to apply the local CLT comparison
to their computation and obtain directly
Theorem

1.2
from their

Theorem

1.1
.

As discussed above, the method of proof of
Theorem

1.2
borrows from the
recent
[
7
,
22
]
, that dealt with the variable
m_{n}^{*}=\min_{z\in\mathbb{S}^{1}}|f(z)|
; we refer to these articles
for historical background. (That the problems are related appears already in the work of Konyagin and Schlag
[
16
]
.)
Step 1 in the sketch above is similar to the analogous step in

[
22
]
, with the variable
Z_{\alpha}
here differing from the variable

Z_{\alpha}
in
[
22
,
7
]
by a factor of
1/|f^{\prime}(z_{\alpha})|
.
In the case
of
m_{n}^{*}
, with
\xi_{i}
Gaussian,
the collection
(Z_{\alpha_{j}})_{j=1}^{k}

for separated
z_{\alpha_{j}}
has the property that writing

\xi_{i}=\sqrt{1-\epsilon^{2}}\xi_{i}^{\prime}+\epsilon\xi_{i}^{\prime\prime}
with
\xi_{i}^{\prime},\xi_{i}^{\prime\prime}

independent and
\epsilon
small (
n
dependent), one has that
Z_{\alpha_{j}}=Z^{\prime}_{\alpha_{j}}+n_{j}

where
Z_{\alpha_{j}}^{\prime}
are the same as
Z_{\alpha_{j}}

except that
\xi_{i}^{\prime}
are used instead of
\xi_{i}
, and
the
n_{j}
are (asymptotically) independent of each other and of the
(Z_{\alpha_{j}}^{\prime})
.
From this, an application of an
invariance result due to Liggett
[
17
]
(see also
[
6
]
) gave the Poisson limit in
the Gaussian case. In the case considered in this paper,
with the definition of
Z_{\alpha}
as in
(
2.6
) and (
2.8
),
one can repeat the computation,
but we cannot show a-priori that
the
n_{j}
’s are asymptotically independent
of the
Z_{\alpha_{j}}^{\prime}
. Thus, Step 2 here differs significantly from
the proof in
[
22
]
(which is used as basis for comparison in
[
7
]
).

X_{n}\xrightarrow{\ d\ }X

. Finally, for

N\in\mathbb{N}

[N]:=\left\{0,1,\ldots,N\right\}
.

Sometimes we write
\mathbb{P}_{{\mathbf{N}}(0,1)}(\cdot),\mathbb{E}_{{\mathbf{N}}(0,1)}(\cdot)

2.
Reduction to the unit circle

For
t\in[0,\pi]
1
1

1

Because
f(\overline{z})=\overline{f(z)}
, it suffices to work with the upper half plane.
we write

\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}f\left((1+\rho)e^{it}\right)=X(\rho,t)+iY(\rho,t)

where

\displaystyle X(\rho,t)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{k=0}^{n}\xi_{k}(1+\rho)^{k}\cos(kt),\qquad Y(\rho,t)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{k=0}^{n}\xi_{k}(1+\rho)^{k}\sin(kt).

On the unit circle we denote

(2.1)

X(t):=X(0,t)\mbox{ and }Y(t):=Y(0,t).

For
S\subset\mathbb{R}
, we define the point process (random measure)
\nu_{f}
of “close-roots” (counting multiplicity) by the formula

\nu_{f}(S):=\left|\left\{z\in Z(f)\mid n^{2}(|z|-1)\in S,\ \mathrm{Im}\,(z)\geqslant 0\right\}\right|.

In fact, Theorem
1.1
exactly states that
\nu_{f}
converges in distribution to a Poisson point process on
\mathbb{R}
. As mentioned in the introduction, the goal of this section is to introduce a new point process that is equivalent to
\nu_{f}
but is easier to work with – this we do in what follows.

We let

N:=\bigg\lfloor\frac{n^{2}}{\log^{K_{0}}n}\bigg\rfloor

for an absolute constant
K_{0}>8
that will be taken sufficiently large over the course of the proof,
and let

(2.2)

{\theta}_{\alpha}:=\frac{\pi\alpha}{N},\quad\alpha=0,\ldots,N.

Also set
I_{\alpha}:=[{\theta}_{\alpha}-\pi/2N,{\theta}_{\alpha}+\pi/2N]
to be a covering of
[0,\pi]
by intervals of equal length. We consider annular domains

C_{\alpha}:=\left\{z\in\mathbb{C}:(1-|z|)\in\left[-\frac{\log n}{n^{2}},\frac{\log n}{n^{2}}\right],\ \text{arg}(z)\in I_{\alpha}\right\}.

Since
m_{\Leb}(C_{\alpha})
is small, we expect that
f(z)\approx f(e^{i{\theta}_{\alpha}})+(z-e^{i{\theta}_{\alpha}})f^{\prime}(e^{i{\theta}_{\alpha}})
for all
z\in C_{\alpha}
.
(We will actually perform the linearization in the
(\rho,t)
-plane.) In particular, this linear approximation will determine, with high probability, whether or not
f
has a root inside the set
C_{\alpha}
, and will also give an approximation to its location if it exists.

2.1.
Linear approximation

For every
\alpha\in[N]
we sample the real and imaginary parts of
\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}f
and their radial derivatives at angle
{\theta}_{\alpha}
, i.e.

(2.3)

\left(X({\theta}_{\alpha}),Y({\theta}_{\alpha}),X^{\prime}({\theta}_{\alpha}),Y^{\prime}({\theta}_{\alpha})\right).

By applying a
2
-dimensional Taylor approximation in the variable
z=(1+\rho)e^{it}
, we arrive at the following linear system:

(2.4)

\begin{pmatrix}X(\rho,t)\\
Y(\rho,t)\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}X({\theta}_{\alpha})\\
Y({\theta}_{\alpha})\end{pmatrix}+\begin{pmatrix}X^{\prime}({\theta}_{\alpha})&Y^{\prime}({\theta}_{\alpha})\\
Y^{\prime}({\theta}_{\alpha})&-X^{\prime}({\theta}_{\alpha})\end{pmatrix}\cdot\begin{pmatrix}t-{\theta}_{\alpha}\\
\rho\end{pmatrix}+\text{error},

where we expect the error term to be small inside
C_{\alpha}
.

We will denote by

(2.5)

F_{\alpha}(t,\rho):=\begin{pmatrix}X({\theta}_{\alpha})\\
Y({\theta}_{\alpha})\end{pmatrix}+\begin{pmatrix}X^{\prime}({\theta}_{\alpha})&Y^{\prime}({\theta}_{\alpha})\\
Y^{\prime}({\theta}_{\alpha})&-X^{\prime}({\theta}_{\alpha})\end{pmatrix}\cdot\begin{pmatrix}t-{\theta}_{\alpha}\\
\rho\end{pmatrix}

as the affine map that approximates
\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}f

inside
C_{\alpha}
. Sometimes for convenience we also write
F_{\alpha}(z)
for
F_{\alpha}({\theta},\rho)
.

By setting
f(z)=0
and neglecting the error term, the system (
2.4
) gives a local candidate for the closest root by

(2.6)

\displaystyle\rho_{\alpha}:=\frac{X^{\prime}({\theta}_{\alpha})Y({\theta}_{\alpha})-X({\theta}_{\alpha})Y^{\prime}({\theta}_{\alpha})}{X^{\prime}({\theta}_{\alpha})^{2}+Y^{\prime}({\theta}_{\alpha})^{2}}

\displaystyle{\tau}_{\alpha}:={\theta}_{\alpha}-\frac{X({\theta}_{\alpha})X^{\prime}({\theta}_{\alpha})+Y({\theta}_{\alpha})Y^{\prime}({\theta}_{\alpha})}{X^{\prime}({\theta}_{\alpha})^{2}+Y^{\prime}({\theta}_{\alpha})^{2}}.

Indeed, it is straightforward to check that
F_{\alpha}\left({\tau}_{\alpha}-{\theta}_{\alpha},\rho_{\alpha}\right)=0
, and so
\rho_{\alpha}
given in (
2.6
) is a linear prediction for the radial position of the closest root to the point
e^{i{\theta}_{\alpha}}
based on the values of
X(\theta_{\alpha}),Y(\theta_{\alpha}),X^{\prime}(\theta_{\alpha}),Y^{\prime}(\theta_{\alpha})
.

Now, we define a new, thinned point process
\mu_{f}
which retains points
\rho_{\alpha}
provided that some good event holds. Set

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{\alpha}:=

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{\alpha}^{\prime}\cap\mathcal{A}_{\alpha}^{\prime\prime},\qquad\mbox{\rm where}

(2.7)

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{\alpha}^{\prime}:=\left\{{\tau}_{\alpha}\in I_{\alpha},\ n^{2}|\rho_{\alpha}|\leq\log n\right\}=\left\{(1+\rho_{\alpha})e^{i{\tau}_{\alpha}}\in C_{\alpha}\right\}

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{\alpha}^{\prime\prime}:=\left\{|X({\theta}_{\alpha})|,|Y({\theta}_{\alpha})|\leq n^{-2/3},\ |X^{\prime}({\theta}_{\alpha})|,|Y^{\prime}({\theta}_{\alpha})|\in[n\log^{-2K_{0}}n,n\log^{2}n]\right\}.

The event
\mathcal{A}_{\alpha}^{\prime}
implies that the linear approximation predicts a root inside
C_{\alpha}
, while the event
\mathcal{A}_{\alpha}^{\prime\prime}
is just typical for such a prediction and tells us that the linear system (
2.6
) is non-degenerate.

With the above notations, we now define our approximating point process as

(2.8)

\mu_{f}:=\sum_{\alpha=0}^{N}\delta_{Z_{\alpha}}\qquad\text{where}\quad Z_{\alpha}:=n^{2}\rho_{\alpha}\cdot\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{A}_{\alpha}}+\infty\cdot\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{A}_{\alpha}^{c}}.

And so,
\mu_{f}
is a point process in
\mathbb{R}
whose values are determined only by the samples of
X,Y,X^{\prime},Y^{\prime}
on the points
\{{\theta}_{\alpha}\}_{\alpha=0}^{N}
.
We will show that
\mu_{f}
serves as a good approximation to
\nu_{f}
. In particular, our argument shows that for any compact interval
U\subset\mathbb{R}
,

(2.9)

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}_{{\mathbf{N}}(0,1)}\left(\mu_{f}(U)=\nu_{f}(U)\right)=1.

2.2.
Smooth points

To extend this comparison to general coefficients we will need to remove points
Z_{\alpha}
coming from certain “bad arcs”.

Definition 2.1

(Smooth points)
.

For
{K}>0
, we say a point
t\in[0,\pi]
is
{K}
-smooth
if

\Big\|\frac{p_{0}t}{\pi}\Big\|_{{\mathbb{R}}/{\mathbb{Z}}}>\frac{{K}}{n}\qquad\forall\;p_{0}\in[1,K+1]\cap{\mathbb{Z}}.

We say a tuple
(t_{1},\dots,t_{m})
is
{K}
-smooth if
t_{r}
is
{K}
-smooth for each
1\leqslant r\leqslant{m}
.

Letting

(2.10)

{E_{\bad}}=\Big\{t\in[0,\pi]:\text{ $t$ is not $n^{\kappa}$-smooth}\Big\}

for some sufficiently small
\kappa>0
that we choose later, we note that
m_{\Leb}({E_{\bad}})=O(n^{2\kappa-1})
. We now define modified point processes

(2.11)

\mu_{f}^{\sharp}:=\sum_{\alpha:{\theta}_{\alpha}\notin{E_{\bad}}}\delta_{Z_{\alpha}}\,,\qquad\qquad\mu_{f}^{\flat}:=\mu_{f}-\mu_{f}^{\sharp}

and

(2.12)

\nu_{f}^{\sharp}:=\sum_{\alpha:\theta_{\alpha}\notin{E_{\bad}}}\sum_{z\in Z(f)\cap C_{\alpha}}\delta_{n^{2}(|z|-1)}\,,\qquad\qquad\nu_{f}^{\flat}:=\nu_{f}-\nu_{f}^{\sharp}.

The following lets us focus on potential angles that are smooth.

Proposition 2.2

(Ruling out bad arcs)
.

With probability
1-O(n^{-\kappa/4})
we have

(2.13)

\mu_{f}^{\flat}({\mathbb{R}})=0.

Furthermore,

(2.14)

\mathbb{P}\Big(\,\exists{\theta}\in{E_{\bad}},z\in Z(f):|z-e^{i{\theta}}|\leqslant n^{-3/2}\Big)=O(n^{-\kappa/4}).

In particular, with probability
1-O(n^{-\kappa/4})
,

\nu_{f}^{\flat}({\mathbb{R}})=0.

We postpone the proof of
2.2
to
Section

4
.
We can now state the modified version of the comparison (
2.9
) allowing us to pass to the point process
\mu_{f}^{\sharp}
in the general case.

Proposition 2.3

(Passage to the unit circle)
.

For any interval
U\subset\mathbb{R}
we have

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(\mu_{f}^{\sharp}(U)=\nu_{f}^{\sharp}(U)\right)=1.

For
x\in\mathbb{R}
and
k\geqslant 1
denote

(x)_{k}:=\max\{0,x(x-1)\cdot\ldots\cdot(x-k+1)\}.

Recall that for a random variable
Z
which has
\text{Poisson}(\lambda)
distribution we have
\mathbb{E}[(Z)_{k}]=\lambda^{k}
for all
k\geqslant 1
. The following states that the factorial moments for
\mu_{f}^{\sharp}
match those of a Poisson process in the limit.

Proposition 2.4

(Moments)
.

Let
U\subset\mathbb{R}
be a finite union of compact intervals. Then

(2.15)

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\mu_{f}^{\sharp}(U)\right)_{k}\right]=\left(\frac{|U|}{12}\right)^{k}\qquad\text{for all}\ k\geqslant 1.

Assuming that Propositions
2.2
,
2.3
and
2.4
holds we can prove our main result.

Proof of
Theorem

1.2
.

By Propositions
2.2
and
2.3
it suffices to show that
\mu_{f}^{\sharp}
has a limiting Poisson distribution as
n\to\infty
. Indeed, Proposition
2.4
together with the classical moment theorem
[
10
, Theorem 3.3.26]
implies that for any finite union of compact intervals
U\subset\mathbb{R}

\mu_{f}^{\sharp}\left(U\right)\xrightarrow{\ d\ }\text{Poisson}\left(\frac{|U|}{12}\right).

Together with a theorem of Kallenberg
[
14
, Theorem 4.7]
, this implies that the sequence of point processes
\mu_{f}^{\sharp}
converge in the vague topology to a Poisson point process of intensity
1/12
, as desired.
∎

2.3.
Joint distribution over spread points

Expanding the factorial moments (
2.15
) leads us to consider the joint events that
k
different samples of our polynomial
f
on the unit circle contributed a point to
\mu_{f}^{\sharp}
which lie in
U
. Since we already imposed a smoothness assumption on the sample points, to compute the desired probabilities we will require all of the points to be separated from one another, in the following sense:

Definition 2.5

(Spread tuples)
.

We say that
\boldsymbol{t}=(t_{1},\dots,t_{k})\in[0,\pi]^{k}
is
\gamma
-spread
if

(2.16)

|t_{i}-t_{j}|\geqslant\frac{{\gamma}}{n}\qquad\forall\,-1\leqslant i<j\leqslant k

where we put
t_{0}:=0
,
t_{-1}:=\pi
.
(We note that this is different from the definition in
[
7
]
for general
(t_{1},\dots,t_{k})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{k}
, but when specialized to
[0,\pi]^{k}
they are equivalent up to modification of
\gamma
by a constant factor.)
Note this definition includes
k=1
, with a single point
t\in[0,\pi]
being
\gamma
-spread if it is distance at least
\gamma/n
from
0
and
\pi
. In particular, if
t
is
1
-smooth then it is 1-spread.

The main step towards the proof of
2.4
is the following:

Proposition 2.6

(Main term)
.

Let
U\subset\mathbb{R}
be a finite union of compact intervals and fix a
k
-tuple of indices
(\alpha_{1},\ldots\alpha_{k})\in[N]^{k}
. Assume that for some
\kappa,\varepsilon>0
the tuple
({\theta}_{\alpha_{1}},\ldots,{\theta}_{\alpha_{k}})
is
n^{\kappa}
-smooth and
n^{\varepsilon}
-spread, where
{\theta}_{\alpha}
is given by (
2.2
). Then,

\mathbb{P}\left(Z_{\alpha_{1}}\in U,\ldots,\ Z_{\alpha_{k}}\in U\right)=\left(\frac{|U|}{12N}\right)^{k}+o\left(N^{-k}\right)

where the rate of convergence depends on
k,\kappa,\varepsilon
and
K_{0}
.

We will prove Proposition
2.6
in Section
5
, and with it conclude Proposition
2.4
in Section
7
. Furthermore, some of the tools which we develop in Section

will be helpful for us to prove Proposition

.

3.
Some supporting lemmas

3.1.
Control on derivatives

We first start with a standard result.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
A,A^{\prime}
be positive constants where
A
is sufficiently large. For any
\lambda\geqslant A\sqrt{\log n}
, with probability at least
1-\exp(-\Theta(\lambda^{2}))
we have that

\max_{||z|-1|\leqslant A^{\prime}/n}|f^{\prime}(z)|,|\partial f/\partial\theta|,|\partial f/\partial\rho|\leqslant\lambda n^{3/2}.

We also have similar bounds for second order derivatives that

\max_{||z|-1|\leqslant A^{\prime}/n}|f^{\prime\prime}(z)|,|\frac{\partial^{2}f}{\partial^{l}\theta\partial^{2-l}\rho}|\ll\lambda n^{5/2},l=0,1,2.

In fact the above holds over
|z|\leqslant 1+A^{\prime}/n
but we don’t need this fact here.

Before proving the result, recall that
f(z)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\xi_{k}z^{k}=f\left((1+\rho)e^{i{\theta}}\right)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\xi_{k}(1+\rho)^{k}\cos(k\theta)+i\sum_{k=0}^{n}\xi_{k}(1+\rho)^{k}\sin(k\theta)=\sqrt{n}(X(\rho,{\theta})+iY(\rho,{\theta}))
. Hence

\displaystyle|\frac{\partial\sqrt{n}X(\rho,{\theta})}{\partial\theta}|

\displaystyle=|\sum_{k=0}^{n}k\xi_{k}(1+\rho)^{k}\sin(k\theta)|=|\sum_{k=0}^{n}(k+1)(k\xi_{k}/(k+1))(1+\rho)^{k}\sin(k\theta)|

\displaystyle=|\sum_{k=0}^{n}(k+1)\xi_{k+1}^{\prime}(1+\rho)^{k}\sin(k\theta)|=|{\operatorname{Re}}(g^{\prime}(z))|,

where
\xi_{k+1}^{\prime}=k\xi_{k}/(k+1)
and
g(z)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\xi_{k+1}^{\prime}z^{k+1}
, and similarly

\displaystyle|\frac{\partial\sqrt{n}X(\rho,{\theta})}{\partial\rho}|

\displaystyle=|\sum_{k=0}^{n}k\xi_{k}(1+\rho)^{k-1}\cos(k\theta)|=|\frac{1}{1+\rho}\sum_{k=0}^{n}(k+1)(k\xi_{k}/(k+1))(1+\rho)^{k}\cos(k\theta)|

\displaystyle=|\frac{1}{1+\rho}\sum_{k=0}^{n}(k+1)\xi_{k+1}^{\prime}(1+\rho)^{k}\sin(k\theta)|=|\frac{1}{1+\rho}{\operatorname{Im}}(g^{\prime}(z))|.

One can also have similar expression for
\frac{\partial\sqrt{n}Y(\rho,{\theta})}{\partial\theta},\frac{\partial\sqrt{n}Y(\rho,{\theta})}{\partial\rho}
. Furthermore, by the same way, the second derivatives
\frac{\partial^{2}X(\rho,\theta)}{\partial^{l}\theta\partial^{2-l}\rho},\frac{\partial^{2}Y(\rho,\theta)}{\partial^{l}\theta\partial^{2-l}\rho}
can be related to
h^{\prime\prime}(z)
, where
h
is a polynomial of degree
n+2
and the coefficients are independent of mean zero, variance almost one, and subgaussian. Hence it suffices to establish Lemma
3.1
only for
\max_{||z|-1|\leqslant A^{\prime}/n|}|f^{\prime}(z)|
and
\max_{||z|-1|\leqslant A^{\prime}/n}|f^{\prime\prime}(z)|
.

Remark 3.2
.

One notes from the Cauchy–Riemann equations in polar form (or Taylor expansion) that for
f
analytic in a neighborhood of
z_{0}=r_{0}e^{i\theta_{0}}
,

|\frac{\partial}{\partial r}f(re^{i\theta_{0}})|_{r=r_{0}}\asymp|f^{\prime}(r_{0}e^{i\theta_{0}})|\,,\qquad|\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}f(r_{0}e^{i\theta})|_{\theta=\theta_{0}}\asymp r_{0}|f^{\prime}(r_{0}e^{i\theta_{0}})|\,.

In particular we have
\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}|f^{\prime}((1+\rho)e^{i\theta}|\asymp(\frac{\partial X}{\partial\theta}(\rho,\theta)^{2}+\frac{\partial Y}{\partial\theta}(\rho,\theta))^{1/2}
uniformly over
(\rho,\theta)\in[-1/2,1/2]\times[0,2\pi]
, say, and we have a similar equivalence for second derivatives.

Proof of Lemma
3.1
.

For the first derivatives, it suffices to show that with probability at least
1-\exp(-\Theta(\lambda^{2}))
we have
\max_{||z|-1|\leqslant A^{\prime}/n}|f^{\prime}(z)|\leqslant\lambda n^{3/2}
. By the maximum principle, it thus suffices to work with
\max|f^{\prime}(z)|
on either
\{(1+A^{\prime}/n)e^{i\theta},\theta\in\mathbb{T}\}
or
\{(1-A^{\prime}/n)e^{i\theta},\theta\in\mathbb{T}\}
. Without loss of generality, let us focus only on the real part of
f^{\prime}(z)
on the larger circle. In other words we will show that with probability at least
1-\exp(-\Theta(\lambda^{2}))
we have
\max_{\theta\in\mathbb{T}}|\sum_{k=0}^{n}\xi_{k}k(1+A^{\prime}/n)^{k-1}\cos((k-1)\theta)|\leqslant\lambda n^{3/2}
. For short denote just for the proof by
h({\theta}):=n^{-3/2}\sum_{k=0}^{n}\xi_{k}k(1+A^{\prime}/n)^{k-1}\cos((k-1)\theta)
. Denote
\lVert h\rVert_{\infty}:=\sup_{{\theta}\in\mathbb{T}}|h({\theta})|
and let
\tilde{{\theta}}\in\mathbb{T}
be such that

\lVert h\rVert_{\infty}=|h(\tilde{{\theta}})|.

Since
h
is a trigonometric polynomial of degree
n
, we can apply the Bernstein inequality and see that for all
|{\theta}-\tilde{{\theta}}|\leq 1/(4n)

(3.1)

|h({\theta})|\geqslant|h(\tilde{{\theta}})|-|h(\tilde{{\theta}})-h({\theta})|\geqslant\lVert h\rVert_{\infty}-|{\theta}-\tilde{{\theta}}|\lVert h^{\prime}\rVert_{\infty}\geqslant\frac{1}{2}\lVert h\rVert_{\infty}.

Furthermore, since the
\{\xi_{k}\}
are assumed to be sub-Gaussian and independent, there exists some
C>0
so that

(3.2)

\mathbb{E}\left[e^{sh({\theta})}\right]=\prod_{k=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\left[e^{sn^{-3/2}\xi_{k}k\left(1+\frac{A^{\prime}}{n}\right)^{k-1}\cos\left((k-1){\theta}\right)}\right]\leq e^{Cs^{2}n^{-3}\sum_{k=1}^{n}k^{2}}\leq e^{Cs^{2}}

for all
s\in\mathbb{R}
.

Applying Markov’s inequality with (
3.1
) and (
3.2
) gives

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\lVert h\rVert_{\infty}\geqslant\lambda\right)

\displaystyle\leq e^{-s\lambda/2}\mathbb{E}\left[e^{s\lVert h\rVert_{\infty}/2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq e^{-s\lambda/2}\mathbb{E}\left[2n\int_{|{\theta}-\tilde{{\theta}}|<\frac{1}{4n}}\left(e^{sh({\theta})}+e^{-sh({\theta})}\right){\rm d}{\theta}\right]

\displaystyle\ll ne^{-\lambda s/2}\int_{{\theta}\in\mathbb{T}}\mathbb{E}(e^{sh({\theta})}+e^{-sh({\theta})}){\rm d}{\theta}

\displaystyle\leqslant ne^{-\lambda s/2}e^{Cs^{2}}.

Choose
s=\lambda/4C
and note that
\lambda\geqslant A\sqrt{\log n}
we obtain as desired.

Finally, for the second derivatives
\max_{||z|-1|\leqslant A^{\prime}/n}|f^{\prime\prime}(z)|
, after applying the maximum principle it suffices to focus only on the two circles,

\Big\{|z|=1+\frac{A^{\prime}}{n}\Big\}\quad\text{and}\quad\Big\{|z|=1-\frac{A^{\prime}}{n}\Big\}\,,

over which the real and imaginary parts are trigonometric polynomials, and hence we can use Bernstein inequality again, the details are left for the reader.
∎

For convenience, denote by

\mathcal{G}:=\left\{\max_{||z|-1|=O(1/n)}|f^{(k)}(z)|,|\frac{\partial^{k}f}{\partial^{l}\theta\partial^{k-l}\rho}|\leqslant n^{k+1/2}\log^{2}n\,,\ k=0,1,2;\,0\leqslant l\leqslant k\right\}.

Corollary 3.3
.

We have

\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{G}^{c}\right)\leqslant\exp\left(-\Theta(\log^{4}n)\right).

3.2.
Control on covariances

Here we gather some results on the joint distribution of
X,Y
and their derivatives at a fixed number of points.
We begin with the distribution at a single point.
In the sequel we denote the matrix

(3.3)

\Sigma_{0}:=\begin{pmatrix}\frac{1}{2}&0&0&\frac{1}{4}\\
0&\frac{1}{2}&-\frac{1}{4}&0\\
0&-\frac{1}{4}&\frac{1}{6}&0\\
\frac{1}{4}&0&0&\frac{1}{6}\end{pmatrix}.

Lemma 3.4
.

For any fixed
\varepsilon>0
and
t\in[n^{-1+\varepsilon},\pi-n^{-1+\varepsilon}]
the (centered) random vector

(3.4)

{W}(t):=\left(X(t),Y(t),\frac{1}{n}X^{\prime}(t),\frac{1}{n}Y^{\prime}(t)\right)

has covariance matrix

\Sigma(t)=\mathbb{E}{W}(t)^{\mathsf{T}}{W}(t)=(\text{Id}+O(n^{-\varepsilon}))\Sigma_{0}\,.

where the error term is a matrix with entries of size
O(n^{-\varepsilon})
.

Proof.

By applying simple trigonometric identities, we see that

\displaystyle\Sigma(t)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=0}^{n}\begin{pmatrix}\cos^{2}(kt)&\frac{1}{2}\sin(2kt)&-\frac{k}{2n}\sin(2kt)&\frac{k}{n}\cos^{2}(kt)\\
\frac{1}{2}\sin(2kt)&\sin^{2}(kt)&-\frac{k}{n}\sin^{2}(kt)&\frac{k}{2n}\sin(2kt)\\
-\frac{k}{2n}\sin(2kt)&-\frac{k}{n}\sin^{2}(kt)&\frac{k^{2}}{n^{2}}\cos^{2}(kt)&-\frac{k^{2}}{2n^{2}}\sin(2kt)\\
\frac{k}{n}\cos^{2}(kt)&\frac{k}{2n}\sin(2kt)&-\frac{k^{2}}{2n^{2}}\sin(2kt)&\frac{k^{2}}{n^{2}}\sin^{2}(kt)\end{pmatrix}.

It remains to note that (see for instance
[
3
, Appendix B]
)

\left|\sum_{k=0}^{n}k^{a}\sin(kt)\right|,\left|\sum_{k=0}^{n}k^{a}\cos(kt)\right|=O(n^{a+1-\varepsilon})

for
a=0,1,2
and
t\in[n^{-1+\varepsilon},\pi-n^{-1+\varepsilon}]
,
giving
\Sigma(t)=\Sigma_{0}+O(n^{-\varepsilon})
. Since
\Sigma_{0}
is invertible we can factor it out of the additive error,
and the claim follows.
∎

Now for
\boldsymbol{t}=(t_{1},\dots,t_{k})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{k}
we denote the random vector

(3.5)

{W}(\boldsymbol{t})=\big({W}(t_{1}),\dots,{W}(t_{k})\big)\;\in\,{\mathbb{R}}^{4k}.

with covariance matrix

\Sigma(\boldsymbol{t})=\mathbb{E}{W}(\boldsymbol{t})^{\mathsf{T}}{W}(\boldsymbol{t}).

Recall Definition
2.5
on spread points. When the points
t_{1},\dots,t_{k}
are
\omega(1)
-spread it is easily seen that the covariance matrix decouples into blocks, as shown in the following:

Lemma 3.5

(Decorrelation for Gaussian field)
.

Fix
t_{1},\dots,t_{k}\in[0,\pi]
and non-negative measurable functions
\varphi_{1},\dots,\varphi_{k}:{\mathbb{R}}^{4}\to{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
supported in
B(0,n^{a_{1}})
. Assume
\boldsymbol{t}=(t_{1},\dots,t_{k})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{k}
is
n^{a_{2}}
-spread.
Let
W_{0}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{4}
be a centered Gaussian vector with covariance
\Sigma_{0}
.
Then

\mathbb{E}_{{\mathbf{N}}(0,1)}\prod_{i=1}^{k}\varphi_{i}({W}(t_{i}))=(1+O(n^{2a_{1}-a_{2}}))\prod_{i=1}^{k}\mathbb{E}\varphi_{i}(W_{0}).

Proof.

We have

(3.6)

\Sigma(\boldsymbol{t})=\begin{pmatrix}\Sigma_{0}&0&\dots&0\\
0&\Sigma_{0}&\dots&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&\dots&0&\Sigma_{0}\end{pmatrix}+E

where
\Sigma_{0}
is given by (
3.3
), and all entries in the matrix
E
are
O(n^{-a_{2}})
.
It is evident from (
3.6
) that
\det(\Sigma(\boldsymbol{t}))=\det(\Sigma_{0})^{k}\left(1+O_{k}(n^{-a_{2}})\right)
and that

\Sigma(\boldsymbol{t})^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}\Sigma_{0}^{-1}&0&\dots&0\\
0&\Sigma_{0}^{-1}&\dots&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&\dots&0&\Sigma_{0}^{-1}\end{pmatrix}+\widetilde{E}

where again all entries of
\widetilde{E}
are
O_{k}(n^{-a_{2}})
.
Since
\det(\Sigma(\boldsymbol{t}))=\det(\Sigma_{0})^{k}\left(1+O_{k}(n^{-a_{2}})\right)
we see that

\displaystyle\frac{\left||\det\Sigma_{0}|^{-k/2}-|\det\Sigma(\boldsymbol{t})|^{-1/2}\right|}{(2\pi)^{k}}

\displaystyle\int_{(\mathbb{R}^{4})^{k}}e^{-\frac{1}{2}w^{T}(\Sigma(\boldsymbol{t})^{-1}-\widetilde{E})w}\prod_{i=1}^{k}\varphi_{i}(w_{i})\,{\rm d}m(w)

(3.7)

\displaystyle\ll_{k}n^{-a_{2}}\prod_{i=1}^{k}\mathbb{E}\varphi_{i}(W_{0})\,.

Since
\varphi_{1},\dots,\varphi_{k}
are supported on
B(0,n^{a})
, using that
1-e^{-x}\ll x
for
x
small we see that

\displaystyle\int_{(\mathbb{R}^{4})^{k}}

\displaystyle\left|e^{-\frac{1}{2}w^{T}\Sigma(\boldsymbol{t})^{-1}w}-e^{-\frac{1}{2}w^{T}(\Sigma(\boldsymbol{t})^{-1}-\widetilde{E})w}\right|\prod_{i=1}^{k}\varphi_{i}(w_{i})\,{\rm d}m(w)

\displaystyle\ll\int_{(\mathbb{R}^{4})^{k}}e^{-\frac{1}{2}w^{T}(\Sigma(\boldsymbol{t})^{-1}-\widetilde{E})w}\left|w^{T}\widetilde{E}w\right|\prod_{i=1}^{k}\varphi_{i}(w_{i})\,{\rm d}m(w)

(3.8)

\displaystyle\ll_{k}n^{2a_{1}-a_{2}}\prod_{i=1}^{k}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{4}}e^{-\frac{1}{2}w_{i}^{T}\Sigma_{0}^{-1}w_{i}}\varphi_{i}(w_{i})\,{\rm d}m(w_{i})\,.

Combining these bounds, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{{\mathbf{N}}(0,1)}

\displaystyle\prod_{i=1}^{k}\varphi_{i}(W(t_{i}))-\prod_{i=1}^{k}\mathbb{E}\varphi_{i}(W_{0})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{2k}\sqrt{|\det\Sigma(\boldsymbol{t})|}}\int_{(\mathbb{R}^{4})^{k}}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}w^{T}\Sigma(\boldsymbol{t})^{-1}w\right)\prod_{i=1}^{k}\varphi_{i}(w_{i})\,{\rm d}m(w)

\displaystyle\qquad-\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{2k}\sqrt{|\det\Sigma_{0}|^{k}}}\int_{(\mathbb{R}^{4})^{k}}e^{-\frac{1}{2}w^{T}(\Sigma(\boldsymbol{t})^{-1}-\widetilde{E})w}\prod_{i=1}^{k}\varphi_{i}(w_{i})\,{\rm d}m(w)

\displaystyle\ll_{k}n^{2a_{1}-a_{2}}\prod_{i=1}^{k}\mathbb{E}\varphi_{i}(W_{0})

as claimed.
∎

At separations
t-t^{\prime}=O(n^{-1})
the vectors
{W}(t),{W}(t^{\prime})
become correlated. The following shows that
\Sigma(\boldsymbol{t})
is still reasonably well conditioned when
\boldsymbol{t}
is only
\gamma
-spread for
\gamma\ll 1
(fixed or going to zero).
Recall that for a symmetric matrix
A
of dimension
m
we order its eigenvalues
{\lambda}_{1}(A)\geqslant\cdots\geqslant{\lambda}_{m}(A)
.

Lemma 3.6
.

[
7
, Lemma 3.6]

If
\boldsymbol{t}=(t_{1},\dots,t_{k})\in[0,\pi]^{k}
is
{\gamma}
-spread for some
0<{\gamma}\leqslant 1
, then

{\lambda}_{4k}(\Sigma(\boldsymbol{t}))\gg_{k}{\gamma}^{6k-3}.

3.3.
Small-ball estimates and CLTs

The following result from
[
7
]
gives a small ball estimate for the distribution of
W(t_{1},\dots,t_{k})
at arbitrary polynomial scales.

Theorem 3.7
.

[
7
, Theorem 3.4]

Let
\boldsymbol{t}=(t_{1},\dots,t_{k})\in[0,\pi]^{k}
be
n^{\kappa}
-smooth and
{\gamma}
-spread for some
{\kappa}\in(0,1)
and
\omega(n^{-1/8k})\leqslant{\gamma}<1
. For any
K<\infty
and any ball
B
of radius
\delta\geqslant n^{-K}
,

\mathbb{P}\big({W}(\boldsymbol{t})\in B\big)=O_{K,{\kappa}}({\gamma}^{-3k}\delta^{4k}).

While the above estimates are quite strong, we will also need bounds at non-smooth points, especially near the edge. We have the following:

Lemma 3.8
.

Assume that
\frac{\lambda}{n}\leqslant t\leqslant\pi-\frac{\lambda}{n}
. Then for any
\delta\gg n^{-1/2}
we have

\mathbb{P}(|f(e^{it})|/\sqrt{n}\leqslant\delta)=O(\lambda^{-2}\delta^{2}).

We will present a proof of this result in Appendix
A
.

Next we recall the following result from
[
7
]
providing a fine-scale comparison of the distribution of
W(\boldsymbol{t})
with a Gaussian vector, under the assumption that the points
t_{1},\dots,t_{k}
are both smooth and spread.

Theorem 3.9
.

[
7
, Theorem 3.2]

Let
\boldsymbol{t}=(t_{1},\dots,t_{k})\in[0,\pi]^{k}
be
n^{\kappa}
-smooth and
1
-spread for some
{\kappa}>0
. Fix
K>0
and let
Q\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{4k}
be a box (direct product of intervals) with side lengths at least

.
Then

\big|\mathbb{P}\big({W}(\boldsymbol{t})\in Q\big)-\mathbb{P}_{{\mathbf{N}}(0,1)}\big({W}(\boldsymbol{t})\in Q\big)\big|\ll n^{-1/2}|Q|

where
|Q|
is the volume of
Q
, and the implied constant depends only on
k,{\kappa},K
,
and the sub-Gaussian constant for
\xi
.

The above results let us control the measure, under the law of
{W}(\boldsymbol{t})
, of domains in
{\mathbb{R}}^{4k}
that can be accurately approximated or covered by unions of cubes or balls of (any) polynomially-small size.

Definition 3.10
.

Say a domain
{\mathcal{D}}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
with piecewise smooth boundary is
(K,L)
-good
if
\partial D
can be covered by a family of cubes
Q
with corners in the scaled lattice
n^{-K}{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
, with total volume
\sum|Q|\leqslant n^{-L}
.

Note that if
{\mathcal{D}}_{1},{\mathcal{D}}_{2}
are
(K,L)
-good, then
{\mathcal{D}}_{1}^{c}
,
{\mathcal{D}}_{1}\cup{\mathcal{D}}_{2}
and
{\mathcal{D}}_{1}\cap{\mathcal{D}}_{2}
are
(K,L-1)
-good (for all
n
sufficiently large).

Theorem

3.7
and a covering argument yield the following:

Proposition 3.11
.

Let
\boldsymbol{t}\in[0,\pi]^{k}
be as in
Theorem

3.7
and let
{\mathcal{D}}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{4k}
be
(K,L)
-good for some

.
Then

\mathbb{P}({W}(\boldsymbol{t})\in{\mathcal{D}})\ll_{K,{\kappa}}\gamma^{-3k}(m({\mathcal{D}})+n^{-L}).

In particular, for
k=1
and

that is

-smooth,

\mathbb{P}({W}(t)\in{\mathcal{D}})\ll_{K,{\kappa}}m({\mathcal{D}})+n^{-L}.

Combining
Theorem

3.9
and
Lemma

3.5
, we obtain that the joint law of
(W(t_{1}),\dots,W(t_{k}))
in phase space approximately factorizes into independent Gaussian measures as soon as the times
t_{1},\dots,t_{k}
are sufficiently spread.

Proposition 3.12
.

Fix
n^{\kappa}
-smooth points
t_{1},\dots,t_{k}\in[0,\pi]
, and let
W_{0}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{4}
be a centered Gaussian with covariance matrix
\Sigma_{0}
as in (

).

(a)

If
t_{1},\dots,t_{k}
are 1-spread, then for any
(K,L)
-good domain
{\mathcal{D}}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{4k}

6.1

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{A}_{\alpha},\ Z_{\alpha}\in U,\ Z(f)\cap U_{\alpha}=\varnothing,\ \mathcal{G}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{A}_{\alpha},\ (1+\rho_{\alpha})e^{i{\tau}_{\alpha}}\in U_{\alpha}\setminus F_{\alpha}^{-1}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}f(U_{\alpha})\right)\,,\,{\mathcal{G}}\,\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\left((1+\rho_{\alpha})e^{i{\tau}_{\alpha}}\in U_{\alpha}^{+}\setminus U_{\alpha}^{-}\,,\ \mathcal{G}\right)\,.

Recalling from
6.1
that
U_{\alpha}^{+}\setminus U_{\alpha}^{-}
is the
\delta
-neighborhood of the boundary of
U_{\alpha}
, we can cover the corresponding set in the
(\rho,t)
by four rectangles
I\times J
of area
O(\delta n^{2})
.
Thus, on the last event we have that
W(\theta_{\alpha})\in{\mathcal{D}}_{I,J,n^{o(1)}}
for one of four possibilities for
(I,J)
, each satisfying
|I|\times|J|\leqslant\delta n^{2}\ll n^{-1/2}
. It follows from
3.11
and (
5.4
) that

\mathbb{P}\left((1+\rho_{\alpha})e^{i{\tau}_{\alpha}}\in U_{\alpha}^{+}\setminus U_{\alpha}^{-}\,,\ \mathcal{G}\right)\ll\frac{1}{n^{1/2-o(1)}N}=o(N^{-1})

Summing over
\alpha
we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}

\displaystyle\left(\mu_{f}^{\sharp}(U)>\nu_{f}^{\sharp}(U)\right)\leq o(1)+\sum_{\alpha:\theta_{\alpha}\notin{E_{\bad}}}\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{A}_{\alpha},\ Z_{\alpha}\in U,\ Z(f)\cap U_{\alpha}=\varnothing,\ \mathcal{G}\right)=o(1).

The proof of the proposition will follow once we show that

(6.3)

\mathbb{P}\left(\mu_{f}^{\sharp}(U)<\nu_{f}^{\sharp}(U)\right)=o(1).

By Claim
6.2
and
3.3
the left hand side above is bounded by

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\bigcup_{\alpha:\theta_{\alpha}\notin{E_{\bad}}}\{\mathcal{A}_{\alpha}^{\prime\prime},\ Z_{\alpha}\not\in U,\ |Z(f)\cap U_{\alpha}|=1\}\right)

(6.4)

\displaystyle\quad+\mathbb{P}\left(\bigcup_{\alpha:\theta_{\alpha}\notin{E_{\bad}}}\{{\left(\mathcal{A}_{\alpha}^{{\prime\prime}}\right)}^{c},\ |Z(f)\cap U_{\alpha}|=1,\mathcal{G}\}\right)+o(1).

Similar to the previous argument, on the event
\{\mathcal{A}_{\alpha}^{\prime\prime},\ Z_{\alpha}\not\in U\}
the map
F_{\alpha}
is non-degenerate and we have

(1+\rho_{\alpha})e^{i{\tau}_{\alpha}}\not\in U_{\alpha},

while the assumption that
|Z(f)\cap U_{\alpha}|=1
implies that
0\in f(U_{\alpha})
. This tells us that

(1+\rho_{\alpha})e^{i{\tau}_{\alpha}}\in F_{\alpha}^{-1}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}f(U_{\alpha})\right)\setminus U_{\alpha}\subset U_{\alpha}^{+}\setminus U_{\alpha}^{-}\,.

Arguing with
6.1
as we did for the events
\{Z_{\alpha}\in U,Z(f)\cap U_{\alpha}=\varnothing\}
, we get that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\bigcup_{\alpha:\theta_{\alpha}\notin{E_{\bad}}}\{\mathcal{A}_{\alpha}^{\prime\prime},\ Z_{\alpha}\not\in U,\ |Z(f)\cap U_{\alpha}|=1\}\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant o(1)+\sum_{\alpha:\theta_{\alpha}\notin{E_{\bad}}}\mathbb{P}\left((1+\rho_{\alpha})e^{i{\tau}_{\alpha}}\in U_{\alpha}^{+}\setminus U_{\alpha},\ \mathcal{G}\right)=o(1).

For the second probability in (
6
),
consider
\alpha\in[N]
with
\theta_{\alpha}\notin{E_{\bad}}
and suppose
\{(\mathcal{A}_{\alpha}^{\prime\prime})^{c},|Z(f)\cap U_{\alpha}|=1,\mathcal{G}\}
holds. Let
\xi\in U_{\alpha}
be the root of
f
. By Taylor expansion and the restriction to
\mathcal{G}
we have

|f(e^{i\theta_{\alpha}})|\leqslant|f(\xi)|+O(n^{3/2}\log^{2}n)|\xi-e^{i\theta_{\alpha}}|\ll\frac{\log^{K_{0}+2}n}{\sqrt{n}}

which implies
|X(\theta_{\alpha})|,|Y(\theta_{\alpha})|\leqslant n^{-1+o(1)}=o(n^{-2/3})
. From this and the restriction to
\mathcal{G}
, if the event
\mathcal{A}_{\alpha}^{\prime\prime}
does not occur then we must have either
|X^{\prime}(\theta_{\alpha})|<n\log^{-2K_{0}}n
or
|Y^{\prime}(\theta_{\alpha})|<n\log^{-2K_{0}}n
. Now, from
Theorem

3.9
and the boundedness of the Gaussian density (or
Theorem

3.7
and a covering by balls of bounded overlap) we get

\mathbb{P}\left(|X(\theta_{\alpha})|,|Y(\theta_{\alpha})|\leqslant\frac{\log^{K_{0}+2}n}{n},\,|X^{\prime}(\theta_{\alpha})|\leqslant n\log^{-2K_{0}}n\right)\leqslant\frac{\log^{4}n}{n^{2}}=o\left(N^{-1}\right),

and similarly

\mathbb{P}\left(|X(\theta_{\alpha})|,|Y(\theta_{\alpha})|\leqslant\frac{\log^{K_{0}+2}n}{n},\,|Y^{\prime}(\theta_{\alpha})|\leqslant n\log^{-2K_{0}}n\right)=o\left(N^{-1}\right).

Taking the union bound over these two cases, we have thus shown

\mathbb{P}((\mathcal{A}_{\alpha}^{\prime\prime})^{c},|Z(f)\cap U_{\alpha}|=1,\mathcal{G})=o(N^{-1})

Applying the above and the union bound over the choices of
\alpha
, we see that (
6
) is
o(1)
, giving (
6.3
) as desired.
∎

7.
Proof of Proposition
2.4

In this section we argue similarly as in
[
7
, Section 5]
with some minor modifications. Expanding the factorial moments
\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\mu_{f}^{\sharp}(U)\right)_{k}\right]
we see that

\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\mu_{f}^{\sharp}(U)\right)_{k}\right]=\sum_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}\in E}\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})\right),

where
E:=\left\{\boldsymbol{\alpha}=(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{k})\in[N]^{k}\mid\alpha_{i}\not=\alpha_{j}\ \forall i\not=j,\,\theta_{\alpha_{i}}\not\in{E_{\bad}}\right\}
and

\mathcal{E}(\alpha):=\left\{Z_{\alpha_{1}}\in U,\ldots,Z_{\alpha_{k}}\in U\right\}.

For some sufficiently small
\varepsilon>0
we consider the set
E^{\prime}:=\left\{\boldsymbol{\alpha}\in E\mid\left(\theta_{\alpha_{1}},\ldots,\theta_{\alpha_{k}}\right)\text{is }n^{\varepsilon}\text{-spread}\right\}
. Since
|E^{\prime}|=N^{k}(1+o(1))
, we can use
2.6
and get that

\sum_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}\in E^{\prime}}\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})\right)=N^{k}\left(\frac{|U|}{12N}\right)^{k}+o(1)=\left(\frac{|U|}{12}\right)^{k}+o(1)

so
2.4
will follow once we show that

(7.1)

\lim_{n\to\infty}\sum_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}\in E\setminus E^{\prime}}\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})\right)=0.

The next lemma shows that close roots are typically separated. The proof is a simple modification of
[
22
, Lemma 2.11]
or
[
7
, Lemma 2.2]
and is deferred to Appendix
B
.

Lemma 7.1
.

On the event
\mathcal{G}
, for
(\alpha,\alpha^{\prime})\in[N]^{2}
with
\theta_{\alpha},\theta_{\alpha^{\prime}}\not\in{E_{\bad}}
we have

(1)

If
\mathcal{A}_{\alpha}
and
\mathcal{A}_{\alpha+1}
occur, then

|{\tau}_{\alpha}-{\theta}_{\alpha}|\in\left[\frac{\pi}{2N}-\frac{\pi}{2N\log^{K_{0}}n},\frac{\pi}{2N}\right]\,.

(2)

\mathcal{A}_{\alpha}
and
\mathcal{A}_{\alpha^{\prime}}
cannot occur simultaneously as long as

|\theta_{\alpha}-\theta_{\alpha^{\prime}}|\in\bigg(\frac{\pi}{N},\frac{1}{n\log^{4K_{0}}n}\bigg]\,.

Proof of Proposition
2.4
.

By the discussion above, we only need to show that (
7.1
) holds. By Corollary
3.3
we can assume that
\mathcal{G}
holds. The second item in Lemma
7.1
implies that we only need to consider tuples
\boldsymbol{\alpha}\in E\setminus E^{\prime}
of the form

(7.2)

\boldsymbol{\alpha}=\left(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{k-\ell},\alpha_{1}+1,\ldots,\alpha_{\ell}+1\right)

consisting of
\ell
tuples of the form
(\alpha_{j},\alpha_{j}+1)
for some
0\leq\ell\leq k/2
, while the
k-\ell
points
\theta_{\alpha_{1}},\ldots,\theta_{\alpha_{k-\ell}}
are pairwise separated by at least
n^{-1}\log^{-4K_{0}}n
in
[0,\pi]
.

We divide the class of such
\alpha
into into two sets
E_{1}
and
E_{2}
, where
E_{1}
consists of all
\alpha\in E\setminus E^{\prime}
of the form (
7.2
) (possibly with
\ell=0
) such that
|\theta_{\alpha_{i}}-\theta_{\alpha_{j}}|\leq n^{-1+\varepsilon}
for some
1\leq i<j\leq k-\ell
, and
E_{2}
is the set of all
\alpha\in E\setminus E^{\prime}
of the form (
7.2
) with
\ell\geqslant 1
and
|\theta_{\alpha_{i}}-\theta_{\alpha_{j}}|>n^{-1+\varepsilon}
for all
1\leq i<j\leq k-\ell
.

For the sum over
E_{1}
, denote by
E_{1}^{\ell}
the tuples
\alpha\in E_{1}
with
\ell
neighboring pairs as in (
7.2
). We have
|E_{1}^{\ell}|=O(N^{k-\ell}/n^{1-\varepsilon})
, as there are
O(N/n^{1-\varepsilon})
choices for the close point with all others fixed.
Recalling the notation (
5
), we have

\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})\subseteq\big\{{W}({\theta}_{\alpha_{i}})\in{\mathcal{D}}_{U,V,2\log^{2}n}\;\forall 1\leqslant i\leqslant k\big\}

with
V=[-\frac{\pi}{2},\frac{\pi}{2}]
.
Since the points
\theta_{\alpha_{1}},\ldots,\theta_{\alpha_{k-\ell}}
are separated by at least
n^{-1}\log^{-4K_{0}}n
, we can apply Proposition
3.11
(recalling
5.1
) along with (
5.4
) with
r=2\log^{2}n
, to get

\sum_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}\in E_{1}}\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})\right)\ll\sum_{\ell=0}^{\lfloor k/2\rfloor}|E_{1}^{\ell}|N^{-k+\ell}\log^{O(k)}n\ll\frac{\log^{O(k)}n}{n^{1-\varepsilon}}=o(1).

For the sum over
E_{2}
, fix a tuple
\boldsymbol{\alpha}\in E_{2}
with
\ell
neighboring pairs with
\ell\geqslant 1
. By the first item in Lemma
7.1
, we have the containment of events

\{Z_{\alpha_{j}}\in U,\,Z_{\alpha_{j+1}}\in U\}\subset\left\{Z_{\alpha_{j}}\in U,\ |{\tau}_{\alpha_{j}}-{\theta}_{\alpha_{j}}|\in\left[\frac{\pi}{2N}-\frac{\pi}{2N\log^{K_{0}}n},\frac{\pi}{2N}\right]\right\}\,.

The event on the right hand side is contained in the event that

W(\theta_{\alpha})\in{\mathcal{D}}_{U,V_{-},2\log^{2}n}\cup{\mathcal{D}}_{U,V_{+},2\log^{2}n}

with
V_{-}=[-\frac{\pi}{2},-\frac{\pi}{2}+\frac{\pi}{2\log^{K_{0}}n}]
and
V_{+}=[\frac{\pi}{2}-\frac{\pi}{2\log^{K_{0}}n},\frac{\pi}{2}]
.
As above, by
3.11
,
5.1
and (
5.4
) we get that for
\boldsymbol{\alpha}\in E_{2}
with
\ell
neighboring pairs,

\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})\right)\ll\frac{1}{N^{k-\ell}}\left(\frac{\log^{8}n}{\log^{K_{0}}n}\right)^{k-\ell}.

Taking
K_{0}>8
, since the number of such tuples is at most
N^{k-\ell}
, the above display implies that

\sum_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}\in E_{2}}\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})\right)=o(1).

We have thus proved (
7.1
), and hence also the proposition.
∎

8.
Extensions

The method presented in the paper allows one to consider other point processes related to
\mu_{f}
of
(
2.8
). Specifically,
consider
the
4
-tuple

\widehat{Z}_{\alpha}=(n^{2}\rho_{\alpha},N(\theta_{\alpha}-\tau_{\alpha}),X^{\prime}(\theta_{\alpha})/n,Y^{\prime}(\theta_{\alpha})/n),

and introduce the point process

(8.1)

\widehat{\mu}_{f}:=\sum_{\alpha=0}^{N}\delta_{(\theta_{\alpha},\widehat{Z}_{\alpha}{\bf 1}_{{\mathcal{A}}_{\alpha}}+(\infty,\infty,\infty,\infty){\bf 1}_{\mathcal{A}_{\alpha}^{c}})}.

Note that
\mu_{f}
can be obtained from
\widehat{\mu}_{f}
by an appropriate contraction. We then have the following.

Proposition 8.1
.

Under the assumptions of Theorem
1.2
,
the process
\widehat{\mu}_{f}
converges to a Poisson point process on

[0,\pi]\times\mathbb{R}^{4}
with intensity

(12/\pi^{2})((x^{\prime})^{2}+(y^{\prime})^{2})e^{-12((x^{\prime})^{2}+(y^{\prime})^{2})}{\bf 1}_{y\in[0,\pi]}d\theta dxdydx^{\prime}dy^{\prime}.

Indeed, the proof of Proposition
8.1

follows that of Theorem
1.2
. The main difference is
in the computation of

\mathbb{P}_{{\mathbf{N}}(0,1)}(\rho_{\alpha}\in U/n^{2},\theta_{\alpha}-\tau_{\alpha}\in V/N,X^{\prime}(\theta_{\alpha})\in nI_{1},Y^{\prime}(\theta_{\alpha})\in nI_{2})
.
Following the lines of the proof of
Lemma

5.2
, for
\theta_{\alpha}\in[n^{-1+\varepsilon},\pi-n^{-1+\varepsilon}]
this probability is given by

\displaystyle\quad\mathbb{P}_{{\mathbf{N}}(0,1)}\left(\frac{YX^{\prime}/n-XY^{\prime}/n}{(X^{\prime}/n)^{2}+(Y^{\prime}/n)^{2}}\in\frac{1}{n}U,\ \frac{XX^{\prime}/n+YY^{\prime}/n}{(X^{\prime}/n)^{2}+(Y^{\prime}/n)^{2}}\in\frac{n}{N}V,\ X^{\prime}\in nI_{1},Y^{\prime}\in nI_{2}\right)

(8.2)

\displaystyle\quad=\frac{3}{\pi}\int_{I_{1}\times I_{2}}e^{-(3+O(n^{-\varepsilon}))|z|^{2}}\mathbb{P}_{z}\left(\frac{\left\langle{(X,Y),z^{\perp}}\right\rangle}{|z|^{2}}\in\frac{1}{n}U,\ \frac{\left\langle{(X,Y),z}\right\rangle}{|z|^{2}}\in\frac{n}{N}V\right){\rm d}m(z),

and the remainder of the proof proceeds along similar lines, with integration over
z\in B(0,r)
replaced by integration over
z\in I_{1}\times I_{2}
.
We omit details.

Proposition
8.1
allows one to compute, among other things, the joint law of
n^{2}\,{\text{dist}}(\mathbb{S}^{1},Z(f))
and

n\min_{z\in\mathbb{S}^{1}}|f(z)|
.
